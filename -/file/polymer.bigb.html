= Polymer
{wiki}

A <polymer> is a large molecular chain or network built from many chemically linked repeating units. Statistical chain models idealize its microscopic structure by segment vectors and describe its conformation through distributions, correlations and scattering. A <freely jointed chain> fixes each segment length while allowing independent orientations; a <Gaussian chain> replaces the segment or coarse-grained displacement distribution by a <Gaussian distribution>. These ideal models neglect excluded-volume and other segment interactions.

= Hookean dumbbell model
{c}
{parent=Polymer}
{title2=$U(\mathbf R)=H|\mathbf R|^2/2$}

Represent a dilute <polymer> by two beads connected by a spring obeying <Hooke's law>, with stiffness $H>0$ and bead drag coefficient $\zeta>0$. Neglect bead inertia and <hydrodynamic interactions>. For component-first <velocity gradient> $K_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$, the configuration <Fokker-Planck probability current> is
$$
\mathbf J=K\mathbf R\,\psi-\frac2\zeta(H\mathbf R\psi+k_BT\nabla_{\mathbf R}\psi),\qquad\partial_t\psi=-\nabla_{\mathbf R}\cdot\mathbf J.
$$
The factor two comes from relative motion of both beads. At rest its normalized <probability density> is $\psi_{\rm eq}=(H/(2\pi k_BT))^{3/2}e^{-H R^2/(2k_BT)}$. The <conformation tensor> $C=\langle\mathbf R\mathbf R^T\rangle$ has dimensions of length squared; $(H/(k_BT))C$ is its dimensionless version. The <Hookean dumbbell in planar extension> is a particular imposed flow in this model.

= Oriented connector crossing count
{parent=Hookean dumbbell model}
{title2=$dN/dS=n|\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf n|\psi\,d^3R$}

For a locally uniform distribution of labelled connectors, a connector crosses a small surface when its midpoint lies in a slab of thickness $|\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf n|$. This gives the displayed unsigned count per unit area. Omitting the absolute value gives a signed count, or requires a restriction to one orientation. The signed spring <force> transmitted from the positive side to the negative side is $H\mathbf R\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf n)$, so multiplication by the unsigned count yields <traction> $nH\langle\mathbf R\mathbf R^T\rangle\mathbf n$. Each labelled dumbbell is counted once.

= Hydrodynamic interaction of a Hookean dumbbell
{parent=Hookean dumbbell model}
{title2=$\dot{\mathbf R}=L\mathbf R-2H(1-3a/(2R))\mathbf R/\zeta$}

For identical no-slip <spheres> of radius $a$ linked by a <zero-rest-length spring>, with $R\gg a$ and $\zeta=6\pi\mu a$, the leading <Oseen tensor> interaction reduces their relative spring relaxation rate by $3a/(2R)$. The midpoint follows an imposed incompressible <linear velocity field>. Add the two bead <force balance> equations to prove midpoint advection; subtract them and use $\mathsf G(\mathbf R)\mathbf R=\mathbf R/(4\pi\mu R)$ to obtain the displayed equation. This deterministic far-field model omits bead inertia, thermal forcing, connector drag and near-contact lubrication.

= Free-energy balance of a Hookean dumbbell
{parent=Hookean dumbbell model}
{title2=$\dot{\mathcal F}=nHC:K-\mathcal D$}

For constant number density $n$, constant <temperature>, a homogeneous incompressible <linear velocity field> and decaying configuration tails, define
$$
\mathcal F=nk_BT\int\psi\log\frac\psi{\psi_{\rm eq}}\,d\mathbf R,\qquad
\mathcal D=\frac{2n}{\zeta}\int\psi|H\mathbf R+k_BT\nabla\log\psi|^2\,d\mathbf R\ge0.
$$
This <free energy> is a <relative entropy> times $nk_BT$. Differentiate it using the configuration <Fokker-Planck probability current>. One <integration by parts> gives $\dot{\mathcal F}=n\int\psi(H\mathbf R+k_BT\nabla\log\psi)\cdot\mathbf V\,d\mathbf R$. Substituting the <probability current velocity> gives the displayed balance: the diffusive affine contribution is $-nk_BT\operatorname{tr}K=0$. The polymer <stress tensor> $\sigma_p=n(HC-k_BTI)$ has mechanical power $\sigma_p:K=nHC:K$. Its instantaneous power need not be nonnegative, because it includes changes of stored <free energy>. The nonnegative quantity is $\mathcal D$, and equality of stress power and dissipation requires a stationary <free energy>.

= Drag dissipation of a bead-spring pair
{parent=Hookean dumbbell model}
{title2=$D_{\rm pair}=\zeta|\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R|^2/2$}

When the midpoint follows a <linear velocity field>, the two beads' slip <velocities> are $\pm(\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R)/2$. Summing their individual drag powers $\zeta|\mathbf v_{\rm slip}|^2$ proves the displayed expression. Using a <probability current velocity> in place of the instantaneous relative velocity defines the corresponding overdamped dissipative functional; adding independent thermal velocities is a different stochastic description.

= Thermal bead drag and heat-bath power
{parent=Drag dissipation of a bead-spring pair}
{title2=$D_{\rm gross}=3n\zeta k_BT/m+(n\zeta/2)\operatorname{tr}(KCK^T)$}

If the relative bead velocity $\mathbf v$ is independent of its separation and has a centered <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> with $\langle v_iv_j\rangle=(2k_BT/m)\delta_{ij}$, averaging the <drag dissipation of a bead-spring pair> gives the displayed gross loss. The cross term vanishes by the zero conditional mean. At zero <velocity gradient>, this remains $3n\zeta k_BT/m>0$, although mechanical stress power is zero. A thermal bath replenishes microscopic kinetic energy at equilibrium. Equating this gross loss to polymer stress power omits heat-bath input and cannot follow from the independently specified Maxwellian velocity distribution. The overdamped <free-energy balance of a Hookean dumbbell> uses a <probability current velocity> instead.

= Hookean dumbbell conformation equation
{c}
{parent=Hookean dumbbell model}
{title2=$\dot C-KC-CK^T=4k_BTI/\zeta-4HC/\zeta$}

Multiply the <Hookean dumbbell model> configuration equation by $R_iR_j$ and integrate twice by parts, with sufficient decay at large separation. The <linear velocity field> contributes $KC+CK^T$, the spring drift contributes $-4HC/\zeta$, and <diffusion> contributes $4k_BTI/\zeta$. Thus the dimensionless <conformation tensor> $A=HC/(k_BT)$ obeys $\dot A-KA-AK^T=-(A-I)/\lambda$, with <polymer relaxation time> $\lambda=\zeta/(4H)$. Its left side is an <upper-convected derivative> for this gradient convention.

= Hookean dumbbell conformation equation with anisotropic mobility
{parent=Hookean dumbbell conformation equation}
{title2=$C^{\nabla}=-(2H/\zeta)(MC+CM)+(4k_BT/\zeta)M$}

Let $C=\langle\mathbf R\mathbf R^T\rangle$ be the unnormalized <conformation tensor>, $G_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$ the <velocity gradient>, and $M/\zeta$ a symmetric positive <hydrodynamic mobility matrix> independent of the individual connector. The connector <Fokker-Planck equation> has drift $G\mathbf R-(2H/\zeta)M\mathbf R$ and <diffusion> matrix $(2k_BT/\zeta)M$. Integrating its second moment twice by parts gives the displayed <upper-convected derivative>, $C^{\nabla}=D_tC-GC-CG^T$. If $M$ is a polynomial in $C$, the two matrices commute and the relaxation term simplifies to $-(4H/\zeta)M(C-k_BT I/H)$. No <Gaussian distribution> assumption is required for this <moment closure>.

= Entropic-trap length separation
{parent=Polymer}

Repeated transitions between a broad reservoir and a narrow channel can accumulate length-dependent capture and relaxation delays. Uniform inside-channel drift alone may be <length> independent and therefore insufficient for separation. A time-dependent drive can retain the length-sensitive delays, while stronger <advection> raises throughput but can erase selectivity. A quantitative ordering requires the actual barrier, chain dynamics and driving protocol.

= Insertion barrier of a charged polymer
{parent=Polymer}
{title2=$F(x)=fx-qEx^2/2$}

If only the inserted <length> $x$ feels a uniform inward <electric field>, its charge experiences <force> $qEx$, where $q$ is charge per contour <length>. Integrating gives electric energy $-qEx^2/2$. Adding a constant entropic confinement <force> gives the displayed free-energy profile. Its interior maximum is at $x=f/(qE)$ and has height $f^2/(2qE)$; entry waiting and subsequent directed insertion are distinct times.

= Confined-polymer entry and recoil times
{parent=Insertion barrier of a charged polymer}
{title2=$\tau_{\rm in}=\zeta_\ell L/(qE),\quad\tau_{\rm out}=\zeta_\ell L^2/(2f)$}

With friction $\gamma(x)=\zeta_\ell x$, inward <force> $qEx$ gives constant entry <speed> after capture. With the field off, constant outward <entropic force> gives $x\dot x=-f/\zeta_\ell$ and a quadratic recoil time. Exterior drag, adsorption and the short final end region are omitted. These ideal transport times alone do not establish steady size separation through a whole uniform channel.

= Copolymer melting-point depression
{parent=Statistical copolymer}
{title2=$1/T_m-1/T_m^0=-(R/\Delta h_f)\ln x$}

An ideal dilution estimate for a random copolymer whose comonomer units are excluded from the crystal. Let $x$ be the crystallizable-unit fraction in the melt and $\Delta h_f$ the molar enthalpy of fusion per such unit. Mixing contributes $RT\ln x$ to its liquid chemical potential. Approximate the unmixed liquid-minus-solid chemical-potential difference by $\Delta h_f(1-T/T_m^0)$. Equating the sum to zero gives the displayed relation. For $x<1$, the predicted melting temperature decreases. Nonideal mixing, finite crystallizable sequences, lamellar surface energy and comonomer inclusion can change the result.

= Elastomer
{parent=Polymer}
{wiki}

An elastomer is a polymeric material capable of large substantially recoverable deformation. Flexible chain segments supply configurational <entropy>, while a network of permanent or sufficiently long-lived <cross-links> prevents unrestricted macroscopic flow on the observation time scale. Its <dynamic modulus> depends on whether segmental relaxation can follow the imposed deformation. The <rubbery plateau> and high-frequency glassy response refer to different accessible relaxation regimes.

= Rubber elasticity
{parent=Elastomer}

In a flexible crosslinked <polymer> network, deformation restricts the configurations of chain segments between <cross-links>. The resulting decrease of <entropy> produces a restoring <entropic force>. For an ideal affine Gaussian network, the <shear modulus> is $G=\nu_s k_BT$, where $\nu_s$ is the <number density> of elastically active strands. A nearly incompressible isotropic rubber has <Young's modulus> $E\simeq3G$. At fixed network density within the rubbery regime, both increase approximately linearly with absolute <temperature>. This statement does not describe crossing the glass transition or chemical degradation.

= Entropic network modulus
{parent=Rubber elasticity}
{title2=$G\sim nk_BT\sim k_BT/m^3$}

A network of flexible thermally fluctuating strands stores an order-$k_BT$ elastic free-energy change per active strand under an order-one deformation. For active-strand <number density> $n$, its <shear modulus> is therefore of order $nk_BT$. If one active element occupies a mesh <volume> $m^3$, this becomes $k_BT/m^3$. The numerical coefficient depends on network connectivity, defects and the deformation model. A mesh size alone does not fix the modulus of a general athermal or bending-dominated network; strand stiffness is then additional input.

= Positive-feedback entropic chain
{parent=Rubber elasticity}
{title2=$x=\tanh[(T_c/T)x],\quad T_c=Naf_0/k_B$}

A chain of $N$ up/down segments of length $a$ has extension $L=Nax$ and binary <configurational entropy>. An external force $f(L)=f_0L/a$ has potential $-f_0L^2/(2a)$, obtained by integrating the force. Its mean-field free-energy minima obey the displayed equation. Expansion gives $F-F(0)=Nk_B(T-T_c)x^2/2+Nk_BTx^4/12+\cdots$, so the ordered extension emerges continuously, $|x|\sim\sqrt{3(T_c-T)/T}$, rather than jumping. The equation matches zero-field mean-field theory of the <Ising model>; finite systems round the ideal singular behavior.

= Cross-link density inferred from rubber elasticity
{parent=Rubber elasticity}
{title2=$\nu_s=G/(k_BT)$}

The ideal <rubber elasticity> relation infers active strand <number density> from the <shear modulus>. If a quoted modulus is instead <Young's modulus>, nearly incompressible behavior gives $\nu_s\simeq E/(3k_BT)$. This is not automatically the junction density: for junction functionality $f$ and no dangling ends or loops, counting strand ends gives $\nu_s=f\nu_j/2$. Network fluctuations and inactive strands modify the modulus, so converting it into a chemical <cross-link> count requires a network model.

= Cross-link
{parent=Polymer}

A cross-link connects different segments of a <polymer> network and restricts their independent motion. Permanent chemical cross-links commonly use <covalent bonds>; physical associations can instead have finite lifetimes. A connected permanent network can sustain long-time shear <stress>, whereas an uncrosslinked melt eventually flows. Cross-link density and network structure influence <elastomer> stiffness.

= Polymer physics
{parent=Polymer}
{wiki}

<Polymer physics> connects the conformations and motion of long <molecules> to the properties of a material. A <polymer> can change shape through many segment rotations while its chemical backbone remains intact. This makes the competition between flow and relaxation important: the same chain can behave as a coil in one experiment and an extended object in another. <Soft matter> includes both these flexible structures and <liquid crystals>, whose orientational order produces a different route to strong mechanical and optical response.

= Inverse spool model
{parent=Polymer physics}

An idealized arrangement of confined <DNA> as approximately concentric loops around a central hole, with neighboring strands locally close-packed. It separates curvature-dependent <DNA bending energy> from interstrand interactions. It is a geometrical model, not a universal experimentally established arrangement in every <capsid>.

= Coil-globule transition
{parent=Polymer physics}

The change of an isolated flexible <polymer> from an expanded coil toward a dense <polymer globule> as its <solvent> becomes less favorable. In the <Flory approximation>, $F/(k_BT)\sim R^2/(Na^2)+vN^2/R^3+uN^3/R^6$, where the positive three-segment coefficient $u$ prevents collapse to zero size. Positive $v$ gives swollen-coil scaling, $v=0$ gives the <theta temperature> condition, and negative $v$ favors a dense state with $R^3\sim uN/|v|$. Finite chain <length> rounds the change; <polymer aggregation> is a distinct multi-chain process and must be controlled in an experiment.

= Polymers at interfaces
{parent=Polymer physics}

An interface changes <polymer> conformations through segment-surface affinity, <solvent> selectivity and <confinement>. Adsorbed chains make bound trains, intervening loops and free tails; end-grafted chains cross from isolated mushrooms to overlapping brushes as coverage rises. Surface binding <energy> competes with chain <conformational entropy> and crowding.

= Rotational isomeric state model
{parent=Polymer physics}

A <polymer> conformation model assigning allowed torsional states and statistical weights to successive backbone bonds. Correlations between torsional states can represent local stiffness beyond the fixed-angle, uniformly rotating <freely rotating chain>.

= Hydrodynamic volume
{parent=Polymer physics}

The effective volume governing a dissolved macromolecule’s hydrodynamic behavior and pore accessibility.

= Zimm model
{c}
{parent=Polymer physics}

A dilute-solution bead-spring model including solvent-mediated <hydrodynamic interactions>. In its scaling regime, a chain of size $R$ has translational <diffusion coefficient> of order $k_BT/(\eta_sR)$ and longest relaxation time of order $\eta_sR^3/(k_BT)$. Intermediate-time segment mean-square displacements scale as $t^{2/3}$. Screening of hydrodynamic interactions in a concentrated solution changes the regime toward <Rouse model> behaviour; constraints from <polymer entanglement> require additional physics.

= Rouse model
{c}
{parent=Polymer physics}

An overdamped bead-spring model of <polymer> dynamics with local friction and no long-range <hydrodynamic interaction>. For an ideal unentangled chain of $N$ identical frictional units, the centre-of-mass <diffusion coefficient> is proportional to $N^{-1}$ and the longest internal relaxation time to $N^2$. A tagged segment has an intermediate-time mean-square displacement proportional to $t^{1/2}$ before ordinary whole-chain diffusion dominates. Entanglement adds constraints beyond this model.

= Free-draining polymer friction
{parent=Rouse model}
{title2=$\zeta_N=N\zeta_0$}

With additive drag from $N$ identical <monomers>, $\zeta_N=N\zeta_0$ and centre-of-mass <diffusion> is $D_N=k_BT/(N\zeta_0)$. For <monomer> <Stokes law> $\zeta_0=6\pi\eta a$, this gives $D_N\propto N^{-1}$. The model neglects long-range hydrodynamic coupling and does not alone describe the long-time spatial <diffusion> of a strongly entangled chain.

= Grafted polymer chain
{parent=Polymer physics}

A <polymer> chain tethered by an end to a surface. Its surface <number density> $\sigma_g$ sets a typical lateral separation $D\sim\sigma_g^{-1/2}$. Comparing $D$ with the isolated coil size determines whether grafted chains behave almost independently or must stretch cooperatively away from the surface.

= Grafting-from
{parent=Grafted polymer chain}

Formation of a tethered <polymer> layer by growing chains from reactive sites on a surface. Small <monomers> reach the growth sites without inserting an entire preformed coil, making high <grafting density> more accessible than in <grafting-to>. Initiator availability, termination and control of chain-length distribution remain important.

= Grafting-to
{parent=Grafted polymer chain}

Formation of a tethered <polymer> layer by attaching reactive ends of preformed chains to a surface. The chains can be characterized before attachment, but inserting further coils into an existing <polymer brush> costs <conformational entropy> and produces a coverage-dependent barrier. Reaction chemistry and surface accessibility also limit the final <grafting density>.

= Polymer brush
{parent=Grafted polymer chain}

A layer of sufficiently crowded tethered <polymer> chains stretched away from their attachment surface. In a <good solvent>, overlap increases the interaction contribution to <free energy>; extension relieves this crowding at a cost of chain <entropy>. The mushroom-to-brush crossover occurs at $\sigma_gR^2\sim1$, where $R$ is an isolated coil size. In a simple good-solvent scaling model, the brush height is $H\sim Nb(v\sigma_g/b)^{1/3}$ for effective segment <excluded volume> $v$ and statistical segment length $b$; if $v\sim b^3$, this becomes $H\sim Nb(\sigma_gb^2)^{1/3}$. The scaling requires strong stretching relative to the isolated coil but extension well below the contour length $Nb$.

= Dense-brush packing limit
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$s\leq a/v_0$}

A dense uniform <polymer brush> with $\phi\simeq1$ must have height $H\simeq Nv_0s$ by <polymer-brush volume fraction>. The contour bound $H\leq Na$ therefore requires $s\leq a/v_0$. If $v_0=a^3$, the limit is $sa^2\leq1$; beyond it the putative incompressible branch demands chains longer than their <polymer contour length>. This is a failure of that microscopic packing model. A <Kuhn length> does not alone determine $v_0$.

= Polymer-brush volume fraction
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$\phi=Nv_0s/H$}

Conservation of material in a uniform <polymer brush> gives its mean <volume fraction> as $Nv_0s/H$, with material <volume> $v_0$ per counted segment, chain <length> $N$, <grafting density> $s$ and height $H$. Identifying $v_0$ with $a^3$ is an extra coarse-graining assumption. The <Alexander-de Gennes brush model> then gives $\phi\sim(sa^2)^{(3\nu-1)/(2\nu)}$, including powers $2/3$ in a <good solvent> and $1/2$ in a <theta solvent>.

= Theta-solvent brush height
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$H\sim Na(sa^2)^{1/2}$}

In the <Alexander-de Gennes brush model>, a <theta solvent> gives $D\sim ag^{1/2}$. Stacking $N/g$ blobs then gives the displayed height, with mean <polymer> <volume fraction> $\phi\sim(sa^2)^{1/2}$ for segment <volume> $a^3$. Cancellation of the two-segment <excluded volume> does not remove three-segment repulsion or make the crowded layer freely interpenetrable at no cost.

= Alexander-de Gennes brush model
{c}
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$H\sim Na(sa^2)^{(1-\nu)/(2\nu)}$}

A scaling model of overlapping end-grafted <polymers> assigns each chain a column of lateral width $D\sim s^{-1/2}$ at <grafting density> $s$. Each <concentration blob> contains $g\sim(D/a)^{1/\nu}$ statistical segments, and stacking $N/g$ blobs gives <polymer brush> height $H\sim ND/g$. For assumed segment <volume> $a^3$, the mean <volume fraction> is $\phi\sim Na^3s/H$. It assumes a long many-segment-blob brush and supplies scaling powers rather than a precise chain-end distribution.

= Polymer mushroom-to-brush crossover
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$\sigma_gR_g^2\sim1$}

End-grafted chains are approximately isolated when their lateral spacing $\sigma_g^{-1/2}$ exceeds their coil size. When the spacing becomes smaller, excluded-volume crowding favors extension normal to the substrate. The crossover criterion is a scaling estimate, not a sharp phase transition.

= Poor-solvent brush height
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$h\sim Nv_0\sigma/\phi$}

In a laterally uniform dense <polymer brush> with approximately fixed polymer <volume fraction> $\phi$, conservation of occupied <volume> gives $\phi h=Nv_0\sigma$, with repeat-unit <volume> $v_0$. At fixed solvent quality this gives $h\propto N\sigma$, unlike the <good-solvent brush height>. Isolated collapsed <polymer globules> and laterally segregated grafted layers need not obey this uniform-layer model.

= Good-solvent brush height
{parent=Polymer brush}
{title2=$h\sim Na(\sigma a^2)^{1/3}$}

For an overlapping layer of long flexible grafted <polymers>, <concentration blobs> of width $D\sim\sigma^{-1/2}$ contain $g\sim(D/a)^{5/3}$ statistical units under the three-dimensional <Flory approximation>. A chain of $N/g$ blobs has height $h\sim ND/g$, giving the displayed <good solvent> scaling. Numerical prefactors are not supplied by this argument; extension must remain below the <polymer contour length>.

= Polymer mushroom
{parent=Grafted polymer chain}

A dilute layer of end-tethered <polymer> coils whose lateral separation greatly exceeds the isolated coil size. Each coil retains approximately its isolated-solvent conformation, modified by the impenetrable tethering surface. Increasing the grafting density drives overlap and the crossover to a <polymer brush>.

= Polymer entanglement
{parent=Polymer physics}

A polymer entanglement is a constraint on a chain's motion caused by the inability of neighbouring chain contours to pass through one another. It is not a permanent chemical <cross-link>. In a dense <polymer melt>, these constraints can create long relaxation times and a transient <rubbery plateau>. Chains eventually release constraints through mechanisms such as <reptation> and motion of their neighbours. Entanglement therefore strongly affects dynamics even when the equilibrium size of a long chain remains approximately that of an <ideal chain> because excluded-volume interactions are screened.

= Polymer melt
{parent=Polymer physics}

A polymer melt is a dense fluid of <polymer> chains with little or no solvent. For a long flexible chain in a homogeneous melt, other chains screen its long-range <excluded volume> interactions: distant segments of the chosen chain displace statistically similar segments belonging to its neighbours. On scales above local stiffness, equilibrium chain dimensions are consequently approximately those of an <ideal chain>, even though monomers still cannot physically overlap. Entanglements can strongly affect dynamics without changing this leading large-scale equilibrium size law. Crystallinity, strong orientational order or unusual topological constraints require separate treatment.

= End-to-end distance of a polymer chain
{parent=Polymer physics}
{title2=$R_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\langle|\mathbf r_N-\mathbf r_0|^2\rangle}$}

The end-to-end vector joins the two ends of a <polymer> chain. In an isotropic unforced <polymer solution>, its vector <expected value> is zero; a useful measure of chain size is its root-mean-square length. For an <ideal chain> with $N$ independent isotropic steps of length $b$, the cross terms in the square of the step sum have zero <expected value>, so $R_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=Nb^2$. For a long <Gaussian chain>, its <radius of gyration> obeys $R_g^2=R_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/6$. If $N$ counts chemical <repeat units> instead of independent <Kuhn length> segments, the statistical length used in $R_{\mathrm{rms}}^2=Nb^2$ must be calibrated to that choice of $N$; it is not automatically the physical bond length or the <Kuhn length> itself.

= Polymer contour length
{parent=End-to-end distance of a polymer chain}
{title2=$L=Na$}

The path <length> along a <polymer> backbone. For $N$ links of length $a$ it is $Na$, whereas the <end-to-end distance of a polymer chain> cannot exceed it. A statistical segment count need not equal the chemical <degree of polymerization>.

= Finite extensibility of a polymer chain
{parent=Polymer contour length}
{title2=$R\leq Na$}

The <end-to-end distance of a polymer chain> with $N$ fixed-length links of <length> $a$ cannot exceed its <polymer contour length> $Na$. A <Gaussian chain> <spring> has no such bound, so it eventually becomes an unphysical extrapolation. For a three-dimensional <freely jointed chain> under <force> $f$, the mean extension along the <force> is $Na[\coth x-1/x]$, where $x=fa/(k_BT)$. Its small-$x$ limit is $Nfa^2/(3k_BT)$, and its large-$x$ limit approaches $Na$ from below.

= Reptation
{parent=Polymer physics}

<Reptation> is the snake-like motion of a long <polymer> through the constraints imposed by neighboring chains or a fixed porous matrix. A tube model restricts large transverse motion while allowing curvilinear motion along the chain contour. For a flexible chain with additive segment friction, contour diffusion scales as $N^{-1}$ and the time to renew a contour of length proportional to $N$ scales as $N^3$. With coil size squared proportional to $N$, this gives a centre-of-mass <diffusion coefficient> proportional to $N^{-2}$. Applying this tube picture to weak-field <DNA> <gel electrophoresis> gives an inverse-length mobility over its appropriate regime; field-induced orientation and trapping can invalidate that simple law.

= Tensile blob
{parent=Polymer physics}
{title2=$\xi_t\sim k_BT/f$}

A <tensile blob> is the scale below which thermal conformations of a stretched <polymer> are weakly disturbed by tension $f$. Balancing stretching <work> $f\xi_t$ with $k_BT$ gives $\xi_t\sim k_BT/f$. For free-chain size law $R\sim bg^\nu$, each blob contains $g_t\sim(\xi_t/b)^{1/\nu}$ <monomers>. A stretched string of blobs has extension $z\sim(n/g_t)\xi_t$ and stretching <free energy> of order $k_BTn/g_t$. This description requires blobs larger than individual statistical segments and smaller than the unperturbed coil.

= Good-solvent force-extension scaling
{parent=Tensile blob}
{title2=$R\sim Na(fa/k_BT)^{2/3}$}

For a long flexible <polymer> in a <good solvent>, a <tensile blob> of size $\xi_t\sim k_BT/f$ contains $g\sim(\xi_t/a)^{1/\nu}$ statistical segments. Aligning $N/g$ blobs gives $R\sim(N/g)\xi_t=Na(fa/k_BT)^{1/\nu-1}$. The three-dimensional <Flory approximation> $\nu=3/5$ gives the displayed power. The regime requires $a\ll\xi_t\ll aN^\nu$: below its <force> range the coil has ordinary <linear response>, and above it finite link extension matters. Numerical amplitudes depend on <solvent> quality and segment conventions.<Excluded Volume Effects and Stretched Polymer Chains by Philip Alan Pincus>[abstract and section I, p. 386]{ref}

= Polymer adsorption
{parent=Polymer physics}

<Polymer adsorption> binds a <polymer> near an attractive interface through contacts of some of its <monomers>. The binding <energy> competes with the loss of conformational <entropy>. A chain need not lie flat: noncontacting loops and tails can extend into the <solvent>. In a weak-adsorption blob model, the layer thickness exceeds the <Kuhn length> and local chain statistics remain approximately those of the free chain below that thickness.

= Adsorbed polymer tail
{parent=Polymer adsorption}

A nonadsorbed terminal section extending from a surface-bound part of an adsorbed chain to a free chain end. It has one surface connection, whereas an adsorbed loop has two.

= Adsorbed polymer loop
{parent=Polymer adsorption}

A nonadsorbed chain section whose two ends connect to surface-bound portions of the same adsorbed <polymer>. Loops extend into the <solvent> and contribute to the thickness, <steric repulsion> and <conformational entropy> of an adsorbed layer.

= Adsorbed polymer train
{parent=Polymer adsorption}

A contiguous sequence of segments making contacts with an adsorbing surface. The <adsorption> <energy> gained per contact competes with the loss of chain configurations. A chain can have several trains separated by loops rather than lying wholly flat.

= Equilibrium peeling force of an adsorbed Gaussian chain
{parent=Polymer adsorption}
{title2=$f=\sqrt{6k_BT\mu}/b$}

Let an adsorbed <Gaussian chain> gain <free energy> $\mu>0$ per bound <monomer>. A pulled segment of $n$ <Kuhn length> steps spanning height $z$ has elastic <free energy> $3k_BTz^2/(2nb^2)$, giving $F=-(N-n)\mu+3k_BTz^2/(2nb^2)$. Minimizing over $n$ yields $n=(z/b)\sqrt{3k_BT/(2\mu)}$ and $F_{\min}=-N\mu+z\sqrt{6k_BT\mu}/b$. Its height <derivative> is the constant peeling <force> displayed above. The plateau lasts while $0<n<N$ and the <Gaussian chain> approximation remains valid. In the slab model $F_{\mathrm{ads}}=Nk_BT[(b/\xi)^2-\epsilon b/\xi]$, optimization gives $\mu=k_BT\epsilon^2/4$ and $f=\sqrt{3/2}\,\epsilon k_BT/b$.

= Adsorption blob
{parent=Polymer adsorption}
{title2=$g\sim(\xi/b)^{1/\nu}$}

An <adsorption blob> is a subchain of size comparable to the adsorbed layer thickness $\xi$. If the free-chain size law is $R\sim bg^\nu$, it contains $g\sim(\xi/b)^{1/\nu}$ <monomers>. Confinement costs of order $k_BT$ per blob produce $F_{\mathrm{conf}}\sim Nk_BT/g$. A simple contact fraction $b/\xi$ gives $F/(Nk_BT)\sim(b/\xi)^{1/\nu}-\epsilon b/\xi$. This model predicts $\xi/b\sim\epsilon^{-\nu/(1-\nu)}$ and binding gain per monomer of order $k_BT\epsilon^{1/(1-\nu)}$. It assumes a many-blob layer and a monomer-scale attraction range; it does not replace the full surface-critical theory of <polymer adsorption>.

= Polymer relaxation time
{parent=Polymer physics}
{title2=$\lambda$}

A characteristic <time> for a chosen <polymer> deformation to relax after forcing stops. Its value depends on the measured quantity and the chain model; one <polymer> can have multiple relaxation modes.

= Coil-stretch transition
{parent=Polymer physics}

A <coil-stretch transition> is a sharp change from a coiled to a substantially extended <polymer> conformation under sufficiently strong <extensional flow>. The driving <extensional strain rate> must compete with the chain's relaxation rate, and the available residence <time> matters. A long <polymer relaxation time> lets a weak but sustained flow produce a large effect. The <Hookean dumbbell in planar extension> below isolates this competition; its unbounded growth signals a model limitation, rather than a prediction of infinitely long <molecules>.

= Residence time in planar extension
{parent=Keller four-roller polymer experiment}
{title2=$x(t)=x_0e^{\dot\epsilon t},\quad y(t)=y_0e^{-\dot\epsilon t}$}

An original local flow model has <velocity> $\mathbf v=(\dot\epsilon x,-\dot\epsilon y,0)$ for constant <extensional strain rate> $\dot\epsilon>0$. Its <divergence> is zero, and its trajectories satisfy the displayed equations. Material approaches along $y$ and leaves along $x$. If the idealized observation region ends at $|x|=L$, a trajectory starting at $0<|x_0|<L$ has residence <time>
$$
t_{\rm exit}=\frac{1}{\dot\epsilon}\log\frac{L}{|x_0|}.
$$
For chosen $\dot\epsilon=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $L=1\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $|x_0|=0.01\,\mathrm{mm}$, this is $\log100\,\mathrm s\simeq4.61\,\mathrm s$. Starting ten times farther from the symmetry line halves this particular residence <time>. The mathematical trajectory $x_0=0$ never exits in this model; <diffusion>, imperfect flow and finite apparatus prevent treating that ideal trajectory as a guaranteed infinite exposure in an experiment.

= Hookean dumbbell in planar extension
{c}
{parent=Coil-stretch transition}
{title2=$\dot C=AC+CA^{\mathsf T}-(C-I)/\lambda$}

Represent one <polymer> by two beads joined by an ideal linear entropic spring, and stipulate a dimensionless <conformation tensor> $C$ with equilibrium value $I$. The displayed model uses <velocity gradient> $A=\operatorname{diag}(\dot\epsilon,-\dot\epsilon,0)$ and positive <polymer relaxation time> $\lambda$. It omits finite chain extensibility, interactions with other chains, and feedback from the <polymer> onto the flow. It is a teaching model, not a fitted description of Keller's apparatus.

Define the <Weissenberg number> for this model as $\mathrm{Wi}=\lambda\dot\epsilon$ and dimensionless <time> $u=t/\lambda$. With an initially isotropic $C=I$, the diagonal entries obey
$$
\frac{dC_{xx}}{du}=(2\mathrm{Wi}-1)C_{xx}+1,\qquad
\frac{dC_{yy}}{du}=-(2\mathrm{Wi}+1)C_{yy}+1,\qquad C_{zz}=1.
$$
Below $\mathrm{Wi}=1/2$, both transverse and longitudinal responses approach positive finite steady values:
$$
\boxed{C_{xx}=\frac1{1-2\mathrm{Wi}},\qquad C_{yy}=\frac1{1+2\mathrm{Wi}},\qquad C_{zz}=1.}
$$
For a chosen $\mathrm{Wi}=0.4$, $C_{xx}=5$ and $C_{yy}=5/9$: the longitudinal mean-square separation is five times its quiescent value, while the transverse separation contracts. A root-mean-square length grows as the square root of a variance, so the longitudinal length factor is $\sqrt5$, not five. The anisotropy $C_{xx}-C_{yy}$ is an orientational indicator; converting it to measured <birefringence> requires an additional calibrated optical relation.

\Image[polymer-coil-stretch-relaxation-competition.png]
{title=Extension competes with relaxation in a chosen dumbbell model}

At $\mathrm{Wi}=1/2$, $C_{xx}=1+u$. Above that value, the growing homogeneous solution prevents a positive finite steady state. For instance, $\mathrm{Wi}=0.6$ gives $C_{xx}=6e^{u/5}-5$. Real chains have finite contour lengths, so this growth identifies the failure of the linear spring model. It does not establish a universal threshold for arbitrary flow histories, finite chains or other definitions of <polymer relaxation time>. Combining the growth equation with <residence time in planar extension> also explains why a large instantaneous <Weissenberg number> alone does not guarantee a strongly stretched exiting chain.

= Flory approximation
{c}
{parent=Polymer}
{title2=$\mathcal F/(k_BT)\sim R^2/(Nb^2)+vN^2/R^d$}

The <Flory approximation> balances the entropic cost of expanding an <ideal chain> against a mean-field <excluded volume> interaction. For $N$ segments of length $b$ in $d$ spatial dimensions, minimizing $R^2/(Nb^2)+vN^2/R^d$ gives $R^{d+2}\sim vN^3b^2$. This yields the scaling exponents $3/5$ in three dimensions and $3/4$ in two dimensions. It is a scaling approximation, not an exact numerical free energy or proof of critical exponents; confinement replaces $R^d$ by the accessible coil volume.

= Two-dimensional Flory approximation
{parent=Flory approximation}
{title2=$R\sim(v_2\ell^2)^{1/4}N^{3/4}$}

For a planar <polymer> with $N$ segments of length $\ell$, approximate its coil <free energy> by $F/(k_BT)\sim R^2/(N\ell^2)+v_2N^2/R^2$. The first term is the entropic stretching cost of an <ideal chain>, while $v_2$ is an excluded-area coefficient. Minimizing gives $R^4\sim v_2\ell^2N^3$ and size exponent $\nu=3/4$. For $v_2\sim\ell^2$, this becomes $R\sim\ell N^{3/4}$. A parameter multiplying $N^2/R^2$ must carry area units; using an unsquared segment length there would be dimensionally inconsistent.

= Polymerization
{parent=Polymer}

<Polymerization> converts a <monomer>, or a mixture of <monomers>, into a <polymer>: repeated <chemical reactions> establish a connected molecular chain or network. The central question is how connections form, not merely whether a material becomes solid. Joining small <molecules> can produce a thread that can be handled, while the individual <molecules> remain invisible. See https://old.iupac.org/publications/pac/1974/pdf/4003x0477.pdf[IUPAC, Basic Definitions of Terms Relating to Polymers, definition 2.2].

In <step-growth polymerization>, complementary reactive groups on <monomers> and on shorter or longer chains can react with one another. A two-unit chain can join a three-unit chain to make a five-unit chain; growth does not require a specially active end to add only free <monomers>. An example is the <nylon 6,10> reaction of $\mathrm{H_2N(CH_2)_6NH_2}$ with $\mathrm{ClC(=O)(CH_2)_8C(=O)Cl}$: each <amine> end can form an <amide> connection with an acid-chloride end, with formal elimination of $\mathrm{HCl}$. Both reactants have two suitable ends, allowing repeated joining. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/10-569-synthesis-of-polymers-fall-2006/5474dc576b0669a8a09e9b70e39b1239_lec02_09082006.pdf[Paula Hammond, MIT 10.569 lecture 2: step-growth joining] and https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/10-569-synthesis-of-polymers-fall-2006/d9f9ac7021b0d0f418d3d3c90dbfb94b_lec05_09152006.pdf[Hammond, lecture 5, p. 4: acid chloride–amine reaction].

In <chain-growth polymerization>, a <monomer> reacts with an active site on a growing chain, and the growth step regenerates an active site. Schematically, $P_j^*+M\longrightarrow P_{j+1}^*$, where $j$ counts incorporated <monomeric units> and the star denotes reactivity rather than a particular <electric charge>. <Ethene> forming <polyethylene> is an example: the double <chemical bond> is converted into single <chemical bonds> as the <carbon> backbone grows. Initiation creates the active chains; subsequent reactions can deactivate them or transfer reactivity to another <molecule>. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C00958/plain[IUPAC: chain polymerization] and https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/31-1-chain-growth-polymers[John McMurry, Organic Chemistry, 31.1: chain growth]. Whether growth is stepwise or carried by an active site is a different question from whether small byproducts are released: <chain-growth polymerization> can also release them.

**A motivating experiment: drawing nylon from a liquid interface.** Feldwinn's UCSB demonstration places $25\,\mathrm{mL}$ of an aqueous <chemical solution> of hexamethylenediamine, $\mathrm{H_2N(CH_2)_6NH_2}$, in a $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ beaker, then gently adds $25\,\mathrm{mL}$ of sebacoyl chloride, $\mathrm{ClC(=O)(CH_2)_8C(=O)Cl}$, in an organic <solvent>. The procedure specifies hexane; other parts of that handout list cyclohexane. The layers remain separate. The instructor catches material at their interface with tweezers and winds it slowly onto a glass rod; the handout reports continued spooling of <nylon 6,10>. It specifies freshly prepared acid-chloride <chemical solution> and washing the thread before touching it. These are observations and conditions of a supervised laboratory demonstration, not measurements of chain lengths. See https://people.chem.ucsb.edu/feldwinn/darby/DemoLibrary/DemoPDFs/Demo044.pdf[Feldwinn, UCSB Demonstration Library, Demo 044, pp. 1–3].

The mechanistic interpretation is that the <monomers> encounter one another near the interface, where <amide> connections produce the film; withdrawing it exposes a region where more material can form. An authored research demonstration independently reports a rapidly formed interfacial film collapsing into a thread on withdrawal, using cyclohexane and an aqueous <chemical solution> containing potassium hydroxide. See https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jchemed.6b00767[Polymer Day: Outreach Experiments for High School Students, Journal of Chemical Education (2017), nylon demonstration]. This is the striking observation: a thread much longer than the beaker can emerge because material is continually supplied to the formation region, rather than because a pre-existing thread was hidden in the liquid.

Useful *proposed controls*, not results reported in those demonstrations, omit one <monomer> at a time while retaining the same <solvents>, liquid amounts, and pulling procedure. The prediction is loss of a renewable drawable <nylon 6,10> film; merely finding cloudiness would not establish <polymerization>, since other <chemical reactions>, including acid-chloride <hydrolysis>, can occur. See https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/21-4-chemistry-of-acid-halides[McMurry, 21.4: competing acid-chloride hydrolysis]. Stopping and restarting withdrawal would probe renewal at the interface. Even successful drawing does not measure the <degree of polymerization>, establish a chain-length <probability distribution>, or prove the entire growth mechanism. Chemical identification and a molecular-size measurement would be needed for those stronger conclusions. These controls and limits are inferences from the setup, not invented experimental data.

**Why almost complete reaction matters in step growth.** For ideal linear <step-growth polymerization>, assume exactly two reactive groups per starting <molecule>, equal numbers of complementary groups, only joining of different chains, no rings, no branching, and no loss of chain material. Let $N_0$ count starting <monomer molecules>, including both reactant species, and let $p$ be the fraction of either type of reactive group consumed. There are initially $N_0$ groups of each type, so $pN_0$ connections reduce the number of chain-bearing <molecules> to $N=N_0(1-p)$; the count excludes small byproducts but includes unreacted <monomers>. The number-average <degree of polymerization>, measured in <monomeric units>, is therefore
$$
X_n=\frac{N_0}{N}=\frac{1}{1-p}.
$$
Thus $p=0.90$, $0.99$, and $0.999$ give $X_n=10$, $100$, and $1000$. Losing the last fraction of unreacted groups matters enormously. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/10-569-synthesis-of-polymers-fall-2006/c7b7a987f7d5b66e0832be42fbf52b7d_lec03_09112006.pdf[Hammond, lecture 3: balanced step-growth conversion]. This counts residues of both feed species; a <nylon 6,10> interior <constitutional repeating unit> contains two such <monomeric units>.

For an $AA+BB$ feed with $A_0\leq B_0$ initial reactive-group counts, put $r=A_0/B_0$ and now let $p$ denote the fraction of the *limiting $A$ groups* consumed. Under the same linear, no-ring assumptions, $N_0=(A_0+B_0)/2$ and $N=N_0-pA_0$, giving
$$
X_n=\frac{1+r}{1+r-2rp}.
$$
For $r=0.98$, even complete consumption of $A$ gives $X_n=(1.98)/(0.02)=99$. <Stoichiometry> controls chain length as well as yield. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/10-569-synthesis-of-polymers-fall-2006/5474dc576b0669a8a09e9b70e39b1239_lec02_09082006.pdf[Hammond, lecture 2, pp. 2–3: stoichiometric imbalance]. The liquid-interface demonstration is not a homogeneous, balanced reactor, so its thread length cannot be inserted into this formula as a molecular chain length.

**A contrasting chain count.** In ideal <living polymerization>, assume no chain termination or irreversible chain transfer, all chains initiated before the counted growth, one chain per effective initiator, a fixed chain count $C$, and every consumed <monomer molecule> incorporated into those chains. The useful connection is between local chemistry and a macroscopic material: selecting reactive ends and their proportions controls connectivity, while the resulting <polymer>'s conformation is a separate problem described by models such as the <freely jointed chain> or <Gaussian chain>. = Monomer functionality
{parent=Polymerization}
{title2=$f$}

The number of reactive connections available on a <monomer> under a specified <polymerization> <chemistry>. One-functional units can terminate chains, two-functional units can build chains, and higher functionality can produce branching and networks.

= Step-growth polymerization
{parent=Polymerization}
{wiki}

In <step-growth polymerization>, suitable reactive groups on <molecules> of different chain lengths can join. The linear count and its assumptions are given in <polymerization>.

A one-unit <monomer>, a two-unit chain, and a twenty-unit chain can all be joining partners if their <functional groups> are compatible and accessible. Schematically, $P_j+P_k\longrightarrow P_{j+k}$: the subscripts count <monomeric units>, and end-group chemistry is suppressed. In <chain-growth propagation>, by contrast, $P_j^*+M\longrightarrow P_{j+1}^*$ requires a free <monomer> and a <polymerization-active site>. An arbitrary pair of existing chains is not a propagation pair in that mechanism. The practical consequence of <step-growth polymerization> is that most <monomers> can have joined something while the average chain is still short.

Whether a joining step releases a small <molecule> is a separate question. <Polycondensation> releases small products during the joining chemistry, as in the <nylon 6,10> demonstration in <polymerization>. <Polyaddition> joins suitable chains without that elimination: reacting a <diol> with a molecule bearing two <isocyanate> groups can build a <polyurethane>. Thus <step-growth polymerization> and <polycondensation> are not synonyms.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[polyaddition, P04720, definition and note 1; polycondensation, P04722, definition and growth-step equation; source recommendation p. 2307]{ref}

= Linear step-growth molecule count
{parent=Step-growth polymerization}
{title2=$N=N_0-J$}

Use an ideal feed in which every starting <molecule> has exactly two suitable <functional groups>: either one $A$ and one $B$, or two $A$ groups mixed with two $B$ groups. Only an $A$–$B$ pairing can form a joining <chemical bond>. Assume no loss of feed-derived material, no splitting of chains, no competing consumption of the counted <functional groups>, no branching, and no <cyclization in step-growth polymerization>. Linear here describes connectivity; a chain can bend in space. Small byproducts of <polycondensation> do not enter the chain-bearing <molecule> count.

Let $N_0$ count all initial feed <molecules>, $J$ the number of intermolecular joins, and $N$ all remaining chain-bearing <molecules>, including unreacted <monomers>. Each join turns two <molecules> into one, so $N=N_0-J$ exactly. If chain $i$ contains $j_i$ feed-derived <monomeric units>, conservation gives $\sum_{i=1}^{N}j_i=N_0$. Consequently the number-average <degree of polymerization> is
$$
X_n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}j_i}{N}=\frac{N_0}{N_0-J}.
$$
This is a count of both feed species' <monomeric units>, rather than a count of interior <constitutional repeating units>. For $AA+BB$ chemistry an interior repeat often contains two feed-derived units, as explained in <monomer>. Equal <functional group> reactivities are not needed for this bookkeeping at a specified $J$; predicting a time course or a chain-length <probability distribution> requires additional assumptions.

= Functional-group conversion in step growth
{parent=Linear step-growth molecule count}
{title2=$p$}

In a balanced bifunctional feed there are initially $N_0$ $A$ groups and $N_0$ $B$ groups. Define the <functional-group conversion in step growth> by $p=J/N_0$: the reacted fraction of either group type. Each join consumes one of each. Therefore $N=N_0(1-p)$. In the eight-$AB$ example, three joins give $p=3/8$ and four give $p=1/2$. In the latter state only three of eight starting <monomer molecules> remain isolated: their disappearance fraction is $5/8$, different from $p=1/2$. Neither this group-based $p$ nor disappearance of isolated <monomers> is the incorporated-feed fraction in the ideal <living polymerization> count.

With unequal initial counts $A_0\leq B_0$, this article always uses $p=J/A_0$, the conversion of the limiting $A$ groups. The reacted fraction of $B$ groups is $J/B_0=rp$, where $r=A_0/B_0$. The fraction of all initial <functional groups> consumed is $2J/(A_0+B_0)=2rp/(1+r)$. At nonzero conversion these three fractions coincide only at $r=1$. Naming the measured group type prevents an apparently correct equation from using the wrong conversion.

= Carothers equation
{c}
{parent=Step-growth polymerization}
{title2=$X_n=1/(1-p)$}

The balanced <Carothers equation> follows immediately from <linear step-growth molecule count> and <functional-group conversion in step growth>:
$$
X_n=\frac{1}{1-p}.
$$
The same <Carothers equation> extends to unequal group counts once the limiting-group convention is specified.<Generalization of the Carothers equation for linear step growth polymers by Emilio Meaurio>[Introduction, equations (1)–(6): Carothers equation, end-group count and limiting-$A$ conversion]{ref}

For an original balanced feed of $2000$ <monomer molecules>, $1900$, $1960$, and $1990$ intermolecular joins give $p=0.95$, $0.98$, and $0.995$. The remaining counts are $100$, $40$, and $10$, hence number-average <degree of polymerization> values $20$, $50$, and $200$. The last thirty joins quadruple the average even though they consume only another $1.5\%$ of the initial groups of either type. These are exact synthetic counts, not experimental measurements.

The <derivative> $dX_n/dp=(1-p)^{-2}$ explains the sensitivity: near $p=0.99$, an absolute conversion uncertainty of $0.001$ changes the predicted <degree of polymerization> substantially. The exact balanced values at $p=0.989$ and $0.991$ are approximately $90.9$ and $111.1$, instead of $100$. An instrument reporting rounded “complete conversion” cannot distinguish those chain lengths. In a finite no-ring system, even one chain retains two ends; the maximum $p$ is $1-1/N_0$ and $X_n=N_0$. The formal divergence at $p=1$ is a large-system limit, not an infinite chain in a finite flask.

= Stoichiometric imbalance in step growth
{parent=Carothers equation}
{title2=$r=A_0/B_0$}

For a strictly bifunctional $AA+BB$ feed, label the limiting <functional groups> $A$, set $0<r=A_0/B_0\leq1$, and define $p=J/A_0$ as in <functional-group conversion in step growth>. The initial <molecule> count is $N_0=(A_0+B_0)/2$, and each intermolecular join consumes one $A$ and one $B$. Thus
$$
N=\frac{A_0+B_0}{2}-pA_0,\qquad
X_n=\frac{1+r}{1+r-2rp}.
$$
This version of the <Carothers equation> uses limiting-group conversion, not the fraction of all groups reacted.<Generalization of the Carothers equation for linear step growth polymers by Emilio Meaurio>[Introduction, equations (2)–(6), especially the paragraph before equation (3): A is the limiting reactant used to define p]{ref}

As an original finite example, mix $95$ $AA$ <monomer molecules> with $100$ $BB$ <monomer molecules>. Then $(N_0,A_0,B_0)=(195,190,200)$ and $r=0.95$. At $p=0.90$, $171$ joins leave $24$ <molecules>, giving $X_n=195/24=8.125$. At $p=1$, $190$ joins leave five chains and ten unreacted $B$ groups; every chain has two $B$ ends, and $X_n=39$. Continuing to wait cannot remove the missing-$A$ constraint within this model.

For $r<1$, complete limiting-group conversion gives the ceiling $X_{n,\max}=(1+r)/(1-r)$. Reaching a target $X_n=K>1$ therefore requires $r\geq(K-1)/(K+1)$, even before considering incomplete conversion. For $K=200$, $r\geq199/201\approx0.99005$. Equality requires $p=1$; any lower conversion demands closer balance. The imbalance $1-r$ is measured relative to the initial number of $B$ groups, not relative to the total number of groups. A monofunctional impurity changes the initial groups-per-<molecule> count, so it cannot be inserted into this strictly bifunctional formula by retaining an unexamined $r$.

= Cyclization in step-growth polymerization
{parent=Step-growth polymerization}

In <cyclization in step-growth polymerization>, compatible <functional groups> on the same chain react and close it into a ring. They consume groups without merging two <molecules>. If $R$ ring-closing events and $J$ intermolecular joins occur in an otherwise balanced bifunctional feed, the group conversion is $p=(J+R)/N_0$, while $N=N_0-J=N_0(1-p)+R$. Thus
$$
X_n=\frac{N_0}{N_0(1-p)+R}.
$$
For example, an $AB$ dimer can close its remaining $A$ and $B$ ends: two group pairings have occurred among two starting <monomer molecules>, giving $p=1$, but the resulting single cyclic <molecule> has only two <monomeric units>. This counterexample shows exactly where the linear <Carothers equation> fails. Rings have no chain ends, so counting every chain-bearing <molecule> as two ends also fails.

The inference is increasing chain size alongside depletion of reactive groups, consistent with <step-growth polymerization>. It is not an exact measurement of $X_n$ in the toy model. = Functionality in polymerization
{parent=Polymerization}
{title2=$f$}

The <functionality in polymerization> is the number of reactive <functional groups> on a <molecule> for the specified joining chemistry. The <linear step-growth molecule count> assumes $f=2$ for every feed <molecule>. A <diol> supplies two <hydroxy groups> in the <polyurethane> example; an impurity supplying only one suitable group can cap a chain. A group count must reflect the selected <chemical reaction>, not simply every chemically recognizable group in the <molecule>.

= Gelation
{parent=Polymerization}

<Gelation> is formation of an extended connected network. In <step-growth polymerization>, feed <molecules> with <functionality in polymerization> above two can introduce branching and permit <gelation>. The <linear step-growth molecule count>'s two-ended-chain interpretation and bifunctional <Carothers equation> must not silently be applied to such networks. Each join between distinct connected pieces still reduces their count by one, but neither that identity nor average size alone specifies the onset of <gelation>.

= Chain-growth polymerization
{parent=Polymerization}
{wiki}

= Chain polymerization
{synonym}

In <chain-growth polymerization>, each growth step joins a <monomer> to a reactive site on a <polymer> chain and regenerates that reactive site. This is the precise mechanistic restriction in IUPAC's definition: growth occurs exclusively through these monomer–chain-site reactions. The word chain refers to the succession of site-regenerating <chemical reactions>. It does not merely mean that the product looks like a long chain. <Chain-growth initiation> starts this succession; <chain-growth propagation> continues it; <chain-growth termination> and <chain transfer in chain polymerization> can change its fate.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[chain polymerization, C00958, definition and notes 1–2; source recommendation p. 2306]{ref}

The contrast with <step-growth polymerization> concerns the permitted joining partners. There, suitable reactive groups can join two existing chains, for example a two-unit chain and a three-unit chain. In <chain-growth propagation>, a free <monomer> adds to a <polymerization-active site>. Release of a small byproduct is a separate classification question: IUPAC explicitly allows condensative <chain polymerization>.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[chain polymerization, C00958, notes 3–6]{ref} The useful consequence is that a few growing chains can incorporate many <monomer molecules> while much of the feed remains unreacted. Their <degree of polymerization> depends on how those units are shared among chains, as <finite chain-growth bookkeeping> makes explicit.

= Polymerization-active site
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

A <polymerization-active site> is the reactive location that enables the next <chain-growth propagation> step. In the schematic
$$
P_j^*+M\longrightarrow P_{j+1}^*,
$$
$M$ is one free <monomer molecule>, $j$ is the chain's <degree of polymerization> in incorporated <monomeric units>, and the star marks the <polymerization-active site>. The site's reactive character survives the growth step, although the <chemical atoms> carrying it can change. The star is not an extra unit, a literal particle, or necessarily a positive or negative <electric charge>. Actual <reaction mechanisms> can involve an unpaired <electron>, a <cation>, an <anion>, or a metal-associated site; the abstract notation does not identify which. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/31.1 Chain-Growth Polymers>[John McMurry, Organic Chemistry, §31.1: successive reactive intermediates and radical, cationic and anionic initiation]{ref}

For bookkeeping below, each linear active chain has exactly one <polymerization-active site>. This is a stipulated model, not a property of every <chain polymerization>: a chain can have several reactive sites, and a reversibly inactive chain can remain capable of future growth. Count currently active sites separately from all chains capable of later reactivation.

= Chain-growth initiation
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

<Chain-growth initiation> creates a chain capable of <chain-growth propagation>. Write
$$
I^*+M\longrightarrow P_1^*.
$$
This schematic includes the first incorporated <monomeric unit>; any preceding production of $I^*$ is a separate step. If $C$ counts <polymer> <molecules>, $A$ counts polymer-bound <polymerization-active sites>, and $S$ counts incorporated <monomeric units>, this event changes $(C,A,S)$ by $(1,1,1)$. The free $I^*$ was excluded from $A$, so this does not assert creation of reactivity from nothing. An initiator feed amount need not equal the number of successful chains: the efficiency of forming productive chains and the number of initiating species per initiator matter. <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/31.1 Chain-Growth Polymers>[McMurry, §31.1, initiating species and the first reactive intermediate]{ref}

= Chain-growth propagation
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

In the one-site linear model, <chain-growth propagation> changes $(C,A,S)$ by $(0,0,1)$: one <monomer molecule> is consumed, one <monomeric unit> is incorporated, and the <polymerization-active site> is regenerated. Thus longer chains do not imply more active sites. Repeated <chain-growth propagation> can increase $S$ substantially with $A$ unchanged. This is a material count; it supplies no universal time dependence or empirical <chemical kinetics> formula.

= Chain-growth termination
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

<Chain-growth termination> removes the ability of a reactive chain carrier to continue the succession of growth steps.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[termination, T06274, definition; source recommendation p. 1173]{ref} Two illustrative outcomes have different molecule counts:
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_j^*+P_k^*&\longrightarrow P_{j+k}, &&\Delta(C,A,S)=(-1,-2,0),\\
P_j^*+P_k^*&\longrightarrow P_j+P_k, &&\Delta(C,A,S)=(0,-2,0).
\end{aligned}
$$
The first joins the two chains into one inactive <polymer> <molecule>. The second leaves two inactive <polymer> <molecules>, as can occur when one chain end transfers <hydrogen> to the other instead of their ends joining. End-group chemistry is suppressed in these unit-count symbols; they are not complete <molecular formulas>. Losing two <polymerization-active sites> does not mean losing two chains or any incorporated <monomeric units>. A temporarily inactive, reactivatable site must not be counted as irreversible <chain-growth termination>.

= Chain transfer in chain polymerization
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

= Chain transfer
{synonym}

In <chain transfer in chain polymerization>, a growing chain can lose its <polymerization-active site> while another <molecule> acquires reactivity. In a common atom-transfer picture, the old chain becomes inactive and the new carrier can initiate further <chain-growth propagation>.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[chain transfer, C00963, definition and subsequent-polymerization example; source recommendation p. 1094]{ref} The number of reactive carriers can survive even though the identity of the growing chain changes. If the new carrier successfully starts a new chain, $C$ increases; a fixed active-carrier count therefore does not imply a fixed <polymer> <molecule> count. Whether transfer is irreversible, reversible, or involves an already existing chain matters for the final bookkeeping.

= Finite chain-growth bookkeeping
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}
{title2=$X_n=S/C$}

Stipulate a feed of exactly $M_0=40$ <monomer molecules>, four successfully initiated linear chains, one <polymerization-active site> per chain, no <chain transfer in chain polymerization>, no loss of incorporated units, and no further <chain-growth initiation>. Count only initiated chains in $C$; exclude unreacted <monomers>. Assume each consumed <monomer molecule> contributes exactly one <monomeric unit>. With incorporated feed fraction $q=S/M_0$, the number-average <degree of polymerization> is the counting identity
$$
X_n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{C}j_i}{C}=\frac{S}{C}=\frac{qM_0}{C}.
$$
This is conditional conservation bookkeeping, not a universal empirical law. Immediately after <chain-growth initiation>, the four lengths are $(1,1,1,1)$, so $(C,A,S)=(4,4,4)$ and $q=0.1$. Stipulate twelve subsequent <chain-growth propagation> events, three on each chain: the lengths become $(4,4,4,4)$, giving $(C,A,S)=(4,4,16)$, $q=0.4$ and $X_n=4$. Sixteen units have been incorporated, while twenty-four free <monomer molecules> remain. The chain-length assignment is deliberately chosen, not a prediction of equal growth in an experiment.

= Living versus terminating chain populations
{parent=Chain-growth polymerization}

The defining idealization of <living polymerization> excludes <chain-growth termination> and irreversible <chain transfer in chain polymerization>. Fast, effective <chain-growth initiation> is an additional condition behind a nearly fixed growing-chain population, not a guarantee contained in the name alone.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[living polymerization, L03597, definition and initiation/propagation comparison; living polymer, LT07156, definition specifies irreversible transfer]{ref} If all four chains in <finite chain-growth bookkeeping> remain active, completing the feed keeps $C=A=4$ and gives $X_n=10$. Stipulate six further additions to each chain, obtaining $(10,10,10,10)$. Other allocations with the same total also satisfy $X_n=10$: living does not mean identical lengths.

Alternatively, stipulate that the first two length-four chains undergo <chain-growth termination> without joining, while the other two each receive twelve more <monomer molecules>. The final lengths are $(4,4,16,16)$, with $(C,A,S)=(4,2,40)$. The mean <degree of polymerization> is still $10$, but two chains have stopped and two carry the remaining growth. The population <variance> of lengths is $36$, compared with zero in the deliberately equal living allocation. These are synthetic populations, not measured molecular distributions and not a claim that all terminating processes are broader than all living processes. Indeed, assigning all twenty-four remaining additions to one still-active chain gives the living allocation $(28,4,4,4)$, with the same mean but <variance> $108$. Comparable start times and opportunities for <chain-growth propagation> are extra assumptions behind an expectation of more uniform lengths.

<Size-exclusion characterization of chain polymers> separates dissolved chains using porous columns and records an elution signal. = Monomer
{parent=Polymer}

A <monomer> is a <chemical substance> whose <molecules> can undergo <polymerization> and contribute units to the essential structure of a <polymer>. A <monomer molecule> is the individual reactant; its incorporated <monomeric unit> is the portion identifiable as derived from that reactant. Incorporation can change <chemical bonds> and release small byproducts, so the unit in the chain need not have the complete <molecular formula> of the feed <molecule>. See https://old.goldbook.iupac.org/html/M/M04019.html[IUPAC: monomer molecule] and https://doi.org/10.1515/pac-2023-0304[IUPAC, A brief guide to polymer terminology: monomeric units].

Three different counts answer three different questions. <Monomer molecules> describe the feed; <monomeric units> trace that feed's contributions to the product; a <constitutional repeating unit> describes the smallest chemical pattern repeating along a regular chain. In <nylon 6,10>, two different <monomers> supply one interior <constitutional repeating unit>,
$$
\mathrm{{-}NH{-}(CH_2)_6{-}NH{-}C(=O){-}(CH_2)_8{-}C(=O){-}}.
$$
Its <molecular formula> is $\mathrm{C_{16}H_{30}N_2O_2}$. The diamine feed is $\mathrm{C_6H_{16}N_2}$, and the diacid-chloride feed is $\mathrm{C_{10}H_{16}Cl_2O_2}$; subtracting two $\mathrm{HCl}$ gives the interior-repeat budget. For a finite linear chain made from $n$ of each reactant, there are $2n-1$ joining connections, hence $2n-1$ formal $\mathrm{HCl}$ eliminations, rather than $2n$. One <amine> end and one acid-chloride end remain in that ideal unmodified chain. This is a <stoichiometry> check on the depicted reaction, not a claim that the washed experimental thread retains those ends. The reacting end-group chemistry is documented in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/10-569-synthesis-of-polymers-fall-2006/d9f9ac7021b0d0f418d3d3c90dbfb94b_lec05_09152006.pdf[Hammond, lecture 5, p. 4]; the specified <nylon 6,10> reactant pair is reported in https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jchemed.6b00767[Polymer Day, nylon demonstration].

<Ethene> shows the reverse counting mismatch. One $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2}$ <monomer molecule> contributes a two-<carbon> $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}}$ <monomeric unit> to ideal linear <polyethylene>, without a small byproduct in that growth step. But the smallest <constitutional repeating unit> of its uniform backbone is just $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}}$: a chain containing $m$ incorporated <ethene> <monomeric units> contains $2m$ such interior one-<carbon> repeats. End groups are additional bookkeeping. The frequently drawn $[\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}}]_m$ is useful for tracing the feed, although it is not the smallest constitutional repeat. The minimum-pattern definition is given in https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01286/plain[IUPAC: constitutional repeating unit]; the <ethene> growth chemistry is described by <Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/31.1 Chain-Growth Polymers>[McMurry, Organic Chemistry, 31.1]{ref}.

A segment of a <freely jointed chain> or <Gaussian chain> instead belongs to a statistical model. It can represent many <chemical bonds> and several <constitutional repeating units>; its length and the segment count are chosen to describe chain conformation at the intended scale. The three-repeats-per-segment drawing in <polymerization-monomers-interface-units> is a deliberately arbitrary coarse-graining example, not a measured <Kuhn length>. Always state the counting convention before substituting a segment count for a <degree of polymerization>. This separation lets chemistry track which <chemical atoms> entered the material while statistical models track how the material bends and extends.

= Nucleic acid
{parent=Polymer}
{wiki}

A <nucleic acid> is a <polymer> built from linked <nucleotides>. The two principal kinds are <DNA> and <RNA>. Each <nucleotide> contributes a <sugar>, a <nucleobase>, and <phosphate> to the chain; the <nucleobase> is only the letter-bearing component, not the entire <nucleotide>. <DNA> uses <deoxyribose>, whereas <RNA> uses <ribose>. A chain of six <nucleotides> is therefore not six free <nucleobases> placed beside one another. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Nucleic-Acids[NHGRI: nucleic acids].

The chemical scaffold and the variable lettering do different jobs. <Covalent bonds> maintain the <sugar>–<phosphate> backbone, while the order of attached <nucleobases> specifies the <nucleic acid sequence>. A <phosphodiester bond> links the $3'$ position of one <sugar> to the $5'$ position of the next through <phosphate>. These position numbers refer to <carbon> <chemical atoms> in the <sugar>; they define a strand's direction even when it bends or forms a circle. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21134/[Brown, Genomes, 2nd edition: the human genome].

This division is useful: the backbone supplies a connected physical record, and <base pairing> allows its letters to recognize partners. <DNA replication> copies that record, and <transcription> uses a <DNA> template to make <RNA>. <RNA> can also fold into functional shapes through <base pairing> within one chain; a <nucleic acid sequence> specifies chemical order, not a requirement for a straight shape. The beauty is that the same kind of <polymer> can carry a message and form a structure that acts on messages.

= Nucleic acid sequence
{parent=Nucleic acid}
{wiki}

= Nucleotide sequence
{synonym}

A <nucleic acid sequence> records the ordered <nucleobases> of a <nucleic acid> strand, conventionally read from $5'$ to $3'$. The standard <DNA> alphabet is <adenine> A, <cytosine> C, <guanine> G, and <thymine> T; the standard <RNA> alphabet uses <uracil> U in place of <thymine> T. Each letter denotes the <nucleobase> attached to one <nucleotide>, so the six-letter <DNA> <nucleic acid sequence> $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ACGTAC}\text{-}3'$ describes six linked <nucleotides>. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Base-Pair[NHGRI: representing DNA by a sequence of letters].

Order carries information that composition loses. Both ACGTAC and AACCGT contain two <adenine>, two <cytosine>, one <guanine>, and one <thymine> <nucleobase>, but their <nucleic acid sequences> differ at positions 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. At position 2, for example, the first has <cytosine> and the second <adenine>. Reporting only the four counts cannot reconstruct either ordered record.

To obtain the partner of $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ACGTAC}\text{-}3'$, apply the <DNA> <base pairing> rules position by position: A selects T, C selects G, G selects C, T selects A, A selects T, and C selects G. Alignment gives $3'\text{-}\mathrm{TGCATG}\text{-}5'$. Its opposite end labels matter because the two are <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>. Reading the partner from its own $5'$ end reverses the aligned lettering and yields the <reverse complement>, $5'\text{-}\mathrm{GTACGT}\text{-}3'$.

\Image[dna-reverse-complement.png]
{title=Complement, then reverse: <DNA> ACGTAC has aligned partner TGCATG and <reverse complement> GTACGT}

Reversal alone gives CATGCA; complementation alone gives aligned TGCATG. Neither operation alone gives the partner written in the usual $5'$-to-$3'$ convention. Applying the <reverse complement> operation twice returns ACGTAC: GTACGT complements to CATGCA, which reverses to ACGTAC. This convention makes a single reported strand sufficient to specify an ideally complementary <DNA> duplex, provided its direction is known.

= Base pair

{title2=bp}

A <base pair> is an association of two <nucleobases> through <hydrogen bonds> in a compatible arrangement. It commonly joins opposite strands of a <nucleic acid double helix>, but can also join two regions of one folded <RNA> strand. Canonical Watson–Crick <DNA> <base pairs> match <adenine> with <thymine> and <guanine> with <cytosine>; canonical <RNA> <base pairs> use <adenine> with <uracil> instead. The <nucleobases> belong to complete <nucleotides>, including their attached <sugars> and backbone <phosphate>. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Base-Pair[NHGRI: base pair].

The abbreviation bp also measures the length of a paired region. Our ACGTAC/TGCATG duplex has six <base pairs>, containing twelve <nucleotides> altogether. Its upper strand contains two <adenine>, two <cytosine>, one <guanine>, and one <thymine> <nucleobase>; the lower contains one, one, two, and two respectively. Combined, the counts are three of each <nucleobase>. Equality of total <adenine> and <thymine>, or of total <guanine> and <cytosine>, need not hold within either individual strand.

= Base pairing
{parent=Base pair}

<Base pairing> is the recognition and association of compatible <nucleobases>. In canonical Watson–Crick geometry, an <adenine>–<thymine> <base pair> has two <hydrogen bonds>, while a <guanine>–<cytosine> <base pair> has three. These counts refer to those specific geometries and usual chemical forms; they are not a definition of every possible <base pair>. <RNA> can also contain other pairings, and canonical <adenine>–<uracil> pairing replaces <adenine>–<thymine> pairing. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/14-2-dna-structure-and-sequencing[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: DNA structure].

= Nucleic acid double helix
{parent=Nucleic acid}
{wiki}

A <nucleic acid double helix> consists of two <nucleic acid> strands winding around a common axis. In the familiar <DNA> arrangement, <sugar>–<phosphate> backbones lie outside and paired <nucleobases> lie inside, stacked along the axis. The strands are <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>. The flattened <base pairing> ladder above displays partners and direction; winding that ladder into a <helix> adds spatial organization. <RNA> can also form double-helical paired regions, so the geometry is not exclusive to <DNA>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26821/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: DNA structure].

The useful surprise is that a structure explains a copying principle. Complementary <base pairs> allow each <DNA> strand to specify the other's <nucleic acid sequence>; separate strands can each act as templates in <DNA replication>. This is a structural constraint, while the <enzymes> and stages of <DNA replication> supply the actual process. A <nucleic acid sequence> specifies letter order without, by itself, giving every detail of the three-dimensional <helix>.

On 25 April 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick published a model with two oppositely directed chains, outside <phosphate> groups, and specific <base pairs>. Their short paper explicitly connected this pairing to a possible copying mechanism, while describing the model as requiring further experimental tests. See the original https://doi.org/10.1038/171737a0[Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid, Nature 171, 737–738]{ref}, with an accessible https://inters.org/files/watsoncrick1953.pdf[reprint of the original paper]{ref}.

Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's paper in the same issue had a different role: it presented <X-ray> <diffraction> evidence from <DNA> fibres and its structural interpretation, including the helical character of the wetter B form and evidence for <phosphate> groups on the outside. They distinguished forms produced at different water contents and assessed compatibility with the Watson–Crick model. Read their original https://doi.org/10.1038/171740a0[Molecular Configuration in Sodium Thymonucleate, Nature 171, 740–741 (1953)]{ref}. The model and the experimental analysis are distinct contributions; a <diffraction> pattern is evidence constraining a <molecular geometry>, rather than a direct picture of letter-by-letter <base pairing>.

= Deoxyribonucleic acid
{parent=Nucleic acid}

= DNA
{c}
{synonym}

<Deoxyribonucleic acid>, or <DNA>, is a <nucleotide> <polymer> that stores biological information in its <nucleotide sequence>. The crucial variable is order: two <DNA> <molecules> can contain the same numbers of each <nucleobase> yet carry different <nucleotide sequences>. This makes a physical <molecule> an inheritable record, rather than merely a mixture of ingredients. <Genes> are functional regions of that record; the whole <DNA> <nucleotide sequence> is not one long <gene>. See https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/Deoxyribonucleic-Acid-Fact-Sheet[NHGRI: DNA fact sheet].

Each <DNA> strand has a backbone of alternating <deoxyribose> <sugars> and <phosphate> groups joined through <covalent bonds>. Attached <nucleobases> supply the familiar four-letter alphabet: <adenine> A, <thymine> T, <guanine> G, and <cytosine> C. In ordinary double-stranded <DNA>, <base pairing> matches A with T and G with C. The backbone connects successive <nucleotides> along a strand; <base pairing> connects partners across strands. Keeping these two connections distinct explains how strands can separate while each retains its own <nucleotide sequence>.

The two backbones have directions, conventionally labelled $5'$ and $3'$ after numbered <carbon> <chemical atoms> in <deoxyribose>. They form <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>: following one from $5'$ to $3'$ takes you opposite to the other's $5'$-to-$3'$ direction. For the chosen <DNA> <nucleotide sequence> $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ATGCC}\text{-}3'$, write its partner directly underneath as $3'\text{-}\mathrm{TACGG}\text{-}5'$. Now read that partner from its own $5'$ end: its <reverse complement> is $5'\text{-}\mathrm{GGCAT}\text{-}3'$. Complementing letters and reversing their order are separate operations. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26821/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the structure and function of DNA].

The diagram's rails represent <sugar>–<phosphate> backbones and its coloured blocks represent <nucleobases>, not individual <chemical atoms>. Reading left to right gives five matched positions: A–T, T–A, G–C, C–G, C–G. There are two A–T-type <base pairs> and three G–C-type <base pairs>; the complete two-strand example contains ten <nucleotides>. Reversing the bottom row changes the reading convention, not the physical partners. Real double-stranded <DNA> winds into a <nucleic acid double helix>; the flattened ladder emphasizes <base pairing> and direction without pretending to show its three-dimensional geometry.

The motivating consequence is copying by a template. After strands separate, each existing <DNA> strand specifies which incoming <nucleotides> should accompany it through <base pairing>. <DNA replication> uses <enzymes> to build complementary strands, allowing the record to persist through successive <biological cells>. <Base pairing> supplies a selection rule, while <enzymes> perform the chemistry: a matching diagram alone is not a complete copying mechanism. The connection between structure and copying was already central to the model published by Watson and Crick on 25 April 1953. See https://www.nature.com/articles/171737a0[Watson and Crick, Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids, Nature 171, 737–738 (1953)].

Stored information needs interpretation. Some <DNA> regions specify <proteins> through <RNA>; others contribute to functional <RNA> or regulate <gene expression>, and some have no known function. <Non-coding DNA> means sequence that does not specify a <protein>'s sequence of <amino acids>, not automatically useless sequence. Nor does a <DNA> <nucleotide sequence> alone determine an <organism>'s fate: <phenotypes> also depend on biological context and environment. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Non-Coding-DNA[NHGRI: non-coding DNA]. Its double-stranded form is often modeled mechanically as a <worm-like chain>, with <elasticity> and effective <excluded volume> depending on solvent conditions.

= Ribonucleic acid
{parent=Nucleic acid}

= RNA
{c}
{synonym}

<Ribonucleic acid>, or <RNA>, is a <nucleotide> <polymer> whose <nucleotide sequence> can both carry information and organize a working molecular shape. Its backbone contains alternating <ribose> <sugars> and <phosphate> groups connected through <covalent bonds>. Its usual <nucleobases> are <adenine> A, <uracil> U, <guanine> G, and <cytosine> C. Thus <RNA> uses U where <DNA> uses T; its <ribose> also differs chemically from <DNA>'s <deoxyribose>. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Ribonucleic-Acid-RNA[NHGRI: RNA].

In <transcription (biology)>, an <enzyme> uses one <DNA> strand as a template for <RNA>. For example, the <DNA> template $3'\text{-}\mathrm{TACGG}\text{-}5'$ specifies $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUGCC}\text{-}3'$ in <RNA>. The <base pairing> rules now include A–U rather than A–T, while G–C remains. This <RNA> matches the other <DNA> strand's $5'$-to-$3'$ lettering after T is replaced by U. Identifying the template strand matters: replacing T by U in an arbitrarily chosen <DNA> strand is not a general method for deriving the <RNA> product. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26887/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: from DNA to RNA].

<RNA> is often a single strand, but single-stranded does not mean straight or entirely unpaired. Complementary regions within one <RNA> <molecule> can undergo <base pairing>, bringing distant positions together. Consider the illustrative twelve-<nucleotide> <nucleotide sequence> $5'\text{-}\mathrm{GCAUUUUUAUGC}\text{-}3'$. Pair positions 1 with 12, 2 with 11, 3 with 10, and 4 with 9. The resulting <stem-loop> has four <base pairs>: G–C, C–G, A–U, U–A. Positions 5–8 remain unpaired in the loop. Both arms belong to the same continuous backbone, and the paired arms have the directions of <antiparallel nucleic acid strands>.

The diagram proposes a possible pairing pattern, not a prediction that this short <RNA> must adopt that shape under every condition. Actual <RNA> folding depends on additional interactions, <temperature>, <ions>, and associated <proteins>; the full three-dimensional arrangement contains more information than the drawn <base pairs>. Different <RNA> roles make this flexibility useful. <Messenger RNA> carries a message that a <ribosome> reads during <translation (biology)> to build a <protein>. <Transfer RNA> helps match that message to <amino acid> building blocks; see https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Transfer-RNA-tRNA[NHGRI: transfer RNA]. <Ribosomal RNA> forms structural and catalytic parts of the <ribosome>. Other <RNA> participates in <gene regulatory networks>, changing how strongly <genes> are expressed. Many <RNA> <molecules> therefore function without being translated into <proteins>; for some <genes>, functional <RNA> is the product itself.

Most strikingly, some folded <RNA> acts as an <enzyme>: these catalytic <RNA> <molecules> are <ribozymes>. The <ribosome>'s bond-forming <active site> is built from <RNA>, although the complete <ribosome> also contains <proteins>. Structural evidence appears in https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10937990/[Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000)]. <RNA> therefore connects a readable record with physical recognition, regulation, and catalysis: the same kind of <polymer> can be a message, an adaptor, or part of molecular machinery.

= Radius of gyration
{parent=Polymer}
{wiki}
{title2=$R_g^2=\frac1{2N^2}\sum_{m,n}\langle|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2\rangle$}

For $N$ equally weighted positions, the squared <radius of gyration> is the average squared distance from the <center of mass>: $R_g^2=N^{-1}\sum_n\langle|\mathbf R_n-\mathbf R_{\rm cm}|^2\rangle$, where $\mathbf R_{\rm cm}=N^{-1}\sum_n\mathbf R_n$. Expanding pair differences gives $\sum_{m,n}|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2=2N\sum_n|\mathbf R_n-\mathbf R_{\rm cm}|^2$, and therefore
$$
R_g^2=\frac1{2N^2}\sum_{m,n}\langle|\mathbf R_m-\mathbf R_n|^2\rangle.
$$
For a <Gaussian chain> or <freely jointed chain> with these $N$ positions and mean squared link length $b^2$, each pair <variance> is $b^2|m-n|$. Summing separations yields $R_g^2=b^2(N^2-1)/(6N)\sim Nb^2/6$. Counting all endpoints of $N$ links instead gives $N+1$ positions and the corresponding finite-size formula.

= Molecular-weight scaling of an ideal-chain gyration radius
{parent=Radius of gyration}
{title2=$R_g\propto M^{1/2}$}

For a long <ideal chain> of $N$ independent statistical steps of <length> $b$, $R_g^2=Nb^2/6$. With fixed chain chemistry and $b$, the <molar mass> $M$ is proportional to $N$. Thus multiplying <molar mass> by $s$ multiplies the <radius of gyration> by $\sqrt{s}$; <solvent quality> can change both the statistical segment <length> and the size exponent.

= Radius of gyration of a uniform sphere
{parent=Radius of gyration}
{title2=$R_g^2=3R^2/5$}

For a uniform solid <sphere> of radius $R$, the squared <radius of gyration> about its centre is the mass-weighted mean of $r^2$:
$$
R_g^2=\frac{\int_0^Rr^2\,4\pi\rho r^2dr}{4\pi\rho R^3/3}=\frac35R^2.
$$
Thus its volume is $(4\pi/3)(5/3)^{3/2}R_g^3$, about $9.01R_g^3$. A nonuniform object or a different shape need not obey this conversion. This distinction matters when estimating molecular <number density> from scattering measurements of a <radius of gyration>.
