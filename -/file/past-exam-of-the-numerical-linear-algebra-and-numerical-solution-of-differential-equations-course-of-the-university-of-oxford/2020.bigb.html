= Solution
{parent=a}

Write the increment function of the <one-step method> as $\Phi(x,v,h)=f(x+\beta h,v+\beta h f(x,v))$. Its <consistency error> is
$$
T_n=\frac{y(x_n+h)-y(x_n)}h-\Phi(x_n,y(x_n),h).
$$
As $h\to0$, the first term tends to $y'(x_n)$ and the second tends to $f(x_n,y(x_n))=y'(x_n)$ by <continuity>. Both limits are uniform along the exact-solution curve, a <compact set>: its small stage perturbations lie in a fixed compact neighborhood, so <uniform continuity> applies there. Thus $\max_{n<N}|T_n|\to0$, proving **consistency for every $\beta\in[0,1]$**.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

By the <chain rule>, $y''=f_x+f_yf$ along the exact solution. Applying <Taylor's theorem> to the exact increment and the two arguments of $f$ gives
$$
\frac{y(x+h)-y(x)}h=f(x,y(x))+\frac h2y''(x)+O(h^2),\qquad
\Phi(x,y(x),h)=f(x,y(x))+\beta h(f_x+f_yf)(x,y(x))+O(h^2).
$$
The assumed differentiability makes the remainders uniform on $0\leq x\leq1-h$. Subtracting gives the <consistency error>
$$
\boxed{T_n=h\left(\frac12-\beta\right)y''(x_n)+O(h^2).}
$$
Here $O$ denotes <Big O notation>. The general <order of a numerical method> is therefore one for $\beta\ne1/2$ and at least two for $\beta=1/2$. For example, on $y'=y$ with unit initial value, $y_N=(1+h+\beta h^2)^{1/h}$. Expanding its <logarithm> gives $y_N=e[1+(\beta-1/2)h+O(h^2)]$, so the first-order term is actually nonzero when $\beta\ne1/2$.

To control accumulation, the <mean value theorem> and the bound on $f_y$ give
$$
|\Phi(x,u,h)-\Phi(x,v,h)|\leq L(1+\beta hL)|u-v|.
$$
Put $K_h=L(1+\beta hL)$, $q_h=1+hK_h$ and $e_n=y(x_n)-y_n$. The exact and numerical updates yield $|e_{n+1}|\leq q_h|e_n|+h|T_n|$, with $e_0=0$. Iterating this recurrence, as in the <Lipschitz one-step error bound>, gives
$$
|e_n|\leq\frac{q_h^n-1}{K_h}\max_{j<n}|T_j|
\leq\frac{e^{K_hx_n}-1}{K_h}\max_{j<n}|T_j|.
$$
Because $K_h\leq L(1+L)$ for $h\leq1$, the prefactor is bounded independently of the mesh. When $\beta=1/2$, the <consistency error> is $O(h^2)$, hence the maximum <global error> is $O(h^2)$. This proves at least second-order <numerical convergence> of the <explicit midpoint method>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $f(x,y)=y$ and $\beta=1/2$, the <explicit midpoint method> multiplies the current value by $R=1+h+h^2/2$. Starting from one, induction gives
$$
\boxed{y_n=\left(1+h+\frac{h^2}{2}\right)^n.}
$$
Let $A=e^h$ and $B=R$. The <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> for the <exponential function> gives
$$
\frac{h^3}{6}\leq A-B\leq\frac{h^3e^h}{6},\qquad 1\leq B\leq A.
$$
The <difference of powers> identity gives $A^n-B^n=(A-B)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A^{n-1-k}B^k$. Every summand lies between one and $A^{n-1}$. Since $nh=x_n$, the <global error> therefore satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{h^2x_n}{6}\leq y(x_n)-y_n\leq\frac{h^2x_ne^{x_n}}6.}
$$
The same inequalities hold at $n=0$, where the error vanishes. At the fixed endpoint $x_N=1$, the lower bound $h^2/6$ prevents any higher <order of a numerical method>, while the upper bound is $eh^2/6$. Thus **the convergence order is exactly two**.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A constant-coefficient <linear multistep method>, normalized so that $\alpha_k=1$, has the form
$$
\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_jY_{n+j}=h\sum_{j=0}^k\beta_jf(x_{n+j},Y_{n+j}),\qquad x_n=x_0+nh.
$$
Here $Y_n$ approximates the solution $y(x_n)$ of the <initial value problem>, and $Y_0,\ldots,Y_{k-1}$ are starting approximations. The coefficients are independent of $n$ and $h$. If $\beta_k=0$, the update is explicit; otherwise it is an <implicit time-stepping method>, whose new value must satisfy the displayed equation.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Fix the following normalization of the <local truncation error>, also called the <consistency error>: insert a smooth exact solution into the <linear multistep method> and divide the residual by $h$,
$$
T_n(h)=\frac1h\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_jy(x_n+jh)-\sum_{j=0}^k\beta_jy'(x_n+jh).
$$
Thus the unscaled defect is $hT_n(h)$. <Consistency of a numerical method> means that $T_n(h)\to0$ uniformly over mesh points in each fixed interval for every sufficiently smooth exact solution. Write the <characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method> as
$$
\rho(z)=\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_jz^j,\qquad \sigma(z)=\sum_{j=0}^k\beta_jz^j.
$$
By <Taylor's theorem>, <consistency of a numerical method> is equivalent to
$$
\sum_j\alpha_j=0,\qquad \sum_jj\alpha_j=\sum_j\beta_j,
$$
or $\rho(1)=0$ and $\rho'(1)=\sigma(1)$.

Second-order accuracy means $T_n(h)=O(h^2)$ for smooth solutions, equivalently an unscaled <local truncation error> $O(h^3)$. In addition to the two consistency conditions, its order conditions require
$$
\sum_jj^2\alpha_j=2\sum_jj\beta_j.
$$
An <order of a numerical method> of exactly two additionally requires that the next order condition fails. These statements concern the local defect; a second-order <global error> requires stability and suitably accurate starting values as well.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a constant-coefficient <linear multistep method>, <zero-stability> means uniform boundedness of the propagation of starting perturbations as $h\to0$. Equivalently, every solution of the homogeneous <linear recurrence>
$$
\sum_{j=0}^k\alpha_jv_{n+j}=0
$$
satisfies $|v_n|\leq K\max_{0\leq j<k}|v_j|$ for all $n\geq0$, with $K$ independent of $n$ and the starting values. The equivalent <root condition for a multistep method> is that every <root of a polynomial> $\zeta$ of the degree-$k$ <polynomial> $\rho$ satisfies $|\zeta|\leq1$, and every root on $|\zeta|=1$ is simple. Indeed, a root of multiplicity $m$ produces recurrence modes $n^q\zeta^n$, $0\leq q<m$: repeated roots strictly inside the disk are harmless, whereas roots outside it or repeated roots on its boundary produce unbounded modes.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The coefficients of this <linear multistep method> are $\alpha_0=b$, $\alpha_1=-a$, $\alpha_2=1$ and $\beta_0=\beta_1=0$, $\beta_2=c$. Substitution into the <order of a numerical method> conditions gives
$$
1-a+b=0,\qquad 2-a-c=0,\qquad 2-\frac a2-2c=0.
$$
The second equation gives $c=2-a$; substituting this into the third gives $-2+3a/2=0$. Consequently there is exactly one second-order choice,
$$
\boxed{a=\frac43,\qquad b=\frac13,\qquad c=\frac23.}
$$
The resulting scheme is the <BDF2 method>,
$$
3Y_{n+2}-4Y_{n+1}+Y_n=2h f(x_{n+2},Y_{n+2}).
$$
For a $C^4$ exact solution, <Taylor's theorem> in the original normalization gives the exact leading order defect
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_n(h)
&=\frac{y(x_n+2h)-\frac43y(x_n+h)+\frac13y(x_n)}h-\frac23y'(x_n+2h)\\
&=-\frac29h^2y'''(x_n)+O(h^3).
\end{aligned}
$$
In particular the coefficient $-2/9$ is nonzero: the method has formal order exactly two, rather than three. Bounded third derivatives already suffice for the uniform estimate $T_n(h)=O(h^2)$; the stronger smoothness above only makes the displayed remainder immediate. Multiplying the recurrence by $3/2$ instead gives the common derivative normalization with leading defect $-h^2y'''(x_n)/3$.

The first of the <characteristic polynomials of a linear multistep method> is
$$
\rho(z)=z^2-\frac43z+\frac13=(z-1)\left(z-\frac13\right).
$$
Both roots are simple and lie in the closed unit disk, so the <root condition for a multistep method> proves <zero-stability>.

The <Dahlquist equivalence theorem> states that, for fixed-coefficient <linear multistep methods> on the usual class of <initial value problems> with a right-hand side continuous in $x$ and uniformly <Lipschitz continuous> in $y$, <convergence of a numerical method> for every such problem and every convergent set of starting values is equivalent to <consistency of a numerical method> and <zero-stability>. The statement is on fixed finite intervals where the exact solution exists, with implicit updates well defined. Its order estimate says that a <zero-stable> method with $T_n(h)=O(h^p)$ and starting errors $O(h^p)$ has <global error> $O(h^p)$ under the corresponding solution smoothness and <Lipschitz continuity> assumptions; merely convergent starting values do not ensure the order-$p$ rate.

For this application, sufficient assumptions are a unique solution $y\in C^3([x_0,x_0+T])$, a uniform <Lipschitz continuity> bound $L$ in $y$ on a neighborhood of its graph, and the nearby solution branch of each implicit update. For sufficiently small $h$, the map
$$
z\longmapsto\frac43Y_{n+1}-\frac13Y_n+\frac23h f(x_{n+2},z)
$$
is a <contraction mapping> with factor at most $2hL/3<1$ on a suitable small closed neighborhood. The <Banach fixed-point theorem> supplies local solvability of the nearby update. With starting errors
$$
|Y_0-y(x_0)|+|Y_1-y(x_0+h)|=O(h^2),
$$
the order estimate gives
$$
\max_{0\leq nh\leq T}|Y_n-y(x_n)|\leq C_T\left(|Y_0-y(x_0)|+|Y_1-y(x_0+h)|+\max_n|T_n(h)|\right)=O(h^2).
$$
Thus the <BDF2 method> is second-order convergent under these standard hypotheses. Exact $Y_0=y(x_0)$ together with one <Euler method> step for $Y_1$ already gives the required starting error when $y''$ is bounded, since only one starting step is taken.

The question's assumption of <continuity> of $f$ alone does not establish uniqueness, the needed solution smoothness, or this uniform convergence estimate. The added <Lipschitz continuity> condition is a sufficient hypothesis for the theorem used here; its failure does not imply that every particular <initial value problem> is nonunique or that its numerical approximations cannot converge.
