= Thermoelectric effect
{wiki}

A <thermoelectric effect> couples <heat transfer> and the transport of <electric charge>: a <temperature gradient> can generate an <electromotive force>, while an <electric current> can carry <heat> and cause heating or cooling at a junction of different materials. This coupling makes electrical measurement of <temperature> possible through a <thermocouple>, and permits conversion of a sustained flow of <heat> into electrical <work>. A finite open-circuit voltage does not itself deliver <power>: electrical <power> requires an <electric current> through a load. Thermoelectric conversion therefore involves both <electromagnetism> and <thermodynamics>. https://thermoelectrics.matsci.northwestern.edu/thermoelectrics/history.html[Northwestern thermoelectrics research group: thermoelectric effects and applications]{ref}.

= Thermoelectric figure of merit
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}
{title2=$ZT$}

The dimensionless material figure of merit is $ZT=S^2\sigma T/(\kappa_e+\kappa_l)$. Device efficiency depends on its temperature dependence and parasitic losses, not only on one peak value.

= Thermoelectric couple figure of merit
{parent=Thermoelectric figure of merit}
{title2=$Z_{\rm pair}$}

For a constant-property pair with Seebeck difference $\alpha_{np}$, electrical resistance $R$ and thermal conductance $K$, $Z_{\rm pair}=\alpha_{np}^2/(RK)$. With identical legs of individual resistivity $\rho$ and thermal conductivity $\kappa$, this is $\alpha_{np}^2/(4\rho\kappa)$, not $\alpha_{np}^2/(\rho\kappa)$.

= Band convergence
{parent=Thermoelectric figure of merit}

Bringing several electronic valleys close in energy to increase useful valley degeneracy. Its transport benefit must be assessed together with mobility and scattering.

= Thermoelectric power factor
{parent=Thermoelectric figure of merit}
{title2=$S^2\sigma$}

The combination $S^2\sigma$ of Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity. Increasing it alone need not increase ZT if thermal conductivity rises as well.

= Thomson effect
{c}
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}
{title2=$\tau=T\,dS/dT$}

The <Thomson effect> is distributed reversible heating or cooling when an <electric current> flows through a homogeneous material with a <temperature gradient>. It is distinct from junction <Peltier effect> heating and even-in-current <Joule heating>.

= Thermoelectric module
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}

A <thermoelectric module> connects thermoelectric material pairs between thermal faces. Electrical series connections and thermal parallel connections combine their transport responses for <thermoelectric cooling> or electrical generation.

= Thermoelectric generator

A generator using the <Seebeck effect> to convert a <temperature> difference into electrical output.

= Seebeck effect
{c}
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}

The <Seebeck effect> is the generation of a thermoelectric <electromotive force> by a <temperature> difference. In a <thermocouple>, two different homogeneous <electrical conductors> A and B connect a measuring junction at $T_h$ to two reference connections at a common $T_c$. With negligible <electric current>, the measured terminal voltage is determined by the difference of their <Seebeck coefficients>. A hot junction alone does not specify the reading: the reference <temperature>, the second material, and the terminal order matter.

\Image[seebeck-thermocouple-experiment.png]
{title=Two-material <thermocouple>: junction <temperatures> and the voltage sign}
{description=An idealized open-circuit <Seebeck effect> experiment. The A and B wires meet at the measuring junction; each reference connection meets an identical meter lead at the same $T_c$. The positive meter input connects to A. The shown $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and $+2\,\mathrm{mV}$ belong to the explicitly toy constant-<Seebeck-coefficient> example below, not measured data.}

For a motivating experiment, join one end of A to one end of B, insulating the wires elsewhere. Connect their free ends to identical meter leads at reference <temperature> $T_c$, for example in an ice-water <heat bath>. A meter with input <electrical resistance> much larger than the <thermocouple>'s <electrical resistance> approximates an open circuit, meaning $I\approx0$. Keep the measuring junction in a separately controlled <heat bath> at $T_h$ and wait for stable junction <temperatures>. Both reference connections must share $T_c$; identical leads with identical endpoint <temperatures> have cancelling thermoelectric contributions, allowing the meter to be elsewhere. https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/director/planning/report97-1.pdf[Marx, Link and Scott, Economic Assessment of the NIST Thermocouple Calibration Program (1997), §2.1]{ref}.

Define the reading as $V_{AB}=V_A-V_B$, with the positive input on the A reference terminal and the negative input on B. Use the <Seebeck coefficient> convention in which the voltage change along a homogeneous wire at zero <electric current> is $dV=-S(T)\,dT$. Following the path from A at $T_c$ to the common junction at $T_h$, then back along B to $T_c$, gives
$$
V_{AB}(T_h,T_c)=\int_{T_c}^{T_h}\bigl[S_A(T)-S_B(T)\bigr]\,dT.
$$
This formula assumes homogeneous materials whose <Seebeck coefficients> depend only on the local <temperature>, and no additional uncompensated lead contributions. Here voltage is the electrical transport voltage associated with <electrochemical potential>, rather than a direct measurement of an isolated equilibrium contact <electric potential>.

Start with $T_h=T_c$, then increase $T_h$ while holding $T_c$ fixed and record $V_{AB}$ at each stable setting. In the ideal model the equal-<temperature> reading is zero. If $S_A-S_B>0$ throughout the interval, A reads positive when $T_h>T_c$. Exchanging meter inputs reverses the reading; exchanging the two junction <temperatures> reverses the oriented <integral>. Repeating the run with identical homogeneous materials gives zero because $S_A=S_B$. These controls test the stated model; a nonzero experimental baseline should be measured rather than silently removed by assuming it is zero.

For an explicitly toy linear example, stipulate constant <Seebeck coefficients> $S_A=25\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ and $S_B=-15\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$. Choose reference <temperature> $T_c=20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and measuring-junction <temperature> $T_h=70\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. A <temperature> difference has the same numerical size in <kelvins> and on the <degree Celsius> scale, so
$$
S_A-S_B=40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K},\qquad \Delta T=50\,\mathrm K,
\qquad V_{AB}=(40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K})(50\,\mathrm K)=+2.00\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
Swapping the inputs gives $-2.00\,\mathrm{mV}$. Bringing both junction regions to the same <temperature> gives zero. With <temperature>-dependent <Seebeck coefficients>, shifting both junction <temperatures> equally while keeping their difference fixed can change the reading. No actual material pair is claimed to have the stipulated coefficients over this interval.

\Image[seebeck-toy-voltage-slope.png]
{title=Toy <Seebeck effect> calibration: voltage slope and reversed inputs}
{description=Calculated lines, not measured data. The solid line is $V_{AB}=(40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K})\Delta T$; the dashed line shows the same device with reversed meter inputs. The <slope> is the difference of the two <Seebeck coefficients>, not either coefficient separately. The reference <temperature> is fixed at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$; negative $\Delta T$ means the measuring junction is colder than the reference connections.}

Real <thermocouples> need a calibrated voltage-versus-<temperature> relation: the constant-<Seebeck-coefficient> approximation is useful only where the coefficient difference changes little. Wire inhomogeneity can make the result depend on where the <temperature gradient> lies, and reference connections at unequal or unknown <temperatures> introduce errors. An electronic reference correction uses a measured reference <temperature> in place of an ice bath. https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/director/planning/report97-1.pdf[Marx, Link and Scott (1997), §2.1: reference correction and wire homogeneity]{ref}. Burns and colleagues provide the standardized reference functions for common letter-designated <thermocouple> types; the toy line above is not a replacement for them. https://www.nist.gov/publications/temperature-electromotive-force-reference-functions-and-tables-letter-designated[Burns et al., Temperature-electromotive force reference functions and tables (1993), NIST Monograph 175]{ref}.

The historical experiment used a different detector. In his 1821–1823 investigations, Seebeck found that a closed <electrical circuit> of two dissimilar materials with junctions at different <temperatures> deflected a compass needle. The modern explanation is that thermoelectric <electromotive force> drives an <electric current>, whose <magnetic field> deflects the needle. The open-circuit experiment above detects voltage while drawing negligible <electric current>. The striking connection is that a thermal imbalance can become an electrical signal and then a magnetic response. https://thermoelectrics.matsci.northwestern.edu/thermoelectrics/history.html[Northwestern thermoelectrics research group: Seebeck's compass experiment]{ref}.

= Thermopower
{synonym}

The <Seebeck coefficient> $S(T)$ quantifies the thermoelectric voltage response per small <temperature> difference at zero <electric current>. Use $S=-dV/dT$ along a homogeneous material, with voltage understood through <electrochemical potential>. Its units are <volts> per <kelvin>, commonly $\mu\mathrm{V/K}$. The word <thermopower> is also used, despite this being a voltage response rather than <power>; some authors use that word for $|S|$, so check their convention. https://thermoelectrics.matsci.northwestern.edu/thermoelectrics/[Northwestern thermoelectrics research group: Seebeck coefficient and thermopower convention]{ref}.

For the A-minus-B terminal order in the <Seebeck effect> experiment,
$$
\frac{\partial V_{AB}}{\partial T_h}=S_A(T_h)-S_B(T_h),\qquad
\frac{\partial V_{AB}}{\partial T_c}=-\bigl[S_A(T_c)-S_B(T_c)\bigr].
$$
Thus a small <temperature> difference around $T_0$ gives $V_{AB}\approx[S_A(T_0)-S_B(T_0)]\Delta T$. The finite-interval ratio $V_{AB}/\Delta T$ is an average of the coefficient difference, not necessarily its value at either endpoint. Ordinary two-material measurements determine a relative <Seebeck coefficient>: to recover $S_A$ from the measured difference, add a separately known $S_B$. Neither the choice of a zero for voltage nor the use of a familiar reference material makes $S_B$ identically zero.

A concrete research example is Martin and colleagues' 2021 certification experiment. They measured small <temperature> differences and voltages on six <silicon>–germanium specimens at 13 base <temperatures> between $295$ and $900\,\mathrm K$. At each setting they acquired 40 voltage-and-<temperature>-difference pairs and used <linear regression> with a free intercept, so a constant voltage offset did not become part of the <slope>. Platinum voltage leads required a reference-material correction; their terminal order gave $S_{\rm measured}=S_{\rm Pt}-S_{\rm specimen}$, hence $S_{\rm specimen}=S_{\rm Pt}-S_{\rm measured}$. This is an actual measurement protocol, unlike the toy plot above. https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=932592[Martin et al., Development of a high-temperature (295–900 K) Seebeck coefficient Standard Reference Material (2021), certification measurements and Eq. (1)]{ref}.

Microscopically, a <temperature gradient> changes <charge carrier> transport until an opposing electrical response balances it at zero net <electric current>. In the usual simple <semiconductor> picture, <electrons> and <electron holes> give opposite <Seebeck-coefficient> signs, and their contributions are weighted by their <electrical conductivities>. The <Seebeck coefficient> is a transport property: <density of states> alone does not determine it, since carrier motion and scattering also matter. Its usefulness extends beyond <temperature> sensing to diagnosing which carriers transport <electric charge>. https://thermoelectrics.matsci.northwestern.edu/thermoelectrics/[Northwestern thermoelectrics research group: Seebeck coefficient, scattering contributions, and bipolar transport]{ref}.

= Thermocouple
{parent=Seebeck effect}
{wiki}

A <thermocouple> uses two different <electrical conductors> and the <Seebeck effect> to infer a measuring-junction <temperature> from a voltage and a known reference <temperature>. The reference material and the reference <temperature> are distinct choices: B specifies the comparison <Seebeck coefficient>, while $T_c$ specifies the lower limit of the <integral>. The two-material experiment in <Seebeck effect> gives the geometry, sign convention, and calibration limitations.

The useful measurement is a pair: the raw terminal <electric potential difference> and the <thermocouple reference junction> <temperature>. For the A-positive terminal order and homogeneous-wire assumptions of <Seebeck effect>, write the open-circuit <electromotive force> as
$$
E(T_h,T_c)=\int_{T_c}^{T_h}[S_A(T)-S_B(T)]\,dT.
$$
Here $T_h$ is the <thermocouple measuring junction> <temperature> and $T_c$ is the common <temperature> of the reference connections. The subscript $h$ names the measuring end; it need not be hotter. A <voltmeter> senses the complete connected thermoelectric path through the wires and its leads, in the <electrochemical potential> convention already described under <Seebeck effect>. It does not measure an absolute <temperature>, the individual <Seebeck coefficient> of A, or an isolated contact <electric potential>.

= Thermopile
{parent=Thermocouple}

A <thermopile> connects multiple <thermocouples> in series to turn a <temperature> difference into a larger electrical signal. Absorbed <thermal radiation> can heat its sensing junctions while reference junctions remain cooler; the signal depends on the thermal and optical design.

A <thermopile> is an array of <thermocouples> connected as a <series circuit> with their <electromotive forces> adding in the chosen terminal order. It measures a collective <temperature> difference through the <Seebeck effect>. An open-circuit <electric potential difference> requires no externally supplied <electric current> through the array: a high-input-<electrical-resistance> <voltmeter> reads the <signal>. The <heat> that maintains the junction <temperature> difference still comes from somewhere, such as absorbed <electromagnetic radiation>.

In a membrane detector, an absorbing film heats the measuring junctions while reference junctions lie on the surrounding support. Actual junction <temperatures> can differ across the membrane.<Thermopile detectors by Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.>[p. 2, §2 Structure, Figs. 2-1–2-2: absorbing film, membrane, junction locations and nonuniform temperature]{ref} This converts difficult-to-observe <heat transfer> into an accessible electrical <signal>; the series connection combines many small contributions without requiring every <thermocouple> to have a separate meter.

= Thermopile series voltage
{parent=Thermopile}
{title2=$V_{\rm oc}=\sum_{i=1}^N E_i$}

Use the A-positive terminal order of <Seebeck effect> for each <thermocouple>. Its <thermocouple measuring junction> has <temperature> $T_{h,i}$ and its reference connections have <temperature> $T_{c,i}$. Define the difference of <Seebeck coefficients> as $\alpha_i(T)=S_{A,i}(T)-S_{B,i}(T)$. Connect the B reference end of one <thermocouple> to the A reference end of the next at a common local <temperature>, so the terminal <electric potential differences> add:
$$
E_i=\int_{T_{c,i}}^{T_{h,i}}\alpha_i(T)\,dT,
\qquad V_{\rm oc}=\sum_{i=1}^{N} E_i.
$$
The first A reference terminal is the positive meter input; the final B reference terminal is the negative input. Following this connected path and adding the individual <Seebeck effect> <integrals> proves the result. Matched meter leads with common endpoint <temperatures> cancel as in an <isothermal terminal block>. The manufacturer's sum formula also allows unequal individual contributions.<Thermopile detectors by Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.>[p. 2, Eq. (2-1): total output is the sum of individual thermocouple outputs]{ref}

\Image[thermopile-series-junction-geometry.png]
{title=<Thermopile> geometry: series junctions, absorber and reference support}
{description=Original idealized wiring, showing three of $N$ <thermocouples>. Each A leg rises from reference <temperature> $T_c$ to absorber <temperature> $T_h$, and each B leg returns. B-to-A connections at $T_c$ join adjacent pairs; the thermally shared absorber and support do not short the electrically distinct junctions. A-positive and B-negative meter connections match the <Seebeck effect> convention. Arrows indicate absorbed <electromagnetic radiation> and escaping <heat>, not <electric current>.}

If all pairs share $T_h,T_c$ and the same nearly constant difference $\alpha$ of <Seebeck coefficients> throughout that <temperature> <interval>, then
$$
V_{\rm oc}\approx N\alpha\Delta T,\qquad \Delta T=T_h-T_c.
$$
This $\alpha$ is the pair's relative <Seebeck coefficient>, not either material's individual coefficient or a universal device constant. As an original constructed example, choose $N=20$, $\alpha=40\,\mu\mathrm{V\,K^{-1}}$ and $\Delta T=5\,\mathrm K$. Each <thermocouple> contributes $0.20\,\mathrm{mV}$ and
$$
V_{\rm oc}=20(40\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{V\,K^{-1}})(5\,\mathrm K)=4.0\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
Reversing the meter connections changes the sign. Exchanging $T_h$ and $T_c$ reverses the <integrals> and hence the <electromotive force>; this reversible sign response does not make a detector with <heat conduction> a reversible thermodynamic process. Reversing the wiring of one pair instead gives $(N-2)\alpha\Delta T$, because one contribution is subtracted rather than added. For this example the result is $3.6\,\mathrm{mV}$, and at equal junction <temperatures> every contribution vanishes.

The open-circuit condition matters. If the array's total <electrical resistance> is $R_{\rm pile}$ and the meter input has <electrical resistance> $R_m$, the elementary <voltage divider> model gives $V_m=V_{\rm oc}R_m/(R_m+R_{\rm pile})$. Thus $R_m\gg R_{\rm pile}$ is needed to suppress <voltmeter loading>. Appreciable <electric current> also permits <Joule heating> and the thermoelectric <heat transfer> to disturb the assumed junction <temperatures>.

= Thermopile absorber
{parent=Thermopile}

A <thermopile absorber> converts incident <electromagnetic radiation> into <heat> near the <thermocouple measuring junctions>. Electrically separate junctions can share its <temperature> through thermal contact. The reference support provides a path for <heat transfer> and a comparison <temperature>; “cold” denotes this reference role, even if it is warmer than the absorber.

Distinguish incident <power> from absorbed <power>. A window, the illuminated <area>, and the absorption of the coating determine their relation. A detector's measured voltage sensitivity can depend on where a small illuminated spot lies.<Thermopile detectors by Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.>[p. 3, §3-1 Sensitivity, Eq. (3-1) and following paragraph: uniform illumination versus a localized spot]{ref} The model in <thermopile thermal response> takes absorbed <power> as its input, so an optical calibration is a separate step.

= Thermopile thermal response
{parent=Thermopile}
{title2=$\tau=C_{\rm th}/G_{\rm th}$}

A useful original model treats the <thermopile absorber> as one approximately uniform <temperature> $T_h(t)$ with <heat capacity> $C_{\rm th}$, connected to a fixed reference <temperature> $T_c$ by <thermal conductance> $G_{\rm th}$. Both parameters are positive constants here. Let $P_{\rm abs}(t)$ be an added absorbed <power> above the initial background. Linearize the outgoing <heat transfer> about that background. With $\Delta T=T_h-T_c$ measured from an initial state having $\Delta T=0$, <conservation of energy> gives the <first-order linear differential equation>
$$
C_{\rm th}\frac{d\Delta T}{dt}=P_{\rm abs}(t)-G_{\rm th}\Delta T.
$$
The left side is the rate of stored <energy>; the two terms on the right are added <power> and outgoing <heat transfer>. The effective <thermal conductance> can include several paths, rather than just <heat conduction> along the wires. The model assumes a stable reference, negligible electrical loading, a common absorber <temperature>, and changes small enough for constant parameters.

For a step from zero to constant $P_0$ at $t=0$, subtract the steady solution $\Delta T_\infty=P_0/G_{\rm th}$. The difference obeys <exponential decay>, so the initial condition fixes
$$
\tau=\frac{C_{\rm th}}{G_{\rm th}},\qquad
\Delta T(t)=\frac{P_0}{G_{\rm th}}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad
V_{\rm oc}(t)\approx N\alpha\frac{P_0}{G_{\rm th}}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right).
$$
The <time constant> has units $(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}})/(\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}})=\mathrm s$. With the same constructed $N=20$ and $\alpha=40\,\mu\mathrm{V\,K^{-1}}$, choose $C_{\rm th}=0.02\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, $G_{\rm th}=0.01\,\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}$ and $P_0=0.03\,\mathrm W$. Then
$$
\tau=2\,\mathrm s,\qquad \Delta T_\infty=3\,\mathrm K,\qquad
V_\infty=2.4\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
At one <time constant>, the response is $1-e^{-1}\approx0.632$ of its final value: $V_{\rm oc}(2\,\mathrm s)\approx1.52\,\mathrm{mV}$. Reaching $95\%$ requires $t=-\tau\ln(0.05)\approx5.99\,\mathrm s$. A <voltmeter> read too soon therefore underestimates the steady <signal> even when its electrical calibration is perfect.

\Image[thermopile-absorber-step-response.png]
{title=Calculated <thermopile> step response: <heat capacity>, <thermal conductance> and settling}
{description=Original model curves for added absorbed <power> $P_0=0.03\,\mathrm W$, $N=20$ and relative <Seebeck coefficient> $\alpha=40\,\mu\mathrm{V\,K^{-1}}$. The solid curve uses $C_{\rm th}=0.02\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $G_{\rm th}=0.01\,\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}$, giving <time constant> $2\,\mathrm s$ and final voltage $2.4\,\mathrm{mV}$. The dashed comparison halves only $G_{\rm th}$: final voltage doubles, but so does the <time constant>. Neither curve is an experimental observation.}

The steady ratio $V_\infty/P_0=N\alpha/G_{\rm th}$ is $0.080\,\mathrm{V\,W^{-1}}$ in this constructed model. Halving <thermal conductance> doubles this ratio but also doubles the <time constant> if <heat capacity> stays fixed. Reducing <heat capacity> alone speeds the response without changing its steady value. Increasing $N$ gives a larger <signal> only at fixed individual <temperature> differences: adding legs can also change <thermal conductance>, <heat capacity> and <electrical resistance>. The algebra identifies coupled design choices, rather than predicting a particular manufactured detector.

A finite <electrical resistance> produces <Thermal electrical noise>. For nearly uniform absolute <temperature> $T$ and effective measurement <signal bandwidth> $B$, its <root mean square> open-circuit voltage is
$$
v_{n,\rm rms}=\sqrt{4k_{\rm B}TR_{\rm pile}B}.
$$
Here $k_{\rm B}$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. Thermal electrical <noise> in the sensing element is an important contribution, and the <signal bandwidth> enters its voltage variance.<Thermopile detectors by Hamamatsu Photonics K.K.>[p. 3, §3-2 Noise, Eq. (3-2): element resistance, absolute temperature and measurement bandwidth]{ref} If identical pairs have fixed <electrical resistance> $r$ and fixed $\Delta T$, then $R_{\rm pile}=Nr$: the <signal> grows as $N$ while this <noise> grows as $\sqrt N$. The resulting <signal-to-noise ratio> grows only as $\sqrt N$ under those assumptions. Reference <temperature> drift, environmental <electromagnetic radiation>, and meter <noise> remain separate limits; reducing <signal bandwidth> trades fast information for less integrated electrical <noise>.

= Thermopile radiative measurement
{parent=Thermopile}

PASCO's documented lamp experiment connects a TD-8553 <thermopile> sensor to a millivoltmeter and places it level with a lamp filament along a meter stick. Lamp and detector axes are aligned. With the lamp off, readings at several positions establish an average ambient <thermal radiation> background. With the lamp on at a fixed supply setting, detector readings at different distances are compared after background subtraction. Between readings, a reflective shield prevents the detector warming; its shutter also protects the reference junction when idle. Moving the shutter can move the sensor, so fixed geometry matters. The manual's lamp limits must be followed.<Thermal Radiation System: Instruction Manual and Experiment Guide, TD-8553, TD-8554A and TD-8555 by PASCO scientific>[p. 1, Radiation Sensor; pp. 9–11, Experiment 2, Fig. 2.1, procedure and calculations: alignment, lamp-off ambient readings, heat shield and distance comparison]{ref}

This protocol tests whether the background-corrected <electric potential difference> approximately follows the <inverse-square law> over a suitable distance range. It is a relative <electromagnetic radiation> measurement; those readings alone do not calibrate absorbed <power> in <watts>. The manual treats the filament as an approximate <point source>.<Thermal Radiation System: Instruction Manual and Experiment Guide, TD-8553, TD-8554A and TD-8555 by PASCO scientific>[p. 3, Stefan-Boltzmann Lamp; p. 11, Experiment 2 calculations: point-source approximation and comparison against inverse squared distance]{ref}

For an absolute calibration, independently known absorbed <power> and a monitored reference <temperature> would be needed. A useful additional control is to repeat a fixed detector position before and after a distance sweep: disagreement reveals possible drift. These additions test the measurement chain rather than assuming that background subtraction fixes every error. A finite source, changing illuminated <area>, reflections, changing reference <temperature>, and insufficient settling can alter the measured dependence. In particular, the <time constant> in <thermopile thermal response> explains why a quick reading and a settled reading may differ. The detector infers its own absorber <temperature> difference; inferring the source's <temperature> additionally requires its <emissivity>, optical geometry and an appropriate <thermal radiation> model.

= Thermocouple measuring junction
{parent=Thermocouple}
{title2=$T_h$}

The <thermocouple measuring junction> joins A and B at the location whose <temperature> is to be inferred. Its own <temperature> is what the electrical measurement estimates. To infer a surrounding object's <temperature>, the junction must approach <thermal equilibrium> with that object: insufficient immersion, <heat conduction> along the wires, or a changing environment can keep the junction at another <temperature>. A stable electrical display alone does not establish that this thermal condition holds. The two-<heat-bath> procedure and terminal-reversal controls under <Seebeck effect> provide the starting experiment; the practical issue here is the reference and conversion chain.

= Thermocouple reference junction
{parent=Thermocouple}
{title2=$T_c$}

The <thermocouple reference junction> is the connection region where the two thermoelements meet the measurement leads. In ordinary <copper>-lead wiring there are two material transitions, A–Cu and B–Cu, rather than a second welded A–B junction. They must have a common known <temperature> $T_c$ for the simple expression $E(T_h,T_c)$ to apply. An ice-water <heat bath> can establish a nominal $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ reference; <cold-junction compensation> instead measures $T_c$ locally. The word “cold” describes the reference role and imposes no requirement that $T_c<T_h$.

= Isothermal terminal block
{parent=Thermocouple reference junction}

An <isothermal terminal block> keeps the two reference transitions and the reference <thermometer> at approximately the same <temperature>, while maintaining electrical insulation between the terminals. Thermal contact is required; an electrical short between A and B would bypass the signal. Merely using <copper> does not eliminate the <Seebeck effect> in the meter leads. If both homogeneous <copper> leads start at $T_c$ and end at a common meter-input <temperature> $T_m$, their contributions have equal magnitude and opposite sign around the measurement path:
$$
\int_{T_c}^{T_m}S_{\rm Cu}(T)\,dT-\int_{T_c}^{T_m}S_{\rm Cu}(T)\,dT=0.
$$
This cancellation uses matched endpoint <temperatures> and homogeneous lead material, rather than $S_{\rm Cu}=0$. A <temperature gradient> along each lead is allowed under these assumptions. If the two transitions are at different <temperatures>, one measured $T_c$ cannot generally correct both; unmatched connectors or wire inhomogeneity can introduce additional <electromotive forces>. The FP-TC-120 documentation explicitly locates the reference sensing in the terminal base or connector block and specifies isothermal versions of these assemblies.<FP-TC-120 and cFP-TC-120 Operating Instructions by National Instruments>[p. 7, Cold-Junction Compensation: terminal sensors and isothermal terminal assemblies]{ref}

= Cold-junction compensation
{parent=Thermocouple reference junction}

= CJC
{synonym}
{c}

<Cold-junction compensation> converts a reading with reference <temperature> $T_c$ into the equivalent reading for a chosen standard reference. Define the <thermocouple reference function> $F(T)=E(T,0)$, where $0$ means $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, not absolute zero. Additivity of the <definite integral> gives
$$
E(T_h,T_c)=F(T_h)-F(T_c),\qquad
F(T_h)=V_{\rm raw}+F(T_c),\qquad
T_h=F^{-1}\!\bigl(V_{\rm raw}+F(T_c)\bigr).
$$
The final <inverse function> requires a suitable one-to-one calibration interval. A terminal <thermometer> supplies $T_c$ independently; the raw <thermocouple> <electric potential difference> supplies only the difference of the two reference-function values. In general, first converting $V_{\rm raw}$ to a <temperature> and then adding $T_c$ is wrong, because the <thermocouple reference function> need not be linear. The SCC-TC manual explicitly adds the reference-equivalent voltage before its final voltage-to-<temperature> conversion.<SCC-TC Series Thermocouple Input Modules User Guide by National Instruments>[pp. 5–6, Scaling Voltage Measurements, steps 1–5]{ref}

For a stipulated model only, take $S_A-S_B=40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ throughout the interval. No actual material pair is assigned this constant. Because <degree Celsius> differences have the same numerical size as <kelvin> differences,
$$
T_h=80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C,\quad T_c=20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
V_{\rm raw}=(40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K})(60\,\mathrm K)=2.4\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
In this model $F(20)=0.8\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $F(80)=3.2\,\mathrm{mV}$. Adding the $0.8\,\mathrm{mV}$ correction produces $3.2\,\mathrm{mV}$, whose inverse calibration gives $80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Applying the zero-reference calibration directly to $2.4\,\mathrm{mV}$ would instead display $60\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

Now physically change only the reference <temperature> to $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, leaving the measuring junction at $80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. The raw reading becomes $2.0\,\mathrm{mV}$; the correction becomes $F(30)=1.2\,\mathrm{mV}$, so the corrected total stays $3.2\,\mathrm{mV}$. Physically changing the reference changes the measured <electric potential difference>. Adding a software correction changes its interpretation and leaves the raw reading intact. If the actual reference is $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ but the software still assumes $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, this model displays $70\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.

An actual implementation in <FP-TC-120 and cFP-TC-120 Operating Instructions by National Instruments> (p. 7, Cold-Junction Compensation) uses terminal-mounted <temperature> sensors, reports their reading as “CJ Temperature”, and enables compensation in <temperature> mode but not voltage mode. This makes the distinction between a raw voltage readout and a compensated <temperature> readout observable. The specified reference-sensor range also produces an out-of-range indication: a calculated number outside a supported range is not a validated measurement.<FP-TC-120 and cFP-TC-120 Operating Instructions by National Instruments>[p. 7, Cold-Junction Compensation: mode selection, CJ Temperature and out-of-range reporting]{ref}

= Thermocouple voltage calibration
{parent=Thermocouple}

<Thermocouple voltage calibration> relates an actual assembled sensor's <electromotive force> to independently established junction <temperatures>. It must be distinguished from selecting a nominal letter-type conversion curve. In <Thermocouples Calibrations Services by National Institute of Standards and Technology> (opening method description and bath-calibration table), NIST describes comparison with a maintained reference <thermocouple>, comparison with a standard platinum <resistance thermometer> in controlled <heat baths>, and fixed-point calibration at the freezing points of zinc, aluminum, silver and gold on the <International Temperature Scale of 1990>. These are independent thermal references, unlike a test that checks only a voltage-input circuit.<Thermocouples Calibrations Services by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[method description; table for base-metal thermocouples compared with an SPRT in a temperature-controlled bath]{ref}

For a concrete electrical check, the SCC-TC guide describes shorting the input after 30 minutes of powered operation and adjusting its offset to a zero-voltage readout. Its separate calibrator procedure requires equal connection <temperatures> at the calibrator and module, measures the supplied voltage, converts it to <temperature>, and compares with the calibrator setting. Such an injected-signal test checks the readout chain; it does not expose a real measuring junction to an independently known bath <temperature>.<SCC-TC Series Thermocouple Input Modules User Guide by National Instruments>[p. 9, Calibrating System Offsets; Calibrating the SCC-TC0X Using a Thermocouple Calibrator]{ref}

Useful additional checks follow from the model: log the raw voltage, the reference <temperature>, and the final inferred <temperature> separately; hold $T_h$ stable while changing $T_c$ and test whether <cold-junction compensation> preserves the inferred $T_h$; compare against an independent <thermometer> at several stable <heat-bath> settings. Check immersion and repeatability instead of assuming a steady display establishes <thermal equilibrium>. These are proposed diagnostic checks, not new experimental results. The equal-<temperature> and polarity controls are already described under <Seebeck effect>.

Limits arise separately from the <thermocouple reference function>, sensor-wire changes, thermal contact, terminal <temperature> differences, and the voltage-input circuit. In particular, the SCC-TC guide warns of connector-to-module <temperature gradients> and voltage drops caused by the fault-detection <electric current> flowing through lead <electrical resistance>.<SCC-TC Series Thermocouple Input Modules User Guide by National Instruments>[p. 6, Uncompensated Connectors and Accuracy; p. 7, Errors Due to Open-Thermocouple Detection Circuitry]{ref} A fitted curve or compensation algorithm cannot make all these errors vanish. No single accuracy is assigned to every <thermocouple>.

= Thermocouple reference function
{parent=Thermocouple voltage calibration}
{title2=$F(T)=E(T,0)$}

A <thermocouple reference function> gives the nominal thermoelectric <electromotive force> versus <temperature> for a specified material pair and reference <temperature>, conventionally $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Real <Seebeck coefficients> vary with <temperature>, so
$$
F'(T)=S_A(T)-S_B(T)
$$
is generally nonconstant. A lookup calibration can use <interpolation> between tabulated values; a fitted calibration can use <polynomials> over stated intervals. Use the correct material type, units, reference and validity interval in both the forward and inverse conversions. The FP-TC-120 manual specifically requires the channel's <thermocouple> type to be configured and describes its use of NIST-175 reference characteristics.<FP-TC-120 and cFP-TC-120 Operating Instructions by National Instruments>[p. 6, Connecting Thermocouple Input Signals: channel type and NIST-175 linearization]{ref} A nominal reference curve is not evidence that an aged or inhomogeneous wire still follows it.

A local error estimate explains why the reference <thermometer> matters. If $\delta V$ is an additive voltage error and $\delta T_c$ is the error in the supplied reference <temperature>, <differentiation> of the compensation equation gives, for $F'(T_h)\ne0$,
$$
\delta T_h\approx\frac{\delta V+F'(T_c)\,\delta T_c}{F'(T_h)}.
$$
A small <slope> $F'(T_h)$ magnifies voltage errors. In the stipulated $40\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ model, a $40\,\mu\mathrm V$ offset gives a $1\,\mathrm K$ inferred-<temperature> error, and a $1\,\mathrm K$ reference error gives a $1\,\mathrm K$ inferred-<temperature> error. These are sensitivities of the chosen model, not universal performance figures for real sensors.

= Peltier effect
{c}
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}

The <Peltier effect> is <heat transfer> associated with an <electric current> crossing a junction between materials with different <Peltier coefficients>. At fixed junction <temperature>, its contribution changes sign when the <conventional current> reverses. A junction can absorb <heat> from its surroundings for one direction and release it for the other. <Joule heating>, proportional to the square of the <electric current>, accompanies this transport but does not reverse sign. Consequently, observing a warm device alone does not identify the <Peltier effect>.

This makes the <Peltier effect> useful as an electrically controlled <heat pump>: reversing the supply can switch an object's thermal role without changing any plumbing. In <thermoelectric cooling>, however, the useful cooling is the junction's absorbed <heat> minus returning <heat conduction> and its share of <Joule heating>. Increasing <electric current> eventually makes cooling worse. The distinction between transported <heat> and net cooling is the central practical lesson of the <thermoelectric cooler energy balance>.

= Peltier coefficient
{c}
{parent=Peltier effect}
{title2=$\Pi$}

At uniform <temperature>, define the material <Peltier coefficient> by transported <heat current> along positive <conventional current>:
$$
\dot Q_{\rm tr}=\Pi I.
$$
Its units are $\mathrm{W/A}=\mathrm{J/C}=\mathrm V$. In one dimension, $q=\Pi j-\kappa\,dT/dx$, with <electric current density> $j$ and <thermal conductivity> $\kappa$. Here <heat> excludes the <electrochemical potential> contribution to total <energy flux>.<Thermoelectric effects by Leon van Dommelen>[A.11.1; A.11.4, Eq. (A.34): heat-flux convention]{ref}

Now orient positive <conventional current> $I$ from material A into material B, at a common junction <temperature> $T$. Define $\dot Q_{\rm abs}>0$ as <heat> taken from the junction's thermal surroundings. The outgoing transported <heat> minus the incoming transported <heat> gives
$$
\Pi_{A\to B}^{\rm abs}=\Pi_B-\Pi_A,\qquad
\dot Q_{\rm abs}=(\Pi_B-\Pi_A)I.
$$
The local released <heat> is $\dot Q_{\rm rel}=-\dot Q_{\rm abs}$. Authors defining a junction coefficient by released <heat> therefore use the opposite sign. Exchanging A and B reverses the coefficient; reversing $I$ at fixed A and B reverses the junction's thermal response. An individual material coefficient and a junction difference are different quantities.

For an original stipulated example, choose $S_A=-100\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ and $S_B=+150\,\mu\mathrm{V/K}$ at $T=300\,\mathrm K$. The <Kelvin thermoelectric relation> gives $\Pi_A=-0.030\,\mathrm V$, $\Pi_B=+0.045\,\mathrm V$. A positive $2\,\mathrm A$ flowing A to B absorbs $(0.075\,\mathrm V)(2\,\mathrm A)=0.150\,\mathrm W$. A separate $0.010\,\Omega$ junction <electrical resistance> would dissipate $0.040\,\mathrm W$ for either direction. If all that dissipation heats the junction, its net absorbed <power> is $0.110\,\mathrm W$ forwards and $-0.190\,\mathrm W$ backwards. These numbers illustrate a chosen thermal accounting boundary, not measured material data or a universal allocation of contact heating.

= Kelvin thermoelectric relation
{c}
{parent=Peltier coefficient}
{title2=$\Pi=TS$}

With the <Seebeck coefficient> convention $dV=-S\,dT$ at zero <electric current>, and the transported-<heat> convention of <Peltier coefficient>, the <Kelvin thermoelectric relation> is
$$
\Pi(T)=T S(T),\qquad
\Pi_{A\to B}^{\rm abs}(T)=T\bigl[S_B(T)-S_A(T)\bigr].
$$
$T$ is absolute <temperature> in <kelvins>; use consistent material references. This scalar <linear response> relation assumes local <thermal equilibrium> and reciprocal transport without time-reversal-breaking fields or order. <Onsager reciprocal relations> supply the symmetry; <conservation of energy> alone is insufficient. A <magnetic field> requires a reversed-field reciprocity comparison.<Thermoelectric effects by Leon van Dommelen>[A.11.6: reciprocity and the Peltier–Seebeck relation]{ref}

In particular, the A-minus-B <thermocouple> coefficient in <Seebeck effect> is $S_A-S_B$, whereas absorbed junction <heat> for <conventional current> A to B uses $S_B-S_A$. There is no contradiction: terminal voltage order and crossing direction are separate choices. Multiplying a measured voltage by an absolute <temperature> without specifying these choices does not determine junction heating.

A short reciprocity calculation makes the factor $T$ visible. At a common reference <temperature>, write the local <linear response> with electrical driving field $\mathcal E=-\nabla V$ and <heat flux> $q$ as
$$
j=L_{11}\frac{\mathcal E}{T}+L_{12}\frac{-\nabla T}{T^2},\qquad
q=L_{21}\frac{\mathcal E}{T}+L_{22}\frac{-\nabla T}{T^2}.
$$
At $j=0$, the <Seebeck coefficient> convention gives $S=L_{12}/(T L_{11})$. At $\nabla T=0$, the <Peltier coefficient> is $q/j=L_{21}/L_{11}$. Thus $L_{21}=L_{12}$ gives $\Pi=TS$. The chosen forces are conjugate to the fluxes in the local <entropy production>, $j\mathcal E/T+q(-\nabla T)/T^2$; arbitrary rescaling of just one force would spoil this argument.

The relation is local. Varying $S(T)$ permits distributed <Thomson effect> heating, with $\tau=T\,dS/dT$; the constant-coefficient model below omits it. Endpoint substitutions into a constant-property formula can therefore break its <energy> accounting.

= Peltier current-reversal experiment
{c}
{parent=Peltier effect}

An actual institutional protocol is CTU's *Measurement of characteristics of a Peltier cell*. Its cooling run uses a water container, an insulating cap and a current-limited supply; both face <temperatures> are recorded every minute while <electric current> is monitored. The apparatus includes a water-cooled exchanger, <copper> blocks and brass containers. The heating run resets the water near room <temperature> before reversing polarity. The handout specifies no <thermometer> type and reports no dataset. Its modular junction geometry is shown separately from the external thermal assembly.<Measurement of characteristics of a Peltier cell by Milan Červenka and Ladislav Sieger>[pp. 3–4, Figs. 1.4–1.5; pp. 5–7, §§1.5.2–1.5.3 and Appendix]{ref}

\Image[peltier-current-reversal-apparatus.png]
{title=<Peltier effect> apparatus and a junction's <conventional current> direction}
{description=Original explanatory schematic, not an apparatus photograph. The external water assembly follows the CTU protocol qualitatively; readout locations are schematic because sensor construction is unspecified. The inset is an idealized material pair, not a reconstruction of that cell's internal fabrication. Dashed arrows mark proposed parasitic <heat transfer> controls.}

The following is a proposed quantitative extension. Keep the physical face labels fixed throughout reversal: c denotes the observed object's side and h the exchanger side, even if their actual <temperatures> change order. Use an independently read <thermometer> at each thermal contact and log the actual signed <electric current> and terminal <electric potential difference>. Maintain reproducible contact pressure and contact layers. A sensor in water measures water <temperature>, which need not equal the face <temperature> during a transient; verify mixing and contact equilibration before using it as a face reading. Do not interchange sensor leads when reversing the supply.

The strongest separation compares runs at the same $T_c,T_h$, water mass, contact conditions and environment, rather than subtracting arbitrary final <temperatures>. One option is short initial-rate runs after resetting the whole assembly to a reproducible state. Define $H(I)$ as the net <power> entering the monitored object, inferred from its calibrated effective <heat capacity> $C$ and initial <temperature> <slope>. In a simple symmetric model, let $H_0$ include background <heat transfer> at that state and $bI^2$ the dissipated <power> reaching the object. Then
$$
H(I)=C\frac{dT_c}{dt}=H_0-aI+bI^2,\qquad a>0,
$$
where $a=\alpha T_c$ in the <thermoelectric cooling> model below. The half-difference and baseline-subtracted half-sum are
$$
H_{\rm odd}(I)=\frac{H(+I)-H(-I)}{2}=-aI,\qquad
H_{\rm even}(I)=\frac{H(+I)+H(-I)}{2}-H(0)=bI^2.
$$
The minus sign in the odd part means positive <electric current> cools the object. The even part has to subtract the zero-current baseline: otherwise <heat conduction> and ambient exchange remain mixed with <Joule heating>. Independently measured <heat capacity> is essential for converting a thermal <slope> into <watts>; include the blocks, container and water that actually equilibrate on the measurement timescale.

Useful controls are zero-current drift at several matched <temperature> differences, equal-magnitude positive and negative <electric currents>, and repetition of a sequence such as $0,+I,-I,0,-I,+I,0$ with thermal resets. A calibrated small heater on the object can test the <heat capacity> and lost-<heat> correction independently. Check <Joule heating> in supply leads and contacts, bypass <heat conduction> through supports and wiring, and ambient <convection> and <thermal radiation>. Insulation reduces a leak; it does not establish that it is zero. Measure terminal <power> at the module boundary so lead losses are not mistaken for module heating.

An odd thermal response supports a reversible current-coupled contribution, but does not by itself locate all of it at one junction: the <Thomson effect> in nonuniform leads can also be odd. Sensor offsets, unequal current magnitudes, drifting thermal contacts and different junction <temperatures> during the two runs can contaminate both parts. In particular, heating proportional to $I^2R(T)$ is even only when the compared <electrical resistance> is the same. CTU's qualitative reversal illustrates switching heating and cooling; the matched-state controls above would be needed for a coefficient measurement.

= Peltier cooling
{synonym}
{c}

<Thermoelectric cooling> uses the <Peltier effect> to remove <heat> from a load using electrical <work>. A typical <thermoelectric module> connects many pairs of different <semiconductors> electrically in a <series circuit> and thermally in parallel between two faces. For an idealized pair with $S_p>S_n$, positive <conventional current> crosses n to p at the cold bridge and p to n at the hot return. With common bridge material and isothermal contacts, the intervening metal contributions telescope: the net pair coefficient is $S_p-S_n$. For $N$ identical pairs define the module <Seebeck coefficient> $\alpha=N(S_p-S_n)>0$.

Choose fixed face <temperatures> $T_h\ge T_c>0$, $\Delta T=T_h-T_c$, module <electrical resistance> $R>0$ and open-circuit <thermal conductance> $K>0$. All three module parameters $\alpha,R,K$ are constants over the chosen operating range. Positive $I$ is the orientation absorbing <Peltier effect> <heat> at c. Define $Q_c$ as net <heat> extracted from the cold reservoir per unit time, $Q_h$ as net <heat> delivered to the hot reservoir per unit time, and $P_{\rm in}$ as electrical <power> entering the module. Despite the conventional letters $Q_c,Q_h$, both denote <power> in <watts>, not accumulated <energy>. Ferrotec gives the corresponding single-stage equations and distinguishes module performance from bypass leaks.<Mathematical Modeling of Modules by Ferrotec>[§11.4(a–f), single-stage equations; §11.7, surrounding air and clamping-screw heat leaks]{ref}

= Thermoelectric cooler energy balance
{parent=Thermoelectric cooling}
{title2=$Q_c=\alpha T_c I-\tfrac12 I^2R-K(T_h-T_c)$}

The chosen steady-state model is
$$
Q_c=\alpha T_c I-\frac12 I^2R-K\Delta T,\qquad
Q_h=\alpha T_h I+\frac12 I^2R-K\Delta T,
$$
$$
U=\alpha\Delta T+IR,\qquad
P_{\rm in}=UI=\alpha\Delta T I+I^2R,\qquad
Q_h-Q_c=P_{\rm in}.
$$
Here $U$ is the terminal voltage drop along positive <electric current> through the module. Its <Seebeck effect> term opposes the cooling supply. The cold <Peltier effect> term removes <heat>; <Joule heating> and returning <heat conduction> reduce the available cooling. At zero <electric current>, $Q_c=Q_h=-K\Delta T$: the passive leak warms c and removes <heat> from h, consistent with the stated signs. Subtracting the two face equations cancels <heat conduction> and gives the electrical <power>, an independent <conservation of energy> check.

The factor one half is an ideal-model result, not a universal rule for every contact. To see its origin, let $x$ run from cold to hot along a uniform equivalent leg of length $L$, cross-sectional <area> $A$, <electrical resistivity> $\rho$ and <thermal conductivity> $\kappa$. With constant coefficients, insulated sides and no <Thomson effect>, steady <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\kappa A T''=-\frac{I^2\rho}{A},\qquad
T(x)=T_c+\frac{\Delta T}{L}x+\frac{I^2\rho}{2\kappa A^2}x(L-x).
$$
The <Fourier's law> <heat current> in the positive $x$ direction is $-\kappa A T'$. At $x=0$ it equals $-K\Delta T-I^2R/2$, and at $x=L$ it equals $-K\Delta T+I^2R/2$, using $K=\kappa A/L$ and $R=\rho L/A$. Adding the local transported <heat current> $\alpha T I$ gives the two face equations. Summing equivalent legs preserves this accounting. Nonuniform material properties, side losses or asymmetric contact dissipation require a revised model.

For an original toy module, stipulate $\alpha=0.040\,\mathrm{V/K}$, $R=2.0\,\Omega$, $K=0.40\,\mathrm{W/K}$, $T_c=280\,\mathrm K$ and $T_h=300\,\mathrm K$. At $I=3.0\,\mathrm A$,
$$
Q_c=33.6-9.0-8.0=16.6\,\mathrm W,\qquad
Q_h=36.0+9.0-8.0=37.0\,\mathrm W,
$$
$$
U=0.8+6.0=6.8\,\mathrm V,\qquad P_{\rm in}=20.4\,\mathrm W,
\qquad \mathrm{COP}=\frac{Q_c}{P_{\rm in}}\approx0.814.
$$
The <coefficient of performance> is defined here only for refrigeration with $Q_c>0$ and $P_{\rm in}>0$. The hot-side assembly must remove $37.0\,\mathrm W$, not just the extracted $16.6\,\mathrm W$. These are stipulated calculations, not measurements or a commercial module specification.

At the same fixed face <temperatures> but $I=-3.0\,\mathrm A$, the same boundary definitions give $Q_c=-50.6\,\mathrm W$, $Q_h=-35.0\,\mathrm W$, $U=-5.2\,\mathrm V$ and $P_{\rm in}=15.6\,\mathrm W$. The formerly cold face is now heated; the formerly hot reservoir supplies $35.0\,\mathrm W$. Still $Q_h-Q_c=15.6\,\mathrm W$. Reversal changes the linear terms, while $I^2R$ and the leak at fixed <temperatures> remain unchanged. Holding the old labels fixed makes this check meaningful.

The corresponding steady <entropy production> of the module is the outgoing reservoir <entropy> minus the incoming reservoir <entropy>:
$$
\dot S_{\rm gen}=\frac{Q_h}{T_h}-\frac{Q_c}{T_c}
=\frac{I^2R}{2}\left(\frac1{T_h}+\frac1{T_c}\right)
+\frac{K(\Delta T)^2}{T_hT_c}\ge0.
$$
The <Peltier effect> terms cancel because they carry the same <entropy> $\alpha I$ at each end. This cancellation explains the reversible coupling; it does not make finite-resistance operation reversible. In the toy example the two positive contributions are approximately $0.06214$ and $0.001905\,\mathrm{W/K}$, giving $\dot S_{\rm gen}=0.06405\,\mathrm{W/K}$ for either current direction. In cooling operation, combining this inequality with the <energy> balance gives the <Carnot refrigerator> limit $\mathrm{COP}\le T_c/(T_h-T_c)$, equal to $14$ for these reservoir <temperatures>. A <coefficient of performance> above one would describe transported <heat> per electrical <work>, not the creation of <energy>.

= Thermoelectric cooling current limit
{parent=Thermoelectric cooling}
{title2=$I_*=\alpha T_c/R$}

At fixed face <temperatures>, <differentiation> of the <thermoelectric cooler energy balance> gives
$$
\frac{dQ_c}{dI}=\alpha T_c-RI,\qquad
\frac{d^2Q_c}{dI^2}=-R<0,\qquad
I_* =\frac{\alpha T_c}{R},\qquad
Q_{c,\max}=\frac{\alpha^2T_c^2}{2R}-K\Delta T.
$$
Thus current chosen for maximum cooling <power> is not automatically current for maximum <coefficient of performance>. Cooling exists only when $(\alpha T_c)^2>2RK\Delta T$, and then only between the two positive roots
$$
I_-<I<I_+,\qquad
I_\pm=\frac{\alpha T_c\pm\sqrt{(\alpha T_c)^2-2RK\Delta T}}{R}.
$$
At equality the parabola merely touches $Q_c=0$. For the toy module the interval is approximately $0.767<I<10.433\,\mathrm A$, with $I_*=5.60\,\mathrm A$ and $Q_{c,\max}=23.36\,\mathrm W$. At $12\,\mathrm A$, the object is heated even in the nominal cooling polarity: $Q_c=-17.6\,\mathrm W$. This mathematical interval supplies no permission to exceed an actual device's current or <temperature> ratings.

\Image[peltier-cooling-current-balance.png]
{title=Chosen <thermoelectric cooling> model: useful cooling and current-reversal separation}
{description=Original calculated curves, not experimental data. Left: positive <Peltier effect> transport competes with <Joule heating> and the fixed leak, leaving a bounded cooling interval. Right: holding the same two face <temperatures>, the half-difference of $Q_c(+I)$ and $Q_c(-I)$ isolates $\alpha T_c I$; the half-sum is $-I^2R/2-K\Delta T$. Subtracting $Q_c(0)$ isolates $-I^2R/2$. The toy parameters are those in <thermoelectric cooler energy balance>.}

The fixed-face constraint matters. With fixed $T_h$ but a freely cooling object, $T_c$ must be solved from its <heat> load and leaks rather than held at $280\,\mathrm K$ while claiming an arbitrary <temperature> drop. For zero load and optimal current, the same model gives
$$
\frac{\alpha^2T_c^2}{2R}=K(T_h-T_c),\qquad
T_{c,\min}=\frac{\sqrt{1+2ZT_h}-1}{Z},\qquad
Z=\frac{\alpha^2}{RK}.
$$
Here $Z$ has units $\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and is a module parameter. The toy model gives $Z=0.0020\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $T_{c,\min}\approx241.62\,\mathrm K$ when $T_h=300\,\mathrm K$. This is an ideal zero-load limit with stipulated constant properties; cooling a real load requires $Q_c$ above zero and reduces the attainable drop. Contact <thermal resistance>, a warming <heat sink>, external leaks and temperature-dependent coefficients further change the result. Electrical control is convenient, but <conservation of energy> still requires a thermal path that removes the extracted <heat> plus the electrical input.
