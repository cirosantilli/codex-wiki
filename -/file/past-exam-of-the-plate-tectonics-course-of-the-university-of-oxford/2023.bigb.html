Worked solutions to the ten marked parts of the <plate tectonics> paper in the <Plate tectonics course of the University of Oxford>, third-year examination, 25 May 2023. The examination required candidates to choose two questions; all three are treated here. https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/earth_science_past_papers_and_ex/response/3419815/attach/4/Papers.zip?cookie_passthrough=1[Source release: Papers.zip], member `Papers/Plate Techonics/FOI-202604-431 - Paper 8 2023.pdf`, printed code A17843W1, three pages. Question wording is not reproduced.

== Question 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose an ocean–continent <convergent margin>. The essential relationship is **recycling of <oceanic lithosphere> by <subduction>, together with production and modification of <crust> in a <volcanic arc>**.

\Image[2023-convergent-margin.png]
{title=An original ocean–continent subduction cross-section, with slab recycling, fluid release and arc crust production}

The incoming <tectonic plate> consists of thin <oceanic crust>, chiefly <basalt> and <gabbro>, above colder <lithospheric mantle>. As that <oceanic lithosphere> approaches the <subduction zone>, bending produces the <subduction trench>. Its cold interior can become denser than the surrounding <mantle>; the resulting <slab pull> helps sustain <subduction>. The descending <slab> carries both <oceanic crust> and <lithospheric mantle> into the interior. Thus the recycled unit is an entire portion of <lithosphere>, rather than just its <crust>.

At the <subduction trench>, some incoming <sediment> is scraped off and stacked by <thrust faults> into an <accretionary prism>. Material can also be added beneath the <overriding plate> by <tectonic underplating>. A <forearc basin> collects <sediment> between the <accretionary prism> and the <volcanic arc>. This is an accreting example: other <convergent margins> lose upper-plate material through <tectonic erosion>, so an <accretionary prism> is not universal.

The cold <slab> undergoes increasing <pressure> and <metamorphism>. Conversion of <basaltic rocks> and <gabbro> towards <eclogite> increases their <density>, while <slab dehydration> releases <water> from hydrated <minerals>. The rising fluid lowers the <solidus> of hot <peridotite> in the <mantle wedge>, causing <flux melting>. The usual source of <arc magma> is therefore the fluid-modified <mantle wedge>; extensive <slab melting> is not required. Thermal-petrological calculations show that the depth and amount of <slab dehydration> depend on the incoming <slab>'s thermal structure.<Subduction factory: 4. Depth-dependent flux of H2O from subducting slabs worldwide by Peter E. van Keken and others>[Abstract and depth-dependent water-release models.]{ref}

Buoyant <magma> rises through the <overriding plate>, freezes in <igneous intrusions> or erupts in the <volcanic arc>. <Fractional crystallization>, interaction with existing <continental crust> and repeated intrusion can produce more evolved <arc magmas> and add new <crust>. Some material merely reworks old <crust>, and simultaneous <tectonic erosion> can remove it: <volcanism> alone does not measure net continental growth. <Tectonic shortening>, <folding> and <thrust faults> can thicken and raise the upper plate.

The <Wadati-Benioff zone> marks <earthquakes> along and within the cold descending <slab>. Shallow interface <earthquakes> reflect unstable slip where the plates are coupled; deeper <earthquakes> require mechanisms such as <dehydration embrittlement> and are not simply dry shallow friction operating at every depth. In contrast, the hot <mantle wedge> deforms mainly by <material creep>. Together, the <subduction trench>, <accretionary prism>, <forearc basin>, <volcanic arc> and inclined <seismicity> connect surface features to the processes recycling and creating <crust>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Slab pull> is generally the largest direct driving contribution for plates attached to substantial cold descending <slabs>.** Cooling makes <oceanic lithosphere> negatively buoyant; sinking <slabs> transmit a pulling force to the rest of the <tectonic plate>. The comparison is a force balance, so the following driving and resisting processes both matter.

* <Ridge push> is gravitational sliding away from an elevated <spreading ridge>. Cooling, contraction and deepening of <oceanic lithosphere> establish the lateral difference in integrated <pressure>; <ridge push> is not simply <magma> squirting sideways into a plate.
* <Basal traction on a tectonic plate> transfers <shear stress> between the <lithosphere> and circulating <mantle>. It can help or oppose <plate motion>, depending on their relative <velocities>. <Slab suction> is the contribution of <slab>-induced <mantle flow> and its associated <pressure> and <traction> to nearby <plate motion>.
* <Frictional sliding> along <faults>, shearing along the plate interface, bending of the incoming <slab> and <viscous dissipation> in the surrounding <mantle> resist <plate motion>. <Continental collision> introduces additional resistance through buoyancy and deformation of thick <continental crust>.
* <Mantle plumes> and <continental rifting> can locally promote uplift, weakening and separation, but do not provide a universal explanation for all <plate motion>.

The first two gravitational contributions ultimately belong to the same coupled <mantle convection> system: the plates are its cold upper <thermal boundary layer>. They are not passive rafts on a prescribed conveyor belt. A plate with little attached descending <slab> need not have <slab pull> as its largest local driver, and a large gross <slab pull> can be partly consumed by bending and interface resistance. Geological observations and force comparisons nonetheless support the general dominance of <slab pull> for rapidly moving subducting plates.<What causes earthquakes? by British Geological Survey>[Section explaining mantle circulation, ridge push and slab pull, including the association between subducting plate edges and faster motion.]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Repeated assembly and dispersal are supported by mutually consistent <paleogeography>, <paleomagnetism>, dated collision belts and subsequent rifted margins.** A useful reconstruction must explain both former connections and later separation, at the same geological ages.

For the youngest well-constrained example, <Pangaea>, matching continental outlines become more compelling when the fit uses the continental edges rather than mobile modern shorelines. More discriminating evidence includes the continuation of <mountain belts>, <sedimentary successions> and distinctive basement rocks across now-separated continents. Correlated terrestrial <fossil assemblages> and the distribution of <glacial deposits> become geographically coherent when the continents are restored. Each observation alone can have alternatives, but a single reconstruction that satisfies several independent tests is much harder to explain by coincidence.

For older examples, the most useful surviving record lies within the <cratons> and their margins. Dated <suture zones>, <ophiolites>, <metamorphic belts> and <pressure-temperature-time paths> document former ocean closure and <continental collision>. Restoring now-separated belts to continuity tests an assembly. Subsequent <continental rifting>, matching <dike swarms>, <large igneous provinces> and the transition to <passive margins> test its breakup. <Uranium-lead dating> of <zircon>, including <detrital zircon> populations and <sedimentary provenance>, helps establish whether the proposed connections existed at the same time.

<Paleomagnetism> supplies an independent quantitative test. A primary <remanent magnetization> constrains ancient latitude and orientation. After a proposed relative rotation brings two blocks together, compatible <apparent polar wander paths> over an interval support their travelling together; divergence of those paths can document separation. <Radiometric dating>, a <paleomagnetic fold test> and a <paleomagnetic reversal test> help identify primary records and distinguish them from <remagnetization>. There remain important ambiguities: conventional <paleomagnetism> does not fix absolute longitude, and sparse ages, later deformation and <true polar wander> complicate a reconstruction.

The broad succession is <Columbia (supercontinent)> or <Nuna>, assembled mainly around 1.9–1.6 billion years ago; <Rodinia>, assembled mainly around 1.1–0.9 billion years ago and dispersed in stages during the later <Proterozoic>; and <Pangaea>, assembled by roughly 300 million years ago and breaking apart from roughly 200 million years ago. These are broad intervals rather than synchronous global events. A quantitative <Nuna> reconstruction combines <paleomagnetism> with the histories of continental margins rather than relying only on matching rock ages.<Assembly and breakup of the core of Paleoproterozoic-Mesoproterozoic supercontinent Nuna by David A. D. Evans and Ross N. Mitchell>[pp. 443–446, reconstruction of the Nuna core and tests using paleomagnetic and tectonostratigraphic records.]{ref}

Repeated global peaks in dated <magmatism> and <metamorphism> offer supporting evidence, but cannot prove a <supercontinent cycle> by themselves: <erosion>, burial and preferential preservation also change the observed age distribution. The persuasive case is the recurrence of dated assembly–breakup sequences in different <continental configurations>, with stronger geometric constraints for <Pangaea> than for the older <supercontinents>. A <Wilson cycle> describes an individual ocean's opening and closure; a <supercontinent cycle> concerns the much larger-scale continental assembly and dispersal.

== Question 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Plate tectonics> changes the environments in which <natural selection>, <gene flow> and <extinction> operate. **It influences both the persistence of habitable conditions and the geographical and chemical opportunities for <evolution>.** It does not prescribe a unique sequence of biological innovations.

Separation of continents by <continental rifting> divides populations and reduces <gene flow>, promoting <allopatric speciation>. <Continental collision> or newly exposed land connections can reunite populations, permit dispersal and introduce competitors or predators. These mechanisms connect <biogeography> to <continental configuration>. Changing the amount and fragmentation of shallow <continental shelf> habitat can also change the opportunities for diversification of marine <organisms>.

Over long intervals, <volcanic outgassing>, <silicate weathering>, carbonate burial and <subduction> exchange <carbon> among the interior, <atmosphere> and <ocean>. In a suitable regime, warmer, wetter conditions increase <silicate weathering>, remove atmospheric <carbon dioxide> and weaken the <greenhouse effect>. This <negative feedback> can support long-lived <habitability>, though it depends on exposed fresh <rock>, available <water> and reaction rates.<A negative feedback mechanism for the long-term stabilization of Earth's surface temperature by James C. G. Walker and others>[Weathering–carbon dioxide–temperature coupling in the proposed long-term stabilization mechanism.]{ref} <Mountain building> changes precipitation, <erosion> and delivery of <nutrients> to the <ocean>, affecting <primary production> and organic <carbon> burial.

The opening and closure of <ocean gateways> changes <ocean circulation>, transport of <heat> and the supply of <oxygen> to deep <water>. Together with movement of land through different latitudes and changes in <sea level>, this reorganizes <climate> and habitat. Enhanced organic <carbon> burial can change atmospheric <oxygen> over long intervals, while a poorly ventilated <ocean> can develop <ocean anoxia>. Such chemical changes alter which forms of <metabolism> are viable.

<Hydrothermal circulation> at <spreading ridges> and through fractured <rock> supplies <chemical disequilibrium> and mineral surfaces. For example, <serpentinization> can produce <hydrogen> usable by <chemolithotrophy> when suitable electron acceptors are available. This supplies a possible setting for <abiogenesis> and early ecosystems; it does not establish that <life> originated there.

There can also be abrupt disruption. Rapid emplacement of a <large igneous province> can change <climate> through gas release; sulfur-bearing <aerosols> can initially cool, whereas sustained <carbon dioxide> release can warm and contribute to <ocean acidification> and <ocean anoxia>. Such perturbations can contribute to <mass extinction>, followed by <adaptive radiation> among survivors. The <Deccan Traps> exemplify the scale of <volcanism> involved, but their association with the <Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary> does not justify attributing the entire biological crisis to <volcanism> alone. Both gradual environmental reorganization and rare rapid perturbations belong in the explanation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret <habitability> as the ability to sustain <life> with broadly terrestrial <biochemistry>. **Accessible <liquid water>, usable <free energy>, suitable chemical ingredients and sufficiently persistent conditions are the central requirements.** Earth-like geography, a breathable <atmosphere> and actual habitation are not part of that definition.

**A viable local physical environment.** The relevant <temperature> and <pressure> must permit <liquid water> where the <organisms> live. For a surface habitat this normally requires an appropriate <atmosphere> and <surface temperature>; the roughly 6.1 millibar triple-point <pressure> of pure <water> is a minimum phase constraint, not a guarantee of a temperate surface. High <salinity>, extreme acidity and low <water activity> can make apparently wet environments unsuitable for known <life>. A subsurface <ocean> beneath ice can provide liquid conditions even when the surface is frozen, so the entire body need not lie in the conventional surface-water <habitable zone>.

**A suitable orbit and rotation.** For a surface-water <planet>, orbital distance must give an absorbed <radiation budget> compatible with liquid conditions after the body's <Bond albedo>, <atmosphere> and <greenhouse effect> are included. The simple <planetary equilibrium temperature> estimate
$$
T_{\rm eq}=\left[\frac{(1-A)S}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4}
$$
uses orbital irradiance $S$, <Bond albedo> $A$ and the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant> $\sigma$, assumes unit thermal emissivity and averages over the whole sphere. It is not a prediction of <surface temperature> beneath a <atmosphere> with a <greenhouse effect>. Excessive absorbed energy risks a <runaway greenhouse>; inadequate surface heating can freeze surface <water>, although <heat> from the interior or <tidal heating> can maintain a subsurface habitat.

The orbit must persist without collision or ejection for a biologically useful interval. Large <orbital eccentricity> can produce extreme variations in heating, but neither exactly circular motion nor Earth's particular <obliquity> is essential. <Rotation period> and <obliquity> govern illumination and seasons; atmospheric and <ocean heat flux> determine how severe those variations become. <Tidal locking> does not automatically make a body uninhabitable, because sufficient heat transport can moderate the day–night contrast. A <natural satellite> also needs a stable orbit around its primary and a tolerable combination of irradiation and <tidal heating>: enough heating may maintain an <ocean>, whereas excessive heating can make the environment hostile. These are constraints on the body's location and path, without requiring a particular kind of surrounding planetary system.

**An adequate chemical inventory and access to it.** Known <life> uses <carbon>, <hydrogen>, <nitrogen>, <oxygen>, <phosphorus> and <sulfur>, together with catalytic trace elements. A <rocky planet> supplies mineral surfaces and reservoirs, but total inventory is not enough: <chemical weathering>, <water-rock interaction> or other transport must make <nutrients> accessible. A water-rich body with an <ocean> isolated from its rocky interior by a thick high-pressure ice layer could have difficulty recycling some <nutrients>; this is a possible limitation, not proof of sterility.

**A usable energy supply.** <Photosynthesis> can exploit light in an illuminated habitat. A dark subsurface habitat instead needs sustained <chemical disequilibrium>, for example oxidized and reduced species brought together by <water-rock interaction>, <hydrothermal circulation> or <radiolysis>. Warm <water> at complete <chemical equilibrium> is not, by itself, an energy supply for sustained <metabolism>.

**Retention, protection and longevity.** The body's <mass>, radius, <surface gravity> and thermal history affect retention of <water> and an <atmosphere>. <Escape velocity> must be considered together with gas <temperature>, molecular mass, <atmospheric escape> and replenishment: there is no universal Earth-mass threshold. An <atmosphere>, ice or <rock> overburden can protect <life> from damaging <ionizing radiation> and short-wavelength light. An intrinsic <planetary magnetic field> can modify charged-particle exposure and escape, but it is neither a universal requirement nor a guarantee of retention.

Finally, conditions must last long enough. Stored formation <heat>, <radiogenic heating> and possibly <tidal heating> can maintain circulation and chemical exchange. <Plate tectonics> can recycle materials and support a <carbonate-silicate cycle>, but it is not established as a necessary condition for every habitable body: conditional long-term <carbon cycling> is possible on a <stagnant-lid planet> as well.<Carbon Cycling and Habitability of Earth-Sized Stagnant Lid Planets by Bradford J. Foley and Andrew J. Smye>[Abstract and coupled thermal/carbon-cycle model; habitability is conditional on degassing, weathering and interior evolution.]{ref} The necessary end result is persistence of a suitable environment, rather than a checklist requiring every feature of the modern <Earth>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Prioritize accessible ancient <lacustrine deposits> at a preserved <river delta>, especially fine-grained, <clay mineral>-bearing <mudstones>, with nearby evidence of <groundwater> or <hydrothermal circulation>.** The target should combine past <habitability>, good <biosignature preservation> and practical access to minimally altered <rock>.

An inlet valley, a <river delta> and a stratified basin together can establish a connected fluvial–lake <paleoenvironment> on <Mars>. A long-lived lake offers <liquid water>, chemical transport and a sustained habitat, while a <river delta> concentrates material from a larger catchment. Distal <mudstone> is particularly attractive: fine particles can adsorb <organic molecules>, burial reduces exposure, and quiet deposition can preserve small-scale structures. Rover observations have independently established ancient lake and <river delta> settings on <Mars>, so this is a geological search strategy grounded in observed environments.<A Habitable Fluvio-Lacustrine Environment at Yellowknife Bay, Gale Crater, Mars by John P. Grotzinger and others>[Abstract: a fluvio-lacustrine sedimentary environment with inferred chemical conditions suitable for microbial life.]{ref}<Perseverance rover reveals an ancient delta-lake system and flood deposits at Jezero crater, Mars by Nicolas Mangold and others>[Abstract: delta-lake deposition followed by later high-energy flooding.]{ref}

A second high-value setting is an ancient <hydrothermal deposit>, particularly <silica>-rich precipitates, spring terraces or mineralized <groundwater> fractures near a heat source. Such environments combine <water-rock interaction>, <chemical disequilibrium> and the possibility of rapid mineral entombment. Distal, moderate-temperature parts of a <hydrothermal circulation> system are preferable to strongly reheated zones where <organic molecules> would be destroyed. Orbital observations identify candidate ancient <hydrothermal deposits> on <Mars>, although their geological interpretation requires testing on the ground.<Ancient hydrothermal seafloor deposits in Eridania basin on Mars by Joseph R. Michalski and others>[Abstract and inferred deep-water hydrothermal setting; evidence for an environment rather than evidence of life.]{ref} <Carbonate>-bearing <sedimentary deposits> can also preserve textures and chemical records of former <water>, but precipitation of <carbonate> alone is not a <biosignature>.

Choose ancient deposits with good <stratigraphy>, limited later <metamorphism> and relatively recent exposure by <erosion>, or sample beneath the irradiated surface. Present exposed <organic molecules> can be destroyed by <cosmic rays>, <radiolysis> and oxidizing surface chemistry, so a drill, fresh scarp or recently excavated material improves the preservation prospects. Laboratory irradiation experiments on Martian analogue materials demonstrate why shallow ancient surface exposure can erase an organic record.<NASA Experiment Suggests Need to Dig Deep for Evidence of Life on Mars by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[27 June 2022, experiments on radiation-driven destruction of amino acids and implications for subsurface sampling.]{ref}

At the site, relate every sample to its <sedimentary facies> and mineral context. Seek several independent, spatially associated <biosignatures>: plausible microscopic structures, distinctive <organic molecules>, and <isotope fractionation> consistent with a biological process. Each has nonbiological alternatives, so test <water-rock interaction>, mineral self-organization and contamination before inferring <life>. Include control samples from adjacent unpromising <rocks> and track terrestrial contamination. A safe landing ellipse with traversable exposures of several related <sedimentary facies> is more useful than a spectacular but inaccessible single outcrop. The desired outcome is contextual evidence that can distinguish former <life> from merely former <habitability>.

== Question 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a <rock strength envelope>: at each depth plot the smaller <differential stress> needed for frictional failure and for sustained ductile <material creep> at a specified <strain rate>. **Shallow <rock> generally strengthens with depth because effective confining <pressure> increases; sufficiently deep hot <rock> weakens because thermally activated <material creep> becomes efficient.**

\Image[2023-strength-profiles.png]
{title=Illustrative rock-strength envelopes for a continental interior and an old central-Pacific plate; these are conceptual profiles, not measurements}

At shallow depth, <Byerlee's law> or a <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion> gives a relation of the form
$$
\tau_f=C+\mu(\sigma_n-P_f),
$$
where $C$ is cohesion, $\mu$ a <coefficient of friction>, $\sigma_n$ the compressive <normal stress> and $P_f$ the <pore pressure>. Increasing overburden generally increases <effective stress>, hence the <frictional strength>. Conversion from fault <shear stress> to <differential stress> depends on the stress regime and fault orientation; a sketch should not label a universal numerical slope. High <pore pressure> or weak <fault gouge> can lower the <frictional strength> substantially.

A simplified <dislocation creep> relation is
$$
\dot\epsilon=A\sigma^n\exp\!\left[-\frac{Q+PV^*}{RT}\right],
\qquad
\sigma_{\rm creep}=\left(\frac{\dot\epsilon}{A}\right)^{1/n}\exp\!\left[\frac{Q+PV^*}{nRT}\right].
$$
Here $Q$ is <activation energy>, $V^*$ <activation volume>, $R$ the <molar gas constant> and $T$ absolute <temperature>; $A$ and $n$ depend on composition and deformation mechanism. At fixed <strain rate>, increasing <temperature> usually decreases the required <stress> strongly. The competing effects of <pressure>, <water weakening of rocks>, <grain size> and composition also matter. The intersection with frictional failure gives a <brittle-ductile transition>, not a change from solid to liquid.

For the Indian continental interior, a useful first sketch has an upper-crustal strength peak, a weaker lower <continental crust>, and renewed strength in <lithospheric mantle> beneath the <Moho>. <Quartz>-bearing <rocks> can undergo ductile <material creep> at conditions where colder, drier <olivine>-rich <mantle> retains appreciable strength. This creates a possible two-peak, or “jelly sandwich”, <rock strength envelope>. With further depth the <mantle> also weakens. A cold <craton> can retain strong <lithospheric mantle> to much greater depths than a tectonically heated continental region.

For the central Pacific, show thin <mafic> <oceanic crust> above a relatively thick layer of strong <lithospheric mantle>. There need not be a conspicuous weak lower-crustal layer. Much of the integrated strength can reside below the <Moho>, with a broad strength peak followed by ductile weakening towards the <asthenosphere>. Cooling and age control the thickness: young hot <oceanic lithosphere> would give a much shallower and weaker profile than an old central-Pacific plate.

The plotted <Moho> depths of about 38 km and 7 km, and all plotted <stresses>, are illustrative assumptions. “India” alone does not specify a unique <geothermal gradient>, <water> content or <strain rate>, and the Pacific includes plates of different ages. The robust comparison is the possible crust–mantle layering in a continent versus the thin <oceanic crust> and mantle-dominated strength of an old oceanic plate. The <Moho> is a compositional boundary; neither it nor the deepest small plotted strength is automatically the seismic <lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**The volume alone does not determine a unique change of elevation.** A <local isostatic response to an added surface layer> depends on its thickness and <density>, so the <Deccan Traps>' footprint must be specified or retained as a parameter.

Let the deposited <basalt> have uniform thickness $H=V/A$ and <density> $\rho_b$, where $V=10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $A$ is the loaded area. Let $w$ be downward displacement of the original land surface, and let $\rho_m$ be the <density> of displaced <mantle>. Assume a subaerial, laterally extensive deposit, negligible air <density>, unchanged thickness and <density> of the pre-existing <crust>, and complete local <isostatic adjustment>. Take the <magma> supply to be external to the local compensation column, and neglect cooling, <erosion>, <magmatic underplating> and any deep thermal or source-depletion anomaly.

Choose a <compensation depth> beneath all the displaced interfaces. The added column <mass> per unit area is $\rho_b H$. Downward translation of the pre-existing <crust> replaces a thickness $w$ of <mantle> within the column, reducing its <mass> by $\rho_m w$. Equality of <hydrostatic pressure> at the <compensation depth> therefore gives
$$
\rho_b gH-\rho_m gw=0,
\qquad
\boxed{w=\frac{\rho_b}{\rho_m}\frac{V}{A}}.
$$
The newly formed top of the <basalt> stands $H$ above the depressed original surface. Consequently the change of the region's *top-surface* elevation is
$$
\boxed{\Delta z=H-w=\left(1-\frac{\rho_b}{\rho_m}\right)\frac{10^{15}}{A}\ \mathrm{m},\quad A\text{ in }\mathrm{m^2}}.
$$
Thus the original substrate **subsides**, but the top of the deposit **rises** if $\rho_b<\rho_m$. Distinguishing these two surfaces prevents reporting the large downward displacement as the change in plateau elevation.

For an explicitly illustrative choice $A=10^{12}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $\rho_b=2900\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\rho_m=3300\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, the deposit is 1000 m thick, the substrate subsides about 879 m, and **the final surface rises about 121 m**. This is a conditional estimate, not a number fixed by the paper. Half that footprint would double all three heights under the same assumptions. For a nonuniform deposit, local <isostasy> gives the same relation pointwise with $H(x,y)$; the total volume still cannot specify a unique local elevation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Elasticity> lets the <lithosphere> share a load with neighbouring regions through <lithospheric flexure>. **Beneath the centre of a localized load, subsidence is generally smaller than the local <isostasy> estimate, so the deposited surface stands higher.** The surrounding plate also bends down, and a more distant <flexural forebulge> may rise. The extra support is regional bending resistance, rather than removal of the load's <mass>.

\Image[2023-volcanic-loading.png]
{title=An original elastic-plate calculation contrasting narrow and broad loads at the same flexural rigidity; a broad load approaches local isostasy}

The controlling comparison is load width against the <lithosphere>'s flexural length, which increases with <flexural rigidity> and decreases with the relevant buoyant <geophysical density contrast>. Equivalently, the <flexural support ratio> becomes large for short-wavelength loading. A very broad nearly uniform load produces little curvature in its interior, so even a strong <elastic lithosphere> has a nearly local <isostatic adjustment> there. Bending matters most near its edges. A narrow load requires more curvature and can be supported appreciably by the surrounding <elastic lithosphere>.

The <Deccan Traps> form a broad continental <large igneous province>. The volcanic edifices of the <Hawaiian Islands> are more localized loads on an old <oceanic lithosphere>, for which regional bending and a flexural moat are prominent. **Under the usual broad-Deccan versus narrow-Hawaiian-load comparison, elastic strength matters more for the <Hawaiian Islands>.** The more coherent mantle-dominated strength of an old oceanic plate can reinforce this contrast. It cannot be deduced simply from “oceanic” versus “continental”: a cold Indian <craton> can be strong, and a site's <effective elastic thickness> depends on its thermal and loading history.

For an initially submerged Hawaiian load, replacing <seawater> changes both the load and buoyant restoring contrast. At fixed deposited thickness, the added load relative to the initial <water> column uses the rock-minus-water <density>, and the restoring term uses the mantle-minus-water <density>. An emerged island requires separate treatment of its subaerial and submerged portions. These corrections change the local <isostasy> reference but do not remove the importance of load width for <lithospheric flexure>. The broad <Hawaiian swell> should also be distinguished from the narrower volcanic load: deeper thermal or dynamic support of the <Hawaiian swell> is not the same mechanism as elastic support of the volcanic ridge.

Finally, over long periods <material creep>, yielding and thermal modification can reduce elastic support. Without measured load geometries, ages and <effective elastic thicknesses>, the ranking is a justified qualitative expectation rather than a theorem valid for every continental and oceanic load.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <asthenosphere> exists because the <mantle>'s ability to deform is strongly dependent on <temperature>, <pressure>, composition, <water> and deformation mechanism. Cooling from the surface creates a strong <lithosphere>. Beneath it, hotter <mantle> deforms by <material creep> at much lower <stress> on geological timescales. **The <asthenosphere> is predominantly solid weak <rock>, not a global ocean of <magma>.** On the much shorter timescale of a <seismic wave>, it still supports <S-waves>.

The hot shallow <mantle> can be close to its <solidus>. <Water weakening of rocks> and, in some settings, a small connected <melt fraction> can further lower effective <viscosity>. At greater depths, changes in <pressure>, <homologous temperature>, mineral structure and <water> storage modify the deformation laws; increasing depth does not imply indefinitely decreasing <viscosity>. Explaining a weak upper-mantle interval does not require every location to contain the same amount of <melt>.

There are several physically distinct boundaries to test. The thermal <lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary> describes a thermal transition, the mechanical boundary a contrast in long-term strength or <viscosity>, and the <seismic lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary> a change in <seismic velocity> or other wave properties. They need not have identical depths. A smooth <geothermal gradient> can explain broad thermal weakening, but a very sharp decrease of <seismic velocity> may require changes in <melt fraction>, hydration, composition or <seismic anisotropy>. A sharp shallow reflector within a <craton> might instead be an internal lithospheric boundary.<A global view of the lithosphere-asthenosphere boundary by Catherine A. Rychert and Peter M. Shearer>[Abstract and pp. 495–498: converted-wave sharpness and the possibility that the imaged cratonic discontinuity lies within thicker lithosphere.]{ref}

Begin with a thermal null model. A <half-space cooling model> gives
$$
T(z,t)=T_s+(T_m-T_s)\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}}\right).
$$
If a thermal boundary corresponds to a fixed <temperature> $T_*$, its depth is
$$
z_*(t)=2\sqrt{\kappa t}\,\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{T_*-T_s}{T_m-T_s}\right),
$$
so it deepens as the square root of plate age. Test that prediction against independently determined seafloor ages, <bathymetry> and <heat flux>. An <oceanic plate cooling model> adds finite thickness and basal heat supply, allowing the old-age depth to approach a finite value. For continents, include <radiogenic heating>, crustal thickness, the history of <continental rifting> or <tectonic thickening>, and advective heating by <magma> or <mantle flow>. A cold, dry <craton> and a recently extended volcanic region should not share the same thermal profile.

Then calculate the observable consequences of competing mechanisms rather than merely assigning a boundary to a map:

* Use <receiver functions> from both <P-to-S conversion> and <S-to-P conversion> to constrain the depth and sharpness of a negative <seismic velocity> contrast. Check for crustal reverberations and whether the result changes with incident direction or frequency. Combine these with <Rayleigh waves>, <Love waves> and <seismic tomography> to constrain the broader <shear-wave velocity> profile.
* Measure <seismic attenuation> and <seismic anisotropy>. Elevated <temperature>, small amounts of <melt> and aligned <minerals> can all affect apparent <seismic velocity>, but predict different attenuation, polarization and directional patterns. Convert laboratory elastic and anelastic measurements into synthetic seismic observations at the relevant frequencies.
* Obtain <magnetotelluric sounding> constraints on <electrical conductivity>, together with surface <heat flux>. Connected <melt> or hydrated material can enhance <electrical conductivity>, but the interpretation depends on composition and connectivity. Joint thermal, seismic and electrical modelling is more discriminating than any single proxy.
* Use <mantle xenoliths> and erupted <magmas> to constrain <pressure>, <temperature>, <water> content, <melt depletion> and composition, recognizing that transport can alter a sample. Relate these observations to <geodynamic modelling> of <mantle convection>, <slab> flow, <mantle plumes> and <lithospheric rejuvenation>.

Compare models against the observed boundary depth *and its sharpness*, including uncertainty and resolution. An age–depth trend with matching <heat flux> favours thermal control; a sharp discontinuity that fails the thermal prediction but coincides with an electrical anomaly can support a melt- or hydration-related transition. Neither observation alone proves a unique cause. The spatially varying boundary is best understood as the result of thermal evolution interacting with composition, fluids and deformation history.
