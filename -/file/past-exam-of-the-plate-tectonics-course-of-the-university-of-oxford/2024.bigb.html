== 1

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**A useful idealization is a thin <sedimentary cover> over a <felsic> upper <continental crust>, an intermediate middle <continental crust>, and a more <mafic> lower <continental crust>, ending at the <Mohorovičić discontinuity> at about 35–40 km.** The boundaries within the <continental crust> are approximate compositional divisions, not three universal sharp interfaces.

\Image[2024-continental-crust.png]
{title=An idealized modern continental crustal column}
{description=An original schematic for a 38 km thick continental crust. Depth ranges illustrate a compositional tendency; individual provinces have different lithologies and boundaries.}

* At the surface, a variable, commonly kilometre-scale <sedimentary cover> contains <sediments> and <sedimentary rocks>. Exposed crystalline basement can instead occur at the surface.
* In the upper roughly 15 km, representative <rocks> are <granite>, <granodiorite> and compositionally related <gneiss>, with <quartz> and <feldspar>. <Sedimentary rocks>, <metamorphic rocks> and volcanic <rocks> make this layer heterogeneous.
* At roughly 15–25 km, intermediate <gneiss> and <amphibolite>-facies assemblages become important. <Diorite> to <granodiorite> compositions and <amphibole>- and <plagioclase>-bearing <rocks> are representative.
* At roughly 25–38 km, <mafic granulite> and <gabbro>-derived <metamorphic rocks> are a useful average, with <plagioclase>, <pyroxene> and sometimes <garnet>. Some provinces instead have substantially intermediate or <felsic> lower <continental crust>.
* Below the <Mohorovičić discontinuity> is <peridotite> of the <mantle>, dominated by <olivine> and <pyroxene>. This is not a fourth crustal layer.

The important trend is increasing average <mafic> character with depth, rather than a change from <granite> directly to <peridotite>. <Granulite> describes a <metamorphic facies>, not a unique chemical composition. The synthesis in <Nature and composition of the continental crust: A lower crustal perspective by Roberta L. Rudnick and David M. Fountain> combines seismic and petrological evidence for these distinctions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**The expected broad contrast is a less mature, commonly thinner early juvenile <continental crust>, with more <mafic> material and abundant sodic <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite>, rather than the more differentiated, potassium-rich granitoid component characteristic of much <Phanerozoic> <continental crust>.** This is a secular tendency, not a single stratified column applicable throughout the <Archean>.

<Archean> terrains commonly expose <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite> <gneiss> associated with <greenstone belts> containing <basalt>, <komatiite> and <metasedimentary rocks>. Many later granitoids are richer in <potassium>, including <granite> and <granodiorite>. The proportions changed during the <Archean>: a reconstruction from <trace element> ratios in <sedimentary rocks> infers a markedly <mafic> upper <continental crust> before about 3.0 Ga and a more <felsic> upper <continental crust> by 2.5 Ga. Thus “all Archean continental crust was mafic” would be wrong. A preserved <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite>-dominated nucleus can be strongly <felsic>, including at depth; its composition is not identical to that of its original juvenile precursor.<Archean upper crust transition from mafic to felsic marks the onset of plate tectonics by Ming Tang and others>{ref}

**Thickness needs a stated comparison.** An influential seismic compilation found preserved <Archean> <crust> about 27–40 km thick, commonly without the thick high-velocity basal layer of its 40–55 km thick <Proterozoic> comparison provinces. That supports a relatively thin endmember, but is not a measurement of every original <Archean> <continental crust>, nor a demonstration that every <Archean> region was thinner than today's 35–40 km reference column. <Tectonic thickening>, <magmatic underplating>, <erosion>, <delamination> and preservation bias all alter what survives. The very thick <lithospheric mantle> beneath a <craton> must also not be mistaken for thick <continental crust>.<Evolution of the Precambrian lithosphere: Seismological and geochemical constraints by Raymond J. Durrheim and Walter D. Mooney>{ref}

The transferable answer is therefore **more <mafic> early juvenile precursors, widespread sodic <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite> production, and variable thickness evolving through time**; late <Archean> <felsic> nuclei can already resemble substantial modern <continental crust>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Today the usual first melt is <basaltic> melt from the <mantle wedge>; in a hotter <Archean> <subduction zone>, melting of hydrated <basaltic crust> itself could generate sodic <felsic> melt of <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite> composition.** Distinguish a primary melt from the evolved melt ultimately added to the upper <continental crust>.

On the modern <Phanerozoic> <Earth>:

* <Hydrothermal alteration> introduces <water> into the incoming <oceanic crust> and locally into <serpentinized mantle>. <Sediments> also carry <water> in <pore space> and hydrous <minerals>.
* As the <slab> descends, heating and <metamorphism> break down hydrous <minerals>. Released fluids enter the hotter <peridotite> of the <mantle wedge> and lower its <solidus>, causing <flux melting>. Most ordinary modern <slabs> release fluids before extensive melting of their <basaltic crust>.
* The primary melt is predominantly hydrous <basaltic> <magma>. During ascent, storage and <fractional crystallization>, <mafic minerals> are removed and the remaining liquid evolves toward <andesite>, <dacite> and <rhyolite> compositions. <Partial melting> of recently emplaced <mafic> lower <continental crust>, <magma mixing> and some <crustal contamination> can contribute to <felsic> <magma>. Intrusive equivalents include <diorite>, <tonalite> and <granodiorite>.

This adds juvenile material derived from the <mantle>; simply remelting old <continental crust> is reworking, not net juvenile addition. The amount and depth of <slab> <water> release depend on its thermal history, as quantified in <Subduction factory: 4. Depth-dependent flux of H2O from subducting slabs worldwide by Peter E. van Keken and others>.

For the hotter <Archean> endmember posited in the question, a hotter <mantle> and a warm descending plate make it easier for hydrated <basaltic crust> to reach its <solidus>. Its <partial melting> can directly make <silica>-rich, <sodium>-rich <tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite> liquids, leaving a more <mafic> residue. Residual <garnet> and <amphibole>, and the presence or absence of residual <plagioclase>, help determine the <rare-earth element> and strontium signatures. These are not ultramafic liquids merely because the surrounding <mantle> was hotter.

**The contrast is therefore commonly fluid-fluxed <mantle> melting followed by differentiation today, versus a greater potential contribution from direct melting of hydrated <mafic crust> in the hotter case.** <Tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite> is not unique evidence for <subduction>: melting of thickened <mafic crust> can produce similar liquids. Nor are all modern arcs devoid of <slab melting>. These qualifications are central to <The composite Archaean grey gneisses: Petrological significance, and evidence for a non-unique tectonic setting for Archaean crustal growth by Jean-François Moyen>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**Hotter <Archean> <mantle> would generally produce thicker, more magnesium-rich <oceanic crust>, together with a more strongly depleted residual <mantle>.** Greater melt production, not merely thermal expansion of an unchanged modern column, is the principal reason for the increased crustal thickness.

At a <spreading ridge>, upwelling <peridotite> undergoes <adiabatic decompression melting>. A higher mantle <potential temperature> makes the ascending <mantle> intersect the <solidus> at greater <pressure> and depth. It therefore melts over a longer decompression interval and generally reaches a larger integrated <melt fraction>. With comparable <melt focusing>, more melt is supplied per unit area of newly formed <oceanic crust>.

Modern normal <oceanic crust> is about 6–7 km thick and contains <basaltic> lavas, a <sheeted dike complex>, and <gabbro> with <mafic> cumulates at depth. An illustrative reconstruction for hot ambient <Archean> <mantle> gives about 25–35 km of <oceanic crust>, rather than a universal fixed thickness. Its primary liquids are more magnesium-rich than typical modern primary ridge liquids; their crystallization produces more <olivine>- and <pyroxene>-rich <cumulates>. The extracted liquid and its crystal products must be distinguished: the entire final <oceanic crust> need not be a homogeneous <komatiite> layer.<Thermal history of the Earth and its petrological expression by Claude Herzberg and others>{ref}

The residual <peridotite> loses more melt components, becoming more refractory and commonly <harzburgite>-rich. Thick buoyant <basaltic crust>, its later <eclogitization>, and the depleted underlying <mantle> can change the mechanics of <subduction>. A hotter <mantle> does not by itself establish how fast plates moved or whether a globally connected modern <plate tectonics> regime operated.

<Komatiites>, with very magnesium-rich primary liquids and high inferred eruption <temperatures>, provide evidence for hot melting, but may sample especially hot <mantle plumes>. Estimates of ambient <mantle> conditions also use less exceptional non-arc <basalts>. Variable source composition, <water> content, cooling, melt extraction and later alteration all limit the precision of a single inferred <Archean> thickness. The robust prediction is **a larger melting column and melt yield, thicker igneous <oceanic crust>, and a more depleted residue**.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**Cascadia subducts a young, warm plate and lacks the enormous plateau and crustal root of the central <Andes>; the central Andean <Nazca Plate> system also supports much deeper intraslab <earthquakes>.** These are regional comparisons: neither western margin is uniform along its length.

First, the <Juan de Fuca Plate> entering <Cascadia> is young, typically of order 10 million years old, because its <spreading ridge> lies close to the margin. Its <lithosphere> is comparatively thin and warm. Much of the <Nazca Plate> entering the central <Andes> is older and colder. A cold plate carries low <temperatures> farther into the <mantle>, sustaining deep intraslab <seismicity>; the warm Cascadia <slab> loses its brittle seismogenic character much shallower. This concerns <earthquakes> within the <slab>, not the size of <megathrust earthquakes>: **both margins can generate very large plate-interface events**. The official warm-slab synthesis treats <Cascadia> as an endmember.https://www.usgs.gov/publications/cascadia-subduction-zone-and-related-subduction-systems-seismic-structure-intraslab[USGS, 2002, warm-slab comparison and the 55 km deep Nisqually earthquake]{ref}

Second, the central <Andes> have undergone major sustained <crustal shortening> and <tectonic thickening>, building a high plateau and locally roughly 60–70 km thick <continental crust>. <Cascadia> has an active <volcanic arc> and mountains but no equivalent continuous central-Andean plateau and crustal root. The difference reflects the accumulated history of convergence, upper-plate deformation, <slab dip>, trench motion and mechanical coupling, not just the instantaneous existence of <subduction>. The <Andes> include flat-slab segments with gaps in arc volcanism as well as normally dipping segments.https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2015/1031/e/pdf/of2015-1031-E.pdf[USGS, Seismotectonics of South America, tectonic summary and depth sections]{ref}https://escweb.wr.usgs.gov/share/mooney/54seismicvelocitystruct.pdf[USGS-hosted crustal seismic synthesis, central-Andean crustal thickness]{ref}

A young warm incoming plate and a faster, colder incoming plate can therefore generate different <thermal structures>, <dehydration> patterns and deformation histories. Plate age alone is insufficient to predict the thickness of the overriding <continental crust>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**For a Seattle resident, the main consequences are <earthquake> shaking and disruption, strongly modified by local ground conditions, plus location-dependent <tsunami>, <landslide> and volcanic hazards.**

Three <earthquake> sources matter: the offshore <Cascadia> <megathrust>, deep events inside the <Juan de Fuca Plate>, and shallow upper-plate <faults>, including the <Seattle Fault>. Shaking can damage buildings, bridges, ports and utility networks, interrupting transport, electricity and water supply. Soft basin <sediments> can amplify or prolong shaking; saturated loose fill can undergo <soil liquefaction> and lateral spreading. Steep slopes can fail as <landslides>. The same regional event can therefore have very different consequences on bedrock and on artificial fill.<Pacific Northwest Hazards by United States Geological Survey>{ref}

Large <Cascadia> <tsunamis> chiefly threaten the outer Pacific coast. Seattle's inland waterways also have local <tsunami> hazards from crustal <faults> and underwater <landslides>; one should not equate a Seattle waterfront site with an open-ocean coastal site. Cascade eruptions can affect the region through <volcanic ash> and transport disruption. <Lahars> are strongly channelled by river valleys, so their exposure is location dependent rather than uniform across Seattle. These geological differences influence infrastructure design, land-use decisions and emergency planning.https://www.dnr.wa.gov/programs-and-services/geology/geologic-hazards/earthquakes-and-faults[Washington Geological Survey, earthquake and fault mechanisms]{ref}https://cmgds.marine.usgs.gov/data/geotech/pugetposter/geologic.html[USGS, Puget Sound deltas, historic liquefaction damage and the Seattle Fault]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**In the interpretation of <Christopher Kincaid> and colleagues, <slab rollback> deforms and splits a <mantle plume>'s thermal anomaly: one branch is swept toward the trench beneath the <High Lava Plains>, whereas the <Snake River Plain> records the overriding plate moving over a deformed plume conduit.** Two opposite age progressions need not represent two stationary hotspots.

In the supplied map, ages decrease toward <Yellowstone> in the northeast and toward <Newberry Volcano> in the northwest. Southwestward <North American Plate> motion leaves older <Snake River Plain> volcanism to the southwest of the younger source expression. Meanwhile, rollback-driven, three-dimensional <mantle flow>, including <toroidal mantle flow> around the <slab>, carries a passive thermal anomaly trenchward beneath the <High Lava Plains>. The associated migrating heating and melting front produces the opposite younging toward <Newberry Volcano>.

The original experiment used a viscous syrup <mantle> analogue, a kinematically moved rigid <slab>, and an injected buoyant thermal plume. The result is **advection of deformed plume heat by subduction-driven flow**, rather than simple radial spreading of an intact plume head under a stationary plate. The mapped systematic progression principally concerns <rhyolitic> volcanism; <basaltic> eruptions are more diffuse in time. This laboratory result establishes a physically plausible explanation, not a unique observational proof of the deep source geometry.<Bifurcation of the Yellowstone plume driven by subduction-induced mantle flow by Christopher Kincaid and others>[2013, Figures 2–3, interpretation of the passive thermal anomaly and deformed conduit]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**Left: ordinary frictional strengthening with depth followed by thermally activated <material creep>. Right: a very weak shallow faulted layer above a narrow strong region near 10 km, followed by essentially the same weakening <material creep> branch.** These are the shapes actually plotted in Figure 2. The right curve is not a strong upper crust over a uniformly weak lower crust; neither diagram displays a second mantle-strength peak.

For the left curve, increasing overburden increases the effective normal load on <faults>. A schematic <Coulomb friction> law is
$$
\tau_f=C+\mu(\sigma_n-P_f),
$$
where $C$ is cohesion, $\mu$ the <coefficient of friction>, $\sigma_n$ the compressive normal <stress> and $P_f$ the <pore pressure>. With ordinary rock friction and approximately <hydrostatic pressure>, $\sigma_n-P_f$ increases roughly linearly with depth. The stress needed for <frictional sliding> therefore increases, as in the straight shallow branch. <Byerlee's law> summarizes the relatively high friction found in many laboratory <rocks>.<Friction of rocks by James Byerlee>{ref}

At higher <temperature>, <dislocation creep> or another ductile mechanism accommodates the imposed deformation at lower <stress>. For example,
$$
\dot\epsilon=A\sigma^n\exp\!\left(-\frac{Q}{RT}\right),\qquad
\sigma_d=\left(\frac{\dot\epsilon}{A}\right)^{1/n}\exp\!\left(\frac{Q}{nRT}\right).
$$
Here $\dot\epsilon$ is <strain rate>, $Q$ an <activation energy>, $R$ the <molar gas constant> and $T$ absolute <temperature>. At fixed <strain rate>, increasing $T$ lowers the required <stress>. The operative envelope is approximately $\min(\sigma_f,\sigma_d)$, expressed in a consistent stress convention; the peak marks the <brittle-ductile transition>. This accounts for the left curve's peak near 100 MPa and its decline toward small strength by about 20 km.

For the right curve, normal high-friction sliding with depth-independent fluid-pressure ratio cannot explain the nearly vertical weak shallow branch. Plausible weakening includes high <pore pressure> tracking the overburden, low-friction <fault gouge>, or a connected network of pre-existing weak <faults>. Roughly constant small $\sigma_n-P_f$ yields roughly constant small <frictional strength>. The abrupt increase near the base of this layer requires a change of conditions: for example, the weak fluid-rich fault network terminates or becomes discontinuous, leaving stronger intact or less altered <rock> below. A change in <mineralogy> or deformation localization can also give a contrast. **Simply saying “there are fluids” does not explain the return to high strength.** Below the strong region, thermal <material creep> again lowers the strength.

The figure alone does not identify the weakening mechanism or a particular lithological boundary. In particular, a 10 km strength maximum is not evidence for a 10 km deep <Mohorovičić discontinuity>. The strong-fault/weak-fault evidence and the distinction between fault strength and surrounding crustal strength are discussed in <Stress orientations at intermediate angles to the San Andreas Fault, California by Jeanne L. Hardebeck and Andrew J. Michael>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Support for the left curve comes from high laboratory friction and substantial in situ <differential stress>; support for the right curve comes from low inferred fault traction, weak fault materials or high <pore pressure>.** No single observation directly measures the whole plotted envelope.

For the **left, stronger shallow-crust model**:

* Many friction experiments on ordinary <rocks> give a <coefficient of friction> around 0.6–0.85. Together with approximately <hydrostatic pressure>, these values predict increasing <frictional strength> with depth.
* Deep-borehole measurements of <stress> and <pore pressure> in some continental settings indicate a crust close to frictional failure at appreciable <differential stress>. Jointly with appropriate <fault> orientations, these support a strong frictional upper <crust>.

For the **right, weak shallow-layer model**:

* On well-studied plate-boundary <faults>, low resolved <shear stress> inferred from the orientation of principal <stresses> relative to the active <fault plane> can support low effective traction.
* The absence of the expected localized long-term <frictional heating> anomaly can support weak sliding. Directly measured high <pore pressure> or very low-friction <fault gouge> would give a physical explanation. The heat-flow and stress evidence is particularly developed for the <San Andreas Fault>; it is an analogue argument, not an observation that the entire Cascadia upper <crust> follows that curve.<Stress orientations at intermediate angles to the San Andreas Fault, California by Jeanne L. Hardebeck and Andrew J. Michael>[2004, introduction and discussion of strong and weak fault models]{ref}

**To discriminate, measure the strength-controlling quantities through depth and across both faults and their surroundings.** Obtain borehole <stress>, <pore pressure> and <temperature> profiles where feasible; use <borehole breakouts> and hydraulic tests to constrain <effective stress>. Recover fault and host <rocks> and test their <friction> and <material creep> at relevant fluid conditions. The right curve specifically predicts an end to the shallow weakening near its sharp rise: investigate changes in <permeability of a porous medium>, alteration and <fault gouge> with depth, rather than testing only a surface sample.

Combine those measurements with <heat flux> transects, <magnetotelluric sounding> for conductive fluids, accurate <earthquake> depths and <focal mechanisms>, and <GNSS> or <InSAR> observations of deformation. The latter can help constrain the depth distribution of locking and <postseismic relaxation>, but neither locking nor <seismicity> alone gives absolute strength. <Earthquake stress drop> is a change in <stress>, not the pre-event <stress>. Low <heat flux> can also reflect heat transport or transient weakening; high electrical conductivity can have causes other than free <water>. A joint explanation of all these data is needed to choose between the models locally.

==== Solution

**A: currently inactive; B: active <left-lateral strike-slip faulting>; C: active <normal faulting>.**

A lies on the continuation of the offset outside the two ridge tips: it is the <fracture zone>, whose two sides are carried in the same overall plate direction. A bathymetric step can remain because the two sides have different ages and thermal histories, without continuing plate-boundary <strike-slip faulting>.

B is the black segment connecting the two white <spreading ridges>. Construct the <plate motion> from divergence away from those ridge axes. Between their tips, the upper side of B belongs to the plate west of the right-hand ridge and moves approximately west-northwest; the lower side belongs to the plate east of the left-hand ridge and moves approximately east-southeast. Looking across B from either side, the opposite side moves to the observer's left. B is therefore a **<left-lateral strike-slip faulting> on a <transform fault>**. The offset preserved outside the active segment must not be read as the sense of present slip.

**The grey arrows in the source image are pointers to <detachment faults>, not plate-motion vectors.** The original publication identifies white lines as <spreading ridges>, black lines as <transform faults>, and arrows as pointers to detachments. Its Figure 1b is the unlettered source of the exam's Figure 3.<How transform fault shear influences where detachment faults form near mid-ocean ridges by Jana C. Schierjott and others>[2023, Figure 1 caption]{ref}

C lies on the white <spreading ridge> extending down from the left-hand ridge-transform intersection. Relative motion there is extensional, so its tectonic <faults> have **normal slip**. <Dike> intrusion also accommodates extension at a <spreading ridge>; “normal slip” is the appropriate category among the four offered, not a claim that all spreading is achieved by a single <normal fault>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**Probably yes in connected fractures or transiently opened <pore space>, especially where a <transform fault> admits circulating <seawater>; a uniform water-filled porous layer several kilometres thick is not guaranteed.** <Porosity> and <permeability of a porous medium> generally decrease as cracks close or seal, while ongoing <faulting> can reopen pathways. Fluid can occur at high <pressure> and <temperature>, including a supercritical state; it need not resemble cold surface <water>.

Several complementary tests address presence and origin:

* **Geological:** recover fresh cores or dredged <fault rocks>. Hydrous alteration assemblages, veins, <serpentinite> replacing <peridotite>, and altered <gabbro> record <water-rock interaction>. Sealed <fluid inclusions> can preserve former fluids. These establish fluid access in the past; they do not by themselves demonstrate currently connected free <pore fluid>.
* **Geochemical:** analyse sampled fluids and <fluid inclusions> for salinity and chlorine, oxygen and hydrogen <isotope ratios>, and strontium or boron <isotope ratios> in alteration products. Compare with <seawater>, host <rocks>, and possible magmatic or metamorphic sources. A seawater-like input modified systematically by <water-rock interaction> supports deep circulation from the <ocean>. Exchange, <isotope fractionation>, mixing and phase separation mean that altered fluids need not retain the exact surface composition.
* **Geophysical:** combine <seismic tomography>, <compressional-to-shear wave speed ratio> and <seismic attenuation> with <magnetotelluric sounding> or controlled-source electrical measurements. A connected saline fluid network can reduce <electrical resistivity> and change elastic <wave speeds>. Sample <hydrothermal vents>, measure <heat flux> and, if drilling is possible, make direct <pore pressure> and permeability measurements.

**No one anomaly uniquely identifies water.** <Seismic velocity> can also reflect cracks, composition and <temperature>; <electrical conductivity> can reflect <melt> or conductive <minerals>. <Serpentinization> demonstrates incorporation of <water> into <minerals>, which must be distinguished from fluid currently occupying <pore space>. At an oceanic <transform fault>, an external <seawater> supply is the natural first hypothesis, but <isotope ratios> and chemical budgets can test contributions from deep fluids rather than presuming the source. A research example of coupled deformation and hydrothermal alteration is <Mass transfer and fluid flow during detachment faulting and development of an oceanic core complex, Atlantis Massif by Chiara Boschi and others>; a detachment system illustrates the methods without proving identical flow at every <transform fault>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Cooling makes the <oceanic lithosphere> denser and contracts it; <isostatic adjustment> therefore lowers the seafloor.** The cooling layer includes substantial <mantle>, not just the approximately fixed-thickness igneous <oceanic crust>.

New plate near a <spreading ridge> is hot and relatively buoyant. As it moves away, conductive cooling penetrates more deeply, and material formerly in the hot <asthenosphere> becomes part of the colder <thermal boundary layer>. To first order,
$$
\delta\rho(z,t)=\rho_m\alpha\,[T_m-T(z,t)],
$$
where $\alpha$ is the volumetric <thermal expansion coefficient>, $\rho_m$ a reference mantle <density>, $T_m$ the hot reference <temperature>, and $t$ plate age. A positive depth-integrated <density> anomaly makes the column negatively buoyant relative to the ridge column. <Isostasy> is restored by downward displacement and mantle redistribution; cold <rock> also occupies less <volume>.

At a fixed sufficiently deep datum, deepening the seafloor by $\Delta d$ removes an equivalent thickness of <rock> from the column and replaces it with less dense <seawater>. In the usual constant-property approximation this gives
$$
(\rho_m-\rho_w)\Delta d\simeq
\int_0^{z_c}\delta\rho(z,t)\,dz,
$$
where $\rho_w$ is <seawater> <density> and $z_c$ lies below the relevant thermal anomaly. This explains why contraction alone and water-loaded seafloor subsidence are not identical numerical quantities.

For a <half-space cooling model>,
$$
T(z,t)=T_s+(T_m-T_s)\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa t}}\right),
$$
so <integration> yields
$$
\boxed{\Delta d(t)=\frac{2\rho_m\alpha(T_m-T_s)}{\rho_m-\rho_w}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa t}{\pi}}.}
$$
Here $\kappa$ is <thermal diffusivity> and $z$ is measured downward from the surface of the thermal model. The leading <oceanic thermal subsidence> is proportional to $\sqrt t$. A finite <oceanic plate cooling model> limits this growth at old ages. <Sediment loading>, <dynamic topography>, anomalous <crust> thickness and hydrothermal heat transport can produce departures from the simple relation.<An analysis of the variation of ocean floor bathymetry and heat flow with age by Barry Parsons and John G. Sclater>{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**Choose a common horizontal <compensation depth> beneath the deepest cooling-induced <density> anomaly, within <mantle> that can relax pressure differences over the timescale considered.** In a normal oceanic plate model, this means below the old plate's thermal <lithosphere>, not at the base of the approximately 7 km thick <oceanic crust>.

The reason is that cooling changes <density> through both <crust> and <lithospheric mantle>. A datum at the <Mohorovičić discontinuity> would omit most of the changing column <mass>. A different datum for each age would also compare unlike columns. Once the common datum is sufficiently deep, extending it farther into laterally uniform <mantle> adds the same <mass> per unit area to each column and does not change the predicted relative subsidence.

There is no unique number fixed by the question. For an illustrative <oceanic plate cooling model> with a thermal thickness about 100–125 km, a common datum beneath that layer, of order 150–200 km below sea level, is a defensible choice provided the model has no substantial lateral <density> anomaly there. These are model choices, not measured universal boundaries. In a <half-space cooling model>, use the oldest age $t_{\max}$ to choose a practical cutoff: $z_c\gtrsim4\sqrt{\kappa t_{\max}}$ makes the remaining <temperature> anomaly less than about 0.5% of its surface contrast. For $\kappa=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $t_{\max}=100$ million years, this is about 225 km. A half-space model has no mathematically sharp finite thermal base.

Finally, equal column <mass> is the local, approximately constant-<gravitational acceleration> representation of equal <pressure> at the datum. A weak <asthenosphere> permits pressure relaxation through slow solid-state <material creep>; it need not be liquid. Short-wavelength loads, <lithospheric flexure> or <dynamic topography> can violate local column-by-column <isostasy>, requiring a more complete mechanical model.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**I would not assign the <lunar mare> an automatic terrestrial 100–125 km compensation level. For present-day mare basins, a local equal-column-mass model may have no physically valid <compensation depth>; first test whether <lithospheric flexure> supports the load.** For ancient thermally driven subsidence that did relax, choose a common datum beneath the cooling layer in deformable <lunar mantle> appropriate to that epoch.

The <Moon> is smaller than <Earth> and has lost a larger fraction of its original heat. Its cold, mechanically strong outer shell can be much thicker relative to its radius than the terrestrial oceanic <lithosphere>. <Lunar mare> <basalts> filled pre-existing impact basins: their loading, basin excavation, mantle uplift, cooling and the evolving strength of the lithosphere all matter. A thick <elastic lithosphere> can support lateral <stress> differences, so equal column <mass> need not hold even after a long time. The strong <gravity anomalies> of lunar <mascons>, measured by <GRAIL>, are evidence that the present mare-basin system is not everywhere locally compensated.<NASA's GRAIL Mission Solves Mystery of Moon's Surface Gravity by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>{ref}

For an explicitly assumed ancient relaxed thermal model, estimate its thermal penetration depth from <thermal diffusivity> and the elapsed cooling time, infer the contemporaneous mechanical lid from <gravity-topography admittance> or basin deformation, and place $z_c$ below that layer where lateral <pressure> anomalies relax. For example, a hypothetical several-hundred-kilometre-thick lid requires a deeper datum than that lid, not the shallow <crust-mantle boundary>. The paper supplies neither an epoch-specific thermal model nor a load wavelength, so a unique kilometre value is not determined. If the chosen datum is a substantial fraction of the lunar radius, spherical geometry and radial variation in <gravity> must replace the flat, constant-gravity column approximation.

**Does the Moon have an <asthenosphere>? Not a demonstrated shallow, globally connected Earth-like layer underlying mobile plates; but “no weak or partially molten mantle anywhere, ever” is too strong.** <Apollo lunar seismology> indicates a cold outer interior and more attenuating deep regions. Reanalyses support a deep low-velocity or partially molten region near the <lunar core>, while <lunar laser ranging> and tidal <Love numbers> constrain deep deformability and <tidal dissipation>. Such a deep weak region is sometimes called a lunar <asthenosphere>; it is not automatically the correct local compensation surface for a mare load.<Seismic detection of the layers of the lunar core by Renee C. Weber and colleagues>{ref}

The inferred melt fraction and distribution remain model dependent: <Dissipation at tidal and seismic frequencies in a melt-free Moon by Francis Nimmo and others> demonstrates that much of the dissipation can also be explained by hot solid-state <anelasticity>, although its model does not reproduce every frequency-dependent constraint. The <Moon> was hotter during mare emplacement and could relax loads differently then. **Choose the datum from the relevant thermal and mechanical history, or use a flexural model when local <isostasy> fails.**
