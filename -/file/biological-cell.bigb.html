= Cell
{synonym}

A <biological cell> is a membrane-bounded basic unit of living organization. In a cell-level experiment it can be an <experimental unit>, provided that treatment assignment and relevant interactions are handled at that level.

The shared architecture of cellular life combines a selective <cell membrane>, internal <cytoplasm>, inherited information in <DNA>, and <ribosomes> that assemble <proteins>. <Metabolism> supplies transformations of matter and <energy> needed for maintenance and growth. These components form a coupled system: a boundary retains useful <molecules>, <enzymes> organize <chemical reactions>, and inherited information supports rebuilding the machinery. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: prokaryotic cells].

A worked structural comparison: in a typical <prokaryote>, <DNA> occupies a region without a surrounding <cell nucleus>; in a typical nucleated <eukaryote>, much of the <DNA> lies inside a membrane-bounded <cell nucleus>. Both still have a <cell membrane>, <cytoplasm> and <ribosomes>. The extra boundary compartmentalizes processes; it does not create a different basic principle of living organization. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: eukaryotic cells].

Shared architecture describes cellular life and its developmental machinery, rather than requiring every specialized <biological cell> to retain every component. A mature mammalian <red blood cell>, for example, lacks a <cell nucleus>, although it develops from nucleated precursors. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/40-2-components-of-the-blood[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: components of blood]. This distinction makes cellular unity compatible with striking specialization.

= Endomembrane system
{parent=Biological cell}
{wiki}

The <endomembrane system> comprises functionally connected internal membranes such as the <endoplasmic reticulum>, <Golgi apparatus>, <endosomes> and <lysosomes>, together with their transport carriers. Connections through carrier traffic differ from direct continuity of the membrane sheets.

= Topology of membrane compartments
{parent=Endomembrane system}

Ordinary vesicle budding and fusion preserve which side of a membrane a protein domain faces: a cytoplasmic domain remains cytoplasmic, and a luminal domain remains luminal. Invagination can enclose previously external space without transporting its contents through a bilayer. The <endoplasmic reticulum> lumen is continuous with the space between the two <nuclear envelope> membranes; it is not the chromosome-containing nucleoplasm. Any proposed evolutionary remodelling must account for these distinct spaces and the membrane connections between them.

= Cell migration
{parent=Biological cell}

<Cell migration> is movement of a <biological cell> relative to its surroundings. In crawling cells, contact forces with a substrate can propel the cell, while <chemotaxis> can bias its direction in response to a chemical <gradient>.

= Tissue advection
{parent=Cell migration}

Displacement of <cells> with bulk tissue motion. Subtracting the surrounding flow is needed to distinguish autonomous crawling from <passive transport>.

= Contact guidance
{parent=Cell migration}

<Contact guidance> is a directional influence of physical structures, such as aligned fibres or grooves, on <cell migration>. It can organize trajectories without an imposed soluble <concentration gradient>.

= Haptotaxis
{parent=Cell migration}
{wiki}

<Haptotaxis> is directed <cell migration> along spatial variation of an immobilized adhesive or guidance cue. It differs from guidance by a soluble <chemoattractant> field.

= Durotaxis
{parent=Cell migration}
{wiki}

<Durotaxis> is biased <cell migration> associated with spatial variation in substrate stiffness. Distinguish a directional bias from changes in speed, attachment or survival on different substrates.

= Chemokinesis
{parent=Cell migration}
{wiki}

<Chemokinesis> is a change in motility associated with a chemical stimulus without a directional bias toward or away from its source. Measure speed and persistence separately from directional <chemotaxis>.

= Membrane bleb
{parent=Cell migration}

A <membrane bleb> is a rounded protrusion formed when the <cell membrane> locally detaches from or loses support by the <cell cortex>. Pressure expands the initially cortex-poor region; cortical reassembly can then stabilize or retract it.

= Lamellipodium
{parent=Cell migration}
{wiki}

A <lamellipodium> is a broad, thin, <actin>-rich protrusion at a cell edge. <Actin> polymerization pushes the <cell membrane>, but net advance also depends on mechanical coupling to the environment.

= Collective cell migration
{parent=Cell migration}

In <collective cell migration>, neighbouring <biological cells> move with coordinated trajectories while retaining interactions that influence their motion. Mechanical coupling, contact-dependent signals and soluble cues can each contribute. A coherent group need not consist of identically behaving cells: leaders and followers may perform different roles.

= Velocity correlation in cell migration
{parent=Collective cell migration}

A quantitative measure of coordinated <cell> velocities. A common external force can create correlation without proving direct <cell>-to-<cell> transmission of guidance.

= Contact inhibition of locomotion
{parent=Collective cell migration}

<Contact inhibition of locomotion> is a contact-triggered change of protrusions and movement direction. It is distinct from contact inhibition of proliferation. = Spectrin
{parent=Cytoskeleton}

= Spectrin unfolding by force spectroscopy
{parent=Spectrin}

A modular <protein> can release additional contour length when a folded domain crosses a mechanical unfolding barrier. At almost fixed end separation this lowers fractional extension and tension, producing a saw-tooth force record. Successive <worm-like chain> fits can share backbone <persistence length> while their contour lengths increase. Rupture force and hysteresis depend on loading history; peak force alone is not an equilibrium unfolding free energy.

= Spatially gated myosin attachment
{parent=Actomyosin}
{title2=$p_b=1-e^{-k_{on}\Delta x/v}$}

A prescribed-velocity motor can attach only over a narrow displacement window, remain attached over a protected interval, then detach with constant rate. Converting time to displacement gives a rising attachment <probability>, a plateau $p_b$, and an exponential tail with spatial length $v/k_{off}$. Independent successive-site encounters require detachment before the next window, not merely a large geometric site separation. Earlier attachments can be retained in a periodic availability correction.

= Constant-force spatially gated motor
{parent=Spatially gated myosin attachment}
{title2=$\langle f\rangle\simeq f_0(x_2/\Delta)(1-e^{-k_1x_1/u})$}

A prescribed-speed <molecular motor> model in which a head attaches only within a phase window of width $x_1$, stays bound to phase $x_2$, and then detaches at a constant rate before the next period $\Delta$. If the binding window and detachment tail are short relative to the bound plateau, the phase-average <force> is the plateau fraction multiplied by its attachment <probability> and bound-head <force> $f_0$.

= Linear-drag load curve for a spatially gated motor
{parent=Constant-force spatially gated motor}
{title2=$L=N\langle f\rangle-\gamma u$}

With positive opposing load magnitude $L$ and positive <viscous drag coefficient> $\gamma$, steady contraction requires $L=N\langle f\rangle-\gamma u$. A decreasing motor <force> curve and an increasing drag-magnitude line have a unique intersection for each load below the limiting stall <force>. Using $+\gamma u$ as the signed drag would instead make drag assist forward motion, reversing its physical role.

= Work of a spatially gated myosin head
{parent=Spatially gated myosin attachment}
{title2=$W=\kappa p_b[x_0^2/2-(v/k_{off})^2]$}

For attached-state <elastic energy> $U=\kappa(x-x_0)^2/2$ and a binding window much narrower than $x_0$, the expected assisting work before $x_0$ is $\kappa p_bx_0^2/2$. The detachment tail produces resisting work $-\kappa p_bv^2/k_{off}^2$. Mean <force> per head is $W/D$ for site spacing $D$. The detachment-time second moment, not the square of its mean, fixes the resisting term. These are idealized prescribed-velocity kinetics, not a complete muscle energy budget.

= Cell line
{parent=Biological cell}

= Cell culture passage accounting
{parent=Subculture}
{title2=$N_{\mathrm{seed}}=fN_{\mathrm{harvest}}$}

In an original constructed example, seed $300\,000$ <biological cells> in $6\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <cell culture medium>. Suppose the recoverable count becomes $1\,200\,000$ before the next <subculture>. Transfer a well-mixed quarter of that harvest into a new vessel and bring its final <volume (thermodynamics)> to $6\,\mathrm{mL}$. Assume complete recovery, no loss during transfer and no division during handling. The selected portion contains
$$
N_{\mathrm{seed}}=\frac14(1\,200\,000)=300\,000,\qquad c_{\mathrm{seed}}=\frac{300\,000}{6\,\mathrm{mL}}=50\,000\,\mathrm{mL}^{-1}.
$$
The other $900\,000$ <biological cells> remain outside the tracked vessel. The lower count after transfer is selection of a portion, not evidence of death. Adding fresh <cell culture medium> lowers the <number density> further without changing the selected count.

If the next interval again changes $300\,000$ to $1\,200\,000$, the two growth intervals contribute $2+2=4$ net <cell line population doublings>. At the second harvest there has been just one intervening passage. Another quarter-transfer would add another passage without adding a growth interval. = Cell line population doublings
{parent=Cell line}
{title2=$d=\log_2(N_1/N_0)$}

For positive recoverable counts $N_0,N_1$ bracketing one growth interval, define its net <cell line population doublings> by
$$
d=\log_2\!\left(\frac{N_1}{N_0}\right)=\frac{\ln(N_1/N_0)}{\ln2}.
$$
The formula counts how many factors of two produce the observed net factor. For exactly two divisions per original <biological cell>, with all daughters recovered, $N_1=2^2N_0$, giving $d=2$. Death or loss of <biological cells> makes this a net estimate of division.<Animal Cell Culture Guide by ATCC>[Passage number and population doubling level, PDL equation and loss caveat]{ref}

Under constant-rate <exponential growth>, $N(t)=N_0e^{rt}$, a growth interval of duration $\Delta t$ gives
$$
r=\frac{\ln(N_1/N_0)}{\Delta t},\qquad t_2=\frac{\ln2}{r}=\frac{\Delta t}{d}.
$$
For the constructed fourfold increase over $48\,\mathrm{h}$, $d=2$ and the model <doubling time> is $24\,\mathrm{h}$. The proof follows by taking the <natural logarithm> and solving $e^{rt_2}=2$. This is a fitted property of that interval and model: it is not a universal <HeLa> <doubling time>, nor the interval between two divisions of each individual <biological cell>. Across successive growth intervals, add their $d$ values; keep transfer losses separate as in <cell culture passage accounting>.

{parent=Cell counting}
{wiki}

A <hemocytometer> has a calibrated grid and depth. ATCC describes $1\,\mathrm{mm}^2$ squares $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep, each sampling $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mL}$.<Animal Cell Culture Guide by ATCC>[Cell counting, chamber geometry; Figure 2]{ref}

$$

$$
Hence the undiluted preparation has dye-excluding <number density> $2(40)/10^{-4}=800\,000\,\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$ and total <number density> $2(45)/10^{-4}=900\,000\,\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$. = Slow and fast limits of a delayed stem-cell model
{parent=Stem cell}
{title2=$u'=g(u(t-1))-g(u)+\epsilon[\mu g(u(t-1))-u]$}

For resting-cell recruitment $g(u)=bu/(1+u^n)$, a positive constant <equilibrium> exists precisely when $\mu b>1$, with $u_*=(\mu b-1)^{1/n}$. On slow time $T=\epsilon t$, expand the delayed value as $u(T-\epsilon)=u-\epsilon u_T+O(\epsilon^2)$. Substitution gives $(1+g'(u))u_T=\mu g(u)-u$ to leading order. This reduction requires smooth slow motion and $1+g'(u)$ bounded away from zero.

On the original fast time, only the explicit order-$\epsilon$ terms are dropped, leaving $u'=g(u(t-1))-g(u(t))$. The unit delay remains order one and cannot be Taylor-expanded on that scale. For the biological two-compartment derivation, a fixed cell-cycle time $\tau$ and proliferating death hazard $\gamma_d$ give survival $e^{-\gamma_d\tau}$ and daughter-cell gain twice the surviving exit rate. Scaling gives $b=\beta_0\tau$, $\epsilon=\delta\tau$ and $1+\epsilon\mu=2e^{-\gamma_d\tau}$. Thus small resting-cell loss over one cycle creates the slow-fast structure.

= Turgor pressure
{parent=Cell wall}
{wiki}
{title2=$P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}$}

<Turgor pressure> is the internal <hydrostatic pressure> above the external <pressure> in a <biological cell>. When <water> enters a walled <biological cell> by <osmosis>, expansion loads the <cell wall>. Rising <pressure> can oppose further net entry while the <cell membrane> retains its <selective permeability>. Mechanical support therefore does not require the <cell wall> to block <water>.

As an original mechanical check, idealize a small flat patch of <cell wall> of <surface area> $A$ under uniform <turgor pressure> $\Delta P=P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}$. Its outward <force> is $F=\Delta P A$. Doubling $A$ at fixed <pressure> doubles the load; equal internal and external <pressures> give zero net pressure load. This is a force balance, not a measured wall strength or a complete model of a curved, growing <biological cell>. It says nothing about which <solutes> cross the <cell membrane>.

= Lysozyme wall-removal experiment
{parent=Protoplast}

The 1953 paper <The isolation of protoplasts from Bacillus megaterium by controlled treatment with lysozyme by Claes Weibull> (pp. 688–690, Figures 2–3) tested <Bacillus megaterium> strain KM. Apparatus included a Klett-Summerson colorimeter with a red filter and a Spencer no. 8 <phase-contrast microscope>. <Lysozyme> in phosphate buffer produced empty residual bodies; adding $0.2\,\mathrm{M}$ <sucrose> preserved granular spheres. Diluting their surroundings caused rapid <osmotic lysis>.

The same study compared untreated, boiled, <lysozyme>-exposed and <sucrose>-protected samples. Filled, stoppered tubes reduced damage from air exposure. After <centrifugation>, a Beckman DU spectrophotometer measured ultraviolet <wave absorption> of the separated liquid. Protected samples resembled untreated controls in releasing little ultraviolet-absorbing material; boiled and unprotected samples released much more. Later-growth cultures reduced spontaneous breakdown as a confounder.<The isolation of protoplasts from Bacillus megaterium by controlled treatment with lysozyme by Claes Weibull>[pp. 688–689, Materials and Methods; pp. 692–694, release comparison and Figure 6]{ref}

Together, the shape and release readouts support removal of mechanical protection while a permeability barrier persists. They do not establish unchanged <selective permeability> to every <solute>, long-term viability, or universal susceptibility.

An instructive extension of this experimental logic is a matched comparison: <lysozyme> present or absent, crossed with osmotic support present or absent. Keep starting material, buffer, observation interval and handling comparable; include a blank to distinguish optical effects of reagents from changes in the <biological cells>. Then dilute already formed <protoplasts> as a separate intervention. This separates access and wall removal from failure after removal. These are proposed controls for interpreting the mechanism, not additional reported results from the historical paper. A fall in optical signal alone cannot establish rupture: shape changes also alter <scattering of light>.

= Isolated mitochondria respiration experiment
{parent=Mitochondria}

<Respiratory control> can be investigated outside an intact <biological cell> by supplying isolated <mitochondria> with <substrates> and <ADP>. This makes the boundary between <oxygen> consumption and <ATP> formation experimentally accessible. The historical <mitochondrial oxygen electrode assay> describes the 1955 experiment; the following authored protocol adds modern preparation and inhibitor controls.

In Functional Assessment of Isolated Mitochondria In Vitro (2009), <Ian R. Lanza> and <K. Sreekumaran Nair> describe human skeletal-muscle biopsy mincing, cold <protease> treatment, homogenization and <differential centrifugation>. A low-speed spin removes coarse material; subsequent spins collect and wash <mitochondria>. A stirred, closed $2\,\mathrm{mL}$ Oxygraph-2k chamber at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ uses a calibrated polarographic <oxygen> <electrode>. Its signal gives dissolved <oxygen> <concentration>; the negative time <derivative> gives consumption. Instrument corrections include electrode consumption and <oxygen> leakage.<Methods in Enzymology, volume 457: Mitochondrial Function, Part B/Functional Assessment of Isolated Mitochondria In Vitro>[Methods in Enzymology, volume 457: Mitochondrial Function, Part B; Tissue homogenization; Separation of mitochondria by differential centrifugation; Mitochondrial respiration, Overview; An oxygraph protocol for mitochondrial assessment, steps 1–2]{ref}

Their additions include <glutamate> plus <malate>, an <ADP> pulse, then saturating <ADP>. Added <cytochrome c> tests outer-membrane damage: stimulation suggests loss of endogenous <cytochrome c>. Later <oligomycin> inhibits <ATP synthase>; <FCCP> produces <respiratory uncoupling>; <antimycin A> inhibits <respiratory complex III> to estimate residual background. Figure 6 includes intervening <succinate dehydrogenase> and <respiratory complex I> interventions; the modeled traces below simplify that sequence. Isolation can damage or select <mitochondria>, and saturating assay conditions differ from those in living <biological tissue>.<Methods in Enzymology, volume 457: Mitochondrial Function, Part B/Functional Assessment of Isolated Mitochondria In Vitro>[Methods in Enzymology, volume 457: Mitochondrial Function, Part B; Figure 3; An oxygraph protocol for mitochondrial assessment, steps 3–11 and Figure 6; Summary, nonphysiological assay conditions]{ref}

The control logic matters more than a single steep slope. If <ADP> accelerates consumption and a finite pulse is followed by slowing, demand is affecting respiratory flux. If <oligomycin> slows consumption, but subsequent <FCCP> accelerates it, the <electron transport chain> can still turn over while <ATP synthase> remains inhibited. That contrast separates restricted coupled turnover from a completely inactive chain. Failure of <FCCP> to restore a rate is less specific: <substrate> limitation, damage, low <oxygen> or excessive uncoupler can also restrict respiration.

Neither the <cytochrome c> test alone nor a large <respiratory control ratio> establishes every aspect of mitochondrial integrity. Likewise, <oxygen> consumption is not a direct <ATP> assay: <respiratory uncoupling> provides a concrete counterexample. $$

$$
With a stipulated mitochondrial <protein> mass of $0.20\,\mathrm{mg}$, that is $250\,\mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}\,mg^{-1}}$. Using a denominator twice as large would halve this reported rate without changing the raw <oxygen> trace. Comparisons therefore need both loading and the chosen denominator.

Across the whole seven-minute example, consumption is $V(c(0)-c(7))=198\,\mathrm{nmol}$, also the sum of the seven interval consumptions. = Golgi cargo processing
{c}
{parent=Golgi apparatus}

<Golgi cargo processing> includes <enzyme>-mediated remodeling of attached <oligosaccharides>. <Glycosylation> can involve addition of <sugars>, while processing can also remove them. Different <Golgi apparatus> regions have different processing <enzymes>; their <substrate> requirements constrain the order of possible <chemical reactions>.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Transport from the ER through the Golgi Apparatus>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; Oligosaccharide Chains Are Processed in the Golgi Apparatus; The Golgi Cisternae Are Organized as a Series of Processing Compartments, Figure 13-28]{ref}

An original model makes the importance of ordering explicit. Write a toy <carbohydrate> chain as $XA$, read with its terminal symbol on the right. Define a trimming <function> $T$ that removes a terminal $A$, and an extension <function> $E$ that adds $B$ only when the terminal symbol is $X$. In every other case the corresponding <function> leaves the chain unchanged. Then
$$
XA\xrightarrow{T}X\xrightarrow{E}XB,\qquad XA\xrightarrow{E}XA\xrightarrow{T}X.
$$
In particular, $E(T(XA))=XB$ but $T(E(XA))=X$: the two <functions> do not commute. Each result follows by inspecting the terminal symbol before applying the stipulated rule. These symbols are artificial labels, rather than a natural <oligosaccharide> sequence or measured <enzyme> pathway. The example explains why “two processing steps occurred” does not determine the product unless their order and <substrate> conditions are also specified. In the <Golgi apparatus>, spatial organization can help arrange successive opportunities for processing.

= Lysosomal enzyme latency
{parent=Lysosome}

<Lysosomal enzyme latency> is the difference between the <enzyme> activity accessible to an added <substrate> before disruption of the surrounding membrane and the activity revealed after disruption. Low accessible activity can reflect separation of <enzyme> from <substrate>, rather than absence of <enzyme>. \Image[lysosome-latency-disruption-assay.png]
{title=<Lysosomal enzyme latency>: matched assay conditions and disruption controls}
{description=Original assay-logic diagram and explicitly chosen activity example. The values $10$, $100$ and $25$ are arbitrary blank-corrected rate units, not historical observations. The soluble-<enzyme> comparison is an additional control proposed for the schematic, not an asserted measurement from the <rat-liver acid-phosphatase latency experiment>.}

Let $F$ be the blank-corrected initial rate measured with a membrane-preserving preparation and $T>0$ the rate after a treatment that exposes all assayable <enzyme>. Use matched amounts of starting material, <pH>, <substrate> and measurement time. Define the apparent latent fraction by
$$
\ell=1-\frac{F}{T}.
$$
This definition requires more than a large treated signal. Disruption must preserve <enzyme> activity, and the treatment must not independently alter the readout. A soluble-<enzyme> comparison with and without treatment helps test direct activation or inhibition; an appropriate blank tests signal unrelated to <enzyme>-catalysed <hydrolysis>. These are controls for interpreting the constructed assay, rather than additional historical observations.

For a deliberately chosen example, take $F=10$ and $T=100$ arbitrary rate units. Then $\ell=0.90$: ninety percent of the assayable activity was initially inaccessible. After hypothetical partial damage, $F=25$ with unchanged $T=100$ gives $\ell=0.75$. The preparation has lost latency without gaining total assayable activity. These percentages describe activity accessibility; they do not count intact <lysosomes>. Unequal <enzyme> contents, partial leakage and mixed particle populations prevent that identification. If $F>T$, the formula is negative, flagging failure of this simple accessibility interpretation rather than a negative population of hidden <enzymes>.

To check the logic, divide the same uniformly mixed preparation into two equal portions. If dilution halves both measured rates, $(F/2)/(T/2)=F/T$, so $\ell$ is unchanged. In contrast, comparing an undiluted untreated portion with a diluted treated portion changes the ratio artificially. Normalization to the same amount of original material is therefore part of the definition, not an optional numerical correction.

A <nuclear pore complex> is an assembly of <proteins> spanning the <nuclear envelope> and regulating passage between the <cell nucleus> and <cytosol>. It is a selective gateway, not simply an unregulated hole: <messenger RNA> export and import of nuclear <proteins> require appropriate transport machinery. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26932/[Alberts et al.: nuclear pore complexes].

The <nuclear pore complex> occupies a junction of both <nuclear envelope> membranes. Its <protein> scaffold surrounds an aqueous passage connecting <cytosol> to the <cell nucleus>, rather than the intermembrane space. <Diffusion> permits small soluble cargo to pass; selective receptors transport other cargo. <Molecular mass> alone is not a universal cutoff.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others>[Nuclear Pore Complexes Perforate the Nuclear Envelope, Figures 12-10–12-11; Nuclear Localization Signals Direct Nuclear Proteins to the Nucleus]{ref}

= NLS
{c}

A <nuclear localization sequence> is an ordered stretch of <amino acid residues> that can direct <protein> import into a <cell nucleus>. In the classical pathway, <importin> α recognizes the <nuclear localization sequence>, and <importin> β mediates passage through the <nuclear pore complex>. Other recognition pathways exist. An exposed <nuclear localization sequence> is a targeting feature, not a guarantee of import under every condition.<The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper>[Selective Transport of Proteins to and from the Nucleus, Figure 8.9; Regulation of Nuclear Protein Import]{ref}

<RNA> export is a different cargo pathway: <RNA> travels associated with <proteins>, as a <ribonucleoprotein>, rather than being imported by its own <nuclear localization sequence>. Export of <messenger RNA> connects nuclear <transcription> to cytoplasmic <biological translation>.<The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper>[Transport of RNAs, opening paragraphs]{ref}

= Nuclear pore localization experiment
{parent=Nuclear pore complex}

In a 1982 experiment, purified <nucleoplasmin> was labeled with radioactive <iodine> and cleaved by <proteolysis>. <Microinjection> delivered cargo into <Xenopus laevis> <oocyte> <cytoplasm>. Manually separated <cell nuclei> and <cytoplasm> were compared by <gel electrophoresis> and <autoradiography>; section <autoradiography> checked internal localization. Tail-bearing cargo entered the <cell nucleus>; tailless cores did not. Direct nuclear <microinjection> retained the cores: the tails mattered for entry, but the assay did not identify a receptor.<A polypeptide domain that specifies migration of nucleoplasmin into the nucleus by Colin Dingwall and others>[Cell 30, 449–458: Figures 2–3, 5 and 7; The Trypsin-resistant Core Remains in the Nucleus after Nuclear Injection]{ref}

= Nuclear pore transport balance
{parent=Nuclear pore complex}
{title2=$R=C_n/C_c$}

Nuclear accumulation should compare <concentrations>, not just total cargo. In an original constructed example, a <cell nucleus> occupies one <volume (thermodynamics)> unit and the accessible <cytosol> occupies nine. Of $130$ tracer units, $40$ are nuclear and $90$ are cytosolic. With $n_n,n_c$ the amounts and $V_n,V_c$ the accessible <volume (thermodynamics)>, the <concentration> ratio is
$$
C_n=\frac{n_n}{V_n}=40,\qquad C_c=\frac{n_c}{V_c}=10,\qquad R=\frac{C_n}{C_c}=4.
$$
Only $40/130=4/13\approx30.8\%$ of the cargo is nuclear, yet nuclear <concentration> is four times cytosolic <concentration>. A majority outside the <cell nucleus> does not rule out nuclear enrichment.

For comparison, freely exchanging nonbinding cargo at equal <concentrations> would have $n_n=13,n_c=117$: the nuclear fraction would equal the nuclear fraction of accessible <volume (thermodynamics)>, $1/10$. These are constructed amounts, not measurements from the <nuclear pore localization experiment>. Binding, unequal recovery or cargo degradation would require additional accounting before interpreting an assay.

A minimal two-compartment <ordinary differential equation> makes the distinction precise. Assume fixed accessible <volume (thermodynamics)>, no synthesis or degradation, and effective first-order exchange rates $a,b>0$:
$$
\frac{dn_n}{dt}=a n_c-b n_n,\qquad n_c=N-n_n,\qquad \frac{dn_c}{dt}=-\frac{dn_n}{dt}.
$$
Total cargo $N=n_n+n_c$ is conserved. Setting the <derivative> to zero gives
$$
n_n^*=\frac{aN}{a+b},\qquad \frac{C_n^*}{C_c^*}=\frac{aV_c}{bV_n}.
$$
For symmetric passive exchange with conductance $P$, $a=P/V_c$ and $b=P/V_n$, so the <concentration> ratio is one even when the amounts differ. Choosing instead $a=4/9$ and $b=1$ in reciprocal model-time units gives $n_n^*=40$ for $N=130,V_n=1,V_c=9$. The exact <ordinary differential equation> solution is
$$
n_n(t)=n_n^*+\bigl(n_n(0)-n_n^*\bigr)e^{-(a+b)t}.
$$
Substitution verifies the solution, and $a+b>0$ makes departures decay. This effective model can describe an asymmetric exchange balance, but does not by itself identify a <nuclear localization sequence>, transport receptor or source of <energy>.

The usefulness of the <nuclear pore complex> is precisely this combination of separation and exchange: a <cell nucleus> can maintain a distinctive <protein> population while receiving new cargo and exporting <RNA>. The <nuclear pore localization experiment> asks what feature controls entry; the <nuclear pore transport balance> asks what an accumulation readout actually means.

= Cell nucleolus
{synonym}

The <cell nucleolus> is a region inside a <cell nucleus> without a surrounding membrane, where much <ribosomal RNA> is made and processed and <ribosome> subunits begin to assemble with <proteins>. The subunits are exported to the <cytoplasm>; the <cell nucleolus> is not itself a translating <ribosome>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 4.3: nucleolus and ribosomal subunits].

= Ribosome
{parent=Biological cell}
{wiki}

A <ribosome> is the molecular machinery for <biological translation>: it reads <messenger RNA> and joins <amino acids> into a <polypeptide> that can become a <protein>. Both <prokaryotes> and <eukaryotes> have <ribosomes>. A <ribosome> consists of <ribosomal RNA> and <proteins> organized into small and large subunits, with no enclosing membrane. <Ribosomal RNA> contributes to both structure and the <active site> that forms <peptide bonds>; a <ribosome> is therefore not merely a collection of <proteins>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 4.3: ribosome composition] and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10937990/[Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000)].

\Image[ribosome-translation-schematic.png]
{title=Nuclear <transcription>, <RNA> export and <ribosome>-mediated <biological translation>}
{description=Original schematic of a nuclear <protein>-coding <gene> in a nucleated <eukaryotic> <biological cell>. The upper route tracks <DNA> to <RNA> by <transcription>, <RNA> export, then <biological translation>. The lower example reads three <messenger RNA> <codons> from $5'$ to $3'$ and shows a growing <polypeptide> segment from its <N-terminus> toward its <C-terminus>. Shapes and distances are illustrative; processing, flanking signals, additional machinery and termination are omitted.}

For a computed decoding example, choose the <reading frame> starting at the first displayed <nucleotide> of $5'\text{-}\mathrm{AUG\ GCU\ GAA}\text{-}3'$. Under the standard <genetic code>, AUG specifies <methionine> (Met), GCU specifies <alanine> (Ala), and GAA specifies a <glutamic acid residue> (Glu) in the growing <polypeptide>. Nine <nucleotides> divide into three <codons>, giving the ordered three-<amino acid residue> segment Met–Ala–Glu. The <ribosome> reads the <messenger RNA> $5'\to3'$, and the chain grows from its <N-terminus> toward its <C-terminus>. If synthesis begins at this AUG in an appropriate <eukaryotic> initiation context, these are the first three <amino acid residues> before any subsequent processing. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-1-the-genetic-code[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 15.1: standard codon assignments].

For this three-<amino acid residue> segment, joining the three delivered <amino acids> requires two <peptide bonds>, one between Met and Ala and another between Ala and Glu. There is no <stop codon> in the displayed <nucleotide sequence>, so this is a growing segment rather than a complete released <protein>. An isolated nine-<nucleotide> <RNA> is not claimed to supply all signals needed for initiation. This small calculation separates three jobs: the <messenger RNA> specifies order, <transfer RNA> delivers the <amino acids>, and the <ribosome> catalyzes their joining. Each new <amino acid residue> is added at the <C-terminus>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/15-5-ribosomes-and-protein-synthesis[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 15.5: initiation and chain elongation].

The beauty of a <ribosome> is its reusable chemistry: the same machinery can assemble many different <proteins> by following different <messenger RNA> templates. The <genetic code> connects an alphabet of <nucleotides> to an alphabet of <amino acids>, while <transfer RNA> links recognition of the message to delivery of building material. Changing the message can therefore change the product without building a different <ribosome> for every <protein>.

In 1955 George E. Palade described small particles in the <cytoplasm>, both free and associated with membranes, in https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2223592/[A small particulate component of the cytoplasm, Journal of Biophysical and Biochemical Cytology 1, 59–68]. The paper documents the structural observations, rather than the full modern decoding mechanism. Complementary biochemical work by Hoagland, Stephenson, Scott, Hecht and Zamecnik described a soluble <RNA> intermediate in <protein> synthesis in 1958, helping establish the role now assigned to <transfer RNA>; see https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13538965/[A soluble ribonucleic acid intermediate in protein synthesis, Journal of Biological Chemistry 231, 241–257]. Together, structural observations and biochemical experiments connected visible cellular machinery with its working parts.

= Ribosome stalling
{parent=Ribosome}

Prolonged delay of a translating <ribosome> at a particular position. Stalls can reduce completed-chain output and cause <ribosome collisions>.

= Peptidyl transfer
{parent=Ribosome}

Formation of a <peptide> bond by transfer of the growing <peptide> to an incoming <aminoacyl-tRNA>. <substrate> sequence can affect the rate independently of decoding accuracy.

= Ribosomal protein
{parent=Ribosome}

A <protein> component of a <ribosome>. The machinery can combine products of different <genomes>, especially in an <organelle>.

= Polysome
{parent=Ribosome}

A group of <ribosomes> translating one <messenger RNA>. Occupancy can increase through faster initiation or slower elongation, so the amount of <polysomes> is not by itself a synthesis-rate measurement.

= Ribosomal subunit
{parent=Ribosome}

One of the major assemblies forming a <ribosome>. Small-subunit scanning and reinitiation can persist after release of the large subunit at a short <upstream open reading frame>.

= Ribosome collision
{parent=Ribosome}

Contact between successive <ribosomes> when a following one reaches a stalled or slowly moving predecessor. Collisions can supply regulatory and quality-control signals rather than simply indicating high productive output.

= Gliding bacterium
{parent=Biological cell}

A <gliding bacterium> moves along a surface without swimming through the bulk fluid. In a thin-fluid-film model, its relative motion dissipates energy through <Couette flow>, although the actuation mechanism can involve additional dissipation.

= Neutrophil
{parent=Biological cell}

A <neutrophil> is an immune <biological cell>. Its adhesive rolling on a surface involves contact mechanics, fluid stresses, and binding and unbinding of adhesive bonds.

= Red blood cell
{parent=Biological cell}

A red blood cell is a specialized <biological cell> that transports <oxygen> in blood. Its deformability lets it pass through vessels narrower than its unstressed size. A compliant-body <lubrication> model isolates the coupling between its deformation, the surrounding viscous film and a driving <pressure> difference; it is an idealization rather than a complete membrane or cytoplasm model.

A <heterokaryon> contains genetically different <cell nuclei> in a shared <cytoplasm>; <cell fusion> can create one.

= Cell fusion

<Cell fusion> combines <biological cells> into a shared <cytoplasm> bounded by a common <cell membrane>; it need not immediately fuse their <cell nuclei>.

= Cell cortex
{parent=Biological cell}

The <cell cortex> is a peripheral layer of <cytoplasm> beneath the <cell membrane>, containing the <actomyosin ring> during typical <animal> <cytokinesis>.

= Cell lineage
{parent=Biological cell}

A <cell lineage> follows descent of <biological cells> through successive divisions. <DNA replication> can transmit a <mutation> to descendant <biological cells>.

= Lineage restriction
{parent=Cell lineage}

A narrowing of the descendant <cell types> available to a progenitor. It should be distinguished from failure to sample rare outputs and from changing <developmental competence>.

= Cell of origin
{parent=Cell lineage}

The normal or precursor cellular lineage in which a tumour-initiating evolutionary process occurs. It can differ from the phenotype of the established malignant cells.

= Lineage tracing
{parent=Cell lineage}

Following descendants of identified cells through development. Marker expression at one instant is a state measurement rather than a complete lineage history.

= Germline
{parent=Cell lineage}

The lineage that gives rise to reproductive cells transmitting genetic information between generations, distinguished from somatic body-cell lineages.

= Germline genome defence
{parent=Germline}

Mechanisms protecting the inherited <genome> from <transposable elements> and other threats. Suppression of transposon <RNA>, <chromatin> repression and removal of damaged <cells> are different protective strategies.

= Genetic lineage tracing
{parent=Cell lineage}

Tracking descendants through an inherited genetic label or sequence change. Labelling specificity, timing and independent activation determine what ancestry can be inferred.

= Lineage-tracing dropout
{parent=Genetic lineage tracing}

Failure to recover a lineage label from a sampled descendant. No recovered label is therefore not equivalent to proof of absent ancestry.

= CRISPR lineage recording
{parent=Genetic lineage tracing}
{c}

Using accumulated CRISPR-induced sequence changes as inherited records of ancestry. Nested scars support a lineage tree only after considering recurrent edits, deletion of earlier scars and missing observations.

= scGESTALT
{parent=CRISPR lineage recording}
{c}

A lineage-recording approach coupling inherited CRISPR barcode edits with single-<cell> transcriptomes. It can relate <cell> identity to developmental ancestry while retaining the limitations of incomplete barcode recovery and edit <homoplasy>.<Simultaneous single-cell profiling of lineages and cell types in the vertebrate brain by Bushra Raj and others>[2018, Figures 1 and 3–6, recording pulses and lineage/cell-type recovery]{ref}

= Genetic barcode
{parent=Genetic lineage tracing}

An inherited sequence label used to distinguish <cell> ancestries. Independent acquisition of the same barcode, loss of the readout and nonuniform barcode frequencies can all confound a clonal inference.

= Barcode collision
{parent=Genetic barcode}
{title2=$\sum_jp_j^2$}

Independent <cells> receive the same apparent label. If barcode probabilities are $p_j$, two independent founders match with <probability> $\sum_jp_j^2$, rather than necessarily the inverse of the nominal library size.

= Clonal lineage tracing
{parent=Cell lineage}

<Lineage tracing> that identifies descendants of a single ancestor. A labelled group is informative about a clone only if labelling collisions and independent activation are excluded.

= Clone-size distribution
{parent=Clonal lineage tracing}

The distribution of descendant counts per clone. Differences can arise from proliferation, survival, migration or sampling rather than altered fate choice alone.

= Sparse labelling
{parent=Clonal lineage tracing}

Labelling few enough founder <cells> to resolve individual clones. The acceptable density depends on spatial mixing, label diversity and the chance of independent activation.

= Clonal multipotency
{parent=Clonal lineage tracing}

Production of more than one differentiated <cell type> from one confirmed progenitor. Several separately labelled unipotent <cells> can imitate this pattern if the label is not unique.

= Neutral clonal competition
{parent=Clonal lineage tracing}

Competition among labelled clones with equivalent average reproductive behaviour. Clone extinction can coexist with tissue maintenance, and surviving clones form a selected subset of all initial clones.
