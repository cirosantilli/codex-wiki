= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Every vector decomposes as $v=Ev+(v-Ev)$, where $Ev\in\operatorname{im}E$ and $E(v-Ev)=0$. If $w$ lies in both the image and kernel, write $w=Eu$ to obtain $w=E^2u=Ew=0$. Thus
$$
V=\operatorname{im}E\oplus\ker E,
$$
and $E$ is the <projection> onto its <image> along its <kernel>. It is the identity on its image and zero on its kernel. Let $N=\dim V$ and $r=\operatorname{rank}E$. A basis adapted to this <direct sum> gives matrix $\operatorname{diag}(I_r,0_{N-r})$. Since $E$ is not the identity, $N-r>0$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\det E=0.}
$$

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

In the adapted basis from part (a), the matrix has $r$ diagonal entries equal to one and $N-r$ equal to zero. With the <characteristic polynomial> convention $\chi_E(X)=\det(XI-E)$,
$$
\boxed{\chi_E(X)=X^{N-r}(X-1)^r.}
$$
The <characteristic polynomial> is independent of the choice of basis.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

If $Ev=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, applying $E^2=E$ gives $\lambda^2v=\lambda v$, hence $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$. The kernel is nonzero since $E\ne I$, so zero always occurs. One occurs exactly when the image is nonzero. Thus the <eigenvalue> set is **$\{0\}$ if $E=0$, and $\{0,1\}$ otherwise**. Their algebraic multiplicities are $N-r$ and $r$ respectively.

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

The polynomial $X(X-1)$ annihilates $E$ by idempotence, so the <minimal polynomial> divides it. If $E=0$, its monic polynomial of smallest positive degree is $X$. If $E\ne0$, both eigenvalues zero and one occur, so both factors must divide every annihilating polynomial. Therefore
$$
\boxed{m_E(X)=\begin{cases}X,&E=0,\\X(X-1),&E\ne0\quad(E\ne I).\end{cases}}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

For real scalars $\alpha,\beta$ and matrices $A,C,B$, distributivity of <matrix multiplication> and linearity of the <trace> give
$$
\varphi(\alpha A+\beta C,B)
=\operatorname{tr}(\alpha AB+\beta CB)
=\alpha\varphi(A,B)+\beta\varphi(C,B).
$$
Thus **$\varphi$ is linear in its first argument**.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Expand the <trace> by matrix entries:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}b_{ji}
=\sum_j\sum_i b_{ji}a_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(BA).
$$
Hence **$\varphi(A,B)=\varphi(B,A)$**. Combined with part (a), this makes $\varphi$ a <symmetric bilinear form>.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For $n\geq2$, take the matrix whose leading $2\times2$ block is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and whose other entries are zero. Its square has leading block $-I_2$, so
$$
\boxed{\varphi(A,A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=-2<0.}
$$
This contradicts the <positive definiteness> required of an <inner product>. Even the nonzero matrix unit $E_{12}$ has $\varphi(E_{12},E_{12})=0$.

The printed assertion has an exception: **when $n=1$, $\varphi(a,b)=ab$ is an inner product** on $\mathbb R$. Thus the requested failure requires $n\geq2$, a restriction absent from the printed question.

= d
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Interpret diagonalizability over the specified underlying field $\mathbb R$. Write $A=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)P^{-1}$ with real eigenvalues. Invariance of the <trace> under <similarity> gives
$$
\varphi(A,A)=\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=\sum_i\lambda_i^2.
$$
If $A\ne0$ is diagonalizable, at least one eigenvalue is nonzero, proving **$\varphi(A,A)>0$**. Real diagonalizability is essential: the rotation block from part (c) is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$ but has eigenvalues $i,-i$ and negative trace of its square.

= e
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}
{c}

Let $E_{ji}$ be the <matrix unit> having its only nonzero entry, one, in position $(j,i)$. Direct multiplication gives $\operatorname{tr}(AE_{ji})=a_{ij}$. If $A\in R$, every such pairing vanishes, forcing every entry $a_{ij}$ to be zero. Conversely, zero pairs to zero with every matrix. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=\{0\}.}
$$
The <matrix trace pairing> is therefore a <nondegenerate bilinear form>, despite its failure to be positive definite when $n\geq2$.

= 3
{parent=2010}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=3}
{c}

First extract the <polynomial content> from $f$:
$$
\boxed{f(X)=2\bigl(X^4-2X^3+4X^2-2X+2\bigr).}
$$
The primitive quartic is <Eisenstein> at the prime $2$: every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $2$, while the constant term is not divisible by $4$. <Eisenstein's criterion> makes it irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, and <Gauss's lemma> makes it irreducible in $\mathbb Z[X]$. The constant factor $2$ is itself irreducible in $\mathbb Z[X]$.

The polynomial $g$ is already irreducible over $\mathbb Q$: it is <Eisenstein> at $3$. Its nonleading coefficients, including the missing zero coefficients, are divisible by $3$, and $-21$ is not divisible by $9$. Thus **$g$ is its own sole nonconstant irreducible factor** over $\mathbb Q$.

Over the field $\mathbb Z_3$, grouping terms gives
$$
\boxed{h(X)=X(X+2)(X^2+1).}
$$
The first two factors are linear and therefore irreducible. The quadratic takes the values $1,2,2$ at $0,1,2$ respectively, so it has no root in $\mathbb Z_3$. A degree-two polynomial over a field is reducible exactly when it has a root, proving its irreducibility.

All three factorizations are **unique up to order and multiplication by units**. The ring $\mathbb Z$ is a <unique factorization domain>, hence so is $\mathbb Z[X]$ by <Gauss's lemma>. A polynomial ring over a field is a <Euclidean domain>, so both $\mathbb Q[X]$ and $\mathbb Z_3[X]$ have unique factorization. The units are $\pm1$ in the first ring, nonzero rational constants in the second, and $1,2$ in the third.

= Solution

In the <subspace topology>, $B$ is relatively open in $A$ if $B=A\cap U$ for an open subset $U$ of the ambient Euclidean space. Equivalently, every $b\in B$ has a radius $\varepsilon>0$ with $A\cap B_\varepsilon(b)\subseteq B$. It is relatively closed if its complement $A\setminus B$ is relatively open, equivalently $B=A\cap F$ for an ambient closed set $F$.

For the upper half of the open disk, **$B$ is closed in $A$, but not open in $A$**. It is the intersection with the closed half-plane $\operatorname{Im}z\geq0$. A relative neighborhood of zero contains points of negative imaginary part, so relative openness fails.

= b
{parent=i}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

The rational cut is **both open and closed in $\mathbb Q$**. It is relatively open as $\mathbb Q\cap(-\infty,\sqrt2)$. Since $\sqrt2$ is irrational, its relative complement is $\mathbb Q\cap(\sqrt2,\infty)$, also relatively open. Thus this cut is a <clopen set> in the <subspace topology> of the rationals.

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

A set is <compact> if every <open cover> of it has a finite subcover. Let $K_1,\ldots,K_m$ be compact and let $\mathcal U$ cover their union. For each $j$, the same family covers $K_j$, so compactness supplies a finite subfamily $\mathcal U_j$ covering $K_j$. The union of these finitely many finite subfamilies is finite and covers $\bigcup_jK_j$. Hence **a finite union of compact sets is compact**.

An arbitrary union need not be compact. Each singleton $\{n\}$, $n\geq1$, is compact, but their union $\mathbb N$ has the open cover $\{(n-1/3,n+1/3):n\geq1\}$ with no finite subcover. Every member covers only one positive integer, so finitely many leave infinitely many integers uncovered.

The <complex line integral> along the smooth parametrized curve is
$$
\int_\gamma f(z)dz:=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)dt.
$$
For the reversed curve, the <chain rule> gives $\widetilde\gamma'(t)=-\gamma'(a+b-t)$. Substitution $s=a+b-t$ yields
$$
\int_{\widetilde\gamma}f(z)dz
=-\int_a^b f(\gamma(a+b-t))\gamma'(a+b-t)dt
=-\int_a^b f(\gamma(s))\gamma'(s)ds.
$$
Consequently **reversing orientation negates the complex line integral**.

An entire <antiderivative> is $F(z)=z\sin z+\cos z$, since differentiation cancels the sine terms and leaves $F'(z)=z\cos z$. The curve runs from $-i$ to $i$. Both terms of $F$ are even, so $F(i)=F(-i)$, and the <complex line integral> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma z\cos z\,dz=0.}
$$

{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Along the circle, $z=z_0+re^{it}$ and $dz=ire^{it}dt$. Expanding the nonholomorphic integrand gives
$$
|z|^2=|z_0|^2+r^2+r\overline z_0e^{it}+rz_0e^{-it}.
$$
After multiplication by $ire^{it}$, all nonconstant integer-frequency exponentials have zero integral over $[0,2\pi]$. The remaining constant term is $ir^2z_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma |z|^2dz=2\pi i r^2z_0.}
$$
This direct parametrization avoids applying a holomorphic-antiderivative argument to $|z|^2$, which is not holomorphic.

For the positively oriented circle $C=\{z:|z-z_0|=r\}$, the <Cauchy derivative formula> states
$$
\boxed{f^{(k)}(z_0)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{k+1}}dz,\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The hypothesis that the closed disk lies in the open holomorphy domain ensures the formula applies on and inside this circle.

= ii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

On the circle, the integrand in the <Cauchy derivative formula> has modulus at most $M/r^{k+1}$, and the circle has length $2\pi r$. The <ML inequality> therefore gives
$$
|f^{(k)}(z_0)|\leq\frac{k!}{2\pi}(2\pi r)\frac M{r^{k+1}}
=\boxed{\frac{k!M}{r^k}.}
$$
The maximum $M$ exists because the modulus of the continuous function $f$ attains a maximum on the compact circle. These are the <Cauchy estimate>.

= iii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Suppose, for a contradiction, that $f$ has no zero in the open disk. The strict boundary inequality implies no boundary zero either. Thus $1/f$ is holomorphic on a neighborhood of the closed disk: compactness and continuity keep $f$ nonzero in a sufficiently small neighborhood. Apply the $k=0$ <Cauchy estimate> to $1/f$:
$$
\frac1{|f(z_0)|}\leq\max_{|z-z_0|=r}\frac1{|f(z)|}
=\frac1{\min_{|z-z_0|=r}|f(z)|}.
$$
This contradicts the assumed strict inequality. If $f(z_0)=0$, the conclusion is immediate without taking a reciprocal. Therefore **$f$ has a zero in the open disk**. This is a local form of the <minimum modulus principle>.

= Solution
{parent=7}
{c}

The product $(\tanh s)y(s)$ is continuous, so the <fundamental theorem of calculus> makes the integral continuously differentiable. Differentiating the integral equation and then evaluating at zero gives $y'=(\tanh x)y$, $y(0)=1$. Since $(\log\cosh x)'=\tanh x$, an <integrating factor> is $1/\cosh x$. Thus $(y/\cosh x)'=0$, and
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\cosh x.}
$$
Put $L(x)=\log\cosh x$, so $L(0)=0$ and $L'=\tanh x$. The claimed <Picard iterate> formula holds for $n=0$. Assuming it at $n$, termwise integration of the finite sum gives
$$
y_{n+1}(x)=1+\sum_{k=0}^n\frac1{k!}\int_0^xL(s)^kL'(s)ds
=1+\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{L(x)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}.
$$
This proves by induction
$$
\boxed{y_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(\log\cosh x)^k}{k!}\longrightarrow e^{\log\cosh x}=\cosh x.}
$$
The same calculation works for negative $x$ because it uses an antiderivative rather than a monotonicity substitution. On every bounded interval $|x|\leq R$, $0\leq L(x)\leq\log\cosh R$, so comparison with the exponential-series tail also proves uniform convergence there.

= Solution
{parent=8}
{c}

Here a periodic solution must mean a nonconstant periodic orbit: constant equilibria are not excluded by the stated divergence hypothesis. Suppose such an orbit exists. Since the vector field is continuously differentiable, uniqueness for the <autonomous differential equation> makes its trajectory a simple closed curve $C$, bounding a region $D$ by the <Jordan curve theorem>. The <Green theorem> gives
$$
\iint_D\left[\partial_x(\rho f)+\partial_y(\rho g)\right]dx\,dy
=\oint_C\rho f\,dy-\rho g\,dx.
$$
Along the orbit, $dx=fdt$ and $dy=gdt$, so the line integral is identically zero. Reversing orientation if necessary does not change this conclusion. The area integral is strictly positive because its continuous integrand is positive everywhere and $D$ has positive area. This contradiction proves the <Bendixson-Dulac criterion> in this case.

For the second-order equation set $y=x'$, giving $x'=y$, $y'=-y-\varepsilon y^3-\sin x$. Choose the <Dulac function> $\rho=-1$. Then
$$
\partial_x(\rho f)+\partial_y(\rho g)=1+3\varepsilon y^2>0.
$$
Thus **there is no nonconstant periodic solution for $\varepsilon>0$**. The equilibria $x(t)=k\pi$ do remain solutions. As a direct check, the mechanical energy $H=y^2/2+1-\cos x$ obeys $H'=-y^2-\varepsilon y^4$, whose integral over a period could vanish only if $y\equiv0$.

= Solution
{parent=9}
{c}

For $p$ in a half-plane of absolute convergence, interchange the integrals using <Fubini's theorem>:
$$
\overline g(p)=\int_0^\infty f(s)\int_s^\infty e^{-pt}dt\,ds
=\boxed{\overline f(p)/p.}
$$
For positive real $p$, integration by parts gives $I_n=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}t^n dt=nI_{n-1}/p$, starting from $I_0=1/p$. Hence $I_n=n!/p^{n+1}$, or
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(t^n/n!)(p)=p^{-n-1}.}
$$
The formula extends throughout $\operatorname{Re}p>0$ by the same integration argument. Write $X=\mathcal Lx$. The integral term is the <convolution> of $t$ with $x(t)$, whose <Laplace transform> is $X/p^2$. The derivative transforms to $pX-x(0)=pX-1$. Therefore
$$
(p+p^{-2})X-1=p^{-2}+p^{-3}+p^{-5},
$$
and elementary factorization gives
$$
X=\frac{p^5+p^3+p^2+1}{p^3(p^3+1)}
=\frac{(p^2+1)(p^3+1)}{p^3(p^3+1)}
=\frac1p+\frac1{p^3}.
$$
Inverting the two polynomial transforms yields
$$
\boxed{x(t)=1+\tfrac12t^2.}
$$
Direct substitution verifies $x'=t$ and $\int_0^t(t-s)(1+s^2/2)ds=t^2/2+t^4/24$, as required. Uniqueness also follows without transform assumptions: differentiating the equation twice gives $x^{(3)}+x=1+t^2/2$ with initial data $x(0)=1$, $x'(0)=0$, $x''(0)=1$, a linear initial-value problem with a unique solution.
