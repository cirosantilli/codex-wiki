{scope}

= Solution
{parent=1}

Let $m_T$ be the <minimal polynomial>. If $Tv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then $0=m_T(T)v=m_T(\lambda)v$, so $m_T(\lambda)=0$. Conversely, if $m_T(\lambda)=0$, write $m_T(X)=(X-\lambda)q(X)$. If $T-\lambda I$ were invertible, the identity $(T-\lambda I)q(T)=0$ would imply $q(T)=0$, contradicting minimality of the degree. Thus $T-\lambda I$ has a nonzero <kernel of a linear map>, and $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue>. This proves the <eigenvalue criterion using the minimal polynomial> for roots in the field under consideration.

For the given matrix, direct expansion gives
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(X)=\det(XI-A)=X^3+3X^2+3X+1=(X+1)^3.}
$$
Put $N=A+I$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
N^2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&1\\-1&-2&-1\\1&2&1\end{pmatrix}\ne0,\qquad N^3=0.
$$
By the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>, $m_T$ divides $(X+1)^3$; the nonzero square rules out every smaller power. Therefore $\boxed{m_T(X)=(X+1)^3}$. Its sole <eigenvalue> is $-1$, but the size of the nilpotent part makes the minimum polynomial cubic rather than linear.

{parent=2005}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

For affine <polynomials> $f(t)=a+bt$ and $g(t)=c+dt$, direct integration gives $\langle f,g\rangle=2ac+2bd/3$. This is bilinear and symmetric, and $\langle f,f\rangle=2a^2+2b^2/3>0$ for every nonzero $f$. Thus it is an <inner product>.

The <adjoint of differentiation on affine polynomials> is determined by $\langle Df,g\rangle=\langle f,D^*g\rangle$. Since $Df=b$, the left side is $2bc$. Writing $D^*g=A+Bt$, the right side is $2aA+2bB/3$. Equality for every $a,b$ forces $A=0$, $B=3c$, hence
$$
\boxed{D^*(c+dt)=3ct.}
$$
This is not simply negative differentiation: there are no endpoint conditions here that would remove the boundary term in an <integration by parts> argument.

= Solution
{parent=3}

An <ideal> is an additive subgroup of a <ring> closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements; in a potentially noncommutative ring, a two-sided ideal must absorb multiplication on both sides. For a <ring homomorphism> $\phi$, its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $K=\{r:\phi(r)=0\}$ contains zero, is closed under subtraction, and satisfies $\phi(rk)=\phi(r)\phi(k)=0$ and $\phi(kr)=0$. Thus $K$ is an ideal.

Define $\widetilde\phi:R/K\to S$ by $r+K\mapsto\phi(r)$. It is well-defined because two representatives differ by an element of $K$, preserves addition and multiplication, is surjective by hypothesis, and is injective because its zero class is precisely $K$. Therefore $\boxed{R/\ker\phi\cong S}$, the <first isomorphism theorem for rings>.

Apply this to the <evaluation homomorphism> $\mathbb C[z]\to\mathbb C$, $p\mapsto p(1)$. Constants prove surjectivity, and division by $z-1$ shows that its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is $(z-1)$. Hence $\boxed{\mathbb C[z]/(z-1)\cong\mathbb C}$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a <smooth curve> $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb C$ and continuous $f$ on its image, the <contour integral> is defined by
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt.}
$$
The derivative carries the direction and speed of traversal; the complex-valued real-parameter integral is defined by integrating its real and imaginary parts.

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <chain rule> gives $dF(\gamma(t))/dt=F'(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)=f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)$. The <fundamental theorem of calculus> therefore yields
$$
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=F(\gamma(b))-F(\gamma(a))=\boxed0,
$$
because the <closed curve> has equal endpoints. The existence of a single-valued <holomorphic antiderivative> on the whole neighborhood is the decisive hypothesis.

= iii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Take the unit circle $\gamma(t)=e^{it}$, $0\le t\le2\pi$, in the <punctured complex plane>. Direct parameter integration gives
$$
\int_\gamma\frac{dz}{z}=\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-it}ie^{it}\,dt=2\pi i\ne0.
$$
A global <holomorphic antiderivative> would make this integral zero by the previous part. Thus **there is no such antiderivative on the punctured plane**. Local primitives can exist without combining into one single-valued function on this domain.

= iv
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

If a <holomorphic function> $g$ on the <punctured complex plane> satisfied $e^{g(z)}=z$, the <chain rule> would give $e^{g(z)}g'(z)=1$, hence $g'(z)=1/z$. This contradicts the previous part. Therefore **there is no global holomorphic logarithm on the punctured plane**. A <branch of the complex logarithm> requires a suitable smaller domain.

{parent=2005}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5}

A <pole> of order $m$ at $a$ means that $f(z)=h(z)/(z-a)^m$ near $a$, where $h$ is holomorphic and $h(a)\ne0$. Equivalently its <Laurent series> has a finite principal part with leading nonzero term $a_{-m}(z-a)^{-m}$. The <residue> $c$ is the coefficient $a_{-1}$. For a simple pole, $m=1$, this coefficient is $h(a)$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Res}_{z=a}f=\lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z).}
$$
For $\cot z=\cos z/\sin z$, the zeros of the denominator are exactly $z=n\pi$, $n\in\mathbb Z$: this follows from $e^{2iz}=1$. Each is simple, because $\sin'(n\pi)=\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n\ne0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\cot z\text{ has simple poles at }n\pi,\quad\operatorname{Res}_{z=n\pi}\cot z=1.}
$$
There are no other finite <singularities>. If infinity is included, it is an accumulation point of these poles, so it is not an isolated singularity to which the usual removable/pole/essential classification could be applied.

= Solution
{parent=i}

A <Möbius transformation> has the form $M(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$ with $ad-bc\ne0$, extended to the <Riemann sphere> by its limiting values at its pole and at infinity. If $c\ne0$, it sends $-d/c$ to infinity and infinity to $a/c$; if $c=0$, infinity maps to infinity.

Represent $M$ by the invertible matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, with nonzero scalar multiples representing the same map. Substitution shows that composition corresponds to matrix multiplication. The product determinant is the product of two nonzero determinants, so the composition is again a Möbius transformation. Solving $w=M(z)$ gives
$$
\boxed{M^{-1}(w)=\frac{dw-b}{a-cw},}
$$
with the same extended-value convention. This inverse proves that $M$ is a <bijection> of the extended complex plane.

= ii
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the <Cayley transform>
$$
\boxed{w=\frac{z-i}{z+i}.}
$$
For $\operatorname{Im}z>0$, its denominator is nonzero, and direct subtraction gives $1-|w|^2=4\operatorname{Im}z/|z+i|^2>0$. Thus the image lies in the <open unit disk>. Conversely, for $|w|<1$ the inverse is $z=i(1+w)/(1-w)$, with $\operatorname{Im}z=(1-|w|^2)/|1-w|^2>0$. Hence it maps the <upper half-plane> bijectively onto the disk.

{parent=2005}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=7}

Since the exponential factor is positive, equilibria must have $y=0$ and $x^4=1$. Thus $\boxed{(x,y)=(-1,0),\ (1,0)}$. At either point the <Jacobian matrix> is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-4x^3&0\end{pmatrix}$. At $(-1,0)$ its <eigenvalues> are $\pm2$, so this is a <saddle point>. At $(1,0)$ they are $\pm2i$: the <linearization> has a <centre equilibrium>.

The requested first-order approximation follows by putting $x=1+u$, $y=v$: $\dot u=v+O(\|(u,v)\|^2)$ and $\dot v=-4u+O(\|(u,v)\|^2)$. The linearized system conserves $4u^2+v^2$, giving the approximate ellipses
$$
\boxed{4(x-1)^2+y^2=\text{constant}.}
$$
However, **the printed nonlinear system has a weak repelling focus at $(1,0)$, rather than a centre**. <A centre in the linearization need not be a nonlinear centre>. Here that distinction can be proved explicitly, without relying on the numerical drift in the figure.

Put $X=2(x-1)$, $Y=y$, $\tau=2t$ and $r^2=X^2+Y^2$. The <weak focus of an exponential polynomial planar system> has the local positive function
$$
H=\frac{X^2+Y^2}{2}+\frac7{12}X^3+\frac12XY^2+\frac13Y^3
+\frac14X^4-\frac3{16}X^3Y+\frac3{16}XY^3.
$$
Expanding the vector field through cubic order and differentiating gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dH}{d\tau}=\frac3{16}r^4+O(r^5)>0\quad\text{for sufficiently small }r>0.}
$$
The cubic correction cancels the cubic terms in the derivative of $H$, and the quartic correction leaves the positive radial term $3r^4/16$. Since $H=r^2/2+O(r^3)$ is positive and the angular velocity stays near $-1$, small nonzero trajectories spiral outward. Equivalently the reversed system has a strict local <Lyapunov function> and an asymptotically stable equilibrium. In particular, a periodic orbit sufficiently near the equilibrium is impossible, because $H$ would have to increase strictly and return to its starting value. This proves the failure of the printed centre assertion while retaining its correct linearized ellipse calculation.

{parent=2005}

= Solution
{parent=8}

The <characteristic equations for a first-order quasilinear equation> are $\dot x=x$, $\dot y=y$, $\dot u=2u$, with characteristic parameter $s$. Through the prescribed point they give
$$
\boxed{(x(s),y(s),u(s))=(x_0e^s,y_0e^s,u_0e^{2s}).}
$$
For a nonzero projected initial point, the <characteristic projections> are rays from the origin, not trajectories crossing through the origin to the opposite ray. If $x_0=y_0=0$, a classical solution graph requires $u_0=0$, because the original equation reduces there to $0=2u$.

Parameterize the initial line by $(x,y,u)=(a,1-a,a^3)$. Along its characteristic, $x+y=e^s>0$, $a=x/(x+y)$ and $u=a^3e^{2s}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\frac{x^3}{x+y},\qquad x+y>0.}
$$
Differentiation verifies the equation, and substitution of $x+y=1$ verifies the initial data. <Characteristic reachability for radial transport> shows why the domain is this positive half-plane: each point there lies on precisely one characteristic from the data line. Rays in $x+y<0$ never meet that line, so their values are not determined by these data. Allowing a negative scaling factor would incorrectly join two different characteristic rays through a degenerate origin.

= Solution

Integrating $e^{-pt}\sin(ct)$, or taking the imaginary part of $\int_0^\infty e^{-(p-ic)t}\,dt$, gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\{\sin ct\}(p)=\frac{c}{p^2+c^2}\qquad(\operatorname{Re}p>0).}
$$
The <Laplace transform convolution theorem> states $\mathcal L(f*g)=\bar f\,\bar g$ whenever the transforms and convolution are defined. Applying it to the <Volterra integral equation> gives $\bar x+\bar x/(p^2+1)=\bar F$, so
$$
\boxed{\bar x(p)=\frac{p^2+1}{p^2+2}\bar F(p)=\left(1-\frac1{p^2+2}\right)\bar F(p).}
$$
Since $\mathcal L^{-1}[(p^2+2)^{-1}]=\sin(\sqrt2t)/\sqrt2$, a second application of the convolution theorem yields the <sine-kernel Volterra resolvent>
$$
\boxed{x(t)=F(t)-\frac1{\sqrt2}\int_0^t\sin(\sqrt2(t-s))F(s)\,ds.}
$$
The transform argument assumes suitable growth for the transforms to exist. The resulting causal resolvent formula also applies to locally integrable forcing on finite intervals, as can be checked by substitution and exchanging integrals over a finite triangle.
