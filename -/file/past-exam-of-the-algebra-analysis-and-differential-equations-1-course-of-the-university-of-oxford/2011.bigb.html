The <annihilator of a vector subspace> is the <vector subspace> of the <dual space> consisting of the <linear functionals> that vanish on $U$:
$$
\boxed{U^\circ=\{\lambda\in V':\lambda(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.}
$$
It is a <vector subspace> because every <linear combination> of functionals vanishing on $U$ still vanishes there.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For the <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, define the <dual basis> by
$$
\boxed{e_i'(e_j)=\delta_{ij},\qquad e_i'\Bigl(\sum_j x_je_j\Bigr)=x_i.}
$$
Uniqueness of the <coordinates> makes each $e_i'$ well-defined and linear. If $\sum_i a_ie_i'=0$, evaluation at $e_j$ gives $a_j=0$, so these <linear functionals> are <linearly independent>. For any $\lambda\in V'$, evaluation on each <basis vector> gives
$$
\lambda=\sum_i\lambda(e_i)e_i'.
$$
Both sides agree on a <basis>, hence on every <linear combination>. Thus they also <span> the <dual space> and are a <basis> of it.

Let $\varepsilon_i$ denote the coordinate <linear functional> dual to the $i$th vector of the <standard basis>. Writing a vector in the supplied <basis> gives
$$
x=c_1v_1+c_2v_2+c_3v_3+c_4v_4
=(c_1+c_2+c_3+c_4,c_2+c_3+c_4,c_3+c_4,c_4)^T.
$$
Successive differences recover the coefficients. Hence the <dual basis> is
$$
\boxed{v_1'=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2,\quad
v_2'=\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_3,\quad
v_3'=\varepsilon_3-\varepsilon_4,\quad v_4'=\varepsilon_4.}
$$
These formulas directly give $v_i'(v_j)=\delta_{ij}$ and exemplify the <dual basis of cumulative coordinate sums>.

The <span> $U$ is the coordinate plane $\{(x_1,x_2,0,0)^T\}$. Consequently its <annihilator of a vector subspace> is $U^\circ=\operatorname{span}\{\varepsilon_3,\varepsilon_4\}$. The <dual map> is composition with $T$, so
$$
T'\varepsilon_3=\varepsilon_3,\qquad T'\varepsilon_4=0.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{T'(U^\circ)=\operatorname{span}\{\varepsilon_3\}.}
$$
Equivalently, $T'(a\varepsilon_3+b\varepsilon_4)$ sends $x$ to $ax_3$.

The <Gram-Schmidt process> subtracts projections onto directions already constructed and then normalizes. Define recursively
$$
w_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\langle v_k,e_j\rangle e_j,\qquad e_k=\frac{w_k}{\|w_k\|},\quad k=1,\ldots,n,
$$
where the empty sum for $k=1$ is zero. Assuming the preceding $e_j$ are <orthonormal>, taking the <inner product> of $w_k$ with $e_i$, $i<k$, gives zero. Moreover $w_k\ne0$: otherwise $v_k$ would belong to the <span> of $v_1,\ldots,v_{k-1}$, contrary to <linear independence>. Normalization is therefore legitimate and produces an <orthonormal basis>. At each stage the first $k$ constructed vectors have the same <span> as the first $k$ original vectors.

A real <inner product> must be symmetric. Here $\beta_a(e_1,e_2)=-2$ and $\beta_a(e_2,e_1)=-a$, forcing $a=2$. For this value,
$$
\beta_2(x,x)=4x_1^2-4x_1x_2+3x_2^2
=4(x_1-x_2/2)^2+2x_2^2.
$$
This is strictly positive for every nonzero <vector>. Bilinearity is already given, so symmetry and <positive definiteness> prove
$$
\boxed{a=2\text{ is the unique admissible value}.}
$$

The <Chinese remainder theorem> for two comaximal two-sided <ideals> $I,J$ of a ring states that the natural <ring homomorphism> induces
$$
\boxed{R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J\qquad(I+J=R).}
$$
Indeed, the map $r\mapsto(r+I,r+J)$ has <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $I\cap J$. Choose $i\in I$, $j\in J$ with $i+j=1$. Given target representatives $a,b\in R$, the element $aj+bi$ is congruent to $a$ modulo $I$ and to $b$ modulo $J$, proving <surjectivity>. The <first isomorphism theorem for rings> gives the result. For a commutative ring, $I\cap J=IJ$: if $x\in I\cap J$, then $x=xi+xj\in IJ$, while the reverse inclusion follows from the ideal properties.

= Solution

Take $R=\mathbb Z$, $I=a\mathbb Z$ and $J=b\mathbb Z$. Since $a,b$ are <coprime integers>, <Bézout's identity> gives $ra+sb=1$, so $I+J=\mathbb Z$. Their intersection is $ab\mathbb Z$: it consists of integers divisible by both $a$ and $b$, and coprimality makes this equivalent to divisibility by $ab$. Thus the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z_{ab}\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_a\times\mathbb Z_b,\qquad
[n]_{ab}\longmapsto([n]_a,[n]_b)}
$$
as a <ring isomorphism>. This includes $a=1$ or $b=1$, where the corresponding factor is the zero ring.

Let $N$ count everyone present, including the host. The grouping, seating and departure conditions give the <modular congruences>
$$
N\equiv4\pmod5,\qquad N\equiv3\pmod9,\qquad N\equiv1\pmod2.
$$
The first two imply $N=39+45k$, and oddness forces $k$ even. By the <Chinese remainder theorem>, the complete solution is
$$
\boxed{N\equiv39\pmod{90}.}
$$
Because not every invitee attended, at most $y-1$ guests came, so $N\leq y$. For $y=120$, the only positive possibility is **39 people**. It is feasible: seven groups of five and one of four; three tables of ten and one of nine; nineteen departing pairs and the host.

For $y=150$, both **39 and 129 people** are possible. The latter uses twenty-five groups of five and one of four, three tables of ten and eleven of nine, and sixty-four departing pairs. Thus the larger invitation count destroys uniqueness; it does not force a different attendance.

In the <subspace topology> on $S$, a subset $A$ is <open> in $S$ precisely when $A=S\cap O$ for some <open set> $O\subseteq\mathbb C$. A subset $B$ is <closed> in $S$ precisely when $S\setminus B$ is open in $S$, equivalently $B=S\cap F$ for some <closed set> $F\subseteq\mathbb C$.

A <compact space> is one for which every <open cover> has a finite <subcover>. Here the cover may use relatively open subsets of $S$, or equivalently ambient open subsets covering it. The <Heine-Borel theorem> states that
$$
\boxed{S\subseteq\mathbb C\text{ is compact}\iff S\text{ is closed and bounded}.}
$$
Closedness and boundedness here refer to the ambient <complex plane>, not merely closedness in $S$ itself.

A <connected space> $S$ cannot be expressed as $S=A\cup B$ with $A,B$ disjoint nonempty relatively <open sets>. Equivalently, the only subsets simultaneously relatively <open> and <closed> are $\varnothing$ and $S$. A decomposition into such $A,B$ is a separation of the <topological space>.

A <path-connected space> $S$ has, for every pair $z_0,z_1\in S$, a <continuous> path $\gamma:[0,1]\to S$ with $\gamma(0)=z_0$ and $\gamma(1)=z_1$. Every <path-connected space> is a <connected space>, because a separation of the target would pull a joining path apart and contradict <connectedness of an interval>.

The <integers> are unbounded in the <complex plane>, so the <Heine-Borel theorem> shows they are **not compact**. They are also **not connected**: $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$ are disjoint nonempty relatively <open sets> covering $\mathbb Z$. Each integer is isolated, so the induced <subspace topology> is discrete.

The disc is a <closed set> and is bounded in the <complex plane>, so it is **compact** by the <Heine-Borel theorem>. It is <convex>: the <triangle inequality> gives $|(1-t)z_0+tz_1|\leq2$ for all $t\in[0,1]$ when both endpoints lie in it. Straight segments therefore supply paths, so it is <path-connected> and hence **connected**.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The union is bounded but is not a <closed set>: the points $-1+1/n$, $n\geq2$, belong to the open disc and tend to $-1$, which lies in neither disc. Thus it is **not compact** by the <Heine-Borel theorem>.

Nevertheless it is **connected**, indeed <path-connected>. Every point of the open disc joins $0$ by a straight segment. The segment from $0$ to $1$ stays in the union, since its points before the endpoint belong to the open disc and the endpoint $1$ belongs to the closed disc. Every point of that closed disc joins $1$ inside it by <convexity>. Concatenating these paths joins any two points of the union. The fact that the two discs are disjoint does not imply disconnection: their contact point belongs to one of them. This illustrates <connectedness at a one-sided contact point>.

An <isolated singularity> at $a$ means that the function is <holomorphic> on some punctured disc $0<|z-a|<r$, while it is undefined or fails to be holomorphic at $a$. The punctured neighbourhood contains no other singularities; this isolation is needed before applying the usual removable/pole/essential classification.

A <removable singularity> at $a$ is an <isolated singularity> for which one can assign a value at $a$ to obtain a <holomorphic function> on the whole disc. Equivalently its <Laurent series> has no negative powers. A finite limit $L$ supplies the extension value $f(a)=L$.

A <pole> of order $m\geq1$ at $a$ means
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac{g(z)}{(z-a)^m},\qquad g\text{ holomorphic near }a,\quad g(a)\ne0.}
$$
Equivalently the lowest nonzero power in its <Laurent series> is $(z-a)^{-m}$. In particular, $(z-a)^mf(z)$ tends to a finite nonzero limit.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

The possible singular points are $i,-i,1$. Near $i$, put $h(z)=1/((z+i)(z-1)^2)$; then
$$
f(z)=\frac{h(z)-1/4}{z-i},\qquad h(i)=\frac14.
$$
The numerator vanishes at $i$, so the apparent pole cancels and **$i$ is removable**. Its extension value is $h'(i)=1/4+3i/8$, obtained from $h'/h=-1/(z+i)-2/(z-1)$.

At $-i$, the first term has a simple <pole> with nonzero <residue>
$$
\lim_{z\to-i}(z+i)f(z)=\frac1{(-2i)(-i-1)^2}=\frac14.
$$
The subtracted term is holomorphic there, so **$-i$ is a simple pole**. At $1$,
$$
\lim_{z\to1}(z-1)^2f(z)=\frac1{1^2+1}=\frac12\ne0,
$$
so **$1$ is a double pole**. There are no other finite singularities.

Away from zero, singularities occur exactly when $e^{1/z}=-1$, namely
$$
\boxed{z_k=\frac1{(2k+1)\pi i},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
For $D(z)=z(e^{1/z}+1)$, differentiation at $z_k$ gives $D'(z_k)=1/z_k\ne0$. Thus every $z_k$ is a simple <pole>, with <residue> $z_k$.

As $|k|\to\infty$, these <poles> accumulate at zero. Therefore **zero is a <non-isolated singularity>**. Every punctured disc around it contains poles, so the function is not holomorphic on any punctured disc there. In particular zero cannot be classified as an isolated <essential singularity>; the isolated-singularity classification does not apply. The displayed poles and their <accumulation point> zero exhaust the finite singularities.

The <contour integral> along a piecewise smooth path is defined by parametrization:
$$
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt,
$$
with a sum over the smooth pieces. The <residue theorem> states that for a positively oriented simple closed contour and a function holomorphic on and inside it apart from finitely many isolated singularities $a_j$ inside,
$$
\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_j\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).
$$
More generally a closed contour uses the <winding number> of each singularity as its weight.

Set $z=e^{it}$, so $dt=dz/(iz)$ and $\cos t=(z+z^{-1})/2$. The required real <integral> becomes
$$
I=\frac1i\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{z}{2z^4+5z^2+2}\,dz.
$$
The denominator factors as $(2z^2+1)(z^2+2)$. Only the simple <poles> $z=\pm i/\sqrt2$ lie inside the <unit circle>. At either one,
$$
\operatorname{Res}\left(\frac z{2z^4+5z^2+2},z_0\right)
=\frac{z_0}{8z_0^3+10z_0}=\frac1{8z_0^2+10}=\frac16.
$$
The <residue theorem> now gives
$$
\boxed{I=\frac1i\,2\pi i\left(\frac16+\frac16\right)=\frac{2\pi}{3}.}
$$

With $y=xv$, direct differentiation gives
$$
x^2y''-2xy'+(x^2+2)y=x^3(v''+v).
$$
The homogeneous <ordinary differential equation> therefore has general solution $y=x(A\cos x+B\sin x)$. Choose
$$
y_1(x)=x\cos x,\qquad y_2(x)=x\sin x,
$$
so $y_1(\pi/2)=0$ and $y_2(\pi)=0$. Their <Wronskian> in the convention $W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2$ is $W(x)=x^2$. There is no nonzero homogeneous solution satisfying both <boundary conditions>, since the first forces $B=0$ and the second forces $A=0$.

For the operator as printed, whose leading coefficient is $x^2$, the <Green's function> is
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{x\cos x\sin s}{s^3},&\pi/2\leq x\leq s\leq\pi,\\
\displaystyle\frac{x\sin x\cos s}{s^3},&\pi/2\leq s\leq x\leq\pi.
\end{cases}}
$$
It satisfies the endpoint <boundary conditions>, is continuous across $x=s$, and its first derivative has jump $1/s^2$. Multiplying that jump by the leading coefficient $s^2$ produces the unit source required by a <Green's function>. This factor matters because the operator was not normalized to leading coefficient one; it is an instance of a <Green function under dependent-variable rescaling>.

Thus $y(x)=\int_{\pi/2}^\pi G(x,s)f(s)\,ds$. For $f(s)=s^3$, split the integral at $s=x$ to obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
y(x)&=x\sin x\int_{\pi/2}^x\cos s\,ds+x\cos x\int_x^\pi\sin s\,ds\\
&=x\sin x(\sin x-1)+x\cos x(1+\cos x).
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x(1+\cos x-\sin x).}
$$
The substitution above gives $v''+v=1$, so the forcing is indeed $x^3$, and both endpoint values vanish.

Both components vanish at
$$
(0,0),\quad(1,0),\quad(0,1),\quad(4,3).
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> of the vector field is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}y-2x+1&x\\-y&4y-x-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> classify these <equilibria> by <linearization of a dynamical system>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{point}&\text{eigenvalues}&\text{type}\\\hline
(0,0)&1,-2&\text{saddle}\\
(1,0)&-1,-3&\text{stable node}\\
(0,1)&2,2&\text{unstable improper node}\\
(4,3)&1-\sqrt{13},\ 1+\sqrt{13}&\text{saddle}
\end{array}}
$$
All real parts are nonzero, so the linear classifications determine the local stability of the nonlinear <autonomous system>.

At the origin, the stable direction is the $y$-axis and the unstable direction is the $x$-axis. At $(1,0)$ the eigendirections are $(1,0)$ for $-1$ and $(1,-2)$ for $-3$. At $(0,1)$,
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}
$$
has only one eigendirection, the $y$-axis; its <linearization of a dynamical system> has solutions $\delta x=Ae^{2t}$, $\delta y=(B-At)e^{2t}$, explaining the <improper node>. At $(4,3)$, stable and unstable tangent vectors are respectively $(4,5-\sqrt{13})$ and $(4,5+\sqrt{13})$.

For the <phase portrait>, the axes are invariant, and the remaining <nullclines> are $y=x-1$ and $y=x/2+1$. Off these lines, the complete directional information is
$$
\operatorname{sgn}\dot x=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\operatorname{sgn}(y-x+1),\qquad
\operatorname{sgn}\dot y=\operatorname{sgn}(y)\operatorname{sgn}(2y-x-2).
$$
In the positive quadrant, arrows point right above $y=x-1$ and left below it, and point up above $y=x/2+1$ and down below it. On the positive $x$-axis, trajectories tend to $(1,0)$. On the $y$-axis, $0<y<1$ flows toward the origin and $y>1$ flows upward. These signs and the saddle directions give the following sketch; the plotted curves illustrate the <phase portrait>, with the marked saddle curves tangent to the calculated <eigenvectors>.

Introduce a characteristic parameter $s$ and label each point of the initial segment by $r\in[0,1]$. The <method of characteristics> gives
$$
\frac{dx}{ds}=x-2,\qquad\frac{dy}{ds}=y,\qquad\frac{dz}{ds}=y,
\qquad (x,y,z)|_{s=0}=(1,r,1).
$$
Integrating gives the parametric solution
$$
\boxed{x=2-e^s,\qquad y=re^s,\qquad z=1+r(e^s-1),\quad s\in\mathbb R,\quad0\leq r\leq1.}
$$
The projected <characteristic curves> satisfy $y=r(2-x)$, so each lies on a straight line through $(2,0)$. Its branch from the data always has $x<2$; the common vertex is approached only as $s\to-\infty$.

Eliminating $s,r$ using $e^s=2-x$ and $r=y/(2-x)$ yields
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=1+y-\frac{y}{2-x}=1+\frac{y(1-x)}{2-x}.}
$$
Its derivatives give $(x-2)z_x+yz_y=y$, and substitution of $x=1$ gives the prescribed value one.

The <Jacobian determinant> of $(s,r)\mapsto(x,y)$ is $-e^{2s}\ne0$, and the inverse just displayed is unique. Hence the data determine the solution exactly on
$$
\boxed{\{(x,y):x<2,\ 0\leq y\leq2-x\}.}
$$
This is the <domain determined by a finite segment under a radial characteristic flow>. Its interior is $x<2$, $0<y<2-x$; the two boundary rays come from the endpoints of the initial segment. Every point there lies on exactly one characteristic from the data. Outside this sector, a characteristic either misses the initial segment or lies on the branch $x>2$, which cannot reach it. Thus the formula may be extended beyond this sector, but such an extension is not uniquely specified by the given data.
