= Solution
{parent=1}
{c}

The <minimal polynomial> $m_T$ is the unique monic polynomial of least degree with $m_T(T)=0$. Existence follows from a linear dependence among powers of $T$ in the finite-dimensional vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$; polynomial division proves uniqueness and that it divides every annihilating polynomial. The <characteristic polynomial> is $\chi_T(t)=\det(tI-[T]_{\mathcal B})$, independent of basis because a change of basis conjugates the matrix.

Write
$$
T^nv=-a_0v-a_1Tv-\cdots-a_{n-1}T^{n-1}v.
$$
In the cyclic basis, the <companion matrix> is
$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-a_2\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For $q(t)=t^n+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$, the displayed vector relation gives $q(T)v=0$. Polynomial evaluation commutes with $T$, so $q(T)T^jv=T^jq(T)v=0$ for every basis vector. Thus $q(T)=0$.

No nonzero polynomial of degree below $n$ can annihilate $T$, since applying it to the <cyclic vector> $v$ would contradict independence of $v,Tv,\ldots,T^{n-1}v$. Hence $m_T=q$. Finally, direct expansion of $\det(tI-C)$, or induction along the last column, gives $\chi_T(t)=t^n+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=q(t)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{m_T=\chi_T=q.}
$$
The argument computes the determinant and annihilator directly and does not invoke the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>.

A <projection> is a linear operator $E$ satisfying $E^2=E$; it need not be an orthogonal projection without an inner product. Pairwise annihilation gives
$$
P^2=\left(\sum_iE_i\right)^2=\sum_iE_i^2+\sum_{i\ne j}E_iE_j=\sum_iE_i=P.
$$
Thus their sum is a projection.

The identities $E_iP=E_i$ imply $T(I-P)=0$. Since $T$ is nonsingular, $P=I$. Also
$$
TE_i=\sum_j\lambda_jE_jE_i=\lambda_iE_i,
$$
so $\operatorname{im}E_i\subseteq V_i$. Conversely, if $Tx=\lambda_ix$, applying $E_j$ gives $(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)E_jx=0$. For $j\ne i$ the eigenvalues are distinct, hence $E_jx=0$. Since $P=I$, $x=E_ix$, proving $\operatorname{im}E_i=V_i$.

Every vector is $\sum_iE_ix$. If $\sum_iv_i=0$ with $v_i\in V_i$, applying $E_j$ gives $v_j=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{V=V_1\oplus\cdots\oplus V_r.}
$$
These operators are the <spectral projections of a diagonalizable matrix>, expressed without choosing an eigenbasis.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For a <self-adjoint operator> on a finite-dimensional complex <inner product space>, every eigenvalue is real and eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. The <spectral theorem> gives an orthonormal eigenbasis. Accordingly
$$
\boxed{V=V_1\mathbin{\perp}\cdots\mathbin{\perp}V_r,}
$$
and the $E_i$ are orthogonal projections satisfying $E_i^*=E_i$. Since $T$ is nonsingular, none of its eigenvalues is zero. Its representing matrix in the orthonormal eigenbasis is real diagonal.

= Solution
{parent=3}
{c}

Reduction modulo the prime $p$ preserves integer addition, multiplication, and one:
$$
\overline{a+b}=\bar a+\bar b,\qquad \overline{ab}=\bar a\bar b.
$$
Every congruence class has an integer representative, so the <ring homomorphism> is onto. Its kernel is exactly $p\mathbb Z=(p)$. The quotient is a field because every class not divisible by $p$ has a multiplicative inverse by <Bézout's identity>.

For the coefficientwise extension to the <polynomial ring>, addition is preserved term by term. If $f=\sum_ia_ix^i$ and $g=\sum_jb_jx^j$, the coefficient of $x^k$ in their product is $\sum_{i+j=k}a_ib_j$. Reducing it modulo $p$ gives $\sum_{i+j=k}\bar a_i\bar b_j$, the coefficient in $\hat\phi(f)\hat\phi(g)$. Thus multiplication and one are preserved as well. Lift each coefficient of a polynomial over $\mathbb Z_p$ to an integer to prove surjectivity. The kernel is
$$
\boxed{\ker\hat\phi=p\mathbb Z[x]=(p).}
$$
Indeed, its elements are precisely the polynomials whose coefficients are all divisible by $p$.

In the <binomial theorem> expansion of $(x-1)^p$, every intermediate coefficient $\binom pj$ is divisible by $p$: the numerator contains the prime factor $p$, while $j!(p-j)!$ does not for $1\leq j<p$. Hence
$$
(x-1)^p=x^p+(-1)^p=x^p-1\quad\text{in }\mathbb Z_p[x].
$$
For $p=2$, the last equality uses $1=-1$ in characteristic two. This is an instance of the <Frobenius endomorphism>.

Write $z=x+iy$, so the real and imaginary components are $u=x^2$ and $v=y^2$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> require $u_x=v_y$, namely $2x=2y$; the other equation is $u_y=-v_x=0$. The first relation fails off the line $x=y$, so **the function is not holomorphic on $\mathbb C$**. It is complex differentiable at points of that line, but a line contains no open neighborhood on which the equations hold.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The reciprocal map is holomorphic for $z\ne0$, and the <complex exponential> is entire. Its denominator vanishes exactly when
$$
e^{2\pi i/z}=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z^{-1}\in\mathbb Z.
$$
All these points are excluded from $U$. The set $U$ is open: it is the inverse image of $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Z$ under the reciprocal map on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. A reciprocal of a nonvanishing holomorphic function is holomorphic. Therefore **$f$ is holomorphic throughout $U$**.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The set $U$ has two open connected components, the left and right half-planes. On each the function is constant in a neighborhood of every point, so its complex derivative is zero everywhere. Thus **$f$ is holomorphic on $U$**. A holomorphic function can take different constant values on different components of a disconnected domain.

At any point on the imaginary axis, approaching from the left gives limit $-1$, while the assigned value and the right-hand limit are $1$. Thus the function is discontinuous on that axis. Complex differentiability implies continuity, so **$f$ is not holomorphic on $\mathbb C$**. It is holomorphic separately in the two open half-planes.

= Solution
{parent=5}
{c}

The <hyperbolic cosine> is entire and $\cosh0=1$. Continuity makes it nonzero in a neighborhood of zero, so its reciprocal is holomorphic there and has a convergent <Taylor series>. Since $\cosh$ and its reciprocal are even, all odd coefficients vanish.

Multiply the proposed expansion by $\cosh z$:
$$
(1+c_2z^2+c_4z^4+\cdots)(1+\tfrac12z^2+\tfrac1{24}z^4+\cdots)=1.
$$
Coefficient comparison gives $c_2+1/2=0$ and $c_4+c_2/2+1/24=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{c_2=-\tfrac12,\qquad c_4=\tfrac5{24}.}
$$
The zeros of $\cosh z$ solve $e^{2z}=-1$, so they are $z=i(\pi/2+k\pi)$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. The nearest ones are $\pm i\pi/2$, and they are simple because $\sinh$ is nonzero there. The <hyperbolic secant> has genuine poles at these points and is holomorphic in the disk of radius $\pi/2$. The <radius of convergence> of its Taylor series is therefore
$$
\boxed{R=\pi/2.}
$$

{parent=2008}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}
{c}

Let $F(z)=e^{i\lambda z}/(z-i)$. For $\lambda>0$, close the interval $[-R,R]$ by the upper semicircle, counterclockwise. Its only enclosed pole is $z=i$, with <residue> $e^{-\lambda}$. The <residue theorem> gives
$$
\int_{-R}^RF(x)\,dx+\int_{\Gamma_R^+}F(z)\,dz=2\pi i e^{-\lambda}.
$$
On $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $|e^{i\lambda z}|=e^{-\lambda R\sin\theta}$ and $|z-i|\geq R-1$. Hence the arc is bounded by
$$
\frac R{R-1}\int_0^\pi e^{-\lambda R\sin\theta}\,d\theta\longrightarrow0
$$
by the supplied estimate, with parameter $\lambda R$.

For $\lambda<0$, close below, with clockwise orientation. The pole at $i$ lies outside, and the exponential decays there. Parametrizing the lower arc yields the same bound with $|\lambda|R$ in place of $\lambda R$. Its contribution again tends to zero, and no residue remains. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R\frac{e^{i\lambda x}}{x-i}\,dx
=\begin{cases}2\pi i e^{-\lambda},&\lambda>0,\\0,&\lambda<0.\end{cases}}
$$

\Image[2008-fourier-contours.png]
{title=The exponential selects the half-plane in which the closing arc decays}

This is the contour-decay mechanism behind <Jordan's lemma>; the pole contributes only when the decaying closure encloses it.

= Solution
{parent=7}
{c}

Rewrite the linear equation as $(x^2y)'=0$. The initial value gives the unique solution
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x^{-2}\quad(x>0).}
$$
The <Picard iteration> is its integral equation
$$
y_{n+1}(x)=1-2\int_1^x\frac{y_n(t)}t\,dt.
$$
The proposed formula holds for $n=0$. If it holds at $n$, termwise integration of the finite sum and $d(\log t)/dt=1/t$ give
$$
y_{n+1}(x)
=1+\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-2\log x)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}
=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\frac{(-2\log x)^k}{k!}.
$$
This proves the expression by induction. The <exponential series> then gives $y_n(x)\to e^{-2\log x}=x^{-2}$. Convergence is also uniform on every compact subinterval of $(0,\infty)$, since $|\log x|$ is bounded there and the exponential majorant series converges. This is <Picard iteration for a simple-pole linear differential equation>.

{parent=2008}

= Solution
{parent=8}
{c}

Write the principal part as $e^{2y}u_{xx}-u_{yy}$. The <characteristic curves> satisfy
$$
e^{2y}(dy)^2-(dx)^2=0,
$$
or $dx/dy=\pm e^y$. Integration gives $x+e^y=\text{constant}$ and $x-e^y=\text{constant}$. Thus take $\xi=x+e^y$, $\eta=x-e^y$. Their <Jacobian determinant> is $-2e^y\ne0$, and their image is $\xi>\eta$.

For $u(x,y)=U(\xi,\eta)$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
u_{xx}=U_{\xi\xi}+2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta},\qquad
u_y=e^y(U_\xi-U_\eta),
$$
$$
u_{yy}=e^{2y}(U_{\xi\xi}-2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta})
+e^y(U_\xi-U_\eta).
$$
The first-derivative terms cancel exactly. The <canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation> is
$$
4e^{2y}U_{\xi\eta}=0,\qquad U=F(\xi)+G(\eta).
$$
On $y=0$, $F(x+1)+G(x-1)=x$. Differentiating this condition in $x$ and using $u_y=1$ gives
$$
F'(x+1)+G'(x-1)=1,\qquad F'(x+1)-G'(x-1)=1.
$$
Hence $F'=1$ and $G'=0$. Substitution into the value condition fixes their combined constant and yields
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=x+e^y-1.}
$$
Direct substitution verifies the PDE and both initial data. The cancellation illustrates a <hyperbolic equation reduced by an exponential characteristic coordinate>.

Under the usual piecewise smoothness and exponential-order conditions, <integration by parts> in the <Laplace transform> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[x'](p)=p\bar x(p)-x(0).}
$$
The boundary term at infinity vanishes in the transform's half-plane of convergence. The value at zero supplies the initial-data term.

The <convolution theorem for Laplace transforms> states
$$
\boxed{\bar x(p)=\bar y(p)\bar z(p)}
$$
when $x(t)=\int_0^ty(s)z(t-s)\,ds$ and the transforms converge appropriately. One proves it by changing variables $t=s+r$ in the double integral over $0\leq s\leq t<\infty$, which becomes the product domain $s,r\geq0$. Absolute convergence justifies the interchange.

Using $\sin t=(e^{it}-e^{-it})/(2i)$ and integrating the two exponentials for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$,
$$
\mathcal L[\sin t](p)=\frac1{2i}
\left(\frac1{p-i}-\frac1{p+i}\right)
=\boxed{\frac1{p^2+1}.}
$$
This also follows directly by two integrations by parts in the <Laplace transform>.

{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

Applying the derivative rule twice and using both zero initial data gives
$$
(p^2+1)\bar x(p)=\frac1{p^2+1},
$$
so $\bar x(p)=1/(p^2+1)^2$. By the <convolution theorem for Laplace transforms>,
$$
x(t)=\int_0^t\sin s\,\sin(t-s)\,ds.
$$
Use the product-to-sum identity $\sin s\sin(t-s)=\frac12[\cos(2s-t)-\cos t]$ to integrate:
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\tfrac12(\sin t-t\cos t).}
$$
It satisfies $x(0)=x'(0)=0$, and differentiation gives $x''+x=\sin t$. The term proportional to $t\cos t$ is the resonant response of the forced <harmonic oscillator>.
