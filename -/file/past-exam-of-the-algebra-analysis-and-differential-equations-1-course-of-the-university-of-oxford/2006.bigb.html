= Solution
{parent=a}

**Yes.** The matrix is a <cyclic shift operator> on three coordinates. Since $e_1,Ae_1,A^2e_1$ are the three standard <basis vectors>, no <polynomial> of degree below three annihilates $A$. Also $A^3=I$, so its <minimal polynomial> is $X^3-1$. Over the <complex numbers> this has the distinct roots $1,\omega,\omega^2$, where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. The <minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability> therefore proves <diagonalizability>.

Explicitly, $v_\lambda=(1,\lambda^2,\lambda)^{\mathsf T}$ satisfies $Av_\lambda=\lambda v_\lambda$ when $\lambda^3=1$. These three <eigenvectors> form an <eigenbasis>. This is a special case of <diagonalization of a cyclic shift over a field>.

= Solution

**No.** The same cyclic-vector argument gives <minimal polynomial> $X^3-1=(X-1)(X^2+X+1)$ over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_2$. The quadratic has no root in $\mathbb F_2$: its value is one at both zero and one. Thus the <minimal polynomial> does not split into linear factors over the specified <field>, violating the <minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability>. It becomes diagonalizable over a suitable larger <finite field>, but that is a different question.

= Solution

**No.** In <characteristic three>, $X^3-1=(X-1)^3$. This is still the <minimal polynomial>, because the three cyclic <basis vectors> remain independent. Its repeated factor violates the <minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability>. Equivalently, the only possible <eigenvalue> is one; a diagonalizable matrix with that sole <eigenvalue> would be the <identity matrix>, whereas this matrix is not the identity.

= Solution

**Yes.** In $\mathbb F_7$, the <minimal polynomial> splits as
$$
X^3-1=(X-1)(X-2)(X-4).
$$
The three factors are distinct, so the <minimal polynomial criterion for diagonalizability> applies. For example the <eigenvectors> $v_\lambda=(1,\lambda^2,\lambda)^{\mathsf T}$ for $\lambda=1,2,4$ give an <eigenbasis> over this <finite field>. The contrast with characteristic three is that the shift polynomial now splits without repeated roots.

{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

Construct the vectors inductively by the <Gram-Schmidt process>, proving its needed properties directly. Suppose $w_1,\ldots,w_{j-1}$ form an <orthonormal basis> of $U_{j-1}$, and put
$$
r_j=u_j-\sum_{k<j}\langle u_j,w_k\rangle w_k.
$$
For every $i<j$, taking an <inner product> with $w_i$ gives $\langle r_j,w_i\rangle=0$. Also $r_j\ne0$, because otherwise $u_j$ would lie in the <linear span> of its predecessors, contrary to <linear independence>. Define
$$
\boxed{w_j=r_j/\|r_j\|.}
$$
It lies in $U_j$, has unit <norm>, and is orthogonal to every $u_i$ with $i<j$, since those vectors lie in $U_{j-1}$. Writing $u_j=r_j+\sum_{k<j}\langle u_j,w_k\rangle w_k$ gives $\langle w_j,u_j\rangle=\|r_j\|>0$. The same identity shows that $w_1,\ldots,w_j$ span $U_j$, completing the induction, including the empty sum at $j=1$.

For uniqueness, any candidate $w\in U_j$ expands in the basis $w_1,\ldots,w_{j-1},r_j$. Orthogonality to every earlier $u_i$, hence to every earlier $w_i$, makes its first $j-1$ coefficients zero. Thus $w=tr_j$. Unit norm forces $t=\pm1/\|r_j\|$, and $\langle w,u_j\rangle>0$ forces the positive sign. This proves <uniqueness in the Gram-Schmidt process with positive normalization> without assuming a projection theorem or any other major orthogonalization result.

The <evaluation homomorphism> $\mathbb R[X]\to\mathbb R$, $p\mapsto p(0)$, is surjective and has <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $J_1=(X)$. Hence $J_1$ is an <ideal>, and the <first isomorphism theorem for rings> gives $\boxed{\mathbb R[X]/J_1\cong\mathbb R}$. Since this quotient is a <field>, $\boxed{J_1\text{ is maximal}}$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**It is not an ideal.** The constant <polynomial> one belongs to $J_2$, but its product with $X$ does not: the <derivative> of $X$ at zero is one. Thus $J_2$ fails closure under multiplication by arbitrary elements of $\mathbb R[X]$, a required <ideal> property. Closure under addition alone would not suffice.

The conditions set the constant and linear <coefficients> to zero, so $\boxed{J_3=(X^2)}$. This is a <principal ideal>, but it is **not maximal**: there is a strict chain $(X^2)\subsetneq(X)\subsetneq\mathbb R[X]$. In the <quotient ring>, the nonzero class of $X$ has square zero, so the quotient is not a <field>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Here $J_4=(X^2+1)$ is a <principal ideal>. The <evaluation homomorphism> $p\mapsto p(i)$ from $\mathbb R[X]$ onto $\mathbb C$ has this <kernel of a ring homomorphism>: division by $X^2+1$ leaves a remainder $a+bX$, and $a+bi=0$ with real $a,b$ forces both to vanish. Surjectivity follows because every complex number is $a+bi$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R[X]/J_4\cong\mathbb C,\qquad J_4\text{ is maximal}.}
$$
Equivalently, $X^2+1$ is an <irreducible polynomial> over the <real numbers>.

{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=4}

A <closed curve> has $\gamma(a)=\gamma(b)$. A <simple curve> has no repeated points apart from the permitted equality of its two endpoints when it is closed. A <smooth curve>, in the regular contour-integration convention, has a continuous nonzero tangent $\gamma'$; piecewise smooth contours are treated by adding the integrals over their smooth pieces. For a continuous integrand, its <contour integral> is defined by
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt.}
$$
For the positively oriented circle, $\gamma'(t)=ire^{it}$ and $(\gamma(t)-z_0)^n=r^ne^{int}$. Direct parameter integration therefore gives
$$
\int_\gamma(z-z_0)^n\,dz=ir^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)t}\,dt
=\boxed{\begin{cases}2\pi i,&n=-1,\\0,&n\ne-1.\end{cases}}
$$
For $n\ne-1$, an elementary <antiderivative> is $e^{i(n+1)t}/[i(n+1)]$ and its endpoint values agree because $n$ is an integer. For $n=-1$, the integrand of the parameter integral is the constant $i$. No integral theorem is needed.

{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5}

Apply the <residue theorem> to $F(z)=(z^2+4)^{-2}$ on the upper semicircle of radius $R>2$, traversed counterclockwise. Its sole enclosed <pole> is the double pole at $2i$, and
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=2i}F
=\left.\frac d{dz}(z+2i)^{-2}\right|_{z=2i}
=-\frac2{(4i)^3}=-\frac i{32}.
$$
On the semicircular arc, $|z^2+4|\ge R^2-4$, so the <ML inequality> bounds its contribution by $\pi R/(R^2-4)^2\to0$. The real integral is absolutely convergent, since its integrand is $O(|x|^{-4})$ at infinity. Taking $R\to\infty$ in the contour identity yields
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{(x^2+4)^2}=2\pi i\left(-\frac i{32}\right)=\frac\pi{16}.}
$$

= 6
{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}

A <holomorphic function> with $f'(z)\ne0$ at every point is locally a <conformal map>. Write $a=z_0$. Since $|a|<1$ and $|z|<1$, $1-\overline az\ne0$, and
$$
\boxed{f'(z)=\frac{|a|^2-1}{(1-\overline az)^2}\ne0.}
$$
Thus the specified <Möbius transformation> is conformal throughout the disk. Expanding the two squared absolute values gives
$$
\boxed{1-|f(z)|^2=\frac{(1-|a|^2)(1-|z|^2)}{|1-\overline az|^2}>0,}
$$
so $f$ maps the <open unit disk> into itself. Solving $w=(a-z)/(1-\overline az)$ gives $z=(a-w)/(1-\overline aw)=f(w)$. Equivalently $f(f(z))=z$. Hence $f$ is its own inverse, proving both <injectivity> and <surjectivity> on the disk. Nonvanishing derivative establishes the local angle-preserving property; the explicit inverse supplies the separate global bijection claim.

{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=7}

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> first gives $y'(x)=1+\int_0^x f(y(s))\,ds$. Integrating again, or differentiating the proposed expression twice and checking its initial values, yields the <Volterra integral equation>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x+\int_0^x(x-s)f(y(s))\,ds.}
$$
For $f(y)=-y$, the <initial value problem> is solved by $\boxed{y(x)=\sin x}$. Its <Picard iteration> constructs the sine series directly. The base approximation is $y_0(x)=x$. If
$$
y_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!},
$$
then elementary integration gives
$$
\int_0^x(x-s)\frac{s^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\,ds=\frac{x^{2k+3}}{(2k+3)!}.
$$
Substituting this finite sum in the next update, then shifting its index, proves the same formula with $n+1$ in place of $n$. This is the required <mathematical induction>. The resulting partial sums converge to $\boxed{\sin x}$ for every real $x$, and uniformly on every bounded interval: the absolute terms on $|x|\le R$ are bounded by the convergent series $\sum_kR^{2k+1}/(2k+1)!$.

The printed update lists $n=1,2,\ldots$, which would leave $y_1$ unspecified if read literally. The usual <Picard iteration>, and the requested induction from $y_0$, use the update starting at $n=0$; that intended convention is used here.

{parent=2006}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=8}

Write the equation as $Ly=f$ with $L=-d^2/dx^2+1$. Homogeneous solutions vanishing at the left and right endpoints are $u_L(x)=\sinh(1+x)$ and $u_R(x)=\sinh(1-x)$. Their <Wronskian> is $u_Lu_R'-u_L'u_R=-\sinh2$. The <Dirichlet Green kernel for minus second derivative plus one> is therefore
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=\frac{\sinh(1+\min(x,s))\sinh(1-\max(x,s))}{\sinh2}.}
$$
It vanishes at $x=\pm1$, is continuous at $x=s$, and its first derivative has jump $-1$. Thus $LG(\cdot,s)=\delta_s$ in the <distributional derivative> sense, with the sign appropriate to $L=-d^2/dx^2+1$. Integration against $f$ gives the required boundary-value solution. The kernel is positive and symmetric, as is also apparent in the figure.

For the specified step forcing only $0\le s\le1$ contributes at $x=0$, giving
$$
y(0)=\frac{\sinh1}{\sinh2}\int_0^1\sinh(1-s)\,ds
=\frac{\sinh1(\cosh1-1)}{2\sinh1\cosh1}
=\boxed{\frac{\cosh1-1}{2\cosh1}.}
$$
The discontinuity in $f$ gives a piecewise twice-differentiable solution with continuous value and first derivative, rather than a jump in $y$ itself.

= Solution
{parent=9}

The <characteristic equation of a partial differential equation> for $u_{xx}+u_{xy}=0$ is $(dy)^2-dx\,dy=0$. Thus the two <characteristic curve> families are $\boxed{y=\text{constant},\quad y-x=\text{constant}}$. Set $\xi=y$ and $\eta=y-x$. The <chain rule> gives $\partial_x=-\partial_\eta$ and $\partial_y=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta$, reducing the equation to
$$
\boxed{U_{\xi\eta}=0.}
$$
Integrating in the two independent characteristic coordinates gives $U=F(\xi)+G(\eta)$, or $u(x,y)=F(y)+G(y-x)$. At $x=0$, the value condition gives $F(y)+G(y)=0$, while the derivative condition gives $-G'(y)=e^y$. Hence $G(y)=-e^y+C$ and $F(y)=e^y-C$, so
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=e^y-e^{y-x}.}
$$
Direct differentiation gives $u_x=e^{y-x}$, $u_{xx}=-e^{y-x}$ and $u_{xy}=e^{y-x}$, verifying both the equation and the two data. The additive constants cancel in $u$, so they do not create different solutions.
