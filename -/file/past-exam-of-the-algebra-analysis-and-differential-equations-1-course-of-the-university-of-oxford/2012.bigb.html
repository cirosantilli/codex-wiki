The <dual space> consists of the <linear functionals> on the original <vector space>, with addition and scalar multiplication defined pointwise:
$$
\boxed{V'=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F).}
$$
The <dual map> acts by <function composition> with $T$:
$$
\boxed{T':V'\longrightarrow U',\qquad T'(\lambda)=\lambda\circ T.}
$$
For $u\in U$, its value is $(T'\lambda)(u)=\lambda(Tu)$. The composite is a <linear functional> because both $T$ and $\lambda$ are <linear maps>. Moreover $T'(a\lambda+b\mu)=aT'\lambda+bT'\mu$, so the <dual map> is itself a <linear map>. Its direction is reversed because a measurement on the output of $T$ becomes a measurement on its input.

The <annihilator of a vector subspace> consists of the <linear functionals> that vanish on every element of that <vector subspace>:
$$
\boxed{W^0=\{\lambda\in V':\lambda(w)=0\text{ for every }w\in W\},\qquad
\dim W^0=\dim V-\dim W.}
$$
For the <dimension> identity, choose a <basis> $w_1,\ldots,w_k$ of $W$ and use <basis extension> to obtain $w_1,\ldots,w_n$ for $V$. A <linear functional> is determined by its values on these $n$ <basis vectors>. Belonging to the <annihilator of a vector subspace> sets its first $k$ values to zero and leaves its last $n-k$ values arbitrary. Equivalently the last $n-k$ elements of the <dual basis> form a <basis> of $W^0$.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For a <linear functional> $\lambda\in V'$, the definition of the <dual map> gives
$$
\lambda\in\ker T'
\iff \lambda(Tu)=0\text{ for every }u\in U
\iff \lambda|_{\operatorname{im}T}=0.
$$
Thus the <kernel of a linear map> is precisely the <annihilator of a vector subspace>
$$
\boxed{\ker T'=(\operatorname{im}T)^0.}
$$
Write $n=\dim U$, $m=\dim V$ and $r=\dim\operatorname{im}T$. The <rank-nullity theorem>, the <dimension> formula for an <annihilator of a vector subspace>, and $\dim V'=m$ give
$$
\dim\operatorname{im}T'=m-\dim\ker T'=m-(m-r)=r.
$$
Applying the same <dimension> formula in $U$ gives
$$
\dim(\ker T)^0=n-\dim\ker T=r.
$$
Finally, if $\alpha=T'\lambda$ and $u\in\ker T$, then $\alpha(u)=\lambda(Tu)=\lambda(0)=0$. Therefore $\operatorname{im}T'\subseteq(\ker T)^0$. These are finite-dimensional <vector subspaces> of equal <dimension>, so inclusion is equality:
$$
\boxed{\dim\operatorname{im}T'=\dim(\ker T)^0,\qquad
\operatorname{im}T'=(\ker T)^0.}
$$
This is the <dual image and kernel annihilator identity>.

A <diagonalizable matrix> over the <field> $F$ admits an <invertible matrix> $P$ with entries in $F$ such that
$$
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=D\quad\text{with }D\text{ a diagonal matrix over }F.}
$$
Equivalently, the <vector space> $F^n$ has a <basis> of <eigenvectors> of $A$. Indeed, $AP=PD$ says that each column of $P$ is an <eigenvector> with the corresponding diagonal entry as its <eigenvalue>. Requiring the <basis> and <eigenvalues> to lie in $F$ makes the choice of <field> essential.

Expansion down the last column gives the <characteristic polynomial>
$$
\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^2-\lambda-1).
$$
The quadratic <polynomial> has <roots> $(1\pm\sqrt5)/2$, which are not <rational numbers>. For example, the <rational root theorem> restricts any rational root of this monic integer <polynomial> to $1$ or $-1$, and neither works. If $A$ had a <diagonalization> over $\mathbb Q$, its <characteristic polynomial> would be a product of linear factors with <rational numbers> as their <roots>. It is not, so **$A$ is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb Q$**.

Over the <complex numbers>, the <eigenvalues>
$$
1,\qquad \lambda_+=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad
\lambda_-=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}
$$
are distinct. Thus <distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizability>, and **$A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$**, in fact already over $\mathbb R$.

An explicit <eigenbasis> is
$$
e_3=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v_+=\begin{pmatrix}1\\\lambda_+\\\lambda_++1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v_-=\begin{pmatrix}1\\\lambda_-\\\lambda_-+1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For either quadratic <root> $\lambda$, the identity $\lambda^2=\lambda+1$ verifies $Av_\lambda=\lambda v_\lambda$. Taking these three columns as $P$ gives $P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(1,\lambda_+,\lambda_-)$. Their distinct <eigenvalues> ensure <linear independence>.

In the <finite field> $\mathbb F_5=\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$, the <characteristic polynomial> becomes
$$
\chi_A(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)^2,
$$
because $(\lambda-3)^2=\lambda^2-\lambda-1$ modulo $5$. Splitting into linear factors does not by itself guarantee <diagonalizability>: the repeated <eigenvalue> needs enough <eigenvectors>.

Solving $(A-3I)(x,y,z)^T=0$ gives
$$
2x+y=0,\qquad x+3y=0,\qquad y+3z=0,
$$
so $y=3x$ and $z=4x$. Hence
$$
\ker(A-3I)=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_5}\{(1,3,4)^T\}
$$
has <dimension> one, although $3$ has <algebraic multiplicity> two. The <geometric multiplicity> is too small, and **$A$ is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb F_5$**. This illustrates <diagonalizability depends on the field>: characteristic zero gave three distinct <eigenvalues>, while reduction modulo $5$ merges two of them.

A <prime ideal> is a proper <ideal> $I\ne R$ such that $ab\in I$ implies $a\in I$ or $b\in I$. A <maximal ideal> is a proper <ideal> $I\ne R$ such that there is no <ideal> strictly between $I$ and $R$.

Every <ideal> of the <integers> has the form $(n)=n\mathbb Z$ for a unique nonnegative integer $n$. Its <prime ideals> and <maximal ideals> are respectively
$$
\boxed{\{(0)\}\cup\{(p):p\text{ prime}\},\qquad
\{(p):p\text{ prime}\}.}
$$
The zero <ideal> is a <prime ideal> because the <integers> form an <integral domain>. For a <prime number> $p$, divisibility of $ab$ by $p$ forces divisibility of one factor, so $(p)$ is a <prime ideal>. Also any <ideal> $(d)$ containing $(p)$ has $d\mid p$, so it equals $(p)$ or $(1)$; thus $(p)$ is a <maximal ideal>.

If $n>1$ is composite, write $n=ab$ with $1<a,b<n$. Then $ab\in(n)$ while neither factor belongs to $(n)$, so $(n)$ is not a <prime ideal>. A <prime number> $p\mid n$ gives $(n)\subsetneq(p)\subsetneq\mathbb Z$, so $(n)$ is not a <maximal ideal> either. The zero <ideal> is not maximal since $(0)\subsetneq(2)\subsetneq\mathbb Z$, and $(1)=\mathbb Z$ is not proper.

{c}

If the <quotient ring> $R/I$ is an <integral domain>, then $I\ne R$, because an <integral domain> has $1\ne0$. For $ab\in I$,
$$
(a+I)(b+I)=0+I.
$$
There are no nonzero <zero divisors> in an <integral domain>, so $a+I=0+I$ or $b+I=0+I$. Thus $a\in I$ or $b\in I$, proving **$I$ is a prime ideal**.

If $R/I$ is a <field>, again $I$ is proper. Let $J$ be an <ideal> properly containing $I$, and choose $a\in J\setminus I$. The nonzero element $a+I$ has a <multiplicative inverse> $b+I$, so $ab-1\in I\subset J$. Since $ab\in J$, subtraction gives $1\in J$, hence $J=R$. Therefore **$I$ is a maximal ideal**. These arguments establish the relevant <prime ideal quotient criterion> and <maximal ideal quotient criterion>.

Set $X=0$ to define a surjective <ring homomorphism>
$$
E:\mathbb Q[X,Y]\longrightarrow\mathbb Q[Y],\qquad E(P)=P(0,Y).
$$
Its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is exactly $(X)$: the constant coefficient in $X$ must vanish, so every remaining monomial is divisible by $X$. The <first isomorphism theorem for rings> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q[X,Y]/(X)\cong\mathbb Q[Y].}
$$
The <polynomial ring> $\mathbb Q[Y]$ is an <integral domain>, because the product of two nonzero <polynomials> has nonzero leading coefficient. It is not a <field>: $Y$ has no polynomial <multiplicative inverse>, since a nonzero product $YQ(Y)$ has positive <degree of a polynomial>. Consequently **$(X)$ is prime and is not maximal**. Concretely, $(X)\subsetneq(X,Y)\subsetneq\mathbb Q[X,Y]$ is a strict chain of <ideals>.

A subset $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is an <open set> if every $x\in\Omega$ admits a radius $r>0$ such that the <open ball>
$$
B(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb R^n:|y-x|<r\}
$$
is contained in $\Omega$. A subset $F\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is a <closed set> if its <complement> $\mathbb R^n\setminus F$ is an <open set>. Equivalently, $F$ contains the <limit> of every <convergent sequence> of its points.

The <distance to a set> is nonnegative and finite here: any fixed $y_0\in F$ gives $0\leq f(x)\leq|x-y_0|$. For every $y\in F$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
|x-y|\leq|x-x'|+|x'-y|.
$$
Taking the <infimum> over $y\in F$ yields $f(x)\leq|x-x'|+f(x')$. Interchanging $x$ and $x'$ proves
$$
\boxed{|f(x)-f(x')|\leq|x-x'|.}
$$
Thus $f$ is a <Lipschitz function> with <Lipschitz constant> one, so it is <continuous>. In the epsilon-delta definition of <continuity>, choose $\delta=\varepsilon$.

If $x\in F$, using $y=x$ in the <infimum> gives $0\leq f(x)\leq0$, so $f(x)=0$.

Conversely, if $f(x)=0$, the definition of the <infimum> allows a choice $y_j\in F$ with $|x-y_j|<1/j$. The <sequence> $y_j$ is therefore a <convergent sequence> with <limit> $x$. Since $F$ is a <closed set>, its <limit> $x$ belongs to $F$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f(x)=0\iff x\in F.}
$$
Closedness matters: without it, zero <distance to a set> characterizes its <closure>, which can contain additional points.

= Solution

Put $F=\mathbb R^n\setminus\Omega$. This is a <closed set>. First assume $F$ is nonempty. The <Heine-Borel theorem> makes the nonempty bounded <closed set> $K$ a <compact set>. By the preceding parts, $x\mapsto d(x,F)$ is a <continuous function> and is strictly positive at every $x\in K$, because $K\subset\Omega$.

The <extreme value theorem> gives $x_*\in K$ attaining its minimum. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{dist}(K,F)=\min_{x\in K}d(x,F)=d(x_*,F)>0.}
$$
The equality with the <distance between two sets> follows by taking the two <infima> successively. Crucially, <compactness> supplies a point attaining the minimum; positivity at each point alone would not make an arbitrary <infimum> positive. This is the principle that a <compact subset of an open set has positive distance from its complement>.

If $\Omega=\mathbb R^n$, its <complement> is empty. With the usual extended-real convention $\inf\varnothing=+\infty$, the stated inequality holds in that case too.

A <holomorphic function> on the <open set> $\Omega$ is <complex differentiable> at every point of $\Omega$: the <limit>
$$
f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0,\ h\in\mathbb C}\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h
$$
exists independently of the direction of approach. Write $f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
To prove necessity, approach first with real $h$, obtaining $f'(z)=u_x+iv_x$. Approach instead with $h=it$ for real $t$, obtaining
$$
f'(z)=\frac1i(u_y+iv_y)=v_y-iu_y.
$$
Equality of real and imaginary parts yields the displayed <Cauchy-Riemann equations>.

A sufficient condition is that $u$ and $v$ have continuous first <partial derivatives> throughout $\Omega$ and satisfy the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> there. Indeed, real <differentiability> gives, for $h=a+ib$,
$$
f(z+h)-f(z)=f_xa+f_yb+o(|h|).
$$
The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> imply $f_y=if_x$, so this is $f_xh+o(|h|)$. Dividing by $h$ proves <complex differentiability> and $f'(z)=f_x(z)$ at every point. Merely having first <partial derivatives> and the equations at a point, without a suitable differentiability hypothesis, would not justify this argument.

For the <continuously differentiable function> $f=u+iv$, the specified <Wirtinger derivative> is
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}
=\frac12\bigl(u_x+iv_x+i(u_y+iv_y)\bigr)
=\frac12\bigl((u_x-v_y)+i(v_x+u_y)\bigr).
$$
A <complex number> vanishes exactly when both real and imaginary parts vanish. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z}=0
\iff u_x=v_y\text{ and }u_y=-v_x.}
$$
Thus the two real <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are one complex equation expressed by the <Wirtinger derivative>.

The <continuously differentiable function> $z\bar z=x^2+y^2$ is a particular solution, since
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar z}(x^2+y^2)
=\frac12(2x+i,2y)=z.

Conversely, every <entire function> $h$ has vanishing <Wirtinger derivative>, so addition of $h$ preserves the prescribed derivative. Therefore all solutions are
$$
\boxed{f(z)=|z|^2+h(z),\qquad h\text{ any entire function}.}
$$
This is an <inhomogeneous Wirtinger equation with holomorphic forcing>: a particular solution plus an arbitrary element of the homogeneous solution space.

A <conformal map> preserves oriented <angles> between intersecting smooth curves at each point, with a nonzero local scale factor. A sufficient criterion is that $f$ be a <holomorphic function> on the <domain> and that $f'(z)\ne0$ everywhere there. Its first-order action then multiplies every tangent direction by the same nonzero <complex number>, combining a rotation and a dilation. A conformal bijection additionally requires <injectivity> and <surjectivity> onto the target.

For $z=x+iy$ in the given half-strip,
$$
w=e^{iz}=e^{-y}(\cos x+i\sin x).
$$
Thus $0<|w|<1$ and $0<\arg w<\pi$. Conversely every such $w$ has a unique argument in $(0,\pi)$ and unique radius in $(0,1)$, giving $x=\arg w$, $y=-\log|w|$. Hence
$$
\boxed{e^{iS}=D_+=\{w:|w|<1,\ \operatorname{Im}w>0\}.}
$$
The <complex exponential> is <injective> on $S$, and its <derivative> $ie^{iz}$ never vanishes.

On $D_+$, use the <Joukowski map> with a sign change,
$$
J(w)=-\left(w+\frac1w\right).
$$
Since
$$
\operatorname{Im}J(w)=\operatorname{Im}w\left(\frac1{|w|^2}-1\right)>0,
$$
it maps into the <upper half-plane>. To prove <injectivity>, $J(w_1)=J(w_2)$ gives
$$
(w_1-w_2)\left(1-\frac1{w_1w_2}\right)=0.
$$
Both moduli are below one, so $w_1w_2\ne1$ and $w_1=w_2$.

For <surjectivity>, take any $\zeta$ in the <upper half-plane> and solve $w^2+\zeta w+1=0$. The two <roots> have product one. Neither can lie on the <unit circle>, since there $-(w+w^{-1})$ is real. Exactly one <root> therefore has modulus below one. For this <root>, the displayed imaginary-part identity forces $\operatorname{Im}w>0$, so it belongs to $D_+$. Finally $J'(w)=w^{-2}-1$ has no <zero> in $D_+$. This proves the <Joukowski map from the upper half-disc to the upper half-plane> is a conformal bijection.

Composing gives the required <conformal map>
$$
\boxed{F(z)=J(e^{iz})=-e^{iz}-e^{-iz}=-2\cos z:\ S\longrightarrow H.}
$$
Its <derivative> $2\sin z$ is nonzero on $S$, since the <zeros> of the complex <sine> are the real integer multiples of $\pi$.

Near $x=0$, <separation of variables> gives $dy/y=dx/(1-x)$, so $\log|y|=-\log(1-x)+C$. The <initial condition> selects
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac1{1-x},\qquad -\infty<x<1.}
$$
Direct substitution verifies both the <ordinary differential equation> and the <initial condition>. The endpoint $x=1$ is excluded because the coefficient and this solution are singular there; the displayed interval is the maximal connected real interval containing the initial point.

For $|x|\leq h<1$ and any two admissible values $y,\widetilde y$,
$$
|f(x,y)-f(x,\widetilde y)|
=\frac{|y-\widetilde y|}{|1-x|}
\leq\frac1{1-h}|y-\widetilde y|.
$$
This is a <Lipschitz condition> in the dependent variable, uniform in $x$, with <Lipschitz constant> $L=(1-h)^{-1}$. Also $|f(x,y)|\leq(1+k)/(1-h)$ on the prescribed rectangle, so the usual local hypotheses of the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> hold.

Integrating from the initial point gives the <integral equation>
$$
y(x)=1+\int_0^x\frac{y(t)}{1-t}\,dt.
$$
Conversely, differentiating this equation using the <fundamental theorem of calculus> recovers the <initial value problem>. Start <Picard iteration> with the constant initial approximation $y_0(x)=1$ and set
$$
y_{n+1}(x)=1+\int_0^x\frac{y_n(t)}{1-t}\,dt.
$$
The first three approximations are
$$
\boxed{y_0=1,\qquad y_1=1-\log(1-x),\qquad
y_2=1-\log(1-x)+\frac12\log^2(1-x).}
$$
To prove the general expression by <mathematical induction>, put $\ell(x)=\log(1-x)$. The initial approximation gives the formula for $n=0$. If it holds for $n$, then the <substitution rule>
$$
\int_0^x\frac{\ell(t)^k}{1-t}\,dt=-\frac{\ell(x)^{k+1}}{k+1}
$$
gives
$$
y_{n+1}(x)=1+\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^{k+1}\ell(x)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}
=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\frac{(-1)^k\ell(x)^k}{k!}.
$$
Thus the formula follows at every stage.

For every fixed real $x<1$, these are partial sums of the <exponential series> evaluated at the finite real number $-\log(1-x)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}y_n(x)=\exp(-\log(1-x))=\frac1{1-x}
\quad\text{for every }x<1.}
$$
This range is larger than $|x|<1$: the expansion is in powers of $\log(1-x)$, not a <Taylor series> in $x$. For example it converges at $x=-2$. It also converges uniformly on every <compact interval> contained in $(-\infty,1)$, because $|\log(1-x)|$ is bounded there and the <exponential series> supplies a uniform summable bound. At $x=1$ the approximations are undefined, while for $x>1$ the real integral from zero crosses the singularity. This is <Picard iteration for a simple-pole linear differential equation>.

For a second-order <linear partial differential equation> with principal part $Au_{xx}+2Bu_{xy}+Cu_{yy}$, the discriminant is $B^2-AC$. Here $A=1$, $B=0$, $C=-x^2$, so
$$
\boxed{B^2-AC=x^2>0\quad(x>0).}
$$
The equation is therefore a <hyperbolic partial differential equation> throughout its domain. The lower-order term $-u_x/x$ does not affect this classification.

The <characteristic curves> satisfy
$$
A(dy)^2-2B\,dx\,dy+C(dx)^2=0,
\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\pm x.
$$
Integrating gives the two families $y\mp x^2/2=\text{constant}$. Choose coordinates constant along these families:
$$
\xi=x^2-2y,\qquad\eta=x^2+2y.
$$
Their <Jacobian determinant> is $8x\ne0$, so they define an invertible <change of variables> on $x>0$, with image $\xi+\eta>0$. Write $u(x,y)=U(\xi,\eta)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=2x(U_\xi+U_\eta),\\
u_{xx}&=2(U_\xi+U_\eta)+4x^2(U_{\xi\xi}+2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta}),\\
u_{yy}&=4(U_{\xi\xi}-2U_{\xi\eta}+U_{\eta\eta}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Both the pure second <partial derivatives> and the first <partial derivatives> cancel in the original equation, leaving
$$
16x^2U_{\xi\eta}=0,\qquad\boxed{U_{\xi\eta}=0.}
$$
This is the full <canonical form of a second-order partial differential equation>, including the effect of the lower-order term. Integrating shows $U_\xi$ is a function of $\xi$ alone, hence
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=f(x^2-2y)+g(x^2+2y),}
$$
with arbitrary twice <differentiable functions> $f,g$. The representation is global on $x>0$: for each fixed $\xi$, the allowed $\eta>-\xi$ form an interval, so $U_\xi$ is independent of $\eta$ throughout that interval; after subtracting its antiderivative, the same reasoning in the other coordinate leaves only $g(\eta)$. These are <quadratic characteristic coordinates for a variable-speed wave equation>.

For the data on $x=1$, set $s=1-2y$, so the other argument is $2-s$. The two <initial conditions> become
$$
f(s)+g(2-s)=1,\qquad 2\bigl(f'(s)+g'(2-s)\bigr)=2.
$$
Differentiating the first equation with respect to $s$ also gives $f'(s)-g'(2-s)=0$. Solving these two linear equations yields $f'(s)=g'(2-s)=1/2$. Since $s$ ranges over all real numbers,
$$
f(s)=\frac{s}{2}+C,\qquad g(t)=\frac{t}{2}-C.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=x^2.}
$$
Indeed $u_{xx}=2$, $u_{yy}=0$, $u_x=2x$, so the equation and both <initial conditions> hold directly.

For the <Laplace transform of a derivative>, first apply <integration by parts> on a finite interval:
$$
\int_0^T e^{-pt}\dot x(t)\,dt
=e^{-pT}x(T)-x(0)+p\int_0^T e^{-pt}x(t)\,dt.
$$
Under the usual transform hypotheses, including sufficient regularity for <integration by parts>, existence of the derivative's <Laplace transform>, and $e^{-pT}x(T)\to0$, passing to the <limit> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\{\dot x\}(p)=p\bar x(p)-x(0).}
$$
These conditions hold, for example, for a continuously differentiable $x$ with $x$ and $\dot x$ of <exponential order>, in a sufficiently far right half-plane. Existence of $\bar x(p)$ alone does not establish all of these derivative-transform hypotheses; the finite-interval identity displays exactly which boundary term must disappear. The polynomial solution in the last part satisfies them for every $\operatorname{Re}p>0$.

For $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, the <complex exponential> $e^{-pT}$ tends to zero, since its modulus is $e^{-T\operatorname{Re}p}$. Therefore direct <integration> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\{1\}(p)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1-e^{-pT}}p=\frac1p.}
$$
The condition on the real part also gives absolute convergence of this <improper integral>.

Write $I_n(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}t^n\,dt$. For $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, <exponential decay> dominates every fixed power of $t$, so the <improper integral> converges absolutely and $e^{-pT}T^n\to0$. For $n\geq1$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
I_n(p)=\left[-\frac{e^{-pt}t^n}p\right]_0^\infty
+\frac np\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}t^{n-1}\,dt
=\frac np I_{n-1}(p).
$$
The preceding part gives the base case $I_0(p)=1/p$. Repeated application, or <mathematical induction>, yields
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L\{t^n\}(p)=\frac{n!}{p^{n+1}},\qquad \operatorname{Re}p>0.}
$$

Taking the <Laplace transform>, using <linearity> and the preceding three formulas, gives
$$
p\bar x(p)-x(0)-\bar x(p)=\frac2{p^2}-\frac2{p^3}
=\frac{2(p-1)}{p^3}.
$$
Since $x(0)=0$, division by $p-1$ gives $\bar x(p)=2/p^3$ for $p\ne1$ in the domain of convergence. There is no genuine singularity at $p=1$: <continuity> of the <Laplace transform> there supplies the same value. One can also first work with $\operatorname{Re}p>1$, where division is harmless.

The formula in part (iii) identifies $2/p^3$ as the <Laplace transform> of $t^2$. By <uniqueness of the Laplace transform>,
$$
\boxed{\bar x(p)=\frac2{p^3},\qquad x(t)=t^2\quad(t\geq0).}
$$
As a direct check, $\dot x-x=2t-t^2$ and $x(0)=0$. Any two solutions of this <initial value problem> differ by $Ce^t$ and have the same initial value, forcing $C=0$. This also verifies uniqueness independently of transform inversion.
