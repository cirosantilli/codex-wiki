= Solution
{parent=1}
{c}

The zero <polynomial> belongs to $I_v$. If $f,g\in I_v$, linearity gives $(f-g)(T)v=0$. If $a\in K[X]$ and $f\in I_v$, <polynomial> evaluation obeys $(af)(T)=a(T)f(T)$, so $(af)(T)v=0$. Thus $I_v$ is an <ideal> of the <polynomial ring> $K[X]$.

The $n+1$ vectors $v,Tv,\ldots,T^nv$ are linearly dependent in the $n$-dimensional <vector space>. Their dependence supplies a nonzero <polynomial> of <degree> at most $n$ in $I_v$. Hence this <ideal> is nonzero. A <polynomial> ring over a <field> is a <Euclidean domain> under <degree>, and every <ideal> in a Euclidean domain is principal. It follows that $I_v=(m_v)$ for a nonzero <polynomial> $m_v$, which can be scaled uniquely to be monic.

For completeness, uniqueness follows because any two generators divide each other and hence differ by a nonzero constant; the monic normalization forces that constant to be one. Since $m_v$ divides the <polynomial> of <degree> at most $n$ constructed above, its <degree> is at most $n$. The generator characterization gives
$$
\boxed{m_v\mid f\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\in I_v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(T)v=0.}
$$
This is the <minimal polynomial of a vector under a linear operator>. If $v=0$, the <ideal> is the whole ring and the unique monic generator is the constant <polynomial> one.

{parent=2009}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}
{c}

A <self-adjoint operator> satisfies $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$ for every $x,y$. The finite-dimensional <finite-dimensional spectral theorem> gives an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors> and real <eigenvalues>; <eigenvectors> for distinct <eigenvalues> are orthogonal. Consequently a matrix represents a self-adjoint transformation in some, possibly nonorthonormal, basis exactly when it is <diagonalizable> with real <eigenvalues>.

For $A_0$, the <eigenvalues> are $2,1,0$, all distinct and real. Eigenvectors are $e_1$, $e_2$, and $(0,-1,1)^T$. Thus **$A_0$ can represent a self-adjoint transformation** after choosing a suitable basis.

For $A_1$, the eigenvalue one has algebraic multiplicity two but its <eigenspace> has dimension one. The lower $2\times2$ block is a nontrivial <Jordan block>, so the matrix is not <diagonalizable>. Hence **$A_1$ cannot represent a self-adjoint transformation** in any basis.

For $A_2$, the <eigenspace> for two is spanned by $e_1$ and $(0,1,1)^T$, and the eigenvalue-one <eigenspace> is spanned by $e_2$. These three vectors form an <eigenbasis>. Therefore **$A_2$ can represent a self-adjoint transformation**. To see sufficiency on the given <inner product space>, first define the real diagonal transformation in an <orthonormal basis> and then apply the inverse of a diagonalizing basis change to obtain the requested matrix. Symmetry of the displayed matrix itself would be required only for an orthonormal representing basis.

{parent=2009}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=3}
{c}

A cubic over a <field> is reducible exactly when it has a <root> in that <field>. For $p(X)=X^3+2X^2+1$ modulo five, direct evaluation gives
$$
\bigl(p(0),p(1),p(2),p(3),p(4)\bigr)=(1,4,2,1,2).
$$
No value is zero, so **$p$ is irreducible in $\mathbb Z_5[X]$**.

The integer <polynomial> in the second part reduces to precisely $p$, because $-2008\equiv2$, $2010\equiv0$ and $-2009\equiv1\pmod5$. It is monic, hence primitive, and its <degree> is preserved by reduction. The <irreducibility by degree-preserving reduction modulo a prime> criterion states that a primitive integer <polynomial> with irreducible reduction of the same <degree> is irreducible over the rationals. This follows from <Gauss's lemma>: a rational factorization would give integer factors whose leading coefficients remain nonzero modulo five, contradicting the irreducible reduction. Thus
$$
\boxed{X^3-2008X^2+2010X-2009\text{ is irreducible in }\mathbb Q[X].}
$$

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The <modulus> map is continuous, the denominator $1+|z|$ is positive everywhere, and sums, products and quotients with nonzero denominator preserve <continuity>. Hence **$f$ is continuous throughout $\mathbb C$**.

For $g$, $|g(z)|=|z|$, so $g(z)\to0=g(0)$ as $z\to0$. The chosen argument is continuous locally away from the negative real axis, making $g$ continuous there as well. At a point $-r$, $r>0$, approach along $z=r e^{i\theta}$. From $\theta\uparrow\pi$, $g(z)\to ir$; from $\theta\downarrow-\pi$, $g(z)\to-ir$. The value assigned on the cut is $ir$. Thus **$g$ is discontinuous at every strictly negative real point**, and therefore is not a continuous map on the entire complex plane. Its radial factor is $r$, as printed, rather than the $\sqrt r$ of a square <root>.

{parent=2009}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=5}
{c}

For $f=u+iv$, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> are $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$. Holomorphy implies these relations. Conversely, if the first partial derivatives exist and are continuous near a point, satisfying the equations there gives complex differentiability; satisfying them on an <open set> gives a <holomorphic function> on that set.

Let $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. On the cut plane, $r+x>0$: equality could occur only at a nonpositive real point, which is excluded. Thus $u=\sqrt{(r+x)/2}$ is strictly positive and $v=y/(2u)$ is well defined and smooth. Since $y^2=(r-x)(r+x)$,
$$
u^2=\frac{r+x}2,\qquad v^2=\frac{r-x}2,\qquad2uv=y.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{(u+iv)^2=x+iy=z.}
$$
One can verify <holomorphy> directly:
$$
u_x=\frac u{2r}=v_y,\qquad u_y=\frac v{2r}=-v_x.
$$
Here $r>0$ throughout the domain, so all partial derivatives are continuous. Therefore **$f$ is holomorphic**, specifically the <principal square root> on the cut plane, with <derivative> $f'(z)=1/[2f(z)]$.

{parent=2009}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6}
{c}

The two bounding circles meet at zero and one, which the <Möbius transformation> sends to zero and infinity. They therefore become straight lines through zero. More explicitly write $w=f(z)$, so $z=w/(1+w)$. The two disk inequalities become
$$
\operatorname{Re}(\overline\zeta w)>0,\qquad\operatorname{Re}(\zeta w)>0.
$$
Since $\zeta=1/2+i\sqrt3/2$, these say $\operatorname{Re}w>\sqrt3|\operatorname{Im}w|$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(R)=\{w\ne0:-\pi/6<\arg w<\pi/6\}.}
$$
The positive real axis belongs to the image because $f(1/2)=1$, fixing which sector is intended. Cubing maps this sector bijectively onto the <right half-plane>: its argument interval of width $\pi/3$ becomes $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. The <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk> $v\mapsto(v-1)/(v+1)$ then maps that half-plane bijectively onto the <unit disk>. Hence a holomorphic bijection is
$$
\boxed{h(z)=\frac{\left(\frac z{1-z}\right)^3-1}{\left(\frac z{1-z}\right)^3+1}
=\frac{z^3-(1-z)^3}{z^3+(1-z)^3}.}
$$
Every denominator is nonzero on the corresponding domain, and the sector width ensures the power map is injective. This is the <conformal map of a symmetric circular lens>.

{parent=2009}

= Solution
{parent=7}
{c}

If two solutions exist, their difference satisfies $d''+d=0$ with $d(0)=d(L)=0$, where $L=\pi/4$. The general solution is $d=a\sin x+b\cos x$. The first endpoint gives $b=0$ and the second gives $a\sin L=0$, hence $a=0$. Thus the solution is unique if it exists.

For the operator $-D^2-1$, choose the homogeneous solutions $\sin x$ and $\sin(L-x)$ satisfying the left and right endpoint conditions. Continuity at $x=s$ and <derivative> jump $G_x(s^+,s)-G_x(s^-,s)=-1$ yield the <Dirichlet Green function>
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=\frac{\sin(\min\{x,s\})\sin(L-\max\{x,s\})}{\sin L}.}
$$
Indeed it vanishes at both endpoints and satisfies $-G_{xx}-G=\delta_s$. Splitting the integral at $s=x$ and differentiating verifies that $y=\int_0^LG(x,s)f(s)ds$ is twice continuously differentiable for continuous $f$ and solves the boundary-value problem, establishing existence as well.

At zero, $G_x(0,s)=\sin(L-s)/\sin L=\cos s-\sin s$, since $\cot L=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y'(0)=\int_0^{\pi/4}f(s)(\cos s-\sin s)ds.}
$$

{parent=2009}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=8}
{c}

The <characteristic equations for a transport equation> are $\dot x=1$, $\dot y=2$, $\dot z=-3z$. Label an initial point by $s\in[0,1]$ and choose <characteristic> time zero on the data segment. Integration gives
$$
x=s+t,\qquad y=2t,\qquad z=e^s e^{-3t}.
$$
Thus $s=x-y/2$ and $t=y/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=e^{x-2y},\qquad0\leq x-y/2\leq1.}
$$
This is the full infinite strip between the parallel <characteristic> lines $y=2x$ and $y=2x-2$. Both positive and negative <characteristic> times are allowed because the problem specifies no forward-time restriction. Each <characteristic> in this strip meets the data segment exactly once. Outside it, the general solution $z=e^{-3y/2}F(x-y/2)$ contains unrestricted values of $F$, so the data do not determine a unique solution there.

= Solution
{parent=9}
{c}

An equilibrium has $y=-x/2$ from the first equation. Substitution in the second gives $-x(x^2+3)/2=0$, so **the origin is the only real equilibrium**. Its <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda=\pm i\sqrt3.
$$
Purely imaginary linear <eigenvalues> alone do not classify the nonlinear equilibrium. Use instead
$$
H=x^2+xy+y^2+\frac{x^4}8.
$$
Its derivatives are $H_x=2x+y+x^3/2$ and $H_y=x+2y$, so the vector <field> is $(H_y,-H_x)$. The <chain rule> gives $\dot H=H_xH_y-H_yH_x=0$. Therefore the nonstationary trajectories in the <phase portrait> lie on the level curves
$$
\boxed{x^2+xy+y^2+x^4/8=C.}
$$
The quadratic part is positive definite, with <eigenvalues> $1/2$ and $3/2$, so $H$ has a strict local minimum at zero. Small positive level sets are smooth closed ovals surrounding it, and the vector <field> never vanishes on them. They are periodic orbits. Thus **the origin is a nonlinear centre, Lyapunov stable but not asymptotically stable**. The near-origin ellipses have their long axis along $y=-x$. At $(x,0)$ with $x>0$, the velocity points downward, fixing clockwise orientation.
