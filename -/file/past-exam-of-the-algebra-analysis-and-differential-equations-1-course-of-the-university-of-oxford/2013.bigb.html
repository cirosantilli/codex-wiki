= Solution
{parent=a}

The <dual space> is the <vector space> of all <linear functionals> on $V$:
$$
\boxed{V'=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F).}
$$
Addition and scalar multiplication are defined pointwise. The <annihilator of a vector subspace> is
$$
\boxed{U^0=\{f\in V':f(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.}
$$
It is a <vector subspace> of $V'$: linear combinations of functionals vanishing on $U$ also vanish on $U$.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ of $U$. Because $v\notin U$, the list $u_1,\ldots,u_m,v$ is <linearly independent>. Extend it to a <basis> of $V$. A <linear functional> is specified uniquely by its values on a <basis>, so define $f$ to be zero on the $u_j$ and on every added basis vector other than $v$, and set $f(v)=1$.

Then $f$ vanishes on every <linear combination> of the $u_j$, so $f\in U^0$, while
$$
\boxed{f(v)=1\neq0.}
$$
This proves that the <annihilator of a vector subspace> separates every vector outside the subspace from that subspace; no <inner product> or preferred coordinates are needed.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Every $g_i\in U^0$ vanishes on $U$, so $U\subseteq\bigcap_i\ker g_i$. Conversely, suppose $v\in\bigcap_i\ker g_i$. Every $f\in U^0$ is a <linear combination> $\sum_i a_ig_i$, giving $f(v)=0$. If $v\notin U$, part (b) supplies a functional in $U^0$ with $f(v)\neq0$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{U=\bigcap_{i=1}^k\ker g_i.}
$$
For the dimension statement, take a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ of $U$ and extend it to a <basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ of $V$. Relative to the corresponding <dual basis>, a functional belongs to $U^0$ precisely when its first $m$ coefficients are zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{k=\dim U^0=n-m=\dim V-\dim U.}
$$
If $U=V$, this says $k=0$ and the empty intersection is understood to be $V$.

In the coordinate example choose the <linear functionals> $g_1(x,y,z)=y$ and $g_2(x,y,z)=z$. Their <kernel of a linear map>[kernels] are the two coordinate planes
$$
\boxed{W_1=\{(x,0,z):x,z\in\mathbb R\},\qquad
W_2=\{(x,y,0):x,y\in\mathbb R\}.}
$$
Their intersection is the stated axis, and $k=2=3-1$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <adjoint operator> $T^*$ is characterized by
$$
\boxed{\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V.}
$$
This identity works with either convention for which argument of the complex <inner product> is linear, provided the convention is used consistently.

For uniqueness, subtract the two given identities to get $\langle v,(S-T^*)w\rangle=0$ for every $v,w$. Fix $w$ and choose $v=(S-T^*)w$. Then <positive definiteness> gives
$$
\|(S-T^*)w\|^2=0,
$$
so $(S-T^*)w=0$. As $w$ was arbitrary, $\boxed{S=T^*}$. The decisive fact is that a vector orthogonal to every vector, including itself, must be zero.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

First suppose $E=E^*$. For $u\in U=\operatorname{im}E$, choose $v$ with $Ev=u$. For $w\in W=\ker E$,
$$
\langle u,w\rangle=\langle Ev,w\rangle=\langle v,Ew\rangle=0.
$$
Thus $U\subseteq W^\perp$. The <direct sum of vector spaces> gives $\dim U=\dim V-\dim W$, while the <orthogonal complement> has $\dim W^\perp=\dim V-\dim W$. Equal finite dimensions imply $U=W^\perp$.

Conversely, suppose $U=W^\perp$. Decompose arbitrary vectors as $v=u+w$ and $v'=u'+w'$, with $u,u'\in U$ and $w,w'\in W$. Since $E$ is the indicated <projection>, $Ev=u$ and $Ev'=u'$. Orthogonality makes the cross terms vanish, so
$$
\langle Ev,v'\rangle=\langle u,u'\rangle=\langle v,Ev'\rangle.
$$
Therefore $E$ satisfies the defining identity of its own <adjoint operator>. By uniqueness from part (a), $E=E^*$. Hence
$$
\boxed{E=E^*\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad U=W^\perp.}
$$
This is the characterization of an <orthogonal projection> among projections along a complementary subspace.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <Euclidean domain> is an <integral domain> $R$ admitting a <Euclidean function> $\delta:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$ such that division with a smaller remainder is possible: for every $a\in R$ and nonzero $b\in R$, there are $q,r\in R$ with
$$
\boxed{a=qb+r,\qquad r=0\ \text{or}\ \delta(r)<\delta(b).}
$$
Thus the <Euclidean function> measures progress in repeated division. Definitions which additionally normalize it to be nondecreasing under multiplication give the same class of <Euclidean domains>; the division property is the one needed below.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the <zero ideal>, choose $d=0$. Otherwise choose a nonzero $d\in I$ for which the <Euclidean function> $\delta(d)$ is minimal; the <well-ordering principle> guarantees such a choice. For any $a\in I$, perform Euclidean division:
$$
a=qd+r,\qquad r=0\ \text{or}\ \delta(r)<\delta(d).
$$
Because $I$ is an <ideal>, $r=a-qd\in I$. A nonzero remainder would contradict the minimal choice of $d$, so $r=0$ and $a\in Rd$. Thus $I\subseteq Rd$. Conversely, $d\in I$ implies $Rd\subseteq I$, proving
$$
\boxed{I=Rd.}
$$
Every <ideal> is therefore a <principal ideal>; in particular, a <Euclidean domain> is a <principal ideal domain>.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For the <Gaussian integers>, use the <Gaussian norm>
$$
\boxed{\delta(a+bi)=a^2+b^2\quad(a+bi\neq0).}
$$
To identify the <ideal>, note that $a^2+b^2$ is even if and only if $a,b$ have the same parity. Also
$$
\frac{a+bi}{1+i}=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}i.
$$
This quotient is a <Gaussian integer> precisely when $a,b$ have the same parity. Therefore the prescribed set consists exactly of the multiples of $1+i$, giving
$$
\boxed{I=\mathbb Z[i](1+i),\qquad d=1+i.}
$$
Any associate of $1+i$ would generate the same <principal ideal>.

= Solution

Under the assumed continuous real differentiability, the necessary and sufficient conditions are the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> at every point of $U$:
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
Necessity follows by computing the complex <derivative> along real and imaginary increments. For sufficiency, real differentiability gives the first-order expansion
$$
f(z+h)-f(z)=(u_x+iv_x)\operatorname{Re}h+(u_y+iv_y)\operatorname{Im}h+o(|h|).
$$
The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> turn the linear part into $(u_x+iv_x)h$, so the complex <derivative> exists and equals $u_x+iv_x$. Thus $f$ is a <holomorphic function> on $U$.

At any $z\neq0$, take sufficiently small $h$ so $z+h\neq0$. The complex difference quotient is
$$
\frac{(z+h)^{-1}-z^{-1}}h=-\frac1{z(z+h)}\longrightarrow-\frac1{z^2}.

Hence the complex <derivative> exists at every point of the punctured plane, and
$$
\boxed{f'(z)=-z^{-2},\qquad f\text{ is holomorphic on }\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
The excluded point is essential to the domain statement: the reciprocal has a <simple pole> there.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use the reciprocal <Möbius transformation>
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\frac1z.}
$$
It is a <holomorphic function> on $U_1$ by part (b). For $z\neq0$,
$$
|F(z)+i|=\frac{|1+iz|}{|z|}=\frac{|z-i|}{|z|}.
$$
Part (c) now gives $z\in U_1$ if and only if $|F(z)+i|>1$, so $F(U_1)\subseteq U_2$. Conversely, $0\notin U_2$ because $|0+i|=1$. For any $w\in U_2$, put $z=1/w$; the same identity gives $|z-i|>|z|$, so $z\in U_1$ and $F(z)=w$. Finally, reciprocation is injective on nonzero complex numbers. This proves bijectivity, with inverse
$$
\boxed{F^{-1}(w)=\frac1w.}
$$
The inverse is also holomorphic, so this is a <biholomorphism>.

For a piecewise continuously differentiable <contour> $\gamma:[a,b]\to U$, the <contour integral> is defined by
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt.}
$$
If the contour has corners, integrate on its finitely many differentiable pieces and sum the results. The integral on the right is taken componentwise as an ordinary real-parameter integral of a complex-valued function. The factor $\gamma'(t)$ encodes both the speed and orientation of the <contour>.

Choose the continuous argument with values in $(-\pi/2,3\pi/2)$ and define
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Log}z=\log|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z,\qquad -\frac\pi2<\operatorname{Arg}z<\frac{3\pi}2.}
$$
Its <branch cut> is the nonpositive imaginary axis, including zero, exactly the removed ray. Locally away from that ray, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> or the local inverse of the exponential show that it is a <holomorphic logarithm>, with <derivative> $1/z$ and $e^{\operatorname{Log}z}=z$. The chosen interval fixes the additive ambiguity by integer multiples of $2\pi i$.

= c
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the branch from part (b) and close the real interval $[-R,R]$ with the upper semicircle, oriented counterclockwise. For $\operatorname{Im}z\geq0$, the number $z+i\alpha$ has positive imaginary part, so the numerator is a <holomorphic logarithm> throughout a neighbourhood of the contour and its interior. The branch point $-i\alpha$ lies strictly below the contour. The only enclosed <pole> of
$$
H(z)=\frac{\operatorname{Log}(z+i\alpha)}{z^2+\beta^2}
$$
is the <simple pole> at $i\beta$, with <residue>
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=i\beta}H=\frac{\operatorname{Log}(i(\alpha+\beta))}{2i\beta}
=\frac{\log(\alpha+\beta)+i\pi/2}{2i\beta}.
$$
To justify discarding the arc, take $R>\max\{\beta,\alpha+1\}$. On it, $|z+i\alpha|$ lies between $R-\alpha$ and $R+\alpha$, and the argument has bounded absolute value. Hence
$$
\left|\int_{\text{arc}}H(z)\,dz\right|
\leq\frac{\pi R\{\log(R+\alpha)+3\pi/2\}}{R^2-\beta^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The real-axis integral is absolutely convergent, since its integrand is $O(\log|x|/x^2)$ at infinity and has no real singularities. The <residue theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Log}(x+i\alpha)}{x^2+\beta^2}\,dx
=\frac\pi\beta\log(\alpha+\beta)+\frac{i\pi^2}{2\beta}.}
$$
Finally, $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Log}(x+i\alpha)=\tfrac12\log(x^2+\alpha^2)$. Taking twice the real part yields
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\log(x^2+\alpha^2)}{x^2+\beta^2}\,dx
=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\log(\alpha+\beta).}
$$
The branch choice affects the first integral's imaginary part; the second integral is real and independent of that choice.

= Solution
{parent=i}

An <isolated singularity> at $a$ means that $f$ is holomorphic in some punctured disk $0<|z-a|<r$, while $a$ itself is an excluded point or a point where the given function is not holomorphic. There are no other singularities arbitrarily close to $a$. By <Laurent's theorem>, the function has a unique <Laurent series> on that punctured disk:
$$
f(z)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}c_j(z-a)^j.
$$
The negative-power terms form its <principal part of a Laurent series>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <removable singularity> is an <isolated singularity> for which the function on the punctured disk extends to a <holomorphic function> on the full disk, by assigning or correcting its value at $a$. In <Laurent series> terms,
$$
\boxed{c_j=0\text{ for every }j<0.}
$$
The remaining power series defines the extension with value $c_0$ at $a$. Conversely, a holomorphic extension has a <Taylor series> there, so uniqueness of the <Laurent series> forces all negative coefficients to vanish.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A <pole> of order $m\geq1$ at $a$ has finitely many negative powers in its <Laurent series>, the lowest being exactly $-m$:
$$
c_{-m}\neq0,\qquad c_j=0\quad(j<-m).
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{f(z)=(z-a)^{-m}h(z),\qquad h\text{ holomorphic near }a,\ h(a)\neq0.}
$$
Thus $(z-a)^m f(z)$ has a holomorphic extension with nonzero value at $a$, whereas multiplying by any smaller power does not remove the singularity.

= iv
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

A <order of a zero of a holomorphic function>[zero] of order $n\geq1$ at $a$ means that $f$ is holomorphic near $a$ and
$$
f(a)=f'(a)=\cdots=f^{(n-1)}(a)=0,\qquad f^{(n)}(a)\neq0.
$$
Equivalently its <Taylor series> starts with the nonzero coefficient of $(z-a)^n$, so
$$
\boxed{f(z)=(z-a)^n h(z),\qquad h\text{ holomorphic near }a,\ h(a)\neq0.}
$$
Unlike a <pole>, a zero is a regular point of the function itself; its reciprocal has a pole of the corresponding order.

By the finite-order <order of a zero of a holomorphic function>[zero] factorizations,
$$
f(z)=(z-a)^mF(z),\qquad g(z)=(z-a)^nG(z),\qquad F(a)G(a)\neq0,
$$
with $F,G$ holomorphic near $a$. Shrinking the disk if necessary, continuity ensures $G$ never vanishes there, so $F/G$ is holomorphic and nonzero at $a$. Consequently
$$
\frac{f(z)}{g(z)}=(z-a)^{m-n}\frac{F(z)}{G(z)}.
$$
If $n>m$, the exponent is $-(n-m)$, with a holomorphic nonvanishing factor. Hence
$$
\boxed{f/g\text{ has a pole of order }n-m.}
$$

= Solution

With the same factors, if $n\leq m$, then
$$
\frac{f(z)}{g(z)}=(z-a)^{m-n}\frac{F(z)}{G(z)}
$$
has no negative power and extends holomorphically across $a$. Thus its apparent singularity is a <removable singularity>, with extension value
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac fg\right)(a)=\begin{cases}F(a)/G(a),&m=n,\\0,&m>n.\end{cases}}
$$
For $m>n$ the extended quotient has a <order of a zero of a holomorphic function>[zero] of order $m-n$; for $m=n$ the extension is nonzero.

= c
{parent=6}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The complex sine has simple zeros exactly at $z=k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, because $\sin(k\pi)=0$ and $\cos(k\pi)=(-1)^k\neq0$. Hence the denominator has a zero of order three at each of these points and nowhere else.

At $0$, the numerator has order two, so the quotient has a <simple pole>. Indeed its leading term is $-\pi^4/z$. At $\pi$, the numerator has order one, so there is a <pole> of order two. At $-\pi$, the numerator has order three, cancelling the denominator's order-three zero, so the singularity is removable. Its extension value is
$$
\lim_{z\to-\pi}\frac{z^2(z-\pi)(z+\pi)^3}{\sin^3z}
=\frac{(-\pi)^2(-2\pi)}{\cos^3(-\pi)}=2\pi^3.
$$
At every remaining $k\pi$, the numerator is nonzero, giving a <pole> of order three. Thus the complete finite-plane classification is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
z&\text{classification}\\\hline
0&\text{pole of order }1\\
\pi&\text{pole of order }2\\
-\pi&\text{removable, extension value }2\pi^3\\
k\pi,\quad k\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{-1,0,1\}&\text{pole of order }3
\end{array}}
$$
Away from this discrete set the denominator is nonzero, so the quotient is holomorphic.

Collect the two dependent variables in a column vector. The <vector field> and initial vector are
$$
\boxed{F(x,Z)=\begin{pmatrix}(|z_2|+x^2+1)^{-1}\\(|z_1|+1)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}.}

The given <system of ordinary differential equations> is then $Y'=F(x,Y)$ with $Y(0)=B$. Both components of $F$ are continuous on all of $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2$, since their denominators are at least one.

= Solution

Each denominator is at least one and positive, so each component of $F$ lies in $(0,1]$. Taking the <maximum norm> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\|F(x,Z)\|_\infty=\max\!\left\{\frac1{|z_2|+x^2+1},\frac1{|z_1|+1}\right\}\leq1.}
$$
This is a global bound, so in particular the constant $M=1$ is valid throughout the specified cube.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $a,b\geq0$ and $c\geq1$,
$$
\left|\frac1{a+c}-\frac1{b+c}\right|=\frac{|a-b|}{(a+c)(b+c)}\leq|a-b|.
$$
Apply this to the first component with $a=|z_2|$, $b=|w_2|$, $c=x^2+1$. The <reverse triangle inequality> gives
$$
|F_1(x,Z)-F_1(x,W)|\leq\big||z_2|-|w_2|\big|\leq|z_2-w_2|.
$$
For the second component use $a=|z_1|$, $b=|w_1|$, $c=1$ to obtain $|F_2(x,Z)-F_2(x,W)|\leq|z_1-w_1|$. Taking the maximum yields
$$
\boxed{\|F(x,Z)-F(x,W)\|_\infty\leq\|Z-W\|_\infty.}
$$
Thus $F$ is globally <Lipschitz continuous> in the dependent vector, uniformly in $x$, with <Lipschitz constant> $1$. Differentiability of the absolute-value function at zero is unnecessary.

= Solution

A <Lipschitz condition> here is uniform in the independent variable: there is a finite constant $L\geq0$ such that, whenever both $(x,Z)$ and $(x,W)$ belong to the cube,
$$
\boxed{\|F(x,Z)-F(x,W)\|_\infty\leq L\|Z-W\|_\infty.}
$$
The same $L$ must work for every allowed $x,Z,W$. Part (c) proves this with $L=1$. This controls changes in the dependent variables; no Lipschitz condition in $x$ is required for the <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The quantitative <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> applies on a rectangle $|x-x_0|\leq a$, $\|Z-B\|_\infty\leq b$. Suppose $F$ is continuous there, bounded in <maximum norm> by $M$, and uniformly <Lipschitz continuous> in $Z$. Then the <initial value problem> $Y'=F(x,Y)$, $Y(x_0)=B$, has a unique solution on
$$
\boxed{|x-x_0|\leq h,\qquad h=\min\{a,b/M\},}
$$
with the usual interpretation $b/M=\infty$ if $M=0$. The solution remains in the dependent-variable ball. Its endpoints are included when $F$ is continuous on the closed rectangle, as here. Equivalently, existence first on every smaller interval and the derivative bound give extension to those endpoints.

For the given cube, $x_0=0$ and $a=b=1$. Thus the theorem gives $h=\min\{1,1/M\}$ once a bound $M$ and a uniform <Lipschitz constant> have been established. The theorem does not require $Lh<1$: convergence of <Picard iteration> can be proved by factorial estimates or by using successive shorter intervals.

{parent=7}

= Solution
{parent=f}

The <vector field> is continuous on the cube. Part (b) gives $M=1$, and part (c) supplies a uniform <Lipschitz constant> $L=1$. In the quantitative <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>, the independent-variable half-width and the dependent-variable radius are both one, so
$$
h=\min\{1,1/1\}=1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{There is a unique solution for every }x\in[-1,1].}
$$
The global derivative bound also makes the cube condition explicit. Along any solution segment starting at the initial point,
$$
\|Y(x)-B\|_\infty
=\left\|\int_0^xF(s,Y(s))\,ds\right\|_\infty\leq|x|.
$$
Thus it cannot leave the dependent-variable ball before $|x|=1$. Bounded derivatives give limiting endpoint values, and local existence extends to them because $F$ is defined continuously on all space. Uniform Lipschitz continuity ensures that all such local pieces agree on overlaps, proving uniqueness throughout the closed interval.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the convention $A u_{xx}+2B u_{xy}+C u_{yy}=0$, with discriminant $B^2-AC$. Here $A=1$, $B=0$, $C=\operatorname{sign}(y)$.

For $y>0$, the discriminant is $-1<0$, so the equation is an <elliptic partial differential equation>, already in the canonical <Laplace equation> form $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$.

For $y<0$, the discriminant is $1>0$, so it is a <hyperbolic partial differential equation>, the <wave equation> $u_{xx}-u_{yy}=0$. Introduce characteristic coordinates
$$
\xi=x+y,\qquad\eta=x-y.
$$
Then $\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta$ and $\partial_y=\partial_\xi-\partial_\eta$, giving
$$
u_{xx}-u_{yy}=4u_{\xi\eta}=0.
$$
On $y=0$, the discriminant vanishes and the principal part has rank one; the degenerate <parabolic type> is $u_{xx}=0$. This is an interface line, rather than an open parabolic subdomain. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
y>0&\text{elliptic}&u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\\
y<0&\text{hyperbolic}&u_{\xi\eta}=0\\
y=0&\text{degenerate parabolic}&u_{xx}=0
\end{array}}
$$
This is a <mixed-type partial differential equation>. The classification concerns the principal part and remains meaningful even though the coefficient jumps at the interface.

= b
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In the lower triangular region, the characteristic coordinates obey $-1\leq\xi<\eta\leq1$. Integrating the canonical <wave equation> $u_{\xi\eta}=0$ gives
$$
u(x,y)=F(\xi)+G(\eta).
$$
On the right characteristic edge, $\eta=1$ and $x=(\xi+1)/2$, so its prescribed value gives $F(\xi)+G(1)=-\sin(\pi\xi)$. On the left edge, $\xi=-1$ and $x=(\eta-1)/2$, giving $F(-1)+G(\eta)=\sin(\pi\eta)$. At the common vertex the two values agree and equal zero; hence $F(-1)+G(1)=0$.

Adding the two edge identities and subtracting this vertex identity determines the entire solution:
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=\sin\bigl(\pi(x-y)\bigr)-\sin\bigl(\pi(x+y)\bigr)
=-2\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),\quad y<0.}
$$
The apparent freedom to add a constant to $F$ and subtract it from $G$ does not change $u$. Every classical lower-region solution has the displayed separated form, so these two characteristic boundary traces determine it uniquely. Substitution into $u_{xx}-u_{yy}=0$ and into both edge values directly verifies existence.

= c
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The lower formula has a smooth continuation as a function of $(x,y)$, and on $y=0$ it has trace $u(x,0)=0$. Its second tangential derivative is
$$
u_{xx}(x,y)=2\pi^2\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi y),
$$
which vanishes at $y=0$. Hence the continued lower formula satisfies the equation restricted to the interface, where the formal equation is $u_{xx}=0$.

Assuming the solution itself is continuous across the interface, the upper region therefore has the <Dirichlet boundary value problem>
$$
\boxed{\begin{gathered}
u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0\quad(-1<x<1,\ 0<y<1),\\
u(-1,y)=u(1,y)=0,\qquad u(x,1)=u(x,0)=0.
\end{gathered}}
$$
The zero function satisfies these data. The <maximum principle for harmonic functions>, applied to both $u$ and $-u$, proves uniqueness among harmonic functions continuous on the closed rectangle. Thus $\boxed{u=0\text{ for }y\geq0}$ is the continuous upper continuation. Satisfaction of the degenerate interface equation does not impose continuity of the normal derivative; that is the issue in part (d).

= d
{parent=8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Differentiate the uniquely determined lower solution and the zero upper solution:
$$
u_y(x,y)=\begin{cases}-2\pi\cos(\pi x)\cos(\pi y),&y<0,\\0,&y>0.\end{cases}
$$
Their one-sided traces are
$$
\boxed{u_y(x,0^-)=-2\pi\cos(\pi x),\qquad u_y(x,0^+)=0.}
$$
They disagree, for example at $x=0$, so the <normal derivative> is not continuous across the interface. The jump, defined as upper trace minus lower trace, is $2\pi\cos(\pi x)$. It vanishes at the two interior points $x=\pm1/2$, but not identically on the interface.

This illustrates that <continuous transmission need not preserve normal derivatives>. In fact, at any point where these traces differ, the two-sided first derivative with respect to $y$ does not exist. The resulting function is continuous and smooth separately on each open side, but is not globally $C^1$. Consequently a globally classical $C^2$ solution satisfying all these data cannot exist. The calculation in part (c) evaluates the smooth lower formula on the interface; it does not supply the missing cross-interface regularity.

Inside the half-plane of convergence, the differentiation theorem for the <Laplace transform> permits differentiation with respect to the transform variable. Since $\partial_p e^{-pt}=-te^{-pt}$,
$$
\boxed{\overline x'(p)=-\int_0^\infty t x(t)e^{-pt}\,dt=\mathcal L[-tx(t)](p).}
$$
Repeating the argument, or applying induction using $\partial_p^n e^{-pt}=(-t)^n e^{-pt}$, gives
$$
\boxed{\overline x^{(n)}(p)=\int_0^\infty(-t)^n x(t)e^{-pt}\,dt
=(-1)^n\mathcal L[t^n x(t)](p),\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
The identities hold for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, within the stated convergence half-plane. The usual transform differentiation theorem uses an interior margin in the real part of $p$ to control the polynomial factors in $t$; it makes no assertion on the boundary $\operatorname{Re}p=0$.

= b
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Integrating the complex exponential and taking its even and odd combinations gives the standard <Laplace transforms>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L[a_1](p)=\frac p{p^2+\omega^2},\qquad
\mathcal L[a_2](p)=\frac\omega{p^2+\omega^2}.}
$$
For example, $\mathcal L[e^{i\omega t}]=(p-i\omega)^{-1}$ for $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, and the real sine and cosine identities follow by combining the two signs of $i\omega$.

Apply part (a) once to the cosine transform and twice to the sine transform:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L[a_3](p)&=-\frac d{dp}\frac p{p^2+\omega^2}
=\boxed{\frac{p^2-\omega^2}{(p^2+\omega^2)^2}},\\
\mathcal L[a_4](p)&=\frac{d^2}{dp^2}\frac\omega{p^2+\omega^2}
=\boxed{\frac{2\omega(3p^2-\omega^2)}{(p^2+\omega^2)^3}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
All four transforms are valid on the stated right half-plane.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $\omega=\sqrt2$ and $Y(p)=\overline y(p)$. The zero initial data and the <Laplace transform of a derivative> give $\mathcal L[y'']=p^2Y$. Transforming the forcing with part (b),
$$
\begin{aligned}
(p^2+2)Y
&=\sqrt2\,\frac{p^2-2}{(p^2+2)^2}
+\frac12\frac{\sqrt2}{p^2+2}\\
&=\frac{\sqrt2(3p^2-2)}{2(p^2+2)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{Y(p)=\frac{\sqrt2(6p^2-4)}{4(p^2+2)^3}.}
$$
Part (b) with $\omega=\sqrt2$ gives $\mathcal L[t^2\sin(\sqrt2t)]=2\sqrt2(3p^2-2)/(p^2+2)^3$. Comparing coefficients and using uniqueness of the <Laplace transform>,
$$
\boxed{y(t)=\frac{t^2}{4}\sin(\sqrt2\,t).}
$$
As a direct check, $y'=\tfrac t2\sin(\sqrt2t)+\tfrac{\sqrt2t^2}4\cos(\sqrt2t)$, so both initial values vanish. Differentiating once more cancels the $t^2\sin(\sqrt2t)$ terms in $y''+2y$, leaving $\sqrt2t\cos(\sqrt2t)+\tfrac12\sin(\sqrt2t)$ as required. This also checks the factor $1/4$ in the inverse transform.
