= Vector space
{wiki}
{title2=$V$}

A vector space over a <field> $\mathbb F$ is a <set> $V$ equipped with <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication> satisfying the <vector space axioms>. Its <vectors> can represent directed displacements, lists of measurements, <polynomials>, or entire <functions>. The common structure is that adding two elements and multiplying an element by a <scalar> remain meaningful operations inside the same space.

For example, $\mathbb R^2$ uses coordinatewise addition and scaling. With
$$
b_1=(2,1),\qquad b_2=(1,2),\qquad v=(5,4),
$$
we have $v=2b_1+b_2$. Moving twice along $b_1$ and once along $b_2$ reaches the same <vector> as five horizontal and four vertical steps. The two descriptions refer to different <bases> of the same space. A <basis> makes such descriptions unique; a <spanning set> makes them possible.

\Image[vector-basis-coordinates.png]
{title=The same vector has coordinates 5,4 in the Cartesian basis and 2,1 in an oblique basis}

The space $\mathbb R[x]_{\leq2}$ of real <polynomials> of degree at most two has <basis> $1,x,x^2$: the <polynomial> $3-2x+x^2$ is the <linear combination> with coordinates $(3,-2,1)$. The <zero vector> here is the zero <polynomial>, and addition combines <coefficients>. A <derivative> is a <linear map> on this space; its <matrix> records how it acts on the three <basis vectors>. The same coordinate machinery therefore handles both geometric motion and operations on <functions>.

The choice of <field> matters. The <complex numbers> form a one-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb C$ with <basis> $1$, but a two-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb R$ with <basis> $1,i$. The question "how many independent directions?" has an answer only after the allowed <scalars> are specified. Likewise, polynomials of degree exactly two do not form a <vector space>: subtraction can remove the leading term, and the zero <polynomial> is absent.

The usefulness of the abstraction is that a <linear map> is determined by its values on a <basis>. Once those images are known, <linear combinations> give its action on every <vector>. The <rank-nullity theorem> relates the number of attainable outputs to the number of invisible input directions, while <change of basis> lets us choose coordinates suited to the problem. <Eigenvectors>, for example, choose directions on which repeated application of a <linear operator> becomes scalar multiplication.

A historical source is Hermann Grassmann's *Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre* (1844). Its full title advertises applications to other branches of mathematics, statics, mechanics, magnetism and crystallography; the original edition is digitized by ETH-Bibliothek. This gives an early setting for treating geometric quantities algebraically across different applications, rather than attributing the modern axiom list to that title alone.\a[https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/1169335][Grassmann, Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre, Leipzig, 1844; ETH-Bibliothek Rar 5128]{ref}

= Vector space axioms
{parent=Vector space}

Addition makes $V$ an <Abelian group>: for all $u,v,w\in V$, $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$ and $u+v=v+u$, there is a <zero vector> $0$, and every $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$. <Scalar multiplication> satisfies, for $a,b\in\mathbb F$,
$$
1v=v,\qquad (ab)v=a(bv),\qquad a(u+v)=au+av,\qquad(a+b)v=av+bv.
$$
Both operations take values in $V$. These requirements make manipulating <linear combinations> independent of how sums are grouped or expanded.

= Zero vector
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$0$}

The <zero vector> of a <vector space> is its additive identity: $v+0=0+v=v$ for every <vector> $v$. It is unique, because if $0'$ is another such identity then $0=0+0'=0'$. Distributivity gives $\alpha0=\alpha(0+0)=\alpha0+\alpha0$, so $\alpha0=0$ after cancellation. In a coordinate <vector> space it has every coordinate zero.

= Quaternionic structure on a complex vector space
{parent=Antilinear map}
{title2=$J^2=-1$}

An antilinear endomorphism $J$ with square $-1$ equips a <complex vector space> with a quaternionic structure: complex multiplication supplies the quaternionic generator $i$ and $J$ supplies $j$, satisfying $Ji=-iJ$. Such a space has even complex dimension. There is no invariant complex line, because $Jv=cv$ would imply $-v=|c|^2v$. In dimension four the invariant complex two-planes are quaternionic lines, forming $\mathbb{HP}^1\cong S^4$. This is the <Euclidean reality structure on twistor space> used in <twistor theory>.

= Majorization
{parent=Real vector space}
{title2=$x\prec y$}
{wiki}

For real vectors of equal length and equal sum, write $x\prec y$ when $\sum_{j=1}^k x_j^\downarrow\leq\sum_{j=1}^k y_j^\downarrow$ for every proper initial segment; the downward arrow means decreasing rearrangement. Majorization compares how unevenly a fixed total is distributed. The uniform probability vector is majorized by every probability vector, while $(1,0,\ldots,0)$ majorizes every probability vector. The order controls exact pure-state entanglement conversion in <Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem>.

= Majorization of summable sequences
{parent=Majorization}
{title2=$x\prec y$}

For decreasing positive sequences in $\ell^1$, write $x\prec y$ if every initial <partial sum> of $x$ is at most the corresponding <partial sum> of $y$ and the finite total sums agree. The finiteness requirement matters: equality of two divergent totals interpreted as infinity is too weak to guarantee a <doubly stochastic realization of summable-sequence majorization>.

= Doubly stochastic realization of summable-sequence majorization
{parent=Majorization of summable sequences}
{title2=$x_i=\sum_jp_{ij}y_j$}

Decreasing strictly positive <summable sequences> with equal totals and $x\prec y$ admit an infinite <doubly stochastic matrix> realizing the displayed relation. Choose $k$ with $y_k\geq x_1>y_{k+1}$ and average the pair to obtain $x_1$ and $y_k+y_{k+1}-x_1$. The remaining decreasing sequence majorizes the remaining target sequence: short initial sums contain values at least $x_1$, and longer sums are the old sums minus $x_1$. Repeat on the tail, freezing one normalized finite-support row at each stage. Limit column sums are at most one; <Tonelli's theorem>, equal finite totals and strict positivity of every $y_j$ force every column sum to equal one.

= Vector space over a finite field
{parent=Vector space}

If $\mathbb F_q$ is a finite field, a vector space of finite dimension $d$ over $\mathbb F_q$ has $q^d$ elements. Every infinite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F_p$ is an infinite elementary abelian $p$-group under addition.

= Finite-field Kakeya set
{parent=Vector space over a finite field}

A subset of $\mathbb F_q^n$ is a finite-field Kakeya set when it contains an <affine line in a vector space> in every one-dimensional direction. The line position may depend on the direction. The <polynomial method in combinatorics> yields the <finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound> on its <cardinality>.

= Tangent-line finite-field Kakeya construction
{parent=Finite-field Kakeya set}
{title2=$K=\{(x,y):x^2-y\text{ is a square}\}\cup\{x=0\}$}

For odd characteristic, the union of the lines $y=mx-m^2/4$ contains a line of every finite slope. For fixed $x$, completing the square shows that it comprises the $(p+1)/2$ values of $y$ for which $x^2-y$ is a square, including zero. Adding the vertical line $x=0$ completes the directions and gives cardinality $(p^2+2p-1)/2=(1/2+o(1))p^2$. This is a <finite-field Kakeya set> whose density tends to one half.

= Finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound
{parent=Finite-field Kakeya set}
{title2=$|A|\geq\binom{q+n-1}{n}\geq q^n/n!$}

For a <finite-field Kakeya set> $A\subseteq\mathbb F_q^n$, a smaller <cardinality> would give a nonzero <polynomial> of <total degree of a polynomial> at most $q-1$ vanishing on $A$, by the <dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space>. Its restriction to each complete <affine line in a vector space> has $q$ <roots of a polynomial> and degree less than $q$, hence vanishes identically. The top <homogeneous polynomial> part then vanishes in every direction. A <polynomial> of degree less than $q$ in each variable cannot vanish everywhere on $\mathbb F_q^n$ unless it is zero, giving a contradiction. For the <prime field>, this is the <polynomial nonvanishing below the field size>.

= Span
{synonym}

The linear span of a subset $S$ of a <vector space> is the <set> of all finite <linear combinations> of elements of $S$. It is the smallest <vector subspace> containing $S$: every <vector subspace> containing the original <vectors> must contain their sums and <scalar multiples>.

In $\mathbb R^3$, the <linear span> of $(1,0,0)$ is a line, the <linear span> of $(1,0,0),(0,1,0)$ is the plane $z=0$, and adding $(0,0,1)$ gives all of $\mathbb R^3$. Adding $(2,0,0)$ to the first set changes none of its attainable <vectors>, because it is already a <scalar multiple> of $(1,0,0)$. Thus a <spanning set> can contain redundant generators; <linear independence> identifies when this redundancy is absent. In an infinite-dimensional space, an algebraic <linear span> still uses finite sums; a <closed linear span> can also include <limits> in a specified <norm>.

= Closed linear span
{parent=Linear span}
{title2=$\overline{\operatorname{span}}S$}

In a <normed vector space>, the closed linear span of $S$ is the <closure> of its <linear span> in the <norm topology>. It includes <limits> of finite <linear combinations>. In a <Hilbert space>, an infinite <orthonormal basis> spans in this closed sense, allowing norm-convergent series in addition to finite sums.

= Spanning set
{parent=Linear span}

A subset $S$ of a <vector space> is a spanning set when its <linear span> is the whole vector space.

For a <vector subspace> $W\leq V$, the quotient vector space $V/W$ consists of cosets $v+W$, with vector operations induced from $V$.

= Parallel lines as a quotient vector space
{parent=Quotient vector space}
{title2=$\mathbb R^3/\operatorname{span}(u)$}

For fixed $u\ne0$, the parallel <affine lines> $l_x=x+\operatorname{span}(u)$ are precisely the cosets of the indicated <quotient vector space>. Changing representatives adds a multiple of $u$, so $l_x+l_y=l_{x+y}$ and $a l_x=l_{ax}$ are well-defined. The zero is the whole line $l_0$, not a single point. If $(u,b_1,b_2)$ is a <basis>, the two line classes $l_{b_1},l_{b_2}$ form a <basis> of the quotient by spanning and independence modulo $\operatorname{span}(u)$.

= Vector subspace
{parent=Vector space}

= Linear subspace
{synonym}

A vector subspace is a nonempty subset of a <vector space> closed under <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>, and is therefore itself a <vector space>. Equivalently, whenever $u,v$ belong to the subset, so does $au+bv$ for all allowed <scalars> $a,b$. In particular it contains the <zero vector>.

For example, the plane $W=\{(x,y,z):x+y+z=0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^3$ is a <vector subspace>, with <basis> $(1,-1,0),(1,0,-1)$. Indeed every element is $(-y)(1,-1,0)+(-z)(1,0,-1)$. The parallel plane $x+y+z=1$ misses the <zero vector> and is an <affine subspace> instead. Homogeneous constraints produce <vector subspaces>: $W$ is the <kernel> of the <linear map> $(x,y,z)\mapsto x+y+z$. This lets us describe all solutions of a homogeneous <linear equation> through a <basis> of its <kernel>.

= Proper vector subspace
{parent=Vector subspace}
{title2=$U\subsetneq V$}

A proper <vector subspace> of a <vector space> is one strictly smaller than the ambient space. In a finite-dimensional <vector space>, this is equivalent to having smaller <dimension>: a <basis> of the subspace can be extended to a <basis> of the ambient space, and strict inclusion requires at least one additional <vector>.

= Flag of a vector space
{synonym}

A flag is a strictly nested chain of <vector subspaces> of a fixed <vector space>. A <complete flag> contains a subspace of every possible dimension. For an action by <linear maps>, an invariant flag means that each of its <vector subspaces> is an <invariant subspace>. A <basis> adapted to an invariant complete flag makes all these maps upper triangular, connecting <complete flags> to <simultaneous triangularization of a Lie algebra representation>.

= Complete flag
{parent=Flag (linear algebra)}
{title2=$0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V$}

A complete flag in an $n$-dimensional <vector space> is a nested sequence of <vector subspaces> with $\dim V_i=i$. An action preserves such a flag exactly when its <matrices> are upper triangular in a <basis> adapted to the flag. The <Lie theorem> supplies an invariant complete flag for every complex finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> of a <solvable Lie algebra>.

= Fixed coordinate subspace
{parent=Vector subspace}
{title2=$\mathbb R_k^p=\{\theta:\theta_j=0\ (j>k)\}$}

The <fixed coordinate subspace> of dimension $k$ in $\mathbb R^p$ consists of vectors whose coordinates after the first $k$ vanish. Restricting a <quadratic form> to it uses only the leading $k$ by $k$ matrix block. It specifies one fixed support; allowing every support of size $k$ would introduce another combinatorial counting step.

= Positive distance between a unit sphere and a disjoint finite-dimensional subspace

= Affine span
{synonym}

The smallest <affine subspace> containing a nonempty set $S$. For any chosen $a\in S$, the displayed formula proves existence and minimality: every containing affine subspace must contain all the indicated differences in its direction space. = Affine hyperplane
{parent=Affine subspace}
{title2=$H=\{v\in V:\ell(v)=c\},\quad\ell\ne0$}

In a finite-dimensional real vector space, a level set of a nonzero <linear functional> is an affine hyperplane. Choosing $v_0$ in that level set gives $H=v_0+\ker\ell$, so its dimension is one less than that of the ambient space. With a Euclidean inner product, translation followed by an orthonormal basis of $\ker\ell$ identifies it isometrically with the lower-dimensional Euclidean space. The coordinate-sum constraint on balanced cut vectors is an example.

= Dimension
{synonym}

The dimension of a <vector space> is the <cardinality> of any of its <bases>. In finite dimension this counts the <scalars> needed to specify an arbitrary <vector> once an <ordered basis> is chosen. The exchange argument behind the <basis extension> theorem shows that any two finite <bases> have the same size: an independent family cannot be larger than a spanning family.

Thus $\mathbb R^2$ has <dimension> two even with an oblique <basis>, $\mathbb R[x]_{\leq2}$ has <dimension> three, and the homogeneous plane in <vector subspace> has <dimension> two. This count concerns independent freedom, so an embedded plane remains two-dimensional while sitting in a three-dimensional ambient <vector space>. <Dimension> also depends on the <field>, as the two interpretations of $\mathbb C$ in <vector space> illustrate.

= Direct sum of vector spaces
{synonym}

The direct sum $U\oplus W$ of two <vector space>[vector spaces] consists of pairs $(u,w)$ with componentwise operations. A vector space is an internal direct sum of subspaces $U$ and $W$ exactly when every vector has a unique expression $u+w$ with $u\in U$ and $w\in W$.

= Common plane for three pairwise complementary subspaces
{parent=Direct sum}

If $V=A_1\oplus A_2=A_2\oplus A_3=A_1\oplus A_3$ with nonzero subspaces, choose $0\ne v\in A_3$ and write $v=a_1+a_2$ using the first <direct sum of vector spaces>. Both components are nonzero. The plane spanned by them meets $A_1$ and $A_2$ in their component lines and meets $A_3$ in the line through $v$. Its intersection with $A_3$ cannot be the whole plane, since $a_1\notin A_3$.

= Orthogonal direct sum
{parent=Direct sum}
{title2=$V\oplus^\perp W$}

An orthogonal direct sum is a <direct sum> whose distinct summands are mutually <orthogonal>. For $v\in V$ and $w\in W$, the <Pythagorean identity> gives $\|v+w\|^2=\|v\|^2+\|w\|^2$. In a <Hilbert space>, a <closed subspace of a Hilbert space> and its <orthogonal complement> give an orthogonal direct sum of the entire space. The component maps are <orthogonal projections> and have <operator norm> one when their ranges are nonzero.

= Direct summand
{parent=Direct sum}

A <submodule> $N$ of a <module> $M$ is a direct summand if some <submodule> $L$ gives $M=N\oplus L$. Equivalently, the inclusion has a left inverse <R-module homomorphism>, or $N$ is the image of an <idempotent> <endomorphism> of $M$.

= Unimodular basis test for an integer direct summand
{parent=Direct summand}
{title2=$\det U=\pm1$}

Integer row vectors forming a square <unimodular matrix> are a basis of the corresponding <free abelian group>: the adjugate formula makes the inverse integral. The subgroup spanned by any subset of that basis is therefore a <direct summand>, complemented by the remaining basis vectors. In contrast, a proper finite-index subgroup of a free abelian group cannot be a direct summand: its nonzero finite quotient would have to embed as a subgroup of a torsion-free group.

= Dimension formula for a sum of subspaces
{parent=Direct sum}

For finite-dimensional subspaces $U,W$ of a common vector space,
$$
\dim(U+W)+\dim(U\cap W)=\dim U+\dim W.
$$

{parent=Direct sum}

For every subspace $U$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, extending a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$ produces a subspace $W$ such that $V=U\oplus W$.

= Linearly independent
{synonym}

<Vectors> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ are linearly independent when
$$
a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n=0
$$
implies $a_1=\cdots=a_n=0$. This means no <vector> in the finite family can be reconstructed from the others by a <linear combination>. It also gives uniqueness: subtracting two representations produces a vanishing <linear combination>, so all corresponding <coefficients> must agree.

The pair $b_1=(2,1)$, $b_2=(1,2)$ is <linearly independent> because $2a+b=0$ and $a+2b=0$ imply $a=b=0$. Adding $b_3=(3,3)$ creates the nontrivial <linear relation> $b_1+b_2-b_3=0$. The <linear span> is unchanged, but the representation of $(5,4)$ becomes nonunique: $2b_1+b_2=b_1+b_3$. This is precisely the redundancy a <basis> avoids.

= Minimally dependent vector family
{parent=Linear independence}

A finite <vector> family is minimally dependent if it is <linearly dependent> and every proper subfamily is <linearly independent>. For $n\geq2$, the family $e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1},\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}e_j$ in $\mathbb R^n$ is an example. The sum supplies a nonzero dependence. If the sum <vector> is omitted, the remaining <vectors> are a coordinate basis; if $e_j$ is omitted, its coordinate in any proposed relation forces the coefficient of the sum <vector> to vanish, then all other coefficients vanish. Every smaller proper subfamily lies in one of these independent $(n-1)$-element subfamilies.

= Distinct diagonal weights break a first column dependence
{parent=Minimally dependent vector family}

Let the first $k$ columns of a <matrix> be the first dependent prefix, with no zero column. Their relation space has dimension one, and every nonzero relation $b$ has at least two nonzero coordinates. If both $Ab=0$ and $A\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j)b=0$, one-dimensionality gives $\lambda_jb_j=cb_j$ for all $j$. Two nonzero coordinates would force two of the distinct weights $\lambda_j$ to agree, a contradiction.

= Linear relation
{parent=Linear dependence}
{title2=$\sum_i a_iv_i=0$}

A linear relation among <vectors> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is a coefficient tuple $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ satisfying $\sum_i a_iv_i=0$. It is nontrivial if at least one coefficient is nonzero. For the <matrix> with these vectors as columns, the space of relations is its <kernel>. The vectors are <linearly independent> exactly when this kernel is zero. An invertible change of coordinates preserves every relation, since $S\sum_i a_iv_i=0$ if and only if $\sum_i a_iv_i=0$.

= Hamel basis
{synonym}
{c}

A basis is a <linearly independent> <spanning set> of <vectors> in a <vector space>. Every <vector> is a unique finite <linear combination> of <basis vectors>. Independence supplies uniqueness, and spanning supplies existence. An <ordered basis> turns the <coefficients> into a <coordinate vector>.

For $\mathcal B=(b_1,b_2)=((2,1),(1,2))$, the <matrix> with these <vectors> as columns is
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Hence the <coordinate vector> of $v=(5,4)$ is $[v]_{\mathcal B}=B^{-1}v=(2,1)$, and $B[v]_{\mathcal B}=v$ recovers the usual coordinates. The <determinant> $3$ is nonzero, so this recovery works for every <vector> in $\mathbb R^2$. A <basis> need not have perpendicular or unit-length <vectors>; an <orthonormal basis> adds those geometric conveniences when an <inner product> is available.

The point of changing <basis> is to simplify the operation being studied. <Diagonalization> uses an <eigenbasis> to turn a coupled <linear map> into independent scalings. In a space of <polynomials>, the <basis> $1,x,x^2$ makes <differentiation> easy to encode, while <orthogonal polynomials> can make approximation errors easier to measure. Gilbert Strang's MIT lecture on independence, basis and dimension develops this connection between attainable columns, redundant columns and coordinates.\a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/][Strang, MIT 18.06SC, Independence, Basis and Dimension]{ref}

= Adjacent sums of a cyclic basis

= Zero-composition subspaces of a linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$M^l(\alpha)=\{\beta:\beta\alpha=0\},\quad M^r(\alpha)=\{\beta:\alpha\beta=0\}$}

For $\alpha:U\to V$, maps in $M^l(\alpha)$ factor uniquely through $V/\operatorname{im}\alpha$; maps in $M^r(\alpha)$ have <image> in $\ker\alpha$. Thus these <vector subspaces> are naturally $L(V/\operatorname{im}\alpha,U)$ and $L(V,\ker\alpha)$, of dimensions $(\dim V-\operatorname{rank}\alpha)\dim U$ and $(\dim U-\operatorname{rank}\alpha)\dim V$. Passing to a <dual map> reverses composition and interchanges these two spaces. This gives a basis-free route to equality of the ranks of a map and its dual.

= One-sided inverses of finite-dimensional endomorphisms
{parent=Linear map}

For endomorphisms $S,T$ of the same finite-dimensional <vector space>, $ST=I$ makes $S$ surjective and $T$ injective. The <rank-nullity theorem> makes both invertible, hence $TS=I$. The finite-dimensional and equal-domain assumptions matter; unilateral shift operators on an infinite-dimensional sequence space give one-sided inverses that are not two-sided.

= Rank of a linear map
{parent=Linear map}

The rank of a <linear map> is the dimension of its <image of a linear map>. In finite-dimensional spaces it equals the <rank of a matrix> representing the map in any bases.

= Rank bounds for a sum of linear maps
{parent=Rank of a linear map}
{title2=$|\operatorname{rank}A-\operatorname{rank}B|\leq\operatorname{rank}(A+B)\leq\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}B$}

The image of $A+B$ lies in $\operatorname{im}A+\operatorname{im}B$. The <dimension formula for a sum of subspaces> gives the upper bound. Applying it to $A=(A+B)-B$ and then interchanging $A,B$ gives the lower bound. Both extremes are sharp: cancelling diagonal blocks gives the difference of ranks; diagonal maps with supports covering the whole space and positive coefficients give full rank when the sum of prescribed ranks is at least the dimension.

= Transvection
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$T=I+vf,\quad f(v)=0$}

For a nonzero <vector> $v$ and nonzero <linear functional> $f$ vanishing on $v$, this invertible map fixes the <hyperplane> $\ker f$ pointwise and has determinant one. Its inverse is $I-vf$, since $(vf)^2=0$. A <symplectic transvection> is obtained by taking $f=cB(-,v)$ for a nondegenerate <alternating bilinear form>.

= Transvection conjugacy over a finite field
{parent=Transvection}

Every nonidentity <transvection> has an adapted basis in which it is $I+E_{12}$. Thus all such maps are conjugate in the <general linear group>. In dimension at least three, a scaling of a third basis vector centralizes this matrix and has arbitrary nonzero determinant; adjusting a conjugator by this scaling proves conjugacy in the <special linear group> as well.

= Image obstruction by an annihilating functional

= Semisimple linear operator
{parent=Linear operator}

A <linear operator> is semisimple if it becomes <diagonalizable> over an algebraic closure; equivalently, its <minimal polynomial> has no repeated root over that closure. If an invertible operator $A$ has finite order $r$ prime to the <characteristic of a field>, its <minimal polynomial> divides $X^r-1$. The derivative $rX^{r-1}$ is coprime to this polynomial, so $A$ is semisimple. Its invariant irreducible summands over a <finite field> become multiplication by scalars in suitable <finite field extensions>.

= Locally nilpotent operator
{parent=Linear operator}

Differentiation on the <polynomial ring> is locally nilpotent in characteristic zero but has no uniform nilpotence exponent.

= Algebraic exponential of a locally nilpotent operator
{parent=Locally nilpotent operator}
{title2=$\exp(T)v=\sum_{j\geq0}T^jv/j!$}

Over a field of characteristic zero, a <locally nilpotent operator> has an algebraically defined exponential, because the sum is finite for each vector. Its inverse is $\exp(-T)$. The nilpotence bound may depend on the vector. In an <sl2 Lie algebra> <Verma module>, the raising operator is locally nilpotent but the lowering operator is not, so the latter's exponential does not define an endomorphism of the ordinary algebraic module.

= Operator commutator
{parent=Linear operator}
{title2=$[A,B]=AB-BA$}

The commutator measures failure of two operators to commute, on a common domain where the products are defined. Direct expansion gives $[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B$. A common <eigenvector> of $A,B$ lies in the kernel of $[A,B]$, so an injective commutator excludes all nonzero common <eigenvectors>. A nonzero commutator need not itself be injective, and therefore does not alone exclude an individual common <eigenvector>.

= Multiplication operator
{parent=Linear operator}
{wiki}
{title2=$M_fu=fu$}

For a finite almost-everywhere measurable function $f$, the multiplication operator on <L2 space> has maximal <operator domain> $D(M_f)=\{u\in L^2:fu\in L^2\}$. It is a <closed operator>: if $u_n\to u$ and $fu_n\to v$ in <L2 space>, subsequences converge almost everywhere, giving $v=fu$. Its domain is dense, since truncating a function to the sets $|f|\leq n$ approximates it in <L2 space>. If $f$ is bounded, this is a bounded <linear operator> of norm equal to the essential supremum of $|f|$.

= Spectrum of a real multiplication operator
{parent=Multiplication operator}
{title2=$\sigma(M_m)=\operatorname{essran}_\mu m$}

For a real <measurable function> $m$ on a <sigma-finite measure> space, multiplication on $D(M_m)=\{f\in L^2:mf\in L^2\}$ is an <unbounded self-adjoint operator>. Testing its adjoint relation on $\{|m|\leq n\}$ identifies the same maximal domain. Outside the <essential range>, multiplication by $(m-z)^{-1}$ is a bounded resolvent. Inside it, normalized indicators of finite-positive-measure sets where $m$ is close to $z$ are <approximate eigenvectors>, so the inverse cannot be bounded.

= Projection
{parent=Linear operator}
{disambiguate=linear algebra}
{wiki}
{title2=$P^2=P$}

{synonym}

= Linear projection
{synonym}

= Projection
{synonym}

= Projector
{synonym}

A <linear projection> is an <idempotent> <linear operator>: $P^2=P$. Every vector decomposes as $x=Px+(I-P)x$, into the <image of a linear map> and the <kernel of a linear map>. Those two <vector subspaces> form a <direct sum>. An <orthogonal projection> additionally uses the <orthogonal complement> in an <inner product space>.

= Kadec-Snobar projection bound
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{c}
{title2=$\|P\|\le\sqrt{\dim E}$}

Every finite-dimensional <vector subspace> $E$ of a <Banach space> $X$ is the range of a bounded <linear projection> with the displayed norm bound. In maximal inscribed Euclidean or Hermitian <ellipsoid> coordinates, write the <John contact decomposition> as $\sum_rc_ru_ru_r^*=I_E$. Its contact functionals $\phi_r(x)=u_r^*x$ have norm one on $E$. Extend them to $X$ by the <Hahn-Banach theorem> and set $Px=\sum_rc_r\widetilde\phi_r(x)u_r$. This restricts to the identity on $E$. The contact synthesis operator has Euclidean norm one, the weights sum to $\dim E$, and the Euclidean unit ball is contained in the norm unit ball; these facts give $\|Px\|_E\le\sqrt{\dim E}\|x\|_X$.

= Inner product adapted to an idempotent linear map
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$\langle u,v\rangle_P=\langle Pu,Pv\rangle+\langle(I-P)u,(I-P)v\rangle$}

For an <idempotent linear map> $P$ on a finite-dimensional real <vector space>, take any positive definite <inner product> on that space. The displayed new <inner product> is positive definite since its squared norm vanishes only if both $Pu$ and $(I-P)u$ vanish. It makes the <kernel of a linear map> and <image of a linear map> orthogonal. Since $P^2=P$, one has $\langle Pu,v\rangle_P=\langle u,Pv\rangle_P$; thus $P$ becomes an <orthogonal projection>.

= Similarity classification of idempotent linear maps
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}

If $T^2=T$, every <vector> splits as $v=(v-Tv)+Tv$ in $\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T$. On these summands $T$ is respectively zero and the identity. Its <matrix> in an adapted <basis> is therefore $\operatorname{diag}(0,I_r)$, where $r$ is its <rank of a linear map>. Two <idempotent linear maps> on the same finite-dimensional <vector space> are <similar matrices> exactly when their ranks agree.

= Oblique projection
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$Q(v+w)=v\quad(H=V\oplus W)$}

If two <closed subspaces of a Hilbert space> give $H=V\oplus W$, the projection onto $V$ along $W$ fixes $V$ and vanishes on $W$. It need not be an <orthogonal projection>. The <bounded inverse theorem> applied to the addition map $(v,w)\mapsto v+w$ on the complete component spaces proves boundedness of the component maps. With both complementary subspaces nonzero, its <operator norm> and that of $I-Q$ equal $\sec\theta_{V,W^\perp}$, with the <directed subspace angle> convention. Indeed, for fixed $v\in V$, the smallest possible <norm> of $v+w$ over $w\in W$ is $\|P_{W^\perp}v\|$, which proves the formula for $\|Q\|$. Equality with $\|I-Q\|$ follows from the block representation $Q=\begin{pmatrix}I&B\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ on $V\oplus V^\perp$: both squared <operator norms> are $1+\|B\|^2$. If a summand is zero, $Q$ is zero or the <identity operator>, and one complementary <operator norm> is zero; those cases must be treated directly.

= Finite-dimensional Hilbert sampling reconstruction
{parent=Oblique projection}
{title2=$\widetilde f=(P_S|_T)^{-1}P_Sf$}

For equal finite-dimensional <closed subspaces of a Hilbert space> $T,S$, positivity of $\cos\theta_{T,S}$ makes $P_S|_T$ invertible. The displayed reconstruction is the unique element of $T$ whose <orthogonal projection> onto $S$ matches that of $f$. It is the <oblique projection> onto $T$ along $S^\perp$. Its <operator norm> is the value of the <secant function> at the <directed subspace angle>, and its error is at most that <secant function> value times the best <orthogonal projection> error. The lower error bound follows from the <Pythagorean identity>. Zero-dimensional cases are handled directly.

For a <unitary operator> $U$ on a <Hilbert space>, the <Cesaro averages>

converge strongly to the <orthogonal projection> onto the fixed-point subspace $\ker(U-I)$.

= Orthogonal decomposition for unitary ergodic averages
{parent=Von Neumann mean ergodic theorem}

For a <unitary operator> $U$, the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{Ran}(I-U)$ is $\ker(I-U^*)=\ker(I-U)$. On the fixed space, <Cesaro averages> act as the identity. = Commuting operators
{parent=Linear operator}

= Two-by-two anticommutator characteristic polynomial

= Matrix pencil

= Total nonnegativity of a matrix
{parent=Matrix}

= Totally nonnegative matrix
{synonym}

A real rectangular <matrix> is totally nonnegative if every square minor, with row and column indices in increasing order, is nonnegative. A nonnegative bidiagonal <matrix> is totally nonnegative, and products of compatible <totally nonnegative matrices> have the same property by the <Cauchy-Binet formula>. This controls signs of inverse entries and underlies stability and shape-preserving properties of <B-spline> collocation. Total nonnegativity permits zero minors; strict total positivity requires every such minor to be positive.

= Checkerboard inverse of a totally nonnegative matrix
{parent=Total nonnegativity of a matrix}
{title2=$(-1)^{i+j}(A^{-1})_{ij}\geq0$}

If an invertible square <totally nonnegative matrix> $A$ has size $n$, then $\det A>0$ and
$$
(-1)^{i+j}(A^{-1})_{ij}\geq0.
$$
Indeed the <adjugate identity> gives $(A^{-1})_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{\widehat j,\widehat i}/\det A$, and the deleted-row/deleted-column minor is nonnegative. Consequently an alternating vector $v_j=(-1)^j$ gives $(A^{-1}v)_i=(-1)^i\sum_j|(A^{-1})_{ij}|$.

= Hadamard matrix
{c}

A <Hadamard matrix> is a real square <matrix> with entries $+1$ and $-1$ whose rows are pairwise <orthogonal>. Each row has squared <norm> $n$, so $HH^T=nI$ and $H/\sqrt n$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. In particular $H$ is invertible, with $H^{-1}=H^T/n$. The four-by-four example

is symmetric and satisfies $H^2=4I$. It supplies an exact intertwiner in <four-tile mixed-boundary transplantation between a disk and a nonorientable surface>.

The row lattice of an integer <matrix> with $m$ rows is the set of their integer linear combinations. = Row Hermite normal form in rank two
{parent=Hermite normal form}
{title2=$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix},\quad a,d>0,\ 0\leq b<d$}

For a <finite-index subgroup> $L\leq\mathbb Z^2$, its projection to the first coordinate is $a\mathbb Z$ and its intersection with the second axis is $\{0\}\times d\mathbb Z$, with $a,d>0$. Choose $(a,b)\in L$, reducing $b$ modulo $d$. Together with $(0,d)$ it is a <basis> of this <row lattice>: subtracting a multiple of $(a,b)$ from any vector leaves a vector on the second axis. These parameters are unique, and reduction of the two coordinates shows $[\mathbb Z^2:L]=ad$. Consequently an integral <matrix> of positive <determinant> $n$ has a unique representative of this form under left multiplication by $SL_2(\mathbb Z)$, with $ad=n$. This proves the lattice facts underlying <determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators>.

= Doubly stochastic matrix
{parent=Matrix}
{wiki}

A square real <matrix> is doubly stochastic if its entries are nonnegative and every row and column sums to $1$. Its positive-entry support satisfies the condition of the <Hall marriage theorem>: for a row subset $S$, $|S|\leq|N(S)|$ by summing column capacities. Consequently its support contains a <permutation matrix>.

= Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem
{c}
{parent=Doubly stochastic matrix}

Every <doubly stochastic matrix> is a <convex combination> of <permutation matrices>. Equivalently, the <extreme points> of the doubly stochastic <convex polytope> are exactly the permutation matrices. The extreme-point assertion follows from an <alternating-cycle perturbation of a doubly stochastic matrix>; the convex-hull assertion follows because a bounded finite-dimensional polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. Permutation matrices are extreme since their zero entries force the same zeros in every convex decomposition.

= Birkhoff decomposition by support matchings
{c}
{title2=$A=\sum_i\alpha_iP_i$}
{parent=Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem}

For a <doubly stochastic matrix>, the <Hall marriage theorem> supplies a <perfect matching> in its positive-entry support. Subtract the smallest matched entry times the corresponding <permutation matrix>. The residual has equal row and column sums and smaller support unless it vanishes. Repeating gives a <convex combination> of <permutation matrices>. With $r$ initially positive entries, there are at most $r-n+1$ terms, since a positive-mass residual has at least $n$ positive entries. Elementary augmenting-path matching gives $O(n^5)$ arithmetic operations.

= Alternating-cycle perturbation of a doubly stochastic matrix
{parent=Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem}

For a <doubly stochastic matrix> with a fractional entry, form the row-column <bipartite graph> of entries strictly between zero and one. Every incident vertex has degree at least two, so there is an even cycle. A matrix supported on that cycle with alternating entries $+1,-1$ has zero row and column sums. A sufficiently small positive multiple can be both added and subtracted while preserving entry bounds. The original matrix is then the midpoint of two distinct doubly stochastic matrices, so it is not an <extreme point>.

= Nonnegative matrix

A real <matrix> is nonnegative when every entry is nonnegative. This entrywise condition differs from being a <positive semidefinite matrix>: the <symmetric matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is nonnegative but has <eigenvalues> $1$ and $-1$.

= Irreducible nonnegative matrix
{parent=Nonnegative matrix}

A square <nonnegative matrix> $A$ is irreducible if for each pair $(i,j)$ there is an integer $k\geq0$ with $(A^k)_{ij}>0$. This says that every index can reach every other through positive entries. The <Perron–Frobenius theorem> then gives a positive leading <eigenvector> and an algebraically <simple eigenvalue>. Irreducibility alone permits other <eigenvalues> of the same <modulus>: the cyclic permutation <matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has <eigenvalues> $1,-1$.

= Primitive nonnegative matrix
{parent=Irreducible nonnegative matrix}

A square <nonnegative matrix> $A$ is primitive if some positive integer power $A^k$ has strictly positive entries. It is therefore an <irreducible nonnegative matrix>. The <Perron–Frobenius theorem> gives a leading <eigenvalue> whose <modulus> is strictly larger than that of every other <eigenvalue>. Every strictly positive <matrix> is primitive; the two-cycle permutation <matrix> is irreducible but not primitive.

= Perron–Frobenius theorem
{parent=Nonnegative matrix}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$|\lambda_j|<\lambda_1\quad(j>1)$}

The strictly positive case states that a real square <matrix> $W$ with $W_{ij}>0$ has a positive <simple eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and positive left and right <eigenvectors>, with all other <eigenvalues> strictly smaller in <modulus>. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> applied to $v\mapsto Wv/(\mathbf1^TWv)$ on the nonnegative unit <simplex> gives $v>0$ and $Wv=\lambda v$. For another <eigenvector> $z$, maximize $|z_i|/v_i$; the <triangle inequality> gives $|\mu|\leq\lambda$. Equality forces every <modulus> ratio and <complex argument> to agree because every entry is positive, so $z$ is proportional to $v$ and $\mu=\lambda$. Apply the same argument to $W^T$ for a positive left <eigenvector>; its positive pairing with $v$ rules out a <generalized eigenvector> at $\lambda$, establishing algebraic simplicity. For merely nonnegative matrices a nonnegative leading <eigenvector> exists. An <irreducible nonnegative matrix> has a positive leading <eigenvector> and simple Perron <eigenvalue>; a <primitive nonnegative matrix> has the strict <modulus> gap.

{parent=Strictly diagonally dominant matrix}

= Analytic determinant square root for accretive symmetric matrices
{parent=Matrix logarithm}
{title2=$d(A)=\exp[\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Log}A]$}

On complex symmetric <matrices> with positive-definite real part, the <determinant> has a nonzero analytic square root normalized positively on real positive <matrices>. It is continued within this accretive domain. For $A=G+iB$, put $C=G^{-1/2}BG^{-1/2}$ with real <eigenvalues> $b_j$; then $d(A)=\sqrt{\det G}\prod_j\sqrt{1+ib_j}$, using positive-real-part roots for each factor. The principal <matrix> logarithm instead tracks all factors consistently.

= Diagonal logarithm concavity bound
{parent=Matrix logarithm}
{title2=$\langle v|\log A|v\rangle\leq\log\langle v|A|v\rangle$}

For a positive-definite <Hermitian operator> $A=\sum_jt_j|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|$ and a unit vector $v$, the weights $|\langle v|u_j\rangle|^2$ form a probability distribution. Scalar logarithmic concavity bounds their average of $\log t_j$ by the logarithm of their average of $t_j$. Positive semidefinite operators follow by a limiting convention. Using the eigenbasis of a <density operator> $\rho$ gives $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq D(r\|q)$, where $r$ is the spectrum of $\rho$ and $q$ the diagonal of $\sigma$ in that basis. This yields <nonnegativity of quantum relative entropy> without commutativity.

= Existence of a logarithm for every invertible complex matrix
{parent=Matrix logarithm}

An invertible complex <Jordan block> $\lambda I+N$, with $N^s=0$, has logarithm $\ell I+\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}(-1)^{k+1}(N/\lambda)^k/k$, where $e^\ell=\lambda$. Applying this construction blockwise and conjugating back proves surjectivity of the <matrix exponential> on the complex <general linear group>. There is no globally single-valued continuous choice of logarithm.

= Klein's inequality
{c}
{parent=Matrix logarithm}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}[A(\ln A-\ln B)]\geq\operatorname{Tr}(A-B)$}

For positive-definite <Hermitian matrices> $A,B$, Klein's inequality gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[A(\ln A-\ln B)]\geq\operatorname{Tr}(A-B),
$$
with equality exactly when $A=B$. To prove it, let $a_i,b_j$ be their <eigenvalues> and $u_i,v_j$ their orthonormal eigenvectors. The weights $w_{ij}=|\langle u_i,v_j\rangle|^2$ have row and column sums one. The difference between the two sides is
$$
\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}\left[a_i\ln\frac{a_i}{b_j}-a_i+b_j\right]\geq0
$$
by the scalar <logarithm inequality> $\ln t\leq t-1$. Equality requires $a_i=b_j$ whenever $w_{ij}>0$, implying $A=B$. Limits extend the result to positive semidefinite matrices with the appropriate support condition. Applying it to trace-one matrices proves <nonnegativity of quantum relative entropy>.

The <column rank> of a <matrix> is the dimension of its image as a <linear map>, equivalently the dimension of the span of its columns. The <Kronecker sum> of square <matrices> combines their <Kronecker products> with <identity matrices>. If $Ax=\lambda x$ and $By=\mu y$, then $x\otimes y$ is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> $\lambda+\mu$. Tensor-product spatial grids therefore turn separated coordinate Laplacians into a <Kronecker sum>. Two <Hermitian matrices> give a Hermitian <Kronecker sum>, allowing an <energy method> without computing every eigenvector.
