= Vector space
{wiki}
{title2=$V$}

A vector space over a <field> $\mathbb F$ is a <set> $V$ equipped with <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication> satisfying the <vector space axioms>. Its <vectors> can represent directed displacements, lists of measurements, <polynomials>, or entire <functions>. The common structure is that adding two elements and multiplying an element by a <scalar> remain meaningful operations inside the same space.

For example, $\mathbb R^2$ uses coordinatewise addition and scaling. With
$$
b_1=(2,1),\qquad b_2=(1,2),\qquad v=(5,4),
$$
we have $v=2b_1+b_2$. Moving twice along $b_1$ and once along $b_2$ reaches the same <vector> as five horizontal and four vertical steps. The two descriptions refer to different <bases> of the same space. A <basis> makes such descriptions unique; a <spanning set> makes them possible.

\Image[vector-basis-coordinates.png]
{title=The same vector has coordinates 5,4 in the Cartesian basis and 2,1 in an oblique basis}

The space $\mathbb R[x]_{\leq2}$ of real <polynomials> of degree at most two has <basis> $1,x,x^2$: the <polynomial> $3-2x+x^2$ is the <linear combination> with coordinates $(3,-2,1)$. The <zero vector> here is the zero <polynomial>, and addition combines <coefficients>. A <derivative> is a <linear map> on this space; its <matrix> records how it acts on the three <basis vectors>. The same coordinate machinery therefore handles both geometric motion and operations on <functions>.

The choice of <field> matters. The <complex numbers> form a one-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb C$ with <basis> $1$, but a two-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb R$ with <basis> $1,i$. The question "how many independent directions?" has an answer only after the allowed <scalars> are specified. Likewise, polynomials of degree exactly two do not form a <vector space>: subtraction can remove the leading term, and the zero <polynomial> is absent.

The usefulness of the abstraction is that a <linear map> is determined by its values on a <basis>. Once those images are known, <linear combinations> give its action on every <vector>. The <rank-nullity theorem> relates the number of attainable outputs to the number of invisible input directions, while <change of basis> lets us choose coordinates suited to the problem. <Eigenvectors>, for example, choose directions on which repeated application of a <linear operator> becomes scalar multiplication.

A historical source is Hermann Grassmann's *Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre* (1844). Its full title advertises applications to other branches of mathematics, statics, mechanics, magnetism and crystallography; the original edition is digitized by ETH-Bibliothek. This gives an early setting for treating geometric quantities algebraically across different applications, rather than attributing the modern axiom list to that title alone.https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/1169335[Grassmann, Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre, Leipzig, 1844; ETH-Bibliothek Rar 5128]{ref}

= Cardinality of a vector space
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$|V|=\max(|K|,\dim_KV)$}

For a nonzero <vector space> with an infinite <field> or infinite <vector space basis>[basis], <cardinality> is the displayed maximum, since vectors are finite <linear combinations> of <basis vectors>. Over a <finite field>, infinite underlying size forces infinite <dimension of a vector space>[dimension]. Over a countably infinite <field>, every positive finite <dimension of a vector space>[dimension] is already countably infinite. The <first-order theory>[theory] of infinite underlying spaces is therefore categorical in every infinite cardinal for a <finite field>, but only in uncountable cardinals for a countably infinite <field>.

= Vector space over a division ring
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$V_\Delta\text{ or }{}_\Delta V$}

A <module> over a <division ring> has the usual <basis> and <linear independence> theory of a <vector space>, with the side of scalar multiplication specified. For a right <module>, a finite relation has the form $\sum_i v_i a_i=0$. Every nonzero <endomorphism> of a one-dimensional right <module> is invertible. If $V$ is a <simple module> on the left over $R$, evaluation makes it a right vector space over the <opposite ring> $\operatorname{End}_R(V)^{\mathrm{op}}$, a <division ring> by <Schur's lemma>. This is the scalar structure used by the <Jacobson density theorem>.

= Complexification of a real vector space
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$V_\mathbb C=V\otimes_\mathbb R\mathbb C$}

The complexification of a real <vector space> $V$ allows complex scalar coefficients while preserving its real linear relations. It can be constructed as the <tensor product> $V\otimes_\mathbb R\mathbb C$, or as formal sums $u+iv$ with $u,v\in V$. A real basis of $V$ becomes a complex basis of $V_\mathbb C$, so its complex dimension equals the original real dimension. Every real <linear map> extends uniquely by complex linearity. In particular a <real representation> extends by viewing its real matrices as complex matrices. Complexifying the <realification of a complex representation> produces the original complex representation together with its complex conjugate; the coordinate change $(u,v)\mapsto(u+iv,u-iv)$ exhibits the two blocks.

= Vector space axioms
{parent=Vector space}

Addition makes $V$ an <Abelian group>: for all $u,v,w\in V$, $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$ and $u+v=v+u$, there is a <zero vector> $0$, and every $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$. <Scalar multiplication> satisfies, for $a,b\in\mathbb F$,
$$
1v=v,\qquad (ab)v=a(bv),\qquad a(u+v)=au+av,\qquad(a+b)v=av+bv.
$$
Both operations take values in $V$. These requirements make manipulating <linear combinations> independent of how sums are grouped or expanded.

A sequence space is a <vector space> of scalar <sequences>, closed under coordinatewise addition and scalar multiplication. Examples include the <l-p sequence space>, the <l-infinity sequence space> and spaces of convergent sequences.

= Partial-sum norm on absolutely summable sequences
{parent=Sequence space}
{title2=$q(x)=\sup_k|\sum_{n\leq k}x_n|$}

On $\ell^1$, $q$ is a <norm>, bounded above by $\|x\|_1$. The partial-sum map $Jx=(\sum_{n\leq k}x_n)_k$ is an isometric linear embedding into the space $c$ of convergent sequences with the <supremum norm>. Its range contains every eventually constant sequence: take successive differences of such a sequence to obtain a finitely supported element of $\ell^1$. These sequences are dense in $c$, so the <completion of a normed space> is $c$.

The range is proper: partial sums of the alternating harmonic series converge but their successive differences do not belong to $\ell^1$. The finite truncations of these differences are $q$-Cauchy and cannot have a limit in $\ell^1$. Thus this normed version of $\ell^1$ is incomplete, despite the completeness of its usual norm.

= Zero vector
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$0$}

The <zero vector> of a <vector space> is its additive identity: $v+0=0+v=v$ for every <vector> $v$. It is unique, because if $0'$ is another such identity then $0=0+0'=0'$. Distributivity gives $\alpha0=\alpha(0+0)=\alpha0+\alpha0$, so $\alpha0=0$ after cancellation. In a coordinate <vector> space it has every coordinate zero.

= Quaternionic structure on a complex vector space
{parent=Antilinear map}
{title2=$J^2=-1$}

An antilinear endomorphism $J$ with square $-1$ equips a <complex vector space> with a quaternionic structure: complex multiplication supplies the quaternionic generator $i$ and $J$ supplies $j$, satisfying $Ji=-iJ$. Such a space has even complex dimension. There is no invariant complex line, because $Jv=cv$ would imply $-v=|c|^2v$. In dimension four the invariant complex two-planes are quaternionic lines, forming $\mathbb{HP}^1\cong S^4$. This is the <Euclidean reality structure on twistor space> used in <twistor theory>.

= Majorization
{parent=Real vector space}
{title2=$x\prec y$}
{wiki}

For real vectors of equal length and equal sum, write $x\prec y$ when $\sum_{j=1}^k x_j^\downarrow\leq\sum_{j=1}^k y_j^\downarrow$ for every proper initial segment; the downward arrow means decreasing rearrangement. Majorization compares how unevenly a fixed total is distributed. The uniform probability vector is majorized by every probability vector, while $(1,0,\ldots,0)$ majorizes every probability vector. The order controls exact pure-state entanglement conversion in <Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem>.

= Majorization of summable sequences
{parent=Majorization}
{title2=$x\prec y$}

For decreasing positive sequences in $\ell^1$, write $x\prec y$ if every initial <partial sum> of $x$ is at most the corresponding <partial sum> of $y$ and the finite total sums agree. The finiteness requirement matters: equality of two divergent totals interpreted as infinity is too weak to guarantee a <doubly stochastic realization of summable-sequence majorization>.

= Doubly stochastic realization of summable-sequence majorization
{parent=Majorization of summable sequences}
{title2=$x_i=\sum_jp_{ij}y_j$}

Decreasing strictly positive <summable sequences> with equal totals and $x\prec y$ admit an infinite <doubly stochastic matrix> realizing the displayed relation. Choose $k$ with $y_k\geq x_1>y_{k+1}$ and average the pair to obtain $x_1$ and $y_k+y_{k+1}-x_1$. The remaining decreasing sequence majorizes the remaining target sequence: short initial sums contain values at least $x_1$, and longer sums are the old sums minus $x_1$. Repeat on the tail, freezing one normalized finite-support row at each stage. Limit column sums are at most one; <Tonelli's theorem>, equal finite totals and strict positivity of every $y_j$ force every column sum to equal one.

= Vector space over a finite field
{parent=Vector space}

If $\mathbb F_q$ is a finite field, a vector space of finite dimension $d$ over $\mathbb F_q$ has $q^d$ elements. Every infinite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F_p$ is an infinite elementary abelian $p$-group under addition.

= Types of pairs of vectors over a finite field
{parent=Vector space over a finite field}
{title2=$|S_2(T)|=q+3,\quad|F_2/E_2(T)|=2^{q+3}$}

For infinite spaces over a <field> of size $q$, two-variable types are determined by the kernel of the <linear map> sending $(a,b)$ to $ax+by$. Its ranks give the zero pair, $q+1$ one-dimensional directions and the independent-pair type. All $q+3$ types are isolated by finite formulas. <Quantifier elimination> makes the <Lindenbaum algebra> their full power-set <Boolean algebra>. The one-variable algebra has just the zero and nonzero <atom of a Boolean algebra>[atoms], hence four <elements>.

= Finite-field Kakeya set
{parent=Vector space over a finite field}

A subset of $\mathbb F_q^n$ is a finite-field Kakeya set when it contains an <affine line in a vector space> in every one-dimensional direction. The line position may depend on the direction. The <polynomial method in combinatorics> yields the <finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound> on its <cardinality>.

= Tangent-line finite-field Kakeya construction
{parent=Finite-field Kakeya set}
{title2=$K=\{(x,y):x^2-y\text{ is a square}\}\cup\{x=0\}$}

For odd characteristic, the union of the lines $y=mx-m^2/4$ contains a line of every finite slope. For fixed $x$, completing the square shows that it comprises the $(p+1)/2$ values of $y$ for which $x^2-y$ is a square, including zero. Adding the vertical line $x=0$ completes the directions and gives cardinality $(p^2+2p-1)/2=(1/2+o(1))p^2$. This is a <finite-field Kakeya set> whose density tends to one half.

= Finite-field Kakeya polynomial bound
{parent=Finite-field Kakeya set}
{title2=$|A|\geq\binom{q+n-1}{n}\geq q^n/n!$}

For a <finite-field Kakeya set> $A\subseteq\mathbb F_q^n$, a smaller <cardinality> would give a nonzero <polynomial> of <total degree of a polynomial> at most $q-1$ vanishing on $A$, by the <dimension of a bounded-total-degree polynomial space>. Its restriction to each complete <affine line in a vector space> has $q$ <roots of a polynomial> and degree less than $q$, hence vanishes identically. The top <homogeneous polynomial> part then vanishes in every direction. A <polynomial> of degree less than $q$ in each variable cannot vanish everywhere on $\mathbb F_q^n$ unless it is zero, giving a contradiction. For the <prime field>, this is the <polynomial nonvanishing below the field size>.

= Span
{synonym}

The linear span of a subset $S$ of a <vector space> is the <set> of all finite <linear combinations> of elements of $S$. It is the smallest <vector subspace> containing $S$: every <vector subspace> containing the original <vectors> must contain their sums and <scalar multiples>.

In $\mathbb R^3$, the <linear span> of $(1,0,0)$ is a line, the <linear span> of $(1,0,0),(0,1,0)$ is the plane $z=0$, and adding $(0,0,1)$ gives all of $\mathbb R^3$. Adding $(2,0,0)$ to the first set changes none of its attainable <vectors>, because it is already a <scalar multiple> of $(1,0,0)$. Thus a <spanning set> can contain redundant generators; <linear independence> identifies when this redundancy is absent. In an infinite-dimensional space, an algebraic <linear span> still uses finite sums; a <closed linear span> can also include <limits> in a specified <norm>.

= Closed linear span
{parent=Linear span}
{title2=$\overline{\operatorname{span}}S$}

In a <normed vector space>, the closed linear span of $S$ is the <closure> of its <linear span> in the <norm topology>. It includes <limits> of finite <linear combinations>. In a <Hilbert space>, an infinite <orthonormal basis> spans in this closed sense, allowing norm-convergent series in addition to finite sums.

= Annihilator of a second-difference span in l2
{parent=Closed linear span}
{title2=$x_k=e_k-2re_{k+1}+e_{k+2},\quad r>1$}

Let $M$ be the closed span of these vectors in the real <l2 sequence space>. A vector $\beta$ is orthogonal to $M$ exactly when $\beta_{k+2}-2r\beta_{k+1}+\beta_k=0$. The characteristic roots are $r\pm\sqrt{r^2-1}$; one grows geometrically and the other, $\rho$, lies in $(0,1)$. Square summability eliminates the growing solution, so $M^\perp=\operatorname{span}\{(\rho^{j-1})_{j\geq1}\}$. Hence $M$ is a closed hyperplane, namely the kernel of the bounded functional $\alpha\mapsto\sum_j\alpha_j\rho^{j-1}$. This combines a recurrence with <density by annihilators>.

= Induced linear map on a quotient vector space
{parent=Quotient vector space}
{title2=$\overline T(v+U)=T(v)+U$}

= Induced linear maps on quotient vector spaces
{synonym}

For a <linear map> $T:V\to V$ and an <invariant subspace> $U$, the rule $\overline T(v+U)=T(v)+U$ defines a <linear map> on the <quotient vector space> $V/U$. Indeed, two representatives differ by an element of $U$, so their images also differ by an element of $U$. Conversely, this rule is well-defined only if $T(U)\subseteq U$, by comparing every $u\in U$ with zero.

= Codimension of a vector subspace
{parent=Quotient vector space}
{title2=$\operatorname{codim}_XW=\dim(X/W)$}

= Vector-space codimension
{synonym}

For a <vector subspace> $W$ of $X$, its codimension is the <dimension> of the <quotient vector space> $X/W$. This definition also applies when $X$ is infinite-dimensional. If $X=W\oplus E$ is a <direct sum>, the map $e\mapsto e+W$ is a <linear isomorphism> from $E$ to $X/W$, so $\operatorname{codim}_XW=\dim E$. In particular, the <kernel> of a nonzero <linear functional> has codimension one: normalize $Te=1$ and write $x=(x-(Tx)e)+(Tx)e$.

= Parallel lines as a quotient vector space
{parent=Quotient vector space}
{title2=$\mathbb R^3/\operatorname{span}(u)$}

For fixed $u\ne0$, the parallel <affine lines> $l_x=x+\operatorname{span}(u)$ are precisely the cosets of the indicated <quotient vector space>. Changing representatives adds a multiple of $u$, so $l_x+l_y=l_{x+y}$ and $a l_x=l_{ax}$ are well-defined. The zero is the whole line $l_0$, not a single point. If $(u,b_1,b_2)$ is a <basis>, the two line classes $l_{b_1},l_{b_2}$ form a <basis> of the quotient by spanning and independence modulo $\operatorname{span}(u)$.

= Vector subspace
{parent=Vector space}

= Linear subspace
{synonym}

A vector subspace is a nonempty subset of a <vector space> closed under <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>, and is therefore itself a <vector space>. Equivalently, whenever $u,v$ belong to the subset, so does $au+bv$ for all allowed <scalars> $a,b$. In particular it contains the <zero vector>.

For example, the plane $W=\{(x,y,z):x+y+z=0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^3$ is a <vector subspace>, with <basis> $(1,-1,0),(1,0,-1)$. Indeed every element is $(-y)(1,-1,0)+(-z)(1,0,-1)$. The parallel plane $x+y+z=1$ misses the <zero vector> and is an <affine subspace> instead. Homogeneous constraints produce <vector subspaces>: $W$ is the <kernel> of the <linear map> $(x,y,z)\mapsto x+y+z$. This lets us describe all solutions of a homogeneous <linear equation> through a <basis> of its <kernel>.

= Coordinate subspace
{parent=Vector subspace}
{title2=$E_{\mathcal B}=\operatorname{span}\{e_i:i\in\mathcal B\}$}

For a chosen <basis> $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$, the <vector subspace> spanned by a specified collection of <basis vectors>. In the standard <basis> of <Euclidean space>, its <vectors> have zero coordinates outside $\mathcal B$. The <block coordinate gradient> is the <orthogonal projection> of the full <gradient> onto this subspace. The <fixed coordinate subspace> consisting of the first $k$ coordinates is a particular example.

= Proper vector subspace
{parent=Vector subspace}
{title2=$U\subsetneq V$}

A proper <vector subspace> of a <vector space> is one strictly smaller than the ambient space. In a finite-dimensional <vector space>, this is equivalent to having smaller <dimension>: a <basis> of the subspace can be extended to a <basis> of the ambient space, and strict inclusion requires at least one additional <vector>.

A flag is a strictly nested chain of <vector subspaces> of a fixed <vector space>. A <complete flag> contains a subspace of every possible dimension. For an action by <linear maps>, an invariant flag means that each of its <vector subspaces> is an <invariant subspace>. A <basis> adapted to an invariant complete flag makes all these maps upper triangular, connecting <complete flags> to <simultaneous triangularization of a Lie algebra representation>.

= Complete flag
{parent=Flag (linear algebra)}
{title2=$0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V$}

A complete flag in an $n$-dimensional <vector space> is a nested sequence of <vector subspaces> with $\dim V_i=i$. An action preserves such a flag exactly when its <matrices> are upper triangular in a <basis> adapted to the flag. The <Lie theorem> supplies an invariant complete flag for every complex finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> of a <solvable Lie algebra>.

= Fixed coordinate subspace
{parent=Vector subspace}

The <fixed coordinate subspace> of dimension $k$ in $\mathbb R^p$ consists of vectors whose coordinates after the first $k$ vanish. = Sum of subspaces
{synonym}

The sum of vector subspaces $U,W\leq V$ is
$$
U+W=\{u+w:u\in U,\ w\in W\}.
$$
It is the smallest <vector subspace> containing both $U$ and $W$.

= Subspace supplement with a prescribed intersection
{parent=Sum of vector subspaces}
{title2=$W\cap W'=U,\quad W+W'=V$}

For <vector subspaces> $U\subseteq W\subseteq V$ of a finite-dimensional <vector space>, such a $W'$ always exists. Extend a <basis> of $U$ to one of $W$, then extend again to one of $V$. Let $W'$ be spanned by the original <basis> of $U$ together with the vectors added in the second extension. Uniqueness of coordinates gives the displayed intersection and sum. Equivalently, choose a <direct sum> complement $C$ with $V=W\oplus C$ and put $W'=U\oplus C$. Necessarily $\dim W'=\dim V-\dim W+\dim U$. In $M_2(\mathbb R)$, the skew-symmetric subspace $U$ inside the traceless subspace $W$ admits $W'=\operatorname{span}\{I,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\}$.

= Addition map of subspaces
{parent=Sum of vector subspaces}
{title2=$g:U\times W\to U+W,\quad (u,w)\mapsto u+w$}

For <vector subspaces> $U,W$ of a common <vector space>, this is a <surjective linear map>. Its <kernel of a linear map> is $\{(x,-x):x\in U\cap W\}$, which has a <linear isomorphism> to $U\cap W$ by $x\mapsto(x,-x)$. The <rank-nullity theorem> and the <direct sum basis> give the <dimension formula for a sum of subspaces>. The paired domain keeps the two contributions separate even when their underlying <vectors> overlap.

= Positive distance between a unit sphere and a disjoint finite-dimensional subspace
{parent=Closed vector subspace}
{title2=$\operatorname{dist}(S_E,F)>0$}

If $E$ is a nonzero <closed linear subspace> of a normed ambient space and $F$ is finite-dimensional with $E\cap F=\{0\}$, the unit sphere of $E$ has positive distance from $F$. = Affine span
{synonym}

The smallest <affine subspace> containing a nonempty set $S$. For any chosen $a\in S$, the displayed formula proves existence and minimality: every containing affine subspace must contain all the indicated differences in its direction space. Equivalently it consists of finite affine combinations $\sum_i\lambda_i s_i$ with $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$. Its dimension can be smaller than the dimension of an affine hyperplane in which the set has been embedded.

= Affine hyperplane
{parent=Affine subspace}
{title2=$H=\{v\in V:\ell(v)=c\},\quad\ell\ne0$}

In a finite-dimensional real vector space, a level set of a nonzero <linear functional> is an affine hyperplane. Choosing $v_0$ in that level set gives $H=v_0+\ker\ell$, so its dimension is one less than that of the ambient space. With a Euclidean inner product, translation followed by an orthonormal basis of $\ker\ell$ identifies it isometrically with the lower-dimensional Euclidean space. The coordinate-sum constraint on balanced cut vectors is an example.

= Affine lines in a vector space
{synonym}

In a <vector space> over a <field> $k$, an affine line is a translate of a one-dimensional <vector subspace>. = Linear combination
{parent=Vector space}
{wiki}
{title2=$\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i$}

Let $V$ be a <vector space> over a specified <field> $F$, and let $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in V$. A <linear combination> is a finite sum
$$
w=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i,\qquad a_i\in F.
$$
The <coefficients> $a_i$ are <scalars> from that same <field>; each term uses <scalar multiplication>, and the terms are joined by <vector addition>. For <coefficients> in the <real numbers>, negative values reverse arrows and fractional values scale them by fractional amounts. The collection of attainable <vectors> is the <linear span>. The column picture in Strang's MIT notes interprets solving a <linear system> as choosing <coefficients> to reach a target <vector>.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/6913aa2f37dada8fdda334168b30339b_MIT18_06SCF11_Ses1.1sum.pdf[MIT 18.06SC, Strang lecture summary 1, pp. 2–3: the column picture]{ref}

For a concrete calculation over the <real numbers>, take
$$
v_1=(1,1),\qquad v_2=(1,-1),\qquad w=(3,1).
$$
Then $2v_1+v_2=(2,2)+(1,-1)=(3,1)=w$. More generally, solving $a+b=x$ and $a-b=y$ gives
$$
(x,y)=\frac{x+y}{2}v_1+\frac{x-y}{2}v_2.
$$
Every <vector> in the <Euclidean plane> is therefore a <linear combination> of $v_1,v_2$, so their <linear span> is all of $\mathbb R^2$. The target $(3,1)$ has <coefficients> $(2,1)$; the target $(0,2)$ has <coefficients> $(1,-1)$, since $(1,1)-(1,-1)=(0,2)$. The displayed formula also makes the <coefficients> unique. Consequently $(v_1,v_2)$ is an <ordered basis>, with <coordinate vector> $[(x,y)]_{(v_1,v_2)}=((x+y)/2,(x-y)/2)^T$.

= Direct sum of vector spaces
{synonym}

The direct sum $U\oplus W$ of two <vector space>[vector spaces] consists of pairs $(u,w)$ with componentwise operations. A vector space is an internal direct sum of subspaces $U$ and $W$ exactly when every vector has a unique expression $u+w$ with $u\in U$ and $w\in W$.

= Direct sum basis
{parent=Direct sum}
{title2=$((u_1,0),\ldots,(u_p,0),(0,v_1),\ldots,(0,v_q))$}

For <bases> $(u_i)$ of $U$ and $(v_j)$ of $W$, the displayed pairs form a <basis> of $U\oplus W$. Expanding each component proves spanning; comparing components of a vanishing <linear combination> proves <linear independence>. Therefore $\dim(U\oplus W)=\dim U+\dim W$ for two <finite-dimensional vector spaces>.

= Common plane for three pairwise complementary subspaces
{parent=Direct sum}

If $V=A_1\oplus A_2=A_2\oplus A_3=A_1\oplus A_3$ with nonzero subspaces, choose $0\ne v\in A_3$ and write $v=a_1+a_2$ using the first <direct sum of vector spaces>. Both components are nonzero. The plane spanned by them meets $A_1$ and $A_2$ in their component lines and meets $A_3$ in the line through $v$. Its intersection with $A_3$ cannot be the whole plane, since $a_1\notin A_3$.

= Orthogonal direct sum
{parent=Direct sum}
{title2=$V\oplus^\perp W$}

An orthogonal direct sum is a <direct sum> whose distinct summands are mutually <orthogonal>. For $v\in V$ and $w\in W$, the <Pythagorean identity> gives $\|v+w\|^2=\|v\|^2+\|w\|^2$. In a <Hilbert space>, a <closed subspace of a Hilbert space> and its <orthogonal complement> give an orthogonal direct sum of the entire space. The component maps are <orthogonal projections> and have <operator norm> one when their ranges are nonzero.

= Direct summand
{parent=Direct sum}

A <submodule> $N$ of a <module> $M$ is a direct summand if some <submodule> $L$ gives $M=N\oplus L$. Equivalently, the inclusion has a left inverse <R-module homomorphism>, or $N$ is the image of an <idempotent> <endomorphism> of $M$.

= Unimodular basis test for an integer direct summand
{parent=Direct summand}
{title2=$\det U=\pm1$}

Integer row vectors forming a square <unimodular matrix> are a basis of the corresponding <free abelian group>: the adjugate formula makes the inverse integral. The subgroup spanned by any subset of that basis is therefore a <direct summand>, complemented by the remaining basis vectors. In contrast, a proper finite-index subgroup of a free abelian group cannot be a direct summand: its nonzero finite quotient would have to embed as a subgroup of a torsion-free group.

= Dimension formula for a sum of subspaces
{parent=Direct sum}

For finite-dimensional subspaces $U,W$ of a common vector space,
$$
\dim(U+W)+\dim(U\cap W)=\dim U+\dim W.
$$

= Direct-sum complement
{parent=Direct sum}

For every subspace $U$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, extending a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$ produces a subspace $W$ such that $V=U\oplus W$.

= Linear independence
{parent=Vector space}
{wiki}
{title2=$\sum_i a_i v_i=0\iff a_1=\cdots=a_n=0$}

= Linearly independent set
{synonym}

= Linearly independent vectors
{synonym}

= Linearly independent
{synonym}

In a <vector space> $V$ over a specified <field> $F$, a finite family of <vectors> $(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ is <linearly independent> when, for <coefficients> $a_i\in F$,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_1=\cdots=a_n=0.
$$
Here the left-hand zero is the <zero vector>, while the right-hand zeros belong to the <field> $F$. Thus <linear independence> excludes every nontrivial <linear relation>. For an infinite family, require this condition for every finite subfamily.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/0bbc30e3f1d7933ea07a2d2e9ab050d9_MIT18_06SCF11_Ses1.9sum.pdf[MIT 18.06SC, Strang lecture summary 9, p. 1: linear independence]{ref}

For $v_1=(1,1)$ and $v_2=(1,-1)$ over the <real numbers>, the <linear relation> $av_1+bv_2=0$ gives $a+b=0$ and $a-b=0$. Adding and subtracting yields $2a=2b=0$, hence $a=b=0$. This verifies <linear independence> directly and explains why the <linear combination> giving $(3,1)$ has exactly the <coefficients> $(2,1)$.

A <linearly independent> family cannot contain the <zero vector>: a <coefficient> of $1$ on that entry and $0$ on every other entry would be a nontrivial <linear relation>. The empty family is <linearly independent>, because its empty sum is the <zero vector> and there are no <coefficients> that could be nonzero. <Linear independence> identifies a collection without redundant <vectors>; combined with a <spanning set>, it gives a <basis>. The <uniqueness of linear combination coefficients> makes its representations unambiguous.

= Independence of a pair is not definable over an infinite field
{parent=Linear independence}

In the one-sorted vector-space language naming each <scalar multiplication>, a <first-order formula> reduces by <quantifier elimination> to finitely many <linear equations>. An independent pair satisfies only the identically zero equations among them. A dependent pair $(x,ax)$ with nonzero $x$ satisfies the same listed equations if the <scalar multiplication>[scalar] $a$ avoids their finitely many forbidden ratios, which is possible over an infinite <field>. Thus no formula defines pair independence. The complete independent-pair type is still <consistent first-order theory>[consistent] in an infinite-dimensional <first-order model>[model]; it is nonisolated.

= Random spanning of a binary vector space
{parent=Linear independence}
{title2=$\mathbb E M=\sum_{j=1}^d(1-2^{-j})^{-1}<d+2$}

Take independent uniform samples from a $d$-dimensional <vector space> over the two-element <field>. At current <matrix rank> $r$, the existing span contains $2^r$ of the $2^d$ possible vectors, so a new sample increases the <matrix rank> with <probability> $1-2^{r-d}$. The mean waiting time for that increase is $1/(1-2^{r-d})$ by the <geometric distribution>. Adding the waiting times gives $\mathbb E M=d+\sum_{j=1}^d(2^j-1)^{-1}<d+2$. For $d\geq1$, the bound follows by separating $j=1$ and using $(2^j-1)^{-1}<2^{1-j}$ for $j\geq2$; for $d=0$, no samples are needed.

After $m=d+t$ samples, failure to span implies that some nonzero <linear functional> vanishes on all samples. Each fixed nonzero functional does so with <probability> $2^{-m}$; the <union bound> over the $2^d-1$ possibilities gives failure less than $2^{-t}$. This provides both an expected sample count and a fixed-budget high-success version of <Simon's algorithm>.

= Uniqueness of linear combination coefficients
{parent=Linear independence}
{title2=$\sum_i a_i v_i=\sum_i b_i v_i\Longrightarrow a_i=b_i$}

A family is <linearly independent> if and only if every <vector> in its <linear span> has unique <coefficients> as a <linear combination> of that family. For a short proof, subtract two representations to get $\sum_i(a_i-b_i)v_i=0$; <linear independence> forces each difference to vanish. Conversely, uniqueness applied to the <zero vector> forces every <linear relation> to use only zero <coefficients>. This explains the practical role of <linear independence>: a <coordinate vector> relative to an <ordered basis> is unambiguous.

= Minimally dependent vector family
{parent=Linear independence}

A finite <vector> family is minimally dependent if it is <linearly dependent> and every proper subfamily is <linearly independent>. For $n\geq2$, the family $e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1},\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}e_j$ in $\mathbb R^n$ is an example. The sum supplies a nonzero dependence. If the sum <vector> is omitted, the remaining <vectors> are a coordinate basis; if $e_j$ is omitted, its coordinate in any proposed relation forces the coefficient of the sum <vector> to vanish, then all other coefficients vanish. Every smaller proper subfamily lies in one of these independent $(n-1)$-element subfamilies.

= Distinct diagonal weights break a first column dependence
{parent=Minimally dependent vector family}

Let the first $k$ columns of a <matrix> be the first dependent prefix, with no zero column. Their relation space has dimension one, and every nonzero relation $b$ has at least two nonzero coordinates. If both $Ab=0$ and $A\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j)b=0$, one-dimensionality gives $\lambda_jb_j=cb_j$ for all $j$. Two nonzero coordinates would force two of the distinct weights $\lambda_j$ to agree, a contradiction.

= Linear dependence
{parent=Linear independence}
{title2=$\sum_i a_i v_i=0,\quad(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\ne0$}

= Linearly dependent
{synonym}

A finite family of <vectors> in a <vector space> over a specified <field> $F$ is <linearly dependent> if it has a nontrivial <linear relation>: $\sum_i a_i v_i=0$ with $a_i\in F$ and at least one <coefficient> nonzero. One can then solve for a <vector> whose <coefficient> is nonzero as a <linear combination> of the others; removing that entry preserves the <linear span>. Repeated <vectors> in a list give <linear dependence>: for example $(v,v)$ has the <linear relation> $1v+(-1)v=0$. A list keeps both entries even though the <set> $\{v,v\}$ has only one element. Strang's notes illustrate redundant columns by expressing one as the sum of two others.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/0bbc30e3f1d7933ea07a2d2e9ab050d9_MIT18_06SCF11_Ses1.9sum.pdf[MIT 18.06SC, Strang lecture summary 9, pp. 2–3: dependent columns]{ref}

Keep $v_1=(1,1)$ and $v_2=(1,-1)$, and add $v_3=(2,0)=v_1+v_2$. The three <vectors> still have <linear span> $\mathbb R^2$, but now
$$
v_1+v_2-v_3=0,\qquad (3,1)=2v_1+v_2=v_1+v_3.
$$
The two <coefficient> triples $(2,1,0)$ and $(1,0,1)$ differ by the nontrivial <linear relation> $(-1,-1,1)$. In fact all <linear combination> representations of this target are
$$
(c_1,c_2,c_3)=(2-t,1-t,t),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$
To check both completeness and correctness, use $c_1v_1+c_2v_2+c_3v_3=(c_1+c_3)v_1+(c_2+c_3)v_2$. <Linear independence> of $v_1,v_2$ forces $c_1+c_3=2$ and $c_2+c_3=1$, giving exactly the displayed family. Redundancy therefore preserves attainable targets while allowing their <coefficients> to move. For solving a <linear system>, these alternatives matter; for assigning unique <coordinate vectors>, one instead chooses a <basis>.

= Linear independence depends on the scalar field
{parent=Linear dependence}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C=2,\quad\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C=1$}

Consider the <vectors> $1,i$ in the <complex numbers>, where $i$ is the <imaginary unit>. With <scalar> <field> $\mathbb R$, a <linear relation> $a\cdot1+b\cdot i=0$ has $a,b\in\mathbb R$. Taking its <real part> and <imaginary part> gives $a=b=0$, so the pair is <linearly independent>. Every <complex number> $x+iy$ also has <coefficients> $(x,y)$ in the <real numbers>, so $1,i$ form a <basis> and the <dimension of a vector space> is two.

With <scalar> <field> $\mathbb C$, the same pair is <linearly dependent>, since
$$
i\cdot1+(-1)\cdot i=0
$$
uses the nonzero <coefficients> $(i,-1)$ from the <complex numbers>. The single <vector> $1$ is now a <basis>, because $z=z\cdot1$ for every <complex number> $z$, giving <dimension of a vector space> one. For example $3+i=3\cdot1+1\cdot i=(3+i)\cdot1+0\cdot i$: the second representation uses a <coefficient> unavailable over the <real numbers>. Specifying the <field> is part of the definition of <linear independence>.

= Linear relation
{parent=Linear dependence}
{title2=$\sum_i a_iv_i=0$}

A linear relation among <vectors> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ is a coefficient tuple $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ satisfying $\sum_i a_iv_i=0$. It is nontrivial if at least one coefficient is nonzero. For the <matrix> with these vectors as columns, the space of relations is its <kernel>. The vectors are <linearly independent> exactly when this kernel is zero. An invertible change of coordinates preserves every relation, since $S\sum_i a_iv_i=0$ if and only if $\sum_i a_iv_i=0$.

= Basis
{parent=Vector space}

= Vector space basis
{synonym}

= Bases
{synonym}

= Hamel basis
{synonym}
{c}

A basis is a <linearly independent> <spanning set> of <vectors> in a <vector space>. Every <vector> is a unique finite <linear combination> of <basis vectors>. Independence supplies uniqueness, and spanning supplies existence. An <ordered basis> turns the <coefficients> into a <coordinate vector>.

For $\mathcal B=(b_1,b_2)=((2,1),(1,2))$, the <matrix> with these <vectors> as columns is
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Hence the <coordinate vector> of $v=(5,4)$ is $[v]_{\mathcal B}=B^{-1}v=(2,1)$, and $B[v]_{\mathcal B}=v$ recovers the usual coordinates. The <determinant> $3$ is nonzero, so this recovery works for every <vector> in $\mathbb R^2$. A <basis> need not have perpendicular or unit-length <vectors>; an <orthonormal basis> adds those geometric conveniences when an <inner product> is available.

The point of changing <basis> is to simplify the operation being studied. <Diagonalization> uses an <eigenbasis> to turn a coupled <linear map> into independent scalings. In a space of <polynomials>, the <basis> $1,x,x^2$ makes <differentiation> easy to encode, while <orthogonal polynomials> can make approximation errors easier to measure. Gilbert Strang's MIT lecture on independence, basis and dimension develops this connection between attainable columns, redundant columns and coordinates.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/[Strang, MIT 18.06SC, Independence, Basis and Dimension]{ref}

= Coordinates relative to a basis
{parent=Basis}

The unique scalar <coefficients> in the finite <linear combination> expressing a <vector> in a chosen <basis>. Spanning gives existence, while <linear independence> makes the difference of two proposed representations have all zero coefficients. Ordering a finite basis turns these coefficients into a <coordinate vector>.

= Equal-cardinality basis criterion
{parent=Basis}

Let $V$ have finite <dimension> $n$ and let $S\subseteq V$ have $n$ <vectors>. Then spanning, <linear independence> and being a <basis> are equivalent. From a <spanning set>, remove redundant <vectors> until an independent <spanning set> remains. A removal would give a <basis> of fewer than $n$ <vectors>, contradicting <dimension> invariance. Conversely, if $S$ is independent and a <vector> lies outside its <span>, adjoining it gives $n+1$ independent <vectors>, again impossible. Thus an independent $S$ <spans>.

= Basis matrix
{parent=Basis}
{title2=$B=[b_1\ \cdots\ b_n]$}

A <matrix> whose columns are the <coordinate vectors> of an ordered <basis> in a fixed reference <basis>. It is an <invertible matrix>, and multiplication by it converts <coordinates> in the column <basis> to the reference <coordinates>: $v=B[v]_B$. For a real <inner product space> with orthonormal reference <basis>, its <Gram matrix> is $B^TB$, and its <reciprocal basis> has <basis matrix> $B^{-T}$.

An ordered basis is a <basis> equipped with an ordering of its <basis vectors>. The order fixes which position of a <coordinate vector> corresponds to each <basis vector>.

= Coordinate vector

For an <ordered basis> $\mathcal B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$, the coordinate vector $[v]_{\mathcal B}=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^T$ records the unique <coefficients> satisfying $v=\sum_i a_i b_i$. <Change of basis> changes this representation while preserving the underlying <vector>.

The <vector> is independent of the chosen <basis>; its list of <coordinates> is not. In an <orthonormal eigenbasis>, the <coordinates> additionally separate the action of a <self-adjoint operator> into independent <scalar multiplications>.

= Adjacent sums of a cyclic basis
{parent=Basis}

For a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ with cyclic indexing, the vectors $e_j+e_{j+1}$ have coefficient matrix $I+S$, where $S$ cyclically shifts coordinates. Its <determinant> is $1-(-1)^n$. They therefore form a <basis> exactly when $n$ is odd over a field of characteristic different from two. For even $n$, alternating coefficients produce a nontrivial <linear dependence>.

= Basis extension
{parent=Basis}

Every <linearly independent> subset of a finite-dimensional <vector space> extends to a <basis>. Starting with its vectors, add a vector outside their current <span> until the span is the whole space. Each addition preserves <linear independence>, and the process stops because the dimension is finite. This also lets a <linear functional> on a <vector subspace> extend to the ambient space by assigning arbitrary values on the additional basis vectors.

= Vector
{parent=Vector space}
{title2=$v\in V$}

A <vector> is an element of a <vector space>. Its defining operations are <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>. An arrow in a <Euclidean space> is one example; a <polynomial> or a <function> can also be a <vector> when the chosen <vector space> uses the corresponding addition and <scalar multiplication>.

For instance, the <polynomials> $p(x)=1+x$ and $q(x)=x-x^2$ belong to the real <vector space> of <polynomials> of degree at most two. Their <vector addition> gives $(p+q)(x)=1+2x-x^2$, and <scalar multiplication> gives $(2p)(x)=2+2x$. In the <monomial basis> $(1,x,x^2)$ these are the <coordinate vectors> $(1,1,0)$, $(0,1,-1)$, $(1,2,-1)$ and $(2,2,0)$ respectively. The <vectors> are the <polynomials>; these tuples are their representations in a chosen <basis>.

A general <vector space> does not itself prescribe lengths or <angles>. A <norm> supplies a size, while an <inner product> also supplies angle information and <orthogonality>. The familiar arrow geometry below belongs to <Euclidean vectors>, where this extra structure is available.

= Euclidean vector
{parent=Vector}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^n$}

A <Euclidean vector> is a <vector> in a finite-dimensional real <inner product space>. Choosing an <orthonormal basis> identifies it with its real <vector components> in $\mathbb R^n$. A geometric arrow represents a <Euclidean vector> by a length and a direction; translating the arrow without changing either represents the same <Euclidean vector>. The <zero vector> has length zero and no distinguished direction.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/2.1 Scalars and Vectors>[University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others; §2.1, Figures 2.2–2.5: vector arrows, equal vectors and displacement]{ref}

For points $P=(1,1)$ and $Q=(4,5)$ in a <Cartesian coordinate system>, the <displacement> is $Q-P=(3,4)$. The arrow from $(-2,0)$ to $(1,4)$ represents the same <Euclidean vector>, even though its endpoints differ. An endpoint is a position; their difference is a <vector>. In an <orthonormal basis> $(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2)$,
$$
\mathbf v=(3,4)=3\mathbf e_1+4\mathbf e_2,\qquad
\|\mathbf v\|_2=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5.
$$
The <Euclidean norm> comes from the <inner product>: $\|\mathbf v\|_2^2=\langle\mathbf v,\mathbf v\rangle$. The sum of squared <vector components> is valid in an <orthonormal basis>; an arbitrary <basis> requires the appropriate <Gram matrix>. This distinction lets the geometric <vector> stay the same while its <coordinate vector> changes.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/2.2 Coordinate Systems and Components of a Vector>[University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others; §2.2, Cartesian coordinates: equations (2.10)–(2.13) and Figure 2.16, unit basis vectors and scalar components]{ref}

<Vector addition> is componentwise. With $\mathbf u=(2,1)$ and $\mathbf w=(1,3)$,
$$
\mathbf u+\mathbf w=(3,4).
$$
Geometrically, translate the second arrow so its tail starts at the first arrow's head. The <vector sum> joins the starting point to the final endpoint. Reversing the order gives the other route around the same <parallelogram>, so <vector addition> is <commutative>. This is the useful bookkeeping behind adding successive <displacements>, <velocities>, or <forces>: direction information survives the calculation. For a <force>, the <Euclidean vector> alone does not specify its point of application, which also affects the <torque>.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/2.1 Scalars and Vectors>[University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others; §2.1, Algebra of Vectors in Two Dimensions: Figure 2.12 and the head-to-tail construction]{ref}

{description=The original <Euclidean vectors> $\mathbf u=(2,1)$ and $\mathbf w=(1,3)$ form a <parallelogram>. Either head-to-tail route gives their <vector sum> $(3,4)$; dashed copies are translated arrows, not additional terms.}

The magnitude of a <vector sum> is generally different from the sum of the magnitudes. Here $\|\mathbf u+\mathbf w\|_2=5$, whereas $\|\mathbf u\|_2+\|\mathbf w\|_2=\sqrt5+\sqrt{10}>5$. The <triangle inequality> explains why the direct <displacement> cannot exceed the length of this two-segment route. A return <displacement> $(-3,-4)$ makes the net <vector sum> zero, although the lengths of the three travelled segments sum to a positive number.

The <dot product> exposes direction quantitatively: for nonzero <Euclidean vectors>, $\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf w\rangle=\|\mathbf u\|_2\|\mathbf w\|_2\cos\theta$. For the displayed pair, the <dot product> is $2\cdot1+1\cdot3=5$, so $\cos\theta=1/\sqrt2$ and the angle is $45^\circ$. For <orthogonal vectors>, that <dot product> is zero. Dividing by magnitudes separates direction from size, which is the purpose of <vector normalization>.

= Unit vector

{parent=Vector}
{wiki}

= Unit direction
{synonym}

A <unit vector> is a <vector> of <norm> one in a <normed vector space>. For a nonzero <vector> $v$, <vector normalization> produces one by dividing by $\|v\|$. In a real <Euclidean space>, this keeps the direction and removes the magnitude. The <zero vector> cannot be normalized because its <norm> is zero.

For $\mathbf v=(3,4)$ with <Euclidean norm> $5$, the corresponding <unit vector> is
$$
\widehat{\mathbf v}=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right),\qquad
\left\|\widehat{\mathbf v}\right\|_2^2=\frac9{25}+\frac{16}{25}=1.
$$
The <Euclidean vector> can therefore be reconstructed as $\mathbf v=5\widehat{\mathbf v}$: size and direction are distinct pieces of information. The <standard basis> vectors are familiar <unit vectors>, but a <unit vector> need not point along a coordinate axis.

= Vector normalization
{parent=Unit vector}
{title2=$\widehat v=v/\|v\|,\quad v\ne0$}

<Vector normalization> converts a nonzero <vector> in a <normed vector space> into a <unit vector>. <Absolute homogeneity of a norm> gives
$$
\left\|\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right\|=\frac{\|v\|}{\|v\|}=1.
$$
The direction is unchanged by multiplying by a positive real <scalar>. For $c>0$, $\|cv\|=c\|v\|$ and $\widehat{cv}=\widehat v$; for a negative real <scalar>, $\widehat{cv}=-\widehat v$. Thus $(3,4)$ and $(6,8)$ normalize to the same <unit vector>, while $(-3,-4)$ normalizes to its negative. This decomposition is useful for specifying a direction independently of a <force>, <velocity> or <displacement> magnitude.

In a complex <normed vector space>, a nonzero complex <scalar> $c$ instead gives $\widehat{cv}=(c/|c|)\widehat v$. The remaining <complex argument> is not removed by normalization. For a pure <quantum state>, vectors differing only by this overall <global phase> represent the same physical state; <vector normalization> alone does not identify them.

The <zero vector> cannot be normalized this way. The construction also applies in a <function space>: an <eigenfunction> of a <Sturm-Liouville problem> can be scaled to unit <weighted norm> by
$$
y_{\rm normalized}(x)=\frac{y(x)}{\sqrt{\int_a^b w(s)|y(s)|^2\,ds}}.
$$
The positive weight $w$ specifies the <weighted inner product>. Multiplication by this nonzero constant preserves the <eigenvalue equation> and its homogeneous <boundary conditions>.

= Vector normalization is not linear
{parent=Vector normalization}

<Vector normalization> is not a <linear map>. With $\mathbf e_1=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf e_2=(0,1)$,
$$
\widehat{\mathbf e_1+\mathbf e_2}
=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)
\ne(1,1)=\widehat{\mathbf e_1}+\widehat{\mathbf e_2}.
$$
Consequently, adding individually normalized directions does not generally give the direction of the original <vector sum>. Compute the intended <vector sum> first, then normalize it if it is nonzero. Cancellation is a further boundary case: $v+(-v)=0$ has no normalized direction even though both nonzero summands do. A <numerical algorithm> must check that case before dividing by the <norm>.

= Cross product
{title2=$\times$}
{parent=Vector}
{wiki}

The cross product $a\times b$ of two three-dimensional <vectors> is perpendicular to both, has magnitude $|a||b|\sin\theta$, and is oriented by the right-hand rule.

= Inverse of a scalar plus a cross-product operator
{parent=Cross product}
{title2=$\alpha\mathbf x+\beta\mathbf n\times\mathbf x=\mathbf b$}

For a <unit vector> $\mathbf n$, real $\alpha\ne0$, and real $\beta$, the displayed <linear equation> has the unique <vector> solution
$$
\mathbf x=\frac{\alpha\mathbf b-\beta\mathbf n\times\mathbf b+(\beta^2/\alpha)\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf b)}{\alpha^2+\beta^2}.
$$
To prove it, split <vectors> into components parallel and <orthogonal> to $\mathbf n$. On the parallel line, the <linear map> is multiplication by $\alpha$. On the perpendicular plane, the operator $N\mathbf v=\mathbf n\times\mathbf v$ obeys $N^2=-I$, by the <vector triple product>. Therefore $(\alpha I-\beta N)(\alpha I+\beta N)=(\alpha^2+\beta^2)I$ on that plane. Combining its inverse with the parallel solution gives the formula. This avoids component calculations in equations with an axial <cross product> term.

= Cross-product operator with a longitudinal rank-one term
{parent=Inverse of a scalar plus a cross-product operator}
{title2=$x+(b\cdot x)b+b\times x=a$}

For any real three-dimensional <vectors> $a,b$, the displayed <linear equation> has the unique solution
$$
x=\frac{a-b\times a}{1+|b|^2}.
$$
Taking a <dot product> with $b$ first yields $(1+|b|^2)(b\cdot x)=a\cdot b$. Taking a <cross product> with $b$ and using the <vector triple product identity> gives
$$
b\times x=b\times a-b(b\cdot x)+|b|^2x.
$$
Substitution into the original equation cancels the longitudinal terms and proves the formula. Conversely direct substitution verifies it for all $b$, including $b=0$, since the denominator is positive. If $C_bx=b\times x$, the underlying identity for <linear maps> is $(I-C_b)(I+C_b+bb^T)=(1+|b|^2)I$, using $C_b^2=bb^T-|b|^2I$.

= Cross product equation with a fixed unit vector
{parent=Cross product}
{title2=$r\times g=f\iff r=g\times f+tg,\quad f\cdot g=0$}

For a fixed <unit vector> $g\in\mathbb R^3$, the <linear map> $r\mapsto r\times g$ has <kernel> $\mathbb Rg$ and <image> the perpendicular <plane> $g^\perp$. Thus $r\times g=f$ is solvable exactly when $f\cdot g=0$, and then its full solution set is
$$
r=g\times f+tg,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$
For the proof, a <cross product> with $g$ must be perpendicular to $g$. Conversely the <vector triple product identity> gives $g\times(r\times g)=r-(r\cdot g)g$, which determines the perpendicular <orthogonal projection> of $r$. The remaining parallel component is arbitrary. Finally $(g\times f)\times g=f$ when $f\cdot g=0$, verifying sufficiency.

Geometrically, the solution set is a <straight line> with direction $g$. Its nearest point to the origin is $g\times f$ and its distance from the origin is $|f|$. For a general nonzero fixed <vector> $a$, the corresponding formula is $r=(a\times f)/|a|^2+ta$, again subject to $f\cdot a=0$.

= Real-linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$f(rx)=rf(x)\quad(r\in\mathbb R)$}

A <real-linear map> is a <linear map> over the <real numbers>. For a map from the <real line> to itself, real linearity means <multiplication> by a fixed <real number>, its value at one. <Rational-linear maps> need not be real-linear without an additional regularity condition.

= Rational-linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$f(qx)=qf(x)\quad(q\in\mathbb Q)$}

A <rational-linear map> is a <linear map> when its domain and codomain are regarded as <vector spaces> over the <rational numbers>. An additive map on a rational <vector subspace> of the <real numbers> is automatically rational-linear: <integer> homogeneity follows by repeated <addition>, and division by a positive <integer> gives <rational homogeneity of an additive function>.

= One-dimensional extension of a rational-linear map
{parent=Rational-linear map}
{title2=$h(d+qt)=f(d)+qa$}

Let $D$ be a rational <vector subspace> of the <real numbers> and let $t\notin D$. Every element of $D+\mathbb Qt$ has a unique expression $d+qt$. For any prescribed <real number> $a$, the rule $h(d+qt)=f(d)+qa$ extends a <rational-linear map> $f$ on $D$ and remains rational-linear. The uniqueness follows because a nonzero rational multiple of $t$ cannot lie in $D$.

= Linear change of coordinates
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$x=Pu,\quad P\text{ invertible}$}

An invertible <linear transformation> replaces one system of <coordinates> by another. If $x=Pu$ in a <linear differential equation> $\dot x=Ax$, then $\dot u=P^{-1}APu$. The <similar matrices> $A$ and $P^{-1}AP$ have the same <characteristic polynomial> and <eigenvalues>, although their coordinate descriptions of the <eigenvectors> differ. Such a change can turn a planar linear system with <eigenvalues> $\alpha\pm i\beta$ into $\dot u=\alpha u-\beta v$, $\dot v=\beta u+\alpha v$.

= Directional stretch
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$I+(\alpha-1)uu^T$}

For a <unit vector> $u$ in a real <inner product space>, stretching by a factor $\alpha$ along $u$ and leaving its perpendicular subspace fixed gives
$$
T=I+(\alpha-1)uu^T.
$$
Decompose $x=(u^Tx)u+(x-(u^Tx)u)$. The first term is the <orthogonal projection> onto the line; the second is perpendicular to it. The displayed map multiplies the first by $\alpha$ and fixes the second. In the plane, if $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$, then $T=R(\theta)\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,1)R(-\theta)$.

= Linear left inverse criterion
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$ST=I_V\iff\ker T=\{0\}$}

A <linear map> $T:V\to W$ between <finite-dimensional vector spaces> admits a linear <left inverse> $S:W\to V$ if and only if its <kernel of a linear map> is $\{0\}$, equivalently if and only if it is <injective>.

Necessity follows from $Tv=0\Longrightarrow v=STv=0$. For sufficiency, choose a <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of $V$. Triviality of the <kernel of a linear map> implies that $Tv_1,\ldots,Tv_n$ are <linearly independent>. By <basis extension>, they extend to a <basis> $Tv_1,\ldots,Tv_n,w_{n+1},\ldots,w_m$ of $W$. Define $S(Tv_i)=v_i$ and $S(w_j)=0$, and extend by <linearity>. Then $STv_i=v_i$ on a <basis>, proving $ST=I_V$.

The freedom in $S$ lies in its values on a <direct-sum complement> of the <image of a linear map>. For example, for $T(x,y)=(x,y,0)$, every <linear map> $S(u,v,w)=(u+aw,v+bw)$ is a <left inverse>, with arbitrary <scalars> $a,b$. A <right inverse> instead requires $T$ to be a <surjective linear map>.

= Axisymmetric linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$M=aI+cbb^T+d[b]_\times$}

For a fixed nonzero real <vector> $b$ in three dimensions, a real <linear map> commuting with every proper rotation about that axis has the displayed form, where $[b]_\times x=b\times x$ is the <cross-product matrix>. On the axis it acts by $a+c\|b\|^2$. On the perpendicular plane its matrix in an oriented <orthonormal basis> is $\begin{pmatrix}a&-d\|b\|\\d\|b\|&a\end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are therefore $a+c\|b\|^2$ and $a\pm id\|b\|$, and
$$
\det M=(a+c\|b\|^2)(a^2+d^2\|b\|^2).
$$
To see the converse, commuting with all rotations preserves their common fixed axis and its perpendicular plane; there can be no nonzero coupling vector fixed by every planar rotation. A planar matrix commuting with every rotation is a scalar identity plus a scalar right-angle rotation. Thus the block description is exhaustive. If $d\ne0$, only the axial eigenvalue is real; if $d=0$, the perpendicular plane also consists of real eigenvectors.

= Zero-composition subspaces of a linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$M^l(\alpha)=\{\beta:\beta\alpha=0\},\quad M^r(\alpha)=\{\beta:\alpha\beta=0\}$}

For $\alpha:U\to V$, maps in $M^l(\alpha)$ factor uniquely through $V/\operatorname{im}\alpha$; maps in $M^r(\alpha)$ have <image> in $\ker\alpha$. Thus these <vector subspaces> are naturally $L(V/\operatorname{im}\alpha,U)$ and $L(V,\ker\alpha)$, of dimensions $(\dim V-\operatorname{rank}\alpha)\dim U$ and $(\dim U-\operatorname{rank}\alpha)\dim V$. Passing to a <dual map> reverses composition and interchanges these two spaces. This gives a basis-free route to equality of the ranks of a map and its dual.

= One-sided inverses of finite-dimensional endomorphisms
{parent=Linear map}

For endomorphisms $S,T$ of the same finite-dimensional <vector space>, $ST=I$ makes $S$ surjective and $T$ injective. The <rank-nullity theorem> makes both invertible, hence $TS=I$. The finite-dimensional and equal-domain assumptions matter; unilateral shift operators on an infinite-dimensional sequence space give one-sided inverses that are not two-sided.

= Rank of a linear map
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}T=\dim\operatorname{im}T$}

The rank of a <linear map> is the dimension of its <image of a linear map>. In finite-dimensional spaces it equals the <rank of a matrix> representing the map in any bases.

= Rank bounds for a sum of linear maps
{parent=Rank of a linear map}
{title2=$|\operatorname{rank}A-\operatorname{rank}B|\leq\operatorname{rank}(A+B)\leq\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}B$}

The image of $A+B$ lies in $\operatorname{im}A+\operatorname{im}B$. The <dimension formula for a sum of subspaces> gives the upper bound. Applying it to $A=(A+B)-B$ and then interchanging $A,B$ gives the lower bound. Both extremes are sharp: cancelling diagonal blocks gives the difference of ranks; diagonal maps with supports covering the whole space and positive coefficients give full rank when the sum of prescribed ranks is at least the dimension.

= Transvection
{parent=Linear map}
{title2=$T=I+vf,\quad f(v)=0$}

For a nonzero <vector> $v$ and nonzero <linear functional> $f$ vanishing on $v$, this invertible map fixes the <hyperplane> $\ker f$ pointwise and has determinant one. Its inverse is $I-vf$, since $(vf)^2=0$. A <symplectic transvection> is obtained by taking $f=cB(-,v)$ for a nondegenerate <alternating bilinear form>.

= Transvection conjugacy over a finite field
{parent=Transvection}

Every nonidentity <transvection> has an adapted basis in which it is $I+E_{12}$. Thus all such maps are conjugate in the <general linear group>. In dimension at least three, a scaling of a third basis vector centralizes this matrix and has arbitrary nonzero determinant; adjusting a conjugator by this scaling proves conjugacy in the <special linear group> as well.

= Elementary transvection matrix

This <matrix> adds $t$ times coordinate $j$ to coordinate $i$. Left multiplication performs the corresponding row addition. Such matrices generate the <special linear group over a finite field>: row elimination also realizes signed interchanges and compensating diagonal scalings using products of these matrices.

= Transvections generate the special linear group

Row additions reduce an invertible matrix to diagonal form without changing determinant. A determinant-one diagonal matrix is a product of diagonal two-coordinate blocks $\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1})$, each expressible as a product of six <elementary transvection matrices>. Therefore these matrices generate the <special linear group> over a field.

= Orthogonality to the image of a matrix
{parent=Image of a linear map}
{title2=$n^TAr=0\ \forall r\iff A^Tn=0$}

For a real <matrix> $A$ and a fixed <vector> $n$, every <vector> in the <image of a linear map> defined by $A$ is <orthogonal> to $n$ exactly when $A^Tn=0$. Indeed, $n^TAr=(A^Tn)^Tr$. If this vanishes for every $r$, choose $r=A^Tn$ to obtain $|A^Tn|^2=0$. Equivalently each column of $A$ is <orthogonal> to $n$. Requiring the statement only for nonzero $r$ changes nothing, since the zero input already has zero <dot product>. This is the <orthogonal complement> relation between the <image of a linear map> and the <kernel of a linear map> defined by its <transpose>.

= Image obstruction by an annihilating functional
{parent=Image of a linear map}
{title2=$n^TA=0,\ n^Tb\ne0$}

If a <linear functional> $\ell$ vanishes on the <image of a linear map> $T$ but $\ell(b)\ne0$, the equation $Tx=b$ has no solution. In Euclidean coordinates, a vector $n$ with $n^TA=0$ provides such an obstruction whenever $n^Tb\ne0$. In finite dimensions these annihilating functionals exactly characterize membership in the image: $b\in\operatorname{im}A$ precisely when it is orthogonal to every vector in $\ker A^T$.

= Accretive operator
{parent=Linear operator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq0$}

An operator on a complex <Hilbert space> is accretive when the displayed inequality holds throughout its domain. Its negative is a <dissipative operator>. For a bounded everywhere-defined operator, expansion gives $\|(I+T)x\|^2\geq\|x\|^2$. Its <adjoint operator> satisfies the same accretivity condition. Consequently $I+T$ has closed range and trivial kernel, and its range is dense because its adjoint has trivial kernel. Thus $I+T$ is onto and $\|(I+T)^{-1}\|\leq1$. Boundedness and the full domain are material here; for unbounded operators accretivity alone need not imply surjectivity.

= Cayley transform of a bounded accretive operator
{c}
{parent=Accretive operator}
{title2=$S=(I-T)(I+T)^{-1}$}

For a bounded <accretive operator>, $I+T$ is invertible and the transform satisfies $\|S\|\leq1$. The identity $\|(I+T)x\|^2-\|(I-T)x\|^2=4\operatorname{Re}\langle Tx,x\rangle$ proves contraction after the substitution $z=(I+T)x$. If the real part vanishes for every $x$, this identity proves norm preservation. Both $T$ and $-T$ are then accretive, making both $I+T$ and $I-T$ invertible; hence $S$ is onto and is a <unitary operator>.

= Tripotent linear operator
{parent=Linear operator}
{title2=$T^3=T$}

For a <linear operator> on a real <vector space>, the identity $T^3=T$ gives an explicit <eigenspace> decomposition
$$
V=\ker T\oplus\ker(T-I)\oplus\ker(T+I).
$$
Indeed, the <linear operators>
$$
P_0=I-T^2,\qquad P_+=\frac{T^2+T}{2},\qquad P_-=\frac{T^2-T}{2}
$$
sum to $I$. Their images lie respectively in the <eigenspaces> for $0$, $1$, and $-1$, since $TP_0=0$, $TP_+=P_+$, and $TP_-=-P_-$. On each of these <eigenspaces>, its corresponding $P$ is the identity and the other two vanish, which proves uniqueness of the decomposition. Thus a finite-dimensional tripotent <linear operator> admits a <diagonalization> with diagonal entries in $\{0,1,-1\}$. The condition $T^2\ne T$ is equivalent to the presence of a nonzero $-1$ <eigenspace>, and $T^2\ne I$ is equivalent to a nonzero <kernel>.

= Semisimple linear operator
{parent=Linear operator}

A <linear operator> is semisimple if it becomes <diagonalizable> over an algebraic closure; equivalently, its <minimal polynomial> has no repeated root over that closure. If an invertible operator $A$ has finite order $r$ prime to the <characteristic of a field>, its <minimal polynomial> divides $X^r-1$. The derivative $rX^{r-1}$ is coprime to this polynomial, so $A$ is semisimple. Its invariant irreducible summands over a <finite field> become multiplication by scalars in suitable <finite field extensions>.

= Locally nilpotent operator
{parent=Linear operator}

A linear operator $T$ is locally nilpotent if for each vector $v$ there is an integer $m(v)$ such that $T^{m(v)}v=0$. In finite dimension this is equivalent to being a <nilpotent operator>. Differentiation on the <polynomial ring> is locally nilpotent in characteristic zero but has no uniform nilpotence exponent.

= Algebraic exponential of a locally nilpotent operator
{parent=Locally nilpotent operator}
{title2=$\exp(T)v=\sum_{j\geq0}T^jv/j!$}

Over a field of characteristic zero, a <locally nilpotent operator> has an algebraically defined exponential, because the sum is finite for each vector. Its inverse is $\exp(-T)$. The nilpotence bound may depend on the vector. In an <sl2 Lie algebra> <Verma module>, the raising operator is locally nilpotent but the lowering operator is not, so the latter's exponential does not define an endomorphism of the ordinary algebraic module.

= Operator commutator
{parent=Linear operator}
{title2=$[A,B]=AB-BA$}

The commutator measures failure of two operators to commute, on a common domain where the products are defined. Direct expansion gives $[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B$. A common <eigenvector> of $A,B$ lies in the kernel of $[A,B]$, so an injective commutator excludes all nonzero common <eigenvectors>. A nonzero commutator need not itself be injective, and therefore does not alone exclude an individual common <eigenvector>.

= Multiplication operator
{parent=Linear operator}
{wiki}
{title2=$M_fu=fu$}

For a finite almost-everywhere measurable function $f$, the multiplication operator on <L2 space> has maximal <operator domain> $D(M_f)=\{u\in L^2:fu\in L^2\}$. It is a <closed operator>: if $u_n\to u$ and $fu_n\to v$ in <L2 space>, subsequences converge almost everywhere, giving $v=fu$. Its domain is dense, since truncating a function to the sets $|f|\leq n$ approximates it in <L2 space>. If $f$ is bounded, this is a bounded <linear operator> of norm equal to the essential supremum of $|f|$.

= Spectrum of a real multiplication operator
{parent=Multiplication operator}
{title2=$\sigma(M_m)=\operatorname{essran}_\mu m$}

For a real <measurable function> $m$ on a <sigma-finite measure> space, multiplication on $D(M_m)=\{f\in L^2:mf\in L^2\}$ is an <unbounded self-adjoint operator>. Testing its adjoint relation on $\{|m|\leq n\}$ identifies the same maximal domain. Outside the <essential range>, multiplication by $(m-z)^{-1}$ is a bounded resolvent. Inside it, normalized indicators of finite-positive-measure sets where $m$ is close to $z$ are <approximate eigenvectors>, so the inverse cannot be bounded.

= Projector
{synonym}

A <linear projection> is an <idempotent> <linear operator>: $P^2=P$. Every vector decomposes as $x=Px+(I-P)x$, into the <image of a linear map> and the <kernel of a linear map>. Those two <vector subspaces> form a <direct sum>. An <orthogonal projection> additionally uses the <orthogonal complement> in an <inner product space>.

= Sum of two linear projections
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$(E+F)^2=E+F\Longleftrightarrow EF+FE=0$}

For <linear projections> $E,F$, expanding the square proves that their sum is a <linear projection> exactly when $EF=-FE$. Multiplying this equality on the left and right by $E$ gives $EF=-EFE$ and $EFE=-FE=EF$, hence $2EF=0$. If the <field> has <characteristic> different from two, this forces $EF=FE=0$, and those conditions also suffice. In <characteristic> two, the condition instead reduces to commutation; $E=F=I$ has sum zero but nonzero product. For three commuting <linear projections>, the square-minus-sum is $2(EF+EG+FG)$, so vanishing of the triple product alone is not sufficient.

= Three projections summing to the identity
{parent=Sum of two linear projections}
{title2=$E_1+E_2+E_3=I\Longrightarrow E_iE_j=0\quad(i\ne j)$}

Over a <field> of <characteristic> different from two, three <linear projections> summing to the identity annihilate one another in both orders. Each pair sum is the complementary projection to the third. The <sum of two linear projections> criterion therefore proves <zero> products. Every <vector> is the sum of its three images; applying each projection to a <zero> sum proves that these images form a <direct sum>. Choosing a <basis> in each image gives a simultaneous <eigenbasis>, with triples of <eigenvalues> $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ or $(0,0,1)$. This is algebraic <orthogonality>, and does not assume that the projections are <orthogonal projections> for a specified <inner product>.

= Minimal polynomial of an affine function of a projection
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$m_{\alpha I+\beta P}(x)=\prod_{\lambda\in\sigma(\alpha I+\beta P)}(x-\lambda)$}

For a <linear projection> $P$ on a nonzero finite-dimensional <vector space>, $V=\ker P\oplus\operatorname{im}P$. The <linear map> $\alpha I+\beta P$ acts by the <scalar> $\alpha$ on the first summand and $\alpha+\beta$ on the second. Its <minimal polynomial> is the product of the distinct corresponding <linear polynomials> whose summands are nonzero. If $\beta=0$ this is $x-\alpha$; if $P=0$ or $P=I$ only the associated single factor remains. Otherwise, for $\beta\ne0$, it is $(x-\alpha)(x-\alpha-\beta)$. This follows directly from an adapted <basis>, so it works in every <characteristic>.

= Kernel-image identities for complementary projectors
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$\ker P=\operatorname{im}(I-P)$}

For an <idempotent> <linear map> $P$, $I-P$ is also <idempotent> and
$$
\ker P=\operatorname{im}(I-P),\qquad \ker(I-P)=\operatorname{im}P.
$$
For the first identity, $P(I-P)=0$ proves one inclusion. If $Pv=0$, then $v=(I-P)v$, proving the reverse inclusion. Apply the same argument to $I-P$ for the second identity. Consequently the ambient <vector space> is the <direct sum> of the image and <kernel of a linear map> of $P$. <Orthogonality> is not required; it is a separate property of an <orthogonal projection>.

= Trace criterion for a two-by-two idempotent
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$P^2=P\implies P\text{ diagonal or }\operatorname{tr}P=1$}

For $P=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, the off-diagonal equations in $P^2=P$ are $b(a+d-1)=c(a+d-1)=0$. Thus either $b=c=0$ or the <trace> is one. The condition alone is not sufficient: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has <trace> one but is not an <idempotent matrix>. For a two-by-two <matrix> of <trace> one, idempotence is equivalent to zero <determinant>, by the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>.

= Exponential of an idempotent linear map
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$e^{\alpha P}=(I-P)+e^\alpha P$}

For an <idempotent linear map> $P$, induction gives $P^j=P$ for all integers $j\geq1$. Summing the <matrix exponential> series therefore proves the displayed identity. The <direct sum> of its <kernel of a linear map> and <image of a linear map> is fixed and scaled by $e^\alpha$, respectively. The result requires no <Hermitian matrix> hypothesis and is valid for real or complex $\alpha$.

= Norm of a two-endpoint interpolation projection
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$\|P\|=\|\,|e_0|+|e_1|\,\|_\infty$}

Let $\ell_0,\ell_1$ be norm-one extensions to $L^\infty((0,1))$ of endpoint <point evaluations> on $C([0,1])$. For <continuous functions> $e_0,e_1$ with $e_i(j)=\delta_{ij}$, the <linear projection> $Pf=\ell_0(f)e_0+\ell_1(f)e_1$ has the displayed <operator norm>, independently of which extensions are chosen.

The <triangle inequality> proves the upper bound. At a point $x_*$ maximizing $|e_0|+|e_1|$, choose unit complex numbers $a_j$ with $a_je_j(x_*)=|e_j(x_*)|$. The <continuous function> $f(x)=(1-x)a_0+xa_1$ has <supremum norm> one and $\ell_j(f)=a_j$, so $(Pf)(x_*)=|e_0(x_*)|+|e_1(x_*)|$. The <L-infinity norm> of the continuous output equals its <supremum norm>, proving equality even at an endpoint.

= Trace of an idempotent equals its rank
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$P^2=P\implies\operatorname{tr}P=\operatorname{rank}P$}

For a finite-dimensional real or complex <linear projection> $P$, every vector splits as $Px+(I-P)x$ in the <image of a linear map> and the <kernel of a linear map>, respectively. Their intersection is zero, so a basis adapted to this <direct sum> represents $P$ as $\operatorname{diag}(I_r,0)$. Its <trace> and <rank> both equal $r$. In particular, a projection's <determinant> is zero unless it is the identity. Hermiticity is unnecessary for this algebraic identity; a <Hermitian matrix> projection is additionally orthogonal.

= Kadec-Snobar projection bound
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{c}
{title2=$\|P\|\le\sqrt{\dim E}$}

Every finite-dimensional <vector subspace> $E$ of a <Banach space> $X$ is the range of a bounded <linear projection> with the displayed norm bound. In maximal inscribed Euclidean or Hermitian <ellipsoid> coordinates, write the <John contact decomposition> as $\sum_rc_ru_ru_r^*=I_E$. Its contact functionals $\phi_r(x)=u_r^*x$ have norm one on $E$. Extend them to $X$ by the <Hahn-Banach theorem> and set $Px=\sum_rc_r\widetilde\phi_r(x)u_r$. This restricts to the identity on $E$. The contact synthesis operator has Euclidean norm one, the weights sum to $\dim E$, and the Euclidean unit ball is contained in the norm unit ball; these facts give $\|Px\|_E\le\sqrt{\dim E}\|x\|_X$.

= Inner product adapted to an idempotent linear map
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$\langle u,v\rangle_P=\langle Pu,Pv\rangle+\langle(I-P)u,(I-P)v\rangle$}

For an <idempotent linear map> $P$ on a finite-dimensional real <vector space>, take any positive definite <inner product> on that space. The displayed new <inner product> is positive definite since its squared norm vanishes only if both $Pu$ and $(I-P)u$ vanish. It makes the <kernel of a linear map> and <image of a linear map> orthogonal. Since $P^2=P$, one has $\langle Pu,v\rangle_P=\langle u,Pv\rangle_P$; thus $P$ becomes an <orthogonal projection>.

= Similarity classification of idempotent linear maps
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}

If $T^2=T$, every <vector> splits as $v=(v-Tv)+Tv$ in $\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T$. On these summands $T$ is respectively zero and the identity. Its <matrix> in an adapted <basis> is therefore $\operatorname{diag}(0,I_r)$, where $r$ is its <rank of a linear map>. Two <idempotent linear maps> on the same finite-dimensional <vector space> are <similar matrices> exactly when their ranks agree.

= Oblique projection
{parent=Projection (linear algebra)}
{title2=$Q(v+w)=v\quad(H=V\oplus W)$}

If two <closed subspaces of a Hilbert space> give $H=V\oplus W$, the projection onto $V$ along $W$ fixes $V$ and vanishes on $W$. It need not be an <orthogonal projection>. The <bounded inverse theorem> applied to the addition map $(v,w)\mapsto v+w$ on the complete component spaces proves boundedness of the component maps. With both complementary subspaces nonzero, its <operator norm> and that of $I-Q$ equal $\sec\theta_{V,W^\perp}$, with the <directed subspace angle> convention. Indeed, for fixed $v\in V$, the smallest possible <norm> of $v+w$ over $w\in W$ is $\|P_{W^\perp}v\|$, which proves the formula for $\|Q\|$. Equality with $\|I-Q\|$ follows from the block representation $Q=\begin{pmatrix}I&B\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ on $V\oplus V^\perp$: both squared <operator norms> are $1+\|B\|^2$. If a summand is zero, $Q$ is zero or the <identity operator>, and one complementary <operator norm> is zero; those cases must be treated directly.

= Finite-dimensional Hilbert sampling reconstruction
{parent=Oblique projection}
{title2=$\widetilde f=(P_S|_T)^{-1}P_Sf$}

For equal finite-dimensional <closed subspaces of a Hilbert space> $T,S$, positivity of $\cos\theta_{T,S}$ makes $P_S|_T$ invertible. The displayed reconstruction is the unique element of $T$ whose <orthogonal projection> onto $S$ matches that of $f$. It is the <oblique projection> onto $T$ along $S^\perp$. Its <operator norm> is the value of the <secant function> at the <directed subspace angle>, and its error is at most that <secant function> value times the best <orthogonal projection> error. The lower error bound follows from the <Pythagorean identity>. Zero-dimensional cases are handled directly.

The domain $\mathcal D(A)$ of an operator $A$ is the set of vectors on which $A$ is defined. For an unbounded differential operator, the domain includes regularity and boundary conditions and is part of the operator's definition.

= Densely defined operator

A <linear operator> $T$ on a <Hilbert space> $H$ is densely defined when its <operator domain> is a <dense subset> of $H$. Density makes the <adjoint operator> unique wherever its defining inner-product identity has a solution.

= Identity operator

The identity operator maps every vector to itself. Every nonzero vector is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> one.

= Composition operator

Given a map $\tau:X\to X$, the associated composition operator on a function space sends $f$ to $f\circ\tau$.

= Invariant functional of a composition operator

A functional $\mu$ is invariant under the composition operator $T$ when $\mu(Tf)=\mu(f)$ for every $f$. On a compact metric space, a point-evaluation functional can be averaged along an orbit, and a weak-star convergent subsequence of those averages produces an invariant functional.

= Unitary operator
{parent=Linear operator}
{wiki}

= Unitarity
{synonym}

= Unitary transformation
{synonym}

A unitary operator $U$ on a complex vector space with an <inner product> satisfies $U^*U=UU^*=I$ and therefore preserves inner products.

= Commuting-involution unitary combination
{parent=Unitary operator}
{title2=$U=(A+iB)/\sqrt2$}

If A and B are commuting <self-adjoint operators> with $A^2=B^2=I$, then the displayed combination is a <unitary operator>. Indeed,
$$
U^\dagger U=\tfrac12(A-iB)(A+iB)=\tfrac12(A^2+B^2+iAB-iBA)=I,
$$
and the reverse product is also I. The factors are <involutions>, and their commutation is essential to cancel the cross terms. Operators $A=U_A\otimes I$ and $B=I\otimes U_B$ on separate tensor factors provide a natural example. More generally, $\cos\theta\,A+i\sin\theta\,B$ is unitary for real $\theta$ by the same proof. Replacing i by a real coefficient usually loses this property.

= Infinitesimal unitary transformation
{parent=Unitary operator}
{title2=$U(\epsilon)=I+i\epsilon F+O(\epsilon^2)$}

Differentiating a <unitary operator> family at the identity gives an anti-Hermitian tangent. In the convention $U(\epsilon)=I+i\epsilon F+O(\epsilon^2)$ for real $\epsilon$, the generator $F$ is <Hermitian>. This is the one-parameter version of the <Hermitian quantum generator convention>. A finite linear truncation need not itself be exactly unitary.

= Unitary equivalence
{parent=Unitary operator}
{title2=$B=UAU^{-1}$}

= Unitarily equivalent
{synonym}

Two operators on <Hilbert spaces> are unitarily equivalent if a <unitary operator> maps their operator domains onto one another and intertwines their actions. Their <spectra> and <eigenvalue> multiplicities then agree. For nonnegative <self-adjoint operators> it suffices that the unitary maps their closed <quadratic form> domains onto one another and preserves the forms, since the associated operator is determined by the form.

= Eigenphase
{parent=Unitary operator}
{title2=$\theta\pmod1$}

An eigenphase of a <unitary operator> is a real number $\theta$ modulo one specifying an <eigenvalue> $e^{2\pi i\theta}$. Every unitary eigenvalue has <modulus> one. <Quantum phase estimation> records the eigenphase corresponding to an input <eigenvector>; a superposition retains the corresponding labels coherently before measurement.

= Unitary conjugation
{parent=Unitary operator}

Unitary conjugation maps an operator $A$ to $UAU^\dagger$ for a <unitary operator> $U$. It preserves <eigenvalues>, <operator norms>, and algebraic relations such as products and adjoints.

= Mean ergodic theorem
{synonym}

For a <unitary operator> $U$ on a <Hilbert space>, the <Cesaro averages>
$$
A_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}U^j
$$
converge strongly to the <orthogonal projection> onto the fixed-point subspace $\ker(U-I)$.

= Orthogonal decomposition for unitary ergodic averages
{parent=Von Neumann mean ergodic theorem}
{title2=$\mathcal H=\ker(I-U)\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(I-U)}$}

For a <unitary operator> $U$, the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{Ran}(I-U)$ is $\ker(I-U^*)=\ker(I-U)$. On the fixed space, <Cesaro averages> act as the identity. On $(I-U)g$ they telescope to $(g-U^Ng)/N$. Their uniform norm bound extends convergence to the closure of the range, proving the <von Neumann mean ergodic theorem>.

= Commuting operators
{parent=Linear operator}

Two <linear operators> $A$ and $B$ commute when $AB=BA$. Their <matrix exponentials> then satisfy $e^{A+B}=e^Ae^B=e^Be^A$.

= Centralizer of a nonscalar two by two matrix
{parent=Commuting operators}
{title2=$AB=BA\iff B=\alpha I+\beta A$}

For any real nonscalar $2\times2$ <matrix> $A$, its commuting matrices are exactly $B=\alpha I+\beta A$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R$. There is a vector $v$ for which $v,Av$ form a <basis>: otherwise every nonzero vector would be an <eigenvector>, and applying this to two independent vectors and their sum would force $A$ to be scalar. Given a commuting $B$, uniquely write $Bv=\alpha v+\beta Av$. Then $BAv=ABv=(\alpha I+\beta A)Av$, so $B$ and $\alpha I+\beta A$ agree on a basis and are equal. Conversely every polynomial in $A$ commutes with it. This generalizes the <centralizer of a planar quarter-turn>; the scalar-matrix exception is essential, because every matrix commutes with a scalar matrix.

= Commuting maps preserve eigenspaces
{parent=Commuting operators}
{title2=$C(E_lambda(D))subseteq E_lambda(D)$}

If $CD=DC$ and $Dx=\lambda x$, then $D(Cx)=C(Dx)=\lambda Cx$. Therefore each <eigenspace> of $D$ is invariant under $C$. The vector $Cx$ may be zero; invariance does not require it to be a nonzero <eigenvector>. This observation is the starting point for <simultaneous diagonalization>.

= Anticommutator
{title2=$\{A,B\}$}
{parent=Linear operator}
{wiki}

The anticommutator of two operators is $\{A,B\}=AB+BA$. They anticommute when this operator vanishes, equivalently when $AB=-BA$.

= Invertible anticommuting matrices have even dimension and zero traces
{parent=Anticommutator}
{title2=$AB=-BA\ \Longrightarrow\ n\in2\mathbb Z,\ \operatorname{Tr}A=\operatorname{Tr}B=0$}

For complex <invertible matrices> $A,B$, $AB=-BA$ implies $B^{-1}AB=-A$ and $A^{-1}BA=-B$. Invariance of <matrix trace> under <matrix similarity> gives both zero traces. Invariance of <determinant> gives $\det A=(-1)^n\det A$, and invertibility forces $n$ even. Equivalently, <eigenvalues> occur in pairs $\lambda,-\lambda$ with equal <algebraic multiplicity>, and zero is excluded. Both invertibility assumptions matter: $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0)$ and $B=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,0)$ anticommute in dimension three but are singular and have nonzero traces.

= Two-by-two anticommutator characteristic polynomial
{parent=Anticommutator}
{title2=$\chi_{B\mapsto AB+BA}(t)=(t-\operatorname{tr}A)^2(t^2-2t\operatorname{tr}A+4\det A)$}

If $A$ is diagonalizable, the <anticommutator> map $B\mapsto AB+BA$ has <eigenvalues> $2\lambda_1$, $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$ twice, and $2\lambda_2$. Their product gives the displayed <characteristic polynomial>. The <density of diagonalizable complex matrices> and continuity of polynomial coefficients prove the formula also for defective matrices.

= Conjugate linear operators
{parent=Linear operator}

Linear operators $\alpha$ and $\beta$ on the same finite-dimensional vector space are conjugate when $\beta=s^{-1}\alpha s$ for some linear isomorphism $s$. Their matrices in any fixed basis are similar.

= Conjugation operator on an endomorphism space
{parent=Conjugate linear operators}

For an invertible operator $\beta$ on $V$, the map
$$
\phi_\beta:\operatorname{End}(V)\to\operatorname{End}(V),
\qquad A\mapsto\beta^{-1}A\beta
$$
is a linear isomorphism. Conjugate choices of $\beta$ induce conjugate operators $\phi_\beta$.

= Monotone matrix
{parent=Matrix}

A rectangular <array> whose entries are nondecreasing in each coordinate. Every <subarray> obtained by restricting to a row interval and a column interval has the same property. Comparing a target with the top-right entry discards a whole row or column, giving <staircase matrix search> in time linear in the sum of the dimensions.

= Hilbert matrix
{parent=Matrix}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$H_{ij}=\frac1{i+j-1},\quad1\leq i,j\leq n$}

A real <symmetric matrix> whose entries are the moments $H_{ij}=\int_0^1x^{i-1}x^{j-1}dx$. Thus it is the <Gram matrix> of the monomials in the real <L2 inner product> on $[0,1]$. For a nonzero real coefficient vector $v$, $v^THv=\int_0^1(\sum_i v_ix^{i-1})^2dx>0$, so every finite Hilbert matrix is <positive definite>. The moment description connects these matrices with polynomial approximation and with the <shifted Hilbert quadratic-form bound>.

= Shifted Hilbert quadratic-form bound
{parent=Hilbert matrix}
{title2=$0\leq\sum_{r,s=1}^n\frac{a_ra_s}{r+s+1}\leq\pi\sum_{r=1}^na_r^2$}

For real $a_r$, put $h(z)=\sum_{r=1}^na_rz^r$. The quadratic form equals $\int_0^1h(x)^2dx$, proving its nonnegativity. Apply the <centred logarithmic keyhole identity> bound to the <entire function> $h^2$. Since $|h(e^{i\theta})^2|=|h(e^{i\theta})|^2$, orthogonality of the finite Fourier sum gives $\int_0^{2\pi}|h(e^{i\theta})|^2d\theta=2\pi\sum_ra_r^2$. Multiplication by the contour estimate's factor $1/2$ proves the upper bound. The matrix $(1/(r+s+1))$ is the principal submatrix of a <Hilbert matrix> obtained by deleting its first row and column.

= Real matrix

A <real matrix> is a <matrix> whose entries are <real numbers>. The <vector space> of $m\times n$ <real matrices> is denoted $M_{m,n}(\mathbb R)$.

= Nonsymmetric matrix
{parent=Matrix}
{title2=$A^T\ne A$}

A nonsymmetric matrix is a square <matrix> unequal to its <transpose>. Its right <eigenvectors> need not be <orthogonal>, and it need not be a <diagonalizable matrix>. For instance, $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ has <eigenvectors> $(1,0)^T$ and $(1,1)^T$ for its distinct <eigenvalues>. A starting <vector> $(1,0)^T$ has nonzero <inner product> with $(1,1)^T$ but zero coefficient in that direction in the <eigenbasis>. This is why <power iteration> and <inverse iteration> require a nonzero spectral component rather than an ordinary nonorthogonality test in the general case.

= Matrix addition

A <matrix> whose entries are <complex numbers>. For an <endomorphism> of $\mathbb C^n$, <Hermitian matrices> and <unitary matrices> are distinguished by their behavior under the <conjugate transpose>.

An integer matrix is a <matrix> whose entries are <integers>. = Cauchy matrix

= Matrix norm

A matrix norm is a <norm> on a <vector space> of <matrices>; common choices include induced <operator norms> and the <Frobenius norm>. A submultiplicative matrix norm additionally satisfies $\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|$. The <matrix 2-norm> is the norm induced by <Euclidean norms> and equals the largest <singular value>.

= Induced matrix norm
{parent=Matrix norm}
{title2=$\|B\|=\sup_{x\ne0}\|Bx\|/\|x\|$}

A <vector norm> on a finite-dimensional real or complex <vector space> induces the displayed <matrix norm> on its <linear operators>. It is the corresponding <operator norm>, and the <supremum> can equivalently be taken over the <unit sphere>. Applying the length bound twice proves <submultiplicativity>: $\|BCx\|\leq\|B\|\|C\|\|x\|$, hence $\|BC\|\leq\|B\|\|C\|$.

= Row-column bound for the matrix 2-norm
{parent=Induced matrix norm}
{title2=$\|B\|_2\leq\sqrt{\|B\|_1\|B\|_\infty}$}

For each row, the weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|(Bx)_i|^2\leq(\sum_j|b_{ij}|)(\sum_j|b_{ij}||x_j|^2)$. Summing over rows bounds the result by the maximum absolute row sum times the maximum absolute column sum times $\|x\|_2^2$. Taking square roots and the supremum proves the displayed <induced matrix norm> inequality. It gives a Euclidean estimate directly from entries without computing <singular values>.

= Matrix norm induced by an energy norm
{parent=Induced matrix norm}
{title2=$\|B\|_A=\|A^{1/2}BA^{-1/2}\|_2$}

For a real <symmetric positive definite matrix> $A$, the <energy norm> is $\|x\|_A=(x^TAx)^{1/2}=\|A^{1/2}x\|_2$. In the defining <supremum> of the <induced matrix norm>, substitute $y=A^{1/2}x$. The <matrix square root> is an <invertible matrix>, so this ranges over every nonzero <vector> and proves the displayed identity. This <matrix similarity> is useful because contraction in the <energy norm> becomes an ordinary Euclidean <operator norm> estimate after a change of coordinates.

= Matrix pencil
{parent=Matrix}
{title2=$A+\lambda B$}

A matrix pencil is an affine one-parameter family of <matrices>. For square matrices of size $m$, its determinant is a <polynomial> of degree at most $m$. If $B$ is invertible, its leading coefficient is $\det B\ne0$, so at most $m$ distinct parameters give a singular matrix. This translates linear dependence in a family of bases into polynomial root counting.

= Total nonnegativity of a matrix
{parent=Matrix}

= Totally nonnegative matrix
{synonym}

A real rectangular <matrix> is totally nonnegative if every square minor, with row and column indices in increasing order, is nonnegative. A nonnegative bidiagonal <matrix> is totally nonnegative, and products of compatible <totally nonnegative matrices> have the same property by the <Cauchy-Binet formula>. This controls signs of inverse entries and underlies stability and shape-preserving properties of <B-spline> collocation. Total nonnegativity permits zero minors; strict total positivity requires every such minor to be positive.

= Checkerboard inverse of a totally nonnegative matrix
{parent=Total nonnegativity of a matrix}
{title2=$(-1)^{i+j}(A^{-1})_{ij}\geq0$}

If an invertible square <totally nonnegative matrix> $A$ has size $n$, then $\det A>0$ and
$$
(-1)^{i+j}(A^{-1})_{ij}\geq0.
$$
Indeed the <adjugate identity> gives $(A^{-1})_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{\widehat j,\widehat i}/\det A$, and the deleted-row/deleted-column minor is nonnegative. Consequently an alternating vector $v_j=(-1)^j$ gives $(A^{-1}v)_i=(-1)^i\sum_j|(A^{-1})_{ij}|$.

= Elementary column operation

{synonym}

An <elementary column operation> interchanges two columns of a <matrix>, scales one by a nonzero scalar, or adds a multiple of another column. It is an <elementary row operation> on the transpose. Hence it has the same three effects on the <determinant>: changing sign, multiplying by the scale factor, or leaving the <determinant> unchanged.

= Reduced column echelon form
{parent=Elementary column operation}
{title2=$\operatorname{rcef}(A)=\operatorname{rref}(A^T)^T$}

The reduced column echelon form of a <matrix> $A$ is defined by taking its <transpose>, forming the <reduced row echelon form>, and transposing back:
$$
\operatorname{rcef}(A)=\bigl(\operatorname{rref}(A^T)\bigr)^T.
$$
It is obtained from $A$ by <elementary column operations>. Its nonzero columns precede its zero columns, the topmost nonzero entry of each nonzero column is $1$, these leading entries move strictly downward as the column number increases, and each leading entry is the only nonzero entry in its row.

Every <elementary column operation> preserves the <column space>, because it and its inverse express each new column as a <linear combination> of the old columns. Thus the nonzero columns of the reduced form give a <basis> of the original <column space>. In contrast, <elementary row operations> preserve the <row space>. There is no general identity $\operatorname{rcef}(A)=\operatorname{rref}(A)^T$: the definition requires reducing $A^T$, not $A$.

For an <invertible matrix> of size $n$, both reduced forms are $I_n$. A singular example showing the distinction is
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{rref}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{rcef}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

= Row Hermite normal form in rank two
{parent=Hermite normal form}

= Doubly stochastic matrix
{parent=Matrix}
{wiki}

A square real <matrix> is doubly stochastic if its entries are nonnegative and every row and column sums to $1$. Its positive-entry support satisfies the condition of the <Hall marriage theorem>: for a row subset $S$, $|S|\leq|N(S)|$ by summing column capacities. Consequently its support contains a <permutation matrix>.

= Entropy increase under doubly stochastic mixing
{parent=Doubly stochastic matrix}
{title2=$H(Aq)\geq H(q)$}

Let $A$ be a <doubly stochastic matrix>, let $q$ be a <probability vector>, and put $w=Aq$. In row $i$ with $w_i>0$, define $r_i(j)=a_{ij}q_j/w_i$ on the <support> where $a_{ij}>0$. The positive row entries $\alpha_i=(a_{ij})$ form a <probability vector>. Expanding <relative entropy> and using the column sums gives
$$
H(w)-H(q)=\sum_{i:w_i>0}w_i d(r_i,\alpha_i)\geq0.
$$
Zero-output rows contribute zero because all their $a_{ij}q_j$ vanish. The row sums supply the normalized comparison laws, while the column sums preserve the input <Shannon entropy>[entropy] contribution. <Equality> holds exactly when $q_j=w_i$ for every positive <matrix> entry $a_{ij}$, including the automatic zero values in zero-output rows.

= Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem
{c}
{parent=Doubly stochastic matrix}

Every <doubly stochastic matrix> is a <convex combination> of <permutation matrices>. Equivalently, the <extreme points> of the doubly stochastic <convex polytope> are exactly the permutation matrices. The extreme-point assertion follows from an <alternating-cycle perturbation of a doubly stochastic matrix>; the convex-hull assertion follows because a bounded finite-dimensional polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. Permutation matrices are extreme since their zero entries force the same zeros in every convex decomposition.

= Birkhoff decomposition by support matchings
{c}
{title2=$A=\sum_i\alpha_iP_i$}
{parent=Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem}

For a <doubly stochastic matrix>, the <Hall marriage theorem> supplies a <perfect matching> in its positive-entry support. Subtract the smallest matched entry times the corresponding <permutation matrix>. The residual has equal row and column sums and smaller support unless it vanishes. Repeating gives a <convex combination> of <permutation matrices>. With $r$ initially positive entries, there are at most $r-n+1$ terms, since a positive-mass residual has at least $n$ positive entries. Elementary augmenting-path matching gives $O(n^5)$ arithmetic operations.

= Alternating-cycle perturbation of a doubly stochastic matrix
{parent=Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem}

For a <doubly stochastic matrix> with a fractional entry, form the row-column <bipartite graph> of entries strictly between zero and one. Every incident vertex has degree at least two, so there is an even cycle. A matrix supported on that cycle with alternating entries $+1,-1$ has zero row and column sums. A sufficiently small positive multiple can be both added and subtracted while preserving entry bounds. The original matrix is then the midpoint of two distinct doubly stochastic matrices, so it is not an <extreme point>.

= Nonnegative matrix

A real <matrix> is nonnegative when every entry is nonnegative. This entrywise condition differs from being a <positive semidefinite matrix>: the <symmetric matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is nonnegative but has <eigenvalues> $1$ and $-1$.

= Irreducible nonnegative matrix
{parent=Nonnegative matrix}

A square <nonnegative matrix> $A$ is irreducible if for each pair $(i,j)$ there is an integer $k\geq0$ with $(A^k)_{ij}>0$. This says that every index can reach every other through positive entries. The <Perron–Frobenius theorem> then gives a positive leading <eigenvector> and an algebraically <simple eigenvalue>. Irreducibility alone permits other <eigenvalues> of the same <modulus>: the cyclic permutation <matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has <eigenvalues> $1,-1$.

= Primitive nonnegative matrix
{parent=Irreducible nonnegative matrix}

A square <nonnegative matrix> $A$ is primitive if some positive integer power $A^k$ has strictly positive entries. It is therefore an <irreducible nonnegative matrix>. The <Perron–Frobenius theorem> gives a leading <eigenvalue> whose <modulus> is strictly larger than that of every other <eigenvalue>. Every strictly positive <matrix> is primitive; the two-cycle permutation <matrix> is irreducible but not primitive.

= Perron–Frobenius theorem
{parent=Nonnegative matrix}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$|\lambda_j|<\lambda_1\quad(j>1)$}

The strictly positive case states that a real square <matrix> $W$ with $W_{ij}>0$ has a positive <simple eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and positive left and right <eigenvectors>, with all other <eigenvalues> strictly smaller in <modulus>. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> applied to $v\mapsto Wv/(\mathbf1^TWv)$ on the nonnegative unit <simplex> gives $v>0$ and $Wv=\lambda v$. For another <eigenvector> $z$, maximize $|z_i|/v_i$; the <triangle inequality> gives $|\mu|\leq\lambda$. Equality forces every <modulus> ratio and <complex argument> to agree because every entry is positive, so $z$ is proportional to $v$ and $\mu=\lambda$. Apply the same argument to $W^T$ for a positive left <eigenvector>; its positive pairing with $v$ rules out a <generalized eigenvector> at $\lambda$, establishing algebraic simplicity. For merely nonnegative matrices a nonnegative leading <eigenvector> exists. An <irreducible nonnegative matrix> has a positive leading <eigenvector> and simple Perron <eigenvalue>; a <primitive nonnegative matrix> has the strict <modulus> gap.

= Spectrum of an all-ones matrix
{parent=All-ones matrix}
{title2=$\operatorname{spec}(J_n)=\{n,0\}\quad(n\geq2)$}

For $u=(1,\ldots,1)^T$, $J_n=uu^T$ maps $v$ to $(\sum_iv_i)u$. Therefore $u$ is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> $n$, while the zero-sum <vector subspace> is its <kernel>. For $n\geq2$ a <basis> of the <kernel> is $e_1-e_n,\ldots,e_{n-1}-e_n$. These vectors and $u$ form an <eigenbasis>, proving <diagonalization> and <characteristic polynomial> $t^{n-1}(t-n)$. Both <algebraic multiplicity> and <geometric multiplicity> are one at $n$ and $n-1$ at zero. At $n=1$ the sole <eigenvalue> is 1; the zero-sum space has dimension zero and contributes no additional root.

= Matrix decomposition
{parent=Matrix}
{wiki}

= Matrix factorization
{synonym}

A <matrix decomposition> expresses a <matrix> as a <matrix product> of factors with simpler structure. An <LU decomposition> uses triangular factors, a <QR decomposition> uses an <orthogonal matrix> and a triangular factor, and a <singular value decomposition> separates input directions, stretching factors and output directions. The factorization is an identity; the chosen structure determines what it helps compute.

For an original two-by-two example,
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1/2&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&5/2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The outer factors are a <lower triangular matrix> and an <upper triangular matrix>, each with unit diagonal as in a <unit triangular matrix>; the middle factor is a <diagonal matrix>. Their <matrix product> is $M$, and the final diagonal entry $5/2$ is the <Schur complement> of the first entry $2$. This is the scalar version of the factorization under <block elimination>.

To solve $Mz=(3,4)^T$, solve with the three factors in their displayed order from left to right: first the lower triangular factor, then the diagonal factor, then the upper triangular factor. The intermediate <vectors> are $(3,5/2)^T$ and $(3/2,1)^T$, giving the concise answer $z=(1,1)^T$. A new right-hand side uses the same factors. This is why <reusing LU factors for multiple right-hand sides> is useful: factorization records work that otherwise would be repeated, while <forward substitution> and <back substitution> apply it cheaply.<Convex Optimization by Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe>[§C.4.1, printed pp. 673–674: block factor-solve interpretation]{ref}

Different decompositions reveal different features. A <Cholesky decomposition> uses <positive definiteness> of a <symmetric matrix>, while a <singular value decomposition> also applies to rectangular <matrices> and exposes <matrix rank>. The existence and numerical behaviour of the factors depend on their own hypotheses; for example, an unpivoted <LU decomposition> can fail when a chosen <pivot element> is zero even though the <matrix> is an <invertible matrix>.

= LQ decomposition
{c}

Transposing a <QR decomposition> of $A^T$ proves existence. Equivalently, right <Householder transformations> clear the part of each row beyond its diagonal while preserving earlier zeros. This factorization is useful for underdetermined systems, complementing the role of <QR decomposition> in overdetermined systems.

= Symmetrization map in characteristic two

Thus the two <dimensions> still add to $n^2$, as required by the <rank-nullity theorem>.

= Analytic determinant square root for accretive symmetric matrices

= Diagonal logarithm concavity bound
{parent=Matrix logarithm}
{title2=$\langle v|\log A|v\rangle\leq\log\langle v|A|v\rangle$}

For a positive-definite <Hermitian operator> $A=\sum_jt_j|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|$ and a unit vector $v$, the weights $|\langle v|u_j\rangle|^2$ form a probability distribution. Scalar logarithmic concavity bounds their average of $\log t_j$ by the logarithm of their average of $t_j$. Positive semidefinite operators follow by a limiting convention. Using the eigenbasis of a <density operator> $\rho$ gives $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq D(r\|q)$, where $r$ is the spectrum of $\rho$ and $q$ the diagonal of $\sigma$ in that basis. This yields <nonnegativity of quantum relative entropy> without commutativity.

= Existence of a logarithm for every invertible complex matrix
{parent=Matrix logarithm}

An invertible complex <Jordan block> $\lambda I+N$, with $N^s=0$, has logarithm $\ell I+\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}(-1)^{k+1}(N/\lambda)^k/k$, where $e^\ell=\lambda$. Applying this construction blockwise and conjugating back proves surjectivity of the <matrix exponential> on the complex <general linear group>. There is no globally single-valued continuous choice of logarithm.

= Klein's inequality
{c}
{parent=Matrix logarithm}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}[A(\ln A-\ln B)]\geq\operatorname{Tr}(A-B)$}

For positive-definite <Hermitian matrices> $A,B$, Klein's inequality gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[A(\ln A-\ln B)]\geq\operatorname{Tr}(A-B),
$$
with equality exactly when $A=B$. To prove it, let $a_i,b_j$ be their <eigenvalues> and $u_i,v_j$ their orthonormal eigenvectors. The weights $w_{ij}=|\langle u_i,v_j\rangle|^2$ have row and column sums one. The difference between the two sides is
$$
\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}\left[a_i\ln\frac{a_i}{b_j}-a_i+b_j\right]\geq0
$$
by the scalar <logarithm inequality> $\ln t\leq t-1$. Equality requires $a_i=b_j$ whenever $w_{ij}>0$, implying $A=B$. Limits extend the result to positive semidefinite matrices with the appropriate support condition. Applying it to trace-one matrices proves <nonnegativity of quantum relative entropy>.

= Cube roots of a matrix
{synonym}

A <cube root of a matrix> $A$ is a <square matrix> $B$ of the same size over the specified <field> satisfying $B^3=A$. Every complex <invertible matrix> has one: put it in <Jordan normal form>, choose a scalar <cube root> of each nonzero <eigenvalue>, and apply the <truncated binomial cube root of the identity plus a nilpotent matrix> on each block. The <nilpotent Jordan block> $J_3(0)$ has none, even though its square is nonzero. If $B^3=J_3(0)$, then $B^9=0$, making $B$ a <nilpotent matrix> of size three; the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> then gives $B^3=0$, a contradiction.

= Matrix exponentiation
{parent=Matrix power}

= Matrix powering
{synonym}

Compute a nonnegative <integer> power $A^n$ by <repeated squaring>. If $n=2k$, use $A^n=(A^k)^2$; if $n=2k+1$, use $A^n=A(A^k)^2$. This needs $O(\log n)$ <matrix multiplications> and permits fast evaluation of fixed-order <linear recurrences> via a <companion matrix>. = Matrix powers for a diagonalizable matrix with two eigenvalues
{parent=Matrix power}
{title2=$A^n=\frac{r^n-s^n}{r-s}A+\frac{rs^n-sr^n}{r-s}I$}

If a <diagonalizable matrix> has distinct <eigenvalues> $r,s$, its <spectral projections of a diagonalizable matrix> are $(A-sI)/(r-s)$ and $(rI-A)/(r-s)$. Raising the diagonal form to a nonnegative integer power gives the displayed formula. Equivalently, interpolate the scalar function $t^n$ by a degree-one polynomial at $r,s$, then apply it to $A$. The <diagonalizability> assumption matters: knowledge of two eigenvalues alone does not exclude nontrivial <Jordan blocks>.

= Matrix rank
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}A$}
{parent=Matrix}
{wiki=Rank_(linear_algebra)}

= Rank of a matrix
{synonym}

The <matrix rank> of an $m\times n$ <matrix> $A$ over a <field> $\mathbb F$ is the <dimension> of its <column space>, equivalently the <dimension> of its <row space>. It counts <linearly independent> column directions, with $0\leq\operatorname{rank}A\leq\min(m,n)$. Counting nonzero <matrix entries> gives a different quantity: an <all-ones matrix> can have arbitrarily many nonzero <matrix entries> and still have <matrix rank> one.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/The Rank Theorem>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §2.9, Definition: rank is column-space dimension; the row-space equality is proved below]{ref}

Consider the following <matrix> over the <real numbers>, with columns written as <vectors>:
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
c_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad
c_2=\begin{pmatrix}2\\4\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad
c_3=\begin{pmatrix}3\\6\\2\end{pmatrix}=c_1+c_2.
$$
The <vectors> $c_1,c_2$ are <linearly independent>: $\alpha c_1+\beta c_2=0$ requires both $\alpha+2\beta=0$ and $\alpha+\beta=0$, so $\beta=\alpha=0$. The third column adds no new direction to their <linear span>, hence $\operatorname{rank}A=2$ despite all nine <matrix entries> being nonzero. The second row likewise repeats twice the first row.

The <column space> is a <plane> through the origin:
$$
\operatorname{col}A=\operatorname{span}\{c_1,c_2\}
=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}u\\2u\\v\end{pmatrix}:u,v\in\mathbb R\right\}.
$$
Every output has second <coordinate> twice its first <coordinate>. Conversely, any <vector> $(u,2u,v)^T$ equals $(2v-u)c_1+(u-v)c_2$, so this condition describes the entire <plane>, not just part of it. For example, $b=(1,2,0)^T=-c_1+c_2$ is attainable, whereas $b'=(1,3,0)^T$ lies outside the <plane>.

\Image[matrix-rank-column-plane.png]
{title=Three column <vectors> span a two-dimensional <plane>: $c_3=c_1+c_2$, $b$ is attainable, and $b'$ is outside the <column space>}

<Elementary row operations> expose the same count algebraically. Apply $R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1$ and $R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_1$, interchange the last two rows, multiply the second row by $-1$, and subtract twice the second row from the first. The resulting <reduced row echelon form> is
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
There are two <pivot columns>, so the <matrix rank> is two. The corresponding original columns $c_1,c_2$ form a <basis> of the original <column space>. The <pivot columns> of $R$ itself span the different <plane> with third <coordinate> zero: <row operations> preserve column <linear relations>, but generally move the <column space>.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/Row Reduction>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §1.2.2, Echelon Forms; §1.2.3, definitions of pivot position and pivot column]{ref}

Solving the <homogeneous linear system> $Rx=0$ gives $x_1+x_3=0$ and $x_2+x_3=0$. Taking the <free variable> $x_3=t$ gives
$$
\ker A=\operatorname{span}\{(-1,-1,1)^T\},\qquad
\operatorname{rank}A+\dim\ker A=2+1=3.
$$
The <kernel> is the input direction that trades one unit of each of the first two columns for one unit of the third. This trade leaves the output unchanged. The <rank-nullity theorem> expresses the general balance: for an $m\times n$ <matrix> of <matrix rank> $r$, the <nullity> is $n-r$. The <column space> describes reachable outputs; the <kernel> describes invisible changes to inputs.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/The Rank Theorem>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §2.9, Rank Theorem: rank plus nullity equals the number of columns]{ref}

For the attainable output $b$, one solution is $x_0=(-1,1,0)^T$. Every solution lies in the <affine solution space of a linear equation>
$$
x=x_0+t(-1,-1,1)^T=\begin{pmatrix}-1-t\\1-t\\t\end{pmatrix},\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$
Indeed, $Ax_0=b$ and $A(-1,-1,1)^T=0$. Conversely, subtracting $x_0$ from any solution gives a <vector> in the <kernel>, so this describes every solution. For $b'$, the same <row operations> on the <augmented matrix> expose $0=1$: the second requirement demands $3$ where twice the first requires $2$.

\Image[matrix-rank-reduction-consistency.png]
{title=Two <pivot columns>, one <kernel> direction, and the same <row operations> distinguishing attainable $b$ from impossible $b'$}

In general, a <linear system> $Ax=b$ is solvable exactly when appending $b$ leaves the <matrix rank> unchanged:
$$
\operatorname{rank}[A\mid b]=\operatorname{rank}A.
$$
Appending a <vector> outside the <column space> adds an independent direction. When a solution $x_0$ exists, every solution is $x_0+\ker A$, because $A(x-x_0)=0$. Thus one count predicts both how many output directions are reachable and, through the <rank-nullity theorem>, how many independent input directions remain free.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/Solution Sets>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §2.4.2, Key Observation and its proof; §2.4.3, Solution Sets and Column Spans]{ref}

= Rank factorization
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$A=BC,\quad B\in\mathbb R^{m\times r},\quad C\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}$}
{wiki}

For a <matrix> of <matrix rank> $r$, choose an ordered <basis> of its <column space> as the columns of $B$. Express every column of $A$ in that basis and put its coordinates in $C$. Then $A=BC$, $B$ has <full column rank> and $C$ has <full row rank>. Choosing independent original columns makes the corresponding columns of $C$ the standard basis <vectors>, directly proving its rank.

The basis choice is not unique: for any invertible $r\times r$ <matrix> $S$, the factors $BS$ and $S^{-1}C$ have the same product. Once $B$ is fixed, its coordinate <matrix> is unique. For rank zero, the empty factors of the displayed shapes represent the zero <matrix>.

= Orthogonal null-space completion of a square matrix
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$M=A+LR^\top$}

Let $A$ be a real $n\times n$ <matrix> of <matrix rank> $n-m$, and let the columns of $L,R$ be <bases> of $\ker A^\top$ and $\ker A$, respectively. Then $B=LR^\top$ has <matrix rank> $m$, $A^\top B=AB^\top=0$, and $M=A+B$ is an <invertible matrix>.

**Proof.** Since $R$ has independent columns, its <Gram matrix> $R^\top R$ is invertible, and $R^\top$ maps onto $\mathbb R^m$. Thus $\operatorname{im}B=\operatorname{im}L=\ker A^\top$ and $\operatorname{rank}B=m$. The basis conditions give the two zero products. In particular,
$$
MM^\top=AA^\top+BB^\top,
\qquad AA^\top BB^\top=0.
$$
If $MM^\top x=0$, taking its <dot product> with $x$ gives $\|A^\top x\|^2+\|B^\top x\|^2=0$. Hence $x\in\ker A^\top=\operatorname{im}B$ and simultaneously $x\in\ker B^\top=(\operatorname{im}B)^\perp$. Therefore $x=0$, and $M$ is invertible. The case $m=0$ is immediate.

For a common <eigenvector> of $AA^\top$ and $BB^\top$, the two <eigenvalues> have zero product and positive sum: exactly one is zero. Thus the added term fills the missing orthogonal output directions without interfering with the original ones.

= Rank bound for a matrix product
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}(AB)\leq\min(\operatorname{rank}A,\operatorname{rank}B)$}

For composable <linear maps>, $\operatorname{im}(AB)=A(\operatorname{im}B)$ lies in $\operatorname{im}A$ and has <dimension> at most that of $\operatorname{im}B$. This proves the bound on the <rank of a matrix> product. When $A$ is $m\times p$, $B$ is $p\times n$, and $m,n\geq p$, the bound $p$ is attained by $A=\binom{I_p}{0}$ and $B=(I_p\ 0)$.

= Column rank
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\dim\operatorname{im}(A)$}

The <column rank> of a <matrix> is the <dimension> of its <image of a linear map>, equivalently the <dimension> of the <linear span> of its columns. Multiplication on the left by an <invertible matrix> preserves all column <linear relations>, and <elementary column operations> preserve their <linear span>. In <row echelon form> the <pivot columns> form a <basis> of the <column space>. Its <dimension> equals the number of nonzero rows, and hence the <row rank>.

= Pivot column
{parent=Column rank}

A <pivot column> of a <matrix> is a column whose position contains a leading nonzero <matrix entry> in its <row echelon form>. The corresponding columns of the original <matrix> form a <basis> of its <column space>. The column positions survive <row operations>, although the column <vectors> themselves generally change.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/Row Reduction>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §1.2.3, definitions of pivot position and pivot column]{ref}

= Row rank
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\dim\operatorname{row}(A)$}

The <row rank> of a <matrix> is the <dimension> of the <vector space> spanned by its rows. <Elementary row operations> preserve this <row space>; multiplication on the right by an <invertible matrix> preserves all <linear relations> among the rows.

To see why <row rank> equals <column rank>, write a <reduced row echelon form> as $R=EA$, where $E$ is a product of <elementary matrices> and is an <invertible matrix>. Reversibility of the <row operations> gives equal <row spaces> for $R$ and $A$. Also $Rz=0$ if and only if $Az=0$, so the columns have exactly the same <linear relations>. If $R$ has $r$ nonzero rows, its <pivot columns> are the first $r$ <standard basis> <vectors> of $\mathbb F^m$ and span its <column space>. Its nonzero rows are <linearly independent>, since their <matrix entries> in the pivot positions form an <identity matrix>. Both <dimensions> therefore equal $r$, and both counts transfer back to $A$.

= Rank orbits of a matrix under left-right multiplication
{synonym}

Two rectangular matrices are related by invertible left and right multiplication exactly when their ranks agree. The rank-$r$ orbit in $\operatorname{Mat}_{b\times a}(k)$ has dimension $r(a+b-r)$: choose its image in a <Grassmannian>, then a surjective map to that image. = Subadditivity of matrix rank
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}(A+B)\leq\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}B$}

The image of the <linear map> $A+B$ is contained in the sum of the images of $A$ and $B$. Since the <dimension of a vector space> of this sum is at most the sum of their dimensions, <matrix rank> is subadditive. Iteration shows that a sum of $r$ <matrices> of <matrix rank> at most one has <matrix rank> at most $r$, over any <field>.

= Full column rank
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}A=n$}

An $m\times n$ <matrix> has <full column rank> when its <matrix rank> is $n$, equivalently when its columns are <linearly independent vectors>. This requires $n\leq m$. Its <kernel> is $\{0\}$, so the represented <linear map> is <injective> and every solvable <linear system> $Ax=b$ has a unique solution. It need not reach every output.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/One-to-one and Onto Transformations>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §3.2.1, One-to-one matrix transformations theorem and Wide matrices do not have one-to-one transformations]{ref}

For example,
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
$$
has <full column rank> two, but its <column space> is the <plane> $b_3=b_1+b_2$ in $\mathbb R^3$. An attainable output fixes the input uniquely as $(b_1,b_2)^T$; an output outside this <plane> has no preimage.

= Full row rank
{parent=Matrix rank}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}A=m$}

An $m\times n$ <matrix> has <full row rank> when its <matrix rank> is $m$, equivalently when its rows are <linearly independent vectors>. This requires $m\leq n$. Its <column space> is all of $\mathbb F^m$, so the represented <linear map> is <surjective> and every <linear system> $Ax=b$ is solvable. If $n>m$, its <nullity> is $n-m>0$, so solutions are not unique.<Interactive Linear Algebra by Dan Margalit and others/One-to-one and Onto Transformations>[Interactive Linear Algebra, §3.2.2, Onto matrix transformations theorem and Tall matrices do not have onto transformations]{ref}

For example, the <matrix transpose> of the <matrix> in <full column rank> is
$$
B^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It has <full row rank> two. Every output $(p,q)^T$ has solutions $(p-t,q-t,t)^T$, with $t\in\mathbb R$. A <square matrix> of size $n$ has <full row rank> and <full column rank> together exactly when its <matrix rank> is $n$; then it is an <invertible matrix>. A nonsquare <matrix> cannot have both properties, and has no two-sided inverse: such an inverse would make its <linear map> a <linear isomorphism> between <vector spaces> of different <dimensions>.

= Boolean matrix multiplication
{c}
{parent=Matrix multiplication}
{title2=$C_{ij}=\bigvee_k(A_{ik}\land B_{kj})$}

For <Boolean> <matrices>, replace numerical <addition> by logical disjunction and <multiplication> by logical conjunction. = Associativity of matrix multiplication
{parent=Matrix multiplication}

For dimension-compatible <matrices>, $(AB)C=A(BC)$. In coordinates both sides have $(i,j)$ entry $\sum_{k,l}A_{ik}B_{kl}C_{lj}$; distributing and regrouping the finite <sums> proves equality. This permits accumulating a product left to right even when a recursive <algorithm> groups the same transformations from the right. It does not permit changing factor order, because <matrix multiplication> is generally not <commutative>.

= Hessenberg reduction
{parent=Hessenberg matrix}
{c}
{title2=$H=Q^TAQ,\quad H_{ij}=0\ (i>j+1)$}

The reduction of a real square <matrix> to upper Hessenberg form uses <orthogonal similarity transformations>. A <Householder reflector> at column $k$ acts on rows $k+1,\ldots,n$ to annihilate entries below row $k+1$, and its matching right action preserves similarity. Previous zeros are preserved. Rank-one application of each reflector gives total $O(n^3)$ work. Subsequent shifted systems with $H-\lambda I$ can be factored in $O(n^2)$ work each because only one entry per column lies below the diagonal.

= Weighted permutation unitarity criterion

If $Ax=\lambda x$ and $By=\mu y$, then $x\otimes y$ is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> $\lambda+\mu$. Tensor-product spatial grids therefore turn separated coordinate Laplacians into a <Kronecker sum>. Two <Hermitian matrices> give a Hermitian <Kronecker sum>, allowing an <energy method> without computing every eigenvector.

= Off-diagonal entry
{synonym}

A <matrix entry> whose row and column indices differ. These entries lie outside the <diagonal matrix entries>. A <diagonal matrix> has all its <off-diagonal entries> equal to zero. Cancellation of these entries when averaging <density matrices> does not require the individual preparations to have zero entries there.

{parent=Matrix entry}
{title2=$a_{ii}$}

= Diagonal entry
{synonym}

A <diagonal matrix entry> uses the same row and column index. The diagonal entries of a <lower triangular matrix> determine its <determinant> by their product.

= Matrix dimensions
{parent=Matrix}
{title2=$r\times c$}

The matrix dimensions $r\times c$ specify the number of rows and columns of a <matrix>. For <matrix multiplication>, an $r\times s$ <matrix> can multiply an $s\times t$ <matrix>, yielding an $r\times t$ <matrix product>.

= Zero matrix
{parent=Matrix}
{title2=$0_{m\times n}$}
{wiki}

A <matrix> all of whose entries are zero. It is the <additive identity> among <matrices> of the same dimensions and represents the zero <linear map> in any choice of <bases>.
