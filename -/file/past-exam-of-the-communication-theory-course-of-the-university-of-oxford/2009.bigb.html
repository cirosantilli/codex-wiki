= Solution
{parent=2009/1/a/i}

For finite-valued <random vectors>, the <conditional entropy> is the average of the entropies of the conditional distributions:
$$
\boxed{H(X\mid Y)=\sum_{y:p_Y(y)>0}p_Y(y)\left[-\sum_xp(x\mid y)\log_2p(x\mid y)\right]}.
$$
Use $0\log_2 0=0$ and omit zero-<probability> conditioning events. Equivalently,
$$
H(X\mid Y)=-\sum_{x,y:p(x,y)>0}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}.
$$
The base two makes all information quantities measured in <bits>; changing the logarithm base scales them by a positive constant and preserves the inequalities.

= ii
{parent=2009/1/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/1/a/ii}

Apply <Gibbs inequality> to the <joint distribution> and the product of its marginals. Restrict to the Cartesian product of their positive supports, so the reference probabilities are strictly positive as the supplied inequality requires. Expanding gives
$$
0\leq\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p_X(x)p_Y(y)}
=H(X)-H(X\mid Y).
$$
This proves the second inequality. For the first, apply the same argument to the conditional <joint distribution> of $(X,Z)$ for each $y$ with $p_Y(y)>0$, and average with those probabilities:
$$
0\leq\sum_y p_Y(y)I(X;Z\mid Y=y)
=H(X\mid Y)-H(X\mid Y,Z).
$$
Here the conditional support restriction again removes zero marginal probabilities. Thus
$$
\boxed{H(X\mid Y,Z)\leq H(X\mid Y)\leq H(X)}.
$$
The nonnegative differences are <conditional mutual information> and <mutual information>, respectively.

= Solution
{parent=2009/1/b/i}

Factoring a joint <probability> into successive conditional probabilities and taking the expected negative logarithm gives the <chain rule for conditional entropy>[chain rule for entropy]:
$$
H_n=\sum_{r=1}^nH(X_r\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{r-1}).
$$
At $r=1$ this means the unconditional <Shannon entropy>[entropy]. Removing conditioning can only increase each term by part (a), so $H_n\leq\sum_rH(X_r)$.

Write $X_{-r}$ for the vector omitting coordinate $r$. Another application of the chain rule gives $H(X_{-r}\mid X_r)=H_n-H(X_r)$. Therefore
$$
\sum_rH(X_{-r}\mid X_r)=nH_n-\sum_rH(X_r)\leq(n-1)H_n.
$$
Divide by $n-1>0$ to obtain the required averaged inequality. The case $n=1$ is excluded precisely because that denominator vanishes.

= ii
{parent=2009/1/b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/1/b/ii}

The <chain rule for conditional entropy>[chain rule for entropy], with $X_r$ placed last, gives
$$
H_n=H(X_{-r})+H(X_r\mid X_{-r}).
$$
Since $X_{-r}$ includes all the preceding coordinates, conditioning monotonicity gives
$$
H(X_r\mid X_{-r})\leq H(X_r\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{r-1}).
$$
This proves the first assertion for every $r$, including $r=1$. Sum over $r$: the conditional terms on the right sum to $H_n$ by the ordinary chain rule. Thus
$$
nH_n\leq\sum_rH(X_{-r})+H_n,
\qquad
\boxed{H_n\leq\frac1{n-1}\sum_rH(X_{-r})}.
$$
This is <Han's entropy inequality>.

= iii
{parent=2009/1/b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/1/b/iii}

For each $r$, conditioning on all the other coordinates gives no greater <conditional entropy> than conditioning only on the preceding ones. Summing and using the <chain rule for conditional entropy>[chain rule for entropy] gives directly
$$
\boxed{\sum_rH(X_r\mid X_{-r})\leq\sum_rH(X_r\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{r-1})=H_n}.
$$
Equivalently, the left side is $nH_n-\sum_rH(X_{-r})$, so the same conclusion follows from <Han's entropy inequality>. The direct proof also covers $n=1$, interpreting conditioning on an empty vector as no conditioning.

= Solution

In the terminology used here, a <block code> assigns a distinct word of the same length to every source symbol. For a binary code with $m$ symbols this common length must satisfy $2^\ell\geq m$. The equal-length property is what matters in this question; elsewhere the term can also refer more generally to coding blocks of source symbols.

= ii
{parent=2009/2/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/2/a/ii}

A <prefix code> has no <codeword> that is a proper prefix of another. Its <code tree> therefore places source symbols at leaves. It is instantaneous because the decoder knows that a source symbol has ended as soon as its leaf is reached, without needing to inspect later transmitted symbols.

= iii
{parent=2009/2/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/2/a/iii}

A <optimal prefix code>[compact code] or <optimal prefix code> minimizes the <expected codeword length> $\sum_xp_x\ell_x$ for the given source probabilities and coding alphabet. The relevant comparison here is among binary <prefix codes>. The minimum is also the minimum among <uniquely decodable codes>, since the <Kraft–McMillan inequality>[Kraft-McMillan inequality] permits every uniquely decodable length list to be realized by a <prefix code> with the same lengths.

= b
{parent=2009/2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/2/b}

Every internal node of an optimal binary prefix tree has two children. If one had only one child, remove that one-edge detour from all descendant words. Prefix-freeness is preserved and at least one positive-<probability> word becomes shorter, strictly lowering the expected length. Thus the tree is full.

Take a leaf at maximum depth $L$. Its sibling exists because the tree is full. That sibling cannot be an internal node, since a descendant would be deeper than $L$. Hence it is another leaf at depth $L$, and the corresponding codewords differ only in their final bit. This is the <deepest sibling property of an optimal prefix code>.

A finite full binary tree satisfies <Kraft inequality>[Kraft equality], $\sum_x2^{-\ell_x}=1$, by dividing mass equally down each branch from the root. If all $2^n$ lengths were at least $n$, with at least one strictly greater, that sum would be less than $2^n2^{-n}=1$, a contradiction. Since the code is not a <block code>, the alternative that all lengths equal $n$ is excluded. Therefore **some word has length below $n$**. Also a longest word must have length above $n$: if all lengths were at most $n$ and some shorter, the Kraft sum would exceed one.

= c
{parent=2009/2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/2/c}

Suppose every sum of two distinct probabilities exceeds every third <probability>. If an optimal code were not a <block code>, part (b) gives two deepest sibling leaves $x,y$ at depth $L>n$ and a leaf $z$ at depth $\ell<n$. Replace the sibling pair by a leaf labelled $z$, and replace the former $z$ leaf by two children labelled $x,y$. The resulting tree is still a prefix tree, and other word lengths are unchanged. The change in expected length is
$$
(p_x+p_y)(\ell+1-L)+p_z(L-1-\ell)
=(L-\ell-1)(p_z-p_x-p_y)<0.
$$
Here $L-\ell-1\geq1$. This contradicts optimality. Thus **every optimal code is a <block code>**.

Conversely, suppose distinct $x,y,z$ satisfy $p_x+p_y\leq p_z$. Starting with the complete length-$n$ code, arrange $x,y$ as a sibling pair, which is possible by permuting the source labels. Its parent at depth $n-1$ does not contain $z$. Label that parent $z$, and split the old $z$ leaf into children $x,y$. This gives lengths $n-1$ for $z$, $n+1$ for $x,y$, and $n$ otherwise, with expected length
$$
n+p_x+p_y-p_z\leq n.
$$
If the optimal mean length is below $n$, no optimal code can be a <block code>: any injective binary <block code> for $2^n$ symbols has length at least $n$. If the optimal mean is $n$, the constructed non-<block code> itself is optimal. In either case there is an optimal non-<block code>. This proves the reverse implication, including the equality case, and hence the **strict** inequality in the criterion is necessary.

This is the <optimality of a fixed-length code for a power-of-two alphabet>.

= Solution
{parent=2009/3/a}

For finite-valued variables, <mutual information> is
$$
\boxed{I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p_X(x)p_Y(y)}=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)}.
$$
Terms of zero joint <probability> are zero. A <discrete memoryless channel> has fixed transition probabilities $W(y\mid x)$ on finite alphabets, and conditional on an input sequence its outputs are independent with the product law $\prod_iW(y_i\mid x_i)$. Its <channel capacity> is
$$
\boxed{C=\max_{P_X}I(X;Y)}
$$
bits per channel use. The maximum is over all input distributions, with the transition law held fixed.

= b
{parent=2009/3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/3/b}

Let the <probability> of the first input be $t$. The two equal output probabilities are $(2+t)/6$, and the third is $s=(1-t)/3$. Writing $h_2$ for the <binary entropy function>, the output <Shannon entropy>[entropy] is $h_2(s)+(1-s)$, while the <conditional entropy> is $t+(1-t)\log_2 3$. Therefore
$$
I(t)=h_2(s)-s\log_2(27/4),\qquad0\leq s\leq\frac13.
$$
The derivative with respect to $s$ is
$$
\log_2\frac{1-s}{s}-\log_2\frac{27}4,
$$
and the second derivative is $-1/(\ln2\,s(1-s))<0$ in the interior. Thus the unique stationary point is the global maximum. It occurs at $s=4/31$, so
$$
\boxed{P_X=(19/31,12/31)}.
$$
The capacity itself is $\boxed{C=\log_2(31/27)}$. The maximization is over $t$, not over arbitrary output distributions; $s=(1-t)/3$ keeps the feasible outputs explicit.

= Solution
{parent=2009/3/c}

Let $E$ record whether the second stage erased the symbol, and write its output as $Z$. The erasure marker is outside the original output alphabet, so $E$ is determined by $Z$. <Independence> makes $E$ independent of both $X$ and the first output $Y$. The <chain rule for mutual information> therefore gives
$$
I(X;Z)=I(X;E,Z)=I(X;E)+I(X;Z\mid E)
=(1-p)I(X;Y).
$$
The erased branch gives zero information because its output is constant; the unerased branch has exactly the original joint input-output law. Maximizing over the same input distributions gives
$$
\boxed{C_{\rm composite}=(1-p)C}.
$$
This includes $p=0$ and $p=1$. It is the <capacity after independent output erasure>; input-dependent erasure would not justify the <independence> step.
