= Quantum mechanics
{wiki}

Quantum mechanics describes a <quantum system> using a <quantum state>, with <observables> represented by <self-adjoint operators>. For a single particle in one dimension, a <pure state> can be described by a <wavefunction> $\psi(x,t)$: its squared <modulus> gives the <probability density> for position. The <Schrödinger equation> predicts how this <probability amplitude> evolves between interventions; <measurement in quantum mechanics> connects it to experimental outcomes through the <Born rule>. A <wavefunction> therefore predicts a distribution of possible positions rather than a single trajectory as in <classical mechanics>.

The <infinite square well> makes three central ideas visible with elementary <sine> functions. Confinement selects discrete <energy eigenvalues>; <wavefunction normalization> converts a shape into quantitative <probabilities>; and a <quantum superposition> can have a changing <probability density> even when each constituent <energy eigenstate> has a stationary one. This combination explains why <quantum mechanics> uses both <linear algebra> and <differential equations>: <eigenfunctions> describe the building blocks, while <quantum phases> determine how they interfere. The same tools help explain the discrete energies of <chemical atoms> and <molecules>, although their <quantum Hamiltonians> require different potentials and often many particles.

In his first 1926 paper, Erwin Schrödinger formulated quantization as an <eigenvalue> problem for a <hydrogen atom> in <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>. On its opening page he compared the emergence of integers with the number of <nodes of a standing wave> on a vibrating string. The <infinite square well> below realizes that appealing idea especially transparently: the allowed integers follow from the <boundary conditions>. This historical analogy concerns the origin of discrete energies; the <Born rule> supplies the statistical interpretation of the modern <wavefunction>. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/andp.19263840404[Schrödinger, Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem, 1926, pp. 361–363]{ref}.

= Circular infinite quantum well
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$E_{m,j}=\hbar^2j_{|m|,j}^2/(2\mu a^2)$}

A particle confined by a <Dirichlet boundary condition> to a disc of radius $a$ has angular functions $e^{im\phi}$, $m\in\mathbb Z$, and regular radial <Bessel functions of the first kind> $J_{|m|}(kr)$. Their boundary zeros give the displayed energies. The ground state belongs to $m=0$: nonzero angular modes add a nonnegative centrifugal term to the kinetic-energy form. At the origin exclude the singular radial solution, rather than accepting every square-integrable logarithmic function as a physical eigenstate.

= Hegerfeldt theorem
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Let $H$ be a self-adjoint Hamiltonian bounded below, $A$ a bounded positive operator and $p_A(t)=\langle e^{-iHt}\psi,Ae^{-iHt}\psi\rangle$. The <Hegerfeldt theorem> states that either $p_A(t)$ vanishes identically or it is nonzero for almost every real time. In particular, vanishing throughout an open interval forces identically vanishing. The proof uses analytic continuation of $A^{1/2}e^{-iHt}\psi$ into the lower half-plane. Applied to an assumed sharp localization effect, this obstructs strict finite-speed localization for positive-energy one-particle dynamics. It does not identify a controllable superluminal signal: the localization observables and admissible local preparation procedures need separate analysis.

= Rotating-wave approximation
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

After expressing a driven <quantum Hamiltonian> in a suitable interaction or rotating frame, discard terms oscillating rapidly compared with the retained dynamics. Near a selected transition, this leaves resonant terms that accumulate coherently while counter-rotating terms average out to leading order. The drive strength and detuning must be small relative to the discarded oscillation frequencies over the intended regime; strong driving can require the neglected frequency shifts and corrections. The approximation supports <resonant quantum control>, but it is not exact merely because the carrier is tuned to resonance.

= Quantum rotor
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$H=\ell^2/(2I),\quad E_n=\hbar^2n^2/(2I)$}

A quantum coordinate on a circle with kinetic Hamiltonian $\ell^2/(2I)$. Periodic wavefunctions have angular momentum $\hbar n$ and energy $\hbar^2n^2/(2I)$ for integer $n$. Its thermal trace has both a momentum-eigenstate sum and a topological winding-path representation. Twisted boundary conditions or flux would modify this spectrum and are separate models.

= Real-time winding representation of a quantum rotor kernel
{parent=Quantum rotor}
{title2=$K=\sqrt{I/(2\pi i\hbar T)}\sum_m e^{iI(\Delta+2\pi m)^2/(2\hbar T)}$}

The <quantum rotor> with angle period $2\pi$ has both the spectral kernel $(2\pi)^{-1}\sum_n e^{in\Delta-i\hbar n^2T/(2I)}$ and the displayed winding representation. The <Poisson summation formula> converts one into the other by a <Gaussian integral>. Each winding sector has a constant-velocity classical path and action $I(\Delta+2\pi m)^2/(2T)$. Quadratic fluctuations give the same prefactor in every sector, so this is exact. Real time is understood by $T\to T-i0$, which fixes the square-root branch and convergence prescription.

= Coupled quantum rotor chain
{parent=Quantum rotor}
{title2=$H=\sum_n[\ell_n^2/(2I)-J\cos(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_n)]$}

A chain with local rotor inertia and a phase-locking interaction $-J\cos(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_n)$. Its smooth-phase limit is an elastic phase field with inertia $I$ and stiffness $J$. Compact winding and phase-slip processes remain part of the full model even when absent from the Gaussian smooth-sector approximation.

= Harmonic phase mode of a quantum rotor chain
{parent=Coupled quantum rotor chain}
{title2=$\omega(q)=2\sqrt{J/I}|\sin(q/2)|$}

Expanding the cosine gives a wave equation $I\phi_{tt}=J\phi_{xx}$ and a gapless phase mode with physical frequency $\sqrt{J/I}|q|$. The exact harmonic lattice frequency is $2\sqrt{J/I}|\sin(q/2)|$. It resembles <superfluid> sound. In one dimension, Gaussian phase fluctuations and possible compact <phase slips> require care before inferring true long-range order from this branch.

= Imaginary-time normalization for a coupled rotor chain
{parent=Coupled quantum rotor chain}
{title2=$\mathcal S=\int d\tau dx[I\dot\phi^2/(2\hbar^2)+J\phi_x^2/2]$}

When imaginary time is divided by $\hbar$ and ranges from zero to $\beta$, integrating rotor momenta gives $I\dot\phi^2/(2\hbar^2)$ but leaves the potential stiffness term $J(\partial_x\phi)^2/2$. Factoring $1/\hbar^2$ out of both terms requires spatial coefficient $\hbar^2J$ inside the bracket. Setting $\hbar=1$ hides this distinction, but restoring units must preserve it.

= Rotor winding-sector path integral
{parent=Quantum rotor}
{title2=$\phi(\beta)-\phi(0)=2\pi m$}

The thermal rotor <path integral> traces paths closed modulo $2\pi$. Lift them to the line and sum endpoints differing by $2\pi m$. In inverse-energy time $0<\tau<\beta$, the kinetic exponent is $I\int\dot\phi^2d\tau/(2\hbar^2)$. Integer $m$ counts trajectory windings, not angular-momentum eigenstates.

= Momentum-winding duality of the quantum rotor
{parent=Rotor winding-sector path integral}
{title2=$\sum_ne^{-an^2}=\sqrt{\pi/a}\sum_me^{-\pi^2m^2/a}$}

<Poisson resummation> relates the momentum sum $\sum_ne^{-\beta\hbar^2n^2/(2I)}$ to the winding sum $\sqrt{2\pi I/(\beta\hbar^2)}\sum_me^{-2\pi^2Im^2/(\beta\hbar^2)}$. Gaussian Fourier transformation proves equality, including the prefactor. The momentum form converges rapidly at low temperature and the winding form at high temperature.

= Rotor return-kernel fluctuation prefactor
{parent=Rotor winding-sector path integral}
{title2=$Z_0=\sqrt{I/(2\pi\hbar^2\beta)}$}

Writing $\phi=\phi_0+2\pi m\tau/\beta+\eta$ with endpoint-fixed $\eta$ separates winding action and Gaussian fluctuations. The latter give the free return kernel on the lifted line, $Z_0=\sqrt{I/(2\pi\hbar^2\beta)}$. The starting-angle integral supplies $2\pi$. Zero winding is not the same as the zero-momentum ground-state sector.

= Semiclassical quantization
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$I/\hbar\gg1$}

<Semiclassical quantization> uses classical trajectories and actions to approximate quantum states in a regime where relevant actions are large compared with $\hbar$. Phase consistency on a periodic orbit leads to <Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization>. Soliton <collective coordinates> can be quantized this way, while fluctuations supply additional corrections to the leading classical <mass>.

= Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition
{c}
{synonym}

Single-valued semiclassical phase on a closed classical orbit quantizes its action, with <Maslov index> $\mu$ accounting for turning-point phase corrections. A cyclic angle has no oscillator turning points: its wavefunctions are $e^{i\ell q}$, with signed $\ell\in\mathbb Z$ for a $2\pi$ period and <canonical momentum> $\hbar\ell$. There is no half-integer shift for that free angular coordinate.

= Operator formalism
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

The <operator formalism> describes a quantum system using states in a <Hilbert space> and observables represented by <self-adjoint operators> with suitable domains on that <Hilbert space>. Evolution and correlation functions are computed from <Hamiltonian operators> and their <time-ordered products>. For free <quantized fields>, the oscillator construction gives a <Fock space>. The <operator-path-integral equivalence> relates these matrix elements to integrals over field histories.

= Quasiparticle
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A quasiparticle is a collective excitation that can be described approximately by particle-like quantum numbers and dynamics, even though it is not an isolated elementary particle. <Excitons> and <polaritons> are examples.

= Bogoliubov quasiparticle
{c}
{parent=Quasiparticle}

A Bogoliubov <quasiparticle> is an excitation created by the diagonal mode operator obtained through a <Bogoliubov transformation>. Its vacuum can contain particles in the original modes because creation and <annihilation operators> mix. Examples include <antiferromagnetic spin waves>, Bose-gas <phonons> and paired-fermion excitations.

= Polariton
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

A <polariton> is a hybrid light–matter excitation formed by strong coupling between a <photon> and a material excitation. For an exciton-polariton, the material component is an <exciton>.

= Exciton
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

An <exciton> is a <bound state> of an <electron> and an <electron hole>. Their opposite <electric charges> attract through the <Coulomb potential>, producing a neutral <quasiparticle> with internal relative motion and motion of its <center of mass>. In a <semiconductor>, promoting an <electron> from the <valence band> into the <conduction band> leaves an <electron hole>; the pair can occupy either an <exciton> <bound state> or an unbound <scattering state>. Merely creating the two <charge carriers> does not establish binding. A neutral <exciton> can transport excitation <energy> without transporting net <electric charge>. Coupling this material excitation strongly to a <photon> can form a <polariton>.

The striking optical consequence is that an <exciton optical transition> can absorb a <photon> with less <energy> than the threshold for creating unbound <electrons> and <electron holes>. The difference is the <exciton binding energy>, not an extra one-electron <energy band> inside the <band gap>. The <Wannier-Mott exciton> supplies a useful large-radius model; the <Frenkel exciton> describes the opposite, tightly localized limit.<Excitons in Semiconductor Quantum Dots by Matjaž Humar>[§3, printed pp. 4–6: bound pairs, effective masses and localized contrast]{ref}

= Exciton binding energy
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$E_B=E_{\rm cont}-E_X>0$}

The <exciton binding energy> is the positive <energy> needed to separate a bound <electron> and <electron hole> into the relevant unbound <scattering states>. Compare the <exciton> <energy> $E_X$ with the continuum threshold $E_{\rm cont}$ at the same total <crystal momentum> and for the same <energy bands>. The negative internal <energy eigenvalue> $-E_B$ and the positive separation cost $E_B$ are different sign conventions for related quantities. An <exciton> may move freely through a perfect crystal while remaining internally bound: “free exciton” does not mean an unbound pair.

= Wannier exciton
{c}
{synonym}

A <Wannier-Mott exciton> has an internal <wave function> extending over many <unit cells>. Its simplest model assumes a three-dimensional bulk crystal, approximately parabolic <valence band> and <conduction band>, positive isotropic scalar <effective masses> $m_e^*,m_h^*$, a constant scalar <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r$, and a dilute pair population. Replace microscopic crystal forces by those <effective masses> and <dielectric screening>, and neglect exchange corrections and coupling to <phonons>. These assumptions must hold on the spatial and <energy> scales of the particular <exciton>.

With relative coordinate $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_h$, the internal <Hamiltonian operator> is the three-dimensional <hydrogen atom> problem with a <reduced mass> and a screened <Coulomb potential>:
$$
H_{\rm rel}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla_{\mathbf r}^2-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r},\qquad
\mu=\frac{m_e^*m_h^*}{m_e^*+m_h^*},\qquad M=m_e^*+m_h^*.
$$
Here $e$ is the <elementary charge>, $\epsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>, and $\hbar$ the reduced <Planck constant>. The internal <energy eigenvalues>, radius scale, and <exciton binding energies> are
$$
a_B^*=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\hbar^2}{\mu e^2},\qquad
R^*=\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)^2\hbar^2},\qquad
E_{{\rm rel},n}=-\frac{R^*}{n^2},\qquad E_{B,n}=\frac{R^*}{n^2}.
$$
The <principal quantum number> is $n=1,2,\ldots$. This reduction and these scales are derived in <Excitons in Semiconductor Quantum Dots by Matjaž Humar> (§3, printed pp. 5–6, equations (13)–(26)).

To check the scales by <dimensional analysis>, write $C=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)$, which has units of <energy> times <length>. Then $a_B^*=\hbar^2/(\mu C)$ is a <length> and $R^*=\mu C^2/(2\hbar^2)$ is an <energy>. Rescaling $r=a_B^*\rho$ makes $H_{\rm rel}/R^*=-\nabla_\rho^2-2/\rho$, the dimensionless <hydrogen atom> operator. The <Bohr radius> $a_B^*$ is a <wave-function> scale, not the radius of a classical orbit; for this model's lowest internal <bound state>, the mean separation is $3a_B^*/2$.

For the diagrams' hypothetical inputs $m_e^*=0.10m_0$, $m_h^*=0.40m_0$, $\epsilon_r=10$ and $E_g=1.500\,\mathrm{eV}$, where $m_0$ is the free <electron> mass, independent substitution gives $\mu=0.080m_0$, $a_B^*\simeq6.615\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $R^*\simeq10.885\,\mathrm{meV}$. Here <electronvolts> express <energy>. The <exciton binding energies> for $n=1,2,3$ are approximately $10.885$, $2.721$ and $1.209\,\mathrm{meV}$. These are model calculations, not measured material parameters. The large-radius approximation additionally requires $a_B^*$ to be much larger than the crystal's <unit-cell> length scale. In a thin layer, with anisotropic <effective masses>, or with spatially varying <dielectric screening>, this three-dimensional formula is not generally valid.

= Frenkel exciton

A <Frenkel exciton> is an <exciton> whose internal excitation is concentrated on the scale of one molecule or <unit cell>. = Exciton optical transition
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$E_{\rm opt}\simeq E_g-E_B$}

For the ideal <Wannier-Mott exciton> in <energy bands> whose edges occur at the same <crystal momentum>, the pair excitation <energy>, measured from the unexcited crystal, is
$$
E_{X,n}(\mathbf K)=E_g-\frac{R^*}{n^2}+\frac{\hbar^2K^2}{2M}.
$$
Here $\hbar\mathbf K$ is the pair's total <crystal momentum>; the last term is <center-of-mass> <kinetic energy>. For a permitted <photon absorption> transition with negligible recoil <energy>, $\hbar\omega\simeq E_g-E_{B,n}$. An <electric-dipole selection rule> and a nonzero transition <matrix element> are still needed: a <bound state> need not produce an observable <spectral line>. If the relevant <energy-band> edges have different values of <crystal momentum>, a <phonon> can also be needed to conserve <crystal momentum>; subtracting a binding scale from a <band gap> alone does not specify the optical threshold.

= Electron hole
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

An <electron hole> is an unoccupied <electron> <quantum state> in an otherwise nearly full <energy band>, often the <valence band>. “Filled” refers to occupation of the allowed <quantum states>, not to filling all space with a continuous fluid of <electrons>. Each state, including its <spin> label, can hold at most one <electron> by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. The almost full <energy band> and its few missing occupants are therefore simpler to describe as a background plus a few <electron holes>. The <electron hole> is a <quasiparticle>, not a <positron> inserted into the crystal.

Removing an <electron> of <electric charge> $-e$ changes the background's <electric charge> by $+e$. Thus an <electron hole> carries positive <electric charge> relative to that background. Promoting an <electron> into the <conduction band> creates an <electron hole> without changing the whole crystal's <electric charge>: $(-e)+(+e)=0$. An unbound pair still consists of two separate <charge carriers>; only a <bound state> of their relative motion is an <exciton>.

The local cartoon explains why a vacancy can move: when a neighboring <electron> fills it, another vacancy appears where that <electron> was. The vacancy has moved opposite to that particular transfer of an <electron>. In an extended <Bloch state>, use <crystal momentum> instead of imagining a permanently empty atomic site. For a completely filled <energy band> the summed <electric current> vanishes. Omitting one <electron> with <group velocity> $\mathbf v_v$ removes its contribution $-e\mathbf v_v$, leaving a contribution $+e\mathbf v_v$. This fixes both the positive <electric charge> and the velocity convention of the <electron hole>.

More precisely, near a parabolic <valence-band> maximum, set its <energy> $E_v(0)$ as reference and define the <electron-hole> <wave vector> by $\mathbf k_h=-\mathbf k_v$. Then
$$
E_h(\mathbf k_h)=E_v(0)-E_v(-\mathbf k_h),\qquad
\mathbf v_h=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\mathbf k_h}E_h=\mathbf v_v(-\mathbf k_h),\qquad
E_h(\mathbf k_h)\simeq\frac{\hbar^2k_h^2}{2m_h^*}.
$$
The occupied <electron> branch has negative curvature there, while the <electron-hole> branch has positive <effective mass> $m_h^*$. The velocity equality compares the <electron hole> with the missing <Bloch state>'s <group velocity>; it does not contradict the opposite vacancy motion in a local transfer cartoon. The sign conventions are listed in <Phys 460, Fall 2006/Phys 460 Lecture 17>.

= Electron-hole recombination
{parent=Electron hole}

An <electron> filling an <electron hole> removes both excitations relative to the filled-band background. Their <electric charges> cancel, consistent with <charge conservation>; the remaining <electrons> are still present. The released <energy> can leave through <photon emission> or other channels, including <phonons>. Formation of an <exciton> instead retains the excited <electron> and <electron hole> as a bound pair; recombination removes the pair. A <valence-band> <electron> filling a nearby vacancy merely moves the <electron hole> and does not remove the total vacancy count.

= Valence band
{parent=Electron hole}

In the simple <semiconductor> description, the highest occupied <energy band> of the unexcited crystal at zero <temperature>. A missing occupant is an <electron hole>.

= Conduction band
{parent=Electron hole}

The initially empty <energy band> above the <band gap> in the simple <semiconductor> description. Excited <electrons> in it can carry <electric current>; they can also bind to <valence-band> <electron holes> as <excitons>.

= Sudden approximation
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

The <sudden approximation> treats a rapid, finite change of a <Hamiltonian operator> as leaving the <quantum state> approximately unchanged during the switch. The <energy> measurement can nevertheless change immediately: the same <quantum state> has different <probability amplitudes> in the new <eigenbasis>. Compute their <inner products> with the new <energy eigenstates>, then apply the <Born rule>. The <two-level sudden quench> below makes this distinction quantitative without assuming the change is small.

An ideal finite step has exact <quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step>; a finite-duration implementation has a small evolution error under the bounded-model conditions below. An <impulsive quantum kick> is different: its <Hamiltonian operator> grows as its duration shrinks, retaining a finite integrated <action> and a finite change of <quantum state>. A <sudden approximation> therefore concerns short evolution during switching, not <conservation of energy> during externally driven change. The subsequent <unitary time evolution> can be substantial.<Fluctuation theorems: Work is not an observable by Peter Talkner and others>[Talkner, Lutz and Hänggi, p. 2, paragraph before equation (7): sudden switch gives the identity time-evolution operator]{ref}

= Quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step
{parent=Sudden approximation}
{title2=$|\psi(0^+)\rangle=|\psi(0^-)\rangle$}

For a fixed <Hilbert space>, a bounded <self-adjoint> <Hamiltonian operator> with a finite step $H_i\to H_f$ at $t=0$ does not deliver an instantaneous rotation of the <quantum state>. Integrating the <Schrödinger equation> across a short interval gives
$$
|\psi(\epsilon)\rangle-|\psi(-\epsilon)\rangle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\,dt.
$$
For a normalized <quantum state> and $\|H(t)\|\le M$ in <operator norm>, the difference has <norm> at most $2\epsilon M/\hbar$, which tends to zero. The time <derivative> can jump even though the <quantum state> is continuous. Here $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. An instantaneous replacement of the <eigenbasis> changes coordinates, not the vector being represented.

For a real switch occupying $0\le t\le\tau$, remove any real scalar contribution $a(t)I$ that produces only a <global phase>, and write $|\widetilde\psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\int_0^t a(s)ds/\hbar}|\psi(t)\rangle$. The same integral argument gives the sufficient bounded-model estimate
$$
\bigl\|\widetilde\psi(\tau)-\psi(0)\bigr\|\le\varepsilon,\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac1\hbar\int_0^\tau\|H(t)-a(t)I\|\,dt.
$$
Thus $\varepsilon\ll1$ controls the <sudden approximation> up to a <global phase>. For any normalized final <energy eigenstate>, its <probability amplitude> differs from the instantaneous-switch value by at most $\varepsilon$, and its <Born-rule> <probability> differs by at most $2\varepsilon$. These are sufficient error bounds, not necessary criteria. For the fixed-gap <two-level sudden quench>, any switching path $H(t)=-\Delta\mathbf n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2$ with a unit vector $\mathbf n(t)$ has <operator norm> $\Delta/2$, giving $\varepsilon\le\Delta\tau/(2\hbar)$.

This estimate is deliberately for bounded <Hamiltonian operators>. An unbounded <energy> spectrum does not supply a finite global $M$; relevant <quantum states>, <operator domains> and excited <energy eigenvalues> require separate control. The <adiabatic theorem> also requires hypotheses about the path and its <spectral gaps>; there is no universal switch-duration criterion covering all spectra. The <cold-atom collapse and revival after a lattice quench> illustrates how a single switch can be sudden for one degree of freedom and slow for another.

= Impulsive quantum kick
{parent=Quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step}
{title2=$U_{\rm kick}=e^{-iK/\hbar}$}

An <impulsive quantum kick> is the limit of a pulse with finite integrated <Hamiltonian operator>, rather than a bounded finite step. For a bounded <self-adjoint operator> $K$ with units of <action>, a rectangular pulse $H_\tau=K/\tau$ lasting $\tau$ has exact <unitary time evolution> $e^{-iK/\hbar}$ for every $\tau$. In the limiting notation $H(t)=K\delta(t)$, $\delta$ is the <Dirac delta function> and
$$
|\psi(0^+)\rangle=e^{-iK/\hbar}|\psi(0^-)\rangle.
$$
The integral estimate for <quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step> no longer tends to zero: the pulse height diverges. A bounded background <Hamiltonian operator> contributes vanishing evolution during this limit. A more general pulse with noncommuting generators can require <time ordering>; integrated area alone then need not specify the result.

For example, $K=\hbar\phi\sigma_y/2$, with a $y$ <Pauli matrix>, sends a <qubit> in $|0\rangle$ to $\cos(\phi/2)|0\rangle+\sin(\phi/2)|1\rangle$. At $\phi=\pi$, the <Born rule> predicts $|1\rangle$ with <probability> one, however short the regularized pulse is. Its finite rotation is precisely what a bounded finite step cannot accomplish at the switching instant.

= Quantum quench
{parent=Sudden approximation}

A <quantum quench> changes a <quantum system>'s control parameters rapidly and studies the subsequent <unitary time evolution>. In the ideal sudden limit, write an initial <energy eigenstate> $|m_i\rangle$ using a complete final <orthonormal eigenbasis>:
$$
|m_i\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n_f\rangle,\qquad c_n=\langle n_f|m_i\rangle,\qquad
|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_ne^{-iE_n^ft/\hbar}|n_f\rangle.
$$
The <Born-rule> <probabilities> for a nondegenerate final <energy measurement> are $|c_n|^2$, with $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$. For a degenerate <energy eigenvalue>, sum the <probabilities> over its orthonormal <energy eigenstates>. Subsequent relative <quantum phases> can alter other measurement <probabilities> even though these final <energy> populations stay fixed. A <quench> need not begin in an <energy eigenstate>; the same expansion applies to any initial <pure state>.

= Two-level sudden quench
{parent=Quantum quench}
{title2=$p_g=\cos^2(\theta/2),\quad p_e=\sin^2(\theta/2)$}

Take an isolated <two-level system>, or a <spin one-half> <qubit>, with <Pauli matrices> $\sigma_x,\sigma_z$. Let $\sigma_z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $\sigma_z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$. The initial and final <Hamiltonian operators> are
$$
H_i=-\frac{\Delta_i}{2}\sigma_z,\qquad
H_f=-\frac{\Delta_f}{2}(\cos\theta\,\sigma_z+\sin\theta\,\sigma_x),\qquad \Delta_i,\Delta_f>0.
$$
The <energy> gaps are $\Delta_i,\Delta_f$. Prepare the initial <ground state> $|0\rangle$, with <energy eigenvalue> $-\Delta_i/2$. A final <orthonormal eigenbasis> is
$$
|g_f\rangle=c|0\rangle+s|1\rangle,\qquad
|e_f\rangle=-s|0\rangle+c|1\rangle,\qquad
c=\cos(\theta/2),\quad s=\sin(\theta/2).
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $H_f|g_f\rangle=-\Delta_f|g_f\rangle/2$ and $H_f|e_f\rangle=+\Delta_f|e_f\rangle/2$. The unchanged initial <quantum state> is $|0\rangle=c|g_f\rangle-s|e_f\rangle$. Its new <probability amplitudes> are the <inner products> $c,-s$; the <Born rule> gives $p_g=c^2,p_e=s^2$. The angle between the real amplitude vectors is $\theta/2$, whereas the Hamiltonian axis on the <Bloch sphere> rotates through $\theta$.

= Work in a two-level sudden quench
{parent=Two-level sudden quench}
{title2=$\langle W\rangle=(\Delta_i-\Delta_f\cos\theta)/2$}

The drive can supply or extract <work> while the <quantum state> stays approximately fixed. For a differentiable bounded <Hamiltonian operator>, the <Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\langle H(t)\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H(t)}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$
The state-evolution terms cancel, leaving the external drive's <power>. For a finite ideal step the change in mean <energy> is $\langle\psi|H_f-H_i|\psi\rangle$. Thus <conservation of energy> for a closed system with a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator> does not assert conservation of the driven subsystem's <energy> during switching.

Specify <work> operationally by two <energy measurements>: one before the switch and one after. For the prepared initial <ground state>, the first outcome is definite; the second samples the new <Born-rule> <probabilities>. This protocol gives a distribution of measured <energy> differences, rather than generally the measurement distribution of the single operator $H_f-H_i$.<Fluctuation theorems: Work is not an observable by Peter Talkner and others>[Talkner, Lutz and Hänggi, p. 2, equations (8)–(10): initial energy outcome, conditional transition probability and two-measurement work distribution]{ref}
$$
W_g=\frac{\Delta_i-\Delta_f}{2}\quad\hbox{with probability }c^2,\qquad
W_e=\frac{\Delta_i+\Delta_f}{2}\quad\hbox{with probability }s^2,
$$
and hence
$$
\langle H_f\rangle=-\frac{\Delta_f}{2}\cos\theta,\qquad
\langle W\rangle=\frac{\Delta_i-\Delta_f\cos\theta}{2},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(W)=\frac{\Delta_f^2}{4}\sin^2\theta.
$$
For $\Delta_i=\Delta_f=\Delta$ and $\theta=\pi/3$, the <work> is $0$ or $\Delta$, with <probabilities> $3/4$ and $1/4$; its <expectation> is $\Delta/4$ and its <variance> is $3\Delta^2/16$. The initial mean <energy> $-\Delta/2$ becomes $-\Delta/4$. Using different initial and final scalar offsets would also shift the measured <work>; the displayed <Hamiltonian operators> fix that convention explicitly.

= Post-quench oscillations in a two-level system
{parent=Two-level sudden quench}
{title2=$P_0(t)=1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2(\Delta_ft/(2\hbar))$}

After the <quantum quench>, hold $H_f$ fixed and evolve without an intervening <energy measurement>. The <unitary time evolution> is
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=c\,e^{+i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}|g_f\rangle-s\,e^{-i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}|e_f\rangle.
$$
Projection onto the original $|0\rangle$ gives <probability amplitude> $c^2e^{+i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}+s^2e^{-i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}$. Squaring its <modulus> yields the <two-level quantum return probability>
$$
P_0(t)=1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta_ft}{2\hbar}\right),\qquad
P_1(t)=\sin^2\theta\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta_ft}{2\hbar}\right).
$$
Their sum is one. For $\theta=\pi/3$, the first minimum of $P_0$ is $1/4$ at $t=\pi\hbar/\Delta_f$, and the return is complete at $T=2\pi\hbar/\Delta_f$. At $\theta=0$ or $\pi$ there is no oscillation, because the initial <quantum state> is already a final <energy eigenstate>.

= Ground-state overlap after sudden expansion of a square well
{parent=Sudden approximation}
{title2=$P=\left[\frac{4\eta^{3/2}\cos(\pi/(2\eta))}{\pi(\eta^2-1)}\right]^2$}

For an <infinite square well> initially on $[-a,a]$, a normalized <ground state> is $a^{-1/2}\cos(\pi x/(2a))$. If the endpoints change instantaneously to $\pm\eta a$, with $\eta>1$, the <sudden approximation> leaves the <wave function> unchanged, extended by zero outside the old well. Its overlap with the new normalized <ground state> $(\eta a)^{-1/2}\cos(\pi x/(2\eta a))$ is $4\eta^{3/2}\cos(\pi/(2\eta))/[\pi(\eta^2-1)]$. The <Born rule> squares this <probability amplitude>; for $\eta=2$ the result is $64/(9\pi^2)$, and the limit as $\eta\downarrow1$ is $1$.

= Canonical quantization of the electromagnetic field

The <electromagnetic four-potential> has a vanishing temporal momentum and a <Gauss law constraint in gauge theory>. Gauge reduction leaves two transverse <canonical pairs>. = Gauge reduction of the Maxwell Hamiltonian

{title2=$H_T=\tfrac12\int[(\Pi^T)^2+(\nabla\times A^T)^2],d^3x$}

For a spatial potential $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf E=-\dot{\mathbf A}-\boldsymbol\nabla A_0$, the <Maxwell Lagrangian> gives $\boldsymbol\Pi=-\mathbf E$ and $\Pi^0=0$. = Berry phase
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\gamma$}

A <Berry phase> is the <geometric phase> acquired when a nondegenerate <energy eigenstate> follows a closed cycle of parameters of a <Hamiltonian operator> in the ideal limit of the <adiabatic theorem>. The <energy eigenstate> must stay separated from other <energy eigenstates> by a nonzero <spectral gap>, and the parameters must change sufficiently slowly. Returning the <Hamiltonian operator> to its starting value returns the state to the same ray, but its <quantum phase> can remember the journey. The total <quantum phase> contains both the <dynamical phase> and the <Berry phase>.

The beauty of the <Berry phase> is that, within the <adiabatic theorem>'s limit, it depends on the oriented path of the parameters rather than how long the journey takes. For a <spin one-half> <qubit>, the <Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude> turns that memory into half a <solid angle>. <Berry-phase interference with a reference path> converts this geometry into a detection <probability>.

Michael Berry's 1984 paper formulated the <geometric phase> for cycles governed by the <adiabatic theorem> and derived the <solid angle> result for <spin>. Its bibliographic details are Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A, volume 392, pages 45–57, DOI 10.1098/rspa.1984.0023. https://michaelberryphysics.wordpress.com/publications/[Berry's authored publication list, entry 120: Quantal phase factors accompanying adiabatic changes (1984)]{ref}; https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/athena/course/8/8.06/spring06/handouts/Berry_1984.pdf[Berry's original paper, available through MIT]{ref}. The wider concept of <geometric phase> does not require every cycle to obey the <adiabatic theorem>: Aharonov and Anandan formulated a <geometric phase> for general cyclic quantum evolution in 1987. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.58.1593[Aharonov and Anandan, Phase change during a cyclic quantum evolution, Physical Review Letters 58, 1593–1596 (1987)]{ref}.

= Berry connection
{c}
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$A_j=i\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$}

For a normalized, smoothly chosen instantaneous <energy eigenstate> $|\psi(\mathbf R)\rangle$, the <Berry connection> has components $A_j=i\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$. Its <line integral> around a closed parameter path $C$ gives the <Berry phase> in a periodic choice of state vector:
$$
\gamma=\oint_C\sum_j A_j\,dR_j\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
Normalization makes the <inner product> $\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$ purely imaginary, so the <Berry connection> is real. Changing the representative to $e^{i\chi(\mathbf R)}|\psi\rangle$ changes the <Berry connection> to $A_j-\partial_{R_j}\chi$. A single-valued phase factor along the closed path changes the <line integral> by an integer multiple of $2\pi$, leaving $e^{i\gamma}$ unchanged. If the chosen vector is not periodic, include the endpoint phase:
$$
\gamma=\arg\langle\psi(0)|\psi(T)\rangle+i\int_0^T\langle\psi(t)|\dot\psi(t)\rangle\,dt\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
Here $\psi$ denotes the instantaneous <energy eigenstate> representative, with its <dynamical phase> removed. This is freedom to choose a representative of a state ray. https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/quantum-hall-effect/qhe1.pdf[David Tong, The Quantum Hall Effect, sections 1.5.1–1.5.2: Berry connection and a spin example; the spin convention below is stated explicitly]{ref}.

= Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude
{c}
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$\gamma=-\pi(1-\cos\theta)=-\Omega/2$}

Choose the <computational basis> $|0\rangle=|\uparrow_z\rangle$, $|1\rangle=|\downarrow_z\rangle$ for a <spin one-half> <qubit>. On the <Bloch sphere>, let $\mathbf n=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$ and use the normalized <quantum state>
$$
|\psi(\theta,\phi)\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle.
$$
This is the $+1$ <eigenstate> of $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, where $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the <Pauli matrices>. To make it an instantaneous <ground state>, take the model <Hamiltonian operator> $H=-\Delta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2$ with constant $\Delta>0$. Its <energy eigenvalues> are $-\Delta/2$ and $+\Delta/2$, so its <spectral gap> is $\Delta$ everywhere. The <spin> points along $\mathbf n$; this explicit convention fixes the sign of the <Berry phase>.

Keep the polar angle $\theta$ fixed and increase the azimuth $\phi$ from $0$ to $2\pi$, slowly enough for the <adiabatic theorem>. The state vector written above is periodic in $\phi$. Directly differentiating gives
$$
\partial_\phi|\psi\rangle=i e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle,\qquad
\langle\psi|\partial_\phi\psi\rangle=i\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}.
$$
Thus the azimuthal <Berry connection> is $A_\phi=-\sin^2(\theta/2)$ and its <line integral> is
$$
\gamma=i\int_0^{2\pi}\langle\psi|\partial_\phi\psi\rangle\,d\phi
=-2\pi\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}=-\pi(1-\cos\theta)=-\frac{\Omega}{2}\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
The north polar cap has oriented <solid angle> $\Omega=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\theta\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\phi=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)$, positive for increasing $\phi$. Reversing the loop reverses the <Berry phase>. The opposite <spin> <eigenstate> has the opposite <Berry phase> modulo $2\pi$.

\Image[berry-phase-bloch-latitude.png]
{title=A 60-degree latitude on the Bloch sphere encloses a north polar cap of solid angle pi}

At $\theta=60^\circ$, the <solid angle> is $\pi$ and the <Berry phase> is $-\pi/2$. At $\theta=90^\circ$, the <solid angle> is $2\pi$ and the <Berry phase> is $-\pi$. At $\theta=0$, the loop collapses and $\gamma=0$. At $\theta=\pi$, the same expression gives $-2\pi\equiv0$: the <Bloch vector> stays at the south pole, while this representative is $e^{i\phi}|1\rangle$. Its winding is only a choice of <global phase>, so the physically relevant phase factor is again one. The displayed formula is a continuous, unwrapped representative, not a unique real-valued <observable>.

= Berry-phase interference with a reference path
{c}
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$P_+=(1+\cos\gamma)/2$}

An isolated <qubit> that acquires only a <global phase> has unchanged measurement <probabilities>. To reveal the <Berry phase>, use two coherent paths of a <Mach-Zehnder interferometer>, represented by a path <qubit>. In one arm the internal <spin one-half> <qubit> follows the loop; in the reference arm it stays in its starting <energy eigenstate>. Assume equal arm amplitudes, identical final internal states, cancelled relative <dynamical phases>, and calibrated fixed apparatus phases. In the model above, hold the reference <Hamiltonian operator> at its initial value for the same duration. Both arms then have <energy eigenvalue> $-\Delta/2$ and acquire the same <dynamical phase> $\Delta T/(2\hbar)$, so their relative <dynamical phase> vanishes in the ideal limit of the <adiabatic theorem>.

The joint <quantum state> before recombination then factors as
$$
\frac{|\mathrm{ref}\rangle+e^{i\gamma}|\mathrm{loop}\rangle}{\sqrt2}\otimes|\psi(\theta,0)\rangle.
$$
The <Berry phase> is now a relative <quantum phase> between the two paths. The calibrated plus output projects onto $|+\rangle=(|\mathrm{ref}\rangle+|\mathrm{loop}\rangle)/\sqrt2$. The <Born rule> gives
$$
P_+=\left|\frac{1+e^{i\gamma}}{2}\right|^2=\frac{1+\cos\gamma}{2},\qquad P_-=1-P_+.
$$
For the <Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude>, the plus-port <probabilities> at $\theta=0,60^\circ,90^\circ,180^\circ$ are respectively $1,1/2,0,1$. This ideal <Mach-Zehnder interferometer> measurement cannot distinguish $\gamma$ from $-\gamma$ or $\gamma+2\pi$ by itself; scanning a known reference phase can resolve the sign. An uncancelled relative <dynamical phase> would shift the fringe as well.

= Photon absorption

<Photon> absorption removes a <photon> and transfers its <energy> and <momentum> to matter. It contributes a negative photon-number collision source. The integrated source can vanish if <photon emission> and absorption balance.

= Photon emission

<Photon> emission creates a <photon>, for example in <bremsstrahlung> or the decay of an excited material state. = Ground state
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A ground state is a normalized <energy eigenstate> that attains the lowest possible <energy> of a <quantum system>. Precisely, let the <Hamiltonian operator> $H$ be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below on a <Hilbert space>, and write $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$ for the <infimum> of its <spectrum>. A <ground state> is a <unit vector> $\psi_0$ in the <operator domain> $D(H)$ satisfying
$$
H\psi_0=E_*\psi_0,\qquad \|\psi_0\|=1.
$$
Thus a <ground state> exists exactly when the spectral bottom $E_*$ is an <energy eigenvalue>. The lowest <energy eigenvalue> among some listed <bound states> is insufficient if other parts of the <spectrum> lie below it. The <ground-state subspace> collects all minimizing <energy eigenstates>; it can have <ground-state degeneracy>. The <quantum variational principle> characterizes $E_*$ as the <infimum> of normalized <energy> <expectation values>. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Gerald Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19]{ref}.

For the one-dimensional <quantum harmonic oscillator>, the <ground state> has <energy> $\hbar\omega/2$ above the minimum of the <potential energy>. This <zero-point energy> is compatible with being the lowest allowed <energy>: a localized <wavefunction> cannot simultaneously have zero <kinetic energy>. For a <free particle> on the whole line, the spectral bottom is zero but is unattained by any <normalizable wavefunction>; see <ground-state energy>. A lower bound therefore need not describe a physically preparable <energy eigenstate>.

The <ground state> supplies a reference for excitation costs and for the <quantum variational principle>. A <ground state> is also a <stationary state> under a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator>: its <quantum phase> changes, while its position <probability density> stays fixed. A nonzero <ground-state energy> does not imply motion along a classical trajectory, and <ground-state degeneracy> means there need not be a unique lowest <quantum state>.

= Ground-state degeneracy
{parent=Ground state}

When a <ground state> exists, its ground-state degeneracy is the <dimension> of the <ground-state subspace>:
$$
g_0=\dim\ker(H-E_0I),\qquad E_0=\inf\sigma(H).
$$
Here $I$ is the <identity operator>, and the <kernel of a linear map> is taken within the <operator domain> of the <Hamiltonian operator> $H$. The count concerns <linearly independent> <energy eigenstates>, rather than every normalized <quantum superposition> or every choice of <global phase>. Even a single <energy eigenspace> contains infinitely many representing vectors; its <dimension> measures independent freedom. <Ground-state degeneracy> is the lowest-energy instance of <degenerate energy levels>.

For an exact finite model with <energy> unit $\varepsilon>0$, let the <Hamiltonian operator> be the <diagonal matrix>
$$
H_{\mathrm{toy}}=\varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(0,0,2,5).
$$
Its <ground-state energy> is zero and $g_0=2$: the first two coordinate <unit vectors> $e_1,e_2$ span the <ground-state subspace>. Every normalized $a e_1+b e_2$, with $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$, is a <ground state>, including $(e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2$. Its <energy measurement> is certain to return zero, even though a measurement distinguishing $e_1$ from $e_2$ need not have a definite outcome. Under the unchanged <Hamiltonian operator>, this <quantum superposition> is a <stationary state> because both components acquire the same <global phase>.

= Ground-state energy
{parent=Ground state}

The ground-state energy $E_0$ is the <energy eigenvalue> attained by a <ground state>. For a <self-adjoint operator> $H$ bounded below, distinguish this attained value from the always-defined spectral bottom $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$. When a <ground state> exists, $E_0=E_*$. Some usage calls $E_*$ the <ground-state energy> even when it is unattained; here an unattained bottom is explicitly identified as an <infimum>. The <quantum variational principle> states
$$
E_*=\inf_{\substack{\psi\in D(H)\\\|\psi\|=1}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle.
$$
Approaching this <infimum> with trial <wavefunctions> does not ensure that a minimizing <wavefunction> exists. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19]{ref}.

For the whole-line <free particle> with mass $m>0$,
$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2},\qquad \sigma(H)=[0,\infty).
$$
Its <spectrum> is continuous and contains no <energy eigenvalues> represented by <normalizable wavefunctions>. The <Fourier transform> makes $H$ multiplication by $\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)$; a fixed <energy> restricts $k$ to a set of zero length, on which a nonzero <square-integrable function> cannot be supported. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, section 7.2, Theorem 7.8]{ref}. An explicit family of normalized <Gaussian wave packets> demonstrates how the bottom is approached:
$$
\psi_s(x)=(2\pi s^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/(4s^2)},\qquad \langle H\rangle_s=\frac{\hbar^2}{8ms^2}\longrightarrow0\quad(s\longrightarrow\infty).
$$
The formula follows from $\psi_s'=-x\psi_s/(2s^2)$, <integration by parts>, and $\langle x^2\rangle_s=s^2$. Every finite width gives positive <kinetic energy>. At zero <energy>, $H\psi=0$ would require an affine function on the whole line, which is never a nonzero <normalizable wavefunction>. Spreading a <Gaussian wave packet> indefinitely produces an unattained <infimum>, rather than a zero-energy <ground state>.

An additive choice of <energy> origin changes the numerical <ground-state energy> without changing the <ground-state subspace>. For $H'=H+C I$, every <energy eigenvalue> becomes $E'=E+C$, while all differences $E_j-E_i$ stay fixed. Time evolution gains only the <global phase> $e^{-iCt/\hbar}$. This makes excitation costs and spectroscopic differences more intrinsic than the isolated number $E_0$.

= Quantum variational principle
{parent=Ground-state energy}
{title2=$\inf\sigma(H)\leq\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle$}

The <quantum variational principle> estimates the lowest energy by choosing a trial <wavefunction> and computing its <expectation value>, without solving the <Schrödinger equation> exactly. Minimizing over a manageable family of trial <wavefunctions> gives an upper bound; enlarging that family can improve the estimate.

Let $H$ be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below, and put $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$. For $\|\psi\|=1$ in its <operator domain> $D(H)$,
$$
\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\geq E_*.
$$
Trial states may also lie in the form domain $Q(H)=D((H-bI)^{1/2})$, where $b\leq E_*$. Then use the <quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian> shifted by $b$:
$$
q_H[\psi]=\|(H-bI)^{1/2}\psi\|^2+b\|\psi\|^2\geq E_*\|\psi\|^2.
$$
This extends the <expectation value> beyond $D(H)$ without requiring $H\psi$ to exist. Equality for a normalized state holds exactly in the <energy eigenspace> at $E_*$, if that <eigenvalue> exists. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Gerald Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19 and equations (3.50)–(3.51)]{ref}.

A short proof makes the direction of the bound memorable. If $H$ has a complete <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates> $\phi_n$, write
$$
\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n,\qquad H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n,\qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1.
$$
The <expectation value> is a weighted average of allowed energies:
$$
q_H[\psi]-E_*=\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_*)\geq0.
$$
Equality requires every occupied <energy eigenstate> to have energy $E_*$. https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-05-quantum-physics-ii-fall-2013/61bc31b8d8bf0680c322733910a71aa0_MIT8_05F13_Chap_01.pdf[Barton Zwiebach, MIT 8.05 Wave Mechanics, section 5, Variational Principle]{ref}. With <continuous spectrum>, the <spectral theorem> replaces the sum by an integral against the <scalar spectral measure>; the same nonnegative-difference argument works.

For an unnormalized trial <wavefunction>, divide by its squared norm: $R_H[\psi]=q_H[\psi]/\|\psi\|^2$ is the form version of the <Rayleigh quotient>. The <quantum variational principle> supplies an upper bound on $E_*$; it supplies neither a lower bound on $E_*$ nor, by itself, a guarantee that a <bound state> exists.

To see the method at work, choose the real <Gaussian wave packet>
$$
\psi_a(x)=\left(\frac a\pi\right)^{1/4}e^{-ax^2/2},\qquad a>0,\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$
The <Gaussian integral> gives <wavefunction normalization> and the two useful position averages:
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|\psi_a|^2\,dx=1,\qquad \langle x^2\rangle_a=\frac1{2a},\qquad \langle x^4\rangle_a=\frac3{4a^2}.
$$
Thus its position <probability density> is a <normal distribution> with <standard deviation> $1/\sqrt{2a}$: large $a$ means narrow confinement. In units $\hbar=m=1$, <integration by parts> and $\psi_a'=-ax\psi_a$ give the <kinetic energy>
$$
\langle T\rangle_a=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}|\psi_a'|^2\,dx=\frac{a^2}2\langle x^2\rangle_a=\frac a4.
$$
These smooth, rapidly decaying trial <wavefunctions> belong to the <operator domains> of both polynomial <quantum Hamiltonians> below. Narrowing the <Gaussian wave packet> increases its <kinetic energy>; widening it increases the <potential energy> in either confining potential.

For the <quantum harmonic oscillator>, take $\hbar=m=\omega=1$:
$$
H_{\mathrm{ho}}=-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac{x^2}2,\qquad E_{\mathrm{ho}}(a)=\frac a4+\frac1{4a}.
$$
The trial values at $a=1/2,1,2$ are respectively $0.625,0.5,0.625$. The <derivative> $E_{\mathrm{ho}}'(a)=(1-a^{-2})/4$ changes sign at $a=1$, where <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> are both $1/4$. Here the trial family contains an exact <ground state>:
$$
H_{\mathrm{ho}}\psi_1=\frac12\psi_1,\qquad E_0=\frac12.
$$
The <zero-point energy> remains positive even at the best width. For comparison, removing the potential leaves $E(a)=a/4\to0$ as $a\to0$: the free <quantum Hamiltonian> on the line has spectral bottom zero but no normalizable zero-energy <energy eigenstate>. A sequence of ever broader trial <wavefunctions> need not approach an actual <bound state>.

For a pure <quartic oscillator>, keep $\hbar=m=1$ and set the quartic coefficient to one:

The trial values at $a=1,2,3$ are respectively $1,0.6875,0.833333\ldots$. Setting the <derivative> to zero gives

depends on $x$ for every $a>0$, so no member of this trial family is an <energy eigenstate>. Nevertheless, a single width parameter already turns a difficult <Schrödinger equation> into a useful, explicit energy estimate.

For $V=-Ae^{-\mu r}/r$, the normalized exponential with scale $\alpha$ gives $E(\alpha)=\hbar^2\alpha^2/(2m)-4A\alpha^3/(\mu+2\alpha)^2$. Choosing $\alpha=\mu/2$ proves binding if $\mu<Am/\hbar^2$. = Power-law quantum virial theorem
{title2=$2\langle T\rangle=n\langle V\rangle$}
{parent=Quantum variational principle}

For a bound stationary state in a homogeneous potential $V(\lambda x)=\lambda^nV(x)$, scaling a normalized wavefunction gives stationarity of $\lambda^2\langle T\rangle+\lambda^{-n}\langle V\rangle$ at $\lambda=1$. Hence $2\langle T\rangle=n\langle V\rangle$.

= Energy eigenvalue
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

An energy eigenvalue is an <eigenvalue> of the <Hamiltonian operator> $H$: there is a nonzero <vector> $\psi$ in its <operator domain> $D(H)$ with
$$
H\psi=E\psi.
$$
For a <self-adjoint operator>, $E$ is real. Normalizing $\psi$ produces an <energy eigenstate>; an ideal <energy measurement> then returns $E$ with probability one. The associated <energy eigenspace> includes all vectors with this same <energy eigenvalue>, including the zero vector, which represents no <quantum state>. Different <energy eigenvalues> have orthogonal <energy eigenspaces>. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Lemma 2.20]{ref}.

The <energy eigenvalues> form the <point spectrum>, which can be only part of the <spectrum>. The term <discrete spectrum> more specifically means isolated <eigenvalues> of finite multiplicity. The usual fixed-nucleus <hydrogen atom> model has discrete negative <bound-state> <energy eigenvalues> together with a continuum at nonnegative <energy>. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, sections 6.4 and 10.2]{ref}. A <quantum system> need not have a ladder of discrete <energy eigenvalues>: a whole-line <free particle> has <continuous spectrum>. A <plane wave> used for a <free particle> is a generalized <eigenfunction> of the <Hamiltonian operator>, rather than a normalized vector in the physical <Hilbert space>. https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-05-quantum-physics-ii-fall-2013/61bc31b8d8bf0680c322733910a71aa0_MIT8_05F13_Chap_01.pdf[Barton Zwiebach, MIT 8.05 Wave Mechanics, sections 2 and 4]{ref}.

For the <infinite square well> $0<x<L$, the <Dirichlet boundary conditions> give
$$
\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2 n^2}{2mL^2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
Substitution into $H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n$ and <wavefunction normalization> verify these statements directly. Here $E_1$ is the <ground-state energy>, even though its index is one; the labeling convention does not determine which <quantum state> is lowest. Equal populations of $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ have <energy> <expectation value> $(E_1+E_2)/2=5E_1/2$, but an <energy measurement> returns $E_1$ or $4E_1$, each with probability $1/2$. An average of allowed <energy eigenvalues> need not itself be an <energy eigenvalue>.

= Energy eigenstate
{parent=Energy eigenvalue}
{title2=$H\phi=E\phi$}

An <energy eigenstate> is a <quantum state> represented by an <eigenvector> of the <quantum Hamiltonian>. For a time-independent <quantum Hamiltonian>, a normalized <eigenfunction> $\phi$ with <energy eigenvalue> $E$ evolves according to the <Schrödinger equation> as
$$
\psi(x,t)=\phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar},\qquad |\psi(x,t)|^2=|\phi(x)|^2.
$$
Here $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. The changing factor is a <global phase>, so this is a <stationary state>: its position <probability density> is constant. For example, the second <infinite square well> mode has two density lobes separated by a <node of a standing wave> at $x=L/2$; both lobes stay fixed while its <quantum phase> rotates. An ideal <energy measurement> returns $E$ with probability one, but a <position measurement> still has a distribution. Stationary density does not describe a particle sitting at the peak of a lobe. Distinct <energy eigenvalues> accumulate different <quantum phases>, making their <quantum superposition> behave differently.

= Energy eigenspace
{parent=Energy eigenvalue}

The energy eigenspace at $E$ is the <vector subspace>
$$
\mathcal E_E=\ker(H-EI)=\{\psi\in D(H):H\psi=E\psi\},
$$
where $H$ is the <Hamiltonian operator> and $I$ is the <identity operator>. It is nonzero exactly when $E$ is an <energy eigenvalue>. Its <dimension> is the multiplicity of that <energy eigenvalue>; <degenerate energy levels> have multiplicity greater than one. The <ground-state subspace> is $\mathcal E_{E_0}$, and its <dimension> is the <ground-state degeneracy>. A vector in an <energy eigenspace> need not be one of a specially chosen <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates>.

<Linearity> makes the useful point immediate: if $H\phi_1=E\phi_1$ and $H\phi_2=E\phi_2$, then $H(a\phi_1+b\phi_2)=E(a\phi_1+b\phi_2)$. Every normalized nonzero combination stays in the same <energy eigenspace>. For a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator>, its evolution is multiplication by the common <global phase> $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, so it is a <stationary state>. A <quantum superposition> within a degenerate <energy eigenspace> can change spatial shape when its coefficients are chosen differently, but its density does not subsequently beat under this unchanged <Hamiltonian operator>.

Across distinct <energy eigenspaces>, relative <quantum phases> evolve. For orthonormal <eigenfunctions> $\phi_1,\phi_2$ of the <Hamiltonian operator> with <energy eigenvalues> $E_1\ne E_2$, an equal-weight <quantum superposition> has
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\phi_1(x)+e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\phi_2(x)}{\sqrt2},
$$
and position <probability density>
$$
|\psi(x,t)|^2=\frac{|\phi_1(x)|^2+|\phi_2(x)|^2}{2}+\operatorname{Re}\!\left[\phi_1(x)^*\phi_2(x)e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar}\right].
$$
The interference term can change local probabilities even though the <Born rule> assigns the same time-independent probability $1/2$ to each <energy measurement> outcome. <Orthogonality> makes its integral zero, preserving <wavefunction normalization>. This <pure state> is not a <stationary state>; some particular measurement distributions may nevertheless remain constant. An incoherent <mixed state> with the same energy populations has no such interference term.

= Excited state
{parent=Energy eigenvalue}
{wiki}

In the stationary-state terminology used here, an excited state is a normalized <energy eigenstate> with <energy eigenvalue> $E>E_0$, where $E_0$ is an attained <ground-state energy>. Its excitation cost above the <ground state> is $\Delta E=E-E_0>0$. Several <energy eigenstates> can share one excited <energy eigenvalue> through <degenerate energy levels>. An arbitrary <quantum superposition> with <energy> <expectation value> above $E_0$ need not be a single stationary <excited state>; its energy content is described by its <Born rule> probabilities or, for a continuum, its <scalar spectral measure>.

An <excited state> can be a <stationary state>: the second <infinite square well> mode is an immediate example. Spontaneous <photon emission> is a transition caused by coupling matter to the <electromagnetic field>; it is absent from an isolated matter-only <Hamiltonian operator>. The relevant <energy eigenstate> of that isolated model is therefore not automatically an <energy eigenstate> of the full interacting system. This distinction explains how an ideal stationary <excited state> can approximate a state with a finite experimental lifetime. See <electric-dipole interaction> for the coupling used in the simplest optical model.

For a one-photon transition between stationary internal states, neglecting recoil, <conservation of energy> requires
$$
h\nu=E_f-E_i\quad\text{for absorption},\qquad h\nu=E_i-E_f\quad\text{for emission}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant> and $\nu$ is the <photon> <frequency>. Energy matching alone does not guarantee a transition: in the <electric-dipole interaction> approximation its strength also involves the <matrix element> $\langle f|\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon|i\rangle$, where $\mathbf d$ is the <electric dipole moment> operator and $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ specifies <photon polarization>. A zero <matrix element> gives an <electric-dipole selection rule>. Thus an absent optical line does not prove the corresponding <energy eigenvalue> is absent; a different interaction can couple different states. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/qmech/lectures/node121.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, Quantum Mechanics, Selection Rules]{ref}. The <Franck-Hertz experiment> gives a particularly instructive comparison between collision excitation and <photon emission>.

The <first excited state> terminology assumes a least <energy eigenvalue> above $E_0$. Such a next level is not guaranteed by <quantum mechanics>: a <continuous spectrum> can start immediately above the spectral bottom, or <energy eigenvalues> can accumulate there. For example, a <Hamiltonian operator> with <energy eigenvalues> $0,\varepsilon,\varepsilon/2,\varepsilon/3,\ldots$, where $\varepsilon>0$, has a <ground-state energy> zero but no least positive <energy eigenvalue>: every candidate $\varepsilon/n$ is exceeded downward by $\varepsilon/(n+1)$. A <free particle> on the whole line has neither a <ground state> nor a first stationary <excited state>. Each use of a discrete excitation ladder therefore needs a specified <Hamiltonian operator> and its <boundary conditions>.

= Franck-Hertz experiment
{c}
{parent=Excited state}
{wiki=Franck–Hertz_experiment}

The Franck-Hertz experiment connects a reproducible loss of <electron> <kinetic energy> with excitation of a <chemical atom>. The original mercury-vapor experiments were performed during 1912–1914 while investigating <ionization>; they were not designed as a test of the <Bohr model>. Their later interpretation distinguished internal excitation from removal of an <electron>. https://labs.physik.kit.edu/prakt-klass-physik-manuals/P2/Franck_Hertz_Versuch/[KIT physics laboratory, H23 Franck-Hertz-Versuch, Motivation]{ref}.

In a modern mercury tube, a heated <cathode> emits <electrons> and a positive perforated grid accelerates them through Hg vapor. A collector slightly below the grid's <electric potential> rejects slow <electrons>. The oven controls vapor density and hence the <mean free path>. Keep heating and retarding settings fixed, sweep the accelerating <electric potential difference> $V$, and record collector <electric current> $I(V)$. These measured quantities indirectly probe atomic excitation. https://physlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Franck_exp_v12015.pdf[Shah, Alam and Anwar, Electron energy loss investigated through the Franck-Hertz Experiment, sections 3–4]{ref}.

The curve shows repeated current minima, typically about $4.9\,\mathrm V$ apart. In the collision model, an <electron> gains approximately $eV$ of <kinetic energy>, with $e$ the positive <elementary charge>. An <inelastic collision> transfers about $4.9\,\mathrm{eV}$ to internal excitation. Near the grid, this can prevent the <electron> crossing the retarding region, lowering collected <electric current>. Further acceleration restores collection; repeated excitation losses produce further minima. These represent multiple collisions, rather than equally spaced atomic <energy eigenvalues>. Contact potentials shift the first minimum, so compare several successive spacings instead of identifying the first dip's voltage with the excitation cost. https://physlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Franck_exp_v12015.pdf[Shah, Alam and Anwar, sections 3.1–4 and Figure 3]{ref}.

The optical comparison supplies a second observable: mercury radiation at a <wavelength> near $253.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ was detected through a window in the tube. Its inferred <photon> <energy> $hc/\lambda\approx4.89\,\mathrm{eV}$ agrees with the characteristic collision loss, with $c$ the <speed of light>. This agreement supports the interpretation as a transition between internal atomic energies. It does not make the collector trace a complete spectrum. In particular, the lowest excited mercury level, labeled $6\,{}^3P_0$, is metastable because an <electric-dipole selection rule> forbids its direct transition to the <ground state>; the nearby $6\,{}^3P_1$ level emits the quoted <ultraviolet> line. Collision excitation and optical detection therefore have different sensitivities to the available <excited states>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-13-14-experimental-physics-i-ii-junior-lab-fall-2016-spring-2017/afdfff9f8bbe067239af19c8b178a764_MIT8_13-14F16-S17exp7.pdf[MIT Junior Lab, The Franck-Hertz Experiment, section VI and the discussion following Figure 6]{ref}.

Initial <electron> <kinetic energy> spread and varying collision positions broaden real minima. Repeat traces at different retarding settings and oven temperatures to distinguish excitation from collection effects. This probes a discrete energy-transfer channel, rather than every <energy eigenspace>, its <dimension>, or an absolute <ground-state energy>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-13-14-experimental-physics-i-ii-junior-lab-fall-2016-spring-2017/afdfff9f8bbe067239af19c8b178a764_MIT8_13-14F16-S17exp7.pdf[MIT Junior Lab, sections IV and VI]{ref}.

= Dicke state
{parent=Quantum state}
{c}
{title2=$|D_m^n\rangle$}

The $n$-<qubit> Dicke state with $m$ excitations is the normalized equal superposition
$$
|D_m^n\rangle=\binom nm^{-1/2}\sum_{|x|=m}|x\rangle,\qquad 0\leq m\leq n,
$$
where $|x|$ is the <Hamming weight> in the <computational basis>. The <binomial coefficient> counts the mutually orthogonal summands, proving normalization. Every permutation of the qubits leaves the vector unchanged. Splitting the sum according to the first bit and using $\binom{n-1}{m}/\binom nm=(n-m)/n$ gives
$$
|D_m^n\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{n}}|0\rangle|D_m^{n-1}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac mn}|1\rangle|D_{m-1}^{n-1}\rangle.
$$
Terms with zero coefficient are omitted at the endpoints. This recursion is useful for <partial traces> and for distributing a logical <qubit> among many symmetric physical qubits.

= W state
{parent=Dicke state}
{c}
{title2=$|W_n\rangle=|D_1^n\rangle$}

The $n$-<qubit> <W state> is the <Dicke state> with one excitation:
$$
|W_n\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^n|0\cdots010\cdots0\rangle=|D_1^n\rangle.
$$
For three <qubits> it is $(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle)/\sqrt3$. Its <reduced density matrix> on any one <qubit> is $\operatorname{diag}((n-1)/n,1/n)$, from the <Dicke state> recursion. For $n>1$ both diagonal entries are nonzero, so that <qubit> is entangled with the rest: a pure <product state> would have a pure marginal. A <symmetric-arm reduction of an exchange Hamiltonian> supplies a dynamical preparation from one initial excitation.

= One-qubit reduction of Dicke-state superpositions
{parent=Dicke state}
{title2=$\rho_{01}=\alpha\beta^*\sqrt{(n-m)(m+1)}/n$}

For $|\Omega\rangle=\alpha|D_m^n\rangle+\beta|D_{m+1}^n\rangle$, $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$ and $0\leq m<n$, the <reduced density matrix> of any one qubit is
$$
\rho=\frac1n\begin{pmatrix}(n-m)|\alpha|^2+(n-m-1)|\beta|^2&\sqrt{(n-m)(m+1)}\,\alpha\beta^*\\\sqrt{(n-m)(m+1)}\,\alpha^*\beta&m|\alpha|^2+(m+1)|\beta|^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Use the <Dicke state> recursion. The rest-of-system vectors of different <Hamming weights> are orthogonal. For the diagonal terms this leaves the probabilities of the distinguished bit being zero and one. In the cross term, only the rest vector $|D_m^{n-1}\rangle$ occurs in both states, with coefficients $\sqrt{(n-m)/n}$ multiplying $|0\rangle$ and $\sqrt{(m+1)/n}$ multiplying $|1\rangle$. Thus $\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{rest}}(|D_m^n\rangle\langle D_{m+1}^n|)=\sqrt{(n-m)(m+1)}|0\rangle\langle1|/n$, proving the matrix. A single <Dicke state> alone gives the diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}((n-m)/n,m/n)$.

= Quantum superposition
{parent=Quantum state}
{wiki}
{title2=$|\psi\rangle=\sum_j a_j|j\rangle,\quad\sum_j|a_j|^2=1$}

A quantum superposition is a <linear combination> of <quantum state> vectors in a complex <Hilbert space>. In a specified <orthonormal basis>, a normalized <pure state> has the displayed expansion, where $a_j$ are <probability amplitudes>. A nonzero <linear combination> can always be normalized; an arbitrary sum of normalized vectors need not already have <norm> one. Having more than one nonzero component depends on the chosen <basis>: the <Hadamard basis> vector $|+\rangle$ is a <quantum superposition> of the two <computational-basis states>, but is a single vector of the <Hadamard basis>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-04-quantum-physics-i-spring-2016/7f930e013cef9cd7dec5aa88baa83f0a_MIT8_04S16_LecNotes1.pdf[Zwiebach, MIT 8.04, lecture 1, section 4, pp. 10–12]{ref}.

The distinction from classical ignorance is quantitative. Consider the normalized <qubit> <quantum state>
$$
|\psi_\phi\rangle=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle+\frac{e^{i\phi}}2|1\rangle.
$$
Its <density operator>, written in the <computational basis>, is
$$
\rho_\phi=|\psi_\phi\rangle\langle\psi_\phi|
=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&(\sqrt3/4)e^{-i\phi}\\(\sqrt3/4)e^{i\phi}&1/4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Preparing $|0\rangle$ with <probability> $3/4$ and $|1\rangle$ with <probability> $1/4$, while discarding the preparation label, instead gives the <mixed state>
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}=\frac34|0\rangle\langle0|+\frac14|1\rangle\langle1|
=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&0\\0&1/4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The diagonal entries agree, so both preparations give the same <computational-basis measurement> <probabilities>. The off-diagonal entries of $\rho_\phi$ retain <quantum coherence> and the relative <quantum phase>. In the <Hadamard basis>, the <Born rule> gives
$$
\langle\pm|\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}|\pm\rangle=\frac12,
\qquad
\langle\pm|\rho_\phi|\pm\rangle=\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\cos\phi.
$$
The <density matrix> representation retains this distinction between a <pure state> and a <mixed state>. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/general-formulation-of-quantum-information/density-matrices/density-matrix-basics[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Density matrix basics, Interpretation of density matrices and Connection to quantum state vectors]{ref}.

For example, at $\phi=0$, the <quantum superposition> yields the plus outcome with <probability> about $0.9330$, whereas the <mixed state> yields it with <probability> $0.5$. At $\phi=\pi/2$ these particular <probabilities> agree, although the <density matrices> still differ: equality for one measurement does not establish equality of <quantum states>. The <probability amplitude> figure shows the comparison throughout a full cycle of relative <quantum phase>.

This is why a <quantum superposition> is useful: <quantum gates> act linearly on its <probability amplitudes>, and <unitary operators> preserve the full <pure state> information, including relative <quantum phases>. A <Hadamard gate> can turn otherwise hidden <quantum coherence> into different outcome <probabilities>. The beautiful feature is that the same basis weights can support different predictions when amplitudes recombine. The expansion specifies a preparation and its measurement predictions; it does not assign every unmeasured outcome a simultaneous classical value. See <projective measurement> for the separate question of the conditional state after a measurement.

= Qubit
{parent=Quantum state}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathcal H\cong\mathbb C^2$}

A <qubit>, or quantum bit, is a <quantum system> whose state space is a two-dimensional complex <Hilbert space>. Choose an <orthonormal basis> $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$, called the <computational basis>. A <pure state> has a normalized representative $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$, where $\alpha,\beta$ are complex <probability amplitudes> and $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$. General <qubit states>, including <mixed states>, require a <density matrix>. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/quantum-information[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Quantum information, Quantum state vectors]{ref}.

The abstract <qubit> describes the chosen degree of freedom: for <spin one-half>, $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ can label spin up and down along a specified axis; for <photon polarization> in a fixed propagation mode, they can label horizontal and vertical polarization, $|H\rangle,|V\rangle$. These implementations share the same two-dimensional <linear algebra>, but their preparation and <measurement in quantum mechanics> use different apparatus. A <qubit> therefore need not be an entire two-level particle. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, sections 2.2.1–2.2.2, spin and photon polarization examples]{ref}.

The usefulness of a <qubit> lies in controlling <quantum superpositions> and their relative <quantum phases>, then converting them into outcome <probabilities> with <quantum gates> and an appropriate measurement. One ideal <computational-basis measurement> returns one label, zero or one. It does not reveal both <probability amplitudes>, their relative <quantum phase>, or all the bits of a larger register's expansion. <Quantum state tomography> instead estimates a <qubit state> using many repetitions of the preparation and suitable measurement settings. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/quantum-information[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Quantum information, Measuring quantum states]{ref}.

An actual preparation and measurement example is Dederick and Beck's 2014 teaching-laboratory experiment. A 405-nm laser pumped two nonlinear crystals, producing signal and idler <photons> near 810 nm. In their non-entangled run the source produced $|H\rangle_s|H\rangle_i$; a quarter-wave plate in the signal arm changed its <photon polarization> to $|L\rangle=(|H\rangle+i|V\rangle)/\sqrt2$ in their convention. The plate supplies a relative <quantum phase> between polarization components. An idler detection selected the signal trials; signal detectors measured horizontal/vertical, diagonal, and <circular polarization> bases on separate runs. Before the polarization analyzer, a half-wave plate at $22.5$ degrees selected the diagonal basis, and a quarter-wave plate at $45$ degrees selected the circular basis. Coincidence counts $N_{AB},N_{AB'}$ gave the conditional positive-outcome frequency $N_{AB}/(N_{AB}+N_{AB'})$. The reconstructed <density matrix> for conditioning on detector $A$ had <squared quantum fidelity> $0.96$ with $|L\rangle$ and <purity of a density operator> $0.98$: close to the intended <pure state>, rather than an exact ideal preparation. https://people.reed.edu/~beckm/papers/Dederick_ajp.pdf[Dederick and Beck, Exploring entanglement with the help of quantum state measurement, American Journal of Physics 82, 962–971 (2014), sections II D and III A–B]{ref}.

With $|0\rangle=|H\rangle$ and $|1\rangle=|V\rangle$, the intended $|L\rangle$ gives equal <probabilities> in both the horizontal/vertical <computational basis> and diagonal <Hadamard basis>, just as the <mixed state> $I/2$ does. = Qubit state
{parent=Qubit}
{title2=$\rho=\tfrac12(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$}

A <qubit state> specifies measurement <probabilities>, rather than a pre-existing answer to every possible measurement. For a <pure state>, normalized vectors differing by a <global phase> represent the same physical state:
$$
|\psi\rangle\sim e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle,\qquad \gamma\in\mathbb R.
$$
Equivalently, the <pure state> is a ray: the one-dimensional complex <linear subspace> spanned by any nonzero representative. Dividing a nonzero vector by its <norm> fixes its length, but leaves the <global phase> free. The <density operator> is unchanged because $(e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle)(e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle)^\dagger=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$. In contrast, multiplying only one nonzero component by $e^{i\phi}$ changes a relative <quantum phase> and can change measurement <probabilities>. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, sections 2.1 and 2.2.1, state rays and qubit parametrization]{ref}.

After choosing a <global phase>, every <pure state> of a <qubit> can be written
$$
|\psi(\theta,\phi)\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle,
\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad 0\leq\phi<2\pi.
$$
At $\theta=0$ or $\pi$, the parameter $\phi$ does not distinguish physical states: only one component survives. Elsewhere it is the relative <quantum phase>. The corresponding unit <Bloch vector> is $\mathbf r=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$ on the existing <Bloch sphere>. Its angles describe a space of states; for a <photon polarization> <qubit> they are not the photon's position or direction of travel. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, section 2.3.2, equations (2.62)–(2.68)]{ref}.

The <Born rule> in the <computational basis> gives $P(0)=\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and $P(1)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$. These <probabilities> discard $\phi$. In the <Hadamard basis> $|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, adding or subtracting the <probability amplitudes> instead gives
$$
P_X(\pm)=\frac12|\alpha\pm\beta|^2
=\frac12\pm\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)
=\frac12\bigl(1\pm\sin\theta\cos\phi\bigr).
$$
This last equality follows by substituting the angular representative. In the basis $|\pm i\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the same calculation gives
$$
P_Y(\pm)=\frac12|\alpha\mp i\beta|^2
=\frac12\pm\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)
=\frac12\bigl(1\pm\sin\theta\sin\phi\bigr).
$$
Thus both the real and imaginary parts of <quantum coherence> can be observed, using different measurement settings. For example, $(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ both give $P(0)=P(1)=1/2$, but the first gives $P_X(+)=1$ and the second $P_X(+)=0$. A <global phase> could never cause this difference.

For a general <qubit state>, write its <density matrix> in the <computational basis> as
$$
\rho=\begin{pmatrix}p&c\\c^*&1-p\end{pmatrix},\qquad
p\in\mathbb R,\quad c\in\mathbb C,\quad
0\leq p\leq1,\quad |c|^2\leq p(1-p).
$$
These are exactly the <Hermitian>, unit-<trace>, <positive semidefinite> constraints. Positivity means every <eigenvalue> is nonnegative, not that every matrix entry is nonnegative. The general definition is given in <density matrix>. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/general-formulation-of-quantum-information/density-matrices/density-matrix-basics[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Density matrix basics, Definition and Connection to quantum state vectors]{ref}.

For this two-by-two <matrix>, the explicit constraint follows from its <determinant>, $\det\rho=p(1-p)-|c|^2$: with <trace> one, a nonnegative <determinant> and real <eigenvalues> rule out a negative <eigenvalue>. Its <eigenvalues> are
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac12\left(1\pm\sqrt{(2p-1)^2+4|c|^2}\right).
$$
The <pure states> have $|c|^2=p(1-p)$ and <eigenvalues> $1,0$; the <mixed states> have strict inequality and two positive <eigenvalues>. For a normalized representative, $p=|\alpha|^2$ and $c=\alpha\beta^*$. A statistical preparation $\rho=\sum_j w_j|\psi_j\rangle\langle\psi_j|$, with $w_j\geq0$ and $\sum_jw_j=1$, averages <probabilities>, rather than adding <probability amplitudes>. Such a preparation is mixed if the positive-weight vectors do not all represent the same ray. A <reduced density matrix> of an entangled pair can also be mixed even when the pair has a <pure state>. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/general-formulation-of-quantum-information/density-matrices/density-matrix-basics[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Density matrix basics, Interpretation of density matrices]{ref}.

For a concrete check, the <pure state> $(\sqrt3|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/2$ and the <mixed state> obtained by preparing $|0\rangle$ with <probability> $3/4$ and $|1\rangle$ with <probability> $1/4$ have
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{pure}}=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&\sqrt3/4\\\sqrt3/4&1/4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&0\\0&1/4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Both have <trace> one and the same <computational-basis measurement> <probabilities>. Their <eigenvalues> are respectively $(1,0)$ and $(3/4,1/4)$; the respective values of their <purity of a density operator> $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)$ are $1$ and $5/8$. Their <Hadamard basis> plus <probabilities> are respectively $1/2+\sqrt3/4\approx0.9330$ and $1/2$. By contrast, setting $p=3/4,c=1/2$ would give <determinant> $-1/16$ and <eigenvalue> $(2-\sqrt5)/4<0$: normalized diagonal entries alone do not make a valid <qubit state>.

The same constraints become especially beautiful in the <Bloch ball> representation. With the <Pauli matrices> $\boldsymbol\sigma=(X,Y,Z)$ and the <identity operator> $I$,
$$
\rho=\tfrac12(I+r_xX+r_yY+r_zZ),\qquad
\mathbf r=(2\operatorname{Re}c,-2\operatorname{Im}c,2p-1),\qquad |\mathbf r|\leq1.
$$
The <eigenvalues> are $(1\pm|\mathbf r|)/2$, so the <Bloch sphere> is precisely the <pure state> boundary, and $I/2$ is the maximally mixed centre. For each axis $j$, the <Born rule> gives $P_j(\pm)=(1\pm r_j)/2$. This unifies a geometric picture, matrix positivity, and experimental frequencies in one description. For the equatorial family in the phase diagram, $p=1/2,c=e^{-i\phi}/2$ and $\mathbf r=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$; replacing it by $I/2$ erases the transverse components and every fringe. See <quantum state tomography> for how independent ensembles measured along the three axes estimate these components. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, section 2.3.2, equations (2.62)–(2.70)]{ref}.

= Dynamical phase
{parent=Quantum phase}

For an <energy eigenstate> with instantaneous <energy eigenvalue> $E(t)$, the <dynamical phase> is
$$
\delta(T)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^T E(t)\,dt.
$$
It comes from the <Schrödinger equation> and depends on the time spent evolving. In an ideal cycle under the <adiabatic theorem>, a periodic choice of instantaneous <energy eigenstate> gives $|\Psi(T)\rangle=e^{i(\delta+\gamma)}|\Psi(0)\rangle$, with <Berry phase> $\gamma$.

= Global phase
{parent=Quantum phase}
{wiki}

A global phase multiplies an entire <quantum state> by one complex number of <modulus> one. It does not change any measurement probability, unlike a relative phase between components.

= Global phase of an uncontrolled quantum gate is unobservable
{parent=Global phase}
{title2=$\mathcal U_{e^{i\gamma}V}=\mathcal U_V$}

A physical <unitary gate> used without coherent control gives the same <quantum channel> for $V$ and $e^{i\gamma}V$:
$$
(e^{i\gamma}V)\rho(e^{i\gamma}V)^\dagger=V\rho V^\dagger.
$$
Interleaving any preparations, known operations and measurements preserves this equality. Equivalently, each fixed measurement branch with $k$ uses gains only the overall factor $e^{ik\gamma}$, which cancels in its <probability>. Thus unrestricted repeated uncontrolled uses do not determine the common phase of the <eigenvalues>. A supplied <controlled unitary gate> is a different resource: $|0\rangle\langle0|\otimes I+|1\rangle\langle1|\otimes V$ changes by a relative control phase when $V$ is replaced by $e^{i\gamma}V$. Such controlled access permits <quantum phase estimation> to measure that phase relative to the identity branch.

= Quantum uncertainty
{parent=Observable}
{title2=$\Delta Q=\|(Q-\langle Q\rangle)\psi\|$}

For a normalized state with finite second moment of a <quantum observable> $Q$, its uncertainty is the standard deviation of the measurement outcome: $(\Delta Q)^2=\langle Q^2\rangle-\langle Q\rangle^2$. This equals $\|(Q-\langle Q\rangle)\psi\|^2$. The <Robertson uncertainty principle> bounds products of uncertainties using the expectation of the <commutator>.

= Expectation value
{parent=Observable}
{title2=$\langle A\rangle_\psi=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$}

= Quantum expectation value
{synonym}

The <expectation value> of a <quantum observable> is the <expected value> of its measurement outcome <random variable> in a specified <quantum state>. It predicts the mean of outcomes from many identical <quantum state preparations>. It need not equal any one possible outcome: for a <spin one-half> particle, equal <probabilities> of $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ give mean zero even though zero is never an outcome of that <spin> component. Here $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>.

Let $A=A^\dagger$ be a <Hermitian operator> on a finite-dimensional <Hilbert space>, and let $|\psi\rangle$ be a normalized <pure state>, $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$. The <spectral theorem> writes
$$
A=\sum_r a_rP_r,\qquad P_rP_s=\delta_{rs}P_r,\qquad \sum_rP_r=I.
$$
The $a_r$ are distinct real <eigenvalues>, $P_r$ are <orthogonal projections> onto their <eigenspaces>, and $I$ is the <identity operator>. For an ideal <projective measurement> of $A$, the <Born rule> and the <expectation value> give
$$
p_r=\langle\psi|P_r|\psi\rangle=\|P_r|\psi\rangle\|^2,\qquad
\sum_rp_r=1,\qquad
\langle A\rangle_\psi=\sum_ra_rp_r=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle.
$$
Thus the <expectation value> is a <probability>-weighted average of <eigenvalues>, including all vectors in a degenerate <eigenspace> in its $P_r$. In an <orthonormal eigenbasis>, $|\psi\rangle=\sum_jc_j|j\rangle$, this is $\sum_j a_j|c_j|^2$, where repeated $a_j$ are allowed. The weighted average lies between the smallest and largest <eigenvalues>; it can lie in a gap between them. The statistical meaning and operator expression are connected in this way in the authored treatment of average energies.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 20: Operators>[The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III, Chapter 20: Operators, §20–2, equations (20.11)–(20.20): outcome frequencies, weighted mean, and operator expectation]{ref}

If a nonzero <vector> $|v\rangle$ has not been normalized, use $\langle v|A|v\rangle/\langle v|v\rangle$; the denominator is essential. A <global phase> cancels between the bra and ket, so $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ have the same <expectation value> and outcome <probabilities>.

The outcome <variance> measures the spread about this mean, whereas the <quantum uncertainty> is its <standard deviation>:
$$
\operatorname{Var}_\psi(A)=\sum_rp_r(a_r-\langle A\rangle_\psi)^2
=\langle A^2\rangle_\psi-\langle A\rangle_\psi^2
=(\Delta A)^2.
$$
For a <pure state>, zero <variance> means that $|\psi\rangle$ lies entirely in one <eigenspace>. That <eigenspace> need not be one-dimensional. Knowing only a mean generally does not determine the entire outcome <probability distribution>.

For a <mixed state>, the same formulas use its <density matrix> $\rho$, a <positive operator> with <trace> one:
$$
p_r=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P_r),\qquad
\langle A\rangle_\rho=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}_\rho(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A^2)-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)^2.
$$
A <pure state> is the special case $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$. For a <quantum state ensemble> $\rho=\sum_kw_k\rho_k$, the <expectation value> is $\sum_kw_k\langle A\rangle_{\rho_k}$. This linearity is useful when preparations vary between trials; different <quantum state ensembles> can have the same <density matrix>.

These formulas require no operator-domain qualifications in finite dimension. For an unbounded <self-adjoint operator>, the <spectral theorem> instead gives a spectral <probability measure>; a finite mean requires an integrable absolute first moment, and finite <variance> requires a finite second moment. Writing $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ as an operator <inner product> additionally requires $|\psi\rangle$ in the <operator domain> of $A$. The <quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian> explains the useful wider form-domain version.

= Spin expectation value
{parent=Expectation value}
{title2=$\langle S_z\rangle=\hbar\cos\theta/2$}

For <spin one-half>, choose the normalized <spin> <eigenstates> $|+z\rangle,|-z\rangle$ with $S_z|\pm z\rangle=\pm(\hbar/2)|\pm z\rangle$. The <spin one-half along an axis> <pure state> in the $xz$ plane is
$$
|\psi_\theta\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|+z\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)|-z\rangle,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.
$$
Its <norm> is one because the two <eigenstates> are <orthogonal> and $\cos^2(\theta/2)+\sin^2(\theta/2)=1$. Applying the <Born rule> to a $z$ <projective measurement> gives
$$
p_+=\cos^2(\theta/2)=\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2},\qquad
p_-=\sin^2(\theta/2)=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}.
$$
Consequently the <expectation value>, second moment, and <variance> are
$$
\langle S_z\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2}(p_+-p_-)=\frac{\hbar}{2}\cos\theta,\qquad
\langle S_z^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar^2}{4},\qquad
(\Delta S_z)^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\sin^2\theta.
$$
At $\theta=\pi/3$, the outcome <probabilities> are $3/4$ and $1/4$, the mean is $\hbar/4$, and the <variance> is $3\hbar^2/16$. Every individual outcome remains $\pm\hbar/2$. At $\theta=\pi/2$, the mean is zero and the <variance> is maximal, $\hbar^2/4$. At either pole, one outcome has <probability> one and the <variance> vanishes.

The diagonal <mixed state> $\rho_{\rm diag}=p_+|+z\rangle\langle+z|+p_-|-z\rangle\langle-z|$ has the same $S_z$ <probabilities>, <expectation value>, and <variance> as $|\psi_\theta\rangle$. = Estimating a quantum expectation value
{parent=Expectation value}
{title2=$\widehat\mu_N=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^NX_i$}

Prepare a fresh copy of the same <quantum state> for each trial and measure the same <observable>. If preparations and readouts are independent, the outcomes $X_i$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with the <Born rule> <probability distribution>. Their <sample mean> is an <unbiased estimator> of the <expectation value>:
$$
\widehat\mu_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^NX_i,\qquad
\mathbb E[\widehat\mu_N]=\langle A\rangle,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_N)=\frac{(\Delta A)^2}{N},\qquad
\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\mu_N)=\frac{\Delta A}{\sqrt N}.
$$
The <law of large numbers> explains convergence of the <sample mean>, while the <standard error> describes its finite-sample spread. It takes four times as many independent trials to halve that <standard error>. This reduces estimation error; it does not reduce the <quantum uncertainty> of a single prepared copy.

For the <spin expectation value>, let $n_+$ and $n_-$ be the two outcome counts, with $N=n_++n_-$. Then
$$
\widehat\mu_N=\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{n_+-n_-}{N},\qquad
n_+\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N,p_+),\qquad
\Pr\left(\widehat\mu_N=\hbar\left(\frac{k}{N}-\frac12\right)\right)
=\binom Nk p_+^k(1-p_+)^{N-k}.
$$
The <binomial distribution> gives the exact sampling model. For $\theta=\pi/3$, the <standard error> is $\hbar\sqrt3/(4\sqrt N)$: approximately $0.1083\hbar$ for $N=16$ and $0.03827\hbar$ for $N=128$. A run can fluctuate away from the predicted mean even as its typical spread decreases.

Repeatedly measuring one surviving system is a different protocol. Under the ideal <Lüders rule>, outcome $a_r$ leaves $P_r|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p_r}$ when $p_r>0$. With no intervening change of <observable> or dynamics moving the state out of that <eigenspace>, the next <projective measurement> returns $a_r$ with certainty. Such a record is correlated and does not sample the original <Born rule> distribution independently. The <quantum nondemolition measurement> discussion describes this repeatability; fresh <quantum state preparations> supply the ensemble needed for the estimator above.

= Stern-Gerlach expectation estimation
{parent=Estimating a quantum expectation value}
{c}

A concrete idealized implementation uses a <Stern-Gerlach experiment> twice: a preparation filter along $\mathbf n=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)$ passes only its plus <spin> channel, and a $z$ analyzer resolves both output channels for many fresh atoms. This prepares the <spin one-half along an axis> state $|\psi_\theta\rangle$. The authored apparatus description uses slit collimation, a nonuniform <magnetic field> with a pointed pole, and counters at the separated outputs.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 5: Spin One>[The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III, Chapter 5: Spin One, §5–1, figure 5–1 and the paragraph proposing output counters; §5–4, slit diffraction and internal-state limitations]{ref} The two-channel <spin one-half> preparation and analyzer are described separately; the source's recombining “improved” apparatus is explicitly idealized, so the setup here is a proposed estimation model, not a report of observed counts.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 6: Spin One-Half>[The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume III, Chapter 6: Spin One-Half, §6–2, figures 6–1 and 6–2: two beams, one-channel preparation, and a second analyzer; idealized apparatus qualification in §5–1 of the preceding chapter]{ref}

Count every accepted analyzer event in its calibrated $+z$ or $-z$ channel and compute $\widehat\mu_N$ from the count difference. The <magnetic field> gradient separates trajectories through their <magnetic moments>; calibrated channel labels convert positions into <spin> <eigenvalues>. The readout is a population imbalance, not an intermediate trajectory carrying <spin> $\hbar\cos\theta/2$. Beam position also depends on flight time and field geometry, so averaging raw positions alone does not establish a calibrated <spin expectation value>.

As controls, feed known $|+z\rangle$ and $|-z\rangle$ preparations to check the channel assignment and leakage, hold the analyzer axis fixed, and repeat short blocks to check preparation drift. Measure background counts and relative channel efficiencies. With efficiencies $\eta_+$ and $\eta_-$ and negligible background, the detected plus fraction is
$$
p_+^{\rm det}=\frac{\eta_+p_+}{\eta_+p_++\eta_-(1-p_+)}.
$$
Unequal efficiencies bias the naive <sample mean>, even for arbitrarily many counts. For example, $p_+=3/4$, $\eta_+=1$, and $\eta_-=1/2$ give detected fraction $6/7$ and naive mean $5\hbar/14$, rather than $\hbar/4$. Background, overlapping beams, detector saturation, angle misalignment, unwanted internal states, and preparation correlations require a better <quantum measurement> model. Increasing $N$ only reduces the independent sampling contribution; it cannot remove these systematic errors. The selected preparation beam is the population being estimated, so losses in preparing it must be distinguished from losses in reading its two outcomes.

= Quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian
{parent=Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$q_H(\psi)=\|H^{1/2}\psi\|^2$}

For a positive self-adjoint <Hamiltonian operator>, finite energy means that a normalized state lies in $D(H^{1/2})$, its form domain, and has <expectation value> $q_H(\psi)$. This domain can be larger than $D(H)$. With a complete orthonormal basis of <energy eigenstates>, $\psi=\sum_nc_ne_n$ and $He_n=E_ne_n$ give $q_H(\psi)=\sum_nE_n|c_n|^2$ by applying $H^{1/2}$ and taking its squared norm. Membership in $D(H)$ instead requires $\sum_nE_n^2|c_n|^2<\infty$. For a Dirichlet free-particle <Hamiltonian operator> on an interval, the energy form is $\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2/(2m)$ on functions with square-integrable weak derivative and zero endpoint values.

= Morse potential
{parent=Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)}
{c}
{title2=$V(x)=D_e(1-e^{-a(x-x_e)})^2$}
{wiki}

The Morse potential models an anharmonic molecular bond with dissociation energy $D_e>0$, equilibrium separation $x_e$, and inverse length $a>0$. Unlike a <quantum harmonic oscillator>, it has finitely many bound levels and a dissociation continuum.

= Morse oscillator
{parent=Morse potential}
{c}
{title2=$E_n=A(n+1/2)-B(n+1/2)^2$}

The quantum Hamiltonian with kinetic energy $p^2/(2\mu)$ and <Morse potential> has bound energies of the displayed form, where $A=\hbar\omega_e$, $B=A^2/(4D_e)>0$ and $\omega_e=a\sqrt{2D_e/\mu}$. Adjacent energy differences are $A-2B(n+1)$, distinct and positive over the physical bound-level range. A finite truncation with nonzero adjacent dipole couplings therefore satisfies <connected nondegenerate transition chain generates special unitary control>. Controllability of such a truncation does not by itself prove controllability on the full space including dissociation states.

= Factorized quantum Hamiltonian
{parent=Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$H=Q^\dagger Q/(2m)$}

For $Q=\hat p-isA(x)$ with real $A$ and positive $s$, the factorization gives potential $(s^2A^2-s\hbar A')/(2m)$ and a nonnegative energy form. Its zero mode is proportional to $\exp[-s\int A\,dx/\hbar]$. Taking $A=x$ and optimizing positivity over $s$ gives the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle> for states with finite second moments.

= Normalizable zero mode of a polynomial factorized Hamiltonian
{parent=Factorized quantum Hamiltonian}
{title2=$\psi_0(x)=C\exp[-\hbar^{-1}\int_0^xf(t)\,dt]$}

For real <polynomial> $f(x)=cx^n+\cdots$, $c\ne0$, the zero mode of $H=(p+if)(p-if)/(2m)$ is normalizable on $\mathbb R$ exactly when $n$ is odd and $c>0$. Its squared modulus has leading exponential $\exp[-2cx^{n+1}/((n+1)\hbar)]$. Odd $n$ gives the same tail sign at both ends; positivity of $c$ makes both tails decay. This kernel is one-dimensional because $(p-if)\psi=0$ is a first-order <differential equation>. On the <operator domain> where integration by parts is valid, the energy form is $\|(p-if)\psi\|^2/(2m)\geq0$.

= Quantum symmetry of a Hamiltonian
{parent=Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)}

A unitary operator $U$ is a symmetry of a time-independent Hamiltonian $H$ when
$$
U^\dagger H U=H,
$$
equivalently $[U,H]=0$. A differentiable one-parameter symmetry $U(s)=e^{-isQ/\hbar}$ has a self-adjoint generator $Q$ satisfying $[Q,H]=0$, so $Q$ is conserved.

= Conserved generator of a continuous quantum symmetry
{parent=Quantum symmetry of a Hamiltonian}

If $U(s)=e^{-isQ/\hbar}$ and $U(s)^\dagger H U(s)=H$, differentiation at $s=0$ gives
$$
[Q,H]=0.
$$
In the <Heisenberg picture>,
$$
\frac{dQ}{dt}=\frac i\hbar[H,Q]=0.
$$

= Unitary time evolution
{parent=Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)}

A time-independent Hamiltonian evolves a state by the unitary operator $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$.

= Perfect quantum state transfer
{parent=Unitary time evolution}
{title2=$e^{-iHt_*}|a\rangle=e^{i\chi}|b\rangle$}

A <Hamiltonian operator> has perfect transfer between <basis> states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ at time $t_*$ if $e^{-iHt_*}|a\rangle=e^{i\chi}|b\rangle$ for a known <global phase> $\chi$. In an excitation-conserving <qubit> network whose vacuum is fixed, the initial <product state> with $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ at site $a$ and zeros elsewhere evolves to $\alpha|\mathrm{vac}\rangle+\beta e^{i\chi}|b\rangle$. A local <phase gate> at $b$ removing $\chi$ restores the unknown <qubit> there. This explains why transfer of the single-excitation <basis> state suffices for <qubit> transfer when the vacuum phase is also accounted for.

{c}

In the Schrödinger picture, states evolve with the <Hamiltonian operator> while observables without explicit time dependence are fixed. For time-independent $H$, the corresponding <Heisenberg picture> operator is $O_H(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}O_Se^{-iHt/\hbar}$.

= Time-evolution operator
{title2=$U(t)$}
{parent=Unitary time evolution}

The time-evolution operator maps an initial <quantum state> to its state at a later time. For a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator> $H$, it is the <unitary operator> $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$.

= Quantum-mechanical propagator
{title2=$K(q_f,t_f;q_i,t_i)$}
{parent=Unitary time evolution}

A quantum-mechanical propagator is the position-space kernel $K(q_f,t_f;q_i,t_i)=\langle q_f|U(t_f,t_i)|q_i\rangle$ of the time-evolution operator. It is the amplitude to evolve between the specified endpoint configurations.

= Quantum-mechanical propagator in a constant force
{parent=Quantum-mechanical propagator}
{title2=$K_F(q_1,q_0;T)$}

In units $\hbar=1$, a particle of mass $m$ with potential $V(q)=-Fq$ has classical path $q_c(t)=q_0+[(q_1-q_0)/T-FT/(2m)]t+Ft^2/(2m)$. Its action and exact <quantum-mechanical propagator> are
$$
S_c=\frac{m(q_1-q_0)^2}{2T}+\frac{FT(q_1+q_0)}2-\frac{F^2T^3}{24m},\qquad K_F=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi iT}}e^{iS_c}.
$$
The fluctuation operator is the free one because the potential is linear. This is a special case of <classical-path factorization of a quadratic path integral>.

= Normalized short-time Schrödinger kernel
{parent=Quantum-mechanical propagator}
{title2=$K_{\Delta t}(q,q^\prime)=(2\pi i\Delta t)^{-1/2}e^{i(q-q^\prime)^2/(2\Delta t)-i\Delta tV(q^\prime)}$}

For unit mass and $\hbar=1$, this prepoint kernel tends to the identity and generates the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>. The square-root branch follows the damped <Fresnel integral>. Its normalized moments are $1$, $0$, and $i\Delta t$ at orders zero, one and two; these fix both the normalization and the kinetic sign.

= Schrödinger equation from a short-time path integral
{c}
{parent=Normalized short-time Schrödinger kernel}
{title2=$i\partial_t\psi=(-\tfrac12\partial_q^2+V)\psi$}

Expand a smooth <wavefunction> and potential in the <normalized short-time Schrödinger kernel>. Its odd moment vanishes and its second moment is $i\Delta t$, so one step changes the wavefunction by $i\Delta t\,\psi^{\prime\prime}/2-i\Delta tV\psi+O(\Delta t^2)$. Division by the time step gives the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>.

= Free-particle propagator
{parent=Quantum-mechanical propagator}

For a free particle of mass $m$ on the line,
$$
K(x_f,T;x_i,0)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}
\exp\!\left(\frac{im(x_f-x_i)^2}{2\hbar T}\right).
$$

= Semiclassical propagator
{parent=Quantum-mechanical propagator}

A semiclassical propagator sums $e^{iS_{\rm cl}/\hbar}$ over classical paths joining its endpoints, weighted by their quadratic fluctuation determinants and phase indices. It is exact for quadratic actions.

= Dirichlet fluctuation determinant of a semiclassical propagator
{c}
{parent=Semiclassical propagator}
{title2=$\mathcal O_c=-m\,d^2/dt^2-V^{\prime\prime}(q_c(t))$}

Expanding a <configuration-space path integral> about a classical trajectory $q_c$ with fixed endpoints gives $S[q_c+f]=S[q_c]+\tfrac12\int f\mathcal O_cf\,dt+O(f^3)$, where $f$ obeys homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. The <Gaussian functional integral> contributes $(\det\mathcal O_c)^{-1/2}$ with the phase prescribed by continuation of the <quantum-mechanical propagator>. Relative to the free operator $\mathcal O_0=-m\,d^2/dt^2$, the prefactor is $D_0(T)[\det\mathcal O_c/\det\mathcal O_0]^{-1/2}$. In a general potential the operator, and thus the prefactor, depends on the endpoints through $q_c$. For a quadratic potential $V^{\prime\prime}$ is constant and all higher fluctuation terms vanish, so the approximation is exact away from singular focusing times. At a zero mode the simple determinant formula requires a limiting prescription or a different treatment of the zero mode.

= Time-reversal symmetry in quantum mechanics
{parent=Unitary time evolution}

A time-reversal operator $T$ intertwines forward and backward evolution:
$$
U(t)T=TU(-t).
$$
It must be antiunitary in ordinary quantum mechanics. Its conjugate linearity sends $i$ to $-i$, allowing it to commute with a time-reversal-invariant Hamiltonian rather than reversing the sign of its energy.

= Quantum time-reversal operator
{parent=Time-reversal symmetry in quantum mechanics}
{title2=$\hat T$}

A quantum time-reversal operator is an <antiunitary operator> reversing spatial <momentum> and <angular momentum>. For a time-reversal-invariant <Hamiltonian operator>, its conjugation of $i$ makes it intertwine forward and backward <unitary time evolution> without reversing the <energy> spectrum. A scalar <momentum eigenstate> may transform as $\hat T|\boldsymbol p\rangle=e^{i\chi(\boldsymbol p)}|-\boldsymbol p\rangle$. With unit-modulus phases and a complete normalized basis, expansion of arbitrary states proves <antiunitarity>.

= Unitary time reversal reverses the energy spectrum
{parent=Time-reversal symmetry in quantum mechanics}

If a unitary $T$ satisfied $U(t)T=TU(-t)$, differentiation at zero would give
$$
HT=-TH.
$$
It would map every energy $E$ to $-E$. A Hamiltonian unbounded above would consequently be unbounded below and have no stable ground state.

= Antiunitary time reversal preserves the energy spectrum
{parent=Time-reversal symmetry in quantum mechanics}

For antiunitary $T$, conjugate linearity gives $T(i\psi)=-iT\psi$. Differentiating
$$
U(t)T=TU(-t)
$$
therefore yields $HT=TH$. Time reversal maps an energy eigenspace to itself and creates no negative-energy instability.

{parent=Quantum mechanics}

= Wavefunction
{synonym}

A <wavefunction> is the position representation of a <pure state>. In the one-dimensional model used here, $\psi(x,t)$ takes values in the <complex numbers>, and the <Born rule> gives
$$
\rho(x,t)=|\psi(x,t)|^2,\qquad \Pr(a<x<b)=\int_a^b\rho(x,t)\,dx.
$$
The <probability density> $\rho$ has units of inverse length; the <wavefunction> has units of inverse square root of length. A value of $\rho$ at a point is not itself a probability: probabilities come from an <integral> over a region. For the <infinite square well> <ground state>, $\rho(L/2)=2/L$, whereas the probability of the exact single point $L/2$ is zero. The interesting question is how much probability a finite interval contains.

Two <wavefunctions> differing only by a <global phase> represent the same <pure state>. Relative <quantum phases> between components of a <quantum superposition> affect <matter-wave interference>, so retaining the full <complex number> amplitude is essential. This scalar description covers a particle without active <spin> degrees of freedom; a general <electron> <wavefunction> can have two <spin> components, with the position <probability density> obtained by adding the squared <modulus> of each component.

= Wavefunction normalization
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\int|\psi|^2\,dx=1$}

Wavefunction normalization makes the total <Born rule> probability equal to one. In an <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates>, $\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n$ is normalized precisely when $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$.

For a proposed <infinite square well> mode $A\sin(n\pi x/L)$, the <integral> of its squared <modulus> is $|A|^2L/2$. Thus <wavefunction normalization> requires $|A|=\sqrt{2/L}$; choosing $A$ real and positive fixes a convenient <global phase>. For the equal <quantum superposition> $(\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt2$, <orthogonality> makes the integrated cross term zero, leaving $1/2+1/2=1$. The cross term can nevertheless be nonzero locally: normalization constrains total probability, not the shape of its <probability density>.

= de Broglie wavelength
{title2=$\lambda=h/p$}
{parent=Matter wave}
{c}
{wiki}

The <de Broglie wavelength> is the distance over which a <matter wave> accumulates one full spatial <quantum phase> cycle in its propagation direction:
$$
\lambda=\frac{h}{|\mathbf p|}=\frac{2\pi}{|\mathbf k|},\qquad \mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k,\qquad \hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\mathbf p$ is the <momentum>, and $\mathbf k$ is the angular <wavevector>. The scalar $p$ in $\lambda=h/p$ means the positive <momentum> magnitude, not a signed component. Reversing a <momentum> reverses the <wavevector> without making the <wavelength> negative. At zero <momentum> there is no finite spatial phase period: the limiting <wavelength> diverges.

This relation makes microscopic <diffraction> accessible with particles: increasing the <momentum> shortens the <wavelength> until it is comparable with the spacings in a <crystal scattering> problem. The <electron accelerating-voltage wavelength> gives a practical way to tune this scale, while the <Davisson–Germer experiment> tests it by comparing <electron diffraction> directions with independently known crystal geometry. The same relation applies to neutral particles in a <neutron interferometer>; <electric charge> is not a requirement for a <matter wave>.

The <wavelength> belongs to the <wavefunction>, not to a sinusoidal path of a particle. A <free-particle momentum convention> makes the distinction especially clear: a <plane wave> has a rotating complex <quantum phase> but a constant <probability density>. A localized <wave packet> contains a range of <momenta> and therefore generally has a range of <wavelengths>, rather than one exact spatial period.

= Free-particle momentum convention
{parent=de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$\psi=Ae^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)},\quad\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$}

For a <free particle> of <mass> $m>0$ in <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>, choose the <potential energy> zero and write
$$
\psi(\mathbf r,t)=A\exp\!\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r-Et)\right],\qquad E=\frac{|\mathbf p|^2}{2m},\qquad \omega=\frac{E}{\hbar}.
$$
Applying the <momentum operator> $-i\hbar\nabla$ returns $\mathbf p\psi$; this fixes the sign convention. The <angular frequency> is measured relative to this chosen <energy> zero. Adding a constant <energy> changes only a time-dependent <global phase>, leaving the spatial <de Broglie wavelength> unchanged. No rest-energy term is included in this <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics> convention.

The <plane wave> has $|\psi|^2=|A|^2$ and <probability current> $\mathbf j=(\mathbf p/m)|A|^2$. Its real part can be drawn as a <sine> or <cosine>, but that graph is a component of a <probability amplitude>, not a transverse displacement. On infinite space this is a generalized <momentum eigenstate>, not a state satisfying <wavefunction normalization>. A narrow <wave packet> instead moves with <group velocity> $\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega=\mathbf p/m$; the <phase velocity> $\omega/|\mathbf k|=|\mathbf p|/(2m)$ is not its transport speed.

The equality of <canonical momentum> and <kinetic momentum> here assumes a field-free region with the <magnetic vector potential> chosen zero. For a charged particle in a <magnetic field>, the <kinetic momentum> is $\boldsymbol\Pi=\mathbf p-q\mathbf A$. An arbitrary canonical phase gradient must not then be substituted into $m\mathbf v$; the <free particle> formula needs its stated assumptions.

= Electron accelerating-voltage wavelength
{parent=de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_e eU}$}

Let an <electron> start with negligible <kinetic energy> and move through an increase $U>0$ in <electric potential>. Its <electric charge> is $q=-e$, where $e>0$ is the <elementary charge>, so the decrease in <potential energy> is $eU$. In a field-free region after acceleration,
$$
K=eU,\qquad |\mathbf p|=\sqrt{2m_e eU},\qquad \lambda=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eU}}\simeq\frac{1.2264\,\mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{U/(1\,\mathrm V)}}.
$$
This is an <electron> formula, not a universal conversion from <voltage> to <wavelength>. It assumes negligible initial <kinetic energy>, negligible losses, and $eU\ll m_ec^2$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>. It describes the <de Broglie wavelength> outside the crystal; the <potential energy> inside a material can change the local <kinetic momentum>.

For $U=54\,\mathrm V$, take $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, and $m_e\simeq9.1093837\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. The rounded <electron> <mass> is consistent with the 2022 recommended constants.<CODATA Value: electron mass by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[CODATA electron mass: numerical value and 2022 adjustment label]{ref}
$$
K=8.65175\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm J=54\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
|\mathbf p|\simeq3.97019\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\lambda\simeq1.66895\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m=0.166895\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
The <electronvolt> is an <energy> unit: $54\,\mathrm{eV}$ must be converted to <joules> before combining it with $m_e$ in SI units. <Dimensional analysis> checks both steps:
$$
[eU]=\mathrm{C\,V}=\mathrm J,\qquad [\sqrt{2m_e eU}]=\sqrt{\mathrm{kg\,J}}=\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}},\qquad [h/p]=\frac{\mathrm{J\,s}}{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}}=\mathrm m.

The resulting <velocity> is about $4.3584\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, or $0.01454c$, so the nonrelativistic assumption is consistent. For a larger accelerating <voltage>, the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> gives $pc=\sqrt{K(K+2m_ec^2)}$, with $K=eU$, while $\lambda=h/p$ still holds. At $54\,\mathrm V$ the relativistic <wavelength> is shorter by about $0.00264\%$.

= Probability amplitude
{parent=Wave function}
{wiki}
{title2=$a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle$}

A probability amplitude is a <complex number> that describes the contribution of a <pure state> to a specified measurement outcome. For a normalized <quantum state> $|\psi\rangle$ and a rank-one outcome associated with a normalized vector $|j\rangle$, it is the <inner product> $a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle$. The <Born rule> gives the corresponding <probability> $p_j=|a_j|^2=a_j^*a_j$, using the <complex conjugate>. Thus an amplitude can be negative or complex, while a <probability> is real and nonnegative. A <wavefunction> is the analogous position amplitude: its squared <modulus> gives a <probability density>, which must be integrated over a region to obtain a <probability>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-04-quantum-physics-i-spring-2016/d87c768c4e1181fde8d4366112169719_MIT8_04S16_LecNotes6.pdf[Zwiebach, MIT 8.04, lecture 6, section 2, pp. 3–4]{ref}.

The extra information is the <quantum phase>. If $a=re^{i\theta}$, its <modulus> $r$ fixes $|a|^2=r^2$, but its angle $\theta$ controls how it adds to another contribution. In the <complex plane>, picture amplitudes as arrows: add the arrows, then square the resulting length. For coherent contributions $A$ and $B$ to the same outcome,
$$
|A+B|^2=|A|^2+|B|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(A^*B).
$$
The last term is <interference>. Adding <probabilities> first loses it. Contributions with different relative <quantum phases> can reinforce or cancel even when their individual squared <moduli> are fixed.

For the <qubit> <quantum superposition> $|\psi_\phi\rangle=(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle+(e^{i\phi}/2)|1\rangle$, project onto the plus vector of the <Hadamard basis>. By linearity, the two contributions combine as
$$
\langle+|\psi_\phi\rangle
=\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2}}_{A}
+\underbrace{\frac{e^{i\phi}}{2\sqrt2}}_{B}.
$$
Here $|A|^2=3/8$ and $|B|^2=1/8$, while the <interference> term is $(\sqrt3/4)\cos\phi$. At $\phi=0$, the arrows align and the plus <probability> is $1/2+\sqrt3/4\simeq0.9330127$. At $\phi=\pi$, they oppose and that <probability> is $1/2-\sqrt3/4\simeq0.0669873$; cancellation is incomplete because the lengths differ. At $\phi=\pi/2$, the arrows are perpendicular and the cross term vanishes. The minus amplitude uses $A-B$, reversing the cross term. These are alternate <projective measurements> on identically prepared copies, rather than a sequence of measurements on one copy.

\Image[quantum-relative-phase-interference.png]
{title=Relative quantum phase redistributes Hadamard-basis probabilities; a diagonal mixture stays at one half}

A <global phase> multiplies every contribution by the same factor $e^{i\gamma}$, giving
$$
|\langle j|e^{i\gamma}\psi\rangle|^2
=|e^{i\gamma}\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2
=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2.
$$
It therefore changes no outcome <probability>. Changing a relative <quantum phase> can change the angle between contributions and hence their <interference>. Keeping amplitudes until the last step makes <quantum circuits> and <matter-wave interference> calculable; discarding their angles prematurely discards physically testable information. The <Born rule> example compares two measurement bases explicitly.

= Born rule
{parent=Probability amplitude}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_j=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2$}

The Born rule connects a <quantum state> to measurement <probabilities>. Let $\{|j\rangle\}$ be a complete <orthonormal basis>, finite or countably infinite, for a <Hilbert space>, and let
$$
|\psi\rangle=\sum_j a_j|j\rangle,
\qquad
a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle,
\qquad
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.
$$
An ideal measurement distinguishing these basis vectors returns label $j$ with <probability>
$$
p_j=|a_j|^2=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2.
$$
This assignment is a physical measurement rule. Its compatibility with total <probability> one follows from <orthonormality> and normalization:
$$
\sum_jp_j=\sum_j|a_j|^2
=\sum_{j,k}a_j^*a_k\langle j|k\rangle
=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.
$$
The <probabilities> are the squared <moduli>, not the amplitudes themselves or the squares $a_j^2$. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/quantum-information[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Quantum information, Measuring quantum states]{ref}.

If the basis is an <eigenbasis> of an <observable>, the reported numerical value is its <eigenvalue>. Several vectors can share one <eigenvalue>; the <probability> of that value is then the sum over the corresponding basis labels. More generally, a <projective measurement> with <orthogonal projections> $P_r$ assigns
$$
p_r=\langle\psi|P_r|\psi\rangle=\|P_r|\psi\rangle\|^2,
\qquad
p_r=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P_r)
$$
for a <pure state> and a general <density matrix> $\rho$, respectively. This also covers <mixed states> and degenerate <eigenspaces>. The <Born rule> determines outcome statistics; the ideal conditional state change is specified separately by the <Lüders rule>. See <projective measurement> for that formulation and its assumptions. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/general-formulation-of-quantum-information/general-measurements/formulations-of-measurements[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Mathematical formulations of measurements, Projective measurements]{ref}.

For a worked example, take the normalized <qubit> <quantum state>
$$
|\psi_\phi\rangle=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle+\frac{e^{i\phi}}2|1\rangle.
$$
The squared <moduli> add to $3/4+1/4=1$. A <computational-basis measurement>, equivalently a measurement of the $Z$ <Pauli matrix>, has
$$
p_0=\left|\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right|^2=\frac34,
\qquad
p_1=\left|\frac{e^{i\phi}}2\right|^2=\frac14.
$$
These <probabilities> do not depend on the relative <quantum phase> $\phi$. Now choose the <Hadamard basis>
$$
|\pm\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},
$$
whose vectors are <eigenstates> of the $X$ <Pauli matrix>, also used by the <Pauli X gate>. The new <probability amplitudes> and <probabilities> are
$$
b_\pm=\langle\pm|\psi_\phi\rangle
=\frac{\sqrt3\pm e^{i\phi}}{2\sqrt2},
\qquad
p_\pm=|b_\pm|^2
=\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\cos\phi.
$$
Both <probabilities> are nonnegative and their sum is one. The relative <quantum phase> becomes visible because the two <probability amplitudes> add before their squared <modulus> is taken:
* At $\phi=0$, $p_+=(2+\sqrt3)/4\simeq0.9330127$ and $p_-\simeq0.0669873$.
* At $\phi=\pi/2$, $p_+=p_-=1/2$.
* At $\phi=\pi$, $p_+\simeq0.0669873$ and $p_-\simeq0.9330127$.

\Image[born-rule-basis-probabilities.png]
{title=The same qubit preparation has phase-independent Z probabilities and phase-sensitive X probabilities}

A <Hadamard gate> followed by a <computational-basis measurement> realizes these $X$ statistics, since $H|\pm\rangle=|0\rangle,|1\rangle$, respectively. Compare the two bases using fresh, identically prepared copies: a preceding measurement may change the state. The <quantum superposition> section shows why replacing the preparation by the <mixed state> $\operatorname{diag}(3/4,1/4)$ keeps the $Z$ statistics but gives $p_+=p_-=1/2$ for every $\phi$.

The usefulness of the <Born rule> is this conversion from <linear algebra> to experimental predictions: calculate overlaps in the basis actually measured, then take squared <moduli>. Its appealing geometry is that changing basis redistributes probability while preserving total squared <norm>. The same rule relates an <energy eigenstate> expansion to an <energy measurement> and a position <wavefunction> to a <probability density>.

Born's 1926 paper on <quantum scattering> interpreted the outgoing-wave coefficients in terms of the <probabilities> of collision outcomes; a corrective footnote specified proportionality to the square of the amplitude. This is a historical scattering formulation, rather than the general <density matrix> formula above. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01397477[Born, Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge, Zeitschrift für Physik 37, 863–867, 1926]{ref}; https://www.neo-classical-physics.info/uploads/3/0/6/5/3065888/born_-_qm_for_collisions_i.pdf[English translation of Born's paper, p. 3 and footnote 1]{ref}.

= Two-level quantum return probability
{parent=Born rule}

A normalized <quantum state> $\chi=\sqrt q\,\psi_1+e^{i\eta}\sqrt{1-q}\,\psi_2$ in two orthonormal <energy eigenstates> returns to its original one-dimensional measurement subspace with probability
$$
|\langle\chi,e^{-iHt/\hbar}\chi\rangle|^2=q^2+(1-q)^2+2q(1-q)\cos((E_1-E_2)t/\hbar).
$$
This follows by applying <unitary time evolution> to each <energy eigenstate>, then the <Born rule>. The relative phase $\eta$ cancels from the return amplitude. Degenerate energies make the probability identically one.

= Two-level observable transition probability
{parent=Born rule}
{title2=$P_{-\to+}(t)=\sin^2((E_2-E_1)t/(2\hbar))$}

For two orthonormal <energy eigenstates> $\psi_1,\psi_2$, let an <observable> have distinct nondegenerate <eigenstates> $\phi_\pm=(\psi_1\pm\psi_2)/\sqrt2$. Starting in $\phi_-$, <unitary time evolution> gives $(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\psi_1-e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\psi_2)/\sqrt2$. Its <probability amplitude> along $\phi_+$ is $(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}-e^{-iE_2t/\hbar})/2$. The <Born rule> therefore gives the stated transition probability. The energy difference determines the oscillation frequency, while a common energy shift has no effect.

= Quantum tunnelling
{parent=Potential barrier}
{wiki}

Quantum tunnelling is transmission through a <potential barrier> even when the particle energy is below the barrier height.

The <wavefunction> penetrates the classically inaccessible region; matching it to an outgoing <scattering state> on the far side gives a nonzero <transmission probability>. For a real, time-independent <potential barrier>, the transmitted <energy> equals the incident <energy>: <quantum tunnelling> does not require a temporary violation of <conservation of energy>. A <tunnel junction> turns this small <transmission probability> into a measurable <electric current>, whose sensitivity to separation makes the <scanning tunnelling microscope> possible.

= Finite rectangular barrier matching
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}

Take a <nonrelativistic particle> of constant <mass> $m$ with <energy> $E>0$, and choose the same zero of <potential energy> on both sides of a <rectangular potential barrier>:
$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}V_0,&0<x<a,\\0,&x<0\text{ or }x>a,\end{cases}
\qquad V_0>0,\quad a>0.
$$
The equality of the exterior <potential energies> matters: it gives the same exterior <wavenumber> $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and makes $T=|t|^2$. Here $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. This model has a finite jump in <potential energy>, no <delta potential>, and no absorbing term.

For $0<E<V_0$, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> gives the spatial <wavefunction>
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}
e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx},&x<0,\\
Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x},&0<x<a,\\
te^{ikx},&x>a,
\end{cases}
\qquad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}.
$$
The full <stationary state> is $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. We set the incident <probability amplitude> to one and impose no incoming wave from the right; this <scattering wavefunction> is not a <normalizable wavefunction>. The <tunnelling decay constant> $\kappa$ describes <exponential decay>, rather than an oscillatory <wavenumber> in the barrier. Both <exponential functions> are needed on the finite interval: the growing term is not excluded by a condition at infinity.

The <wavefunction> and its <derivative> are <continuous> at each face, giving four linear matching conditions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
1+r&=A+B,&ik(1-r)&=\kappa(A-B),\\
Ae^{\kappa a}+Be^{-\kappa a}&=te^{ika},&
\kappa(Ae^{\kappa a}-Be^{-\kappa a})&=ikte^{ika}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Their solution supplies the <transmission amplitude>
$$
t=\frac{e^{-ika}}{\cosh(\kappa a)+i\frac{\kappa^2-k^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)}.
$$
A short derivation is to use the two right-face equations to express $Ae^{\kappa a}$ and $Be^{-\kappa a}$ in terms of $t$, then substitute into the left-face equations. Taking the squared <modulus> gives the <rectangular barrier transmission probability>.

= Tunnelling decay constant
{parent=Finite rectangular barrier matching}
{title2=$\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$}

The <tunnelling decay constant> is the inverse length controlling the <exponential decay> of an <evanescent wave> in a constant <potential barrier> with $E<V_0$. The <wavefunction> amplitude decay length is $1/\kappa$; the squared-amplitude decay length is $1/(2\kappa)$.

= Rectangular barrier transmission probability
{parent=Finite rectangular barrier matching}
{title2=$T(E,a)$}

For equal exterior <potential energies> and constant <mass>, the exact <transmission probability> is
$$
T(E,a)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1},&0<E<V_0,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{mV_0a^2}{2\hbar^2}\right]^{-1},&E=V_0,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(E-V_0)}\sin^2(qa)\right]^{-1},&E>V_0,
\end{cases}
\qquad q=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}.
$$
Below the barrier, the <hyperbolic sine> is positive for $a>0$, so $0<T<1$. At $E=V_0$, the interior <wavefunction> is linear in $x$; either neighbouring formula has the displayed finite <limit>, since $\sinh z/z$ and $\sin z/z$ tend to one. Above the barrier, the interior <wavefunction> oscillates: the <quantum reflection probability> generally remains nonzero, but $qa=n\pi$ for a positive integer $n$ gives perfect <transmission probability> $T=1$ through constructive <wave interference>. These are <scattering resonances>, not below-barrier <quantum tunnelling>.

At half height, $E=V_0/2$, we have $k=\kappa$ and $T=\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa a)$, consistent with <finite square barrier transmission at half barrier height> after replacing this width $a$ by that example's width $2a$. In the illustration, the width is four times $\ell=\hbar/\sqrt{2mV_0}$ and $E/V_0=1/2$, giving $T\simeq0.01388$. The plotted <wavefunction> comes from solving the four interface equations; the <transmission probability> curve uses the closed formula.

= Opaque barrier approximation
{parent=Rectangular barrier transmission probability}
{title2=$T\sim\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}$}

For fixed $0<E<V_0$ and $\kappa a\gg1$, the exact <rectangular barrier transmission probability> gives
$$
T\sim\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{2a\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}\right].
$$
This is the leading term of an <asymptotic expansion>: the <wavefunction> amplitude scale is $e^{-\kappa a}$, and the <transmission probability> scale is $e^{-2\kappa a}$, with an interface prefactor. Increasing width by $\Delta a$ multiplies this approximation by $e^{-2\kappa\Delta a}$. At fixed incident <energy>, increasing barrier height increases the <tunnelling decay constant> as $\sqrt{V_0-E}$; heavier <nonrelativistic particles> also have stronger suppression. For the numerical example in <rectangular barrier transmission probability>, the approximation gives $0.01397$, compared with the exact $0.01388$.

The regime condition matters. Near $E=V_0$ at fixed width, $\kappa a$ tends to zero and the <opaque barrier approximation> fails; use the exact threshold value. For $\kappa a\ll1$, expansion of the <hyperbolic sine> gives
$$
T\simeq\left[1+\frac{mV_0^2a^2}{2\hbar^2E}\right]^{-1}.
$$
This tends to one as $a\to0$ at fixed $E,V_0$, but the condition $\kappa a\ll1$ alone does not imply nearly perfect transmission if the remaining factor is large. At $E>V_0$, use the oscillatory formula and its <scattering resonances>, rather than an <exponential decay> law.

= Evanescent probability current
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}
{title2=$J=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$}

A nonzero <probability density> inside a barrier does not by itself imply transport. In one dimension, the <probability current> of a <wavefunction> is $J=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$. A single <evanescent wave> $Ce^{-\kappa x}$ has $\psi^*\psi'=-\kappa|C|^2e^{-2\kappa x}$, a real quantity, hence $J=0$. This is what happens on the inaccessible side of a <potential barrier> extending to infinity with $E<V_0$.<Quantum Physics I (8.04), Spring 2016/Lecture 6>[p. 5, equations (3.4)-(3.6): probability continuity equation and probability current]{ref}

For the finite-barrier <wavefunction> $Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x}$, the cross terms instead give
$$
J=\frac{2\hbar\kappa}{m}\operatorname{Im}(B^*A).
$$
The relative <quantum phase> of the two <probability amplitudes> permits a nonzero, spatially constant <probability current>, even though neither <exponential function> separately carries it. The <probability continuity equation> gives $\partial_xJ=0$ for this <stationary state>. Matching the <wavefunction> and its <derivative> also makes $J$ continuous at both faces, so
$$
J_{\mathrm{inc}}=\frac{\hbar k}{m},\qquad
J_{\mathrm{ref}}=-\frac{\hbar k}{m}|r|^2,\qquad
J_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|t|^2,\qquad
R+T=1.
$$
Here $R=|r|^2$ is the <quantum reflection probability>, and $T=J_{\mathrm{tr}}/J_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is the <transmission probability>. The net exterior <probability current> equals the interior <probability current>; it is not lost as the <wavefunction> decreases. A physical incident <wave packet> supplies finite total probability, while the <stationary state> describes the <energy> component used to compute its transmitted flux.

= Current-distance test of vacuum tunnelling
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}

<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others> made barrier width experimentally adjustable. Their apparatus used a pointed <electrical conductor> opposite a conducting plate, with fine positioning by <piezoelectricity>. Rubber-tube support and magnetic levitation above a cooled bowl exhibiting <superconductivity> suppressed vibrations; the junction itself remained near room temperature.<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others>[p. 178, Fig. 1: tungsten tip, platinum plate, piezodrive and helium-cooled superconducting lead bowl]{ref}

The readout was <electrical resistance> versus displacement at a fixed <electric potential difference> of 60 mV of either polarity. They observed an <exponential function> dependence spanning four orders of magnitude. Cleaning and better <vacuum> increased the inferred barrier from approximately 0.6-0.7 eV to 3.2 eV. Reproducible curves and the cleaning response argued against deforming contamination layers; an appropriate slope also helped exclude an ordinary conducting contact. The paper reports residual contamination, electronics-limited range and a jump attributed to release of thermal stress; its curve origins generally do not determine absolute gap widths.<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others>[p. 179, equation (1), Fig. 2 and accompanying control/limitation discussion]{ref}

The ideal <tunnel junction> model explains the slope. At fixed small <electric potential difference>, fixed tip geometry and approximately unchanged <density of states>, take an effective barrier <energy> $\Phi$ above the relevant <electron> states and approximate the current magnitude by
$$
I(s)\simeq C\exp(-2\kappa s),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}}{\hbar},\qquad
-\frac{d\ln I}{ds}\simeq2\kappa.
$$
The coefficient $C$ includes the applied <electric potential difference>, available occupied and empty <quantum states>, and tip geometry. A single <transmission probability> is not an <electric current>: a junction combines contributions from many <electron> states in both directions, with the <electric potential difference> producing a net imbalance. Its tilted, material-dependent barrier is only approximated by the equal-exterior <rectangular potential barrier>; the exact single-particle formula above is not a complete junction model.

For the chosen model value $\Phi=4$ <electronvolts>, $\kappa\simeq10.25\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}$. Increasing separation by $0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ multiplies $I$ by about $0.129$, a reduction by about $7.8$. = Attractive potential ordering does not order quantum reflection
{parent=Finite square well}

For a square well of width $L$, exterior <wavenumber> $k$ and interior <wavenumber> $q>k$, matching the <wavefunction> and its <derivative> gives the <reflection probability> $R=C\sin^2(qL)/(1+C\sin^2(qL))$, where $C=(q^2-k^2)^2/(4k^2q^2)$. For example $q=3k$ and $kL=\pi/2$ give $R=16/25$, whereas zero potential gives $R=0$. Moreover deeper wells at $qL\in\pi\mathbb Z$ have zero reflection by <resonant transmission through a square well>. Reflection therefore cannot be ordered solely by pointwise ordering of attractive potentials.

= Spherical square well
{parent=Finite square well}

A <spherical square well> is a central potential equal to a negative constant inside a sphere and zero outside it. = Spherically symmetric bound state of a finite well
{parent=Spherical square well}
{title2=$U>\pi^2\hbar^2/(8ma^2)$}

For well depth $U$ and radius $a$, let $u=r\psi$ for a regular spherically symmetric <wavefunction>. At energy $-U<E<0$, the interior is $u=C\sin(kr)$ and the exterior is proportional to $e^{-\kappa r}$, with $k^2=2m(U+E)/\hbar^2$ and $\kappa^2=-2mE/\hbar^2$. Matching gives $k\cot(ka)=-\kappa$. The first negative-energy solution exists strictly above the displayed threshold. At equality the exterior zero-energy wave behaves as $1/r$ and is not square-integrable, so the threshold is not itself a bound state.

= Bound-state thresholds for a square well with one hard wall
{parent=Finite square well}
{title2=$\mathcal U_j=\hbar^2\pi^2(j+1/2)^2/(2ma^2)$}

At equality the new threshold solution is not <normalizable>, so exactly one bound state corresponds to $U_0\in(\mathcal U_0,\mathcal U_1]$.

= Delta potential
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A delta potential is a point interaction imposing continuity of the wavefunction and a jump in its derivative.

= Central delta barrier in a symmetric infinite well
{parent=Delta potential}
{title2=$\tan(\lambda a)=-\hbar^2\lambda/(m\kappa)$}

Inside walls at $\pm a$, a central <delta potential> of strength $\kappa$ leaves odd <parity operator> eigenstates unchanged because their wavefunctions vanish at zero. For even states, continuity and the derivative jump $\psi'(0+)-\psi'(0-)=2m\kappa\psi(0)/\hbar^2$ give the displayed quantization equation. For $\kappa>0$, one root lies between $(n+1/2)\pi/a$ and $(n+1)\pi/a$ for every $n\ge0$.

= High-energy shift from a central delta barrier
{parent=Central delta barrier in a symmetric infinite well}
{title2=$E_n-E_n^{(0)}\to\kappa/a$}

Put $c=m\kappa/\hbar^2$, $k_n=(n+1/2)\pi/a$, and $\delta_n=a(\lambda_n-k_n)$. The quantization equation gives $\delta_n=\arctan(c/\lambda_n)$, so $\lambda_n\delta_n\to c$ and $\delta_n\to0$. Therefore $\lambda_n^2-k_n^2\to2c/a$, proving the displayed absolute <energy> shift. Its ratio to the unperturbed energy tends to zero. The normalized unperturbed even state has probability density $1/a$ at the origin, so its first-order delta-barrier expectation is also $\kappa/a$.

= Odd bound state and resonance of two delta barriers
{parent=Delta potential}
{title2=$2\kappa=-U_0(1-e^{-2\kappa a})$}

For equal delta potentials at $\pm a$, odd parity makes the interior wave proportional to $\sin(kx)$. The derivative jump gives the odd-channel denominator $k\cot(ka)+U_0-ik$. At $k=i\kappa$, its zero is the displayed equation. The function $2\kappa/(1-e^{-2\kappa a})$ increases strictly from $1/a$, proving exactly one odd bound state when $U_0a<-1$. For $U_0a=u\gg1$, the outgoing pole near $ka=\pi$ is $\pi-\pi/u+(\pi-i\pi^2)/u^2+O(u^{-3})$, with positive resonance width.

= Odd bound state of a symmetric double-delta potential

= Odd resonance of a strongly repulsive symmetric double-delta potential
{parent=Odd-parity S-matrix of a symmetric double-delta potential}

For $U_0a\gg1$, the lowest odd <scattering resonance> pole lies near the first odd mode trapped between the barriers:
$$
k=\frac\pi a\left(1-\frac1{U_0a}+O((U_0a)^{-2})\right)
-i\frac{\pi^2}{U_0^2a^3}\left(1+O((U_0a)^{-1})\right)

in its real part and to leading nonzero order in its imaginary part. Writing $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)=E_R-i\Gamma/2$, the survival probability decays as $e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$.

= Scattering by a delta potential
{parent=Delta potential}

For $V(x)=V_0\delta(x)$, $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, and $\gamma=mV_0/(\hbar^2k)$, continuity and the derivative jump give

Quantum scattering compares a wavefunction's incoming and outgoing asymptotic components.

= Quantum reflection probability
{parent=Quantum scattering}
{title2=$R=|r|^2$}

= Reflection probability
{synonym}

For one-dimensional scattering from a real potential, write the left exterior <wavefunction> as $e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}$ with $k>0$. The reflected-to-incident probability-current ratio is $R=|r|^2$, since both components have the same speed and their currents have opposite signs. This probability differs from the complex reflection amplitude $r$. With transmission into a right exterior <wavenumber> $q$, the <transmission probability> is $T=(q/k)|t|^2$ and conservation of current gives $R+T=1$ when there is no absorption.

For one-dimensional <quantum scattering>, $t$ is the complex amplitude of the outgoing transmitted wave relative to the incoming wave. The <transmission probability> is $|t|^2$ times the ratio of transmitted and incident flux velocities; with equal asymptotic dispersion it is $|t|^2$. A <reflectionless potential> has $r=0$ and a unit-modulus transmission amplitude whose phase shifts continuum quantization. The <supersymmetric factorization of the one-soliton potential> gives $t(k)=(k+im)/(k-im)$ for its single-bound-state potential.

= Unit-modulus hyperbolic scattering block
{parent=S-matrix}
{title2=$S_u(\theta)=\sinh[(\theta+iu)/2]/\sinh[(\theta-iu)/2]$}

For real $u$, this scalar <S-matrix> block satisfies $S_u(\theta)S_u(-\theta)=1$ and <Hermitian analyticity of a two-particle S-matrix>. It therefore has modulus one for real <rapidity> differences. For $0<u<\pi$ its <pole> at $\theta=iu$ has <residue> $2i\sin u$ and admits a direct-channel <bound-state pole> interpretation. Its crossed block is $\cosh[(\theta-iu)/2]/\cosh[(\theta+iu)/2]$. The endpoints $u=0$ and $u=\pi$ are removable degeneracies giving constant amplitudes, so a particle cannot be inferred there from the uncancelled denominator alone. This block specifies an amplitude; a complete theory must also obey the remaining <crossing symmetry> and <bootstrap fusion> constraints.

= Physical rapidity strip
{parent=S-matrix}

For relativistic two-body scattering in one spatial dimension, this strip is the standard domain containing bound-state and crossed-channel <poles>. A stable-particle interpretation requires appropriate <pole> kinematics and residues; not every singularity automatically represents a new particle. Crossing relates <rapidity> $\vartheta$ to $i\pi-\vartheta$.

= Crossed-channel pole in diagonal factorized scattering
{parent=Physical rapidity strip}
{title2=$t(i\alpha)=m_a^2+m_b^2-2m_am_b\cos\alpha$}

A physical-strip <pole> of a diagonal <S-matrix> can come from exchange of an existing particle in the crossed channel. For external <masses> $m_a,m_b$, use the momentum difference, rather than the sum, to identify the exchanged <mass> through the displayed invariant. <Crossing symmetry> turns the pole at $i\alpha$ into a direct pole at $i(\pi-\alpha)$ of the particle-antiparticle channel. = Relativistic bound-state mass from a rapidity pole

where $r$ and $t$ are the reflection and transmission amplitudes. Probability-current conservation makes $S$ unitary for a real potential.

For a <parity>[parity-symmetric] one-dimensional potential, the even and odd parity states diagonalize the <one-dimensional S-matrix>:

S_{+-}=S_{-+}=0,
\qquad

For a real potential, <unitary operator>[unitarity] further gives $|S_{++}|=|S_{--}|=1$ at real positive energy.

\begin{pmatrix}A_R\\B_R\end{pmatrix}

For elastic scattering by a real central potential, the phase shift $\delta_l(k)$ measures the phase difference between the asymptotic radial wave in angular-momentum channel $l$ and the corresponding free wave. Its channel <partial-wave S-matrix> is $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$.

For a reflectionless <scattering wavefunction> whose two asymptotic phases differ by $\delta(k)$, a large periodic box gives $k_nL+\delta(k_n)=2\pi n$. An S wave is the rotationally invariant angular-momentum channel $l=0$. At sufficiently low energy it normally dominates short-range scattering because higher partial waves are suppressed by their centrifugal barriers.

= Partial-wave expansion of a scattering amplitude

For a central potential and the convention $S_l=1+2if_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$, the <scattering amplitude> is
$$
f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)f_lP_l(\cos\theta),
\qquad

$$
The identity $|S_l|=1$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{Im}f_l=|f_l|^2$.

At low momentum, the <S wave> normally supplies the leading term.

= Unitarity and reflection identities for a partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

For a real central potential and real $k$, radial-current conservation gives $S_l(k)^*S_l(k)=1$. Invariance of the radial equation under $k\mapsto-k$ gives $S_l(k)S_l(-k)=1$, so a continuous phase convention has $\delta_l(-k)=-\delta_l(k)$.

= Scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix

For a barrier of radius $a$ and potential $\hbar^2\gamma^2/(2m)$, matching the regular inner hyperbolic-sine wave to the outer sine wave gives $a_s=a-\tanh(\gamma a)/\gamma$. The low-energy $s$-wave <scattering amplitude> is $-a_s$.

= Zero-energy scattering resonance

A zero-energy scattering resonance occurs when an S-wave bound state reaches the continuum threshold. The zero-energy radial solution then approaches a nonzero constant rather than a generic linear function, and the <scattering length from a partial-wave S-matrix>[scattering length] diverges and changes sign.

= Bound-state poles of a partial-wave S-matrix
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

A pole at $k=i\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$ represents a normalizable bound state of energy $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$. Resonance poles instead lie away from the imaginary axis on the analytically continued unphysical sheet.

= Hyperbolic-tangent S-wave exterior solution
{c}
{parent=Bound-state poles of a partial-wave S-matrix}

For the exterior wave
$$
\psi(r)=\frac{e^{-ikr}}r
+\frac{k+i\lambda\tanh(\lambda r)}{k-i\lambda}
\frac{e^{ikr}}r,
$$
the partial-wave scattering matrix is
$$
S_0(k)=-\frac{k+i\lambda}{k-i\lambda}
=\frac{\lambda-ik}{\lambda+ik}.
$$
Thus $\tan\delta_0=-k/\lambda$, the scattering length is $1/\lambda$, and the pole at $k=i\lambda$ represents a bound state of energy $-\hbar^2\lambda^2/(2m)$ whose exterior wavefunction is proportional to $\operatorname{sech}(\lambda r)/r$.

= Scattering resonance
{parent=Partial-wave S-matrix}

A scattering resonance is a metastable state represented by a pole of the analytically continued scattering matrix. Near an isolated narrow resonance, a cross-section has a Breit-Wigner peak whose energy width is the inverse lifetime up to $\hbar$.

= Resonance pole
{parent=Scattering resonance}
{title2=$E=E_R-i\Gamma/2,\quad\Gamma>0$}

A pole of the continued <scattering matrix> associated with outgoing boundary conditions and an unstable trapped state. A pole below the real energy axis gives time dependence $e^{-iE_Rt/\hbar}e^{-\Gamma t/(2\hbar)}$, so the probability lifetime is $\hbar/\Gamma$. Bound-state poles instead lie on the positive imaginary wavenumber axis and produce square-integrable decaying spatial waves.

At an interface where a finite potential changes discontinuously, continuity of a radial wavefunction and its derivative is equivalent to matching $u'/u$ on the two sides. This removes the arbitrary normalization constants and gives an equation for the <scattering phase shift>.

The hard-sphere limit of a repulsive spherical potential makes penetration into a sphere of radius $a$ vanish. Its low-energy <S wave> scattering length tends to $a$, and its quantum total cross-section tends to $4\pi a^2$.

= Optical theorem
{parent=Scattering amplitude}

Write the <S-matrix> as $S=1+iT$. <Unitarity> gives $-i(T-T^\dagger)=T^\dagger T$, so the diagonal forward matrix element obeys $2\operatorname{Im}T_{ii}=\sum_f\int d\Phi_f\,|T_{fi}|^2$. = Electron diffraction
{parent=Diffraction}
{wiki}

<Electron diffraction> is <interference> between scattered <electron> <probability amplitudes>. It connects the <de Broglie wavelength> of the incident beam to the arrangement of the scattering material. In <elastic scattering> from a fixed crystal, $|\mathbf k_{\rm out}|=|\mathbf k_{\rm in}|=2\pi/\lambda$, and constructive <crystal scattering> selects <wavevector> transfers $\mathbf Q=\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}$ in the <reciprocal lattice>. The physical <momentum transfer> to the <electron> is $\hbar\mathbf Q$ in this convention.

<Bragg's law> expresses the same condition as $2d\sin\theta_B=n\lambda$, where $d$ is the spacing of the chosen lattice planes, $n$ the <diffraction order>, and $\theta_B$ the angle to those planes. The angle between incoming and outgoing propagation directions is $\chi=2\theta_B$ for that reflection geometry. In normal-incidence backscattering, a detector's angle $\phi$ from the outward surface normal instead gives $\chi=\pi-\phi$; neither detector colatitude nor angle to the surface normal should silently replace $\theta_B$. A <finite-row electron diffraction model> uses a different spacing, the spacing between sites along a surface row, and makes that distinction explicit.

= Davisson–Germer experiment
{parent=Electron diffraction}
{c}
{wiki}

The <Davisson–Germer experiment> turned <elastic scattering> from <nickel> into evidence for a <matter wave>. The search became a deliberate wave test in 1926, with <diffraction> beams found early in 1927. The earlier crystal investigation had begun in 1925; the <de Broglie wavelength> proposal was already present in the 1924 thesis.<The discovery of electron waves by Clinton J. Davisson>[The discovery of electron waves, printed p. 390: retrospective chronology and the change to a deliberate wave test]{ref}

A heated <tungsten> ribbon supplied <electrons>; gun apertures selected a narrow beam. Two $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ apertures were $8\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart; the gun ended $7\,\mathrm{mm}$ from a <nickel> crystal cut parallel to its $\{111\}$ planes. An adjustable <electric potential difference> set the speed. The beam struck normally inside an evacuated, field-free enclosure with target and outer electrodes at one potential. A double-box collector, $11\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the target with a $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ entrance, selected directions. A retarding <electric potential difference> rejected slower <electrons>; the inner collector fed a sensitive <galvanometer>. Moving the collector scanned colatitude; rotating the crystal scanned azimuth.<Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel by C. Davisson and L. H. Germer>[The apparatus, printed pp. 707–710, figures 2–4: source, apertures, potentials, collector and target preparation]{ref}

The readout was collected <electric current>, not a photograph of <electron> paths. Voltage scans held the gun-plate current constant because incident-beam current could not be held constant; the overall current trend therefore needed caution. A strong feature developed at $54\,\mathrm V$ near $50^\circ$ colatitude. Surface heating distinguished crystal beams from adsorbed-gas beams; finite apertures and background shifted or broadened apparent peaks.<Diffraction of Electrons by a Crystal of Nickel by C. Davisson and L. H. Germer>[Printed p. 705, abstract; pp. 715–719, figures 9–12: voltage-scan control, 54 V feature, background subtraction and instrumental widths]{ref}

At the bright beam's colatitude, an azimuth scan revealed three companion directions, matching crystal symmetry. The test compared <wavelengths> inferred from <diffraction> angles and known crystal spacings with <wavelengths> inferred from accelerating <voltage> and <momentum>; the 1937 lecture displayed this comparison against $U^{-1/2}$. Agreement across beam directions supplied more evidence than one angular peak.<The discovery of electron waves by Clinton J. Davisson>[Printed pp. 391–393, figures 2–4: angular and azimuth scans and independent wavelength comparison]{ref}

The inference is about coherent <quantum phases> in scattering probabilities. It does not establish a wavy particle trajectory, and a collector-current experiment does not resolve individual detection events. Predicting the full intensity additionally requires the material's <scattering amplitude>, its surface condition, and instrumental angular acceptance; the <de Broglie wavelength> alone predicts none of those strengths. The following <finite-row electron diffraction model> isolates phase addition without attempting to reproduce the <nickel> measurements.

= Finite-row electron diffraction model
{parent=Electron diffraction}
{title2=$I(\phi)/I(0)=N^{-2}|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}|^2$}

Take $N$ equally spaced scattering sites at $x_j=jd$ on a line, a normally incident <plane wave>, equal site <scattering amplitudes>, and observation far enough away that outgoing directions are effectively parallel. Retain only one scattering event per amplitude, as in the <Born approximation>. For angle $\phi$ from the outward normal, write the adjacent-site <quantum phase> difference as $\delta=kd\sin\phi$, with $k=2\pi/\lambda$; reversing its overall sign leaves the intensity unchanged. Summing a <geometric series> gives
$$
\frac{I(\phi)}{I(0)}=\frac{1}{N^2}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}\right|^2
=\left[\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{N\sin(\delta/2)}\right]^2,\qquad \delta=\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\phi.
$$
At $\delta=2\pi m$ use the continuous limit $I/I(0)=1$, rather than treating $0/0$ as a missing peak. The integer $m$ is the <diffraction order>: $d\sin\phi=m\lambda$. This is a surface-row spacing, not the plane spacing in <Bragg's law>. The plotted intensity is normalized to its central value; it is not an integrated detection probability or an absolute collector <electric current>.

= Reciprocal-lattice peaks of a finite crystal

= Supersymmetric quantum mechanics
{title2=SUSY QM}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

Supersymmetric quantum mechanics factors partner Hamiltonians through first-order operators. Its odd supercharges anticommute to the Hamiltonian; on a Riemannian manifold, the Hilbert space can be realized by square-integrable differential forms with supercharges given by a differential and its adjoint.

= Supersymmetric factorization and zero-mode normalizability
{parent=Supersymmetric quantum mechanics}
{title2=$A=\partial_x+w(x),\quad H_-=A^\dagger A/2,\quad H_+=AA^\dagger/2$}

The candidate zero modes are $e^{-\int^xw}$ in the minus sector and $e^{+\int^xw}$ in the plus sector; this follows from the positive squared-norm expressions for their energy. Their membership in the <Hilbert space> determines whether <supersymmetry> is unbroken. For $w=x$ only the first is normalizable. For $w=x^2+a$, $a>0$, neither is normalizable, while both partner potentials grow quartically and have discrete spectra. Thus their strictly positive lowest energy is paired and <supersymmetry> is broken, with zero <Witten index>.

= Witten index
{c}
{parent=Supersymmetric quantum mechanics}
{title2=$I(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}((-1)^F e^{-\beta H})$}

With a well-defined regulated supertrace, positive-energy states of <supersymmetric quantum mechanics> are paired by an odd invertible charge. Their opposite <fermion parities> cancel, leaving $I=n_B^0-n_F^0$, independent of $\beta$. A nonzero index proves the existence of a <supersymmetric vacuum>; zero does not prove its absence. Discrete-spectrum or suitable infrared conditions matter, because continuum thresholds and states escaping to infinity can invalidate naive trace manipulations or deformation invariance.

= Gradient superpotential Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics
{parent=Supersymmetric quantum mechanics}
{title2=$H=\{q,q^\dagger\}$}

For a real smooth function $\chi$ on <Euclidean space>, put $A_a=\partial_a-\partial_a\chi$ and $q=\sum_a\psi_a^\dagger A_a$. The <adjoint operator> for the usual $L^2$ <inner product> is $A_a^\dagger=-\partial_a-\partial_a\chi$. Since $[A_a,A_b]=0$, the <canonical anticommutation relations> give $q^2=0$. Expanding $H=\{q,q^\dagger\}$ and using $[A_a,A_b^\dagger]=-2\partial_a\partial_b\chi$ gives
$$
H=(-\Delta+|\nabla\chi|^2+\Delta\chi)I-2\sum_{a,b}(\partial_a\partial_b\chi)\psi_a^\dagger\psi_b.
$$
In the empty <fermionic Fock space> sector the last term vanishes; in the filled sector $\psi_a^\dagger\psi_b=\delta_{ab}$. Consequently the two scalar <Hamiltonian operators> are $H_0=-\Delta+|\nabla\chi|^2+\Delta\chi$ and $H_d=-\Delta+|\nabla\chi|^2-\Delta\chi$. On the compatible <self-adjoint operator> domain the <Hamiltonian operator> is nonnegative because $\langle v,Hv\rangle=\|qv\|^2+\|q^\dagger v\|^2$.

= Zero mode in the fermion-vacuum sector
{parent=Gradient superpotential Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics}
{title2=$f=Ce^\chi$}

For the <gradient superpotential Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics>, $H_0=\sum_aA_a^\dagger A_a$. A zero-energy <normalizable wavefunction> therefore satisfies $A_af=0$ for every $a$, because its energy is $\sum_a\|A_af\|^2$. Equivalently $\partial_a(e^{-\chi}f)=0$, so on a connected configuration space $f=Ce^\chi$. It exists in the <Hilbert space> precisely when $e^\chi$ is <square-integrable>. In three dimensions with radial $\chi$, this requires $4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2e^{2\chi(r)}\,dr<\infty$. The sign depends on the convention $A=\partial-\partial\chi$; the filled sector instead has the possible zero mode $e^{-\chi}$.

= Fermion-number sectors in supersymmetric quantum mechanics
{parent=Supersymmetric quantum mechanics}
{title2=$\mathcal H_n=L^2(\mathbb R^d)\otimes\Lambda^n\mathbb C^d$}

For $d$ <fermionic creation operators> with the <canonical anticommutation relations>, the <Hilbert space> $L^2(\mathbb R^d)\otimes\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^d$ splits into $\mathcal H_n=L^2(\mathbb R^d)\otimes\Lambda^n\mathbb C^d$, $0\leq n\leq d$. The <fermion number operator> $N=\sum_a\psi_a^\dagger\psi_a$ has value $n$ on $\mathcal H_n$; its internal multiplicity is $\binom dn$. An odd <supercharge> $Q=q+q^\dagger$, with $q$ raising $n$ and $q^2=0$, gives $H=\{q,q^\dagger\}$ and preserves these sectors.

On an <eigenspace> with $E>0$, $Q/\sqrt E$ is an <odd involution pairing bosonic and fermionic states>. More precisely,
$$
v=E^{-1}(qq^\dagger+q^\dagger q)v
$$
gives an <orthogonal decomposition> of each state into the images of $q$ and $q^\dagger$: their <inner product> vanishes because $(q^\dagger)^2=0$. If $u\in\operatorname{im}q^\dagger$, then $q^\dagger u=0$, $q^\dagger qu=Eu$ and $\|qu\|^2=E\|u\|^2$. Thus $q/\sqrt E$ pairs such a state in degree $n$ with one in degree $n+1$, and $q^\dagger/\sqrt E$ is the inverse. States with zero energy are killed by both operators and may remain unpaired. The pairing applies to positive spectral subspaces as well; discreteness is not implied.

= Radial superpartner in three-dimensional supersymmetric quantum mechanics
{parent=Fermion-number sectors in supersymmetric quantum mechanics}

Let $\chi=\chi(r)$, $r=|x|$, and let $f(r)|0\rangle$ be a normalized <eigenstate> of $H_0$ with $E>0$, where $|0\rangle$ is the <Clifford vacuum>. Acting with the <supercharge> differentiates only its <wavefunction>:
$$
Qf(r)|0\rangle=(f'-\chi'f)\psi_r^\dagger|0\rangle,
\qquad \psi_r^\dagger=\sum_a\frac{x_a}{r}\psi_a^\dagger.
$$
The normalized partner is $E^{-1/2}(f'-\chi'f)\psi_r^\dagger|0\rangle$. Indeed $[H,Q]=0$, and $\|Qf|0\rangle\|^2=E\|f|0\rangle\|^2$; acting with $Q$ again returns $\sqrt E$ times the original state. The expression at $r=0$ is interpreted by the regular extension of the original state. Although its coefficient depends only on $r$, its degree-one components contain the radial unit vector.

= Fermionic Fock space
{parent=Supersymmetric quantum mechanics}

The fermionic Fock space of $n$ modes is the exterior algebra $\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^n$. Creation acts by exterior multiplication and annihilation by contraction.

= Polarized fermionic Fock space
{parent=Fermionic Fock space}
{title2=$\mathcal F_P=\Lambda(PH)\,\widehat\otimes\,\Lambda(\overline{(I-P)H})$}

An orthogonal projection $P$ splits a one-particle <Hilbert space> into empty and filled modes. Take completed exterior Fock spaces of empty particles and of holes in the filled modes, with the graded tensor product enforcing the <canonical anticommutation relations> between both factors. The vacuum is killed by particle annihilation in $PH$ and by particle creation in $(I-P)H$. Only finite particle-hole excitations are needed for a dense basis. = Fermion-boson correspondence on the circle
{parent=Polarized fermionic Fock space}
{title2=$\mathcal F_P\cong\bigoplus_{q\in\mathbb Z}\mathcal F_{\mathrm{boson}}$}

In charge $q$, let $\Omega_q$ fill all integer modes below $q$. Its rotation energy is $q(q-1)/2$. The negative <Heisenberg current algebra of a fermion on the circle> modes create mutually commuting oscillators $b_n^*=J_{-n}/\sqrt n$ with $[b_n,b_m^*]=\delta_{nm}$. Their normalized occupation monomials applied to $\Omega_q$ are orthonormal. The <Jacobi triple product> identifies the fermionic charge-energy generating function with $\sum_q u^q t^{q(q-1)/2}\prod_{n>0}(1-t^n)^{-1}$, proving completeness in each finite-dimensional energy space. Thus the rotation Hamiltonian is $Q(Q-1)/2+\sum_{n>0}n b_n^*b_n$.

= Fermionic bilinear charge algebra
{parent=Canonical anticommutation relations}
{title2=$[Q_A,Q_B]=Q_{[A,B]},\quad Q_A=\int d^3x:\!\psi^\dagger A\psi\!:$}

$$
with the corresponding spatial delta functions for fields. The quartic terms cancel after fermion reordering. = Triplet charged-current closure on electromagnetic charge

= Stone-von Neumann theorem
{c}
{parent=Weyl relations}

= Gaussian projection in a Weyl representation
{parent=Weyl relations}

= Ehrenfest theorem
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{c}
{wiki}

Ehrenfest’s theorem makes expectation values obey Hamiltonian commutator equations analogous to classical motion.

For a possibly time-dependent observable $O$,
$$
\frac d{dt}\langle O\rangle
=\frac1{i\hbar}\langle[O,H]\rangle
+\left\langle\frac{\partial O}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$

= Proof of Ehrenfest theorem from the Schrodinger equation
{parent=Ehrenfest theorem}

Differentiating $\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$ and using $i\hbar|\dot\psi\rangle=H|\psi\rangle$ and its adjoint gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\langle O\rangle
=\frac i\hbar\langle[H,O]\rangle
+\left\langle\frac{\partial O}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$

= Classical equations from Ehrenfest theorem
{parent=Ehrenfest theorem}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+U(x)$, the <canonical commutation relation> gives
$$
m\frac d{dt}\langle x\rangle=\langle p\rangle,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle U'(x)\rangle,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}\langle H\rangle=0.
$$
These are the expectation-value analogues of <Newton's second law> and conservation of energy.

= Holstein–Primakoff transformation
{parent=Spin}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$S^z=S-a^\dagger a$}

The Holstein–Primakoff transformation represents a spin-$S$ <operator algebra> with a bosonic <creation operator> and <annihilation operator>:
$$
S^+=\sqrt{2S}\sqrt{1-\frac{a^\dagger a}{2S}}\,a,\qquad S^-=\sqrt{2S}\,a^\dagger\sqrt{1-\frac{a^\dagger a}{2S}},\qquad S^z=S-a^\dagger a.
$$
The order of the square root and oscillator operator matters. Their squared difference gives $[S^+,S^-]|n\rangle=2(S-n)|n\rangle$, proving the <spin commutation relations>. Expanding the square root yields the <linear spin-wave approximation> and its interaction corrections.

= Unitary highest-weight termination
{parent=Irreducible spin representation}
{title2=$\|(J_-)^\ell|j,j\rangle\|^2=\ell(2j-\ell+1)\|(J_-)^{\ell-1}|j,j\rangle\|^2$}

For Hermitian $J_3$ and adjoint <angular momentum ladder operators> $J_+^\dagger=J_-$ on a positive-definite inner-product space, a <highest-weight vector> of weight $j$ obeys $J_+|j,j\rangle=0$. The commutation relations give $J_+(J_-)^\ell|j,j\rangle=\ell(2j-\ell+1)(J_-)^{\ell-1}|j,j\rangle$. Taking <inner products> forces $2j$ to be a nonnegative integer: otherwise the first negative factor would give a negative squared norm. The norm first vanishes at $\ell=2j+1$, so the generated <unitary representation> has <dimension> $2j+1$. Without positivity an abstract highest-weight module can remain infinite-dimensional even at an integral <highest weight>.

= Magnetization sectors of a spin chain
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$S=N-2k,\quad\dim\mathcal H_k=\binom Nk$}

With $S=\sum_{n=1}^NZ_n$, a computational basis vector containing $k$ excitations has eigenvalue $N-2k$. There are $N+1$ distinct eigenspaces, of dimensions $\binom Nk$. Any Hamiltonian commuting with $S$ preserves each sector and their population weights. In particular, a state supported in one sector cannot be transferred to a different sector by such controls.

= Excitation-number conservation in XXZ spin chains
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$[H,\sum_nZ_n]=0$}

For $H=\sum_{m<n}c_{mn}(X_mX_n+Y_mY_n)+\gamma_{mn}Z_mZ_n$, Pauli <commutators> give cancellation between the $XX$ and $YY$ terms in $[H,Z_m+Z_n]$, while each $ZZ$ term commutes individually. Hence total spin magnetization is conserved. Local $Z_k$ controls preserve the same law, so dynamics split into fixed-excitation sectors even when a particular sector is controllable.

= Single-excitation subspace
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$\mathcal H_1=\operatorname{span}\{|10\cdots0\rangle,\ldots,|0\cdots01\rangle\}$}

For a network of $N$ spin-one-half sites with $Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$, the single-excitation subspace has exactly one spin in state $|1\rangle$ and dimension $N$. Excitation-conserving exchange interactions preserve it. Its restricted Hamiltonian is a hopping matrix whose entries connect the corresponding sites. Controllability within this subspace need not imply controllability on the full $2^N$-dimensional space.

= XY exchange interaction
{parent=Single-excitation subspace}
{c}
{title2=$E=(X\otimes X+Y\otimes Y)/2$}

The isotropic XY exchange interaction between two <qubits> is $E=(X\otimes X+Y\otimes Y)/2$. Its action is
$$
E|00\rangle=E|11\rangle=0,\qquad E|01\rangle=|10\rangle,\qquad E|10\rangle=|01\rangle.
$$
Indeed, $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$, $X|1\rangle=|0\rangle$, $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$, and $Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle$. The two terms cancel on equal bits and add on unequal bits. Thus a network <Hamiltonian operator> $H=\sum_{a,b}K_{ab}E_{ab}$ preserves excitation number; in the <single-excitation subspace>, it is the weighted adjacency <matrix>, moving an excitation from $a$ to $b$ with hopping coefficient $K_{ab}$.

= Symmetric-arm reduction of an exchange Hamiltonian
{parent=XY exchange interaction}
{title2=$HF=FH_T,\quad |s_k\rangle=q^{-1/2}\sum_a|a,k\rangle$}

Consider $q$ identical length-$n$ arms joined to one hub, with <XY exchange interactions> of strength $J_0/\sqrt q$ from the hub to each first site and strength $J_k$ between sites $k,k+1$ on each arm. Let $|s_0\rangle$ be the hub excitation and $|s_k\rangle=q^{-1/2}\sum_{a=1}^q|a,k\rangle$. This <orthonormal> symmetric <single-excitation subspace> is invariant, and its restricted <Hamiltonian operator> is
$$
H|s_0\rangle=J_0|s_1\rangle,\qquad H|s_k\rangle=J_{k-1}|s_{k-1}\rangle+J_k|s_{k+1}\rangle,
$$
with endpoint terms omitted. At the first link the $q$ hub contributions add to $J_0$; all other links act identically on the arms. Thus the <linear isometry> $F|k\rangle=|s_k\rangle$ intertwines $H$ with the path <Hamiltonian operator> $H_T=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}J_k(|k\rangle\langle k+1|+|k+1\rangle\langle k|)$: $HF=FH_T$. Expanding the <matrix exponential> gives $e^{-iHt}F=Fe^{-iH_Tt}$. <Perfect quantum state transfer> from zero to $n$ on the path therefore produces an equal excitation superposition at the $q$ arm tips. Since all other <qubits> are zero, the tip state is a pure <W state>, up to the transfer's common phase.

= Endpoint control of an XX spin chain
{c}
{parent=Single-excitation subspace}
{title2=$\mathfrak g|_{\mathcal H_1}=\mathfrak u(N)\ (N\ge3)$}

In the <single-excitation subspace>, take a connected real nearest-neighbor hopping matrix $A$ with every coupling nonzero and endpoint control $B=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)$. Endpoint commutators isolate the first transition, then propagate along the chain to generate both off-diagonal skew-Hermitian directions and adjacent diagonal differences. These generate $\mathfrak{su}(N)$. For $N\ge3$, $\operatorname{Tr}B=N-2\ne0$ supplies the central direction, giving $\mathfrak u(N)$. For $N=2$ the algebra is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, which still gives full state controllability but omits an arbitrary global phase. A broken coupling separates an unreachable chain component.

= Bipartite spin rotation
{parent=Heisenberg antiferromagnet}

A <bipartite spin rotation> rotates the <spin> on one sublattice by $\pi$, converting an alternating classical <Néel state> to an all-up reference state. Rotation about $x$ sends $S^z\mapsto-S^z$ and $S^\pm\mapsto S^\mp$ on that sublattice. For an isotropic nearest-neighbor <Heisenberg antiferromagnet>, a bond becomes $-S_i^zS_j^z+(S_i^+S_j^++S_i^-S_j^-)/2$. The transformation is unitary and preserves the <spin commutation relations>; it changes the reference frame, not the model's spectrum.

= Ferromagnetic ground-state multiplet

A connected isotropic spin-$S$ ferromagnet with positive exchanges has a fully polarized <ground state> and a maximal-total-spin multiplet. = Infinite square well
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki=Particle_in_a_box}
{title2=$E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$}

= Particle in a box
{synonym}

The <infinite square well> is an ideal confinement model with <potential energy> $V(x)=0$ for $0<x<L$ and infinite walls outside. The walls impose <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $\phi(0)=\phi(L)=0$ and a vanishing exterior <wavefunction>. Inside, the time-independent <Schrödinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2}=E\phi,\qquad \phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The first wall removes the <cosine> term; the second requires $\sin(kL)=0$, hence $k=n\pi/L$. The $n=0$ solution is identically zero and cannot satisfy <wavefunction normalization>. Each <energy eigenstate> has $n-1$ interior <nodes of a standing wave>, at $x=jL/n$ for $j=1,\ldots,n-1$. More curvature costs more <kinetic energy>, explaining the $n^2$ growth. The positive <ground-state energy> is a confinement effect: a constant zero-energy <wavefunction> cannot satisfy both walls and remain normalized.

For an <electron> with mass $m_e=9.1093837139\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ in a box of length $L=1\,\mathrm{nm}$, use $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$. These constants are from the 2022 CODATA adjustment. <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>{ref}. Substitution gives
$$
E_1=\frac{h^2}{8m_eL^2}=6.024667\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J\approx0.376030\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad E_2=4E_1\approx1.504121\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Doubling $L$ quarters every <energy eigenvalue>; doubling $m$ halves them. This scaling makes the <particle in a box> useful for estimating confinement energies before attempting a more realistic calculation. A <finite square well> permits exterior tails for <bound states> and has only finitely many bound <energy eigenstates>. For an <electron>, the scalar box calculation assumes fixed <spin> and no spin-dependent interactions.

For the <ground state>, the probability of finding the particle in the middle half of the box is fully computable:
$$
\Pr\left(\frac L4<x<\frac{3L}4\right)=\int_{L/4}^{3L/4}\frac2L\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\,dx=\left[\frac xL-\frac{\sin(2\pi x/L)}{2\pi}\right]_{L/4}^{3L/4}=\frac12+\frac1\pi\approx0.818310.
$$
Thus half the available length contains about $81.8\%$ of the probability. The figure contrasts the signed <wavefunctions> with their nonnegative <probability densities>: a negative lobe is an amplitude with opposite <quantum phase>, not negative probability.

An equal <quantum superposition> of the first two <energy eigenstates> gives a simple example of moving density:
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{\phi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\phi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}}{\sqrt2},\qquad |\psi|^2=\frac{\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2}{2}+\phi_1\phi_2\cos\frac{(E_2-E_1)t}{\hbar}.
$$
Its density period is $T=h/(E_2-E_1)\approx3.66608\,\mathrm{fs}$ for the <electron> box above. At $t=0$ the cross term enhances the left side; at $T/4$ it vanishes; at $T/2$ it enhances the right side. More quantitatively,
$$
\Pr(0<x<L/2)=\frac12+\frac4{3\pi}\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T},
$$
which takes the values $0.924413$, $0.5$, and $0.075587$ at those times. <Orthogonality> keeps the total probability one throughout. An <energy measurement> still returns $E_1$ or $E_2$ with equal probabilities: changing position density arises from relative <quantum phase>, not changing populations. A <mixed state> describing a statistical mixture with those populations has the constant <probability density> $(\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2)/2$; it lacks this <matter-wave interference> term.

= Quantum harmonic oscillator
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}
{title2=$H=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2x^2/2$}

The <quantum harmonic oscillator> describes a particle of <mass> $m>0$ in a quadratic <potential energy> with <angular frequency> $\omega>0$. In the position representation its <quantum Hamiltonian> is
$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac{m\omega^2x^2}2.
$$
The model is useful far beyond a literal spring: a <Taylor series> about a stable equilibrium with positive potential curvature has this quadratic term as its leading nonconstant approximation. The pure <quartic oscillator> has zero curvature at its minimum, so that particular approximation does not apply. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-04-quantum-physics-i-spring-2013/808334d09369e3a726f6f20d82315c20_MIT8_04S13_Lec08.pdf[Allan Adams, MIT 8.04 Lecture 8, Quantum Harmonic Oscillator, pp. 3–4]{ref}.

In units with $\hbar=1$, <creation and annihilation operators> give
$$
H=\omega\left(N+\frac12\right),
\qquad N=A^\dagger A.
$$
Here $N$ is the <number operator>. Restoring the reduced <Planck constant>, the <energy eigenvalues> are $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)$ for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$: neighboring levels are separated by $\hbar\omega$, and the <ground-state energy> is the <zero-point energy> $\hbar\omega/2$.

With length scale $\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega)}$ and dimensionless coordinate $u=x/\ell$, the <ground state> is the normalized <Gaussian wave packet>
$$
\phi_0(x)=\frac1{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt\ell}e^{-u^2/2},\qquad \langle x^2\rangle_0=\frac{\ell^2}2.
$$
For $\hbar=m=\omega=1$, this is precisely the $a=1$ trial <wavefunction> in the <quantum variational principle>. The balance of <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> therefore recovers the exact <ground-state energy> using only <Gaussian integrals>.

= Stability of monomial ladder-operator perturbations

= Pauli exclusion principle
{c}
{parent=Fermion}
{wiki}

The <Pauli exclusion principle> says that each complete <one-particle state> can have <fermionic occupation number> zero or one. “Complete” includes <spin> and any other internal labels, as well as the spatial <wavefunction>. Thus two <electrons> can share a <spatial orbital> with opposite <spin> projections: these are two different <spin orbitals>. Equal energy alone does not mean equal <quantum state>, because <degenerate energy levels> contain several independent <one-particle states>.

This distinction explains how an <electron shell> can accommodate several <electrons> while exclusion still allows only one per complete <one-particle state>. It also makes a finite model of <fermionic state counting> surprisingly simple: two <spatial orbitals> with two <spin> choices each give four complete <one-particle states>, and exactly six two-<electron> basis states. The following diagram enumerates them; its boxes represent <occupation numbers>, not locations in space.

Pauli's <Exclusion principle and quantum mechanics by Wolfgang Pauli> is his retrospective account, delivered on 13 December 1946 for the 1945 Nobel Prize. = Spatial orbital

A <spatial orbital> is a normalized spatial <wavefunction> used in a one-particle description. An <atomic orbital> is the corresponding notion for a <chemical atom>; a <molecular orbital> belongs to a <molecule>. A <spatial orbital> is not a trajectory and does not by itself specify <spin>.

= Spin orbital
{parent=Spatial orbital}
{title2=$u_{a\sigma}(\mathbf r,s)=\phi_a(\mathbf r)\chi_\sigma(s)$}

When spatial and <spin> factors separate, a <spin orbital> combines a <spatial orbital> $\phi_a$ with a normalized <spin> state $\chi_\sigma$. For an <electron>, choose <spin one-half along an axis> with $\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow$. The two <spin orbitals> $u_{a\uparrow}$ and $u_{a\downarrow}$ are <orthogonal> even though their <spatial orbital> is identical, since their <inner product> contains $\langle\chi_\uparrow|\chi_\downarrow\rangle=0$. When spatial and <spin> variables do not separate, the complete <one-particle state> is instead a multicomponent <wavefunction> $u(\mathbf r,s)$; exclusion still applies to that complete <quantum state>.

= Two-particle antisymmetrization
{parent=Pauli exclusion principle}
{title2=$A_{12}=(I-P_{12})/2$}

The <particle exchange operator> $P_{12}$ swaps the complete particle slots, including <spin>. Its antisymmetric <projector> is $A_{12}=(I-P_{12})/2$. For <orthonormal> <one-particle states> $u,v$, the normalized <multiparticle quantum state> is
$$
|u\wedge v\rangle=\frac{|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle-|v\rangle\otimes|u\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
But attempting the same construction with $v=u$ gives the zero vector:
$$
A_{12}(|u\rangle\otimes|u\rangle)=\frac{|u\rangle\otimes|u\rangle-|u\rangle\otimes|u\rangle}{2}=0.
$$
The zero vector cannot be normalized to a <quantum state>. This is exclusion as cancellation of <probability amplitudes>, rather than a repulsive force. More generally, for normalized $u,v$ the squared <norm> of the numerator divided by $\sqrt2$ is $1-|\langle u|v\rangle|^2$; the expression has unit <norm> only when $u,v$ are <orthogonal>. An overall <quantum phase> difference between $u,v$ describes the same <one-particle state> and also gives zero. Pauli explicitly connects exclusion with a vanishing antisymmetric <wavefunction>.<Exclusion principle and quantum mechanics by Wolfgang Pauli>[p. 31, antisymmetric functions with repeated states]{ref}

= Slater determinant

{parent=Two-particle antisymmetrization}

{title2=$\Psi(x_1,\ldots,x_N)=\det\!\left[u_j(x_i)\right]/\sqrt{N!}$}

A <Slater determinant> extends <two-particle antisymmetrization> to $N$ <fermions>. Here $x_i$ contains position and <spin> or other internal variables. = Exclusion versus particle position
{parent=Pauli exclusion principle}

Exclusion constrains complete <one-particle states>, not a collection of little spatial boxes. In particular, two opposite-<spin> <electrons> in the same <spatial orbital> have
$$
\Psi(\mathbf r_1,s_1;\mathbf r_2,s_2)=\phi_a(\mathbf r_1)\phi_a(\mathbf r_2)\frac{\chi_\uparrow(s_1)\chi_\downarrow(s_2)-\chi_\downarrow(s_1)\chi_\uparrow(s_2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The spatial factor is symmetric; the <spin-one-half singlet state> supplies the required antisymmetry. At $\mathbf r_1=\mathbf r_2$, this <wavefunction> need not vanish for opposite <spin> labels. For equal <spin> labels it does vanish. In a continuous position description, an exact coincident point has zero measure anyway; the useful comparison concerns joint <probability densities> or detection in finite regions. Equal positions do not imply equal complete <quantum states>.

The <fermionic occupation numbers> $n_\alpha=0,1$ refer to a chosen <orthonormal basis> of <one-particle states>. A <quantum superposition> need not have definite <occupation numbers> in that basis, and an <expectation value> $\langle n_\alpha\rangle$ can be fractional. For example, one <electron> in $(u+v)/\sqrt2$ has mean <occupation number> $1/2$ in each of two <orthogonal> <spin orbitals>, without splitting into two particles. The <canonical anticommutation relations> give $(a_\alpha^\dagger)^2=0$ and make $a_\alpha^\dagger a_\alpha$ a <projector>, so its <expectation value> stays between zero and one in every normalized <quantum state>.

= Zero-temperature filling of finite fermion levels
{parent=Pauli exclusion principle}
{title2=$E_0(N)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\epsilon_\alpha$}

For $M$ complete <energy eigenstates> of a one-particle <quantum Hamiltonian> for identical noninteracting <fermions>, order their energies $\epsilon_1\le\cdots\le\epsilon_M$. At fixed particle number $0\le N\le M$, the <quantum Hamiltonian> and the particle-number constraint are
$$
H=\sum_{\alpha=1}^M\epsilon_\alpha n_\alpha,\qquad \sum_{\alpha=1}^Mn_\alpha=N,\qquad n_\alpha\in\{0,1\}.
$$
The <ground state> occupies the $N$ lowest complete <one-particle states>. A short minimization argument suffices: moving an occupant from a higher energy to an empty lower energy decreases the total energy. This filling describes the <ground state> at <temperature> $T=0$; exclusion by itself also applies to excited <quantum states> and at positive <temperature>. The underlying thermal description is the <Fermi-Dirac distribution>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[Sections 3.6–3.6.2, occupation restriction, spin multiplicity and zero-temperature filling]{ref}

If the last occupied energy belongs to a group of $g$ <one-particle states> at the same energy and $q$ of them must be occupied, with all lower states filled, the <ground-state> subspace has <dimension> $\binom gq$. Exclusion does not select a particular <basis> or a particular <quantum superposition> in that subspace. The factor of two for an <electron> <spatial orbital> assumes both <spin> projections are included; a model restricted to one <spin> projection has only one state per <spatial orbital>. A <magnetic field> can split the <degenerate energy levels>, and interactions can change the lowest-energy <electron configuration>. Thus this finite noninteracting filling rule is not a complete prescription for interacting <chemical atoms>.

= Fermionic state counting
{parent=Zero-temperature filling of finite fermion levels}
{title2=$\dim\Lambda^N\mathbb C^M=\binom MN$}

Choosing $N$ different complete <one-particle states> from an <orthonormal basis> of size $M$ gives $\binom MN$ independent <Slater determinants>. This is the <binomial coefficient> because only the occupied subset matters: permutations change the sign of the <Slater determinant>, not the physical <quantum state>. Summing over particle numbers gives $2^M$ states in the finite <fermionic Fock space>.

For the diagram's two <spatial orbitals> $A,B$, take energies $0,\Delta$ with $\Delta>0$, each independent of <spin>. In the complete-state order $(A\uparrow,A\downarrow,B\uparrow,B\downarrow)$, the six $N=2$ <occupation number> strings are $1100,1010,1001,0110,0101,0011$. Their total energies are $0,\Delta,\Delta,\Delta,\Delta,2\Delta$, giving energy multiplicities $1,4,1$. For $N=3$, the <ground-state> energy is $\Delta$ with two independent <ground states>, according to which <spin orbital> in $B$ is occupied. Across $N=0,1,2,3,4$, the counts are $1,4,6,4,1$, summing to $16$. These are exact counts for this model, not measured atomic energies.

= Pair detection correlation
{parent=Fermionic atom antibunching}
{title2=$g^{(2)}(\Delta\mathbf r)$}

The <pair detection correlation> divides joint detection probability by the product of single detection probabilities. The baseline $g^{(2)}=1$ means independent detection; $g^{(2)}<1$ means suppressed pairs. Its value depends on the prepared <quantum state> and detector resolution, so exclusion alone does not require every experimental curve to reach zero.

= Spin-statistics theorem
{c}
{parent=Indistinguishable particles}
{wiki}

The spin-statistics theorem associates integer-spin particles with bosonic exchange symmetry and half-integer-spin particles with fermionic exchange symmetry in relativistic quantum field theory under the standard locality and positivity assumptions.

= Two identical spin-one bosons
{parent=Indistinguishable particles}

For two spin-one particles, the spin tensor product has a six-dimensional symmetric subspace and a three-dimensional antisymmetric subspace. A symmetric spatial state combines with symmetric spin, while an antisymmetric spatial state combines with antisymmetric spin, so that the total bosonic state remains symmetric.

= Degeneracy of two identical spin-one bosons with equally spaced orbital levels
{parent=Two identical spin-one bosons}

If $E_n=E_0+n\Delta$, the two-particle level $2E_0+N\Delta$ has degeneracy
$$
g_N=\begin{cases}
\dfrac92N+6,&N\text{ even},\\[3pt]
\dfrac92(N+1),&N\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$
Each unequal orbital pair contributes six symmetric-spin states and three antisymmetric-spin states; an equal orbital pair exists only for even $N$ and contributes six states.

= Time-dependent perturbation theory
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Expanding a state in unperturbed energy eigenstates with their free phases converts the time-dependent Schrodinger equation into coupled equations for slowly varying amplitudes. To first order, the perturbation on the right-hand side acts on the initial unperturbed state.

This is the time-dependent branch of <perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)>; its corrections describe transition amplitudes.

= Electric-dipole interaction
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}

In the long-wavelength approximation, a particle of charge $q$ in a spatially uniform electric field has perturbation
$$
\Delta(t)=-q\mathbf E(t)\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf r.
$$
Its transition amplitudes are controlled by matrix elements of the position operator.

= Electric-dipole selection rule
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}

= Dipole selection rule
{synonym}

Because the position operator has odd <parity>, an electric-dipole matrix element vanishes between states of the same parity. For orbital-angular-momentum eigenstates, the full rule is $\Delta\ell=\pm1$ and $\Delta m=0,\pm1$, with the field polarization selecting the allowed change in $m$.

= Continuum transition probability at long times
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}

For a harmonic perturbation and continuum detuning $\Omega(k)$, first-order amplitudes contain
$$
\frac{\sin(\Omega(k)t/2)}{\Omega(k)/2}.
$$
The distributional limit
$$
\frac{\sin^2(\Omega t/2)}{(\Omega/2)^2}
\longrightarrow 2\pi t\,\delta(\Omega)
$$
selects energy-conserving continuum states and produces a transition probability linear in time.

= Time-independent perturbation theory
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Time-independent perturbation theory expands the eigenvalues and eigenstates of $H_0+V$ in powers of a small perturbation $V$.

This is the stationary branch of <perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)>.

= Hellmann–Feynman theorem
{c}
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$E'(\lambda)=\langle\psi,H'(\lambda)\psi\rangle$}

For a differentiable normalized <eigenstate> of a differentiable <self-adjoint> <Hamiltonian>, differentiate $H\psi=E\psi$ and pair with $\psi$. The terms involving $\psi'$ cancel because $H$ is <self-adjoint>, leaving the stated formula. It turns energy <derivatives> into observable expectations. A smoothly chosen eigenbranch and suitable operator-domain regularity are required, particularly at degeneracies or crossings.

= First-order energy correction
{title2=$E_n^{(1)}$}
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

The first-order energy correction is the coefficient of the perturbation parameter in the expansion of an energy eigenvalue.

= Nondegenerate energy eigenvalue
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

An <energy eigenvalue> is nondegenerate when its <eigenspace> is one-dimensional. Its normalized <eigenstate> is then unique up to a complex phase.

= First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For a normalized eigenstate $|n\rangle$ belonging to a <nondegenerate energy eigenvalue>,
$$
E_n^{(1)}=\langle n|V|n\rangle,
\qquad
|n^{(1)}\rangle
=\sum_{m\ne n}|m\rangle
\frac{\langle m|V|n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
$$

= First-order ground state of the quartic oscillator
{parent=First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory}

For a <quantum harmonic oscillator> perturbed by $\lambda X^4$, write $X=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(A+A^\dagger)$. Since
$$
(A+A^\dagger)^4|0\rangle=3|0\rangle+6\sqrt2|2\rangle+2\sqrt6|4\rangle,
$$
<first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> gives
$$
\boxed{|0_\lambda\rangle
=|0\rangle-\frac{\hbar\lambda}{4m^2\omega^3}
\left(3\sqrt2|2\rangle+\sqrt{\frac32}|4\rangle\right)+O(\lambda^2).}
$$

= Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For a normalized nondegenerate eigenstate $|n\rangle$,
$$
E_n^{(2)}
=\sum_{m\ne n}
\frac{|\langle m|V|n\rangle|^2}
{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
$$
For a ground state, every denominator is negative, so the correction is nonpositive.

= Imaginary-coupled two-level Hamiltonian
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}E_-&-i\lambda\\i\lambda&E_+\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \Delta=E_+-E_->0,
$$
the exact energies are
$$
E_{\pm}^{\rm exact}
=\frac{E_-+E_+}{2}
\pm\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^2}{4}+\lambda^2}.
$$
The perturbation series around $\lambda=0$ has radius $\Delta/2$, set by the branch points $\lambda=\pm i\Delta/2$.

= Bright and dark state reduction of a star-coupled Hamiltonian
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

Suppose one state $|0\rangle$ couples to degenerate states $|1\rangle,\ldots,|m\rangle$ with amplitudes $v_1,\ldots,v_m$, while the degenerate states do not couple to each other. The normalized bright state proportional to $\sum_jv_j|j\rangle$ is the only combination coupled to $|0\rangle$. Every orthogonal combination is a dark state and remains an exact eigenstate at the degenerate energy. The remaining spectrum comes from a two-dimensional Hamiltonian whose off-diagonal entry is $(\sum_j|v_j|^2)^{1/2}$.

= Degenerate perturbation theory

First-order degenerate perturbation theory diagonalizes the perturbing operator within each degenerate eigenspace of the unperturbed Hamiltonian.

= Perturbation diagonal within a degenerate eigenspace
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}

If the perturbation is already diagonal in a basis of a degenerate eigenspace, those basis vectors receive its diagonal entries as first-order shifts.

= Exact diagonalization within a degenerate subspace
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}

When a perturbation preserves each unperturbed degenerate block and has no inter-block coupling, diagonalizing each block can give the exact spectrum.

= Resonant two-to-one oscillator coupling
{parent=Exact diagonalization within a degenerate subspace}

For oscillators of frequencies $1$ and $2$, the perturbation
$$
H'=A^{\dagger2}B+A^2B^\dagger
$$
preserves $N_A+2N_B$. On the energy-$9/2$ basis $|3,0\rangle,|1,1\rangle$, it is the matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt6\\\sqrt6&0\end{pmatrix},
$$
so the symmetric and antisymmetric combinations are exact eigenstates with shifts $\pm\sqrt6\lambda$.

= Rectangular potential barrier

A <rectangular potential barrier> has a constant elevated potential over a finite interval and a lower constant potential outside it. Matching wavefunctions and derivatives at the interval endpoints gives its reflection and transmission amplitudes. It is a special <potential barrier>.

= Hartree equations
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

The <Hartree equations> are self-consistent one-particle equations obtained by approximating a many-body <wave function> by a <tensor product> of orbital <wave functions>. Each orbital sees the potential averaged over the other particles' <probability densities>; attractive Newtonian mean-field variants include the <gravitational Hartree equation>.
