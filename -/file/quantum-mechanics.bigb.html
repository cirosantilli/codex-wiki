= Quantum mechanics
{wiki}

Quantum mechanics describes a <quantum system> using a <quantum state>, with <observables> represented by <self-adjoint operators>. For a single particle in one dimension, a <pure state> can be described by a <wavefunction> $\psi(x,t)$: its squared <modulus> gives the <probability density> for position. The <Schrödinger equation> predicts how this <probability amplitude> evolves between interventions; <measurement in quantum mechanics> connects it to experimental outcomes through the <Born rule>. A <wavefunction> therefore predicts a distribution of possible positions rather than a single trajectory as in <classical mechanics>.

The <infinite square well> makes three central ideas visible with elementary <sine> functions. Confinement selects discrete <energy eigenvalues>; <wavefunction normalization> converts a shape into quantitative <probabilities>; and a <quantum superposition> can have a changing <probability density> even when each constituent <energy eigenstate> has a stationary one. This combination explains why <quantum mechanics> uses both <linear algebra> and <differential equations>: <eigenfunctions> describe the building blocks, while <quantum phases> determine how they interfere. The same tools help explain the discrete energies of <chemical atoms> and <molecules>, although their <quantum Hamiltonians> require different potentials and often many particles.

In his first 1926 paper, Erwin Schrödinger formulated quantization as an <eigenvalue> problem for a <hydrogen atom> in <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>. On its opening page he compared the emergence of integers with the number of <nodes of a standing wave> on a vibrating string. The <infinite square well> below realizes that appealing idea especially transparently: the allowed integers follow from the <boundary conditions>. This historical analogy concerns the origin of discrete energies; the <Born rule> supplies the statistical interpretation of the modern <wavefunction>. <Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem (Erste Mitteilung) by Erwin Schrödinger>[Schrödinger, Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem, 1926, pp. 361–363]{ref}.

= Energy-level degeneracy
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$g$}

The number of independent quantum states sharing one <energy eigenvalue>. A level of angular momentum $J$ has $2J+1$ magnetic substates when no interaction splits them.

= Magnetic-gradient quartic confinement
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$V_k(x)=[\hbar k-q\alpha x^2/2]^2/(2m)$}

A spinless charged particle in $B_z=\alpha x$ admits <vector potential> $A_y=\alpha x^2/2$. Separating a plane wave along y produces a one-dimensional quartic <potential energy>. For nonzero $q\alpha$, $k/(q\alpha)>0$ gives two minima at $\pm\sqrt{2\hbar k/(q\alpha)}$; the opposite sign gives one minimum at zero. The field gradient creates confinement even though there is no scalar potential. The quartic potential is even, so its nondegenerate eigenstates have definite spatial <parity>.

= Double-well oscillator scale in a linear magnetic field
{parent=Magnetic-gradient quartic confinement}
{title2=$\omega_d=\sqrt{2\hbar q\alpha k}/m$}

For $q\alpha k>0$, let $x_0=\sqrt{2\hbar k/(q\alpha)}$. Expansion of the <magnetic-gradient quartic confinement> at either minimum gives $V(x_0+\delta x)=(q\alpha)^2x_0^2(\delta x)^2/(2m)+\cdots$. The local oscillator frequency is therefore $|q\alpha|x_0/m$. Both lowest parity states have leading energy $\hbar\omega_d/2$ if $x_0$ greatly exceeds the oscillator width. Local anharmonicity changes their common energy, while <quantum tunnelling> gives their much smaller splitting.

= Signed group velocity of a magnetic-gradient doublet
{parent=Double-well oscillator scale in a linear magnetic field}
{title2=$v_y=\hbar q\alpha/[2m\sqrt{2\hbar q\alpha k}]$}

Differentiating $E_k\simeq\sqrt{\hbar^3q\alpha k/2}/m$ gives the displayed <group velocity> when $q\alpha k>0$. Its sign is the sign of $q\alpha$, equivalently of k on that branch. Writing only a positive square root of $q\alpha/k$ would lose this sign. Exactly, the <Hellmann–Feynman theorem> gives $v_y=\langle\hbar k-q\alpha x^2/2\rangle/m$. Evaluating this small drift from a localized wavefunction requires enough anharmonic accuracy: a leading centred Gaussian alone need not reproduce it.

= Translation separation in a magnetic field gradient
{parent=Magnetic-gradient quartic confinement}

In a gauge with potentials independent of y, the canonical <momentum> $p_y$ commutes with the <Hamiltonian>. A simultaneous eigenbasis can be chosen as $e^{iky}\Phi_k(x)$, with $p_y=\hbar k$. Periodic length $L_y$ gives $k=2\pi n/L_y$. The kinetic y-momentum is instead $\hbar k-qA_y(x)$ and is not spatially constant. Confusing these two momenta loses the magnetic contribution to the velocity.

= Relativistic quantum mechanics
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

A description of <quantum states> and dynamics compatible with <special relativity>. Relativistic one-particle equations have a useful domain, while interactions that create or destroy particles generally require <quantum field theory>.

= Bipartite quantum system
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$\mathcal H_{AB}=\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B$}

A <quantum system> with two specified subsystems has a <tensor product> <Hilbert space>. A joint <density operator> determines subsystem $A$'s measurement statistics through its <partial trace> $\rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_B\rho_{AB}$. A joint <pure state> need not give a pure reduced state: the <Bell state> $(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$ gives $\rho_A=I/2$.

= Quantum virial theorem
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

For a stationary bound state with suitable operator domains, $2\langle K\rangle=\langle\mathbf x\cdot\nabla V\rangle$. In a quadratic harmonic potential this gives $\langle K\rangle=\langle V\rangle=E/2$.

= Quantum transport
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Transport of particles or charge when quantum mode structure, transmission and occupation of reservoir states determine the measured current.

= Universal conductance fluctuations
{parent=Quantum transport}
{title2=$\delta G\sim e^2/h$}

A coherent disordered mesoscopic conductor has reproducible, sample-dependent conductance variations when magnetic field, carrier energy or disorder configuration changes. Their typical scale is of order $e^2/h$ in a fully coherent diffusive sample, with numerical factors determined by symmetry, dimensionality and geometry. Thermal averaging and finite <phase coherence length> reduce the measured amplitude.

= Phase coherence length
{parent=Quantum transport}
{title2=$L_\phi$}

The characteristic distance over which phase-breaking processes suppress electron-path interference. For diffusion, $L_\phi\sim\sqrt{D\tau_\phi}$; ballistic phase propagation instead gives a scale $v\tau_\phi$. This differs from the elastic <mean free path>, since elastic scattering can change momentum while retaining coherence.

= Quasi-one-dimensional quantum transport
{parent=Quantum transport}

A narrow conductor has discrete transverse modes and nearly continuous longitudinal motion. Its conductance depends on the transmissions of occupied <quantum wire> subbands, spin counting and contacts. The <Landauer formula> connects these transmissions to two-terminal conductance. Disorder, interference, dephasing and interactions distinguish ballistic, diffusive, localized and interacting regimes.

= Transverse electronic mode
{parent=Quasi-one-dimensional quantum transport}

A transverse <confinement> <eigenstate> that defines a threshold for propagation along a narrow conductor. Above that threshold, its longitudinal dispersion supplies an incident channel from each reservoir. <spin> can label separate channels; an unresolved spin-degenerate mode contributes twice the single-spin <spin-resolved conductance quantum>. This physical use of mode is unrelated to the most probable value of a <probability> distribution.

= Landauer formula
{parent=Quantum transport}
{c}

A transport expression relating current to occupied incident modes and their transmission probabilities. For spin-degenerate independent <electrons>, $I=(2e/h)\sum_i\int T_i(E)[f_L(E)-f_R(E)]\,dE$, with the direction convention chosen consistently. In <linear response> a perfectly transmitted mode contributes $2e^2/h$.

= Flux-normalized transmission matrix
{parent=Landauer formula}

An element $t_{nm}$ is the amplitude for an incident unit-flux channel $m$ to emerge in outgoing unit-flux channel $n$. Then $|t_{nm}|^2$ is a transmission probability and $\operatorname{tr}(t^\dagger t)$ sums transmitted channels. Unnormalized wave amplitudes require velocity ratios.

= Spin-resolved conductance quantum
{parent=Landauer formula}

A perfectly transmitted spin-resolved channel contributes $e^2/h$ to two-terminal linear conductance. Two degenerate spin channels give $2e^2/h$. Flux normalization cancels group velocity against the longitudinal state density.

= Thermally rounded channel threshold
{parent=Spin-resolved conductance quantum}

For perfect transmission above threshold $E_n$, integrating $-df/dE$ gives $G_n=(e^2/h)f(E_n)$. Thermal occupation rounds a zero-temperature conductance step over an energy range of order $k_BT$.

= Flux carried by a directed electronic mode
{parent=Landauer formula}
{title2=$d\dot N=dE/h$}

For one directed spin-resolved channel, the density of longitudinal states per unit length and wave number is $1/(2\pi)$. Since $v=\hbar^{-1}dE/dk$, velocity times density per energy is $1/h$. A reservoir occupation imbalance therefore contributes particle flux $\int\delta f(E)dE/h$; in zero-temperature <linear response> it is $\delta\mu/h$. Multiplying by charge and transmission gives the Landauer current per <mode>.

= Landauer-Büttiker formula
{c}
{parent=Landauer formula}
{title2=$I_i=(e^2/h)[N_iV_i-\sum_jT_{ij}V_j]$}

A multi-reservoir <quantum transport> relation. Here $I_i$ is conventional current injected from reservoir $i$, $N_i$ counts outgoing spin-resolved modes and $T_{ij}$ counts transmission from reservoir $j$ into $i$. Ideal voltage probes draw zero net current and equilibrate incident carriers. On a chiral integer Hall edge with $N$ modes, $I_i=(Ne^2/h)(V_i-V_{i-1})$ for the stated circulation order.

= Mode-count balance in a ballistic junction
{parent=Landauer-Büttiker formula}

Let $T_{ij}$ count transmitted <modes> from injector $j$ to collector $i$, excluding reflection $R_{ii}$. Scattering conservation gives $\sum_{i\ne j}T_{ij}=N_j-R_{jj}$; <unitarity> also gives the corresponding row balance. The conductance matrix has zero row sum, making equal lead potentials carry no current, and zero column sum, conserving total current.

= Equilibrating voltage probe
{parent=Landauer-Büttiker formula}

An ideal <voltage> probe is a reservoir that equilibrates incident carriers but draws zero net average current. Its <chemical potential> is fixed by that zero-current condition, and can differ from a nearby geometrical point's inferred diffusive potential in a ballistic device. Reading the probe therefore requires the transmission network, not just a local Ohm law.

= Ballistic conductance quantization
{parent=Landauer formula}

Stepwise conductance as transverse propagating modes open in a clean constriction. Each fully transmitted spin-degenerate subband contributes $2e^2/h$; finite <temperature>, imperfect transmission and unresolved modes round the steps.

= Oscillator strength
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$f_{ij}$}

A dimensionless measure of the intensity of a specified optical transition. It combines the transition matrix element and transition energy; <selection rules>, <Polarization> and the distribution of electronic states determine which transitions carry appreciable absorption. Peak position alone does not give the absorbed fraction.

= Wigner-Seitz radius
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{c}
{title2=$r_s$}

The radius $r_s$ of a sphere containing one electron at a specified density: $4\pi r_s^3\rho/3=1$.

= Dimensionless Wigner-Seitz radius
{parent=Wigner-Seitz radius}
{c}
{title2=$r_s$}

Write $(3/(4\pi n))^{1/3}=r_sa_B^*$, where $a_B^*=4\pi\varepsilon\hbar^2/(m^*e^2)$. Here $r_s$ is dimensionless, unlike a <Wigner-Seitz radius> measured as a length. Electron-gas <energy> formulas quoted in effective atomic units commonly use this convention.

= Local density approximation
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

A density-functional approximation using the energy per particle of a uniform electron gas at the local density. Its exchange-energy-per-electron tail decays exponentially with an exponentially decaying density.

= Generalized gradient approximation
{parent=Local density approximation}

A density-functional approximation using local density and its gradient. Gradient dependence incorporates some information about spatial inhomogeneity.

= Becke 1988 exchange functional
{parent=Generalized gradient approximation}
{c}

A gradient-corrected exchange functional fitted to atomic exchange energies and designed to recover a conventional exchange-energy-per-electron tail $-1/(2r)$ for finite systems. This differs from the exchange potential tail.

= Hohenberg-Kohn theorem
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{c}

Ground-state density-potential uniqueness and a density variational principle for electrons with fixed interaction. The elementary uniqueness proof assumes nondegenerate <ground states> and fixes the potential only up to a constant.

= Hartree-Fock wavefunction
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{c}

An antisymmetric single <Slater determinant> whose occupied orbitals make its energy stationary under occupied-virtual orbital rotations. A stationary solution need not be a stable or global minimum.

= Brillouin's theorem
{parent=Hartree-Fock wavefunction}
{c}

At Hartree-Fock stationarity, $\langle\Phi_0|H|\Phi_i^a\rangle=0$ for each occupied-virtual single excitation.

= Non-crossing rule
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

For a generic one-parameter Hermitian <Hamiltonian>, two coupled states of the same conserved <symmetry> avoid an exact crossing. Opposite <symmetry> or a vanishing coupling can permit crossings. The rule does not supply numerical energies or prove that every possible reaction path shares the stipulated <symmetry>.

= Symmetry operator
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{title2=$UHU^{-1}=H$}

An operator implementing a symmetry of quantum states and observables. The ordinary spatial symmetries considered in molecular spectroscopy are unitary and commute with the <Hamiltonian>. More generally, an antiunitary symmetry such as <time reversal> needs its corresponding treatment.

= Quantum confinement
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

<Quantum confinement> restricts a particle's wavefunction over a length comparable with its characteristic quantum length scales, altering the allowed <energy levels> relative to a bulk material. For a parabolic band confined over width $L$, a characteristic spacing is of order $\hbar^2/(m^*L^2)$, with boundary-dependent numerical factors. A <quantum dot> confines all three directions; confinement in only one direction can leave continuous motion in the other two. Finite barrier height and interactions modify the simplest infinite-wall estimates.

= Quantum wire
{parent=Quantum confinement}

Electronic confinement in two transverse directions leaves propagating motion along the wire. Each open transverse subband supplies longitudinal transport channels, with spin counted explicitly. A wire may be ballistic or disordered; one-dimensional subband structure alone does not ensure perfect transmission.

= Carrier confinement versus photonic confinement
{parent=Quantum confinement}

Carrier confinement quantizes electronic and hole states and changes transition energies, <joint density of states> and envelope overlaps. A <waveguide> or cavity confines electromagnetic <modes> and changes optical overlap, resonances or emission rates. Both can occur in a semiconductor light source, but a small electronic island is not automatically a designed <optical cavity>.

= Second quantization
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\hat\psi(x)=\sum_\alpha\varphi_\alpha(x)a_\alpha$}

Second quantization describes identical-particle <quantum states> in <Fock space> using <creation operators> and <annihilation operators>. Relative to an <orthonormal basis> of <one-particle states>, a one-body operator becomes $\sum_{\alpha\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}a_\alpha^\dagger a_\beta$. The <canonical commutation relation> implements <bosonic> statistics, while the <canonical anticommutation relations> implement <fermionic> statistics. Expanding the <quantized field> in a complete basis gives a delta-function equal-time bracket; restricting the expansion to finitely many modes replaces that delta function by the corresponding <projection operator> kernel.

= Two-mode approximation for a Bose gas
{parent=Second quantization}
{title2=$\hat\psi(\mathbf r)\simeq\varphi_L(\mathbf r)a_L+\varphi_R(\mathbf r)a_R$}

Project a <quantized field> onto two low-<energy> <orbitals>. A sufficient regime has <temperature>, hopping and interaction shifts small compared with the <energy gap> to discarded <orbitals>. Small interwell <contact interaction> integrals can then be neglected while retaining a small <quantum tunnelling> matrix element. The projected field's <operator commutator> is the two-mode <projection operator> kernel, rather than a complete spatial <Dirac delta function>.

= Bose-Hubbard model
{parent=Second quantization}
{c}
{title2=$H=-\sum_{ij}t_{ij}a_i^\dagger a_j+\tfrac U2\sum_i n_i(n_i-1)-\mu\sum_i n_i$}

A lattice <boson> <Hamiltonian operator> combining hopping and on-site repulsion. The on-site interaction counts distinct particle pairs through $n_i(n_i-1)/2$. It is the bosonic counterpart of the <Hubbard model>; projecting a continuum <contact interaction> onto localized <orbitals> gives $U=g\int|\varphi_i|^4$ when intersite overlaps are negligible.

= Two-site Bose-Hubbard model
{parent=Bose-Hubbard model}
{title2=$H=-t(a_L^\dagger a_R+a_R^\dagger a_L)+\tfrac U2\sum_{j=L,R}n_j(n_j-1)$}

Two localized <orbitals> coupled by <quantum tunnelling> and on-site interactions give the smallest <Bose-Hubbard model>. A symmetric <double-well potential> with lowest one-particle <energies> $E_0,E_1$ has $t=(E_1-E_0)/2$ and common diagonal <energy> $(E_0+E_1)/2$. At fixed total <particle number>, that common diagonal term is constant. A <fixed-number two-mode condensate> gives a simple <variational method> for this model, while stronger interactions can require occupation-correlated trial <quantum states>.

= Contact interaction
{synonym}

In a one-dimensional identical-particle model, a contact interaction has two-body potential $U(x,x')=g\delta(x-x')$, using the <Dirac delta distribution>. For <bosons>, its <second quantization> is the displayed normally ordered <Hamiltonian operator>. Projection onto <orthonormal> modes gives $H_{\rm int}=(g/2)\sum_{ijkl}I_{ijkl}a_i^\dagger a_j^\dagger a_ka_l$, where $I_{ijkl}=\int\varphi_i^*\varphi_j^*\varphi_k\varphi_l\,dx$. The overlap integrals determine which mode-changing processes survive. A finite-mode model describes that projection; discarded modes can still matter to the full physical system.

= Momentum-space contact-interaction Hamiltonian
{parent=Contact interaction (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$H_{\rm int}=\frac{u}{2V}\sum_{k,p,q}a_{k+q}^\dagger a_{p-q}^\dagger a_pa_k$}

For a periodic box of <volume> $V$, expanding $\psi(\mathbf r)=V^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}a_{\mathbf k}$ in $H_{\rm int}=\tfrac u2\int d^3r\,\psi^\dagger\psi^\dagger\psi\psi$ gives the displayed <normal ordering>. The spatial <integral> is $V\delta_{\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2,\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4}$ by <Fourier orthogonality>, enforcing <momentum conservation> and leaving the factor $V^{-1}$. The coupling $u$ in three dimensions has units of <energy> times <volume>.

= Contact-interaction vacuum-energy renormalization
{parent=Contact interaction (quantum mechanics)}

A low-energy contact coupling is defined through a physical scattering length. Ultraviolet pieces of zero-point energy cannot be interpreted independently of the matching or counterterms fixing that coupling.

= Two-mode Bose gas on a ring
{parent=Contact interaction (quantum mechanics)}
{c}
{title2=$H=\mathcal E N_1+(U/4\pi)[N(N-1)+2N_0N_1]$}

Project a <bosonic> field on a ring onto angular-momentum modes zero and one. The kinetic level spacing is $\mathcal E=\hbar^2/(2mR^2)$. The contact-interaction <integral> enforces <angular momentum conservation>, leaving two same-mode terms and four mixed terms. The displayed projected <Quantum Hamiltonian> follows by normal ordering. Couplings to discarded modes belong to the full model and can limit the two-mode approximation.

= Contact interaction between two box modes
{parent=Contact interaction (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$I_{1111}=I_{2222}=3/(2L),\quad I_{1122}=1/L$}

For distinct box modes $\varphi_1=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(p\pi x/L)$ and $\varphi_2=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(q\pi x/L)$, the displayed <contact interaction> overlaps produce self terms $3g\sum_jn_j(n_j-1)/(4L)$, a density term $2gn_1n_2/L$, and pair conversion $g(a_1^{\dagger2}a_2^2+a_2^{\dagger2}a_1^2)/(2L)$. Pair conversion preserves total <particle number> but prevents diagonalization by individual <occupation numbers>. The additional overlaps are $I_{1112}=-\delta_{q,3p}/(2L)$ and $I_{1222}=-\delta_{p,3q}/(2L)$, following from $\sin^3u=(3\sin u-\sin3u)/4$. Third-harmonic mode pairs therefore also permit single-particle transfer assisted by the other particles. For the first two box modes these additional terms vanish.

= Contact interaction between two ring modes
{parent=Contact interaction (quantum mechanics)}
{title2=$H_{\rm int}=\frac g{2L}[n_1(n_1-1)+n_2(n_2-1)+4n_1n_2]$}

Two distinct normalized <plane waves> on a periodic one-dimensional ring of length $L$ give the displayed projected <contact interaction>. <Momentum conservation> excludes pair conversion between these two modes. Four mixed operator orderings give the coefficient of $n_1n_2$. At fixed <particle number> $N$, the <energy> is $g[N(N-1)+2n_1(N-n_1)]/(2L)$. For repulsive coupling, its minima are the two endpoint <Fock states> with all particles in one mode; a one-particle energy difference selects the lower mode. This ground-state statement concerns the two-mode projection.

= Postulates of quantum mechanics
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

For an isolated quantum system, pure states are normalized rays in a complex <Hilbert space>, observables are <self-adjoint operators>, and their probabilities obey the <Born rule>. A spectral projector $P_a$ has outcome probability $\langle\psi|P_a|\psi\rangle$; an ideal repeatable <projective measurement> has conditional state $P_a\psi/\sqrt{\langle\psi|P_a|\psi\rangle}$. The <Schrödinger equation> specifies continuous unitary evolution between such ideal measurements, given a <Hamiltonian operator>. General measurements need not have the projective state-update rule.

Composite systems use the <tensor product> of their state spaces. Identical particles additionally obey the bosonic or fermionic exchange symmetry appropriate to their species. The <photoelectric effect>, <Davisson–Germer experiment> and <double-slit experiment> motivate energy quanta, matter-wave phases and probability interference, respectively; these experiments constrain the framework but do not separately prove every abstract postulate or specify every system's Hamiltonian.

= Hyperfine multiplet
{parent=Hyperfine structure}

The set of <hyperfine levels> for fixed nuclear spin I and electronic J. Its total angular momenta run from F=|I-J| to F=I+J; each zero-field F level contains 2F+1 magnetic substates. The multiplet is the collection, rather than a synonym for one member level.

= Hyperfine level
{parent=Hyperfine structure}
{title2=$F=|I-J|,\ldots,I+J$}

An <energy level> resolved by coupling the <nuclear spin> $I$ to electronic total <angular momentum> $J$. For a rotationally invariant <Hamiltonian>, a fixed $F$ has $2F+1$ degenerate <magnetic sublevels>. Spectral component counts identify the number of upper <hyperfine levels> only when lower <hyperfine structure>, <selection rules>, unresolved overlaps and missing lines have been accounted for.

= Ground-state hyperfine qubit
{parent=Hyperfine level}

A <qubit> encoded in two resolved <hyperfine levels> of the same electronic <ground state>. Such states avoid optical excited-state decay and can support coherent radiofrequency or microwave control through a <magnetic dipole transition>. Technical magnetic noise and differential shifts can still dephase them; first-order field insensitivity requires additional choices of states and operating point.

= Clock transition
{parent=Hyperfine structure}
{title2=$\partial\omega_{21}/\partial B=0$}

A transition whose <frequency> is stationary to first order in a specified perturbation at an operating point. For magnetic sensitivity, the derivative shown vanishes, but second and higher derivatives can remain nonzero. A <clock transition> need not have zero individual <energy> slopes: equal slopes suffice. Optical light shifts and other perturbations require their own sensitivity analysis.

= Quadratic Zeeman shift of a clock transition
{parent=Clock transition}
{title2=$\Delta\nu=(g_J-g_I)^2\mu_B^2B^2/(2h\Delta_{\rm hf})$}

For the two $m=0$ branches of the <Breit-Rabi formula>, their splitting is $\sqrt{\Delta_{\rm hf}^2+[(g_J-g_I)\mu_BB]^2}$. Expanding at small field gives the quadratic <frequency> shift displayed above. Vanishing linear <frequency> sensitivity at zero field therefore does not eliminate field-gradient <dephasing> at a finite bias field.

= Rubidium-87 clock transition
{parent=Clock transition}
{title2=$|1,0\rangle\leftrightarrow|2,0\rangle$}

The ground-hyperfine magnetic-dipole transition between $F=1,m_F=0$ and $F=2,m_F=0$ has vanishing first-order Zeeman shift near zero field. It obeys $\Delta F=1,\Delta m_F=0$. Its residual quadratic shift follows from the <Breit-Rabi formula>, rather than from assigning both states permanently zero <magnetic dipole moment>.

= Rubidium-87 clock qubit
{c}
{parent=Rubidium-87 clock transition}
{title2=$|0\rangle=|F=1,m_F=0\rangle,\ |1\rangle=|F=2,m_F=0\rangle$}

A <qubit> encoded in the two $m_F=0$ <ground-state> <hyperfine levels> of <rubidium-87>. The electronic $J=1/2$ and <nuclear spin> $I=3/2$ combine to $F=1,2$. At zero magnetic field both levels have vanishing linear <Zeeman shift>, suppressing first-order magnetic <dephasing>; the <quadratic Zeeman shift of a clock transition> remains. Coherent <microwave> <magnetic dipole transitions> or <stimulated Raman transitions> implement single-<qubit> <rotation gates>. These are coupled electron-nucleus states, not bare nuclear-spin states.

= Hyperfine coupling for a spin-one nucleus
{parent=Hyperfine structure}

For <electron spin> $s=1/2$, first add orbital and spin angular momenta to obtain $j=L\pm1/2$. For <nuclear spin> $I=1$, add again to obtain $F=|j-1|,|j-1|+1,\ldots,j+1$. The projection has $2F+1$ possible values. A hydrogenic bound state still requires $L\leq n-1$.

= Hyperfine interaction
{parent=Hyperfine structure}

An interaction between a nucleus's magnetic or electric multipole moments and its electronic surroundings. Magnetic contact and dipolar terms can couple <nuclear spin> to <electron spin> or orbital magnetic moments. The resulting small energy shifts contribute to <hyperfine structure>; the contact term can also mediate <conduction-electron-mediated exchange> between nuclear moments in a metal.

= Electric-quadrupole hyperfine interaction
{parent=Hyperfine interaction}

Coupling of the nuclear electric-quadrupole moment to the electronic electric-field gradient. Its first-order splitting requires appropriate nonzero nuclear and electronic tensor moments; the usual I and J must each be at least one. It can produce deviations from a pure magnetic-dipole <Landé interval rule>.

= Magnetic-dipole hyperfine interaction
{parent=Hyperfine interaction}
{title2=$H_{M1}=A\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J$}

The interaction of a <nuclear magnetic moment> with the electronic magnetic field at the nucleus. Within an isolated electronic J manifold, rotational symmetry gives the displayed effective Hamiltonian for angular momenta in hbar units. Its adjacent F intervals obey the <Landé interval rule>.

= Hyperfine anomaly
{parent=Hyperfine interaction}

A departure of ratios of magnetic-dipole <hyperfine structure> constants from ratios of nuclear <gyromagnetic ratios>. Finite spatial distributions of nuclear charge and magnetization alter electronic contact fields, so even the same nominal electronic <energy level> does not provide an exactly isotope-independent proportionality constant.

= Fermi contact interaction
{c}
{parent=Hyperfine interaction}
{title2=$H_{\rm c}\propto|\psi_s(0)|^2\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf s$}

The isotropic magnetic interaction of a nuclear <magnetic dipole moment> with <electron spin> density at the <atomic nucleus>. A nonrelativistic penetrating $s$ <orbital> can have $|\psi_s(0)|^2\ne0$ and a substantial contact contribution to <hyperfine structure>. A filled paired shell cancels its net contact <spin> density in the independent-particle approximation; unpaired occupation and core polarization can restore it. Relativistic and finite nuclear-size effects modify the point-contact description.

= Breit-Rabi formula
{c}
{parent=Hyperfine interaction}
{title2=$x=(g_J-g_I)\mu_BB/\Delta_{\rm hf}$}

For electronic $J=1/2$, nuclear <spin> $I$ and conserved <angular momentum> component $m$, define $\Delta_{\rm hf}=A(I+1/2)$ and the dimensionless field $x$ shown in the title. The hyperfine-plus-magnetic energies are
$$

= Hegerfeldt theorem
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Let $H$ be a self-adjoint Hamiltonian bounded below, $A$ a bounded positive operator and $p_A(t)=\langle e^{-iHt}\psi,Ae^{-iHt}\psi\rangle$. The <Hegerfeldt theorem> states that either $p_A(t)$ vanishes identically or it is nonzero for almost every real time. In particular, vanishing throughout an open interval forces identically vanishing. The proof uses analytic continuation of $A^{1/2}e^{-iHt}\psi$ into the lower half-plane. Applied to an assumed sharp localization effect, this obstructs strict finite-speed localization for positive-energy one-particle dynamics. It does not identify a controllable superluminal signal: the localization observables and admissible local preparation procedures need separate analysis.

= Rotating-wave approximation
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

After expressing a driven <quantum Hamiltonian> in a suitable interaction or rotating frame, discard terms oscillating rapidly compared with the retained dynamics. Near a selected transition, this leaves resonant terms that accumulate coherently while counter-rotating terms average out to leading order. The drive strength and detuning must be small relative to the discarded oscillation frequencies over the intended regime; strong driving can require the neglected frequency shifts and corrections. The approximation supports <resonant quantum control>, but it is not exact merely because the carrier is tuned to resonance.

= Laboratory spin motion under a resonant linear drive
{parent=Rotating-wave approximation}
{title2=$\mathbf s=(\sin\Omega t\sin\omega_zt,\sin\Omega t\cos\omega_zt,\cos\Omega t)$}

For $H=-\gamma B_zS_z-\gamma B_xS_x\cos(\omega t)$, choose $\omega_z=\gamma B_z>0$ and nominal resonance $\omega=\omega_z$. Starting from up spin, the <rotating-wave approximation> gives the normalized <Bloch vector> in the title, with signed $\Omega=\gamma B_x/2$. It follows by transforming the spinor $(\cos(\Omega t/2),i\sin(\Omega t/2))^T$ from the <interaction picture> back to the <Schrödinger picture>. Thus weak transverse driving combines slow meridional <Rabi oscillations> with fast <Larmor precession>.

This result requires $|B_x|\ll|B_z|$ and cannot be extrapolated as a controlled strong-drive formula. The exact equation is $\dot{\mathbf s}=\gamma\mathbf s\times(B_x\cos\omega t,0,B_z)$. For $|\gamma B_z|t\ll1$, neglecting the bias gives the controlled leading solution
$$
\mathbf s\simeq(0,\sin\Theta,\cos\Theta),\qquad
\Theta=\frac{\gamma B_x}{\omega}\sin\omega t.
$$
The fixed-axis driving generators commute at all times, making this solution exact when the bias is zero at any prescribed drive frequency. At nominal resonance in a strong-drive experiment, the initial rotation scale is $\Theta\simeq\gamma B_xt$, twice the incorrectly extrapolated rotating-wave rate. Bias effects must be retained on longer times.

= Three-mode revival of a planar quantum rotor
{parent=Quantum rotor}
{title2=$T=\pi I/\hbar$}

This is interference between a stationary component and a degenerate pair of counter-rotating components; it is not a uniformly rotating rigid profile. Angular-momentum and energy probabilities stay constant.

= Operator formalism

The <operator formalism> describes a quantum system using states in a <Hilbert space> and observables represented by <self-adjoint operators> with suitable domains on that <Hilbert space>. Evolution and correlation functions are computed from <Hamiltonian operators> and their <time-ordered products>. For free <quantized fields>, the oscillator construction gives a <Fock space>. = Matrix mechanics
{parent=Operator formalism}
{wiki}

<Matrix mechanics> represents an <observable> by an array of <matrix elements> between chosen <quantum states>. In an <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates>, each a <stationary state>, the diagonal entries of the <Hamiltonian operator> are the <energy levels>; off-diagonal entries of other <observables> describe couplings between those states. The same array can therefore answer two different questions: which values can a measurement return, and which pairs of <energy eigenstates> can a specified interaction connect? The <eigenvalues> answer the first question; the relevant <matrix elements>, together with the <energy> differences, answer the second.

This makes <linear algebra> a practical language for <atomic physics>. One can calculate <energy levels>, <electric-dipole selection rules> and <probability amplitudes> for transitions without assigning an <electron> an unobserved classical <orbit>. The three-state example below separates a missing optical coupling from a missing <energy level>; the <finite oscillator commutator boundary defect> explains why a finite array requires care when approximating a <quantum harmonic oscillator>.

= Observable matrices in an energy basis
{parent=Matrix mechanics}
{title2=$A_{mn}=\langle m|A|n\rangle$}

Choose normalized <energy eigenstates> $|n\rangle$ of a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator>, with $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$, and assume first that they form a complete <orthonormal basis>. For a <pure state> and an <observable> $A$,
$$
|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle,\qquad A_{mn}=\langle m|A|n\rangle,\qquad (A\psi)_m=\sum_n A_{mn}c_n,\qquad \langle A\rangle=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*A_{mn}c_n.
$$
The column index $n$ labels the input <basis vector>, and row $m$ labels the output component. <Self-adjointness> gives $A_{nm}=A_{mn}^*$, so a finite example is a <Hermitian matrix>. A <matrix element> can be negative or complex; by itself it is not generally a <probability>. The <Born rule> assigns measurement <probabilities> using the <eigenstates> of $A$, which need not be <energy eigenstates>. For an unbounded <observable>, these expressions require a suitable <operator domain>; a system with <continuous spectrum> also needs the corresponding spectral integrals.

= Transition frequencies and matrix multiplication
{parent=Observable matrices in an energy basis}
{title2=$\omega_{mn}=(E_m-E_n)/\hbar$}

In the <Heisenberg picture>, an <observable> without explicit time dependence has
$$
A_H(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}Ae^{-iHt/\hbar},\qquad (A_H)_{mn}(t)=A_{mn}e^{i\omega_{mn}t},\qquad \omega_{mn}=\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}.
$$
Here $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. An emitted <photon> on a transition $n\to m$ with $E_n>E_m$ has positive <angular frequency> $(E_n-E_m)/\hbar=|\omega_{mn}|$. The sign of the array's <quantum phase> follows its index convention; it is not a negative measured <photon> <frequency>. The frequencies involve differences of <energy levels>, rather than the absolute values $E_n/\hbar$. Adding a constant to $H$ leaves these frequencies unchanged.

Insert the complete <orthonormal basis> between two <linear operators>:
$$
(AB)_{mn}=\sum_k A_{mk}B_{kn},\qquad \omega_{mk}+\omega_{kn}=\omega_{mn}.
$$
The sum runs over intermediate states. The second identity follows by cancellation of $E_k$; it makes every term in the product acquire the same time factor $e^{i\omega_{mn}t}$. This is a useful reason for <matrix multiplication>: it composes the two indexed arrays consistently with the addition of transition frequencies. It does not multiply matching entries independently. Swapping $A$ and $B$ generally changes the intermediate-state sum and hence the result.

= Three-level observable and missing spectral line
{parent=Observable matrices in an energy basis}

Consider an original ideal <quantum system> with positive scales $\epsilon,d$ and
$$
H=\epsilon\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=d\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&2\\0&2&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Interpret $D$ as one component of an <electric dipole moment>, so its coupling to the corresponding <electric field> is the <electric-dipole interaction> $-\mathcal E(t)D$. The gaps are $2\epsilon,3\epsilon,5\epsilon$, but $D_{02}=0$: this interaction has no direct first-order $2\leftrightarrow0$ coupling. The nonzero squared <matrix elements> are $|D_{01}|^2=d^2$ and $|D_{12}|^2=4d^2$. These are coupling weights; actual <spectral line> intensities also depend on populations and radiation factors. In particular, there is no promise that an experiment populates the upper state of every allowed transition.

A measurement of $D$ itself has possible outcomes $0,\sqrt5d,-\sqrt5d$, since

= Noncommuting observables in two dimensions
{parent=Matrix mechanics}
{title2=$[A,B]=AB-BA$}

For two dimensionless <observables> on a <two-level system>, take
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Both are <Hermitian matrices>: $A$ has <eigenvalues> $1,0$ and $B$ has <eigenvalues> $1,-1$. Yet
$$
AB=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad BA=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad [A,B]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\ne0.
$$
For instance, $AB|0\rangle=0$ whereas $BA|0\rangle=|1\rangle$. In $AB$, $B$ acts first. Neither product is <self-adjoint>, so the product of two <observables> is not automatically another <observable>. The <commutator> records this dependence on order. These operator products by themselves do not specify the outcomes of consecutive measurements; that additionally requires <measurement in quantum mechanics>.

= From Heisenberg arrays to Born-Jordan commutation
{parent=Matrix mechanics}

In 1925, <Werner Heisenberg> proposed replacing classical orbital quantities with arrays of transition amplitudes and frequencies. His paper's opening demanded relations between quantities observable in principle; section 1 built products by summing over an intermediate state and noted their dependence on order. The formulas above express that construction in modern <linear operator> notation.<On the quantum reinterpretation of kinematical and mechanical relationships by Werner Heisenberg>[Heisenberg, 1925; introduction and section 1, equations (5), (8), (15), (20), (24)–(25); Doria translation]{ref}

Pumping and a heated bath prepared <mercury> vapor around a hot <platinum> wire, mesh and collector in the original <Franck-Hertz experiment>. A <galvanometer> read collector <electric current> while accelerating <voltage> varied at fixed retarding <voltage>. Retarding scans distinguished outgoing <electron> energies.<Über Zusammenstöße zwischen Elektronen und den Molekülen des Quecksilberdampfes und die Ionisierungsspannung desselben by James Franck and Gustav Ludwig Hertz>[1914, pp. 459–461, Figs. 1–2]{ref} The losses constrain excitation of <chemical atoms>; they do not determine every optical coupling. The <Franck-Hertz experiment> explains the original mistaken <ionization> interpretation.

Such excitation and <spectral line> evidence motivated a mechanics of discrete <energy levels> and transitions. Replacing one representation by another was a theoretical construction; the <Franck-Hertz experiment> does not measure a <matrix product> or establish a unique representation of an <observable>.

Later in 1925, <Max Born> and <Pascual Jordan> developed the array calculus systematically. Their section 3 states $pq-qp=hI/(2\pi i)$, equivalently the modern <canonical commutation relation>
$$
[X,P]=i\hbar I.
$$
This relates <position> and <momentum> through an intrinsically noncommuting product.<Zur Quantenmechanik by Max Born and Pascual Jordan>[Born and Jordan, Zur Quantenmechanik, 1925; section 3, equation (38), translation p. 14]{ref}

For the <quantum harmonic oscillator>, the <canonical commutation relation> and <creation and annihilation operators> give $H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+I/2)$, recovering both the spacing and <zero-point energy> algebraically. One must retain infinitely many <number states> for the exact relation: the finite cutoff below reveals the obstruction explicitly.

= Matrix and wave mechanics equivalence
{parent=Matrix mechanics}

In 1926, <Erwin Schrödinger> constructed arrays from differential operators and complete orthogonal function systems, showing how their products reproduce the array calculus. His paper connected <wave mechanics> with <matrix mechanics>; the translation's sections 2–3 develop the product and commutation rules.<On the Relation between the Quantum Mechanics of Heisenberg, Born, and Jordan, and that of Schrödinger by Erwin Schrödinger>[Collected Papers on Wave Mechanics, On the Relation between the Quantum Mechanics of Heisenberg, Born, and Jordan, and that of Schrödinger, pp. 48–51, equations (6)–(10)]{ref}

The modern proof is a change of <orthonormal basis>. If $\phi_n(x)$ form a complete <orthonormal basis> of the physical <Hilbert space>, map a <wavefunction> to its coefficients:
$$
c_n=\int\phi_n(x)^*\psi(x)\,dx,\qquad A_{mn}=\int\phi_m(x)^*(A\phi_n)(x)\,dx.
$$
Completeness makes this map <unitary>: it preserves the <inner product> and reconstructs $\psi$ from its coefficients. Inserting the complete <orthonormal basis> proves the <matrix multiplication> rule. The <Schrödinger equation> becomes
$$
i\hbar\dot c_m=\sum_nH_{mn}c_n.
$$
For a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator>, the <Heisenberg picture> transfers the same evolution to the <observables>, preserving measurement predictions. <Unitary equivalence> requires matching <operator domains> as well as formal entries. A finite selection of <eigenfunctions> gives a compression, not an exact replacement of the full <Hilbert space>; a <continuous spectrum> needs its spectral integrals. This equivalence concerns representations of specified operators, and does not remove the ordering question when quantizing an arbitrary classical expression, as <Weyl ordering> illustrates.

= Finite oscillator commutator boundary defect
{parent=Matrix mechanics}
{title2=$[X_N,P_N]=i\hbar(I_N-N|N-1\rangle\langle N-1|)$}

Retain the first $N\ge2$ <number states> of a <quantum harmonic oscillator>. Let $\Pi_N$ be the <orthogonal projection> onto their span and compress $X_N=\Pi_NX\Pi_N$, $P_N=\Pi_NP\Pi_N$, restricted to this span. The compressed <annihilation operator> $a_N$ satisfies
$$
a_N|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle,\qquad a_N^\dagger|N-1\rangle=0,\qquad [a_N,a_N^\dagger]=I_N-N|N-1\rangle\langle N-1|.
$$
For $n<N-1$, the two products give $n+1$ and $n$; on the top state they give $0$ and $N-1$. Thus, with $\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega)}$,
$$
X_N=\frac{\ell}{\sqrt2}(a_N+a_N^\dagger),\qquad P_N=\frac{i\hbar}{\sqrt2\ell}(a_N^\dagger-a_N),\qquad [X_N,P_N]=i\hbar(I_N-N|N-1\rangle\langle N-1|).
$$
On every <quantum superposition> of $|0\rangle,\ldots,|N-2\rangle$, this <commutator> acts exactly as $i\hbar I_N$. For a normalized retained state with coefficients $c_n$,
$$
\frac{\langle[X_N,P_N]\rangle}{i\hbar}=1-N|c_{N-1}|^2.
$$
Small probability at the cutoff must therefore be judged relative to $1/N$ for this expectation. For $N=8$, the diagonal of $[X_N,P_N]/(i\hbar)$ is $(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,-7)$, with zero <trace>.

This is unavoidable for finite <matrices>: <trace of a matrix commutator> gives $\operatorname{tr}(XP-PX)=0$, whereas $\operatorname{tr}(i\hbar I_N)=i\hbar N\ne0$. Compression does not preserve products: $\Pi_NXP\Pi_N$ can use intermediate states outside the retained span, while $X_NP_N$ cannot. Accordingly, compressing the full oscillator <Hamiltonian operator> gives $\Pi_NH\Pi_N=\hbar\omega(a_N^\dagger a_N+I_N/2)$, but building it from the compressed squares gives
$$
\frac{P_N^2}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^2X_N^2}{2}=\Pi_NH\Pi_N-\frac{N\hbar\omega}{2}|N-1\rangle\langle N-1|.
$$
The two prescriptions agree on lower <number states> and disagree at the boundary. In the infinite oscillator, the <canonical commutation relation> holds on the common invariant <dense subspace> of finite <number-state> combinations. Its unbounded <position> and <momentum operators> do not admit the finite <trace> argument; writing the formal identity does not assert that both products act on every vector of the <Hilbert space>.

= Quasiparticle
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A quasiparticle is a collective excitation that can be described approximately by particle-like quantum numbers and dynamics, even though it is not an isolated elementary particle. <Excitons> and <polaritons> are examples.

= Bogoliubov quasiparticle
{c}
{parent=Quasiparticle}

A Bogoliubov <quasiparticle> is an excitation created by the diagonal mode operator obtained through a <Bogoliubov transformation>. Its vacuum can contain particles in the original modes because creation and <annihilation operators> mix. Examples include <antiferromagnetic spin waves>, Bose-gas <phonons> and paired-fermion excitations.

= Fermion number of a Bogoliubov excitation
{parent=Bogoliubov quasiparticle}
{title2=$\Delta\langle N\rangle=\epsilon/E$}

For a normalized spinless paired <ground state> $u|0\rangle+i v c_p^\dagger c_{-p}^\dagger|0\rangle$, its mean <fermion number> is $2v^2$. The canonical <Bogoliubov quasiparticle> <creation operator> produces $c_p^\dagger|0\rangle$, containing exactly one fermion. With <BCS coherence factors> $v^2=(1-\epsilon/E)/2$, the change in mean <fermion number> is $1-2v^2=\epsilon/E$. This need not be an integer because the pairing <Hamiltonian> preserves <fermion parity> while failing to conserve the original <fermion number operator>. Every measurement of that <number operator> still has integer outcomes.

= Polariton
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

A <polariton> is a hybrid light–matter excitation formed by strong coupling between a <photon> and a material excitation. For an exciton-polariton, the material component is an <exciton>.

= Phonon polariton
{parent=Polariton}

A phonon polariton is a normal mode mixing an electromagnetic field with a polar optical lattice vibration. In a lossless isotropic oscillator model, $c^2k^2=\omega^2\epsilon_\infty(\omega_{\rm LO}^2-\omega^2)/(\omega_{\rm TO}^2-\omega^2)$. With $u=c^2k^2/\epsilon_\infty$, its two transverse branches obey $\omega_\pm^2=[\omega_{\rm LO}^2+u\pm\sqrt{(\omega_{\rm LO}^2+u)^2-4u\omega_{\rm TO}^2}]/2$. The nearly flat longitudinal optical mode is distinct from the upper transverse branch.

= Exciton
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

An <exciton> is a <bound state> of an <electron> and an <electron hole>. Their opposite <electric charges> attract through the <Coulomb potential>, producing a neutral <quasiparticle> with internal relative motion and motion of its <center of mass>. In a <semiconductor>, promoting an <electron> from the <valence band> into the <conduction band> leaves an <electron hole>; the pair can occupy either an <exciton> <bound state> or an unbound <scattering state>. Merely creating the two <charge carriers> does not establish binding. A neutral <exciton> can transport excitation <energy> without transporting net <electric charge>. Coupling this material excitation strongly to a <photon> can form a <polariton>.

The striking optical consequence is that an <exciton optical transition> can absorb a <photon> with less <energy> than the threshold for creating unbound <electrons> and <electron holes>. The difference is the <exciton binding energy>, not an extra one-electron <energy band> inside the <band gap>. The <Wannier-Mott exciton> supplies a useful large-radius model; the <Frenkel exciton> describes the opposite, tightly localized limit.<Excitons in Semiconductor Quantum Dots by Matjaž Humar>[§3, printed pp. 4–6: bound pairs, effective masses and localized contrast]{ref}

= Exciton diffusion length
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$L_D$}

A characteristic transport length before <exciton> decay, commonly $L_D=\sqrt{D_X\tau_X}$ in a diffusion-decay convention. A root-mean-square displacement over a fixed time has dimension-dependent factors and is not automatically this same convention.

= Electron-hole Coulomb interaction
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$V(r)=-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r)$}

The attractive electrostatic interaction between an <electron> and an <electron hole>. In a screened effective-mass approximation its potential has the displayed form. It can bind an <exciton> and reduce its optical excitation energy below the corresponding unbound-pair threshold.

= Exciton reduced mass
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$\mu^*$}

The reduced band mass for relative electron–hole motion, $\mu^*=m_e^*m_h^*/(m_e^*+m_h^*)$. It differs from the total mass that controls centre-of-mass motion.

= Biexciton
{parent=Exciton}

A <biexciton> is a two-<electron>, two-<electron-hole> excitation. If $E_X$ and $E_{XX}$ are total excitation <energies> relative to the empty emitter, the cascade $XX\to X\to0$ emits photons of <energies> $E_{XX}-E_X$ and $E_X$. Define its binding energy by $B_{XX}=2E_X-E_{XX}$. The first cascade photon then has <energy> $E_X-B_{XX}$. Positive binding shifts that line below the single-<exciton> line; confinement can also produce negative binding, shifting it above. The cascade does not emit a single photon of twice the <exciton> energy.

= Exciton binding energy
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$E_B=E_{\rm cont}-E_X>0$}

The <exciton binding energy> is the positive <energy> needed to separate a bound <electron> and <electron hole> into the relevant unbound <scattering states>. Compare the <exciton> <energy> $E_X$ with the continuum threshold $E_{\rm cont}$ at the same total <crystal momentum> and for the same <energy bands>. The negative internal <energy eigenvalue> $-E_B$ and the positive separation cost $E_B$ are different sign conventions for related quantities. An <exciton> may move freely through a perfect crystal while remaining internally bound: “free exciton” does not mean an unbound pair.

= Thermal ionization of excitons
{parent=Exciton binding energy}

Thermal <energy> comparable with an <exciton binding energy> promotes dissociation and often washes out narrow bound-exciton resonances. Comparing $k_BT$ with binding <energy> is a scale estimate, not an exact disappearance criterion: densities, screening, oscillator strength and linewidths also matter.

= Wannier exciton
{c}
{synonym}

A <Wannier-Mott exciton> has an internal <wave function> extending over many <unit cells>. Its simplest model assumes a three-dimensional bulk crystal, approximately parabolic <valence band> and <conduction band>, positive isotropic scalar <effective masses> $m_e^*,m_h^*$, a constant scalar <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r$, and a dilute pair population. Replace microscopic crystal forces by those <effective masses> and <dielectric screening>, and neglect exchange corrections and coupling to <phonons>. These assumptions must hold on the spatial and <energy> scales of the particular <exciton>.

With relative coordinate $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_e-\mathbf r_h$, the internal <Hamiltonian operator> is the three-dimensional <hydrogen atom> problem with a <reduced mass> and a screened <Coulomb potential>:
$$
H_{\rm rel}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla_{\mathbf r}^2-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r},\qquad
\mu=\frac{m_e^*m_h^*}{m_e^*+m_h^*},\qquad M=m_e^*+m_h^*.
$$
Here $e$ is the <elementary charge>, $\epsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>, and $\hbar$ the reduced <Planck constant>. The internal <energy eigenvalues>, radius scale, and <exciton binding energies> are
$$
a_B^*=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\hbar^2}{\mu e^2},\qquad
R^*=\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)^2\hbar^2},\qquad
E_{{\rm rel},n}=-\frac{R^*}{n^2},\qquad E_{B,n}=\frac{R^*}{n^2}.
$$
The <principal quantum number> is $n=1,2,\ldots$. This reduction and these scales are derived in <Excitons in Semiconductor Quantum Dots by Matjaž Humar> (§3, printed pp. 5–6, equations (13)–(26)).

To check the scales by <dimensional analysis>, write $C=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)$, which has units of <energy> times <length>. Then $a_B^*=\hbar^2/(\mu C)$ is a <length> and $R^*=\mu C^2/(2\hbar^2)$ is an <energy>. Rescaling $r=a_B^*\rho$ makes $H_{\rm rel}/R^*=-\nabla_\rho^2-2/\rho$, the dimensionless <hydrogen atom> operator. The <Bohr radius> $a_B^*$ is a <wave-function> scale, not the radius of a classical orbit; for this model's lowest internal <bound state>, the mean separation is $3a_B^*/2$.

For the diagrams' hypothetical inputs $m_e^*=0.10m_0$, $m_h^*=0.40m_0$, $\epsilon_r=10$ and $E_g=1.500\,\mathrm{eV}$, where $m_0$ is the free <electron> mass, independent substitution gives $\mu=0.080m_0$, $a_B^*\simeq6.615\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $R^*\simeq10.885\,\mathrm{meV}$. Here <electronvolts> express <energy>. The <exciton binding energies> for $n=1,2,3$ are approximately $10.885$, $2.721$ and $1.209\,\mathrm{meV}$. These are model calculations, not measured material parameters. The large-radius approximation additionally requires $a_B^*$ to be much larger than the crystal's <unit-cell> length scale. In a thin layer, with anisotropic <effective masses>, or with spatially varying <dielectric screening>, this three-dimensional formula is not generally valid.

= Finite-width quantum-well exciton
{parent=Wannier-Mott exciton}
{title2=$V(\rho)=-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}\int\frac{|\chi_e(z_e)|^2|\chi_h(z_h)|^2}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z_e-z_h)^2}}\,dz_e\,dz_h$}

In the envelope approximation, a <quantum well> confines <electron> and <hole> motion perpendicular to the plane. Averaging their screened attraction over the confinement envelopes gives the displayed effective in-plane potential. Its narrow-well limit approaches the ideal <two-dimensional exciton>; finite width softens the short-distance attraction. Dielectric mismatch, nonparabolic bands and strong <electron>–<hole> confinement mixing require more than this single-subband model.

= Two-dimensional exciton
{parent=Wannier-Mott exciton}

In the ideal screened-Coulomb model confined to a plane, the relative-motion energies are $-R^*/(\nu-1/2)^2$ for $\nu=1,2,\ldots$, where $R^*=(\mu/m_e)13.6\,\mathrm{eV}/\varepsilon_r^2$. The ground-state binding is $4R^*$ rather than the three-dimensional value $R^*$. Finite-width wells need not be exactly planar.

The first two optically active hydrogenic levels have separation $32R^*/9$ in the planar model, versus $3R^*/4$ in the bulk model. The separation cancels an uncertain common continuum threshold, making it useful when rounded band parameters do not reproduce every absolute peak <energy>.

The characteristic relative-motion length of a hydrogenic <Wannier-Mott exciton>, with dielectric screening and <exciton reduced mass> $\mu^*$. Comparing a <quantum dot>'s dimensions with this scale helps distinguish strong carrier confinement from confinement of a comparatively intact bound pair.

= Exciton Rydberg series

The hydrogenic sequence of bound relative electron–hole energies $E_n=E_g-R^*/n^2$ below the free-pair threshold, where $R^*=(\mu^*/m_e)R_{\mathrm H}/\varepsilon_r^2$. The effective-mass, screened-Coulomb approximation and optical selection rules limit the model.

= Relative permittivity
{parent=Wannier-Mott exciton}
{title2=$\epsilon_r=\epsilon/\epsilon_0$}
{wiki}

The dimensionless ratio of a medium's <permittivity> to <vacuum permittivity>. The constant scalar value used in a <Wannier-Mott exciton> is an approximation to the relevant material response.

= Dielectric function
{parent=Relative permittivity}
{title2=$\varepsilon(\mathbf q,\omega)$}

The generally complex, frequency- and wavevector-dependent <relative permittivity> describing linear <polarization> response. Its imaginary part represents dissipative response with the sign fixed by the time convention; optical prefactors connect it to an absorption coefficient.

= Static Lindhard dielectric function
{parent=Dielectric function}
{title2=$\epsilon(q,0)=1+\kappa^2F(q/2k_F)/q^2$}

The static independent-electron density response of a uniform three-dimensional, two-spin <Fermi gas>, combined with self-consistent <Poisson equation> screening, gives the displayed <dielectric function>. For bare electron mass and vacuum background, $\kappa^2=m_ee^2k_F/(\epsilon_0\pi^2\hbar^2)$ and
$$
F(x)=\frac12+\frac{1-x^2}{4x}\ln\left|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right|.
$$
Here $F(0)=1$, $F(1)=1/2$ by continuous extension, and $F(x)\sim1/(3x^2)$ at large $x$. A host dielectric background and <band effective mass> require replacing the vacuum parameters appropriately. This nonlocal response contains <Friedel oscillations>, which its long-wavelength <Thomas-Fermi screening> limit misses.

= Kohn singularity of static Lindhard screening
{parent=Static Lindhard dielectric function}
{c}
{title2=$F(1+u)=\tfrac12+\tfrac{u}{2}\ln|u|+O(u)$}

The three-dimensional static screening function is finite at $q=2k_F$, but its <derivative> diverges logarithmically. Expanding the explicit <static Lindhard dielectric function> gives the title's nonanalytic term. The singularity comes from particle–hole excitations connecting opposite parts of the <Fermi surface>. It produces a <Friedel density tail from a derivative singularity>; analogous electronic response can cause a <Kohn anomaly> in a coupled lattice vibration.

= Dielectric screening
{parent=Relative permittivity}

Modification of the interaction between <electric charges> by a medium's electrical response. In the simplest <Wannier-Mott exciton> model, the <Coulomb potential> is reduced by a constant <relative permittivity>; actual screening can depend on spatial and temporal scales.

= Radial induced-charge screening kernel
{parent=Dielectric screening}
{title2=$g(q)=q[1-\epsilon(q)^{-1}]/(2\pi^2)$}

For a point source $Ze$ and the <Fourier transform> convention $\rho(\mathbf q)=\int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\rho(\mathbf r)d^3r$, self-consistent screening gives $\rho_{\rm ind}(q)=Ze[\epsilon(q)^{-1}-1]$. Angular inversion yields $\rho_{\rm ind}(r)=-(Ze/r)\int_0^\infty g(q)\sin(qr)dq$. The factor $1/(2\pi^2)$ includes the inverse-transform normalization and spherical angular integral. Perfect long-wavelength screening implies total induced charge $-Ze$.

= Friedel oscillations
{c}
{parent=Dielectric screening}
{wiki}

The sharp <Fermi surface> gives a nonlocal oscillatory electronic response around an impurity. In a three-dimensional isotropic metal its far-field oscillations have characteristic wavevector $2k_F$ and algebraically decaying amplitude. They are absent from the purely exponential <Thomas-Fermi screening> potential. The local screening formula is a useful long-wavelength model, not an exact asymptotic expression at every distance.

= Friedel oscillation wavelength from carrier density
{parent=Friedel oscillations}
{c}
{title2=$\lambda=\pi/(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$}

A single isotropic three-dimensional valley with two equal <spin> populations has $n=k_F^3/(3\pi^2)$. A density oscillation at <wavevector> $2k_F$ therefore has the wavelength in the title. This depends on <carrier density>, independently of effective mass and dielectric screening strength. Extra valley degeneracy, spin polarization or reduced dimensionality changes the state-counting relation.

= Friedel density tail from a derivative singularity
{parent=Friedel oscillations}
{c}
{title2=$g''(q)\sim C/(q-Q)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho_{\rm ind}(r)\sim ZeC\pi\cos(Qr)/r^3$}

For the <radial induced-charge screening kernel>, integrate the sine transform twice by parts, with $g(0)=g(\infty)=0$ and vanishing remaining endpoint terms. This gives $\rho_{\rm ind}=Ze\int g''(q)\sin(qr)dq/r^3$. A <Cauchy principal value> singularity at $Q>0$ contributes $C\pi\cos(Qr)$ to that integral: set $s=q-Q$ and use $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin(sr)ds/s=\pi$ for $r>0$. Smoothly localizing around $Q$ justifies the large-$r$ extension of the limits. In a three-dimensional isotropic <Fermi gas>, $Q=2k_F$.

= Thomas-Fermi screening
{c}
{parent=Dielectric screening}
{wiki=Thomas–Fermi_screening}

In the long-wavelength static response of a degenerate conductor, a small electric potential $\phi$ changes the electron density by $\delta n=e g(E_F)\phi$, where $g(E_F)$ is the spin-summed <density of states> per volume per energy. The induced charge is therefore $-e^2g(E_F)\phi$. With background <permittivity> $\varepsilon_b$, <Poisson equation> becomes
$$
(\nabla^2-k_{\rm TF}^2)\phi=-\rho_{\rm ext}/\varepsilon_b,\qquad
k_{\rm TF}^2=e^2g(E_F)/\varepsilon_b.
$$
A point charge $Q$ then has $\phi(r)=Qe^{-k_{\rm TF}r}/(4\pi\varepsilon_b r)$. Larger electronic density of states gives stronger screening. This local response approximation does not capture the microscopic oscillatory tail of <Friedel oscillations>.https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/em/el6.pdf[Sections 7.7.3–7.7.5: Thomas-Fermi screening and its nonlocal corrections]{ref}

= Thomas-Fermi screening length
{c}
{parent=Thomas-Fermi screening}
{title2=$\lambda_{\rm TF}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_b/[e^2g(E_F)]}$}

The <Thomas-Fermi screening length> is the exponential decay scale of a statically screened potential in the local degenerate-electron approximation. For a three-dimensional parabolic <free electron model>, $g(E_F)=3n/(2E_F)$, so $\lambda_{\rm TF}^2=2\varepsilon_b E_F/(3ne^2)$. At fixed mass and background permittivity it scales as $n^{-1/6}$, rather than the classical Debye density power at fixed temperature.

= Frenkel exciton
{c}
{parent=Exciton}

A <Frenkel exciton> is an <exciton> whose internal excitation is concentrated on the scale of one molecule or <unit cell>. The local excitation can transfer between sites; localized internal structure does not require an immobile <center of mass>. = Exciton optical transition
{parent=Exciton}
{title2=$E_{\rm opt}\simeq E_g-E_B$}

$$
E_{X,n}(\mathbf K)=E_g-\frac{R^*}{n^2}+\frac{\hbar^2K^2}{2M}.
$$
Here $\hbar\mathbf K$ is the pair's total <crystal momentum>; the last term is <center-of-mass> <kinetic energy>. For a permitted <photon absorption> transition with negligible recoil <energy>, $\hbar\omega\simeq E_g-E_{B,n}$. An <electric-dipole selection rule> and a nonzero transition <matrix element> are still needed: a <bound state> need not produce an observable <spectral line>. If the relevant <energy-band> edges have different values of <crystal momentum>, a <phonon> can also be needed to conserve <crystal momentum>; subtracting a binding scale from a <band gap> alone does not specify the optical threshold.

= Electron hole
{parent=Quasiparticle}
{wiki}

= Hole
{synonym}

An <electron hole> is an unoccupied <electron> <quantum state> in an otherwise nearly full <energy band>, often the <valence band>. “Filled” refers to occupation of the allowed <quantum states>, not to filling all space with a continuous fluid of <electrons>. Each state, including its <spin> label, can hold at most one <electron> by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. The almost full <energy band> and its few missing occupants are therefore simpler to describe as a background plus a few <electron holes>. The <electron hole> is a <quasiparticle>, not a <positron> inserted into the crystal.

Removing an <electron> of <electric charge> $-e$ changes the background's <electric charge> by $+e$. Thus an <electron hole> carries positive <electric charge> relative to that background. Promoting an <electron> into the <conduction band> creates an <electron hole> without changing the whole crystal's <electric charge>: $(-e)+(+e)=0$. An unbound pair still consists of two separate <charge carriers>; only a <bound state> of their relative motion is an <exciton>.

\Image[electron-hole-band-occupation.png]
{title=<Energy-band> occupation and the motion and filling of an <electron hole>}
{description=Original occupation schematic. Blue filled circles are occupied spin-resolved states; open circles are empty states. A promotion conserves the displayed <electron> count while changing occupation of the <valence band> and <conduction band>. The lower, localized cartoon shows an <electron> filling a neighboring vacancy so that the vacancy moves oppositely, and a <conduction-band> <electron> filling a <valence-band> <electron hole>. The local cartoon explains why a vacancy can move: when a neighboring <electron> fills it, another vacancy appears where that <electron> was. The vacancy has moved opposite to that particular transfer of an <electron>. In an extended <Bloch state>, use <crystal momentum> instead of imagining a permanently empty atomic site. For a completely filled <energy band> the summed <electric current> vanishes. Omitting one <electron> with <group velocity> $\mathbf v_v$ removes its contribution $-e\mathbf v_v$, leaving a contribution $+e\mathbf v_v$. This fixes both the positive <electric charge> and the velocity convention of the <electron hole>.

More precisely, near a parabolic <valence-band> maximum, set its <energy> $E_v(0)$ as reference and define the <electron-hole> <wave vector> by $\mathbf k_h=-\mathbf k_v$. Then
$$
E_h(\mathbf k_h)=E_v(0)-E_v(-\mathbf k_h),\qquad
\mathbf v_h=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\mathbf k_h}E_h=\mathbf v_v(-\mathbf k_h),\qquad
E_h(\mathbf k_h)\simeq\frac{\hbar^2k_h^2}{2m_h^*}.
$$
The occupied <electron> branch has negative curvature there, while the <electron-hole> branch has positive <effective mass> $m_h^*$. The velocity equality compares the <electron hole> with the missing <Bloch state>'s <group velocity>; it does not contradict the opposite vacancy motion in a local transfer cartoon. The sign conventions are listed in <Phys 460, Fall 2006/Phys 460 Lecture 17>.

= Heavy hole
{parent=Electron hole}

A hole in the upper valence manifold associated near a chosen propagation axis with the $j=3/2$, $m_j=\pm3/2$ branch. Its band curvature is relatively small, hence its <effective mass> relatively large. The label is direction dependent in a general anisotropic band structure.

= Electron-hole pair
{parent=Electron hole}

An <electron-hole pair> consists of an excited <electron> and the unoccupied valence state described as an <electron hole>. Promoting a valence <electron> across a <semiconductor> <band gap> creates such a pair without changing the total <electric charge>. The carriers can separate under an <electric field>, or undergo <electron-hole recombination>. A bound electron-hole pair is an <exciton>; an arbitrary unbound pair need not be an exciton.

= Electron-hole recombination
{parent=Electron hole}

An <electron> filling an <electron hole> removes both excitations relative to the filled-band background. Their <electric charges> cancel, consistent with <charge conservation>; the remaining <electrons> are still present. The released <energy> can leave through <photon emission> or other channels, including <phonons>. Formation of an <exciton> instead retains the excited <electron> and <electron hole> as a bound pair; recombination removes the pair. A <valence-band> <electron> filling a nearby vacancy merely moves the <electron hole> and does not remove the total vacancy count.

= Minority carrier lifetime
{parent=Electron-hole recombination}
{title2=$\partial_t\Delta n=-\Delta n/\tau$}

In a weak-injection regime where the majority population is nearly fixed, a small excess minority <charge carrier> population often relaxes exponentially with time constant $\tau$. This lifetime describes carrier removal by recombination. It differs from the <momentum relaxation time> governing <charge carrier mobility>: many momentum-scattering events can occur before a carrier recombines. Traps and spatial transport can make a single exponential inadequate.

= Radiative electron-hole recombination
{parent=Electron-hole recombination}
{title2=$R_{\rm net}=B(np-n_i^2)$}

In a <semiconductor>, a conduction <electron> fills a vacant valence state and emits a <photon>. In a simple nondegenerate model the recombination rate is $Bnp$, while the equilibrium reverse generation rate is $Bn_i^2$; their difference gives the displayed net pair-loss rate. This subtraction enforces <detailed balance> at thermal equilibrium. Photon momentum is small on the scale of a <Brillouin zone>, so a <direct band gap> permits a direct transition while an <indirect band gap> generally requires a <phonon> or another source of momentum.

= Auger recombination
{c}
{parent=Electron-hole recombination}

In Auger recombination an electron-hole pair annihilates while transferring its energy to another electron or hole rather than to an emitted photon. The hot recipient subsequently loses energy, commonly to phonons. In a simple nondegenerate carrier-density model the rate is $R_A=C_n n^2p+C_pnp^2$, with electron and hole number densities $n,p$ and material-dependent coefficients. This makes the process especially important at high carrier injection. It is the reverse type of energy transfer to <impact ionization>, although detailed thresholds and matrix elements depend on the bands.

= Valence band
{parent=Electron hole}

In the simple <semiconductor> description, the highest occupied <energy band> of the unexcited crystal at zero <temperature>. A missing occupant is an <electron hole>.

= Split-off valence band
{parent=Valence band}

<Spin-orbit coupling> separates the $j=1/2$ valence states from the upper $j=3/2$ manifold at the zone centre in common cubic <semiconductors>. If its maximum lies $\Delta_{\rm so}>0$ below the upper <valence-band> edge, its thermal <electron-hole> population is suppressed by $e^{-\Delta_{\rm so}/(k_BT)}$ apart from <effective density of states> factors. Neglect requires $k_BT\ll\Delta_{\rm so}$; it is not valid at every <temperature> or in every material.

= Heavy-hole and light-hole bands
{parent=Valence band}

Near the zone centre of many cubic <semiconductors>, the upper <valence bands> have different curvatures and hence different <band effective masses>. In the isotropic approximation, the $j=3/2$ states with angular-momentum projection magnitude $3/2$ along the propagation direction form the heavy-hole branch; projection magnitude $1/2$ gives the light-hole branch. A flatter downward electron dispersion corresponds to a larger positive <electron-hole> effective mass. Crystal anisotropy, strain and confinement can mix these branches, so the labels do not imply direction-independent exact masses.

= Conduction band
{parent=Electron hole}

The initially empty <energy band> above the <band gap> in the simple <semiconductor> description. Excited <electrons> in it can carry <electric current>; they can also bind to <valence-band> <electron holes> as <excitons>.

= Conduction electron
{parent=Conduction band}

An <electron> occupying an extended state in a <conduction band>. Its response to an applied <electric field> depends on its band <group velocity>, occupation and scattering. Merely counting such electrons does not by itself determine <electrical conductivity>.

= Sudden approximation
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

The <sudden approximation> treats a rapid, finite change of a <Hamiltonian operator> as leaving the <quantum state> approximately unchanged during the switch. The <energy> measurement can nevertheless change immediately: the same <quantum state> has different <probability amplitudes> in the new <eigenbasis>. Compute their <inner products> with the new <energy eigenstates>, then apply the <Born rule>. The <two-level sudden quench> below makes this distinction quantitative without assuming the change is small.

An ideal finite step has exact <quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step>; a finite-duration implementation has a small evolution error under the bounded-model conditions below. An <impulsive quantum kick> is different: its <Hamiltonian operator> grows as its duration shrinks, retaining a finite integrated <action> and a finite change of <quantum state>. A <sudden approximation> therefore concerns short evolution during switching, not <conservation of energy> during externally driven change. The subsequent <unitary time evolution> can be substantial.<Fluctuation theorems: Work is not an observable by Peter Talkner and others>[Talkner, Lutz and Hänggi, p. 2, paragraph before equation (7): sudden switch gives the identity time-evolution operator]{ref}

= One-sided sudden expansion of an infinite square well
{parent=Sudden approximation}
{title2=$P_1=32/(9\pi^2)$}

A normalized <ground state> initially supported on $(0,a)$ is $\sqrt{2/a}\sin(\pi x/a)$. Expanding the <infinite square well> to $(0,2a)$ leaves this <wavefunction> unchanged during an ideal sudden switch. Its overlap with the new <ground state> $a^{-1/2}\sin(\pi x/(2a))$ is $4\sqrt2/(3\pi)$, so the <Born rule> gives $32/(9\pi^2)$. This differs from <ground-state overlap after sudden expansion of a square well> with a fixed midpoint, because translation of the new well changes the overlap. Under a fixed final <Hamiltonian operator>, all final <energy eigenstate> <probabilities> remain constant during subsequent <unitary time evolution>.

= Sudden hydrogenic charge change
{parent=Sudden approximation}

In the <sudden approximation>, changing the nuclear charge of a one-electron <hydrogen atom> from $Z_i e$ to $Z_f e$ leaves its electronic state unchanged during the change. Its subsequent probability to occupy a final state is the squared overlap with that state. Using normalized $1s$ <wavefunctions> $\psi_Z=(\beta_Z^3/\pi)^{1/2}e^{-\beta_Zr}$, $\beta_Z=Z/a_0$, gives
$$
\langle1s,Z_f|1s,Z_i\rangle=\frac{8(\beta_i\beta_f)^{3/2}}{(\beta_i+\beta_f)^3},\qquad \boxed{P_{1s}=\frac{64(Z_iZ_f)^3}{(Z_i+Z_f)^6}.}
$$
This follows by integrating $4(\beta_i\beta_f)^{3/2}r^2e^{-(\beta_i+\beta_f)r}$ over positive $r$. A spherically symmetric initial state has zero overlap with every final state of nonzero <orbital angular momentum>, by the <orthogonality> of the <spherical harmonics>. Nuclear recoil and interactions with emitted particles can invalidate the perfectly spherical sudden-change model.

= Quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step
{parent=Sudden approximation}
{title2=$|\psi(0^+)\rangle=|\psi(0^-)\rangle$}

For a fixed <Hilbert space>, a bounded <self-adjoint> <Hamiltonian operator> with a finite step $H_i\to H_f$ at $t=0$ does not deliver an instantaneous rotation of the <quantum state>. Integrating the <Schrödinger equation> across a short interval gives
$$
|\psi(\epsilon)\rangle-|\psi(-\epsilon)\rangle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle\,dt.
$$
For a normalized <quantum state> and $\|H(t)\|\le M$ in <operator norm>, the difference has <norm> at most $2\epsilon M/\hbar$, which tends to zero. The time <derivative> can jump even though the <quantum state> is continuous. Here $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. An instantaneous replacement of the <eigenbasis> changes coordinates, not the vector being represented.

For a real switch occupying $0\le t\le\tau$, remove any real scalar contribution $a(t)I$ that produces only a <global phase>, and write $|\widetilde\psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\int_0^t a(s)ds/\hbar}|\psi(t)\rangle$. The same integral argument gives the sufficient bounded-model estimate
$$
\bigl\|\widetilde\psi(\tau)-\psi(0)\bigr\|\le\varepsilon,\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac1\hbar\int_0^\tau\|H(t)-a(t)I\|\,dt.
$$
Thus $\varepsilon\ll1$ controls the <sudden approximation> up to a <global phase>. For any normalized final <energy eigenstate>, its <probability amplitude> differs from the instantaneous-switch value by at most $\varepsilon$, and its <Born-rule> <probability> differs by at most $2\varepsilon$. These are sufficient error bounds, not necessary criteria. For the fixed-gap <two-level sudden quench>, any switching path $H(t)=-\Delta\mathbf n(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2$ with a unit vector $\mathbf n(t)$ has <operator norm> $\Delta/2$, giving $\varepsilon\le\Delta\tau/(2\hbar)$.

This estimate is deliberately for bounded <Hamiltonian operators>. An unbounded <energy> spectrum does not supply a finite global $M$; relevant <quantum states>, <operator domains> and excited <energy eigenvalues> require separate control. The <adiabatic theorem> also requires hypotheses about the path and its <spectral gaps>; there is no universal switch-duration criterion covering all spectra. The <cold-atom collapse and revival after a lattice quench> illustrates how a single switch can be sudden for one degree of freedom and slow for another.

= Impulsive quantum kick
{parent=Quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step}
{title2=$U_{\rm kick}=e^{-iK/\hbar}$}

An <impulsive quantum kick> is the limit of a pulse with finite integrated <Hamiltonian operator>, rather than a bounded finite step. For a bounded <self-adjoint operator> $K$ with units of <action>, a rectangular pulse $H_\tau=K/\tau$ lasting $\tau$ has exact <unitary time evolution> $e^{-iK/\hbar}$ for every $\tau$. In the limiting notation $H(t)=K\delta(t)$, $\delta$ is the <Dirac delta function> and
$$
|\psi(0^+)\rangle=e^{-iK/\hbar}|\psi(0^-)\rangle.
$$
The integral estimate for <quantum state continuity at a finite Hamiltonian step> no longer tends to zero: the pulse height diverges. A bounded background <Hamiltonian operator> contributes vanishing evolution during this limit. A more general pulse with noncommuting generators can require <time ordering>; integrated area alone then need not specify the result.

For example, $K=\hbar\phi\sigma_y/2$, with a $y$ <Pauli matrix>, sends a <qubit> in $|0\rangle$ to $\cos(\phi/2)|0\rangle+\sin(\phi/2)|1\rangle$. At $\phi=\pi$, the <Born rule> predicts $|1\rangle$ with <probability> one, however short the regularized pulse is. Its finite rotation is precisely what a bounded finite step cannot accomplish at the switching instant.

= Off-diagonal two-state impulse
{parent=Impulsive quantum kick}

A Hermitian impulse $U\delta(t)$ with zero diagonal has kick operator $e^{-iU/\hbar}$. If $u=U_{21}$, its transition <probability> is $\sin^2(|u|/\hbar)$, reducing to $|u|^2/\hbar^2$ for a weak impulse. The integrated operator has action units, and a pulse integrated across its full support has no extra half-delta factor.

= Quantum quench
{parent=Sudden approximation}

A <quantum quench> changes a <quantum system>'s control parameters rapidly and studies the subsequent <unitary time evolution>. In the ideal sudden limit, write an initial <energy eigenstate> $|m_i\rangle$ using a complete final <orthonormal eigenbasis>:
$$
|m_i\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n_f\rangle,\qquad c_n=\langle n_f|m_i\rangle,\qquad
|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_ne^{-iE_n^ft/\hbar}|n_f\rangle.
$$
The <Born-rule> <probabilities> for a nondegenerate final <energy measurement> are $|c_n|^2$, with $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$. For a degenerate <energy eigenvalue>, sum the <probabilities> over its orthonormal <energy eigenstates>. Subsequent relative <quantum phases> can alter other measurement <probabilities> even though these final <energy> populations stay fixed. A <quench> need not begin in an <energy eigenstate>; the same expansion applies to any initial <pure state>.

= Two-level sudden quench
{parent=Quantum quench}
{title2=$p_g=\cos^2(\theta/2),\quad p_e=\sin^2(\theta/2)$}

Take an isolated <two-level system>, or a <spin one-half> <qubit>, with <Pauli matrices> $\sigma_x,\sigma_z$. Let $\sigma_z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $\sigma_z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$. The initial and final <Hamiltonian operators> are
$$
H_i=-\frac{\Delta_i}{2}\sigma_z,\qquad
H_f=-\frac{\Delta_f}{2}(\cos\theta\,\sigma_z+\sin\theta\,\sigma_x),\qquad \Delta_i,\Delta_f>0.
$$
The <energy> gaps are $\Delta_i,\Delta_f$. Prepare the initial <ground state> $|0\rangle$, with <energy eigenvalue> $-\Delta_i/2$. A final <orthonormal eigenbasis> is
$$
|g_f\rangle=c|0\rangle+s|1\rangle,\qquad
|e_f\rangle=-s|0\rangle+c|1\rangle,\qquad
c=\cos(\theta/2),\quad s=\sin(\theta/2).
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $H_f|g_f\rangle=-\Delta_f|g_f\rangle/2$ and $H_f|e_f\rangle=+\Delta_f|e_f\rangle/2$. The unchanged initial <quantum state> is $|0\rangle=c|g_f\rangle-s|e_f\rangle$. Its new <probability amplitudes> are the <inner products> $c,-s$; the <Born rule> gives $p_g=c^2,p_e=s^2$. The angle between the real amplitude vectors is $\theta/2$, whereas the Hamiltonian axis on the <Bloch sphere> rotates through $\theta$.

= Work in a two-level sudden quench
{parent=Two-level sudden quench}
{title2=$\langle W\rangle=(\Delta_i-\Delta_f\cos\theta)/2$}

The drive can supply or extract <work> while the <quantum state> stays approximately fixed. For a differentiable bounded <Hamiltonian operator>, the <Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\langle H(t)\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H(t)}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$
The state-evolution terms cancel, leaving the external drive's <power>. For a finite ideal step the change in mean <energy> is $\langle\psi|H_f-H_i|\psi\rangle$. Thus <conservation of energy> for a closed system with a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator> does not assert conservation of the driven subsystem's <energy> during switching.

Specify <work> operationally by two <energy measurements>: one before the switch and one after. For the prepared initial <ground state>, the first outcome is definite; the second samples the new <Born-rule> <probabilities>. This protocol gives a distribution of measured <energy> differences, rather than generally the measurement distribution of the single operator $H_f-H_i$.<Fluctuation theorems: Work is not an observable by Peter Talkner and others>[Talkner, Lutz and Hänggi, p. 2, equations (8)–(10): initial energy outcome, conditional transition probability and two-measurement work distribution]{ref}
$$
W_g=\frac{\Delta_i-\Delta_f}{2}\quad\hbox{with probability }c^2,\qquad
W_e=\frac{\Delta_i+\Delta_f}{2}\quad\hbox{with probability }s^2,
$$
and hence
$$
\langle H_f\rangle=-\frac{\Delta_f}{2}\cos\theta,\qquad
\langle W\rangle=\frac{\Delta_i-\Delta_f\cos\theta}{2},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(W)=\frac{\Delta_f^2}{4}\sin^2\theta.
$$
For $\Delta_i=\Delta_f=\Delta$ and $\theta=\pi/3$, the <work> is $0$ or $\Delta$, with <probabilities> $3/4$ and $1/4$; its <expectation> is $\Delta/4$ and its <variance> is $3\Delta^2/16$. The initial mean <energy> $-\Delta/2$ becomes $-\Delta/4$. Using different initial and final scalar offsets would also shift the measured <work>; the displayed <Hamiltonian operators> fix that convention explicitly.

= Post-quench oscillations in a two-level system
{parent=Two-level sudden quench}
{title2=$P_0(t)=1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2(\Delta_ft/(2\hbar))$}

After the <quantum quench>, hold $H_f$ fixed and evolve without an intervening <energy measurement>. The <unitary time evolution> is
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=c\,e^{+i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}|g_f\rangle-s\,e^{-i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}|e_f\rangle.
$$
Projection onto the original $|0\rangle$ gives <probability amplitude> $c^2e^{+i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}+s^2e^{-i\Delta_ft/(2\hbar)}$. Squaring its <modulus> yields the <two-level quantum return probability>
$$
P_0(t)=1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta_ft}{2\hbar}\right),\qquad
P_1(t)=\sin^2\theta\sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta_ft}{2\hbar}\right).
$$
Their sum is one. For $\theta=\pi/3$, the first minimum of $P_0$ is $1/4$ at $t=\pi\hbar/\Delta_f$, and the return is complete at $T=2\pi\hbar/\Delta_f$. At $\theta=0$ or $\pi$ there is no oscillation, because the initial <quantum state> is already a final <energy eigenstate>.

That constant differs from the oscillation of the unmeasured <pure state>. The <work> protocol and the oscillation protocol therefore use separate preparations.

A concrete <quantum quench> experiment loaded a <Bose-Einstein condensate> of $^{87}\mathrm{Rb}$ <chemical atoms> from a <magnetic field> trap into an <optical lattice>: three orthogonal $838\,\mathrm{nm}$ <laser> <standing waves>, with approximately $125\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ <beam waists>. An $80\,\mathrm{ms}$ loading ramp preceded a $50\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ increase in lattice depth. This suppressed <quantum tunnelling> while retaining each well's vibrational <ground state>. = Ground-state overlap after sudden expansion of a square well
{parent=Sudden approximation}
{title2=$P=\left[\frac{4\eta^{3/2}\cos(\pi/(2\eta))}{\pi(\eta^2-1)}\right]^2$}

For an <infinite square well> initially on $[-a,a]$, a normalized <ground state> is $a^{-1/2}\cos(\pi x/(2a))$. If the endpoints change instantaneously to $\pm\eta a$, with $\eta>1$, the <sudden approximation> leaves the <wave function> unchanged, extended by zero outside the old well. Its overlap with the new normalized <ground state> $(\eta a)^{-1/2}\cos(\pi x/(2\eta a))$ is $4\eta^{3/2}\cos(\pi/(2\eta))/[\pi(\eta^2-1)]$. The <Born rule> squares this <probability amplitude>; for $\eta=2$ the result is $64/(9\pi^2)$, and the limit as $\eta\downarrow1$ is $1$.

= Canonical quantization of the electromagnetic field
{parent=Canonical quantization}

The <electromagnetic four-potential> has a vanishing temporal momentum and a <Gauss law constraint in gauge theory>. Gauge reduction leaves two transverse <canonical pairs>. Their <canonical commutation relations> give the <photon> creation and <annihilation operators>. Covariant <Gupta-Bleuler quantization> instead retains auxiliary polarization modes and selects the same physical state space by a subsidiary condition and null-state quotient.

= Gauge reduction of the Maxwell Hamiltonian

{title2=$H_T=\tfrac12\int[(\Pi^T)^2+(\nabla\times A^T)^2],d^3x$}

For a spatial potential $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf E=-\dot{\mathbf A}-\boldsymbol\nabla A_0$, the <Maxwell Lagrangian> gives $\boldsymbol\Pi=-\mathbf E$ and $\Pi^0=0$. Its <Hamiltonian> is $\int[(\boldsymbol\Pi^2+\mathbf B^2)/2+A_0\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\Pi],d^3x$. Preserving the temporal momentum <first-class constraint> gives the <Gauss law constraint in gauge theory>. In free space, <Coulomb gauge> and decay at spatial infinity set $A_0=0$ and leave only transverse modes. The reduced <Dirac bracket> uses the projector $\delta_{ij}-k_i k_j/|\mathbf k|^2$, so quantization gives the <transverse equal-time commutator> and the positive Hamiltonian displayed above.

= Berry phase
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\gamma$}

A <Berry phase> is the <geometric phase> acquired when a nondegenerate <energy eigenstate> follows a closed cycle of parameters of a <Hamiltonian operator> in the ideal limit of the <adiabatic theorem>. The <energy eigenstate> must stay separated from other <energy eigenstates> by a nonzero <spectral gap>, and the parameters must change sufficiently slowly. Returning the <Hamiltonian operator> to its starting value returns the state to the same ray, but its <quantum phase> can remember the journey. The total <quantum phase> contains both the <dynamical phase> and the <Berry phase>.

The beauty of the <Berry phase> is that, within the <adiabatic theorem>'s limit, it depends on the oriented path of the parameters rather than how long the journey takes. For a <spin one-half> <qubit>, the <Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude> turns that memory into half a <solid angle>. <Berry-phase interference with a reference path> converts this geometry into a detection <probability>.

Michael Berry's 1984 paper formulated the <geometric phase> for cycles governed by the <adiabatic theorem> and derived the <solid angle> result for <spin>. Its bibliographic details are Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A, volume 392, pages 45–57, DOI 10.1098/rspa.1984.0023. https://michaelberryphysics.wordpress.com/publications/[Berry's authored publication list, entry 120: Quantal phase factors accompanying adiabatic changes (1984)]{ref}; https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/athena/course/8/8.06/spring06/handouts/Berry_1984.pdf[Berry's original paper, available through MIT]{ref}. The wider concept of <geometric phase> does not require every cycle to obey the <adiabatic theorem>: Aharonov and Anandan formulated a <geometric phase> for general cyclic quantum evolution in 1987. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.58.1593[Aharonov and Anandan, Phase change during a cyclic quantum evolution, Physical Review Letters 58, 1593–1596 (1987)]{ref}.

= Cyclic phase of a spin in a rotating field
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$e^{i\Phi_\mu}=(-1)^{2s}e^{-i\mu\Omega T}$}

An <eigenstate> of the <rotating-frame Rabi Hamiltonian> returns to the same laboratory ray after a full drive period. Its <quantum phase> includes both the rotating-frame <energy> and the sign of a full <spin> rotation. For <spin> $s$ and magnetic label $\mu$, the factor is $(-1)^{2s}e^{-i\mu\Omega T}$. Expanding the rotating-frame splitting to first order in the drive <frequency> and subtracting the adiabatic <dynamical phase> leaves $-\mu$ times the oriented <solid angle> of the field loop. Dropping the full-rotation sign gives a spurious half-integer-spin offset.

{title2=$A_j=i\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$}

For a normalized, smoothly chosen instantaneous <energy eigenstate> $|\psi(\mathbf R)\rangle$, the <Berry connection> has components $A_j=i\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$. Its <line integral> around a closed parameter path $C$ gives the <Berry phase> in a periodic choice of state vector:
$$
\gamma=\oint_C\sum_j A_j\,dR_j\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
Normalization makes the <inner product> $\langle\psi|\partial_{R_j}\psi\rangle$ purely imaginary, so the <Berry connection> is real. Changing the representative to $e^{i\chi(\mathbf R)}|\psi\rangle$ changes the <Berry connection> to $A_j-\partial_{R_j}\chi$. A single-valued phase factor along the closed path changes the <line integral> by an integer multiple of $2\pi$, leaving $e^{i\gamma}$ unchanged. If the chosen vector is not periodic, include the endpoint phase:
$$
\gamma=\arg\langle\psi(0)|\psi(T)\rangle+i\int_0^T\langle\psi(t)|\dot\psi(t)\rangle\,dt\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
Here $\psi$ denotes the instantaneous <energy eigenstate> representative, with its <dynamical phase> removed. This is freedom to choose a representative of a state ray. https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/quantum-hall-effect/qhe1.pdf[David Tong, The Quantum Hall Effect, sections 1.5.1–1.5.2: Berry connection and a spin example; the spin convention below is stated explicitly]{ref}.

= Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude
{c}
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$\gamma=-\pi(1-\cos\theta)=-\Omega/2$}

Choose the <computational basis> $|0\rangle=|\uparrow_z\rangle$, $|1\rangle=|\downarrow_z\rangle$ for a <spin one-half> <qubit>. On the <Bloch sphere>, let $\mathbf n=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$ and use the normalized <quantum state>
$$
|\psi(\theta,\phi)\rangle=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle.
$$
This is the $+1$ <eigenstate> of $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, where $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the <Pauli matrices>. To make it an instantaneous <ground state>, take the model <Hamiltonian operator> $H=-\Delta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2$ with constant $\Delta>0$. Its <energy eigenvalues> are $-\Delta/2$ and $+\Delta/2$, so its <spectral gap> is $\Delta$ everywhere. The <spin> points along $\mathbf n$; this explicit convention fixes the sign of the <Berry phase>.

Keep the polar angle $\theta$ fixed and increase the azimuth $\phi$ from $0$ to $2\pi$, slowly enough for the <adiabatic theorem>. The state vector written above is periodic in $\phi$. Directly differentiating gives
$$
\partial_\phi|\psi\rangle=i e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle,\qquad
\langle\psi|\partial_\phi\psi\rangle=i\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}.
$$
Thus the azimuthal <Berry connection> is $A_\phi=-\sin^2(\theta/2)$ and its <line integral> is
$$
\gamma=i\int_0^{2\pi}\langle\psi|\partial_\phi\psi\rangle\,d\phi
=-2\pi\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}=-\pi(1-\cos\theta)=-\frac{\Omega}{2}\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
The north polar cap has oriented <solid angle> $\Omega=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\theta\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\phi=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)$, positive for increasing $\phi$. Reversing the loop reverses the <Berry phase>. The opposite <spin> <eigenstate> has the opposite <Berry phase> modulo $2\pi$.

At $\theta=60^\circ$, the <solid angle> is $\pi$ and the <Berry phase> is $-\pi/2$. At $\theta=90^\circ$, the <solid angle> is $2\pi$ and the <Berry phase> is $-\pi$. At $\theta=0$, the loop collapses and $\gamma=0$. At $\theta=\pi$, the same expression gives $-2\pi\equiv0$: the <Bloch vector> stays at the south pole, while this representative is $e^{i\phi}|1\rangle$. Its winding is only a choice of <global phase>, so the physically relevant phase factor is again one. The displayed formula is a continuous, unwrapped representative, not a unique real-valued <observable>.

= Berry-phase interference with a reference path
{c}
{parent=Berry phase}
{title2=$P_+=(1+\cos\gamma)/2$}

An isolated <qubit> that acquires only a <global phase> has unchanged measurement <probabilities>. To reveal the <Berry phase>, use two coherent paths of a <Mach-Zehnder interferometer>, represented by a path <qubit>. In one arm the internal <spin one-half> <qubit> follows the loop; in the reference arm it stays in its starting <energy eigenstate>. Assume equal arm amplitudes, identical final internal states, cancelled relative <dynamical phases>, and calibrated fixed apparatus phases. In the model above, hold the reference <Hamiltonian operator> at its initial value for the same duration. Both arms then have <energy eigenvalue> $-\Delta/2$ and acquire the same <dynamical phase> $\Delta T/(2\hbar)$, so their relative <dynamical phase> vanishes in the ideal limit of the <adiabatic theorem>.

The joint <quantum state> before recombination then factors as
$$
\frac{|\mathrm{ref}\rangle+e^{i\gamma}|\mathrm{loop}\rangle}{\sqrt2}\otimes|\psi(\theta,0)\rangle.
$$
The <Berry phase> is now a relative <quantum phase> between the two paths. The calibrated plus output projects onto $|+\rangle=(|\mathrm{ref}\rangle+|\mathrm{loop}\rangle)/\sqrt2$. The <Born rule> gives
$$
P_+=\left|\frac{1+e^{i\gamma}}{2}\right|^2=\frac{1+\cos\gamma}{2},\qquad P_-=1-P_+.
$$
For the <Berry phase of a Bloch-sphere latitude>, the plus-port <probabilities> at $\theta=0,60^\circ,90^\circ,180^\circ$ are respectively $1,1/2,0,1$. This ideal <Mach-Zehnder interferometer> measurement cannot distinguish $\gamma$ from $-\gamma$ or $\gamma+2\pi$ by itself; scanning a known reference phase can resolve the sign. An uncancelled relative <dynamical phase> would shift the fringe as well.

= Parity
{title2=$\mathcal P$}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki=Parity_(physics)}

Parity is the eigenvalue $+1$ or $-1$ of a state under <spatial reflection>.

= Natural parity
{parent=Parity}
{title2=$\pi=(-1)^J$}

For a state of integer total <angular momentum> $J$, natural <parity> means $\pi=(-1)^J$: $0^+,1^-,2^+,3^-,\ldots$. The other sign is called unnatural parity. The terminology is useful when a spin-zero positive-parity parent emits a spin-zero positive-parity particle: <angular momentum conservation> fixes relative <orbital angular momentum> $L=J$, and <parity conservation> then forces natural parity for the daughter.

= Parity conservation
{parent=Parity}

An interaction commuting with the <parity operator> preserves total parity. A two-body final state has the product of its particles' intrinsic parities times the orbital factor $(-1)^\ell$, giving the <parity selection rule for a two-body decay>.

= Parity operator
{parent=Parity}
{title2=$P$}

The parity operator acts on a spatial <wavefunction> by $(P\psi)(x)=\psi(-x)$ and satisfies $P^2=I$. For a <Hamiltonian operator> with an even potential and parity-invariant domain, $HP=PH$. A <nondegenerate energy eigenvalue> has a one-dimensional <eigenspace>, so its eigenfunction is an eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue $+1$ or $-1$.

= Midpoint parity operator
{parent=Parity operator}
{title2=$R_a\psi(x)=\psi(a-x)$}

= Reflection about the midpoint of an interval
{synonym}

On $L^2(0,a)$, the <midpoint parity operator> is $(R_a\psi)(x)=\psi(a-x)$. Substitution $y=a-x$ gives $\langle f,R_ag\rangle=\langle R_af,g\rangle$ and $\|R_af\|=\|f\|$, so it is a bounded <self-adjoint operator> and a <unitary operator>, with $R_a^2=I$. It is the <parity operator> in the centered coordinate $x-a/2$.

For an <infinite square well>, $\psi_n=\sqrt{2/a}\sin(n\pi x/a)$ obeys $R_a\psi_n=(-1)^{n+1}\psi_n$. Reflection preserves the <Dirichlet boundary conditions> and commutes with the second derivative, so $R_a$ commutes with the <Hamiltonian operator> on its <operator domain>. By contrast, differentiating once gives $PR_a=-R_aP$ for the <momentum operator>. This distinction permits conserved midpoint <parity> with changing mean momentum.

= Parity as odd-mode number parity
{parent=Parity operator}
{title2=$\mathcal P=e^{i\pi N_{\rm odd}}$}

For a free intrinsic-even <real scalar field>, momentum reflection $Rf(\mathbf p)=f(-\mathbf p)$ is a self-adjoint involution on the one-particle space. Its odd <orthogonal projector> is $(1-R)/2$. The corresponding <second quantization> is $N_{\rm odd}=\tfrac12\int d^3p/(2\pi)^3\,a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger(a_{\mathbf p}-a_{-\mathbf p})$, with integration over all <momentum space>. Conjugation by $e^{i\pi N_{\rm odd}}$ fixes even modes and reverses odd modes, hence sends $a_{\mathbf p}$ to $a_{-\mathbf p}$ and implements the <parity operator>. Its square is one because $N_{\rm odd}$ has integer <eigenvalues>. The equivalent positive expression is $N_{\rm odd}=\tfrac14\int d^3p/(2\pi)^3\,(a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger-a_{-\mathbf p}^\dagger)(a_{\mathbf p}-a_{-\mathbf p})$.

= Photon-number parity
{parent=Parity operator}
{title2=$\Pi=(-1)^N$}

Even and odd occupation sectors have <eigenvalues> $+1$ and $-1$. Pair creation preserves parity, whereas one <photon addition> or <photon subtraction> reverses it. An <optical squeezed vacuum> is even and a <squeezed one-photon state> is odd.

= Orbital parity
{title2=$(-1)^\ell$}
{parent=Parity}

An <orbital angular momentum> eigenstate with quantum number $\ell$ changes by the factor $(-1)^\ell$ under <spatial reflection>. Equivalently,
$$
Y_\ell^m(-\widehat{\mathbf r})=(-1)^\ell Y_\ell^m(\widehat{\mathbf r}).
$$

= Multiparticle parity
{title2=$\eta_1\eta_2(-1)^\ell$}
{parent=Parity}

The parity of a two-particle centre-of-mass state with intrinsic parities $\eta_1,\eta_2$ and relative <orbital angular momentum> $\ell$ is
$$
\eta_1\eta_2(-1)^\ell.
$$

{parent=Multiparticle parity}

If parity is conserved when a particle of intrinsic parity $\eta_i$ decays into two particles of intrinsic parities $\eta_1,\eta_2$, then their relative partial wave obeys
$$
\eta_i=\eta_1\eta_2(-1)^\ell.
$$

<temperature> can smear measured spectral or transport features through the spread of occupied energies and through temperature-dependent scattering. Fermi-edge occupation broadening and phonon-induced <lifetime broadening> are different mechanisms and should not be equated.

A <nuclear energy level> is an <energy eigenvalue> of an <atomic nucleus>. Differences between levels give nuclear <excitation energies> and the approximate <gamma-ray> energies of transitions.

<Excitation energy> is an <energy level>'s energy above a specified <ground state>. Emitted <photon> <energies> measure differences between levels, with a small <recoil> correction when the emitter has finite <mass>.

= Photon
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A photon is a quantum of the electromagnetic field with energy $E=h\nu=\hbar\omega$.

= Landau-Yang theorem
{parent=Photon}
{c}
{wiki=Landau–Yang_theorem}

A massive <spin>-one state cannot decay into two identical on-shell <photons>. In their <centre-of-momentum frame>, opposite <photon helicities> have helicity difference two and require $J\geq2$. Equal helicities have difference zero; exchanging the two identical <bosons> multiplies their total-<angular momentum> wave by $(-1)^J$, so <Bose-Einstein statistics> requires even $J$. Neither case permits $J=1$. The on-shell and identical-particle assumptions are essential; the theorem does not exclude a decay with one virtual <photon>.

= Photon number flux density
{parent=Photon}
{title2=$\Phi_N=I/(hf)$}

The <photon number flux density> is the number of <photons> crossing unit normal <area> per unit time. A monochromatic beam of <light intensity> $I$ and <frequency> $f$ has $\Phi_N=I/(hf)$ because each <photon> carries <energy> $hf$. Its units are $\mathrm{m^{-2}s^{-1}}$. For a mixed spectrum, integrate the spectral <light intensity> divided by the corresponding <photon> <energy>.

= Photon momentum
{parent=Photon}
{title2=$\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$}

In <vacuum>, a <photon> of <wave vector> $\mathbf k$ carries <momentum> $\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$ and <energy> $E=\hbar c|\mathbf k|$. In a direct optical interband transition this <momentum> is generally small compared with relevant crystal <momenta>, motivating the nearly vertical-transition approximation in <wave vector> space. Electromagnetic momentum in matter requires additional conventions.

= Single-photon state
{parent=Photon}
{title2=$|1_\psi\rangle$}

A <single-photon state> lies in the one-excitation sector of the quantized <electromagnetic field>. It can be a <quantum superposition> over spatial ports or temporal modes while its total <photon number> remains one. Squared coefficients give exclusive event probabilities only when the corresponding wavepacket modes are orthonormal; overlapping temporal histories must be added coherently.

= Single-photon source
{parent=Photon}

A <single-photon source> prepares light with strongly suppressed simultaneous two-<photon> emission. One useful implementation repeatedly excites one discrete emitter and collects a selected radiative transition. <Photon antibunching>, measured through the zero-delay second-order correlation $g^{(2)}(0)$, tests the multiphoton suppression; a weak coherent beam retains Poissonian number fluctuations. Collection efficiency and multiphoton probability are distinct properties.

= Photon energy
{parent=Photon}
{wiki}
{title2=$E=h\nu=\hbar\omega=hc/\lambda$}

The <energy> carried by one <photon> is proportional to its <frequency> $\nu$, with proportionality constant the <Planck constant> $h$. The equivalent forms use <angular frequency> $\omega=2\pi\nu$ and vacuum <wavelength> $\lambda=c/\nu$. Different observers can measure different <photon energies> because they measure different <frequencies>; the <photon> remains massless. In signature $(+---)$, an observer with <four-velocity> $u^\mu$ measures $E=p_\mu u^\mu$, where $p^\mu$ is the photon's <four-momentum> and $u^\mu u_\mu=c^2$.

= Photon emission rate from monochromatic power
{parent=Photon energy}
{title2=$\dot N=P/(hf)$}

A monochromatic beam of <frequency> $f$ and mean radiated <power> $P$ carries mean <photon> rate $\dot N=P/(hf)$. It follows by dividing <energy> per unit time by <energy> per <photon>. A broadband source instead requires integrating spectral <power> divided by the frequency-dependent <photon energy>; electrical input <power> must first be converted to radiated <power>.

= Nuclear resonance fluorescence

Absorption of a <gamma ray> into an excited nuclear level followed by re-emission. The absorber-frame photon <energy> must overlap the absorption line; nuclear recoil, <Doppler broadening> and the natural <decay width> affect resonance. = Electric quadrupole transition
{parent=Gamma ray}
{title2=$E2$}

An <electric quadrupole transition> is a radiative transition of an <atomic nucleus> with electric multipole order two. The emitted <photon> carries two units of <angular momentum> and positive <parity>, so the <atomic nucleus> has unchanged <parity>.<NuDat 3 Glossary by National Nuclear Data Center>[Multipolarity, E2 entry]{ref}

<Addition of angular momentum> imposes $|I_i-I_f|\le2\le I_i+I_f$. Thus $2^+\to0^+$ and $2^+\to2^+$ can be E2, whereas $0^+\to0^+$ cannot emit one E2 <photon>. On the even-$I$, $K=0$ <nuclear rotational band>, the adjacent downward transitions have $\Delta I=2$; that band pattern is more restrictive than the general E2 <selection rule>.

= Photon polarisation
{synonym}

For a <photon> in a fixed spatial mode, the two transverse <photon polarizations> span a <qubit> space. A pure <photon polarization> state is $\alpha|H\rangle+\beta|V\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$, and a general state is a two-dimensional <density matrix>. Its <Bloch vector> gives the normalized Stokes parameters. Polarization-dependent path separation can correlate this internal degree of freedom with a path <qubit>.

= Polarization state
{parent=Photon polarization}

The <quantum state> associated with the two transverse polarization degrees of freedom of a photon in a specified propagation mode. In a horizontal-vertical basis, a pure state is $\alpha|H\rangle+\beta|V\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$; a <density matrix> also describes mixtures. Linear polarizers test real combinations of these basis states, while phase-sensitive elements allow elliptical and circular polarization measurements.

= Photon polarization vector
{parent=Photon}
{title2=$\epsilon^\mu(k)$}

= Photon polarisation vector
{synonym}

For a real <on-shell> <photon> of null momentum $k$, a physical polarization satisfies $k\cdot\epsilon=0$ and is defined modulo $\epsilon\mapsto\epsilon+\alpha k$. The two-dimensional quotient carries the two physical <photon> helicities. In a transverse gauge choose $\epsilon^0=0$ and $\epsilon^*\cdot\epsilon=-1$. A <Ward identity> ensures that replacing an external polarization by its momentum gives zero in the summed physical amplitude.

= Two physical polarizations of a photon
{parent=Photon}
{title2=$N_{\rm phys}=2$}

Two <first-class constraints> in Maxwell theory remove four phase-space variables from the eight variables of a four-component potential and its momenta. The remaining four phase-space variables form two <canonical pairs>. In <radiation gauge> these are transverse polarization modes; circular combinations have <helicity> $+1$ and $-1$. Auxiliary covariant-gauge components do not add physical <photon> states.

= Photon absorption
{parent=Photon}

<Photon> absorption removes a <photon> and transfers its <energy> and <momentum> to matter. It contributes a negative photon-number collision source. It differs from <Compton scattering>, which redistributes a <photon>'s <energy> and direction while preserving the count. The integrated source can vanish if <photon emission> and absorption balance.

= Absorption cross section
{parent=Photon absorption}
{title2=$\sigma_{\rm abs}(\lambda)$}

The effective area per target for absorbing incident radiation at a specified <wavelength>. For independent absorbers with number column $N$, the absorption <optical depth> is $\tau=\sigma_{\rm abs}N$. Absorption does not guarantee a particular chemical product; a channel's <quantum yield> is also needed in its <photolysis rate coefficient>.

= Saturable absorption in a degenerate two-level system
{parent=Absorption cross section}
{title2=$I_{s,a}=\hbar\omega/[\sigma_{12}\tau(1+g_1/g_2)]$}

For a closed <two-level system> with total population $N$, excited-state lifetime $\tau$ and statistical sublevel populations, let $W_a=\sigma_{12}I/(\hbar\omega)$. The steady <rate equation> $0=W_a[N_1-(g_1/g_2)N_2]-N_2/\tau$ gives <absorption coefficient> $\kappa=N\sigma_{12}/(1+I/I_{s,a})$. For a uniform slab of thickness $L$ and transmission $T=I_{\rm out}/I_{\rm in}$, integrating $dI/dz=-\kappa I$ proves $\ln T+I_{\rm in}(T-1)/I_{s,a}=-N\sigma_{12}L$. Resolved sublevel <optical pumping> requires a more detailed model.

= Photovoltaic effect
{parent=Photon absorption}
{wiki}

The <photovoltaic effect> is the production of an <electric potential difference> or an <electric current> by <photon absorption> in a material or device. In a <semiconductor>, absorbed <photons> generate <electrons> and <electron holes>; asymmetric transport and selective collection can maintain distinct <quasi-Fermi levels> and deliver electrical <power> to a load. An equilibrium <built-in potential> alone supplies no continuous electrical <power>.

= Solar cell
{parent=Photovoltaic effect}
{wiki}

A <solar cell> converts incident <light> into electrical <power> through the <photovoltaic effect>. A <semiconductor> <p-n junction> separates and collects photogenerated <electrons> and <electron holes>. An ideal illuminated junction has output <current density>
$$
J(V)=J_{\rm sc}-J_0\left(e^{eV/(k_BT)}-1\right),\qquad
V_{\rm oc}=\frac{k_BT}{e}\log\left(1+\frac{J_{\rm sc}}{J_0}\right).
$$
Here $J_{\rm sc}$ is the short-circuit <current density>, $J_0$ is the <diode saturation current>, and $V_{\rm oc}$ is the open-circuit <electric potential difference>. The conversion efficiency is the largest delivered $VJ$ divided by the incident <power> per unit area; the short-circuit and open-circuit operating points themselves deliver zero <power>.

= Carrier multiplication
{parent=Solar cell}

Production of more than one electronic excitation from a sufficiently energetic absorbed photon. It changes the one-pair-per-photon assumption in the conventional single-junction detailed-balance model; competing relaxation and collection losses remain relevant.

= Hot-carrier solar cell

A device concept that seeks to extract carriers through energy-selective contacts before their excess photon <energy> is lost by <thermalization>. Suppressing cooling while maintaining extraction is the central challenge.

= Tandem solar cell
{parent=Solar cell}

A stack of junctions with different <band gaps> divides the incident spectrum, reducing both below-gap transmission and above-gap <thermalization> compared with one optimized junction. Current matching and optical and electrical losses remain constraints.

= Single-junction solar cell
{parent=Solar cell}

A photovoltaic device with one main absorption gap and one junction. The ordinary detailed-balance limit assumes one collected <electron-hole pair> per above-gap photon, thermalized carriers and <radiative recombination>.

= Organic solar cell

A photovoltaic device whose active <semiconductor> includes organic molecules or polymers.

A photovoltaic device combining absorbers with different bandgaps to convert different spectral regions. A monolithic two-terminal stack has a common series current and therefore requires photocurrent matching; independently contacted junctions relax that constraint.

= Measuring a solar cell's current-voltage characteristic
{parent=Solar cell}

Keep the illumination and cell <temperature> approximately constant. Connect an <ammeter> and an adjustable <resistor> as a <series circuit> across the <solar cell>; connect a high-resistance <voltmeter> across its terminals. Vary the <electrical resistance> and record pairs of <electric potential difference> $V$ and delivered <electric current> $I$. This measures the illuminated <current-voltage characteristic>.

A load close to a <short circuit> estimates $I_{\rm sc}$ at $V\simeq0$. With the load disconnected, an <open circuit> measurement using a high-resistance <voltmeter> estimates $V_{\rm oc}$ at $I\simeq0$. Neither endpoint delivers useful <power>: $P=VI$ vanishes there. The intermediate readings locate the maximum of $VI$ and determine the <solar-cell fill factor>. Holding illumination fixed matters because the photogenerated <electric current> changes with <light intensity>; holding <temperature> fixed matters because the <diode saturation current> and junction response change with it.

This experiment distinguishes producing an <electric potential difference> from delivering <energy> to a load. To determine conversion efficiency as well, measure the incident <power> on the illuminated area, rather than equating it to a lamp's electrical rating.

= Shockley-Queisser limit
{c}
{parent=Solar cell}
{wiki=Shockley–Queisser_limit}

The <Shockley-Queisser limit> is the radiative conversion-efficiency bound for an ideal single-junction <solar cell>, with one collected <electron>-<electron hole> pair per absorbed above-gap <photon>, carrier thermalization, and <radiative recombination> required by the reverse optical process. Photons below the <band gap> are not absorbed, while excess photon <energy> above it is thermalized. Maximizing the electrical <power> over the operating <electric potential difference> and then the <band gap> gives a ceiling of roughly thirty percent for ordinary unconcentrated sunlight; its precise value depends on the incident spectrum, cell <temperature> and emission geometry. The original calculation by <William Shockley> and <Hans-Joachim Queisser> obtained approximately $30\%$ at $1.1\,\mathrm{eV}$ for a $6000\,\mathrm K$ <blackbody> source and a cell at $300\,\mathrm K$.<Detailed Balance Limit of Efficiency of p-n Junction Solar Cells by William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser>[p.510, abstract: "maximum efficiency is found to be 30%"; band gap 1.1 eV, blackbody source 6000 K, cell 300 K, radiative fraction equal to one]{ref}

= Solar-cell fill factor
{parent=Solar cell}
{title2=$\mathrm{FF}=P_{\max}/(V_{\rm oc}I_{\rm sc})$}

The <solar-cell fill factor> compares the maximum delivered electrical <power> with the rectangle formed by the open-circuit <electric potential difference> and short-circuit <electric current>. It measures the shape of the illuminated <current-voltage characteristic>. The conversion efficiency is $\eta=V_{\rm oc}I_{\rm sc}\mathrm{FF}/P_{\rm in}$.

= Photoelectric effect
{parent=Photon absorption}
{wiki}

The <photoelectric effect> is the release of <electrons> from matter following <photon absorption>. Here the focus is emission from a metal surface into <vacuum>: a <photon> supplies <energy>, the <work function> pays the escape cost, and the remainder can become the emitted <electron>'s <kinetic energy>. A <photocurrent> counts collected <electric charge>; a <stopping potential> probes the upper <kinetic energy> limit. These are different measurements.

In 1905, <Albert Einstein> proposed that an individual light quantum could transfer its whole <energy> to one <electron>. His §8 allowed losses before escape, distinguished the fastest emitted <electrons> from the rest, and predicted a straight <stopping potential> versus <frequency> relation with a material-independent slope. He separately predicted that changing <light intensity> changes the number emitted without changing their <velocity> distribution under otherwise identical conditions. He also discussed incomplete transfer and limits to the account.<Einstein's Miraculous Year/On a Heuristic Point of View Concerning the Production and Transformation of Light by Albert Einstein>[Einstein's Miraculous Year; §8, printed pp. 193–196; full 21-page English translation of the 1905 paper]{ref}

The useful modern starting model is a clean, homogeneous metal with independent single-<photon> events, negligible initial thermal broadening, negligible lowering of the surface barrier by an applied <electric field>, and negligible <electric fields> from the emitted <electrons> themselves. Specify the surface and its <work function>; it is not enough to name the bulk material. The <Einstein photoelectric equation> describes the upper edge of the emission <energy> distribution. It neither predicts that every absorbed <photon> liberates an <electron> nor supplies the whole <photocurrent> curve. Its appeal is that a complicated surface process still exposes the universal <Planck constant> through a simple slope.

= Photoelectric absorption
{parent=Photoelectric effect}

A <photon> transfers its energy to a bound <electron>, ejecting it if energetically allowed. Subsequent atomic relaxation can emit characteristic radiation or additional electrons. Its material and energy dependence differs from <Compton scattering>, which matters when converting <computed tomography> attenuation to the higher photon energy of <positron emission tomography>.

= Grain photoelectric heating
{parent=Photoelectric effect}

Ultraviolet <photons> can eject electrons from dust grains in interstellar gas. The electron's excess <kinetic energy> heats the gas through collisions. The efficiency depends on the grain population and electric charge, so absorbed ultraviolet power is not automatically equal to gas-heating power.

= Einstein photoelectric equation
{c}

= Einstein's photoelectric equation
{c}
{synonym}

For the ideal single-<photon> metal model, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
h\nu=\Phi+K_{\max},\qquad
K_{\max}=h\nu-\Phi,\qquad
\nu\geq\nu_0=\frac{\Phi}{h}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\nu$ the incident <frequency>, $\Phi$ the <work function>, and $K_{\max}$ the largest emitted <kinetic energy> relative to the nearby <vacuum> level. An <electron> at the highest occupied <energy> can reach this edge if it escapes without an additional loss. An <electron> starting lower or losing <energy> within the metal emerges below it. The material takes recoil <momentum>; its recoil <energy> is neglected in this model.

For an original chosen example, take $\Phi=2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $\nu=8.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Using $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and the <elementary charge> $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ gives $h\nu=3.308534\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $K_{\max}=1.008534\,\mathrm{eV}$. The ideal <stopping potential> is therefore $1.008534\,\mathrm V$. At $4.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ the same model supplies only $1.654267\,\mathrm{eV}$ per <photon>, so this single-<photon> channel is closed. A negative result from $h\nu-\Phi$ means no emission through that channel, not an emitted <electron> with negative <kinetic energy>.

= Photoelectric threshold frequency
{parent=Einstein photoelectric equation}
{title2=$\nu_0=\Phi/h$}

The <photoelectric threshold frequency> is the lower <frequency> boundary allowed by the ideal single-<photon> <energy> balance. With <vacuum> <wavelength> $\lambda=c/\nu$ and <speed of light> $c$, its corresponding longest <wavelength> is $\lambda_0=hc/\Phi$. The chosen $2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$ <work function> gives $\nu_0\simeq5.561375\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\lambda_0\simeq539.062\,\mathrm{nm}$. These are model parameters, not a measured threshold for a named metal. Detectable <photocurrent> need not begin abruptly at the ideal boundary: collection, finite spectral width, thermal broadening and surface inhomogeneity matter.

= Stopping potential
{parent=Photoelectric effect}
{title2=$eV_s=K_{\max}$}

A <stopping potential> $V_s\geq0$ is the magnitude of the retarding <electric potential difference> just sufficient to stop collection of even the fastest relevant <electrons> in an ideal collection geometry. Since the <electron>'s <electric charge> is $-e$, the positive <energy> barrier is $eV_s$. After the <photoelectric contact-potential correction>,
$$
V_s=\frac{h}{e}\nu-\frac{\Phi}{e}.
$$
The maximum here is an emission <energy> edge, not the mean <kinetic energy>. In an ideal planar retarding geometry, transmission instead selects the component normal to the plates; identifying its endpoint with the full edge requires collection of trajectories that attain that edge in the relevant direction.

To make a complete illustrative curve, choose a uniform distribution of outward normal <kinetic energy> $K_\perp$ on $0\leq K_\perp\leq K_{\max}$, complete collection at zero barrier, and zero background. This is a deliberately chosen distribution, not a prediction of the <Einstein photoelectric equation>. For an effective retarding barrier $eV_r$, the collected fraction is
$$
F(V_r)=\begin{cases}
1,&V_r\leq0,\\
1-V_r/V_s,&0<V_r<V_s,\\
0,&V_r\geq V_s.
\end{cases}
$$
Integrating that uniform <probability density> above $eV_r$ proves the middle line. Thus $I(V_r)=I_{\rm sat}F(V_r)$ has a common endpoint when incident <light intensity> changes, even though its vertical scale changes. Real emission distributions need not give straight segments.

= Photoelectric saturation current
{parent=Photoelectric effect}
{title2=$I_{\rm sat}=e\eta P/(h\nu)$}

The <photoelectric saturation current> is the <photocurrent> reached when increasing the collecting <electric potential difference> no longer increases the fraction of available emitted <electrons> collected. It is saturation with collecting voltage at fixed illumination. It does not mean that increasing incident <light intensity> cannot increase emission.

If $P$ is incident <radiant flux> on the emitting area and $\eta$ is the external <quantum efficiency> for producing escaping <electrons>, then the incident <photon> arrival rate is $P/(h\nu)$ and complete collection gives $I_{\rm sat}=e\eta P/(h\nu)$. For the example's $\nu=8.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, choose $\eta=0.10$ and $P=1.00\,\mathrm{\mu W}$. The calculated <photon> arrival rate is $1.886488\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and $I_{\rm sat}=30.224866\,\mathrm{nA}$. Doubling $P$ doubles this current to $60.449731\,\mathrm{nA}$ while leaving $V_s$ fixed. At fixed illuminated area this doubles the <irradiance>. Changing <frequency> at fixed $P$ changes the arrival rate as well as the <photon> <energy>; $\eta$ can also change, so there is no general rule that a higher <frequency> gives a larger <photocurrent>.

A missing plateau can indicate incomplete collection or an <electric field> altered by the emitted <electric charge>. Heating, surface changes and additional emission channels can invalidate a constant $\eta$. Even a small amount of unwanted higher-<frequency> radiation can move an apparent endpoint. A <stopping potential> inferred from zero net current additionally needs background subtraction and a check for emission from the collector itself.

<Robert Andrews Millikan>'s 1916 experiment used a magnetically turned wheel and rotary knife to expose sodium or lithium in <vacuum>. A surrounding copper-gauze/solid-copper collector caught <electrons>; an <electrometer> recorded accumulated <electric charge> over thirty seconds at each applied voltage. He located endpoints graphically from <photocurrent> curves. Mercury <spectral lines>, selected by a monochromator and filters, controlled <frequency>; rejecting shorter-<wavelength> contamination protected the endpoint. An oxidized collector suppressed reverse emission for selected lines. A copper test plate measured the <contact potential difference> by a displacement-null method; a certified <voltmeter> set the electrical scale. He compared stable-surface sweeps with rapid alternating-<frequency> measurements to control drift. The slope tested $h/e$; the threshold required the contact correction. His gauge did not measure mercury vapor. Confirmation of the equation coexisted with his continued skepticism about localized light quanta.<A Direct Photoelectric Determination of Planck's h by Robert Andrews Millikan>[§§2–3, pp. 361–367, Figs. 1–3; §4, pp. 372–375, Figs. 5–6; §5, p. 380; Theories of photo emission, pp. 383–385; publication verified by Caltech record 45t7b-bvc23: March 1916, Physical Review 7(3), 355–388]{ref}

= Contact potential difference
{parent=Millikan photoelectric experiment}
{title2=$V_c$}

A <contact potential difference> is an electrostatic <electric potential difference> associated with different conducting surfaces brought into electrical equilibrium. Surface condition and different <work functions> can create a <vacuum> gap field even when an external <voltmeter> reads zero. Connecting the metals establishes equilibrium of their electronic <chemical potentials> including electrostatic <energy>; it need not equalize the electrostatic <electric potential> in the gap.

= Photoelectric contact-potential correction
{parent=Contact potential difference}
{title2=$V_s=V_{\rm app,stop}+V_c$}

Define $V_c$ as positive when the contact field adds to the retarding barrier, and let $V_{\rm app}$ be the signed externally imposed retarding voltage. In this chosen sign convention the barrier is $e(V_{\rm app}+V_c)$, hence
$$
V_{\rm app,stop}=\frac{h}{e}\nu-\frac{\Phi}{e}-V_c,
\qquad V_s=V_{\rm app,stop}+V_c.
$$
This convention specifies a model of the gap; an instrument's polarity labels must be translated into it. With a fixed $V_c$, subtraction at two <frequencies> removes both the <work function> and the offset:
$$
\frac{V_{\rm app,stop}(\nu_2)-V_{\rm app,stop}(\nu_1)}{\nu_2-\nu_1}=\frac{h}{e}.
$$
For an original example with the same $\Phi=2.30\,\mathrm{eV}$ and an additional $V_c=+0.200\,\mathrm V$, choose $\nu_1=6.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\nu_2=1.00\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The predicted terminal endpoints are $-0.018599$ and $1.635668\,\mathrm V$. The negative first endpoint calls for a small externally accelerating voltage because the contact field already over-retards the fastest <electrons>. Their difference is $1.654267\,\mathrm V$, giving $h/e\simeq4.135668\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\,s}$. Ignoring $V_c$ in the intercept would falsely infer $2.50\,\mathrm{eV}$ for the <work function>. The terminal line's zero would then be mistaken for a material threshold. If $V_c$ drifts between the two readings, even the slope is biased. These are calculated examples, not Millikan's measurements.

= Multiphoton photoemission
{parent=Photoelectric effect}

The single-<photon> <photoelectric threshold frequency> is a regime-dependent statement. In <multiphoton photoemission>, multiple <photons> can supply the escape <energy> in one emission process. An ideal $n$-<photon> <energy> balance would instead allow $K_{\max}=nh\nu-\Phi$ when $nh\nu\geq\Phi$, before additional losses or field effects. Thus increasing <light intensity> below the single-<photon> threshold cannot open that single-<photon> channel, but can make other channels relevant. The simple balance specifies neither their rates nor the illumination needed for them to dominate. Heating can also change the surface and emission mechanism. These cases require a different model from independent single-<photon> emission at a fixed <work function>.

= Photon emission
{parent=Photon}

<Photon> emission creates a <photon>, for example in <bremsstrahlung> or the decay of an excited material state. It produces a positive photon-number collision source when not balanced by inverse processes. The <photon number current> is conserved only if its integrated creation and destruction rates cancel.

= Free particle
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A free particle has no potential energy, so its Hamiltonian is $H=\mathbf p^2/(2m)$. On unbounded Euclidean space its energy spectrum has no normalizable lowest-energy eigenstate.

= Ground state
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A ground state is a normalized <energy eigenstate> that attains the lowest possible <energy> of a <quantum system>. Precisely, let the <Hamiltonian operator> $H$ be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below on a <Hilbert space>, and write $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$ for the <infimum> of its <spectrum>. A <ground state> is a <unit vector> $\psi_0$ in the <operator domain> $D(H)$ satisfying
$$
H\psi_0=E_*\psi_0,\qquad \|\psi_0\|=1.
$$
Thus a <ground state> exists exactly when the spectral bottom $E_*$ is an <energy eigenvalue>. The lowest <energy eigenvalue> among some listed <bound states> is insufficient if other parts of the <spectrum> lie below it. The <ground-state subspace> collects all minimizing <energy eigenstates>; it can have <ground-state degeneracy>. The <quantum variational principle> characterizes $E_*$ as the <infimum> of normalized <energy> <expectation values>. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Gerald Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19]{ref}.

For the one-dimensional <quantum harmonic oscillator>, the <ground state> has <energy> $\hbar\omega/2$ above the minimum of the <potential energy>. This <zero-point energy> is compatible with being the lowest allowed <energy>: a localized <wavefunction> cannot simultaneously have zero <kinetic energy>. For a <free particle> on the whole line, the spectral bottom is zero but is unattained by any <normalizable wavefunction>; see <ground-state energy>. A lower bound therefore need not describe a physically preparable <energy eigenstate>.

The <ground state> supplies a reference for excitation costs and for the <quantum variational principle>. A <ground state> is also a <stationary state> under a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator>: its <quantum phase> changes, while its position <probability density> stays fixed. A nonzero <ground-state energy> does not imply motion along a classical trajectory, and <ground-state degeneracy> means there need not be a unique lowest <quantum state>.

The probability that an <energy measurement> selects a <ground state>. For a nondegenerate normalized mode $\phi_1$, it is $|\langle\phi_1,\psi\rangle|^2$; for a degenerate ground eigenspace, use its full orthogonal projection.

The <ground-state subspace> is the eigenspace of a <Hamiltonian operator> at its lowest <eigenvalue> $E_0$. A <computational history state> construction can have many valid <quantum witnesses>, hence a degenerate <ground space>. Perturbation bounds must optimize on this entire subspace, rather than on a chosen basis of ground states.

= Ground-state degeneracy
{parent=Ground state}

When a <ground state> exists, its ground-state degeneracy is the <dimension> of the <ground-state subspace>:
$$
g_0=\dim\ker(H-E_0I),\qquad E_0=\inf\sigma(H).
$$
Here $I$ is the <identity operator>, and the <kernel of a linear map> is taken within the <operator domain> of the <Hamiltonian operator> $H$. The count concerns <linearly independent> <energy eigenstates>, rather than every normalized <quantum superposition> or every choice of <global phase>. Even a single <energy eigenspace> contains infinitely many representing vectors; its <dimension> measures independent freedom. <Ground-state degeneracy> is the lowest-energy instance of <degenerate energy levels>.

For an exact finite model with <energy> unit $\varepsilon>0$, let the <Hamiltonian operator> be the <diagonal matrix>
$$
H_{\mathrm{toy}}=\varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(0,0,2,5).
$$
Its <ground-state energy> is zero and $g_0=2$: the first two coordinate <unit vectors> $e_1,e_2$ span the <ground-state subspace>. Every normalized $a e_1+b e_2$, with $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$, is a <ground state>, including $(e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2$. Its <energy measurement> is certain to return zero, even though a measurement distinguishing $e_1$ from $e_2$ need not have a definite outcome. Under the unchanged <Hamiltonian operator>, this <quantum superposition> is a <stationary state> because both components acquire the same <global phase>.

<Ground-state degeneracy> matters when an interaction selects or splits previously equal <energy eigenvalues>. = Ground-state energy
{parent=Ground state}

The ground-state energy $E_0$ is the <energy eigenvalue> attained by a <ground state>. For a <self-adjoint operator> $H$ bounded below, distinguish this attained value from the always-defined spectral bottom $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$. When a <ground state> exists, $E_0=E_*$. Some usage calls $E_*$ the <ground-state energy> even when it is unattained; here an unattained bottom is explicitly identified as an <infimum>. The <quantum variational principle> states
$$
E_*=\inf_{\substack{\psi\in D(H)\\\|\psi\|=1}}\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle.
$$
Approaching this <infimum> with trial <wavefunctions> does not ensure that a minimizing <wavefunction> exists. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19]{ref}.

For the whole-line <free particle> with mass $m>0$,
$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2},\qquad \sigma(H)=[0,\infty).
$$
Its <spectrum> is continuous and contains no <energy eigenvalues> represented by <normalizable wavefunctions>. The <Fourier transform> makes $H$ multiplication by $\hbar^2 k^2/(2m)$; a fixed <energy> restricts $k$ to a set of zero length, on which a nonzero <square-integrable function> cannot be supported. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, section 7.2, Theorem 7.8]{ref}. An explicit family of normalized <Gaussian wave packets> demonstrates how the bottom is approached:
$$
\psi_s(x)=(2\pi s^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/(4s^2)},\qquad \langle H\rangle_s=\frac{\hbar^2}{8ms^2}\longrightarrow0\quad(s\longrightarrow\infty).
$$
The formula follows from $\psi_s'=-x\psi_s/(2s^2)$, <integration by parts>, and $\langle x^2\rangle_s=s^2$. Every finite width gives positive <kinetic energy>. At zero <energy>, $H\psi=0$ would require an affine function on the whole line, which is never a nonzero <normalizable wavefunction>. Spreading a <Gaussian wave packet> indefinitely produces an unattained <infimum>, rather than a zero-energy <ground state>.

An additive choice of <energy> origin changes the numerical <ground-state energy> without changing the <ground-state subspace>. For $H'=H+C I$, every <energy eigenvalue> becomes $E'=E+C$, while all differences $E_j-E_i$ stay fixed. Time evolution gains only the <global phase> $e^{-iCt/\hbar}$. This makes excitation costs and spectroscopic differences more intrinsic than the isolated number $E_0$.

= Quantum variational principle
{parent=Ground-state energy}
{title2=$\inf\sigma(H)\leq\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle$}

The <quantum variational principle> estimates the lowest energy by choosing a trial <wavefunction> and computing its <expectation value>, without solving the <Schrödinger equation> exactly. Minimizing over a manageable family of trial <wavefunctions> gives an upper bound; enlarging that family can improve the estimate.

Let $H$ be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below, and put $E_*=\inf\sigma(H)$. For $\|\psi\|=1$ in its <operator domain> $D(H)$,
$$
\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\geq E_*.
$$
Trial states may also lie in the form domain $Q(H)=D((H-bI)^{1/2})$, where $b\leq E_*$. Then use the <quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian> shifted by $b$:
$$
q_H[\psi]=\|(H-bI)^{1/2}\psi\|^2+b\|\psi\|^2\geq E_*\|\psi\|^2.
$$
This extends the <expectation value> beyond $D(H)$ without requiring $H\psi$ to exist. Equality for a normalized state holds exactly in the <energy eigenspace> at $E_*$, if that <eigenvalue> exists. https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-schroe/schroe.pdf[Gerald Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, Theorem 2.19 and equations (3.50)–(3.51)]{ref}.

A short proof makes the direction of the bound memorable. If $H$ has a complete <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates> $\phi_n$, write
$$
\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n,\qquad H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n,\qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1.
$$
The <expectation value> is a weighted average of allowed energies:
$$
q_H[\psi]-E_*=\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_*)\geq0.
$$
Equality requires every occupied <energy eigenstate> to have energy $E_*$. https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-05-quantum-physics-ii-fall-2013/61bc31b8d8bf0680c322733910a71aa0_MIT8_05F13_Chap_01.pdf[Barton Zwiebach, MIT 8.05 Wave Mechanics, section 5, Variational Principle]{ref}. With <continuous spectrum>, the <spectral theorem> replaces the sum by an integral against the <scalar spectral measure>; the same nonnegative-difference argument works.

For an unnormalized trial <wavefunction>, divide by its squared norm: $R_H[\psi]=q_H[\psi]/\|\psi\|^2$ is the form version of the <Rayleigh quotient>. The <quantum variational principle> supplies an upper bound on $E_*$; it supplies neither a lower bound on $E_*$ nor, by itself, a guarantee that a <bound state> exists.

To see the method at work, choose the real <Gaussian wave packet>
$$
\psi_a(x)=\left(\frac a\pi\right)^{1/4}e^{-ax^2/2},\qquad a>0,\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$
The <Gaussian integral> gives <wavefunction normalization> and the two useful position averages:
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|\psi_a|^2\,dx=1,\qquad \langle x^2\rangle_a=\frac1{2a},\qquad \langle x^4\rangle_a=\frac3{4a^2}.
$$
Thus its position <probability density> is a <normal distribution> with <standard deviation> $1/\sqrt{2a}$: large $a$ means narrow confinement. In units $\hbar=m=1$, <integration by parts> and $\psi_a'=-ax\psi_a$ give the <kinetic energy>
$$
\langle T\rangle_a=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}|\psi_a'|^2\,dx=\frac{a^2}2\langle x^2\rangle_a=\frac a4.
$$
These smooth, rapidly decaying trial <wavefunctions> belong to the <operator domains> of both polynomial <quantum Hamiltonians> below. Narrowing the <Gaussian wave packet> increases its <kinetic energy>; widening it increases the <potential energy> in either confining potential.

For the <quantum harmonic oscillator>, take $\hbar=m=\omega=1$:
$$
H_{\mathrm{ho}}=-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac{x^2}2,\qquad E_{\mathrm{ho}}(a)=\frac a4+\frac1{4a}.
$$
The trial values at $a=1/2,1,2$ are respectively $0.625,0.5,0.625$. The <derivative> $E_{\mathrm{ho}}'(a)=(1-a^{-2})/4$ changes sign at $a=1$, where <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> are both $1/4$. Here the trial family contains an exact <ground state>:
$$
H_{\mathrm{ho}}\psi_1=\frac12\psi_1,\qquad E_0=\frac12.
$$
The <zero-point energy> remains positive even at the best width. For comparison, removing the potential leaves $E(a)=a/4\to0$ as $a\to0$: the free <quantum Hamiltonian> on the line has spectral bottom zero but no normalizable zero-energy <energy eigenstate>. A sequence of ever broader trial <wavefunctions> need not approach an actual <bound state>.

For a pure <quartic oscillator>, keep $\hbar=m=1$ and set the quartic coefficient to one:
$$
H_4=-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+x^4,\qquad E_4(a)=\frac a4+\frac3{4a^2}.

The trial values at $a=1,2,3$ are respectively $1,0.6875,0.833333\ldots$. Setting the <derivative> to zero gives
$$
E_4'(a)=\frac14-\frac3{2a^3}=0,\qquad a_{\mathrm{opt}}=6^{1/3}\approx1.817121.
$$
The sign of this <derivative> changes from negative to positive at the optimum, giving the smallest energy within this <Gaussian wave packet> family:
$$
E_{0,4}\leq\min_{a>0}E_4(a)=\frac38\,6^{1/3}\approx0.681420.
$$
At this width, $\langle T\rangle\approx0.454280$ and $\langle V\rangle\approx0.227140$, so $2\langle T\rangle=4\langle V\rangle$, as expected from the <power-law quantum virial theorem> for a scale-optimized trial <wavefunction>. $$
\frac{H_4\psi_a}{\psi_a}=x^4-\frac{a^2x^2}2+\frac a2
$$
depends on $x$ for every $a>0$, so no member of this trial family is an <energy eigenstate>. Nevertheless, a single width parameter already turns a difficult <Schrödinger equation> into a useful, explicit energy estimate.

= Exponential variational bound for a capped Coulomb potential
{parent=Quantum variational principle}
{title2=$V_R(r)=-\kappa/\max(R,r)$}

For $\kappa>0$ and the radial exponential trial state $e^{-\alpha r}$, $\alpha>0$, put $a=\hbar^2/(m\kappa)$. Direct radial integration gives the <Rayleigh quotient>
$$
E(\alpha)=\kappa\left[\frac{a\alpha^2}{2}
-\frac{1-(1+\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}}R\right].
$$
The kinetic expectation is $\hbar^2\alpha^2/(2m)$; the potential expectation follows by splitting its radial integral at $R$. The common prefactor cancels from the stationary condition, giving $a\alpha=(1+2\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}$. Its positive solution is unique, since the left side increases and the right side decreases. For $\varepsilon=R/a\to0$, it satisfies $a\alpha=1-2\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^3)$.

The <quantum variational principle> makes $E(\alpha)$ an upper bound for the ground energy. The explicit choice $\alpha=1/a$ gives $E_0(R)\leq(\kappa/a)[1/2-\{1-(1+\varepsilon)e^{-2\varepsilon}\}/\varepsilon]$, whose first correction to $-\kappa/(2a)$ is $2\kappa R^2/(3a^3)$. The true energy is no lower than the point-Coulomb value because capping makes the potential less attractive.

= Excited-state variational constraint
{parent=Quantum variational principle}

Unrestricted minimization of a <Rayleigh quotient> seeks the ground state. To bound the $k$th excited <energy> from above, restrict normalized trial <wavefunctions> to the <orthogonal complement> of the exact lower-energy eigenspaces. In an exact <orthonormal eigenbasis>, all remaining expansion coefficients multiply energies at least $E_k$, so their weighted average is at least $E_k$. A known <symmetry> sector can supply some of these exclusions automatically. <Orthogonality> only to inaccurate approximate lower states does not automatically retain the same rigorous excited-state upper bound.

= Trial wavefunction
{parent=Quantum variational principle}
{title2=$\widetilde\Psi$}

An admissible <wavefunction> chosen from a tractable family for the <variational method>. Its <Hamiltonian> expectation gives an upper bound on the <ground-state energy>; parameters can be adjusted to lower that bound.

= Exponential variational bound for the Yukawa potential
{parent=Quantum variational principle}

For $V=-Ae^{-\mu r}/r$, the normalized exponential with scale $\alpha$ gives $E(\alpha)=\hbar^2\alpha^2/(2m)-4A\alpha^3/(\mu+2\alpha)^2$. Choosing $\alpha=\mu/2$ proves binding if $\mu<Am/\hbar^2$. Failure to obtain a negative trial expectation outside this range does not prove absence of a <bound state>. At equality a small positive admixture of the scale-$\mu$ exponential has a negative cross matrix element and proves binding too.

= Power-law quantum virial theorem

= Quantum tunnelling
{parent=Potential barrier}
{wiki}

Quantum tunnelling is transmission through a <potential barrier> even when the particle energy is below the barrier height.

The <wavefunction> penetrates the classically inaccessible region; matching it to an outgoing <scattering state> on the far side gives a nonzero <transmission probability>. For a real, time-independent <potential barrier>, the transmitted <energy> equals the incident <energy>: <quantum tunnelling> does not require a temporary violation of <conservation of energy>. = Finite rectangular barrier matching
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}

Take a <nonrelativistic particle> of constant <mass> $m$ with <energy> $E>0$, and choose the same zero of <potential energy> on both sides of a <rectangular potential barrier>:
$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}V_0,&0<x<a,\\0,&x<0\text{ or }x>a,\end{cases}
\qquad V_0>0,\quad a>0.
$$
The equality of the exterior <potential energies> matters: it gives the same exterior <wavenumber> $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and makes $T=|t|^2$. Here $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. This model has a finite jump in <potential energy>, no <delta potential>, and no absorbing term.

For $0<E<V_0$, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> gives the spatial <wavefunction>
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}
e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx},&x<0,\\
Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x},&0<x<a,\\
te^{ikx},&x>a,
\end{cases}
\qquad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}.
$$
The full <stationary state> is $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. We set the incident <probability amplitude> to one and impose no incoming wave from the right; this <scattering wavefunction> is not a <normalizable wavefunction>. The <tunnelling decay constant> $\kappa$ describes <exponential decay>, rather than an oscillatory <wavenumber> in the barrier. Both <exponential functions> are needed on the finite interval: the growing term is not excluded by a condition at infinity.

The <wavefunction> and its <derivative> are <continuous> at each face, giving four linear matching conditions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
1+r&=A+B,&ik(1-r)&=\kappa(A-B),\\
Ae^{\kappa a}+Be^{-\kappa a}&=te^{ika},&
\kappa(Ae^{\kappa a}-Be^{-\kappa a})&=ikte^{ika}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Their solution supplies the <transmission amplitude>
$$
t=\frac{e^{-ika}}{\cosh(\kappa a)+i\frac{\kappa^2-k^2}{2k\kappa}\sinh(\kappa a)}.
$$
A short derivation is to use the two right-face equations to express $Ae^{\kappa a}$ and $Be^{-\kappa a}$ in terms of $t$, then substitute into the left-face equations. Taking the squared <modulus> gives the <rectangular barrier transmission probability>.

= Tunnelling decay constant
{parent=Finite rectangular barrier matching}
{title2=$\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$}

The <tunnelling decay constant> is the inverse length controlling the <exponential decay> of an <evanescent wave> in a constant <potential barrier> with $E<V_0$. The <wavefunction> amplitude decay length is $1/\kappa$; the squared-amplitude decay length is $1/(2\kappa)$.

= Rectangular barrier transmission probability
{parent=Finite rectangular barrier matching}
{title2=$T(E,a)$}

For equal exterior <potential energies> and constant <mass>, the exact <transmission probability> is
$$
T(E,a)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1},&0<E<V_0,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{mV_0a^2}{2\hbar^2}\right]^{-1},&E=V_0,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\left[1+\frac{V_0^2}{4E(E-V_0)}\sin^2(qa)\right]^{-1},&E>V_0,
\end{cases}
\qquad q=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}.
$$
Below the barrier, the <hyperbolic sine> is positive for $a>0$, so $0<T<1$. At $E=V_0$, the interior <wavefunction> is linear in $x$; either neighbouring formula has the displayed finite <limit>, since $\sinh z/z$ and $\sin z/z$ tend to one. Above the barrier, the interior <wavefunction> oscillates: the <quantum reflection probability> generally remains nonzero, but $qa=n\pi$ for a positive integer $n$ gives perfect <transmission probability> $T=1$ through constructive <wave interference>. These are <scattering resonances>, not below-barrier <quantum tunnelling>.

At half height, $E=V_0/2$, we have $k=\kappa$ and $T=\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa a)$, consistent with <finite square barrier transmission at half barrier height> after replacing this width $a$ by that example's width $2a$. In the illustration, the width is four times $\ell=\hbar/\sqrt{2mV_0}$ and $E/V_0=1/2$, giving $T\simeq0.01388$. The plotted <wavefunction> comes from solving the four interface equations; the <transmission probability> curve uses the closed formula.

= Opaque barrier approximation
{parent=Rectangular barrier transmission probability}
{title2=$T\sim\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}$}

For fixed $0<E<V_0$ and $\kappa a\gg1$, the exact <rectangular barrier transmission probability> gives
$$
T\sim\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{2a\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}\right].
$$
This is the leading term of an <asymptotic expansion>: the <wavefunction> amplitude scale is $e^{-\kappa a}$, and the <transmission probability> scale is $e^{-2\kappa a}$, with an interface prefactor. Increasing width by $\Delta a$ multiplies this approximation by $e^{-2\kappa\Delta a}$. At fixed incident <energy>, increasing barrier height increases the <tunnelling decay constant> as $\sqrt{V_0-E}$; heavier <nonrelativistic particles> also have stronger suppression. For the numerical example in <rectangular barrier transmission probability>, the approximation gives $0.01397$, compared with the exact $0.01388$.

The regime condition matters. Near $E=V_0$ at fixed width, $\kappa a$ tends to zero and the <opaque barrier approximation> fails; use the exact threshold value. For $\kappa a\ll1$, expansion of the <hyperbolic sine> gives
$$
T\simeq\left[1+\frac{mV_0^2a^2}{2\hbar^2E}\right]^{-1}.
$$
This tends to one as $a\to0$ at fixed $E,V_0$, but the condition $\kappa a\ll1$ alone does not imply nearly perfect transmission if the remaining factor is large. At $E>V_0$, use the oscillatory formula and its <scattering resonances>, rather than an <exponential decay> law.

= Evanescent probability current
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}
{title2=$J=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$}

A nonzero <probability density> inside a barrier does not by itself imply transport. In one dimension, the <probability current> of a <wavefunction> is $J=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$. A single <evanescent wave> $Ce^{-\kappa x}$ has $\psi^*\psi'=-\kappa|C|^2e^{-2\kappa x}$, a real quantity, hence $J=0$. This is what happens on the inaccessible side of a <potential barrier> extending to infinity with $E<V_0$.<Quantum Physics I (8.04), Spring 2016/Lecture 6>[p. 5, equations (3.4)-(3.6): probability continuity equation and probability current]{ref}

For the finite-barrier <wavefunction> $Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x}$, the cross terms instead give
$$
J=\frac{2\hbar\kappa}{m}\operatorname{Im}(B^*A).
$$
The relative <quantum phase> of the two <probability amplitudes> permits a nonzero, spatially constant <probability current>, even though neither <exponential function> separately carries it. The <probability continuity equation> gives $\partial_xJ=0$ for this <stationary state>. Matching the <wavefunction> and its <derivative> also makes $J$ continuous at both faces, so
$$
J_{\mathrm{inc}}=\frac{\hbar k}{m},\qquad
J_{\mathrm{ref}}=-\frac{\hbar k}{m}|r|^2,\qquad
J_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|t|^2,\qquad
R+T=1.
$$
Here $R=|r|^2$ is the <quantum reflection probability>, and $T=J_{\mathrm{tr}}/J_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is the <transmission probability>. The net exterior <probability current> equals the interior <probability current>; it is not lost as the <wavefunction> decreases. A physical incident <wave packet> supplies finite total probability, while the <stationary state> describes the <energy> component used to compute its transmitted flux.

= Current-distance test of vacuum tunnelling
{parent=Quantum tunnelling}

<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others> made barrier width experimentally adjustable. Their apparatus used a pointed <electrical conductor> opposite a conducting plate, with fine positioning by <piezoelectricity>. Rubber-tube support and magnetic levitation above a cooled bowl exhibiting <superconductivity> suppressed vibrations; the junction itself remained near room temperature.<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others>[p. 178, Fig. 1: tungsten tip, platinum plate, piezodrive and helium-cooled superconducting lead bowl]{ref}

The readout was <electrical resistance> versus displacement at a fixed <electric potential difference> of 60 mV of either polarity. They observed an <exponential function> dependence spanning four orders of magnitude. Cleaning and better <vacuum> increased the inferred barrier from approximately 0.6-0.7 eV to 3.2 eV. Reproducible curves and the cleaning response argued against deforming contamination layers; an appropriate slope also helped exclude an ordinary conducting contact. The paper reports residual contamination, electronics-limited range and a jump attributed to release of thermal stress; its curve origins generally do not determine absolute gap widths.<Tunneling through a controllable vacuum gap by Gerd Binnig and others>[p. 179, equation (1), Fig. 2 and accompanying control/limitation discussion]{ref}

The ideal <tunnel junction> model explains the slope. At fixed small <electric potential difference>, fixed tip geometry and approximately unchanged <density of states>, take an effective barrier <energy> $\Phi$ above the relevant <electron> states and approximate the current magnitude by
$$
I(s)\simeq C\exp(-2\kappa s),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}}{\hbar},\qquad
-\frac{d\ln I}{ds}\simeq2\kappa.
$$
The coefficient $C$ includes the applied <electric potential difference>, available occupied and empty <quantum states>, and tip geometry. A single <transmission probability> is not an <electric current>: a junction combines contributions from many <electron> states in both directions, with the <electric potential difference> producing a net imbalance. Its tilted, material-dependent barrier is only approximated by the equal-exterior <rectangular potential barrier>; the exact single-particle formula above is not a complete junction model.

For the chosen model value $\Phi=4$ <electronvolts>, $\kappa\simeq10.25\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}$. Increasing separation by $0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ multiplies $I$ by about $0.129$, a reduction by about $7.8$. = Constant-current scanning tunneling microscopy
{parent=Scanning tunneling microscopy}
{title2=$I(x,y,z)=I_0$}

Choose a fixed tip-sample <electric potential difference> and a positive target <electric current> magnitude $I_0$. Since increasing the gap reduces <quantum tunnelling>, excessive <electric current> calls for retraction; insufficient <electric current> calls for approach. = Electronic contrast in scanning tunneling microscopy
{parent=Scanning tunneling microscopy}
{title2=$z=h+\ln\rho/(2\kappa)+\mathrm{constant}$}

Separate geometry from electronic response in an original one-dimensional toy <tunnel junction>. Write the geometric surface height as $h(x)$, tip height as $z(x)$, and positive gap as $s(x)=z(x)-h(x)$. Assume low fixed tip-sample <electric potential difference>: the sampled <energy> window is narrow enough that the tip <density of states> is featureless and the sample <local density of states> varies little across that window. Neglect thermal broadening, changes of tip shape and lateral averaging. Let $\rho(x)>0$ be the effective sample <local density of states> divided by a positive reference value, so it is dimensionless. Choose a spatially constant effective barrier <energy> $\Phi>0$, a positive coefficient $C$ with units of <electric current>, and
$$
I(x,z)=C\rho(x)\exp\bigl[-2\kappa(z-h(x))\bigr],\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m_e\Phi}}{\hbar}>0.
$$
Here $m_e$ is the <electron> <mass>, $\hbar$ the reduced <Planck constant>, and $\kappa$ the <tunnelling decay constant>. This is an illustrative low-bias model, not a universal calibration formula. At fixed $I=I_0>0$, taking a <logarithm> gives
$$
\ln(I_0/C)=\ln\rho-2\kappa(z-h),\qquad
z(x)=h(x)+\frac{\ln\rho(x)}{2\kappa}+s_0,\qquad
s_0=\frac{\ln(C/I_0)}{2\kappa}.
$$
The <logarithms> have dimensionless arguments. Only constant $\rho$ makes the recorded height differ from geometric height by a constant. Multiplying $\rho$ by $r>0$ raises the apparent height by $\ln r/(2\kappa)$ without moving the geometric surface.

For a concrete ambiguity, choose $\kappa=10\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}$, $a=0.020\,\mathrm{nm}$, lateral period $L=1\,\mathrm{nm}$, $s_0=0.50\,\mathrm{nm}$, and $q(x)=\cos(2\pi x/L)$. Compare
$$
\begin{aligned}
h_A(x)&=a q(x),&\rho_A(x)&=1,\\
h_B(x)&=0,&\rho_B(x)&=\exp\bigl(2\kappa a q(x)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Both produce exactly $z(x)=s_0+a q(x)$ at $I_0=C e^{-2\kappa s_0}=C e^{-10}$. Substitution checks this without identifying $\rho$ with geometric height: case A has constant gap $s_0$, while case B has gap $s_0+a q(x)$ and a compensating electronic factor. The chosen $\rho_B$ ranges from $e^{-0.4}$ to $e^{0.4}$, approximately $0.6703$ to $1.4918$; every gap remains positive. The chosen $\kappa$ corresponds to $\Phi\simeq3.81$ <electronvolts>, a positive effective barrier.

At fixed tip height $z_f=s_0$, both cases instead give
$$
\frac{I(x,z_f)}{I_0}=\exp\bigl[0.4q(x)\bigr].
$$
Their maximum-to-minimum <electric current> ratio is $e^{0.8}\simeq2.226$. Thus even adding this fixed-height scan at the same <electric potential difference> cannot distinguish these deliberately chosen surfaces within the toy model. Independent structural information or additional electronic measurements are required. Changing <electric potential difference> can supply such information, but also changes the assumptions used to define $C$ and $\rho$.

For the <finite square well> $V=-V_0$ on $|x|<a$ and $V=0$ outside, with $a,V_0>0$, there is always an even <bound state>, however small $V_0$ is. For the odd equation, $h(\kappa)=\nu(1-e^{-2a\kappa})-\kappa$ is strictly <concave>, starts at zero with slope $2a\nu-1$, and tends to negative infinity. It has one positive <equation root> precisely when $2a\nu>1$. Thus there is one <bound state> for $-\hbar^2/(2ma)\leq g<0$, and two for $g<-\hbar^2/(2ma)$. For $g\geq0$ positivity of the <Hamiltonian operator> excludes negative <energy>, and square integrability excludes nonnegative-<energy> exterior solutions. At $g=g_c$ the odd zero-<energy> solution is bounded but not square integrable: it is a <zero-energy scattering resonance>, not a second <bound state>.

= Odd-parity S-matrix of a symmetric double-delta potential
{title2=$S_{--}$}
{c}
{parent=Symmetric double-delta potential}

For positive <wavenumber> $k$, the odd channel of the <symmetric double-delta potential> has
$$
S_{--}(k)=e^{-2ika}
\frac{k e^{ika}+U_0\sin(ka)}{k e^{-ika}+U_0\sin(ka)}.
$$
For real $k$ its numerator and denominator are complex conjugates, making the channel <unitary operator>[unitary]. Its poles are the zeros of $k e^{-ika}+U_0\sin(ka)$.

= Odd bound state of a symmetric double-delta potential
{parent=Odd-parity S-matrix of a symmetric double-delta potential}

For $U_0<0$, an odd bound state has $k=i\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$ and obeys
$$
2\kappa=-U_0\left(1-e^{-2\kappa a}\right).
$$
A nonzero positive solution exists exactly when $-U_0a>1$; equality is the zero-energy threshold.

= Odd resonance of a strongly repulsive symmetric double-delta potential
{parent=Odd-parity S-matrix of a symmetric double-delta potential}

For $U_0a\gg1$, the lowest odd <scattering resonance> pole lies near the first odd mode trapped between the barriers:
$$
k=\frac\pi a\left(1-\frac1{U_0a}+O((U_0a)^{-2})\right)
-i\frac{\pi^2}{U_0^2a^3}\left(1+O((U_0a)^{-1})\right)
$$
in its real part and to leading nonzero order in its imaginary part. Writing $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)=E_R-i\Gamma/2$, the survival probability decays as $e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$.

= Scattering by a delta potential

For $V(x)=V_0\delta(x)$, $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, and $\gamma=mV_0/(\hbar^2k)$, continuity and the derivative jump give

\qquad
r=\frac{-i\gamma}{1+i\gamma}.

They obey $|r|^2+|t|^2=1$ and $r^*t+t^*r=0$.

= Stone-von Neumann theorem
{c}
{parent=Weyl relations}

= Gaussian projection in a Weyl representation
{parent=Weyl relations}
{title2=$P=\pi^{-1}\int e^{-|a|^2/2}W(a)\,da$}

The integrated Gaussian in any nonzero regular representation of the <Weyl relations> is an orthogonal projection and obeys $PW(a)P=e^{-|a|^2/2}P$. Gaussian integration with the Weyl phase proves both identities. It cannot be zero: conjugating it by all Weyl operators would otherwise make the Fourier transform of every Gaussian-weighted matrix coefficient vanish, including its nonzero value at the identity. = Ehrenfest theorem
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{c}
{wiki}

Ehrenfest’s theorem makes expectation values obey Hamiltonian commutator equations analogous to classical motion.

For a possibly time-dependent observable $O$,
$$
\frac d{dt}\langle O\rangle
=\frac1{i\hbar}\langle[O,H]\rangle
+\left\langle\frac{\partial O}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$

= Proof of Ehrenfest theorem from the Schrodinger equation
{parent=Ehrenfest theorem}

Differentiating $\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$ and using $i\hbar|\dot\psi\rangle=H|\psi\rangle$ and its adjoint gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\langle O\rangle
=\frac i\hbar\langle[H,O]\rangle
+\left\langle\frac{\partial O}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
$$

= Classical equations from Ehrenfest theorem
{parent=Ehrenfest theorem}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+U(x)$, the <canonical commutation relation> gives
$$
m\frac d{dt}\langle x\rangle=\langle p\rangle,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle U'(x)\rangle,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}\langle H\rangle=0.
$$
These are the expectation-value analogues of <Newton's second law> and conservation of energy.

= Particle in a box
{synonym}

The <infinite square well> is an ideal confinement model with <potential energy> $V(x)=0$ for $0<x<L$ and infinite walls outside. The walls impose <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $\phi(0)=\phi(L)=0$ and a vanishing exterior <wavefunction>. Inside, the time-independent <Schrödinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2}=E\phi,\qquad \phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The first wall removes the <cosine> term; the second requires $\sin(kL)=0$, hence $k=n\pi/L$. The $n=0$ solution is identically zero and cannot satisfy <wavefunction normalization>. Each <energy eigenstate> has $n-1$ interior <nodes of a standing wave>, at $x=jL/n$ for $j=1,\ldots,n-1$. More curvature costs more <kinetic energy>, explaining the $n^2$ growth. The positive <ground-state energy> is a confinement effect: a constant zero-energy <wavefunction> cannot satisfy both walls and remain normalized.

For an <electron> with mass $m_e=9.1093837139\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ in a box of length $L=1\,\mathrm{nm}$, use $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$. These constants are from the 2022 CODATA adjustment. <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>{ref}. Substitution gives
$$
E_1=\frac{h^2}{8m_eL^2}=6.024667\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J\approx0.376030\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad E_2=4E_1\approx1.504121\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Doubling $L$ quarters every <energy eigenvalue>; doubling $m$ halves them. This scaling makes the <particle in a box> useful for estimating confinement energies before attempting a more realistic calculation. A <finite square well> permits exterior tails for <bound states> and has only finitely many bound <energy eigenstates>. For an <electron>, the scalar box calculation assumes fixed <spin> and no spin-dependent interactions.

For the <ground state>, the probability of finding the particle in the middle half of the box is fully computable:
$$
\Pr\left(\frac L4<x<\frac{3L}4\right)=\int_{L/4}^{3L/4}\frac2L\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\,dx=\left[\frac xL-\frac{\sin(2\pi x/L)}{2\pi}\right]_{L/4}^{3L/4}=\frac12+\frac1\pi\approx0.818310.
$$
Thus half the available length contains about $81.8\%$ of the probability. The figure contrasts the signed <wavefunctions> with their nonnegative <probability densities>: a negative lobe is an amplitude with opposite <quantum phase>, not negative probability.

An equal <quantum superposition> of the first two <energy eigenstates> gives a simple example of moving density:
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{\phi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\phi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}}{\sqrt2},\qquad |\psi|^2=\frac{\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2}{2}+\phi_1\phi_2\cos\frac{(E_2-E_1)t}{\hbar}.
$$
Its density period is $T=h/(E_2-E_1)\approx3.66608\,\mathrm{fs}$ for the <electron> box above. At $t=0$ the cross term enhances the left side; at $T/4$ it vanishes; at $T/2$ it enhances the right side. More quantitatively,
$$
\Pr(0<x<L/2)=\frac12+\frac4{3\pi}\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T},
$$
which takes the values $0.924413$, $0.5$, and $0.075587$ at those times. <Orthogonality> keeps the total probability one throughout. An <energy measurement> still returns $E_1$ or $E_2$ with equal probabilities: changing position density arises from relative <quantum phase>, not changing populations. A <mixed state> describing a statistical mixture with those populations has the constant <probability density> $(\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2)/2$; it lacks this <matter-wave interference> term.

= Half revival in an infinite square well
{parent=Infinite square well}
{title2=$t_*=2ma^2/(\pi\hbar),\quad U(t_*)=-R_a$}

For the <infinite square well> of length $a$, the <energy eigenvalues> are $E_n=n^2E_1$, with $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2ma^2)$. At $t_*=\pi\hbar/E_1$, the <dynamical phase> is $e^{-iE_nt_*/\hbar}=(-1)^n$. Since the <midpoint parity operator> has eigenvalues $(-1)^{n+1}$ in the same complete <orthonormal basis>, the <time-evolution operator> is exactly $U(t_*)=-R_a$ on every state. At $2t_*$ every phase is one, giving a full revival.

Thus a state becomes its reflected copy, up to a <global phase>, at the half revival. Its position <probability density> is reflected, while its mean momentum changes sign because $PR_a=-R_aP$. For an initially reflection-symmetric <probability density>, the initial and half-revival position distributions agree even when their <probability currents> point in opposite directions.

= Parabolic variational trial for a particle in a box

For an <infinite square well> of width $a$, the normalized trial $\widetilde\psi=\sqrt{30/a^5}\,x(a-x)$ obeys both <Dirichlet boundary conditions> and has no interior <nodes of a wavefunction>. Its <kinetic energy> is

= Ground state at the top of a central rectangular barrier
{parent=Infinite square well}
{title2=$\psi_{\rm middle}=\text{constant}$}

Consider hard walls at $x=0,3L$, zero <potential energy> on $0<x<L$ and $2L<x<3L$, and a central rectangular barrier whose height equals the <ground state> energy $E$. The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> gives $\psi''=0$ on the central interval. Reflection symmetry and the <nodeless theorem for a one-dimensional ground state> require an even, positive state about $3L/2$, hence a constant central segment. <Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step> makes the derivatives vanish at $L,2L$. The side segments are proportional to $\sin(\pi x/(2L))$ and $\sin(\pi(3L-x)/(2L))$. Their common plateau value is $1/\sqrt{2L}$ after <wavefunction normalization>, and $E=\pi^2\hbar^2/(8mL^2)$. = Boundary force in an infinite square well
{parent=Infinite square well}
{title2=$P_j=\hbar^2|\partial_x\psi(x_j)|^2/(2m)$}

For a normalized sufficiently regular solution of the <Schrödinger equation> on $[0,L]$ with Dirichlet walls, the instantaneous outward forces on the walls have magnitudes
$$
P_0=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\psi'(0,t)|^2,\qquad P_L=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\psi'(L,t)|^2.
$$
A wall displacement changes the kinetic-energy quadratic form by the boundary term $-P_j\,dL_j$, giving the local force. Equivalently, direct integration by parts in the time derivative of the mean particle momentum gives
$$
\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(|\psi'(0,t)|^2-|\psi'(L,t)|^2\right)=P_0-P_L.
$$
The force on the particle is the opposite of the net force $-P_0+P_L$ on the walls. For a stationary <energy eigenstate> each magnitude equals $2E_n/L$. For the <equal superposition of the first two box states>, writing $C=E_1/L$ and $\Omega=3E_1/\hbar$ gives $P_0=C(5+4\cos\Omega t)$ and $P_L=C(5-4\cos\Omega t)$. Both time averages equal $2\langle E\rangle/L$, the classical mean force on one wall at that energy, even though the quantum forces vary continuously in time.

= Equal superposition of the first two box states
{parent=Infinite square well}

For an <infinite square well> on $[0,L]$, choose $\phi_n=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $E_n=n^2E_1$, $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$. The state $\psi=(\phi_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\phi_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar})/\sqrt2$ has constant <expectation value> $\langle E\rangle=5E_1/2$, but

\langle x\rangle=\frac L2-\frac{16L}{9\pi^2}\cos\left(\frac{3E_1t}{\hbar}\right).
$$
Indeed, the diagonal position <matrix elements> are $L/2$, whereas the off-diagonal element is

\langle1|x|2\rangle=\frac2L\int_0^L x\sin\frac{\pi x}L\sin\frac{2\pi x}L\,dx=-\frac{16L}{9\pi^2}.
$$
A different initial relative phase shifts the oscillation in time. The oscillation comes from <quantum interference>, while the constant mean energy follows from the fixed populations of the two <energy eigenstates>.

= Midpoint current of a two-mode box state

= Position matrix elements in a symmetric infinite square well
{parent=Infinite square well}

To derive it, set $y=x+a$ and use $2\sin Ay\sin By=\cos[(A-B)y]-\cos[(A+B)y]$. For unequal positive integer indices $r,s$, the constant part of $x=y-a$ integrates to zero by <orthogonality>, while

Taking the difference for $j=r-s$ and $j=r+s$ yields the formula after setting $r=k+1,s=n+1$. It vanishes for equal parity and gives $x_{01}=-32a/(9\pi^2)$ for this phase convention.

On $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$, with infinite exterior potential, separation of variables gives the normalized eigenbasis
$$
\psi_{nm}=\frac2{\sqrt{ab}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}a\sin\frac{m\pi y}b,\qquad E_{nm}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2M}\left(\frac{n^2}{a^2}+\frac{m^2}{b^2}\right),\quad n,m\geq1.
$$
The boundary values vanish and the functions are orthonormal. A general state is a superposition of these products. A degenerate energy eigenspace can contain nonseparable linear combinations of products at the same energy, so a single separated product is not the most general stationary wavefunction in that eigenspace.

= Third-level degeneracy in a rectangular quantum box
{parent=Two-dimensional infinite square well}
{title2=$b/a=\sqrt{8/3}$}

For $b>a$ in a <two-dimensional infinite square well>, the lowest two states are $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$, both nondegenerate. The next energy is the smaller of $E_{21}$ and $E_{13}$: every other candidate lies above at least one of these. Requiring equality gives $3/a^2=8/b^2$, or $b/a=\sqrt{8/3}$. In units $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2Ma^2)$ the lowest distinct energies are then $11/8$, $5/2$ and $35/8$, with degeneracies one, one and two. The third-level pair is exactly $(2,1)$ and $(1,3)$.

= Orbital angular momentum
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Orbital angular momentum is $L=-i\hbar,x\times\nabla$; in particular $L_3=-i\hbar(x_1\partial_{x_2}-x_2\partial_{x_1})$.

= Highest-weight complex-coordinate orbital wavefunction
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}
{title2=$L^2[(x+iy)^nf(r)]=\hbar^2n(n+1)(x+iy)^nf(r)$}

For a differentiable radial factor and integer $n\geq0$, the coordinate polynomial obeys $L_3\psi=n\hbar\psi$ and $L_+\psi=0$. The identity $L^2=L_-L_++L_3^2+\hbar L_3$ gives the displayed angular <eigenvalue>. Lowering gives $L_-\psi=-2n\hbar z(x+iy)^{n-1}f(r)$, a nonzero <eigenfunction> with the same angular <eigenvalue> when $n>0$ and the original state is nonzero. At $n=0$ lowering gives the zero function, which is not an <eigenfunction>. Negative powers satisfy local differential identities off the axis but are not regular angular states.

= Angular momentum of a homogeneous harmonic polynomial
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}
{title2=$\hat L^2H_l=l(l+1)\hbar^2H_l$}

For a homogeneous <harmonic polynomial> $H_l$ of degree $l$ in three dimensions, the <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> gives $DH_l=lH_l$, where $D=\mathbf x\cdot\nabla$. The <orbital angular momentum> identity $\hat L^2=-\hbar^2(r^2\Delta-D^2-D)$ therefore gives $\hat L^2H_l=l(l+1)\hbar^2H_l$. This constructs <angular momentum eigenstates> directly from polynomials.

= Angular momentum of a radial function times a linear polynomial
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}
{title2=$\hat L^2[H(r)(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x)]=2\hbar^2H(r)(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x)$}

Orbital <angular momentum operators> annihilate a radial multiplier $H(r)$. Their squared sum sends every degree-one homogeneous <polynomial> to $2\hbar^2$ times that polynomial, so a nonzero normalizable state $H(r)(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf x)$ has angular momentum quantum number $l=1$. With real $H$ and real coefficients, its third-component <expected value> is zero by angular symmetry, though it need not be an eigenstate of that component.

= Angular momentum ladder operator
{title2=$L_\pm$}
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}

The operators $L_\pm=L_x\pm iL_y$ satisfy
$$
[L_z,L_\pm]=\pm\hbar L_\pm,
\qquad
[L^2,L_\pm]=0.
$$
They therefore change the <magnetic quantum number> $m$ by $\pm1$ while preserving the total angular-momentum quantum number $\ell$.

= Angular momentum raising operator
{parent=Angular momentum ladder operator}
{title2=$L_+=L_1+iL_2$}

= Raising operator for angular momentum
{synonym}

The <angular momentum raising operator> $L_+=L_1+iL_2$ satisfies $[L_3,L_+]=\hbar L_+$ and $[L^2,L_+]=0$. It preserves the total <angular momentum> while increasing the third-component <magnetic quantum number> by one, whenever its result is nonzero. Its <adjoint operator> is the <angular momentum lowering operator> $L_-$. Expanding the product using the <angular momentum commutation relations> gives $L_-L_+=L^2-L_3^2-\hbar L_3$, hence
$$
\|L_+\psi\|^2=\hbar^2[\lambda-m(m+1)]\|\psi\|^2
$$
for simultaneous <eigenvalues> $\lambda\hbar^2,m\hbar$. For normalized $|l,m\rangle$ this yields $L_+|l,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{(l-m)(l+m+1)}|l,m+1\rangle$ after fixing consistent phases. At $m=l$ the result is zero, the upper <angular momentum ladder endpoint>. For a different <quantization axis>, cyclically permute the components; for example $L_2+iL_3$ raises the $L_1$ projection.

= Angular momentum ladder endpoint
{parent=Angular momentum ladder operator}

The norm of a raised or lowered <angular momentum> state must be nonnegative. At the upper or lower endpoint, the relevant <ladder operator> annihilates the state; the zero vector is not an <eigenvector>.

= Squared orbital angular momentum in position and momentum operators

Operator ordering gives

$$

In position representation,

\boxed{L^2=-\hbar^2\nabla_{S^2}^2}.
$$
The radial derivatives cancel after substituting $P=-i\hbar\nabla$ into the Cartesian identity for $L^2$.

= Angular momentum ladder variable
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}

The coordinate combinations $x_1\pm ix_2$ have $L_3$ eigenvalues $\pm\hbar$, so their powers carry <magnetic quantum numbers> $\pm m$.

= Angular momentum of a complex-coordinate Gaussian state
{parent=Angular momentum ladder variable}

If $\psi=z e^{-r^2/(2a^2)}$ in two dimensions, then $L_z\psi=\hbar\psi$ and $L_z\psi^*=-\hbar\psi^*$. Orthogonality therefore makes the angular-momentum expectation of $\alpha\psi+\beta\psi^*$ equal to $(|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2)\hbar$ for normalized coefficients.

= Rotational invariance of a central-potential Hamiltonian
{parent=Orbital angular momentum}

For
$$
H=\frac{P^2}{2m}+U(|X|),
$$
both $P^2$ and $|X|^2$ are rotational scalars, so $[H,L_i]=0$. Consequently $H,L^2,L_3$ commute pairwise and can be simultaneously diagonalized.

= Separation of a two-dimensional central-potential eigenstate
{parent=Rotational invariance of a central-potential Hamiltonian}
{title2=$\psi_k(r,\phi)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}f(r)e^{ik\phi}$}

An energy and <orbital angular momentum> <eigenstate> for a real two-dimensional <central potential> can be chosen with integer $k$ and real radial function $f$. Its radial equation is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(f''+r^{-1}f'-k^2r^{-2}f\right)+Vf=Ef,\qquad \int_0^\infty r f^2\,dr=1.
$$
<Quantum degeneracy> permits other <stationary states>, including $f(r)\cos(k\phi)/\sqrt\pi$, which are not single angular exponentials. <Separation of variables> supplies a basis rather than describing every superposition in a degenerate eigenspace.

= Three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Its energies are $E_N=\hbar\omega(N+3/2)$, and level $N$ has degeneracy $\binom{N+2}{2}$.

= Cartesian number state of the three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator
{title2=$|n_1,n_2,n_3\rangle$}
{parent=Three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}

From the normalized <ground state> annihilated by every $A_i$, the normalized Cartesian number states are
$$
|n_1,n_2,n_3\rangle
=\prod_{i=1}^3\frac{(A_i^\dagger)^{n_i}}{\sqrt{n_i!}}|0\rangle.
$$
They have energy $\hbar\omega(n_1+n_2+n_3+3/2)$.

= Orbital angular momentum in oscillator ladder operators
{title2=$L_i=-i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}A_j^\dagger A_k$}
{parent=Three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}

For the <three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator>, substituting $X_i=\sqrt{\hbar/(2\mu\omega)}(A_i+A_i^\dagger)$ and $P_i=i\sqrt{\mu\hbar\omega/2}(A_i^\dagger-A_i)$ into $L_i=\varepsilon_{ijk}X_jP_k$ gives $L_i=-i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}A_j^\dagger A_k$. On the first-excited Cartesian states $|j\rangle=A_j^\dagger|0\rangle$, this is the three-dimensional vector representation and $L^2|j\rangle=2\hbar^2|j\rangle$, so the whole first-excited level has orbital quantum number $\ell=1$.

= Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

For unit mass and angular frequency,
$$
H=a_x^\dagger a_x+a_y^\dagger a_y+1.
$$
The level with total occupation $N=n_x+n_y$ has energy $N+1$ and degeneracy $N+1$ in units where $\hbar=1$.

= Circular ladder operators of a two-dimensional oscillator
{parent=Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}
{title2=$L_z=\hbar(N_+-N_-)$}

For Cartesian <creation and annihilation operators> $a_x,a_y$, define
$$
a_+^\dagger=\frac{a_x^\dagger+ia_y^\dagger}{\sqrt2},\qquad
a_-^\dagger=\frac{a_x^\dagger-ia_y^\dagger}{\sqrt2},\qquad
N_\pm=a_\pm^\dagger a_\pm.
$$
Their <Hermitian adjoints> define $a_\pm$. The <canonical commutation relations> imply $[a_\pm,a_\pm^\dagger]=1$, with mixed <commutators> zero. Direct expansion gives
$$
H=\hbar\omega(N_++N_-+1),\qquad
L_z=-i\hbar(a_x^\dagger a_y-a_y^\dagger a_x)=\hbar(N_+-N_-).
$$
Each circular creation adds one <energy> quantum and <angular momentum> $+\hbar$ or $-\hbar$. At total occupation $N$, the allowed <angular momentum> values are $N\hbar,(N-2)\hbar,\ldots,-N\hbar$, each once in this <energy eigenspace>.

= Shifted two-dimensional oscillator in a uniform electric field
{parent=Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}
{title2=$H'=H(x-a,y)-a^2/2,\quad a=e\mathcal E$}

Completing the square in $H'=\tfrac12(p_x^2+p_y^2+x^2+y^2)-e\mathcal E x$ shifts the oscillator centre to $(a,0)$ and lowers every energy by $a^2/2$. Translated eigenfunctions keep their normalization. The <angular momentum> about the new centre is $L'=(x-a)p_y-yp_x$, which commutes with $H'$, whereas the <angular momentum> about the old centre has $[L,H']=-i\hbar ay$. For $a\ne0$, a common normalizable eigenfunction of $L,H'$ would have $y\psi=0$ and hence vanish in $L^2(\mathbb R^2)$. This stronger argument excludes individual common eigenstates; noncommutativity alone generally excludes only a complete common eigenbasis.

= Oscillator bilinear commutator
{parent=Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}

For bosonic modes satisfying $[a_i,a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$, the number-preserving bilinears $T_{ij}=a_i^\dagger a_j$ obey
$$
[T_{ij},T_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}T_{il}-\delta_{il}T_{kj}.
$$

= Schwinger boson representation
{parent=Two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator}
{c}
{wiki}

Contracting two-mode oscillator bilinears with the Pauli matrices,
$$
T^a=\frac12\sigma^a_{ij}a_i^\dagger a_j,
$$
gives $[T^a,T^b]=i\varepsilon_{abc}T^c$. The subspace with total occupation $N$ is the spin-$N/2$ irreducible representation and has dimension $N+1$.

= Local spin constraint in the Schwinger boson representation
{parent=Schwinger boson representation}
{title2=$(a_n^\dagger a_n+b_n^\dagger b_n)|\psi\rangle=2s|\psi\rangle$}

= Schwinger boson constraint
{c}
{synonym}

The <Schwinger boson representation> satisfies $S_n^2=(N_n/2)(N_n/2+1)$, where $N_n$ is the local total <boson> <number operator>. Thus a prescribed local <spin> $s$ requires the displayed operator constraint at every site. A condition on $\langle N_n\rangle$ alone is insufficient: for $\langle N_n\rangle=2s$, $\langle S_n^2\rangle=s(s+1)+\operatorname{Var}(N_n)/4$. Projecting onto the required <eigenspace> at each site removes the unwanted local <irreducible spin representations>.

= Two-mode oscillator Casimir identity
{parent=Schwinger boson representation}
{title2=$H^2=4\omega^2J^2+\hbar^2\omega^2$}

For the <two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator>, $H=\hbar\omega(N+1)$ and the <Schwinger boson representation> gives $J^2=\hbar^2N(N+2)/4$. Substitution yields the displayed relation, including its quantum constant term. The fixed-total-occupation sector is a spin-$j=N/2$ representation, so <energy> is $\hbar\omega(2j+1)$ with degeneracy $2j+1$.

= Bosonic superexchange

Effective exchange of internal states through virtual <boson> hopping into energetically costly occupation sectors. For two <bosons> initially one per site and charging energy $U\Delta^2/4$, the second-order operator is $-(t^2/U)(3+4\mathbf s_a\cdot\mathbf s_b)$ for dimensionless <spins>. The triplet is lowered and the singlet is unchanged in this example; signs for other energy functions require their actual denominators.

= Time-dependent perturbation theory
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Expanding a state in unperturbed energy eigenstates with their free phases converts the time-dependent Schrodinger equation into coupled equations for slowly varying amplitudes. To first order, the perturbation on the right-hand side acts on the initial unperturbed state.

This is the time-dependent branch of <perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)>; its corrections describe transition amplitudes.

= Time-dependent quantum perturbation
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{title2=$V(t)$}

An additional time-dependent operator in a quantum <Hamiltonian>. It may couple stationary states or shift their phases. It is the physical operator being treated, whereas <time-dependent perturbation theory> is a method for approximating the response.

= Counter-rotating weak-transition amplitude
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{title2=$c_2=-\Omega\sum_{\pm}(e^{i(\omega_0\pm\omega)t}-1)/[2(\omega_0\pm\omega)]$}

The first-order response to a cosine drive contains both resonant and counter-rotating terms. The <rotating-wave approximation> removes the rapidly oscillating contribution only under near-resonant, weak-drive and appropriate time-scale assumptions. It does not remove the separate requirement of weak excitation when the initial amplitude is held fixed.

= Symmetric exponential excitation pulse
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{title2=$f(t)=e^{-b|t|}$}

For $b>0$, the <Fourier transform> of $f$ is $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{ixt}dt=2b/(b^2+x^2)$. A weak interaction $D_0f(t)\cos\omega t$ between <energy levels> of gap $\hbar\omega_0$ gives final <probability amplitude> $-idb\{[b^2+(\omega_0-\omega)^2]^{-1}+[b^2+(\omega_0+\omega)^2]^{-1}\}/\hbar$, where $d$ is the transition <matrix element>. Only when $b,|\omega_0-\omega|\ll\omega_0$ does the single resonant lobe give an approximately squared <Lorentzian line shape> excitation profile. Its <full width at half maximum> is $2b\sqrt{\sqrt2-1}$ in <angular frequency>.

= Exponentially decaying electric-field pulse
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{title2=$\widetilde E(\omega)=E_0\tau/(1-i\omega\tau)$}

A uniform field $E(t)=E_0e^{-t/\tau}\Theta(t)$ couples through $V=-qEx$. Its first-order transition amplitude is $-q\langle f|x|i\rangle\widetilde E(\omega_{fi})/(i\hbar)$, so the leading probability is $q^2|x_{fi}|^2E_0^2\tau^2/[\hbar^2(1+\omega_{fi}^2\tau^2)]$. A real pulse has equal spectral intensity at opposite frequencies; populations and dipole elements determine which upward or downward transitions are possible. Small peak amplitude does not remove the need to check total transition probability.

= Sudden-onset spectral tail of an exponential pulse
{parent=Exponentially decaying electric-field pulse}

The step at the beginning of $E_0e^{-t/\tau}\Theta(t)$ retains high-frequency weight. For fixed nonzero transition frequency, $|\widetilde E|\to E_0/|\omega|$ as $\tau\to\infty$. A slowly decaying pulse with an abrupt onset is therefore not an adiabatically switched perturbation. The diagonal accumulated phase can separately grow with duration.

= Square-pulse resonance profile
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{title2=$\left|\int_0^Te^{i\delta t}dt\right|^2=T^2\operatorname{sinc}^2(\delta T/2)$}

A constant-amplitude drive lasting $T$ gives the <Fourier transform> in the title, where $\operatorname{sinc}x=\sin x/x$ and its value at zero is one. The <transition probability> in weak driving has a finite resonant maximum, first zeros at $\delta=\pm2\pi/T$, and decreasing side lobes. Longer duration increases the peak as $T^2$ and narrows its width as $1/T$. The <rotating-wave approximation> discards a rapidly oscillating counterrotating term; it can have a small absolute error but a large relative error at the resonant term's zeros.

= Fermi's golden rule
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}
{c}
{title2=$\Gamma_{i\to f}=(2\pi/\hbar)|V_{fi}|^2\delta(E_f-E_i)$}

For a weak time-independent perturbation switched on at zero time, first-order <time-dependent perturbation theory> gives a transition <probability>
$$
P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}
\frac{4\sin^2[(\omega_f-\omega_i)t/2]}{(\omega_f-\omega_i)^2}.
$$
The frequency kernel has area $2\pi t$ and width of order $1/t$. Against a smoothly varying continuum of final states, it approaches $2\pi t\delta(\omega_f-\omega_i)$, giving the rate $2\pi|V_{fi}|^2\delta(\omega_f-\omega_i)/\hbar^2$. Using $E=\hbar\omega$ gives the energy form above. One integrates the <Dirac delta function> against the appropriate <density of states>; it is not an ordinary finite number for one isolated resonant level.

The time must be long enough to resolve the smooth continuum and short enough that the total transition <probability> remains small. For continuum variation scale $\Delta\omega$ and total rate $\Gamma$, the usual window is $(\Delta\omega)^{-1}\ll t\ll\Gamma^{-1}$. A discrete finite spectrum also limits this approximation through its recurrences.

= Golden-rule derivation of the Born cross-section
{parent=Fermi's golden rule}

For box-normalized <plane waves> in volume $\mathcal V$, a potential has <matrix element> $\widetilde W(\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i)/\mathcal V$. The final-state density per energy and <solid angle> is $\mathcal V m k_f/[(2\pi)^3\hbar^2]$. Dividing the golden-rule rate by incident flux $\hbar k_i/(m\mathcal V)$ gives $(m^2/4\pi^2\hbar^4)(k_f/k_i)|\widetilde W|^2$. Elastic scattering has $k_f=k_i$; the arbitrary box volume cancels.

= Electric-dipole interaction
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}

In the long-wavelength approximation, a particle of charge $q$ in a spatially uniform electric field has perturbation
$$
\Delta(t)=-q\mathbf E(t)\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf r.
$$
Its transition amplitudes are controlled by matrix elements of the position operator.

= Electric dipole operator
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}
{title2=$\mathbf d=\sum_a q_a\mathbf r_a$}

The <electric dipole operator> is the charge-weighted position operator of a system. It is odd under <parity> and transforms as a rank-one <spherical tensor operator>. In the nonrelativistic electric-dipole approximation it acts on spatial coordinates without directly changing spin, explaining the parity, angular-momentum and approximate spin selection rules.

= Diagonal electric-dipole matrix elements and parity
{parent=Electric dipole operator}

A <parity> eigenstate has zero expectation of an odd coordinate, hence no diagonal electric-dipole coupling to a uniform oscillatory field. Stationarity alone does not prove this: an oscillator displaced by $x_c$ has $\langle x\rangle=x_c$. Degenerate subspaces or a parity-breaking static field require a separate symmetry analysis.

= Off-resonant photon scattering
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}
{title2=$\Gamma_{\rm sc}\sim\Gamma|\Omega|^2/(4\Delta^2)$}

A detuned optical drive still admixes a decaying <excited state>. Its spontaneous scattering rate is of order $\Gamma|\Omega|^2/(4\Delta^2)$ for weak far-detuned driving with coupling $\hbar\Omega/2$. Increasing detuning at fixed optical power reduces both unwanted scattering and useful coherent coupling.

= Electric dipole transition
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}

An <electric dipole transition> is a transition driven, in the long-wavelength approximation, by the <electric-dipole interaction> $H_{\mathrm{int}}=-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E(t)$. Its leading amplitude is proportional to a <matrix element> of the <electric dipole moment> between the initial and final states. A vanishing element gives an <electric-dipole selection rule>; a nonzero element alone does not ensure a large probability, since the drive frequency, duration and strength also matter. For a charged <quantum harmonic oscillator> driven along its axis, $x=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(a+a^\dagger)$ gives the single-quantum rule $\Delta n=\pm1$.

= Two-photon transition
{parent=Electric dipole transition}

A coherent transition involving two atom-field interactions. <two-photon absorption> uses a sum of <photon> <energies>; stimulated absorption-emission uses their difference. A second-order intermediate contribution need not represent real population in a stationary atomic state.

= Two-photon absorption
{parent=Two-photon transition}
{title2=$E_f-E_i=\hbar(\omega_1+\omega_2)$}

<absorption> of two <photons> whose summed <energy> matches an atomic transition. In the weak-field regime its rate scales with the product of the two intensities. Two <electric-dipole interactions> preserve the initial <parity>.

= Virtual intermediate state in two-photon absorption
{parent=Two-photon absorption}

An intermediate contribution to a second-order transition amplitude need not be an occupied, on-shell stationary atomic state. Both time orderings must be summed with their <energy> denominators. Large detuning can suppress real intermediate occupation while preserving a coherent two-photon coupling. Near resonance, finite linewidth, saturation and sequential population transfer must be treated explicitly.

= Doppler-free two-photon absorption
{parent=Two-photon absorption}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta_{\rm Doppler}=(\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2)\cdot\mathbf v$}

Absorbing one <photon> from each of two opposing equal-frequency beams cancels the first-order Doppler shift through their nearly zero summed <wavevector>. Absorbing both <photons> from the same beam does not share this cancellation and can create a broad background.

= Degenerate two-photon selection rule
{parent=Two-photon absorption}

For identical equal-frequency electric-dipole <photons>, exchange symmetry removes the antisymmetric rank-one transition tensor. Ranks zero and two survive. A zero-to-zero angular-momentum transition can occur, while zero-to-one is forbidden in this approximation; the rank-two triangle condition still permits some nonzero-to-nonzero transitions with a change of one.

= Electric dipole transition moment
{parent=Electric dipole transition}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\mu_{fi}=\langle\psi_f|\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}|\psi_i\rangle$}

= Transition dipole moment
{synonym}

The <matrix element> of the <electric dipole moment> operator between two <quantum states>. Electric-dipole transition strength is proportional to its squared magnitude; <molecular symmetry> can force every Cartesian component to vanish.

= Electric-dipole approximation
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}
{title2=$H_{\rm int}\simeq-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E(\mathbf R,t)$}

For a localized charge distribution in an external field varying little across its extent, expand the external scalar potential about a reference point $\mathbf R$. The constant term couples to total charge; the first spatially varying term gives the <electric-dipole interaction> $-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E(\mathbf R,t)$, where $\mathbf d$ is the <electric dipole moment>. Higher spatial multipoles are neglected.

For a passing charge at minimum distance $b$, the atomic extent must be small compared with $b$. For a plane electromagnetic wave, the analogous long-wavelength condition is $ka\ll1$, as in the <electric-dipole approximation for radiation>. A short interaction time and a spatially uniform field across the atom are different assumptions; neither implies the other.

= Electric-dipole selection rule
{parent=Electric-dipole interaction}

= Dipole selection rule
{synonym}

Because the position operator has odd <parity>, an electric-dipole matrix element vanishes between states of the same parity. For orbital-angular-momentum eigenstates, the full rule is $\Delta\ell=\pm1$ and $\Delta m=0,\pm1$, with the field polarization selecting the allowed change in $m$.

= First-order parity selection in an infinite well
{parent=Electric-dipole selection rule}

For $0<x<w$, normalized states $\psi_n=\sqrt{2/w}\sin[(n+1)\pi x/w]$ have centre-reflection eigenvalue $(-1)^n$. Since $x-w/2$ is odd, off-diagonal electric-dipole matrix elements vanish between equal-parity states. Ground state n=0 cannot reach n=2 at first order; an initial n=1 can reach even labels including n=0. This does not impose an all-orders prohibition: two dipole interactions can restore the original parity. The general nonzero elements follow from <position matrix elements in a symmetric infinite square well> after shifting the origin.

= Higher-order parity-allowed transitions in an infinite well
{parent=First-order parity selection in an infinite well}

= Laporte rule
{parent=Electric-dipole selection rule}
{c}

For an inversion-symmetric system, electric-dipole transitions require opposite parities. Transitions between d-shell terms of the same gerade parity can acquire weak vibronic or symmetry-breaking intensity.

= Continuum transition probability at long times
{parent=Time-dependent perturbation theory}

For a harmonic perturbation and continuum detuning $\Omega(k)$, first-order amplitudes contain
$$
\frac{\sin(\Omega(k)t/2)}{\Omega(k)/2}.
$$
The distributional limit
$$
\frac{\sin^2(\Omega t/2)}{(\Omega/2)^2}
\longrightarrow 2\pi t\,\delta(\Omega)
$$
selects energy-conserving continuum states and produces a transition probability linear in time.

= Time-independent perturbation theory
{parent=Quantum mechanics}

Time-independent perturbation theory expands the eigenvalues and eigenstates of $H_0+V$ in powers of a small perturbation $V$.

This is the stationary branch of <perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)>.

= Linear confinement correction to Coulomb bound states
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}
{title2=$\Delta E_{nl}=b\langle r\rangle_{nl}$}

For <reduced mass> $\mu$ and Coulomb strength $a$, put $a_q=\hbar^2/(\mu a)$. The perturbation $br$ shifts the $1s,2s,2p$ energies by $3ba_q/2,6ba_q,5ba_q$, respectively. These values follow by integrating $r^3|R_{nl}|^2$. Because the perturbation is central, angular <orthogonality> makes its <matrix> diagonal inside each Coulomb <energy> <eigenspace>. It splits the accidental degeneracy between different orbital angular momenta while preserving magnetic degeneracy.

= Gaussian perturbation of a quantum harmonic oscillator
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}
{title2=$\Delta E_1/\Delta E_0=\alpha^2/(\alpha^2+\beta^2)$}

For perturbation $V_0e^{-\beta^2x^2}$ and oscillator inverse length $\alpha=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}$, direct Gaussian expectation values give $\Delta E_0=V_0\alpha/(\alpha^2+\beta^2)^{1/2}$ and $\Delta E_1=V_0\alpha^3/(\alpha^2+\beta^2)^{3/2}$. A narrow perturbation samples the odd state's <node> and suppresses its shift by two extra <powers> of $\alpha/\beta$. A broad perturbation approaches a constant <energy> offset for both states.

= Nondegenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

<Perturbation theory> for an isolated <eigenvalue> with a one-dimensional <eigenspace>. <First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> gives the diagonal perturbation expectation, while <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> accounts for coupling to other unperturbed eigenstates through energy denominators. Symmetry can permit its use within one block even if another, uncoupled symmetry block has the same unperturbed eigenvalue.

= Cosine perturbation of the ground level in a one-dimensional box
{parent=Nondegenerate perturbation theory}
{title2=$E=\dfrac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}-\dfrac\epsilon2-\dfrac{ma^2\epsilon^2}{16\pi^2\hbar^2}+O(\epsilon^3)$}

For the normalized sine eigenfunctions of a <particle in a box> on $(0,a)$, multiplying the lowest one by $\epsilon\cos(2\pi x/a)$ gives $\epsilon(\phi_3-\phi_1)/2$. Thus the first-order diagonal shift is $-\epsilon/2$, and the only second-order coupling is to level three. With $E_3=9E_1$, its contribution is $(\epsilon^2/4)/(E_1-E_3)=-\epsilon^2/(32E_1)$, giving the displayed expansion. This simple selection rule makes the full second-order <perturbation theory> sum a single term.

= Perturbative mixing parameter
{parent=Nondegenerate perturbation theory}

The ratio $|\langle k|V|i\rangle/(E_i-E_k)|$ measures a leading admixture between unperturbed eigenstates. Small <matrix elements> compared with relevant gaps justify a nondegenerate expansion locally. A small overall perturbation does not suffice near a coupled degeneracy, where the nearly degenerate block needs diagonalization.

= Hellmann–Feynman theorem
{c}
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$E'(\lambda)=\langle\psi,H'(\lambda)\psi\rangle$}

For a differentiable normalized <eigenstate> of a differentiable <self-adjoint> <Hamiltonian>, differentiate $H\psi=E\psi$ and pair with $\psi$. The terms involving $\psi'$ cancel because $H$ is <self-adjoint>, leaving the stated formula. It turns energy <derivatives> into observable expectations. A smoothly chosen eigenbranch and suitable operator-domain regularity are required, particularly at degeneracies or crossings.

= First-order energy correction
{title2=$E_n^{(1)}$}
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

The first-order energy correction is the coefficient of the perturbation parameter in the expansion of an energy eigenvalue.

= Apparent level crossing from a finite-interval oscillator perturbation
{parent=First-order energy correction}
{title2=$H'=\tfrac12m\omega^2(a^2-x^2)\mathbf1_{[-a,a]}$}

Let $\sigma^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)$ and $g(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ be the <quantum harmonic oscillator> ground-state <probability density>. Put $p(a)=\int_{-a}^ag(x)\,dx$. The first excited-state density is $x^2g(x)/\sigma^2$. <Integration by parts>, using $g'=-xg/\sigma^2$, gives the first-order difference
$$
E'_1-E'_0=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}[p(a)-2ag(a)].
$$
The bracket grows from zero to one, since its derivative is $2a^2g(a)/\sigma^2$. Thus the first-order predicted level gap for $H_0+\lambda H'$ is $\hbar\omega[1-\lambda\{p(a)-2ag(a)\}/2]$. For every $\lambda>2$ it becomes negative at sufficiently large $a$.

For $\lambda>1$ the full potential has a central barrier and symmetric minima at $\pm a$. The unperturbed even <ground state> samples the central barrier more strongly than the odd <excited state>, whose node reduces its shift. This explains the perturbative tendency, but the negative predicted gap is a failure diagnostic of the first-order truncation. The exact one-dimensional <ground state> remains nodeless and below the first excited state; in a high symmetric barrier the actual pair has a small positive <double-well tunneling splitting>.

= Nondegenerate energy eigenvalue
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

An <energy eigenvalue> is nondegenerate when its <eigenspace> is one-dimensional. Its normalized <eigenstate> is then unique up to a complex phase.

= First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For a normalized eigenstate $|n\rangle$ belonging to a <nondegenerate energy eigenvalue>,
$$
E_n^{(1)}=\langle n|V|n\rangle,
\qquad
|n^{(1)}\rangle
=\sum_{m\ne n}|m\rangle
\frac{\langle m|V|n\rangle}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
$$

= First-order ground state of the quartic oscillator
{parent=First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory}

For a <quantum harmonic oscillator> perturbed by $\lambda X^4$, write $X=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(A+A^\dagger)$. Since
$$
(A+A^\dagger)^4|0\rangle=3|0\rangle+6\sqrt2|2\rangle+2\sqrt6|4\rangle,
$$
<first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> gives
$$
\boxed{|0_\lambda\rangle
=|0\rangle-\frac{\hbar\lambda}{4m^2\omega^3}
\left(3\sqrt2|2\rangle+\sqrt{\frac32}|4\rangle\right)+O(\lambda^2).}
$$

= Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For a normalized nondegenerate eigenstate $|n\rangle$,
$$
E_n^{(2)}
=\sum_{m\ne n}
\frac{|\langle m|V|n\rangle|^2}
{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}.
$$
For a ground state, every denominator is negative, so the correction is nonpositive.

= Ground-state second-order energy shift is nonpositive
{parent=Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory}

For a nondegenerate lowest state, every excited-state denominator $E_0-E_k$ is negative. Since each numerator $|V_{k0}|^2$ is nonnegative, their sum is nonpositive, and strictly negative if a coupled higher state exists. The statement assumes the perturbative expansion and appropriate sum or spectral integral are defined.

= Imaginary-coupled two-level Hamiltonian
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

For
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}E_-&-i\lambda\\i\lambda&E_+\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \Delta=E_+-E_->0,
$$
the exact energies are
$$
E_{\pm}^{\rm exact}
=\frac{E_-+E_+}{2}
\pm\sqrt{\frac{\Delta^2}{4}+\lambda^2}.
$$
The perturbation series around $\lambda=0$ has radius $\Delta/2$, set by the branch points $\lambda=\pm i\Delta/2$.

= Bright and dark state reduction of a star-coupled Hamiltonian
{parent=Time-independent perturbation theory}

Suppose one state $|0\rangle$ couples to degenerate states $|1\rangle,\ldots,|m\rangle$ with amplitudes $v_1,\ldots,v_m$, while the degenerate states do not couple to each other. The normalized bright state proportional to $\sum_jv_j|j\rangle$ is the only combination coupled to $|0\rangle$. Every orthogonal combination is a dark state and remains an exact eigenstate at the degenerate energy. The remaining spectrum comes from a two-dimensional Hamiltonian whose off-diagonal entry is $(\sum_j|v_j|^2)^{1/2}$.

= Degenerate perturbation theory
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

First-order degenerate perturbation theory diagonalizes the perturbing operator within each degenerate eigenspace of the unperturbed Hamiltonian.

= First-order cancellation for an exchange-odd perturbation
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}
{title2=$PVP=0$}

Let $X$ be a <unitary operator> with $X^2=I$, and let $P$ be the orthogonal <projection operator> onto an unperturbed <energy eigenspace>. Suppose $X$ acts as the same sign $\varepsilon=\pm1$ throughout that <eigenspace> and $XVX=-V$. For any $u,v$ in the <eigenspace>, preservation of the <inner product> gives $\langle u,Vv\rangle=\langle Xu,XVv\rangle=-\langle\varepsilon u,V\varepsilon v\rangle=-\langle u,Vv\rangle$. Hence every projected <matrix element> vanishes, so $PVP=0$ and <degenerate perturbation theory> gives zero first-order <energy> corrections. A zero diagonal alone would be insufficient. For equal <spins>, the <exchange parity of two coupled identical spins> is constant on each total-<spin> sector, while their spin-projection difference changes sign under exchange.

= Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}
{c}

A perturbative <unitary conjugation> eliminating couplings between chosen separated energy sectors. For $H=H_0+V$ and an <anti-Hermitian> $S$ satisfying $[S,H_0]=-V$ at leading order, the transformed <Hamiltonian> contains $\tfrac12[S,V]$ at second order. Exactly resonant coupled states must be retained in a common block. The method was used for the Anderson-to-Kondo relation by <John Robert Schrieffer> and <P. A. Wolff>.

= Resonance obstruction to perturbative elimination
{parent=Schrieffer-Wolff transformation}

A perturbative generator dividing by an unperturbed energy difference cannot eliminate a nonzero coupling between exactly degenerate states. Those states must remain in the retained degenerate block. Small denominators also invalidate an expansion when the actual hopping <matrix> element, including occupation enhancement, is not small in comparison.

= Occupation-dependent virtual hopping
{parent=Schrieffer-Wolff transformation}

If site-transfer energies are $\mathcal E(\Delta)$, the left-ordered raising-hop generator has coefficient $f(\Delta)=t/[\mathcal E(\Delta)-\mathcal E(\Delta-2)]$. Nonlinear occupation energies generally make the effective second-order shifts and spin-exchange amplitudes depend on the occupation sector.

= Occupancy-preserving second-order bosonic hopping
{parent=Occupation-dependent virtual hopping}
{title2=$H_{\rm occ}^{(2)}=t[f(\Delta)XY-f(\Delta+2)YX]$}

With $X=\sum_s a_s^\dagger b_s$, $Y=X^\dagger$ and a real coefficient $f$, the fixed-occupancy second-order operator is $t[f(\Delta)XY-f(\Delta+2)YX]$. <Normal ordering> gives $XY=N_a+P$ and $YX=N_b+P$, where $P=\sum_{ss^\prime}a_s^\dagger a_{s^\prime}b_{s^\prime}^\dagger b_s$ exchanges internal states without changing site totals.

= Equal-coupling three-state perturbation
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}

A real perturbation with zero diagonal and every off-diagonal element $t$ has one <eigenvalue> $2t$ on the symmetric <vector> and a twofold <eigenvalue> $-t$ on its <orthogonal complement>. It is $t(J_3-I)$, where $J_3$ is the <all-ones matrix>. Diagonal entries alone do not give first-order energies in a degenerate subspace.

= Degenerate orbital exchange splitting from a product perturbation
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}
{title2=$\delta E_\pm=\pm\alpha|\langle r|x|s\rangle|^2$}

Two distinguishable particles with equal one-particle spectra have degenerate ordered orbital products $|r,s\rangle$ and $|s,r\rangle$ for $r\ne s$. Suppose both individual position means vanish, as in a centred parity-symmetric well. The perturbation $\alpha x_1x_2$ then has zero diagonal <matrix elements>, but its restriction to this two-dimensional subspace is
$$
W=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0&|x_{rs}|^2\\|x_{rs}|^2&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad x_{rs}=\langle r|x|s\rangle.
$$
<Degenerate perturbation theory> gives normalized symmetric and antisymmetric <eigenfunctions> $(|r,s\rangle\pm|s,r\rangle)/\sqrt2$ with shifts $\pm\alpha|x_{rs}|^2$. Thus vanishing diagonal expectations in a chosen degenerate basis do not imply vanishing first-order energy shifts. Distinguishability permits both exchange symmetries; it does not forbid their energy splitting.

For an <infinite square well> of width $a$, the first two orbitals have $|x_{12}|^2=256a^2/(81\pi^4)$ by the <position matrix elements in a symmetric infinite square well>. If the particles are identical <spin-one-half> <fermions>, the spatially symmetric and antisymmetric combinations must instead be paired with the <singlet state> and <triplet states>, respectively. A spin-independent perturbation leaves each allowed spin multiplicity intact.

= Perturbation diagonal within a degenerate eigenspace
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}

If the perturbation is already diagonal in a basis of a degenerate eigenspace, those basis vectors receive its diagonal entries as first-order shifts.

= Exact diagonalization within a degenerate subspace
{parent=Degenerate perturbation theory}

When a perturbation preserves each unperturbed degenerate block and has no inter-block coupling, diagonalizing each block can give the exact spectrum.

= Resonant two-to-one oscillator coupling
{parent=Exact diagonalization within a degenerate subspace}

For oscillators of frequencies $1$ and $2$, the perturbation
$$
H'=A^{\dagger2}B+A^2B^\dagger
$$
preserves $N_A+2N_B$. On the energy-$9/2$ basis $|3,0\rangle,|1,1\rangle$, it is the matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt6\\\sqrt6&0\end{pmatrix},
$$
so the symmetric and antisymmetric combinations are exact eigenstates with shifts $\pm\sqrt6\lambda$.

= Pauli X eigenstate

The Pauli $X$ eigenstates are $(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$.

These are the eigenvectors of one <Pauli matrix>, not an alternative name for the whole matrix family.

= Rectangular potential barrier
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

A <rectangular potential barrier> has a constant elevated potential over a finite interval and a lower constant potential outside it. Matching wavefunctions and derivatives at the interval endpoints gives its reflection and transmission amplitudes. It is a special <potential barrier>.

= Rectangular-barrier tunnelling probability
{parent=Rectangular potential barrier}
{title2=$T=[1+V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)/(4E(V_0-E))]^{-1}$}

For a <rectangular potential barrier> of height $V_0$, width $a$, and equal constant potentials outside, a particle with $0<E<V_0$ has exterior <wave number> $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and interior decay constant $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. Write the three <wavefunctions> as $e^{ikx}+re^{-ikx}$, $Ae^{\kappa x}+Be^{-\kappa x}$, and $te^{ikx}$. Continuity of the <wavefunction> and its derivative at both interfaces determines the <transmission probability> $T=|t|^2$ in the title. Equal exterior velocities are what permit the last equality.

For $\kappa a\gg1$, $\sinh^2(\kappa a)\simeq e^{2\kappa a}/4$, so
$$
T\simeq\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}.
$$
Keeping only $e^{-2\kappa a}$ estimates the exponentially dominant <quantum tunnelling> suppression; the interface prefactor can still change a numerical answer by a factor of order one.

= Perturbation theory
{disambiguate=quantum mechanics}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

<Perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)> expands a quantum problem around a solvable Hamiltonian. <Time-independent perturbation theory> expands stationary energies and eigenstates; <time-dependent perturbation theory> expands dynamical amplitudes and transitions.

= Hartree equations
{c}
{parent=Quantum mechanics}
{wiki}

The <Hartree equations> are self-consistent one-particle equations obtained by approximating a many-body <wave function> by a <tensor product> of orbital <wave functions>. Each orbital sees the potential averaged over the other particles' <probability densities>; attractive Newtonian mean-field variants include the <gravitational Hartree equation>.

= Hartree method
{parent=Hartree equations}
{c}

= Hartree approximation
{c}
{synonym}

A variational product of one-electron orbitals produces coupled one-electron equations, each containing the electrostatic density of the other occupied orbitals. Iterating orbitals and potentials gives a <self-consistent field>. The product omits fermionic exchange; an antisymmetric <Slater determinant> instead leads to Hartree-Fock theory.

= Pair-energy double counting in Hartree orbital sums
{parent=Hartree method}

For Hartree direct integrals $J_{ij}$ and orbital <eigenvalues> $\epsilon_i$, the product-state <energy> is $E=\sum_i\epsilon_i-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}$. Each pair enters two orbital mean fields but only once in the total interaction <energy>. Orbital energies are therefore not independent additive particle energies.

= Hartree product state
{parent=Hartree equations}
{c}

A many-particle trial state built from one-particle factors, without the additional correlations of a general many-body <wavefunction>. For identical <bosons> occupying one normalized orbital $\varphi$, the symmetric fixed-number form is $(a_\varphi^\dagger)^N|0\rangle/\sqrt{N!}$. Its field expectation is zero because annihilation changes particle number, while its one-body <density matrix> has the macroscopic eigenvalue $N$. An antisymmetrized multi-orbital fermionic trial state instead belongs to the Hartree-Fock construction.

= Hartree potential
{parent=Hartree equations}

The local mean <Coulomb potential> generated by the occupied <electron> density. A separate positive background or external charge contribution must be included where appropriate. Hartree-Fock exchange is nonlocal and removes equal-orbital self-interaction from the total direct-plus-exchange result.

= Hartree electrostatic energy
{parent=Hartree potential}
{c}
{title2=$E_H=e^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0)\iint n(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r')/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\,d^3r\,d^3r'$}

The classical electrostatic self-energy functional of an electron number density is the expression in the title. The factor one-half prevents counting each pair twice. Its functional derivative is the <Hartree potential>; integrating density times that potential gives $2E_H$. The functional includes an unphysical one-electron self-interaction unless other terms cancel it. It differs from the <Hartree energy> atomic unit.
