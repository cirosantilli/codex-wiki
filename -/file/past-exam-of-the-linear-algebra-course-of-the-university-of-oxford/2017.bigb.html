All seventeen source subparts are answered below. Part 1(b)(iii) needs a <separable polynomial> hypothesis over a general <field>; its intended case, a counterexample to the unrestricted claim, and the corrected general result are distinguished in that solution. This paper uses the <characteristic polynomial> convention $\chi_T(x)=\det(T-xI)$, which differs by $(-1)^{\dim V}$ from the monic convention.

The department's https://www.maths.ox.ac.uk/members/students/undergraduate-courses/examinations-assessments/overview-examinations[solution-release policy] covers even examination years and the exceptional release for 2019; it does not advertise 2017 solutions. = Solution
{parent=i}

For a <linear transformation> $T$ of a <finite-dimensional vector space> over a <field> $F$, its <minimal polynomial> $m_T$ is the unique <monic polynomial> of least degree satisfying $m_T(T)=0$. There is at least one nonzero <polynomial> with this property because the powers $I,T,T^2,\ldots$ lie in the finite-dimensional <vector space> of linear maps $V\to V$. Dividing any other such <polynomial> by $m_T$ leaves a remainder of smaller degree that also vanishes at $T$, so that remainder must be zero. This also proves uniqueness.

If $C$ represents $T$ in any <basis>, the paper's <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=\det(C-xI).}
$$
It is independent of the <basis>, since changing the <basis> replaces $C-xI$ by a <similar matrix>, preserving its <determinant>. Its degree is $\dim V$ and its leading coefficient is $(-1)^{\dim V}$; $m_T$ is always <monic polynomial>[monic].

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(T)=0.}
$$
Let $d=\dim V$. Extend the <field> to an <algebraically closed field> and choose a <basis> in which the <matrix> of $T$ is an <upper triangular matrix>, as permitted. Write its diagonal entries as $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$ and put $V_i=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_i)$. Each $V_i$ is an <invariant subspace>, and
$$
(T-\lambda_iI)V_i\subseteq V_{i-1}.
$$
Apply $T-\lambda_dI$ first, then $T-\lambda_{d-1}I$, and continue. The resulting product sends $V_d$ into $V_0=\{0\}$. These factors are <commuting operators>, so
$$
\prod_{i=1}^d(T-\lambda_iI)=0.
$$
The <determinant> of an <upper triangular matrix> gives $\chi_T(x)=(-1)^d\prod_i(x-\lambda_i)$. Thus $\chi_T(T)=0$ after the <field extension>. Its entries were in the original <field>, whose embedding is injective, so they were already zero there. The zero-dimensional case is immediate as well.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The displayed <matrix> is the transpose convention for a <companion matrix>. Expanding $\det(xI-A)$ along its last row gives
$$
\det(xI-A)=x^n+\alpha_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+\alpha_1x+\alpha_0=f(x).
$$
Indeed, the last entry contributes $(x+\alpha_{n-1})x^{n-1}$ and the entry in column $j<n$ contributes $\alpha_{j-1}x^{j-1}$; the cofactor signs cancel those from the superdiagonal entries $-1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\chi_T(x)=(-1)^nf(x).}
$$
The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> gives $f(A)=0$, so $m_T$ divides $f$. To exclude a smaller <minimal polynomial>, let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be the <standard basis>. The first $n-1$ rows of $A$ show
$$
e_1^{\mathsf T}A^j=e_{j+1}^{\mathsf T}\qquad(0\leq j<n).
$$
If $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_jA^j=0$, multiplying on the left by $e_1^{\mathsf T}$ gives $\sum_jc_je_{j+1}^{\mathsf T}=0$, so every coefficient is zero. No nonzero <polynomial> of degree below $n$ vanishes at $A$. Since $f$ and $m_T$ are <monic polynomials>,
$$
\boxed{m_T=f.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $B=D+K$, where $D$ has $A$ in each diagonal block and zero elsewhere, while $K$ has $I$ immediately above the diagonal and zero elsewhere. These <block matrices> satisfy $DK=KD$: either product has $A$ immediately above the diagonal. Also, $K^i$ has $I$ on its $i$th superdiagonal and is zero elsewhere.

The <binomial theorem for commuting matrices> therefore yields
$$
B^j=\sum_{r=0}^j\binom jrD^{j-r}K^r.
$$
Only $r=i$ can contribute to block $(k,k+i)$, provided that block lies in the <matrix>. Hence
$$
\boxed{(B^j)_{k,k+i}=\begin{cases}
0,&j<i,\\
\binom ji A^{j-i},&j\geq i.
\end{cases}}
$$
Thus $\beta_{i,j}=\binom ji$, interpreted as an element of the <field>. The formula remains valid in positive <field characteristic>: some of these coefficients can then vanish. This possibility matters in the next part.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Write $f=m_T$. Part (ii) identifies the first superdiagonal block exactly:
$$
\boxed{\bigl(f(B)\bigr)_{k,k+1}=f'(A),}
$$
where $f'$ is the <formal derivative>. Thus the candidate is $g=f'$. If the <irreducible polynomial> $f$ has $f'\ne0$, its <polynomial degree> makes $f$ and $f'$ coprime. Equivalently, $f$ is a <separable polynomial>. This includes every irreducible <polynomial> over a <field> of characteristic zero, and every irreducible <polynomial> over a <perfect field>.

Under this separability hypothesis, <Bézout's identity for polynomials> gives $a(x)f(x)+b(x)f'(x)=1$. Evaluating at $A$ proves that $C=f'(A)$ is an <invertible matrix>. Moreover, $f(B)$ has zero diagonal blocks, because $f(A)=0$. It is therefore a <nilpotent matrix> with $f(B)^N=0$. The only product of $N-1$ strictly upward block steps that reaches block $(1,N)$ uses the first superdiagonal every time, so
$$
\bigl(f(B)^{N-1}\bigr)_{1,N}=C^{N-1}\ne0.
$$
For $N=1$, the corresponding assertion is simply $f(B)^0=I\ne0$. The <block upper triangular matrix> $B$ has
$$
\chi_U(x)=\det(B-xI)=\det(A-xI)^N=(-1)^{nN}f(x)^N.
$$
The <minimal polynomial> divides this <characteristic polynomial>. Since $f$ is an <irreducible polynomial>, it must be a power of $f$; the exact <nilpotency index> just found forces
$$
\boxed{m_U=f^N,\qquad \chi_U=(-1)^{nN}f^N\quad\text{if }f\text{ is separable}.}
$$

**The arbitrary-field qualification is necessary.** In the <rational function field> $F=\mathbb F_2(t)$, take $f(x)=x^2-t$. This is an <irreducible polynomial>: a square of a rational function has even order of vanishing at $t=0$, while $t$ has odd order. Its <formal derivative> is zero. With $N=2$,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\t&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}A&I\\0&A\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B^2=tI_4.
$$
The off-diagonal block in $f(B)$ is zero, and $m_U=f$, rather than $f^2$. No alternative <polynomial> $g$ coprime to $f$ could represent that block: $g(A)=0$ would imply $m_T=f\mid g$. Thus the printed coprimality claim fails as stated over a general <field>.

There is also a precise general replacement. In characteristic $p>0$, let $q=p^r$ be the largest power for which $f(x)=h(x^q)$, so that $h'\ne0$; set $q=1$ in characteristic zero. Irreducibility of $f$ implies that $h$ is an <irreducible polynomial>, so $h$ and $h'$ are coprime. The <binomial theorem> in characteristic $p$ gives $B^q=D^q+K^q$. Expanding $h(D^q+K^q)$, with the coefficients equivalently supplied by the <Hasse derivatives>, gives
$$
f(B)=K^qH,
$$
where $H$ commutes with $K$ and is a <block upper triangular matrix> whose diagonal blocks are $h'(A^q)$. These blocks are invertible by <Bézout's identity for polynomials>, since $h(A^q)=f(A)=0$. Thus $H$ is invertible, and
$$
f(B)^s=K^{qs}H^s=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad qs\geq N.
$$
The same <characteristic polynomial> and divisibility argument now prove the corrected arbitrary-field answer:
$$
\boxed{m_U(x)=f(x)^{\lceil N/q\rceil},\qquad
\chi_U(x)=(-1)^{nN}f(x)^N.}
$$
This is the <minimal polynomial of a companion block chain>; $q=1$ recovers the intended separable case.

The <dual space> is
$$
V'=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F),
$$
the <vector space> of <linear functionals>, with pointwise operations
$$
(\phi+\psi)(v)=\phi(v)+\psi(v),\qquad
(a\phi)(v)=a\phi(v).
$$
Given the <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, its <dual basis> consists of the coordinate <linear functionals>
$$
\boxed{f_i\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^na_je_j\right)=a_i,
\qquad f_i(e_j)=\delta_{ij}.}
$$
In particular, $\dim V'=\dim V=n$.

= Solution

The <dual map> acts by <function composition>:
$$
\boxed{T':W'\longrightarrow V',\qquad T'(\phi)=\phi\circ T.}
$$
The composition is a <linear functional> on $V$, and pointwise operations give $T'(a\phi+b\psi)=aT'\phi+bT'\psi$. Thus this really is a <linear transformation>; its direction reverses that of $T$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $\phi\in V'$ and $v\in V$,
$$
(I'\phi)(v)=\phi(Iv)=\phi(v),
$$
so $I'$ is the <identity map> on the <dual space>. If $S:U\to V$ and $T:V\to W$, then, for $\psi\in W'$ and $u\in U$,
$$
\bigl((TS)'\psi\bigr)(u)=\psi(T(Su))
=\bigl(S'(T'\psi)\bigr)(u).
$$
Equality on all vectors proves
$$
\boxed{(TS)'=S'T'.}
$$
Apply this to $RR^{-1}=R^{-1}R=I$. It gives both $R'(R^{-1})'=I'$ and $(R^{-1})'R'=I'$. Consequently the <dual map> is invertible and
$$
\boxed{(R')^{-1}=(R^{-1})'.}
$$

= Solution

The <annihilator of a vector subspace> is
$$
U^\circ=\{\phi\in V':\phi(u)=0\text{ for every }u\in U\}.
$$
Choose a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ of $U$ and extend it to a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ of $V$. In the <dual basis>, precisely $f_{r+1},\ldots,f_d$ span $U^\circ$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dim U^\circ=\dim V-\dim U.}
$$
The canonical restriction <linear transformation>
$$
\rho:V'\longrightarrow U',\qquad \rho(\phi)=\phi|_U
$$
has <kernel of a linear map> $U^\circ$. It is surjective: extend any <linear functional> on $U$ by assigning arbitrary values, for instance zero, to the additional <basis> vectors. The <first isomorphism theorem for vector spaces> gives the explicit <linear isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{V'/U^\circ\ \longrightarrow U',\qquad
\phi+U^\circ\longmapsto\phi|_U.}
$$
It is natural because the displayed map itself makes no <basis> choice; a <basis> was used only to prove surjectivity. In particular, $U'$ is canonically a <quotient vector space> of $V'$, rather than canonically a subspace of $V'$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $\phi=T'\psi$ and $v\in\ker T$, then $\phi(v)=\psi(Tv)=0$. Thus $\operatorname{im}T'\subseteq(\ker T)^\circ$, using the <annihilator of a vector subspace>.

Conversely, let $\phi$ vanish on the <kernel of a linear map> $\ker T$. Define a <linear functional> on $\operatorname{im}T$ by $\psi_0(Tv)=\phi(v)$. This is well-defined: $Tv=Tw$ implies $v-w\in\ker T$, so $\phi(v)=\phi(w)$. Extend $\psi_0$ to a <linear functional> $\psi$ on $W$, using the extension argument from part (i). Then $T'\psi=\phi$, proving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}T'=(\ker T)^\circ.}
$$
The dimension formula from part (i) and the <rank-nullity theorem> now give
$$
\operatorname{rank}T'=\dim(\ker T)^\circ
=\dim V-\dim\ker T=\operatorname{rank}T.
$$
To deduce the <eigenvalue> statement, apply this equality of <rank of a linear map> to $T-\lambda I$. Its <dual map> is $T'-\lambda I$, so their <kernel of a linear map> dimensions agree. Thus the same $\lambda$ are <eigenvalues>, with the same <geometric multiplicities>.

For <algebraic multiplicities> as well, part (a)(iii) gives
$$
\bigl((T-\lambda I)^r\bigr)'=(T'-\lambda I)^r,
$$
so equality of <rank of a linear map>[rank] applies to every power. Hence
$$
\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)^r=\dim\ker(T'-\lambda I)^r.
$$
After extending to a <field> where the <characteristic polynomials> split, take $r\geq\dim V$. These <kernel of a linear map> spaces are the <generalized eigenspaces>; the <primary decomposition theorem for linear maps> identifies their dimensions with the <algebraic multiplicities>. The same proof works over the original <field> for every <eigenvalue> in it. We obtain
$$
\boxed{\text{$T$ and $T'$ have the same nonzero eigenvalues,
with both kinds of multiplicity equal.}}
$$
In fact the argument also includes the zero <eigenvalue>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the convention that the <inner product> is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second, as in part (b). The <adjoint operator> is characterized by
$$
\boxed{\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle
\qquad(v,w\in V).}
$$
Existence and uniqueness follow from the finite-dimensional representation of <linear functionals> by the <inner product>. More concretely, choose an <orthonormal basis> $e_i$ and let $a_{ij}=\langle e_i,Te_j\rangle$. Then
$$
\langle e_i,T^*e_j\rangle=\langle Te_i,e_j\rangle
=\overline{a_{ji}},
$$
which defines $T^*$ uniquely and verifies the required identity by expansion in that <basis>. Thus its <matrix> is the conjugate transpose, proved here rather than assumed. A <self-adjoint operator> satisfies $T^*=T$, equivalently $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,Tw\rangle$ for every pair of vectors.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Proceed by induction on $\dim V$. The result is immediate for dimension zero. In positive dimension, the <characteristic polynomial> has a root over the <complex numbers>, so there is a nonzero <eigenvector> $v$ with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$. Normalize it to have norm one. <Self-adjointness> gives
$$
\overline\lambda\langle v,v\rangle
=\langle Tv,v\rangle
=\langle v,Tv\rangle
=\lambda\langle v,v\rangle,
$$
so $\lambda$ is real.

The <orthogonal complement> $v^\perp$ is an <invariant subspace>: if $w\perp v$, then
$$
\langle v,Tw\rangle=\langle Tv,w\rangle
=\overline\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=0.
$$
The restriction of $T$ to $v^\perp$ is again <self-adjoint>, since the same <inner product> identity holds on that subspace. By induction it has an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>. Adjoining $v$ gives
$$
\boxed{\text{an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$}.}
$$
The existence of the first <eigenvector> is the essential use of the <complex numbers> in this proof of the finite-dimensional <spectral theorem>.

= Solution

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ be the given <orthonormal basis>, with $Te_j=\lambda_je_j$. From the defining identity of the <adjoint operator>,
$$
\langle e_i,T^*e_j\rangle
=\langle Te_i,e_j\rangle
=\overline\lambda_i\delta_{ij}.
$$
These coordinates prove $T^*e_j=\overline\lambda_je_j$ without assuming a <matrix> formula. Therefore
$$
T^*Te_j=|\lambda_j|^2e_j=TT^*e_j
$$
for every <basis> vector. Thus $T$ is a <normal operator>, and
$$
\boxed{T^*T=TT^*,\qquad
T^*=T\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \lambda_j\in\mathbb R\text{ for every }j.}
$$
The condition of possessing an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors> is already part of the hypothesis; real <eigenvalues> alone would not imply <self-adjointness> for an arbitrary <linear transformation>.

= Solution

The integral is conjugate-linear in $f$, linear in $g$, and conjugate-symmetric, by the corresponding properties of the integral and of <complex conjugation>. Also,
$$
\langle f,f\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2e^{-x^2}\,dx\geq0.
$$
If a <polynomial> $f$ is not zero, there is a real point $x_0$ with $f(x_0)\ne0$: a nonzero <polynomial> cannot vanish at every real point. By <continuity>, $|f|$ has a positive lower bound on a sufficiently small closed interval about $x_0$. The continuous function $e^{-x^2}$ also has a positive lower bound there. The integral over this interval is strictly positive. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle f,f\rangle=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f=0.}
$$
Together with the finiteness allowed in the question, these properties prove that the formula defines an <inner product> on $V=\mathbb C[x]$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Differentiate the expression from the source before applying any finite-dimensional result. The exponential factors cancel:
$$
\boxed{Tf=f''-2xf'-2f.}
$$
This is a <polynomial> whenever $f$ is a <polynomial>, and its <polynomial degree> is at most that of $f$. Differentiation and multiplication are linear, so $T$ is a <linear transformation> $V\to V$, and every $V_n$ is an <invariant subspace>.

For <self-adjointness>, put $w(x)=e^{-x^2}$. Then $w'=-2xw$ and $wTf=(wf')'-2wf$. <Integration by parts> therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle Tf,g\rangle
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl((w\overline{f'})'-2w\overline f\bigr)g\,dx\\
&=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w\overline{f'}g'\,dx
-2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w\overline f g\,dx\\
&=\langle f,Tg\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
The discarded endpoint terms are $w\overline{f'}g$ and $w\overline f g'$. They vanish at both infinities because exponential decay dominates every <polynomial>. The same decay justifies the intermediate integrals. Consequently
$$
\boxed{T(V_n)\subseteq V_n,\qquad
\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle f,Tg\rangle\quad(f,g\in V).}
$$
Here the question's <self-adjointness> is in the sense of an <adjoint on an inner product space>: $T$ itself represents the adjoint on the polynomial space. Each finite-dimensional restriction $T|_{V_n}$ is a <self-adjoint operator>. No choice of operator domain in a completed <Hilbert space> is needed for this argument. The resulting <Gaussian polynomial operator> will produce <Hermite polynomials> in part (iv).

= Solution

If $f\in V_n\setminus V_{n-1}$, write $f(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots$ with $a_n\ne0$. In the expression $Tf=f''-2xf'-2f$, the first term has <polynomial degree> at most $n-2$, while the other two contribute
$$
-2na_nx^n-2a_nx^n=-2(n+1)a_nx^n.
$$
Thus, if $Tf=\lambda f$, comparison of the leading coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-2(n+1).}
$$
For $n=0$ take $V_{-1}=\{0\}$; the same result follows from $T1=-2$. This identifies the <eigenvalue> conditional on the existence of the <eigenvector>; existence is established next.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Construct compatible choices in the nested spaces, rather than choosing unrelated <orthonormal bases> for different $V_n$. Set $V_{-1}=\{0\}$ and define
$$
L_n=V_n\cap V_{n-1}^{\perp}.
$$
The <orthogonal complement> is taken for the <inner product> from part (i). Since $\dim V_n=n+1$ and $\dim V_{n-1}=n$, the space $L_n$ has dimension one and $V_n=V_{n-1}\oplus L_n$.

It is an <invariant subspace>. Indeed, if $v\in L_n$ and $u\in V_{n-1}$, part (ii) gives
$$
\langle u,Tv\rangle=\langle Tu,v\rangle=0,
$$
because $Tu\in V_{n-1}$. Also $Tv\in V_n$. Hence $Tv\in L_n$. Any nonzero vector in this one-dimensional space is consequently an <eigenvector>. Choose a unit vector $g_n\in L_n$. It has <polynomial degree> exactly $n$: membership in $V_{n-1}$ would make it orthogonal to itself and thus zero. Part (iii) now supplies its <eigenvalue>.

For $m<n$, $g_m\in V_{n-1}$ and $g_n\perp V_{n-1}$, so the choices are pairwise orthogonal. We have proved
$$
\boxed{\langle g_m,g_n\rangle=\delta_{mn},\qquad
\deg g_n=n,\qquad Tg_n=-2(n+1)g_n\quad(n\geq0).}
$$
An explicit compatible construction is the <Gram-Schmidt process> on $1,x,x^2,\ldots$:
$$
r_n=x^n-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\langle g_j,x^n\rangle g_j,
\qquad g_n=\frac{r_n}{\sqrt{\langle r_n,r_n\rangle}}.
$$
The leading term of $r_n$ is $x^n$, so it is never zero. This fixes the positive real leading coefficient and yields the normalized <Hermite polynomials>. For example, the <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
g_0=\pi^{-1/4},\qquad
g_1=\sqrt2\,\pi^{-1/4}x,\qquad
g_2=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4}}.
$$
Every <polynomial> is a finite linear combination of these <eigenvectors>, since $g_0,\ldots,g_n$ form an <orthonormal basis> of $V_n$ for each $n$.
