= Solution
{parent=1}

The paper uses $T_{\mu\nu}{}^\lambda=\Gamma_{\nu\mu}{}^\lambda-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}{}^\lambda$, the negative of a common <torsion tensor> convention. Keep that sign throughout. Also, $\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\varphi$ denotes the covariant <Hessian>, so the second derivative acts on the lower index $\nu$ of the <covector> $\nabla_\nu\varphi$; it is not just a composition of two directional derivatives on a scalar.

Since the <covariant derivative> of a <scalar field> is its ordinary derivative,
$$
\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu\varphi
=\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\varphi-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}{}^\lambda\partial_\lambda\varphi.
$$
Commuting the ordinary partial derivatives therefore gives
$$
\boxed{(\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu-\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu)\varphi
=T_{\mu\nu}{}^\lambda\nabla_\lambda\varphi.}
$$
For a <vector field>, write $D_\nu X^\lambda=\partial_\nu X^\lambda+\Gamma_{\nu\rho}{}^\lambda X^\rho$. This is a <tensor> with one covariant and one contravariant index. Hence
$$
\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu X^\lambda
=\partial_\mu(D_\nu X^\lambda)+\Gamma_{\mu\sigma}{}^\lambda D_\nu X^\sigma
-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}{}^\sigma D_\sigma X^\lambda.
$$
Subtracting the expression with $\mu,\nu$ exchanged, the first two terms combine to
$$
\left(\partial_\mu\Gamma_{\nu\rho}{}^\lambda
-\partial_\nu\Gamma_{\mu\rho}{}^\lambda
+\Gamma_{\mu\sigma}{}^\lambda\Gamma_{\nu\rho}{}^\sigma
-\Gamma_{\nu\sigma}{}^\lambda\Gamma_{\mu\rho}{}^\sigma\right)X^\rho.
$$
The coefficients of first derivatives of $X$ in those two terms cancel. The last terms give $(\Gamma_{\nu\mu}{}^\sigma-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}{}^\sigma)D_\sigma X^\lambda$. Identifying the <curvature> coefficient yields
$$
\boxed{[\nabla_\mu,\nabla_\nu]X^\lambda
=R_{\mu\nu\rho}{}^\lambda X^\rho
+T_{\mu\nu}{}^\rho\nabla_\rho X^\lambda.}
$$
Here the bracket denotes antisymmetrization of the <tensorial> second <covariant derivative>, consistently with the preceding calculation.

To check tensoriality directly, antisymmetrize the supplied transformation law of the <connection coefficients> in the two lower indices. The inhomogeneous term vanishes because mixed coordinate second derivatives are symmetric. The remaining transformation law is
$$
T_{\nu\lambda}{}^\mu
=\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial x^{\mu'}}
\frac{\partial x^{\nu'}}{\partial x^\nu}
\frac{\partial x^{\lambda'}}{\partial x^\lambda}
T_{\nu'\lambda'}{}^{\mu'},
$$
which is precisely that of a $(1,2)$ <tensor>. Intrinsically, with the paper's sign,
$$
\boxed{T(X,Y)=\nabla_YX-\nabla_XY+[X,Y].}
$$
The derivative terms involving the components of $X,Y$ cancel against their <Lie bracket>, leaving $(\Gamma_{\nu\mu}{}^\lambda-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}{}^\lambda)X^\mu Y^\nu$. This also shows that the expression is linear over smooth functions in each argument.

Finally take the <torsion-free connection> and the commuting parameter fields $Y,Z$ along the <geodesic> family. Vanishing torsion gives $\nabla_YZ=\nabla_ZY$, while affine <geodesic> parametrization gives $\nabla_YY=0$. The <curvature> operator in the specified convention is
$$
R(Y,Z)W=\nabla_Y\nabla_ZW-\nabla_Z\nabla_YW-\nabla_{[Y,Z]}W.
$$
Therefore
$$
\nabla_Y\nabla_YZ
=\nabla_Y\nabla_ZY
=R(Y,Z)Y+\nabla_Z\nabla_YY+\nabla_{[Y,Z]}Y
=R(Y,Z)Y.
$$
In components this is the <geodesic deviation> equation
$$
\boxed{\frac{D^2Z^\mu}{d\tau^2}
=R_{\nu\lambda\rho}{}^\mu Y^\nu Z^\lambda Y^\rho.}
$$
The connecting field represents infinitesimal separation within the family, so this is a linear equation for relative free-fall acceleration, rather than an exact coordinate difference for widely separated worldlines.

= Solution
{parent=2}

For this question use the <Levi-Civita connection>, its <metric compatibility> and the usual <curvature> symmetries. In the index ordering of Question 1, put $R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=g_{\sigma\lambda}R_{\mu\nu\rho}{}^\lambda$ and $R_{\nu\rho}=g^{\mu\sigma}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$. Contract the <second Bianchi identity> in its derivative index $\mu$ and <curvature> index $\rho$. The <curvature> symmetries give
$$
\nabla^\rho R_{\nu\lambda\rho\sigma}
=\nabla_\lambda R_{\nu\sigma}-\nabla_\nu R_{\lambda\sigma}.
$$
Now contract $\lambda,\sigma$. The left-hand side becomes $-\nabla^\rho R_{\nu\rho}$, while the right-hand side becomes $\nabla^\sigma R_{\nu\sigma}-\nabla_\nu R$. Thus
$$
2\nabla^\mu R_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_\nu R.
$$
Using $\nabla g=0$ yields the <contracted Bianchi identity>
$$
\boxed{\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad
G_{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}R.}
$$
Taking the <covariant divergence> of the <Einstein field equations>, with constant $G$, then gives $8\pi G\nabla^\mu T_{\mu\nu}=0$. The matter equation is therefore consistent with the geometric identity rather than imposing an extra incompatible condition on the metric.

<Stress-energy conservation> expresses the local balance of <energy> and <momentum>, including their transport and the effects of the gravitational <Levi-Civita connection>. In a freely falling frame at a point it reduces there to the familiar ordinary divergence law. A global conserved <energy> requires additional geometric structure, such as a timelike <Killing vector field>; it does not follow in an arbitrary <spacetime> just by replacing a <covariant derivative> with an ordinary derivative.

For <relativistic dust>,
$$
0=\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu u^\nu)
=u^\nu\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu)+\rho u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu.
$$
With signature $(+,-,-,-)$, $u^\nu u_\nu=1$ implies $u_\nu u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu=0$. Contracting gives $\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu)=0$; where $\rho>0$, substitution then gives $u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu=0$. Thus the continuity law combines rest-<mass> conservation and free <geodesic> motion for this matter model.

For the <Newtonian limit>, take $c=1$ and $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, consistently with Question 3. Use the static weak field sourced by the time-independent, slowly moving matter, retaining first order in $\epsilon$ and leading order in the spatial speed. Write spatial gradients with $\partial^i=\delta^{ij}\partial_j$ here; these are Euclidean spatial indices in the Newtonian equation. The <Levi-Civita connection> formula gives
$$
\Gamma_{00}{}^i=-\tfrac12\eta^{ij}\partial_j(\epsilon h_{00})
=\frac\epsilon2\delta^{ij}\partial_jh_{00}.
$$
The exact change from <proper time> to $t$ in the spatial <geodesic equation> is
$$
\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2}
=-\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}{}^i v^\alpha v^\beta
+\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}{}^0v^\alpha v^\beta v^i,\qquad v^0=1.
$$
Terms containing spatial velocities are negligible at leading order, leaving
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2}=-\partial^i\Phi,\qquad
\Phi=\frac\epsilon2h_{00}.}
$$
An additive constant in $\Phi$ is immaterial to this acceleration and is normally fixed by the asymptotic time normalization. With units restored, $g_{00}=1+2\Phi_{\rm physical}/c^2$.

At the same order, staticity and the <curvature> definition give
$$
R_{00}=\partial_\lambda\Gamma_{00}{}^\lambda
-\partial_0\Gamma_{\lambda0}{}^\lambda+O(\epsilon^2)
=\frac\epsilon2\delta^{ij}\partial_i\partial_jh_{00}
=\nabla^2\Phi.
$$
The trace of the four-dimensional Einstein equations is $R=-8\pi GT$, so their trace-reversed form is
$$
R_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12g_{\mu\nu}T\right).
$$
For nonrelativistic dust, $u^0\simeq1$, $u^i\simeq0$, so $T_{00}\simeq\rho$ and $T\simeq\rho$. Keeping leading matter terms therefore gives $R_{00}=4\pi G\rho$. Combining this with the geometric expression yields
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho.}
$$
This trace reversal is essential: equating $R_{00}$ directly to $8\pi G\rho$ would miss the factor of two. The approximation is the quasistatic Newtonian sector; arbitrary independently imposed gravitational radiation is not part of that sector.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Let $f(r)=1-2m/r$ and work in the exterior $r>2m$, with $G=c=1$. The affinely parametrized <geodesic Lagrangian> is
$$
L=\tfrac12\left[f\dot t^2-f^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\theta^2-r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right].
$$
Its polar-angle equation is
$$
\ddot\theta+2\frac{\dot r}{r}\dot\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=0.
$$
The initial data $\theta=\pi/2$, $\dot\theta=0$ therefore remain in that plane. <Rotational symmetry> allows the axes to be chosen so that any nonradial initial trajectory has those data: its conserved angular-<momentum> direction fixes an orbital plane through the centre. Radial trajectories can also be placed in an equatorial plane.

The coordinates $t,\phi$ are cyclic in $L$, so their canonical momenta are constant:
$$
p_t=f\dot t=E,\qquad p_\phi=-r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi=-J.
$$
The corresponding <Killing vector fields> are $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\phi$. With the displayed signature, $g(\partial_t,\dot x)=E$ and $g(\partial_\phi,\dot x)=-J$, explaining the sign in the second <canonical momentum>. Thus $E$ is conserved <energy> per unit <rest mass> for a massive particle parametrized by <proper time>, and $J$ is its axial <angular momentum> per unit <rest mass>. For a <photon>, affine rescaling rescales both constants; after the tangent is normalized to the physical <four-momentum> they represent its <energy> at infinity and axial <angular momentum>. Their ratio $b=J/E$ is the signed <impact parameter>.

In the equatorial plane, the <photon> satisfies the <null geodesic> constraint
$$
0=f\dot t^2-f^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\phi^2,
$$
which gives
$$
\dot r^2=E^2-\frac{J^2}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right).
$$
For $J\ne0$, put $u=m/r$ and $\alpha=Em/J$. Then
$$
\frac{du}{d\phi}=-\frac mJ\dot r,
$$
and consequently
$$
\boxed{(u')^2=\alpha^2+2u^3-u^2.}
$$
The $J=0$ radial case is not represented by $\phi$ as an orbit parameter.

There is a circular <photon> orbit at $r=3m$, but **it is unstable**. The radial <effective potential> is
$$
V(r)=\frac{J^2}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right),\qquad
V'(r)=\frac{2J^2(3m-r)}{r^4}.
$$
A circular orbit requires $V'(r)=0$ and $E^2=V(r)$, giving
$$
\boxed{r_c=3m,\qquad \alpha^2=\frac1{27},\qquad |b_c|=3\sqrt3\,m.}
$$
Its second derivative is
$$
V''(3m)=-\frac{2J^2}{81m^4}<0.
$$
It is a maximum, rather than a minimum, so radial perturbations depart from the orbit. Equivalently the second-order orbit equation is $u''=3u^2-u$. Linearizing about $u_c=1/3$ gives $\delta u''=\delta u$, with modes $e^{\phi}$ and $e^{-\phi}$. The existence of a growing mode directly contradicts the printed stability claim. Exact initial data can remain circular, but that does not make the orbit stable.

The supplied implicit expression describes the critical-<energy> family with $\alpha^2=1/27$, as fixed by the circular orbit. Define
$$
F(u)=\frac{1-3u}{(\sqrt3+\sqrt{1+6u})^2},\qquad s=\sqrt{1+6u}.
$$
Rationalizing once gives $F=(\sqrt3-s)/(2(\sqrt3+s))$ and
$$
F'(u)=-\frac{3\sqrt3}{s(\sqrt3+s)^2}.
$$
Differentiating $F(u)=Ae^\phi$ gives $F'u'=F$, so
$$
u'=-\frac{s(1-3u)}{3\sqrt3},\qquad
(u')^2=\frac{(1+6u)(1-3u)^2}{27}
=\frac1{27}+2u^3-u^2.
$$
This verifies the first-order equation for every allowed integration constant $A$ on its real branch. For $A=0$ the derivative argument is replaced by direct substitution: $u=1/3$ is constant and solves the critical equation. The displayed family does not solve the equation at an independently arbitrary value of $\alpha$.

For $A>0$, write $q=Ae^\phi$. On the physical exterior branch,
$$
u=\frac{1-8q+4q^2}{3(1+2q)^2},\qquad
0<q<1-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}.
$$
As $\phi\to-\infty$, $q\to0^+$ and
$$
u=\frac13-4Ae^\phi+O(e^{2\phi}),\qquad
r=3m+36mAe^\phi+O(e^{2\phi}).
$$
Thus for $A=0$ the <photon> remains exactly on the circular orbit. For $A>0$ it approaches $r=3m$ from outside through indefinitely many revolutions in the negative-$\phi$ limit. With increasing $\phi$, this same branch peels away outward and reaches infinity when $q=1-\sqrt3/2$. Reversing the direction of motion gives an incoming critical ray asymptotically approaching the <photon> circle. These are <separatrix> trajectories, not evidence of stability.
