For the <Levi-Civita connection>, the all-lowered <Riemann curvature tensor> has the algebraic symmetries
$$
\boxed{R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}=-R_{\mu\lambda\nu\kappa}
=-R_{\lambda\mu\kappa\nu},\qquad
R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}=R_{\nu\kappa\lambda\mu}.}
$$
The <first Bianchi identity> is the cyclic identity
$$
\boxed{R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}+R_{\lambda\nu\kappa\mu}+R_{\lambda\kappa\mu\nu}=0.}
$$
These statements concern the <Riemann curvature tensor> at each point and are unchanged by reversing its overall <curvature sign convention>.

A semicolon denotes the <covariant derivative> for the <Levi-Civita connection>. For example, the four lower indices of the <Riemann curvature tensor> contribute four connection terms:
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa;\eta}
={}&\partial_\eta R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
-\Gamma^a_{\lambda\eta}R_{a\mu\nu\kappa}
-\Gamma^a_{\mu\eta}R_{\lambda a\nu\kappa}\\
&-\Gamma^a_{\nu\eta}R_{\lambda\mu a\kappa}
-\Gamma^a_{\kappa\eta}R_{\lambda\mu\nu a}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\partial_\eta=\partial/\partial x^\eta$ is a <partial derivative>, $\Gamma^a_{b\eta}$ are <Christoffel symbols>, and repeated indices are summed from $0$ to $3$. Write $g_{\mu\nu}$ for the <metric tensor>, $g^{\mu\nu}$ for its <inverse metric>, $R_{\mu\nu}=g^{\lambda\nu'}R_{\lambda\mu\nu'\nu}$ for the <Ricci tensor>, and $R=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}$ for the <Ricci scalar>. This <tensor contraction> agrees with the paper's $R_{\mu\nu}=-R^a{}_{\mu\nu a}$.

By <metric compatibility>, the <inverse metric> may pass through each <covariant derivative>. Contracting the <second Bianchi identity> with $g^{\lambda\nu}$ gives
$$
\nabla_\eta R_{\mu\kappa}-\nabla_\kappa R_{\mu\eta}
+\nabla^\lambda R_{\lambda\mu\kappa\eta}=0,
$$
where $\nabla_\eta$ is the same <covariant derivative> as the semicolon. Next contract with $g^{\mu\kappa}$. Antisymmetry of the first pair gives $g^{\mu\kappa}R_{\lambda\mu\kappa\eta}=-R_{\lambda\eta}$, so
$$
\partial_\eta R-\nabla^\mu R_{\mu\eta}-\nabla^\lambda R_{\lambda\eta}=0.
$$
Thus the <contracted Bianchi identity> is $\nabla^\mu R_{\mu\eta}=\tfrac12\partial_\eta R$. Raising the remaining index and using <metric compatibility> proves
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad
G^{\mu\nu}=R^{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}R.}
$$
The <Einstein tensor> $G^{\mu\nu}$ therefore has identically zero <covariant divergence>, without imposing any field equation. This makes the <Einstein field equations> compatible with <stress-energy conservation>, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$, where $T^{\mu\nu}$ is the <stress-energy tensor>. The connection terms describe how <energy> and <momentum> are compared at neighboring points; ordinary componentwise <partial derivatives> would not give a coordinate-independent conservation law in curved <spacetime>.

Take the <tensor contraction> of the <Einstein field equations> with the <inverse metric> $g^{\mu\nu}$. In four-dimensional <spacetime>, $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu}=4$, hence
$$
R-\frac12(4)R=CT,\qquad T=g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu},\qquad R=-CT.
$$
Here $T$ is the <tensor contraction> of the <stress-energy tensor>. Substituting this result back into the <Einstein field equations>, while keeping both free indices lowered, gives the <trace-reversed stress-energy tensor>:
$$
\boxed{R_{\mu\nu}=C\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac12g_{\mu\nu}T\right)
=CS_{\mu\nu}.}
$$
The factor $1/2$ uses the dimension four: in dimension $n>2$, the <trace-reversed Einstein field equations in D dimensions> have $1/(n-2)$ in place of $1/2$.

Use $x^0=ct$ and <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$. With the source's <curvature sign convention>, the <Ricci tensor> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\mu\nu}&=-R^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu\lambda}\\
&=\partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\lambda}
-\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}
+\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}
-\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\nu}\Gamma^\lambda_{\sigma\lambda}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Its time-time component is therefore
$$
R_{00}=\partial_0\Gamma^\lambda_{0\lambda}
-\partial_\lambda\Gamma^\lambda_{00}
+\Gamma^\sigma_{0\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{0\sigma}
-\Gamma^\sigma_{00}\Gamma^\lambda_{\sigma\lambda}.
$$
For a slowly moving, pressure-negligible source, the <stress-energy tensor> is approximated by <relativistic dust>: $T^{\mu\nu}=\rho U^\mu U^\nu$, where $\rho$ is <mass density> and $U^\mu$ is the <four-velocity>. In its approximate rest <local inertial frame>, $U^\mu\simeq(c,0,0,0)$ and $U_0\simeq-c$. Thus
$$
T_{00}\simeq\rho c^2,\qquad T_{ij}\ll T_{00},\qquad
T\simeq-\rho c^2,
$$
and the <trace-reversed stress-energy tensor> satisfies
$$
S_{00}=T_{00}-\frac12g_{00}T\simeq\frac12\rho c^2.
\qquad\boxed{R_{00}\simeq\frac12C\rho c^2.}
$$

Independently, introduce the <metric perturbation> $g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}$, with $|h_{\mu\nu}|\ll1$ and constant <Minkowski metric> $\eta_{\mu\nu}$. The <Christoffel symbols> are first order in $h$, so their products are discarded in the <weak-field approximation>. With a comma denoting a <partial derivative>, the linearized <Riemann curvature tensor> is
$$
R_{\lambda\mu\nu\kappa}
=\frac12\left(h_{\lambda\nu,\kappa\mu}
-h_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}
-h_{\lambda\kappa,\nu\mu}
+h_{\mu\kappa,\nu\lambda}\right)+O(h^2).
$$
Taking the <tensor contraction> gives, to this order,
$$
R_{00}=\sum_{i=1}^3R_{i0i0}
=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^3\left(\partial_0^2h_{ii}
-2\partial_0\partial_i h_{0i}+\partial_i^2h_{00}\right).
$$
In the static <Newtonian limit>, all time <partial derivatives> vanish. The constant $\eta_{00}$ also has zero <partial derivatives>, leaving
$$
\boxed{R_{00}\simeq\frac12\nabla^2h_{00}
=\frac12\nabla^2g_{00}.}
$$
Here $\nabla^2=\sum_i\partial_i^2$ is the spatial <Laplace operator> in the approximately Cartesian background coordinates. This sign follows the paper's <curvature sign convention>.

For the <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi$, the weak static <metric tensor> has $g_{00}\simeq-1-2\Phi/c^2$. The <Newtonian limit> derived above consequently gives
$$
R_{00}\simeq-\frac1{c^2}\nabla^2\Phi
=\frac12C\rho c^2,
\qquad
\nabla^2\Phi=-\frac12C c^4\rho.
$$
Matching the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>, $\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho$, fixes
$$
\boxed{C=-\frac{8\pi G}{c^4}.}
$$
Here $G$ is the <gravitational constant> and $c$ is the <speed of light>. This negative coupling agrees with the source formula sheet. The familiar positive coupling uses the opposite <curvature sign convention> for the <Ricci tensor>; the physical <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> is the same.

Choose <normal coordinates> $\xi^\alpha$ centered at an event $p$. At $p$, the <metric tensor> in these coordinates is the constant <Minkowski metric> $\eta_{\alpha\beta}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$, with $\xi^0$ measured in length units, and its first <partial derivatives> vanish. In arbitrary coordinates $x^\mu$, the <chain rule> for the <line element> gives
$$
ds^2\big|_p=\eta_{\alpha\beta}\,d\xi^\alpha d\xi^\beta
=g_{\mu\nu}(p)\,dx^\mu dx^\nu,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{g_{\mu\nu}(p)=\left.
\eta_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial\xi^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}
\frac{\partial\xi^\beta}{\partial x^\nu}\right|_p.}
$$
This construction is pointwise. At nearby events, the <metric tensor> in the chosen <normal coordinates> is $g^{(\xi)}_{\alpha\beta}(\xi)=\eta_{\alpha\beta}+O(|\xi|^2)$, with the quadratic corrections determined by the <Riemann curvature tensor>. Replacing $g^{(\xi)}$ by one constant <Minkowski metric> throughout an open region would make that region flat, so it cannot describe general curved <spacetime> exactly.

For a contravariant <vector field> $A^\lambda$, the <covariant derivative> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\nu A^\lambda=A^\lambda{}_{;\nu}
=\partial_\nu A^\lambda+\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}A^\mu.}
$$
The <Levi-Civita connection> is the unique <torsion-free connection> satisfying <metric compatibility>. Its <Christoffel symbols> in a <coordinate basis> are
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}
=\frac12g^{\lambda\sigma}
\left(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu}
+\partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu}-\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}\right),
\qquad\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu}.}
$$
There is generally no symmetry interchanging the upper index with a lower one. The <Christoffel symbols> themselves are not the components of a <tensor>.

An ordinary <partial derivative> of the components of a <vector field> includes the apparent change caused by the varying <coordinate basis>. The connection term corrects for that change, so $\nabla_\nu A^\lambda$ obeys the <tensor component transformation law>. It gives coordinate-independent ways to express <parallel transport>, <geodesics> and <stress-energy conservation> in curved <spacetime>.

Each lower index contributes a minus connection term to the <covariant derivative> of the <metric tensor>:
$$
\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}
=\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}
-\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}g_{\rho\nu}
-\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}g_{\mu\rho}.
$$
Substitute the <Christoffel symbols> of the <Levi-Civita connection> and use the <inverse metric> identity $g_{\rho\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}=\delta^\sigma_\nu$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
g_{\rho\nu}\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}
&=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}
+\partial_\lambda g_{\nu\mu}-\partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda}),\\
g_{\mu\rho}\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}
&=\tfrac12(\partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda}
+\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}-\partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding cancels the first and third terms and leaves exactly $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}=0.}
$$
This is <metric compatibility>: the <Levi-Civita connection> preserves the <inner product> under <parallel transport>.

The <metric tensor> in a basis $\mathbf e_a$ is $g_{ab}=\mathbf e_a\cdot\mathbf e_b$. Differentiating this <inner product> with the <product rule> and using the assumed basis-derivative relation yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_c g_{ab}
&=(\partial_c\mathbf e_a)\cdot\mathbf e_b
+\mathbf e_a\cdot(\partial_c\mathbf e_b)\\
&=\Gamma^d_{ac}\,\mathbf e_d\cdot\mathbf e_b
+\Gamma^d_{bc}\,\mathbf e_a\cdot\mathbf e_d\\
&=\boxed{\Gamma^d_{ac}g_{db}+\Gamma^d_{bc}g_{ad}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the coefficients satisfy <metric compatibility>. For a <coordinate basis> with zero <torsion tensor>, symmetrizing this relation in $a,c$, adding the corresponding two equations and subtracting the third gives
$$
\Gamma^b_{ac}=\frac12g^{bd}
(\partial_a g_{dc}+\partial_c g_{da}-\partial_d g_{ac}).
$$
They are therefore the <Christoffel symbols> of the <Levi-Civita connection>. <Metric compatibility> by itself does not establish zero <torsion tensor>; that also uses the <coordinate basis> and its commuting coordinate derivatives.

For an embedded curved surface, the stated ambient basis-derivative relation needs a qualification: the <Gauss formula> is $\partial_c\mathbf e_a=\Gamma^b_{ac}\mathbf e_b+h_{ac}\mathbf n$, where $\mathbf n$ is a <unit normal> and $h_{ac}$ is the <second fundamental form>. The normal term has zero <inner product> with each tangent basis vector, so the displayed identity for $\partial_c g_{ab}$ still holds. For a concrete counterexample to dropping that term, on a <two-sphere> one has $\partial_\theta\mathbf e_\theta=-R\mathbf n$, whereas every $\Gamma^b_{\theta\theta}$ is zero. Thus the intrinsic connection describes the tangential projection of the ambient derivative.

In standard <spherical coordinates>, parametrize the <two-sphere> by
$$
\mathbf x(\theta,\phi)=R(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta).
$$
The <coordinate basis> transforms from the fixed Cartesian basis according to $\mathbf e_a=(\partial x^i/\partial q^a)\mathbf e_i$. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_\theta&=\partial_\theta\mathbf x
=R(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta),\\
\mathbf e_\phi&=\partial_\phi\mathbf x
=R(-\sin\theta\sin\phi,\sin\theta\cos\phi,0).
\end{aligned}
$$
Their <inner products> are $\mathbf e_\theta^2=R^2$, $\mathbf e_\theta\cdot\mathbf e_\phi=0$ and $\mathbf e_\phi^2=R^2\sin^2\theta$. Thus the <induced metric> is
$$
\boxed{(g_{ij})=R^2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad
ds^2=R^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2).}
$$
The source's intermediate display places an extra $R^2$ outside entries already labelled $g_{\theta\theta},\ldots$. Interpreting those entries as the actual <metric tensor> components would double-count the factor: for example, $R=2$ gives $g_{\theta\theta}=4$ directly from $\mathbf e_\theta^2$, not $16$. Its final matrix is the correct <induced metric>; the entries of its intermediate matrix must instead denote dimensionless coefficients.

The only nonconstant entry of the <metric tensor> is $g_{\phi\phi}=R^2\sin^2\theta$, whose <partial derivative> is $\partial_\theta g_{\phi\phi}=2R^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. The nonzero <Christoffel symbols> are
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,\qquad
\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta}=\cot\theta.}
$$
Along a fixed latitude with $0<\theta<\pi$, <parallel transport> gives
$$
\frac{dV^\theta}{d\phi}=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,V^\phi,
\qquad
\frac{dV^\phi}{d\phi}=-\cot\theta\,V^\theta.
$$
Set $X=V^\theta$ and $Y=\sin\theta\,V^\phi$. Since $\theta$ is fixed, this <linear ordinary differential equation> becomes
$$
X'=\cos\theta\,Y,\qquad Y'=-\cos\theta\,X.
$$
It is a rotation at constant rate $-\cos\theta$. Its general solution, also valid at the equator where the rate is zero, is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
V^\theta&=A\cos(\phi\cos\theta)+B\sin(\phi\cos\theta),\\
V^\phi&=\frac{-A\sin(\phi\cos\theta)+B\cos(\phi\cos\theta)}{\sin\theta}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The initial data give $A=1$ and $B=0$. At $\theta=\pi/3$, a complete circuit has $\phi\cos\theta=\pi$, so
$$
\boxed{(V^\theta,V^\phi)_{\phi=2\pi}=(-1,0).}
$$
The physical squared length uses the <metric tensor>, rather than the Euclidean sum of coordinate components:
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf V|^2=R^2\bigl[(V^\theta)^2+\sin^2\theta(V^\phi)^2\bigr]
=R^2(A^2+B^2).}
$$
It is constant and equals $R^2$ for these data. More generally, <metric compatibility> gives $d\,g(V,V)/d\phi=(\nabla_{\partial_\phi}g)(V,V)+2g(\nabla_{\partial_\phi}V,V)=0$. The final change of direction is <holonomy>, exemplified by <parallel transport around a latitude of the unit sphere>; <parallel transport> preserves length without requiring the direction to return unchanged.

A <photon> follows a <null geodesic>, whose spatial path approaches straight lines far from the deflecting <mass>. Extend the incoming asymptote forward: this is the reference path the <photon> would follow if the <mass> were removed while its incoming direction and transverse offset were held fixed. The <impact parameter> $b$ is the perpendicular distance from the <mass> to that incoming asymptote. It is not, in general, the distance of closest approach.

{description=Original schematic, with bending exaggerated. The dashed horizontal line is the undeflected reference path. The outgoing dashed asymptote makes angle delta with it; b is measured to the incoming asymptote, not to the bent ray.}

The positive deflection angle $\delta$ is the angle between the outgoing propagation direction and the continued incoming direction. The <photon> bends toward the <mass>, so a ray passing above it has a downward outgoing slope in the figure. In the <weak-field approximation> for an isolated spherical <mass>, <Schwarzschild light deflection> gives $\delta\simeq4GM/(bc^2)$ for distant endpoints. This angle differs from the apparent image angle measured from the observer's line of sight to the <mass>.

By spherical symmetry, choose the orbital plane to be $\theta=\pi/2$. The <geodesic Lagrangian> of the <Schwarzschild metric> is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac12\left(-Bc^2\dot t^2+B^{-1}\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2\right),
\qquad B=1-\frac{2GM}{rc^2}.
$$
For a massive <test particle>, dots denote <proper time> derivatives and $2\mathcal L=-c^2$. The <cyclic coordinates> $t$ and $\phi$ give, through the <Euler-Lagrange equations>, two constants:
$$
B\dot t=\mathcal E,\qquad r^2\dot\phi=\ell.
$$
At infinity, $\mathcal E=\gamma_\infty=1/\sqrt{1-v_\infty^2/c^2}$ is the <Lorentz factor>. The constant $\ell$ is the <specific angular momentum>, and the energy per unit <rest mass> is $E_\infty/m=c^2\gamma_\infty$. The normalization of the <four-velocity> gives
$$
-c^2=-\frac{c^2\gamma_\infty^2}{B}
+\frac{\dot r^2}{B}+\frac{\ell^2}{r^2},
\qquad
\dot r^2=c^2\gamma_\infty^2-B\left(c^2+\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}\right).
$$
Use $dr/d\phi=\dot r\,r^2/\ell$ and define the <energy-normalized angular momentum for a Schwarzschild geodesic>
$$
J=\frac{\ell}{\gamma_\infty}=\frac{c^2L_\infty}{E_\infty}.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2
+r^2B\left(1+\frac{c^2r^2}{\gamma_\infty^2J^2}\right)
=\frac{c^2r^4}{J^2}.}
$$
For an incoming massive <test particle> with <impact parameter> $b$, $L_\infty=m\gamma_\infty v_\infty b$, so $J=bv_\infty$. In particular, $J$ is not the same as the <specific angular momentum> $\ell$ unless $\gamma_\infty=1$.

A <photon> has no <proper time> parametrization and no finite <Lorentz factor>. For it, use an <affine parameter> in the same <geodesic Lagrangian> and impose $2\mathcal L=0$. The ratio $J=c^2L_\infty/E_\infty=bc$ remains well defined, and the null result is
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2+r^2B=\frac{r^4}{b^2}.}
$$
Equivalently this is the ultrarelativistic limit of the massive formula at fixed $J$, with $1/\gamma_\infty^2\to0$. The printed massive formula needs this interpretation before it is applied to a <photon>.

Let $m_g=GM/c^2$, $u=1/r$, and use a prime for $d/d\phi$. The <null geodesic> first integral obtained above becomes
$$
(u')^2+u^2-2m_g u^3=\frac1{b^2}.
$$
Differentiating with respect to $\phi$ and dividing by $2u'$ away from a turning point gives
$$
\boxed{u''+u=3m_g u^2=\frac{3GM}{c^2}u^2.}
$$
The same <differential equation> holds at the turning point by continuity. When $m_g=0$, the general solution is $u_0=C_1\cos\phi+C_2\sin\phi$. Choose the angular origin so that the incoming asymptote has $\phi=0$ and the unperturbed closest approach has $\phi=\pi/2$. The <impact parameter> then fixes
$$
\boxed{u_0=\frac{\sin\phi}{b},\qquad r\sin\phi=b.}
$$
This is the straight reference path, not a curved orbit.

For <perturbation theory> with $m_g/b\ll1$, write $u=u_0+u_1+O(m_g^2/b^3)$, where $u_1=O(m_g/b^2)$. Inserting this into the <differential equation> and discarding second-order terms gives
$$
u_1''+u_1=3m_g u_0^2
=\boxed{\frac{3GM}{c^2}\frac{1-\cos2\phi}{2b^2}.}
$$
A particular solution and the complementary solution are
$$
u_1=\frac{m_g}{2b^2}(3+\cos2\phi)+A_1\cos\phi+B_1\sin\phi.
$$
Choosing $A_1=B_1=0$ retains reflection symmetry about $\phi=\pi/2$ and the specified <impact parameter> to first order. The two distant zeros of $u$ then occur at $\phi_{\rm in}\simeq-2m_g/b$ and $\phi_{\rm out}\simeq\pi+2m_g/b$. Their separation exceeds $\pi$ by $4m_g/b$, consistently giving the <Schwarzschild light deflection> $\delta\simeq4GM/(bc^2)$.

In the <thin-lens approximation for gravitational lensing>, consider a ray crossing the lens plane at transverse distance $b$ above the aligned source-observer axis. With the observer at axial position $0$, the <black hole> at $L$ and the source at $D$, the segment joining the lens plane to the source has slope $-b/(D-L)$, while the segment joining the observer to the lens plane has slope $b/L$. The observer sees the ray at apparent angle $\theta\simeq b/L$ to the axis. When following the <photon> toward the observer, the two small angles to the axis have magnitudes $b/(D-L)$ and $b/L$. The physical bend is their sum:
$$
\delta\simeq\frac bL+\frac b{D-L}
=\frac{bD}{L(D-L)}.
$$
Substitute the <Schwarzschild light deflection>, $\delta\simeq4GM/(bc^2)$, and use $b\simeq L\theta$:
$$
b^2\simeq\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{L(D-L)}D,
\qquad
\boxed{2\theta\simeq2\sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D-L}{LD}}.}
$$
Every azimuth around the axis is equivalent by rotational symmetry. Isotropic emission therefore supplies rays at all these azimuths, and the ideal image is an <Einstein ring> centered on the <black hole>, with <Einstein angular radius> $\theta$ and angular diameter $2\theta$. A finite source size or imperfect alignment broadens or breaks the ideal ring.

The stated expression uses small angles, distant endpoints and <weak-field approximation> $b\gg2GM/c^2$, with ordinary Euclidean distances. For cosmological <gravitational lensing>, use <angular diameter distances> $D_l,D_s,D_{ls}$ in $\theta^2=4GM D_{ls}/(c^2D_lD_s)$; generally $D_{ls}\ne D_s-D_l$.

\boxed{v=2.86\left(\frac{H_0}{70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}}\right)

The <Earth>-<Moon> pair is a <gravitationally bound system>, so its separation does not obey $\dot r=H_0r$. The <Hubble flow> describes the motion of the approximately homogeneous cosmological matter distribution; it is not a compulsory extra velocity assigned to the members of every local orbit. Thus the numerical proximity of the counterfactual rate to $3.8\,\mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$ is not evidence for that explanation.

The observed recession arises principally from <tidal dissipation>. The <Earth> rotates faster than the <Moon> orbits, so the displaced tidal response transfers <angular momentum> from the <Earth>'s spin into the lunar orbit. For a nearly circular orbit, increasing orbital <angular momentum> increases its size. A model of this effect must include the appropriate tidal response, rather than add a universal <Hubble flow> recession.

A residual <local cosmological tidal acceleration> can exist, but its background scale is $|(\ddot a/a)r|\sim H_0^2r$, not an imposed first-order secular drift $H_0r$. At $r=4\times10^8\,\mathrm m$,
$$
H_0^2r\sim2\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},
\qquad
\frac{G(M_E+M_M)}{r^2}\sim2.5\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},
$$
using $G(M_E+M_M)\simeq4\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$. The cosmological scale is about $10^{-24}$ of the mutual <gravitational acceleration>. Its detailed coefficient depends on how the local system is embedded in the cosmological background. **Cosmological expansion can be neglected for the lunar recession calculation at the stated precision**; this conclusion follows from binding and the tiny residual <local cosmological tidal acceleration>, rather than from comparing two recession speeds.

Let $t_0$ be the initial epoch, let $r(t)$ be the physical radius of the outer matter shell, and put $r(t_0)=r_0$. The background <Einstein-de Sitter universe> has <critical density> $\rho_b=3H_i^2/(8\pi G)$ at that epoch. Its mass inside $r_0$ is $M_b=M-\delta M$, where $M$ is the actual conserved mass. Hence the background <Friedmann equation> implies
$$
H_i^2=\frac{2G(M-\delta M)}{r_0^3},\qquad
\dot r_0^2=H_i^2r_0^2=\frac{2G(M-\delta M)}{r_0}.
$$
For <spherical collapse> of pressureless matter, with no <shell crossing>, the <shell theorem> gives $\ddot r=-GM/r^2$. Its <conservation of energy> first integral is
$$
\frac12\dot r^2-\frac{GM}{r}
=\frac12\dot r_0^2-\frac{GM}{r_0}
=-\frac{G\delta M}{r_0}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\dot r^2=\frac Ar-B,\qquad
\boxed{A=2GM,\qquad B=\frac{2G\delta M}{r_0}.}
$$
For $\delta M>0$, the initially expanding branch reaches <cosmological turnaround> at $r_{\rm ta}=A/B=Mr_0/\delta M$. Since $dt=dr/\dot r$ on that branch, the elapsed <cosmic time> is
$$
\boxed{\tau=t_{\rm ta}-t_0
=\int_{r_0}^{A/B}\sqrt{\frac r{A-Br}}\,dr.}
$$
This is <spherical turnaround from an unperturbed Hubble velocity>. The integral measures the comoving matter observers' time interval in the pressureless model. The integration variable is the physical radius, not the radius divided by the background <scale factor>. If an absolute <cosmic time> is wanted, add $t_0$ to the displayed interval.

Set $u=Br/A$, retaining $r_0$ in $B$. Replacing only the lower integration limit by zero gives
$$
\tau\simeq\frac{A}{B^{3/2}}\int_0^1\sqrt{\frac u{1-u}}\,du
=\frac{\pi A}{2B^{3/2}}.
$$
Substitution of the constants yields
$$
\boxed{\tau\simeq\frac{\pi GM}{(2G\delta M/r_0)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\pi M r_0^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2G}\,(\delta M)^{3/2}}.}
$$
To quantify the approximation, write $\epsilon=\delta M/M\ll1$. The true lower limit is $u_0=Br_0/A=\epsilon$, and an elementary <integration by substitution>, $u=\sin^2\chi$, gives
$$
\tau_{\rm exact}=\frac{A}{B^{3/2}}
\left[\frac\pi2-\arcsin\sqrt\epsilon+\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}\right].
$$
Since $\arcsin\sqrt\epsilon-\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}=\tfrac23\epsilon^{3/2}+O(\epsilon^{5/2})$, the approximate time is too large by a relative fraction $\tfrac4{3\pi}\epsilon^{3/2}+O(\epsilon^{5/2})$. This explains why changing the lower limit introduces little error for a small excess mass. The <cosmological turnaround> time diverges as $\delta M\to0^+$, consistently with the absence of finite <cosmological turnaround> in an exactly critical <Einstein-de Sitter universe>.

There are two first-order effects: an intrinsic <temperature> change from <adiabatic compression>, and the <gravitational redshift> of the emerging <photons>. Evaluate the excess <Newtonian gravitational potential> at the boundary of the spherical fluctuation, with the exterior reference potential subtracted:
$$
\delta\Phi=-\frac{G\delta M}{r_0}.
$$
In the <weak-field approximation>, <gravitational time dilation> gives $d\tau_{\rm local}/dt\simeq1+\delta\Phi/c^2$. A local emitter's successive wave crests therefore arrive farther apart at a reference observer outside the potential well. Their <frequency> changes by $\delta\nu/\nu=\delta\Phi/c^2<0$. Since a redshifted <black-body radiation> spectrum has its <temperature> scaled by the same factor as its <frequency>, the gravitational contribution is
$$
\left(\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right)_{\rm grav}
=\frac{\delta\Phi}{c^2}=-\frac{G\delta M}{r_0c^2}.
$$

For the intrinsic effect, compare the local <scale factor> $R+\Delta R$ with the unperturbed value $R$ at the reference epoch. An equilibrium <photon gas> has <energy density> $u=a_{\rm rad}T^4$ and <pressure> $p=u/3$. For <adiabatic compression>, the <first law of thermodynamics> applied to a comoving volume $V\propto R^3$ gives
$$
d(uV)=-p\,dV
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
4\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac43\frac{dV}{V}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac{dR}{R}.
$$
Thus $TR$ is constant along this <adiabatic process>, and the initial intrinsic contribution is $\Delta T/T=-\Delta R/R$. The smaller local expansion associated with the fluctuation leaves its radiation hotter; this is the compression or local expansion-history contribution, distinct from the clock-rate effect on the escaping radiation.

Multiplying the two first-order factors, and cancelling the common subsequent background redshift, gives
$$
\frac{T_{\rm obs}}{\bar T_{\rm obs}}
\simeq\left(1-\frac{\Delta R}{R}\right)
\left(1+\frac{\delta\Phi}{c^2}\right),
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\Delta T}{T}=-\frac{G\delta M}{r_0c^2}-\frac{\Delta R}{R}.}
$$
For the overdense region produced by <adiabatic compression>, $\Delta R<0$, so **the final term is positive**. It competes with the negative <gravitational redshift> term. Their sum is the intrinsic-plus-endpoint part of the <Sachs-Wolfe combination>; computing a full observed <cosmic microwave background anisotropy> can also require <Doppler CMB anisotropy> and the <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect>.<Cosmology by David Tong>[Sections 3.4 and 3.4.1, equations (3.210)–(3.212): adiabatic temperature perturbations and gravitational redshift contributions]{ref}
