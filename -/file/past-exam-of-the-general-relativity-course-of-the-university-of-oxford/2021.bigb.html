= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

The apple's <proper time> $\tau$ is the time read by a clock carried with it, with $d\tau^2=-ds^2/c^2$ for <metric signature> $-+++$. At any event choose a <local inertial frame> with coordinates $\xi^\alpha$, in which free motion satisfies $d^2\xi^\alpha/d\tau^2=0$ at that event. The <chain rule> gives
$$
0=\frac{\partial\xi^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\xi^\alpha}{\partial x^\nu\partial x^\lambda}
\frac{dx^\nu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\lambda}{d\tau}.
$$
Multiplying by the inverse coordinate <Jacobian matrix> yields the exact <geodesic equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2}+\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}u^\nu u^\lambda=0,\qquad
u^\mu=\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau},\qquad
\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}=\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial\xi^\alpha}
\frac{\partial^2\xi^\alpha}{\partial x^\nu\partial x^\lambda}.}
$$
Here $x^\mu$ are the observer's coordinates, $u^\mu$ is the apple's <four-velocity>, and the <Christoffel symbols> describe the <Levi-Civita connection>. The last expression applies when the $\xi$ coordinates are locally inertial at the event considered; one such chart need not remain inertial throughout a finite region.

Choose $x^0=ct$, so $u^0=c\,dt/d\tau$. In the <weak-field approximation> and at small spatial <speed> compared with $c$, the leading spatial <geodesic equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2x^i}{d\tau^2}+c^2\Gamma^i_{00}\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^2\simeq0.}
$$
It is exact at the release event when all spatial $u^i$ vanish, and is the intended approximation to the displayed expression in the source. Terms $2\Gamma^\mu_{0j}u^0u^j$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{ij}u^iu^j$ cannot generally be dropped; the time component must also be retained to find changing $dt/d\tau$. For a concrete counterexample to the unrestricted expression, a straight free trajectory $x=v\gamma\tau$, $y=b$ in flat <spacetime> has polar radius $r=\sqrt{b^2+v^2\gamma^2\tau^2}$ and $d^2r/d\tau^2=v^2\gamma^2b^2/r^3\ne0$, although $\Gamma^r_{00}=0$. The missing angular term $\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}(d\phi/d\tau)^2$ supplies that radial coordinate <acceleration>.

Approximate Earth by an isolated, nonrotating, spherically symmetric body. By <Birkhoff's theorem>, the exterior <vacuum> <metric tensor> is the <Schwarzschild metric>. With $x^0=ct$ it is
$$
\boxed{ds^2=-f(r)c^2dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{f(r)}+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2),\qquad
f(r)=1-\frac{2GM}{rc^2}.}
$$
Here $r$ is the <areal radius>, $\theta$ and $\phi$ are spherical angles, $t$ is <Schwarzschild time> normalized to the <proper time> of a static observer at infinity, $M$ is Earth's <mass>, $G$ the <gravitational constant>, and $c$ the <speed of light>. The nonzero diagonal <metric tensor> components are $g_{00}=-f$, $g_{rr}=f^{-1}$, $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2$, $g_{\phi\phi}=r^2\sin^2\theta$.

The apple is a <test particle>; neglect its effect on the <metric tensor>, air resistance, Earth's rotation and nonspherical structure, and external <tidal forces>. Near Earth's surface, $GM/(R_Ec^2)\simeq7\times10^{-10}$, $h/R_E\ll1$, and the falling <speed> remains much smaller than $c$. Thus the <Newtonian limit> of this <metric tensor> is appropriate, while the exact radial <geodesic> is available as a check. A static observer under the tree has <proper time> $T=\sqrt{f(R_E)}\,t$, up to an additive constant. Such an observer is supported by the ground and has nonzero <proper acceleration>; the released apple instead has zero <proper acceleration>.

Write $\mu=GM/c^2$ and $r_0=R_E+h$. The <Christoffel symbols> follow from
$$
\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}=\frac12g^{\alpha\delta}
(\partial_\beta g_{\delta\gamma}+\partial_\gamma g_{\delta\beta}-\partial_\delta g_{\beta\gamma}).
$$
The relevant time and radial entries, using $x^0=ct$, are
$$
\Gamma^r_{00}=\frac12ff'=\frac{\mu f}{r^2},\qquad
\Gamma^0_{0r}=\Gamma^0_{r0}=\frac{f'}{2f}=\frac{\mu}{r^2f},\qquad
\Gamma^r_{rr}=-\frac{\mu}{r^2f},\qquad
\Gamma^0_{00}=\Gamma^\theta_{00}=\Gamma^\phi_{00}=0.
$$
For the angular <geodesic equations>, $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi}=1/r$, $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$, and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\cot\theta$. Thus, with primes on coordinates here denoting <proper time> <derivatives>,
$$
\theta''+\frac{2r'}r\theta'-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(\phi')^2=0,\qquad
\phi''+\frac{2r'}r\phi'+2\cot\theta\,\theta'\phi'=0.
$$
Initial conditions $\theta'=\phi'=0$ are preserved by these <ordinary differential equations>. The motion is therefore radial, with constant angular coordinates. Choose the polar axis so the apple is away from the angular coordinate singularities.

The exact remaining radial <geodesic equation> and <four-velocity> normalization are
$$
r''+\frac{GMf}{r^2}\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^2
-\frac{\mu}{r^2f}(r')^2=0,\qquad
fc^2\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^2-\frac{(r')^2}{f}=c^2.
$$
Eliminating $dt/d\tau$ gives the particularly simple exact result $r''=-GM/r^2$. The supported observer's <proper time> $T$ differs from the apple's <proper time> by relative corrections of order $GM/(R_Ec^2)$ and $v^2/c^2$. Consequently, to leading order in the <weak-field approximation> and $h/R_E$, $d^2r/dT^2\simeq-GM/R_E^2=-g$, with $r(0)=r_0$ and zero initial radial <velocity>. Hence
$$
\boxed{r(T)\simeq R_E+h-\frac12gT^2,\qquad g=\frac{GM}{R_E^2},\qquad T_{\rm fall}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}.}
$$
The proper height measured along a static radial ruler is $\int_{R_E}^r dr/\sqrt f\simeq r-R_E$ at this accuracy. The use of $T$ means the ground clock's elapsed time, with propagation delays corrected; raw visual arrival times include the changing light travel time.

If variation of $g$ over the fall is retained but relativistic corrections are neglected, a useful parametric solution is
$$
r=r_0\cos^2\eta,\qquad
T\simeq\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2GM}}(\eta+\sin\eta\cos\eta),\qquad \eta\ge0.
$$
Its small-$\eta$ expansion gives the same constant-$g$ result. For an exact check on the choice of clock, the initial coordinate <acceleration> measured using $T$ is $-GMf(r_0)/[r_0^2f(R_E)]$, which reduces to the displayed approximation.

The time-translation <Killing vector field> of the <Schwarzschild metric> gives a conserved <Killing energy> per unit <rest mass>,
$$
\mathcal E=-g_{tt}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=c^2f(r)\frac{dt}{d\tau}.
$$
For unit <rest mass>, this is the total <energy> relative to infinity, including <rest energy>. At release $dr/d\tau=0$, so the <four-velocity> normalization gives $dt/d\tau=f(r_0)^{-1/2}$ and
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=c^2\sqrt{1-\frac{2GM}{r_0c^2}}
=c^2-\frac{GM}{r_0}+O\left(\frac{G^2M^2}{r_0^2c^2}\right).}
$$
Substitution into the radial <four-velocity> normalization yields
$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2=\frac{\mathcal E^2}{c^2}-c^2f(r)
=2GM\left(\frac1r-\frac1{r_0}\right).
$$
Choosing the inward square root and evaluating at the ground proves
$$
\boxed{\frac12\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2_{r=R_E}
=GM\left(\frac1{R_E}-\frac1{R_E+h}\right).}
$$
This identity is exact within the assumed <Schwarzschild metric>, despite its familiar <Newtonian mechanics> form. The quantity $dr/d\tau$ is not exactly the locally measured <speed>. A <Schwarzschild static observer> measures $v_{\rm loc}=(dr/dt)/f=(dr/d\tau)/\sqrt{f(r_0)}$. Thus the distinction disappears in the stated <weak-field approximation>, giving $v_{\rm loc}^2\simeq2gh$ for $h\ll R_E$.

**Yes, for genuine precession of a nonspherical disc.** Let its unit normal be $\mathbf n=(\sin\alpha\cos\omega t,\sin\alpha\sin\omega t,\cos\alpha)$. Its trace-free <mass quadrupole moment> has the axisymmetric form
$$
Q_{ij}=Q_0\left(n_i n_j-\frac13\delta_{ij}\right),\qquad Q_0\ne0.
$$
The $xz,yz$ entries vary at $\omega$, and $xx-yy,xy$ at $2\omega$. Their nonzero second and third <time derivatives> give an oscillating far-field <metric perturbation> and positive average radiated <power>. For $\alpha=\pi/2$ the $\omega$ terms vanish but the $2\omega$ terms remain. The precise condition is $\omega\ne0$ and $\sin\alpha\ne0$: the literal inequality $\alpha\ne0$ also allows $\alpha=\pi$, where the normal is fixed and no such <gravitational radiation> occurs. For a uniform thin disc of <mass> $M_d$ and radius $b$, $Q_0=-M_db^2/4$, confirming that it is not zero.

Use <metric signature> $-+++$ and <transverse-traceless gauge>, $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+h_{ij}^{\rm TT}$. To resolve the source's origin ambiguity, put the detector centre at $z=0$ and the source centre at $z=-r_0$, so the <gravitational wave> travels in the positive $z$ direction. Reversing that direction replaces $z$ by $-z$. Work in the <weak-field approximation>, with $\omega l\ll c$, $r_0\gg l,c/\omega$, and detector size $a\ll c/\omega,r_0$. The <radiation reaction> is neglected, so the rod maintains the prescribed steady motion.

At the retarded source time $u=t-(r_0+z)/c$, take the source positions as $\mathbf x_\pm=\pm l(\cos(\omega u+\beta),\sin(\omega u+\beta),0)$. The arbitrary constant $\beta$ specifies the initial source phase. The <second mass moment tensor> $I_{ij}=\sum_A Mx_{Ai}x_{Aj}$ obeys
$$
I_{xx}=Ml^2[1+\cos\Phi],\quad I_{yy}=Ml^2[1-\cos\Phi],\quad
I_{xy}=I_{yx}=Ml^2\sin\Phi,\qquad
\Phi=2\omega\left(t-\frac{r_0+z}{c}\right)+2\beta.
$$
The <quadrupole formula> $h_{ij}^{\rm TT}=2G\ddot I_{ij}^{\rm TT}/(c^4r_0)$ projects away the transverse trace, which is already constant here. Differentiating twice therefore gives
$$
\boxed{h_{xx}=-H\cos\Phi,\qquad h_{yy}=H\cos\Phi,\qquad
h_{xy}=h_{yx}=-H\sin\Phi,\qquad
H=\frac{8GMl^2\omega^2}{c^4r_0}.}
$$
All time and longitudinal <metric perturbation> components vanish in <transverse-traceless gauge>. The <gravitational-wave frequency> is $2\omega$ in angular units; $M$ is each source <mass>, not their sum, and the detector <mass> $m$ does not enter the strain. The <plane wave> approximation holds the amplitude fixed at $r_0$; retaining spherical spreading replaces its denominator by $r_0+z$ near the axis. The retarded <quadrupole formula> and the distinction between source and wave <frequency> are derived in <General Relativity by David Tong>[Sections 5.3.1–5.3.2, retarded source solution and circular binary]{ref}.

Displacement must be specified relative to an observer. In <transverse-traceless gauge> the free particles can have fixed coordinates, while their measured <proper lengths> oscillate. In a local, freely falling <orthonormal frame>, the <geodesic deviation> equation for an initial separation $\mathbf X=(X,Y,0)$ is, to first order in $H$,
$$
\ddot\xi_i=\frac12\ddot h_{ij}(t,0)X^j,\qquad \ddot\xi_z=0.
$$
Its general solution with physical displacement $\boldsymbol\xi(0)=0$ and initial physical <velocity> $\mathbf V$ is
$$
\boxed{\xi_i(t)=\frac12[h_{ij}(t,0)-h_{ij}(0,0)]X^j
+\left[V_i-\frac12\dot h_{ij}(0,0)X^j\right]t.}
$$
The source has been running indefinitely, so there is no wave-free initial state. Its <initial conditions for an eternal monochromatic gravitational wave> need more than positions to fix $\mathbf V$. The usual bounded, periodic response selects $V_i=\dot h_{ij}(0,0)X^j/2$; if the particles instead start at physical rest at $t=0$, set $V_i=0$ and retain the linear term. Both solve the same linear <geodesic deviation> problem. The distinction between fixed TT coordinates and measured separation is explained in <General Relativity by David Tong>[Section 5.2.2, response of free particles to a wave]{ref}.

Choose $\beta$ so the wave phase at the detector is $\Phi(t,0)=2\omega t$. For the bounded response the four initial placements then give
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\mathbf X&\xi_x&\xi_y&\xi_z\\ \hline
(\sigma a,0,0)&\dfrac{\sigma aH}{2}(1-\cos2\omega t)&-\dfrac{\sigma aH}{2}\sin2\omega t&0\\[2pt]
(0,\sigma a,0)&-\dfrac{\sigma aH}{2}\sin2\omega t&\dfrac{\sigma aH}{2}(\cos2\omega t-1)&0
\end{array}\qquad (\sigma=\pm1).
$$
These are displacements from the specified positions at $t=0$; positions are $\mathbf X+\boldsymbol\xi$. Equivalently, relative to mean positions the usual oscillatory displacement is $\boldsymbol\xi_{\rm osc}=h\mathbf X/2$ to first order. The response repeats after $\pi/\omega$.

Both <plus polarization> and <cross polarization> occur with equal amplitude and a quarter-cycle phase difference. Their superposition is <circular gravitational-wave polarization>, with handedness reversed when the viewing direction or source rotation is reversed. The principal stretching axis turns through half the wave phase. There is no leading longitudinal motion.

<Gravitational radiation> admits <polarization>: <general relativity> has two transverse tensor modes, <plus polarization> and <cross polarization>, whose relative amplitudes and phase give linear, elliptical or <circular gravitational-wave polarization>. Their axes transform through twice a rotation of the transverse coordinate frame, unlike the vector <polarization> of <electromagnetic radiation>. Measuring these modes is physically plausible with several differently oriented detectors; a single detector generally measures only one projected combination.

<Gravitational waves> can exhibit <interference>. In <linearized gravity>, overlapping strains add, so coherent contributions can enhance or cancel the measured response. Independent astrophysical sources generally have unrelated phases and average to a stochastic background rather than stable fringes. More plausible coherent paths arise when <gravitational lensing> sends different parts of the same signal to the detector with different delays; fringes require sufficient overlap and coherence. The relevant quadratic radiative <energy flux> contains the cross terms even though the leading wave equation is linear.

In a <local inertial frame> in <vacuum>, <gravitational waves> travel at $c$ in <general relativity>; there is no ordinary material <index of refraction> analogous to a transparent dielectric. Ordinary matter couples so weakly that laboratory prisms for <gravitational waves> are implausible. Propagation through a varying background <gravitational field> nevertheless changes ray directions and travel times, giving <gravitational lensing> and <Shapiro time delay>. In weak static coordinates $ds^2\simeq-(1+2\Phi_N/c^2)c^2dt^2+(1-2\Phi_N/c^2)d\mathbf x^2$, the null condition gives an effective coordinate <index of refraction> $n_{\rm eff}\simeq1-2\Phi_N/c^2$. This useful description depends on coordinates and does not change the local propagation <speed>. Thus astronomical gravitational refraction is plausible, while a substantial everyday material effect is not.

<Gravitational waves> also exhibit <diffraction>: the <geometrical optics> ray description fails when a propagation structure or lens delay is not large compared with the wave's <wavelength> or period. For a compact lens of <mass> $M_L$, a useful <dimensionless> parameter is $w=4GM_L\omega_{\rm gw}/c^3$; $w$ of order one signals potentially appreciable wave effects, with cosmological lens redshift supplying an additional factor $1+z_L$ when needed. Astrophysical <gravitational lensing> can therefore produce <diffraction> and frequency-dependent amplification. Ordinary objects are poor opaque screens for <gravitational waves>, so everyday slit-and-screen demonstrations are implausible despite the large <wavelengths>, which are about $3000$ km at $100$ Hz.

The intended orbit is a freely falling <timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic>. Use the <Schwarzschild metric> with $t$ rather than $ct$ as the zeroth coordinate, and put the orbit at $\theta=\pi/2$. Thus $g_{tt}=-fc^2$, $g_{rr}=1/f$, $g_{\phi\phi}=r^2$, with $f=1-2GM/(rc^2)$. The radial <geodesic equation> at constant $r$ is
$$
\Gamma^r_{tt}(u^t)^2+\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}(u^\phi)^2=0,
\qquad \Gamma^r_{tt}=\frac{GMf}{r^2},\qquad \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}=-rf.
$$
Consequently $\Omega^2=(u^\phi/u^t)^2=GM/r^3$. The <four-velocity> normalization gives
$$
-c^2=-fc^2(u^t)^2+r^2\Omega^2(u^t)^2
=-c^2\left(1-\frac{3GM}{rc^2}\right)(u^t)^2,
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{u^0=u^t=\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\left(1-\frac{3GM}{rc^2}\right)^{-1/2}.}
$$
Here $t$ is <Schwarzschild time>, $\tau$ the orbiting clock's <proper time>, $G$ the <gravitational constant>, $M$ the central <mass>, $r$ the constant <areal radius>, and $c$ the <speed of light>. The dimensionless $u^0$ follows the question's time-valued zeroth-coordinate convention; with $x^0=ct$ the zeroth <four-velocity> component would be $c\,dt/d\tau$.

The condition is $r>3GM/c^2$ for a massive circular <geodesic>; radial stability further requires $r>6GM/c^2$, with equality marginal. The phrase “any circular orbit” is false without free fall: a powered circular trajectory has $dt/d\tau=[f-r^2\Omega^2/c^2]^{-1/2}$ with independently chosen $\Omega$. For example, at $r=10GM/c^2$ choose $\Omega=\tfrac12\sqrt{GM/r^3}$; this valid accelerated circular trajectory has $dt/d\tau=\sqrt{40/31}$, instead of the displayed $\sqrt{10/7}$.

Let $\mu=GM/c^2$, $f=1-2\mu/r$, $q=\sqrt{1-3\mu/r}=1/u^0$, and choose $\Omega>0$. A torque-free test <gyroscope> moving on a <geodesic> has a <spin four-vector> obeying <parallel transport>,
$$
\frac{ds^\mu}{dt}+\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^\alpha}{dt}s^\beta=0,
\qquad g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu s^\nu=0.
$$
The orthogonality condition says the temporal spin component vanishes in the body's <rest frame>, not necessarily in Schwarzschild coordinates. Here it gives
$$
s^t=\frac{r^2\Omega}{fc^2}s^\phi.
$$
In addition to the radial <Christoffel symbols> in part (a), use $\Gamma^t_{tr}=\mu/(r^2f)$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi}=1/r$. On the equator the <parallel transport> equations become
$$
\frac{ds^t}{dt}=-\frac{\mu}{r^2f}s^r,\qquad
\frac{ds^r}{dt}=r\Omega q^2s^\phi,\qquad
\frac{ds^\phi}{dt}=-\frac{\Omega}{r}s^r,\qquad
\frac{ds^\theta}{dt}=0.
$$
The radial equation follows by substituting $s^t$ into $ds^r/dt+\Gamma^r_{tt}s^t+\Omega\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}s^\phi=0$. Eliminating $s^\phi$ gives $d^2s^r/dt^2+\Omega^2q^2s^r=0$.

Let $s_0$ be the invariant spin magnitude. Initially radial spin means $s^r(0)=s_0\sqrt f$ and $s^t(0)=s^\phi(0)=s^\theta(0)=0$. The complete solution is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
s^r(t)&=s_0\sqrt f\cos(\Omega qt),\\
s^\phi(t)&=-\frac{s_0\sqrt f}{rq}\sin(\Omega qt),\\
s^\theta(t)&=0,\\
s^t(t)&=-\frac{s_0r\Omega}{c^2\sqrt f\,q}\sin(\Omega qt).
\end{aligned}}
$$
To interpret the orientation, convert coordinate components into the orbiting <orthonormal frame>. The <Lorentz factor> relative to a <Schwarzschild static observer> is $\gamma=\sqrt f/q$, so the body's measured radial and tangential spin components are
$$
S^{\hat r}=\frac{s^r}{\sqrt f}=s_0\cos(\Omega qt),\qquad
S^{\hat\phi}=\frac{rs^\phi}{\gamma}=-s_0\sin(\Omega qt).
$$
Their squared sum is $s_0^2$, and their angle relative to the outward radial direction decreases uniformly at rate
$$
\boxed{\Omega_r=\Omega q=\frac{\Omega}{u^0}.}
$$
The differing coordinate amplitudes in $s^r,s^\phi$ must not be interpreted as a nonuniform physical spin rotation. After one orbital period the radial basis has turned by $2\pi$ while the spin has turned by $-2\pi q$ relative to it. The residual <geodetic precession> is $\Delta\psi=2\pi(1-q)$ in the orbital direction.

For a <timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic>, the coordinate <orbital period> and onboard <proper time> per orbit are
$$
T_\infty=\frac{2\pi}{\Omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}},\qquad
T_{\rm crew}=\int_0^{T_\infty}\frac{dt}{u^0}
=T_\infty\sqrt{1-\frac{3GM}{Rc^2}}.
$$
Using $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,{\rm m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ and $c=299792458\,{\rm m\,s^{-1}}$, the <Schwarzschild radius> and orbital <areal radius> are $R_s=2GM/c^2\simeq1.21789\times10^{10}\,\mathrm m$ and $R=200R_s\simeq2.43578\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
T_\infty&\simeq1.02100\times10^6\,\mathrm s=11.8172\,\mathrm{days},\\
T_{\rm crew}&=T_\infty\sqrt{\frac{397}{400}}
\simeq1.01717\times10^6\,\mathrm s=11.7728\,\mathrm{days}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The crew accumulates about $3836$ fewer seconds, or $63.9$ fewer minutes, per orbit. Both <gravitational time dilation> and kinematic <time dilation> contribute: a static clock at $R$ has $dT_{\rm static}=\sqrt{1-R_s/R}\,dt$, while the orbiting clock additionally has $d\tau=dT_{\rm static}/\gamma$, where $\gamma=\sqrt{398/397}$. A distant observer measures the coordinate period; comparing arrivals emitted at the same orbital phase also removes the repeated light propagation delay.

$$
The orbital position and <four-velocity> repeat after a full orbit, so the direction to distant Earth in the instantaneous <rest frame> repeats. The freely transported <gyroscope> does not: its beam axis has turned through $\Delta\psi$ relative to the original aiming direction. This applies to any initial in-plane spin direction by linearity of <parallel transport>, not only the radial initial condition used in part (b). The beam's angular diameter is $1^\circ$, giving a half-angle of $0.5^\circ$. Thus **Earth lies outside the beam after one orbit**; comparing the offset with the diameter would already show a miss, but the correct acceptance angle is the half-angle.

The initial radial ray must be outward from the <black hole> for a direct path to Earth. A precisely inward radial <null geodesic> falls into the <black hole> and cannot provide the assumed initial communication. For <outgoing near-radial Schwarzschild light deflection>, the angular correction is especially small. If a static observer at $R$ measures a small emission angle $\psi$ from the outward radial direction, its <impact parameter> is $b=R\sin\psi/\sqrt{f(R)}$. The outgoing <null geodesic> gives
$$
\Delta\phi=\int_R^\infty\frac{b\,dr}{r^2\sqrt{1-f(r)b^2/r^2}}
=\frac{b}{R}+O\left(\frac{b^3}{R^3}\right)
=\frac{\psi}{\sqrt{f(R)}}+O(\psi^3).
$$
Relative to flat propagation, <curvature> changes the small pointing angle and beam radius by the fraction $f(R)^{-1/2}-1\simeq0.00251$. At an offset of $1.35^\circ$ this is only about $0.0034^\circ$; the beam's $0.5^\circ$ radius changes by about $0.0013^\circ$. The exactly radial central ray has zero angular deflection. This is not the geometry of the usual $4GM/(bc^2)$ scattering formula, which assumes a ray coming from infinity, passing the <black hole>, and returning to infinity.

<Relativistic aberration> also distinguishes a coordinate-radial light ray from a beam aimed radially in the moving crew's frame. Here $v/c=1/\sqrt{398}\simeq0.0501$. Successful initial aiming includes that fixed correction; the same correction applies at the repeated orbital position. More precisely, an outgoing static-radial ray initially has crew-frame angle $\psi'_0=-\arcsin(v/c)$ from the radial axis. The local small-angle aberration factor about that aim is $d\psi/d\psi'=\gamma=\sqrt{398/397}$; including the outgoing propagation factor gives $\gamma/\sqrt f=1/q\simeq1.00377$. Thus the asymptotic miss is about $1.358^\circ$ and the beam half-angle about $0.502^\circ$, with the same conclusion. The small distortion of the outward beam cannot correct the much larger accumulated <geodetic precession>.

On large scales the appropriate background is the spatially flat <FLRW metric>,
$$
\boxed{ds^2=-c^2dt^2+R^2(t)\left[dr^2+r^2d\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right].}
$$
Here $t$ is <cosmic time>, the <proper time> of a <comoving observer>; $R(t)$ is the <scale factor>; and $(r,\theta,\phi)$ are dimensionless <comoving coordinates>. With this convention $R$ has units of <length>. A fixed comoving radial interval has instantaneous <proper length> $R(t)\,dr$. The <cosmological principle> assumes <spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> on the scales where this background description applies.

The general <FLRW metric> instead has $dr^2/(1-kr^2)$ in its radial part, with normalized $k=+1,0,-1$ describing positive, zero or negative <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>. The best simple description takes $k=0$; observations constrain spatial <curvature> to be small rather than proving it identically zero. The late accelerated expansion is encoded by the time dependence of $R$, determined by the <stress-energy tensor> through the <Friedmann equations>, rather than by an additional term in the <FLRW metric>. Small-scale galaxies and other inhomogeneities require perturbations of this background.

For a <perfect fluid in general relativity>, let $\rho$ be rest-frame total <energy> density divided by $c^2$, $P$ its isotropic <pressure>, and $U^\mu$ its <four-velocity>, normalized by $U^\mu U_\mu=-c^2$. Then
$$
T^{\mu\nu}=\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right)U^\mu U^\nu+Pg^{\mu\nu}.
$$
The <contracted Bianchi identity> and the <Einstein field equations> give <stress-energy conservation>, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$, for a constant <cosmological constant>. Contract with $U_\nu$ and use the normalization to obtain
$$
U^\mu\partial_\mu\rho+\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right)\nabla_\mu U^\mu=0.
$$
In <comoving coordinates> $U^\mu=(1,0,0,0)$ and the <covariant divergence> is $\nabla_\mu U^\mu=3\dot R/R=3H$, where a dot is a <cosmic time> <derivative> and $H$ the <Hubble parameter>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot\rho+3H\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right)=0.}
$$
For a fixed region in <comoving coordinates>, let $V(t)=V_*R^3(t)$ be its physical <volume>, where $V_*$ is a fixed coordinate-volume integral, and let $E=\rho c^2V$. Since $\dot V=3HV$,
$$
\frac{dE}{dt}+P\frac{dV}{dt}
=c^2V\left[\dot\rho+3H\left(\rho+\frac{P}{c^2}\right)\right]=0.
$$
Thus the <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> is exactly equivalent to the adiabatic <first law of thermodynamics>, $\boxed{dE=-P\,dV}$, for every nonzero such region. Conversely, expanding that <first law of thermodynamics> and dividing by $c^2V$ recovers the same equation. No <heat> or <particle> flux crosses the comoving boundary; interacting components separately need source terms, although the total still obeys this conservation equation.

There is a sign inconsistency in the supplied formula sheet worth isolating. Its <Riemann curvature tensor> and <Ricci tensor> conventions, together with its stated $-+++$ <metric signature>, give $G_{tt}=-3H^2$ for $R(t)=e^{Ht}$ with constant $H$. Its vacuum <Einstein field equations> with $+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ then require $H^2=-\Lambda c^2/3$, whereas its printed vacuum <Friedmann equation> gives $H^2=+\Lambda c^2/3$. For example, $H>0$, $\rho=P=0$ and $\Lambda=3H^2/c^2$ satisfy the latter but leave the former's $tt$ component equal to $-6H^2$. Changing the geometric <cosmological constant> term to $-\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ repairs this mismatch. The conservation derivation is unaffected; below take the geometric <cosmological constant> to be zero and include any <vacuum energy> as a fluid, so no ambiguous sign enters.

Write the <equation of state> as $P=w\rho c^2$. Integrating the <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> for constant $w$ gives $\rho\propto R^{-3(1+w)}$. With spatial flatness and the geometric <cosmological constant> set to zero, the <Friedmann equation> is $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3$.

In S1, $w=-1$ describes <vacuum energy>: $T_{\mu\nu}=-\rho c^2g_{\mu\nu}$ and $\rho$ is constant. Its negative <pressure> drives accelerated expansion, $\ddot R/R=8\pi G\rho/3=H_1^2$, and the expanding solution is $R\propto e^{H_1t}$. This is the ideal <de Sitter spacetime> approximation to <cosmic inflation>. An exactly constant vacuum component cannot end its phase unaided; a phase transition or <reheating> mechanism is required for the assumed change to S2.

In S2, $w=1/3$ describes an isotropic bath of ultrarelativistic <particles>, such as <photons>, with negligible <rest mass> relative to their <energy>. The number per physical <volume> falls as $R^{-3}$, while the <energy> per particle redshifts as $R^{-1}$, so $\rho\propto R^{-4}$. This <radiation-dominated universe> has $R\propto(t-t_*)^{1/2}$ on its expanding branch.

In S3, $w=0$ describes <pressureless matter>, or <relativistic dust>, with negligible random <kinetic energy>. Conserved particle number and approximately fixed <rest energy> give $\rho\propto R^{-3}$. A flat universe containing only this component is the <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, with $R\propto(t-t_*)^{2/3}$. The constants $t_*$ are integration offsets in the individual phases; they need not represent one common start time when phases are matched.

For monotonically expanding <scale factor> $R$, $dt=dR/(RH)$, so the elapsed <cosmic time> is
$$
\boxed{t_0-t_i=\int_{R_i}^{R_0}\frac{dR}{R\,H(R)}
=\int_0^{z_i}\frac{dz}{(1+z)H(z)}.}
$$
The second expression uses <cosmological redshift> $1+z=R_0/R$; the <age of the universe> is the limit to its earliest specified state, if that limit is finite.

An <age comparison for an initial vacuum-dominated phase> needs a common normalization. Match the two histories at the end of S1, with $R=R_1$ and $H=H_1>0$, and give them identical subsequent S2 and S3 evolution, including transition radii and today's state. Below $R_1$, the history with S1 has constant $H=H_1$. The comparison history extends S2 backwards, so $\rho\propto R^{-4}$ and $H=H_1(R_1/R)^2$. Starting both at $R_i=\epsilon R_1$, where $0<\epsilon<1$, their early durations are
$$
\Delta t_{\rm S1}=\frac1{H_1}\ln\frac1\epsilon,\qquad
\Delta t_{\rm S2}=\int_{\epsilon R_1}^{R_1}\frac{R\,dR}{H_1R_1^2}
=\frac{1-\epsilon^2}{2H_1}.
$$
The difference is positive because
$$
\boxed{\Delta t_{\rm S1}-\Delta t_{\rm S2}
=\frac1{H_1}\int_\epsilon^1\left(\frac1x-x\right)dx>0.}
$$
The common later evolution adds the same time to both histories. Thus S1 makes this matched universe older. More strongly, as $R_i\to0$, the exact S1 duration diverges logarithmically, whereas a radiation beginning has finite duration $1/(2H_1)$ to the match. The exact <de Sitter spacetime> expanding patch reaches $R=0$ only as $t\to-\infty$ for its <comoving observers>; a finite “age since $R=0$” does not exist in this ideal model. This does not establish past <geodesic completeness> of the patch.

The matching assumption is essential for a finite cutoff or realistic finite period of <cosmic inflation>: arbitrary changes of the present <Hubble parameter> or transition densities prevent a universal age comparison. The literal zero-size limit in this question is instead the infinite-duration idealization just derived.

The actual large-scale expansion is well described by the <Lambda-CDM model>: <radiation in cosmology>, ordinary baryonic <matter>, <cold dark matter> and late-time <dark energy> coexist. A component becomes dominant through continuous changes in their density ratios, rather than every previous component disappearing at an instantaneous phase boundary. Matter <pressure> is small in its nonrelativistic regime, but <radiation> persists, and massive <neutrinos> interpolate between relativistic and nonrelativistic behavior.

There is also a late accelerated era, absent from a model ending permanently in S3. It is consistent with nearly constant <vacuum energy>, whose negative <pressure> eventually dominates because <pressureless matter> dilutes as $R^{-3}$. The <scale factor> then ceases to follow the pure $t^{2/3}$ <Einstein-de Sitter universe> law.

An early phase of <cosmic inflation> is a well-motivated model rather than a directly observed exact $w=-1$ phase. Successful <cosmic inflation> must end and transfer its <energy> to matter and <radiation> through <reheating>; realistic models generally have slowly varying $H$ and $w$ close to, rather than exactly equal to, $-1$. Extrapolating a strictly constant $H$ to arbitrarily small $R$ is not a measurement of the universe's past duration. Finally, <spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> apply statistically on large scales: galaxies, <gravitational waves> and other perturbations are absent from the exact <FLRW metric>, and small spatial <curvature> remains an observational possibility.
