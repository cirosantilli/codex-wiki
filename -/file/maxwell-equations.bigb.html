= Maxwell's equations
{c}
{synonym}

The <Maxwell equations> are the field equations of classical <electromagnetism>. In <SI> units, in vacuum with prescribed <charge density> $\rho$ and <current density> $\mathbf J$, they are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho/\epsilon_0,
&\nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\
\nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},
&\nabla\times\mathbf B&=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\mathbf E$ is the <electric field> in volts per metre, $\mathbf B$ the <magnetic field> in teslas, $\rho$ in coulombs per cubic metre, and $\mathbf J$ in amperes per square metre. The constants are <vacuum permittivity> $\epsilon_0$ and the vacuum value $\mu_0$ of <magnetic permeability>. “Vacuum” specifies the field laws without a material response; “source-free” additionally means $\rho=0$ and $\mathbf J=0$. Matter described by averaged fields instead requires <Maxwell equations in matter> and <constitutive relations>.

The first equation, <Gauss's law>, makes <electric charge> a source of <electric flux>. <Gauss's law for magnetism> sets the net <magnetic flux> through a closed surface to zero. The two <curl> equations, <Faraday's law> and the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>, couple changes of the <electric field> and <magnetic field>. Appropriate <initial conditions> and <boundary conditions> select a physical solution; the two <divergence> equations constrain the initial fields. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/2d2f227e6fd26ee25f366759e02f03dc_chapte13em_waves.pdf[MIT 8.02 notes, §13.3: Maxwell's equations]{ref}.

= Quasistatic approximation
{synonym}

An approximation that keeps slow electromagnetic time variation while treating propagation across characteristic size $L$ as rapid compared with the variation time $1/\omega$. The dimensionless parameter $\omega L/c$ measures that separation. Which terms of the <Maxwell equations> can be dropped still depends on the physical scaling; small propagation delay alone does not justify deleting both <displacement current> and <electromagnetic induction>. For a slowly driven <parallel-plate capacitor>, a nearly uniform gap <electric field> can coexist with the <magnetic field> generated by its changing <electric flux>.

= Electromagnetic duality
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{wiki}

Vacuum <Maxwell equations> without sources are invariant under $E'=E\cos\alpha+cB\sin\alpha$, $cB'=cB\cos\alpha-E\sin\alpha$. At $\alpha=\pi/2$ this is $E'=cB$, $B'=-E/c$. Electric-conductor boundary conditions are not invariant under arbitrary duality rotations; the transformed fields must be tested separately against the wall conditions.

= Derivative-order ambiguity in covariant Maxwell potential equations
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{title2=$\nabla_b\nabla^a A^b-\nabla^a\nabla_bA^b=-C^a{}_bA^b$}

Use the negative curvature convention defined in <curvature-sign convention in Killing derivative identities>, and $C_{ab}=C^c{}_{acb}$. Covariant derivatives of a vector obey $[\nabla_b,\nabla^a]A^b=-C^a{}_bA^b$. Hence the flat-space equivalent expressions $\Box A^a-\partial_b\partial^aA^b$ and $\Box A^a-\partial^a\partial_bA^b$ acquire a difference $+C^a{}_bA^b$ under the <comma-to-semicolon prescription>. Covariantizing $F_{ab}=2\nabla_{[a}A_{b]}$ and its <Maxwell equations> gives $\Box A^a-\nabla^a(\nabla_bA^b)+C^a{}_bA^b=\mu_0J^a$. This form respects <gauge invariance>. Dropping the Ricci term is generally not gauge invariant. The two expressions coincide in a Ricci-flat <vacuum spacetime>; vanishing matter alone with nonzero <cosmological constant> does not imply Ricci flatness.

= Magnetic Galilean limit of the pre-Maxwell equations
{parent=Maxwell equations}

The magnetic nonrelativistic limit neglects displacement current and uses $\partial_t\mathbf B=-\nabla\times\mathbf E$, $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Under a <Galilean transformation> $\mathbf x'=\mathbf x-\mathbf vt$, $t'=t$, its consistent field transformations are $\mathbf B'=\mathbf B$, $\mathbf J'=\mathbf J$ and $\mathbf E'=\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. Indeed $\partial_{t'}=\partial_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla$ and $\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=-(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B$, giving covariance of the induction equation. The full charge-current transformation has $\mathbf J'=\mathbf J-\rho_e\mathbf v$; neglecting its convective correction belongs to the magnetic quasineutral approximation, not exact relativistic electrodynamics. With $\mathbf u'=\mathbf u-\mathbf v$, the combination $\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$ is invariant. Vanishing electric field in the fluid rest frame therefore gives the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation>.

= Gauss's law for magnetism
{c}
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{wiki}

Gauss's law for magnetism is $\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Its integral form says that the net magnetic flux through every closed surface vanishes, so the normal magnetic field is continuous across an ordinary interface.

= Axisymmetric magnetic field near a regular axis
{parent=Gauss's law for magnetism}
{title2=$B_r(r,z)=-\frac r2B_0'(z)+O(r^3)$}

Let a smooth <magnetic field> with <axisymmetry> have axial value $B_0(z)$ and no line singularity on its axis. <Gauss's law for magnetism> gives $\partial_r(rB_r)=-r\partial_zB_z$. Since $B_z(r,z)=B_0(z)+O(r^2)$, integration from the regular axis gives the displayed expansion. A falling axial field therefore implies outward field lines above a positive-source loop. In a current-free region, <Ampère's law> also gives $B_z(r,z)=B_0(z)-r^2B_0''(z)/4+O(r^4)$, which follows by equating $\partial_zB_r=\partial_rB_z$.

= Near-axis radial magnetic field
{parent=Gauss's law for magnetism}
{title2=$B_\rho=-\tfrac12\rho B_0'(z)+O(\rho^3)$}

For a smooth axisymmetric <magnetic field>, write $B_z(\rho,z)=B_0(z)+O(\rho^2)$. The absence of magnetic monopoles gives $\rho^{-1}\partial_\rho(\rho B_\rho)=-B_0'(z)+O(\rho^2)$. Integrating from the axis and excluding a singular $1/\rho$ term proves the displayed relation. An axial field decreasing along $+z$ has an outward radial component, even when the field points purely axially on the axis itself.

= Vacuum Maxwell equations
{c}
{parent=Maxwell equations}

For the electromagnetic two-form $F$, the source-free Maxwell equations are $dF=0$ and $d(*F)=0$. The first is the Bianchi identity and the second is the dynamical equation.

= ASD Maxwell equations
{synonym}
{c}

In the <two-component spinor> convention with primed symmetric <gauge curvature> self-dual, an <Abelian> <Maxwell field> is anti-self-dual when its primed symmetric <gauge curvature> <two-component spinor> vanishes. For a locally exact field $F=dA$, the <Bianchi identity> gives $dF=0$; the duality relation then also gives $d{*}F=0$. Thus it obeys the source-free <Maxwell equations>. In complexified Lorentzian signature the <Hodge star> <eigenvalues> are $\pm i$; the <two-component spinor> condition specifies which component is meant without imposing a Euclidean eigenvalue on that signature.

= Maxwell equations in matter
{c}
{parent=Maxwell equations}

In macroscopic matter with free charge density $\rho$ and free current density $J$,
$$
\nabla\cdot D=\rho,
\quad
\nabla\cdot B=0,
\quad
\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB,
\quad
\nabla\times H=J+\partial_tD.
$$
The electric displacement $D$ and magnetic field strength $H$ encode the material response.

These are the <Maxwell equations> after separating free and bound material sources.

= Constitutive relations
{parent=Maxwell equations in matter}

Constitutive relations specify how matter responds to fields. A homogeneous isotropic linear electromagnetic medium obeys $\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$.

= Magnetic permeability

Electromagnetic permeability relates magnetic flux density to magnetic field strength through $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$ in an isotropic linear medium.

= Vacuum permeability
{parent=Permeability (electromagnetism)}
{title2=$\mu_0$}

Vacuum permeability is the <magnetic permeability> appearing in the vacuum <Maxwell equations>. In <SI>, it relates vacuum magnetic field strength to <magnetic flux density> through $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$ and obeys $\mu_0\varepsilon_0c^2=1$, where $c$ is the <speed of light> and $\varepsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>.

= Linear anisotropic dielectric
{parent=Maxwell equations in matter}

A linear anisotropic medium has constitutive relations $D_i=\varepsilon_{ij}E_j$ and $B_i=\mu_{ij}H_j$. Symmetric, time-independent tensors make the field-energy density a quadratic form.

Here <permittivity> is a tensor, so the electric displacement need not be parallel to the electric field.

= Birefringent
{synonym}

<Birefringence> is the existence of different optical propagation constants for different eigenpolarizations in an anisotropic material. It provides the propagation-mode separation needed for <adiabatic optical following in a twisted nematic>.

Two different <refractive indices> can produce two displaced images, as in the <calcite double-image experiment>, or a <phase retardation> between overlapping components, as in a <waveplate>. Spatial separation concerns where <energy flux> travels; <phase retardation> concerns the relative <wave phase>. A specimen need not show two visibly separated images to have <birefringence>.

= Birefringent optical compensator
{parent=Birefringence}

A known retarder inserted in a <polarization> microscope to compare <phase> delay with an unknown specimen. Parallel slow directions add signed retardations; a slow direction paired with a fast one subtracts them. General relative angles require composing <polarization> transformations rather than scalar addition.

= Quartz compensator
{parent=Birefringent optical compensator}

A <quartz> retarder used to identify specimen fast and slow directions through changes of <interference colour> or compensation. Its own retardation and orientation must be specified before unique final colours can be predicted.

= Extraordinary phase-index angular dependence
{parent=Birefringence}

For a lossless uniaxial <dielectric>, $n_{\rm ex}^{-2}=\cos^2\vartheta/n_o^2+\sin^2\vartheta/n_e^2$, where $\vartheta$ is the angle between the <wave vector> and <optic axis>. Along the axis $n_{\rm ex}=n_o$, while perpendicular propagation gives $n_{\rm ex}=n_e$.

= Interference colour
{parent=Birefringence}

Colour produced when wavelength-dependent <interference> selectively transmits or reflects part of white light. Between crossed <polarizers>, a uniform birefringent plate gives a factor $\sin^2(\pi\Gamma/\lambda)$, where $\Gamma$ is its <optical path difference>. The observed colour also depends on illumination and the spectral response of the observer.

= Spectral extinction between crossed polarizers
{parent=Interference colour}
{title2=$\Gamma=m\lambda$}

A birefringent plate with retardation $\Gamma=t\Delta n$ gives $I/I_P=\sin^2(2\alpha)\sin^2(\pi\Gamma/\lambda)$. At non-extinction azimuths, wavelengths satisfying $\Gamma=m\lambda$ are removed by a crossed analyser. At a principal-axis azimuth every wavelength is extinguished.

= Form birefringence
{parent=Birefringence}

<Birefringence> produced by subwavelength structure rather than solely by intrinsic crystalline anisotropy. For ideal dielectric lamellae, continuity of tangential <electric field> gives $\epsilon_\parallel=f\epsilon_1+(1-f)\epsilon_2$, while continuity of normal <electric displacement field> gives $\epsilon_\perp^{-1}=f/\epsilon_1+(1-f)/\epsilon_2$. The unequal effective <permittivities> give different principal <refractive indices>.

= Linear birefringence
{parent=Birefringence}

<Linear birefringence> means that two orthogonal <linear polarizations> have different <refractive indices>. Their different propagation phases produce <phase retardation>. In a lossless <waveplate>, with fast-axis angle $\theta$ and slow-minus-fast retardation $\delta$, the <Jones matrix>, up to a common phase, is
$$
J_\delta(\theta)=R(-\theta)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\delta}\end{pmatrix}R(\theta),\qquad R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},
$$
using time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$. <Circular birefringence> instead has circular eigenpolarizations.

= Calcite double-image experiment
{parent=Birefringence}

Place a clear https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite[calcite] specimen on a transparency bearing printed words on an overhead projector. The projected readout is two copies of the lettering. Put a rotatable linear <polarizer> after the specimen: suitable orientations suppress one copy or the other. These are the actual apparatus and observations in the University of Iowa demonstration.<Polarization - Calcite & Iceland Spar by University of Iowa, Department of Physics and Astronomy>[Demonstration 6H35.15, Equipment and Procedure: crystals, polarizers, printed overhead transparency, two sets of words and selective extinction; final note on natural defects]{ref}

For a useful control, remove the specimen while keeping the transparency, projector and screen fixed: the doubling should disappear. Replace it, hold it stationary and rotate only the <polarizer>; complementary brightness changes distinguish two <linear polarizations> from an accidental duplicate transparency. These are suggested controls, rather than additional reported measurements. Ideally the two copies obey complementary <Malus's law> curves, $I_o(\alpha)=I_{o,\max}\cos^2\alpha$ and $I_e(\alpha)=I_{e,\max}\sin^2\alpha$, with <angle> $\alpha$ measured from the exiting <ordinary wave>'s <linear polarization>. Unequal maxima do not invalidate the test. Finite <polarizer> extinction, poor focus and specimen defects limit visibility; image separation alone does not determine either <refractive index> without the specimen geometry.

= Uniaxial optical medium

A transparent, nonmagnetic <linear anisotropic dielectric> is uniaxial when two principal <permittivities> agree. With the <optic axis> along $z$, its <permittivity> <tensor> has the displayed <diagonal matrix> form. = Optic axis
{parent=Uniaxial optical medium}

The symmetry direction of a <uniaxial optical medium>. For a <wavevector> along it, the two <refractive indices> coincide: there is no <birefringence> for that propagation direction, even though $n_e\ne n_o$.

= Ordinary wave
{parent=Uniaxial optical medium}
{title2=$n_o$}

The <ordinary wave>'s <electric field> is perpendicular to the plane containing the <wavevector> and <optic axis>. Its <refractive index> $n_o$ is direction independent at fixed <frequency>.

= Extraordinary wave
{parent=Uniaxial optical medium}
{title2=$n_{\mathrm{ex}}(\theta)$}

The <extraordinary wave>'s <electric displacement field> lies in that plane and is perpendicular to the <wavevector>; its <electric field> need not be. For <angle> $\theta$ between the <wavevector> and <optic axis>,
$$
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{ex}}(\theta)^2}=\frac{\cos^2\theta}{n_o^2}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{n_e^2}.
$$
Thus $n_{\mathrm{ex}}(0)=n_o$ and $n_{\mathrm{ex}}(\pi/2)=n_e$.<Fundamentals of Photonics and Quantum Electronics lecture notes by Franz X. Kärtner>[§§2.8.2–2.8.3, printed p. 156, equations 2.410–2.413 and longitudinal electric-field discussion]{ref}

A short derivation starts from the existing <plane wave in an anisotropic dielectric> equation. Put $\mathbf k=(k_x,0,k_z)$ and $k_0=\omega/c$, with <angular frequency> $\omega$ and vacuum <speed of light> $c$. The $y$ component gives the <ordinary wave> relation $k_x^2+k_z^2=n_o^2k_0^2$. The <determinant> of the $x,z$ system gives
$$
\frac{k_x^2}{n_e^2}+\frac{k_z^2}{n_o^2}=k_0^2.
$$
Substituting $k_x=n_{\mathrm{ex}}k_0\sin\theta$ and $k_z=n_{\mathrm{ex}}k_0\cos\theta$ yields the displayed directional <refractive index>.

= Birefringent walk-off
{parent=Extraordinary wave}
{title2=$\rho=\phi-\theta$}

The <extraordinary wave>'s <Poynting vector> can differ in direction from its <wavevector>. The latter specifies <wave phase> propagation, while the former specifies <energy flux>. This angular separation is walk-off.<Fundamentals of Photonics and Quantum Electronics lecture notes by Franz X. Kärtner>[§2.8.3, printed pp. 157–158, equations 2.414–2.419: Poynting direction and walk-off]{ref}

In the lossless, nondispersive model above, take the <gradient> of $\omega=c\sqrt{k_x^2/n_e^2+k_z^2/n_o^2}$ to obtain the <group velocity>, parallel to the <Poynting vector>. If its <angle> to the <optic axis> is $\phi$, this gives $\tan\phi=(n_o^2/n_e^2)\tan\theta$. Consequently the <extraordinary wave> generally carries <energy> away from its <wavevector> direction. An internal <wavevector> normal to a slab face therefore does not guarantee a normal energy path. <Birefringent walk-off> vanishes at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi/2$; the latter direction still has unequal <refractive indices>. This is a useful <waveplate> geometry: overlapping components can acquire <phase retardation> without spatial splitting. A thin specimen, insufficient imaging resolution, or excitation of only one <linear polarization> can also hide a double image, for different reasons.

= Electric-displacement angle from birefringent walk-off
{parent=Birefringent walk-off}
{title2=$|\angle(\mathbf E,\mathbf D)|=|\angle(\mathbf S,\mathbf k)|$}

For a lossless nonmagnetic <uniaxial optical medium>, the <electric displacement field> of a <plane electromagnetic wave> is perpendicular to its <wavevector>. The magnetic field is perpendicular to the plane containing the <electric field> and <wavevector>, while the <Poynting vector> is perpendicular to the electric field in that same plane. Thus the angle between $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf D$ equals the walk-off angle between energy flow and phase propagation. At <normal incidence> on a parallel slab of thickness $L$, a lateral separation $\Delta$ of the ordinary and extraordinary exiting rays gives $\alpha_e=\arctan(|\Delta|/L)$, while $\alpha_o=0$. The output rays can be parallel even though their internal energy paths differ.

= Wave plate
{synonym}

A <waveplate> uses <birefringence> to change <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> through <phase retardation>. Its fast axis has the smaller <refractive index>, hence the larger <phase velocity>; its slow axis has the larger index. Consider normal incidence with the <optic axis> in the plate face, so both components follow the same straight path. A <linear polarization> at $45^\circ$ to these axes excites equal <wave amplitudes>.

A complementary experiment uses two sheet <polarizers> with a cellophane sheet between them. First cross the <polarizers> and remove the sheet: the field is dark. Insert and rotate the sheet about the beam: light returns at intermediate orientations, but extinction recurs at two perpendicular orientations. The sheet has changed the <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>, without requiring a resolved double image. With white <light>, different <wavelengths> can produce different transmitted colors.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others>[I §33–3, Fig. 33–3 and the paragraphs describing removal, rotation and white-light colors of cellophane]{ref}. Brightness alone does not establish <circular polarization>: an ideal rotating linear <polarizer> also gives orientation-independent <light intensity> for <unpolarized light>.

= Conditional path probabilities after a quarter-wave plate
{parent=Waveplate}

A detected lower-path <photon> with vertical <polarization> evolves under an angle-dependent <Jones matrix>. For fast-axis angle $\eta$, its horizontal <amplitude> is $(1-i)\sin\eta\cos\eta$ and its vertical <amplitude> is $\sin^2\eta+i\cos^2\eta$. A later vertical-path <probability> therefore depends on the plate orientation and on whether the earlier lower-path event has been conditioned on.

= Retardance
{parent=Waveplate}
{title2=$\Delta$}

The relative phase accumulated by orthogonal eigenpolarizations is $\Delta=2\pi L(n_2-n_1)/\lambda_0$. A <quarter-wave plate> has relative phase $\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$, while a <half-wave plate> has $\pi$ modulo $2\pi$.

= Phase retardation
{parent=Waveplate}
{title2=$\delta=2\pi\Delta n\,d/\lambda_0$}

For the overlapping <monochromatic wave> components, let $d$ be the thickness and $\lambda_0$ the vacuum <wavelength>. Define $\Delta n=n_s-n_f>0$ using the slow and fast <refractive indices>. With <complex amplitude> convention $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, the accumulated slow-minus-fast <phase difference> is
$$
\delta=(k_s-k_f)d=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}(n_s-n_f)d.
$$
This is the <optical path difference> $\Delta n\,d$ expressed as a <wave phase>. It is a signed accumulation convention; with the opposite time convention the sign reverses. The outgoing <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> depends on $\delta$ modulo $2\pi$. Here the positive slow-minus-fast $\Delta n$ differs from the signed principal-index $n_e-n_o$ used in the parent <birefringence> entry when $n_e<n_o$.

For an original worked example, stipulate $\Delta n=0.0100$ at $\lambda_0=600\,\mathrm{nm}$ and a uniform thickness $d=15.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. These are design assumptions, not measured material data. The <optical path difference> is $150\,\mathrm{nm}$, so
$$
\delta=2\pi\frac{150\,\mathrm{nm}}{600\,\mathrm{nm}}=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$
It is a <quarter-wave plate>. At the same <wavelength> and <refractive indices>, doubling $d$ to $30.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ gives $\delta=\pi$, a <half-wave plate>. A $1.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thickness increase changes $\delta$ by $\pi/30$, or $6^\circ$; retaining the same <birefringence> at $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ would instead give $\delta=3\pi/5$ for the $15.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ plate. Real <refractive indices> also vary with <wavelength>, so this last calculation explicitly neglects <optical dispersion>.

= Waveplate retardance detuning
{parent=Phase retardation}
{title2=$\epsilon\simeq(d/c)(\Delta n_0+\omega_0\Delta n'_0)\Delta\omega$}

A zero-order <quarter-wave plate> satisfies $\omega_0d\Delta n(\omega_0)/c=\pi/2$. Its <retardance> error at nearby <angular frequency> is $\epsilon=\omega d\Delta n(\omega)/c-\pi/2$. Taylor expansion gives the title expression, including material dispersion. Neglecting that dispersion yields $\epsilon=(\pi/2)\Delta\omega/\omega_0$. A <half-wave plate> made from twice the same material thickness has exactly twice this <phase> error. Common propagation <phase> can be removed from its <Jones matrix> without changing <polarization>.

= Quarter-wave plate
{parent=Waveplate}
{title2=$\delta=\pm\pi/2\pmod{2\pi}$}

A <quarter-wave plate> gives a quarter-cycle <phase retardation>. Equal transmitted <wave amplitudes> initially in <linear polarization> at $45^\circ$ become <circular polarization>; unequal <wave amplitudes> generally give <elliptical polarization>. Reversing the sign reverses the traversal of the same circle.

= Half-wave and quarter-wave achromatic circularizer
{parent=Quarter-wave plate}
{title2=$\mathbf J(\epsilon)=[1+i(3-\sqrt3)\epsilon/2]\mathbf J(0)+O(\epsilon^2)$}

Take a <half-wave plate> with fast axis at $15^\circ$ to a linearly polarized input, followed by a <quarter-wave plate> at a further $60^\circ$, so the second axis is at $75^\circ$ to the input. With shared quarter-wave <retardance> error $\epsilon$ and half-wave error $2\epsilon$, use $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,ie^{i\epsilon})$ and $H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-e^{2i\epsilon})$. Rotating each matrix into the input axes and multiplying gives $\mathbf J(0)=e^{i75^\circ}(1,-i)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2$ and the title expansion. Its first-order change is common <phase> only, so the output remains circular to first order. This input-specific local compensation does not prove a frequency-independent quarter-wave operator for arbitrary input across a finite spectral band.

= Two-element polarization identification
{parent=Quarter-wave plate}

Suppose the unknown ideal elements are a linear <polarizer> and a <quarter-wave plate>, and the two unknown sources are unpolarized and circularly polarized. Test both element orders while rotating their relative axes and monitoring output <intensity>. Only circular input followed by the <quarter-wave plate> and then the <polarizer> gives complete modulation: the plate makes linear <light> and the analyzer obeys <Malus's law>. Polarizer-first output is constant half-intensity because the following lossless plate changes polarization but not <intensity>. Unpolarized input is constant in either order. This identifies both elements and both sources without comparing source brightnesses.

= Pure-polarization preparation with two quarter-wave plates
{parent=Quarter-wave plate}

Two rotatable ideal <quarter-wave plates> can transform a fixed <circular polarization> into any <pure polarization> represented by a <Jones vector>. Use time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, $Q(\theta)=R(-\theta)\operatorname{diag}(1,i)R(\theta)$ and input $J_L=(1,i)^T/\sqrt2$. Every target <polarization ellipse> can be written, up to a common phase, as $R(-\beta)(\cos\eta,i\sin\eta)^T$, with signed ellipticity angle $-\pi/4\leq\eta\leq\pi/4$. The first <quarter-wave plate> at $\theta_1=\beta+\eta+\pi/4$ produces <linear polarization> at $\beta+\eta$. The second, at $\theta_2=\beta$, therefore produces the required target. The two angles supply the two independent parameters of pure <polarization>.

= Double-pass quarter-wave plate
{parent=Quarter-wave plate}

For a reciprocal ideal <quarter-wave plate> followed by a mirror at <normal incidence>, use the same fixed transverse laboratory axes for the forward and returning <Jones vectors>. If the axes coincide with the plate's fast and slow axes, each pass has <Jones matrix> $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$ up to a common <phase factor>. A polarization-independent mirror has scalar reflection coefficient $r_m$, so the returned <Jones vector> is $r_mJ^2\mathbf e=r_m\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\mathbf e$. The plate acts twice as a <half-wave plate>, rather than undoing its <phase retardation>.

For input <linear polarization> at <angle> $\alpha$ to the fast axis, the returned line is at $-\alpha$ modulo $\pi$. At $\alpha=45^\circ$ it is perpendicular to the original line. An axis-aligned input instead returns along the same line. General <elliptical polarization> requires its full input <Jones vector>; no unique returned state follows from the word “polarized” alone. The propagation direction reverses, so comparing descriptions of <circular polarization> must also specify the viewing convention.

= Mirror return through an ordered polarizer-retarder pair
{parent=Double-pass quarter-wave plate}

Take a linear <polarizer> $P=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and a reciprocal <quarter-wave plate> $Q=2^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}$ in fixed physical transverse axes. A near-normal isotropic mirror has $M=-I_2$ up to global <phase>. If the source/detector side encounters P before Q, the round-trip matrix is $PQM QP=-PQ^2P=0$. Reversing the filter gives $QPM P Q=-QPQ$, which transmits half an initially unpolarized <intensity>. The result is double-pass rejection, not optical nonreciprocity; changing direction without changing the input polarization does not preserve the same physical input state.

A <half-wave plate> reverses the relative sign of the two components. For initial <linear polarization> at <angle> $\alpha$ to the fast axis, the outgoing line is at $-\alpha$ modulo $\pi$. The $45^\circ$ input therefore rotates by $90^\circ$.

<Circular birefringence> means different <refractive indices> for opposite <circular polarizations>. In a transparent isotropic medium, their relative <wave phase> changes rotate the direction of transmitted <linear polarization>; <optical activity> gives the solution measurement and convention.

The <rotatory power> is the rotation of transmitted <linear polarization> per unit path length, in $\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}$. = Displacement current
{title2=$\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$}
{parent=Ampère's circuital law}
{wiki}

In vacuum, <displacement current> is the term $\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$ in the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>. It has the units of <current density>, but need not describe <electric charge> crossing the region. In <Maxwell equations in matter>, the corresponding term is $\partial_t\mathbf D$, with $\mathbf D$ the <electric displacement field>.

Consider a slowly charging <parallel-plate capacitor> with vacuum gap, plate area $A$, and charge $Q(t)$ on its positive plate. Neglect fringing and assume the charging timescale is long compared with the light-travel time across the apparatus. The nearly uniform gap <electric field> has magnitude $E=Q/(\epsilon_0A)$, so the <displacement current> through the gap is
$$
I_D=\epsilon_0 A\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{dQ}{dt}=I.
$$
For $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and charging <electric current> $I=1.0\,\mathrm{mA}$, this gives $dE/dt\simeq1.13\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ and $J_D=0.10\,\mathrm{A/m^2}$. No conducting <electric current> crosses the gap. A spanning surface cutting the wire and another passing between the plates nevertheless give the same magnetic circulation when the full <Ampère-Maxwell equation> is used. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_18.html#Ch18-S2[Feynman lectures, II.18.2: charging a capacitor]{ref}.

The local consistency check is short. Without <displacement current>, taking the <divergence> of $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$ would force $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$, allowing no local accumulation of <electric charge>. With <displacement current>, the <divergence of a curl is zero> and <Gauss's law> give
$$
0=\mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\partial_t\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\right)
=\mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho\right).
$$
This is precisely the <charge continuity equation>. <Electric charge conservation> fixes the required <divergence> of the added term; it does not by itself exclude other divergence-free additions. The specific <Maxwell equations> also yield the <electromagnetic wave equation>, linking this consistency correction to propagation at the <speed of light>.

In vacuum, $\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$. It is not transport of free charges through the vacuum gap of a <capacitor>; its <surface integral> nevertheless supplies the term needed for compatibility with the <continuity equation>.

= Magnetic field between circular capacitor plates
{parent=Displacement current}
{title2=$\widetilde B_\phi(s)=-i\omega\mu_0\epsilon_0V_0s/(2d)$}

For a circular air-filled <parallel-plate capacitor> with voltage phasor $V_0e^{-i\omega t}$, uniform gap $d$ and radius $a$, the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> on a circular loop of radius $s<a$ gives the displayed <magnetic field>. The enclosed <displacement current> is proportional to $s^2$, whereas the circulation is $2\pi sB_\phi$. Consequently its amplitude grows linearly from zero on the axis. This assumes negligible <fringing fields> and a <quasistatic approximation> $\omega a/c\ll1$.

= Axisymmetric high-frequency capacitor field
{parent=Displacement current}
{title2=$E_z(r)\propto J_0(\omega r/c)$}

If a thin <capacitor> interior is modeled with axially uniform axisymmetric fields, Maxwell's equations give $r^{-1}(rE_z')'+(\omega/c)^2E_z=0$. The solution regular at the axis is proportional to $J_0(\omega r/c)$. Normalizing to a central sinusoidal <voltage> gives $E_z=-(V_0/L)J_0(\omega r/c)\sin\omega t$ in this ideal interior model. = Conduction-to-displacement current ratio
{parent=Displacement current}

For a uniform ohmic material under harmonic forcing, the magnitudes obey $|J_{\rm cond}|/|J_{\rm disp}|=\sigma/(\omega\epsilon)$. A large ratio supports a local conduction-dominated approximation; it does not remove the displacement term from Maxwell's exact continuity relation.

= Faraday's law of induction

= Faraday law
{c}
{synonym}

<Faraday's law of induction> relates a changing <magnetic field> to circulation of the <electric field>. For a fixed <oriented surface> $S$ with boundary $C=\partial S$, choose the positive direction around $C$ by the <right-hand rule>: the fingers follow $C$ while the thumb follows the chosen <unit normal>. The fixed-loop electromagnetic <electromotive force> is
$$
\mathcal E=\oint_C\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S
=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.
$$
The final <derivative> of <magnetic flux> is an ordinary time derivative, with the <surface> held fixed. Reversing both the <unit normal> and the boundary direction reverses both signs. The loop may be an imagined path: the field law does not require a wire or an <electric current>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_17.html#Ch17-S1[Feynman lectures, II.17.1: electric-field circulation and induction]{ref}.

For a computed example, a fixed single-turn loop has <area> $A=0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and out-of-page <unit normal>. Prescribe an external uniform <magnetic field> in that direction with $B(t)=0.10\,\mathrm T+(0.30\,\mathrm{T/s})t$ for $0\le t\le1.0\,\mathrm s$. Then
$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)=AB(t)
=0.0020\,\mathrm{Wb}+(0.0060\,\mathrm{Wb/s})t,
\qquad
\mathcal E_{\mathrm{CCW}}=-0.0060\,\mathrm V.
$$
In a quasistatic resistive model with <electrical resistance> $R=2.0\,\Omega$, neglect the loop's <self-inductance> and its own contribution to changing <magnetic flux>. <Ohm's law> gives
$$
I_{\mathrm{CCW}}=\frac{\mathcal E_{\mathrm{CCW}}}{R}=-0.0030\,\mathrm A.
$$
The <magnetic flux> rises from $0.0020\,\mathrm{Wb}$ to $0.0080\,\mathrm{Wb}$ while the induced <electromotive force> stays constant: it measures the rate of change, rather than the amount of <magnetic flux>. The negative sign means clockwise <electric current>; its <magnetic field> through the loop points into the page, as explained by <Lenz's law>. The toy model describes the interval of steady ramping, excluding switching transients at its ends.

For a moving conducting loop $C(t)$, the electromagnetic <electromotive force> instead includes the <Lorentz force> contribution from the local <velocity> $\mathbf v$ of each conductor segment:
$$
\mathcal E(t)=\oint_{C(t)}(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l
=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$
This form assumes a thin-wire model with a well-defined closed contour and spanning <oriented surface> that move compatibly, a <magnetic field> satisfying <Gauss's law for magnetism>, and conductor speeds small compared with the <speed of light>. Ideal sliding contacts such as those in <motional electromotive force> can be treated piecewise. Arbitrary changes of electrical connections require checking the actual contour and conductor velocities; differentiating the flux through an arbitrary chosen moving path is insufficient. The <velocity> here describes conductor motion, not the charge carriers' drift along the wire. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_17.html#Ch17-S2[Feynman lectures, II.17.2: limits of the flux rule]{ref}.

A short derivation of the fixed-loop integral statement applies <Stokes theorem> to the local <curl> equation and differentiates under the fixed <surface integral>. For the moving loop, the <magnetic flux transport for a moving circuit> adds the swept-area term $-\oint_C(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l$ to $d\Phi_B/dt$. Combining these two identities gives the moving-loop statement. This derives the integral forms from the local field law; it is not a proof of that empirical law from mathematics alone.

The useful unity is geometric: changing the <magnetic field>, the enclosed <area>, or the orientation can change <magnetic flux> and hence produce an <electromotive force>. A coil's multiple turns are accounted for by <magnetic flux linkage>, rather than by multiplying a single-turn flux without checking which flux each turn receives. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/13-1-faradays-law[OpenStax University Physics 2, §13.1: flux changes and coils]{ref}.

Faraday's report read on 24 November 1831 describes opposite momentary responses when a neighboring circuit is connected and disconnected, with no continuing response while its steady <electric current> is maintained. These are concrete experimental roots of the changing-flux idea. The report predates the modern <Maxwell equations> notation used above. <Experimental Researches in Electricity, Volume 1 by Michael Faraday>[Faraday, Experimental Researches in Electricity, First Series (1831), §§10–11, reprinted in volume I]{ref}.

= Induced current in a tilted resistive loop
{parent=Faraday's law of induction}
{title2=$I(t)=\pi r^2B_0\omega\cos\theta\sin(\omega t)/R$}

Choose the positive loop normal and its positive circulation by the <right-hand rule>. A spatially uniform external <magnetic field> of fixed direction and signed magnitude $B_0\cos\omega t$, at angle $\theta$ to that normal, gives <magnetic flux> $\Phi=\pi r^2B_0\cos\theta\cos\omega t$. Neglecting the loop's <self-inductance>, <Faraday's law of induction> and <Ohm's law> give the displayed <electric current>. At a quarter-cycle the external field is zero but its normal component is decreasing most rapidly, so the current produces a field in the direction of the initial normal flux, as required by <Lenz's law>. With appreciable self-inductance the circuit instead obeys $L\dot I+RI=-\dot\Phi_{\rm ext}$.

<Michael Faraday>'s induction ring separates two questions: can one <electrical circuit> affect another without an electrical connection, and does that effect continue under steady conditions? The contrast between a switching response and a quiet steady state makes <mutual induction> tangible. A steady primary <electric current> can maintain <magnetic flux> without sustaining a secondary <electromotive force>. Changes in <magnetic flux linkage> matter, as expressed by <Faraday's law of induction>. This is why a driven <electrical transformer> uses repeated changes in <magnetic flux> to supply secondary <power>.

The <galvanometer readout of an induction pulse> also separates the measured motion from the inferred <electric current>. Its <moment of inertia> and <damping> mean that a deflection is not an instantaneous trace of <electric current>. The following <coupled RL induction model> calculates currents in a stipulated circuit, rather than fitting Faraday's needle motion or reconstructing his switching times.

= Coupled RL induction model
{parent=Faraday induction-ring experiment}
{title2=$\mathbf L\dot{\mathbf i}+\mathbf R\mathbf i=\mathbf v$}

Take two <electrical circuits> containing <coupled inductors>. Let their constant <self-inductances> be $L_1,L_2>0$, <mutual inductance> $M$, and positive <electrical resistances> $R_1,R_2$. Choose current directions so that the off-diagonal entries of the <inductance matrix> are $+M$. Assume a linear, quasistatic model, with no capacitive effects or core <hysteresis>. The <magnetic flux linkages> are $\lambda_1=L_1i_1+Mi_2$ and $\lambda_2=Mi_1+L_2i_2$. Combining their time derivatives with resistive <electric potential differences> gives
$$
L_1\dot i_1+M\dot i_2+R_1i_1=V(t),\qquad
M\dot i_1+L_2\dot i_2+R_2i_2=0.
$$
The secondary is a closed, undriven <electrical circuit>. Require $M^2<L_1L_2$, making $\mathbf L$ a <positive-definite matrix>. The chosen current convention fixes the signs; reversing the secondary's reference direction changes the sign assigned to $M$ and $i_2$ without changing the physical circuit.

There is an essential switching assumption: **the primary loop remains closed when $V$ becomes zero**. An ideal <voltage source> then imposes zero <electric potential difference>, allowing the current to decay. Literally opening an inductive circuit requires additional physics to determine the interruption, including possible <capacitance> and an <electric arc>. This closed-return model explains transient induction while keeping that distinction from Faraday's historical making and breaking of contact explicit.

= Equal-loop RL induction step
{parent=Coupled RL induction model}
{title2=$\tau_\pm=(L\pm M)/R$}

For an exactly soluble example, set $L_1=L_2=L$, $R_1=R_2=R$, and $0<M<L$. Start with zero <electric currents>, and apply a constant <electric potential difference> $V_0>0$ at $t=0$. The sums $s=i_1+i_2$ and $d=i_1-i_2$ obey separate <first-order linear differential equations>:
$$
(L+M)\dot s+Rs=V_0,\qquad
(L-M)\dot d+Rd=V_0.
$$
Their <time constants> are $\tau_+=(L+M)/R$ and $\tau_-=(L-M)/R$. Thus, for $t\geq0$,
$$
i_1(t)=\frac{V_0}{2R}\left(2-e^{-t/\tau_+}-e^{-t/\tau_-}\right),\qquad
i_2(t)=\frac{V_0}{2R}\left(e^{-t/\tau_-}-e^{-t/\tau_+}\right).
$$
Since $\tau_-<\tau_+$, **$i_2(t)<0$ for $t>0$, but $i_2(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$**. The primary approaches $V_0/R$. The secondary response opposes the rise of linked <magnetic flux>, in accordance with <Lenz's law>. Removing <mutual inductance> by setting $M=0$ makes the two exponentials equal and gives zero secondary <electric current>.

For stipulated $L=0.1\,\mathrm H$, $M=0.06\,\mathrm H$, $R=10\,\Omega$ and $V_0=1\,\mathrm V$, the two <time constants> are $16\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $4\,\mathrm{ms}$. Setting $\dot i_2=0$ gives its largest negative magnitude at
$$
t_* =\frac{\tau_+\tau_-}{\tau_+-\tau_-}\log\frac{\tau_+}{\tau_-}
\simeq7.394\,\mathrm{ms},\qquad
i_2(t_*)\simeq-23.62\,\mathrm{mA}.
$$
The continuing primary <electric current> eventually reaches $100\,\mathrm{mA}$ while the secondary returns to zero. These numbers belong to the stated model, not the 1831 apparatus.

Apply $V_0$ for $0\leq t<T$ and then set the drive to zero while retaining the closed primary return. By <superposition>, each pulse-response <electric current> is its <equal-loop RL induction step> response at $t$ minus that response at $t-T$, with the step response defined as zero before its onset. All currents remain continuous at $T$; the drive changes their <derivatives>.

= Energy balance of coupled RL circuits
{parent=Coupled RL induction model}
{title2=$\dot W=Vi_1-R_1i_1^2-R_2i_2^2$}

The <magnetic energy> of this constant-<inductance> model is
$$
W=\frac12L_1i_1^2+Mi_1i_2+\frac12L_2i_2^2

The <positive-definite matrix> condition makes $W\geq0$. Multiplying the two circuit equations by $i_1$ and $i_2$, then adding, gives the displayed <conservation of energy> statement: input <power> $Vi_1$ supplies stored <magnetic energy> and <Joule heating>. = Lenz's law
{parent=Faraday's law of induction}
{c}
{wiki}

<Lenz's law> expresses the minus sign in <Faraday's law of induction>: the induced <electromotive force> drives a passive resistive loop's <electric current> in the direction whose own <magnetic field> opposes the imposed change in <magnetic flux>. **It opposes the change of flux, not invariably the external magnetic field.** Choose an <oriented surface> and its positive boundary direction first, find the sign of $d\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}/dt$, and then use the <right-hand rule> to translate the signed <electric current> into a physical direction. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/13-2-lenzs-law[OpenStax University Physics 2, §13.2: current direction and changing flux]{ref}.

In the fixed-loop example in <Faraday's law of induction>, out-of-page <magnetic flux> increases at $0.0060\,\mathrm{Wb/s}$. With counterclockwise positive, $I=-0.0030\,\mathrm A$ is clockwise, producing an in-page <magnetic field> through the loop. Reverse the ramp to $B(t)=0.40\,\mathrm T-(0.30\,\mathrm{T/s})t$ on the same one-second interval: now $\mathcal E=+0.0060\,\mathrm V$ and $I=+0.0030\,\mathrm A$. The counterclockwise <electric current> produces an out-of-page <magnetic field>, supporting the decreasing external <magnetic flux>. A steady out-of-page <magnetic field> with fixed loop geometry gives no induced <electromotive force> in either direction.

For the increasing-area example in <motional electromotive force>, clockwise <electric current> runs down the right-hand rod. Its <magnetic force on a current-carrying wire> points left, opposing the imposed motion to the right. Writing $I_{\mathrm{mag}}=B\ell v/R$ for the current magnitude, the <power> required to maintain constant <speed> is
$$
P_{\mathrm{drive}}=(I_{\mathrm{mag}}\ell B)v
=\frac{(B\ell v)^2}{R}=I_{\mathrm{mag}}^2R=P_{\mathrm{Joule}}.
$$
Thus the sign agrees with <conservation of energy>: the external drive supplies the <Joule heating>. The <magnetic forces do no work> on individual charges; mechanical <work> by the drive supplies the electrical <energy>. This also explains the usefulness of <electromagnetic braking of a sliding rod> and the <terminal speed of a falling conducting loop>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/13-3-motional-emf[OpenStax University Physics 2, §13.3: mechanical power and resistive heating]{ref}.

The sign of induced <electromotive force> alone does not determine an arbitrary circuit's instantaneous <electric current>. Retaining constant <self-inductance> $L$ gives $L\,dI/dt+RI=-d\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}/dt$, so a pre-existing <electric current> can persist while the induced drive opposes its change. The distinction matters in the <lossless limit of magnetic loop braking>. The resistive toy examples use $L=0$ as an approximation, not a general consequence of <Lenz's law>.

= Terminal speed of a falling conducting loop
{parent=Lenz's law}

If a loop of resistance $R$ has magnetic flux $\Phi_B=-kz$, then its induced current is $I=k\dot z/R$. When its magnetic force is $-kI$ in the vertical direction, its equation under gravity is
$$
m\ddot z=-mg-\frac{k^2}{R}\dot z,
$$
so its downward terminal speed is $Rmg/k^2$.

= Lossless limit of magnetic loop braking
{parent=Terminal speed of a falling conducting loop}
{title2=$\mathcal L I+Kz=\text{constant}$}

For an external loop flux $Kz$, positive current orientation and upward <Lorentz force> $KI$, including <self-inductance> gives $\mathcal L\dot I+RI=-K\dot z$ and $m\ddot z=-mg+KI$. At zero resistance total linked flux is conserved. If initially $I=0$ at $z=z_0$, then $I=-K(z-z_0)/\mathcal L$ and the mechanical motion is an undamped oscillator about $z_0-mg\mathcal L/K^2$. The finite-resistance terminal speed tends to zero as $R\to0$, but neglecting <self-inductance> is a singular approximation in that limit. A lossless loop does not dispose of <energy> through <Joule heating>.

= Gauss's law
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0$}

= Gauss law
{c}
{synonym}

<Gauss's law> relates the <electric field> on a <closed surface> to the net <electric charge> inside it. In <SI> units, with outward <unit normal> $\mathbf n$,
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}=\int_V\rho\,dV.}
$$
Here $\rho$ describes a volume <charge density>, and $\varepsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>; for ideal point or surface charges, use the enclosed <electric charge> directly rather than an ordinary volume-density integral. The left side is <electric flux>: the <surface integral> of the outward normal component of the <electric field>. Field directed outward contributes positively; field directed inward contributes negatively. The equality uses net signed <electric charge>, so positive and negative enclosed charges can cancel. The <Gaussian surface> is an imagined accounting boundary, not necessarily a material object. See https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/6-2-explaining-gausss-law[OpenStax, explaining Gauss's law].

Where the <electric field> and <charge density> are smooth, the local statement is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\varepsilon_0$. The <divergence theorem> connects this <divergence> to the <surface integral>. This is the satisfying connection between local sources and global accounting: a surface can register how much net <electric charge> it encloses without revealing where that charge is located within it. A <point charge> requires care with the singular field at its position; the integral formulation remains useful with a boundary that avoids the charge.

The law fixes total <electric flux>, rather than every value of the <electric field>. In an uncharged region, a uniform <electric field> $\mathbf E=(E_0,0,0)$ crosses a cube of side $\ell$. Its left and right faces contribute $-E_0\ell^2$ and $+E_0\ell^2$; the other four faces contribute zero. Thus **the net electric flux is zero while the electric field is nonzero everywhere**. Charges outside a <Gaussian surface> can change the field on it without changing its net <electric flux>. Conversely, fields cannot generally be reconstructed from enclosed <electric charge> alone. <Electrostatics> also requires the <electric field> to be the negative <gradient> of an <electric potential>, with appropriate boundary information. This distinction is emphasized in https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_05.html[Feynman lectures II.5, application of Gauss' law].

<Spherical symmetry> supplies the extra information needed for a particularly simple calculation. An isolated <spherically symmetric> charge distribution in vacuum produces a radial <electric field>, constant in magnitude on each concentric <sphere>. Its <electric flux> is therefore $4\pi r^2E_r(r)$, giving
$$
E_r(r)=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}(r)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}.
$$
Outside the distribution, doubling $r$ quadruples the <surface area> and divides the radial <electric field> by four, while the <electric flux> stays $Q/\varepsilon_0$. The inverse-square dependence is an area-dilution result. The <electric field of a uniformly charged ball> and <electric field of a uniformly charged spherical shell> show that the same total charge can give different interior fields. These symmetry arguments and their limits are developed in https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/6-3-applying-gausss-law[OpenStax, applying Gauss's law].

= Negative sheet on a positive volume-charged slab
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$E_z=[\rho(2z+d)+\sigma]/(2\epsilon_0)$}

Place a fixed sheet of charge density $-\sigma$ at $z=0$ and a uniform fixed volume charge $\rho$ in $-d<z<0$. With no imposed uniform background field, the above-sheet and below-slab fields are $\pm(\rho d-\sigma)/(2\epsilon_0)$. The interior field is the title expression, obtained by adding the slab's linear field to the sheet's constant field. If $0<\sigma<\rho d$, it vanishes at $z=-(d+\sigma/\rho)/2$, inside the lower half of the slab. At the sheet it jumps down by $\sigma/\epsilon_0$, while it remains continuous at the slab's uncharged lower face.

= Cube flux from a corner charge
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$\Phi_{\rm opposite}=q/(24\epsilon_0)$}

For a <point charge> exactly at a cube corner, each of the three opposite faces has outward <electric flux> $q/(24\epsilon_0)$. Reflecting the cube in its three incident faces makes a larger cube centred on the charge and proves the result by <symmetry>. The three incident faces have zero normal <electric field> away from their singular corner. Their improper integrals vanish; the total is $q/(8\epsilon_0)$. This boundary convention differs from moving a charge to the corner from the interior after computing a closed-surface integral.

= Electric field of a uniformly charged infinite cylinder
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$\mathbf E_{\rm in}=\rho\mathbf r/(2\epsilon_0)$}

For an infinite insulating cylinder of <radius> $R$ and uniform fixed <charge density> $\rho$, <Gauss's law> gives interior <electric field> $\rho\mathbf r/(2\epsilon_0)$ and exterior <electric field> $\rho R^2\mathbf r/(2\epsilon_0|\mathbf r|^2)$, where $\mathbf r$ is transverse displacement from its axis. The <electric field> is continuous at $|\mathbf r|=R$ when there is no separate <surface charge density>. The interior linear dependence makes displaced-cylinder <superposition> particularly simple.

= Uniform electric field in an off-axis cylindrical cavity
{parent=Electric field of a uniformly charged infinite cylinder}
{title2=$\mathbf E=\rho\mathbf a/(2\epsilon_0)$}

Remove an axial cylindrical hole, wholly contained in an infinite insulating cylinder with uniform fixed <charge density> $\rho$. Let $\mathbf a$ point from the original axis to the hole axis. The <superposition principle> replaces the removed material by a cylinder of <charge density> $-\rho$. At any point $\mathbf r$ inside the hole, the two interior <electric fields> add to $\rho[\mathbf r-(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)]/(2\epsilon_0)=\rho\mathbf a/(2\epsilon_0)$. The result is uniform throughout the hole, even when the hole is not concentric; it does not apply to a hole in an <electrical conductor> whose <electric charges> can redistribute.

= Electrostatic shell theorem
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$\mathbf E(r)=Q\mathbf e_r/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$}

Outside a bounded <spherically symmetric charge distribution> in vacuum, the <electric field> is that of a central <point charge> with the same total <electric charge>. With zero <electric potential> at infinity, the exterior <electric potential> is $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$. A uniform <surface charge> on a <sphere> has zero <electric field> inside.

= Net charge from the asymptotic radial electric field
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$Q=4\pi\epsilon_0\lim_{r\to\infty}r^2E_r(r)$}

For a spherically symmetric <electric field> in <vacuum>, integrate <Gauss's law> over a sphere. Its <electric flux> is $4\pi r^2E_r(r)$, so taking the enclosing <radius> to infinity gives the displayed total charge, whenever that limit and total charge exist. In particular, a radial field decaying faster than $r^{-2}$ can be nonzero locally while its total source charge is zero. The origin must also be checked: a point charge contributes a nonzero limiting flux as $r\to0$.

= Electric field for a radial power-law charge density
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$\mathbf E(r)=\frac{Ar^{p+1}}{\epsilon_0(p+3)}\widehat{\mathbf r}$}

Let the spherically symmetric <volume> <charge density> be $\rho(r)=Ar^p$, with $p>-3$ so the origin contains finite charge. Integration gives $Q(r)=4\pi Ar^{p+3}/(p+3)$. <Gauss's law> yields the radial <electric field> $\mathbf E=Ar^{p+1}\widehat{\mathbf r}/[\epsilon_0(p+3)]$ for $r>0$. The field tends to zero at the centre if $p>-1$; finite enclosed charge alone does not guarantee a regular central field.

= Electric field of a uniformly charged ball
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$E_r(r)=Qr/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)\quad(0\leq r\leq R)$}

= Electric field of a uniformly charged solid sphere
{synonym}

Distribute a positive <electric charge> $Q$ uniformly through a <closed ball> of radius $R$ in vacuum, with no external field. Its volume <charge density> is $3Q/(4\pi R^3)$. For $0<r<R$, the enclosed fraction is $(r/R)^3$. Applying <Gauss's law> on a concentric <Gaussian surface> gives
$$
E_r(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{Qr}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3},&0\leq r\leq R,\\[4pt]\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2},&r\geq R.\end{cases}
$$
The <electric field> grows linearly inside and decays as an inverse square outside. With $E_R=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$, its values at $r/R=1/2,1,2$ are $E_R/2,E_R,E_R/4$. The field vanishes at the centre by <spherical symmetry> and is continuous at the surface because this ideal volume distribution has no singular surface layer. Its interior behavior differs from a <conductor in electrostatic equilibrium>, where mobile charge redistributes.

= Electric field of a uniformly charged spherical shell
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$E_r(r)=0\ (r<R),\quad E_r(r)=Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)\ (r>R)$}

Place the same positive <electric charge> $Q$ uniformly on the <sphere> of radius $R$, with no external field. Every concentric interior <Gaussian surface> encloses zero charge; <spherical symmetry> then makes the interior <electric field> zero. Outside, it is again $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$. At $R$, the inner and outer limits differ by $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)=\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, with <surface charge density> $\sigma=Q/(4\pi R^2)$. The ideal surface charge makes the field discontinuous, so the plot displays one-sided limits rather than assigning a single field value on the charged sheet. An isolated charged conducting sphere in <electrostatic equilibrium> realizes this exterior/interior pattern.

= Hyperbolic-space Coulomb field
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$E(\xi)=Q/[4\pi\epsilon_0R^2\sinh^2(\xi/R)]$}

In static time times round three-dimensional <hyperbolic space> of curvature radius $R$, a geodesic sphere has area $4\pi R^2\sinh^2(\xi/R)$. The <Gauss law> therefore gives the displayed radial field. It approaches the flat-space inverse-square field near the charge and falls exponentially at large geodesic distance. The curvature changes the area of a sphere, not the conserved flux.

= Force between hemispheres of a uniformly charged ball
{parent=Gauss's law}
{title2=$F=3Q^2/(64\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$}

For uniform <charge density> $\rho_e=3Q/(4\pi R^3)$, <Gauss's law> gives the interior <electric field> $E=\rho_e r/(3\varepsilon_0)$. Integrate its vertical force density $\rho_e^2z/(3\varepsilon_0)$ over the upper hemisphere: $\int z\,dV=\pi R^4/4$. Internal pair forces in that hemisphere cancel, so its net force is exactly the force from the lower hemisphere. The result is upward and has magnitude $3Q^2/(64\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$.

= Gauss surface
{c}
{synonym}

A <Gaussian surface> is a <closed surface> used with the integral form of <Gauss's law> to relate <electric flux> to enclosed <electric charge>. It need not coincide with the surface of any charged body. Choosing it to match a <symmetry> can make the normal component of the <electric field> constant on a portion of it, allowing that component to be taken outside a <surface integral>. A concentric <sphere> works for an isolated <spherically symmetric> charge distribution; an arbitrarily placed <sphere> generally does not.

Zero enclosed <electric charge> implies zero total <electric flux>. Inferring zero <electric field> additionally needs information such as the <spherical symmetry> used in the <electric field of a uniformly charged spherical shell>. The imagined boundary does not itself block or redirect the <electric field>.

= Gaussian pillbox
{c}
{parent=Gauss's law}

A Gaussian pillbox is a thin cylindrical <Gaussian surface> crossing an interface. Shrinking its height converts a divergence law into a jump condition for the field's normal component.

= Charge conservation from Maxwell equations
{parent=Maxwell equations}

Taking the divergence of the Ampère-Maxwell equation and using Gauss's law gives
$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot J=0.
$$
Hence the charge in a fixed volume changes only through current crossing its boundary.

= Charge continuity equation
{parent=Charge conservation from Maxwell equations}
{title2=$\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$}

= Continuity equation for electric charge
{synonym}

The local form of <conservation of electric charge> relates <charge density> to <current density>. Taking the <divergence> of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> and substituting <Gauss's law> proves the equation, using that the <divergence of a curl is zero>.

= Covariant Maxwell equation with the minus-plus-plus-plus metric
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{c}

With metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$, $A_0=-\Phi/c$, and $j^\mu=(c\rho,J)$, the field tensor satisfies
$$
\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0j^\mu,
\qquad
\partial_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]}=0.
$$
Its components are $F_{0i}=-E_i/c$ and $F_{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, so these tensor equations reproduce all four Maxwell equations.

= Source-free Maxwell equations in a linear medium
{parent=Maxwell equations}

For $D=\epsilon E$ and $B=\mu H$ with no free charge or current,
$$
\nabla\cdot D=0,\qquad
\nabla\cdot B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB,\qquad
\nabla\times H=\partial_tD.
$$

= Plane electromagnetic wave in a linear medium
{parent=Source-free Maxwell equations in a linear medium}

A plane wave in a homogeneous linear medium satisfies
$$
\omega^2=\frac{|k|^2}{\epsilon\mu},
\qquad
v=\frac1{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}},
\qquad
B_0=\frac1\omega k\times E_0.
$$

= Transversality of an electromagnetic plane wave
{parent=Plane electromagnetic wave in a linear medium}

The source-free divergence equations imply $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0=0$, so both fields are transverse to the wavevector.
