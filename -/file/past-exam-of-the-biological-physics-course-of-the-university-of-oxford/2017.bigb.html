Start both systems from zero and compare their times to reach half of their **own** <Steady state>[steady concentrations]. Without regulation,
$$
Y(t)=\frac\beta\alpha(1-e^{-\alpha t}),\qquad
Y_{\rm st}^{(0)}=\frac\beta\alpha,\qquad T_{1/2}^{(0)}=\frac{\log2}{\alpha}.
$$
In the strong <logic approximation for gene regulation>, <Gene expression>[production] is initially fully on and the <concentration> rises towards the repression threshold. The regulated level is pinned near $Y_{\rm st}\simeq K$, so it reaches $K/2$ before switching:
$$
\boxed{T_{1/2}^{\rm logic}=-\frac1\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{\alpha K}{2\beta}\right),\qquad
\frac{T_{1/2}^{\rm logic}}{T_{1/2}^{(0)}}=
\frac{-\log[1-\alpha K/(2\beta)]}{\log2}
\simeq\frac{\alpha K}{2\beta\log2}\ll1.}
$$
The faster relative response comes from allowing high initial <Gene expression>[production] while keeping the final abundance low. <Negative autoregulation> can reduce response delays and <Sensitivity analysis>[sensitivity] to <Gene expression>[production] fluctuations, and can reduce unnecessary accumulation of a <protein>. It does not suppress every possible noise source at every frequency.

A perfectly discontinuous on/off right-hand side has a threshold-switching ambiguity rather than an ordinary smooth equilibrium at $K$. Here threshold pinning means the limiting rapid-switching or steep smooth-response idealization. A continuous repression law resolves that idealization. Comparing half of the same prescribed <concentration>, rather than half of each model's final <concentration>, would be a different response-time comparison.

For the <strong Hill autorepression response>, in the requested approximation, $\dot Y=\beta K^nY^{-n}-\alpha Y$. Put $z=Y^{n+1}$. Then
$$
\dot z=(n+1)(\beta K^n-\alpha z),\qquad
z(t)=\frac{\beta K^n}{\alpha}[1-e^{-(n+1)\alpha t}],
$$
so the formal zero-initial-concentration solution is
$$
\boxed{Y(t)=\left(\frac{\beta K^n}{\alpha}\right)^{1/(n+1)}
[1-e^{-(n+1)\alpha t}]^{1/(n+1)},\qquad
Y_{\rm st}=\left(\frac{\beta K^n}{\alpha}\right)^{1/(n+1)}.}
$$
Setting $Y(T_{1/2})=Y_{\rm st}/2$ gives
$$
\boxed{T_{1/2}=\frac{-\log(1-2^{-(n+1)})}{(n+1)\alpha},\qquad
\frac{T_{1/2}}{T_{1/2}^{(0)}}=
\frac{-\log(1-2^{-(n+1)})}{(n+1)\log2}.}
$$
The formal ratio decreases with positive $n$; for $n=1$ it is about $0.208$, and for $n=2$ about $0.0642$. Its large-$n$ asymptote is $2^{-(n+1)}/[(n+1)\log2]$. This is a <Nonlinear ordinary differential equation>[nonlinear] half-response time, not simply the near-equilibrium <Time constant>[relaxation time] $1/[(n+1)\alpha]$.

**Approximation qualification.** The omitted initial layer is precisely the one the source permits us to ignore: the approximate derivative diverges at $Y=0$, whereas the full <Hill equation> has initial slope $\beta$. The approximation is not uniform as $n\to\infty$ at fixed $R=\beta/(\alpha K)$. Strong repression already at half of the final <concentration> needs $R^{1/(n+1)}/2\gg1$. The actual <Hill equation> obeys $\dot Y\le\beta$, so $T_{1/2}\ge Y_{\rm st}/(2\beta)$. Its steep-switch limit approaches the logic-response scale in <1/a>; the formal vanishing large-$n$ result above cannot be extrapolated past its regime of validity.

For the <parameter sensitivity of autorepressed expression>, unregulated <Gene expression>[production] has $Y_{\rm st}=\beta/\alpha$, so its logarithmic <Sensitivity analysis>[sensitivity] is one. In the strong Hill approximation, $Y_{\rm st}=(\beta K^n/\alpha)^{1/(n+1)}$, with $K,\alpha,n$ held fixed. Hence
$$
\boxed{S(Y_{\rm st},\beta)=1\quad\text{without feedback},\qquad
S(Y_{\rm st},\beta)=\frac1{n+1}\quad\text{with strong Hill repression}.}
$$
The ideal logic level $Y_{\rm st}\simeq K$ instead gives $S\simeq0$, because changing <Gene expression>[production] primarily changes the switching duty fraction, not the threshold. Finite Hill repression has a residual <Gene expression>[production] <Sensitivity analysis>[sensitivity].

The exact Hill <Steady state>[steady-state] relation makes the comparison continuous. Write $s=(Y_{\rm st}/K)^n$; then $\beta=\alpha Y_{\rm st}(1+s)$ and $d\beta/dY_{\rm st}=\alpha[1+(n+1)s]$. Thus
$$
\boxed{S(Y_{\rm st},\beta)=\frac{1+s}{1+(n+1)s}.}
$$
It tends to one for weak repression and to $1/(n+1)$ for strong repression. The calculation concerns a local relative parameter change, not the response to an arbitrary large <Gene expression>[production] perturbation.

Use steady or initial-rate conditions with effectively clamped <Substrate>[substrate] <Concentration>[concentrations]. For either <Substrate>[substrate] $X\in\{S,R\}$, put $U_X=[AX]$ and $V_X=[AX^*]$. The intermediate balances are
$$
0=k_+^X[A][X]-(k_-^X+m)U_X,\qquad
0=mU_X-(l_-^{X*}+V_A)V_X.
$$
The <Reaction rate>[product flux] is therefore
$$
J_X=V_AV_X=
\frac{k_+^X[A][X]\,mV_A}{(k_-^X+m)(l_-^{X*}+V_A)}.
$$
The <Enzyme>[free enzyme] <concentration> is common to the two competing pathways, so it cancels even when <enzyme> sequestration changes its magnitude. Consequently
$$
\boxed{F=\frac{J_R}{J_S}=
\frac{[R]}{[S]}\frac{k_+^R}{k_+^S}
\frac{k_-^S+m}{k_-^R+m}
\frac{l_-^{S*}+V_A}{l_-^{R*}+V_A}.}
$$
Each post-binding factor is the ratio of <Probability>[probabilities] of proceeding rather than dissociating at that stage. The expression assumes positive common forward rates and no significant product back reaction. Without the steady/intermediate-balance or initial-rate assumption, the ratio can depend on intermediate populations and observation time; binding and <Dissociation rate constant>[dissociation] constants alone would not determine a unique transient ratio.

With equal <Substrate>[substrate] abundance and association rates, let $a=k_-^S<b=k_-^R$ and $c=l_-^{S*}<d=l_-^{R*}$. Then
$$
F(m,V_A)=\frac{a+m}{b+m}\frac{c+V_A}{d+V_A}.
$$
Both factors increase with their forward rate: for example $\partial_m[(a+m)/(b+m)]=(b-a)/(b+m)^2>0$. Thus maximum discrimination requires enough waiting at each stage for the less stable incorrect complex to be rejected. In the slow-forward limit,
$$
\boxed{m\ll k_-^S,k_-^R,\quad V_A\ll l_-^{S*},l_-^{R*},\qquad
F\longrightarrow\frac{k_-^S}{k_-^R}\frac{l_-^{S*}}{l_-^{R*}}.}
$$
The two rejection stages multiply their discrimination. Fast modification or fast product formation bypasses the corresponding <Dissociation rate constant>[dissociation] test and drives that factor towards one.

There is a <kinetic proofreading fidelity-throughput tradeoff>: taking either forward rate literally to zero gives zero <Reaction rate>[product flux], not a useful finite-output optimum. If <Dissociation rate constant>[dissociation] rates are well separated, a practical high-yield discrimination window is $k_-^S\ll m\ll k_-^R$ and $l_-^{S*}\ll V_A\ll l_-^{R*}$. Correct complexes then usually pass both stages while incorrect complexes usually dissociate; its fidelity is not the mathematical infimum obtained with vanishing forward rates. The irreversible modification represents a driven, energy-consuming step, permitting successive nonequilibrium checks rather than merely repeating one equilibrium binding comparison. No finite positive optimum can be selected from minimization of $F$ alone without a speed or yield requirement.

A skeletal-muscle fibre contains parallel <Myofibril>[myofibrils] assembled from repeating <sarcomeres> between Z discs. Thin <actin filaments> extend from the Z discs; bipolar thick <myosin II> <Myofilament>[filaments] occupy the central region. Overlap between them allows many <Myosin>[myosin heads] to interact with <actin> in parallel.

A rise in cytosolic <calcium> exposes <actin> binding sites through the <troponin>–<tropomyosin> regulatory system. <ATP> binding releases a strongly attached <myosin> head; hydrolysis primes its <Myosin lever arm>[lever arm], attachment and product release permit a force-generating stroke, and another <ATP> allows detachment. The cycle converts <Gibbs free energy>[chemical free energy] into <Work>[mechanical work] rather than consuming <actin> or shortening the <Myofilament>[filaments] themselves.

Relative filament <Facilitated target search>[sliding] draws the Z discs together. The I band and H zone shorten as overlap increases, whereas the thick-filament A-band length is approximately unchanged. <Calcium> removal restores inhibition and permits relaxation. The many-head ensemble sustains <force> even though individual cross-bridges repeatedly attach and detach. This sliding-filament description concerns <myosin II> in <muscle>; the processive two-head walking of <myosin V> in <6/a> is a related but distinct motor arrangement.

In <spatially gated myosin attachment>, for a freshly available head encountering an isolated site, take $P=0$ before the attachment window. Converting time derivatives to position derivatives with $dx/dt=v$ gives $vP'=k_{on}(1-P)$ in the binding window, $P'=0$ in the protected bound region, and $vP'=-k_{off}P$ after detachment becomes allowed. Continuity gives
$$
\boxed{P(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
1-e^{-k_{on}x/v},&0\le x\le\Delta x,\\
p_b,&\Delta x<x\le x_0,\\
p_b e^{-k_{off}(x-x_0)/v},&x>x_0,
\end{cases}\qquad p_b=1-e^{-k_{on}\Delta x/v}.}
$$
For $v<k_{on}\Delta x$, the plateau exceeds $1-e^{-1}$ and approaches one for much slower motion. For $v>k_{on}\Delta x$, the <Probability>[binding probability] is lower, becoming $k_{on}\Delta x/v$ at very high speed. The spatial detachment tail has length $v/k_{off}$, so faster <Facilitated target search>[sliding] makes a longer low-probability tail. The following is an original calculation of those profiles; the <force> panel is derived in <2/c>.

**Reset condition.** Geometric separation $D\gg x_0$ does not alone guarantee detachment before the next site: independently available encounters also need $(D-x_0)k_{off}/v\gg1$. Without that extra timescale separation the head may still be attached to an earlier site.

For completeness, the periodic <Steady state>[steady-state] correction can be written explicitly. Put $a=k_{on}/v$, $b=k_{off}/v$, $A_0=1-e^{-a\Delta x}$ and
$$
C=\frac a{a-b}(e^{-b\Delta x}-e^{-a\Delta x}),\qquad
Q_0=\frac{A_0e^{-b(D-x_0)}}{1-e^{-bD}+Ce^{-b(D-x_0)}}.
$$
Here $Q_0$ is the <probability> of entering the new window still attached to an earlier site. In the window the new-site <probability> is $P(x)=1-e^{-ax}-Q_0a(e^{-bx}-e^{-ax})/(a-b)$; after it, use its value $p_b=P(\Delta x)$ in the plateau and tail above. For $a=b$, replace $a(e^{-bx}-e^{-ax})/(a-b)$ by $ax e^{-ax}$. This follows from the old-site <probability> $Q(x)=Q_0e^{-bx}$ and new attachment rate $a[1-P-Q]$. The isolated-site result is recovered as $Q_0\to0$.

For the <work of a spatially gated myosin head>, the mechanical <force> exerted by an attached head in the increasing-$x$ direction is $f(x)=-dU/dx=\kappa(x_0-x)$. It assists motion before $x_0$ and resists it afterwards. For one site encounter the expected work is
$$
W=\int_0^\infty P(x)\kappa(x_0-x)\,dx.
$$
In the narrow binding-window limit, retain the capture <probability> $p_b=1-e^{-k_{on}\Delta x/v}$ but neglect the attachment <displacement> compared with $x_0$. The positive work is $p_b\kappa x_0^2/2$. After $x_0$, use $s=x-x_0$ to obtain the negative contribution $-p_b\kappa\int_0^\infty se^{-k_{off}s/v}ds=-p_b\kappa(v/k_{off})^2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{W\simeq\kappa(1-e^{-k_{on}\Delta x/v})
\left[\frac{x_0^2}{2}-\left(\frac v{k_{off}}\right)^2\right].}
$$
An independent <energy> calculation gives the same factor: binding near zero loads <energy> $\kappa x_0^2/2$, while an <Exponential distribution>[exponentially distributed] post-threshold attachment time has $\langle t^2\rangle=2/k_{off}^2$, leaving mean <elastic energy> $\kappa v^2/k_{off}^2$ to be lost upon detachment. The factor of two in that <second moment> is essential.

One site passes every distance $D$, so average work per distance, or power divided by $v$, gives
$$
\boxed{\overline F(v)=\frac{W}{D}\simeq
\frac\kappa D(1-e^{-k_{on}\Delta x/v})
\left[\frac{x_0^2}{2}-\frac{v^2}{k_{off}^2}\right].}
$$
The <force> tends to $\kappa x_0^2/(2D)$ as $v\downarrow0$ and changes sign at $v=k_{off}x_0/\sqrt2$ in this idealized model. Above that speed forced <Facilitated target search>[sliding] is resisted on average. The formula uses the reset condition in <2/b>, as well as $\Delta x\ll x_0$; if reset fails, replace $p_b$ by the periodic capture <probability> to leading order in $\Delta x/x_0$. For arbitrary window width, the isolated-site work is exactly $\int_0^{\Delta x}(k_{on}/v)e^{-k_{on}x/v}[\kappa(x_0-x)^2/2-\kappa v^2/k_{off}^2]dx$. The loaded <Spring>[spring] <energy> must be supplied by the binding/power-stroke chemistry; this model does not specify an <ATP> efficiency. Real force-dependent transition rates and <energy> budgets limit extrapolation of the toy <Work of a spatially gated myosin head>[force-velocity] law.

In <spectrin unfolding by force spectroscopy>, the <cantilever> measures <Tension>[tension] through its calibrated deflection, while <displacement> of the pulling point increases the separation of the <protein> attachments. The <protein> extension is obtained after allowing for <cantilever> compliance and any linker extension; it is not simply the commanded <displacement>.

A folded <spectrin> repeat stores much of its backbone in a compact domain. Between <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] events, the already released chain is stretched: increasingly straight conformations have fewer accessible configurations, and the resisting <entropic force> rises nonlinearly. A peak is reached when a still-folded domain crosses an <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] barrier on the timescale of the imposed loading. Its released backbone increases the available <Polymer contour length>[contour length] abruptly. At nearly the same end-to-end distance the fractional extension $x/L$ is then smaller, so <Tension>[tension] drops. Pulling resumes on the next, longer-chain elastic branch. Repeated release of domains produces the saw-tooth sequence.

The record is therefore evidence for sequential mechanical <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] of modular structure, not repeated stretching of a fixed-length rod. For the nominal eight-domain construct, successive branches correspond to increasing numbers of unfolded repeats. The order and rupture <Force>[forces] can fluctuate because barrier crossing is thermally activated and depends on loading rate, structural heterogeneity and pulling geometry; equivalent repeats need not rupture at identical <force>.

The horizontal separation between worm-like-chain fits at the same <force> estimates the added <Polymer contour length>[contour length], whereas the vertical <force> drop at one extension reflects redistribution of <Tension>[tension]. These are distinct measurements. A repeat increment near the specified $31.7\,\mathrm{nm}$ is consistent with repeated similar structural units. The gain is a net release relative to the folded span and attachment geometry; $31.7/106\,\mathrm{nm}$ is not automatically the universal <Polymer contour length>[contour length] of a <peptide> residue.

During unloading, relaxation and possible refolding can restore compact domains. <Hysteresis> would indicate dissipative or kinetically delayed processes. Neither the peak <force> alone nor an arbitrary saw-tooth area directly equals an equilibrium <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] <Gibbs free energy>[free energy]: one must separate chain <Elasticity (physics)>[elasticity], <cantilever> storage, irreversible loss and pulling-rate effects. The worm-like-chain branches describe released-chain <Elasticity (physics)>[elasticity], not the molecular transition state responsible for a rupture peak.

Write $u=x/L$ and $A=k_BT L_p$. The <worm-like chain force-extension interpolation> is
$$
f=\frac{k_BT}{L_p}\left[\frac1{4(1-u)^2}-\frac14+u\right].
$$
A fit to an elastic rising branch estimates its <Polymer contour length>[contour length] $L$ and, if not fixed independently, its <persistence length> $L_p$. Comparing successive fitted <Polymer contour length>[contour lengths] gives the length released by a domain and helps distinguish whole-repeat <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] from partial events. $L_p$ estimates the flexibility of the unfolded chain and the <Flexural rigidity>[bending rigidity] $A$; at a stated <temperature>, $A=k_BT L_p$.

For small extension, expansion gives $f\simeq[3k_BT/(2L_pL)]x$, so the effective entropic <spring constant> is $3k_BT/(2L_pL)$. Close to full extension, $f\sim k_BT/[4L_p(1-x/L)^2]$, giving strong strain <Elasticity (physics)>[stiffening]. These regimes constrain the fit differently and correlate its parameters if the measured interval is short. The fit does not by itself identify a unique folding barrier or <Protein denaturation>[unfolding] kinetic rate.

**Persistence-length convention.** In a three-dimensional isotropic <worm-like chain> the standard definition is $\langle\mathbf t(s)\cdot\mathbf t(0)\rangle=e^{-s/L_p}$. Thus at $s=L_p$, $\arccos\langle\cos\theta\rangle=\arccos(e^{-1})\simeq68.4^\circ$. This is not literally the mean angle $\langle\theta\rangle$. For a simple counterexample, equally probable angles $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ have mean angle $45^\circ$ but $\arccos\langle\cos\theta\rangle=60^\circ$. The source's angle description is shorthand for the tangent-correlation convention.

After a domain unfolds, its backbone joins the same continuous chain as previously unfolded material. The new branch differs mainly by an added <Polymer contour length>[contour length], while the local backbone bending statistics remain similar. It is therefore consistent to fit successive branches with different $L_j$ and one common $L_p\simeq0.8\,\mathrm{nm}$. <Tension>[Tension] is transmitted through serial portions of the same chain; one should not add their tensile <Force>[forces] as though they were independent parallel springs.

The <persistence length> is short compared with the contour increment per repeat, so each released domain contributes many flexible bending segments. Stretching suppresses those thermal modes and raises <Gibbs free energy>[free energy] through loss of <entropy>, which is precisely the worm-like-chain mechanism. The common $L_p$ is a property of the unfolded backbone under the measurement conditions, not the <Protein domain>[folded domain]'s axial length or the number of domains already released. With $T=300\,\mathrm K$ only as an illustrative value, $k_BT L_p\simeq3.3\,\mathrm{pN\,nm^2}$.

A common persistence-length fit is evidence for approximately repeat-independent chain flexibility over the observed branches; it is not proof that a continuum inextensible model describes every molecular scale. Residual folded structure, heterogeneous linkers, force-induced backbone stretching, and very short-chain effects can cause deviations. The formal <force> divergence at $x=L$ signals the model's inextensibility assumption, not a physical infinite tensile <force>.

The <sodium> trace is a rapid inward current after depolarization, followed by loss of current even while <voltage> remains depolarized. A <voltage-gated sodium channel> therefore activates rapidly and then becomes unavailable through <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation]. Under ordinary cardiac ionic gradients, the test potential is below the <sodium> <reversal potential>, so opening carries inward positive charge. Rapid <sodium> entry supports the fast action-potential upstroke and propagation in working myocardium; <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] and subsequent recovery contribute to <Neuronal refractory period>[refractoriness].

The <potassium> trace develops more slowly, is outward during the positive step, and remains approximately sustained until repolarization. It is consistent with a delayed outward <potassium> <Electrical conductance>[conductance]: activation counteracts depolarization, and deactivation follows the return towards the holding <voltage>. <Potassium> currents contribute to repolarization and stable diastolic <voltage>. The trace does not identify a unique cardiac <Voltage-gated potassium channel>[potassium-channel] subtype, and not all <potassium> channels lack <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation].

Together, fast inward activation initiates excitation and slower outward activation terminates it. Current direction is set by electrochemical driving <force> as well as gating: $I=N_{\rm ch}g_{\rm single}p_o(V_m-E_{\rm ion})$ in an ohmic approximation. A <voltage clamp> fixes $V_m$ and reveals those currents; in a freely firing cell the currents instead jointly determine the <voltage> through the membrane-capacitance equation. <Calcium> current is also important for the cardiac plateau, so these two traces alone are not a complete ventricular action-potential model. The sodium-potassium pump maintains long-term gradients but is not the fast gating mechanism seen in these recordings.

<Voltage> gating and <ion> selectivity are different structural functions. A typical <voltage-gated potassium channel> has four pore-forming subunits; their <Voltage-sensing domain>[voltage-sensing] regions move charged residues in the membrane electric field and couple that motion to a pore gate. Carbonyl coordination in the <selectivity filter> compensates <potassium> dehydration in an ion-specific geometry. The smaller <sodium> <ion> is not automatically passed more readily: its hydration and coordination energetics differ. Structural work on a mammalian Kv channel resolves the sensor/pore architecture and an open activation gate.<Crystal structure of a mammalian voltage-dependent Shaker family K+ channel by Stephen B. Long and others>[Kv1.2 structural study, abstract and open-pore/voltage-sensor architecture]{ref}

A <Voltage-gated sodium channel>[sodium-channel] alpha subunit contains four <Biological homology>[homologous] domains assembled into one pore, with separate <Voltage-sensing domain>[voltage sensors]. Rapid activation and fast <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] are distinguishable conformational transitions; an intracellular <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] apparatus stabilizes nonconducting states after activation. Repolarization favours recovery of availability. A channel can therefore be closed but activatable, or inactivated and temporarily unavailable, despite zero current in both cases.

Several types of evidence test these ideas. Ionic <Reversal potential>[reversal potentials] shift when ionic gradients are changed, establishing selectivity and driving <force>. Single-channel recordings show discrete conducting levels and stochastic dwell times; whole-cell current changes often reflect changed <Two-state ion-channel gating>[open probability] rather than a continuously varying single-channel current. Small <Ion-channel gating current>[gating-charge] transients can remain when ionic permeation is blocked, demonstrating sensor movement rather than <ion> flux. Conditioning and paired-pulse records separate activation, <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] and recovery. Structural perturbations of <Voltage-sensing domain>[voltage sensors] and of <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] machinery alter different kinetic properties, supporting their functional distinction.

These observations constrain models but do not make an individual macroscopic trace a unique structural reconstruction. Different distributions of closed and inactivated states can produce similar averages. In particular, the sustained <potassium> trace supports little <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] on the displayed interval; it does not justify assigning every <Voltage-gated potassium channel>[potassium channel] a universal absence of an <Ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] mechanism.

At fixed <voltage> let $a$ and $b$ be opening and closing rates. The <Two-state ion-channel gating>[open probability] obeys
$$
\dot p=a(1-p)-bp,\qquad
\boxed{p_\infty=\frac a{a+b},\quad\tau=\frac1{a+b},\quad
p(t)=p_\infty+[p(0)-p_\infty]e^{-t/\tau}.}
$$
This illustrates <equal open probability with unequal ion-channel relaxation times>: both channels have $p_\infty=1/2$, but channel A has $\tau_A=1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and channel B has $\tau_B=0.1\,\mathrm{ms}$. Starting closed, $p_A(t)=\tfrac12(1-e^{-t/(1\,\mathrm{ms})})$ and $p_B(t)=\tfrac12(1-e^{-t/(0.1\,\mathrm{ms})})$. If channel number, unitary <Electrical conductance>[conductance] and driving <force> agree, both have the same steady macroscopic current; B approaches it ten times faster.

An individual channel remains either open or closed. Its mean closed and open dwell times are $1/a$ and $1/b$, respectively: both are $2\,\mathrm{ms}$ in A and $0.2\,\mathrm{ms}$ in B. Thus identical <Two-state ion-channel gating>[equilibrium open probability] does not imply identical <Chemical kinetics>[kinetics] or noise correlation time. Ensemble <probability> relaxation depends on the sum of rates, while the mean dwell in a given state depends on its exit rate.

A <Two-state ion-channel gating>[two-state] closed–open scheme at fixed <voltage> relaxes monotonically and cannot by itself produce a sodium-current transient during a maintained step with constant driving <force>. Millisecond or faster relaxation usually completes much more readily during a long diastolic interval. Write $A=V/D_3$ and $B=L/D_1$. The given mean-search scaling is $\tau=A/\lambda+B\lambda$. Three-dimensional relocation sees an effective capture length of order $\lambda$, giving a bulk encounter time $V/(D_3\lambda)$. A <Facilitated target search>[sliding] excursion explores length of order $\lambda$ in time of order $\lambda^2/D_1$; sampling order $L/\lambda$ contour segments gives a total <Facilitated target search>[sliding] cost of order $L\lambda/D_1$. Prefactors, correlations and repeated visits are coarse-grained in this scaling model.

Small <Facilitated target search>[sliding] lengths make relocation inefficient; very long <Facilitated target search>[sliding] wastes time revisiting a local <DNA> region rather than relocating to another. The optimum balances both contributions:
$$
\frac{d\tau}{d\lambda}=-\frac{V}{D_3\lambda^2}+\frac L{D_1}=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\lambda_* =\sqrt{\frac{VD_1}{LD_3}},\qquad
\tau_{\min}=2\sqrt{\frac{VL}{D_1D_3}}.}
$$
Since $d^2\tau/d\lambda^2=2V/(D_3\lambda^3)>0$, it is the unique minimum for unrestricted positive $\lambda$. At the optimum, bulk and <Facilitated target search>[sliding] times are equal. With $s=\lambda/\lambda_*$,
$$
\boxed{\tau/\tau_{\min}=\tfrac12(s^{-1}+s).}
$$

Usable biological <Gibbs free energy>[free energy] has several interconvertible forms. At approximately fixed <temperature> and pressure, <Chemical reaction>[chemical reactions] are described by <Chemical potential>[chemical potentials], $\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\log a_i$, and $\Delta_rG=\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i$. <ATP hydrolysis>, for example, has $\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^{\circ\prime}+RT\log(a_{\rm ADP}a_{\rm Pi}/a_{\rm ATP})$ under the specified biochemical standard-state convention. The <Thermodynamic activity>[activities] and <proton>/<ion> convention matter. Breaking a bond alone costs <energy>; the net free-energy change includes the products, solvent and all bond changes, not a store released simply by snapping one bond.

<Redox>[Redox reactions] provide <Gibbs free energy>[free energy] when electrons move between donors and acceptors with a favourable potential difference: $\Delta G=-nF\Delta E$. Electron-transfer chains can couple that drop to <Membrane transport>[ion transport], building an <electrochemical gradient>. For transfer from an outer to an inner reservoir,
$$
\Delta\widetilde\mu=RT\log\frac{a_{\rm in}}{a_{\rm out}}+zF(V_{\rm in}-V_{\rm out}).
$$
Both <concentration> difference and <Electric potential>[electrical potential] contribute. <ATP synthase> couples downhill <ion> flow to nucleotide <Gibbs free energy>[free energy]; driven pumps can perform the reverse conversion and maintain gradients used by transporters and excitability.

<Work>[Mechanical work] appears as $F\,dx$ or torque times angle, and can be stored elastically, for example $\kappa x^2/2$. Molecular motors convert <Gibbs free energy>[chemical free energy] into movement or <force>; mechanically driven reactions can conversely reshape chemical transitions. Electrical <energy> can also be stored in <membrane capacitance>, while bulk ionic <concentration> gradients store additional <Gibbs free energy>[chemical free energy]. Light supplies photon <energy> $h\nu$, which photosynthetic machinery can convert into redox and <chemical potential> differences.

Coupling is essential: an overall favourable reaction can drive an unfavourable one only when their processes are linked, rather than merely present in the same cell. At fixed conditions the extracted work is bounded by the consumed free-energy decrease. Dissipation produces <heat> and <entropy>, so <energy> conservation does not mean all <energy> remains available for work. <Thermal fluctuation>[Thermal fluctuations] can drive random motion, but a persistent directional cycle requires a nonequilibrium free-energy source; an equilibrium asymmetric structure alone is not a free motor.

A <point mutation> changes a nucleotide at one genomic position. Replication errors and chemical damage can generate <Nucleotide substitution>[substitutions]; repair and other proofreading processes influence the retained <mutation rate>. A coding change may be synonymous or alter an <amino acid>, while a noncoding change can affect regulation. Its evolutionary effect depends on context, so <mutation>, functional consequence and eventual <Fixation probability>[fixation] are distinct events.

<Mutation> supplies new variants, while <genetic drift>, <Natural selection>[selection] and population history determine their persistence. Under a simple neutral model, increasing population size raises the number of new variants but lowers each one's <Fixation probability>[fixation] <probability>; the neutral <Nucleotide substitution>[substitution] rate is approximately the <mutation rate>. Selective constraint, generation time and lineage-specific processes can therefore complicate a universal <molecular clock>. Molecular evolution is not directed towards anticipated needs.

To infer a <Phylogenetic tree>[phylogeny], compare <Biological homology>[homologous] positions in appropriately chosen <Orthology>[orthologous] sequences and estimate the tree and branch lengths under a <Nucleotide substitution>[substitution] model. Shared derived characters support <Common descent>[common ancestry], but reversals and parallel <Nucleotide substitution>[substitutions] mean raw mismatches underestimate evolutionary distance. In a simple equal-rates four-state illustration, a mismatch fraction $p$ gives corrected pairwise distance $d=-(3/4)\log(1-4p/3)$. The formula's strong divergence as $p\to3/4$ reflects saturation and loss of reliable historical information, not arbitrarily precise evidence for an ancient split.

The <molecular-clock calibration and rate-time identifiability> issue is fundamental: branch lengths first measure expected <Nucleotide substitution>[substitutions] per site. A clock relates them to time by $d_i=r_it_i$; for two equal-rate lineages separated for time $t$, pairwise distance is $d=2rt$, so $t=d/(2r)$. Absolute times require external information, such as independently dated fossils or geological constraints, or informative sampling dates when the data contain a temporal signal. Sequence differences alone generally identify only rate-times-time, not both quantities separately.

Time-calibrated inference should propagate uncertainty in topology, <Nucleotide substitution>[substitution] rates, ancestral-state reconstruction and calibration bounds. Relaxed clocks permit different lineage rates. Fossil minima are not automatically exact split dates; orthology mistakes, <incomplete lineage sorting>, <Genetic recombination>[recombination], <Horizontal gene transfer>[horizontal transfer] and <Natural selection>[selection] can cause discordance between one <Phylogenetic tree>[gene tree] and <species> history. Multiple independent loci and well-justified calibrations make a stronger historical argument than a single uncalibrated sequence comparison. No unique numerical tree or date can be constructed here without actual sequence observations and calibration data.
