=== a

==== Solution

In bacterial <transcription initiation>, the <RNA polymerase> holoenzyme uses a <promoter>-specific <sigma factor> to recognize <promoter> elements and form a closed <DNA>-bound complex. Subsequent local <DNA melting> opens a <transcription> bubble, positioning the template strand in the active site: this is an <open promoter complex>. The enzyme can then initiate <RNA> synthesis and escape into elongation. <LacI>, the lac repressor, binds the <lac operator> overlapping or close to the <promoter> and can prevent productive <promoter> binding or its progression to an open complex; operator arrangement and <DNA> looping can strengthen repression. One should not assume every repressor acts at exactly the same microscopic step.

A high-resolution <optical tweezers> assay mechanically connects one trapped bead to <RNA polymerase> and another bead to an upstream or downstream <DNA> handle. Polymerase motion changes the tether length; measuring bead displacement and the calibrated restoring <force> reports elongation under a known load. A feedback <force> clamp can keep that load approximately constant. Reversing the tether geometry permits assisting and opposing loads. The left panel below shows the mechanical arrangement rather than a copied apparatus image.

A base-pair advance is only about $0.34\,\mathrm{nm}$ along B-form <DNA>. Resolving that increment requires exceptional mechanical stability, suppression of drift and laser-pointing noise, calibration of trap and tether compliance, and sufficient temporal sampling to separate steps from thermal bead fluctuations. Position histograms, dwell detection and their spatial correlations can expose a repeated increment; one should also test <nucleotide>-dependent motion, pauses and backtracking. Stabilized dual-trap measurements combined with <transcription>-product analysis established approximately one base-pair advance per incorporated <nucleotide>.<Direct observation of base-pair stepping by RNA polymerase by Elio A. Abbondanzieri and others>[Single-molecule displacement measurement and independent gel analysis supporting one-base-pair stepping]{ref}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Treat the target as a stationary perfectly absorbing sphere of reaction radius $r_0$, surrounded by an effectively uniform reservoir of <protein> <number density> $C_A$. At <steady state> the radial <diffusion equation> gives
$$
\nabla^2c=0,\quad c(r_0)=0,\quad c(\infty)=C_A,\qquad c(r)=C_A\left(1-\frac{r_0}r\right).
$$
The inward capture rate per target is the <integral> of the <Fick's first law>:
$$
J=4\pi r_0^2D_A\left.\frac{dc}{dr}\right|_{r_0}=4\pi D_Ar_0C_A.
$$
For dilute independent targets of <number density> $C_B$, the volume association rate is $JC_B$, so
$$
\boxed{k_a=4\pi D_Ar_0.}
$$
This is the <Smoluchowski association rate>, in volume per time because the <concentrations> are number densities. If the target also diffuses, use the relative <diffusion coefficient> $D_A+D_B$. Orientation constraints, incomplete absorption and <chromosome> geometry can reduce the actual association rate relative to this ideal collision limit.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The given sphere has radius $a=5\,\mathrm{nm}$. The <Stokes–Einstein relation> at an illustrative $T=300\,\mathrm K$ gives
$$
D_A=\frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a}=4.39\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}=4.39\,\mu\mathrm m^2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$
For $N=30$ well-mixed independent searchers in cell volume $V$, the target-arrival rate is $Nk_a/V$. The corresponding mean first arrival time is
$$
\boxed{\tau_3\simeq\frac{V}{30\,4\pi D_Ar_0}
=0.121\,\mathrm s\left(\frac{V}{1\,\mu\mathrm m^3}\right)\left(\frac{5\,\mathrm{nm}}{r_0}\right)\left(\frac{300\,\mathrm K}{T}\right).}
$$
The source does not fix $V$, the reaction radius or <temperature>. For an order-of-magnitude estimate, a bacterial volume around $1\,\mu\mathrm m^3$ and reaction radius $5$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$ give about $0.06$–$0.12\,\mathrm s$. A 20-base-pair B-form site is about $6.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ long, supporting a nanometre-scale capture radius but not uniquely defining its spherical value. The estimate is the ideal <diffusion>-limited time, not a measured intracellular search time: nonspecific binding, orientational matching, crowding and limited accessibility are excluded.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If the outcome is the first successful binding to *any* operator, ten well-separated functionally independent unoccupied targets give ten times the total capture rate. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tau_{3,10}\simeq\frac{V}{30\times10\times k_a}=\frac{\tau_3}{10},}
$$
or roughly $6$–$12\,\mathrm{ms}$ for the estimates above. This assumes nonoverlapping capture zones and no significant depletion before the first event. The first-arrival time at one designated operator is not divided by ten merely because other sites are added, and filling all ten sites is a different occupancy problem.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The term $V/(D_3\lambda)$ is the bulk-<diffusion> search cost: increasing the effective length of <DNA> searched after each encounter enlarges the useful capture region and reduces this contribution. The term $L\lambda/D_1$ is the accumulated sliding cost. Approximately $L/\lambda$ <DNA> landing-and-sliding episodes, each taking order $\lambda^2/D_1$, give $L\lambda/D_1$. These are scaling estimates; geometric constants and correlated revisits are omitted by the supplied model.

Thus the <facilitated target search> is slow as $\lambda\to0$, because too little sequence is examined per encounter, and slow at very large $\lambda$, because sliding and repeated local exploration take too long. A finite intermediate minimum balances 1D scanning against 3D relocation. The right panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-biological-physics-course-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/1/a/solution> shows the two contributions and their sum. The model requires a useful range of sliding lengths below the full <chromosome> length, finite positive <diffusion coefficients> and effective relocation between episodes; it is not a claim that arbitrarily large $\lambda$ is physically available.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $A=V/D_3$ and $B=L/D_1$. Then $\tau=A/\lambda+B\lambda$, so
$$
\tau'=-\frac A{\lambda^2}+B=0,\qquad \tau''=\frac{2A}{\lambda^3}>0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_*=\sqrt{\frac{VD_1}{LD_3}},\qquad
\tau_{\min}=2\sqrt{\frac{VL}{D_1D_3}}.}
$$
At the optimum, the two contributions are equal. Expressing the optimal sliding length directly in terms of the minimum time gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_*=\frac{D_1\tau_{\min}}{2L}=\frac{2V}{D_3\tau_{\min}}.}
$$
These relations concern the optimal point; a general nonminimal time can correspond to two different sliding lengths. A geometric or kinetic restriction on the allowed sliding length can instead place the optimum at a boundary.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a short inducer pulse to release <DNA>-bound fluorescent <LacI>, then remove extracellular <IPTG> rapidly and start the search clock after washout. The supplied entry, binding and dissociation times imply a synchronization uncertainty of order a few tenths of a second, which must be measured and separated from the search latency. If rebinding is faster than that dead time, the protocol alone cannot resolve the true mean without a kinetic correction or faster triggering.

Image individual cells rapidly enough to distinguish freely diffusing <proteins> from a persistent immobilized fluorescent spot at the single operator. Prelocate that locus from an independent position marker or a previous binding event. Score the first specific capture after each reset; controls lacking a functional target distinguish stable operator binding from nonspecific <DNA> residence. Correct for localization error, <photobleaching>[bleaching], blinking, missed brief events and target motion, and verify that the fluorescent tag does not substantially change the search kinetics.

With four independent freely searching <molecules>, each having an approximately exponential single-<molecule> target-arrival time of mean $\tau_1$, the first-arrival survival is
$$
S(t)=e^{-4t/\tau_1},\qquad\boxed{\tau_1=4\langle t_{\rm first}\rangle.}
$$
Thus report both the observed first capture among four and the inferred mean for one searcher. If the number of visible available <molecules> varies, use $S(t)=\exp[-\int_0^t n(s)ds/\tau_1]$ and account for censoring rather than blindly multiplying by four. Repeated resets and many cells estimate the distribution; deviations from an exponential test the independent well-mixed approximation. The rapid inducer manipulation defines a synchronization strategy, not an assumption that all subsequent intracellular search paths are identical.

===== Solution

Take $V=V_{\rm inside}-V_{\rm outside}$. Equality of <electrochemical potentials> for a monovalent cation gives the <Nernst equilibrium potential>
$$
E=\frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{c_{\rm outside}}{c_{\rm inside}}.
$$
At $T=293.15\,\mathrm K$, $RT/F=25.26\,\mathrm{mV}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm Na}=25.26\ln(120/15)=+52.5\,\mathrm{mV},\qquad
E_{\rm K}=25.26\ln(5/100)=-75.7\,\mathrm{mV}.}
$$
The signs refer to the stated electrode convention, not to the direction of current at every voltage. Activities are approximated by <concentrations> and the bulk ionic <concentrations> are treated as fixed during a single spike.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The intracellular recording by <Alan Hodgkin> and <Andrew Huxley> shows the membrane voltage rising from a negative resting value to a positive overshoot, with the outside held at zero reference. The <action potential> therefore does more than transiently abolish a resting polarization: it reverses the sign across the membrane. A simple hypothesis that excitation only makes an otherwise <potassium>-dominated membrane equally leaky cannot explain that positive overshoot.

The result helped motivate a transient, selective change in membrane permeability, later resolved into rapid inward <sodium> current followed by outward <potassium> current. The positive <sodium> <equilibrium> potential calculated above makes the overshoot physically plausible, while <potassium> conductance favours a negative resting voltage and <repolarization>. The 1939 voltage trace itself did not identify individual channel <proteins> or establish the full later voltage-dependent gating mechanism; those conclusions required ionic substitution and <voltage clamp>[voltage-clamp] evidence.<Action Potentials Recorded from Inside a Nerve Fibre by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley>[1939 intracellular recording and reversal of membrane voltage during excitation]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Panel B steps the membrane from approximately $-80$ to $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$ and holds it there. The <sodium> current becomes inward, peaks rapidly, and decays almost to zero despite maintained <depolarization>. At $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$ the <sodium> driving <force> $V-E_{\rm Na}$ is negative, consistent with the negative current convention. A conductance description is
$$
I_{\rm Na}=N\gamma p_O(V-E_{\rm Na}),
$$
where $N$ is channel number, $\gamma$ single-channel conductance and $p_O$ the open <probability>. The trace requires rapid voltage-dependent activation followed by slower <ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation], rather than a passive pore whose only response is an instantaneous constant current. Recovery normally requires <repolarization>, helping produce a refractory period and directional propagation of excitation.

A typical eukaryotic <voltage-gated sodium channel> contains four homologous membrane-spanning domains in one large pore-forming <protein>. Charged S4 helices sense voltage; pore-associated regions form a selective pathway and activation gate. Fast <ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] involves intracellular machinery coupled to the pore, classically the interdomain III–IV region, and should not be identified automatically with the tethered <potassium>-channel blocker. The pore permits rapid downhill electrochemical transport while maintaining selectivity against other <ions>.

Evidence separates these properties: clamp-step experiments distinguish voltage dependence and activation from decay at a fixed voltage; changing extracellular <sodium> shifts reversal potential; selective blockers isolate <sodium> current; <patch clamp>[patch-clamp] records resolve stochastic single-channel openings; and <mutations> of sensors, filter residues or the fast-<ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] machinery alter the corresponding function. A single macroscopic trace alone cannot uniquely determine all microscopic <rate constants>.

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Add an unavailable nonconducting inactivated state to the two-state gating scheme, for example
$$
C\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftarrows}^{k_o(V)}_{k_c(V)}}O
\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftarrows}^{k_i(V)}_{k_r(V)}}I.
$$
The probabilities obey
$$
\dot p_O=k_op_C-(k_c+k_i)p_O+k_rp_I,\qquad
\dot p_I=k_ip_O-k_rp_I,\qquad p_C+p_O+p_I=1.
$$
<Depolarization> increases activation, initially raising $p_O$; subsequent transfer to $I$ reduces current while voltage is held constant. Slow recovery at the depolarized voltage permits most channels to become unavailable. <Repolarization> promotes recovery and deactivation. More closed states and closed-state <ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] may be needed for quantitative data, but this minimal extension already explains the rise followed by decay that fixed-rate $C\leftrightarrow O$ gating cannot produce from a closed population.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <sodium>-channel gain-of-function mechanism in <Long QT syndrome> can reduce the <ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] rate $k_i$ or increase recovery/reopening from inactivated states, leaving a small persistent open population. The resulting late inward <sodium> current opposes repolarizing outward currents while $V<E_{\rm Na}$, prolonging the cardiac <action potential>. Slower entry into $I$ would also extend the typical conducting episode relative to its normal duration. The altered effect is prolonged <sodium> entry, not reversal of <sodium>'s charge or a pump-driven uphill flux through the channel.

The precise rate change depends on the <mutation>: the clinical label alone does not uniquely specify one transition constant. Long QT syndromes also include other channel mechanisms; the question here concerns its stated <sodium>-channel variant.<Cardiac sodium channel mutations in patients with long QT syndrome, an inherited cardiac arrhythmia by Qing Wang and others>[Sodium-channel defects associated with the inherited repolarization phenotype]{ref}

==== Solution

The original Panel A is readable: the baseline is roughly $-45\,\mathrm{mV}$ and the peak about $+40\,\mathrm{mV}$, giving a voltage excursion near $85\,\mathrm{mV}$. Using the specific <membrane capacitance> and surface area,
$$
C=0.01\times10^{-9}=10^{-11}\,\mathrm F,\quad
Q=C\Delta V\simeq8.5\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm C,
\quad\boxed{N_{\rm Na,net}=\frac{Q}{e}\simeq5.3\times10^6\text{ ions}.}
$$
A coarser $100\,\mathrm{mV}$ spike estimate gives $6.2\times10^6$ <ions>, illustrating the precision appropriate to the graph. These are the net monovalent charges needed to charge the <capacitor> if <sodium> supplies the inward charge; simultaneous opposing currents can make the actual <sodium> influx larger.

The cell contains $0.015\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\times3\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm L\times N_A\simeq2.71\times10^9$ intracellular <sodium> <ions>. The capacitive estimate adds only about $0.20\%$, or $0.029\,\mathrm{mM}$. Thus a spike does not equilibrate bulk <concentrations>. The <sodium-potassium ATPase> maintains the <gradients> over many spikes, exporting three <sodium> <ions> per two <potassium> <ions> imported per <ATP>; the estimated <sodium> load alone corresponds to at least about $1.8\times10^6$ pump cycles. Rapid <repolarization> itself comes mainly from changing channel conductances, while sustained fast firing requires adequate energy supply and <gradient> restoration. The small stated volume and large area are used as supplied, without assuming a particular cell geometry.

==== Solution

Let the common production and removal constants be $\beta$ and $\alpha$, so an unrepressed <protein> approaches $S=\beta/\alpha$. A simple constructive motif is
$$
X\longrightarrow Y,\qquad X\longrightarrow Z_1,\qquad
X\longrightarrow Z_2,\qquad Y\longrightarrow Z_3.
$$
Choose thresholds $K_{XY}=K_{XZ_1}=0.2S$, $K_{XZ_2}=0.4S$, and $K_{YZ_3}=0.5S$. The <logic approximation for gene regulation> gives $\dot Y=\beta H(X-0.2S)-\alpha Y$, $\dot Z_1=\beta H(X-0.2S)-\alpha Z_1$, $\dot Z_2=\beta H(X-0.4S)-\alpha Z_2$, and $\dot Z_3=\beta H(Y-0.5S)-\alpha Z_3$. These unequal thresholds are permitted in this part; the production and degradation rates remain equal.

After input onset, $X=S[1-e^{-\alpha(t-T_1)}]$. Production of $Z_1$ and $Y$ begins at $T_1+0.2231/\alpha$, $Z_2$ at $T_1+0.5108/\alpha$, and $Z_3$ at $T_1+(0.2231+\ln2)/\alpha=T_1+0.9163/\alpha$. Thus the required on-order is obtained. Take an input long enough that all these events occur, for example $T_2-T_1=6/\alpha$.

At input termination, $X=X_2e^{-\alpha(t-T_2)}$, where $X_2=S[1-e^{-\alpha(T_2-T_1)}]$. The higher direct threshold is crossed first: $Z_2$ production stops at $t_2=T_2+\alpha^{-1}\ln[X_2/(0.4S)]$, and $Z_1$ and $Y$ production stop at $t_1=T_2+\alpha^{-1}\ln[X_2/(0.2S)]$. At that instant $Y_1=S\{1-e^{-\alpha[t_1-T_1-0.2231/\alpha]}\}$, so $Z_3$ stops later at $t_3=t_1+\alpha^{-1}\ln[Y_1/(0.5S)]$. For the example, the delays after $T_2$ are approximately $0.914$, $1.607$ and $2.300$ in units of $\alpha^{-1}$:
$$
\boxed{Z_1\ \hbox{on before}\ Z_2\ \hbox{on before}\ Z_3;\qquad
Z_2\ \hbox{off before}\ Z_1\ \hbox{off before}\ Z_3.}
$$
<Protein> <concentrations> rise or decay continuously after these switches; “off” denotes cessation of production, not instantaneous disappearance. The first panel below marks the thresholds and the resulting <concentrations>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $h=K/S$, require $0<h<1$, and use the dimensionless time $s=\alpha t$. While X is produced, $X/S=1-e^{-s}$; afterwards $X/S=(1-e^{-\alpha T})e^{-\alpha(t-T)}$. X crosses the activation threshold on its rising branch at
$$
t_a=\frac{-\ln(1-h)}\alpha.
$$
Symmetry keeps $Y=Z$. They initially receive production through X alone and need another interval $t_a$ of active input to reach $K$. If this happens while X is still above threshold, their mutual OR activation sustains production permanently. X remains above $K$ until
$$
t_b=T+\frac1\alpha\ln\frac{1-e^{-\alpha T}}h
=\frac1\alpha\ln\frac{e^{\alpha T}-1}h.
$$
The <persistence correction to a gene-circuit pulse threshold> uses the limiting latch condition is $t_b=2t_a$, yielding
$$
\boxed{T_{\min}=\frac1\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{h}{(1-h)^2}\right).}
$$
For $T>T_{\min}$, $Y$ and $Z$ cross their common threshold and approach $S$ even after X decays away. For $T<T_{\min}$ they either remain zero or make a subthreshold pulse and return to zero. Exact equality is sensitive to the convention for the ideal threshold, so robust switching requires a strict excess. At $h=1/2$, $T_{\min}=\ln3/\alpha$, whereas $Y$ and $Z$ cross at $2\ln2/\alpha$ in absolute time.

The shorter required *production duration* reflects X's persistence after production stops. Requiring $T\ge2t_a$ would incorrectly discard that tail when all activating <ligands> remain present. If $K\ge S$, no finite pulse reaches the required feedback-active state. The second panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-biological-physics-course-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/3/a/solution> contrasts pulses shorter and longer than $\ln3/\alpha$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the dynamically regulated interpretation, take $X(0)=S$, $Y(0)=Z(0)=0$, and let X production respond to Y repression after the <ligand> appears. Define $h=K/S\in(0,1)$. Initially active X drives both Y and Z, giving $Y=Z=S(1-e^{-\alpha t})$. Y reaches $K$ at $t_1=-\ln(1-h)/\alpha$ and switches X production off. X then decays from $S$ to $K$, reaching it at
$$
t_2=t_1+\frac{\ln(1/h)}\alpha=\frac1\alpha\ln\frac1{h(1-h)}.
$$
Until $t_2$, X remains active and Y keeps being produced. Thus $Y_2=S(1-h+h^2)$. At $t_2$, Y production stops, but Y still activates Z. Its subsequent decay crosses $K$ at
$$
\boxed{t_{\max1}=t_3=t_2+\frac1\alpha\ln\frac{Y_2}{K}
=\frac1\alpha\ln\frac{1-h+h^2}{h^2(1-h)}.}
$$
Immediately afterwards both inputs to the Z OR gate are below threshold, so Z production stops and its <derivative> changes from positive to negative. Since Z has been continuously produced from zero until $t_3$,
$$
\boxed{Z_{\max1}=S(1-e^{-\alpha t_3})
=S\left[1-\frac{h^2(1-h)}{1-h+h^2}\right].}
$$
For $h=1/2$, $t_{\max1}=\ln6/\alpha$ and $Z_{\max1}=5S/6$. Y repression is lifted at $t_3$, but X must first rebuild from a subthreshold value, so Z has a genuine first local maximum before any later response.

**Wording qualification:** if “X is always present at its <steady state> <concentration>” is read as an external clamp maintaining $X=S$ *after* <ligand> arrival, it overrides Y's repression and changes the problem. Then $Z=S(1-e^{-\alpha t})$ rises monotonically, with no finite first maximum. The finite-peak result uses the consistent initial-steady-state interpretation in which the stated <negative feedback> can act.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $x=X/S$, $y=Y/S$, $z=Z/S$, $h=K/S$ and $s=\alpha t$. The <repressilator> in the stated <logic approximation for gene regulation>[logic approximation] is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dx}{ds}=H(h-z)-x,\quad
\frac{dy}{ds}=H(h-x)-y,\quad
\frac{dz}{ds}=H(h-y)-z,\quad(x,y,z)(0)=(0,1,0).}
$$
Assume $0<h<1$. Initially low Z allows X production; high Y blocks Z; and Y is initially at its unrepressed steady level. X first rises through $h$ at $s_1=a=-\ln(1-h)$, turning Y production off. Y then decays through $h$ at $s_2=a+b$, where $b=-\ln h$, allowing Z to rise. Z reaches $h$ at $s_3=2a+b$ and switches X production off. At this point $x_3=1-h(1-h)^2$, so X falls through $h$ at $s_4=s_3+\ln(x_3/h)$ and releases Y production.

Y's rise then switches Z off, Z's fall releases X again, and the sequence repeats. Every segment is exponential relaxation towards one if its production gate is on, or towards zero if off. For a rising segment starting at $v_0<h$, the next threshold time is $\ln[(1-v_0)/(1-h)]$; for a falling segment starting at $v_0>h$, it is $\ln(v_0/h)$. These rules determine the whole trace without pretending the <concentrations> are square waves. X, Z and Y peak in sequence after the stated initial condition; the last panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-biological-physics-course-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/3/a/solution> gives an illustrative $h=1/2$ trajectory.

The delayed sequence of three inhibitory links supplies <negative feedback> with finite response times. Equal rates make the cycling symmetric after the initial transient, not simultaneous. If $h\ge1$, repression is never reliably engaged and the <proteins> approach their unrepressed levels instead; oscillation is not implied for arbitrary thresholds or for every smooth regulatory law replacing the ideal relay.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

A fluorescent reporter associated with one phase of the <repressilator> should show repeated rises and falls in a tracked cell, with the same underlying oscillatory period but generally delayed and smoothed relative to instantaneous <promoter> activity. If mature <GFP> is fused to a repressor and is sufficiently fast to mature, <fluorescence> approximately tracks that <protein>'s abundance. If <GFP> is a separately expressed reporter, its own production, maturation, degradation and dilution introduce an additional filter.

For example, $\dot G=\beta_Gg(t)-\delta_GG$ converts a periodic gate $g(t)$ into a periodic, phase-lagged <concentration>. Very stable <GFP> can accumulate a large baseline and obscure oscillations rather than reproducing the regulator trace exactly; maturation delay and <photobleaching>[bleaching] also matter. Biological noise and cell-to-cell phase differences can make individual cycles irregular and wash out oscillations in a population average. The appropriate sketch is therefore a delayed, rounded oscillatory <fluorescence> trace, with amplitude and baseline depending on reporter turnover, rather than an unqualified copy of a Boolean gate.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

At <equilibrium>, <detailed balance> requires zero net <probability> current along each reversible link: $k_fp_A^{\rm eq}=k_bp_B^{\rm eq}$. The <equilibrium> probabilities satisfy $p_i^{\rm eq}\propto e^{-G_i/(k_BT)}$, where each state <free energy> includes its internal degeneracy. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{k_f}{k_b}=\frac{p_B^{\rm eq}}{p_A^{\rm eq}}
=e^{-(G_B-G_A)/(k_BT)}.}
$$
In the source sketch B is lower, and the labelled vertical drop is naturally $\Delta G_{AB}=G_A-G_B>0$. With that drop convention the ratio is $\boxed{k_f/k_b=e^{\Delta G_{AB}/(k_BT)}}$. If instead $\Delta G_{AB}$ means the conventional reaction change $G_B-G_A$, the exponent has the minus sign. For example a drop of $2k_BT$ gives a forward/backward ratio $e^2$, not $e^{-2}$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $B(t)$ for the <probability> of state B, so $A(t)=1-B(t)$. Gain minus loss gives
$$
\dot B=k_f(1-B)-k_bB=k_f-(k_f+k_b)B.
$$
With $B(0)=0$, the solution is
$$
\boxed{B(t)=\frac{k_f}{k_f+k_b}\left[1-e^{-(k_f+k_b)t}\right].}
$$
It rises monotonically from zero, initially with slope $k_f$, towards $B_{\rm eq}=k_f/(k_f+k_b)$ on time scale $(k_f+k_b)^{-1}$. The first panel of the original figure below shows this shape for illustrative <equilibrium> <probability> $0.8$.

The other <equilibrium> <probability> is $A_{\rm eq}=k_b/(k_f+k_b)$, and their ratio is $k_f/k_b$. Applying <detailed balance> from part (a) gives
$$
\frac{B_{\rm eq}}{A_{\rm eq}}=e^{-(G_B-G_A)/(k_BT)},
$$
which is precisely the two-state <Boltzmann distribution>. It fixes the ratio but not the sum of the rates, so <equilibrium> measurements alone do not determine the relaxation time.

{scope}

==== Solution

Take forward as increasing $x$, and specify the sinusoidal amplitude convention by
$$
U(x)=A[1-\cos(2\pi x/d)]-Fx.
$$
Thus the unforced barrier is $2A$, not $A$. Let $s=Fd/(2\pi A)=0.7051$ and $\theta=\arcsin s$. Stationary points exist because $F<2\pi A/d=7.09\,\mathrm{pN}$. A minimum is at $x_m=d\theta/(2\pi)$ and its forward saddle at $x_+=d(\pi-\theta)/(2\pi)$. The distances and activation barriers are
$$
\ell_+=\frac d2-\frac{d\theta}\pi=1.756\,\mathrm{nm},\quad
\ell_-=d-\ell_+=5.244\,\mathrm{nm},
$$
$$
\Delta U_+=2A\sqrt{1-s^2}-F\ell_+=2.422\,\mathrm{pN\,nm},\qquad
\Delta U_-=2A\sqrt{1-s^2}+F\ell_-=37.422\,\mathrm{pN\,nm}.
$$
Use an <Arrhenius equation>[Arrhenius estimate] with the supplied distance-squared waiting-time scaling, $\tau\propto\ell^2e^{\Delta U/(k_BT)}$. Calibrate at $F=0$ with $\ell_0=d/2$ and rate $k_0=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ in each direction. At the explicit room-<temperature> estimate $T=300\,\mathrm K$, $k_BT=4.142\,\mathrm{pN\,nm}$, giving
$$
\boxed{k_+\simeq k_0\left(\frac{d/2}{\ell_+}\right)^2e^{(2A-\Delta U_+)/(k_BT)}\simeq100\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
Applying the same distance rule separately backwards gives $k_-\simeq0.00241\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and hence the literal scaling-model estimate
$$
\boxed{f_-^{\rm distance\ rule}=\frac{k_-}{k_++k_-}\simeq2.4\times10^{-5}.}
$$
**Thermodynamic qualification:** for isothermal <diffusion> in this translated tilted potential, adjacent wells differ by $Fd=35\,\mathrm{pN\,nm}$, so <local detailed balance> instead requires $k_+/k_-=e^{Fd/(k_BT)}$. The two separate distance prefactors introduce an extra factor $(\ell_-/\ell_+)^2\simeq8.91$, inconsistent with that requirement. Keeping the forward estimate while enforcing the proper ratio gives
$$
\boxed{k_-^{\rm DB}\simeq0.0215\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
f_-^{\rm DB}=\frac1{1+e^{Fd/(k_BT)}}\simeq2.14\times10^{-4}.}
$$
Both interpretations are shown because the supplied distance heuristic is not, by itself, a thermodynamically consistent prescription for both directional prefactors. Additional hidden chemical driving would require its own affinity, not an unexplained prefactor asymmetry. A common transition-state prefactor, as in a consistent <Kramers escape rate>[Kramers model], preserves the <local detailed balance>[Boltzmann rate ratio]. Moreover the forward barrier is only about $0.58k_BT$, so rare-escape exponential dwell kinetics and the numerical rate should be regarded as rough estimates. If “amplitude” meant the entire unforced barrier height, the maximum restoring <force> would be only $\pi A/d=3.55\,\mathrm{pN}$ and no dwell wells would survive at $5\,\mathrm{pN}$; the amplitude convention is therefore essential.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume identical dwell states and memoryless forward/backward exits with constant rates $k_+$ and $k_-$; put $K=k_++k_-$. The <probability> density for the first exit to be forward at $t_1$ is $k_+e^{-Kt_1}$. For the very next exit also to be forward at time $t$, multiply by $k_+e^{-K(t-t_1)}$ and integrate over the unobserved first time:
$$
\boxed{\Pr(\hbox{next two exits forward, total time in }dt)
=\int_0^t k_+e^{-Kt_1}k_+e^{-K(t-t_1)}dt_1\,dt
=k_+^2t e^{-Kt}\,dt.}
$$
This density integrates to $(k_+/K)^2$, the <probability> that both directions are forward. Conditional on that event, the normalized total-time density is the <Erlang distribution>
$$
\boxed{p(t\mid++)=K^2t e^{-Kt},\qquad t_{\rm mode}=\frac1K\simeq0.010\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Its mean is $2/K$, so the most probable time is not the mean. Since both backward-rate conventions in part (c) have $k_-\ll k_+$, neglecting backwards steps gives the usual approximation $p(t)=k_+^2te^{-k_+t}$ and mode $1/k_+$. If “two forward steps” instead permits intervening backwards events, thinning independent Poisson exits gives that latter rate-$k_+$ distribution directly; it describes a different conditioning. The low barrier noted in part (c) limits the physical accuracy of all these memoryless dwell assumptions.

==== Solution

In a tensile <atomic force microscopy> measurement, a <DNA> tether connects the <cantilever> tip and a surface. Moving the support stretches the <molecule>, and calibrated <cantilever> deflection supplies the tensile <force>; extension must be corrected for <cantilever> deflection and any linkers. At low <force>, extension mainly straightens an initially fluctuating coil, giving approximately linear <entropic elasticity>. At higher <force> the available conformations become increasingly aligned, so additional extension costs progressively greater <force>. The readable source curve then continues beyond the nominal inextensible-model contour length, which signals a failure of that simple model or of the stated parameter interpretation, rather than negative elasticity.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

An inextensible <freely jointed chain> of $N$ links of <Kuhn length> $b$ has contour length $L_c=Nb$ and cannot be extended beyond it at any finite <force>. There is an explicit numerical contradiction in the printed parameters:
$$
\boxed{186\times7\,\mathrm{nm}=1302\,\mathrm{nm}=1.302\,\mu\mathrm m,\quad\text{not }26\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
The discrepancy is present in the original page, not introduced by digitization. To obtain $26\,\mu\mathrm m$ with $186$ links requires $b\simeq140\,\mathrm{nm}$; to obtain it with $b=7\,\mathrm{nm}$ requires $N\simeq3714$. No single inextensible FJC can satisfy all three stated numbers. The graph's much larger extension is an immediate counterexample to treating the literal $N,b$ as an accurate global fit.

Separately, accepting the graph's nominal $26\,\mu\mathrm m$ contour as an effective low-<force> model, its continuation to $32.7\,\mu\mathrm m$ is about $26\%$ beyond that contour. An inextensible <ideal chain> contains orientational <entropy> only; it excludes stretching of segments, energetic compliance of bonds, changes in helix structure or <base pairing>, and heterogeneous attachments. An <extensible freely jointed chain> or a semiflexible-chain model with appropriate energetic terms can describe some departures. Calibration or contour-length assignment can also be wrong. The graph alone does not identify which mechanism contributes, and its displayed moderate <forces> do not justify assigning a specific high-<force> <DNA> structural transition. Arithmetic inconsistency in $Nb$ is not explained away by these physical mechanisms.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the applied <force> be $\mathbf f=f\hat{\mathbf z}$. A segment with polar angle $\theta$ has energy $-fb\cos\theta$. Independent 3D orientations give the one-link <partition function>
$$
Z_1=\int e^{fb\cos\theta/(k_BT)}d\Omega
=4\pi\frac{\sinh u}{u},\qquad u=\frac{fb}{k_BT},\qquad Z_N=Z_1^N.
$$
Taking the <force> <derivative> of $k_BT\ln Z_N$ yields the mean end-to-end projection,
$$
\langle R_z\rangle=Nb\left(\coth u-\frac1u\right).
$$
For $u\ll1$, $\coth u-1/u=u/3+O(u^3)$, so
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathbf R\rangle=\frac{Nb^2}{3k_BT}\mathbf f+O(f^3),\qquad
\mathbf f=\frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}\langle\mathbf R\rangle.}
$$
<Isotropy> supplies the <vector> relation. It is the mean <vector> or its projection, not the mean magnitude of a fluctuating chain; at zero <force> $\langle\mathbf R\rangle=0$ while $\langle R^2\rangle=Nb^2$ remains positive. The last panel of the model figure in <past-exam-of-the-biological-physics-course-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/4/b/solution> shows the exact orientational response and its weak-<force> tangent.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The weak-<force> <spring constant> is
$$
\boxed{k_{\rm chain}=\frac{3k_BT}{Nb^2}.}
$$
Using the literal printed $N=186$, $b=7\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $25^\circ\mathrm C=298.15\,\mathrm K$ gives
$$
\boxed{k_{\rm literal}=1.355\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}=1.355\,\mathrm{pN\,\mu m^{-1}}.}
$$
This is a calculation with the quoted parameters, not a self-consistent fit to the plotted $26\,\mu\mathrm m$ contour. If instead $N=186$ and $L_c=26\,\mu\mathrm m$ are retained, then $b=L_c/N$ and $k=3.40\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Retaining $b=7\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $L_c=26\,\mu\mathrm m$ gives $k=6.79\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. These distinct alternatives quantify the unresolved parameter inconsistency rather than silently choosing a correction.

The restoring <force> is entropic. Stretching selects a small fraction of the many disordered orientations, reducing <conformational entropy>. The <Gaussian chain> <free energy>[free-energy] cost is $\Delta\mathcal F\simeq3k_BTR^2/(2Nb^2)$, whose <gradient> gives the Hooke-law coefficient above. In an ideal inextensible chain, individual bonds need not change their length to generate this <force>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

At fixed $N$ and $b$, the <entropic spring> constant is proportional to absolute <temperature>, because the <free energy>[free-energy] penalty for a given loss of <conformational entropy> is $-T\Delta S$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{k(10^\circ\mathrm C)=k(25^\circ\mathrm C)\frac{283.15}{298.15}=0.94969\,k(25^\circ\mathrm C).}
$$
For the literal printed chain this is $\boxed{1.287\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}}$. The same factor multiplies either alternative contour-consistent <spring constant> from part (d), provided its <Kuhn length> is held fixed. Real <DNA>'s <persistence length>, <base pairing> and solvent interactions can themselves vary with <temperature> and salt; then $b(T)$ contributes through $k\propto T/b(T)^2$ at fixed segment count, or $k\propto T/[L_cb(T)]$ if the physical contour is instead held fixed. The simple linear <temperature> result belongs to the specified ideal-chain model.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

After the load is removed, a reversibly stretched <DNA> <molecule> relaxes towards a thermally fluctuating coil, releasing stored <free energy> as <heat> through the surrounding fluid. There are many more disordered chain conformations than strongly aligned ones, so <entropy> favours retraction; viscous drag sets how rapidly it occurs. At long times an unconstrained <ideal chain> has zero mean end-to-end <vector> but a nonzero typical size, $R_{\rm rms}=b\sqrt N$; it does not contract to a point.

At finite <temperature> the chain continues fluctuating, and an anchored or otherwise constrained <molecule> need not regain an isotropic ensemble. If stretching broke attachments, denatured the <DNA> or caused irreversible structural changes, recovery can be incomplete or hysteretic. Those outcomes cannot be decided from the ideal FJC alone.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The hydrophobic core of a <lipid bilayer> makes transfer of a hydrated <ion> into bare lipid energetically costly. An <ion channel> supplies a water-accessible route across that barrier. Its narrow <selectivity filter> presents coordinating chemical groups that compensate for loss or rearrangement of the <ion>'s <hydration shell>. Selectivity depends on the <free energy>[free-energy] balance of hydration, coordination, <electrostatics> and geometry, rather than a sieve that passes all smaller <ions>.

A <potassium>-channel filter presents backbone carbonyl oxygens in a geometry favourable for <potassium>; smaller <sodium> does not automatically gain the same energetic compensation. <Sodium> and <calcium> channels use different filter architectures: charged side chains and their coordination environments favour particular <ions>, with <ion> occupancy and competing species also affecting permeation. In canonical vertebrate <voltage-gated sodium channels>, the DEKA selectivity ring illustrates chemical discrimination distinct from <potassium>-channel carbonyl coordination. The precise mechanism therefore depends on the channel family.

Rapid flux requires low *successive* <free energy>[free-energy] barriers, not permanent binding of the preferred <ion>. Several occupied sites, nearby water and <ion>–<ion> interactions can facilitate passage without requiring the <ion> to cross the lipid. For scale, a $100\,\mathrm{pS}$ open channel driven by $100\,\mathrm{mV}$ carries $10\,\mathrm{pA}$, approximately $6\times10^7$ monovalent <ions> per second. A nanometre-scale route with favourable coordination can support such rapid, thermally driven motion. An <ion channel> is not an <ATP>-driven pump: net flux follows the <electrochemical potential> difference,
$$
\boxed{\Delta\mu= k_BT\ln\frac{c_{\rm in}}{c_{\rm out}}+ze(V_{\rm in}-V_{\rm out}),}
$$
which vanishes at the species' <Nernst equilibrium potential>. <ATP>-consuming pumps maintain the <gradients> that make sustained channel currents possible.

Permeation selectivity and opening <probability> are separate properties. Voltage-sensor movement, <ligand> binding or mechanical tension can regulate access to the pore, while <ion-channel inactivation>[inactivation] can terminate conduction during a maintained stimulus. <patch clamp>[Patch-clamp] unitary currents establish discrete conducting states; reversal potentials under changed ionic compositions quantify selectivity. Structure determination locates the filter and gates, and <mutations> or specific blockers test their functional roles. Agreement between these measurements is stronger evidence than structure or current shape alone.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

A charged biological <molecule> attracts counterions and repels <ions> of the same sign. Thermal <diffusion> opposes complete localization, producing a mobile ionic atmosphere whose charge partly cancels the <molecule>'s <electric field> at larger distances. Solvent polarization also reduces the field through its <permittivity>, but that dielectric response is distinct from the mobile-<ion> screening cloud.

For an ideal <electrolyte> in <equilibrium> at <temperature> $T$, an <ion> of valence $z_i$ has local <number density>
$$
n_i(\mathbf r)=n_i^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{z_ie\phi(\mathbf r)}{k_BT}\right].
$$
For $|z_ie\phi|\ll k_BT$, linearization about an electroneutral bulk gives
$$
\rho_{\rm ions}\simeq-\phi\frac{\sum_i n_i^{\infty}z_i^2e^2}{k_BT},\qquad
\nabla^2\phi=\kappa^2\phi,\qquad
\boxed{\lambda_D=\kappa^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon k_BT}{e^2\sum_i n_i^{\infty}z_i^2}}.}
$$
This is the <Debye length>. Here $n_i^{\infty}$ counts *each species separately* and $\epsilon$ is the absolute solvent <permittivity>. For a point charge $Q$, outside its immediate molecular environment the linearized solution is
$$
\boxed{\phi(r)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon r}e^{-r/\lambda_D}.}
$$
It is a screened potential, not a claim that the molecular charge has disappeared.

In terms of <ionic strength> $I=\tfrac12\sum_i c_i z_i^2$ with $c_i$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, the density sum is $2N_A10^3I$, so
$$
\lambda_D=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon k_BT}{2e^2N_A10^3I}}.
$$
In water near $298\,\mathrm K$, $\lambda_D\simeq0.304\,\mathrm{nm}/\sqrt{I/(1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})}$: about $9.6\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $1\,\mathrm{mM}$ and $0.79\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $0.15\,\mathrm M$. Increasing <ion> density or valence shortens the range; <temperature> and solvent <permittivity> also matter. The displayed <temperature> dependence holds at fixed <permittivity>, which itself changes with <temperature> in water.

The derivation assumes dilute mobile <ions>, a continuum uniform dielectric and weak potentials. Near highly charged <DNA> or <protein> surfaces, nonlinear <Poisson-Boltzmann equation> behaviour, <counterion condensation>, finite <ion> sizes, specific binding and correlations can matter. At physiological salt the predicted length approaches molecular scales, so the formula is a useful far-field scale with limited microscopic accuracy, rather than a complete account of the first hydration layer.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

<Fluorescence localization precision> can be much finer than the image width: a fluorescent <molecule> produces a <diffraction>-broadened <point spread function>, but the centre of that distribution can be estimated much more precisely than its width. Fit the measured camera counts to a calibrated point-spread model with position, brightness and background parameters. For an ideal <Gaussian function>[Gaussian] image of <standard deviation> $s$ and $N$ independent detected <photons>, the centroid precision scales as
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm loc}\simeq\frac{s}{\sqrt N}.}
$$
Pixelation and background worsen this limit. A useful approximate <variance> for square pixels of width $a$ and background rms noise $b$ <photons> per pixel is
$$
\sigma_{\rm loc}^2\simeq\frac{s^2+a^2/12}{N}+\frac{8\pi s^4b^2}{a^2N^2}.
$$
This estimate requires a correctly specified isolated image and appropriate noise model. Drift, optical aberration, dye motion, linkage offset, <photobleaching>[bleaching] and <motion blur> introduce further errors; localization precision alone does not guarantee absolute positional accuracy.<Precise nanometer localization analysis for individual fluorescent probes by Russell E. Thompson and others>[Photon number, pixel size and background dependence of localization uncertainty]{ref}

In the <Myosin V> measurements, <total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy> reduced background and a two-dimensional <Gaussian function>[Gaussian] fit localized a single dye attached to a light chain with roughly $1.5\,\mathrm{nm}$ precision. The motor's centre advances about $37\,\mathrm{nm}$ per cycle. A <hand-over-hand motion of a two-headed motor>[hand-over-hand mechanism] instead moves the rear head about $74\,\mathrm{nm}$ past the stationary front head, exchanging their roles. A label displaced by $x$ from the midpoint therefore gives alternating displacements $37+2x$ and $37-2x$ nm; near a head this tends towards alternating $74$ and $0$ nm. An <inchworm motion of a two-headed motor>[inchworm model] would give approximately uniform $37$ nm label displacements. Resolved unequal alternating steps therefore distinguish the mechanisms, with interpretation depending on the label geometry.<Myosin V Walks Hand-Over-Hand: Single Fluorophore Imaging with 1.5-nm Localization by Ahmet Yildiz and others>[Single-light-chain fluorophore localization, alternating displacements and hand-over-hand interpretation; Science 300, 2061–2065 (2003)]{ref}

For sub-<diffraction> imaging, arrange that only a sparse, spatially separable subset of <fluorophores> emits in each frame, localize those emitters, then combine many frames into a map. Photoactivation or reversible stochastic switching provides the sparse subsets. Distinct nearby <molecules> can then be localized at different times, circumventing overlap of their simultaneously emitted <diffraction> patterns. The achievable structural resolution depends on <photon>-limited precision, label density and linkage, repeated blinking, drift correction and specimen motion; arbitrarily many fits to the same dye do not supply missing labels elsewhere. This is the principle behind <PALM> and <STORM>, with their different switching strategies.<Sub-diffraction-limit imaging by stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) by Michael J. Rust and others>[Sequential sparse activation and localization of fluorophores to reconstruct a super-resolution image]{ref}
