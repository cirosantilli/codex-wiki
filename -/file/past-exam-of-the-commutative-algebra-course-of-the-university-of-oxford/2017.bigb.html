A <commutative ring> $R$ is a <Noetherian ring> if every <ideal> has a finite <generating set of an ideal>. Equivalently, it satisfies the <ascending chain condition> on <ideals>. Indeed, the union of an ascending chain is an <ideal>, and finitely many generators all belong to one term, after which the chain stabilizes. Conversely, an <ideal> without a finite <generating set of an ideal> would allow us to choose successively $a_{n+1}\notin(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$, producing a strictly ascending chain of <ideals>.

Let $\phi:R\to S$ be a <surjective> <ring homomorphism>, where $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>. For any <ideal> $J$ of $S$, its inverse image is an <ideal> of $R$, say $\phi^{-1}(J)=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$. Taking images gives
$$
J=(\phi(a_1),\ldots,\phi(a_r)).
$$
Thus <homomorphic images of noetherian rings are noetherian>. This also applies to the image of an arbitrary <ring homomorphism>, by taking its image as the codomain.

The <Hilbert basis theorem> states that if $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, then the <polynomial ring> $R[t]$ is a <Noetherian ring>. Repeating this gives the result for finitely many variables.

To prove it, fix an <ideal> $K\subseteq R[t]$. For $n\geq0$, define the <coefficient ideals of a polynomial ideal>
$$
A_n=\{a\in R:\text{some }f\in K\text{ of degree at most }n\text{ has coefficient }a\text{ at }t^n\}.
$$
Here the zero <polynomial> is allowed. Each $A_n$ is an <ideal>: addition and multiplication by constants preserve the required degree bound. Multiplying a representing <polynomial> by $t$ gives $A_n\subseteq A_{n+1}$. The <ascending chain condition> therefore supplies $N$ such that $A_n=A_N$ for every $n\geq N$.

For each $0\leq n\leq N$, choose a finite <generating set of an ideal> $a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nr_n}$ for $A_n$, omitting zero generators, and choose $f_{nj}\in K$ of <polynomial degree> $n$ with <leading coefficient> $a_{nj}$. If $A_n=0$, choose no such <polynomials>. We claim that the finitely many $f_{nj}$ generate $K$.

Take a nonzero $f\in K$ of <polynomial degree> $d$. Put $n=\min(d,N)$. Its <leading coefficient> belongs to $A_d=A_n$, so write it as $\sum_jb_ja_{nj}$. The <polynomial>
$$
g=f-\sum_jb_jt^{d-n}f_{nj}
$$
belongs to $K$ and is either zero or has <polynomial degree> strictly below $d$. Induction on <polynomial degree> now expresses $f$ as a combination of the chosen $f_{nj}$; when $d=0$, the displayed difference is zero. Hence $K$ is finitely generated. Since $K$ was arbitrary, $R[t]$ is a <Noetherian ring>.

For $x\in X$, let $e_x$ be the <indicator function> of $\{x\}$. In the <ring of functions> $R=\mathbb R^X$, multiplication is pointwise, so
$$
e_xf=f(x)e_x.
$$
Suppose first that $X$ is finite, and let $J$ be an <ideal> of $R$. Define $S=\{x\in X:f(x)\ne0\text{ for some }f\in J\}$. If $x\in S$, choose such an $f$; multiplying it by $e_x/f(x)$ shows that $e_x\in J$. Every $f\in J$ satisfies
$$
f=\sum_{x\in S}f(x)e_x,
$$
where the real scalars are constant <functions>. Thus $J=(e_x:x\in S)$ has a finite <generating set of an ideal>, so $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>. If $X$ is empty, $R$ is the <zero ring>, and the conclusion still holds.

If $X$ is infinite, consider the <ideal>
$$
F=\{f\in\mathbb R^X:\{x:f(x)\ne0\}\text{ is finite}\}.
$$
It is an <ideal> because a sum has <support> contained in the union of the two <supports>, and multiplication by any <function> cannot enlarge <support>. If $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ generated $F$, their <supports> would have a finite union $T$. Every element of $(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$ would vanish outside $T$, whereas $e_x\in F$ for any $x\in X\setminus T$. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R^X\text{ is Noetherian}\iff X\text{ is finite}.}
$$

**No.** Write $x_i=t_i+I$ in the <quotient ring> $R=L/I$. Its defining relations are
$$
x_1^2=0,\qquad x_i=x_{i+1}^2\quad(i\geq1).
$$
Consequently $x_i^{2^{i-1}}=x_1\ne0$, using the permitted assumption, while $x_i^{2^i}=0$. Thus every $x_i$ is a nonzero <nilpotent element>.

The <principal ideals> satisfy
$$
(x_1)\subseteq(x_2)\subseteq(x_3)\subseteq\cdots.
$$
Every inclusion is strict. Otherwise $x_{i+1}=a x_i=a x_{i+1}^2$ for some $a\in R$, and hence
$$
x_{i+1}(1-a x_{i+1})=0.
$$
The fact that <one minus a nilpotent element is a unit> applies: if $z^m=0$, then $(1-z)(1+z+\cdots+z^{m-1})=1$. Here $z=a x_{i+1}$ is <nilpotent>, so multiplication by this inverse would give $x_{i+1}=0$, a contradiction. The strictly ascending chain of <ideals> violates the <ascending chain condition>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L/I\text{ is not Noetherian}.}
$$

The <radical of an ideal> $I$ is
$$
\boxed{\sqrt I=\operatorname{rad}(I)=\{a\in R:a^n\in I\text{ for some }n\geq1\}.}
$$
We first establish the properties needed below. If $a^m,b^n\in I$, every term of the <binomial theorem> expansion of $(a+b)^{m+n-1}$ contains either $a^m$ or $b^n$, so $a+b\in\sqrt I$. Also $(-a)^m\in I$ and $(ra)^m=r^ma^m\in I$. Thus $\sqrt I$ is an <ideal>. The definition gives $I\subseteq\sqrt I$ and shows that $I\subseteq J$ implies $\sqrt I\subseteq\sqrt J$. Finally, if $a^n\in\sqrt I$, then $(a^n)^m\in I$ for some $m$, so $a\in\sqrt I$. Hence $\sqrt{\sqrt I}=\sqrt I$; in other words, $\sqrt I$ is a <radical ideal>.

We prove the stronger fact that the <radical of an ideal is the intersection of the prime ideals containing it>:
$$
\sqrt I=\bigcap_{\substack{P\supseteq I\\P\text{ prime}}}P.
$$
If $a\in\sqrt I$ and $P\supseteq I$ is a <prime ideal>, then $a^n\in P$ for some $n$, and repeated application of the definition of a <prime ideal> gives $a\in P$. This proves one inclusion.

Conversely, fix $a\notin\sqrt I$. The <multiplicatively closed set> $T=\{1,a,a^2,\ldots\}$ is disjoint from $I$. Consider the <ideals> containing $I$ and disjoint from $T$. This collection is nonempty, and the union of any chain in it is another such <ideal>. By <Zorn's lemma>, there is a maximal member $P$. It is proper because $1\in T$.

To prove that $P$ is a <prime ideal>, suppose $bc\in P$ but $b,c\notin P$. Maximality forces both $P+(b)$ and $P+(c)$ to meet $T$. Thus, for some $u,v\geq0$,
$$
a^u=p+rb,\qquad a^v=q+sc,
\qquad p,q\in P,\quad r,s\in R.
$$
Multiplication gives $a^{u+v}\in P$, since every term lies in $P$, including $rsbc$. This contradicts disjointness from $T$. Therefore $P$ is a <prime ideal> containing $I$ and avoiding $a$. This proves the other inclusion, including the required <prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset> argument without using any unproved facts about the <nilradical>.

If $I=\sqrt I$, the displayed intersection represents $I$ as required. Conversely, an <intersection of ideals> that are all <prime ideals> is a <radical ideal>: if $a^n$ belongs to every one of them, then so does $a$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I=\sqrt I\iff I\text{ is an intersection of prime ideals}.}
$$
The <unit ideal> $I=R$ is included by interpreting the empty intersection as $R$.

If $\sqrt I+\sqrt J=R$, write $1=a+b$ with $a\in\sqrt I$ and $b\in\sqrt J$. Choose $m,n\geq1$ such that $a^m\in I$ and $b^n\in J$. In the <binomial theorem> expansion
$$
1=(a+b)^{m+n-1},
$$
each term has either at least $m$ factors $a$, or at least $n$ factors $b$. Hence every term belongs to the <sum of ideals> $I+J$, and
$$
\boxed{\sqrt I+\sqrt J=R\ \Longrightarrow\ I+J=R.}
$$
Thus <comaximal ideals> can be recognized from their <radicals of ideals>.

For the general identity, $I+J\subseteq\sqrt I+\sqrt J$ gives
$$
\sqrt{I+J}\subseteq\sqrt{\sqrt I+\sqrt J}.
$$
On the other hand, $I,J\subseteq I+J$ implies $\sqrt I,\sqrt J\subseteq\sqrt{I+J}$. Since the latter is an <ideal>, $\sqrt I+\sqrt J\subseteq\sqrt{I+J}$. Taking <radicals of ideals> and using the idempotence proved in <2/a> yields
$$
\sqrt{\sqrt I+\sqrt J}\subseteq\sqrt{\sqrt{I+J}}=\sqrt{I+J}.
$$
Therefore the <radical of a sum of ideals> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{rad}(I)+\operatorname{rad}(J))=\operatorname{rad}(I+J).}
$$

Fix $n\geq1$ and suppose, for a contradiction, that $I^n=I^{n+1}$. Set $M=I^n$. Since $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, this <ideal> is a <finitely generated module>, say $M=(u_1,\ldots,u_r)$. It is nonzero: if $0\ne a\in I$, then $a^n\ne0$ because $R$ is an <integral domain>.

The equality $M=IM$ allows us to write
$$
u_i=\sum_{j=1}^r a_{ij}u_j,\qquad a_{ij}\in I.
$$
Let $A=(a_{ij})$ and $B=\operatorname{Id}_r-A$. Then $B(u_1,\ldots,u_r)^{\mathsf T}=0$. The <adjugate matrix> identity $\operatorname{adj}(B)B=(\det B)\operatorname{Id}_r$ implies that $\det B$ annihilates every $u_i$. In the <determinant> expansion of $\operatorname{Id}_r-A$, the constant term is $1$ and every other term contains an entry of $A$, so
$$
\det B=1-b\quad\text{for some }b\in I.
$$
This is the <determinant trick>. Since some $u_i\ne0$, <multiplicative cancellation in an integral domain> gives $1-b=0$. But then $1=b\in I$, contradicting $I\ne R$. We have proved the <strict descent of powers of a nonzero proper ideal in a noetherian domain>:
$$
\boxed{I\supsetneq I^2\supsetneq I^3\supsetneq\cdots.}
$$
All these <powers of an ideal> have the same <radical of an ideal>. Indeed, $I^n\subseteq I$ gives $\sqrt{I^n}\subseteq\sqrt I$. Conversely, if $x^m\in I$, then $x^{mn}\in I^n$, so $x\in\sqrt{I^n}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{I^n}=\sqrt I\quad(n\geq1),}
$$
and $J=I,I^2,I^3,\ldots$ gives infinitely many distinct <ideals> with the prescribed <radical of an ideal>. If the convention $0\in\mathbb N$ is used, the additional inequality $I^0=R\ne I$ follows immediately from properness.

**No.** A <domain with a nonzero idempotent maximal ideal> is a counterexample. Consider
$$
R=\bigcup_{n\geq0}\mathbb Q[t^{1/2^n}],\qquad
u_n=t^{1/2^n},\qquad u_n=u_{n+1}^2.
$$
This can be realized concretely as a <ring of functions> on $t>0$: its elements are finite sums of rational multiples of nonnegative <dyadic rational> powers of $t$. Each stage $\mathbb Q[u_n]$ is a <polynomial ring>, because a nonzero <polynomial> cannot vanish for all $u_n>0$. Any two elements lie in one stage, which is an <integral domain>, so their product is nonzero if both are nonzero. Thus $R$ is an <integral domain>.

The constant term defines a <surjective> <ring homomorphism> $\epsilon:R\to\mathbb Q$. It is well defined because replacing $u_n$ by $u_{n+1}^2$ preserves the constant term. Its <kernel of a ring homomorphism>
$$
\mathfrak m=\{f\in R:\text{the constant term of }f\text{ is }0\}
$$
is a <maximal ideal>, since $R/\mathfrak m\cong\mathbb Q$ is a <field>, by the <maximal ideal quotient criterion>. Also $u_0=t\in\mathfrak m$ is nonzero.

If $f\in\mathfrak m$, choose a stage in which $f=u_ng(u_n)$ for a <polynomial> $g\in\mathbb Q[z]$. Then
$$
f=u_{n+1}\bigl(u_{n+1}g(u_{n+1}^2)\bigr).
$$
Both factors belong to $\mathfrak m$, so $f\in\mathfrak m^2$. The reverse inclusion holds for every <ideal>, proving that $\mathfrak m$ is an <idempotent ideal>. Hence
$$
\boxed{0\ne\mathfrak m\text{ is maximal},\qquad\mathfrak m^n=\mathfrak m^{n+1}=\mathfrak m\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
The <Noetherian ring> hypothesis in <2/c> is therefore essential even for a <maximal ideal>.

The <Krull dimension> of a <commutative ring> $R$ is
$$
\boxed{\dim R=\sup\{d:\text{there exist prime ideals }P_0\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq P_d\}.}
$$
The length of a <prime chain> counts strict inclusions, rather than the number of <prime ideals>.

Let $R$ be a <principal ideal domain> that is not a <field>. We first show that every nonzero <prime ideal> is a <maximal ideal>. Write such a <prime ideal> as $P=(p)$ with $p\ne0$, and suppose $P\subseteq(a)$. Then $p=ab$ for some $b\in R$. Since $ab\in P$, either $a\in P$, in which case $(a)=P$, or $b\in P$. In the second case $b=pc$ for some $c$, and $p=apc$ gives $1=ac$ by <multiplicative cancellation in an integral domain>. Thus $(a)=R$. This proves maximality.

Since $R$ is an <integral domain>, $(0)$ is a <prime ideal>, and every other <prime ideal> is maximal. Consequently every <prime chain> has length at most one. For the opposite inequality, choose a nonzero <nonunit> $a$, which exists because $R$ is not a <field>. The proper <principal ideal> $(a)$ is contained in a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$; existence follows from <Zorn's lemma> applied to the proper <ideals> containing $(a)$. A <maximal ideal> is a <prime ideal>, since its <quotient ring> is a <field>. Therefore $(0)\subsetneq\mathfrak m$ is a <prime chain> of length one. Thus the <Krull dimension of a principal ideal domain> that is not a <field> is
$$
\boxed{\dim R=1.}
$$

Let $P$ be a <prime ideal> of $R$ disjoint from the <multiplicatively closed set> $Y$. In the <localization> $S=Y^{-1}R$, its <extension of an ideal> is
$$
SP=\{p/y:p\in P,\ y\in Y\}.
$$
Indeed, finite sums of such fractions have a common denominator in $Y$ and numerator in $P$, and every generator $p/1$ has this form.

We show that its <contraction of an ideal> recovers $P$:
$$
\boxed{\{r\in R:r/1\in SP\}=P.}
$$
One inclusion is immediate. Conversely, if $r/1=p/y$ with $p\in P$ and $y\in Y$, equality of fractions in the <field of fractions> of the <integral domain> $R$ gives $yr=p\in P$. Since $y\notin P$ and $P$ is a <prime ideal>, $r\in P$.

If $SP_1=SP_2$, contracting would therefore give $P_1=P_2$, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence
$$
\boxed{SP_1\ne SP_2.}
$$
We will also need the fact that $SP$ is a <prime ideal>, part of the <prime ideal correspondence for localization>. It is proper by the contraction identity, since otherwise $1\in P$. If $(a/u)(b/v)\in SP$, multiplication by the denominator gives $ab/1\in SP$, and contraction gives $ab\in P$. Thus $a\in P$ or $b\in P$, so one of the two fractions belongs to $SP$.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that $P_2\cap\mathbb Z=(0)$. Then $P_1\cap\mathbb Z=(0)$ too. Both <prime ideals> are disjoint from the <multiplicatively closed set> $Y=\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$. The <localization> is
$$
Y^{-1}\mathbb Z[t]=\mathbb Q[t]:
$$
each fraction on the left has rational coefficients, and every <polynomial> with rational coefficients has a common nonzero integer denominator.

By <3/b>, the extended <prime ideals> form a strict inclusion
$$
\mathbb Q[t]P_1\subsetneq\mathbb Q[t]P_2.
$$
The first is nonzero, because $P_1$ contains a nonzero <polynomial> and the map $\mathbb Z[t]\to\mathbb Q[t]$ is <injective>.

The <polynomial ring> $\mathbb Q[t]$ is a <principal ideal domain>: in any nonzero <ideal>, a nonzero <polynomial> of least <polynomial degree> generates the <ideal>, since the <polynomial division algorithm> gives a remainder of smaller <polynomial degree>, which must be zero. By <3/a>, every nonzero <prime ideal> of this <principal ideal domain> is maximal. The strict inclusion of extended <prime ideals> is therefore impossible. We obtain
$$
\boxed{P_2\cap\mathbb Z\ne(0).}
$$

For any <prime number> $p$, there is a <prime chain>
$$
(0)\subsetneq(p)\subsetneq(p,t)
$$
in $\mathbb Z[t]$. These are <prime ideals> because the respective <quotient rings> are $\mathbb Z[t]$, $\mathbb F_p[t]$, and $\mathbb F_p$, all <integral domains>. The inclusions are strict because $p\ne0$ and $t\notin(p)$. Hence $\dim\mathbb Z[t]\geq2$.

To prove the upper bound, suppose there were a <prime chain> of length at least three. Since $\mathbb Z[t]$ is an <integral domain>, we could insert $(0)$ at its beginning if necessary and select a chain
$$
(0)\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq P_2\subsetneq P_3.
$$
By <3/c>, $P_2\cap\mathbb Z\ne(0)$. This intersection is a proper <prime ideal> of $\mathbb Z$: if a product of integers lies in it, primality of $P_2$ puts one factor in it. Therefore $P_2\cap\mathbb Z=(p)$ for a <prime number> $p$, and $(p)\subseteq P_2$.

We need to check that $P_2\ne(p)$. Here <prime ideals below a principal prime> give the necessary restriction: no nonzero <prime ideal> can lie strictly below $(p)$. If $0\ne P\subsetneq(p)$, then $p\notin P$. Choose $0\ne f\in P$ and factor out the largest common power of $p$ from its integer coefficients:
$$
f=p^kg,\qquad g\notin(p).
$$
Such a finite $k$ exists because $f$ has at least one nonzero integer coefficient. Since $p\notin P$ and $P$ is a <prime ideal>, repeated primality gives $g\in P\subseteq(p)$, a contradiction. Applied to $P_1\subsetneq P_2$, this rules out $P_2=(p)$.

It follows that $P_2/(p)$ is a nonzero <prime ideal> of
$$
\mathbb Z[t]/(p)\cong\mathbb F_p[t].
$$
As in <3/c>, the <polynomial division algorithm> makes $\mathbb F_p[t]$ a <principal ideal domain>, so its nonzero <prime ideals> are maximal by <3/a>. Thus $P_2/(p)$ is maximal. The <ideal correspondence theorem> makes $P_2$ a <maximal ideal> of $\mathbb Z[t]$, contradicting $P_2\subsetneq P_3$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim\mathbb Z[t]=2.}
$$
