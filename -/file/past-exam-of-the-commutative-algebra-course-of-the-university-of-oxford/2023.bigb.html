An <ideal> $\mathfrak q$ is $\mathfrak p$-<primary> if it is a proper <primary ideal> whose <radical of an ideal> is $\mathfrak p$. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{\mathfrak q}=\mathfrak p,\qquad ab\in\mathfrak q,\ a\notin\mathfrak q\ \Longrightarrow\ b^n\in\mathfrak q\text{ for some }n\geq1.}
$$
Equivalently, $ab\in\mathfrak q$ and $b\notin\mathfrak p$ imply $a\in\mathfrak q$: multiplication by every element outside $\mathfrak p$ is <injective> on the <quotient ring> $R/\mathfrak q$.

A <minimal primary decomposition> is a finite expression
$$
\boxed{I=\bigcap_{j=1}^t\mathfrak q_j}
$$
in proper <primary ideals> such that the <prime ideals> $\mathfrak p_j=\sqrt{\mathfrak q_j}$ are pairwise distinct and no <primary ideal> can be omitted. More explicitly, for every $j$,
$$
\bigcap_{i\ne j}\mathfrak q_i\not\subseteq\mathfrak q_j.
$$
Thus minimality means both irredundancy and distinct <radicals of ideals>; it does not assert that the <primary ideals> themselves are uniquely determined. For $I=R$, one may use the empty intersection convention.

= Solution

In the terminology of a <minimal primary decomposition>, an <associated prime of a primary decomposition> of $I$ is a <prime ideal> occurring as $\sqrt{\mathfrak q_j}$ in such a decomposition. The <first uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition> says that this collection is independent of the chosen <minimal primary decomposition>. Among these <prime ideals>, an <isolated prime of a primary decomposition> is minimal under inclusion, equivalently a <minimal prime over an ideal> $I$; an embedded <prime ideal> strictly contains another member of the collection. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{isolated}=\text{minimal over }I,\qquad\text{embedded}=\text{not minimal over }I.}
$$
When $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, these are precisely the <associated primes of a module> $R/I$. In that case the equivalent definition is
$$
\mathfrak p=\operatorname{Ann}_R(x+I)=(I:x)
\quad\text{for some }x\notin I,
$$
where $(I:x)$ is a <colon ideal>. The decomposition terminology applies when a <minimal primary decomposition> exists; a general <ring> need not provide one for every <ideal>.

Suppose first that $\mathfrak a$ is $\mathfrak m$-<primary>. Since $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, choose a finite <generating set of an ideal> $\mathfrak m=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)$. The equality of <radicals of ideals> $\sqrt{\mathfrak a}=\mathfrak m$ supplies positive integers $e_i$ with $x_i^{e_i}\in\mathfrak a$. Set
$$
n=1+\sum_{i=1}^d(e_i-1).
$$
Every product of $n$ generators has some $x_i$ appearing at least $e_i$ times. Such products generate the <product of ideals> $\mathfrak m^n$, so $\mathfrak m^n\subseteq\mathfrak a$. Also $\mathfrak a\subseteq\sqrt{\mathfrak a}=\mathfrak m$. If $\mathfrak m=0$, take $n=1$ directly.

Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak m^n\subseteq\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak m$. Taking <radicals of ideals> gives
$$
\mathfrak m=\sqrt{\mathfrak m^n}\subseteq\sqrt{\mathfrak a}\subseteq\mathfrak m.
$$
Hence $\sqrt{\mathfrak a}=\mathfrak m$. The only <maximal ideal> containing $\mathfrak a$ is $\mathfrak m$: any such <maximal ideal> contains $\sqrt{\mathfrak a}$ and therefore equals $\mathfrak m$. By the <correspondence theorem for ideals>, the <quotient ring> $R/\mathfrak a$ is a <local ring> with <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak a$. Every element outside this <maximal ideal> is a <unit>.

If $ab\in\mathfrak a$ and $a\notin\mathfrak a$, then $b$ cannot be outside $\mathfrak m$, since multiplying by its inverse in the <quotient ring> would give $a\in\mathfrak a$. Thus $b\in\mathfrak m$ and $b^n\in\mathfrak a$, proving the required <primary ideal> condition. We have proved the <maximal-primary ideal criterion>
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak a\text{ is }\mathfrak m\text{-primary}\iff\mathfrak m^n\subseteq\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathfrak m\text{ for some }n\geq1.}
$$

Put $A=\mathbb C[X,Y]$, $P=(X)$ and $Q=(X^2,Y)$. The <quotient ring> $A/P\cong\mathbb C[Y]$ is an <integral domain>, so $P$ is a <prime ideal> and hence a <primary ideal>. Put $\mathfrak m=(X,Y)$. Then
$$
\mathfrak m^2=(X^2,XY,Y^2)\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathfrak m.
$$
The <polynomial ring> $A$ is a <Noetherian ring> by the <Hilbert basis theorem>, and $\mathfrak m$ is a <maximal ideal> since $A/\mathfrak m\cong\mathbb C$. Therefore the <maximal-primary ideal criterion> from <1/c> makes $Q$ $\mathfrak m$-<primary>. In particular its <radical of an ideal> is $\mathfrak m$, distinct from $P$.

Neither <primary ideal> can be omitted: $X\in P\setminus Q$, while $Y\in Q\setminus P$. Thus the given intersection is already a <minimal primary decomposition>. To express the <ideal> by generators, write an element of $P\cap Q$ as $Xh=X^2u+Yv$. Reducing modulo the <prime ideal> $(X)$ gives $Y\bar v=0$ in the <integral domain> $\mathbb C[Y]$, so $v=Xw$. Cancelling $X$ in the <integral domain> $A$ gives $h=Xu+Yw$. Hence
$$
\boxed{J=(X^2,XY)=(X)\cap(X^2,Y).}
$$
By the <first uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition>, the <associated primes of a module> $A/J$ are exactly the two <radicals of ideals>. Their strict inclusion $P\subsetneq\mathfrak m$ gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ass}_A(A/J)=\{(X),(X,Y)\},\quad (X)\text{ isolated},\quad (X,Y)\text{ embedded}.}
$$
One can also see both <associated primes of a module> directly through <annihilators>:
$$
\operatorname{Ann}_A(Y+J)=(J:Y)=(X),\qquad
\operatorname{Ann}_A(X+J)=(J:X)=(X,Y).
$$
For the first <colon ideal>, reducing $hY\in J$ modulo $(X)$ forces $h\in(X)$, and the reverse inclusion follows from $XY\in J$. For the second <colon ideal>, cancel $X$ in $hX\in X(X,Y)$. Both represented elements of the <quotient module> $A/J$ are nonzero.

Because $(r)$ is a proper <prime ideal>, each $(r^k)$ is proper and its <radical of an ideal> is
$$
\sqrt{(r^k)}=(r)=\mathfrak p.
$$
Indeed, $(r^k)\subseteq(r)$ gives one inclusion of <radicals of ideals>, while $(rc)^k\in(r^k)$ gives the other.

We prove the saturation condition for a <primary ideal>: if $ab\in(r^k)$ and $a\notin\mathfrak p$, then $b\in(r^k)$. For $k=1$ this is the defining property of a <prime ideal>. For $k>1$, write $ab=r^kc$. Since $a\notin(r)$, the <prime ideal> condition gives $b=rb_1$. The element $r$ is a <non-zero-divisor>, so cancellation gives
$$
ab_1=r^{k-1}c.
$$
Induction gives $b_1\in(r^{k-1})$, hence $b\in(r^k)$. Together with the computed <radical of an ideal>, this proves
$$
\boxed{(r^k)\text{ is }\mathfrak p\text{-primary for every }k\geq1.}
$$

Since the <radical of an ideal> $\sqrt{\mathfrak q}$ is $(r)$, some positive power of $r$ belongs to $\mathfrak q$. Choose the least such exponent $k$. Then $(r^k)\subseteq\mathfrak q$ and $r^j\notin\mathfrak q$ for $0\leq j<k$, with $r^0=1$ excluded because the <primary ideal> $\mathfrak q$ is proper.

Take $x\in\mathfrak q$. If $x\notin(r^k)$, choose the largest $j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}$ for which $x\in(r^j)$, and write $x=r^ju$. Maximality of $j$ implies $u\notin(r)=\sqrt{\mathfrak q}$: otherwise $x\in(r^{j+1})$, a contradiction, including when $j=k-1$. The <primary ideal> condition applied to $r^ju\in\mathfrak q$ therefore forces $r^j\in\mathfrak q$, contrary to the choice of $k$.

Consequently every $x\in\mathfrak q$ lies in $(r^k)$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak q=(r^k),\qquad k=\min\{n\geq1:r^n\in\mathfrak q\}.}
$$
Together with <1/e/i>, this classifies the <primary ideals for a regular principal prime>. The exponent is unique: if $(r^k)=(r^{k+1})$, then $r^k=r^{k+1}c$, and cancellation of the <non-zero-divisor> $r^k$ would give $1=rc$, contradicting that $(r)$ is proper. No <Noetherian> hypothesis is needed.

The image $f/1$ is a <unit> in the <localization at a prime ideal> $R_{\mathfrak p}$, because $f\notin\mathfrak p$. The <universal property of localization>, applied to the <ring homomorphism> $R\to R_{\mathfrak p}$ and the <multiplicative subset> $\{1,f,f^2,\ldots\}$, gives a unique <ring homomorphism> fixing the image of $R$:
$$
\boxed{\theta_f:R_f\longrightarrow R_{\mathfrak p},\qquad \theta_f(a/f^n)=a/f^n.}
$$
Fixing the image of $R$ is exactly the required compatibility with the <algebra over a ring> structures. Uniqueness also follows directly: such a <ring homomorphism> must send $1/f$ to the inverse of $f/1$, and these elements together with $R$ generate $R_f$.

Choose $f=1$, so that $R_f=R$ and $f\notin\mathfrak p$. Consider the <kernel of a ring homomorphism>
$$
K=\ker(R\longrightarrow R_{\mathfrak p})
=\{a\in R:sa=0\text{ for some }s\notin\mathfrak p\}.
$$
Since $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, this <ideal> has a finite <generating set of an ideal>, say $K=(a_1,\ldots,a_t)$. For each $i$, choose $s_i\notin\mathfrak p$ with $s_i a_i=0$, and put
$$
f=\prod_{i=1}^t s_i.
$$
Then $f\notin\mathfrak p$ by the <prime ideal> condition, and $fK=0$. If $K=0$, take the empty product $f=1$.

If $a/f^n$ maps to zero under the <ring homomorphism> $\theta_f$ from <2/a>, then $a/1=0$ in $R_{\mathfrak p}$ because $f/1$ is a <unit> there. Hence $a\in K$, so $fa=0$. The <vanishing criterion in a module localization> now gives $a/f^n=0$ already in $R_f$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ker\theta_f=0,\qquad\theta_f:R_f\hookrightarrow R_{\mathfrak p}.}
$$
This is the <finite generation clears localization kernels> argument: the finite <generating set of an ideal> permits a single element outside $\mathfrak p$ to annihilate the entire <kernel of a ring homomorphism>.

For $n\geq1$, let $K_n=\ker(\phi^n)$, where the power denotes repeated composition of the <ring endomorphism>. These <ideals> form an ascending chain:
$$
K_1\subseteq K_2\subseteq K_3\subseteq\cdots.
$$
The <ascending chain condition> for a <Noetherian ring> gives $K_N=K_{N+1}$ for some $N\geq1$. Take $a\in\ker\phi$. Since $\phi$ is <surjective>, its iterate $\phi^N$ is <surjective>, so choose $b\in R$ with $\phi^N(b)=a$. Then
$$
\phi^{N+1}(b)=\phi(a)=0,
$$
so $b\in K_{N+1}=K_N$. Therefore $a=\phi^N(b)=0$.

Thus the <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $\phi$ is zero. This proves that a <surjective endomorphism of a Noetherian ring is an automorphism>, and in particular
$$
\boxed{\phi\text{ is injective}.}
$$

Use the <direct product of rings>
$$
T=\prod_{n\geq0}\mathbb R,
$$
with coordinatewise addition and multiplication and unit $(1,1,\ldots)$. Define the shift
$$
g(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots)=(a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots).
$$
Coordinatewise operations show that $g$ preserves addition, multiplication and the unit, so it is a <ring endomorphism>. It is <surjective> because $(0,b_0,b_1,\ldots)$ maps to any prescribed $(b_0,b_1,\ldots)$. Its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is
$$
\boxed{\ker g=\{(a,0,0,\ldots):a\in\mathbb R\}\ne0,}
$$
so it is not <injective>. This is the <noninjective surjective shift on a product of fields>.

This <ring> fails the <Noetherian> hypothesis in <2/c>. Indeed, let $e_n$ have a single $1$ in coordinate $n$. The <ideals> $I_n=(e_0,\ldots,e_n)$ form the strictly ascending chain
$$
I_0\subsetneq I_1\subsetneq I_2\subsetneq\cdots,
$$
since $e_{n+1}\notin I_n$.

A <ring> is <reduced> if its only <nilpotent element> is zero. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{x^n=0\text{ for some }n\geq1\ \Longrightarrow\ x=0,\qquad\text{or }\sqrt{(0)}=0.}
$$
The second expression says that its <nilradical> vanishes. A <reduced ring> may have <zero divisors>; for example, the <direct product of rings> $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ is <reduced> but $(1,0)(0,1)=0$.

Write $N=\sqrt{(0)}$. Every element of the <extension of an ideal> $NR_{\mathfrak p}$ is a fraction $a/s$ with $a\in N$ and $s\notin\mathfrak p$: a finite sum of such fractions has a common denominator and numerator still in the <ideal> $N$. If $a^n=0$, then $(a/s)^n=0$, so
$$
NR_{\mathfrak p}\subseteq\sqrt{(0)_{R_{\mathfrak p}}}.
$$
Conversely, let $a/s$ be a <nilpotent element> of the <localization at a prime ideal> $R_{\mathfrak p}$. For some $n\geq1$, $a^n/s^n=0$. The <vanishing criterion in a module localization> supplies $t\notin\mathfrak p$ with $ta^n=0$. It follows that
$$
(ta)^n=t^{n-1}(ta^n)=0,
$$
so $ta\in N$. Since $ts\notin\mathfrak p$,
$$
\frac as=\frac{ta}{ts}\in NR_{\mathfrak p}.
$$
This proves that the <nilradical commutes with localization>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Nil}(R_{\mathfrak p})=NR_{\mathfrak p}.}
$$
The multiplication by $t$ is necessary: the numerator $a$ of a <nilpotent element> in a <localization> need not itself be <nilpotent> in $R$.

If $R$ is <reduced>, its <nilradical> $N$ is zero. By <3/b>, every <localization at a prime ideal> has zero <nilradical>, so in particular $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is <reduced> for every <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$.

Conversely, suppose every such <localization> is <reduced>. Take a <nilpotent element> $x\in R$. Then $x/1$ is <nilpotent> in every $R_{\mathfrak m}$, hence zero there. To see directly that $x=0$, suppose otherwise and consider the proper <annihilator>
$$
\operatorname{Ann}_R(x)=\{a\in R:ax=0\}.
$$
Choose a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$ containing this <annihilator>. The equality $x/1=0$ in $R_{\mathfrak m}$ would imply $sx=0$ for some $s\notin\mathfrak m$, by the <vanishing criterion in a module localization>. But then $s\in\operatorname{Ann}_R(x)\subseteq\mathfrak m$, a contradiction. This is the principle that <localization detects zero elements>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{R\text{ is reduced}\iff R_{\mathfrak m}\text{ is reduced for every maximal ideal }\mathfrak m.}
$$
The argument also includes the <zero ring>, which is <reduced> and has no <maximal ideals>.

Let $N$ be the <nilradical> of $R$. By <3/b>, the <reduced locus of a ring> is
$$
\mathcal R=\{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R:NR_{\mathfrak p}=0\}.
$$
The <Noetherian ring> hypothesis makes the <ideal> $N$ finitely generated; write $N=(n_1,\ldots,n_t)$. Fix $\mathfrak p\in\mathcal R$. For every $i$, the <vanishing criterion in a module localization> gives $s_i\notin\mathfrak p$ with $s_in_i=0$. The product $f=s_1\cdots s_t$ lies outside the <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$ and satisfies $fN=0$. When $N=0$, take $f=1$.

For every <prime ideal> $\mathfrak q$ in the <distinguished open set>
$$
D(f)=\{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}R:f\notin\mathfrak q\},
$$
the element $f/1$ is a <unit> in $R_{\mathfrak q}$. The equality $fN=0$ therefore implies $NR_{\mathfrak q}=0$, so $R_{\mathfrak q}$ is <reduced>. Thus $\mathfrak p\in D(f)\subseteq\mathcal R$, proving that $\mathcal R$ is a <Zariski-open subset> of the <spectrum of a ring>.

More precisely, let $A=\operatorname{Ann}_R(N)$, the <annihilator of a module> $N$. The argument shows that $NR_{\mathfrak p}=0$ if and only if some $f\in A$ lies outside $\mathfrak p$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R=\bigcup_{f\in\operatorname{Ann}_R(N)}D(f)
=\operatorname{Spec}R\setminus V(\operatorname{Ann}_R(N)).}
$$
Equivalently, the complement is the <support of a module> $N$. This is <vanishing of a finitely generated module near a prime> applied to $N$: its finite generation lets the separately chosen denominators be combined into one element annihilating all of $N$.

Set $L=M/\mathfrak aM$, a <quotient module>. The <exactness of localization> gives, for every <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$,
$$
L_{\mathfrak m}\cong M_{\mathfrak m}/\mathfrak aR_{\mathfrak m}M_{\mathfrak m}.
$$
If $\mathfrak m\supseteq\mathfrak a$, the hypothesis $M_{\mathfrak m}=0$ makes this <quotient module> zero. If $\mathfrak m\not\supseteq\mathfrak a$, choose $s\in\mathfrak a\setminus\mathfrak m$. Its image in $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is a <unit>, so $\mathfrak aR_{\mathfrak m}=R_{\mathfrak m}$ and the displayed <quotient module> is again zero. Thus
$$
L_{\mathfrak m}=0\qquad\text{for every maximal ideal }\mathfrak m.
$$
For completeness, if $0\ne\ell\in L$, its <annihilator> is a proper <ideal>, and hence lies in a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. The <vanishing criterion in a module localization> shows $\ell/1\ne0$ in $L_{\mathfrak m}$: any element annihilating $\ell$ lies inside $\mathfrak m$. This contradicts the vanishing just proved. Hence $L=0$, and
$$
\boxed{M/\mathfrak aM=0,\qquad\mathfrak aM=M.}
$$
The use of <localization detects zero elements> is valid for every <module>; neither finite generation nor a <Noetherian> hypothesis is needed.
