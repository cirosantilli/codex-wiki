= Convergence of random variables
{wiki}

The main modes include <almost sure convergence>, <convergence in probability>, <convergence in Lp>, and <convergence in distribution>. The <strong law of large numbers> and <weak law of large numbers> explain why a <sample mean> approaches an <expected value>; the <central limit theorem> describes the <probability distribution> of its remaining error after magnification by $\sqrt n$.

= Contiguity of probability measures
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{title2=$Q_n\triangleleft P_n$}

= Contiguity
{synonym}

A sequence $Q_n$ is contiguous with respect to $P_n$ if $P_n(A_n)\to0$ implies $Q_n(A_n)\to0$ for every measurable sequence of events. It transfers negligible-event assertions, including <consistency (statistics)>, between changing sampling laws. It need not imply small <total variation distance>.

= Le Cam's first lemma
{c}
{parent=Contiguity of probability measures}

= Le Cam first lemma
{c}
{synonym}

Let $L_n$ be the <probability density function> of the absolutely continuous part of $Q_n$ relative to $P_n$. If $L_n$ converges in distribution under $P_n$ to $L>0$ almost surely with $\mathbb EL=1$, then $P_n,Q_n$ are <mutually contiguous>. Indeed, $\mathbb E L_n\le1$ and convergence to a mean-one limit imply <uniform integrability> and vanishing singular mass, giving forward <contiguity>. For reverse <contiguity>, $P_n(A_n)\le P_n(L_n\le c)+Q_n(A_n)/c$, and one first takes $n\to\infty$ and then $c\downarrow0$.

= Mutual contiguity
{parent=Contiguity of probability measures}
{title2=$P_n\triangleleft Q_n,\quad Q_n\triangleleft P_n$}

= Mutually contiguous
{synonym}

Two sequences of probability laws are <mutually contiguous> when <contiguity of probability measures> holds in both directions. A positive mean-one limit of their <likelihood ratio> is a standard sufficient criterion.

= Convergence in Lp
{title2=$X_n\to X\text{ in }L^p$}
{parent=Convergence of random variables}

Random variables converge in $L^p$ when $\mathbb E|X_n-X|^p\to0$.

= Convergence in L1
{parent=Convergence in Lp}
{title2=$\mathbb E|X_n-X|\to0$}

= L1 convergence
{c}
{synonym}

<Convergence in L1> means that the <expected value> of the absolute error tends to zero. It implies <convergence in probability> and convergence of <expected values>.

= Scheffé lemma
{c}
{parent=Convergence in L1}
{title2=$f_n\to f\text{ a.e.},\ \int f_n\to\int f\ \Longrightarrow\ \|f_n-f\|_1\to0\quad(f_n,f\ge0)$}

= Scheffé's lemma
{c}
{synonym}

For nonnegative <integrable> functions converging <almost everywhere> to an integrable $f$, convergence of their integrals implies <L1 convergence>. Indeed $\min(f_n,f)\to f$ almost everywhere and is dominated by $f$, so the <dominated convergence theorem> gives convergence of its integral. Use $|f_n-f|=f_n+f-2\min(f_n,f)$. This applies to <probability densities>, giving convergence in <total variation distance>.

= Convergence in L2
{title2=$X_n\to X\text{ in }L^2$}
{parent=Convergence in Lp}

= Mean-square convergence
{synonym}

Convergence in $L^2$ means $\mathbb E[(X_n-X)^2]\to0$. It implies <convergence in probability> by <Markov inequality>.

= Almost-sure convergence and convergence of second moments
{parent=Convergence in L2}

If square-integrable random variables satisfy $X_n\to X$ <almost surely>, then $X_n\to X$ in $L^2$ exactly when $\mathbb E[X_n^2]\to\mathbb E[X^2]$. Boundedness in $L^2$ and almost-sure convergence first give weak convergence in the Hilbert space $L^2$; weak convergence together with convergence of norms gives strong convergence.

= Convergence in distribution
{title2=$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$}
{parent=Convergence of random variables}

= Weak convergence of random variables
{synonym}

Random variables $X_n$ converge in distribution, or converge weakly, to $X$ when
$$
\mathbb E[f(X_n)]\longrightarrow\mathbb E[f(X)]
$$
for every bounded continuous function $f$. For real random variables this is equivalent to convergence of the distribution functions at every continuity point of the limiting distribution function.

= Atoms obstruct a continuous distributional limit
{parent=Convergence in distribution}

If $Z_n$ has probability at least p at $z_n$, with $p>0$ and $z_n\to z$, every weak limit has probability at least p at z. Apply the closed-set bound of the <Portmanteau theorem> to arbitrarily small closed intervals containing z and eventually $z_n$, then decrease their radii. Thus a persistent atom prevents convergence to a continuous law, including a chi-squared law. This provides a direct check when bounded-information estimation fails standard asymptotic-normal theory.

= Marginal weak convergence does not control sums
{parent=Convergence in distribution}

Let $Z$ take values $1,-1$ with equal probabilities. Set $X_n=Z$ for every $n$, and $Y_n=Z$ for even $n$, $Y_n=-Z$ for odd $n$. Both marginal laws are constant, but $X_n+Y_n$ alternates between the law of $2Z$ and a point mass at zero. Thus separate <weak convergence of random variables> does not determine a joint limit or a sum limit. The obstruction disappears when one marginal limit is constant, as in the <Slutsky theorem>.

= Skorokhod representation theorem
{c}
{parent=Convergence in distribution}

= Skorohod representation theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For <convergence in distribution> of random elements in a separable complete <metric> space, one can construct copies of the sequence and its limit on one probability space which converge almost surely. The copies preserve the individual laws; they need not preserve the original dependence between sequence elements. Suitable more general versions permit a separably supported limit in a <metric> space.

= Method of moments
{parent=Convergence in distribution}
{wiki}
{disambiguate=probability theory}

= Method of moments in probability
{synonym}

= Moment method
{synonym}

If all moments of real <random variables> converge to those of a moment-determinate limit law, then the variables converge to that law in the sense of <convergence in distribution>. <Moment determinacy> is essential; the <standard normal distribution> meets this condition.

= Weak convergence of probability measures
{parent=Convergence in distribution}

Probability measures $\mu_n$ on a metric space converge weakly to $\mu$ when $\int f\,d\mu_n\to\int f\,d\mu$ for every bounded continuous real function $f$.

= Compact containment
{parent=Weak convergence of probability measures}
{title2=$\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_n\mathbb P(\sup_{t\le T}|X_t^n|>R)=0$}

A family of path laws satisfies compact containment when for every finite horizon and every error tolerance there is a compact set which contains the whole path up to that horizon with at least the complementary probability, uniformly over the family. In Euclidean state space this is the displayed condition. It prevents escape to distant regions where local coefficient approximations give no control. A nonnegative <Lyapunov function> with uniformly bounded generator growth often proves it by the <optional stopping theorem>.

= Scaling limit of a random curve
{parent=Weak convergence of probability measures}

= Scaling limit
{synonym}

A <scaling limit> of random curves is a <weak convergence of probability measures> after spatial rescaling, usually modulo monotone reparameterization. Tightness specifies the topology and yields subsequential limits; identification of their laws gives a unique continuum model. <SLE> is a central family of such limiting curve laws.

= Skorokhod J1 topology
{c}
{parent=Weak convergence of probability measures}
{title2=$J_1$}

On càdlàg paths on a compact time interval, convergence in this topology means that increasing continuous bijections of the time interval can be chosen converging uniformly to the identity, while the corresponding time-changed paths converge uniformly to the limit. At a continuous limiting path this reduces to uniform convergence. It is a standard topology for weak limits of accelerated discrete-time chains, whose paths are step functions.

= Weak topology of probability measures
{parent=Weak convergence of probability measures}
{title2=$\mu\mapsto\int h\,d\mu\quad(h\in C_b(X))$}

This is the coarsest topology making all displayed bounded-continuous test integrals continuous. On a separable <metric> space it is metrized by the <bounded-Lipschitz metric>. This probability-<measure> topology should be distinguished from the weak topology of a Banach space.

= Bounded-Lipschitz metric
{parent=Weak topology of probability measures}
{title2=$\beta(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\|h\|_\infty+\operatorname{Lip}(h)\le1}|\mu(h)-\nu(h)|$}

The bounded-Lipschitz <metric> tests <probability measures> against a uniformly bounded Lipschitz class. It metrizes <weak convergence of probability measures> on separable <metric> spaces. With a complete compatible base <metric>, it is complete on the <probability measures> of a <Polish space>. Replacing the sum norm by the maximum norm changes the <metric> only by uniform constant factors.

= Prokhorov's theorem
{c}
{parent=Weak convergence of probability measures}
{wiki}

= Prohorov's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

On a Polish space, a family of probability measures is relatively compact for weak convergence exactly when it is tight. For finite measures, one additionally requires uniformly bounded total masses.

= Closed uniformly tight compactness criterion
{parent=Prokhorov's theorem}

A weakly closed <uniformly tight> family of Borel <probability measures> on a <Polish space> is weakly compact. Embed the underlying space into a compact <metric> closure, take subsequential pushforward limits, and use the closed-set <Portmanteau theorem> on the common tightness compact sets. The limit retains full mass in the original space; inverse continuity of the <probability pushforward embedding theorem> then returns the weak limit.

= Compactness of probability measures on a compact metric space
{parent=Prokhorov's theorem}

On a <compact metric space>, every sequence of <Borel probability measures> has a subsequence with <weak convergence of probability measures> to a <Borel probability measure>. To see this, choose a countable uniformly dense subset of continuous functions, extract a diagonal subsequence of their bounded integrals, and extend the resulting positive normalized functional to all continuous functions. The <Riesz representation theorem> supplies its <Borel probability measure>. This is useful for constructing an <invariant measure> from orbit <empirical measures>.

= Bounded moment criterion for weak convergence
{parent=Convergence in distribution}

For random variables supported in one fixed compact interval, convergence of every moment is equivalent to weak convergence. One direction tests monomials; the other follows by uniformly approximating continuous functions by polynomials.

= Moment determinacy on a compact interval
{parent=Bounded moment criterion for weak convergence}

Two <probability measures> supported on one compact interval are equal if all their moments are equal. Indeed, equality on monomials gives equality on <polynomials>, the <Weierstrass approximation theorem> extends it to every continuous function on the interval, and <compactly supported continuous functions determine a finite Borel measure>.

= Portmanteau theorem
{parent=Convergence in distribution}
{wiki}

= Portmanteau criterion
{synonym}

The Portmanteau theorem gives equivalent formulations of weak convergence, including inequalities on open and closed sets and convergence of expectations for every bounded measurable function whose discontinuity set has zero limiting probability.

= Continuous mapping theorem
{parent=Convergence in distribution}
{wiki}

If $X_n\xrightarrow dX$ and a measurable function $g$ is continuous at $X$ almost surely, then $g(X_n)\xrightarrow d g(X)$. The same principle holds for random elements of metric spaces.

= Law of large numbers
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{wiki}
{title2=$\overline X_n\to\mathbb E X_1$}

Laws of large numbers give conditions under which <sample means> approach a deterministic <expected value>. For <independent and identically distributed random variables> with $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$, the <strong law of large numbers> gives <almost sure convergence>, while the <weak law of large numbers> gives <convergence in probability>. These conclusions specify different modes of <convergence of random variables>, rather than a universal numerical accuracy guarantee. The <central limit theorem> is a separate result about the remaining fluctuations after rescaling.

= Almost sure convergence
{title2=$X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X$}
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{wiki}

= Almost surely
{synonym}

A sequence of random variables converges almost surely when the set of outcomes on which pointwise convergence fails has probability zero.

= Complete convergence of random variables
{parent=Almost sure convergence}

= Complete convergence
{synonym}

A sequence converges completely to $X$ when $\sum_n\Pr(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)<\infty$ for every $\epsilon>0$. The first Borel-Cantelli lemma implies almost sure convergence. For independent $X_n$, any limit in probability is almost surely constant; the second Borel-Cantelli lemma then gives the converse from almost sure to complete convergence. Independence is essential: $X_n=1_{\{U\le1/n\}}$ for one uniform random variable $U$ converges almost surely to zero but its exceedance probabilities have a divergent sum.

= Almost sure equality
{title2=$X=Y\ \mathrm{a.s.}$}
{parent=Almost sure convergence}

Random variables $X$ and $Y$ are almost surely equal when $\mathbb P(X=Y)=1$. Probability theory conventionally identifies random variables that differ only on a null event.

= Strong law of large numbers
{parent=Almost sure convergence}
{wiki}
{title2=$\overline X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mu$}

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be real <independent and identically distributed random variables> with $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$, and let $\mu=\mathbb E[X_1]$ be their <expected value>. Their <sample mean> satisfies
$$
\overline X_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mu.
$$
This is <almost sure convergence>: with <probability> one, an entire infinite sequence of observations has averages that eventually stay within every prescribed positive tolerance of $\mu$. The <variance> need not be finite. The <strong law of large numbers> implies the <weak law of large numbers>, which asserts <convergence in probability> at each increasing sample size.

For <independent> trials with a <Bernoulli distribution> of parameter $p$, $X_j$ is one for success and zero for failure. Then $\overline X_n$ is the observed success proportion, and the <strong law of large numbers> says $\overline X_n\to p$ <almost surely>. This turns a theoretical <probability> into a quantity one can estimate by repetition. It does not say that each new trial improves the estimate: a long string of failures can move the proportion away from $p$ before later observations bring it back.

{title=Bernoulli averages approach their mean: sample paths and exact error probabilities}

The <strong law of large numbers> gives the limiting location of the <sample mean>, while the <central limit theorem> describes its fluctuations when the <variance> is finite and positive. The two assertions can hold together:
$$
\overline X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mu,
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
$$
The first error shrinks to zero; the second has been enlarged enough to retain a nontrivial <probability distribution>. The second assertion is <convergence in distribution>, not a claim that the enlarged error along one sequence approaches a particular <normal random variable>.

The assumptions make a practical difference. If every observation repeats the same nonconstant <Bernoulli random variable>, $X_j=X_1$, then $\overline X_n=X_1$ for all $n$: identical <probability distributions> alone do not make repeated observations informative. For <independent> observations with a <standard Cauchy distribution>, the <Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution>, so it does not concentrate around a constant. Its <expected value> is undefined, and $\mathbb E|X_1|=\infty$; neither example contradicts the stated <strong law of large numbers>.

A brief proof outline in the case of finite <variance> uses the <Kolmogorov maximal inequality> for the centered partial sums. The <probability> that their maximum up to $2^m$ exceeds $\varepsilon2^m$ is at most $\sigma^2/(\varepsilon^2 2^m)$. These bounds are summable, so the <Borel-Cantelli lemma> controls all sufficiently large such blocks and hence every intermediate sample size. The general case $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$ requires truncation to control large observations; the argument using finite <variance> alone does not prove that full version.

= Strong law for uniformly L2-bounded uncorrelated random variables
{parent=Strong law of large numbers}
{title2=$\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n(X_k-\mathbb E X_k)\to0\text{ a.s.}$}

If $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|^2<\infty$ and the $X_n$ are pairwise <uncorrelated random variables>, then $n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^n(X_k-\mathbb E X_k)\to0$ almost surely. <Independence> is unnecessary for this version of the <strong law of large numbers>. A uniformly bounded <martingale difference sequence> meets its hypotheses, since the differences have mean zero and are orthogonal in $L^2$.

= Strong law for adjacent products
{parent=Strong law of large numbers}

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_j$ with $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$ and $m=\mathbb EX_1$, the <sample mean> of $X_jX_{j+1}$ tends to $m^2$ <almost surely>. Split into odd and even indexed products; each subsequence consists of <independent and identically distributed random variables> that are <integrable random variables>. Apply the <strong law of large numbers> separately.

= Kolmogorov convergence theorem
{c}
{parent=Almost sure convergence}

If independent centered random variables $(Y_n)$ satisfy $\sum_n\operatorname{Var}(Y_n)<\infty$, then $\sum_nY_n$ converges almost surely. It follows from Kolmogorov's maximal inequality and a Cauchy-tail argument.

= Law of the iterated logarithm
{parent=Almost sure convergence}
{wiki}

For independent identically distributed centered variables of variance one under standard moment assumptions, the law of the iterated logarithm describes the almost-sure envelope of their partial sums on the scale $\sqrt{2n\log\log n}$.

= Brownian upper law of the iterated logarithm
{parent=Law of the iterated logarithm}
{c}
{title2=$\limsup_{t\to\infty}B_t/\sqrt{2t\log\log t}\leq1$}

For standard <Brownian motion>, the <Brownian reflection principle> bounds the probability that its maximum up to $a^n$ exceeds $(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{2a^n\log\log(a^n)}$ by $2(n\log a)^{-(1+\varepsilon)^2}$. This is summable for every $a>1$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The <Borel-Cantelli first lemma> controls these geometric times, monotonicity of the normalizing function controls the intervening times, and countable choices $a\downarrow1$, $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ give the bound. Equality requires a separate lower-bound proof.

= Upper law of the iterated logarithm
{parent=Law of the iterated logarithm}
{title2=$\limsup_n\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}\le1$}

For a <simple symmetric random walk>, geometric blocking and the <Borel-Cantelli first lemma> yield the upper estimate $\limsup_n S_n/\sqrt{2n\log\log n}\le1$ <almost surely>. Equality requires a separate lower-bound argument.

= Law of the iterated logarithm for a simple symmetric random walk
{parent=Law of the iterated logarithm}

For a <simple symmetric random walk>, almost surely
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=1,
\qquad
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=-1.
$$

= Cramér–Wold theorem
{c}
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{wiki}

Random vectors $X_n$ converge weakly to $X$ exactly when every linear projection $u\cdot X_n$ converges weakly to $u\cdot X$. The reverse implication follows because these one-dimensional limits give pointwise convergence of the multivariate characteristic functions.

= Convergence in probability
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{wiki}

Random variables $X_n$ converge in probability to $X$ when
$$
\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\to0
$$
for every $\varepsilon>0$.

= Uniform convergence in probability
{parent=Convergence in probability}
{title2=$\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}|Q_n(\theta)-Q(\theta)|\xrightarrow{P}0$}

Uniform convergence in <probability> means that the supremum of the absolute discrepancy on the entire index set tends to zero in <probability>. It controls evaluation at random indices in that set, unlike pointwise <convergence in probability>. On a fixed compact index set it is a special case of <uniform convergence on compacts in probability>.

= Boundedness in probability
{parent=Convergence in probability}

= Bounded in probability
{synonym}

A sequence of <random variables> $(X_n)$ is bounded in probability when $\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_n\mathbb P(|X_n|>K)=0$. A sequence converging in <probability> to a finite <random variable> has this property, and its product with a sequence tending to zero in <probability> also tends to zero in <probability>.

= Stochastic order
{parent=Boundedness in probability}
{title2=$O_{\mathbb P}(a_n)$}

The notation $Z_n=O_{\mathbb P}(a_n)$ means that $Z_n/a_n$ is <bounded in probability> asymptotically: for every $\varepsilon>0$ some $C$ gives $\limsup_n\mathbb P(|Z_n|>Ca_n)\leq\varepsilon$. A tail bound $\mathbb P(|Z_n|>za_n)\leq C_0/z^2$ is sufficient. In statements involving bandwidths, specify whether the constants must be uniform over bandwidth sequences.

= Uniform pointwise probability bounds do not bound a random supremum
{parent=Stochastic order}

A bound on $\sup_x\mathbb P(|Z_n(x)|>t)$ does not give the same bound on $\mathbb P(\sup_x|Z_n(x)|>t)$. Even <independent> standard <normal random variables> each have uniformly bounded tails while their <Gaussian maximum> over $N$ indices grows at scale $\sqrt{\log N}$. This distinction matters in <nonparametric statistics> when a shrinking bandwidth permits many spatial windows.

= Convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability
{parent=Convergence in probability}

If $X_n\xrightarrow d c$ for fixed real $c$, convergence of the <distribution functions> at $c-\varepsilon$ and $c+\varepsilon$ shows $\mathbb P(|X_n-c|>\varepsilon)\to0$. Thus <convergence in distribution> to a deterministic limit implies <convergence in probability> to that limit, even though the implication fails for nonconstant limits on a specified <probability space>.

= Weak law of large numbers
{parent=Convergence in probability}
{wiki}
{title2=$\overline X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu$}

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be real <independent and identically distributed random variables> with $\mathbb E|X_1|<\infty$ and <expected value> $\mu=\mathbb E[X_1]$. The <weak law of large numbers> states that their <sample mean> has <convergence in probability> to $\mu$: for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\left|\overline X_n-\mu\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
\overline X_n=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nX_j.
$$
Thus a large sample is unlikely to give an average with a specified fixed-size error. The <strong law of large numbers> makes the stronger statement of <almost sure convergence> along an entire sequence. Under the displayed assumptions both laws apply; their names distinguish the conclusions, not two competing predictions.

When the <variance> $\sigma^2$ is finite, <variance additivity for independent random variables> gives $\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. The <Chebyshev inequality> immediately supplies a useful quantitative form and a brief proof:
$$
\mathbb P\left(\left|\overline X_n-\mu\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)
\leq\min\left(1,\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\right).
$$
For <independent> trials with a <Bernoulli distribution> of parameter $p=0.2$, the <expected value> is $0.2$ and the <variance> is $0.16$. At $n=1000$, the chance of an error of at least $0.1$ is therefore at most $0.016$. The exact <binomial distribution> gives a much smaller <probability>, as the figure under the <strong law of large numbers> shows. The bound is conservative, but it is valid for every sample size under these assumptions and does not require a <normal approximation>.

The <weak law of large numbers> addresses a fixed tolerance $\varepsilon$. The <central limit theorem> instead resolves errors on the shrinking scale $\sigma/\sqrt n$ and gives their approximate <normal distribution>. In particular, the <weak law of large numbers> alone supplies neither a bell-shaped error law nor a universal rate of approach. For example, a <Pareto distribution> with minimum one and shape $3/2$ has <expected value> three but infinite <variance>: both the <weak law of large numbers> and <strong law of large numbers> apply, while the finite-<variance> statement of the <central limit theorem> does not.

= Weak law without an absolute first moment
{parent=Weak law of large numbers}

Let $X$ have a <symmetric probability distribution> with $\mathbb P(|X|>x)=1/(x\log x)$ for $x\ge e$, mass $1-1/e$ at zero, and no mass with $0<|X|<e$. Then $\mathbb E|X|=\infty$, but independent copies satisfy the <weak law of large numbers> with limit zero. Indeed $n\mathbb P(|X|>n)=1/\log n\to0$. Their symmetric truncations $Y_{n,j}=X_j\mathbf1_{\{|X_j|\le n\}}$ have mean zero and second moment $O(n/\log n)$, so <Chebyshev's inequality> gives $n^{-1}\sum_{j\le n}Y_{n,j}\to0$ in probability. A union bound shows that the original and truncated sums differ with probability tending to zero. This also gives a differentiable <characteristic function> at zero whose derivative cannot be interpreted as an absolutely convergent expectation.

= Weak law forces a characteristic-function derivative
{parent=Weak law of large numbers}
{title2=$S_n/n\to a\text{ in probability}\;\Longrightarrow\;\phi'(0)=ia$}

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> with <characteristic function> $\phi$, suppose their <sample mean> converges in probability to a finite constant $a$. Bounded exponential tests give $e^{-iau}\phi(u/n)^n\to1$ uniformly on bounded $u$ intervals: the error is at most $T\delta+2\mathbb P(|S_n/n-a|>\delta)$ on $|u|\le T$. Taking the continuous <complex logarithm> vanishing at zero shows $n\log\phi(u/n)-iau\to0$ uniformly; an integer-valued branch difference is continuous and therefore zero. For arbitrary $h\to0$, put $n=\lfloor1/|h|\rfloor$ and $u=nh$, so $1/2\le|u|\le1$. Dividing the uniform limit by $u$ proves $\log\phi(h)/h\to ia$, and thus $\phi'(0)=ia$. No absolute first moment is assumed.

= Uniform law of large numbers
{title2=ULLN}
{parent=Weak law of large numbers}
{wiki}

A class $\mathcal G$ satisfies a uniform law of large numbers when its empirical means converge uniformly to their expectations:
$$
\sup_{g\in\mathcal G}|P_ng-Pg|\to0.
$$

= Pointwise separable function class
{parent=Uniform law of large numbers}

A measurable-function class is pointwise separable if it has a countable subclass such that every member is the pointwise limit of a sequence from that subclass. With an integrable common envelope, <dominated convergence theorem> makes both empirical averages and population expectations converge along each such sequence. Thus the supremum of the absolute empirical errors equals the supremum over the countable subclass and is measurable. A finite integrable bracketing cover provides an integrable envelope by taking the maximum absolute value of all its endpoints.

= Bracketing of a function class
{parent=Uniform law of large numbers}
{title2=$[\ell,u],\quad\|u-\ell\|_{L^1(P)}$}

= Function bracket
{synonym}

A bracket $[\ell,u]$ contains every measurable function $h$ satisfying $\ell\le h\le u$ pointwise. For a probability measure $P$, its $L^1(P)$ width is $P(u-\ell)$, with integrable endpoints. Finite bracketing at every positive width is a sufficient complexity condition for a <uniform law of large numbers>. Pointwise inequalities, or inequalities outside a single common null set, allow empirical inequalities to hold simultaneously throughout the class.

= Integrable envelope of a function class
{parent=Bracketing of a function class}
{title2=$|h|\le M,\quad PM<\infty$}

A measurable function $M$ is an <integrable envelope of a function class> for a class $\mathcal H$ under a probability law $P$ if $|h(x)|\le M(x)$ for every $h\in\mathcal H$ and $PM<\infty$. Such an envelope permits the <dominated convergence theorem> to control shrinking <function brackets>.

= Uniform strong law from finite L1 bracketing
{parent=Bracketing of a function class}
{title2=$\sup_h|P_nh-Ph|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0$}

Suppose an integrable measurable-function class can be covered by finitely many <function brackets> of every positive $L^1(P)$ width. For an independent sample with common law $P$, the <empirical measure> satisfies $\sup_h|P_nh-Ph|\to0$ on a common probability-one event. For one finite $\varepsilon$-cover, the supremum is bounded by $\varepsilon$ plus the largest empirical error among its endpoints. The <strong law of large numbers> makes that finite maximum vanish. Taking a countable sequence of widths decreasing to zero proves the assertion. = Quantile proof of the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem
{c}
{parent=Glivenko-Cantelli theorem}

For a <cumulative distribution function> $G$, let $Q(u)=\inf\{x:G(x)\geq u\}$, $0<u<1$. Right continuity gives $Q(u)\leq x$ exactly when $u\leq G(x)$, including at atoms. Thus applying $Q$ to independent uniform variables $U_i$ constructs an independent sample with distribution function $G$. This construction proves the theorem for distributions with atoms as well as continuous distributions.

= Large deviations of a scaled geometric random variable
{parent=Large deviation principle}

= Large deviations of a fixed sum of geometric random variables

= Fixed exponential perturbation of a Gaussian empirical mean

$$

= Exponential tightness upgrades a weak large deviation principle
{parent=Weak large deviation principle}

Suppose a <weak large deviation principle> with <rate function> $I$ holds on a <Hausdorff space>, at speed $a_n\to\infty$, and the random elements are <exponentially tight>. For a finite $r$, choose a <compact set> $K$ whose complement has upper exponential rate strictly below $-M$, with $M>r$. The open-set lower bound on $K^c$ implies $\inf_{K^c}I>M$, so $\{I\leq r\}\subset K$. <Lower semicontinuity> makes this <sublevel set> closed, hence compact. = Slower-speed degeneration of a large deviation principle

{synonym}

= Varadhan's integral theorem
{c}
{synonym}

If random elements satisfy a <large deviation principle> at speed $a_n\to\infty$ with <rate function> I, then a bounded continuous real function f satisfies the displayed exponential-integral identity. = Laplace principle
{c}
{synonym}

The exponential integral of a fixed measurable function is controlled, on its logarithmic scale, by the function's <essential infimum>. Suppose $0<\int_Ae^{-\phi}dx<\infty$ and $\alpha=\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_A\phi$ is finite. For $\theta\geq1$, the integral is at most $e^{-(\theta-1)\alpha}\int_Ae^{-\phi}dx$. = Laplace principle for probability measures
{parent=Laplace principle (large deviations theory)}
{c}
{title2=$\lim_n a_n^{-1}\log\mathbb E e^{a_nf(X_n)}=\sup_x[f(x)-I(x)]$}

A family satisfies this principle with speed $a_n\to\infty$ and <rate function> $I$ when the displayed variational identity holds for every bounded <continuous> real function $f$. A <large deviation principle> implies it by the <Varadhan lemma>. The lower bound restricts the <expectation> to a neighborhood of a chosen point; the upper bound partitions the bounded range of $f$ into small intervals and applies the closed-set probability bounds.

For nonnegative $X_n$, the identity also gives

$$
For $\mu>0$, truncate $f(s)=-\mu s$ below at $-\mu R$. The resulting bounded-function identity applies, while the difference of the two exponential <expectations> is at most $e^{-\mu Ra_n}$. Choosing $R$ sufficiently large makes this error negligible on the claimed exponential scale. The case $\mu=0$ is immediate. This extension identifies moment-decay rates of a <passive scalar> from fluctuations of its logarithmic stretching.

= Gärtner–Ellis theorem
{parent=Large deviation principle}
{c}
{title2=$I=\Lambda^*$}

Let $X_N\in\mathbb R^d$ and $a_N\to\infty$. Define the scaled <cumulant-generating function>
$$
\Lambda_N(\theta)=a_N^{-1}\log\mathbb E e^{a_N\langle\theta,X_N\rangle}.
$$
Suppose its pointwise limit $\Lambda$ exists, is <lower semicontinuous>, has $0$ in the interior of its effective domain, and is essentially smooth: it is differentiable on that interior, which is nonempty, and its gradient norm diverges at every finite boundary point approached from the interior. Then $X_N$ satisfies a <large deviation principle> with <good rate function> given by the <Legendre-Fenchel transform>
$$
\Lambda^*(x)=\sup_\theta\{\langle\theta,x\rangle-\Lambda(\theta)\}.
$$
The <Chernoff bound> supplies the upper estimate. The lower estimate uses <exponential tilting> at a parameter whose gradient selects the required mean; essential smoothness supplies the boundary approximation needed for the full lower bound. A finite boundary slope can leave an affine branch of $\Lambda^*$ outside this argument, so a separate lower-bound proof is then necessary.

= Product large-deviation principle
{parent=Large deviation principle}

Suppose $X_N$ and $Y_N$ are <independent random variables> in Polish spaces, each satisfying a <large deviation principle> at speed $a_N$, with <good rate functions> $I$ and $K$. Their pair has <good rate function> $I(x)+K(y)$. On open rectangles, <independence> makes the logarithmic lower bounds add. For the upper bound, the marginal families are <exponentially tight>, and so is their pair. Cover a closed set intersected with the product of the two compact containment sets by finitely many small rectangles and add the corresponding local upper bounds. The finite union bound and then increasing the containment level give the closed-set bound. Goodness follows because $\{I+K\leq M\}$ is closed inside $\{I\leq M\}\times\{K\leq M\}$.

= Exponential equivalence
{parent=Large deviation principle}

Coupled random elements $X_N,Y_N$ in a <metric space> are exponentially equivalent at <large-deviation speed> $a_N$ if for every $\eta>0$
$$
\limsup_N a_N^{-1}\log\mathbb P(d(X_N,Y_N)>\eta)=-\infty.
$$
If $X_N$ satisfies a <large deviation principle> with a <good rate function> $I$, then so does $Y_N$, with the same speed and <rate function>. For the upper bound, $\{Y_N\in F\}$ is contained in $\{X_N\in F^\eta\}\cup\{d(X_N,Y_N)>\eta\}$, where $F^\eta$ is the closed $\eta$-neighborhood. For an open-set lower bound, choose a ball about any $x\in G$ with a slightly larger ball still in $G$, subtract the superexponentially small error from the probability that $X_N$ belongs to the smaller ball, and shrink the ball. No <independence> assumption is required.

= Exponential tightness

A family with <large-deviation speed> $a_N$ is exponentially tight if for every $M>0$ there is a <compact set> $K_M$ such that

= Large-deviation speed
{parent=Large deviation principle}

A <large deviation principle> with speed $a_N\to\infty$ and <rate function> $I$ means that every open set $G$ and closed set $F$ satisfy

\limsup_N a_N^{-1}\log\mathbb P(X_N\in F)\leq-\inf_F I.

Multiplying the <large-deviation speed> by $c>0$ divides the <rate function> by $c$. The speed must match the rare-event scale: a fixed <exponential random variable> divided by $N^p$ has speed $N^p$, whereas a sum of $N$ <independent random variables> with a <normal distribution>, divided by $N^p$, has speed $N^{2p-1}$ when $p>1$.

= Good rate function
{parent=Rate function}

A <rate function> is a <lower semicontinuous> map $I:E\to[0,\infty]$. It is good if every sublevel set $\{I\leq M\}$ is a <compact set>. = A good rate function is separated from zero away from its minimizer
{parent=Good rate function}

If a <good rate function> has unique zero mu, every <closed set> missing mu has strictly positive <infimum> rate, possibly infinity. Otherwise a sequence of rates tending to zero lies eventually in the compact unit <sublevel set>; a subsequence converges to a point of the <closed set>, and <lower semicontinuity> makes that point another zero. Compactness also ensures existence of a zero whenever the <infimum> is zero.

= Freidlin--Wentzell action
{c}
{parent=Large deviation principle}
{wiki}

For a small-noise diffusion $dX=a(X)dt+\sqrt\epsilon\,\sigma(X)dW$, the Freidlin--Wentzell action of a smooth path is
$$
I[\phi]=\frac12\int(\dot\phi-a)^T(\sigma\sigma^T)^{-1}(\dot\phi-a)\,dt.
$$

= Geometric minimum action
{parent=Freidlin--Wentzell action}

On a zero-Hamiltonian infinite-time fluctuation path, the Freidlin--Wentzell action can be parametrized independently of time as $I=\int\|a\|ds-a\mathbin\cdot d\phi$ in the diffusion metric.

= Central limit theorem
{parent=Convergence of random variables}
{wiki}
{title2=$\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)$}

= CLT
{c}
{synonym}

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be real <independent and identically distributed random variables> with <expected value> $\mu=\mathbb E[X_1]$ and finite, strictly positive <variance> $\sigma^2=\mathbb E[(X_1-\mu)^2]$. Write $S_n=\sum_{j=1}^nX_j$ and $\overline X_n=S_n/n$ for their sum and <sample mean>. The <central limit theorem> states
$$
Z_n=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}
=\frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
$$
Precisely, for every real $z$,
$$
\mathbb P(Z_n\leq z)\longrightarrow\Phi(z),
\qquad
\Phi(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^z e^{-u^2/2}\,du,
$$
where $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution function>. The conclusion is <convergence in distribution> to the <standard normal distribution>. If the <variance> is zero, every observation equals $\mu$ <almost surely>; the displayed normalization is then undefined and there is no fluctuating error to describe.

The striking feature is that the observations need not have a <normal distribution>. They may be binary, bounded, or strongly skewed. Their detailed <probability distribution> disappears from this limit except through the <expected value> and <variance>. The subtraction removes predictable growth: $\mathbb E[S_n]=n\mu$. The division implements <variance normalization>: <variance additivity for independent random variables> gives $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$, so every $Z_n$ has <expected value> zero and <variance> one. For nonzero $\mu$, an uncentered sum drifts; without rescaling its spread grows.

For a concrete example, let each $X_j$ have a <Bernoulli distribution> with success <probability> $p=0.2$. Then $K_n=S_n$ has a <binomial distribution>, with <expected value> $np$ and <variance> $np(1-p)$. Its exact <probability mass function> and its normalized count are
$$
\mathbb P(K_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\ldots,n,
\qquad
Z_n=\frac{K_n-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}.
$$
Here $\binom nk$ is a <binomial coefficient>. Individual trials are conspicuously skewed, yet their centered, rescaled counts approach the same <standard normal distribution> as sums of fair trials.

{title=Exact standardized binomial distributions approach the standard normal curve}

Heights are mass divided by spacing, so they can be compared with the orange <probability density function> of the <standard normal distribution>. All four normalized counts have <expected value> zero and <variance> one. The rectangles visualize discrete masses; they do not make the discrete <probability distributions> continuous.}

At $n=100$, $K_n$ has <expected value> $20$ and <standard deviation> $4$. The exact <binomial distribution> gives $\mathbb P(16\leq K_n\leq24)=0.740141\ldots$. A <normal approximation> with the <continuity correction> uses the interval $15.5$ to $24.5$:
$$
\mathbb P(16\leq K_n\leq24)
\approx\Phi\left(\frac{24.5-20}{4}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{15.5-20}{4}\right)
=0.739411\ldots.
$$
The small discrepancy is an approximation error, not an equality supplied by the <central limit theorem>.

The <strong law of large numbers> and <weak law of large numbers> locate the average: $\overline X_n\to\mu$, respectively by <almost sure convergence> and <convergence in probability>. The <central limit theorem> magnifies that shrinking error and describes its shape. In terms of the <sample mean>, the resulting <normal approximation> is $N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$, with <standard error> $\sigma/\sqrt n$. This explains why four times as many <independent> observations halve the <standard error>. When $\sigma$ is known, $\overline X_n\pm1.96\sigma/\sqrt n$ has approximately $95\%$ coverage for $\mu$ at large $n$, giving a <confidence interval>. This coverage refers to repeated samples, not a guarantee for an individual interval.

A useful application is the <Monte Carlo method>: estimate a success <probability> by the proportion of successful simulated trials. Nicholas Metropolis and S. Ulam's original paper, "The Monte Carlo Method" (1949), illustrates this with games of solitaire and estimation of a region's volume by randomly selected points, and discusses why estimates require probabilistic error control. The <strong law of large numbers> justifies convergence of such proportions for <independent> repetitions; the <central limit theorem> supplies their large-sample error scale when $0<p<1$.\a[https://hedibert.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/1949MetropolisUlam.pdf][Metropolis and Ulam, The Monte Carlo Method, JASA 44 (1949), 335–341, especially pp. 336–337]{ref}

There are three useful limits to this picture:
* A <heavy-tailed distribution> can have infinite <variance>, so the displayed hypothesis must be checked. A <Pareto distribution> with minimum one and shape $3/2$ has finite <expected value> three but infinite <variance>. The <strong law of large numbers> still applies, but the stated <central limit theorem> does not. More dramatically, the <Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution>: even averaging need not concentrate when the <expected value> is undefined. A <heavy-tailed distribution> with finite <variance> is allowed by the stated <central limit theorem>.
* <Independence> matters. If all $X_j$ equal the same nonconstant <Bernoulli random variable>, then $S_n=nX_1$ and $\overline X_n=X_1$. There is no accumulation of new information, and the <variance> of the sum grows like $n^2$, not $n$. Other versions of the <central limit theorem> can allow dependence, but require hypotheses controlling it.
* <Convergence in distribution> is a limiting assertion, with no universal sample size or error rate under finite <variance> alone. It does not automatically give accurate relative estimates of very small tail <probabilities>. Nor does it mean that discrete point masses converge as <probability density functions>: a <binomial distribution> remains discrete for every finite $n$. If the success <probability> changes with $n$ so that $np_n\to\lambda$ and $p_n\to0$, the relevant result is the <Poisson limit theorem>, rather than the fixed-$p$ argument above.

A historical original-paper reference is J. W. Lindeberg's "Eine neue Herleitung des Exponentialgesetzes in der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung" (1922), published in Mathematische Zeitschrift 15, pp. 211–225. The <Lindeberg condition> and <Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem> below describe a version for sums whose summands need not share a <probability distribution>.\a[https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01494395][Lindeberg, Eine neue Herleitung des Exponentialgesetzes in der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung (1922)]{ref}

A brief proof outline uses the <characteristic function> of $Y=(X_1-\mu)/\sigma$. Its <expected value> is zero and its <variance> is one, so the finite <second moment> gives
$$
\varphi_Y(u)=1-\frac{u^2}{2}+o(u^2)\quad(u\to0).
$$
The <Taylor expansion> of the complex exponential, with its remainder controlled by $Y^2$, and the <dominated convergence theorem> justify this step without a third-moment assumption. <Independence> then gives the <characteristic function> of $Z_n$:
$$
\varphi_{Z_n}(t)=\left(\varphi_Y(t/\sqrt n)\right)^n
=\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+o(n^{-1})\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-t^2/2}.
$$
The limit is the <characteristic function> of the <standard normal distribution>; the <Lévy continuity theorem> turns this into <convergence in distribution>.

= Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem
{c}
{parent=Central limit theorem}
{title2=$\sum_i Z_{n,i}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma^2)$}

For each $n$, let $Z_{n,i}$ be independent centered <random variables>. If the sum of their <variances> tends to $\sigma^2>0$ and $\sum_i\mathbb E[Z_{n,i}^2\mathbf1_{\{|Z_{n,i}|>\varepsilon\}}]\to0$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, their sum converges in distribution to the centered <normal distribution> with variance $\sigma^2$. The second hypothesis is the <Lindeberg condition>. If the total variance tends to zero, the corresponding degenerate limit follows directly from the <Chebyshev inequality>.

= Lyapunov condition
{parent=Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem}
{c}
{title2=$s_n^{-2-\delta}\sum_i\mathbb E|Z_{n,i}|^{2+\delta}\to0$}

For independent centered <random variables> with total <variance> $s_n^2$, the Lyapunov condition requires the displayed expression to tend to zero for some $\delta>0$. The inequality $z^2\mathbf1_{\{|z|>\varepsilon s_n\}}\le |z|^{2+\delta}/(\varepsilon s_n)^\delta$ implies the <Lindeberg condition> after normalization. The <Lindeberg-Feller central limit theorem> then makes the normalized sum converge to the standard <normal distribution>.

= Gaussian tails force an unbounded normalized partial-sum limsup
{c}
{parent=Central limit theorem}
{title2=$\limsup_n S_n/\sqrt n=+\infty\quad\text{almost surely}$}

For independent identically distributed mean-zero variables with finite positive variance, the <central limit theorem> gives a positive eventual lower bound for $\mathbb P(S_n/\sqrt n\geq K)$ for every finite $K$. The decreasing events $\bigcup_{n\geq m}\{S_n/\sqrt n\geq K\}$ have probabilities bounded below, so <continuity from above of a measure> gives positive probability that the limsup is at least $K$. The limsup itself is unchanged by removing any finite initial sum, hence is tail measurable. <Kolmogorov zero-one law> makes each event “limsup at least $K$” certain; intersect over integer $K$. The result is false for zero variance. One should apply the zero-one law to the limsup, rather than assume the exact threshold infinitely-often event is unaffected by a vanishing change.

= Normal approximation
{parent=Central limit theorem}

= Gaussian approximation
{c}
{synonym}

A normal approximation replaces a sampling distribution by a <normal distribution>, often through a <central limit theorem>. In a <Wald test>, approximate <statistical power> is determined by the standardized difference under the alternative. This yields <Neyman allocation> for fixed treatment-specific <variances>.

= Continuity correction
{parent=Normal approximation}
{wiki}
{title2=$t\mapsto t-\tfrac12\text{ for an upper tail}$}

A continuity correction shifts a discrete probability boundary by half a lattice spacing before using a continuous <normal approximation>. For an upper-tail event $T\ge t$ on a unit lattice, the approximation is $1-\Phi((t-\tfrac12-E(T))/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(T)})$. Lower tails use the boundary $t+\tfrac12$. The correction is used in approximate <Wilcoxon signed-rank tests> and <Wilcoxon rank sum tests>; tied ranks also require the appropriate variance adjustment.

= Lindeberg condition
{c}
{parent=Central limit theorem}
{wiki}

The Lindeberg condition requires the contribution of unusually large summands in a triangular array to become negligible compared with the array's total variance. Together with convergence of that variance, it gives the Lindeberg central limit theorem.

= Hilbert-space central limit theorem
{c}
{parent=Central limit theorem}

For independent identically distributed centered random variables in a separable <Hilbert space> with finite second moment, $n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$ converges in distribution to a centered <Gaussian random element> with the same <covariance operator>.

= Self-normalized central limit theorem with a second-moment denominator
{parent=Central limit theorem}

For independent identically distributed $Z_i$ with mean zero and variance one,
$$
\frac{n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i}{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i^2}
\xrightarrow dN(0,1).
$$
The numerator obeys the central limit theorem, the denominator converges almost surely to one by the strong law, and Slutsky's theorem combines them.

= Fourth-moment tightness of polygonal random walks
{parent=Donsker's theorem}
{title2=$\mathbb E|S_t^N-S_s^N|^4\le\max(3,m_4)|t-s|^2$}

For centered unit-variance <independent and identically distributed> increments with $m_4=\mathbb E\xi^4<\infty$, an interpolated increment is $N^{-1/2}\sum_jc_j\xi_j$. Here $c_j$ is the length of the overlap of $(Ns,Nt)$ with the $j$th unit cell, so $0\le c_j\le1$ and $\sum c_j=N(t-s)$. <Independence> gives its <fourth moment> as $N^{-2}[3(\sum c_j^2)^2+(m_4-3)\sum c_j^4]$. This is at most $\max(3,m_4)N^{-2}(\sum c_j^2)^2$, and $\sum c_j^2\le N(t-s)$ gives the bound, including intervals crossing a mesh point. A uniform fourth-moment continuity criterion yields <tightness of probability measures> on $C_0[0,1]$.

= Random-walk maximum limit from Donsker invariance
{parent=Donsker's theorem}
{title2=$n^{-1/2}\max_{j\leq n}S_j\Rightarrow\sup_{s\leq1}B_s$}

For a <random walk> with centered <independent> identically distributed increments of <variance> one, the maximum of its polygonal interpolation is the maximum at its grid vertices. The maximum functional is Lipschitz for the <supremum norm>, so the <continuous mapping theorem> applied to the <Donsker invariance principle> proves the displayed <convergence in distribution>. The <Brownian reflection principle> then gives upper-tail limits $2(1-\Phi(a))$ at all $a>0$, since the limiting maximum has no atom there.

= Integrated random-walk limit
{parent=Donsker's theorem}
{title2=$n^{-3/2}\sum_{k=1}^nS_k\xrightarrow d\int_0^1B_t\,dt$}

For a <random walk> with independent identically distributed steps of mean zero and <variance> one, the <Donsker invariance principle> and the <continuous mapping theorem> for integration give this limit. The integral of the polygonal interpolation differs from the right-endpoint sum by $S_n/(2n^{3/2})$, whose squared <expectation> is $1/(4n^2)$. The <Slutsky theorem> removes that error. The limiting variable is the time-one value of <integrated Brownian motion>, with law $N(0,1/3)$.

= Multivariate central limit theorem
{parent=Central limit theorem}

For independent identically distributed random vectors $Z_i$ with mean $\mu$ and finite covariance matrix $V$,
$$
\sqrt n\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i-\mu\right)\xrightarrow dN(0,V).
$$
