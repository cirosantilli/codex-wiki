= Solution
{parent=1}

For hydrogen, the leading characteristic scales are
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm gross}\sim\alpha^2m_ec^2,\qquad
E_{\rm fine}\sim\alpha^4m_ec^2,\qquad
E_{\rm hf}\sim\alpha^4\frac{m_e^2}{m_p}c^2.}
$$
Order-one <spin> and nuclear magnetic factors, and state-dependent factors, are suppressed here. <Gross atomic structure> is Coulomb binding. <Fine structure> includes relativistic kinetic and spin-orbit effects, with <Thomas precession> affecting the spin-orbit coefficient. <Hyperfine structure> couples electronic motion and <spin> to the <nuclear magnetic moment>, whose inverse nuclear <mass> makes it much weaker. The contact <spin> interaction can remain in an s state where orbital spin-orbit coupling vanishes.

For <positronium>, the <reduced mass> is $m_e/2$. Its gross levels and separation scale are
$$
\boxed{E_n=-\frac{\alpha^2m_ec^2}{4n^2}\simeq-\frac{6.80\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^2},
\qquad a_{\rm Ps}=2a_0.}
$$
Thus the mean ground-state <electron>–<positron> separation is twice the infinite-proton-mass hydrogen value: both means have the same factor $3/2$ multiplying their respective Bohr radii. Retaining <proton> recoil makes the ratio $2m_p/(m_p+m_e)$, very slightly below two. Separation is not the distance of either <positronium> constituent from its <centre of mass>.

Interpret the <spin> operators in units of $\hbar$, as required by the printed Zeeman term, so $A$ has units of <energy>. Two <spin> halves give **four eigenstates**. At zero field, total <spin> $F=0,1$ and projection $m_F$ label the singlet and triplet. At high field relative to the hyperfine scale, the individual projections $m_{s,e},m_{s,p}=\pm1/2$ become approximately good <quantum numbers>. Total projection remains exact at every field, but $F^2$ does not for the two mixed states.

Choose the quantization axis along $\mathbf B$. The aligned-spin states $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$ and $|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$ both have $E=A/4$ because their Zeeman shifts cancel. In the other product-state sector,
$$
H_0=\begin{pmatrix}-A/4+2\mu_BB&A/2\\A/2&-A/4-2\mu_BB\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boxed{E_\pm=-\frac A4\pm\frac12\sqrt{A^2+16\mu_B^2B^2}.}
$$
For physical <positronium> $A>0$. At $B=0$ there is a triplet at $A/4$ and a singlet at $-3A/4$. At large positive field the mixed states approach $|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$ and $|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$, with energies $-A/4+2\mu_BB$ and $-A/4-2\mu_BB$ respectively, while the aligned pair remains degenerate at $A/4$.

\Image[paper-1-aop-positronium-levels.png]
{title=Four positronium spin states: an exactly degenerate aligned pair and two mixed branches}

The dimensionless mixing parameter is $x=4\mu_BB/A$. A crossover estimate is $x\sim1$, rather than a sharp transition. The scale $A\sim\alpha^4m_ec^2$ already gives a field of a few tesla. Using the physical hyperfine interval $A/h\simeq203.39\,\mathrm{GHz}$ gives
$$
\boxed{B_\times\sim\frac{A}{4\mu_B}\simeq3.63\,\mathrm T.}
$$
The numerical hyperfine interval is external physical input, not a number supplied by the symbolic <Hamiltonian>.<New Precision Measurement of Hyperfine Splitting of Positronium by A. Ishida and others>[arXiv:1310.6923, abstract, measured interval approximately 203.39 GHz]{ref} The high-field limit here still presumes that orbital distortion is negligible compared with Coulomb binding.

Set the ground-state <energy> to zero. Bose-Einstein occupation of an excited level is $[e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1]^{-1}$, with $\mu\le0$. As particles are added, $\mu$ approaches zero and the excited-state population saturates. Separating the ground state from the density-of-states integral therefore gives
$$
\boxed{N_c(T)=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon}{e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1},
\qquad\frac{N_0}{N}=1-\frac1\eta.}
$$
The excited-state integral must actually converge: $g\to0$ alone is not a sufficient condition in complete generality. A <power> law $g\propto\epsilon^s$ with $s>0$ suffices at low <energy>, as in the stated three-dimensional box.

Let $\mathcal C=Vm^{3/2}/(\sqrt2\pi^2\hbar^3)$ denote the density-of-states prefactor, not a <heat capacity>. Substitution $x=\epsilon/(k_BT)$ gives
$$
N_c=\mathcal C(k_BT)^{3/2}\Gamma(3/2)\zeta(3/2)
=\boxed{\zeta(3/2)\frac V{\lambda^3}}.
$$
The condensate has zero <energy> on this reference, so a further factor of $\epsilon$ in the integral gives
$$
E=\mathcal C(k_BT)^{5/2}\Gamma(5/2)\zeta(5/2),
\qquad
\boxed{\frac EN=\frac32\frac{N_c}{N}
\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}k_BT.}
$$
Relative to the classical value, the ratio is $\zeta(5/2)/[\eta\zeta(3/2)]\simeq0.5134/\eta<0.5134$. Macroscopic occupation of the zero-energy state and the Bose distribution both lower the average <energy>.

At fixed volume the condensed gas has $E\propto T^{5/2}$. Unbiased random loss keeps average $E/N$ constant, hence
$$
\boxed{T\propto N^{2/5}.}
$$
Removing a condensate atom removes essentially no <energy> and by itself leaves the excited cloud's <temperature> unchanged. Removing an excited atom removes its <energy>; during re-equilibration, some zero-energy condensate atoms join the excited population and share the remaining <energy>, lowering <temperature>. Averaging over unbiased loss gives the stated constant <energy> per particle, even though each individual type of loss need not preserve it separately.

Using $m\simeq87u$ and the supplied volume,
$$
\lambda_i\simeq0.837\,\mathrm{\mu m},\quad
N_c(T_i)\simeq1.78\times10^5,\quad
\boxed{\eta_i\simeq1.68.}
$$
There is only one occupied internal <spin> state, so no factor $2F+1$ belongs in the <density of states>. During loss, $\eta\propto N/T^{3/2}\propto N^{2/5}$, hence $\eta/\eta_i=T/T_i$. Condensation disappears at $\eta_f=1$:
$$
\boxed{T_f=\frac{T_i}{\eta_i}\simeq29.7\,\mathrm{nK}.}
$$
As a consistency check, $N_f=N_i\eta_i^{-5/2}\simeq8.15\times10^4$ agrees with $N_c(T_f)$.

Both this process and <evaporative cooling of atoms> require particle loss and re-equilibration. Ordinary evaporation preferentially removes energetic atoms, reducing $E/N$, and can cool an uncondensed classical gas. Here loss is <energy> independent, $E/N$ stays constant, and cooling relies on the condensed gas's unusual <equation of state>; random loss of a classical <ideal gas> would leave its <temperature> unchanged. Exact noninteraction would prevent collisional rethermalization: the imposed equilibrium assumption can be understood as weak thermalizing interactions whose equation-of-state corrections are neglected.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <electric dipole transition> has amplitude proportional to $\langle f|\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon|i\rangle$. The <electric dipole operator> is odd under <parity>, so the initial and final states must have **opposite <parity>**. As a rank-one spherical <tensor> it also requires **$\Delta J=0,\pm1$, excluding $J=0\leftrightarrow0$, and $\Delta m_J=0,\pm1$**. These follow from angular-momentum addition and the <Wigner-Eckart theorem>; vanishing <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> can impose further restrictions. Relative to the quantization axis, $\pi$ <polarization> drives $\Delta m=0$, while $\sigma^\pm$ drive $\Delta m=\pm1$.

For a single active <electron>, <parity> and the orbital triangle rule combine to give $\Delta l=\pm1$. In <LS coupling>, the spin-independent electric dipole operator gives $\Delta S=0$ and $\Delta L=0,\pm1$, excluding $L=0\leftrightarrow0$; total <parity> must still change. Spin-orbit mixing can weakly relax the approximate <spin> rule. For resolved hyperfine states the corresponding triangle rule is $\Delta F=0,\pm1$, excluding $F=0\leftrightarrow0$, with $\Delta m_F=0,\pm1$. These labels require the appropriate coupling regime. There is no universal E1 rule demanding a particular change in <principal quantum number>. An E1-forbidden line may occur through another multipole or a multiphoton process.

= Solution

The two-level Doppler limit, $k_BT_D=\hbar\Gamma/2$, balances velocity-dependent scattering friction with <momentum> diffusion. Multilevel atoms have additional, slower internal dynamics. Counterpropagating beams with <orthogonal> linear polarizations create a spatially varying <polarization>, giving different position-dependent light shifts for ground-state magnetic sublevels. A moving atom climbs a light-shift hill, loses <kinetic energy> and is optically pumped into a lower-energy sublevel: this repeated <Sisyphus cooling> cycle dissipates motion through emitted light.

<Optical pumping> responds with a finite delay. Its rate, rather than only the excited-state linewidth, sets the low-velocity response scale. In the opposite-circular-polarization arrangement, motion instead produces unequal sublevel populations and unbalanced <radiation pressure>. Both mechanisms can cool below the two-level Doppler <temperature>; diffusion heating still balances the cooling, and recoil matters at the lowest temperatures.<Laser cooling below the Doppler limit by polarization gradients: simple theoretical models by Jean Dalibard and Claude Cohen-Tannoudji>[1989, pages 2023–2025, two polarization-gradient mechanisms and pumping timescale]{ref}

**Ground-state substructure, <optical pumping> and <polarization> gradients enable the extra cooling.** Magnetic fields that disturb the required sublevel dynamics can suppress it. <Optical molasses> damps <velocity> but does not by itself provide position-restoring confinement.

**Sound propagation.** In the 1997 experiment, a focused laser beam abruptly modified the trapping potential of a dilute condensate, producing a local excitation. A rapid sequence of nondestructive <phase-contrast imaging> measurements followed the resulting <density> motion. Measuring propagation <speed> at different densities tested the sound-like collective excitation spectrum, rather than individual free-particle flight.<Propagation of Sound in a Bose-Einstein Condensate by M. R. Andrews and others>[1997, primary abstract and author publication record; Physical Review Letters 79, 549–552, with 1998 erratum]{ref} For a weakly interacting uniform condensate, the <Bogoliubov spectrum> is
$$
\epsilon(p)=\sqrt{\frac{p^2}{2m}\left(\frac{p^2}{2m}+2gn\right)},
\qquad c_s=\lim_{p\to0}\frac{\epsilon(p)}p=\sqrt{\frac{gn}{m}}.
$$
<Density> dependence and trapped-geometry averaging matter when comparing a measured pulse <speed> with this local uniform formula. A strictly <ideal gas> would have no nonzero interaction-supported sound <speed>.

**Moving-obstacle dissipation.** In the 1999 experiment, a blue-detuned focused beam repelled atoms and acted as an obstacle swept through a sodium condensate. Heating was inferred from the <condensate fraction> after stirring and re-equilibration. The heating rose markedly near $1.6\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$, below the quoted peak sound <speed> $6.2\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$.<Evidence for a Critical Velocity in a Bose-Einstein Condensed Gas by C. Raman and others>[pages 2502–2504, obstacle method, heating onset and comparison with sound]{ref} Landau's criterion $v_L=\inf_p\epsilon(p)/p$ motivates a dissipation threshold, but <density> variation, flow acceleration around the obstacle and <vortex> nucleation make the observed threshold different from simply substituting the peak sound <speed>. **Together these experiments probe a collective excitation spectrum and the onset of dissipative flow; condensation alone is not a complete demonstration of <superfluidity>.**
