Use the <condensate wavefunction> convention $\int |\psi|^2\,d^3r=N$, so $n(\mathbf r)=|\psi(\mathbf r)|^2$ is the <number density> and $\psi/\sqrt N$ is a normalized one-particle orbital. The <contact interaction> strength is $U_0=4\pi\hbar^2a/m$ for dilute atoms with <s-wave scattering length> $a$. The <chemical potential> $\mu=\partial E/\partial N$ fixes the stationary time dependence $e^{-i\mu t/\hbar}$ and enforces normalization when minimizing the <energy>. The <Gross–Pitaevskii equation> follows from an <energy functional> that contains the pair-counting factor $1/2$:
$$
E[\psi]=\int\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2+V|\psi|^2+\frac{U_0}{2}|\psi|^4\right]d^3r.
$$
Varying $E-\mu\int|\psi|^2d^3r$ gives the stationary <Gross–Pitaevskii equation>; the interaction contribution to that equation is $U_0 n\psi$, without the factor $1/2$.

For the broadening discussed here assume **repulsive interactions**, $a>0$. The <Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate> neglects the <gradient> <kinetic energy> compared with the trapping and interaction <energies>. At fixed spatial scale, increasing $N U_0$ increases the mean-field potential. Expansion then reduces the <kinetic energy> further: for a cloud radius $R$, its scale per atom is $\hbar^2/(mR^2)$, whereas the trap <energy> scale is $m\omega^2R^2$. Their ratio decreases as $(a_0/R)^4$. In the weak-interaction limit the <kinetic energy> instead balances confinement, giving the <Gaussian wave packet> <ground state> of the <quantum harmonic oscillator>.

The <spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate> has
$$
 n_{\rm TF}(r)=\frac{\mu-\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2}{U_0}\quad(0\leq r\leq R),\qquad n_{\rm TF}(r)=0\quad(r>R),\qquad \mu=\tfrac12m\omega^2R^2.
$$
Integrating this <density> gives $N=8\pi\mu R^3/(15U_0)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=a_0(15Na/a_0)^{1/5},\qquad \mu_{\rm TF}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}(15Na/a_0)^{2/5}.}
$$
At fixed $N$, stronger repulsion produces a wider, lower <wavefunction>: the <Gaussian wave packet> crosses over towards the <square root> of an inverted <parabola>, with an almost flat central region and a steeper outer drop. The *<density>* is the inverted <parabola>; the *<wavefunction>* is its <square root>. The exact <wavefunction> remains smooth and has a tail, since <kinetic energy> cannot be neglected near the <Thomas–Fermi condensate edge rounding> region. The <healing length> must be small compared with the cloud size in the bulk. Attractive interactions would not follow this large-$N$ expansion argument.

The original plot compares the noninteracting endpoint with two normalized repulsive <spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate> profiles. The latter are asymptotic shapes, rather than exact intermediate solutions of the <Gross–Pitaevskii equation>. Each has the same integrated atom number; $\chi=Na/a_0$ labels the illustrative interaction strengths.

{title=Condensate broadening}

Choose $A$ real and positive. The <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
N=A^2\pi^{3/2}a_0^3,\qquad
\boxed{A=\frac{\sqrt N}{\pi^{3/4}a_0^{3/2}},\quad \mu_0=\frac32\hbar\omega,\quad E_0=\frac32N\hbar\omega.}
$$
The three <zero-point energies> add for each atom. The ideal-gas <chemical potential> is a one-atom <energy>; the total <energy> includes all $N$ atoms.

For weak interactions, the first-order correction in <perturbation theory> is the <energy functional> of the <contact interaction> evaluated on the unperturbed <Gaussian wave packet>. The <integral> needed is
$$
\int |\psi_0|^4d^3r=\frac{N^2}{\pi^3a_0^6}\left(\frac{\pi a_0^2}{2}\right)^{3/2}=\frac{N^2}{(2\pi)^{3/2}a_0^3}.
$$
Consequently the <Gaussian interaction energy of a trapped condensate> is
$$
\boxed{E_I=\frac{N^2U_0}{2(2\pi)^{3/2}a_0^3},\qquad \frac{E_I}{\hbar\omega}=\frac{N^2a}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_0}.}
$$
Here $\hbar\omega=\hbar^2/(ma_0^2)$. These are the mean-field expressions consistent with the supplied equation and $\int|\psi|^2=N$. Exact first-order <perturbation theory> for precisely $N$ atoms in one orbital replaces $N^2$ by $N(N-1)$ through the number of distinct pairs; this distinction is negligible in the large-$N$ regime of the crossover estimate.

To judge the ideal-gas approximation, compare the interaction <energy> with the *total* ideal <energy>, or compare the interaction <energy> per atom with $\hbar\omega$:
$$
\frac{E_I}{E_0}=\frac{2}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{Na}{a_0}.
$$
Thus the <crossover from Gaussian to Thomas–Fermi condensate> is governed by $\chi=Na/a_0$: <perturbation theory> is reliable for $\chi\ll1$, while the regime of the <Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate> requires $\chi\gg1$. **The characteristic crossover number is $N_c\sim a_0/a$; there is no sharp critical atom number here.** If one specifically defines crossover by $E_I=E_0$ using the weak-interaction expression, it gives $N_c=(3\sqrt{2\pi}/2)a_0/a$. That prefactor is a convention-dependent estimate extrapolated to the edge of <perturbation theory>, not a phase transition. Comparing $E_I$ itself to a single $\hbar\omega$ would incorrectly omit a factor of $N$.

For the numerical estimate use $m\simeq87\times1.66054\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $\hbar\simeq1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$. Then
$$
a_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m(2\pi\times50\,\mathrm{s^{-1}})}}\simeq1.52\,\mathrm{\mu m},\qquad
\boxed{N_c\sim\frac{a_0}{a}\simeq3.0\times10^2\ \text{atoms}.}
$$
The particular equality $E_I=E_0$ would instead give about $1.15\times10^3$ atoms. These quantify different choices within a smooth crossover; a well-developed <spherical Thomas–Fermi condensate> profile requires going substantially beyond the characteristic scale.

Fix the <qubit polarization> convention by $\rho=(I+\vec P\cdot\vec\sigma)/2$, where the <Pauli matrices> are ordered in the basis $(|1\rangle,|2\rangle)$ and $\sigma_z=|1\rangle\langle1|-|2\rangle\langle2|$. Thus the <ground state> is the north pole of the <Bloch sphere>. In a <rotating frame> and within the <rotating-wave approximation>, choose the drive phase and detuning sign so that
$$
H_R=\frac\hbar2(\Omega\sigma_x+\Delta\sigma_z),\qquad
\dot{\vec P}=(\Omega,0,\Delta)\times\vec P.
$$
For example, with this ground-state convention one may take $\Delta=\omega_L-\omega_0$ and choose the transverse coupling positive. The <Bloch vector> precesses around the effective field $(\Omega,0,\Delta)$ at <angular frequency> $\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}$, tracing a circle at fixed projection on that axis. Its length is conserved under coherent <unitary time evolution>. On resonance the axis is transverse and the <Rabi oscillation> can fully invert an initially ground-state atom. Away from resonance the axis tilts, and
$$
P_{1\to2}(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}\right).
$$
There is no relaxation towards an equilibrium <Bloch vector> in this ideal model. An inhomogeneous ensemble can nevertheless lose its *averaged* <qubit polarization> through <dephasing> even without <spontaneous emission>; the circle description assumes a common drive and detuning.

For $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$, the stated <quantum state> gives
$$
\rho=|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|=
\begin{pmatrix}|a|^2&ab^*\\a^*b&|b|^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\vec P=(2\operatorname{Re}(a^*b),\,2\operatorname{Im}(a^*b),\,|a|^2-|b|^2).
$$
The diagonal elements are the two state populations. The off-diagonal elements are <quantum coherences>: they retain the <relative quantum phase> and determine <interference> under a subsequent pulse. **This is a <pure state>**, even if both populations are nonzero: $\rho^2=\rho$, $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=1$, and $|\vec P|=1$. A <mixed state> averages projectors over preparation <probabilities> and generally has $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2<1$. Equal populations alone do not distinguish an equal coherent <superposition> from an unpolarized mixture.

For use in all three cases, multiplication of the gates gives the <Hadamard-phase-Hadamard qubit rotation>
$$
U=HP_\alpha H=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+e^{i\alpha}&1-e^{i\alpha}\\1-e^{i\alpha}&1+e^{i\alpha}\end{pmatrix}
=e^{i\alpha/2}\exp(-i\alpha\sigma_x/2).
$$
The <global phase> cancels in $\rho'=U\rho U^\dagger$. Thus the <Bloch vector> map is
$$
\boxed{(P_x,P_y,P_z)\longmapsto(P_x,\,P_y\cos\alpha-P_z\sin\alpha,\,P_y\sin\alpha+P_z\cos\alpha).}
$$

The final unlabelled <Ramsey spectroscopy> comparison applies to the three cases as follows. In an <atomic clock>, an initial coherent pulse prepares a <superposition>, a free interval $T$ accumulates a <relative quantum phase> proportional to detuning times $T$, and a final coherent pulse converts that phase to a population. Here $\alpha$ plays the role of that accumulated phase, with sign and fringe offset set by pulse conventions. In (a), the first <Hadamard gate> creates the needed <superposition> and the final gate reveals full-contrast <Ramsey interferometry>. In (b), the first <Hadamard gate> instead puts the state in $|1\rangle$, so the intervening <phase gate> has no <relative quantum phase> to imprint: the output is independent of $\alpha$. In (c), the unpolarized <density matrix> remains unchanged under every gate, so the ensemble has no <Ramsey interferometry> contrast. A coherent <unitary gate> alone cannot initialize a maximally mixed ensemble into a pure clock state.

The <Ramsey interferometry> analogy concerns preparation, phase accumulation, and readout; **a <Hadamard gate> is not literally a transverse $\pi/2$ <rotation> on every input**. In this basis $H=R_y(\pi/2)\sigma_z$, and its <Bloch sphere> action is a $\pi$ <rotation> about $(\hat x+\hat z)/\sqrt2$, up to a <global phase>. The phase conventions of physical <Ramsey spectroscopy> pulses must be specified to compare their outputs for arbitrary initial states.

Following the <pure state> through the first two gates gives $(|1\rangle+e^{i\alpha}|2\rangle)/\sqrt2$. After the last <Hadamard gate> it is
$$
|\Psi_f\rangle=e^{i\alpha/2}\left[\cos(\alpha/2)|1\rangle-i\sin(\alpha/2)|2\rangle\right].
$$
Using the <qubit polarization> convention defined above,
$$
\boxed{\vec P_f=(0,-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha),\qquad p_2=\sin^2(\alpha/2).}
$$
The output remains pure and has unit <Bloch vector> length. The population oscillation is the <Ramsey interferometry> fringe: the first gate supplies <quantum coherence>, and the last turns the acquired <relative quantum phase> into a measurable population difference.

The first <Hadamard gate> maps the prepared <superposition> $|+\rangle=(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)/\sqrt2$ to $|1\rangle$. The <phase gate> leaves that state unchanged, and the last <Hadamard gate> returns it to $|+\rangle$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|\Psi_f\rangle=|+\rangle,\qquad \vec P_f=(1,0,0),\qquad p_2=\tfrac12.}
$$
This is a fully polarized <pure state>, not an unpolarized ensemble. Its <Bloch vector> lies along the <rotation> axis of the combined operation. There is **no <Ramsey interferometry> fringe as $\alpha$ varies**, because the state during phase accumulation occupies only one basis level. <Quantum coherence> in the *final* state alone does not guarantee phase sensitivity to the specified sequence.

For an unpolarized ensemble $\rho_i=I/2$. Every <unitary gate> satisfies $U(I/2)U^\dagger=I/2$, so
$$
\boxed{\rho_f=\tfrac12I,\qquad \vec P_f=(0,0,0),\qquad p_1=p_2=\tfrac12.}
$$
The <purity> remains $1/2$. Equal populations here come with zero <quantum coherence>, unlike the state in (b). There is **no <Ramsey interferometry> fringe**: the two orthogonal components of any equal-weight ensemble decomposition give cancelling population responses. <Quantum state preparation> that reduces the ensemble <entropy> is needed for the usual polarized clock interrogation.

**<Laser cooling> removes atomic <kinetic energy> through a velocity-dependent optical <force>, with scattered <photons> carrying away <energy> and <entropy>.** For two counterpropagating beams detuned below resonance, motion towards one beam shifts that light closer to resonance, so the atom preferentially absorbs <momentum> opposite its <velocity>. At small <velocity> the net <force> is $F\simeq-\gamma_v v$ with $\gamma_v>0$. Three orthogonal beam pairs form <optical molasses>. <Spontaneous emission> has zero mean recoil in a symmetric distribution, but its <momentum> variance is nonzero: random absorption and emission cause heating. The cooling and heating balance sets a finite <temperature>.

For a weakly saturated two-level transition, write $\delta=\omega_L-\omega_0<0$ and let $\Gamma$ be the excited-state population decay rate. A useful one-axis <Doppler cooling> expression is
$$
k_BT(\delta)=\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{4}\frac{1+(2\delta/\Gamma)^2}{-2\delta/\Gamma},\qquad
\boxed{\delta_{\rm opt}=-\Gamma/2,\quad T_D=\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2k_B}.}
$$
This limit belongs to the weak-intensity <Doppler cooling> model, with <recoil energy> small compared with $\hbar\Gamma$. Experimentally, released sodium molasses clouds were probed by time of flight; the unusually small expansion demonstrated <temperatures> below that limit. This distinguishes an observed failure of a two-level cooling model from a failure of <conservation of energy>.<Nobel Lecture: Laser cooling and trapping of neutral atoms by William D. Phillips>[pp. 729–732, Doppler limit, optical molasses, and release/time-of-flight thermometry]{ref}

Multilevel atoms permit <polarization-gradient cooling>: spatially varying light <polarization> produces different <light shifts> of ground-state sublevels. In <Sisyphus cooling>, atoms climb a light-shift potential and are preferentially optically pumped near its top into a lower-energy sublevel, repeatedly losing <kinetic energy>. The <optical pumping> and emitted light supply dissipation. This can reach below the <Doppler cooling limit>; the recoil scale
$$
E_R=\frac{(\hbar k)^2}{2m},\qquad T_R=E_R/k_B
$$
then becomes important. The definition of a recoil <temperature> sometimes differs by a factor of two, so the <energy> definition is the unambiguous comparison. Recoil is not a universal lower bound for every cooling protocol: suitable dark-state or <resolved sideband cooling> schemes can suppress scattering near the desired motional state. <Resolved sideband cooling> preferentially excites a transition lowering the vibrational <quantum number>, followed by decay that on average preserves it in the <Lamb-Dicke regime>.<Nobel Lecture: Superposition, entanglement, and raising Schrödinger’s cat by David J. Wineland>[Section IV, cooling of quantized ion motion]{ref}

<Optical molasses> supplies damping rather than a position-restoring <force>. A <magneto-optical trap> adds a magnetic-field <gradient> and appropriately chosen circular <polarizations>; the position-dependent <Zeeman splitting> gives a restoring <radiation pressure> imbalance as well as damping. In real alkali atoms, repumping light returns population that would otherwise accumulate in an uncoupled hyperfine level. Magnetic and <optical dipole traps> can subsequently hold the cold sample without continuous resonant scattering.

**Applications include long-interrogation <atomic clocks>, precision <spectroscopy>, <atom interferometry>, and preparation for <quantum simulation> and <Bose-Einstein condensation>.** Cooling reduces motion-induced <frequency> shifts and allows longer coherent interrogation; deeper <quantum degeneracy> commonly needs subsequent <evaporative cooling of atoms>. Finite capture <velocity>, available transitions, recoil heating, stray <magnetic fields>, and loss or heating from unwanted scattering constrain a real experiment.

**A <quantum computing> apparatus needs a scalable <quantum register> of controllable <qubits>, reproducible initialization, a <universal quantum gate set>, sufficiently long <quantum coherence>, and selective readout.** A <qubit> is a two-dimensional state space; several <qubits> occupy a <tensor product> space and can develop <entanglement>. <Quantum state preparation> must establish a known low-entropy input and supply fresh <ancillas> during <quantum error correction>. A <universal quantum gate set> may use arbitrary single-qubit <rotations> with an entangling two-qubit gate such as <controlled-NOT gate>. <Quantum coherence> and control fidelity must suffice for the intended circuit, and measurement must recover chosen output <probabilities> without uncontrolled disturbance of other <qubits>. These are the five <DiVincenzo criteria>.<The Physical Implementation of Quantum Computation by David P. DiVincenzo>[Section III, requirements 1–5]{ref}

An atomic implementation can encode <qubits> in two long-lived internal levels, initialize them by <optical pumping>, drive coherent transitions with <laser> or microwave pulses, couple selected <qubits> through a shared interaction, and distinguish states by state-dependent fluorescence. For trapped ions, a shared vibrational mode can mediate entangling gates, so motional control and cooling are part of the computational apparatus. The motion is a resource only while uncontrolled heating does not erase the required <quantum coherence>.

The computational resource is coherent control of amplitudes and <entanglement>, with <interference> making useful outcomes likely. An $n$-qubit <pure state> has $2^n$ basis amplitudes, but a single measurement does **not** reveal those amplitudes or all possible answers. Algorithms exploit structure rather than simply reading out parallel classical computations. The <Shor algorithm> gives factoring with polynomial resource scaling in the input bit length, compared with the best known classical algorithms' superpolynomial scaling; an exponential classical lower bound has not been proved.<Polynomial-Time Algorithms for Prime Factorization and Discrete Logarithms on a Quantum Computer by Peter W. Shor>[Abstract, polynomial running time in the number of input digits]{ref} <Grover's algorithm> uses order $\sqrt M$ oracle queries for unstructured search among $M$ possibilities, a quadratic query improvement.<A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search by Lov Grover>[Abstract and algorithmic query scaling]{ref} <Quantum simulation> is a further motivation because interacting quantum systems can require exponentially many classical amplitudes for a direct generic representation.

The main challenges are <quantum decoherence>, fluctuating control fields, imperfect gates, leakage out of the <qubit> subspace, readout errors, crosstalk, and the difficulty of scaling connectivity and calibration. Faster control can introduce extra errors, while stronger isolation can make preparation and readout harder. <Quantum error correction> encodes logical information redundantly in several physical <qubits> and extracts error syndromes without measuring the unknown logical state itself. <Fault-tolerant quantum computation> prevents the correction apparatus and gates from spreading a few faults into uncorrectable logical errors. It requires error rates below an architecture- and noise-dependent threshold and substantial resources; there is no universal numerical threshold for all implementations. **Advantages are task-dependent, while reliable scaling requires both coherent hardware and error control.**

**<Evaporative cooling of atoms> selectively removes atoms with unusually high total <energy>, then <elastic collisions> rethermalize those remaining.** Loss of particles alone is insufficient: the lost atoms must carry more than the mean <energy> per particle if the <temperature> is to fall. The process is open and dissipative, so it can increase the <phase-space density of an atomic gas> in the remaining sample while reducing its atom number. As the <temperature> falls, the cutoff must be lowered to keep replenishing a useful escaping tail; a fixed high cutoff eventually gives negligible evaporation.

An experimental sequence first loads laser-cooled atoms into a conservative trap and suppresses resonant-light heating. In a trap using <magnetic trapping of neutral atoms>, a <radiofrequency evaporation knife> drives a <Zeeman splitting> transition from a trapped weak-field-seeking state to an untrapped state on a surface where the splitting matches the radiofrequency <photon> <energy>. Only atoms energetic enough to reach that surface escape. Lowering the radiofrequency cutoff progressively removes less energetic atoms. Alternatively, lowering an <optical dipole trap>'s depth permits energetic atoms to spill out. This changes confinement as well as the escape cutoff, so it can reduce the collision rate. Radiofrequency-selective evaporation and the need for favourable elastic-collision and background-loss rates are documented in the original condensate experiments.<Nobel Lecture: Bose-Einstein condensation in a dilute gas, the first 70 years and some recent experiments by Eric A. Cornell and Carl Wieman>[pp. 880–882, elastic/background collisions, magnetic confinement, and radiofrequency evaporation]{ref}

The <elastic collision> rate $\Gamma_{\rm el}\sim n\sigma_{\rm el}\bar v$ must be large enough for <elastic rethermalization of a trapped atomic gas> throughout evaporation, and much faster than unwanted loss and heating. A clean vacuum reduces background collisions; suitable trapping states suppress inelastic spin-changing loss, and <density> must not make <three-body recombination> dominant. The cutoff ramp should allow equilibration without taking so long that background losses dominate. A large <evaporation truncation parameter> $\eta=\epsilon_{\rm cut}/(k_BT)$ selects energetically valuable atoms, but an excessively large value suppresses their escape rate. A very small value discards too many useful low-energy atoms. If <elastic collisions> become more frequent as evaporation proceeds, the resulting <runaway evaporative cooling> can improve efficiency; it is not automatic in a weakening optical trap.

The <energy selectivity for evaporation to increase phase-space density> can be made precise in a classical three-dimensional harmonic trap with *fixed <frequencies>*. <Equipartition> gives $E=3Nk_BT$. If each escaping atom removes mean <energy> $\epsilon_{\rm ev}$, <energy> balance is $dE=\epsilon_{\rm ev}\,dN$ with $dN<0$, so
$$
\alpha_{\rm ev}\equiv\frac{d\ln T}{d\ln N}=\frac{\epsilon_{\rm ev}/(k_BT)-3}{3}.
$$
The peak <harmonic-trap phase-space density> is $\mathcal D=N(\hbar\bar\omega/(k_BT))^3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\ln\mathcal D}{d\ln N}=4-\frac{\epsilon_{\rm ev}}{k_BT}.}
$$
Cooling requires $\epsilon_{\rm ev}>3k_BT$; increasing $\mathcal D$ despite atom loss requires the stronger condition $\epsilon_{\rm ev}>4k_BT$. These inequalities assume rapid thermalization and no work from changing trap <frequencies>. They distinguish lower <temperature> from genuinely improved <quantum degeneracy>. Eventually <quantum statistics> and inelastic processes require a refinement of this classical estimate. **Efficient evaporation combines energy-selective loss, rapid elastic redistribution, and low unwanted loss; it can bring a precooled gas to <Bose-Einstein condensation>.**
