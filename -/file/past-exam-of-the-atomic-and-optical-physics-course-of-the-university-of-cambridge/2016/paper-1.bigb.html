= Solution
{parent=1}

Take the field along $z$ and write $x=\mu_BB$, with <Bohr magneton> $\mu_B$. The printed <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> uses dimensionless angular-momentum operators, with <eigenvalues> $L(L+1)$ and $m_L$; physical angular <momenta> carry an additional $\hbar$. Thus $\beta$ and $x$ are <energies>. Assume the usual positive <spin-orbit interaction> coefficient $\beta>0$. Reversing its sign reverses the zero-field ordering but not the state count.

An <electron> has $S=1/2$. <addition of angular momentum> gives
$$
\boxed{J=L+\frac12,\quad L-\frac12\qquad(L>0).}
$$
The <coupled angular-momentum state count> can be checked directly. The product <basis> contains $(2L+1)\times2=4L+2$ states. The coupled <basis> contains
$$
[2(L+\tfrac12)+1]+[2(L-\tfrac12)+1]=(2L+2)+2L=\boxed{4L+2}.
$$
Coupling therefore reorganizes the states rather than creating or losing them. There is no hyperfine multiplicity because $I=0$.

Use $\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$ to obtain
$$
\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S
=\frac12(\mathbf J^2-\mathbf L^2-\mathbf S^2),\qquad
E_{\rm SO}(J)=\frac{\beta}{2}[J(J+1)-L(L+1)-3/4].
$$
The two <spin-orbit interaction> <energies> are $\beta L/2$ and $-\beta(L+1)/2$. In a weak field, project the <Zeeman effect> onto a fixed $J$ multiplet:
$$
P_JH_ZP_J=xg_JJ_z,\qquad
\Delta E_Z=xg_Jm_J,
$$
$$
g_{L+1/2}=1+\frac1{2L+1},\qquad
g_{L-1/2}=1-\frac1{2L+1}.
$$
This is the first-order <energy> formula, not a statement that $H_Z$ has no <matrix elements> between different $J$ values. For a common $m=m_J$ and a conventional choice of relative phase, its complete coupled-basis block is
$$
H_Z=x\begin{pmatrix}
g_{L+1/2}m&-\dfrac{\sqrt{(L+1/2)^2-m^2}}{2L+1}\\
-\dfrac{\sqrt{(L+1/2)^2-m^2}}{2L+1}&g_{L-1/2}m
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <inter-multiplet Zeeman coupling> terms give second-order weak-field corrections because the two <spin-orbit interaction> multiplets are already separated.

In the strong-field <basis> the Zeeman part is diagonal, $E_Z=x(m_L+2m_S)$. Write
$$
H_{\rm SO}=\beta\left[L_zS_z+\frac12(L_+S_-+L_-S_+)\right].
$$
Its diagonal <matrix elements> are $\beta m_Lm_S$. Its off-diagonal <matrix elements> connect $(m_L,+1/2)$ to $(m_L+1,-1/2)$ with coefficient
$$
\frac{\beta}{2}\sqrt{L(L+1)-m_L(m_L+1)},
$$
and the reverse <spin> change has $m_L+1$ replaced by $m_L-1$ in the corresponding ladder coefficient. These terms preserve $m_J$ but mix the two product states. To first order in $\beta/x$, the <energy> correction is simply $\beta m_Lm_S$.

For the P state, $L=1$. Measured relative to its gross-structure centroid, the six low-field levels are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
E_{3/2,m}&=\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac43xm,\qquad m=\pm\frac32,\ \pm\frac12,\\
E_{1/2,m}&=-\beta+\frac23xm,\qquad m=\pm\frac12.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Thus the upper quartet starts at $\beta/2$ with slopes $+2,+2/3,-2/3,-2$ in a plot against $x$; the lower doublet starts at $-\beta$ with slopes $\pm1/3$. Their zero-field separation is $3\beta/2$.

At high field all six product-state <energies>, retaining the term linear in $\beta$, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
m_L&m_S&E_P-E_P^{(0)}\\ \hline
-1&-1/2&-2x+\beta/2\\
-1&+1/2&-\beta/2\\
0&-1/2&-x\\
0&+1/2&+x\\
+1&-1/2&-\beta/2\\
+1&+1/2&+2x+\beta/2
\end{array}
$$
The two zero-slope levels have different $m_J$ and no mutual <matrix element>, so their first-order degeneracy does not require an additional degenerate perturbation. At finite field they split at order $\beta^2/x$.

An exact interpolation makes the requested whole-field sketch unambiguous. The stretched levels are
$$
E_{\pm3/2}=\frac{\beta}{2}\pm2x.
$$
For $m=\pm1/2$, use product states $|m-1/2,+1/2\rangle$ and $|m+1/2,-1/2\rangle$. Their <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> block is
$$
H_m=\left(mx-\frac{\beta}{4}\right)\mathbf1+
\frac{x+\beta m}{2}\sigma_z+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\sigma_x,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{E_m^\pm=mx-\frac{\beta}{4}\pm\frac12
\sqrt{x^2+2\beta mx+\frac94\beta^2}.}
$$
These <energies> reduce to the quartet and doublet at zero field and to the six strong-field expressions above. Within a fixed $m_J$, the nonzero off-diagonal element prevents a crossing of the two branches. Levels in different $m_J$ sectors can cross without hybridizing. For the S state, $L=0$ and the <spin-orbit interaction> term vanishes, leaving
$$
\boxed{E_{S,\pm1/2}=E_S^{(0)}\pm x}
$$
throughout this <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)>'s range of validity.

{title=Weak-field multiplets evolve into the Paschen-Back product-state pattern}
{description=Original exact diagonalization in units of $\beta$. P and S <energies> use separate gross-structure references because their separation is not specified. Dashed high-field lines retain the correction linear in $\beta$; labels identify the asymptotic product states.}

For <electric dipole transitions> at low field, $\Delta J=0,\pm1$ and $\Delta m_J=0,\pm1$ apply, with the usual exclusion of $J=0\to0$. Each P state has opposite <parity> to the S state. The $P_{1/2}\to S_{1/2}$ transition has four allowed sublevel pairs. For $P_{3/2}\to S_{1/2}$, the two stretched upper states each have one destination and the other two each have two, giving six. Hence there are **10 allowed state-to-state channels at weak nonzero field**. Relative to the two zero-field transition <energies>, their Zeeman shifts are
$$
P_{1/2}:\quad x\left\{-\frac43,-\frac23,\frac23,\frac43\right\},\qquad
P_{3/2}:\quad x\left\{-\frac53,-1,-\frac13,\frac13,1,\frac53\right\}.
$$

In the decoupled high-field limit, an electric dipole acts on orbital coordinates, so $\Delta m_S=0$. Each of the six P product states connects to the S state with the same <spin> and $m_L'=0$. Thus there are **6 allowed channels in the limiting product-state description**. Their <photon> <energies> are
$$
\hbar\omega=\Delta_0+xm_L+\beta m_Lm_S,\qquad
\Delta_0=E_P^{(0)}-E_S^{(0)}.
$$
This <Paschen-Back transition-channel count> is not the count of distinct frequencies: to first order in $\beta$ the two $m_L=0$ channels coincide, giving **5 distinct frequencies**, with shifts $0,\ \pm(x+\beta/2),\ \pm(x-\beta/2)$. If the residual <spin-orbit interaction> splitting is unresolved there are the three usual orbital Zeeman groups.

The apparent loss of four channels is a change in <eigenstate> mixing, not a change in Hilbert-space <dimension>. At finite large field, four <weak spin-forbidden Paschen-Back lines> arise from small opposite-spin admixtures, with intensities vanishing as $(\beta/x)^2$. A generic finite-field calculation therefore retains ten nonzero channels; six carry finite strength in the complete-decoupling limit. Observation direction and polarization select which of these channels a detector actually receives.

<evaporative cooling (atomic physics)> removes atoms selectively from the high-energy tail of a trapped gas. The remaining sample has less <energy> per atom; <elastic collisions> then redistribute <energy> and restore a thermal distribution at lower <temperature>. Random atom loss would generally remove average <energy> and would not provide the same cooling mechanism. Repeated <energy> selection and rethermalization, rather than one removal event, is the essential cycle.

Using the <evaporation truncation parameter> $\eta$, let the escape threshold be $\epsilon_{\rm cut}=\eta k_BT$. As the gas cools, a fixed threshold is populated by fewer atoms, so evaporation stalls. In <forced evaporative cooling> the threshold is lowered during the sequence. In a magnetic trap, a <radiofrequency evaporation knife> flips trapped weak-field-seeking atoms into untrapped <spin> states where the Zeeman splitting matches the <photon> <energy>. Energetic atoms reach that resonance surface; sweeping the radiofrequency lowers the <energy> knife. In an optical dipole trap, lowering the trap depth offers a different implementation, although it usually also weakens confinement.

A simple <evaporative cooling energy balance> shows when atom loss is useful. For a classical gas in a three-dimensional harmonic trap of fixed frequencies, equipartition gives $E=3Nk_BT$, counting both kinetic and <potential energy>. Suppose escaping atoms remove average <energy> $\epsilon_{\rm ev}=(\eta+\kappa)k_BT$, with $\kappa$ a distribution-dependent excess above the threshold. For $dN<0$,
$$
d(3Nk_BT)=\epsilon_{\rm ev}\,dN,\qquad
\alpha\equiv\frac{d\ln T}{d\ln N}=\frac{\eta+\kappa-3}{3}.
$$
Thus preferential removal cools when the removed <energy> exceeds $3k_BT$. More stringently, cooling must raise the <phase-space density of an atomic gas>, not merely lower the <temperature>:
$$
\mathcal D=n_{\rm peak}\lambda_T^3
=N\left(\frac{\hbar\bar\omega}{k_BT}\right)^3,\qquad
\lambda_T=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}.
$$
For this <harmonic-trap phase-space density>, with fixed geometric-mean trap <frequency> $\bar\omega$, $d\ln\mathcal D/d\ln N=1-3\alpha$. Therefore **phase-space density rises during atom loss if $\alpha>1/3$**. The numerical factors here belong to the stated harmonic-trap model, not every evaporative apparatus.

\Image[paper-1-evaporation.png]
{title=Energy-selective loss lowers the mean <energy> before rethermalization}
{description=Original classical harmonic-trap <energy> distribution, with an explicitly illustrative dimensionless cutoff. The post-cut distribution is truncated and is not yet a <thermal equilibrium> distribution.}

There must be enough <elastic collisions> to repopulate the tail: $\Gamma_{\rm el}\sim n\sigma\bar v$ should exceed damaging loss and heating rates, and the ramp should allow rethermalization. Increasing density can improve <elastic collisions> but also enhances inelastic and three-body losses. Very rapid removal can leave a nonequilibrium cloud; very slow removal exposes it to background-gas losses. Lowering an optical trap can reduce density and collision rate, so a falling <temperature> is not by itself evidence of efficient evaporation.

The dilute-gas condensation experiments made this tradeoff concrete. <Eric A. Cornell> and <Carl Wieman>'s account explains how selective escape and contraction of a trapped cloud raise phase-space density, while the need for rapid two-body equilibration and slow three-body loss restricts usable density. It also describes expansion imaging as an experimental diagnostic.<Nobel Lecture: Bose-Einstein condensation in a dilute gas, the first 70 years and some recent experiments by Eric A. Cornell and Carl Wieman>[pp. 876–878, equilibration, selective escape and imaging of expanded clouds]{ref} Useful controls compare different knife ramps and hold times, measure both atom number and expansion <temperature>, and check trap-frequency calibration. Pure adiabatic expansion can cool without increasing phase-space density. **Successful evaporation pays an atom-number cost to increase the remaining gas's phase-space occupancy.**

For an ideal weakly driven two-level atom, opposing red-detuned beams give velocity-dependent <absorption> and a friction force near zero velocity. Random <photon> recoils also cause <momentum> diffusion. Their balance gives the minimum Doppler <temperature>
$$
\boxed{k_BT_D=\frac{\hbar\Gamma}{2}}
$$
at detuning $\Delta=-\Gamma/2$ in the low-saturation model, where $\Gamma$ is the spontaneous population-decay rate, equivalently the natural full linewidth expressed as an <angular frequency>. This limit assumes the atom has no useful ground-state internal structure.

Real atoms possess <magnetic sublevels>. In a suitable multilevel transition, position-dependent polarization gives different optical couplings and different AC Stark <energy> shifts for those sublevels. Counterpropagating beams with orthogonal linear polarizations have nearly uniform total <intensity> but a periodically varying polarization. Thus spatially varying <light shifts> can exist even without an <intensity> standing wave.

In <Sisyphus cooling>, an atom on one ground-state branch climbs a light-shift hill and loses <kinetic energy>. Near the upper part of the hill, <optical pumping> tends to transfer population to a lower branch. <spontaneous emission> makes this transfer dissipative and randomizes the emitted <photon> direction. The atom can then climb another hill. Finite pumping time is essential: instantaneous local equilibration would remove the delayed internal-state response that produces friction. The original multilevel analysis relates these optical-pumping cycles to the spatially displaced <light shifts>.<Laser cooling below the Doppler limit by polarization gradients: simple theoretical models by Jean Dalibard and Claude Cohen-Tannoudji>[§3 A, pp. 2027–2028, Figs. 3–4]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-sisyphus-cycle.png]
{title=Climbing light-shift hills and dissipative <optical pumping>}
{description=Original illustrative sublevel potentials. The horizontal motion converts kinetic to <potential energy>; downward transfers represent optical-pumping cycles, not coherent conservative evolution on a single potential.}

For large detuning compared with the natural linewidth, useful scales are
$$
|U_{\rm ls}|\sim\frac{\hbar|\Omega|^2}{|\Delta|},\qquad
\Gamma_{\rm op}\sim\frac{\Gamma|\Omega|^2}{\Delta^2},
$$
up to angular-coupling and Rabi-frequency convention factors. Here $\Omega$ is the optical <Rabi angular frequency>. The slow-atom capture range is of order $kv\lesssim\Gamma_{\rm op}$. Typical <energies> in the Sisyphus regime track the light-shift scale rather than only $\hbar\Gamma$, so reducing <intensity> can lower <temperature> below the Doppler limit. Capture becomes narrower, however, and recoil heating, imperfect polarization, <magnetic fields> and scattered light eventually matter. Define <recoil energy> $E_r=(\hbar k)^2/(2m)$; this is an important scale, not a claim that every sub-Doppler method has the same universal final <temperature>.<Manipulating Atoms with Photons by Claude Cohen-Tannoudji>[§2, Sub-Doppler cooling; §2.1, Sisyphus effect]{ref}

The experimental test must establish a narrow velocity distribution independently of fluorescence brightness. <William D. Phillips>'s account describes moving a time-of-flight probe above and below the cloud, deliberately heating the atoms to check a previously absent signal, and release-and-recapture comparisons that included <gravity> and finite beam geometry. These tests separated very cold atoms from an insensitive detector or a faulty thermometer.<Nobel Lecture: Laser cooling and trapping of neutral atoms by William D. Phillips>[pp. 731–732, Figs. 15–16; pp. 733–734, multilevel interpretation]{ref} In a modern implementation, field cancellation and polarization checks test the mechanism, while independent expansion thermometry checks the inferred <temperature>. **<sub-Doppler cooling> exploits internal-state dynamics omitted from the two-level Doppler model; it does not contradict that model within its assumptions.**

A <two-photon transition> transfers population through two interactions with the electric-dipole operator. For <absorption> of two <photons>,
$$
E_f-E_i=\hbar(\omega_1+\omega_2).
$$
The intermediate atomic states enter a second-order amplitude rather than necessarily being populated. Suppressing field-normalization factors, its structure is
$$
\mathcal M_{fi}\propto\sum_r\left[
\frac{(\mathbf d_{fr}\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon_2)(\mathbf d_{ri}\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon_1)}
{E_i+\hbar\omega_1-E_r}
+
\frac{(\mathbf d_{fr}\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon_1)(\mathbf d_{ri}\cdot\boldsymbol\epsilon_2)}
{E_i+\hbar\omega_2-E_r}
\right].
$$
Both time orderings contribute coherently. Close to a real intermediate resonance, linewidths and intermediate-state population must be treated explicitly; an off-resonant virtual-state approximation then ceases to be adequate. Well away from saturation the <absorption> rate scales as $I_1I_2$, or as $I^2$ for a single applied <frequency>.

Each <electric-dipole interaction> reverses <parity>, so two such interactions connect states of the same <parity>. The product operator contains angular tensor ranks $0,1,2$, subject to triangle and polarization <selection rules>. Under the <degenerate two-photon selection rule>, identical equal-frequency <photons> have no antisymmetric rank-one contribution cancels; ranks zero and two remain. This permits a $J=0\to0$ transition and excludes $J=0\to1$ in that degenerate E1-E1 case. It does not universally forbid every $\Delta J=1$ transition, because a rank-two tensor can also connect some such pairs. For a single orbital <electron>, the corresponding orbital changes are $\Delta L=0,\pm2$, with <spin> unchanged in the nonrelativistic electric-dipole approximation.

In <Doppler-free two-photon absorption>, counterpropagating equal-frequency <photons> give first-order Doppler cancellation when one <photon> is absorbed from each beam:
$$
\Delta_{\rm Doppler}=(\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2)\cdot\mathbf v\simeq0,\qquad
\Delta\mathbf p=\hbar(\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2)\simeq0.
$$
The <laser> <frequency> is half the transition <frequency>. Same-beam two-photon events can still produce a broader Doppler-shifted background; residual beam-angle errors, unequal frequencies, <AC Stark shifts> and finite transit time limit precision. Thus a narrow two-photon resonance requires control of beam geometry and <intensity>, not just high <laser> power.

A <stimulated Raman transition> is the absorption-emission version: the atom absorbs from one beam and emits into the other, transferring between two long-lived states through an off-resonant upper state. It instead obeys
$$
E_f-E_i=\hbar(\omega_1-\omega_2),\qquad
\Delta\mathbf p=\hbar(\mathbf k_1-\mathbf k_2).
$$
Counterpropagation now enhances <momentum> transfer rather than cancelling it. With single-photon <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> <matrix elements> $\hbar\Omega_i/2$ and a common large angular detuning $\Delta$, eliminating the <excited state> gives
$$
|\Omega_{\rm eff}|=\frac{|\Omega_1\Omega_2|}{2|\Delta|},\qquad
\Gamma_{\rm sc}\sim\frac{\Gamma(|\Omega_1|^2+|\Omega_2|^2)}{4\Delta^2}.
$$
The two-photon detuning must include differential AC Stark and recoil shifts. Large detuning suppresses spontaneous scattering but also reduces coherent coupling at fixed optical power.

\Image[paper-1-two-photon-paths.png]
{title=<two-photon absorption> and stimulated Raman transfer have different <energy> and <momentum> sums}
{description=Original level and photon-path schematics. A virtual intermediate line is not an additional stationary <eigenstate>; intermediate detunings are schematic, with no assumed atomic species or numerical level spacing.}

The coherent Raman route is useful for atomic beam splitters and mirrors. <Mark Kasevich> and <Steven Chu> demonstrated a laser-cooled sodium matter-wave interferometer using <stimulated Raman transitions>; its controlled internal-state changes accompanied separated external wave packets.<Atomic interferometry using stimulated Raman transitions by Mark Kasevich and Steven Chu>[Physical Review Letters 67, pp. 181–184 (1991), abstract and interferometer principle]{ref} Testing this interpretation requires scanning the two-photon detuning, controlling relative <laser> phase and checking output populations against pulse timing. **A two-photon process is defined by its coherent two-interaction pathway; absorption-absorption and absorption-emission have different resonance and recoil conditions.**

= Solution
{parent=3}

Write $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, $E_k=\hbar\omega$, and take $\mu>0$. The <Bogoliubov spectrum> is gapless and positive for $k\ne0$. Its small- and large-momentum limits are
$$
\boxed{E_k\simeq\hbar c|k|,\qquad c=\sqrt{\mu/m}\quad(\epsilon_k\ll\mu),}
$$
$$
E_k=\epsilon_k+\mu+O(\mu^2/\epsilon_k)\qquad(\epsilon_k\gg\mu).
$$
The low-energy excitations are collective sound modes; high-energy excitations approach particles with a mean-field <energy> offset. With <healing length> $\xi=\hbar/\sqrt{2m\mu}$ and $q=|k|\xi$,
$$
\frac{E_k}{\mu}=\sqrt{q^2(q^2+2)}.
$$
The crossover occurs at $q$ of order one, not at an additional <energy> gap.

The <critical velocity of a homogeneous condensate> follows because the <Landau criterion> compares an excitation <energy> with the <energy> that flow can release by transferring its <momentum>. Creating a mode is energetically possible when $E_k-\hbar\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v\leq0$. For this isotropic spectrum,
$$
v_c=\inf_{k>0}\frac{E_k}{\hbar k}
=\inf_{k>0}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{m}+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{4m^2}}
=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{m}}}.
$$
The infimum is the long-wavelength <speed of sound>. This tests energetic stability against the stated excitations; it is not a calculation of a dissipation rate or a universal vortex-nucleation threshold.

For an external potential varying slowly on the healing scale, the <Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate> neglects density-gradient <kinetic energy> in the stationary <Gross–Pitaevskii equation>. In occupied regions it gives
$$
g\rho(\mathbf r)\simeq\mu-V(\mathbf r),\qquad
c(\mathbf r)^2=\frac{g\rho(\mathbf r)}m,\qquad
\boxed{v_c(\mathbf r)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{\mu-V(\mathbf r)}m}.}
$$
Here $\rho$ is the appropriate local <number density>; in the ring it is a linear density and $g$ is the one-dimensional coupling. This is the <local-density sound speed of a condensate>. The formula applies where $\mu>V$, not in an empty region where its square root would be imaginary.

Normalize the condensate order parameter to the atom number:
$$
N=\int_0^{2\pi}|\psi(\theta)|^2R\,d\theta,\qquad
\boxed{\psi_0=\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\pi R}}}
$$
up to an arbitrary constant phase. A <wavefunction> normalized to one instead has amplitude $1/\sqrt{2\pi R}$, with $N|\psi|^2$ then providing density. Acting with $L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\theta$ gives **<angular momentum> $n\hbar$ per atom and $Nn\hbar$ in total** for the uniform ring. The <superfluid velocity> is
$$
v=\frac{\hbar}{mR}\partial_\theta\arg\psi=\frac{n\hbar}{mR}.
$$
The interaction <chemical potential> is $\mu_0=gN/(2\pi R)$; a flowing uniform state also has the constant kinetic contribution $\hbar^2n^2/(2mR^2)$ to its total <chemical potential>. Its <speed of sound> is set by the interaction term. Applying $|v|<\sqrt{\mu_0/m}$ gives
$$
\boxed{n^2<\frac{mR^2\mu_0}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
For a <uniform ring condensate>, this is the continuum <phonon> criterion used here. Quantization of the available excitation <wavelengths> in a very small ring can modify a finite-size threshold.

For the nonrotating equilibrium with a raised left half, let $\rho_L$ and $\rho_R$ be the bulk linear densities. Within the same <Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate>,
$$
g\rho_L=\mu-V_0,\qquad g\rho_R=\mu,\qquad
N=\pi R(\rho_L+\rho_R).
$$
When both halves are occupied, normalization fixes
$$
\boxed{\mu=\mu_0+\frac{V_0}{2},\quad
N_L=\frac N2\left(1-\frac{V_0}{2\mu_0}\right),\quad
N_R=\frac N2\left(1+\frac{V_0}{2\mu_0}\right),}
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{N_L}{N_R}=\frac{2\mu_0-V_0}{2\mu_0+V_0}.}
$$
These expressions require $V_0<2\mu_0$. Beyond that threshold, extending them would give a negative left density and is unphysical. Instead the left bulk empties, all $N$ atoms occupy a segment of length $\pi R$, and
$$
\boxed{N_L=0,\quad N_R=N,\quad \mu=\frac{Ng}{\pi R}=2\mu_0
\qquad(V_0>2\mu_0).}
$$
At equality the left bulk just reaches zero density. <kinetic energy> rounds the interface and produces tails beyond the <Thomas–Fermi approximation for a condensate>, so an exactly empty half is the idealized bulk result.

A nonconstant $V(\theta)$ breaks <rotational symmetry>:
$$
[H,L_z]=i\hbar\,\partial_\theta V\ne0.
$$
Consequently <angular momentum> is no longer a good <quantum number>, and an eigenfunction need not be $e^{in\theta}$ times a constant amplitude. Nevertheless, while the order parameter is nonzero around the whole ring, single-valuedness requires
$$
\boxed{\oint d\phi=2\pi n,\qquad
\oint v\,ds=\frac{2\pi n\hbar}{m}.}
$$
The integer is a <phase winding of a condensate>, not an assertion that the inhomogeneous state remains an angular-momentum <eigenstate>. A <superfluid phase slip> can change it if the amplitude vanishes locally.

Conservation of particles gives $\partial_t\rho+\partial_s(\rho v)=0$. For steady density the one-dimensional particle current $j=\rho v$ is constant. Since the two halves have equal lengths,
$$
\rho_Lv_L=\rho_Rv_R,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{v_L}{v_R}=\frac{\rho_R}{\rho_L}=\frac{N_R}{N_L}.}
$$
The <current bottleneck in an inhomogeneous condensate> occurs because a lower-density region has faster flow at the same current; it also has a smaller <speed of sound>. This makes the raised half the limiting region.

For $V_0=2\mu_0/3$ the equilibrium approximation gives
$$
\mu=\frac43\mu_0,\quad g\rho_L=\frac23\mu_0,\quad
g\rho_R=\frac43\mu_0,\quad v_L=2v_R.
$$
The winding condition and equal lengths require
$$
\pi R(v_L+v_R)=\frac{2\pi n\hbar}{m},\qquad
v_R=\frac{2n\hbar}{3mR},\quad v_L=\frac{4n\hbar}{3mR}.
$$
Thus the left-hand sound-speed test is
$$
\frac{16n^2\hbar^2}{9m^2R^2}<\frac{2\mu_0}{3m}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{n^2<\frac38\frac{mR^2\mu_0}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
The right-hand condition is only $n^2<3mR^2\mu_0/\hbar^2$, so it is less restrictive.

This <local sound-speed estimate for ring-flow stability> uses the <step-potential equilibrium in a ring condensate> densities together with the local sound-speed test, as suggested by the preceding approximations. A quantitatively self-consistent finite-current solution also includes $mv^2/2$ and the <quantum pressure of a condensate> in the stationary <Gross–Pitaevskii equation>, allowing flow to change the density. Smooth steps suppress reflection but do not alone remove those terms. **The $3/8$ result is the stated local-density estimate; it should not be promoted to an exact stability theorem for every finite interacting ring.**
