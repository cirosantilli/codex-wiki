= Solution
{parent=i}

Generate <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ with <probability density function> $p$, evaluate $\phi(X_i)$ for each draw, and return the <Monte Carlo estimator>
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\phi(X_i).}
$$
<Linearity of expectation> makes this an <unbiased estimator>: $\mathbb E\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}}=\theta$. Independence and the specified <variance> give $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}})=1/n$. Its <Monte Carlo standard error> is therefore $n^{-1/2}$, and the <strong law of large numbers> gives convergence to $\theta$ <almost surely>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Apply <Chebyshev's inequality> to the <Monte Carlo estimator>:
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\left|\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}}-\theta\right|\geq0.01\right)
\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}})}{0.01^2}
=\frac{10^4}{n}.
$$
Thus $10^4/n\leq0.01$ suffices, giving
$$
\boxed{n=1{,}000{,}000.}
$$
This is a finite-sample <probability> guarantee for every target satisfying the given <variance> assumption. It is a sufficient sample count supplied by the inequality, rather than a claim that every such target actually needs a million draws.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <central limit theorem> gives $\sqrt n(\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}}-\theta)\Rightarrow N(0,1)$, so, with $\Phi$ the <cumulative distribution function> of the <standard normal distribution>,
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\left|\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{MC}}-\theta\right|<0.01\right)
\approx 2\Phi(0.01\sqrt n)-1.
$$
The error has two tails. Using the supplied $2.58$ <quantile>, whose two-sided coverage is approximately $0.9902$, gives
$$
0.01\sqrt n\geq2.58,
\qquad
\boxed{N:=n=\left\lceil\frac{2.58^2}{0.01^2}\right\rceil=66{,}564.}
$$
The supplied $2.33$ <quantile> would be appropriate for a one-sided $99\%$ calculation; it would give only approximately $98.02\%$ two-sided coverage. Using the exact $0.995$ <quantile> instead of $2.58$ would give $66{,}349$ draws; we consistently use the paper's rounded value $N=66{,}564$ below.

For routine <Monte Carlo integration>, the <central limit theorem> calculation is preferable as a practical budget: it uses about one fifteenth as many draws as the <Chebyshev's inequality> bound. However, its coverage is approximate. Finite <variance> alone supplies no universal rate of convergence to the <normal distribution>; an integrand with large <skewness> or a <heavy-tailed distribution> can make the approximation poor even at a large sample count. If an unconditional finite-sample $99\%$ guarantee is required under only the stated assumptions, use the <Chebyshev's inequality> budget.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Integrating the envelope $p\leq Mq$ shows $M\geq1$. One trial of <rejection sampling> draws $Y\sim q$ and an <independent> $U$ from the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$, and accepts $Y$ if $U\leq p(Y)/(Mq(Y))$. Repeat with fresh <independent random variables> after each rejection. Ratios need only be evaluated where $q>0$; the envelope makes the target zero wherever $q=0$.

For any <measurable set> $B$, integration of the <conditional probability> of acceptance gives
$$
\mathbb P(Y\in B,\mathrm{accept})=\int_Bq(y)\frac{p(y)}{Mq(y)}\,dy=\frac1M\int_Bp(y)\,dy.
$$
Taking the whole domain gives acceptance <probability> $1/M$, and dividing gives $\mathbb P(Y\in B\mid\mathrm{accept})=\int_Bp(y)\,dy$. Fresh trials therefore supply <independent and identically distributed random variables> $X_1,\ldots,X_m$ with target <probability density function> $p$. The corresponding <Monte Carlo estimator> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_m^{\mathrm{RS}}=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\phi(X_j).}
$$
The proposal count $T$ up to and including one acceptance has the <geometric distribution>
$$
\mathbb P(T=t)=(1-1/M)^{t-1}/M,\quad t=1,2,\ldots,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb ET=M.}
$$
For example, the <tail-sum formula> gives $\mathbb ET=\sum_{t\geq0}(1-1/M)^t=M$. Thus the expected cost is $M$ proposal draws per accepted target draw.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Stop <rejection sampling> when exactly $N=66{,}564$ target draws have been accepted. The total proposal count $T_N$ is a sum of $N$ <independent> waiting times with <geometric distribution>, equivalently a <negative binomial distribution> counting trials. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb ET_N=MN=66{,}564M,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(T_N)=NM(M-1).}
$$
The accepted-sample <Monte Carlo estimator> then has the approximate $99\%$ precision from (a)(iii). The displayed proposal cost is an <expected value>, which need not be an integer. Spending this many proposals in advance does not ensure that $N$ target draws will have been accepted.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

With a fixed budget of $n$ <independent> proposal trials, the accepted count $K_n$ has <binomial distribution> with success <probability> $1/M$. Hence
$$
\mathbb EK_n=\frac nM,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(K_n)=\frac{n(M-1)}{M^2}.
$$
For $M>1$, the <central limit theorem> gives the one-sided approximation
$$
\mathbb P(K_n\geq N)\approx
\Phi\!\left(\frac{n-MN}{\sqrt{n(M-1)}}\right).
$$
Use $z=2.33$, since now only the lower tail of the accepted count matters. Requiring $n-MN\geq z\sqrt{n(M-1)}$ and solving the resulting <quadratic equation> in $t=\sqrt n$ gives the <one-sided normal approximation for a rejection proposal budget>
$$
\boxed{
n_0=\left\lceil
\left(\frac{z\sqrt{M-1}+\sqrt{z^2(M-1)+4MN}}2\right)^2
\right\rceil.}
$$
Equivalently, before rounding, this is $MN+z^2(M-1)/2+(z/2)\sqrt{(M-1)[z^2(M-1)+4MN]}$. For the value in (a)(iii),
$$
\boxed{
n_0=\left\lceil
\left(\frac{2.33\sqrt{M-1}+\sqrt{5.4289(M-1)+266256M}}2\right)^2
\right\rceil.}
$$
When $M=1$, every proposal is accepted and $\boxed{n_0=N=66{,}564}$ exactly. Otherwise the positive safety margin above $MN$ allows for random fluctuations in the number accepted. The average cost in (b)(ii) describes stopping at the $N$th acceptance; this answer chooses a deterministic budget that reaches that acceptance with approximately $99\%$ <probability>.

The approximation requires a sufficiently accurate <normal distribution> approximation to the <binomial distribution>. An exact $99\%$ budget can instead be found from its tail probabilities. Also, a $99\%$ chance of enough accepted draws and approximate $99\%$ precision conditional on obtaining them are separate claims: their intersection is not automatically a $99\%$ event.

= Solution

Draw <independent and identically distributed random variables> $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\sim q$ and use the <importance weight> $w=p/q$. The <importance sampling> estimator is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nw(Y_i)\phi(Y_i).}
$$
The envelope supplies the <support condition for importance sampling>. Since $\mathbb E_q[w(Y)\phi(Y)]=\mathbb E_p\phi(X)=\theta$, this is an <unbiased estimator>. Moreover, $0\leq w\leq M$, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_q[w(Y)^2\phi(Y)^2]
&=\int w(x)\phi(x)^2p(x)\,dx\\
&=\mathbb E_p[w(X)\phi(X)^2]\\
&\leq M\mathbb E_p[\phi(X)^2]
=M(1+\theta^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\mathbb E_p\phi^2=\operatorname{Var}_p(\phi)+\theta^2=1+\theta^2$. Subtracting the squared <expected value> and dividing by $n$ proves the <bounded-weight importance-sampling moment bound> in the requested form:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}})
=\frac{\mathbb E_p[w\phi^2]-\theta^2}{n}
\leq\frac{M+(M-1)\theta^2}{n}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The definition of $a$ and (c)(i) give $a\leq M$. Nonnegativity of the <variance> strengthens the lower bound to
$$
\boxed{\frac{\theta^2}{1+\theta^2}\leq a\leq M.}
$$
In fact the <variance> of one weighted observation is exactly
$$
v_{\mathrm{IS}}:=\operatorname{Var}_q(w(Y)\phi(Y))
=a(1+\theta^2)-\theta^2.
$$
It is finite by the envelope. For $v_{\mathrm{IS}}>0$, the <central limit theorem> gives approximate two-sided coverage $2\Phi(0.01\sqrt{n/v_{\mathrm{IS}}})-1$, so the paper's rounded <quantile> yields
$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{IS}}=\left\lceil66{,}564\,[a(1+\theta^2)-\theta^2]\right\rceil.}
$$
If $v_{\mathrm{IS}}=0$, the weighted observation equals $\theta$ <almost surely>, and one draw is sufficient. These are theoretical budgets: $a$ and $\theta$ may themselves be unknown and need preliminary estimation in an implementation.

Compare with the average <rejection sampling> cost $66{,}564M$. Ignoring integer rounding, <importance sampling> uses fewer proposal draws at the same approximate precision precisely when
$$
a(1+\theta^2)-\theta^2<M,
\qquad
\boxed{a<\frac{M+\theta^2}{1+\theta^2}.}
$$
For literal integer budgets, the comparison is $\lceil66{,}564v_{\mathrm{IS}}\rceil<66{,}564M$. The simpler strict inequality is the intended leading-order condition. The bound $a\leq M$ alone does not ensure an improvement when $\theta\ne0$; this is a <proposal-budget variance comparison of importance and rejection sampling>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write $w=p/q$ where $q>0$, assigning zero elsewhere. The envelope ensures $0\leq w/M\leq1$, and $\pi$ is nonnegative. Integrating its <joint probability density> over the auxiliary coordinate gives
$$
\int_0^1\pi(x,u)\,du=Mq(x)\frac{w(x)}M=p(x).
$$
Consequently $\int_\Omega\int_0^1\pi(x,u)\,du\,dx=\int_\Omega p(x)\,dx=1$. This proves <normalization of a probability distribution>; the change of integration order is justified by <Tonelli's theorem>.

The coordinates are generally dependent. For $p(x)>0$, their <conditional probability density> is
$$
\pi(u\mid x)=\frac M{w(x)}\mathbf1_{[0,w(x)/M]}(u).
$$
Thus $U\mid X=x$ has a <uniform distribution> on an interval whose endpoint depends on $x$. They are <independent> exactly when $w(x)$ is constant for $p$-almost every $x$, since only then do these conditional laws agree. In particular, $p=q$ is an exception: $X\sim p$ and $U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1/M)$ are <independent>. An unconditional answer that they can never be <independent> would therefore be too strong.

Integrating the <joint probability density> over the auxiliary coordinate, as in (i), gives the <marginal distribution>
$$
\boxed{\pi_X(x)=\int_0^1\pi(x,u)\,du=p(x).}
$$
In particular, augmenting with $U$ preserves the target <probability distribution> of $X$. The other <marginal distribution>, though not needed to identify $X$, is $\pi_U(u)=M\int_\Omega q(x)\mathbf1_{\{w(x)\geq Mu\}}\,dx$ for $0<u<1$. It need not be a <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$.

For the proposed <joint probability density> $\rho$, define the acceptance <indicator function>
$$
A_i:=\mathbf1_{\{V_i\leq w(Y_i)/M\}}.
$$
On the proposal's <support> the <importance weight> is $\pi(Y_i,V_i)/\rho(Y_i,V_i)=MA_i$. Therefore the requested augmented-space <importance sampling> estimator is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac Mn\sum_{i=1}^nA_i\phi(Y_i).}
$$
Its <expected value> is $\theta$, provided $\mathbb E_p|\phi(X)|<\infty$. This is not the ordinary estimator $n^{-1}\sum_iw(Y_i)\phi(Y_i)$ from question 1. Taking the <conditional expectation> of this augmented estimator given all $Y_i$ produces that ordinary estimator, because $\mathbb E[A_i\mid Y_i]=w(Y_i)/M$. This is the <Rao-Blackwell identity for a fixed-denominator rejection estimator>.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Use the same acceptance <indicator functions> and set $K_n=\sum_{i=1}^nA_i$, $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nA_i\phi(Y_i)$. The <rejection sampling> estimator based on these $n$ proposals is the mean over the accepted observations:
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{RS}}=\frac{S_n}{K_n}\quad(K_n>0).}
$$
For $K_n=0$, any fixed convention, such as zero, is harmless asymptotically. The $A_i$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with <Bernoulli distribution> and success <probability> $1/M$, so the <strong law of large numbers> gives $K_n/n\to1/M$ <almost surely>. In particular $K_n>0$ eventually <almost surely>. The exact identity
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{MK_n}{n}\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{RS}}}
$$
then holds whenever $K_n>0$. Wherever $S_n\ne0$, it follows that
$$
\frac{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}}}{\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{RS}}}
=\frac{MK_n}{n}\longrightarrow1\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
A qualification is missing from the printed claim: the quotient must be defined eventually. A convenient sufficient assumption is $\mathbb E_p|\phi|<\infty$ and $\theta\ne0$. Indeed, another application of the <strong law of large numbers> gives $S_n/n\to\theta/M$, ensuring that $S_n$ is eventually nonzero. This proves the stated quotient limit under that assumption.

As printed, the assertion is false as a universally defined quotient. For example, $\phi\equiv0$ makes both estimates zero for every $n$. Even excluding that example is insufficient: take $p=q$ to be the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$, $M=1$, and $\phi(y)=1$ for $y<1/2$ and $-1$ otherwise. Then $S_n$ is a <simple symmetric random walk>, which returns to zero infinitely often <almost surely>, leaving the quotient undefined infinitely often.

With integrability but no assumption $\theta\ne0$, the exact multiplicative identity remains valid, and both estimates converge to $\theta$ <almost surely>. In particular $\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{IS}}-\widehat\theta_n^{\mathrm{RS}}\to0$ <almost surely>. One may alternatively define the quotient's removable $0/0$ cases as $MK_n/n$; that explicitly extended quantity always converges to one. These are valid repairs of the source's unrestricted statement.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Fix a proposal $y$ with $\widetilde q(y)>0$, and abbreviate $B=M\widetilde q(y)$, $H=h(y)$ and $P=\widetilde p(y)$. The envelope says $0\leq H\leq P\leq B$. Assuming fresh <independent> <uniform distribution> draws at both stages, the <law of total probability> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(\mathrm{accept}\mid Y=y)
&=\frac HB+\left(1-\frac HB\right)\frac{P-H}{B-H}\\
&=\frac HB+\frac{P-H}{B}
=\boxed{\frac{\widetilde p(y)}{M\widetilde q(y)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second term includes both reaching the residual test and passing it. If $H=B$, the envelope forces $P=B$ and the first test accepts with <probability> one; the second-stage $0/0$ ratio is never evaluated. This is <squeeze rejection sampling>.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Average the <conditional probability> in (i) over $Y\sim q=\widetilde q/Z_q$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(\mathrm{accept})
&=\int_\Omega\frac{\widetilde q(y)}{Z_q}
\frac{\widetilde p(y)}{M\widetilde q(y)}\,dy\\
&=\frac1{MZ_q}\int_\Omega\widetilde p(y)\,dy
=\boxed{\frac{Z_p}{MZ_q}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is exactly the <acceptance mass for an unnormalized rejection envelope>. In normalized form the envelope is $p\leq(MZ_q/Z_p)q$, giving the same acceptance <probability>. Here $M$ bounds the ratio of the unnormalized functions, so its value need not equal the envelope constant for the normalized <probability density functions>.

The squeeze changes the number of expensive target evaluations, while preserving the per-proposal acceptance <probability>. The expected number of proposals per accepted draw is $MZ_q/Z_p$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a <measurable set> $C$, (i) gives the joint acceptance <probability>
$$
\mathbb P(Y\in C,\mathrm{accept})
=\int_C\frac{\widetilde q(y)}{Z_q}
\frac{\widetilde p(y)}{M\widetilde q(y)}\,dy
=\frac1{MZ_q}\int_C\widetilde p(y)\,dy.
$$
Divide by $Z_p/(MZ_q)$ from (ii). The resulting <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y\in C\mid\mathrm{accept})
=\frac1{Z_p}\int_C\widetilde p(y)\,dy
=\int_Cp(y)\,dy.}
$$
Thus every accepted value has target <probability density function> $p$. Repeating rejected trials with fresh <independent random variables> does not change this law: the contribution from acceptance on trial $j$ is multiplied by $(1-Z_p/(MZ_q))^{j-1}$, and summing that <geometric series> gives the same conditional law. Independent repetitions produce <independent> target draws.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The proposal's <normalization of a probability distribution> is
$$
\boxed{Z_q=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|x|}\,dx=2,\qquad q(x)=\tfrac12e^{-|x|}.}
$$
This is the <Laplace distribution> with location zero and scale one. For example, generate its magnitude from the <exponential distribution> with rate one and give it an <independent> fair sign. The target is the <standard normal distribution>, with $Z_p=\sqrt{2\pi}$.

To obtain the tight envelope, let $t=|x|$. By <completing the square>,
$$
\frac{\widetilde p(x)}{\widetilde q(x)}
=e^{-t^2/2+t}=e^{1/2}e^{-(t-1)^2/2}\leq e^{1/2}.
$$
Equality occurs at $x=\pm1$, so $\boxed{M_*=\sqrt e}$ is the smallest valid envelope constant. It maximizes the acceptance <probability> for this fixed proposal. The supplied lower bound must be clipped at zero to satisfy the algorithm's nonnegativity requirement:
$$
\boxed{h(x)=\max\{0,1-x^2/2\}.}
$$
This is valid because $e^{-s}\geq1-s$ for $s\geq0$, while $\widetilde p$ is everywhere positive. Its <support> is $[-\sqrt2,\sqrt2]$.

Here is the fully specified <squeeze rejection sampling> procedure. Draw $Y$ with <probability density function> $q(y)=e^{-|y|}/2$, independently draw $U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$, and compute $H=\max(0,1-Y^2/2)$ and $B=\sqrt e\,e^{-|Y|}$. If $U\leq H/B$, return $Y$ immediately. Otherwise evaluate $P=e^{-Y^2/2}$, independently draw $V\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$, and return $Y$ if $V\leq(P-H)/(B-H)$. On rejection restart with fresh draws. In this example $B>H$ everywhere, so the residual denominator is positive.

Avoiding a target evaluation means accepting at the first test. Its unconditional <probability> for one proposal is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(\text{no target evaluation})
&=\int_{\mathbb R}q(y)\frac{h(y)}{M\widetilde q(y)}\,dy\\
&=\frac1{2\sqrt e}\int_{-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}(1-y^2/2)\,dy\\
&=\boxed{\frac{2\sqrt2}{3\sqrt e}\approx0.571843.}
\end{aligned}
$$
For comparison, the overall acceptance <probability> is $\sqrt{2\pi}/(2\sqrt e)=\sqrt{\pi/(2e)}\approx0.760173$. The first-stage saving $0.571843$ is the fraction of proposals requiring no target evaluation, including both eventual accepted and rejected proposals in the denominator; it is not conditional on acceptance.

The following <R programming language> code implements the given <recursion> for the <Cholesky decomposition>. The input is assumed to be a real <symmetric matrix> that is a <positive-definite matrix>.

\C[[
mychol <- function(A) {
  n <- nrow(A)
  if (n == 1L) return(matrix(sqrt(A[1L, 1L]), 1L, 1L))
  j <- 2L:n
  L <- matrix(0, n, n)
  L[1L, 1L] <- sqrt(A[1L, 1L])
  b <- A[j, 1L, drop = FALSE] / L[1L, 1L]
  L[j, 1L] <- b
  S <- A[j, j, drop = FALSE] - tcrossprod(b)
  L[j, j] <- mychol(S)
  L
}
]]

Here `tcrossprod(b)` is the <outer product> $bb^T$. The `drop = FALSE` arguments preserve <matrix> dimensions, including the final one-by-one subproblem, and the <base case> also returns a <matrix>.

To verify correctness, partition $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&r^T\\r&B\end{pmatrix}$. The code sets $\ell=\sqrt a$, $b=r/\ell$ and recursively factors $S=B-bb^T$. This <Schur complement> is a <positive-definite matrix>: for $z\ne0$,
$$
z^TSz=
\begin{pmatrix}-r^Tz/a\\z\end{pmatrix}^{\!T}
A
\begin{pmatrix}-r^Tz/a\\z\end{pmatrix}>0.
$$
If $CC^T=S$ is the recursive answer, then the returned <lower triangular matrix> satisfies
$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}\ell&0\\b&C\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
LL^T=\begin{pmatrix}a&\ell b^T\\\ell b&bb^T+CC^T\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}a&r^T\\r&B\end{pmatrix}=A.
$$
The one-dimensional identity starts this <mathematical induction> proof. Each recursive call reduces the <matrix> size by one and therefore terminates.

= Solution
{parent=b}

An <inverse matrix> exists only if the <lower triangular matrix> has no zero <diagonal entries>; this necessary assumption is implicit in the request for $L^{-1}$. Partition
$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\b&C\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Writing its inverse in the same block form and multiplying gives the <recursive inverse of a lower triangular matrix>:
$$
\boxed{L^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{-1}&0\\-C^{-1}b/a&C^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Indeed the lower-left block of the product is $b/a-C(C^{-1}b/a)=0$, and the diagonal blocks are identities. This gives an implementation with no call to `solve`:

\C[[
ltinv <- function(L) {
  n <- nrow(L)
  if (n == 1L) return(matrix(1 / L[1L, 1L], 1L, 1L))
  j <- 2L:n
  Cinv <- ltinv(L[j, j, drop = FALSE])
  B <- matrix(0, n, n)
  B[1L, 1L] <- 1 / L[1L, 1L]
  B[j, j] <- Cinv
  B[j, 1L] <- -as.vector(Cinv %*% L[j, 1L, drop = FALSE]) / L[1L, 1L]
  B
}
]]

The preserved <matrix> dimensions also handle $n=2$. A <mathematical induction> using the block identity proves that every returned <matrix> is the desired inverse. Applied to the <Cholesky decomposition> from (a), all <diagonal entries> are positive, so all divisions are valid.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Each state has exactly $k(n-k)$ neighbours: choose one occupied coordinate and one unoccupied coordinate to swap. The normalized <proposal distribution> is therefore
$$
q(v\mid u)=\frac1{k(n-k)}\mathbf1_{\{v\sim u\}}.
$$
The swap relation is symmetric and the number of neighbours is constant, so $q(v\mid u)=q(u\mid v)$. The <discrete uniform distribution> used as the <prior distribution> is also constant on $S_k$, with mass $\binom nk^{-1}$. Both factors cancel in the <Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability>.

Let $Q=\Sigma^{-1}$ and $d=v-u$. The displayed <multivariate normal distribution> presupposes that the <covariance matrix> $\Sigma$ is a <positive-definite matrix>, so $Q$ is a <symmetric matrix>. Expanding the difference of <quadratic forms> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{\pi(v\mid x)}{\pi(u\mid x)}
&=-\tfrac12\{(x-v)^TQ(x-v)-(x-u)^TQ(x-u)\}\\
&=d^TQ(x-u)-\tfrac12d^TQd\\
&=d^TQ\left(x-\frac{u+v}{2}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\log\alpha(u,v)=\min\!\left\{0,\ (v-u)^T\Sigma^{-1}\left(x-\frac{u+v}{2}\right)\right\}.}
$$
The truncation at zero is essential: a log acceptance ratio can be positive, but the <logarithm> of an acceptance <probability> cannot.

For the particular proposed swap, let $a=i_1$ be the coordinate changing from zero to one and $b=i_2$ the coordinate changing from one to zero. Then $d=e_a-e_b$, and the untruncated scalar simplifies further to
$$
\boxed{(Q(x-u))_a-(Q(x-u))_b-\tfrac12(Q_{aa}+Q_{bb}-2Q_{ab}).}
$$
Only two rows and four entries of $Q$ are needed for this evaluation. This is <Metropolis sampling on fixed-cardinality subsets>.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The source's function name suggests a log return value, and (c)(i) asks for $\log\alpha$, while the wording here and in (c)(iv) asks for the acceptance <probability>. We use the literal probability-returning convention and explicitly exponentiate the result of (c)(i):

\C[[
mylogapp <- function(x, u, v, sigma_inv) {
  d <- v - u
  log_ratio <- as.numeric(crossprod(d, sigma_inv %*% (x - (u + v) / 2)))
  log_alpha <- min(0, log_ratio)
  exp(log_alpha)
}
]]

Thus `mylogapp` returns $\alpha\in[0,1]$, and (c)(iv) compares it directly with a <uniform distribution> draw. Taking the minimum before exponentiating prevents overflow. The arguments $u,v$ are assumed to be valid neighbouring states. A log-returning convention would instead return `log_alpha` and compare `log(runif(1))` with it; the two conventions implement the same <Metropolis–Hastings algorithm>, provided the caller uses the matching comparison.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <Cholesky decomposition> gives $\Sigma=LL^T$. If $B=L^{-1}$, reversal of factors in a <matrix inverse> gives
$$
\boxed{\Sigma^{-1}=(L^T)^{-1}L^{-1}=B^TB.}
$$
Hence the required <R programming language> implementation is

\C[[
invsigma <- function(sigma) {
  L <- mychol(sigma)
  B <- ltinv(L)
  crossprod(B)
}
]]

Here `crossprod(B)` computes $B^TB$. Using `tcrossprod(B)` would compute $BB^T$, which generally differs from $\Sigma^{-1}$. Both helper functions preserve the one-by-one <matrix> case.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Choose $k$ distinct coordinates uniformly for the starting ones; each element of $S_k$ then has starting <probability> $1/\binom nk$. At each iteration choose one current one and one current zero uniformly and swap them. This implements the normalized <proposal distribution> from (c)(i), remains in $S_k$, and needs just one precomputed <matrix inverse>.

\C[[
mymcmc <- function(x, k, sigma) {
  n <- length(x)
  stopifnot(length(k) == 1L, is.finite(k), k == floor(k),
            k > 0L, k < n, is.matrix(sigma),
            all(dim(sigma) == c(n, n)))
  sigma_inv <- invsigma(sigma)
  u <- integer(n)
  u[sample.int(n, k, replace = FALSE)] <- 1L
  draws <- matrix(0, 100L, n)
  for (iteration in seq_len(100L)) {
    ones <- which(u == 1L)
    zeros <- which(u == 0L)
    i <- ones[sample.int(length(ones), 1L)]
    j <- zeros[sample.int(length(zeros), 1L)]
    v <- u
    v[i] <- 0L
    v[j] <- 1L
    alpha <- mylogapp(x, u, v, sigma_inv)
    if (runif(1L) < alpha) u <- v
    draws[iteration, ] <- u
  }
  colMeans(draws)
}
]]

Sampling positions with `sample.int` and then indexing into `ones` or `zeros` also works when $k=1$ or $n-k=1$. In <R programming language>, a call such as `sample(ones, 1)` would incorrectly sample from `1:ones` when `ones` is a single numeric value greater than one.

The code records the state after each of exactly $100$ transitions, including repetitions after rejection. Its answer is the vector <Monte Carlo estimator>
$$
\boxed{\widehat m=\frac1{100}\sum_{t=1}^{100}U^{(t)}\approx\mathbb E[U\mid X=x].}
$$
This estimates the <posterior mean> coordinate by coordinate; because each coordinate is binary, its entries estimate the <posterior probabilities> of inclusion. Every stored state has coordinate sum $k$, so the estimated means lie in $[0,1]$ and sum to $k$.

For neighbouring states, the symmetry of the proposal and the <Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability> give <detailed balance>:
$$
\pi(u\mid x)q(v\mid u)\alpha(u,v)
=q(v\mid u)\min\{\pi(u\mid x),\pi(v\mid x)\}
=\pi(v\mid x)q(u\mid v)\alpha(v,u).
$$
Thus the <posterior distribution> is a <stationary distribution>. Any two states in $S_k$ can be connected by moving the ones that occupy the wrong positions, and the <multivariate normal distribution> gives strictly positive target masses. The resulting <Markov chain> is therefore an <irreducible Markov chain>, and its long-run state averages estimate the <posterior mean>.

The requested $100$ iterations are a finite approximation, without a convergence guarantee or discarded <burn-in>. A randomly chosen starting subset has the <prior distribution>, rather than generally the <posterior distribution>. No <aperiodicity> assumption is needed for the finite-state <Markov chain ergodic theorem> for averages; indeed a uniform two-state target with $n=2,k=1$ gives a period-two alternating chain. This does not invalidate either the code or the state-average estimator.
