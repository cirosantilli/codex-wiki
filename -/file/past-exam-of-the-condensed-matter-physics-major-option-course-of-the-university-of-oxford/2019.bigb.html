<Infrared spectroscopy> measures absorption as the incident <photon> frequency is swept through molecular <normal mode> frequencies. In the electric-dipole approximation the interaction is $-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$. Expanding the molecular <electric dipole moment> in a <normal coordinate> $Q$ gives
$$
\mathbf p(Q)=\mathbf p(0)+\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf p}{\partial Q}\right)_0Q+\cdots.
$$
The fundamental one-quantum vibrational <transition dipole moment> is proportional to the derivative multiplying $Q$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\left(\partial\mathbf p/\partial Q\right)_0\ne0}
$$
is the criterion for <vibrational infrared activity>; the derivative must also have a component along the incident <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>. A permanent molecular <electric dipole moment> is not necessary. Absorption occurs near $\hbar\omega=E_{v+1}-E_v$, with broadened lines reflecting finite lifetimes and the molecular environment.

Use the indicated out-of-plane displacements and the equivalence of the four <oxygen> sites and of the two <carbon> sites in the isolated planar <oxalate> ion. In $V_1$, the two <oxygen> atoms on one side move oppositely to those on the other side, and the two <carbon> atoms also move oppositely. Their dipole changes cancel. The mode is even under molecular <inversion symmetry>, which independently forbids an electric-dipole transition.

In $V_2$, all four <oxygen> atoms move together out of the plane while both <carbon> atoms move in the opposite direction. The positive and negative charge distributions acquire a relative out-of-plane displacement, producing a nonzero derivative of the molecular <electric dipole moment>.

In $V_3$, two <oxygen> atoms move out and two move in, in a checkerboard arrangement, while the <carbon> atoms are stationary. The equivalent oxygen contributions cancel. This mode illustrates that being odd under <inversion symmetry> is necessary but not sufficient for <vibrational infrared activity>: its full symmetry does not transform like any component of an <electric dipole moment>.
$$
\boxed{V_2\text{ is IR active};\qquad V_1,V_3\text{ are IR inactive}.}
$$
These conclusions concern the isolated-molecule displacements drawn in the source; a different crystal environment can alter selection rules through additional symmetry breaking.

There are six atoms in the nonlinear <oxalate> ion, hence $3\cdot6=18$ positional degrees of freedom. Subtract three rigid translations and three rigid rotations to obtain $3N-6=12$ vibrational <normal modes>. Planarity does not remove out-of-plane motions. Since three modes have been displayed,
$$
\boxed{12-3=9\text{ additional vibrational normal modes}.}

The <primitive unit cell> contains two <carbon> atoms and four edge-shared <oxygen> atoms, each contributing one half: $n=2+4/2=4$ atoms. Equivalently, one molecular repeat contributes its two carbon atoms but only half of each of its four shared oxygen atoms. There are therefore $3n=12$ <phonon branches>.

At the <Brillouin zone> centre, three rigid translations give three <acoustic phonon branches>; the remaining $3n-3$ are <optical phonon branches>:
$$
\boxed{3\text{ acoustic}+9\text{ optical}=12\text{ phonon branches}.}
$$
A free layer has two in-plane acoustic modes and an out-of-plane flexural acoustic mode. A two-dimensional lattice does not imply that its atoms can move only in two directions. Artificially suppressing all out-of-plane motion would instead define a different model with two acoustic and six optical branches.

The primitive translations connect the two centred-rectangular sublattices. In the orientations marked in the source, $\mathbf a_p$ points right and down, whereas $\mathbf b_p$ points right and up. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_p=\frac12(\mathbf a_r-\mathbf b_r),\qquad\mathbf b_p=\frac12(\mathbf a_r+\mathbf b_r).}
$$
Their parallelogram has half the area of the rectangular cell, confirming that the latter contains two <primitive unit cells>.

Let the <reciprocal lattice vector> be $\mathbf G=h_r\mathbf a_r^*+k_r\mathbf b_r^*=h_p\mathbf a_p^*+k_p\mathbf b_p^*$, with each dual pair obeying $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a^*=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b^*=2\pi$ and zero cross products. Its coefficients are obtained by dotting with the corresponding direct-space <primitive lattice translation vectors>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
h_p&=\frac{\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_p}{2\pi}=\frac{h_r-k_r}{2},\\
k_p&=\frac{\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf b_p}{2\pi}=\frac{h_r+k_r}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{h_p=\tfrac12h_r-\tfrac12k_r,\qquad k_p=\tfrac12h_r+\tfrac12k_r.}
$$
For actual integer-indexed <reciprocal lattice vectors>, this also explains the centred-rectangular condition that $h_r$ and $k_r$ have the same parity. General <phonon> <wave vectors> need not have integer coefficients.

For ordinary one-phonon <infrared> <optical absorption>, both an energy and a momentum selection rule must hold. The optical <photon> has negligible <crystal momentum> on the scale of the <Brillouin zone>, so <crystal momentum conservation> selects $\mathbf q\simeq0$ modulo a genuine <reciprocal lattice vector>. In addition, the mode must change the macroscopic <electric polarization>, with a component along the incident <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>.

In terms of atomic displacements $\mathbf u_j$ and <Born effective charge> tensors $Z_j^*$, the relevant derivative is proportional to $\sum_jZ_j^*\mathbf u_j$ per <primitive unit cell>. Thus an <optical phonon> at $\mathbf q=0$ is not automatically infrared active: its dipole contributions may cancel. Disorder-assisted and multiphonon processes relax these simple first-order selection rules.

For a nondegenerate band $E(\mathbf k)$, define the <reciprocal effective mass tensor> by
$$
\boxed{(M^{-1})_{ij}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}.}
$$
It is the curvature tensor of the <energy band>. Near an extremum, $E\simeq E_0+\hbar^2\delta\mathbf k^TM^{-1}\delta\mathbf k/2$, so positive principal curvatures give positive electron <effective masses>. Together with the <group velocity> $\mathbf v=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf k}E$ and $\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=\mathbf F$, it yields $\dot v_i=(M^{-1})_{ij}F_j$. Negative electron curvature at a <valence band> maximum is conventionally recast as a positive <band effective mass> for missing electrons.

= Solution

In a parabolic conduction valley, $\mathbf v=\hbar M^{-1}\mathbf k$. The <Lorentz force> on an <electron> gives $M\dot{\mathbf v}=-e\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=e\mathbf B\times\mathbf v$. In the principal axes of the stated <effective mass tensor>,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\dot v_x\\\dot v_y\\\dot v_z\end{pmatrix}
=e\begin{pmatrix}0&-B_z/m_1&B_y/m_1\\B_z/m_1&0&-B_x/m_1\\-B_y/m_2&B_x/m_2&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}v_x\\v_y\\v_z\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Call the matrix on the right $A$. Its characteristic polynomial is
$$
\det(\lambda I-A)=\lambda\left[\lambda^2+e^2\left(\frac{B_z^2}{m_1^2}+\frac{B_x^2+B_y^2}{m_1m_2}\right)\right].
$$
One mode has zero frequency, corresponding to constant drift along the field; the other two have eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$. Thus
$$
\omega^2=e^2\frac{m_1(B_x^2+B_y^2)+m_2B_z^2}{m_1^2m_2},
\qquad
\boxed{\omega=\frac{e|\mathbf B|}{m_{\mathrm{cr}}},\quad m_{\mathrm{cr}}=|\mathbf B|\sqrt{\frac{m_1^2m_2}{m_1B_x^2+m_1B_y^2+m_2B_z^2}}.}
$$
More generally the <ellipsoidal cyclotron mass> is $m_{\mathrm{cr}}^2=\det M/(\widehat{\mathbf B}^TM\widehat{\mathbf B})$. The tensor axes must be rotated for the differently oriented valleys.

Use time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and assume the dopants supply $N$ mobile <electrons> per unit volume. The <Drude model> equation is
$$
m_e^*(\ddot{\mathbf x}+\gamma\dot{\mathbf x})=-e\mathbf E_0e^{-i\omega t},\qquad
\mathbf x_0=\frac{e\mathbf E_0}{m_e^*(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)}.
$$
The free-carrier <electric polarization> is $\mathbf P_c=-Ne\mathbf x$, so, after adding the bound-electron background,
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty\mathbf E+\mathbf P_c
=\epsilon_0\left[\epsilon_\infty-\frac{Ne^2}{\epsilon_0m_e^*(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)}\right]\mathbf E.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=\epsilon_\infty\left(1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}\right),\qquad\omega_p^2=\frac{Ne^2}{\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty m_e^*}.}
$$
The symbol $\omega_p$ here is the background-screened <plasma frequency>; the unscreened carrier frequency is $\sqrt{\epsilon_\infty}\,\omega_p$. For positive $\gamma$, the <imaginary part> of $\epsilon_r$ is positive in this time convention, consistent with absorption. Incomplete dopant ionization would replace the nominal doping density by the actual mobile <carrier density>.

For a nonmagnetic sample bordering air, the normal-incidence <Fresnel equations> give
$$
R=\left|\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}-1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}+1}\right|^2.
$$
At short infrared wavelengths the carrier response becomes small and $R$ approaches the background value $R_\infty$. Extrapolating the short-wavelength trend gives about $0.29$, hence
$$
\epsilon_\infty=\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{R_\infty}}{1-\sqrt{R_\infty}}\right)^2\simeq\boxed{11}.
$$
The leftmost point at $4\,\mu\mathrm m$ is about $0.27$ and has not quite reached that asymptote. As a consistency check, using the minimum below and $R(4\,\mu\mathrm m)\simeq0.27$ gives $\epsilon_\infty\simeq11$ without treating that point as the limiting value.

The minimum is near $\lambda_{\min}\simeq13\,\mu\mathrm m$. For weak damping, zero <optical reflectance> means refractive-index matching, $\epsilon_r=1$, rather than $\epsilon_r=0$. Thus
$$
\omega_p^2=\left(1-\frac1{\epsilon_\infty}\right)\omega_{\min}^2,
\qquad
\boxed{m_e^*=\frac{Ne^2\lambda_{\min}^2}{4\pi^2\epsilon_0c^2(\epsilon_\infty-1)}\simeq0.082m_e,}
$$
using $N=5.4\times10^{24}\,\mathrm m^{-3}$. Given graphical uncertainty, about $0.08m_e$ is an appropriate estimate. This is a <carrier effective mass from a reflectivity minimum>, not an independent determination of the bare <electron mass>.

Just beyond the minimum, $\operatorname{Re}\epsilon_r$ passes through zero near $\lambda_p=\lambda_{\min}/\sqrt{1-1/\epsilon_\infty}\simeq13.6\,\mu\mathrm m$. At longer wavelengths it becomes negative and reflection rises sharply toward the conducting regime. Finite damping rounds the edge, prevents a strictly exact zero minimum, and keeps the plotted long-wavelength reflectivity below unity.

The broad feature has the scale expected for a carrier <plasmon>, a longitudinal collective oscillation of the mobile electron density. Using the parameters just estimated and $N=10^{24}\,\mathrm m^{-3}$ gives
$$
\widetilde\nu_p=\frac{\omega_p}{2\pi c}=\frac1{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac{Ne^2}{\epsilon_0\epsilon_\infty m_e^*}}\simeq\boxed{316\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}}.

Conversely a bare screened <plasma oscillation> at $340\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ would require $N\simeq1.16\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ for the same parameters, consistent with the source's approximate density. Its broadness is also consistent with carrier damping, unlike the much sharper lattice line near $270\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

This identifies a **plasmon-related** feature, not a uniquely determined undamped pure electronic mode. <Gallium arsenide> is polar, and its longitudinal <optical phonon> couples to the carrier <plasmon>. A more complete dielectric model has longitudinal eigenfrequencies
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\frac12\left[\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2+\omega_p^2\pm\sqrt{(\omega_{\mathrm{LO}}^2+\omega_p^2)^2-4\omega_p^2\omega_{\mathrm{TO}}^2}\right].

The measured <Raman scattering> maximum can also shift with damping and the scattering matrix elements. Experimental <Raman scattering> from these coupled modes and carrier-collision broadening is established independently.<Raman Scattering by Coupled Optical-Phonon-Plasmon Modes in GaAs (1968)>[Abstract: differently doped samples, coupled modes, and linewidths dominated by electron collisions]{ref} The supplied approximate density and one broad spectrum do not determine a unique coupled-mode fit. Treating $340\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ as an exact pure-<plasmon> eigenfrequency would overstate the available information.

= Solution

For a free transition-metal ion, degeneracy among different orbital states can support an orbital contribution to its <magnetic moment>. In a crystal, the electric fields and bonding of the surrounding ligands split those states. If the orbital ground state becomes nondegenerate and is separated from excited orbital states, its wavefunction can usually be chosen real in zero field. Since the <orbital angular momentum> operator is imaginary in a real-orbital basis, its expectation value then vanishes.

This suppression is <orbital quenching>. The remaining leading <magnetic moment> is the spin contribution, approximately $g\mu_B\mathbf S$ with $g\simeq2$. <Spin-orbit coupling> admixes excited orbital states and produces residual anisotropy and shifts in the <g-factor>; quenching does not mean that every orbital matrix element or every higher-order orbital response vanishes. A degenerate ground orbital multiplet need not be quenched until a further distortion removes its degeneracy.

For the regular octahedron, repulsion from the six oxygen ligands raises the two $e_g$ orbitals, $d_{z^2}$ and $d_{x^2-y^2}$, above the three $t_{2g}$ orbitals, $d_{xy},d_{xz},d_{yz}$. The latter point between the metal-ligand axes and experience less direct repulsion. The <crystal field splitting> is therefore a lower triplet and an upper doublet.

For the $3d^2$ V$^{3+}$ ion, <Hund's rules> put the two electrons into different $t_{2g}$ orbitals with parallel spins, giving $S=1$. There are three choices for the unoccupied $t_{2g}$ orbital, so the orbital ground state is triply degenerate. Its spin degeneracy is separate. This orbital degeneracy makes a symmetry-lowering <Jahn-Teller effect> possible.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Take the elongation axis as $z$. The axial oxygen ligands move farther away while the equatorial ones move closer, at fixed mean bond length. Orbitals containing $z$ are stabilized relative to their in-plane partners. Thus $d_{xz},d_{yz}$ move below $d_{xy}$ within $t_{2g}$, while $d_{z^2}$ moves below $d_{x^2-y^2}$ within $e_g$. The schematic level ordering appears in the figure below; the octahedral gap remains open for a small distortion.

The two parallel-spin electrons occupy $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$, one each. Filling the entire lower doublet gives a unique orbital configuration, so its orbital degeneracy is one and the spin is still $S=1$. This is the orbitally quenched configuration relevant to the later spin Hamiltonian.

The energetic preference can be made explicit in a traceless linear splitting model. For an elongation coordinate $Q>0$, write the $t_{2g}$ levels relative to their barycentre as $(-aQ,-aQ,2aQ)$ with $a>0$. Their two-electron ground energy decreases by $2aQ$. For an equal-magnitude compression, the levels are $(-2aQ,aQ,aQ)$ and the two-electron energy decreases only by $aQ$. With the same quadratic elastic cost, minimizing $-2aQ+kQ^2/2$ gives four times the stabilization of minimizing $-aQ+kQ^2/2$. Thus the small-distortion <Jahn-Teller effect> favors elongation for this high-spin $d^2$ filling. The argument assumes the linear coupling and harmonic elastic model, rather than claiming that symmetry alone fixes all real-material distortions.

For axial compression along $z$, the ordering is reversed: $d_{xy}$ lies below the $d_{xz},d_{yz}$ doublet, and $d_{x^2-y^2}$ lies below $d_{z^2}$ within $e_g$. One electron occupies $d_{xy}$ and the other occupies either $d_{xz}$ or $d_{yz}$, with parallel spins in the weak-distortion regime. The orbital degeneracy is therefore two, with $S=1$.

The retained orbital degeneracy allows further symmetry lowering and explains why this compressed arrangement does not provide the fully quenched equilibrium of the elongated case in the specified simple model.

The term $-\alpha\epsilon$ is the leading electronic energy gain from splitting the initially degenerate orbitals and occupying the lower levels. Its negative sign expresses the lowering of the occupied-electron energy for the chosen elongation branch of the <Jahn-Teller effect>. The positive coefficient $\alpha$ is a linear electron-lattice coupling in this reduced model.

The term $\beta\epsilon^2/2$ is the harmonic elastic cost of moving the ligands away from the undistorted reference structure. Mechanical stability requires $\beta>0$. The competition between linear gain and quadratic cost produces a finite equilibrium distortion rather than indefinite elongation.

Complete the square:
$$
E(\epsilon)=\frac\beta2\left(\epsilon-\frac\alpha\beta\right)^2-\frac{\alpha^2}{2\beta}.
$$
The minimum is stable because $d^2E/d\epsilon^2=\beta>0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_0=\frac\alpha\beta,\qquad E_{\mathrm{JT}}=E(0)-E(\epsilon_0)=\frac{\alpha^2}{2\beta}.}
$$
Here the <Jahn-Teller stabilization energy> is the positive magnitude of the energy reduction.

The low-temperature bond-length difference is $\epsilon_0=2.05-1.98=0.07\,\text{\AA}$. With the question's thermal criterion,
$$
E_{\mathrm{JT}}\simeq k_BT_{\mathrm{JT}}=(8.6173\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}})(200\,\mathrm K)=0.01723\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Use $\alpha=\beta\epsilon_0$ and $E_{\mathrm{JT}}=\beta\epsilon_0^2/2$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{2E_{\mathrm{JT}}}{\epsilon_0}\simeq0.492\,\mathrm{eV\,\text{\AA}^{-1}},\qquad\beta=\frac{2E_{\mathrm{JT}}}{\epsilon_0^2}\simeq7.03\,\mathrm{eV\,\text{\AA}^{-2}}.}
$$
In SI units these are about $7.89\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm N$ and $113\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, respectively. They are estimates within the stipulated energy criterion; a cooperative transition also involves interactions and entropy, so its temperature is not in general exactly an isolated-ion stabilization energy divided by $k_B$.

Above the transition, $\epsilon=0$ and both site types have the same <g-factor> $g$. For the given spin-only Zeeman Hamiltonian, adjacent $S=1$ levels have the same energy spacing, so fixed-frequency <electron paramagnetic resonance> gives a single resonance field $B_0=h\nu/(g\mu_B)$, ignoring unresolved additional interactions.

Below the transition, half the elongation axes lie along $x$ and half along $y$. A field along $x$ is parallel to the long bond on one sublattice and perpendicular on the other. The line splits into
$$
\boxed{B_\parallel=\frac{h\nu}{\mu_B(g+\epsilon/\gamma)}<B_0,\qquad B_\perp=\frac{h\nu}{\mu_B(g-\epsilon/\delta)}>B_0.}
$$
The separation grows on cooling as the distortion grows. Equal site populations give comparable integrated weights when the microwave coupling to the two sites is equivalent; populations alone do not fix all polarization-dependent intensities.

A suitable staggered structural <order parameter> is
$$
\Psi=\frac12[(l_x-l_y)_A-(l_x-l_y)_B].
$$
For an $x$-elongated A site and a $y$-elongated B site, $\Psi=\epsilon$, with the opposite sign for the domain interchanging the pattern. The <electron paramagnetic resonance> data determine its magnitude from
$$
\boxed{|\Psi|=\frac{(h\nu/\mu_B)(B_\parallel^{-1}-B_\perp^{-1})}{\gamma^{-1}+\delta^{-1}}.}
$$
For small splitting this is equivalently proportional to $B_\perp-B_\parallel$, but the reciprocal-field formula follows directly from the stated <g-factors>. It does not fix the sign of the structural domain. In a continuous transition $|\Psi|$ tends continuously to zero at $T_{\mathrm{JT}}$ and grows below it, eventually saturating. A mean-field sketch has $|\Psi|\propto(T_{\mathrm{JT}}-T)^{1/2}$ close to the transition; continuity alone does not determine that exponent. The last panel of the original orbital figure shows this illustrative behavior.

Choose the four basis-spin signs as $(+,-,-,+)$ in the order of positions listed in the source; reversing all signs changes no diffraction intensity. Apart from the common <magnetic form factor> and perpendicular-moment factor, the <magnetic structure factor> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_M(hkl)&=m[e^{i\pi h}-e^{i\pi k}-e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(k+l)}]\\
&=m[(-1)^h-(-1)^k][1-(-1)^l].
\end{aligned}
$$
The first factor is nonzero exactly when $h$ and $k$ have opposite parity, and the second exactly when $l$ is odd. Thus the magnetic Bragg peaks require
$$
\boxed{h+k=2n+1,\qquad l=2m+1.}
$$
When both conditions hold, $|F_M|=4|m|$ before geometric and magnetic-form-factor weights. These are magnetic selection rules for the antiferromagnetic component, not conditions forbidding all nuclear diffraction at other indices.

Measure several allowed magnetic reflections from an oriented single crystal by <magnetic neutron diffraction>, preferably with polarization analysis. A <neutron> couples to the component of the ordered <magnetic moment> perpendicular to the scattering vector $\mathbf Q$. For a collinear arrangement, the geometric intensity factor is

Compare reflections whose $\mathbf Q$ point in different crystal directions, correcting for the <magnetic form factor> and known experimental geometry. Their relative intensities distinguish candidate spin directions; a moment parallel to $\mathbf Q$ gives no magnetic signal from that component. Polarized <neutrons> help separate nuclear and magnetic contributions. Observing only peak positions establishes periodicity, not the spin direction.

Treat the ordered spins as classical vectors of fixed length $S$, as in the requested mean-field energy. Each iron site has six nearest neighbors: four in the $xy$ plane and two along $z$. All are antiparallel in the collinear state. Each exchange bond contributes $-JS^2$ and is counted only once, giving $-(6/2)JS^2=-3JS^2$ per spin.

The easy-axis term contributes $-KS^2$ because $S_i^x=\pm S$. The cross product of two collinear spins is zero, so the term proportional to $D$ contributes nothing. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_0=-S^2(3J+K).}
$$

The canted pattern has opposite $x$ components but a common $z$ component. Write neighboring spins as $\mathbf S_A=S(\cos\theta,0,\sin\theta)$ and $\mathbf S_B=S(-\cos\theta,0,\sin\theta)$. Their scalar product is $-S^2\cos2\theta$, and the cross-product magnitude is $S^2|\sin2\theta|$.

There are three exchange bonds per spin, two eligible $xy$ bonds per spin for the $D$ term, and one single-ion term per spin. Hence
$$
E=-S^2[3J\cos2\theta+K\cos^2\theta+2D|\sin2\theta|].
$$
For the branch $\theta\ge0$ close to the collinear state,
$$
\boxed{f(\theta)=\cos2\theta,\qquad g(\theta)=\cos^2\theta,\qquad h(\theta)=\sin2\theta.}
$$
Minimization is equivalent to maximizing $(3J+K/2)\cos2\theta+2D\sin2\theta$, which gives
$$
\boxed{\tan2\theta=\frac{4D}{6J+K},\qquad\theta=\frac12\arctan\frac{4D}{6J+K}\simeq0.00518\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq0.297^\circ.}
$$
The chosen branch is a minimum of the energy because it maximizes that positive-amplitude sinusoid. The resulting state is a <canted antiferromagnet> with a small common $z$ moment. The source's absolute-value interaction selects a canting magnitude but is symmetric between the two signs of the weak moment; it is not an oriented vector <Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction> specifying a unique handedness.

In a conventional <superconductor>, an attractive pairing interaction forms <Cooper pairs> and a phase-coherent condensate. The elementary fermionic excitations have a <superconducting energy gap> $\Delta$; breaking a pair into two excitations costs at least $2\Delta$. The density of thermally excited <quasiparticles> is exponentially small for $k_BT\ll\Delta$. Nevertheless the vanishing DC <electrical resistivity> below the critical temperature is a property of the coherent supercurrent, not a claim that all thermal quasiparticles disappear. Below the relevant critical-current and critical-field limits, a homogeneous bulk sample carries dissipationless current; above the superconducting transition the normal resistivity returns.

In a <semiconductor>, the <band gap> separates filled valence states from empty conduction states because of the periodic crystal potential and orbital hybridization. Thermally exciting carriers across the gap produces both conduction <electrons> and valence <holes>. For an intrinsic sample,
$$
n=p\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)},\qquad\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h).
$$
Thus its <electrical resistivity> generally rises rapidly on cooling, with an activated factor $e^{E_g/(2k_BT)}$ up to mobility and density-of-states prefactors. This differs from the zero-resistivity condensate response. Dopant freeze-out and extrinsic-carrier regimes require their own activation scales; the intrinsic formula is not universal for every doped sample.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Use a normal-metal--insulator--superconductor junction with a weak tunnelling barrier. For a nearly constant normal-metal <density of states>, the differential conductance probes the thermally broadened superconducting quasiparticle <density of states>. At sufficiently low temperature, the ideal <BCS theory> result is
$$
N_s(E)/N_0=\operatorname{Re}\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}},\qquad R_N\frac{dI}{dV}=N_s(eV)/N_0.
$$
There is very little single-electron tunnelling for $|eV|<\Delta$, while a sharp onset occurs at $|eV|=\Delta$. The gap can therefore be obtained from the two symmetric onset voltages, $\Delta=e|V_{\mathrm{onset}}|$. For this one-superconductor junction the threshold is $\Delta/e$, not $2\Delta/e$.

Integrating at zero temperature gives the useful current sketch
$$
\boxed{I(V)=\frac{\operatorname{sgn}V}{eR_N}\sqrt{(eV)^2-\Delta^2}\,\Theta(|eV|-\Delta).}
$$
The curve is odd, nearly flat inside the gap, and approaches the normal-state line $V/R_N$ at large $|V|$. The ideal gap-edge singularity is in the slope, not in the current itself. At finite temperature below the transition the onset rounds and thermally activated subgap current appears. Measuring several temperatures distinguishes this rounding from the closing of $\Delta(T)$.

Rearrange the source's condensate-current expression and integrate around a closed path lying in the superconducting material:
$$
\hbar\oint\nabla\theta\cdot d\mathbf l=q\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l+\frac{m}{n_sq}\oint\mathbf J_s\cdot d\mathbf l.
$$
Single-valuedness of the superconducting wavefunction requires $\oint\nabla\theta\cdot d\mathbf l=2\pi n$, and <Stokes' theorem> gives $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l=\Phi$. Thus the exact result is <fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor>:
$$
\boxed{\Phi+\frac{m}{n_sq^2}\oint\mathbf J_s\cdot d\mathbf l=\frac{nh}{q}.}
$$
Reversing the winding orientation absorbs the sign of $q$. Since a <Cooper pair> has $|q|=2e$, the positive <superconducting flux quantum> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi_0=\frac{h}{2e}\simeq2.068\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{Wb}.}
$$
For a thick ring, choose the path deep enough that the supercurrent is negligible; then the enclosed <magnetic flux> is $n\Phi_0$. This is the flux quantization usually intended in the question.

**Qualification of the unqualified flux claim:** a thin ring can have a nonzero kinetic contribution around every available path, so it is the <fluxoid> that is exactly quantized. For example, with zero applied flux and geometric and kinetic inductances $L_g=L_k$, a metastable winding-$1$ state has $(L_g+L_k)I=\Phi_0$ but $\Phi=L_gI=\Phi_0/2$. This concrete counterexample satisfies the given current law while its magnetic flux is not an integer multiple of $\Phi_0$.

Let $x$ cross the barrier from electrode 1 to 2, let $z$ run along its width, and take $\mathbf B=B\widehat{\mathbf y}$. In the ideal barrier-only model choose $A_x=Bz$, which has $(\nabla\times\mathbf A)_y=B$. Then the crossing integral at fixed $z$ is $Btz$. With the sign convention used in the source,
$$
\boxed{\varphi(z)=\varphi(0)-\frac{2\pi Bt}{\Phi_0}z=\varphi(0)-\frac{2\pi\Phi}{W\Phi_0}z,\qquad\Phi=BtW.}
$$
Reversing the field or the phase orientation reverses the slope and does not change the interference envelope. The same result follows by comparing two paths across the junction and using the enclosed <magnetic flux>.

If field penetration into thick identical electrodes is included, the effective magnetic thickness is approximately $t_{\mathrm{eff}}=t+2\lambda_L$, where $\lambda_L$ is the <London penetration depth>. Replace $t$ and $\Phi$ consistently by $t_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $Bt_{\mathrm{eff}}W$. The linear phase profile assumes a uniform applied field and negligible magnetic self-field of the junction current.

= Solution

Let $L$ be the junction length perpendicular to the width and $J_0$ be uniform. Put $\kappa=2\pi\Phi/(W\Phi_0)$. Integrating the local <current-phase relation of a superconducting weak link> over the junction gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_s(\Phi;\varphi_c)&=J_0L\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\sin(\varphi_c-\kappa z)\,dz\\
&=J_0LW\sin\varphi_c\,\frac{\sin(\kappa W/2)}{\kappa W/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The odd-in-$z$ term integrates to zero. At fixed central phase $\varphi_c=\varphi(0)$, define $I(0)=J_0LW\sin\varphi_c$. Then
$$
\boxed{I_s(\Phi)=I(0)\frac{\sin(\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)}{\pi\Phi/\Phi_0}.}
$$
The value at zero flux is the continuous limit, and zeros occur at nonzero integer multiples of $\Phi_0$. This signed formula is the source's expression for a fixed phase. If the measured quantity is the maximum sustainable current, maximize over the central phase instead:
$$
\boxed{I_c(\Phi)=J_0LW\left|\frac{\sin(\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)}{\pi\Phi/\Phi_0}\right|.}
$$
For example, at $\Phi=3\Phi_0/2$ the signed coefficient is $-2/(3\pi)$, whereas the critical current is positive. The absolute-value envelope is the <Fraunhofer pattern of a Josephson junction>, shown in the right panel of the original tunnelling figure. Its simple form relies on uniform $J_0$ and the linear phase profile; it is not the two-junction cosine pattern of a <DC SQUID>.
