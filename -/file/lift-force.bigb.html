= Lift
{synonym}

<Lift> is the component of the <fluid> <force> perpendicular to the far-field flow relative to the body. It need not point vertically: a tilted flow changes the lift direction, and a banked wing can produce a sideways component. In a planar problem choose a unit <vector> $\mathbf e_D$ along the incoming relative <velocity> $\mathbf U_\infty$ and a perpendicular unit <vector> $\mathbf e_L$ pointing towards the chosen positive-lift side. Then
$$
U_\infty=|\mathbf U_\infty|,\qquad
D=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf e_D,\qquad
L=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf e_L,\qquad
\mathbf F=D\mathbf e_D+L\mathbf e_L.
$$
Here $D$ is <drag> and $L$ is signed <lift>; $L<0$ means a <force> towards the opposite side. In three dimensions the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf U_\infty$ also admits a separate side-force direction, so a lift axis must be specified. <Lift> is also distinct from <buoyancy>, which can exist without a relative flow. https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/aerodynamic-forces/[NASA Glenn, Aerodynamic Forces: components relative to the flow]{ref}

The <force> comes from adding local surface <tractions>, rather than assigning a single <pressure> to each side. Let $\Sigma$ be the wetted body surface and $\mathbf n$ its unit <normal vector> pointing out of the body into the <fluid>. The <pressure> contribution pushes inwards; the <viscous stress tensor> also contributes, including tangential <shear stress>:
$$
\mathbf F=\int_\Sigma\left[-(p-p_\infty)\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}\mathbf n\right]dA,
\qquad
L=\int_\Sigma\left[-(p-p_\infty)\mathbf n+\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}\mathbf n\right]\cdot\mathbf e_L\,dA.
$$
Using a constant reference <pressure> $p_\infty$ is legitimate on a closed surface because $\int_\Sigma\mathbf n\,dA=0$. This <surface integral> makes the direction explicit: a surface-normal <force> is generally neither pure <lift> nor pure <drag>. <Pressure> and skin <shear stress> both enter the resultant, although the <pressure> term usually dominates <lift> on an <airfoil>. https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/aerodynamic-forces/[NASA Glenn, Aerodynamic Forces: pressure and shear contributions]{ref}

The <lift coefficient> isolates a useful scale. For a finite wing of reference area $S$ (normally its projected planform area), and for a two-dimensional <airfoil> section of chord length $c$, respectively,
$$
q_\infty=\frac12\rho_\infty U_\infty^2,\qquad
C_L=\frac{L}{q_\infty S},\qquad
c_l=\frac{L'}{q_\infty c}.
$$
Here $q_\infty$ is <dynamic pressure>, $\rho_\infty$ is far-field <mass density>, and $L'$ is <lift> per unit span, with units $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. The signs of $C_L$ and $c_l$ follow the chosen lift axis. For stipulated values $\rho_\infty=1.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $U_\infty=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $S=0.50\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and $C_L=0.60$, $q_\infty=240\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $L=72\,\mathrm N$. Doubling $U_\infty$ gives four times the <lift> only if $C_L$ stays fixed: changes in <Reynolds number>, <Mach number>, surface condition or <angle of attack> can change it. https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/lift-coefficient/[NASA Glenn, Lift Coefficient: normalization and similarity limits]{ref}

A motivating experiment is to compare two independently observed quantities: the <lift> registered by a force balance, and the <lift> inferred from surface <pressures>. A low-speed wind tunnel holds the model fixed while air passes it at adjustable speed; a calibrated balance measures <forces>, while small flush holes on both surfaces connect by tubing to differential-pressure sensors referenced to $p_\infty$. Iowa State's GA(W)-1 teaching model has $43$ taps around a $101\,\mathrm{mm}$ chord; its instructions give their coordinates and close the contour by repeating the first tap. A classroom comparison can combine this pressure-panel method with a balance on the same model; this is an experiment design, not a claim that those instructions report a combined measurement. https://www.aere.iastate.edu/~huhui/teaching/2026-08F/AerE344/lab-instruction/AerE344L-Lab-05-Pressure-Airfoil-instruction.pdf[Hui Hu, Iowa State, AerE344 Lab 05, p. 2: GA(W)-1 taps and pressure panels]{ref} https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/drag-measurement/[NASA Glenn, Drag Measurement: force balances and calibration]{ref}

Before turning on the wind, apply known positive and negative loads to the balance through its resolving centre; use $mg$ for the known load from a mass. Record all balance channels during loading and unloading, including response to <drag> and <torque> loads, to detect cross coupling, hysteresis and zero drift. Record a wind-off tare at each model orientation so its <weight> does not become an aerodynamic <lift> reading. Map tunnel flow direction and uniformity, rather than using the fan setting as a speed measurement. Covert's MIT guide describes these calibration, tare and flow-survey requirements. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-621-experimental-projects-i-spring-2003/f38ecde8773fd36c0d8f9069e6aa1df5_student_wt_guide.pdf[Eugene E. Covert, A Student's Introduction to the Wright Brothers Wind Tunnel at MIT, pp. 3–8]{ref}

For each pressure channel, apply zero and several known <pressure> differences spanning the expected range; determine the voltage-to-pressure relation and recheck it after a run. Inspect tubing for leaks and blocked taps, and wait for readings to settle before averaging: tubing can delay a pressure signal. This calibration principle is used in NASA's electronically scanned pressure systems, where several applied calibration pressures determine each sensor's response and repeated calibration tracks drift. A simple pressure standard can instead use a liquid-column height difference and the <hydrostatic pressure> law. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19890009044/downloads/19890009044.pdf[Patsy S. Fulton, NASA-TM-101531, p. 8: pressure-module calibration]{ref}

Measure far-field static and total <pressure> outside the model's <fluid wake>; in low-speed <incompressible flow> their difference gives $q_\infty$ by the <Bernoulli equation>, so $U_\infty=\sqrt{2q_\infty/\rho_\infty}$. Record <temperature> and ambient <pressure> to determine <mass density> using the <ideal gas law>. At fixed $q_\infty$, step the <angle of attack>, repeat selected settings, and sweep back down to check reproducibility. Record balance <lift>, each tap's mean <pressure>, and fluctuation sizes. NASA describes pressure probes connected to differential transducers and emphasizes recording their reference <pressure>. https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/tunptm.html[NASA Glenn, Total Pressure Measurement: probes, tubing and pressure references]{ref}

Convert each tap to a <pressure coefficient> $C_{p,i}=(p_i-p_\infty)/q_\infty$. For consecutive taps bounding a panel of length $\Delta s_i$ with outward <normal vector> $\mathbf n_i$, a <trapezoidal rule> estimate of the pressure <force> per unit span is
$$
\mathbf F'_p\simeq-q_\infty\sum_i\frac{C_{p,i}+C_{p,i+1}}2\mathbf n_i\Delta s_i,\qquad
L'_p=\mathbf F'_p\cdot\mathbf e_L.
$$
Compare this with balance <lift> divided by span only when the spanwise flow is approximately uniform. Agreement tests the pressure-resultant picture; disagreement calls for checking unresolved leading-edge pressure variations, skin <shear stress>, support loads and three-dimensional end effects before rejecting it. A force balance senses the total load, while taps alone reconstruct the pressure part. Tunnel walls and model blockage alter the effective flow and require corrections; end plates or a model extending between walls approximate two-dimensional flow but do not guarantee it. Record the <Reynolds number> and <Mach number> with the result, and do not interpret tunnel <lift coefficients> as universal wing data. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-621-experimental-projects-i-spring-2003/f38ecde8773fd36c0d8f9069e6aa1df5_student_wt_guide.pdf[Covert, pp. 8–9: flow characterization and wall effects]{ref} https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/lift-coefficient/[NASA Glenn, Lift Coefficient: Reynolds and Mach number dependence]{ref}

= Lift coefficient
{parent=Lift (force)}
{title2=$C_L=L/(q_\infty S)$}

The signed <lift> divided by <dynamic pressure> times a stated reference area. For a two-dimensional <airfoil>, use $c_l=L'/(q_\infty c)$ instead. Reference area and experimental flow conditions are part of the definition of a reported coefficient; see <lift> for a worked scale and a pressure/balance experiment.

= Dynamic pressure
{parent=Lift coefficient}
{title2=$q=\rho U^2/2$}

<Dynamic pressure> is the bulk <kinetic energy> per unit <volume> associated with <fluid> motion at <speed> $U$ in a specified <reference frame>:
$$
q=\frac12\rho U^2,\qquad
\frac{\tfrac12mU^2}{V}=\frac12\rho U^2,\qquad
\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}=\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
Here $\rho=m/V$ is <mass density>. The word "pressure" identifies its units and its use as a <pressure> scale; $q$ is not an extra isotropic <stress> to add to the local <static pressure> in every surface <traction>. A stationary <fluid> can have large <static pressure> with $q=0$. Conversely, knowing $U$ and $\rho$ fixes $q$ without fixing <static pressure>. The definition remains useful in <viscous fluid flow> and <compressible flow>, even when the simplest <Bernoulli equation> is inapplicable. <Dynamic Pressure by NASA Glenn Research Center>{ref}

For stipulated <mass density> $\rho_\infty=1.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and reference <speed> $U_\infty=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $q_\infty=240\,\mathrm{Pa}$. At $40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with the same <mass density>, $q_\infty=960\,\mathrm{Pa}$. This quadratic scaling makes <dynamic pressure> a useful normalization for the <lift coefficient>, <drag coefficient> and <pressure coefficient>; it predicts four times a particular <force> only if its corresponding coefficient remains unchanged.

{title=A <Pitot-static tube> compares <stagnation pressure> with <static pressure>}
{description=Original schematic with separate <pressure> passages and a differential readout. The connection with <stagnation pressure> requires hypotheses. For <steady flow>, constant <mass density>, <inviscid flow>, and <gravity> as the only <body force>, the <Bernoulli equation> gives, along one <streamline>,
$$
p+\frac12\rho U^2+\rho gz=B.
$$
Here $p$ is <static pressure>, $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>, and $z$ is height. Bring that flow to rest without losses at the same height and the limiting <static pressure> is the <stagnation pressure> $p_0$:
$$
p_0=p+q,\qquad p_0-p=\frac12\rho U^2.
$$
There must be no intervening pump supplying <work> or other energy input. Constancy along one <streamline> does not assert equal $B$ on all <streamlines>; <irrotational flow> in a connected region supplies that additional conclusion. Changing height requires retaining $\rho gz$. In <compressible flow> the stopping <pressure> difference generally differs from $\rho U^2/2$, and <viscous dissipation> can reduce recovered <stagnation pressure>. <Bernoulli’s Equation by NASA Glenn Research Center>{ref}

A <Pitot-static tube> turns this relation into a measurement: its upstream-facing opening senses <stagnation pressure>, while separate side openings sample <static pressure>. Connect these to the positive and negative ports of a differential <pressure transducer> or <manometer>. Under the hypotheses above, the measured difference $\Delta p$ gives
$$
U=\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta p}{\rho}}.
$$
Thus the stipulated $240\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $1.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ imply $20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, not an independently measured <speed>. Align the probe with the incoming flow and keep its passages clear: blockage and pinched tubing invalidate the comparison. At very low <speed>, the difference can be smaller than instrument error; at high <Mach number>, <compressibility> and possibly a <shock wave> require a different reduction. <Pitot – Static Tube – Speedometer by NASA Glenn Research Center>{ref}

A useful control is <differential-pressure calibration>. For a digital <manometer>, zeroing establishes the reading at equal port <pressures>; it does not establish the gain away from zero. The manufacturer's <Series 475 Handheld Digital Manometer: Specifications - Installation and Operating Instructions by Dwyer Instruments, Inc.> directs the user to vent both ports to the atmosphere before zeroing. Its stated accuracy is a fraction of full scale, not of the reading, and depends on instrument <temperature>. Select a suitable range and record the instrument variant and units; the manual covers several ranges.<Series 475 Handheld Digital Manometer: Specifications - Installation and Operating Instructions by Dwyer Instruments, Inc.>[p. 1: Specifications, Model Chart and ZERO instructions]{ref}

An independent physical check can use a liquid-column <manometer>: apply a stable <pressure> difference to both instruments and compare the digital reading with the <hydrostatic pressure> inferred from the vertical level difference. For equal-height connections carrying a gas of <mass density> $\rho_g$ above a manometric liquid of <mass density> $\rho_m$,
$$
|\Delta p|=(\rho_m-\rho_g)g|h|.
$$
This follows by balancing <hydrostatic pressure> down and up the two connected columns. Use vertical height, even if a tube is inclined. Known <mass densities>, measured height and <gravitational acceleration> set the comparison scale. Check several differences across the working range and reverse the port connections to check the sign. This is a proposed independent check, not a claim that the cited teaching experiment performed a full instrument calibration. Recheck zero after reconnecting the sealed tubing; a zero adjustment alone cannot detect a blocked passage or an incorrect scale.

= Pressure coefficient
{parent=Lift (force)}
{title2=$C_p=(p-p_\infty)/q_\infty$}

The <pressure coefficient> compares a local <static pressure> with a specified reference <static pressure>, using a fixed reference <dynamic pressure>:
$$
C_p(\mathbf x)=\frac{p(\mathbf x)-p_\infty}{q_\infty},\qquad
q_\infty=\frac12\rho_\infty U_\infty^2>0.
$$
The reference <mass density>, <speed>, <pressure> and location must accompany a reported <pressure coefficient>. Keep the same $q_\infty$ throughout a surface distribution; replacing it by local $\rho U^2/2$ gives a different quantity and fails at a <stagnation point>. The definition itself requires neither <Bernoulli's principle> nor <inviscid flow>, so measured <pressure coefficients> remain useful when those idealizations fail.

Positive $C_p$ means $p>p_\infty$; zero means equality; negative $C_p$ means $p<p_\infty$. This sign does not specify the sign of <absolute pressure>. For stipulated $p_\infty=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ absolute and $q_\infty=240\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $C_p=-3$ gives $p=100605\,\mathrm{Pa}$ absolute: a $720\,\mathrm{Pa}$ deficit, not a negative <absolute pressure>. If $p_\infty$ equals <atmospheric pressure>, the numerator is <gauge pressure>; a <wind tunnel>'s reference <static pressure> need not equal room <atmospheric pressure>. These are reference choices, not different physical <pressures>.

Under the same-height, constant-<mass density> $\rho=\rho_\infty$, <steady flow> and <inviscid flow> hypotheses of the <Bernoulli equation>, and with the same <Bernoulli invariant> as the reference flow,
$$
p+\frac12\rho U^2=p_\infty+\frac12\rho U_\infty^2,
\qquad C_p=1-\left(\frac{U}{U_\infty}\right)^2.
$$
The first equation immediately gives the second after subtracting $p_\infty$ and dividing by $q_\infty$. Hence a lossless <stagnation point> has $C_p=1$, $U=U_\infty$ gives $C_p=0$, and $U=2U_\infty$ gives $C_p=-3$. For a real stationary wall satisfying the <no-slip boundary condition>, the <velocity> is zero right at the wall; inserting that wall <velocity> in an <inviscid flow> formula would wrongly predict $C_p=1$ everywhere. A thin <boundary layer> approximation instead relates the wall <pressure> to the outer-flow <pressure> and uses the outer <velocity> where the <Bernoulli equation> applies.

For <potential flow around a circular cylinder> of radius $a$ with zero <circulation>, the surface tangential <velocity> is $u_\theta=-2U_\infty\sin\theta$. Thus the ideal <pressure coefficient> is
$$
C_p(\theta)=1-4\sin^2\theta.
$$
With $\theta$ measured from downstream, the front is at $\pi$ and the rear at zero. Both ideal <stagnation points> have $C_p=1$, while the sides at $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$ have $C_p=-3$. This exact model is a useful baseline for an experiment; its complete rearward <pressure> recovery is not a prediction for a separated real <fluid wake>.

To obtain a <force>, integrate the local normal contributions with their directions. Let $\mathbf n$ be the unit <normal vector> from the body into the <fluid> and $\Sigma$ its closed wetted surface:
$$
\mathbf F_p=-\int_\Sigma p\mathbf n\,dA
=-q_\infty\int_\Sigma C_p\mathbf n\,dA,
\qquad \int_\Sigma\mathbf n\,dA=\mathbf0.
$$
The second equality removes a spatially constant reference <pressure> over the closed surface; it does not remove a nonuniform <hydrostatic pressure> or justify dropping the reference term on an isolated open patch. A negative $C_p$ contributes outwards in this reference-subtracted calculation, even though a positive <absolute pressure> still pushes inwards locally. Project $\mathbf F_p$ onto the chosen <lift> and <drag> directions. The full <fluid> <force> also includes $\int_\Sigma\boldsymbol\sigma_{\rm visc}\mathbf n\,dA$ from the <viscous stress tensor>, including tangential <shear stress>; <pressure tapping> does not measure that contribution.

For the two-dimensional cylinder, <force> per unit span follows from $dA=a\,d\theta$ per unit span and $\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$:
$$
(D'_p,L'_p)=-q_\infty a\int_0^{2\pi}C_p(\theta)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta.
$$
Substituting $C_p=1-4\sin^2\theta$ gives $(0,0)$: opposite points have identical <pressure coefficients> and opposite <normal vectors>. Zero <drag> despite a strongly nonuniform <pressure> is the instructive limitation associated with <D'Alembert's paradox>. Real <viscosity>, <boundary layers> and <flow separation> can prevent rearward <pressure> recovery and produce <pressure drag>. Thus the measured curve can test the model and estimate a <pressure> contribution without proving that total <drag> vanishes.

A motivating measurement is Virginia Tech's <AOE 3054 laboratory manual/Experiment 3 - Flow Past a Circular Cylinder>. Its open-jet <wind tunnel> holds a rotatable $140\,\mathrm{mm}$ cylinder with end plates and 36 one-millimetre <pressure tappings> at ten-degree intervals. A reference <Pitot-static tube> supplies $p_{0\infty}$ and $p_\infty$ to one digital <manometer>; a second connects a surface tap to its positive port and reference <static pressure> to its negative port. Record the simultaneous readings:
$$
\Delta p_{\rm ref}=p_{0\infty}-p_\infty,\qquad
\Delta p_i=p_i-p_\infty,\qquad
C_{p,i}=\frac{\Delta p_i}{\Delta p_{\rm ref}}.
$$
The last equality uses the low-speed stopping relation, not an additional <speed> measurement. The manual requires sealed tubing and zero checks with the tunnel off. It also provides a water-column <manometer>: read each column's initial level, and subtract the independently measured reference-to-atmosphere <pressure> from the tap-to-atmosphere readings. Record tap positions after rotation. Compare the two instruments and check spanwise uniformity; end plates do not guarantee two-dimensional flow. <AOE 3054 laboratory manual/Experiment 3 - Flow Past a Circular Cylinder>{ref}

The inferential distinction matters: the <manometers> directly measure <pressure> differences, the ratio estimates <pressure coefficients>, and a <surface integral> estimates the pressure part of the <force>. Measure room <absolute pressure> and <temperature> if converting the reference difference to <speed> through the <ideal gas law> $\rho=p_{\rm abs}/(RT)$; use <thermodynamic temperature> in that equation. For <force> reconstruction, cover the complete contour and use <numerical integration> with the tap geometry. Finite tap spacing, unknown end effects, probe disturbance and fluctuations limit that reconstruction; tubes and instruments also average or delay changing <pressures>. Retain the signed raw readings, repeated readings and reference readings so that these limits can be assessed. A small reference difference magnifies any zero error in the ratio, rather than producing a reliably large $C_p$.

= Pressure tapping
{parent=Pressure coefficient}

A small flush opening communicating the local surface <static pressure> through tubing to a <manometer> or <pressure transducer>. An upstream-facing <Pitot tube> instead brings incident flow to rest to measure <stagnation pressure>.
