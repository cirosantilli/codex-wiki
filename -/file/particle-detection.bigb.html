= Particle detection

<Particle detection> infers a <particle>'s passage from a physical change in matter. A <cloud chamber> makes the spatial pattern of <ionization> visible; an <ionization chamber> instead collects <electric charge> from <ionization>. Choosing the readout determines what can be inferred from a single event.

The <richter-ting-independent-j-psi-discovery-experiments> show how different readouts can establish the same <particle resonance>: reconstruct daughter <invariant masses> after a beam hits a stationary target, or tune the initial <center-of-mass energy> of two colliding beams. Both require an ensemble of events and calibrated <particle detectors>.

= Collision pileup
{parent=Particle detection}

Additional collisions overlapping an event in the same beam crossing or detector response window. Extra tracks and <calorimeter> deposits affect particle-jet reconstruction, isolation and <missing transverse momentum>. Vertex association, subtraction and response <calibration> reduce the effect but leave uncertainties that depend on collision conditions.

= Active detector veto
{parent=Particle detection}

An additional active detector region or system whose correlated signal identifies events to reject from a signal sample. Its efficiency, time window, dead time and accidental coincidences must be measured. It can reject some backgrounds without proving that every unvetoed event is signal.

= Signal region
{parent=Particle detection}

A pre-specified region of reconstructed event space selected for sensitivity to a hypothesized signal. Observed events are compared with a background prediction constrained by <particle-physics control regions> and other measurements. A <blind analysis> can hide this region while choices and uncertainty estimates are finalized.

= Particle-physics control region
{parent=Particle detection}

An event sample designed to constrain a background contribution to a <signal region>, while having little expected signal contamination. Changed particle-identification, kinematic or tagging requirements can isolate a process. The model must account for its relation to the signal sample, shared uncertainties and any signal leakage. A separate validation sample can test extrapolation without tuning the discovery selection.

= Control-region transfer factor
{parent=Particle-physics control region}
{title2=$b_{\rm SR}=T\,b_{\rm CR}$}

A ratio translating a background yield in a <particle-physics control region> into its yield in a <signal region>. It may be estimated with simulation and <calibration> measurements. Its uncertainty must include acceptance, response and physics differences between the regions; contaminating backgrounds are subtracted or fitted jointly. A <profile likelihood> can constrain the control and signal counts together rather than treating the transferred estimate as exact.

= Detector acceptance
{parent=Particle detection}

The fraction of produced events falling within a detector's geometrical and kinematic selection. Reconstruction and trigger efficiencies are additional factors; conventions must state whether they are folded into the quoted acceptance. A changed decay spectrum can alter acceptance even at the same parent <mass>.

= Displaced decay vertex
{parent=Particle detection}

= Displaced vertex
{synonym}

A reconstructed decay position measurably separated from the primary collision point. Its distribution constrains a particle's flight distance and lifetime, after correcting for boost, geometry, resolution and selection. It is one possible signature of a long-lived new particle.

= Fiducial volume
{parent=Particle detection}

A selected inner detector volume in which the response and background rejection meet specified criteria. Position cuts can suppress surface backgrounds, but the selected target <mass> and position-reconstruction uncertainty must be calibrated.

= Electron recoil
{parent=Particle detection}

An <energy> deposition in a target's electronic degrees of freedom, as opposed to a <nuclear recoil>. <gamma rays> and <beta decay> can produce backgrounds in this channel. Detector discrimination is statistical because the measured light and charge distributions have finite widths.

= Time projection chamber
{parent=Particle detection}
{title2=TPC}

A detector that reconstructs one coordinate from the time required for <ionization> electrons to drift in an <electric field>, with the other coordinates measured by the charge or light pattern. A two-phase <xenon> implementation obtains depth from the delay between prompt <particle scintillation> and gas-phase charge readout.

= Dual-phase xenon detector
{parent=Time projection chamber}

A liquid target with a gaseous charge-readout layer. An interaction produces prompt <particle scintillation>, conventionally S1, and <ionization> electrons that drift to the liquid surface. Extracted electrons produce gas <electroluminescence>, conventionally S2. Timing and light-pattern information give three-dimensional position; the S2/S1 distribution helps distinguish <electron recoils> from <nuclear recoils>. It does not remove neutron-induced single recoils, and the discrimination requires <calibration>.

= Fiducial self-shielding in a xenon detector
{parent=Dual-phase xenon detector}

Selecting an inner <fiducial volume> reduces externally produced <radiation> because outer <xenon> attenuates it before it reaches the selected target. This trades target <mass> for reduced background. It does not remove uniformly distributed internal radioactivity or every neutron recoil, so purification, veto information and <calibration> remain important.

= Initial-state radiation
{parent=Particle detection}

<radiation> emitted by an incoming particle before the hard collision. At a hadron collider an energetic recoil <jet> can help trigger and measure events whose nearly degenerate parent-daughter decays otherwise yield soft visible particles.

= Collider signal region
{parent=Particle detection}

A predefined event selection in which observed counts or distributions are tested against constrained background predictions. Validation selections and uncertainty propagation test the reliability of the background extrapolation.

= Collider control region
{parent=Particle detection}

A data selection enriched in a relevant background and used to constrain its normalization or shape. Transfer to a separate signal selection requires acceptance and systematic-uncertainty checks; a control fit is not itself a signal test.

= Transition-edge sensor
{parent=Particle detection}

A <transition-edge sensor> detects deposited <energy> using the strong change in <electrical resistance> near a <superconducting transition temperature>.

= Flavor tagging
{parent=Particle detection}

= Flavour tagging
{synonym}

Inferring a <quark flavor> from the charges and identities of observed decay products. A mistaken assignment dilutes a flavour-dependent <decay rate> difference.

= Decay vertex
{parent=Particle detection}

The reconstructed location at which a <particle> decays, inferred from the trajectories of its decay products. Comparing two <decay vertices> can determine a decay-time difference when the motion of the parent system is known.

= Particle identification
{parent=Particle detection}

<Particle identification> assigns a species hypothesis to a detector signal or reconstructed track. Curvature in a <magnetic field> supplies <magnetic rigidity>; additional responses, such as those of a <Cherenkov detector> or a <particle shower>, help distinguish an <electron> from a <hadron>. The identity hypothesis enters the <energy–momentum relation> and therefore the reconstructed <invariant mass>. An apparatus can observe a peak before it can identify every decay channel, as the <SPEAR psi energy-scan experiment> illustrates.

= Time of flight
{parent=Particle detection}
{wiki}
{title2=$t=L/v$}

The elapsed time for a <particle> to traverse a specified path. For constant <speed> $v$ and known path <length> $L$, $t=L/v$; varying <speed> instead requires integrating $dt=ds/v(s)$.

= Track reconstruction
{parent=Particle detection}

Inferring a <particle> <trajectory> from recorded detector positions or a continuous trail. The inferred <trajectory> depends on <spatial resolution>, detector geometry and the assumed particle-motion model; it is not a direct picture of the <particle> itself.

= Stopping power
{parent=Particle detection}
{wiki=Stopping_power_(particle_radiation)}
{title2=$S=-dE/dx$}

The mean <energy> lost by a <charged particle> per unit path <length> through a material, with the <particle> species, material and <energy> specified. It is distinct from the <particle range> obtained by following the <particle> until it stops.

= Inverse-energy stopping-power model
{parent=Stopping power}
{title2=$S=k/E,\quad R=E_0^2/(2k)$}

For an ideal particle energy-loss law $dE/dx=-k/E$, integration gives $E(x)=\sqrt{E_0^2-2kx}$ up to the formal range. <Stopping power> grows as the endpoint is approached but its integral remains finite. This simple law does not describe endpoint broadening, scattering or every low-energy stopping mechanism.

= Nuclear stopping
{parent=Stopping power}
{title2=$S_n=-({dE}/{dx})_n$}

Energy loss of a moving particle through collisions transferring <momentum> to target nuclei. Recoiling target atoms can initiate <displacement cascades>.

= Electronic stopping
{parent=Stopping power}
{title2=$S_e=-({dE}/{dx})_e$}

Energy loss of a moving particle to <electronic excitation> and <ionization>. It is distinct from direct <momentum> transfer to target nuclei; electronic energy deposition can also couple to atomic motion in a material-dependent way.

= High-speed charge and isotope scaling of electronic stopping
{parent=Stopping power}
{title2=$\delta E\propto Z^2/v^2\simeq Z^2M/(2E)$}

For a fast ion traversing a fixed material thickness with only a small fractional <energy> loss, the dipole excitation <probability> per atomic encounter scales as $Z^2/v^2$ at fixed <impact parameter>. Summing transferred excitation energies and integrating the encounter measure $2\pi b\,db$ gives the leading electronic <stopping power> scaling, with slower logarithmic dependence from the allowed excitation energies and impact-parameter range.

At fixed ion <kinetic energy> $E=Mv^2/2$, a heavier isotope is slower, so the leading loss scales as $Z^2M/E$. At fixed velocity, isotopes of the same element instead have approximately the same electronic loss; plotting against energy per nucleon removes the leading isotope mass dependence. The charge should be the effective projectile charge if its bound electrons screen the nucleus. At lower speeds, excitation thresholds, slow-passage suppression, charge exchange and nuclear energy transfer invalidate a simple extrapolation of the high-speed law.

= Wire-address position model
{parent=Multiwire proportional chamber}
{title2=$\sigma_x=p/\sqrt{12}$}

Consider an original idealized <track reconstruction> model. Let equally spaced wires have positions $x_k=kp$ and assign each event to the nearest wire, reporting $\widehat x=x_k$. Assume **exactly one correct wire address**, perfect <detection efficiency>, exact wire positions and a <uniform distribution> of true positions within each cell. These assumptions explicitly exclude missed pulses, false addresses and several unresolved tracks.

Writing $u=x-x_k$, the <measurement error> is $e=\widehat x-x=-u$, with $u$ uniform on $[-p/2,p/2]$. Symmetry gives zero <expected value>. Its <variance> and <standard deviation> follow by integration:
$$
\mathbb E[e]=0,\qquad
\sigma_x^2=\mathbb E[e^2]=\frac{1}{p}\int_{-p/2}^{p/2}u^2\,du=\frac{p^2}{12},\qquad
\sigma_x=\frac{p}{\sqrt{12}}.
$$
For chosen <wire pitches> $p=2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ this gives $0.577\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $0.289\,\mathrm{mm}$ respectively. These are model <standard deviations>, not measured chamber <spatial resolutions>. The largest possible absolute error in this model is $p/2$, which is a different quantity.

A useful subtlety is that the formula depends on the illumination. For an unresolved pencil beam fixed at $x=x_k+0.4p$, every reconstructed position is $x_k$. The repeated estimates have zero <variance>, but every error is $-0.4p$. Small repeat-to-repeat spread can coexist with a biased position. The uniform-cell result therefore cannot be transferred to an arbitrary beam profile or detector selection.

The wire address supplies one transverse coordinate in this model. Combining several separated measurements supports <track reconstruction>; a single address does not specify a complete <trajectory>. If two <charged particles> enter the same cell during one accepted interval, an address-only record can be identical to that from one particle. No reconstruction of that record can recover the lost multiplicity without additional information.

= Calibrated two-wire charge centroid
{parent=Multiwire proportional chamber}
{title2=$\widehat x=pq_R/(q_L+q_R)$}

Can two readout values locate a hit between wires? **Only with a specified response model.** Place two readout wires at $0$ and $p$, put $u=x/p\in[0,1]$, and stipulate calibrated, noiseless <electric charges>
$$
q_L=Q(1-u),\qquad q_R=Qu,\qquad Q>0.
$$
These are assumptions for a toy sensor response, not the natural response law of adjacent <anode> wires. Under these assumptions the <centroid>
$$
\widehat x=\frac{0q_L+pq_R}{q_L+q_R}=pu=x
$$
recovers position exactly. The apparent gain in precision comes from adding calibrated analogue information. Taking the mean of two fired wire addresses without their <electric charges> would instead give the fixed midpoint $p/2$.

For a chosen noise model, replace the two <electric charges> by $q_L+n_L$ and $q_R+n_R$, with <independent random variables> $n_L,n_R$ of zero <expected value> and common <standard deviation> $s$. Assume $s/Q\ll1$ and no threshold censoring. First-order expansion of the ratio gives
$$
\delta x\simeq\frac{p}{Q}\bigl[(1-u)n_R-un_L\bigr],\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{lin}}(u)=\frac{ps}{Q}\sqrt{(1-u)^2+u^2}.
$$
For stipulated $p=2\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $s/Q=0.02$, this approximation gives $0.0283\,\mathrm{mm}$ at the midpoint and $0.0400\,\mathrm{mm}$ at either endpoint. The endpoint values describe uncensored signed channel readouts, including small negative values after baseline subtraction. They do not assume that both channels would pass a positive threshold. A ratio with a fluctuating denominator also need not have exactly zero bias; this is a local small-noise approximation.

Three original counterexamples show why calibration and selection matter:

* If the right channel retains a relative gain $g=1.10$, then $\widehat x/p=gu/(1-u+gu)$. At $u=1/2$, the bias is $p/42=0.0476\,\mathrm{mm}$ for the chosen pitch, larger than the midpoint noise estimate above.
* Suppose symmetric electronic leakage transforms the normalized right signal to $v=\epsilon+(1-2\epsilon)u$. Treating $v$ as $u$ gives a position bias $p\epsilon(1-2u)$. A known $\epsilon\ne1/2$ permits inversion; $\epsilon=1/2$ destroys position information entirely. This is a stipulated crosstalk model, not a measurement.
* Requiring both noiseless channels to exceed $0.1Q$ accepts only $0.1<u<0.9$: $80\%$ of uniformly distributed hits. Computing a position spread after that cut describes a selected sample. It does not establish improved <spatial resolution> for all incident hits.

Likewise, two equal hits at $u=1/4$ and $u=3/4$ have exactly the same total two-channel response as one hit at $u=1/2$ with twice the <electric charge>. A <centroid> can hide multiplicity even when each charge measurement is exact.

<Diffusion> enabled the lecture's Orsay interpolation, without establishing this model's linear response.<Electronic Imaging of Ionizing Radiation with Limited Avalanches in Gases by Georges Charpak>[p. 30]{ref}

= Neutrino event classification model
{parent=Lederman-Schwartz-Steinberger two-neutrino beam experiment}
{title2=$K\sim\operatorname{Bin}(N,q)$}

How can the relative abundance of tracks and showers distinguish hypotheses? Consider an original teaching model, **with chosen inputs throughout**. Condition on a sample of $N$ selected events, each assigned exactly one of two labels: track or shower. Multitrack events and unclassified events lie outside this model. A shower label need not identify an <electron>; background can imitate it. Conversely, a true <electron> interaction need not receive a shower label.

Let $b$ be the background fraction among selected events. Among the selected signal events, let $f$ be the fraction whose associated <charged lepton> is an <electron>, with the remainder associated with a <muon>. These are fractions after event selection, rather than fractions of the incident <neutrino> <particle flux>. Define the <conditional probabilities>
$$
a=\Pr(\mathrm{shower}\mid e,\mathrm{signal}),\qquad
r=\Pr(\mathrm{shower}\mid\mu,\mathrm{signal}),\qquad
q_b=\Pr(\mathrm{shower}\mid\mathrm{background}).
$$
Here $a$ and $r$ describe classification within the selected sample. They are not measured historical <detection efficiencies>. The <law of total probability> gives the overall shower <probability>
$$
q(f,b)=(1-b)\bigl[af+r(1-f)\bigr]+bq_b.
$$
This formula is the bridge between an interaction hypothesis and a visible label. It keeps beam contamination, background and mistaken classification conceptually distinct: genuine <electron neutrino> interactions belong in $f$, while non-signal events belong in $b$.

Assume <independent random variables> for the event labels, all with this same $q$. If $K$ counts shower labels, every specified pattern with $k$ showers has <probability> $q^k(1-q)^{N-k}$. Counting those patterns with a <binomial coefficient> yields the <binomial distribution>
$$
\Pr(K=k)=\binom Nk q^k(1-q)^{N-k},\qquad
\mathbb E[K]=Nq,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(K)=Nq(1-q).
$$
The track count is $N-K$, so its <expected value> is $N(1-q)$. At fixed $N$, the two counts cannot fluctuate independently. The <variance> above measures sampling fluctuations under the stipulated model, not uncertainty in $a$, $r$, $b$ or $q_b$.

Choose $N=40$, $a=0.90$, $r=0.05$, $b=0.10$ and $q_b=0.40$. Compare a signal with only <muon>-associated interactions ($f=0$) against one with equal selected <electron>- and <muon>-associated interactions ($f=1/2$). These give $q=0.085$ and $q=0.4675$, respectively. Their expected shower counts are $3.4$ and $18.7$; their expected track counts are $36.6$ and $21.3$. A background-only model with the specified $q_b$ gives $16$ showers and $24$ tracks on average. These are expectations, so they need not be integers.

\Image[neutrino-track-shower-classification-model.png]
{title=Chosen <binomial distribution> model: expected track and shower counts, with the shower-count distributions}
{description=Original calculations for forty selected events and the stipulated classification and background probabilities. No historical counts, fitted efficiencies or reconstructed significance are plotted.}

For a hypothetical observation $K=4$, the <likelihood ratio> of the two signal models is
$$
\frac{\mathcal L(f=0)}{\mathcal L(f=1/2)}
=\left(\frac{0.085}{0.4675}\right)^4
\left(\frac{0.915}{0.5325}\right)^{36}
\simeq3.18\times10^5.
$$
The <binomial coefficient> cancels because the same hypothetical sample is used in both <likelihoods>. This ratio is not the <probability> that either hypothesis is true. A separate original check gives $\Pr(K\leq5)\simeq0.880$, $4.57\times10^{-6}$ and $1.44\times10^{-4}$ for the muon-associated, equal-mixture and specified background-only models, respectively.

Why are the controls essential? If the background-only model may choose any $q_b$, taking $q_b=K/N$ can fit the observed shower fraction. Counts alone then fail to distinguish it from a signal model. Independent background measurements and calibrated <particle identification> constrain that freedom. Changing the decay path additionally tests where the candidate-producing <particles> originate. The power of the experiment comes from combining apparatus interventions with event classification; the small <binomial distribution> model isolates only the latter part of that reasoning.

= Super-Kamiokande atmospheric-neutrino experiment

{parent=Super-Kamiokande}

= Two-flavour vacuum survival model
{parent=Super-Kamiokande atmospheric-neutrino experiment}
{title2=$P_{\mu\mu}$}

For a deliberately simplified <neutrino oscillation> calculation, take two <neutrino flavors> related to two <mass eigenstates> by a real <rotation matrix> with angle $\theta$. $$
Here $L$ is path <length> and $E$ is <neutrino> <energy>; the numerical coefficient incorporates unit conversion.

The path dependence has a geometrical origin. In an original spherical-<Earth> model, let $R$ be the radius and $h$ a fixed production height, neglecting detector depth. Let $\Theta$ be the <zenith distance> of the direction in the sky from which the <neutrino> arrives, so $c=\cos\Theta=+1$ means downward propagation. U=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad

= Neutrino-flux sensitivity inversion
{parent=Sudbury Neutrino Observatory}
{title2=$\mathbf y=A\boldsymbol\phi$}

Why compare inferred <neutrino fluxes> rather than raw event counts? For channel $j$, exposure time $T$, target population $N_j$, <cross section> $\sigma_{j\alpha}$, and <detection efficiency> $\epsilon_{j\alpha}$ including reconstruction and selection, a forward model is
$$
\mathbb E[N_j]-B_j=T N_j^{\mathrm{target}}\sum_\alpha\int\phi_\alpha(E)\sigma_{j\alpha}(E)\epsilon_{j\alpha}(E)\,dE.
$$
Here $B_j$ is expected background; $N_j$ on the left counts events, while $N_j^{\mathrm{target}}$ counts targets. Distinct <cross sections>, target numbers, thresholds and <detection efficiencies> prevent a direct CC/NC count ratio from being an <electron neutrino> fraction. Channel populations themselves can be correlated estimates from overlapping event patterns.

An original reduced <linear algebra> model isolates the inference. Assume specified spectral shapes and divide each background-corrected channel rate by its own calibrated <electron neutrino> response. Put $\phi_x=\phi_\mu+\phi_\tau$ and let $r$ be the spectrum- and response-weighted non-electron/electron ES sensitivity ratio. Then
$$
\begin{pmatrix}y_{\mathrm{CC}}\\y_{\mathrm{NC}}\\y_{\mathrm{ES}}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&r\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\phi_e\\\phi_x\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The equal $\mu$ and $\tau$ responses determine their sum, not their separate <neutrino fluxes>. Changing the assumed spectra generally changes the integrated coefficients; with arbitrary unknown spectra this small <matrix> is insufficient.

Choose **illustrative inputs**, not historical measurements: $r=0.15$, $\phi_e=2$ and $\phi_x=3$, with every <neutrino flux> in the same arbitrary unit. The forward prediction is $(y_{\mathrm{CC}},y_{\mathrm{NC}},y_{\mathrm{ES}})=(2,5,2.45)$. CC and NC independently invert to $\phi_e=y_{\mathrm{CC}}=2$, $\phi_x=y_{\mathrm{NC}}-y_{\mathrm{CC}}=3$; substituting into ES returns $2+0.15(3)=2.45$. Alternatively, CC and ES give $\phi_x=(y_{\mathrm{ES}}-y_{\mathrm{CC}})/r=3$. Thus the third channel tests the response model and the inferred mixture.

= Neutrino directional-coincidence model
{parent=IceCube Neutrino Observatory}
{title2=$q(\psi)=(1-\cos\psi)/2$}

How much does a small directional uncertainty help? Consider an original idealized model: a source direction fixed in advance, uniform background directions over the full <sphere>, and circular acceptance radius $\psi$. The accepted <solid angle> and single-event background <probability> are
$$
\Omega(\psi)=\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\phi\int_0^\psi\sin\theta\,d\theta=2\pi(1-\cos\psi),\qquad
q(\psi)=\frac{\Omega}{4\pi}=\frac{1-\cos\psi}{2}.
$$
The derivation integrates the area element on a unit <sphere>. For small $\psi$ in <radians>, $q\simeq\psi^2/4$: halving the radius quarters the accidental acceptance, to leading order. At $psi=0$ acceptance vanishes; at $\psi=\pi$ it covers the full <sphere>. The <solid angle> has units of <steradians>; $q$ is dimensionless.

For $0<\psi\leq\pi$, take a deliberately crude <point-spread function>: stipulate that every signal direction lies uniformly inside that cap. The signal and background <probability density functions> per <steradian> are $S=1/\Omega$ inside the cap and zero outside, and $B=1/(4\pi)$ everywhere. Both integrate to one. For an event inside the cap their <likelihood ratio> is
$$
\frac{S}{B}=\frac1q.
$$
This ratio compares two specified directional models; it is not the <probability> that the event came from the source. Such a <conditional probability> would additionally require prior source odds and the other selection information. For fixed source fraction $f$ among selected events, a normalized <mixture distribution> has density $(1-f)B+fS$. For independent events the <likelihood> is the product of those densities; its ratio to background alone is
$$
\Lambda(f)=\prod_i\left[1+f\left(\frac{S_i}{B_i}-1\right)\right].
$$
Outside-cap events contribute factors $1-f$ rather than being silently discarded. At $f=0$, $\Lambda=1$; for a full-sky cap, $S=B$ and every $f$ gives $\Lambda=1$. A directional model that cannot distinguish source from background carries no directional evidence.

= Accidental coincidence
{parent=Coincidence circuit}
{title2=$R_{\rm acc}\simeq r_1r_2w$}

Suppose two independent background streams follow <Poisson processes> with rates $r_1$ and $r_2$. Define a coincidence by $|t_2-t_1|<w/2$: **$w$ is the full accepted time-difference width**. Around each first-counter signal, the expected number of second-counter signals is $r_2w$. The expected pair rate is therefore $r_1r_2w$. A circuit producing at most one output per first signal instead gives
$$
R_{\rm acc}=r_1(1-e^{-r_2w})\simeq r_1r_2w\qquad(r_2w\ll1).
$$
The distinction matters when multiple detections occur in one window. If a different convention accepts $|t_2-t_1|<\tau$, its full width is $w=2\tau$ and the dilute expression is $2r_1r_2\tau$.

For a chosen numerical model, take background rates $r_1=100\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $r_2=80\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $w=20\,\mu\mathrm s$. The dilute pair rate is $0.16\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. These are stipulated teaching inputs, not measurements of the historical chamber.

Making $w$ smaller also rejects some genuine events. Suppose true pairs arrive at rate $S_0$ before the timing cut, and their time difference has a centered <normal distribution> with <standard deviation> $\sigma_t$. Its accepted fraction, using the <error function>, is
$$
\epsilon_t(w)=\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{w}{2\sqrt2\sigma_t}\right),\qquad
S(w)=S_0\epsilon_t(w).
$$
For chosen $S_0=2.0\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\sigma_t=5\,\mu\mathrm s$, the $20\,\mu\mathrm s$ window retains about $95.45\%$ of true pairs. In the dilute background model the fraction of accepted events that are true is $S/(S+R_{\rm acc})\simeq92.27\%$. A narrow window trades <detection efficiency> against background; it does not make either quantity equal to one. Delaying one counter's signal far beyond genuine event correlations gives a proposed background check under stationarity, without assuming that the zero-delay excess itself is accidental.

= Multiplicity bias from a detector trigger
{parent=Trigger (particle physics)}
{title2=$\Pr(N=n\mid\mathrm{trigger})\propto A(n)\Pr(N=n)$}

A trigger changes which particle populations are common in the recorded sample. Let $N$ be the number of particles in an event, and stipulate an independent <Bernoulli distribution> for each particle's ability to produce an accepted telescope signal, with success <probability> $\epsilon$. If any successful particle suffices, the event acceptance is
$$
A(n)=1-(1-\epsilon)^n.
$$
This is a deliberately simplified model. Its $\epsilon$ includes the joint geometrical acceptance and response of the whole telescope; it is not an assumed universal single-counter efficiency. Actual shower directions, spatial correlations and shared electronics can violate the independence assumption.

The <conditional probability> formula gives
$$
\Pr(N=n\mid\mathrm{trigger})=
\frac{A(n)\Pr(N=n)}{\sum_m A(m)\Pr(N=m)}.
$$
For chosen underlying fractions $\Pr(N=1)=0.90$, $\Pr(N=5)=0.10$ and $\epsilon=0.15$, the two acceptances are $0.15$ and $0.5563$. Five-particle events consequently form about $29.18\%$ of recorded events. **A population that is only $10\%$ of incoming events can occupy nearly $30\%$ of the photographs.** This is <selection bias>, not evidence that the source population changed.

= Detector dead time
{parent=Particle detector}

The interval during which a detector or readout cannot register another event while recovering from a response. A <detector trigger> may be fast while the camera, chamber or data acquisition is still busy.

In a simple nonextending model, every accepted event starts a fixed busy interval $\tau_d$; arrivals during it are lost and do not extend it. With <Poisson process> input rate $R$, the average cycle lasts $\tau_d+1/R$, giving observed rate
$$
R_{\rm obs}=\frac{R}{1+R\tau_d}.
$$
This is a specified mathematical model, not a universal correction. An instrument whose busy interval is extended by further arrivals behaves differently. Correcting the total rate also does not remove the <multiplicity bias from a detector trigger>.

= Cloud-chamber expansion model
{parent=Expansion cloud chamber}
{title2=$S=p_v/p_{\rm sat}(T)$}

Consider an illustrative <water>–air <mixture>, initially saturated at $T_0=293.15\,\mathrm K$. Let $\lambda=V/V_0$ be the expansion ratio and approximate the carrier <gas> by a uniform <ideal gas> with constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma=1.40$. Before significant <condensation>, assume fixed numbers of carrier and <vapor> <molecules>, negligible incoming <heat>, and the reversible <adiabatic expansion> relation. The <first law of thermodynamics>, $nC_V\,dT=-p\,dV$, with the <ideal gas law>, gives
$$
T(\lambda)=T_0\lambda^{1-\gamma},\qquad p_v(\lambda)=p_0\lambda^{-\gamma}.
$$
The <vapor> follows the same <temperature>, so its <partial pressure> scales as $T/V$. A rapid real <piston> motion need not be reversible; the formula is an idealized bulk estimate after neglecting flow and losses.

Use a physically motivated <integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation> for the equilibrium <water> <vapor pressure>, with stipulated rounded constants $p_0=2339\,\mathrm{Pa}$ at $T_0$, molar <latent heat> $L_m=45000\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$ and <molar gas constant> $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$:
$$
p_{\rm sat}(T)=p_0\exp\left[\frac{L_m}{R}\left(\frac1{T_0}-\frac1T\right)\right],\qquad
\ln S=-\gamma\ln\lambda+\frac{L_m}{RT_0}\left(\lambda^{\gamma-1}-1\right).
$$
These constants define the example, without fitting any historical measurements. Throughout the plotted interval $1\leq\lambda\leq1.17$, the <temperature> stays above the freezing point of <water>. Initially
$$
\left.\frac{d\ln S}{d\ln\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=1}
=-\gamma+(\gamma-1)\frac{L_m}{RT_0}\simeq5.99>0.
$$
Thus the decrease in <vapor pressure> initially beats dilution. At $\lambda=1.15$, the model gives $T=277.21\,\mathrm K$, $p_v=1923.33\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $p_{\rm sat}=809.07\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $S=2.3772$.

Why does $S>1$ not instantly make every <molecule> a visible drop? In the capillarity approximation of <classical nucleation theory>, a neutral spherical drop of radius $r$ has a positive <surface energy> cost and a negative bulk <Gibbs free energy> contribution. For molecular <liquid> volume $v_l$, <surface tension> $\sigma$ and <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$,
$$
\Delta G(r)=4\pi\sigma r^2-\frac{4\pi r^3}{3v_l}k_BT\ln S,\qquad
r_* =\frac{2\sigma v_l}{k_BT\ln S},\qquad
\Delta G_* =\frac{16\pi\sigma^3v_l^2}{3(k_BT\ln S)^2}.
$$
Differentiating $\Delta G$ shows that the <critical nucleus> at $r_*$ is a maximum: smaller neutral drops tend to shrink, whereas larger ones can grow. With stipulated $\sigma=0.075\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$, $v_l=2.99\times10^{-29}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, the $\lambda=1.15$ example gives $r_*=1.353\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\Delta G_*/(k_BT)=150.3$. A substantial neutral-drop barrier can coexist with <supersaturated vapor>.

This is deliberately a neutral-drop comparison. An <ion> changes the cluster <free energy> through electrical interactions and molecular hydration; the formula does not calculate the ion-assisted <nucleation> threshold. At nanometre radii, using bulk <surface tension> is itself approximate. There is no predicted droplet count, growth time or detection efficiency here. Once many drops grow, <latent heat> release, <vapor> depletion and <heat transfer> from the chamber walls invalidate the dry <adiabatic expansion> trajectory. The example therefore explains the direction of the effect and the need for <nucleation>, without extrapolating its curves to Wilson's cloud limits.

= Bubble-chamber nucleation model
{parent=Bubble chamber}
{title2=$r_*=2\sigma/\Delta p$}

Why can hot <liquid> wait for a <charged particle> rather than immediately turn into <vapor>? A small cavity costs <surface energy>. Consider an original macroscopic capillarity model at fixed <temperature> with <surface tension> $\sigma>0$, imposed <liquid> <pressure> $p_l$, and driving difference $\Delta p=p_{\rm sat}(T)-p_l>0$, where $p_{\rm sat}$ is the planar-interface <vapor pressure>. Neglect dissolved <gas>, interface thickness, <heat transfer> and curvature dependence of $\sigma$. The reversible formation-work approximation is
$$
\Delta G(r)=4\pi\sigma r^2-\frac{4\pi}{3}\Delta p\,r^3.
$$
The first term charges for surface area; the second rewards the new <vapor> <volume (thermodynamics)>. This is a macroscopic <free energy> model, not the <energy> that an actual detector must receive as localized <heat>. It shares the capillarity balance used in the <cloud-chamber expansion model>, but the new phase is now a bubble.

Differentiating gives
$$
\frac{d\Delta G}{dr}=4\pi r(2\sigma-\Delta p\,r),\qquad
r_* =\frac{2\sigma}{\Delta p},\qquad
\Delta G_* =\frac{16\pi\sigma^3}{3\Delta p^2}.
$$
At the <critical nucleus>, $d^2\Delta G/dr^2=-8\pi\sigma<0$. The barrier is a maximum: cavities smaller than $r_*$ tend to shrink, while larger ones can grow in this model. Doubling $\Delta p$ halves $r_*$ and quarters $\Delta G_*$ if $\sigma$ stays fixed. This explains the strong sensitivity to operating conditions without claiming a universal detector threshold.

Choose $\sigma=0.010\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$ and $\Delta p=2.0\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$ solely for a teaching example. Then
$$
r_*=100\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad
\Delta G_*=4.189\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm J\simeq2.614\,\mathrm{keV}.
$$
For the same chosen $\sigma$ and twice the driving <pressure>, $r_*=50\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\Delta G_*\simeq0.654\,\mathrm{keV}$. These are calculated formation-work scales, not measured bubble thresholds for <diethyl ether> or any other substance.

A simplified way to discuss localization is to stipulate deposited <energy> $E_{\rm loc}$ within a segment of <length> $\ell$ and a trial conversion fraction $0<\eta\leq1$. A toy criterion $\eta E_{\rm loc}\gtrsim\Delta G_*$ gives an <energy> per unit <length> scale $\Delta G_*/(\eta\ell)$. It is only a bookkeeping estimate: a real bubble also needs <latent heat>, must exchange <heat>, and is affected by the spatial pattern and time scale of <energy> deposition. Consequently neither a single <stopping power> value nor this barrier alone predicts <detection efficiency> or the number of bubbles. Two tracks with the same mean <stopping power> can distribute their <energy> differently on the scale of a <critical nucleus>.

= Bubble-chamber stereo reconstruction
{parent=Alvarez hydrogen-bubble-chamber reconstruction and resonances}

<Stereoscopy> supplies depth because different <cameras> see different projections of the same track. A functional reconstruction first matches the same interaction vertex and track segments across views. A calibrated lens and window model maps each measured image coordinate back to a ray inside the <liquid>. <Refraction> at the windows must be included. Intersecting the rays, or finding their closest agreement when measurements have errors, estimates three-dimensional coordinates. Several points then determine a <trajectory>, rather than only a projected arc.

An original ideal-camera model makes the geometry explicit. Take two parallel pinhole <cameras> with centers $(0,0,0)$ and $(b,0,0)$, focal <length> $f$, and optical axes along $z$. Use virtual image planes in front of the pinholes so that image coordinates have the same orientation as the scene. A point $(X,Y,Z)$ then gives
$$
u_L=\frac{fX}{Z},\qquad u_R=\frac{f(X-b)}{Z},\qquad
v_L=v_R=\frac{fY}{Z}.
$$
Subtracting the horizontal image coordinates gives disparity $d=u_L-u_R=fb/Z$, hence
$$
Z=\frac{fb}{d},\qquad X=\frac{Zu_L}{f},\qquad Y=\frac{Zv_L}{f}.
$$
For stipulated $b=0.20\,\mathrm m$, $f=0.050\,\mathrm m$, $u_L=0.010\,\mathrm m$, $u_R=0.005\,\mathrm m$ and $v_L=0.002\,\mathrm m$, the reconstructed point is $(0.40,0.08,2.0)\,\mathrm m$. These are ideal optical inputs, not dimensions of Alvarez's apparatus. This model omits <refraction> and lens distortion; actual calibration replaces its simple ray geometry. Since $|dZ/dd|=fb/d^2$, the same disparity error gives worse depth precision for distant points.

= Bubble-chamber kinematic fitting
{parent=Alvarez hydrogen-bubble-chamber reconstruction and resonances}

Alvarez describes a progression from measured coordinates to <momenta> and angles, then conservation-constrained fits testing alternative track identities. Computer programs made the repeated tests practical; an insufficiently constrained event could retain several hypotheses.<Recent Developments in Particle Physics by Luis Walter Alvarez>[UCRL-18696, printed pp. 30–33: HYDRO, PANG, GUTS, KICK and SUMX]{ref}

A mathematical formulation explains what such a fit can establish. Let $y$ collect measured <momenta> and angles, with positive-definite <covariance matrix> $V$. For a chosen reaction hypothesis, adjust them to $x$ by minimizing a <generalized least squares> objective,
$$
\chi^2=(x-y)^{\mathsf T}V^{-1}(x-y),\qquad g(x)=0.
$$
The constraints $g$ express <four-momentum conservation> and any independently known daughter <masses>. For each proposed identity, calculate <energy> from the <energy–momentum relation>, $E_i^2=m_i^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf p_i|^2$. A missing <neutral particle> adds unknowns; a measured decay vertex can add geometric constraints. Compatibility depends on the measurement errors and the independent constraints, not just the smallest raw $\chi^2$ among unrelated fits.

A simple independent check uses a single <momentum conservation> constraint. Suppose two measured one-dimensional <momenta> are $0.32$ and $-0.28\,\mathrm{GeV}/c$, each with independent <standard deviation> $0.02\,\mathrm{GeV}/c$, and a stipulated parent at rest requires their sum to vanish. Minimizing the two equal-weight squared changes gives fitted values $0.30$ and $-0.30\,\mathrm{GeV}/c$, with $\chi^2=2$. A fit repairs a mismatch according to a hypothesis; it does not prove that hypothesis. This one-component illustration is not a complete reaction fit.

= Invariant-mass selection of bubble-chamber events
{parent=Alvarez hydrogen-bubble-chamber reconstruction and resonances}
{title2=$M^2c^4=(\sum_i E_i)^2-c^2|\sum_i\mathbf p_i|^2$}

Why add the daughter <four-momenta>? Consider a selected combination of final <particles> with
$$
P^\mu=\sum_i p_i^\mu
=\left(\frac{\sum_i E_i}{c},\sum_i\mathbf p_i\right).
$$
Using the $+---$ <Minkowski metric>, its scalar square is
$$
P^\mu P_\mu=\frac{(\sum_iE_i)^2}{c^2}-\left|\sum_i\mathbf p_i\right|^2.
$$
In the combination's <center-of-momentum frame>, its total <momentum> vanishes and its total <energy> is $Mc^2$. Therefore $P^\mu P_\mu=M^2c^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{M^2c^4=(\sum_iE_i)^2-c^2\left|\sum_i\mathbf p_i\right|^2}.
$$
<Lorentz transformations> preserve this scalar square, so the reconstructed <invariant mass> does not depend on the combination's laboratory motion. It includes relative <kinetic energy> and need not equal the sum of the daughter <rest masses>. A random pair has an <invariant mass> too; that number does not itself establish a common parent.

For two daughters, expanding the square and substituting each <energy–momentum relation> gives
$$
M^2c^4=m_1^2c^4+m_2^2c^4+2(E_1E_2-c^2\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2).
$$
Here $M$ and $m_i$ are <masses>, not <energies>. Reporting $M$ in $\mathrm{GeV}/c^2$ and <momenta> in $\mathrm{GeV}/c$ is equivalent to calculating numerically with $Mc^2$ and $c\mathbf p_i$ in $\mathrm{GeV}$. Setting $c=1$ in <natural units> suppresses these conversion factors; it does not change the measured quantity.

Alvarez's lecture illustrates <invariant mass> searches with $K\pi$ combinations and contrasts resonance lifetimes near $10^{-23}\,\mathrm s$ with the much longer lived states the chambers originally targeted. These resonances decayed before producing a resolvable parent track.<Recent Developments in Particle Physics by Luis Walter Alvarez>[UCRL-18696, printed pp. 46–47, Figs. 15–17: effective-mass histograms and short lifetimes]{ref}

Operationally, choose a reaction hypothesis, require an acceptable <bubble-chamber kinematic fitting> result, and form specified daughter combinations. Accumulate their <invariant masses> in a <histogram> across events. **A <particle resonance> is inferred from the ensemble's dynamics; its short-lived parent is not directly photographed.** Daughter tracks can be visible even when the production and decay positions of the parent cannot be separated. Wrong combinations create background, and several combinations from one event are statistically correlated.

= Invariant-mass resolution
{parent=Invariant-mass selection of bubble-chamber events}
{title2=$\sigma_M$}

The simulation separates a stipulated physical distribution from measurement broadening. Its <Cauchy distribution> is only a convenient resonance-shaped input, not a complete <quantum scattering> model. <Spatial resolution>, <magnetic field> calibration, <multiple Coulomb scattering>, <stopping power> and incorrect daughter identities all propagate into the measured <invariant mass>. Instrumental broadening must be understood before interpreting an observed width as a lifetime.

Selection also changes the shape. A <momentum> threshold can remove low-<invariant mass> pairs; a fit that fixes the candidate parent's <mass> would artificially concentrate that same <mass> and cannot supply an independent search distribution. Compare accepted simulated events with the input distribution, vary justified selection criteria, and check neighboring <invariant mass> intervals. A peak supported only by one untested selection is weaker evidence than a structure that survives calibration and background checks. The smooth background in the figure is a stipulated control component, not a claim that every physical background is smooth.

The common question was whether the observed excess represented a new <particle resonance>, rather than a peculiarity of <particle identification>, acceptance or calibration. The important difference was the horizontal coordinate of the search: **Brookhaven reconstructed a final-state <invariant mass>; SPEAR scanned an initial-state <center-of-mass energy>.** The <invariant-mass selection of bubble-chamber events> uses the first kind of reasoning, despite a very different recording medium.

= Brookhaven J pair-spectrometer experiment
{c}
{parent=richter-ting-independent-j-psi-discovery-experiments}

Brookhaven's <proton> beam struck stationary <beryllium>. A two-arm magnetic pair spectrometer measured <electron>–<positron> <momenta> and directions. <Cherenkov detectors>, tracking chambers and shower counters rejected the much larger <hadron> background. Calibration used an <electron> beam; timing and track matching rejected <accidental coincidences>. The pair <invariant-mass> distribution showed a narrow excess. Reducing magnet currents left its reconstructed position unchanged; changing target thickness and reversing spectrometer polarity supplied further checks. The unresolved intrinsic width did not mean a zero <decay width>.<Experimental Observation of a Heavy Particle J by J. J. Aubert>[Experimental Observation of a Heavy Particle J, pp. 1404–1405, Fig. 1 and checks 1, 2 and 7: target, spectrometer, calibration and stability tests]{ref}

The logic of the magnet-current check is especially useful. A real state should retain its reconstructed <invariant mass> when a calibrated change of <magnetic field> makes its daughters follow different accepted paths. A feature tied to one detector location or one setting need not do so. This checks a possible instrumental origin; it does not by itself determine the state's <quark> composition.

= SPEAR psi energy-scan experiment
{c}
{parent=richter-ting-independent-j-psi-discovery-experiments}

At SPEAR, counterrotating <electron> and <positron> beams collided inside a magnetic <particle detector>. Finer steps in stored-beam <energy> resolved a sharp production peak. A magnetometer and measured orbit positions monitored the energy settings; <Bhabha scattering> normalized the <cross sections>. The paper attributed the observed width to beam-energy spread and radiation effects, reporting an upper limit on intrinsic width. Its <muon> identification system was not functioning: the non-electron two-body excess was not conclusively a <muon> pair.<Discovery of a Narrow Resonance in e+e- Annihilation by J.-E. Augustin>[Discovery of a Narrow Resonance in electron–positron Annihilation, pp. 1406–1408: scan, energy monitoring, normalization, width limit and two-body identification caveat]{ref}

A scan must compare normalized production rates, since more selected events can result from more beam encounters or different <detection efficiency>. A proposed diagnostic is to revisit energies on either side of the peak while monitoring the energy calibration and event selection. Such neighboring settings test the local production background; their event totals alone are not an independent measurement of the intrinsic <decay width>.

= Pair mass versus collision energy
{parent=richter-ting-independent-j-psi-discovery-experiments}
{title2=$M_{ee}c^2$ versus $E_{\rm cm}$}

For a reconstructed <electron>–<positron> pair, <four-momentum conservation> gives
$$
M_{ee}^2c^4=(E_++E_-)^2-c^2|\mathbf p_++\mathbf p_-|^2.
$$
Neglecting the <electron mass> compared with the daughter <energies>, and calling the opening angle $\theta$, this becomes
$$
M_{ee}^2c^4\simeq2E_+E_-(1-\cos\theta).
$$
As an original example, choose $E_+=2\,\mathrm{GeV}$, $E_-=3\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and $\theta=60^\circ$. Then $M_{ee}c^2=\sqrt6\,\mathrm{GeV}\simeq2.449\,\mathrm{GeV}$, although the pair's laboratory <energy> is $5\,\mathrm{GeV}$. The pair carries net <momentum>. Neither its laboratory <energy> nor the original <proton> beam <energy> is its <invariant mass>; unobserved products carry additional <four-momentum>.

For equal, oppositely directed incoming beams with equal total <energy> $E_b$, their total <momentum> vanishes. Thus the incoming <center-of-mass energy> is
$$
E_{\rm cm}=\sqrt{(E_b+E_b)^2-c^2|\mathbf p+(-\mathbf p)|^2}=2E_b.
$$
Stipulated $1.50\,\mathrm{GeV}$ beams therefore provide $3.00\,\mathrm{GeV}$ of incoming <center-of-mass energy>. This identity is exact for the stated equal-beam geometry. Radiation before annihilation can reduce the <energy> forming the final state; the apparatus also has a distribution of beam <energies>. A nominal machine setting is therefore not an exact event-by-event final-state <invariant mass>.

= Narrow resonance and instrumental resolution
{parent=richter-ting-independent-j-psi-discovery-experiments}
{title2=$\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$}

A simple isolated <particle resonance> model starts with a survival amplitude proportional to
$$
a(t)=e^{-iE_0t/\hbar}e^{-\Gamma t/(2\hbar)},\qquad t\geq0.
$$
Its survival <probability> is $|a(t)|^2=e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$, giving mean rest-frame lifetime $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$. The <Fourier transform> of this one-sided amplitude is proportional to $(\Gamma/2-i(E-E_0))^{-1}$; its squared modulus gives a <Lorentzian line shape>. Write its normalized density as
$$
L(E)=\frac1\pi\frac{\Gamma/2}{(E-E_0)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2},\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}L(E)\,dE=1.
$$
At $E-E_0=\pm\Gamma/2$ the density is half its peak, so its intrinsic <full width at half maximum> is $\Gamma$. This is a local isolated-line approximation, extending the offset coordinate over the real line; physical production thresholds, interference and energy-dependent <decay widths> can require a richer <quantum scattering> model.

Now stipulate a centered <normal distribution> for the measurement response, with <standard deviation> $\sigma$. A measured coordinate $x$ has density
$$
G_\sigma(x-E)=\frac{e^{-(x-E)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma},\qquad
O(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}L(E)G_\sigma(x-E)\,dE.
$$
This <convolution> describes the smearing of an underlying line, with constant acceptance and no background. For a pair spectrometer the response summarizes <invariant-mass resolution>; for an idealized collider scan it summarizes incoming <center-of-mass energy> spread. These are different experimental sources of uncertainty even when the same mathematical response is useful.

Choose an arbitrary scale $E_*$, intrinsic <decay width> $\Gamma=0.20E_*$ and response <standard deviations> $0.40E_*$ and $1.20E_*$. Numerical integration gives apparent <full width at half maximum> values of approximately $1.05E_*$ and $2.93E_*$, respectively. The state and its rest-frame lifetime are identical in both cases. The <normal distribution>'s width alone is $2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma$; adding that width to $\Gamma$ in quadrature is not an exact rule for this <convolution>.

For a purely illustrative $E_*=1\,\mathrm{MeV}$, taking $\hbar\simeq6.5821\times10^{-22}\,\mathrm{MeV\,s}$ gives $\tau\simeq3.2911\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm s$. These are invented line parameters, not <j-psi-meson> measurements. Substituting the larger apparent width into $\hbar/\Gamma$ would incorrectly shorten the inferred lifetime by nearly a factor of fifteen. An unresolved width can support an upper bound on intrinsic <decay width>, and consequently a lower bound on lifetime, only after the instrumental response and model assumptions have been assessed.

The discovery peak and its later interpretation answer different questions. The <j-psi-meson> is understood as <charmonium>, a bound <charm quark>–<charm antiquark> system. Richter's 1976 lecture distinguishes the initial discovery from subsequent tests of competing explanations, including the related level structure and later evidence for particles carrying uncancelled charm. A narrow peak alone did not establish all these properties in 1974.<From the Psi to Charm – The Experiments of 1975 and 1976 by Burton Richter>[From the Psi to Charm – The Experiments of 1975 and 1976, introduction and sections 7.4–8: charmonium interpretation and subsequent charm searches]{ref}

= W and Z discovery experiments
{c}
{parent=Particle detection}

A <W boson> decay can leave one visible <charged lepton> and a large <missing transverse momentum>; a <Z boson> decay can instead supply two visible <leptons> whose <four-momenta> determine an <invariant mass>. The two signatures answer the same existence question with different missing information. The <electroweak unification history> recognized by the 1979 <Nobel Prize in Physics> predates these 1983 observations: the <W boson> and <Z boson> were predictions to test, rather than particles first proposed in 1983.

The <Super Proton-Antiproton Synchrotron> stored counterrotating <proton> and <antiproton> beams; <stochastic cooling> made accumulation of the scarce <antiprotons> practical. <Carlo Rubbia>'s 1984 lecture describes <track reconstruction> in a large <drift chamber> inside a <magnetic field>, surrounded by segmented <particle calorimeters>. Their nearly complete angular coverage allowed an imbalance test for unobserved <neutrinos>.<Experimental Observation of the Intermediate Vector Bosons W+, W− and Z0 by Carlo Rubbia>[1984 lecture, section 3, p.247, Fig.5; section 4, Figs.7–8]{ref}

The 1983 <UA1 experiment> paper used <proton>–<antiproton> collisions at $\sqrt{s}=540\,\mathrm{GeV}$. A high-<transverse momentum> <electron> candidate required a track matched to an electromagnetic <particle shower>, with little energy behind it in the hadronic <particle calorimeter>. = Z pair mass and W transverse-mass limits
{c}
{parent=W and Z discovery experiments}

For two visible, approximately massless <leptons>, both measured <four-momenta> enter
$$
m_{\ell\ell}^2=(p_{\ell^+}+p_{\ell^-})^2
=2p_{T+}p_{T-}\bigl[\cosh(y_+-y_-)-\cos\Delta\phi\bigr].
$$
This is a reconstructed <invariant mass>, including longitudinal information, rather than the lower bound obtained from <transverse mass>. As an original example, two opposite-azimuth <leptons> with $p_{T+}=p_{T-}=45\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and equal <rapidities> have $m_{\ell\ell}=90\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Changing their <rapidity> difference to one raises it to about $101.486\,\mathrm{GeV}$, even though their <transverse momenta> are unchanged.

The ideal <transverse mass> bound concerns the actual event's parent <invariant mass>, not a perfectly sharp universal number. A finite <decay width> spreads parent <invariant masses>; <particle jet> recoil changes daughter directions; <invariant-mass resolution>, momentum resolution and imperfect reconstruction can smear an observed endpoint. Recoil alone does not invalidate the ideal inequality when both daughter <transverse momenta> are correct. Additional invisible <particles>, radiated <photons> and a massless-daughter approximation can invalidate the simple two-body reconstruction. Likewise, a <Z boson> pair-mass peak requires control of identification, <detection efficiency>, instrumental response and backgrounds. A peak position and an endpoint are different summaries of selected event populations.

= Transverse momenta
{synonym}

Choose the beam as the $z$ direction. A <particle>'s <transverse momentum> is its <momentum> projected onto the perpendicular plane; $p_T=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2}$. A <Lorentz boost> along the beam changes <energy> and longitudinal <momentum>, while leaving <transverse momentum> unchanged. Large <transverse momentum> identifies motion substantially across the beam rather than merely along it.

= Missing transverse momentum
{parent=Transverse momentum}
{title2=$\mathbf p_T^{\rm miss}=-\sum_{\rm visible}\mathbf p_T$}

<Missing transverse momentum> is the negative <vector sum> of reconstructed visible <transverse momenta>, with detector corrections specified. Under the ideal hypotheses of zero total incoming <transverse momentum>, complete visible reconstruction and exactly one invisible <neutrino>, <momentum conservation> gives
$$
\mathbf p_T^{\rm miss}=\mathbf p_{T\nu}.
$$
The <neutrino> is **not directly seen**. Even under these ideal hypotheses, its longitudinal <momentum> $p_{z\nu}$ is not measured. Beam remnants can carry substantial unobserved longitudinal <momentum>; the colliding <quarks>' longitudinal <momenta> are also unknown event by event. Imposing an assumed parent <mass> can constrain $p_{z\nu}$, but that imports a hypothesis rather than measuring it. Finite coverage, poorly reconstructed <particle jets> and other invisible <particles> can also generate or modify <missing transverse momentum>.

= Collider effective mass
{parent=Missing transverse momentum}
{title2=$m_{\rm eff}=p_T^{\rm miss}+\sum_jp_{Tj}$}

A scalar sum of <missing transverse momentum> magnitude and selected high-energy transverse momenta, used as a hard-event-scale diagnostic in some <supersymmetry> searches. Which jets and <leptons> enter must be defined. It is not an invariant <mass> or the solid-state effective <mass>, and no universal equality with a produced particle <mass> holds.

= Transverse mass
{parent=Missing transverse momentum}
{wiki}
{title2=$m_T^2=2p_{T\ell}p_T^{\rm miss}(1-\cos\Delta\phi)$}

For a two-body <W boson> decay into a <charged lepton> $\ell$ and a <neutrino>, neglect both daughter <masses> and use <natural units> $c=1$. Let $\Delta\phi$ be the angle between the daughter <transverse momenta>. Identifying the <missing transverse momentum> with the <neutrino>'s gives
$$
m_T^2=2p_{T\ell}p_T^{\rm miss}(1-\cos\Delta\phi).
$$
This observable uses no $p_{z\nu}$. The 1983 <UA1 experiment> paper already used <transverse mass> as a lower bound on parent <mass>, before making additional decay-model assumptions for a mass estimate.<Experimental Observation of Isolated Large Transverse Energy Electrons with Associated Missing Energy at sqrt(s) = 540 GeV by G. Arnison>[Section 10, pp.113–114, Figs.8–10: bound versus model-dependent mass fit]{ref}

The bound follows directly from the <energy–momentum relation>. For each massless daughter write its <four-momentum>, using <rapidity> $y$ and azimuth $\phi$, as
$$
p^\mu=(p_T\cosh y,\ p_T\cos\phi,\ p_T\sin\phi,\ p_T\sinh y).
$$
Using the <Minkowski inner product> with positive time sign, $p^2=E^2-|\mathbf p|^2$, the two daughter <four-momenta> give the true parent <invariant mass> $M$:
$$
M^2=2p_{T\ell}p_{T\nu}\bigl[\cosh(y_\ell-y_\nu)-\cos\Delta\phi\bigr],\qquad
M^2-m_T^2=2p_{T\ell}p_{T\nu}\bigl[\cosh(y_\ell-y_\nu)-1\bigr]\geq0.
$$
Since $\cosh u\geq1$, **$m_T\leq M$**, with equality at equal daughter <rapidities> when both <transverse momenta> are nonzero. Thus the unmeasured longitudinal information can enlarge the true <invariant mass>, but cannot lower it below this ideal <transverse mass>.

For an original example, stipulate $p_{T\ell}=p_{T\nu}=40\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and $\Delta\phi=\pi$. Then $m_T=80\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Equal <rapidities> give $M=80\,\mathrm{GeV}$; choosing $y_\ell-y_\nu=1$ with exactly the same transverse readout gives $M=80\cosh(1/2)\simeq90.210\,\mathrm{GeV}$. One measured transverse configuration therefore permits different true parent <masses>.
