=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat $p_n(t)$ as the <probability> of the complete coarse-grained state $n$ at time $t$, with $p_n\geq0$ and $\sum_np_n=1$. In the column-vector convention of the <master equation>, $W_{nm}$ for $n\ne m$ is the transition rate **from $m$ to $n$**. The short-time transition law is
$$
P(n,t+dt\mid m,t)=\delta_{nm}+W_{nm}dt+o(dt).
$$
Nonnegative transition <probabilities> and conservation of total <probability> require
$$
\boxed{W_{nm}\geq0\ (n\ne m),\qquad
\sum_nW_{nm}=0,\qquad W_{mm}=-\sum_{n\ne m}W_{nm}\leq0.}
$$
For a <finite state space> these conditions define a <continuous-time Markov chain>. The <Markov property> means that, conditional on the present state, no further history affects the transition <probabilities>. A time-independent $W$ additionally assumes time-homogeneous rates; the waiting time in a state is exponential, and multiple jumps in an infinitesimal interval contribute beyond first order. A reduced description that omits relevant slow variables can retain memory and need not obey this closed equation. <Quantum coherences> are likewise absent from this classical population model. For infinite state spaces one also needs conditions preventing infinitely many jumps in finite time. Neither equal forward/backward rates nor <detailed balance> is required merely to conserve <probability>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use states $0,A,B$, where $0$ denotes an empty molecule and the other states denote its single occupied site. The source arrows give $0\to A$ at rate $f_1$, $A\to0$ at rate $f_4$, $A\to B$ at rate $f_2$, and $B\to A$ at rate $f_3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}p_0\\p_A\\p_B\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
-f_1&f_4&0\\
f_1&-(f_4+f_2)&f_3\\
0&f_2&-f_3
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p_0\\p_A\\p_B\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Each diagonal entry is the negative total escape rate from its column's state. In particular, the empty state cannot make a direct jump to $B$: the reservoir touches $A$ alone. Conversely, $A\to B$ is not a second charge arriving on the molecule; it is an internal hop of its existing charge. Summing the three equations gives $d(p_0+p_A+p_B)/dt=0$, which checks the <master equation> convention and the single-charge constraint.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive rates the three states communicate, so the finite <continuous-time Markov chain> has a unique stationary <probability> vector. The first and third components of $Wp^{\rm st}=0$ give
$$
f_1p_0^{\rm st}=f_4p_A^{\rm st},\qquad
f_2p_A^{\rm st}=f_3p_B^{\rm st}.
$$
These two edge-balance relations and <normalization> yield, with $D=f_3f_4+f_1f_3+f_1f_2$,
$$
\boxed{p_0^{\rm st}=\frac{f_3f_4}{D},\qquad
p_A^{\rm st}=\frac{f_1f_3}{D},\qquad
p_B^{\rm st}=\frac{f_1f_2}{D}.}
$$
Their sum is one and each is nonnegative. The middle balance equation then follows from the other two, as expected from <probability> conservation. A line graph has no cycle that could carry a nonzero stationary circulation: each edge's forward and backward stationary traffic balances even if the bare rates differ. If some rates vanish, the process need not be an <irreducible Markov chain> and its stationary distribution need not be unique. The displayed expression remains valid when its denominator is nonzero and its limits select the communicating class reached, but it must not be used as a unique late-time law when disconnected closed classes retain dependence on the initial state.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Condition on the molecule being empty. Its instantaneous incoming-charge jump rate is $f_1$, so the unconditional stationary number of arrivals per unit time is
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm in}=f_1p_0^{\rm st}=\frac{f_1f_3f_4}{f_3f_4+f_1f_3+f_1f_2}.}
$$
This <stationary jump traffic at a single contact> is a unidirectional jump count, not a signed net current. Stationarity also gives $R_{\rm out}=f_4p_A^{\rm st}=R_{\rm in}$. Multiplying their difference by the charge gives zero net stationary electrical current through the molecule's single contact, even though individual arrival and departure events continue.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

An $A\to B$ hop can occur only while the charge occupies $A$. Hence its stationary number per unit time is
$$
\boxed{R_{A\to B}=f_2p_A^{\rm st}
=\frac{f_1f_2f_3}{f_3f_4+f_1f_3+f_1f_2}.}
$$
The reverse count is $R_{B\to A}=f_3p_B^{\rm st}$, which equals this result. There is therefore no signed stationary current along that edge. The individual hopping traffic need not equal the contact-arrival traffic; their ratio is $R_{A\to B}/R_{\rm in}=f_2/f_4$. A charge can make several internal visits before leaving, so neither conservation nor the single-charge constraint forces those two one-way rates to coincide.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For equal rates, the <master equation> generator is $f$ times the symmetric path-graph matrix
$$
W/f=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\1&-2&1\\0&1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvectors> may be chosen as $(1,1,1)$, $(1,0,-1)$, and $(1,-2,1)$, with <eigenvalues> $0,-f,-3f$. Resolve the initial empty state as
$$
(1,0,0)=\tfrac13(1,1,1)+\tfrac12(1,0,-1)+\tfrac16(1,-2,1).
$$
Each nonstationary eigencomponent decays with its own rate, giving
$$
\boxed{p_B(t)=\frac13-\frac12e^{-ft}+\frac16e^{-3ft}.}
$$
The remaining <probabilities> are $p_0(t)=1/3+e^{-ft}/2+e^{-3ft}/6$ and $p_A(t)=(1-e^{-3ft})/3$. These satisfy the full equations and sum to one. The limit $p_B(\infty)=1/3$ agrees with the stationary result. At short times $p_B(t)=f^2t^2/2+O(t^3)$: two successive jumps, $0\to A\to B$, are necessary, so a term linear in $t$ would signal an incorrectly included direct transition.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The negative sign of the exchange term and $J>0$ make this a <Heisenberg ferromagnet>. Each classical bond minimizes its energy when its two fixed-length spins are parallel. On the connected <cubic lattice> all ground states are therefore uniform:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf S_j=S\mathbf n,\qquad |\mathbf n|=1,}
$$
using length $S$ for the classical large-spin convention. Any two orientations are related by a common spatial rotation of every spin. The global spin-rotation symmetry is $SO(3)$, and a chosen direction retains its $SO(2)$ subgroup of rotations about that axis.

<Spontaneous symmetry breaking> means selecting one member of this continuous set in the <thermodynamic limit>, for example by taking volume to infinity before sending an aligning field to zero. The zero-field <Hamiltonian operator> remains rotationally invariant even though the selected state is not. There are gapless transverse <spin waves>. Although two rotation generators are broken, an isotropic ferromagnet has one quadratic <magnon> branch, whose two transverse spin components form a conjugate dynamical pair. It is not necessary to invent two independent linear-dispersion branches from the number of broken generators. The long-wavelength dispersion is derived in <2/d>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The field term explicitly selects the positive $x$ direction. For $B>0$ the uniform minimizing state is $\mathbf S_j=S\hat{\mathbf x}$, with no remaining continuous degeneracy of its orientation. Rotations about the $x$ axis still leave the <Hamiltonian operator> invariant, and the selected state respects that residual symmetry.

Tilting the <magnetization> now costs a field energy. Accordingly the transverse <spin wave> acquires a gap
$$
\boxed{\Delta=BS,}
$$
as confirmed directly in <2/d>. The factor $S$ is present because the source couples the field as $-BS\sum_jS_j^x$ rather than $-B\sum_jS_j^x$. Gapless zero-field <Goldstone modes> need not survive an explicit symmetry-breaking field.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a local spin quantized along its positive $\tilde z$ axis, the exact <Holstein-Primakoff transformation> is
$$
\boxed{\tilde S_j^z=S-n_j,\quad
\tilde S_j^+=\sqrt{2S-n_j}\,a_j,\quad
\tilde S_j^-=a_j^\dagger\sqrt{2S-n_j},\quad n_j=a_j^\dagger a_j.}
$$
The bosonic operators obey $[a_j,a_k^\dagger]=\delta_{jk}$. The physical local subspace is $0\leq n_j\leq2S$; the larger unconstrained boson <Fock space> is not itself the finite spin Hilbert space. The ordering of the square roots matters, since $n_j$ does not commute with $a_j$.

This represents spin lowering using a bosonic <creation operator>, making a fully polarized spin state the boson vacuum. On the physical subspace it reproduces the spin <commutators> and the Casimir $\tilde{\mathbf S}_j^2=S(S+1)$. Expanding the square roots for small occupation relative to $S$ organizes a controlled large-$S$ <linear spin-wave approximation>; it is not an assumption that a spin has an infinite number of physical levels.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Quantize along the selected field direction by the right-handed relabeling $\tilde S^z=S^x$, $\tilde S^x=S^y$, $\tilde S^y=S^z$. Then the exact <Holstein-Primakoff transformation> in <2/c> gives $S_j^x=S-n_j$. Keep exchange terms of order $S^2$ and $S$, with $J$ and the printed $B$ held fixed. For a nearest-neighbor bond,
$$
\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_k
=S^2-S(n_j+n_k)+S(a_j^\dagger a_k+a_k^\dagger a_j)+O(S^0).
$$
On a periodic <cubic lattice> with $N$ sites and spacing $a$, there are $3N$ undirected bonds. Consequently,
$$
H_{\rm LSW}=-NS^2(3J+B)
+JS\sum_{\langle j,k\rangle}(n_j+n_k-a_j^\dagger a_k-a_k^\dagger a_j)
+BS\sum_jn_j.
$$
Use the unitary lattice <Fourier transform> $a_j=N^{-1/2}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r_j}a_{\mathbf k}$. Each positive lattice direction contributes $2(1-\cos k_\alpha a)$, so the <Hamiltonian operator> is already diagonal in momentum space:
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm LSW}=E_{\rm GS}+\sum_{\mathbf k}\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}a_{\mathbf k}^\dagger a_{\mathbf k},\qquad
\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=S\left[B+2J\sum_{\alpha=1}^3(1-\cos k_\alpha a)\right],\quad
E_{\rm GS}=-NS^2(3J+B).}
$$
There are no anomalous $aa$ or $a^\dagger a^\dagger$ terms, so no <Bogoliubov transformation> or extra zero-point correction is needed. At long wavelengths,
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=BS+JSa^2|\mathbf k|^2+O(k^4).}
$$
At $B=0$ this is the quadratic ferromagnetic <Goldstone mode>; for $B>0$ it is gapped by $BS$. Energies are written with $\hbar=1$; an excitation frequency is $\omega_{\mathbf k}=\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}/\hbar$. In this isotropic ferromagnet the fully aligned vacuum and its one-magnon dispersion are also exact; higher-magnon interactions are among the terms discarded from the linear spin-wave <Hamiltonian operator>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the positive-$x$ polarized ground state selected by $B>0$, or its symmetry-selected $B\to0^+$ limit. It is exactly the boson vacuum in the <Holstein-Primakoff transformation>. Thus $n_j|\mathrm{GS}\rangle=0$ and $S_j^x=S-n_j$ give
$$
\boxed{\langle S_j^x\rangle=S,\qquad\langle(S_j^x)^2\rangle=S^2.}
$$
For the transverse components, $S^y=(\tilde S^++\tilde S^-)/2$ and $S^z=(\tilde S^+-\tilde S^-)/(2i)$. In the vacuum, the only nonzero quadratic ladder-operator expectation is
$$
\langle\tilde S^+\tilde S^-\rangle=2S,
$$
which follows using the **full ordered square-root operators**, or directly from $\tilde S^-|S,S\rangle=\sqrt{2S}|S,S-1\rangle$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle S_j^y\rangle=\langle S_j^z\rangle=0,\qquad
\langle(S_j^y)^2\rangle=\langle(S_j^z)^2\rangle=S/2.}
$$
An independent exact spin argument starts from $S_j^x|S,S\rangle_x=S|S,S\rangle_x$ and $(S_j^x)^2+(S_j^y)^2+(S_j^z)^2=S(S+1)$. Rotational invariance about $x$ makes the two transverse second moments equal, so each is $[S(S+1)-S^2]/2=S/2$. This agrees exactly with the full boson representation.

The first moments describe the local <magnetization>. Subtracting their squares from the second moments gives <spin component variances>: zero longitudinal variance and transverse variance $S/2$. Transverse <quantum fluctuations> persist in a perfectly polarized state even though its <magnon> occupation is zero. Their relative scale decreases as $S^{-1/2}$ in the classical limit.

At precisely $B=0$, “the ground state” requires a choice within the ferromagnetic multiplet. For example, when $NS$ is an integer, a maximum-total-spin state with total $x$ projection zero is also a ground state but has $\langle S_j^x\rangle=0$. The displayed local moments refer to the explicitly specified polarized member, not to every superposition or rotationally invariant mixture of zero-field ground states.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an analytic, local <Landau theory> with independent global rotations of the <magnetization> vector, a uniform scalar potential can depend only on $\rho=\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf m$. Keeping terms through fourth order in the field, dropping an additive constant, and retaining the leading isotropic positive gradient term gives the displayed quadratic-plus-quartic functional. The stiffness has been absorbed into the <normalization> of length and field. Higher powers and higher derivatives are neglected in this long-wavelength approximation.

The quartic truncation is stable at large $|\mathbf m|$ when $u>0$. The coefficient $t$ changes sign at the mean-field critical point; close to it one commonly writes $t\propto T-T_c$. If $u<0$, higher stabilizing powers are essential, so the displayed truncation alone cannot determine a stable first-order phase diagram. For $u=0$ and $t<0$ it is likewise unbounded.

For $u>0$, spatially uniform stationary points obey $(t+4u\rho)\mathbf m=0$. The global minima are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf m=0\quad(t\geq0),\qquad
|\mathbf m|^2=-\frac{t}{4u}\quad(t<0).}
$$
The corresponding minimum free-energy density is zero and $-t^2/(16u)$, respectively. The disordered phase gives way continuously to a ferromagnet with an arbitrary direction of <magnetization>. At this mean-field transition the amplitude vanishes as $(-t)^{1/2}$; long-wavelength critical fluctuations can modify mean-field exponents in three dimensions.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§4.2, vector order parameter and rotationally invariant Landau expansion]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Vary each field component independently and integrate the gradient term by parts, taking fixed boundary values or vanishing surface variations. With $\rho=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_3^2$, the <Euler-Lagrange equations> for the free-energy saddle are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
-\nabla^2m_1+(t+r)m_1+4u\rho m_1&=0,\\
-\nabla^2m_2+(t-r)m_2+4u\rho m_2&=0,\\
-\nabla^2m_3+t m_3+4u\rho m_3&=0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The anisotropy shifts the first two inverse susceptibilities in opposite directions. The derivative of $u\rho^2$ is $4u\rho m_j$, which fixes the factor needed for both the <magnetization> and the free energies in <3/c>. Under natural free-boundary conditions the integration by parts instead supplies $\partial_n m_j=0$ at the surface.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume $u>0$, as required for the stable quartic model, and retain the stated domain $t>0$. For the <opposite-axis quadratic anisotropy of a vector order parameter>, fix the magnitude $\rho$. Then the anisotropy lowers the energy most by putting all <magnetization> along component 1 if $r<0$ and component 2 if $r>0$. Component 3 has positive quadratic coefficient $t$ and cannot lower the uniform minimum. Minimizing the remaining amplitude gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{region}&\text{uniform equilibrium magnetization}&f_{\min}\\\hline
r<-t&\bigl(\pm\sqrt{-(t+r)/(4u)},0,0\bigr)&-(t+r)^2/(16u)\\
-t\leq r\leq t&(0,0,0)&0\\
r>t&\bigl(0,\pm\sqrt{(r-t)/(4u)},0\bigr)&-(t-r)^2/(16u)
\end{array}}
$$
The component-1 and component-2 ordered regions each break a discrete sign symmetry, giving two easy-axis orientations rather than a sphere of equivalent directions. To check stability, in the component-1 minimum the longitudinal curvature is $-2(t+r)>0$, while the two transverse curvatures are $-2r>0$ and $-r>0$. The component-2 case similarly has longitudinal curvature $-2(t-r)$ and transverse curvatures $2r,r$, all positive in its region. The disordered state is stable exactly when $t\pm r\geq0$.

Both boundaries $r=-t$ and $r=t$ are continuous, second-order mean-field transitions: the selected component vanishes with a square root, and the first derivatives of the minimum <free energy> agree across the boundary. In the stipulated upper half-plane $t>0$, the two ordered phases are separated by a disordered wedge. There is no direct first-order boundary between them in that domain. Extrapolating a phase boundary through $t<0$ would answer a different, larger phase-diagram question.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Specify the <Fourier transform> before performing the <Gaussian integral>:
$$
m_j(\mathbf q)=\int d^3x\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}m_j(\mathbf x),\qquad
m_j(\mathbf x)=\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}m_j(\mathbf q).
$$
At $u=0$ the functional is diagonal in both component and momentum, with inverse kernels $q^2+t+r$, $q^2+t-r$, and $q^2+t$. The condition $0<r<t$ makes all three positive. The <covariance> of a <Gaussian distribution> with statistical weight $e^{-F/(k_BT)}$ is $k_BT$ times the inverse kernel, hence
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\langle m_1(\mathbf q)m_1(\mathbf p)\rangle&=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf q+\mathbf p)\frac{k_BT}{q^2+t+r},\\
\langle m_2(\mathbf q)m_2(\mathbf p)\rangle&=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf q+\mathbf p)\frac{k_BT}{q^2+t-r}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
If $F$ is already the dimensionless <free energy> divided by $k_BT$, the numerators are one instead. Translational invariance forces opposite momenta to pair. There is no complex conjugate on the second field, so the delta function is at $\mathbf p=-\mathbf q$, not at $\mathbf p=\mathbf q$. A correlation with $m_j(\mathbf p)^*$ would have the latter convention. Rotational symmetry in real space leaves dependence on $q^2$ alone once the momentum-conservation delta function is factored out. The <correlation lengths> are $\xi_1=(t+r)^{-1/2}$ and $\xi_2=(t-r)^{-1/2}$; the second component becomes soft as $r\uparrow t$. The skipped letter in the source is preserved: this solution is part (e), with no inferred part (d).

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$, and sum over all $2^N$ configurations satisfying the periodic identification. The <canonical partition function> and finite-size <free energy> per site are
$$
\boxed{Z_N=\sum_{\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_N=\pm1}
\exp\!\left[-\beta J\sum_j\sigma_j\sigma_{j+1}+\beta B\sum_j\sigma_j\right],\qquad
f_N=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\log Z_N.}
$$
The plus sign multiplying $J$ in the energy means that $J>0$ favors opposite neighboring spins: this is an <antiferromagnetic Ising model>. Even $N$ permits an alternating configuration to close without frustration. The finite-size formula does not require taking a <thermodynamic limit>; that limit is taken only after evaluating $Z_N$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Share each on-site field energy equally between its two neighboring bonds. In the ordered basis $\sigma=+1,-1$, the symmetric <transfer matrix for a classical spin chain> is
$$
\boxed{T_{ss'}=e^{-\beta Jss'+\frac12\beta B(s+s')},\qquad
T=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-K+h}&e^K\\e^K&e^{-K-h}\end{pmatrix},\quad K=\beta J,\ h=\beta B.}
$$
The product $\prod_jT_{\sigma_j\sigma_{j+1}}$ recovers each bond and each field term once. Summing the periodic indices closes a <trace>, giving $Z_N=\operatorname{Tr}T^N$. The two <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\pm=e^{-K}\cosh h
\pm\sqrt{e^{-2K}\sinh^2h+e^{2K}},\qquad Z_N=\lambda_+^N+\lambda_-^N.}
$$
The square-root argument is positive. Moreover $\det T=e^{-2K}-e^{2K}<0$, so $\lambda_-<0$ even at nonzero field. Its sign must not be discarded merely because all matrix entries are positive. The <Perron-Frobenius theorem> ensures that the positive <eigenvalue> $\lambda_+$ has the larger modulus, and the infinite-chain <free energy> is $f_\infty=-\beta^{-1}\log\lambda_+$. For even $N$, both terms in the finite-size <partition function> are positive.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Insert the two observed spins into the configuration sum for the <transfer matrix for a classical spin chain> <trace>. Since the diagonal matrix $\sigma^z$ has entry $s$ on state $s$, multiplication by it at positions $j$ and $j+r$ produces the factor $\sigma_j\sigma_{j+r}$. The <trace> representation is consequently
$$
\boxed{g(r)=\frac1{Z_N}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[T^{j-1}\sigma^zT^r\sigma^zT^{N+1-j-r}\right].}
$$
There are exactly $N$ transfer factors: $(j-1)+r+(N+1-j-r)=N$. The distance between the two insertions is $r$ bonds, not $r-1$. Cyclicity of the <trace> combines the outside factors and gives the equivalent expression
$$
g(r)=Z_N^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma^zT^r\sigma^zT^{N-r}),
$$
which makes independence of the starting site manifest. No assumption that $T$ commutes with $\sigma^z$ is used; in general it does not. For $r=0$ the two insertions multiply to the identity and $g(0)=1$, a useful <normalization> check.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

At zero field the normalized <transfer matrix for a classical spin chain> <eigenvectors> and <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{|+\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad
|-\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\lambda_+=2\cosh K,\quad\lambda_-=-2\sinh K.}
$$
The spin insertion exchanges the two <eigenvectors>: $\sigma^z|+\rangle=|-\rangle$ and conversely. Evaluating the <trace> in <4/c> therefore gives, with $x=\lambda_-/\lambda_+=-\tanh K$,
$$
\boxed{g_N(r)=\frac{x^r+x^{N-r}}{1+x^N}.}
$$
This is the finite-ring result. In the <thermodynamic limit>, or for $1\ll r\ll N$ away from the wrap-around image, it becomes
$$
\boxed{g(r)=(-\tanh\beta J)^r=(-1)^r e^{-r/\xi},\qquad
\xi=-\frac1{\log\tanh\beta J}.}
$$
Here $\xi$ is in lattice-site units. The alternating sign is antiferromagnetic order at short distances; the exponential envelope shows the absence of long-range order at every positive temperature in this one-dimensional finite-range model. As $T\to0$, $\xi$ diverges, asymptotically $\xi\sim\tfrac12e^{2\beta J}$. For the even ring the zero-temperature result is $g(r)=(-1)^r$, consistent with the two alternating ground configurations. At fixed finite $N$, “large distance” must still respect periodicity: $g_N(r)=g_N(N-r)$ rather than decaying indefinitely beyond the ring.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

At $B=0$ a global reversal $\sigma_j\mapsto-\sigma_j$ leaves the energy and statistical weight unchanged but changes the sign of the one-spin observable. Pairing reversed configurations gives
$$
\boxed{\langle\sigma_j\rangle_\beta=0.}
$$
Equivalently, $\langle\pm|\sigma^z|\pm\rangle=0$ in the transfer <eigenbasis>, so $\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma^zT^N)=0$. This holds for every finite even ring and all finite temperatures, and for the zero-field Gibbs limit. A symmetry-selected zero-temperature alternating state can have nonzero **staggered** <magnetization>; its uniform <magnetization> is still zero. The classical spin variable in this model has no separate quantum spin-operator dynamics despite the conventional matrix notation $\sigma^z$ used for the insertion.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With the <Minkowski metric> $(+---)$, the momentum conjugate to the real field vector is $\boldsymbol\pi=\dot{\boldsymbol\phi}$. The <Hamiltonian density> is
$$
\mathcal H=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\pi^2+\tfrac12\sum_i(\partial_i\boldsymbol\phi)^2+V(\boldsymbol\phi).
$$
**Assume the potential has a <global minimum>** $V_{\min}$ and the boundary conditions permit a constant minimizing field. Then
$$
E-\int d^dx\,V_{\min}
=\int d^dx\left[\tfrac12\boldsymbol\pi^2+\tfrac12(\nabla\boldsymbol\phi)^2+V(\boldsymbol\phi)-V_{\min}\right]\geq0.
$$
The bound is attained by
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\phi(t,\mathbf x)=\boldsymbol\phi_*,\qquad
V(\boldsymbol\phi_*)=V_{\min},\qquad\dot{\boldsymbol\phi}_*=0.}
$$
The <homogeneous vacuum minimum criterion> proves existence of a homogeneous lowest-energy configuration under those assumptions. On infinite space one subtracts the constant <vacuum energy> before comparing finite energy differences. This identifies the classical vacuum configuration; it does not assert that the quantum field has no fluctuations.

The claim does not follow from “no derivative dependence in $V$” alone. For example, the allowed functional form $V(\phi)=-\phi^4$ is unbounded below, so there is no state of lowest energy to identify. Boundary conditions forcing different vacua at opposite ends can instead require an inhomogeneous minimum, such as a domain wall. The positive quartic potential used in the following parts does have homogeneous minima, so these caveats do not obstruct its vacuum calculation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Any constant real <orthogonal matrix> $R$ acting by $\boldsymbol\phi\mapsto R\boldsymbol\phi$ preserves both the Euclidean field-space norm and the kinetic term. The full internal symmetry is $O(2)$, with connected component $SO(2)\simeq U(1)$; reflections add a discrete component.

For $v\ne0$, the vacuum manifold is the circle $\boldsymbol\phi^T\boldsymbol\phi=v^2$. Expand about the chosen point as $\phi_1=v+h$, $\phi_2=\pi$. The potential becomes
$$
V=4\lambda v^2h^2+4\lambda v h(h^2+\pi^2)+\lambda(h^2+\pi^2)^2.
$$
The kinetic terms remain canonically normalized real-scalar kinetic terms. Comparing the quadratic potential with $m_h^2h^2/2+m_\pi^2\pi^2/2$ gives
$$
\boxed{m_h^2=8\lambda v^2,\qquad m_\pi^2=0.}
$$
There is one massive radial mode and one massless tangential <Goldstone boson>. A chosen vacuum leaves only the reflection that fixes that point, so there is one broken continuous generator. For negative $v$ the chosen point lies on the opposite side of the same circle and the mass depends on $v^2$; the radius is $|v|$. If $v=0$, the vacuum circle collapses to the origin, no continuous generator is broken, and both quadratic masses vanish without either field being a <Goldstone mode>. The nonzero-$v$ assumption is essential to the spontaneous-breaking interpretation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At an arbitrary nonzero vacuum write $\boldsymbol\phi_*=|v|\mathbf n$, where $\mathbf n^T\mathbf n=1$. The <Hessian matrix> of the potential is
$$
\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\phi_a\partial\phi_b}
=8\lambda\phi_a\phi_b+4\lambda(\boldsymbol\phi^2-v^2)\delta_{ab}.
$$
On the vacuum circle this reduces to
$$
\boxed{M^2=8\lambda v^2\mathbf n\mathbf n^T.}
$$
It has <eigenvalue> $8\lambda v^2$ along $\mathbf n$ and <eigenvalue> zero along the one perpendicular direction. Rotating the vacuum conjugates this matrix by an <orthogonal matrix> and cannot change its <eigenvalues>. Thus every point on the circle has exactly one tangential massless <Goldstone boson>.

Geometrically, motion along the circle costs no potential energy whereas motion normal to it does. The circle's dimension, equivalently $\dim O(2)-\dim O(1)=1$, is independent of its point. Counting the number of real field components would instead give two and would incorrectly count the massive radial direction as a <Goldstone mode>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A real antisymmetric generator of the connected rotation group is
$$
T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad R(\alpha)=e^{\alpha T}.
$$
Choose $D_\mu\boldsymbol\phi=(\partial_\mu-gA_\mu T)\boldsymbol\phi$. Under $\boldsymbol\phi\mapsto R(\alpha(x))\boldsymbol\phi$ and $A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha/g$, the <gauge covariant derivative> transforms in the same way as the field. Include the canonical <gauge kinetic term> $-F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}/4$ so the vector's mass <normalization> is specified.

In <unitary gauge>, $\boldsymbol\phi=(v+h,0)^T$. The relevant <Lagrangian density> is
$$
\mathcal L=-\tfrac14F^2+\tfrac12(\partial h)^2
+\tfrac12g^2(v+h)^2A_\mu A^\mu-\lambda(2vh+h^2)^2.
$$
Reading the quadratic vector term as $m_A^2A^2/2$ gives
$$
\boxed{m_A=|gv|.}
$$
The angular scalar supplies the longitudinal polarization of the massive vector; it is not a separate physical massless particle after gauging. The physical scalar still has $m_h^2=8\lambda v^2$.

The interactions among the physical fields are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_{\rm int}=-4\lambda vh^3-\lambda h^4
+g^2vhA_\mu A^\mu+\tfrac12g^2h^2A_\mu A^\mu.}
$$
Thus the complete physical vertex list is $hhh$, $hhhh$, $AAh$, and $AAhh$. An Abelian <gauge kinetic term> generates no $AAA$ or $AAAA$ self-interaction. Gauge-dependent Goldstone vertices in another gauge do not add physical particle species to this list. These statements concern the broken phase $v\ne0$; when $v=0$ the unitary-gauge broken-phase parametrization is unavailable.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Evaluate the <two-body Lorentz-invariant phase space> in the decaying particle's rest frame, $p=(m,\mathbf0)$. The spatial delta function sets $\mathbf q_2=-\mathbf q_1$. Both massless particles have energy $q=|\mathbf q_1|$, leaving
$$
d\Pi_2=\frac1{16\pi^2}\,dq\,d\Omega\,\delta(m-2q)
=\frac{d\Omega}{32\pi^2}.
$$
Here the factor $1/2$ in the second equality is the Jacobian of the energy delta function. Integrating the solid angle gives $\int d\Pi_2=1/(8\pi)$. If the decay is isotropic, the squared amplitude is angle independent, so
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=\frac1{2m}\frac{|\mathcal A|^2}{8\pi}
=\frac{|\mathcal A|^2}{16\pi m}.}
$$
This uses distinguishable final particles or an amplitude/phase-space convention with any required identical-particle symmetry factor already included. Two genuinely identical final bosons require an additional $1/2!$ if the full angular domain is integrated without such a factor. The complex particle and antiparticle in the following parts are distinct, so no such division is required there.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Define the free <Feynman propagators> by
$$
D_0(x)=\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i\,e^{-ik\cdot x}}{k^2+i0},\qquad
D_m(x)=\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i\,e^{-ik\cdot x}}{k^2-m^2+i0}.
$$
The interaction preserves the global phase symmetry $\varphi\mapsto e^{i\alpha}\varphi$, with the real $\phi$ neutral. Therefore the charge-two correlator vanishes in a symmetry-invariant perturbative vacuum:
$$
\boxed{\langle T\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\phi(z)\rangle=0.}
$$
In <Wick contractions>, a complex field contracts only with its conjugate; one vertex has one of each and cannot absorb both external $\varphi$ fields. The symmetry shows this vanishing holds to all perturbative orders, not only first order.

The neutral connected correlator has one allowed contraction to each external point at order $g$:
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm conn}(x,y,z)=-ig\int d^4w\,
D_0(x-w)D_0(y-w)D_m(z-w)+O(g^3).}
$$
The propagators are even functions of their separation; their orientations encode the conjugate-field contractions. Equivalently, with every external momentum Fourier transformed as incoming,
$$
G_{\rm conn}(x,y,z)=(-ig)\int\frac{d^4k\,d^4\ell\,d^4r}{(2\pi)^{12}}
(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(k+\ell+r)e^{-i(kx+\ell y+rz)}
\frac{i}{k^2+i0}\frac{i}{\ell^2+i0}\frac{i}{r^2-m^2+i0}+O(g^3).
$$
No factor of two accompanies the vertex: $\varphi$ and $\varphi^\dagger$ are distinct fields.

**For the full correlator as literally written, a <disconnected scalar three-point tadpole> is also possible.** Contract the two external complex fields with each other and contract the complex pair at the vertex into a loop. Unless <normal ordering> is imposed on the interaction or a <tadpole counterterm> fixes $\langle\phi\rangle=0$, add
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm disc}(x,y,z)=D_0(x-y)\left[-igD_0(0)\int d^4w\,D_m(z-w)\right]+O(g^3).}
$$
The coincident propagator is regulator dependent; in <dimensional regularization> the massless scaleless <tadpole diagram> vanishes. More generally this is $\langle T\varphi^\dagger(x)\varphi(y)\rangle_0\langle\phi(z)\rangle$ to this order and is removed by taking the connected correlator or imposing the stated vacuum-renormalization condition. Normalizing by vacuum bubbles alone does not remove an external-leg disconnected <tadpole diagram>. The charged correlator remains zero in every one of these conventions.<Quantum Field Theory by David Tong>[§§3.3.2 and 3.7.1, Wick expansion, connected Green functions and vacuum normalization]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At a single interaction insertion, the <Dyson series> supplies $i\mathcal L_{\rm int}=-ig\varphi^\dagger\varphi\phi$. Amputating the three external propagators in <6/b> gives the momentum-space rule
$$
\boxed{\varphi^\dagger\!\text{--}\varphi\!\text{--}\phi\ \text{vertex}:\quad -ig.}
$$
If the momentum-conservation factor is included in the vertex convention, append $(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(p_\phi+p_\varphi+p_{\varphi^\dagger})$, with all momenta incoming. There is one charged line entering and one leaving the vertex, and a neutral real-scalar line. The absence of a factorial in the interaction is appropriate because its three fields are distinct. The amputated decay amplitude has magnitude $|g|$ with the usual convention that a graph computes $i\mathcal A$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The leading decay is into one complex particle and its antiparticle, which are distinguishable. Using $|\mathcal A|^2=g^2$ in <6/a> gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(\phi\to\varphi\bar\varphi)=\frac{g^2}{16\pi m}.}
$$
In four space-time dimensions a canonically normalized scalar has mass dimension one, so $g$ has mass dimension one. The amplitude starts at order $g$, a rate is quadratic in that amplitude, and the only mass scale in the massless-daughter decay is $m$. Those facts already require $\Gamma\propto g^2/m$, leaving only a dimensionless numerical factor to calculate.

For <low-energy charged-scalar scattering through a neutral scalar>, label the incoming particle and antiparticle as $1,2$ and their outgoing counterparts as $3,4$. Charge flow allows $s$-channel annihilation and $t=(p_1-p_3)^2$ exchange of the real massive scalar. It forbids a separate $u$-channel graph for this fixed assignment of distinguishable final charges. The tree amplitude is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal M=-g^2\left(\frac1{s-m^2}+\frac1{t-m^2}\right)
=\frac{2g^2}{m^2}+O\!\left(\frac{g^2s}{m^4},\frac{g^2t}{m^4}\right).}
$$
At low energy it is approximately angle independent. The massless two-body scattering formula then gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm tot}\simeq\frac{|\mathcal M|^2}{16\pi s}
=\frac{g^4}{4\pi m^4s}.}
$$
In particular the requested scale estimate is $\sigma\sim g^4/(m^4s)$, not $g^4/m^6$ with the incoming-energy dependence omitted. Integrating out $\phi$ gives $\mathcal L_{\rm eff}=g^2(\varphi^\dagger\varphi)^2/(2m^2)$ at this order, whose complex-scalar four-leg combinatorics reproduce the same $2g^2/m^2$ amplitude. These are weak-coupling perturbative results about the specified vacuum; possible ultraviolet stabilizing interactions are not determined by the cubic term alone.

== 7
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Separate the <Klein-Gordon equation> as $\phi(t,x)=e^{\pm i\omega t}X(x)$. The spatial equation is $X''+k^2X=0$, with $\omega^2=k^2+m^2$. The first <Dirichlet boundary condition> removes the cosine solution, and the second requires $\sin(kL)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\qquad
\omega_n=\sqrt{k_n^2+m^2}.}
$$
There is no nonzero $n=0$ Dirichlet mode. The real field is built from both complex-conjugate time dependences, with mode functions
$$
\boxed{u_n(t,x)=\frac{e^{-i\omega_nt}\sin(k_nx)}{\sqrt{\omega_nL}},\qquad
u_n^*(t,x)=\phi_n^{(+)}(t,x).}
$$
The sine functions satisfy $\int_0^L\sin(k_nx)\sin(k_mx)dx=(L/2)\delta_{nm}$. Consequently the factor $1/\sqrt{\omega_nL}$ gives unit Klein-Gordon inner product for each positive-frequency mode and the canonical equal-time field <commutator> after quantization. A general solution is a superposition of these modes, rather than a claim that a single mode exhausts all solutions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Hamiltonian density> is $\mathcal H=[\dot\phi^2+(\partial_x\phi)^2+m^2\phi^2]/2$. In the vacuum only $\langle a_na_m^\dagger\rangle=\delta_{nm}$ contributes. Hence
$$
\langle\dot\phi^2\rangle=\sum_n\frac{\omega_n}{L}\sin^2(k_nx),\quad
\langle(\partial_x\phi)^2\rangle=\sum_n\frac{k_n^2}{\omega_nL}\cos^2(k_nx),\quad
\langle\phi^2\rangle=\sum_n\frac{\sin^2(k_nx)}{\omega_nL}.
$$
Substitution, using $\omega_n^2=k_n^2+m^2$, yields
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathcal H(x)\rangle
=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left[\frac{\omega_n}{2L}-\frac{m^2}{2\omega_nL}\cos(2k_nx)\right].}
$$
This <Dirichlet scalar vacuum energy density> illustrates why a massive field's local unregularized energy density need not be uniform. The cosine integrates to zero over the interval, so
$$
\boxed{E_0^{\rm BC}(L)=\int_0^Ldx\,\langle\mathcal H(x)\rangle
=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^\infty\omega_n.}
$$
These divergent expressions are understood with a common regulator before exchanging sums, integrals or derivatives. <Normal ordering> the <Hamiltonian operator> separately in each geometry would erase the vacuum terms one is trying to compare; a common regulated subtraction is used instead in <7/c> and <7/d>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $m=0$, the frequencies are $\omega_n=n\pi/L$. The stipulated spectral regulator therefore supplies $e^{-n/(L\Lambda)}$. With $\epsilon=1/(L\Lambda)$,
$$
E_0^{\rm BC}(L,\Lambda)=\frac\pi{2L}\sum_{n=1}^\infty ne^{-\epsilon n}
=\boxed{\frac\pi{2L}\frac{e^\epsilon}{(e^\epsilon-1)^2}.}
$$
The sum identity follows by differentiating the convergent geometric series $\sum_{n\geq1}z^n=z/(1-z)$ with $z=e^{-\epsilon}$. Its small-$\epsilon$ expansion is
$$
\frac{e^\epsilon}{(e^\epsilon-1)^2}
=\frac1{\epsilon^2}-\frac1{12}+\frac{\epsilon^2}{240}+O(\epsilon^4),
$$
so
$$
\boxed{E_0^{\rm BC}(L,\Lambda)
=\frac{\pi L\Lambda^2}{2}-\frac\pi{24L}
+O\!\left(\frac1{L^3\Lambda^2}\right).}
$$
In the <frequency-regulated Dirichlet Casimir energy>, the inverse-length scale $\Lambda$ suppresses modes with frequency much above $\pi\Lambda$ in units $c=1$. It is an ultraviolet cutoff, removed after comparing the same underlying vacuum convention in the two geometries. Physically an ideal perfectly reflecting boundary also ceases to be adequate at sufficiently high frequency, but that material scale is not specified here. The source calls this heat-kernel regularization; its explicit regulator is exponential in frequency, rather than the alternative exponential in frequency squared.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

On the unbounded line the momentum density of states in a reference length $L$ is $L\,dk/(2\pi)$. Count both signs of $k$, each with <zero-point energy> $|k|/2$ and the same frequency regulator:
$$
\boxed{E_0(L,\Lambda)=\frac L2\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dk}{2\pi}|k|e^{-|k|/(\pi\Lambda)}
=\frac L{2\pi}\int_0^\infty dk\,ke^{-k/(\pi\Lambda)}
=\frac{\pi L\Lambda^2}{2}.}
$$
The extensive divergent term agrees exactly with <7/c>. Subtract it before taking the cutoff away:
$$
E_{\rm Cas}(L)=\lim_{\Lambda\to\infty}[E_0^{\rm BC}(L,\Lambda)-E_0(L,\Lambda)]
=-\frac\pi{24L}.
$$
The force tending to change the interval length is
$$
\boxed{F_L=-\frac{dE_{\rm Cas}}{dL}=-\frac\pi{24L^2},\qquad
F_L=-\frac{\pi\hbar c}{24L^2}\ \text{with units restored}.}
$$
Its negative sign means attraction: separating the boundaries increases this negative vacuum-energy difference toward zero. The bulk subtraction must be made before interpreting the force, otherwise differentiating an unphysical cutoff-dependent bulk energy would contaminate it.

This is the one-dimensional scalar Dirichlet **Casimir force**. The source's name “Casimir-Polder” normally refers to a retarded atom–atom or atom–surface dispersion force, whose geometry and power law differ. No atom polarizability or electromagnetic mode multiplicity enters the scalar result derived here.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The continuous internal symmetry is local $U(1)$ <gauge invariance>. With the source's <gauge covariant derivative> convention it acts as
$$
\boxed{\varphi\mapsto e^{i\alpha(x)}\varphi,\qquad
A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\frac1e\partial_\mu\alpha,}
$$
so $D_\mu\varphi$ transforms like $\varphi$ and $F_{\mu\nu}$ is unchanged. Constant phases are its global subgroup, not an additional independent internal rotation group.

In flat four-dimensional space-time the continuous external symmetry is the <Poincaré group>: four translations and six <Lorentz transformations>. Noether's theorem associates translations with conserved energy and momentum, spatial rotations with <angular momentum>, and boosts with the relativistic center-of-energy/boost charges. In tensor notation the latter two are the six components of the conserved total angular-momentum tensor $M^{\mu\nu}$, including field spin contributions where appropriate. The nonzero dimensionful parameter $\mu^2$ prevents exact scale invariance. Discrete transformations, such as parity, are outside the requested continuous-symmetry list.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ and define the physical electromagnetic source by $\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu}=J^\mu$. For a constant infinitesimal phase $\delta\varphi=ie\epsilon\varphi$, the matter <Noether current> is
$$
\boxed{J^\mu=ie\left[\varphi(D^\mu\varphi)^*-\varphi^*D^\mu\varphi\right]
=ie(\varphi\partial^\mu\varphi^*-\varphi^*\partial^\mu\varphi)-2e^2|\varphi|^2A^\mu.}
$$
A definition using phase parameter $\delta\varphi=i\epsilon\varphi$ instead divides this expression by $e$; the Maxwell source includes the electric coupling shown here. Current signs must be tied to the printed $D_\mu=\partial_\mu-ieA_\mu$ convention.

To verify the equation of motion, vary the matter kinetic term with respect to $A_\mu$:
$$
\delta\mathcal L_{\rm matter}=-J^\mu\delta A_\mu.
$$
Varying $-F^2/4$ and integrating by parts gives $(\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu})\delta A_\mu$. The stationary action therefore requires
$$
\boxed{\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu}=J^\mu.}
$$
The potential has no explicit dependence on $A_\mu$ and contributes no extra source term. Antisymmetry of $F^{\nu\mu}$ implies $\partial_\mu J^\mu=0$ on the equations of motion; the same conservation law follows from the scalar equations. The source current is <gauge invariant> although its expanded derivative and vector-potential pieces are not separately invariant.<Quantum Field Theory by David Tong>[§§6.1.1 and 6.3.2, gauge symmetry and scalar coupling; the sign here follows the source's minus-sign covariant derivative]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Keep the index convention of <8/b>: $A^\mu=(A_0,\mathbf A)$, so $A_\mu=(A_0,-\mathbf A)$ and the spatial <gauge covariant derivative> is $\nabla+ie\mathbf A$. The physical three-current is the spatial part of $J^\mu$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf j=ie(\varphi^*\nabla\varphi-\varphi\nabla\varphi^*)-2e^2|\varphi|^2\mathbf A.}
$$
Equivalently, writing $\varphi=R e^{i\theta}$ gives $\mathbf j=-2eR^2(\nabla\theta+e\mathbf A)$. Under the <gauge transformation> in <8/a>, $\theta\mapsto\theta+\alpha$ and $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A-\nabla\alpha/e$, so this expression is manifestly invariant. Switching to a convention with the opposite spatial gauge sign reverses the phase-gradient term, not the screening term proportional to $e^2$.

For static fields the spatial <Ampère-Maxwell equation> is $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mathbf j$, while $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Taking its curl yields the general magnetic-field equation
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf B=-\nabla\times\mathbf j
=-2ie\,\nabla\varphi^*\times\nabla\varphi
+2e^2\nabla|\varphi|^2\times\mathbf A
+2e^2|\varphi|^2\mathbf B.}
$$
No uniform-condensate approximation has yet been used. For a varying scalar amplitude the gradient terms must be kept; simply replacing $|\varphi|^2$ by a constant before deriving the current would conceal their origin. The homogeneous-condensate limit supplies the <London screening> equation in <8/d>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The scalar potential is $V=-\mu^2|\varphi|^2/2+\lambda|\varphi|^4/4$. Minimizing it with respect to $|\varphi|^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{v^2=\frac{\mu^2}{\lambda},\qquad v=\frac{\sqrt{\mu^2}}{\sqrt\lambda}.}
$$
Choose a gauge with constant condensate phase and take the homogeneous-amplitude London limit, $\varphi=v$. Then <8/c> reduces to
$$
\boxed{\mathbf j=-2e^2v^2\mathbf A,\qquad
\nabla^2\mathbf B=2e^2v^2\mathbf B.}
$$
The <London screening with an unnormalized complex condensate> has a factor of two from the complex-field kinetic term as printed; no $1/\sqrt2$ has been included in the definition of the real positive $v$. Equivalently, the gauge mass is $m_A^2=2e^2v^2$.

For a planar surface at $x=0$ and conductor in $x>0$, take a tangential field $B_z(x)$. The solution bounded at large depth is
$$
\boxed{B_z(x)=B_z(0)e^{-x/\lambda_L},\qquad
\lambda_L=\frac1{\sqrt2|e|v}=\frac{\sqrt\lambda}{\sqrt2|e|\sqrt{\mu^2}}.}
$$
The exponentially growing solution is excluded. The screening current $j_y=-\partial_xB_z=B_z(0)e^{-x/\lambda_L}/\lambda_L$ is nonzero near the surface, demonstrating the <Meissner effect> through a persistent surface current. The constant-amplitude approximation describes <London screening> away from any region where the condensate itself is strongly suppressed.

In the stipulated static gauge, $\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\nabla A_0=0$. Where this equilibrium screening current is nonzero, the assumed local <Ohm's law> $\mathbf E=\rho\mathbf j$ therefore demands $\rho=0$. The simultaneous equality $\mathbf E=\mathbf j=0$ deep in the bulk would by itself place **no** constraint on $\rho$; it is not a valid standalone proof of <superconductivity>.

The lossless condensate also supplies the dynamical check. With fixed amplitude and phase in temporal gauge, differentiating the London relation gives $\partial_t\mathbf j=2e^2v^2\mathbf E$. A steady field accelerates the supercurrent rather than producing a stationary dissipative current. Thus the ideal condensate has zero <electrical resistivity>, or an infinite <electrical conductivity>, consistent with the static screening solution. This is the superconducting component of the toy model; normal carriers and dissipation are not included. The nonzero field expectation is understood in a gauge-fixed description of the Higgs phase, not as a gauge-invariant local observable.
