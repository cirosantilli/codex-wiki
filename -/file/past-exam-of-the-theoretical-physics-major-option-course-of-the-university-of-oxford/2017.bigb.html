== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$ and label the two <Spin>[spin values] in the order $+1,-1$. Assign each bond its <Boltzmann weight>,
$$
V_{st}=e^{-\beta E_0(s,t)},\qquad
V=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad s,t\in\{+1,-1\}.
$$
The <symmetry> of the <Energy>[bond energy] makes $V$ a <Symmetric matrix>[real symmetric matrix]. For a fixed configuration the product of successive <matrix> entries is its <Boltzmann weight>. Summing over all <spins> and identifying the last index with the first therefore gives
$$
\boxed{Z_L=\sum_{\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_L}V_{\sigma_1\sigma_2}\cdots V_{\sigma_L\sigma_1}=\operatorname{tr}V^L=\lambda_+^L+\lambda_-^L,}
$$
where
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{a+d\pm\sqrt{(a-d)^2+4b^2}}2.
$$
This replaces a sum over $2^L$ configurations by the powers of a two-by-two <matrix>. For finite bond <energies> at $T>0$, every <matrix> entry is positive, so the <Perron–Frobenius theorem> gives $\lambda_+>0$ and $|\lambda_-|<\lambda_+$. Hence the <Helmholtz free energy> per <spin> tends to $-k_BT\log\lambda_+$. A diagonal local <observable> $O$ can be inserted at one site: $\langle O_1\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(OV^L)/Z_L$. Its limiting average is the <expectation> in the <Normalization>[normalized] <eigenvector> of $\lambda_+$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**The sum needs a stated interpretation.** The original equation leaves the second bond outside explicit parentheses, with its index then formally free. The natural <Translation invariance>[translation-invariant], equal-bond interpretation used here is the <energy> $-J\sum_j\tau_j(\sigma_j+\sigma_{j+1})$.

Let $K=\beta J$. Sum over each decorating <spin> before summing over the remaining <spins>. The <decimation of an alternating mixed-spin chain> gives the effective <transfer matrix>:
$$
V_{st}=\sum_{t_0=-1}^{1}e^{Kt_0(s+t)}=1+2\cosh[K(s+t)]
=\begin{pmatrix}1+2\cosh2K&3\\3&1+2\cosh2K\end{pmatrix}_{st}.
$$
No on-site entropy has been omitted: the term $1$ is the weight of the zero decorating <spin>. The <Normalization>[normalized] <eigenvectors> are $v_\pm=(1,\pm1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2$, with <eigenvalues>
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=4+2\cosh2K=2+4\cosh^2K,\qquad \lambda_-=2\cosh2K-2=4\sinh^2K.}
$$
There are $L$ transfer steps but $2L$ physical sites, so
$$
\boxed{Z_L=\lambda_+^L+\lambda_-^L,\qquad
f=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{-k_BT\log Z_L}{2L}=-\frac{k_BT}{2}\log(4+2\cosh2\beta J).}
$$
The <free energy> per two-site <unit cell> is twice this value. At $J=0$, the formula gives $Z_L=6^L$, as required by two states on one sublattice and three on the other. As $T\to0$, the <free energy> per physical site tends to $-J$, the <energy> of an aligned chain with two satisfied bonds per <unit cell>. These checks also fix the factor of two in the site count.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Insert $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ at the first undecorated site. In the <eigenbasis> of the <transfer matrix>, $D$ exchanges $v_+$ and $v_-$, and both diagonal <matrix elements> vanish. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{\langle\sigma_1\rangle_L=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(DV^L)}{\operatorname{tr}V^L}
=\frac{\lambda_+^L v_+^{\mathsf T}Dv_++\lambda_-^L v_-^{\mathsf T}Dv_-}{\lambda_+^L+\lambda_-^L}=0.}
$$
Thus its large-$L$ limit is zero at every fixed $T>0$. The result is exact even before taking that limit. Equivalently, simultaneous reversal of all <spins> pairs configurations of equal <energy> and opposite $\sigma_1$. Positivity of the <transfer matrix> at fixed <Temperature>[positive temperature] prevents a degenerate dominant <eigenvalue> and <spontaneous magnetization> in this one-dimensional zero-field chain. Selecting one aligned state at strictly <Absolute zero>[zero temperature] is a different order of limits.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Insert the decorating-spin <observable> into the one bond that contains it. The <decorated-spin occupation insertion> uses the <matrix>:
$$
Q_{st}=\sum_{t_0=-1}^{1}t_0^2e^{Kt_0(s+t)}=2\cosh[K(s+t)]
=\begin{pmatrix}2\cosh2K&2\\2&2\cosh2K\end{pmatrix}_{st}.
$$
The <partition function> still contains $L$ decorating-spin sums; one of them has been replaced by this weighted sum. Hence
$$
\langle\tau_1^2\rangle_L=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(QV^{L-1})}{Z_L}
=\frac{q_+\lambda_+^{L-1}+q_-\lambda_-^{L-1}}{\lambda_+^L+\lambda_-^L},
\qquad q_+=2\cosh2K+2,\quad q_-=2\cosh2K-2.
$$
Taking the dominant <eigenvector> gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{L\to\infty}\langle\tau_1^2\rangle_L=\frac{q_+}{\lambda_+}
=\frac{\cosh2K+1}{\cosh2K+2}=\frac{2\cosh^2K}{1+2\cosh^2K}.}
$$
This is the <probability> that the decorating site is in either nonzero state. It tends to $2/3$ at high <temperature>, when its three states are equally likely, and to one at low <temperature>, when the nonzero aligned states gain <Energy>[bond energy]. Unlike the <magnetization>, this even <observable> is not forced to vanish by <spin> reversal.

==== Solution

Interpret the two displayed exchange components as belonging to the common nearest-neighbour sum with coefficient $-J$. Write $H_f=|h|$ and, for $h\ne0$, $s=\operatorname{sgn}h$. The source explicitly fixes $J>0$ but does not separately constrain $h$ in this question; keeping $H_f$ covers either <Magnetic field>[field] direction.

For <Vector>[classical vectors] of length $S$, define $\mathbf q_j=(S_j^x,S_j^y)$. The <Cauchy–Schwarz inequality> gives $\mathbf q_j\cdot\mathbf q_{j+1}\le|\mathbf q_j||\mathbf q_{j+1}|\le S^2$, and $hS_j^x\le H_fS$. Each term is therefore bounded below, so
$$
E_{\mathrm{cl}}\ge-LJS^2-LH_fS^2.
$$
Uniform alignment along the <Magnetic field>[field] saturates every bound:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf S_j=(sS,0,0),\qquad E_{\mathrm{cl,min}}=-L(J+H_f)S^2\quad(h\ne0).}
$$
Equality requires all planar <vectors> to be parallel with length $S$, and the nonzero <Magnetic field>[field] then fixes their direction. At $h=0$, every uniformly oriented <vector> in the $xy$ plane is a <Ground state>[classical ground state]:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf S_j=S(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),\qquad E_{\mathrm{cl,min}}=-LJS^2.}
$$
A nonuniform configuration cannot improve these simultaneously saturated bond bounds, including on the periodic ring.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Holstein–Primakoff transformation> realizes a <Spin commutation relations>[spin algebra] using <bosonic creation operators> and <bosonic annihilation operators>. A <boson> counts a unit of <spin> deviation from the local positive quantization axis: $\widetilde S^z=S-n$ with $n=a^\dagger a$. The square root in $\widetilde S^+$ supplies the correct <Spin ladder operator>[spin-ladder] <matrix elements>. In particular,
$$
\widetilde S^+|n\rangle=\sqrt{n(2S-n+1)}|n-1\rangle,\qquad
\widetilde S^-|n\rangle=\sqrt{(n+1)(2S-n)}|n+1\rangle.
$$
Their <commutator> is $2(S-n)$ on every physical <Fock state>[number state], reproducing the <Spin commutation relations>. Although the <Bosonic Fock space>[oscillator Fock space] is infinite, the <Holstein–Primakoff occupation constraint>[physical subspace] is only
$$
\boxed{n=0,1,\ldots,2S.}
$$
The order of the square root and oscillator <Linear operator>[operator] matters in obtaining these coefficients.

When the <Magnon>[spin deviations] are dilute, expand the square root in $n/(2S)$. The leading transverse <Linear operators>[operators] are linear in the <bosonic creation operators> and <bosonic annihilation operators> and turn <exchange interactions> into a <Quadratic bosonic Hamiltonian>[quadratic Hamiltonian], which can be diagonalized. Higher powers describe interactions between <spin waves>. The method therefore organizes corrections by inverse powers of $S$ about an ordered classical configuration. Large $S$ alone is insufficient: the average <One-dimensional planar spin-wave depletion>[depletion] must remain small compared with $S$. Possible problems include the <Bosonic occupation number>[occupation] constraint after truncation, noncollinear order requiring site-dependent axis <rotations>, <zero modes>, and <Infrared divergence>[infrared fluctuations] that destroy the assumed dilute expansion.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the local positive $\widetilde z$ axis along the <classical spin>. For nonzero <Magnetic field>[field], a <Rotation>[proper rotation] is
$$
\boxed{S_j^x=s\widetilde S_j^z,\qquad S_j^y=\widetilde S_j^y,\qquad S_j^z=-s\widetilde S_j^x.}
$$
It preserves the <Spin commutation relations> and turns the <Magnetic field>[field] term into $-H_fS\sum_j\widetilde S_j^z$. At zero <Magnetic field>[field] choose the classical direction $+x$, so $s=1$. Expanding about the original $z$ axis without this <rotation> would put the reference state away from the classical minimum and would not give a controlled small-deviation expansion.

To the required order,
$$
\widetilde S_j^z=S-n_j,\qquad
\widetilde S_j^x=\sqrt{\frac S2}(a_j+a_j^\dagger)+O(S^{-1/2}),\qquad
\widetilde S_j^y=-i\sqrt{\frac S2}(a_j-a_j^\dagger)+O(S^{-1/2}).
$$
The longitudinal exchange gives $-JS^2+JS(n_j+n_{j+1})+O(S^0)$ on each bond. The transverse exchange gives
$$
-J\widetilde S_j^y\widetilde S_{j+1}^y
=\frac{JS}{2}\left[a_ja_{j+1}+a_j^\dagger a_{j+1}^\dagger-a_j^\dagger a_{j+1}-a_{j+1}^\dagger a_j\right]+O(S^0).
$$
Finally the <Magnetic field>[field] contributes its classical constant and $H_fSn_j$. Each site belongs to two bonds, so after removing the order-$S^2$ constant,
$$
\boxed{A=-\frac{JS}{2},\qquad B=\frac{JS}{2},\qquad C=S(2J+H_f).}
$$
These are the order-$S$ terms; the discarded products of <number operators> and the next square-root terms are order $S^0$ for <Bosonic occupation numbers>[occupations] held fixed as $S\to\infty$. A different transverse-axis phase can reverse the sign of $B$ without changing the <Energy eigenvalue>[spectrum], but the sign above follows from the stated <rotation> and oscillator convention.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <Discrete Fourier transform>[unitary discrete Fourier transform]
$$
a_j=\frac1{\sqrt L}\sum_ke^{ikj}a_k,\qquad k=\frac{2\pi n}{L},\quad n=0,\ldots,L-1.
$$
The transformed <Linear operators>[operators] obey $[a_k,a_q^\dagger]=\delta_{kq}$. The hopping terms contribute $2A\cos k$ to the number coefficient. In the pairing sum, the odd sine part cancels between $k$ and $-k$. Thus
$$
H_{\mathrm{LSW}}=\sum_k\left[\alpha_k a_k^\dagger a_k+\frac{\Delta_k}{2}(a_ka_{-k}+a_k^\dagger a_{-k}^\dagger)\right],
$$
where
$$
\alpha_k=S(H_f+2J-J\cos k),\qquad \Delta_k=JS\cos k.
$$
The factor one half in the full <Sum>[momentum sum] avoids counting an <Bosonic Bogoliubov diagonalization>[anomalous pair] twice. For $H_f>0$, $\alpha_k>|\Delta_k|$ for every allowed <momentum>. Set
$$
a_k=\cosh\theta_k\,b_k-\sinh\theta_k\,b_{-k}^\dagger,\qquad
\tanh2\theta_k=\frac{\Delta_k}{\alpha_k}.
$$
Real even <angles> ensure consistency between opposite <momenta>, and $\cosh^2\theta_k-\sinh^2\theta_k=1$ preserves the <canonical commutation relations>. The anomalous coefficient becomes $\Delta_k\cosh2\theta_k-\alpha_k\sinh2\theta_k=0$. <Normal ordering> then gives
$$
\boxed{H_{\mathrm{LSW}}=\sum_k\epsilon(k)b_k^\dagger b_k+\frac12\sum_k[\epsilon(k)-\alpha_k],}
$$
with
$$
\boxed{\epsilon(k)=\sqrt{\alpha_k^2-\Delta_k^2}=S\sqrt{(H_f+2J)[H_f+2J(1-\cos k)]}.}
$$
The <field-polarized planar ferromagnetic spin wave> has <energy gap> $\epsilon(0)=S\sqrt{H_f(H_f+2J)}$. The <momenta> run over the whole <Brillouin zone> once, including the self-inverse <momenta> $0$ and, for even $L$, $\pi$; these use <Single-mode squeeze operator>[single-mode squeezing] rather than introducing an extra independent partner.

**The zero-field case has a <zero-mode obstruction to bosonic Bogoliubov diagonalization>.** Its nonzero-momentum modes have $\epsilon(k)=2\sqrt2JS|\sin(k/2)|$, but $\alpha_0=\Delta_0=JS$, so no finite <Bogoliubov transformation>[squeezing angle] exists for the uniform mode. With $Q_0=(a_0+a_0^\dagger)/\sqrt2$, its <Hamiltonian> is $JSQ_0^2-JS/2$, not an identically zero oscillator. For example, its <matrix element> between the <Fock vacuum>[vacuum] and the two-boson <Fock state>[number state] is $JS/\sqrt2$, whereas $\epsilon(0)b_0^\dagger b_0$ would vanish. Consequently the requested oscillator diagonalization is literal for nonzero <Magnetic field>[field]; at zero <Magnetic field>[field] treat the collective orientation separately or introduce a <Magnetic field>[field] regulator before taking a limit.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For nonzero <Magnetic field>[field], the <Spin wave>[spin-wave] <ground state> is the <Fock vacuum>[vacuum] of all $b_k$. The <Bogoliubov transformation>[inverse Bogoliubov transformation] gives $\langle a_k^\dagger a_q\rangle=\delta_{kq}\sinh^2\theta_k$ and vanishing one-point oscillator averages. <Translation invariance>[Translational invariance] therefore gives the <One-dimensional planar spin-wave depletion>[depletion]
$$
\boxed{\delta S=\langle n_j\rangle=\frac1L\sum_k\sinh^2\theta_k
=\frac1{2L}\sum_k\left[\frac{H_f+2J-J\cos k}{\sqrt{(H_f+2J)[H_f+2J(1-\cos k)]}}-1\right].}
$$
Rotating back to the physical axes yields, to the retained <Spin wave>[spin-wave] order,
$$
\boxed{\langle S_j^x\rangle=s(S-\delta S),\qquad \langle S_j^y\rangle=0,\qquad\langle S_j^z\rangle=0.}
$$
The zero transverse components also follow from <rotation> by $\pi$ about the physical $x$ axis in a symmetry-preserving <ground state>. The nonzero <One-dimensional planar spin-wave depletion>[depletion] describes <Quantum fluctuation>[zero-point quantum fluctuations]: the original <boson> <Fock vacuum>[vacuum] is not the <ground state> because the <Bosonic Bogoliubov diagonalization>[anomalous terms] create pairs. It is nonnegative and must satisfy $\delta S\ll S$ for the expansion to be reliable. At strong <Magnetic field>[field] it tends to zero, with $\delta S\sim J^2/(8H_f^2)$ in the infinite-chain limit.

For fixed $H_f>0$, the <Sum>[momentum sum] has a finite <thermodynamic limit> obtained by $L^{-1}\sum_k\to(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dk$. At zero <Magnetic field>[field] the uniform mode already has no oscillator <Fock vacuum>[vacuum], as explained in part d. Even after removing that mode, the nonzero modes behave as
$$
\sinh^2\theta_k=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2|k|}-\frac12+O(|k|),\qquad
\delta S\sim\frac{\log L}{2\pi\sqrt2}+O(1).
$$
Thus assigning a finite ordered <moment> by simply setting $h=0$ in the infinite <Sum>[momentum sum] is invalid. The divergence indicates failure of this expansion about a fixed planar orientation; it is not a negative physical <magnetization>. At finite size an exact zero-field state chosen to have definite total $S^z$ has zero planar one-point <magnetization> by the <Global spin-rotation symmetry about one axis>[continuous rotation symmetry]. Establishing its full <correlations> requires a treatment beyond the divergent ordered-state approximation.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

This is a <birth-death master equation>: $q_n$ is the <transition rate> $n\to n+1$ and $r_n$ is the <transition rate> $n\to n-1$. The first two terms are incoming <probability> flows from the adjacent states; the last removes <probability> at the total escape rate $q_n+r_n$. Rates must be nonnegative. For a population on the nonnegative integers, take $r_0=0$ and no incoming flux from negative states.

Summing over all states and shifting the two incoming indices gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\sum_nP_n=\sum_n r_nP_n+\sum_nq_nP_n-\sum_n(q_n+r_n)P_n=0.
$$
Thus an initially normalized <probability distribution> remains normalized. The cancellation assumes no boundary flux and legitimate summation; on an infinite state space this means the nonexplosive process or a boundary prescription that conserves total <probability>. All rates used in the later parts satisfy this requirement.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Multiply the <master equation> by $z^n$ and sum over $n$. Shifting indices produces
$$
\boxed{\partial_tF=(z-1)\sum_nq_nz^nP_n+(z^{-1}-1)\sum_nr_nz^nP_n.}
$$
The apparent negative power causes no problem at $z=0$ when $r_0=0$. Let $D=z\partial_z$, so $Dz^n=nz^n$. If the rates are polynomials in $n$, replace their arguments by $D$ to obtain a finite-order <partial differential equation>:
$$
\boxed{\partial_tF=\bigl[(z-1)q(D)+(z^{-1}-1)r(D)\bigr]F.}
$$
For arbitrary rates the weighted sums still give an exact generating-function evolution, but need not reduce to a finite-order differential operator. The <probability generating function> obeys
$$
\boxed{F(1,t)=1,}
$$
which is the normalization condition. The evolution has $\partial_tF(1,t)=0$ when the rate sums converge, so an initially normalized distribution stays normalized. Its derivatives at $z=1$ also give the factorial <moments>, and expansion around $z=0$ recovers individual <probabilities>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <Poisson process>, $q_n=g$ and $r_n=0$, so
$$
\partial_tF=g(z-1)F,\qquad F(z,0)=1.
$$
This is an <ordinary differential equation> in time for each fixed $z$, with solution
$$
\boxed{F(z,t)=e^{gt(z-1)}=e^{-gt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(gt)^n}{n!}z^n.}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{P_n(t)=e^{-gt}\frac{(gt)^n}{n!},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The initial condition is interpreted as $P_0(0)=1$, with all other initial <probabilities> zero. Normalization follows from $F(1,t)=1$. The <mean> and <variance> are both $gt$, as expected for independent events arriving at constant rate.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

With $n$ individuals, each of the $n$ can reproduce at rate $\lambda$ and independently die at rate $\lambda$. Thus the total birth and death rates are $q_n=r_n=\lambda n$. The gain into state $n$ comes from a birth in state $n-1$ and a death in state $n+1$, while its loss rate is $2\lambda n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot P_n=\lambda(n-1)P_{n-1}+\lambda(n+1)P_{n+1}-2\lambda nP_n.}
$$
At $n=0$, the birth inflow from negative states is absent and $\dot P_0=\lambda P_1$. The empty state is absorbing: once the population reaches zero, neither process can restart it. Equal birth and death rates make this a <critical branching process>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The rates in part d turn the generating-function equation into
$$
\partial_tF=\lambda\bigl[(z-1)+(z^{-1}-1)\bigr]z\partial_zF
=\lambda(z-1)^2\partial_zF,\qquad F(z,0)=z.
$$
For the <distribution of a critical linear birth-death population>, the <method of characteristics> gives $\dot z=-\lambda(z-1)^2$ and $\dot F=0$. Setting $w=(1-z)^{-1}$ gives $\dot w=-\lambda$, so the initial characteristic coordinate is $w_0=w+\lambda t$. Hence
$$
\boxed{F(z,t)=1-\frac{1-z}{1+\lambda t(1-z)}.}
$$
It has the correct initial value and $F(1,t)=1$. With $a=\lambda t$, rewrite the denominator as $(1+a)[1-az/(1+a)]$ and expand its <geometric series>. The <probabilities> are
$$
\boxed{P_0(t)=\frac{a}{1+a},\qquad
P_n(t)=\frac{a^{n-1}}{(1+a)^{n+1}}\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
At $t=0$ this is understood by continuity: $P_1=1$ and all other <probabilities> vanish. Summing the positive-state <probabilities> gives $1/(1+a)$, which together with $P_0$ verifies normalization directly.

There is eventual extinction with <probability> one, because $P_0(t)\to1$. Nevertheless $\langle n\rangle=F_z(1,t)=1$ at every finite time, while $\operatorname{Var}(n)=2\lambda t$. There is no contradiction: the increasingly rare surviving populations have conditional <mean> $\langle n\mid n>0\rangle=1+\lambda t$. This is the distinction between a constant ensemble <mean> and survival of a typical realization.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The continuous internal <global symmetry in field theory> is
$$
\boxed{\varphi(x)\longmapsto e^{i\alpha}\varphi(x),\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb R\text{ constant},\qquad G=U(1).}
$$
Both $|\varphi|^2$ and $\partial_\mu\varphi^*\partial^\mu\varphi$ are unchanged. The group is the <circle group>, acting by phase <rotations>. The ordinary derivatives do not make it a local <gauge symmetry>: spacetime-dependent $\alpha$ would add derivative terms. There is also the discrete <symmetry> of <complex conjugation>; in the two-real-field description the full internal invariance is $O(2)$, whose connected component is this $U(1)$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <scalar potential> is $V=-m^2|\varphi|^2+\lambda|\varphi|^4/4$. Completing the square gives
$$
V=\frac\lambda4\left(|\varphi|^2-\frac{2m^2}{\lambda}\right)^2-\frac{m^4}{\lambda}.
$$
The <Hamiltonian density> also contains the nonnegative terms $|\dot\varphi|^2+|\nabla\varphi|^2$. Its lowest-energy configurations therefore have no time or spatial variation and minimize the displayed square. Thus
$$
\boxed{|\varphi|^2=v^2=\frac{2m^2}{\lambda},\qquad v=\frac{\sqrt2\,m}{\sqrt\lambda}>0.}
$$
The minima form a circle of phases. Selecting the real positive representative $\varphi=v$ chooses one <vacuum> and spontaneously breaks the continuous <global symmetry in field theory>. The stationarity at $\varphi=0$ is a local maximum of the radial potential, not the <ground state>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $r=v+\sigma/\sqrt2$. Direct differentiation of $\varphi=re^{i\pi/f}$ cancels the mixed radial-angular terms, leaving
$$
|\partial\varphi|^2=\frac12(\partial\sigma)^2+\frac{r^2}{f^2}(\partial\pi)^2.
$$
Use $\lambda=2m^2/v^2$ from part b to expand the potential. The complete local expression is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L={}&\frac12(\partial\sigma)^2+\frac{v^2}{f^2}(\partial\pi)^2-m^2\sigma^2
-\frac{m^2}{\sqrt2v}\sigma^3-\frac{m^2}{8v^2}\sigma^4\\
&+\frac{\sqrt2v}{f^2}\sigma(\partial\pi)^2
+\frac1{2f^2}\sigma^2(\partial\pi)^2+\frac{m^2v^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The radial <field> is canonically normalized, giving $m_\sigma^2=2m^2$. For arbitrary $f$, the canonically normalized angular <field> is $\pi_c=\sqrt2v\,\pi/f$ and has zero <mass>:
$$
\boxed{m_\sigma=\sqrt2\,m,\qquad m_{\pi_c}=0.}
$$
The interactions comprise radial cubic and quartic self-couplings, a derivative $\sigma\pi\pi$ <coupling> and a derivative $\sigma\sigma\pi\pi$ <coupling>. In canonical variables the latter coefficients are $1/(\sqrt2v)$ and $1/(4v^2)$ respectively. The additive constant shifts the <vacuum> <energy> and does not affect these <masses> or interactions.

The original phase <symmetry> becomes the exact shift $\pi\mapsto\pi+f\alpha$, with $\sigma$ unchanged. Any undifferentiated polynomial $\pi^3$ or $\pi^4$ would violate this shift, so such terms are absent. This argument allows derivative interactions; it forbids an angular potential. The massless angular excitation is the <Goldstone boson> for the broken global $U(1)$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the current convention corresponding to $\delta\varphi=i\alpha\varphi$. <Noether's theorem> gives
$$
J^\mu=i\bigl[(\partial^\mu\varphi^*)\varphi-\varphi^*\partial^\mu\varphi\bigr]
=\frac{2r^2}{f}\partial^\mu\pi.
$$
For the requested terms with no $\sigma$ and $f=\sqrt2v$, the <Lagrangian> is $\tfrac12(\partial\pi)^2$ and the current becomes $J^\mu=f\partial^\mu\pi$. The supplied covariant oscillator normalization cancels the invariant measure when the <annihilation operator> contracts with $a^\dagger(p)$, so
$$
\langle0|\pi(x)|\pi(p)\rangle=e^{-ip\cdot x},\qquad
\boxed{\langle0|J^\mu(x)|\pi(p)\rangle=-ifp^\mu e^{-ip\cdot x}.}
$$
The <momentum> is on the massless shell, $p^2=0$. Differentiating once more therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\langle0|\partial_\mu J^\mu(x)|\pi(p)\rangle=-fp^2e^{-ip\cdot x}=0.}
$$
This <Goldstone current matrix element in a phase-only scalar theory> shows that a conserved current can have a nonzero <matrix> element proportional to a null <momentum>. Thus current conservation is fully consistent with the current creating a massless one-particle state from the <vacuum>; the nonzero coefficient $f$ is its Goldstone-current <coupling>. It is the divergence that vanishes, not the current <matrix> element. Reversing the current convention reverses the nonzero <matrix> element but leaves this zero divergence unchanged.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Proca action> is invariant under spacetime translations and proper continuous <Lorentz transformations>, together forming the connected <Poincaré group>. The fixed nonzero <mass> excludes scale invariance. In four dimensions, a dimensionless <action> and the canonical <kinetic term> imply
$$
\boxed{[A^\mu]=1,\qquad[F^{\mu\nu}]=2,\qquad[m_A]=1.}
$$
These are <mass dimensions>. The field-strength term is invariant under $A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chi$, but the <mass> term is not: its change contains $m_A^2 A^\mu\partial_\mu\chi+\tfrac12m_A^2(\partial\chi)^2$. Thus the displayed massive-vector theory has no Maxwell-type local gauge invariance. The massless limit has a different constraint structure.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Varying the <vector field> gives the <Proca equation>,
$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m_A^2A^\nu=0.
$$
Its divergence uses antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ to cancel the first term. Since $m_A\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\partial_\nu A^\nu=0,\qquad(\Box+m_A^2)A^\nu=0.}
$$
For an on-shell <momentum> this imposes $p_\nu\epsilon^\nu=0$ on the four <polarization> components, leaving three independent solutions. In the rest frame, $p=(m_A,\mathbf0)$ makes $\epsilon^0=0$, leaving precisely the three spatial <polarizations> of a massive spin-one particle.

There is no fourth independent oscillator hidden in $A^0$. Its canonical <momentum> is zero because the <Lagrangian> has no $\dot A^0$, and the temporal equation constrains $A^0$ in terms of the spatial data. Equivalently the primary and secondary <second-class constraints> remove two variables from the eight-dimensional <phase space>, leaving six phase-space variables, or three physical degrees of freedom.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $p^\mu=(e,0,0,k)$ with $e^2=k^2+m_A^2$. A real orthonormal basis that satisfies the source's stated bilinear normalization is
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_+^\mu=(0,1,0,0),\qquad
\epsilon_-^\mu=(0,0,1,0),\qquad
\epsilon_L^\mu=\frac1{m_A}(k,0,0,e).}
$$
Each obeys $p\cdot\epsilon_i=0$. The two transverse norms are $-1$, while $\epsilon_L^2=(k^2-e^2)/m_A^2=-1$, and all cross products vanish. For <momentum> in the opposite direction, one consistent longitudinal choice is $\epsilon_L(-k)=(k,0,0,-e)/m_A$ for $k>0$; an overall sign of any <polarization> is immaterial.

Here $+$ and $-$ label the two linear transverse basis vectors. If those symbols are intended to denote circular <helicities>, choose $\epsilon_\pm^{\mathrm{hel}}=(\epsilon_x\pm i\epsilon_y)/\sqrt2$ instead. The corresponding orthonormality relation must contain <complex conjugation>, $\epsilon_i^*\cdot\epsilon_j=-\delta_{ij}$. Without that conjugation, a circular <polarization> has $\epsilon_+^{\mathrm{hel}}\cdot\epsilon_+^{\mathrm{hel}}=0$, a concrete counterexample to interpreting the printed bilinear condition as a <helicity> normalization.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For <longitudinal-vector annihilation through a scalar contact interaction>, first use the **supplied vertex** $2ig^2\eta_{\mu\nu}$. Let $s=(p_1+p_2)^2$ and work in the <center-of-mass frame>. The amplitude is $\mathcal M=2g^2\epsilon_1\cdot\epsilon_2$, up to its overall phase. For two longitudinal incoming vectors,
$$
\epsilon_L(p_1)\cdot\epsilon_L(p_2)=\frac{p_1\cdot p_2}{m_A^2}=\frac{s-2m_A^2}{2m_A^2},
\qquad
|\mathcal M_{LL}|^2=g^4\frac{(s-2m_A^2)^2}{m_A^4}.
$$
This grows as $s^2/m_A^4$, whereas transverse amplitudes stay of order $g^2$ and mixed longitudinal-transverse amplitudes vanish in this frame. Consequently the leading high-energy contribution is longitudinal.

For an **<unpolarized cross section>[unpolarized]** total <cross section>, average over the $3\times3$ incoming <polarizations>. The massive-vector <polarization> sum gives
$$
\sum_{i,j}|\epsilon_i(p_1)\cdot\epsilon_j(p_2)|^2
=2+\frac{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2}{m_A^4}.
$$
The identical final scalars require a factor $1/2!$ in the two-body <phase space>. With $\beta_A=\sqrt{1-4m_A^2/s}$ and $\beta_h=\sqrt{1-4m_h^2/s}$, the angle-independent result is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{unpol}}(s)=\frac{\beta_h}{\beta_A}\frac{g^4}{288\pi s}
\left[\frac{(s-2m_A^2)^2}{m_A^4}+8\right]
\underset{s\gg m^2}{\sim}\frac{g^4s}{288\pi m_A^4}.}
$$
For a prepared longitudinal pair there is no incoming <polarization> average, giving instead
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{LL}(s)\sim\frac{g^4s}{32\pi m_A^4}=9\sigma_{\mathrm{unpol}}(s).}
$$
There is no extra initial-state factor $1/2$: a <cross section> uses the incoming beam flux, while the final-state factor removes double counting of indistinguishable outgoing states.

Define the <energy> in the stated bound to be the total <center-of-mass energy>, $E=\sqrt s$. Equating the leading expressions to $4\pi/E^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{unpol}}=(1152\pi^2)^{1/4}\frac{m_A}{g}\simeq2.58\ \mathrm{TeV},\qquad
E_{LL}=(128\pi^2)^{1/4}\frac{m_A}{g}\simeq1.49\ \mathrm{TeV},}
$$
for $m_A=100\ \mathrm{GeV}$ and $g=0.4$. The second value shows that a longitudinal channel reaches the supplied perturbative bound earlier. If the source's unspecified $E$ denotes the <energy> of *one* incoming particle and the bound is literally $4\pi/E^2$, substitute $s=4E^2$ instead: the corresponding values are $1.83\ \mathrm{TeV}$ <unpolarized cross section>[unpolarized] and $1.05\ \mathrm{TeV}$ longitudinal. Stating the <energy> and <polarization> conventions is necessary for a numerical answer.

**Interaction-normalization qualification:** the written $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}=g^2 A_\mu A^\mu h^2$ differentiates to $4ig^2\eta_{\mu\nu}$, because there are $2!$ vector assignments and $2!$ scalar assignments. For example, differentiating $g^2(A^0)^2h^2$ twice in each variable gives $4g^2$. The supplied $2ig^2\eta_{\mu\nu}$ would instead follow from $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}=g^2 A_\mu A^\mu h^2/2$. Thus all <cross sections> above follow the explicitly requested rule. Using the literal written interaction multiplies them by four and divides every quoted threshold <energy> by $\sqrt2$. The <energy> growth itself is the physical issue: a contact interaction of massive longitudinal vectors needs additional dynamics before its perturbative high-energy extrapolation becomes reliable.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Introduce three independent local parameters $\alpha_j(x)$. A consistent convention for the three Abelian <gauge transformations> is
$$
\boxed{B_\mu^j\longmapsto B_\mu^j-\frac1g\partial_\mu\alpha_j,\qquad
\phi_1\longmapsto e^{i(\alpha_1-q\alpha_2)}\phi_1,\qquad
\phi_2\longmapsto e^{i(\alpha_2-q\alpha_3)}\phi_2.}
$$
Indeed, $g(B_\mu^k-qB_\mu^{k+1})$ shifts by $-\partial_\mu(\alpha_k-q\alpha_{k+1})$, cancelling the derivative of the scalar phase. Thus $D_\mu\phi_k$ transforms by the same phase as $\phi_k$. The two <kinetic terms>, the absolute-value potentials and all Abelian <field> strengths are invariant.

In units where the gauge <coupling> is $g$, the charge vectors under the three factors are
$$
\boxed{Q(\phi_1)=(1,-q,0),\qquad Q(\phi_2)=(0,1,-q).}
$$
Locally this is the gauge algebra of $U(1)^3$. To interpret it globally as three compact circle groups, the charges must belong to a compatible charge lattice: in the stated unit-charge convention integer $q$ is sufficient, while arbitrary irrational $q$ is not a single-valued compact-group representation. The local transformations and <mass> calculation remain meaningful for general positive real $q$ as a Lie-algebra calculation, or with noncompact Abelian factors.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For this <three-site Abelian Higgs chain>, the potential is minimized by $|\phi_1|=|\phi_2|=v$. Choose their phases real in a local <unitary gauge>. The scalar <kinetic terms> then give
$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mass}}=g^2v^2\left[(B^1-qB^2)^2+(B^2-qB^3)^2\right]
=g^2v^2B^{\mathsf T}KB,
$$
where Lorentz contractions are understood and
$$
K=R^{\mathsf T}R=\begin{pmatrix}1&-q&0\\-q&1+q^2&-q\\0&-q&q^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R=\begin{pmatrix}1&-q&0\\0&1&-q\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The nonzero <eigenvalues> of $K$ equal those of
$$
RR^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}1+q^2&-q\\-q&1+q^2\end{pmatrix},
$$
namely $1+q^2-q$ and $1+q^2+q$. The third <eigenvalue> is zero because $R$ has rank two. Both nonzero <eigenvalues> are positive for $q>0$.

**Mass-normalization correction:** the canonical vector <mass> term is $\tfrac12B^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^2B$, so the <Lagrangian> actually gives $\mathsf M^2=2g^2v^2K$. Its physical <masses> are therefore
$$
\boxed{m_1=0,\qquad m_2=\sqrt2gv\sqrt{q^2-q+1},\qquad
m_3=\sqrt2gv\sqrt{q^2+q+1}.}
$$
The printed dimensionless <eigenvalue> pattern is correct, but its common scale must be $m=\sqrt2gv$, not $gv$, for the written <kinetic terms> and <vacuum> value. For a concrete counterexample set $q=1$ and $B=(A,0,-A)/\sqrt2$. This is a canonically normalized eigenmode and its <mass> term is $g^2v^2A_\mu A^\mu$, giving physical squared <mass> $2g^2v^2$ rather than the printed $g^2v^2$. The printed scale $gv$ would require a <vacuum> modulus $v/\sqrt2$ or a corresponding change in scalar normalization, neither of which is the specified potential minimum.

The unbroken transformations preserve both <vacuum> phases:
$$
\alpha_1-q\alpha_2=0,\qquad\alpha_2-q\alpha_3=0,
\qquad (\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)=\alpha(q^2,q,1).
$$
Thus the local breaking pattern is
$$
\boxed{U(1)^3\longrightarrow U(1).}
$$
Two scalar phases supply the longitudinal <polarizations> of the two massive <vector bosons>; one massless vector remains. This is the <Higgs mechanism>, with its <mass> <matrix> fixed by the scalar charge <matrix> and the <vacuum> normalization.<Gauge Theory by David Tong>[Section 2.5.2, expanding scalar kinetic terms around a condensate to obtain a vector mass]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A massless <eigenvector> must lie in the <null space> of $R$, so its coefficients satisfy $c_1=qc_2$ and $c_2=qc_3$. Hence they are proportional to $(q^2,q,1)$. The original gauge <kinetic terms> have the Euclidean unit <matrix> in species space, so normalize this vector by its ordinary squared length. The massless <field> is
$$
\boxed{A_1^\mu=\frac{q^2B_1^\mu+qB_2^\mu+B_3^\mu}{\sqrt{q^4+q^2+1}}.}
$$
The remaining two <eigenvectors> can be chosen orthogonal to it, making the full transformation orthogonal and preserving canonical vector <kinetic terms>. At $q=1$, the formula is exactly $(B_1^\mu+B_2^\mu+B_3^\mu)/\sqrt3$ as required. This null direction and its normalization are unaffected by the common mass-scale correction in part b.

For $q>1$, the massless mode has geometrically unequal components; a matter <field> coupled to one end can therefore couple more weakly to this surviving vector than a <field> coupled to the other end. This follows directly from the <eigenvector> coefficients, without changing the equal microscopic gauge <coupling> $g$.
