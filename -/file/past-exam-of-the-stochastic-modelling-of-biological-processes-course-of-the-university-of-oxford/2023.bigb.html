= Solution

{c}

Write $n=n_1$ and $m=n_2$. The three <stoichiometric vectors> are $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$. The <law of mass action> gives the respective <reaction propensity functions>
$$
a_1(n,m)=2,\qquad a_2(n,m)=\frac n2,\qquad a_3(n,m)=nm.
$$
In particular, producing the second <chemical species> does not consume the first <chemical species>. Since the two reactants in the third <chemical reaction> are different <chemical species>, the number of possible pairs is $nm$.

For each <Markov chain state>, the <chemical master equation> adds incoming <probability> flux and subtracts outgoing <probability> flux:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial p(n,m,t)}{\partial t}
={}&2p(n-1,m,t)+\frac n2p(n,m-1,t)\\
&+(n+1)(m+1)p(n+1,m+1,t)\\
&-\left(2+\frac n2+nm\right)p(n,m,t).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $p(n,m,t)=0$ if either index is negative, and the <initial condition> is $p(n,m,0)=\mathbf1_{\{n=m=0\}}$. These conventions also give the correct <chemical master equation> on the coordinate axes: for example, the third <chemical reaction> has zero outgoing <reaction propensity function> when either <chemical species> is absent.

At the empty <Markov chain state>, only the constant-rate production <chemical reaction> is possible. Thus the <first-passage time> $\tau_1$ has an <exponential distribution> of rate $2$:
$$
\mathbb P(\tau_1>t)=e^{-2t},\qquad t\geq0.
$$
Its <expected value> can be obtained by integrating this <survival function>, and its second <moment> by integrating $2t$ times the <survival function>:
$$
\mathbb E\tau_1=\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt=\frac12,
\qquad
\mathbb E\tau_1^2=2\int_0^\infty te^{-2t}\,dt=\frac12.
$$
Consequently the requested <expected value> and <variance> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\tau_1=\frac12,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\tau_1)=\frac14.}
$$

**Conditioning on the observed time.** The native PDF defines $\tau_2$ as the first production time and then conditions on that production occurring at $\tau_2$. If the intended information is its numerical value $\tau_2=t$, the answer depends on $t$. We first compute this <conditional probability> using a <conditional probability density>; conditioning on an exact value of a continuous <random variable> means a density ratio, since $\mathbb P(\tau_2=t)=0$.

Before $\tau_2$, no molecules of the second <chemical species> are present, so the joint-removal <chemical reaction> cannot occur. Molecules of the first <chemical species> arrive as a rate-$2$ <Poisson process>, and each independently starts a clock with a rate-$1/2$ <exponential distribution> for producing the first molecule of the second <chemical species>. Define the unnormalized <probability mass function>
$$
q_n(t)=\mathbb P\bigl(A_1(t)=n,\ \tau_2>t\bigr),\qquad
F(z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty q_n(t)z^n.
$$
The <chemical master equation> before the first production, with production treated as killing, is
$$
q_n'=2q_{n-1}-\left(2+\frac n2\right)q_n,
\qquad q_n(0)=\mathbf1_{\{n=0\}}.
$$
Multiplying by $z^n$ and summing gives a <partial differential equation> for the <probability generating function>:
$$
F_t=2(z-1)F-\frac z2F_z,\qquad F(z,0)=1.
$$
Its solution, verifiable by direct <differentiation>, is
$$
F(z,t)=\exp\bigl[-2t+m(t)z\bigr],
\qquad m(t)=4(1-e^{-t/2}).
$$
Thus $q_n(t)=e^{-2t}m(t)^n/n!$, the <survival function> is $S(t)=F(1,t)=e^{-2t+m(t)}$, and the <conditional distribution> of $A_1(t)$ given survival to $t$ is a <Poisson distribution> of <mean> $m(t)$.

Production in $(t,t+dt)$ from <Markov chain state> $n$ has joint <probability> $(n/2)q_n(t)\,dt+o(dt)$. Therefore the <probability density function> of $\tau_2$ is
$$
f_{\tau_2}(t)=\frac12\sum_{n\geq1}nq_n(t)
=\frac12m(t)S(t),\qquad t>0.
$$
The first production leaves $A_1$ unchanged. Taking the density ratio gives a <size-biased distribution> of the surviving count:
$$
\mathbb P\bigl(A_1(\tau_2)=n\mid\tau_2=t\bigr)
=\frac{nq_n(t)}{m(t)S(t)}
=e^{-m(t)}\frac{m(t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!},\qquad n\geq1.
$$
In particular, under this reading the requested <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\bigl(A_1(\tau_2)=2\mid\tau_2=t\bigr)
=4(1-e^{-t/2})\exp\!\bigl[-4(1-e^{-t/2})\bigr],\quad t>0.}
$$
The shift by one matters: an observation made exactly when production occurs preferentially selects larger counts, since their production <reaction propensity function> is larger.

**Without observing the numerical time.** If the conditioning merely repeats the definition of $\tau_2$, the requested <probability> is instead the unconditional count at that <stopping time>. Before production, $A_1$ increases by one at rate $2$, while production occurs at rate $n/2$ in <Markov chain state> $n$. The <competing exponential clocks> imply
$$
\mathbb P\bigl(A_1(\tau_2)=2\bigr)
=\frac{2}{2+1/2}\frac{1}{2+1}
=\boxed{\frac4{15}}.
$$
The first factor requires a second arrival before production from count one; the second requires production before a third arrival. The first arrival itself is certain. Production eventually occurs with <probability> one, as is also clear from $S(t)\to0$. As a consistency check, averaging the time-conditioned answer against $f_{\tau_2}$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty m(t)e^{-m(t)}\frac{m(t)}2e^{-2t+m(t)}\,dt
=8\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}(1-e^{-t/2})^2\,dt
=\frac4{15}.
$$
These are two distinct, explicitly specified interpretations of the printed conditioning.

A <product-Poisson stationary distribution> is suggested by balancing the three reaction fluxes around the directed cycle of <reaction complexes>. If their <mean> counts are $a,b$, this <complex balance> condition is
$$
2=\frac a2=ab,
$$
which gives $a=4$ and $b=1/2$. This suggests the <joint probability mass function>
$$
\pi(n,m)=e^{-9/2}\frac{4^n}{n!}\frac{(1/2)^m}{m!},\qquad n,m\geq0.
$$
It is normalized because it is the product of two <Poisson distributions>. We verify it directly in the <chemical master equation>, rather than assuming a <product-Poisson stationary distribution> theorem. The three incoming terms satisfy
$$
\begin{aligned}
2\pi(n-1,m)&=\frac n2\pi(n,m),\\
\frac n2\pi(n,m-1)&=nm\pi(n,m),\\
(n+1)(m+1)\pi(n+1,m+1)&=2\pi(n,m).
\end{aligned}
$$
With negative-index values set to zero, these identities also hold on the axes. Their sum equals $(2+n/2+nm)\pi(n,m)$, precisely the outgoing <probability> flux. The stationary average total exit rate is $2+\mathbb E_\pi n/2+\mathbb E_\pi(nm)=6<\infty$, so these <stationary balance equations> do define an <invariant distribution of a continuous-time Markov chain> for the nonexplosive model verified below. This is <complex balance>; it does not assert pairwise <detailed balance> for the irreversible <chemical reactions>.

To identify this <invariant distribution> with the limiting <probability distribution> in the question, first check that the model is a <nonexplosive continuous-time Markov chain>. On any finite time interval, $A_1$ is bounded by its initial count plus the finite number of arrivals in a rate-$2$ <Poisson process>. Conditional on that bound, the rate of producing $A_2$ is bounded, so only finitely many such productions occur. The number of joint removals cannot exceed the available molecules. Thus only finitely many <chemical reactions> occur in finite time.

The <continuous-time Markov chain> is also an <irreducible Markov chain>. From a <Markov chain state> with both counts positive, repeated joint removals reduce both. On the $A_2=0$ axis, producing one $A_2$ and then removing a pair reduces $A_1$ by one. On the $A_1=0$ axis, adding one $A_1$ and then removing a pair reduces $A_2$ by one. Each prescribed finite sequence has positive <probability>, so every <Markov chain state> reaches $(0,0)$. Conversely, arrivals followed by productions reach any $(n,m)$ with $n>0$; to reach $(0,m)$, reach $(1,m+1)$ and remove one pair. The <continuous-time Markov chain> is therefore <positive recurrent>, and the <continuous-time Markov chain convergence theorem> gives $\phi=\pi$ from the empty <initial condition>.

The two <Poisson> factors happen to have cancelling numerical coefficients at $(2,2)$:
$$
\boxed{\phi(2,2)=e^{-9/2}\frac{4^2}{2!}\frac{(1/2)^2}{2!}=e^{-9/2}.}
$$

The native PDF repeats $dW_y$ in its list of independent noise terms, whereas its third <stochastic differential equation> uses $dW_z$. We use the intended three independent coordinate <Brownian motions> $W_x,W_y,W_z$.

Put $\mathbf X=(X,Y,Z)$, $r=|\mathbf X|$ and $S=r^2$. Before the origin is reached, <Itô's formula> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
dS&=2\mathbf X\mathbin{\cdot}d\mathbf X+(dX)^2+(dY)^2+(dZ)^2\\
&=(6-2\alpha)\,dt+2\sqrt2\,\mathbf X\mathbin{\cdot}d\mathbf W.
\end{aligned}
$$
The $6$ is the sum of three <quadratic variation> terms, each $2\,dt$. The <drift> contribution is $-2\alpha\,dt$ because the numerator is $|\mathbf X|^2$. The radial <stochastic integral> can be written using a scalar <Brownian motion> $B$:
$$
dS=2(3-\alpha)\,dt+2\sqrt{2S}\,dB,
\qquad
d(S/2)=(3-\alpha)\,dt+2\sqrt{S/2}\,dB.
$$
Thus $S/2$ is a <squared Bessel process> of dimension $\delta=3-\alpha$ until it reaches zero. If this equation is taken globally with the usual nonnegative <squared Bessel process> continuation, taking <expected values> gives
$$
\mathbb E S(t)=r_0^2+2(3-\alpha)t,
\qquad
\boxed{\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E S(t)}t=6-2\alpha.}
$$
The <stochastic integral> has zero <expected value>: stopping first at large levels bounds the integrands, yields a finite first <moment> for $S$, and then makes $\mathbb E\int_0^tS(u)\,du$ finite, so the unrestricted integral is a square-integrable <martingale>.

**Boundary qualification.** The punctured domain in the question does not itself specify that continuation. The boundary classification of a <Bessel process> says that the origin is inaccessible from $r_0>0$ when $\delta\geq2$, equivalently $\alpha\leq1$. In this parameter range the original <stochastic differential equation> is global in the stated domain and the boxed result holds as written.

For $1<\alpha<3$, we have $0<\delta<2$: the origin is reached in finite time with <probability> one. One can also see accessibility directly from the radial <generator of an SDE diffusion>
$$
L_rf=f''+\frac{2-\alpha}{r}f'.
$$
For $r<b$, the <harmonic function> $1-(r/b)^{\alpha-1}$ is the <probability> of reaching zero before $b$. Finite-time access follows by using the nonnegative function
$$
v_b(r)=\frac{b^{3-\alpha}r^{\alpha-1}-r^2}{2(3-\alpha)},
\qquad L_rv_b=-1,\qquad v_b(0)=v_b(b)=0,
$$
in the stopped <Itô formula>; it bounds the <expected value> of the time to reach zero or $b$. Letting $b\to\infty$ gives almost sure finite hitting of zero. The coefficients of the original vector <stochastic differential equation> are undefined there, so its stated punctured-domain version has no prescribed long-time evolution in this range.

The natural continuing radial <squared Bessel process> gives $6-2\alpha$, but stopping and holding the particle at the origin would give a different answer. Indeed, with $T_0$ the first hit of zero, <Itô's formula> for the stopped process gives
$$
\mathbb E S(t\wedge T_0)=r_0^2+2(3-\alpha)\mathbb E(t\wedge T_0).
$$
Since $T_0<\infty$ almost surely, <dominated convergence> applied to $(t\wedge T_0)/t\leq1$ gives
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E S(t\wedge T_0)}t=0.
$$
Therefore **$6-2\alpha$ is the usual continued-radial answer; for $1<\alpha<3$ a boundary convention is necessary.** It is not a uniquely defined limit for the punctured-domain model alone.

At $\alpha=0$ the transverse coordinates are independent <Brownian motions> with <diffusion coefficient> $D=1$. The <cylinder> is infinitely long, so the axial coordinate cannot affect its first contact time. By <rotational invariance of Brownian motion>, the <mean first-passage time> $u$ depends only on the transverse <radius> $\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$.

The <Kolmogorov backward equation> for a <mean first-passage time> is $Lu=-1$. Here $L=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$, so the radial <boundary value problem> is
$$
u''(\rho)+\frac1\rho u'(\rho)=-1\quad(0<\rho<R),
\qquad u(R)=0.
$$
The <absorbing boundary condition for a diffusion> is imposed on the circular cross-section. At $\rho=0$, the solution must extend smoothly over the axis. Multiplying by $\rho$ and integrating gives
$$
(\rho u')'=-\rho,
\qquad u'=-\frac\rho2+\frac C\rho,
\qquad u=-\frac{\rho^2}4+C\log\rho+C_0.
$$
Smoothness at the axis excludes the <logarithm> term, and $u(R)=0$ fixes $C_0=R^2/4$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\tau(\widehat r_0)=\frac{R^2-\widehat r_0^{\,2}}4.}
$$
This also verifies the finiteness assumption behind the <Kolmogorov backward equation>. Let $T$ be the first contact time and apply the stopped <Itô formula> to the bounded nonnegative function $u$ just obtained. Its bounded derivatives on the disk make the stopped <stochastic integral> a <martingale>, giving
$$
\mathbb E(t\wedge T)=u(\widehat r_0)-\mathbb E u\bigl(\rho(t\wedge T)\bigr)\leq u(\widehat r_0).
$$
By <monotone convergence>, $T$ has finite <expected value>; continuity and <dominated convergence> then make the final term vanish as $t\to\infty$. Hence $u(\widehat r_0)=\mathbb ET$. In particular, the <mean first-passage time> is $R^2/4$ on the axis and tends to zero at the adsorbing wall.

For a radial function $f(r)$ in three dimensions, the <Laplacian> is $f''+2f'/r$, while the imposed radial <drift> contributes $-\alpha f'/r$. Thus the radial <generator of an SDE diffusion> is
$$
L_rf=f''+\frac{2-\alpha}{r}f'.
$$
The <hitting probability> for the inner <sphere> is <harmonic> for this <diffusion generator> in the shell, with value one on the inner <sphere> and zero on the outer <sphere>. At $\alpha=1$, the <boundary value problem> is therefore
$$
p''(r)+\frac1rp'(r)=0,
\qquad p(R_1)=1,\qquad p(R_2)=0.
$$
Integrating $(rp')'=0$ gives $p(r)=A\log r+B$. The two <boundary conditions> determine
$$
\boxed{p(r_0)=\frac{\log(R_2/r_0)}{\log(R_2/R_1)}.}
$$
Both arguments of the <logarithms> are dimensionless. The result decreases from one to zero across the shell and lies strictly between zero and one for $R_1<r_0<R_2$.

For a direct <probability> interpretation, stop at the first contact $T$ with either <sphere>. The stopped process $p(r(t\wedge T))$ is a bounded <martingale> by <Itô's formula>. The shell has positive inner <radius>, so its <drift> is bounded and its <diffusion> is nondegenerate; exit occurs almost surely. <Optional stopping> and <dominated convergence> therefore give
$$
p(r_0)=\mathbb E p(r(T))=\mathbb P\bigl(r(T)=R_1\bigr).
$$
The <logarithm> appears because the inward <drift> cancels one of the two geometric $f'/r$ terms in the three-dimensional radial <Laplacian>, leaving the same radial <diffusion generator> as a planar <Brownian motion> with <diffusion coefficient> one.

We use the equilibrium <generalized Langevin equation> and its <generalized fluctuation-dissipation relation>, with the factor $5$ in the noise <autocorrelation> fixing the equilibrium <velocity> <variance>. Let $g$ be the <velocity> relaxation function and let a tilde denote a one-sided <Laplace transform>:
$$
g'(t)=-\int_0^t\kappa(u)g(t-u)\,du,
\qquad g(0)=1,
\qquad
\widetilde g(s)=\frac1{s+\widetilde\kappa(s)}.
$$
The equilibrium <velocity> <autocorrelation function> is $\chi(t)=5g(t)$, so
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\chi(s)=\frac5{s+\widetilde\kappa(s)},\qquad \chi(0)=5.}
$$
For completeness, this relation follows without assuming independence of the noise and <velocity> in a stationary infinite history. The two-sided noise <power spectral density> is $S_R(\omega)=10\operatorname{Re}\widetilde\kappa(i\omega)$. The stable linear equation has <velocity> transfer function $H(i\omega)=[i\omega+\widetilde\kappa(i\omega)]^{-1}$, so
$$
S_V(\omega)=|H(i\omega)|^2S_R(\omega)
=10\operatorname{Re}H(i\omega).
$$
This is precisely the <power spectral density> of the even extension of $5g(t)$. Stable relaxation removes initial transients with a finite second <moment>, giving the long-time <autocorrelation function> defined in the question. All the parameter choices used below have stable relaxation.

Integrating the <velocity autocorrelation function> twice gives the <mean squared displacement> from a fixed initial <position>:
$$
\mathbb E\bigl[(X(t)-X(0))^2\bigr]
=2\int_0^t(t-u)\chi(u)\,du.
$$
A decaying initial transient does not change its asymptotic slope. Therefore the <Green-Kubo formula for diffusion> gives
$$
D=\int_0^\infty\chi(u)\,du
=\widetilde\chi(0)
=\frac5{\widetilde\kappa(0)}.
$$
For the given friction <memory kernel>, direct <integration> yields
$$
\widetilde\kappa(s)=\frac{c_1}{s+c_3}+\frac{c_1c_2}{(s+c_3)^2},
\qquad
\widetilde\kappa(0)=\frac{c_1}{c_3}+\frac{c_1c_2}{c_3^2}.
$$
At $c_1=c_2=c_3=1$, the integrated friction is $2$, hence
$$
\boxed{D=\frac52.}
$$
The relaxation denominator here is $s^3+2s^2+2s+2$. Its roots have negative real parts by the cubic <Routh-Hurwitz criterion>, since all coefficients are positive and $2\cdot2>2$. This justifies the decay and <integrability> used above. The <diffusion coefficient> depends on the area under the friction <memory kernel>, not just its value at zero.

For $c_1=2$, $c_2=4$, $c_3=5$, the friction <memory kernel> and its <Laplace transform> are
$$
\kappa(t)=2(1+4t)e^{-5t},
\qquad
\widetilde\kappa(s)=\frac2{s+5}+\frac8{(s+5)^2}
=\frac{2(s+9)}{(s+5)^2}.
$$
Using the <generalized fluctuation-dissipation relation> with equilibrium <velocity> <variance> $5$, the <velocity> <autocorrelation function> has <Laplace transform>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde\chi(s)
&=\frac5{s+\widetilde\kappa(s)}\\
&=\frac{5(s+5)^2}{s(s+5)^2+2(s+9)}\\
&=\frac{5(s+5)^2}{s^3+10s^2+27s+18}\\
&=\frac{5(s+5)^2}{(s+1)(s+3)(s+6)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
All three poles are negative, so the stationary relaxation is stable. A <partial fraction decomposition> gives
$$
\widetilde\chi(s)=\frac8{s+1}-\frac{10/3}{s+3}+\frac{1/3}{s+6}.
$$
Taking the inverse <Laplace transform> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi(\tau)=8e^{-\tau}-\frac{10}3e^{-3\tau}+\frac13e^{-6\tau},\qquad \tau\geq0.}
$$
The negative middle coefficient is compatible with an <autocorrelation function>; individual terms in a <partial fraction decomposition> need not themselves be separate physical modes with positive weights. Several useful checks follow directly:
$$
\chi(0)=8-\frac{10}3+\frac13=5,
\qquad
\chi'(0)=-8+10-2=0,
\qquad
\chi''(0)=8-30+12=-10.
$$
The <Volterra integral equation> for $\chi$ predicts exactly $\chi'(0)=0$ and $\chi''(0)=-\kappa(0)\chi(0)=-10$. Its <integral> is also $8-10/9+1/18=125/18=5/\widetilde\kappa(0)$, agreeing with the <Green-Kubo formula for diffusion>.

Write $c=c_1=c_3$, so the friction <memory kernel> is $\kappa_c(t)=ce^{-ct}$ and has area one for every $c>0$. Its <Laplace transform> is $\widetilde\kappa_c(s)=c/(s+c)$. The <generalized fluctuation-dissipation relation> therefore gives
$$
\widetilde\chi_c(s)=\frac5{s+c/(s+c)}
=\frac{5(s+c)}{s^2+cs+c}
\longrightarrow\frac5{s+1}
\quad(c\to\infty).
$$
To verify the pointwise time-domain limit, rather than relying only on <Laplace transform> convergence, consider $c>4$. The two roots
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4c}}2
$$
are negative and satisfy $\lambda_+\to-1$, $\lambda_-\to-\infty$. Since $\chi_c(0)=5$ and $\chi_c'(0)=0$, the inverse <Laplace transform> is
$$
\chi_c(\tau)=5\frac{-\lambda_-e^{\lambda_+\tau}+\lambda_+e^{\lambda_-\tau}}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}.
$$
The coefficient of the slow exponential tends to $5$, and that of the fast exponential tends to zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lim_{c_1\to\infty}\chi(\tau)=5e^{-\tau},\qquad \tau\geq0.}
$$
This is the <velocity> <autocorrelation function> of an <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process> satisfying $dV=-V\,dt+\sqrt{10}\,dW$. The friction <memory kernel> becomes local in time. Consistently, the even noise <autocorrelation> $5ce^{-c|\tau|}$ tends to $10\delta(\tau)$ in the sense of <distributions>, fixing the <white noise> normalization. The finite-$c$ right derivative $\chi_c'(0)=0$ does not converge to the right derivative $-5$ of the limiting function: there is a short initial relaxation layer.

The integrated friction is exactly
$$
\widetilde\kappa_c(0)=\int_0^\infty ce^{-ct}\,dt=1.
$$
For every finite $c>0$, the roots of $s^2+cs+c$ have negative real parts. The <Green-Kubo formula for diffusion> therefore gives the <diffusion coefficient> directly:
$$
D_c=\widetilde\chi_c(0)=\frac5{\widetilde\kappa_c(0)}=5.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lim_{c_1\to\infty}D=5.}
$$
Integrating the limiting <velocity> <autocorrelation function> gives the same value, $\int_0^\infty5e^{-\tau}\,d\tau=5$. Computing $D_c$ before taking the limit also avoids having to justify an interchange of the infinite-time <integral> and the parameter limit.
