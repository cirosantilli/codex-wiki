= Solution

A <distribution> on $(a,b)$ is a continuous <linear functional> on the <space of test functions> $\mathcal D(a,b)=C_c^\infty(a,b)$. It has <order of a distribution> at most $m$ if, for every <compact set> $K\subset(a,b)$, there is $C_K$ such that
$$
|\langle u,\phi\rangle|\leq C_K\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\|\phi^{(j)}\|_\infty
\qquad(\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq K).
$$
The <order of a distribution> is the least such $m$. When saying “of order $m$” means merely that this estimate holds, the wording allows a smaller least order.

For each such $K$, the <cutoff function> theorem supplies a nonnegative $\chi\in\mathcal D(a,b)$ equal to one near $K$. If $M=\|\phi\|_\infty$ and $\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq K$, then $M\chi\pm\phi\geq0$ everywhere. Positivity of the <linear functional> $V$ therefore gives
$$
0\leq M V(\chi)\pm V(\phi),\qquad
\boxed{|V(\phi)|\leq V(\chi)\|\phi\|_\infty.}
$$
Here $V(\chi)\geq0$ is finite and independent of $\phi$. This is precisely the local bound for an <order-zero distribution>, so the given <positive distribution> is continuous on the <space of test functions>. The zero <distribution> also satisfies the estimate.

The <distributional derivative> of $u$ is defined by
$$
\boxed{\langle u',\phi\rangle=-\langle u,\phi'\rangle\quad(\phi\in\mathcal D(a,b)).}
$$
It is a <distribution> because $\phi\mapsto\phi'$ is a continuous map of the <space of test functions> into itself. If $f\in C^1(a,b)$ represents a <regular distribution>, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\langle f',\phi\rangle=-\int_a^b f\phi'=\int_a^b f'\phi.
$$
The <boundary term> vanishes because $\phi$ has <compact support>. Thus the <distributional derivative> agrees with the ordinary <derivative>.

For $\operatorname{supp}\phi\subseteq K$, the assumed <order of a distribution> estimate gives
$$
|\langle u',\phi\rangle|\leq C_K\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\|\phi^{(j+1)}\|_\infty
\leq C_K\max_{0\leq j\leq m+1}\|\phi^{(j)}\|_\infty.
$$
Consequently $\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(u')\leq m+1}$.

On each interval $[k,k+1]$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, the <function> is $d(x)=x-k$ on $[k,k+\tfrac12]$ and $d(x)=k+1-x$ on $[k+\tfrac12,k+1]$. Its ordinary <derivative> exists off $\tfrac12\mathbb Z$ and is the <locally integrable function>
$$
s(x)=\begin{cases}
1,&k<x<k+\tfrac12,\\
-1,&k+\tfrac12<x<k+1,
\end{cases}\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Assign arbitrary values at the endpoints. For a <test function> $\phi$, split $-\int d\phi'$ at these corners and apply <integration by parts> on each piece. The adjacent <boundary terms> cancel because $d$ is continuous, and only finitely many pieces meet the <compact support> of $\phi$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle d',\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb R}s(x)\phi(x)\,dx,\qquad d'=s.}
$$
The <distributional derivative> is exactly the <regular distribution> of the ordinary <derivative> defined <almost everywhere>. There are no <Dirac delta distributions> in this first <distributional derivative>: corners change slope, while a jump of the <function> itself would create a point mass.

The bound $0\leq d_n\leq2^{-n-1}$ proves <uniform convergence> of the series by the <Weierstrass M-test>. Each summand is continuous and satisfies $d_n(x+1)=d_n(x)$, so its sum $t$, the <Takagi function>, is continuous and has period one.

For $n\geq m$, both $2^nu_m$ and $2^nv_m$ are integers, giving $d_n(u_m)=d_n(v_m)=0$. Therefore
$$
S_m:=\frac{t(v_m)-t(u_m)}{v_m-u_m}
=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{d_n(v_m)-d_n(u_m)}{v_m-u_m}.
$$
The corners of $d_n$ occur on the grid $2^{-n-1}\mathbb Z$. When $n<m$, that grid is contained in $2^{-m}\mathbb Z$, so the interior of $[u_m,v_m]$ contains no corner of $d_n$. Its slope on this interval is consequently $+1$ or $-1$.

The interval $[u_{m+1},v_{m+1}]$ is a half of $[u_m,v_m]$. All the old slopes persist on this half, and the new summand contributes one more slope in $\{\pm1\}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{S_{m+1}-S_m\in\{\pm1\}.}
$$
If $t$ had a finite <derivative> $L$ at $x_0$, write $t(y)=t(x_0)+L(y-x_0)+r(y)$ with $r(y)=o(|y-x_0|)$. Since $u_m\leq x_0<v_m$ and both endpoints tend to $x_0$,
$$
|S_m-L|\leq\frac{|r(v_m)|+|r(u_m)|}{v_m-u_m}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Indeed $|v_m-x_0|+|u_m-x_0|=v_m-u_m$; when $u_m=x_0$, its remainder is zero. This is the <secants straddling a differentiability point> argument, including dyadic $x_0$. But the displayed increments prevent $S_m$ from converging. Hence $\boxed{t\text{ is nowhere differentiable}}$, using periodicity to cover all real $x_0$.

For the <distributional derivative>, set $r_n(x)=s(2^nx)$, using $s$ from part (iii). The change of variable in the <distributional derivative> pairing gives $d_n'=r_n$; these are the <Rademacher functions>. <Uniform convergence> implies <weak convergence of distributions>, and the <continuity of distributional differentiation> allows termwise differentiation in that topology. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{t'=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_n\quad\text{in }\mathcal D'(\mathbb R),\qquad
\langle t',\phi\rangle=-\int_{\mathbb R}t(x)\phi'(x)\,dx.}
$$
This series assertion is a <distributional convergence> statement. Its remainder satisfies the concrete estimate
$$
\left|\left\langle t'-\sum_{n=0}^{N}r_n,\phi\right\rangle\right|
\leq2^{-N-1}\|\phi'\|_{L^1}\longrightarrow0.
$$
It supplies a well-defined <distributional derivative> even though the ordinary <derivative> exists nowhere.

**No.** Consider the continuous even <function>
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x=0,\\
1/\log(1/|x|),&0<|x|\leq e^{-1},\\
1,&|x|\geq e^{-1}.
\end{cases}
$$
The formulas agree at $|x|=e^{-1}$, and $f(x)\to0$ at zero. Part (i) defines its <even-weight principal-value distribution>. However,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{e^{-1}}\frac{|f(x)|}{x}\,dx
=\int_1^\infty\frac{ds}{s}=\infty,\qquad s=\log(1/x).}
$$
If this were a <regular distribution> represented by $g\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb R)$, testing on either side of zero would force $g=f/x$ <almost everywhere> there, by uniqueness of the <locally integrable function> representing a <regular distribution>. Changing the value at zero cannot remove the displayed divergence, contradicting <local integrability> of $g$.

More generally, the exact condition is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{pv}(f/x)\text{ is regular}\iff
\int_0^r\frac{|f(x)|}{x}\,dx<\infty\text{ for some }r>0.}
$$
Necessity is the preceding argument; sufficiency follows because $f/x$ is then a <locally integrable function> and its truncated integrals converge absolutely. The value $f(0)=0$ alone provides no such rate of decay.

Convergence $\phi_j\to\phi$ in the <space of test functions> means that all <supports> lie in one <compact set> $K\subset\mathbb R$ and $\|\phi_j^{(r)}-\phi^{(r)}\|_\infty\to0$ for every integer $r\geq0$. An eventual common <compact support> is equivalent, since finitely many initial <supports> can be added to $K$.

Write the standard <mollifier> as $\rho_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^{-1}\rho(x/\varepsilon)$, where $\rho\in\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$ is nonnegative, supported in $[-1,1]$, and has integral one. For $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, the <convolution> $\rho_\varepsilon*\phi$ has <support> in the fixed <compact set> $\operatorname{supp}\phi+[-1,1]$. The given <convolution derivative identity> yields
$$
(\rho_\varepsilon*\phi)^{(r)}(x)-\phi^{(r)}(x)
=\int_{-1}^{1}\rho(y)\bigl[\phi^{(r)}(x-\varepsilon y)-\phi^{(r)}(x)\bigr],dy.
$$
Each $\phi^{(r)}$ is uniformly continuous; more explicitly, the <mean value theorem> bounds the absolute value by $\varepsilon\|\phi^{(r+1)}\|_\infty\int|y|\rho(y)\,dy$, uniformly in $x$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\rho_\varepsilon*\phi\longrightarrow\phi\text{ in }\mathcal D(\mathbb R).}
$$

If $\phi_j\to0$ in the <space of test functions> and $K$ is a common <compact support>, then $\|\phi_j\|_{L^1}\leq|K|\|\phi_j\|_\infty\to0$, where $|K|$ is its <Lebesgue measure>.

The <mollifiers> themselves have no limit in the <L1 space>. If $\rho_\varepsilon\to g$ in that <norm>, then for each $\delta>0$ the integral of $|g|$ on $\{|x|>\delta\}$ is zero, because $\rho_\varepsilon$ vanishes there for $\varepsilon<\delta$. Hence $g=0$ <almost everywhere>. This contradicts $\|\rho_\varepsilon\|_{L^1}=1$. Therefore $\boxed{\rho_\varepsilon\text{ does not converge in }L^1(\mathbb R)}$.

The <weak convergence of distributions> $u_j\to u$ means
$$
\langle u_j,\phi\rangle\longrightarrow\langle u,\phi\rangle
\quad\text{for every }\phi\in\mathcal D(\mathbb R).
$$
For $\theta\in\mathcal D(\mathbb R)$, its <convolution> with a <distribution> is the <smooth function>
$$
(\theta*u)(x)=\langle u_y,\theta(x-y)\rangle.
$$
The subscript records the variable on which the <distribution> acts. With $\check\theta(x)=\theta(-x)$, its <regular distribution> satisfies the useful pairing identity
$$
\langle\theta*u,\phi\rangle=\langle u,\check\theta*\phi\rangle.
$$
Indeed integrating $\theta(x-y)\phi(x)$ in $x$ gives $(\check\theta*\phi)(y)$; continuity of the <distribution> justifies moving the pairing through that integral, by approximation with sums of <test functions> in one common <compact support>.

Part (i), applied to the reflected <mollifier>, gives $\check\rho_\varepsilon*\phi\to\phi$ in the <space of test functions>. Continuity of $u$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\langle\rho_\varepsilon*u,\phi\rangle
=\langle u,\check\rho_\varepsilon*\phi\rangle\longrightarrow\langle u,\phi\rangle,
\qquad\rho_\varepsilon*u\to u\text{ in }\mathcal D'.}
$$
Reflection is harmless even if the chosen standard <mollifier> is not even.

For the <mollifiers> themselves, a change of variable gives
$$
\langle\rho_\varepsilon,\phi\rangle=\int\rho(y)\phi(\varepsilon y)\,dy\longrightarrow\phi(0)\int\rho(y)\,dy=\phi(0).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\rho_\varepsilon\to\delta_0\text{ in }\mathcal D'(\mathbb R)}$, with limit the <Dirac delta distribution>, despite the failure of convergence in the <L1 space>.

The <L1-normalized dilation of a distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\langle u_\varepsilon,\phi\rangle=\langle u,\phi(\varepsilon\,\cdot)\rangle.}
$$
For a <regular distribution> this is the <function> $u_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^{-1}u(x/\varepsilon)$. In terms of the wiki's <dilation of a distribution> convention, $u_\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{-1}\delta_{1/\varepsilon}u$; the normalization preserves the <L1 space> norm.

If $u\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, the <dominated convergence theorem>, with dominating <integrable function> $|u(y)|\|\phi\|_\infty$, gives
$$
\langle u_\varepsilon,\phi\rangle=\int u(y)\phi(\varepsilon y)\,dy
\longrightarrow\phi(0)\int u(y)\,dy.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{u_\varepsilon\longrightarrow\left(\int_{\mathbb R}u\right)\delta_0
\quad\text{in }\mathcal D'(\mathbb R).}
$$
An arbitrary <distribution> need not have this behavior. Take the constant <regular distribution> $u=1$. Then $u_\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{-1}$, so for a <test function> with integral one,
$$
\langle u_\varepsilon,\phi\rangle=\varepsilon^{-1}\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$
Hence $\boxed{u=1\text{ gives no distributional limit}}$.

Using $(Rf)'=\int_0^1f-f$ and <integration by parts>, with $\phi$ a <test function>, gives
$$
\int_0^1Rf\,\phi'=\int_0^1f\phi-\left(\int_0^1f\right)\left(\int_0^1\phi\right).

For each $k\geq1$, apply the same identity to $R^kf$ and the <test function> $\phi^{(k)}$. Since $\int_0^1\phi^{(k)}=0$, it gives
$$
\int_0^1R^{k+1}f\,\phi^{(k+1)}=\int_0^1R^kf\,\phi^{(k)}.
$$
Starting from $k=1$ and iterating proves
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1f\phi-\left(\int_0^1f\right)\left(\int_0^1\phi\right)
=\int_0^1R^kf\,\phi^{(k)}\quad(k\geq1).}
$$
The intermediate means disappear because derivatives of a <test function> have zero integral; no assumption that $R^kf$ has zero mean is needed.

For the bound, write
$$
Rf(x)=\int_0^1\bigl(x-\mathbf1_{\{t<x\}}\bigr)f(t)\,dt.

The <triangle inequality> and <Fubini's theorem> imply
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Rf\|_{L^1}
&\leq\int_0^1|f(t)|\left[\int_0^t x\,dx+\int_t^1(1-x)\,dx\right]dt\\
&=\frac12\int_0^1\bigl[t^2+(1-t)^2\bigr]|f(t)|\,dt
\leq\frac12\|f\|_{L^1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $\boxed{c=\tfrac12\text{ is valid}}$, and every iterate $R^kf$ is in the <L1 space>.

To repeat <integration by parts> across whole periods, choose <periodic primitives with zero mean>. Set $h_0=f$, and recursively define
$$
h_{r+1}=Rh_r-\int_0^1Rh_r.
$$
Every $h_r$ has zero mean. Part (i) gives $h_{r+1}'=-h_r$ <almost everywhere>, and $h_{r+1}(0)=h_{r+1}(1)$ because $Rh_r$ vanishes at both endpoints. Its <periodic extension> is consequently locally an <absolutely continuous function>, with no jump at the integers. Part (ii) gives the uniform estimate
$$
\|h_{r+1}\|_{L^1}\leq2\|Rh_r\|_{L^1}\leq\|h_r\|_{L^1}\leq\|f\|_{L^1}.
$$
The <weak derivative> identity on the whole line is $D\overline h_{r+1}=-\overline h_r$. After scaling by the integer $j$ and repeating <integration by parts> $N$ times,
$$
\int_0^1\overline f(jx)\phi(x)\,dx
=j^{-N}\int_0^1\overline h_N(jx)\phi^{(N)}(x)\,dx.
$$
The <boundary terms> vanish because $\phi$ has <compact support> in $(0,1)$. The interval contains exactly $j$ periods, so
$$
\int_0^1|\overline h_N(jx)|\,dx=\int_0^1|h_N(t)|\,dt.
$$
Taking $N=m+1$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\left|j^m\int_0^1f_j\phi\right|
\leq j^{-1}\|f\|_{L^1}\|\phi^{(m+1)}\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This <rapid distributional decay of periodic oscillations> uses only <integrability> of $f$ and smoothness of the <test function>.

In Euclidean <open sets>, one can choose $J$ countable and the individual <supports> compact in their assigned cover members. Define two <open sets> by
$$
Z(u)=\bigcup\{U\subseteq\Omega:U\text{ open},\ u|_U=0\},\qquad
G(u)=\bigcup\{U\subseteq\Omega:U\text{ open},\ u|_U\in C^\infty(U)\}.
$$
The <support of a distribution> and its <singular support> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{supp}u=\Omega\setminus Z(u),\qquad
\operatorname{sing\,supp}u=\Omega\setminus G(u).}
$$
Both are relatively closed because the removed sets are open. The <locality of distributions> gives $u|_{Z(u)}=0$. On an overlap of two open smoothness regions, the two <smooth functions> represent the same <distribution>, so their difference is zero <almost everywhere> and hence everywhere by <continuity>. They therefore join to one <smooth function> on $G(u)$. The same <partition of unity> argument shows that it represents $u$ there.

For $u=\mathbf1_\omega$, openness of $\omega$ implies
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{supp}\mathbf1_\omega=\overline\omega^{\,\Omega}.}
$$
Indeed every neighborhood of a point in this relative closure meets $\omega$ in an <open set> containing a nonnegative <test function> with positive integral; outside the closure the <indicator function> is identically zero near the point.

The <singular support of an indicator function> needs a measure-sensitive boundary. For a <Lebesgue measurable set> $A\subseteq\Omega$, define its <essential closure> by
$$
\operatorname{esscl}_\Omega A
=\{x\in\Omega:|A\cap B_r(x)|>0\text{ whenever }r>0\text{ and }B_r(x)\subseteq\Omega\}.
$$
Then the general answer is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sing\,supp}\mathbf1_\omega
=\overline\omega^{\,\Omega}\cap\operatorname{esscl}_\Omega(\Omega\setminus\omega).}
$$
Outside this intersection, some ball has either no points of $\omega$ or a complement of <Lebesgue measure> zero. The <regular distribution> is respectively the constant zero or one on that ball and is smooth. Conversely, at a point of the intersection, every sufficiently small ball has positive <Lebesgue measure> in both sets. If a <smooth function> represented the <indicator function> there, its values would be in $\{0,1\}$ everywhere: any other value, by <continuity>, would give a ball of positive <Lebesgue measure> on which equality <almost everywhere> fails. On a connected ball such a continuous two-valued <function> is constant, contradicting the two positive measures.

In particular, the answer need not be the full topological <boundary>. For $\Omega=\mathbb R^n$ and $\omega=\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}$, the <indicator function> is the constant one <almost everywhere>, so its <singular support> is empty although the <boundary> is $\{0\}$. The full relative <boundary> is the answer when every neighborhood of each boundary point contains a positive <Lebesgue measure> portion of the complement, as for an <open set> with smooth boundary.

Let $u,v\in\mathcal D'(\mathbb R^n)$, with at least one a <compactly supported distribution>, so their <convolution> is defined. The <support and singular support rules for convolution> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{supp}(u*v)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}u+\operatorname{supp}v,\qquad
\operatorname{sing\,supp}(u*v)\subseteq

More generally, the same rules hold when addition is a <proper map> on $\operatorname{supp}u\times\operatorname{supp}v$, which is another sufficient condition for defining the <convolution>. The second inclusion explains why a compactly supported <smooth function> smooths every <distribution> under <convolution>.

Write $z=x+iy$, $r=|z|$, and $L=(4\pi)^{-1}\log(x^2+y^2)$. The <Wirtinger derivatives> satisfy $4\partial_{\bar z}\partial_z=\Delta$. Away from zero, ordinary differentiation gives
$$
4\partial_z L=2(\partial_x-i\partial_y)L
=\frac{x-iy}{\pi(x^2+y^2)}=\frac1{\pi z}.
$$
This equality also holds for <distributional derivatives>. To check that no <Dirac delta distribution> was lost at zero, integrate by parts outside the disk $r\leq\varepsilon$ against a <test function>. The new <boundary term> has absolute value at most $C\varepsilon|\log\varepsilon|\|\phi\|_\infty\to0$. Both $L$ and its displayed first <derivatives>, of size $O(r^{-1})$, are <locally integrable functions> in two dimensions, so the remaining integrals converge to their full-plane values.

Using the supplied <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation> identity $\Delta L=\delta_0$, and commutation of <distributional derivatives>, now gives
$$
\boxed{E=4\partial_zL=\frac1{\pi z},\qquad
\partial_{\bar z}E=4\partial_{\bar z}\partial_zL=\Delta L=\delta_0.}
$$
Thus the <Cauchy kernel fundamental solution> has exactly the required normalization.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $F$ is any other <fundamental solution>, then $H=F-E$ satisfies $\partial_{\bar z}H=0$ as a <distribution>. Applying $4\partial_z$ gives $\Delta H=0$. <Elliptic regularity> for the <Laplacian> implies that the initially arbitrary <distribution> $H$ is a <smooth function>. Its equation is then the ordinary <Cauchy-Riemann equation>, so $H$ is an <entire function>.

For a direct justification of the needed regularity, choose a radial <mollifier> $\eta_r$. Locally, $H*\eta_\varepsilon$ is a smooth <harmonic function>. On a ball with sufficient room for both radii, the <mean value property> gives $(H*\eta_\varepsilon)*\eta_r=H*\eta_\varepsilon$. Passing to the <weak convergence of distributions> limit $\varepsilon\to0$ yields $H*\eta_r=H$ on a smaller ball. The left side is smooth, proving the claim locally. This establishes the <distributional Cauchy-Riemann lemma> without assuming in advance that $H$ is a <locally integrable function>.

Conversely every <entire function> $h$ satisfies $\partial_{\bar z}h=0$ and can be added to $E$. Hence all <fundamental solutions> are
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\frac1{\pi z}+h(z),\qquad h\text{ any entire function}.}
$$
No growth condition at infinity was imposed, so $h$ need not be a <polynomial>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Because $f$ is a <compactly supported distribution>, the <convolution of distributions with a compactly supported factor> defines $E*f$. The <convolution derivative identity> and part (i) give
$$
\partial_{\bar z}(E*f)=(\partial_{\bar z}E)*f=\delta_0*f=f.
$$
The difference between any two solutions has zero $\partial_{\bar z}$ <distributional derivative>. Part (ii) therefore proves that the general solution of the <partial differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{u=\left(\frac1{\pi z}\right)*f+h,\qquad h\text{ any entire function}.}
$$
This formula is an identity of <distributions>; a pointwise integral is not required for arbitrary $f$.

If $u$ were a <smooth function>, its <Wirtinger derivative> $\partial_{\bar z}u=f$ would also be smooth, contradicting $\operatorname{sing\,supp}f\ne\varnothing$. In fact the <singular support> is exactly preserved here:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sing\,supp}u=\operatorname{sing\,supp}f.}
$$
One inclusion follows because a <differential operator> cannot create a singularity where $u$ is smooth. For the reverse inclusion, $E$ is smooth off zero, and its <singular support> is exactly $\{0\}$: if it were smooth near zero, $\partial_{\bar z}E=\delta_0$ would be smooth there. The <support and singular support rules for convolution> give $\operatorname{sing\,supp}(E*f)\subseteq\{0\}+\operatorname{sing\,supp}f$, and adding the smooth <entire function> $h$ leaves this <singular support> unchanged. Thus there is no globally smooth solution when the given <singular support> is nonempty.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Interchange the two <Wirtinger derivatives> in part (i). The same real <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation> $L$ gives
$$
4\partial_{\bar z}L=\frac{x+iy}{\pi(x^2+y^2)}=\frac1{\pi\bar z},\qquad
\partial_z(4\partial_{\bar z}L)=\Delta L=\delta_0.
$$
The calculation is valid for <distributions> by the same vanishing <boundary term> estimate. Any other <fundamental solution> differs by a <distribution> $H$ with $\partial_zH=0$. Then $\Delta H=0$, so the regularity argument of part (ii) makes $H$ smooth, and its <Cauchy-Riemann equations> make it an <antiholomorphic function>. On the whole plane it can be written $H(z)=h(\bar z)$ with $h$ an <entire function>. Conversely every such $H$ has $\partial_zH=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\frac1{\pi\bar z}+h(\bar z),\qquad h\text{ any entire function}.}
$$
