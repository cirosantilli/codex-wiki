= Solution

For the one-mole convention, **$b$ is the excluded <molar volume>**: finite molecular size reduces the volume available for translational motion from $V$ to $V-b$. It is an effective <excluded volume>, not simply the sum of the bare molecular volumes. **$a/V^2$ corrects for attractive <intermolecular forces>**, which reduce the <pressure> exerted on the wall. The attraction per molecule scales with <number density>, and the number of affected molecules also scales with <density>, producing the density-squared correction. The <van der Waals equation> consequently raises the measured <pressure> to the corresponding repulsive-gas value before multiplying by the available volume. Both corrections vanish relative to the ideal-gas terms at sufficiently low <density>.

Neglect ends and take the exterior as vacuum. With polar angle measured from the applied field, regularity inside and the far-field condition allow
$$
\phi_{\rm in}=-B E_0s\cos\theta,\qquad
\phi_{\rm out}=-E_0s\cos\theta+A E_0a^2\frac{\cos\theta}{s}.
$$
Continuity of potential gives $B=1-A$; continuity of normal electric <displacement>, with no free surface charge, gives $\epsilon_rB=1+A$. Hence the <transverse electrostatic field inside a dielectric cylinder> is uniform, parallel to the applied field, with magnitude
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm in}=\frac{2E_0}{1+\epsilon_r}.}
$$
This cylindrical result differs from the spherical depolarization factor. It returns $E_0$ for $\epsilon_r=1$ and tends to zero for large positive $\epsilon_r$.

Let $N$ be circumferential <tension>. A small arc subtending $d\theta$ has inward resultant $N\,d\theta$ and <mass> $\rho A r\,d\theta$. Its <centripetal acceleration> gives $N=\rho A\omega^2r^2$. Circumferential <stress> is $N/A$ and the <strain> is $\Delta(2\pi r)/(2\pi r)=\Delta r/r$. Thus the <centrifugal strain of a thin elastic hoop> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta r}{r}\simeq\frac{\rho\omega^2r^2}{E}.}
$$
Use the unstrained radius and cross-section at this order. The approximation requires $\rho\omega^2r^2/E\ll1$, a slender hoop and linear elastic response; the area cancels.

A <transmission line> stores electric and magnetic <energy> along its length, so <voltage> and current depend on both position and time. A distributed circuit uses <inductance> $L'$ and <capacitance> $C'$ per length, together with resistance $R'$ and leakage conductance $G'$. Applying <voltage> and charge balance to a short segment gives the <telegrapher's equations>,
$$
\partial_xV=-R'I-L'\partial_tI,\qquad
\partial_xI=-G'V-C'\partial_tV.
$$
For a lossless line, both variables obey <wave equations> with speed $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ and <characteristic impedance> $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. Forward and backward <waves> obey $I^+=V^+/Z_0$ and $I^-=-V^-/Z_0$. Matching a termination $Z_L$ gives the <voltage> <reflection coefficient>
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_L=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}.}
$$
A matched load absorbs without reflection; an open circuit has $\Gamma_L=1$, and a short has $\Gamma_L=-1$. Reflections can produce <standing waves>. A coaxial line confines the fields between conductors and, for a uniform <dielectric> TEM <wave>, has $v=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$. Finite loss attenuates <waves>; frequency-dependent losses or material properties can also distort pulses. **A long line is a wave-carrying distributed system, so it cannot generally be replaced by one lumped capacitor.**

= Solution

<Polarization density> $\mathbf P$ is <electric dipole moment> per unit volume. Displacing positive and negative <bound charges> leaves the bulk nearly neutral locally but can produce uncompensated charge where polarization varies or terminates. For a <dielectric> occupying a volume with outward unit normal $\mathbf n$,
$$
\boxed{\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\qquad \sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf n.}
$$
The <divergence theorem> makes their total sum zero for a body described entirely by bound dipoles. Uniform polarization gives no interior volume charge but generally gives opposite surface charges on different faces.

In a linear isotropic <dielectric>, $\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\epsilon\mathbf E$. <Gauss's law> becomes $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f$, separating free from <bound charge>. Across a static interface, tangential <electric field> is continuous, while the normal <displacement> jumps by the free surface charge. Polarization charges alter the internal field, as in the <dielectric> cylinder calculation. Molecular distortion and orientation contribute differently, and response can depend on <temperature> and frequency. **<Bound charge> is a redistribution of material charge, not an additional free-charge reservoir.**

{parent=B6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write <magnetization> as <magnetic dipole moment> per volume. In weak-field linear response, $\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)$.

**<Paramagnetism>** has positive susceptibility: permanent spin or orbital moments tend to align with a field against thermal disorder. Independent localized moments in the classical weak-field regime give $\chi\propto1/T$, the <Curie law>. Conduction-electron <Pauli paramagnetism> has a different origin and need not follow that <temperature> law.

**<Diamagnetism>** is the induced magnetic response opposing the applied field, giving negative susceptibility. Changes in orbital motion underlie this response; it exists even without permanent moments. It is often relatively weak and only weakly <temperature> dependent, although particular quantum systems have stronger or more complicated behaviour.

**<Ferromagnetism>** arises from interactions favouring parallel magnetic order. Below the <Curie temperature> a domain can have spontaneous <magnetization> even at zero applied field. A sample can nevertheless have nearly zero net moment if differently oriented domains cancel. Domain rearrangement and rotation produce a nonlinear <magnetization> curve, saturation, <remanence> and <coercivity>; <hysteresis> dissipates <energy> in a field cycle. Above the ordering <temperature> the spontaneous order disappears, and a Curie-Weiss susceptibility is a useful mean-field approximation. **The signs of weak-field susceptibility distinguish para- and <diamagnetism>; spontaneous order and domain dynamics distinguish <ferromagnetism> from independent-moment alignment.**

= Solution

Use a cold, unmagnetized, collisionless <electron> plasma with immobile ions. For an oscillating field proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$, the <electron> <displacement> follows $-m_e\omega^2\mathbf x=-e\mathbf E$. Hence $\mathbf P=-n_ee\mathbf x=-n_ee^2\mathbf E/(m_e\omega^2)$ and
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{n_ee^2}{\epsilon_0m_e}.}
$$
The induced polarization opposes the field because <electron> inertia changes the phase of its <displacement>. <Maxwell's equations> give transverse electromagnetic <dispersion>
$$
\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2.
$$
Thus <transverse waves> propagate above the <plasma frequency>, while below it the wavenumber is imaginary and a semi-infinite lossless plasma reflects an incident <wave> with an evanescent interior field. In the cold electrostatic model, a longitudinal <density> oscillation instead has frequency $\omega_p$.

Collisions at rate $\nu$ give $\epsilon_r=1-\omega_p^2/[\omega(\omega+i\nu)]$ for this time convention, introducing absorption. A background <magnetic field> makes the response anisotropic, and thermal motion supplies additional <dispersion> and static screening. The divergent zero-frequency cold collisionless formula should not be mistaken for a complete description of static Debye screening. **The simple <dielectric> function is a dynamical electron-inertia response under stated assumptions.**

= Solution
{parent=B7}

In microscopic SI form, <Maxwell's equations> couple charge <density> $\rho$ and current <density> $\mathbf J$ to electric and <magnetic fields>:
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
Gauss's electric law expresses electric flux from charge and reduces to the inverse-square Coulomb field for a point charge. Gauss's magnetic law gives zero net <magnetic flux> through every closed surface; <magnetic field> lines do not begin or end on magnetic monopoles in this theory.

Faraday's induction law says changing <magnetic flux> produces a circulating <electric field>. For a fixed loop, its circulation is minus the rate of change of enclosed <magnetic flux>, explaining the sign of induction and <Lenz's law>. For example, changing a primary coil's <electric current> changes the flux through a stationary secondary coil and induces a <voltage>. A moving wire also has the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> on its charges; the fixed-loop field equation and the full motional-emf calculation must be distinguished.

The Ampère-Maxwell law relates magnetic circulation to conduction current and <displacement current>. The latter is essential around a charging capacitor: a surface cutting the lead and another passing through the gap must predict the same circulation. Taking the divergence gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho=0,
$$
using Gauss's electric law. <Charge conservation> therefore fixes the consistency of the time-dependent equations. In matter, the equivalent macroscopic equations use $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf H$, free sources and constitutive relations. The <Lorentz force> <density> $\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B$ describes exchange of <momentum> with charges.

In source-free vacuum, take the curl of Faraday's law and use $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$. Both fields satisfy
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E,\qquad
\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf B,
\qquad c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.}
$$
For a <plane wave>, $\omega=ck$, $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\mathbf B=\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E/c$. The fields are transverse, mutually perpendicular and in phase. Their <energy density> and <energy flux> are
$$
u=\frac{\epsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},\qquad
\mathbf S=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{\mu_0},\qquad
\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E.
$$
This is <Poynting's theorem>: field <energy> is transported and can do <work> on matter. **<Electromagnetic radiation> is a propagating, energy-carrying solution of the coupled electric and <magnetic field> equations.**

Let a frame's origin have inertial acceleration $\mathbf a_0$ and its axes rotate with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega(t)$. For position $\mathbf r$ and <velocity> $\mathbf v'$ measured in that frame, Newton's law becomes
$$
m\mathbf a'=\mathbf F-m\mathbf a_0
-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'
-m\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r
-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
The extra terms are translational, Coriolis, Euler and centrifugal inertial <forces>. <Centrifugal force> points away from the rotation axis; <Coriolis force> deflects moving bodies sideways and does no instantaneous <work> on their relative motion, since it is perpendicular to $\mathbf v'$. The Euler term appears when the rotation rate or axis changes.

For constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$, <centrifugal force> has potential $U_{\rm cf}=-m|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2$. It can be combined with a conservative real potential to find rotating-frame equilibria. The Coriolis term remains <velocity> dependent. These <forces> arise from describing acceleration in a noninertial frame; they are not additional interactions between bodies. They are essential for trajectories on rotating apparatus and for large-scale terrestrial motions. **Frame-dependent terms must be included consistently with the chosen coordinates.**

= Solution

A small <displacement> $\mathbf u$ in a homogeneous isotropic elastic solid obeys <linear elasticity>. With Lamé constants $\lambda,\mu$ and <mass> <density> $\rho$, <momentum> balance gives
$$
\rho\partial_t^2\mathbf u=(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u.
$$
A longitudinal <plane wave> has <displacement> parallel to its propagation direction and changes local volume. A <transverse wave> has perpendicular <displacement> and produces shear. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{c_L=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho}},\qquad
c_T=\sqrt{\frac\mu\rho}.}
$$
An ideal fluid has no static <shear modulus> and cannot support this bulk transverse <elastic wave>. A thin rod with free lateral surfaces instead supports an extensional long-wavelength mode with speed $\sqrt{E/\rho}$; its transverse contraction changes the effective stiffness, so it should not be identified with the unbounded-solid longitudinal mode.

<Stress> and <displacement> boundary conditions produce reflection, transmission, mode conversion and guided <waves>. <Viscoelasticity> and scattering can attenuate <waves>; finite geometry can make guided modes dispersive even when the bulk elastic constants are frequency independent. **<Wave> speed depends on both material stiffness and the allowed deformation.**

Choose body-fixed <principal axes> through a fixed point or the centre of <mass>, with principal moments $I_1,I_2,I_3$. <Angular momentum> has components $L_i=I_i\omega_i$. The inertial <derivative> is the body <derivative> plus $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L$, so <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> read
$$
\boxed{I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=\tau_1,\quad
I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1=\tau_2,\quad
I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2=\tau_3.}
$$
They avoid differentiating the rotating <inertia tensor> in laboratory coordinates. <Torques> must refer to the chosen point, and the displayed fixed moments require a <rigid body>.

For zero <torque>, laboratory <angular momentum> is constant, while its components can change in the body frame. Both $L^2$ and rotational <kinetic energy> $T=\tfrac12\sum I_i\omega_i^2$ are conserved. The intersections of their constant-value surfaces constrain the motion. For distinct $I_1<I_2<I_3$, steady rotation about either extreme <principal axis> is stable against small perturbations, whereas rotation about the intermediate axis is unstable. For example perturbing rotation $\Omega$ about axis 2 gives
$$
\ddot{\delta\omega_1}=
\Omega^2\frac{(I_2-I_3)(I_1-I_2)}{I_1I_3}\,\delta\omega_1,
$$
whose coefficient is positive. A symmetric body has additional simplifications and can precess. **Conservation of laboratory <angular momentum> does not imply constant body-frame <angular velocity>.**

{parent=B8}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For <conservative forces> and ideal constraints that do no <work>, <mechanical energy> $E=T+U$ is constant. Work-energy balance remains useful with nonconservative <forces>: their power gives $dE/dt$. In one dimension,
$$
\tfrac12m\dot q^2+U(q)=E
$$
locates accessible regions and turning points, and reduces the trajectory to a quadrature $dt=dq/\sqrt{2(E-U)/m}$. A local minimum of potential is a stable equilibrium; a local maximum is unstable. Near a nondegenerate minimum, $U=U_0+K(q-q_0)^2/2+\cdots$ gives $\omega^2=K/m$.

For a <central force>, conserved <angular momentum> reduces radial motion to an <effective potential> $U_{\rm eff}=U(r)+L^2/(2mr^2)$. Its extrema determine circular orbits and their radial stability. In several coordinates, <small oscillations> follow from the kinetic-energy <mass matrix> $M$ and potential Hessian $K$ through $\det(K-\omega^2M)=0$.

<Energy> is also central to the <Lagrangian> $\mathcal L=T-U$ and the <Euler-Lagrange equations>. A time-independent natural mechanical system has conserved <energy>; <cyclic coordinates> yield additional momenta. <Energy> alone does not normally determine every coordinate of a multi-degree-of-freedom motion. **Its value is to expose allowed motion, stability and reductions before solving the full differential equations.**

A <velocity field> $\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)$ assigns a <velocity> to each point. Following a fluid parcel rather than holding position fixed gives the material or convective <derivative>
$$
\boxed{\frac D{Dt}=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla.}
$$
The convective term can accelerate fluid even in a steady flow, for example as a stream narrows. Conservation of <mass> is $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0$; constant-density <incompressible flow> has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$.

For an incompressible <Newtonian fluid> of constant <density> and <viscosity>, with conservative body-force potential per <mass> $\Phi$, <momentum> balance is
$$
\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\frac{\nabla p}{\rho}-\nabla\Phi+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u.
$$
Neglect <viscosity> and assume steady flow. Dotting with an element along a streamline, or using $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(u^2/2)-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$, gives
$$
\boxed{\frac p\rho+\frac{u^2}{2}+\Phi=B\quad\text{along a streamline}.}
$$
This is Bernoulli's equation: <pressure> <work>, <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy> interconvert. In a connected irrotational region under the same assumptions, $B$ is the same for all streamlines. In a rotational flow it can vary between them. Viscous dissipation, pumps or unsteady motion require the corresponding modified balance; the steady inviscid expression cannot be applied indiscriminately.

<Vorticity> $\boldsymbol\zeta=\nabla\times\mathbf u$ measures local fluid rotation. For constant-density <incompressible flow> it evolves by
$$
\frac{D\boldsymbol\zeta}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\zeta\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\zeta,
$$
showing vortex stretching and viscous diffusion. A <potential flow> has $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$ locally, so irrotational incompressible motion satisfies $\nabla^2\phi=0$. Boundary conditions then determine flows around simple shapes. A multiply connected region can have circulation even when its local <vorticity> vanishes; ideal <potential flow> also misses no-slip <boundary layers> and viscous drag.

For a <Newtonian fluid>, <shear stress> is proportional to <strain> rate. No-slip at solid walls produces <velocity> gradients and dissipation. Fully developed laminar pipe flow, for example, has
$$
u_z(s)=\frac{\Delta p}{4\mu\ell}(R^2-s^2),\qquad
Q=\frac{\pi R^4\Delta p}{8\mu\ell}.
$$
The <Reynolds number> $\operatorname{Re}=UL/\nu$ compares inertial and viscous terms. Small values favour creeping-flow approximations; large values can permit thin <boundary layers> and <turbulence>, without guaranteeing <turbulence> in every geometry. **The appropriate flow model follows from conservation laws, constitutive properties, boundaries and scale comparisons.**

= Solution

At coexistence, the two phases have equal <chemical potential>. For a single component, $d\mu=-s\,dT+v\,dp$, with <entropy> and volume measured on the same molar or <mass> basis. Equating the phase <derivatives> along coexistence gives $\Delta v\,dp=\Delta s\,dT$. Reversible <latent heat> satisfies $L=T\Delta s$, hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{L}{T\Delta v}.}
$$
Here $p$ and $T$ are coexistence <pressure> and absolute <temperature>, $L$ is <latent heat> for transforming phase 1 to phase 2, and $\Delta v=v_2-v_1$ uses the same amount of material as $L$.

For vaporization, neglect liquid <molar volume> compared with ideal-vapour volume $RT/p$. The ideal-vapour Clausius-Clapeyron approximation becomes
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{pL_e}{RT^2},\qquad
\ln\frac p{p_b}=-\frac{L_e}{R}\left(\frac1T-\frac1{T_b}\right).
$$
Using the normal boiling reference $T_b=373.15\,\mathrm K$, $p_b=1.01325\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and the supplied constant $L_e$ gives
$$
\boxed{p(273\,\mathrm K)\simeq8.2\times10^2\,\mathrm{Pa}.}
$$
This deliberately constant-latent-heat extrapolation does not reproduce the supplied triple-point <pressure> exactly. It predicts the liquid-vapour branch, metastable against ice below the triple-point <temperature>; it is not a precision sublimation-pressure prediction.

The <phase diagram> has solid, liquid and vapour regions meeting at the <triple point>. The <sublimation> and vaporization boundaries have positive slopes, whereas the ordinary ice–water boundary slopes negatively because ice has larger specific volume. The liquid–vapour line terminates at a <critical point>; the original schematic below emphasizes topology and marks the supplied <triple point>.

\Image[paper-2-water-phase.png]
{title=Schematic pressure–temperature phase topology of ordinary water, with negative melting slope and the supplied triple point}

For the melting-pressure estimate, <Archimedes' principle> gives $\rho_i=0.90\rho_w=900\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, so
$$
\Delta v=v_w-v_i=\frac1{1000}-\frac1{900}
=-1.111\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}}.
$$
Convert the molar evaporation <heat> using water <molar mass> $M\simeq0.0180\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$, rounded from the tabulated value $18.0153\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$.<NIST Chemistry WebBook, SRD 69>[Water entry, CAS 7732-18-5, molecular weight 18.0153]{ref} An <enthalpy> cycle through vapour then gives
$$
L_f=L_s-\frac{L_e}{M}
\simeq2.8\times10^6-\frac{40700}{0.0180}
\simeq5.39\times10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}.
$$
This uses the question's temperature-independent evaporation <heat> all the way to the <triple point>; no replacement experimental fusion <heat> is inserted. The <melting pressure inferred from flotation and latent heats>, on holding $L_f$ and $\Delta v$ constant, is
$$
\boxed{p-p_t=\frac{L_f}{\Delta v}\ln\frac T{T_t}
\simeq5.37\times10^7\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
p\simeq54\,\mathrm{MPa},}
$$
using $T_t=273.16\,\mathrm K$ and $T=270.15\,\mathrm K$. Linearizing the logarithm about the <triple point> gives $53.4\,\mathrm{MPa}$ instead of $53.7\,\mathrm{MPa}$. Both are estimates within the stated constant-property approximation; the negative melting slope makes a positive <pressure> necessary to lower the melting <temperature>.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

**Capacity convention.** The printed equation requires $c$ to be <volumetric heat capacity>, in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$. If “<specific heat capacity>” means per unit <mass> instead, the storage term is $\rho_m c\,\partial_tT$ and both denominators in the printed equation must contain $\rho_m c$. This distinction is fixed by dimensions.

For a fixed material region $V$, conservation of thermal <energy> gives the <heat balance with volumetric heat generation>,
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_VcT\,dV
=-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf n\,dS+\int_VH\,dV.
$$
Use the <divergence theorem>, $\mathbf J=-\kappa\nabla T$ and constant coefficients. Since the region is arbitrary,
$$
\boxed{c\partial_tT=\kappa\nabla^2T+H,\qquad
\partial_tT=\frac\kappa c\nabla^2T+\frac Hc.}
$$
The <heat> source is power per volume, while $\kappa$ is <thermal conductivity> rather than <thermal diffusivity>.

For uniform <electrical conductivity> and imposed current, current <density> is $j=I/(\pi a^2)$ and <Joule heating> is $H=j^2/\sigma$. A long, steady, cylindrically symmetric wire therefore satisfies
$$
\frac1s\frac d{ds}\left(s\frac{dT}{ds}\right)=-\frac H\kappa.
$$
Integration gives $T'=-Hs/(2\kappa)+C/s$. Regularity at $s=0$ makes $C=0$, and the surface condition fixes the remaining constant. Thus the <Joule-heated cylindrical wire> has
$$
\boxed{T(s)=T_0+\frac{I^2}{4\pi^2a^4\kappa\sigma}(a^2-s^2).}
$$
Here $s$ denotes the printed radial coordinate $\rho$ and avoids confusing it with <mass> <density>. The centre is hotter by $I^2/(4\pi^2a^2\kappa\sigma)$.

For the air boundary, the internal flux at $s=a$ is $-\kappa T'(a)=Ha/2$. Equate this to $\alpha[T(a)-T_{\rm air}]$. The <Newton cooling of a Joule-heated wire> gives
$$
\boxed{T(a)=T_{\rm air}+\frac{Ha}{2\alpha}
=T_{\rm air}+\frac{I^2}{2\pi^2a^3\sigma\alpha}.}
$$
Multiplication by circumference $2\pi a$ verifies that surface loss per length equals $I^2/(\pi a^2\sigma)$, the electrical power per length. The full air-cooled profile uses this surface <temperature> plus the same parabolic interior excess, assuming constant material properties and negligible end losses.

= Solution
{parent=C12}

A <heat engine>'s <thermal efficiency> is net <work> out divided by <heat> absorbed,
$$
\boxed{\eta=\frac{W_{\rm out}}{Q_{\rm in}}=1-\frac{Q_{\rm out}}{Q_{\rm in}},}
$$
where both <heat> quantities are positive magnitudes. The last equality follows from the first law over a complete cycle.

Use $n$ moles of an <ideal gas> with constant molar $C_V,C_p$ and $\gamma=C_p/C_V>1$. For the <Otto cycle>, reversible <adiabats> give $T_2=T_1r^{\gamma-1}$ and $T_3/T_4=r^{\gamma-1}$. Isochoric <heat> input and rejection are $Q_{\rm in}=nC_V(T_3-T_2)$ and $Q_{\rm out}=nC_V(T_4-T_1)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\eta_O=1-\frac{T_4-T_1}{T_3-T_2}=1-r^{1-\gamma}.}
$$
The denominator's <temperature> difference is $r^{\gamma-1}$ times the numerator's difference.

For the ideal <Atkinson cycle>, heating at fixed volume gives $T_3/T_2=r_p$. The longer expansion ends when $p_{4'}=p_1$. Combining the adiabatic <pressure>–<temperature> relation with $p_2/p_1=r^\gamma$ yields
$$
\boxed{T_2=T_1r^{\gamma-1},\qquad
T_3=T_1r_p r^{\gamma-1},\qquad
T_{4'}=T_3\left(\frac{p_1}{p_3}\right)^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}
=T_1r_p^{1/\gamma}.}
$$
Also $V_{4'}=V_1r_p^{1/\gamma}>V_1$ for $r_p>1$. The PDF asks explicitly for $T_3,T_{4'}$; the transcription additionally names $T_2$, which is supplied above too.

<Heat> input remains $nC_VT_1r^{\gamma-1}(r_p-1)$, while the isobaric return rejects $nC_pT_1(r_p^{1/\gamma}-1)$. Hence the <Atkinson efficiency advantage from extended expansion> is quantified by
$$
\boxed{\eta_A=1-\gamma r^{1-\gamma}
\frac{r_p^{1/\gamma}-1}{r_p-1}.}
$$
Compare cycles with the same initial state, compression ratio and <heat> input. The extra expansion and constant-pressure return enclose positive extra <work>,
$$
\Delta W=\int_{V_1}^{V_{4'}}[p_{\rm expansion}(V)-p_1]\,dV>0.
$$
It uses <heat> that the <Otto cycle> would reject, while $Q_{\rm in}$ is unchanged. Equivalently, <concavity> gives $\gamma(r_p^{1/\gamma}-1)<r_p-1$, so **$\eta_A>\eta_O$ for $r_p>1$ under this matched comparison**. This is an ideal-cycle result; the question's named real-engine application is not needed to establish it.

= Solution
{parent=C13}

For a simple compressible one-component equilibrium system, the fundamental thermodynamic relation is
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN.
$$
The <internal energy> has natural variables $(S,V,N)$: its <derivatives> give $T$, $-p$ and <chemical potential> $\mu$. The <natural variables of a thermodynamic potential> identify the independent arguments whose conjugates appear directly in a potential's differential. A <Legendre transform> exchanges a variable for its conjugate, giving potentials suited to different controlled conditions:

\Table[
\Tr[\Th[potential]\Th[definition]\Th[natural variables]\Th[differential]]
\Tr[\Td[<internal energy>]\Td[$U$]\Td[$S,V,N$]\Td[$dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN$]]
\Tr[\Td[<enthalpy>]\Td[$H=U+pV$]\Td[$S,p,N$]\Td[$dH=T\,dS+V\,dp+\mu\,dN$]]
\Tr[\Td[<Helmholtz free energy>]\Td[$F=U-TS$]\Td[$T,V,N$]\Td[$dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV+\mu\,dN$]]
\Tr[\Td[<Gibbs free energy>]\Td[$G=U+pV-TS$]\Td[$T,p,N$]\Td[$dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp+\mu\,dN$]]
]

For fixed composition and the appropriate closed-system constraints, equilibrium minimizes $U$ at fixed $S,V$, $H$ at fixed $S,p$, $F$ at fixed $T,V$, or $G$ at fixed $T,p$, with other allowable internal variations understood. <Enthalpy> is useful for constant-pressure <heat> exchange with only <pressure>–volume <work>. <Helmholtz free energy> is suited to fixed-temperature, fixed-volume systems, and <Gibbs free energy> to fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure phase or chemical equilibrium. Coexisting phases of one substance have equal <chemical potentials>.

Where a potential is twice continuously differentiable, equality of mixed <derivatives> gives <Maxwell relations>. At fixed $N$, the four usual forms are
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S
=-\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial S}\right)_V,
\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_S
=\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_p,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T
=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V,
\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial p}\right)_T
=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.}
$$
The fixed subscripts and signs follow from the chosen natural variables. <Derivatives> through a nonsmooth phase boundary require separate phase descriptions rather than automatic use of the smooth single-phase formulas.

As an application, the Helmholtz relation turns <pressure> data into an isothermal <entropy> change. For an <ideal gas>, $(\partial p/\partial T)_V=nR/V$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{T}=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_VdV
=nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}.}
$$
Combining the same relation with $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$ yields $(\partial U/\partial V)_T=T(\partial p/\partial T)_V-p$, which vanishes for an <ideal gas> and reveals attractive-energy effects for a van der Waals gas. **<Thermodynamic potentials> organize equilibrium constraints, while <Maxwell relations> convert difficult <entropy> <derivatives> into measurable response <derivatives>.**
