= Solution
{parent=A1}

Use absolute <thermodynamic temperatures> and assume a constant-parameter <Arrhenius equation>, with composition held fixed so that <reaction rate> is proportional to the <rate constant>. Write $R(T)=A e^{-\beta/T}$, where $\beta=E_a/\mathcal R$. <two-temperature Arrhenius extrapolation> eliminates the unknown <Arrhenius prefactor> and gives
$$
\beta=\frac{\ln10}{1/273.15-1/293.15}=9218.85\,\mathrm K,\qquad
\ln\frac{R(313.15)}{R_0}=\ln10\,\frac{1/273.15-1/313.15}{1/273.15-1/293.15}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{R(40\,{}^\circ\mathrm C)=10^{2(293.15)/(313.15)}R_0\simeq74.5R_0.}
$$
A tenfold increase for one $20\,\mathrm K$ interval does not imply another tenfold increase for the next interval: the <Arrhenius plot> is linear in inverse <temperature>. Two measured rates alone do not specify a unique extrapolation without a <rate law> for its <temperature> dependence; the result above states that modelling assumption explicitly.

Choose the plate polarity so that the gap <electric field> points along $+z$ when $V>0$. Away from the plate edges, the <quasistatic electromagnetic approximation> gives $E_z=V_0\sin\omega t/d$. A circular <Ampere-Maxwell law> contour of <radius> $r<a$ then gives
$$
2\pi r B_\phi=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^r E_z\,2\pi s\,ds,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf B(r,t)=\frac{\mu_0\epsilon_0\omega V_0}{2d}\,r\cos\omega t\,\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}\quad(0\leq r<a).}
$$
It is <displacement current>, rather than a <conduction current> across the air, that supplies the enclosed current term. The direction follows the <right-hand rule> for increasing positive <electric flux>; it reverses every half cycle. In the ideal extension that sets the gap <electric field> to zero outside the plate footprint, $B_\phi=\mu_0\epsilon_0\omega V_0a^2\cos\omega t/(2dr)$ for $r>a$. That extension is an edge approximation, not an exact exterior solution for finite plates. Neglect <fringing field of a capacitor> and retardation, requiring $d\ll a$ and $\omega a/c\ll1$.

For a hollow, air-filled <rectangular electromagnetic waveguide> with conducting walls, transverse confinement gives
$$
f_{mn}=\frac c2\sqrt{\left(\frac m w\right)^2+\left(\frac n h\right)^2}.
$$
A <transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide> permits one zero index, but not two; a <transverse magnetic mode of a rectangular waveguide> requires both indices positive. Take $w=20\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $h=10\,\mathrm{mm}$. The dominant mode is $\mathrm{TE}_{10}$, with
$$
\boxed{f_c=\frac{c}{2w}=7.49\,\mathrm{GHz}\simeq7.5\,\mathrm{GHz}.}
$$
The <waveguide cutoff frequency> is the limiting value: positive axial <group velocity> and propagating power require $f>f_c$. There is no <transverse electromagnetic mode> in this single hollow conducting pipe; otherwise a zero cutoff would incorrectly follow.

The <Stefan–Boltzmann law> gives solar <luminosity> $L_\odot=4\pi R_\odot^2\sigma T_\odot^4$. At orbital distance $r$, the incident <irradiance> is $\sigma T_\odot^4R_\odot^2/r^2$. A spherical absorber intercepts a disc of <area> $\pi R_M^2$ but radiates over $4\pi R_M^2$. Thus the uniform-temperature <radiative equilibrium> estimate is
$$
\pi R_M^2\sigma T_\odot^4\frac{R_\odot^2}{r^2}=4\pi R_M^2\sigma T_M^4,
\qquad
\boxed{T_M=T_\odot\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot}{2r}}\simeq245\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The planetary <radius> cancels. This is a <black-body radiation> estimate with zero reflected fraction and complete redistribution of absorbed <heat>. More generally the balance fixes $\langle T^4\rangle^{1/4}$, rather than the arithmetic mean of a nonuniform surface <temperature>. It excludes atmospheric heating and thermal lag; no measured surface <temperature> is inferred.

For the <inertia tensor of a uniform cube>, reflection <symmetry> makes every off-diagonal element of the central <inertia tensor> vanish. Permutation of the three axes makes its diagonal elements equal. Since $M=8\rho a^3$ and the mean of each squared coordinate in the <cube> is $a^2/3$,
$$
I_{xx}=\int(y^2+z^2)\,dm=\frac23Ma^2,\qquad
\mathsf I=\frac23Ma^2\mathsf1.
$$
Every central axis is therefore a <principal axis>, including the body diagonal. The <angular momentum> is parallel to the <angular velocity>, and its magnitude is
$$
\boxed{J=\frac{16}{3}\rho a^5\omega.}

= Solution
{parent=B6}

In a charge-free region of homogeneous <permittivity>, <Gauss's law> and $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ give <Laplace's equation>, $\nabla^2V=0$. Prescribed surface <electric potentials> specify a <Dirichlet problem>. Its <Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> permits replacing conductors by fictitious charges outside the physical solution region: a harmonic candidate with the same <boundary conditions> is the physical solution. For an exterior problem its behaviour at infinity must also be prescribed. For a proof, subtract two candidate potentials to obtain $u$ with zero boundary values. <Green's first identity> gives $\int|\nabla u|^2d^3x=0$, so $u$ is constant and its boundary value fixes it to zero. Conditions on a normal <electric field> instead determine a potential only up to an additive constant when the usual compatibility condition holds.

A cylindrical <Gaussian surface> around an infinite <line charge> gives $E_r=\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$. The <electric potential difference> is therefore
$$
V(r_2)-V(r_1)=-\int_{r_1}^{r_2}E_r\,dr
=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{r_1}{r_2}.
$$
An isolated infinite <line charge> has no finite potential zero at infinity. For the opposite pair, however, the logarithmic growth terms cancel and one can choose $V\to0$ there. Define $r_b^2=(x-b)^2+y^2$ and $r_c^2=(x-c)^2+y^2$. <superposition> gives
$$
V(x,y)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{r_c}{r_b}.
$$
On $x^2+y^2=bc$,
$$
r_c^2=c(b+c-2x),\qquad r_b^2=b(b+c-2x),
\qquad
\boxed{V=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac cb.}
$$
Thus the circle, and its cylindrical extension, is an <equipotential>. This is an <Apollonius circle>: a constant ratio of distances gives a circle rather than a constant distance from either charge.

For the cylinder above the plane, call its centre height $H>a$ and set $s=\sqrt{H^2-a^2}$. The <cylinder-plane image charges> are $+\lambda$ at height $s$ and $-\lambda$ at height $-s$. Their distances are equal on the grounded plane, while their distance ratio is $(H+s)/a$ on the circular surface. The positive fictitious line lies inside the metal, so the exterior is charge-free and <Uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> applies. A <Gaussian surface> enclosing the physical cylinder confirms that its <charge per unit length> is indeed $\lambda$. Its <voltage> is
$$
V_c=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\operatorname{arcosh}\frac Ha.
$$
The physical field <energy> per unit <length> at fixed cylinder charge is $U'=\lambda V_c/2$. Differentiate with respect to height, keeping $a$ and $\lambda$ fixed:
$$
\frac{F_z}{\ell}=-\frac{\partial U'}{\partial H}
=-\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{H^2-a^2}},
\qquad
\boxed{\left.\frac{\mathbf F}{\ell}\right|_{H=2a}
=-\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt3\,a}\,\widehat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The <electrostatic attraction per unit length of a cylinder above a plane> is downward for either sign of charge. Placing the fictitious line at the cylinder centre would give the wrong answer at this separation. The long-cylinder approximation neglects the ends.

For fixed, magnetically linear geometry, <self-inductance> relates a coil's own <magnetic flux linkage> to its <electric current>, $\Lambda_i=L_iI_i$. <mutual inductance> relates the linkage in one coil to the current in the other, $\Lambda_{21}=MI_1$. These coefficients enter the induced <electromotive forces> through $\mathcal E_i=-d\Lambda_i/dt$; reversing a chosen winding orientation reverses the sign assigned to $M$.

In the ideal confined-field <toroid>, a circular <magnetostatic Ampère law> contour encloses $N_1I$, so
$$
\boxed{H_\phi(r)=\frac{N_1I}{2\pi r}\quad(a<r<b).}
$$
Write $w=b-a$ for the height of the square cross-section. The <inductance of a rectangular-section toroid> follows by integrating the <magnetic flux density> over that cross-section; it cannot be replaced by its value at an arbitrary mean <radius>:
$$
\Phi=w\int_a^b\mu_0\mu_rH_\phi(r)\,dr
=\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_1Iw}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba.
$$
Consequently, for winding normals chosen consistently,
$$
\boxed{L_i=\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_i^2(b-a)}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba,\qquad
M_0=\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_1N_2(b-a)}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba=\sqrt{L_1L_2}.}
$$
The equality means complete shared <magnetic flux linkage>; with leakage one instead has $|M|<\sqrt{L_1L_2}$.

For the crack, use the <radial-path approximation for a cracked toroid>, with an air <length> $d$ on each path and core <length> $2\pi r-d$. Continuity of the normal <magnetic flux density> at a crack face gives $H_g=\mu_rH_c$. Hence
$$
N_1I=\frac{B(r)}{\mu_0\mu_r}(2\pi r-d)+\frac{B(r)}{\mu_0}d,
\qquad
B(r)\simeq\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_1I}{2\pi r+(\mu_r-1)d}.
$$
Integrating the radial paths yields
$$
M_d\simeq\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_1N_2w}{2\pi}
\ln\frac{b+q}{a+q},\qquad q=\frac{(\mu_r-1)d}{2\pi},
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{M_d-M_0}{M_0}\simeq
\frac{\ln[(b+q)/(a+q)]}{\ln(b/a)}-1=-0.529.}
$$
Here $q=1.5915\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $M_d/M_0=0.47079$: **about a 53% reduction**. The small ratio $d/a$ does not justify expanding in $\mu_rd/a$, which is about ten. The crack's low <magnetic permeability> gives appreciable <magnetic reluctance>. This is an estimate within a confined-flux model; actual redistribution near the slit is outside its accuracy. Narrow-gap circuit models assume small gap width relative to transverse dimensions and approximately directed flux.<Electromagnetic Fields and Energy by Hermann A. Haus and James R. Melcher/Magnetic Circuits>[§9.7, Example 9.7.1 and the paragraph after it: air-gap circuit approximation and need for a more general field solution]{ref}

For the intact ring, ideal poloidal winding currents and the no-leakage boundary condition admit $\mathbf H=N_1I\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}/(2\pi r)$ with zero radial and vertical components. It is curl-free inside the current-free core, divergence-free for uniform <magnetic permeability>, tangent to the core boundary, and has the required <circulation>; <uniqueness of the Neumann problem> removes an additional homogeneous scalar-potential field. This explains zero non-azimuthal components in the stipulated intact-ring idealization. A literal slit breaks that rotational <symmetry>, so **zero transverse components everywhere is not exact for the cracked ring**: <magnetic fringing> and flux redistribution occur. In fact, extending the approximate $B(r)\propto1/(r+q)$ unchanged around a current-free core would give $[\nabla\times\mathbf H]_z\ne0$. Thus that radial-path calculation and a claim of exact purely azimuthal field in the cracked geometry cannot both be exact. The numerical answer above retains the intended circuit approximation while making its limitation explicit.

For a steady thin-wire <electric current>, the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
d\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}
\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3},
$$
where the source element follows positive current. For a circular loop, source points are $(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,0)$ and the observer is $(0,0,z)$. Their <cross product> with $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ has axial component $R^2d\phi$; transverse components integrate to zero. Thus the <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop>, multiplied by the number of coincident turns, is
$$
\boxed{B_0(z)=B_z(0,z)=\frac{\mu_0N_1I_1R^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
Take positive current counterclockwise as seen from $+z$. <magnetic field lines> pass upward through the loop and return around its exterior, closing rather than terminating. They fan outward above its centre and converge below it. The original numerical <Biot-Savart law> integration below illustrates these directions; the wire singularities are masked.

\Image[paper-1-coil-field.png]
{title=Original circular-loop <magnetic field lines> in a meridional cross-section}

The <axisymmetric magnetic field near a regular axis> follows from <Gauss's law for magnetism>. In <cylindrical coordinates>, regularity gives $B_\rho(0,z)=0$, while <symmetry> gives $B_z(\rho,z)=B_0(z)+O(\rho^2)$. Integrate $\partial_\rho(\rho B_\rho)=-\rho B_0'(z)$ from the axis:
$$
B_\rho(\rho,z)=-\frac\rho2 B_0'(z)+O(\rho^3),
\qquad
\boxed{B_\rho(\rho,z)\simeq
\frac{3\mu_0N_1I_1R^2\rho z}{4(R^2+z^2)^{5/2}}.}
$$
A formal $C(z)/\rho$ term is excluded by regularity. For $z>0$ the axial field decreases upward, so the radial component is outward, consistent with the sketch.

On the small receiving coil, $d\boldsymbol\ell=a\,d\phi\,\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. The <Lorentz force> $I_2d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ has axial component $-I_2a B_\rho(a,d)d\phi$. Contributions from the axial field cancel radially around the loop. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F_z\simeq-2\pi N_2I_2aB_\rho(a,d)
=-\frac{3\pi\mu_0N_1N_2I_1I_2R^2a^2d}{2(R^2+d^2)^{5/2}}.}
$$
With both currents giving <magnetic dipole moments> along $+z$ and $d>0$, this is attraction. Reversing one current reverses the <force>. The small coil has $m_2=N_2I_2\pi a^2$, so the result is exactly the leading <magnetic dipole force> $F_z=m_2B_0'(d)$. Equivalently $U=-\mathbf m_2\cdot\mathbf B_0$. The approximation requires $a\ll R$ and negligible variation over the receiving coil; corrections begin at relative order $a^2/(R^2+d^2)$.

Use peak-amplitude <phasors> with time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ and $\mu=\mu_0$. <Faraday's law of induction> and the <Ampere-Maxwell law> give
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=i\omega\mu_0\mathbf H,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=(\sigma-i\omega\epsilon)\mathbf E,
\qquad k^2=\mu_0\epsilon\omega^2+i\mu_0\sigma\omega.
$$
In the good-conductor limit $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$, take the root with positive attenuation into the slab:
$$
k\simeq\frac{1+i}{\delta},\qquad \delta=\sqrt{\frac2{\mu_0\sigma\omega}},
\qquad
\boxed{\widetilde E_y(x)=E_0 e^{(i-1)x/\delta}.}
$$
The real physical field is $\operatorname{Re}[\widetilde E_y e^{-i\omega t}]$. A thick slab excludes a returning wave from its rear face. The <skin depth> is the electric-field <amplitude> decay <length>; the intensity decay <length> is $\delta/2$.

<Faraday's law of induction> fixes $\widetilde H_z=k\widetilde E_y/(\mu_0\omega)$. The <surface impedance of a good conductor> and the complete transmitted <magnetic field> are consequently
$$
\boxed{Z=\frac{\widetilde E_y}{\widetilde H_z}
=\frac{\mu_0\omega}{k}\simeq(1-i)\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0\omega}{2\sigma}}
=\frac{1-i}{\sigma\delta},\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf H}=\frac{1+i}{\mu_0\omega\delta}E_0e^{(i-1)x/\delta}\widehat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The sign of the imaginary part belongs to the stated time convention. Average the <Poynting vector> using complex conjugation and the factor one half appropriate to peak <phasors>:
$$
\langle\mathbf N\rangle=\frac12\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde{\mathbf E}\times\widetilde{\mathbf H}^{\,*})
=N_0e^{-2x/\delta}\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\boxed{N_0=\frac{|E_0|^2}{2\mu_0\omega\delta}=\frac{\sigma\delta|E_0|^2}{4}.}
$$
As an independent <energy> check, $-\partial_x\langle N_x\rangle=\sigma|E_0|^2e^{-2x/\delta}/2$, the mean <Joule heating> per unit <volume>.

<Ohm's law> gives $\widetilde J_y=\sigma\widetilde E_y$ and $\widetilde B_z=\mu_0\widetilde H_z$. Hence the averaged <Lorentz force> per unit <volume> is
$$
\langle f_x\rangle=\frac12\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde J_y\widetilde B_z^*)
=\sigma\mu_0 N_0 e^{-2x/\delta}.
$$
Integrating through the illuminated half-space gives the positive inward <radiation pressure>
$$
\boxed{p=\int_0^\infty\langle f_x\rangle dx=\frac{\sigma\delta\mu_0}{2}N_0.}
$$
Finally let $Z_0=\sqrt{\mu_0/\epsilon_0}$ be the <vacuum> <wave impedance>. Tangential-field <boundary conditions> at normal incidence give the electric-amplitude <reflection coefficient> $r=(Z-Z_0)/(Z+Z_0)$. Since $|Z|\ll Z_0$,
$$
R=|r|^2=1-\frac{4Z_0\operatorname{Re}Z}{|Z+Z_0|^2}
\simeq1-\frac{4\operatorname{Re}Z}{Z_0},\qquad
\boxed{R\simeq1-2\sqrt{\frac{2\omega\epsilon_0}{\sigma}}.}
$$
Here $N_0=(1-R)I_{\rm inc}$ by surface <energy> balance. Combining the leading formulas gives $p\simeq2I_{\rm inc}/c$, the nearly perfect-reflector limit. The displayed reflection expansion also assumes a good conductor relative to the incident <vacuum> wave, $\omega\epsilon_0/\sigma\ll1$.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

A <normal mode> is a motion in which all <displacement> coordinates oscillate at one <angular frequency> with fixed <amplitude> ratios. For small conservative oscillations, the symmetric stiffness and <mass> matrices give the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations>, $\mathsf K\mathbf u=\omega^2\mathsf M\mathbf u$. For positive <mass> and a stable equilibrium, its mass-orthogonal <eigenvectors> span the motion. A <superposition> of these modes, with phases and <amplitudes> fixed by initial <displacements> and <velocities>, gives the general solution; different modes need not share a phase.

Use upward end <displacements>. The <spring> compressions are $x_0-x_1$ and $x_0-x_2$, while the beam's centre rises by $q=(x_1+x_2)/2$. The <potential energy> is
$$
V=\frac k2[(x_0-x_1)^2+(x_0-x_2)^2]+\frac{mg}{2}(x_1+x_2).
$$
Static <force balance> gives $2kx_0=mg$, cancelling the linear terms and leaving
$$
\boxed{V=\tfrac12k(x_1^2+x_2^2)+kx_0^2.}
$$
For a uniform beam, its central <moment of inertia> in the vertical plane is $ml^2/12$. To first order in the tilt, $\theta=(x_2-x_1)/l$. Its translational and rotational <kinetic energies> are
$$
T=\frac m8(\dot x_1+\dot x_2)^2+\frac m{24}(\dot x_2-\dot x_1)^2
=\frac m6(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_1\dot x_2+\dot x_2^2).
$$
Thus $\mathsf M=(m/6)\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathsf K=k\mathsf1$. The <normal modes of a rigid beam on two end springs> have <eigenvectors>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u_s=(1,1),\quad\omega_s=\sqrt{2k/m};\qquad
\mathbf u_a=(1,-1),\quad\omega_a=\sqrt{6k/m}.}
$$
The first translates without tilt; the second rocks about a stationary centre. Ordinary <frequencies> are $\omega_s/(2\pi)$ and $\omega_a/(2\pi)$.

Assume the system had settled before the end load leaves, and put that load at end 1. Its extra static <spring> load is $Mg$ at that end and zero at the other. Therefore $x_1(0)=-Mg/k$, $x_2(0)=0$, and both initial <velocities> vanish because no <impulse> is imparted. Decomposing into the two <normal modes> gives
$$
x_1(t)=-\frac{Mg}{2k}(\cos\omega_st+\cos\omega_at),\qquad
x_2(t)=-\frac{Mg}{2k}(\cos\omega_st-\cos\omega_at).
$$
Only the symmetric mode contributes to the centre position:
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm CM}(t)-z_{\rm eq}=-\frac{Mg}{2k}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{2k}{m}}\,t\right).}
$$
The datum $z_{\rm eq}$ is the unloaded beam's equilibrium height; no absolute height is supplied. The rocking mode is present even though it cancels out of this coordinate. These results use small tilt, $Mg/(kl)\ll1$, ideal light vertical <springs> and negligible <damping>.

For an attractive <inverse-square force>, write $V(r)=-\kappa/r$ with $\kappa>0$, and take nonzero conserved <angular momentum> $J$. The <conic section> orbit has
$$
r=\frac{J^2/(m\kappa)}{1+e\cos\psi},\qquad
e^2=1+\frac{2EJ^2}{m\kappa^2}.
$$
The physically allowed cases are **<ellipse> for $-m\kappa^2/(2J^2)\leq E<0$, <parabola> for $E=0$, and <hyperbola> for $E>0$**. The minimum allowed <energy> gives the circular special case $e=0$. Here $E$ is measured relative to infinity. Zero <angular momentum> instead gives radial collision or escape trajectories, interpreted as degenerate limits rather than regular closed <ellipses>.

For a repulsive <inverse-square force>, $V(r)=+\kappa/r$. The <effective potential> is positive and decreases from infinity to zero, so a trajectory reaching a finite <radius> has $E>0$. For nonzero <angular momentum>, it is the accessible branch of a **<hyperbola>**, with $e^2=1+2EJ^2/(m\kappa^2)>1$. There are no bound <ellipses> or finite-distance zero-energy <parabolas>. At zero <angular momentum>, the motion is straight inward followed by straight outward after reflection at a radial turning point.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

For the scattering calculation, take the heavy target as fixed and define $\kappa=2Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)>0$. The incoming <kinetic energy> and <angular momentum> are $E=mv^2/2$ and $J=mvb$. In the original sketch, the horizontal dashed incoming asymptote has perpendicular distance $b$ from the nucleus, while $d$ is the <radius> from the nucleus to the point where $\dot r=0$.

\Image[paper-1-scattering.png]
{title=Original repulsive <Rutherford scattering> trajectory with <impact parameter> and closest approach}

At closest approach, the <velocity> is tangential and $v_t=J/(md)=vb/d$. <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\frac12mv^2=\frac{J^2}{2md^2}+\frac\kappa d,
\qquad mv^2d^2-2\kappa d-mv^2b^2=0.
$$
Choose the positive root and put $s=\kappa/(mv^2)$:
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm rep}=s+\sqrt{s^2+b^2}.}
$$
Head-on incidence gives $d_{\rm rep}=2s$, and repulsion always gives $d_{\rm rep}>b$. With an attractive <Coulomb force>, change the sign of the potential term:
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm att}=\sqrt{s^2+b^2}-s.}
$$
For $b>0$, the <attractive and repulsive inverse-square closest approaches> obey $d_{\rm att}d_{\rm rep}=b^2$. For $b=0$ attraction causes a collision with the point centre, so $d=0$ is a singular endpoint, not a regular scattering turning point. Finite nuclear size is excluded by the stated point-force model.

For the repulsive <inverse-cube central force> $\mathbf F=\kappa_3\widehat{\mathbf r}/r^3$, the <torque> vanishes, $\mathbf r\times\mathbf F=0$, and <angular momentum> is still conserved. Its <potential energy> is $+\kappa_3/(2r^2)$, since $-dV/dr=+\kappa_3/r^3$. Thus
$$
\frac12mv^2=\frac{m^2v^2b^2}{2md^2}+\frac{\kappa_3}{2d^2},\qquad
\boxed{d=\sqrt{b^2+\frac{\kappa_3}{mv^2}}.}
$$
The new coefficient is denoted $\kappa_3$ because its units are $\mathrm{N\,m^3}$, unlike the $\mathrm{N\,m^2}$ units of the inverse-square coefficient. Conserving <angular momentum> depends on centrality, not on a particular radial power law.

= Solution
{parent=C12}

Measure $\theta$ from the upward vertical, let $\mathbf e_3$ point from the support along the <symmetry> axis, and use the support as the reference point for all <moments of inertia>. Choose $\mathbf e_2=(\widehat{\mathbf z}-\cos\theta\mathbf e_3)/\sin\theta$ and $\mathbf e_1=\mathbf e_2\times\mathbf e_3$. These form an instantaneous principal-axis frame; its transverse axes need not spin with marks painted on the body. The three infinitesimal <Euler angles> rotations add as vectors:
$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\dot\theta\mathbf e_1+\dot\phi\widehat{\mathbf z}+\dot\chi\mathbf e_3.
$$
Resolving the vertical direction gives
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\omega=(\dot\theta,\dot\phi\sin\theta,\dot\phi\cos\theta+\dot\chi),\qquad
\mathbf J=(I_1\dot\theta,I_1\dot\phi\sin\theta,I_3[\dot\phi\cos\theta+\dot\chi]).}
$$
A convention matters here: in genuinely material-fixed transverse axes, the first two components are rotated by the spin angle $\chi$. For one conventional choice they are $\dot\theta\cos\chi+\dot\phi\sin\theta\sin\chi$ and $-\dot\theta\sin\chi+\dot\phi\sin\theta\cos\chi$. Since $I_1=I_2$, that rotation changes neither <kinetic energy> nor the axial and vertical conserved momenta. The simplified displayed components use the instantaneous transverse frame just defined.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong/The Motion of Rigid Bodies>[§3.5.2, angular-velocity decomposition and body-frame components; §3.6, symmetric-top conserved momenta]{ref}

For a frictionless fixed support, the support does no work and gravity is conservative, so the total <energy> is constant. The <heavy symmetric top Lagrangian> is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac{I_1}{2}(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)
+\frac{I_3}{2}(\dot\chi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2-mgh\cos\theta.
$$
The two <cyclic coordinates> $\chi,\phi$ give
$$
p_\chi=J_3=I_3(\dot\chi+\dot\phi\cos\theta),\qquad
p_\phi=J_z=I_1\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+J_3\cos\theta.
$$
Equivalently, gravitational <torque> has no component along the vertical or <symmetry> axis. Eliminate $\dot\phi=(J_z-J_3\cos\theta)/(I_1\sin^2\theta)$ and the axial spin from $E=T+V$:
$$
\boxed{E=\frac{I_1}{2}\dot\theta^2+U_{\rm eff}(\theta),\qquad
U_{\rm eff}=\frac{(J_z-J_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I_1\sin^2\theta}
+mgh\cos\theta+\frac{J_3^2}{2I_3}.}
$$
This is the <heavy symmetric top reduction> at fixed $J_3,J_z$. A constant inclination must satisfy the reduced <equation of motion> $I_1\ddot\theta=-U_{\rm eff}'$, not merely have zero instantaneous <velocity>. Differentiation at fixed conserved momenta gives, for $0<\theta<\pi$ and $\Omega=\dot\phi$,
$$
U_{\rm eff}'=\sin\theta\,[J_3\Omega-I_1\Omega^2\cos\theta-mgh].
$$
Therefore <uniform precession of a heavy symmetric top> requires
$$
\boxed{I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-J_3\Omega+mgh=0.}
$$
For a prescribed upward inclination $0<\theta<\pi/2$, real <precession> rates exist only if the quadratic <discriminant> is nonnegative:
$$
\Omega_\pm=\frac{J_3\pm\sqrt{J_3^2-4I_1mgh\cos\theta}}{2I_1\cos\theta},\qquad
\boxed{|J_3|_{\min}(\theta)=2\sqrt{I_1mgh\cos\theta}.}
$$
For positive spin the absolute-value sign can be dropped. The two roots are the slow and fast branches and coalesce at the threshold. The familiar upright-limit critical magnitude is $2\sqrt{I_1mgh}$; strict inequality gives <gyroscopic stabilization of an inverted symmetric top>. This limiting stability condition should not be confused with a positive minimum over every possible inclination. As $\theta$ approaches $\pi/2$ from below, the fixed-inclination threshold approaches zero. At the horizontal angle $\Omega=mgh/J_3$ for $J_3\ne0$; below the horizontal the quadratic admits real solutions even at $J_3=0$. The stated minimum therefore needs the inclination convention made explicit.

= Solution
{parent=C13}

Use the <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> for a uniform slender <elastic beam>, small deflection and small slope, neglecting axial tension, shear deformation, rotational inertia of a cross-section and <damping>. Let $E$ be <Young's modulus>, $I=\int_A z^2dA$ the <second moment of area> about the <neutral axis>, and $\rho$ the <mass> per unit <length>. If <density> instead denotes <mass> per <volume>, replace this $\rho$ by $\rho_{\rm bulk}A$. The <bending stiffness> is $EI$; the small-slope <curvature> is $y_{xx}$. The bending and <kinetic energies> are
$$
U=\frac{EI}{2}\int_0^L y_{xx}^2dx,\qquad
T=\frac\rho2\int_0^L y_t^2dx.
$$
Twice integrating the variation of $U$ by parts gives the restoring load $-EIy_{xxxx}$ per unit <length>. <Newton's second law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\rho y_{tt}+EIy_{xxxx}=0.}
$$
At a pivot, <displacement> is fixed but slope is free; zero end <bending moment> gives $EIy_{xx}=0$. This also follows from the natural boundary term $EIy_{xx}\delta y_x$ in the variation.

With <separation of variables>, $y=XT$ gives
$$
\frac{T''}{T}=-\frac{EI}{\rho}\frac{X''''}{X}=-\omega^2.
$$
Choose the time normalization $T(0)=1$. Initial rest removes the <sine> time term, so $T(t)=\cos\omega t$. The spatial characteristic roots are $\pm ik,\pm k$, giving
$$
X=A\sin kx+B\cos kx+C\sinh kx+D\cosh kx,
\qquad
\boxed{\omega=k^2\sqrt{EI/\rho}.}
$$
The two conditions at $x=0$ give $B+D=0$ and $-B+D=0$, hence $B=D=0$. At $L$, addition and subtraction of $X(L)=X''(L)=0$ give $C\sinh kL=0$ and $A\sin kL=0$. Nontrivial positive-frequency modes therefore have $C=0$ and $k_n=n\pi/L$. Thus the <simply supported beam normal modes> are
$$
\boxed{X_n(x)=\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad
\omega_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho}},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The zero-frequency cubic solution also vanishes under these four endpoint conditions, so $n=0$ is not an additional mode.

For the specified release, small slope requires $|a|L\ll1$. Expand the initial deflection in the <Fourier sine series>. Its coefficients are
$$
A_n=\frac{2a}{L}\int_0^L x(x-L)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx
=-\frac{4aL^2[1-(-1)^n]}{n^3\pi^3}.
$$
Writing $\omega_1=\pi^2\sqrt{EI/\rho}/L^2$, the resulting motion is
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=-\frac{8aL^2}{\pi^3}\sum_{j=0}^\infty
\frac{\sin[(2j+1)\pi x/L]}{(2j+1)^3}
\cos[(2j+1)^2\omega_1t].}
$$
Only odd <normal modes> occur because the initial profile is symmetric about the midpoint, and their <amplitudes> decrease as $n^{-3}$. The <frequencies> grow as $n^2$, so the motion is dispersive and is not a single shape multiplied by one <cosine>. Nevertheless the ideal undamped solution is exactly periodic with period $2\pi/\omega_1$. At $t=\pi/(2\omega_1)$ all odd-mode <cosines> vanish, so the bar is instantaneously straight; at $t=\pi/\omega_1$ it has the opposite initial deflection. The first mode dominates <displacement>, with third-mode <amplitude> only $1/27$ of the first.

There is <initial curvature incompatibility at a moment-free beam end>: the prescribed <parabola> has $y_{xx}(x,0)=2a$, whereas a moment-free pivot requires zero <curvature> at an endpoint. Therefore the data do not define a classical solution with <curvature> continuous through the two end-time corners. They do define a finite-energy <weak solution> after release of the <forces> or end moments that prepared the initial shape. The <sine> expansion represents that solution: its <displacement> reproduces the initial <parabola>, each mode satisfies the released endpoint conditions, and $\sum_n n^4|A_n|^2<\infty$ gives finite bending <energy>. One must not differentiate the initial series twice at its endpoints and assign the interior <parabola>'s <curvature> there. This qualification leaves the modal <displacement> prediction intact.
