For <entropy gain from impact and thermal equilibration>, let the large lake be a reservoir at an initially unspecified <temperature> $T_L$, in kelvin, and take the final meteorite <temperature> to be $T_L$. Its initial <temperature> is $T_i=1273.15\,\mathrm K$. The meteorite loses thermal energy $mc(T_i-T_L)$, and its <kinetic energy> $mv^2/2$ is also dissipated into the lake. Thus
$$
\Delta S_{\rm met}=mc\log(T_L/T_i),\qquad\Delta S_{\rm lake}=\frac{mc(T_i-T_L)+mv^2/2}{T_L},
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm universe}=mc\left[\log\frac{T_L}{T_i}+\frac{T_i}{T_L}-1\right]+\frac{mv^2}{2T_L}.}
$$
Both <heat> equilibration and dissipation contribute positively. For the requested estimate, assume a lake <temperature> $T_L=300\,\mathrm K$. This gives **$\Delta S_{\rm universe}\simeq2.93\times10^3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$**. Taking $273$–$300\,\mathrm K$ instead gives about $3.3$–$2.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}$; this shows the estimate's sensitivity without asserting an unprovided lake <temperature>. The boxed expression retains the full <temperature> dependence. The model uses the stated constant <heat capacity>, no phase change of the meteorite, and a lake large enough that its <temperature> change is negligible.<Classical Thermodynamics by David Tong>[Section 4.3.3: entropy changes and thermal reservoirs]{ref}

For the <image force on a line charge above a conducting plane>, with no imposed external <electric field>, use the <method of images>: replace the plane by a parallel image line of charge $-\lambda$ at height $-h$. The image is $2h$ from the real line, and its field at the real line has magnitude $\lambda/(4\pi\epsilon_0h)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbf F}{\text{length}}=-\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0h}\,\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
Here $\hat{\mathbf z}$ points upward from the plane. **The force is attractive and normal to the conductor**, for either sign of $\lambda$. The image reproduces the induced surface charge field; it is not an extra physical charge, and no factor $1/2$ is inserted in the force on the actual line.

Interpret the two radial turning values as $r_{\min}=r_0$ and $r_{\max}=4r_0$; the printed maximum/minimum order is reversed. The potential is $V(r)=Ar^2/2$. At either turning point,
$$
E=\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}+\frac A2r^2.
$$
Equating these two energies gives $L^2=mA r_{\min}^2r_{\max}^2$. A radial impulse changes the radial velocity and energy but exerts no torque about the centre, so it preserves $L$. For <radial-impulse circularization of a harmonic central orbit>, the circular orbit satisfies $L^2/(mr_c^3)=Ar_c$. Hence
$$
\boxed{r_c=\sqrt{r_{\min}r_{\max}}=2r_0.}
$$
The original orbit crosses this radius. Cancelling its radial velocity there leaves the tangential speed required for the circular orbit; the impulse is not applied at an original radial turning point.

For a thick good conductor in the normal <skin effect> regime, the <skin-effect surface resistance> is determined by
$$
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu\sigma\omega}},\qquad R_s=\frac1{\sigma\delta}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu\omega}{2\sigma}}.
$$
Identical guide geometry then gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{R_{\rm steel}}{R_{\rm gold}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\rm steel}\sigma_{\rm gold}}{\mu_{\rm gold}\sigma_{\rm steel}}}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{4.1\times10^7}{1.5\times10^6}}=5.23,}
$$
where the numerical ratio assumes both coatings are nonmagnetic, with $\mu\simeq\mu_0$. A magnetic stainless-steel grade would require its <magnetic permeability>; conductivity alone does not fix that alternative.

Using $\mu_0\simeq4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$, the gold <skin depth> at the stated frequency is
$$
\boxed{\delta_{\rm gold}\simeq7.86\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m=0.79\,\mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
The <metal coating thickness relative to skin depth> sets the useful scale. Thus a layer of order **$1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$** begins to be useful, and a few <skin depths>, roughly **$2$–$3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$**, substantially screen the poorer substrate. This is a thickness scale, not a specified attenuation threshold. It assumes a continuous coating thicker than the relevant microscopic surface scale.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Chapter 7: wave propagation in conductors and skin depth]{ref}<CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants 2014 by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Vacuum permeability in the then-current SI convention]{ref}

Take upward as positive and interpret the stated $97\,\mathrm{kg}$ as the total mass immediately before launch, including the harpoon. The remaining probe mass is $M=95.6\,\mathrm{kg}$. <Conservation of momentum> during the short firing interval gives
$$
-97(1)=Mv_+ -1.4(70),\qquad\boxed{v_+=\frac{1}{95.6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=0.01046\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The harpoon speed used here is relative to the comet, as printed. From $v_{\rm esc}^2=2GM_c/R$, the near-surface <gravitational acceleration> is
$$
g_c=\frac{v_{\rm esc}^2}{2R}=4.84\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.
$$
For the <near-surface recoil height with constant downward thrust>, the upward motion has downward acceleration $a=g_c+F/M$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H=\frac{v_+^2}{2(g_c+F/M)}=0.358\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Since $H/R\simeq1.8\times10^{-4}$, treating gravity as constant is consistent. This uses only the stated gravity and thrust after firing; a taut tether could reduce this free-flight height, but no tether length or elasticity is specified. If instead $97\,\mathrm{kg}$ meant the probe mass after firing, the pre-firing mass would be $98.4\,\mathrm{kg}$ and its momentum would leave the probe still moving downward, with no upward excursion. This illustrates <nearly cancelling recoil momenta in payload ejection>. The mass convention matters because the launch momenta nearly cancel.

At an interface with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing from medium 1 to medium 2, the magnetic <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0,\qquad\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f.}
$$
The first follows from a pillbox applied to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. The <tangential magnetic field jump from a free surface current> follows from a shrinking rectangular Ampère loop; $\mathbf K_f$ is the free surface current. With no free sheet current, normal $B$ and tangential $H$ are continuous. In linear isotropic media $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$, so neither all components of $B$ nor all components of $H$ generally remain continuous.

For <magnetic field-line refraction at a permeability interface>, with angles measured from the normal, these conditions give $\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1=\mu_2/\mu_1$. They determine the bending of <magnetic flux> at a material interface. In a slender core electromagnet, flux continuity and <Ampère's law> lead to the magnetic-circuit estimate
$$
\Phi\simeq\frac{NI}{\sum_j l_j/(\mu_jA_j)}.
$$
An air gap can dominate the reluctance even when the iron core has high <magnetic permeability>. <Boundary conditions> also determine <demagnetizing fields>; fringing, <saturation> and <hysteresis> limit the simple linear circuit model. A high-<magnetic permeability> material redirects flux, whereas a perfect-conductor boundary is a different physical assumption.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Chapter 7: magnetic fields in matter and interface conditions]{ref}

An <inductor> stores magnetic energy and opposes changes of current through <electromagnetic induction>. For a fixed linear device, the flux linkage is $\Psi=N\Phi=LI$, the self-induced <electromotive force> is $-L\,dI/dt$, and the passive terminal voltage is $V=L\,dI/dt$. Hence
$$
\boxed{U=\int_0^I LI'\,dI'=\tfrac12LI^2,\qquad |X_L|=\omega L.}
$$
The voltage leads a sinusoidal current by a quarter cycle. A long solenoid has $B\simeq\mu NI/l$ and **$L\simeq\mu N^2A/l$**; its energy agrees with the volume integral of $B^2/(2\mu)$.

For two coupled coils, $\Psi_1=L_1I_1+\mathcal M I_2$, $\Psi_2=\mathcal M I_1+L_2I_2$, with signs set by coil orientations. Reciprocity gives the same <mutual inductance> in both equations. For the <energy stored in a nonlinear inductor>, $U=\int I\,d\Psi$ replaces the simple quadratic formula, and <hysteresis> can dissipate energy. Real coils also have resistance, core loss and parasitic capacitance, so an ideal <inductance> model has a finite useful frequency range.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Section 4.1.2: inductance and magnetostatic energy]{ref}

In a linear, nondispersive, lossless medium, the field <energy density> and energy flux are
$$
\boxed{u_{\rm em}=\tfrac12(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H),\qquad\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H.}
$$
The <Poynting theorem> follows by dotting the two curl <Maxwell equations> with the other field and subtracting:
$$
\frac{\partial u_{\rm em}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E.
$$
The right-hand side is power transferred from the field to free charges. In vacuum the <energy density> is $\epsilon_0E^2/2+B^2/(2\mu_0)$.

Static electric or magnetic fields store energy even though their time derivatives vanish. <Static Poynting flow> occurs because static crossed fields can also have a nonzero Poynting flux: for example, a steady current-carrying coaxial line has radial electric and azimuthal magnetic fields, carrying energy along the line toward the load. Time independence therefore does not imply zero energy flow.

For phasors whose real parts represent the physical fields,
$$
\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf E_0\times\mathbf H_0^*),\qquad\langle u_{\rm em}\rangle=\tfrac14(\epsilon|E_0|^2+\mu|H_0|^2).
$$
For a travelling <plane wave> in vacuum the electric and magnetic contributions are equal and $\langle S\rangle=c\langle u_{\rm em}\rangle$. In dispersive matter the simple macroscopic energy formula needs modification to include the material response; it is not a universal formula for plasma energy.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Chapter 4: conservation of electromagnetic energy and the Poynting vector]{ref}

Reflected and transmitted waves arise because the fields must satisfy the electromagnetic <boundary conditions>. With no free surface charge or current, tangential $E,H$ and normal $D,B$ are continuous. At normal incidence between lossless media, define the real <wave impedance> $Z_j=\sqrt{\mu_j/\epsilon_j}$. Writing $r=E_r/E_i$ and $t=E_t/E_i$ in a common transverse electric-field direction gives
$$
1+r=t,\qquad\frac{1-r}{Z_1}=\frac{t}{Z_2},\qquad\boxed{r=\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1},\quad t=\frac{2Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}.}
$$
For the <power normalization of Fresnel amplitudes>, power fractions are **$R=|r|^2$** and **$T=(Z_1/Z_2)|t|^2$**, so $R+T=1$. An electric-field amplitude coefficient alone is not a transmitted power fraction.

At oblique incidence, phase matching along the interface gives <Snell's law>, $n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t$. Applying the same <boundary conditions> separately to the two linear <polarizations> gives the <Fresnel equations>. For nonmagnetic dielectrics the parallel-polarized reflection vanishes at $\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1$. Above the critical angle into a lower-index lossless medium, the transmitted field is evanescent and reflection is total. A perfect conductor instead has zero tangential <electric field> and unit reflected power; finite conductivity introduces penetration and absorption.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Chapter 7: reflection and transmission at dielectric interfaces]{ref}

**<Displacement current> makes <Ampère's law> compatible with <charge conservation>.** The macroscopic <Maxwell equations> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D.}
$$
Taking the divergence of the last equation and using <Gauss's law> gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\rho_f=0$. Omitting $\partial_t\mathbf D$ would wrongly require the conduction current to be divergence free during charging. The displacement-current density $\partial_t\mathbf D$ has current-density units, but need not represent free charges crossing a region.

The <surface independence of Ampère circulation during capacitor charging> illustrates the need directly. A surface spanning the circuit can cut the wire or pass between the plates. For ideal parallel plates, $Q=\epsilon_0AE$ and
$$
I=\dot Q=\epsilon_0 A\dot E=\int_{\rm gap}\partial_t\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf A.
$$
The corrected circulation of $\mathbf H$ is independent of that surface choice. Free conduction current stops at a plate; <displacement current> supplies the gap contribution.

**In vacuum the two time-dependent curl equations support waves.** Taking a curl of Faraday's law in a source-free region yields
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0,\qquad c=(\mu_0\epsilon_0)^{-1/2}.
$$
The corresponding magnetic equation is identical. For a <plane wave>, $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\mathbf B=\mathbf k\times\mathbf E/\omega$. The fields carry energy through the Poynting flux. Dropping <displacement current> everywhere would remove these freely propagating solutions.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Sections 4.2–4.3: Maxwell's equations, charge conservation and electromagnetic waves]{ref}

**In a metal, conduction and displacement compete.** For a uniform ohmic material with $\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E$, constant $\epsilon,\mu$, and transverse waves,
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\epsilon\partial_t^2\mathbf E+\mu\sigma\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
Use $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-i\omega t}$. The <dispersion relation> and <conduction-to-displacement current ratio> are
$$
\boxed{k^2=\mu\epsilon\omega^2+i\mu\sigma\omega,\qquad\frac{|J_{\rm cond}|}{|J_{\rm disp}|}=\frac\sigma{\omega\epsilon}.}
$$
When $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$, $k\simeq(1+i)/\delta$ with $\delta=\sqrt{2/(\mu\sigma\omega)}$: fields are screened within a <skin depth> and energy is dissipated by <Joule heating>. This quasistatic limit neglects <displacement current> locally compared with conduction; it does not invalidate the full Maxwell law. At sufficiently high frequency, conductivity and permittivity are frequency dependent, so a constant-$\sigma$ approximation must be replaced by a material model.

**In an unmagnetized cold plasma, electron inertia gives a cutoff.** With immobile ions and negligible collisions, $m_e\dot{\mathbf v}=-e\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf J=-n_e e\mathbf v$ give $\sigma(\omega)=in_ee^2/(m_e\omega)$. For the <transverse wave cutoff in a cold unmagnetized plasma>, combining this carrier current with vacuum <displacement current> gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{\rm eff}=\epsilon_0\left(1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}\right),\quad\omega_p^2=\frac{n_ee^2}{m_e\epsilon_0},\quad\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2.}
$$
Transverse waves propagate above $\omega_p$ and are evanescent below it. The <group velocity> is $c\sqrt{1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2}<c$ even though the <phase velocity> exceeds $c$. The cold model also has longitudinal charge-density oscillations at $\omega_p$, distinct from transverse light waves. Collisions add <damping>, and <pressure> or magnetic fields modify these simple dispersions. To avoid <carrier-current double counting in effective permittivity>, once the carrier response is included in $\epsilon_{\rm eff}$ it must not be counted a second time as a separate conductivity. <Displacement current> therefore connects circuit continuity, vacuum radiation, conductor screening and plasma propagation within one consistent field theory.<Electromagnetism by David Tong>[Sections 7.6.2–7.6.3: conductors, plasma frequency and longitudinal plasma oscillations]{ref}

A torque-free <rigid body> has constant <angular momentum> $\mathbf L$ in inertial space. Its <angular velocity> need not be parallel to $\mathbf L$, because $\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega$. In body principal axes, <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body> are
$$
I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\quad I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\quad I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2.
$$
For a <symmetric top> $I_1=I_2=I_\perp$, $I_3=I_\parallel$, the component $\omega_3$ is constant and
$$
\boxed{\omega_1+i\omega_2=C e^{i\Omega_b t},\qquad\Omega_b=\frac{I_\parallel-I_\perp}{I_\perp}\omega_3.}
$$
This is the signed rotation frequency of the transverse angular-velocity components in the body frame. In space the symmetry axis instead obeys
$$
\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\frac{\mathbf L}{I_\perp}\times\mathbf e_3,\qquad\boxed{\Omega_{\rm space}=L/I_\perp.}
$$
Its angle to $\mathbf L$ is constant, with $\cos\theta=I_\parallel\omega_3/L$. The <body-frame and space-frame frequencies of free symmetric-top precession> describe different frames. A spherical body has $\boldsymbol\omega\parallel\mathbf L$ and no corresponding wobble. For a triaxial body, rotations about the largest and smallest principal-inertia axes are stable, whereas rotation about the intermediate axis is unstable.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[Sections 3.4.2–3.4.3: free symmetric top and stability of the asymmetric top]{ref}

For generalized coordinates $q_i$ with <Lagrangian> $L(q,\dot q,t)$, the <conjugate momentum> is
$$
\boxed{p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i},\qquad\dot p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}.}
$$
A coordinate absent from $L$ is cyclic, and its <conjugate momentum> is conserved. More generally, <Noether's theorem> connects continuous symmetries of the action to conserved quantities: spatial translations give linear momentum and rotations give <angular momentum>. If $L$ has no explicit time dependence, the conserved energy function is
$$
E=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L,\qquad\frac{dE}{dt}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}=0.
$$
The momentum conjugate to an angle is an <angular momentum>, not a linear velocity. <Canonical momentum> can also differ from <mechanical momentum>: for $L=\tfrac12m v^2+q\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf v-q\phi$, **$\mathbf p=m\mathbf v+q\mathbf A$**. Thus a conserved <conjugate momentum> must be identified from the actual <Lagrangian> and its symmetry, rather than assumed to equal $m\mathbf v$. Gauge-dependent canonical momenta require the corresponding gauge choice or gauge-covariant symmetry interpretation.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[Chapters 2 and 4: conservation laws, conjugate momentum and the Hamiltonian]{ref}

For positions measured from the chosen origin, the <inertia tensor> is
$$
\boxed{I_{ij}=\int\rho(\mathbf r)(r^2\delta_{ij}-x_ix_j)\,d^3r.}
$$
It relates <angular momentum> and <rotational kinetic energy> to <angular velocity>:
$$
\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad T_{\rm rot}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
The real symmetric tensor has orthogonal principal axes, in which $\mathbf I=\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3)$. Its <eigenvalues> are nonnegative moments of inertia. Rotation about a <principal axis> has $\mathbf L\parallel\boldsymbol\omega$; a general rotation does not. The moment about a unit axis $\mathbf n$ is $I_{\mathbf n}=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf I\mathbf n$.

Changing from the centre of mass to a parallel origin displaced by $\mathbf a$ gives the <tensor parallel-axis theorem>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf I_O=\mathbf I_{\rm CM}+M(a^2\mathbf 1-\mathbf a\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}).}
$$
For example, a uniform solid sphere has $\mathbf I=(2MR^2/5)\mathbf1$, while a thin rod along $x$ has principal moments $0,Ml^2/12,Ml^2/12$ in the ideal zero-radius limit. The tensor is needed to describe off-axis rotation and torque-free <precession>; a single scalar inertia only suffices after an axis is specified.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[Sections 3.1–3.3: inertia tensor, principal axes and the parallel-axis theorem]{ref}

For steady, inviscid, <incompressible flow>, Euler's equation integrated along a <streamline> gives
$$
\boxed{p+\tfrac12\rho v^2+\rho gz=\text{constant along that streamline}.}
$$
This is <Bernoulli's equation>. The constant is common to different <streamlines> if the flow is also irrotational; incompressibility alone does not imply that. Viscous loss or work by a pump requires additional terms.

A <Pitot-static tube> compares <stagnation pressure> $p_0$ at a forward-facing opening with nearby static <pressure> $p$. For equal height, the stagnation point has $v=0$, so
$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{2(p_0-p)/\rho}.}
$$
This measures flow speed when compressibility corrections are negligible.

In a flowmeter using the <Venturi effect>, continuity gives $A_1v_1=A_2v_2$. For a horizontal constriction with $A_2<A_1$, Bernoulli gives $p_1-p_2=\rho(v_2^2-v_1^2)/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{v_1=\sqrt{\frac{2(p_1-p_2)}{\rho[(A_1/A_2)^2-1]}}.}
$$
The fluid accelerates and static <pressure> falls in the throat. This is a second practical application of the conversion between <pressure> and <kinetic energy>; real instruments need calibration for flow losses.

**A <normal mode> is a motion in which all coordinates have a fixed relative pattern and oscillate at one frequency.** Near a stable equilibrium, let the quadratic kinetic and potential energies be $T=\dot{\mathbf q}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf q}/2$ and $V=\mathbf q^{\mathsf T}\mathbf K\mathbf q/2$. With positive-definite <mass matrix> $\mathbf M$, the equations and harmonic ansatz give
$$
\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf K\mathbf q=0,\qquad\boxed{\mathbf K\mathbf a_s=\omega_s^2\mathbf M\mathbf a_s.}
$$
The stiffness and mass matrices are symmetric. For distinct frequencies, their symmetry gives $(\omega_s^2-\omega_r^2)\mathbf a_r^{\mathsf T}\mathbf M\mathbf a_s=0$, so the mode vectors are orthogonal in the mass inner product. A basis can also be chosen in a degenerate eigenspace. Expanding $\mathbf q=\sum_s Q_s\mathbf a_s$ decouples the equations into $\ddot Q_s+\omega_s^2Q_s=0$; initial displacements and velocities determine the coefficients. Positive stiffness gives real frequencies, a zero <eigenvalue> can describe a free symmetry motion, and a negative one signals instability rather than an oscillatory mode.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[Section 2.6: small oscillations and generalized normal-mode eigenvalue equations]{ref}

For **two equal masses between fixed walls with three equal springs**, the <stiffness matrix> is $k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and the <mass matrix> is $m\mathbf1$. The patterns $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ have frequencies $\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\sqrt{3k/m}$. An initial displacement of one mass excites both modes and can produce <beats>.

For **a uniform taut string fixed at both ends**, the <wave equation> $\partial_t^2y=c_s^2\partial_x^2y$, with $c_s=\sqrt{\mathcal T/\mu_\ell}$, has patterns and angular frequencies
$$
\boxed{y_n(x)\propto\sin(n\pi x/l),\qquad\omega_n=n\pi c_s/l,\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The <boundary conditions> select the spectrum. <Fourier expansion> of arbitrary admissible initial data provides the mode amplitudes, illustrating the extension from a finite set of coordinates to a field.

For the <normal modes of inductively coupled LC circuits>, take two identical circuits with self-inductance $L$, capacitance $C$ and <mutual inductance> $\mathcal M$, where $|\mathcal M|<L$. The <capacitor> charges obey $L\ddot q_1+\mathcal M\ddot q_2+q_1/C=0$ and the exchanged equation. In-phase and opposite-phase charge patterns have
$$
\boxed{\omega_+=\frac1{\sqrt{C(L+\mathcal M)}},\qquad\omega_-=\frac1{\sqrt{C(L-\mathcal M)}}.}
$$
The <positive inductance matrix for two identical coils> is the physical counterpart of positive mass. Here <inductance> plays the role of the <mass matrix>. Mechanical, wave and electrical examples therefore share the same <eigenvalue> structure. Weak forcing excites modes according to its spatial overlap with each pattern; <damping> broadens the <resonances>, while strong nonlinearity can invalidate a fixed linear-mode decomposition.

A liquid-gas <thermodynamic critical point> is the endpoint of the coexistence curve where distinct liquid and gas densities merge. In the van der Waals illustration, write the original constants as $a_v,b_v$ to distinguish them from the later polynomial coefficients. The critical isotherm has a horizontal inflection in $p(V)$; setting its first two volume derivatives to zero gives
$$
\boxed{V_c=3Nb_v,\qquad k_BT_c=\frac{8a_v}{27b_v},\qquad p_c=\frac{a_v}{27b_v^2}.}
$$
Below this <temperature> the homogeneous van der Waals loop contains an unstable region; physical equilibrium instead separates into liquid and gas.<Classical Gases by David Tong>[Section 2.5.2: van der Waals equation and its critical point]{ref}

For the <critical point of a cubic density equation of state>, assume $b,c>0$. Its density derivatives are
$$
p_n=k_BT-bn+\tfrac c2n^2,\qquad p_{nn}=-b+cn.
$$
Because $n=N/V$ is a smooth monotone function of $V$ at finite positive density, a stationary inflection obeys $p_n=p_{nn}=0$ as well. Solving these equations and substituting back gives
$$
\boxed{n_c=\frac bc,\qquad k_BT_c=\frac{b^2}{2c},\qquad p_c=\frac{b^3}{6c^2}.}
$$
The symbols $b,c$ here have different units from the van der Waals excluded-volume parameter. With $x=n/n_c$, $t=T/T_c$, the isotherms become
$$
\frac p{p_c}=x^3-3x^2+3tx,\qquad\frac{d(p/p_c)}{dx}=3[(x-1)^2+t-1].
$$
The <spinodal densities of a cubic gas> delimit the two-extrema regime. The curves are monotone for $t>1$, have a stationary inflection at $(x,p/p_c)=(1,1)$ for $t=1$, and have two extrema $x=1\pm\sqrt{1-t}$ for $0<t<1$.

For the <critical-density compressibility of a cubic gas>, since $\kappa_T=(1/n)(\partial n/\partial p)_T$, evaluation at the critical density gives
$$
\boxed{\kappa_T(n_c,T)=\frac1{n_ck_B(T-T_c)}.}
$$
It diverges as $T\to T_c^+$ in this mean-field model. For $T<T_c$ it is negative on the homogeneous branch at $n_c$, violating <mechanical stability>: a <pressure> increase would expand rather than compress that branch.

**The actual equilibrium below criticality is coexistence of two phases**, with equal <pressure> and <chemical potential> and different densities. A sample's mean density can change by changing phase proportions. The unstable homogeneous branch is not a realizable equilibrium isotherm. The <Maxwell construction in density coordinates> requires care: the equal-area construction is performed in $p$ versus volume, not by using unweighted area in $p$ versus density. The truncated polynomial is an approximate mean-field equation, so its predictions need not give quantitative critical behaviour for a real gas.

The <Boltzmann distribution> assigns probability $P_i=e^{-E_i/(k_BT)}/Z$ to each microstate. For the <equilibrium height distribution in a uniform gravitational field>, take a dilute gas above a floor in a constant gravitational field. Uniform cross-section and no relevant upper boundary give the normalized height density
$$
P(z)=\frac{mg}{k_BT}e^{-mgz/(k_BT)},\qquad z\geq0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle U_g\rangle=\int_0^\infty mgz\,P(z)\,dz=k_BT.}
$$
The gravitational potential is linear in height, so its mean is not a quadratic-mode contribution of $k_BT/2$.

The <equipartition> states that each independent quadratic term in a classical equilibrium Hamiltonian contributes mean energy $k_BT/2$, provided the relevant degrees of freedom are thermally accessible. Equivalently, $\langle x_i\partial H/\partial x_i\rangle=k_BT$ when the phase-space integration by parts has vanishing boundary terms. Three translational velocity components therefore contribute $3k_BT/2$.

For <rotational equipartition in a rough-ball gas>, collisions exchange both translational and rotational energy. In the intended three-dimensional classical equilibrium model there are three translational and three rotational quadratic terms, giving $3k_BT$ in total <kinetic energy> per ball. Adding $\langle U_g\rangle=k_BT$, and conserving the initial energy $mgh$ per ball, gives
$$
\boxed{mgh=\left(\tfrac32+\tfrac32+1\right)k_BT=4k_BT,\qquad T=\frac{mgh}{4k_B}.}
$$
Roughness is relevant because it allows rotation to thermalize. The <effective mechanical temperature of released rough balls> describes the balls' mechanical degrees of freedom, not their internal material <temperature>; finite ball size and excluded-volume effects are neglected.<Classical Gases by David Tong>[Sections 2.2.1 and 2.3: equipartition and classical thermal distributions]{ref}

The height tail is
$$
\boxed{P(z>h)=\int_h^\infty P(z)\,dz=e^{-mgh/(k_BT)}=e^{-4}=0.0183\simeq1.83\%.}
$$
This can be nonzero because collisions redistribute energy between balls; an individual ball is not restricted to its original energy. The exponential distribution is the large-number statistical approximation under the stated effective-equilibrium assumption.

For the <angularly resolved molecular arrival flux>, let $f(v)$ be normalized by $\int_0^\infty f(v)\,dv=1$. Isotropic velocity directions have probability $d\Omega/(4\pi)$. Integrating over azimuth gives $(1/2)\sin\theta\,d\theta$, while the speed normal to a stationary piston is $v\cos\theta$. For incident directions $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$, the arrival flux is therefore
$$
\boxed{d\Gamma=\frac12nf(v)v\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,dv.}
$$
An elastic collision transfers normal momentum $2mv\cos\theta$ to the piston. Integrating the momentum flux gives
$$
p=nm\int_0^\infty v^2f(v)\,dv\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=\boxed{\frac13nm\langle v^2\rangle}.
$$
No specific Maxwellian speed distribution was needed for this isotropic kinetic-<pressure> identity.

Use the figure's $+x$ direction toward the piston and let its inward speed $u>0$ mean piston velocity $-u\hat{\mathbf x}$. For <reflection from a translating planar piston>, in the piston frame the incoming normal velocity is $v_x+u$. Elastic reflection reverses it, and transforming back gives
$$
\boxed{(v_x,v_y,v_z)\longmapsto(-v_x-2u,v_y,v_z).}
$$
The exact kinetic-energy change in this ideal massive-piston model is $\Delta K=2muv_x+2mu^2$. Thus, to first order in the small piston speed,
$$
\boxed{K_{\rm after}=K_{\rm before}+2muv_x+O(u^2).}
$$
The <first-order collision-flux approximation for a slow piston> applies: the incident collision flux changes at order $u$, but its product with the order-$u$ energy increment changes the energy rate only at order $u^2$. Hence one may use the stationary flux at leading order. For piston area $S$,
$$
\frac{dU}{dt}=S\int(2muv\cos\theta)\,d\Gamma=\frac13Snmu\langle v^2\rangle=\boxed{pSu=-p\frac{dV}{dt}.}
$$
Here $dV/dt=-Su$. This <compression work from molecular impacts>, with no <heat> flow, is the first law for work done on the gas by compression. The derivation assumes $u$ small compared with thermal speeds and an approximately isotropic distribution at the piston; rapid compression can produce nonequilibrium <pressure> and higher-order corrections.

**The <second law of thermodynamics> restricts which energy-conserving processes can occur.** In the <Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law>, no cyclic engine can have as its sole effect the extraction of <heat> from one reservoir and its complete conversion into work. In the <Clausius statement of the second law>, no process can have as its sole effect transferring <heat> from a colder reservoir to a hotter one. An engine or refrigerator combined with the reverse of a reversible device shows that a violation of either statement would violate the other.

A <heat engine> takes <heat> $Q_h>0$ from a reservoir at $T_h$, rejects $Q_c>0$ to one at $T_c<T_h$, and returns its working substance to the initial state. Energy conservation gives $W=Q_h-Q_c$ and efficiency $\eta=W/Q_h=1-Q_c/Q_h$. **<Carnot's theorem>** states that no engine between these reservoirs is more efficient than a reversible one, and that all reversible engines between them have the same efficiency. Otherwise, drive a reversible engine backwards with the work of a more efficient engine; the composite cycle transfers <heat> from cold to hot without external work, contradicting the Clausius statement.

A reversible <Carnot cycle> consists of two isotherms joined by two reversible adiabats. For an ideal gas, $Q_h=Nk_BT_h\log(V_2/V_1)$ and $Q_c=Nk_BT_c\log(V_3/V_4)$. The adiabats give $V_2/V_1=V_3/V_4$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}=\frac{T_c}{T_h},\qquad\eta_{\rm reversible}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.}
$$
<Carnot's theorem> makes this independent of the working substance and defines the thermodynamic absolute-<temperature> scale consistently with ideal-gas <temperature>.<Classical Thermodynamics by David Tong>[Sections 4.3.1–4.3.2: heat engines, Carnot's theorem and temperature]{ref}

For any reversible cycle, $\oint\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T=0$. This makes the integral between two equilibrium states independent of the reversible path, defining the <state function> <entropy>:
$$
\boxed{S_B-S_A=\int_A^B\frac{\delta Q_{\rm rev}}T.}
$$
<Heat> itself is path dependent; <entropy> is not. An irreversible change can be evaluated using any imagined reversible path between its equilibrium endpoints. For example, a fixed amount of ideal gas with constant $C_V$ has $\Delta S=C_V\log(T_B/T_A)+Nk_B\log(V_B/V_A)$.

For a general cycle the <Clausius inequality> is $\oint\delta Q/T_{\rm boundary}\leq0$. The <entropy> balance for a process is $\Delta S\geq\int\delta Q/T_{\rm boundary}$, with equality for reversible <heat> exchange and no <entropy production>. The surroundings' <entropy> must be included, giving **$\Delta S_{\rm universe}\geq0$**. A system's own <entropy> can decrease by exporting <entropy>; an isolated system's cannot.

The engine's reservoirs have net <entropy> change $Q_c/T_c-Q_h/T_h\geq0$, so
$$
\boxed{\eta\leq1-\frac{T_c}{T_h},}
$$
with equality for a reversible engine. Friction, <heat> flow through a finite <temperature> difference and unrestrained expansion produce <entropy> and reduce usable work. Thus energy conservation fixes the balance of <heat> and work, while the second law fixes their direction and the attainable efficiency.<Classical Thermodynamics by David Tong>[Section 4.3.3: entropy as a state function and the second law]{ref}
