For a smooth isolated <conducting sphere>, <Gauss's law> gives the surface <electric field> $E_s=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0a^2)$ and <electric potential> $V=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)=aE_s$. At the specified <dielectric strength>, $V_{\max}=3.0\times10^3\ \mathrm V$. The <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere> is $C=4\pi\epsilon_0a$, so its <capacitor energy> is
$$
U_{\max}=\frac12 C V_{\max}^2=2\pi\epsilon_0a^3E_s^2
=\boxed{5.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm J\simeq0.50\ \mu\mathrm J.}
$$
This is the ideal <breakdown-limited energy of a conducting sphere>, taking air's <permittivity> approximately equal to <vacuum permittivity> and neglecting nearby objects and surface irregularities. The <vacuum permittivity> used numerically is $8.8542\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, vacuum electric permittivity entry]{ref}

Take the initial thrower and receiver positions to be $(-a,0)$ and $(a,0)$, with counterclockwise <angular velocity> $\omega$. Interpret $v$ as the launch <speed> relative to the thrower. The ball inherits the thrower's tangential <velocity>, so in an <inertial frame> its initial horizontal <velocity> is $(v,-a\omega)$. The flight time across the diameter is $t_f\simeq2a/v$. During that time the receiver moves approximately $+a\omega t_f$ sideways while the ball moves $-a\omega t_f$. Thus the <transverse miss on a rotating platform> is
$$
\boxed{d\simeq2a\omega t_f=\frac{4a^2\omega}{v}.}
$$
The ball passes behind the receiver in the sense of rotation. Equivalently, in the rotating frame its transverse <Coriolis acceleration> has magnitude $2\omega v$ to leading order; integrating over $t_f$ gives $d=\tfrac12(2\omega v)t_f^2$. Terms neglected here are higher order in $a\omega/v$. Specifying an initially radial launch <velocity> in the inertial frame instead would define a different throw and would lose the inherited tangential <velocity>.

The <dynamic viscosity> measures the transport of tangential <momentum> between adjacent layers. The <kinetic estimates of gas transport coefficients> give
$$
\eta\sim\frac13nm\overline v\lambda,\qquad
\lambda\sim\frac1{\sqrt2n\sigma_c},\qquad
\overline v\propto\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}},
$$
where $n$ is number density, $m$ the molecular <mass>, $\lambda$ the <mean free path>, and $\sigma_c$ a collision <cross-section>. At fixed <temperature>, raising the <pressure> increases the number of momentum carriers but shortens their <mean free path> by the same factor. Consequently **the <dynamic viscosity> of a dilute gas is approximately independent of <pressure>**.

For a temperature-independent collision <cross-section>, the same estimate gives $\eta\propto\sqrt{mk_BT}/\sigma_c$, so **$\eta\propto T^{1/2}$ in the hard-sphere model**. Faster molecules carry <momentum> across layers more rapidly. Real intermolecular interactions make the effective collision <cross-section> temperature-dependent, changing this simple exponent. The pressure-independence also fails when wall collisions, rather than molecule–molecule collisions, set the transport distance; the continuum estimate assumes a small <Knudsen number>.

The varying <magnetic flux> through the ring induces an <electromotive force> by <Faraday's law of induction>. Its direction follows <Lenz's law>. With solenoid current $I_s$, ring current $I_r$, ring <self-inductance> $L_r$, <electrical resistance> $R$, and <mutual inductance> $M(z)$,
$$
L_r\dot I_r+RI_r=-M\dot I_s,\qquad F_z=I_sI_r\frac{dM}{dz}.
$$
The ring sits in a nonuniform <magnetic field>: coupling decreases above the solenoid, so $M>0$ and $M'<0$ with a common orientation for the two windings. For $I_s=I_0\cos\omega t$, the in-phase part of the induced ring current is negative:
$$
\langle I_sI_r\rangle=-\frac{I_0^2\omega^2ML_r}{2(R^2+\omega^2L_r^2)},\qquad
\boxed{\langle F_z\rangle=-\frac{I_0^2\omega^2ML_rM'}{2(R^2+\omega^2L_r^2)}>0.}
$$
Thus <alternating-current induction levitation> can balance the ring's weight when $\langle F_z\rangle=mg$. A falling mean lifting <force> with height supplies vertical <stability>. In the strongly inductive limit the two currents are almost opposite: the ring repels the solenoid during both half cycles. Finite <self-inductance> matters; an ideal purely resistive loop would carry a current exactly in quadrature with $I_s$ and give zero average <force> in this model. The source supplies the <Joule heating> in the real ring, and any mechanical work while it rises.

After the <photon> has thermalized, its <energy> $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$ belongs to the macroscopic body's internal <energy>. The body's <heat capacity> makes the resulting <temperature> change negligible. Its equilibrium <entropy> therefore increases by
$$
\boxed{\Delta S\simeq\frac{hc}{\lambda T}.}
$$
The <Boltzmann entropy> $S=k_B\ln\Omega$ then gives the <microstate multiplier from photon absorption>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Omega_f}{\Omega_i}=\exp(\Delta S/k_B)\simeq
\exp\!\left(\frac{hc}{\lambda k_BT}\right).}
$$
For scale, explicitly choosing a representative visible <wavelength> $\lambda=500\ \mathrm{nm}$ and room <temperature> $T=300\ \mathrm K$ gives $\Delta S=1.32\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, or $\Delta S/k_B=95.9$, and **a multiplier approximately $4.5\times10^{41}$**. These are illustrative choices, not additional supplied data: the exact multiplier depends exponentially on the particular visible <wavelength> and <temperature>. The exact <Planck constant>, <speed of light>, and <Boltzmann constant> fix the conversion.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, Planck constant, speed of light in vacuum, and Boltzmann constant entries]{ref}

In <electrostatics>, the <Maxwell equations in matter> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf0,
\qquad \mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P.
$$
Here $\rho_f$ is free <charge density> and $\mathbf P$ is <polarization density>. For a homogeneous isotropic linear <dielectric>, the <constitutive equation> is $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\mathbf E$. Integrating the first equation over a thin pillbox straddling an interface, with unit normal $\mathbf n$ from medium 1 to medium 2, gives $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f$. Integrating the second around a vanishingly narrow rectangular loop gives $\mathbf n\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=\mathbf0$. These are the <electrostatic interface boundary conditions>. When there is no free surface <electric charge>, the normal <electric displacement field> and tangential <electric field> are continuous; the normal <electric field> generally is not.

Neglect end <fringing field of a capacitor>. In either axial segment of the <coaxial cylindrical capacitor>, a cylindrical <Gaussian surface> gives $D_r=\lambda_f/(2\pi r)$, where $\lambda_f$ is free <electric charge> per length. Integrating the radial <electric field> between the conductors gives
$$
V=\frac{\lambda_f}{2\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}\ln\frac ba,
\qquad C'=\frac{\lambda_f}{V}=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}{\ln(b/a)}.
$$
The liquid and air segments share the same conductor <electric potential difference>, so their capacitances add in parallel. Thus the <partially filled coaxial capacitor> has
$$
\boxed{C(h)=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}[L-h+\epsilon h].}
$$
Here the question's $\epsilon$ is relative <permittivity>, and air's relative <permittivity> is approximated by one.

Write $x$ for the rise above the external level. The wide reservoir keeps that level approximately fixed. At constant applied <voltage>, one must include work done by the source: the appropriate electrical contribution to the mechanical potential is $-C V^2/2$. The upward <dielectric insertion force at fixed voltage> is consequently
$$
F_e=\frac12V^2\frac{dC}{dx}
=\frac{\pi\epsilon_0(\epsilon-1)V^2}{\ln(b/a)}.
$$
The excess <hydrostatic pressure> $\rho gx$ acts over the annular area $A=\pi(b^2-a^2)$. Alternatively its gravitational <potential energy> is $\rho gAx^2/2$, since liquid initially at the reservoir level must be raised through all heights from zero to $x$. Balancing the derivatives gives
$$
\boxed{x=\Delta h=\frac{\epsilon_0(\epsilon-1)V^2}{\rho g(b^2-a^2)\ln(b/a)}.}
$$
This assumes $h+x<L$, a communicating liquid reservoir, negligible capillarity, and a sufficiently long <capacitor> that end effects are small. Differentiating gives the <dielectric-rise voltmeter>'s sensitivity at a bias $V$:
$$
\boxed{\frac{dx}{dV}=\frac{2\epsilon_0(\epsilon-1)V}{\rho g(b^2-a^2)\ln(b/a)}.}
$$
To obtain its numerical value in $\mu\mathrm m\,\mathrm V^{-1}$, multiply the right-hand side in SI units by $10^6$. A convenient parametric scale, with $\beta=b/a$, is
$$
\frac{dx}{dV}=1.805\times10^{-3}\,
\frac{\epsilon-1}{(\beta^2-1)\ln\beta}
\left(\frac V{1\ \mathrm V}\right)
\left(\frac{10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}{\rho}\right)
\left(\frac{1\ \mathrm{mm}}a\right)^2
\left(\frac{9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}}g\right)
\ \mu\mathrm m\,\mathrm V^{-1}.
$$
For a concrete estimate, choose $a=1\,\mathrm{mm}$, $b=2\,\mathrm{mm}$, <mass density> $\rho=10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, <relative permittivity> $\epsilon=2$ and operating <voltage> $V=100\,\mathrm V$. These are illustrative choices, not supplied apparatus data. They give
$$
\boxed{\frac{dx}{dV}\simeq0.087\,\mu\mathrm m\,\mathrm V^{-1},\qquad x\simeq4.3\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
Thus this millimetre-scale example requires fine optical <displacement> measurement for volt-level changes. The parametric formula states exactly how the estimate changes with the chosen geometry, liquid and bias.

The displacement is quadratic in <voltage>, cannot distinguish its sign, and has zero first-order sensitivity at zero bias. Smaller gaps and larger <relative permittivity> increase sensitivity, but capillarity, leakage, <dielectric breakdown>, temperature-dependent liquid properties, and finite available rise then limit performance. A nonconducting liquid avoids a sustained conduction load, although charging still draws <electric charge> and leakage is possible. A high <voltage> can give a readable displacement; a low-voltage instrument would require fine displacement measurement and careful calibration.

= Solution

The differential form of <Faraday's law of induction> is
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.
$$
By <Stokes' theorem>, for a stationary oriented circuit it becomes $\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-d\Phi_B/dt$, where $\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S$ is <magnetic flux> and $\mathcal E$ is <electromotive force>. Circuit orientation and surface normal obey the <right-hand rule>. <Lenz's law> is the physical meaning of the minus sign: the induced current's <magnetic field> opposes the change in linked <magnetic flux>. For a moving circuit, the force per unit <electric charge> is $\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$, and its line integral is the corresponding <electromotive force>. Motion through a static <magnetic field> can therefore induce a <voltage> even when $\partial_t\mathbf B=0$.

Let $\theta$ be the wire's angle from the downward vertical, $r=L\sin\theta$, and choose $\boldsymbol\omega$ and $\mathbf B$ upward, as in the figure. The motional term along the wire has radial component $B\omega s$ at cylindrical radius $s$. In steady electrical <equilibrium>, integrating from the grounded axis to the bob gives
$$
V_b=\int_0^rB\omega s\,ds=\frac12B\omega r^2,
\qquad Q=CV_b=\frac12BC\omega r^2.
$$
The <Lorentz force> on this positive <electric charge> is radially outward. Its ratio to the required centripetal term is $QB/(m\omega)\lesssim CB^2L^2/(2m)$, so the stated heavy-bob condition makes its effect on the zero-field <conical pendulum> geometry negligible. With wire tension $T_w$, $T_w\cos\theta=mg$ and $T_w\sin\theta=m\omega^2r$, giving $\cos\theta=g/(\omega^2L)$. Hence the <magnetically charged conical pendulum> has
$$
\boxed{Q_0=\frac12BC\left(\omega L^2-\frac{g^2}{\omega^3}\right).}
$$
A nonzero-radius orbit requires $\omega^2L>g$. Reversing the sense of rotation or the <magnetic field> reverses the sign of $Q_0$.

The response to the added <electric field> requires an electrical convention. In the usual mechanical approximation, treat the pre-existing <electric charge> as $Q_0$, include its upward <electric force> $Q_0E$, and neglect all magnetic-force feedback. Then
$$
\cos\theta(E)=\frac{mg-Q_0E}{m\omega^2L},\qquad
\frac{\delta r}{r}=-\frac{\cos\theta\,\delta(\cos\theta)}{\sin^2\theta}
=\frac{Q_0E g}{m\omega^4r^2}
=\boxed{\frac{CBgE}{2m\omega^3}.}
$$
This predicts an increased radius for $Q_0E>0$. It is the result when the newly applied field is used only as a probe force on the original <electric charge>.

There is a first-order qualification if the bob remains electrically connected to the grounded pivot and the uniform added field's potential is included consistently. Put $s=\cos\theta$ and set the external <electric potential> to zero at the pivot. The external potential at the bob is then $ELs$, so the charging relation and full force balance become
$$
Q=C\!\left[\frac12B\omega L^2(1-s^2)-ELs\right],\qquad
\omega^2Ls+\frac Qm(B\omega Ls+E)=g.
$$
The second equation follows by eliminating the wire tension between vertical balance and radial balance, including the outward magnetic <Lorentz force>. Expanding both equations to first order in $E$, and to leading order in $CB^2L^2/m$, with $s_0=g/(\omega^2L)$, gives
$$
\delta s=-\frac{CBLE}{2m\omega}(1-3s_0^2),\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\delta r}{r}=\frac{CBgE}{2m\omega^3}\,
\frac{1-3s_0^2}{1-s_0^2}.}
$$
The extra factor arises from the added field changing $Q$ by $-CELs_0$ and therefore changing the radial magnetic <force>. That term is linear in $E$ too; the heavy-bob assumption alone does not justify retaining $Q_0E$ while dropping it. The two expressions refer to the two electrical approximations just specified. The expansion requires a small change relative to the initial radius and is nonuniform as that radius tends to zero.

For an electrical measurement, route the grounded connection through a sensitive <electrometer> operated near zero input <voltage>, and integrate the charging <electric current> when $B$ or $\omega$ is changed. Starting with the field off, the transferred <electric charge> is $Q_0$ in this ideal capacitance model. Reversing $B$ separates the induced signal from offsets. Alternatively, isolate the charged bob briefly and measure its <electric potential> with a high-input-impedance <electrometer>, accounting for the instrument's extra <capacitance> and for the fact that isolation changes the electrical boundary condition.

The <characteristic impedance> of a lossless <transmission line> is the ratio of the <voltage> to the current amplitude of a single forward-travelling wave. If $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal C$ denote <inductance> and <capacitance per unit length>, the lossless telegrapher equations give
$$
\partial_z V=-\mathcal L\partial_t I,\qquad
\partial_z I=-\mathcal C\partial_t V,
\qquad \boxed{Z=\sqrt{\mathcal L/\mathcal C}},\qquad
v_p=(\mathcal L\mathcal C)^{-1/2}.
$$
A backward wave has $I_-=-V_-/Z$ when current is positive along $+z$. Thus <characteristic impedance> is not a resistor's <electrical resistance>: it describes travelling-wave amplitudes and determines <impedance matching>, power transfer and reflections. A semi-infinite line, or a line terminated by a matching load, presents that impedance at its input.

At a junction with no lumped branch, <voltage> and <electric current> are continuous:
$$
V_i+V_r=V_t,\qquad \frac{V_i-V_r}{Z_1}=\frac{V_t}{Z_2}.
$$
Eliminating $V_t$ gives the voltage <reflection coefficient>
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{V_r}{V_i}=\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1}.}
$$
For real positive $Z_1$, incident and reflected waves occupy the same line, so their power ratio is $|r|^2$.

For the slightly mismatched <coaxial cables>, the change in <characteristic impedance> is
$$
\Delta Z=\frac{Z_{\rm vac}}{2\pi n}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{\Delta b}b\right)
\simeq\frac{Z_{\rm vac}}{2\pi n}\frac{\Delta b}b.
$$
Consequently
$$
r\simeq\frac{\Delta Z}{2Z}=\frac1{2\ln(b/a)}\frac{\Delta b}b,\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal R\simeq\frac{1}{4\ln^2(b/a)}\left(\frac{\Delta b}b\right)^2.}
$$
The requested power limit $0.01\%=10^{-4}$ implies $|\Delta b|/b<0.02\ln(b/a)$. Since $\ln(b/a)=2\pi nZ/Z_{\rm vac}=1.918$ for the stated line, **the adjacent cables' outer radii must match within approximately $3.84\%$**. If a tolerance is quoted separately as a symmetric manufacturing range $\pm\delta b$ and arbitrary worst-case pairs can differ by $2\delta b$, the corresponding individual tolerance is approximately **$\pm1.92\%$**. The leading calculation is for small mismatches; the exact logarithmic expression gives slightly asymmetric positive and negative limits. The vacuum <wave impedance> used here is approximately $376.73\ \Omega$.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, characteristic impedance of vacuum entry]{ref}

A needle is a shunt <electrical resistance> $R_n$, so the load seen at its location is the continuation of the line in parallel with $R_n$: $Z_L=ZR_n/(Z+R_n)$. The local voltage <reflection coefficient> and transmission amplitude are
$$
\boxed{r=-\frac{Z}{2R_n+Z}\simeq-\frac{Z}{2R_n}},\qquad t=1+r.
$$
For <periodic resistive loading of a transmission line>, the reflection from the next needle travels an additional round trip $2L$ and crosses each previous needle twice. Taking the first needle as phase reference, the <single-return approximation for periodic line reflections> therefore sums to
$$
R_{\rm first}=r\sum_{j=0}^\infty[t^2e^{i\phi}]^j
=\frac r{1-(1+r)^2e^{i\phi}},\qquad
\phi=2kL=\frac{4\pi\nu L}{v_p}.
$$
Here $v_p$ is the propagation <phase velocity> in the cable, $c/n$ for the stated nondispersive nonmagnetic filling. Squaring the amplitude gives the requested expression
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R_{\rm first}(\nu)=\left|\frac r{1-(1+r)^2e^{i\phi(\nu)}}\right|^2.}
$$
For a finite number $N$ of needles the amplitude has the additional numerator $1-[t^2e^{i\phi}]^N$. The displayed semi-infinite result assumes sufficiently many needles, no terminal reflection, and neglects intrinsic propagation loss.

For real $r<0$, write $q=t^2$. The denominator of the power coefficient is $1+q^2-2q\cos\phi$. It has constructive peaks at $\phi=2\pi j$ and minima at $\phi=(2j+1)\pi$, repeating with **frequency spacing $\Delta\nu=v_p/(2L)$**. Within this approximation,
$$
\mathcal R_{\max}=\frac1{(2+r)^2}\simeq\frac14,\qquad
\mathcal R_{\min}=\frac{r^2}{[1+(1+r)^2]^2}\simeq\frac{r^2}{4}.
$$
Weak individual shunts thus give narrow interference peaks, with phase width of order $|r|$.

The requested sum excludes paths that reflect repeatedly between different needles. It should not be read as an exact multiple-scattering result, particularly at the coherent peaks. For example the exact semi-infinite amplitude satisfies
$$
R=r+\frac{t^2e^{i\phi}R}{1-r e^{i\phi}R}.
$$
At $\phi=2\pi j$ this equation reduces to $(R+1)^2=0$: the exact ideal infinite array has unit power reflection there, rather than the first-return approximation's value near $1/4$. This does not change the requested geometric-series derivation, but identifies its physical limitation.

Choose the guide axis as $z$, with $0<x<a$ and $0<y<b$. The <electric field> is along $y$, parallel to the walls' $b$ dimension. Take a homogeneous lossless nonmagnetic <dielectric> with <relative permittivity> $\epsilon$, ideal conducting walls, and time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$. The lowest <transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide> is
$$
\widetilde{\mathbf E}=\widehat{\mathbf y}E_0\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}a\right)e^{i\beta z},\qquad
\beta^2=\frac{\epsilon\omega^2}{c^2}-\frac{\pi^2}{a^2},\qquad \omega=2\pi f.
$$
At $x=0,a$, this tangential <electric field> vanishes. At $y=0,b$ the <electric field> is normal to the wall; a nonzero value is supported by surface <electric charge> and violates no conducting boundary condition. The field has zero <divergence>. The associated normal <magnetic field> also vanishes at each wall, as verified below. The fundamental sine across the wider dimension minimizes the nonzero transverse wave number; $\mathrm{TE}_{00}$ is not a physical propagating mode of a hollow simply connected guide.

Below the <waveguide cutoff frequency> $f_c=c/(2a\sqrt\epsilon)$, put $\beta=i\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$ to select decay for $z>0$. The <evanescent transverse electric mode in a rectangular waveguide> then has
$$
\widetilde E_y=E_0\sin(\pi x/a)e^{-\kappa z},\qquad
\boxed{\ell^{-2}=\kappa^2=\pi^2\!\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac{4\epsilon f^2}{c^2}\right).}
$$
For the fixed <frequency> in the question,
$$
\boxed{\frac\ell a=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{1-\epsilon/4}}\quad(0<\epsilon<4).}
$$
The decay length increases monotonically and diverges as $\epsilon\to4^-$; its formal limit at $\epsilon\to0^+$ is $a/\pi$. At $\epsilon=4$ the mode is at cutoff, with zero axial wave number. For $\epsilon>4$, $\beta=(\pi/a)\sqrt{\epsilon/4-1}$ is real and the mode propagates without axial attenuation in this ideal lossless model. There is no real finite evanescent decay length to continue beyond cutoff. The paper's graph notation $l$ denotes the same $\ell$ as its preceding formula.

\Image[paper-1-waveguide-cutoff.png]
{title=Evanescent decay length and propagating axial wave number on opposite sides of cutoff}

From <Faraday's law of induction>, $\nabla\times\widetilde{\mathbf E}=i\omega\widetilde{\mathbf B}$. Writing $q=\pi/a$ gives
$$
\boxed{\widetilde B_x=-\frac{i\kappa}{\omega}E_0\sin(qx)e^{-\kappa z},\quad
\widetilde B_y=0,\quad
\widetilde B_z=-\frac{iq}{\omega}E_0\cos(qx)e^{-\kappa z}.}
$$
Thus $B_x$ vanishes on the $x$ walls and $B_y$ vanishes on the $y$ walls, meeting the normal magnetic boundary conditions. The fields also satisfy $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. For real $E_0$ the physical fields are $E_y=E_0\sin(qx)e^{-\kappa z}\cos\omega t$, $B_x=-(\kappa/\omega)E_0\sin(qx)e^{-\kappa z}\sin\omega t$, and $B_z=-(q/\omega)E_0\cos(qx)e^{-\kappa z}\sin\omega t$.

The time-averaged <Poynting vector> is
$$
\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac1{2\mu_0}\operatorname{Re}
(\widetilde{\mathbf E}\times\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{\,*})=\mathbf0,
\qquad \boxed{\langle P_z\rangle=0.}
$$
All products are imaginary because the <electric field> and <magnetic field> are in quadrature. A single decaying <evanescent wave> stores reactive <energy> without transmitting average power into a semi-infinite lossless guide. A finite below-cutoff section can transmit power when both independent evanescent solutions are present; that is a different boundary-value problem.

The <skin effect> also gives exponential field decay, but in a good metal $k$ has both real and imaginary parts and there is a real inward mean <Poynting vector>. That flux supplies <Joule heating> $\sigma|\widetilde E|^2/2$. Here attenuation comes from geometric cutoff in a lossless region, with no conductivity and no dissipative power sink; exponential decay alone does not establish absorption.

= Solution
{parent=c10}

For the attractive <inverse-square force>, choose zero <potential energy> at infinity, so $U=-A/r$. At either radial turning point $r_p,r_a$, the <kinetic energy> is purely tangential, with conserved <angular momentum> $J$. Thus
$$
E=\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}-\frac Ar,\qquad
Er^2+Ar-\frac{J^2}{2m}=0.
$$
Its two roots are the periapsis and apoapsis distances. Their sum is $r_p+r_a=-A/E$, whereas the <ellipse> has $r_p+r_a=2a$. Therefore **$a=-A/(2E)$**, with $E<0$ for a nondegenerate bound <ellipse>. The circular case follows by the double-root limit.

For the <two-body problem>, define $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A$, total <mass> $M=m_A+m_B$, <reduced mass> $\mu=m_Am_B/M$, and <center of mass> $\mathbf R$. The relative equation is $\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf F$, while the isolated <center of mass> has $\ddot{\mathbf R}=0$. Once the relative <orbit> is known,
$$
\mathbf r_A=\mathbf R-\frac{m_B}{M}\mathbf r,\qquad
\mathbf r_B=\mathbf R+\frac{m_A}{M}\mathbf r.
$$
The relative <energy> uses $\mu$ in its <kinetic energy> and $A=Gm_Am_B$ in the gravitational <potential energy>.

In the equal-mass case the initial relative <speed> is $u$, $\mu=m/2$, and the center-of-mass <velocity> is half B's initial <velocity>. Thus
$$
E_{\rm rel}=\frac14mu^2-\frac{Gm^2}{a_1}.
$$
Negative relative <energy> requires $u^2<4Gm/a_1$. The **escape threshold is $u_E=2\sqrt{Gm/a_1}$**; equality is marginal parabolic escape, so a strictly bound pair has $u<u_E$.

For $u=u_E/2=\sqrt{Gm/a_1}$, $E_{\rm rel}=-3Gm^2/(4a_1)$ and the relative semimajor axis is $a=2a_1/3$. The initial transverse <speed> is below the relative circular value $\sqrt{2Gm/a_1}$, so the initial turning point is apoapsis. Hence
$$
\boxed{r_p=2a-a_1=\frac{a_1}{3},\qquad e=\frac12.}
$$
Choose the initial separation along $+x$ from A to B, with B's initial <velocity> $+u\widehat{\mathbf y}$. <Conservation of angular momentum> gives relative periapsis <speed> $3u$. At the opposite end of the <ellipse> the direction is $-\widehat{\mathbf y}$, so
$$
\mathbf V_{\rm CM}=\frac u2\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad
\mathbf v_{B,p}=\mathbf V_{\rm CM}+\frac12\mathbf v_{{\rm rel},p}
=\boxed{-u\widehat{\mathbf y}}.
$$
Thus B has the same <speed> as initially but the opposite <velocity>. A has $2u\widehat{\mathbf y}$ at that instant. A value $3u/2$ for B would be its center-of-mass-frame <speed>, not the requested initial laboratory frame result.

The relative <orbital period> follows from <Kepler's third law> with total gravitating <mass> $2m$:
$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(2a_1/3)^3}{2Gm}}
=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt3}\sqrt{\frac{a_1^3}{Gm}}
=\frac{4\pi a_1}{3\sqrt3\,u}.}
$$
In the center-of-mass frame each star follows an <ellipse> of semimajor axis $a_1/3$ and <eccentricity> $1/2$, with the <center of mass> at a focus; they remain opposite on their simultaneous position vectors. In the laboratory frame add the uniform drift $ut/2$ along $y$. The <laboratory paths of an equal-mass binary> are therefore not closed <ellipses>, even though their relative motion repeats.

= Solution
{parent=c11}

In <Lagrangian mechanics>, choose independent <generalized coordinates> $q_i$ compatible with the mechanical <constraints>, write $L=T-U$, and apply the <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.
$$
Equivalently, the physical path makes the <action> stationary under variations with fixed endpoints. For ideal holonomic <constraints>, their unknown reaction forces need not appear in the reduced equations. Direct use of <Newton's laws of motion> instead resolves vector <forces> and reactions and enforces the <constraints> separately. Both approaches describe the same mechanics; the scalar <Lagrangian> is often shorter in curvilinear coordinates. Nonconservative generalized forces must be added separately. Here a drive maintains the prescribed azimuthal <angular velocity> $\Omega$.

The mass has polar <speed> $a\dot\theta$ and azimuthal <speed> $a\Omega\sin\theta$, so its <kinetic energy> is $ma^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)/2$. The elastic <potential energy> is $\mu\theta^2/2$, and the height is $a\cos\theta$, giving gravitational <potential energy> $mga\cos\theta$. Therefore
$$
L=\frac12ma^2\dot\theta^2+\frac12ma^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta
-\frac12\mu\theta^2-mga\cos\theta,
$$
and the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta=\Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta
-\frac\mu{ma^2}\theta+\frac ga\sin\theta.}
$$
For $\mu=2mga$, define $q=\Omega^2a/g$ and $F(\theta)=q\sin\theta\cos\theta-2\theta+\sin\theta$, so $\ddot\theta=(g/a)F(\theta)$. Near zero,
$$
F(\theta)=(q-1)\theta-\left(\frac{2q}{3}+\frac16\right)\theta^3+O(\theta^5).
$$
When $q<1$ the upright <equilibrium> is linearly stable and the <small oscillations> have **$\omega_1^2=g/a-\Omega^2$**.

For $q>1$, the graph of $F$ initially rises, whereas $F(\pi/2)=1-\pi<0$. There must therefore be a zero inside the interval. More strongly, for $0<\theta<\pi/2$,
$$
\frac{F(\theta)}{\sin\theta}=q\cos\theta+1-\frac{2\theta}{\sin\theta}
$$
is strictly decreasing: $\cos\theta$ decreases and $\theta/\sin\theta$ increases because $\sin\theta-\theta\cos\theta>0$. The ratio goes from $q-1>0$ to $1-\pi<0$, giving exactly one zero $\theta_0$ in that interval. At the zero, $F'(\theta_0)<0$, so it is a stable <equilibrium>. The <rotating torsion-stabilized inverted pendulum> also has a symmetric stable angle $-\theta_0$ if both signs of tilt are permitted.

\Image[paper-1-rotating-pendulum.png]
{title=Acceleration curves after the upright instability and the quartic potential at the threshold}

At $\theta_0=\pi/4$, setting $F(\theta_0)=0$ gives $q/2-\pi/2+1/\sqrt2=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\sqrt{\frac ga(\pi-\sqrt2)}.}
$$
For $\theta=\theta_0+\xi$, the linearized equation is $\ddot\xi=(g/a)F'(\theta_0)\xi$. Since $F'=q\cos2\theta-2+\cos\theta$,
$$
\boxed{\omega_2^2=\frac ga\left(2-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
At the threshold $q=1$ the linear restoring term vanishes, but the cubic term remains:
$$
\ddot\theta=-\frac{5g}{6a}\theta^3+O(\theta^5),\qquad
U_{\rm eff}(\theta)-U_{\rm eff}(0)=\frac{5mga}{24}\theta^4+O(\theta^6).
$$
**The upright state remains nonlinearly stable, with no nonzero infinitesimal harmonic frequency.** Small finite disturbances produce <quartic oscillator> motion whose <frequency> is proportional to its small angular <amplitude>; its <period> tends to infinity as that amplitude tends to zero. Zero linear stiffness is not the same as an absent restoring <torque>.

= Solution
{parent=12}

Use positive <normal stress> for tension. On a small rectangular material element, $\tau_i$ is the normal force per unit area on the face perpendicular to axis $i$, and $e_i=\Delta\ell_i/\ell_i$ is the corresponding <normal strain>. <Young's modulus> $E$ is the ratio of axial <normal stress> to axial <strain> in uniaxial stress; <Poisson's ratio> $\sigma$ is minus the transverse-to-axial strain ratio in the same test. Thus isotropic <linear elasticity> gives $Ee_1=\tau_1-\sigma\tau_2-\sigma\tau_3$ and cyclic permutations. The stress symbols here follow the question; $\sigma$ denotes <Poisson's ratio>, not a stress component.

\Image[paper-1-elastic-layer.png]
{title=Normal stresses on a material element and the distinct deformation fields in twist and tilt}

For $e_2=e_3=0$, the two transverse compliance equations imply $\tau_2=\tau_3=\sigma\tau_1/(1-\sigma)$. Substitution into the first gives
$$
Ee_1=\frac{(1+\sigma)(1-2\sigma)}{1-\sigma}\tau_1,\qquad
\boxed{M=\frac{E(1-\sigma)}{(1+\sigma)(1-2\sigma)}.}
$$
This <uniaxial strain modulus> is greater than the uniaxial-stress <Young's modulus> for the positive <Poisson's ratio> of interest, because the lateral <constraints> prevent free Poisson contraction.

For a small twist $\phi$, the bonded faces' relative tangential displacement at radius $r$ is $r\phi$. The layer's <shear strain> is $r\phi/h$, so the <shear stress> is $Gr\phi/h$. Integrating the <torque> gives
$$
T_V=-\frac{G\phi}h\int_A r^2\,dA
=-\frac{G\phi}h\int_0^a2\pi r^3dr
=\boxed{-\frac\pi2\frac{Ga^4}h\phi.}
$$
The minus sign denotes a restoring <torque>; the question quotes its magnitude. This uses the disk's <polar second moment of area>.

For tilt about H, choose $y$ in the plate perpendicular to H. The vertical displacement of the bonded top face is $y\theta$ to first order, giving <normal strain> $y\theta/h$. The thin layer is constrained laterally by bonding, so its leading normal traction is $My\theta/h$, not $Ey\theta/h$. Integrating its moment about H gives
$$
T_H=-\frac{M\theta}h\int_Ay^2dA
=\boxed{-\frac\pi4\frac{Ma^4}h\theta,}
$$
since the diametric <second moment of area> is $\pi a^4/4$. This is the specified thin-layer approximation to the <torsion and tilt stiffness of a bonded elastic disk>. Relaxation near the free cylindrical edge is neglected; for a nearly incompressible layer it can limit quantitative accuracy unless the thinness is sufficient.

The thin square plate's <moment of inertia> about V is $I_V=2mb^2/3$. Its two perpendicular in-plane central moments are equal and sum to $I_V$, so the diagonal H has $I_H=mb^2/3$. Dividing the two angular stiffnesses by the appropriate <moments of inertia> gives
$$
\boxed{\omega_V=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi Ga^4}{4hmb^2}},\qquad
\omega_H=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi Ma^4}{4hmb^2}}.}
$$
The geometry factors cancel in their ratio. Using the supplied relation between <shear modulus> and <Young's modulus>,
$$
R^2=\left(\frac{\omega_H}{\omega_V}\right)^2=\frac MG
=\frac{2(1-\sigma)}{1-2\sigma},\qquad
\boxed{\sigma=\frac{R^2-2}{2(R^2-1)}=0.4919\quad(R=7.9).}
$$
The result lies just below the incompressible isotropic limit $1/2$, as expected for a rubber with a large compression stiffness compared with its <shear modulus>. It is an inference within the bonded-layer model, rather than an independently measured material constant.

= Solution
{parent=13}

For a <Newtonian fluid> with <velocity> $u(z)\widehat{\mathbf x}$, $\tau=\eta\,\partial_z u$ is the $x$ component of traction on a plane whose normal points along $+z$. The opposite face has the opposite traction. The coefficient $\eta$ is <dynamic viscosity>, with units $\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Faster layers transfer positive $x$ <momentum> toward slower neighbours; the internal <shear stress> resists relative sliding.

\Image[paper-1-viscous-flow.png]
{title=Shear-traction convention, Couette-Poiseuille profiles, and oscillatory viscous penetration}

Take a thin fluid element with horizontal area $A$ and thickness $dz$. The difference between its upper and lower viscous tractions is $A\eta u_{zz}dz$. The <pressure gradient> supplies $-A p_xdz$. Incompressibility is satisfied because $u$ has no $x$ dependence, and the convective <acceleration> vanishes: $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=u\partial_x\mathbf u=0$. Equating net force to <mass> times <acceleration> therefore gives the relevant component of the <Navier-Stokes equation>:
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}
+\eta\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial z^2}.}
$$
For steady flow, integrate $\eta u''=p'$ twice and impose the <no-slip boundary condition> at each plate, $u(0)=0$ and $u(a)=u_0$. The <Couette-Poiseuille flow> profile is
$$
\boxed{u(z)=u_0\frac za+\frac{p'}{2\eta}z(z-a).}
$$
The linear term is <Couette flow> and the parabolic term is <plane Poiseuille flow>. A negative $p'$ drives flow in $+x$. Integrating the profile across the gap gives the volumetric flow per unit width,
$$
\boxed{q=\int_0^au(z)\,dz=\frac{u_0a}{2}-\frac{p'a^3}{12\eta}.}
$$
It has units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$; multiplying by the plate width gives the actual volume per time.

With zero <pressure gradient>, substituting $u=\operatorname{Re}[A e^{i(kz-\omega t)}]$ gives $-i\rho\omega=-\eta k^2$. Select the root that decays into fluid occupying $z>0$:
$$
\boxed{k=(1+i)\sqrt{\frac{\rho\omega}{2\eta}}=\frac{1+i}{\delta},\qquad
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\rho\omega}}.}
$$
The other sign applies to decay in the opposite half-space. With a real wall velocity amplitude $U_0$, the established periodic solution of <Stokes' second problem> is
$$
u(z,t)=U_0e^{-z/\delta}\cos(z/\delta-\omega t).
$$
The oscillation's <amplitude> decays exponentially and its phase lags with distance from the plate. This is diffusion of tangential <momentum>, not a freely propagating undamped elastic wave. Initial transients must have decayed before this purely periodic expression applies.

The fluid-displacement amplitude and the wall-displacement amplitude have the same ratio as their velocity amplitudes, since both are divided by the common $\omega$. Thus for the specified amplitudes,
$$
\frac{0.002}{0.10}=e^{-z/\delta},\qquad
\boxed{z=\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\rho(2\pi f)}}\ln50.}
$$
Using the standard approximate water <density> $\rho\simeq10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, explicitly an approximation rather than another value printed on the paper, gives $\delta=0.564\ \mathrm{mm}$ and **$z\simeq2.21\ \mathrm{mm}$ from either face of a sufficiently large plate**. The <viscous penetration depth> varies as $\rho^{-1/2}$ if a more precise water <density> is used. The ideal parallel-flow solution ignores finite plate edges and tank walls.
