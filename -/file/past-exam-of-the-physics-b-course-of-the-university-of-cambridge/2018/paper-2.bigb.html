For the usual hollow <rectangular electromagnetic waveguide>, let the propagation axis be $z$ and the interior be $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$. A <transverse magnetic polarization> has $B_z=0$ and $E_z\ne0$. At a <perfect conductor> wall the tangential <electric field> vanishes. Since $E_z$ is tangential to all four side walls, it satisfies a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition> around the rectangle.

Separating the transverse <Helmholtz equation> therefore gives the <transverse magnetic mode of a rectangular waveguide>
$$
E_z=E_0\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)\sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{b}\right)e^{i(k_z z-\omega t)},
\qquad n,m=1,2,\ldots.
$$
A zero value of either index makes $E_z$ identically zero, rather than producing a nonzero field uniform along that direction. The transverse electric and <magnetic fields> of this TM family are obtained from transverse <derivatives> of $E_z$, so they also vanish. Thus **both TM indices must be nonzero: neither $\mathrm{TM}_{n0}$ nor $\mathrm{TM}_{0m}$ exists** in this rectangular hollow-guide model. In contrast, transverse electric modes obey a <Neumann boundary condition> on $B_z$ and may have one zero index. The claim is about this <waveguide> geometry, not all possible guides.

Use absolute <temperatures> $T_c=278.15\,\mathrm K$ and $T_h=298.15\,\mathrm K$. Let $\dot Q_c$ be the <heat> extracted from the ground, $\dot Q_h$ the <heat> delivered indoors, and $P$ the electrical input <power>. For a reversible <heat pump>, the <entropy> transferred per unit time balances:
$$
\frac{\dot Q_h}{T_h}=\frac{\dot Q_c}{T_c},
\qquad \dot Q_h=\dot Q_c+P.
$$
Consequently the <coefficient of performance> is
$$
\mathrm{COP}_{\rm heating}=\frac{\dot Q_h}{P}
=\frac{T_h}{T_h-T_c}=\frac{298.15}{20}=14.9075.
$$
The requested <heat> delivery is a rate, since the duration is unspecified:
$$
\boxed{\dot Q_h\simeq14.9\,\mathrm{kW}.}
$$
Of this, $13.9\,\mathrm{kW}$ comes from the ground and $1.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ from the electricity. A <coefficient of performance> greater than one is consistent with <conservation of energy> because the <heat pump> transports <heat> as well as converting electrical <energy>.

Let the particle have <mass> $m$, and assume its <force> is derived from a time-independent <potential energy> $V(x)$ during the stated interval. Differentiation gives
$$
\dot x=nAt^{n-1},\qquad \ddot x=n(n-1)At^{n-2}.
$$
For $n\ne0$, choose a branch on which $t=(x/A)^{1/n}$ is real. <Conservation of energy> is the quickest integration of <Newton's second law>:
$$
\frac12m\dot x^2+V(x)=E,
\qquad
\boxed{V(x)=E-\frac{mn^2A^2}{2}\left(\frac{x}{A}\right)^{2-2/n}.}
$$
The additive constant $E$ is arbitrary. For instance, the displayed expression applies directly for $t>0$, $A\ne0$ and $x/A>0$, without raising a possibly negative $A$ to a fractional power. Its <derivative> gives $-V'(x)=mn(n-1)At^{n-2}$ along the trajectory, as required. The second term has units of <kinetic energy> because $A$ has units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-n}}$.

For $n=1$, the <velocity> is constant, so the <potential energy> is constant on the visited interval; the expression above reduces to such a constant. For $n=0$, or $A=0$, the particle is stationary: the trajectory only fixes $V'$ to vanish at its position and does not determine the <potential energy> elsewhere. These exceptional cases cannot determine a unique nonconstant function.

Model air as a dilute <ideal gas> of <molecules> described by the <hard sphere> model. The <number density> and <mean free path> are
$$
n=\frac{p}{k_BT},\qquad \lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,n\pi d^2}
=\frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2p}.
$$
The factor $\sqrt2$ accounts for the relative motion of the colliding <molecules>. A molecular <collision> diameter has to be estimated because none is supplied. Taking $d\simeq0.37\,\mathrm{nm}$, appropriate as an order-of-magnitude size for the main constituents of air, gives
$$
n=\frac{10^5}{(1.380649\times10^{-23})(298.15)}
\simeq2.43\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},
\qquad
\lambda\simeq6.8\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Thus **the <mean free path> is about $7\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m$, or $70\,\mathrm{nm}$**. A slightly smaller assumed diameter $0.35\,\mathrm{nm}$ gives about $76\,\mathrm{nm}$, illustrating the intended precision of the estimate.

The appropriate quantity to minimize is the <energy> including exchange of particles with the <electron> reservoir, $\mathcal E=E-\mu N$. The <occupation of an interacting localized electron level> follows from
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
N&E&\text{number of spin states}&E-\mu N\\\hline
0&0&1&0\\
1&\epsilon&2&\epsilon-\mu\\
2&2\epsilon+U&1&2\epsilon+U-2\mu
\end{array}
$$
**For repulsion $U>0$**, the first <electron> costs $\epsilon$ and the second costs $\epsilon+U$. Comparing all three grand <energies> gives
$$
\boxed{N(\mu)=\begin{cases}0,&\mu<\epsilon,\\1,&\epsilon<\mu<\epsilon+U,\\2,&\mu>\epsilon+U.\end{cases}}
$$
**For attraction $U<0$**, the singly occupied state never minimizes the grand <energy>. At the crossing of the empty and doubly occupied states, $\mu_*=\epsilon+U/2$, its grand <energy> is $-U/2>0$, while both competing states have zero grand <energy>. Hence
$$
\boxed{N(\mu)=\begin{cases}0,&\mu<\epsilon+U/2,\\2,&\mu>\epsilon+U/2.\end{cases}}
$$
For $U=0$ the two thresholds coincide at $\mu=\epsilon$, giving a direct $0\to2$ step. At a threshold, distinct occupations are degenerate; zero-<temperature> minimization alone does not select a unique occupation. A <grand canonical ensemble> at small positive <temperature> smooths the steps, with
$$
\Xi=1+2e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+e^{-\beta(2\epsilon+U-2\mu)},\qquad
\langle N\rangle=\frac{2e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+2e^{-\beta(2\epsilon+U-2\mu)}}{\Xi}.
$$
In particular, the equal-weight empty/pair limit for $U<0$ gives <mean> occupation one exactly at its crossing; this is not a finite-width singly occupied plateau.

**<Magnetic field> and <force>.** A steady <electric current> produces a <magnetic field>. In <vacuum> the <Biot-Savart law> gives
$$
\mathbf B_1(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I_1}{4\pi}
\int_{C_1}\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell_1\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_1)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_1|^3}.
$$
The <Lorentz force> on the charge carriers transfers <force> to the material carrying them. The resulting <force> on a current element of another circuit is $d\mathbf F_2=I_2\,d\boldsymbol\ell_2\times\mathbf B_1$, or, for a continuous current distribution, the magnetic <force> per unit <volume (thermodynamics)> is $\mathbf f=\mathbf J\times\mathbf B$.

For two long parallel straight wires a distance $r$ apart, <Ampère's law> gives $B_1=\mu_0 I_1/(2\pi r)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{F}{L}=\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi r}.}
$$
The <force> is **attractive for parallel currents and repulsive for opposite currents**, by the <cross product> rule. Its units are $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. This approximation neglects wire thickness and end effects; a complete circuit includes its return current. Element-by-element <forces> need not form an isolated Newton's-third-law pair, but complete stationary circuits have balanced mutual <forces> when the entire system is included.

**<Energy> description.** For two linear coupled circuits the <magnetic energy> is
$$
W_B=\frac12L_1I_1^2+\frac12L_2I_2^2+M(q)I_1I_2.
$$
Here $q$ is a mechanical coordinate and $M$ the signed <mutual inductance>. At prescribed currents, the generalized <force> is $I_1I_2\,\partial M/\partial q$ if the self-inductances do not vary. This positive <derivative> is obtained from <magnetic coenergy>, or by including the electrical sources' <work>; differentiating $W_B$ with a minus sign at fixed currents would omit that source <work>. At prescribed flux linkages the <force> instead equals the negative <derivative> of stored <magnetic energy> with those linkages fixed. The geometry thus determines both <inductance> and mechanical <force>.

**<Integral> conservation.** <Electric charge> cannot be created by ordinary transport. For a fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$, outward current reduces the enclosed charge:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V\rho_e\,dV=-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf J\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dA.
$$
Here $\rho_e$ has units $\mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}$ and $\mathbf J$ has units $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$. Applying the <divergence theorem> to an arbitrary <volume (thermodynamics)> gives the local <continuity equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\rho_e}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0.}
$$
It is local <charge conservation>: a net outflow must be accompanied by depletion. In a steady state, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$. Integrating over a small circuit junction then gives <Kirchhoff's current law>. During a transient the junction can accumulate charge and the incoming and outgoing conduction currents need not yet match.

**Consistency with <Maxwell's equations>.** In <vacuum>, take the <divergence> of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E,
\qquad
0=\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E).
$$
<Gauss's law>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_e/\epsilon_0$, reproduces the <continuity equation>. The <displacement current> term makes these equations consistent for time-dependent charge distributions, such as a charging <capacitor>. It is not a stream of free charges across the insulating gap. In matter, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D$ give the analogous <conservation law> for <free charge>; total charge also includes <bound charge>.

An <electrical transformer> transfers alternating electrical <power> between windings through a shared changing <magnetic flux>. A core with large <magnetic permeability> confines most of the flux, so both windings link approximately the same flux $\Phi(t)$. <Faraday's law> gives induced electromotive <forces>
$$
\mathcal E_p=-N_p\frac{d\Phi}{dt},\qquad
\mathcal E_s=-N_s\frac{d\Phi}{dt}.
$$
For an ideal <electrical transformer>, negligible winding resistance and leakage flux imply the terminal-voltage magnitude ratio
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p}.}
$$
The relative phase depends on the winding sense and terminal convention. At a loaded secondary, Lenz's law makes the secondary current oppose the flux change. The primary draws an additional current which approximately cancels the secondary magnetomotive <force>: $N_pI_p\simeq N_sI_s$ in magnitude when the magnetizing current is negligible. Consequently
$$
\boxed{V_pI_p=V_sI_s,\qquad \frac{I_s}{I_p}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}}
$$
for a resistive load, using <root mean square> values. More generally the ideal device conserves instantaneous <power>, with the appropriate current signs. A load impedance $Z_L$ appears at the primary as $Z_{\rm in}=(N_p/N_s)^2Z_L$, enabling <impedance matching> and electrical isolation.

For sinusoidal flux, $\Phi=\Phi_{\max}\sin\omega t$ gives $V_{\rm rms}=\omega N\Phi_{\max}/\sqrt2$. At a given supply voltage, reducing <frequency> increases the required peak flux and can drive the core into <magnetic saturation>. Real losses include winding <Joule heating>, magnetic <hysteresis>, <eddy currents> and leakage flux; a laminated core suppresses eddy-current loops. A steady <direct current> produces no continuing secondary electromotive <force>, while its small winding resistance can allow a damaging primary current after switching transients.

The absence of magnetic monopoles is expressed by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. In a regular simply connected region, a magnetic <vector> potential may be introduced so that
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.}
$$
Its units are $\mathrm{T\,m}=\mathrm{Wb\,m^{-1}}$. It is not unique: adding a gradient leaves the <magnetic field> unchanged because $\nabla\times\nabla\chi=0$. In time-dependent electromagnetism,
$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A,
\qquad
\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\quad
\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi
$$
is a <gauge transformation> preserving both physical fields. <Stokes theorem> gives $\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S=\Phi_B$ for a contour bounding an appropriate surface.

For localized steady currents, choose the <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Substitution into <Ampère's law> and the curl-of-curl identity gives
$$
-\nabla^2\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf J,
\qquad
\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'.
$$
<Boundary conditions> are needed in addition to the gauge choice. For a uniform <magnetic field> one convenient choice is $\mathbf A=\tfrac12\mathbf B\times\mathbf r$. For a magnetic dipole, $\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf m\times\mathbf r/(4\pi r^3)$ outside its source. The magnetic <vector> potential simplifies field calculations and appears directly in the <Lagrangian> of a charged particle, $L=\tfrac12m v^2+q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A-q\phi$. In multiply connected regions, a field-free region can retain nontrivial circulation of $\mathbf A$, relevant to the <Aharonov-Bohm effect>; a local gauge argument cannot remove every global circulation.

**From molecular dipoles to macroscopic fields.** A <dielectric> contains charges which remain bound to its atoms or <molecules>. An applied <electric field> displaces positive and negative charge centres and can orient permanent <electric dipoles>. The <electric polarization> $\mathbf P$ is <electric dipole moment> per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>, in $\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}$. Its associated <bound charge> is
$$
\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\qquad \sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}
$$
at the boundary of the polarized material, where $\widehat{\mathbf n}$ points outward. A uniform <electric polarization> has no <volume (thermodynamics)> <bound charge>, but generally has surface <bound charge>. The <electric field> remains the <force> per unit test charge; it includes the fields of both free and bound charges.

Introducing the <electric displacement field> $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P$ packages the free-charge source separately:
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho_f+\rho_b}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E=0.
$$
For a homogeneous linear isotropic <dielectric>,
$$
\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad \epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r.
$$
The <electric susceptibility> $\chi_e$ is dimensionless; the <permittivity> $\epsilon$ has units $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$. This is a constitutive approximation. An anisotropic <dielectric> requires a <permittivity tensor>, while a nonlinear <dielectric> need not have <electric polarization> proportional to <electric field>. The field in these equations is the macroscopic field, not necessarily the <local field> at an individual <molecule>.

**Interfaces.** Let $\widehat{\mathbf n}$ point from medium 1 into medium 2. Integrating <Gauss's law> over a thin pillbox, and the electrostatic <curl> equation around a thin loop, gives the <electrostatic interface boundary conditions>
$$
\boxed{\widehat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f,
\qquad \widehat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=0.}
$$
Thus normal electric <displacement> is continuous when there is no free surface charge, whereas tangential <electric field> is continuous even if the <permittivity> changes. Normal <electric field> generally jumps because the interface carries <bound charge>. The electric potential is continuous for an ordinary interface without an ideal dipole sheet. These conditions, together with <Laplace's equation> in each homogeneous charge-free region, determine the field.

**<Energy> and electrical sources.** Reversibly assembling <free charge> requires incremental <work> $\delta W=\int\mathbf E\cdot\delta\mathbf D\,dV$. For a linear <dielectric> whose response can be reversibly integrated from zero field, the <electrostatic energy> is
$$
\boxed{W=\frac12\int\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D\,dV.}
$$
This includes the material polarization response, rather than simply using $\epsilon_0E^2/2$ everywhere. In a <capacitor>, $W=Q^2/(2C)=CV^2/2$. Inserting a <dielectric> increases <capacitance>. At fixed charge the stored <energy> decreases. At fixed voltage the stored field <energy> increases, but the voltage source supplies $V\,dQ$; the appropriate mechanical <energy> including that source changes by $-V^2dC/2$. Both cases predict attraction of <dielectric> into the <capacitor>. A <force> calculation must state which electrical quantity is fixed. A lossy or hysteretic <dielectric> also dissipates <energy>, so the reversible quadratic expression does not describe its full history.

**Shape and depolarization.** Suppose the <dielectric> is surrounded by <vacuum> and the distant imposed <electric field> is $\mathbf E_0$. Bound surface charge produces a <depolarization field>. For a uniformly polarized <ellipsoid>, along a <principal axis>,
$$
E_{\rm in}=E_0-\frac{N_dP}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
P=\epsilon_0(\epsilon_r-1)E_{\rm in},
\qquad
\boxed{E_{\rm in}=\frac{E_0}{1+N_d(\epsilon_r-1)}.}
$$
Here $N_d$ is the <depolarization factor of a dielectric ellipsoid>. This self-consistent field explains why the same material responds differently in different shapes.

An infinite slab with <electric field> normal to its faces has $N_d=1$, giving $E_{\rm in}=E_0/\epsilon_r$; its opposite surface bound charges reduce the internal field. For <electric field> parallel to the faces, $N_d=0$, giving $E_{\rm in}=E_0$. End effects are neglected.

A very long circular rod in an axial <electric field> has negligible depolarization away from its ends, so $E_{\rm in}\simeq E_0$. In a transverse <electric field> the infinite-cylinder solution has $N_d=1/2$, giving $E_{\rm in}=2E_0/(\epsilon_r+1)$. To see the latter directly, take the applied field along $x$. The interior and exterior electric potentials can be written $\phi_{\rm in}=-B r\cos\theta$ and $\phi_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+A\cos\theta/r$. Continuity of potential and normal electric <displacement> at $r=a$ gives $B=2E_0/(\epsilon_r+1)$.

For a <sphere> of radius $a$, $N_d=1/3$ and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm in}=\frac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}\mathbf E_0,
\qquad \mathbf p=4\pi\epsilon_0a^3\frac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\mathbf E_0.}
$$
For example, write $\phi_{\rm in}=-B r\cos\theta$ and $\phi_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+C\cos\theta/r^2$. Continuity of potential gives $B=E_0-C/a^3$, and continuity of normal electric <displacement> gives $\epsilon B=\epsilon_0(E_0+2C/a^3)$. Solving yields $B=3E_0/(\epsilon_r+2)$ and $C=a^3E_0(\epsilon_r-1)/(\epsilon_r+2)$. The $1/r^2$ potential is that of the <electric dipole moment> above. For $\epsilon_r=1$ all these induced effects vanish; as $\epsilon_r$ becomes very large the normal-field <sphere> and slab approach the corresponding conductor field exclusion. The ideal infinitely long rod in an exactly axial imposed field has a different, singular end-free limit.

A <Hohmann transfer orbit> connects two coplanar circular orbits using two tangential, effectively instantaneous <velocity> changes. Take outward transfer radii $r_2>r_1$, and write $\mu_g=GM$ for the central body's <gravitational parameter>. The intervening ellipse has <periapsis> $r_1$, <apoapsis> $r_2$, <semimajor axis> $a_t=(r_1+r_2)/2$ and <orbital eccentricity> $e=(r_2-r_1)/(r_1+r_2)$.

The circular speed is $\sqrt{\mu_g/r}$. Conservation of orbital <energy> gives the <vis-viva equation> $v^2=\mu_g(2/r-1/a_t)$. Therefore the transfer speeds at the two impulses are
$$
v_{t1}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu_g r_2}{r_1(r_1+r_2)}},\qquad
v_{t2}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu_g r_1}{r_2(r_1+r_2)}}.
$$
The first prograde impulse raises the far side of the orbit; the second prograde impulse raises the near side to circularize at $r_2$. Their magnitudes are
$$
\boxed{\Delta v_1=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_g}{r_1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2r_2}{r_1+r_2}}-1\right),
\quad
\Delta v_2=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_g}{r_2}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2r_1}{r_1+r_2}}\right).}
$$
The transfer takes half an ellipse period. <Kepler's third law> gives
$$
\boxed{t_t=\pi\sqrt{\frac{[(r_1+r_2)/2]^3}{\mu_g}}.}
$$
For inward transfer the same ellipse is traversed from <apoapsis> to <periapsis> and both burns are retrograde. A rendezvous also needs the correct initial phase of the target: for outward transfer its initial angular lead is $\pi-\sqrt{\mu_g/r_2^3}\,t_t$ modulo $2\pi$. The method assumes a two-body <gravitational field>, negligible atmospheric <drag> and burns short compared with an orbit. It is an economical two-impulse transfer between these circles; it is not universally the least-$\Delta v$ transfer if additional impulses and very large intermediate radii are permitted.

Let a coordinate frame rotate with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$ relative to an <inertial frame>. For any <vector> $\mathbf u$,
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf u}{dt}\right)_{\rm inertial}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf u}{dt}\right)_{\rm rotating}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u.
$$
Applying this twice to position, with coincident nonaccelerating origins, gives
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm inertial}=\mathbf a_{\rm rotating}
+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rotating}
+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)
+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r.
$$
<Newton's second law> written using rotating coordinates therefore contains the <Coriolis force>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_C=-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rotating}.}
$$
The other inertial corrections are the <centrifugal force> and, for changing <rotation> rate, the <Euler force>. An accelerating origin introduces an additional translational <fictitious force>.

The <Coriolis force> depends on <velocity> and is perpendicular to it, so $\mathbf F_C\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm rotating}=0$: **the <Coriolis force> does no <work> in the <rotating frame>**. It bends trajectories without directly changing their rotating-frame speed. It is absent for motion parallel to the <rotation> axis.

For locally horizontal motion on Earth at latitude $\lambda$, take $x$ east, $y$ north and $z$ up. Keeping the vertical component $\Omega\sin\lambda$ gives
$$
\dot v_x=fv_y,\qquad \dot v_y=-fv_x,
\qquad f=2\Omega\sin\lambda.
$$
Horizontal motion is deflected right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern hemisphere. In the ideal absence of other horizontal <forces> these equations give inertial circles with <angular frequency> $|f|$. The same velocity-dependent correction explains the <precession> of a <Foucault pendulum>, whose precession-rate magnitude is $\Omega|\sin\lambda|$. Terrestrial vertical motions also involve Earth's horizontal <rotation> component, so the reduced horizontal equations are not the full <force> law.

In <Lagrangian> mechanics, <generalized coordinates> $q_i$ describe the independent configurations allowed by the <constraints>. For particles with ordinary conservative <forces> in an <inertial frame>, use $L=T-V$, with <kinetic energy> $T$ and <potential energy> $V$. <Holonomic constraints> can often be built into the coordinates, avoiding explicit <constraint> <forces>.

The <principle of stationary action> makes the <action> $\mathcal S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt$ stationary for variations vanishing at the endpoints. Integrating the <velocity> variation by parts gives
$$
\delta\mathcal S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum_i
\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\right)\delta q_i\,dt.
$$
Since each independent variation is arbitrary, the <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
If nonconservative <generalized forces> are present, their components appear on the right. The formalism is invariant under suitable coordinate changes and adding a total time <derivative> to $L$ leaves the fixed-endpoint equations unchanged.

The <generalized momentum> is $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$. If $q_i$ is cyclic, $\partial L/\partial q_i=0$ and $p_i$ is conserved. If $L$ has no explicit time dependence, the conserved <energy> function is
$$
E_L=\sum_i\dot q_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-L.
$$
For a <kinetic energy> quadratic in <velocities> and a velocity-independent <potential energy>, this equals $T+V$. This connects <symmetry> to <conservation laws>.

For example, a <simple pendulum> of <mass> $m$ and length $\ell$ has
$$
L=\frac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2-mg\ell(1-\cos\theta),
\qquad \ddot\theta+\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta=0.
$$
Its <tension> does not appear because the fixed-length <constraint> is already imposed. Expanding for small $\theta$ gives <simple harmonic motion> with $\omega=\sqrt{g/\ell}$. More generally, expansion of $T$ and $V$ to quadratic order about a <stable equilibrium> leads directly to the normal-mode <eigenvalue> problem.

For a slender <elastic beam> undergoing small bending, assume plane cross-sections remain plane and normal to the <neutral axis>. Fibres a signed distance $z$ from that axis have longitudinal <strain> $\varepsilon_{xx}=-z/R$, where $R$ is the local <radius of curvature>. <Hooke's law> gives <stress> $\sigma_{xx}=-Ez/R$, where $E$ is <Young's modulus>. Zero resultant axial <force> places the <neutral axis> through the section's <centroid> for a homogeneous beam.

The <bending moment> is the moment of this <stress> distribution. Its magnitude is
$$
\boxed{M=\frac{EI_A}{R},\qquad I_A=\int_{\rm section}z^2\,dA.}
$$
The <second moment of area> $I_A$ has units $\mathrm{m^4}$, and the <bending stiffness> $EI_A$ has units $\mathrm{N\,m^2}$. A rectangular section of width $b$ and depth $h$ has $I_A=bh^3/12$, so placing more material far from the <neutral axis> strongly resists bending. This is the purpose of an I-section.

For small deflection $y(x)$, the signed <curvature> is approximately $y''$. A consistent variational sign convention follows from the <elastic energy> and a transverse load $q(x)$:
$$
\Pi[y]=\frac12\int EI_A(y'')^2\,dx-\int qy\,dx,
\qquad \boxed{(EI_Ay'')''=q.}
$$
For constant <bending stiffness> this is the <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> $EI_Ay''''=q$. A clamped end fixes $y=y'=0$; a simply supported end has $y=0$ and zero <bending moment>; an unloaded free end has zero moment and shear, $y''=y'''=0$ for uniform $EI_A$. Endpoint loads replace the corresponding zero-force <boundary conditions>.

For a cantilever of length $L$ carrying a tip <force> $F$, choose positive deflection along $F$. The internal bending gives $EI_Ay''=F(L-x)$, with $y(0)=y'(0)=0$. Two integrations give
$$
y(x)=\frac{Fx^2(3L-x)}{6EI_A},
\qquad \boxed{y(L)=\frac{FL^3}{3EI_A}.}
$$
This approximation requires small slopes, elastic strains and a length large compared with section dimensions. Shear deformation, rotary inertia, plastic yielding and large <rotations> require extensions. Adding inertia gives <flexural waves> with $\omega^2=(EI_A/\rho_m A_s)k^4$ for a uniform beam, where $\rho_m A_s$ is <mass> per unit length.

= Solution

**Definition and equations.** A <normal mode> is a motion in which every component of a <linear system> has the same time dependence with fixed amplitude ratios and phases. For a conservative system near equilibrium, the most useful form is
$$
\mathbf q(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{a\,\mathbf u\,e^{-i\omega t}\}.
$$
The <vector> $\mathbf u$ describes the <mode shape>, while $\omega$ is its <angular frequency>. Linear combinations of modes describe arbitrary sufficiently small motions when the modes form a complete <basis>.

Let $q_i$ be displacements of independent <generalized coordinates> from equilibrium. Expand the <Lagrangian> to quadratic order:
$$
L=\frac12\dot{\mathbf q}^{\mathsf T}M\dot{\mathbf q}
-\frac12\mathbf q^{\mathsf T}K\mathbf q,
\qquad M\ddot{\mathbf q}+K\mathbf q=0.
$$
The <mass matrix> $M$ defines a <positive-definite quadratic form> on the independent coordinates, while the <stiffness matrix> $K$ is the <Hessian> of the <potential energy>. Substituting a mode gives the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations>
$$
\boxed{K\mathbf u=\omega^2M\mathbf u,
\qquad \det(K-\omega^2M)=0.}
$$
A practical calculation identifies the equilibrium, chooses independent coordinates, computes the quadratic kinetic and potential <energies>, solves for the <eigenvalues> and then obtains their <eigenvectors>. One can equivalently diagonalize the real symmetric <matrix> $M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}$. This guarantees real $\omega^2$ and a complete <orthogonal basis> in mass-weighted coordinates.

For distinct <eigenvalues>, the fact that $K$ and $M$ are real <symmetric matrices> implies
$$
(\omega_i^2-\omega_j^2)\mathbf u_j^{\mathsf T}M\mathbf u_i=0.
$$
Thus distinct modes are <orthogonal> in the <inner product> weighted by the <mass matrix>. Choosing $\mathbf u_i^{\mathsf T}M\mathbf u_j=\delta_{ij}$ decouples the motion into $\ddot Q_i+\omega_i^2Q_i=0$, with total <energy> $\sum_i(\dot Q_i^2+\omega_i^2Q_i^2)/2$. A <positive-definite matrix> $K$ gives positive <eigenvalues> and oscillatory stability. Zero modes can describe rigid translations or <rotations> without restoring <force>; a negative <eigenvalue> gives exponential instability rather than an oscillatory mode.

**<Symmetry>.** A <symmetry> preserving both quadratic <energies> maps modes into modes of the same <frequency>. Coordinates adapted to <symmetry> divide the <eigenvalue> problem into smaller independent blocks. For two identical subsystems, exchanging them gives symmetric and antisymmetric modes. The <degeneracy of normal modes> means that independent modes share one <frequency>. Higher-dimensional <group representations> of a <symmetry> can enforce such degeneracies; <symmetry> does not require every pair of modes to be degenerate, and accidental degeneracies can also occur. Within a degenerate <eigenspace>, <orthogonal> linear combinations are equally valid <mode shapes>.

For two equal masses $m$, each attached to a fixed support by a spring of stiffness $k$ and coupled to the other by a spring of stiffness $k_c$,
$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}k+k_c&-k_c\\-k_c&k+k_c\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=m\mathsf I.
$$
The symmetric mode $\mathbf u_+\propto(1,1)$ leaves the coupling spring unstretched and has $\omega_+^2=k/m$. The antisymmetric mode $\mathbf u_-\propto(1,-1)$ stretches it and has $\omega_-^2=(k+2k_c)/m$. Superposing the two gives <beats> when their <frequencies> are close.

**Continuous systems.** The same idea replaces matrices by <differential operators> and mode <vectors> by functions satisfying <boundary conditions>. For a taut string of length $L$, <tension> $\mathcal T$ and <mass> per unit length $\mu_\ell$, the <wave equation> and fixed endpoints give
$$
y_n(x,t)=A_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_nt+\phi_n),
\qquad \omega_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal T}{\mu_\ell}},\quad n\geq1.
$$
<Orthogonality> of the <sine> functions allows an arbitrary initial <displacement> to be decomposed into modes. Changing the endpoints changes the spectrum: <boundary conditions> are part of the physical mode problem.

A uniform chain of equal masses $m$ joined by springs $k_s$, with spacing $a$, has travelling <normal modes> $q_j\propto e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$ with
$$
\omega^2(k)=\frac{4k_s}{m}\sin^2(ka/2).
$$
Their quantization gives <phonons>; the long-wavelength limit is a <sound> wave. <Rectangular electromagnetic cavity> modes satisfy <Maxwell's equations> and conductor <boundary conditions>, with $\omega=c|\mathbf k|$ in <vacuum>. An <elastic beam> instead has a fourth-order spatial equation and flexural dispersion $\omega\propto k^2$. These examples show how restoring <forces> and geometry determine the <frequency spectrum>.

**Real systems.** Small-amplitude motion is often approximately linear even when the full equations are nonlinear. Large amplitudes produce <frequency> shifts, mode coupling and transfer of <energy> between modes. Friction broadens resonances and makes free oscillations decay. A <damping> <matrix> proportional to $M$ or $K$ can retain the undamped <mode shapes>; general <damping> need not, and the appropriate eigenfrequencies and modes can be complex. External periodic forcing predominantly excites modes near <resonance>, with amplitude set by coupling to the forcing and dissipation. Imperfections split ideal degeneracies, while radiation or coupling to a surrounding fluid can remove <energy> from the chosen subsystem. **<Normal modes> remain useful when <linearization>, the <boundary conditions> and the treatment of <damping> accurately describe the motion being measured.**

**Equilibrium radiation and cavity <frequencies>.** A <blackbody> absorbs all incident radiation at every <frequency> and direction. In <thermal equilibrium> its emission spectrum depends only on its <temperature>. Radiation inside an isothermal absorbing cavity, sampled through a small aperture that hardly disturbs equilibrium, realizes <black-body radiation> to a good approximation. Microscopic absorption and emission establish the equilibrium distribution; a perfectly reflecting wall is the convenient idealization used to count the modes once that distribution is specified.

In a <rectangular electromagnetic cavity>, the <wave equation> and <perfect conductor> wall <boundary conditions> select component wavenumbers $k_x=\pi l/L_x$, $k_y=\pi m/L_y$, $k_z=\pi n/L_z$. Combining the <vacuum> <dispersion relation> $\omega=c|\mathbf k|$ and $\nu=\omega/(2\pi)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\nu_{lmn}=\frac{c}{2\pi}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\pi l}{L_x}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{\pi m}{L_y}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\pi n}{L_z}\right)^2}.}
$$
For example, electric-field components can have the spatial dependence
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_x&=A_x\cos(k_xx)\sin(k_yy)\sin(k_zz),\\
E_y&=A_y\sin(k_xx)\cos(k_yy)\sin(k_zz),\\
E_z&=A_z\sin(k_xx)\sin(k_yy)\cos(k_zz).
\end{aligned}
$$
For three positive indices, <Gauss's law> requires $k_xA_x+k_yA_y+k_zA_z=0$, leaving two independent <polarizations of an electromagnetic wave>.

**Index qualification.** The original paper describes the indices as positive integers. That describes the three-nonzero-index family but omits valid electromagnetic modes with exactly one zero index, which have one <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>. For instance, with $n=0$, $l,m\geq1$, only $E_z$ above remains; it is normal to the top and bottom faces and vanishes at the side walls. Two zero indices give no nontrivial cavity field. The omitted one-zero-index family does not affect the leading three-dimensional mode count, but is essential to the thin-cavity limit below. Retaining the paper's strict positivity restriction for every mode would instead give a thickness gap and would not yield its requested two-dimensional <power law>.

**Three-dimensional mode count.** A lattice cell in positive $\mathbf k$ space has <volume (thermodynamics)> $\pi^3/V$, where $V=L_xL_yL_z$. In a continuum approximation, a shell of radius $k$ and thickness $dk$ contains its positive octant, multiplied by two polarizations:
$$
dN=2\frac{V}{\pi^3}\frac18\,4\pi k^2\,dk
=\frac{V}{\pi^2}k^2\,dk.
$$
Using $k=2\pi\nu/c$ gives
$$
\boxed{dN=V\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}\,d\nu.}
$$
For a finite cavity the exact <density of states> is discrete; this formula is its bulk continuum limit, appropriate when many modes contribute and wall corrections are small.

**<Mean> <photon number>.** A mode is a <quantum harmonic oscillator> with thermal excitation <energies> $s h\nu$, $s=0,1,2,\ldots$. The <zero-point energy> does not enter its thermal occupation. Put $q=e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$. Its <partition function> and <mean> occupation are
$$
Z_\nu=\sum_{s=0}^\infty q^s=\frac1{1-q},\qquad
\langle s\rangle=q\frac{\partial\ln Z_\nu}{\partial q}
=\frac{q}{1-q}
=\boxed{\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}.}
$$
This is the <Bose-Einstein distribution> with zero <photon> <chemical potential>: equilibrium absorption and emission do not conserve <photon number>.

**Thermal <energy>.** Multiply the <density of states> by the <mean> excitation <energy> $h\nu\langle s\rangle$ and integrate:
$$
U=V\int_0^\infty\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}\frac{h\nu}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}\,d\nu.
$$
The substitution $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ isolates all the dependence on cavity dimensions and <temperature>:
$$
U=\frac{8\pi V(k_BT)^4}{h^3c^3}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx.
$$
Expanding $1/(e^x-1)=\sum_{r=1}^\infty e^{-rx}$ gives an <integral> $\sum_r6/r^4=6\zeta(4)=\pi^4/15$. Hence
$$
\boxed{U=\frac{8\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^3}VT^4
=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}L_xL_yL_zT^4.}
$$
This is the thermal <energy> only, excluding the zero-point contribution. The three-dimensional continuum regime requires $k_BT$ large compared with the geometric level spacings in all three directions.

$$

$$
At small <frequency> it rises as $8\pi k_BT\nu^2/c^3$, while at large <frequency> it decays exponentially. Raising <temperature> increases the spectrum at every fixed positive <frequency>, moves the peak to larger <frequency> in proportion to $T$, and increases the integrated <area> in proportion to $T^4$. A plot per unit <wavelength> has a different peak position because the integration variable changes.

= Solution

**Origin of <diffusion>.** In a material near local <thermal equilibrium>, microscopic carriers transfer <energy> from hotter regions to colder regions. On distances long compared with their <mean free path> and times long compared with relaxation times, the local constitutive law is <Fourier's law>,
$$
\mathbf J_Q=-\kappa\nabla T.
$$
Combining this temperature-gradient-driven flux with local <conservation of energy> produces a <diffusion equation>. This description assumes diffusive <thermal conduction>; it is not generally valid for <ballistic transport>, and flow would add advective transport.

**<Energy> balance with heating.** Choose any fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$, let $c$ be its uniform <volumetric heat capacity> and $H$ its <heat> generation rate per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>. The rate of increase of <internal energy> equals <heat> generation minus outward <heat flux>:
$$
\int_V c\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\,dV
=-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf J_Q\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dA+\int_VH\,dV
=\int_V\left[\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)+H\right]dV.
$$
Since $V$ is arbitrary, for constant <thermal conductivity>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\frac{\kappa}{c}\nabla^2T+\frac{H}{c}.}
$$
The ratio $\kappa/c$ is the <thermal diffusivity> in $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Here $c$ is in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}K^{-1}}$, $\kappa$ in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$ and $H$ in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$, so both right-hand terms have units $\mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$. If <thermal conductivity> varies with position or <temperature>, the <divergence> $\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla T)$ must be retained rather than replacing it by $\kappa\nabla^2T$.

In a long wire away from its ends, the steady <temperature> is independent of axial position and azimuth. The <heat equation> reduces to
$$
\frac1\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\left(\rho\frac{dT}{d\rho}\right)=-\frac{H}{\kappa}.

$$
\boxed{T(a)=T_{\rm air}+\frac{Ha}{2\gamma}
=T_{\rm air}+\frac{I^2}{2\pi^2\sigma\gamma a^3}.}
$$
Here $\gamma$ is a surface <heat transfer> coefficient in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$, unrelated to the <surface tension> symbol used in C10. An independent energy-balance check uses a length $\ell$: the electrical input $I^2\ell/(\sigma\pi a^2)$ must equal the surface loss $2\pi a\ell\gamma[T(a)-T_{\rm air}]$, which gives the same answer.

The complete <temperature> profile is
$$
T(\rho)=T_{\rm air}+\frac{Ha}{2\gamma}+\frac{H(a^2-\rho^2)}{4\kappa}.
$$
<Thermal conductivity> controls the internal gradient, but cancels from the surface-temperature rise because all generated <power> must eventually cross the prescribed surface. This assumes positive constant $\gamma$, uniform <electrical conductivity> and negligible end or additional radiative losses; temperature-dependent material properties could make the heating and boundary problem nonlinear.

= Solution
{parent=C13}

**<Entropy> at <absolute zero>.** The <third law of thermodynamics> concerns the low-temperature limit of equilibrium states. In the zero-<entropy> formulation, the <entropy> of a perfect crystal with a nondegenerate <ground state> tends to zero as $T\to0$. The statistical interpretation is $S=k_B\ln\Omega$: a unique accessible <ground state> has $\Omega=1$. The entropy-difference formulation of the <third law of thermodynamics> states that <entropy> differences associated with regular changes of mechanical parameters tend to zero as <absolute zero> is approached. These statements require equilibrium and appropriate ground-state assumptions; a frozen disordered material or a degenerate <ground state> can carry <residual entropy>.

For a phase of fixed composition and <pressure>,
$$
S(T,p)-S(0,p)=\int_0^T\frac{C_p(t,p)}{t}\,dt.
$$
A finite limiting <entropy> constrains this <integral>. Ordinary equilibrium <heat capacities> therefore vanish as <temperature> approaches zero, with familiar examples $C\propto T^3$ for three-dimensional acoustic <phonons> and $C\propto T$ for a normal degenerate <Fermi liquid>. A constant positive <heat capacity> extrapolated to zero would give a logarithmically divergent <entropy> difference and is impossible in this regime. Discontinuous transitions and anomalous limits need separate treatment rather than extending a high-temperature approximation indefinitely.

**<Gibbs free energy>.** For fixed particle numbers,
$$
dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp,
\qquad
\boxed{S=-\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_p.}
$$
Thus, when $S\to0$, the <Gibbs free energy> approaches its zero-temperature value with a horizontal tangent:
$$
\boxed{G(T,p)\to H(0,p),\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_p\to0,
\qquad G-H=-TS\to0.}
$$
The equality $G(0,p)=H(0,p)$ uses the <enthalpy> $H=U+pV$; neither limiting value needs to be zero. The <low-temperature Gibbs free-energy expansion> makes the approach quantitative. For $C_p=aT^r$ with $r>0$ and $S(0)=0$,
$$
S=\frac{a}{r}T^r,\qquad
G(T,p)=G(0,p)-\frac{a}{r(r+1)}T^{r+1}.
$$
The second <derivative> is $\partial_T^2G=-C_p/T\leq0$, although it need not tend to zero. For a reaction between such equilibrium phases, $\partial_T\Delta G=-\Delta S\to0$, and $\Delta G$ and $\Delta H$ approach one another. If a genuine <residual entropy> $S_0$ is retained instead, $G=G(0,p)-S_0T+\cdots$ has limiting slope $-S_0$. A horizontal tangent cannot be asserted without the zero-entropy condition.

**Why <helium> remains a <liquid>.** <Helium> atoms attract each other weakly and are exceptionally light. Their <zero-point energy> is large compared with the binding that would localize them in a <solid>. Both <helium-4> and <helium-3> can therefore remain <liquid> down to the lowest <temperatures> at low <pressure>; substantial applied <pressure> is needed to solidify them. This is not a violation of the third law. A quantum <liquid> can have a unique low-entropy <ground state>, and <zero-point motion> does not itself imply thermal <entropy>. The third law does not require every substance to crystallize at atmospheric <pressure>.

A <helium-4> atom has integer <spin> zero and is a <boson>. Below the <lambda point> near $2.17\,\mathrm K$ at saturated <vapor pressure>, its <liquid> is <superfluid>. Its strong interactions mean that it is not quantitatively an ideal Bose gas, and the <condensate fraction> need not equal the <superfluid fraction>. The <two-fluid model of superfluidity> separates an entropy-carrying <normal component of a superfluid> from the <superfluid> component, whose <entropy> is zero. At sufficiently low <temperature> the dominant thermal excitations are acoustic <phonons>: their linear dispersion in three dimensions gives $U-U_0\propto VT^4$, $C_V\propto VT^3$ and $S\propto VT^3$. Higher-energy <rotons> are exponentially suppressed. Thus the <normal component of a superfluid> and <entropy> disappear as $T\to0$, while <superfluid> flow and quantum motion can persist.

A <helium-3> atom has <spin> $1/2$ and is a <fermion>. Well below its <Fermi temperature>, but above its <superfluid> transition, <liquid> <helium-3> is a degenerate interacting <Fermi liquid>. Only <quasiparticles> close to the <Fermi surface> are thermally excited, giving $C_V\propto T$ and $S\propto T$. At <temperatures> of order a millikelvin, <helium-3> also becomes <superfluid> through formation of <Cooper pairs> of <fermions>. This is not <Bose-Einstein condensation> of individual <helium-3> atoms. Pairing reorganizes the low-energy excitation spectrum; in the fully gapped bulk B phase, the fermionic <quasiparticle> contribution to <heat capacity> is exponentially suppressed at <temperatures> well below the <pairing gap>. Gapless collective modes can still give thermal terms following a <power law>. The <entropy> nevertheless tends to zero. The normal Fermi-liquid law cannot be extrapolated through its pairing transition unchanged.<The Extraordinary Phases of Liquid 3He by David Morris Lee>[1996 Nobel lecture, discussion of <helium> isotopes and paired <superfluid> phases]{ref}

<Helium-3> also illustrates the importance of <nuclear spin> <entropy>. Over an intermediate low-temperature range, localized <spins> in the <solid> can retain approximately $k_B\ln2$ of <entropy> per atom, while the degenerate <liquid> has much less. Reversible conversion of <liquid> to the higher-entropy <solid> then absorbs <heat>; adiabatic compression along the <melting> curve can cool the sample. This is <Pomeranchuk cooling>. At still lower <temperature>, interactions between the nuclear <spins> remove this nearly free-spin <entropy>, so a constant $k_B\ln2$ cannot be extrapolated to <absolute zero>.<The Pomeranchuk Effect by Robert Coleman Richardson>[1996 Nobel lecture, <entropy> comparison and compression cooling]{ref}

**Liquefaction and cooling methods.** A gas must be below its <liquid-gas critical point> to form a distinct <liquid> phase by compression. Compression above the <critical temperature> alone cannot achieve ordinary liquid-vapour coexistence. Practical liquefaction removes the compression <heat>, cools the compressed gas in a <heat exchanger> and uses expansion to lower its <temperature> further.

A throttling valve operates approximately at constant <specific enthalpy> when kinetic-energy changes and <heat> leakage are negligible. The <Joule-Thomson coefficient> is
$$
\mu_{\rm JT}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_H
=\frac{T(\partial V/\partial T)_p-V}{C_p}
$$
using consistent molar quantities, or consistent total quantities for a fixed amount. A <pressure> drop cools the gas if $\mu_{\rm JT}>0$. An <ideal gas> has zero <Joule-Thomson coefficient>; real intermolecular interactions are essential. In particular, room-temperature <helium> warms on throttling, so it must first be brought into a region of positive <Joule-Thomson coefficient>. Expansion through an engine or turbine can instead remove <work> and produce approximately isentropic cooling.

A regenerative liquefier sends the cold returning gas through a <counterflow heat exchanger> to precool the incoming compressed gas before its next expansion. Expansion engines and external precooling can supply the initial cooling; repeated operation then produces <liquid>. Modern <helium> liquefaction commonly combines work-producing expansion with a final throttle. https://trc.nist.gov/cryogenics/Papers/Review/2020-Review_of_Refrigeration_Methods.pdf[Review of Refrigeration Methods, <helium> liquefiers and Claude cycles]{ref}

Once <liquid> exists, pumping away vapour lowers its <saturation pressure> and boiling <temperature>. The escaping vapour carries <latent heat> away, cooling the remaining <liquid>. At still lower <temperatures>, a <dilution refrigerator> exploits the different <entropy> carried by <helium-3> in concentrated and dilute <helium> mixtures; <adiabatic demagnetization> and <Pomeranchuk cooling> provide other routes.

**Approaching zero.** The unattainability formulation of the third law states that regular cooling operations with finite resources cannot reach <absolute zero> in finitely many stages. As the <entropy> available to remove becomes small and thermal couplings weaken, each method loses its ability to take the final step. An isentropic cooling trajectory starting at nonzero <entropy> cannot reach a common zero-entropy endpoint through regular finite changes of parameters. A fully general finite-time claim also requires assumptions about available controls and heat-transfer rates. **Quantum liquids and refrigeration methods can approach $0\,\mathrm K$ while remaining consistent with vanishing equilibrium <entropy>; they do not provide a finite-operation route to <absolute zero>.**
