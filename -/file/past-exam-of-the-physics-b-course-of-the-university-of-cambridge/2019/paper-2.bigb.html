The units supplied for 130 indicate <specific heat capacity>, so the block's total <heat capacity> is $C=mc=0.5(130)=65\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Treat it as constant, and the lake as a <thermal reservoir> at $T_0=283.15\,\mathrm K$. The initial block <temperature> is $T_i=373.15\,\mathrm K$.

Compute the block's <entropy> change using a reversible comparison path, even though the actual cooling is irreversible:
$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{block}}=\int_{T_i}^{T_0}\frac{C\,dT}{T}
=C\ln\frac{T_0}{T_i}=-17.940\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
The lake receives $Q=C(T_i-T_0)=5850\,\mathrm J$ at constant <temperature>, so
$$
\Delta S_{\mathrm{lake}}=\frac Q{T_0}=20.660\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\mathrm{Universe}}=2.72\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
This is <entropy generated by cooling a finite body in a reservoir>. The positive total is consistent with the <second law of thermodynamics>, although the cooling block's own <entropy> decreases.

Initially and finally the block and lake are at the same <temperature>. After the falling block has come to rest, its lost <gravitational potential energy> has dissipated into <internal energy> of the lake and surroundings. In the idealization that all $mgh$ is ultimately thermalized in the lake at $T_0=283.15\,\mathrm K$,
$$
Q=mgh=(0.5)(9.81)(200)=981\,\mathrm J.
$$
The block returns to its original thermal state, so its net <entropy> change is zero. Mechanical <potential energy> carries no thermal <entropy> that must be subtracted. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\mathrm{Universe}}=\frac{mgh}{T_0}=3.46\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
Use of $g=9.80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ gives the same three-significant-figure answer. Splashing and any temporary heating do not change this final-state balance if their mechanical <energy> is eventually dissipated in the same reservoir.

Three <phase coexistence curves> meet at the <triple point>, where <ice>, <liquid> <water> and <water> vapour have equal <chemical potential>. In the ordinary-pressure region, the low-temperature phase is <ice>, the high-pressure moderate-temperature phase is <liquid>, and the low-pressure high-temperature phase is vapour. The <triple point> is approximately
$$
\boxed{T_t=273.16\,\mathrm K,\qquad p_t=611.7\,\mathrm{Pa}.}
$$
Below $p_t$, warming stable ordinary <ice> crosses the <sublimation> boundary directly into vapour; there is no intervening stable <liquid>. Above $p_t$, <melting> and then boiling can occur. The <liquid>–vapour curve terminates at the <liquid-gas critical point>, approximately $647.1\,\mathrm K$ and $22.06\,\mathrm{MPa}$, beyond which there is no <liquid>–vapour phase boundary.

The distinctive feature is the negative slope of the ordinary <ice>–<water> <melting> line. The <Clapeyron equation> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v}.
$$
<Melting> increases <entropy> but reduces <volume (thermodynamics)> because ordinary <ice> is less dense than <liquid> <water>, so $\Delta s>0$ and $\Delta v<0$. Increasing <pressure> consequently lowers the <melting> <temperature> locally. <Sublimation> and <vaporization> both increase <volume (thermodynamics)> and <entropy>, giving positive slopes. The following original schematic shows these signs; it is not a quantitative fit and omits the additional high-pressure <ice> polymorphs.

Assume an air-filled or evacuated lossless <electromagnetic waveguide>, and a pulse bandwidth narrow enough to use its carrier's local <group velocity>. The longitudinal <dispersion relation> is
$$
\omega^2=c^2k_z^2+\omega_c^2.
$$
It gives
$$
v_{\mathrm p}=\frac\omega{k_z}=\frac c{\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}},
\qquad
v_{\mathrm g}=\frac{d\omega}{dk_z}=c\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}.
$$
Here $f_c/f=20/40=1/2$, so
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm p}=\frac{2c}{\sqrt3}=3.46\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
v_{\mathrm g}=\frac{\sqrt3c}{2}=2.60\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The <phase velocity> exceeds $c$, but the pulse envelope travels at the subluminal <group velocity>; a moving constant-phase surface does not by itself transport information. Their product is $v_{\mathrm p}v_{\mathrm g}=c^2$. A broad pulse can distort because this <waveguide> is dispersive.

The original PDF calls $\epsilon_r$ a relative <magnetic permeability> while also specifying a nonmagnetic <dielectric>. These descriptions are inconsistent if read literally. The physically consistent interpretation of the notation is <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r=2$ and relative <magnetic permeability> $\mu_r=1$.

Let $a=3\,\mathrm{mm}$ and let $b=6\,\mathrm{mm}$ be the inner radius of the outer conductor. For line charge density $\lambda$, <Gauss's law> gives $E_r=\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon r)$, hence
$$
V=\int_a^b E_r\,dr=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}\ln\frac ba,
\qquad C'=\frac\lambda V=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}{\ln(b/a)}.
$$
The <magnetic field> in the <dielectric> is $H_\varphi=I/(2\pi r)$. Its <energy> per unit length gives
$$
\frac12L'I^2=\int_a^b\frac{\mu H_\varphi^2}{2}\,2\pi r\,dr,
\qquad L'=\frac{\mu_0\mu_r}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba.
$$
Using $b/a=2$,
$$
\boxed{C'=1.61\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}=161\,\mathrm{pF\,m^{-1}},}
$$
$$
\boxed{L'=1.39\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}=139\,\mathrm{nH\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
These are the <lossless coaxial line parameters>, assuming negligible field penetration into the conductors. If the quoted radii instead referred to an outer diameter or if $\mu_r=2$ were intended, the data would need clarification; the stated <dielectric> and nonmagnetic assumptions support the interpretation above.

For an ideal <electrical transformer>, let $n=N_p/N_s$. The reflected secondary load is $R_{\mathrm{in}}=n^2R_L$. Matching a $4\,\Omega$ load to an $8\,\Omega$ source therefore requires
$$
\boxed{n=\sqrt{\frac84}=\sqrt2.}
$$
With the generator replaced by its internal resistance, its resistance referred to the secondary is
$$
\boxed{R_{\mathrm{out}}=\left(\frac{N_s}{N_p}\right)^2(8\,\Omega)=4\,\Omega.}
$$
This is the source output resistance relevant to matching and maximum <power> transfer.

To find the windings, use the <inductance of a toroidal winding>. The <mean> magnetic path is $\ell=2\pi(0.10)\,\mathrm m$, the core <area> is $A=30\times10^{-4}=0.003\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and $\mu=500\mu_0$. Thus
$$
L_p=\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_p^2A}{\ell},
\qquad N_p=\sqrt{\frac{(0.75)2\pi(0.10)}{\mu_0(500)(0.003)}}=500.
$$
The ideal secondary turn count is $N_s=500/\sqrt2=353.55$, giving the practical nominal design
$$
\boxed{N_p=500,\qquad N_s\simeq354.}
$$
With integer turns the irrational ratio cannot be exact: $500:354$ reflects the $4\,\Omega$ load as $7.98\,\Omega$, an approximately $0.25\%$ resistance mismatch. These turn counts use the stated $0.75\,\mathrm H$ self-inductance; no operating <frequency> was specified from which to set a separate minimum <inductance>.

The $200\,\Omega$ condition must refer to the unloaded magnetizing reactance, not to the matched loaded input resistance. With $L_p=0.75\,\mathrm H$,
$$
|X_p|=2\pi fL_p\geq200\,\Omega
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\geq42.4\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$
The secondary self-inductance is $L_s=L_p/n^2=0.375\,\mathrm H$, so its corresponding unloaded coil impedance satisfies
$$
\boxed{Z_{s,\mathrm{coil}}=i\omega L_s,\qquad |Z_{s,\mathrm{coil}}|\geq100\,\Omega.}
$$
If output impedance means this transformed coil reactance, $100\,\Omega$ is its minimum magnitude. It is distinct from the $4\,\Omega$ transformed source resistance. This is the <magnetizing reactance and reflected resistance in a transformer> distinction. A finite magnetizing <inductance> adds a parallel branch, $Z_{\mathrm{in}}=(8\,\Omega)\parallel(i\omega L_p)$, so exact purely resistive matching is an ideal large-reactance approximation; interpreting the loaded input itself as both $8\,\Omega$ and at least $200\,\Omega$ would be impossible.

In a homogeneous linear medium, write $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$. Without free charge or current, <Maxwell's equations> become
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$
Taking the <curl> of <Faraday's law>, using $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$, gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0,
\qquad\nabla^2\mathbf B-\mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf B=0.}
$$
Their plane-wave solutions have $\omega^2=k^2/(\mu\epsilon)$ and speed $v=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$; in vacuum this is $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$. For a wave $\operatorname{Re}[\mathbf E_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}]$, the <divergence> equations give $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0=0$, while <Faraday's law> gives $\mathbf B_0=\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0/\omega$. Thus the fields and propagation direction form an <orthogonal> triad, with $B_0=E_0/v$.

The two independent transverse electric components allow linear, circular and elliptical <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>. Their relative amplitudes and phase determine the polarization state. In a lossless nondispersive medium the <energy density> and <energy> flux are
$$
u=\frac12\epsilon E^2+\frac{B^2}{2\mu},\qquad
\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H.
$$
A travelling <plane wave> has equal electric and <magnetic energy> contributions and time-averaged intensity $\langle S\rangle=E_0^2/(2Z)$, where $Z=\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ is its <wave impedance> and $E_0$ denotes peak electric-field amplitude. Reflection and refraction follow by imposing the electromagnetic <boundary conditions>. Frequency-dependent constitutive response introduces dispersion; conductivity introduces attenuation, so the simple real constant-speed result has a specified domain of validity.

A <dielectric> responds to an <electric field> mainly by displacing or orienting bound charges, rather than by sustaining a steady conduction current. The <polarization density> $\mathbf P$ is <electric dipole moment> per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>. It creates bound charge
$$
\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\qquad\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf n.
$$
Separating these charges from free charge motivates
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f.
$$
For an isotropic linear <dielectric>, $\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r.}
$$
The <electric susceptibility> $\chi_e$ describes material polarization and is not the <magnetic susceptibility>. More generally it is a <tensor> and can depend on <frequency>, field strength and <temperature>.

Electronic polarization displaces an <electron> cloud relative to its nucleus; ionic polarization displaces positive and negative ions; orientational polarization partially aligns permanent molecular dipoles against thermal disorder. For independent permanent moments of magnitude $p$, weak-field orientational response gives $\chi_e\simeq np^2/(3\epsilon_0k_BT)$ when local-field corrections and interactions are negligible. This explains a common inverse-temperature contribution without asserting that every <dielectric> follows that law.

At a static interface, $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f$ and the tangential <electric field> is continuous. Bound surface charge is already included through $\mathbf P$. For a homogeneous filled <capacitor>, $C=\epsilon A/d$, and a reversible linear <dielectric> stores <energy density> $u=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D/2$. At fixed voltage, insertion raises the stored field <energy> but the voltage source also supplies <energy>; <force> arguments must include that source. At fixed charge the <capacitor energy> instead decreases on insertion.

At <frequencies> too high for a polarization mechanism to follow the field, its contribution weakens. A complex <permittivity> represents dissipative <dielectric loss> as well as phase lag; the sign of its imaginary part depends on the time convention. Strong fields can cause nonlinear response or <dielectric breakdown>, beyond the reversible linear model.

In a homogeneous Ohmic conductor, $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$. Combining this with <Maxwell's equations> gives
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\mathbf E+\mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E.
$$
For a good conductor, $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$, so conduction dominates <displacement current>. With time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and depth $z>0$, take $E\propto e^{ikz}$. Then
$$
k^2=i\mu\sigma\omega,\qquad k=\frac{1+i}{\delta},
\qquad
\boxed{\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu\sigma\omega}}.}
$$
The field is consequently proportional to $e^{-z/\delta}e^{iz/\delta}$: it both attenuates and changes phase with depth. The <skin depth> is the distance for amplitude to fall by $e^{-1}$; <power> density falls as $e^{-2z/\delta}$, so its attenuation length is $\delta/2$.

Physically, changing fields induce currents whose <magnetic field> opposes penetration. Equivalently, after neglecting <displacement current>, <magnetic diffusion> obeys $\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf B$. Faster changes have less time to diffuse into the metal. Increasing <frequency>, conductivity or <magnetic permeability> reduces $\delta$, provided the relevant material parameters remain applicable.

For a thick conductor the tangential surface <electrical impedance> is $Z_s=(1-i)/(\sigma\delta)$ with the stated time convention and consistent field orientation. Its real part $1/(\sigma\delta)$ represents dissipative resistance per square. Current crowding near the surface raises the AC resistance of a wire when $\delta$ is small compared with its radius; thin strands and laminated magnetic cores reduce suitable eddy-current losses. When $\sigma\not\gg\omega\epsilon$, the full complex propagation constant must replace the good-conductor approximation. In a magnetic metal the <magnetic permeability> can itself be nonlinear and <frequency> dependent.

A <transmission line> must be treated as a distributed circuit when its propagation time is significant on the <signal> timescale. Let $R',L',G',C'$ denote resistance, <inductance>, leakage conductance and <capacitance> per unit length. Applying <Kirchhoff's voltage law> and <Kirchhoff's current law> to a short length $dx$ gives the <telegrapher's equations>
$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=-R'I-L'\frac{\partial I}{\partial t},\qquad
\frac{\partial I}{\partial x}=-G'V-C'\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}.
$$
For a lossless line, $R'=G'=0$. Differentiating again gives <wave equations> with
$$
\boxed{v=\frac1{\sqrt{L'C'}},\qquad Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}.}
$$
The travelling solutions can be written $V=V_+(t-x/v)+V_-(t+x/v)$ and $I=[V_+(t-x/v)-V_-(t+x/v)]/Z_0$. The minus sign for the backward wave is essential to <power> transport.

At a load $Z_L$, continuity of voltage and current gives the voltage <reflection coefficient>
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_L=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}.}
$$
A matched termination $Z_L=Z_0$ absorbs the incident <signal> without reflection. An open circuit has $\Gamma_L=+1$ and a short circuit has $\Gamma_L=-1$. Superposed incident and reflected waves produce <standing waves>. A sinusoidal lossless line of length $\ell$ transforms the termination into
$$
Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_0\frac{Z_L+iZ_0\tan(k\ell)}{Z_0+iZ_L\tan(k\ell)}
$$
for the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention. At a quarter <wavelength>, $Z_{\mathrm{in}}=Z_0^2/Z_L$, allowing impedance transformation. A coaxial line supports a transverse electromagnetic mode whose ideal $L',C'$ are calculated in A4. Its <energy> travels in the fields between conductors; the <characteristic impedance> is not the wire's DC resistance. Losses introduce attenuation and make the <characteristic impedance> and propagation constant complex.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

**<Magnetization> and field response.** A magnetic material contains microscopic magnetic moments associated with <electron> <spin> and orbital motion. The <magnetization> $\mathbf M$ is <magnetic dipole moment> per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>. In SI the macroscopic fields satisfy
$$
\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M).
$$
Here $\mathbf H$ separates free-current excitation from the material response. A uniform isotropic linear material has $\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H$, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H,\qquad\mu_r=1+\chi_m.}
$$
This constant scalar <magnetic permeability> model is useful only within its linear range. Crystals can have a susceptibility <tensor>; strongly magnetic materials depend on field and magnetic history. For a finite specimen the internal $\mathbf H$ also includes its demagnetizing field. In a uniformly magnetized ellipsoid, $H_{\mathrm{int}}=H_{\mathrm{applied}}-N_dM$ along a <principal axis>, so sample shape affects the apparent susceptibility.

**Diamagnetic and paramagnetic response.** <Diamagnetism> is a weak negative susceptibility caused by induced orbital response opposing the applied field. It occurs in all matter but can be masked by larger positive contributions. Closed-shell materials such as bismuth or many organic compounds show it clearly. Ordinary <diamagnetism> is often only weakly <temperature> dependent; superconducting flux exclusion is a separate collective phenomenon and is not described by an arbitrarily small negative susceptibility.

<Paramagnetism> arises when available magnetic moments partially align with a field. Thermal disorder prevents complete alignment at weak fields. For independent localized moments of magnitude $m$, the classical weak-field result is
$$
M\simeq\frac{nm^2B}{3k_BT},\qquad
\chi_m\simeq\frac{\mu_0nm^2}{3k_BT},
$$
where the second expression uses $B\simeq\mu_0H$ in a weakly magnetic sample. Thus localized moments obey a <Curie law> in this regime. Quantum <angular momentum> changes the coefficient to the appropriate $J(J+1)$ expression and gives saturation at strong field. Conduction <electrons> instead exhibit <Pauli paramagnetism>: only states near the <Fermi surface> can change <spin> populations, so a degenerate gas has a nearly temperature-independent response. Its orbital <diamagnetism> must also be included for a full metallic susceptibility.

**Collective magnetic order.** <Exchange interactions> can favor parallel alignment, producing <ferromagnetism> below the <Curie temperature>. Iron, cobalt and nickel are familiar examples. A material may contain regions of spontaneous <magnetization>, or <magnetic domains>, while having little net external moment because the domains point in different directions. Applied fields move domain walls and rotate moments; at large fields the <magnetization> approaches saturation. Pinning and <energy> barriers make this process hysteretic. <Remanence> is the <magnetization> left after removing the driving field, and <coercivity> measures the reverse field needed to bring the chosen response back to zero.

The <area> of a closed $B$–$H$ loop is the <energy> dissipated per unit <volume (thermodynamics)> per quasistatic cycle, $\oint H\,dB$, with positive dissipated magnitude. Consequently the <magnetic permeability> is neither one universal constant nor enough information to describe a ferromagnet: differential <magnetic permeability> $dB/dH$, saturation and <hysteresis> all matter. Above the ordering <temperature> the spontaneous moment disappears, and a Curie–Weiss law is often an appropriate approximate paramagnetic description away from the critical region.

In <antiferromagnetism>, ordered opposing sublattices have equal moments and cancel macroscopically in the ideal compensated case. The order disappears above the <Néel temperature>. In <ferrimagnetism>, opposing sublattices have unequal moments and leave a net <magnetization>. Ferrites combine ferrimagnetic response with relatively high electrical resistivity, making them valuable when eddy-current losses would be substantial in a metallic core.

**<Electromagnets> and engineering choice.** A coil supplies free-current magnetomotive <force> $NI$. For a simple uniform magnetic path, <Ampère's law> gives $H\ell\simeq NI$ and flux $\Phi\simeq\mu ANI/\ell$. A high-<magnetic permeability> soft magnetic core concentrates flux and reduces the current required for a given field. With an air gap $g$,
$$
NI\simeq\frac{B\ell_c}{\mu_0\mu_r}+\frac{Bg}{\mu_0}.
$$
Even a short gap can dominate the <magnetic reluctance>. The approximate attractive <force> across one uniform gap is $F\simeq B^2A/(2\mu_0)$, obtained from the field <energy>, with fringing neglected.

Soft magnetic materials have small <coercivity> and narrow <hysteresis> loops; they suit relays, <electrical transformer> cores and controllable <electromagnets>. Hard magnetic materials retain their <magnetization> and suit <permanent magnets>. Laminating metallic cores breaks large eddy-current loops; ferrites are useful at higher <frequencies> because of their resistivity. Increasing current cannot increase $B$ indefinitely through the linear formula: core saturation, coil heating and leakage limit performance. A good design selects material for the field amplitude, switching <frequency> and acceptable loss, rather than simply choosing the largest quoted <magnetic permeability>.

Let the <rotating frame> have origin <acceleration> $\mathbf A_0$ and <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega(t)$ relative to an <inertial frame>. For a <vector> referred to the moving axes, the transport rule is
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\mathrm I}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\mathrm R}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf a.
$$
Apply it twice to position. With rotating-frame <velocity> $\mathbf v$ and <acceleration> $\mathbf a_R$,
$$
\mathbf a_I=\mathbf A_0+\mathbf a_R+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v
+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r
+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
Thus Newton's equation expressed in the <rotating frame> is
$$
\boxed{m\mathbf a_R=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{real}}-m\mathbf A_0
-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v-m\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r
-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).}
$$
The added terms are translational, Coriolis, Euler and centrifugal <fictitious forces>. They reflect the frame's <acceleration>, rather than additional interactions with nearby objects.

The <centrifugal force> points outward from the <rotation> axis and, for constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$, has potential $-m|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2$. The <Coriolis force> is perpendicular to <velocity>, so does no <work> on the particle measured in that <rotating frame>; it changes direction rather than speed directly. The <Euler force> is present when the frame's <rotation> rate or direction changes.

On Earth, gravity and <centrifugal acceleration> combine into effective gravity. For slow horizontal motion at latitude $\lambda$, the vertical component of Earth's <rotation> gives Coriolis parameter $f=2\Omega\sin\lambda$: an initially northward motion deflects eastward in the northern hemisphere. This supplies a useful local explanation of rotating flows and Foucault <precession>. A bucket of rotating <liquid> illustrates the <centrifugal potential>: its steady free surface is an equipotential of $gz-\Omega^2r^2/2$, giving $z=\Omega^2r^2/(2g)+\mathrm{constant}$. These equations require a declared frame; adding a <centrifugal force> while retaining inertial-frame <acceleration> would count the same kinematic effect twice.

For a slender <elastic beam> undergoing small bending, cross-sections remain approximately plane and perpendicular to the centreline. At distance $z$ from the <neutral axis>, longitudinal <strain> is $\epsilon_{xx}=-z\kappa$, where $\kappa$ is <curvature>. <Linear elasticity> gives $\sigma_{xx}=E\epsilon_{xx}$, so the <bending moment> has magnitude
$$
M=EI\kappa,\qquad I=\int_A z^2\,dA.
$$
Here $I$ is the second moment of cross-sectional <area>, not a <mass> <moment of inertia>. For width $b$ and depth $h$ in the bending direction, $I=bh^3/12$. The cubic depth dependence explains why a deep beam resists bending more effectively than the same material spread sideways.

For a uniform beam with transverse <displacement> $y(x)$, $\kappa\simeq y''$ and bending <energy> is $\tfrac12\int EI(y'')^2dx$. Balancing transverse load $q(x)$ yields the <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> $EIy''''=q$ under a consistent sign convention. Supports specify values of deflection, slope, moment or shear; the equation alone does not fix the response.

Now apply a compressive end load $P>0$. A small deflection shortens the axial projection by $\tfrac12\int(y')^2dx$, so the total <potential energy> relative to the straight configuration is
$$
\Pi[y]=\frac12\int_0^\ell\left[EI(y'')^2-P(y')^2\right]dx.
$$
Its stationary equation is
$$
EIy''''+Py''=0.
$$
For pinned ends, $y(0)=y(\ell)=0$ and the end moments vanish, so $y''(0)=y''(\ell)=0$. The candidate shapes $y=A\sin(n\pi x/\ell)$ require $P=EI(n\pi/\ell)^2$. The first loss of stability is consequently
$$
\boxed{P_{\mathrm{cr}}=\frac{\pi^2EI}{\ell^2}\quad\text{for a pinned–pinned strut}.}
$$
At smaller $P$, the quadratic <energy> is positive; beyond this threshold the straight configuration no longer minimizes it. End restraint changes the threshold: clamped–clamped gives $4\pi^2EI/\ell^2$ and clamped–free gives $\pi^2EI/(4\ell^2)$, equivalently an effective-length correction. A column buckles in the direction of smaller flexural rigidity. Euler buckling is a geometric instability within elastic response; yielding or crushing can occur first for a short thick strut. Large deflections, shear deformation and imperfections require refinements of the ideal slender-beam model.

Take a fixed <sphere> of radius $R$ in an unbounded incompressible inviscid fluid, with uniform <velocity> $U\mathbf e_z$ at infinity. Assume <irrotational flow>, so $\mathbf v=\nabla\phi$ and <incompressibility> gives $\nabla^2\phi=0$. Axisymmetry and the dipolar angular dependence of the far field suggest
$$
\phi=(Ar+B/r^2)\cos\theta.
$$
Matching the far field gives $A=U$. The no-penetration condition $v_r(R)=0$ then gives $B=UR^3/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi=U\left(r+\frac{R^3}{2r^2}\right)\cos\theta,}
$$
$$
v_r=U\left(1-\frac{R^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta,\qquad
v_\theta=-U\left(1+\frac{R^3}{2r^3}\right)\sin\theta.
$$
The surface has <stagnation points> at its front and rear poles, while the equatorial speed is $3U/2$. Fluid slips tangentially past the <sphere>; imposing a no-slip condition would contradict this inviscid potential-flow model.

With gravity omitted or the hydrostatic <pressure> subtracted, steady Bernoulli gives
$$
p+\frac12\rho v^2=p_\infty+\frac12\rho U^2.
$$
At the surface,
$$
\boxed{p(R,\theta)=p_\infty+\frac12\rho U^2
\left(1-\frac94\sin^2\theta\right).}
$$
The front–rear symmetry makes the <integral> of <pressure> <force> along the flow vanish: terms proportional to $\cos\theta$ integrate to zero over the <sphere>. This is <d'Alembert's paradox>, not a prediction that real spheres have no <drag>. <Viscosity> creates <boundary layers>; separation and a wake break the fore–aft symmetry. <Potential flow> remains valuable for the outer <velocity> field when viscous regions are thin, but it cannot supply their <drag> by itself.

A spatially uniform external <gravitational acceleration> moves a body's <centre of mass> without stretching it. <Tidal forces> arise from the variation of that <acceleration> across the body. Let a distant <mass> $M$ lie at distance $D$ along <unit vector> $\mathbf n$, and let $|\mathbf r|\ll D$ denote position relative to the body's centre. Subtract the centre's <acceleration>. To first order,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_{\mathrm{tidal}}=\frac{GM}{D^3}
\left[3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\mathbf n-\mathbf r\right].}
$$
The <tidal tensor> is $T_{ij}=GM(3n_in_j-\delta_{ij})/D^3$. It stretches along the line to the source with <eigenvalue> $2GM/D^3$ and compresses in the two perpendicular directions with <eigenvalues> $-GM/D^3$. Its <trace> vanishes outside the source, consistently with <Laplace's equation> for the <gravitational potential>.

After removal of the constant and uniform-acceleration terms, the tidal potential is
$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{tidal}}=-\frac{GM}{2D^3}
\left[3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)^2-r^2\right].
$$
An ideal equilibrium ocean on a <sphere> of radius $R$ follows an equipotential of the body's gravity plus this perturbation. For surface gravity $g$, the leading <displacement> is
$$
\delta h(\psi)=-\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{tidal}}(R,\psi)}g
=\frac{GMR^2}{2gD^3}(3\cos^2\psi-1),
$$
up to a constant fixing total <water> <volume (thermodynamics)>. The quadrupolar form produces bulges on both the near and far sides; the far-side bulge follows from differential <acceleration>, without postulating a repulsive gravitational <force>.

The Moon and Sun both raise terrestrial <tides>, with relative strength set mainly by $M/D^3$, rather than by the total gravitational pull $M/D^2$. Their contributions reinforce near new and full Moon to produce <spring tides>, and partly cancel near quadrature to produce <neap tides>. Earth's <rotation> relative to the tidal pattern gives a strong semidiurnal component in the simplest geometry. Real <tide> heights and timing depend on ocean depth, coastlines, basin resonances, <Coriolis acceleration>, friction and the declination of the forcing bodies. <Tidal dissipation> produces phase lags and <torques>, exchanging rotational and <orbital angular momentum> and gradually altering the <spin> and orbit. The equilibrium bulge is therefore a useful starting model, not an exact local <tide> forecast.

**Inertia is a <tensor>.** For a <rigid body>, distances between material points are fixed. Write each position relative to the <centre of mass> as $\mathbf r$, so its rotational <velocity> is $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$. Integrating $\mathbf r\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)$ gives <angular momentum>
$$
L_i=I_{ij}\omega_j,\qquad
I_{ij}=\int(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_ir_j)\,dm.
$$
The <rotational kinetic energy> is
$$
T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
The <inertia tensor> is real and symmetric, so <orthogonal> <principal axes> diagonalize it. <Angular momentum> is parallel to <angular velocity> only along a <principal axis>, or within a subspace of equal principal moments. For translation plus <rotation> about the <centre of mass>, $T=Mv_{\mathrm{CM}}^2/2+T_{\mathrm{rot}}$. Moving the reference to a parallel axis adds $M(d^2\mathbf1-\mathbf d\mathbf d^{\mathsf T})$ to the <tensor>, the <tensor> form of the parallel-axis theorem.

**Euler's equations.** The external <torque> about the <centre of mass> obeys $\boldsymbol\tau=(d\mathbf L/dt)_{\mathrm{space}}$. In body axes, the transport rule gives
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\left(\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\right)_{\mathrm{body}}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L.
$$
The principal moments are constant in that frame, hence
$$
\boxed{I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=\tau_1,}
$$
$$
I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1=\tau_2,\qquad
I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2=\tau_3.
$$
These are <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)>. With zero <torque>, spatial <angular momentum> and <kinetic energy> are conserved, although the body components generally vary. For distinct moments, small perturbations about <rotation> around the largest or smallest principal moment oscillate, whereas those about the intermediate moment grow. This is the intermediate-axis instability.

**A lamina.** Choose the lamina's plane as $z=0$. Then $I_{xz}=I_{yz}=0$, so the normal is a <principal axis>, and
$$
\boxed{I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)dm=I_x+I_y.}
$$
This perpendicular-axis theorem applies to a planar <mass> distribution; it is not a general identity for a three-dimensional body. Rotate the in-plane axes to diagonalize the remaining symmetric two-by-two block. For a uniform rectangular lamina with sides $a,b$ and <mass> $M$, its central principal moments are $I_x=Mb^2/12$, $I_y=Ma^2/12$ and $I_z=M(a^2+b^2)/12$. If $a>b$, then $I_x<I_y<I_z$: torque-free <rotation> about the long in-plane axis or the normal is stable, while that about the short in-plane axis is unstable. Coincident moments in a square require the degenerate, rather than strict intermediate-axis, case. Under a constraint to rotate only about the normal, the motion reduces to the scalar equation $\tau_z=I_z\dot\omega_z$.

**A <symmetric top> without <torque>.** Let $I_1=I_2=I_\perp$ and $I_3=I_\parallel$. Euler's equations reduce to
$$
\dot\omega_3=0,\qquad
\dot\omega_1=-\nu\omega_2,\qquad
\dot\omega_2=\nu\omega_1,
\qquad \nu=\frac{I_\parallel-I_\perp}{I_\perp}\omega_3.
$$
Thus $\omega_1+i\omega_2$ rotates at signed body-frame <frequency> $\nu$, while the axial component stays fixed. The symmetry axis has constant angle to fixed spatial <angular momentum>. Indeed $\mathbf L=I_\perp\boldsymbol\omega+(I_\parallel-I_\perp)\omega_3\mathbf e_3$, so
$$
\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3
=\frac{\mathbf L}{I_\perp}\times\mathbf e_3.
$$
It precesses in space about $\mathbf L$ with angular speed $L/I_\perp$. This spatial <precession> and the body-frame <frequency> $\nu$ describe different motions and should not be identified.

**A heavy top.** If a <symmetric top> is pivoted a distance $\ell$ from its <centre of mass>, gravity gives <torque> $\mathbf r_{\mathrm{CM}}\times M\mathbf g$. Here the relevant moments are about the fixed pivot. The axial <angular momentum> and the vertical angular-momentum component are conserved, because gravity supplies neither an axial <torque> nor a <torque> about the vertical. Using tilt $\theta$, <precession> angle $\phi$ and <spin> angle $\psi$, its <Lagrangian> is
$$
\mathcal L=\frac12I_\perp(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)
+\frac12I_\parallel(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2-Mg\ell\cos\theta.
$$
Eliminating the two conserved cyclic momenta leaves one-dimensional motion in an effective tilt potential; bounded oscillation in tilt is <nutation>. Steady <precession> at $\dot\phi=\Omega$ and fixed tilt satisfies
$$
I_\perp\Omega^2\cos\theta-L_3\Omega+Mg\ell=0.
$$
For a rapidly spinning top on the slow branch, the first term is small and
$$
\boxed{\Omega\simeq\frac{Mg\ell}{L_3}=\frac{Mg\ell}{I_\parallel\omega_3}.}
$$
Gravity then changes the direction of <angular momentum> rather than simply tipping the top straight down. The approximation fails near conditions where <nutation> or the fast-precession branch matters; the full equations retain those possibilities.

**<Pressure> and its physical terms.** Solving the molar <Van der Waals equation> for <pressure> gives
$$
\boxed{P=\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac a{V^2},\qquad V>b.}
$$
The excluded-volume parameter $b$ reduces the <volume (thermodynamics)> available for translational motion, raising the kinetic <pressure> relative to $RT/V$. The attraction parameter $a$ lowers the <pressure> because attractive interactions reduce the momentum transferred to the boundary. This mean-field model treats $a,b$ as temperature-independent material constants. For one mole, $V$ is the sample <volume (thermodynamics)> with the molar amount already fixed; $b$ has units of that <volume (thermodynamics)> and $a$ of <pressure> times <volume (thermodynamics)> squared.

**<volume (thermodynamics)> dependence of <entropy>.** The <Helmholtz free energy> has differential $dF=-S\,dT-P\,dV$. Equality of mixed <derivatives> yields the <Maxwell relation>
$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T
=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$
For $a=0$ this <derivative> is $R/(V-b)$, giving
$$
\boxed{S(V,T)-S(V_*,T)=R\ln\frac{V-b}{V_*-b},\qquad V,V_*>b.}
$$
Equivalently, $S=R\ln(V-b)+f(T)$ with a common reference <volume (thermodynamics)> understood inside the <logarithm>. The <equation of state> fixes this <volume (thermodynamics)> dependence, not the otherwise unspecified function of <temperature>.

**Excess <entropy> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> and <temperature>.** The <ideal gas> has $(\partial S_{\mathrm{id}}/\partial V)_T=R/V$. Therefore
$$
\left(\frac{\partial\Delta S^{\mathrm{ex}}}{\partial V}\right)_T
=R\left(\frac1{V-b}-\frac1V\right).
$$
Integrate from infinite <volume (thermodynamics)>, where the <entropy> difference is specified to vanish:
$$
\Delta S^{\mathrm{ex}}(V,T)
=\int_\infty^V R\left(\frac1{v-b}-\frac1v\right)dv
=\boxed{R\ln\left(1-\frac bV\right).}
$$
It is negative because excluding configurations reduces the accessible state count. It tends to zero as $V\to\infty$ and has leading dilute limit $-Rb/V$. This is the <entropy excess of a van der Waals gas>.

**Effect of attraction.** Restoring a temperature-independent $a$ changes the <pressure> by $-a/V^2$, but not its <derivative> with respect to <temperature>. With the same infinite-volume <entropy> reference, it therefore does not change $\Delta S^{\mathrm{ex}}$ at fixed $V,T$. This can also be seen from
$$
F_{\mathrm{ex}}=-RT\ln(1-b/V)-\frac aV,
\qquad S_{\mathrm{ex}}=-\left(\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{ex}}}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$
The attraction affects <internal energy> and the equilibrium <volume (thermodynamics)>, so it can affect <entropy> indirectly when different state points are compared. If $a$ were <temperature> dependent, its <derivative> would contribute directly to the <entropy>; that is outside the stipulated constant-parameter model.

**Coexistence isotherm.** Below the <Van der Waals critical point>, the formal homogeneous isotherm has a local minimum and maximum. Its middle branch has $\partial P/\partial V>0$, hence negative <isothermal compressibility>, and is mechanically unstable. Portions of the negative-slope branches can be <metastable> rather than globally stable. The equilibrium isotherm replaces the loop between <liquid> <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_l$ and vapour <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_g$ by a constant saturation <pressure> $P_s$. The two <volume (thermodynamics)> are fixed by
$$
P(V_l,T)=P(V_g,T)=P_s,
\qquad
\boxed{\int_{V_l}^{V_g}[P(V,T)-P_s]dV=0.}
$$
The <Maxwell equal-area construction> expresses equality of the two phases' chemical potentials. Intermediate total <volume (thermodynamics)> are mixtures: changing the vapour fraction changes total <volume (thermodynamics)> at the same <pressure>, rather than following the unstable homogeneous curve. The <spinodals> are the turning points of the formal isotherm; they are distinct from the coexistence endpoints.

\Image[paper-2-c10-coexistence.png]
{title=Original subcritical van der Waals isotherm showing the equal-area plateau, coexistence volumes and spinodals}

**Dense-phase <volume (thermodynamics)> at low <temperature>.** At a general subcritical <temperature>, the two coexistence equations above must be solved together. For this <equation of state> the <area> condition is explicitly
$$
RT\ln\frac{V_g-b}{V_l-b}
+a\left(\frac1{V_g}-\frac1{V_l}\right)
=P_s(V_g-V_l).
$$
This supplies an exact implicit determination of the <liquid> <volume (thermodynamics)> even when a simple closed form is unavailable.

For the asymptotically low-temperature limit, put $\tau=RTb/a\ll1$. The <liquid> is dense, $V_l\simeq b$, while the vapour is dilute, $P_sV_g\simeq RT$ and $V_g\gg V_l$. The <area> condition then gives
$$
\ln\frac{V_g}{V_l-b}\simeq1+\frac{a}{RTV_l}.
$$
Thus the vapour <volume (thermodynamics)> grows exponentially in $1/\tau$, and the saturation <pressure> is exponentially small. It is consequently consistent to neglect $P_s$ in the <liquid> equation to algebraic order in $\tau$:
$$
0\simeq\frac{RT}{V_l-b}-\frac a{V_l^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad RTV_l^2-aV_l+ab\simeq0.
$$
Choose the root near $b$, not the large-volume zero-pressure root:
$$
\boxed{V_l\simeq\frac a{2RT}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{4RTb}{a}}\right)
=\frac{2b}{1+\sqrt{1-4\tau}}.}
$$
Expanding gives the leading requested dense-phase behavior,
$$
\boxed{V_l=b+\frac{RTb^2}{a}+O(b\tau^2),\qquad T\to0\ \text{within the model}.}
$$
This is the <low-temperature liquid volume of a van der Waals gas>. Setting $P_s=0$ is an asymptotic approximation, not an exact coexistence condition at every <temperature> below $T_c$. The figure illustrates generic subcritical coexistence; its finite-temperature <liquid> <volume (thermodynamics)> is determined by the full equal-area equations. The van der Waals <liquid> also ceases to be a realistic description at sufficiently low physical <temperature>, where other phases or quantum effects can matter.

**Two equivalent second-law statements.** The <Clausius statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is transferring <heat> from a colder reservoir to a hotter one. A <refrigerator> can make that transfer when supplied with <work>, so the sole-effect qualification matters. The <Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole net effect is drawing <heat> from a single reservoir and converting all of it into <work>. A <heat engine> can produce <work> when it also rejects <heat> to a colder reservoir.

The two statements are equivalent by combining a supposed violating device with an ordinary reversible engine or <refrigerator>: one can cancel one reservoir exchange and obtain a violation of the other statement. The following original schematic shows the forbidden sole effects, with arrows indicating <energy> flow.

\Image[paper-2-c11-second-law.png]
{title=Forbidden sole effects in the Clausius and Kelvin–Planck formulations of the second law}

**Microscopic fluctuations.** The second law is a macroscopic statistical law. A finite fluctuation can transfer a small <heat> amount $Q$ from cold to hot, with reservoir <entropy> change $Q/T_h-Q/T_c<0$. Such an individual event does not imply that a cyclic engine can extract systematic useful <work> from equilibrium. Entropy-decreasing trajectories become exceedingly improbable when the <entropy> involved is large compared with $k_B$. Under suitable microscopic reversibility conditions, fluctuation relations quantify the relative <probabilities> of positive and negative <entropy> production and give nonnegative <mean> total <entropy> production. Thus a deterministic no-fluctuation reading has a limit at small scales or short times; the statistical second law remains applicable.

**Maximum heating rate.** Interpret the quoted $0.5\,\mathrm{kW}$ as the maximum work-input rate $\dot W_{\max}$ to an ideal reversible <heat pump>. This interpretation is needed to obtain a heating rate larger than its electrical input; the wording does not explicitly distinguish input <power> from delivered <heat>. A reversible cycle satisfies
$$
\frac{\dot Q_h}{T_h}=\frac{\dot Q_c}{T_c},\qquad
\dot W=\dot Q_h-\dot Q_c.
$$
Therefore the heating <coefficient of performance> is
$$
\mathrm{COP}_h=\frac{\dot Q_h}{\dot W}=\frac{T_h}{T_h-T_c}.
$$
With $T_h=293\,\mathrm K$ and $T_c=273\,\mathrm K$,
$$
\boxed{\dot Q_{h,\max}=(0.5)\frac{293}{20}=7.325\,\mathrm{kW}\simeq7.33\,\mathrm{kW}.}
$$
The additional <heat> comes from the outdoor reservoir; it is not produced by converting <work> into more <energy> than was supplied.

**Lowest outdoor <temperature>.** Let $\Delta T=T_h-T_c>0$. The largest available heating rate is $\dot W_{\max}T_h/\Delta T$, while the house loses $\lambda\Delta T$. Equilibrium can be maintained when
$$
\lambda\Delta T\leq\frac{\dot W_{\max}T_h}{\Delta T},
\qquad
\Delta T^2\leq\frac{\dot W_{\max}T_h}{\lambda}.
$$
At the limiting outdoor <temperature>,
$$
\Delta T_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{(0.5\,\mathrm{kW})(293\,\mathrm K)}{0.1\,\mathrm{kW\,K^{-1}}}}
=38.275\,\mathrm K.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{T_{c,\min}=254.725\,\mathrm K\simeq255\,\mathrm K
\quad( -18.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm C).}
$$
This is the <indoor-temperature limit of a Carnot heat pump>. It assumes the ideal pump retains its stated input-power capability over the operating range. Real <heat> exchangers require finite <temperature> differences and incur irreversibility, so the reversible result is an ideal upper performance bound.

**Conventional heater.** At this limiting outdoor <temperature>, the required delivered heating <power> is simply the house's <heat> loss:
$$
\boxed{\dot Q_{\mathrm{gas}}=\lambda\Delta T_{\max}=3.83\,\mathrm{kW}.}
$$
This is <heat> delivered to the house. The fuel-input <power> would be $3.83/\eta\,\mathrm{kW}$ for heater efficiency $\eta$. At the earlier outdoor <temperature> of $273\,\mathrm K$, the required delivered <heat> would instead be $0.1(293-273)=2.00\,\mathrm{kW}$; the final question's reference <temperature> must be kept clear.

If $0.5\,\mathrm{kW}$ is read literally as a maximum delivered <heat> rate rather than a work-input rating, the alternative answers are $\dot Q_{h,\max}=0.5\,\mathrm{kW}$, $T_{c,\min}=293-0.5/0.1=288\,\mathrm K$, and a conventional heater delivering $0.5\,\mathrm{kW}$ at that limit. Such a pump could not balance the stated house loss at $273\,\mathrm K$. Both readings follow consistently once the ambiguous <power> rating is defined; the Carnot calculation above uses the work-input reading.

**Configuration count.** Choose the end-to-end direction as positive and let $N_R,N_L$ count rightward and leftward links. Then
$$
N_R+N_L=N,\qquad a(N_R-N_L)=L,
\qquad N_R=\frac{N+L/a}{2},\quad N_L=\frac{N-L/a}{2}.
$$
A configuration is determined by choosing the positions of its leftward links, so
$$
\boxed{\Omega(L)=\frac{N!}{N_R!N_L!}.}
$$
The counts must be nonnegative integers; in particular, allowed signed extensions occur in steps of $2a$. If distance means $|L|$ with neither direction selected, there are two corresponding sets for $L>0$, adding a factor of two. That constant does not affect the <tension>. There is no excluded-volume penalty in this one-dimensional idealized count.

**<Entropy> from the multiplicity.** Put $x=L/(Na)$, so $|x|<1$. Applying the leading <Stirling's approximation> to all three large factorials gives
$$
S=k_B\ln\Omega\simeq k_B[N\ln N-N_R\ln N_R-N_L\ln N_L]
$$
$$
\boxed{S(x)\simeq Nk_B\ln2-\frac{Nk_B}{2}
\left[(1+x)\ln(1+x)+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right].}
$$
Terms subleading in $N$, including logarithmic factorial prefactors, have been omitted consistently. Expanding near zero extension gives
$$
S(L)\simeq Nk_B\ln2-\frac{k_BL^2}{2Na^2}
+O\left(Nk_Bx^4\right).
$$
Stretching reduces the number of available configurations and hence the <entropy>.

**<Tension> and the range of <Hooke's law>.** The model assigns no extension-dependent <internal energy>. At fixed <temperature>, the externally applied <tension> is $\mathcal F=(\partial F/\partial L)_T=-T(\partial S/\partial L)_T$. Differentiating the full leading <entropy> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F=\frac{k_BT}{2a}\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}
=\frac{k_BT}{a}\operatorname{artanh}x.}
$$
It is the external <force> needed to oppose the chain's restoring <entropic force>. Only when $|L|\ll Na$ can one put $\operatorname{artanh}x\simeq x$, yielding
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\simeq\frac{k_BT}{Na^2}L,\qquad |L|\ll Na.}
$$
The paper's displayed linear law therefore requires this small-extension approximation, which is not explicitly stated in its premise $L<L_0$. It is not the exact <force> law up to full extension. The entropic spring constant grows with <temperature> because stronger thermal disorder makes a given reduction in configurational <entropy> cost more free <energy>. This is the <one-dimensional entropic chain force-extension relation>.

**A sparse number of backward links.** Write $q=N_L\ll N$, so $N_R=N-q$ and $L=a(N-2q)$. Exactly,
$$
\Omega=\frac{N(N-1)\cdots(N-q+1)}{q!}
=\frac{N^q}{q!}\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}\left(1-\frac jN\right).
$$
For fixed $q$ and large $N$, each numerator factor approaches $N$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Omega\simeq\frac{N^{N_L}}{N_L!}.}
$$
More precisely, the <logarithm> of the product is $-q(q-1)/(2N)+O(q^3/N^2)$. A small relative error in the multiplicity requires $q^2/N\ll1$; merely $q/N\ll1$ need not make the omitted product close to one. The weaker sparse-link condition does suffice for the leading <entropy> density and leading high-extension <tension>. These are <sparse backward links near full chain extension>.

**<Tension> in that limit.** In the overlap regime $1\ll q\ll N$, use Stirling also for $q!$:
$$
S\simeq k_B[q\ln N-q\ln q+q],\qquad
\frac{dq}{dL}=-\frac1{2a}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\simeq\frac{k_BT}{2a}\ln\frac N{N_L}.}
$$
The full leading <entropy> would give $(k_BT/(2a))\ln[(N-N_L)/N_L]$, which has the same dominant <logarithm> when $N_L/N\to0$.

**Approaching full extension.** The continuum <force> law diverges logarithmically as $N_L\to0$, or $L\to Na$. One must not literally apply Stirling to a zero factorial: at the discrete endpoint there is exactly one directed configuration, $\Omega=1$, and its <entropy> is zero. A continuous fixed-extension <derivative> is not defined at that endpoint.

An independent force-ensemble calculation makes the limiting physical statement precise. For applied <tension> $\mathcal F$, a link has <partition function> $z=2\cosh(\beta\mathcal Fa)$, giving the exact <mean> extension
$$
\boxed{\langle L\rangle=Na\tanh(\beta\mathcal Fa).}
$$
It approaches $Na$ only as $\mathcal F\to\infty$ at positive <temperature>, and inversion reproduces the logarithmic <force> law for <mean> extension. In a real chain, bond extension or failure eventually replaces the ideal inextensible-link model.

**<Temperature> as an equilibrium relation.** Classical thermodynamics begins with the <zeroth law of thermodynamics>: <thermal equilibrium> is transitive. A <thermometer> can therefore assign a common empirical <temperature> to systems in mutual <thermal equilibrium>. Different <thermometer> properties initially give different monotone empirical scales; a resistance reading by itself does not define the absolute scale.

The <second law of thermodynamics> provides a substance-independent thermodynamic scale. A reversible engine operating between reservoirs has a <heat> ratio depending only on their <temperatures>. One chooses absolute <temperature> so that $Q_h/Q_c=T_h/T_c$, with $Q_h,Q_c$ the positive <heat> magnitudes. Its reversible efficiency is $1-T_c/T_h$. Equivalently, reversible <heat> defines <entropy> through $dS=\delta Q_{\mathrm{rev}}/T$. For an equilibrium simple compressible system,
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN,
\qquad
\boxed{\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N}.}
$$
Classical thermodynamics uses this relation without needing a microscopic account of <entropy>. A dilute-gas <thermometer> realizes an empirical scale approaching the thermodynamic scale after corrections for nonideal behavior.

**Statistical meaning of the same <derivative>.** In the microcanonical description, an equilibrium <energy> shell containing $\Omega(U,V,N)$ accessible states has $S=k_B\ln\Omega$. Its statistical <temperature> is consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac1T=k_B\left(\frac{\partial\ln\Omega}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N}.}
$$
To see why this is an equilibrium <temperature>, let two weakly coupled systems exchange a small <energy> while their total <energy> stays fixed. The combined multiplicity is $\Omega_A(U_A)\Omega_B(U_{\mathrm{tot}}-U_A)$. Maximizing its <logarithm> gives
$$
\frac{\partial\ln\Omega_A}{\partial U_A}
=\frac{\partial\ln\Omega_B}{\partial U_B},
$$
which is equality of the two inverse <temperatures>. Stability requires the total <entropy> maximum, not only stationarity. The overwhelmingly dominant <macrostate> in a large system therefore realizes the <thermodynamic equilibrium> condition.

A large reservoir gives a small subsystem the canonical <probabilities> $p_i\propto e^{-\beta E_i}$, with $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. In that ensemble, $F=-k_BT\ln Z$ and $S=-k_B\sum_i p_i\ln p_i$ agree with macroscopic thermodynamic relations in their usual equilibrium regime. The equipartition identity for a classical dilute monatomic gas, $\langle p^2/(2m)\rangle=3k_BT/2$, is a useful operational connection between microscopic motion and <temperature>, but is not a universal definition: quantum modes need not obey equipartition, and a system away from equilibrium need not have one <temperature> at all.

For finite systems one must specify the <entropy> convention and <energy> resolution; surface and fluctuation corrections can distinguish definitions that agree in the ordinary <thermodynamic limit>. Bounded-energy population-inverted systems can also have a negative <entropy> <derivative> and hence a negative statistical <temperature>. That possibility lies outside an ordinary positive-temperature ideal-gas <thermometer>; it does not <mean> <temperature> is merely an arbitrary average <kinetic energy>.

**Obtaining the <energy>–<temperature> conversion.** The <Boltzmann constant> connects <entropy> measured in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ with the dimensionless <logarithm> of a state count. Historically it could be determined from the <ideal gas law> $pV=Nk_BT$: measure the <molar gas constant> $R$ and independently determine the <Avogadro constant> $N_A$, then obtain $k_B=R/N_A$. The independent particle-number determination matters; using $R$ alone would not fix <energy> per particle.

An electrical method uses <Johnson-Nyquist noise>. A resistor of resistance $R_e$ at known <temperature> has, in the classical range $hf\ll k_BT$, a one-sided open-circuit voltage-noise spectrum $S_V(f)=4k_BTR_e$. Over a calibrated flat bandwidth $\Delta f$,
$$
\boxed{k_B=\frac{\langle v^2\rangle}{4TR_e\Delta f}.}
$$
A practical measurement uses an equilibrium resistor, a calibrated low-noise amplifier and a known <transfer function>, subtracts or independently characterizes amplifier <noise>, and replaces a nominal bandwidth by the actual <effective noise bandwidth>. Varying <temperature> and fitting the mean-square voltage is more informative than treating one amplified voltage sample as the thermal <variance>. Other primary methods relate <temperature> to sound speed in a dilute gas or to calibrated particle <diffusion> and <mobility>.

There is a historically relevant unit-definition qualification: by the date of this exam, the revised SI had already taken effect on 20 May 2019. The <kelvin> is now defined by fixing
$$
\boxed{k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\quad\text{exactly}.}
$$
The same experiments now realize or compare thermodynamic <temperatures> using this fixed constant, whereas historically they measured the conversion constant on an independently defined <temperature> scale.
