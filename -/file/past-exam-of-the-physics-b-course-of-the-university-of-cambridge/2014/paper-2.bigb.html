Treat the vapour as an <ideal gas>, neglect the liquid's <molar volume>, and take the molar <latent heat> as constant across this small interval. Integrating the <Clausius-Clapeyron equation> gives
$$
\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}=\frac{L_m}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right),
\qquad
\boxed{L_m=\frac{R\ln(1.1)}{1/230.7-1/233.4}
\simeq15.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.}
$$
Only the <pressure> ratio enters, so conversion from atmospheres to pascals is unnecessary.

The <Hohmann transfer orbit> has <periapsis> $r$ and <apoapsis> $2r$, so its <semi-major axis> is $3r/2$. <Kepler's third law> gives a period $(3/2)^{3/2}$ times the original <orbital period>. Only half the <ellipse> is traversed:

= Solution

Use $N$ turns of mean radius $a\simeq1.05\,\mathrm{mm}$, including the wire radius, on a light nonmagnetic rod. With wire radius $r_w$, length $l$ and <density> $\rho$, neglecting helical pitch gives
$$
m_B=NI\pi a^2,\qquad M=2\pi aN\rho\pi r_w^2,
\qquad J=M\left(\frac{l^2}{12}+\frac{a^2}{2}\right).
$$
The last expression is the transverse central <moment of inertia> of the uniformly distributed turns. The <magnetic torque> is $-m_BB_h\sin\theta$, so the <small-angle magnetic dipole oscillation> obeys $J\ddot\theta+m_BB_h\theta=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\rho r_w^2}{IaB_h}
\left(\frac{l^2}{12}+\frac{a^2}{2}\right)}\simeq4.0\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The number of turns cancels. Taking the turn radius as $1.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ instead gives $4.04\,\mathrm s$, consistent at the accuracy of the thin-winding model. The rod's inertia, damping and lead-wire <torque> are neglected.

= Solution

For slow gyroscopic <precession>, the gravitational <torque> turns the spin <angular momentum>: $Mg\ell\sin\theta=\Omega I_3\omega\sin\theta$, with $I_3=MR^2/2$. Thus
$$
\Omega\simeq\frac{2g\ell}{R^2\omega},\qquad
\boxed{T_p\simeq\frac{\pi R^2\omega}{g\ell}
=1.61\,\mathrm s,\qquad\omega=40\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
The inclination cancels at this order. More precisely, <uniform precession of a heavy symmetric top> satisfies
$$
\frac{I_1}{M}\cos\theta\,\Omega^2
-\frac{I_3}{M}\omega\Omega+g\ell=0,
\qquad \frac{I_1}{M}=\frac{R^2}{4}+\ell^2.
$$
Its slow root gives $T_p\simeq1.57\,\mathrm s$. **About $1.6\,\mathrm s$** is the slow-precession answer; arbitrary initial conditions can also produce <nutation> rather than exact steady <precession>.

= Solution

The <Biot-Savart law> gives the <magnetic field> of a steady thin-wire <electric current> in vacuum:
$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}
\int\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}
{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}.
$$
The cross product fixes the circulation direction by the <right-hand rule>; fields from different <electric current> elements superpose. Integration gives $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ outside a straight infinite wire and $B=\mu_0I/(2a)$ at the centre of a circular loop of radius $a$. A distributed <electric current> replaces $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$ by $\mathbf J\,dV'$. **This is a magnetostatic law:** arbitrary time-dependent <electric currents> require retarded electromagnetic fields, not instantaneous substitution.

= b
{parent=B6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In <magnetostatics>, <Ampère's circuital law> in vacuum is
$$
\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm enc},
\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J.
$$
It is especially useful when symmetry makes the tangential field constant on an <Amperian loop>. It gives $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ for an infinite straight wire, $B\simeq\mu_0nI$ inside a long <solenoid>, and $B\simeq\mu NI/(2\pi r)$ in an ideal <toroid>. The long-solenoid and toroidal expressions neglect fringing.

For time-dependent fields, <displacement current> is essential:
$$
\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}.
$$
In matter, the corresponding macroscopic form is $\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_{\rm free}+d\!\int\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf A/dt$. **The enclosed <electric current> alone is sufficient only in the magnetostatic limit.**

= Solution
{parent=c}

Because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$, a <magnetic vector potential> can be introduced with $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. It is not unique: $\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ gives the same field. In <magnetostatics>, the <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ gives $\nabla^2\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J$ in vacuum. For localized <electric currents>,
$$
\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int
\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,dV'.
$$
A <magnetic scalar potential> is more restricted. In a simply connected current-free region of constant permeability, write $\mathbf B=-\nabla\psi$; then $\nabla^2\psi=0$. Alternatively define the scalar potential for $\mathbf H$, differing by a constant permeability factor. **A single-valued scalar potential generally cannot cover a domain encircling a net <electric current>**, even where the local curl vanishes. The <vector> potential does not require the field itself to be curl-free.

{parent=B6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Guided electromagnetic waves propagate along a structure whose boundaries select transverse field patterns. At a perfect conductor the tangential <electric field> vanishes. In a hollow rectangular guide, TE modes have $E_z=0$ and TM modes have $H_z=0$. Their longitudinal <wavenumber> obeys
$$
k_z^2=\frac{\omega^2}{v^2}-k_\perp^2,
\qquad k_\perp^2=\left(\frac{m\pi}{a}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^2.
$$
Below the <waveguide cutoff frequency> $\omega_c=vk_\perp$, that mode is evanescent rather than freely propagating. TM modes require both indices positive; TE modes may have one zero but not both. For $a>b$, the lowest cutoff is the TE$_{10}$ mode, with $f_c=v/(2a)$. A hollow single-conductor guide has no TEM mode, whereas a two-conductor transmission line can support one without this geometrical cutoff.<Electromagnetic Fields and Energy by Hermann A. Haus and James R. Melcher/Rectangular Waveguide Modes>[§13.4, mode boundary conditions, index restrictions and lowest-cutoff discussion]{ref}

For a propagating TE or TM mode, $v_p=v/\sqrt{1-\omega_c^2/\omega^2}$ and $v_g=v\sqrt{1-\omega_c^2/\omega^2}$. The <phase velocity> can exceed $v$ without superluminal information transfer. Frequency-dependent group delay can broaden pulses; excitation of several modes adds intermodal <dispersion>.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

<Electromagnetic induction> produces an <electromotive force> when <magnetic flux linkage> changes. For an oriented moving loop,
$$
\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}.
$$
For a stationary loop this reduces to <Faraday's law of induction>, $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$. Motion in a static field can instead supply the motional term. <Lenz's law> gives the minus sign: the induced <electric current> opposes the flux change, with the required electrical <energy> supplied by mechanical <work> or the field source.

For linear fixed-geometry circuits, flux linkages satisfy $\Lambda_1=L_1I_1+MI_2$ and $\Lambda_2=MI_1+L_2I_2$. <Self-inductance> gives $\mathcal E_1=-L_1\dot I_1$; <mutual inductance> gives the additional term $-M\dot I_2$. Reciprocity gives the same mutual coefficient for both circuits, with its sign determined by winding orientation. The stored field <energy> is
$$
W_B=\frac12L_1I_1^2+\frac12L_2I_2^2+MI_1I_2,
\qquad |M|\le\sqrt{L_1L_2}.
$$
The bound follows from nonnegative <energy> for arbitrary <electric currents>. In a linear medium the equivalent field expression is $W_B=\tfrac12\int\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H\,dV$.

An ideal <electrical transformer> links both windings with one changing core flux, giving $V_1/V_2=N_1/N_2$ for corresponding voltage magnitudes. A loaded secondary drives an opposing magnetomotive force and increases primary <electric current>; ideally $I_1/I_2=N_2/N_1$ and input and output powers agree. **A <electrical transformer> exchanges voltage for <electric current>, not for extra <power>.** Real devices have winding resistance, leakage flux, eddy-current and <hysteresis> losses, and finite magnetizing <electric current>. A steady DC input gives no continuing secondary voltage after the switching transient and may cause excessive primary <electric current> or core saturation.

= Solution

An isotropic linear elastic solid has two independent elastic constants. For infinitesimal <strain>,
$$
\sigma_{ij}=2G\epsilon_{ij}+\lambda\epsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij},
\qquad K=\lambda+\frac23G.
$$
The <shear modulus> $G$ governs shape change and the <bulk modulus> $K$ governs volume change. <Young's modulus> $E$ and <Poisson's ratio> $\nu$ are equivalent parameters:
$$
G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\qquad K=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}.
$$
Positive <strain> <energy> requires $G,K>0$, hence $-1<\nu<1/2$; $\nu\to1/2$ is the incompressible limit. Small-amplitude bulk waves have speeds $c_P=\sqrt{(K+4G/3)/\rho}$ and $c_S=\sqrt{G/\rho}$. **Isotropy removes directional dependence, not the distinction between compression and shear.** These laws apply within the elastic small-strain regime, not after yielding or fracture.

{parent=B8}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a rigid body about a chosen origin,
$$
I_{ij}=\int\rho(\mathbf r)(r^2\delta_{ij}-r_ir_j)\,dV,
\qquad \mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega,
\qquad T_{\rm rot}=\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
The <inertia tensor> is real and symmetric, so <orthogonal> <principal axes> diagonalize it. <Angular momentum> is parallel to <angular velocity> for rotation about a <principal axis>, but need not be parallel in a general direction. Its <eigenvalues> are the <principal moments of inertia>.

If the origin is translated from the <centre of mass> by $\mathbf a$, the parallel-axis theorem becomes $I_{ij}=I_{ij}^{\rm CM}+M(a^2\delta_{ij}-a_ia_j)$. In body axes, Euler's equation is $\boldsymbol\tau=(d\mathbf L/dt)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L$. Thus **the full <tensor> is needed whenever the rotation is not confined to one fixed <principal axis>**.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For two bodies interacting through $U(\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2)$, define
$$
\mathbf R=\frac{m_1\mathbf r_1+m_2\mathbf r_2}{M},\quad
\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2,\quad
M=m_1+m_2,\quad\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{M}.
$$
Their <kinetic energy> separates exactly:
$$
T=\frac12M\dot{\mathbf R}^{\,2}+\frac12\mu\dot{\mathbf r}^{\,2}.
$$
Without external forces, the <centre of mass> moves uniformly while $\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\nabla U$. **The relative orbit is a one-body problem with <reduced mass> $\mu$**, not generally either individual <mass>. For $m_2\gg m_1$, $\mu\simeq m_1$; for equal masses $m$, $\mu=m/2$. If the interaction is central, relative <angular momentum> is conserved and the relative motion is planar.

= Solution
{parent=d}

<Dynamic viscosity> measures resistance to a <velocity> gradient. In Newtonian simple shear, $\tau_{xy}=\eta\,\partial u_x/\partial y$; positive <viscosity> dissipates mechanical <energy> into <heat>. <Kinematic viscosity> is $\nu=\eta/\rho$. For an incompressible fluid of constant <viscosity>,
$$
\rho\left(\partial_t\mathbf u+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u\right)
=-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u+\mathbf f.
$$
Scaling <velocity> by $U$ and length by $L$ gives the <Reynolds number> $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\eta=UL/\nu$, the ratio of inertial to viscous terms. At small <Reynolds number>, creeping-flow approximations neglect inertia; at large <Reynolds number>, inertia dominates much of the bulk but <viscosity> can remain crucial in boundary layers. **A large <Reynolds number> permits instability and <turbulence>; it does not by itself specify a universal transition threshold.** Geometry and disturbances also matter.

= Solution
{parent=B9}

A <central potential> conserves <angular momentum> and confines a nonradial orbit to a plane. With <reduced mass> $\mu$, the radial motion obeys
$$
E=\frac12\mu\dot r^2+U_{\rm eff}(r),\qquad
U_{\rm eff}=U(r)+\frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}.
$$
Turning points satisfy $E=U_{\rm eff}$. A radially bounded allowed interval gives bound motion, whereas an interval reaching infinity permits escape. The <energy> sign alone classifies these only with suitable assumptions about $U(\infty)$ and barriers. Circular orbits satisfy $U_{\rm eff}'=0$ and are radially stable when $U_{\rm eff}''>0$. Bound orbits in a general <central potential> need not close: successive periapses can precess.

For attractive inverse-square gravity, $U=-\kappa/r$ and
$$
r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\phi},\quad p=\frac{L^2}{\mu\kappa},\quad
 e^2=1+\frac{2EL^2}{\mu\kappa^2}.
$$
Ellipses have $E<0$, $e<1$, and the attracting centre at a focus; parabolic and hyperbolic escape have $e=1$ and $e>1$. Conservation of <angular momentum> gives equal swept areas in equal times. The inverse-square law additionally yields $T^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(m_1+m_2)]$. These are Kepler's laws; a harmonic <central potential> is another example with closed bound orbits, while generic central laws produce <precession>.

In a <gravitational assist>, a spacecraft's asymptotic <speed> relative to a planet is approximately unchanged but its direction turns. Transforming to the frame in which the planet has <velocity> $\mathbf V$ gives
$$
\Delta\varepsilon=\mathbf V\cdot(\mathbf u_{\rm out}-\mathbf u_{\rm in}).
$$
The spacecraft can gain or lose orbital <energy>; the planet supplies the compensating <energy> and <momentum>, with a very small <velocity> change because of its large <mass>.

Repulsive Coulomb scattering instead has $U=\kappa_C/r$. For incident relative <kinetic energy> $E$ and <impact parameter> $b$, the hyperbolic orbit gives
$$
b=\frac{\kappa_C}{2E}\cot\frac\theta2,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\left(\frac{\kappa_C}{4E}\right)^2
\csc^4\frac\theta2.}
$$
This <Rutherford scattering> formula follows by equating the incident annulus $2\pi b\,db$ to the scattered solid-angle ring. It assumes classical elastic scattering by a point Coulomb centre; screening, finite nuclear size or quantum effects can change the applicable regime.

A <heat engine> operates cyclically, absorbing <heat> $Q_h$ from a hotter source, rejecting <heat> $Q_c$ to a colder sink and delivering net <work> $W=Q_h-Q_c$. Its efficiency is $\eta=W/Q_h$. The working substance returns to its original state; the reservoirs and surroundings need not.

= a
{parent=C10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Suppose an engine were more efficient than a reversible Carnot engine between the same reservoirs. Couple it to a reversed Carnot engine, scaling the cycles to have equal <work>. With no net external <work>, their combined effect would be to transfer <heat> from the colder reservoir to the hotter one, contradicting the <second law of thermodynamics>. **No such engine can exceed $\eta_C=1-T_c/T_h$.** Equivalently, the cyclic <entropy> inequality gives $-Q_h/T_h+Q_c/T_c\ge0$, hence the same bound.

= b
{parent=C10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The reversible working substance changes <entropy> by $Q_h/T_h$ on its hot isotherm and $-Q_c/T_c$ on its cold isotherm, with zero change on the two reversible adiabats. Closure requires $Q_h/T_h=Q_c/T_c$. The substance has zero <entropy> change over a cycle, while the reservoirs have changes $-Q_h/T_h$ and $Q_c/T_c$. Thus **$\Delta S_{\rm universe}=0$** for the complete <Carnot cycle>.

= Solution
{parent=C10}
{disambiguate=cycle calculations}

Put $P_*=p_1V_1=nRT_1$. For the specified <ideal gas>, $C_V=3nR/2$, $C_p=5nR/2$ and $\gamma=5/3$. Isobaric expansion doubles <temperature>, so $T_2=2T_1$. Reversible adiabatic closure gives $TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}$, hence
$$
x\equiv\frac{T_3}{T_1}=2^{-2/3}=0.62996,
\qquad
\boxed{W_{31}^{\rm on}=\frac32(1-x)P_*=0.5551P_*.}
$$
There is no <heat> input on that compression. The other stages give
$$
Q_{12}=\frac52P_*,\quad W_{12}^{\rm by}=P_*,\quad
\boxed{Q_{23}=\frac32(x-2)P_*=-2.0551P_*.}
$$
Therefore $W_{\rm net}^{\rm by}=P_*-W_{31}^{\rm on}=0.44494P_*$ and
$$
\boxed{\eta=\frac{0.44494}{2.5}=0.17798\simeq17.8\%.}
$$
The hot reservoir changes <entropy> by $-Q_{12}/(2.5T_1)=-P_*/T_1$. The cold reservoir changes by $-Q_{23}/(0.5T_1)=4.11012P_*/T_1$. Since the gas completes a cycle,
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm universe}=3.11012\,\frac{p_1V_1}{T_1}>0.}
$$
Although the mechanical paths can be quasistatic, <heat> flows across finite <temperature> differences in both contacts. This <entropy> generation explains why the efficiency is well below the Carnot value $1-0.5/2.5=80\%$ between these reservoirs.

For an isothermal final compression at $T_1$, the preceding constant-volume cooling must instead end at $T_3=T_1$; keeping the old state 3 would not close the cycle. Compression then requires <work> $P_*\ln2$, while the <heat> input remains $5P_*/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm iso}=\frac{1-\ln2}{5/2}=0.12274\simeq12.3\%,}
$$
which is **lower**: this isothermal compression occurs at a higher <temperature> throughout than the original adiabat and consumes more <work>. It also rejects <heat> during compression.

With $F=U-TS$, the <thermodynamics of a liquid film> gives
$$
\boxed{dF=-S\,dT+\gamma\,dA.}
$$
Equality of mixed derivatives of $F(T,A)$ gives the <Maxwell relation>
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T
=-\left(\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial T}\right)_A.}
$$
Use the <area> convention already specified by $\gamma\,dA$; do not insert an additional factor for two film faces.

{parent=C11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Since $\gamma'=d\gamma/dT<0$, the <Maxwell relation> gives $(\partial S/\partial A)_T=-\gamma'>0$. Thus **isothermal stretching increases the film's <entropy>**.

At fixed <area>, $(\partial S/\partial T)_A=C_A/T$. Therefore
$$
dS=\frac{C_A}{T}\,dT-\gamma'\,dA.
$$
For reversible adiabatic stretching, $dS=0$ and
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{dT}{dA}\right)_S=\frac{T\gamma'}{C_A}<0.}
$$
The film **cools**, assuming positive <heat capacity>. Adiabaticity alone would not ensure constant <entropy> if the deformation were irreversible.

= Solution
{parent=C11}
{disambiguate=film calculations}

Reading the approximately straight trend in the supplied graph gives $\gamma(330\,\mathrm K)\simeq22\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ and $\gamma'\simeq-0.13\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. These are graph-read estimates, not more precise material constants. For $\Delta A=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, reversible isothermal stretching gives
$$
\boxed{W_{\rm on}=\gamma\Delta A\simeq2.2\,\mathrm{\mu J},
\qquad Q=T\Delta S=-T\gamma'\Delta A\simeq4.3\,\mathrm{\mu J}.}
$$
Both are positive: stretching does mechanical <work> on the film and requires <heat> input to prevent cooling. Its <internal energy> increases by about $6.5\,\mathrm{\mu J}$.

Write $\gamma=\alpha-\beta T$, with $\beta=-\gamma'\simeq1.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$. Constant $C_A$ then makes the reversible adiabatic condition
$$
\boxed{C_A\ln(T/T_*)+\beta A=\mathrm{constant},}
$$
where $T_*$ is an arbitrary fixed reference <temperature>; absorbing it into the constant gives the requested logarithm convention. Hence
$$
T_f=T_i e^{-\beta\Delta A/C_A},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta T\simeq-\frac{\beta T_i\Delta A}{C_A}
\simeq-6.1\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm K.}
$$
Here $C_A=0.070\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Since $\gamma-T\gamma'=\alpha$, integration gives $\Delta U=C_A(T_f-T_i)+\alpha\Delta A$. To first order this is
$$
\boxed{\Delta U\simeq\gamma(T_i)\Delta A\simeq+2.2\,\mathrm{\mu J}.}
$$
There is no <heat> exchange on this reversible adiabat. **Cooling and increasing <internal energy> are compatible:** positive <area> <work> more than offsets the decrease of the temperature-dependent <energy> term.

= Solution
{parent=C12}

In the <canonical ensemble>, $Z(\beta)=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ normalizes the probabilities $p_i=e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$. For temperature-independent levels,
$$
\boxed{\overline E=-\frac{1}{Z}\frac{dZ}{d\beta}
=-\frac{d\ln Z}{d\beta},\qquad
C=\frac{d\overline E}{dT}
=-\frac{1}{k_BT^2}\frac{d\overline E}{d\beta}.}
$$
The <derivative> holds external parameters, including <magnetic field>, fixed.

For one <independent symmetric three-level system> put $\epsilon=\mu B$ and $x=\beta\epsilon$. Then $Z=1+2\cosh x$ and
$$
\boxed{\overline E=-\frac{2\epsilon\sinh x}{1+2\cosh x}.}
$$
At nonzero fixed field, the ground state dominates as $T\to0$, so $\overline E\to-\mu|B|$. At high <temperature> all three levels become nearly equally populated and their mean tends to zero from below. At fixed positive <temperature> the <energy> is even in $B$, is quadratic near zero field, and approaches $-\mu|B|$ at large $|B|$ as the lowest level becomes occupied. The <heat capacity> vanishes both when excitations are frozen out and when populations are almost equal, with a positive <Schottky anomaly> between those limits.

\Image[paper-2-b2-three-level-curves.png]
{title=Three-level mean energy versus temperature and field, and its Schottky heat-capacity peak}

At $|x|\ll1$, expanding the hyperbolic functions gives
$$
\boxed{\overline E\simeq-\frac{2\mu^2B^2}{3k_BT},
\qquad C\simeq\frac{2\mu^2B^2}{3k_BT^2}.}
$$
Interpreting the stated <heat capacity> on the same one-atom basis as the preceding calculation, its tail coefficient gives
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac1B\sqrt{\frac{3ak_B}{2}}
\simeq2.89\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,T^{-1}}
=4.64\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\simeq0.500\,\mu_B.}
$$
Here $k_B=8.61733\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}$ is used consistently with $a$ in electronvolt kelvin. For a sample of $N$ independent atoms, the total coefficient would be $N$ times the one-atom coefficient; it must first be divided by $N$.

Finally, two derivatives of $Z$ supply the second <energy> moment:
$$
\overline{E^2}=\frac{Z''}{Z},\qquad
\frac{d\overline E}{d\beta}
=-\left(\frac{Z''}{Z}-\frac{Z'^2}{Z^2}\right)
=-\operatorname{Var}(E).
$$
Combining this with the heat-capacity <derivative> yields the <canonical energy fluctuation> relation
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(E)}{k_BT^2},
\qquad\Delta E=\sqrt{k_BT^2C}.}
$$
It also explains why this canonical <heat capacity> is nonnegative.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§§1.3.1–1.3.2, printed pages 18–20, equations (1.25)–(1.27)]{ref}

= Solution

**Molecules.** In a dilute classical gas, collisions establish a <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. The normalized <speed> distribution and its first two moments are
$$
f(v)=4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}v^2e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)},
\quad \overline v=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}},\quad
\overline{v^2}=\frac{3k_BT}{m}.
$$
<Momentum> transfer to a wall gives $p=nm\overline{v^2}/3=nk_BT$. Translational <internal energy> is $U=3Nk_BT/2$; rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom add <energy> when thermally populated, without changing the dilute ideal-gas <pressure> law. Classical <equipartition> gives $k_BT/2$ per quadratic contribution, but quantum level spacing can freeze out modes.

Only the molecules moving towards a surface contribute to its one-sided number flux:
$$
J=\frac14n\overline v=\frac{p}{\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}}.
$$
For an <effusive molecular energy flux>, faster molecules are preferentially sampled, giving mean translational <energy> $2k_BT$ per crossing molecule and $F_E=2k_BTJ$. This differs from multiplying $J$ by the bulk mean $3k_BT/2$. Collisions and finite molecular interactions produce transport and departures from the ideal-gas <equation of state>.

**<Photons>.** All <photons> travel at $c$ in vacuum, so their thermal distribution is over <frequency>, not over a range of speeds. The two polarizations and Bose-Einstein occupation with zero <chemical potential> give
$$
u_\nu\,d\nu=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}
\frac{d\nu}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1},\qquad
u\equiv\int_0^\infty u_\nu\,d\nu=a_RT^4,
\quad a_R=\frac{8\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^3}.
$$
Here the spectral quantity is $u_\nu$ and the total <energy> <density> is $u$. <Momentum flux> gives $p=u/3$, while isotropic angular averaging gives the one-sided <energy> flux $F_E=cu/4=\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4$ through a small aperture of a black-body cavity. The <photon> number flux is similarly $cn_\gamma/4$.

**Both pressures arise from <momentum> transfer, but their <equations of state> differ:** a classical molecular gas has $pV=Nk_BT$ with fixed molecule number, whereas a <photon gas> has $pV=U/3$, $U=a_RVT^4$ and an equilibrium <photon> number that changes with <temperature>. <Photon> creation and absorption by the walls are essential to the zero-chemical-potential equilibrium description.
