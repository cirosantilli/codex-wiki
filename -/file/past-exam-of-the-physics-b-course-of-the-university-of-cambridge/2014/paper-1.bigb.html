= A1

= Solution
{parent=A1}

For <rolling without slipping> through height $h$, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
mgh=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12I(v/R)^2,\qquad
\boxed{v^2=\frac{2gh}{1+I/(mR^2)}.}
$$
A uniform solid sphere has $I/(mR^2)=2/5$, a solid cylinder $1/2$, and a pipe $(1+r_{\rm in}^2/R^2)/2>1/2$. Thus **the ball is fastest, then the solid cylinder, then the pipe**. A smaller <moment of inertia> leaves more <energy> in translation. This assumes release from rest through the same height, adequate <static friction> and negligible rolling resistance; a thin pipe has $v^2=gh$.

= A2
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=A2}

The <Maxwell speed distribution> is proportional to $v^2e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}$. Setting its logarithmic derivative to zero gives the most probable <speed>:
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm mp}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}}
\simeq3.95\times10^2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Use molecular <oxygen>, with $M\simeq0.032\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$, and $T=300\,\mathrm K$.

= A3
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=A3}

For steady thin beams a distance $d$ apart, let $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ be their laboratory charge per <length>. Their current magnitudes are $|\lambda_j|v$, giving <force> magnitudes per <length>
$$
f_E=\frac{|\lambda_1\lambda_2|}{2\pi\epsilon_0d},\qquad
f_B=\frac{\mu_0|\lambda_1\lambda_2|v^2}{2\pi d},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{f_E}{f_B}=\frac{c^2}{v^2}.}
$$
The <electric force> is repulsive and the magnetic <force> attractive for the parallel electron <velocities>. The net <force> remains repulsive for $v<c$.

= A4
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=A4}

For isotropic planar motion, the <planar effusion flux> gives an escape rate through a gap of width $w$ is $\Gamma=w(N/\mathcal A)\langle v\rangle/\pi$, where $\mathcal A$ is tray <area>. At fixed equilibrium <kinetic energy>, or fixed shape of the scaled <speed distribution>, $v\propto m^{-1/2}$. Hence **halving the <mass> increases the rate by $\sqrt2$**. This planar <effusion> result assumes the same spatial <density> and an unrestricted small opening. Strictly, average <energy> alone fixes $\langle v^2\rangle$, not $\langle v\rangle$; an arbitrary change of distribution shape would need more information.

= A5
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=A5}

Assume uniform charge per unit <length> and the same angular <speed> $\Omega$. A charge element at distance $r$ has $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r$, so the <Biot-Savart law> gives the <centre field of a rigidly rotating charged loop> as $dB=\mu_0\Omega\,dq/(4\pi r)$. For one square side, with $b=a/2$,
$$
\int_{-b}^{b}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+b^2}}=2\ln(1+\sqrt2).
$$
Using $dq=Q\,dx/(4a)$ on each side gives
$$
B_{\square}=\frac{\mu_0Q\Omega}{2\pi a}\ln(1+\sqrt2),\qquad
B_{\circ}=\frac{\mu_0Q\Omega}{2\pi a}.
$$
Thus **$B_{\square}/B_{\circ}=\ln(1+\sqrt2)\simeq0.8814$**: the circular field is larger by $1/\ln(1+\sqrt2)\simeq1.135$. This is a rigidly rotating charge distribution, not a current constrained to the sides of a stationary square.

= Solution
{parent=B6}

Specifying potential on the complete boundary gives a <Dirichlet boundary condition> and a unique harmonic solution. Specifying its normal derivative gives a <Neumann boundary condition>, with the zero-net-flux compatibility condition and an undetermined additive constant. Mixed conditions on complementary portions also give uniqueness when a potential reference is fixed. In an exterior domain, conditions at infinity are needed. These statements follow by applying <Green's first identity> to the difference of two solutions. A metal in <electrostatic equilibrium> is an equipotential: $\Phi=V_0$, tangential <electric field> zero, and normal field $E_n=\sigma/\epsilon_0$ just outside a vacuum interface.

For the <conducting-wedge potential expansion>, put $u=\Phi-V_0$ in the empty sector $0<\theta<\alpha$. For $u=r^\nu\Psi(\theta)$, Laplace's equation gives $\Psi''+\nu^2\Psi=0$. Vanishing values on both faces require $\Psi\propto\sin(n\pi\theta/\alpha)$, hence
$$
\boxed{\Phi=V_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_nr^{n\pi/\alpha}+b_nr^{-n\pi/\alpha}\right)\sin\frac{n\pi\theta}{\alpha}.}
$$
Bounded potential at the corner sets $b_n=0$. The coefficients still require outer boundary data; the metal potential alone cannot select them. For a generic nonzero $a_1$, put $p=\pi/\alpha$. Differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{E_r\simeq-pa_1r^{p-1}\sin p\theta,\qquad E_\theta\simeq-pa_1r^{p-1}\cos p\theta.}
$$
If $a_1=0$, the first nonzero higher harmonic replaces this leading term. For $\alpha=\pi/2$, $u\simeq2a_1xy$, so $\mathbf E=-2a_1(y,x)$ and field lines satisfy **$x^2-y^2=\mathrm{constant}$**, meeting the metal faces normally and tending to zero field at the corner. For $\alpha=3\pi/2$, the <electrostatic corner-field exponent> is $-1/3$. A real tip's rounding cuts off this divergence; breakdown or <field emission> can limit the attainable field.

= B7
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B7}

Applying <Gauss's law for magnetism> to a pillbox and <Ampère's law> to an interface-crossing rectangle gives
$$
\boxed{\hat n\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0,\qquad
\hat n\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f.}
$$
Here $\hat n$ points from material 1 to 2 and $\mathbf K_f$ is free surface current. With no such current, $B_n$ and $H_t$ are continuous. For linear isotropic media, $B_{t2}/\mu_2=B_{t1}/\mu_1$ and $\mu_2H_{n2}=\mu_1H_{n1}$. The <magnetic fields> need not have equal magnitudes on either side.

With negligible leakage, <Gauss's law for magnetism> makes each cross-section of a <magnetic flux> tube carry the same flux $\mathcal F$. High permeability guides it through the core; a short gap permits neglect of fringing. Flux conservation across these particular geometric sections is an approximation, not a consequence of zero divergence without controlling side leakage.

Let $L$ be the constant-radius arc marked in the figure, and $\mu_r=\mu/\mu_0$. For each straight taper, the <radius> changes linearly from $R$ to $r$, giving the <reluctance of a conical magnetic pole>
$$
\int_{\rm taper}\frac{dx}{\mu\pi a(x)^2}=\frac{s}{\mu\pi Rr}.
$$
Summing <magnetic reluctances> to find the <gap field of a tapered electromagnet>, and using $B_g=\mathcal F/(\pi r^2)$ gives
$$
NI=\mathcal F\left(\frac{L}{\mu\pi R^2}+\frac{2s}{\mu\pi Rr}+\frac{h}{\mu_0\pi r^2}\right),\qquad
\boxed{H_g=\frac{NI}{h+\mu_r^{-1}(Lr^2/R^2+2sr/R)},\quad B_g=\mu_0H_g.}
$$
If $L$ is instead defined as the total magnetic-material <length>, replace the displayed uniform-arc <length> by $L-2s$. The comparison untapered core has uniform <radius> $R$ and material <length> $L+2s$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{H_g}{H_{g0}}=
\frac{h+(L+2s)/\mu_r}{h+(Lr^2/R^2+2sr/R)/\mu_r}.}
$$
**The original PDF specifies $s/L\ll r/R$**; the TeX transcription reverses this inequality. In the printed limit the taper reluctances are small relative to that of the broad arc. The gain is approximately $[h+L/\mu_r]/[h+Lr^2/(\mu_rR^2)]$, approaching $(R/r)^2$ if the gap reluctance is also negligible. If the gap dominates, both fields instead approach $NI/h$ and the gain approaches one. <magnetic saturation> near the small poles, gap fringing, leakage and winding heating limit practical enhancement.

= B8
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B8}

The macroscopic <Maxwell equations>, with free charge and current, are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D.
$$
Inside this body $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$. The wave <magnetic field> lies along $z$; its sign follows the local wave component. Faraday's and Ampère's laws give
$$
\partial_xE_y=-\mu_0\partial_tH_z,\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf J=(0,-\partial_xH_z-\epsilon_0\partial_tE_y,0).}
$$
Static background fields are excluded from this wave solution. The magnetic <force> <density> is along $x$, with $f_x=\mu_0J_yH_z$. For a steady periodic state, $\overline{H_z\partial_tE_y}=-\overline{E_y\partial_tH_z}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\overline f_x=-\partial_x\left(\tfrac12\mu_0\overline{H_z^2}+\tfrac12\epsilon_0\overline{E_y^2}\right).}
$$
The result gives <radiation-force density without Ohm's law>. Integrating through the body gives its <pressure> as the boundary value of the averaged <electromagnetic energy> <density> minus its value deep inside. In both limiting cases below the latter is zero. Take $A$ as the incident <electric field>'s peak amplitude and write $Z_v=\sqrt{\mu_0/\epsilon_0}$.

= a
{parent=B8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

With no reflected field, $\overline{E_y^2}=A^2/2$ and $\overline{H_z^2}=A^2/(2Z_v^2)$ at the entrance. Thus
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm abs}=\frac12\epsilon_0A^2=\frac{\overline S_{\rm inc}}c,\qquad
\overline S_{\rm inc}=\frac{A^2}{2Z_v}.}
$$
Complete absorption transfers the incident normal <momentum flux> to the body.

= b
{parent=B8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

At a perfect conductor, the reflected <electric field> cancels the incident tangential field, while their <magnetic fields> add. Thus $E_y(0)=0$ and $H_z(0)$ has peak amplitude $2A/Z_v$. Taking the zero-penetration limit, including its surface <force>, gives
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm refl}=\epsilon_0A^2=\frac{2\overline S_{\rm inc}}c.}
$$
Reversal of the normal momentum doubles the <pressure> compared with absorption at the same incident Poynting flux.

{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B9}

$Z$ is the terminating load impedance, $Z_0$ the line's <characteristic impedance>, and $k$ the propagation <wave number>; $l$ is its <length>. In the lossless convention printed, an open circuit gives $Z_{\rm in}=-iZ_0\cot kl$, a short circuit gives $iZ_0\tan kl$, and a matched load $Z=Z_0$ gives **$Z_{\rm in}=Z_0$ for every <length>**. The divergent or zero limits at isolated electrical <lengths> are understood continuously.

Put $\theta=kl$, $c_\theta=\cos\theta$, $s_\theta=\sin\theta$. The voltage/current <matrix> of one section is
$$
M_j=\begin{pmatrix}c_\theta&iZ_js_\theta\\is_\theta/Z_j&c_\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a pair beginning with $Z_1$, write $M_1M_2=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its entries are $A=c_\theta^2-(Z_1/Z_2)s_\theta^2$, $D=c_\theta^2-(Z_2/Z_1)s_\theta^2$, $B=i(Z_1+Z_2)s_\theta c_\theta$, and $C=i(1/Z_1+1/Z_2)s_\theta c_\theta$. The <alternating-impedance transmission line> has unchanged input $X$, its <Bloch impedance>, obeying $X=(AX+B)/(CX+D)$. Hence, away from the removable dividing zeros,
$$
iX^2(Z_1+Z_2)+X(Z_1^2-Z_2^2)\tan\theta-iZ_1Z_2(Z_1+Z_2)=0,
$$
$$
\boxed{X=\frac{i(Z_1-Z_2)\tan\theta\ \pm\sqrt{4Z_1Z_2-(Z_1-Z_2)^2\tan^2\theta}}2.}
$$
For real positive $Z_1,Z_2$, select the branch carrying <energy> into the line or decaying into it. A positive real part selects the pass-band root; infinitesimal passive loss resolves the stop-band branch. At $\theta=m\pi$ the cell <matrix> is the identity, so the exact fixed-point condition alone does not determine $X$; at odd quarter-wave <lengths> the appropriate limiting impedance can be zero or infinite.

In a <transmission-line stop band>, a lossless semi-infinite ladder totally reflects because its physical input is reactive. Equivalently its cell has
$$
\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M=1-\frac{(Z_1+Z_2)^2}{2Z_1Z_2}\sin^2\theta<-1.
$$
Thus, for nonzero contrast, the stop-band interiors satisfy
$$
\boxed{|\tan kl|>\frac{2\sqrt{Z_1Z_2}}{|Z_1-Z_2|},\quad\text{equivalently}\quad
\sin^2kl>\frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2}.}
$$
With $\theta_c=\arcsin[2\sqrt{Z_1Z_2}/(Z_1+Z_2)]$, these are $m\pi+\theta_c<kl<(m+1)\pi-\theta_c$; band-edge reflection follows by a limit. If $k=\omega/u$, multiply the endpoints by $u/l$ for <angular frequencies>. Identical impedances have no stop band. A finite ladder can transmit by tunnelling, unlike the semi-infinite idealization.

= C10
{parent=Section C}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=C10}

A <fictitious force> accounts for acceleration of the chosen <reference frame> rather than an additional interaction. For axes rotating at constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$, differentiating a vector uses $(d/dt)_{\rm lab}=(d/dt)_{\rm rot}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times$. Twice applying this gives
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm lab}=\mathbf a+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v+
\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r),
$$
so moving the last two terms to the <force> side produces the Coriolis and <centrifugal forces>. Here $\mathbf v$ is measured in rotating axes.

The <rotating saddle trap> has potential <force> $(-m\omega^2x,+m\omega^2y)$. Therefore
$$
\ddot x=(\Omega^2-\omega^2)x+2\Omega\dot y,\qquad
\ddot y=(\Omega^2+\omega^2)y-2\Omega\dot x.
$$
The trial solution gives
$$
(\epsilon^2+\Omega^2)^2-\omega^4-4\Omega^2\epsilon^2=0,
\qquad\boxed{\epsilon_\pm^2=\Omega^2\pm\omega^2.}
$$
Both modes are bounded for arbitrary sufficiently small initial displacements and <velocities> precisely when **$|\Omega|>\omega$**. At equality there is a zero-frequency secular mode; the exactly stationary solution does not establish stability.

For <rapidly rotating saddle confinement> with $\Omega\gg\omega>0$, real mode ellipses can be written
$$
(x,y)_+=A\left(\cos\epsilon_+t,-\frac\Omega{\epsilon_+}\sin\epsilon_+t\right),\qquad
(x,y)_-=B\left(\cos\epsilon_-t,-\frac{\epsilon_-}\Omega\sin\epsilon_-t\right).
$$
They are nearly circular clockwise motions at rates $\epsilon_\pm\simeq\Omega\pm\omega^2/(2\Omega)$. Rotating these axes back to the laboratory cancels the large angular rate: **the leading laboratory motion is a slow approximately harmonic ellipse with <frequency> $\omega^2/(2\Omega)$**, plus small rapid micromotion. It results from the changing saddle <force>, not a static potential minimum. At next order the ellipse precesses prograde at $\omega^4/(8\Omega^3)$. The rotating-potential model and this guiding-centre distinction are analysed in the primary work cited here.<Rotating saddle trap as Foucault's pendulum by Oleg N. Kirillov and Mark Levi>[preprint pp. 2 and 6–7, equations (12)–(18)]{ref}

= C11
{parent=Section C}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=C11}

A <normal mode> is a <small oscillation> with fixed relative displacement amplitudes, all coordinates oscillating at one <frequency>. <linearization> gives $M\ddot{\mathbf y}=-K\mathbf y$, so mode shapes solve $K\mathbf u=\omega_n^2M\mathbf u$.

Write $C=q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. <force> balance on an outer ion gives $m\omega^2x_0=C/x_0^2+C/(2x_0)^2$, hence
$$
\boxed{x_0=\left(\frac{5q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0m\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
A pair separated by $d$ has quadratic <energy> $C(y_j-y_i)^2/d^3$, equivalent to spring constant $2C/d^3$. The adjacent-pair constants are $8m\omega^2/5$, and the outer-pair constant is $m\omega^2/5$. Adding the trap stiffness gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf y}=-\frac{\omega^2}{5}
\begin{pmatrix}14&-8&-1\\-8&21&-8\\-1&-8&14\end{pmatrix}\mathbf y.
$$
In the <three-ion axial normal modes>, the translation mode leaves every separation unchanged. The antisymmetric breathing mode leaves the central ion fixed; the remaining vector is <orthogonal> to both. Direct multiplication yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{mode vector}&\text{angular frequency}\\\hline
(1,1,1)&\omega\\
(1,0,-1)&\sqrt3\omega\\
(1,-2,1)&\sqrt{29/5}\,\omega
\end{array}}
$$
For <breathing excitation after a trap-frequency quench>, put $\omega'=\omega+\delta\omega$ and $x_0'=x_0(\omega/\omega')^{2/3}$. The displacement from the new equilibrium is solely along the breathing vector and the initial <velocity> is zero. To first order in the fractional quench,
$$
\boxed{x_2(t)=0,\qquad x_{1,3}(t)=\mp\left[x_0'+(x_0-x_0')\cos(\sqrt3\omega't)\right],
\quad x_0-x_0'\simeq\frac{2x_0\delta\omega}{3\omega}.}
$$
The <frequency> shown is the linearized <frequency> about the new equilibrium. Nonlinear amplitude corrections are beyond this small-quench result.

= C12
{parent=Section C}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=C12}

At distance $y$ from the <neutral axis>, bending <strain> has magnitude $y/R$ and the corresponding <stress> is $Ey/R$. Its moment is therefore
$$
\boxed{B=\int_A y\frac{Ey}{R}\,dA=\frac{EI}{R},\qquad I=\int_Ay^2\,dA.}
$$
The <second moment of area> is about the neutral bending axis and has units of <length> to the fourth power. This assumes a slender linear-elastic beam, small <strains> and plane sections remaining plane, not necessarily small deflections.

For horizontal compression $F>0$, moment balance across a short arc gives $dB/ds=-F\,dy/ds=-F\sin\theta$. Since $B=EI\theta'$, the exact-angle <Euler elastica> equation and its first integral are
$$
\boxed{EI\theta''+F\sin\theta=0,\qquad
\frac{EI}{2}\theta'^2-F\cos\theta=C.}
$$
The first integral follows by multiplying by $\theta'$ and integrating; no small-angle replacement has been made.

For tension set $F=-T$. The straight far-field ends give $C=T$, so on the increasing-angle <tensile elastica loop> branch
$$
\theta'=2\sqrt{\frac{T}{EI}}\sin\frac\theta2,\qquad
\ln\tan\frac\theta4=s\sqrt{\frac{T}{EI}}.
$$
Choosing $s=0$ at $\theta=\pi$ gives
$$
\boxed{\theta(s)=4\arctan e^{\alpha s},\qquad\alpha=\sqrt{T/(EI)}.}
$$
The terminal <angles> are $0$ and $2\pi$, the same physical tangent direction. At the top, $\theta'(0)=2\alpha$ and the <bending moment> is $2EI\alpha$. The horizontal tensile <force> has lever arm $h$, so
$$
\boxed{Th=2EI\alpha,\qquad h=2\sqrt{EI/T}.}
$$
Integrating $(x',y')=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ independently gives $x=s-2\tanh(\alpha s)/\alpha$, $y=2\operatorname{sech}(\alpha s)/\alpha$, confirming the height. This ideal loop neglects self-contact and thickness at its crossing.

= C13
{parent=Section C}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=C13}

In <irrotational flow>, the <velocity> has a <velocity potential> locally, and globally in a simply connected flow domain. <incompressibility> then gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=\nabla^2\Phi=0$. For a sphere moving at <speed> $U$ along $z$ through fluid at rest far away, the <potential of a translating sphere> is a decaying axisymmetric dipole. Its solution and its impermeability condition give
$$
\Phi=\frac{\beta\cos\theta}{r^2},\quad
\left.\partial_r\Phi\right|_a=U\cos\theta,
\qquad\boxed{\beta=-\frac12Ua^3.}
$$
These are laboratory fluid <velocities> expressed relative to a moving origin. A <velocity> field measured in the sphere-rest frame instead requires adding $-Ur\cos\theta$ to the potential.

**The vector identity printed in the PDF has its signs reversed.** The product rule gives the consistent identity $v^2=\nabla\cdot(\Phi\mathbf v)-\Phi\nabla\cdot\mathbf v$. With outward normal of the exterior fluid equal to $-\hat r$ at the sphere, the <divergence theorem> yields
$$
\int_{r>a}v^2\,dV=\int_{r=a}\Phi(-v_r)\,dS
=\frac{U^2a}{2}\int\cos^2\theta\,dS=\frac{2\pi a^3U^2}{3}.
$$
The integral at infinity vanishes. Hence
$$
K_f=\frac{\pi\rho_0a^3U^2}{3}=\tfrac12m_aU^2,
\qquad\boxed{m_a=\frac{2\pi\rho_0a^3}{3},\quad m_{\rm eff}=\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}(\rho+\rho_0/2).}
$$
This <added mass of a sphere> is half the displaced fluid <mass>. For a spring of unchanged stiffness, <buoyancy> only shifts the static equilibrium. Its <oscillation frequency with fluid added mass> is
$$
\boxed{\omega_s'=\omega_s\sqrt{\frac\rho{\rho+\rho_0/2}}.}
$$
For a small-angle <simple pendulum>, <buoyancy> also reduces restoring <torque> from $mg\ell\theta$ to $(m-\rho_0V)g\ell\theta$, while the inertia is $(m+m_a)\ell^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_p'=\omega_p\sqrt{\frac{\rho-\rho_0}{\rho+\rho_0/2}}.}
$$
The downward equilibrium is stable only for $\rho>\rho_0$. The ideal results assume unbounded fluid, negligible <viscosity>, small excursions, and no important free-surface or container corrections.
