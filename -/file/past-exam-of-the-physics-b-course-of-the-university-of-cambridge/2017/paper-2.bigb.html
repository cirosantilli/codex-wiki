Assume a hollow square <electromagnetic waveguide> with perfectly conducting walls, filled with <vacuum> or air. For side $a$, the <waveguide cutoff frequency> is
$$
f_{c,mn}=\frac{c}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac m a\right)^2+\left(\frac n a\right)^2}.
$$
A transverse electric mode can have one zero index, but not both. A transverse magnetic mode requires both indices positive. Thus the lowest cutoff belongs to the two degenerate transverse electric modes $\mathrm{TE}_{10}$ and $\mathrm{TE}_{01}$, with
$$
\boxed{f_c=\frac{c}{2a}=\frac{2.9979\times10^8}{2\times10^{-2}}\,\mathrm{Hz}
\simeq15.0\,\mathrm{GHz}.}
$$
The corresponding cutoff <vacuum> <wavelength> is $2a=2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Strictly, a travelling mode carrying <power> along the guide needs $f>f_c$: at the cutoff itself, the axial <wavenumber> and <group velocity> are zero. The number above is therefore the lower <frequency> threshold, not a propagating mode with nonzero longitudinal <energy flux> exactly at that <frequency>. A <dielectric> filling would replace $c$ by the wave <speed> in that medium.

Treat the lake as a large <thermal reservoir> at $T_f=283.15\,\mathrm K$, and take the copper's <heat capacity> $C=50\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ constant over the cooling interval from $T_i=783.15\,\mathrm K$. Although the actual cooling is irreversible, <entropy> is a state function, so its change can be calculated along a hypothetical reversible path:
$$
\Delta S_{\rm block}=\int_{T_i}^{T_f}\frac{C}{T}\,dT
=C\ln\left(\frac{T_f}{T_i}\right)
=\boxed{-50.9\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
The lake receives $Q=C(T_i-T_f)=2.50\times10^4\,\mathrm J$ at constant <temperature>, giving
$$
\Delta S_{\rm lake}=\frac{Q}{T_f}=88.3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
Therefore, neglecting other changes,
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm Universe}=\Delta S_{\rm block}+\Delta S_{\rm lake}
=37.4\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}>0.}
$$
The positive total is the <entropy> generated by transferring <heat> across a finite <temperature> difference. The copper's <entropy> decreases, but the colder lake gains a greater amount.

Let $s(t)$ be the <length> of the free straight segment from the current contact point to the <mass>, and let $\theta$ measure the contact point's <rotation> from first contact. For an inextensible taut rope, winding through angle $\theta$ uses <length> $R\theta$:
$$
s=L-R\theta.
$$
The point <mass> follows an <involute> of the pole's circular cross-section. Its instantaneous <velocity> is perpendicular to the free rope. To verify the geometry explicitly, use planar polar <unit vectors> at the contact point:
$$
\mathbf x=R\mathbf e_r(\theta)+s\mathbf e_\theta(\theta),
\qquad \frac{d\mathbf x}{d\theta}
=(R+s')\mathbf e_\theta-s\mathbf e_r=-s\mathbf e_r.
$$
The rope's <tension> therefore does no <work>, and the <speed> stays at its initial value $v$. The geometry gives $v=s\dot\theta$. Consequently
$$
\frac{ds}{dt}=-R\dot\theta=-\frac{Rv}{s},
\qquad s^2=L^2-2Rvt.
$$
The distance from the pole's axis is $\sqrt{R^2+s^2}$, so a point <mass> first reaches the pole when $s=0$. The <winding time around a circular pole> is thus
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm hit}=\frac{L^2}{2Rv}.}
$$
This has units of <time>. The model assumes a light inextensible rope, no <energy> loss at contact and a point <mass>. The required <tension> $mv^2/s$ diverges at the ideal final contact, so finite strength and size matter very close to impact.

The <symmetry> is <electromagnetic reciprocity> applied to <mutual inductance>. For thin closed circuits in <vacuum>, the magnetic <vector> potential generated by circuit B is
$$
\mathbf A_B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I_B}{4\pi}\oint_B\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell_B}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_B|}.
$$
Stokes theorem converts the <magnetic flux> through circuit A into its magnetic <vector> potential <circulation>. Dividing by $I_B$ gives <Neumann's mutual-inductance formula>,
$$
M_{AB}=\frac{\Phi_{A\leftarrow B}}{I_B}
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_A\oint_B
\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell_A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell_B}{|\mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B|}.
$$
The <dot product> and separation are unchanged by interchanging A and B, so
$$
\boxed{M_{AB}=M_{BA}.}
$$
Each has units $\mathrm{Wb\,A^{-1}}=\mathrm H$. The winding orientations must be used consistently to determine the common sign. The same reciprocity holds in ordinary linear reciprocal magnetic media; it is not an unrestricted statement about biased nonreciprocal materials. Equivalently, a reversible <magnetic energy> function has equal mixed <derivatives> with respect to the two currents.

Assume all rings have the same <circulation> $\Gamma$ and orientation, and that there is no imposed background flow. <Vortex lines> describe the direction of <vorticity>, not the direction of <velocity>. For an incompressible <fluid>, the <velocity> induced by a thin <vortex filament> has the same Biot-Savart <integral> form as the <magnetic field> of a current filament:
$$
\mathbf u(\mathbf x)=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi}\oint
\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell\times(\mathbf x-\mathbf x')}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|^3}.
$$
A closely spaced coaxial array of <vortex rings> is therefore analogous to a solenoid. The <velocity field> passes **along the common axis through the rings and returns outside a finite array**. Its orientation reverses if every <circulation> is reversed. In a long array, far from its ends and vortex cores, the <velocity field of a dense coaxial vortex-ring array> is approximately
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\simeq\frac{\Gamma}{l}\widehat{\mathbf z}\quad(\rho<r),
\qquad \mathbf u\simeq0\quad(\rho>r).}
$$
The exterior field is exactly zero only in the ideal continuous infinitely long limit, with the background <velocity> set to zero. In a finite array the <streamlines> spread out at one end, run back outside, and converge into the other. A large number of closely spaced rings alone does not guarantee that the total array <length> greatly exceeds $r$; if it does not, the end and return flows remain substantial. Different unspecified ring circulations would require superposing their actual signed contributions.

Maxwell's <displacement current> is the electric-field contribution needed to make <Ampère's law> consistent with <charge conservation>. In macroscopic form,
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.}
$$
The <displacement current> density $\mathbf J_d=\partial_t\mathbf D$ has units $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$. Since $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P$, it contains the <vacuum> field term and, in matter, the polarization-current contribution. It need not represent <free charge> moving through a material.

Taking the <divergence> gives $0=\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f+\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D)$. <Gauss's law>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f$, then yields the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho_f+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_f=0$. Without the <displacement> term, Ampère's original law would incorrectly require every current distribution to be divergence-free even while charge accumulates.

A charging parallel-plate <capacitor> makes this concrete. Ignoring fringing, $D=Q/A$, so the <displacement current> across its <dielectric> gap is $I_d=A\partial_tD=dQ/dt$, equal to the conduction <electric current> in its wire. The <integral> law
$$
\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\int_S\mathbf J_f\cdot d\mathbf S+\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf S
$$
therefore gives the same answer whether the spanning surface cuts the wire or passes through the gap. Together with <Faraday's law>, this field term also permits self-consistent <electromagnetic waves> in <vacuum>, with <speed> $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$.

For a cold, unmagnetized, collisionless <plasma>, take an equilibrium <electron> density $n_e$, <electrons> of <mass> $m_e$ and charge $-e$, and approximately stationary ions. Use fields proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$. The <electron> <displacement> satisfies
$$
m_e\ddot{\mathbf x}=-e\mathbf E,
\qquad \mathbf x=\frac{e\mathbf E}{m_e\omega^2}.
$$
Its <electric polarization> is $\mathbf P=-n_ee\mathbf x$, hence the <cold collisionless plasma dielectric response>
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2},
\qquad \omega_p^2=\frac{n_ee^2}{\epsilon_0m_e}.}
$$
The negative susceptibility arises from the inertial <electron> response, rather than a restoring <force> binding an <electron> to a particular atom.

A transverse <electromagnetic wave> then obeys
$$
k^2c^2=\omega^2\epsilon_r=\omega^2-\omega_p^2.
$$
For $\omega>\omega_p$, the <plasma> supports propagation, with <refractive index> $n_r=\sqrt{1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2}<1$. The <phase velocity> is greater than $c$, while the <group velocity> is $c\sqrt{1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2}<c$; their product is $c^2$, and no superluminal <signal> is implied. Below the <plasma frequency>, the bulk <wavenumber> is imaginary and the field is evanescent. A sufficiently thick <plasma> largely reflects such radiation in the lossless model.

Including an <electron> momentum-relaxation rate $\nu_c$ replaces the denominator by $\omega(\omega+i\nu_c)$:
$$
\epsilon_r=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega(\omega+i\nu_c)}.
$$
Its <imaginary part> describes absorption with the stated <time> convention. Finite <electron> <temperature> also produces <plasma screening> of slowly varying electrostatic fields, with $\lambda_D=\sqrt{\epsilon_0k_BT_e/(n_ee^2)}$. That spatial, longitudinal screening problem is distinct from simply taking $\omega=0$ in the cold transverse-wave formula. Magnetic bias, ion motion and kinetic resonances require more general <dielectric> responses.

For weak fields in a linear isotropic material, <magnetization> is $\mathbf M=\chi_m\mathbf H$, with <magnetic susceptibility> $\chi_m$. **<Paramagnetism> has $\chi_m>0$**, while **<diamagnetism> has $\chi_m<0$**. In either case, $\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M)=\mu_0(1+\chi_m)\mathbf H$. These weak responses generally disappear when the applied field is removed, unlike hysteretic <ferromagnetism>.

A paramagnet can contain permanent <magnetic moments> from unpaired <electrons> or atomic <angular momentum>. Alignment lowers the <energy> $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$, while thermal disorder opposes it. For independent classical moments of magnitude $\mu$, their weak-field average projection is $\mu^2B/(3k_BT)$, so
$$
\chi_m\simeq\frac{\mu_0n\mu^2}{3k_BT}.
$$
This is <Curie's law>; it assumes weak <magnetization> and no important interactions between moments. With quantum <angular momentum> $J$, replace $\mu^2$ by $(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)$. Strong-field <magnetization> saturates instead of growing linearly without limit. Conduction <electrons> also exhibit <Pauli paramagnetism>: only occupations near the <Fermi surface> readily redistribute between <spin> states, giving a nearly temperature-independent susceptibility when $T\ll T_F$.

<Diamagnetism> is an induced orbital response opposing the applied <magnetic field>. For bound <electrons>, a weak field changes orbital motion and induces an opposing <magnetic moment>. For an isotropic collection of atoms, the elementary orbital estimate is
$$
\chi_{\rm dia}=-\frac{\mu_0ne^2}{6m_e}\sum_j\langle r_j^2\rangle,
$$
where the sum runs over the <electrons> of one atom. It is usually small and weakly <temperature> dependent. Delocalized <electrons> also have orbital Landau <diamagnetism>; for an ideal three-dimensional free <electron> <gas> with a quadratic isotropic dispersion and <electron spin> with $g=2$, $\chi_L=-\chi_P/3$. Both <spin> and orbital contributions must be included to determine the total response. <Diamagnetism> is not a consequence of permanent dipoles choosing to align against the field; that orientation would increase their <energy>.

The <Poynting vector> is the <electromagnetic energy> flux density,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H,\qquad [\mathbf S]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.}
$$
In <vacuum> it equals $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$. For a linear nondispersive medium with time-independent constitutive coefficients, <Maxwell's equations> and the identity $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)=\mathbf H\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf E-\mathbf E\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf H$ give <Poynting's theorem>,
$$
\frac{\partial u_{\rm EM}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E,
\qquad
u_{\rm EM}=\frac12\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\frac12\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H.
$$
The source term is <work> done by the fields on free charges; for an Ohmic conductor it is the positive <local Joule heating rate> $\sigma E^2$. Integrating over a <volume (thermodynamics)> states that loss of stored field <energy> plus outward <energy> flow equals <energy> transferred to matter. In dispersive media, such as a <plasma>, material motion contributes to stored <energy> and the simple nondispersive expression cannot be used indiscriminately.

For a <vacuum> <plane wave>, $B=E/c$ and $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf H$ and the propagation direction form a right-handed triad. With real peak field $E_0$, the time-averaged <wave intensity> is
$$
\langle S\rangle=\frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0c}=\frac12\epsilon_0cE_0^2.
$$
More generally, complex peak amplitudes give $\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf E_0\times\mathbf H_0^*)$. Along a <transmission line>, the cross-section <integral> of the longitudinal <Poynting vector> is the transmitted electrical <power> $VI$. <Energy> flows through the electromagnetic fields in the surrounding <dielectric>, even though the conductors guide the currents.

**Distributed circuit model.** A <transmission line> must be treated as a distributed system when its propagation <time> is appreciable compared with the <signal> timescale. Divide a uniform two-conductor line into elements of <length> $dx$. Each has series resistance $R'dx$ and <inductance> $L'dx$, and shunt <electrical conductance> $G'dx$ and <capacitance> $C'dx$. The primes denote quantities per unit <length>, with units $\mathrm{\Omega\,m^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, respectively.

Kirchhoff's <voltage> and current laws give the <Telegrapher's equations>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=-R'I-L'\frac{\partial I}{\partial t},
\qquad \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}=-G'V-C'\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}.}
$$
For a lossless line, $R'=G'=0$, differentiation gives $V_{xx}=L'C'V_{tt}$ and the same <wave equation> for $I$. The propagation <speed> and <characteristic impedance> are
$$
\boxed{u=\frac1{\sqrt{L'C'}},\qquad Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}.}
$$
A general lossless <signal> has $V=V^+(t-x/u)+V^-(t+x/u)$ and $I=[V^+(t-x/u)-V^-(t+x/u)]/Z_0$. Thus <characteristic impedance> is the <voltage>/current ratio of one travelling wave, not simply the resistance of the wires. It can be real even when the line dissipates no <energy>, because <energy> is carried away by propagation.

For sinusoidal quantities with convention $e^{i\omega t}$, the general lossy-line formulas are
$$
\gamma_{\rm prop}=\sqrt{(R'+i\omega L')(G'+i\omega C')},
\qquad Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R'+i\omega L'}{G'+i\omega C'}}.
$$
Choose the square-root branches for forward attenuation and positive forward <power>. The <real part> of $\gamma_{\rm prop}$ is attenuation per unit <length>; its <imaginary part> is the phase <wavenumber>. For small losses, attenuation is approximately $R'/(2Z_0)+G'Z_0/2$, using the lossless real $Z_0$ in this estimate.

**Fields and geometry.** Two separated conductors support a <transverse electromagnetic mode>, with electric and <magnetic fields> transverse to the propagation axis. For a homogeneous <dielectric> and negligible conductor penetration, both examples below have $L'C'=\mu\epsilon$ and hence $u=1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$. The distributed circuit model follows from the field <energy> and charges in each slice.

For a <coaxial cable> with inner radius $a$ and inner surface of the outer conductor at $b$, <Gauss's law> and <Ampère's law> give
$$
E_\rho=\frac{V}{\rho\ln(b/a)},\qquad H_\phi=\frac{I}{2\pi\rho},\quad a<\rho<b.
$$
The <electric field> is radial and the <magnetic field> circles the axis. Integration of <voltage> and field <energy> gives
$$
C'=\frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad
L'=\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln(b/a),\qquad
\boxed{Z_0=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\ln\frac ba.}
$$
The equal and opposite return current on the outer conductor confines the ideal external field to the annulus.

For wide parallel plates of width $w$ separated by $d\ll w$, neglect fringing. The <electric field> crosses the gap, $E\simeq V/d$, while the <magnetic field> lies in the gap parallel to the plates and perpendicular to propagation, $H\simeq I/w$. Therefore
$$
C'=\frac{\epsilon w}{d},\qquad L'=\frac{\mu d}{w},\qquad
\boxed{Z_0=\frac d w\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}.}
$$
For a forward wave, $\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$ points along the line. Integrating this <Poynting vector> over either cross-section gives instantaneous <power> $VI$. For complex peak amplitudes, average <power> is $\operatorname{Re}(VI^*)/2$, or $|V^+|^2/(2Z_0)$ for a single forward wave on a lossless line. The conductors guide an <energy flux> predominantly in the <dielectric> between them.

**Terminations and reflection.** At a load $Z_L$, require $V_L=Z_LI_L$. If $V^+$ and $V^-$ are the incident and reflected <voltage> amplitudes there, the total current is $(V^+-V^-)/Z_0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_V=\frac{V^-}{V^+}=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0},\qquad \Gamma_I=-\Gamma_V.}
$$
A matched load, $Z_L=Z_0$, absorbs the incident <power> with no reflection. An <open circuit> has $\Gamma_V=+1$, a current node and a doubled <voltage> at the end. A <short circuit> has $\Gamma_V=-1$, a <voltage> node and a doubled current there. A purely reactive load on a lossless line has $|\Gamma_V|=1$ and accepts no average <power>. For real $Z_0$, the reflected <power> fraction is $|\Gamma_V|^2$ and the accepted fraction is $1-|\Gamma_V|^2$.

Incident and reflected waves produce a standing-wave pattern with <voltage> <standing wave ratio> $(1+|\Gamma_V|)/(1-|\Gamma_V|)$. The <input impedance> of a lossless <length> $\ell$ is
$$
Z_{\rm in}=Z_0\frac{Z_L+iZ_0\tan(k\ell)}{Z_0+iZ_L\tan(k\ell)}.
$$
This gives $iZ_0\tan(k\ell)$ for a short and $-iZ_0\cot(k\ell)$ for an open. A quarter-wave section gives $Z_{\rm in}=Z_0^2/Z_L$, enabling impedance transformation. After a source launches a pulse, a distant termination affects it only after the reflected pulse returns; the return <time> is $2\ell/u$. Real conductor resistance, <dielectric loss> and departures from a TEM field profile limit the ideal model at high <frequency>.

An Euler strut is a slender elastic column under axial compression. Let its <bending stiffness> about the weaker section axis be $EI_A$ and its <length> be $L$. With small lateral deflection $y(x)$ and a dead compressive load $P$, the quadratic total <potential energy> is
$$
\Pi[y]=\frac12\int_0^L EI_A(y'')^2\,dx
-\frac P2\int_0^L(y')^2\,dx.
$$
The first term is bending <energy>; the negative term is <work> released by the load as bending shortens the end-to-end distance. Stationarity gives $EI_Ay''''+Py''=0$. For pinned ends, $y=y''=0$ at $0,L$, and $y\propto\sin(n\pi x/L)$ requires $P_n=n^2\pi^2EI_A/L^2$. The first instability is <Euler's critical load>,
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm cr}=\frac{\pi^2EI_A}{L^2}.}
$$
Below this load, the straight state has positive quadratic <energy> against these perturbations. At the threshold, the lowest bending mode has zero quadratic restoring <energy>; above it the straight state is unstable. For a rectangular section, $I_A=bh^3/12$ about the appropriate bending axis, so the smaller <second moment of area> controls the first buckling direction.

Endpoint <constraints> matter. Write $P_{\rm cr}=\pi^2EI_A/(KL)^2$: $K=1$ for pinned-pinned, $2$ for fixed-free and $1/2$ for fixed-fixed conditions. This elastic instability differs from compressive material failure. Imperfections cause bending before the ideal threshold, and a short column may yield before <Euler buckling> occurs.

A <gravitational assist> changes a spacecraft's <velocity> relative to the Sun by scattering it in the <gravitational field> of a moving planet. In the planet's rest frame, ignore other <forces> during the encounter and take the spacecraft <mass> negligible. The incoming and outgoing asymptotic <velocities> have equal magnitude,
$$
|\mathbf u_{\rm in}|=|\mathbf u_{\rm out}|=u_\infty,
$$
because the encounter is conservative, but their directions differ along a hyperbolic <orbit>. If the planet has approximately constant heliocentric <velocity> $\mathbf V_p$, the heliocentric spacecraft <velocities> are $\mathbf v_{\rm in,out}=\mathbf V_p+\mathbf u_{\rm in,out}$. Its specific <energy> change is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\mathcal E=\frac12(v_{\rm out}^2-v_{\rm in}^2)
=\mathbf V_p\cdot(\mathbf u_{\rm out}-\mathbf u_{\rm in}).}
$$
Thus a flyby can increase or decrease heliocentric <energy> depending on its geometry. The planet exchanges <momentum> and orbital <energy> with the spacecraft; its <speed> change is extremely small because its <mass> is much greater. There is no <energy> creation and no increase of asymptotic <speed> in the planet's own frame.

For a point-mass planet, <conservation of energy> and <angular momentum> gives the hyperbolic eccentricity and deflection angle
$$
e=1+\frac{r_pu_\infty^2}{GM_p},\qquad
\delta=2\sin^{-1}(1/e),
\qquad \tan\frac\delta2=\frac{GM_p}{b u_\infty^2},
$$
where $r_p$ is <periapsis> distance and $b$ the <impact parameter>. The velocity-vector change has magnitude $2u_\infty\sin(\delta/2)$, so the maximum energy-change magnitude for this fixed deflection is $2V_pu_\infty\sin(\delta/2)$. A small <periapsis> increases the turning angle, but <collision>, atmosphere and <tidal forces> limit how close a real spacecraft may pass. Multiple encounters allow large changes without expending the propellant that equivalent direct <velocity> changes would require.

= Solution

Take axes fixed in a <rigid body>, chosen along its <principal axes>, with moments of inertia $I_1,I_2,I_3$. The <angular momentum> components are $H_i=I_i\omega_i$. Newton's rotational equation is $\boldsymbol\tau=(d\mathbf H/dt)_{\rm inertial}$, while the rotating-frame transport rule gives
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf H}{dt}\right)_{\rm inertial}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf H}{dt}\right)_{\rm body}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf H.
$$
Thus <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3&=\tau_1,\\
I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1&=\tau_2,\\
I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2&=\tau_3.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The nonlinear cross terms arise because the <principal axes> rotate. Even with no external <torque>, the body-frame components of <angular momentum> can vary while the <angular momentum> <vector> stays fixed in inertial space.

With zero <torque>, the equations conserve
$$
E=\tfrac12\sum_i I_i\omega_i^2,\qquad
H^2=\sum_iI_i^2\omega_i^2.
$$
Uniform <rotation> about any <principal axis> is a torque-free solution. If $I_1<I_2<I_3$, <linearization> about the intermediate-axis solution $\omega_2=\Omega$ gives
$$
\delta\ddot\omega_1=\Omega^2\frac{(I_2-I_1)(I_3-I_2)}{I_1I_3}\delta\omega_1.
$$
The positive coefficient gives exponential growth: intermediate-axis <rotation> is unstable, whereas <rotation> about the largest or smallest moment is linearly stable. For a symmetric body, <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> also describe the <precession> of the transverse <angular velocity> around its <symmetry> axis. <Torques> from gravity or an applied couple enter through the right-hand sides rather than being absorbed into the inertial cross terms.

{parent=B8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

<Archimedes' principle> states that the resultant <hydrostatic pressure> <force> on a body equals the weight of the <fluid> displaced, directed opposite to gravity. Let $V_d$ be the displaced <fluid> region, with outward normal $\widehat{\mathbf n}$ from the body into the <fluid>. Since the <fluid> <pressure> <force> is inward,
$$
\mathbf F_b=-\oint_{\partial V_d}p\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dA
=-\int_{V_d}\nabla p\,dV.
$$
Hydrostatic balance is $\nabla p=\rho_f\mathbf g$, with downward <gravitational acceleration> $\mathbf g$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_b=-\int_{V_d}\rho_f\mathbf g\,dV
=-\rho_fV_d\mathbf g}
$$
for uniform <fluid> density and gravity. Uniform atmospheric <pressure> gives no net <force> on a closed surface. For a floating body, the same displaced-volume construction includes the free surface; any significant air <buoyancy> can be included as a second displaced <fluid>.

A freely floating body of <mass> $m$ satisfies $mg=\rho_f gV_d$, so its displaced <volume (thermodynamics)> is $m/\rho_f$. A homogeneous body of density $\rho_b<\rho_f$ floats with submerged fraction $\rho_b/\rho_f$; if $\rho_b>\rho_f$, hydrostatic <buoyancy> alone cannot support it. The <buoyant force> acts through the <centre of buoyancy>, the <centroid> of the displaced <volume (thermodynamics)> for constant density, while weight acts through the <centre of mass>. Their relative positions determine rotational equilibrium and stability. For a fully submerged body, a <centre of mass> below the <centre of buoyancy> gives a restoring <torque>; a floating body's changing displaced shape additionally requires the <metacentric height> criterion. <Surface tension>, externally driven flow and contact <forces> can alter the simple <force> balance.

= B9
{c}
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B9}

**Following the moving <fluid>.** The Eulerian <velocity field> $\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)$ assigns a <velocity> to each position. A <fluid> <particle> satisfies $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf u$, so the <chain rule> gives the <material derivative>, also called the convective <derivative>,
$$
\boxed{\frac D{Dt}=\frac\partial{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla,
\qquad \mathbf a=\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}.}
$$
Even in steady flow a <particle> can accelerate because it moves between regions with different <velocity>. For example, flow around a curved obstacle changes direction despite $\partial_t\mathbf u=0$. <Mass> conservation is
$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0,
\qquad \frac{D\rho}{Dt}+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
For a constant-density incompressible <fluid>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$.

**<Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> and <Bernoulli's equation>.** Neglect <viscosity> and take the body <force> per unit <mass> to be $-\nabla\Phi$. <Newton's second law> for a <fluid> element gives
$$
\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla p-\rho\nabla\Phi.
$$
For steady flow, dot with <displacement> along a <streamline>. The tangential component of $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u$ is the <derivative> of $u^2/2$ along that line. For constant density, integration gives
$$
\boxed{\frac p\rho+\frac{u^2}{2}+\Phi=B,\qquad B\text{ constant along a streamline}.}
$$
In uniform gravity, $\Phi=gz$. Thus <pressure>, <kinetic energy> per unit <mass> and <gravitational potential energy> per unit <mass> exchange along a <streamline>. For a barotropic compressible <fluid>, replace $p/\rho$ by $\int dp/\rho(p)$. Different <streamlines> need not have the same Bernoulli constant.

**<Vorticity> and <potential flow>.** <Vorticity> is $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$. A small <fluid> element rotates locally with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\omega/2$ after its deformational part is separated. The <vector> identity
$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(u^2/2)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega
$$
gives $\nabla B=\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$ for steady constant-density <inviscid flow>. Thus an irrotational region has one common Bernoulli constant on each connected region. In a simply connected irrotational domain, introduce a <velocity potential> $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. <Incompressibility> then gives <Laplace's equation>, $\nabla^2\phi=0$. For unsteady <irrotational flow>,
$$
\partial_t\phi+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho+\Phi=C(t).
$$
An impermeable stationary wall fixes $\partial_n\phi=0$, but ideal <potential flow> does not enforce no slip. In a multiply connected domain, <irrotational flow> may still have nonzero <circulation> around an excluded vortex core, so a globally single-valued potential requires care.

For example, uniform two-dimensional flow past a circular cylinder has $\phi=U(r+a^2/r)\cos\theta$ outside $r=a$. Its normal <velocity> vanishes at the cylinder, and its surface tangential <velocity> is $-2U\sin\theta$. <Bernoulli's equation> predicts a symmetric <pressure> distribution and zero <drag>. This is <D'Alembert's paradox>: the assumptions omit the viscous <boundary layers>, separation and wake responsible for real <drag>.

For an incompressible <fluid> of constant <kinematic viscosity> $\nu$, the <curl> of the motion equation gives
$$
\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
The first term stretches and tilts <vortex lines>; the second diffuses <vorticity>. In two-dimensional incompressible <inviscid flow> the stretching term is absent and <vorticity> is carried with the <fluid>. In a three-dimensional <inviscid flow> it can be amplified by stretching. Density gradients not parallel to <pressure> gradients provide an additional source in a general compressible <fluid>.

**Viscous flow.** A <Newtonian fluid>'s shear <stress> is proportional to rate of <strain>. For constant <viscosity> and <incompressibility> the <Navier-Stokes equation> is
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla p-\rho\nabla\Phi+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u,}
$$
with <dynamic viscosity> $\eta$ and <kinematic viscosity> $\nu=\eta/\rho$. At an ordinary <solid> wall, the <no-slip boundary condition> sets <fluid> <velocity> equal to the wall <velocity>. <Viscosity> transfers <momentum> between neighbouring layers and converts mechanical <energy> into <heat>; unlike the <pressure> <force>, its shear contribution depends on <velocity> gradients.

For plane <Couette flow> between plates $y=0,h$, with the upper plate moving at $U$, the steady solution is $u_x=Uy/h$, with shear <stress> $\eta U/h$. For pressure-driven flow through a pipe of radius $a$, a constant <pressure> gradient gives the parabolic Poiseuille profile
$$
u_z(r)=\frac{-dp/dz}{4\eta}(a^2-r^2),\qquad
Q=\frac{\pi a^4}{8\eta}\left(-\frac{dp}{dz}\right).
$$
The <Reynolds number> $\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\eta$ compares convective inertia with viscous <force>. Small <Reynolds number> often permits the Stokes-flow approximation; large <Reynolds number> can produce thin <boundary layers> and, under suitable conditions, <turbulence>. It does not justify neglecting the wall conditions. Bernoulli's inviscid constant is generally not conserved along a viscous <streamline>, and pipe flow instead shows a <pressure> loss corresponding to dissipation.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

A <Carnot cycle> consists of four reversible stages: isothermal expansion at the hot-reservoir <temperature> $T_h$, adiabatic expansion to $T_c$, isothermal compression at $T_c$, and adiabatic compression back to the starting state. The adiabatic stages exchange no <heat>. For one mole of an <ideal gas>, the two isothermal stages have heats $Q_h=RT_h\ln(V_B/V_A)$ and $Q_c=RT_c\ln(V_C/V_D)$ in magnitude. The reversible adiabatic relations ensure $V_B/V_A=V_C/V_D$.

More generally, the total <entropy> change over any reversible cycle is zero. The two <heat> exchanges therefore satisfy
$$
\frac{Q_h}{T_h}-\frac{Q_c}{T_c}=0.
$$
With <work> output $W=Q_h-Q_c$, its <thermal efficiency> is
$$
\boxed{\eta_C=\frac W{Q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.}
$$
The <temperatures> must be absolute. The second law makes this the maximum efficiency for an engine exchanging <heat> only with those two reservoirs; irreversible <entropy> production lowers the efficiency.

The <Diesel cycle> has a compression <adiabat> $1\to2$, a horizontal constant-pressure heat-input segment $2\to3$, an expansion <adiabat> $3\to4$, and a vertical constant-volume cooling segment $4\to1$.

= State temperatures
{parent=C10}

= Solution
{parent=State temperatures}

For one mole, $pV=RT$, and constant <heat capacities> satisfy $C_p-C_V=R$. The specified diatomic-gas model has
$$
\gamma=1.4=\frac75,\qquad C_V=\frac{R}{\gamma-1}=\frac52R,
\qquad C_p=\frac72R.
$$
A reversible <adiabatic process> satisfies $TV^{\gamma-1}=\mathrm{constant}$. Constant-pressure expansion satisfies $T\propto V$. Applying these in order gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
T_2&=T_1\left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1},\\

\end{aligned}}

Every <volume (thermodynamics)> ratio is dimensionless, so the expressions have units of <temperature>. For the intended <volume (thermodynamics)> ordering, the minimum <temperature> is $T_1$ and the maximum is $T_3$.

= Heat exchanges and efficiency
{parent=C10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=Heat exchanges and efficiency}

Use <heat> into the <gas> as positive. The two adiabatic stages have $Q_{12}=Q_{34}=0$. During constant-pressure heating and constant-volume cooling,
$$
\boxed{Q_{23}=C_p(T_3-T_2)>0,\qquad Q_{41}=C_V(T_1-T_4)<0.}
$$
The first law over a complete cycle gives $W_{\rm out}=Q_{23}+Q_{41}$, since the <internal energy> returns to its initial value. Therefore
$$
\eta=1-\frac{C_V(T_4-T_1)}{C_p(T_3-T_2)}.
$$
Substitute the <temperatures>:
$$
\frac{T_4-T_1}{T_3-T_2}
=\frac{(V_3/V_2)^\gamma-1}
{(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1}(V_3/V_2-1)}
=\frac{V_3^\gamma-V_2^\gamma}{V_1^{\gamma-1}(V_3-V_2)}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\eta=1-\frac1\gamma
\frac{(V_3/V_1)^\gamma-(V_2/V_1)^\gamma}
{V_3/V_1-V_2/V_1}.}
$$
Equivalently, define compression ratio $r=V_1/V_2$ and cutoff ratio $\rho=V_3/V_2$:
$$
\boxed{\eta=1-\frac{\rho^\gamma-1}{\gamma r^{\gamma-1}(\rho-1)}.}
$$
These formulas use the ideal constant-heat-capacity model and reversible adiabatic strokes. The heat-addition and heat-rejection strokes occur over changing <gas> <temperatures>, unlike the isothermal <heat> exchanges of a Carnot engine.

= Limit of vanishing heat addition
{parent=C10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=Limit of vanishing heat addition}

For $\rho\to1^+$, <Taylor expansion> gives $\rho^\gamma-1=\gamma(\rho-1)+O((\rho-1)^2)$. The <vanishing-cutoff limit of the Diesel cycle> is
$$
\boxed{\eta\longrightarrow1-\frac1{r^{\gamma-1}}
=1-\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^{0.4}.}
$$
The maximum <temperature> tends to $T_2=T_1r^{\gamma-1}$, while the minimum stays at $T_1$. A Carnot engine between those limiting extrema has
$$
\eta_C=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}=1-r^{1-\gamma},
$$
so **the limiting efficiencies coincide**. At exactly $V_3=V_2$, however, both <heat> input and net <work> vanish, and the cycle retraces its compression <adiabat>. The efficiency there is an undefined $0/0$; the boxed value is the continuous limit for neighbouring nonzero cycles, not finite <work> at zero <heat> input.

For a finite cutoff ratio $\rho>1$, the Carnot comparison uses $T_{\max}=T_3=T_1r^{\gamma-1}\rho$, giving $\eta_C=1-1/(r^{\gamma-1}\rho)$. The <Diesel cycle> has smaller efficiency. For example,

\frac{\rho^\gamma-1}{\gamma(\rho-1)}
=\frac1{\rho-1}\int_1^\rho x^{\gamma-1}\,dx>1>\frac1\rho,

which makes its rejected/input <heat> ratio larger than the Carnot ratio.

= Solution
{parent=Equations of state and transitions}

An <equation of state> relates equilibrium state variables such as <pressure>, <volume (thermodynamics)>, <temperature> and material amount. At fixed composition, it reduces the number of independent thermodynamic variables. It does not by itself specify all caloric properties: information such as <heat capacity> or <internal energy> is also needed.

At a liquid-gas first-order transition, an equilibrium <isotherm> can contain a constant-pressure interval in which changing total <volume (thermodynamics)> changes the phase fractions. A <metastable> single-phase extrapolation can instead show a region with $(\partial p/\partial V)_T>0$; that region has negative <isothermal compressibility> and is mechanically unstable. <Thermodynamic phase> coexistence requires equal <temperature>, <pressure> and <chemical potential>, and a consistent free-energy description replaces the unstable region by coexistence. For an appropriate analytic <fluid> <equation of state>, a <liquid-gas critical point> occurs where $(\partial p/\partial V)_T=(\partial^2p/\partial V^2)_T=0$.

These tests diagnose the phase behaviour represented by that <equation of state>. They do not guarantee detection of every possible transition involving an additional <order parameter> which the mechanical equation omits.

Start with the fundamental relation at fixed material amount, $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$, and expand <entropy> in independent variables $T,V$:
$$
dS=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_VdT
+\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_TdV
=\frac{C_V}{T}dT+\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_VdV.

$$
dU=C_V\,dT+\left[T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V-p\right]dV.

For the <excluded-volume ideal gas>, $(\partial p/\partial T)_V=R/(V-b)=p/T$, so the bracket vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T=0,
\qquad U=U(T),\qquad dU=C_V\,dT.}
$$
Once $U$ depends only on <temperature>, $C_V=dU/dT$ cannot depend on <volume (thermodynamics)> either. Its stipulated <temperature> independence therefore makes it a constant, giving $U=C_VT+U_*$ with arbitrary <energy> reference $U_*$. <Excluded volume> changes the <pressure> without adding the volume-dependent attractive <internal energy> that a more general interacting-gas model might have.

= Solution

For a classical dilute <ideal gas> in <thermal equilibrium>, the <probability> of a single-particle <velocity> is proportional to the <Boltzmann factor> for <kinetic energy>, $e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}$. <Velocities> with <speed> between $v$ and $v+dv$ fill a spherical shell of velocity-space <volume (thermodynamics)> $4\pi v^2dv$. f(v)=4\pi\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_BT}\right)^{3/2}v^2e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)},\qquad v\geq0.
$$
The factor $v^2$ counts states, rather than representing an extra Boltzmann <energy> factor.

Set $\alpha=m/(2k_BT)$ and use $I_j=\int_0^\infty v^j e^{-\alpha v^2}dv$. Normalization cancels in ratios, so the given recurrence gives
$$
\overline v=\frac{I_3}{I_2}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}
=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}},\qquad
\overline{v^2}=\frac{I_4}{I_2}=\frac{3}{2\alpha}=\frac{3k_BT}{m}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\overline{v^2}=\frac{3\pi}{8}\,\overline v^{\,2}.}
$$
The <root mean square> <speed> is larger than the <mean> <speed>, consistent with the positive <variance> of the <speed distribution>.

= Wall arrival rate

= Solution

Choose a flat wall and let $\theta$ measure the angle from the direction towards it. <Particles> of <speed> $v$ in <solid angle> $d\Omega$ occupy fraction $f(v)dv\,d\Omega/(4\pi)$ of an isotropic <gas>. = Energy of incident particles
{parent=C12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=Energy of incident particles}

Wall arrivals are not an unbiased sample of the <gas>: faster atoms arrive more frequently. The normalized <speed distribution> among incident atoms is
$$
f_{\rm hit}(v)=\frac{vf(v)}{\overline v}.
$$
The angular factor in the arrival rate cancels when calculating the <mean> <kinetic energy>. Thus
$$
\langle K\rangle_{\rm hit}
=\frac m2\frac{\int_0^\infty v^3f(v)\,dv}{\int_0^\infty vf(v)\,dv}

\boxed{\langle K\rangle_{\rm hit}=\frac m\alpha=2k_BT.}

The arrival weighting raises the normal-direction contribution to $k_BT$ while each tangential direction still contributes $k_BT/2$. This is also the <mean> <energy> carried by atoms effusing through a thin aperture from an equilibrated reservoir.

= Pressures in heated connected vessels
{parent=C12}

= Solution
{parent=Pressures in heated connected vessels}

Denote the heated and cold vessels by subscripts $h,c$. For <argon>, the opposing effusive <particle> rates are equal in the steady state:
$$
n_h\overline v(T_1)=n_c\overline v(T_0).
$$
Because $\overline v\propto\sqrt T$ and $p=nk_BT$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{p_h}{\sqrt{T_1}}=\frac{p_c}{\sqrt{T_0}},
\qquad \frac{p_h}{p_c}=\sqrt{\frac{T_1}{T_0}}.}
$$
This is <thermal transpiration> in the free-molecular limit. It fixes a ratio; the initial conditions determine the absolute pressures.

For the stated two identical vessels, take their fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> to be $V$ and the combined system sealed. Initially it contains $2p_0V/(k_BT_0)$ atoms. Hence
$$
\frac{p_hV}{k_BT_1}+\frac{p_cV}{k_BT_0}=\frac{2p_0V}{k_BT_0}.
$$
Let $q=\sqrt{T_1/T_0}$. Combining this inventory with $p_h=qp_c$ gives the <closed-vessel thermal transpiration> result
$$
\boxed{p_c=\frac{2p_0q}{q+1},\qquad
p_h=\frac{2p_0q^2}{q+1},\qquad
\Delta p=p_h-p_c=\frac{2p_0q(q-1)}{q+1}.}
$$
Heating makes the hot side less dense but at higher <pressure>. Both expressions reduce to $p_0$ when $T_1=T_0$. For a small imposed <temperature> rise $\Delta T$, the <pressure> difference starts as $p_0\Delta T/(2T_0)$.

If instead the cold vessel is interpreted as an externally maintained <pressure> reservoir with $p_c=p_0$, the answer becomes $\Delta p=p_0(q-1)$. That alternative requires the fixed-pressure reservoir assumption; it does not conserve the initial <particle> inventory of two sealed equal-volume vessels. Merely describing the vessels as large does not keep their pressures fixed through the redistribution. The pressures in the boxed answer assume the natural closed-vessel interpretation.

\boxed{\begin{aligned}
n_{{\rm Ar},L}&=\tfrac12n_0(1+e^{-t/\tau_{\rm Ar}}),&
n_{{\rm Ar},R}&=\tfrac12n_0(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\rm Ar}}),\\
n_{{\rm He},L}&=\tfrac12n_0(1-e^{-t/\tau_{\rm He}}),&
n_{{\rm He},R}&=\tfrac12n_0(1+e^{-t/\tau_{\rm He}}).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Using atomic masses of approximately $40$ and $4$ atomic <mass> units gives $\tau_{\rm He}/\tau_{\rm Ar}\simeq1/\sqrt{10}\simeq0.32$. <Helium> therefore transfers faster. Each species eventually reaches $n_0/2$ in each vessel, but its transient is different. In particular, the total density temporarily rises in the initially <argon> vessel and falls in the initially <helium> vessel, before both return to $n_0$.

For unequal maintained <temperatures> $T_L,T_R$, replace the rate equation by
$$
\dot n_{s,L}=-\frac{A_h}{4V}\left[\overline v_s(T_L)n_{s,L}-\overline v_s(T_R)n_{s,R}\right].
$$
With each species' total density sum still $n_0$, its final densities and relaxation <time> are
$$
\boxed{n_{s,L}^{\infty}=\frac{n_0\sqrt{T_R}}{\sqrt{T_L}+\sqrt{T_R}},\qquad
n_{s,R}^{\infty}=\frac{n_0\sqrt{T_L}}{\sqrt{T_L}+\sqrt{T_R}},\qquad
\tau_s=\frac{4V}{A_h[\overline v_s(T_L)+\overline v_s(T_R)]}.}
$$
Every species has the same final density ratio $\sqrt{T_R/T_L}$ and partial-pressure ratio $\sqrt{T_L/T_R}$. Thus there is no permanent composition separation in this ideal steady state, although the rates remain <mass> dependent. A maintained hot side has lower density and higher <pressure>. Equal <particle> rates do not imply <thermal equilibrium>: atoms arriving from the hotter side carry more <energy>, so external heating and cooling are required to hold different <temperatures>. The model assumes thin-hole <effusion>, local equilibrium within each vessel and negligible aperture <volume (thermodynamics)>.

= Solution
{parent=C13}

**Molecular transport across gradients.** A <gas> can transport <momentum>, thermal <energy> and species abundance even when its average bulk <velocity> is zero. <Molecules> arriving at a point carry properties acquired near their preceding <collisions>. A small gradient makes the populations arriving from opposite sides slightly different, producing transport from larger to smaller values of the carried property. A continuum transport law requires a <mean free path> $\lambda$ small compared with the gradient <length>, and relaxation times short compared with the observation <time>.

For a dilute <hard-sphere gas>,
$$
\overline v=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}},\qquad
\lambda=\frac1{\sqrt2\,n\pi d^2},
$$
where $d$ is molecular <collision> diameter. Both <speed> and <collision> distance matter: high <speed> alone does not determine a transport coefficient.

A simple kinetic estimate makes the common structure explicit. For a quantity $a(y)$ carried per <particle>, a <molecule> moving at angle $\theta$ to $+y$ brings approximately $a(y-\lambda\cos\theta)$. Expanding to first order and averaging an isotropic <velocity> distribution gives
$$
J_a\sim n\overline v\left\langle\cos\theta\,[a(y)-\lambda\cos\theta\,a'(y)]\right\rangle
=-\frac13n\overline v\lambda\frac{da}{dy},
$$
since $\langle\cos\theta\rangle=0$ and $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3$. This mean-free-path argument estimates the coefficients; a detailed <collision> calculation changes their numerical prefactors and distinguishes different relaxation rates.

**<Momentum> transport and <viscosity>.** For a <shear flow> with <mean> <velocity> $U_x(y)$, the transported directed <momentum> per <molecule> is $mU_x$. The net flux of $x$ <momentum> in the $+y$ direction is
$$
\Pi_{yx}=-\eta\frac{dU_x}{dy},\qquad
\boxed{\eta\sim\frac13nm\overline v\lambda.}
$$
Here $\eta$ is <dynamic viscosity> in $\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. The associated shear <stress> on a material surface has the corresponding traction sign convention; the flux carries <momentum> from the faster-moving layer towards the slower-moving one. The <kinematic viscosity> is $\nu=\eta/(nm)$, with units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. <Viscosity> smooths <velocity> gradients, and the lost ordered mechanical <energy> becomes <heat>.

**<Heat> transport.** Let $c_v=d\varepsilon/dT$ be the thermal <heat capacity> *per <molecule>*, in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Substituting $a=\varepsilon(T)$ gives <Fourier's law>,
$$
\mathbf J_Q=-\kappa\nabla T,\qquad
\boxed{\kappa\sim\frac13n c_v\overline v\lambda.}
$$
<Thermal conductivity> has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}$. For a <monatomic gas>, translational equipartition gives $c_v=3k_B/2$. Rotational or vibrational excitations in other <gases> can increase the <energy> carried per <molecule>, while <collisions> govern how those modes exchange <energy>. A <temperature gradient> can therefore be transported differently from directed <momentum> even when the dimensional estimates look similar.

**Species transport.** For a dilute tracer or a mixture with prescribed total density and negligible bulk flow, <molecules> diffuse down concentration gradients. The first-gradient number flux is <Fick's first law>,
$$
\mathbf J_s=-D\nabla n_s,\qquad
\boxed{D\sim\frac13\overline v\lambda.}
$$
The <diffusivity> $D$ has units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$; multiplying number flux by the species <mass> gives <mass> flux. More generally one uses <diffusion> flux relative to the mixture's bulk <velocity> and specifies <mass> or mole fractions. The approximation is not a claim that an arbitrary pure-gas <pressure> gradient can remain at rest while only a Fick flux acts. In a mixture, <collision> cross-sections and relative molecular <speeds>, including the <reduced mass>, determine the species diffusivities.

**Dependence on density and <temperature>.** The <kinetic estimates of gas transport coefficients> predict, for fixed <collision> diameter and molecular <heat capacity>,
$$
\eta\propto\frac{\sqrt{m k_BT}}{d^2},\qquad
\kappa\propto\frac{c_v}{d^2}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}},\qquad
D\propto\frac1{nd^2}\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{m}}.
$$
Thus <dynamic viscosity> and <thermal conductivity> are approximately density independent in the dilute continuum regime. Reducing density reduces the number of carriers but increases their <mean free path> in the same proportion. At fixed density all three estimates grow as $T^{1/2}$; at fixed <pressure>, $n=p/(k_BT)$ and
$$
\boxed{D\propto\frac{T^{3/2}}p,\qquad
\eta\propto T^{1/2},\qquad \kappa\propto T^{1/2}.}
$$
<Kinematic viscosity> and <thermal diffusivity> also grow roughly as $T^{3/2}/p$ at fixed <pressure> in this model. Real interaction potentials make effective <collision> cross-sections <temperature> dependent, and internal excitations change $c_v$. Dense-gas correlations and boundaries invalidate the simple cancellation. In particular, when the <mean free path> approaches the apparatus scale, transport becomes free molecular; it cannot be described by extending the same local, pressure-independent Fourier and <viscosity> coefficients indefinitely.

**<Heat> <diffusion equation>.** For a stationary <gas> with constant <volumetric heat capacity> $c=n c_v$, constant <thermal conductivity> and negligible compression <work>, the <energy> balance in any fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V cT\,dV=-\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf J_Q\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\,dA+\int_VH\,dV.
$$
Here $H$ is a possible <heat> generation rate per <volume (thermodynamics)>. The <divergence theorem> and <Fourier's law> yield
$$
\boxed{c\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\kappa\nabla^2T+H,
\qquad \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\alpha\nabla^2T+\frac Hc,
\qquad \alpha=\frac\kappa c.}
$$
<Thermal diffusivity> $\alpha$ has units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and is of order $\overline v\lambda/3$. In a moving or compressing <gas>, the <material derivative>, pressure-volume <work> and viscous heating must also enter the internal-energy equation. The displayed pure <diffusion equation> describes the approximation in which those effects are absent or negligible.

**Representative solutions.** With no source, a steady one-dimensional slab between <temperatures> $T_a,T_b$ at $x=0,L$ has $T=T_a+(T_b-T_a)x/L$ and uniform <heat flux> $-\kappa(T_b-T_a)/L$. Thus a constant spatial gradient transports <heat> without changing local <temperature> in <time> when the boundaries continually supply and remove it.

For an infinite medium and an initial sinusoidal <temperature> perturbation,
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=T_b+A\cos(kx)e^{-\alpha k^2t}.}
$$
Short spatial wavelengths decay faster, and the characteristic smoothing <length> after <time> $t$ is $\sqrt{\alpha t}$. A finite slab with both ends held at $T_b$ has a <sine> expansion with factors $e^{-\alpha(n\pi/L)^2t}$; its slowest relaxation <time> is $L^2/(\pi^2\alpha)$.

An instantaneous <heat> input $Q_A$ per unit transverse <area> at $x=0$ in an otherwise uniform infinite medium gives the <Gaussian> solution
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)-T_b=\frac{Q_A}{c\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\alpha t}\right),\quad t>0.}
$$
Its <integral> $\int c(T-T_b)dx=Q_A$ conserves the deposited <heat>. The peak falls as $t^{-1/2}$ while its width grows as $\sqrt t$; <diffusion> redistributes <energy> instead of transporting a sharp front at a single <velocity>.

Finally, a semi-infinite body initially at $T_i$ and held at surface <temperature> $T_s$ from $t=0$ has
$$
\boxed{T(x,t)=T_i+(T_s-T_i)\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right),\quad x\geq0.}
$$
It has $T(0,t)=T_s$ and approaches $T_i$ far from the boundary at finite <time>. These solutions demonstrate steady conduction, mode decay, spreading of localized <heat> and boundary-driven <diffusion>. Their idealized behaviour at extremely short times or arbitrarily long distances must be interpreted within the continuum regime rather than as an assertion of instantaneous microscopic communication.
