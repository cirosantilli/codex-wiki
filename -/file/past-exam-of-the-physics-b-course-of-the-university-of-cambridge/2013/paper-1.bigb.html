= Solution
{parent=A1}

For <hydrostatic volume strain from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio> in isotropic <linear elasticity>, the <bulk modulus> is $K=E/[3(1-2\nu)]=5.56\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Hydrostatic compression therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta V=-\frac{pV}{K}=-1.82\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3}\simeq-18.2\,\mathrm{cm^3}.}
$$
Here $p=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is the <pressure> increase, and the negative sign denotes contraction. Equivalently each normal <strain> is $-(1-2\nu)p/E$ and the three <strains> add. This is the small-strain isotropic model; concrete's microstructure is represented only through the supplied elastic constants.

= Solution
{parent=A2}

Put <line charges> $+\lambda$ and $-\lambda$ on the two axes. For $d\gg a_1,a_2$, the surface potentials differ by
$$
V_1-V_2\simeq\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(\ln\frac d{a_1}+\ln\frac d{a_2}\right).
$$
Thus the <capacitance per unit length> in vacuum is
$$
\boxed{C'=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln[d^2/(a_1a_2)]}
=\frac{\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln[d/\sqrt{a_1a_2}]}.}
$$
The two logarithms belong to one potential difference, not two independent <capacitances> in parallel. A homogeneous <dielectric> replaces $\epsilon_0$ by its absolute <permittivity>; finite radii and proximity corrections are neglected.

= Solution

With the standard liquid-water approximation $\rho\simeq10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, each portion has <mass> $100\,\mathrm{kg}$. The equal <heat capacities> give final <temperature> $T_f=310\,\mathrm K$ by <energy> conservation. For an insulated mixing process with no other changes,
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm universe}=mc\ln\frac{T_f}{280}
+mc\ln\frac{T_f}{340}
=4.20\times10^5\ln\frac{310^2}{280\times340}
\simeq3.95\times10^3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
The reversible integrals $mc\int dT/T$ evaluate <entropy> differences between <equilibrium> states; the actual <heat> transfer is irreversible. These are two portions of the same substance, so there is no additional ideal-mixture compositional <entropy>.

= Solution

Let absorbed solar flux per unit surface area be $F_\odot=\sigma T_0^4$. The <single-layer greenhouse model> emits infrared upward and downward. Top-of-atmosphere balance gives $\sigma T_a^4=F_\odot$, hence $T_a=T_0$. Surface balance is $\sigma T_s^4=F_\odot+\sigma T_a^4$, so
$$
\boxed{T_s=2^{1/4}T_0\simeq333\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The layer simultaneously satisfies $\sigma T_s^4=2\sigma T_a^4$. This ideal radiative model excludes convection, spectral windows and atmospheric <temperature> structure; it is not a quantitative prediction for the real atmosphere.

For the <tangent-axis inertia of a spherical shell>, subtract the missing solid core from a solid sphere:
$$
M=\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}(R_2^3-R_1^3),\qquad
I_c=\frac{8\pi\rho}{15}(R_2^5-R_1^5).
$$
The <parallel axis theorem> shifts the axis a distance $R_2$:
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm tangent}=\frac{8\pi\rho}{15}(R_2^5-R_1^5)
+\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}(R_2^3-R_1^3)R_2^2.}
$$
It tends to $7MR_2^2/5$ for a solid sphere and $5MR_2^2/3$ for a thin <spherical shell>.

= Solution
{parent=B6}

The <image charge> construction replaces conductor-induced charge by fictitious sources outside the physical domain. Match the conductor potentials and the far-field condition; uniqueness then fixes the potential in the physical region. For a grounded plane, reflect a charge and reverse its sign, giving attraction $q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0h^2)$. For a grounded sphere of radius $a$, a charge at distance $R>a$ has image $-qa/R$ at radial distance $a^2/R$. These are examples with prescribed zero potential, not arbitrary isolated conductors.<Method of Images by Richard Fitzpatrick>[grounded plane construction and its uniqueness argument]{ref}

Use horizontal $x$ and upward $y$ in the upper right-angle wedge. The sheets meet electrically, so take their common potential as zero and assume no externally imposed field. For a real charge at $(0,y)$, the <image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge> are $-q$ at $(y,0)$ and $(-y,0)$, and $+q$ at $(0,-y)$. The first two contributions have cancelling horizontal components. With $K_e=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$,
$$
F_y=-\frac{K_eq^2}{\sqrt2y^2}+\frac{K_eq^2}{4y^2}
=-\frac\beta{y^2},\qquad
\boxed{\beta=\frac{q^2(2\sqrt2-1)}{16\pi\epsilon_0}.}
$$
Thus the <force> magnitude at the original height is $\beta/y_0^2$, directed down toward the intersection. The sphere is treated as a <point charge>, with size much smaller than its distances from the sheets.

Since gravity is already balanced in the uncharged <equilibrium>, the charged vertical <equilibrium> satisfies $k(y_e-y_0)+\beta/y_e^2=0$. For $\beta/(ky_0^3)\ll1$, $y_e\simeq y_0-\beta/(ky_0^2)$. Differentiating the <force> gives
$$
\omega_y^2=\frac1m\left(k-\frac{2\beta}{y_e^3}\right),\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\omega_y-\omega_0}{\omega_0}\simeq-\frac{\beta}{ky_0^3},\quad\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}.}
$$
The <electrostatic softening above a conducting wedge> reduces the restoring <force>; the small-charge vertical <equilibrium> remains stable.

For the <lateral stability of a spring-supported charge in a wedge>, the off-axis images of $(x,y)$ are $-q$ at $(y,x)$ and $(-y,-x)$ and $+q$ at $(-x,-y)$. Expansion near the bisector gives
$$
F_x=D_xx+O(x^3),\qquad
D_x=\frac{K_eq^2}{y_e^3}\left(\sqrt2+\frac14\right)>0.
$$
So **the electrostatic <force> is laterally destabilizing**: moving toward either sheet strengthens its attraction. In the simplified model where the <spring> supplies only a vertical <force>, the overall <equilibrium> is a saddle and is unstable.

A three-dimensional <spring> suspension also has geometrical transverse stiffness. If its charged-equilibrium tension is $T$ and <length> is $\ell$, a small sideways displacement gives <spring> <force> $-(T/\ell)x$, with $T=mg+\beta/y_e^2$. The full stability conditions in this suspension model are
$$
\boxed{k>\frac{2\beta}{y_e^3},\qquad\frac T\ell>D_x.}
$$
Motion parallel to the intersection line has no electrostatic stiffness in the ideal infinite-wedge geometry and is stabilized by the positive <spring> tension. Thus **lateral electrostatic destabilization alone does not prove instability of a real hanging spring**. At fixed finite support geometry, a sufficiently small charge preserves stability because $D_x=O(q^2)$ while $T/\ell\to mg/\ell>0$. The given vertical-displacement approximation does not specify the attachment <length> or by itself establish the transverse inequality for an arbitrary charge. The general criterion above states what can be concluded; the saddle verdict applies to the distinct idealization with zero lateral support stiffness.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

For distributed <inductance> $L$ and <capacitance> $C$, a small segment obeys $V_z=-L I_t$ and $I_z=-C V_t$. Differentiation gives $V_{zz}=LCV_{tt}$ and $I_{zz}=LCI_{tt}$. For a forward wave,
$$
\boxed{v=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad Z=\frac{V^+}{I^+}=\sqrt{L/C}.}
$$
A backward <voltage> wave has current $I^-=-V^-/Z$. This sign matters at an impedance discontinuity.

The source figure is the stacked-strip <parallel-plate transmission line> geometry, despite its use of the word coplanar. <Gauss's law> gives $C=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r a/d$. Opposite surface currents give <magnetic field> $B=\mu_0I/a$ between the strips by <Ampère's law>; the <magnetic flux> per unit <length> is $Bd$, hence $L=Bd/I=\mu_0d/a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{v=\frac{c}{\sqrt{2.3}}\simeq1.98\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\quad
Z=\frac da\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{2.3\epsilon_0}}
\simeq248.4\frac da\,\Omega.}
$$
No numerical $a/d$ is given; the impedance is appropriately left in terms of that ratio. The line is taken to be nonmagnetic and lossless.

Increasing the gap by $\alpha$ changes $L$ to $\alpha L$ and $C$ to $C/\alpha$, so $Z_2=\alpha Z_1$ while $v$ is unchanged. <voltage> and current continuity at the step give
$$
V_i+V_r=V_t,\qquad \frac{V_i-V_r}{Z_1}=\frac{V_t}{Z_2}.
$$
Consequently the <voltage> and energy-flux <transmission coefficients> are
$$
\boxed{t_V=\frac{2\alpha}{1+\alpha},\qquad
\mathcal T=\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}|t_V|^2=\frac{4\alpha}{(1+\alpha)^2},}
\qquad r_V=\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}.
$$
Here amplitudes are peak <voltage> amplitudes, with mean <power> $|V|^2/(2Z)$. The reciprocal gaps have $r_V(1/\alpha)=-r_V(\alpha)$: equal reflected <energy> but opposite <voltage> phase. A <voltage> <transmission coefficient> above one does not imply <energy> gain.

For a <finite impedance section of a transmission line>, put $\ell=l_2-l_1$ and $\delta=\omega\ell/v$. Multiple reflections inside the altered section interfere. Summing their <geometric series>, relative to the first boundary,
$$
t_{\rm whole}=\frac{(1-r_V^2)e^{i\delta}}{1-r_V^2e^{2i\delta}},\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal T_{\rm whole}=\left[1+\frac{(\alpha^2-1)^2}{4\alpha^2}\sin^2\delta\right]^{-1}.}
$$
Transmission is unity at $\delta=n\pi$ and minimal at $\delta=(n+1/2)\pi$, with minima $4\alpha^2/(1+\alpha^2)^2$. Resonances are separated by $\Delta f=v/(2\ell)$; the long-wavelength limit also transmits fully. Matching the far termination removes end echoes, not the two internal reflections.

= Solution
{parent=B8}

For a moving loop, <Faraday's law of induction> is $\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi_B/dt$, where $\mathbf u$ is the local wire <velocity>, $\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S$, and the oriented boundary and surface follow the right-hand rule. For a fixed loop the motional term vanishes. <Lenz's law> is the minus sign: the induced response opposes the change of linked flux, not necessarily the applied field itself.

Let $s$ increase downhill and choose positive current $I$ to produce an uphill magnetic <force>. The normal component of the imposed field is $B\cos\theta$, so the changing external flux is $Bl\cos\theta\,s$. With initially zero current and no resistance, conservation of <flux linkage> gives $LI=Bl\cos\theta\,s$. The rod feels gravity vertically downward, a rail reaction normal to the slope, and magnetic <force> of magnitude $IlB$ horizontally uphill. Its downhill component is $-IlB\cos\theta$; its normal component presses the rod into the rails, giving $N=mg\cos\theta+IlB\sin\theta$.

The <inductive oscillation of a sliding rod on inclined rails> follows from
$$
m\ddot s=mg\sin\theta-\frac{B^2l^2\cos^2\theta}{L}s,
\qquad\boxed{\kappa=\frac{l^2\cos^2\theta}{L}.}
$$
The magnetic <force> is not itself parallel to the rails. Defining $\omega_B=Bl\cos\theta/\sqrt{mL}$ and $s_e=mg\sin\theta/(m\omega_B^2)$, release from rest gives
$$
s(t)=s_e(1-\cos\omega_Bt),\qquad
\boxed{s_{\rm first\ stop}=2s_e=\frac{2mgL\sin\theta}{B^2l^2\cos^2\theta}.}
$$
The first stop occurs at $t=\pi/\omega_B$; the rod then returns and oscillates between $0$ and $2s_e$. At $s_e$ the <force> is zero but the <speed> is maximal. Mechanical <potential energy> is reversibly exchanged with <kinetic energy> and $LI^2/2$; a perfectly conducting circuit provides no damping.

For the <resistive long-time drift of an inductive sliding rod>, with loop resistance $R>0$, use
$$
L\dot I+RI=Bl\cos\theta\,\dot s,\qquad
m\ddot s=mg\sin\theta-Bl\cos\theta\,I.
$$
<Joule heating> damps the transient oscillation. On sufficiently long rails the asymptotic motion is steady sliding, not permanent rest:
$$
\boxed{I_\infty=\frac{mg\sin\theta}{Bl\cos\theta},\qquad
v_\infty=\frac{Rmg\sin\theta}{B^2l^2\cos^2\theta}.}
$$
The zero-resistance model has persistent oscillations; its late-time limit cannot be interchanged with taking $R\to0$. These results assume $B\cos\theta\ne0$, smooth contacts, negligible rod self-inductance and constant geometry.

= Solution
{parent=B9}

For cold, collisionless, unmagnetized electrons with stationary heavy ions, $-m_e\omega^2\mathbf x=-e\mathbf E$. Therefore $\mathbf P=-ne\mathbf x=-ne^2\mathbf E/(m_e\omega^2)$ and
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2},\qquad
\omega_p=\sqrt{\frac{ne^2}{m_e\epsilon_0}}.}
$$
This is the <cold collisionless plasma dielectric response>. For a propagating transverse wave, $\mathbf B=\mathbf k\times\mathbf E/\omega$, so $E/B=c$ in vacuum and $E/B=c/n_p$ in <plasma>, where $n_p=\sqrt{\epsilon_r}$. Equivalently $E/H=Z_0$ and $Z_0/n_p$ for $\mu=\mu_0$.

Converting the <density> gives $n=10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, so $\omega_p=5.64\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $f_p=8.98\,\mathrm{GHz}$. At $10\,\mathrm{GHz}$,
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_r\simeq0.19384,\qquad n_p\simeq0.4403.}
$$
At an interface without independently imposed surface charge or sheet current, tangential $\mathbf E,\mathbf H$ and normal $\mathbf B,\mathbf D$ are continuous, with the electron response included in the effective <permittivity>. Normal $E$ need not be continuous; the induced electron charge is represented by that response. In a convention treating it explicitly as a surface charge, the normal vacuum-displacement jump equals that charge instead.

For the downward transmitted wave, transversality gives $k_xE_x+k_zE_z=0$. The measured field therefore has $k_x/(-k_z)=3$, so $\tan\theta_t=3$ and $\sin\theta_t=3/\sqrt{10}$. Tangential <phase matching> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta_i=\sin^{-1}\left(n_p\frac3{\sqrt{10}}\right)\simeq24.69^\circ.}
$$
For <inferring incident power from a transmitted plasma field>, choose $\mathbf E_i=E_i(\cos\theta_i,0,\sin\theta_i)$ and $\mathbf E_r=E_r(\cos\theta_i,0,-\sin\theta_i)$. Their <magnetic fields> have opposite $y$ directions. The two tangential conditions are
$$
(E_i+E_r)\cos\theta_i=1\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}},\qquad
E_i-E_r=n_p\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}.
$$
Hence $E_i\simeq1.2464\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ and $E_r\simeq-0.1458\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$. Since these are peak phasor amplitudes,
$$
\boxed{\langle S_i\rangle=\frac{E_i^2}{2Z_0}
\simeq2.06\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.}
$$
This is incident <power> per area perpendicular to its propagation. Per area of the interface the incoming normal flux is $\langle S_i\rangle\cos\theta_i\simeq1.87\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. The reflected sign is a phase reversal, not negative reflected <energy>.

At $1\,\mathrm{GHz}<f_p$, the <relative permittivity> is negative and there is no propagating transverse bulk wave. The field decays evanescently into the <plasma> and the ideal collisionless half-space reflects all incoming normal <power>. Collisions would allow absorption; a <complex refractive index> here does not by itself imply dissipative heating.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

A <fictitious force> is a term required when writing <Newton's second law> in an accelerating frame; it comes from the coordinate choice rather than a new interaction. For axes rotating at constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$ about a fixed inertial origin,
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\!I}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\!R}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf A.
$$
Apply this identity twice to position to get $\mathbf a_I=\mathbf a_R+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$. Moving the last two terms to the right of $m\mathbf a_I=\mathbf F$ gives the stated Coriolis and <centrifugal forces>. An accelerating origin or changing rotation rate would add further terms.

A slowly rotating fluid planet follows an equipotential of gravitational plus <centrifugal potential>. The <centrifugal potential> is $-\Omega^2r^2\cos^2\lambda/2$, most negative at the equator. An outward equatorial shift raises <gravitational potential> and restores the balance, producing an oblate figure. Self-gravity also changes with the shape; no flattening coefficient follows from rotation alone without a <density> model.

Using local north and radial-up <unit vectors>, the <centrifugal acceleration> on a stationary <mass> is
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm cf}=\Omega^2R\big(\cos^2\lambda\,\mathbf e_U
-\sin\lambda\cos\lambda\,\mathbf e_N\big).
$$
It points away from the rotation axis: upward and equatorward, at angle $\lambda$ to radial up in the northern hemisphere. Its vertical fraction of gravitational <force> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{F_{{\rm cf},U}}{mg}=\frac{\Omega^2R}{g}\cos^2\lambda
\simeq3.45\times10^{-3}\cos^2\lambda,}
$$
using a $24\,\mathrm h$ day for this approximate number. The resultant effective weight tilts equatorward relative to radial down. A plumb-defined local vertical already follows effective gravity; the component calculation uses the spherical radial vertical. A supported stationary <mass> of course has zero net <acceleration>.

For the <Coriolis deflection of vertical launch and drop>, retain only first order in $\Omega$, use effective constant $g$, and neglect horizontal <velocities> in the Coriolis term as instructed. With east positive, vertical <velocity> gives $\ddot x=-2\Omega\cos\lambda\,\dot z$ and no northward <acceleration>. For launch, $z=ut-gt^2/2$ and the flight time is $2u/g$. Integrating with zero initial horizontal <velocity> gives
$$
\dot x=-2\Omega\cos\lambda\,z,\qquad
\boxed{x_X=-\frac{4\Omega u^3\cos\lambda}{3g^2},\quad y_X=0.}
$$
The landing is westward. The westward <velocity> accumulated on ascent is only recovered on descent, so the two displacement contributions do not cancel.

For release from rest at $h_0=u^2/(2g)$, $z=h_0-gt^2/2$, $t_{\rm fall}=u/g$, and $\ddot x=2\Omega g t\cos\lambda$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x_{X'}=\frac{\Omega u^3\cos\lambda}{3g^2}=-\frac14x_X,\quad y_{X'}=0.}
$$
The drop lands eastward with one quarter the launch-return displacement magnitude. North–south corrections from horizontal Coriolis coupling are beyond the retained approximation.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

The <shell theorem> gives enclosed halo <mass> $4\pi\rho r^3/3$. Define $\alpha=4\pi\rho/3$. Then **$F_r=-GMm/r^2-\alpha Gmr$**. Interior to the uniform halo its <potential energy> may be written
$$
U(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r}+\frac12\alpha Gmr^2+U_0,
\quad
E=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)+U(r).
$$
Outer shells alter the additive constant, not this interior <force>. An actually infinite uniform <mass> distribution has no finite potential referenced to infinity; only local potential differences are needed here.

The <central force> has zero <torque>, so $\mathbf J=\mathbf r\times m\dot{\mathbf r}$ is conserved and the motion is planar. With $J=mr^2\dot\phi$, the effective radial potential and equation are
$$
U_{\rm eff}=\frac{J^2}{2mr^2}-\frac{GMm}{r}+\frac12\alpha Gmr^2,
\qquad \ddot r=\frac{J^2}{m^2r^3}-\frac{GM}{r^2}-\alpha Gr.
$$
For circular radius $r_0$, $J=mr_0^2\Omega$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega^2=\frac{GM}{r_0^3}+\alpha G.}
$$
Set $r=r_0+\xi$ at fixed $J$. The <derivative> of the right-hand side at <equilibrium> is $-3\Omega^2+2GM/r_0^3-\alpha G$, so
$$
\boxed{\ddot\xi+\omega^2\xi=0,\qquad
\omega^2=\frac{GM}{r_0^3}+4\alpha G.}
$$
Both <frequencies> are positive, but radial and azimuthal motion no longer close with the Kepler ratio. At first order in <eccentricity> $e$, one may write $r=r_0(1-e\cos\omega t)$ and $\phi=\Omega t+(2\Omega e/\omega)\sin\omega t$. For a weak halo this is a nearly Keplerian ellipse whose apsis rotates at angular rate $\Omega-\omega$.

Put $\Omega_K=\sqrt{GM/r_0^3}$ and $\varepsilon=\alpha r_0^3/M\ll1$. Expansion yields
$$
\boxed{\dot\varpi=\Omega-\omega\simeq-\frac32\Omega_K\varepsilon
=-\frac{3\alpha G}{2\Omega_K}=-\frac{2\pi G\rho}{\Omega_K}.}
$$
The precession is retrograde. In cycles per unit time the precession <frequency> is $\dot\varpi/(2\pi)$; per radial period the apsidal shift is $2\pi(\Omega/\omega-1)\simeq-3\pi\varepsilon$. This weak-halo interpretation avoids confusing a focus-centred Kepler ellipse with the centred ellipse of a purely harmonic potential.

{parent=Section C}

= Solution
{parent=C12}

A <normal mode> is a pattern in which all coordinates share one <frequency> and fixed relative amplitudes. For a conservative linear system, it solves a generalized <eigenvalue> equation with <stiffness matrix> $K$ and <mass matrix> $M$. For this <free three-mass spring chain>,
$$
K=k\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad M=m\operatorname{diag}(1,\alpha,1).
$$
Solving $K\mathbf u=\omega^2M\mathbf u$, for $\alpha>0$, gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\omega^2&\mathbf u\\\hline
0&(1,1,1)\\
k/m&(1,0,-1)\\
(k/m)(1+2/\alpha)&(1,-2/\alpha,1)
\end{array}.}
$$
Their overall normalization is arbitrary; distinct modes are <orthogonal> in the <mass> <inner product>. The zero mode is rigid translation because neither end is fixed. In the antisymmetric mode the middle <mass> stays still, so changing its <mass> cannot alter the <frequency>. The symmetric internal mode keeps the <centre of mass> fixed through its middle displacement $-2/\alpha$. Its <frequency> rises without bound as $\alpha\to0$ and approaches $\sqrt{k/m}$ as $\alpha\to\infty$, becoming degenerate with the antisymmetric <frequency> in that limit.

For equal <masses> put $\omega_1=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\omega_2=\sqrt{3k/m}$. Positions are <equilibrium> positions plus displacements
$$
\mathbf q(t)=(A_0+B_0t)\mathbf u_0
+(A_1\cos\omega_1t+B_1\sin\omega_1t)\mathbf u_1
+(A_2\cos\omega_2t+B_2\sin\omega_2t)\mathbf u_2.
$$
The translational mode needs the linear-in-time term; a cosine at zero <frequency> alone would miss arbitrary centre-of-mass <velocity>.

Taking the <springs> initially at <equilibrium>, the <impulse> gives $\dot{\mathbf q}(0)=(v,0,0)=(v/3)\mathbf u_0+(v/2)\mathbf u_1+(v/6)\mathbf u_2$. Their squared Euclidean norms are $3,2,6$, so their initial <kinetic energies> are $mv^2/6,mv^2/4,mv^2/12$. Relative to total $mv^2/2$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{E_0}{E}=\frac13,\qquad\frac{E_1}{E}=\frac12,\qquad\frac{E_2}{E}=\frac16.}
$$
Subsequent motion conserves each mode's total <energy>, exchanging its kinetic and <spring> contributions. The zero-mode share remains centre-of-mass <kinetic energy>.

= Solution
{parent=C13}

Locally, zero curl permits $\mathbf v=\nabla\Phi$; for a global single-valued potential the <circulation> around every noncontractible loop must also vanish. <incompressibility> gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=\nabla^2\Phi=0$. The exterior of a cylinder is multiply connected, so local irrotationality alone does not exclude a circulating solution.

For inviscid constant-density flow under conservative gravity, Euler's equation is $\partial_t\mathbf v+(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v=-\nabla(p/\rho+\phi_g)$. The identity $(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v=\nabla(v^2/2)-\mathbf v\times(\nabla\times\mathbf v)$ gives, for <irrotational flow>,
$$
\partial_t\Phi+\frac p\rho+\frac{v^2}{2}+\phi_g=C(t).
$$
For <steady flow>, absorb the potential's time-only gauge term and obtain **$p+\rho v^2/2+\rho\phi_g=\mathrm{constant}$** throughout the connected fluid region. Without irrotationality the steady constant need only be common along each <streamline>.

Use $U$ for the uniform upstream <speed> and take the flow along $+x$. The <boundary conditions> are $v_r(a,\theta)=0$ and $\mathbf v\to U\mathbf e_x$ at infinity. A perfect inviscid model imposes no penetration, not no slip. For the intended no-circulation solution, require a globally single-valued potential, so the coefficient of $\theta$ is zero. The logarithmic term would be a net source and violates no penetration, so its coefficient is also zero. Growing terms with $n\geq2$ violate the uniform far field. For each remaining $n\geq2$, the surface normal condition then <forces> its decaying coefficient to vanish. The $n=1$ terms satisfy $C_1=U$ and $D_1=Ua^2$:
$$
\boxed{\Phi=U(r+a^2/r)\cos\theta,\qquad
v_r=U(1-a^2/r^2)\cos\theta,\quad
v_\theta=-U(1+a^2/r^2)\sin\theta.}
$$
An irrelevant additive potential constant is understood. At the same elevation, Bernoulli balance with the upstream stream gives
$$
\boxed{p(a,\theta)=p_\infty+\frac12\rho U^2(1-4\sin^2\theta).}
$$
The <force> per unit height is
$$
\mathbf F'=-a\int_0^{2\pi}p(a,\theta)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta
=\boxed{\mathbf0.}
$$
<pressure> <symmetry> gives zero <drag> and zero <lift> in this zero-circulation idealization. <D'Alembert's paradox> is the failure of this inviscid model to represent real separated viscous wakes and their <drag>, not a claim that a real pole feels no <force>.

The zero-circulation assumption matters: $\Phi\to\Phi+\Gamma\theta/(2\pi)$ leaves the stated <velocity> <boundary conditions> intact but adds $\Gamma/(2\pi a)$ to the surface tangential <speed>. It produces $F'_y=-\rho U\Gamma$ for positive counterclockwise <circulation>. Thus the <boundary conditions> alone do not justify excluding the angular term; an initially noncirculating inviscid start, or an explicitly single-valued potential, supplies the missing condition.<Flow Past a Cylindrical Obstacle by Richard Fitzpatrick>[equations 5.73–5.81 and discussion after 5.83: circulation, force and the zero-circulation initial condition]{ref}
