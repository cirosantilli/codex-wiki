For the <force on a current loop in a bilinear magnetic field>, the arrow gives current in the positive $y$ direction on the edge $x=a$, and in the negative $x$ direction on the edge $y=a$. Use $d\mathbf F=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$. The edges on the coordinate axes contribute zero because the applied field vanishes there. The other two give
$$
\mathbf F_{x=a}=I\alpha a\int_0^a y\,dy\,\hat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf F_{y=a}=I\alpha a\int_0^a x\,dx\,\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$
Thus the force on the square itself, excluding the supply leads, is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=\frac{I\alpha a^3}{2}(\hat{\mathbf x}+\hat{\mathbf y})
=2.5(\hat{\mathbf x}+\hat{\mathbf y})\,\mathrm N,\quad |\mathbf F|=3.54\,\mathrm N.}
$$
It lies in the plane along the diagonal towards increasing $x$ and $y$, at $45^\circ$ to either positive axis.

In the ideal <semidiurnal tide> model, successive high tides occur after the <Earth>–<Moon> relative angle advances by $\pi$. Since the motions have the same sense, subtract their <angular speeds>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\frac{\pi}{\Omega_E-\Omega_M}
=\frac1{2(T_E^{-1}-T_M^{-1})}.}
$$
The lunar <orbital period> is sidereal, so the consistent terrestrial <rotation> period is $T_E=23.9345\,\mathrm h$. With $T_M=27.3\times24\,\mathrm h$, this gives **about 12 h 25 min**. The sidereal terrestrial period is distinct from the 24-hour solar day.<Earth Fact Sheet by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Orbital parameters, sidereal rotation period 23.9345 h and length of day 24 h]{ref}

If the <Earth>'s <rotation> is approximated as one revolution in 24 hours, the same calculation instead gives **12 h 27 min**. That small difference is the day convention, not a change of sign in the relative <angular speed>. Actual coastal <tide> times also depend on ocean dynamics and the solar <tide>.

For the <uniform magnetic field in an off-axis cylindrical cavity>, let $\mathbf b$ point from the conductor's axis to the hole's axis and let the current flow in $+z$. Its uniform <current density> in the metal is
$$
\mathbf J=\frac{I}{\pi(R^2-a^2)}\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Superpose a complete cylinder carrying $\mathbf J$ and a cylinder occupying the hole carrying $-\mathbf J$. Inside a uniformly filled cylinder, <Ampère's law> gives $\mathbf B=(\mu_0/2)\mathbf J\times\mathbf r$. For any point in the cavity,
$$
\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{2}\bigl[\mathbf J\times\mathbf r-\mathbf J\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf b)\bigr]
=\boxed{\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi(R^2-a^2)}\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf b.}
$$
There is no remaining position dependence, so the field is uniform. Its magnitude is $\mu_0Ib/[2\pi(R^2-a^2)]$, perpendicular to the axis-offset <vector> in the cross-sectional plane. Reversing the current reverses the field.

Use the <rotating reference frame> equilibrium model, in which the <centrifugal potential> <energy> of a particle of <mass> $m_i$ is $U_i(r)=-m_i\omega^2r^2/2$. The <Boltzmann distribution> then gives
$$
\frac{n_i(r)}{n_i(a)}=
\exp\left[\frac{m_i\omega^2(r^2-a^2)}{2k_BT}\right].
$$
Define the concentration ratio as $c=n_1/n_2$. At the axis,
$$
\boxed{c_{\rm axis}=c_{\rm wall}
\exp\left[\frac{(m_2-m_1)\omega^2a^2}{2k_BT}\right].}
$$
Thus the axis favours the lighter species. The expression assumes a dilute classical mixture at one <temperature>, equilibrium in the <rotating reference frame>, and sampling sufficiently close to $r=0$.

Define $q=N_+-N_-$, where $N_+$ counts particles in the $+\epsilon$ level. Then $N_\pm=(N\pm q)/2$, so
$$
\boxed{U=\epsilon q,\qquad
S=k_B\ln\frac{N!}{[(N+q)/2]!\,[(N-q)/2]!}.}
$$
The <binomial entropy of independent two-level particles> follows from counting which of the $N$ independently labelled particles occupy each nondegenerate level. This is the usual distinguishable, or localized, two-level model; occupation constraints of a degenerate quantum gas would require different counting.

For large occupations, <Stirling's approximation> with $x=q/N$ gives
$$
\boxed{S\simeq Nk_B\left[\ln2-\frac{1+x}{2}\ln(1+x)-\frac{1-x}{2}\ln(1-x)\right].}
$$
Allowed $q$ runs from $-N$ to $N$ in steps of two. <Entropy> is largest near equal populations and is zero when all particles occupy one level; use $0\ln0=0$ at the endpoints.

In vacuum, **<displacement current> contributes the additional term** $\epsilon_0\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$:
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$
Taking the divergence and using <Gauss law> gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial\rho/\partial t=0$, the local conservation of charge. Without <displacement current> the curl equation would incorrectly require a divergence-free current even during charge accumulation.

For a charging <capacitor>, a surface cutting the wire carries conduction current while a surface passing through the gap carries changing <electric flux>. The <displacement current> term makes the circulation independent of the spanning surface. It is not transport of free charge across the vacuum gap. In matter the macroscopic equation is $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$, with <electric polarization> included in $\mathbf D$. Together with <Faraday's law of induction>, the vacuum equations give <electromagnetic waves> of <speed> $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$.

An applied <electric field> displaces <bound charges> or reorients permanent dipoles, producing <electric polarization> $\mathbf P$, the dipole moment per unit volume. It gives <bound charges> $\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ and $\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf n$. Define
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P,\qquad
\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad
\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)
$$
for a **linear isotropic <dielectric>**. Then $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f$ separates free charge from the bound-charge response. Electronic, ionic and orientational <electric polarization> respond on different timescales; real <permittivity> is <frequency> dependent, and its dissipative part describes <dielectric loss>.

A uniform <dielectric> filling a <parallel-plate capacitor> raises its <capacitance> to $\epsilon A/d$. At fixed charge this reduces field <energy> $Q^2/(2C)$; at fixed <voltage> the stored <energy> $CV^2/2$ increases because the <voltage> source supplies charge and <energy>. Insulation and <energy> storage are useful applications, limited by loss and <dielectric breakdown>. Static interface conditions are continuous tangential $\mathbf E$ and a jump in normal $\mathbf D$ equal to free surface charge.

**A current in one circuit links <magnetic flux> through another.** It produces <magnetic flux linkage> through another. With fixed geometry and a linear reciprocal medium, choose consistent winding orientations and write
$$
\Lambda_1=L_1I_1+MI_2,\qquad \Lambda_2=MI_1+L_2I_2.
$$
The shared coefficient is **<mutual inductance>**; reversal of one winding reverses its sign. <Faraday's law of induction> gives the mutual induced emf $\mathcal E_2=-M\dot I_1$ for fixed $M$. The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>.

The <magnetic energy> is
$$
W=\tfrac12L_1I_1^2+MI_1I_2+\tfrac12L_2I_2^2.
$$
Nonnegative <energy> for every current pair requires $|M|\leq\sqrt{L_1L_2}$, motivating the coupling coefficient $M/\sqrt{L_1L_2}$. Transformers use strong common flux to exchange <energy> between circuits; leakage flux reduces coupling. A shared core increases linkage, but saturation, <hysteresis> and eddy-current losses limit the linear description. A moving geometry also contributes $-I_1\dot M$ to the mutual emf.

= Solution

Find the potential from $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$ and $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\nabla\phi)=-\rho_f$. One must specify boundary data: a potential on each Dirichlet boundary, a normal displacement on each Neumann boundary, or an appropriate mixture. Conductors are equipotentials; at <dielectric> interfaces $\phi$ is continuous and the normal displacement jumps by the specified free surface charge. With no singular dipole sheet, tangential <electric field> is continuous.

To establish <uniqueness of Poisson equation>, subtract two solutions with the same charge and boundary data, writing their difference as $u$. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_V\epsilon|\nabla u|^2\,dV
=\int_{\partial V}u\epsilon\partial_nu\,dA,
$$
since $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\nabla u)=0$. The right side vanishes for identical Dirichlet data, identical Neumann data, or their usual mixed combination. Interface terms cancel under the homogeneous difference conditions. With positive <permittivity>, $\nabla u=0$: **the field is unique**, and the potential is unique if any suitable reference potential is fixed. For a pure Neumann problem an additive constant remains, and the prescribed outward displacement flux must equal the enclosed free charge. This theorem justifies image-charge solutions once their boundary data have been checked.

<Magnetization> $\mathbf M$ is <magnetic dipole moment> per unit volume. In SI units,
$$
\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M),\qquad
\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H,\qquad \mu=\mu_0(1+\chi)
$$
in a linear isotropic response. The internal field matters: for a uniformly magnetized <ellipsoid> it is $\mathbf H_{\rm int}=\mathbf H_{\rm applied}-\mathbf N_d\mathbf M$. Sample shape therefore affects an apparent <magnetic susceptibility>.

**<Diamagnetism>** opposes the applied field, giving small negative <magnetic susceptibility> from the induced orbital response. It is often only weakly <temperature> dependent. **<Paramagnetism>** aligns pre-existing moments and gives positive <magnetic susceptibility>; independent localized moments obey approximately $\chi=C/T$ in weak fields before saturating. <Pauli paramagnetism> of conduction electrons is a different, weakly temperature-dependent mechanism.

**<Ferromagnetism>** involves collective exchange-driven alignment and spontaneous <magnetization> below the <Curie temperature>. A specimen can nevertheless have nearly zero total moment because <magnetic domains> point in different directions. Field-driven domain-wall motion and <rotation> change the net <magnetization>. <Antiferromagnets> have compensating ordered sublattices below a <Néel temperature>; <ferrimagnets> have oppositely ordered but unequal sublattice moments and retain a net moment. Ferrites are useful examples of electrically resistive ferrimagnetic core materials.

Ferromagnetic response is nonlinear and history dependent. A <hysteresis> loop displays <remanent magnetization>, <magnetic coercivity> and saturation. A single constant <magnetic permeability> cannot describe the entire loop: differential <magnetic permeability> describes its local slope. The quasistatic <energy> loss per unit volume and cycle is the loop <area> $\oint H\,dB$. <Soft magnetic materials> have low <magnetic coercivity> and usually high useful <magnetic permeability>; <hard magnetic materials> retain <magnetization> and suit permanent magnets.

**An <electromagnet> normally uses a soft, high-permeability core** to guide flux generated by a coil. For a uniform-section core with length $\ell_c$ and air gap $g$, neglecting leakage and fringing,
$$
NI\simeq H_c\ell_c+\frac{Bg}{\mu_0},\qquad
\boxed{B\simeq\frac{\mu_0NI}{g+\ell_c/\mu_r}.}
$$
An air gap can dominate the <magnetic reluctance> even when the core <magnetic permeability> is large. Core saturation limits the improvement with current, while winding resistance causes heating. For alternating operation, laminations suppress eddy-current loops in metallic cores; high-resistivity ferrites also reduce those losses. Low <hysteresis> loss is important in repeatedly cycled cores, whereas high <magnetic coercivity> is valuable in a permanent magnet. Useful design therefore depends on the full magnetic response, electrical losses and operating <frequency>, rather than the largest quoted <magnetic permeability> alone.

<Generalized coordinates> incorporate <holonomic constraints> without solving separately for every constraint force. For conservative mechanics use $L=T-V$. The <principle of stationary action> makes the <action> $\int L\,dt$ stationary for variations vanishing at the endpoints. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
The <canonical momentum> is $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$. A <cyclic coordinate> gives conserved $p_i$; absent explicit time dependence, $\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L$ is conserved. These are symmetry–conservation relationships, not assumptions that all momenta are constant. Nonconservative generalized forces enter the right side.

For example, a pendulum has $L=m\ell^2\dot\theta^2/2+mg\ell\cos\theta$, giving $m\ell^2\ddot\theta+mg\ell\sin\theta=0$. The method extends directly to coupled coordinates and velocity-dependent interactions when the correct <Lagrangian> is used; $T-V$ with a position-only potential is not the most general form.

<Viscosity> transfers <momentum> between adjacent fluid layers. A <Newtonian fluid> has <shear stress> proportional to <strain> rate: for simple shear, $\boxed{\tau_{xy}=\eta\,\partial u_x/\partial y}$. Thus parallel plates of <area> $A$, separation $d$ and relative <speed> $U$ require force magnitude $\eta AU/d$ in a steady linear profile.

For an incompressible isotropic <Newtonian fluid>,
$$
\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+\eta(\partial_i u_j+\partial_j u_i),\qquad
\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u+\rho\mathbf g
$$
for constant <viscosity>. With <strain-rate tensor> $e_{ij}$, the <viscous dissipation> rate per unit volume is $2\eta e_{ij}e_{ij}\geq0$. <Kinematic viscosity> $\nu=\eta/\rho$ is the <momentum> diffusivity: a disturbance spreads over distance of order $\sqrt{\nu t}$.

The <Reynolds number> $\rho UL/\eta$ compares inertial and viscous forces. Small <Reynolds number> supports a creeping-flow approximation, while large values permit thin viscous <boundary layers> and possible <turbulence>. It does not mean molecular <viscosity> has disappeared. Gas <viscosity> results from molecular <momentum> transport; a liquid's microscopic transport depends strongly on intermolecular interactions. Non-Newtonian materials require a more general constitutive law.

For an attractive <inverse-square potential>, let the <reduced mass> be $\mu$, potential $-k/r$, and nonzero orbital <angular momentum> $\ell$. Conservation of <angular momentum> makes the orbit planar. The orbit equation is
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{\ell^2/(\mu k)}{1+e\cos\varphi},\qquad
e^2=1+\frac{2E\ell^2}{\mu k^2}.}
$$
Positive relative <energy> gives $e>1$ and an unbound <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>, arriving from and returning to infinity. Its asymptotes satisfy $\cos\varphi_\infty=-1/e$. At infinity $E=\mu v_\infty^2/2$ and $\ell=\mu b v_\infty$ for <impact parameter> $b$, so the deflection angle is
$$
\boxed{\chi=2\arcsin(1/e)
=2\arctan\frac{k}{\mu b v_\infty^2}.}
$$
This describes gravitational encounters in the Newtonian two-body approximation. The body speeds up near <periapsis> while total <energy> remains positive. It is distinct from an elliptic bound orbit and from the zero-energy parabolic limit; repulsive Coulomb scattering also gives hyperbolae but uses a different orientation of the force and orbit.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a homogeneous isotropic solid with small displacement $\mathbf u$, <linear elasticity> gives $\sigma_{ij}=\lambda_L(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij}$. <Momentum> balance becomes
$$
\rho\,\partial_t^2\mathbf u=(\lambda_L+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u.
$$
A <plane wave> with displacement parallel to its <wavevector> is longitudinal, whereas perpendicular displacement is transverse. Writing $K=\lambda_L+2\mu/3$ for the <bulk modulus> and $\mu$ for the <shear modulus>, their bulk speeds are
$$
\boxed{c_P=\sqrt{\frac{K+4\mu/3}{\rho}},\qquad c_S=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}}.}
$$
There are two independent transverse polarizations. The <bulk modulus> supplies resistance to compression; shear rigidity supplies transverse restoring <stress>. An ordinary fluid has no static shear rigidity and cannot sustain these propagating bulk shear waves. A thin rod with freely contracting sides instead has longitudinal <speed> $\sqrt{Y/\rho}$, because its lateral <boundary conditions> differ from a bulk <plane wave>. <Reflection>, <refraction> and mode conversion occur at interfaces; a free surface also supports <Rayleigh waves>.

= Solution

For torque-free <rotation>, <angular momentum> is fixed in inertial space, but <angular velocity> need not point along it. In principal body axes,
$$
I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=0
$$
and cyclic permutations. A <symmetric top> with transverse moment $A$ and axial moment $C$ has constant $\omega_3$, while $\omega_1+i\omega_2$ rotates in body coordinates at signed <frequency> $(C-A)\omega_3/A$. The symmetry axis precesses around the fixed <angular momentum> in space at rate $L/A$. These body-frame and space-frame rates describe different motions and need not coincide.

**<Poinsot construction>** expresses the same torque-free motion geometrically. The <energy> <ellipsoid> $\sum I_i\omega_i^2=2T$ is fixed in the body. Its normal at the angular-velocity point is parallel to $\mathbf L$, and $\mathbf L\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=2T$ places that point in a fixed plane perpendicular to $\mathbf L$. The <ellipsoid> rolls on this invariable plane without slipping: the instantaneous <velocity> of its contact point is proportional to $\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\omega=0$. The contact curve is the <polhode> on the body <ellipsoid> and the <herpolhode> on the plane.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[§3.4, Euler equations, stability and Poinsot construction]{ref}

For $I_1<I_2<I_3$, linearization about spin $\Omega$ on the third axis gives
$$
\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)}{I_1I_2}\omega_1.
$$
The extreme-moment axes are stable; the intermediate one is unstable. The <major-axis relaxation theorem> adds weak internal dissipation while retaining negligible external <torque>. At fixed $L$,
$$
T=\sum_i\frac{L_i^2}{2I_i}\geq\boxed{\frac{L^2}{2I_3}.}
$$
<Energy> loss favours <rotation> about the axis of largest principal moment. Here “major axis” means maximum inertia, not necessarily the body's longest geometric direction. **A perfectly rigid torque-free body does not dissipatively settle**; its <energy> is conserved. Distinguishing stability from relaxation also explains why minimum-inertia <rotation> can be stable in the ideal rigid model.

**Forced <precession>** occurs when an external <torque> changes $\mathbf L$, with $\dot{\mathbf L}=\boldsymbol\tau$. For steady <precession>, $\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\Omega_p\times\mathbf L$. A rapidly spinning, pivoted heavy <gyroscope> has $L\simeq C\omega_3$ along its symmetry axis, giving
$$
\boxed{\Omega_p\simeq\frac{Mg\ell}{C\omega_3}.}
$$
The <torque> is perpendicular to the nearly axial <angular momentum>, so it mainly changes its direction. For a steady inclined <symmetric top>, the more complete condition is
$$
A\Omega_p^2\cos\theta-C\omega_3\Omega_p+Mg\ell=0.
$$
It exhibits slow and fast <precession> branches when real solutions exist. A generic release excites <nutation> as well: inclination oscillates instead of staying fixed. Axis constraints and bearing forces must be included when specifying which <angular momentum> and pivot are being used.

For <speed> distribution $f(v)$ normalized to one and isotropic directions, the incident number flux is
$$
J=n\int_0^\infty vf(v)\,dv\,
\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta
=\boxed{\frac14n\overline v.}
$$
The positive normal <velocity> weights the population reaching the wall. Consequently its mean translational <energy> is
$$
\langle E\rangle_{\rm hit}=\frac m2\frac{\int_0^\infty v^5e^{-av^2}\,dv}{\int_0^\infty v^3e^{-av^2}\,dv}
=\frac m2\frac{a^{-3}}{(2a^2)^{-1}}
=\boxed{2k_BT},\qquad a=\frac{m}{2k_BT}.
$$
The bulk value $3k_BT/2$ would omit the arrival weighting.

For the two vessels, let $r=\sqrt{T_1/T_0}$, with subscripts h and c for hot and cold. Equal opposing <effusion> rates give $p_h/\sqrt{T_1}=p_c/\sqrt{T_0}$. Number conservation in equal, fixed volumes adds $p_h/T_1+p_c/T_0=2p_0/T_0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{p_c=\frac{2p_0r}{1+r},\quad p_h=\frac{2p_0r^2}{1+r},\quad
p_h-p_c=\frac{2p_0r(r-1)}{1+r}.}
$$
Both final <pressures> must be determined; holding the cold <temperature> fixed does not hold its <particle number> fixed.

For aperture <area> $A_h=\pi d^2/4$, the common one-way flux is $J=p_c/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT_0}$. The translational <heat> transfer from hot to cold is therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot Q=2k_B(T_1-T_0)A_hJ.}
$$
This is the total <heat> transfer for a monatomic gas with no active internal modes, neglecting radiation and conduction through vessel walls. For a molecular gas, add $A_hJ[u_{\rm int}(T_1)-u_{\rm int}(T_0)]$ for its <internal energy> per molecule. The maintained unequal <temperatures> describe a stationary nonequilibrium state even though the net particle current is zero.

A <thermodynamic potential> packages the equilibrium state in variables suited to the imposed constraints. For a simple fluid, $U(S,V,N)$ has differential $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN$. <Legendre transforms> give $H=U+pV$, $F=U-TS$ and $G=U+pV-TS$, with <natural variables of a thermodynamic potential> $(S,p,N)$, $(T,V,N)$ and $(T,p,N)$ respectively. At fixed <particle number>, **<Helmholtz free energy> is minimized at fixed $T,V$; <Gibbs free energy> at fixed $T,p$**.

A flow vessel is an open <control volume>, even if its walls are enclosed. For each inlet, <pressure> forces carry flow <work> $p\,dV$ in addition to <internal energy>. The steady first-law balance is
$$
\sum_{\rm in}\dot m(h+v^2/2+gz)+\dot Q-\dot W_{\rm shaft}
=\sum_{\rm out}\dot m(h+v^2/2+gz).
$$
With no <heat> exchange, shaft <work>, or significant kinetic and gravitational <energy> differences, the <enthalpy> fluxes are equal. <Mass> flow must also balance. This equality concerns mass-flow-weighted specific enthalpies, not an unweighted sum over pipes.

Use the <liquefaction yield with separate warm-gas and liquid outlets>. The wording specifies that the outgoing gas is at 14 K; it does not require the outgoing <liquid> to have that <temperature>. The two outlet streams need not be in mutual <thermal equilibrium>. Taking $H$ as the printed <specific enthalpy>, the <steady-flow energy balance> gives
$$
H(p)=\alpha H_\ell+(1-\alpha)H(p_0),\qquad
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{H(p_0)-H(p)}{H(p_0)-H_\ell}.}
$$
For fixed outlet enthalpies the greatest yield occurs at the smallest inlet <enthalpy>. **Among the tabulated <pressures> this is 30 atm.** Approximating the atmospheric-pressure gas <enthalpy> by the tabulated zero-pressure limiting value gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha\simeq\frac{8.74-7.18}{8.74-1.01}=0.202,\quad p\simeq30\,\mathrm{atm}.}
$$
This uses a low-pressure approximation: $p_0=1$ atm is not literally the zero-pressure table entry. Linear interpolation to 1 atm would instead give $H(p_0)\simeq8.651\times10^4\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ and $\alpha\simeq0.193$. The sparse table does not determine an exact continuous optimum between entries.

Atmospheric-pressure liquid <helium> must be much colder than the 14 K gas outlet: its normal boiling point is about 4.224 K.<Index of Semiconductor Process Gases - Helium by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Helium-4 characteristic-property table: normal boiling point 4.224 K]{ref} A <counterflow heat exchanger> can return cold gas through the incoming stream before that gas leaves the overall <control volume>, while the collected <liquid> leaves separately at the cold end. This is a possible interpretation of the whole apparatus, rather than an extra diagram specified by the question. The enthalpy calculation needs only the stated inlet and outlet properties; it does not assume one equilibrium vessel containing both phases at 14 K. In particular, $H(p_0)-H_\ell$ is an outlet-stream enthalpy difference, not the <latent heat> of vaporization at one common <temperature>.

A <heat pump> delivers $Q_H=Q_S+W$: electrical <work> moves <heat> from a source as well as contributing <energy> itself. Its heating <coefficient of performance> can therefore exceed one, whereas direct resistance heating supplies at most its electrical input as useful room <heat>. For an ideal reversible pump,
$$
\mathrm{COP}_H=\frac{T_H}{T_H-T_S},\qquad W=Q_H\frac{T_H-T_S}{T_H},
$$
with absolute <temperatures> and $T_S<T_H$. This is an ideal limit; actual costs also depend on compressor performance and tariffs.

For the <annual ground-temperature wave>, model the soil as a homogeneous semi-infinite conductor, with depth positive downward and a prescribed surface <temperature>. Here $\kappa$ is <thermal conductivity> and $C$ is <heat capacity> per unit volume, so the <thermal diffusivity> is $\kappa/C$. Conservation of <energy> gives $C\partial_tT=\kappa\partial_z^2T$. After transients decay, put $T-T_0=\operatorname{Re}[A e^{i\omega t-qz}]$. Substitution gives $q^2=i\omega C/\kappa$; the solution bounded at depth uses $q=(1+i)/\delta$. Matching the surface then gives
$$
\boxed{\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega C}},\quad
T_S=T_0-T_Ae^{-z/\delta}\cos(\omega t-z/\delta).}
$$
This approximation identifies the ground surface with air <temperature> and neglects the pump's perturbation of the ground, as required by the model. The attenuation and phase delay arise from <heat> <diffusion>, not propagation at one fixed wave <speed>.

The graph below covers a full annual cycle. At $z/\delta=\pi/2$, the amplitude is $20e^{-\pi/2}=4.16$ K and the minimum lags January by a quarter-year. At $z/\delta=\pi$, the amplitude is only $0.864$ K and the minimum lags by half a year: the ground is slightly warmer than its annual mean in January.

{title=Annual air and ground temperatures, with attenuation and depth-dependent phase delay}

The <seasonal source depth for a reversible heat pump> has a subtle limit: a deep source approaches $T_0$ and avoids severe winter cooling, but infinite depth is not the thermal optimum of this single-frequency model. In January, with $x=z/\delta$, maximize $T_S=T_0-T_Ae^{-x}\cos x$. The first and largest maximum is at $x=3\pi/4$, where $\cos x+\sin x=0$, giving **$z=3\pi\delta/4$ and $T_S\simeq16.34^\circ\mathrm C$**. It is also the optimum of the ideal annually integrated heating <work>: the heating load is even about January, so its weighted sine term integrates to zero and the depth dependence is again proportional to $e^{-x}\cos x$. Installation cost, finite tube spacing, <heat> extraction, weather and real pump performance alter this result. The supplied model therefore supports a finite seasonal thermal optimum, while not fixing a practical burial depth in metres.

= Solution

<Phase equilibrium> minimizes <Gibbs free energy> at specified <temperature>, <pressure> and composition. Coexisting phases have equal <temperature>, <pressure> and <chemical potential>. For a pure substance, a first-order transition has continuous equilibrium <Gibbs free energy> but a jump in its first derivatives, such as <entropy> or volume; the <latent heat> is $T\Delta S$. Continuous transitions lack that <latent heat> and can instead have singular or discontinuous higher responses.

The van der Waals molar equation
$$
p=\frac{RT}{v-b}-\frac a{v^2}
$$
combines an excluded-volume correction with attractive interactions. Its inflection conditions give
$$
\boxed{v_c=3b,\quad T_c=\frac{8a}{27Rb},\quad p_c=\frac{a}{27b^2}.}
$$
Below $T_c$, the oscillatory isotherm contains a mechanically unstable segment with $(\partial p/\partial v)_T>0$. Equilibrium instead replaces the loop by a constant-pressure coexistence line. Equality of chemical potentials gives the <Maxwell construction>,
$$
\int_{v_\ell}^{v_g}[p(v,T)-p_{\rm sat}]\,dv=0.
$$
On isothermal compression the gas reaches saturation, then converts progressively into liquid at fixed <pressure>. The mean <molar volume> obeys $v=(1-x)v_\ell+xv_g$, where $x$ is the gas fraction. Metastable superheated or supercooled states can persist when <nucleation> is delayed; they are not the global equilibrium selected by this construction.

\Image[paper-2-b2-phase-coexistence.png]
{title=Illustrative reduced van der Waals coexistence and a generic pure-substance phase diagram}

A <pressure>–<temperature> <phase diagram> contains coexistence curves separating solid, liquid and gas. Three phases meet at a <triple point>; the liquid–gas coexistence curve ends at a <thermodynamic critical point>, above which liquid and gas can be connected without crossing a first-order phase boundary. A generic solid–liquid line continues beyond the liquid–gas critical <temperature>. For one component the <Gibbs phase rule> is $F=3-P$, so a three-phase equilibrium fixes both <temperature> and <pressure>.

Along a two-phase coexistence curve, $d\mu=-s\,dT+v\,dp$ for each phase gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v}=\frac{L}{T\Delta v}.}
$$
This is the Clapeyron equation, valid with consistent molar or specific quantities. For vaporization, neglecting the liquid volume and treating the vapour as ideal gives $\Delta v\simeq RT/p$, hence $d\ln p/dT=L/(RT^2)$. With approximately constant molar <latent heat>,
$$
\boxed{\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}=-\frac LR\left(\frac1{T_2}-\frac1{T_1}\right).}
$$
This Clausius–Clapeyron approximation explains the rapid rise of vapour <pressure> with <temperature>. It fails close to the <thermodynamic critical point>, where phase volumes converge, and is not a universal expression for melting. The sign of a melting-line slope depends on its volume change; water's denser liquid gives a familiar negative slope.
