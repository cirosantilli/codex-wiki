= Solution
{parent=A1}

This is the one-mole, or equivalently <molar volume>, form of the <Van der Waals equation>, an approximate <equation of state> for a <real gas>:
$$
p=\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac{a}{V^2}.
$$
Here $p$ is the gas <pressure>, $T$ its absolute <temperature>, $R$ the <molar gas constant>, and $V$ the <volume> occupied by one mole. If $V$ denotes <molar volume> explicitly, its units are $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$.

The positive parameter $b$ represents <excluded volume> arising from the finite size and short-range repulsion of the <molecules>. The <volume> available for translational motion is reduced from $V$ to $V-b$. This is an effective <excluded volume>, not simply the sum of the <molecules>' geometric <volumes>. Its molar units are $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$.

The positive parameter $a$ measures intermolecular attraction in a <mean-field approximation>. Attractions reduce the <force> exerted on the walls, lowering the measured <pressure> by $a/V^2$ relative to the repulsion-only expression. Thus $p+a/V^2$ is the <pressure> corrected for this attraction. The squared inverse-volume dependence reflects a contribution proportional to the square of the molecular <number density>. The molar units of $a$ are $\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$.

For an arbitrary amount $n$ measured in moles, with total container <volume> $V_{\rm tot}$, the corresponding formula is
$$
\boxed{\left(p+\frac{an^2}{V_{\rm tot}^2}\right)(V_{\rm tot}-nb)=nRT.}
$$
At low <density>, when $b\ll V$ and $a/V^2\ll RT/V$, it reduces to the <ideal gas law>. The model captures qualitative attraction, repulsion and liquid-gas behavior, but does not supply an exact <equation of state> for every material.

The <third law of thermodynamics>, in its perfect-crystal form, states that the <entropy> of a pure, perfectly ordered <crystal> with a unique <ground state> tends to zero as $T\to0$. Its more general entropy-difference formulation says that the <entropy> difference between equilibrium states related by a finite change of a regular external control parameter tends to zero at <absolute zero>:
$$
\lim_{T\to0}\left[S(T,X_2)-S(T,X_1)\right]=0.
$$
The common limiting <entropy> is independent of $X$. Ground-state degeneracy or frozen disorder can produce <residual entropy>, so zero <entropy> is not an unconditional assertion about every sample.

In the usual regular thermodynamic setting, <absolute zero> cannot be attained from positive <temperature> by a finite sequence of cooling operations with finite resources. An entropy-temperature plot shows why a finite reversible adiabatic step cannot reach the common zero-temperature endpoint from a state with positive <entropy> above that endpoint: an <isentropic process> follows a horizontal line, while all the regular <entropy> curves meet only at $T=0$.

\Image[paper-2-a2-zero-temperature.png]
{title=Entropy curves and a cooling staircase approaching absolute zero}

The figure illustrates this with two possible settings having $S-S_0=a_1T$ and $S-S_0=a_2T$, where $0<a_1<a_2$. First change the setting isothermally from the upper to the lower curve, rejecting <heat>; then change it reversibly and adiabatically back to the upper curve. <Entropy> conservation on the latter step gives
$$
a_1T_n=a_2T_{n+1},\qquad T_{n+1}=\frac{a_1}{a_2}T_n,
\qquad T_n=T_0\left(\frac{a_1}{a_2}\right)^n>0
$$
for every finite $n$. The staircase approaches the origin but reaches it only after infinitely many such stages. Irreversibility generates <entropy> and cannot improve on the reversible endpoint.

This is a thermodynamic illustration of <unattainability of absolute zero>, not a claim that every cooling apparatus has this particular linear <entropy> law. Its conclusion presumes regular equilibrium states and bounded control parameters; dynamical statements about finite cooling time additionally require physical assumptions about the available interactions and cooling rates.

Assume a hollow, perfectly conducting <rectangular electromagnetic waveguide> filled with air approximated by vacuum. Let its wider side be $a=0.040\,\mathrm m$ and its narrower side $b=0.020\,\mathrm m$. The longitudinal propagation constant of a mode is
$$
k_z^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\left(\frac{m\pi}{a}\right)^2-\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^2,
$$
so the <waveguide cutoff frequency> is
$$
f_{c,mn}=\frac c2\sqrt{\left(\frac ma\right)^2+\left(\frac nb\right)^2}.
$$
For a mode with <transverse electric polarization>, one of $m,n$ may vanish, but they cannot both vanish. For a mode with <transverse magnetic polarization>, both must be positive. A single hollow conducting guide has no <transverse electromagnetic mode> with zero cutoff.

The lowest cutoff therefore belongs to the $\mathrm{TE}_{10}$ mode, varying across the wider side:
$$
\boxed{f_c=\frac{c}{2a}=\frac{2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{0.080\,\mathrm m}=3.75\times10^9\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq3.75\,\mathrm{GHz}.}
$$
Strictly, a traveling mode transporting <energy> down the guide requires $f>f_c$. At exactly $f_c$, $k_z=0$ and the axial <group velocity> vanishes. Below cutoff the field is evanescent. A different <dielectric> filling would replace $c$ by the <wave speed> in that material.

For a fixed <magnetic dipole moment> in an externally imposed uniform <magnetic field>, the <torque> is $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$. Its component in the direction of increasing $\theta$ is $-mB\sin\theta$. Since $\tau_\theta=-dU/d\theta$, integration gives
$$
\boxed{U(\theta)=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B=-mB\cos\theta,}
$$
where the arbitrary additive constant has been chosen so that $U=0$ at $\theta=\pi/2$.

\Image[paper-2-a4-dipole-energy.png]
{title=Potential energy of a permanent magnetic dipole in a uniform magnetic field}

The aligned orientation $\theta=0$ has the minimum $U=-mB$ and is stable against small rotations; the anti-aligned orientation $\theta=\pi$ has the maximum $U=+mB$ and is unstable. The graph repeats every $2\pi$. Rotating quasistatically from alignment to anti-alignment requires external work $\Delta U=2mB$.

For <charge sharing between distant connected spheres>, use the standard approximation that the spheres are sufficiently far apart for mutual electrostatic effects to be negligible, and that the wire adds negligible <capacitance>. The <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere> is $C=4\pi\epsilon_0r$. Thus
$$
C_1=5.56\,\mathrm{pF},\qquad C_2=11.1\,\mathrm{pF}=2C_1.
$$
The initial charges are $Q_i=C_iV_i$. Connecting the spheres allows charge to flow until their potentials are equal. <Charge conservation> gives
$$
V_f=\frac{C_1V_1+C_2V_2}{C_1+C_2}
=\frac{(2\,\mathrm{kV})+2(4\,\mathrm{kV})}{3}
=3.333\,\mathrm{kV}.
$$
The initial and final <electrostatic energies> are $U_i=\tfrac12C_1V_1^2+\tfrac12C_2V_2^2$ and $U_f=\tfrac12(C_1+C_2)V_f^2$. Their difference becomes <Joule heating> in the resistive wire:
$$
Q_{\rm heat}=U_i-U_f=\frac12\frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}(V_1-V_2)^2.
$$
Numerically,
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm heat}=\frac12\left(3.71\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm F\right)(2000\,\mathrm V)^2=7.42\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm J=7.42\,\mu\mathrm J.}
$$
The resistance determines how fast this happens, not the final <energy> difference. Sufficient damping suppresses sustained charge-current oscillations; the calculation also neglects <energy> radiated away. At finite sphere separation the <capacitance matrix> would be needed, and the radii and initial potentials alone would not specify an exact correction.

For <torque-free precession of a symmetric top>, take principal <moments of inertia> $I_1=I_2=I_\perp$ and $I_3=I_\parallel$, with no external <torque>. In an <inertial frame> moving with its <centre of mass>, its <angular momentum> $\mathbf L$ is constant in an <inertial frame>, and its <rotational kinetic energy> is conserved.

In the principal body axes, <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body> reduce to
$$
I_\perp\dot\omega_1+(I_\parallel-I_\perp)\omega_2\omega_3=0,\qquad
I_\perp\dot\omega_2-(I_\parallel-I_\perp)\omega_3\omega_1=0,\qquad
I_\parallel\dot\omega_3=0.
$$
Therefore $\omega_3$ is constant and
$$
\omega_1+i\omega_2=\omega_\perp e^{i(\Omega_bt+\alpha)},\qquad
\boxed{\Omega_b=\frac{I_\parallel-I_\perp}{I_\perp}\,\omega_3.}
$$
Thus the transverse <angular velocity> rotates uniformly in the body frame; its signed rate reverses between oblate and prolate inertia tensors. This body-frame wobble rate should be distinguished from the space-frame <precession> of the symmetry axis.

Let $\mathbf e_3$ be the body's symmetry axis. Since
$$
\mathbf L=I_\perp\boldsymbol\omega+(I_\parallel-I_\perp)\omega_3\mathbf e_3,
$$
its angle $\theta$ to $\mathbf L$ is constant, with $\cos\theta=I_\parallel\omega_3/L$, and
$$
\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3
=\frac{\mathbf L}{I_\perp}\times\mathbf e_3.
$$
Hence the symmetry axis describes a cone about fixed $\mathbf L$ at the space-frame <precession> rate
$$
\boxed{\dot\phi=\frac L{I_\perp}.}
$$
There is no <nutation> of this angle for torque-free motion of an exactly <symmetric top>. In the usual <Euler angles>, $\omega_3=\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta$, so $\dot\psi=-\Omega_b$. Both rates describe the same motion in different axes. A freely tossed axisymmetric disc illustrates the effect; a top supported at a point under gravity is a different, externally torqued problem.

<Lagrangian mechanics> expresses dynamics through <generalized coordinates> $q_i$ that respect the constraints. For a system with conservative, velocity-independent <forces> and standard <kinetic energy>, the <Lagrangian> is $L=T-V$. Its equations follow from the <principle of stationary action>:
$$
\mathcal S[q]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt,\qquad \delta\mathcal S=0
$$
for variations that vanish at the endpoints. Varying the <integral> and integrating the $\delta\dot q_i$ term by parts gives
$$
\delta\mathcal S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum_i\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}-\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\right]\delta q_i\,dt.
$$
Independent variations therefore yield the <Euler-Lagrange equations>:
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
Additional nonconservative <generalized forces> appear on the right-hand side as $Q_i$. The <action> is stationary, not necessarily a minimum. For ideal <holonomic constraints>, using suitable <generalized coordinates> removes the need to solve explicitly for constraint <forces>.

The <conjugate momentum> is $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$. If a coordinate is cyclic, $\partial L/\partial q_i=0$ and its <conjugate momentum> is conserved. If $L$ has no explicit time dependence, the <energy> function
$$
E=\sum_i\dot q_ip_i-L
$$
is conserved. It equals $T+V$ for the usual quadratic <kinetic energy> with time-independent geometry, but that identification is not universal for every <Lagrangian> or constraint.

For example, a <simple pendulum> with angle $\theta$, length $\ell$ and bob mass $m$ has
$$
L=\frac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\cos\theta,
\qquad \ddot\theta+\frac g\ell\sin\theta=0.
$$
The <tension> does no <virtual work> in the allowed angular displacement and never needs to be calculated to obtain the angular equation. The method scales naturally to coupled systems and coordinates with complicated geometry.

For a slender <elastic beam>, the <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> assumes small <strain>, small deflection slopes and cross-sections that remain plane and normal to the deflected centreline. A fibre at signed distance $y$ from the <neutral axis> has longitudinal <strain> $\epsilon_{xx}=-y\kappa$, where $\kappa$ is the centreline <curvature>. <Hooke's law> gives $\sigma_{xx}=-Ey\kappa$, with <Young's modulus> $E$.

Choose the internal bending-moment sign so that $M=-\int y\sigma_{xx}\,dA$. Then
$$
\boxed{M=EI_A\kappa,\qquad I_A=\int_Ay^2\,dA.}
$$
Here $I_A$ is the <second moment of area>, with units $\mathrm{m^4}$, not the mass <moment of inertia> of a rotating body. For a uniform material under pure bending, zero axial resultant places the <neutral axis> through the section's <centroid>.

For small slopes, $\kappa\simeq w''(x)$. Combining the moment-curvature relation with transverse <force> and moment balance gives, with a consistent load sign convention,
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\left(EI_A\frac{d^2w}{dx^2}\right)=q(x).}
$$
For constant stiffness this becomes $EI_Aw^{(4)}=q$. The <bending stiffness> $EI_A$ has units $\mathrm{N\,m^2}$. <Boundary conditions> specify displacement and slope at a clamped end, or <bending moment> and shear <force> at a free end.

For a cantilever of length $\ell$ carrying a transverse end load $F$, take deflection positive towards the load. The moment magnitude is $M(x)=F(\ell-x)$, and integration with $w(0)=w'(0)=0$ gives
$$
w(x)=\frac{Fx^2(3\ell-x)}{6EI_A},\qquad
\boxed{w(\ell)=\frac{F\ell^3}{3EI_A}.}
$$
The free-end moment vanishes and the tip shear supplies the applied load. For a rectangular section of width $b$ and bending thickness $h$, $I_A=bh^3/12$, so increasing thickness strongly increases bending resistance. The model ceases to be reliable for large deflections, plastic <strains> or short thick beams where shear deformation matters.

The <Magnus effect> is the transverse <force> produced when a spinning body moves relative to a <fluid>. Rotation changes the flow and <circulation> around the body; the resulting asymmetric <pressure> and wake deflect the <fluid>, with the opposite transverse <force> on the body. Examples include the curved flight of spinning balls and the lift of rotating-cylinder sails.

A useful quantitative model is steady two-dimensional <incompressible flow> past a cylinder of radius $a$, with <fluid> <velocity> $\mathbf U=U\hat{\mathbf x}$ far away. Let <circulation> $\Gamma$ be positive anticlockwise as viewed from $+z$. The potential-flow <velocity> at the cylinder surface is
$$
v_\theta(a,\theta)=-2U\sin\theta+\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi a}.
$$
The <Bernoulli equation> outside the <boundary layer> gives
$$
p(\theta)=p_\infty+\frac\rho2\left(U^2-v_\theta^2\right).
$$
Integrating the <pressure> <force> around the cylinder, per unit span, yields
$$
F_y'=-a\int_0^{2\pi}p(\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta=-\rho U\Gamma.
$$
Thus the <Kutta–Joukowski theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F'=\rho\mathbf U\times(\Gamma\hat{\mathbf z}),\qquad |\mathbf F'|=\rho U|\Gamma|.}
$$
The sign here refers to the <fluid> <velocity> relative to the cylinder. For a body moving through otherwise stationary <fluid>, that relative <fluid> <velocity> points opposite to the body's translational <velocity>.

Real <viscosity> and the <no-slip boundary condition> transmit the surface rotation to the <fluid> and help set the <circulation> and separation pattern. Pure inviscid flow with only an impermeability condition does not determine $\Gamma$ from the cylinder's <angular speed>. Therefore the <circulation> must not be assigned a universal spin-rate formula without additional assumptions. The cylinder result illustrates the lift mechanism; quantitative <forces> on a finite spinning ball also depend on its geometry, <Reynolds number>, rotation rate, surface roughness and wake.

= Solution

<Electromagnetic waves> drive mobile charges in a conducting medium. Their current changes both the phase and attenuation of the wave. A description in terms of a real, frequency-independent <refractive index> is therefore generally insufficient: the material response and the propagation constant are complex and frequency dependent.

Use <phasors> proportional to $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, with $k=k'+ik''$ and $k''\geq0$ for a passive medium. In a homogeneous, <isotropic>, linear material, let $\epsilon_b$ represent the <permittivity> associated with bound-charge <electric polarization>, $\mu$ the <magnetic permeability> and $\sigma(\omega)$ the mobile-charge conductivity. The macroscopic <Maxwell equations> give
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=i\omega\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=(\sigma-i\omega\epsilon_b)\mathbf E.
$$
For a transverse plane wave, taking another curl yields
$$
\boxed{k^2=\mu\epsilon_b\omega^2+i\mu\sigma(\omega)\omega
=\mu\omega^2\epsilon_{\rm eff}(\omega),\qquad
\epsilon_{\rm eff}=\epsilon_b+\frac{i\sigma(\omega)}\omega.}
$$
This division of the response avoids counting mobile <electrons> both in $\epsilon_b$ and in $\sigma$. Define the <complex refractive index> $\widetilde n=ck/\omega=n+i\kappa$. For a nonmagnetic medium, $\widetilde n^2=\epsilon_{\rm eff}/\epsilon_0$. Its real part determines the phase variation, while the positive <optical extinction coefficient> $\kappa$ gives amplitude attenuation $e^{-\omega\kappa z/c}$ and intensity attenuation $e^{-2\omega\kappa z/c}$. The amplitude penetration length is
$$
\delta=\frac1{k''}=\frac c{\omega\kappa}.
$$

A simple microscopic explanation comes from the <Drude model>. Let <electrons> of charge $-e$, mass $m$ and <number density> $n_e$ move against an effectively stationary positive background. Collisions relax their mean <velocity> at rate $\nu$:
$$
m(\dot{\mathbf v}+\nu\mathbf v)=-e\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf J=-n_ee\mathbf v.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\sigma(\omega)=\frac{n_ee^2}{m(\nu-i\omega)}
=\frac{\epsilon_0\omega_p^2}{\nu-i\omega},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{n_ee^2}{m\epsilon_0}.}
$$
With a vacuum-like bound-charge background, this gives
$$
\frac{\epsilon_{\rm eff}}{\epsilon_0}
=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega(\omega+i\nu)}.
$$
Its imaginary part is positive for positive $\omega,\nu$, consistent with dissipation under the chosen <phasor> convention. Real materials can require an effective <electron> mass, a nontrivial bound-charge response, or additional absorption associated with the <electronic band structure>.

A <plasma> is a conducting ionized medium with mobile <electrons> and <ions>, approximately neutral on large scales because of <plasma screening>. For the electron-frequency transverse waves considered here, the heavy <ions> can often be treated as stationary. In a dilute weakly collisional <plasma>, $\nu\ll\omega$, so
$$
\boxed{n^2=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2},\qquad
\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2.}
$$
For $\omega>\omega_p$, $k$ is real in this collisionless model and the wave propagates. Its <phase velocity> is $v_{\rm ph}=c/n>c$, while its <group velocity> is $v_g=cn<c$; their product is $c^2$. A phase crest exceeding $c$ does not imply superluminal information transport. For $\omega<\omega_p$, the refractive-index square is negative and
$$
k=i\frac{\sqrt{\omega_p^2-\omega^2}}c,
\qquad \delta=\frac c{\sqrt{\omega_p^2-\omega^2}}.
$$
The transmitted field is evanescent rather than a traveling bulk wave. A sufficiently thick collisionless <plasma> reflects such radiation without Ohmic absorption. Collisions make the response complex and permit absorption. This accounts for a <plasma> cutoff and, qualitatively, the reflection of some radio frequencies by ionized atmospheric layers. The simple model describes transverse waves; <plasmas> also support longitudinal collective modes.

In a <metal>, positive <ions> usually form a <crystal> lattice. Mobile conduction <electrons> occupy states described by the <electronic band structure> and normally form a degenerate <Fermi gas>. The conduction-electron <density> is usually much higher than in a dilute gas <plasma>. Thus its <plasma frequency> is commonly in the optical or <ultraviolet> range, rather than the lower radio-frequency values possible in dilute <plasmas>. <Electron> scattering from the lattice, defects and other excitations gives a finite relaxation rate. The distinction is not that every <plasma> is collisionless and every <metal> collisional: the controlling ratio for either material is $\omega/\nu$, together with its carrier <density> and bound-charge response.

At sufficiently low frequency, $\omega\ll\nu$, the <metal>'s conductivity is approximately real, $\sigma_0=n_ee^2/(m\nu)$. In a good conductor the conduction current dominates the displacement current, $\sigma_0\gg\omega\epsilon_b$. The wave equation then gives
$$
k^2\simeq i\mu\sigma_0\omega,\qquad
\boxed{k\simeq(1+i)\sqrt{\frac{\mu\sigma_0\omega}{2}}=\frac{1+i}{\delta},
\quad\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu\sigma_0\omega}}.}
$$
For example, a tangential electric field varies as
$$
E(z,t)=E_0e^{-z/\delta}\cos\left(\frac z\delta-\omega t\right).
$$
The current $\mathbf J=\sigma_0\mathbf E$ is therefore concentrated within a few skin depths of the surface: this is the <skin effect>. As frequency increases within this regime, $\delta\propto\omega^{-1/2}$. In a wire whose radius greatly exceeds $\delta$, only a thin outer region carries appreciable alternating current, increasing the effective resistance. In a conducting shield, the same penetration suppresses the field transmitted through a sufficiently thick wall.

The <Poynting theorem> identifies the loss as <Joule heating>. The cycle-averaged heating <density> for a complex conductivity is
$$
\langle\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\rangle=\frac12\operatorname{Re}\sigma\,|\mathbf E|^2.
$$
The <wave impedance> is $Z=\mu\omega/k$. For incidence from vacuum at normal incidence, the electric reflection amplitude is $r=(Z-Z_0)/(Z+Z_0)$, where $Z_0=\sqrt{\mu_0/\epsilon_0}$. A good conductor has small $|Z|$ and therefore strong reflection, while the part of the incident power entering it is dissipated over the <skin depth>.

At high enough frequency that $\omega\gg\nu$, a <metal>'s free-electron response also approaches the collisionless <plasma> form. The low-frequency formula $\delta\propto\omega^{-1/2}$ must not simply be extrapolated into that regime. Real optical behavior additionally depends on the <electronic band structure> and transitions between bands. Both <plasmas> and <metals> are therefore described by the same electromagnetic response framework, but their relevant densities, scattering rates and frequency regimes differ.

The relation describes the slope of the <phase coexistence curve> of a pure substance in the pressure-temperature plane:
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{L}{T\Delta V}.}
$$
Here $p(T)$ is the common equilibrium <pressure> of the two phases, $T$ is their common absolute <temperature> in kelvins, and the <derivative> is taken along their coexistence curve. Choose a direction of transformation from phase 1 to phase 2. Then $L$ is the reversible <latent heat> for that transformation and $\Delta V=V_2-V_1$ is the corresponding <volume> change, using the same <amount of substance> for both quantities.

In the molar convention used below, $L=h_{m,2}-h_{m,1}$ has units $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta V=v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}$ has units $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$, so the right-hand side has units $\mathrm{Pa\,K^{-1}}$. For vaporization, both $L$ and $\Delta V$ are positive. The displayed general relation is also called the <Clapeyron equation>; the ideal-vapor approximation used in (c) gives its usual Clausius form.

= Solution
{parent=b}

At coexistence, the two phases of a pure substance have equal <chemical potential>, or equivalently equal molar <Gibbs free energy>:
$$
\mu_1(T,p)=\mu_2(T,p).
$$
For either phase, $d\mu=-s_m\,dT+v_m\,dp$. Move an infinitesimal distance along the coexistence curve, where the equality must remain true. Subtracting the two differentials gives
$$
0=-\left(s_{m,2}-s_{m,1}\right)dT+\left(v_{m,2}-v_{m,1}\right)dp,
\qquad \frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta s_m}{\Delta v_m}.
$$
A reversible transformation at this common <temperature> absorbs <latent heat> $L=T\Delta s_m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta s_m}{\Delta v_m}=\frac{L}{T\Delta v_m}.}
$$
The derivation assumes <thermodynamic equilibrium> between two bulk phases of a single pure substance, with the same <temperature> and <pressure>, and only ordinary pressure-volume work. Surface-curvature shifts, additional external-field work and composition changes are omitted. It applies to a first-order <phase boundary> where the <latent heat> and <volume> discontinuity are defined. No ideal-gas assumption and no constant-$L$ assumption are needed for this differential relation. At the critical endpoint both discontinuities vanish, so their ratio requires a limiting treatment rather than direct substitution of two zeros.

= Solution

For liquid-vapor equilibrium, use molar quantities. At low vapor <pressure> the gas is dilute, approximately ideal, and has a much larger <molar volume> than the liquid. Hence
$$
\Delta v_m=v_{m,g}-v_{m,l}\simeq v_{m,g}\simeq\frac{RT}{p}.
$$
Substitution into the <Clapeyron equation> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}\simeq\frac{Lp}{RT^2},\qquad
\frac{d\ln p}{dT}\simeq\frac{L}{RT^2}.
$$
If the molar <latent heat> varies sufficiently slowly to be treated as constant over the <temperature> interval, integration gives
$$
\ln p=\text{constant}-\frac{L}{RT},\qquad
\boxed{p=p_0\exp\left(-\frac{L}{RT}\right).}
$$
The positive constant $p_0$ has <pressure> units and is an integration constant. It is not necessarily a real infinite-temperature <pressure>, since the approximations fail before that limit.

For <mercury (element)> between $300$ and $500\,\mathrm K$, the original plot places this range above the <triple point> and below the normal boiling and critical <temperatures>. Its vapor <pressure> is low relative to the critical <pressure>, making a dilute-gas description reasonable; the vapor's <molar volume> greatly exceeds that of the dense liquid. The interval is also far from the critical region where $L$ tends to zero, so a roughly constant <latent heat> is a useful first approximation. The approximations are not exact: <Kirchhoff's law of thermochemistry> gives, at fixed <pressure>, the <temperature> <derivative> of a transformation enthalpy as the difference between the two molar <heat capacities>, so a precise fit over a wide interval would allow $L$ to vary.

Taking a <derivative> of the logarithmic form gives a direct way to use the plot's base-ten vertical axis:
$$
\boxed{L\simeq R\ln10\,T^2\frac{d\log_{10}(p/\mathrm{Pa})}{dT}.}

Alternatively, use two nearby readings and eliminate the unknown $p_0$:

L\simeq R\ln10\,

$$
Reading the original PDF graph approximately gives $\log_{10}(p/\mathrm{Pa})\simeq-0.6$ at $300\,\mathrm K$ and $\simeq3.8$ at $500\,\mathrm K$. These bracket the requested <temperature> and give an average <latent heat> near $400\,\mathrm K$:

L\simeq(8.31\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}})(2.3026)
\frac{4.4}{1/300-1/500}\,\mathrm K
\simeq6.3\times10^4\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}.

Thus a suitable graph-based estimate is

The graph does not warrant several significant figures; a local tangent or different reasonable nearby readings give a comparable estimate. The factor $\ln10$ is essential when using a base-ten logarithmic axis.

The <phase diagram> must contain three coexistence curves meeting at the <triple point>: a solid-gas sublimation curve, a solid-liquid melting curve and a liquid-gas vaporization curve. The vaporization curve ends at a <liquid-gas critical point>; beyond it liquid and gas are continuously connected as a <supercritical fluid>.

\Image[paper-2-c8-mercury-phases.png]
{title=Schematic pressure-temperature phase diagram of mercury with positive melting-line slope}

The imposed contraction on freezing means $v_{m,s}<v_{m,l}$. Melting therefore has $\Delta v_m=v_{m,l}-v_{m,s}>0$ and $L_{\rm fusion}>0$. The <Clapeyron equation> then requires
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{dp}{dT}\right)_{\rm melting}=\frac{L_{\rm fusion}}{T(v_{m,l}-v_{m,s})}>0.}
$$
Thus raising the <pressure> raises the melting <temperature>. The liquid-gas and solid-gas slopes are also positive because the transformations into gas increase both <entropy> and <volume>. The liquid region lies between the positively sloping melting line and the vaporization curve, while gas occupies the low-pressure side. The figure is a qualitative solution sketch, not a reproduction or fit of the paper's vapor-pressure measurements; its axes are schematic.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The expression is the <canonical partition function> for a system in <thermal equilibrium> with a large <heat bath> at fixed <temperature> $T$. The system exchanges <energy> with the bath, while its particle number and external mechanical parameters, such as <volume>, are fixed. The bath is large enough that its <temperature> is not appreciably changed by this exchange.

The system has allowed <energy levels> $\epsilon_j$. The index $j$ labels distinct <energy> values, and the integer <degeneracy factor> $g_j$ counts distinct <quantum states> with that same <energy>. $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, and $\exp[-\epsilon_j/(k_BT)]$ is a state's <Boltzmann factor>. The dimensionless sum $Z$ normalizes the probabilities:
$$
\boxed{P(\text{one state at }\epsilon_j)=\frac{e^{-\epsilon_j/(k_BT)}}Z,\qquad
P(\text{level }j)=\frac{g_je^{-\epsilon_j/(k_BT)}}Z.}
$$
Thus the degeneracy multiplies the probability of occupying the level, not the probability assigned to each individual state within it.

The canonical model is not restricted to a classical <ideal gas>. It applies to a quantum rotor, for example, provided the sum counts all of that system's allowed states with their proper degeneracies. For a many-particle system, $\epsilon_j$ would be the <energy> of a many-particle state, with any quantum-statistical restrictions already built into the state count.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <internal energy> is the canonical mean of the <energy>:
$$
U_{\rm sys}=\sum_j\epsilon_j\,\frac{g_je^{-\beta\epsilon_j}}Z.
$$
Holding the external mechanical parameters fixed, so that the spectrum and degeneracies do not change when differentiating with respect to $\beta$, gives
$$
\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}=-\sum_jg_j\epsilon_je^{-\beta\epsilon_j}.
$$
Comparison yields
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm sys}=-\frac1Z\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}=-\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta},\qquad \beta=\frac1{k_BT}.}
$$
An explicitly temperature-dependent effective Hamiltonian would require care: differentiating its partition sum would also differentiate the assigned <energy levels>. The stated result uses the fixed physical spectrum of the canonical system.

For the supplied <linear rigid rotor> spectrum, sum over every nonnegative integer $J$ and include all $2J+1$ magnetic substates:
$$
\boxed{Z_{\rm rot}=\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}(2J+1)
\exp\left[-\frac{\hbar^2J(J+1)}{2Ik_BT}\right].}
$$
It is convenient to introduce the <rotational temperature>
$$
\theta_r=\frac{\hbar^2}{2Ik_B},
$$
so that the first terms are $Z_{\rm rot}=1+3e^{-2\theta_r/T}+5e^{-6\theta_r/T}+\cdots$. Here $I$ is the <moment of inertia> and $\hbar$ is the <reduced Planck constant>, as confirmed in the original PDF. The stipulated degeneracies are used as given, without adding nuclear-spin weights or omitting selected $J$ values.

= Solution
{parent=d}

At low <temperature>, $T\ll\theta_r$, retain the ground level and the first excited level. Their <energies> are $0$ and $\hbar^2/I$, with degeneracies $1$ and $3$. Therefore
$$
Z_{\rm rot}=1+3e^{-\hbar^2/(Ik_BT)}+O\left(e^{-3\hbar^2/(Ik_BT)}\right),
$$
so in the notation of the question,
$$
\boxed{A=3,\qquad B=\frac{\hbar^2}{Ik_B}=2\theta_r.}
$$
$A$ is dimensionless and $B$ has units of kelvins. The first omitted level has three times the first excitation <energy>, explaining the exponent in the error term.

At high <temperature>, $T\gg\theta_r$, many rotational levels are occupied and the sum can be replaced to leading order by an <integral>:
$$
Z_{\rm rot}\simeq\int_0^\infty(2J+1)e^{-(\theta_r/T)J(J+1)}\,dJ.
$$
Set $u=J(J+1)$, so that $du=(2J+1)dJ$. Then
$$
Z_{\rm rot}\simeq\int_0^\infty e^{-(\theta_r/T)u}\,du=\frac T{\theta_r}
=\frac{2Ik_B}{\hbar^2}T.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{2Ik_B}{\hbar^2}=\frac1{\theta_r},\qquad [C]=\mathrm{K^{-1}}.}
$$
The high-temperature approximation here concerns the rotational spectrum. For a real <molecule> it should still be used below <temperatures> where neglected vibrational excitation or dissociation invalidates the rigid-rotor model.

= Solution

Using $Z_{\rm rot}\simeq CT=C/(k_B\beta)$ gives
$$
\ln Z_{\rm rot}=\ln\left(\frac{C}{k_B\beta}\right).
$$
The internal-energy identity from (b), followed by <differentiation> with respect to <temperature> at fixed <moment of inertia>, yields
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm rot}\simeq\frac1\beta=k_BT,\qquad C_{V,\rm rot}=\left(\frac{\partial U_{\rm rot}}{\partial T}\right)_I\simeq k_B.}
$$
For one mole of independent rotors, the rotational <energy> is $RT$ and the rotational molar <heat capacity> is $R\simeq8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

This agrees with the <equipartition theorem>. The given spectrum describes a linear rotor with two independent rotational <degrees of freedom>, each contributing $k_BT/2$ to the high-temperature mean <energy>. Rotation about the molecular axis is not an additional <degree of freedom> of this ideal linear-rotor model, so the result is $k_B$ rather than $3k_B/2$ per rotor. These are rotational contributions alone; translational and vibrational <heat capacities> have not been included. At low <temperature> the discrete excitation gap freezes rotational excitation out, so the rotational <heat capacity> tends to zero instead of remaining at its classical value.
