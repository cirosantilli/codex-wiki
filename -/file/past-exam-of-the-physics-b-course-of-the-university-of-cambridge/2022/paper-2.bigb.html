= Solution
{parent=A1}

Interpret the applied <pressure> as a uniform increase of external <hydrostatic pressure> on all six faces, and approximate the rock by an <isotropic> material obeying <linear elasticity>. Taking tensile <stress> as positive, $\sigma_x=\sigma_y=\sigma_z=-p$. By <Hooke's law>, the <strain> along any one axis is
$$
\epsilon_x=\frac{\sigma_x-\nu(\sigma_y+\sigma_z)}{E}=-\frac{(1-2\nu)p}{E}.
$$
To first order in the small <strain>, the fractional <volume> change is the sum of the three axial <strains>:
$$
\frac{\Delta V}{V}=\epsilon_x+\epsilon_y+\epsilon_z=-\frac{3(1-2\nu)p}{E}=-\frac pK,
\qquad K=\frac{E}{3(1-2\nu)}.
$$
Thus the <bulk modulus> is $K=40\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Using the specified <Young's modulus>, <Poisson's ratio>, $V=1\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $p=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta V=-2.53\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}=-2.53\,\mathrm{cm^3}.}
$$
The sign indicates contraction. This is the change relative to the unloaded reference state; simply maintaining a specimen already equilibrated at this same <pressure> would produce no further change.

Use the pipe-diameter convention for the <Reynolds number>, with mean <velocity> $\bar v$, <density> $\rho$ and <dynamic viscosity> $\mu$:
$$
\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho\bar vD}{\mu},\qquad
D_{\rm crit}=\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\rm crit}\mu}{\rho\bar v}.
$$
The <Reynolds number> compares inertial and viscous effects in the <fluid flow>. A conventional estimate for the onset of <turbulence> in a pipe is $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm crit}\simeq2000$, giving
$$
D_{\rm crit}=\frac{2000(3.16\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s})}{(789\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}})(0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})}=1.60\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Hence the usual onset estimate is $\boxed{D\gtrsim1.6\,\mathrm{mm}}$. Using the also common transition criterion $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm crit}=2300$ gives $1.84\,\mathrm{mm}$, whereas $\mathrm{Re}\gtrsim4000$, used for established <turbulence>, gives $D\gtrsim3.2\,\mathrm{mm}$. There is no universal sharp minimum: inlet disturbances and pipe conditions affect the transition from <laminar flow>, so the threshold convention must accompany the answer.

**(a)** Treat the lake as a <heat bath> at fixed <temperature> $T_0=288.15\,\mathrm K$ and the block's <heat capacity> as constant. Its initial <temperature> is $T_i=473.15\,\mathrm K$, and $C=mc=355\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Although the cooling is irreversible, <entropy> is a <state function>, so evaluate the block's change along an imagined <reversible thermodynamic process>:
$$
\Delta S_{\rm block}=\int_{T_i}^{T_0}\frac{C\,dT}{T}=C\ln\frac{T_0}{T_i}=-176.1\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
The lake receives <heat> $Q=C(T_i-T_0)=65675\,\mathrm J$, and therefore
$$
\Delta S_{\rm lake}=\frac{Q}{T_0}=227.9\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
$$
Adding the two contributions gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm Universe}=C\left[\ln\frac{T_0}{T_i}+\frac{T_i-T_0}{T_0}\right]=51.9\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
The positive result is consistent with the <second law of thermodynamics> for <heat transfer> across a finite <temperature> difference. The calculation uses $c=710\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ exactly as printed in both the original PDF and the transcription; it does not replace the supplied value by a tabulated value for lead.

**(b)** The block starts and eventually finishes at the lake's <temperature>, so its net <entropy> change is approximately zero. Neglecting buoyancy, air resistance and the final immersion depth, the lost <gravitational potential energy> $mgh$ becomes <internal energy> in the lake after the motion and waves have dissipated. The lake remains at $T_0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm Universe}\simeq\frac{mgh}{T_0}=\frac{(0.5\,\mathrm{kg})(9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})(200\,\mathrm m)}{288.15\,\mathrm K}=3.40\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
Temporary heating of the block or temporary storage in <kinetic energy> does not change this final <energy> and <entropy> balance once <thermal equilibrium> is restored.

For a fixed amount of a <simple compressible system>, write the <internal energy> as $U(S,V)$. Its exact <differential> is
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV,\qquad T=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_V,\qquad -p=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_S.
$$
Assuming the <thermodynamic potential> has continuous second <partial derivatives>, equality of its <mixed partial derivatives> yields the first <Maxwell relation>:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S=\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial V\partial S}=-\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial S}\right)_V.}
$$
For the second <Maxwell relation>, use the <Legendre transform> $H=U+pV$, the <enthalpy>. Its natural variables are $S,p$ since
$$
dH=dU+p\,dV+V\,dp=T\,dS+V\,dp.
$$
Thus $T=(\partial H/\partial S)_p$ and $V=(\partial H/\partial p)_S$. Equality of the <mixed partial derivatives> now gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_S=\frac{\partial^2H}{\partial p\partial S}=\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_p.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=B6}

<Newton's second law> takes its familiar form $m\mathbf a=\mathbf F$ in an <inertial frame>. Axes fixed to a turning apparatus or to the <Earth> form a <rotating reference frame>. Extra <inertial forces> account for the <acceleration> of these coordinates. Their values depend on the chosen <reference frame>; physical interactions such as <gravity>, <tension> and contact <forces> must still be included separately.

Let the rotating origin have inertial <acceleration> $\mathbf A$, and let its axes have <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega(t)$. For any <vector> $\mathbf q$, the <rotating-frame derivative formula> is
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf q}{dt}\right)_{\rm I}=\left(\frac{d\mathbf q}{dt}\right)_{\rm R}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf q.
$$
Each rotating <basis vector> changes by $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf e_i$, which proves this rule by expanding $\mathbf q$ in that basis. Apply it twice to the position relative to the moving origin. With $\mathbf v'$ and $\mathbf a'$ measured in the rotating axes,
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm I}=\mathbf A+\mathbf a'+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'+\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
Consequently the equation in the <rotating reference frame> is
$$
m\mathbf a'=\mathbf F-m\mathbf A-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'-m\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
The additional terms are the translational <inertial force>, <Coriolis force>, <Euler force> and <centrifugal force>, respectively. The last two simplify when the <angular velocity> is constant: the <Euler force> vanishes, while the other rotation terms remain.

The <centrifugal force> points away from the rotation axis. If $\mathbf r_\perp$ is the perpendicular displacement from that axis, then
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm cf}=m\Omega^2\mathbf r_\perp.}
$$
For uniform rotation it has <potential energy>
$$
\Phi_{\rm cf}=-\frac12m|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2,\qquad \mathbf F_{\rm cf}=-\nabla\Phi_{\rm cf}.
$$
A mass at rest relative to a turntable can therefore be described as having an inward <tension> or <friction> balancing its outward <centrifugal force>. In an <inertial frame>, the inward physical <force> instead produces the required <centripetal acceleration>. These are two descriptions of the same motion; one must not insert the rotating-frame outward term into the inertial equation as an additional physical interaction.

A rotating liquid gives a useful quantitative example. For a liquid in <hydrostatic equilibrium> relative to a vessel rotating uniformly about the vertical axis,
$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial s}=\rho\Omega^2s,\qquad \frac{\partial p}{\partial z}=-\rho g.
$$
Along the <free surface> the <pressure> is constant, so $\Omega^2s\,ds-g\,dz=0$ and
$$
z(s)=z(0)+\frac{\Omega^2s^2}{2g}.
$$
The surface is a <elliptic paraboloid>. This is the balance of <gravity> and the <centrifugal force> in the rotating description. On the <Earth>, the same outward <acceleration> reduces effective <gravity>, most strongly at the equator, and contributes to the planet's equatorial bulge.

The <Coriolis force> depends on relative motion:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm cor}=-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'.}
$$
It is perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol\Omega$ and $\mathbf v'$, vanishes for motion along the rotation axis, and reverses when the relative <velocity> reverses. The <dot product> gives
$$
\mathbf F_{\rm cor}\cdot\mathbf v'=0.
$$
Thus the <Coriolis force> does no <work> in the <rotating reference frame>: it changes the direction of motion rather than directly changing the rotating-frame <kinetic energy>. For example, on a horizontal turntable rotating anticlockwise as viewed from above, an outward-moving slider has a clockwise <Coriolis acceleration>, whereas an inward-moving slider has an anticlockwise one. The deflection can be observed by comparing a laboratory camera with a camera rotating with the table.

For slow horizontal motion on the <Earth> at latitude $\lambda$, keep the vertical component $\Omega_E\sin\lambda$ of its <angular velocity>. In east-north coordinates $(u,v)$ the horizontal <Coriolis acceleration> is
$$
(a_E,a_N)=(fv,-fu),\qquad f=2\Omega_E\sin\lambda.
$$
It deflects motion to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere. In large-scale atmospheric and oceanic motion it can balance a horizontal <pressure gradient>, giving <geostrophic balance>. With $\hat{\mathbf z}$ upward, the steady horizontal equation without <friction> is
$$
f\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf v=-\frac1\rho\nabla_h p.
$$
The resulting <velocity> runs along lines of equal <pressure>. This approximation requires rotation to dominate local inertial changes; it is inappropriate close to the equator where $f$ tends to zero.

The <Foucault pendulum> gives another terrestrial example. In the <small-angle approximation>, its horizontal coordinates satisfy
$$
\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=f\dot y,\qquad \ddot y+\omega_0^2y=-f\dot x,\qquad \omega_0^2=\frac g\ell.
$$
To first order in the small ratio $\Omega_E/\omega_0$, the oscillation plane precesses relative to the ground at angular rate $-\Omega_E\sin\lambda$: clockwise in the northern hemisphere, zero at the equator, and one revolution per sidereal day at a pole. The <Coriolis force> explains this changing orientation without requiring <energy> input. Together these examples show why <inertial forces> are useful whenever the apparatus or environment supplies naturally rotating coordinates.

The <method of images> solves certain <electrostatics> problems by replacing a conductor by fictitious <electric charges> outside the region where the solution is required. The replacement must satisfy the actual boundary values of the <electric potential> as well as the prescribed sources. The <uniqueness of Poisson equation> then establishes that the constructed <electric potential> equals the physical one in that region. The image locations are mathematical devices; they are not real charges inside the metal.

For a <point charge> $q$ at $(0,0,a)$ above an infinite grounded conducting plane $z=0$, put an image charge $-q$ at $(0,0,-a)$. In a <cylindrical coordinate system> with coordinates $(s,z)$ the potential in the physical region is
$$
\phi(s,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{s^2+(z-a)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{s^2+(z+a)^2}}\right],\qquad z>0.
$$
This vanishes at $z=0$, has the required singularity at the real charge, and tends to zero at infinity. With <electric field> $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$, the induced <surface charge density> follows from the normal field immediately above the conductor:
$$
\sigma(s)=\epsilon_0E_z(s,0^+)=-\frac{qa}{2\pi(s^2+a^2)^{3/2}},\qquad
\int_0^\infty\sigma(s)\,2\pi s\,ds=-q.
$$
The <force> on $q$ is the field of the image evaluated at the real charge:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0a^2}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The <electrostatic energy> relative to infinite separation is $U=\tfrac12q\phi_{\rm image}=-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0a)$. The factor $1/2$ accounts for the fact that the induced charge changes as the real charge is assembled; treating the image as an independently fixed physical charge would give the wrong <energy>.

A second standard geometry is a grounded conducting sphere of radius $b$ with a charge $q$ a distance $d>b$ from its centre. Its exterior field is generated by an image $q'=-qb/d$ at distance $b^2/d$ from the centre on the line towards the real charge. On the sphere the distances to the real and image charges are in the ratio $d/b$, making their potentials cancel. For an isolated neutral sphere, an additional central image $+qb/d$ makes the total induced charge zero and leaves the surface an <equipotential>. The construction is powerful for suitable symmetric boundaries, but arbitrary boundaries do not in general admit a finite image system.

= Solution
{parent=b}

An <electrical insulator> has no freely conducting bulk charge in the ideal static description, but an applied <electric field> can displace bound positive and negative charge relative to one another. The resulting <electric dipole moments> per unit <volume> define the <polarization density> $\mathbf P$. Distortion of electronic charge clouds, relative displacement of <ions>, and partial alignment of permanent molecular <electric dipoles> can all contribute. Thermal agitation opposes the last mechanism.

For a weak field in a linear <isotropic> <dielectric>, the constitutive law is
$$
\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad
\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r.
$$
Here $\chi_e$ is the <electric susceptibility>, $\epsilon$ is the <permittivity>, and $\mathbf D$ is the <electric displacement field>. In an <anisotropic> material, $\chi_e$ is a <tensor> and $\mathbf P$ need not be parallel to $\mathbf E$. These response laws describe the macroscopic field, not necessarily the microscopic field acting on one <molecule>.

Spatial variation and surface termination of the <polarization density> give <bound charge and bound current> through the bound charge densities
$$
\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\qquad \sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n},
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is outward from the material. Thus uniform <polarization density> has no bulk bound charge, but generally has opposite bound charges on opposing surfaces. <Gauss's law> can be rewritten as
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_{\rm free}.
$$
With no <free surface> charge, the normal component of $\mathbf D$ is continuous across an interface; the tangential <electric field> is continuous in <electrostatics>.

For example, fill a <parallel-plate capacitor> of plate area $A$ and gap $d$ with a uniform <dielectric>, neglecting fringing. At fixed free plate charge $Q$, $D=Q/A$, so
$$
E=\frac{Q}{\epsilon A},\qquad V=Ed,\qquad
\boxed{C=\frac{\epsilon A}{d}=\epsilon_rC_0.}
$$
The induced bound surface charges oppose the original field, reducing the <electric field> and potential difference at fixed $Q$. At fixed applied potential difference, the field remains $V/d$ and the battery instead supplies more free charge. The <electric displacement field> separates these free-charge and bound-charge roles.

= Solution

Because the <magnetic field> obeys $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$, it can be represented locally as the <curl> of a <magnetic vector potential>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.}
$$
This automatically enforces the absence of magnetic monopoles. It is also a global representation throughout ordinary all-space problems without monopole singularities. It is not unique: the <gauge transformation>
$$
\mathbf A\longmapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi
$$
leaves $\mathbf B$ unchanged because the <curl> of a <gradient> vanishes. In a time-dependent problem,
$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t},\qquad
\phi\longmapsto\phi-\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial t}
$$
makes the same transformation leave both physical fields unchanged and automatically satisfies <Faraday's law>.

In vacuum <magnetostatics>, choose the <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Substitution into <Ampère's law> and the identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A$ gives
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J,\qquad
\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'
$$
for a localized steady <electric current density> and a potential vanishing at infinity. Taking the <curl> recovers the <Biot-Savart law>. The <magnetic vector potential> converts a constrained <vector> field problem into componentwise <Poisson equations> once a gauge is chosen.

For a uniform <magnetic field>, $\mathbf A=\tfrac12\mathbf B\times\mathbf r$ is a convenient choice. For a localized <magnetic dipole moment> $\mathbf m$, the leading far-field potential is
$$
\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\mathbf m\times\mathbf r}{r^3}.
$$
Another useful relation is <Stokes theorem>, which relates a closed-path <integral> to the <magnetic flux> through a spanning surface:
$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$
The units of $\mathbf A$ are $\mathrm{T\,m}$, equivalently $\mathrm{Wb\,m^{-1}}$.

= Solution

An incident <electromagnetic wave> generally produces reflected and transmitted waves because the two media have different electromagnetic response. Consider a flat interface between homogeneous, linear, lossless media with <permittivities> $\epsilon_j$ and <magnetic permeabilities> $\mu_j$. Their wave speeds and <wave impedances> are
$$
v_j=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_j\epsilon_j}},\qquad Z_j=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_j}{\epsilon_j}}.
$$
For each <plane electromagnetic wave>, $\mathbf H=\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E/Z_j$. With no <free surface> charge or current, the <interface conditions for electromagnetic fields> require continuous tangential $\mathbf E,\mathbf H$ and normal $\mathbf D,\mathbf B$. Equality of the <phase> at every point on the boundary gives equal <angular frequencies> and tangential <wavevectors>. Hence the reflection <angle> equals the incidence <angle> and <Snell's law> is
$$
n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t,
$$
where $n_j=c/v_j$ and each <angle> is measured from the normal.

At normal incidence use the same tangential axis for all electric <amplitudes>. The two independent boundary equations are
$$
E_i+E_r=E_t,\qquad \frac{E_i-E_r}{Z_1}=\frac{E_t}{Z_2}.
$$
Solving gives the <amplitude> <Fresnel equations>
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{E_r}{E_i}=\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1},\qquad t=\frac{E_t}{E_i}=\frac{2Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}.}
$$
For nonmagnetic media, these become $r=(n_1-n_2)/(n_1+n_2)$ and $t=2n_1/(n_1+n_2)$. Reflection into a medium of larger <refractive index> therefore reverses the electric <amplitude>'s sign at normal incidence.

The normal <Poynting vector> determines the reflected and transmitted fractions of incident <power>. At normal incidence,
$$
\mathcal R=|r|^2,\qquad \mathcal T=\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}|t|^2,\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1.}
$$
It is the <power> fractions, rather than the squared electric <amplitudes> alone, that add to unity.

At oblique incidence separate <transverse electric polarization> ($s$, <electric field> perpendicular to the incidence plane) from <transverse magnetic polarization> ($p$, <electric field> in that plane). For nonmagnetic media, writing $c_i=\cos\theta_i$ and $c_t=\cos\theta_t$, the <Fresnel equations> are
$$
r_s=\frac{n_1c_i-n_2c_t}{n_1c_i+n_2c_t},\qquad
t_s=\frac{2n_1c_i}{n_1c_i+n_2c_t},
$$
$$
r_p=\frac{n_2c_i-n_1c_t}{n_2c_i+n_1c_t},\qquad
t_p=\frac{2n_1c_i}{n_2c_i+n_1c_t}.
$$
Here choose a common $s$ direction and, for each ray, the $p$ direction as $\hat{\mathbf s}\times\hat{\mathbf k}$. This makes the reflected $p$ basis reverse its tangential direction at normal incidence, explaining why the displayed $r_p$ then has the opposite sign to $r_s$. Physical <reflectance> is independent of this sign convention. For either polarization with propagating transmission,
$$
\mathcal R=|r|^2,\qquad \mathcal T=\frac{n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta_i}|t|^2.
$$
For $p$ polarization, zero reflection occurs at the <Brewster angle> $\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1$. If $n_1>n_2$, incidence above the <critical angle for total internal reflection> $\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1$ produces an <evanescent field in total internal reflection> and $\mathcal R=1$ for an isolated lossless interface. A lossy second medium requires complex response coefficients and an absorption term in the <energy> balance.

Use an <ideal gas> to illustrate the <Carnot cycle>, with hot and cold <heat baths> at $T_h=T_2$ and $T_c=T_1$. The four processes are all <reversible thermodynamic processes>. Lettered states below belong only to this illustration; the engine later in the question uses numbered states.

\Image[paper-2-c8-carnot-pv.png]
{title=An ideal-gas Carnot cycle: two isotherms joined by two reversible adiabats}

Starting at $A$, perform the following four stages:

* $A\to B$: <isothermal expansion> at $T_h$. The gas absorbs <heat> $Q_h$ from the hot <heat bath> and does <work>. Its <internal energy> is unchanged, so $Q_h=W_{AB}=nRT_h\ln(V_B/V_A)$.
* $B\to C$: <reversible ideal-gas adiabat> during expansion. Insulation prevents <heat transfer>; <work> done by the gas reduces its <internal energy> and lowers its <temperature> from $T_h$ to $T_c$.
* $C\to D$: <isothermal compression> at $T_c$. <Work> done on the gas is rejected as <heat> $Q_c$ to the cold <heat bath>, with $Q_c=nRT_c\ln(V_C/V_D)>0$.
* $D\to A$: insulated <adiabatic compression>, performed reversibly, raises the <temperature> back to $T_h$ and closes the cycle.

The clockwise enclosed area in the <pressure>-<volume> diagram is the positive net <work> per cycle, $W=\oint p\,dV=Q_h-Q_c$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Because the working substance returns to its original <thermodynamic state>, its net <entropy> change is zero. There is no <entropy> change on either <reversible ideal-gas adiabat>; on each <isothermal process> the <entropy> transfer is the exchanged <heat> divided by the bath's absolute <temperature>. Hence
$$
\frac{Q_h}{T_h}-\frac{Q_c}{T_c}=0,\qquad \frac{Q_c}{Q_h}=\frac{T_c}{T_h}.
$$
The <first law of thermodynamics> over a complete cycle gives $W=Q_h-Q_c$. The <thermal efficiency> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm Carnot}=\frac{W}{Q_h}=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_2}.}
$$
The use of kelvins is essential. This result does not depend on the working substance. For the <ideal gas> illustration, the two <reversible ideal-gas adiabats> imply $V_C/V_B=V_D/V_A=(T_h/T_c)^{1/(\gamma-1)}$, so $\ln(V_B/V_A)=\ln(V_C/V_D)$; the explicit <heat> expressions in (a) give the same ratio.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The complete cycle must be a <reversible thermodynamic process>: no <friction>, viscous dissipation, unrestrained expansion, or other source of <entropy>. Each moving boundary must be arbitrarily close to <mechanical equilibrium>. <Heat transfer> to or from a bath must occur across an infinitesimal <temperature> difference, and the insulated stages must be genuinely <adiabatic processes>. There must also be no direct <heat> leak between the <heat baths>.

For an engine exchanging <heat> only with these two <heat baths>, the <second law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
S_{\rm gen}=\frac{Q_c}{T_c}-\frac{Q_h}{T_h}\geq0,
$$
and thus
$$
\eta=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}-\frac{T_cS_{\rm gen}}{Q_h}\leq\eta_{\rm Carnot}.
$$
Practical engines generate <entropy> through finite <temperature> differences, <friction> and other dissipative effects. They therefore fall below the <Carnot cycle> limit. Approaching reversible <heat> exchange generally requires vanishingly slow operation for a finite heat-transfer apparatus, limiting useful <power>.

{parent=C8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For the numbered engine, denote the low and high <pressures> by $p_1=p_4=p_l$ and $p_2=p_3=p_h$. Let $R_g$ denote the <molar gas constant>. The <ideal gas law> makes the constant-pressure branches straight rays in a <temperature>-<volume> diagram:
$$
T=\frac{p_h}{nR_g}V\quad(2\to3),\qquad
T=\frac{p_l}{nR_g}V\quad(4\to1).
$$
The high-pressure ray is steeper. For a constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma$, each <reversible ideal-gas adiabat> obeys
$$
TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}.
$$
Consequently $1\to2$ curves upward and left during compression; $2\to3$ goes upward and right along the steeper ray; $3\to4$ curves downward and right during expansion; and $4\to1$ goes downward and left along the shallower ray.

= Solution
{parent=e}

This is the <Brayton cycle>. Assume fixed gas amount, constant <heat capacities>, and internally reversible <adiabatic processes>, as intended by the ideal engine model. Put $r_p=p_h/p_l=p_2/p_1>1$ and
$$
a=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma},\qquad k=r_p^a.
$$
Combining $pV^\gamma=\text{constant}$ with the <ideal gas law> gives $T\propto p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ along either <reversible ideal-gas adiabat>. Therefore
$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1}=k,\qquad \frac{T_3}{T_4}=k,
\qquad T_3-T_2=k(T_4-T_1).
$$
During a constant-pressure process the <heat> supplied equals the <enthalpy> increase. If $C_p$ is the molar <heat capacity at constant pressure>, the positive <heat> input and output are
$$
Q_{\rm in}=nC_p(T_3-T_2),\qquad
Q_{\rm out}=nC_p(T_4-T_1).
$$
The insulated branches exchange no <heat>, so the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $W=Q_{\rm in}-Q_{\rm out}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\eta=1-\frac{Q_{\rm out}}{Q_{\rm in}}=1-\frac1k=1-\left(\frac{p_2}{p_1}\right)^{(1-\gamma)/\gamma}.}
$$
An <adiabatic process> alone need not be an <isentropic process>; the relations used here also require internal reversibility. Dissipative compression or expansion would require extra information and would not, in general, obey this efficiency formula.

{parent=C8}

= Solution
{parent=f}

For a diatomic <ideal gas> in the usual regime with three translational and two rotational quadratic <degrees of freedom>, but frozen vibrational excitation, the <equipartition theorem> gives
$$
C_V=\frac52R_g,\qquad C_p=\frac72R_g,\qquad \gamma=\frac75.
$$
The <Brayton cycle> efficiency is therefore
$$
\boxed{\eta=1-10^{-2/7}=0.4821\simeq48.2\%.}
$$
The constant-pressure expansion has $T_3/T_2=V_3/V_2=2$, while the compression has $T_2/T_1=10^{2/7}=1.93070$. The same relations give $T_4/T_1=2$. Thus, in units of the minimum <temperature>,
$$
(T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4)=T_1(1,1.93070,3.86140,2).
$$
The maximum and minimum <temperatures> are $T_3$ and $T_1$. A <Carnot cycle> between those extremes has
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm Carnot}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_3}=1-\frac1{2\,10^{2/7}}=0.7410\simeq74.1\%.}
$$
The difference is about $25.9$ percentage points. In the <Brayton cycle>, <heat> is supplied and rejected over ranges of <temperature>; the whole input is not supplied at $T_3$, nor is the whole rejection made at $T_1$. This explains the lower efficiency even for mechanically ideal stages. Absolute state <temperatures> are not supplied, so $\gamma=7/5$ is an explicit standard diatomic-gas approximation rather than a conclusion about all possible rotational and vibrational excitation regimes.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A cubic reflecting cavity supports <standing waves> with positive integer mode indices and <wavevector> components $k_i=\pi n_i/L$. A cell in mode space has <volume> $(\pi/L)^3$. In the continuum approximation, a spherical shell of radius $k$ and thickness $dk$ has one-eighth of its <volume> in the positive octant. There are two independent transverse <photon polarizations> for a generic <wavevector>. Hence
$$
dN=2\times\frac18\times4\pi k^2dk\left(\frac L\pi\right)^3=\frac{L^3k^2}{\pi^2}\,dk.
$$
The <photon> <energy> is $\epsilon=\hbar\omega=\hbar ck$, so $dk=d\epsilon/(\hbar c)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{dN=\frac{L^3\epsilon^2}{\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}\,d\epsilon.}
$$
This is the leading bulk <density of states>, valid when the cavity is large compared with the relevant <wavelengths> and many modes lie in the counting interval. A finite cavity has a discrete mode spectrum, with boundary corrections beyond this approximation. Modes with vanishing indices require separate boundary counting but do not change the leading $L^3$ term. Here $\hbar$ is the <reduced Planck constant>, as printed in the original PDF; replacing it by $h$ would introduce an erroneous factor of $(2\pi)^3$.

= b
{parent=C9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Each independent cavity mode is a <quantum harmonic oscillator> of <angular frequency> $\omega$. Its occupation numbers $n=0,1,\ldots$ represent the numbers of <photons> in that mode. At <thermal equilibrium>, <photons> can be absorbed and emitted by the walls, so their number is not conserved and the <photon chemical potential> is zero. The <Boltzmann distribution> of the oscillator excitations then gives
$$
\langle n\rangle=\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1},\qquad
\bar U=\hbar\omega\langle n\rangle.
$$
The expression supplied in the paper is the thermal excitation <energy>. The oscillator's temperature-independent <zero-point energy> is excluded from the emitted <thermal radiation>.

Putting $\epsilon=\hbar\omega$ and $d\epsilon=\hbar\,d\omega$ into the mode count from (a) gives the number of modes per unit cavity <volume>:
$$
\frac{dN}{L^3}=\frac{\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}\,d\omega.
$$
Multiplying by the mean thermal <energy> in each mode yields <Planck's law> in the angular-frequency convention:
$$
\boxed{u(T,\omega)\,d\omega=\frac{\omega^2\,d\omega}{\pi^2c^3}\frac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}.}
$$
The two <photon polarizations> are already included in the <density of states>; no further factor of two is needed. $u(T,\omega)d\omega$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, whereas $u(T,\omega)$ has units $\mathrm{J\,s\,m^{-3}}$.

= c
{parent=C9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The equilibrium <photon gas> is <isotropic>, so the <energy density> per unit <solid angle> in a narrow angular-frequency interval is $u(T,\omega)d\omega/(4\pi)$. For rays making <angle> $\theta$ with an outward surface normal, a surface element $dA$ is crossed in time $dt$ by rays initially in a <volume> $c\,dt\,dA\cos\theta$. Only the outward hemisphere contributes. Thus
$$
e(T,\omega)=\frac{cu(T,\omega)}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta
=\boxed{\frac{cu(T,\omega)}4}.
$$
The hemispherical restriction supplies a factor $1/2$ and the directional projection supplies another $1/2$. A small aperture in a cavity therefore emits this spectral <power> per unit area. A <blackbody> at the same <temperature> emits the same amount: otherwise radiation exchange between it and the aperture would violate <thermal equilibrium>.

Integrating <Planck's law> over all positive <angular frequencies> gives the total emitted <heat flux density>
$$
M(T)=\int_0^\infty e(T,\omega)\,d\omega
=\frac{\hbar}{4\pi^2c^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega^3\,d\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Set $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)$. Then
$$
M(T)=\frac{k_B^4T^4}{4\pi^2\hbar^3c^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}.
$$
The remaining <integral> is independent of <temperature>, proving $M\propto T^4$. Its value follows by expanding $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{m=1}^\infty e^{-mx}$ and integrating termwise:
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}=\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac6{m^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$
Consequently the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> is
$$
\boxed{M(T)=\sigma T^4,\qquad \sigma=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{60\hbar^3c^2}=5.67037\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}.}
$$
This is emitted <power> alone. A body in a radiation environment also absorbs incoming radiation, which must be included when calculating net <radiative cooling>.

{parent=C9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let the spacecraft radius be $a$, and assume that <heat> redistribution keeps its whole surface at a common <temperature> $T$. Neglect background radiation, and let $R\gg r,a$. The Sun's <luminosity>, from the <Stefan–Boltzmann law>, is
$$
L_S=4\pi r^2\sigma T_S^4.
$$
At distance $R$, the incident <irradiance> is $F_S=L_S/(4\pi R^2)=\sigma T_S^4(r/R)^2$. The spacecraft intercepts radiation over its projected disc, area $\pi a^2$, but emits from its whole spherical area $4\pi a^2$. The <radiative equilibrium> condition is therefore
$$
\pi a^2\sigma T_S^4\frac{r^2}{R^2}=4\pi a^2\sigma T^4.
$$
Cancelling the common factors gives
$$
\boxed{T=T_S\sqrt{\frac r{2R}}\ \propto\ T_S\sqrt{\frac rR}.}
$$
The radius of the spacecraft cancels. The factor $1/\sqrt2$ follows from the ratio of absorbing projected area to emitting surface area. Uniform surface <temperature> is the intended additional idealization behind a single equilibrium surface <temperature>. A poorly conducting spacecraft would have position-dependent <temperatures>; for example, a locally reradiating patch facing the Sun would instead have $T_{\rm subsolar}=T_S\sqrt{r/R}$, and a different maximum-temperature constraint.

{parent=C9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

<Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation> equates spectral absorptivity and <spectral emissivity> at the same <wavelength> and direction. A surface that is a poor absorber in the thermal <infrared> is also a poor emitter there; a coating cannot be assigned its solar absorption independently of its thermal emission spectrum.

**(i) All-wavelength black paint.** It absorbs the sunlight and also radiates maximally at the spacecraft's own <temperature>. With the isothermal-sphere assumptions of (d), the limiting black-surface distance is
$$
\boxed{\frac{R_{\min,\rm black}}r=\frac12\left(\frac{T_S}{T_{\max}}\right)^2=\frac12\left(\frac{6000}{600}\right)^2=50.}
$$
The spacecraft can operate at $R\geq50r$ in this model. A surface already assumed to be a <blackbody> is not improved by another black coating; relative to option (ii), however, this paint permits closer operation.

**(ii) Black at short <wavelengths> and grey in the <infrared>.** By <Wien's displacement law>, changing the <black-body radiation> <temperature> from $6000\,\mathrm K$ to $600\,\mathrm K$ shifts the <wavelength> peak from about $0.5\,\mu\mathrm m$ to about $5\,\mu\mathrm m$. Most solar <power> is therefore in the highly absorbing short-wavelength band, whereas most spacecraft emission lies beyond $2\,\mu\mathrm m$, where the <spectral emissivity> is reduced. The coating retains most of the heating but suppresses much of the cooling. Under the intended two-band <grey body> approximation, **option (ii) makes the spacecraft hotter at the same distance and requires a larger minimum distance than black paint.**

To express the comparison without inventing a grey absorptivity, let $\bar\alpha_S$ be <radiometric absorptance> averaged over the solar spectrum and $\bar\epsilon_T$ be emissivity averaged over the spacecraft's own thermal spectrum. The <radiative equilibrium> equation becomes
$$
\bar\alpha_S\sigma T_S^4\frac{r^2}{R^2}=4\bar\epsilon_T\sigma T^4,
\qquad
T=T_S\sqrt{\frac r{2R}}\left(\frac{\bar\alpha_S}{\bar\epsilon_T}\right)^{1/4}.
$$
At the failure threshold,
$$
\boxed{\frac{R_{\min}}r=50\sqrt{\frac{\bar\alpha_S}{\bar\epsilon_{600}}}.}
$$
For an ideal two-band coating take a constant <radiometric absorptance> $g<1$ beyond $\lambda_c=2\,\mu\mathrm m$, and unity below it. If $f(T)$ is the fraction of the <power> emitted by a <blackbody> below $\lambda_c$, <Planck's law> gives
$$
f(T)=\frac{15}{\pi^4}\int_{hc/(\lambda_ck_BT)}^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx,
\qquad
\bar\alpha_S=g+(1-g)f(T_S),\quad
\bar\epsilon_{600}=g+(1-g)f(600\,\mathrm K).
$$
Since $f(T_S)>f(600\,\mathrm K)$, this model has $\bar\alpha_S>\bar\epsilon_{600}$ and hence $R_{\min}>50r$. The paper supplies neither a numerical value of $g$ nor a detailed spectrum, so no unique numerical distance for the grey paint follows. If its long-wavelength absorption is not approximately grey, the spectrum must be integrated explicitly rather than inferred from a peak alone. A coating designed to improve close approach would instead reflect much of the solar radiation while retaining strong thermal <infrared> emission.
