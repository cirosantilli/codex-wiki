= Solution
{parent=A1}

The transfer is a <Hohmann transfer orbit>. Write $r_0$ for the original <circular Kepler orbit> radius and $a_t$ for the transfer <ellipse>'s <semi-major axis>. The diagram measures both radii from <Earth>'s centre, giving $r_1=1.2r_0$, and hence
$$
a_t=\frac{r_0+r_1}{2}=1.1r_0.
$$
The <Hohmann transfer time> is half the transfer <orbital period>, taking the satellite to the opposite end of the <elliptic orbit>. By <Kepler's third law>, relative to the original <geostationary orbit>'s <orbital period> $T_0$,
$$
\frac{T_t}{T_0}=\left(\frac{a_t}{r_0}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{T_0}{2}(1.1)^{3/2}\simeq13.8\ \mathrm h.}
$$
Using one <sidereal day>, $T_0=86164\ \mathrm s$, gives $t=4.9703\times10^4\ \mathrm s=13.806\ \mathrm h$; the usual $24\ \mathrm h$ approximation gives $13.844\ \mathrm h$. The arrival impulse changes the <velocity> into that of the new <circular Kepler orbit> without adding coast time in the impulsive model.

There is a real discrepancy between the wording and the diagram: an increase in altitude above <Earth>'s surface is different from the radius increase drawn. If altitude is interpreted literally, let $R_E$ be <Earth>'s radius. Then
$$
r_1=R_E+1.2(r_0-R_E),\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{T_0}{2}\left(1.1-0.1\frac{R_E}{r_0}\right)^{3/2}\simeq13.5\ \mathrm h,}
$$
using $R_E\simeq6371\ \mathrm{km}$ and $r_0\simeq42164\ \mathrm{km}$. Thus $13.8\ \mathrm h$ answers the explicitly labelled geometry; $13.5\ \mathrm h$ answers the literal altitude wording.

= Solution
{parent=A2}

To find the <two-mass three-spring normal modes>, choose both displacements positive to the right, measured from <mechanical equilibrium>. The middle <spring>'s extension changes by $x_2-x_1$. <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> therefore give
$$
m_1\ddot x_1=-kx_1+k(x_2-x_1)=-2kx_1+kx_2,\qquad
m_2\ddot x_2=-kx_2-k(x_2-x_1)=kx_1-2kx_2.
$$
In a <normal mode> both displacements have the same <angular frequency>: take $x_j=A_j\cos(\omega t+\phi)$. A nonzero amplitude <vector> exists when the <determinant> vanishes:
$$
\begin{vmatrix}2k-m_1\omega^2&-k\\-k&2k-m_2\omega^2\end{vmatrix}=0,
\qquad m_1m_2\omega^4-2k(m_1+m_2)\omega^2+3k^2=0.
$$
Writing $D=\sqrt{m_1^2-m_1m_2+m_2^2}$, the two <angular frequencies> are
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m_1m_2}(m_1+m_2\pm D)}.}
$$
The corresponding <eigenvectors> can be specified by
$$
\frac{A_2}{A_1}=\frac{2k-m_1\omega_\pm^2}{k}
=\frac{m_2-m_1\mp D}{m_2}.
$$
Since $D>|m_2-m_1|$ for positive masses, the lower <normal mode> $\omega_-$ has $A_2/A_1>0$: both bodies move in the same direction at any instant. The higher <normal mode> $\omega_+$ has $A_2/A_1<0$: they move in opposite directions. Their amplitudes are generally unequal. For the useful check $m_1=m_2=m$, these reduce to $\omega_-=\sqrt{k/m}$ with $A_2=A_1$, and $\omega_+=\sqrt{3k/m}$ with $A_2=-A_1$. Each <angular frequency> has units $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$.

An <electron> has <electric charge> $-e$, so its gain of <kinetic energy> through an accelerating <electric potential difference> of magnitude $V$ is $eV$. Starting from rest, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\frac12m_ev^2=eV,\qquad
v=\sqrt{\frac{2eV}{m_e}}.
$$
With $e=1.60218\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm C$, <electron mass> $m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}$, and $V=150\ \mathrm V$,
$$
\boxed{v=7.26\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is a speed; the <velocity> points along the accelerating path, opposite the <electric field>. Here $v/c\simeq0.0242$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>, so the classical <kinetic energy> approximation is accurate. As a check, <special relativity> gives $\gamma=1+eV/(m_ec^2)$ and $v=c\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}\simeq7.262\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, agreeing to the quoted precision.

The quoted <capacitance> is that of the filled <parallel-plate capacitor>, $C_i=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm F$. Disconnecting the source imposes <charge conservation> on the isolated plates:
$$
Q=C_iV_i=(1.0\times10^{-6})(2500)=2.50\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm C.
$$
On removing the <dielectric>, the <capacitance> falls by its <relative permittivity>, so $C_f=C_i/5=2.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm F$. The <capacitor energy> at fixed <electric charge> is $U=Q^2/(2C)$. Therefore
$$
U_i=\frac12C_iV_i^2=3.125\ \mathrm J,\qquad
U_f=\frac{Q^2}{2C_f}=5U_i=15.625\ \mathrm J,
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\Delta U=U_f-U_i=+12.5\ \mathrm J.}
$$
The increase is supplied by the external <work> needed to withdraw the <dielectric> against its attraction into the <electric field>, in the lossless quasistatic limit. The final <electric potential difference> is $Q/C_f=12500\ \mathrm V$, not the original charging value.

= Solution

For the propagating mode of an air-filled or evacuated <rectangular electromagnetic waveguide>, the longitudinal <dispersion relation> is
$$
\omega^2=c^2k_z^2+\omega_c^2.
$$
The <phase velocity> and <group velocity> along the guide are consequently
$$
v_p=\frac{\omega}{k_z}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}},\qquad
v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk_z}=c\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}.
$$
With <waveguide cutoff frequency> $f_c=10\ \mathrm{GHz}$ and carrier <frequency> $f=30\ \mathrm{GHz}$, $\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}=\sqrt8/3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{v_p=\frac{3c}{\sqrt8}=3.18\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
v_g=\frac{\sqrt8\,c}{3}=2.83\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The pulse envelope travels at the <group velocity> for a sufficiently narrow range of <frequencies>. The <phase velocity> exceeding the <speed of light> does not describe superluminal signal transport. The check $v_pv_g=c^2$ follows directly from the <dispersion relation>. A different uniform filling medium would require replacing $c$ by its electromagnetic propagation speed; the stated numerical answer uses the usual empty-guide assumption.

= Solution

Use orthonormal basis <vectors> $\mathbf e_i(t)$ fixed in the <rotating reference frame>, and expand the <angular momentum> as $\mathbf J=\sum_iJ_i\mathbf e_i$. Over time $dt$, the basis undergoes infinitesimal <rotation> $\boldsymbol\Omega\,dt$, so
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf e_i}{dt}\right)_{S_0}=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf e_i.
$$
The <product rule> gives the <rotating-frame derivative formula>:
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf J}{dt}\right)_{S_0}
=\sum_i\dot J_i\mathbf e_i+\sum_iJ_i\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf e_i
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf J}{dt}\right)_S+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf J.
$$
Since the applied <couple> is the rate of change of <angular momentum> in the <inertial frame>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf G=\left(\frac{d\mathbf J}{dt}\right)_S+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf J.}
$$
The second term accounts for the changing basis, even when the components of <angular momentum> are constant in the <rotating reference frame>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

One application is the slow steady <precession> of a rapidly spinning <gyroscope> supported at one point. Let its <centre of mass> be a distance $\ell$ from the pivot along its spin axis, at angle $\alpha$ to the upward vertical. Write $J\simeq I_1\omega$ for its dominant spin <angular momentum>. In a <reference frame> precessing steadily about the vertical with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega_p$, the spin <angular momentum> is approximately stationary, so
$$
\mathbf G\simeq\boldsymbol\Omega_p\times\mathbf J.
$$
The gravitational <torque> has magnitude $mg\ell\sin\alpha$. The <cross product> on the right has magnitude $\Omega_pJ\sin\alpha$, giving, for a nonvertical axis,
$$
\boxed{\Omega_p\simeq\frac{mg\ell}{I_1\omega}.}
$$
Its direction follows from the <torque> equation. This is the slow <precession> approximation: the additional <angular momentum> associated with <precession> is small compared with the spin contribution.

For this <gyrocompass>, let $\mathbf n,\mathbf w,\mathbf z$ be local north, west and upward unit <vectors>, forming a right-handed basis. <Earth's rotation> has local components
$$
\boldsymbol\Omega_E=\Omega_E\cos\lambda\,\mathbf n+\Omega_E\sin\lambda\,\mathbf z.
$$
Let the spin axis point along $\mathbf u=\cos\theta\,\mathbf n+\sin\theta\,\mathbf w$, and write its dominant <angular momentum> as $J_s\mathbf u$, where $J_s=I_1\omega>0$. In <mechanical equilibrium> relative to <Earth>, this leading <angular momentum> is fixed in the terrestrial <rotating reference frame>. The bearings can exert horizontal <torques> to keep the axle horizontal, but free rotation about the vertical implies $G_z=0$. The vertical component of the <rotating-frame derivative formula> therefore requires
$$
0=(\boldsymbol\Omega_E\times J_s\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf z
=\Omega_EJ_s\cos\lambda\sin\theta.
$$
Away from either geographical pole, $\cos\lambda\ne0$, hence
$$
\boxed{\theta=0\ \text{or}\ \pi:\quad\text{the axle lies north--south}.}
$$
This is a necessary <equilibrium> condition; the next part distinguishes its stable and unstable spin orientations. The rapid-spin approximation neglects <angular momentum> terms of order $I_2\Omega_E$ compared with $I_1\omega$. At either pole the horizontal component of <Earth's rotation> vanishes, and this north-seeking mechanism gives no preferred horizontal direction. The paper explicitly excludes $90^\circ$; if latitude is signed, $-90^\circ$ must likewise be excluded.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For the <gyrocompass oscillation>, use the same local basis and define $\theta$ about the stable orientation, with spin <angular momentum> directed north at $\theta=0$. Because the vertical is perpendicular to the horizontal axle, the extra vertical <angular momentum> from the slow azimuthal motion is $I_2\dot\theta$. To leading order in <Earth>'s small <angular velocity>,
$$
\mathbf J\simeq J_s\mathbf u+I_2\dot\theta\,\mathbf z,\qquad J_s=I_1\omega.
$$
A constant vertical contribution $I_2\Omega_E\sin\lambda$ from <Earth's rotation> has zero terrestrial time <derivative>; the transverse terrestrial contributions affect the following equation only at order $\Omega_E^2$. Taking the vertical component of the <torque> equation gives
$$
0=I_2\ddot\theta+J_s\Omega_E\cos\lambda\sin\theta.
$$
The terrestrial <derivative> of $J_s\mathbf u$ is horizontal, as is $\boldsymbol\Omega_E\times(I_2\dot\theta\mathbf z)$, so neither contributes to this component. Thus the small <angle> satisfies, using the <small-angle approximation> $\sin\theta\simeq\theta$,
$$
\ddot\theta+\frac{I_1}{I_2}\omega\Omega_E\cos\lambda\,\theta\simeq0,
\qquad
\boxed{\omega_{\rm shm}^2\simeq\frac{I_1}{I_2}\omega\Omega_E\cos\lambda.}
$$
This is <simple harmonic motion>, $\theta=A\cos(\omega_{\rm shm}t+\phi)$, with positive squared <angular frequency> away from the poles. The approximation requires rapid spin, small angular displacement and negligible bearing <friction>. Near the opposite orientation, putting $\theta=\pi+\delta$ instead yields $\ddot\delta-\omega_{\rm shm}^2\delta\simeq0$, which is unstable. Consequently, the paper's oscillation claim applies to the stable <equilibrium>, not both north--south spin directions. Without <damping> the ideal <gyrocompass> continues to oscillate rather than settling.

= Solution

The <bending moment> $B(x)$ is the internal <torque> transmitted across a cut through an <elastic beam>, about the transverse bending axis. It balances the moments of the external <forces> on the isolated portion on one side of the cut. Its sign depends on the chosen convention, and its units are $\mathrm{N\,m}$.

The relevant “moment of area” is the <second moment of area> of the cross-section about its <neutral axis>:
$$
\boxed{I=\int_A z^2\,dA,}
$$
where $z$ is perpendicular distance from that axis within the section. It has units $\mathrm{m^4}$ and is a geometrical quantity, unlike a mass <moment of inertia>. For a homogeneous beam under pure bending, the <neutral axis> passes through the section's <centroid>, and its longitudinal <strain> is zero.

= b
{parent=B7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Assume a slender homogeneous <elastic beam> obeying <linear elasticity>, with plane sections remaining plane and no resultant longitudinal <force>. A length element of its <neutral axis> subtending a small <angle> $d\phi$ has length $R\,d\phi$. A neighbouring fibre at distance $z$ from that axis has a length change of magnitude $z\,d\phi$, giving longitudinal <strain> $\varepsilon=z/R$ with signs chosen consistently for tensile and compressed fibres. By <Hooke's law>, its longitudinal <stress> is $\sigma=E\varepsilon=Ez/R$.

The <torque> of these longitudinal <forces> about the <neutral axis> is therefore
$$
B=\int_A z\sigma\,dA=\frac E R\int_Az^2\,dA=\frac{EI}{R},
\qquad
\boxed{R=\frac{EI}{B}.}
$$
Here $E$ is <Young's modulus> and $I$ the <second moment of area>. The displayed relation uses a signed radius and <bending moment>; an unsigned geometrical radius is $|R|=EI/|B|$. For consistency with the negative <bending moments> in the later parts, take the deflection $y$ positive downward and the signed <curvature> as $1/R\simeq y''$. Then the small-slope <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> reads $EIy''=B$. Reversing the vertical coordinate reverses the corresponding sign relation but leaves the physical deflection unchanged.

By vertical <force> balance and reflection <symmetry>, each support exerts an upward <force> $W/2$. Use the paper's convention in which the sagging <bending moment> is negative. For a cut at $0<x<L/2$, the left support's moment about that cut gives
$$
B(x)=-\frac W2x.
$$
For $L/2<x<L$, include the central downward <force> when taking moments of all loads to the left:
$$
B(x)=-\frac W2x+W\left(x-\frac L2\right)=-\frac W2(L-x).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{B(x)=\begin{cases}-Wx/2,&0<x<L/2,\\-W(L-x)/2,&L/2<x<L.\end{cases}}
$$
The <bending moment> is continuous at the load, with $B(L/2)=-WL/4$, and vanishes at both freely rotating supports. The jump is in its first <derivative>, reflecting the concentrated transverse <force>.

The uniform weight per unit length is $q=\rho abg$, where $\rho$ is the <mass density> and $g$ the <gravitational acceleration>. The total weight is $qL$, so each support provides an upward <force> $qL/2$. The weight on the portion from $0$ to $x$ is $qx$ acting at that portion's midpoint, a distance $x/2$ from the cut. In the same <bending moment> convention as part (c),
$$
B(x)=-\frac{qL}{2}x+(qx)\frac x2,
\qquad
\boxed{B(x)=-\frac12\rho abg\,x(L-x).}
$$
The units are $(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}})\mathrm{m^2}=\mathrm{N\,m}$. Its greatest magnitude occurs at $x=L/2$, where $B=-qL^2/8$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Take downward deflection $y(x)$ positive, with the support level $y=0$. The <rectangular section second moment of area> about the horizontal <neutral axis> is
$$
I=a\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}z^2\,dz=\frac{ab^3}{12}.
$$
For small slopes the <curvature> relation from part (b), with the stated sign convention, is
$$
EIy''=-\frac q2x(L-x).
$$
Integrating twice gives
$$
EIy'=-\frac{qLx^2}{4}+\frac{qx^3}{6}+C_1,\qquad
EIy=-\frac{qLx^3}{12}+\frac{qx^4}{24}+C_1x+C_2.
$$
The support <boundary conditions> $y(0)=y(L)=0$ give $C_2=0$ and $C_1=qL^3/24$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{q}{24EI}\left(x^4-2Lx^3+L^3x\right)
=\frac{\rho g}{2Eb^2}\left(x^4-2Lx^3+L^3x\right),\quad0\le x\le L.}
$$
This is a downward displacement; its upward component is $-y(x)$. As checks, reflection <symmetry> gives $y'(L/2)=0$, and the maximum downward deflection is
$$
\boxed{y_{\max}=y(L/2)=\frac{5qL^4}{384EI}=\frac{5\rho gL^4}{32Eb^2}.}
$$
The units are length: $\rho g/(Eb^2)$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. The width $a$ cancels because both weight and bending resistance scale with it. The small-slope approximation requires $\rho gL^3/(Eb^2)\ll1$; <shear strain> is also neglected, as appropriate for a slender <elastic beam>.

The <Poynting vector> is the local <electromagnetic energy> flux density:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf N=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H.}
$$
Here $\mathbf E$ is the <electric field> and $\mathbf H$ the <magnetic field intensity>. For an oriented area element $d\mathbf S$, $\mathbf N\cdot d\mathbf S$ is the electromagnetic <power> crossing that element in its positive normal direction. Thus $\int_S\mathbf N\cdot d\mathbf S$ is outward <power> through a closed <surface>, in watts. The <Poynting vector> has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and its direction describes local <electromagnetic energy> transport.

= b
{parent=C8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The unit <relative permittivity> and vacuum-valued <magnetic permeability> imply $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$. The <electromagnetic energy> per unit volume is
$$
\boxed{U=\frac12\epsilon_0\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf E+\frac12\mu_0\mathbf H\cdot\mathbf H,\qquad[U]=\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}.}
$$
The two contributions are the stored electric and magnetic <energies>. Although called $U$ in this paper, this quantity is an <energy density>; the total <electromagnetic energy> is $\int_VU\,dV$.

= c
{parent=C8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the <divergence> identity for a <cross product>, followed by <Faraday's law of induction> and the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>:
$$
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)
=\mathbf H\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H),
$$
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu_0\frac{\partial\mathbf H}{\partial t},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$
With the <electromagnetic energy> density of part (b), these give
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf N
=-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J-\mu_0\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf H-\epsilon_0\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf E
=-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J-\frac{\partial U}{\partial t}.
$$
Integrate over a fixed volume $V$ and apply the <divergence theorem>, choosing $d\mathbf S$ outward:
$$
\boxed{\oint_S\mathbf N\cdot d\mathbf S
=-\int_V\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J\,dV-\frac d{dt}\int_VU\,dV.}
$$
This is <Poynting's theorem>. The outward <electromagnetic energy> flux equals minus the <power> transferred to matter and minus the rate at which <electromagnetic energy> accumulates inside. For the dissipative <electrical conductor> in this question, the matter term is <Ohmic heating>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Take the <electric current> along $+z$, and use <cylindrical coordinates>. A <uniform current in a straight homogeneous wire> has
$$
\mathbf J=\frac I{\pi a^2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
The local <Ohm's law> $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$ gives the longitudinal <electric field>
$$
\mathbf E=\frac I{\sigma\pi a^2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
For a circular <Amperian loop> of radius $r\le a$, the enclosed <electric current> is $I_{\rm enc}=Ir^2/a^2$. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> gives $2\pi rH_\phi=I_{\rm enc}$, hence
$$
\mathbf H=\frac{Ir}{2\pi a^2}\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}.
$$
Since $\widehat{\mathbf z}\times\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}=-\widehat{\mathbf r}$, the <Poynting vector> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf N(r)=-\frac{I^2r}{2\sigma\pi^2a^4}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad0\le r\le a.}
$$
It is zero at the centre and points radially inward elsewhere. Its units are $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. These are the interior fields in the long-wire approximation, neglecting end and contact effects.

{parent=C8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <Ohmic heating> per unit volume is $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J=J^2/\sigma$, uniform in this wire. Multiplying by its volume gives the dissipated <power>
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm loss}=\frac1\sigma\left(\frac I{\pi a^2}\right)^2\pi a^2L
=\frac{I^2L}{\sigma\pi a^2}=I^2R_{\rm wire},}
$$
where the <electrical resistance> is $R_{\rm wire}=L/(\sigma\pi a^2)$. The result is in watts; over a time interval $\Delta t$ the dissipated <energy> is $P_{\rm loss}\Delta t$.

= Solution
{parent=f}

On the curved wire <surface>, the outward normal is $\widehat{\mathbf r}$ and the <Poynting vector> has the constant radial value $N_r(a)=-I^2/(2\sigma\pi^2a^3)$. Its outward <flux> is
$$
\Phi_N=\int_{\rm curved}\mathbf N\cdot d\mathbf S
=N_r(a)(2\pi aL)
=\boxed{-\frac{I^2L}{\sigma\pi a^2}=-P_{\rm loss}.}
$$
The <Poynting vector> is radial, so it has zero normal component on the two end faces in this approximation. This is therefore also the outward <flux> through the complete wire <surface>. Equivalently, positive electromagnetic <power> $P_{\rm loss}$ flows inward.

The fields are steady, so the stored <electromagnetic energy> does not change. <Poynting's theorem> consequently requires the incoming <power> to equal the <Ohmic heating> in part (e), exactly as found. The energy reaches the wire through the surrounding <electromagnetic field>. A local check is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf N=r^{-1}\partial_r(rN_r)=-I^2/(\sigma\pi^2a^4)=-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J$.

Let the unit normal $\mathbf n$ point from material 1 to material 2, and let their relative <magnetic permeabilities> be $\mu_1,\mu_2$, so $\mathbf B_i=\mu_0\mu_i\mathbf H_i$ for linear isotropic media. Apply <Gauss's law for magnetism> to an infinitesimal <Gaussian pillbox> straddling the interface. The side contribution vanishes as its height tends to zero, leaving
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0.}
$$
The normal <magnetic flux density> is continuous.

Next use a narrow rectangular <Amperian loop> crossing the interface. The two short-side contributions vanish in the zero-height limit. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> for <magnetic field intensity> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf n\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f,}
$$
where $\mathbf K_f$ is any free <surface current density>. Thus, if there is no free <surface current density> on the interface, the tangential <magnetic field intensity> is continuous. In that case the component relations are
$$
\boxed{B_{1n}=B_{2n},\qquad H_{1t}=H_{2t},\qquad
\mu_1H_{1n}=\mu_2H_{2n},\qquad
\frac{B_{1t}}{\mu_1}=\frac{B_{2t}}{\mu_2}.}
$$
The distinction between free and bound <electric currents> matters: bound currents due to <magnetization> are already incorporated in the definition of <magnetic field intensity>. A change of <magnetic permeability> alone does not make the tangential <magnetic flux density> continuous.

The starting point is <Gauss's law for magnetism>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$: a <flux tube> carries the same <magnetic flux> through successive transverse sections. To identify that tube approximately with the physical core, one must also make a negligible-leakage approximation. The large core <magnetic permeability> makes the core route much less reluctant than competing paths through the surrounding air; the small gap further reduces the incentive for a long external return path. The gentle taper, $s\gg R-r$, lets the field follow the changing section with only small transverse corrections.

For two cuts and the intervening lateral <surface>, the <divergence theorem> gives $\Phi_2-\Phi_1=-\Phi_{\rm side}$. Neglecting lateral leakage gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi\simeq B(x)A(x)\simeq\text{constant}.}
$$
The normal <magnetic flux density> is continuous at each core–air interface by part (a), so this same <magnetic flux> crosses the gap. To use the gap's geometrical area $\pi r^2$ in the later formulas, the <magnetic fringing> must also be negligible, normally requiring $h\ll r$. This is the small-gap interpretation of the paper's approximation. Large <magnetic permeability> alone does not eliminate leakage or <magnetic fringing> for arbitrary gap geometry.

For the closed untapered torus, apply the <magnetostatic Ampère law> to a circular path through the core. It links all $N$ turns, each carrying <electric current> $I$:
$$
\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\mathbf l=NI.
$$
Since $L\gg R$, variation of the path length and of the tangential <magnetic field intensity> across the section is negligible. Thus $H_{\rm in}L\simeq NI$, giving, within this thin-torus approximation,
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm in}=\frac{NI}{L}.}
$$
The units are $\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. The core <magnetic flux density> is $B_{\rm in}=\mu_0\mu NI/L$ in the linear material model.

With uniform section and negligible <magnetic fringing>, constant <magnetic flux> gives equal <magnetic flux densities> in the core and gap:
$$
\mu_0\mu H_{\rm in}=\mu_0H_{\rm gap},\qquad H_{\rm in}=H_{\rm gap}/\mu.
$$
Here $L$ is the core arc length and $h$ the additional gap length. Applying the <magnetostatic Ampère law> around the complete <magnetic circuit>,
$$
H_{\rm in}L+H_{\rm gap}h=NI,
\qquad
H_{\rm gap}\left(\frac L\mu+h\right)=NI.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm gap}=\frac{\mu NI}{\mu h+L}.}
$$
This tends to $NI/h$ when the gap dominates the <magnetic reluctance>, $\mu h\gg L$, and reduces to the no-gap result as $h\to0$. The gap <magnetic flux density> is $B_{\rm gap}=\mu_0H_{\rm gap}$.

Let the approximately conserved <magnetic flux> be $\Phi$. Then
$$
\Phi=\mu_0H_{\rm gap}\pi r^2.
$$
The length $L$ shown in the original diagram is the untapered core arc; the two tapers are separate lengths $s$ each. In the untapered core, <flux> conservation gives
$$
H_R=\frac{\Phi}{\mu_0\mu\pi R^2}
=\frac{H_{\rm gap}}\mu\frac{r^2}{R^2}.
$$
For one taper use distance $u$ from its narrow end. The straight taper in the diagram has local radius
$$
a(u)=r+\frac{R-r}{s}u,\qquad0\le u\le s,
$$
and <magnetic field intensity>
$$
H(u)=\frac{\Phi}{\mu_0\mu\pi a(u)^2}
=\frac{H_{\rm gap}}\mu\frac{r^2}{a(u)^2}.
$$
Its contribution to the <line integral> in the <magnetostatic Ampère law> is found explicitly:
$$
\int_0^s\frac{du}{a(u)^2}
=\frac{s}{R-r}\left(\frac1r-\frac1R\right)=\frac{s}{rR},
\qquad
\int_0^sH(u)\,du=\frac{H_{\rm gap}}\mu\frac{sr}{R}.
$$
Adding the gap, the untapered arc and both tapers gives
$$
NI=H_{\rm gap}h+\frac{H_{\rm gap}}\mu\frac{r^2}{R^2}L
+2\frac{H_{\rm gap}}\mu\frac{sr}{R}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm gap}=\frac{\mu NI}{\mu h+L(r/R)^2+2s(r/R)}.}
$$
This uses a linear radius profile, approximately uniform <magnetic flux density> over each section, a linear constant <magnetic permeability>, and negligible leakage and <magnetic fringing>. For a non-linear taper profile $a(u)$ the honest generalization is
$$
H_{\rm gap}=\frac{\mu NI}{\mu h+Lr^2/R^2+2r^2\int_0^sdu/a(u)^2}.
$$
Thus an arbitrary smooth taper with the same end radii and length need not give the printed formula; the straight-sided geometry supplies the additional information.

Set $\alpha=r/R<1$. Comparing with the untapered expression in part (d), using the same $N,I,\mu,h,L$ and large radius $R$,
$$
\frac{H_{\rm gap,taper}}{H_{\rm gap,untaper}}
=\frac{\mu h+L}{\mu h+L\alpha^2+2s\alpha}.
$$
The stated limit $s/L\ll\alpha$ makes $2s\alpha\ll L\alpha^2$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{H_{\rm gap,taper}}{H_{\rm gap,untaper}}\simeq
\frac{\mu h+L}{\mu h+L\alpha^2}>1.}
$$
The taper concentrates <magnetic flux> into a smaller pole-face area, increasing the gap <magnetic field intensity> and <magnetic flux density> for the same winding <electric current>. If $\mu h\ll L\alpha^2$, the enhancement approaches $1/\alpha^2=(R/r)^2$. If instead $\mu h\gg L$, the gap dominates the <magnetic reluctance> and the enhancement approaches unity: both fields approach $NI/h$.

The benefit is therefore strongest when the untapered core's contribution is significant, while the taper lengths contribute little. The same-core-length convention is the comparison defined by parts (d) and (e); if one instead fixes the full iron path length, the untapered comparator has length $L+2s$, a difference negligible in the stated limit. A smaller gap area can increase the field even though the total <magnetic flux> need not increase. <Magnetic saturation> and <magnetic fringing>, excluded from this model, limit the ideal concentration.
