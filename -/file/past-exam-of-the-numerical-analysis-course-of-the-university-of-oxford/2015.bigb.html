= Solution
{parent=a}

For $a<b$, the one-panel <trapezoidal rule> is
$$
\boxed{\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\ \approx\ T(f):=\frac{b-a}{2}\bigl(f(a)+f(b)\bigr).}
$$
It integrates the <linear interpolation> between the two endpoint samples. Indeed the <affine function>
$$
\ell(x)=\frac{b-x}{b-a}f(a)+\frac{x-a}{b-a}f(b)
$$
has <definite integral> $T(f)$. The two <quadrature nodes> are $a,b$ and both <quadrature weights> are $(b-a)/2$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

It suffices to assume $f\in C^2[a,b]$. The <polynomial interpolation error> for the endpoint <linear interpolant> gives, for every interior $x$, some $\xi_x\in(a,b)$ with
$$
f(x)-\ell(x)=\frac{f''(\xi_x)}2(x-a)(x-b).
$$
Let $M=\max_{[a,b]}|f''|$, which exists by <continuity> of the <second derivative> and the <extreme value theorem>. Since $(x-a)(b-x)\geq0$,
$$
|f(x)-\ell(x)|\leq\frac M2(x-a)(b-x).
$$
Integrating this pointwise inequality and using the <triangle inequality for an integral> yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\int_a^bf(x)\,dx-T(f)\right|
&\leq\frac M2\int_a^b(x-a)(b-x)\,dx\\
&=\frac M2\int_0^{b-a}t\bigl((b-a)-t\bigr)\,dt
=\frac{M(b-a)^3}{12}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{|I(f)-T(f)|\leq\frac{(b-a)^3}{12}\max_{[a,b]}|f''|.}
$$
This derivation does not require choosing $\xi_x$ as a continuous <function> of $x$; the pointwise upper bound is sufficient.

The constant is sharp. Take the <quadratic polynomial> $f(x)=(x-a)^2$ and write $L=b-a$. Its exact <integral> is $L^3/3$, its <trapezoidal rule> value is $L^3/2$, and $\max|f''|=2$. Consequently
$$
|I(f)-T(f)|=\frac{L^3}{6}=\frac{L^3}{12}\max|f''|.
$$
Equality holds, so **no smaller universal constant than $1/12$ works** under these assumptions. This is the <sharp one-panel trapezoidal error bound>.

= Solution

The <trapezoidal rule> is exact for every <affine function> $f(x)=\alpha+\beta x$, equivalently every <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most one: the <linear interpolant> is the <function> itself. These <functions> have $f''=0$, so the preceding upper bound is also zero. There is **no contradiction**: zero error is precisely what the bound predicts.

The <degree of precision of a quadrature rule> is one, since the <quadratic polynomial> in part (i) is not integrated exactly. This does not exclude exactness for particular higher-degree or nonpolynomial <functions>. For example $f(x)=(x-m)^3$, $m=(a+b)/2$, has zero <integral> and opposite endpoint values, so the <trapezoidal rule> also gives zero. Its <second derivative> is generally nonzero, making the upper bound positive; an upper bound need not be attained. Signed <quadrature error> can cancel.

On a <partition of an interval> $a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_N=b$, apply the <trapezoidal rule> separately to each panel and add:
$$
\boxed{T_N(f)=\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{x_j-x_{j-1}}2\bigl(f(x_{j-1})+f(x_j)\bigr).}
$$
For uniform spacing $h=(b-a)/N$, this <composite trapezoidal rule> becomes
$$
\boxed{T_N(f)=h\left[\frac12f(a)+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}f(a+jh)+\frac12f(b)\right].}
$$
Each interior sample occurs in two panels; their half-<quadrature weights> combine to $h$. The endpoint <quadrature weights> are $h/2$.

= Solution

Use the nonpolynomial <function> $f(x)=1+\sin x$ on $[-1,1]$. Its exact <integral> is
$$
\int_{-1}^1(1+\sin x)\,dx=2,
$$
because the <sine> is an <odd function>.

For every uniform <partition of an interval> with $N\geq1$ panels, the <quadrature nodes> satisfy $x_{N-j}=-x_j$. Their <composite trapezoidal rule> <quadrature weights> agree, so the two sampled sine values cancel. If there is a node at zero its sine value is already zero. The constant term is integrated exactly, giving
$$
\boxed{T_N(1+\sin x)=2\quad\text{for every }N\geq1.}
$$
This is <symmetric trapezoidal exactness for odd functions>, applied after adding a <constant function> so that the resulting <definite integral> is nonzero. It is exactness for this particular <integrand>, rather than a claim that every nonpolynomial <function> is integrated exactly.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Write $E(h)$ for the supplied approximation. The <asymptotic expansion> is
$$
Y=E(h)+ch^2+r(h),\qquad r(h)=O(h^3),
$$
where $O$ is <Big O notation>. At the refined parameter $h/3$,
$$
Y=E(h/3)+\frac{ch^2}{9}+r(h/3).
$$
Multiply this identity by nine and subtract the first one. The unknown leading <truncation error> coefficient cancels:
$$
8Y=9E(h/3)-E(h)+9r(h/3)-r(h).
$$
Thus the <Richardson extrapolation> estimate is
$$
Y_{\rm ext}(h)=\frac{9E(h/3)-E(h)}8,\qquad
Y-Y_{\rm ext}(h)=O(h^3).
$$
The two supplied values give
$$
\boxed{Y_{\rm ext}(1)=\frac{9\cdot3-2}{8}=\frac{25}{8}=3.125.}
$$
The correction to the finer approximation is $(3-2)/8=1/8$. Neglecting the higher-order remainder would also give $c=9/8$.

The conclusion is asymptotic: the leading error order improves from two to at least three when both parameter values are in the range of the stated <asymptotic expansion>. With no bound on the remainder constant, the two finite observations alone do not guarantee that $25/8$ is closer to $Y$. Indeed, for any proposed value $L$, the <polynomial> model
$$
E(h)=L-c_Lh^2-d_Lh^3,\qquad
c_L=13L-\frac{79}{2},\qquad d_L=\frac{75}{2}-12L
$$
satisfies $E(1)=2$, $E(1/3)=3$ and the required <asymptotic expansion> with true <limit> $L$. This is why <finite observations do not certify Richardson extrapolation accuracy>: asymptotic cancellation provides an improved-order estimator, while a numerical error certificate needs quantitative remainder information.

= Solution

The <polynomial> $p_n$ interpolates $f$ at the <interpolation nodes> when
$$
p_n(x_i)=f(x_i)\qquad(0\leq i\leq n).
$$
For distinct <interpolation nodes>, define the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>
$$
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
$$
The denominators are nonzero, each <polynomial degree> is at most $n$, and $\ell_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}$, with $\delta_{ij}$ the <Kronecker delta>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^nf(x_i)\ell_i(x)}
$$
is a <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> belonging to the <vector space of univariate polynomials> $\Pi_n$, and it has all the required sampled values.

If $r_n\in\Pi_n$ also works, $p_n-r_n$ has $n+1$ distinct <roots of a polynomial> while its <polynomial degree> is at most $n$. The <root bound for a polynomial> forces it to be the <zero polynomial>, proving uniqueness. The phrase “degree $n$” denotes the allowed maximum; the actual <polynomial degree> may be smaller. No <continuity> assumption is needed merely to construct the <interpolation polynomial> from these finitely many values.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Assume $f\in C^{n+1}[a,b]$ and set $\omega(t)=\prod_{i=0}^n(t-x_i)$, a <monic polynomial> of <polynomial degree> $n+1$. Fix $x\in[a,b]$ distinct from all the <interpolation nodes>, and choose
$$
C=\frac{f(x)-p_n(x)}{\omega(x)}.
$$
The auxiliary <function>
$$
F(t)=f(t)-p_n(t)-C\omega(t)
$$
vanishes at the $n+2$ distinct points $x_0,\ldots,x_n,x$. Order those points and repeatedly apply <Rolle's theorem>. After $n+1$ applications, there is some $\xi$ strictly between their smallest and largest values, hence in $(a,b)$, such that $F^{(n+1)}(\xi)=0$.

Because $p_n$ has <polynomial degree> at most $n$ and $\omega$ is monic of <polynomial degree> $n+1$,
$$
0=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)-C(n+1)!.
$$
Substituting the resulting value of $C$ proves the <polynomial interpolation error> formula
$$
\boxed{f(x)-p_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i).}
$$
At an <interpolation node>, both sides are zero, so any $\xi\in(a,b)$ works. Such an interior point exists because $n\geq1$ and the <interpolation nodes> are distinct. The selected $\xi$ generally depends on $x$; the theorem makes an existence assertion, not a regularity assertion about that dependence.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Assume $f\in C^{n+1}[a,b]$ and reorder the <interpolation nodes> increasingly as $x_0<\cdots<x_n$. The <polynomial interpolation error> $e=f-p_n$ vanishes at all of them. <Rolle's theorem> supplies
$$
\eta_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i),\qquad e'(\eta_i)=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
$$
These $n$ points are distinct because their containing <intervals> are disjoint. Thus $q=p_n'$ is a <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $n-1$ that interpolates the <function> $f'$ at the <interpolation nodes> $\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n$.

Apply the <polynomial interpolation error> theorem to $f'$ with these $n$ <interpolation nodes>. Since the $n$th <derivative> of $f'$ is $f^{(n+1)}$, for every $x\in[a,b]$ there is a $\xi_x\in(a,b)$ with
$$
\boxed{f'(x)-q(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi_x)}{n!}\widehat\pi_n(x),\qquad
\widehat\pi_n(x)=\prod_{i=1}^n(x-\eta_i).}
$$
For $x=\eta_i$ the equality is zero on both sides; otherwise the same auxiliary-<function> and repeated-<Rolle's theorem> proof applies. This also covers $n=1$, where $q$ is a <constant polynomial> and the proof is the ordinary <mean value theorem> for $f'$.

Writing $M=\max_{[a,b]}|f^{(n+1)}|$ gives the genuine error bound
$$
\boxed{|f'(x)-q(x)|\leq\frac M{n!}\prod_{i=1}^n|x-\eta_i|
\leq\frac M{n!}(b-a)^n.}
$$
The first formula is sharper, but its intermediate <interpolation nodes> depend on $f$; the last bound eliminates those unknown locations. This is <derivative interpolation error from Rolle points>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The remainder in part (b) involves an unspecified $\xi=\xi(x)$. Its existence for every $x$ does not ensure a differentiable selection. Even if such a selection existed and $f$ had an additional <derivative>, the <product rule> and <chain rule> would give
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))}{(n+1)!}\omega(x)\right]
=\frac{f^{(n+2)}(\xi(x))\xi'(x)\omega(x)+f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))\omega'(x)}{(n+1)!}.
$$
The first term cannot be discarded or bounded from the given information. Neither $f^{(n+2)}$ nor $\xi'$ is supplied by the original theorem.

For an explicit demonstration, interpolate the <cubic polynomial> $f(x)=x^3$ at zero and one. Then $p_1(x)=x$, and
$$
f(x)-p_1(x)=x^3-x=(x+1)x(x-1).
$$
Differentiating correctly gives
$$
f'(x)-p_1'(x)=x(x-1)+(x+1)(2x-1)=3x^2-1.
$$
Treating the remainder coefficient $x+1$ as a constant would omit $x(x-1)$ and give a wrong answer at every interior point. **An existential intermediate point cannot be frozen when differentiating.** Part (i) instead applies <Rolle's theorem> directly to the <polynomial interpolation error>.

= Solution

Write $\widetilde p_1$ and $\widetilde q$ for the quantities computed from the perturbed second sample. The endpoint <linear interpolation> and its <derivative> are
$$
\widetilde p_1(x)=\frac{x}{h}\bigl(\sin(ah)+a\epsilon\bigr),\qquad
\widetilde q(x)=\frac{\sin(ah)}h+\frac{a\epsilon}{h}.
$$
Compare them on their interpolation <interval> $0\leq x\leq h$, with fixed $a>0$. Put $x=th$, $0\leq t\leq1$. The <Taylor series> of the <sine> gives, uniformly in $t$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde p_1(th)-\sin(ath)
&=at\epsilon+\frac{a^3h^3}{6}(t^3-t)+O(h^5),\\
\widetilde q-f'(0)
&=\widetilde q-a=\frac{a\epsilon}{h}-\frac{a^3h^2}{6}+O(h^4).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the second equality compares with $f'(0)=a$. More generally, the exact <derivative> is $f'(th)=a\cos(ath)$ and
$$
\widetilde q-f'(th)=\frac{a\epsilon}{h}+a^3h^2\left(\frac{t^2}{2}-\frac16\right)+O(h^4).
$$
The input perturbation itself changes the value <interpolant> by exactly $a\epsilon x/h$, and changes its <derivative> by exactly $a\epsilon/h$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\|\widetilde p_1-p_1\|_{\infty,[0,h]}=a|\epsilon|,\qquad
|\widetilde q-q|=\frac{a|\epsilon|}{h}.}
$$
For fixed nonzero $\epsilon$, the value approximation has a bounded noise floor: its maximum error approaches $a|\epsilon|$, and at $x=h$ that error is exactly $a|\epsilon|$. It **does not converge uniformly to the exact values** as $h\to0$. However, it does not amplify the absolute sample perturbation on $[0,h]$. The <derivative> error instead grows without bound like $a|\epsilon|/h$.

With exact samples, $\epsilon=0$, both approximations do converge on the shrinking <interval>: the value error is $O(h^3)$ for this particular <sine>, and the <derivative> error is $O(h^2)$. With step-dependent noise, value convergence requires $\epsilon(h)\to0$, whereas <derivative> convergence requires the stronger $\epsilon(h)=o(h)$, where $o$ denotes <little o notation>.

This is <noise amplification by differentiation of a linear interpolant>. Value <interpolation> has an absolute perturbation bound independent of $h$ on the sampled <interval>. Numerical <differentiation> has a perturbation factor $1/h$, expressing the <ill-posedness of numerical differentiation> from values with fixed absolute uncertainty. The amplification follows from the mathematical data map even in exact arithmetic; it is distinct from additional <roundoff error> in a particular implementation. For fixed $x$ outside $[0,h]$, the operation becomes <extrapolation>, and the value perturbation $a\epsilon x/h$ can also diverge.

= Solution

A real square <matrix> $Q$ is an <orthogonal matrix> when $Q^TQ=I$, equivalently its columns form an <orthonormal basis>. Here $Q^T$ is the <transpose> and $I$ the <identity matrix>. For every <vector> $x$,
$$
\|Qx\|_2^2=(Qx)^T(Qx)=x^TQ^TQx=x^Tx=\|x\|_2^2.
$$
Taking nonnegative <square roots> proves
$$
\boxed{\|Qx\|_2=\|x\|_2.}
$$
Thus an <orthogonal matrix> preserves the <Euclidean norm>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Since $Q^{-1}=Q^T$, the <QR factorization> gives
$$
Ax=b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Rx=Q^Tb.
$$
First compute $y=Q^Tb$ by a <matrix-vector product>, costing $n^2$ scalar multiplications and $n(n-1)$ additions for dense $Q$. Then solve the <upper triangular matrix> system $Rx=y$ by <back substitution>:
$$
\boxed{x_i=\frac{y_i-\sum_{j=i+1}^nr_{ij}x_j}{r_{ii}},\qquad i=n,n-1,\ldots,1.}
$$
Every denominator is nonzero because $R=Q^TA$ is invertible. The <back substitution> costs $n(n-1)$ multiplications and subtractions together, plus $n$ divisions, for $n^2$ <arithmetic operations>. Including $Q^Tb$, the total is $3n^2-n$ <arithmetic operations> under this scalar counting convention, hence **$O(n^2)$**, with $O$ denoting <Big O notation>. The already supplied <QR factorization> is reused; no <matrix inverse> needs to be computed.

Choose the following consistent sign convention. The <Givens rotation> $J(i,j,\theta)$ equals the <identity matrix> except for the block on coordinates $i,j$:
$$
J\big|_{i,j}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus it changes only the $i$th and $j$th coordinates of a <vector>. Write $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. Its two-coordinate block satisfies
$$
\begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}c^2+s^2&0\\0&c^2+s^2\end{pmatrix}=I_2.
$$
All remaining coordinates are unchanged, so $J^TJ=I_n$. Hence **every such <Givens rotation> is an <orthogonal matrix>**. With this convention the two-coordinate <rotation matrix> rotates through angle $-\theta$.

= Solution

Let $r=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_j^2}$. If $r>0$, choose $\theta$ so that
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{x_i}{r},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{x_j}{r}.
$$
Such an angle exists because the two ratios have squared sum one. Then the changed coordinates are
$$
y_i=\frac{x_i^2+x_j^2}{r}=r,\qquad
y_j=-\frac{x_jx_i}{r}+\frac{x_ix_j}{r}=0.
$$
All other coordinates satisfy $y_k=x_k$. If $r=0$, both input coordinates are zero; take $\theta=0$, giving the <identity matrix> on this pair. In all cases,
$$
\boxed{y_i=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_j^2},\qquad y_j=0,\qquad y_k=x_k\ (k\ne i,j).}
$$
This fixes a nonnegative retained coordinate and handles division by zero explicitly. For floating-point computation, the <stable construction of a Givens rotation> uses scaled <Euclidean norm> evaluation to avoid unnecessarily overflowing or underflowing the squares.

Apply the product from right to left, choosing every <Givens rotation> from the current first column. First $J(1,2)$ replaces $(a_1,a_2)$ by $(\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2},0)$. Next $J(1,3)$ combines that retained entry with $a_3$ and sets the third entry to zero. Inductively, after eliminating coordinates $2,\ldots,k$, the first coordinate is
$$
\sqrt{a_1^2+\cdots+a_k^2},
$$
and coordinates $2,\ldots,k$ are zero. Later <Givens rotations> involve only coordinate one and a new coordinate, so those zeros remain zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Q_1a=(\|a\|_2,0,\ldots,0)^T=\|a\|_2e_1,}
$$
where $e_1$ is the first <standard basis> <vector>. The zero <vector> is included by choosing the <identity matrix> for every zero pair. The nonnegative sign follows from the convention fixed in part (c)(ii). This is the first-column stage of <QR decomposition by Givens rotations>.

= Solution

Put $C=Q_1A$ and write its second column as $v$. For $n\geq3$, take
$$
\boxed{Q_2=J(2,n)J(2,n-1)\cdots J(2,3),}
$$
where each angle is computed from the current second column, in the right-to-left application order. These <Givens rotations> eliminate its entries in rows $3,\ldots,n$ by accumulating their <Euclidean norm> in row two.

Each rotation acts only on rows with indices at least two. The first column of $C$ is $\|a\|_2e_1$, whose entries in all those rows are zero. Mixing two zeros leaves them zero, and row one is untouched. Thus the first column is unchanged. The resulting <matrix> has the form
$$
\boxed{Q_2Q_1A=
\begin{pmatrix}
\|a\|_2&v_1\\
0&\sqrt{v_2^2+\cdots+v_n^2}\\
0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}\quad(n\geq3).}
$$
This verifies both required properties, including the case of zero second-column tail. When $n=2$ there are no rows to eliminate: take $Q_2=I_2$, the empty product. No sign change of $v_2$ is required in that case. Each <Givens row-pair update> preserves previously eliminated columns, which is the central invariant of <QR decomposition by Givens rotations>.

= Solution

The <orthonormal> columns satisfy $\|a\|_2=\|b\|_2=1$ and $a^Tb=0$. By part (i), $Q_1a=e_1$. A product of <orthogonal matrices> is an <orthogonal matrix>, since $Q_1^TQ_1=I$. Consequently
$$
0=a^Tb=(Q_1a)^T(Q_1b)=e_1^TQ_1b.
$$
The last expression is the first coordinate of the transformed second column. Thus **the first row is**
$$
\boxed{(Q_1A)_{1,:}=(1,0).}
$$
The one in the first entry uses the nonnegative retained-coordinate convention. A different consistent sign convention could give $(-1,0)$ instead; the second entry must still be zero by preservation of the <inner product>.
