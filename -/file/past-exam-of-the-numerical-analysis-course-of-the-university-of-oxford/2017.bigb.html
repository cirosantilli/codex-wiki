= Solution

For each <interpolation node> define the <Lagrange cardinal polynomial>
$$
\ell_j(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\leq k\leq n\\k\ne j}}\frac{x-x_k}{x_j-x_k},\qquad 0\leq j\leq n.
$$
All denominators are nonzero because the <interpolation nodes> are distinct. Each <polynomial> has <polynomial degree> at most $n$, and $\ell_j(x_i)=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. Consequently the <linear combination>
$$
p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n f_j\ell_j(x)
$$
belongs to the <vector space of univariate polynomials> $\Pi_n$ and has the required values. This proves existence of the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial>.

For uniqueness, suppose $p_n$ and $r_n$ both work. Their difference is a <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$ with $n+1$ distinct <roots of a polynomial>. The <root bound for a polynomial> forces $p_n-r_n$ to be the <zero polynomial>.

The seven supplied values follow $f_i=i^2+1$. The <quadratic polynomial> $x^2+1$ belongs to $\Pi_6$ and takes all seven values, so uniqueness gives
$$
\boxed{p_6(x)=x^2+1.}
$$
The subscript bounds the <polynomial degree>; it does not require <polynomial degree> exactly six.

The <product rule> applied at the <interpolation node> $x_k$ gives
$$
w'(x_k)=\prod_{j\ne k}(x_k-x_j)\ne0,\qquad
\ell_k(x)=\frac{w(x)}{(x-x_k)w'(x_k)}=w(x)\frac{w_k}{x-x_k}.
$$
Thus <Lagrange interpolation> gives, away from the <interpolation nodes>,
$$
p_n(x)=w(x)\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{w_k f_k}{x-x_k}.
$$
Apply the same <Lagrange interpolation> formula to the <constant polynomial> one. Since its <interpolant> is itself,
$$
1=\sum_{k=0}^n\ell_k(x)=w(x)\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{w_k}{x-x_k}.
$$
Dividing the preceding two identities proves the <barycentric interpolation formula>
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n w_k f_k/(x-x_k)}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^n w_k/(x-x_k)}.}
$$
The denominator is exactly $1/w(x)$, so is nonzero away from the <interpolation nodes>. At $x=x_j$, multiply numerator and denominator by $x-x_j$ and take the <limit>: their values tend to $w_jf_j$ and $w_j$, respectively. The <removable singularity> therefore has value $f_j$; in numerical evaluation return the stored value directly at an <interpolation node>.

A <Newton-Cotes quadrature> integrates a <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> at equally spaced <quadrature nodes>. For the <Newton-Cotes closed quadrature>, the endpoints are included: write $x_j=a+jh$, $h=(b-a)/n$, and use
$$
\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\ \approx\ \int_a^bp_n(x)\,dx
=\sum_{j=0}^n W_jf(x_j),\qquad W_j=\int_a^b\ell_j(x)\,dx.
$$
These <quadrature weights> depend on the <quadrature nodes>, not on the sampled <function>. Open <Newton-Cotes quadrature> uses equally spaced interior <quadrature nodes> instead.

For <Simpson's rule>, use $n=2$ and set $x=a+ht$, so $0\leq t\leq2$. The <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> become
$$
L_0(t)=\frac{(t-1)(t-2)}2,\qquad L_1(t)=t(2-t),\qquad L_2(t)=\frac{t(t-1)}2.
$$
Integrating these <quadratic polynomials> gives $1/3$, $4/3$, $1/3$, respectively. The change of variable contributes $h$, so
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\ \approx\ \frac h3\bigl[f(a)+4f(a+h)+f(b)\bigr]
=\frac{b-a}{6}\left[f(a)+4f\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)+f(b)\right].}
$$
Its <degree of precision of a quadrature rule> is three: it integrates every <quadratic polynomial> by construction, and also the cubic nodal <polynomial> $(x-a)(x-a-h)(x-b)$, whose <integral> vanishes by odd symmetry about $a+h$. Any <cubic polynomial> differs from its <quadratic polynomial> <interpolant> by a constant multiple of this nodal <polynomial>.

Read the differential order $dx\,dy$ literally: take <quadrature nodes> $X=(c,(c+d)/2,d)$ in $x$ and $Y=(a,(a+b)/2,b)$ in $y$. Applying <Simpson's rule> first in $x$ and then in $y$ multiplies the one-dimensional <quadrature weights>. With $s=(1,4,1)$, this <tensor-product quadrature> is
$$
Q(g)=\frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{36}\sum_{i=0}^2\sum_{j=0}^2s_i s_jg(X_i,Y_j).
$$
Equivalently, the nine sampled <function> values have the <quadrature weight> array
$$
\frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{36}
\begin{pmatrix}1&4&1\\4&16&4\\1&4&1\end{pmatrix},
$$
with rows indexed by the $x$ <quadrature nodes> and columns by the $y$ <quadrature nodes>. The <quadrature weights> sum to the <rectangle>'s area. If the intended coordinate assignment is instead $x\in[a,b]$, $y\in[c,d]$, exchange $X$ and $Y$ in the formula. Both conventions give the same calculation on the unit square used below.

For the <separable integrand>, <tensor-product quadrature> factors into two one-dimensional applications of <Simpson's rule>:
$$
Q_x\bigl(x(1-x)\bigr)=\frac16\left[0+4\cdot\frac14+0\right]=\frac16,
\qquad
Q_y(y^q)=\frac{1+4\,2^{-q}}6,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{Q(g)=\frac{1+2^{2-q}}{36}.}
$$
The exact <double integral>, evaluated by <Fubini's theorem> and the <power rule for integration>, is
$$
I(g)=\left(\int_0^1x(1-x)\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^1y^q\,dy\right)
=\frac{1}{6(q+1)}.
$$
The $x$ factor is always integrated exactly. All <quadrature error> therefore comes from applying <Simpson's rule> to the $y$ factor.

<Gaussian elimination> uses <elementary row operations> to remove entries below successive <pivot elements>, reducing a <linear system> to one with an <upper triangular matrix>. <Back substitution> then solves that system. It also computes a reusable <matrix factorization>, so additional right-hand sides can be handled by <forward substitution> and <back substitution> without repeating the elimination.

For elimination without row exchanges, let $A^{(1)}=A$. At step $k$, provided the <pivot element> $a_{kk}^{(k)}$ is nonzero, form the <elimination multipliers>
$$
m_{ik}=\frac{a_{ik}^{(k)}}{a_{kk}^{(k)}},\qquad i=k+1,\ldots,n,
$$
and perform $\text{row}_i\leftarrow\text{row}_i-m_{ik}\text{row}_k$, applying the same operation to the right-hand side. More explicitly the resulting entries satisfy $a_{ij}^{(k+1)}=a_{ij}^{(k)}-m_{ik}a_{kj}^{(k)}$ for $i>k$, $j\geq k$.

Let $E_k=I-\sum_{i>k}m_{ik}e_i e_k^T$, where $e_i$ are the <standard basis> <vectors>. Then
$$
U=E_{n-1}\cdots E_1A,\qquad
A=LU,\qquad L=E_1^{-1}\cdots E_{n-1}^{-1}.
$$
The <matrix> $L$ is a <unit lower triangular matrix> storing the <elimination multipliers>, while $U$ is an <upper triangular matrix>:
$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&\cdots&0\\
m_{21}&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
m_{n1}&m_{n2}&\cdots&1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U=\begin{pmatrix}
u_{11}&u_{12}&\cdots&u_{1n}\\
0&u_{22}&\cdots&u_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&u_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Solving $Ax=b$ becomes $Ly=b$ followed by $Ux=y$. If a required <pivot element> vanishes, an unpermuted <LU decomposition> can fail even for an <invertible matrix>. Row exchanges, typically selected by <partial pivoting>, instead produce $PA=LU$ with a <permutation matrix> $P$; then solve $Ly=Pb$, $Ux=y$.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Since every $u_{jj}>0$, the <diagonal matrix> $D$ is invertible. Set
$$
\widehat U=D^{-1}U,\qquad
\widehat u_{ij}=\frac{u_{ij}}{u_{ii}}.
$$
The division is by the diagonal entry of row $i$. Thus $\widehat U$ is an <upper triangular matrix> with diagonal entries one and $A=LD\widehat U$.

To use symmetry without assuming the conclusion, multiply by the <matrix inverse> of $L$ and its <transpose>:
$$
M=L^{-1}AL^{-T}=D\widehat U L^{-T}.
$$
The <matrix> $M$ is symmetric because $A$ is a <symmetric matrix>. It is also an <upper triangular matrix>, since every factor on the right is an <upper triangular matrix>. A symmetric <upper triangular matrix> is diagonal. Its diagonal is exactly $D$, because $\widehat U$ and $L^{-T}$ have diagonal entries one. Hence $M=D$, giving
$$
\boxed{A=LDL^T,\qquad \widehat U=L^T.}
$$
This is the <LDL decomposition>.

Take the positive <square roots> of the diagonal entries:
$$
D^{1/2}=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{u_{11}},\ldots,\sqrt{u_{nn}}),\qquad
\widehat L=LD^{1/2}.
$$
The <matrix> $\widehat L$ is a <lower triangular matrix> with positive diagonal, and
$$
\boxed{A=\widehat L\widehat L^T.}
$$
This is a <Cholesky decomposition>. For a nonzero <vector> $x$, the <vector> $\widehat L^Tx$ is nonzero because $\widehat L^T$ is invertible. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x^TAx=(\widehat L^Tx)^T(\widehat L^Tx)=\|\widehat L^Tx\|_2^2>0.}
$$
Here $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the <Euclidean norm>. Thus $A$ is a <positive-definite matrix>.

= Solution

For a real square <matrix>, a <QR factorization> writes $B=QR$ with $Q$ an <orthogonal matrix>, $Q^TQ=I$, and $R$ an <upper triangular matrix>. If $B$ is invertible, so is $R$, and
$$
Bx=b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Rx=Q^Tb.
$$
Compute $Q^Tb$ and use <back substitution>; no <matrix inverse> needs to be formed.

Apply the <Gram-Schmidt process> to the columns $b_1,b_2,b_3$. The first column has <Euclidean norm> two, giving $q_1=(1,0,0)^T$ and $r_{11}=2$. For the second column,
$$
r_{12}=q_1^Tb_2=1,\qquad b_2-r_{12}q_1=(0,4,3)^T,
\qquad r_{22}=5,\qquad q_2=(0,4/5,3/5)^T.
$$
For the third column,
$$
r_{13}=q_1^Tb_3=0,\qquad r_{23}=q_2^Tb_3=7,
\qquad b_3-7q_2=(0,-3/5,4/5)^T.
$$
This residual already has <Euclidean norm> one. Set $q_3=(0,-3/5,4/5)^T$ and $r_{33}=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&4/5&-3/5\\0&3/5&4/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&5&7\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The columns of $Q$ are <orthonormal>, and <matrix multiplication> verifies $QR=B$.

Using precisely this <QR factorization>, transform the right-hand side:
$$
Q^T\begin{pmatrix}-3\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\(4+6)/5\\(-3+8)/5\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
<Back substitution> in $Rx=Q^Tb$ gives
$$
x_3=1,\qquad 5x_2+7=2\ \Longrightarrow\ x_2=-1,
\qquad 2x_1-1=-3\ \Longrightarrow\ x_1=-1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{x=(-1,-1,1)^T.}
$$

A <linear spline> is a <continuous function> whose restriction to every <interval> $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ is a <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most one. With $h_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$ and $\ell_i=\ell(x_i)$, <linear interpolation> gives
$$
\ell(x)=\frac{x_i-x}{h_i}\ell_{i-1}+\frac{x-x_{i-1}}{h_i}\ell_i,
\qquad x_{i-1}\leq x\leq x_i.
$$
Integrating the two <linear polynomials> and adding the contributions gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{x_0}^{x_n}\ell(x)\,dx=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{h_i}{2}\bigl(\ell_{i-1}+\ell_i\bigr).}
$$
This is the <composite trapezoidal rule> applied to the nodal values, and it is exact for the <linear spline> itself, including nonuniform <spline knots>.

A <cubic spline> on this <partition of an interval> is a <function> $S\in C^2[x_0,x_n]$ that restricts to a <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most three on every <interval> $[x_{i-1},x_i]$. Thus its <second derivative> is continuous and piecewise of <polynomial degree> at most one: **$S''$ is a <linear spline> on the same <spline knots>**. Its <third derivative> is constant on each open <interval> and can jump across the <spline knots>; its <fourth derivative> vanishes inside each open <interval>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Put $E=T-S$. Matching <interpolation> data and endpoint <derivatives> gives
$$
E(x_i)=0\quad(0\leq i\leq n),\qquad E'(x_0)=E'(x_n)=0.
$$
The endpoint slope constraints make $S$ a <clamped cubic spline>. The decisive step in its <minimum roughness property of a clamped cubic spline> is the <orthogonality> identity
$$
\int_{x_0}^{x_n}S''(x)E''(x)\,dx=0.
$$
Prove it separately on each <interval>. Two applications of <integration by parts>, using the vanishing <fourth derivative> of the cubic piece, give
$$
\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}S''E''\,dx
=[S''E']_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}-[S'''E]_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}
=[S''E']_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}.
$$
The second boundary term is zero because $E$ vanishes at both endpoints of this <interval>. Sum over $i$. At each interior <spline knot>, the two values of $S''E'$ agree because both factors are continuous, so the contributions cancel. The remaining outer boundary terms vanish because $E'$ is zero at both outer endpoints. This proves the identity.

Expanding the square now gives the stronger exact equality
$$
\boxed{\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(T'')^2\,dx
=\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(S'')^2\,dx
+\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(T''-S'')^2\,dx.}
$$
The last <integral> is nonnegative, proving the required inequality. It measures the excess <second derivative roughness penalty> of $T$ over $S$.

The argument applies directly to twice continuously differentiable competitors. More generally it holds when $T'$ is an <absolutely continuous function> and $T''$ is <square-integrable>, the usual finite-energy class for this <second derivative roughness penalty>. Mere existence of pointwise <second derivatives> does not by itself guarantee that all displayed <integrals> exist; the variational statement is understood for admissible competitors with this regularity.

Let $R$ be a second <cubic spline> matching the same values and endpoint <derivatives>. The <minimum roughness property of a clamped cubic spline> applied with $S$ as the minimizing <cubic spline> gives $J(R)\geq J(S)$, where $J(u)=\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(u'')^2\,dx$. Applying the same argument with their roles exchanged gives $J(S)\geq J(R)$. Hence equality holds, and the exact identity from part (i) yields
$$
\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(R''-S'')^2\,dx=0.
$$
The <integrand> is continuous and nonnegative. If it were positive somewhere, it would have a positive <integral> over a small <interval>. It follows that $R''=S''$ everywhere. Consequently $R-S$ is an <affine function>. Its value and <derivative> vanish at $x_0$, so it is identically zero. Thus $R=S$, proving uniqueness of the <clamped cubic spline> with the prescribed data. Existence was assumed in the question; this argument establishes the remaining uniqueness claim.

= Solution

Fix $i$, write $\alpha=x_{i-1}$, $\beta=x_i$, and let $E=T-S$ on this <interval>. The <Hermite interpolation> conditions give both
$$
E(\alpha)=E(\beta)=0,\qquad E'(\alpha)=E'(\beta)=0.
$$
Two applications of <integration by parts> therefore give
$$
\int_\alpha^\beta E T^{(4)}\,dx
=[ET'''-E'T'']_\alpha^\beta+\int_\alpha^\beta E''T''\,dx
=\int_\alpha^\beta E''T''\,dx.
$$
On this single cubic piece, the same calculation also gives
$$
\int_\alpha^\beta E''S''\,dx
=[E'S''-ES''']_\alpha^\beta+\int_\alpha^\beta ES^{(4)}\,dx=0.
$$
Here $S^{(4)}=0$ inside the <interval>, and all four endpoint values of $E,E'$ are zero. Since $T''=E''+S''$, we obtain the exact identity
$$
\boxed{\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}(T-S)T^{(4)}\,dx
=\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}(T''-S'')^2\,dx\geq0.}
$$
This is an <integral> inequality, with no assertion that the <integrand> on the left has one sign pointwise. The stronger nodal <derivative> conditions make the boundary terms vanish on each <interval> separately; part (i) only needed their cancellation after summing.

The stated <Hermite interpolating polynomial> exists uniquely in $\Pi_{2n+1}$. Indeed, a <polynomial> in the <kernel of a linear map> of the value-and-<derivative> data map has a double <root of a polynomial> at each of $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes>. A nonzero such <polynomial> would have <polynomial degree> at least $2n+2$, which is impossible. The map is therefore an <injective function> and a <linear map> between <vector spaces> of the same finite <dimension of a vector space>, $2n+2$, and hence is a <linear isomorphism>. No higher smoothness of the original <function> $f$ is required to construct it.

An explicit illustration uses <interpolation nodes> $0,1,2$, values $0,1,0$, and zero <derivatives> at every <interpolation node>. The <clamped cubic spline> is $S(x)=3x^2-2x^3$ on $[0,1]$ and its reflection on $[1,2]$. Its <Hermite interpolating polynomial> is the <quartic polynomial> $T(x)=x^2(2-x)^2$. On $[0,1]$, $T-S=x^2(1-x)^2$, and the corresponding reflected formula holds on $[1,2]$. Direct <integration> gives $J(S)=24$, $J(T)=128/5$, and $J(T-S)=8/5$, exactly as the energy identity predicts.
