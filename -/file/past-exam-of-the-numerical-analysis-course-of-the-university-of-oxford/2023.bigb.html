= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

For a complex $n\times n$ <matrix> $B=(b_{ij})$, put
$$
R_i=\sum_{j\ne i}|b_{ij}|,\qquad
D_i=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-b_{ii}|\leq R_i\}.
$$
The <Gershgorin circle theorem> states that
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{spec}(B)\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^nD_i.}
$$
To prove it, let $Bv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, and choose $i$ for which $|v_i|=\max_j|v_j|>0$. The $i$th coordinate of the <eigenvalue equation> gives
$$
(\lambda-b_{ii})v_i=\sum_{j\ne i}b_{ij}v_j.
$$
The <triangle inequality>, followed by division by $|v_i|$, gives
$$
|\lambda-b_{ii}|\leq\sum_{j\ne i}|b_{ij}|\frac{|v_j|}{|v_i|}
\leq\sum_{j\ne i}|b_{ij}|=R_i.
$$
Thus $\lambda\in D_i$, proving the assertion for every <eigenvalue>.

The second theorem, <Gershgorin eigenvalue counting>, says that if a union of $k$ indexed <Gershgorin discs> is disjoint from the remaining $n-k$ <Gershgorin discs>, that union contains exactly $k$ <eigenvalues>, counted with <algebraic multiplicity>. In particular,
$$
\boxed{\text{An isolated Gershgorin disc contains exactly one simple eigenvalue.}}
$$
Discs that touch are not disjoint; overlapping <Gershgorin discs> cannot individually be assigned one <eigenvalue> each.

The <power iteration> starts from $v_0\ne0$, normally scaled to unit <Euclidean norm>, and repeats
$$
w_{k+1}=Av_k,\qquad
\boxed{v_{k+1}=\frac{w_{k+1}}{\|w_{k+1}\|_2}.}
$$
Normalization prevents the rapidly growing or decaying magnitudes of $A^kv_0$ from obscuring its direction. The iteration requires $w_{k+1}\ne0$.

Because $A$ is a <diagonalizable matrix>, choose an <eigenbasis> $u_1,\ldots,u_n$, with corresponding <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1=\lambda,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n$, and expand $v_0=\sum_jc_ju_j$. First suppose the <dominant eigenvalue> is simple and $c_1\ne0$. Then
$$
A^kv_0
=c_1\lambda^k\left(u_1+\sum_{j=2}^n\frac{c_j}{c_1}
\left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda}\right)^ku_j\right).
$$
For
$$
q=\max_{j\geq2}\left|\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda}\right|<1,
$$
the sum in parentheses after $u_1$ is $O(q^k)$ in <Big O notation>. Consequently the <power iteration direction error> is $O(q^k)$: the iterates approach the line spanned by $u_1$. For a real <matrix>, a uniquely dominant <eigenvalue> is real, since otherwise its complex conjugate would have the same <modulus>. If $\lambda>0$, $v_k$ approaches one unit <eigenvector>; if $\lambda<0$, successive $v_k$ alternate signs. In either case,
$$
\boxed{\min_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}}
\left\|v_k-\sigma\frac{u_1}{\|u_1\|_2}\right\|_2=O(q^k).}
$$
This is the <sign and phase in power iteration> qualification. Also, the necessary assumption is a nonzero coefficient $c_1$ in the <eigenbasis>, rather than merely a nonzero ordinary <inner product> with $u_1$: those tests can differ for a <nonsymmetric matrix>. If $c_1=0$, the method cannot generate the missing component.

If the dominant value $\lambda$ has a higher-dimensional <eigenspace>, the same argument replaces $c_1u_1$ by the nonzero sum of all starting components in that <eigenspace>. The limiting direction is then that of this sum, which is still an <eigenvector> for $\lambda$. The usual algorithm assumes $\lambda\ne0$; a strict dominance gap guarantees this whenever another <eigenvalue> is present.

An estimate retaining the sign of $\lambda$ is the <Rayleigh quotient>
$$
\boxed{\widehat\lambda_k=\frac{v_k^TAv_k}{v_k^Tv_k}\longrightarrow\lambda.}
$$
Continuity of this quotient at a nonzero <eigenvector> proves the limit even when $A$ is a <nonsymmetric matrix>. Alternatively, $(Av_k)_i/(v_k)_i\to\lambda$ for a coordinate whose limiting <eigenvector> component is nonzero. The scaling factor $\|Av_k\|_2$ alone tends only to $|\lambda|$.

Choose a shift $\mu$ which is not an <eigenvalue>. <Inverse iteration> applies <power iteration> to $B=(A-\mu I)^{-1}$, implemented by a <linear system> solve rather than by forming an explicit <matrix inverse>:
$$
\boxed{(A-\mu I)w_{k+1}=v_k,\qquad
v_{k+1}=\frac{w_{k+1}}{\|w_{k+1}\|_2}.}
$$
A single <LU decomposition> of $A-\mu I$ can be reused for all the solves at this fixed shift. An <eigenpair> $Au_j=\lambda_ju_j$ transforms into
$$
Bu_j=\frac1{\lambda_j-\mu}u_j.
$$
Thus an <eigenvalue> $\lambda_*$ uniquely closest to $\mu$ becomes the <dominant eigenvalue> of $B$. For a <diagonalizable matrix>, the argument in the preceding subpart gives <convergence of diagonalizable fixed-shift inverse iteration>, provided the starting coefficient in the target <eigenspace> is nonzero, with <power iteration direction error> $O(q_\mu^k)$, where
$$
\boxed{q_\mu=
\frac{|\lambda_*-\mu|}{\min_{j:\lambda_j\ne\lambda_*}|\lambda_j-\mu|}<1.}
$$
For any prescribed <simple eigenvalue>, taking a sufficiently close but unequal shift makes the numerator small while the denominator stays close to its positive separation from the remaining <eigenvalues>. This makes $q_\mu$ small and gives very fast convergence per iteration. The <Rayleigh quotient> of $v_k$ estimates $\lambda_*$ directly. If $\beta_k$ estimates the associated <eigenvalue> of $B$, the corresponding estimate is $\mu+\beta_k^{-1}$.

The selection principle also applies when the other <eigenvalues> have nontrivial blocks in the <Jordan normal form>: their contributions acquire powers of $k$, multiplying strictly smaller geometric factors. A simple uniquely closest <eigenvalue> still attracts the direction when the <Riesz projection> of $v_0$ onto its generalized <eigenspace> is nonzero. For nonreal target <eigenvalues>, allow complex shifts and arithmetic; a real shift cannot distinguish a conjugate pair of equal distances. Setting $\mu=\lambda_*$ makes the solve singular, and an exactly zero starting target component prevents convergence to that target in exact arithmetic. When $\lambda_*<\mu$ is real, account for the <sign behavior of fixed-shift inverse iteration>.

The four row <Gershgorin discs> have centres $9,3,-4,-5$ and radii $1,2,2,1$, respectively. Their real-axis intersections are
$$
[8,10],\qquad[1,5],\qquad[-6,-2],\qquad[-6,-4].
$$
None of the <Gershgorin discs> contains zero, so the <Gershgorin circle theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{0\notin\operatorname{spec}(A),\quad A\text{ is nonsingular}.}
$$
The discs centred at $9$ and $3$ are each isolated. By <Gershgorin eigenvalue counting>, they contain exactly one <eigenvalue> apiece, and both <eigenvalues> are simple. Each must be real: the <characteristic polynomial> is real, so a nonreal root would bring a distinct conjugate root into the same disc. Denote these <eigenvalues> by $\beta\in[8,10]$ and $\alpha\in[1,5]$. The remaining two <eigenvalues> lie in the union of the negative-centred discs.

For the shift $4$, the distances satisfy
$$
|\alpha-4|\leq3,\qquad |\beta-4|\geq4,
\qquad |\gamma-4|\geq6
$$
for either of the other <eigenvalues> $\gamma$, since its real part is at most $-2$. Thus $\alpha$ is uniquely closest to $4$. Once $4$ is checked not to be an <eigenvalue>, the natural full target-disc <interval enclosure> and a conservative asymptotic direction factor are
$$
\boxed{[a,b]=[1,5],\qquad q_4\leq\frac34.}
$$
As always, <inverse iteration> requires a nonzero initial target component. The following calculation verifies the shift and gives sharper information.

For the <tridiagonal matrix>, let $p_j(t)$ be the <determinant> of the leading $j\times j$ block of $tI-A$. Expansion along the last row gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_0(t)&=1,&p_1(t)&=t-9,\\
p_2(t)&=(t-3)(t-9)+1=t^2-12t+28,\\
p_3(t)&=(t+4)p_2(t)-p_1(t)=t^3-8t^2-21t+121,\\
p_4(t)&=(t+5)p_3(t)-p_2(t)
=t^4-3t^3-62t^2+28t+577.
\end{aligned}
$$
In particular, $\det A=577$, $p_4(4)=-239$ and $p_4(5)=-583$. Both shifted <linear systems> are nonsingular. Further exact evaluations are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
t&-6&-5&-4&-3&3&4&8&9\\\hline
p_4(t)&121&-113&-79&97&103&-239&-607&181.
\end{array}
$$
The <intermediate value theorem> gives a real root in each of $(-6,-5)$, $(-4,-3)$, $(3,4)$ and $(8,9)$. As $p_4$ has <polynomial degree> four, these are all its roots and each is simple. In particular, $A$ is a <diagonalizable matrix>, $\alpha\in(3,4)$ and $\beta\in(8,9)$. The closest competitor to $\alpha$ at the shift $4$ is $\beta$, so this refinement yields
$$
\boxed{\alpha\in[3,4],\qquad q_4=\frac{4-\alpha}{\beta-4}<\frac14.}
$$
For the shift $5$, the original <Gershgorin discs> only give $|\alpha-5|\leq4$ and $|\beta-5|\geq3$; those bounds do not decide which is closer. The refined <interval enclosures> do:
$$
1<5-\alpha<2,\qquad3<\beta-5<4,
\qquad|\gamma-5|>8
$$
for the negative <eigenvalues>. Therefore the answer for this particular <matrix> is yes:
$$
\boxed{\mu=5:\quad\lambda_*=\alpha\in[3,4],\qquad
q_5=\frac{5-\alpha}{\beta-5}<\frac23.}
$$
Both shifts select the same <eigenvector> line and alternate signs because $\alpha<\mu$. The factor bounds concern asymptotic directional convergence, rather than a universal error reduction from the first iterate for a <nonsymmetric matrix>. The wider interval $[1,5]$ remains a valid full-disc enclosure for both shifts; $[3,4]$ records the stronger information obtained from the <characteristic polynomial>.

For the distinct <interpolation nodes>, define the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>
$$
L_i(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j},\qquad i=0,\ldots,n.
$$
Every denominator is nonzero, each $L_i$ has <polynomial degree> at most $n$, and $L_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Hence the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial>
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n f_iL_i(x)}
$$
belongs to $\Pi_n$ and has $p_n(x_j)=f_j$. This proves existence.

If $p_n$ and $\widetilde p_n$ both fit the data, their difference has <polynomial degree> at most $n$ but vanishes at the $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes>. Repeated application of the <factor theorem> implies that $\prod_{j=0}^n(x-x_j)$ divides that difference. A nonzero <polynomial> of degree at most $n$ cannot have such a divisor of degree $n+1$. Thus $p_n-\widetilde p_n=0$, proving uniqueness. When $n=0$, the empty product defining $L_0$ is $1$, so the argument includes the constant case.

The <monomial> $x^k$ lies in $\Pi_n$ and already fits the data. Uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=x^k.}
$$
Apply this result with a nonnegative integer $m\leq n$. Its <Lagrange interpolation> representation is the <polynomial identity>
$$
x^m=\sum_{i=0}^n x_i^m
\frac{\prod_{j\ne i}(x-x_j)}{\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)}.
$$
The numerator in each summand is a <monic polynomial> of degree $n$, so the coefficient of $x^n$ on the right is exactly $\sum_i x_i^m/\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)$. The coefficient on the left is zero for $m<n$ and one for $m=n$. Comparing coefficients proves the <Lagrange leading-coefficient identity>
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{x_i^m}{\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)}
=\begin{cases}0,&0\leq m<n,\\1,&m=n.\end{cases}}
$$
The intended exponent range is $m\geq0$: arbitrary negative exponents would not give this <polynomial identity>. For $m=0$, $x_i^0$ denotes the value of the constant <polynomial> $1$, including at a zero <interpolation node>.

Clearing the denominators in the <rational interpolation> conditions gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^m a_jx_i^j+
f_i\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}b_jx_i^j=f_ix_i^\ell,
\qquad i=0,\ldots,n.}
$$
These are $n+1$ linear equations for $(m+1)+\ell=n+1$ unknowns. The $i$th row of the coefficient <matrix>, in the order $a_0,\ldots,a_m,b_0,\ldots,b_{\ell-1}$, is
$$
\left(1,x_i,\ldots,x_i^m,\ f_i,f_ix_i,\ldots,f_ix_i^{\ell-1}\right).
$$
This <rational interpolation linearization> is equivalent to the original fraction conditions only if $r(x_i)\ne0$ at every <interpolation node>. If $r(x_i)=0$, the cleared equation merely forces $q(x_i)=0$, leaving the fraction undefined.

For $f_i=x_i^{m+1}$, the coefficient row becomes
$$
(1,x_i,\ldots,x_i^m,x_i^{m+1},\ldots,x_i^{m+\ell})
=(1,x_i,\ldots,x_i^n).
$$
This is the <Vandermonde matrix>, whose <determinant> is $\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq n}(x_j-x_i)\ne0$. Equivalently, a vector in its <null space> would define a degree-at-most-$n$ <polynomial> vanishing at all $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes>, which is zero by the preceding uniqueness proof. Thus the cleared <linear system> has exactly one solution.

There is also a constructive description of that solution. Define the <monic polynomial>
$$
W(x)=\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i).
$$
The <polynomial> $x^{m+1}r(x)-q(x)$ is monic of degree $n+1$ and vanishes at every <interpolation node>. It must therefore equal $W(x)$. By <polynomial division>, write uniquely
$$
W(x)=x^{m+1}R(x)+S(x),\qquad\deg S\leq m.
$$
The quotient $R$ is monic of degree $\ell$, and
$$
\boxed{r(x)=R(x),\qquad q(x)=-S(x)}
$$
is the unique coefficient solution. This does not assert that $R$ is nonzero at every <interpolation node>.

For a counterexample with $m=0$, $\ell=1$, choose $x_0=0,x_1=1$ and the specified data $f_i=x_i$, so $f_0=0,f_1=1$. The cleared equations reduce to
$$
a_0=0,\qquad a_0+b_0=1,
$$
giving the unique solution $q(x)=0$, $r(x)=x-1$. But at $x_1=1$ the quotient is $0/0$. Indeed, any genuine <rational interpolant> of this prescribed form would need $r(0)\ne0$; then $q(0)/r(0)=0$ forces the constant numerator to be zero. At a nonzero denominator its value at $1$ would also be zero, never one. Cancelling the common factor in the cleared candidate would merely give the identically zero <rational function>, which still fails the data. Hence
$$
\boxed{(x_0,f_0)=(0,0),\ (x_1,f_1)=(1,1):
\quad\text{unique cleared solution, but no rational interpolant}.}
$$

Integrate the <ordinary differential equation> across one step, using the <fundamental theorem of calculus>:
$$
y(x_{n+1})-y(x_n)=\int_{x_n}^{x_n+h}f(x,y(x))\,dx.
$$
The <midpoint quadrature rule> approximates the integral by $h f(x_n+h/2,y(x_n+h/2))$. Approximate the unknown midpoint state by the midpoint value of <linear interpolation> between the endpoint states:
$$
y(x_n+h/2)\approx\frac{y_n+y_{n+1}}2.
$$
Combining these approximations gives the <implicit midpoint rule>
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=y_n+h f\left(x_n+\frac h2,\frac{y_n+y_{n+1}}2\right),
\qquad y_0=\alpha.}
$$
It is an <implicit time-stepping method> because its new state occurs inside the evaluation of $f$. An equivalent derivation uses a straight-line approximation on the step and imposes the <ordinary differential equation> at its midpoint, a one-stage <collocation method>.

Work on a fixed finite interval $[a,a+T]$. Write $Y_n=y(x_n)$, $t=x_n+h/2$, and use the paper's <normalized local truncation error>
$$
\tau_n=\frac{Y_{n+1}-Y_n}{h}
-f\left(t,\frac{Y_{n+1}+Y_n}2\right).
$$
Assume $y$ has bounded continuous <derivatives> through order three on this interval, with $\|y''\|\leq M_2$ and $\|y'''\|\leq M_3$. Assume also that the <Jacobian matrix> $f_y$ has <operator norm> at most $L$ on the segments joining $y(t)$ to the endpoint averages. For a scalar equation this last hypothesis is simply $|\partial f/\partial y|\leq L$ there.

Two symmetric estimates separate the errors. By <Taylor's theorem> applied to $y'$ about $t$, the linear term integrates to zero on $[-h/2,h/2]$, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\frac{Y_{n+1}-Y_n}{h}-y'(t)\right\|
&=\left\|\frac1h\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\bigl(y'(t+s)-y'(t)\bigr)\,ds\right\|\\
&\leq\frac{M_3}{2h}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}s^2\,ds
=\frac{M_3h^2}{24}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Likewise the two linear terms in the endpoint expansions cancel, and their second-order remainders give
$$
\left\|\frac{Y_{n+1}+Y_n}2-y(t)\right\|\leq\frac{M_2h^2}{8}.
$$
The derivative bound on $f$ gives a <Lipschitz condition> in its second variable. Since $y'(t)=f(t,y(t))$, the <triangle inequality> now yields
$$
\boxed{\|\tau_n\|\leq
\left(\frac{M_3}{24}+\frac{LM_2}{8}\right)h^2
=Kh^2.}
$$
The constant is independent of $n$ and $h$ as long as the steps remain in the fixed interval. This <implicit midpoint consistency bound> proves <numerical consistency> of order two. The corresponding unnormalized residual is $h\tau_n=O(h^3)$; the two powers conventionally called <local truncation error> differ because the paper divides by the step size. If local error instead means the error of an exactly solved numerical step started at $Y_n$, the small-step <Lipschitz condition> gives the bound $h\|\tau_n\|/(1-hL/2)=O(h^3)$ as well.

Let $f$ be continuous in its first variable and uniformly <Lipschitz continuous> in its second variable, with constant $L\geq0$. Retain the finite-interval smoothness assumptions and the bound $\|\tau_n\|\leq Kh^2$ from the preceding subpart. We first establish solvability of each implicit step. For a proposed starting state $u$, the map
$$
G_u(v)=u+h f\left(x_n+\frac h2,\frac{u+v}2\right)
$$
has <Lipschitz constant> $hL/2$. If $hL/2<1$, the <contraction mapping theorem> on the complete state space gives a unique next state $v=\Psi_{n,h}(u)$.

To compute a useful <Lipschitz constant> for the method, introduce its increment function by
$$
\Psi_{n,h}(u)=u+h\Phi_{n,h}(u),\qquad
\Phi_{n,h}(u)=f\left(x_n+\frac h2,u+\frac h2\Phi_{n,h}(u)\right).
$$
For two starting states $u,w$,
$$
\|\Phi_{n,h}(u)-\Phi_{n,h}(w)\|
\leq L\|u-w\|+\frac{hL}{2}
\|\Phi_{n,h}(u)-\Phi_{n,h}(w)\|.
$$
Rearranging gives the <implicit midpoint Lipschitz estimate>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Lip}(\Phi_{n,h})\leq\frac{L}{1-hL/2},
\qquad
\operatorname{Lip}(\Psi_{n,h})\leq
R_h:=\frac{1+hL/2}{1-hL/2}.}
$$
Thus, for example, $hL\leq1$ gives an increment <Lipschitz constant> at most $2L$, uniformly as $h\to0$.

For an explicit <global error> estimate, set $e_n=Y_n-y_n$. Subtract the numerical formula from the exact-value identity containing $h\tau_n$:
$$
e_{n+1}=e_n+h\left[
f\left(x_n+\frac h2,\frac{Y_n+Y_{n+1}}2\right)
-f\left(x_n+\frac h2,\frac{y_n+y_{n+1}}2\right)\right]+h\tau_n.
$$
The <Lipschitz condition> and the <triangle inequality> imply
$$
(1-hL/2)\|e_{n+1}\|
\leq(1+hL/2)\|e_n\|+h\|\tau_n\|,
$$
and therefore
$$
\|e_{n+1}\|\leq R_h\|e_n\|+
\frac{Kh^3}{1-hL/2}.
$$
Iterating this inequality is the elementary <discrete Gronwall inequality> argument. For $L>0$, summing the <geometric series> and using $R_h-1=hL/(1-hL/2)$ gives
$$
\|e_n\|\leq R_h^n\|e_0\|+
\frac{Kh^2}{L}(R_h^n-1).
$$
For $hL\leq1$, $R_h=1+hL/(1-hL/2)\leq1+2hL\leq e^{2hL}$. If $nh\leq T$, this proves
$$
\boxed{\max_{nh\leq T}\|e_n\|
\leq e^{2LT}\|e_0\|+
\frac{K}{L}(e^{2LT}-1)h^2.}
$$
With the exact initial value, $e_0=0$, the <global error> is $O(h^2)$ and tends uniformly to zero on every such finite interval. If $L=0$, the recurrence instead gives $\|e_n\|\leq\|e_0\|+KTh^2$, with the same conclusion. Starting values merely converging to the exact initial value also suffice for <numerical convergence>; second-order accuracy requires their errors to be $O(h^2)$. The result concerns exact implicit solves and convergence on fixed finite intervals, not uniform accuracy over an unbounded time interval.

Applying the <implicit midpoint rule> to the <simple harmonic oscillator> system gives
$$
y_{n+1}-y_n=\frac h2(z_{n+1}+z_n),\qquad
z_{n+1}-z_n=-\frac h2(y_{n+1}+y_n).
$$
Factor the change in the <quadratic form> $y^2+z^2$ and substitute these equations:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&(y_{n+1}^2+z_{n+1}^2)-(y_n^2+z_n^2)\\
&=(y_{n+1}-y_n)(y_{n+1}+y_n)
+(z_{n+1}-z_n)(z_{n+1}+z_n)\\
&=\frac h2(z_{n+1}+z_n)(y_{n+1}+y_n)
-\frac h2(y_{n+1}+y_n)(z_{n+1}+z_n)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus every step preserves the <first integral>
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}^2+z_{n+1}^2=y_n^2+z_n^2
=y_0^2+z_0^2.}
$$
For completeness, the implicit equations are solvable for every real $h$: putting $s=h/2$, their coefficient <matrix> has <determinant> $1+s^2>0$ and the update is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}y_{n+1}\\z_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}
=\frac1{1+s^2}
\begin{pmatrix}1-s^2&2s\\-2s&1-s^2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}y_n\\z_n\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This is an <orthogonal matrix>, as follows from $(1-s^2)^2+4s^2=(1+s^2)^2$. It therefore preserves the <Euclidean norm>, explaining geometrically the exact energy conservation. This is a concrete instance of <Runge-Kutta conservation of quadratic invariants>. Preservation of the circle does not assert that the numerical and exact solutions have the same phase at a fixed nonzero step size.
