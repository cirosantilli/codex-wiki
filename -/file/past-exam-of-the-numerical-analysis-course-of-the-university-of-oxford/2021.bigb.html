There are $n+2$ distinct <interpolation nodes> but only $n+1$ free <coefficients> in a <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$. Thus <polynomial interpolation> is possible precisely when the data satisfy one compatibility equation. To identify it, form the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> through all the data:
$$
q(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}f_i
\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n+1\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
$$
This is the unique <interpolation polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $n+1$. Its <leading coefficient>, which is also the order-$(n+1)$ <divided difference>, is
$$
D=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}\frac{f_i}
{\displaystyle\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n+1\\j\ne i}}(x_i-x_j)}.
$$
Consequently the required condition is
$$
\boxed{D=0.}
$$
Necessity follows because any qualifying $p_n$ must equal $q$ by uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation>; sufficiency follows because $D=0$ removes the only possible term of <polynomial degree> $n+1$ from $q$.

When this condition holds, a slightly shorter expression uses only the first $n+1$ <interpolation nodes>:
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_i
\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.}
$$
This <polynomial> agrees with $q$ at $n+1$ distinct points, so the <root bound for a polynomial> gives $p_n=q$ and hence $p_n(x_{n+1})=f_{n+1}$ as required. Equivalently, the compatibility condition says that this expression, evaluated at the last <interpolation node>, gives $f_{n+1}$.

Write $\mathcal P_n$ for the <vector space> of <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$, and put $p_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j$. As the question calls the weighted integral an <inner product>, its finiteness is implicit; for example, a positive integrable weight $w$ suffices for all the <continuous functions> involved here.

For every $q\in\mathcal P_n$, differentiating the squared <weighted norm> $\|f-p_n-tq\|_w^2$ at its minimum $t=0$ gives the <orthogonality> equations
$$
\langle f-p_n,q\rangle_w=0.
$$
In particular, the requested <linear system> is
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^n a_j\int_{-1}^1w(x)x^{j+k}\,dx
=\int_{-1}^1w(x)f(x)x^k\,dx,\qquad k=0,\ldots,n.}
$$
Its <Gram matrix> $G_{kj}=\int_{-1}^1w(x)x^{k+j}\,dx$ is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>: for any nonzero <vector> $a$,
$$
a^TGa=\int_{-1}^1w(x)\left(\sum_{j=0}^na_jx^j\right)^2dx>0.
$$
Indeed a nonzero <polynomial> is nonzero on some open interval, and the weight is positive. Therefore $G$ is an <invertible matrix>, proving uniqueness. Conversely, if these equations hold, the <Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space> gives, for every $q\in\mathcal P_n$,
$$
\|f-(p_n+q)\|_w^2=\|f-p_n\|_w^2+\|q\|_w^2,
$$
so the solution really is the unique <best approximation>.

Let $r=f-p_n$ be the <continuous function> describing the error. The preceding <orthogonality> equations say
$$
\int_{-1}^1w(x)r(x)q(x)\,dx=0\qquad(q\in\mathcal P_n).
$$
If $r$ vanishes identically, any $n+1$ distinct points work. If $r$ already has at least $n+1$ distinct zeros, the assertion also follows. Suppose instead that it has at most $n$ distinct zeros. Among its interior zeros, list those at which its sign changes as $t_1<\cdots<t_m$, where $m\leq n$, and define the <polynomial>
$$
q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^m(x-t_j),
$$
with $q=1$ if $m=0$. Between consecutive zeros of $r$, its sign is constant by the <intermediate value theorem>. At each $t_j$, both $r$ and $q$ change sign; at every other zero of $r$, neither sign changes. Hence $rq$ has one fixed sign throughout $[-1,1]$ wherever it is nonzero. Replacing $q$ by $-q$ if necessary makes $rq\geq0$ everywhere.

Since $r$ is not identically zero and $q$ has only finitely many zeros, $rq$ is strictly positive on some open interval. Positivity of $w$ gives
$$
\int_{-1}^1w(x)r(x)q(x)\,dx>0,
$$
contradicting <orthogonality> because $q\in\mathcal P_n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(x_i)=p_n(x_i)\text{ at at least }n+1\text{ distinct points in }[-1,1].}
$$
This is the <interpolation property of weighted least-squares polynomial approximation>. It supplies <interpolation nodes> depending on $f$ and $w$, rather than nodes prescribed in advance.

Let $L_{n+1}$ denote the conventional <Legendre polynomial>, normalized by $L_{n+1}(1)=1$. The $n+1$ <quadrature nodes> are its distinct <polynomial roots> in $(-1,1)$. For these nodes form the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>
$$
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}
\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}
=\frac{L_{n+1}(x)}{(x-x_i)L_{n+1}'(x_i)}.
$$
Choose the <quadrature weights> by integrating these <polynomials>:
$$
\boxed{w_i=\int_{-1}^1\ell_i(x)\,dx,\qquad
\widehat I_n(f)=\sum_{i=0}^nw_if(x_i).}
$$
An equivalent standard formula is
$$
\boxed{w_i=\frac{2}{(1-x_i^2)[L_{n+1}'(x_i)]^2}.}
$$
The reason for selecting these <quadrature nodes> is the resulting <degree of precision of a quadrature rule>. For any <polynomial> $p$ of <polynomial degree> at most $2n+1$, <polynomial division> gives
$$
p=L_{n+1}s+r,\qquad \deg s\leq n,\quad\deg r\leq n.
$$
The <orthogonality> of the <Legendre polynomial> makes $\int_{-1}^1L_{n+1}s=0$, while $p(x_i)=r(x_i)$ at each <quadrature node>. The definition of the <quadrature weights> integrates $r$ exactly, so
$$
\boxed{\widehat I_n(p)=\int_{-1}^1p(x)\,dx\qquad(\deg p\leq2n+1).}
$$
In particular, $\sum_iw_i=2$. Applying this exactness to $\ell_i^2$ also proves $w_i=\int_{-1}^1\ell_i(x)^2dx>0$.

Truncate the <Chebyshev series> at the full <degree of precision of a quadrature rule>:
$$
s_{2n+1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2n+1}c_jT_j(x),\qquad R_n=f-s_{2n+1}.
$$
Since $|T_j(x)|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$, the <geometric series> estimate gives
$$
\|R_n\|_\infty\leq\sum_{j=2n+2}^\infty e^{-Kj}
=\frac{e^{-K(2n+2)}}{1-e^{-K}}.
$$
The same bound proves <uniform convergence> of the original <Chebyshev series>, so its sum and the tail estimate are justified. <Gauss-Legendre quadrature> is exact on $s_{2n+1}$. Its positive <quadrature weights> sum to $2$, hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
|I-\widehat I_n|
&=\left|\int_{-1}^1R_n(x)\,dx-\sum_{i=0}^nw_iR_n(x_i)\right|\\
&\leq2\|R_n\|_\infty+\left(\sum_{i=0}^nw_i\right)\|R_n\|_\infty\\
&\leq\frac{4e^{-2K}}{1-e^{-K}}e^{-2Kn}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore one explicit choice proving <exponential convergence of Gaussian quadrature> is
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{4e^{-2K}}{1-e^{-K}},\qquad L=2K.}
$$
Using exactness through <polynomial degree> $2n+1$ doubles the exponent compared with truncating at <polynomial degree> $n$.

Set $W=\operatorname{diag}(w_0,\ldots,w_n)$. For column indices $j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}$, the product $P_{j-1}P_{k-1}$ has <polynomial degree> at most $2n$, so exactness of <Gauss-Legendre quadrature> and <orthonormality> give
$$
(A^TWA)_{jk}=\sum_{i=0}^nw_iP_{j-1}(x_i)P_{k-1}(x_i)
=\int_{-1}^1P_{j-1}(x)P_{k-1}(x)\,dx=\delta_{jk}.
$$
Thus $Q=W^{1/2}A$ is a square <matrix> with $Q^TQ=I$, and therefore is an <orthogonal matrix>: $QQ^T=I$ as well. It follows that
$$
\boxed{AA^T=W^{-1},\qquad
(AA^T)_{ij}=\begin{cases}
w_{i-1}^{-1},&i=j,\\
0,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}}
$$
Equivalently, the rows of the <generalized Vandermonde matrix> are <orthogonal> and
$$
\sum_{k=0}^nP_k(x_i)^2=\frac1{w_i}.
$$
The normalization matters: here $P_{n+1}=\sqrt{(2n+3)/2}\,L_{n+1}$, where $L_{n+1}$ is the conventional <Legendre polynomial> used above. Thus a formula containing only the nodes and the question's <orthonormal> <polynomials> is
$$
\boxed{(AA^T)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
\frac{(1-x_{i-1}^2)[P_{n+1}'(x_{i-1})]^2}{2n+3}.}
$$
This identity is the <discrete orthogonality of Gaussian quadrature>.

All $n$ <singular values> are positive, so $\Sigma$ and $V$ are <invertible matrices> and $A$ has <matrix rank> $n$. The <image of a linear map> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{range}(A)=\operatorname{span}\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\},}
$$
with those columns furnishing an <orthonormal basis>. Also $Ax=0$ implies $\Sigma V^Tx=0$ and hence $x=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\ker A=\{0\},\qquad\text{its basis is the empty set}.}
$$
Finally $A^Tz=V\Sigma U^Tz=0$ exactly when $U^Tz=0$. The remaining columns of the full <orthogonal matrix> span this <orthogonal complement>:
$$
\boxed{\ker A^T=\operatorname{range}(U_\perp),\qquad
\text{a basis consists of the }m-n\text{ columns of }U_\perp.}
$$
If $m=n$, this last <null space> also equals $\{0\}$ and has the empty basis.

For $m=n$, $U$ and $V$ are square <orthogonal matrices>. Multiply the <linear system> successively by $U^T$, $\Sigma^{-1}$ and $V$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{x=V\Sigma^{-1}U^Tb
=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}v_j.}
$$
With the <singular value decomposition> already supplied, compute $c=U^Tb$, divide $c_j$ by $\sigma_j$, then form $x=Vc$. The two dense <matrix-vector products> each cost $O(n^2)$ <arithmetic operations> in <Big O notation>, and the diagonal scaling costs $O(n)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{Cost per right-hand side with supplied factors: }O(n^2).}
$$
If the <singular value decomposition> first has to be computed, its usual dense cost is $O(n^3)$; that is a separate setup cost and can be reused for further right-hand sides.

The <Euclidean norm> is invariant under an <orthogonal matrix>, so the full <singular value decomposition> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Ax-b\|_2^2
&=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma V^Tx-U^Tb\\-U_\perp^Tb\end{pmatrix}\right\|_2^2\\
&=\|\Sigma V^Tx-U^Tb\|_2^2+\|U_\perp^Tb\|_2^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second term is independent of $x$; the first attains its unique minimum zero because $\Sigma$ is an <invertible matrix>. The <linear least squares> solution is therefore
$$
\boxed{x_*=V\Sigma^{-1}U^Tb=A^+b,\qquad
\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=\|U_\perp^Tb\|_2.}
$$
Here $A^+=V\Sigma^{-1}U^T$ is the <Moore-Penrose inverse>, and the fitted <vector> is the <orthogonal projection> $Ax_*=UU^Tb$ onto the <image of a linear map> defined by $A$.

With the <singular value decomposition> supplied, forming $U^Tb$ costs $O(mn)$ <arithmetic operations>, diagonal scaling costs $O(n)$, and multiplication by $V$ costs $O(n^2)$. Since $m\geq n$,
$$
\boxed{\text{Cost per right-hand side: }O(mn+n^2)=O(mn).}
$$
One need not form $U_\perp$, $A^+$ or $A^TA$ to calculate $x_*$. Computing the needed thin <singular value decomposition> first has standard dense cost $O(mn^2)$; explicitly forming an entire $m\times m$ orthogonal completion can require additional work when $m$ is much larger than $n$.

Retain the first $r$ terms in the <singular value decomposition>:

\boxed{A_r=\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_ju_jv_j^T
=U\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r,0,\ldots,0)V^T.}

The <Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem> says that this <truncated singular value decomposition> is a best <low-rank approximation> in the <spectral norm>. The remaining <singular values> of the difference are $\sigma_{r+1},\ldots,\sigma_n$, together with zeros, so

\boxed{\|A-A_r\|_2=\sigma_{r+1}.}

For $r\geq1$ the displayed <matrix> has <matrix rank> exactly $r$, since every retained <singular value> is positive. For $r=0$, the only <matrix> of <matrix rank> zero is the zero <matrix>, and the same formula gives error $\sigma_1$.

Take the <identity matrix>

**No. Distinct <singular values> do not force uniqueness in the <spectral norm>.** For an explicit example take
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_t=\begin{pmatrix}2+t&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad -1\leq t\leq1.
$$
The <singular values> of $A$ are $2>1>0$. Every $B_t$ has <matrix rank> one, and its error in the <spectral norm> is
$$
\boxed{\|A-B_t\|_2=\max\{|t|,1\}=1=\sigma_2(A).}
$$
Thus there are infinitely many best rank-one approximations even with a strict gap between the <singular values>.

The same mechanism works for every $1\leq r<n$ under the question's positive <singular values>. Starting with $A_r$, define
$$
B_t=A_r+t u_1v_1^T,\qquad |t|\leq\sigma_{r+1}.
$$
When the <singular values> are distinct, $\sigma_1+t\geq\sigma_1-\sigma_{r+1}>0$, so every $B_t$ has <matrix rank> $r$, while
$$
\|A-B_t\|_2=\max\{|t|,\sigma_{r+1}\}=\sigma_{r+1}.
$$
The <spectral norm> measures only the largest remaining <singular value>, leaving room to change retained components without increasing that maximum. This is the <nonuniqueness of spectral-norm low-rank approximation>. The exceptional case $r=0$ is unique for the elementary reason given above.

We use the <spectral theorem> to construct a sufficiently accurate approximate <eigenpair>. Complete $x$ to an <orthonormal eigenbasis> $x,x_2,\ldots,x_n$ of the <symmetric matrix> $B$, with corresponding <eigenvalues> $\lambda,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n$. Simplicity of $\lambda$ means that every $\lambda_j-\lambda$ below is nonzero. Put
$$
a=x^TEx,\qquad
v=-\sum_{j=2}^n\frac{x_j^TEx}{\lambda_j-\lambda}x_j.
$$
The <orthogonal decomposition> of $Ex$ shows that $x^Tv=0$ and
$$
(B-\lambda I)v=-(E-aI)x.
$$
With $u_\varepsilon=x+\varepsilon v$ and $\mu_\varepsilon=\lambda+\varepsilon a$, direct expansion cancels every first-order term:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(B+\varepsilon E)u_\varepsilon-\mu_\varepsilon u_\varepsilon
&=\varepsilon\big((B-\lambda I)v+(E-aI)x\big)
+\varepsilon^2(E-aI)v\\
&=\varepsilon^2(E-aI)v.
\end{aligned}
$$
Also $\|u_\varepsilon\|_2^2=1+\varepsilon^2\|v\|_2^2\geq1$. Thus its <eigenpair residual> has <Euclidean norm> $O(\varepsilon^2)$ in <Big O notation>.

For completeness, a real <symmetric matrix> $M$ always has an <eigenvalue> within $\|(M-\mu I)u\|_2/\|u\|_2$ of any real $\mu$ and nonzero $u$. Indeed, writing $u=\sum_jc_jq_j$ in an <orthonormal eigenbasis> with <eigenvalues> $\theta_j$ gives
$$
\|(M-\mu I)u\|_2^2
=\sum_j(\theta_j-\mu)^2c_j^2
\geq\left(\min_j|\theta_j-\mu|\right)^2\|u\|_2^2.
$$
Apply this <symmetric eigenvalue residual bound> to $M=B+\varepsilon E$. There is an <eigenvalue> $\lambda_\varepsilon$ such that
$$
|\lambda_\varepsilon-(\lambda+\varepsilon a)|
\leq\varepsilon^2\|(E-aI)v\|_2,
$$
which proves
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\varepsilon=\lambda+\varepsilon(x^TEx)+O(\varepsilon^2).}
$$
This establishes the <first-order perturbation of a simple symmetric eigenvalue> without presupposing differentiability of the perturbed <eigenvector>. If $n=1$, the sum defining $v$ is empty and the displayed <eigenvalue> formula is exact.

Here $y'=x/(1+y^2)$ and $y(0)=1$. Since $x\geq0$, the solution is nondecreasing, so $y(x)\geq1$. In fact $0\leq y'\leq1/2$ on $[0,1]$, which also bounds the solution and ensures it extends across this entire interval. The smooth right-hand side makes $y$ twice continuously differentiable, with <second derivative>
$$
y''=\frac1{1+y^2}-\frac{2x^2y}{(1+y^2)^3}
=\frac{(1+y^2)^2-2x^2y}{(1+y^2)^3}.
$$
For $y\geq1$ and $0\leq x\leq1$,
$$
(1+y^2)^2\geq4y^2\geq2y\geq2x^2y,
$$
so $y''\geq0$. The subtracted term in the first expression is nonnegative, giving the useful bound
$$
\boxed{0\leq y''(x)\leq\frac1{1+y(x)^2}\leq\frac12\qquad(0\leq x\leq1).}
$$
The integral form of the <Taylor remainder> now gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_n
&=\frac{y(x_{n+1})-y(x_n)}h-f(x_n,y(x_n))\\
&=\frac1h\int_{x_n}^{x_{n+1}}(x_{n+1}-t)y''(t)\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore the <normalized local truncation error> satisfies the stronger estimate
$$
\boxed{0\leq\tau_n\leq\frac h4\leq\frac h2.}
$$
This proves the requested bound for $n=1,\ldots,N-1$, and also for $n=0$, which will be needed to start the <global error> estimate. For $N=1$ the stated range is empty, but the estimate at $n=0$ still applies.

For $0\leq x\leq1$ and every real $z$,
$$
|\partial_yf(x,z)|=\frac{2x|z|}{(1+z^2)^2}
\leq\frac{x}{1+z^2}\leq1,
$$
where $2|z|\leq1+z^2$ was used. The <mean value theorem> therefore proves <Lipschitz continuity> in $y$ with constant $1$.

Let $e_n=y(x_n)-y_n$. The <explicit Euler method> and the exact-solution identity from part (i) give
$$
e_{n+1}=e_n+h\big(f(x_n,y(x_n))-f(x_n,y_n)\big)+h\tau_n.
$$
Using the requested <local truncation error> bound $|\tau_n|\leq h/2$, including at $n=0$, yields
$$
|e_{n+1}|\leq(1+h)|e_n|+\frac{h^2}{2},\qquad e_0=0.
$$
Iteration and the finite <geometric series> give
$$
|e_N|\leq\frac{h^2}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1+h)^j
=\frac h2\big((1+h)^N-1\big).
$$
Since $1+h\leq e^h$ and $Nh=1$,
$$
\boxed{|y(1)-y_N|\leq\frac{e-1}{2}h.}
$$
Keeping the stronger bound $|\tau_n|\leq h/4$ from part (i) improves the same proof to $(e-1)h/4$. The requested inequality follows already from the weaker bound.

The <backward Euler method> uses the new point:
$$
y_{n+1}=y_n-hx_{n+1}y_{n+1}^2,
\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+h.
$$
Writing $a=h(x_n+h)>0$ gives the <quadratic equation> $ay_{n+1}^2+y_{n+1}-y_n=0$. Starting from $y_n>0$, its two <polynomial roots> have opposite signs. The positive root, which continues the solution as $h\to0$, is
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4h(x_n+h)y_n}}{2h(x_n+h)}.}
$$
The other root is negative and does not approach $y_n$ as $h\to0$. Rationalizing the numerator gives an equivalent form that avoids <cancellation error> when the <step size> is small:
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=\frac{2y_n}{1+\sqrt{1+4h(x_n+h)y_n}}.}
$$
The defining <quadratic equation> gives $0<y_{n+1}<y_n$ for this root, so positivity is maintained inductively from $y_0=1$. The branch choice is part of specifying the <backward Euler method> here: the algebraic update alone has two solutions.

p(z)=z^5+tz^2+h^{-1}z-h^{-1}y_n=0.
$$
Construct its $5\times5$ <companion matrix>
$$
\boxed{C_n=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0&0&y_n/h\\
1&0&0&0&-1/h\\
0&1&0&0&-t\\
0&0&1&0&0\\
0&0&0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\det(zI-C_n)=p(z)$. Thus the <polynomial roots> needed for this update are exactly the <eigenvalues> of $C_n$.

At each step, assemble $C_n$, compute its <eigenvalues>, for example with the <QR algorithm>, and choose its unique positive real <eigenvalue> as $y_{n+1}$. To justify this selection, let
$$
F(z)=hz^5+htz^2+z-y_n.
$$
If $y_n>0$, then $F(0)=-y_n<0$, $F(y_n)=hy_n^5+hty_n^2>0$, and
$$
F'(z)=5hz^4+2htz+1>0\qquad(z\geq0).
$$
The <intermediate value theorem> and strict <monotonicity> prove there is exactly one positive <polynomial root>, located in $(0,y_n)$. Starting at $y_0=1$ therefore makes every update well defined, positive and decreasing. In numerical arithmetic, select the nearly real <eigenvalue> in this interval and check the <polynomial residual> $|F(z)|$; a final <Newton root-finding iteration> can refine it. This reduces each update to the requested <eigenvalue problem>.

As appropriate for a genuine $k$-step <linear multistep method>, take $\alpha_k\ne0$. Suppose the <characteristic polynomial> has a <polynomial root> $\xi$ with <modulus> $|\xi|>1$. From $\rho(\xi)=0$ and the <triangle inequality>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\alpha_k||\xi|^k
&=\left|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha_j\xi^j\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha_j||\xi|^j\\
&\leq|\xi|^{k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\alpha_j|\\
&\leq|\alpha_k||\xi|^{k-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Dividing by the positive number $|\alpha_k||\xi|^{k-1}$ gives $|\xi|\leq1$, a contradiction. Hence
$$
\boxed{|\xi_i|\leq1\qquad(i=1,\ldots,k).}
$$
The nonzero leading <coefficient> matters: if $\alpha_k=0$, the assumed inequality forces all the $\alpha_j$ to vanish, so $\rho$ is the zero <polynomial> rather than a <characteristic polynomial> with $k$ roots. This degenerate case does not define the method in the question.

**Yes: for $k=2$ the stated inequality guarantees <zero-stability>.** Part (ii) puts every <polynomial root> in the closed <unit disc>. The remaining <root condition for a multistep method> is that every root on the <unit circle> has <multiplicity of a root> one.

If this failed for a <quadratic polynomial>, it would have a repeated root $\xi$ with $|\xi|=1$, and
$$
\rho(z)=\alpha_2(z-\xi)^2
=\alpha_2z^2-2\alpha_2\xi z+\alpha_2\xi^2.
$$
Consequently $|\alpha_1|=2|\alpha_2|$ and $|\alpha_0|=|\alpha_2|$, contradicting
$$
|\alpha_2|\geq|\alpha_0|+|\alpha_1|=3|\alpha_2|,
$$
because $\alpha_2\ne0$. Thus all roots on the <unit circle> are simple and
$$
\boxed{\text{The method is zero-stable; no counterexample satisfies the assumption.}}
$$
This conclusion does not assert <consistency of a numerical method>: part (i) rules out <consistency of a numerical method> whenever the coefficient inequality is strict. <Zero-stability> and <consistency of a numerical method> are separate requirements.
