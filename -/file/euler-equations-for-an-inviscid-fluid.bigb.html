= Euler equation in a uniformly rotating frame
{c}
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{title2=$\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u=-\nabla\Pi$}

For constant <mass density> $\rho_0$, a frame with constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$ absorbs <centrifugal acceleration> into reduced <pressure> $\Pi=p/\rho_0-|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2$. Indeed $\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)=-\nabla|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2$. Transforming the <material acceleration> in the <Euler equations> gives the displayed equation. The <Coriolis acceleration> remains velocity dependent and cannot be absorbed into a scalar potential. A conservative physical <body force> can be included by adding its potential to $\Pi$.

= Density scaling invariance of incompressible Euler flow
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{title2=$\rho\mapsto C\rho,\ p\mapsto Cp$}

A homogeneous fluid in a prescribed gravitational field obeys $D\mathbf u/Dt=-\nabla(p/\rho)+\mathbf g$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. Multiplying both constant <mass density> and <pressure> by the same positive factor preserves the <velocity> solution, provided initial and boundary data remain compatible. Thus velocity-only observations do not determine density in this model. Independently prescribed pressure gradients or the fluid's own gravity can break this invariance.

= Linearized Euler equations
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}

The linearized Euler equations retain only first-order perturbations about a specified inviscid fluid state. Together with the linearized <continuity equation> and a pressure-density constitutive relation, they describe small-amplitude <acoustic waves>. About a uniform resting state, $\rho_0\partial_t\mathbf u'=-\nabla p'$, $\partial_t\rho'+\rho_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf u'=0$, and an isentropic pressure perturbation has $p'=c_0^2\rho'$. Gradients and advection of a moving background must be retained when its variation is not negligible.

= Conservative energy flux of a polytropic ideal gas
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{title2=$E=\tfrac12\rho u^2+\frac p{\gamma-1},\quad F_E=u_x(E+p)$}

For an unmagnetized <ideal gas> without gravity, the <pressure> equation gives internal-energy balance $\partial_te+\partial_x(u_xe)=-p\partial_xu_x$, where $e=p/(\gamma-1)$. The momentum equation gives kinetic-energy balance with source $-u_x\partial_xp$. Their sum is conservative with the displayed total-energy flux. Tangential <velocities> contribute to <kinetic energy> even when all derivatives are in the normal direction. Smooth <polytropic flow> advects its <entropy> parameter; a physical shock need not preserve that parameter across the discontinuity.

= Crocco form of the unsteady Euler equation
{c}
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{title2=$\partial_t\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf u+\nabla\varepsilon=T\nabla s$}

For a fixed-composition inviscid fluid, <specific enthalpy> satisfies $dw=Tds+dp/\rho$. Combining this identity with the <vorticity cross-product identity> gives the displayed equation, with $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$ and $\varepsilon=u^2/2+\Phi+w$. It exposes entropy gradients as the thermodynamic source of a mismatch between <Bernoulli function> gradients and the vorticity term. In a steady <adiabatic process>, its projection along a <streamline> makes $\varepsilon$ constant there.

= Yudovich characteristic flow
{c}
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{title2=$\dot X(t)=u(t,X(t))$}

For a planar Euler velocity obtained from vorticity in $L^\infty_{\mathrm{loc}}(dt;L^1\cap L^\infty)$, the <planar vorticity velocity kernel> gives a locally integrable speed bound and <log-Lipschitz modulus>. Spatial mollification gives existence of global trajectories; the <Osgood uniqueness criterion> gives uniqueness and continuous dependence. Measurability in time and an initial trace are needed. This establishes the flow for a fixed Euler solution; nonlinear uniqueness of the Euler solution itself requires further comparison arguments.

= Bernoulli principle
{c}
{synonym}

The <Bernoulli principle> relates <pressure>, <kinetic energy> per unit volume and <Newtonian gravitational potential> along a <streamline> of <steady flow> with <inviscid flow>. For constant <mass density>, the <Bernoulli equation> gives $p/\rho+u^2/2+\Phi$ constant along that <streamline>. Constancy across different <streamlines> requires further hypotheses, such as <irrotational flow>.

Precisely, suppose a <fluid> of constant positive <mass density> obeys the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>, its <velocity field> and <pressure> are steady, and the body <force> per unit <mass> is $-\nabla\Phi$. Then
$$
B=\frac{p}{\rho}+\frac{|\mathbf u|^2}{2}+\Phi,\qquad \mathbf u\cdot\nabla B=0.
$$
Every term has units of <energy> per unit <mass>. For a uniform gravitational field, <Newtonian gravitational potential> is $\Phi=gz$, up to an additive constant. The <pressure> term represents work by surrounding <fluid>, so acceleration can exchange <pressure> work with <kinetic energy>. Dotting the steady <Euler equations> with $\mathbf u$ gives the displayed <directional derivative>: this is why constancy follows a <streamline>. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/mem2/FLUIDS-IB/dyn.pdf[Cambridge, Fluid Dynamics IB, Dynamics, section 2.4]{ref}

Continue the horizontal water constriction example from <incompressible flow>. Assume negligible viscous losses, <steady flow> and approximately <uniform flow> at both measurement sections, so the <streamline> speeds there equal the mean speeds $0.20$ and $0.80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Equal height cancels the <Newtonian gravitational potential> terms. The ideal <pressure> drop is
$$
p_1-p_2=\frac{\rho}{2}(U_2^2-U_1^2)=\frac{1000}{2}(0.80^2-0.20^2)=300\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
This is the <Venturi effect> in its ideal limit: the gain in <kinetic energy> density is supplied by <pressure> work. Cambridge's treatment uses this relation to connect a measured <pressure> difference to <volumetric flow rate>. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/mem2/FLUIDS-IB/dyn.pdf[Cambridge, Fluid Dynamics IB, Dynamics, section 2.5]{ref}

The $300\,\mathrm{Pa}$ result is conditional, rather than a prediction of the actual viscous pipe. <Mass conservation> fixes $Q$ and the ratio of mean speeds even with <viscosity>, but nonuniform <velocity profiles> require an integrated <energy> balance, and wall friction dissipates mechanical <energy>. A pump would supply additional work. In particular, constant mean speed in a straight pipe does not imply constant <pressure>: <Poiseuille flow> needs a continuing <pressure gradient> to overcome <viscosity>. https://uclnatsci.github.io/2021/Electromagnetism-Fluids-and-Waves/fluids/bernoulli.html[UCL, Bernoulli's equation, assumptions and boundary limitations]{ref}

= Venturi effect
{c}
{parent=Bernoulli's principle}
{wiki}

In ideal steady flow, a contraction of a pipe increases the speed and lowers the pressure. For constant <mass density>, equal body-force potential at the ends and approximately uniform endpoint speeds, <conservation of mass> and the <Bernoulli equation> give $\dot M=S_1S_2\sqrt{2\rho(p_1-p_2)/(S_1^2-S_2^2)}$ for flow from a wider to a narrower section. Elevation changes contribute a body-force potential difference, and nonuniform profiles or viscous losses require additional information.

= Axisymmetric inviscid flow between moving parallel plates
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}

For plates $z=\pm h(t)$, incompressibility, impermeability and regularity on the axis determine a z-independent radial velocity $u_r=-r\dot h/(2h)$ and vertical velocity $u_z=z\dot h/h$. A possible azimuthal velocity requires additional rotational data; continuity alone does not determine it. For a smooth regular initial profile $U(r)$ independent of $z$, its azimuthal Euler equation transports it as $u_\theta(r,t)=\lambda U(\lambda r)$, where $\lambda=\sqrt{h/h_0}$. If the rotation is solid-body with angular velocity $\Omega(t)$, the <vorticity equation> gives $\Omega=\Omega_0h/h_0$. Along a particle path, $r^2h$ and $r^2\Omega$ are constant, illustrating <conservation of angular momentum>.

= Unsteady Bernoulli equation
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{c}

For time-dependent <irrotational flow> with $u=\nabla\phi$, constant <mass density>, and no body force, the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> integrate to
$$
\partial_t\phi+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho=C(t).
$$
A time-dependent change of gauge in $\phi$ may set $C(t)=0$.

This is the unsteady potential-flow version of <Bernoulli's principle>, with a time-dependent potential gauge.

= Constant-tension balloon discharge
{parent=Unsteady Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$R^3\ddot R+2R^2\dot R^2=-K,\quad K=\gamma a^2/(2\rho L)$}

A spherical balloon feeding an inviscid uniform tube plug, with entrance excess <pressure> $2\gamma/R$, satisfies $L\dot u=2\gamma/(\rho R)$ and $4\pi R^2\dot R=-\pi a^2u$. Eliminating $u$ yields the displayed equation. Its <first integral> is $R^4\dot R^2+KR^2=C$. From rest at radius $R_0$, $C=KR_0^2$; integration gives $t=R_0^2K^{-1/2}(\pi/4-\theta/2+\sin(2\theta)/4)$, where $\theta=\arcsin(R/R_0)$. The ideal emptying time is $\pi R_0^2/(4\sqrt K)$. Independently prescribed initial flow changes $C$ and therefore this time.

= Inviscid startup in a pressure-driven tube
{parent=Unsteady Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$U(t)=\sqrt{2P/\rho}\tanh\left(\frac tL\sqrt{\frac P{2\rho}}\right)$}

For a slender tube of length $L$ supplied from a large reservoir with excess pressure $P$, negligible gravity and entrance inertia, the <unsteady Bernoulli equation> gives $L\dot U+U^2/2=P/\rho$. A stationary initial liquid column has the displayed speed and <volume flux> $\pi a^2U$ for tube radius $a$. The inertial term controls the early linear acceleration; the kinetic-energy term sets the terminal inviscid speed. This is a leading slender-tube model while the reservoir remains large and the tube full.

= Linearly elastic balloon discharge
{parent=Unsteady Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$\Omega^2=K\pi a^2/(\rho L)$}

An inviscid uniform tube plug driven by entrance excess pressure $KV$ obeys $\rho L\dot u=KV$ and $\dot V=-\pi a^2u$. From rest, the first zero of $V$ occurs after a quarter harmonic period, independent of its initial volume. The maximum speed is proportional to that initial volume. Entrance losses, a reservoir kinetic head or independently prescribed initial flow change this ideal model.

= Clebsch-potential variational derivation of incompressible Euler flow
{parent=Euler equations for an inviscid fluid}
{c}

For $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi+\beta\nabla\alpha$, the density
$$
\mathcal L=-\beta\alpha_t-\frac12|\mathbf u|^2
$$
gives incompressibility and material conservation of $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Differentiating the Clebsch representation then yields the Euler momentum equation with
$$
p=-\frac12|\mathbf u|^2-\phi_t-\beta\alpha_t.
$$

= Bernoulli invariant
{c}
{parent=Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$b=u^2/2+\Phi+w$}

In a steady adiabatic inviscid fluid with a conservative body force, the <Bernoulli invariant> is the displayed specific energy, constant along each <streamline>. Dotting the <Euler equations> with velocity and using the <specific enthalpy> identity gives $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla b=0$. In <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>, magnetic work need not vanish; the <planar ideal-MHD Bernoulli invariant> includes the corresponding electromagnetic correction.

= Hydraulic control over a smooth hump
{parent=Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$D+h+Q^2/(2gh^2)=\text{constant}$}

A slowly varying, steady, inviscid channel flow has constant discharge $Q=uh$ per unit width and the displayed <Bernoulli equation>. The bed-height function of depth has its maximum at $h_c=(Q^2/g)^{1/3}$, where the <Froude number> is one. At a nondegenerate critical crest, a differentiable profile changes between the <subcritical flow> and <supercritical flow> branches. With subcritical upstream squared Froude number $F<1$ and upstream depth $h_1$, the shock-free supercritical downstream depth is $h_1(F+\sqrt{F^2+8F})/4$.

= Bernoulli function for planar constant-vorticity flow
{parent=Bernoulli equation}
{c}
{title2=$|\mathbf u|^2/2+\omega\psi+p/\rho=C$}

With the <streamfunction> convention $(u,v)=(\psi_y,-\psi_x)$, constant scalar <vorticity> gives $\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega=-\omega\nabla\psi$. For steady inviscid constant-density flow without body force, this integrates to
$$
\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2+\omega\psi+p/\rho=C
$$
throughout a connected fluid region. Reversing the streamfunction convention changes the sign of its term; the declared convention matters.

= Steady rotating-frame Bernoulli integral
{parent=Bernoulli equation}
{title2=$\tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2+p/\rho+\Phi-\tfrac12\Omega^2r_\perp^2$}

For <steady flow> of constant-density fluid in a uniformly rotating frame with conservative body forces, the displayed quantity is constant along each relative <streamline>. The <centrifugal potential> contributes the negative quadratic term, and the <Coriolis force> contributes no work because it is perpendicular to the relative velocity. The result follows by dotting the rotating <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> with that velocity.

= Draining time of a uniform tank through a siphon
{parent=Bernoulli equation}

If a tank and siphon tube have areas $A$ and $a$, the tube outlet is $H$ below an inlet initially submerged by $h_0$, and both free boundaries are at <atmospheric pressure>, continuity and Bernoulli's equation give
$$
t=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{A^2}{a^2}-1\right)^{1/2}
\frac{\sqrt{H+h_0}-\sqrt H}{\sqrt g}
$$
for the free surface to reach the inlet.

= Holding force on a contracting nozzle
{parent=Integral momentum equation}
{title2=$F=\rho Q^2(A_0/A_1-1)^2/(2A_0)$}

For steady ideal flow through a horizontal contracting nozzle, <Bernoulli equation> gives inlet gauge pressure $p_0=\rho Q^2(A_1^{-2}-A_0^{-2})/2$. The <integral momentum equation> gives the fluid's force on the nozzle as $p_0A_0-\rho Q^2(A_1^{-1}-A_0^{-1})$ in the outlet direction. Simplification yields the displayed nonnegative magnitude. The external holding force has equal magnitude and the opposite direction.
