= Algebra
{wiki}

Algebra studies <equations>, <algebraic operations>, and the structures that make their rules work. Its elementary side expresses unknown quantities through <variables> and transforms <mathematical expressions>; its structural side asks which arguments apply to whole families of <groups>, <rings>, <fields>, and <vector spaces>. The useful step is identifying exactly which properties a calculation needs, so that it can be reused in a new setting.

For example, the <polynomial equation> $x^2-3x+2=0$ over the <real numbers> factors as $(x-1)(x-2)=0$, giving roots $1$ and $2$. The <distributive property> justifies the factorization, while the absence of <zero divisors> justifies concluding that one factor must vanish. Over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_2$, the same integer coefficients reduce to $x^2+x=x(x+1)$, with roots $0$ and $1$. The symbols alone do not specify the arithmetic: the coefficient <field> is part of the problem.

In a <ring> with <zero divisors>, a familiar step can fail. Modulo $8$, all four residue classes $1,3,5,7$ satisfy $x^2=1$. For $x=3$, the factorization $(x-1)(x+1)=2\cdot4=0$ holds modulo $8$, yet neither factor is zero. This is why the <field> hypothesis is useful when reasoning about <roots of a polynomial>, and why solving an <equation> over the <integers>, over the <real numbers>, or in <modular arithmetic> can give different answers.

The structure is also useful beyond scalar arithmetic. <Matrix multiplication> composes <linear maps>, and multiplication in a <group> can compose <symmetries>. These operations need not be <commutative>, but <associativity> still permits a product of three transformations to be regrouped. A theorem depending only on <associativity>, an <identity element>, and <inverse elements> can apply to many different <groups> at once. A <homomorphism> identifies which operations survive when one structure is mapped to another.

Historically, al-Khwārizmī's treatise organizes equations involving squares, unknown quantities, and numbers. Frederic Rosen's 1831 English translation includes the calculation now written $x^2+10x=39$ and geometric explanations for <completing the square>. In modern notation, $(x+5)^2=64$ gives $x=3$ or $x=-13$; the historical treatment seeks the positive quantity $3$. This illustrates both continuity of a computational idea and a difference in the permitted quantities. \a[https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing/Compendium_on_calculating_by_completion_and_reduction][Al-Khwārizmī, Compendium on calculating by completion and reduction, Rosen's 1831 translation, pp. 5–11]{ref}

= Equation
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki}
{title2=$L=R$}

An equation asserts that two <mathematical expressions> have the same value. A solution assigns values to its <variables> that make the assertion true in the specified domain. For example, $2x=1$ has solution $1/2$ over the <rational numbers>, but no solution over the <integers>. Its solutions are unchanged by reversible transformations; division by a potentially zero or noninvertible quantity requires care.

= Algebraic K-theory
{parent=Algebra}

<Algebraic K-theory> studies invariants built from <projective modules> and their automorphisms over <rings>. Its lowest <groups> record the additive projective-module classes and stable invertible <matrices>, respectively. It differs from <topological K-theory>, although vector-bundle clutching and module patching have parallel forms.

= Milnor square
{c}
{parent=Algebraic K-theory}

A cartesian square of unital <rings> $A=A_1\times_{A_0}A_2$ is a <Milnor square> when at least one map $A_i\to A_0$ is surjective. <Finitely generated projective modules> over $A$ can then be assembled from <projective modules> over the two upper <rings> together with an <isomorphism> of their reductions over $A_0$.

= Cyclotomic Milnor square
{parent=Milnor square}
{title2=$\mathbb Z[C_p]=\mathbb Z\times_{\mathbb F_p}\mathbb Z[\zeta_p]$}

Evaluation of the cyclic generator at $1$ and at a primitive $p$th root gives the displayed <pullback>. The maps to the common <field> reduce integers modulo $p$ and set $\zeta_p=1$. If a representative <polynomial> for the second component has value $c$ at one, adding an integer multiple of $\Phi_p$ adjusts that value by a multiple of $p$ without changing the second component. This proves surjectivity onto compatible pairs.

= Mayer-Vietoris sequence in algebraic K-theory
{c}
{parent=Milnor square}
{title2=$K_1A\to K_1A_1\oplus K_1A_2\to K_1A_0\to K_0A\to K_0A_1\oplus K_0A_2\to K_0A_0$}

The diagonal maps are <extension of scalars>; the maps to $A_0$ are differences. The connecting map sends $g$ to the difference of its patched <free module> and the corresponding free $A$-module. Elementary lifting and common free stabilization prove exactness at every internal term. The displayed fragment does not assert injectivity at its first term or surjectivity at its last.

= Milnor patching of projective modules
{c}
{parent=Milnor square}

A compatible triple $(P_1,P_2,\phi)$ patches to $\{(x_1,x_2):\phi(\bar x_1)=\bar x_2\}$. It is finitely generated projective and recovers $P_i$ after <extension of scalars>. For <free modules> glued by $g$, the block identity $\operatorname{diag}(g,g^{-1})\in E_\infty(A_0)$ lifts through the surjective side and makes the <direct sum> with inverse gluing free. Complements reduce general projective triples to that free case. Conversely an <idempotent> over $A$ is exactly a compatible pair of <idempotents> over its two factors.

= K1 of a ring
{c}
{parent=Algebraic K-theory}
{title2=$K_1(A)=\mathrm{GL}_\infty(A)/E_\infty(A)$}

Stable invertible <matrices>, with stabilization $g\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(g,1)$, form the <stable general linear group>. Its elementary subgroup is its <commutator subgroup>, and the displayed quotient is its <abelianization>. <Elementary matrices> lift along any surjective <ring> map, even when arbitrary invertible <matrices> do not lift. Over a <field>, <determinant> identifies $K_1$ with its multiplicative unit <group>.

= Stable general linear group
{parent=K1 of a ring}
{title2=$\mathrm{GL}_\infty(A)=\varinjlim_n\mathrm{GL}_n(A)$}

The union of finite invertible <matrices> under $g\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(g,1)$. Every element and relation occurs in a finite <matrix> size; adding identity blocks permits comparisons between different sizes without changing the stable class.

= Stable elementary subgroup
{parent=Stable general linear group}
{title2=$E_\infty(A)=\langle I+ae_{ij}:i\ne j\rangle$}

The subgroup generated by elementary <transvections> in finite <matrix> sizes, with identity stabilization. A third distinct index expresses each <transvection> as a commutator; stable block identities show that this subgroup is normal and that its quotient in the <stable general linear group> is abelian. Thus it is precisely the <commutator subgroup>.

= K0 of a ring
{c}
{parent=Algebraic K-theory}
{title2=$K_0(A)$}

The <Grothendieck group> of finitely generated projective right $A$-modules under <direct sum>. An element is a formal difference $[P]-[Q]$. Equality of two positive classes means $P\oplus T\cong Q\oplus T$ for some projective $T$; adding a complement of $T$ converts this into an <isomorphism> after a common finite free summand. This stabilization criterion is essential in patching arguments.

= Algebraic structure
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki}

An algebraic structure is a <set> equipped with specified <algebraic operations> satisfying selected axioms. An operation's domain is part of the structure: a <binary operation> on $S$ takes two elements of $S$ and produces another element of $S$. <Groups> specify one such operation, <rings> specify addition and multiplication, and <vector spaces> specify vector addition and scalar multiplication by a separate <field>.

Different choices of operations can give the same underlying <set> different structures. The <integers> form a <group> under addition, since every integer has an additive inverse. They do not form a <group> under multiplication: for example, $2$ has no multiplicative inverse among the <integers>. Even the nonzero <integers> fail that inverse requirement. In contrast, nonzero <rational numbers> form a <group> under multiplication.

Finite multiplication tables make such distinctions visible. Arithmetic modulo $5$ gives a <field>; arithmetic modulo $6$ gives a <commutative ring> with <zero divisors>. In the former, multiplying by any nonzero element permutes all residue classes. In the latter, multiplying by $2$ reaches only $0,2,4$, and $2\cdot3=0$ even though both factors are nonzero.

The goal is to extract reusable properties rather than memorize isolated tables. A <homomorphism> preserves the specified operations, and an <isomorphism> shows that two structures have identical operation patterns after relabeling.

= Vertex operator algebra
{parent=Algebraic structure}
{wiki}
{title2=$a\mapsto Y(a,z)$}

A <vertex operator algebra> is an integer-graded <vector space> equipped with a state-field correspondence $a\mapsto Y(a,z)$, a vacuum, translation operator and conformal vector satisfying vacuum, translation, locality and compatibility axioms. It packages the local <operator product expansions> of a chiral <conformal field theory>. A <string vertex operator> is an individual state insertion, rather than this complete <algebraic structure>.

= Incline
{parent=Algebraic structure}

An incline has two associative commutative operations $+$ and multiplication, with multiplication distributing over addition, idempotent addition and absorption $x+xy=x$. Neither operation is required here to have an identity. Its natural order is $x\le y\iff x+y=y$, making addition a <join>. Multiplication is <order-preserving>, and products lie below both factors. A finitely generated incline is well-quasi-ordered by its reversed natural order, via the <reverse-inclusion well-quasi-ordering of monomial ideals> applied to exponent supports.

= Finitely generated incline is well-quasi-ordered by reverse absorption
{parent=Incline}
{title2=$x\preceq y\iff x+y=x$}

Expand every term in finitely many generators as a finite nonempty sum of <monomials>, and associate its exponent support with the upward-closed <subset> of $\mathbb N^r$ that it generates. The <reverse-inclusion well-quasi-ordering of monomial ideals> supplies an earlier support upset containing a later one. Every later <monomial> is then a multiple of an earlier <monomial>, hence is absorbed by it under $a+ab=a$. The earlier sum absorbs the later sum. Thus every infinite term <sequence> has an earlier element preceding a later one in the reverse natural order. Reflexivity, antisymmetry and transitivity follow directly from commutative idempotent addition.

= Homomorphism
{parent=Algebraic structure}
{wiki}
{title2=$f:A\to B$}

A homomorphism is a map between <algebraic structures> preserving their specified operations. For a single <binary operation>, this means $f(a\ast b)=f(a)\star f(b)$, where the source and target may use different operation symbols. A <group homomorphism> preserves the group operation; a <ring homomorphism> preserves addition and multiplication, with the identity convention specified; a <module homomorphism> preserves addition and scalar multiplication.

Reduction modulo $5$ is a <ring homomorphism> $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$: adding or multiplying first and then reducing gives the same residue as reducing first and then operating. For example, $12\cdot14=168\equiv3\pmod5$, while the reduced operands give $2\cdot4=8\equiv3\pmod5$. This lets a complicated integer calculation be projected into simpler finite arithmetic. It also explains why a <polynomial equation> over the <integers> imposes necessary <modular arithmetic> conditions on any integer solution.

A homomorphism can lose information: $0$ and $5$ have the same image under that reduction. An <isomorphism> loses none of the structure because it has an operation-preserving inverse. Preservation of the operation, rather than preservation of arbitrary labels or sizes, is the essential condition.

= Derivation

= Algebraic derivation
{synonym}

An algebraic derivation of an <algebra over a field> is a <linear map> satisfying the <Leibniz rule> for its multiplication: $D(xy)=D(x)y+xD(y)$. This definition makes sense even when the multiplication is not associative. A <derivation of an algebra> uses an associative product; a <derivation of a Lie algebra> uses the <Lie bracket> as its product.

= Normed algebra
{parent=Algebra}
{title2=$(A,\|\cdot\|)$}

A normed algebra is an <algebra over a field> with a <norm> satisfying $\|ab\|\le\|a\|\|b\|$. Its completion is a <Banach algebra>, since multiplication extends continuously. Completeness is additional structure, rather than part of the definition of a normed algebra.

= Dimension bound for a commutative Euclidean normed algebra
{parent=Normed algebra}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb R}A\leq2$}

A finite-dimensional <commutative> real algebra with a Euclidean multiplicative <norm> has dimension at most two. <Automatic real bilinearity from a multiplicative norm> supplies bilinearity if it was not part of the initial description. On its <unit sphere>, squaring has derivative $v\mapsto2xv$, injective because there are no <zero divisors>. The only equal squares are antipodal pairs, so the map induces an injective <immersion> $\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}\to S^{n-1}$. <Compactness> and the <inverse function theorem> make it a <diffeomorphism> when $n\geq2$. For $n\geq3$, the <fundamental groups> $\mathbb Z/2$ and zero contradict this. The real and complex number algebras attain dimensions one and two.

= Automatic real bilinearity from a multiplicative norm
{parent=Normed algebra}
{title2=$\|xy\|=\|x\|\|y\|$}

Suppose a <ring> has an underlying real <normed vector space> and its norm is multiplicative, without initially assuming real bilinearity of multiplication. For fixed $x$, distributivity makes $y\mapsto xy$ additive, while $\|xy-xz\|=\|x\|\|y-z\|$ makes it continuous. Additivity gives rational scalar compatibility, and continuity extends it to every real scalar. Apply the same argument in the other variable to obtain a <bilinear map>. The norm identity also excludes nonzero <zero divisors>.

= Normed division algebra
{parent=Normed algebra}
{title2=$D$}

A normed division algebra is a nonzero unital <normed algebra> in which every nonzero element has a two-sided multiplicative inverse. The complex <Gelfand-Mazur theorem> forces such an algebra to be $\mathbb C$, even when completeness is not initially assumed: embed it in its <Banach algebra> completion and use <nonemptiness of the Banach-algebra spectrum>.

= Semigroup algebra
{parent=Algebra}
{title2=$k[D]$}

For an additive <semigroup> $D$ with identity zero, the semigroup algebra $k[D]$ has a $k$-basis of formal symbols $t^q$, $q\in D$, and multiplication $t^qt^{q'}=t^{q+q'}$. For $D=\mathbb Z[1/2]_{\ge0}$ it is the <integral domain> $\bigcup_{h\ge0}k[t^{1/2^h}]$. Its positive-exponent <ideal> is a nonzero <idempotent ideal>, and the strictly ascending chain $(t)\subsetneq(t^{1/2})\subsetneq\cdots$ shows that the algebra is not a <Noetherian ring>.

= Semiring
{parent=Algebra}

A semiring has a <commutative monoid> operation $(S,+,0)$ and a <monoid> operation $(S,\cdot,1)$, with multiplication distributing over addition on both sides and $0$ absorbing multiplication. Additive inverses are not required. The <natural numbers> with their usual addition and multiplication are a basic example. Regarding multiplication as composition yields a one-object <category> with <commutative-monoid enrichment>.

Bernoulli's inequality states that $(1+x)^r\geq1+rx$ for every integer $r\geq0$ and real $x\geq-1$. In particular, $(1-x)^r\geq1-rx$ for $0\leq x\leq1$.

= Distributive property

= Distributivity
{synonym}

For two <binary operations>, multiplication distributes over addition on the left or right when the respective displayed identity holds. If multiplication is <commutative>, either law gives the other. Repeated distribution expands expressions in an <incline> into finite sums of <monomials>. This property is distinct from <associativity> of either operation.

= Idempotence
{parent=Binary operation}
{wiki}
{title2=$x\ast x=x$}

For a <binary operation>, idempotence means repeating an input does not change it: $x\ast x=x$. An associative commutative idempotent operation defines a <join> order by $x\le y\iff x\ast y=y$.

= Commutativity
{parent=Binary operation}
{wiki}

= Commutative
{synonym}

A <binary operation> is commutative if $a*b=b*a$ for every pair in its domain. A particular pair may commute even when the operation is not commutative. For example, <ordinal addition> and <ordinal multiplication> are not generally commutative.

= Associative operation
{parent=Binary operation}

A binary operation $*$ is associative when $(x*y)*z=x*(y*z)$ for every $x,y,z$.

= Semigroup
{parent=Associative operation}
{wiki}

A semigroup is a <set> equipped with an <associative operation>.

= Rectangular band
{parent=Semigroup}
{title2=$x*x=x,\quad(x*y)*(z*w)=x*w$}

A rectangular band is a <semigroup> satisfying the displayed identities; these also imply associativity. Every nonempty such algebra is a product $B\times C$ with $(b,c)*(b',c')=(b,c')$. Fixing $e$, the factor sets are $B=\{x*e\}$ and $C=\{e*x\}$, and $x\mapsto(x*e,e*x)$ has inverse $(b,c)\mapsto b*c$. Homomorphisms between nonempty products are exactly pairs of maps on the two factors.

= Cartesian closed category of rectangular bands
{parent=Rectangular band}

The category of <rectangular bands> is <cartesian closed>. For nonempty products $A=B\times C$, $D=E\times F$, the exponential is $E^B\times F^C$. Also $D^0=1$ and $0^A=0$ for nonempty $A$. It has no <subobject classifier>: the classifier would have two elements, hence would be left-zero or right-zero, while its characteristic maps on a two-by-two product could not single out a one-point subalgebra.

= Idempotent element of a semigroup
{parent=Semigroup}
{title2=$e^2=e$}

= Semigroup idempotent
{synonym}

An element of a <semigroup> is idempotent when multiplying it by itself leaves it unchanged. Every element of a left-zero or right-zero semigroup is idempotent, whereas a <group> has only its identity as an idempotent. The <Ellis-Numakura lemma> guarantees such an element in a nonempty compact one-sided topological semigroup. Additive idempotence on ultrafilters gives the <idempotent ultrafilter> relation $p+p=p$.

= Semigroup presentation
{parent=Semigroup}
{title2=$\langle X\mid u_i=v_i\rangle$}

A semigroup presentation specifies generators $X$ and equalities between nonempty words. The resulting <semigroup> is the quotient of the free semigroup $X^+$ by the smallest congruence containing these equalities. Two words are equal precisely when one can be transformed into the other by finitely many replacements in contexts, using the defining equalities in either direction.

= Finite semigroup presentation
{parent=Semigroup presentation}

A semigroup presentation is finite when both its generator set and its list of defining equalities are finite. A finite transition table for a <Turing machine> can be encoded this way: state-and-tape words simulate transitions, boundary rules supply blank cells, and halting-state rules erase the configuration to one distinguished symbol.

= Semigroup simulation of a Turing machine
{parent=Finite semigroup presentation}
{title2=$hq_1wh=q$}

A finite deterministic <Turing machine> can be represented by a <finite semigroup presentation> whose generators are tape symbols, state symbols, a boundary marker $h$, and an accept symbol $q$. Defining equalities replace the local scanned symbol and state according to machine instructions, with separate boundary cases for implicit blanks. Only terminal-state equalities erase tape letters and then replace $hq_Hh$ by $q$. A derivation from an initial description to $q$ must encounter a terminal state before its first erasure. Simulation equalities preserve halting membership in either direction: for a deterministic transition $C\to D$, $C$ halts if and only if $D$ halts. Thus reverse use of defining equalities cannot create a false halting certificate. Boundary and erasure rules require explicit checking for every chosen encoding.

= Subsemigroup
{parent=Semigroup}

A subsemigroup is a nonempty subset of a <semigroup> closed under its <associative operation>. In a <compact Hausdorff space> carrying a <left-topological semigroup>, descending chains of nonempty compact subsemigroups have a nonempty compact subsemigroup as their intersection. This supplies the minimal object used in the <Ellis–Numakura lemma>.

= Monoid
{parent=Semigroup}
{wiki}

A monoid is a <semigroup> with a two-sided identity element.

= Monoid action
{parent=Monoid}
{title2=$M\times A\to A$}

A left monoid action assigns each $m\in M$ a map $a\mapsto m\cdot a$ with $1\cdot a=a$ and $(mn)\cdot a=m\cdot(n\cdot a)$. It need not be invertible or injective. A left <M-set> is equivalently a covariant functor from the one-object category determined by $M$.

= Right monoid action
{parent=Monoid action}
{title2=$X\times M\to X,\quad(xm)n=x(mn)$}

A right <monoid action> has $x1=x$ and $(xm)n=x(mn)$. It is a contravariant <functor> from the one-object category of the <monoid> to sets. The regular right action is multiplication on $M$ itself. Products of right actions use the diagonal action.

= Exponential of right monoid actions
{parent=Right monoid action}
{title2=$Y^X=\operatorname{Hom}_M(M\times X,Y)$}

Give $M\times X$ the diagonal <right monoid action>. On its <equivariant maps> into $Y$, define $(ek)(g,x)=e(kg,x)$. The <evaluation map of an exponential object> is $e(1,x)$ and <currying> of $h:Z\times X\to Y$ is $\widehat h(z)(g,x)=h(zg,x)$. Both maps are equivariant, and they are inverse under evaluation. The left multiplication $kg$ in this right action is essential in a noncommutative <monoid>.

= Exponential of right group actions
{parent=Exponential of right monoid actions}
{title2=$(fg)(x)=f(xg^{-1})g$}

For a <group>, evaluation at the identity identifies the <monoid> exponential with all set functions $X\to Y$. Its inverse is $e_f(g,x)=f(xg^{-1})g$, and the resulting function action is the displayed formula. Fixed functions are precisely <equivariant maps>. The inverse on the input is necessary for equivariance of evaluation and for the right-action law.

= Equivariant map of monoid sets
{parent=Monoid action}
{title2=$h(m\cdot a)=m\cdot h(a)$}

= Equivariant maps of monoid sets
{synonym}

An equivariant map $h:A\to B$ between left <M-sets> obeys $h(m\cdot a)=m\cdot h(a)$. These are the natural transformations of the corresponding functors from the one-object monoid category.

= M-set
{parent=Monoid action}
{c}
{title2=$m\cdot a$}

An M-set is a set carrying a <monoid action>. This terminology here uses left actions. Morphisms are <equivariant maps of monoid sets>, and the resulting <functor category> $[M,\mathbf{Set}]$ is an <elementary topos>.

= Monoid homomorphism
{parent=Monoid}
{title2=$h(xy)=h(x)h(y),\quad h(1_M)=1_N$}

A <monoid> homomorphism $h:M\to N$ preserves multiplication and identity: $h(xy)=h(x)h(y)$ and $h(1_M)=1_N$. It therefore preserves every finite ordered product, including the empty product. The <monoids> with these maps form the category $\mathbf{Mon}$.

= Monoid automorphism
{parent=Monoid}

A monoid automorphism is a bijective operation- and identity-preserving self-map of a <monoid>. Its inverse is also a monoid homomorphism. Permuting <prime numbers> and extending by <prime factorization> gives all automorphisms of the multiplicative monoid of positive natural numbers; extending by $0\mapsto0$ gives automorphisms of the multiplicative monoid including zero.

= Free monoid
{parent=Monoid}
{wiki}

The free monoid on an alphabet consists of all finite words in its letters, including the empty word, under concatenation. Distinct words represent distinct elements.

= Commutative monoid
{parent=Monoid}
{wiki}

A commutative monoid is a <monoid> whose operation is commutative. The natural numbers with addition form a commutative monoid that is not an <abelian group>, because positive elements have no additive inverses.

= Free commutative monoid
{parent=Commutative monoid}
{title2=$\mathbb N^d$}

The <free commutative monoid> on $d$ generators consists of the nonnegative integral sums of those generators, with unique coefficients. Its <semigroup algebra> over a <commutative ring> is the <polynomial ring> in $d$ variables. In a smooth toric chart, the dual-cone lattice points form such a monoid, yielding affine-space coordinates.

= Cancellative commutative monoid
{parent=Commutative monoid}
{title2=$a+c=b+c\implies a=b$}

A <commutative monoid> is cancellative when $a+c=b+c$ implies $a=b$. Cancellation makes the natural map into its <Grothendieck group> injective.

= Congruence submonoid of the positive integers
{parent=Cancellative commutative monoid}
{title2=$S_m=\{n\geq1:n\equiv1\pmod m\}$}

For an integer $m\geq2$, the positive integers congruent to one modulo $m$ form a <cancellative commutative monoid> under multiplication. The identity is one, and it is the only unit. Proper factors outside this congruence class do not witness reducibility inside the monoid. Hence <irreducible elements of a commutative monoid> need not coincide with ordinary <prime numbers>.

= Nonunique factorization in a congruence submonoid
{parent=Congruence submonoid of the positive integers}
{title2=$21\cdot221=51\cdot91$}

The <congruence submonoid of the positive integers> with modulus ten has four distinct <irreducible elements of a commutative monoid> $21,221,51,91$ with equal pairwise products. Their ordinary prime factors are respectively $(3,7),(13,17),(3,17),(7,13)$. None of these proper prime factors is congruent to one modulo ten. The <Euclid lemma> proves that a product of two distinct primes has no other proper positive divisors, so all four are irreducible in this monoid. This gives nonunique factorization without assuming the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.

= Irreducible element of a commutative monoid
{parent=Cancellative commutative monoid}
{title2=$a=bc\Longrightarrow b\in M^\times\ \text{or}\ c\in M^\times$}

= Irreducible elements of a commutative monoid
{synonym}

A nonunit element of a <commutative monoid> is irreducible if each factorization has a unit factor. In a submonoid of the positive integers under multiplication, the only unit is one. Irreducibility is relative to the allowed factors: a composite integer can be irreducible in a smaller monoid even though it is not a <prime number>.

= Torsion-free cancellative commutative monoid
{parent=Cancellative commutative monoid}
{title2=$na=nb\implies a=b$}

A <cancellative commutative monoid> is torsion-free in this sense when $na=nb$ implies $a=b$ for every positive integer $n$. Its <Grothendieck group> is then a <torsion-free Abelian group>, because $n(a-b)=0$ forces $na=nb$. This is the condition inherited by a submonoid of a <torsion-free Abelian group>.

= Category of commutative monoids
{title2=$\mathbf{CMon}$}
{parent=Commutative monoid}

This <category> has <commutative monoids> and their homomorphisms. Pointwise addition makes each hom-set a commutative monoid, and composition is additive in both variables. Finite cartesian products are also coproducts, using the coordinate injections with zero in the other coordinates and summing images. Therefore it is a <semi-additive category>, but it is not an <additive category>. The reflexive pair from the submonoid $\{(m,n):m\leq n\leq2m\}$ of $\mathbb N^2$ to $\mathbb N$, using the two projections and section $m\mapsto(m,m)$, has an arrow from $1$ to $2$ and none from $2$ to $1$ in its represented graph. Thus a <reflexive pair> here need not yield a <groupoid>.

= Additive semigroup
{parent=Semigroup}

An additive semigroup is a <semigroup> whose operation is written as addition.

= Faces of an additive monoid

= Semigroup face
{synonym}

A face $F$ of an additive monoid $S$ is a submonoid such that $a+b\in F$ implies $a,b\in F$. If $S=C\cap M$ for a rational polyhedral cone, its faces are precisely $S\cap\nu$ for faces $\nu$ of $C$. Finite generation and clearing rational denominators identify these monoid faces with the exposed faces of the cone.

= Algebra over a field

= Algebras over a field
{synonym}

An algebra over a <field> $k$ is a <vector space> $A$ over $k$ equipped with a bilinear multiplication $A\times A\to A$.

= Poisson algebra
{c}
{parent=Algebra over a field}
{wiki}
{title2=$\{fg,h\}=f\{g,h\}+g\{f,h\}$}

A <Poisson algebra> is a commutative associative algebra with a <Lie bracket> that is a derivation in each argument. Smooth functions on a <symplectic manifold> are its standard example. The linear span of moment-map components may form a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra, while closing those components under multiplication normally produces a much larger <Poisson algebra>.

= Poisson bracket from a central deformation parameter
{c}
{parent=Poisson algebra}
{title2=$\{\bar a,\bar b\}=h^{-1}[a,b]\bmod h$}

Let $h$ be central and a non-zero-divisor in an associative algebra, with commutative quotient modulo $h$. Dividing commutators by $h$ and reducing modulo $h$ is independent of the lifts, since changing a lift by $hc$ changes the quotient by a commutator, zero modulo $h$. Antisymmetry, the Leibniz rule and the Jacobi identity descend from the corresponding commutator identities. For the <order filtration of a Weyl algebra>, the convention is $\{\xi_i,x_j\}=\delta_{ij}$, with the opposite sign for $\{x_i,\xi_j\}$.

= Algebra isomorphism
{parent=Algebra over a field}

An algebra isomorphism is a bijective <linear map> preserving multiplication and identity between two <algebras> over the same <field>. In classification of a <square-zero extension of an algebra>, an equivalence must additionally induce the identity on the prescribed quotient and kernel.

= Division algebra
{parent=Algebra over a field}

A division algebra over a <field> $k$ is a <division ring> equipped with a unital <algebra> structure over $k$, with $k$ mapping into its <center of an associative algebra>. Every nonzero element is invertible; it need not be finite-dimensional over $k$.

= Finite-dimensional real division-algebra classification
{parent=Division algebra}
{title2=$D\cong\mathbb R,\mathbb C,\mathbb H$}

= Frobenius theorem on real division algebras
{c}
{synonym}

A finite-dimensional associative <division algebra> over $\mathbb R$ is one of the three displayed algebras. A nonreal element has an irreducible quadratic <minimal polynomial> and yields $i^2=-1$. Its centralizer is a <division algebra> over central $\mathbb C$, hence is $\mathbb C$ by splitting <minimal polynomials>. Decompose the whole algebra into the plus and minus eigenspaces of conjugation by $i$. If the minus space is zero, the algebra is $\mathbb C$; otherwise it is $\mathbb Cj$, since multiplying by $j^{-1}$ takes that space into the centralizer. One has $jz=\overline z j$ and $j^2\in\mathbb R$. Division forces $j^2<0$, which can be rescaled to $-1$, yielding $\mathbb H$. Of these, only $\mathbb R,\mathbb H$ have center $\mathbb R$.

= Central division algebra
{parent=Division algebra}
{title2=$Z(D)=K$}

A <central division algebra> over $K$ is a finite-dimensional <division algebra> whose center is exactly $K$. It is a <central simple algebra>. Every <Brauer group> class has a unique <central division algebra> representative up to isomorphism.

= Finite-dimensional division algebra over an algebraically closed field
{parent=Division algebra}
{title2=$D=k$}

Let $D$ be a finite-dimensional <division algebra> over an <algebraically closed field> $k$. Left multiplication by $a\in D$ has an <eigenvalue> $\lambda\in k$ and a nonzero eigenvector $v$. Thus $(a-\lambda)v=0$; multiplying by $v^{-1}$ gives $a=\lambda$. Every element is scalar, so $D=k$.

= Quaternion
{parent=Division algebra}
{title2=$\mathbb H$}
{wiki}

A quaternion is $a+bi+cj+dk$ with real coefficients, where $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$. They form a noncommutative real <division algebra>. The norm squared is $a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2$, and every nonzero quaternion has inverse its conjugate divided by that squared norm. The unit quaternions form the <Lie group> $S^3$.

= Complex matrix representation of quaternions
{parent=Quaternion}
{title2=$\begin{pmatrix}a+ib&c+id\\-c+id&a-ib\end{pmatrix}$}

The displayed complex <matrices>, with real coefficients, form a four-dimensional real <associative algebra>. Their three imaginary <basis> <matrices> square to minus the identity and satisfy $JK=L$, $KL=J$, $LJ=K$, with the reversed products negative. For $\alpha=aI+bJ+cK+dL$, quaternionic conjugation gives $\alpha\overline\alpha=(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)I$. Every nonzero <matrix> therefore has inverse $\overline\alpha/(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)$ inside this algebra. This realizes the real <division algebra> of <quaternions> within $M_2(\mathbb C)$; it is not a complex <vector> subspace.

= Unit quaternion
{parent=Quaternion}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sp}(1)\cong S^3$}

A <quaternion> of norm $1$ is a unit quaternion. They form the <topological group> $\operatorname{Sp}(1)$ and identify with the sphere $S^3\subset\mathbb R^4$. Multiplication and inversion preserve the norm. The <Hurewicz theorem> identifies $\pi_3(S^3)$ with $H_3(S^3;\mathbb Z)$; hence <homotopy-group addition in a topological group> shows that $q\mapsto q^m$ has <mapping degree> $m$, rather than $m^2$.

= Subalgebra
{parent=Algebra over a field}

A <linear subspace> of an <algebra over a field> closed under multiplication is a subalgebra. If a unital subalgebra is intended, it must also contain the ambient identity. A closed subalgebra of a normed algebra is closed in the <norm topology>.

For a <field> $k$, a homomorphism of $k$-algebras is a unital <ring homomorphism> preserving scalar multiplication by every element of $k$. = Points of a noncommutative algebra
{parent=Algebra homomorphism over a field}
{title2=$\operatorname{Hom}_{k\text{-alg}}(A,R)$}

An $R$-point of a unital <algebra over a field> $A$ is a unital <algebra homomorphism over a field> $A\to R$. For the <quantum plane> over the <complex numbers>, scalar points lie on the two coordinate axes when $q\ne1$, while matrix-valued points are pairs $X,Y$ with $YX=qXY$.

= Spectral decomposition of a quantum-plane point
{parent=Points of a noncommutative algebra}
{title2=$V=V_X\oplus V_Y\oplus U$}

For finite matrices satisfying $YX=qXY$, with $q$ not a <root of unity>, $Y$ sends the <generalized eigenspace> of $X$ at $\lambda$ into the one at $q^{-1}\lambda$. Finiteness of the <spectrum> makes $Y$ nilpotent on the sum of the nonzero $X$ <generalized eigenspaces>. Exchanging $X,Y$ gives the corresponding statement for $X$. Splitting the zero <generalized eigenspace> of $X$ by that of $Y$ gives the displayed invariant direct sum; both operators are nilpotent on $U$.

= Structure constant

For a basis $(e_i)$ of a finite-dimensional algebra, its structure constants are the scalars defined by

= Finitely generated algebra
{parent=Algebra over a field}
{wiki}

A $k$-algebra is finitely generated when finitely many elements generate it under addition, multiplication, and scalar multiplication. Equivalently, it is a quotient of a polynomial ring $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.

= Infinite cotangent space obstructs finite algebra generation
{parent=Finitely generated algebra}
{title2=$\dim_k(I/I^2)=\infty\Longrightarrow A\text{ is not finitely generated}$}

Let $A$ be a commutative algebra over a <field> $k$, with a homomorphism $A\to k$ restricting to the identity on $k$, and let $I$ be its kernel. If $A$ is generated by finitely many elements, subtracting their images in $k$ gives finitely many generators in $I$. Every polynomial in those generators, modulo $I^2$, is a linear combination of their classes. Thus $I/I^2$, the <algebraic cotangent space> at this point, is finite-dimensional. For $A=k+xk[x,y]$ and $I=xk[x,y]$, the classes $xy^j$, $j\ge0$, form an infinite basis modulo $I^2=x^2k[x,y]$. This is a subalgebra of a <polynomial ring> which is not a <finitely generated algebra>.

= Finite presentation of an algebra
{parent=Finitely generated algebra}
{title2=$A\cong k[x_1,\dots,x_N]/(f_1,\dots,f_s)$}

A presentation of an <algebra over a field> with finitely many generators and relations. The <Hilbert basis theorem> ensures that every <finitely generated algebra> over a field admits such a presentation. Its <module of Kähler differentials> is presented by the <Jacobian matrix> of the relations.

= Finite generation from finitely many principal localizations
{parent=Finitely generated algebra}

Suppose a $k$-algebra $A$ has elements $f_i,g_i$ with $\sum_i g_if_i=1$, and each $A_{f_i}$ is a <finitely generated algebra>. Let $B\subseteq A$ contain the $f_i,g_i$ and numerators of finite generating sets of all $A_{f_i}$. Then $B$ is finitely generated and $B_{f_i}=A_{f_i}$. For $a\in A$, clear denominators so that all $f_i^Na$ belong to $B$. A <unit-ideal identity for powers> gives $1=\sum_i c_if_i^N$ in $B$, hence $a=\sum_i c_i f_i^Na\in B$. Thus $A=B$.

= Artin--Tate lemma
{c}
{parent=Finitely generated algebra}
{wiki}

Let $A\subseteq B\subseteq C$ be rings. If $A$ is Noetherian, $C$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra, and $C$ is a finite $B$-module, then $B$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra.

= Finite generation descends along a field extension
{parent=Finitely generated algebra}

Let $L/k$ be a <field extension>. If $A\otimes_kL$ is a <finitely generated algebra> over $L$, then $A$ is finitely generated over $k$. Choose finitely many generators of $A\otimes_kL$ and collect the finitely many coefficients from $A$ occurring in them. The $k$-subalgebra $A_0$ generated by those coefficients satisfies $(A/A_0)\otimes_kL=0$, and <faithfully flat module>[faithful flatness] of $L/k$ gives $A=A_0$.

= Clifford algebra
{c}
{wiki}
{parent=Algebra over a field}

For a vector space with quadratic form $Q$, its Clifford algebra is generated by vectors subject to $v^2=Q(v)1$. In a basis this is equivalently $\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu+\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu=2\eta^{\mu\nu}1$.

= Complex Clifford spinor dimension
{parent=Clifford algebra}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathrm{Dirac}_{2k}=2^k$}

After complexifying a nondegenerate metric in $D=2k$ dimensions, pair <gamma matrices> into $k$ fermionic creation and annihilation operators. Their binary occupation states span $2^k$ dimensions, and the <gamma matrices> generate all endomorphisms of this space. The Dirac module is therefore irreducible as a Clifford module. The even algebra preserves the chirality grading, splitting its Lorentz-spinor representation into two irreducible Weyl modules of dimension $2^{k-1}$. In $D=2k+1$, adjoining the old <chirality matrix> as the extra gamma gives an irreducible $2^k$-dimensional <Spin group> representation with no Weyl splitting. The two possible signs of that extra gamma distinguish Clifford modules but give equivalent <Spin group> modules.

= Clifford bundle
{c}
{synonym}

A <vector bundle> $E$ with a fibrewise <quadratic form> $Q$ defines a bundle whose fibre over $x$ is the <Clifford algebra> $\operatorname{Cl}(E_x,Q_x)$. When the polar form is a <nondegenerate bilinear form>, local orthonormal frames identify its fibres with a fixed <Clifford algebra>, and orthogonal transition maps induce the algebra transition maps. A <Clifford module bundle> is a bundle of modules over these algebras, rather than the algebra bundle itself. For a <Riemannian manifold>, use its <cotangent bundle> and the metric <quadratic form>, with the sign convention specified.

= Clifford multiplication
{c}
{parent=Clifford algebra}
{title2=$c(v)c(w)+c(w)c(v)=-2\langle v,w\rangle$}

Clifford multiplication is the action of a <Clifford algebra> on a <module>. The displayed relation uses the negative <Riemannian metric> convention; in a compatible <Hermitian inner product>, $c(v)^*=-c(v)$ for real $v$. Another convention writes $C(v)=ic(v)$, giving self-adjoint generators with positive anticommutator. Switching between these conventions switches a geometric <Dirac operator> between self-adjoint and skew-adjoint realizations.

= Dimension bound from paired Clifford involutions
{parent=Clifford algebra}
{title2=$\dim V\geq2^n$}

For $2n$ hermitian Euclidean Clifford generators, the commuting hermitian involutions $J_j=i\gamma^{2j-1}\gamma^{2j}$ admit a common eigenvector. Each odd-indexed generator flips one joint eigenvalue. The $2^n$ resulting vectors are nonzero and mutually orthogonal. Thus any nonzero invariant complex representation has dimension at least $2^n$.

= Real independence of Clifford-generated vectors
{parent=Clifford algebra}

For hermitian generators satisfying $\{\gamma^I,\gamma^J\}_+=2\delta^{IJ}$ and any nonzero vector $v$, the vectors $\gamma^Iv$ are linearly independent over the real numbers. Indeed, $A=\sum_Ia_I\gamma^I$ with real $a_I$ obeys $A^2=(\sum_Ia_I^2)\mathbf1$, so $\|Av\|^2=(\sum_Ia_I^2)\|v\|^2$. Complex coefficients need a different argument.

= Gamma-matrix vector sandwich
{parent=Gamma matrices}
{title2=$\gamma_{ab}\gamma^m\gamma^{ab}=-(D-1)(D-4)\gamma^m$}

For <gamma matrices> with $\{\gamma^a,\gamma^b\}=2\eta^{ab}$ and $\gamma^{ab}=[\gamma^a,\gamma^b]/2$, contracted <Clifford algebra> gives $\gamma_a\gamma^m\gamma^a=(2-D)\gamma^m$. For signature $+---$, use $\gamma^0=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&I_2\\I_2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $\gamma^i=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\sigma^i\\-\sigma^i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, where $\sigma^i$ are <Pauli matrices>. = Hermitizing matrix
{parent=Gamma matrices}
{title2=$H\gamma^a=(H\gamma^a)^\dagger$}

A <Hermitizing matrix> for a set of <gamma matrices> is an invertible <Hermitian matrix> $H$ satisfying $\gamma^{a\dagger}=H\gamma^aH^{-1}$. Equivalently, each $H\gamma^a$ is Hermitian. It defines a covariant adjoint $\bar\psi=\psi^\dagger H$. If $\gamma'^a=M\gamma^aM^{-1}$, then $H'=M^{-\dagger}HM^{-1}$ has the same property. In a standard Lorentzian <Dirac basis>, $H=\gamma^0$ is a conventional choice.

= Gamma matrix adjoint and transpose identities
{parent=Gamma matrices}
{title2=$\gamma^{a\dagger}=H\gamma^aH^{-1}$}

In the standard <Dirac representation of the gamma matrices>, $(\gamma^0)^\dagger=\gamma^0$ and $(\gamma^i)^\dagger=-\gamma^i$, so $\gamma^{a\dagger}=\gamma^0\gamma^a\gamma^0$. The transpose signs in that representation are positive for $a=0,2$ and negative for $a=1,3$. The <charge-conjugation matrix> expresses the basis-independent intertwining relation $C^{-1}\gamma^aC=-(\gamma^a)^T$. Under an arbitrary nonunitary <similarity transformation>, the <Hermitizing matrix> changes by inverse Hermitian congruence; it need not equal the transformed temporal <gamma matrix>.

= Gamma matrix trace identities
{parent=Gamma matrices}

With metric $(+---)$, $\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ and $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$, odd ordinary <gamma matrix> traces vanish, $\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=4g^{\mu\nu}$, and
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma)=4(g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}-g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}+g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\rho}),\qquad \operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma)=-4i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}.
$$
The first follows by repeated <Clifford algebra> anticommutation and cyclicity of the <trace>. The second is an antisymmetric tensor, whose normalization is fixed by the $0123$ component.

= Hopf algebra
{c}
{parent=Algebra over a field}
{wiki}

A Hopf algebra is a compatible algebra and coalgebra equipped with a counit and antipode. Coordinate rings of <affine algebraic groups> are commutative Hopf algebras.

= Primitive element of a Hopf algebra
{parent=Hopf algebra}
{title2=$\Delta x=x\otimes1+1\otimes x$}

= Primitive elements of a Hopf algebra
{synonym}

An element $x$ of positive degree is primitive when $\Delta x=x\otimes1+1\otimes x$. Graded <commutators> of primitives are primitive, so they form a <graded Lie algebra>. In a <universal enveloping algebra> over $\mathbb Q$, the <primitive elements of a Hopf algebra> are exactly its given <Lie algebra>: the associated graded algebra of the word-length filtration is symmetric, where no nonzero homogeneous polynomial of length greater than one is additive under the diagonal.

= Module algebra
{parent=Hopf algebra}
{title2=$h(ab)=\sum(h_{(1)}a)(h_{(2)}b)$}

An <algebra over a field> acted on by a <bialgebra> is a module algebra when multiplication and unit are <module> maps: the displayed product identity holds and $h1=\varepsilon(h)1$. This expresses the product rule determined by <comultiplication>.

= Quantum enveloping algebra of sl2
{parent=Hopf algebra}
{title2=$U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$}

Over a <field> with $q\ne0$ and $q^2\ne1$, this <Hopf algebra> has generators $E,F,K,K^{-1}$ and relations $KE=q^2EK$, $KF=q^{-2}FK$ and $EF-FE=(K-K^{-1})/(q-q^{-1})$. One <comultiplication> convention is $\Delta E=E\otimes K+1\otimes E$, $\Delta F=F\otimes1+K^{-1}\otimes F$ and $\Delta K=K\otimes K$. Its <counit> takes $E,F$ to zero and $K$ to one. The <antipode> takes $E$ to $-EK^{-1}$, $F$ to $-KF$ and $K$ to $K^{-1}$.

= Highest-weight string for quantum sl2
{parent=Quantum enveloping algebra of sl2}
{title2=$EF^jv=[j]\frac{\lambda q^{1-j}-\lambda^{-1}q^{j-1}}{q-q^{-1}}F^{j-1}v$}

If $Ev=0$ and $Kv=\lambda v$, induction using the defining commutator gives the displayed identity. When $q$ is not a <root of unity>, a finite string of length $n+1$ in a <simple module> has $\lambda=\epsilon q^n$, $\epsilon=\pm1$. Its distinct $K$ <eigenvalues> make the string vectors independent, and the nonzero raising coefficients prove simplicity.

= Quantum integer
{parent=Quantum enveloping algebra of sl2}
{title2=$[n]_q=(q^n-q^{-n})/(q-q^{-1})$}

The symmetric quantum integer is the displayed <Laurent polynomial>, with $[0]_q=0$. It obeys $[n+1]_q=q^{-1}[n]_q+q^n$ and $[r]_q[s+1]_q-[s]_q[r+1]_q=[r-s]_q$. At a primitive odd $\ell$th <root of unity>, $[n]_q=0$ exactly when $\ell$ divides $n$.

= RTT construction
{c}
{parent=Hopf algebra}
{title2=$RT_1T_2=T_2T_1R$}

= Faddeev-Reshetikhin-Takhtajan construction
{c}
{synonym}

Given a <Yang-Baxter R-matrix>, take generators $t_{ij}$ subject to the displayed matrix relation, with $T_1=T\otimes I$, $T_2=I\otimes T$. The coproduct $\Delta t_{ij}=\sum_k t_{ik}\otimes t_{kj}$ and counit $\varepsilon(t_{ij})=\delta_{ij}$ give a <bialgebra>. An antipode requires additional inverses or an appropriate determinant quotient; an arbitrary RTT bialgebra is not automatically a Hopf algebra.

= FRT bialgebra
{c}
{parent=RTT construction}
{title2=$CT_1T_2=T_1T_2C$}

Given an invertible <braiding> $C$ on $V\otimes V$, the universal <bialgebra> coacting on $V$ for which $C$ is a <comodule> map is generated by the matrix coefficients $t_{ij}$. Its <comultiplication> is $\Delta t_{ij}=\sum_k t_{ik}\otimes t_{kj}$. Colinearity gives quadratic relations. Writing $R=\tau C$ turns the relation into the familiar <RTT construction> with the appropriate ordered tensor factors. A <Yang-Baxter R-matrix> supplies a scalar <coquasitriangular structure>.

= Quantum determinant
{parent=RTT construction}
{title2=$D_q=ad-qbc$}

For the two-by-two <RTT construction> with the standard <Yang-Baxter R-matrix>, the displayed determinant commutes with the matrix generators and satisfies $\Delta D_q=D_q\otimes D_q$ and $\varepsilon(D_q)=1$. Imposing $D_q=1$ gives the <coordinate Hopf algebra of quantum SL2>; adjoining its inverse instead gives the quantum general-linear coordinate algebra.

= Coordinate Hopf algebra of quantum SL2
{parent=RTT construction}
{title2=$\mathcal O_q(\operatorname{SL}_2)$}

The generators $a,b,c,d$ obey $ab=qba$, $ac=qca$, $bd=qdb$, $cd=qdc$, $bc=cb$, $ad-da=(q-q^{-1})bc$ and $ad-qbc=1$. Matrix multiplication gives the coproduct, and the antipode matrix is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}d&-q^{-1}b\\-qc&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. For $q\ne0,\pm1$, its quotient with $c=0$, $a=u$, $d=u^{-1}$ and $b=v$ is the quantum Laurent-plane algebra $uv=qvu$, proving noncommutativity. The same quotient distinguishes $\Delta b$ from its flipped coproduct.

= Cocommutative Hopf algebra
{parent=Hopf algebra}

A <Hopf algebra> is cocommutative when flipping the two tensor factors leaves its coproduct unchanged. A <group algebra> has coproduct $g\mapsto g\otimes g$, so it is cocommutative even for a nonabelian group, whose group algebra is not commutative.

= Commutative Hopf algebra
{parent=Hopf algebra}

A <Hopf algebra> is commutative when its multiplication is commutative. This is independent of whether its coproduct is cocommutative; function algebras of finite nonabelian groups give commutative but noncocommutative examples.

= Function Hopf algebra of a finite group
{parent=Commutative Hopf algebra}
{title2=$k^G$}

Functions on a finite group form a <Hopf algebra> under pointwise multiplication, with $(\Delta f)(g,h)=f(gh)$, $\varepsilon(f)=f(1)$ and $(Sf)(g)=f(g^{-1})$. The coproduct is cocommutative exactly when the group is abelian, since delta functions distinguish distinct products $gh,hg$.

For a left <module> over a <Hopf algebra>, a vector $v$ is invariant when $hv=\varepsilon(h)v$. In the <internal hom for modules over a Hopf algebra>, invariant maps are exactly the <R-module homomorphisms> for the acting algebra.

The full $\operatorname{Hom}_k(M,N)$ carries the action $(hf)(u)=\sum h_{(1)}f(S(h_{(2)})u)$, making it the internal hom right adjoint to $-\otimes M$. No finite-dimensionality or inverse <antipode> is needed.

= Quiver
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki=Quiver_(mathematics)}

A quiver is a directed multigraph, allowing loops and multiple arrows.

= Kronecker quiver
{parent=Quiver}
{c}
{title2=$K_2$}

The Kronecker quiver has two vertices and two parallel arrows from the first to the second. For a representation with arrows $I,N$, an <endomorphism> consists of the same matrix at both vertices and must commute with $N$. If $N$ is a single <nilpotent Jordan block>, this centralizer is $k[N]$, yielding an indecomposable non-brick when the block size exceeds one.

= Acyclic quiver
{parent=Quiver}

An <acyclic quiver> has no oriented cycle. For a finite <quiver>, this is equivalent to finite-dimensionality of its <path algebra>: paths have bounded length without cycles, while powers of a cycle are infinitely many distinct basis elements.

= Subspace quiver
{parent=Quiver}
{title2=$S_r$}

This <quiver> has $r$ source vertices, each with one arrow to a common sink. Its <path algebra> has the vertex <idempotents> and the arrows as basis. After extracting source kernels from a representation, its remaining arrows identify the source spaces with subspaces of the sink.

= Four-subspace quiver
{parent=Subspace quiver}
{title2=$S_4$}

The <four-subspace quiver> has four sources with arrows into one sink. Four one-dimensional sources mapped to the lines $ke_1$, $ke_2$, $k(e_1+e_2)$ and $k(\lambda e_1+e_2)$ in $k^2$ give a <brick module> for every $\lambda$. Preserving the first three lines forces scalar endomorphisms. Its <Tits form of a quiver> is zero, so the <Ringel form> gives one-dimensional self-extensions.

= Three-subspace quiver
{parent=Subspace quiver}
{title2=$S_3$}

The <subspace quiver> with three sources reduces to the problem of three subspaces in one vector space. The <three-subspace decomposition> gives eleven indecomposables with vertex dimensions at most one, and one <exceptional indecomposable of the three-subspace quiver>.

= Three-subspace decomposition
{parent=Three-subspace quiver}

Every triple of subspaces is a direct sum of one-dimensional membership blocks and two-dimensional three-line blocks. Split off the triple intersection, then the pairwise intersections using projections killing the third subspace. Next split complements to $U_i\cap(U_j+U_\ell)$, using projections killing the other two subspaces. The remaining subspaces are pairwise disjoint and each lies in the sum of the other two. Their sum is $U_1\oplus U_2$, with $U_3$ the graph of an isomorphism $U_1\to U_2$. A graph basis splits this into two-dimensional blocks. A complement to the total span supplies zero-membership blocks. This elementary argument classifies the <three-subspace quiver>.

= Exceptional indecomposable of the three-subspace quiver
{parent=Three-subspace decomposition}
{title2=$\dim X=(1,1,1;2)$}

Take sources $k,k,k$, sink $k^2$, and arrows sending $1$ to $e_1,e_2,e_1+e_2$. An endomorphism preserving these three lines must be scalar, so the representation has <endomorphism ring> $k$ and is a <brick module>. It is the only indecomposable of the <three-subspace quiver> with a two-dimensional sink.

= Kronecker quiver with one arrow
{parent=Quiver}
{c}
{title2=$K_1$}

= One-arrow quiver
{synonym}

The <quiver> has two vertices and one arrow. Splitting a representation's kernel, image and complementary target decomposes it into source-only and sink-only simples and copies of the identity $k\to k$. These are its three indecomposables. Fixed-dimension isomorphism classes are <rank orbits of a matrix under left-right multiplication>.

= Quiver with r loops
{parent=Quiver}
{title2=$L_r$}

This <quiver> has one vertex and $r$ loops. Paths are words in the loops, so its <path algebra> is a <free associative algebra>. A representation assigns an arbitrary endomorphism to each loop.

= Quiver with one loop
{parent=Quiver with r loops}
{title2=$L_1$}

Its <path algebra> is $k[t]$. Finite-dimensional representations are vector spaces with a single endomorphism. Thus oriented cycles can give an infinite-dimensional algebra even when the representations studied are finite-dimensional.

= Sink of a quiver
{parent=Quiver}

A vertex of a <quiver> is a sink when no arrow has that vertex as its source. Dually, a source is a vertex at which no arrow ends.

= Representation of a quiver
{parent=Quiver}

= Quiver representation
{synonym}

A representation of a quiver over a field assigns a vector space to each vertex and a linear map to each arrow. A morphism is a compatible family of vertexwise linear maps.

= Interval representations of an equioriented three-vertex quiver
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$I[a,b],\quad1\le a\le b\le3$}

For $1\to2\to3$, $I[a,b]$ has $k$ on the interval of vertices $a,\ldots,b$, zero elsewhere and identity maps along its internal arrows. These six representations are exactly the indecomposables. To decompose $V_1\xrightarrow fV_2\xrightarrow gV_3$, split $\operatorname{im}f\cap\ker g$, its complements inside each of $\operatorname{im}f$ and $\ker g$, and a complement to their sum. Lift the image basis through $f$ and transport the complementary nonkernel basis through $g$. This yields interval blocks directly, without the <Gabriel theorem>.

= Rank classification of a two-step linear map
{parent=Interval representations of an equioriented three-vertex quiver}
{title2=$(r,s,t)=(\operatorname{rank}f,\operatorname{rank}g,\operatorname{rank}(gf))$}

For fixed dimensions $(d_1,d_2,d_3)$, a pair $V_1\xrightarrow fV_2\xrightarrow gV_3$ is classified up to vertexwise change of basis by its three ranks $r,s,t$. The multiplicities of its <interval representations of an equioriented three-vertex quiver> are $m_{13}=t$, $m_{12}=r-t$, $m_{23}=s-t$, $m_{11}=d_1-r$, $m_{22}=d_2-r-s+t$ and $m_{33}=d_3-s$. Nonnegativity is precisely the existence condition. For dimensions $(2,2,2)$ there are ten orbits, of which $(2,2,2)$ is open; its boundary has nine orbits. The two cases $(1,1,0)$ and $(1,1,1)$ show why the individual arrow ranks are insufficient.

= Quiver representation space
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$\operatorname{Rep}_Q(\mathbf n)$}

= Representation space of a quiver
{synonym}

At fixed dimension vector, $\operatorname{Rep}_Q(\mathbf n)=\prod_{\rho:i\to j}\operatorname{Hom}_k(k^{n_i},k^{n_j})$ is an irreducible <affine space> of dimension $\sum_\rho n_{s(\rho)}n_{t(\rho)}$. Its points are arrow-matrix collections, and its base-change orbits are isomorphism classes.

= Base change action on quiver representations
{parent=Quiver representation space}
{title2=$(g\cdot f)_\rho=g_{t(\rho)}f_\rho g_{s(\rho)}^{-1}$}

The group $\operatorname{GL}(\mathbf n)=\prod_i\operatorname{GL}_{n_i}(k)$ acts by $(g\cdot f)_\rho=g_{t(\rho)}f_\rho g_{s(\rho)}^{-1}$. Inverse entries are regular on a <general linear group>, so this is an <algebraic group action>. Its stabilizer is the <automorphism group of a quiver representation>.

= Normal space to a quiver orbit
{parent=Base change action on quiver representations}
{title2=$N_x\mathcal O_X\cong\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)$}

The infinitesimal <base change action on quiver representations> is $\xi_x(u)_\rho=u_{t(\rho)}x_\rho-x_\rho u_{s(\rho)}$. Its image is the <Zariski tangent space> to the orbit, because the stabilizer is a smooth open subset of the <endomorphism ring>. The <extension complex of quiver representations> identifies the quotient of the ambient tangent space by this image with $\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)$. Consequently <rigid quiver representations have open orbits>.

= Acyclic quiver representations degenerate to zero
{parent=Base change action on quiver representations}

Choose integer vertex weights increasing along every arrow of a finite acyclic <quiver>. Acting at vertex $i$ by $t^{w_i}I$ multiplies each arrow by a positive power of $t$. At $t=0$ all arrows vanish. Thus every orbit closure contains the origin of the <quiver representation space>.

= Projective base change group of a quiver
{parent=Base change action on quiver representations}
{title2=$\operatorname{PGL}(\mathbf n)=\operatorname{GL}(\mathbf n)/\Delta k^\times$}

For a nonzero dimension vector, quotient $\prod_i\operatorname{GL}_{n_i}(k)$ by the common scalar subgroup $(\lambda I_{n_i})_i$. It acts trivially, so the quotient acts algebraically on the <quiver representation space>. This is one scalar quotient, rather than a product of the individual projective general linear groups. At dimension vector zero the action is trivial.

= Automorphism group of a quiver representation
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$\operatorname{Aut}_Q(X)$}

= Quiver automorphism group
{synonym}

This is $\operatorname{End}_Q(X)^\times$, a connected <linear algebraic group> by the <units of a finite-dimensional algebra> argument. It is the stabilizer under base change. For $X=\bigoplus_aM_a^{\oplus m_a}$, reduction modulo the radical gives quotient $\prod_a\operatorname{GL}_{m_a}(k)$.

= Levi decomposition of a quiver automorphism group
{parent=Automorphism group of a quiver representation}
{c}

Fixing a <Krull-Schmidt decomposition> embeds $L=\prod_a\operatorname{GL}_{m_a}(k)$ by its actions on multiplicity spaces. This splits reduction modulo the <Jacobson radical>. Hence $\operatorname{Aut}_Q(X)=(1+J(\operatorname{End}_Q(X)))\rtimes L$, with a closed normal unipotent first factor. The explicit section works in every characteristic.

= Rigid quiver representation
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)=0$}

= Representation without self-extensions
{synonym}

A finite-dimensional <quiver representation> is rigid when $\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)=0$. Its <extension complex of quiver representations> then has surjective differential. Equivalently, it has an open orbit under base change. Rigidity constrains arrow ranks and path identities.

= Rigid quiver representations have open orbits
{parent=Rigid quiver representation}

The stabilizer is the <automorphism group of a quiver representation>, an open set in its endomorphism algebra. Thus $\dim\mathcal O_X=\sum_i n_i^2-\dim\operatorname{End}_Q(X)$. The <Ringel form> gives orbit codimension $\dim\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)$. Since orbits are locally closed and the representation space is irreducible, rigidity is equivalent to an open dense orbit.

= Path identities in a rigid quiver representation
{parent=Rigid quiver representations have open orbits}

A base-change invariant polynomial identity holding on a rigid representation holds on its dense orbit and hence throughout the representation space. A path through nonzero vertex spaces cannot vanish identically there: assign every involved arrow a rank-one map carrying a chosen source vector to the chosen target vector. This works for repeated arrows. Consequently a nonzero path image in a rigid representation cannot be killed by an outgoing arrow into a nonzero vertex space.

= Ringel form
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{c}
{title2=$\langle\mathbf n,\mathbf m\rangle_Q$}

= Euler form of a quiver
{synonym}
{c}

This bilinear form is $\langle\mathbf n,\mathbf m\rangle_Q=\sum_i n_im_i-\sum_{\rho:i\to j}n_im_j$. The <standard projective resolution of a quiver representation> gives its value as $\dim\operatorname{Hom}_Q(X,Y)-\dim\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,Y)$ for the dimension vectors of $X,Y$. Its diagonal is the <Tits form of a quiver>.

= Tits form of a quiver
{parent=Ringel form}
{c}
{title2=$q_Q(\mathbf n)$}

The quadratic form is $q_Q(\mathbf n)=\langle\mathbf n,\mathbf n\rangle_Q=\sum_i n_i^2-\sum_{\rho:i\to j}n_in_j$. It depends on the underlying graph rather than its orientation. For a representation it equals $\dim\operatorname{End}_Q(X)-\dim\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,X)$. Its positive definiteness forces indecomposables to be rigid <brick modules>, using the <Ringel lemma on bricks>.

= Positive root of a quiver
{parent=Tits form of a quiver}
{title2=$q_Q(\mathbf n)=1$}

In the positive definite case this is a nonnegative integer vector with $q_Q(\mathbf n)=1$, so it is nonzero. The <positive definite Tits form indecomposable classification> gives one indecomposable isomorphism class per positive root.

= Positive roots of type A
{parent=Positive root of a quiver}
{title2=$\mathbf n=1_{[a,b]},\quad1\leq a\leq b\leq m$}

For the chain, with $n_0=n_{m+1}=0$, the quadratic form is $\tfrac12\sum_{i=0}^m(n_{i+1}-n_i)^2$. Its value is one precisely for interval vectors: one $+1$ difference followed by one $-1$, all other differences zero. Thus there are $m(m+1)/2$ positive roots, independently of orientation.

= Positive definite Tits form indecomposable classification
{parent=Positive root of a quiver}

The <Ringel lemma on bricks> makes every indecomposable a <brick module>; positive definiteness and the <Ringel form> then force $q_Q=1$ and rigidity. Conversely, a maximal-orbit representation at a positive root cannot split: nonzero cross extensions increase orbit dimension, while vanishing cross extensions make the quadratic form of a split sum at least two. Open dense orbits give uniqueness. A compact bound on the integer roots gives finite representation type.

= Dimension vector of a quiver representation
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$\dim X=(\dim_kX_i)_{i\in Q_0}$}

= Dimension vectors of quiver representations
{synonym}

The dimension vector of a finite-dimensional <quiver representation> is $(\dim_kX_i)_{i\in Q_0}$. <Direct sums> add dimension vectors. Fixing the vector gives an <affine space> of possible arrow matrices, the <quiver representation space>.

= Quiver representation morphism
{parent=Representation of a quiver}
{title2=$h_{t(\rho)}f_\rho=g_\rho h_{s(\rho)}$}

For <quiver representations> $X=(X_i,f_\rho)$ and $Y=(Y_i,g_\rho)$, a morphism consists of maps $h_i:X_i\to Y_i$ satisfying $h_{t(\rho)}f_\rho=g_\rho h_{s(\rho)}$ for every arrow. Composition is vertexwise. These families are the kernel of the <extension complex of quiver representations>.

= Quiver representation isomorphism
{parent=Quiver representation morphism}
{title2=$X\cong Y$}

A <quiver representation morphism> is an isomorphism exactly when all its vertex maps are invertible. Choosing bases turns this into the <base change action on quiver representations>, whose orbits coincide with isomorphism classes.

= Finite representation type of a quiver
{parent=Representation of a quiver}

A <quiver> has finite representation type over a <field> when it has only finitely many <isomorphism>[isomorphism classes] of indecomposable finite-dimensional <representation of a quiver>[representations].

= Gabriel's theorem
{c}
{parent=Finite representation type of a quiver}
{wiki}

= Gabriel theorem
{synonym}
{c}

A connected quiver has finite representation type exactly when its underlying unoriented graph is a Dynkin diagram of type $A$, $D$, or $E$. In that case, dimension vectors give a bijection from indecomposable representations to positive roots.

= Bernstein–Gelfand–Ponomarev reflection functor
{c}
{parent=Finite representation type of a quiver}

At a sink $i$, the Bernstein–Gelfand–Ponomarev reflection functor reverses every arrow into $i$ and replaces the vector space at $i$ by the kernel of the sum of its incoming maps. Away from the simple representation supported at $i$, reflection at the resulting source is an inverse functor.

= Path algebra
{parent=Quiver}
{wiki}

The path algebra $kQ$ has the directed paths in a quiver $Q$ as a basis; multiplication concatenates composable paths and is zero otherwise. Representations of $Q$ are naturally modules over $kQ$.

= Closed-path corner of a path algebra is a domain
{parent=Path algebra}
{title2=$e_ikQe_i$}

Closed paths at vertex $i$ form a <basis> of $e_ikQe_i$. For nonzero finite linear combinations $f,g$, take their largest path lengths $a,b$. In the length-$a+b$ part of $fg$, a concatenated path has a unique cut into lengths $a,b$. Thus a product of nonzero top-degree coefficients cannot cancel, and $fg\ne0$. The corner is a <noncommutative domain> and has no <idempotents> except zero and its identity $e_i$. This argument works with oriented cycles and proves indecomposability of the <vertex projective module of a path algebra> without falsely assuming that its endomorphism ring is local.

= Vertex projective module of a path algebra
{parent=Path algebra}
{title2=$P(i)=kQe_i$}

The <vertex projective module of a path algebra> $P(i)=kQe_i$ has at vertex $j$ the span of paths from $i$ to $j$. Each arrow appends itself to such paths. It is a <projective module> because it is a summand of the free module $kQ$. Its <endomorphism ring> is $(e_ikQe_i)^{\mathrm{op}}$, acting by right multiplication. The <closed-path corner of a path algebra is a domain>, so this ring has no nontrivial <idempotents> and $P(i)$ is indecomposable. Directed cycles may make these projectives infinite-dimensional.

= Evaluation isomorphism for a vertex projective
{parent=Vertex projective module of a path algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Hom}_{kQ}(kQe_i,X)\cong e_iX$}

Evaluation at $e_i$ identifies a <module homomorphism> $h:kQe_i\to X$ with $h(e_i)\in e_iX$. Given $x\in e_iX$, the inverse sends $p$ to $px$. The isomorphism is natural and shows that <Hom functor> evaluation on a vertex projective is exact.

= Path-algebra module equivalence
{parent=Path algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Rep}(Q)\simeq kQ\text{-}\operatorname{Mod}$}

For a <quiver> with finitely many vertices, send a <representation of a quiver> $(X_i,f_\rho)$ to $\bigoplus_iX_i$. Vertex <idempotents> act as projections, and paths act as composites of the arrow maps. Conversely, a unital <module> $M$ gives vertex spaces $e_iM$ and arrow maps $x\mapsto\rho x$. Compatible vertex maps are exactly <module homomorphisms>. These constructions give inverse equivalences of <categories>.

= Arrow ideal of a path algebra
{parent=Path algebra}

This two-sided ideal is spanned by paths of positive length; its $m$th power is spanned by paths of length at least $m$. The quotient is a product of copies of the ground field indexed by vertices. For a finite acyclic <quiver>, the ideal is nilpotent and equals the <Jacobson radical>. A loop shows that the equality can fail in general.

= Isomorphism
{parent=Morphism}
{wiki}

= Isomorphic
{synonym}

An isomorphism is a <morphism> with a two-sided inverse that is also a <morphism>. Two objects are isomorphic when such a map exists between them. For <groups>, <rings>, and <vector spaces>, a bijective structure-preserving map has a structure-preserving inverse; in other settings the inverse condition needs separate attention, as for a <homeomorphism> between <topological spaces>.

For example, $f(k)=2k$ is a <group isomorphism> from the additive <integers> to the additive <even integers>. It is <bijective> onto that target, satisfies $f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)$, and has inverse $2k\mapsto k$. The two groups have the same addition pattern even though they are different <sets>. It is not a <ring isomorphism> for their inherited multiplications, since $f(ab)=2ab$ while $f(a)f(b)=4ab$.

= Group isomorphism

= Field
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki=Field_(mathematics)}

A field is a <commutative ring> with $0\ne1$ in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. Examples include the <rational numbers>, <real numbers>, and <complex numbers>. The <integers> are a <ring> but not a field: dividing $1$ by $2$ leaves them. For a <prime number> $p$, the <prime field> $\mathbb F_p=\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$ is a finite example, because every nonzero residue is <coprime> to $p$ and hence has a <modular inverse>.

In $\mathbb F_5$, the nonzero inverse pairs are $1^{-1}=1$, $2^{-1}=3$, $3^{-1}=2$, and $4^{-1}=4$. Thus $2x=3$ has solution $x=3\cdot3=4$ in this field; the <real number> solution would be $3/2$. Dividing by a nonzero coefficient is valid in both settings, but it means division according to the chosen field's operations.

A field has no <zero divisors>: if $ab=0$ and $a\ne0$, multiplying by $a^{-1}$ gives $b=0$. This short argument supports <polynomial> factorization arguments and pivot division in <Gaussian elimination>. It does not hold in every <commutative ring>: modulo $6$, the nonzero residues $2$ and $3$ multiply to zero. The distinction is visible in the figure under <algebraic structure>.

= Hilbertian field
{c}
{parent=Field}

= Hilbertian
{c}
{synonym}

A <field> $K$ is Hilbertian if each finite family of <polynomials> irreducible over $K(T)$ and separable in its root variable remains irreducible of the same degree at infinitely many $T=a\in K$, after any finitely many prescribed bad parameter values are excluded. Equivalently, every finite separable extension of $K(T)$ has infinitely many unramified $K$-rational places whose residue extension retains its full degree.

= Hilbert's irreducibility theorem
{c}
{parent=Hilbertian field}
{wiki}

= Hilbert irreducibility theorem
{c}
{synonym}

The rational numbers, and every <number field>, are <Hilbertian fields>. The classical integral form supplies infinitely many integral specializations avoiding a prescribed finite exceptional set. The theorem preserves irreducibility, rather than asserting that every specialization works: $X^2-T$ becomes reducible at square parameters.

= Galois-preserving Hilbert specialization
{c}
{parent=Hilbert's irreducibility theorem}

For a <finite Galois extension> $E/K(T)$, take a primitive element and exclude all ramification and denominator problems. A <Hilbert subset> keeping its minimal <polynomial> irreducible has residue degree $[E:K(T)]$. The residue group is an unramified decomposition subgroup of the generic <Galois group> with the same order, so it is the whole group. This specializes a regular Galois realization over $K(T)$ to realizations over $K$ when $K$ is <Hilbertian>.

= Hilbert subset
{c}
{parent=Hilbertian field}
{title2=$H_K(f_1,\ldots,f_r;h)$}

= Hilbert set
{c}
{synonym}

For a finite family of irreducible separable <polynomials> $f_i(T,X)$ and a nonzero exclusion <polynomial> $h(T)$, this is the set of parameters $a\in K$ with $h(a)\ne0$ for which every specialization remains irreducible of its original degree. A <Hilbertian field> has infinitely many points in each such set. Denominators, leading coefficients and discriminants can be included in $h$.

= Ci field
{c}
{parent=Field}
{title2=$N>d^i$}

= Cr field
{c}
{synonym}

A field is $C_i$ if every <homogeneous polynomial> of degree $d\ge1$ in more than $d^i$ variables has a nonzero zero. Algebraically closed fields are $C_0$, <finite fields> are $C_1$, and the <Lang-Nagata theorem for Ci fields> controls function-<field extensions>.

= Lang-Nagata theorem for Ci fields
{c}
{parent=Ci field}
{title2=$K\text{ is }C_i\ \Longrightarrow\ L\text{ is }C_{i+s}$}

= Lang theorem for Ci fields
{c}
{synonym}

A finitely generated <field extension> of transcendence degree $s$ of a $C_i$ field is $C_{i+s}$. In particular a function field of a curve over a <C1 field> is $C_2$. The theorem includes stability under finite algebraic extensions when $s=0$.

= C2 field
{c}
{parent=Ci field}
{title2=$C_2$}

A $C_2$ field is a <Ci field> with the bound $N>d^2$. Function fields of curves over <finite fields> are examples. The extra-variable construction in <surjectivity of reduced norms over C2 fields> makes this polynomial-solvability property useful for <division algebras>.

= Surjectivity of reduced norms over C2 fields
{parent=C2 field}
{title2=$\operatorname{Nrd}(D)=K$}

For a <central division algebra> $D$ of degree $d$ over a <C2 field>, the form $\operatorname{Nrd}(X)-aT^d$ has degree $d$ in $d^2+1$ variables. Its nontrivial zero must have $T\ne0$, because the <reduced norm> of a <division algebra> is anisotropic. Dividing $X$ by $T$ gives norm $a$. Zero is the norm of zero.

= C1 field
{c}
{parent=Ci field}
{title2=$C_1$}

= Quasi-algebraically closed field
{synonym}

A $C_1$ field is a <Ci field> with the bound $N>d$. The <Chevalley-Warning theorem> gives finite-field examples; the <Tsen theorem> gives function fields of curves over algebraically closed fields. A $C_1$ field has trivial <Brauer group>.

= Trivial Brauer group of a C1 field
{parent=C1 field}
{title2=$\operatorname{Br}(K)=0$}

A <central division algebra> of degree $d>1$ has an anisotropic <reduced norm> of degree $d$ in $d^2>d$ variables. That contradicts the <C1 field> property. Thus only degree one is possible, and every <central simple algebra> is split.

= Finite extension stability of C1 fields
{parent=C1 field}

For a degree-$m$ extension $L/K$, substitute a $K$-basis of $L$ into a homogeneous degree-$d$ polynomial in $N>d$ variables over $L$, then take its <field norm>. The resulting degree-$md$ polynomial has $mN>md$ variables over $K$. A nonzero zero over a <C1 field> gives a nonzero zero of the original form because the norm of a field element vanishes only at zero. Separability is unnecessary.

= Tsen theorem
{c}
{parent=C1 field}

The function field of a curve over an <algebraically closed field> is a <C1 field>. Consequently its <Brauer group> is zero. This is the input for <cyclic splitting by extension of constants> over a finite constant field.

= Cyclic splitting by extension of constants
{parent=Tsen theorem}

For the function field $K$ of a curve over a finite full constant field $k$, extend constants to $\overline k$. The resulting curve function field is $C_1$ by the <Tsen theorem>, so every <central simple algebra> splits there. The finite collection of coefficients of a splitting isomorphism descends to some finite constant extension $k^{\prime}/k$. Regularity makes $Kk^{\prime}/K$ cyclic, giving a finite cyclic splitting field.

= Formally real field
{parent=Field}
{title2=$-1\ne\sum_{j=1}^m a_j^2$}
{wiki}

A <field> is formally real when $-1$ is not a finite sum of squares. The <Artin-Schreier ordering criterion> says this is equivalent to the existence of an ordering compatible with the field operations. Such a field has characteristic zero and embeds into a <real closed field>.

= Artin-Schreier ordering criterion
{parent=Formally real field}
{c}

A <field> can be ordered compatibly with addition and multiplication if and only if it is a <formally real field>. This ordering criterion concerns real algebra, and is different from <Artin–Schreier theory> of cyclic extensions in positive characteristic.

= Real closed field
{parent=Formally real field}
{wiki}

A real closed field is a <formally real field> with no proper formally real algebraic extension. Equivalently every positive element in its unique compatible order is a square and every odd-degree polynomial has a root. Its positive cone is definable in the field language as the nonzero squares. The <theory of real closed fields> is model-complete.

= Real closure
{parent=Real closed field}
{title2=$K^{\mathrm{rc}}$}

A real closure of an ordered <field> $K$ is a real closed algebraic extension whose order extends the chosen order of $K$. Existence is the <real closure theorem>; uniqueness holds up to ordered field isomorphism over $K$. A different ordering of a formally real field can produce a different ordered real closure.

= Real closure theorem
{parent=Real closure}

Every ordered <field> has an ordered algebraic <real closure>. Combined with the <Artin-Schreier ordering criterion>, every <formally real field> embeds into a <real closed field>. This is one embedding direction in the <model companion> relation between their theories.

= Valuation

{parent=Field}
{wiki=Valuation_(algebra)}

A valuation assigns sizes to elements of a <field> in a way compatible with multiplication and addition. In additive notation, $v(xy)=v(x)+v(y)$ and $v(x+y)\geq\min\{v(x),v(y)\}$.

= Equivalent valuations
{synonym}

Two additive <valuations> are equivalent when they have the same <valuation ring>. Their ordered value-group images then identify compatibly with the <valuations>; for nontrivial real-valued rank-one <valuations> this is multiplication by a positive constant.

= Rank-one valuation
{parent=Valuation}
{title2=$v:K^\times\to\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb R$}

A nontrivial additive <valuation> has rank one when its ordered value group is Archimedean and therefore embeds order-preservingly in $\mathbb R$. Exponentiating a real-valued representative gives a <non-Archimedean absolute value>; changing the embedding rescales the valuation positively. Higher-rank ordered value groups cannot be represented by real-valued <absolute values on a field>.

= Weighted Gauss valuation
{parent=Valuation}
{title2=$w_t(\sum_j a_jX^j)=\min_j(v(a_j)+jt)$}

For a <polynomial> over a <discretely valued field> and a rational weight $t$, this coefficient-minimum function is multiplicative. Scale the variable by an element of <valuation> $t$ in a valued extension and reduce the initial terms; nonzero residue polynomials have nonzero product. For a monic <polynomial> factored into roots, $w_t(f)=\sum_r\min(t,v(r))$. Its supporting lines determine the lower <Newton polygon>, giving the <Newton polygon root valuation theorem> and its multiplicities.

= Non-Archimedean valuation
{parent=Valuation}
{wiki}

A non-Archimedean valuation satisfies the strong triangle inequality
$$
v(x+y)\geq\min\{v(x),v(y)\}.
$$
In multiplicative absolute-value notation this becomes $|x+y|\leq\max\{|x|,|y|\}$.

= Classification of nontrivial valuations on the rational numbers
{parent=Non-Archimedean valuation}
{title2=$v=c\,v_p$}

An additive nontrivial <valuation> on the rational field is equivalent to exactly one <p-adic valuation>. Integers have nonnegative values; some prime must have positive value, and <Bézout's identity> prevents two distinct primes from doing so. Unique factorization then gives the formula, with $c>0$ in the real-valued case. The trivial <valuation> is a separate possibility if admitted by the definition.

= Rationalization of a square-root difference
{parent=Square root}
{title2=$\sqrt u-\sqrt v=(u-v)/(\sqrt u+\sqrt v)$}

For $u,v\ge0$ with $u+v>0$, multiplication by the sum of the <square roots> gives
$$
\sqrt u-\sqrt v=\frac{u-v}{\sqrt u+\sqrt v}.
$$
This identity exposes small differences hidden by cancellation. For example, $\sqrt{n^2+n}-n=1/(\sqrt{1+1/n}+1)\to1/2$. It also converts normalized square-root increments into terms comparable with the <harmonic series>.

= Integer norm lower bound for rational approximation to a quadratic irrational
{parent=Quadratic irrational number}
{title2=$|\sqrt D-a/q|\geq c_D/q^2$}

For a positive nonsquare integer $D$ and positive integers $a,q$, the integer $Dq^2-a^2$ is nonzero. Therefore $|\sqrt D-a/q|=|Dq^2-a^2|/(q^2(\sqrt D+a/q))$. If $a/q\leq\sqrt D+1$, the denominator factor is at most $2\sqrt D+1$; otherwise the approximation error already exceeds one. This proves that a quadratic irrational of this form is a <badly approximable number>. For $D=2$, splitting instead at $a/q=2$ gives the strict bound $|\sqrt2-a/q|>1/(4q^2)$.

= Bezout identity
{parent=Algebra}

{title2=$\gcd(a,b)=ax+by$}

= Bézout's identity
{c}
{synonym}

For <integers> $a,b$ not both zero, there are <integers> $x,y$ such that $ax+by=\gcd(a,b)$. The <extended Euclidean algorithm> constructs them. For example, $4\cdot252-5\cdot198=18$ and $7\cdot5-2\cdot17=1$; the second identity also gives the <modular inverse> of $5$ modulo $17$.

The identity converts a <greatest common divisor> into a solvability certificate. The <linear Diophantine equation> $ax+by=c$ is solvable in <integers> precisely when $\gcd(a,b)$ divides $c$. Every common <divisor> divides any integer <linear combination>; conversely, multiplying a Bézout identity by $c/\gcd(a,b)$ constructs a solution when that quotient is integral. The coefficients generally are not unique, as the description of <all solutions of a linear Diophantine equation> makes explicit.

= Multiquadratic field
{parent=Field extension}
{title2=$K(\sqrt{a_1},\ldots,\sqrt{a_r})$}

A <field extension> obtained by adjoining finitely many square roots in characteristic different from two. If the classes of $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ are independent in $K^\times/(K^\times)^2$, the extension has degree $2^r$ and <Galois group> $(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^r$: each square root's sign can be changed independently. = Quadratic subfields of a multiquadratic extension
{parent=Multiquadratic field}

In characteristic different from two, a field generated by finitely many nonzero square roots is the splitting field of a collection of separable quadratic polynomials. = Algebraic independence

Algebraic independence means the absence of a nonzero polynomial relation over the base <field>. It extends the distinction between algebraic and transcendental elements to finite tuples.

= Algebraically independent real sequence
{parent=Algebraic independence}
{title2=$f(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\ne0$}

There is a sequence of <real numbers> for which every finite initial segment consists of <algebraically independent elements> over the rationals. Given an independent initial segment, specialize each nonzero integer polynomial in one extra variable at those values. At least one coefficient remains nonzero, so each resulting polynomial has finitely many roots. There are countably many such polynomials by <countability of integer polynomial rings>. Choosing the next real outside the countable union of their roots continues the construction. Clearing denominators makes independence over the integers equivalent to independence over the rationals.

= Transcendence basis
{parent=Algebraic independence}

A transcendence basis of a <field extension> $L/k$ is an algebraically independent set $T$ such that $L/k(T)$ is algebraic. Its cardinality is the <transcendence degree>. For finitely generated extensions it is finite, and the remaining algebraic extension is finite.

= Separating transcendence basis
{parent=Transcendence basis}

A <transcendence basis> $T$ for $L/k$ is separating if $L/k(T)$ is a <separable field extension>. In this case the $dt$, $t\in T$, form a basis of the <Kähler differentials>. In characteristic zero every transcendence basis is separating. In positive characteristic the condition on the chosen basis is essential.

= Differentials detect algebraic independence in characteristic zero
{parent=Separating transcendence basis}

For a finitely generated <field extension> $L/k$ in characteristic zero, a tuple $t_i$ is algebraically independent exactly when its differentials $dt_i$ are linearly independent in the <Kähler differentials>. A relation of minimum total degree gives a nontrivial differential relation; an independent tuple extends to a <transcendence basis>. In positive characteristic, derivatives of nonconstant relations can vanish identically.

A quadratic extension is a field extension of degree two. It is normal because the other root of the minimal polynomial $T^2+bT+c$ of any non-base-field element is $-b-\alpha$ and lies in the same extension.

= Quadratic extensions from square classes
{parent=Quadratic extension}
{title2=$\{K(\sqrt a)\}/K\text{-isomorphism}\;\longleftrightarrow\;K^\times/K^{\times2}\setminus\{1\}$}

For a field of characteristic different from two, adjoining a square root of a nonsquare gives every <quadratic extension>. If two such extensions are isomorphic over $K$, write $\sqrt a=x+y\sqrt b$; the vanishing of its cross coefficient forces $x=0$, giving $a/b=y^2$. This explains why one subtracts the identity class when counting extensions from the <square-class group>.

= Quadratic extensions of an odd-residue-characteristic local field

{title2=$K^\times/K^{\times2}\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$}

For a <p-adic field> with odd residue characteristic, the square map is an isomorphism on $1+\mathfrak m_K$ by <Hensel lemma>. The parity of the valuation and the square class of the residue unit therefore determine the <square-class group of a field>. Its three nontrivial classes give the three <quadratic extensions>: one unramified extension and two totally ramified extensions.

This is a closure property of <algebraic extensions>, not an alternative name for an extension.

= Finite field

A finite field is a field with finitely many elements; its size is a prime power.

= Cyclotomic class of a finite field
{parent=Finite field}
{title2=$C_j=\gamma^j\langle\gamma^m\rangle,\quad m\mid q-1$}

= Cyclotomic classes of a finite field
{synonym}

The <cosets> of the index-$m$ <subgroup> of the <multiplicative group of a finite field> are its cyclotomic classes of index $m$. For a <character> with $\chi(\gamma)=\zeta_m$, their indicators are $\mathbf1_{C_j}(x)=m^{-1}\sum_{h=0}^{m-1}\zeta_m^{-jh}\chi^h(x)$, with every <character> extended by zero at zero. <Jacobi sums of finite-field characters> imply $|C_i\cap(t+C_j)|=q/m^2+O_m(\sqrt q)$ uniformly for $t\ne0$.

= Cyclotomic pattern extension by a second-moment bound
{parent=Cyclotomic class of a finite field}

Fix finitely many prescribed cyclotomic classes for pairwise differences. Such patterns are realized in every sufficiently large compatible <finite field>. For $r$ already chosen coordinates, let $M(a)$ count extensions with prescribed classes for $x-a_i$. Averaging over all $a\in\mathbb F_q^r$ gives $\mathbb EM=q/m^r+O(1)$ and, from the uniform two-class intersection estimate, $\operatorname{Var}M=O_{m,r}(q^{3/2})$. The <Chebyshev inequality> makes the fraction with $M<q/(2m^r)$ tend to zero. Inductively each fixed pattern on $r$ coordinates occupies a positive proportion of all tuples, so some positive proportion have many extensions. This proves the pattern-existence claim without an unproved greedy-extension assumption.

= Field with nine elements
{parent=Finite field}
{title2=$\mathbb F_9$}

The <finite field> $\mathbb F_9$ can be represented as $\mathbb F_3[X]/(X^2+1)$, since the quadratic has no root modulo three. Its elements are $a+b\beta$ for $a,b\in\mathbb F_3$, with $\beta^2=-1$. Multiplication reduces to $(a+b\beta)(c+d\beta)=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)\beta$ modulo three.

= Field with four elements
{parent=Finite field}
{title2=$\mathbb F_4$}

The <quotient by an irreducible polynomial is a field> construction gives $\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X+1)$. The quadratic has no root in $\mathbb F_2$. With $\alpha=[X]$, the four elements are $0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1$, and $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, $\alpha(\alpha+1)=1$, $(\alpha+1)^2=\alpha$. These relations determine the full multiplication table.

= Polynomial characterization of a finite field
{title2=$X^{q^n}-X$}
{parent=Finite field}

In a splitting field over $\mathbb F_q$, the polynomial $X^{q^n}-X$ has $q^n$ distinct roots because its derivative is $-1$. Its roots are closed under addition, multiplication, additive inverses, and nonzero inverses, so they form the field $\mathbb F_{q^n}$. The splitting field consequently has degree $n$ over $\mathbb F_q$.

= Irreducible factors of a finite-field Frobenius polynomial
{title2=$X^{q^n}-X$}
{parent=Polynomial characterization of a finite field}

Every monic irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$ whose degree divides $n$ divides $X^{q^n}-X$. In particular, if $f$ has degree $n$ and $\alpha$ is one of its roots, then $\mathbb F_q(\alpha)$ has $q^n$ elements, so $\alpha^{q^n}=\alpha$ and the minimal polynomial $f$ divides $X^{q^n}-X$.

= Number of monic irreducible polynomials over a finite field
{title2=$N_q(n)$}
{parent=Irreducible factors of a finite-field Frobenius polynomial}

Let $N_q(n)$ be the number of <monic> <irreducible polynomials> of <polynomial degree> $n$ over $\mathbb F_q$. Factoring $X^{q^n}-X$ into all such polynomials whose degrees divide $n$ gives
$$
q^n=\sum_{d\mid n}dN_q(d).
$$
The <Möbius inversion formula> therefore gives
$$
N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}.
$$

= Finite field extension
{parent=Finite field}

Every degree-$n$ extension of $\mathbb F_q$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb F_{q^n}$.

= Quadratic finite-field norm and trace surjectivity
{parent=Finite field extension}
{title2=$N(a)=a^{q+1},\quad\operatorname{Tr}(a)=a+a^q$}

For $\mathbb F_{q^2}/\mathbb F_q$, the <field norm> maps the nonzero elements onto $\mathbb F_q^*$: the <multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic>, and the power $q+1$ has image of order $q-1$. The <field trace> is also onto. Indeed it is an $\mathbb F_q$-linear map into $\mathbb F_q$, and is not zero because its polynomial $X+X^q$ has degree $q$ and cannot vanish at all $q^2$ field elements. Consequently $a\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(ac)$ is onto whenever $c\ne0$.

= Multiplicative group of a finite field
{parent=Finite field}

The multiplicative group of a finite field $\mathbb F_q$ is the group $\mathbb F_q^\times$ of its $q-1$ nonzero elements under multiplication.

= Primitive element of a finite field
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}(\alpha)=q-1$}

A primitive element of $\mathbb F_q$ generates its multiplicative group. This implies $\mathbb F_q=\mathbb F_p(\alpha)$, but an element generating the <field extension> need not generate the multiplicative group. For instance $u\in\mathbb F_3[u]/(u^2+1)$ generates the quadratic extension but has order four, whereas $1+u$ has order eight and is multiplicatively primitive.

= Primitive polynomial over a finite field
{parent=Primitive element of a finite field}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}(\alpha)=q^m-1$}

A <monic polynomial> of degree $m$ over $\mathbb F_q$ that is an <irreducible polynomial> is primitive in the finite-field sense when one of its roots generates the <multiplicative group of a finite field> $\mathbb F_{q^m}^{\times}$. All its roots are Frobenius conjugates and then have the same order $q^m-1$. This notion differs from a <primitive polynomial> over an integral domain, which concerns the common divisor of its coefficients. Primitive finite-field <polynomials> provide explicit <field> representations and <primitive cyclic Reed-Solomon codes> or <BCH codes>.

= Quartic residue
{title2=$a\in(\mathbb F_q^\times)^4$}
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field}

A nonzero element $a$ of a <finite field> is a quartic residue when $a=b^4$ for some nonzero $b$. If $q\equiv1\pmod4$, the <multiplicative group of a finite field> is cyclic of order divisible by four, so the <quartic residues> form a subgroup of index four; equivalently $a^{(q-1)/4}=1$.

= Multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field}

The nonzero elements of every finite field form a cyclic group. For a field with $q$ elements, combine elements realizing each prime-power factor of the group exponent to obtain an element of exponent order; the root bound for $X^m-1$ then forces that order to be $q-1$.

= Finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic}

Every finite <subgroup> $H$ of the multiplicative group of a <field> is <cyclic group>[cyclic]. If $m$ is the exponent of $H$, commutativity lets one combine elements whose orders realize the prime-power factors of $m$, producing an element of order $m$. Every element of $H$ is a root of $X^m-1$, so the <Lagrange root bound over a field> gives $|H|\leq m$. Since $m\leq|H|$, equality holds and that element generates $H$.

= Power roots in a finite field
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic}

In a finite field with $q$ elements, the equation $x^k=1$ has exactly $\gcd(k,q-1)$ solutions. Indeed, after choosing a generator $g$ of the multiplicative group, $x=g^j$ is a solution exactly when $(q-1)\mid kj$.

= Index-two square-class argument for three related residues
{parent=Multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic}

For odd $p$, the nonzero squares form an index-two subgroup of $\mathbb F_p^\times$. Given nonzero $a,b$, either $a$ or $b$ is a square, or both occupy the nontrivial coset and $ab$ is a square. Taking $a=2$ and $b=3$ shows that one of $2,3,6$ is a square, apart from the immediate zero case $p=3$.

= Irrationality of the square root of two
{parent=Irrational number}
{title2=$\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$}

If $\sqrt2=p/q$ in lowest terms, then $p^2=2q^2$ makes $p$ even. Substituting $p=2r$ then makes $q$ even, contradicting that the integers are coprime. More quantitatively, $|2q^2-p^2|\geq1$ for every nonzero integer $q$, and factoring the difference of squares gives $|\sqrt2-p/q|\geq1/(4q^2)$. To verify the constant, for $p/q\in[1,2]$ the other factor $\sqrt2+p/q$ is less than four; outside this interval the approximation error is at least $\sqrt2-1>1/4$.

= Density of the irrational numbers
{parent=Irrational number}
{title2=$\overline{\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q}=\mathbb R$}

Every nonempty open real interval contains an <irrational number>. Thus the irrational numbers form a <dense subset> of the real line. Together with rational density, this gives approaching <sequences> of both types at every real point, useful for testing <continuity> of functions defined differently on the two sets.

= Exponential forcing in a linear recurrence
{parent=Inhomogeneous linear recurrence}
{title2=$P(E)y_n=r^n$}

Let $E$ be the forward shift on sequences and $P$ the polynomial defining a constant-coefficient <linear recurrence relation>. For a nonzero base $r$, $P(E)r^n=P(r)r^n$, so $r^n/P(r)$ is a <particular solution> when $P(r)\ne0$. At a simple root of the <characteristic equation of a linear recurrence>, the correct trial acquires a factor $n$:
$$
P(E)(nr^n)=r^n\bigl(nP(r)+rP'(r)\bigr),\qquad
y_n=\frac{nr^n}{rP'(r)}.
$$
The identity follows by substituting $(n+j)r^{n+j}$ into each shift term. A root of multiplicity $m$ analogously requires a degree-$m$ polynomial factor; the lower-degree homogeneous terms are supplied by the <repeated characteristic root of a linear recurrence>.

= Discrete integrating factor
{parent=Variable-coefficient affine recurrence}
{title2=$U_n^{-1}$}

For $x_{n+1}=a_nx_n+b_n$, put $U_0=1$ and $U_n=\prod_{j=1}^na_j$, with $a_j\ne0$. Dividing by this accumulated homogeneous growth gives $z_n=x_n/U_{n-1}$ and $z_{n+1}-z_n=b_n/U_n$. A <telescoping series> then proves

$$
This is the discrete counterpart of the <integrating factor> for a <first-order linear differential equation>.

= Bounded-degree coefficient proof of Hilbert basis theorem
{parent=Hilbert basis theorem}

For an <ideal> $I$ in $R[x]$, let $J_n$ contain the coefficients of $x^n$ in its elements of degree at most $n$. These are <ideals> of $R$ and form an ascending chain, since multiplying by $x$ embeds one into the next. If $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, the chain stabilizes at $N$, and each $J_n$ for $0\le n\le N$ has finitely many generators. Choose corresponding polynomials in $I$. Their union generates $I$: for an element of degree $d$, subtract a combination of the chosen polynomials for $J_d$ if $d<N$, or the polynomials for $J_N$ multiplied by $x^{d-N}$ otherwise. This cancels its leading coefficient and lowers its degree. Induction completes the proof without assuming that $R$ is an <integral domain>.

= Spectrum of a finite-dimensional commutative algebra
{parent=Artinian ring}
{title2=$\operatorname{Spec}C=\operatorname{MaxSpec}C,\quad|\operatorname{Spec}C|\leq\dim_KC$}

If $C$ is a commutative algebra of finite dimension over a <field> $K$, every <prime ideal> is maximal and $|\operatorname{Spec}C|\leq\dim_KC$. Indeed, the quotient by a prime is a finite-dimensional <integral domain>. Multiplication by any nonzero element is an <injective> linear <endomorphism>, hence <surjective>, so the quotient is a <field>. Distinct <maximal ideals> are pairwise comaximal. For any finite list $\mathfrak m_1,\ldots,\mathfrak m_r$, construct $e_i\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak m_i}$ and $e_i\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak m_j}$ for $j\ne i$ by multiplying elements in $\mathfrak m_j$ congruent to one modulo $\mathfrak m_i$. These give a <surjection> $C\to\prod_iC/\mathfrak m_i$. Each factor has positive $K$-dimension, so $r\leq\dim_KC$, excluding an infinite list.

= Prime ideals of a commutative Artinian ring
{parent=Artinian ring}

A commutative <Artinian ring> has finitely many <prime ideals>, and every one is a <maximal ideal>. If $\mathfrak p$ is prime, the Artinian domain $R/\mathfrak p$ is a field: stabilization of $(x)\supseteq(x^2)\supseteq\cdots$ gives $x^n=x^{n+1}a$, and cancellation gives $xa=1$. \mathfrak m_1\mathfrak m_2\mathfrak m_3\supsetneq\cdots,
$$
because distinct maximal ideals are comaximal.

= Nilradical of a commutative Artinian ring
{parent=Artinian ring}

The <nilradical> $N$ of a commutative <Artinian ring> is a <nilpotent ideal>. Its powers stabilize, say $N^r=N^{r+1}=I$. If $I\ne0$, choose an ideal $J$ minimal subject to $IJ\ne0$, and then $x\in J$ with $Ix\ne0$. Minimality and $I^2=I$ give $Rx=Ix$, so $x=ax$ for some $a\in I$. Since $a$ is nilpotent, $1-a$ is a <unit>, contradicting $(1-a)x=0$ and $x\ne0$.

= Principal ideals in an Artinian local ring with one-dimensional cotangent space

using modular inverses for negative exponents.

= RSA private exponent reveals the factorization
{parent=RSA cryptosystem}
{c}

Knowledge of a valid public and private exponent gives $ed-1$, a nonzero multiple of $\lambda(N)$. Writing it as $2^st$ with $t$ odd and testing random bases produces a nontrivial square root of one modulo $N$ with high probability; a greatest common divisor then factors $N$. Thus one customer's private key under a shared semiprime modulus compromises every exponent using that modulus.

= Complementary RSA fixed points
{c}
{parent=RSA cryptosystem}

An RSA fixed point satisfies $m^e\equiv m\pmod N$. Since every valid public exponent $e$ is odd, $(-m)^e\equiv-m^e\equiv-m\pmod N$; therefore $N-m$ is a fixed point whenever $m$ is.

= Root of unity
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki}

= Roots of unity
{synonym}

An $N$th root of unity is a <complex number> $z$ satisfying $z^N=1$. The primitive root $e^{2\pi i/N}$ generates all $N$th roots.

= Square-power rigidity of a finite nonzero spectrum
{parent=Root of unity}

Let $S$ be a finite multiset of nonzero complex numbers such that taking the $m^2$th powers permutes it for every positive integer $m$. Taking $m=2$ and iterating the resulting permutation shows that each $z\in S$ obeys $z^{4^r}=z$ for some $r\geq1$, hence is a <root of unity>. Let $d$ be a common multiple of their orders. The $d^2$th powers are all one, and the assumed permutation then forces every original element to be one. For a <unitary matrix>, the <spectral theorem for normal operators> further forces the matrix itself to be the identity.

= Root of unity over a finite field
{parent=Root of unity}
{title2=$E(n,q)=\{a\in\overline{\mathbb F}_q:a^n=1\}$}

= Roots of unity over a finite field
{synonym}

These roots are taken in an <algebraic closure>, not merely in $\mathbb F_q$. If the <characteristic of a field> is $p$ and $n=p^a m$ with $p\nmid m$, then $X^n-1=(X^m-1)^{p^a}$; hence there are $m$ distinct roots. They form a <cyclic group> by the <finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic> theorem. In particular they have order $n$ when $\gcd(n,q)=1$.

= Splitting degree of roots of unity over a finite field
{parent=Root of unity over a finite field}
{title2=$\min\{s\geq1:m\mid q^s-1\}=\operatorname{ord}_m(q)$}

With $m$ the characteristic-prime-free part of $n$, all <roots of unity over a finite field> lie in $\mathbb F_{q^s}$ exactly when $m\mid q^s-1$. This follows from the cyclicity of the <multiplicative group of a finite field>. For $m=1$ the least positive degree is one. A primitive root of order $m$ has the same <field> degree, because its Frobenius orbit closes exactly when $m\mid q^s-1$.

= Ramanujan sum
{parent=Root of unity}
{c}
{title2=$c_n(k)$}
{wiki}

The Ramanujan sum is $c_n(k)=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n,\,(j,n)=1}e^{2\pi i jk/n}$. It is an integer: insert $\mathbf1_{(j,n)=1}=\sum_{d\mid(j,n)}\mu(d)$, sum the geometric series, and obtain $c_n(k)=\sum_{r\mid(n,k)}r\mu(n/r)$. Here $\mu$ is the <Möbius function>. Such sums connect <roots of unity> with integer <character of a representation> values.

= Primitive root of unity
{parent=Root of unity}

A primitive $n$th root of unity has multiplicative order exactly $n$.

= Orthogonality of roots of unity
{parent=Root of unity}

For $\omega=e^{2\pi i/N}$,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\omega^{kn}
=\begin{cases}N,&N\mid k,\\0,&N\nmid k.\end{cases}
$$
This finite geometric sum is the orthogonality relation underlying the <discrete Fourier transform>.

= Root-of-unity filter
{parent=Root of unity}

For $\omega=e^{2\pi i/N}$,
$$
\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\omega^{my}
=\begin{cases}N,&N\mid m,\\0,&N\nmid m.\end{cases}
$$
This is the finite <geometric series> identity and expresses orthogonality of the characters of $\mathbb Z_N$.

= Smith normal form
{parent=Algebra}
{c}
{wiki}

For every matrix $A$ over a <principal ideal domain>, there are invertible matrices $U,V$ such that
$$
UAV=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0,\ldots,0),
\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r.
$$
The nonzero diagonal entries are unique up to multiplication by units. Over the <integer>[integers], $U$ and $V$ are unimodular and the $d_i$ may be chosen positive; their products $d_1\cdots d_k$ are the greatest common divisors of the $k$-rowed minors.

= Determinantal divisor
{parent=Smith normal form}
{title2=$\Delta_j(M)=\gcd\{\text{minors of size }j\text{ of }M\}$}

For an integer <matrix> $M$, its $j$th determinantal divisor is the nonnegative greatest common divisor of all $j$-by-$j$ <matrix minors>, with $\Delta_0=1$. If all these minors vanish, take $\Delta_j=0$. Unimodular row and column operations preserve these divisors. For nonzero invariant factors $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_r$ in the <Smith normal form>, $\Delta_j=d_1\cdots d_j$ for $j\leq r$, so $d_j=\Delta_j/\Delta_{j-1}$.

= Unique factorization domain

= Failure of unique factorization in the ring of integers generated by square root of minus five
{parent=Unique factorization domain}
{title2=$\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$}

This <integral domain> embeds in $\mathbb C$ but has the inequivalent factorizations $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. The multiplicative <norm> $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2$ takes neither the value two nor three, proving that the four displayed factors are <irreducible elements>. Its only <units> are $\pm1$, so those two factorizations cannot be related by units and reordering.

= Coprime elements of a unique factorization domain
{parent=Unique factorization domain}
{title2=$\gcd(a,b)\in R^*$}

Two nonzero elements with no common nonunit irreducible factor. In a <unique factorization domain> this is equivalent to their greatest common divisor being a unit. Coprimality of numerator and denominator makes a fraction reduced, so simultaneous restrictions on its denominator can force it to be a <regular function>.

= Unique factorization in an integral domain
{parent=Unique factorization domain}
{title2=$a=u\prod_{i=1}^r p_i$}

= Unique factorization
{synonym}

Every nonzero nonunit in a <unique factorization domain> is a finite product of <irreducible elements>. Such a factorization is unique up to permutation and multiplication of the factors by units. Irreducibles are <prime elements>, so divisibility can be checked by comparing their multiplicities. This applies to a <polynomial ring> over a <field> and is useful for intersections of localizations inside its <fraction field>.

= Minimal-prime criterion for a Noetherian unique factorization domain
{c}
{parent=Unique factorization domain}

A Noetherian integral domain is a unique factorization domain exactly when every prime ideal minimal among the nonzero prime ideals is principal. The converse uses the Krull principal ideal theorem to prove that every irreducible element generates a prime ideal.

= Height-one prime in a unique factorization domain
{parent=Unique factorization domain}

Every height-one prime ideal in a unique factorization domain is principal and generated by an irreducible element. Consequently, two elements contained in the same height-one prime have a common irreducible factor.

= Nagata criterion for unique factorization domains
{c}
{parent=Unique factorization domain}

Let $A$ be an <integral domain> that is a <Noetherian ring>, and let $x\in A$ generate a <prime ideal>. If the <localization of a ring> $A_x$ is a <unique factorization domain>, then $A$ is a unique factorization domain. Indeed, integrality of $a/x^n$ over $A$, with $n$ minimal, forces $x\mid a$ and hence proves that $A$ is an <integrally closed domain>. The <Nagata theorem for divisor class groups> then says that $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$ is generated by the height-one primes containing $x$; the only one is the principal prime $(x)$, so $\operatorname{Cl}(A)=0$.

= Fundamental theorem of algebra

= Brownian proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra
{c}
{parent=Fundamental theorem of algebra}

If a nonconstant complex polynomial $P$ had no zeros, its reciprocal $g=1/P$ would be an entire <holomorphic function>, bounded and tending to zero at infinity. Its real and imaginary parts are <harmonic functions>. Thus $g(z+B_t)$, for <planar Brownian motion> started at zero, is a bounded complex <martingale> with expectation $g(z)$. For fixed $R$, the Gaussian density bounds $\mathbb P(|z+B_t|\leq R)$ by $R^2/(2t)$. Outside a sufficiently large disk, $|g|\leq\varepsilon$. Hence $|g(z)|\leq\varepsilon+\|g\|_\infty R^2/(2t)$. Let $t\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ to obtain $g(z)=0$, a contradiction. This proves the <fundamental theorem of algebra> without the <maximum modulus principle>.

= Monomial containment in an ideal of coordinate powers
{parent=Homogeneous polynomial}
{title2=$(X_0^{d_0},\ldots,X_n^{d_n})\supseteq k[X_0,\ldots,X_n]_{\sum d_i}$}

For integers $d_i\ge0$, every <monomial> of total degree $\sum_i d_i$ is divisible by some $X_i^{d_i}$. If any $d_i=0$ the ideal is the whole ring. = Homological algebra
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki}

Homological algebra studies <chain complexes>, <homology>, <cohomology>, and the derived constructions that measure failure of exactness.

= Derived category of sheaves
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$D(X)$}

The derived category of sheaves is formed by localizing cochain complexes of sheaves at quasi-isomorphisms, equivalently localizing their homotopy category. The bounded, bounded below and bounded above variants are denoted $D^b(X)$, $D^+(X)$ and $D^-(X)$. Cohomology sheaves are invariant under quasi-isomorphism and therefore define functors on these categories. Constructible bounded complexes form a natural subcategory on which <Verdier duality> reverses arrows and, under the usual finiteness hypotheses, is an involution.

= Ghost morphism in a derived category
{parent=Derived category of sheaves}

A ghost is a morphism inducing zero on every <cohomology sheaf>, but it need not itself be zero. For the equator $j:S^1\hookrightarrow S^2$ and complement $i:U\hookrightarrow S^2$, the connecting morphism $j_*\mathbb Q_{S^1}\to i_!\mathbb Q_U[1]$ is a ghost. Its source and target have <cohomology sheaves> in different degrees, but its induced map $H^1(S^1)\to H_c^2(U)$ is nonzero, proving that the derived morphism is nonzero.

= Verdier duality
{parent=Derived category of sheaves}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$D_X$}

On bounded constructible complexes over locally compact stratified spaces, <Verdier duality> is $D_XE=R\mathcal Hom(E,\omega_X^\bullet)$, where $\omega_X^\bullet$ is the dualizing complex. For an oriented real $N$-manifold, $D_X\mathbb Q_X\cong\mathbb Q_X[N]$. Proper direct image commutes with this duality, and $i_x^!D_XE\cong D_{\{x\}}i_x^*E$. These properties exchange stalk support conditions with <costalk> conditions.

= Constructible complex of sheaves
{parent=Derived category of sheaves}

A bounded complex is constructible for a given stratification when each of its <cohomology sheaves> restricts to a finite-rank <local system> on every stratum. Constructibility concerns <cohomology sheaves>, not an arbitrary choice of resolution.

= Bounded below derived category of sheaves
{parent=Derived category of sheaves}
{title2=$D^+(X)$}

The category $D^+(X)$ is obtained from bounded below <cochain complexes> of <sheaves> by passing to cochain-homotopy classes of maps and then inverting <quasi-isomorphisms>. It can equally be modeled by injective resolutions. The cohomology-sheaf functors descend to this localization, and an object is zero exactly when all its <cohomology sheaves> vanish.

= Exact triangle in a derived category
{parent=Bounded below derived category of sheaves}

= Exact triangle
{synonym}

= Distinguished triangle
{synonym}

An exact, or distinguished, triangle is one isomorphic to $E\xrightarrow{f}F\to\operatorname{Cone}(f)\to E[1]$. For a degreewise short exact sequence $0\to E\to F\to G\to0$, the map $\operatorname{Cone}(f)\to G$ taking $(v,e)$ to the image of $v$ is a <quasi-isomorphism>: its kernel is the contractible cone of the identity on $E$. Consequently the short exact sequence defines such a triangle.

= Left derived functor
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$L_iF(M)=H_i(F(P_\bullet))$}

For an additive right-exact <functor> on module categories, apply it to a <projective resolution> and take <homology>. Lifts of module maps exist by projectivity; any two lifts are <chain homotopic>, giving independence and functoriality. The zeroth derived functor is naturally $F$, positive degrees vanish on projective modules, and the <horseshoe lemma> produces a long exact homology sequence from each short exact sequence. A <Tor functor> is the left derived functor of a <tensor product of modules> functor.

= Left derived functors on an abelian category
{parent=Left derived functor}
{title2=$L_nF(A)=H_n(FP_\bullet)$}

For a <right-exact additive functor> out of an <abelian category with enough projectives>, apply the <functor> to a <projective resolution> and take <homology objects>. The zeroth derived <functor> is $F$, positive derived <functors> vanish on projective objects, and the degreewise split <horseshoe lemma> produces a long exact sequence from every <short exact sequence in an abelian category>. The target can be any abelian <category>, including an <opposite category> of one.

= Injective resolution
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$0\to M\to I^0\to I^1\to\cdots$}

An exact augmented cochain complex with injective module terms. Module categories have enough injectives: coinduction $J=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(A,\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z)$ is injective by its character adjunction, and the maps $m\mapsto(a\mapsto\phi(am))$, over all additive characters $\phi$ of $M$, embed $M$ into a product of copies of $J$. Products of injectives are injective by coordinatewise extension. Repeat on cokernels to obtain a resolution.

= Right derived functor
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$R^iF$}

For an additive covariant <left-exact functor> on module categories, choose an <injective resolution> $M\to I^\bullet$ and set $R^iF(M)=H^i(F(I^\bullet))$. For a contravariant functor, use a <projective resolution> instead, as in <contravariant right derived functor>. Resolution comparison and homotopies give independence and functoriality; short exact sequences give long exact derived-functor sequences. The zeroth functor is naturally $F$.

= Contravariant right derived functor
{parent=Right derived functor}
{title2=$R^iF(M)=H^i(F(P_\bullet))$}

For an additive contravariant <left-exact functor>, apply it to a <projective resolution> of $M$. Reversal of the arrows gives a <cochain complex>. Comparison maps lift using projectivity, and any two lifts are chain homotopic, so its cohomology is independent of the resolution. The functors are contravariant, vanish on projective modules in positive degrees, and send $0\to M'\to M\to M''\to0$ to $0\to F(M'')\to F(M)\to F(M')\to R^1F(M'')\to\cdots$. Degreewise split resolutions from the <horseshoe lemma> yield this sequence.

= Spectral sequence
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$(E_r^{p,q},d_r)$}
{wiki}

A succession of bigraded pages with $E_{r+1}=H(E_r,d_r)$. In the cohomological convention, the differential has bidegree $(r,1-r)$. A filtered complex produces such successive approximations to its <cohomology>.

= Künneth spectral sequence
{c}
{parent=Spectral sequence}
{title2=$E_2^{p,q}=\operatorname{Tor}_{-p}^R(H^qP,M)\Rightarrow H^{p+q}(P\otimes_RM)$}

For a bounded complex $P$ of projective right modules and a left module $M$ of finite <projective dimension>, a finite projective resolution of $M$ yields a bounded <double cochain complex>. Its two <spectral sequences> give the displayed abutment. Over a noncommutative ring, the handedness of the two tensor factors is essential.

= Two-column degeneration of a Künneth spectral sequence
{parent=Künneth spectral sequence}
{title2=$E_2=E_\infty$}

If only the columns $p=-1,0$ are nonzero, a cohomological differential of bidegree $(r,1-r)$ has no possible nonzero source-target pair for $r\geq2$. Projective boundaries in a complex of projectives give this condition because every cohomology module has projective dimension at most one.

= Convergence of a spectral sequence
{parent=Spectral sequence}
{title2=$E_\infty^{p,q}\cong F^pH^{p+q}/F^{p+1}H^{p+q}$}

The stable page identifies the <associated graded module> of a filtration of the abutment. For a finite filtration this is an exhaustive, separated finite extension description. It does not itself determine a canonical splitting. Unbounded filtrations require further convergence hypotheses.

A finite filtration of a <cochain complex> produces a convergent <spectral sequence>. The induced abutment filtration is the image of $H^n(F^pC)\to H^n(C)$. Finiteness in each degree suffices; a bounded range of cochain degrees is not required.

= Tor functor
{c}
{parent=Homological algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M,N)$}
{wiki}

The Tor functor is the <homology> of a <tensor product of modules> with a <free resolution>: $\operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M,N)=H_i(F_\bullet\otimes_RN)$ for a <free resolution> $F_\bullet$ of $M$. It measures the failure of tensoring to preserve <exact sequences>. For a <commutative ring>, one may resolve either argument; the <double complex> of the two resolutions proves that both computations agree.

= Tor for a principal non-zero-divisor quotient
{c}
{parent=Tor functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tor}_0^A(A/(a),M)=M/aM,\quad\operatorname{Tor}_1^A(A/(a),M)=\ker(a:M\to M)$}

For a <non-zero-divisor> $a\in A$, resolve $A/(a)$ by $0\to A\xrightarrow{a}A\to A/(a)\to0$. Tensoring with $M$ gives the kernel and cokernel in the displayed formula; all higher <Tor functors> vanish. Resolving either argument gives the same homology by the two filtrations of the double complex of the two free resolutions. For $M=A/(a)$, both degrees zero and one equal $A/(a)$.

= First Tor of a cyclic quotient
{parent=Tor functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tor}_1^A(A/I,M)=\ker(I\otimes_AM\to M)$}

Start a <free resolution> of $A/I$ with $P_0=A$ and a free module $P_1$ surjecting onto $I$. The next differential presents the relations in $I$. Right exactness of the <tensor product of modules> identifies $\operatorname{coker}(P_2\otimes_AM\to P_1\otimes_AM)$ with $I\otimes_AM$. Taking degree-one homology then gives the displayed kernel formula. Hence vanishing for every ideal is equivalent to the <flatness criterion using ideals>.

= Cochain complex
{title2=$(C^\bullet,d)$}
{parent=Homological algebra}
{wiki}

A cochain complex is a sequence of abelian groups or modules and maps $d^n:C^n\to C^{n+1}$ satisfying $d^{n+1}d^n=0$. Its cohomology is $H^n(C)=\ker d^n/\operatorname{im}d^{n-1}$.

= Cochain map
{parent=Cochain complex}
{title2=$d_Ff=fd_E$}

A <cochain map> $f:E\to F$ consists of degreewise maps $f^i:E^i\to F^i$ satisfying $d_F^i f^i=f^{i+1}d_E^i$. This identity sends cocycles to cocycles and coboundaries to coboundaries, hence induces maps on <cohomology>. For complexes of <sheaves> the same identity holds as an identity of <sheaf> morphisms.

= Cocycle

= Boundary module

= Ext from a principal non-zero-divisor quotient
{c}
{parent=Ext functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_A^0(A/(a),M)=\ker a,\quad\operatorname{Ext}_A^1(A/(a),M)=M/aM$}

Directly in an <injective resolution> of the second variable, multiplication by $a$ is surjective in every term: the map $aA\to I$ taking $ax$ to $xm$ extends to $A\to I$. Thus $0\to\operatorname{Hom}_A(A/(a),I^\bullet)\to I^\bullet\xrightarrow{a}I^\bullet\to0$ is a short exact sequence of complexes and gives the same result. The displayed Hom functor is covariant in $M$; changing it to $\operatorname{Hom}_A(M,A/(a))$ changes the variable that is resolved.

= Nonzero Ext of the rationals with integer coefficients
{parent=Ext functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathbb Z}^1(\mathbb Q,\mathbb Z)\ne0$}

A <free resolution> of $\mathbb Q$ has generators $a_n=1/n!$ and independent relations $a_n-(n+1)a_{n+1}$. Thus the <Ext functor> gives the cokernel of the map on integer sequence products
$$
(u_n)\longmapsto(u_n-(n+1)u_{n+1}).
$$
The constant sequence one is not in its image. A preimage would require $u_1\equiv\sum_{j=1}^N j!\pmod{(N+1)!}$ for every $N$. For $N\geq3$, the sum is below $2N!$, tends to infinity, and its distance from $(N+1)!$ also tends to infinity. No fixed integer satisfies all these congruences. Hence $\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathbb Z}^1(\mathbb Q,\mathbb Z)\ne0$.

= Long exact sequence of Ext groups
{parent=Ext functor}

Applying the contravariant <Hom functor> $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-,W)$ to $0\to I\to X\to C\to0$ gives
$$
0\to\operatorname{Hom}(C,W)\to\operatorname{Hom}(X,W)\to\operatorname{Hom}(I,W)\to\operatorname{Ext}^1(C,W)\to\operatorname{Ext}^1(X,W)\to\operatorname{Ext}^1(I,W)\to\operatorname{Ext}^2(C,W)\to\cdots.
$$
The connecting map sends $I\to W$ to its <pushout of a module extension>. Its vanishing means that map extends to $X\to W$. For a <hereditary ring>, $\operatorname{Ext}^2(C,W)=0$, making the indicated restriction on first <extension groups> surjective. The covariant variable has its corresponding long exact sequence.

= Extension group
{parent=Ext functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_R^n(M,N)$}

An <extension group> is a value of an <Ext functor>. In degree one it classifies <short exact sequences> $0\to N\to E\to M\to0$ up to isomorphisms fixing the endpoints. The zero class is the split extension; its addition is the <Baer sum>. Higher degrees can be represented by longer exact extensions, or computed from a <projective resolution>.

= Baer sum
{parent=Extension group}
{c}

To add two extensions of $M$ by $N$, take their <direct sum>, form a <pullback in a category> along the diagonal $M\to M\oplus M$, and form a <pushout in a category> along addition $N\oplus N\to N$. This produces another extension of $M$ by $N$ and defines addition in $\operatorname{Ext}^1_R(M,N)$. In a <quiver representation>, vertexwise splittings express the two extensions by off-diagonal arrow matrices, and the Baer sum adds those matrices modulo the <extension complex of quiver representations> coboundaries.

= Localization of Ext over a Noetherian ring
{parent=Ext functor}
{title2=$S^{-1}\operatorname{Ext}_R^i(N,M)\cong\operatorname{Ext}_{S^{-1}R}^i(S^{-1}N,S^{-1}M)$}

Over a <Noetherian ring>, a <finitely generated module> $N$ has a free resolution with every term finitely generated. Hom from a finite <free module> commutes with localization, and <exactness of localization> commutes with cohomology. Thus the displayed isomorphism holds for every $i\geq0$ and arbitrary $M$. The finiteness condition is on the first argument; the second need not be finitely generated.

= Ext groups between polynomial-ring residue modules
{c}
{parent=Ext functor}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathbb Z[X]}^*(\mathbb Z/p,\mathbb Z/q)$}

When $X$ acts as zero, resolve the first module with the <Koszul resolution> on $(p,X)$. Writing $N=\mathbb Z/q$, the resulting groups are $\ker(p:N\to N)$ in degree zero, $(N/pN)\oplus\ker p$ in degree one, and $N/pN$ in degree two, with zero higher groups. This works for composite as well as prime moduli.

= Koszul self-Ext algebra
{c}
{parent=Ext groups between polynomial-ring residue modules}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_R^*(R/I,R/I)\cong\Lambda_{R/I}^*((I/I^2)^\vee)$}

For an ideal generated by a finite <regular sequence> in a commutative ring, the self-<Ext functor> algebra is exterior on the degree-one dual generators. Contraction chain maps on the <Koszul resolution> represent those generators, square to zero, and anticommute. The exterior square-zero relations also apply in characteristic two.

= Ext separation of finite-length modules
{c}
{parent=Ext functor}

Partition the isomorphism classes of <simple modules> into two sets $\mathcal C_1,\mathcal C_2$, with $\operatorname{Ext}^1(S,T)=\operatorname{Ext}^1(T,S)=0$ whenever $S,T$ lie in different sets. Every <module> $M$ of finite <composition length> then has a unique decomposition $M=U_1\oplus U_2$ into its largest <submodules> whose <Jordan–Hölder factors> belong to the respective sets.

For a simple $S\in\mathcal C_1$ and a finite-length $N$ with factors in $\mathcal C_2$, induction through the long exact sequence of the <Ext functor> gives $\operatorname{Ext}^1(S,N)=0$. Induct on the length of $M$: take $0\to N\to M\to S\to0$, split $N=N_1\oplus N_2$, and suppose $S\in\mathcal C_1$. The quotient extension $0\to N_2\to M/N_1\to S\to0$ splits. The inverse image of its $S$-summand complements $N_2$ in $M$ and has only factors in $\mathcal C_1$. Finally, Hom between modules with factors in different sets is zero, since any nonzero image would have a factor in both sets. Projecting any proposed <submodule> to the opposite summand therefore proves maximality and uniqueness.

= Projective resolution
{parent=Homological algebra}
{wiki}

A projective resolution of an $R$-module $M$ is an exact sequence
$$
\cdots\longrightarrow P_2\longrightarrow P_1\longrightarrow P_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0
$$
in which every $P_i$ is a <projective module>.

= Resolution by finitely generated free modules
{parent=Projective resolution}

A <finitely generated module> over a <Noetherian ring> admits a possibly infinite <free resolution> whose every term is finitely generated. Surject a finite free module onto it; the kernel is finitely generated because a finite free module is <Noetherian>. Repeat this construction on the successive kernels. If the original module has finite <projective dimension> $n$, its $n$th syzygy is projective by dimension shifting of the <Ext functor>, giving a length-$n$ resolution with finite projective terms.

= Horseshoe lemma
{parent=Projective resolution}

For a short exact sequence of modules with projective resolutions of its outer terms, there is a projective resolution of the middle term forming a degreewise split short exact sequence of resolutions. Lift the degree-zero projective cover of the quotient into the middle module and add the projective cover of the submodule; this surjects onto the middle module. Its kernel fits into the corresponding short exact sequence of kernels. Repeating this construction proves the claim. Additive contravariant functors preserve the degreewise split exact sequences with their direction reversed.

= Standard projective resolution of a quiver representation
{parent=Projective resolution}

Set $A=kQ$, $P_i=Ae_i$, $X_i=e_iX$. There is an exact sequence
$$
0\to\bigoplus_{\rho:i\to j}P_j\otimes_kX_i\xrightarrow{d}\bigoplus_iP_i\otimes_kX_i\to X\to0,
$$
where $d(p\otimes x)=p\rho\otimes x-p\otimes f_\rho(x)$ in the source and target summands, and the augmentation is $p\otimes x\mapsto px$. Each $P_i$ is a summand of $A$, so the two terms are <projective modules>. This also works for infinite-dimensional modules.

= Extension complex of quiver representations
{parent=Standard projective resolution of a quiver representation}
{title2=$C^0\xrightarrow{\delta}C^1$}

Put $C^0=\bigoplus_i\operatorname{Hom}(X_i,Y_i)$ and $C^1=\bigoplus_{\rho:i\to j}\operatorname{Hom}(X_i,Y_j)$. The differential has components $g_\rho h_i-h_jf_\rho$. Its kernel is $\operatorname{Hom}_Q(X,Y)$ and its cokernel is $\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,Y)$. In a vertexwise splitting of an extension, the off-diagonal arrow blocks change by these coboundaries when the splitting changes.

= Kernel-cokernel obstruction to splitting a quiver extension
{parent=Extension complex of quiver representations}

Restriction at one arrow induces a surjection $\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(X,Y)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_k(\ker f_\rho,\operatorname{coker}g_\rho)$. Coboundaries vanish on the kernel after quotienting by the image, while every map between these spaces can be lifted and extended. Nonzero kernel and cokernel therefore force a nonsplit extension. For $X=Y$, rigidity forces every arrow to have maximal rank.

= Projective presentation
{parent=Projective resolution}
{title2=$0\to K\to P\to M\to0$}

A <short exact sequence> presenting a <module> as a quotient of a <projective module>. The kernel need not be projective. Continuing a projective presentation gives a <projective resolution>.

= Koszul resolution
{parent=Projective resolution}
{c}

For a <regular sequence> $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ in a commutative <ring> $R$, its Koszul complex is the finite free <projective resolution> of $R/(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$ with terms $\bigwedge^jR^r$ and <boundary map> contracting by $(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$. Exactness follows by induction: append $f_r$ using the <mapping cone> for multiplication by $f_r$, which is injective on the preceding quotient. For a <polynomial ring> viewed as a <bimodule>, use the <regular sequence> $X_{i,\ell}-X_{i,r}$ in the enveloping <algebra>.

A free resolution is a <projective resolution> in which every resolving module is a <free module>.

= Hilbert syzygy theorem
{synonym}
{c}

Every <finitely generated module> with a grading over the <polynomial ring> $S=k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ has a finite graded <free resolution> of length at most $n$. The <Koszul resolution> of $k$ has length $n$, so $\operatorname{Tor}_i^S(M,k)=0$ for $i>n$. In a <minimal graded free resolution>, this <Tor functor> is $F_i/(X_1,\ldots,X_n)F_i$; the <graded Nakayama lemma> forces $F_i=0$ for $i>n$.

= Minimal graded free resolution
{parent=Free resolution}
{title2=$F_\bullet$}

Over $S=k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ with its standard grading and $\mathfrak m=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, a graded <free resolution> is minimal when $d_i(F_i)\subseteq\mathfrak mF_{i-1}$ for every $i\ge1$. It is constructed by choosing minimal homogeneous generators for each successive <syzygy module>, using the <graded Nakayama lemma>. After tensoring with $k=S/\mathfrak m$, all its differentials vanish, so $F_i/\mathfrak mF_i\cong\operatorname{Tor}_i^S(M,k)$; these dimensions and degrees are intrinsic to $M$.

= Syzygy module
{parent=Free resolution}
{title2=$\Omega_R^i(M)$}

Given a <free resolution> $\cdots\to F_1\to F_0\to M\to0$, its first syzygy module is $\ker(F_0\to M)$, the <module> of relations among the chosen generators of $M$. Higher syzygy modules are successive <kernels>. They depend on the choice of resolution; a <minimal graded free resolution> gives canonical ranks and degrees of generators over a standard <graded ring> structure on a <polynomial ring>.

= Bar resolution of an associative algebra
{parent=Free resolution}
{title2=$B_n(A)$}

The bar resolution of an <associative algebra> $A$ over a <field> has degree-$n$ term $A^{\otimes(n+2)}$ as an $A^e$-<module>, with the outer factors carrying the left and right actions. Its boundary multiplies each consecutive pair with alternating signs, and its augmentation is multiplication $A\otimes A\to A$. It is exact: the $k$-linear contracting homotopy inserts $1$ at the left. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_{A^e}(-,M)$ gives the <Hochschild cochain complex> of the <bimodule> $M$.

= Tensor product of fields
{parent=Algebra}
{wiki}

The <tensor product of fields> $L\otimes_K M$ is a $K$-algebra, not necessarily a field. If $L$ and $M$ sit in one overfield, the map $l\otimes m\mapsto lm$ has an image that generates their compositum as a field. When the extensions are algebraic, this image is already a field. Injectivity of this multiplication map is equivalent to <linear disjointness>.
