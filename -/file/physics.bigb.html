= Physics
{wiki}

Physics investigates matter, motion, and interactions by building <mathematical models> and testing their consequences against observations. A <physical law> supplies a relation; a model specifies the objects, conditions, and approximations used to apply it. <Classical mechanics>, <electromagnetism>, <thermodynamics>, and <quantum mechanics> organize different aspects of these investigations. Their usefulness comes from turning measurements into predictions that can be checked and refined.

Consider a <simple pendulum>: how does its <oscillation period> change when its <length> changes? For small <amplitude>, the ideal model gives $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$, where $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>. Taking $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ as the model parameter, a one-metre pendulum has predicted period $2.0061\,\mathrm s$. Increasing its <length> to four metres gives $4.0121\,\mathrm s$: quadrupling the length doubles the period. The point mass cancels from the equation, so changing it alone does not change this model's answer.

The prediction identifies a mechanism and a way to challenge it. The <small-angle approximation> replaces $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$; the full ideal equation predicts a longer period at large <amplitude>. At $60^\circ$, its period is about $1.07318T_0$, already a $7.3\%$ change. Measurements at such amplitudes can reveal the limit of the approximation, while appreciable drag requires a <damped pendulum>. These distinctions are developed in <simple pendulum> and <dimensional analysis>. The small-amplitude model is also derived in https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-4-pendulums[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums].

\Image[pendulum-period-scaling.png]
{title=Pendulum predictions connect a measurable scaling law with the limit of a small-angle approximation}

Several different experiments can become one calculation. <Nondimensionalization> rewrites the pendulum equation using $\tau=t\sqrt{g/\ell}$, leaving $d^2\theta/d\tau^2+\sin\theta=0$. All lengths share that dimensionless equation; the <amplitude> remains a parameter. Similarly, <conservation laws> and <symmetry> reveal features that many models share. This is one source of the subject's beauty: a compact relation can organize a large range of phenomena.

The connection to evidence remains essential when a model is elegant. In his introductory lectures, <Richard Feynman> emphasizes testing proposed relations by experiment and using idealizations whose accuracy depends on the conditions. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_01.html[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, Atoms in motion, section 1–1]{ref} A discrepancy can motivate a better measurement, a better approximation, or a different physical model; the mathematical calculation and the empirical assessment are both part of the explanation.

= Two-body scattering cross section
{parent=Cross section (physics)}
{title2=$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac1{64\pi^2s}\frac{|\mathbf k_f|}{|\mathbf p_i|}\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}$}

For distinguishable final particles, the <centre-of-momentum frame> cross section is the displayed product of the invariant amplitude, incoming flux and <relativistic two-body phase space>. Initial unpolarized states are averaged; unobserved final polarizations are summed. An isotropic amplitude integrates to $\sigma=(|\mathbf k_f|/|\mathbf p_i|)\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}/(16\pi s)$. A full-solid-angle integral for identical final particles additionally needs its symmetry factor.

= Physical law
{parent=Physics}

A physical law expresses a reproducible relation used to describe or predict physical phenomena within its domain of applicability. Applying it requires a model specifying the system and conditions: <Newton's second law>, together with a <force> model, predicts a body's <acceleration>; <conservation of energy> constrains exchanges within a specified system. = Gravity
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

Gravity governs falling bodies and <orbits>. <Newtonian gravity> describes it through a <gravitational field> acting on masses; <general relativity> instead relates matter and radiation to the geometry of <spacetime> through the <Einstein field equations>.

= Gravitational field
{parent=Gravity}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbf g$}

In <Newtonian gravity>, the gravitational field $\mathbf g$ specifies the <acceleration> of a freely falling <test particle> at each position. A <gravitational potential> $\Phi$ gives $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi$, with the <gradient> taken in the spatial geometry of the model. In <general relativity>, the <metric tensor> describes gravitational effects, and <tidal forces> are encoded by <spacetime> curvature.

= Metrology
{parent=Physics}

<Metrology> asks what a measurement means and how closely it can be reproduced. An instrument reading becomes useful when it is related to a reference through <measurement calibration>, with a stated <measurement uncertainty>. = Measurement accuracy

Measurement of <length> and geometrical dimensions. A <length standard> supplies a reference; a <differential length comparator> measures the difference between two lengths rather than treating its own structure as perfectly invariant.

= Length standard

= Differential length comparator
{parent=Dimensional metrology}

An instrument for comparing two <lengths> through their difference. Suppose a test rod and reference both have length $L_0$ at <temperature> $T_0$, and have constant <linear thermal expansion coefficients> $\alpha_s$ and $\alpha_r$ over a small interval. Their first-order models give
$$
L_s(T)=L_0[1+\alpha_s(T-T_0)],\qquad L_r(T)=L_0[1+\alpha_r(T-T_0)].
$$
For the observed difference $D(T)=L_s(T)-L_r(T)$, the <derivative> $b=dD/dT$ therefore determines
$$
\boxed{\alpha_s=\alpha_r+\frac{b}{L_0}}.
$$
The reference's independently calibrated <linear thermal expansion coefficient> is essential: the difference alone identifies $\alpha_s-\alpha_r$, not either coefficient separately.

For an original numerical example, stipulate $L_0=1.00\,\mathrm m$, $\alpha_s=1.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $\alpha_r=9.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. Heating both by $10\,\mathrm K$ gives sample and reference increases of $10\,\mu\mathrm m$ and $90\,\mu\mathrm m$. Thus **the sample expands even though its measured difference decreases** by $80\,\mu\mathrm m$. These are chosen model parameters, not measured values for a historical <Invar> specimen or reference.

If $\alpha_r$ and $b$ have independent <standard uncertainties> $0.2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$ and $0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m\,K^{-1}}$, and uncertainty in $L_0$ is negligible, <variance additivity for independent random variables> gives
$$
u(\alpha_s)=\sqrt{u(\alpha_r)^2+\frac{u(b)^2}{L_0^2}}
\simeq0.36\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}.
$$
Repeating the difference measurement can reduce its random contribution; it does not remove uncertainty in the reference. A <temperature gradient> or unequal rod <temperatures> also breaks the common-temperature model and can introduce a <systematic error>.

= ABBA measurement sequence
{c}
{parent=Differential length comparator}
{title2=$\widehat{B-A}=(y_1+y_2-y_0-y_3)/2$}

Alternating sample and reference readings can suppress instrument drift. In this original four-reading model, measure objects $A,B,B,A$ at equally spaced times $0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,3\Delta t$. Assume the objects are stable during that short sequence and each reading is its object's <length> plus the same <affine function> of time, $a+bt$. Then
$$
\widehat{B-A}=\frac{y_1+y_2-y_0-y_3}{2}=L_B-L_A,
$$
because both pairs have mean time $3\Delta t/2$. The constant offset and linear drift cancel. With a further drift term $ct^2$, direct substitution instead leaves bias $-2c\Delta t^2$. A shorter sequence reduces that term, but neither this ordering nor averaging corrects unequal <temperatures>, sample changes or reference <measurement uncertainty>. This is a modern illustrative protocol; the historical account below establishes alternation, without attributing this particular four-reading sequence to <Charles-Édouard Guillaume>.

= Guillaume thermal expansion experiment
{c}
{parent=Dimensional metrology}

<Charles-Édouard Guillaume> sought affordable, stable <length standards>. His 1920 lecture describes two micrometer <microscopes> on stone supports sighting marks on a rod immersed in water. Their separation was not fixed: one method alternated the sample with a reference in a second trough at nearly constant <temperature>. His relative method compared sample and <platinum>–<iridium> reference in the same bath, adding the reference's known expansion to the measured difference. Composition and treatment affected the nickel–iron alloys; low expansion held over a limited <temperature> range.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1901–1921/Invar and Elinvar by Charles-Édouard Guillaume>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1901–1921; Invar and elinvar, lecture 11 December 1920, pp. 444–448: motivation and comparator; pp. 451–453: composition, treatment and temperature limits]{ref}

The <differential length comparator> calculation explains why a calibrated reference is needed even when its expansion is larger than the sample's. Keeping both rods in the same bath targets one source of <systematic error>; allowing for changes in the instrument baseline targets another. = Systematic uncertainty
{parent=Systematic error}

Uncertainty associated with imperfect knowledge of <calibration>, efficiency, backgrounds or other persistent model inputs. Repeating the same measurement does not necessarily average it away. Correlated <nuisance parameters> can propagate it through a <likelihood>; estimating such an uncertainty is distinct from asserting that the systematic error has a known sign and magnitude.

= Common-offset cancellation by signal reversal
{parent=Systematic error}
{title2=$S=(y_+-y_-)/2,\quad b=(y_++y_-)/2$}

If a controlled reversal changes the desired <signal>'s sign while leaving an additive offset unchanged, readings $y_+=S+b$ and $y_-=-S+b$ separate them by half-difference and half-sum. Offset drift, gain changes, hysteresis or a background that also reverses violate the simple cancellation. Interleaving polarities and independent blank measurements test those assumptions; <signal> reversal does not cancel an arbitrary <systematic error>.

= Systematic-error cancellation by reversal
{parent=Systematic error}

If a wanted response changes sign on reversing a controlled setting while an offset remains unchanged, the difference of the reversed readings isolates the response. For $V_\pm=\pm RI+V_0$, the estimator $(V_+-V_-)/(2I)$ cancels $V_0$. The method requires offset stability between readings. It does not cancel a common current-scale <calibration> error, and it does not remove even-in-current <Joule heating>. A useful reversal test specifies which physical effects change sign, then tests the remaining effects independently.

= Measurement calibration
{parent=Metrology}

Comparing an instrument indication with a reference of known value to establish their relation and its <measurement uncertainty>.

= Measurement zero offset
{parent=Measurement calibration}
{title2=$D=S+b$}

A constant additive contribution $b$ to an instrument reading is a zero offset. A blank or zero-signal reading can estimate it, and a <systematic-error cancellation by reversal> can cancel it if it remains unchanged while the desired signal changes sign. The residual correction uncertainty remains part of the <measurement uncertainty>.

= Plasma
{synonym}

A <plasma> is a gas of mobile charged particles whose collective electromagnetic response is important to its dynamics. It commonly contains <ions> and <electrons>, and may also contain a neutral component: complete <ionization> is not required. A multi-species fluid description has <current density> $\mathbf J=\sum_s q_s n_s\mathbf u_s$. The relative motion of the species can matter even when a single-fluid <magnetohydrodynamics> approximation describes the large-scale dynamics.

= Quasineutrality
{parent=Plasma (physics)}
{title2=$\sum_s q_s n_s\simeq0$}

On spatial scales much larger than the <Debye length>, a <plasma> may have nearly cancelling positive and negative <electric charge> densities while still carrying an <electric current> and having a nonzero <electric field>. Quasineutrality is an approximation on appropriate slow macroscopic scales, not an assertion that each fluid element has exactly zero charge or that charge separation can be ignored at every boundary.

A <plasmon> is a quantum of a collective oscillation of <electric charge> density in a <plasma> or conducting material. Long-range <Coulomb interaction> can give its long-wavelength longitudinal mode a nonzero frequency.

= Plasma oscillation
{parent=Plasmon}

A <plasma oscillation> is a collective oscillation of charge density and its restoring electric field. In a three-dimensional bulk charged superfluid, long-range Coulomb forces give the longitudinal collective mode a nonzero long-wave plasma <frequency>. Different geometry and electromagnetic <boundary conditions> can change that limit.

= Surface plasmon polariton
{parent=Plasmon}

A <transverse magnetic polarization> <electromagnetic wave> bound to an interface through coupling to the material's collective charge response. Its fields propagate along the interface and decay away from it on both sides. In an ideal nonmagnetic lossless planar vacuum-material interface, a real negative relative <permittivity> below $-1$ supports the bound branch described by the <lossless planar surface-plasmon dispersion>. Material losses generally make the propagation constant complex.

= Lossless planar surface-plasmon dispersion
{parent=Surface plasmon polariton}
{title2=$k^2=(\omega^2/c^2)\epsilon/(\epsilon+1)$}

For a vacuum-material interface at $z=0$, write the magnetic phasor as $H_y e^{ikx-\beta_jz}$, with $\beta_1>0$ in vacuum and $\beta_2<0$ in the material. The <Ampere-Maxwell law> gives $E_x=i\beta_jH_y/(\omega\varepsilon_0\epsilon_j)$ and $E_z=-kH_y/(\omega\varepsilon_0\epsilon_j)$. Tangential field continuity gives $\beta_2=\epsilon\beta_1$. Together with $k^2-\beta_j^2=\epsilon_j\omega^2/c^2$, this gives the displayed relation and requires $\epsilon<-1$ for real propagating $k$ and decay on both sides. For $\epsilon=1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2$, the branch has $0<\omega<\omega_p/\sqrt2$ and approaches that upper frequency only as $k\to\infty$. At low frequency, $k=(\omega/c)[1+\omega^2/(2\omega_p^2)+O(\omega^4/\omega_p^4)]$.

= Plasma screening
{parent=Plasma (physics)}

Collective <electric charge> rearrangement modifies particle interactions in a <plasma>. In <stellar nuclear fusion>, this response can alter the <Coulomb barrier> used to infer a <thermonuclear reaction rate>.

= Debye length
{c}
{parent=Plasma screening}
{wiki}
{title2=$\lambda_D=\sqrt{\epsilon_0 k_BT_e/(n_e e^2)}$}

For mobile thermal <electrons> with a fixed neutralizing background, the Debye length is the characteristic distance over which <plasma screening> suppresses an electrostatic perturbation. Linearizing the Boltzmann density response and using <Poisson's equation> gives the displayed scale. Other mobile species contribute additively to $\lambda_D^{-2}$ through $n_s q_s^2/(\epsilon_0k_BT_s)$.

= Plasma frequency
{parent=Plasma (Physics)}
{title2=$\omega_{ps}=\sqrt{n_s q_s^2/(\epsilon_0m_s)}$}

The plasma frequency is the characteristic electrostatic oscillation frequency of a charged species displaced against a neutralizing background. For number density $n_s$, charge $q_s$ and mass $m_s$, the displayed formula uses SI units; in <Gaussian units> it is $\omega_{ps}=\sqrt{4\pi n_sq_s^2/m_s}$. The <ion skin depth> is $c/\omega_{pi}$ for the ion species. Different species have different plasma frequencies.

= Transverse wave cutoff in a cold unmagnetized plasma
{parent=Plasma frequency}

With immobile ions, negligible collisions and electron density $n_e$, the <plasma frequency> is $\omega_p^2=n_ee^2/(m_e\epsilon_0)$. Combining inertial electron current with vacuum <displacement current> gives $\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2$. Transverse fields are evanescent below $\omega_p$ and propagate above it.

= Carrier-current double counting in effective permittivity
{parent=Transverse wave cutoff in a cold unmagnetized plasma}

= Cold collisionless plasma dielectric response
{parent=Plasma frequency}
{title2=$\varepsilon_r=1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2$}

In an unmagnetized <plasma>, an electron displacement driven by $\mathbf E e^{-i\omega t}$ obeys $-m_e\omega^2\mathbf x_e=-e\mathbf E$. Its dipole moment is $-e\mathbf x_e$, giving <electric polarization> $\mathbf P=-ne^2\mathbf E/(m_e\omega^2)$. With $\mathbf D=\varepsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P$, this yields the displayed <relative permittivity> and $\omega_p^2=ne^2/(m_e\varepsilon_0)$. Ions contribute a further negative term smaller by $Zm_e/m_i$ for one ion species of charge $Ze$. Neglecting it requires heavy ions at electron-wave frequencies; collisions, background magnetization and pressure dispersion are excluded. The transverse <electromagnetic wave> relation is $c^2k^2=\omega^2-\omega_p^2$.

= Inferring incident power from a transmitted plasma field
{parent=Cold collisionless plasma dielectric response}

For a downward transverse wave in an isotropic <plasma>, $E_z/E_x=\tan\theta_t$ fixes the transmitted direction. Tangential <phase matching> gives $\sin\theta_i=n_p\sin\theta_t$. Continuity of tangential electric and <magnetic fields> then separates incident and reflected amplitudes. A measured transmitted electric amplitude alone cannot be identified with the incident one; mean <power> for peak phasors includes a factor of one half, and interface-normal flux includes a cosine.

= Oblique total reflection at an unmagnetized plasma
{parent=Cold collisionless plasma dielectric response}

For vacuum incidence on a sharp planar, collisionless <plasma> interface, the transmitted normal <wavenumber> obeys $k_z^2=(\omega^2/c^2)(\cos^2\theta-\omega_p^2/\omega^2)$. Negative $k_z^2$ produces an evanescent field and total reflection in this lossless model.

= Ion skin depth
{parent=Plasma (Physics)}
{title2=$d_i=c/\omega_{pi}$}

For a singly charged ion species in <Gaussian units>, $\omega_{pi}=(4\pi ne^2/m_i)^{1/2}$ is its <plasma frequency> and $d_i=c\sqrt{m_i/(4\pi ne^2)}$. The ion skin depth marks a characteristic scale below which ion and electron motion can separate in a magnetized <plasma>. Together with the <Alfvén speed>, it satisfies $v_Ad_i=cB_0/(4\pi en)$, the coefficient of guide-field propagation in <electron magnetohydrodynamics>.

= Theoretical physics

= Philosophy of physics
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

<Philosophy of physics> studies the interpretation and explanatory structure of physical theories, including the relation between mathematical models, observable phenomena and claims about what exists. Its questions include <theoretical reduction>, <emergence> and competing formulations of physical locality.

= Stochastic Einstein locality
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

A formulation of <stochastic Einstein locality> restricts an event's objective chance to its relevant causal past, rather than spacelike-separated interventions or facts. Different versions constrain either marginal chances or joint conditional probabilities. A one-wing marginal condition alone does not supply <outcome independence>. To derive a <Bell theorem> by this route, the proposed locality assumptions must include the appropriate screening-off relation for the two outcomes, together with a common setting-independent past distribution.

= Reichenbach common cause principle
{c}
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

= Common cause principle
{synonym}

A classical common-cause explanation seeks past variables which screen off a correlation: conditioned on a sufficiently complete common cause, the correlated events become independent. A <Bell local causality> model adds local dependence on settings and a single compatible past-variable distribution for all measurement contexts. Providing a different setting-dependent screening variable for each pair of settings does not establish that stronger Bell model. The distinction between a causal mechanism and a purely probabilistic screening construction matters when interpreting this principle.

= Emergence
{parent=Philosophy of physics}
{wiki}

<Emergence> concerns novel collective patterns or explanatory structures relative to a lower-level description. Robust effective behaviour can emerge through coarse-graining or singular limits while remaining derivable from the microscopic theory. The stronger claim that genuinely new causal powers cannot be reduced to that theory requires an additional argument; nonanalyticity of a limiting function alone does not establish it.

= Theoretical reduction
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

<Theoretical reduction> relates theories by deriving the laws or predictions of one from another together with explicit identifications of quantities and appropriate domain assumptions. A reduction can require approximation, coarse-graining or a limiting construction. Deduction of an ideal limiting model is different from exact deduction of the same properties for every finite member of a model sequence.

= Muonic atom
{parent=Atom (Physics)}

A <muonic atom> has a negative <muon> bound in the <Coulomb potential> of an <atomic nucleus>. Its much larger <reduced mass> than in an electronic <chemical atom> greatly reduces the orbital scale, increasing sensitivity of transition <energies> to the <nuclear charge radius> and <nuclear charge distribution>. Measuring muonic X-ray transitions therefore complements <electron scattering>. Strong relativistic and nuclear-size effects in heavy systems require a treatment beyond a point-nucleus nonrelativistic Coulomb spectrum.

= Muon capture

{title2=$\mu^-p\to n\nu_\mu$}

A bound negative <muon> can be absorbed by a <nucleus> through the <weak charged current>, changing a <proton> into a <neutron> and emitting a <muon neutrino>. This competes with ordinary <muon decay> and changes the observable disappearance lifetime in a <muonic atom>.

= Monovalent atom
{parent=Atom (Physics)}

An atom is monovalent in a specified bonding or conduction model when its relevant valence is one. In the simple metallic <free electron model>, one <monovalent atom> per primitive <unit cell> supplies one conduction <electron> per cell. Counting the two <spin> states then fills half of a single <energy band> at zero <temperature>.

= Screening effect
{parent=Atom (Physics)}

<Electrons> partially shield an <atomic nucleus> <electric charge>. At large distances a neutral <chemical atom> therefore does not generate the unscreened <Coulomb potential> of its bare <atomic nucleus>.

The study of <atomic nuclei>, their structure, reactions and interactions. <Nuclear forces> between <nucleons> and <nuclear binding energies> connect it to the low-energy consequences of <quantum chromodynamics>.

= Symmetry energy

= Nuclear symmetry energy
{synonym}

<Nuclear symmetry energy> is the energy cost associated with proton-neutron imbalance in nuclear matter or finite <nuclei>. A finite-nucleus <semi-empirical mass formula> includes the positive asymmetry term $a_A(N-Z)^2/A$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> and strong-interaction dynamics both contribute.

= Nuclear polarization
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=$P=\langle J_z\rangle/(I\hbar)$}

The mean orientation of nuclear <spin angular momentum> in a chosen direction. For nuclei with nonzero <spin> quantum number $I$, the vector-polarization component is $P=\langle J_z\rangle/(I\hbar)$ and lies between $-1$ and $1$. Equal populations of all projections give $P=0$; complete alignment gives $P=1$.

= Nuclear fission
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

= Fast fission
{parent=Nuclear fission}

<Nuclear fission> induced before the incident <neutron> has slowed to thermal energies. It allows otherwise non-thermal-fissile fuel constituents such as <uranium-238> to contribute to fission power. A change in their fission fraction need not mean a change in total power.

= Fission product
{parent=Nuclear fission}

A nuclide formed in or descended from <nuclear fission>. The inventory includes unstable species and their decay products and evolves with irradiation and elapsed time.

= Nuclear chain reaction
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{wiki}

A sequence in which particles released by one <nuclear reaction> induce further reactions. In a neutron-driven fission chain, <neutron capture>, leakage and non-fission absorption compete with neutron production. A sustained chain requires an effective reproduction factor of one; whether material is <fissile material> cannot be determined from a single positive fission <Q value>.

= Fertile material
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{wiki}

Material convertible into a <fissile material> by <neutron capture> followed by nuclear transformations. A central example is <uranium-238> capturing a neutron and undergoing two <beta-minus decays> to <plutonium-239>. Fertile does not mean that thermal-neutron capture immediately causes fission.

= Fissile material
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{wiki}

Material capable of sustaining a <nuclear chain reaction> with slow neutrons in a suitable configuration. Fissility involves capture and fission probabilities, neutron production and losses, rather than positive fission <Q value> alone. <Uranium-235> and <plutonium-239> are common examples; fissile is more specific than fissionable by sufficiently energetic neutrons.

= Neutron-induced fission threshold
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{title2=$S_n(A+1,Z)+E_n^{\rm CM}\gtrsim B_f(A+1,Z)$}

In a simple compound-nucleus picture, compare the <neutron-capture excitation energy> to the <fission barrier> of the capture product. If its <neutron separation energy> already exceeds the barrier, slow-neutron capture can lead to prompt fission without a positive kinetic-energy threshold. If it lies below, finite neutron kinetic energy is needed for appreciable over-barrier fission; sub-barrier tunnelling and detailed resonance widths soften a literal sharp-threshold picture.

= Fission-fragment mass distribution
{parent=Nuclear fission}

The yield of fragments as a function of fragment <mass number> or mass fraction. Low-excitation actinide <nuclear fission> commonly gives two peaks, corresponding to complementary lighter and heavier fragments. Smooth <liquid-drop fission energy> by itself does not predict this yield: nuclear-shell structure and the dynamics of the descent toward scission matter. Increasing excitation can weaken shell effects and change the distribution. A two-peaked illustrative curve is therefore not a universal measured spectrum.

= Spontaneous fission
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{wiki}

<Nuclear fission> without an incoming particle supplying the activation energy. A metastable <atomic nucleus> can tunnel through a <fission barrier> along collective deformation coordinates. The rate depends exponentially on a barrier action involving its height, width and collective inertia, so positive <liquid-drop fission energy> alone does not imply an observable rate.

= Fission barrier
{parent=Nuclear fission}

The increase in a nucleus's collective deformation <potential energy> between a local minimum and a saddle on a path toward separated fragments. A positive final-state <Q value> does not remove this intermediate barrier. A nucleus can cross it after sufficient <excitation energy>, or undergo <quantum tunnelling>. The relevant barrier for neutron-induced fission belongs to the compound nucleus formed after capture, not automatically to the target before capture.

= Liquid-drop fission energy
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{title2=$Q(y)=a_SA^{2/3}[1-y^{2/3}-(1-y)^{2/3}]+a_CZ^2A^{-1/3}[1-y^{5/3}-(1-y)^{5/3}]$}

For two spherical fragments with the same proton fraction as the parent, let $(A_1,Z_1)=y(A,Z)$ and $(A_2,Z_2)=(1-y)(A,Z)$. In the <semi-empirical mass formula> without <nuclear pairing>, nucleon rest energies, the volume term and the <nuclear symmetry energy> cancel in the parent-minus-fragment mass difference. The remaining <Q value> is the displayed competition between increased surface energy and reduced <nuclear Coulomb energy>. Its symmetric value is positive when
$$
\frac{Z^2}{A}>\frac{a_S}{a_C}\frac{2^{1/3}-1}{1-2^{-2/3}}.
$$
This is an energy-release condition, not the disappearance of a <fission barrier> or a prediction of a short <mean lifetime>.

= Symmetric fission maximizes the positive liquid-drop energy release
{parent=Liquid-drop fission energy}

Ignore <nuclear pairing> and use the continuous proportional-fragment model of <liquid-drop fission energy>. Put $S=a_SA^{2/3}$, $x=a_CZ^2/(a_SA)$ and $t=y^{1/3}+(1-y)^{1/3}$. As $y$ runs from zero to $1/2$, $t$ rises from $1$ to $2^{2/3}$. The identity $u^3+v^3=1$ with $u=y^{1/3}$ and $v=(1-y)^{1/3}$ gives
$$
\frac QS=1+x-\frac{t^2+2/t}{3}+\frac{x}{9}(t^5-5t^2-5/t),\qquad
\frac{d(Q/S)}{dt}=\frac{t^3-1}{9t^2}[5x(t^3-1)-6].
$$
The derivative changes sign at most once, from negative to positive. Thus the global maximum on the closed continuous interval occurs at either the no-split endpoint $Q=0$ or the symmetric split. If positive fission energy is available, the symmetric value is the global maximum. This qualification matters: symmetry alone makes $y=1/2$ stationary but does not make it a maximum for all parameters. In fact, $Q''(1/2)<0$ requires $Z^2/A>(2/5)a_S/a_C$; below that value the symmetric split is a local minimum. Real discrete fragments and nuclear-shell corrections are outside this continuous smooth model.

= Nuclear reactor
{parent=Nuclear fission}
{wiki}

= Nuclear criticality
{parent=Nuclear reactor}
{title2=$k_{\rm eff}=1$}

A self-sustaining neutron balance in which production offsets absorption and leakage. In a steady <one-group neutron diffusion equation>, criticality is a compatibility condition between material parameters and the allowed spatial mode.

= Neutron leakage
{parent=Nuclear reactor}

Escape of <neutrons> from a specified region before they participate in further reactions there. A leakage fraction depends on the region's geometry, surrounding materials and energy-dependent transport and absorption, rather than its dimensions alone.

= Under-moderated reactor
{parent=Nuclear reactor}

A <nuclear reactor> on the side of a specified moderation optimum where adding moderator increases neutron multiplication. Removing water then tends to reduce <reactor reactivity>, under the comparison's other fixed conditions. The optimum and the derivative depend on composition, geometry and operating state.<Nuclear Physics and Reactor Theory, Volume 2 by the United States Department of Energy>[Module NP-03, pp.24–25, Moderator Effects]{ref}

= Neutron poison
{parent=Nuclear reactor}

A constituent that absorbs <neutrons> and thereby reduces their availability for a <nuclear fission> chain reaction. Effectiveness depends on <isotope>, <neutron> spectrum and spatial distribution, not just one thermal cross section.

= Xenon poisoning
{parent=Neutron poison}

The reduction of <reactor reactivity> caused by neutron absorption by xenon-135. In a simplified model its abundance is fed by radioactive iodine decay, while its removal is by <radioactive decay> and <neutron capture>. Changes in these pathways can delay its response to a change of <neutron flux>.

= Capacity factor
{parent=Nuclear reactor}
{title2=$f_{\rm cap}$}

Energy generated in a period divided by rated power times elapsed time. At fixed efficiency, it scales annual thermal energy and fuel consumption.

= Conversion ratio
{parent=Nuclear reactor}
{title2=$CR$}

The ratio of the rate of formation of fissile nuclei to the rate at which the specified fissile inventory is consumed, under stated reaction and accounting conventions. A <conversion ratio> below one indicates a replacement shortfall in that inventory. It is distinct from neutron multiplication: a reactor's instantaneous neutron balance and its long-term fuel-inventory balance are different questions.

= Point kinetics model
{parent=Nuclear reactor}

A reduced time-dependent model for a <neutron> population amplitude coupled to <delayed neutron precursor> populations. Spatial and spectral shape changes are neglected or represented through prescribed effective coefficients. Such a model illustrates timescale separation without establishing the spatial behaviour or operating limits of an actual reactor.

= Prompt neutron generation time
{parent=Point kinetics model}
{title2=$\Lambda$}

The effective time associated with successive prompt fission generations in a specified <point kinetics model>. It is distinct from a macroscopic population growth period, which also depends on <reactor reactivity> and <delayed neutrons>.

= Prompt jump approximation
{parent=Point kinetics model}

A timescale-separation approximation that eliminates the rapidly adjusting neutron amplitude in favour of the slower <delayed neutron precursor> reservoir. It describes behaviour after the short prompt transient and need not resolve the initial adjustment.

= Inhour equation
{parent=Point kinetics model}

The characteristic relation between imposed <reactor reactivity> and exponential growth or decay rates in a <point kinetics model>. Several kinetic modes can coexist; the late-time behaviour is determined by the mode with greatest real growth rate that is actually excited.

= Radioactivity
{synonym}

<Radioactive decay> is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable <atomic nucleus> or nuclear state. Its timing can be unpredictable for an individual <atomic nucleus> while a large population has a reproducible average behavior. A constant <nuclear decay constant> $\lambda>0$ gives the familiar <exponential decay> law. The <half-life> describes how quickly the parent population falls, while <radioactive activity> describes its rate of transformations.<University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others/10.3 Radioactive Decay>[University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others; Radioactive Decay Law, equations 10.7–10.20: decay constant, half-life and activity]{ref}

This connection between randomness and a regular population law makes <radioactive decay> useful for measuring elapsed time and for understanding sources of <radiogenic heating>. It also distinguishes two questions in an experiment: what the source emits, and what an instrument records. <Becquerel's uranium-radiation experiment> used a <photographic plate>; the <Curie ionization-current measurement> measured an electrical consequence of radiation. Their readouts reveal different aspects of the same investigation.

= Alpha preformation probability
{parent=Alpha decay}
{title2=$\lambda=\nu P_{\rm pre}T$}

The <alpha preformation probability> is a model-dependent nuclear-structure factor multiplying an interior assault <frequency> and barrier <transmission probability> in an alpha-decay rate estimate. It accounts for the availability of an alpha-like cluster at the matching surface. It is not fixed by the exterior Coulomb tunnelling exponent alone.

= Alpha-particle assault frequency
{parent=Alpha decay}
{title2=$\nu\sim v/L$}

In a semiclassical escape model, an <alpha-particle assault frequency> is the interior rate scale multiplying the barrier <transmission probability>. Confinement alone gives $p\sim\hbar/L$ and hence $\nu\sim\hbar/(m_\alpha L^2)$ up to geometric factors. Counting encounters with either wall differs by a factor of two from returns to one wall. A stationary bound <wavefunction> does not itself follow a classical collisional trajectory.

= Nuclear alpha-decay recoil correction
{parent=Alpha decay}
{title2=$T_\alpha\simeq Q_\alpha M_d/(M_d+m_\alpha)$}

In two-body <alpha decay> of a parent at rest, <momentum conservation> makes daughter and alpha momenta equal and opposite. The nonrelativistic kinetic <energies> therefore satisfy $T_d/T_\alpha=m_\alpha/M_d$, giving
$$
T_\alpha=Q_\alpha\frac{M_d}{M_d+m_\alpha},\qquad T_d=Q_\alpha\frac{m_\alpha}{M_d+m_\alpha}.
$$
An excited daughter reduces the available <Q value>. To leading recoil order, an alpha line separation $\Delta T_\alpha$ corresponds to excitation $E_x=(1+m_\alpha/M_d)\Delta T_\alpha$. Thus ratios of excitation energies inferred from line separations do not need the common recoil factor. These formulas assume kinetic energies small compared with rest <masses>; an exact <relativistic two-body decay> calculation can replace them when necessary.

= Alpha-decay angular-momentum and parity selection rule
{parent=Alpha decay}
{title2=$J_X=0,\ \pi_X=+1\ \Longrightarrow\ J_Y=L,\ \pi_Y=(-1)^L$}

The <ground state> of an <even-even nucleus> has $J^P=0^+$, and the emitted <alpha particle> also has $0^+$. In an <alpha decay> to daughter <spin> $J_Y$, <addition of angular momentum> permits total zero only when the relative <orbital angular momentum> is $L=J_Y$. <Parity conservation> gives $+1=\pi_Y(-1)^L$, hence the daughter has <natural parity>, $0^+,1^-,2^+,3^-,\ldots$. This rule does not itself restrict $J_Y$ to even integers. An even-$J$ sequence such as $0^+,2^+,4^+$ requires additional nuclear structure, for example a positive-parity ground-state <nuclear rotational band>.

= Geiger-Nuttall law
{parent=Alpha decay}
{c}
{title2=$\log_{10}T_{1/2}\simeq aZ_d/\sqrt{Q_\alpha}+b$}

The <Geiger-Nuttall law> expresses the extreme sensitivity of an <alpha decay> <half-life> to its <Q value>. In a restricted range of heavy <nuclei>, a useful local form is $\log_{10}[T_{1/2}/T_*]\simeq aZ_d/\sqrt{Q_\alpha}+b$, where $Z_d$ is the daughter <atomic number>, and the constants depend on the chosen units and nuclear family. It is named for <Hans Geiger> and <John Mitchell Nuttall>.

The <Coulomb barrier> outside the daughter has $V(r)=2Z_d\alpha/r$ in <natural units>. Its outer turning point is $r_t=2Z_d\alpha/Q_\alpha$. The <WKB approximation> gives a penetration probability
$$
P\simeq\exp\left[-2\int_R^{r_t}\sqrt{2\mu\,[V(r)-Q_\alpha]}\,dr\right],
$$
where $\mu$ is the daughter-alpha <reduced mass>. The leading large-barrier exponent is $2\pi(2Z_d\alpha)\sqrt{\mu/(2Q_\alpha)}$, producing the approximate $Z_d/\sqrt{Q_\alpha}$ dependence. Finite nuclear size modifies the exponent and the slowly varying intercept. The <nuclear decay constant> also contains an assault frequency and an alpha-cluster formation probability. Extra <orbital angular momentum> raises the barrier further, and odd-nucleon structure can introduce substantial hindrance. Thus extrapolation from even-even decays gives an order-of-magnitude reference rather than a precision prediction for an odd-odd <nucleus>.

= Atomic and nuclear beta-decay energy conventions
{parent=Beta decay}
{title2=$Q_{\beta^-}=[M_{\rm nuc}(i)-M_{\rm nuc}(f)-m_e]c^2$}

For ground-to-ground beta-minus decay of bare <nuclei>, the nuclear <mass> difference supplies the <electron> rest <energy> as well as the <kinetic energy> shared among products. Neglecting recoil and <neutrino> <mass>, $T_{\max}=Q_{\beta^-}$. For neutral atomic <masses> the additional final atomic <electron> cancels the emitted <electron> rest-mass subtraction, giving $Q\simeq[M_{\rm atom}(i)-M_{\rm atom}(f)]c^2$ up to electron-binding corrections. A daughter excitation reduces the endpoint by its <excitation energy>.

= Kurie plot
{parent=Beta decay}

A <kurie plot> removes known <electron> phase-space and Coulomb factors from a beta spectrum and takes a square root. For negligible <neutrino> <mass>/recoil and constant nuclear shape factor, its ordinate is proportional to $Q-T$, giving a straight line whose kinetic-energy intercept is the beta endpoint. The precise factors depend on whether the measured spectrum is per <momentum> or per <energy>. Forbidden transitions generally need an additional shape correction.

= Shape-corrected Kurie plot
{parent=Kurie plot}
{title2=$K_C=\sqrt{N(p)/[p^2F(p)C(T)]}$}

If a beta spectrum has nonconstant shape factor $C(T)$, divide by it as well as by the Coulomb and phase-space factors before taking a square root. A leading unique first-forbidden shape contains $p_e^2+p_\nu^2$ in <natural units>, with Coulomb and nuclear corrections. Dividing only by a Coulomb <Fermi function> does not remove that shape. A straight uncorrected line must therefore be treated as an approximation, not an exact consequence of forbidden-transition <selection rules>.

= Momentum-bin Kurie ordinate
{parent=Kurie plot}
{title2=$K(p)=\sqrt{N(p)/[p^2F(p)]}$}

For counts $N(p)dp$ per <electron> <momentum> interval, the allowed spectrum is proportional to $p^2F(p)(Q-T)^2dp$ when recoil and <neutrino> <mass> are neglected. Thus the displayed ordinate is linear in <kinetic energy> under a constant-shape assumption. Counts per kinetic-energy interval instead have the factor $pE_e$ in <natural units>; the Jacobian $dT/dp=p/E_e$ accounts for the difference. Mixing those two binning conventions changes the inferred ordinate.

= Beta decay transition
{parent=Beta decay}
{wiki}

A nuclear <beta decay> connects initial and final <quantum states> through vector and axial-vector parts of the <weak charged current>. At leading order in the lepton wavelength expansion, <Fermi transitions> carry lepton-pair <spin> $S=0$, while <Gamow-Teller transitions> carry $S=1$. If the lepton pair carries <orbital angular momentum> $l$, its total <angular momentum> is $J=l$ for $S=0$, or $J=|l-1|,\ldots,l+1$ for $S=1$. The nuclear states must obey $|J_i-J_f|\leq J\leq J_i+J_f$, and their <parities> satisfy $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^l$ in this leading orbital classification. The least permitted $l$ labels the degree of forbiddenness. Real forbidden transitions involve several weak multipole operators; the elementary spin classification does not assert that only one operator or nuclear matrix element exists.

= Comparative half-life
{parent=Beta decay transition}
{title2=$ft$}

The product of a dimensionless beta-decay statistical phase-space factor $f$ and the partial <half-life> $t$ for the transition. The tabulated $\log ft$ normally means $\log_{10}[ft/(1\,\mathrm s)]$. Factoring out this approximate <phase space> dependence allows comparisons of nuclear weak matrix elements. Strong superallowed <Fermi transitions> have small $\log ft$; <forbidden beta decays> generally have larger values, with nuclear-structure exceptions and overlaps between categories.

= Forbidden beta decay
{parent=Beta decay transition}
{title2=$\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^l$}

A <beta decay transition> for which the leading $l=0$ lepton orbital term vanishes by the <angular momentum> or <parity> selection rules. The least contributing orbital order $l=n\geq1$ gives the name $n$th forbidden. First-forbidden transitions change <parity> and can carry total lepton <angular momentum> $0,1,2$; second-forbidden transitions preserve <parity> and can carry $1,2,3$, with Fermi-type $S=0$ contributions restricted to $J=l$. Forbidden is suppression by higher powers of lepton momentum times nuclear size, rather than an absolute prohibition.

= Unique forbidden beta decay
{parent=Forbidden beta decay}
{title2=$\Delta J=l+1,\quad\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^l$}

An $l$th-forbidden <beta decay transition> with the maximal leading angular-momentum transfer $l+1$. Only the spin-dependent $S=1$ coupling can attain that transfer at this orbital order. Thus a $0^+\to2^-$ transition is first-forbidden unique, whereas a same-parity $\Delta J=2$ transition is second-forbidden nonunique. Unique refers to the leading weak multipole structure, not a claim that every correction to the decay vanishes.

= Shell-model prediction for yttrium-91 beta decay
{parent=Unique forbidden beta decay}

Using the orbital order after 28 supplied in the simple shell model, <proton> counts 32 and 38 fill $2p_{3/2}$ and $1f_{5/2}$, so the odd 39th <proton> occupies $2p_{1/2}$. With paired valence <neutrons> this gives a $1/2^-$ yttrium-91 ground-state prediction. The daughter has paired 40 <protons> and one <neutron> beyond the 50-neutron closure in $2d_{5/2}$, giving $5/2^+$. The inferred ground-state beta transition is therefore first-forbidden unique. This is a stated paired-shell model deduction; an endpoint diagram alone does not determine the <spin> assignment or an evaluated <isotope> <mass>.

= First-forbidden unique beta-decay phase space
{parent=Unique forbidden beta decay}
{title2=$C(E)\propto p_e^2+E_\nu^2$}

A first-forbidden unique <beta decay> has $|\Delta J|=2$ with a <parity> change. Its leading lepton shape factor contains two additional powers of momentum relative to an <allowed beta decay>. In the massless, no-Coulomb limit, $\int_0^Q E^2(Q-E)^2[E^2+(Q-E)^2]dE=2Q^7/105$. Thus its microscopic reduced nuclear matrix element multiplies a seventh-power phase-space factor, rather than the fifth power in the <Sargent rule>. A fifth-power comparison across different forbiddenness classes instead extracts convention-dependent effective strengths.

= Allowed beta decay
{parent=Beta decay transition}

A <beta decay transition> whose leading lepton orbital term has $l=0$. <Fermi transitions> allow $\Delta J=0$ and <Gamow-Teller transitions> allow $\Delta J=0,1$, excluding a Gamow-Teller $0\to0$ transition. Both preserve nuclear <parity>. Allowed describes the selection rules; cancellations in the nuclear matrix element can still make an allowed transition slow.

= Sargent rule
{parent=Allowed beta decay}
{c}
{title2=$\Gamma\propto Q^5$}

The approximate fifth-power scaling of an allowed <beta decay> rate with available <energy> when <electron> <mass> and Coulomb distortion are negligible and the nuclear <matrix element> is held fixed. It follows from $\int_0^Qp^2(Q-p)^2dp=Q^5/30$. Different nuclear <matrix elements> or nonrelativistic <electron> kinematics spoil an exact universal law.

= Allowed beta-decay momentum spectrum
{parent=Allowed beta decay}
{title2=$d\Gamma/dp=C F(Z,E)p^2(E_0-E)^2$}

With a momentum-independent allowed nuclear <matrix element>, negligible <nuclear recoil> and negligible <neutrino mass>, the <electron> spectrum in <natural units> is $d\Gamma/dp=C F(Z,E)p^2(E_0-E)^2$, where $E=\sqrt{p^2+m_e^2}$. Transforming to an <energy> spectrum requires the <Jacobian determinant> $dp/dE=E/p$.

= Fermi function
{parent=Allowed beta decay}
{c}
{title2=$F(Z,E)$}

The electron-nucleus Coulomb-distortion factor multiplying an otherwise plane-wave <beta decay> spectrum. It depends on daughter charge, <lepton> charge and <lepton> <energy>. Its <energy> dependence means that a pure fifth-power phase-space rule is not generally exact.

= Superallowed beta decay
{parent=Allowed beta decay}

An especially strong <allowed beta decay>, conventionally including pure <Fermi transitions> between $0^+$ <isobaric analog states>. Those states have closely related spatial and spin structure, so the <isospin> ladder operator has a large overlap. A $0^+\to0^+$ assignment alone fixes the allowed Fermi character, but does not establish analog-state overlap or a large rate; a small <comparative half-life> supplies additional evidence.

= Gamow-Teller transition
{parent=Beta decay transition}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf M_{GT}=\langle f|\sum_k\boldsymbol\sigma_k\tau_k^\pm|i\rangle$}

The spin-dependent axial-vector contribution to an <allowed beta decay>. Its rotational rank is one, permitting $\Delta J=0,1$ with unchanged <parity>, but excluding $0\to0$. Here $\boldsymbol\sigma_k$ acts on <nucleon> <spin> and $\tau_k^\pm$ on <isospin>. Higher-orbital spin-dependent contributions are conventionally described as Gamow-Teller-type forbidden transitions.

= Fermi transition
{parent=Beta decay transition}
{c}
{title2=$M_F=\langle f|\sum_k\tau_k^\pm|i\rangle$}

The spin-independent vector contribution to an <allowed beta decay>. The <isospin> operator $\tau_k^\pm$ changes a <neutron> into a <proton> or the reverse; the sign convention depends on the assigned nucleon isospin projection. Its rotational rank is zero, so $J_f=J_i$ and the <parity> is unchanged. The phrase Fermi-type forbidden contribution extends the $S=0$ classification to higher <orbital angular momentum>.

= Isospin ladder strength in analogue beta decay
{parent=Fermi transition}
{title2=$|M_F|^2=T(T+1)-T_z(T_z\pm1)$}

The <Fermi transition> operator is an <isospin> ladder operator. Acting between normalized members of one <isospin multiplet> gives the displayed squared <matrix element>. An edge transition in a $T=3/2$ multiplet has Fermi strength three, whereas the transition between the two $T=1/2$ mirror members has strength one. This fixes the vector contribution; nonzero-spin analogue transitions can also have a <Gamow-Teller transition> contribution that is not fixed by this counting.

= Beta stability
{parent=Beta decay}

A <nuclide> is beta-stable if it has no energetically allowed single <beta-minus decay>, <beta-plus decay>, or <electron capture> to a lower-mass neighboring <nuclear isobar>, with electron thresholds treated consistently. Beta stability need not imply stability against <alpha decay>, <double beta decay>, or <nuclear fission>.

The band is distinct from the particle-emission boundaries set by <nuclear separation energies>.

= Light-Majorana exchange in neutrinoless double beta decay
{parent=Neutrinoless double beta decay}

Two charged-current beta vertices can be connected by a virtual <Majorana neutrino>. With light exchanged <masses> relative to nuclear <momentum> scales, the <chirality> connection makes the amplitude proportional to $\sum_iU_{ei}^2m_i$, not an incoherent <mass> average. Squaring introduces the <effective Majorana mass>, <nuclear matrix elements> and <phase space>. = Double-beta summed-electron-energy signature
{parent=Neutrinoless double beta decay}

A contained neutrinoless event deposits approximately the nuclear Q-value in the two-electron sum, apart from recoil and losses. Ordinary two-neutrino <double beta decay> has a continuous electron-sum <radiation spectrum>. Resolution, escape losses and backgrounds broaden or mimic features, so a peak assignment requires calibrated response, sidebands and appropriate event topology. A null peak search is a limit on a <decay rate> under an efficiency/background model, not proof of Dirac <neutrinos>.

= Inverse beta decay
{parent=Beta decay}
{wiki}

In the reaction displayed here, an <electron antineutrino> interacts with a <proton> to produce a <positron> and a <neutron>. A prompt <positron> signal followed by <neutron capture> can identify a candidate much more selectively than a single energy deposit. <Inverse beta decay> is a <weak interaction>; the incident <electron antineutrino> is detected through its reaction products.

= Inverse beta decay threshold
{parent=Inverse beta decay}
{title2=$E_{\nu,\mathrm{thr}}=\frac{(m_n+m_e)^2-m_p^2}{2m_p}c^2$}

The <neutron> and <positron> together have more rest <mass> than the initial <proton>, so a finite incident <energy> is necessary even before recoil is considered. In the <proton>'s <rest frame>, use <four-momentum> conservation and a negligible <neutrino mass>. The initial squared center-of-mass <energy> is $m_p^2c^4+2m_pc^2E_\nu$; the least final squared center-of-mass <energy> is $(m_n+m_e)^2c^4$. Equating them gives the threshold above. At threshold the final particles share one center-of-mass <velocity>; they are not both at rest in the laboratory.

Writing $\Delta=m_n-m_p$, the result separates into
$$
E_{\nu,\mathrm{thr}}=(\Delta+m_e)c^2+\frac{(\Delta+m_e)^2}{2m_p}c^2.
$$
The positive second term is the laboratory recoil correction. With rounded rest <energies> $m_pc^2=938.272\,\mathrm{MeV}$, $m_nc^2=939.565\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $m_ec^2=0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$, the calculated threshold is about $1.806\,\mathrm{MeV}$. These rounded inputs follow the recommended particle <masses>.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, proton, neutron and electron mass-energy equivalents in MeV]{ref}

As a further ideal <energy> balance, suppose the <positron> stops and annihilates with an <electron>, and all prompt annihilation <gamma rays> are contained. Its prompt visible <energy> is $T_{e^+}+2m_ec^2$. Neglecting <neutron> recoil gives $E_{\rm vis}\simeq E_\nu-(m_n-m_p)c^2+m_ec^2\simeq E_\nu-0.782\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Escaping <gamma rays>, detector response and recoil change that measured sum. The delayed capture signal is a separate energy deposit, not an extra contribution to the incident <electron antineutrino>'s prompt energy reconstruction.

= Delayed neutron-capture coincidence window
{parent=Cowan-Reines reactor antineutrino experiment}
{title2=$\epsilon(W)=1-e^{-W/\tau}$}

Why not accept every delayed pulse? A longer window captures more genuine <neutron capture> signals, but also admits more <accidental coincidences>. Consider an original model with a capture waiting <time> following an <exponential distribution> of <mean> $\tau>0$. Assume detection after a prompt signal accepts $0<t<W$, with perfect time measurement and no lower cut. Integrating the normalized density gives
$$
\epsilon(W)=\int_0^W\frac{e^{-t/\tau}}{\tau}\,dt=1-e^{-W/\tau}.
$$
This assumes that moderation, competing capture channels, boundaries and electronic recovery can be represented or neglected in a single effective waiting-time model. It is not a fitted historical capture law.

Let independent prompt-like and delayed-like background streams have <Poisson process> rates $R_p$ and $R_d$. The <accidental coincidence> pair rate in a directed window of full width $W$ is $R_pR_dW$. Electronics producing at most one accepted delayed match per prompt instead give $R_p(1-e^{-R_dW})$. These definitions coincide to first order when $R_dW\ll1$. Correlated backgrounds, such as one external <neutron> producing both a prompt recoil and a later capture, require separate controls; an independent-stream estimate cannot remove them.

For chosen $\tau=20\,\mu\mathrm s$ and $W=50\,\mu\mathrm s$, the accepted fraction is $1-e^{-2.5}\simeq0.918$. With chosen $R_p=40\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $R_d=30\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the pair rate is $0.0600\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, while the at-most-one rate is approximately $0.05996\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. These are teaching parameters, not reported Savannah River rates. A measured uncorrelated background can be tested using displaced time windows; a reactor-off measurement addresses a different question and cannot by itself characterize every reactor-correlated background.

= Optimum capture window in a background-dominated model
{parent=Delayed neutron-capture coincidence window}
{title2=$e^x=1+2x,\quad x=W/\tau$}

If genuine pairs arrive at rate $S_0$ before the timing cut, an exposure $T$ gives $S=S_0T(1-e^{-W/\tau})$ and expected accidental pairs $B=R_pR_dWT$. In a background-dominated large-count approximation, maximizing $S/\sqrt B$ reduces to maximizing $f(x)=(1-e^{-x})/\sqrt x$ for $x=W/\tau>0$. Differentiation gives the positive stationary solution $e^x=1+2x$, or $x\simeq1.25643$. The endpoint $x=0$ solves the rearranged equation but is outside the domain of this derivative calculation; $f(x)$ tends to zero at both positive-domain extremes.

Thus the chosen $\tau=20\,\mu\mathrm s$ gives $W\simeq25.13\,\mu\mathrm s$ for this particular criterion, accepting about $0.715$ of genuine delayed signals. This differs from maximizing signal counts alone, which would favor longer windows. Small samples, appreciable signal fluctuations, unknown background, dead time and multiple-candidate vetoes require a fuller <likelihood> model. The calculation isolates a tradeoff rather than prescribing an experimental cut.

= Nuclear decay constant
{parent=Radioactive decay}
{title2=$\lambda$}

In the constant-rate <mathematical model>, an undecayed <atomic nucleus> has conditional probability
$$
\Pr(T\le t+\Delta t\mid T>t)=1-e^{-\lambda\Delta t}
=\lambda\Delta t+O((\lambda\Delta t)^2)
$$
of decaying during the next positive interval $\Delta t$. The <nuclear decay constant> has units of inverse <time>. Its value describes a rate; it does not specify a scheduled death time for one <atomic nucleus>. The conditional probability is independent of the age $t$, the <memorylessness of the exponential distribution>.

= Finite-population radioactive decay
{parent=Radioactive decay}
{title2=$N(t)\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(N_0,e^{-\lambda t})$}

Take $N_0$ independently decaying <atomic nuclei>, each with lifetime $T_i$ drawn from an <exponential distribution> of rate $\lambda$. With no replenishment, the survivor count is
$$
N(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_0}\mathbf1_{\{T_i>t\}},\qquad
p(t)=e^{-\lambda t}.
$$
Each <indicator variable> has success probability $p(t)$, so the <binomial distribution> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[N(t)]=N_0p(t),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(N(t))=N_0p(t)(1-p(t)).}
$$
The smooth population law is the <expected value> $\overline N(t)=\mathbb E[N(t)]$, satisfying $\overline N'=-\lambda\overline N$. One realization instead loses whole <atomic nuclei> at discrete times. An observed count above the smooth curve need not contradict the <mathematical model>.

For an original chosen example, let $N_0=1000$ and <half-life> $3\,\mathrm h$. Then $\lambda=\log2/(3\,\mathrm h)$. After $9\,\mathrm h$, the survival probability is $1/8$, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[N(9\,\mathrm h)]=125,\qquad
\operatorname{SD}(N(9\,\mathrm h))=\sqrt{109.375}\simeq10.46.}
$$
The <standard deviation> describes variation between repeated populations governed by these assumptions. It is not an uncertainty interval for a noiseless deterministic curve.

{title=Random survivor counts and the smooth radioactive-decay law}

These are model calculations, not measurements from a historical sample.}

The <half-life> and the mean lifetime answer different questions:
$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{\log2}{\lambda},\qquad
\mathbb E[T_i]=\frac1\lambda.
$$
The first is the <median> of one lifetime, or the time when half the population survives on average. The second is the lifetime's <expected value>. In this example they are $3\,\mathrm h$ and approximately $4.33\,\mathrm h$.

= Radioactive activity
{parent=Radioactive decay}
{title2=$A=\lambda N$}

With $n$ parent <atomic nuclei> present, the conditional expected number of transformations in a short interval is approximately $\lambda n\Delta t$. The corresponding <radioactive activity> is $A=\lambda n$. Averaging over the <finite-population radioactive decay> model gives $\overline A(t)=\lambda\overline N(t)$. Its unit is the <becquerel>.<University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others/10.3 Radioactive Decay>[University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others; Equations 10.17–10.20 and the following becquerel definition]{ref}

For a finite counting interval, depletion matters. Suppose each transformation is recorded independently with <detection efficiency> $\varepsilon$, with at most one count per transformation and no background. The <expected value> of the count between $t$ and $t+\Delta t$ is
$$
\mathbb E[C]=\varepsilon\overline N(t)(1-e^{-\lambda\Delta t}).
$$
Only when $\lambda\Delta t\ll1$ does this become approximately $\varepsilon\overline A(t)\Delta t$. A count rate therefore needs a detector model before it can be interpreted as the source's <radioactive activity>.

= Nuclear statistical equilibrium
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}
{title2=NSE}

At sufficiently high temperature and density, forward and inverse nuclear reactions can balance so that abundances are determined by thermodynamic conditions, nuclear binding and conserved quantities. <Silicon burning> approaches such an equilibrium among iron-group nuclei. = Nickel–iron nuclear equilibrium ratio
{parent=Nuclear statistical equilibrium}
{title2=$n_{56}/n_{54}=K n_p^2T^{-3}e^{\Delta B/(kT)}$}

Nickel-56 and iron-54 have the same <neutron> number and differ by two <protons>. Dividing their <nuclear statistical equilibrium> abundance formulas cancels the <neutron> density and leaves the displayed ratio, with $\Delta B=B_{56}-B_{54}$ and $K$ containing temperature-independent statistical and <mass> factors in the simplified model. For material with equal total <proton> and <neutron> numbers and only these nuclei plus free <protons>, charge balance gives $n_p=2n_{54}$. Thus $n_{56}/n_{54}^3\propto T^{-3}e^{\Delta B/(kT)}$. This has a finite <temperature> maximum only if the signed energy difference is negative; conventional positive total nuclear binding energies for these two actual isotopes instead give a positive difference.

= Electron fraction in stellar matter
{parent=Nuclear statistical equilibrium}
{title2=$Y_e=\sum_i X_i Z_i/A_i$}

For electrically neutral fully ionized matter, $Y_e$ is the number of electrons, or total <protons>, per baryon. With isotope <mass> fractions $X_i$, charge numbers $Z_i$ and <mass> numbers $A_i$, the displayed sum neglects small nuclear mass-defect corrections. Strong and electromagnetic reactions preserve it, while <electron capture> and <beta-plus decay> reduce it. The proton-to-neutron number ratio is $Y_e/(1-Y_e)$.

= Neutron excess
{parent=Electron fraction in stellar matter}
{title2=$\eta=1-2Y_e$}

The <neutron excess> is the number of <neutrons> minus <protons> per baryon. A mixture of carbon-12 and oxygen-16 has zero <neutron excess>, while neon-22 contributes $2/22$ per baryon in that isotope. Thus a neon-22 <mass> fraction $X_{22}$ gives $\eta=X_{22}/11$. A nonzero excess shifts iron-group ashes from symmetric nickel-56 toward neutron-rich isotopes, even before additional weak reactions occur.

= Neutron capture
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}
{title2=$^{A}_{Z}X+n\longrightarrow{}^{A+1}_{Z}X+\gamma$}

Capture of a neutron increases mass number without immediately changing atomic number. It avoids the charged-particle <Coulomb barrier>. Subsequent <beta-minus decay> can increase atomic number. Whether capture is slow or fast compared with relevant beta decays distinguishes the <slow neutron-capture process> from the <rapid neutron-capture process>.

= Neutron-capture excitation energy
{parent=Neutron capture}
{title2=$E^*=S_n(A+1,Z)+E_n^{\rm CM}$}

After neutron capture on $(A,Z)$, the compound nucleus is $(A+1,Z)$. With negligible incoming kinetic energy, conservation of <energy> gives $E^*=M(A,Z)+m_n-M(A+1,Z)$ in <natural units>. This is the <neutron separation energy> of the compound nucleus. At finite incident energy, add the kinetic energy in the initial <centre-of-momentum frame>; in a nonrelativistic heavy-target approximation it is $A/(A+1)$ times the laboratory neutron kinetic energy. <Nuclear pairing> can significantly change capture excitation between neighboring even- and odd-mass targets.

= Nucleosynthesis
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

<Nucleosynthesis> is the production of <atomic nuclei> through <nuclear reactions>. It includes <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>, <stellar nucleosynthesis> and production in explosive or compact-remnant environments. Making nuclei and delivering them to the <interstellar medium> are separate steps in chemical enrichment.

= Stellar nucleosynthesis
{parent=Nucleosynthesis}
{wiki}

<Stellar nucleosynthesis> encompasses nuclear production during stellar evolution and explosions, including <hydrogen burning>, <core helium burning>, advanced burning and neutron-capture processes. Stellar mass, composition, mixing and mass loss govern which nuclei form and which eventually escape. <Slow neutron-capture process> and <rapid neutron-capture process> build many nuclei beyond the iron group.

= Neon-22 neutron excess from nitrogen-14
{parent=Stellar nucleosynthesis}
{title2=$X_{22}=\frac{22}{14}X_{14},\quad Y_e=\frac12-\frac{X_{14}}{14}$}

Two alpha captures and an intervening <beta-plus decay> turn each nitrogen-14 nucleus into neon-22, incorporating eight baryons from <helium> and converting one <proton> to a <neutron>. With no loss or dilution and nuclear <mass> defects neglected, a nitrogen-14 <mass> fraction $X_{14}$ therefore yields neon-22 <mass> fraction $(22/14)X_{14}$, not $X_{14}$. In otherwise symmetric carbon-oxygen matter this gives the displayed <electron fraction in stellar matter>. A final <neon> <mass> fraction and a pre-helium-burning metallicity must not silently be treated as identical.

= Gamma-process nucleosynthesis

{synonym}

Hot explosive material can use <photodisintegration> on pre-existing heavy seed nuclei through neutron, proton and alpha emission. This gamma process supplies some proton-rich isotopes that the principal neutron-capture paths bypass. It is a particular mechanism, rather than a synonym for every proposed proton-rich nucleosynthetic process.

= r-process

{c}
{wiki}

= Rapid neutron-capture process
{synonym}

At sufficiently large neutron density, successive <neutron captures> outrun relevant beta decays and produce very neutron-rich nuclei. Decay toward stability afterward yields many heavy isotopes, including some actinides. Neutron-rich ejecta from <neutron star mergers> provide an established site; particular magnetically driven collapse outflows are additional candidates. = Nuclear reaction
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

A <nuclear reaction> transforms one or more <atomic nuclei> through interactions with particles or other nuclei. Total energy, momentum, electric charge and nucleon number constrain ordinary low-energy reaction bookkeeping. <Nuclear fusion>, <alpha capture>, <neutron capture> and <photodisintegration> are important examples in <stellar nucleosynthesis>. Weak processes can also change the proton-to-neutron ratio.

= Compound nucleus
{parent=Nuclear reaction}

An intermediate excited <atomic nucleus> formed when the reactants' available <energy> is redistributed among nuclear degrees of freedom before decay. Its resolved levels can appear as resonances. An isolated level's formation and decay into particular channels are described by <partial decay widths> in the <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section>; direct reactions and overlapping resonances need not obey that simple one-level description.

= Nuclear reaction cross-section
{parent=Nuclear reaction}
{title2=$\sigma(E)$}

The effective <area> for a specified <nuclear reaction> channel at given incident <energy>. Multiplying incident <particle flux>, target count and this <cross-section> gives an ideal event rate; target losses and <detection efficiency> require separate corrections.

A reaction coefficient formed from target <number densities> and microscopic <nuclear reaction cross-sections>. It has inverse-length units. Multiplying it by the scalar <neutron flux> gives the corresponding reaction rate per volume, under the stated energy averaging.

= Neutron absorption cross section
{parent=Nuclear reaction cross-section}

A cross section for removal of an incident neutron by absorption, including capture and neutron-induced fission where relevant. Its value depends on nuclide and neutron energy.

The initial minus final rest <energy> in a <nuclear reaction>; positive values represent <energy> available for outgoing <kinetic energy> in addition to the incident <kinetic energy>.

= Resonance channel threshold conversion

= Beta-minus decay

An energetically allowed weak transition converts a neutron into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. Free <neutron> decay is an example; in nuclei, the nuclear binding energy determines whether the transition is possible.

= Nuclear fusion
{parent=Nuclear physics}

Fusion combines nuclear reactants into heavier compound nuclei or products. A net decrease in rest mass releases energy; heavier fusion reactions can instead require energy. <Stellar nuclear fusion> uses exothermic networks to power stars, and <quantum tunnelling> through the <Coulomb barrier> permits many charged-particle reactions at stellar temperatures.

= Alpha capture

Capture of an <alpha particle> increases the mass number by four and atomic number by two. Radiative alpha capture releases a photon, while reactions such as $(\alpha,n)$ can instead emit a neutron. <Carbon-12 alpha capture> competes with helium production of carbon during <core helium burning>.

Capture of an <alpha particle> by <carbon>-12 makes <oxygen>-16. Competition between this reaction and the <triple-alpha process> helps determine the carbon-to-oxygen ratio after <core helium burning>.

= Acoustic eikonal equation in a shear flow
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$(\omega-Uk_x)^2=c_0^2|\mathbf k|^2$}

For a slowly varying parallel <shear flow> $U(y)\mathbf e_x$, the leading <WKB method> neglects mean-velocity gradients while retaining local advection. The <continuity equation> and <linearized Euler equations> then give the displayed acoustic <dispersion relation>. On the positive intrinsic-frequency branch, $\omega=Uk_x+c_0|\mathbf k|$ and the <group velocity> is $U\mathbf e_x+c_0\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|$. A <wavefront> normal is parallel to $\mathbf k$; the acoustic energy-ray direction generally differs because of the advection term.

= Ray turning in a wall shear layer
{parent=Acoustic eikonal equation in a shear flow}
{title2=$k_y^2=(\omega-Uk_x)^2/c_0^2-k_x^2$}

In a stationary, horizontally homogeneous wall <boundary layer>, <Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations> conserve $\omega$ and $k_x$. If $U$ increases away from the wall, downstream-oriented $k_x>0$ waves have decreasing positive intrinsic <frequency> and decreasing $k_y^2$. At $U=\omega/k_x-c_0$, the transverse <wavenumber> vanishes and the acoustic ray turns. For $U(0)=0$, a downstream launch angle $\beta_0$ above the wall can reach a uniform stream of <Mach number> $M_\infty$ only if $\cos\beta_0\le(1+M_\infty)^{-1}$. This uses the positive acoustic branch, constant <sound speed> and no intervening critical layer; a turning point itself requires a uniform local approximation rather than the ordinary <WKB method>.

= Small-height Neumann scattering
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$\partial_z\psi-h'\partial_x\psi=0\quad(z=h(x))$}

For a rigid rough boundary and incident pressure $Ae^{i\alpha x-i\beta z}$, use time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and $\alpha^2+\beta^2=k^2$. The flat reflected wave is $Ae^{i\alpha x+i\beta z}$. Expanding the <Neumann boundary condition> at $z=0$ gives $\partial_z\psi_1=2Ae^{i\alpha x}(\beta^2h+i\alpha h')$. For height transform $\widehat h(q)=\int h(x)e^{-iqx}dx$, the outgoing correction is $(2A/(2\pi i))\int (k^2-\alpha q)\widehat h(q-\alpha)e^{iqx+i\beta(q)z}/\beta(q)\,dq$, with $\beta(q)=\sqrt{k^2-q^2}$ on the outgoing sheet. Controlled height expansions also require adequate slope and spectral regularity; small incident phase displacement alone does not bound every normal-derivative correction.

= Oblique coherent reflection from a stationary rigid rough surface
{parent=Small-height Neumann scattering}
{title2=$r_N=1-\frac1{\pi\beta}\int\frac{(k^2-\alpha q)^2}{\beta(q)}S_h(q-\alpha)\,dq+\cdots$}

= Acoustic waveguide

An acoustic waveguide confines sound transversely while allowing longitudinal propagation. Rigid walls impose a homogeneous normal-velocity condition, producing Neumann transverse modes. Each transverse eigenvalue gives a longitudinal wave number: modes below their cutoff decay evanescently, while cut-on modes carry outgoing sound. = Independent-edge radiation from an open planar duct
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$\phi_d\sim-i(-1)^n D(r)\cos(\alpha/2)\sin(\theta/2)\sin(k_0h\sin\theta)/(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha)$}

For the even <acoustic waveguide> mode $\cos(n\pi y/h)e^{-ikx}$, set $\sin\alpha=n\pi/(k_0h)$ and $k=k_0\cos\alpha>0$. At leading independent-edge order for large $k_0h$, the two incident travelling constituents each have amplitude $(-1)^n/2$ at the relevant wall endpoint. Reflecting the upper-edge coordinates reverses the edge-wave sign. Their path phases are $e^{\pm ik_0h\sin\theta}$. Thus <Sommerfeld half-plane diffraction> gives the displayed sum, where $D(r)=\sqrt{2/(\pi k_0r)}e^{-ik_0r-i\pi/4}$. For $n>0$, the apparent poles at $\theta=\pm\alpha$ are removable in the combined expression, with limit $ikhD(r)/2$. Single-edge nonuniform far-field poles are not physical infinities. = Wiener-Hopf solution for an open parallel-plate waveguide

= Acoustic equipartition in a rigid waveguide
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$\langle E_{\rm kin}\rangle=\langle E_{\rm comp}\rangle$}

For a separated acoustic potential with transverse Neumann eigenvalue $\kappa^2$, integration by parts gives $\int|\nabla_\perp F|^2=\kappa^2\int F^2$. The time-averaged kinetic energy is proportional to $(k^2+\kappa^2)\int F^2$, while compression energy is proportional to $\omega^2\int F^2/c_0^2$. The dispersion relation $\omega^2=c_0^2(k^2+\kappa^2)$ proves equality. At a ray speed below $c_0$, nondegenerate stationary phase gives dispersive amplitudes of order $t^{-1/2}$; the zero transverse mode instead preserves an undispersed pulse on the sound-speed ray.

= Outgoing modes of an open rigid acoustic waveguide

= Acoustic analogy
{parent=Acoustics}

An acoustic analogy rearranges fluid conservation equations into a chosen linear acoustic propagation operator plus effective forcing. The rearrangement can be exact before source approximations are made. A term classified as forcing can also contain propagation effects omitted from the reference operator; this produces <propagation-source ambiguity in an acoustic analogy>. The <Lighthill acoustic analogy> is a familiar homogeneous reference-medium example.

= Propagation-source ambiguity in an acoustic analogy
{parent=Acoustic analogy}

The distinction between generation and propagation depends partly on the reference operator chosen in an <acoustic analogy>. For example, a homogeneous-Laplacian operator with spatially varying reference sound speed omits the first-gradient terms of the <stratified acoustic pressure equation>. These terms then reappear as effective forcing, even for a freely propagating small disturbance in a static medium. A right-hand-side term is therefore not automatically an independent physical noise source.

= Pressure-based acoustic analogy with a potential body force
{parent=Acoustic analogy}
{title2=$Q,W_{ij}$}

Eliminating momentum between the <continuity equation> and <conservation of momentum> gives $\rho_{tt}=\partial_i\partial_j(\rho u_i u_j-\sigma_{ij})+\nabla^2(p+\chi)$ when the force density is $-\nabla\chi$. For $q=p-(P-\chi)$ and time-independent reference fields,
$$
(\widehat c_0^{-2}\partial_t^2-\nabla^2)q=\partial_i\partial_jW_{ij}+Q_{tt},\qquad
W_{ij}=\rho u_i u_j-\sigma_{ij},\qquad Q=q/\widehat c_0^2-(\rho-\widehat\rho_0).
$$
No energy equation is needed for this identity. The arbitrary reference fields should match the stationary medium where the outgoing sound is evaluated.

= Small-height Dirichlet scattering
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$\psi(x,h(x))=0,\quad h=\varepsilon\eta$}

<Small-height Dirichlet scattering> expands the acoustic boundary condition about a mean plane for a fixed regular surface profile. The zeroth-order field includes the flat reflection; higher orders solve outgoing <Helmholtz equation> problems with induced boundary data. Small $|kh|$ controls the incident-field Taylor expansion, but rapidly varying roughness can require additional control of normal derivatives and spectral moments.

= Coherent reflection from a stationary rough surface
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\langle\psi_s(x,0)\rangle=-1+(k/\pi)\int\beta S_h$}

Through second order in height, a normally incident acoustic plane wave has the displayed mean reflected <amplitude> for a Dirichlet boundary. The propagating part of the <power spectrum of surface height> contributes a positive real correction to the negative flat reflection coefficient, while the evanescent part contributes an imaginary correction. The mean linear rough correction is zero.

= Height-correlation dependence of coherent reflection
{parent=Coherent reflection from a stationary rough surface}

The quadratic coherent correction weights the <power spectrum of surface height> by $\beta(q)$, so equal <root mean square> heights can give different mean fields. Only an additional long-spatial-scale approximation, with spectrum concentrated at $|q|\ll k$, reduces it to $2k^2\sigma^2$. = Power spectrum of surface height
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$S_h(q)=\int C_h(\xi)e^{-iq\xi}d\xi$}

For a real zero-mean stationary height process, its <power spectrum of surface height> is the <Fourier transform> of the height <covariance function>. It is even and nonnegative, and $\sigma^2=(2\pi)^{-1}\int S_h(q)dq$. More general stationary processes have a spectral measure rather than a density. Weighted spectral moments control the existence of boundary derivatives and second-order mean scattering corrections.

= Physical surface trace and reference-plane trace
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}

For normal incidence on a Dirichlet rough surface, the scattered trace on the moving boundary is exactly $-e^{-ikh(x)}$. Its trace on the mean plane is instead $-1+2ikh+2k h\mathcal B h+\cdots$. The two traces are related by a normal Taylor expansion and must not be identified. Their ensemble averages can therefore have different quadratic coefficients without inconsistency.

= Second-order rough-surface scattered field
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\psi_s^{[2]}(x,0)=2k h\mathcal B h$}

For normal plane-wave incidence, the second-order <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives $\psi_s^{[2]}=-h\partial_z\psi_s^{[1]}$ at the mean plane, since $\partial_z^2\psi^{[0]}$ vanishes there. The <Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for a Helmholtz half-space> then yields $2k h\mathcal B h$. This nonlocal expression is not generally $2k^2h^2$; that local limit needs roughness varying slowly compared with the wavelength.

= First-order rough-surface scattered field
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\psi_s^{[1]}=-\mathcal E[h\partial_z\psi^{[0]}|_0]$}

Taylor expansion of the <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives the first-order boundary trace $-h\partial_z\psi^{[0]}$ at the mean plane. The <outgoing angular spectrum> then propagates it. A zero-mean surface gives zero mean for this linear rough correction, while the complete <scattered field> still contains its nonzero flat reflection.

= Specular first-order mean reflection
{parent=First-order rough-surface scattered field}
{title2=$\langle\psi_{\rm sc}\rangle=(-1+2iq_i\langle f\rangle)e^{ip_ix+iq_iz}$}

= Rough-surface far-field intensity per illuminated length

= Outgoing angular spectrum
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}

The <outgoing angular spectrum> propagates prescribed scalar boundary data into a half-space. = Height propagation of an angular-spectrum correlation
{parent=Outgoing angular spectrum}
{title2=$m(\xi,z)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int S(\nu)e^{i\nu\xi-2\operatorname{Im}q(\nu)z}d\nu$}

= Reference-plane validity of a rough-surface angular spectrum
{parent=Outgoing angular spectrum}

An upward <angular spectrum> obtained from a horizontal field trace is exact in the homogeneous half-space above that reference plane. For a rough boundary $z=f(x)$, choose the plane above $\sup f$. = Acoustic inverse source problem

= Fixed-frequency nonradiating source
{parent=Acoustic inverse source problem}
{title2=$Q=(\Delta+k^2)\chi,\qquad \chi\in C_c^\infty(A)$}

A source is nonradiating at frequency $k$ if its outgoing field has zero <far-field pattern>. For every $\chi\in C_c^\infty(A)$, the source $Q=(\Delta+k^2)\chi$ has outgoing field $\psi=\chi$ and is invisible outside $A$. Integration by parts shows that its <Fourier transform> vanishes on $|\boldsymbol\xi|=k$, so it belongs to the <kernel of a linear map> defined by the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency>. This family proves nonuniqueness independently of the instability of the inverse.

= Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency
{parent=Acoustic inverse source problem}
{title2=$T_k:L^2(A)\to L^2(S_1)$}

For $(\Delta+k^2)\psi=Q$, the <outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation> yields the <far-field pattern>
$$
(T_kQ)(\boldsymbol\theta)=-\frac1{4\pi}\int_A e^{-ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf r}Q(\mathbf r)\,d^3\mathbf r,\qquad |\boldsymbol\theta|=1.
$$
The sign corresponds to the Green function satisfying $(\Delta+k^2)G=-\delta$. This <Hilbert-Schmidt operator> samples the source <Fourier transform> on the sphere $|\boldsymbol\xi|=k$; for a ball of radius $r_0$, its squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm is $r_0^3/3$.

= Normal kernel of the source-to-far-field operator
{parent=Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency}
{title2=$K(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\frac1{4\pi}\frac{\sin(k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)}{k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$}

The <adjoint source-to-far-field operator> composed with the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency> has volume kernel $K(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=(4\pi)^{-1}\operatorname{sinc}(k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)$, where $\operatorname{sinc}(s)=\sin(s)/s$ and $\operatorname{sinc}(0)=1$. This follows from $\int_{S_1}e^{ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf d}dS=4\pi\operatorname{sinc}(k|\mathbf d|)$. Its smoothing action helps explain the unstable <normal equation for a linear inverse problem>.

= Adjoint source-to-far-field operator
{parent=Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency}
{title2=$T_k^*$}

With complex <inner products> linear in the first argument, the <adjoint operator> of the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency> is
$$
(T_k^*g)(\mathbf r)=-\frac1{4\pi}\int_{S_1}g(\boldsymbol\theta)e^{ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf r}\,dS(\boldsymbol\theta).
$$
It is a normalized <Herglotz wave function>. Complex conjugation reverses the phase sign, while retaining the real factor $-1/(4\pi)$.

= Relativistic three-body decay

= Soft-particle endpoint
{parent=Relativistic three-body decay}

A boundary of <phase space> approached as a massless outgoing particle's <energy> tends to zero. A zero four-vector is not a physical positive-energy one-particle state. An energy bound requiring such a particle is a <supremum> or <infimum>, even when the closed kinematic interval is conventionally described using minimum and maximum. This distinction can matter when applying three-body formulas at mixed massive-massless recoil thresholds.

= Three-body decay energy endpoints
{parent=Relativistic three-body decay}
{title2=$m_1\leq E_1\leq[M^2+m_1^2-(m_2+m_3)^2]/(2M)$}

Use <natural units> $c=1$ and assume open <phase space>, $M>m_1+m_2+m_3$. In the parent's <rest frame>, define $s_{23}=(p_2+p_3)^2$. <Four-momentum conservation> gives
$$
E_1=\frac{M^2+m_1^2-s_{23}}{2M},\qquad (m_2+m_3)^2\leq s_{23}\leq(M-m_1)^2.
$$
The upper recoil-mass limit follows from $E_1\geq m_1$. For positive $m_2,m_3$, the lower recoil-mass limit follows in their pair <rest frame> and is attained when their relative momentum vanishes. If both recoil particles are massless, $s_{23}=2E_2E_3(1-\cos\theta_{23})$ reaches zero with nonzero parallel momenta. If exactly one is massless, $s_{23}$ approaches the nonzero daughter's squared mass only when the massless particle becomes soft. Thus that upper $E_1$ bound is a supremum for a genuine three-body event with all massless particles of positive energy. If $m_1=0$, the lower endpoint is likewise a soft limit. Multiplying the mass expressions for $E_1$ by $c^2$ restores energy units.

= Quark-model baryon-decuplet construction
{parent=Quark-model constituent bookkeeping}
{title2=$\dim\operatorname{Sym}^3\mathbb C^3=\binom{5}{3}=10$}

A particularly economical example is the <baryon decuplet>. Restrict to fully symmetric three-<quark> flavor states. The nonnegative occupation numbers satisfy $n_u+n_d+n_s=3$, giving $\binom{3+3-1}{3}=10$ possibilities by <stars and bars>. Their additive labels are
$$
Q=n_u-1,\qquad I_3=\frac{n_u-n_d}{2},\qquad Y=1-n_s.
$$
These formulas follow directly from the constituent table. With fixed $n_s$, there are $4-n_s$ possible values of $n_u$, so the <weight diagram> has rows of four, three, two and one states. Its outer triangle is therefore a consequence of the constituent construction, rather than an arbitrary drawing convention.

= Diquark

= Meson hyperfine mass splitting
{parent=Meson}
{title2=$M_1-M_0=A/(m_1m_2)$}

In a simple constituent <quark model>, a spin-dependent contribution $A\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2/(m_1m_2)$ splits a spin-zero and spin-one <meson> having the same spatial configuration. For two spin-half constituents, <addition of angular momentum> gives $\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=[S(S+1)-3/2]/2$, equal to $-3/4$ for $S=0$ and $+1/4$ for $S=1$. Thus
$$
M_0=M_{\rm base}-\frac{3A}{4m_1m_2},\qquad M_1=M_{\rm base}+\frac{A}{4m_1m_2},\qquad M_1-M_0=\frac{A}{m_1m_2}.
$$
The spin-averaged mass is $(M_0+3M_1)/4=M_{\rm base}$. Positive $A$ places the vector state above the pseudoscalar. Determining $A$ from just $M_0$ needs an additional assumption about $M_{\rm base}$; setting it equal to $m_1+m_2$ neglects all other spin-independent binding terms. A splitting below the <pion> mass closes a one-pion strong decay to the lower meson, often making the allowed electromagnetic transition with <photon> emission dominant.

= Quarkonium
{parent=Meson}

A <quarkonium> is a <bound state> of a heavy <quark> and its own <antiquark>. Examples are <charmonium> and the bottom-antibottom states called <Upsilon mesons>. Spectroscopic labels $n\,{}^{2S+1}L_J$ distinguish radial excitation, total quark <spin>, relative <orbital angular momentum> and total <angular momentum>.

= Electromagnetic production and decay of vector quarkonium
{parent=Quarkonium}
{title2=$J^{PC}=1^{--},\quad\Gamma_{ee}\propto\alpha^2Q_q^2|\psi(0)|^2/M^2$}

Single-photon <electron-positron annihilation> can directly form <quarkonium> with $J^{PC}=1^{--}$. A vector S-wave <quarkonium> can annihilate back through a virtual <photon> into a charged-lepton pair. At leading nonrelativistic order, its electronic partial <decay width> is proportional to $\alpha^2Q_q^2|\psi(0)|^2/M^2$, where the wavefunction at the origin describes the quark-antiquark annihilation overlap.

Below the threshold for a pair of heavy-light <mesons>, hadronic decays require annihilation, primarily into three <gluons> for a color-singlet $1^{--}$ state. One gluon cannot carry a color-singlet initial state, and two gluons have the wrong charge-conjugation parity. Above the heavy-light pair threshold, a <strong interaction> can instead separate the heavy quark and antiquark into different mesons after creating a light pair. This can greatly increase the total <decay width> without comparably changing the short-distance electronic partial width. A specific example is the contrast between the first three <Upsilon mesons> and the $4S$ state. Higher radial states can additionally undergo hadronic or radiative transitions to lower quarkonium states.

= Meson parity and charge conjugation
{parent=Meson}
{title2=$P=(-1)^{L+1},\quad C=(-1)^{L+S}$}

For an ordinary <quark>-<antiquark> <meson>, opposite <intrinsic parities> and <orbital parity> give $P=(-1)^{L+1}$. For a self-conjugate flavor state, <charge conjugation> exchanges the two constituents. Reversal of their relative momentum gives $(-1)^L$, spin exchange gives $(-1)^{S+1}$, and reordering the two fermionic creation operators gives one extra minus sign. Their product is $C=(-1)^{L+S}$. Electrical neutrality alone does not imply that a state is an eigenstate of charge conjugation; charged mesons have no individual C eigenvalue.

= Neutral meson mixing
{parent=Meson}

Transitions between a neutral <meson> carrying flavour and its <antiparticle>. Decays are described on this two-state subspace by an effective <Hamiltonian operator> $M-i\Gamma/2$ with <Hermitian matrices> $M,\Gamma$.

= Effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing
{parent=Neutral meson mixing}
{title2=$H_{\rm eff}=M-i\Gamma/2$}

A decaying particle-antiparticle pair is described by a two-dimensional <effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing> with dispersive matrix $M$ and decay matrix $\Gamma$, both <Hermitian matrices>. The <effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing> itself is generally not a <Hermitian matrix>. <CPT> symmetry equates its diagonal entries, by separately equating diagonal <masses> and <decay widths>. For $H_{\rm eff}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&a\end{pmatrix}$ the <eigenvalues> are $a\pm\sqrt{bc}$ and generic <eigenvectors> are $(\sqrt b,\pm\sqrt c)^T$, with correlated square-root branches. Complex <eigenvalues> give <masses> and <decay widths> through $\mu=m-i\Gamma/2$.

= Kaon CP eigenstates
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$CP|K_{1,2}\rangle=\pm|K_{1,2}\rangle$}

The <CP eigenstates> of neutral <kaons> depend on a flavor-state phase convention. Using $CP|K^0\rangle=-|\bar K^0\rangle$, set $|K_1\rangle=(|K^0\rangle-|\bar K^0\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|K_2\rangle=(|K^0\rangle+|\bar K^0\rangle)/\sqrt2$. They are respectively <CP> even and odd. Reversing the flavor-exchange sign reverses which written sum is even; it does not change the <CP> eigenvalues. With <CP violation>, the physical short- and long-lived states need not equal these <CP eigenstates>; <kaon CP mixing parameter> and <neutral-kaon CP impurity and two-pion decay> describe the mixtures.

= Kaon CP mixing parameter
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$\epsilon=\frac{\sqrt{R_{12}}-\sqrt{R_{21}}}{\sqrt{R_{12}}+\sqrt{R_{21}}}$}

In the flavor convention $K_1=(K^0-\bar K^0)/\sqrt2$ and $K_2=(K^0+\bar K^0)/\sqrt2$, the parameter $\epsilon=(a-b)/(a+b)$ writes normalized <mass> states as $(K_1+\epsilon K_2)/\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}$ and $(K_2+\epsilon K_1)/\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}$. In the Hermitian <mass>-only approximation with correlated roots, $\epsilon=i\tan(\varphi/2)$ and the states are <orthogonal>. For the full non-Hermitian decay matrix the real part need not vanish. The parameter depends on the flavor-state phase convention; observable <CP violation> involves convention-invariant combinations with decay amplitudes.

= Exchange-positive CP convention for neutral kaon mixing
{parent=Kaon CP mixing parameter}
{title2=$|K_\pm\rangle=\frac{|K_{1,2}\rangle+\epsilon|K_{2,1}\rangle}{\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}}$}

In the phase convention $CP|K^0\rangle=|\bar K^0\rangle$, the symmetric flavor combination is CP even and the antisymmetric one CP odd. For an equal-diagonal effective matrix with entries b,c off diagonal, choose correlated roots r,s with $r^2=b$, $s^2=c$ and eigenvalues $a\pm rs$. Its eigenvectors are $(r,\pm s)^T$. Writing $\epsilon=(r-s)/(r+s)$ gives the displayed CP-basis mixtures, up to an overall phase. Their deviation is small only if $|\epsilon|\ll1$; noninvariance alone does not guarantee that. This convention must not be mixed with one in which flavor exchange carries a minus sign.

= Neutral-kaon mass matrix diagonalization
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$m_{S,L}=r_0\mp|z|$}

With <CPT symmetry>, a Hermitian <kaon> <mass matrix> is $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r_0&z\\z^*&r_0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its <eigenvalues> are $r_0\pm|z|$. For correlated roots $a^2=z$, $b^2=z^*$ and $ab=|z|$, the normalized lower and upper <eigenvectors> are $(a,-b)/\sqrt{2|z|}$ and $(a,b)/\sqrt{2|z|}$. They are <orthogonal>. This is a <mass>-only result; including decay requires the generally non-Hermitian effective matrix $M-i\Gamma/2$.

= Kaon mixing square-root branch convention
{parent=Neutral-kaon mass matrix diagonalization}
{title2=$\sqrt z\sqrt{z^*}=|z|$}

In a Hermitian neutral-<kaon> <mass matrix>, independently choosing square-root signs of $z$ and $z^*$ can interchange the <eigenvalue> labels. Choose $z=re^{i\varphi}$, $a=\sqrt r e^{i\varphi/2}$ and $b=\sqrt r e^{-i\varphi/2}$, so $ab=r>0$, continuously from the desired <CP>-conserving limit. Then $(a,-b)$ is the lower-<mass> <eigenvector>. At a degenerate off-diagonal zero the <mass> eigenbasis is not fixed by diagonalization alone.

= Charged weak box contribution to kaon mixing
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\lambda_jF(m_i,m_j)$}

A <Standard Model> charged-current <box diagram> converts $d\bar s$ into $s\bar d$. The amplitude is of order $g^4$ and second order in the <weak interaction>.

= Omega baryon discovery experiment
{c}
{parent=Omega baryon}

The 1964 report exposed Brookhaven's 80-inch hydrogen <bubble chamber> to a mass-separated $5.0\,\mathrm{GeV}/c$ negative-<kaon> beam. A photographed decay chain, track <momenta> and bubble density supported the <particle identification>. Two converted <photons> helped reconstruct a neutral <pion>. Alternative assignments were tested kinematically. The authors reported an <omega baryon> candidate with $Q=-1$, $S=-3$ and <mass> $1686\pm12\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, reserving a detailed systematic-error discussion for further events.<Observation of a Hyperon with Strangeness Minus Three by V. E. Barnes>[Original Barnes et al. letter, Phys.Rev.Lett.12(1964), pp.204–206: Fig.2, TableI and final identification]{ref}

This is an inference from several linked observations. A <charged particle> can leave a visible <bubble-chamber track>, while a neutral segment must be reconstructed from its production and decay products. Agreement between possible <decay channels>, <electric charge>, <momentum conservation> and the reconstructed <mass> is stronger evidence than a suggestive track shape alone. The event tests a predicted <hadron> pattern; it does not photograph the three <strange quarks> individually.

For example, an independently constructed two-body kinematic check uses
$$
M^2c^4=(E_1+E_2)^2-c^2|\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2|^2.
$$
In a parent rest frame with equal and opposite daughter <momenta>, the momentum sum vanishes, giving $Mc^2=E_1+E_2$. In units $c=1$, stipulated daughter <masses> $m_1=1$, $m_2=1/4$ and momentum magnitude $p=3/4$ give $E_1=5/4$, $E_2=\sqrt{10}/4$ and $M=(5+\sqrt{10})/4\simeq2.04057$. A common <Lorentz boost> changes the daughter <energies> and <momenta> while preserving this reconstructed <invariant mass>. These chosen units and numbers are an illustration, not a reanalysis of the 1964 event.

= Pentaquark
{parent=Baryon}
{title2=$qqqq\bar q$}

A <baryon> with constituent content four <quarks> and one <antiquark>, whose net <baryon number> is one. Such content admits more flavour representations than three quarks alone. A representation in the tensor product is not evidence by itself of a bound state.

= Light pentaquark strong-decay channels
{parent=Pentaquark}

Exotic flavour weights can decay into a <baryon> and a <meson> while preserving every strong quantum number: $uudd\bar s\to pK^0$ or $nK^+$, $ddss\bar u\to\Xi^-\pi^-$, and $uuss\bar d\to\Xi^0\pi^+$. These are quark rearrangements. If above threshold with unsuppressed couplings, such states are short-lived strong resonances. Flavour symmetry alone fixes neither masses nor widths.

= Pentaquark antidecuplet
{parent=Pentaquark}
{title2=$\overline{\mathbf{10}}$}

Two antisymmetric flavour quark pairs can each transform in $\overline{\mathbf3}$, combine into $\overline{\mathbf6}$, and with an <antiquark> give $\overline{\mathbf6}\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}=\overline{\mathbf{10}}\oplus\mathbf8$. The antidecuplet rows are $(Y,I)=(2,0),(1,1/2),(0,1),(-1,3/2)$. Exactly three weights are absent from all three-quark states: $(I_3,Y)=(0,2)$ and $(\pm3/2,-1)$.

= Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets
{c}
{parent=Baryon}

In a <three-quark colour singlet>, a symmetric spatial state requires symmetric spin-flavour symmetry. Completely symmetric decuplet flavour couples to symmetric spin $3/2$; mixed octet flavour couples to mixed spin $1/2$ in the symmetric permutation combination. Antisymmetric singlet flavour cannot have a symmetric S-wave orbital state, since $\bigwedge^3\mathbb C^2=0$ forbids a completely antisymmetric state of three spin-$1/2$ quarks. Mixed orbital and spin states can instead realize the required antisymmetry.

= Light-diquark spin-isospin correlation
{parent=Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets}
{title2=$(I,S_{ud})=(0,0)\text{ or }(1,1)$}

Two light <quarks> in a symmetric spatial state and the antisymmetric colour pair inside a <three-quark colour singlet> require symmetric spin-flavour symmetry. Their <isospin> singlet and spin singlet are both antisymmetric, while their respective triplets are symmetric. Thus the allowed pairs have $(I,S_{ud})=(0,0)$ or $(1,1)$; opposite exchange parities are excluded. Coupling an isospin-zero spin-one-half <quark> gives an $I=0,J=1/2$ <baryon> or $I=1,J=1/2,3/2$ <baryons>.

= Baryons with one isospin-singlet quark
{parent=Light-diquark spin-isospin correlation}
{title2=$Q(h[ud])=q_h+1/3$}

For an <isospin> singlet <quark> $h$ of charge $q_h$, the antisymmetric light-flavour pair gives an $I=0,J=1/2$ state with charge $q_h+1/3$. The symmetric light-flavour pairs $uu,\{ud\},dd$ give $I=1$ triplets of spins $1/2$ and $3/2$, with charges $q_h+4/3,q_h+1/3,q_h-2/3$. These follow from <light-diquark spin-isospin correlation> and addition of constituent <electric charges>, with zero <orbital angular momentum>.

= Symmetric spin-flavour SU6 representation
{parent=Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets}
{title2=$\mathbf{56}=(\mathbf{10},\mathbf4)\oplus(\mathbf8,\mathbf2)$}

Three light <quarks> have a six-dimensional single-quark spin-flavour space $\mathbb C^3\otimes\mathbb C^2$. A symmetric spatial ground state and the antisymmetric <three-quark colour singlet> require the symmetric cube of that space. Its dimension is $\binom83=56$, and restriction to flavour $SU(3)$ and spin $SU(2)$ gives a <baryon decuplet> of spin $3/2$ and a <baryon octet> of spin $1/2$. The two mixed-permutation multiplicity spaces pair once in the symmetric sector, explaining why the two flavour-octet copies in the unconstrained <tensor> cube do not represent two distinct ground-state octets.

= Three-quark colour singlet
{parent=Baryon}
{title2=$|1_c\rangle=\epsilon_{abc}|abc\rangle/\sqrt6$}

= Three-quark color singlet
{synonym}

The invariant colour state of three fundamental <quarks> is the normalized completely antisymmetric tensor $\epsilon_{abc}/\sqrt6$. With this colour factor, the <Pauli exclusion principle> requires the remaining spatial-spin-flavour wavefunction to be symmetric. Colour and approximate <flavour symmetry> are distinct SU(3) actions.

= Flavour singlet of three scalar quarks
{parent=Three-quark colour singlet}

If three <quarks> are hypothetically spin-zero <bosons>, their total state is symmetric under particle exchange. In an $L=0$ symmetric spatial state, the unchanged antisymmetric <three-quark colour singlet> therefore requires an antisymmetric <quark flavour> state. With three flavours its space is $\bigwedge^3\mathbb C^3$, of dimension one. The sole flavour state contains one quark of each flavour and has total <angular momentum> zero.

= Identical-quark pair spin in a ground-state baryon
{parent=Three-quark colour singlet}
{title2=$S_{qq}=1$}

For an identical-flavour pair in the symmetric spatial ground state of a <baryon>, the flavour factor is symmetric and the <three-quark colour singlet> is antisymmetric under their exchange. The <Pauli exclusion principle> therefore requires a symmetric pair <spin> factor. Two spin-one-half <quarks> then form a <triplet state>, $S_{qq}=1$, rather than a spin singlet. The conclusion applies to the identical pair in both spin-one-half and spin-three-half ground-state baryons.

= Wigner crystal
{c}
{parent=Electron gas}
{wiki}

A Wigner crystal is an ordered arrangement of electrons stabilized by their mutual repulsion when localization costs sufficiently little kinetic or thermal energy. In a degenerate low-density gas, the <Coulomb-to-kinetic energy ratio of an electron gas> increases with mean spacing. Classical electrons can also order at large Coulomb-to-thermal coupling, as in <electron crystallization on liquid helium>. A neutralizing background or confining environment is needed to discuss a stable electron assembly. Dimension, screening and disorder affect the transition.

= Electron crystallization on liquid helium
{parent=Wigner crystal}

The 1979 experiment of <C. C. Grimes> and colleagues studied a two-dimensional sheet of electrons above liquid helium. It reported crystallization at densities $3\times10^8$ to $9\times10^8\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$, with melting temperatures $0.35$ to $0.65\,\mathrm K$. The sheet was classical: Coulomb energy competed with thermal motion, unlike the kinetic-energy comparison used for a zero-temperature degenerate <Wigner crystal>. It supplies evidence for electron ordering while keeping these two physical regimes distinct.<Evidence for a Liquid-to-Crystal Phase Transition in a Classical, Two-Dimensional Sheet of Electrons by C. C. Grimes and others>[Physical Review Letters 42, pp. 795–798, abstract: classical sheet and reported density and melting-temperature intervals]{ref}

= Coulomb-to-kinetic energy ratio of an electron gas
{c}
{parent=Wigner crystal}
{title2=$E_C/E_F\sim r_s$}

In three dimensions, let $a=(3/4\pi n)^{1/3}$ be the mean-spacing radius and $a_B^*=4\pi\varepsilon\hbar^2/(m^*e^2)$ the effective Bohr radius. The Coulomb scale is $E_C\sim e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon a)$ and the <Fermi energy> scale is $E_F\sim\hbar^2/(m^*a^2)$, so $E_C/E_F\sim a/a_B^*=r_s$. Decreasing density favors repulsion relative to delocalization energy, motivating a <Wigner crystal>. This scaling does not determine the numerical quantum melting value of $r_s$. A classical nondegenerate gas instead compares Coulomb energy with $k_BT$.

= Reflection symmetry
{parent=Symmetry (physics)}

Invariance under a spatial <reflection>. A one-dimensional <quantum Hamiltonian> invariant under $x\mapsto a-x$ admits <energy eigenfunctions> of definite even or odd midpoint <parity>. Even and odd functions are <orthogonal> on a reflection-symmetric interval.

A plane through which reflection leaves an object unchanged. In a molecular skeleton, sites exchanged by a symmetry preserving all substituent identities can be chemically equivalent in an achiral <NMR spectroscopy> experiment.

A point such that replacing every position $\mathbf r$ relative to it by $-\mathbf r$ leaves the structure unchanged. Molecular orbitals can then be classified as $g$ or $u$ according to their <parity>.

= Translation symmetry
{synonym}

Invariance of an action, equation or configuration under a <translation>. For a continuous spatial translation symmetry, <Noether's theorem> supplies the conserved <momentum>; spacetime translation gives the <canonical stress-energy tensor>.

= Discrete symmetry
{parent=Symmetry (physics)}
{wiki}

A discrete symmetry has transformations separated from the identity rather than connected to it by continuously varying symmetry parameters.

= Continuous symmetry
{parent=Symmetry (physics)}
{wiki}

A continuous symmetry belongs to a group whose parameters can vary continuously. Spontaneous breaking of such a symmetry can produce <Goldstone bosons>.

= Helical symmetry
{parent=Continuous symmetry}
{title2=$(\theta,z)\mapsto(\theta+\epsilon,z+b\epsilon)$}

A <helical symmetry> combines an axial <rotation> and a proportional axial <translation>. A <helix> with $z=b\theta$ is unchanged as a set by this transformation. If a mechanical <Lagrangian> is invariant, the <Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry> is $L_z+bP_z$, where the two momenta include all moving components. Pure axial <rotation> and pure axial <translation> need not separately be <symmetries>. The <helical symmetry of a potential> is a related special case in which the constraint is replaced by an invariant potential.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[online chapter 2, §2.4.1, conserved momentum for a continuous coordinate transformation]{ref}

= Signed-area displacement under a helical constraint
{parent=Helical symmetry}
{title2=$\Delta z=-2A/b$}

If zero conserved helical momentum reduces to $r^2\dot\theta+b\dot z=0$, a closed transverse projection $C$ has axial displacement $\Delta z=-b^{-1}\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx)=-2A/b$. Here $b\ne0$ and $A$ is the <signed area>, with winding multiplicity for self-intersections. This is a path-dependent displacement even when the transverse coordinates return to their initial values. It follows from the momentum constraint and <Green's theorem>; it is not a prediction that every chosen closed curve is dynamically realizable.

= Translation invariance
{parent=Continuous symmetry}

Translation invariance means that shifting every position by the same constant vector leaves the system unchanged. It prevents an isolated interface or localized solution in a homogeneous infinite domain from having a preferred absolute position.

= Molecular beam
{parent=Experimental apparatus}
{wiki}

A directed stream of <molecules> used to prepare samples for <spectroscopy>.

A <molecular beam> also includes a stream of <chemical atoms>, then called an <atomic beam>. The <effusive beam speed distribution> explains which particles leave the source, while <gravity deflection of an atomic beam> explains how that distribution becomes a pattern on a detector.

= Effusive beam speed distribution
{parent=Molecular beam}
{title2=$g(v)=vf(v)/\langle v\rangle$}

Which <speed distribution> does an arrival counter measure? Suppose an equilibrium, nonrelativistic <ideal gas> escapes by <effusion> through a thin opening into <vacuum>. The opening is small compared with the <mean free path>; the reservoir remains at <temperature> $T$. Let $f(v)$ denote its normalized <Maxwell speed distribution>, with <mass> $m$ and $a=m/(2k_BT)$:
$$
f(v)=\frac{4a^{3/2}}{\sqrt\pi}v^2e^{-av^2},\qquad v>0.
$$
At a given direction toward the opening, the number crossing per unit time is proportional to the normal component $v\cos\theta$. Integrating over a fixed accepted cone contributes an angular factor independent of $v$. Thus the normalized <probability density function> for speeds among arriving particles is
$$
\boxed{g(v)=\frac{vf(v)}{\langle v\rangle}=2a^2v^3e^{-av^2}}.
$$
Its <integral> is one because $\int_0^\infty v^3e^{-av^2}\,dv=1/(2a^2)$. The reservoir <mode> is $v=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}$; the arrival <mode> is $\sqrt{3k_BT/m}$. Faster particles get more opportunities to cross the opening. This is a selection effect, not heating after emission.

Specify the observable before applying the formula. An instantaneous count in a fixed segment of the beam weights the arrival flux by the time spent there, proportional to $1/v$; in this ideal model its normalized speed law returns to $f(v)$. A detector's speed-dependent efficiency, source <collisions> or a velocity selector changes the arriving law again. **Counting arrivals and counting particles currently present are different measurements.**

= Rotating-wheel molecular velocity selector
{parent=Molecular beam}

<Otto Stern>'s lecture describes two co-rotating toothed wheels: transit between them must match an advance between openings. He explicitly distinguishes this method from the first historical speed measurement.<The Method of Molecular Rays by Otto Stern>[The method of molecular rays, lecture 12 December 1946, p. 9: rotating-wheel timing, transmission and limited accuracy]{ref}

For an idealized example, let aligned wheels have $N$ equally spaced openings, separation $L$, and rotation rate $f$ in revolutions per second. A particle with constant longitudinal <speed> $v$ takes $L/v$ between them. The second wheel advances through $fL/v$ revolutions; transmission peaks when that advance is an integer number $k$ of opening pitches:
$$
\boxed{v_k=\frac{NfL}{k}},\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.
$$
With chosen $N=60$, $f=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $L=0.080\,\mathrm m$, the first two orders select $240$ and $120\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. A second transmission maximum therefore need not represent a second source <temperature>. Finite opening widths admit speed intervals around each order, and an additional measurement is needed to distinguish orders. The ideal expression assumes negligible <acceleration> and constant rotation rate during a flight, with a fixed relative phase between the wheels.

= Gravity deflection of an atomic beam
{parent=Molecular beam}
{title2=$y=gL^2/(2v^2)$}

<Immanuel Estermann>, <O. C. Simpson> and <Otto Stern> used gravitational deflection to measure atomic speeds. Stern describes scanning a hot-wire <surface ionization detector> through a <cesium> beam and recording <ion> <electric current>.<The Method of Molecular Rays by Otto Stern>[The method of molecular rays, lecture 12 December 1946, pp. 9–11: collaborators, falling beam and ion-current readout]{ref} Their 1947 paper reports <potassium> and <cesium> beams; a deficit of slow arrivals increased with oven <pressure>, supporting a source-slit <collision> explanation.<The Free Fall of Atoms and the Measurement of the Velocity Distribution in a Molecular Beam of Cesium Atoms by Immanuel Estermann, Oliver Cecil Simpson, and Otto Stern>[Estermann, Simpson and Stern, Physical Review 71 (15 February 1947), p. 238, abstract: gravity deflection, modified Maxwellian distribution and pressure-dependent slow-atom deficit]{ref}

First consider an original point-launch model, with horizontal <speed> $v>0$, zero initial vertical <velocity>, flight distance $L$, and uniform <gravitational acceleration> $g$. Downward displacement $y$ follows from flight time $t=L/v$:
$$
y=\frac{gL^2}{2v^2}=\frac{C}{v^2},\qquad C=\frac{gL^2}{2}.
$$
Slow particles fall farther. However, a detector scan counts arrivals per interval of height, not per interval of <speed>. <Change of variables for probability densities> supplies the <Jacobian determinant>:
$$
p_y(y)=g\!\left(\sqrt{C/y}\right)\frac{\sqrt C}{2y^{3/2}}
=\frac{b^2}{y^3}e^{-b/y},\qquad b=aC=\frac{mgL^2}{4k_BT},\qquad y>0.
$$
Here $g(v)$ denotes the <effusive beam speed distribution>; the unadorned constant $g$ in $C$ denotes <gravitational acceleration>. The <integral> of $p_y$ is one: substituting $u=b/y$ reduces it to $\int_0^\infty ue^{-u}\,du=1$. Its <mode> is $y=b/3$. Simply mapping the most probable arrival <speed> into a fall distance instead gives $2b/3$. The peaks differ because equal height bins cover unequal speed intervals.

\Image[effusive-beam-speeds-and-gravity-profile.png]
{title=<Effusion> changes the arrival speed distribution, and a detector-height scan requires another <Jacobian determinant>}
{description=Original calculated distributions with chosen $m=133\,\mathrm u$, $T=500\,\mathrm K$, $L=1.50\,\mathrm m$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. These are not historical measurements.}

For those chosen parameters, $b\simeq176.54\,\mu\mathrm m$, so the detector-height <probability density> peaks near $58.85\,\mu\mathrm m$. A detector covering only $0<y<Y$ accepts the fraction
$$
F(Y)=\int_0^Yp_y(y)\,dy=e^{-b/Y}\left(1+\frac bY\right).
$$
At $Y=0.80\,\mathrm{mm}$ this is about $97.9\%$, even before allowing for imperfect detection. Discarding the remainder changes the observed normalization.

Real slits transmit a range of launch heights and vertical <velocities>. Their geometrical response must be combined with the gravitational deflection and detector width; the point-launch formula is not an exact reduction of the historical apparatus. In the point-launch model, an initial vertical <velocity> $w$ would add $wL/v$ to $y$. Source <collisions>, detector efficiency and alignment can also distort a profile. Varying oven <pressure> helps distinguish such source losses from an incorrect thermal distribution; inferring a source <temperature> from the peak alone ignores both selection and the detector's coordinate transformation.

= Charge density wave
{parent=Condensed matter physics}
{wiki}
{title2=$\rho(x)=\rho_0+\rho_1\cos(Qx+\varphi)$}

A periodic modulation of electronic <charge density>, often coupled to a lattice distortion. In a one-dimensional metal, a <Peierls distortion> with $Q=2k_F$ can open a gap at opposite <Fermi points>, lowering the occupied electron <energy> at an elastic energy cost. A charge modulation and a <spin density wave> are distinct: the latter can modulate spin while leaving the total charge nearly uniform.

= Spin density wave
{parent=Condensed matter physics}
{title2=$\mathbf S(x)=\mathbf S_0\cos(Qx+\varphi)$}

A spatially modulated spin density, distinct from a <charge density wave>. Opposite modulations of spin-up and spin-down occupations can give a periodically varying <magnetization> while their total <charge density> stays uniform.

= Electron-gas correlation energy
{parent=Electron correlation}

The exact ground-state <energy> minus the minimum Hartree-Fock <energy> for the same Hamiltonian and variational constraints. It is nonpositive when compared with the unrestricted variational minimum. Correlations beyond a single determinant can substantially change phase boundaries at low density.

= Dissociation error of a doubly occupied bonding orbital
{parent=Electron correlation}

A normalized product of two identical bonding orbitals built from separated orthogonal centers has finite weight in configurations with both <electrons> on one center. Repulsion penalizes that ionic weight even when hopping disappears. Mixing configurations can instead approach one <electron> per center while preserving the required <spin> and exchange symmetries.

= Dynamic electron correlation
{parent=Electron correlation}

The correlated instantaneous avoidance of <electrons> beyond a single <Slater determinant>. It can remain important even when one configuration dominates, as for an isolated closed-shell helium atom; it is distinct from the near-degeneracy problem of <static electron correlation>.

= Static electron correlation
{parent=Electron correlation}

<Electron correlation> caused by the need for multiple important configurations, often when several <molecular orbitals> are nearly degenerate. Stretching a homonuclear two-electron bond makes a single doubly occupied orbital retain unphysical ionic contributions; a suitable combination of configurations cancels them.

= Heavy fermion material
{parent=Electron correlation}
{wiki}

= Heavy fermion
{synonym}

A strongly correlated material whose low-energy electronic <quasiparticles> have a very large thermodynamic <effective mass>. An enhanced <electronic heat capacity coefficient>, spin susceptibility and quantum-oscillation <cyclotron effective mass> are complementary signatures; they need not all be related by a single isotropic band mass. A <Kondo lattice> is an important setting for such behaviour. In <CeAl3>, low-temperature specific heat and quadratic resistivity provided an early quantitative example.<4f-Virtual-Bound-State Formation in CeAl3 at Low Temperatures by Klaus Andres and others>[Abstract: low-temperature electronic heat capacity and electrical resistivity]{ref}

= Electronic heat capacity of a heavy Fermi liquid
{parent=Heavy fermion material}

For <quasiparticles> with <density of states> $g(E_F)$ including both spins, the leading <electronic heat capacity> is $C=\gamma T$ with $\gamma=\pi^2k_B^2g(E_F)/3$. A narrow heavy band makes this coefficient large. The <density of states> and heat capacity must use consistent extensive, molar or volumetric normalization.

= Low-temperature calorimetry and resistivity of CeAl3
{parent=Heavy fermion material}

The 1975 measurements of <CeAl3> below $0.2\,\mathrm K$ found an <electronic heat capacity coefficient> of $1.62\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}$ and a quadratic electrical-resistivity coefficient of $35\,\mathrm{\mu\Omega\,cm\,K^{-2}}$. These are complementary thermal and transport signatures of an unusually dense low-energy electronic spectrum.<4f-Virtual-Bound-State Formation in CeAl3 at Low Temperatures by Klaus Andres and others>[Abstract: the two measured coefficients and low-temperature range]{ref} A temperature sweep and a $C/T$ versus $T^2$ fit separate the electronic intercept from a Debye lattice term; the transport comparison tests a <Fermi liquid> temperature law.

= Kondo effect
{c}
{parent=Electron correlation}
{wiki}

Antiferromagnetic exchange between a localized magnetic moment and conduction <electrons> produces strong low-energy correlations and screening of the moment. In a dilute magnetic-impurity metal, a resistance minimum and a low-temperature logarithmic increase are characteristic signatures over an appropriate temperature range. The strong-coupling low-temperature regime cannot be obtained by extending the logarithm indefinitely.

= Kondo lattice
{c}
{parent=Kondo effect}

A periodic array of local moments coupled to conduction <electrons>. Competition between screening, coherent hybridization and intersite magnetic interactions can produce a <heavy fermion material>, magnetic order or a correlated insulating state.

= Kondo-lattice coherence temperature
{parent=Kondo lattice}
{c}

A <Kondo lattice> develops coherent low-energy <quasiparticle> bands below a material-dependent coherence scale. This lattice scale need not equal the single-impurity Kondo screening scale. Coherence can produce a heavy <Fermi liquid> with narrow bands, large <electronic heat capacity> and low-<temperature> quadratic resistivity.

= Synthetic fluid membrane
{parent=Soft matter}

A <synthetic fluid membrane> is a thin self-assembled sheet of synthetic <amphiphiles>, fluid under static in-plane shear while resisting <area> changes and bending. It provides a nonbiological realization of a two-dimensional fluid interface with <curvature> <elasticity>. A <Helfrich energy> describes long-wavelength shape costs; its material parameters and any <spontaneous curvature> depend on composition and conditions. Lateral fluidity does not eliminate <membrane bending rigidity> or <membrane tension>. The <membrane fluctuation spectrum under tension> separates bending-dominated and tension-dominated <length> scales.
