= Physics
{wiki}

Physics investigates matter, motion, and interactions by building <mathematical models> and testing their consequences against observations. A <physical law> supplies a relation; a model specifies the objects, conditions, and approximations used to apply it. <Classical mechanics>, <electromagnetism>, <thermodynamics>, and <quantum mechanics> organize different aspects of these investigations. Their usefulness comes from turning measurements into predictions that can be checked and refined.

Consider a <simple pendulum>: how does its <oscillation period> change when its <length> changes? For small <amplitude>, the ideal model gives $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$, where $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>. Taking $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ as the model parameter, a one-metre pendulum has predicted period $2.0061\,\mathrm s$. Increasing its <length> to four metres gives $4.0121\,\mathrm s$: quadrupling the length doubles the period. The point mass cancels from the equation, so changing it alone does not change this model's answer.

The prediction identifies a mechanism and a way to challenge it. The <small-angle approximation> replaces $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$; the full ideal equation predicts a longer period at large <amplitude>. At $60^\circ$, its period is about $1.07318T_0$, already a $7.3\%$ change. Measurements at such amplitudes can reveal the limit of the approximation, while appreciable drag requires a <damped pendulum>. These distinctions are developed in <simple pendulum> and <dimensional analysis>. The small-amplitude model is also derived in \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-4-pendulums][Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums]{ref}.

\Image[pendulum-period-scaling.png]
{title=Pendulum predictions connect a measurable scaling law with the limit of a small-angle approximation}

Several different experiments can become one calculation. <Nondimensionalization> rewrites the pendulum equation using $\tau=t\sqrt{g/\ell}$, leaving $d^2\theta/d\tau^2+\sin\theta=0$. All lengths share that dimensionless equation; the <amplitude> remains a parameter. Similarly, <conservation laws> and <symmetry> reveal features that many models share. This is one source of the subject's beauty: a compact relation can organize a large range of phenomena.

The connection to evidence remains essential when a model is elegant. In his introductory lectures, <Richard Feynman> emphasizes testing proposed relations by experiment and using idealizations whose accuracy depends on the conditions. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_01.html][Feynman, Leighton and Sands, Atoms in motion, section 1–1]{ref} A discrepancy can motivate a better measurement, a better approximation, or a different physical model; the mathematical calculation and the empirical assessment are both part of the explanation.

= Physical law
{parent=Physics}

A physical law expresses a reproducible relation used to describe or predict physical phenomena within its domain of applicability. Applying it requires a model specifying the system and conditions: <Newton's second law>, together with a <force> model, predicts a body's <acceleration>; <conservation of energy> constrains exchanges within a specified system. Its predictions must be compared with physical observations.

= Plasma
{synonym}

A <plasma> is a gas of mobile charged particles whose collective electromagnetic response is important to its dynamics. It commonly contains <ions> and <electrons>, and may also contain a neutral component: complete <ionization> is not required. A multi-species fluid description has <current density> $\mathbf J=\sum_s q_s n_s\mathbf u_s$. The relative motion of the species can matter even when a single-fluid <magnetohydrodynamics> approximation describes the large-scale dynamics.

= Plasma frequency
{parent=Plasma (Physics)}
{title2=$\omega_{ps}=\sqrt{n_s q_s^2/(\epsilon_0m_s)}$}

The plasma frequency is the characteristic electrostatic oscillation frequency of a charged species displaced against a neutralizing background. For number density $n_s$, charge $q_s$ and mass $m_s$, the displayed formula uses SI units; in <Gaussian units> it is $\omega_{ps}=\sqrt{4\pi n_sq_s^2/m_s}$. The <ion skin depth> is $c/\omega_{pi}$ for the ion species. Different species have different plasma frequencies.

= Ion skin depth
{parent=Plasma (Physics)}
{title2=$d_i=c/\omega_{pi}$}

For a singly charged ion species in <Gaussian units>, $\omega_{pi}=(4\pi ne^2/m_i)^{1/2}$ is its <plasma frequency> and $d_i=c\sqrt{m_i/(4\pi ne^2)}$. The ion skin depth marks a characteristic scale below which ion and electron motion can separate in a magnetized <plasma>. Together with the <Alfvén speed>, it satisfies $v_Ad_i=cB_0/(4\pi en)$, the coefficient of guide-field propagation in <electron magnetohydrodynamics>.

= Mole
{synonym}

The mole is the <SI> base unit of <amount of substance>. One mole contains exactly $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ specified entities, as fixed by the <Avogadro constant>. It counts entities rather than a fixed <mass> or volume. \a[https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole][BIPM, SI base unit: mole]{ref}

= Amount of substance
{parent=Physical quantity}
{wiki}
{title2=$n$}

Amount of substance measures a count of specified entities, such as <chemical atoms>, <molecules>, <ions>, or <electrons>, using the <mole> as its <SI> base unit. It differs from <mass>: equal amounts of substance of different <chemical substances> contain equal entity counts but generally have different <masses>. If $N$ entities are present, $n=N/N_{\mathrm A}$, where $N_{\mathrm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant>. The specified entity matters: one <mole> of <water> <molecules> contains two <moles> of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>. \a[https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/mole][BIPM, definition of amount of substance and the mole]{ref}

= Dimensionless
{synonym}

A dimensionless quantity has zero exponent for every base physical dimension. It can be formed by dividing two quantities with the same dimensions, such as $x=r/R$ or $q=m/M$. Its value is independent of the choice of units for those dimensions. The quantities $GM^2/R$ and $Lt$ both have dimensions of energy, so $RLt/(GM^2)$ is dimensionless. A <dimensionless variable> is such a quantity used as an unknown or independent variable in an equation.

= Philosophy of physics
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

<Philosophy of physics> studies the interpretation and explanatory structure of physical theories, including the relation between mathematical models, observable phenomena and claims about what exists. Its questions include <theoretical reduction>, <emergence> and competing formulations of physical locality.

= Stochastic Einstein locality
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

A formulation of <stochastic Einstein locality> restricts an event's objective chance to its relevant causal past, rather than spacelike-separated interventions or facts. Different versions constrain either marginal chances or joint conditional probabilities. A one-wing marginal condition alone does not supply <outcome independence>. To derive a <Bell theorem> by this route, the proposed locality assumptions must include the appropriate screening-off relation for the two outcomes, together with a common setting-independent past distribution.

= Reichenbach common cause principle
{c}
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

= Common cause principle
{synonym}

A classical common-cause explanation seeks past variables which screen off a correlation: conditioned on a sufficiently complete common cause, the correlated events become independent. A <Bell local causality> model adds local dependence on settings and a single compatible past-variable distribution for all measurement contexts. Providing a different setting-dependent screening variable for each pair of settings does not establish that stronger Bell model. The distinction between a causal mechanism and a purely probabilistic screening construction matters when interpreting this principle.

= Emergence
{parent=Philosophy of physics}
{wiki}

<Emergence> concerns novel collective patterns or explanatory structures relative to a lower-level description. Robust effective behaviour can emerge through coarse-graining or singular limits while remaining derivable from the microscopic theory. The stronger claim that genuinely new causal powers cannot be reduced to that theory requires an additional argument; nonanalyticity of a limiting function alone does not establish it.

= Theoretical reduction
{parent=Philosophy of physics}

<Theoretical reduction> relates theories by deriving the laws or predictions of one from another together with explicit identifications of quantities and appropriate domain assumptions. A reduction can require approximation, coarse-graining or a limiting construction. Deduction of an ideal limiting model is different from exact deduction of the same properties for every finite member of a model sequence.

= Nuclear physics
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

The study of <atomic nuclei>, their structure, reactions and interactions. <Nuclear forces> between <nucleons> and <nuclear binding energies> connect it to the low-energy consequences of <quantum chromodynamics>.

= Mössbauer effect
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{c}
{wiki}

A nucleus bound in a solid can emit or absorb a <photon> without exciting lattice vibrations, transferring momentum to the solid as a whole. The resulting recoil energy is negligible compared with the isolated-nucleus recoil, making very narrow resonant emission and absorption lines observable. The <Pound-Rebka experiment> uses this effect to resolve the small frequency shift from <gravitational redshift>.

= Nuclear statistical equilibrium
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}
{title2=NSE}

At sufficiently high temperature and density, forward and inverse nuclear reactions can balance so that abundances are determined by thermodynamic conditions, nuclear binding and conserved quantities. <Silicon burning> approaches such an equilibrium among iron-group nuclei. Weak reactions can separately change the electron fraction, so the favored mixture need not be pure <iron>.

= Nickel–iron nuclear equilibrium ratio
{parent=Nuclear statistical equilibrium}
{title2=$n_{56}/n_{54}=K n_p^2T^{-3}e^{\Delta B/(kT)}$}

Nickel-56 and iron-54 have the same <neutron> number and differ by two <protons>. Dividing their <nuclear statistical equilibrium> abundance formulas cancels the <neutron> density and leaves the displayed ratio, with $\Delta B=B_{56}-B_{54}$ and $K$ containing temperature-independent statistical and <mass> factors in the simplified model. For material with equal total <proton> and <neutron> numbers and only these nuclei plus free <protons>, charge balance gives $n_p=2n_{54}$. Thus $n_{56}/n_{54}^3\propto T^{-3}e^{\Delta B/(kT)}$. This has a finite <temperature> maximum only if the signed energy difference is negative; conventional positive total nuclear binding energies for these two actual isotopes instead give a positive difference.

= Electron fraction in stellar matter
{parent=Nuclear statistical equilibrium}
{title2=$Y_e=\sum_i X_i Z_i/A_i$}

For electrically neutral fully ionized matter, $Y_e$ is the number of electrons, or total <protons>, per baryon. With isotope <mass> fractions $X_i$, charge numbers $Z_i$ and <mass> numbers $A_i$, the displayed sum neglects small nuclear mass-defect corrections. Strong and electromagnetic reactions preserve it, while <electron capture> and <beta-plus decay> reduce it. The proton-to-neutron number ratio is $Y_e/(1-Y_e)$.

= Neutron excess
{parent=Electron fraction in stellar matter}
{title2=$\eta=1-2Y_e$}

The <neutron excess> is the number of <neutrons> minus <protons> per baryon. A mixture of carbon-12 and oxygen-16 has zero <neutron excess>, while neon-22 contributes $2/22$ per baryon in that isotope. Thus a neon-22 <mass> fraction $X_{22}$ gives $\eta=X_{22}/11$. A nonzero excess shifts iron-group ashes from symmetric nickel-56 toward neutron-rich isotopes, even before additional weak reactions occur.

= Neutron capture
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}
{title2=$^{A}_{Z}X+n\longrightarrow{}^{A+1}_{Z}X+\gamma$}

Capture of a neutron increases mass number without immediately changing atomic number. It avoids the charged-particle <Coulomb barrier>. Subsequent <beta-minus decay> can increase atomic number. Whether capture is slow or fast compared with relevant beta decays distinguishes the <slow neutron-capture process> from the <rapid neutron-capture process>.

= Nucleosynthesis
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

<Nucleosynthesis> is the production of <atomic nuclei> through <nuclear reactions>. It includes <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>, <stellar nucleosynthesis> and production in explosive or compact-remnant environments. Making nuclei and delivering them to the <interstellar medium> are separate steps in chemical enrichment.

= Stellar nucleosynthesis
{parent=Nucleosynthesis}
{wiki}

<Stellar nucleosynthesis> encompasses nuclear production during stellar evolution and explosions, including <hydrogen burning>, <core helium burning>, advanced burning and neutron-capture processes. Stellar mass, composition, mixing and mass loss govern which nuclei form and which eventually escape. <Slow neutron-capture process> and <rapid neutron-capture process> build many nuclei beyond the iron group.

= Neon-22 neutron excess from nitrogen-14
{parent=Stellar nucleosynthesis}
{title2=$X_{22}=\frac{22}{14}X_{14},\quad Y_e=\frac12-\frac{X_{14}}{14}$}

Two alpha captures and an intervening <beta-plus decay> turn each nitrogen-14 nucleus into neon-22, incorporating eight baryons from <helium> and converting one <proton> to a <neutron>. With no loss or dilution and nuclear <mass> defects neglected, a nitrogen-14 <mass> fraction $X_{14}$ therefore yields neon-22 <mass> fraction $(22/14)X_{14}$, not $X_{14}$. In otherwise symmetric carbon-oxygen matter this gives the displayed <electron fraction in stellar matter>. A final <neon> <mass> fraction and a pre-helium-burning metallicity must not silently be treated as identical.

= Gamma-process nucleosynthesis
{parent=Stellar nucleosynthesis}

= Gamma process
{synonym}

Hot explosive material can use <photodisintegration> on pre-existing heavy seed nuclei through neutron, proton and alpha emission. This gamma process supplies some proton-rich isotopes that the principal neutron-capture paths bypass. It is a particular mechanism, rather than a synonym for every proposed proton-rich nucleosynthetic process.

= r-process
{parent=Stellar nucleosynthesis}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$r$-process}

= Rapid neutron-capture process
{synonym}

At sufficiently large neutron density, successive <neutron captures> outrun relevant beta decays and produce very neutron-rich nuclei. Decay toward stability afterward yields many heavy isotopes, including some actinides. Neutron-rich ejecta from <neutron star mergers> provide an established site; particular magnetically driven collapse outflows are additional candidates. This does not imply that every ordinary <core-collapse supernova> produces a complete heavy-element pattern.

= Nuclear reaction
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

A <nuclear reaction> transforms one or more <atomic nuclei> through interactions with particles or other nuclei. Total energy, momentum, electric charge and nucleon number constrain ordinary low-energy reaction bookkeeping. <Nuclear fusion>, <alpha capture>, <neutron capture> and <photodisintegration> are important examples in <stellar nucleosynthesis>. Weak processes can also change the proton-to-neutron ratio.

= Beta-minus decay
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=$n\longrightarrow p+e^-+\overline\nu_e$}

An energetically allowed weak transition converts a neutron into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. Free <neutron> decay is an example; in nuclei, the nuclear binding energy determines whether the transition is possible.

= Nuclear fusion
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=$Q=(m_{\rm initial}-m_{\rm final})c^2$}

Fusion combines nuclear reactants into heavier compound nuclei or products. A net decrease in rest mass releases energy; heavier fusion reactions can instead require energy. <Stellar nuclear fusion> uses exothermic networks to power stars, and <quantum tunnelling> through the <Coulomb barrier> permits many charged-particle reactions at stellar temperatures.

= Alpha capture
{parent=Nuclear fusion}
{title2=$^{A}_{Z}X+\alpha\longrightarrow{}^{A+4}_{Z+2}Y+\gamma$}

Capture of an <alpha particle> increases the mass number by four and atomic number by two. Radiative alpha capture releases a photon, while reactions such as $(\alpha,n)$ can instead emit a neutron. <Carbon-12 alpha capture> competes with helium production of carbon during <core helium burning>.

= Carbon-12 alpha capture
{parent=Alpha capture}
{title2=$^{12}\mathrm C(\alpha,\gamma){}^{16}\mathrm O$}

Capture of an <alpha particle> by <carbon>-12 makes <oxygen>-16. Competition between this reaction and the <triple-alpha process> helps determine the carbon-to-oxygen ratio after <core helium burning>.

= Coulomb barrier
{c}
{parent=Nuclear fusion}
{title2=$V(r)=Z_1Z_2e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$}

Repulsion between positively charged nuclei creates this electrostatic energy barrier outside the short-range nuclear interaction. <Quantum tunnelling> allows reaction at thermal energies below its classical height and contributes the penetration factor in the <Gamow peak>.

= Nuclear photodisintegration
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=$\gamma+\text{nucleus}\longrightarrow\text{lighter nuclear products}$}

= Photodisintegration
{synonym}

A sufficiently energetic photon can break up a nucleus, consuming energy for an endothermic breakup. In <neon burning> and <silicon burning>, such steps combine with captures to form a larger reaction network whose net energy balance can be positive.

= Beta-plus decay
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=${}^A_ZX\longrightarrow{}^A_{Z-1}Y+e^++\nu_e$}

An energetically allowed weak nuclear transition converts a proton into a neutron, emitting a positron and electron <neutrino>. Positron annihilation deposits additional electromagnetic energy, while the <neutrino> can escape. These transitions are steps of the <CNO cycle> and the pp III branch; an isolated free proton cannot undergo this decay.

= Electron capture
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{title2=${}^A_ZX+e^-\longrightarrow{}^A_{Z-1}Y+\nu_e$}

A nucleus can capture an electron when energetically allowed, converting a proton into a neutron and emitting an electron <neutrino>. Beryllium-7 <electron capture> participates in one of the <proton-proton chain branches>. Stellar capture rates depend on ionization, <mass density> and electron phase space.

= Nuclear force
{parent=Nuclear physics}
{wiki}

The residual <strong interaction> between <nucleons>. Its long-distance part involves <pion> exchange and depends on <spin> and <isospin>. It is distinct from directly exchanging gluons between color-singlet nucleons.

= Nucleus
{synonym}

The central system of <protons> and <neutrons> in an atom. Its mass number $A$ is its <baryon number> in ordinary nuclear states; $Z$ is the proton number and $N$ the neutron number.

= Alpha particle
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$A=4,\quad Z=2,\quad J=I=0$}
{wiki}

The helium-four <atomic nucleus>, containing two <protons> and two <neutrons>. Its ground-state <spin> and <isospin> are zero. A quantized <cubic four-Skyrmion> supplies a corresponding nuclear state in the soliton description.

= Deuteron
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$A=2,\quad J=1,\quad I=0$}

The bound <atomic nucleus> containing one <proton> and one <neutron>. Its ground-state <spin> is one and its isospin is zero. The appropriately quantized <toroidal two-Skyrmion> models these quantum numbers.

= Nuclear binding energy
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$E_{\rm bind}=(Zm_p+Nm_n-M_A)c^2$}
{wiki}

The positive energy needed to separate a bound <atomic nucleus> into its constituent <protons> and <neutrons>. It is the mass defect times $c^2$. In a <Skyrmion model of a nucleus>, both separated nucleon masses and the multi-soliton state require a consistent quantization and calibration.

= Semiconductor
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

A semiconductor has an electronic <band gap> and a conductivity strongly affected by temperature, impurities and carrier injection. Optical absorption can create mobile electrons and holes, enabling a <photodiode>.

= Acceptor impurity
{parent=Semiconductor}

An impurity that can accept an electron from the valence band, leaving a mobile hole. A shallow acceptor creates an <impurity level in a crystal> near and above the valence-band edge. Donor and acceptor levels are descriptions of appropriate dopants, not universal features of every impurity species.

= Donor impurity
{parent=Semiconductor}

An impurity that can supply an electron to the conduction band. A shallow donor has a localized occupied <impurity level in a crystal> near and below the conduction-band edge; thermal or other ionization releases its electron. The binding energy depends on the host dielectric response and effective mass.

= Acoustic eikonal equation in a shear flow
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$(\omega-Uk_x)^2=c_0^2|\mathbf k|^2$}

For a slowly varying parallel <shear flow> $U(y)\mathbf e_x$, the leading <WKB method> neglects mean-velocity gradients while retaining local advection. The <continuity equation> and <linearized Euler equations> then give the displayed acoustic <dispersion relation>. On the positive intrinsic-frequency branch, $\omega=Uk_x+c_0|\mathbf k|$ and the <group velocity> is $U\mathbf e_x+c_0\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|$. A <wavefront> normal is parallel to $\mathbf k$; the acoustic energy-ray direction generally differs because of the advection term.

= Ray turning in a wall shear layer
{parent=Acoustic eikonal equation in a shear flow}
{title2=$k_y^2=(\omega-Uk_x)^2/c_0^2-k_x^2$}

In a stationary, horizontally homogeneous wall <boundary layer>, <Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations> conserve $\omega$ and $k_x$. If $U$ increases away from the wall, downstream-oriented $k_x>0$ waves have decreasing positive intrinsic <frequency> and decreasing $k_y^2$. At $U=\omega/k_x-c_0$, the transverse <wavenumber> vanishes and the acoustic ray turns. For $U(0)=0$, a downstream launch angle $\beta_0$ above the wall can reach a uniform stream of <Mach number> $M_\infty$ only if $\cos\beta_0\le(1+M_\infty)^{-1}$. This uses the positive acoustic branch, constant <sound speed> and no intervening critical layer; a turning point itself requires a uniform local approximation rather than the ordinary <WKB method>.

= Small-height Neumann scattering
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$\partial_z\psi-h'\partial_x\psi=0\quad(z=h(x))$}

For a rigid rough boundary and incident pressure $Ae^{i\alpha x-i\beta z}$, use time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and $\alpha^2+\beta^2=k^2$. The flat reflected wave is $Ae^{i\alpha x+i\beta z}$. Expanding the <Neumann boundary condition> at $z=0$ gives $\partial_z\psi_1=2Ae^{i\alpha x}(\beta^2h+i\alpha h')$. For height transform $\widehat h(q)=\int h(x)e^{-iqx}dx$, the outgoing correction is $(2A/(2\pi i))\int (k^2-\alpha q)\widehat h(q-\alpha)e^{iqx+i\beta(q)z}/\beta(q)\,dq$, with $\beta(q)=\sqrt{k^2-q^2}$ on the outgoing sheet. Controlled height expansions also require adequate slope and spectral regularity; small incident phase displacement alone does not bound every normal-derivative correction.

= Oblique coherent reflection from a stationary rigid rough surface
{parent=Small-height Neumann scattering}
{title2=$r_N=1-\frac1{\pi\beta}\int\frac{(k^2-\alpha q)^2}{\beta(q)}S_h(q-\alpha)\,dq+\cdots$}

For a zero-mean stationary rough height, the mean first-order correction vanishes. At second order, the boundary derivative is $-h\partial_z^2\psi_1+h'\partial_x\psi_1$. Averaging this expression using the <power spectrum of surface height> gives the displayed coherent reflection coefficient. Thus the <root mean square> height alone does not fix the coherent mean: the height correlation spectrum matters. For roughness varying slowly enough that $q\simeq\alpha$ in the integral, $r_N\simeq1-2\beta^2\sigma^2$. A local random-height phase average needs this additional approximation and is not a general consequence of stationarity.

= Sonar
{parent=Acoustics}
{wiki}

Sonar uses sound propagation and reflected signals to detect objects or measure distances. Upward-looking sonar can record <sea-ice draft> along a moving platform's track; beam width, return selection and processing affect the inferred profile.

= Acoustic waveguide
{parent=Acoustics}

An acoustic waveguide confines sound transversely while allowing longitudinal propagation. Rigid walls impose a homogeneous normal-velocity condition, producing Neumann transverse modes. Each transverse eigenvalue gives a longitudinal wave number: modes below their cutoff decay evanescently, while cut-on modes carry outgoing sound. An open semi-infinite guide can be studied with the <Wiener-Hopf method>.

= Independent-edge radiation from an open planar duct
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$\phi_d\sim-i(-1)^n D(r)\cos(\alpha/2)\sin(\theta/2)\sin(k_0h\sin\theta)/(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha)$}

For the even <acoustic waveguide> mode $\cos(n\pi y/h)e^{-ikx}$, set $\sin\alpha=n\pi/(k_0h)$ and $k=k_0\cos\alpha>0$. At leading independent-edge order for large $k_0h$, the two incident travelling constituents each have amplitude $(-1)^n/2$ at the relevant wall endpoint. Reflecting the upper-edge coordinates reverses the edge-wave sign. Their path phases are $e^{\pm ik_0h\sin\theta}$. Thus <Sommerfeld half-plane diffraction> gives the displayed sum, where $D(r)=\sqrt{2/(\pi k_0r)}e^{-ik_0r-i\pi/4}$. For $n>0$, the apparent poles at $\theta=\pm\alpha$ are removable in the combined expression, with limit $ikhD(r)/2$. Single-edge nonuniform far-field poles are not physical infinities. This combined far-field limit assumes a radiation distance beyond the aperture's near field and neglects further edge interactions; it is not uniform at duct cutoff.

= Wiener-Hopf solution for an open parallel-plate waveguide
{c}
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$L(k)=\gamma\sinh(\gamma b)e^{-\gamma b}$}

For rigid plates $x>0$, $y=\pm b$, an even incident plane wave travelling toward the opening can be scattered using <Wiener-Hopf factorization>. With time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$ and transform $\Phi(k)=\int\phi(x)e^{ikx}dx$, take $\gamma=\sqrt{k^2-k_0^2}$ on the outgoing sheet. The common normal derivative on $y=b$ is supported on $x<0$; its transform is $D^-=iL^+(-k_0)L^-(k)/(k+k_0)$. The interior transform is $D^-\cosh(\gamma y)/(\gamma\sinh(\gamma b))$, while the exterior transform is $-D^-e^{-\gamma(|y|-b)}/\gamma$. These follow from continuity of normal velocity and the half-line pressure jump, followed by <pole subtraction in a Wiener-Hopf equation> and a finite-energy edge condition.

= Upstream decay of an open parallel-plate waveguide
{parent=Wiener-Hopf solution for an open parallel-plate waveguide}
{title2=$\phi_{\rm tot}(x,y)\sim b\sqrt{2k_0/(\pi|x|)}\,e^{ik_0x+i\pi/4}$}

For fixed $|y|<b$ and $x\to-\infty$, the incident pole in the scattered transform cancels the analytic continuation of the incident plane wave. Near $k=-k_0$, the remaining transform is proportional to $(k+k_0)^{-1/2}$, so the total outgoing field has the displayed inverse-square-root cylindrical decay. A claim of square-root growth at negative infinity is incompatible with this outgoing solution.

= Acoustic equipartition in a rigid waveguide
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$\langle E_{\rm kin}\rangle=\langle E_{\rm comp}\rangle$}

For a separated acoustic potential with transverse Neumann eigenvalue $\kappa^2$, integration by parts gives $\int|\nabla_\perp F|^2=\kappa^2\int F^2$. The time-averaged kinetic energy is proportional to $(k^2+\kappa^2)\int F^2$, while compression energy is proportional to $\omega^2\int F^2/c_0^2$. The dispersion relation $\omega^2=c_0^2(k^2+\kappa^2)$ proves equality. At a ray speed below $c_0$, nondegenerate stationary phase gives dispersive amplitudes of order $t^{-1/2}$; the zero transverse mode instead preserves an undispersed pulse on the sound-speed ray.

= Outgoing modes of an open rigid acoustic waveguide
{parent=Acoustic waveguide}
{title2=$k_n^2=k_0^2-(n\pi/b)^2$}

For an even field between rigid plates at $y=\pm b$, the transverse modes are $\cos(n\pi y/b)$, with longitudinal wave numbers $k_n=\sqrt{k_0^2-(n\pi/b)^2}$. With time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$, outgoing modes into $x>0$ use $e^{-ik_nx}$: choose positive real $k_n$ for cut-on modes and negative imaginary $k_n$ for cut-off modes. A lower-half-plane <Fourier inversion> extracts these modes by <residues>. Their decaying components are <evanescent waves>, not backward-propagating waves. At an exact cutoff one takes the radiation continuation before its limit.

= Acoustic analogy
{parent=Acoustics}

An acoustic analogy rearranges fluid conservation equations into a chosen linear acoustic propagation operator plus effective forcing. The rearrangement can be exact before source approximations are made. A term classified as forcing can also contain propagation effects omitted from the reference operator; this produces <propagation-source ambiguity in an acoustic analogy>. The <Lighthill acoustic analogy> is a familiar homogeneous reference-medium example.

= Propagation-source ambiguity in an acoustic analogy
{parent=Acoustic analogy}

The distinction between generation and propagation depends partly on the reference operator chosen in an <acoustic analogy>. For example, a homogeneous-Laplacian operator with spatially varying reference sound speed omits the first-gradient terms of the <stratified acoustic pressure equation>. These terms then reappear as effective forcing, even for a freely propagating small disturbance in a static medium. A right-hand-side term is therefore not automatically an independent physical noise source.

= Pressure-based acoustic analogy with a potential body force
{parent=Acoustic analogy}
{title2=$Q,W_{ij}$}

Eliminating momentum between the <continuity equation> and <conservation of momentum> gives $\rho_{tt}=\partial_i\partial_j(\rho u_i u_j-\sigma_{ij})+\nabla^2(p+\chi)$ when the force density is $-\nabla\chi$. For $q=p-(P-\chi)$ and time-independent reference fields,
$$
(\widehat c_0^{-2}\partial_t^2-\nabla^2)q=\partial_i\partial_jW_{ij}+Q_{tt},\qquad
W_{ij}=\rho u_i u_j-\sigma_{ij},\qquad Q=q/\widehat c_0^2-(\rho-\widehat\rho_0).
$$
No energy equation is needed for this identity. The arbitrary reference fields should match the stationary medium where the outgoing sound is evaluated.

= Small-height Dirichlet scattering
{parent=Acoustics}
{title2=$\psi(x,h(x))=0,\quad h=\varepsilon\eta$}

<Small-height Dirichlet scattering> expands the acoustic boundary condition about a mean plane for a fixed regular surface profile. The zeroth-order field includes the flat reflection; higher orders solve outgoing <Helmholtz equation> problems with induced boundary data. Small $|kh|$ controls the incident-field Taylor expansion, but rapidly varying roughness can require additional control of normal derivatives and spectral moments.

= Coherent reflection from a stationary rough surface
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\langle\psi_s(x,0)\rangle=-1+(k/\pi)\int\beta S_h$}

Through second order in height, a normally incident acoustic plane wave has the displayed mean reflected <amplitude> for a Dirichlet boundary. The propagating part of the <power spectrum of surface height> contributes a positive real correction to the negative flat reflection coefficient, while the evanescent part contributes an imaginary correction. The mean linear rough correction is zero.

= Height-correlation dependence of coherent reflection
{parent=Coherent reflection from a stationary rough surface}

The quadratic coherent correction weights the <power spectrum of surface height> by $\beta(q)$, so equal <root mean square> heights can give different mean fields. Only an additional long-spatial-scale approximation, with spectrum concentrated at $|q|\ll k$, reduces it to $2k^2\sigma^2$. Second-order surface scattering therefore contains information absent from first-order coherent reflection.

= Power spectrum of surface height
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$S_h(q)=\int C_h(\xi)e^{-iq\xi}d\xi$}

For a real zero-mean stationary height process, its <power spectrum of surface height> is the <Fourier transform> of the height <covariance function>. It is even and nonnegative, and $\sigma^2=(2\pi)^{-1}\int S_h(q)dq$. More general stationary processes have a spectral measure rather than a density. Weighted spectral moments control the existence of boundary derivatives and second-order mean scattering corrections.

= Physical surface trace and reference-plane trace
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}

For normal incidence on a Dirichlet rough surface, the scattered trace on the moving boundary is exactly $-e^{-ikh(x)}$. Its trace on the mean plane is instead $-1+2ikh+2k h\mathcal B h+\cdots$. The two traces are related by a normal Taylor expansion and must not be identified. Their ensemble averages can therefore have different quadratic coefficients without inconsistency.

= Second-order rough-surface scattered field
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\psi_s^{[2]}(x,0)=2k h\mathcal B h$}

For normal plane-wave incidence, the second-order <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives $\psi_s^{[2]}=-h\partial_z\psi_s^{[1]}$ at the mean plane, since $\partial_z^2\psi^{[0]}$ vanishes there. The <Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for a Helmholtz half-space> then yields $2k h\mathcal B h$. This nonlocal expression is not generally $2k^2h^2$; that local limit needs roughness varying slowly compared with the wavelength.

= First-order rough-surface scattered field
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\psi_s^{[1]}=-\mathcal E[h\partial_z\psi^{[0]}|_0]$}

Taylor expansion of the <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives the first-order boundary trace $-h\partial_z\psi^{[0]}$ at the mean plane. The <outgoing angular spectrum> then propagates it. A zero-mean surface gives zero mean for this linear rough correction, while the complete <scattered field> still contains its nonzero flat reflection.

= Specular first-order mean reflection
{parent=First-order rough-surface scattered field}
{title2=$\langle\psi_{\rm sc}\rangle=(-1+2iq_i\langle f\rangle)e^{ip_ix+iq_iz}$}

For incident amplitude one, $\psi_i=e^{ip_ix-iq_iz}$ with $q_i>0$, Taylor expansion of a small-height <Dirichlet boundary condition> gives $\psi_{\rm sc}^{[1]}(x,0)=2iq_if(x)e^{ip_ix}$. If the mean height is constant, its mean transform is supported only at tangential wavenumber $p_i$, giving the displayed specular mean through first order. Small height alone is insufficient: deterministic mean height $a\cos Kx$ produces mean sidebands $p_i\pm K$. Stationary roughness has constant mean and therefore satisfies the first-order requirement.

= Rough-surface far-field intensity per illuminated length
{parent=First-order rough-surface scattered field}
{title2=$\frac{\langle|\psi_s|^2\rangle}{D}\sim\frac{2kq_i^2|A_i|^2\cos^2\theta_s}{\pi r}S_h(k\sin\theta_s-p_i)$}

First-order Dirichlet roughness has mean-plane trace $2iq_iA_i h(x)e^{ip_ix}$, where $p_i^2+q_i^2=k^2$ and $q_i>0$. <Stationary phase> applied to the <outgoing angular spectrum> evaluates the far-field amplitude at tangential wavenumber $k\sin\theta_s$. For a stationary surface illuminated over length $D$, the mean squared height transform divided by $D$ tends to its <power spectrum of surface height>. Thus the diffuse intensity samples the spectrum at the tangential momentum transfer. An infinite stationary surface has no finite total far-field amplitude without a window or an intensity-density convention.

= Outgoing angular spectrum
{parent=Small-height Dirichlet scattering}
{title2=$\mathcal E g=(2\pi)^{-1}\int\widehat g(q)e^{iqx+i\beta(q)z}dq$}

The <outgoing angular spectrum> propagates prescribed scalar boundary data into a half-space. For $e^{-i\omega t}$ time dependence, $\beta(q)=\sqrt{k^2-q^2}$ has nonnegative real part on propagating modes and nonnegative imaginary part on evanescent modes. Its trace at the boundary is $g$, and each mode solves the <Helmholtz equation>. The spectral representation can be interpreted through distributions for nondecaying plane-wave data.

= Height propagation of an angular-spectrum correlation
{parent=Outgoing angular spectrum}
{title2=$m(\xi,z)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int S(\nu)e^{i\nu\xi-2\operatorname{Im}q(\nu)z}d\nu$}

Use $\widehat\psi(\nu)=\int\psi(x,0)e^{-i\nu x}dx$ and $q(\nu)=\sqrt{k^2-\nu^2}$ on the upward branch. If the uncentered field correlation is translationally homogeneous and $\langle\widehat\psi(\nu)\widehat\psi(\nu')^*\rangle=2\pi S(\nu)\delta(\nu-\nu')$, its correlation at height $z$ is the displayed integral. The propagating spectrum is unchanged; evanescent contributions decrease as $e^{-2\sqrt{\nu^2-k^2}z}$. A <spectral measure of a stationary random field> permits discrete coherent components. Without correlation homogeneity a double spectral covariance integral depends on both observation coordinates rather than only their separation.

= Reference-plane validity of a rough-surface angular spectrum
{parent=Outgoing angular spectrum}

An upward <angular spectrum> obtained from a horizontal field trace is exact in the homogeneous half-space above that reference plane. For a rough boundary $z=f(x)$, choose the plane above $\sup f$. A trace at $z=0$ is not physically defined in the medium if any part of the surface lies above it. Using such a mean-plane trace requires an analytic continuation convention; convergence of the continued spectrum down to the rough boundary is an additional hypothesis. Small-height boundary perturbation uses the reference plane formally order by order and is distinct from an exact expansion throughout an arbitrary rough domain.

= Acoustic inverse source problem
{parent=Acoustics}

The <Helmholtz equation> forward problem is specified with the <Sommerfeld radiation condition>. At a single frequency, the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency> has invisible <fixed-frequency nonradiating sources>, and its compactness makes recovery of the visible component unstable.

= Fixed-frequency nonradiating source
{parent=Acoustic inverse source problem}
{title2=$Q=(\Delta+k^2)\chi,\qquad \chi\in C_c^\infty(A)$}

A source is nonradiating at frequency $k$ if its outgoing field has zero <far-field pattern>. For every $\chi\in C_c^\infty(A)$, the source $Q=(\Delta+k^2)\chi$ has outgoing field $\psi=\chi$ and is invisible outside $A$. Integration by parts shows that its <Fourier transform> vanishes on $|\boldsymbol\xi|=k$, so it belongs to the <kernel of a linear map> defined by the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency>. This family proves nonuniqueness independently of the instability of the inverse.

= Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency
{parent=Acoustic inverse source problem}
{title2=$T_k:L^2(A)\to L^2(S_1)$}

For $(\Delta+k^2)\psi=Q$, the <outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation> yields the <far-field pattern>
$$
(T_kQ)(\boldsymbol\theta)=-\frac1{4\pi}\int_A e^{-ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf r}Q(\mathbf r)\,d^3\mathbf r,\qquad |\boldsymbol\theta|=1.
$$
The sign corresponds to the Green function satisfying $(\Delta+k^2)G=-\delta$. This <Hilbert-Schmidt operator> samples the source <Fourier transform> on the sphere $|\boldsymbol\xi|=k$; for a ball of radius $r_0$, its squared Hilbert-Schmidt norm is $r_0^3/3$.

= Normal kernel of the source-to-far-field operator
{parent=Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency}
{title2=$K(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\frac1{4\pi}\frac{\sin(k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)}{k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$}

The <adjoint source-to-far-field operator> composed with the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency> has volume kernel $K(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=(4\pi)^{-1}\operatorname{sinc}(k|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)$, where $\operatorname{sinc}(s)=\sin(s)/s$ and $\operatorname{sinc}(0)=1$. This follows from $\int_{S_1}e^{ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf d}dS=4\pi\operatorname{sinc}(k|\mathbf d|)$. Its smoothing action helps explain the unstable <normal equation for a linear inverse problem>.

= Adjoint source-to-far-field operator
{parent=Source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency}
{title2=$T_k^*$}

With complex <inner products> linear in the first argument, the <adjoint operator> of the <source-to-far-field operator at fixed frequency> is
$$
(T_k^*g)(\mathbf r)=-\frac1{4\pi}\int_{S_1}g(\boldsymbol\theta)e^{ik\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf r}\,dS(\boldsymbol\theta).
$$
It is a normalized <Herglotz wave function>. Complex conjugation reverses the phase sign, while retaining the real factor $-1/(4\pi)$.

= Atomic physics
{parent=Physics}
{wiki}

= Fine structure
{parent=Atomic physics}

Fine structure splits atomic levels through relativistic corrections and interactions involving spin, such as spin-orbit coupling. Different total angular momenta can then have distinct energies. Even a small ground-term splitting can matter for density-sensitive <metastable atomic levels>, because weak radiative decay between the levels competes with collisional mixing.

= Metastable atomic level
{parent=Atomic physics}

A metastable atomic level has no strong allowed radiative decay and therefore a comparatively long radiative lifetime. Weak multipole decays can still occur. At low density radiative decay can keep its population small; as density increases, collisional population and mixing compete with that decay. Metastable levels provide density-sensitive feeding of other spectral lines.

= Collisional-radiative model
{parent=Atomic physics}

A collisional-radiative model solves coupled atomic population equations using collisions, radiative transitions, ionization and recombination. For one fixed ion stage, a common stationary approximation is $0=\sum_{j\ne i}(n_jP_{ji}-n_iP_{ij})$, supplemented by the total ion population. Electron-impact rates contribute $n_eq_{ij}(T)$ and spontaneous downward transitions contribute $A_{ij}$. Additional radiation, proton collisions and adjacent-ion source terms are included when significant.

= Electron-density diagnostic from metastable populations
{parent=Collisional-radiative model}

In a two-level ground system, balancing collisional upward excitation against radiative and collisional downward loss gives $n_2/n_1=n_eq_{12}/(A_{21}+n_eq_{21})$. Excited lines fed in different proportions by these levels have a density-sensitive ratio. At low density the population ratio is proportional to density; at high density it saturates at $q_{12}/q_{21}$. A reliable calibration uses a full multilevel model when upper-state branching and cascades matter.

= Emission-weighted effective density from a line ratio
{parent=Electron-density diagnostic from metastable populations}

For optically thin lines with local ratio $R(n_e,T)$, their integrated observed ratio is $\int j_1(s)R(n_e(s),T(s))ds/\int j_1(s)ds$. Inverting a uniform-plasma calibration yields an effective diagnostic density. It is generally not the volume mean, nor the density obtained by simply averaging the local densities, because emissivity weighting and the ratio response are nonlinear.

= Atomic oscillator strength
{parent=Atomic physics}
{title2=$f_{ij}$}

An atomic oscillator strength is a dimensionless measure of an optical transition's absorption strength relative to a classical electron oscillator. It is related to its spontaneous radiative rate through the transition energy and the statistical weights of the two levels. The absorption convention $f_{ij}$ fixes the lower state $i$ and upper state $j$; interchanging that convention can lose a weight factor.

= Statistical weight of an atomic level
{parent=Atomic physics}
{title2=$g_J=2J+1$}

= Statistical weights of atomic levels
{synonym}

The statistical weight of an atomic level counts its degenerate quantum states. With no external field resolving magnetic substates, a specified total angular momentum $J$ gives $2J+1$ states. An unresolved term can have a larger summed weight. These weights enter the <Boltzmann distribution>, <Saha ionization equation> and detailed-balance relations; term and resolved-level weights must not be interchanged.

= Spontaneous emission
{parent=Atomic physics}
{title2=$P_e(t)=P_e(0)e^{-\Gamma t}$}
{wiki}

<Spontaneous emission> is radiative decay of an excited matter <quantum state> by emission of a <photon>, without an applied resonant drive. In a simple Markov decay model the excited-level population obeys $\dot P_e=-\Gamma P_e$. During driven control, the expected emission count is governed by $\Gamma\int P_e(t)dt$; suppressing the lossy level's population is therefore the purpose of dark-state adiabatic transfer.

= Radiative cascade
{parent=Spontaneous emission}

A radiative cascade is a sequence of downward photon-emitting transitions following excitation or recombination. Cascades feed lower excited levels and change their emitted line intensities. An <effective recombination coefficient> includes all relevant cascade paths under its stated optical-depth and density assumptions.

= Critical density of an atomic transition
{parent=Spontaneous emission}
{title2=$n_{\rm crit}=A_{ul}/q_{ul}$}

The critical density of an atomic transition is the electron density at which its collisional de-excitation rate equals its spontaneous radiative decay rate. A multilevel atom requires summed competing rates rather than a single-transition ratio. This atomic density scale is distinct from cosmological critical density.

= Einstein coefficients
{c}
{parent=Spontaneous emission}
{title2=$A_{ji},B_{ij},B_{ji}$}

Einstein coefficients describe spontaneous emission, stimulated emission and absorption between atomic levels. With $B$ multiplying radiation energy density per frequency, thermal detailed balance gives $g_iB_{ij}=g_jB_{ji}$ and $A_{ji}/B_{ji}=8\pi h\nu^3/c^3$. Coefficients defined using mean specific intensity instead have a different normalization; specify the radiation convention.

= Collisional excitation
{parent=Atomic physics}
{title2=$A+e^-\to A^*+e^-$}

A collision can transfer kinetic energy to an atom or ion and excite a bound <electron>. Subsequent radiative de-excitation removes that energy from an optically thin gas. The thermal excitation rate is strongly reduced when the transition energy exceeds $k_BT$, giving the threshold behavior of <atomic line cooling>.

= Collision rate coefficient for an atomic transition
{parent=Collisional excitation}
{title2=$q_{ij}(T)=\langle\sigma_{ij}v\rangle$}

For a normalized electron speed distribution $f_T(v)$, the atomic transition rate coefficient is $q_{ij}(T)=\int\sigma_{ij}(v)v f_T(v)dv$. Its dimensions are volume per time, so the rate per target atom is $n_eq_{ij}$. Maxwellian detailed balance gives $q_{ji}=(g_i/g_j)e^{(E_j-E_i)/kT}q_{ij}$ for $E_j>E_i$.

= Ionization
{parent=Atomic physics}
{wiki}

= Three-body recombination
{parent=Ionization}

Three-body recombination captures an <electron> while a second free electron carries away energy, the inverse of direct electron-impact ionization. Its rate scales as the parent-ion density times the square of electron density. It is usually negligible compared with two-body <radiative recombination> and <dielectronic recombination> in the low-density solar corona, but matters in sufficiently dense plasmas.

= Dielectronic recombination
{parent=Ionization}

A free <electron> is resonantly captured while exciting the ionic core, producing a doubly excited state above the parent-ion threshold. Capture becomes permanent recombination only if the state undergoes <radiative stabilization> rather than <autoionization>. For resonance $d$, entrance ion $i$, and Maxwellian electrons, $\alpha_d=(g_d/(2g_i))(h^2/(2\pi mkT))^{3/2}e^{-E_d/kT}A^a_{di}A^r_{\rm stab}/(A^a_{\rm tot}+A^r_{\rm tot})$. Detailed balance determines the capture rate; a steady population balance determines the survival factor. The rate coefficient has dimensions volume per time.

= Resonance-temperature dependence of dielectronic recombination
{parent=Dielectronic recombination}
{title2=$\alpha_d(T)\propto T^{-3/2}e^{-E_d/kT}$}

For temperature-independent atomic rates and an isolated positive-energy resonance, <dielectronic recombination> has the displayed dependence. Differentiating its logarithm gives a maximum at $kT=2E_d/3$. A sum of resonances can have several peaks, and near-threshold resonances change the low-temperature behavior. Collisional destruction of captured high levels can introduce density dependence.

= Radiative stabilization
{parent=Dielectronic recombination}

Radiative stabilization occurs when a photon emission from an autoionizing state leads to a bound state, preventing electron re-ejection. For one radiative channel and one autoionization channel, its probability is $A_r/(A_a+A_r)$. With several channels, use summed losses and the total rate of successful stabilizing decays; a cascade through another autoionizing state requires its eventual survival probability.

= Dielectronic capture
{parent=Dielectronic recombination}

Dielectronic capture is the resonant capture stage inverse to a specified <autoionization> channel. For an entrance ion of weight $g_i$ and resonance of weight $g_d$ and energy $E_d>0$, its Maxwellian coefficient is $C_d=(g_d/(2g_i))(h^2/(2\pi mkT))^{3/2}e^{-E_d/kT}A^a_{di}$. This follows by equating capture and autoionization fluxes at thermodynamic equilibrium. Capture alone need not produce a permanently recombined ion.

= Autoionization
{parent=Ionization}

An excited atom or ion above an electron-emission threshold can decay without a photon by ejecting an electron and leaving a more highly charged ion. This is autoionization. Its rate competes with <radiative stabilization> in <dielectronic recombination>; the inverse electron-capture channel is <dielectronic capture>.

= Photoionization
{parent=Ionization}
{title2=$h\nu\geq E_{\rm bind}$}

<Photoionization> removes a bound electron when an atom or ion absorbs a sufficiently energetic photon. The threshold is its <ionization energy>. The excess photon energy becomes kinetic energy of the products; subsequent collisions can heat the gas or cause secondary ionizations. A <photoionization> rate and a <photoionization equilibrium> are distinct concepts: equilibrium also requires specified recombination and other reaction rates.

= Photoionization rate
{parent=Photoionization}
{title2=$\Gamma$}

For isotropic <specific intensity> $J_\nu$, absorption cross-section $\sigma_\nu$, and threshold frequency $\nu_0$, the photoionization rate per target atom is
$$
\Gamma=4\pi\int_{\nu_0}^\infty\frac{J_\nu\sigma_\nu}{h\nu}\,d\nu.
$$
For a directed incident flux, replace $4\pi J_\nu$ by its <spectral flux density>. The factor $h\nu$ converts energy flux into photon flux. This rate alone does not imply <photoionization equilibrium>.

= Photoionization heating
{parent=Photoionization}

= Photoheating
{synonym}

Absorption of an ionizing photon leaves energy in a photoelectron above the binding energy. Thermalization supplies heat to the gas. <Photoionization heating> competes with the <astrophysical cooling function>, can establish thermal equilibrium, and can raise the pressure enough to inhibit baryon accretion into shallow <dark-matter haloes>.

= Radiative recombination
{parent=Ionization}
{title2=$A^++e^-\to A+\gamma$}

A free <electron> can be captured by an ion while a photon carries away binding and kinetic energy. This <radiative recombination> process contributes free-bound radiation to plasma cooling and balances <collisional ionization> in a collisional-equilibrium plasma.

= Recombination line
{parent=Radiative recombination}

A recombination line is a <spectral line> produced after a free <electron> is captured, often through a subsequent <radiative cascade> among bound levels. Its emissivity is computed with an <effective recombination coefficient>, distinct from the free-bound recombination continuum.

= Effective recombination coefficient
{parent=Radiative recombination}
{title2=$\alpha_\ell^{\rm eff}$}

The effective recombination coefficient for a <spectral line> counts recombinations and subsequent <radiative cascades> producing that line. At sufficiently low density, its photon production rate per volume is $n_en_{\rm parent}\alpha_\ell^{\rm eff}$. It depends on gas temperature, optical-depth assumptions such as <Case B recombination>, and sometimes density. It differs from the total rate coefficient removing the parent ion.

= Collisional ionization
{parent=Ionization}
{title2=$A+e^-\to A^++2e^-$}

In <collisional ionization>, an energetic collision frees a bound <electron>. The process consumes kinetic energy and changes the <electron> and ion populations controlling plasma cooling. In a thermal gas its rate competes with recombination and depends strongly on the <ionization> threshold relative to temperature.

= Excitation-autoionization
{parent=Collisional ionization}

Electron impact can excite an ion into a state above an electron-emission threshold, which then ejects a bound electron by <autoionization>. This two-stage excitation-autoionization contributes to <collisional ionization> in addition to direct impact removal.

= Collisional ionization equilibrium
{parent=Collisional ionization}
{title2=$n_iC_i(T)=n_{i+1}\alpha_{i+1}(T)$}

In <collisional ionization equilibrium>, collision-driven <ionization> and recombination balance for the populated ion stages, determining their relative abundances at a given temperature. The resulting cooling curve includes the loss of line-emitting bound states as temperature increases. Rapidly cooling gas or external photoionization can invalidate this equilibrium assumption.

= Coronal approximation
{parent=Collisional ionization equilibrium}

The coronal approximation treats an optically thin, low-density plasma with electron-impact ionization balanced by radiative and dielectronic recombination. Excited-level populations are determined mainly by collisional excitation and radiative decay, not by a Boltzmann distribution over levels. Ion fractions can depend primarily on electron temperature while metastable populations retain density dependence; this distinction enables density diagnostics.

= Multipole moment
{parent=Physics}

A multipole moment is a coefficient in the far-field angular expansion of a localized source. Successive moments describe progressively finer departures from spherical symmetry.

= Stone-Čech compactification
{c}
{parent=Compactification (physics)}
{title2=$\beta X$}

For a <completely regular Hausdorff space>, the Stone-Čech compactification is the weak-star closure of the evaluation functionals in the dual unit ball of $C_b(X)$. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> gives compactness; complete regularity makes evaluation a <topological embedding>. Every bounded real <continuous function> extends by $\widetilde f(p)=p(f)$, yielding an isometric isomorphism $C_b(X)\cong C(\beta X)$. For a continuous map into a <compact Hausdorff space> $K$, the adjoint of pullback sends this closure into the closed evaluation copy of $K$, yielding the unique continuous extension. Uniqueness follows from density. It is a universal compactification, not a homeomorphism between $X$ and the whole dual space.

= Clopen separation in discrete Stone-Čech compactifications
{parent=Stone-Čech compactification}
{title2=$\beta X=U\mathbin{\dot\cup}V$}

In the <Stone-Čech compactification> of a <discrete space>, the closure of each subset of $X$ is <clopen>, because its indicator extends to a continuous zero-one-valued function. For disjoint closed sets $A,B\subseteq\beta X$, choose $u=0$ on $A$ and $u=1$ on $B$ by <Urysohn's lemma>. Then $U=\overline{\{x\in X:u(x)<1/2\}}$ and $V=\beta X\setminus U$ are disjoint clopen sets containing $A$ and $B$, respectively. Density proves these containments.

= Discrete Stone-Čech compactifications are extremally disconnected
{parent=Stone-Čech compactification}

For a <discrete space> $X$ and $D\subseteq X$, the continuous extension of $\mathbf1_D$ to $\beta X$ remains idempotent by density, so it takes values in $\{0,1\}$. Its one-set is the clopen closure of $D$. If $O$ is any <open set> in $\beta X$, density gives $\overline O=\overline{O\cap X}$, so this closure is clopen as well. Thus $\beta X$ is an <extremally disconnected space>. For infinite $X$, this does not imply that every subset of $\beta X$ has open closure.

= Dimensional analysis
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

Dimensional analysis checks the <physical dimensions> of quantities and identifies combinations unchanged by the choice of units. It catches impossible equations and reveals scaling before a full dynamical calculation. Adding two quantities requires the same <physical dimension>; replacing metres by centimetres must change numerical values consistently while preserving physical predictions.

For the ideal <simple pendulum>, suppose its <oscillation period> $T$ depends only on mass $m$, <length> $\ell$, <gravitational acceleration> $g$, and dimensionless angular <amplitude> $\theta_0$. Write $T=m^a\ell^bg^c F(\theta_0)$. Since $[m]=M$, $[\ell]=L$, and $[g]=LT^{-2}$, matching <physical dimensions> gives $a=0$, $b+c=0$, and $-2c=1$. Consequently
$$
T=\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\,F(\theta_0).
$$
The angular <amplitude> is dimensionless when expressed in <radians>. Dimensional analysis establishes the length/gravity scaling and the absence of a mass factor under these assumptions, but cannot determine $F$. The <harmonic oscillator equation> gives $F\to2\pi$ at small <amplitude>; the full <simple pendulum> supplies its finite-amplitude correction. A model with drag or a finite massive support has additional parameters, so the same dimensional argument does not automatically apply.

The result is useful for comparison: at fixed angular <amplitude> and $g$, lengths in the ratio $1:4$ give periods in the ratio $1:2$. The ratio $T\sqrt{g/\ell}$ should be the same for both in this ideal model. A failure to collapse onto the same value points to measurement error or an omitted parameter. Correct <physical dimensions> are a necessary consistency check, while the coefficient and the adequacy of the model require more information.

= Nondimensionalization
{parent=Dimensional analysis}
{wiki}

Rescaling dimensional quantities by chosen characteristic scales produces <dimensionless variables>. The remaining dimensionless ratios identify independent parameters and possible asymptotic regimes.

In the <simple pendulum> equation, choose the time scale $t_* =\sqrt{\ell/g}$ and put $\tau=t/t_*$. The <chain rule> gives $\ddot\theta=(g/\ell)d^2\theta/d\tau^2$, so the equation becomes $d^2\theta/d\tau^2+\sin\theta=0$. This removes $\ell$ and $g$ from the equation, while the <initial conditions> still include angular <amplitude> and dimensionless initial angular speed. A solution can therefore be calculated once and rescaled to many physical lengths and gravity values with the same dimensionless initial data.

= Ray in a linear-speed medium
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$(x-h)^2+y^2=R^2$}

For light speed $c(y)=y>0$, the travel-time integrand is $\sqrt{1+y'^2}/y$. The <Beltrami identity> gives $y\sqrt{1+y'^2}=R$, so nonvertical rays are semicircles centred on $y=0$. Boundary endpoints at $y=0$ have infinite travel time: along any rectifiable path entering positive height, $ds/y\geq |dy|/y$ and the integral diverges. These semicircles are therefore ideal-endpoint <hyperbolic geodesics>, not finite-time optical minimizers joining zero-speed endpoints.

= Ray in an exponential-speed medium
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$e^{\lambda y}=\cos(\lambda x)/\cos(\lambda a)$}

For positive $\lambda$ and light speed $c(y)=c_0e^{\lambda y}$, the <Fermat principle> uses the time integrand $c_0^{-1}e^{-\lambda y}\sqrt{1+y'^2}$. The <Beltrami identity> sets $e^{-\lambda y}/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ constant. Solving this first integral gives the displayed symmetric ray between $(\pm a,0)$ when $0<\lambda a<\pi/2$. Its travel time is $2\sin(\lambda a)/(c_0\lambda)$. The ray's central height diverges as $\lambda a\uparrow\pi/2$, while its travel time tends to $2/(c_0\lambda)$.

= Neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution

{title2=$f(N,z)=\partial^2\mathcal N/(\partial N\,\partial z)$}

= Ionized gas density from an absorption distribution
{parent=Neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution}
{title2=$\Omega_g=\frac{H_0\mu_Hm_H}{c\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}\int\frac{N}{x_{\rm HI}}f_X(N,z)\,dN$}

A neutral-hydrogen <column density> $N$ traces total hydrogen column $N/x_{\rm HI}$ only after specifying its neutral fraction $x_{\rm HI}$. Integrating the latter against the distribution per <absorption distance> gives the comoving gas <mass density> in the displayed expression. The factor $\mu_H$ includes helium if desired. In <photoionization equilibrium> for optically thin, almost fully ionized hydrogen, $x_{\rm HI}\simeq\alpha(T)n_H/\Gamma$, so gas density, <temperature>, <photoionization rate> and absorber size are required. = Neutral gas density from an absorption distribution
{parent=Neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution}
{title2=$\Omega_{\rm HI}=\frac{H_0m_H}{c\rho_{\rm crit,0}}\int Nf_X(N,z)\,dN$}

Let $f_X=\partial^2\mathcal N/(\partial N\partial X)$ count neutral <hydrogen> absorbers per <column density> and <absorption distance>. The summed column per unit proper light-path length is the mean neutral <number density>. Since $d\ell=c\,dz/[(1+z)H(z)]$ and $dX/dz=H_0(1+z)^2/H(z)$, that density is $(H_0/c)(1+z)^3\int Nf_X\,dN$. Dividing the proper <mass density> by $(1+z)^3$ gives the comoving density in the displayed formula. = Absorber incidence and comoving number density

= Random-orientation absorbing-disk cross-section
{parent=Lyman-alpha absorption}
{title2=$\langle\sigma_{\rm abs}\rangle=\pi R^2/2$}

A circular, opaque, geometrically thin disk has projected area $\pi R^2|\cos i|$. = Translating accelerated reference frame
{parent=Reference frame}
{title2=$m\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf F-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$}

If the origin of a nonrotating <reference frame> has inertial <position> $\mathbf a(t)$ and a particle has relative <position> $\mathbf R$, then $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf a$. <Newton's second law> becomes $m\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf F-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$. The effective inertial <force> is therefore $-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$. No rotation-dependent terms arise because the coordinate axes remain parallel to the inertial axes.

= Inertial frame
{parent=Reference frame}
{wiki}

An inertial frame is a <reference frame> in which a force-free particle moves with constant <velocity>.

= Rest frame
{parent=Inertial frame}
{title2=$\mathbf p=0$}

The rest frame of a massive particle is an <inertial frame> in which its spatial <momentum> is zero. Its <four-momentum> is then $(mc,\mathbf0)$ and its energy is $mc^2$. Such a frame exists for a future-directed timelike four-momentum: the <Lorentz transformation> with boost velocity $\mathbf v=c^2\mathbf p/E$ makes the spatial momentum zero, and the <mass shell> relation implies $|\mathbf v|<c$. A massless particle has no rest frame, because $E=c|\mathbf p|$ would require a boost with speed $c$.

= Local inertial frame
{parent=Inertial frame}

A local inertial frame is a coordinate frame in which, at one chosen spacetime event, the <metric tensor> is Minkowskian and the <Levi-Civita connection> vanishes. Freely falling experiments confined to a sufficiently small neighbourhood therefore obey the laws of <special relativity> up to tidal corrections.

= Rotating frame

= Energy balance in a uniformly rotating frame
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$\dot E=(\partial_t\phi)_{r'}$}

For uniform <angular velocity> $\omega$, potential $\phi$, and additional <forces> doing no work in the rotating frame,
$$
E=\frac m2|v'|^2+\phi-\frac m2|\omega\times r|^2,
\qquad \dot E=(\partial_t\phi)_{r'}.
$$
The <Coriolis force> does no work; the <centrifugal potential> accounts for the remaining apparent <force>. This energy is conserved precisely when the potential has no explicit time dependence in the rotating coordinates. An inertial time-independent <conservative force> need not meet that condition unless its potential is also invariant under the frame's rotation. The balance prevents applying a rotating-frame conservation law to a nonaxisymmetric stationary inertial potential.

= Particle on a uniformly rotating inclined plane
{parent=Rotating reference frame}

For a smooth plane at inclination $\theta$ rotating about a vertical axis at constant <angular velocity> $\omega$, choose rotating coordinates $\xi$ horizontally and $\eta$ uphill, with their cross product the upward <normal vector>. A sliding particle under gravity satisfies
$$
\ddot\xi=\omega^2\xi+2\omega\dot\eta\cos\theta,\qquad\ddot\eta=\omega^2\eta\cos^2\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\cos\theta-g\sin\theta.
$$
The <normal reaction> is $N=m(g\cos\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\sin\theta+\omega^2\eta\sin\theta\cos\theta)$ while contact is maintained. The <Coriolis force> does no work in the rotating frame, giving the conserved specific <energy>
$$
\frac12(\dot\xi^2+\dot\eta^2)+g\eta\sin\theta-\frac{\omega^2}{2}(\xi^2+\eta^2\cos^2\theta).
$$
The last term is the <centrifugal potential>; this quantity need not equal the inertial <mechanical energy>.

= Bead on a horizontal hoop rotating about its rim
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$\ddot\theta=-\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$}

For a smooth horizontal circular hoop of radius $a$, rotated at constant <angular velocity> $\omega$ about a vertical axis through a rim point, measure $\theta$ at that pivot from the diameter toward the bead. In rotating <Cartesian coordinate system> coordinates, $\mathbf r=a(1+\cos2\theta,\sin2\theta,0)$. The tangential component of the <centrifugal acceleration> gives the displayed equation; the <Coriolis acceleration> is normal to the hoop. The farthest point $\theta=0$ is stable for $\omega\ne0$, with <small oscillation> angular frequency $|\omega|$. The pivot itself is unstable. This geometry differs from a vertical rotating hoop and has no tangential gravitational term.

= Rotating-frame derivative formula
{parent=Rotating reference frame}

If a frame rotates with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$ relative to an inertial frame, the derivatives of any vector $\mathbf a(t)$ satisfy
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\rm inertial}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\rm rotating}
+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf a.
$$
Applying this formula twice to a <position> gives the <Coriolis acceleration>, <Euler acceleration>, and <centrifugal acceleration> terms.

At colatitude $\theta$, ignore terms quadratic in Earth angular speed and use uniform vertical gravity. A vertically launched particle has zeroth-order height $z=vt-gt^2/2$. = Pauli equation
{parent=Schrodinger equation}
{c}
{title2=$i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=H_P\Psi$}

For a nonrelativistic spin-one-half electron of charge $-e$, with $e>0$, the Pauli Hamiltonian is
$$
H_P=\frac1{2m}(-i\hbar\nabla+eA)^2+\frac{e\hbar}{2m}\sigma\cdot B-e\phi.
$$
The wavefunction has two spin components and $B=\nabla\times A$. If $\pi=-i\hbar\nabla+eA$, then $[\pi_i,\pi_j]=-ie\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$; the <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives $(\sigma\cdot\pi)^2=\pi^2+e\hbar\sigma\cdot B$, explaining the spin-coupling sign. Thus $i\hbar\partial_t\Psi=H_P\Psi$ extends the <Schrodinger equation> to include the electron's magnetic moment.

= Zero modes of a positive transverse Pauli field
{parent=Pauli equation}
{title2=$\chi_k=e^{-ky-\int_0^y a(s)ds}$}

For $B=B(y)e_z$ with $B(y)\ge B_0>0$, take $a(y)=(e/\hbar)\int_0^yB(s)ds$. The spin-down transverse Hamiltonian at momentum label $-k$ is proportional to $M^\dagger M$, where $M=\partial_y+k+a(y)$. It is nonnegative, and $M\chi_k=0$ gives the displayed zero mode. Since $a'\ge eB_0/\hbar$, its exponent has a negative quadratic upper bound at both infinities, proving square integrability for every $k$. The continuously varying momentum label supplies an infinitely degenerate zero-energy space.

= Madelung equations
{c}
{parent=Time-dependent Schrodinger equation}
{wiki}
{title2=$\psi=R e^{iS}$}

Writing the <wavefunction> in modulus and dimensionless <quantum phase> gives a <probability continuity equation> and a <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> with a <quantum potential>:

\partial_t(R^2)+\nabla\cdot\left(R^2\frac\hbar m\nabla S\right)=0,\qquad

The phase equations hold locally away from <wavefunction> nodes. They follow by equating real and imaginary parts of the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>.

= Variable-width shallow-water characteristics
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$D_\pm(u\pm2c)=\mp cu\,b'/b$}

For a rectangular channel with stationary width $b(x)$ and constant <reduced gravity> $g'$, <volume conservation> and <momentum conservation> give $h_t+uh_x+hu_x=-hu b'/b$ and $u_t+uu_x+g'h_x=0$. With $c=\sqrt{g'h}$ the <characteristic curves> have speeds $u\pm c$. The combinations $u\pm2c$ obey the displayed compatibility equations; they are <Riemann invariants> only when the width source term vanishes.

= Boundary-driven shallow-water wave steepening
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$x_b=2gH/(3u_b'(0))$}

For an outgoing <simple wave> into water initially at rest at depth $H$, the incoming <Riemann invariant> is $u-2\sqrt{gh}=-2\sqrt{gH}$. Thus $\sqrt{gh}=\sqrt{gH}+u/2$ and the outgoing <characteristic speed> is $v=\sqrt{gH}+3u/2$. A boundary characteristic emitted at time $s$ is $x=v(s)(t-s)$. Its envelope satisfies $t=s+v/v'$ and $x=v^2/v'$. At a zero-velocity onset with positive acceleration, the leading-edge intersection is the displayed distance. Later <characteristic curves> can form a shock earlier elsewhere in a sufficiently strong waveform.

= Momentum correction for lateral mass addition
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$uu_x=-g(h+H)_x+(Q_x/A)(u_a-u)$}

In a sectionally uniform flow, a volume source $Q_x$ per downstream length brings axial velocity $u_a$. <Momentum conservation> gives the displayed source correction, with $A$ the wetted area. The head derivative is $[h+H+u^2/(2g)]_x=Q_x(u_a-u)/(gA)$. Removing fluid at the local velocity preserves the head in this approximation. Adding fluid at rest does not: its acceleration reduces that head. The volume source alone cannot specify the momentum exchange without a velocity assumption.

= Shallow-water specific energy
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$E=h+u^2/(2g)$}

For a steady rectangular-channel flow with discharge $Q=bhu$, the <shallow-water specific energy> relative to the bed is $E=h+Q^2/(2gb^2h^2)$. At fixed discharge and width, $E_h=1-F^2$, where the <Froude number> satisfies $F^2=Q^2/(gb^2h^3)$. The minimum is $E_c=3h_c/2$, with $h_c=(Q^2/(gb^2))^{1/3}$. Without dissipation, $E+H$ is the conserved <Bernoulli equation> head for bed elevation $H$.

= Variable-section channel characteristics
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$c^2=gS/S_h$}

For cross-sectional area $S(x,h)$ and cross-sectionally uniform velocity, <volume conservation> gives $S_h(h_t+uh_x)+Su_x+uS_x=0$, while hydrostatic inviscid momentum gives $u_t+uu_x+gh_x=0$. Their <characteristic speeds> are $u\pm c$. With $D_\pm=\partial_t+(u\pm c)\partial_x$, the robust compatibility equation is $(g/c)D_\pm h\pm D_\pm u=-ucS_x/S$. Replacing $D_\pm h$ by $D_\pm c/c_h$ requires $c_x=0$ at fixed height; otherwise an explicit geometry term remains.

= Standing modes in an exponentially tapered channel
{parent=Variable-section channel characteristics}
{title2=$k_n^2=(n\pi/L)^2+\beta^2/4$}

For constant long-wave speed and $S_0\propto e^{\beta x}$, a standing free-surface amplitude obeys $\eta''+\beta\eta'+k^2\eta=0$. The transformation $\eta=e^{-\beta x/2}Y$ gives $Y''+(k^2-\beta^2/4)Y=0$. Impermeable ends at $0,L$ impose $\eta'=0$, yielding the displayed nonzero-frequency eigenvalues and $\eta_n\propto e^{-\beta x/2}[\cos(n\pi x/L)+\beta L\sin(n\pi x/L)/(2n\pi)]$. A separate zero-frequency mode changes the uniform water level.

= Hydraulic jump
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{wiki}

A hydraulic jump connects shallow <supercritical flow> to deeper <subcritical flow> while conserving discharge and momentum and dissipating mechanical energy. Its <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> uses the channel's actual area and integrated <hydrostatic pressure>, which depend on cross-sectional geometry.

= Hydraulic bore
{parent=Hydraulic jump}

A hydraulic bore is a propagating abrupt depth change described as a moving <hydraulic jump>. In the <shock frame>, an admissible rightward through-flow goes from <supercritical flow> to <subcritical flow>, with the appropriate <characteristic curves> entering the jump.

= Prismatic-channel shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$A_t+(Au)_x=0,\quad(Au)_t+(Au^2+P)_x=0$}

In a channel whose transverse width $b(z)$ does not depend on downstream position, the wetted area is $A(h)=\int_0^h b(z)\,dz$ and the integrated <hydrostatic pressure> force divided by density is $P(h)=g\int_0^h(h-z)b(z)\,dz$. With cross-sectionally uniform velocity, <volume conservation> and <momentum conservation> give the displayed <shallow water equations>. Since $P'(h)=gA(h)$, the two <characteristic speeds> are $u\pm c$ with $c^2=gA/A'$. For $b(z)=Cz^p$, $c^2=gh/(p+1)$ and the <Riemann invariants> are $u\pm2(p+1)c$.

= Dry-bed dam break with cubic wetted area
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$c^2=gh/3,\quad J_\pm=u\pm6c$}

A channel with width $b(z)=\alpha z^2$ has wetted area $A=\alpha h^3/3$. Its inviscid <shallow water equations> have characteristic speeds $u\pm c$ and the displayed <Riemann invariants>. Releasing a reservoir of depth $H$ at rest into a dry channel gives a left-family <rarefaction wave>: $c=(6c_0-x/t)/7$, $u=6(c_0+x/t)/7$, $h/H=[(6-x/(c_0t))/7]^2$ between $x=-c_0t$ and $x=6c_0t$, where $c_0=\sqrt{gH/3}$. This width law differs from a geometrically parabolic trough, whose width is proportional to $\sqrt z$.

= Finite-reservoir reflection of a cubic-area dam-break rarefaction
{parent=Dry-bed dam break with cubic wetted area}
{title2=$x_r=6c_0t-7c_0t_1^{2/7}t^{5/7},\quad t_1=\ell/c_0$}

An impermeable wall at $x=-\ell$ first receives the upstream-moving head at $t_1$. The leading downstream <characteristic curve> of the reflected adjustment obeys $\dot x=u+c=(12c_0+5x/t)/7$ in the unmodified <rarefaction wave>, giving the displayed curve. It stays behind the inviscid dry tip $x_f=6c_0t$ at every finite time. The finite reservoir drains and its upstream depth decreases, while only the region behind $x_r$ has yet received the wall signal. Bottom drag or other tip physics can change the front law.

= Triangular-channel dry-bed dam break
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$h/h_0=[(4-x/(c_0t))/5]^2,\quad c_0=\sqrt{gh_0/2}$}

In a prismatic triangular channel with wetted area proportional to $h^2$, the <Riemann invariants> are $u\pm4c$, with $c^2=gh/2$. Releasing water initially at rest into a dry channel produces a <rarefaction wave> with $u+4c=4c_0$ and $x/t=u-c$. Hence $c=(4c_0-x/t)/5$, $u=4(c_0+x/t)/5$ and the displayed depth profile for $-c_0t<x<4c_0t$. The dry front advances at $4c_0$ before finite-reservoir or varying-channel effects become important.

= Parabolic-channel dry-bed dam break
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$h/H=(3-x/(c_0t))^2/16$}

For water initially at rest at depth $H$ on one side of a dam and a dry bed on the other, a channel with $b(z)=C\sqrt z$ has $c_0=\sqrt{2gH/3}$. The centered <rarefaction wave> has $u+3c=3c_0$ and $x/t=u-c$. Thus $c=(3c_0-x/t)/4$ and $u=3(c_0+x/t)/4$ within $-c_0t<x<3c_0t$. The reservoir remains undisturbed to the left and the bed dry to the right. This is an outer <shallow-water approximation> before finite-reservoir boundary signals return.

= Shallow-water dispersion relation
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$\omega^2=gh\kappa^2$}

For linear surface gravity waves with wavelength much larger than the depth, $\omega^2=gh\kappa^2$. It follows from $\omega^2=g\kappa\tanh(\kappa h)$ when $\kappa h\ll1$, and the wave speed is $\sqrt{gh}$.

= Rigid-lid approximation
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$\nabla_h\cdot\mathbf u=0\quad(H=\mathrm{constant})$}

= Rigid lid
{synonym}

The rigid-lid approximation suppresses <free surface> displacement in a fluid layer while retaining a variable pressure that enforces the mass constraint. At constant depth it gives horizontally nondivergent motion. In a one-layer <quasi-geostrophic approximation>, removing free-surface stretching eliminates the $-\psi/R_D^2$ term from <shallow-water quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity>. This is also the small-horizontal-scale limit compared with the <Rossby deformation radius>, when that term is negligible rather than exactly absent.

= Rigid-lid pressure in two-layer flow
{parent=Rigid-lid approximation}
{title2=$\chi=f_0(\psi_1-\psi_2)/g\prime$}

The <rigid-lid approximation> removes external surface displacement from volume conservation while retaining its pressure as a constraint multiplier. It does not mean zero horizontal pressure gradient. In two-layer geostrophic flow, $f_0\psi_1=\Pi$ and $f_0\psi_2=\Pi-g\prime\chi$, so the upper-layer depth anomaly is $\chi=f_0(\psi_1-\psi_2)/g\prime$. This pressure difference supplies internal potential-vorticity coupling.

= Variable-buoyancy shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}

A vertically mixed <gravity current> with horizontally varying <reduced gravity> $b$ obeys $h_t+(hu)_x=0$ and $(hu)_t+(hu^2+bh^2/2)_x=0$. Consequently the <material acceleration> is $Du/Dt=-b h_x-hb_x/2$. Advected density variables add repeated contact <characteristic curves> of speed $u$ to the gravity-wave <characteristic curves> of speeds $u\pm\sqrt{bh}$.

= Shallow-water approximation
{parent=Shallow water equations}

= Shallow water
{synonym}

The shallow-water approximation applies when a fluid layer's vertical scale is much smaller than its horizontal scale. Vertical acceleration is then negligible to leading order, pressure is <hydrostatic pressure>, and the horizontal velocity can be represented by a depth average.

= Linearized shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}

For a homogeneous layer of mean depth $H_0$ on an f-plane, linearization about rest gives
$$
u_t-fv=-g\eta_x,\qquad
v_t+fu=-g\eta_y,\qquad
\eta_t+H_0(u_x+v_y)=0.
$$

= Variable-Coriolis shallow-water height equation
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$\mathcal F_y=\partial_t^2+f(y)^2$}

On a <beta plane> with $f=f_0+\beta y$, set $\mathcal F_y=\partial_t^2+f(y)^2$. Exact elimination of the <depth-integrated shallow-water transports> gives
$$
\mathcal F_y\left[\nabla_h^2\eta-\frac{\mathcal F_y\eta}{c^2}\right]_t=\beta(\eta_{xtt}+2f\eta_{yt}-f^2\eta_x).
$$
The essential commutator is $\partial_y(\mathcal F_yV)=\mathcal F_yV_y+2f\beta V$. Freezing undifferentiated $f$ factors to $f_0$ is a local approximation. The slow-wave limit gives the <shallow-water Rossby-wave dispersion relation> with a negative zonal phase velocity for $\beta>0$.

= Depth-integrated shallow-water transport
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$(U,V)=H(u,v)$}

For a depth-independent horizontal velocity in a layer of reference depth $H$, the linear transports are $U=Hu$, $V=Hv$. They have dimensions of area per time and satisfy the surface <continuity equation> $\eta_t+U_x+V_y=0$. Their momentum equations are obtained by multiplying the depth-independent velocity equations by $H$.

= Depth independence of hydrostatic shallow-water flow
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}

In a homogeneous layer under the <hydrostatic approximation>, the horizontal <pressure gradient> is independent of depth. The depth derivatives of velocity obey $(u_z)_t-fv_z=0$, $(v_z)_t+fu_z=0$. If the motion starts without shear, these derivatives remain zero. Hydrostatic pressure alone does not eliminate a pre-existing freely oscillating shear.

= Rotating shallow-water height equation with variable depth
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$\partial_t[\eta_{tt}+f^2\eta-g\nabla_h\cdot(H\nabla_h\eta)]+fgH_y\eta_x=0$}

For linear rotating <shallow water equations> with fixed depth $H(y)$ and constant <Coriolis parameter> $f$, elimination of the horizontal velocity gives
$$
\partial_t\left[\eta_{tt}+f^2\eta-g\nabla_h\cdot(H\nabla_h\eta)\right]+fgH_y\eta_x=0.
$$
At a depth step this is interpreted distributionally, with continuity of surface elevation and normal volume flux. Because velocity elimination raises the time order, initial height alone is insufficient; the original velocity and continuity equations determine the remaining initial derivatives.

= Diffusive flux

A diffusive flux transports a density $u$ down its <gradient> according to <Fick's first law>. For scalar <diffusion coefficient> $D>0$, $\mathbf j=-D\nabla u$ and the <conservation law> $u_t+\operatorname{div}\mathbf j=0$ gives a <diffusion equation>. = Local conservation law
{parent=Conservation law}

= Volume conservation

Volume conservation states that the volume of incompressible material can change only through boundary fluxes or specified sources and sinks. In a one-dimensional shallow layer of depth $h$ and depth-averaged speed $u$, it has the form $h_t+(hu)_x=s$.

= Mass flux

= Mass loss rate

The mass loss rate is the positive rate at which a body loses mass. For a steady spherical <stellar wind>, the <continuity equation> gives $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u_r$, constant across spheres enclosing the source. It is positive for outward radial flow; an accretion convention often uses a separate positive inward rate.

= Momentum flux

Momentum flux includes advected momentum and stress. In a one-dimensional inviscid flow it is $p+\rho u^2$.

= Energy flux
{parent=Conservation law}

Energy flux is the energy crossing a unit area per unit time. For a one-dimensional inviscid gas with internal-energy density $W$, it is
$$

$$

{parent=Meson}
{title2=$P=(-1)^{L+1},\quad C=(-1)^{L+S}$}

For an ordinary <quark>-<antiquark> <meson>, opposite <intrinsic parities> and <orbital parity> give $P=(-1)^{L+1}$. For a self-conjugate flavor state, <charge conjugation> exchanges the two constituents. Reversal of their relative momentum gives $(-1)^L$, spin exchange gives $(-1)^{S+1}$, and reordering the two fermionic creation operators gives one extra minus sign. Their product is $C=(-1)^{L+S}$. Electrical neutrality alone does not imply that a state is an eigenstate of charge conjugation; charged mesons have no individual C eigenvalue.

= Omega meson
{parent=Meson}
{title2=$\omega$}
{wiki}

The <omega meson> is an isospin-zero vector <meson> with <charge conjugation> value minus one and consequently <G-parity> minus one. Three-pion decay is compatible with the isospin-symmetric <strong interaction>, whereas two-pion decay violates its G-parity rule.

= Rho meson
{parent=Meson}
{title2=$\rho^{+,0,-}$}
{wiki}

The <rho meson> is an isospin-one vector <meson>. Its neutral member has <charge conjugation> value minus one, so all three charge states have <G-parity> plus one. The two-pion decay is allowed by G parity, whereas a purely strong isospin-conserving three-pion decay is excluded.

= Vector meson
{parent=Meson}
{wiki}
{title2=$J^P=1^-$}

A <meson> with total angular momentum one and negative <parity>. In the ordinary <quark model>, a <quark>-<antiquark> pair with <orbital angular momentum> zero and total <spin> one has these quantum numbers; its neutral self-conjugate states have <charge conjugation> $C=-1$. The light-flavour tensor product gives an octet and a singlet, forming a vector nonet, just as spin-zero negative-parity pairs form a <pseudoscalar meson nonet>. Nonzero orbital excitations can also produce vector states.

= Pseudoscalar meson
{parent=Meson}
{wiki}
{title2=$J^P=0^-$}

A <meson> with zero <spin angular momentum> and negative <parity>. The <pion> and <kaon> are familiar examples. Between two <pseudoscalar mesons>, <parity conservation> in the strong interaction forces the <axial current> matrix element to vanish, while the <vector current> has <pseudoscalar-to-pseudoscalar form factors>. The weak decay as a whole need not conserve <parity>.

= Pseudoscalar meson nonet
{parent=Pseudoscalar meson}
{title2=$\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf1,\quad J^P=0^-$}

In the <quark model>, the light-flavour triplet and its antiquark conjugate give an octet and a singlet. A colour-singlet pair with orbital angular momentum zero and total spin zero has parity minus one, giving a pseudoscalar nonet. Its six outer weights are charged <pions> and four <kaons>. At the centre the octet contains the neutral pion and octet eta direction; the additional singlet direction gives a third state at the same weight.

= Eta and eta prime mesons
{parent=Pseudoscalar meson nonet}
{title2=$\eta,\eta^\prime$}

These neutral isospin-zero <pseudoscalar mesons> have $J^{PC}=0^{-+}$. Their light-quark components mix the <eta octet state> and <eta singlet state>. The singlet axial anomaly contributes to the unusually large eta-prime mass, while flavour breaking also affects both masses and the mixing. The simple orthogonal one-angle flavour model is useful for explaining the nonet; detailed decay-constant descriptions can require more than one parameter.

= Eta singlet state
{parent=Pseudoscalar meson nonet}
{title2=$\eta_1=(u\overline u+d\overline d+s\overline s)/\sqrt3$}

This flavour combination is the invariant scalar in the <octet and singlet in a fundamental SU3 tensor product>. Its isospin and flavour hypercharge vanish. Although it shares the central weight with the <eta octet state>, it lies in a different <irreducible representation>. = Pseudoscalar-to-pseudoscalar form factor

= Neutral meson mixing
{parent=Meson}

Transitions between a neutral <meson> carrying flavour and its <antiparticle>. Decays are described on this two-state subspace by an effective <Hamiltonian operator> $M-i\Gamma/2$ with <Hermitian matrices> $M,\Gamma$.

= Effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing
{parent=Neutral meson mixing}
{title2=$H_{\rm eff}=M-i\Gamma/2$}

A decaying particle-antiparticle pair is described by a two-dimensional <effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing> with dispersive matrix $M$ and decay matrix $\Gamma$, both <Hermitian matrices>. The <effective Hamiltonian for neutral-meson mixing> itself is generally not a <Hermitian matrix>. <CPT> symmetry equates its diagonal entries, by separately equating diagonal <masses> and <decay widths>. For $H_{\rm eff}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&a\end{pmatrix}$ the <eigenvalues> are $a\pm\sqrt{bc}$ and generic <eigenvectors> are $(\sqrt b,\pm\sqrt c)^T$, with correlated square-root branches. Complex <eigenvalues> give <masses> and <decay widths> through $\mu=m-i\Gamma/2$.

= Neutral kaon mixing
{title2=$K^0\leftrightarrow\bar K^0$}
{parent=Kaon}

The neutral flavour states $d\bar s$ and $s\bar d$ can mix through second-order weak interactions. Box diagrams with <W bosons> and internal up-type quarks induce a strangeness-changing-by-two four-quark operator. The <GIM mechanism> removes its flavour-independent part.

= Kaon CP mixing parameter
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$\epsilon=\frac{\sqrt{R_{12}}-\sqrt{R_{21}}}{\sqrt{R_{12}}+\sqrt{R_{21}}}$}

In the flavor convention $K_1=(K^0-\bar K^0)/\sqrt2$ and $K_2=(K^0+\bar K^0)/\sqrt2$, the parameter $\epsilon=(a-b)/(a+b)$ writes normalized <mass> states as $(K_1+\epsilon K_2)/\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}$ and $(K_2+\epsilon K_1)/\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}$. In the Hermitian <mass>-only approximation with correlated roots, $\epsilon=i\tan(\varphi/2)$ and the states are <orthogonal>. For the full non-Hermitian decay matrix the real part need not vanish. The parameter depends on the flavor-state phase convention; observable <CP violation> involves convention-invariant combinations with decay amplitudes.

= Exchange-positive CP convention for neutral kaon mixing
{parent=Kaon CP mixing parameter}
{title2=$|K_\pm\rangle=\frac{|K_{1,2}\rangle+\epsilon|K_{2,1}\rangle}{\sqrt{1+|\epsilon|^2}}$}

In the phase convention $CP|K^0\rangle=|\bar K^0\rangle$, the symmetric flavor combination is CP even and the antisymmetric one CP odd. For an equal-diagonal effective matrix with entries b,c off diagonal, choose correlated roots r,s with $r^2=b$, $s^2=c$ and eigenvalues $a\pm rs$. Its eigenvectors are $(r,\pm s)^T$. Writing $\epsilon=(r-s)/(r+s)$ gives the displayed CP-basis mixtures, up to an overall phase. Their deviation is small only if $|\epsilon|\ll1$; noninvariance alone does not guarantee that. This convention must not be mixed with one in which flavor exchange carries a minus sign.

= Neutral-kaon mass matrix diagonalization
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$m_{S,L}=r_0\mp|z|$}

With <CPT symmetry>, a Hermitian <kaon> <mass matrix> is $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r_0&z\\z^*&r_0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its <eigenvalues> are $r_0\pm|z|$. For correlated roots $a^2=z$, $b^2=z^*$ and $ab=|z|$, the normalized lower and upper <eigenvectors> are $(a,-b)/\sqrt{2|z|}$ and $(a,b)/\sqrt{2|z|}$. They are <orthogonal>. This is a <mass>-only result; including decay requires the generally non-Hermitian effective matrix $M-i\Gamma/2$.

= Kaon mixing square-root branch convention
{parent=Neutral-kaon mass matrix diagonalization}
{title2=$\sqrt z\sqrt{z^*}=|z|$}

In a Hermitian neutral-<kaon> <mass matrix>, independently choosing square-root signs of $z$ and $z^*$ can interchange the <eigenvalue> labels. Choose $z=re^{i\varphi}$, $a=\sqrt r e^{i\varphi/2}$ and $b=\sqrt r e^{-i\varphi/2}$, so $ab=r>0$, continuously from the desired <CP>-conserving limit. Then $(a,-b)$ is the lower-<mass> <eigenvector>. At a degenerate off-diagonal zero the <mass> eigenbasis is not fixed by diagonalization alone.

= Charged weak box contribution to kaon mixing
{parent=Neutral kaon mixing}
{title2=$\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\lambda_jF(m_i,m_j)$}

A <Standard Model> charged-current <box diagram> converts $d\bar s$ into $s\bar d$. Internal <quarks> run over $u,c,t$, and the flavor sum contains $\lambda_i\lambda_j$ with $\lambda_i=V_{is}^*V_{id}$. The loop function depends on internal masses; <CKM matrix> unitarity gives $\sum_i\lambda_i=0$, producing the <GIM mechanism> cancellation of flavor-independent terms. The amplitude is of order $g^4$ and second order in the <weak interaction>.

= Kaon bag parameter
{parent=Charged weak box contribution to kaon mixing}
{title2=$B_K$}

For relativistically normalized <kaon> states and $Q=(\bar s\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)d)(\bar s\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)d)$, define $\langle\bar K^0|Q|K^0\rangle=(8/3)f_K^2m_K^2B_K$. The dimensionless kaon bag parameter measures <strong interaction> corrections to the vacuum-insertion normalization. The coefficient and <matrix element> must be defined in the same <renormalization scheme> and at the same scale. Dividing by $2m_K$ gives the corresponding two-state mixing-Hamiltonian normalization.

= Omega baryon

{parent=Baryon}
{title2=$\Omega^-$}

The light-quark $sss$ member of the spin-three-halves <baryon decuplet> has <strangeness> $S=-3$, <baryon number> $B=1$, <flavor hypercharge> $Y=-2$, and <isospin> zero. = Pentaquark
{parent=Baryon}
{title2=$qqqq\bar q$}

A <baryon> with constituent content four <quarks> and one <antiquark>, whose net <baryon number> is one. Such content admits more flavour representations than three quarks alone. A representation in the tensor product is not evidence by itself of a bound state.

= Light pentaquark strong-decay channels
{parent=Pentaquark}

Exotic flavour weights can decay into a <baryon> and a <meson> while preserving every strong quantum number: $uudd\bar s\to pK^0$ or $nK^+$, $ddss\bar u\to\Xi^-\pi^-$, and $uuss\bar d\to\Xi^0\pi^+$. These are quark rearrangements. If above threshold with unsuppressed couplings, such states are short-lived strong resonances. Flavour symmetry alone fixes neither masses nor widths.

= Pentaquark antidecuplet
{parent=Pentaquark}
{title2=$\overline{\mathbf{10}}$}

Two antisymmetric flavour quark pairs can each transform in $\overline{\mathbf3}$, combine into $\overline{\mathbf6}$, and with an <antiquark> give $\overline{\mathbf6}\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}=\overline{\mathbf{10}}\oplus\mathbf8$. The antidecuplet rows are $(Y,I)=(2,0),(1,1/2),(0,1),(-1,3/2)$. Exactly three weights are absent from all three-quark states: $(I_3,Y)=(0,2)$ and $(\pm3/2,-1)$.

= Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets
{c}
{parent=Baryon}

In a <three-quark colour singlet>, a symmetric spatial state requires symmetric spin-flavour symmetry. Completely symmetric decuplet flavour couples to symmetric spin $3/2$; mixed octet flavour couples to mixed spin $1/2$ in the symmetric permutation combination. Antisymmetric singlet flavour cannot have a symmetric S-wave orbital state, since $\bigwedge^3\mathbb C^2=0$ forbids a completely antisymmetric state of three spin-$1/2$ quarks. Mixed orbital and spin states can instead realize the required antisymmetry.

= Light-diquark spin-isospin correlation
{parent=Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets}
{title2=$(I,S_{ud})=(0,0)\text{ or }(1,1)$}

Two light <quarks> in a symmetric spatial state and the antisymmetric colour pair inside a <three-quark colour singlet> require symmetric spin-flavour symmetry. Their <isospin> singlet and spin singlet are both antisymmetric, while their respective triplets are symmetric. Thus the allowed pairs have $(I,S_{ud})=(0,0)$ or $(1,1)$; opposite exchange parities are excluded. Coupling an isospin-zero spin-one-half <quark> gives an $I=0,J=1/2$ <baryon> or $I=1,J=1/2,3/2$ <baryons>.

= Baryons with one isospin-singlet quark
{parent=Light-diquark spin-isospin correlation}
{title2=$Q(h[ud])=q_h+1/3$}

For an <isospin> singlet <quark> $h$ of charge $q_h$, the antisymmetric light-flavour pair gives an $I=0,J=1/2$ state with charge $q_h+1/3$. The symmetric light-flavour pairs $uu,\{ud\},dd$ give $I=1$ triplets of spins $1/2$ and $3/2$, with charges $q_h+4/3,q_h+1/3,q_h-2/3$. These follow from <light-diquark spin-isospin correlation> and addition of constituent <electric charges>, with zero <orbital angular momentum>.

= Symmetric spin-flavour SU6 representation
{parent=Pauli constraint on three-quark flavour multiplets}
{title2=$\mathbf{56}=(\mathbf{10},\mathbf4)\oplus(\mathbf8,\mathbf2)$}

Three light <quarks> have a six-dimensional single-quark spin-flavour space $\mathbb C^3\otimes\mathbb C^2$. A symmetric spatial ground state and the antisymmetric <three-quark colour singlet> require the symmetric cube of that space. Its dimension is $\binom83=56$, and restriction to flavour $SU(3)$ and spin $SU(2)$ gives a <baryon decuplet> of spin $3/2$ and a <baryon octet> of spin $1/2$. The two mixed-permutation multiplicity spaces pair once in the symmetric sector, explaining why the two flavour-octet copies in the unconstrained <tensor> cube do not represent two distinct ground-state octets.

= Weighted acoustic Green-function reciprocity

$$
a(x)G(x,y;s)=a(y)G(y,x;s).
$$
Multiplying its frequency-domain equation by $a$ and using the bilinear <Green second identity> proves the relation. Both Green functions must use the same outgoing or causal prescription. Exchanging source and receiver preserves time lag, and involves no complex conjugation.

= Time-harmonic wave
{parent=Wave}
{title2=$\psi(\mathbf r)e^{-i\omega t}$}

= Wave scattering
{parent=Wave}

= Scattered wave
{parent=Wave scattering}
{title2=$\psi_s=\psi-\psi_i$}

= Scattered field
{synonym}

The <scattered wave> is the part of the total solution produced by the scatterer relative to the prescribed <incident wave>. It obeys an outgoing radiation condition. For a rough reflecting surface, this convention includes both specular flat-surface reflection and the roughness-dependent correction. Its <coherent field> is its ensemble mean, which need not equal the full mean intensity.

= Incident wave
{parent=Wave scattering}
{title2=$\psi_i$}

= Incident field
{synonym}

The <incident wave> is the prescribed background solution that illuminates a scatterer. It solves the unperturbed <Helmholtz equation> in scalar time-harmonic models. The scattered contribution is defined by subtracting this field from the total field; reflected waves from a planar boundary are included unless a separate reference reflection is explicitly removed.

= Differential cross-section for scalar wave scattering
{parent=Wave scattering}
{title2=$d\sigma/d\Omega=|f_\infty|^2$}

If a unit incident <plane wave> has scattered field $\psi_s=e^{ikR}f_\infty(\widehat{\mathbf r},\widehat{\mathbf r}_0)/R+O(R^{-2})$, the differential wave scattering cross-section is
$$
 \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f_\infty(\widehat{\mathbf r},\widehat{\mathbf r}_0)|^2.
$$
It is the far-field limit of $R^2$ times the scattered-to-incident <energy flux> ratio. The common physical amplitude normalization cancels when the incident and scattered waves are in the same background medium. The formula assumes that the $1/(4\pi)$ Green-function factor, if present, is already included in the <far-field pattern>.

= Finite-volume random scattering spectrum
{parent=Differential cross-section for scalar wave scattering}
{title2=$\langle\sigma_d\rangle\simeq\mu^2k^4|V|S_W(\mathbf k_s)/(4\pi^2)$}

In the <Born approximation for scalar wave scattering>, with $n=1+\mu W$ and zero-mean stationary unit-variance $W$, define $S_W(\mathbf q)=\int C(\mathbf s)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf s}d^3s$. The leading <expectation> of the <differential cross-section for scalar wave scattering> is
$$
 \langle\sigma_d\rangle=\frac{\mu^2k^4}{4\pi^2}\int C(\mathbf s)|V\cap(V-\mathbf s)|e^{-i\mathbf k_s\cdot\mathbf s}\,d^3s+O(\mu^3),
 \quad\mathbf k_s=k(\widehat{\mathbf r}-\widehat{\mathbf r}_0).
$$
For a regular volume much larger than the covariance range, the overlap is approximately $|V|$, giving $\mu^2k^4|V|S_W(\mathbf k_s)/(4\pi^2)$. <Statistical isotropy>, beyond <statistical homogeneity>, permits the scalar argument $|\mathbf k_s|=2k\sin(\theta/2)$. If the spectrum is defined for the index fluctuation rather than $W$, it already contains $\mu^2$.

= Random medium
{parent=Wave scattering}

= Wave propagation in a random medium
{parent=Random medium}

= Uncentered wave-field correlation
{parent=Wave propagation in a random medium}
{title2=$C(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r)E(\mathbf r')^*]$}

= Wave-field correlation
{synonym}

For a complex random <wave amplitude>, this two-point <expectation> retains the <coherent field> and uses a <complex conjugate> on the second amplitude. It is not a normalized <wave-field correlation> coefficient. Subtracting $\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r)]\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r')]^*$ gives the centered <covariance function>. At coincident points the uncentered <wave-field correlation> equals the <mean wave intensity>. Under second-order <stationarity> it depends only on the separation and has a <spectral measure of a stationary random field>. A deterministic field is included by taking a degenerate ensemble.

= Ensemble-averaged wave intensity
{parent=Wave propagation in a random medium}
{title2=$\mathbb E[|E|^2]=|\mathbb E[E]|^2+\mathbb E[|E-\mathbb E[E]|^2]$}

= Mean wave intensity
{synonym}

For a random complex wave amplitude $E$, its mean squared amplitude is $I=\mathbb E[|E|^2]$. Expanding $E=m+(E-m)$, with $m=\mathbb E[E]$, gives
$$
 \mathbb E[|E|^2]=|m|^2+\mathbb E[|E-m|^2].
$$
The mixed terms vanish. Thus the squared <coherent field> is only one part of the mean intensity; the other is the diffuse contribution. A fixed acoustic or optical normalization converts this squared-amplitude quantity to physical <energy flux>. This ensemble mean differs from the angularly averaged radiative-transfer <mean intensity>.

= Coherent wave field
{parent=Wave propagation in a random medium}
{title2=$m=\mathbb E[E]$}

= Coherent field
{synonym}

In <wave propagation in a random medium>, the coherent field is the <expectation> of the complex wave amplitude, $m(\mathbf r)=\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r)]$. The remaining field $E-m$ has zero <expectation>. This is the <coherent and diffuse wave fields> decomposition. <Statistical homogeneity> of the medium does not make $m$ spatially constant when the incident field is localized: translation symmetry applies to the response together with its incident data. The coherent component can attenuate while the total <ensemble-averaged wave intensity> remains substantial.

= Gaussian coherent-field propagation in the Markov approximation
{parent=Coherent wave field}
{c}
{title2=$m=e^{-\gamma(x-x_0)}E_{\mathrm{free}}$}

For the linear weak-index <parabolic wave equation>, a <Markov approximation> with integrated covariance $B(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb R}C(s,\eta)ds$ gives
$$
 m_x=\frac{i}{2k}m_{zz}-\gamma m,\qquad \gamma=\frac{k^2\mu^2B(0)}2.
$$
The drift follows by converting the multiplicative <Stratonovich integral> $ik\mu E\circ d\mathcal B$ to an <Itô integral> for a <Brownian field> with transverse covariance $B$. For a <Gaussian beam with one transverse coordinate> entering the random region at $x_0$, $m(x,z)=e^{-\gamma(x-x_0)}E_{\mathrm{free}}(x,z)$. The <coherent field> retains transverse beam structure. The white-noise closure needs short longitudinal correlations and the corresponding separation of propagation scales; Gaussian one-point statistics alone are insufficient.

= Weak-fluctuation memory equation for the coherent field
{parent=Coherent wave field}

For a <parabolic wave equation> with $n=1+\mu W$, a zero-mean stationary real field $W$ of unit variance, and $L_0=i\partial_z^2/(2k)$, finite-correlation perturbation theory gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
 m_x&=L_0m+\frac{ik\mu^2}{2}m\\
 &\quad-k^2\mu^2\int_{x_0}^x\int_{\mathbb R}C(x-s,z-\zeta)G_{x-s}(z-\zeta)m(s,\zeta)\,d\zeta\,ds+O(\mu^3).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $m$ is the <coherent field>, $C$ is the <autocorrelation function of a random field> and $G$ is the one-coordinate <Fresnel propagator>. Expand the random solution once using the <Duhamel principle>, multiply by $W$ and average to obtain the memory term. The local phase term comes from the $\mu^2W^2$ term in $n^2$. The expansion is for fixed propagation distances with suitable covariance regularity and moment bounds. A <Markov approximation> is an additional scale assumption that can turn this integral equation into a local attenuation equation.

= Quadratic refractive-index shift in the coherent field
{parent=Weak-fluctuation memory equation for the coherent field}
{title2=$\partial_xm\supset ik\mu^2m/2$}

If the physical <refractive index> is $n=1+\mu W$, then $n^2-1=2\mu W+\mu^2W^2$. In the <parabolic wave equation>, the last term produces $ik\mu^2\mathbb E[W^2E]/2$ in the mean equation. For unit-variance, finite-correlation fluctuations, $\mathbb E[W^2E]=m+O(\mu)$ in a weak-fluctuation expansion, giving a coherent phase drift $ik\mu^2m/2$ to second order. This term is absent if the model deliberately linearizes $n^2-1$ to $2\mu W$. It is not obtained by squaring ideal <Gaussian white noise>, whose pointwise square is not the finite-variance random variable $W^2$.

= Frequency

Frequency is the number of repetitions of a periodic phenomenon per unit time. For a <wave> of period $T$, its frequency is $\nu=1/T$, measured in hertz. The <angular frequency> is $\omega=2\pi\nu$.

= Doppler effect
{c}
{parent=Wave}
{wiki}

= Doppler shift
{c}
{synonym}

The Doppler effect changes the observed <frequency> of a <wave> when source and observer move relative to one another. For light and a small radial recession speed $v_r\ll c$, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda\simeq v_r/c$, where $\lambda$ is <wavelength>. This is the basis of <Doppler spectroscopy>.

= Relativistic Doppler effect
{parent=Doppler effect}
{title2=$\nu=\gamma\nu'(1+\beta\cos\theta')$}

The <Lorentz transformation> changes the <photon> <four-momentum> and therefore its frequency, producing the displayed formula for a boost along the first axis. Here the primed ray angle is measured in the emitting frame and $\beta=v/c$. The angle and frequency transforms must be used together: <aberration of light> changes the direction, and a photon-number angular fraction need not equal the corresponding radiant-energy fraction.

= Kinematic redshift
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$1+z_{\rm kin}=\sqrt{(1+v/c)/(1-v/c)}$}

A kinematic redshift is the <relativistic Doppler effect> from relative motion of emitter and receiver. With positive radial recession speed $v$, its small-velocity limit is $z_{\rm kin}=v/c+O(v^2/c^2)$. A <peculiar velocity> gives such a factor in addition to <cosmological redshift>.

<Time evolution> describes the change of a physical system's state with time. A closed quantum system has <unitary time evolution>; dissipative dynamics need not be unitary.
