= Protein
{wiki}

A <protein> is a biological <molecule> made of one or more <polypeptide chains>: ordered <amino acid residues> joined by <peptide bonds>. <Biological translation> assembles a chain in the order specified by <messenger RNA> <codons>. <Protein folding> then brings parts of that chain into spatial relationships that support its function. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9879/[Cooper, The Cell: protein composition and structure]. These processes can overlap in a <biological cell>; the sequence-to-shape explanation does not require waiting until <biological translation> finishes before any folding begins. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9843/[Cooper, The Cell: protein folding and processing].

The useful connection is from information to physical work. A <protein>'s <protein primary structure> records an order; its <protein tertiary structure> can bring distant <amino acid residues> together at an <active site>. An <enzyme> uses such an arrangement to recognize a <substrate> and accelerate a <chemical reaction>. Other <proteins> serve as <receptors>, <membrane transport proteins> or structural material. For example, <actin> assembles into filaments whose mechanical properties can be described by <biopolymer mechanics>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26911/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: protein function and binding].

Order matters beyond composition. Let A stand for <alanine> and M for <methionine>, and read from the <N-terminus> to the <C-terminus>. The illustrative strings AMAMAMAMAMAM and AAAAAAMMMMMM each contain exactly six A and six M <amino acid residues>. Each has $12-1=11$ <peptide bonds> when made into one unbranched <polypeptide chain>. In the first string all eleven neighboring pairs have different letters; in the second only the pair at positions 6 and 7 does. A composition <histogram> therefore loses information about which <amino acid residues> are neighbors. Their arrangements can affect <protein folding> and function; these short invented strings neither specify a known functional <protein> nor establish what shape either would adopt.

<Protein structure> is a moving arrangement, not a permanently rigid outline. <Substrate> binding can change an <enzyme>'s shape through <induced fit>, and a functional <protein> can include flexible regions. Sequence, surroundings and interacting <molecules> must therefore be considered together. = Sequential-enzyme Hill limit
{parent=Hill equation}
{title2=$\rho=E_0 k_n^+S^n/(\prod_iK_i+S^n)$}

For sequential <enzyme> binding, a <quasi-steady-state approximation> gives occupied-state weights $\phi_jS^j$, $\phi_j=\prod_{i=1}^jK_i^{-1}$, relative to free <enzyme>. A connectivity argument explains the three products. Treat each continuous backbone segment as a <graph vertex>, and retained interchain <disulfide bonds> as <edges>. For a constructed mixture, start with $5\,\mathrm{pmol}$ of intact <IgG> and stipulate that $60\%$ undergoes the complete two-cut event while the rest remains intact. Count <amount of substance> of each product:
$$

$$
The total amount of separately connected products becomes $11\,\mathrm{pmol}$, although the starting amount was $5\,\mathrm{pmol}$. Each digested <IgG> replaces one connected assembly with three. The <antibody binding site> inventory is conserved in this model:
$$

The factor two counts sites per intact <IgG>, not extra <immunoglobulin light chains> per <Fab fragment>. = Avidity
{parent=Antibody}

= Antibody equilibrium occupancy
{parent=Antibody affinity}
{title2=$\theta$}

<Antibody equilibrium occupancy> counts occupied <antibody binding sites>, rather than retained assay label. For an original 1:1 model, let $A$ denote an unoccupied site, $E$ free <antigen> bearing one accessible <epitope>, and $AE$ their complex. At <chemical equilibrium>, write
$$
A+E\rightleftharpoons AE,\qquad c=[E],\qquad K_d=\frac{[A]c}{[AE]},\qquad A_T=[A]+[AE],\qquad \theta=\frac{[AE]}{A_T}=\frac{c}{K_d+c}.
$$
Square brackets mean <concentrations>. The <dissociation constant> $K_d$ here has <concentration> units. This is the same independent-site calculation as <receptor binding occupancy>, now applied to an <antibody binding site>. The last equality follows by substituting $[AE]=[A]c/K_d$ into the occupied fraction and cancelling $[A]$.

Assume identical independent sites, reversible 1:1 binding, dilute <chemical solution> behavior, fixed <temperature> and <buffer solution>, and enough time for <chemical equilibrium>. Exclude two-arm bridging, <cross-reactivity> and inaccessible sites from this model. Crucially, $c$ is free <antigen> <concentration>; substituting the amount supplied per volume requires negligible depletion by binding. Independent <IgG> arms could obey this model site by site, but it does not describe <avidity> from bridging a surface.

Stipulate $K_d=20\,\mathrm{nM}$, with $1\,\mathrm{nM}=10^{-9}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. Free <antigen> <concentrations> $2$, $20$ and $200\,\mathrm{nM}$ give $\theta=1/11$, $1/2$ and $10/11$. A second stipulated <dissociation constant> of $200\,\mathrm{nM}$ instead gives $\theta=1/11$ at $c=20\,\mathrm{nM}$. Both curves approach one at large $c$: a nearly saturated readout can conceal an <antibody affinity> difference. These are invented values, with no universal threshold for a useful <antibody>.

= Western blot normalization
{parent=Western blot}

Total-<protein> <Western blot normalization> compares a target-band signal with a signal representing many transferred <proteins> in that lane. It is distinct from measuring one reference <protein>, and from measuring the target itself. Its denominator is a loading proxy: normalization estimates target abundance relative to detected total <protein>, rather than target amount per <biological cell>. Different sample compositions, selective <Western blot transfer> losses or unequal stain responses can still matter.

For an original calculation, suppose background-corrected target signals $I_A=12$, $I_B=24$ and total-<protein> signals $P_A=30$, $P_B=60$ are in their respective proportional response ranges. Then
$$
\frac{I_B/P_B}{I_A/P_A}=\frac{24/60}{12/30}=1.
$$
The invented signals double together, so this calculation supplies no evidence for a higher target fraction. With $I_B=24$ but $P_B=30$, the same calculation gives $2$. These examples assume a common target response and a common total-<protein> response between lanes, negligible remaining background, and comparable target recovery; they do not equate total-<protein> staining with target abundance. Multiplying both signals in a lane by the same loading factor cancels in their <ratio>; a selective target loss does not cancel.

= Western blot response saturation
{parent=Western blot}

<Western blot response saturation> means that increasing target amount no longer increases the recorded signal proportionally. It can arise before imaging, through limited binding or reagent availability, as well as through detector limits. Shortening exposure can address a detector limit without repairing an already unresponsive assay. Test the target and normalization readouts with a dilution series under the actual assay conditions; merely seeing a gray band does not establish a proportional response.

In a 2013 methods experiment, a <biological cell> extract containing added purified <yeast> <protein> was separated on gels, transferred with a Trans-Blot Turbo to <polymer> membranes, and read with an <enzyme>-linked <secondary antibody>. A ChemiDoc MP imager and film recorded the same dilution-series blots. The target and an abundant reference <protein> had different usable response ranges; transferred total-<protein> imaging and the reference bands did not track loading equally well. This demonstrates the need for assay-specific response checks, rather than a universal conversion from darkness to amount. <A Defined Methodology for Reliable Quantification of Western Blot Data by Sean C. Taylor and others>[Taylor, Berkelman, Yadav and Hammond, Materials and Methods; Figures 2–4 and 5–7: transfer apparatus, film/imager comparison and loading normalization]{ref}

An illustrative response model separates background $b$, target amount $T$, retained fraction $q$, response scale $S$ and characteristic amount $K$:
$$
I(T)=b+S\frac{qT}{K+qT},\qquad S,K>0,\quad 0<q\leq1.
$$
This is an assumed model, not a fitted <Western blot> calibration. When $qT\ll K$, $I-b\approx(Sq/K)T$. When $qT\gg K$, $I-b$ approaches $S$. For two otherwise identical lanes, doubling $T$ gives the background-corrected <ratio>
$$
\frac{I(2T)-b}{I(T)-b}=\frac{2(K+qT)}{K+2qT}.
$$
At $qT=K$ the <ratio> is $4/3$, despite a true twofold amount change. Thus <Western blot normalization> cannot recover a lost amount difference merely by dividing two saturated readouts.

The membrane-disruption test was performed by <David D. Sabatini> and <Günter Blobel> in 1970. = Sanger's insulin sequencing experiment
{c}
{parent=Protein primary structure}

= Insulin sequencing experiment
{synonym}

The motivating question was experimental: how could one recover the order in a <protein> when breaking all its <peptide bonds> destroys that order? Frederick Sanger and colleagues used complementary tests on portions of <insulin>. The following is an explanation of their evidence, not a laboratory recipe.

First, mark ends before destroying the chains. Sanger used the colored reagent fluorodinitrobenzene, abbreviated FDNB, to label free amino groups. After acid <hydrolysis>, the attached label remained on the end-residue products. Separating these derivatives by their different movement through solvents and comparing them with reference derivatives made the labels readable. The <N-terminus> products identified <glycine> and <phenylalanine>. Sanger emphasized that finding a stable label and a way to separate its products were both essential. See https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1985/A1985ADE5900002.pdf[Sanger, Citation Classic (1985), discussing The free amino groups of insulin (1945)]. FDNB also labels suitable free amino groups on <amino acid side chains>, so a labeled product is not automatically an <N-terminus> product. See https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/sanger-lecture.pdf[Sanger, The Chemistry of Insulin, Nobel lecture (1958), pp. 546–547].

Next, separate chains and preserve local order. <Oxidation> with performic acid broke the <protein disulfide bonds>, allowing A and B chains to be isolated. Partial <hydrolysis> and <enzyme> treatments produced fragments; these were separated and their composition and local sequences characterized. <Insulin> is a <protein> <hormone>. Its mature form has two <polypeptide chains>; identifying their sequences and <protein disulfide bonds> provided the motivating example in <Sanger's insulin sequencing experiment>. A meal illustrates the <insulin> side of <blood glucose> <homeostasis>: rising <glucose> stimulates <beta cells> in the <pancreatic islets>, and released <insulin> favors uptake and storage of fuel. <Alpha cells> produce <glucagon>, while <delta cells> produce <somatostatin>, which inhibits release of both <insulin> and <glucagon>. A <hormone> changes <signal transduction>, not the identity of the fuel it regulates. Human mature <insulin> has 51 <amino acid residues> in two <polypeptide chains>. <Beta cells> process <proinsulin> to <insulin> and <C-peptide>, storing the products in <secretory granules> for <exocytosis>. Increased <glucose> <metabolism> raises the <ATP>-to-<ADP> <ratio>, tending to close <ATP-sensitive potassium channels>; <depolarization> and entry of <calcium> <ions> then trigger <exocytosis>. = Glucose clamp technique
{parent=Insulin}
{wiki}

<Glucose clamp technique: a method for quantifying insulin secretion and resistance. by R. A. DeFronzo and others> separates two questions. In a hyperglycemic clamp, variable <glucose> infusion holds <blood glucose> above baseline while sampled <insulin> reports the response. The 1979 article's abstract reports an early release burst followed by a slower rise. In a euglycemic <insulin> clamp, infused <insulin> is held elevated and variable <glucose> infusion holds <blood glucose> at baseline. Frequent blood sampling, a <glucose> analyzer, and controlled infusion apparatus make the imposed <concentration> a maintained condition rather than an uncontrolled consequence. <Glucose clamp technique: a method for quantifying insulin secretion and resistance. by R. A. DeFronzo and others>{ref}<Endotext/Assessing Insulin Sensitivity and Resistance in Humans>[pp. 4–6]{ref}

The maintained <glucose> level controls an important <confounder>: a change in <blood glucose> would itself change uptake and <hormone> release. At a <steady state>, <conservation of mass> gives uptake equal to infused supply plus endogenous production, after accounting for losses. Consequently, infusion alone estimates uptake only if endogenous production is negligible and the other balance assumptions hold. Labelled <glucose> measurements can estimate residual endogenous production; matching infused <insulin> across comparisons matters. Whole-body uptake cannot by itself identify the responding tissue, and plasma <insulin> reflects both release and removal. This interpretation uses the accessible 1979 abstract and the later authored methods chapter; the original article's full methods were not available for inspection.<Endotext/Assessing Insulin Sensitivity and Resistance in Humans>[pp. 4–6]{ref}

= Alpha helices
{synonym}

An <alpha helix> is a coiled <protein secondary structure> with a repeated pattern of <backbone hydrogen bonds>. In the standard pattern, the backbone group of <amino acid residue> $i$ bonds to that of residue $i+4$: for example, positions 2 and 6. This is an additional spatial interaction, not a replacement <peptide bond> between those positions. Pauling, Corey and Branson proposed the helical arrangement in their 1951 paper by reasoning from the geometry of small <molecules>. = Beta sheet
{parent=Protein secondary structure}
{wiki}
{title2=$\beta$}

A <beta sheet> consists of extended stretches of <protein backbone> lying alongside one another, joined by <backbone hydrogen bonds>. The stretches can belong to different portions of the same <polypeptide chain> or to different chains.

Each extended stretch is a <beta strand>. Neighboring <beta strands> pack side by side, with repeated <backbone hydrogen bonds> between their backbone $\mathrm{C=O}$ and $\mathrm{N-H}$ groups: the <oxygen> is a <hydrogen bond acceptor>, and the backbone <nitrogen> with its attached <hydrogen> provides a <hydrogen bond donor>. These are interactions of the shared <protein backbone>, not merely contacts between <amino acid side chains>. Unlike an <alpha helix>, a <beta sheet> joins adjacent extended stretches rather than coiling one stretch around an axis. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-4-proteins[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 3.4: backbone groups in secondary structure].

Read each <beta strand> in the sequence direction from the <N-terminus> toward the <C-terminus>, even when it is an internal part of a longer <polypeptide chain>. In a <parallel beta sheet>, neighboring <beta strands> run in the same N→C direction; in an <antiparallel beta sheet>, they run in opposite N→C directions. A three-strand example can therefore have arrows right–right–right or right–left–right. A <beta sheet> can also mix the two arrangements. Direction does not identify chain membership: a single <polypeptide chain> can fold back to supply several <beta strands>, and different <polypeptide chains> can contribute to one <beta sheet>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26830/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: beta-sheet strand arrangements].

The pleated appearance comes from the geometry of the <protein backbone>. Successive <amino acid residues> carry <amino acid side chains> on alternating faces of an ideal <beta strand>, conventionally drawn above and below the sheet. The attachment positions are the <amino acid alpha carbons>. Thus a six-position cartoon has groups 1, 3 and 5 on one face and 2, 4 and 6 on the other. This alternation is a spatial pattern, not a rule that alternating positions must contain identical <amino acids>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-4-proteins[Clark, Douglas and Choi: side chains above and below the pleats].

Real <beta sheets> commonly twist and curve; pleating does not make them perfectly flat plates. Which <beta strands> are neighbors, how connecting regions join them, and how their faces pack are features of the three-dimensional <protein structure>. An alternating pattern in the <protein primary structure> alone neither establishes a <beta sheet> nor specifies a unique fold independently of surroundings and interacting <molecules>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK6824/[Breda et al., Protein Structure, Modelling and Applications: extended strands, sheet twist and three-dimensional organization] and <protein folding>.

Why this arrangement is useful: repeated <backbone hydrogen bonds> organize extended chain regions into a framework that can contribute to the interior of a folded <protein>. <Amino acid side chains> and connecting loops give that framework chemical variety and can shape a <protein binding site> for a <ligand>. Its attractive feature is the combination of a reusable backbone pattern with variable exposed groups: similar <beta sheets> can support different recognition surfaces. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26830/[Alberts et al.: beta-sheet cores and loops that form binding sites].

Historically, Linus Pauling and Robert B. Corey published *The Pleated Sheet, a New Layer Configuration of Polypeptide Chains* in PNAS 37, 251–256 (1951). They reasoned from <peptide bond> geometry and possible <backbone hydrogen bonds>, proposing an idealized layer rather than measuring every modern <beta sheet>. Their initial model should not be identified with all present-day <beta sheet> geometries; they published further pleated-sheet configurations later in 1951. See https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/esvfg-ahe09[Pauling and Corey: original 1951 paper and scan], https://williams.chemistry.gatech.edu/course_Information/6572/papers/Pauling_PNAS-1951_sheet.pdf[accessible scan of the original paper]{ref}, and https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/hq2fj-hqw64[Pauling and Corey, Configurations of Polypeptide Chains With Favored Orientations Around Single Bonds: Two New Pleated Sheets, PNAS 37, 729–740 (1951)].

= Protein folding
{parent=Protein}
{wiki}

<Protein folding> is the process by which a <polypeptide chain> acquires an organized spatial arrangement. The interactions of its <amino acid residues> with one another and the surroundings favor some arrangements over others. <Protein folding> can bring chemically useful groups together at an <active site> without changing their order along the <protein backbone>. Its importance is practical: knowing a sequence alone does not tell us whether a sample has the structure needed for function. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9843/[Cooper: acquiring functional protein structure].

The <ribonuclease A refolding experiment> showed that an isolated <protein> could recover its native properties without a pre-existing folded template under suitable laboratory conditions. Anfinsen interpreted this through a thermodynamic hypothesis: the native arrangement minimizes the whole system's <Gibbs free energy> in the appropriate environment. His Nobel lecture was delivered on 11 December 1972 and published in Science in 1973. The specified environment is part of the claim, not a disposable qualification. See https://web.iitd.ac.in/~amittal/1973_Anfinsen_Science.pdf[Anfinsen, Principles that Govern the Folding of Protein Chains, Science 181, 223–230 (1973): Nobel lecture, especially p. 223].

This does not imply that every <protein> rapidly folds unaided. <Molecular chaperones> can assist <protein folding> and reduce <protein aggregation>; required partners, surroundings and time still matter. Goloubinoff and colleagues' 1989 reconstitution experiments demonstrated assistance from two <molecular chaperones> and <ATP> in recovering an active <enzyme> from unfolded chains. This is experimental evidence that assistance can matter even when the chains already have their specified sequences. See https://www.nature.com/articles/342884a0[Goloubinoff, Christeller, Gatenby and Lorimer, 1989: chaperone-assisted reconstitution of an active enzyme].

= Conformational entropy
{parent=Protein folding}
{title2=$S_{\rm conf}=k_B\ln\Omega$}

Conformational entropy measures the multiplicity of molecular conformations compatible with specified macroscopic constraints. For equally weighted accessible conformations it is $k_B\ln\Omega$; for probabilities $p_i$, it is $-k_B\sum_i p_i\ln p_i$. A folded <protein> generally has less chain conformational entropy than its unfolded ensemble, but folding can still lower total <Gibbs free energy> when solvent and interaction contributions are included.

= Ribonuclease A
{parent=Protein}

<Ribonuclease A> is an <enzyme> that catalyzes cleavage of <RNA>. The bovine pancreatic <ribonuclease A> studied by Anfinsen and colleagues has 124 <amino acid residues> and four internal <protein disulfide bonds>. = Ribonuclease A refolding experiment
{parent=Ribonuclease A}

Anfinsen, Haber, Sela and White's 1961 experiments began by treating bovine pancreatic <ribonuclease A> with <mercaptoethanol> in $8\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ <urea>. <Urea> promotes unfolding; <mercaptoethanol> breaks <protein disulfide bonds>. The <peptide bonds> along the chain remain intact. After separating the chain from those reagents, the authors allowed its reactive <sulfur> groups to form <protein disulfide bonds> in the presence of <molecular oxygen>, at <pH> $8.2$ and <temperature> $23$–$24\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C$. They examined <protein> <concentrations> of $0.1$ and $2.0\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$. Measurements showed recovery of native properties under these conditions; the isolated major component had normal activity toward both <RNA> and a synthetic <substrate>. See https://williams.chemistry.gatech.edu/course_Information/6572_fall_2021/papers/ANFINSEN_1961.pdf[Anfinsen, Haber, Sela and White, The Kinetics of Formation of Native Ribonuclease during Oxidation of the Reduced Polypeptide Chain, PNAS 47, 1309–1314 (1961), pp. 1309–1311].

The observation supports the sufficiency of that intact sequence to recover native <protein structure> in the tested surroundings without a folded template. It does not establish that every <protein> refolds, that the original living <biological cell> needed no assistance, or that activity always returns immediately. <Protein folding>, forming <protein disulfide bonds> and recovering an <active site> are related processes rather than interchangeable measurements.

Anfinsen's lecture also discusses forming <protein disulfide bonds> while the chain remains in concentrated <urea>. This gave mostly inactive material; after removing <urea>, a small amount of <mercaptoethanol> permitted rearrangement toward the native form. As an exact combinatorial check, eight labeled reactive <sulfur> groups have $7\times5\times3\times1=105$ ways to be paired into four <protein disulfide bonds>: choose the partner of one group, then repeat with the unpaired groups. Only one pairing is the native one. Equal counting does not mean that all pairings are equally likely experimentally, nor that actual <protein folding> searches them uniformly. See https://web.iitd.ac.in/~amittal/1973_Anfinsen_Science.pdf[Anfinsen's Nobel lecture, 1973 publication: scrambled ribonuclease and the 105 pairings, p. 223].

= Receptor
{synonym}

A biochemical <receptor> is a <protein> that recognizes a signal and couples its detection to a biological effect. Its binding partner is a <ligand>; recognition depends on compatible shape and chemical interactions at a binding site within the <protein structure>. Specificity is selective recognition, not a guarantee that only one <ligand> can bind. Binding also need not activate the <receptor>: a competing <ligand> may occupy it while preventing activation by another <ligand>. A <cell-surface receptor> can detect a <hydrophilic> <ligand> without transporting it through the <cell membrane>. In contrast, some membrane-permeable signals reach <intracellular receptors> that regulate <gene expression>. Location and downstream coupling are separate distinctions: an <ionotropic receptor> directly gates an <ion channel>, whereas a <metabotropic receptor> works through intracellular <signal transduction>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-1-signaling-molecules-and-cellular-receptors[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.1: receptor locations and mechanisms].

<Receptor binding occupancy> quantifies recognition separately from response. A <dissociation constant> measures binding in a specified equilibrium model; it does not by itself predict the cellular effect. This separation is useful when comparing signals that bind equally well but produce different responses, or when interpreting a binding experiment alongside a functional measurement.

= Ionotropic receptor
{parent=Receptor (biochemistry)}

An <ionotropic receptor> is a <receptor> in which binding of a <ligand> directly gates an <ion channel> within the same <protein> complex. The binding site detects the chemical signal; the pore supplies a path across the <cell membrane>. At a <chemical synapse>, a <neurotransmitter> therefore changes <synaptic conductance> without an obligatory intervening <G protein> or <second messenger>. <Neuroscience, 2nd edition edited by D. Purves and others/Two Families of Postsynaptic Receptors>{ref}

A familiar example is the nicotinic <acetylcholine> <receptor> of skeletal muscle: <acetylcholine> binding opens a pore passing <cations>, including <sodium> and <potassium>. The muscarinic <acetylcholine> <receptor> instead uses intracellular <signal transduction>; the same <neurotransmitter> can engage different mechanisms. Direct gating does not mean that an <ionotropic receptor> pumps <ions>: <electrochemical gradients> drive passage through the open <ion channel>. Nor does “ionotropic” specify excitation. The <reversal potential>, initial <membrane potential> and other conductances determine whether the effect favors an <action potential>, causes <hyperpolarization>, or supplies <shunting inhibition>. <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes>{ref}

A concrete electrical approximation separates opening from drive. Let $N$ identical <ion channels> each have open-channel <electrical conductance> $\gamma$ and opening <probability> $p(t)$. At fixed <membrane potential> $V$, with common <reversal potential> $E_{\mathrm{rev}}$, the <expected value> of inward-positive <electric current> is
$$
I_{\mathrm{in}}(t)=N\gamma p(t)(E_{\mathrm{rev}}-V).
$$
For stipulated values $N=100$, $\gamma=20\,\mathrm{pS}$, $p=0.2$, $V=-60\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $E_{\mathrm{rev}}=0$, the mean open count is $20$, the population <electrical conductance> is $0.4\,\mathrm{nS}$ and $I_{\mathrm{in}}=24\,\mathrm{pA}$. One open <ion channel> carries $1.2\,\mathrm{pA}$. These are illustrative values, not measurements. At $V=E_{\mathrm{rev}}$, the net <electric current> vanishes despite open <ion channels>; neither binding nor opening alone determines the readout. The approximation omits changes in <ion> <concentrations> and voltage-dependent open-channel <electrical conductance>.

A motivating experiment made the elementary electrical events visible. As Bert Sakmann recounts, chronically denervated frog muscle fibers had <acetylcholine>-sensitive <receptors> spread beyond the original nerve contact. Mild <enzyme> treatment exposed the <cell membrane>; a polished glass pipette sealed over a small patch to reduce background noise. Two intracellular electrodes held the local <membrane potential> through <voltage clamp>, while the patch pipette recorded tiny <electric currents>. Applying suberyldicholine, a <ligand> that activates the <acetylcholine> <receptor> and prolongs openings, revealed abrupt, approximately rectangular current events. Their amplitudes agreed reasonably with estimates from whole-membrane current fluctuations. This established discrete opening and closing events; it did not make every <ligand> response instantaneous. Background noise and finite recording bandwidth concealed sufficiently small or brief events.<Elementary Steps in Synaptic Transmission Revealed by Currents through Single Ion Channels by Bert Sakmann>[pp. 33–35]{ref}

Recording an intact membrane patch alone does not exclude intracellular relays. Tank, Huganir, Greengard and Webb supplied a complementary isolation test in 1983: they purified nicotinic <acetylcholine> <receptors> from an electric ray's electric organ, incorporated them into artificial <lipid> membranes, and fused small vesicles into larger vesicles by freezing and thawing. Sealed glass-pipette recordings from isolated membrane patches resolved functional <ion channels> with properties resembling those in muscle. Removing the intact <cytoplasm> and its signaling machinery supports the sufficiency of the <receptor> complex for channel function; an artificial <lipid> environment still cannot reproduce every native regulatory interaction.<Patch-recorded single-channel currents of the purified and reconstituted Torpedo acetylcholine receptor by D. W. Tank and others>[abstract]{ref}

Direct gating supports millisecond communication at many <chemical synapses>. Response duration also depends on <neurotransmitter clearance>, binding and channel-state transitions. Continued <ligand> exposure can prolong activity. Classify an <ionotropic receptor> by its direct connection between recognition and pore gating, rather than a universal duration cutoff. <Neuroscience, 2nd edition edited by D. Purves and others/Two Families of Postsynaptic Receptors>{ref}

= Metabotropic receptor
{parent=Receptor (biochemistry)}
{wiki}

A <metabotropic receptor> couples <ligand> recognition to intracellular <signal transduction>, which can regulate a separate <ion channel>, alter <enzyme> activity or change <gene expression>. In the usual <neurotransmitter> context, <metabotropic receptors> are <G protein-coupled receptors>: receptor activation is relayed by a <G protein>. There need not be a change in <membrane potential> at all. <Neuroscience, 2nd edition edited by D. Purves and others/Two Families of Postsynaptic Receptors>{ref}

Two routes should be distinguished. An activated <G protein> can regulate an <ion channel> locally at the <cell membrane>, without an obligatory diffusible <second messenger>. Alternatively, it can regulate an <enzyme> that changes the availability of a <second messenger>, such as <cAMP> or <inositol trisphosphate>. Downstream <proteins> then change channel opening, <phosphorylation> or other cellular activities. The <receptor>, <G protein>, <second messenger> and final target are distinct components; an activated <G protein> is a relay <protein>, rather than itself a small-molecule <second messenger>.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/General Principles of Cell Communication>[Figure 15-15]{ref}

Do not turn this useful <chemical synapse> classification into the claim that every <receptor> is a <GPCR>, or that every indirect response must pass through a <G protein>. For example, the <insulin> <receptor> is an <enzyme>-linked <cell-surface receptor> whose intracellular catalytic activity initiates <protein> <phosphorylation>. <Intracellular receptors> supply another category. “Metabotropic” commonly names the <GPCR> branch in <neuroscience>; the broader idea of biochemical coupling does not make all non-channel <receptors> structurally identical. <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Signaling through Enzyme-Linked Cell-Surface Receptors>{ref}

Separating recognition from execution allows <signal amplification> and branching. A persistently activated <receptor> can activate several relay <proteins>; a regulated <enzyme> can produce many <second messenger> <molecules>. As an explicitly invented count example, suppose one activated <receptor> activates four relay <proteins> during one interval, and each relay activates a distinct <enzyme> that produces fifteen <second messenger> <molecules> during the next interval. With sufficient substrates and no loss, this gives $4\times15=60$ <molecules>. Those counts do not specify a physiological gain or response duration. A directly gated <ion channel> also passes many <ions> while open, so amplification is not an exclusive property of <metabotropic receptors>: always name the input and output being compared. <The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Pathways of Intracellular Signal Transduction>{ref}

A particularly instructive electrophysiology experiment separated a chemical trigger from the <ion channel> carrying its eventual <electric current>. In 1987, Hirono, Ito and Sugiyama injected <messenger RNA> from rat brain into frog egg cells, then tested acquired responses to neurotensin and muscarinic <acetylcholine> stimulation. Under <voltage clamp> at $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$, neurotensin elicited transient, oscillatory inward <electric currents> after a delay of several tens of seconds. This delay refers to that response and preparation. <Reversal potential> measurements implicated increased permeability to chloride <anions>, rather than a pore specific to the applied <ligand>. Both neurotensin and <acetylcholine> responses were inhibited by pertussis toxin, which interferes with susceptible <G proteins>, and by intracellular EGTA, which binds free <calcium>. Injecting <inositol trisphosphate> mimicked the responses; a large prior dose of <inositol trisphosphate> reduced subsequent responses to either extracellular signal. These interventions implicate a shared intracellular relay and <calcium>-dependent channel opening.<Neurotensin and acetylcholine evoke common responses in frog oocytes injected with rat brain messenger ribonucleic acid by C. Hirono and others>[abstract]{ref}

The informative comparison with the nicotinic <acetylcholine> <receptor> experiment is mechanistic: an extracellular <ligand> can produce an inward <electric current> either by directly gating the pore or by mobilizing an intracellular <second messenger> and stored <calcium> that activate another pore. Intracellular <inositol trisphosphate> bypasses reception, whereas EGTA tests the need for free <calcium>. However, neither inhibition nor mimicry alone identifies every molecular connection: a perturbation can affect several targets, and an injected mediator can activate a pathway without proving that it is the only natural route. The egg cell's endogenous machinery and the mixed <messenger RNA> preparation also limit extrapolation to an intact <neuron>. The experiment establishes functional coupling in its preparation, rather than the exact molecular composition or timing of every <metabotropic receptor>.

Even <G protein>-dependent channel regulation need not involve a lengthy <second messenger> cascade. Pfaffinger and colleagues reported in 1985 that <acetylcholine>-evoked <potassium> channel activation in embryonic chick atrial cells required intracellular GTP, a <guanine>-containing <nucleotide>, and was abolished by pertussis-toxin pretreatment. Changes in cyclic <nucleotides>, including <cAMP>, did not account for the channel response. The report discusses intrinsic activation delays of roughly $30$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$, illustrating relatively rapid <metabotropic receptor> action. These observations support a <G protein> coupling step without cyclic-nucleotide <second messengers>; they do not by themselves exclude every possible intermediary or prove the specific subunit-channel interaction.<GTP-binding proteins couple cardiac muscarinic receptors to a K channel by P. J. Pfaffinger and others>[abstract]{ref}

A small timing model makes the extra steps visible without inventing an experimental trace. Let $u(t)$ jump from zero to one at $t=0$ and remain on. For a direct-gating model output $d$, one relay variable $x$, and its downstream output $y$, take zero <initial conditions> and
$$
\tau_d\frac{dd}{dt}=u-d,\qquad \tau_x\frac{dx}{dt}=u-x,\qquad \tau_y\frac{dy}{dt}=x-y.
$$
All outputs are normalized <dimensionless variables>; they are neither recorded <electric currents> nor fitted <concentrations>. With illustrative <time constants> $\tau_d=2\,\mathrm{ms}$, $\tau_x=20\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $\tau_y=100\,\mathrm{ms}$, their <exponential function> solutions for $t\geq0$ are
$$
d(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau_d},\qquad x(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau_x},\qquad y(t)=1-\frac{\tau_y e^{-t/\tau_y}-\tau_x e^{-t/\tau_x}}{\tau_y-\tau_x}.
$$
Initially $d'(0)=1/\tau_d$, while $y'(0)=0$ because its relay must first build up. Both outputs eventually approach one. Equal normalized final values do not imply equal biological amplitudes or absence of <signal amplification>; normalization deliberately removes that question.

Actual kinetics also depend on <ligand> delivery, relay deactivation, <neurotransmitter clearance> and the final target. A local <G protein> route can act promptly, while an <ionotropic receptor> can remain active during continued exposure or feed a longer-lasting intracellular response. The beauty of <metabotropic receptor> coupling is its flexibility: the same recognition event can adjust electrical responsiveness and other cellular tasks through different targets. To classify an observed response, combine timing with molecular identity and controlled perturbations.

{wiki}

The mature <insulin receptor> already contains two extracellular $\alpha$ chains and two membrane-spanning $\beta$ chains, joined through <protein disulfide bonds>, before <insulin> binds. <Insulin> induces rearrangement of this $(\alpha\beta)_2$ assembly. Partner-mediated <phosphorylation> of the inhibitory activation loop permits increased <protein kinase> activity. Ligand-induced creation of a previously absent dimer is therefore an unsuitable universal explanation.<Cell signaling by receptor-tyrosine kinases by Mark A Lemmon and Joseph Schlessinger>[“Mechanisms of Receptor Activation” and “TKD autoinhibition by the activation loop: insulin and FGF receptors”, Figure 2E]{ref}

= Receptor phosphorylation assay
{parent=Receptor tyrosine kinase}

A <receptor phosphorylation assay> asks whether, and how much, a specified <receptor> is modified by <phosphorylation> under defined conditions. It is distinct from a binding assay for <receptor binding occupancy>, a test of <protein kinase> activity on a supplied <substrate>, and a test of downstream <signal transduction>. Those measurements answer related but different questions.

For a <Western blot> implementation, compare matched samples with <ligand> omitted or added, detect a specified phosphorylated <epitope>, and separately detect total <receptor> abundance. Preserve the sampled state by stopping further <enzyme> reactions during sample preparation; otherwise <protein kinase> or <protein phosphatase> activity after sampling can change the result. A general anti-<phosphotyrosine residue> <antibody> recognizes many modified <proteins>; a site-selective <antibody> gives a narrower readout, but still needs <Western blot antibody specificity controls>.

Reason through the controls before interpreting the difference. Loss of the proposed signal after receptor-specific <gene deletion> supports <protein> identity. Treatment with a <protein phosphatase>, alongside preservation of the total-<receptor> signal, tests phosphate-dependent recognition. <Western blot loading controls> and <Western blot transfer controls> address sample recovery; a usable detection range addresses <Western blot response saturation>. Lower signal after perturbing <protein kinase> activity supports dependence on that perturbation, but alternative targets and altered <receptor> abundance can complicate the inference. These are proposed assay comparisons, not outcomes attributed to the historical membrane experiment.

An original idealized measurement model explains the normalization. Let $N$ be the retained number of <receptor> <molecules>, $f$ the fraction carrying the selected modified <epitope>, and $c_p,c_t>0$ fixed detection gains. After subtracting their respective backgrounds, suppose the phosphorylated and total-<receptor> signals lie in a linear response range:
$$
A=c_pNf,\qquad B=c_tN,\qquad \frac{A}{B}=\frac{c_p}{c_t}f.
$$
Dividing by total <receptor> cancels $N$ under these assumptions. It differs from total-<protein> <Western blot normalization>, whose denominator includes many other <proteins>. For invented values $c_p=c_t=1$, samples with $(N,f)=(100,0.2)$ and $(200,0.2)$ give $A=20$ and $40$, but both give $A/B=0.2$. Twice the phospho signal here means twice the retained <receptor> amount, with no change in fractional modification. Unequal recovery, changed <epitope> accessibility, nonzero uncorrected background or <Western blot response saturation> invalidates the cancellation. Unknown relative gains prevent reading an absolute modified fraction directly from the <ratio>.

= Ligand-dependent membrane phosphorylation experiment
{parent=Receptor phosphorylation assay}

A motivating experiment used isolated membranes from A-431 <biological cells>, which supplied abundant <EGFR>. Separate membrane aliquots were incubated at $0\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C$ with <ATP> carrying radioactive <phosphorus> in its terminal <phosphate> group, plus <magnesium> or <manganese> <ions>. Radioactivity in material recovered by <chemical acid>-induced <precipitation (chemistry)> supplied the readout. Adding <EGF> beforehand enhanced incorporation relative to the unstimulated preparation; this placed a ligand-dependent biochemical response in a cell-free membrane system.<Epidermal Growth Factor by Stanley Cohen>[Cohen, Epidermal Growth Factor, printed p. 339: A-431 membrane aliquots, terminally labeled ATP, divalent ions and trichloroacetic acid-insoluble incorporation]{ref}

The follow-up comparisons separated increased addition from suppressed removal: <phosphate> removal was rapid but unaffected by <EGF>, and anti-<EGF> <antibody> reversed activation by removing the <ligand>. These observations support reversible regulation of membrane-associated <protein kinase> activity.<Epidermal Growth Factor by Stanley Cohen>[printed p. 341, numbered observations 1–4: rapid activation, dephosphorylation, lack of soluble kinase release and anti-EGF reversal]{ref} Total incorporation alone cannot identify which <protein> or <amino acid residue> received the label, prove intrinsic receptor <catalysis>, or predict an intact <biological cell>'s response.

The same account records an instructive identification error: receptor <phosphorylation> was initially assigned to the wrong <amino acid residue> because released modified <amino acids> migrated together in the electrophoretic <electric field>. Reanalysis identified <phosphotyrosine residues>. Retained radioactivity is evidence about the assay's separation and detection, not unique chemical identity.<Epidermal Growth Factor by Stanley Cohen>[printed p. 341: co-migration in the electrophoretic system and subsequent identification of tyrosine phosphorylation]{ref}

= Reversible receptor phosphorylation model
{parent=Receptor tyrosine kinase}

<Protein kinase> activity adds modification, while <protein tyrosine phosphatase> activity can remove it. An original one-site model shows why a <receptor phosphorylation assay> cannot determine either process from a single modified fraction. Let $f(t)\in[0,1]$ be the fraction of a fixed <receptor> population carrying one selected <phosphotyrosine residue>. Stipulate constant effective first-order <rate constants> $k_+,k_->0$, with <ATP> availability absorbed into $k_+$, and no <receptor> synthesis, degradation or trafficking:
$$
\frac{df}{dt}=k_+(1-f)-k_-f,\qquad
f_* = \frac{k_+}{k_++k_-},\qquad
\tau=\frac{1}{k_++k_-}.
$$
Here $f_*$ is the <steady state> fraction and $\tau$ the <time constant>. The <ordinary differential equation> has the <exponential function> solution
$$
f(t)=f_*+(f(0)-f_*)e^{-t/\tau}.
$$
The balance equation and a single relaxation step give this result; it is an illustrative model, rather than a fit or a claim about every <RTK>.

With stipulated <rate constants> $(k_+,k_-)=(2,1)\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$, $f_*=2/3$ and $\tau=1/3\,\mathrm{min}$. Doubling both <rate constants> leaves $f_*=2/3$ but changes $\tau$ to $1/6\,\mathrm{min}$. The balanced <phosphate> turnover per site,
$$
J_*=k_+(1-f_*)=k_-f_*=\frac{k_+k_-}{k_++k_-},
$$
doubles from $2/3$ to $4/3\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$. Identical <steady state> modification therefore permits different catalytic fluxes. Removing the addition term instead gives $f(t)=f(0)e^{-k_-t}$; this illustrates why a perturbation that stops <protein kinase> activity can expose removal dynamics, subject to the model's unchanged-removal assumption. A maintained <steady state> with ongoing <ATP> consumption is not <chemical equilibrium>.

= Receptor binding occupancy
{parent=Receptor (biochemistry)}
{title2=$\theta$}

<Receptor binding occupancy> is the fraction of binding sites occupied by a <ligand>. Consider reversible, noncooperative binding at <chemical equilibrium>, with homogeneous independent sites: every site has the same binding properties, and binding at one site does not change binding at another. Let $L$ denote free <ligand>, $R$ an unoccupied <receptor> site and $RL$ the bound complex. In this dilute <concentration> model,
$$
L+R\rightleftharpoons RL,\qquad K_d=\frac{[L][R]}{[RL]},\qquad R_T=[R]+[RL],\qquad \theta=\frac{[RL]}{R_T}=\frac{[L]}{K_d+[L]}.
$$
Square brackets denote <concentrations>; $R_T$ is the total site <concentration>. Here the <dissociation constant> $K_d$ has <concentration> units. Use free <ligand> in excess so binding negligibly depletes it; only under that approximation may the supplied total <ligand> <concentration> substitute for $[L]$. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5521016/[Analysis of Receptor–Ligand Interactions: equilibrium binding and saturation].

For an illustrative <dissociation constant> $K_d=10\,\mathrm{nM}$, where $1\,\mathrm{nM}=10^{-9}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, free <ligand> <concentrations> $1$, $10$ and $100\,\mathrm{nM}$ give $\theta=1/11\approx0.091$, $\theta=1/2=0.5$ and $\theta=10/11\approx0.909$, respectively. At $[L]=K_d$, exactly half the sites are occupied in this model. Raising $[L]$ tenfold from $10$ to $100\,\mathrm{nM}$ raises occupancy from $50\%$ to about $91\%$, rather than tenfold: the finite pool of sites causes saturation.

\Image[receptor-binding-occupancy.png]
{title=<Receptor binding occupancy> for a toy <dissociation constant> of $10\,\mathrm{nM}$}
{description=The same independent-site <chemical equilibrium> model on linear and logarithmic <ligand> <concentration> axes. Marked examples are $1/11$, $1/2$ and $10/11$. The logarithmic axis changes the apparent curve shape without introducing cooperative binding. The vertical axis is fraction occupied, not cellular response.}

A short derivation uses the <law of mass action>: at <chemical equilibrium>, association balances dissociation, giving $[RL]=[L][R]/K_d$. Substitute this into $\theta=[RL]/([R]+[RL])$ and cancel $[R]$ to obtain the displayed formula. The limiting values follow directly: zero free <ligand> gives zero occupancy, and increasing $[L]$ far above $K_d$ drives occupancy toward one.

<Receptor binding occupancy> is not automatically the fraction of maximal response. <Signal amplification>, differing effects of bound <ligands>, and downstream saturation can change the relationship. Consequently, $[L]=K_d$ specifies half occupancy here; it does not universally specify either half-maximal or maximal cellular response. These numbers describe a toy binding model, not measured responses of every <receptor>. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11267233/[Higham and Colquhoun, 2024: the affinity–efficacy problem].
