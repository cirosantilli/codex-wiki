= Genetics
{wiki}

<Genetics> studies how <genes> are inherited, how their <alleles> vary, and how that variation contributes to <phenotypes>. Its central distinction is between an inherited description, the <genotype>, and what an <organism> actually expresses, the <phenotype>. Following <alleles> through a <pedigree> answers a family-level question; following <allele frequencies> through generations leads to <population genetics> and <evolution>.

The beauty of <Mendelian segregation> is that discrete inherited copies can generate predictable statistical patterns without making every offspring identical. A single <genetic locus> already connects <meiosis>, <probability> and observable variation. The <Hardy-Weinberg principle> then supplies a population baseline: it separates reshuffling inherited copies from processes such as <natural selection> that change their frequencies. These simple models are useful precisely because their assumptions can be stated and checked.

An offspring or descendant combining ancestry from distinguishable parental lineages. A <hybrid> can be viable and fertile; its existence alone neither proves nor disproves strong <reproductive isolation> in natural <biological populations>.

Crossing between genetically distinct lineages or populations. It can introduce combinations of <alleles> into descendants and contribute to <gene flow> or <hybrid speciation>. This process is distinct from <DNA hybridization>, the pairing of complementary nucleic-acid strands.

= Genetic rescue experiment
{parent=Genetics}

Restoration of a perturbed phenotype by a specified genetic intervention. Controls must separate restored target function from nonspecific intervention effects.

= Candidate gene
{parent=Genetics}

A gene proposed to influence a trait on functional, positional or comparative grounds. Candidate status is not a demonstration of causation.

= Microsatellite
{parent=Genetics}

A short tandem repeat in <DNA>. Repeat-length changes can arise through <DNA polymerase slippage>, making both biological instability and amplification artifacts relevant to measurement.

= Genotype-phenotype linkage
{parent=Genetics}

<Genotype-phenotype linkage> in experimental selection keeps the sequence encoding a candidate associated with the function used to select it. Clonal compartments or display particles carrying the encoding DNA prevent active products from indiscriminately rescuing unrelated inactive variants.

= Transposable element
{parent=Genetics}

A genetic element capable of moving or producing copies that insert at new positions in a <genome>. Repression of its expression can prevent mobilization before insertional damage occurs.

= Hybrid dysgenesis
{parent=Transposable element}

A cross-dependent syndrome of <germline> defects caused in classic <Drosophila> systems by failure to control inherited <transposable elements>. Reciprocal crosses can differ because of maternal <piRNA> inheritance.

= Retrotransposon
{parent=Transposable element}

A mobile element whose replication involves an RNA intermediate.

= Endogenous retrovirus
{parent=Transposable element}

A retroviral sequence inherited as part of the host <genome>. Some families retain mobilization capacity, whereas others are inactive remnants.

= Epigenetic inheritance
{parent=Genetics}

Transmission of a regulatory state beyond the initiating event without requiring a change to the underlying <DNA> sequence. Persistence over time must be distinguished from continued exposure to the original inducing signal.

= Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance
{parent=Epigenetic inheritance}

Transmission of an epigenetic effect beyond directly exposed generations, after excluding alternative explanations such as sequence change and parental provisioning.

= Epigenetic state
{parent=Epigenetic inheritance}

A regulatory state associated with <chromatin>, DNA modification or a self-maintaining regulatory process. Persistence through cell divisions and transmission through sexual reproduction are distinct properties.

= Genetic variation
{parent=Genetics}

Inherited differences among members of a <biological population>, including alternative <alleles> and combinations of <alleles>. Their phenotypic consequences depend on <genetic background> and environment.

= Common garden experiment
{parent=Genetics}

Comparison of lineages raised under standardized conditions. Replicated families and randomized rearing help distinguish inherited differences from immediate environmental effects; persistent maternal effects require additional controls.

= Diminishing-returns epistasis
{parent=Genetics}

<epistasis> in which the benefit of a <mutation> tends to be smaller in fitter <genetic backgrounds>. Its measured magnitude depends on the chosen scale of <genotype fitness>.

= Polymorphism
{parent=Genetics}

Coexistence of distinct inherited variants in a <biological population>. Opposing selective effects do not alone guarantee persistence: a protected <polymorphism> requires the relevant alternatives to increase when rare under the specified model.

= Gene dosage
{parent=Genetics}

The number of copies of a <gene> available in the specified <biological cell> or <genotype>. A dosage change can alter an outcome even before duplicated copies diverge in function.

= Heterogametic sex
{parent=Genetics}

The sex producing distinct classes of <gametes> with respect to the <sex chromosomes>, as with XY males or ZW females in familiar systems.

= Epistasis
{parent=Genetics}

Dependence of the effect of one genetic change on another. Double-mutant and bypass experiments can order functional requirements, but phenotype suppression alone does not prove a direct molecular interaction.

= Genetic bypass experiment
{parent=Epistasis}

Testing whether forced activity of a downstream candidate restores an output after another <gene> is impaired. Rescue supports functional sufficiency in the tested context, but parallel routes and overexpression can also bypass a defect.

= Sign epistasis
{parent=Epistasis}

An interaction in which the sign of a genetic change’s fitness effect depends on the genetic background. It constrains which steps increase fitness along an <adaptive walk>.

= Genetic mosaic
{parent=Genetics}

An organism or tissue containing cells with different <genotypes>. Marked clones can separate the tissue requiring a gene product from tissues responding to it.

= Biological strain
{synonym}

A <biological strain> is a maintained variant or lineage within a <species>. Naming the <species> alone does not specify a laboratory <genotype> or its <genetic background>.

= Genetic background
{parent=Genetics}

The <genetic background> is the rest of the <genotype> against which a focal <allele> or <gene deletion> is studied. Other <genes> can influence the resulting <phenotype>, so comparisons should specify which background is held fixed.

= Functional complementation
{parent=Genetics}

<Functional complementation> is restoration of a specified function or <phenotype> by introducing a <gene> into a deficient recipient. In the <yeast cross-species complementation experiment>, the restored property is growth without supplied <methionine>, under the assay conditions. Rescue need not restore every property of the original <genotype>.

= Complementation testing
{parent=Functional complementation}

A genetic test asking whether two mutant genomes together restore a function. Failure of recessive alleles to complement can support a shared affected <genetic locus>, subject to exceptions such as interacting loci and deletions.

= Ploidy
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}
{title2=$n,2n,\ldots$}

<Ploidy> counts complete sets of <chromosomes> in a <cell nucleus>. Write $n$ for the number of <chromosomes> in one set: <haploid> means one set, <diploid> two. <DNA replication> produces <sister chromatids> without changing <ploidy>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/Cell Division>[Biology 2e, §10.1, Genomic DNA; Eukaryotic Chromosomal Structure and Compaction, final paragraph]{ref}

Consider an illustrative <species> with $2n=6$, hence $n=3$. One set contains three distinguishable <chromosome> types, $A,B,D$; the <diploid> <cell nucleus> contains two <homologous chromosomes> of each type. Labels describe <chromosome> identity, not particular <alleles>. Let $C$ be the <nuclear DNA content> of one unreplicated set. All entries below refer to one completed product of division, or the starting <cell nucleus>, rather than the sum over products. Here “<chromatid> count” includes the single <DNA> copy in each unreplicated <chromosome>.

The arithmetic follows the allocation rules. <DNA replication> replaces each of six single copies with two associated <sister chromatids>: $6\times2=12$ copies still grouped as six <chromosomes>. <Meiosis I> allocates one <homologous chromosome> of every type to each product: three replicated <chromosomes>, with $3\times2=6$ copies. <Meiosis II> allocates one copy from each pair of <sister chromatids> to each final product: three single-copy <chromosomes>. Compare <meiotic DNA content> for the general symbolic bookkeeping. <Chromosomal crossover> can change the <alleles> on a <chromatid> without changing these counts when exchanges preserve material.

Keeping <ploidy>, <chromosome> count and <nuclear DNA content> separate makes the beauty of <meiosis> explicit: copying supplies material for two divisions, while sorting determines which complete sets each product inherits. It also prevents interpreting a doubled <DNA> signal as evidence of an extra <homologous chromosome> set.

= Haploid
{parent=Ploidy}
{title2=$n$}

A <haploid> <cell nucleus> has one complete set of <chromosomes>. For the stipulated $n=3$ example, both products after <meiosis I> and products after <meiosis II> are <haploid>; their <nuclear DNA contents> differ because the former retain associated <sister chromatids>. <Haploid> therefore does not mean “unreplicated”.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Process of Meiosis>[Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others; §11.1, Telophase I and Cytokinesis, final two paragraphs; Meiosis II, opening paragraph]{ref}

= Diploid
{parent=Ploidy}
{title2=$2n$}

Diploid <biological cells> have two sets of <chromosomes>. In the ordinary one-locus inheritance model, a <diploid> <genotype> therefore contains two <allele> copies at each <genetic locus>.

Two <homologous chromosomes> of a given type are distinct inherited members of the two sets; the two <sister chromatids> of one replicated <chromosome> are its copied members. In the stipulated $2n=6$ example, a <diploid> <G2 phase> <cell nucleus> has six replicated <chromosomes>, not twelve sets or twelve independently counted <chromosomes>.

= Nuclear DNA content
{c}
{parent=Ploidy}
{title2=$C,2C,4C$}

<Nuclear DNA content> is the amount of <DNA> in a specified <cell nucleus>. Define $1C$ relative to one unreplicated <haploid> set of the chosen <species>. It includes coding and <non-coding DNA> and excludes <DNA> outside the <cell nucleus>, such as a <mitochondrion>'s <genome>. $C$ is an amount, whereas $n$ is a <chromosome> count; neither fixes the other across different <species>.

For an original numerical model, assign the three <chromosome> types unreplicated <DNA> amounts of 3, 2 and 1 arbitrary units. Then $C=3+2+1=6$ units: <diploid> <G1 phase> contains 12 units, <G2 phase> 24, each completed <meiosis I> product 12, and each completed <meiosis II> product 6. The totals $2\times12=24$ and $4\times6=24$ check conservation after copying. Equal <chromosome> counts alone would not justify equal <DNA> amounts if the sets had different sequence lengths.

\Image[nuclear-dna-content-versus-ploidy.png]
{title=Illustrative <nuclear DNA content> versus <ploidy>: the same $2C$ amount can belong to different set counts}
{description=Original stipulated states and allocation paths, not measured signals or a fitted <histogram>. <DNA replication> moves upward without changing <ploidy>; <meiosis I> reduces both axes; <meiosis II> halves <nuclear DNA content> while retaining <haploid> <ploidy>. The right panel isolates the ambiguous $2C$ amount.}

How can an experiment measure the amount independently of <chromosome> counting? UCL's https://www.ucl.ac.uk/child-health/sites/child_health/files/facilities-flowcyt-pi_cell_cycle.pdf_.pdf[Cell Cycle Analysis by Propidium Iodide Staining], pp. 1–2, specifies a <flow cytometric DNA-content assay>. Washed <biological cells> receive cold 70% <ethanol> dropwise while vortexing, then at least 30 minutes on ice. After centrifugation and two washes, <ribonuclease A> removes <RNA> that would also bind the dye. <Propidium iodide> stains the preparation. A <flow cytometry> instrument such as the listed BD LSR II records individual-event <fluorescence> on a linear scale. Signal area versus height or width excludes doublets and clumps; acquisition is below 400 events per second, retaining at least 10,000 single-cell events. These are protocol settings, not measured results.<Cell Cycle Analysis by Propidium Iodide Staining by UCL Institute of Child Health Flow Cytometry Core Facility>[pp. 1–2, Materials, Equipment, Procedure steps 1–9, and Flow analysis]{ref}

For a <ploidy> comparison, add a known <diploid> <G1 phase> reference processed with the same staining and detector settings, and an unstained background control. These are proposed comparison controls, beyond the protocol's clump-exclusion procedure. Proportional <fluorescence> then estimates relative <nuclear DNA content>; it does not count <chromosomes>. A $2C$ event could be a <diploid> <G1 phase> product or a replicated <haploid> product; $4C$ could be replicated <diploid> material or an unreplicated four-set <cell nucleus>. Peaks alone therefore cannot establish <ploidy> or <cell cycle> stage without a reference and biological context. Uneven staining, residual <RNA> and unresolved doublets limit this inference; two $2C$ particles can mimic one $4C$ event.

= Random mating
{synonym}

Panmixia is random mating within a breeding population, without systematic choice by genotype or phenotype. In a homogeneous genetic model it makes the two parental gamete draws independent, yielding the <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium> genotype probabilities $p_i^2$ and $2p_ip_j$. It is a modelling assumption; <assortative mating> and <population stratification> are distinct ways a population can depart from it.

= Mutation
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A mutation changes inherited genetic sequence. Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, every mutation occurs at a previously unmutated position, so mutations below the sample's common ancestor correspond to <segregating sites>.

More precisely, a <mutation> changes a genetic <nucleotide sequence>; in cellular <organisms> this is a <DNA> sequence. An altered sequence can be copied by <DNA replication> into descendant <biological cells>, changing their <genotype> whether or not an observable <phenotype> changes. Inheritance along a <cell lineage> does not require transmission to an offspring <organism>. In animals, <germline mutations> can enter offspring through <gametes>, whereas <somatic mutations> remain in body-cell lineages.<Mutation by National Human Genome Research Institute>[Definition and Narration: DNA sequence change, germline transmission and descendants of somatic cells]{ref}

A <DNA lesion> is damage to the physical <DNA> molecule, such as a damaged <nucleobase> or a broken strand. It need not become a lasting <mutation>: <DNA repair> can restore the original sequence. Conversely, a copied <nucleotide substitution> can leave an ordinary, correctly paired <DNA> duplex whose sequence differs from the ancestor. The distinction is between a damaged template and a changed inherited message, not simply between harmful and harmless effects.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others>[Chapter 5, DNA Repair, Without DNA Repair, Spontaneous DNA Damage Would Rapidly Change DNA Sequences; Figures 5-46–5-49]{ref}

Small <mutations> include <nucleotide substitutions> and <indels>. Larger changes can remove a <gene>, duplicate a <chromosome> region or rearrange its order. <Genetic recombination> can bring existing <alleles> together without creating a new <nucleobase> at the exchanged positions; the mechanism and the resulting sequence change are different questions. A molecular description of a <mutation>, its effect on a <gene> product and its effect on a <phenotype> are likewise three separate statements.

For an original consequence ledger, choose the <coding strand> segment $5'\text{-}\mathrm{ATG\ TTT\ GAA\ TAA}\text{-}3'$ with initiation at the first displayed <codon>. Use the standard <genetic code>, the same <reading frame> and suitable <gene expression> context throughout. Its <messenger RNA> has U in place of T, and the predicted <amino acid> chain is <methionine>–<phenylalanine>–<glutamic acid residue>, followed by a <stop codon>. This is a constructed coding segment, not an experimentally measured <gene>.

\Image[mutation-sequence-consequence-ledger.png]
{title=Original <mutation> ledger: sequence changes and predicted coding consequences}
{description=All <DNA> segments are written $5'$ to $3'$ in the same chosen <reading frame>. Colored letters mark changed or inserted <nucleotides>; a gap marks a removed <codon>. Products follow the standard <genetic code> under the stated initiation assumption. The ledger predicts <amino acid> order or termination, not a measured <phenotype>.}

In the second row, position 6 changes from T to C, replacing TTT by TTC. Both corresponding <RNA> <codons> specify <phenylalanine>, so the predicted chain is unchanged. Changing position 5 from T to C instead gives TCT, whose <RNA> <codon> specifies <serine>. Changing position 7 from G to T gives TAA and therefore a <stop codon> before the original <glutamic acid residue>. Each is one <nucleotide substitution>, but their coding consequences differ. Even preservation of the <amino acid> chain does not guarantee unchanged <gene expression> or <phenotype>.

Insert GGA after ATG to add a <glycine> <codon>, or remove the complete TTT <codon> to remove <phenylalanine>. Both <indels> preserve the downstream <reading frame> because their lengths are three <nucleotides>. A one-<nucleotide> insertion at that location instead produces a <frameshift mutation>; the positional explanation and a worked regrouping appear under <frameshift mutation>. The ledger illustrates why interpreting a <genetic variant> requires its position and context, rather than just the label mutation.

<Mutation> supplies new inherited variation on which <natural selection> can act. The <Luria-Delbrück experiment> makes the distinction unusually visible: <natural selection> reveals resistant descendants, while the timing of a prior <mutation> determines how many descendants are available to reveal. This connects an invisible change in one <biological cell> to a visible pattern across <cell cultures>.

= Drug-resistant allele
{parent=Mutation}

An allele that reduces a specified drug response. Resistance can arise through altered target binding, altered target function or other cellular mechanisms; biochemical and exposure controls distinguish them.

= Deletion mutation
{parent=Mutation}

Loss of sequence from a genetic molecule. Its effects depend on location and length, including whether the <reading frame> is preserved.

= Insertion mutation
{parent=Mutation}

Addition of sequence to a genetic molecule. In a coding region an insertion whose length is not divisible by three can change the downstream <reading frame>.

= Base substitution
{parent=Mutation}

Replacement of a base-pair state by another. A newly formed mismatch is an intermediate and need not become a heritable substitution if repaired.

= Gene fusion
{parent=Mutation}

A genetic rearrangement joining parts of different <genes>. It can create an altered <protein> or expression context without inventing entirely new molecular components.

= Gain-of-function mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <mutation> increasing an existing activity or producing a new functional effect. A changed substrate reaction is more specific than merely higher expression.

= Neomorphic mutation
{parent=Gain-of-function mutation}

A <mutation> giving a qualitatively altered activity, such as a new substrate reaction. Mutant <IDH1> production of <2-hydroxyglutarate> provides an example.

= Loss-of-function mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A sequence change reducing a relevant <gene> activity. Complete deletion, partial loss and context-dependent defects can have different phenotypes.

= Loss of heterozygosity
{parent=Mutation}

Loss of one of the distinguishable allelic states at a locus in a lineage, through deletion or other genetic events. Copy-number change and allelic change should be measured separately.

= Copy-number alteration
{parent=Mutation}

A change in the number of copies of a genomic region. <cancer> analysis must account for purity, ploidy and the boundaries of the altered segment before assigning a specific <gene> as a driver.

= Gene amplification
{parent=Mutation}

An increase in genomic copy number of a <gene> or region. It can raise expression or activity, but an amplified <gene> may instead accompany another selected alteration. It is distinct from laboratory <PCR amplification>.

= Temperature-sensitive mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A mutation whose phenotype changes with temperature. Conditional loss of an essential function permits study of its consequences after an organism has grown under permissive conditions.

= Temperature-shift experiment
{parent=Temperature-sensitive mutation}

Changing between permissive and restrictive temperatures at defined stages tests when a conditional <gene> product is required. Reciprocal shifts and short pulses separate initiation from maintenance, subject to inactivation kinetics and <protein perdurance>.

= Separation-of-function mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <mutation> that selectively disrupts one measured activity while preserving others relevant to the test. Preservation must be demonstrated, rather than inferred from unchanged abundance.

= Insertional oncogenesis
{parent=Mutation}

<Insertional oncogenesis> is oncogenic change caused by insertion of genetic material at a site affecting cellular gene expression or function. Recurrent sites in independent tumours and functional validation help distinguish a causal driver from an incidental insertion.

= Passenger mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <mutation> not responsible for the selective advantage that expands the observed clone. It can be carried along with a driver and can recur through high <mutation> opportunity.

= Driver mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <mutation> conferring a selective advantage on a somatic lineage in a specified environment. Its effect depends on cellular background and can concern initiation, progression or treatment resistance.

= Mutant
{parent=Mutation}

An <organism>, <biological cell> or molecular variant carrying a specified <mutation>. A mechanistic rescue experiment must check whether a <mutant> changes abundance, folding or location as well as the proposed activity.

= Zygotic-effect mutation
{parent=Mutation}

In an embryonic genetic analysis, a mutation whose developmental effect is assigned to the embryo's own <genotype>, in contrast to a <maternal-effect mutation>. This usage does not assert when the sequence change first arose.

= Maternal-effect mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <mutation> whose offspring <phenotype> depends on the mother's <genotype>, for example through products deposited during <oogenesis>, rather than solely on the offspring's own genotype.

= Point mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <point mutation> affects a single <base pair>. Terminology varies: the broad usage includes a one-<base pair> insertion or deletion as well as a <nucleotide substitution>. Describing the actual sequence change avoids this ambiguity.<Point Mutation by National Human Genome Research Institute>[Definition: a single base pair added, deleted or changed]{ref}

= Nucleotide substitution
{parent=Point mutation}

A <nucleotide substitution> replaces a <nucleotide> at a specified position without changing sequence length. In a fixed <reading frame>, a single substitution can preserve an <amino acid> assignment, change it or introduce a <stop codon>; the <mutation> ledger exhibits all three possibilities.

= Jukes-Cantor model
{c}
{parent=Nucleotide substitution}
{title2=$P_{ii}(t)=\tfrac14+\tfrac34e^{-4qt}$}

The <Jukes-Cantor model> is a symmetric four-state <continuous-time Markov chain> for <DNA> evolution. Each change to a specified different base has rate $q$, so the total rate of actual changes is $3q$. Its same-base probability is $1/4+(3/4)e^{-4qt}$ and its probability for one specified different base is $(1/4)(1-e^{-4qt})$. The probability of any mismatch is three times the latter, tending to $3/4$ at long times. Distinguish the exponent parameter $4q$ from the actual per-site <mutation rate> $3q$ when fitting this model.

= Indel
{parent=Mutation}

An <indel> is an insertion or deletion of <nucleotides> relative to a specified reference <nucleotide sequence>. Its length and location matter: within a coding region, a length not divisible by three changes the downstream <reading frame>, producing a <frameshift mutation>. A <gene deletion> is a larger removal defined by the <gene> it eliminates.

= Germline mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <germline mutation> occurs in the lineage that makes <gametes> and can therefore be inherited by an offspring <organism>. Transmission depends on whether the affected lineage actually contributes a <gamete> to that offspring. Compare <somatic mutation>.

= Somatic mutation
{parent=Mutation}

A <somatic mutation> occurs in a body-cell lineage outside the <germline mutation> route. Its descendant <biological cells> can inherit it through <DNA replication>, even though it is not transmitted through <gametes>. Thus heritability within a <cell lineage> differs from inheritance between sexually reproducing <organisms>.

= Luria-Delbrück experiment
{c}
{parent=Mutation}
{wiki=Luria–Delbrück_experiment}

= Fluctuation test
{synonym}

In 1943, <Salvador Luria> and <Max Delbrück> grew separate <Escherichia coli> B <cell cultures> from small equal inocula, without the challenging <bacteriophage>. They used nutrient broth or an asparagine–glucose <cell culture medium>. On nutrient agar plates, they first spread excess <bacteriophage> and then the bacterial sample centrally, leaving an outer margin. After incubation, resistant <biological colonies> provided the readout; counts were usually made at 24 and 48 hours. Their control plated repeated samples from one shared culture. Independently grown cultures varied much more than this sampling comparison. That contrast supported resistant lineages arising before challenge: early <mutations> could produce many resistant descendants before <natural selection> revealed them.<Mutations of Bacteria from Virus Sensitivity to Virus Resistance by Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück>[pp. 501–504, Material and Methods; Experimental, plating reliability, Table 1 and independent cultures, Table 2]{ref}

This was evidence about the timing of heritable resistance, not <DNA sequencing> of the responsible <genes>. It does not establish uniform <mutation rates> or exclude environmentally induced <mutations>; the paper itself discusses physiological dependence and delayed expression.<Mutations of Bacteria from Virus Sensitivity to Virus Resistance by Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück>[pp. 505–507, discussion of excess variation, physiological state, delayed resistance and genetic relatedness]{ref}

Now construct a deliberately small <mathematical model> of the timing effect. Start with one sensitive <biological cell> at generation $g=0$. Every <biological cell> divides into two in each of five synchronous rounds, with no death or growth difference. There are $N_g=2^g$ <biological cells> immediately after round $g$, ending with $N_5=32$. In each scenario, exactly one newly formed daughter changes to resistance immediately after round $g$; its descendants inherit resistance without reversion or further <mutations>. Resistance is expressed immediately, and the final challenge allows every resistant <biological cell>, and no sensitive one, to form one separate <biological colony>.

\Image[mutation-timing-clonal-jackpot.png]
{title=Hypothetical five-doubling <cell lineages>: one early <mutation> makes a larger resistant family}
{description=Two constructed scenarios with 32 final <biological cells> each. An event after round 1 leaves four doublings and produces 16 resistant descendants; an event after round 4 leaves one doubling and produces 2. Orange marks the affected <cell lineage>. The descendant count obeys
$$
R_g=2^{5-g},\qquad g\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}.
$$
At the moment of the event there is one resistant <biological cell>; each remaining round doubles its descendants. This proves the formula by repeated multiplication, with terminal case $R_5=1$. An event after round 1 gives $R_1=16$ resistant and 16 sensitive <biological cells>; an event after round 4 gives $R_4=2$ resistant and 30 sensitive <biological cells>. Both scenarios contain one <mutation> event. A culture with no event gives zero resistant <biological colonies> under these assumptions. The difference is family size, rather than the number of events.

For comparison, suppose each of the 32 final sensitive <biological cells> independently acquires resistance only on challenge, with <probability> $p=1/16$ and no subsequent multiplication before counting. Then the resistant count has a <binomial distribution>, with <expected value> $32p=2$ and <variance> $32p(1-p)=15/8$. This alternative <mathematical model> has no pre-challenge resistant family to amplify. Its independent trials are an assumption of the comparison, not a consequence of using physically distinct <biological cells>. The clonal scenarios explain how a jackpot can occur; they do not assign <probabilities> to the event times or estimate a real <mutation rate>.

= Mendelian segregation
{parent=Genetics}
{c}
{title2=$Aa\times Aa:\quad\tfrac14AA,\ \tfrac12Aa,\ \tfrac14aa$}

In ordinary <diploid> <meiosis>, the two copies at a <genetic locus> separate: each <gamete> receives one copy. With fair transmission, a <heterozygote> $Aa$ produces <gametes> carrying $A$ or $a$ with <probability> $1/2$ each. This is a transmission mechanism conditional on a parental <genotype>, rather than a statement about how common either <allele> is in a population.

For the one-locus cross $Aa\times Aa$, assume independent parental transmissions, no new <mutation>, and no differences in <fertilization> or survival between the resulting <genotypes>. Multiplying the two <gamete> <probabilities> gives four equally likely ordered outcomes: $AA,Aa,aA,aa$, each with <probability> $1/4$. Combining the two orders of the <heterozygote> gives
$$
P(AA)=0.25,\qquad P(Aa)=0.50,\qquad P(aa)=0.25.
$$
Only under the additional ideal model of <complete dominance> of $A$, complete <penetrance>, and no environmental changes to the scored characteristic do $AA$ and $Aa$ share the dominant <phenotype>. Its <probability> is then $0.25+0.50=0.75$. The inheritance calculation alone does not determine a <phenotype> or predict a generic <disease> risk.

\Image[mendelian-segregation.png]
{title=Fair Mendelian transmission in an Aa × Aa cross, with phenotype grouping under complete dominance and penetrance}

These are <probabilities>, not a requirement that every four offspring contain one $AA$, two $Aa$ and one $aa$. For 100 independent offspring under the ideal model, the <expected values> of the three counts are $25,50,25$; the $aa$ count has a <binomial distribution> with <standard deviation> $\sqrt{100(0.25)(0.75)}\approx4.33$. Each new transmission keeps the same <conditional probabilities> given the parental <genotypes>.

In his paper published in 1866, Mendel reported 5,474 round and 1,850 wrinkled pea seeds: $5474/7324\approx0.7474$ round, close to the ideal $0.75$. Following descendants also distinguished two inherited classes within the round <phenotype>, illustrating why <genotype> cannot always be read from appearance.<Experiments in Plant Hybridization by Gregor Mendel>[sections 5–6]{ref} At several <genetic loci>, transmission patterns additionally depend on <genetic linkage> and <genetic recombination>.

= Independent assortment
{parent=Mendelian segregation}
{title2=$\theta=\tfrac12$}

<Independent assortment> means that transmission at different <genetic loci> has <conditional independence> given the parental <genotype>. With fair <Mendelian segregation>, a parent <heterozygous> at two <genetic loci>, $AaBb$, produces $AB,Ab,aB,ab$ <gametes> with <probability> $1/4$ each. For example,
$$
P(A,B\mid AaBb)=P(A\mid AaBb)P(B\mid AaBb)=\frac12\frac12=\frac14.
$$
Here $AB$ labels an <allele> combination; it does not assert that the two <genetic loci> lie on the same <chromosome>.

The physical model is stochastic orientation of different <homologous chromosome> pairs toward the two poles of the <spindle apparatus> in <meiosis I>. One pair's orientation does not determine another's. For two pairs, one division can send $A,B$ together and $a,b$ together; another can send $A,b$ together and $a,B$ together. See <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/The Process of Meiosis> (§11.1, Metaphase I and Figure 11.5: random orientation of chromosome pairs).

Fixing $A$ at the left pole removes the duplicate obtained by reversing both poles. Each displayed orientation has <probability> $1/2$. A single division gives two copies each of two complementary combinations; all four equally likely combinations appear across divisions.}

With $k$ distinguishable <homologous chromosome> pairs, there are $2^k$ choices of parental <homologous chromosome> origin for a product at a specified pole, each with <probability> $2^{-k}$ under this model. For $k=3$, enumerate $MMM,MMP,MPM,MPP,PMM,PMP,PPM,PPP$, where M and P mark parental origins. Whole-pole reversal pairs complementary choices, leaving $2^{k-1}$ unordered partitions for $k\geq1$. For $k=2$ there are four choices for a specified pole but only the two relative orientations drawn. This counts possible products across <meioses>, not eight different products of one division when $k=3$. Identical <alleles> at scored <genetic loci> can also make different parental-origin choices observationally indistinguishable.

<Independent assortment> of different <chromosome> pairs does not imply independence of every pair of <genes>. Nearby <genetic loci> on the same <chromosome> can show <genetic linkage>. <Chromosomal crossover> can create new <haplotypes>, but does not automatically erase <genetic linkage>. See <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/Laws of Inheritance> (§12.3, Law of Independent Assortment and Linked Genes Violate the Law of Independent Assortment). In the ordinary fair two-locus model with parental <haplotypes> $AB/ab$ and <recombination fraction> $r$,
$$
P(AB)=P(ab)=\frac{1-r}{2},\qquad P(Ab)=P(aB)=\frac r2.
$$
Thus $r=0$ gives only the two parental <haplotypes>, while $r=1/2$ gives four equal <probabilities>. A <recombination fraction> of $1/2$ alone does not establish that the <genetic loci> lie on different <chromosomes>.

A motivating experiment appears in <Experiments in Plant Hybridization by Gregor Mendel> (§9, “The Reproductive Cells of the Hybrids”). In <peas>, hybrids for round versus wrinkled <seeds> and yellow versus green <seeds> were crossed reciprocally with constant wrinkled-green <plants>. The readout was visible <seed> shape and color: hybrid seed parents gave 31 round-yellow, 26 round-green, 27 wrinkled-yellow and 26 wrinkled-green; the reciprocal cross gave 24, 25, 22 and 27.

The apparatus was controlled <pollination>: opening immature <flowers>, removing <stamens> with forceps, and applying selected <pollen> to the <stigma (botany)>. Two-year trials established constant parental characters; greenhouse pots checked interference from insects in outdoor beds. Reciprocal crosses tested both transmission directions. These procedures are described in <Experiments in Plant Hybridization by Gregor Mendel> (§§2–3).

With <complete dominance> at both <genetic loci>, this <test cross> makes each <phenotype> report one hybrid <gamete> combination. The ideal <expected values> are $110/4=27.5$ and $98/4=24.5$ per class. The observations support four transmitted combinations, approximately equally frequent. They do not directly image <chromosome> orientation or prove independence for all <genes>; failed development and <seed> germination also limit the inference, as §9 notes.

= Identical by descent
{synonym}

Allele copies are identical by descent if they derive from the same specified ancestral copy. Identical observed states need not imply this ancestry. = Sibling IBD ascertainment under multiplicative penetrance

For <full siblings> in an <outbred pedigree>, condition on both being affected under <multiplicative diallelic penetrance> and on residual disease outcomes being independent given their <genotypes>. = Jacquard identity coefficients
{parent=Identity by descent}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta_1,\ldots,\Delta_9$}

For two individuals carrying labelled copies $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$, condensed identity coefficients give the <probabilities> of nine <identity by descent> patterns, identifying states that differ only by exchanging the two homologues within either individual. They retain within-individual <autozygosity>, which three ordinary outbred sharing coefficients omit. One convention is
$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
1&abcd\\2&ab\mid cd\\3&abc\mid d\text{ or }abd\mid c\\4&ab\mid c\mid d\\5&acd\mid b\text{ or }bcd\mid a\\6&cd\mid a\mid b\\7&ac\mid bd\text{ or }ad\mid bc\\8&\text{one cross-individual pair, two singletons}\\9&a\mid b\mid c\mid d
\end{array}
$$
Each block consists of copies identical by descent, and different blocks have distinct ancestral origins. For siblings whose parents are otherwise outbred <first cousins>, these coefficients are $(1,0,2,1,2,1,15,30,12)/64$. This follows by conditioning on whether the parents share one ancestral copy and enumerating the four independent <Mendelian segregation> choices to their two children. The <kinship coefficient> is $\Delta_1+(\Delta_3+\Delta_5+\Delta_7)/2+\Delta_8/4$, here $9/32$. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2585748/[Weir's discussion of identity states] uses this convention.

= Identity-by-descent sharing of inbred full siblings
{parent=Jacquard identity coefficients}
{title2=$(P(J=0),P(J=1),P(J=2))=(7/32,17/32,1/4)$}

Let $J$ be the largest number of chromosome copies that can be paired across the two <full siblings> as <identical by descent>, counting repeated copies when a child is <autozygous>. For offspring of otherwise outbred <first cousins>, with probability $3/4$ the parental copies have no common ancestral origin, giving ordinary sibling sharing $(1/4,1/2,1/4)$. With probability $1/4$, write the parental copies as $(A,B)$ and $(A,C)$ with three distinct origins. Each child independently has one of $AA,AB,AC,BC$, equally likely. Among sixteen ordered child pairs, two share no copy, ten share one, and four share two. The mixture gives the displayed probabilities. These three numbers do not determine all <Jacquard identity coefficients>; the formula $\phi=\mathbb E[J]/4$ is restricted to noninbred individuals and fails here.

= First-cousin identity-by-descent probabilities
{parent=Identity by descent}
{title2=$(k_0,k_1,k_2)=(3/4,1/4,0)$}

For <first cousins> in an otherwise <outbred pedigree>, compare the copies transmitted from their related parents, who are <full siblings>. These transmissions trace to the same shared grandparent with <probability> $1/2$ and then to the same homologous copy of that grandparent with probability $1/2$, giving $k_1=1/4$. The unrelated parental contributions cannot match, so two shared copies have probability zero. Selecting one random copy from each cousin chooses those two potentially shared copies with probability $1/4$, giving <kinship coefficient> $k_1/4=1/16$.

= Identical-by-descent segment
{parent=Identity by descent}

= IBD segment
{synonym}
{c}

An identical-by-descent segment is a chromosomal tract inherited from the same ancestral chromosome without an intervening breakpoint in the tracked ancestry. With a specified total number $m$ of independent <meioses> and a uniform recombination map, the probability that a fixed tract of genetic length $d$ survives without <genetic recombination> is approximately $e^{-md}$. Recent common ancestry therefore tends to give longer shared tracts. Lengths of tracts selected by covering a random point are length-biased relative to tracts sampled by their boundaries.

= Kinship coefficient
{parent=Identity by descent}
{title2=$\phi_{XY}$}

The kinship coefficient $\phi_{XY}$ is the <probability> that one randomly selected <allele> copy from each of $X,Y$ is <identical by descent>. For a noninbred individual, $\phi_{XX}=1/2$, and more generally $\phi_{XX}=(1+F_X)/2$. For distinct individuals with parents $M_X,P_X$, <Mendelian segregation> gives $\phi_{XY}=[\phi_{M_XY}+\phi_{P_XY}]/2$. The offspring's <inbreeding coefficient> equals the parents' kinship coefficient.

= Finite-population chromosomal coancestry
{parent=Kinship coefficient}
{title2=$\Phi_{G+1}=\Phi_G+\dfrac{1+F_G-2\Phi_{m,G}}{4(N-1)}$}

For $N$ individuals in monogamous families of exactly two offspring, let $\Phi_G$ be <mean> pairwise <kinship coefficient>, $F_G$ <mean> inbreeding and $\Phi_{m,G}$ <mean> kinship of mates. A random distinct offspring pair are siblings with <probability> $1/(N-1)$. Accounting separately for within-family and cross-family pairs gives the displayed recurrence, while $F_{G+1}=\Phi_{m,G}$. = Sex-linked cousin kinship
{parent=Kinship coefficient}
{title2=$F_X\in\{0,1/8,3/16\}$}

For otherwise outbred opposite-sex <first cousins>, the <probability> that the mother's randomly transmitted X copy matches the father's X by descent depends on which of their parents are siblings. It is zero when the male cousin is related through his father, $1/8$ when the female cousin is related through her father and the male through his mother, and $3/16$ when their mothers are sisters. Daughters can be X-autozygous; sons have one X and a Y and are not homozygous for a paired X chromosome. Recombination and pseudoautosomal regions require a locus-based formulation instead of whole-chromosome blocks.

A genetic association is a statistical dependence between a <genetic variant> and a <phenotype>. An associated marker need not be causal: <linkage disequilibrium> can correlate it with a causal locus, and <population stratification> can produce an association without such a link.

= Genome-wide association study

A study testing associations between genomic variants and a measured outcome across many genomic positions. Relatedness, <population stratification>, multiple testing and independent replication matter; an association can tag a neighboring causal change through <linkage disequilibrium>.

= Genotypic relative risk
{synonym}

The genotype relative risk compares the <penetrance> for <genotype> $g$ with that of a chosen baseline $g_0$. The common baseline factor cancels when computing <conditional probabilities> of transmitted genotypes given an affected offspring. It is a ratio of disease probabilities, unlike an <odds ratio>.

= Disease-ascertained transmission probability
{parent=Genotype relative risk}
{title2=$P(g\mid g_f,g_m,D)=\frac{q_gR_g}{\sum_hq_hR_h}$}

Let $q_g=P(g\mid g_f,g_m)$ be the <Mendelian segregation> probability, and assume the child's disease status depends on the parental <genotypes> only through its own genotype, with <penetrance> proportional to $R_g$. <Bayes theorem> gives the displayed conditional law. For a diallelic parent pair there are four equally likely labelled transmission outcomes. If an unordered genotype occurs in $m_g$ of them, $q_g=m_g/4$, so its probability is $m_gR_g/\sum_hm_hR_h$. Dropping multiplicities would, for example, misweight a heterozygous child of two heterozygotes.

= Haplotype-relative-risk
{parent=Genetic association}
{wiki}
{title2=$\widehat{\mathrm{OR}}=\frac{wz}{xy}$}

The haplotype-relative-risk design compares transmitted <alleles> to the corresponding untransmitted parental copies, used as internal controls for an affected offspring. If allele-1/allele-2 transmitted counts are $w,x$ and untransmitted counts are $y,z$, the allele-1 <odds ratio> estimate is $wz/(xy)$. An unmatched <Pearson chi-squared test of independence> treats this as a two-by-two table. The <transmission disequilibrium test> instead keeps parent-specific pairing and uses only discordant transmissions. The sampling assumptions of the unmatched calculation and any missing-parent reconstruction must be justified separately.

= Linkage-disequilibrium marker tagging
{parent=Association mapping}

An observed <genetic marker> tags an unobserved causal <genetic variant> when <linkage disequilibrium> correlates their allele indicators. Their squared <allelic correlation> $r^2$ measures prediction strength. Under standard local-alternative score-test approximations, using the marker rather than the causal allele reduces the noncentrality parameter by roughly $r^2$, suggesting a sample-size multiplier about $1/r^2$. This is an approximation requiring the same disease model and sampling design, not a universal power identity.

= Transmission disequilibrium test
{parent=Genetic association}
{wiki}
{title2=$\chi^2_{\mathrm{TDT}}=\frac{(b-c)^2}{b+c}$}

= TDT
{synonym}
{c}

For affected offspring and heterozygous parents at a diallelic locus, let $b$ count transmissions of allele 1 with allele 2 untransmitted and $c$ count the reverse. Under the fair-transmission null, conditional on $b+c$, $b$ is binomial with probability $1/2$. The asymptotic statistic is $(b-c)^2/(b+c)$, with a one-degree-of-freedom chi-squared null distribution. Homozygous-parent transmissions give no contrast. Conditioning within families protects this test from ordinary <population stratification>.

= Incomplete-trio transmission-selection bias
{parent=Transmission disequilibrium test}

If one parent is <heterozygous> and the other parent's <genotype> is missing, a homozygous child reveals the observed parent's transmitted <allele>, whereas a heterozygous child need not. Selecting only revealed transmissions can therefore bias the <transmission disequilibrium test>. For allele-1 frequency $p$ under random mating and fair transmission, the probabilities of an allele-1 or allele-2 homozygous child from an observed heterozygous parent are $p/2$ and $(1-p)/2$. Among selected homozygous children, the observed allele-1 transmission probability is $p$, not generally $1/2$. A complete-trio analysis avoids this reconstruction rule; methods retaining missing-parent families need an explicit <likelihood function> or a valid conditional adjustment. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3297829/[Family-based association tests using genotype data with uncertainty] discusses this bias.

= Family pseudo-control genotype
{parent=Transmission disequilibrium test}

Combine the untransmitted maternal and paternal alleles into a hypothetical control <genotype>, paired with the child's transmitted genotype. Even when two heterozygous parents have a heterozygous child and parent-specific phase is ambiguous, the combined untransmitted genotype is unambiguously heterozygous. Such pseudo-controls are matched family controls, not independent random individuals; <Hardy-Weinberg principle> is therefore not guaranteed merely by this construction.

= Transmitted and untransmitted alleles under multiplicative penetrance
{parent=Family pseudo-control genotype}

Under random mating and <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium>, label each parental allele by whether it is transmitted. Let $(A,B)$ and $(C,D)$ be the transmitted/untransmitted pairs of two parents. Before ascertainment all four draws are independent with population frequencies $\pi$. If offspring disease weights the transmitted pair by $\psi_A\psi_C$, then
$$
\mathbb P(A=a,B=b,C=c,D=d\mid\text{offspring disease})=\pi_a^*\pi_c^*\pi_b\pi_d.
$$
The transmitted pair is an affected-case genotype and the untransmitted pair a population <family pseudo-control genotype> under this homogeneous model. For unordered genotypes, compatible labelled transmissions must be summed. Conditioning on parental genotypes instead removes nuisance population frequencies and gives the usual <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Genetic linkage
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Why can two individually fair <allele> transmissions fail to be independent? <Genetic loci> on the same <chromosome> can travel together through <meiosis>. This tendency is <genetic linkage>; in an ordinary two-locus model, detectable <genetic linkage> means a <recombination fraction> below $1/2$. A <genetic marker> can therefore help track a neighboring <genetic locus> through a <pedigree>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/13-1-chromosomal-theory-and-genetic-linkage[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e: chromosomal theory and genetic linkage].

Take a <diploid> individual <heterozygous> at two <genetic loci>, with <phase-known genotype> $AB/ab$: one <homologous chromosome> carries <alleles> $A,B$, the other $a,b$. The parental <haplotypes> are $AB,ab$; the recombinant <haplotypes> are $Ab,aB$. Assume fair <Mendelian segregation>, equal <probabilities> within each reciprocal pair of <gametes>, and no <allele>-dependent differences in <gamete> recovery. If the <recombination fraction> is $r=0.2$, the <gamete> <probabilities> are $0.4,0.4,0.1,0.1$ in that order. Each <genetic locus> still transmits its two <alleles> equally, but $P(AB)=0.4$ exceeds $P(A)P(B)=0.25$: fairness at each <genetic locus> does not imply <independence> between <genetic loci>.

\Image[linked-locus-gamete-probabilities.png]
{title=<Gamete> <probabilities> from the <phase-known genotype> AB/ab: parental versus recombinant <haplotypes> at three <recombination fractions>}

At $r=0$, only the parental <haplotypes> occur, each with <probability> $0.5$: this is <complete genetic linkage>. At $r=0.5$, all four <haplotypes> have <probability> $0.25$, giving <independent assortment>. A <recombination fraction> near $0.5$ alone cannot establish whether the <genetic loci> lie on different <chromosomes> or far apart on one <chromosome>.

<Genetic linkage> concerns joint transmission through <meiosis>. <Linkage disequilibrium> concerns <haplotype> frequencies in a population, while <genetic association> compares <allele> or <genotype> frequencies with a <phenotype>. These questions require different observations; <pedigrees> directly reveal co-transmission.

= Genetic map
{parent=Genetic linkage}

A genetic map turns observations of inherited <haplotypes> into an ordered account of <genetic loci> along a <chromosome>. Its distances describe <genetic recombination>, which makes the map useful for locating a <genetic locus> relative to known <genetic markers>. A <genetic map> measures inheritance separation; separation measured in <base pairs> describes positions along <DNA>. <Recombination hotspots> help explain why equal numbers of <base pairs> need not give equal <genetic map> distances. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21116/[T. A. Brown, Genomes, 2nd edition (2002), chapter 5: Mapping Genomes].

Write $d$ for the <expected value> of the number of <chromosomal crossovers> encountered along one transmitted <chromatid> between the chosen <genetic loci>, measured in <morgans>; $100d$ is the distance in <centimorgans>. This counts exchanges along that transmitted <chromatid>, rather than all exchanges in the four-<chromatid> meiotic bundle. For a short interval with very few multiple exchanges, the <recombination fraction> satisfies $r\approx d$: about $1\%$ recombinant <gametes> then corresponds to about one <centimorgan>.

For a longer interval, hidden even numbers of <chromosomal crossovers> make that approximation inaccurate. Under the <Haldane mapping function>, $d=0.2$ <morgans> gives $r\approx0.164840$; conversely $r=0.2$ gives $d\approx0.255413$ <morgans>, or $25.5413$ <centimorgans>. Applying the inverse formula to an already specified <genetic map> distance would confuse a count with a <probability>.

<Genetic map> distances add along ordered adjacent intervals because the total exchange count is the sum of its interval counts. For <independent random variables> describing counts in disjoint intervals of the <Haldane mapping function> model, the endpoint <recombination fraction> instead obeys
$$
r_{12}=r_1(1-r_2)+(1-r_1)r_2=r_1+r_2-2r_1r_2.
$$
Exactly one interval must change parental origin. Thus $r_1=r_2=0.2$ gives $r_{12}=0.32$, although the two <genetic map> distances add to approximately $0.510826$ <morgans>. The simple composition formula requires the stated <independence>; <crossover interference> can invalidate it.

= Haldane mapping function
{parent=Genetic map}
{c}
{title2=$r(d)=\tfrac12(1-e^{-2d})$}

= Haldane's mapping function
{synonym}
{c}

The Haldane mapping function connects additive <genetic map> distance to endpoint <recombination fraction> in a toy model with no <crossover interference>. Specify the model directly along a transmitted <chromatid>: the exchange count $N$ in the interval has a <Poisson distribution> with <expected value> $d$, where $d$ is the distance in <morgans>. Counts in disjoint intervals are <independent random variables>. This is the transmitted-<chromatid> convention; $d$ is not the mean number of exchanges across all four <chromatids> in <meiosis>.

An odd exchange count changes parental phase. Summing the odd terms of the <Poisson distribution> gives
$$
r=P(N\text{ is odd})=e^{-d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}=\frac{1-e^{-2d}}{2}.
$$
For $0\le r<1/2$, the inverse and short-interval approximation are
$$
d=-\frac12\log(1-2r),\qquad r=d-d^2+O(d^3).
$$
Here $\log$ is the <natural logarithm>. At $d=0.2$, the <recombination fraction> is $0.16483997698$; at $r=0.2$, the distance is $0.2554128119$ <morgans>, or about $25.5413$ <centimorgans>. The linear approximation $r\approx d$ is useful only near zero. The <recombination fraction> approaches $1/2$ as $d$ grows; $r=1/2$ has no finite inverse in this model.

The model is a useful baseline, not a universal rule for every interval during <meiosis>. <Crossover interference> can change the relationship between distance and <recombination fraction>. = Mean allele-sharing test for affected siblings
{parent=Nonparametric linkage analysis}
{title2=$Z=(\bar J-1)/\sqrt{1/(2n)}$}

For independent pairs of affected <full siblings> with unrelated, noninbred parents and a fully informative <genetic marker>, the no-linkage null gives shared <identity by descent> count $J\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,1/2)$. Therefore $EJ=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}J=1/2$. An excess-sharing test uses the displayed approximately <standard normal> statistic and an upper-tail critical value. = Affected-sib-pair analysis

Without <genetic linkage> to the disease locus, the probabilities of sharing zero, one and two copies are $1/4,1/2,1/4$. Excess sharing supplies evidence for linkage under disease models that preferentially transmit a common risk allele to both siblings.

= Recessive affected-sib-pair likelihood
{parent=Affected-sib-pair analysis}
{title2=$L_0=u^2,\ L_1=uv,\ L_2=v^2$}

For unaffected <genetic carriers> of a disease with complete <penetrance> and <recessive inheritance>, suppose parental marker-disease <haplotype> phases are equally likely and the marker is fully informative. Write $u=\theta(1-\theta)$ and $v=1/2-u$, where $\theta$ is the <recombination fraction>. Conditional on sharing a parental marker copy, the probability that both siblings inherit that parent's disease allele is $[(1-\theta)^2+\theta^2]/2=v$; conditional on inheriting different marker copies it is $\theta(1-\theta)=u$. Independent paternal and maternal transmissions therefore give $L_0=u^2,L_1=uv,L_2=v^2$. Conditional instead on both siblings being affected, the IBD probabilities are $s^2,2s(1-s),(1-s)^2$, where $s=2u\in[0,1/2]$.

= Allele-sharing sufficiency for recessive linkage
{parent=Recessive affected-sib-pair likelihood}

For independent affected sib pairs with IBD counts $n_0,n_1,n_2$, the <likelihood function> of the <recombination fraction> is proportional to $s^{2n_0+n_1}(1-s)^{2n_2+n_1}$, with $s=2\theta(1-\theta)$. At fixed sample size, the total shared-copy count $2n_2+n_1$ is a <sufficient statistic> by the <Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem>. Thus a one-sided <likelihood-ratio test> has the same ordering as a test for excess mean IBD sharing. This equivalence depends on the stated fully penetrant recessive model and does not extend to arbitrary disease models.

= Complete linkage
{synonym}

<Complete linkage> in a two-locus model means <recombination fraction> zero: only parental <haplotype> combinations are transmitted. Multiple <genetic markers> in a nonrecombining block represent one <haplotype> segregation observation.

= Homozygosity mapping
{parent=Genetic linkage}

Homozygosity mapping searches for shared homozygous marker regions in individuals with a rare recessive condition, often in an <inbred> pedigree. Under a single ancestral disease copy, affected individuals inherit two copies identical by descent. Marker homozygosity alone gives <identity by state>, so founder haplotype frequencies matter. If a particular haplotype has founder frequency $p$ and $K$ distinct founder chromosome copies underlie all selected descendant copies, their marker-pattern probability is $E[p^K]$. For complete linkage and a single disease copy, the affected-only numerator is $p$, yielding a <LOD score> $\log_{10}(p/E[p^K])$ under linkage-equilibrium founder assumptions.

= LOD score
{parent=Genetic linkage}
{c}
{title2=$Z(\theta)=\log_{10}[L(\theta)/L(1/2)]$}

The LOD score compares the likelihood at recombination fraction $\theta$ with independent assortment:
$$
Z(\theta)=\log_{10}\frac{L(\theta)}{L(1/2)}.
$$
When phenotype likelihood is independent of $\theta$, it can be computed from marker probabilities conditional on phenotype and ascertainment. Positive values support linkage; the observed marker pattern must be specified precisely because affected-only and affected-plus-unaffected observations give different likelihoods.

= Genetic recombination
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Genetic recombination rearranges inherited genetic material into new combinations. In the <meiosis> example considered here, <chromosomal crossover> exchanges segments between nonsister <chromatids> of <homologous chromosomes>. A transmitted <chromatid> can consequently contain <DNA> from both parental homologues. This produces new <haplotypes> without requiring a new <mutation> at either scored <genetic locus>. See <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Meiosis>.

For <phase-known genotype> $AB/ab$, a single <chromosomal crossover> between the selected <genetic loci> can yield $Ab$ and $aB$ on the participating <chromatids>; the other two <chromatids> can remain $AB$ and $ab$. Thus an exchange in the four-<chromatid> bundle does not mean every resulting <gamete> is recombinant. Only a <chromosomal crossover> encountered along the transmitted <chromatid> within the selected interval affects its two-locus score.

Follow parental origin along the transmitted <chromatid> from the first <genetic locus> to the second. Each exchange switches that origin. An odd number of such <chromosomal crossovers> changes the endpoint combination; an even number restores parental phase. Two exchanges can therefore leave the scored <haplotype> as $AB$ even though an internal segment came from the other parental <homologous chromosome>. Recombinant status is relative to the chosen <genetic loci>, not a complete record of exchanges along the <chromosome>.

This <integer parity> distinction connects the physical process of <genetic recombination> to the observed <recombination fraction>. It also explains why a long <genetic map> interval can accumulate many exchanges while its endpoint <recombination fraction> approaches $1/2$ in the <Haldane mapping function> model.

= Crossover interference
{parent=Chromosomal crossover}

Crossover interference means that an exchange affects the occurrence of other nearby <chromosomal crossovers>. The <Haldane mapping function> assumes this dependence is absent. See <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Meiosis>.

= Four-gamete test
{parent=Genetic recombination}
{wiki}

Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, two variable sites on one unrecombined rooted tree have nested or disjoint derived-allele clades. At most three of the four biallelic <haplotypes> can then occur. Observation of all four haplotypes proves that the single-tree, single-mutation-per-site model cannot hold: some ancestral <genetic recombination> is necessary if recurrent mutation is excluded. The test gives no proof of absence of recombination when only three haplotypes occur.

= Recombination fraction
{parent=Genetic recombination}
{title2=$0\le\theta\le\tfrac12$}

The recombination fraction is the <probability> that a transmitted <gamete> carries a recombinant <haplotype> between two specified <genetic loci>, relative to the parent's known <haplotype> phase. Write it as $r$ here; the symbol $\theta$ is also common in <genetic linkage> analysis. In the ordinary symmetric two-locus model, $0\le r\le1/2$, with $r=0$ for <complete genetic linkage> and $r=1/2$ for <independent assortment>.

For <phase-known genotype> $AB/ab$, fair <Mendelian segregation> and equal reciprocal <gamete> <probabilities> give
$$
P(AB)=P(ab)=\frac{1-r}{2},\qquad P(Ab)=P(aB)=\frac r2.
$$
For example, 1,000 independent informative transmissions with observed counts $400,400,100,100$ in this order have empirical <recombination fraction> $(100+100)/1000=0.2$. Those illustrative counts match the <expected values> in the toy model, but actual counts fluctuate. The <genetic linkage> diagram shows both the parental and recombinant classes, so their separate <probabilities> are not confused with the total $r$.

The <recombination fraction> measures the <probability> of an odd number of origin switches along the transmitted <chromatid>. It is neither the <expected value> of the exchange count nor the <probability> of at least one exchange: even positive counts can be invisible at the two endpoints. Converting an observed <recombination fraction> to a <genetic map> distance therefore needs an explicit model such as the <Haldane mapping function>. The distinction between transmitted-<chromatid> counts and four-<chromatid> exchange counts is discussed in https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2704907/[Weeks, Tang and Kwon (2009), Casares' map function: No need for a 'corrected' Haldane's map function, Genetica 135, 305–307].

= Pedigree
{parent=Genetics}

A pedigree records parent–offspring relationships. A <founder in a pedigree> has no recorded parents; all other individuals have a genotype conditional on their parental genotypes. Inbreeding can produce loops in the undirected diagram while the directed ancestry remains acyclic.

= Outbred pedigree
{parent=Pedigree}

For calculations of <identity by descent>, an outbred pedigree has unrelated, noninbred <founders in a pedigree>, and no additional ancestral relationships beyond those explicitly specified. Founder gene copies are treated as distinct ancestral copies even if they have <identity by state>.

= Pedigree likelihood
{parent=Pedigree}

For latent <genotypes> $G_i$ and observed <phenotypes> $X_i$, assume conditional phenotype independence, independent founder genotypes, and conditionally independent Mendelian transmissions. The <likelihood function> is
$$
\sum_{(G_i)}\prod_i P(X_i\mid G_i)\prod_{i\in F}P(G_i)\prod_{i\notin F}P(G_i\mid G_{m(i)},G_{f(i)}).
$$
This follows by the chain rule applied to the acyclic ancestry graph, then the law of total probability over all latent genotypes. <Penetrance> supplies the phenotype factors and <genetic recombination> supplies multilocus transmission factors.

= Phase averaging in a linkage likelihood
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}
{title2=$L(\theta)=\sum_s\pi_sL_s(\theta)$}

An unphased multilocus <genotype> can have several pairs of <haplotypes>. Its <pedigree likelihood> must average over these possible phases with prior weights $\pi_s$, rather than maximize over phases after observing the offspring. Under <linkage disequilibrium>, the weights need not be equal. They can be obtained by conditioning population haplotype frequencies on the observed parental <genotype>; different choices of what is conditioned on change only parameter-independent factors when those frequencies are held fixed.

= Score equation for a phase-averaged linkage likelihood
{parent=Phase averaging in a linkage likelihood}

For a known-phase contribution $L_1(\theta)\propto\theta^{r_1}(1-\theta)^{m_1-r_1}$ and an independent phase-averaged contribution $L_2(\theta)$, an interior maximum satisfies
$$
[r_1-m_1\theta]L_2(\theta)+\theta(1-\theta)L_2'(\theta)=0.
$$
This follows by differentiating $\log L_1+\log L_2$ and multiplying by $\theta(1-\theta)L_2$. The constrained <maximum likelihood estimate> on $[0,1/2]$ must also be compared with the endpoints. An interior root cannot be asserted without the observed pedigree counts; complete nonrecombinant data, for example, have their maximum at zero.

= Pedigree graphical model
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}

Assign one <genotype> <random variable> to each person in a <pedigree>. Independent <founders in a pedigree> carry population genotype laws, and each other genotype has its <conditional distribution> given its two parents from <Mendelian segregation>. The ancestry <directed acyclic graph> is a <Bayesian network> for the joint genotype law. Its <moral graph> contains each parent-child edge and each coparent edge. Each founder factor and each child-with-parents factor belongs to a <clique>, giving an undirected graphical representation. The graph is an independence representation; equality with the minimal <conditional independence graph> needs additional faithfulness assumptions, particularly because inheritance can produce zero probabilities.

= Elston-Stewart algorithm
{parent=Pedigree likelihood}
{c}

Pedigree peeling eliminates latent <genotypes> by moving sums inside products whenever the other factors do not depend on the eliminated genotype. For one parent genotype $g$ and one child genotype $h$, the likelihood becomes
$$
\sum_g P(g)P(X_p\mid g)\left[\sum_hP(h\mid g)P(X_c\mid h)\right].
$$
The inner bracket is the child's message to its parent. With two states each, it needs four multiplications and two additions; multiplying the two parent weights and messages needs four more multiplications and the final sum one addition. The unrearranged four terms instead use twelve multiplications. Larger pedigrees use the same variable-elimination identity, with additional handling of pedigree loops.

A founder is an individual whose parents are outside the recorded <pedigree>. = Recessive inheritance
{parent=Genetics}

In a fully penetrant autosomal recessive model with normal allele $A$ and disease allele $a$, $aa$ is affected and $AA,Aa$ are unaffected. A carrier cross $Aa\times Aa$ has offspring probabilities $1/4,1/2,1/4$ for $AA,Aa,aa$, respectively, from the two independent parental meioses.

= Sibling recurrence risk
{parent=Recessive inheritance}

Sibling recurrence risk is the <conditional probability> that another child has the disease, given an affected sibling. For unaffected parents, complete <penetrance> and a single recessive disease allele, the affected child identifies both parents as <genetic carriers>. Independent <Mendelian segregation> then gives recurrence probability $1/4$, whether or not the parents are related. Incomplete penetrance, phenocopies, different causal loci or affected parents change this calculation.

= Genetic carrier
{parent=Recessive inheritance}

A genetic <genetic carrier> has one disease-associated recessive <allele> and one normal <allele> at a <genetic locus>, and is unaffected under a fully penetrant recessive model. Two <genetic carriers> have affected-offspring <probability> $1/4$ under <Mendelian segregation>; a <genetic carrier> and an individual with no disease <allele> have affected-offspring <probability> zero under that same mutation-free model.

= Phenotype
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A <phenotype> is an observable characteristic of an <organism>, arising from its <genotype>, environment and other influences. Seed shape is a <phenotype>; $Aa$ is a <genotype>. The distinction prevents appearance from being mistaken for a complete inherited description. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Phenotype[NHGRI: phenotype].

For a specified binary <phenotype> $T$, let its <penetrance> in <genotype> $g$ be $r_g=P(T\mid g)$. The <law of total probability> gives $P(T)=\sum_g r_gP(g)$. Thus the ideal $Aa\times Aa$ example has $P(T)=r_{AA}/4+r_{Aa}/2+r_{aa}/4$: the familiar $3/4$ follows only when these <penetrances> are $1,1,0$. A <pedigree likelihood> keeps inheritance and the conditional <phenotype> model as separate factors.

= Phenotypic plasticity
{parent=Phenotype}

Dependence of the <phenotype> expressed by a <genotype> on the environment. An immediate response can be beneficial or harmful; evolutionary change additionally requires a change in inherited properties across generations.

= Developmental plasticity
{parent=Phenotypic plasticity}

Environment-dependent variation in developmental trajectories within a genotype. Plasticity need not be adaptive, and an environmentally induced developmental change need not be reversible.

= Costs of phenotypic plasticity
{parent=Phenotypic plasticity}

Fitness costs associated with maintaining or using a responsive system, distinct from poor performance of a particular expressed <phenotype>. Comparisons should match the expressed outcome while varying the capacity for response.

= Behavioural plasticity
{parent=Phenotypic plasticity}

Environment- or experience-dependent change in <animal behaviour> expressed by an individual. A change within a lifetime must be distinguished from an evolutionary change in its <reaction norm>.

= Cost of behavioural flexibility
{parent=Behavioural plasticity}

Costs of acquiring information, maintaining responsiveness or switching action. They must be distinguished from a maladaptive action caused by an unreliable cue.

= Phenotypic buffering
{parent=Phenotypic plasticity}

Reduction of phenotypic or fitness effects of environmental variation through plastic responses. It can reduce immediate selection on other inherited traits.

= Phenotypic trait
{parent=Phenotype}

A distinguishable measurable characteristic of an <organism>. Trait definitions matter: several correlated measurements need not represent independent dimensions of a <phenotype>.

= Dominance
{disambiguate=genetics}
{parent=Phenotype}

= Complete dominance
{parent=Dominance (genetics)}

For a specified characteristic, complete dominance means that the <heterozygote> has the same <phenotype> as one of the two <homozygotes>. It does not mean that the dominant <allele> is more common, more advantageous, or transmitted more often. <Penetrance> specifies the <conditional probability> of an expressed <phenotype> given a <genotype> and is a separate modeling assumption.

= Penetrance
{parent=Phenotype}
{wiki}

Penetrance is the probability of an expressed <phenotype> conditional on a specified <genotype>. It can depend on age, sex and environment. A fully penetrant recessive model assigns disease probability one to the recessive homozygote and zero to other genotypes; incomplete penetrance or phenocopies replace these indicators by intermediate probabilities.

= Genetic variance of penetrance
{parent=Penetrance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}\{\mathbb E(Y\mid G)\}$}

For a binary disease trait $Y$ with <genotype> $G$, the genetic variance of <penetrance> is the between-genotype term in the <law of total variance>. The averaged within-genotype term is $\mathbb E[q_G(1-q_G)]$. Under <multiplicative diallelic penetrance>, the genetic term is $p^2(\beta^2-\alpha^4)$. This decomposition attributes variance to the modeled locus, not automatically to all hereditary causes.

= Multiplicative diallelic penetrance
{parent=Penetrance}
{title2=$q_G=p\theta^G,\quad G\sim\operatorname{Bin}(2,\phi)$}

With <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium>, let $G$ count copies of the risk <allele>. The <penetrance> $p\theta^G$ must lie in $[0,1]$ for every possible <genotype>. Writing $\alpha=1-\phi+\phi\theta$ and $\beta=1-\phi+\phi\theta^2$ gives $\mathbb E(q_G)=p\alpha^2$ and $\mathbb E(q_G^2)=p^2\beta^2$.

= Genotype
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A <genotype> records the inherited <alleles> carried by an individual at one or more <genetic loci>. At a two-copy <diploid> <genetic locus> with <alleles> $A,a$, the possible unordered <genotypes> are $AA,Aa,aa$. The first and last are <homozygotes>; the middle is a <heterozygote>. The notation records inherited states, without yet asserting their <phenotypes>. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/genotype[NHGRI: genotype].

An unordered $Aa$ combines the two parental orders $Aa$ and $aA$. This small bookkeeping point explains the factor two in <Hardy-Weinberg proportions> and in an $Aa\times Aa$ cross. If parental origin or arrangement across several <genetic loci> matters, a <phase-known genotype> retains extra information about the two <haplotypes>.

= Genotyping
{parent=Genotype}

<Genotyping> determines the <alleles> carried at chosen <genetic loci>. A <diploid> <genotype> call need not identify its two <haplotypes>; that additionally requires a <phase-known genotype> or <haplotype estimation>.

= Genotyping error
{parent=Genotype}

A <genotyping error> is an incorrect observed <allele> or <genotype> call. A probabilistic observation model replaces the deterministic <genetic marker> factor by $P(X=x\mid G=g)$, allowing nonzero <probability> of an observed call inconsistent with the latent <genotype>. Such error can bias family transmission tests and linkage <likelihoods>.

= Heterozygosity

= Heterozygous
{synonym}

<Heterozygosity> means that the two <allele> states at a diploid <genetic locus> differ; an individual with that property is a <heterozygote>. <Heterozygous> parental <genotypes> provide alternative <alleles> for a <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Homozygosity
{parent=Genotype}

= Homozygous
{synonym}

<Homozygosity> means that the two <allele> states at a diploid <genetic locus> are the same; an individual with that property is a <homozygote>. Observed <homozygosity> does not by itself establish <identity by descent>.

= Heterozygote
{parent=Genotype}

A heterozygote carries different <alleles> at a diploid locus. Under ordinary Mendelian segregation each is transmitted to an offspring with probability $1/2$. Only heterozygous parents provide a comparison between alternative transmissions in the <transmission disequilibrium test>.

= Homozygote
{parent=Genotype}

A homozygote carries two identical <alleles> at a locus; a <heterozygote> carries different alleles. Homozygosity of an observed <haplotype> is identity by state, and need not be <identity by descent>.

= Haplotype
{parent=Genotype}
{wiki}

A haplotype is the set of <alleles> on one chromosome across specified <genetic loci>. Closely linked markers without intervening <genetic recombination> are transmitted as one haplotype; they are not independent observations of inheritance.

= Haplotype frequency
{parent=Haplotype}
{title2=$f_{AB}$}

A <haplotype frequency> is the proportion of chromosome copies carrying specified <alleles> together across chosen <genetic loci>. Its <marginal distributions> give <allele frequencies>; its departures from products of those frequencies are <linkage disequilibrium>.

= Haplotype estimation
{parent=Haplotype}
{wiki}

<Haplotype estimation> reconstructs which <alleles> share a <chromosome> from observed <genotypes> and other information, such as <pedigrees>. A doubly <heterozygous> <genotype> can be $AB/ab$ or $Ab/aB$; a <phase-known genotype> distinguishes them. Uncertainty in that reconstruction must propagate to estimated <haplotype frequencies> and <linkage disequilibrium>.

= Haplotype block
{parent=Haplotype}
{wiki}

A haplotype block is a chromosomal region with limited historical <genetic recombination>, strong local <linkage disequilibrium> and comparatively few common <haplotypes>. Its definition is operational and population-dependent; it does not mean that recombination never occurs within it.

= Phase-known genotype
{parent=Genotype}

At several <genetic loci>, a phase-known genotype specifies which <alleles> occur together on each chromosome, hence its two <haplotypes>. An unphased multilocus <genotype> may be compatible with several haplotype pairs. For two heterozygous loci, $AB/ab$ and $Ab/aB$ have identical unphased genotypes but different transmission probabilities when <genetic linkage> is present.

= Genetic locus
{parent=Genetics}

= Locus (genetics)
{synonym}

A locus is a position or region of a genome. An <allele> specifies an inherited state there; a <haplotype> specifies states at several loci on one chromosome.

= Allele
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

An <allele> is one of the alternative inherited states at a <genetic locus>, often distinguished by a difference in <DNA> sequence. A population can contain many <alleles>, although a two-copy <diploid> individual carries at most two at that <genetic locus>, one inherited from each parent. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Allele[NHGRI: allele].

Count copies, not just individuals, when computing <allele frequency>: in 100 individuals with <genotype> counts $49\ AA$, $42\ Aa$, $9\ aa$, there are $2(49)+42=140$ copies of $A$ among 200, so $p_A=0.70$. The <heterozygotes> contribute to both <allele> counts. This converts observed <genotypes> into the population quantity needed by the <Hardy-Weinberg principle>.

= Allele frequency
{parent=Allele}
{title2=$p_A$}

The allele frequency is the proportion of gene copies at a specified <genetic locus> carrying an <allele>. Two unrelated copies can have the same observed allele without being <identical by descent>; under independent sampling their matching probability is the sum of squared allele frequencies. Pedigree <autozygosity> instead traces copies to the same ancestral copy.

= Allele fixation
{parent=Allele frequency}
{title2=$u=0\text{ or }u=1$}

An <allele> fixes when all copies at the relevant locus in the population are that <allele>. Fixation can occur through selection or <genetic drift>. Spatial polymorphism means that fixation within an individual habitat need not imply fixation across a <metapopulation>.

The probability that an <allele> eventually occupies every copy at the specified <genetic locus> under a population model. = Hardy-Weinberg proportions
{c}
{synonym}

The <Hardy-Weinberg principle> describes the <genotype> proportions produced by independently combining two parental <gametes> from the same <allele> pool. For <alleles> $A,a$ with <allele frequencies> $p,q$, where $p+q=1$, the <Hardy-Weinberg proportions> are
$$
P(AA)=p^2,\qquad P(Aa)=2pq,\qquad P(aa)=q^2.
$$
The two ordered <heterozygote> outcomes contribute $pq+qp$. These proportions sum to $(p+q)^2=1$, and their implied $A$ <allele frequency> is $p^2+(2pq)/2=p$. Reshuffling copies therefore preserves their frequencies.

For stable <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium> across generations, use a large population with <random mating>, equal <allele frequencies> in the parental pools, ordinary <Mendelian segregation>, and no <natural selection>, <mutation>, <gene flow> or <genetic drift>. Negligible <genetic drift> is an idealization of large population size. The <one-generation random-mating equilibrium> needs independent union of <gametes>; persistence additionally requires processes that change <allele frequencies> to be absent.

At $p=0.70,q=0.30$, the predicted <genotype> proportions are $0.49,0.42,0.09$. For 100 sampled individuals the <expected values> of the counts are $49,42,9$, rather than the $25,50,25$ of a specified $Aa\times Aa$ cross. The latter fixes both parental <genotypes>; <Hardy-Weinberg proportions> average over population mating.

\Image[hardy-weinberg-proportions.png]
{title=Hardy-Weinberg genotype proportions as allele frequency varies, highlighting p = 0.70}

An observed population need not have these proportions. For example, $70\ AA$, no $Aa$, and $30\ aa$ among 100 individuals still gives $p=0.70$, but has no <heterozygotes>. <Random mating> predicts $0.42$ <heterozygosity> in the next generation under the stated model. A finite sample can also depart by chance, so a discrepancy is a starting point for investigating <inbreeding>, <population stratification> or <genotyping error>, rather than proof of one cause.

Hardy's 1908 note made the conceptual payoff explicit: a dominant <phenotype> does not by itself make its <allele> spread. <Complete dominance> describes expression; changes in frequency require a separate mechanism such as <natural selection>. See https://www-leland.stanford.edu/~huatang/gene244/readings/HardyWeinberg.pdf[Hardy, Mendelian Proportions in a Mixed Population, Science 28 (1908), 49–50].

= Multiplicative penetrance preserves Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}
{title2=$\pi_i^*=\pi_i\psi_i/\sum_u\pi_u\psi_u$}

For ordered alleles $I,J$ independently drawn with probabilities $\pi_i$, suppose disease <penetrance> is $c\psi_i\psi_j$. Conditional on disease, the allele draws remain independent with probabilities $\pi_i^*=\pi_i\psi_i/\sum_u\pi_u\psi_u$. This follows by normalizing the product $c\pi_i\pi_j\psi_i\psi_j$. Unordered homozygote probabilities are $(\pi_i^*)^2$ and heterozygote probabilities are $2\pi_i^*\pi_j^*$. Unaffected controls do not generally share this factorization: their genotype weights contain $1-c\psi_i\psi_j$.

= One-generation random-mating equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}
{title2=$p=u+v/2,\quad(p^2,2p(1-p),(1-p)^2)$}

If parental <genotype> frequencies are $(u,v,w)$ and the two parental gametes are independently sampled from the same <allele> pool, <Mendelian segregation> gives allele frequency $p=u+v/2$. Their independent union has the displayed <Hardy-Weinberg proportions>, irrespective of parental genotype proportions. Equal allele frequencies in the two sexes and no transmission or viability distortion are part of this conclusion.

= Allele-frequency estimator under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
{parent=Hardy-Weinberg principle}

From genotype counts in $n$ independent diploid individuals, the unbiased and maximum-likelihood estimator of the frequency of allele $B$ is
$$
\widehat\theta=\frac{2n_{BB}+n_{AB}}{2n}.
$$
It is the sample proportion among the $2n$ sampled alleles, so it is consistent and
$$
\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)
\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}2\right).
$$

= Genetic variant
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

A genetic variant is a heritable difference in a DNA sequence relative to a chosen reference. Genetic variants can be used as <instrumental variables> in <Mendelian randomization> when the required causal assumptions are credible.

= Structural variant
{parent=Genetic variant}

An inherited change in the organization or amount of a genomic segment, rather than a single changed base. Comparable assemblies and sequence coverage are important because reference mapping can miss lineage-specific material.

= Single-nucleotide polymorphism
{parent=Genetic variant}
{wiki}

= SNP
{synonym}
{c}

A single-nucleotide polymorphism is variation at one nucleotide position in sampled genomes. Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, the number of such variable positions in a sample equals the number of mutations on the genealogy below its most recent common ancestor.

= Tag SNP
{parent=Single-nucleotide polymorphism}
{wiki}

A tag SNP represents other nearby <single-nucleotide polymorphisms> whose <alleles> are strongly correlated through <linkage disequilibrium>. Tagging reduces the number of markers needed for <association mapping>; it does not establish that the tag itself causes the phenotype.

= Quantitative genetics
{parent=Genetics}
{wiki}

Quantitative genetics studies how genetic and environmental variation contribute to continuously varying traits.

= Selection differential
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$S$}

The difference between the mean phenotype of selected parents and the population mean under a specified selection scheme. It differs from the partial regression coefficients in a multivariate selection gradient.

= Breeder's equation
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$R=h^2S$}

A one-trait short-term response model $R=h^2S$, where $R$ is the inherited change in mean, $S$ the phenotypic selection differential and $h^2$ narrow-sense <heritability>. It assumes an appropriate additive inheritance model and does not turn environmental phenotype change into inherited response.

= Animal model in quantitative genetics
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

A mixed model using pedigree or genomic relationships to estimate genetic contributions to phenotypic variation. A donor identity effect alone is not proof of an indirect genetic effect.

= Paternal half-sib design
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

A breeding design in which offspring share a sire but have different dams. Relatedness patterns help distinguish inherited covariance from shared full-family environment.

= Maternal effect
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

An influence of the mother on offspring phenotype beyond simply transmitting offspring alleles. It can include genetic and non-genetic components.

= Direct genetic effect
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

An effect of an individual's own genotype on its phenotype, defined relative to the model and its other genetic and environmental contributions.

= Indirect genetic effect
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

= IGE
{synonym}
{c}

An effect of one individual's genotype on another individual's phenotype, mediated through the environment or social interaction. Maternal effects are genetic only insofar as maternal genetic variation contributes to them.

= Quantitative trait locus
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

= QTL
{synonym}
{c}

A genomic region statistically associated with variation in a quantitative trait. A mapped interval can contain multiple causal variants or genes.

= Genetic architecture
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

The genetic variants, their effects, interactions and arrangement that contribute to a specified phenotype in a stated population and environment.

= Reaction norm
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$z(C)$}

The relationship between environment and the <phenotype> expressed by a specified <genotype>. In the illustrative form $z=a+bC$, $a$ describes baseline expression and $b$ describes responsiveness to cue $C$.

= Social reaction norm
{parent=Reaction norm}

A relation between an individual’s behaviour and social conditions. Baseline differences and differences in responsiveness are separate components.

= Polygenic trait
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

A <phenotypic trait> influenced by variation at multiple <genetic loci>. Absence of a single shared large-effect region does not exclude repeated evolution through many small inherited effects.

= Genotype-environment interaction
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

Dependence of a difference between <genotypes> on the environment. Crossing <reaction norms> provides a clear example: one <genotype> can outperform another in one environment but reverse rank elsewhere.

= Quantitative trait locus mapping
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

Mapping inherited differences in a quantitative <phenotypic trait> to genomic intervals through segregation and <genetic recombination>. A detected interval need not contain only one causal <gene> or <genetic variant>.

= Antagonistic pleiotropy
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

<pleiotropy> in which a change has benefits and costs in different traits, environments, sexes or life stages. Whether an <allele> spreads depends on its net contribution to <genotype fitness>, rather than the sign of any one effect.

= Genetic covariance
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

Shared variation in inherited contributions to different <phenotypic traits>. It can arise from <pleiotropy> or associations between separate causal <genetic loci>; measuring it does not distinguish these mechanisms by itself.

= Genetic variance
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$V_G$}

The component of variation in a specified trait attributable to differences among <genotypes> in the stated environment and population. Additive <genetic variance> is distinguished from contributions due to <dominance (genetics)> and <epistasis>.

= Additive genetic covariance matrix
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$\mathbf G$}

The <covariance matrix> of additive inherited contributions to a collection of <phenotypic traits>. Its diagonal entries are additive <genetic variances>; off-diagonal entries describe additive <genetic covariance>. It summarizes available local directions of response but can change as <allele frequencies> and associations change.

= Multivariate breeder's equation
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$\Delta\overline{\mathbf z}=\mathbf G\boldsymbol\beta$}

= Lande's equation
{synonym}
{c}

The local response approximation $\Delta\overline{\mathbf z}=\mathbf G\boldsymbol\beta$, where $\mathbf G$ is the <additive genetic covariance matrix> and $\boldsymbol\beta$ is a <phenotypic selection gradient>. Correlated inheritance can facilitate one direction of response and inhibit another.<Quantitative genetic analysis of multivariate evolution, applied to brain:body size allometry by Russell Lande>[Evolution 33, pp. 402–416]{ref}

= Phenotypic selection gradient
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\beta$}

A <vector> describing direct selection on measured <phenotypic traits>, conditional on the other measured traits. In a local deterministic fitness model it can be written $\boldsymbol\beta=\nabla_{\mathbf z}\log w$; population regression estimates require their own sampling and fitness assumptions. This differs in context from a rare-mutant invasion <selection gradient>.

= Cost of complexity
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

A proposed reduction in the rate of adaptation when <mutations> affect more independently selected dimensions. In <Fisher's geometric model>, a large random step can improve one component while damaging others. The prediction depends on the scaling of mutational effects and need not follow from a simple count of organismal traits.

= Pleiotropy
{parent=Quantitative genetics}

An effect of one <gene> or <genetic variant> on multiple distinguishable <phenotypic traits>. It can constrain evolution when effects oppose simultaneous improvement, or facilitate coordinated improvement when their directions align with <natural selection>. Distinguish a single causal change from several linked changes.

= Genetic risk score
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{title2=$G_{\rm score}=\sum_j w_jG_j$}

A genetic risk score combines allele counts from several <genetic variants>, usually using specified weights. In <Mendelian randomization> it can act as a combined <instrumental variable> for an <exposure>; interpretation still requires <instrument relevance>, <instrumental-variable independence>, and the <exclusion restriction>.

= Heritability
{title2=$h^2$}
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{wiki}

Heritability is the fraction of phenotypic variance in a specified population and environment attributed to genetic variation. It is a population parameter, not the genetic fraction of one individual's trait.

= Narrow-sense heritability
{parent=Heritability}
{title2=$h^2=V_A/V_P$}

The ratio of additive genetic variance to total phenotypic variance in a specified population and environment. It is not the fraction of one individual attributable to genes.

= Twin study
{parent=Heritability}
{wiki}

A twin study compares trait resemblance between twins with different degrees of genetic relatedness to separate genetic and environmental variance components.

= Monozygotic twin
{title2=MZ twin}
{parent=Twin study}

This is the single-zygote subtype of a <twin>.

Monozygotic twins develop from one zygote and are modeled as sharing essentially all additive genetic variation.

= Dizygotic twin
{title2=DZ twin}
{parent=Twin study}

This is the two-zygote subtype of a <twin>.

Dizygotic twins develop from two zygotes and, absent assortative mating and other complications, share one half of additive genetic variation on average.

= ACE model
{c}
{parent=Twin study}
{wiki}

The ACE model decomposes trait variation into additive genetic effects $A$, common environmental effects $C$, and unique environmental effects $E$.

= Falconer's formula
{c}
{parent=ACE model}
{wiki}

Falconer's formula estimates narrow-sense heritability from monozygotic and dizygotic twin correlations:
$$
h^2=2(r_{\rm MZ}-r_{\rm DZ}).
$$
It relies on equal relevant environments and a dizygotic additive-genetic correlation of one half.

= Assortative mating
{parent=Quantitative genetics}
{wiki}

Assortative mating is nonrandom mating in which partners are more or less similar than random pairs for a trait or correlated characteristic. Positive assortment can increase genetic similarity among siblings.
