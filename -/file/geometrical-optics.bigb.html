= Geometrical optics
{wiki}

= Geometric optics
{synonym}

Geometrical optics describes propagation by rays and <Snell's law>, neglecting <diffraction> on scales much larger than the <wavelength>.

A <light ray> is a path in an approximation, not a physical thread of zero width. In a transparent, homogeneous, isotropic medium it is straight and perpendicular to a <wavefront>; boundaries redirect it by the <law of reflection> or <Snell's law>. The approximation is useful when the <wavelength> is small compared with relevant apertures, surface curvature scales, and distances over which the <refractive index> changes. For an illustrative <wavelength> of $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and a $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ aperture, their ratio is $5\times10^{-5}$. That small ratio supports <ray tracing> for image location, but does not eliminate <diffraction> when calculating the fine structure of a focused spot. At a <wave caustic>, <geometrical optics> can fail despite large optical elements. <MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>{ref}.

The mechanism of <refraction> is a change in <phase velocity>. For transparent isotropic media at the chosen <frequency>, the <refractive index> is
$$
n=\frac{c}{v_{\mathrm{phase}}},\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2,
$$
where $c$ is the vacuum <speed of light> and the <angles> are measured from the interface <normal vector>. At a stationary planar interface the <frequency> and tangential <wavevector> match across the boundary. Since the <wavenumber> is $k=n\omega/c$, that matching gives <Snell's law>. The transmitted <wavelength> changes while the <frequency> stays fixed. Here $n$ uses <phase velocity>, which need not equal <group velocity>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-3-refraction[OpenStax: Refraction]{ref}. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/ce6075363fa304e0a61078f7048e96f9_maxwell_eq_iso.pdf[MIT: Classical electromagnetism and optics, phase and group velocities]{ref}.

Choose model air with $n_1=1$ and model glass with $n_2=1.5$, without claiming these are exact values for every sample or <wavelength>. At $\theta_1=45^\circ$, <Snell's law> gives
$$
\theta_2=\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\sin45^\circ}{1.5}\right)\approx28.1255^\circ.
$$
The transmitted <light ray> bends toward the <normal vector>; its <phase velocity> is $c/1.5$, and its <wavelength> is two thirds of the air value. By <reversibility of an optical ray>, incidence from glass at $28.1255^\circ$ returns the outgoing air <angle> $45^\circ$.

Incidence from glass at $45^\circ$ is a different experiment. It would require $\sin\theta_2=1.5\sin45^\circ\approx1.06066$, so there is no real transmitted <angle>. The <critical angle for total internal reflection> is
$$
\theta_c=\arcsin(1/1.5)\approx41.8103^\circ.
$$
Above this <angle>, an ideal lossless glass-air boundary produces <total internal reflection>. The <physical optics> description still permits an <evanescent wave> in the air, although no propagating transmitted <light ray> crosses the boundary. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-4-total-internal-reflection[OpenStax: Total internal reflection]{ref}. https://web.mit.edu/2.710/Fall06/2.710-wk7-b-ho.pdf[MIT optics: Total internal reflection and evanescent waves, slide 40]{ref}.

The usefulness of <geometrical optics> is that complicated instruments can be assembled from these local rules. Applying <Snell's law> at successive curved surfaces changes a spreading bundle into a converging one. The <Fermat principle> unifies the construction through stationary <optical path length>; stationarity does not require a global minimum. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_26.html[Feynman Lectures: The principle of least time, section 26-5]{ref}. Fang's MIT notes explicitly connect this principle to <specular reflection>, <refraction>, and <thin lens> imaging, providing a route from individual paths to complete instruments. <MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>{ref}.

= Optical rays in a reciprocal-height refractive index
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$\mu(y)=1/y,\quad (x-c)^2+y^2=R^2$}

For the <refractive index> $\mu=1/y$ on the upper half-plane, <Fermat principle> and the <Beltrami identity> give $1/[y\sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^2}]=1/R$. The nonvertical rays are arcs of <circles> centered on $y=0$; vertical lines are the remaining rays. These are also <geodesics> of the <Poincare half-plane model>. A ray entering the strip $0<y\leq1$ toward decreasing $y$ at angle $\theta$ to the vertical has limiting horizontal displacement $\tan(\theta/2)$, at most one. The line $y=0$ is approached only after infinite <optical path length> because the <refractive index> diverges there.

= Optical momentum of a ray
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$p=\mu t,\quad dp/ds=\nabla\mu$}

For a <light ray> parametrized by <arc length> $s$, with <unit tangent vector> $t$ in a smooth positive <refractive index> $\mu$, the optical momentum is $p=\mu t$. This is the momentum for optical path-length normalization. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> for <Fermat principle>, $\int\mu(r)|r'(\tau)|d\tau$, gives $d(\mu t)/ds=\nabla\mu$. Therefore an index independent of $z$ conserves $p_z=\mu\,dz/ds$. In coordinates $r(z),\theta(z)$ this is $\mu/\sqrt{1+r'^2+r^2\theta'^2}$, the first integral supplied by the <Beltrami identity for several dependent variables>.

= Helical rays in a radially graded refractive index
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$\omega^2=-\mu'(R)/[R(\mu(R)+R\mu'(R))]$}

For a positive <refractive index> $\mu(r)$ in <cylindrical coordinates>, a <light ray> with constant radius $R>0$ and varying axial coordinate has the form $\theta(z)=\theta_0+\omega z$ when it exists. <Fermat principle> makes $L=\mu(r)\sqrt{1+r'^2+r^2\theta'^2}$ stationary. Conservation of the momentum conjugate to the cyclic angle makes $\theta'$ constant along a constant-radius path. The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> is then
$$
\mu'(R)(1+R^2\omega^2)+\mu(R)R\omega^2=0,
$$
giving the displayed formula. A nonzero real <helix> requires $0<-R\mu'(R)/\mu(R)<1$. If $\mu'(R)=0$, $\omega=0$ gives a straight axial <light ray>; a generic radial profile need not support a constant-radius <helix>. Its <helical pitch> is $2\pi/|\omega|$. This ray model is useful for a <graded-index optical fiber> when the <refractive index> varies smoothly and <geometrical optics> applies; it does not by itself impose wave-mode quantization or boundary confinement.

= Mirage
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{wiki}

A mirage is an apparent displaced image caused by <refraction> through a spatially varying <refractive index>. Above a hot surface, low-index air near the ground can bend a <light ray> back upwards before it reaches the surface. An observer tracing the arriving ray backwards along its local tangent can then infer an apparent image below the turning region. This explains the appearance of a distant reflective surface without requiring reflection at the road itself.

= Catenary light ray in a linear refractive index
{parent=Mirage}
{title2=$x(y)=-1/\beta+(c/\beta)\cosh[\beta(y-y_0)/c]$}

For height $x$ and horizontal position $y$, <Fermat principle> in a positive index profile $n(x)=1+\beta x$, $\beta>0$, conserves $n(x)y'/\sqrt{1+(y')^2}$ on a graph $y(x)$. For positive magnitude $c$ of the horizontal optical momentum, integration gives the displayed <catenary>. Its minimum height is $x_0=(c-1)/\beta$, at $y=y_0$. Thus $c>1$ produces a smooth turning ray wholly above a road at $x=0$. The two graph branches have opposite signs of $dy/dx$; $x(y)$ continues smoothly through their common horizontal tangent. For a signed negative graph constant, use $|c|$ in the prefactor; $c=0$ gives a vertical ray separately.

= Two stationary catenary rays in an inverse-height speed profile
{parent=Catenary light ray in a linear refractive index}
{title2=$y=a\cosh(x/a),\quad a\cosh(d/a)=H$}

For speed $c(y)=1/y$ in $y>0$, <Fermat principle> gives the elapsed-time integrand $y\sqrt{1+(y')^2}$. Its symmetric stationary <light rays> through $(\pm d,H)$ are the displayed <catenaries>. Set $t=d/a$. The endpoint equation is $\cosh t/t=H/d$. This function decreases and then increases, with unique minimum where $t\tanh t=1$. Thus above the minimum there are two stationary rays, at the minimum one, and below it none. The travel time on either ray is $T=ad+(a^2/2)\sinh(2d/a)$, and its minimum height is $a$.

For $d=1$ and $H=\cosh1$, one solution is $a=1$, with $T=1+\sinh2/2$. A second has $a\simeq0.7021026952$, with $T\simeq2.8224491447$. Hence these endpoints do not uniquely specify the ray. Stationarity is distinct from global minimization: paths descending almost to $y=0$ have limiting travel time $H^2$, below either stationary value in this example.

= Optical lever
{parent=Geometrical optics}

An angular readout using a reflected <light ray>: rotating a <mirror> by $\theta$ rotates the reflected <light ray> by $2\theta$, giving spot <displacement> $x\simeq2D\theta$ at distance $D$ for small <angles>.

= Wave caustic
{parent=Geometrical optics}

= Caustic
{synonym}

A <wave caustic> is a locus where the projection of a family of rays or wavefronts becomes singular. Several ray paths may coalesce there, and the geometrical approximation can predict divergent intensity. Finite-wavelength <diffraction> replaces that ideal divergence by a resolved wave pattern. The construction applies to acoustic as well as optical waves.

= Light ray
{parent=Geometrical optics}

A path of light propagation in the <geometrical optics> approximation. In a smoothly varying propagation speed, <Fermat principle> makes its travel time stationary.

= Circular light ray in a linear speed profile
{parent=Light ray}
{title2=$(x-x_0)^2+(y+c_0)^2=R^2$}

For speed $c(y)=y+c_0>0$, <Fermat principle> and the <Beltrami identity> yield circular <light rays> whose centres lie on $y=-c_0$. The ray between $(\pm a,0)$ in the upper half-plane is $y=\sqrt{a^2+c_0^2-x^2}-c_0$.

= Meridional ray
{parent=Light ray}

A <light ray> whose path remains in a plane containing the <optical axis>. In a straight <step-index optical fiber>, this geometry gives the complementary axis and interface-normal <angles> used to derive the <optical fiber acceptance angle>.

= Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf k|^2$}

For the <wave equation> $\varphi_{tt}-\nabla\cdot(c^2\nabla\varphi)=0$ with slow variables and phase $e^{i\theta/\varepsilon}$, define $\omega=-\theta_T$ and $\mathbf k=\nabla\theta$. The leading balance is $\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf k|^2$. Its positive-frequency <dispersion relation> is $\Omega=c|\mathbf k|$, and the <group velocity> is $c\mathbf k/|\mathbf k|$.

= Wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave
{parent=Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$(\omega A^2)_T+\nabla\cdot(c^2\mathbf kA^2)=0$}

The first-order imaginary balance of the <wave equation> in <geometrical optics> gives $\partial_T(\omega A^2)+\nabla\cdot(c^2\mathbf k A^2)=0$ for a real leading <amplitude>. The action density $\omega A^2$ travels with the <group velocity> $c^2\mathbf k/\omega$. For a complex <amplitude> replace $A^2$ by $|A|^2$.

= Stationary wave amplitude in a stratified wavespeed
{parent=Wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$A=\mu/(c\sqrt\ell)$}

For an individual monochromatic component in $c=c(Y)$, conserved $\omega,k$ give $\ell=\sqrt{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}$. With amplitude independent of $X,T$, <wave-action transport for a variable-speed wave> conserves $c^2\ell A^2$ on a positive-$\ell$ branch, hence $A=\mu/(c\sqrt\ell)$. The divergence at $\ell=0$ signals failure of the outer <WKB approximation>.

= Phase compatibility in geometrical optics
{parent=Eikonal equation for a variable-speed wave}
{title2=$k_Y=\ell_X,\quad\mathbf k_T=-\nabla\omega$}

A smooth phase gives $\partial_{X_i}k_j=\partial_{X_j}k_i$ and $\mathbf k_T=-\nabla\omega$. These mixed-<derivative> identities allow the spatially differentiated <dispersion relation> to be rewritten as <Hamiltonian ray equations for a local dispersion relation>. Away from phase singularities they express the curl-free character of a local wavevector.

= Lagrange optical invariant
{c}
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$H=n(y_1u_2-y_2u_1)$}

For paraxial rays, the <Lagrange optical invariant> is $H=n(y_1u_2-y_2u_1)$. Lossless paraxial ray transfer preserves this quantity: in coordinates $(y,nu)$, free propagation and a <thin lens> act by <matrices> with <determinant> one, which preserve the oriented area of two ray vectors. Consequently a full angular <optical slit> width $\theta$ at an <optical pupil> <diameter> $D$ satisfies $D\theta=A_{\rm cam}p/f_{\rm cam}$ for its image width $p$ and <optical camera> <optical pupil> width $A_{\rm cam}$ in the same meridional plane.

= Etendue
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{title2=$G=n^2A\Omega$}

<Etendue> measures how much position-and-direction space a bundle of <light rays> occupies. A small spot accepting a wide range of directions can have the same <etendue> as a large spot accepting a narrow range. This makes <etendue> useful for deciding whether an <optical instrument> can deliver an extended source's entire admitted <radiant flux> to a small receiver. For forward crossings of a surface, the <geometrical optics> definition is
$$
G=\int_{\mathcal B}n^2\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega,
\qquad 0\leq\theta<\frac{\pi}{2},
$$
where $\mathcal B$ is the occupied set of surface positions and directions, $n$ is the local <refractive index>, $dA$ is actual surface <area>, $d\Omega$ is <solid angle>, and $\theta$ is the <angle> to the forward <unit normal>. The <cosine> supplies the <projected area>. Opposite crossing directions must be counted separately with positive <projected area>, rather than cancelling them in a signed <integral>. <Étendue (Photon Snacks) by R. John Koshel>[Étendue, p. 1, equation (1.1): refractive-index-weighted projected-area integral]{ref}

The shorthand $G\simeq n^2A\Omega$ assumes constant <refractive index>, a narrow nearly normal bundle, and the same admitted directions across the whole <area> $A$. At larger <angles>, use the <cosine>-weighted <integral>; a position-dependent angular acceptance cannot generally be replaced by the product of two independent extents.

= Conservation of etendue
{parent=Etendue}
{title2=$G_{\rm out}=G_{\rm in}$}

Lossless reversible propagation preserves the <etendue> of the same bundle of <light rays>. Here the model uses stationary isotropic media, <specular reflection> and <refraction>, with every selected <light ray> reaching the output and with no <optical gain> or <scattering of light>. An <aperture> that blocks some incident <light rays> changes which bundle is counted; the full incident and transmitted <etendue> need not be equal. <Étendue (Photon Snacks) by R. John Koshel>[Étendue, pp. 1–2, lossless-system and entrance-pupil restrictions; p. 3, Figure 1, preserved generalized phase-space element]{ref}

The underlying <phase space> has four coordinates. In a planar section with constant <refractive index>, let $(x,y)$ locate a <light ray> and set
$$
p_x=n\sin\theta\cos\phi,\qquad p_y=n\sin\theta\sin\phi.
$$
The <Jacobian determinant> from <spherical coordinates> gives
$$
dp_x\,dp_y=n^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi
=n^2\cos\theta\,d\Omega,
\qquad dG=dx\,dy\,dp_x\,dp_y.
$$
Thus <etendue> is a four-dimensional volume, preserved by the optical counterpart of <Liouville theorem in Hamiltonian mechanics>. Individual position and direction extents may change, and correlations between them matter. The <Lagrange optical invariant> is a related <paraxial approximation> invariant for pairs of <light rays>, rather than the full four-dimensional <measure>.

= Basic radiance
{parent=Conservation of etendue}
{title2=$L/n^2$}

= Reduced radiance
{synonym}

<Basic radiance> is <radiance> divided by the square of the <refractive index>. For <radiance> $L$ at a fixed <wavelength>,
$$
d\Phi=\frac{L}{n^2}\,dG.
$$
Lossless propagation conserves <reduced radiance> along each <light ray>; $L$ itself is conserved only when the endpoint <refractive indices> agree. A larger <irradiance> at a smaller image can result from admitting more directions without increasing <reduced radiance>. Uniform <wave absorption> can lower <radiant flux> while leaving the occupied geometric <etendue> unchanged; an opaque <aperture> removes part of that occupied set. <Radiative Transfer lecture notes by John E. Greivenkamp>[Section 22, slides 22-18 and 22-20: lossless radiance conservation and invariant basic radiance L/n²]{ref}

= Etendue of a circular aperture and cone
{parent=Etendue}
{title2=$G=n^2A\pi\sin^2\theta_{\max}$}

Consider a planar circular <aperture> of <diameter> $D$ in constant <refractive index> $n$. Suppose every position in its <area> $A=\pi D^2/4$ is filled with directions inside the same rotationally symmetric cone about the <unit normal>, with half-<angle> $\theta_{\max}$. Using <spherical coordinates>, the exact <etendue> is
$$
G=n^2A\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\theta_{\max}}
\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi
=n^2A\pi\sin^2\theta_{\max}=\pi A\,\mathrm{NA}^2,
$$
where $\mathrm{NA}=n\sin\theta_{\max}$ is the appropriate side's <numerical aperture>. The ordinary cone <solid angle> is $2\pi(1-\cos\theta_{\max})$; its <cosine>-weighted value is $\pi\sin^2\theta_{\max}$. A full forward <hemisphere> therefore gives $G=n^2A\pi$, rather than $2\pi n^2A$. <Radiative Transfer lecture notes by John E. Greivenkamp>[Section 22, slides 22-3–22-4, solid-angle element and exact cone solid angle; slide 22-7, projected-area hemisphere factor]{ref}

For an original model example, choose $n=1$, $D=10\,\mathrm{mm}$, and $\theta_{\max}=30^\circ$. Then
$$
A=25\pi\,\mathrm{mm^2},\qquad
G=\frac{25\pi^2}{4}\,\mathrm{mm^2\,sr}
\approx61.685\,\mathrm{mm^2\,sr}.
$$
If the <radiance> is uniform across this filled bundle and equals $L=100\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$, its <radiant flux> is $\Phi=LG/n^2\approx6.1685\,\mathrm{mW}$. Neither the geometry nor the chosen <radiance> is an experimental measurement.

= Area-angle tradeoff
{parent=Etendue}
{title2=$n_1^2A_1\sin^2\theta_1=n_2^2A_2\sin^2\theta_2$}

When both endpoint bundles have the filled circular-cone geometry of <etendue of a circular aperture and cone>, <conservation of etendue> gives
$$
n_1^2A_1\sin^2\theta_1=n_2^2A_2\sin^2\theta_2.
$$
For the same <refractive index>, quadrupling the <area> halves the <sine> of the cone half-<angle>. In the $10\,\mathrm{mm}$, $30^\circ$ example, an ideal output of <diameter> $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ must have $\sin\theta_2=1/4$, so $\theta_2\approx14.4775^\circ$. The exact result differs from simply halving $30^\circ$. Conversely, reducing the <diameter> to $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ requires a complete forward <hemisphere>, $\theta_2=90^\circ$, as a limiting case.

Equal bounding <area> and <solid angle> do not alone prove equal <etendue>: an actual output can have unoccupied directions or position-direction correlations. The filled-cone equation is an exact model of the specified bundles and a capacity constraint on an <optical instrument>, not a guarantee of an aberration-free implementation.

= Aperture truncation of etendue
{parent=Etendue}
{title2=$G_{\rm transmitted}/G_{\rm incident}=(d/D)^2$}

Insert a centred opaque circular stop of <diameter> $d=6\,\mathrm{mm}$ directly in the plane of the $D=10\,\mathrm{mm}$ bundle. It transmits the same $30^\circ$ directions at every surviving position, but discards the surrounding annulus. Therefore
$$
\frac{G_{\rm transmitted}}{G_{\rm incident}}=\frac{d^2}{D^2}=0.36,
\qquad
G_{\rm transmitted}=\frac{9\pi^2}{4}\,\mathrm{mm^2\,sr}
\approx22.207\,\mathrm{mm^2\,sr}.
$$
With the uniform <radiance> of <etendue of a circular aperture and cone>, the transmitted <radiant flux> is $2.2207\,\mathrm{mW}$ and the blocked fraction is $64\%$. Surviving <light rays> retain their <radiance>; the stop has not concentrated them. Lossless propagation after the stop preserves the <etendue> of this surviving subset, not that of the original incident bundle. A stop placed elsewhere can select positions and directions together, so its physical <area> ratio alone need not give the transmitted <radiant flux> fraction.

= Optical concentrator
{parent=Etendue}
{title2=$C=A_{\rm in}/A_{\rm out}$}

An <optical concentrator> redirects <light> from an entrance <area> $A_{\rm in}$ to a smaller receiver <area> $A_{\rm out}$. Its geometric concentration ratio is $C=A_{\rm in}/A_{\rm out}$. Specify the admitted angular distribution and losses before interpreting $C$ as an <irradiance> gain: if a fraction $\eta$ of incident <radiant flux> reaches the receiver, the ratio of mean receiver to mean entrance <irradiance> is $\eta C$.

= Etendue concentration limit
{parent=Optical concentrator}
{title2=$C\leq n_{\rm out}^2/(n_{\rm in}^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm in})$}

Suppose an extended source fills every position of the entrance <aperture> with a cone of half-<angle> $\theta_{\rm in}$, and all these <light rays> must reach a one-sided planar receiver. If the receiver accepts at most a cone of half-<angle> $\theta_{\rm out}$, its available <etendue> bounds the admitted <etendue>:
$$
n_{\rm in}^2A_{\rm in}\pi\sin^2\theta_{\rm in}
\leq n_{\rm out}^2A_{\rm out}\pi\sin^2\theta_{\rm out}.
$$
Consequently
$$
C\leq\frac{n_{\rm out}^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm out}}
{n_{\rm in}^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm in}}
\leq\frac{n_{\rm out}^2}{n_{\rm in}^2\sin^2\theta_{\rm in}}.
$$
This original derivation combines <conservation of etendue> with the exact cone <integral>; the second inequality allows the complete output <hemisphere>. It bounds capacity and does not prove that a chosen shape attains the bound.

\Image[etendue-aperture-truncation-concentration.png]
{title=<Aperture> clipping discards <etendue>; full-flux concentration requires a larger output angular range}
{description=Left: a $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ stop transmits $36\%$ of the spatial disk with unchanged directional acceptance. For the $30^\circ$ input cone with $n_{\rm in}=n_{\rm out}=1$, $C\leq4$: a $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ entrance cannot deliver the entire filled bundle to a circular receiver smaller than $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in <diameter>. If the output half-<angle> is restricted to $60^\circ$, $C\leq3$ and the receiver <diameter> must be at least $10/\sqrt3\approx5.7735\,\mathrm{mm}$. For a receiver medium with model <refractive index> $n_{\rm out}=1.5$, the full-<hemisphere> capacity bound instead gives $C\leq9$; that larger value also depends on transmitting the bundle into that medium.

Attempting $C=10$ in the air-to-air example forces rejection of some input. For the uniform <radiance> already specified, even filling the entire output <hemisphere> gives
$$
\eta=\frac{\Phi_{\rm out}}{\Phi_{\rm in}}
\leq\frac{G_{\rm out,capacity}}{G_{\rm in}}=\frac4{10}=0.4.
$$
The maximum mean <irradiance> gain remains $\eta C\leq4$. With nonuniform <radiance>, rejecting a given fraction of geometric <etendue> need not reject the same fraction of <radiant flux>.

These bounds use a filled extended-source bundle in passive reversible <geometrical optics>, comparing the same <wavelength> and the same radiation channels. An ideal <optical point source> does not fill that four-dimensional set. <Diffraction>, <optical coherence>, or coupling a <Gaussian beam> into an <optical fiber> require a <physical optics> calculation; the cone bound is not a universal guarantee for a single wave mode. Likewise, <optical gain> or <fluorescence> changes the propagation assumptions.

= Etendue bench measurement
{parent=Etendue}

A proposed <optical bench> test uses a steady illuminated diffuse patch, a relay made from two <optical lens> elements, interchangeable centred circular stops, and a calibrated <optical power meter> large enough to collect the whole output bundle. Start with small cone <angles> so an ordinary relay approximates the <area-angle tradeoff>. Measure the patch's image <diameter> and the accepted angular extent at both conjugate planes; the <transverse magnification> gives the spatial scale, but does not by itself measure <etendue>.

Map the local <radiance> with a baffled <photodiode> on translation and rotation stages, using two small aligned holes to select a narrow known <solid angle>. Keep the sampled position fixed while scanning direction, calibrate the sampling footprint and angular response, and repeat at several positions. Numerically integrate $n^2\cos\theta\,dA\,d\Omega$ over the occupied samples, and integrate $(L/n^2)dG$ to predict the collected <radiant flux>. This checks the filled-cone assumption instead of inferring it from two outer dimensions.

Compare the relay without a stop and with a stop of $0.6$ times the illuminated <diameter> in the same plane. The uniform-bundle prediction is a <radiant flux> ratio of $0.36$, with unchanged local <radiance> in the surviving samples. Cover the input for a dark reading, monitor source drift, repeat the open-stop reading, check detector linearity with calibrated <optical attenuators>, and enlarge the collecting detector or scan it to check for missed <light rays>. Lens losses, angular response, illumination gradients, alignment errors and <diffraction> can spoil the ideal prediction; their effects must be measured separately. This is a procedure to try, with predicted readouts, not purported experimental data.

= Optical axis

An optical axis is the reference line through the centres of rotational symmetry of an optical system. A folded system can have successive axis segments related by reflection.

= Law of reflection
{parent=Geometrical optics}

The incident ray, reflected ray and surface normal lie in one plane; incidence and reflection angles measured from the normal are equal. For a unit incident direction $\mathbf d$ and unit normal $\mathbf n$, reflection gives $\mathbf d-2(\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n$.

= Reversibility of an optical ray
{parent=Geometrical optics}

In a stationary reciprocal optical medium, reversing the direction of a ray produces a ray along the same path. <Snell's law> is unchanged when its two media and ray directions are interchanged.

= Optical lens
{synonym}

= Lens
{synonym}

An <optical lens> is a transparent element, or assembly of elements, that redirects a bundle of <light rays> to form images or change how the bundle spreads. In an ordinary glass <optical lens>, <refraction> at the surfaces provides the redirection: their changing <normal vectors> make <Snell's law> bend different <light rays> by different amounts. A converging <optical lens> brings incoming <light rays> parallel to its <optical axis> toward an <optical focus>; a diverging <optical lens> makes them spread as if from an <optical focus> on the incoming side. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-4-thin-lenses[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, OpenStax University Physics Volume 3, §2.4: Thin lenses]{ref}.

For a usual glass <thin lens> in air, a form thicker at the centre than at the edges normally converges, with positive <focal length>; one thinner at the centre normally diverges. Shape alone is insufficient: the <refractive index> relative to the surroundings also matters, as the <lensmaker's equation> shows. An <aperture> selects which <light rays> pass; an <optical lens> also changes their directions. The distinction explains how an <optical camera> can gather <light> from one object point over many incoming directions and deliver it to one image location.

The beautiful simplification is that an entire bundle can be characterized by a <focal length>, and two constructed <light rays> can locate an image. The <thin lens> examples below turn that geometry into a prediction of image position, orientation and <transverse magnification>. A real <optical lens> retains <optical aberrations> and <diffraction>, so the construction is a useful leading model rather than an exact solution of <Maxwell's equations>.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>[§H]{ref}

Early mathematical treatments include <Johannes Kepler>'s 1611 Dioptrice, whose title explicitly concerns vision through newly invented instruments using transparent glass, and René Descartes's 1637 La Dioptrique, whose seventh discourse discusses improving vision with shaped glass. These are landmarks in explaining and designing <optical instruments>, rather than dates for the invention of the <optical lens>. https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/120202[Kepler, Dioptrice, 1611, original edition digitized by ETH-Bibliothek]{ref}; https://classiques.uqam.ca/classiques/Descartes/dioptrique/dioptrique.html[Descartes, La Dioptrique, 1637, seventh discourse, UQAM text edition]{ref}.

= Optical focus
{synonym}

An <optical focus> is a point where a specified bundle of <light rays> converges, or where its backward extensions meet. For a converging <thin lens> receiving <light rays> parallel to the <optical axis>, the rear <optical focus> is $(f,0)$ when the <thin lens> is at $x=0$; its front <optical focus> is $(-f,0)$. An object's image is generally elsewhere: the <optical focus> describes the parallel incoming bundle, while the <thin lens> equation locates the image for a finite object distance.

= Focal length
{parent=Lens (optics)}
{wiki}
{title2=$f$}

The <focal length> $f$ specifies the signed distance from an ideal <thin lens> to its <optical focus> for incident <light rays> parallel to the <optical axis>. A converging <thin lens> has $f>0$, with a rear <optical focus> beyond the <thin lens>; a diverging <thin lens> has $f<0$, with a virtual <optical focus> on the incoming side. The <focal length> is a property of the <optical lens> in its specified surroundings, not the image distance for every object. For example, a converging <thin lens> with $f=1\,\mathrm m$ images an object $3\,\mathrm m$ away at $1.5\,\mathrm m$, as computed below. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_27.html[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics I, §§27-3–27-4]{ref}.

= Optical power
{parent=Focal length}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Phi=1/f$}

The <optical power> is reciprocal signed <focal length>, $\Phi=1/f$. With $f$ expressed in metres, <optical power> is expressed in <dioptres>: $f=1\,\mathrm m$ gives $\Phi=+1\,\mathrm D$, while $f=-0.5\,\mathrm m$ gives $\Phi=-2\,\mathrm D$. The sign distinguishes converging and diverging <optical lens> elements; the magnitude measures how strongly they change the direction of a <light ray> at a given height. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses[Urone and Hinrichs, OpenStax College Physics 2e, §25.6: Optical power and diopters]{ref}.

For two ideal <thin lens> elements in contact in air, <optical powers> add: $\Phi_{\rm total}=\Phi_1+\Phi_2$. Thus $+2\,\mathrm D$ and $-1\,\mathrm D$ combine to give $+1\,\mathrm D$, or $f_{\rm total}=1\,\mathrm m$. Separation changes the rule: for two ideal <thin lens> elements separated by $d$ in air, their equivalent <optical power> is $\Phi_1+\Phi_2-d\Phi_1\Phi_2$. The contact rule is the $d=0$ case.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>[§J: Composite lenses]{ref}

= Thin lens
{parent=Lens (optics)}
{wiki}
{title2=$1/u+1/v=1/f$}

A <thin lens> models an <optical lens> by collapsing its refracting surfaces into one plane, neglecting thickness relative to the relevant <radius of curvature> values and object and image distances. Here use an ideal converging <thin lens> in air and the <paraxial approximation>: <light rays> make small <angles> with the <optical axis>, so <Snell's law> can be linearized using the <small-angle approximation>. Equal surrounding <refractive indices> on both sides give the familiar symmetric equation.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>[§§E–G]{ref}

Put the <thin lens> at $x=0$, with incident <light> travelling to the right. An object at $x=-u$ has $u>0$. The image coordinate is $x=v$: $v>0$ for a <real image> and $v<0$ for a <virtual image>. The converging <thin lens> has <focal length> $f>0$, and heights $h_o,h_i$ are signed relative to the <optical axis>. The <thin lens> equation and <transverse magnification> are
$$
\frac1f=\frac1u+\frac1v,\qquad v=\frac{fu}{u-f},\qquad m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}=-\frac vu.
$$
The expression for $v$ requires $u\ne f$. Negative <transverse magnification> means inverted; positive <transverse magnification> means upright. Remember that the positive distance $u$ differs in sign from the object's coordinate $-u$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-4-thin-lenses[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, OpenStax University Physics Volume 3, §2.4: Sign conventions]{ref}.

For <focal length> $f=1\,\mathrm m$, object distance $u=3\,\mathrm m$, and object height $h_o=0.1\,\mathrm m$, calculation gives
$$
\frac1v=\left(1-\frac13\right)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}=\frac23\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\qquad v=1.5\,\mathrm m,\qquad m=-0.5,\qquad h_i=-0.05\,\mathrm m.
$$
The <real image> is inverted, half as tall, and located $1.5\,\mathrm m$ to the right of the <thin lens>. A screen at that position receives the image because the outgoing <light rays> actually meet there.

For the same <focal length> $f=1\,\mathrm m$ and object height $h_o=0.1\,\mathrm m$, move the object inside the front <optical focus>, to $u=0.5\,\mathrm m$. Then
$$
\frac1v=(1-2)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}=-1\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\qquad v=-1\,\mathrm m,\qquad m=2,\qquad h_i=0.2\,\mathrm m.
$$
The <virtual image> is upright, twice as tall, and apparently $1\,\mathrm m$ to the left of the <thin lens>. Outgoing <light rays> still travel to the right; only their backward extensions intersect at the <virtual image>. A screen there cannot collect that image from the outgoing bundle. This geometry explains why a converging <optical lens> can act as a magnifier when held close to an object. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses[Urone and Hinrichs, OpenStax College Physics 2e, §25.6: Virtual images inside the focus]{ref}.

Both diagrams use two rules for <ray tracing>. A <light ray> from the object's tip parallel to the <optical axis> reaches the <thin lens> at $(0,h_o)$ and exits along the <straight line> through the rear <optical focus> $(f,0)$. Another <light ray> through the centre $(0,0)$ continues undeviated in the ideal <thin lens> model. Their intersection, or the intersection of their backward extensions, locates the image tip. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/2-4-thin-lenses[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, OpenStax University Physics Volume 3, §2.4: Ray tracing]{ref}.

A short geometric proof outline makes the signs transparent. The two outgoing <straight lines> are
$$
y_{\rm parallel}(x)=h_o\left(1-\frac xf\right),\qquad y_{\rm centre}(x)=-\frac{h_o}{u}x.
$$
For nonzero $h_o$, equating their heights at $x=v$ gives $1-v/f=-v/u$, hence the <thin lens> equation; the centre <light ray> gives $h_i/h_o=-v/u$, hence the <transverse magnification>. This derives the image equations from the ideal <ray tracing> rules, whose physical justification comes from <Snell's law> in the <paraxial approximation>.

At $u=f$, the two outgoing <straight lines> are parallel: there is no finite image position. For $u>f$ the <real image> has $m<0$; for $0<u<f$ the <virtual image> has $m>0$. These cases let an <optical camera> designer choose a receiving position and let a user understand a magnifier. An actual <optical lens> has finite <apertures>, which cause <diffraction> and impose <diffraction-limited resolution>; <optical aberrations> can further blur the image. The diagrams are ideal <geometrical optics>, with exact coordinates within that model, rather than exact predictions of <Maxwell's equations>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_27.html[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics I, §§27-6–27-7: Aberrations and resolving power]{ref}.

= Two equal lenses with a virtual intermediate object
{parent=Thin lens}

For two thin converging lenses, the first lens may form its would-be image beyond the second lens. The rays entering lens 2 then converge, so its Cartesian real-object distance is negative. Apply $1/f=1/u+1/v$ separately at each lens rather than treating that intermediate focus as an object upstream. In the example $u_1=2f$ with separation $d<f$, $u_2=-(2f-d)$ and $v_2=f(2f-d)/(3f-d)$. Multiplying the two signed <transverse magnifications> determines final inversion; a positive outgoing image distance means a <real image>.

= Virtual object
{parent=Thin lens}

For an optical element, a <virtual object> is a point toward which incident rays already converge, so they would meet beyond the element if it were removed. It is distinct from a <virtual image>, which is an apparent origin of outgoing rays. In the real-is-positive thin-lens convention, a <virtual object> beyond the lens has $u<0$, and $1/u+1/v=1/f$. Thus $u\to-\infty$ gives a <real image> in the rear focal plane of a converging lens.

= Newton's lens formula
{c}
{parent=Thin lens}
{title2=$u'v'=f^2$}

For a converging <thin lens> forming a <real image> of a real object, let $u,v$ be positive distances from the lens and $f$ the <focal length>. Set $u'=u-f$ and $v'=v-f$, measuring from the neighboring <focal points>. Substituting into $1/u+1/v=1/f$ gives $(f+u')(f+v')=f(2f+u'+v')$, so $u'v'=f^2$. Other object-image geometries require an explicitly consistent signed-distance convention.

= Bessel lens displacement method
{c}
{parent=Thin lens}
{title2=$f=(L^2-d^2)/(4L)$}

The <Bessel lens displacement method> measures a converging <thin lens>'s <focal length> by moving only the <optical lens> between a fixed object and screen. If their separation is $L>4f$, two settings give sharp <real images>: one enlarged, the other reduced. Read their separation $d$ on an <optical bench>, then calculate
$$
d=\sqrt{L^2-4Lf},\qquad f=\frac{L^2-d^2}{4L}.
$$
The two settings exchange object and image distances, so their signed <transverse magnifications> are reciprocals. This makes the experiment a visible test of <conjugate optical planes> as well as a <focal length> measurement.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>[p. 13, Practice problem 2 and adjacent magnification relation]{ref}

For an original stipulated example, put the object at $x=0$, the screen at $x=L=45\,\mathrm{cm}$, and take an ideal <thin lens> with $f=10\,\mathrm{cm}$. Its two settings are $x_-=15\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $x_+=30\,\mathrm{cm}$, with $d=15\,\mathrm{cm}$. The <transverse magnifications> are $m_-=-2$ and $m_+=-1/2$: both <real images> are inverted. An object $0.50\,\mathrm{cm}$ tall would give signed image heights $-1.00\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $-0.25\,\mathrm{cm}$. Conversely, stipulated readings $L=45\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $d=15\,\mathrm{cm}$ give $f=(45^2-15^2)/(4\times45)=10\,\mathrm{cm}$. These are model calculations, not measured data or a manufacturer's calibration.

A fixed offset between a carrier's reference mark and an ideal <thin lens> plane cancels when subtracting the two carrier readings. That useful cancellation does not make a <thick lens> equivalent to one unknown optical centre: its two <principal planes> can be separated. The <Bessel lens displacement experiment> below makes this limitation explicit.

= Bessel two focus positions
{c}
{parent=Bessel lens displacement method}
{title2=$x_\pm=(L\pm\sqrt{L^2-4Lf})/2$}

Place a converging <thin lens> at coordinate $x$ between the object at $0$ and the screen at $L$. In air and the <paraxial approximation>, the required object and image distances are $u=x$ and $v=L-x$. The <thin lens> equation gives a <quadratic equation>:
$$
\frac1f=\frac1x+\frac1{L-x}=\frac{L}{x(L-x)},\qquad x^2-Lx+Lf=0.
$$
Its <discriminant> is $L^2-4Lf$. For $L>4f>0$, the two roots and their separation are
$$
x_\pm=\frac{L\pm d}{2},\qquad d=x_+-x_-=\sqrt{L^2-4Lf},\qquad x_-+x_+=L.
$$
Both roots lie inside $(0,L)$, and both object and image distances exceed $f$. The two roots therefore describe finite <real images> on the screen. Exchanging the roots exchanges $u,v$; from <transverse magnification> $m=-v/u$,
$$
m_-=-\frac{L+d}{L-d},\qquad m_+=-\frac{L-d}{L+d},\qquad m_-m_+=1.
$$
The image-height ratio between the enlarged and reduced settings is $|m_-|/|m_+|=|m_-|^2$, rather than $|m_-|$.

At $L=4f$, the roots coincide: $d=0$, $x=L/2=2f$, $u=v=2f$ and $m=-1$. There is one setting, not two separately measurable settings. For $0<L<4f$ the <discriminant> is negative, so no position between the fixed object and screen gives a sharp <real image> there. This does not forbid a <real image> elsewhere if the screen is moved. Another short proof observes that $x(L-x)$ has maximum $L^2/4$ at $x=L/2$; the focusing requirement $x(L-x)=Lf$ is possible exactly when $L\ge4f$.

= Bessel lens displacement experiment
{c}
{parent=Bessel lens displacement method}

KIT's teaching apparatus uses a glass spoke-wheel or test negative as the object, a halogen lamp with an illuminating <optical lens>, an imaging <optical lens>, a screen and a triangular rail with a position scale. The illuminating <optical lens> concentrates the lamp filament's image on the imaging <optical lens>'s centre; translucent paper checks that illumination. This reduces the contribution of peripheral <light rays> and their <spherical aberration>. The instructions require fixed object-screen separation exceeding $4f$, at least ten displacement measurements, and separate red- and blue-filter measurements to test <chromatic aberration>.<Brennweite dünner Linsen by Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Faculty of Physics teaching laboratory>[Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, Brennweite dünner Linsen, Durchführung and Aufgaben 1a and 2: target, illumination check, rail readout, repeated Bessel measurements and colour controls]{ref}

The following measurement protocol elaborates that apparatus rather than reporting additional KIT results. Align the target centre, <optical lens> and screen normal along one <optical axis>; measure $L$ between the target pattern plane and receiving screen face. Lock the target and screen. Slide the imaging <optical lens> to obtain the enlarged <real image>, then the reduced <real image>, reading the same carrier reference mark at each setting. Record $r_-,r_+$ and $d=r_+-r_-$ for each pair. Record corresponding target and image intervals to test the reciprocal <transverse magnifications> independently; merely observing different image sizes is a weaker test.

Use the same central target detail to judge best <optical focus>. Bracket each setting by moving through both visibly blurred sides, repeat from both travel directions, and record the accepted position range as well as repeat scatter. Rail divisions set a readout limit but do not alone specify focusing uncertainty. Check scale calibration, carrier play and drift; a common additive carrier offset cancels in $d$, whereas scale-factor error remains. Keep illumination, <aperture>, target, screen and <optical lens> orientation fixed within a series. Change the <aperture> and colour filter in separate control series: <spherical aberration> and <chromatic aberration> can shift the apparent best <optical focus>, while a very small <aperture> introduces stronger <diffraction> and makes focus discrimination harder. Record these settings alongside the inferred <focal length>, rather than interpreting all disagreement as random <measurement error>.

For a rigid <thick lens> in air, let $h=x_{H'}-x_H$ be the signed separation of its rear and front <principal planes> along the direction of propagation. Then the positive object and image distances measured from the <principal planes> obey $u+v=L-h$. Translating the whole assembly without reversing it moves both <principal planes> equally, so the measured carrier displacement still equals the difference of the two solutions for $u$. The same algebra therefore gives
$$
d^2=(L-h)^2-4f(L-h),\qquad f=\frac{(L-h)^2-d^2}{4(L-h)},\qquad L-h>4f.
$$
Here $f$ is the effective <focal length> measured from the <principal planes>. This deduction uses the principal-plane imaging equation, not the uncorrected <thin lens> formula.<Refracting systems (details), University of Tennessee physics teaching resource>[Refracting systems (details), principal-plane definition and final paragraph on object/image distances]{ref} Mechanical centre thickness is not generally $h$. Record thickness and <optical lens> orientation, but use a justified bound or model for $h$ if it matters at the claimed precision. The two readings $L,d$ alone cannot determine both $f$ and unknown $h$; cancelling a carrier offset does not cancel this limitation.

= Bessel focal length uncertainty
{c}
{parent=Bessel lens displacement method}
{title2=$\sigma_f^2\simeq A^2\sigma_L^2+B^2\sigma_d^2+2AB\operatorname{Cov}(L,d)$}

For small <measurement errors>, write $f=L/4-d^2/(4L)$. Its <partial derivatives> and first-order <propagation of uncertainty> are
$$
A=\frac{\partial f}{\partial L}=\frac14\left(1+\frac{d^2}{L^2}\right),\qquad B=\frac{\partial f}{\partial d}=-\frac{d}{2L},
$$
$$
\sigma_f^2\simeq A^2\sigma_L^2+B^2\sigma_d^2+2AB\operatorname{Cov}(L,d).
$$
Here the $\sigma$ values are <standard deviations> used to describe uncertainty, and $\operatorname{Cov}(L,d)$ is the <covariance>. Independent carrier-reading errors give $\sigma_d^2=\sigma_{r_-}^2+\sigma_{r_+}^2$; correlated readings require the term $-2\operatorname{Cov}(r_-,r_+)$. Averaging repeated independent pairs reduces the random component of the uncertainty of the mean, but does not remove calibration or model errors.

As a stipulated uncertainty calculation, let $L=45\,\mathrm{cm}$, $d=15\,\mathrm{cm}$, $\sigma_L=0.10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $\sigma_{r_-}=\sigma_{r_+}=0.05\,\mathrm{cm}$, with all three input errors independent. Then $\sigma_d=0.0707\,\mathrm{cm}$, $A=5/18$, $B=-1/6$ and $\sigma_f\simeq0.0302\,\mathrm{cm}$. The result would be $f=10.000\,\mathrm{cm}$ with this standard uncertainty in the stipulated <thin lens> model; these numbers are not a measured uncertainty budget. A small neglected principal-plane separation gives an additional first-order bias $f_{\rm thin}-f\simeq Ah$, distinct from that random budget.

The two kinds of sensitivity must be distinguished. At fixed <focal length>,
$$
\frac{\partial d}{\partial L}=\frac{L-2f}{d},\qquad \frac{\partial x_\pm}{\partial L}=\frac12\left(1\pm\frac{L-2f}{d}\right).
$$
These <derivatives> diverge as $L$ approaches $4f$ from above: a small change in object-screen spacing strongly changes the separation of the two settings. Yet the inferred <focal length>'s displacement sensitivity $|B|=d/(2L)$ tends to zero. That does not make coalescing settings easy to measure. For fixed $L$, define the axial mismatch between the predicted <real image> and the screen as $g(x)=fx/(x-f)-(L-x)$, for $x>f$. At a focused setting,
$$
g'(x)=1-\frac{f^2}{(x-f)^2}=1-m^2.
$$
Near $u=v=2f$, moving the <optical lens> changes this mismatch only weakly, making visual discrimination of the two settings difficult. At the example's settings $g'(15\,\mathrm{cm})=-3$ and $g'(30\,\mathrm{cm})=3/4$, so equal carrier motion produces different axial <defocus> responses. This is an axial mismatch calculation, not a full prediction of perceived image sharpness.

Choose $L$ sufficiently above $4f$ to separate the two accepted focusing ranges, while retaining both <real images> on the available screen and rail. There is no universal best $L$: focusing uncertainty itself depends on <aperture>, <transverse magnification>, <optical aberrations> and the target. Close to $d=0$, the nonnegative measured separation and the quadratic term in $d$ also make first-order <propagation of uncertainty> unreliable if its error is comparable to its value. Repeatability, controls and the <principal plane> correction determine whether the elegant two-distance formula is an adequate measurement model.

= Lensmaker's equation
{parent=Thin lens}

The <lensmaker's equation> connects <focal length> to the <refractive index> and shape of a <thin lens>. For a <thin lens> in air with surfaces shaped as parts of <spheres>, <refractive index> $n$, and incident <light> travelling to the right,
$$
\frac1f=(n-1)\left(\frac1{R_a}-\frac1{R_b}\right).
$$
Each signed <radius of curvature> is positive when its centre of <curvature> lies to the right of that surface, and negative when it lies to the left. A symmetric biconvex <thin lens> with $n=1.5$, $R_a=1\,\mathrm m$, and $R_b=-1\,\mathrm m$ therefore has $1/f=0.5(1+1)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}=1\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, giving $f=1\,\mathrm m$ and <optical power> $+1\,\mathrm D$. This is an ideal shape example consistent with the <thin lens> diagrams, not a specification of their finite thickness.

In a uniform surrounding medium of <refractive index> $n_s$ on both sides, replace $n-1$ by $n/n_s-1$. Thus the same shape reverses its focusing sign if its <refractive index> is lower than its surroundings: for $n=1.5$ and $n_s=2$, the same radii give $1/f=-0.5\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, or $f=-2\,\mathrm m$. Positive <focal length> therefore cannot be inferred from convex shape in every environment. A proof outline applies <Snell's law> with the <small-angle approximation> at each surface, adds their direction changes, and neglects the distance between them. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_27.html[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics I, §27-3, equations 27.10–27.12]{ref}.

= Optical aberration
{parent=Geometrical optics}
{wiki}

An <optical aberration> is a departure from ideal <image formation>: <light rays> from one object point may miss their intended common image point, or image positions may depart from the intended mapping. <Spherical aberration>, <coma>, and <astigmatism> describe different ways of spreading a point's <light rays>; <optical distortion> can change image shape without spreading each point. In <physical optics>, these departures can be described by <wavefront error> relative to a reference <wavefront>. <Diffraction> also gives a finite <point spread function> in a perfect <optical instrument>, so an extended image alone does not establish an <optical aberration>.

= Seidel aberration
{c}
{parent=Optical aberration}

The five Seidel aberrations of a rotationally symmetric optical system are <spherical aberration>, <coma>, <astigmatism>, <field curvature>, and <optical distortion>.

= Spherical aberration
{parent=Seidel aberration}
{wiki}

Spherical aberration makes rays at different pupil radii focus at different axial positions. Its primary pupil dependence is quartic; the balanced <Zernike spherical mode> also contains quadratic and constant terms to remove defocus and piston.

= Coma
{synonym}

<Coma> makes an off-axis <optical point source> appear asymmetric, often with a compact head and a flaring tail like a comet. Different parts of the <optical pupil> send its <light rays> to different transverse image positions. A sharp central <star> image therefore does not guarantee sharp images across an <optical telescope>'s <field of view>. In an idealized, aligned system with <rotational symmetry>, <coma> vanishes on the <optical axis>; misalignment can introduce <coma> even at the nominal field centre.<Amateur Telescope Optics by Vladimir Sacek/Coma aberration>[Amateur Telescope Optics, §4.2, opening paragraphs and Figure 39: field coma versus misalignment coma]{ref}

<Coma> differs from <spherical aberration>, whose dependence on <optical pupil> radius can blur an on-axis point without selecting a transverse direction. <Astigmatism> gives different <optical foci> in two perpendicular sections of an off-axis bundle; a through-focus sequence can change its elongated image's orientation. <Diffraction> persists when these <optical aberrations> vanish. A real asymmetric <point spread function> may mix several effects, so its appearance alone is not a unique diagnosis of <coma>.

The classical primary description belongs to the nineteenth-century <Seidel aberration> classification of five primary monochromatic image errors.<Amateur Telescope Optics by Vladimir Sacek/Seidel aberrations, wavefront aberration function>[Amateur Telescope Optics, §3.5.1, opening paragraph: historical classification and equation 5]{ref} It remains useful in modern design: OpticStudio's <Seidel Coefficients by ANSYS, Inc.> documentation lists <coma> as $W_{131}$ and tabulates each surface's contribution as well as the system sum (Discussion). Cancellation of contributions can reduce <coma> without making every surface individually free of it. The same documentation limits this calculation to specified systems with axially symmetric surfaces; higher-order terms and broken <rotational symmetry> require more general <ray tracing>.

= Seidel coma pupil mapping
{c}
{parent=Coma (optics)}
{title2=$w=h\rho^3\cos\theta$}

Consider an original, explicitly normalized model of primary <coma>, rather than a prescription for a particular <optical lens>. Let $(u,v)$ be <Cartesian coordinates> in a circular <optical pupil> divided by its radius $a$, with $u^2+v^2\leq1$. Let $h=\alpha/\alpha_0$ be a signed small field <angle> divided by an arbitrary positive reference <angle>. With <polar coordinates> $u=\rho\cos\theta$, $v=\rho\sin\theta$, prescribe the <wavefront error>
$$
W(u,v)=W_0w(u,v),\qquad
w(u,v)=h\rho^3\cos\theta=h\,u(u^2+v^2).
$$
Here $W_0$ is an arbitrary positive <optical path difference> scale, not a measured error. The cubic <optical pupil> dependence and linear field dependence are the classical Seidel form.<Amateur Telescope Optics by Vladimir Sacek/Coma aberration>[Amateur Telescope Optics, §4.2, classical coma formula after equation 15 and equation 13: pupil-cubic and field-linear dependence]{ref}

In the <paraxial approximation>, <light ray> direction errors follow the transverse <gradient> of <wavefront error>. For a reference sphere a distance $L$ from the exit <optical pupil> in a uniform image medium of <refractive index> $n$, the corresponding image-error scale is $s=LW_0/(na)$. Choose image-error coordinate signs so the normalized displacement is $\nabla w$; reversing the convention reverses the plotted orientation. Image coordinates below are errors relative to the ideal image of this particular object point, rather than positions of different objects:
$$
X=\frac{\partial w}{\partial u}=h(3u^2+v^2),\qquad
Y=\frac{\partial w}{\partial v}=2huv.
$$
The <partial derivatives> explain the asymmetry: opposite <optical pupil> points $(u,v)$ and $(-u,-v)$ land together, rather than on opposite sides of the ideal image. For $h>0$, every $X$ is nonnegative, while $Y$ can have either sign. The figures stipulate this single <wavefront error>, omit other <optical aberrations> and <diffraction>, and assign no instrument dimensions.

For a ring of fixed <optical pupil> radius $\rho$, the double-angle formulas for <trigonometric functions> give
$$
X=h\rho^2(2+\cos2\theta),\qquad
Y=h\rho^2\sin2\theta,\qquad
(X-2h\rho^2)^2+Y^2=h^2\rho^4.
$$
Thus each ring maps into a <circle> of radius $|h|\rho^2$ whose centre is displaced by $2h\rho^2$. Doubling $\rho$ quadruples both sizes; for $h=1$, the edge ring has centre $(2,0)$ and radius $1$, while the half-radius ring has centre $(1/2,0)$ and radius $1/4$. Superposing these displaced <circles> produces the comet-like envelope.

For uniform <irradiance> over the circular <optical pupil>, equal <area> samples give an equal-weight geometrical image. Its <centroid> is $(h,0)$ because $\langle u^2\rangle=\langle v^2\rangle=1/4$ and $\langle uv\rangle=0$. Moving the coordinate origin to that <centroid> translates the image; it does not remove its asymmetric shape. Adding a linear term to $w$ likewise translates all <light rays> equally. These origin choices must be distinguished from changing the <optical aberration> itself.

The model's full transverse extent is $3|h|$ along $X$ and $2|h|$ along $Y$. Halving the field <angle> halves the blur, and reversing it reverses the pattern about the ideal image. This isolates why <coma> matters for an <optical camera>'s usable <field of view>: collecting more <light> does not ensure that off-axis points remain compact. Near <diffraction-limited resolution>, a <physical optics> calculation of the <point spread function> is needed instead of interpreting the geometrical tip as an infinitely sharp image.<Amateur Telescope Optics by Vladimir Sacek/Coma aberration>[Amateur Telescope Optics, §4.2, Figure 41: geometrical spots versus diffraction images]{ref}

= Off-axis pinhole test for coma
{parent=Coma (optics)}

The institution-issued <AY 105 Lab Experiment 3: Optical aberrations by California Institute of Technology> (Spring 2011 offering, Part A, pp. 2–3, 7–8) specifies a concrete <optical bench>: a lamp, opal diffuser and condensing <optical lens> illuminate a $25$ or $30\,\mu\mathrm m$ <optical pinhole>; a photographic telephoto <optical lens> acts as a <collimator>, producing an approximately $75\,\mathrm{mm}$ beam. A test <optical lens> sits on a rotation stage, followed by an adjustable <optical microscope>. The procedure establishes the on-axis image, then rotates the test <optical lens> a few degrees and examines the off-axis image through <optical focus>. It compares this readout with through-focus <ray tracing> at the corresponding field <angles> and offers a <Ronchi ruling> to calibrate the measuring eyepiece.

Part B (pp. 9–10) substitutes a spherical mirror of $1\,\mathrm m$ radius of <curvature>, with the <optical microscope> beside the incoming beam so the image is necessarily off-axis. It calls for measuring the field <angle>, calibrating the eyepiece and recording the image's length and width at minimum size. These are specified apparatus dimensions and instructions, not supplied measurement results or dimensions of the normalized figures.

A controlled extension would record images at zero and equal positive and negative field <angles>, repeat zero afterwards, and scan through <optical focus> at every setting. Keep the illuminated <optical pupil> and illumination fixed, recenter the <optical microscope> without clipping the bundle, and use its calibrated scale to separate a mere image displacement from a change in blur shape. The pure <Seidel coma pupil mapping> predicts reversal and linear growth; a stationary asymmetric central image instead calls for checking alignment. A focus-dependent change between perpendicular elongations suggests an <astigmatism> contribution. These comparisons test the model rather than assuming every off-axis flare is pure <coma>.

The <optical pinhole> has finite angular size, approximately its <diameter> divided by the <collimator>'s <focal length>; the <collimator> and observing <optical microscope> also contribute their own <optical aberrations>. A broadband lamp can mix <chromatic aberration> with the geometric blur, and <diffraction> limits the smallest visible structure. A narrow spectral band, repeated field settings and a reference image help separate these effects. Consequently a measured length and width are useful checks on off-axis <image formation>, but they cannot by themselves determine a unique <coma> coefficient or recover the full <point spread function>.

= Chromatic aberration
{parent=Optical aberration}
{wiki}

An <optical camera> can focus a white object sharply in one colour while leaving coloured fringes around its edges in another. <Chromatic aberration> is the dependence of image position or image scale on <wavelength>, caused by <optical dispersion> in the <optical lens> materials. <Axial chromatic aberration> separates the <optical foci> along the <optical axis>; <lateral chromatic aberration> changes the <transverse magnification> with <wavelength>. Moving the receiving plane can favour one colour without bringing all colours into focus. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/302l/lectures/node142.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, University of Texas at Austin, Chromatic aberration]{ref}; https://imaging.nikon.com/sport-optics/guide/fieldscopes/choosing/choosing_03/[Nikon, Comparison of ED glass and ordinary lens]{ref}.

For ordinary <optical glass> in the visible range, the blue <refractive index> exceeds the red <refractive index>. For a fixed positive <thin lens> in air, the <lensmaker's equation> then gives
$$
\Phi(\lambda)=\frac{1}{f(\lambda)}=[n(\lambda)-1]\left(\frac{1}{R_a}-\frac{1}{R_b}\right),\qquad n_{\rm blue}>n_{\rm red}\ \Longrightarrow\ \Phi_{\rm blue}>\Phi_{\rm red}>0,\quad f_{\rm blue}<f_{\rm red}.
$$
Here $\lambda$ is <wavelength>, $n$ is <refractive index>, $\Phi$ is <optical power>, $f$ is <focal length>, and $R_a,R_b$ are the signed surface <radius of curvature> values in the <lensmaker's equation>. The blue <light rays> therefore bend more strongly and meet sooner. This ordering is a statement about ordinary visible <optical dispersion>, not a universal rule for every material and every spectral range. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/302l/lectures/node142.html[Fitzpatrick, Chromatic aberration, colour-dependent focusing]{ref}.

For a worked example, take a single positive <thin lens> with reference <optical power> $\Phi_d=5\,\mathrm D$ and <Abbe number> $V_d=60$, hence reference <focal length> $f_d=0.2\,\mathrm m$. Its F–C <optical power> difference is $\Delta\Phi=\Phi_F-\Phi_C=\Phi_d/V_d=5/60\,\mathrm D\approx0.08333\,\mathrm D$, by the <achromatic doublet power balance> relation below. This difference alone does not locate the reference <optical power> within the F–C interval. To draw a transparent example, introduce a synthetic colour coordinate $q$, a <dimensionless quantity>, with red $q=-1/2$, reference $q=0$, and blue $q=+1/2$, and choose the toy <optical dispersion> model
$$
\Phi(q)=\Phi_d\left(1+\frac{q}{V_d}\right),\qquad f(q)=\frac{1}{\Phi(q)}.
$$
The diagram's red and blue endpoints represent the C and F endpoints of this toy model. Its reference point is placed symmetrically in <optical power> by construction; $q$ is not a measured <wavelength> scale, and the model is not measured <optical glass> data. The endpoint <focal lengths> are
$$
f_{\rm red}=\frac{1}{5(1-1/120)}\,\mathrm m=\frac{24}{119}\,\mathrm m\approx0.201681\,\mathrm m,\qquad f_{\rm blue}=\frac{1}{5(1+1/120)}\,\mathrm m=\frac{24}{121}\,\mathrm m\approx0.198347\,\mathrm m.
$$
Thus the <axial chromatic aberration> in this example separates the endpoint <optical foci> by about $3.334\,\mathrm{mm}$. A single receiving plane cannot coincide with both <optical foci>.

The useful design idea is to cancel changes of <optical power> while retaining a positive total <optical power>. An <achromatic lens> does this with materials having different <Abbe numbers>. It greatly reduces <chromatic aberration>, but correcting two chosen <wavelengths> still leaves the possibility of a <secondary spectrum>.

= Achromatic lens
{parent=Chromatic aberration}
{wiki}

An <achromatic lens> combines <optical lens> elements with different <optical dispersion> so that two specified <wavelengths> have the same <focal length> in the <paraxial approximation>. This brings their parallel incoming <light rays> to a common <optical focus>, helping an <optical camera> form a sharper image under mixed-colour illumination. A common implementation is an <achromatic doublet>: a positive <crown glass> element paired with a negative <flint glass> element. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/302l/lectures/node142.html[Fitzpatrick, Chromatic aberration, compound lenses]{ref}.

The <achromatic doublet> does not require either material to have zero <optical dispersion>. Instead, the positive element gains positive <optical power> toward blue, while the negative element becomes more negative. Suitable <Abbe numbers> and signed <optical powers> let these changes cancel even though their reference <optical powers> do not cancel. For example, the <achromatic doublet power balance> calculation below combines $+10\,\mathrm D$ with $-5\,\mathrm D$ to obtain $+5\,\mathrm D$, or <focal length> $0.2\,\mathrm m$, with zero primary F–C <optical power> difference. The smaller <Abbe number> of the negative element makes its weaker reference <optical power> sufficient to oppose the positive element's colour change.

An <achromatic lens> is corrected at two chosen <wavelengths>, not necessarily at every <wavelength> between them. The remaining <focal length> variation is the <secondary spectrum>. Correcting <axial chromatic aberration> also does not automatically remove <lateral chromatic aberration> throughout an imaging system, <spherical aberration>, or <diffraction>. Actual designs therefore need <ray tracing> with the intended <wavelengths>, element thicknesses, separations and <apertures>. https://imaging.nikon.com/sport-optics/guide/fieldscopes/choosing/choosing_03/[Nikon, ED glass and secondary spectrum]{ref}; <AY 105 Lab Experiment 3: Optical aberrations by California Institute of Technology>[Caltech AY 105, Optical aberrations, 2011, Part A: doublet ray tracing and through-focus comparison]{ref}.

= Achromatic doublet
{parent=Achromatic lens}

An <achromatic doublet> is an <achromatic lens> with two <optical lens> elements. In the contact <thin lens> model, their <optical powers> add and their primary colour changes cancel according to <achromatic doublet power balance>. The conventional positive <achromatic doublet> uses a converging <crown glass> element and a weaker diverging <flint glass> element; these names describe a common choice, not a universal ordering of every modern <optical glass> composition.

= Achromatic doublet power balance
{parent=Achromatic lens}

<Achromatic doublet power balance> chooses two signed <optical powers> to meet two goals: obtain the required <focal length> and cancel the primary F–C colour difference. Model two <thin lens> elements in contact in air, using the <paraxial approximation>. Let $i=c,f$ label the <crown glass> and <flint glass> elements, and let $\Phi_{i,d}$ denote each element's <optical power> at the reference d line. The <Abbe number> for each material is
$$
V_i=\frac{n_{i,d}-1}{n_{i,F}-n_{i,C}}.
$$
The subscripts label standard <spectral lines>: blue F near $486.1\,\mathrm{nm}$, red C near $656.3\,\mathrm{nm}$, and reference d near $587.6\,\mathrm{nm}$. These are three specified <wavelengths>, not colour bands. A larger <Abbe number> means less relative <optical dispersion> between F and C. https://media.schott.com/api/public/content/b37dbd8fa7e64662b2d0ae523ae56238?download=true&v=97f67105[SCHOTT, Optical Glass 2025, §1.1 and §10: Wavelength table]{ref}.

For a fixed <thin lens> shape in air, write the <lensmaker's equation> as $\Phi_i(\lambda)=K_i[n_i(\lambda)-1]$, with signed shape factor $K_i=1/R_{i,a}-1/R_{i,b}$. Subtracting its C value from its F value gives the primary <optical power> difference
$$
\Delta\Phi_i=\Phi_{i,F}-\Phi_{i,C}=K_i(n_{i,F}-n_{i,C})=\frac{\Phi_{i,d}}{V_i}.
$$
The negative element has negative $\Delta\Phi_i$: its blue <optical power> is more negative, not less negative. The contact rule for <optical power> therefore gives
$$
\Phi_d=\Phi_{c,d}+\Phi_{f,d}=\frac{1}{f_d},\qquad \Delta\Phi_{\rm total}=\frac{\Phi_{c,d}}{V_c}+\frac{\Phi_{f,d}}{V_f}=0.
$$
This zero is equality of the total F and C <optical powers>, hence equality of their <focal lengths> when the total <optical power> is nonzero. The relation follows directly from the <lensmaker's equation> and the <Abbe number>; it is exact within the fixed-shape contact <thin lens> model. https://phys.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Optics/Geometric_Optics_(Tatum)/02:_Lens_and_Mirror_Calculations/2.10:_Designing_an_Achromatic_Doublet[Jeremy Tatum, University of Victoria, Geometric Optics §2.10, equations 2.10.8–2.10.11, authored text hosted by LibreTexts]{ref}. Tatum uses a yellow D-line reference; here the <Abbe number> convention uses the d line consistently.

For a toy design, choose $V_c=60$, $V_f=30$, $\Phi_{c,d}=10\,\mathrm D$ and $\Phi_{f,d}=-5\,\mathrm D$. Then
$$
\Phi_d=(10-5)\,\mathrm D=5\,\mathrm D,\qquad f_d=\frac{1}{5\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}}=0.2\,\mathrm m,\qquad \Delta\Phi_{\rm total}=\left(\frac{10}{60}-\frac{5}{30}\right)\mathrm D=0.
$$
The positive element contributes $+1/6\,\mathrm D$ to the F–C difference and the negative element contributes $-1/6\,\mathrm D$. This retains the same reference <focal length> as the single $5\,\mathrm D$, $V_d=60$ <thin lens> in <chromatic aberration>, while cancelling its primary colour split. The values are illustrative design parameters, not a catalogue <optical glass> pair.

Use the same synthetic colour coordinate $q=-1/2,0,+1/2$ and toy <optical dispersion> as in <chromatic aberration>. The two element <optical powers> and their sum become
$$
\Phi_c(q)=10\left(1+\frac{q}{60}\right)\mathrm D,\qquad \Phi_f(q)=-5\left(1+\frac{q}{30}\right)\mathrm D,\qquad \Phi_{\rm total}(q)=\left(5+q\left[\frac{10}{60}-\frac{5}{30}\right]\right)\mathrm D=5\,\mathrm D.
$$
At the red endpoint, the element <optical powers> are $9.916667\,\mathrm D$ and $-4.916667\,\mathrm D$; at the blue endpoint, they are $10.083333\,\mathrm D$ and $-5.083333\,\mathrm D$. Both sums are $5\,\mathrm D$. In this first-order toy model alone, the sum is constant for every $q$ and the <focal length> remains $0.2\,\mathrm m$ throughout. Real material <optical dispersion> is not captured by these two straight slopes, so the flat line is not a claim that a real <achromatic doublet> focuses all colours perfectly.

{title=<Achromatic doublet power balance>: opposite colour changes preserve $5\,\mathrm D$ in the first-order toy model}
{description=The left panel shows each signed <optical power> and their sum against synthetic colour coordinate $q$. The right panel magnifies their changes from the reference <optical powers>: equal and opposite slopes cancel. The <crown glass> and <flint glass> labels denote toy elements with $V_c=60$ and $V_f=30$, not measured <optical glass>. For specified nonzero reference <optical power> $\Phi_d$ and distinct <Abbe numbers>, solving the two balance equations gives
$$
\Phi_{c,d}=\frac{\Phi_d V_c}{V_c-V_f},\qquad \Phi_{f,d}=-\frac{\Phi_d V_f}{V_c-V_f}.
$$
Thus $\Phi_d>0$ and $V_c>V_f>0$ require positive <crown glass> <optical power> and negative <flint glass> <optical power>. If the two finite <Abbe numbers> were equal, cancellation would force zero total <optical power>. If they are close, the required element <optical powers> become large compared with the total: cancelling primary colour can demand much stronger individual elements.

The contact assumption matters. For two <thin lens> elements separated by distance $s$ in air, the equivalent <optical power> is $\Phi_c+\Phi_f-s\Phi_c\Phi_f$, evaluated at each <wavelength>; its colour change need not satisfy the contact balance. Finite thickness, departures from the <paraxial approximation>, and the <secondary spectrum> also limit the simple prediction.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Lecture Notes on Geometrical Optics>[§J: Composite lenses]{ref}

= Secondary spectrum
{parent=Achromatic lens}

The <secondary spectrum> is residual <chromatic aberration> after an <achromatic lens> brings two selected <wavelengths> to the same <optical focus>. For example, equal red and blue <focal lengths> can coexist with a different green <focal length>. The <Abbe number> fixes one F–C relative difference, not the complete shape of <optical dispersion> between or beyond those <wavelengths>. The perfectly flat sum in the <achromatic doublet power balance> diagram deliberately omits this residual effect. https://imaging.nikon.com/sport-optics/guide/fieldscopes/choosing/choosing_03/[Nikon, ED glass and secondary spectrum]{ref}.

= Optical dispersion
{synonym}

Optical dispersion is the dependence of <refractive index> or <phase velocity> on <frequency>. It gives an <optical prism> wavelength-dependent deviation and causes <chromatic aberration>.

= Normal dispersion
{parent=Dispersion (optics)}

In <normal dispersion>, the <refractive index> increases with frequency, $dn/d\omega>0$, equivalently $dn/d\lambda<0$ for vacuum wavelength $\lambda$. Transparent regions away from isolated absorption resonances often have this behaviour. Near a resonance the slope can reverse, producing <anomalous dispersion> accompanied by absorption.

= Minimum deviation
{parent=Prism (optics)}
{wiki}
{title2=$\gamma_{\min}$}

For a homogeneous <optical prism> in air, with apex angle $\phi$ and <refractive index> $n>1$, minimum deviation occurs at the symmetric ray path. Then both internal angles are $\phi/2$ and both external angles are $(\gamma_{\min}+\phi)/2$, so <Snell's law> gives
$$
n=\frac{\sin[(\gamma_{\min}+\phi)/2]}{\sin(\phi/2)}.
$$
Measuring the deviation therefore determines the refractive index at the chosen <wavelength>.
