**A: conjugated <ester>; B: unconjugated <ester>; C: terminal <alkyne> <acetal>.**

A has a strong <ester> C=O band at roughly $1715$–$1730\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, lowered by <conjugation> with C=C. B's unconjugated <ester> <carbonyl> is nearer $1735$–$1750\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$; it also has terminal-alkene features, including the approximately $990/910\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ pair.

C has **no <carbonyl> band**. Its terminal <alkyne> gives a relatively sharp sp C-H stretch near $3300\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and a usually weaker C≡C feature near $2100$–$2150\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. C-O stretching alone is less diagnostic because <esters> and the <acetal> all contain C-O bonds. The numbers are characteristic ranges, not exact frequencies inferred from an unprinted IR trace.

\Image[paper-1-q1-proton-assignments.png]{title=Approximate proton shifts and coupling patterns derived from the printed spectra}

**A, spectrum 4:** ethoxy CH$_3$ at about $1.3$ is a <NMR triplet>, and OCH$_2$ at about $4.15$ is a <NMR quartet>. The two <alkene> methyls at about $1.9$ and $2.2$ each integrate to three <protons>; they are apparent <NMR singlets> on the overview but have small allylic <NMR doublet> splitting. The vinyl CH at about $5.7$ is correspondingly a narrow <NMR multiplet>, formally a <quartet of quartets> when its two methyl couplings are unequal, or approximately a <NMR septet> if they coincide. A broadened overview must not be used to declare all those small couplings exactly zero.

**C, spectrum 5:** the <acetal> CH at about $5.25$ and terminal C≡CH at about $2.55$ each integrate to one <proton> and show their small mutual long-range <NMR doublet> splitting. The ethoxy methyls give a six-proton <NMR triplet> near $1.25$. The OCH$_2$ region has two sets near $3.6$ and $3.75$, each integrating to two <protons>. These are an <ABX3 spin system>: <geminal coupling> and coupling to CH$_3$ produce approximately one <doublet of quartets> for each <proton> type, with second-order distortion in the expanded trace. Treating the whole region as one ideal four-proton <NMR quartet> misses the printed structure.

**B, spectrum 6:** the internal <alkene> CH near $5.95$ is a <doublet of doublets of triplets> from trans/cis coupling to CH$_2$ and <vicinal coupling> to OCH$_2$. The two terminal <alkene> <protons> near $5.2$–$5.35$ are distinct <doublet of doublets>-type signals, with possible additional small allylic splitting. OCH$_2$ near $4.6$ is principally a <NMR doublet>. The acyl-alpha CH$_2$ near $2.3$ is a <NMR triplet>, the middle CH$_2$ near $1.65$ an approximate <NMR sextet> or overlapping <NMR triplet> of quartets, and the terminal methyl near $0.95$ a <NMR triplet>.

**A:** ethoxy CH$_2$ ↔ CH$_3$, $J\simeq7\,\mathrm{Hz}$; vinyl CH ↔ each <alkene> methyl, small $^4J\simeq1$–$1.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

**C:** ethoxy CH$_2$ ↔ CH$_3$, about $7\,\mathrm{Hz}$; the two OCH$_2$ <proton> types have a <geminal coupling> of about $9$–$10\,\mathrm{Hz}$; <acetal> CH ↔ C≡CH has $^4J\simeq2\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

**B:** internal vinyl CH ↔ trans terminal H, about $17\,\mathrm{Hz}$; ↔ cis terminal H, about $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$; terminal CH$_2$ <geminal coupling> about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Vinyl CH ↔ OCH$_2$ is about $6\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Both vicinal links along the butanoate alkyl chain are about $7$–$8\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

Use $J=300\,\Delta\delta$ when a separation is measured in ppm. Thus $0.057\,\mathrm{ppm}$ corresponds to about $17\,\mathrm{Hz}$, whereas $0.023\,\mathrm{ppm}$ corresponds to about $7\,\mathrm{Hz}$. These are approximate readings, not a fitted <spin> simulation.

The small four-bond splittings illustrate <allylic scalar coupling> and <acetal-alkyne long-range coupling>.

\Image[paper-1-q2-molecular-orbitals.png]{title=Qualitative valence molecular-orbital occupancies for nitrogen, oxygen, lithium and beryllium dimers}

Suppress the filled 1s core pair, which contributes zero net <bond order>. For N$_2$, use the valence ordering
$$
\sigma_{2s}<\sigma_{2s}^*<\pi_{2p}<\sigma_{2p_z}
<\pi_{2p}^*<\sigma_{2p_z}^*
$$
and occupy it as $(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2(\pi_{2p})^4(\sigma_{2p_z})^2$.

For O$_2$, the ordering of the bonding 2p levels is reversed: $\sigma_{2p_z}<\pi_{2p}$. Occupancy is $(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2(\sigma_{2p_z})^2(\pi_{2p})^4(\pi_{2p_x}^*)^1(\pi_{2p_y}^*)^1$, with parallel <spins> in the two degenerate antibonding pi orbitals.

Li$_2$ has $(\sigma_{2s})^2$; Be$_2$ has $(\sigma_{2s})^2(\sigma_{2s}^*)^2$ in the elementary valence description. Empty 2p-derived levels must also be included to discuss mixing and Be$_2$'s weak bonding; their precise energies are not set by this qualitative diagram.

**N$_2$:** <HOMO> $\sigma_{2p_z}$; <LUMO> the degenerate $\pi_{2p}^*$ pair.

**O$_2$:** the two $\pi_{2p}^*$ orbitals are the highest occupied, singly occupied spatial orbitals. The next completely empty spatial orbital is $\sigma_{2p_z}^*$. An added <electron> can instead occupy the opposite-spin vacancy in a singly occupied pi-star orbital; this distinction matters if “<LUMO>” is being used for an electron-addition <spin> orbital.

**Li$_2$:** <HOMO> $\sigma_{2s}$; next empty sigma orbital conventionally denoted $\sigma_{2s}^*$ in this elementary diagram. **Be$_2$:** <HOMO> $\sigma_{2s}^*$; the low empty 2p manifold supplies the <LUMO> candidates, drawn as $\pi_{2p}$ in the stated light-diatomic ordering. Mixing makes these labels descriptions of dominant atomic-orbital character, not strictly unmixed atomic functions.

This distinction is the <open-shell frontier orbital convention>.

Both Be$_2$'s occupied bonding and antibonding 2s levels cancel in the simplest diagram. <Beryllium>'s relatively accessible empty 2p orbitals allow mixing and correlated configurations to change that cancellation and produce a **weak bond**. An s-only single-configuration picture is therefore insufficient; spectroscopy and accurate correlated calculations establish its binding.<Beryllium dimer caught in the act of bonding by Jeremy M. Merritt and colleagues>{ref}

For He$_2$, $(\sigma_{1s})^2(\sigma_{1s}^*)^2$ similarly gives zero elementary <covalent bond> order, but the empty-shell excitation gap is much larger and there is no comparable low-energy 2s/2p near-degeneracy. Thus it has **no ordinary stable <covalent bond>**.

The literal claim that He$_2$ does not exist needs qualification: an extraordinarily weak, cold van der Waals <helium dimer> has been observed. That does not contradict the zero covalent-bond-order diagram.<The weakest bond: experimental observation of helium dimer by Fei Luo and colleagues>{ref}

The <beryllium dimer> and <helium dimer> illustrate two physically different ways that the elementary bond-order picture can miss weak binding.

All comparisons assume the same temperature and comparable reagent thermodynamic activities in <methanol>.

* **Iodomethane:** faster, because iodide is a better <leaving group> and the C-I bond is more readily broken than C-Cl.

\Image[paper-1-q3-cyclization.png]{title=Bromonium formation and intramolecular opening give the six-membered cyclic bromoether}

The <alkene> <pi bond> attacks Br$_2$ as the Br-Br bond breaks to bromide, forming a bridged, unsymmetrical **<bromonium ion>**. The <carbon> bearing the two <methyl groups> has greater carbocation-like character.

The tethered <alcohol> <oxygen> attacks that <carbon> from the side opposite the bromine bridge. Its <lone pair> forms the new O-C bond while the attacked C-Br bridge bond opens, giving a six-membered cyclic oxonium ion. Bromide or <solvent> removes the O-H <proton> to give the neutral bromoether.

This explains why O becomes attached at the more substituted <alkene> <carbon> while Br remains at the other <carbon>. Polar <solvent> assists charge separation, and intramolecular capture competes effectively with external bromide attack. Opening is anti; attack on the two faces of the achiral starting material gives a racemic product at the newly formed CHBr stereocentre.

The ring-forming step is <bromoetherification>.

\Image[paper-1-q3-acetal-lactonization.png]{title=Benzylidene-acetal hydrolysis releases a diol that closes to an unsaturated gamma-lactone}

First hydrolyze the benzylidene <acetal>. Protonate one <acetal> <oxygen> and break its bond to the Ph-CH centre, giving one free <alcohol> and a benzylic <oxocarbenium ion>. Water attacks that centre; <proton> transfers give a <hemiacetal>. Protonate the remaining acetal-derived OR group and cleave it to release the second <alcohol> and **<benzaldehyde>**.

The resulting unsaturated hydroxy <ester> is
$$
\mathrm{HO-CH(CH_2CH{=}CH_2)-C(CH_2OH){=}CH-C(O)OMe}.
$$
The OH on the allyl-substituted methine is gamma to the <ester> <carbonyl>. <Protonation> of that <carbonyl> enables intramolecular OH attack to give a five-membered <tetrahedral intermediate>. <Proton> transfer to methoxy and collapse expel <methanol>, followed by <deprotonation> to give the **alpha,beta-unsaturated gamma-lactone**.

The other liberated OH remains CH$_2$OH. Both C=C units survive: one is the allyl substituent and the other becomes the <lactone>'s conjugated double bond. <Hydrolysis> to a hydroxy acid followed by <lactonization> is an alternative sequence under the aqueous acid; direct acid-catalysed intramolecular <ester> exchange gives the same atom mapping.

Write both <electrode> reactions first as <reductions>:
$$
\mathrm{CH_3COO^-+5H^++4e^-\longrightarrow C_2H_5OH+H_2O},
$$
$$
\mathrm{CeO_2+4H^++e^-\longrightarrow Ce^{3+}+2H_2O}.

The **conventional cell reaction** runs with <oxidation> on the left and <reduction> on the right. Reverse the first equation and multiply the second by four:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{C_2H_5OH+4CeO_2+11H^+
\longrightarrow CH_3COO^-+4Ce^{3+}+7H_2O}}.
$$
Four <electrons> pass through the external circuit per reaction as written. This direction consumes the oxide; deposition requires the reverse <electrolysis> direction.

* **A:** OH$^-$ starts in 100-fold excess, so its consumption changes its <concentration> by at most about 1%. Set $h\simeq h_0=0.01\,\mathrm M$: $-dx/dt=k h_0x$, a <pseudo-first-order reaction> law.

**A and C:** integrate $dx/x=-k h_{\rm const}\,dt$:
$$
\boxed{x(t)=x_0e^{-k h_{\rm const}t}},
$$
using $h_{\rm const}=10^{-2}\,\mathrm M$ for A and $10^{-4}\,\mathrm M$ for C.

**B:** integrate $dx/x^2=-k\,dt$:
$$
\frac1{x(t)}-\frac1{x_0}=kt,\qquad
\boxed{x(t)=\frac{x_0}{1+kx_0t}}.
$$
For completeness, unbuffered unequal initial <concentrations> obey $h=x+\delta$, $\delta=h_0-x_0$, and the exact expression is
$$
\ln\frac{h(t)x_0}{x(t)h_0}=k\delta t.
$$
It approaches the equimolar second-order expression as $\delta\to0$ and the excess-hydroxide exponential when depletion is negligible.

The unequal-concentration expression is the <unequal-concentration second-order integration> formula.

A **transition structure is a <saddle point>** on the potential-energy surface: motion along the <reaction coordinate> lowers energy in either direction. It has one unstable vibrational direction and no intermediate residence basin.

An **intermediate is a local minimum**, separated from neighboring states by barriers, with a finite lifetime. It may be short-lived, but that does not make it a transition structure. The elementary <SN2 reaction> step has a transition structure and no distinct bonded intermediate.

**C-C is strongest; N-N is much weaker despite <nitrogen>'s smaller size.** <Carbon>'s effective nuclear attraction and good overlap of compact second-row valence orbitals favor a strong C-C <sigma bond>. The larger, more electron-deficient <boron> centres give less effective B-B bonding in the stated molecule, despite its relatively high-s-character trigonal bonding.

In hydrazine, the two neighboring <nitrogen> <lone pairs> strongly repel one another, destabilizing the N-N bond. Bond strength therefore cannot be predicted from atomic size alone; lone-pair repulsion explains the especially low N-N value.

This illustrates <bonding trends in second-period single bonds>.

The expected order is **B-F > C-F > N-F**.

B-F has a large favorable polar/ionic contribution because electron-deficient <boron> and <fluorine> have a large <electronegativity> difference. <Fluorine> lone-pair donation into <boron>'s empty 2p orbital can additionally strengthen bonding in planar BF$_3$. C-F is a strong, polar second-row <sigma bond>, but tetrahedral CF$_4$ has no equivalent empty <carbon> 2p acceptor for that pi donation.

N-F has less favorable bond polarity and substantial repulsion between <nitrogen> and <fluorine> <lone pairs>, making it weaker. Invoking occupied <boron> d orbitals is unnecessary and inappropriate for this second-row comparison.

**Group 6:** cohesion increases from <chromium> to <molybdenum> to <tungsten>. partially occupied d bands make a major contribution to metallic cohesion. Relative to localized 3d orbitals in Cr, more extended 4d/5d orbitals support stronger overlap and broader bonding bands. Strong 5d bonding and the compact dimensions associated with lanthanide/relativistic effects help make W especially cohesive.

**Group 11:** cohesion increases from <silver> to <copper> to <gold>. the filled d shell changes the balance from Group 6. Going from Cu to larger Ag weakens overlap and lowers cohesion. <Gold> reverses that simple size trend: relativistic 6s stabilization/contraction, changes in the 5d orbitals and stronger s-d participation enhance bonding. A size-only argument cannot explain Au exceeding both Ag and Cu.

**Group 15, As > Sb > Bi:** bonding in these elemental solids involves directional p-orbital interactions. Increasing size and longer separations weaken overlap down the group, reducing <enthalpy of atomization>. Changes in detailed solid structure and relativistic effects refine the numbers, but the underlying weakening of p-derived bonding explains the trend.

<Enthalpy of atomization> measures removal from the entire standard-state solid to gaseous ground-state atoms; it is not simply the dissociation energy of one selected diatomic bond.

**<Graphite>:** each <carbon> forms three strong in-plane <sigma bonds> in an sp$^2$ honeycomb layer. The remaining p orbitals form delocalized pi bands, allowing electrical conduction especially along the layers. Much weaker interlayer attraction permits sliding, explaining softness without implying weak in-plane C-C bonds.

**Carborundum is <silicon> carbide, SiC.** A suitable structure is a three-dimensional, tetrahedrally coordinated covalent network: each Si has four C neighbors and each C four Si neighbors. Strong directional bonding in all directions resists slip and gives hardness. Its filled bonding states are separated from empty states by a substantial <band gap>, giving low conductivity when pure and undoped.

“Non-conductor” is the intended contrast with <graphite>; SiC is more precisely a <wide-bandgap semiconductor>, whose conductivity can change with doping and temperature.

<Potassium> intercalates between <graphite> layers and **donates <electron> density to the graphitic pi system**. Occupation of formerly less populated electronic states raises the mobile-carrier density, increasing <electrical conductivity> under the stated comparison.

The gain is electronic doping of the <carbon> framework, rather than a claim that <potassium> strengthens every C-C bond or simply acts as a separate wire through the sample. Intercalation also changes layer separation and scattering, so conductivity depends on both carrier population and <mobility>.

The material is <potassium graphite>, a <graphite intercalation compound>.
