**A is <3-chlorobutan-2-one>, $\mathrm{CH_3C(=O)CH(Cl)CH_3}$.** Counting <chlorine> as one <hydrogen> in the index of <index of hydrogen deficiency> gives $(2\times4+2-7-1)/2=1$. A single <carbonyl> accounts for that unsaturation; the resonance at $203.2$ ppm identifies a <ketone>, while the $59.0$ ppm methine bears <chlorine>.

The <carbon-13 NMR> assignments are $\delta_C=203.2$ for $\mathrm{C=O}$, $25.7$ for $\mathrm{CH_3CO}$, $59.0$ for $\mathrm{CHCl}$ and $20.1$ ppm for $\mathrm{CH(Cl)CH_3}$. The four distinct <carbon> environments agree with the unsymmetrical structure. The small signal near 77 ppm belongs to the usual deuterated chloroform <solvent>, rather than to a fifth <carbon> of A.

Its relatively high-frequency <infrared spectroscopy> band identifies an <acyl chloride>, while two equivalent <methyl groups> explain why four <carbons> produce only three <carbon-13 NMR> resonances.

\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{site}&\delta_H/\mathrm{ppm}&\text{integral}&\text{multiplicity}\\\hline
a&6.480&1&\text{doublet of doublets}\\
b&4.220&1&\text{doublet of doublets}\\
c&4.072&1&\text{doublet of doublets}\\
d&3.948&2&\text{triplet}\\

\end{array}

For the $a$ <doublet of doublets>, pairing corresponding lines gives $J_{ab}\simeq300[(6.516-6.468)+(6.492-6.445)]/2=14.25$ Hz and $J_{ac}\simeq300[(6.516-6.492)+(6.468-6.445)]/2=7.05$ Hz. The larger value identifies <trans vinylic coupling>; the smaller identifies <cis vinylic coupling>. The $b$ resonance confirms the large splitting, approximately $14.25$ Hz, and has a small <geminal coupling> to $c$.

The small spacings are not perfectly reciprocal if treated as exact <First-order NMR multiplets>: the $b$ lines imply $|J_{bc}|\simeq2.55$ Hz, whereas the $c$ lines imply $|J_{bc}|\simeq2.10$ Hz and $J_{ac}\simeq6.60$ Hz. Thus report **$J_{ab}\simeq14.3$ Hz, $J_{ac}\simeq7$ Hz and $|J_{bc}|\simeq2$–$3$ Hz**, rather than asserting incompatible exact constants. Printed precision and departures from a simple first-order pattern limit this line-picking estimate; a full spin-system fit would be needed for refined values. Splittings alone do not establish the signs of these couplings.

The two <methylene groups> split one another into approximate <NMR triplets>, with $J_{de}\simeq300(3.727-3.708)=5.7$ Hz; the oxygen-bound triplet gives approximately the same value from its rounded line labels. The three vinylic <protons> plus two methylenes give the required seven-<proton> integral. The terminal vinylic <protons> are <diastereotopic>, because replacing one or the other creates different alkene geometries.

The <vinyl ether> has an important <resonance contributor>
$$
\mathrm{CH_2=CH-O-R\ \leftrightarrow\ {}^-CH_2-CH=O^+-R},\qquad R=\mathrm{CH_2CH_2Cl}.
$$
Donation from <oxygen> into the <conjugated system> increases <electron> density at the terminal unsaturated <carbon>, helping explain its unusually shielded $\delta_C=87.4$ ppm and its terminal <protons> near 4.1–4.2 ppm. The directly oxygen-bound unsaturated <carbon> is strongly deshielded at $151.1$ ppm; its <proton> at $6.480$ ppm also experiences the <oxygen> substituent and <alkene> magnetic anisotropy. These trends are consistent with the whole delocalized electronic structure; a <chemical shift> is not simply a meter for an atom's <partial atomic charge>.

For the saturated part, the stronger local deshielding by <oxygen> places $\mathrm{OCH_2}$ at $\delta_C=67.8$, $\delta_H\simeq3.948$, while $\mathrm{CH_2Cl}$ lies at $41.9$ and $3.708$ ppm. The two terminal vinylic <protons> have different geometrical relationships to the <oxygen> substituent and therefore different <chemical shifts> as well as different <vicinal couplings>.

The one-electron species are $\mathrm{C^{5+}}$ and $\mathrm{N^{6+}}$, so there is no <electron>–<electron> screening and the nuclear charges remain $Z=6$ and $7$.
$$
\boxed{E_{n=2}(\mathrm{C^{5+}})=-13.6\frac{6^2}{4}=-122.4\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad E_{n=2}(\mathrm{N^{6+}})=-13.6\frac{7^2}{4}=-166.6\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
Within the stated <hydrogen-like ion> model, $2s$ and all three $2p$ <atomic orbitals> of a given species have the same energy. The energy zero is a separated <electron> and the residual nucleus.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The configurations are $\mathrm{C}:1s^22s^22p^2$ and $\mathrm{N}:1s^22s^22p^3$. A simple complete-core-screening estimate subtracts the two $1s$ <electrons>, giving **$Z_{\rm eff}\approx4$ for <carbon> and $5$ for <nitrogen>**. This neglects screening by the other valence <electrons>, and is an explicitly rough estimate.

A more systematic estimate using <Slater's rules> includes $0.85$ for each $1s$ <electron> and $0.35$ for each other <electron> in the $2s,2p$ group:
$$
Z_{\rm eff}(\mathrm C)=6-[2(0.85)+3(0.35)]=3.25,\qquad Z_{\rm eff}(\mathrm N)=7-[2(0.85)+4(0.35)]=3.90.
$$
Both models predict the important trend: <nitrogen>'s valence shell experiences a greater <effective nuclear charge>. There is no unique numerical <effective nuclear charge> without specifying an orbital and a screening prescription; the one-electron formula in (a) does not by itself define neutral-atom orbital energies.<Atomic Shielding Constants by John C. Slater>[1930, printed pp. 58–59: screening prescription and grouping of ns and np electrons]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The $2s$ <atomic orbital> is spherically symmetric and has one <radial node>. A real $2p$ <atomic orbital> has two lobes of opposite <quantum phase>, one <atomic-orbital nodal plane> through the nucleus and no <radial node>. The three spatial orientations $2p_x,2p_y,2p_z$ remain degenerate in a spherically symmetric atom when external fields and fine structure are neglected.

In a many-electron atom, $2s$ penetrates the screened core more effectively than $2p$, experiences a stronger attraction and is lower in energy: **$\varepsilon_{2s}<\varepsilon_{2p}$**. The <hydrogen-like ion> degeneracy therefore does not survive screening. Both valence orbital types are generally more tightly bound and spatially contracted in <nitrogen> than in <carbon>, because of the larger <effective nuclear charge>. These are central-field orbital statements; the detailed atomic multiplet structure also involves <electron> repulsion and <Hund's rules>.

= Solution

Take the bond axis as $z$. The heavy-atom $2s$ and $2p_z$ <atomic orbitals> can mix with <hydrogen> $1s$ to make three sigma-symmetry <molecular orbitals>. In the qualitative valence ordering, these are a low predominantly heavy-atom $2s$ level, a bonding $\sigma$ level and a higher $\sigma^*$ level. The perpendicular $2p_x,2p_y$ orbitals form a degenerate approximately nonbonding $\pi$ pair because <hydrogen> has no valence orbitals of the matching sideways symmetry. The filled heavy-atom $1s$ core is omitted from the diagram.

Counting valence <electrons> gives
$$
\mathrm{CH}:\quad(\sigma_s)^2(\sigma_b)^2(\pi_x,\pi_y)^1,\qquad
\mathrm{NH}:\quad(\sigma_s)^2(\sigma_b)^2(\pi_x)^1(\pi_y)^1.
$$
Thus **both exhibit <paramagnetism>: CH has one unpaired <electron> and NH has two in its ground state.** For NH, <Hund's rules> favour separate parallel-spin occupation of the two degenerate $\pi$ orbitals. The corresponding observed ground terms are $X\,{}^2\Pi$ and $X\,{}^3\Sigma^-$; the total <bond order> is approximately one in this simple sigma-bond/nonbonding-pi description.<NIST Chemistry WebBook, SRD 69>[Methylidyne, CAS 3315-37-5, and Imidogen, CAS 13774-92-0: diatomic constants, ground-state rows]{ref}

The nitrogen-derived orbital energies lie lower than the corresponding carbon-derived ones on an approximate common energy scale, while the isolated <hydrogen> $1s$ reference is unchanged. The bonding orbitals consequently have different polarizations and mixing coefficients; drawing identical energies for C and N would miss the screening trend. The figure specifies ordering and occupancy, not measured gaps or a numerical prediction from (a).

The framework has a planar $\mathrm{H_2C}$ group attached to a straight C–N–N axis. Two useful octet <resonance contributors> are
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{H_2C=N^+=N^-\ \leftrightarrow\ H_2C^--N^+\equiv N}.}

An internally consistent elementary <orbital hybridisation> model is **$sp^2$ at <carbon> and $sp$ at both <nitrogen> atoms; the hydrogens use $1s$ orbitals**. The three <carbon> hybrids form two C–H <sigma bonds> and one C–N <sigma bond>, leaving one perpendicular <p orbital>. The central <nitrogen> uses opposite $sp$ hybrids for its two collinear sigma connections, leaving two perpendicular <p orbitals> for the two pi systems.

At terminal <nitrogen>, one $sp$ hybrid points toward the central <nitrogen> and the other houses an axial <lone pair>. One perpendicular <p orbital> forms the second, N–N-only pi system; the other participates in the three-atom C–N–N pi system. This description remains the same across both <resonance contributors>; counting lone-pair domains in just one drawing and assigning a different terminal hybridisation to each contributor would obscure the delocalization. <orbital hybridisation> is a useful choice of localized basis, not a uniquely measurable label: advanced treatments of <diazomethane> require a superposition of bonding patterns.<On the electronic structure of the diazomethane molecule by André G. H. Barbosa and João G. S. Monteiro>[Abstract: limitations of single bonding patterns and multiconfigurational description]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=g}

The four <sigma bonds> are the two C–H connections, C–N and N–N, containing eight electrons. A terminal-nitrogen axial <lone pair> contains two more. Of the remaining six electrons, two occupy an N–N <pi bond> lying in the molecular plane, while four occupy a perpendicular <three-centre four-electron pi bond> extending over C–N–N. There are therefore 16 <valence electrons> in total.

For orientation, a three-site model with equal on-site energy $\alpha$ and adjacent interaction $\beta<0$ gives perpendicular pi levels at $\alpha+\sqrt2\beta$, $\alpha$, and $\alpha-\sqrt2\beta$. The lower two are doubly occupied and the upper is empty. The middle level has a terminal-end combination with little central-atom amplitude; unequal C and N orbital energies change its coefficients and make it only approximately nonbonding.

The **<highest occupied molecular orbital> is the upper occupied perpendicular pi orbital**, with substantial <carbon> amplitude, broadly above the strongly bonding sigma framework and the lower occupied pi orbitals. It is not the terminal axial <nitrogen> <lone pair>. Real <diazomethane> has C–N bonding and N–N antibonding character in this frontier orbital, rather than being an exactly nonbonding equal-site model. Photoelectron and Rydberg-state experiments support this assignment.<Vibronic structure and ion core interactions in Rydberg states of diazomethane: an experimental and theoretical investigation by Igor Fedorov and others>[Abstract and discussion of ionization: removal from a C–N-bonding, N–N-antibonding pi orbital]{ref}

An intense band near $2088\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is consistent with the high-frequency coupled C–N–N stretching motion of a <diazo group>, with substantial N–N multiple-bond character. It lies above a typical isolated C=N double-bond stretch, and supports contributions with a strong N–N connection. It does not establish that the molecule is a literal localized C–N single bond plus an unperturbed N≡N triple bond: a <normal mode> moves the coupled framework, and the <resonance contributors> describe one electronic structure.

Protonation at the nucleophilic <carbon> gives **<methyldiazonium>, $\mathrm{CH_3-N^+\equiv N}$**. The carbon-rich <highest occupied molecular orbital> and the carbon-negative <resonance contributor> rationalize this site. Subsequent displacement of <molecular nitrogen> can be strongly favourable; the initial protonation product still contains both nitrogens.

First, <proton transfer> from <acetic acid> to the <carbon> of <diazomethane> gives <methyldiazonium> and an <acetate ion>:
$$
\mathrm{CH_2N_2+CH_3CO_2H\longrightarrow[CH_3N_2]^++CH_3CO_2^-}.

Second, an acetate <oxygen> attacks the diazonium methyl <carbon> by an <SN2 reaction>; an arrow from the C–N bond to <nitrogen> expels neutral $\mathrm{N_2}$:

\boxed{\mathrm{CH_3CO_2^-+[CH_3-N^+\equiv N]\longrightarrow CH_3CO_2CH_3+N_2}.}
$$
The acid's hydroxyl <hydrogen> has become the third <hydrogen> of the newly installed <methyl group>. This is a methyl-transfer sequence driven by formation of <molecular nitrogen>, without forming a free methyl <carbocation> or invoking <Fischer esterification>.

For the ordinary <ester>, the <carbon> nucleophile of a <grignard reagent> attacks the <carbonyl> <carbon> and moves the C=O pi pair onto <oxygen>. Collapse of the <tetrahedral intermediate> expels <ethoxide>, transiently giving $\mathrm{R^1COR}$. This <ketone> is more electrophilic than the starting <ester> because it lacks an alkoxy group's stabilizing <resonance donation>. A second equivalent of the <grignard reagent> therefore adds to it before isolation:
$$
\mathrm{R^1CO_2Et\ \longrightarrow\ R^1C(O^-)(OEt)R\ \longrightarrow\ R^1COR\ \longrightarrow\ R^1C(O^-)(R)_2\ \xrightarrow{H^+}\ R^1C(OH)(R)_2}.
$$
The negatively charged intermediates are associated with <magnesium> counterions. Merely reducing the amount of reagent to one equivalent does not generally trap the <ketone> selectively; it can leave unreacted <ester> together with the double-addition product.

In the first successful preparation, <magnesium> insertion into the primary C–Br bond gives **n-butylmagnesium <bromide>**. The <carbon> chain has four carbons, so its acetylation produces **hexan-2-one, $\mathrm{CH_3CO(CH_2)_3CH_3}$**, not pentan-2-one. Attack on <acetic anhydride> forms $\mathrm{CH_3C(O^-)(nBu)OCOCH_3}$. Coordination of the anhydride-derived oxygens to <magnesium> can stabilize this tetrahedral addition complex. Under suitably cold, controlled-addition conditions it survives sufficiently to delay release of the free <ketone> until aqueous work-up has destroyed the remaining <grignard reagent>. Protonation followed by collapse releases acetate/<acetic acid> and the <ketone>.

This interpretation has direct experimental precedent: <Melvin S. Newman> and <William T. Booth Jr.> obtained methyl ketones by cold addition of <grignard reagents> to <acetic anhydride>, attributing selectivity to a persistent addition complex. The <temperature> and reagent-addition regime are genuine conditions on the explanation, rather than proof that an anhydride can never undergo overaddition; the exam scheme omits them.<The Preparation of Ketones from Grignard Reagents by Melvin S. Newman and William T. Booth Jr.>[1945, printed p. 154: proposed intermediate I and low-temperature dependence]{ref}

For the <benzonitrile> route, n-butyl attacks the <nitrile> <carbon> and one C≡N pi pair moves to <nitrogen>, producing the <magnesium> <ketimine> salt $\mathrm{PhC(nBu)=N^-MgBr^+}$. This anionic nitrogen-containing intermediate resists a second <nucleophilic addition> much more than a neutral <carbonyl> does. The **<ketone> $\mathrm{PhC(=O)nBu}$ appears only during acidic aqueous work-up**, after organomagnesium has been quenched. Protonation gives the <iminium ion>; water attacks its <carbon>, <proton> transfers give a <carbinolamine>, and C–N cleavage from its nitrogen-protonated form produces the protonated <ketone> and <ammonia>, followed by deprotonation. In the acidic mixture <ammonia> is present largely as <ammonium>. This sequence explains why <nitrile> addition stops at a <ketone> without requiring the <ketone> itself to be resistant to <grignard reagents>.

The atom-labelled hydrolysis sequence is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{PhC(nBu)=N^-}&\xrightarrow{2H^+}\mathrm{PhC(nBu)=NH_2^+}\\
&\xrightarrow{H_2O}\mathrm{PhC(nBu)(OH_2^+)NH_2}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{proton transfers}}\mathrm{PhC(nBu)(OH)NH_3^+}\\
&\longrightarrow\mathrm{PhC(nBu)=OH^++NH_3}\longrightarrow\mathrm{PhC(nBu)=O}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Water's <lone pair> attacks the iminium carbon while its C=N pi pair moves to <nitrogen>; the <carbinolamine> oxygen then reforms C=O while the C–N pair moves to the departing protonated amino group. Final deprotonation gives the neutral <ketone>. The arrows involving protonation and hydrolysis also exchange protons with the acidic aqueous reservoir; the condensed sequence tracks the carbon–nitrogen and carbon–oxygen transformations rather than displaying every solvent molecule.

The two starting <ester> groups are adjacent on the <benzene> ring. Select one <ester> <carbon> as the site receiving both incoming <methyl groups>. A first methyl addition gives a <tetrahedral intermediate>; its collapse expels <methoxide> to give the ortho-substituted <ketone> $\mathrm{o\!-!MeO_2C-C_6H_4-C(=O)Me}$. A second methyl addition produces $\mathrm{o\!-!MeO_2C-C_6H_4-C(O^-)Me_2}$, with the <alkoxide> bound to <magnesium>.

The newly generated <oxygen> is now positioned to attack the remaining <ester> intramolecularly. Draw an arrow from its <lone pair> to that <ester> <carbonyl> and another from the C=O <pi bond> to its <oxygen>. Collapse of the resulting <tetrahedral intermediate> reforms the <carbonyl> and expels <methoxide>, closing a five-membered <lactone>. Protonation of magnesium-bound species in the work-up gives **<3,3-dimethylphthalide>**. Equivalently, protonation can first give the ortho hydroxy <ester>, followed by acid-assisted <lactonization> with loss of methanol; both descriptions show the same required bond formation.

The <oxygen> in the new ring is the <oxygen> of the first <ester>'s original <carbonyl>; the surviving <lactone> <carbonyl> comes from the second <ester>. The product retains the six ring carbons and both original acyl carbons, loses the two <ester> methoxy carbons, and gains two carbons from <methylmagnesium bromide>. The favourable intramolecular five-membered closure accounts for the displayed product. It does not make the isolated <lactone> universally inert to a large excess of <grignard reagent>; stoichiometry and trapping/work-up conditions matter.

The <oxygen> substituent changes position through a <halohydrin>–<epoxide> sequence. First, <hydroxide> attacks the acetate <carbonyl> of $\mathrm{BrCH_2C(Me)_2OAc}$, giving a <tetrahedral intermediate>. Collapse expels the substrate <alkoxide>, and <proton transfer> gives $\mathrm{BrCH_2C(Me)_2OH}$ plus acetate. This is <saponification>, breaking the acyl–<oxygen> bond rather than replacing <bromide> directly with <hydroxide>.

A small equilibrium population of $\mathrm{BrCH_2C(Me)_2O^-}$ undergoes intramolecular <SN2 reaction> at the adjacent primary <carbon>: the O <lone pair> attacks $\mathrm{CH_2Br}$ and its C–Br pair leaves with <bromide>. The result is **<2,2-dimethyloxirane>**. Addition of HBr protonates the ring <oxygen>. <bromide> then attacks preferentially at the more substituted <carbon>, where the protonated ring has greater <carbocation> character, breaking that C–O bond and leaving <oxygen> attached to the former primary <carbon>:
$$
\mathrm{BrCH_2C(Me)_2OH\ \longrightarrow\ (CH_2)O(CMe_2)\ \xrightarrow{HBr}\ HOCH_2C(Br)(Me)_2}.
$$

The <Clausius statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is transfer of <heat> from a colder <thermal reservoir> to a hotter one. The <Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is complete conversion of <heat> drawn from a single <thermal reservoir> into <work>. Combining $\Delta_rG^\circ=\Delta_rH^\circ-T\Delta_rS^\circ$ with the equilibrium relation gives
$$
\boxed{\ln K=-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ}{R}\frac1T+\frac{\Delta_rS^\circ}{R}.}
$$
If <reaction enthalpy> and <reaction entropy> are approximately constant over the measurement interval, a plot of $y=\ln K$ against $x=1/T$ is straight, with **slope $-\Delta_rH^\circ/R$ and intercept $\Delta_rS^\circ/R$**. An exothermic reaction therefore has a positive slope. In the more general local form, the <Gibbs–Helmholtz equation> gives
$$
\frac{\mathrm d\ln K}{\mathrm d(1/T)}=-\frac{\Delta_rH^\circ(T)}R,
$$
which is the <Van't Hoff equation> without assuming a constant <enthalpy>.

Use a dimensionless <equilibrium constant> built from <thermodynamic activities>. Measure sufficiently separated temperatures after verifying true equilibrium, and avoid phase changes or a changing chemical reaction. Temperature-dependent <heat capacities> cause curvature: the fitted straight-line slope then estimates an interval-dependent <enthalpy>. Small errors in the equilibrium measurements can produce large slope errors over a narrow $1/T$ range. The <entropy> intercept is particularly sensitive because $1/T=0$ requires distant extrapolation, and slope/intercept errors are correlated. A numerical line through a few points is not, by itself, evidence that all these assumptions hold.

In molecular <boron trichloride>, <boron> uses three approximately $sp^2$ hybrids to form three equivalent B–Cl <sigma bonds> in a trigonal plane. Its perpendicular empty $2p$ orbital can accept <electron> density from the compatible combination of filled <chlorine> $3p$ <lone pairs>. The resulting <pi donation> is delocalized over the three B–Cl connections.

In the elementary model, one net <pi bond> is distributed over three equivalent <sigma bonds>, giving **formal average B–Cl <bond order> $1+1/3=4/3$**. The corresponding <resonance contributors> each put a B=Cl connection in a different position, with formal negative <boron> and positive donating <chlorine>. They do not mean one experimentally distinct double bond and two single bonds. The $4/3$ value is the idealized sigma-plus-delocalized-pi bookkeeping value; a computed bond index need not equal that exact rational number.

The dimer has four terminal chlorides and two <bridging chloride ligands>. Each <aluminium> is approximately tetrahedrally surrounded by two terminal and two bridging chlorines. A <chlorine> <lone pair> from one $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$ fragment donates into an empty orbital of the other <Lewis acid> centre; reciprocal donation closes the four-membered $\mathrm{Al_2Cl_2}$ ring.

The localized <coordinate covalent bond> model has **single Al–Cl bonds, formal <bond order> one, for both terminal and bridging connections**. Donation describes the origin of a pair, not half an electron-pair bond. A bridge <chlorine> has two bonded pairs and two remaining <lone pairs> in this all-single-bond Lewis accounting; the original terminal chlorines have three <lone pairs>. Bridging contacts are longer and weaker than terminal ones, so equal formal order does not imply equal strength or equal numerical quantum-chemical bond indices. In particular, these <chloride> bridges should not be assigned the electron-deficient methyl-bridge half-bonds of (b).

The simplest assignment is **two phenylethynyl bridges and four equivalent terminal <methyl groups>**, represented as $\mathrm{Al_2Me_4(\mu-C\equiv CPh)_2}$.
