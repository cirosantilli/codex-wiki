= Abelian variety
{wiki}

An abelian variety is a complete connected group variety. Despite its name, commutativity follows from completeness and the rigidity lemma.

= Prime-to-characteristic torsion of an abelian variety
{parent=Abelian variety}
{title2=$A[N]\cong(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^{2\dim A}$}

If $N$ is invertible in the base <field>, multiplication by $N$ on an <abelian variety> is finite étale: its differential is $N$ times the identity, and properness turns its zero-dimensional fibres into finite fibres. The <Theorem of the Cube> gives $[N]^*L\cong L^{N^2}$ for a symmetric <ample line bundle>. Top <intersection products> give degree $N^{2\dim A}$. For each prime power $\ell^r\mid N$, the kernels killed by $\ell^s$ have orders $\ell^{2s\dim A}$; the classification of <finite abelian groups> forces $2\dim A$ cyclic factors of length $r$. Combining the prime powers gives the displayed group.

= Jacobian variety
{c}
{parent=Abelian variety}
{title2=$J(C)=\operatorname{Pic}^0(C)$}
{wiki}

The <Jacobian variety> of a smooth projective <algebraic curve> parametrizes degree-zero <line bundles>. Its dimension is the genus of the <algebraic curve>. An effective degree-$d$ <Cartier divisor> defines a point in a degree-$d$ torsor through the <Abel map of an algebraic curve>. The <theta divisor> induces an isomorphism with the <dual abelian variety>.

= Torelli theorem
{c}
{parent=Jacobian variety}
{wiki}

A <smooth projective curve> over the <complex numbers> is determined up to isomorphism by its <Jacobian variety> together with its canonical principal <polarization of a complex torus>. For genus at least three, the <branch locus of the theta Gauss map> recovers the canonical curve by <projective biduality theorem>, or recovers the canonical <rational normal curve> and the branch points of a <hyperelliptic curve>. In genus two the <theta divisor> itself is the curve; in genus one the <Jacobian variety> is the curve after choosing an origin. The polarization is essential data in this statement.

= Universal property of the Jacobian variety
{parent=Jacobian variety}

For a smooth connected projective curve $C$ with chosen basepoint $p$, the <Abel map of an algebraic curve> $i_p:C\to J(C)$ sends $q$ to $[q-p]$. Every morphism $g:C\to A$ to an <abelian variety> with $g(p)=0$ factors uniquely through a homomorphism $J(C)\to A$. This universal property converts a curve embedded in an abelian variety into a homomorphism from its Jacobian.

= Theta divisor
{parent=Jacobian variety}
{title2=$\Theta\subset\operatorname{Pic}^{g-1}(C)$}
{wiki}

The <theta divisor> consists of degree-$g-1$ <line bundles> with a nonzero <global section>. It is the irreducible image of the <symmetric product of a curve> $C^{(g-1)}$, with its reduced <Cartier divisor> structure. The <determinant description of the theta divisor> supplies equations, multiplicities and pullbacks in families.

= Riemann vanishing theorem
{c}
{parent=Theta divisor}
{title2=$\{z:\theta(z,B)=0\}=W_{g-1}-\kappa$}

For the period matrix of a genus-$g$ <compact Riemann surface>, there is a vector $\kappa$ depending on the marking and base point such that the <Riemann theta function> vanishes exactly on this translate of the image of the <Abelian sum map>. Pairing sides of the cut surface proves that a nonzero pullback $\theta(u(p)-z)$ has a degree-$g$ zero <divisor> whose Abel sum is $z+\kappa$. At a theta zero that pullback includes the base point. Irreducibility and dimension of the effective-divisor locus give equality of supports; the primitive <first Chern class> of the theta <line bundle> makes the <divisor> reduced.

= Theta pullback zero-divisor identity
{parent=Riemann vanishing theorem}
{title2=$\deg D_z=g,\qquad\operatorname{AJ}(D_z)=z+\kappa$}

If $p\mapsto\theta(u(p)-z)$ is not identically zero, its zero <divisor> $D_z$ has these properties. The logarithmic derivative changes by $-2\pi i\omega_j$ across the paired $b_j$ translation, giving zero count $g$. For a variation of $z$, the logarithm changes there by $2\pi i\delta z_j$; integrating by parts in the first-moment contour integral gives $\delta\operatorname{AJ}(D_z)=\delta z$. Thus the difference is constant. Weighted zero <divisors>, rather than a distinct-zero assumption, make the statement valid at collisions.

= Vector of Riemann constants
{parent=Riemann vanishing theorem}
{title2=$\kappa=\operatorname{AJ}(D_z)-z$}

= Riemann constants
{c}
{synonym}

The vector of <Riemann constants> is the parameter-independent class obtained from the zeros of $p\mapsto\theta(u(p)-z)$ using the displayed formula. Under this sign convention the <theta divisor> is $W_{g-1}-\kappa$. It depends on the base point and normalized symplectic marking; some accounts use its negative. Changing the marking or sign convention must also change the translation formula consistently.

= Gauss map of a theta divisor
{c}
{parent=Theta divisor}
{title2=$\Gamma:\Theta_{\mathrm{sm}}\to\mathbb P(T_0J^*)$}

Translate the tangent hyperplane at a smooth point of the <theta divisor> to the identity of the <Jacobian variety>. Its annihilator defines the Gauss map. At $L=\mathcal O_X(D)$ with $h^0(L)=1$, the <derivative of the Abelian sum map> identifies this annihilator with the unique line $H^0(K_X(-D))$. Thus $\Gamma(L)=[\omega]$ implies $D\le\operatorname{div}(\omega)$. This connects the intrinsic geometry of the <theta divisor> to the <canonical map> of the curve.

= Branch locus of the theta Gauss map
{parent=Gauss map of a theta divisor}
{title2=$B\subset\mathbb P(T_0J^*)$}

Normalize the target in the <function field> of the generically finite <Gauss map of a theta divisor>; its finite map has an intrinsic branch locus. For a nonhyperelliptic <smooth projective curve>, this locus is the <projective dual variety> of the canonical curve. For a <hyperelliptic curve> of genus at least three, it is the union of the dual hypersurface of its canonical <rational normal curve> and the $2g+2$ hyperplanes corresponding to branch points. Locally, ramification exchanges two roots of a hyperplane section, or exchanges the two lifts of a root passing through a branch point. Biduality then recovers the curve or its double-cover data.

= Riemann singularity theorem
{c}
{parent=Theta divisor}
{title2=$\operatorname{mult}_L\Theta=h^0(X,L)$}

For a <compact Riemann surface> of genus $g\ge1$, the <theta divisor> in $\operatorname{Pic}^{g-1}(X)$ has the displayed multiplicity. A local <determinant description of the theta divisor> reduces to an $r$ by $r$ <matrix> vanishing at $L$, where $r=h^0(L)$. Its linear term is cup product with a tangent class in $H^1(\mathcal O_X)$. The <invertible cup-product direction for a line bundle on a curve> shows that its <determinant> has order exactly $r$. Consequently the <singular locus> consists precisely of classes with at least two independent <global sections>.

= Schur complement presentation of a theta singularity
{c}
{parent=Riemann singularity theorem}
{title2=$\det A=\text{unit}\cdot\det M,\quad M(0)=0$}

For a square evaluation matrix representing a degree-$g-1$ <line bundle> $L$, remove an invertible minor of size $N-h^0(L)$. <Holomorphic> row and column operations leave a square matrix $M$ of size $r=h^0(L)$ with zero constant term. Its linear part in a tangent direction $\xi\in H^1(\mathcal O_X)$ is $\xi\smile-:H^0(L)\to H^1(L)$. The <invertible cup-product direction for a line bundle on a curve> makes the leading degree-$r$ <determinant> nonzero, establishing order exactly $r$ rather than just a lower bound.

= Tangent cone to a theta divisor
{parent=Riemann singularity theorem}
{title2=$\det(\xi\smile-)=0$}

The <tangent cone to a theta divisor> at a degree-$g-1$ <line bundle> $L$ is cut out in $H^1(\mathcal O_X)$ by the <determinant> of $\xi\smile-:H^0(L)\to H^1(L)$. Under <Serre duality> its <matrix> entries are $\langle\xi,s_i t_j\rangle$ for bases of $H^0(L)$ and $H^0(K_X\otimes L^{-1})$. For $h^0(L)=1$, its tangent hyperplane is annihilated by the nonzero <holomorphic differential form> $s_1t_1$.

= Theta autoduality of a Jacobian
{parent=Theta divisor}
{title2=$\lambda_\Theta:J(C)\xrightarrow{\sim}\widehat{J(C)}$}

For $T_q(x)=x+q$, the <homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety> gives $\lambda_\Theta(q)=T_q^*\mathcal O(\Theta)\otimes\mathcal O(\Theta)^{-1}$. Restrict to the negative <Abel map of an algebraic curve>. The <restriction of the theta divisor to an Abel curve> identifies this restriction with the degree-zero class $q$. Pullback of algebraically trivial <line bundles> does not change when the embedding is translated. Thus pullback $\rho:\widehat{J(C)}\to J(C)$ satisfies $\rho\lambda_\Theta=1$. Equal dimensions and properness make $\lambda_\Theta$ an isomorphism, including on schemes, rather than only on closed points.

= Restriction of the theta divisor to an Abel curve
{parent=Theta divisor}
{title2=$\phi_D^*\Theta=D_0$}

For a degree-$g$ <divisor class> $D$ with $h^0(\mathcal O_C(D))=1$, let $D_0$ be its unique effective representative. Under $\phi_D(P)=[D-P]$, evaluation of the unique <global section> gives $\phi_D^*\Theta=D_0$, including multiplicities. If $h^0(\mathcal O_C(D))\ge2$, every evaluation kernel is nonzero and the entire curve lies in the <theta divisor>. These restrictions also show that $h^0(\mathcal O(\Theta))=1$: after pulling back to a dominant product of curves, the ratio of any section to the defining section of the <theta divisor> is constant on each general coordinate curve, hence constant everywhere.

= Determinant description of the theta divisor
{parent=Theta divisor}

Choose an effective <Cartier divisor> $E$ of degree at least $g$ on a smooth projective genus-$g$ <algebraic curve>. For a family $M$ of degree-$g-1$ <line bundles>, the evaluation map from $\pi_*M(E)$ to $\pi_*(M(E)|_E)$ is between <vector bundles> of the same rank. Its <determinant> vanishes exactly when $H^0(C,M)$ is nonzero. Changing $E$ changes the presentation by cancelling an invertible determinant factor; the zero <Cartier divisor> is the <theta divisor>. At a point with $h^0(M)=h^1(M)=1$, the derivative is the cup-product map $H^1(\mathcal O_C)\to\operatorname{Hom}(H^0(M),H^1(M))$. By <Serre duality> it is nonzero, since the product of nonzero sections of $M$ and $K_C\otimes M^{-1}$ is nonzero. Hence this <Cartier divisor> is generically reduced.

= Invertible cup-product direction for a line bundle on a curve
{parent=Determinant description of the theta divisor}
{title2=$\xi\smile-:H^0(L)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^1(L)$}

Suppose a <line bundle> $L$ of degree $g-1$ on a <smooth projective curve> has $h^0(L)=h^1(L)=r$. Choose $r$ distinct points where evaluation is invertible for both $H^0(L)$ and $H^0(K_X\otimes L^{-1})$. Such choices exist simultaneously: each <determinant> defines a nonempty open subset of $X^r$, since independent sections cannot vanish at every point. Let $\xi\in H^1(\mathcal O_X)=H^0(K_X)^*$ be a sum of nonzero weighted evaluation functionals at these points. The <Serre duality> <matrix> of $\xi\smile-$ factors as the product of the two invertible evaluation <matrices> and an invertible diagonal <matrix>. Its <determinant> is nonzero. This gives the nonzero leading <determinant> needed for the <Riemann singularity theorem>.

= Evaluation determinant on a product of curves
{parent=Determinant description of the theta divisor}
{title2=$\det\pi_*(L|_{\sum\Gamma_i})$}

On $C\times C^n$, let $\Gamma_i$ be the graph of the $i$th coordinate and $D=\sum_i\Gamma_i$. Successive restriction exact sequences give
$$
\det\pi_*(L|_D)\cong\bigotimes_i\operatorname{pr}_i^*L\otimes\mathcal O\left(-\sum_{i<j}\Delta_{ij}\right).
$$
When $h^0(L)=n$ and $h^1(L)=0$, the <determinant> of evaluation vanishes at tuples where $H^0(L(-D))$ is nonzero. On distinct points it is the ordinary evaluation <determinant>; the diagonal factors remove its forced zero when two points coincide. This explains why the formula remains valid at repeated points, using jets instead of repeated evaluations.

= Torsion trivialization in a determinant quotient
{parent=Evaluation determinant on a product of curves}

Evaluation <determinants> for $L\otimes P$ and $L$ have a quotient which is a rational section of $\bigotimes_i\operatorname{pr}_i^*P$, not intrinsically a scalar. If $P^N\cong\mathcal O_C$, its $N$th power becomes a <rational function> after choosing that trivialization. In <Cartier divisor> notation, write $P=\mathcal O_C(p)$ and $\operatorname{div}(h)=Np$. With raw meromorphic evaluations the scalar expression is $r^N\prod_i h(z_i)$. The extra product accounts for the line-bundle frames; it is suppressed when evaluation already uses compatible torsion trivializations.

= Abelian sum map
{synonym}

The <Abel map of an algebraic curve> sends an effective <Cartier divisor> $D$ to its <line bundle> $\mathcal O_C(D)$. Its scheme fibre over a <line bundle> $L$ is the <projective space> of one-dimensional subspaces of $H^0(C,L)$. This follows in families: a relative effective <Cartier divisor> in the fixed class determines a nowhere fibre-zero section of $L$ up to a <line bundle> from the parameter space, equivalently a line subbundle of $H^0(C,L)\otimes\mathcal O_T$. Thus the scheme fibre is smooth, not merely reduced on its points. The dimensions can nevertheless jump, so the <Abel map of an algebraic curve> need not be a smooth morphism.

= Derivative of the Abelian sum map
{parent=Abel map of an algebraic curve}
{title2=$d u_D:H^0(\mathcal O_D(D))\to H^1(\mathcal O_X)$}

For an effective <divisor on an algebraic curve> $D$ on a <smooth projective curve>, the <Zariski tangent space> to its <symmetric product of a curve> is $H^0(\mathcal O_D(D))$. The derivative of the <Abelian sum map> is the <connecting homomorphism> of $0\to\mathcal O_X\to\mathcal O_X(D)\to\mathcal O_D(D)\to0$. Its dual under <Serre duality> is restriction of <holomorphic differential forms> to $D$, including all jets at repeated points. Hence its rank is $\deg D+1-h^0(\mathcal O_X(D))$, and its image annihilates $H^0(K_X(-D))$.

= Repeated-point Abel differential formula
{parent=Derivative of the Abelian sum map}
{title2=$d u_D(v)(\omega)=\sum_p\operatorname{res}_p(v_p\omega)$}

At a multiplicity-$r$ point, choose a local parameter and elementary symmetric coordinates $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ for the nearby roots. The tangent <principal part> is $v=\sum_{k=1}^r(-1)^{k-1}\delta e_k z^{-k}$. <Newton's identities> give $d(\sum z_i^k)=(-1)^{k-1}k\,d e_k$. Expanding the primitive of a <holomorphic one-form> proves the <residue> formula, including its sign. The dual map is restriction to the length-$r$ scheme, so it sees all jets through order $r-1$.

= Curve generation criterion for an abelian variety
{parent=Abelian variety}

A smooth projective curve $C\subset A$ generates $A$ by its point differences if and only if it meets every prime <Weil divisor>. If a prime <Weil divisor> avoids $C$, its restriction to $C$ has degree zero; <constancy of line bundle degree in a family> shows that every translate either contains $C$ or misses it. This forces invariance under $C-C$ and puts the generated subgroup inside the <line bundle translation stabilizer>. If this subgroup were all of $A$, the divisor would be algebraically trivial, contradicting its positive intersection with an <ample line bundle>. Conversely a proper closed generated subgroup admits a disjoint divisor by the <pole divisor avoiding a fiber> construction, after translating the fiber to contain $C$.

= Dual abelian variety
{parent=Abelian variety}
{title2=$\widehat A=\operatorname{Pic}^0(A)$}
{wiki}

The dual of an <abelian variety> represents algebraically trivial <line bundles> rigidified at the origin. Its group law is <tensor product>. A <group homomorphism> $f:A\to B$ induces $\widehat f:\widehat B\to\widehat A$ by pullback of these <line bundles>.

= Weil pairing for an abelian variety
{c}
{parent=Dual abelian variety}
{title2=$e_N:A[N]\times\widehat A[N]\to\mu_N$}

For an <abelian variety> and an integer $N$ invertible in the base <field>, a torsion <line bundle> $M\in\widehat A[N]$ has trivial pullback by $[N]$. Choose a trivialization. The descent action of the kernel $A[N]$ on that trivial <line bundle> acts through a character $\chi_M:A[N]\to\mu_N$. Define $e_N(P,M)=\chi_M(P)$. This is independent of rescaling the trivialization and bilinear under translation and <tensor product>. A trivial character descends the trivial bundle, so $M$ is trivial. Since both groups have order $N^{2\dim A}$, the character map is an isomorphism and the <Weil pairing for an abelian variety> is perfect. The <theta autoduality of a Jacobian> turns it into a pairing on two copies of the <Jacobian variety>'s torsion group.

= Poincaré line bundle
{c}
{parent=Dual abelian variety}
{title2=$P_A\text{ on }A\times\widehat A$}

The normalized universal <line bundle> restricts to the class $\xi$ on $A\times\{\xi\}$ and is trivialized on both zero sections. For a <line bundle> $L$ on an <abelian variety>, its <homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety> satisfies
$$
(\operatorname{id},\phi_L)^*P_A\cong m^*L\otimes p_1^*L^{-1}\otimes p_2^*L^{-1}
$$
as <Picard group> classes. A choice of trivialization of $L_0$ normalizes the right side; uniqueness then follows from the <Seesaw theorem>.

= Mumford rigidity lemma
{c}
{parent=Abelian variety}

One form of Mumford's rigidity lemma says that if $X$ is complete, $Y$ is connected, and a morphism $f:X\times Y\to Z$ maps one fiber $X\times\{y_0\}$ to a point, then $f$ factors through the projection to $Y$ under the usual pointed hypotheses.

= Invariant differential on a group scheme
{parent=Abelian variety}

Translation identifies the cotangent sheaf of a group scheme with the constant bundle determined by its cotangent space at the identity:
$$
\Omega_{G/k}\cong\mathcal O_G\otimes_k\Omega_{G/k}(e).
$$

= Theorem of the Cube
{c}
{parent=Abelian variety}
{wiki}

The Theorem of the Cube says that the third finite difference of a line bundle on an abelian variety is trivial. On $X^3$, it is the alternating tensor product of the pullbacks along the seven nonempty partial-sum maps.

= Arithmetic cube identity for Weil heights
{parent=Theorem of the Cube}
{title2=$h(P+Q+R)-h(P+Q)-h(P+R)-h(Q+R)+h(P)+h(Q)+h(R)-h(O)=O(1)$}

For a <line bundle> on an <elliptic curve> over a <number field>, the geometric cube identity remains exact as a line-bundle identity. The <Weil height machine> translates it into the displayed bounded-error identity for a chosen logarithmic height, uniformly on the rational triples. This distinction is essential: arbitrary naive heights do not obey an exact quadratic identity. Dividing the identity for $2^nP,2^nQ,2^nR$ by $4^n$ produces an exact identity for the <canonical height of an elliptic curve>.

= Cohomological proof of the cube theorem for elliptic curves
{parent=Theorem of the Cube}

If a <line bundle> on $E^3$ is trivial on each zero-coordinate face, its <first Chern class> vanishes: the integral second cohomology consists of single-factor second cohomology and cross-products of first cohomology from pairs, and each summand is detected on a coordinate face. The <holomorphic exponential sequence> then puts the bundle in $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E^3)$. The decomposition of first cohomology, including <Dolbeault cohomology>, identifies this group with $\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)^3$. Triviality on the three coordinate axes makes all three factors trivial. Applying this cube principle to the alternating partial-sum pullbacks proves the usual addition-map <Theorem of the Cube>.

= Bilinear cross-effect of a line bundle on an abelian variety
{parent=Theorem of the Cube}
{title2=$D_L(f,g)=(f+g)^*L\otimes(f^*L)^{-1}\otimes(g^*L)^{-1}$}

For <group homomorphisms> $f,g:A\to B$, this <Picard group> class is symmetric and additive in both arguments. The <Theorem of the Cube> says precisely that the third additive difference of $h\mapsto[h^*L]$ vanishes, proving additivity of the cross-effect. The <Poincaré line bundle> expresses it as $(f,\phi_Lg)^*P_B$, so it depends only on the <Néron-Severi group> class of $L$. Its associated homomorphism is $\widehat f\phi_Lg+\widehat g\phi_Lf$.

= Theorem of the square
{c}
{parent=Theorem of the Cube}
{wiki}

For a line bundle $L$ on an abelian variety and points $x,y$,
$$
T_{x+y}^*L\otimes L\cong T_x^*L\otimes T_y^*L.
$$

= Homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety
{title2=$\phi_L$}
{parent=Theorem of the square}

For a line bundle $L$ on an abelian variety $X$,
$$
\phi_L:X(k)\to\operatorname{Pic}X,
\qquad x\mapsto T_x^*L\otimes L^{-1},
$$
is a homomorphism by the <Theorem of the square>.

= Line bundle translation stabilizer
{parent=Homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety}
{title2=$K(L)=\ker\phi_L$}

For a <line bundle> $L$ on an <abelian variety> $A$, this closed subgroup consists of $a$ such that $T_a^*L\cong L$. It is a stabilizer of a <Picard group> class, which need not be the same as the stabilizer of an individual <Weil divisor> representing that class.

= Identity component of the Picard group
{title2=$\operatorname{Pic}^0X$}
{c}
{parent=Homomorphism associated to a line bundle on an abelian variety}

For an abelian variety,
$$
\operatorname{Pic}^0X=\{L\in\operatorname{Pic}X:\phi_L=0\}
$$
is the subgroup of translation-invariant line bundles.

= Seesaw theorem
{c}
{parent=Identity component of the Picard group}

The seesaw theorem determines a line bundle on a product from its restrictions to fibers: if it is trivial on all fibers over one factor and on one transverse section, then it is trivial.
