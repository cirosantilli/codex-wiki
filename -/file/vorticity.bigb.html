= Vorticity
{wiki}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$}

Use <vorticity> to distinguish the local spin of a <fluid element> from the motion of its center along a <pathline>. Circular <streamlines> can occur both with and without <vorticity>: the two <velocity fields> below even have the same <circulation> around the unit <circle>.

\Image[vorticity-and-circulation-fields.png]{title=Solid-body rotation and a potential line vortex: equal unit-circle circulation, different local vorticity}

For a continuously differentiable <velocity field> $\mathbf u=(u_x,u_y,u_z)$, <vorticity> is the <curl> vector
$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u=\left(\frac{\partial u_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial u_y}{\partial z},\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial u_z}{\partial x},\frac{\partial u_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}\right).
$$
Each <partial derivative> divides <velocity> by distance, so <vorticity> has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. In a planar <velocity field> independent of $z$, only $\omega_z=\partial_xu_y-\partial_yu_x$ survives. Positive $\omega_z$ points along $+\mathbf e_z$: the <right-hand rule> associates it with counterclockwise spin viewed from positive $z$ toward the plane. https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/fluid-mechanics/fluids1.pdf[David Tong, Fluid Mechanics, section 2.2]{ref}

The local rotational <angular velocity> is half the <vorticity>, $\boldsymbol\Omega_{\rm spin}=\boldsymbol\omega/2$. To see the factor, split the <velocity gradient> $L_{ij}=\partial_ju_i$ into its symmetric <rate-of-strain tensor> $E=(L+L^T)/2$ and antisymmetric <spin tensor> $W=(L-L^T)/2$. A nearby displacement $\delta\mathbf x$ has relative <velocity> $L\delta\mathbf x$; its rotational part satisfies $W\delta\mathbf x=(\boldsymbol\omega/2)\times\delta\mathbf x$. This is a proof outline of the local spin interpretation. The <rate-of-strain tensor> can also change the angle of a particular <material line>, so that line's <angular velocity> need not equal $\boldsymbol\omega/2$.

For <solid-body rotation>, $\mathbf u=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)$ gives $\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,2\Omega)$ and $\boldsymbol\Omega_{\rm spin}=(0,0,\Omega)$. Taking $\Omega=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the <vorticity> is $2\mathbf e_z\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, although the <angular velocity> is only $1\mathbf e_z\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. For the potential <line vortex>, $\mathbf u=K\mathbf e_\theta/r$, the planar <curl> is $r^{-1}\partial_r(ru_\theta)=r^{-1}\partial_rK=0$ wherever $r>0$. Its circular <pathlines> therefore coexist with zero local spin.

This distinction makes <vorticity> useful for recognizing <shear flow> as well as swirling motion; taking the <curl> of the <Euler equations> also removes a <pressure gradient> and leads to the <vorticity equation>. A historical landmark is Hermann von Helmholtz's 1858 paper on <fluid vortex> motion, an early study devoted to <vortex dynamics>. https://zenodo.org/records/1448878[H. Helmholtz, Über Integrale der hydrodynamischen Gleichungen, welche den Wirbelbewegungen entsprechen (1858)]{ref}

= Vorticity of a linear shear flow
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$\omega_z=-\gamma,\quad\Omega_{{\rm spin},z}=-\gamma/2$}

Use a <linear shear flow> to see why straight <pathlines> can still carry local spin. Faster layers pull past slower ones, and a small symmetric paddle can turn clockwise while its center travels straight ahead.

\Image[shear-flow-vorticity.png]{title=Straight particle paths in linear shear coexist with clockwise local spin and material-line deformation}

For the planar <simple shear flow> $\mathbf u=(\gamma y,0,0)$ with constant signed <shear rate> $\gamma$, direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\omega_z=\partial_xu_y-\partial_yu_x=-\gamma,\qquad\Omega_{{\rm spin},z}=-\frac\gamma2.
$$
With $\gamma=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, these are $-1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $-0.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$: both signs indicate clockwise orientation under the <right-hand rule>. Yet a particle initially at $(x_0,y_0)$ follows the straight <pathline> $x(t)=x_0+\gamma y_0t$, $y(t)=y_0$. The <curl> measures differences of <velocity> between neighboring particles, rather than bending of one particle's <pathline>.

The <velocity gradient> has $L_{xy}=\gamma$ as its only nonzero entry. Its <spin tensor> supplies clockwise rotation, while its <rate-of-strain tensor> supplies simultaneous deformation. For a <material line> making angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal, the equation $\dot{\delta\mathbf x}=L\delta\mathbf x$ gives $\dot\alpha=-\gamma\sin^2\alpha$. Initially horizontal and vertical <material lines> therefore have <angular velocities> $0$ and $-\gamma$; their mean is $-\gamma/2$. At $t=0.5\ \mathrm{s}$ with $\gamma=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, an initially vertical displacement becomes $(0.5,1)$ times its original length and has angle $\arctan 2\approx63.435^\circ$, a clockwise change of $26.565^\circ$. The plotted cross deforms; an actual paddle's response also depends on its shape and interaction with the fluid. The exact local spin statement concerns the <spin tensor>, rather than every tracer's orientation.

= Vortex line
{parent=Vorticity}

A <vortex line> is an <integral> curve of the <vorticity> vector field: its tangent is parallel to $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$. In a two-dimensional <incompressible flow>, these lines are perpendicular to the flow plane and the <vorticity equation> gives $D\omega/Dt=0$. Since material cross-sectional area is preserved, the flux of <vorticity> through a material <vortex tube>, equal to its <circulation>, is constant.

= Fluid vortex
{synonym}

A fluid vortex is a region in which <velocity field> circulates about an axis or center. <Vorticity> measures local rotation, while <circulation> measures the integral of velocity around a closed path. A <phase vortex> is instead a <topological defect> defined by winding of an order parameter; superfluid examples connect the two notions.

= Vortex dynamics
{parent=Vortex (fluid mechanics)}

The evolution of a <vorticity> distribution together with the <velocity field> it induces. A transport equation advances <vorticity>; diagnostic inversion recovers <velocity> from it and the specified <boundary conditions>.

= Slow-drain angular-momentum amplification
{parent=Vortex dynamics}
{title2=$r^2\omega\simeq\omega_0R^2$}

Axisymmetric <inviscid flow> has no azimuthal pressure torque, so each parcel conserves specific axial <angular momentum> $j=ru_\theta=r^2\omega$. If late draining fluid originally lay near the rim $R$ of a uniformly rotating pool, then $j\simeq\omega_0R^2$ and $\omega\simeq\omega_0R^2/r^2$. The late-origin assumption is essential; torque conservation alone gives $j=\omega_0r_0^2$ for each parcel's own initial radius $r_0$. Quasistatic radial and vertical pressure balance then gives free-surface depression $h(r)=\omega_0^2R^4(r^{-2}-R^{-2})/(2g)$. The singular profile applies outside a finite drain or vortex core, not at the rotation axis.

= Nonrotating layerwise-two-dimensional vortex dynamics
{parent=Vortex dynamics}
{title2=$\partial_t\zeta+J(\psi,\zeta)=0,\quad\nabla_h^2\psi=\zeta$}

In the limiting model of infinitely strong stable <density stratification>, small <Froude number> and fixed vertical scales suppress vertical displacement. Each horizontal level evolves by <two-dimensional vortex dynamics>; $z$ labels independent copies of the horizontal <Poisson equation>. The leading normalized <Ertel potential vorticity> is $Q/N^2=\zeta$. This is a constrained asymptotic model: finite <buoyancy frequency> restores vertical motion and coupling, and arbitrarily short vertical scales invalidate the independent-layer limit. https://pordlabs.ucsd.edu/wryoung/theorySeminar/pdf14/McIntyrePV.pdf[McIntyre's discussion of potential-vorticity inversion] explains the limiting independent-layer picture.

= Two-dimensional vortex dynamics
{parent=Vortex dynamics}
{title2=$\zeta_t+J(\psi,\zeta)=0,\quad\nabla^2\psi=\zeta$}

For inviscid planar <incompressible flow>, write <velocity> as $(-\psi_y,\psi_x)$ and <vorticity> as $\zeta=\nabla^2\psi$. Taking <curl> of the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> removes <pressure> and gives the displayed transport equation, with $J(\psi,\zeta)=\psi_x\zeta_y-\psi_y\zeta_x$. The <Poisson equation> then recovers the <streamfunction> at each instant. A velocity tangent to the plane cannot stretch its perpendicular <vorticity> vector.

= Steady planar vorticity as a local function of stream function
{parent=Two-dimensional vortex dynamics}
{title2=$\Delta\psi=F(\psi)\quad\text{locally where }\nabla\psi\ne0$}

In a steady inviscid planar <incompressible flow>, <vorticity> is constant along <streamlines>. A regular <stream function> supplies a local transverse coordinate, so locally $\Delta\psi$ depends only on $\psi$. A global single-valued function needs the same value on every component of each level set. This can fail: $\psi=x^3-x$ gives the steady Euler shear $(u,v)=(0,1-3x^2)$ with constant pressure, but $\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0$ while $\Delta\psi$ is respectively $0$ and $6$.

= Line vortex
{parent=Vortex (fluid mechanics)}
{title2=$\mathbf u=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\frac{\mathbf e_z\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^2}$}

= Point vortex
{synonym}

= Point vortices
{synonym}

An ideal straight <line vortex> in a two-dimensional <incompressible flow> has <circulation> $\Gamma$ and induces velocity
$$
\mathbf u(\mathbf r)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\frac{\mathbf e_z\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^2}.
$$
Its <stream function>, with $\mathbf u=(\psi_y,-\psi_x)$, is $\psi=-\Gamma\log|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|/(2\pi)$. Positive $\Gamma$ means counterclockwise <circulation>. In an ideal point-vortex description, the vortex position is advected by the regular velocity produced by the other vortices and the boundaries; its own singular velocity is excluded.

= Motion of two point vortices
{parent=Line vortex}
{title2=$\dot{\mathbf d}=(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)J\mathbf d/(2\pi|\mathbf d|^2)$}

Take vortex strength to mean circulation and $J(x,y)=(-y,x)$. Each <point vortex> moves with the <velocity> induced by the other, so for $\mathbf d=\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2$ the displayed equation holds. Since $\mathbf d\cdot J\mathbf d=0$, separation is constant; weighting the two velocities also gives $d(\kappa_1\mathbf x_1+\kappa_2\mathbf x_2)/dt=0$. Equal strengths at opposite points of a circle of radius $a$ rotate with angular speed $\kappa/(4\pi a^2)$.

= Image vortex at a plane wall
{parent=Line vortex}
{title2=$U=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi b}$}

The <method of images> enforces impermeability at a plane wall by reflecting a <line vortex> across it and reversing its <circulation>. The two logarithmic <stream functions> cancel on the wall, which is consequently a <streamline>. For a vortex of <circulation> $\Gamma$ at $(0,b)$ above $y=0$, the image of <circulation> $-\Gamma$ at $(0,-b)$ induces velocity $(\Gamma/(4\pi b),0)$ at the real vortex. The signed drift parallel to the wall is therefore $\Gamma/(4\pi b)$.

= Line-vortex trajectory in a quarter-plane
{parent=Image vortex at a plane wall}
{title2=$r\sin2\theta=\text{constant}$}

In the quadrant $x,y>0$, a <line vortex> of <circulation> $\Gamma$ at $(x,y)$ has three images: circulations $-\Gamma$ at $(-x,y)$ and $(x,-y)$, and $+\Gamma$ at $(-x,-y)$. Their induced velocity is
$$
\dot x=\frac{\Gamma x^2}{4\pi y(x^2+y^2)},\qquad\dot y=-\frac{\Gamma y^2}{4\pi x(x^2+y^2)}.
$$
In <polar coordinates>, these equations give $\dot r=\Gamma\cot(2\theta)/(2\pi r)$ and $\dot\theta=-\Gamma/(4\pi r^2)$. Direct <differentiation> yields $\frac d{dt}(r\sin2\theta)=0$, so the trajectory obeys $r\sin2\theta=\text{constant}$.

= Hydrodynamic Biot-Savart kernel
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$\mathbf u(\mathbf x)=\frac1{4\pi}\int\frac{\boldsymbol\omega(\mathbf y)\times(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^3}\,d^3y$}

In an unbounded incompressible fluid with localized <vorticity> and no added harmonic background, velocity is reconstructed by $\mathbf u(\mathbf x)=(4\pi)^{-1}\int\boldsymbol\omega(\mathbf y)\times(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)/|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^3\,d^3y$. Its multipole expansion relates <hydrodynamic impulse> to a dipolar far-field velocity.

= Hydrodynamic impulse
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$\mathbf I=\frac12\int\mathbf x\times\boldsymbol\omega\,dV$}

<Hydrodynamic impulse> per unit density is $\mathbf I=\tfrac12\int\mathbf x\times\boldsymbol\omega\,dV$ for localized <vorticity>. In unforced all-space <incompressible flow> its derivative vanishes when far-field fluxes vanish. For a spherical all-space limit, $\mathbf I=(3/2)\lim_R\int_{V_R}\mathbf u\,dV$; a dipolar velocity tail makes the distinction from a bare <momentum> integral important.

= Dipolar velocity field of a localized eddy
{parent=Hydrodynamic impulse}
{title2=$\mathbf u_{\rm far}=[3\mathbf n(\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf n)-\mathbf I]/(4\pi r^3)$}

A localized <vorticity> distribution with nonzero <hydrodynamic impulse> induces $\mathbf u\sim[3\mathbf n(\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf n)-\mathbf I]/(4\pi r^3)$. This <curl>-free exterior velocity can remain long-ranged even when the <vorticity> is localized. In a random ensemble it produces dipolar longitudinal and transverse correlation tails.

= Vortex tube
{parent=Vorticity}

A <vortex tube> is a bundle of vortex lines bounded by a surface to which <vorticity> is tangent. <Circulation> measures its <vorticity> flux. Stretching a nearly incompressible tube reduces its cross-sectional area and increases its <vorticity>. The <Burgers vortex> is a local model in which this concentration balances viscous diffusion.

= Vortex filament
{parent=Vortex tube}

A <vortex filament> is the ideal thin-core limit of a <vortex tube>, concentrating a finite <circulation (physics)> on a curve. In an unbounded incompressible <fluid> with <velocity> tending to zero at infinity, its induced <velocity field> follows the <vorticity> version of the <Biot-Savart law>. The ideal field is singular on the filament; finite-core models are needed there. A closed circular filament is a thin <vortex ring>.

= Vortex ring
{parent=Vortex tube}
{wiki}

A <vortex ring> is a <vortex tube> bent into a closed loop, often approximately a <circle>. Its <vorticity> runs around the loop, while the induced <velocity field> passes through the centre and returns outside. For a thin ring of radius $r$ and <circulation (physics)> $\Gamma$, the <Biot-Savart law> for incompressible <vorticity> gives an axial <velocity> $u_z(z)=\Gamma r^2/[2(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$, measured relative to the ring centre, with its sign set by the <circulation> orientation. A <vortex ring> is not a loop of <fluid> whose <velocity> everywhere merely follows the circular <vortex line>.

= Velocity field of a dense coaxial vortex-ring array
{parent=Vortex ring}
{title2=$u_{\rm inside}\simeq\Gamma/l$}

A long array of equal co-oriented <vortex rings>, spaced by $l\ll r$, approximates an azimuthal <vortex sheet> on a cylinder of radius $r$. The <circulation> per unit axial <length> is $\Gamma/l$. The conditions $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf u=\boldsymbol\omega$ are mathematically analogous to the magnetostatic equations for a <solenoid>. Away from its ends, an array long compared with $r$ produces nearly uniform axial flow $\Gamma/l$ inside and weak flow outside. A finite array has return flow outside connecting its two ends. The uniform-core approximation fails close to individual vortex cores and near the ends, and the field requires a stated <circulation> orientation and background flow.

= Enstrophy
{parent=Vorticity}
{wiki}
{title2=$Z=\langle\omega^2\rangle/2$}

<Enstrophy> measures squared <vorticity>: a local density is $\omega^2/2$, and its spatial integral or ensemble mean gives a global measure. In homogeneous <incompressible flow>, <viscous dissipation> satisfies $\epsilon=\nu\langle\omega^2\rangle$. <Vortex stretching> can produce <enstrophy> in three dimensions; <kinematic viscosity> destroys it.

= Mean enstrophy balance
{parent=Enstrophy}
{title2=$\dot Z=\langle\omega_i\omega_jS_{ij}\rangle-\nu\langle|\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega|^2\rangle$}

For unforced homogeneous <incompressible flow>, the <vorticity equation> gives $\dot Z=\langle\omega_i\omega_jS_{ij}\rangle-\nu\langle|\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega|^2\rangle$. <Curl> and full-gradient squared averages agree because the <vorticity> is divergence free and boundary divergences average to zero. In established high-Reynolds-number <turbulence>, slow integral-time evolution makes production and destruction nearly balance.

= Planar vorticity velocity kernel
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$K(z)=\pm z^\perp/(2\pi|z|^2)$}

= Planar Biot-Savart kernel
{synonym}

The <velocity> induced by planar <vorticity> is a <convolution> with this kernel, whose sign depends on the <stream function> and vorticity conventions. Its magnitude is $(2\pi|z|)^{-1}$ and its derivative is bounded by $C|z|^{-2}$. Splitting the convolution at radius $R$ gives $\|K*\omega\|_\infty\leq R\|\omega\|_\infty+\|\omega\|_1/(2\pi R)$. The same singularity yields a <log-Lipschitz modulus>, enough for a <Yudovich characteristic flow>.

= Uniform-vorticity circular flow in an annulus
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$u_\phi=V(4a^2/r-r)/(3a)$}

Circular streamlines and constant <vorticity> imply $u_\phi=\omega r/2+C/r$. Zero velocity at $2a$ and positive azimuthal velocity $V$ at $a$ give
$$
u_\phi=\frac V{3a}(4a^2/r-r),\qquad \omega=-\frac{2V}{3a}.
$$
The radial <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> give $p_r=\rho u_\phi^2/r$, yielding outer-minus-inner pressure $(15-16\log2)\rho V^2/18$. The pressure difference is independent of circulation orientation.

= Vorticity cross-product identity
{parent=Vorticity}
{title2=$(\nabla\times U)\times v=(v\cdot\nabla)U-(\nabla U)^Tv$}

For differentiable vector fields, the displayed identity relates <vorticity> to the antisymmetric part of the <velocity gradient>. Together with the <curl of a cross product>, it converts the <adjoint linearized Navier-Stokes evolution> between component and rotational forms.

= Convective acceleration identity
{parent=Vorticity cross-product identity}
{title2=$(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$}

The local vector-calculus identity
$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2)-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)
$$
separates the convective <acceleration> into a kinetic-energy gradient and a <vorticity> cross-product contribution. Substitution into the steady <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> yields the vorticity form of the <Bernoulli equation>.

= Kinetic helicity
{synonym}

= Hydrodynamic helicity
{synonym}

Kinetic helicity measures the alignment and linking of a <velocity field> and its <vorticity>. It is the volume integral of $\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\omega$, with boundary conditions needed for its conservation.

The integral measures linkage and twisting of <vortex lines> through the <velocity> and <vorticity> fields.

= Kinetic helicity density
{parent=Hydrodynamical helicity}
{title2=$q=\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\omega$}

The local density of <kinetic helicity> is $q=\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\omega$. Conservation of its volume integral does not imply that $q$ is a materially advected scalar.

= Helicity vector of a solenoidal Fourier mode
{parent=Kinetic helicity density}
{title2=$\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(i\hat{\mathbf u}^*\times\hat{\mathbf u})=H\mathbf k$}

For a transverse complex velocity amplitude $\hat{\mathbf u}$ and real nonzero <wavevector> $\mathbf k$, write $\hat{\mathbf u}=\mathbf a+i\mathbf c$. Solenoidality makes both real vectors perpendicular to $\mathbf k$, so $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(i\hat{\mathbf u}^*\times\hat{\mathbf u})=-\mathbf a\times\mathbf c=H\mathbf k$. The corresponding spatial average of <kinetic helicity density> is $-Hk^2$. Cross-product order determines this sign.

= Kinetic helicity conservation law
{parent=Kinetic helicity density}

For smooth inviscid <barotropic fluid> motion without gravity or magnetic forces, let $w$ be the <specific enthalpy> with $\nabla w=\rho^{-1}\nabla p$, and set $q=\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\omega$. Then
$$
\partial_tq+\nabla\cdot\left[q\mathbf u+\left(w-\frac{u^2}{2}\right)\boldsymbol\omega\right]=0.
$$
This follows by dotting the <barotropic vorticity transport> equation with $\mathbf u$ and using the <divergence of a cross product>. A volume integral is constant when the resulting boundary flux vanishes.

= Material conservation of kinetic helicity density
{parent=Kinetic helicity conservation law}

The <kinetic helicity conservation law> implies
$$
\frac{Dq}{Dt}=\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{u^2}{2}-w\right)-q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u.
$$
Therefore $q$ is materially constant exactly when the right side vanishes. <Incompressible flow> together with constancy of $w-u^2/2$ along vortex lines is sufficient. In compressible flow, the density-normalized quantity satisfies $D(q/\rho)/Dt=-(\boldsymbol\omega/\rho)\cdot\nabla(w-u^2/2)$.

= Absolute vorticity
{parent=Vorticity}
{wiki}

Absolute vorticity is the vorticity measured in an inertial frame. In a frame rotating with angular velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega$, it is $\boldsymbol\omega+2\boldsymbol\Omega$.

= Planetary vorticity
{parent=Absolute vorticity}
{title2=$f=2\Omega\sin\phi$}

The planetary contribution to absolute <vorticity> is supplied by the rotating frame. Its local vertical component is the <Coriolis parameter> $f=2\Omega\sin\phi$. On a <beta plane>, motion with northward velocity $v$ changes this contribution at the rate $\beta v$.

= Absolute-vorticity flux tensor
{parent=Absolute vorticity}
{title2=$T_{ij}=u_i\omega_j-\omega_i u_j$}

For a flow satisfying <incompressibility>, this antisymmetric tensor writes advection and <vortex stretching> in conservative form. With its first index as transport direction, $(\nabla\cdot T)_j=\partial_iT_{ij}$.

= Radial vorticity conservation in a shearing sheet
{parent=Absolute-vorticity flux tensor}
{title2=$\partial_t\omega_x+\partial_iT_{ix}=0$}

When the only nonpotential force points radially, its <curl> has no radial component. The radial <absolute vorticity> therefore obeys a local flux <conservation law>, although <vortex stretching> can change it along a fluid trajectory.

= Relative vorticity
{title2=$\boldsymbol\omega$}
{parent=Vorticity}

Relative vorticity is the curl of velocity measured in the chosen rotating frame. Adding the planetary vorticity gives <absolute vorticity>.

= Circulation
{synonym}

Use <circulation> to summarize how much a <velocity field> runs along an oriented <closed curve> $C$, such as a <circle> around a <fluid vortex>. Its definition is the <line integral>
$$
\Gamma_C=\oint_C\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf l,
$$
where $d\mathbf l$ is tangent to $C$ in the chosen direction. Reversing that direction reverses the sign of <circulation>. Its units are $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, because <velocity> is multiplied by a length. Counterclockwise <circulation> in the horizontal plane is positive when the chosen <unit normal vector> is $+\mathbf e_z$, according to the <right-hand rule>.

For <solid-body rotation> $\mathbf u=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)$, a counterclockwise <circle> of radius $R$ has constant tangential <velocity> $\Omega R$. Multiplying by its circumference gives
$$
\Gamma_C=2\pi\Omega R^2.
$$
For the potential <line vortex> $\mathbf u=K\mathbf e_\theta/r$, the same <circle> instead gives $\Gamma_C=(K/R)(2\pi R)=2\pi K$, independent of $R$. Stipulate $\Omega=1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $R=1\ \mathrm m$ and $K=1\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$: both <velocity fields> have $\Gamma_C=2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\approx6.283185\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, although their <vorticities> in the fluid are $2\mathbf e_z\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and zero respectively. On a <circle> of radius $2\ \mathrm m$, their <circulations> are $8\pi\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $2\pi\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. The comparison plot in <vorticity> shows this difference in radius dependence.

The bridge between <circulation> and <vorticity> is <Stokes theorem>:
$$
\Gamma_{\partial S}=\int_S\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
Here the <velocity field> must be continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of the entire spanning surface $S$ inside the fluid, and its boundary orientation must match the <unit normal vector> $\mathbf n$. For <solid-body rotation>, the disk is admissible and its <surface integral> is $2\Omega\pi R^2$. For the potential <line vortex>, the origin is singular and excluded from the fluid: a disk crossing it is not admissible. On an <annulus>, <Stokes theorem> instead gives $\Gamma_{R_{\rm out}}-\Gamma_{R_{\rm in}}=0$, with both <circulations> here measured counterclockwise; the induced inner boundary direction is clockwise. Nonzero <circulation> therefore does not imply nonzero local <curl> in the available fluid. https://davidtong.org/pdfs/teaching/fluid-mechanics/fluids1.pdf[David Tong, Fluid Mechanics, section 2.2, circular-flow example]{ref}

This is useful because <Stokes theorem> lets a local <vorticity> measurement determine a loop's <circulation> when a suitable surface exists, while <Kelvin's circulation theorem> tracks <circulation> along a moving <material curve>. The beauty is the precise link between a local <derivative> and a global <line integral>, with the domain deciding which conclusions are valid.

= Kelvin's circulation theorem
{parent=Circulation (physics)}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$d\Gamma/dt=0$}

In an inviscid barotropic fluid subject to conservative body force, the <circulation> of a material loop is constant. For constant density, parameterize that loop by $\mathbf r(s,t)$ with $\mathbf r_t=\mathbf u$. Differentiating $\Gamma=\int\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf r_s\,ds$ gives an integral of $(D\mathbf u/Dt)\cdot d\mathbf r$ plus $\int\partial_s(|\mathbf u|^2/2)ds$. The latter vanishes on a closed loop, and the <Euler equations> make the former the integral of a gradient, also zero. For a barotropic density, $\nabla p/\rho$ is still a gradient of specific enthalpy. Constant circulation is consistent with changing local <vorticity> when a vortex tube changes its cross-sectional area.

= Irrotational circulation in an exterior domain
{parent=Kelvin's circulation theorem}

In smooth barotropic inviscid flow with conservative <body force>, <Kelvin's circulation theorem> preserves circulation of <material curves>. Initially zero <vorticity> therefore gives zero circulation on contractible loops for all times. A loop winding around an excluded obstacle can have a nonzero constant circulation: the planar field $\mathbf u=\Gamma\mathbf e_\theta/(2\pi r)$ is <irrotational> outside the origin but has circulation $\Gamma$ on a positively oriented enclosing loop.

= Irrotational flow
{parent=Vorticity}
{wiki}
{title2=$\nabla\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0$}

Use <irrotational flow> to identify where a <velocity potential> can describe the <velocity field> locally. An <irrotational flow> has $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0$ throughout the fluid region. For a continuously differentiable <velocity field>, the <Poincare lemma> supplies a <velocity potential> on sufficiently small neighborhoods. A global single-valued <velocity potential> requires zero <circulation> around every <closed curve>; a <simply connected domain> is a sufficient domain assumption.

The potential <line vortex> $\mathbf u=K\mathbf e_\theta/r$ is an <irrotational flow> on the punctured plane, or on an <annulus> $a<r<b$ with $a>0$, even though its <pathlines> are circular. Every sufficiently small loop bounding a disk in that fluid has zero <circulation>, by <Stokes theorem>. A loop winding once counterclockwise around the excluded origin has <circulation> $2\pi K$. Thus local <irrotational flow> and nonzero <circulation> around a hole are compatible: the enclosing disk passes outside the domain of the <velocity field>.

An <irrotational flow> is a kinematic condition on a <velocity field>, independent of whether a particular model neglects <viscosity> or assumes <incompressible flow>. Adding <incompressible flow> makes the local <velocity potential> satisfy the <Laplace equation>. This reduces the number of unknowns and makes <potential flow> a powerful starting point for flow around bodies and for <gravity waves>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-016-hydrodynamics-13-012-fall-2005/c472432debcf6ee250209b68cf18cc12_2005reading4.pdf[A.
