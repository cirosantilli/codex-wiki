= Solution

On a <probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$, a standard one-dimensional <Brownian motion> is a real-valued <stochastic process> $B=(B_t)_{t\geq0}$ with $B_0=0$ <almost surely>, continuous <sample paths> <almost surely>, and <independent increments>, such that
$$
\boxed{B_t-B_s\sim N(0,t-s)\qquad(0\leq s<t).}
$$
Here the second parameter is the <variance>. Thus its <Brownian increments> have the <normal distribution> with <mean> zero and <variance> equal to elapsed time. Equivalently, relative to a specified <filtration> $(\mathcal F_t)$, require $B$ to be an <adapted process> and $B_t-B_s$ to be <independent> of $\mathcal F_s$ with this <normal distribution>. Taking the <natural Brownian filtration> recovers the first definition.

= Solution

In the <Lévy construction of Brownian motion>, start with $B_0=0$ and $B_1=Z_0$, where $Z_0$ has the <standard normal distribution>. Suppose values are defined at the endpoints $u=k2^{-n}$ and $v=(k+1)2^{-n}$. Set
$$
B_{(u+v)/2}=\frac{B_u+B_v}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{v-u}}2Z_{n,k},
$$
using fresh <independent> <standard normal random variables> $Z_{n,k}$, also <independent> of everything previously used. Join the values by straight segments to obtain a continuous piecewise linear process $B^{(n)}$ on the dyadic grid.

To verify the <Brownian increments>, write $D=B_v-B_u$ and $\ell=v-u$. The two new increments are $D/2+\sqrt\ell Z_{n,k}/2$ and $D/2-\sqrt\ell Z_{n,k}/2$. If $D\sim N(0,\ell)$, their <variances> are $\ell/2$ and their <covariance> is zero. They are <jointly Gaussian>, so they are <independent>. Induction proves the required <Brownian increments> on every dyadic grid.

For convergence, successive interpolants differ by tents of disjoint interiors, so
$$
\|B^{(n+1)}-B^{(n)}\|_\infty
=2^{-n/2-1}\max_{0\leq k<2^n}|Z_{n,k}|.
$$
The <Gaussian tail bound> and the <union bound> give
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\max_{k<2^n}|Z_{n,k}|>n\right)
\leq2^{n+1}e^{-n^2/2}.
$$
These <probabilities> are summable. The <first Borel-Cantelli lemma> and $\sum_n n2^{-n/2}<\infty$ therefore prove <uniform convergence> of the interpolants <almost surely>. Their limit has continuous <sample paths>. Approximating any finite set of times by dyadic times passes the <jointly Gaussian> increment laws to the limit, giving a standard <Brownian motion> on $[0,1]$.

Equivalently, with $\psi_{n,k}(t)=\int_0^tH_{n,k}(s)\,ds$, these tents give the <Lévy-Ciesielski expansion>
$$
\boxed{B_t=Z_0t+\sum_{n=0}^\infty\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-1}Z_{n,k}\psi_{n,k}(t),
\quad\text{uniformly almost surely on }[0,1].}
$$

Let $S_N=\sum_{n=0}^N\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-1}a_{n,k}H_{n,k}$. The assumed <orthonormality> of the <Haar wavelets> gives
$$
\|S_N\|_{\mathcal H}^2=\sum_{n=0}^N\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-1}a_{n,k}^2.
$$
Enumerate the coefficients level by level as $A_1,A_2,\ldots$. They are <independent and identically distributed> <standard normal random variables>, so the squares have <mean> one. The <strong law of large numbers> implies
$$
\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^mA_j^2\longrightarrow1\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\|S_N\|_{\mathcal H}^2\longrightarrow\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
A convergent sequence in a <Hilbert space> must be bounded in <norm>. Hence this random <Haar wavelet> series diverges in $\mathcal H$ <almost surely>.

Enumerate the <orthonormal basis> consisting of $H_0$ and the <Haar wavelets> $H_{n,k}$ as $(e_j)_{j\geq1}$, and enumerate the corresponding <independent> <standard normal random variables> as $(A_j)_{j\geq1}$. Set $c_j=\langle h,e_j\rangle_{\mathcal H}$. The <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> gives
$$
\sum_{j=1}^\infty c_j^2=\|h\|_{\mathcal H}^2<\infty.
$$
The partial sums $T_m=\sum_{j=1}^mc_jA_j$ form a <martingale> for $\sigma(A_1,\ldots,A_m)$, since the next summand is <independent> of the past and has <mean> zero. Moreover,
$$
\mathbb ET_m^2=\sum_{j=1}^mc_j^2\leq\|h\|_{\mathcal H}^2.
$$
The <martingale convergence theorem> for a <martingale> bounded in <L2 space> proves convergence <almost surely> and <convergence in L2>. Equivalently one may apply the <Kolmogorov convergence theorem> to the summable <variances> $c_j^2$.

A finite <linear combination of independent normal random variables> is normal. Its <characteristic function> is $\mathbb E e^{iuT_m}=\exp(-u^2\sum_{j\leq m}c_j^2/2)$. Passing to the limit identifies
$$
\boxed{Wh=\lim_{m\to\infty}T_m\quad\text{almost surely and in }L^2(\mathbb P),
\qquad Wh\sim N(0,\|h\|_{\mathcal H}^2).}
$$
For $h=0$ this is the constant zero <random variable>. Each fixed $h$ has its own permissible null set; simultaneous pathwise convergence for all $h\in\mathcal H$ is unnecessary.

A finite family is <jointly Gaussian> precisely when every real linear combination has a possibly degenerate <normal distribution>. Thus every finite subfamily of $(Wh)_{h\in\mathcal H}$ is <jointly Gaussian>, which is the meaning of joint Gaussianity for this collection.

Write $e_t=\mathbf1_{[0,t]}$ as an element of <L2 space>. Parts (iii) and (iv) show that $B_t=We_t$ is a centered <Gaussian process> with <covariance function>
$$
\mathbb E[B_sB_t]=\langle e_s,e_t\rangle=\min(s,t).
$$
In particular $B_t-B_s\sim N(0,|t-s|)$. The fourth <moment> of a centered <normal random variable> of <variance> $v$ is $3v^2$, so
$$
\mathbb E|B_t-B_s|^4=3|t-s|^2.
$$
The <Kolmogorov continuity theorem>, with moment exponent $4$ and time exponent $2>1$, provides a <continuous modification> $\widehat B$. At each finite set of times this <modification of a stochastic process> has the same joint law as $B$.

For $0\leq t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_m\leq1$, the successive increments are <jointly Gaussian>. The <covariance> of two increments on disjoint intervals equals the <inner product> of their disjoint interval indicators and is zero. Zero cross-<covariances> of <jointly Gaussian> variables imply <independence>, and each increment has <variance> $t_j-t_{j-1}$. Finally $e_0=0$ in <L2 space>, so $\widehat B_0=0$ <almost surely>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\widehat B\text{ is standard Brownian motion on }[0,1].}
$$
Independence from the whole past in its <natural Brownian filtration> follows by extending finite-dimensional <independence> to the generated <sigma-algebra>.

For any real <orthonormal basis> $(G_i)$, the argument of (b)(ii) gives <almost sure convergence> and <convergence in L2> of $\widetilde Wh$. Finite linear combinations have <normal distributions>, and the <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> gives
$$
\mathbb E[(\widetilde Wf)(\widetilde Wg)]=\sum_i\langle f,G_i\rangle\langle g,G_i\rangle
=\langle f,g\rangle.
$$
Thus $W$ and $\widetilde W$ are centered <Gaussian processes> with the same <covariance function>. More explicitly, their finite-dimensional <characteristic functions> are both
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\exp\!\left(i\sum_{j=1}^mu_jWh_j\right)
=\exp\!\left(-\frac12\left\|\sum_{j=1}^mu_jh_j\right\|_{\mathcal H}^2\right).}
$$
This proves the <basis invariance of an isonormal Gaussian process>: equality of all <finite-dimensional distributions>, and hence equality in law of the indexed collections.

For the specified cosine <orthonormal basis>, put $\lambda_i=\pi(i-\tfrac12)$. Its coefficient against an interval indicator is
$$
\langle\mathbf1_{[0,t]},G_i\rangle
=\sqrt2\int_0^t\cos(\lambda_i s)\,ds
=\frac{\sqrt2\sin(\lambda_i t)}{\lambda_i}.
$$
Consequently the <Brownian half-integer sine expansion>, a <Karhunen–Loève expansion>, is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde B_t
=\sqrt2\sum_{i=1}^\infty b_i\frac{\sin(\pi(i-\tfrac12)t)}{\pi(i-\tfrac12)}.}
$$
For every fixed $t$, the sum converges <almost surely> and in <L2 space> because the coefficient squares sum to $\|\mathbf1_{[0,t]}\|^2=t$. Its <continuous modification> is a standard <Brownian motion> by (b)(v). The expansion also holds in $L^2(\Omega\times[0,1])$: the <expectation> of the squared tail is the integral of the coefficient-square tail, which tends to zero by the <dominated convergence theorem>, with bound $t$.

To realize this expansion for the original $B$, choose $b_i=WG_i$. These are <jointly Gaussian> with <covariance matrix> equal to the identity, hence are <independent> <standard normal random variables>. The finite <orthogonal projections> of $\mathbf1_{[0,t]}$ onto $G_1,\ldots,G_N$ converge in $\mathcal H$, and the identity $\mathbb E|Wh|^2=\|h\|^2$ transfers that convergence to the displayed expansion for $B_t$. With an independently chosen sequence $(b_i)$ the resulting <Brownian motion> has the same law; a pathwise identification uses the coupling just given.

Let $0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_m<\infty$ and let $\xi_j$ be bounded $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-<measurable> <random variables>. For the bounded <simple predictable process>
$$
H_s=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\xi_j\mathbf1_{(t_j,t_{j+1}]}(s),
$$
the <Itô integral> against a <continuous martingale> $M\in\mathcal H^{2,c}$ is defined by the increment sum
$$
\boxed{(H\mathbin\cdot M)_t=\int_0^tH_s\,dM_s
:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\xi_j\bigl(M_{t\wedge t_{j+1}}-M_{t\wedge t_j}\bigr).}
$$
The same definition applies to <elementary predictable processes with stopping-time intervals>, replacing $t_j$ by ordered <stopping times> $T_j$ and taking bounded $\mathcal F_{T_j}$-<measurable> coefficients. Refinement of the intervals leaves the increment sum unchanged. The initial value of the <integral> is zero, and its <martingale> property follows from the <conditional expectation> identity for subsequent increments of $M$.

For a bounded <simple predictable process> $H$ and a <continuous martingale> $M\in\mathcal H^{2,c}$, the <Itô isometry> states
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left|\int_0^tH_s\,dM_s\right|^2
=\mathbb E\int_0^tH_s^2\,d\langle M\rangle_s.}
$$
Here $\langle M\rangle$ is the <predictable quadratic variation>, equal to $[M]$ for a <continuous martingale>. On the whole half-line, the terminal form is
$$
\mathbb E|(H\mathbin\cdot M)_\infty|^2
=\mathbb E\int_0^\infty H_s^2\,d\langle M\rangle_s.
$$
The <Itô integral> starts at zero even when $M_0\ne0$. For <Brownian motion>, $d\langle B\rangle_s=ds$.

For completeness, cross terms of the finite increment sum have <expectation> zero by conditioning on the earlier endpoint of the later increment. Also $M^2-\langle M\rangle$ is a <martingale>, so the remaining terms satisfy
$$
\mathbb E[\xi_j^2(M_v-M_u)^2]
=\mathbb E[\xi_j^2(\langle M\rangle_v-\langle M\rangle_u)]
\quad(u=t_j<t,\ v=t_{j+1}\wedge t).
$$
This proves the displayed identity without a change-of-variables formula. Completing bounded <simple predictable processes> in the norm $(\mathbb E\int H^2d\langle M\rangle)^{1/2}$ defines the <Itô integral> of every <square-integrable stochastic integrand>. This completion will justify the unbounded Brownian integrands below.

Fix $t>0$, a deterministic <partition of an interval> $\pi$, and write $\eta(s)=t_j$ on $[t_j,t_{j+1})$. Its mesh is $|\pi|=\max_j(t_{j+1}-t_j)$. The <Brownian increment> <variance> gives
$$
\mathbb E\int_0^t|B_s-B_{\eta(s)}|^2ds
=\frac12\sum_j(\Delta t_j)^2\leq\frac{t|\pi|}{2}.
$$
Thus the left-endpoint sums $\sum_jB_{t_j}\Delta B_j$ converge in <L2 space> to the <Itô integral>. Although these step coefficients are unbounded, clipping them at finite levels first makes them bounded <simple predictable processes>; the <Itô isometry> and their finite second <moments> remove the clipping.

The elementary identity $B_{t_{j+1}}^2-B_{t_j}^2=2B_{t_j}\Delta B_j+(\Delta B_j)^2$ gives
$$
2\sum_jB_{t_j}\Delta B_j=B_t^2-\sum_j(\Delta B_j)^2.
$$
The squared <Brownian increments> are <independent>, with <means> $\Delta t_j$ and <variances> $2(\Delta t_j)^2$, since a centered <normal random variable> of <variance> $v$ has fourth <moment> $3v^2$. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E\left|\sum_j(\Delta B_j)^2-t\right|^2
=2\sum_j(\Delta t_j)^2\leq2t|\pi|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Passing to the <convergence in L2> limits yields
$$
\boxed{\int_0^tB_s\,dB_s=\frac12(B_t^2-t).}
$$
This holds for each fixed $t$; continuous <sample paths> of both sides extend equality from rational times to all $t$ on one event of <probability> one.

To establish the <Brownian weighted quadratic variation in L2> for the deterministic <partition of an interval>, decompose
$$
\sum_jB_j(\Delta B_j)^2-\int_0^tB_s\,ds
=U_\pi+V_\pi,
$$
where $U_\pi=\sum_jB_j((\Delta B_j)^2-\Delta t_j)$ and $V_\pi=\sum_jB_j\Delta t_j-\int_0^tB_sds$.

The summands in $U_\pi$ are <martingale differences>: $B_j$ is $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-<measurable>, and the following <Brownian increment> is <independent> of $\mathcal F_{t_j}$. Their conditional <means> are zero, so distinct summands are <orthogonal> in <L2 space>. Since $\mathbb EB_j^2=t_j$ and $\mathbb E[((\Delta B_j)^2-\Delta t_j)^2]=2(\Delta t_j)^2$,
$$
\mathbb EU_\pi^2=2\sum_jt_j(\Delta t_j)^2
\leq2t^2|\pi|\longrightarrow0.
$$
For the <Riemann sum> error, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb EV_\pi^2
\leq t\sum_j\int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}}\mathbb E|B_j-B_s|^2ds
=\frac t2\sum_j(\Delta t_j)^2
\leq\frac{t^2|\pi|}{2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the triangle inequality in <L2 space> proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_jB_j(\Delta B_j)^2
\longrightarrow\int_0^tB_s\,ds\quad\text{in }L^2(\mathbb P).}
$$
The time integration variable is $s$.

+3\sum_jB_j(\Delta B_j)^2

\mathbb E\left|\sum_j(\Delta B_j)^3\right|^2

The second sum converges in <L2 space> to $3\int_0^tB_sds$ by (i).

\mathbb E|B_s^2-B_j^2|^2
=\mathbb E|2B_jD+D^2|^2=4t_ju+3u^2.

\mathbb E\int_0^t|B_s^2-B_{\eta(s)}^2|^2ds

\boxed{\int_0^tB_s^2\,dB_s=\frac13B_t^3-\int_0^tB_s\,ds.}

The <optional stopping theorem> for a right-continuous <martingale> $M$ on a <filtered probability space> satisfying the <usual conditions for a filtration> states that, for bounded <stopping times> $\sigma\leq\tau$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_\tau\mid\mathcal F_\sigma]=M_\sigma,
\qquad \mathbb EM_\tau=\mathbb EM_\sigma.}
$$
For a <submartingale> the conditional equality becomes $\mathbb E[M_\tau\mid\mathcal F_\sigma]\geq M_\sigma$, and for a <supermartingale> it becomes the reverse inequality.

For a <uniformly integrable martingale>, boundedness of the <stopping times> can be removed, with the terminal limit used if a <stopping time> is infinite. A useful application is to a bounded <stopped martingale> $M_{t\wedge\tau}$ with $\tau<\infty$ <almost surely>: apply the bounded-time theorem first and then the <bounded convergence theorem> to obtain $\mathbb EM_\tau=\mathbb EM_0$. Finiteness of $\tau$ alone is not a sufficient hypothesis for an arbitrary <martingale>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $X=(X^1,\ldots,X^d)$ be a <continuous semimartingale>, with $X^j=X_0^j+M^j+A^j$, where $M^j$ is a zero-starting <continuous local martingale> and $A^j$ is a continuous <finite-variation process> starting at zero. For $f\in C^{1,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb R^d)$, the <Itô formula> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f(t,X_t)=f(0,X_0)
&+\int_0^t\partial_sf(s,X_s)\,ds
+\sum_{j=1}^d\int_0^t\partial_jf(s,X_s)\,dX_s^j\\
&+\frac12\sum_{j,k=1}^d\int_0^t\partial_{jk}f(s,X_s)\,d[M^j,M^k]_s.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $[M^j,M^k]$ is the <quadratic covariation>; the <finite-variation process> parts contribute no such term. The integrals against the <finite-variation processes> are understood pathwise, and those against the <continuous local martingales> are <Itô integrals> after localization.

For $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion> with <independent> standard coordinates, $d[B^j,B^k]_s=\delta_{jk}ds$. In particular, if $f\in C^2$ is independent of time,
$$
f(B_t)=f(B_0)+\sum_j\int_0^t\partial_jf(B_s)\,dB_s^j
+\frac12\int_0^t\Delta f(B_s)\,ds,
$$
where $\Delta$ is the <Laplace operator>. The same formulas hold up to exit from an open domain on which $f$ is smooth.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $\rho=|z_0|$ and $f(x,y)=\log\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. On the <annulus> $r<\sqrt{x^2+y^2}<R$, its <gradient> and <Laplace operator> are
$$
f_x=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\qquad f_y=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},
\qquad \Delta f=0.
$$
Thus $f$ is a <harmonic function> there. The stopped <Itô formula> gives
$$
\log|B_{t\wedge\tau}|=\log\rho
+\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{B_1(s)}{|B_s|^2}\,dB_1(s)
+\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{B_2(s)}{|B_s|^2}\,dB_2(s).
$$
The sum of the squared coefficients is $|B_s|^{-2}\leq r^{-2}$ before $\tau$. The <Itô isometry> therefore makes the stochastic term a true <square-integrable> <martingale> on every finite interval. Continuity prevents overshoot at exit, so $\log r\leq\log|B_{t\wedge\tau}|\leq\log R$. This proves the required bounded <martingale> property.

Before taking the terminal <expectation>, prove that $\tau$ is finite. Set $\theta_R=\inf\{t\geq0:|B_t|=R\}$. The <Itô formula> for $|B|^2$ shows that
$$
|B_{t\wedge\theta_R}|^2-\rho^2-2(t\wedge\theta_R)
$$
is a <martingale>, since its stopped stochastic integrands are bounded. Taking <expectations> yields
$$
2\mathbb E(t\wedge\theta_R)
=\mathbb E|B_{t\wedge\theta_R}|^2-\rho^2\leq R^2-\rho^2.
$$
The <monotone convergence theorem> gives $\mathbb E\theta_R\leq(R^2-\rho^2)/2<\infty$. Hence $\tau\leq\theta_R<\infty$ <almost surely>.

The <bounded convergence theorem>, equivalently the <optional stopping theorem> applied to this bounded <martingale>, gives $\mathbb E\log|B_\tau|=\log\rho$. The two exit events partition the sample space up to a null set. Writing $p=\mathbb P(\tau_r<\tau_R)$,
$$
\log\rho=p\log r+(1-p)\log R.
$$
Solving gives the <planar Brownian annulus hitting probability>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|B_\tau|=r)=\mathbb P(\tau_r<\tau_R)
=\frac{\log(R/\rho)}{\log(R/r)}.}
$$

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Fix an outer radius $R>\rho=|z_0|$. By continuity, reaching zero before radius $R$ requires first reaching every radius $\varepsilon\in(0,\rho)$. The <planar Brownian annulus hitting probability> from (i) therefore implies
$$
0\leq\mathbb P(\tau_0<\tau_R)
\leq\mathbb P(\tau_\varepsilon<\tau_R)
=\frac{\log(R/\rho)}{\log(R/\varepsilon)}\longrightarrow0
\quad(\varepsilon\downarrow0).
$$
Thus $\mathbb P(\tau_0<\tau_R)=0$ for every fixed $R>\rho$.

On any continuous <sample path> that reaches zero in finite time, the radius has a finite maximum before that time. There is therefore some integer $m>\rho$ for which zero is reached before radius $m$. Consequently
$$
\{\tau_0<\infty\}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb N,\,m>\rho}\{\tau_0<\tau_m\}.
$$
Every event on the right has <probability> zero, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\tau_0=\infty)=1.}
$$
This proves that <planar Brownian motion avoids a fixed point> even though it can reach arbitrarily small positive radii.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

First suppose $0<r<\rho=|z_0|$. For every $R>\rho$, the <planar Brownian annulus hitting probability> gives
$$
\mathbb P(\tau_r<\infty)
\geq\mathbb P(\tau_r<\tau_R)
=\frac{\log(R/\rho)}{\log(R/r)}\longrightarrow1
\quad(R\to\infty).
$$
Hence $\tau_r<\infty$ <almost surely>. This uses increasing outer radii; letting the inner radius tend to zero instead proves the different claim in part (ii).

For $r>\rho$, the disk-exit estimate in (i), with outer radius $r$, directly yields $\mathbb E\tau_r\leq(r^2-\rho^2)/2<\infty$.

For $r=\rho$, retain the definition $\tau_r=\inf\{t>0:|B_t|=r\}$. The first case shows that radius $\rho/2$ is reached in finite time. Starting there, the <strong Markov property> of <Brownian motion> and the disk-exit argument show that radius $\rho$ is subsequently reached in finite time. Thus the infimum over positive hitting times is finite in this case as well. Altogether,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\tau_r<\infty)=1\qquad\text{for every }r>0.}
$$
The conclusion can also be made simultaneous in all $r>0$: intersect the probability-one events for positive rational radii, then use continuity to cross each intermediate radius.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $f(y)=|y|$ away from zero. Its first and second <partial derivatives> are
$$
\partial_kf(y)=\frac{y_k}{|y|},\qquad
\partial_{jk}f(y)=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{|y|}-\frac{y_jy_k}{|y|^3}.
$$
Thus its <Laplace operator> is $\Delta f(y)=(n-1)/|y|$. The <quadratic covariations> of the <independent> Brownian coordinates satisfy $d[B_j,B_k]_s=\delta_{jk}ds$. The stopped <Itô formula> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{X_{t\wedge\tau}=|x|+\frac{n-1}{2}\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{ds}{X_s}
+\sum_{k=1}^n\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{B_k(s)}{X_s}\,dB_k(s).}
$$
For $t<\tau$, this is the required integral representation.

To identify the driving <Brownian motion>, the stopped stochastic term alone is insufficient: its <quadratic variation> is $t\wedge\tau$. Extend its unit direction after exit. Define a vector <predictable process> $u(s)$ by
$$
u(s)=\begin{cases}B(s)/X_s,&s\leq\tau,\\(1,0,\ldots,0),&s>\tau,\end{cases}
\qquad
W_t=\sum_{k=1}^n\int_0^tu_k(s)\,dB_k(s).
$$
Predictability follows from the continuous adapted direction before stopping and the left-continuous adapted indicator $\mathbf1_{\{s\leq\tau\}}$. This $W$ is a zero-starting <continuous martingale>, and
$$
[W]_t=\int_0^t\sum_{k=1}^nu_k(s)^2ds=t.
$$
The <Lévy characterization of Brownian motion> identifies $W$ as a one-dimensional <Brownian motion>. Before $\tau$, the preceding representation becomes the <stochastic differential equation> of the dimension-$n$ <Bessel process>:
$$
\boxed{dX_t=\frac{n-1}{2X_t}\,dt+dW_t\qquad(t<\tau).}
$$
The fully stopped equation has both integrals stopped at $\tau$.

For $n=2$, apply the one-dimensional <Itô formula> to the <logarithm>. Before $\tau$, $d[X]_t=dt$, so the two drift terms cancel:
$$
d\log X_t=\frac1{X_t}\left(\frac1{2X_t}\,dt+dW_t\right)
-\frac1{2X_t^2}\,dt=\frac1{X_t}\,dW_t.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\log X_{t\wedge\tau}=\log|x|+\int_0^{t\wedge\tau}\frac{dW_s}{X_s}
\quad\text{is a bounded martingale}.}
$$
Indeed $X_s\geq r$ before stopping, so the <Itô isometry> bounds the stochastic term's second <moment> by $t/r^2$, and $\log X_{t\wedge\tau}\in[\log r,\log R]$. This is the <martingale> assertion under the paper's standing stopping assumption. Without stopping, the same calculation establishes only a <local martingale>; the stopped qualification is essential for the stated argument.
