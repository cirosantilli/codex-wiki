= Thermionic emission

Release of <electrons> from a heated material; one source of <electrons> in an <X-ray tube>.

Heating a <metal> gives some <electrons> enough <energy> to escape its surface. A heated <diode cathode> can therefore supply an <electric current> across a <vacuum> gap to a collecting <diode anode>. This makes <temperature> a control for the supply of <electrons>. Conversely, measuring that supply at several <temperatures> lets us estimate the surface <work function>: the <energy> barrier measured from the <Fermi level> to the nearby <vacuum> level. A <Richardson plot> turns the strong <exponential function> dependence into a slope.

Three questions must be kept separate: how many <electrons> cross the surface, how many traverse the gap, and how many the collecting <diode anode> records. The <Richardson-Dushman law> answers the first under specified surface assumptions. <Space charge> can obstruct the second, while incomplete collection can alter the third.

= Richardson-Dushman law
{parent=Thermionic emission}
{c}
{title2=$J=A T^2\exp[-W/(k_BT)]$}

= Richardson law
{synonym}
{c}

The <Richardson-Dushman law> models the magnitude of emitted <electric current density> as
$$
J=A T^2\exp\!\left(-\frac{W}{k_BT}\right).
$$
Here $T$ is absolute <thermodynamic temperature>, $W$ is the <work function>, $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, and $A$ is the <thermionic Richardson constant>. The signed <electric current density> points opposite to the outgoing <electron> motion; $J$ here is positive. For uniform emission over area $S$, the emitted <electric current> magnitude is $I=SJ$.

The <Richardson-Dushman law> assumes an emitter close to <thermal equilibrium>, a surface with a well-defined and approximately constant <work function> over the fitted <temperature> range, and predominantly thermal escape over the barrier. Interpreting a measured collector <electric current> with this law additionally requires an <emission-limited thermionic current>, known emitting area, and negligible return emission from the collector. Strong <electric fields> can lower the surface barrier or enable <quantum tunnelling>; then this zero-field expression is insufficient.

For a deliberately chosen teaching model, take $W=2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $A=120\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}\,K^{-2}}$, expressing the barrier in <electronvolts>. These are illustrative parameters, not measured properties of a named material. Using $k_B=8.617333262\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}$, at $1000\,\mathrm K$ the <Boltzmann factor> is about $8.33\times10^{-11}$, yet
$$
J(1000\,\mathrm K)\simeq0.00999\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}},
\qquad
J(1200\,\mathrm K)\simeq0.6885\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}.
$$
A 20 percent increase in <temperature> raises the predicted <electric current density> by about 69 times. For a chosen emitting area $S=0.010\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, these correspond to approximately $0.100\,\mathrm{mA}$ and $6.89\,\mathrm{mA}$ if collection is complete. The small escaping fraction is replenished by the external <electrical circuit>.

The sensitivity is especially clear from the <logarithmic derivative> at fixed $A,W$:
$$
\frac{d\ln J}{dT}=\frac{2}{T}+\frac{W}{k_BT^2}.
$$
In the chosen model at $1000\,\mathrm K$, a $1\,\mathrm K$ rise changes $J$ by about 2.5 percent to first order. Accurate <temperature> measurement matters even when <electric current> measurement is easy.

= Thermionic Richardson constant
{parent=Richardson-Dushman law}
{c}
{title2=$A$}

The coefficient $A$ has dimensions of <electric current density> divided by squared <temperature>. For ideal <nonrelativistic particles> with the free <electron mass>, two <spin> states, a planar uniform surface, and unit escape transmission, the <thermionic tail-flux derivation> gives
$$
A_0=\frac{4\pi m_e e k_B^2}{h^3}
\simeq1.202\times10^6\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}\,K^{-2}}
=120.2\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}\,K^{-2}},
$$
where $e$ is the positive <elementary charge> and $h$ is the <Planck constant>. The two area units differ by a factor of $10^4$. A fitted $A$ need not equal $A_0$: reflection at the surface, the <electronic band structure>, and uncertain emitting area affect the inferred coefficient. A constant escape transmission $\tau$ would give $A=\tau A_0$ in this simple model; an <energy>-dependent transmission generally requires an integral instead.

The name joins <Owen Willans Richardson> with <Saul Dushman>. Dushman's 1923 paper develops a thermodynamic treatment of <electron> emission analogous to <evaporation> and evaluates the prefactor using quantum ideas.<Electron Emission from Metals as a Function of Temperature by Saul Dushman>[Abstract, general equation and theoretical evaluation of A]{ref}

= Richardson plot
{parent=Richardson-Dushman law}
{c}
{title2=$\ln[J/(A_{\rm ref}T^2)]$ versus $1/T$}

Choose a fixed positive reference $A_{\rm ref}$ with the same units as $A$. Taking the <natural logarithm> of a dimensionless ratio gives
$$
x=\frac1T,\qquad
y=\ln\!\left(\frac{J}{A_{\rm ref}T^2}\right)
=\ln\!\left(\frac{A}{A_{\rm ref}}\right)-\frac{W}{k_B}x.
$$
Thus the <Richardson plot> is a <straight line> when $A$ and $W$ are constant, with slope $-W/k_B$ and intercept $\ln(A/A_{\rm ref})$. The familiar notation $\ln(J/T^2)$ silently chooses units; the reference makes that choice explicit. Changing $A_{\rm ref}$ shifts the intercept and leaves the inferred <work function> unchanged. If the horizontal axis instead uses $1000\,\mathrm K/T$, its slope is $-W/(1000\,\mathrm K\,k_B)$.

For the chosen $2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ example, choose $A_{\rm ref}=120\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}\,K^{-2}}$. Then $y(1000\,\mathrm K)=-23.2090$ and $y(1200\,\mathrm K)=-19.3409$. Their slope is approximately $-23209\,\mathrm K$, giving $W=2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$. Two <temperatures> determine a line algebraically; several <temperatures> allow curvature and drift to be detected. These values are calculated, rather than experimental observations.

\Image[thermionic-emission-temperature-richardson-plot.png]
{title=One chosen emission model as a temperature curve and a dimensionless Richardson plot}
{description=The marked values are calculations with constant $W=2.0\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $A=A_{\rm ref}=120\,\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}\,K^{-2}}$. They illustrate the <Richardson-Dushman law>, rather than a particular material or a historical measurement.}

A straight trace alone does not establish a unique surface <work function>. <Adsorption>, changing <surface coverage>, or a mixture of regions with different barriers can change the inferred slope. For independent emitting patches with area fractions $f_i$, constant $A_i$ and barriers $W_i$,
$$
J=T^2\sum_i f_i A_i e^{-W_i/(k_BT)},\qquad \sum_i f_i=1.
$$
Lower barriers contribute disproportionately. Even with fixed patches, the slope of the <Richardson plot> gives a <temperature>-dependent weighted mean of the $W_i$, rather than their simple area average. If $A$ or $W$ themselves vary with <temperature>, the apparent slope-derived barrier becomes
$$
W_{\rm app}=-k_B\frac{dy}{d(1/T)}
=W-T\frac{dW}{dT}+k_BT^2\frac{d\ln A}{dT}.
$$
Check collection, <temperature> calibration, and surface stability before assigning curvature to a new emission mechanism.

= Thermionic tail-flux derivation
{parent=Richardson-Dushman law}
{title2=$T^{3/2}\times T^{1/2}=T^2$}

Consider a planar surface with outward normal along $z$. For an auxiliary equilibrium <electron gas> just outside it, let $n_v$ be the total <number density>, counting both signs of $v_z$. Its <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> has normal-component density
$$
f_z(v_z)=\sqrt{\frac{m_e}{2\pi k_BT}}
e^{-m_ev_z^2/(2k_BT)}.
$$
An <electron> with $v_z>0$ crosses more area per unit time when it moves faster. The outgoing number flux therefore uses $v_z f_z$, rather than just the probability of $v_z>0$:
$$
\Gamma=n_v\int_0^\infty v_zf_z(v_z)\,dv_z
=n_v\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m_e}}.
$$
This integral contributes only $T^{1/2}$. Holding a classical bulk <number density> fixed and imposing a normal <kinetic energy> threshold $W$ would instead give $\Gamma=n\sqrt{k_BT/(2\pi m_e)}e^{-W/(k_BT)}$; that argument alone does not produce $T^2$.

For the ideal surface model, measure external <electron> energies from the metal's <chemical potential> $\mu$. A freely propagating external state has $E-\mu=W+p^2/(2m_e)$. When $W\gg k_BT$, its <Fermi-Dirac distribution> reduces to the <Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics> tail. Counting two <spin> states per <phase space> cell of volume $h^3$ gives
$$
n_v=\frac{2}{h^3}\int_{\mathbb R^3}
e^{-[W+p^2/(2m_e)]/(k_BT)}\,d^3p
=2\left(\frac{2\pi m_e k_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
e^{-W/(k_BT)}.
$$
Each of the three <Gaussian integrals> supplies $T^{1/2}$. Combining this $T^{3/2}$ state count with the $T^{1/2}$ crossing factor gives
$$
J=e\Gamma
=\frac{4\pi m_e e k_B^2}{h^3}\,T^2e^{-W/(k_BT)}.
$$
The auxiliary atmosphere supplies an equilibrium state-counting argument; an operating collector removes the outgoing <electrons>. It need not maintain a real equilibrium gas throughout the gap. Unit transmission and the ideal free <electron> state count are substantive assumptions, so this calculation does not determine an arbitrary real surface's measured $A$.

In particular, <electrons> inside a <metal> generally form a degenerate <Fermi gas>. A classical distribution for the escaping tail does not imply a classical distribution for the whole interior. Richardson himself emphasized this distinction in his lecture.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/Thermionic Phenomena and the Laws which Govern Them by Owen Willans Richardson>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; pp. 230–231, internal versus external electron statistics]{ref}

= Emission-limited thermionic current
{parent=Thermionic emission}

An <emission-limited thermionic current> is set by the rate at which the heated surface supplies <electrons>, with nearly complete collection. At fixed <temperature>, increasing the attracting <electric potential difference> then produces an approximate collection plateau. That plateau is the useful regime for fitting the <Richardson-Dushman law>, provided the <electric field> has not significantly lowered the surface barrier.

The emitting surface still matters in this regime. <Adsorption> changes the <work function>; different crystal faces can have different barriers. Heating may desorb contaminants or change a coating, so repeated measurements at the same <temperature> need not agree unless the surface preparation is controlled. For fixed $A,T$, a barrier change $\Delta W$ multiplies $J$ by $e^{-\Delta W/(k_BT)}$. In the chosen model at $1000\,\mathrm K$, raising the barrier by $0.10\,\mathrm{eV}$ reduces $J$ to about 0.313 of its previous value.

= Space-charge-limited thermionic current
{parent=Thermionic emission}

A <space-charge-limited thermionic current> occurs when the negative <space charge> of emitted <electrons> creates an <electric field> that obstructs further transport across the gap. Raising the emitter <temperature> may then increase the surface supply without producing the corresponding collector <electric current>. The gap geometry and collecting <electric potential difference> matter even when the surface is stable.

To distinguish this from an <emission-limited thermionic current>, vary the collecting <electric potential difference> at fixed emitter <temperature>, then repeat at another <temperature>. A voltage-dependent transport region followed by a collection plateau separates a gap constraint from a supply constraint. A weak collector <electric current> alone cannot establish a high <work function>. Nor does simply choosing a very large collecting <electric potential difference> guarantee a valid zero-field measurement: the surface barrier may then change.

= Richardson hot-wire emission experiment
{parent=Thermionic emission}
{c}

In his Nobel lecture, delivered on 12 December 1929 for the 1928 prize, <Owen Willans Richardson> recalled heating wires for weeks while hand-pumping away gas released by the apparatus. With <platinum>, he found a finite emission per unit area that rose rapidly with <temperature>. His initial 1901 formula used $T^{1/2}$; he preferred the $T^2$ form in 1911.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/Thermionic Phenomena and the Laws which Govern Them by Owen Willans Richardson>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; pp. 224–225 and 229, preparation and chronology]{ref}

The early apparatus surrounded a heated <platinum> wire with a metal cylinder connected to earth through a <galvanometer>. Wire <electrical resistance> supplied the <temperature> readout. After checking collection saturation, Richardson measured at a wire potential of $-120\,\mathrm V$, over roughly $1300$–$1600\,\mathrm K$; reversing polarity suppressed the negative discharge.<Histories of the Electron: The Birth of Microphysics edited by Jed Z. Buchwald and Andrew Warwick/O. W. Richardson and the Electron Theory of Matter, 1901–1916 by Ole Knudsen>[Histories of the Electron: The Birth of Microphysics edited by Jed Z. Buchwald and Andrew Warwick; pp. 229–231, resistance thermometry and Fig. 7.2, reproducing the 1903 apparatus]{ref}

The inference rests on several controls. A <temperature> sweep probes thermal escape; reversing the collecting <electric field> tests the sign of the mobile <electric charge>; varying collection voltage tests whether transport is limiting. Prolonged pumping and heating test whether a transient gas release is responsible. These controls address different alternatives, and none by itself establishes a clean, uniform surface.

For a modern repetition, stabilize each <temperature>, measure the collector <electric current> against collecting <electric potential difference>, and record the pressure and surface preparation. Repeat selected <temperatures> after cooling to reveal drift. Use the collection plateau and the actual emitting area to construct a <Richardson plot>. The resulting slope estimates the <work function> only within the stated surface and transport assumptions; it does not turn an uncontrolled wire into a reference material.
