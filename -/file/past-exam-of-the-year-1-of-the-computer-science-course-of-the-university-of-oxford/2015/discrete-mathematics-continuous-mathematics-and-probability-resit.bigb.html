=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <image of a function> $f:A\to B$ is
$$
\boxed{f(A)=\{f(a):a\in A\}\subseteq B.}
$$
The <function> is <surjective>, or onto, exactly when every $b\in B$ equals $f(a)$ for at least one $a\in A$, equivalently $f(A)=B$. It is <injective>, or one-to-one, exactly when
$$
\boxed{\forall a,a'\in A,\quad f(a)=f(a')\Longrightarrow a=a'.}
$$
Thus <surjectivity> concerns whether the entire <codomain> is reached; <injectivity> concerns whether distinct inputs have distinct outputs.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $x^2+1\geq2|x|$, the <image of a function> is contained in $[-1/2,1/2]$. Both endpoints occur, at $x=-1$ and $x=1$. By <continuity> and the <intermediate value theorem> applied on $[-1,1]$, every intermediate value occurs too. Hence
$$
\boxed{f(\mathbb R)=[-1/2,1/2],\qquad f\text{ is neither onto nor one-to-one}.}
$$
It is not <surjective> onto $\mathbb R$ because, for example, $1$ is not attained. It is not <injective> because $f(2)=f(1/2)=2/5$ although $2\ne1/2$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $0\leq x<y$, then $\sqrt x<\sqrt y$ and $x<y$, so $\sqrt x+x<\sqrt y+y$. Thus the <function> is <strictly increasing> on its <domain> and is <injective>. Its values are nonnegative, so it is not <surjective> onto $\mathbb R$:
$$
\boxed{\text{one-to-one, but not onto}.}
$$
If $\mathbb R_+$ means $(0,\infty)$ its <image of a function> is $(0,\infty)$; if it includes zero its <image of a function> is $[0,\infty)$. Indeed, for any $u>0$, set $t=(\sqrt{1+4u}-1)/2>0$ and $x=t^2$; then $\sqrt x+x=t+t^2=u$. This convention does not change the classification.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

With the convention $\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, the even inputs $2j$ map to $j$ for $j\geq0$, while the odd inputs $2j+1$ map to $-(j+1)$ for $j\geq0$. These two <images of a function> are disjoint and together equal $\mathbb Z$. Within either class there are no repeated outputs. More explicitly the <inverse function> is
$$
\boxed{f^{-1}(z)=\begin{cases}2z,&z\geq0,\\-2z-1,&z<0.\end{cases}}
$$
Consequently $f$ is both <injective> and <surjective>, hence a <bijection>. If instead $\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$, the input zero is absent: $f$ remains <injective> but its <image of a function> is $\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$, so it is not onto. The printed paper does not specify this convention; the zero-based convention gives the apparent intended <bijection>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take every <complement of a set> relative to $\mathcal U$. By <set difference>, <De Morgan's laws>, and distribution of <set intersection> over <set union>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\setminus(B\cap A\cap C)
&=A\cap(B^c\cup A^c\cup C^c)\\
&=(A\cap B^c)\cup(A\cap A^c)\cup(A\cap C^c)\\
&=A\cap(B^c\cup C^c)\\
&=\boxed{A\setminus(B\cap C)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the middle term is the <empty set>, since a <set> and its <complement of a set> are disjoint.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <set difference>, associativity of <set intersection>, and <De Morgan's laws>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\setminus B)\setminus C
&=(A\cap B^c)\cap C^c\\
&=A\cap(B^c\cap C^c)\\
&=A\cap(B\cup C)^c\\
&=\boxed{A\setminus(B\cup C)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The commutativity and associativity of <set intersection> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\setminus B)\setminus C
&=A\cap B^c\cap C^c\\
&=A\cap C^c\cap B^c\\
&=\boxed{(A\setminus C)\setminus B}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equivalently, part (ii) reduces both sides to removal of the same <set union> $B\cup C=C\cup B$.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For every <set> $S$, the definition of a <power set> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
S\in\mathcal P(A\cap B)
&\Longleftrightarrow S\subseteq A\cap B\\
&\Longleftrightarrow (S\subseteq A\text{ and }S\subseteq B)\\
&\Longleftrightarrow S\in\mathcal P(A)\cap\mathcal P(B).
\end{aligned}
$$
Equality by identical membership therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\mathcal P(A\cap B)=\mathcal P(A)\cap\mathcal P(B).}
$$
The essential observation is that being a <subset> of a <set intersection> means being a <subset> of both factors.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

First suppose the <sets> are finite, with <cardinalities> $a=|A|\geq2$ and $b=|B|\geq2$. The <power set> and <Cartesian product> counting rules give
$$
|\mathcal P(A\times B)|=2^{ab},\qquad
|\mathcal P(A)\times\mathcal P(B)|=2^{a+b}.
$$
Since $ab-a-b=(a-1)(b-1)-1\geq0$, the desired inequality follows.

The <power-set product cardinality inequality> also has an <injection> proof that does not assume finiteness. Choose distinct $a_0,a_1\in A$ and distinct $b_0,b_1\in B$. For $S\subseteq A$ and $T\subseteq B$, define the <subset> of $A\times B$
$$
\Phi(S,T)=(S\times\{b_0\})\cup(\{a_0\}\times(T\setminus\{b_0\}))\cup E_T,
\qquad
E_T=\begin{cases}\{(a_1,b_1)\},&b_0\in T,\\\varnothing,&b_0\notin T.\end{cases}
$$
These three encoding regions are disjoint. The row with second coordinate $b_0$ recovers $S$; the column with first coordinate $a_0$ and second coordinate different from $b_0$ recovers $T\setminus\{b_0\}$; the extra position $(a_1,b_1)$ records whether $b_0\in T$. Thus $\Phi$ is <injective>, proving for arbitrary <sets>
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal P(A\times B)|\geq|\mathcal P(A)\times\mathcal P(B)|.}
$$
Encoding by $S\times T$ alone would fail: all pairs with either factor empty would have the same output.

===== Solution

Let $x_i$ be the number of objects in box $i$. The arrangements correspond exactly to nonnegative <integer> solutions of $x_1+\cdots+x_k=n$. By <stars and bars>, place $n$ stars and $k-1$ separating bars in a row. The number of stars between consecutive bars records the occupancy of each distinguishable box, with adjacent bars allowing an empty box. Choosing the bar positions gives
$$
\boxed{\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}}\qquad(k\geq1).
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put one object into each box first. The remaining $n-k$ objects can be distributed without restrictions, so <stars and bars> gives
$$
\boxed{\binom{(n-k)+k-1}{k-1}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}.}
$$
Equivalently, the substitution $u_i=x_i-1$ gives a <bijection> between positive <integer> solutions of $\sum_i x_i=n$ and nonnegative <integer> solutions of $\sum_i u_i=n-k$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <mathematical induction>. For $n=1$, the left side is $2^0=1=2^1-1$. If the identity holds at $n$, then
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n}2^k
=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^k\right)+2^n
=(2^n-1)+2^n
=2^{n+1}-1.
$$
The base case and induction step establish the <geometric series> identity
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}2^k=2^n-1\quad(n\geq1).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the base case $n=2$, $1+1/\sqrt2>\sqrt2$, since multiplying by $\sqrt2$ reduces this to $\sqrt2+1>2$. Suppose the claimed inequality holds for some $n\geq2$. The identity
$$
\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}<\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}}
$$
then gives
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\frac1{\sqrt k}
>\sqrt n+\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}}
>\sqrt{n+1}.
$$
Thus <mathematical induction> proves
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n<\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{\sqrt k}\quad(n\geq2).}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The initial values of the two <sequences> agree: $c_0=d_0=1$ and $c_1=-1+c_0=0=d_1$. For $n\geq2$, use the first-order <recurrence relation> at $n$ and $n-1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_n-(n-1)(c_{n-1}+c_{n-2})
&=(-1)^n+c_{n-1}-(n-1)c_{n-2}\\
&=(-1)^n+(-1)^{n-1}=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore $c_n$ satisfies exactly the same <derangement recurrence> as $d_n$. By <strong induction>, equality of the preceding two terms implies equality of the next term. Consequently
$$
\boxed{c_n=d_n\quad\text{for every }n\geq0.}
$$
The first-order <recurrence relation> therefore also counts <derangements>.

=== d
{scope}

For a <binary relation> $R\subseteq A\times A$, <reflexivity> means $aRa$ for every $a\in A$; a <symmetric relation> satisfies $aRb\Rightarrow bRa$ for every $a,b\in A$; an <antisymmetric relation> satisfies $(aRb\text{ and }bRa)\Rightarrow a=b$ for every $a,b\in A$; <transitivity> means $(aRb\text{ and }bRc)\Rightarrow aRc$ for every $a,b,c\in A$; an <irreflexive relation> satisfies $\neg(aRa)$ for every $a\in A$. In particular, failure of <reflexivity> means at least one missing diagonal pair, whereas <irreflexivity> requires every diagonal pair to be missing.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

There are $n^2$ possible ordered pairs and hence $2^{n^2}$ <binary relations>. A <reflexive relation> must contain the $n$ diagonal pairs, while its remaining $n^2-n$ pairs are unrestricted. Subtracting the <reflexive relations> gives
$$
\boxed{2^{n^2}-2^{n^2-n}}
$$
<binary relations> that are not <reflexive>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <symmetric relation> has $n$ independent choices on the diagonal. For each of the $\binom n2$ unordered pairs $\{a,b\}$ of distinct elements, either both ordered pairs are included or neither is. Thus there are $2^{n+\binom n2}$ <symmetric relations>, so the requested number is
$$
\boxed{2^{n^2}-2^{n+\binom n2}=2^{n^2}-2^{n(n+1)/2}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For each unordered pair $\{a,b\}$ with $a\ne b$, <antisymmetry> allows three choices: neither direction, only $(a,b)$, or only $(b,a)$. This contributes $3^{\binom n2}$. The diagonal is unrestricted by <antisymmetry>, but being not <irreflexive> requires a nonempty <subset> of the $n$ diagonal pairs. There are $2^n-1$ such choices. Hence
$$
\boxed{(2^n-1)3^{\binom n2}.}
$$
This allows some diagonal pairs to be absent; “not <irreflexive>” does not require a <reflexive relation>.

===== Solution

Because $T$ is positive, the definition of <big O notation> becomes
$$
\boxed{\exists C>0\ \exists n_0\in\mathbb N_+\ \forall n\geq n_0:\quad T(n)\leq Cn^2.}
$$
Here $C$ and $n_0$ are fixed independently of $n$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

We prove the stronger bound $T(n)\leq2n^2$ for every $n\geq1$ by <strong induction>. The initial case is $T(1)=1\leq2$. For $n\geq2$, put $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Then $1\leq m<n$, so the induction hypothesis and the defining <recurrence relation> give
$$
T(n)=2T(m)+n^2\leq4m^2+n^2\leq4(n/2)^2+n^2=2n^2.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{T(n)\leq2n^2\ (n\geq1),\qquad T(n)=O(n^2).}
$$
The <recurrence relation> must be read as applying for $n\geq2$; applying it at $n=1$ would invoke $T(0)$ outside the printed <domain>. No value of $T(0)$ is needed.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

By <modular arithmetic>, $a_1-b_1=nu$ and $a_2-b_2=nv$ for some <integers> $u,v$. Adding gives
$$
(a_1+a_2)-(b_1+b_2)=n(u+v).
$$
Since $u+v$ is an <integer>, this proves
$$
\boxed{a_1+a_2\equiv b_1+b_2\pmod n.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Using $a_i=b_i+nu_i$ with <integers> $u_i$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_1a_2-b_1b_2
&=(b_1+nu_1)(b_2+nu_2)-b_1b_2\\
&=n(b_1u_2+b_2u_1+nu_1u_2).
\end{aligned}
$$
The quantity in parentheses is an <integer>, so the <divisibility> criterion for <modular arithmetic> gives
$$
\boxed{a_1a_2\equiv b_1b_2\pmod n.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

By <Euclidean division>, write $a=qn+r$ and $b=sn+t$, with <integers> $q,s$ and $0\leq r,t<n$. The hypothesis is $a\equiv b\pmod n$. Apply the addition rule from part (b)(i) with $-qn\equiv-sn\equiv0\pmod n$ to obtain
$$
r=a-qn\equiv b-sn=t\pmod n.
$$
Therefore $n\mid r-t$. But $-n<r-t<n$, and the only multiple of $n$ in that interval is zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{r=t,}
$$
so the two <remainders> agree.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The decimal expansion is $a=\sum_{i=0}^m a_i10^i$. Since $10\equiv1\pmod3$, repeated use of the multiplication rule in part (b)(ii) gives $10^i\equiv1\pmod3$ for every $i\geq0$. Repeated use of the addition rule in part (b)(i) now gives
$$
a=\sum_{i=0}^m a_i10^i\equiv\sum_{i=0}^m a_i\pmod3.
$$
If $3\mid a$, the left side is congruent to zero, whence
$$
\boxed{3\mid\sum_{i=0}^m a_i.}
$$
This is the <decimal digit-sum divisibility test> for three, derived directly from <modular arithmetic>.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $k=\lfloor x\rfloor$. The definition of the <floor function> gives $k\leq x<k+1$. Adding the <integer> $m$ yields
$$
k+m\leq x+m<k+m+1.
$$
Since $k+m$ is an <integer>, it is exactly the <floor> of $x+m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lfloor x+m\rfloor=\lfloor x\rfloor+m.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $k=\lfloor y^{1/n}\rfloor$. Because $y>0$, $k$ is a nonnegative <integer>, and
$$
k\leq y^{1/n}<k+1.
$$
The <function> $t\mapsto t^n$ is <strictly increasing> for $t\geq0$, so $k^n\leq y<(k+1)^n$. Both powers are <integers>. Taking the <floor function> therefore gives
$$
k^n\leq\lfloor y\rfloor<(k+1)^n.
$$
Taking nonnegative $n$th roots now gives $k\leq\lfloor y\rfloor^{1/n}<k+1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\left\lfloor\lfloor y\rfloor^{1/n}\right\rfloor=k=\left\lfloor y^{1/n}\right\rfloor.}
$$
The argument also covers $0<y<1$, when both outer <floors> are zero.

===== Solution

By the <fundamental theorem of calculus>,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1 3x^2\,dx=[x^3]_0^1=1.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <composite trapezoidal rule>, the mesh width is $h=1/N$ and the nodes are $x_n=n/N$. The endpoint weights are halves and the interior weights are ones, so
$$
\boxed{T_N=\frac1N\left[\frac{3(0)^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}3\left(\frac nN\right)^2+\frac{3(1)^2}{2}\right]
=\frac3{N^3}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n^2+\frac3{2N}.}
$$
This formula is an approximation to the <definite integral>, not an exact equality with it for finite $N$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $S_N=N^{-3}\sum_{n=1}^N n^2$. The expression in part (ii) gives
$$
T_N=3\left(S_N-\frac1N\right)+\frac3{2N}=3S_N-\frac3{2N}.
$$
Because $3x^2$ is <continuous> on $[0,1]$, the <composite trapezoidal rule> converges to its <definite integral>, which is $1$ by part (i). In fact its standard <composite trapezoidal error bound> is $|T_N-1|\leq1/(2N^2)$, since $f''=6$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{T_N}{3}+\frac1{2N}\right)=\frac13.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Taking the <partial derivatives> of the <parabolic coordinates> transformation, holding the other coordinate fixed each time, gives
$$
\boxed{x_s=t,\qquad x_t=s,\qquad y_s=-s,\qquad y_t=t.}
$$
Thus its <Jacobian matrix> is $J=\begin{pmatrix}t&s\\-s&t\end{pmatrix}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <determinant> of the <Jacobian matrix> is $s^2+t^2$. Wherever $(s,t)\ne(0,0)$, the <inverse function theorem> permits a local inverse of the <parabolic coordinates> transformation, and
$$
\frac{\partial(s,t)}{\partial(x,y)}
=J^{-1}
=\frac1{s^2+t^2}\begin{pmatrix}t&-s\\s&t\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Reading off its entries gives
$$
\boxed{s_x=\frac t{s^2+t^2},\quad s_y=-\frac s{s^2+t^2},\quad
 t_x=\frac s{s^2+t^2},\quad t_y=\frac t{s^2+t^2}.}
$$
These are local formulas on a chosen branch: $(s,t)$ and $(-s,-t)$ have the same image, and at the origin the <Jacobian matrix> is singular, so this inverse-derivative formula does not apply there.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the multivariable <chain rule> to a differentiable <function> $f(s,t)$ on the chosen local branch of the <parabolic coordinates>:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
=\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\frac{\partial t}{\partial x}.
$$
Substituting part (ii) gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{t f_s+s f_t}{s^2+t^2},\qquad(s,t)\ne(0,0).}
$$

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $y=1+x^2$, the <derivatives> are $y'=2x$ and $y''=2$. Its endpoint values are $y(0)=1$ and $y(2)=5$, so it satisfies both the <ordinary differential equation> and the <boundary conditions>. Moreover any solution of $y''=2$ has the form $x^2+Ax+B$; the <boundary conditions> force $B=1$ and $A=0$. Thus the unique solution of the <boundary value problem> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=1+x^2.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The endpoint <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{y_0=1,\qquad y_N=5.}
$$
The <finite difference> equation can be written $y_{n+1}-2y_n+y_{n-1}=2h^2$. Set $d_n=y_n-y_{n-1}$. Then $d_{n+1}-d_n=2h^2$, so $d_n=d_1+2h^2(n-1)$. Summing from $1$ to $n$ gives
$$
y_n=1+nd_1+h^2n(n-1).
$$
Using $y_N=5$ and $h=2/N$ yields $Nd_1=4-h^2N(N-1)=4/N$, hence $d_1=4/N^2=h^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y_n=1+h^2n^2=1+\left(\frac{2n}{N}\right)^2=y(x_n),\qquad 0\leq n\leq N.}
$$
This also covers $N=1$, where there are no interior equations. Uniqueness follows because the difference of two solutions has zero second <finite difference>, hence is linear in $n$, and vanishes at both endpoints. This is an <exact finite difference solution for constant second derivative>: the method is exact at the nodes here because the centered second <finite difference> differentiates this quadratic <polynomial> exactly.

==== i

===== Solution

The <characteristic equation> of the constant-coefficient <linear ordinary differential equation> is
$$
4r^2+8r+5=0,
$$
whose <roots of a polynomial> are $r=-1\pm i/2$. Taking real and imaginary parts of the corresponding <exponential functions> gives the real general solution
$$
\boxed{y_h(x)=e^{-x}\left(A\cos\frac x2+B\sin\frac x2\right),\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <particular solution>, try $y_p=a\sin x+b\cos x$. Substitution gives
$$
4y_p''+8y_p'+5y_p=(a-8b)\sin x+(8a+b)\cos x.
$$
Matching coefficients requires $a-8b=1$ and $8a+b=8$, so $a=1$ and $b=0$. Adding the <homogeneous solution> from part (i), the general solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sin x+e^{-x}\left(A\cos\frac x2+B\sin\frac x2\right).}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $a=\pi/2$. Substituting $x=-a$ and $x=a$ into part (ii), using $\cos(\pi/4)=\sin(\pi/4)=1/\sqrt2$, gives
$$
-1+\frac{e^a}{\sqrt2}(A-B)=e^a,
\qquad
1+\frac{e^{-a}}{\sqrt2}(A+B)=e^{-a}.
$$
Thus $A-B=\sqrt2(1+e^{-a})$ and $A+B=\sqrt2(1-e^a)$. Solving these two <linear equations> gives
$$
A=\sqrt2(1-\sinh a),\qquad B=-\sqrt2\cosh a.
$$
Consequently the solution of the <boundary value problem> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sin x+\sqrt2 e^{-x}\left[\left(1-\sinh\frac\pi2\right)\cos\frac x2-\cosh\frac\pi2\sin\frac x2\right].}
$$
The two independent <boundary conditions> determine $A,B$ uniquely.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $x>-1$, divide by $x+1$ to obtain the first-order <linear ordinary differential equation>
$$
y'-\frac3{x+1}y=(x+1)^4.
$$
An <integrating factor> is $\mu(x)=e^{-3\log(x+1)}=(x+1)^{-3}$. Multiplication by this factor gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x+1)^{-3}y\right]=x+1.
$$
After <integration>, $(x+1)^{-3}y=\tfrac12(x+1)^2+C$, so
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac12(x+1)^5+C(x+1)^3,\qquad C\in\mathbb R,\quad x>-1.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $g_1=x^2+y^2+z^2-4$ and $g_2=(x-3)^2+y^2+z^2-4$. Subtracting the two constraint equations gives $-6x+9=0$, hence $x=3/2$ and $y^2+z^2=7/4$. The feasible <set> is therefore a <circle>.

For <Lagrange multipliers> $\lambda,\mu$, the stationarity equation $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g_1+\mu\nabla g_2$ becomes
$$
0=2\lambda x+2\mu(x-3),\qquad
1=2(\lambda+\mu)y,\qquad
0=2(\lambda+\mu)z.
$$
At $x=3/2$, the first equation gives $\lambda=\mu$. The second ensures $\lambda+\mu\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, so the third gives $z=0$. The constraints then force $y=\pm\sqrt7/2$. Both points do satisfy the multiplier equations, taking $\lambda=\mu=1/(4y)$.

There are no missing singular constraint points: on the feasible <circle>, the two constraint <gradients> $(3,2y,2z)$ and $(-3,2y,2z)$ are <linearly independent>, since $y^2+z^2=7/4>0$. Thus the complete list of constrained <critical points> and <critical values> is
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\left(\frac32,\pm\frac{\sqrt7}{2},0\right),\qquad f=\pm\frac{\sqrt7}{2}.}
$$
The bound $|y|\leq\sqrt7/2$ on the feasible <circle> shows that the positive value is the global <maximum> and the negative value is the global <minimum>.

{scope}

===== Solution

A <function> $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is <periodic> with period $\alpha>0$ when
$$
\boxed{f(x+\alpha)=f(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The period need not be the smallest positive one; for example, every positive <integer> multiple of a period is also a period.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the period-two <Fourier series> convention
$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl[a_n\cos(n\pi x)+b_n\sin(n\pi x)\bigr].
$$
Away from its jumps this <square wave> is an <odd function>, so $a_0=a_n=0$. Its <Fourier sine coefficients> are
$$
b_n=\int_{-1}^1f(x)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx
=2\int_0^1\sin(n\pi x)\,dx
=\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}.
$$
Only odd <Fourier harmonics> remain. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f(x)\sim\frac4\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2j+1)\pi x)}{2j+1}.}
$$
The <Fourier series> equals $f(x)$ away from the jumps; at a jump it gives the midpoint of the one-sided limits by <Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series>. The printed definition leaves the values at the integers unspecified. Choosing finite values there consistent with period two changes neither the <Fourier coefficients> nor these limiting values.

\Image[discrete-resit-2015-square-wave.png]
{title=Odd Fourier harmonics approximate the period-two square wave and vanish at every jump}

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The left and right limits at zero are $-1$ and $1$. By <Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series>, the <Fourier series> converges to
$$
\boxed{\frac{f(0^-)+f(0^+)}2=\frac{-1+1}{2}=0.}
$$
Indeed every term $\sin((2j+1)\pi x)$ is zero at $x=0$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

At $x=1$, the left limit is $1$. The right limit is $-1$ because of the period-two extension. Therefore <Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier series> gives the <Fourier series> limit
$$
\boxed{\frac{f(1^-)+f(1^+)}2=\frac{1-1}{2}=0.}
$$
Every <Fourier partial sum> is also exactly zero there because $\sin((2j+1)\pi)=0$.

==== i

===== Solution

The <derivative> factors as
$$
g'(x)=4x^3-24x^2+36x=4x(x-3)^2.
$$
The <polynomial> is differentiable everywhere, so its <critical points> occur precisely where this <derivative> vanishes. Hence
$$
\boxed{x=0\quad\text{and}\quad x=3.}
$$
Their values are $g(0)=5$ and $g(3)=32$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The relevant <derivatives> at $x=3$ are
$$
g(3)=32,\qquad g'(3)=0,\qquad
g''(3)=0,\qquad g'''(3)=24.
$$
Thus the cubic <Taylor polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{T_3(x)=32+4(x-3)^3.}
$$
In fact the exact expansion is $g(x)=32+4(x-3)^3+(x-3)^4$, so the omitted term has order $O((x-3)^4)$ in <big O notation>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

At $x=0$, $g''(0)=36>0$, so the <second derivative test> gives a strict local <minimum>. In fact $g'(x)=4x(x-3)^2$ is negative for $x<0$ and positive for $x>0$ except at $x=3$, so $g(0)=5$ is the global <minimum> as well.

At $x=3$, the <second derivative test> is inconclusive because $g''(3)=0$. But $g''(x)=12(x-1)(x-3)$ changes from negative to positive across $3$, while $g'(3)=0$. Hence this is a <stationary inflection point>. Equivalently, the leading nonconstant term $4(x-3)^3$ in the <Taylor polynomial> changes sign across $3$. The paper explicitly uses “saddle point” to mean an <inflection point>, so in its terminology the classification is
$$
\boxed{x=0:\text{ minimum};\qquad x=3:\text{ saddle point};\qquad\text{no maxima}.}
$$
Here the “saddle point” is a one-dimensional <stationary inflection point>, rather than the usual multivariable use of <saddle point of a scalar function>.<Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability resit examination by the University of Oxford (2015)>[Page 8, Question 6(b)(iii), explicit definition of “saddle point”]{ref}

==== i

===== Solution

For <events> $A,B$ with $\mathbb P(B)>0$, the <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)}.}
$$
This renormalizes the <probability> of $A$ within $B$. The elementary ratio definition does not assign a value when $\mathbb P(B)=0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Since the $B_i$ form a <partition of a set> of the <sample space>, the <events> $A\cap B_i$ are pairwise disjoint and their <set union> is $A$. By <countable additivity> of <probability>, followed by the definition of <conditional probability>,
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(A\cap B_i)
=\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i)}.
$$
Every ratio is legitimate because each $\mathbb P(B_i)>0$. This proves the countable <law of total probability>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

To obtain the $(k+1)$st head on toss $n+1$, the first $n$ tosses must contain exactly $k$ heads, and the last toss must be a head. There are $\binom nk$ choices of the first $k$ head positions. By <independence>, each such arrangement followed by a head has <probability> $r^k(1-r)^{n-k}r$. The arrangements are disjoint, so
$$
\boxed{h_{n,k}(r)=\binom nk(1-r)^{n-k}r^{k+1},\qquad n\geq k\geq0.}
$$
This is the <negative binomial stopping argument>, with $n+1$ counting all tosses up to and including the $(k+1)$st success. It is not merely the <probability> of $k+1$ heads somewhere among $n+1$ tosses: the last toss is prescribed to be a head.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $D=1-(1-p)(1-q)=p+q-pq$. By the <law of total probability>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(Y=k)
&=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}p(1-p)^n\binom nkq^k(1-q)^{n-k}\\
&=p[(1-p)q]^k\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\binom nk[(1-p)(1-q)]^{n-k}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For $0\leq t<1$, termwise differentiation of the <geometric series>, valid inside its <radius of convergence>, gives
$$
\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\binom nk t^{n-k}
=\frac1{k!}\frac{d^k}{dt^k}\frac1{1-t}
=\frac1{(1-t)^{k+1}}.
$$
Taking $t=(1-p)(1-q)$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y=k)=\frac pD\left(\frac{(1-p)q}{D}\right)^k,\qquad k\geq0.}
$$
Since $D-p=(1-p)q$, this is the zero-based <geometric distribution> with parameter
$$
\boxed{p_* =\frac{p}{p+q-pq},\qquad \mathbb P(Y=k)=p_*(1-p_*)^k.}
$$
This illustrates <Bernoulli thinning of a geometric count>. Here $0<p_*<1$, as $0<p,q<1$.

For the reverse <conditional distribution>, <Bayes' theorem> gives, for $n\geq k$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X=n\mid Y=k)
&=\frac{p(1-p)^n\binom nkq^k(1-q)^{n-k}}
{p[(1-p)q]^k/D^{k+1}}\\
&=\binom nk[(1-p)(1-q)]^{n-k}D^{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=n\mid Y=k)=h_{n,k}(r),\qquad r=D=p+q-pq.}
$$
For $n<k$ the <conditional probability> is zero. The <conditional distribution> of $X-k$ is the failure-count form of a <negative binomial distribution>, with $k+1$ successes and success parameter $r$: the <geometric-binomial posterior is shifted negative binomial>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <probability generating function> of a nonnegative <integer>-valued <random variable> is
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_X(k)s^k,}
$$
using $s^0=1$ also at $s=0$. It is always defined for $0\leq s\leq1$, and indeed the <power series> converges absolutely for all real or complex $s$ with $|s|\leq1$, because
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_X(k)|s|^k\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_X(k)=1.
$$
It may be defined beyond the unit disk where this <power series> converges absolutely; such a larger <domain> depends on the <distribution>. In particular $G_X(0)=\mathbb P(X=0)$ and $G_X(1)=1$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$. By <independence>, the <expectation of a product of independent random variables> factors, and the identical <distributions> give
$$
\boxed{G_{S_n}(s)=\mathbb E\left[\prod_{i=1}^n s^{X_i}\right]
=\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[s^{X_i}]=[G_X(s)]^n.}
$$
This is valid for $|s|\leq1$ and wherever the required absolute <expectations> exist. It is the <probability generating function of an independent sum>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq s\leq1$, condition on $N=n$. Its <independence> from the $X_i$ preserves their independent common <distribution>, so part (ii) gives
$$
\mathbb E[s^Y\mid N=n]=[G_X(s)]^n.
$$
For $n=0$, this is $1$, agreeing with $Y=0$. By the <law of total expectation>,
$$
G_Y(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb P(N=n)[G_X(s)]^n
=\boxed{G_N(G_X(s))}.
$$
This proves the <probability generating function of an independent random sum>. For nonnegative $s$ outside $[0,1]$, the same argument is valid whenever the right side is finite, by <Tonelli's theorem> for nonnegative sums. For signed or complex $s$, a sufficient absolute-convergence condition is $G_N(G_X(|s|))<\infty$; it holds automatically on $|s|\leq1$. Outside this domain, mere evaluation of an analytically extended expression is not a substitute for existence of the <probability generating function>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the two masses of the <Bernoulli distribution>,
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=(1-p)s^0+ps=1-p+ps.}
$$
This <probability generating function> is a <polynomial>, so it exists for every finite $s$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $I_i$ indicate that student $i$ stays awake. These indicators have independent <Bernoulli distributions> with parameter $p$, and $W=\sum_{i=1}^M I_i$. The <Poisson distribution> has <probability generating function>
$$
G_M(u)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}u^m
=e^{\lambda(u-1)}.
$$
The <probability generating function of an independent random sum> now gives
$$
G_W(s)=G_M(1-p+ps)=e^{\lambda p(s-1)}.
$$
Recognizing the <Poisson distribution>,
$$
\boxed{W\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda p),\qquad
\mathbb P(W=w)=e^{-\lambda p}\frac{(\lambda p)^w}{w!},\quad w\geq0.}
$$
This is <Poisson thinning>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For each student, the <probability> of both falling asleep and dreaming about <mathematics> is $(1-p)q$. These indicators are independent across students, so the same <Poisson thinning> calculation gives, for the requested count $D$,
$$
\boxed{D\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(1-p)q).}
$$
Explicitly $G_D(s)=\exp(\lambda(1-p)q(s-1))$, the <probability generating function> of that <Poisson distribution>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Given $M=m$, there are exactly $m$ independent opportunities to stay awake, each with <probability> $p$. Hence
$$
\boxed{W\mid M=m\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(m,p).}
$$
The standard <binomial distribution> formulas are $\mathbb E[W\mid M]=pM$ and $\operatorname{var}(W\mid M)=Mp(1-p)$. The <Poisson distribution> formulas are $\mathbb E[M]=\lambda$, $\operatorname{var}(M)=\lambda$, and therefore $\mathbb E[M^2]=\lambda^2+\lambda$. By the <law of total expectation>,
$$
\mathbb E[WM]=\mathbb E\bigl[M\mathbb E[W\mid M]\bigr]
=p\mathbb E[M^2]=p(\lambda^2+\lambda),
\qquad \mathbb E[W]=p\lambda.
$$
Using the definition of <covariance>,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cov}(W,M)=\mathbb E[WM]-\mathbb E[W]\mathbb E[M]=p\lambda.}
$$
Thus $W$ and $M$ are not independent, even though the separate awake and asleep counts produced by <Poisson thinning> are independent.

===== Solution

A <probability density function> must be nonnegative and have <definite integral> one. Here
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=\alpha\int_0^2x^2\,dx=\frac{8\alpha}{3}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac38,\qquad f\text{ is a valid density}.}
$$
This value is positive, so the nonnegativity requirement also holds.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <cosine function> is positive on $(0,\pi/2)$ and negative on $(\pi/2,2)$. Thus any nonzero $\alpha$ makes $\alpha\cos x$ negative on an interval of positive length. Taking $\alpha=0$ makes the total <definite integral> zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{No real value of }\alpha\text{ gives a probability density.}}
$$
Normalization alone would suggest $\alpha=1/\sin2$, but that candidate still violates nonnegativity and therefore is not a <probability density function>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

We need $0<\alpha<2$. Indeed, if $\alpha<0$, the <function> is negative on $(\alpha,0)$; if $\alpha=0$, the <improper integral> diverges at zero; and if $\alpha\geq2$, its support interval is empty. For $0<\alpha<2$,
$$
\int_\alpha^2\frac{dx}{x}=\log\frac2\alpha.
$$
Setting this equal to one gives the unique admissible value
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac2e.}
$$
On this interval $1/x>0$, so both requirements for a <probability density function> are met.

==== i

===== Solution

Since $X$ is nonnegative, $F_X(x)=0$ for $x<0$. For $x\geq0$, use $\frac{d}{dt}e^{-t^2/2}=-te^{-t^2/2}$ to obtain
$$
F_X(x)=\int_0^xte^{-t^2/2}\,dt=1-e^{-x^2/2}.
$$
Thus the <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\1-e^{-x^2/2},&x\geq0.\end{cases}}
$$
In particular it tends to one as $x\to\infty$, confirming normalization of this unit-scale <Rayleigh distribution>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <cumulative distribution function> is <continuous> and <strictly increasing> for $x>0$, so the <median> is the unique positive $c$ satisfying $F_X(c)=1/2$. Consequently $e^{-c^2/2}=1/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{c=\sqrt{2\log2}.}
$$
There is no atom at $c$, so $\mathbb P(X>c)=1-F_X(c)=1/2$ as required.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $X\geq0$, the <function> $X\mapsto X^2$ is <strictly increasing> on its support. For $y\geq0$,
$$
F_Y(y)=\mathbb P(X^2\leq y)=F_X(\sqrt y)=1-e^{-y/2},
$$
and $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y<0$. Differentiating the <cumulative distribution function> gives
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12e^{-y/2},&y\geq0,\\0,&y<0.\end{cases}}
$$
The density value at the single point $y=0$ is immaterial. Hence $Y$ has an <exponential distribution> with rate $1/2$. Direct <integration by parts>, or its standard <expected value>, yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X^2]=\mathbb E[Y]=\int_0^\infty\frac y2e^{-y/2}\,dy=2.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

By the density formula for <expected value>,
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2/2}\,dx.
$$
For the standard <normal distribution>, the integrand in the second <moment> is even, so
$$
\mathbb E[Z^2]=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z^2e^{-z^2/2}\,dz
=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty z^2e^{-z^2/2}\,dz.
$$
Comparing these two <integrals> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,\mathbb E[Z^2],\qquad\beta=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}.}
$$

==== v

===== Solution

The standard <normal distribution> has <mean> zero and <variance> one, so $\mathbb E[Z^2]=\operatorname{var}(Z)+(\mathbb E[Z])^2=1$. Part (iv) therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}.}

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2=2-\frac\pi2=\frac{4-\pi}{2}.}
