== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <transition matrix> acts on a column <probability vector> in the order Central, Headington, Botley. Thus column $j$ records the destinations of buses starting at station $j$. The Central-to-Headington, Headington-to-Botley and Botley-to-Central entries give
$$
\boxed{A=B=C=0.1,\qquad P=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.1&0.1\\0.1&0.8&0.2\\0.3&0.1&0.7\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Each column sums to one, as required for this column convention for a <stochastic matrix>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a square <matrix> $M$, an <eigenvalue>–<eigenvector> pair consists of a <scalar> $\lambda$ and a **nonzero** <vector> $v$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{Mv=\lambda v,\qquad v\ne0.}
$$
The <linear map> represented by $M$ therefore scales $v$ by $\lambda$. The exclusion of the zero <vector> matters: otherwise every <scalar> would satisfy the equation trivially.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <matrix multiplication> to evolve the <probability vector> by $x_{n+1}=Px_n$. With $x_0=(0.4,0.4,0.2)^{\mathsf T}$,
$$
x_1=\begin{pmatrix}0.24+0.04+0.02\\0.04+0.32+0.04\\0.12+0.04+0.14\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.30\\0.40\\0.30\end{pmatrix},\qquad x_2=\begin{pmatrix}0.18+0.04+0.03\\0.03+0.32+0.06\\0.09+0.04+0.21\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.25\\0.41\\0.34\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Hence, in Central, Headington, Botley order,
$$
\boxed{\text{day 1: }(30\%,40\%,30\%),\qquad\text{day 2: }(25\%,41\%,34\%).}
$$
Both <probability vectors> have nonnegative entries summing to one.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Define the <characteristic polynomial> using $p(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-P)$, where $I$ is the <identity matrix>. Expanding the <determinant> along the first row gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\lambda)&=(\lambda-0.6)\bigl((\lambda-0.8)(\lambda-0.7)-0.02\bigr)\\
&\quad+0.1\bigl(-0.1(\lambda-0.7)-0.06\bigr)-0.1\bigl(0.01+0.3(\lambda-0.8)\bigr)\\
&=\lambda^3-2.1\lambda^2+1.4\lambda-0.3\\
&=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-0.6)(\lambda-0.5).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <characteristic equation> is
$$
\boxed{p(\lambda)=\lambda^3-\frac{21}{10}\lambda^2+\frac75\lambda-\frac3{10}=0.}
$$
The convention $\det(P-\lambda I)$ changes the sign of this cubic <polynomial> but gives the same <roots>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The factorization of the <characteristic polynomial> shows that $\lambda=1$ is an <eigenvalue>. To find its normalized <eigenvector>, solve $(P-I)v=0$ together with $v_C+v_H+v_B=1$. The first two independent <linear equations> are
$$
-4v_C+v_H+v_B=0,\qquad v_C-2v_H+2v_B=0.
$$
The first equation and normalization give $5v_C=1$. The second gives $v_H-v_B=v_C/2=1/10$, while $v_H+v_B=4/5$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{v=\begin{pmatrix}1/5\\9/20\\7/20\end{pmatrix},\qquad Pv=v.}
$$
All entries are positive and sum to one. This <probability vector> is the <stationary distribution>.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

The remaining <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda_2=\frac35,\qquad\lambda_3=\frac12.}
$$
They have absolute value less than one. The three distinct <eigenvalues> give a <basis> of <eigenvectors>, which may be chosen as $v$, $u=(0,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $w=(-1,-1,2)^{\mathsf T}$, with $Pu=(3/5)u$ and $Pw=(1/2)w$. Write an arbitrary initial <probability vector> as $x_0=cv+\alpha u+\beta w$. Summing its entries shows $c=1$, because the entries of $v$ sum to one and those of $u,w$ sum to zero. Therefore
$$
P^nx_0=v+\alpha\left(\frac35\right)^nu+\beta\left(\frac12\right)^nw\longrightarrow v.
$$
This proves <convergence> for every initial <probability vector>, rather than merely identifying a possible limit. In particular,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=(20\%,45\%,35\%)^{\mathsf T}.}
$$
For the given initial <probability vector>, $\alpha=1/4$ and $\beta=-1/5$; substituting $n=1,2$ also recovers the previous answers. The slowest decaying <eigenvector> component has factor $(3/5)^n$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an $m\times n$ <matrix> $A$ with real entries, its <row space> is the <linear span> of its rows, a <linear subspace> of $\mathbb R^n$. Its <column space> is the <linear span> of its columns, a <linear subspace> of $\mathbb R^m$ and the <image of a linear map> $x\mapsto Ax$. Its <null space> is
$$
\ker A=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:Ax=0\},
$$
a <linear subspace> of $\mathbb R^n$. Finally,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}A=\dim\operatorname{Col}A=\dim\operatorname{Row}A.}
$$
Thus the <rank of a matrix> counts the number of <linearly independent> columns, equivalently rows. The <nullity> is $\dim\ker A=n-\operatorname{rank}A$ by the <rank-nullity theorem>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Perform the <elementary row operations> $R_2\gets R_2-R_1$ and $R_3\gets R_3+R_1$, then $R_3\gets R_3-R_2$:
$$
A\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1&4\\0&0&5&-3\\0&0&5&-3\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&2&-1&4\\0&0&5&-3\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These operations preserve the <row space>. The two nonzero rows of this <row echelon form> are <linearly independent>: only the first has a nonzero first entry, and the second is nonzero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{a row-space basis is }\{(1,2,-1,4),(0,0,5,-3)\},\qquad\operatorname{rank}A=2.}
$$
Further <Gaussian elimination> gives the <reduced row echelon form>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&17/5\\0&0&1&-3/5\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},
$$
which will also identify the <free variables> in the <null space> calculation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <pivot columns> in the <row echelon form> are columns 1 and 3. Use those columns of the **original** <matrix> for a <basis> of the original <column space>:
$$
\boxed{\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-1\\4\\6\end{pmatrix}\right\}.}
$$
For a direct spanning check, column 2 is twice column 1 and column 4 is $(17/5)$ times column 1 minus $(3/5)$ times column 3. The displayed columns are <linearly independent>, since the first two coordinates of $\alpha c_1+\beta c_3=0$ give $\alpha-\beta=0$ and $\alpha+4\beta=0$, so $\alpha=\beta=0$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <reduced row echelon form> gives
$$
x_1=-2x_2-\frac{17}{5}x_4,\qquad x_3=\frac35x_4.
$$
Taking the <free variables> as $x_2=s$ and $x_4=5t$, the <null space> is
$$
\ker A=\left\{s\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-17\\0\\3\\5\end{pmatrix}:s,t\in\mathbb R\right\}.
$$
The two displayed <vectors> are <linearly independent>, as their second and fourth coordinates show, and each satisfies $Ax=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{a null-space basis is }\{(-2,1,0,0)^{\mathsf T},(-17,0,3,5)^{\mathsf T}\},\quad\dim\ker A=2.}
$$
This also agrees with the <rank-nullity theorem>: $4-2=2$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the same <elementary row operations> to the <augmented matrix>:
$$
\left(\begin{array}{rrrr|r}1&2&-1&4&2\\1&2&4&1&7\\-1&-2&6&-7&3\end{array}\right)\sim\left(\begin{array}{rrrr|r}1&2&-1&4&2\\0&0&5&-3&5\\0&0&0&0&0\end{array}\right).
$$
The last row establishes consistency; equivalently the third original row is the second minus twice the first, and the right-hand entries obey $3=7-2\cdot2$. With $x_2=s$ and $x_4=5t$, the second equation gives $x_3=1+3t$ and the first gives $x_1=3-2s-17t$. All real solutions are therefore
$$
\boxed{x=\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}+s\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}-17\\0\\3\\5\end{pmatrix},\qquad s,t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This <affine solution space> is one particular solution plus the <null space>. The two <free variables> show that there are no additional restrictions.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $U,V$ be $n\times n$ <upper triangular matrices>. For an entry below the diagonal, $i>j$, <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
(UV)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^nU_{ik}V_{kj}.
$$
If $k<i$, then $U_{ik}=0$. If $k\geq i$, then $k>j$, so $V_{kj}=0$. Every summand is zero, giving
$$
\boxed{i>j\implies(UV)_{ij}=0.}
$$
Thus $UV$ is an <upper triangular matrix>. Equivalently, a potentially nonzero summand would require $i\leq k\leq j$, impossible below the diagonal.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Suppose an <invertible matrix> has two <LU factorizations>, $A=L_1U_1=L_2U_2$, with $L_1,L_2$ <unit lower triangular matrices> and $U_1,U_2$ <upper triangular matrices>. All four factors are <invertible matrices>, since their square products are invertible. Multiplication by the appropriate <inverse matrices> gives
$$
L_2^{-1}L_1=U_2U_1^{-1}.
$$
By the supplied closure properties, the left side is a <unit lower triangular matrix> and the right side is an <upper triangular matrix>. A <matrix> of both types is diagonal; its diagonal entries are all one. Thus both sides are the <identity matrix>, whence
$$
\boxed{L_1=L_2,\qquad U_1=U_2.}
$$
**Qualification of the printed wording:** this establishes uniqueness **if the specified <LU factorization> exists**. Invertibility alone does not guarantee an unpivoted <LU factorization>. For example $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is invertible, but in a product $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\ell&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}\\0&u_{22}\end{pmatrix}$, the zero top-left entry forces $u_{11}=0$, making the bottom-left entry $\ell u_{11}=0$ as well, contrary to the required value one. A <permutation matrix> is needed in general, as in <PLU factorization>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <Gaussian elimination> without row exchanges. First apply $R_2\gets R_2-3R_1$ and $R_3\gets R_3-2R_1$; then apply $R_3\gets R_3-R_2$:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\3&8&14\\2&6&13\end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\0&2&2\\0&2&5\end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow U=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\0&2&2\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The elimination multipliers are $m_{21}=3$, $m_{31}=2$ and $m_{32}=1$. Put them below the diagonal of a <unit lower triangular matrix>:
$$
\boxed{L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\3&1&0\\2&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad U=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&4\\0&2&2\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=LU.}
$$
To see why these multipliers reconstruct the rows, row 2 of $LU$ is three times row 1 of $U$ plus row 2 of $U$, giving $(3,8,14)$. Row 3 of $LU$ is twice row 1 plus row 2 plus row 3 of $U$, giving $(2,6,13)$. This directly verifies the <LU factorization>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <elementary row operations> used above add multiples of other rows, so preserve the <determinant>. The <determinant> of an <upper triangular matrix> is the product of its diagonal entries. Hence
$$
\boxed{\det A=\det U=1\cdot2\cdot3=6.}
$$
Equivalently, $\det L=1$ and <multiplicativity of the determinant> gives $\det A=\det L\det U=6$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use three positive-edge-triggered <D flip-flops>, all driven by the same <clock signal> $CLK$. Write the current <binary representation> as $B2\,B1\,B0$, with $B0$ the <least significant bit>. The next state is the current value plus one when $A=1$ and minus one when $A=0$, in each case modulo eight. A <bit> toggles under <exclusive or> with a one.

For upward counting, $B1$ toggles when $B0=1$, while $B2$ toggles when $B1=B0=1$, the respective <carry> conditions. For downward counting the corresponding <borrow> conditions are $B0=0$ and $B1=B0=0$. Define the shared inverted direction $N=\neg A$ and the two direction-match signals
$$
T_0=B0\oplus N,\qquad T_1=B1\oplus N.
$$
Each $T_i$ is one exactly when $Bi=A$: it equals $Bi$ for upward counting and $\neg Bi$ for downward counting. Thus the <Boolean functions> for the three D inputs are
$$
\boxed{D_0=\neg B0,\qquad D_1=B1\oplus T_0,\qquad D_2=B2\oplus(T_0\land T_1).}
$$
Implement this with two <NOT gates> for $N,D_0$, four two-input <exclusive or> gates for $T_0,T_1,D_1,D_2$, and one two-input <AND gate>. Connect $D_i$ to the <D flip-flop> whose output is $Bi$; feed the three outputs back into the indicated gates. All three <flip-flops> capture simultaneously, so each equation uses the old state.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For an estimate, use the usual equal-device <CMOS> series-stack model: a $k$-input <NAND gate> or <NOR gate> has a worst-case <propagation delay> approximately $k$ times that of an inverter, because a conducting path contains $k$ series <transistors>. An <AND gate> or <OR gate> adds an output inverter. With a 1 ns inverter this gives, for $k\geq2$,
$$
\boxed{t_{\mathrm{AND},k}\simeq t_{\mathrm{OR},k}\simeq(k+1)\,\mathrm{ns}.}
$$
For $k=2$ it reproduces the stated 3 ns. This is a technology estimate under equal sizing and comparable loading, not a unique physical law inferable from a single measured delay. Actual <propagation delay> also depends on <capacitance>, <fan-out> and <transistor> sizing. If instead only the supplied two-input gates were allowed, a balanced tree would have worst-case depth $\lceil\log_2k\rceil$ and delay $3\lceil\log_2k\rceil$ ns; that is a different construction from a single $k$-input gate.

For the explicit circuit in part (a), the longest <combinational logic> path starts at $A$, passes through the shared inverter, either direction-match <exclusive or>, the <AND gate>, and the final <exclusive or> feeding $D_2$:
$$
\boxed{t_{\max}=1+2+3+2=8\,\mathrm{ns}.}
$$
The path $A\to D_1$ takes 5 ns, the paths from $B0$ or $B1$ to $D_2$ take at most 7 ns, and $B0\to D_0$ takes 1 ns. Both direction-match <exclusive or> gates act in parallel; their delays are not added together. The 8 ns excludes <clock-to-Q delay>, <setup time>, <hold time> and clock-distribution effects, because the question asks only for the <combinational logic> delay.

==== Solution

For a normal <half-precision floating-point number>, write the stored fields as sign $s$, biased <exponent> $E$ and ten-<bit> fraction $f$. The value is $(-1)^s(1.f)_2\,2^{E-15}$ for $1\leq E\leq30$. The leading one is implicit: the ten stored fraction <bits> give eleven significant <binary digits> including that one. The normalized <binary representations> and stored fields are

|| Value
|| Normalized <binary representation>
|| Sign, biased <exponent>, fraction

| $1/2$
| $1.0_2\,2^{-1}$
| `0 01110 0000000000`

| $5/8$
| $1.01_2\,2^{-1}$
| `0 01110 0100000000`

| $-3/16$
| $-1.1_2\,2^{-3}$
| `1 01100 1000000000`

| $15\frac12$
| $1.1111_2\,2^3$
| `0 10010 1111000000`

Thus the four 16-<bit> words are
$$
\boxed{0011100000000000,\quad0011100100000000,\quad1011001000000000,\quad0100101111000000.}
$$
Every conversion is exact: all four numbers have terminating <binary representations> within the available precision.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Both signs are positive, so perform <addition> of the aligned <significands>. The true <exponents> are $-1$ and $3$, a difference of four. Shift the smaller <significand> right four places, retaining its low <bits>:
$$
\frac58=0.000101_2\,2^3,\qquad15\frac12=1.111100_2\,2^3.
$$
Add at the common <exponent>:
$$
\begin{array}{r}
1.111100_2\\
+\;0.000101_2\\\hline
10.000001_2
\end{array}
\qquad\text{so}\qquad10.000001_2\,2^3=1.0000001_2\,2^4.
$$
Renormalization raises the true <exponent> to $4$ and the biased <exponent> to $E=19=10011_2$. There are only seven fraction places to retain, so no <floating-point rounding> changes the result:
$$
\boxed{\frac58+15\frac12=16\frac18,\qquad\text{stored fields }0\;10011\;0000001000.}
$$
The complete word is `0100110000001000`.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The smallest positive normal <half-precision floating-point number> uses $E=1$ and a zero fraction, while the largest uses $E=30$ and all ten fraction <bits> equal to one. Hence
$$
\boxed{x_{\min,\mathrm{normal}}=1.0_2\,2^{1-15}=2^{-14},\qquad x_{\max,\mathrm{normal}}=(2-2^{-10})2^{15}=65504.}
$$
The corresponding words are `0 00001 0000000000` and `0 11110 1111111111`. The stored <exponent> $31$ is reserved and cannot extend the finite normal range.

For a <subnormal number>, $E=0$ and the implicit leading <bit> becomes zero, while the scale remains $2^{1-15}=2^{-14}$. The least positive nonzero fraction is $0.0000000001_2=2^{-10}$, giving
$$
\boxed{x_{\min,\mathrm{subnormal}}=2^{-10}2^{-14}=2^{-24},\qquad\text{word }0\;00000\;0000000001.}
$$
Using scale $2^{-15}$ here would be incorrect: the <subnormal number> convention uses the minimum normal scale, not the ordinary biased-<exponent> formula at $E=0$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <unsigned interpretation> of the <binary string>, the set positions have weights $2^{15},2^{13},2^{12},2^{11},2^{10},2^8,2^6,2^5,2^4$. Thus
$$
\boxed{u=2^{15}+2^{13}+2^{12}+2^{11}+2^{10}+2^8+2^6+2^5+2^4=48496.}
$$
In 16-<bit> <two's complement>, the <most significant bit> is set, so subtract $2^{16}$ from that <unsigned interpretation>:
$$
\boxed{z=48496-65536=-17040.}
$$
For a <half-precision floating-point number>, split the fields at their actual boundaries rather than the printed groups of four:
$$
1\;01111\;0101110000.
$$
The sign is negative, the true <exponent> is $15-15=0$, and the fraction is $2^{-2}+2^{-4}+2^{-5}+2^{-6}=23/64$. It is a normal finite value, so
$$
\boxed{x=-\left(1+2^{-2}+2^{-4}+2^{-5}+2^{-6}\right)2^0=-\frac{87}{64}=-1.359375.}
$$

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <zero-address instruction> has no explicit operand addresses, so its operands and result location must be implicit in the <instruction set architecture>. A <stack machine> supplies these naturally: operations remove their inputs from the top of an operand <stack> and push the result. A fixed-<processor register> design is logically possible, so “almost certainly” is appropriately weaker than a proof that only a <stack machine> could work.

Write the <stack> from bottom to top, with its top on the right. Let $S$ stand for any unchanged prefix. Suitable effects are
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{add}:S,u,v\mapsto S,u+v,\qquad\mathrm{const}\ c:S\mapsto S,c.}
$$
Thus `add` pops two operands and pushes one <sum>, reducing <stack> occupancy by one. The special `const c` instruction pushes its immediate constant and increases occupancy by one. Only `const` needs an explicit value in its encoding.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use two <zero-address instructions> for indirect access to <computer memory>. Define `load` to replace the top <memory address> by the value at that address, and define `store` to pop a value followed by the address just beneath it:
$$
\mathrm{load}:S,p\mapsto S,M[p],\qquad\mathrm{store}:S,p,v\mapsto S\quad\text{with }M[p]\gets v.
$$
Both have no encoded address operand; the <stack> supplies it. If the known fixed <memory addresses> of the variables are $p_x,p_y$, implement the assignment with
``
const p_x
const p_y
load
store
``
The successive <stack> suffixes are $p_x$, then $p_x,p_y$, then $p_x,M[p_y]$, then empty. Hence
$$
\boxed{M[p_x]\gets M[p_y].}
$$
The surrounding <stack> is restored. Other `store` conventions are possible, but the operand order must be stated and used consistently.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A computed <memory address> is just another <integer> on the operand <stack>. For a <pointer> held at fixed address $p$, `const p; load` obtains the pointed-to address; another `load` obtains its contents. More generally, any expression that leaves an address on top may be followed by `load`, or may be retained beneath a computed value for `store`.

For an <array> whose base address is $b$ and whose element size is $w$ address units, the address of entry $i$ is $b+wM[p_i]$, where $p_i$ stores the index. Add <zero-address instructions> `mul` and `dup`, with `dup` mapping $S,p$ to $S,p,p$. A suitable sequence is
``
const b
const p_i
load
const w
mul
add
dup
load
const 1
add
store
``
The first six instructions leave the element address $p=b+wM[p_i]$. The remaining suffix evolves as
$$
S,p\longmapsto S,p,p\longmapsto S,p,M[p]\longmapsto S,p,M[p],1\longmapsto S,p,M[p]+1\longmapsto S,
$$
with the final effect
$$
\boxed{M[b+wM[p_i]]\gets M[b+wM[p_i]]+1.}
$$
The address is computed once and duplicated, so both accesses refer to the same <array> element. For unit-sized elements omit `const w; mul`. If the base address is itself held in a variable, replace `const b` by the instructions loading that variable's value. No instruction other than `const` requires an explicit operand.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $d_e=\operatorname{depth}(e)$ and $d_f=\operatorname{depth}(f)$ measure the extra operand <stack> space required from the current base, with each evaluation leaving one result. Evaluating $e$ first uses $d_e$ slots. Its result occupies one slot while evaluating $f$, so that stage uses $1+d_f$ slots. The final `add` reduces two slots to one. Thus
$$
\boxed{d_{e\text{ first}}=\max(d_e,1+d_f),\qquad d_{f\text{ first}}=\max(d_f,1+d_e).}
$$
By <commutativity> of <addition>, choose the smaller of these two bounds. Evaluate the child of larger depth first. The optimum recurrence on a <binary expression tree> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{depth}(e+f)=\begin{cases}\max(d_e,d_f),&d_e\ne d_f,\\d_e+1,&d_e=d_f.\end{cases}}
$$
Assign depth one to a literal or a simple already-addressable value, and compute the child depths recursively. If $d_e>d_f$, the depths are <integers>, so $d_e\geq1+d_f$; evaluating $e$ first therefore attains $d_e$, while reversing the order needs $d_e+1$. If the depths are equal, whichever child goes second needs the extra retained-result slot. These lower bounds prove the recurrence is optimal for evaluating each child subtree without interleaving or spilling. Apply it at every node to minimize peak operand <stack> occupancy for the given <binary expression tree>; this is <optimal stack evaluation of a binary expression tree>.

This changes child evaluation order, not the parenthesization. For example, a chain-shaped <binary expression tree> can have depth two, whereas a balanced tree of eight leaves has depth four under this model. The reordering assumes expressions have no order-sensitive <side effects> or observable exceptions. Reassociating <floating-point addition> would require additional care because it can change <floating-point rounding>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix `sub` to mean $S,u,v\mapsto S,u-v$. If the depth-minimizing order evaluates the right operand $f$ before the left operand $e$, the <stack> ends in $S,f,e$, so ordinary `sub` would compute the wrong sign. Two possible <instruction set architecture> extensions are:

* Add `swap`, with $S,u,v\mapsto S,v,u$. After computing $f$ and then $e$, use `swap; sub` to obtain $e-f$. One general <zero-address instruction> handles every noncommutative binary operation and is useful elsewhere, but each reversed evaluation needs an extra instruction and extra execution time. It can be implemented without an additional operand <stack> slot.
* Add reverse-operand variants, such as `rsub` with $S,u,v\mapsto S,v-u$, and analogously reverse division and other required operations. After computing $f$ and then $e$, a single `rsub` obtains $e-f$. This avoids the extra executed `swap`, but requires more opcodes and decoder support, usually one reverse form for each affected operation.

Both preserve the same peak operand <stack> bound:
$$
\boxed{\text{deeper child first; use either operand swapping or a reverse-operand opcode.}}
$$
The first trades extra dynamic instructions for a smaller instruction set; the second trades a larger instruction set for fewer executed instructions. Neither extension makes order-sensitive <side effects> safe to reorder.

==== i

===== Solution

In <natural deduction>, <conjunction introduction> combines derivations of $\phi$ and $\psi$ under the available assumptions into $\phi\land\psi$. <Disjunction elimination> takes $\phi\lor\psi$ and two <subproofs> of the same conclusion $\kappa$, one assuming $\phi$ and the other assuming $\psi$, and concludes $\kappa$. <Implication introduction> takes a <subproof> deriving $\psi$ from a <temporary assumption> $\phi$, and concludes $\phi\to\psi$.

The rectangular boxes mark the scopes of <temporary assumptions>. The <disjunction elimination rule> discharges its two case assumptions, while the <implication introduction rule> discharges the antecedent assumption. Other surrounding assumptions remain in force; intermediate results depending on the boxed assumptions cannot be exported arbitrarily. Schematically,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\phi\quad\psi}{\phi\land\psi},\qquad\frac{\phi\lor\psi\quad[\phi]\ \vdots\ \kappa\quad[\psi]\ \vdots\ \kappa}{\kappa},\qquad\frac{[\phi]\ \vdots\ \psi}{\phi\to\psi}.}
$$
Here the square brackets designate the assumptions discharged by the corresponding <inference rule>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A common-context <sequent> version of the <disjunction elimination rule> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma\vdash\phi\lor\psi\qquad\Gamma,\phi\vdash\kappa\qquad\Gamma,\psi\vdash\kappa}{\Gamma\vdash\kappa}.}
$$
The second and third premises record exactly the two boxed <subproofs> of the <natural deduction> presentation. Their additional case assumptions do not occur in the conclusion: that is <assumption discharge>. The surrounding context $\Gamma$ remains throughout. A formulation with different surrounding contexts is equivalent after retaining their union by <weakening>.

1&E\to(F\to(G\to H))&\text{premise}\\
2&\vert\ G&\text{assumption}\\
3&\vert\ \vert\ F&\text{assumption}\\
4&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ E&\text{assumption}\\
5&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ F\to(G\to H)&\to\text{-elim }1,4\\
6&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ G\to H&\to\text{-elim }5,3\\
7&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ H&\to\text{-elim }6,2\\
8&\vert\ \vert\ E\to H&\to\text{-intro }4\text{--}7\\

\end{array}

Each application of the <implication introduction rule> discharges only its matching <temporary assumption>. Thus

\boxed{E\to F\to G\to H\ \vdash\ G\to F\to E\to H.}

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

In <proof by contradiction>, assume the <negation> of the desired conclusion and derive <logical falsity>; discharge that assumption and infer the desired conclusion. Its <sequent> rule is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma,\neg\phi\vdash\bot}{\Gamma\vdash\phi}.}
$$
This is a rule of <classical propositional logic>. It follows from the paper's <negation introduction rule>, which first yields $\neg\neg\phi$, and <double-negation elimination>, which yields $\phi$. This is stronger than merely deriving $\neg\phi$ by showing that $\phi$ leads to <logical falsity>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <proof by contradiction>, expanding the final classical step explicitly into <negation introduction> followed by <double-negation elimination>. First the assumption $\neg(\neg P\lor\neg Q)$ forces $P$: otherwise $\neg P$ immediately gives the prohibited <logical disjunction>. It similarly forces $Q$. The formal <natural deduction> derivation is
$$
\begin{array}{rll}
1&\neg(P\land Q)&\text{premise}\\
2&\vert\ \neg(\neg P\lor\neg Q)&\text{assumption}\\
3&\vert\ \vert\ \neg P&\text{assumption}\\
4&\vert\ \vert\ \neg P\lor\neg Q&\lor\text{-intro-L }3\\
5&\vert\ \vert\ \bot&\neg\text{-elim }4,2\\
6&\vert\ \neg\neg P&\neg\text{-intro }3\text{--}5\\
7&\vert\ P&\neg\neg\text{-elim }6\\
8&\vert\ \vert\ \neg Q&\text{assumption}\\
9&\vert\ \vert\ \neg P\lor\neg Q&\lor\text{-intro-R }8\\
10&\vert\ \vert\ \bot&\neg\text{-elim }9,2\\
11&\vert\ \neg\neg Q&\neg\text{-intro }8\text{--}10\\
12&\vert\ Q&\neg\neg\text{-elim }11\\
13&\vert\ P\land Q&\land\text{-intro }7,12\\
14&\vert\ \bot&\neg\text{-elim }13,1\\
15&\neg\neg(\neg P\lor\neg Q)&\neg\text{-intro }2\text{--}14\\
16&\neg P\lor\neg Q&\neg\neg\text{-elim }15
\end{array}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\neg(P\land Q)\ \vdash\ \neg P\lor\neg Q.}
$$

===== Solution

A <variable> $v$ is fresh for the surrounding context $\Gamma$ when it does not occur free in any undischarged assumption in $\Gamma$. Choose it also distinct from bound variables that would cause <variable capture> in the intended substitution. The <universal quantifier introduction rule> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Gamma\vdash\phi(v)}{\Gamma\vdash\forall x\,\phi(x)}\quad(v\text{ fresh for }\Gamma).}
$$
Its justification is that the <logical derivation> of $\phi(v)$ used no special information about $v$, so it establishes the property of an arbitrary object. Semantically, changing the assignment of $v$ preserves the truth of $\Gamma$; therefore $\phi(v)$ holds for every possible assigned object. Without the <eigenvariable condition>, the assumption $R(v)$ could incorrectly be generalized to $\forall x\,R(x)$ in an interpretation where only one of two objects satisfies $R$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose an <eigenvariable> $v$ fresh for the premise. Use the <existential quantifier introduction rule> to contradict the premise under a <temporary assumption> $R(v)$:
$$
\begin{array}{rll}
1&\neg\exists x\,R(x)&\text{premise}\\
2&\vert\ R(v)&\text{assumption; }v\text{ fresh}\\
3&\vert\ \exists x\,R(x)&\exists\text{-intro }2\\
4&\vert\ \bot&\neg\text{-elim }3,1\\
5&\neg R(v)&\neg\text{-intro }2\text{--}4\\
6&\forall y\,\neg R(y)&\forall\text{-intro }5
\end{array}
$$
At line 6 the assumption $R(v)$ has been discharged and the sole remaining premise has no free $v$, satisfying the <eigenvariable condition>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\neg\exists x\,R(x)\ \vdash\ \forall y\,\neg R(y).}
$$

==== Solution

The premise limits every possible <existential witness> to the objects named by $c_0,c_1$; it does not assert that these constants name distinct objects. Instantiate it at a fresh witness $v$, then apply <disjunction elimination>. In the first case <equality elimination rule> transfers $R(v)$ to $R(c_0)$; in the second it transfers $R(v)$ to $R(c_1)$, contradicting the assumed <negation>. The full <natural deduction> derivation is
$$
\begin{array}{rll}
1&\forall x\,(x=c_0\lor x=c_1)&\text{premise}\\
2&\vert\ \neg R(c_1)&\text{assumption}\\
3&\vert\ \vert\ \exists x\,R(x)&\text{assumption}\\
4&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ R(v)&\text{witness assumption; }v\text{ fresh}\\
5&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ v=c_0\lor v=c_1&\forall\text{-elim }1\\
6&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ v=c_0&\text{first case assumption}\\
7&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ R(c_0)&=\text{-elim }6,4\\
8&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ v=c_1&\text{second case assumption}\\
9&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ R(c_1)&=\text{-elim }8,4\\
10&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \bot&\neg\text{-elim }9,2\\
11&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ \vert\ R(c_0)&\bot\text{-elim }10\\
12&\vert\ \vert\ \vert\ R(c_0)&\lor\text{-elim }5,6\text{--}7,8\text{--}11\\
13&\vert\ \vert\ R(c_0)&\exists\text{-elim }3,4\text{--}12\\
14&\vert\ (\exists x\,R(x))\to R(c_0)&\to\text{-intro }3\text{--}13\\
15&\neg R(c_1)\to((\exists x\,R(x))\to R(c_0))&\to\text{-intro }2\text{--}14
\end{array}
$$
The sibling case <subproofs> end before the witness <subproof> closes. At line 13, $v$ is absent from $R(c_0)$ and from all surrounding assumptions, so no <witness escape> occurs. The final two applications of <implication introduction> give
$$
\boxed{\forall x\,(x=c_0\lor x=c_1)\ \vdash\ \neg R(c_1)\to(\exists x\,R(x))\to R(c_0).}
$$
The <logical falsity elimination rule> is needed to complete the contradictory second case with the same conclusion as the first case.
