==== i

===== Solution

The <set difference> consists of elements belonging to the first <set> but not the second:
$$
\boxed{A\setminus B=\{x\in\mathcal U:x\in A\text{ and }x\notin B\}.}
$$
The <symmetric difference> consists of elements belonging to exactly one of the two <sets>:
$$
\boxed{A\oplus B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A).}
$$
In particular, the two <sets> in this <union> are <disjoint sets>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Every <complement of a set> is taken in the same <universal set> $\mathcal U$. Apply <De Morgan's laws> for <propositional logic> to the membership propositions. For every $x\in\mathcal U$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
x\in\overline{A\cap B}
&\iff\neg(x\in A\land x\in B)\\
&\iff(x\notin A\lor x\notin B)
\iff x\in\overline A\cup\overline B,\\
x\in\overline{A\cup B}
&\iff\neg(x\in A\lor x\in B)\\
&\iff(x\notin A\land x\notin B)
\iff x\in\overline A\cap\overline B.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equality of membership for every element proves the <set identities>
$$
\boxed{\overline{A\cap B}=\overline A\cup\overline B,
\qquad\overline{A\cup B}=\overline A\cap\overline B.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Using <set difference> as an <intersection> with a <complement of a set>, and using <double negation>, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline A\oplus\overline B
&=(\overline A\cap B)\cup(\overline B\cap A)\\
&=(B\setminus A)\cup(A\setminus B)
=A\oplus B.
\end{aligned}
$$
Interchanging the two <sets> simply interchanges the two terms of this <union>, whose <commutativity> gives
$$
\boxed{\overline A\oplus\overline B=A\oplus B,
\qquad A\oplus B=B\oplus A.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

We use a <double inclusion proof>. First, let $x\in\overline{A\oplus B}$. If $x\in A$, then $x\in B$, since otherwise $x\in A\setminus B\subseteq A\oplus B$. Hence in this case $x\in A\cap B$. If $x\notin A$, then $x\notin B$, since otherwise $x\in B\setminus A\subseteq A\oplus B$. Hence in this case $x\in\overline{A\cup B}$. This proves the first <set inclusion>.

Conversely, an element of $A\cap B$ belongs to neither $A\setminus B$ nor $B\setminus A$. An element of $\overline{A\cup B}$ also belongs to neither <set difference>. Thus every element of $(A\cap B)\cup\overline{A\cup B}$ lies outside $A\oplus B$, proving the reverse <set inclusion>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\overline{A\oplus B}=(A\cap B)\cup\overline{A\cup B}.}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <identity function> fixes every element, and <function composition> means applying the inner <function> first:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{id}_A:A\longrightarrow A,\quad x\longmapsto x,}
\qquad
\boxed{f\circ f:A\longrightarrow A,\quad x\longmapsto f(f(x)).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $f(x)=f(y)$, applying the <function> $f$ gives $f(f(x))=f(f(y))$. Since $f\circ f=\operatorname{id}_A$, this implies $x=y$, proving <injectivity>. Also every $y\in A$ is the image of $f(y)$, because $f(f(y))=y$, proving <surjectivity>.

Thus $f$ is a <bijection> and its <inverse function> is itself:
$$
\boxed{f^{-1}=f.}
$$
Such a <function> is an <involution>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take the <function>
$$
\boxed{f:\mathbb R\longrightarrow\mathbb R,\qquad f(x)=-x.}
$$
Then $f(f(x))=-(-x)=x$ for every <real number> $x$, while $f(1)=-1\ne1$. Thus it is an <involution> different from the <identity function>.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Seek an <affine function> $f(x)=cx+d$. Its <function composition> with itself is
$$
f(f(x))=c^2x+(c+1)d.
$$
Equating coefficients requires $c^2=a$ and $(c+1)d=b$. Because $a>0$ and $a\ne1$, either choice $c=\sqrt a$ or $c=-\sqrt a$ has $c+1\ne0$. Thus two valid <functions> are
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sqrt a\,x+\frac{b}{1+\sqrt a}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\boxed{f(x)=-\sqrt a\,x+\frac{b}{1-\sqrt a}.}
$$
Substitution into $c^2x+(c+1)d$ verifies each answer. These provide the requested maps; no assumption that every possible solution is <affine function> is needed.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Try a <linear function> $f(x)=cx$. The <functional equation> becomes $c^2x=(c+1)x$ for all $x$, so $c$ must be a <root of a polynomial> $c^2-c-1$. Its two <roots of a polynomial> give
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}x}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}x.}
$$
For either choice, $c^2=c+1$ proves the required equality directly. The positive coefficient is the <golden ratio>.

==== i

===== Solution

There are $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$ ordered selections without replacement. Each unordered $k$-element <subset> occurs in exactly $k!$ orders, so the <binomial coefficient> is
$$
\boxed{\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.}
$$
One precise specification of its <domain>, <codomain>, and map is
$$
\boxed{C:D\longrightarrow\mathbb N_0,\qquad
D=\{(n,k)\in\mathbb N_0^2:0\le k\le n\},\qquad
(n,k)\longmapsto\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.}
$$
Here $\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$, and $0!=1$ includes the single empty selection. The actual values on $D$ are positive, so $\mathbb N_+$ is also a valid <codomain>. For fixed $n$, one can instead use the <function> $C_n:\{0,\ldots,n\}\to\mathbb N_+$ with $C_n(k)=\binom nk$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the factorial formula for the <binomial coefficients>:
$$
\binom n2+\binom{n+1}2
=\frac{n(n-1)}2+\frac{n(n+1)}2
=\boxed{n^2}.
$$
For $n=0$ or $n=1$, the same equality holds with the conventional zero value when the lower index exceeds the upper index.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\le k\le n$, the adjacent <binomial coefficients> obey
$$
\binom n{k+1}=\binom nk\frac{n-k}{k+1},
\qquad
g(k)=\binom nk\frac{n-2k-1}{k+1}.
$$
Since $\binom nk>0$ and $k+1>0$, the sign is determined by the numerator:
$$
\boxed{g(k)>0\iff0\le k<\frac{n-1}{2}.}
$$
The printed <codomain> $\mathbb N_+$ is inconsistent with the displayed map: $g(n)=-1$, and when $n$ is odd, $g((n-1)/2)=0$. With the usual extension $\binom nk=0$ for $k>n$, $g$ is a <function> $\mathbb N_0\to\mathbb Z$, and $g(k)=0$ for $k>n$; the positive range above remains unchanged. Without that extension, the natural <domain> is $\{0,\ldots,n\}$.<Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability examination (2015) by University of Oxford>[Printed page 3, Question 2(b): the map is printed with codomain N+]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

In the intended range $0\le k\le n$, division by the positive <binomial coefficient> $\binom nk$ gives
$$
\frac{n-2k-1}{k+1}=1,
\qquad n=3k+2.
$$
Consequently the intended <remainder> is
$$
\boxed{n\bmod3=2.}
$$
There is a necessary qualification to the printed unrestricted <domain>. If <binomial coefficients> outside $0\le k\le n$ are extended by zero, then every $k>n$ satisfies $g(k)=\binom nk=0$, regardless of $n\bmod3$. For example, $n=3,k=4$ satisfies the literal equality but has $n\bmod3=0$. Thus the literal extended-domain premise alone does not determine a <remainder>; the boxed conclusion requires $0\le k\le n$, or equivalently here $\binom nk\ne0$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For adjacent <binomial coefficients>, the <ratio>
$$
\frac{\binom n{k+1}}{\binom nk}=\frac{n-k}{k+1}
$$
is greater than $1$ when $k<(n-1)/2$, equal to $1$ at $k=(n-1)/2$, and less than $1$ afterwards. For odd $n$, the row is therefore increasing up to its two equal central entries and decreasing after them. The <maximum> is attained exactly at
$$
\boxed{k^*=\frac{n-1}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad k^*=\frac{n+1}{2}.}
$$
They are distinct, including when $n=1$, so $k^*$ is **not unique**. This is the <unimodality of binomial coefficients>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

We prove <Cassini's identity> by <mathematical induction>. At $n=1$,
$$
F_0F_2-F_1^2=0\cdot1-1=-1=(-1)^1.
$$
Suppose $D_n=F_{n-1}F_{n+1}-F_n^2=(-1)^n$. The <Fibonacci recurrence> gives $F_{n-1}=F_{n+1}-F_n$ and hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{n+1}
&=F_nF_{n+2}-F_{n+1}^2\\
&=F_n(F_{n+1}+F_n)-F_{n+1}^2\\
&=-\bigl((F_{n+1}-F_n)F_{n+1}-F_n^2\bigr)
=-D_n=(-1)^{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The induction proves
$$
\boxed{F_{n-1}F_{n+1}-F_n^2=(-1)^n\qquad(n\ge1).}
$$

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a nonzero <geometric sequence>, substitute $H_n=cq^n$ into the <linear recurrence> and cancel $cq^{n-1}$:
$$
q^2=q+1.
$$
The <characteristic equation of a linear recurrence> therefore has roots
$$
\boxed{q=\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\quad\text{or}\quad
q=\psi=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}.}
$$
The zero <sequence> $c=0$ is a degenerate solution for any $q$ for which the displayed expression is defined. A nonzero <sequence> with $q=0$ cannot satisfy the recurrence at $n=1$, since it would require $0=0+H_0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <linearity> of the <Fibonacci recurrence> allows a <linear combination>
$$
F_n=A\phi^n+B\psi^n.
$$
The <initial conditions> give $A+B=0$ and $A\phi+B\psi=1$. Since $\phi-\psi=\sqrt5$, we obtain $A=1/\sqrt5$ and $B=-1/\sqrt5$. Thus <Binet's formula> is
$$
\boxed{F_n=\frac{\phi^n-\psi^n}{\sqrt5}
=\frac1{\sqrt5}\left[\left(\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n-
\left(\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}\right)^n\right].}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <mathematical induction> with two base cases. At $n=0$, the formula gives $(1-1)/\sqrt5=0=F_0$. At $n=1$, it gives $(\phi-\psi)/\sqrt5=1=F_1$.

If it holds at $n-1$ and $n$, then the <Fibonacci recurrence> gives
$$
F_{n+1}=\frac{\phi^n+\phi^{n-1}-\psi^n-\psi^{n-1}}{\sqrt5}.
$$
The identities $\phi^2=\phi+1$ and $\psi^2=\psi+1$ turn the numerator into $\phi^{n+1}-\psi^{n+1}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{F_n=(\phi^n-\psi^n)/\sqrt5\quad\text{for every }n\ge0.}
$$

=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For <functions> $f,g:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$, <big O notation> means
$$
\boxed{f=O(g)\iff\exists C>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:
|f(n)|\le C|g(n)|.}
$$
Using the <natural logarithm> and <exponential function>, $3^{\log n}=n^{\log3}$. Since $\log3>1$, for every $n\ge1$ we have $n\le n^{\log3}$. The witnesses $C=1,N=1$ therefore prove
$$
\boxed{n=O(3^{\log n}).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n\ge1$, $\log3<2$ implies $3^{\log n}=n^{\log3}\le n^2$, and also $n\le n^2$. Consequently
$$
0\le(n+3^{\log n})^2\le(2n^2)^2=4n^4.
$$
The definition of <big O notation> holds with $C=4,N=1$:
$$
\boxed{(n+3^{\log n})^2=O(n^4).}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose the definition of <big O notation> held with constants $C>0,N$. Choose an odd <integer> $n>\max(C,N)$. At this input, $f(n)=n$ and $g(n)=1$, so the required <inequality> would be $n\le C$, a contradiction. Thus
$$
\boxed{f\ne O(g).}
$$
The infinitely many even inputs, where $f(n)/g(n)=1/n$, cannot repair the failure on odd inputs because the bound must hold for **every** sufficiently large input.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

A direct <mathematical induction> proves $0\le F_n\le2^n$. The base cases $F_0=0\le1$ and $F_1=1\le2$ hold. If the bound holds at $n-1$ and $n$, then the <Fibonacci recurrence> gives
$$
F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}\le2^n+2^{n-1}\le2^{n+1}.
$$
Thus the definition of <big O notation> holds with $C=1$, proving
$$
\boxed{F_n=O(2^n).}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

By <divisibility>, write $a=nu$ and $b=nv$ for positive <integers> $u,v$. Then
$$
a^2-b^2=n^2(u^2-v^2).
$$
The factor $u^2-v^2$ is an <integer>, so
$$
\boxed{n^2\mid(a^2-b^2).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Euclidean division> gives $a=qb+r$ with $q\in\mathbb N_0$ and $0\le r<b$. Writing $a=nu,b=nv$ gives
$$
r=a-qb=n(u-qv).
$$
Since $u-qv$ is an <integer>, this proves the <divisibility>
$$
\boxed{n\mid r,}
$$
including $r=0$.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <greatest common divisor> is the largest positive <integer> dividing both inputs:
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\max\{d\in\mathbb N_+:d\mid a\text{ and }d\mid b\}.}
$$
This <maximum> exists because $1$ belongs to the <set> and all its elements are at most $b$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $a=qb+r$ using the <Euclidean division>. If $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, then $d\mid(a-qb)=r$. Conversely, if $d\mid b$ and $d\mid r$, then $d\mid(qb+r)=a$. Therefore $(a,b)$ and $(b,r)$ have exactly the same positive <common divisors>, proving
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,r).}
$$
For $r=0$, this agrees with $\gcd(b,0)=b$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $(a_0,b_0)=(a,b)$. At each iteration with $b_i>0$, compute the <remainder> $r_i=a_i\bmod b_i$, then set
$$
\boxed{(a_{i+1},b_{i+1})=(b_i,r_i).}
$$
Stop at the first index $n$ with $b_n=0$ and return $a_n$. The positive second coordinates strictly decrease, so <algorithm termination> follows from the <well-ordering principle>. The equality of <greatest common divisors> established in (ii) is a <loop invariant>, and at termination it gives
$$
\boxed{a_n=\gcd(a_n,0)=\gcd(a,b).}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Consider one iteration with $a_i>b_i>0$ and $r_i=a_i\bmod b_i$. If $b_i\le a_i/2$, then $r_i<b_i\le a_i/2$. If $b_i>a_i/2$, the <quotient> is $1$, so $r_i=a_i-b_i<a_i/2$. In either case $r_i<a_i/2$, whence
$$
a_{i+1}b_{i+1}=b_ir_i<\frac{a_ib_i}{2}.
$$
Repeated application, or <mathematical induction>, proves
$$
\boxed{a_nb_n<\frac{ab}{2^n}\qquad(n\ge1\text{ performed iterations}).}
$$
This also holds for the final iteration, whose left side is zero. For zero iterations the correct relation is equality $a_0b_0=ab$, rather than a strict <inequality>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Count the final division producing zero as an iteration, and let the total be $n$. The terminal product $a_nb_n=0$ alone gives no useful bound. Instead apply (i) to each nonterminal product, which is a positive <integer> and hence at least $1$.

If $n\ge2$, the penultimate product satisfies
$$
1\le a_{n-1}b_{n-1}<\frac{ab}{2^{n-1}},
\qquad n-1<\log_2(ab).
$$
As $n$ is an <integer>, this implies
$$
\boxed{n\le\lceil\log_2(ab)\rceil.}
$$
For $n=1$, the same bound holds since $a>b\ge1$ implies $ab\ge2$. In particular the <Euclidean algorithm> uses $O(\log a+\log b)$ divisions, in <big O notation>. The convention that includes the final zero-producing division explains the one-step offset in the argument.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The second coordinate after two iterations satisfies
$$
b_{i+2}=b_i\bmod b_{i+1}<\frac{b_i}{2}
$$
whenever those two divisions are needed, by the same <remainder> bound, now applied to $b_i>b_{i+1}>0$. Let $m=\lfloor\log_2 b\rfloor$. If the <Euclidean algorithm> has not already stopped after $2m$ iterations, then its positive second coordinate is at most $1$: for $m\ge1$,
$$
0<b_{2m}<\frac b{2^m}<2.
$$
For $m=0$ the original $b$ is already $1$. A second coordinate of $1$ requires just one more division. Consequently
$$
\boxed{n\le2\lfloor\log_2 b\rfloor+1,\qquad n=O(\log b)\text{ as }b\to\infty.}
$$
This removes dependence on the potentially much larger input $a$. It improves the scale $\log_2(ab)$ to one involving only the smaller input; the rounded numerical bound need not be smaller for every pair. One may always take the minimum of the bounds in (ii) and (iii).

==== Solution

Assume the <homogeneous function> is <differentiable>. Applying the <multivariable chain rule> to $f(tx,ty)=t^mf(x,y)$ gives, for $t$ near $1$,
$$
x f_x(tx,ty)+y f_y(tx,ty)=mt^{m-1}f(x,y).
$$
Setting $t=1$ proves <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions>:
$$
\boxed{x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=mf.}
$$
Only positive $t$ near $1$ are needed, so the derivation remains valid even when a noninteger $m$ makes the printed phrase “all real $t$” require qualification.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <linear ordinary differential equation> has differential operator $(D-3)^2$. Put $y=e^{3x}v$ and use the <product rule> twice:
$$
y'=e^{3x}(3v+v'),\qquad
y''=e^{3x}(9v+6v'+v'').
$$
Substitution cancels the $v$ and $v'$ terms, leaving $v''=4$. Two <integrations> give $v=2x^2+C_1x+C_2$. Hence the <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{3x}(2x^2+C_1x+C_2),\qquad C_1,C_2\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The two arbitrary constants account for the order of the <differential equation>; the $x^2$ factor in the particular term reflects the <repeated root> $3$ of the <characteristic polynomial>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

On an interval with $x\ne0$, set $y=xv$. The <product rule> gives $y'=v+xv'$, and substitution gives
$$
x^2(v+xv')=x^2+x^2v+x^2v^2,
\qquad\boxed{xv'=1+v^2.}
$$
This is a <separable differential equation>. Since $1+v^2>0$ for real $v$, separation loses no real constant solution:
$$
\frac{dv}{1+v^2}=\frac{dx}{x},
\qquad \arctan v=\log|x|+C.
$$
Thus the <general solution> on any interval avoiding zero and the poles of the <tangent function> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x\tan(\log|x|+C).}
$$
At $x=0$ the original <differential equation> forces $y(0)=0$, but no real solution from this family extends over a whole interval through zero: $\log|x|+C$ encounters infinitely many poles as $x\to0$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For $x\ne\pm1$, write the <linear ordinary differential equation> as
$$
y'+\frac{2x}{1-x^2}y=\frac{x(1-x)}{1-x^2}.
$$
An <integrating factor> on each interval avoiding $\pm1$ is $\mu(x)=1/(1-x^2)$; multiplication by a nonzero constant, including a sign, is immaterial. Therefore
$$
\left(\frac{y}{1-x^2}\right)'
=\frac{x(1-x)}{(1-x^2)^2}
=\frac{x}{(1-x)(1+x)^2}.
$$
The <partial fraction decomposition> is
$$
\frac{x}{(1-x)(1+x)^2}
=\frac{1}{4(1-x)}+\frac{1}{4(1+x)}-\frac{1}{2(1+x)^2}.
$$
Integrating gives the <general solution>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{1-x}{2}+
(1-x^2)\left(C+\frac14\log\left|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right|\right),
\qquad x\ne\pm1.}
$$
The constant is independent on each connected interval of definition. At the singular points the undivided <differential equation> forces $y(1)=0$ and $y(-1)=1$. The displayed family tends to these values, but its <derivative> has a logarithmic divergence there, so no member extends as a <differentiable function> through either singular point.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <chain rule> gives
$$
f'(x)=e^{x^3-x^5}(3x^2-5x^4)
=e^{x^3-x^5}x^2(3-5x^2).
$$
The <exponential function> never vanishes, so all <critical points> are
$$
\boxed{x=0,\qquad x=\sqrt{3/5},\qquad x=-\sqrt{3/5}.}
$$
In particular $f'(0)=0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <Taylor series> $e^u=1+u+u^2/2+\cdots$ with $u=x^3-x^5$. Since $u$ begins at cubic order and $u^2$ begins at sixth order, through cubic terms the <Taylor series> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=1+x^3+O(x^5).}
$$
The linear and quadratic coefficients are zero. The stronger remainder $O(x^5)$, in <big O notation>, follows because the fourth-order term is also zero; the next term is $-x^5$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Near zero, $x^3-x^5=x^3(1-x^2)$ has the sign of $x$, so $f(x)<1$ just to the left and $f(x)>1$ just to the right. Thus the <critical point> is neither a <local minimum> nor a <local maximum>. Moreover the <Taylor series> gives $f''(x)=6x+O(x^3)$, which changes sign at zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(0,1)\text{ is a stationary inflection point}.}
$$
This is the point called a “saddle point” in the question; <stationary inflection point> specifies the one-variable meaning.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Solving the <linear ordinary differential equation> and imposing the <initial condition> gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=2e^{-\lambda x}.}
$$
As $\lambda>0$, the requested <limit> is
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}y(1)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}2e^{-\lambda}=0.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $h=1/N$, $x_j=jh$, and let $y_j$ approximate $y(x_j)$. The <forward Euler method> gives the <difference equation>
$$
\boxed{y_{j+1}=y_j+h(-\lambda y_j)
=\left(1-\frac\lambda N\right)y_j,\qquad y_0=2.}
$$
This <geometric sequence> has solution
$$
\boxed{y_j=2\left(1-\frac\lambda N\right)^j\quad(0\le j\le N),
\qquad y_N=2\left(1-\frac\lambda N\right)^N.}
$$
For fixed $\lambda$, the <limit> as $N\to\infty$ is $2e^{-\lambda}$, as required by the exact solution.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <amplification factor> is $R=1-\lambda/N$. To reproduce decay, the <forward Euler method> requires $|R|<1$, equivalently
$$
-1<1-\frac\lambda N<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{N>\lambda/2.}
$$
At $N=\lambda/2$, $R=-1$ and the approximations alternate with constant magnitude $2$; for $N<\lambda/2$, $|R|>1$ and the magnitude grows. This is the condition of <absolute stability> for this decaying <differential equation>.

Stability is only a minimum requirement. Values just inside the bound can still give a poor endpoint approximation; resolving the decay accurately generally requires a smaller <step size>. If positivity at every step is desired, the stronger condition $N\ge\lambda$ ensures $R\ge0$. The figure shows the difference between decay of the exact solution and numerical oscillation.

For positive edge lengths the <volume> is $V=abc$ and the <surface area> constraint is $2(ab+ac+bc)=S$. Introduce a <Lagrange multiplier> $\mu$:
$$
\mathcal L=abc-\mu\bigl(2ab+2ac+2bc-S\bigr).

The <stationary point> equations are
$$
bc=2\mu(b+c),\qquad ac=2\mu(a+c),\qquad ab=2\mu(a+b).
$$
Multiplying these by $a,b,c$, respectively, and equating their left sides gives $\mu c(a-b)=0$ and $\mu b(a-c)=0$. Because all edge lengths are positive, the equations imply $\mu>0$; hence $a=b=c$. The constraint gives $6a^2=S$.

=(abc)^{2/3}.

Equality holds exactly when $ab=ac=bc$, or $a=b=c$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V_{\max}=\left(\frac S6\right)^{3/2},\qquad

===== Solution

For a nonzero <separable solution> $u=XT$, <separation of variables> gives
$$
\frac{T'}{DT}=\frac{X''}{X}=-\kappa,
\qquad
\boxed{X''+\kappa X=0,\qquad X(0)=X(L)=0.}
$$
These <Dirichlet boundary conditions> follow because $T$ is not identically zero. If $\kappa=0$, the <affine function> $X=A+Bx$ obeys both conditions only when $A=B=0$. If $\kappa=-\rho^2<0$, $X=A\cosh(\rho x)+B\sinh(\rho x)$ also has only the zero solution under both conditions.

For $\kappa=\omega^2>0$, $X=A\cos(\omega x)+B\sin(\omega x)$. The first <boundary condition> forces $A=0$, and the second forces $\omega L=n\pi$ for a nonzero solution. Hence all nonzero spatial modes are
$$
\boxed{\kappa_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2,\qquad
X_n(x)=B_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\quad B_n\ne0.}
$$
The identically zero <function> is also a solution. Allowing $B_n=0$ includes it in the displayed family. Here $L>0$ is the interval length and $n$ labels the <eigenvalues> of the operator $-d^2/dx^2$ with these <boundary conditions>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the spatial mode with <eigenvalue> $\kappa_n=(n\pi/L)^2$, the temporal <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{T_n'+D\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2T_n=0.}
$$
Its <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{T_n(t)=C_n\exp\left(-\frac{Dn^2\pi^2}{L^2}t\right),\qquad C_n\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The printed reference to “each solution $X(x)$ ... in part (b)” refers to (b)(i). The zero spatial mode gives $u=0$ irrespective of $T$ and contributes no additional nonzero solution.<Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability examination (2015) by University of Oxford>[Printed page 7, Question 6(b)(ii), internal part reference]{ref}

{scope}

===== Solution

The <heat equation> and its homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are <linear>, so <superposition> of the separated modes gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
\exp\left(-\frac{Dn^2\pi^2t}{L^2}\right).}
$$
The <Fourier sine basis> represents the initial profile on the interior of the interval. At $t=0$ its <Fourier sine series> must equal $x(1-x)$. Multiplying by $\sin(m\pi x/L)$, integrating from $0$ to $L$, and using <orthogonality> gives
$$
\int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,dx
=\begin{cases}L/2,&n=m,\\0,&n\ne m.\end{cases}
$$
Therefore the required <Fourier sine coefficients> are
$$
\boxed{\alpha_n=\frac2L\int_0^L x(1-x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx,
\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
For every $t>0$, the exponential factors ensure convergence of the differentiated series, and every term satisfies the <partial differential equation> and the <boundary conditions>. The <Fourier sine series> recovers the smooth initial profile at every $0<x<L$ as $t\downarrow0$.

The scan explicitly prints $x(1-x)$ on $(0,L)$; it must not silently become $x(L-x)$. For $L\ne1$, the interior profile tends to $L(1-L)\ne0$ at the right endpoint, whereas the prescribed <boundary condition> is zero for $t>0$. The solution is therefore not continuous at the corner $(L,0)$, although it satisfies every condition on the printed open ranges. ===== Solution

A <partition of a sample space> is a finite or countable family of nonempty <events> such that
$$
\boxed{B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing\quad(i\ne j),\qquad
\bigcup_iB_i=\Omega.}
$$
Thus exactly one event in the family occurs in each outcome. To use ordinary event-conditioned <conditional expectations>, one usually assumes $\mathbb P(B_i)>0$; any zero-<probability> blocks can instead be omitted from the expectation formula.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For an <integrable random variable> $X$ and a <partition of a sample space> $(B_i)$, the <law of total expectation> states
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\sum_{i:\mathbb P(B_i)>0}
\mathbb E[X\mid B_i]\,\mathbb P(B_i).}
$$
It follows by writing $X=\sum_iX\mathbf1_{B_i}$ and using $\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{B_i}]=\mathbb P(B_i)\mathbb E[X\mid B_i]$. Integrability justifies the countable sum. The same equality is valid with nonnegative, possibly infinite expectations when $X\ge0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $\tau$ be the <absorption time>. Its <expectation> is finite: from every interior state, a string of at most $n$ successive left moves reaches $0$, and the <probability> of such a string is at least $p=3^{-n}$. Hence the <Markov property> gives $\mathbb P_k(\tau>jn)\le(1-p)^j$, a geometric tail bound, and $\mathbb E_k[\tau]<\infty$.

For $1\le k\le n-1$, one step is always spent before absorption or the remaining walk. Apply <first-step analysis> and the <law of total expectation> to the three possible first moves. The <conditional expectations> of the remaining <absorption time> are $e_{k+1},e_k,e_{k-1}$, respectively; independence of future moves gives the same law as a fresh <random walk> from the new position. Thus
$$
e_k=1+\frac13e_{k+1}+\frac13e_k+\frac13e_{k-1},
$$
which rearranges to
$$
\boxed{\frac13e_{k+1}-\frac23e_k+\frac13e_{k-1}=-1.}
$$
The term $1$ also appears when the walk stays at $k$, because staying still consumes a step.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <absorbing states> give $e_0=e_n=0$. Multiplying the <difference equation> in (i) by $3$ gives
$$
e_{k+1}-2e_k+e_{k-1}=-3.
$$
The second <finite difference> of $k^2$ is $2$, so a <particular solution> is $-3k^2/2$; the homogeneous solutions are $A+Bk$. Imposing the two <boundary conditions> gives $A=0,B=3n/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{e_k=\frac32k(n-k),\qquad0\le k\le n.}
$$
Uniqueness follows because the difference of any two solutions has zero second <finite difference>, hence is <affine function>, and vanishes at both endpoints. This is the <mean absorption time of a lazy symmetric random walk> with holding probability $1/3$.

===== Solution

For each <random variable>, the <probability mass function> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X_i]=\frac{-1+0+1}{3}=0,
\qquad \mathbb E[X_i^2]=\frac{1+0+1}{3}=\frac23,
\qquad\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\frac23.
$$

$$
The <independence> is needed for the <variance> calculation, but not for the <mean>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Each $X_i$ has the same <probability distribution> as $-X_i$. By <independence>, the entire vector and its negative have the same joint law, so $S_m$ has a <symmetric distribution> about zero. The two disjoint tail <events> consequently have equal <probabilities>:
$$
\mathbb P(S_m>m/2)=\mathbb P(S_m<-m/2)
=\frac12\mathbb P(|S_m|>m/2).
$$
Apply <Chebyshev's inequality> with the <mean> and <variance> from (i):
$$
\mathbb P(S_m>m/2)
\le\frac12\frac{2m/3}{(m/2)^2}
=\boxed{\frac4{3m}}.
$$
Using only containment in the two-sided event would give $8/(3m)$; the <symmetry> supplies the necessary extra factor $1/2$.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <probability generating function> of a nonnegative <integer-valued random variable> is
$$
\boxed{G(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p(j)s^j.}
$$
Use $s^0=1$, including at $s=0$. The <power series> converges absolutely for every real $s\in[-1,1]$, since $\sum_jp(j)|s|^j\le1$. In particular it is always defined on the usual probability-generating interval $[0,1]$. It may also be defined for $|s|>1$, depending on the <radius of convergence> and convergence at its endpoints; the precise real <domain> of finite expectation is $\{s\in\mathbb R:\sum_jp(j)|s|^j<\infty\}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

By hypothesis the <power series> converges at every positive real argument, so its <radius of convergence> is infinite. Termwise <differentiation of a power series> is therefore valid in a neighborhood of $s=1$. For $k\ge1$,
$$
G^{(k)}(s)=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}
j(j-1)\cdots(j-k+1)p(j)s^{j-k}.
$$
At $s=1$, the right side is the <expectation> of the <falling factorial> of $X$:
$$
\boxed{G^{(k)}(1)=\mathbb E[X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)].}
$$
The special case $k=1$ gives
$$
\boxed{G'(1)=\mathbb E[X].}
$$
For $k=0$, take the empty product as $1$; the equality becomes $G(1)=1$. These are the <factorial moments>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $X^2=X(X-1)+X$, the <probability generating function> identities give
$$
\mathbb E[X^2]=G''(1)+G'(1).
$$
Subtracting the squared <mean> yields the <variance>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X)=G''(1)+G'(1)-\bigl(G'(1)\bigr)^2.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <Poisson distribution> with parameter $1$, the <exponential series> gives
$$
G(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-1}s^j}{j!}
=\boxed{e^{s-1}},\qquad s\in\mathbb R.
$$
Every <derivative> is $G^{(k)}(s)=e^{s-1}$, so the <factorial moments> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)]=1\qquad(k\ge0).}
$$
In particular $G'(1)=G''(1)=1$, and the <variance> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X)=1+1-1^2=1.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Each of the $n$ distinguishable elements has $x$ independent choices of colour. By the <multiplication principle>, the number of <functions> from the $n$-element <set> to the $x$-element colour <set> is
$$
\boxed{x^n.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Group the elements of a colouring by equality of colour. This creates a unique <partition of a set> into nonempty unlabelled blocks, each of one colour, with distinct blocks assigned distinct colours.

For a <partition of a set> with $k$ blocks, assigning distinct colours to the blocks gives the <falling factorial> $x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)$ possibilities. To see that no extra factor $k!$ is needed, order the blocks canonically by their smallest elements, then assign colours in that order. There are $A(n,k)$ possible <set partitions> with $k$ blocks; these numbers are the <Stirling numbers of the second kind>.

Every colouring is counted exactly once in exactly one value of $k$, so
$$
\boxed{x^n=\sum_{k=1}^n A(n,k)x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1).}
$$
For $k>x$, no colouring of the blocks is possible and the displayed <falling factorial> is zero, because one factor is $x-x$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the <probability mass function> of the <Poisson distribution> directly gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X^n]=\frac1e\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j^n}{j!}.}
$$
For each fixed positive <integer> $n$, this <series> converges: the <ratio> of successive nonzero terms is $(1+1/j)^n/(j+1)$, which tends to zero. Equivalently, using the <polynomial identity> in (ii) and the <factorial moments> from (a)(iv) gives $\mathbb E[X^n]=\sum_{k=1}^nA(n,k)$; the extension of that identity to $X=0,1$ is justified in (iv).

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Both sides of (ii) are <polynomials> in the colour count, and they agree at every <integer> $x\ge2$. A nonzero <polynomial> has only finitely many roots, so this is a <polynomial identity> and holds also at $x=0,1$. It can therefore be evaluated at every possible value of the <Poisson random variable> $X$.

Take <expectations> and use the <factorial moments> from (a)(iv). The sum over $k$ is finite, so <linearity of expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X^n]
=\sum_{k=1}^nA(n,k)\mathbb E[X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)]
=\sum_{k=1}^nA(n,k)=B(n).
$$
Combining this with (iii) proves <Dobiński's formula> for the <Bell numbers>:
$$
\boxed{B(n)=\frac1e\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j^n}{j!}\qquad(n\ge1).}
$$
For completeness, the empty <set> has one <partition of a set>, giving $B(0)=1$; that case uses the empty-product conventions $0^0=1$ in this identity and a $k=0$ term in the partition sum.

===== Solution

For a <continuous random variable> with <probability density function> $f_X$, its <mean> is
$$
\boxed{\mu=\mathbb E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx,}
$$
provided $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|f_X(x)\,dx<\infty$. Its <variance> is the <expectation> of the squared deviation from its <mean>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^2f_X(x)\,dx.}
$$
This is finite when $\mathbb E[X^2]<\infty$; otherwise it may be $+\infty$ if the finite <mean> exists.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume a finite second <moment>, so the following <expectations> are finite. Expand the square and use <linearity of expectation>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{var}(X)
&=\mathbb E[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]\\
&=\mathbb E[X^2]-2\mu\mathbb E[X]+\mu^2
=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb E[X])^2.}
$$
The same equality holds in the extended sense when the <mean> is finite and the second <moment> is infinite.

===== Solution

The <random variable> $Y=Z^2/2$ is nonnegative, so its <cumulative distribution function> is zero for $y<0$. If $y\ge0$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y)&=\mathbb P(Z^2/2\le y)
=\mathbb P(-\sqrt{2y}\le Z\le\sqrt{2y})\\
&=\Phi(\sqrt{2y})-\Phi(-\sqrt{2y})
=2\Phi(\sqrt{2y})-1.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last step uses the <symmetry> of the <standard normal distribution>, $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
2\Phi(\sqrt{2y})-1,&y\ge0,\\
0,&y<0.
\end{cases}}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiate the <cumulative distribution function> for $y>0$ using the <chain rule> and the <standard normal density>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_Y(y)&=2\Phi'(\sqrt{2y})\frac1{\sqrt{2y}}\\
&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{2y}}e^{-y}
=\frac{e^{-y}}{\sqrt{\pi y}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus a <probability density function> is
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{e^{-y}}{\sqrt{\pi y}},&y>0,\\
0,&y\le0.
\end{cases}}
$$
The value assigned at zero is immaterial. The <probability density function> is unbounded near zero, but $F_Y(0)=0$ and continuity of the <cumulative distribution function> there show that there is no point mass. This is a <gamma distribution> with shape $1/2$ and rate $1$.
