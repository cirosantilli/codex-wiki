===== Solution

The repeated root of the <characteristic equation> suggests factoring out $e^x$. Set $y=e^xu$. The <product rule> gives
$$
y'=e^x(u+u'),\qquad y''=e^x(u+2u'+u''),\qquad y''-2y'+y=e^xu''.
$$
Thus the <ordinary differential equation> reduces to $u''=1$, with <general solution> $u=x^2/2+Ax+B$. The first <initial condition> gives $B=1$. Since $y'(0)=A+B=2$, the other <initial condition> gives $A=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^x\left(1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Substitution into the reduced <ordinary differential equation> and the two <initial conditions> verifies the result.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $y_1=(1-x)^{-1}$, the <derivatives> are $y_1'=(1-x)^{-2}$ and $y_1''=2(1-x)^{-3}$. Consequently the left-hand side of the <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\frac{2}{1-x}-\frac{4}{1-x}+\frac{2}{1-x}=0
$$
where $x\ne1$.

Use <reduction of order>, writing $y=u/(1-x)$. A direct application of the <product rule> gives
$$
(1-x)^2y''-4(1-x)y'+2y=(1-x)u''-2u'.
$$
For $v=u'$ the resulting <first-order ordinary differential equation> is $v'/v=2/(1-x)$ whenever $v\ne0$. <Integration> yields $v=C(1-x)^{-2}$ and $u=A+B/(1-x)$; the case $v=0$ is already included. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{A}{1-x}+\frac{B}{(1-x)^2}}
$$
on either interval $x<1$ or $x>1$. The two displayed <solutions of a differential equation> are <linearly independent>, so they span the <solution space of a homogeneous linear differential equation> on either interval. The point $x=1$ is a <singular point of a differential equation> and cannot be crossed by a nonzero member of this family that remains finite there.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Multiplying the <ordinary differential equation> by two suggests using the <dependent variable> $Y=y^2$, whose <derivative> is $Y'=2yy'$. On the interval through $x=1$, this gives the <linear ordinary differential equation>
$$
xY'=Y+x^2,\qquad \left(\frac Yx\right)'=1.
$$
After <integration>, $Y=x^2+Cx$. The <initial condition> $Y(1)=4$ gives $C=3$. As $y(1)>0$, <continuity> selects the positive branch:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt{x^2+3x}\quad(x>0),\qquad y(2)=\sqrt{10}.}
$$
The interval $x>0$ is the maximal real interval through the given <initial condition>: at $x=0$ the formula approaches $0$, but its <derivative> is unbounded, so it does not extend through zero as a differentiable <solution of a differential equation>.

==== i

===== Solution

Assume all <partial derivatives> needed below are continuous. The <multivariable chain rule> is
$$
\boxed{f_x=g_uu_x+g_vv_x,}
$$
where the <partial derivatives> of $g$ are evaluated at $(u(x,y),v(x,y))$. Apply the <product rule> and the <multivariable chain rule> once more:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{xx}&=(g_{uu}u_x+g_{uv}v_x)u_x+g_uu_{xx}\\
&\quad +(g_{vu}u_x+g_{vv}v_x)v_x+g_vv_{xx}.
\end{aligned}
$$
<Clairaut's theorem> gives $g_{vu}=g_{uv}$, so
$$
\boxed{f_{xx}=g_uu_{xx}+g_vv_{xx}+g_{uu}u_x^2+2g_{uv}u_xv_x+g_{vv}v_x^2.}
$$
Differentiating $f_x$ with respect to $y$ instead gives
$$
\boxed{f_{xy}=g_uu_{xy}+g_vv_{xy}+g_{uu}u_xu_y+g_{uv}(u_xv_y+v_xu_y)+g_{vv}v_xv_y.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For this <change of variables>,
$$
u_x=2x,\quad u_y=-2y,\quad v_x=2y,\quad v_y=2x,
$$
and $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0=v_{xx}+v_{yy}$. The <second derivative chain rule> therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
f_{xx}+f_{yy}
&=g_{uu}(4x^2+4y^2)+2g_{uv}(4xy-4xy)+g_{vv}(4y^2+4x^2)\\
&=4(x^2+y^2)(g_{uu}+g_{vv}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the transformation rule for the <Laplacian> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{x,y}f=4(x^2+y^2)\Delta_{u,v}g.}
$$
For $f=x+y$, its <Laplacian> is zero. At every point with $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$, division by $4(x^2+y^2)$ proves
$$
\boxed{g_{uu}+g_{vv}=0.}
$$
This conclusion applies to each local inverse branch of the <change of variables>: its <Jacobian determinant> is $4(x^2+y^2)$, so such a branch exists away from the origin by the <inverse function theorem>. A single globally defined $g$ cannot represent $x+y$ on the entire plane, since $(x,y)$ and $(-x,-y)$ give the same $(u,v)$ but generally opposite values of $x+y$. Nor can a differentiable $g$ represent this $f$ in a neighborhood of the origin: the <multivariable chain rule> there would give $f_x=f_y=0$, contradicting $f_x=f_y=1$. The requested <harmonic function> conclusion is therefore a local statement on nonsingular branches.<Calculus, probability, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2003)>[Printed p. 2, Question 2(a)(ii)]{ref}

==== Solution

Write $z(x,y)=Z(r,s)$ in the new <coordinates>. Since
$$
\partial_x=\partial_r-\partial_s,\qquad \partial_y=\partial_r+\partial_s,
$$
the <partial differential equation> becomes
$$
z_{xx}-z_{yy}=-4Z_{rs}=0.
$$
For a twice continuously differentiable <solution of a differential equation>, $Z_r$ is independent of $s$, so <integration> first in $r$ and then in $s$ gives
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=\phi(y+x)+\psi(y-x).}
$$
This is the decomposition of the <one-dimensional wave equation> into waves traveling in opposite directions.

The first <initial condition> gives $\phi(y)+\psi(y)=\cos y$. The second <initial condition> gives $\phi'(y)-\psi'(y)=0$. Differentiating the first relation and combining the two relations yields $\phi'=psi'=-\tfrac12\sin y$. Thus $\phi(y)=\tfrac12\cos y+C$ and $\psi(y)=\tfrac12\cos y-C$. The constants cancel, and the <cosine addition formula> gives
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=\frac{\cos(y+x)+\cos(y-x)}2=\cos x\cos y.}
$$
Both <initial conditions> and the <partial differential equation> follow directly by differentiation.

===== Solution

For a <linear map> $T:V\to W$, its <image of a linear map> and <kernel of a linear map> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}\subseteq W,\qquad \ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0_W\}\subseteq V.}
$$
Their <dimensions> define the <rank of a linear map> and <nullity>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}T=\dim(\operatorname{im}T),\qquad\operatorname{nullity}T=\dim(\ker T).}
$$
The <image of a linear map> measures which output <vectors> can occur, while the <kernel of a linear map> measures which input <vectors> are sent to zero.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <rank-nullity theorem> states that a <linear map> $T:V\to W$ with finite-dimensional $V$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\dim V=\operatorname{nullity}T+\operatorname{rank}T.}
$$
Choose a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_r$ of $\ker T$ and use <basis extension> to obtain a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_r,u_1,\ldots,u_s$ of $V$. We prove that $Tu_1,\ldots,Tu_s$ is a <basis> of $\operatorname{im}T$.

Every $v\in V$ is a <linear combination> of the extended <basis>. Applying $T$ kills the $k_i$ terms, so $Tv$ is a <linear combination> of $Tu_1,\ldots,Tu_s$. This proves the <linear span> assertion. For <linear independence>, if $\sum_j b_jTu_j=0$, then $\sum_j b_ju_j$ belongs to the <kernel of a linear map>. Hence $\sum_j b_ju_j=\sum_i a_ik_i$ for some <scalars> $a_i$. The <linear independence> of the extended <basis> forces every $a_i$ and $b_j$ to vanish. Consequently $\operatorname{rank}T=s$ and $\operatorname{nullity}T=r$, while $\dim V=r+s$, proving the theorem.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

By <diagonalizability>, $V$ has an <eigenbasis> $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ with $Te_j=\lambda_je_j$. Arrange the <basis> so that $\lambda_1=\cdots=\lambda_r=0$ and the remaining <eigenvalues> are nonzero. If there are no zero or no nonzero <eigenvalues>, the corresponding <linear span> below is simply the zero <vector subspace>.

For $v=\sum_j a_je_j$, the equation $Tv=0$ holds precisely when $a_j=0$ for every $j>r$. Thus
$$
\ker T=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\ldots,e_r\}.
$$
Every output is in $\operatorname{span}\{e_{r+1},\ldots,e_m\}$, and conversely $e_j=T(\lambda_j^{-1}e_j)$ for each $j>r$. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{im}T=\operatorname{span}\{e_{r+1},\ldots,e_m\}.
$$
These two disjoint parts of a <basis> span $V$ and their <linear spans> intersect only at zero. This is exactly the <direct sum of vector spaces> assertion
$$
\boxed{V=\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take the <linear map>
$$
\boxed{T(x,y)=(y,0),\qquad [T]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its <kernel of a linear map> consists of all $(x,0)$, and its <image of a linear map> is the same line:
$$
\ker T=\operatorname{im}T=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}.
$$
Their <set intersection> is nonzero and their sum is only one-dimensional. In particular, $(0,1)$ cannot be written as a sum of one <vector> from each of these <vector subspaces>. The nonzero <set intersection> also prevents an internal <direct sum of vector spaces>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb R^2\ne\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T.}
$$
The <matrix> is a nonzero <nilpotent matrix>; its failure of <diagonalizability> explains why the preceding result does not apply.

==== i

===== Solution

The three <elementary row operations> are:

* interchange two distinct rows, $R_i\leftrightarrow R_j$;
* multiply a row by a nonzero <scalar>, $R_i\leftarrow cR_i$ with $c\ne0$;
* add a <scalar multiple> of another row, $R_i\leftarrow R_i+cR_j$ with $i\ne j$.

The nonzero requirement in the second operation ensures that each <elementary row operation> is reversible.
$$
\boxed{R_i\leftrightarrow R_j;\qquad R_i\leftarrow cR_i\ (c\ne0);\qquad R_i\leftarrow R_i+cR_j\ (i\ne j).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a square <matrix>, the corresponding changes in its <determinant> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
R_i\leftrightarrow R_j &: \quad \det\longmapsto-\det,\\
R_i\leftarrow cR_i &: \quad \det\longmapsto c\det,\\
R_i\leftarrow R_i+cR_j &: \quad \det\longmapsto\det.
\end{aligned}}
$$
These follow respectively from the alternating property, <multilinearity> in the rows, and the fact that a <determinant> with two equal rows vanishes.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If a square <matrix> $A$ is an <invertible matrix>, <multiplicativity of the determinant> gives
$$
1=\det I=\det(AA^{-1})=(\det A)(\det A^{-1}),
$$
so $\det A\ne0$.

Conversely, suppose $\det A\ne0$. Use <Gaussian elimination> to reduce $A$ to <row echelon form> $U$. Each <elementary row operation> multiplies the <determinant> by a nonzero factor, so $\det U\ne0$. If this square <matrix> had fewer than $n$ <pivots>, its <row echelon form> would have a zero row and hence zero <determinant>, a contradiction. Therefore it has a <pivot> in every row and column, and further <elementary row operations> reduce it to $I$.

Each <elementary row operation> is multiplication on the left by an <invertible matrix>, its inverse being the reverse operation. Thus $E_k\cdots E_1A=I$ implies $A=E_1^{-1}\cdots E_k^{-1}$, a product of <invertible matrices>. Hence
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is invertible}\iff\det A\ne0.}
$$

==== i

===== Solution

Let $A_n$ denote the given <tridiagonal matrix> and put $D_n=\det A_n$, including $D_0=1$ for the empty <determinant>. The starting values are
$$
D_0=1,\qquad D_1=1,\qquad D_2=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
For $n\ge3$, apply the following <elementary row operations> in the indicated order, always using the updated rows:
$$
R_2\leftarrow R_2-R_1,\qquad R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_2,\qquad R_2\leftrightarrow R_3.
$$
The first three rows now begin
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&1&0&1&\cdots\\
0&0&1&0&\cdots
\end{pmatrix},
$$
with the fourth column omitted if $n=3$. When $n\ge4$, also use $R_4\leftarrow R_4-R_3$. The resulting <block upper triangular matrix> has a $3\times3$ <upper triangular matrix> with diagonal entries all one in its top left corner, and $A_{n-3}$ in its bottom right corner. All <elementary row operations> preserved the <determinant> except the single row interchange. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D_n=-D_{n-3}\quad(n\ge3).}
$$
Applying this <recurrence relation> to the three starting values proves by <mathematical induction> that
$$
\boxed{D_n=\begin{cases}
(-1)^k,&n=3k,\\
(-1)^k,&n=3k+1,\\
0,&n=3k+2.
\end{cases}}
$$
Equivalently, the sequence is periodic with period six, with values $1,1,0,-1,-1,0$ starting at $n=0$. A compact closed form is $D_n=\sin((n+1)\pi/3)/\sin(\pi/3)$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A square <matrix> is an <invertible matrix> exactly when its <determinant> is nonzero. The preceding <recurrence relation> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{A_n\text{ is invertible}\iff n\not\equiv2\pmod3.}
$$

==== i

===== Solution

For <events> $A,B$ with $\mathbb P(B)>0$, the <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)}.}
$$
It measures the <probability> of $A$ after restricting the <sample space> to $B$ and renormalizing. This quotient does not define <conditional probability> given an <event> of zero <probability>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The partition theorem is the <law of total probability>. If $B_1,B_2,\ldots$ form a finite or countable <partition of a sample space> into pairwise <disjoint events> with positive <probabilities>, then for any <event> $A$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\sum_j\mathbb P(A\mid B_j)\mathbb P(B_j).}
$$
Indeed, $A$ is the <disjoint union> of the <events> $A\cap B_j$, so <countable additivity> gives the formula after applying the definition of <conditional probability>. Zero-probability members can be omitted.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $X_j$ be the <indicator random variable> that coin $j$ shows a head. Each <expectation> is $\mathbb E X_j=1/2$, so <linearity of expectation> gives the expected number of heads:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left[\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\right]=\frac n2.}
$$
For the game, write $p_i$ for the <probability> that $H$ eventually holds all $n$ coins when starting with $i$. The states $0,n$ are absorbing, so $p_0=0$ and $p_n=1$. Conditioning on the first toss and using the <law of total probability> gives
$$
p_i=\frac12p_{i-1}+\frac12p_{i+1},\qquad 1\le i\le n-1.
$$
Thus $p_{i+1}-p_i=p_i-p_{i-1}$: successive differences are constant, and $p_i=A+Bi$. The boundary values give $A=0$, $B=1/n$, hence the <gambler's ruin> answer is
$$
\boxed{p_i=\frac in.}
$$
For completeness, the game terminates with <probability> one. From any nonabsorbing state, a run of $n$ consecutive heads would reach $n$ within the next $n$ tosses, and that run has <probability> $2^{-n}$. Conditional on survival, the same bound applies to the next block. Therefore the <probability> of surviving $kn$ tosses is at most $(1-2^{-n})^k$, which tends to zero.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $W$ be the <event> that $H$ wins, and $F$ the <event> that his first toss is a tail. For a nontrivial starting state $1\le i\le n-1$, a first tail has <probability> $1/2$ and changes his holding to $i-1$. By the <chain rule for probabilities>,
$$
\mathbb P(F\cap W)=\frac12p_{i-1}=\frac{i-1}{2n}.
$$
Since $\mathbb P(W)=i/n>0$, the definition of <conditional probability> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(F\mid W)=\frac{i-1}{2i}.}
$$
In particular, when $i=1$, losing the first coin would end the game in defeat, so the conditional answer is zero. At $i=0$ conditioning on victory is undefined, while at $i=n$ the game has already ended and no first toss occurs.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $I$ be the number of heads in the initial allocation tosses. Then $I$ has the <binomial distribution> with parameters $n$ and $1/2$. Given $I=i$, the winning <probability> is $i/n$, including the already finished games $i=0,n$. The <law of total probability> and <linearity of expectation> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(W)&=\sum_{i=0}^n\mathbb P(I=i)\frac in
=\frac{\mathbb E I}{n}
=\frac{n/2}{n}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, for $n\ge1$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(H\text{ finishes with all coins})=\frac12.}
$$

The <binary relation> $x\le y$ on the <natural numbers> is **not** a <Euclidean relation>. Choose $x=1$, $y=3$, $z=2$. Then $1\le3$ and $1\le2$, but $3\not\le2$. Thus

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <divisibility> <binary relation> on positive <natural numbers> is **not** a <Euclidean relation>. With $x=1$, $y=2$, $z=3$, we have $1\mid2$ and $1\mid3$ but $2\nmid3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mid\text{ is not Euclidean}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Equality of <remainders> after division by eight is a <Euclidean relation>. If $x$, $y$ have the same remainder and $x$, $z$ have the same remainder, then $y$, $z$ have the same remainder. In the notation of <modular arithmetic>,
$$
x\equiv y\pmod8,\quad x\equiv z\pmod8\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y\equiv z\pmod8.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{MOD}\ 8\text{ is Euclidean}.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Inequality on a domain containing at least two people is **not** a <Euclidean relation>. Choose distinct people $a,b$ and put $x=a$, $y=z=b$. Both premises $a\ne b$ hold, but the conclusion would be $b\ne b$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\ne\text{ is not Euclidean on the domain of people}.}
$$
The possibility $y=z$ matters: the defining <universal quantifiers> do not require distinct arguments. On a domain with at most one element the inequality <binary relation> is empty and the condition holds by <vacuous truth>.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

The “same age” <binary relation> is a <Euclidean relation>, with age understood as one consistently measured value at a fixed time. If $x$ has the same age as both $y$ and $z$, the ages of $y$ and $z$ are equal. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{same age is Euclidean}.}
$$
This is another instance of equality of the values of a <function> inducing an <equivalence relation>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on a <set> $X$ is an <equivalence relation> when it is a <reflexive relation>, a <symmetric relation>, and a <transitive relation>. In <first-order logic>, these three requirements are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
&\forall x\in X\;xRx,\\
&\forall x,y\in X\;(xRy\Rightarrow yRx),\\
&\forall x,y,z\in X\;((xRy\land yRz)\Rightarrow xRz).
\end{aligned}}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose $R$ is an <equivalence relation>, and $xRy$, $xRz$. Since it is a <symmetric relation>, $yRx$. Since it is a <transitive relation>, $yRx$ together with $xRz$ implies $yRz$. This proves
$$
\boxed{R\text{ an equivalence relation}\Longrightarrow R\text{ Euclidean}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose $R$ is a <reflexive relation> and a <Euclidean relation>. To prove it is a <symmetric relation>, assume $xRy$. Reflexivity gives $xRx$, so applying the Euclidean implication to $xRy$ and $xRx$ gives $yRx$.

To prove it is a <transitive relation>, assume $xRy$ and $yRz$. The symmetry just established gives $yRx$. Apply the Euclidean implication with common predecessor $y$ to $yRx$ and $yRz$; the conclusion is $xRz$. Thus all three requirements hold:
$$
\boxed{R\text{ reflexive and Euclidean}\Longrightarrow R\text{ an equivalence relation}.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

On the nonempty <set> $X=\{a\}$ take the empty <binary relation> $R=\varnothing$. There are no triples for which $xRy\land xRz$ holds. The defining implication is therefore true for every triple by <vacuous truth>, making $R$ a <Euclidean relation>.

But $aRa$ is false, so $R$ is not a <reflexive relation> and hence is not an <equivalence relation>:
$$
\boxed{(X,R)=(\{a\},\varnothing)\text{ is a counterexample}.}
$$

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

The answer is **no**, even when both original <binary relations> are <equivalence relations>. On $X=\{a,b,c\}$, let $R_1$ have <equivalence classes> $\{a,b\},\{c\}$ and $R_2$ have <equivalence classes> $\{a\},\{b,c\}$. Each is a <transitive relation> and, by the earlier proof, a <Euclidean relation>.

Use exactly the paper's convention for <composition of relations>: $xRy$ means $xR_1z$ and $zR_2y$ for some $z$. The possible successors are
$$
R(a)=R(b)=\{a,b,c\},\qquad R(c)=\{b,c\}.
$$
Indeed, from $a$ or $b$ the first step can reach $a$ or $b$, whose $R_2$ successors together exhaust $X$; from $c$ the first step must stay at $c$, whose $R_2$ successors are only $b,c$. Consequently $cRb$ and $bRa$, but not $cRa$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{R_1\text{ and }R_2\text{ transitive and Euclidean does not force }R\text{ transitive}.}
$$

==== Solution

First, the <product order> is a <partial order>. Reflexivity and transitivity hold in each coordinate. If $(p,q)\le_R(p',q')$ and $(p',q')\le_R(p,q)$, antisymmetry in $P,Q$ gives $p=p'$ and $q=q'$, proving antisymmetry in the product.

Let $D$ be a nonempty <directed set> in $P\times Q$. Its coordinate projections
$$
D_P=\{p:\exists q\;(p,q)\in D\},\qquad D_Q=\{q:\exists p\;(p,q)\in D\}
$$
are <directed sets>. For example, if $p_1,p_2\in D_P$, choose $(p_1,q_1),(p_2,q_2)\in D$. Directedness supplies $(p_3,q_3)\in D$ above both, so $p_3\in D_P$ is a common <upper bound> of $p_1,p_2$ inside $D_P$. The argument for $D_Q$ is identical.

By the assumed <directed-complete partial order> property, $p_* =\sup D_P$ and $q_* =\sup D_Q$ exist. Every $(p,q)\in D$ satisfies $p\le_Pp_*$ and $q\le_Qq_*$, so $(p_*,q_*)$ is an <upper bound> of $D$. If $(u,v)$ is any other <upper bound>, then $u$ bounds $D_P$ and $v$ bounds $D_Q$. Hence $p_*\le_Pu$, $q_*\le_Qv$, proving leastness. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sup D=(\sup D_P,\sup D_Q),\qquad P\times Q\text{ is directed-complete}.}
$$
The printed definition of a <directed set> also permits $D=\varnothing$.<Calculus, probability, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2003)>[Printed p. 5, definitions preceding Question 7]{ref} Under that convention, the hypotheses require $P,Q$ to have <least elements>, namely the <least upper bounds> of their empty subsets. Their pair is the <least element> of the product and is $\sup\varnothing$ there. This handles the empty case as well; under the convention restricting <directed sets> to nonempty sets, only the preceding nonempty argument is needed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the five-element <partially ordered set> $P=\{\bot,a,b,u,v\}$. Its strict comparisons are $\bot<a$, $\bot<b$, and $a<u$, $a<v$, $b<u$, $b<v$, together with their transitive consequences. The elements $a,b$ are incomparable, as are $u,v$.

\Image[math-prelims-2003-completeness-counterexamples.png]
{title=Two different failures of order completeness}

A <finite directed set has a greatest element> whenever it is nonempty: list its elements and repeatedly use directedness to find an internal <upper bound> of the previously chosen element and the next one. The final element is above every member of the <directed set>, and is its <least upper bound>. Thus every nonempty <directed set> in $P$ has a <least upper bound>. The empty subset also has a <least upper bound>, namely $\bot$. Therefore $P$ satisfies the paper's completeness condition under either convention.

However, $\{a,b\}$ is bounded above by both $u$ and $v$. A <least upper bound> would have to be an <upper bound> lying below both $u$ and $v$. Neither $u$ nor $v$ has that property, and none of $\bot,a,b$ is an <upper bound> of $\{a,b\}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{P\text{ is directed-complete but not bounded-complete}.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the <natural numbers> including zero with their usual <total order>:
$$
\boxed{P=(\{0,1,2,\ldots\},\le).}
$$
A nonempty subset with an <upper bound> $m\in P$ is contained in the finite <set> $\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$. It therefore has a <greatest element>, which is its <least upper bound>. The empty subset has <least upper bound> $0$. Thus $P$ is a <bounded-complete partially ordered set>.

But $D=P$ is a <directed set>, since $\max(x,y)\in P$ is above any two members. It has no <upper bound> in $P$: for any proposed bound $m$, the member $m+1$ is larger. It therefore has no <least upper bound>. Hence
$$
\boxed{P\text{ is bounded-complete but not directed-complete}.}
$$

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With all variables ranging over the domain of the <partially ordered set>, the three <first-order logic> formulas are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{reflexivity: }&\forall x\;R(x,x),\\
\text{antisymmetry: }&\forall x\forall y\;((R(x,y)\land R(y,x))\Rightarrow x=y),\\
\text{transitivity: }&\forall x\forall y\forall z\;((R(x,y)\land R(y,z))\Rightarrow R(x,z)).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The middle formula defines an <antisymmetric relation>; it does not forbid $R(x,y)$ for distinct $x,y$, but forbids both directions simultaneously for distinct elements.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mathbf A$ be the one-element <partially ordered set> $\{a\}$ with $R(a,a)$. Taking $x=a$ witnesses the <existential quantifier>, and the only $y$ is comparable with $a$. Thus $\mathbf A\models\varphi$.

Let $\mathbf B$ be the two-element <antichain> $\{a,b\}$, with $R$ consisting only of $(a,a)$ and $(b,b)$. This is a <partial order>. If $x=a$, choose $y=b$, and if $x=b$, choose $y=a$. In either case both $R(x,y)$ and $R(y,x)$ are false. Therefore no witness for the <existential quantifier> exists:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A=(\{a\},=)\models\varphi,\qquad\mathbf B=(\{a,b\},=)\not\models\varphi.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In the language of <first-order logic>, define
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Bottom}(b)\;\equiv\;\forall y\;R(b,y),\qquad\operatorname{Minimal}(b)\;\equiv\;\forall y\;(R(y,b)\Rightarrow y=b).}
$$
Thus a <least element> lies below every element, whereas a <minimal element> merely has no distinct element below it. The latter definition allows multiple incomparable <minimal elements>.

Here is a formal <natural deduction> proof, with the antisymmetry axiom available as a premise. Assume $\operatorname{Bottom}(b)$ and take a fresh arbitrary variable $t$. Assume $R(t,b)$. By <universal elimination> applied to $\operatorname{Bottom}(b)$, obtain $R(b,t)$. By <conjunction introduction>, obtain $R(t,b)\land R(b,t)$. Instantiate antisymmetry at $t,b$, and use <modus ponens rule> to obtain $t=b$. Discharge the assumption $R(t,b)$ by <implication introduction>, obtaining $R(t,b)\Rightarrow t=b$. Since $t$ was arbitrary and is free in no undischarged assumption, <universal introduction> gives $\operatorname{Minimal}(b)$. Discharge the initial assumption and universally quantify $b$ to conclude
$$
\boxed{\forall b\;\bigl(\operatorname{Bottom}(b)\Rightarrow\operatorname{Minimal}(b)\bigr).}
$$
Only antisymmetry was needed for this implication; reflexivity and transitivity are part of the ambient <partial order> axioms.

==== Solution

The assertion is **true**. Every single finite <string> $w=b_1\cdots b_k$ over $\{0,1\}$ is denoted by the <regular expression> $b_1\cdots b_k$. The empty <string> is denoted by $\epsilon$. Hence if $F=\{w_1,\ldots,w_m\}$ is a finite <formal language>, the <regular expression>
$$
\boxed{w_1+w_2+\cdots+w_m}
$$
denotes precisely $F$, with each $w_j$ read as its concatenation of symbols. This uses the defining union and concatenation operations for <regular expressions>. For $F=\varnothing$, use the <regular expression> $\varnothing$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Every finite binary language is regular}.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The assertion is **false**. Use the <pumping lemma for regular languages>: if $L$ is a <regular language>, there is $p\ge1$ such that every $w\in L$ with $|w|\ge p$ admits a factorization $w=xyz$ with $|xy|\le p$, $|y|\ge1$, and $xy^kz\in L$ for every integer $k\ge0$.

Suppose the stated <formal language> were a <regular language>, and take $w=0^{2p}1^p$. This <string> has twice as many zeros as ones, so it belongs to $L$. In every allowed factorization, the first $p$ positions are all zeros, forcing $y=0^d$ with $1\le d\le p$. Pumping down with $k=0$ produces
$$
xz=0^{2p-d}1^p.
$$
It still has $p$ ones but fewer than $2p$ zeros, contradicting the <pumping lemma for regular languages>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\{w:|w|_0=2|w|_1\}\text{ is not regular}.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The assertion is **false**. Suppose the <formal language> $L=\{ww:w\in\{0,1\}^*\}$ were a <regular language>, and let $p$ be its constant from the <pumping lemma for regular languages>. Consider
$$
t=0^p1\,0^p1\in L.
$$
In every decomposition $t=xyz$ with $|xy|\le p$ and $|y|\ge1$, the pumped factor is $y=0^d$ with $1\le d\le p$. Pumping down gives
$$
xz=0^{p-d}1\,0^p1.
$$
This <string> has exactly two ones and ends in a one. If it were $vv$, each copy of $v$ would contain exactly one one, and each copy would end in a one. The first copy would consequently be $0^{p-d}1$, while the second would be $0^p1$. They have different lengths because $d>0$, so they cannot be equal. This contradiction to the <pumping lemma for regular languages> proves
$$
\boxed{\{ww:w\in\{0,1\}^*\}\text{ is not regular}.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The assertion is **true**. Write $A=L(\alpha)$ and $B=L(\beta)$. The semantics of <regular expressions> reduce the claim to
$$
(A^*B^*)^*=(A\cup B)^*,
$$
where juxtaposition is <concatenation of formal languages> and a star is the <Kleene star>.

Every element of $A^*B^*$ is a finite concatenation of members of $A\cup B$, so belongs to $(A\cup B)^*$. Concatenating any finite number of such elements still belongs to $(A\cup B)^*$, proving $(A^*B^*)^*\subseteq(A\cup B)^*$.

Conversely, $A\subseteq A^*B^*$ because one may take a single member of $A$ followed by the empty <string> from $B^*$. Likewise $B\subseteq A^*B^*$ by taking the empty <string> from $A^*$. Hence any finite concatenation of members of $A\cup B$ lies in $(A^*B^*)^*$. The empty <string> belongs to both sides. Thus
$$
\boxed{L((\alpha^*\beta^*)^*)=L((\alpha+\beta)^*).}
$$

===== Solution

A <context-free grammar> with start symbol $P$, terminal <alphabet> $\{0,1\}$, and the following productions generates all binary <palindromes>:
$$
\boxed{P\longrightarrow\epsilon\mid0\mid1\mid0P0\mid1P1.}
$$
Each production preserves the <palindrome> property: it either supplies a central <string> of length zero or one, or wraps the same symbol around a shorter <palindrome>. This proves soundness by <structural induction> on the <parse tree>.

Conversely, any <palindrome> of length at least two has equal first and last symbols. Remove them to obtain a shorter <palindrome>, apply <mathematical induction> on length, and then use the corresponding wrapping production. The three base cases are $\epsilon$, $0$, and $1$, proving completeness.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use start symbol $S$ and an auxiliary symbol $A$ in the <context-free grammar>
$$
\boxed{S\longrightarrow A1A1A1A,\qquad A\longrightarrow0A\mid1A\mid\epsilon.}
$$
The symbol $A$ generates exactly $\{0,1\}^*$, so every generated <string> has the three explicitly displayed ones. Conversely, any <string> with at least three ones can be split at any three of them as $u_0\,1\,u_1\,1\,u_2\,1\,u_3$, and each $u_j$ can be generated by $A$. This proves both inclusions. For example,
$$
S\Rightarrow A1A1A1A\Rightarrow^*00101110
$$
by taking the four $A$ substrings to be $00$, $0$, $1$, and $0$ respectively.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the following <context-free grammar>, with start symbol $S$:
$$
\boxed{S\longrightarrow\epsilon\mid S0\mid S1B0,\qquad B\longrightarrow\epsilon\mid B1B0.}
$$
The symbol $B$ generates the balanced members of the <suffix-nonnegative binary language>, while $S$ permits extra zeros. To prove the claim and prepare the ambiguity argument, reverse every generated <string>. The reversed productions are
$$
U\longrightarrow\epsilon\mid0U\mid0D1U,\qquad D\longrightarrow\epsilon\mid0D1D.
$$
Here $U$ represents the reversals of the $S$ <strings>, and $D$ represents the reversals of the $B$ <strings>. A <string> belongs to the desired <formal language> precisely when its reversal has no <string prefix> with more ones than zeros. Associate a path with each reversed <string>, counting a zero as a step $+1$ and a one as a step $-1$.

The nonterminal $D$ generates exactly the paths that stay nonnegative and finish at zero. The production $0D1D$ consists of an initial up-step, a balanced path one level higher, a down-step to zero, and another balanced path. Conversely, any nonempty balanced nonnegative path has a first return to zero, and splitting there gives exactly this form. <Mathematical induction> on length proves the characterization.

The productions for $U$ generate nonnegative paths: $0U$ stays strictly above zero after its first step, while $0D1U$ first executes a balanced excursion and then another nonnegative path. Conversely, a nonempty nonnegative path starts with zero. If it never returns to height zero, removing that initial zero leaves a nonnegative path, giving $0U$. If it does return, split at its first return to obtain $0D1U$. These shorter components can be generated by <mathematical induction>. This proves that the original <context-free grammar> generates exactly the required <formal language>.

For instance, in the original <context-free grammar>,
$$
S\Rightarrow S1B0\Rightarrow S01B0\Rightarrow01B0\Rightarrow01B1B00\Rightarrow011B00\Rightarrow01100,
$$
while $S\Rightarrow S1B0\Rightarrow S1B01B0\Rightarrow^*1010$. The <string> $110$ is excluded since its entire length is a <string suffix> having two ones and only one zero.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <context-free grammar> is an <ambiguous grammar> if some terminal <string> has two distinct <parse trees>, equivalently two distinct leftmost derivations. Different orders of expanding independent nonterminals do not by themselves constitute ambiguity.

The <context-free grammar> just given is **unambiguous**. It suffices to prove this for the reversed productions, because reversing a <parse tree> preserves whether two <parse trees> are distinct.

For $D$, the empty <string> forces the empty production. A nonempty <string> must use $D\to0D1D$. In any resulting nonnegative balanced path, the displayed one is the unique first return to zero. It therefore uniquely determines the two shorter substrings generated by $D$. <Mathematical induction> on length makes both smaller <parse trees> unique.

For $U$, the empty <string> again forces the empty production. A nonempty path with no return to zero must use $U\to0U$, since $0D1U$ always returns to zero. A path with a return must use $U\to0D1U$, since $0U$ stays positive after its first step. In the latter case, the first return uniquely determines the $D$ substring and the remaining $U$ substring. Applying <mathematical induction> to these shorter substrings proves uniqueness throughout. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{The grammar in part (b)(i) is unambiguous}.}
$$
