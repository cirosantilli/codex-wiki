=== a

==== Solution

Reduction of both coefficients in <modular arithmetic> gives
$$
754=8\cdot91+26,\qquad -507=-6\cdot91+39.
$$
More explicitly,
$$
754x+507=91(8x+6)+(26x-39).
$$
The first expression is divisible by $91$ exactly when the last parenthesis is. By the definition of a <integer congruence>, **the two congruences are equivalent**.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
91=3\cdot26+13,\qquad26=2\cdot13,
$$
so $\gcd(754,91)=\gcd(26,91)=13$. Since $13\mid39$, the criterion for a <linear congruence> guarantees a solution. One can also exhibit it directly: $26\cdot5+91(-1)=39$, or in the original coefficients,
$$
754\cdot5+91(-47)=-507.
$$
Thus **$x=5$ is a solution**, with an explicit <Bézout coefficient> certificate.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Divide the <divisibility> relation $91\mid26x-39$ by $13$ to obtain $7\mid2x-3$. Since $2\cdot4\equiv1\pmod7$, multiplication by the <modular multiplicative inverse> gives $x\equiv12\equiv5\pmod7$. Therefore all <integer> solutions are $x=5+7q$, $q\in\mathbb Z$.

Modulo $91=13\cdot7$, the choices $q=0,\ldots,12$ give
$$
\boxed{x\equiv5,12,19,26,33,40,47,54,61,68,75,82,89\pmod{91}.}
$$
They are distinct <residue classes>: equality of two such classes would imply $91\mid7(q-q')$, hence $13\mid q-q'$. Every <integer> $q$ is congruent to one of those thirteen choices, so the list is exhaustive.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $d=\gcd(c,n)$, and write $c=dc'$, $n=dn'$ with positive <integers> $c',n'$. If an <integer> $e>1$ divided both $c'$ and $n'$, then $de$ would divide both $c$ and $n$, contradicting the maximality of their <greatest common divisor> $d$. Thus **$\gcd(c',n')=1$**. Equivalently, <Bézout's identity> for $c,n$ can be divided by $d$ to express $1$ as an <integer> combination of $c',n'$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $d=\gcd(c,n)$, $c=dc'$, and $n=dn'$. The preceding part gives $\gcd(c',n')=1$. The <integer congruence> on the left is equivalent to
$$
n\mid c(a-b)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
n'\mid c'(a-b).
$$
By <Euclid's lemma>, coprimality of $c'$ and $n'$ makes this equivalent to $n'\mid a-b$. The reverse implication is also immediate by multiplying a <divisibility> relation by $dc'$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{ac\equiv bc\pmod n
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a\equiv b\pmod{n/\gcd(c,n)}.}
$$
This is <cancellation of a common factor in a congruence>. The printed equality sign in the right-hand expression is interpreted as equality of <residue classes>, hence the displayed <integer congruence>. Ordinary equality of the <integers> $a,b$ would be false: the following example has $a\ne b$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take **$(a,b,c,n)=(1,4,2,6)$**. These are four distinct positive <integers> and $n\geq3$. Their products are $ac=2$ and $bc=8$, whose difference is $6$, so $ac\equiv bc\pmod6$. Here the correct reduced modulus is $6/\gcd(2,6)=3$, and indeed $1\equiv4\pmod3$. The example illustrates why cancelling a noninvertible factor may change the modulus in <modular arithmetic>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For the base case $n=2$, the product is $1-1/4=3/4=(2+1)/(2\cdot2)$. Assume the identity holds for some $n\geq2$. Multiplying by the next factor gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\prod_{i=2}^{n+1}\left(1-\frac1{i^2}\right)
&=\frac{n+1}{2n}\left(1-\frac1{(n+1)^2}\right)\\
&=\frac{n+1}{2n}\frac{n(n+2)}{(n+1)^2}
=\frac{n+2}{2(n+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the desired expression with $n$ replaced by $n+1$, so <mathematical induction> proves the identity for every $n\geq2$. The factorization $1-i^{-2}=(i-1)(i+1)/i^2$ also explains the cancellation as a <telescoping product>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n=1$, both sides are $1/3$. Assuming the formula through $n$, adding the next term gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac1{(2i-1)(2i+1)}
&=\frac{n}{2n+1}+\frac1{(2n+1)(2n+3)}\\
&=\frac{2n^2+3n+1}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}
=\frac{n+1}{2n+3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last expression is the required formula at $n+1$, completing <mathematical induction>. The <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
\frac1{(2i-1)(2i+1)}=\frac12\left(\frac1{2i-1}-\frac1{2i+1}\right)
$$
provides a second explanation: this is a <telescoping sum>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose, for a <proof by contradiction>, that an <integer> $n$ is both an <even number> and an <odd number>. Then $n=2u$ and $n=2v+1$ for some <integers> $u,v$. Subtracting yields $u-v=1/2$. The left side is an <integer>, while $1/2$ is not. This contradiction proves that **no <integer> is both even and odd**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <contrapositive> is that an <integer> <perfect square> has residue $0$ or $1$ modulo $4$. Let $n=t^2$ for an <integer> $t$. If $t=2u$ is an <even number>, then $t^2=4u^2\equiv0\pmod4$. If $t=2u+1$ is an <odd number>, then $t^2=4u(u+1)+1\equiv1\pmod4$. These exhaust the possible parities of an <integer>. Thus a residue of $2$ or $3$ excludes being a <perfect square>, as required by the <contrapositive>.

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(1,2)$. Their first coordinates are equal, so both $uRv$ and $vRu$ hold, although $u\ne v$. Thus $R$ is not an <antisymmetric relation>, and hence **it is not a partial order**. The failure arises because its comparisons ignore the second coordinate entirely.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Check each <partial order> axiom. For every $(a,b)$, $a\leq a$ and $b\geq b$, so $S$ is a <reflexive relation>. If both $(a,b)S(c,d)$ and $(c,d)S(a,b)$, the first coordinates satisfy $a\leq c\leq a$ and the second coordinates satisfy $b\geq d\geq b$. Therefore $a=c$ and $b=d$, which proves $S$ is an <antisymmetric relation>.

Finally, if $(a,b)S(c,d)$ and $(c,d)S(e,f)$, then $a\leq c\leq e$ and $b\geq d\geq f$. Hence $(a,b)S(e,f)$, making $S$ a <transitive relation>. All three axioms hold, so **$S$ is a partial order**. It is a <product order>, with the usual order on the first coordinate and the reversed order on the second.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <subset> is determined uniquely by deciding, for each of the $n$ elements, whether to include it. Each decision has two possibilities. By the <rule of product>, there are $2\cdot2\cdots2=2^n$ decision strings. The correspondence between such binary strings and <subsets> is a <bijection>, so **$|\mathcal P(\mathcal U)|=2^n$**. The all-zero string corresponds to the empty <subset>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $q\geq1$, choose an ordered list of $q$ distinct elements: there are $n(n-1)\cdots(n-q+1)$ such lists. Every $q$-element <subset> occurs in exactly $q!$ orders, the number of <permutations> of its elements. Thus the count is the <binomial coefficient>
$$
\boxed{\binom nq=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-q+1)}{q!}.}
$$
For fixed $q$ and $n\geq2q$, every numerator factor lies between $n/2$ and $n$. Consequently
$$
\frac{n^q}{2^q q!}\leq\binom nq\leq\frac{n^q}{q!}.
$$
The constants depend on the fixed $q$, not on $n$. These inequalities prove $\binom nq=\Theta(n^q)$ in <big theta notation>. More precisely, dividing the product by $n^q$ gives the <fixed-cardinality binomial coefficient asymptotic> $\binom nq\sim n^q/q!$. For $q=0$, there is exactly one empty <subset>, and the assertion is $1=\Theta(1)$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $q=1,\ldots,5$, choose the first $q$ elements of $\{1,2,4,8,16\}$. Every <sum of a subset> has a unique <binary representation>, so distinct <subsets> cannot have the same sum. To see this directly, take the largest power of two belonging to exactly one of two proposed <subsets>. It exceeds the sum of all smaller powers, and therefore cannot be cancelled by their difference. Thus these chosen sets are <dissociated sets> and do not have the two-sum property.

If cardinality zero is included, the empty <set> has only one <subset>, so it cannot supply two distinct ones either. **There is a <counterexample> at every cardinality from $0$ through $5$.**

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix any seven-element <subset> $S$. Its <power set> supplies $2^7=128$ pigeons. Map each <subset> to its <integer> sum. Since the elements are positive, all such sums lie between $0$ and $\sum_{s\in S}s$. The latter is at most the sum of the seven largest available elements,
$$
10+11+\cdots+16=91.
$$
There are therefore at most $92$ possible sums, fewer than the $128$ <subsets>. The <pigeonhole principle> forces two distinct <subsets> to have the same sum. **Every seven-element subset has the two-sum property.** This is the <subset-sum pigeonhole bound>. The weaker estimate $\sum_{s\in S}s\leq7\cdot16=112$ would also suffice, since $128>113$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The number is
$$
\boxed{\binom{16}{6}=8008.}
$$
A direct search over every $q$-element <subset> must inspect $\binom nq$ candidates. Testing one candidate by generating its <subset sums> creates $2^q$ values; storing previously seen sums detects a collision. Generating them incrementally needs $O(2^q)$ arithmetic operations, or $O(q2^q)$ for the straightforward independent calculation of each sum. Thus the simple overall search has cost on the scale of $\binom nq2^q$, apart from <integer>-arithmetic and data-structure costs.

The important <time complexity> distinction is that **fixed $q$ gives polynomial growth in $n$, of degree $q$**, but this can still become impractically large. If $q$ increases with $n$, both the candidate count and the internal <power set> can grow exponentially. For example, near $q=n/2$ the <binomial coefficient> is already exponentially large. An exhaustive implementation can therefore encounter a <combinatorial explosion>; the fixed-$q$ estimate $\Theta(n^q)$ must not be described as exponential in $n$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the standard spacing convention for <machine epsilon>: the next representable <floating-point number> above $1$ is $1+2^{-40}$. In a normalized binary representation this corresponds to $41$ significant binary digits, including the leading digit. The spacing throughout $[1,2)$ is therefore $2^{-40}$.

With <rounding> to nearest, the associated <unit roundoff> is half that spacing, $u=2^{-41}$; for a normal result the relative <round-off error> is bounded by $u$. Some texts instead call $u$ machine epsilon. **Here $2^{-40}$ denotes the spacing scale**, and the iteration counts below use that convention. It describes representation precision, not a guarantee that an arbitrarily evaluated <function> has a relative error this small.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The second-order remainder form of <Taylor's theorem> gives, for a point $\xi_x$ between $0$ and $x$,
$$
f(x)=-5+3x+\tfrac12f''(\xi_x)x^2.
$$
For $x\ne0$, the given curvature bounds imply
$$
-5+3x+x^2\leq f(x)<-5+3x+\tfrac32x^2.
$$
In particular $f(1)<-1/2<0$ and $f(2)\geq5>0$. Differentiability implies <continuity>, so the <intermediate value theorem> gives a root in $(1,2)$.

The <mean value theorem> applied to $f'$ gives $f'(x)=3+xf''(\eta_x)\geq3+2x>0$ for $x\in(1,2)$. Thus $f$ is strictly increasing there, and it cannot have two roots. **There is exactly one root in $(1,2)$.**

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At a root of size between $1$ and $2$, the spacing of <floating-point numbers> is $\epsilon=2^{-40}$. With ordinary stable <function> evaluations, a sensible generic absolute position tolerance has scale **$2^{-40}\approx9.09\times10^{-13}$**; sub-spacing progress cannot generally be represented. This is a resolution estimate, not a lower bound excluding an accidentally exactly representable root.

Define one <bisection method> step as replacing the current bracket by the half retaining the sign change. Starting from $[1,2]$, the width after $N$ such updates is $2^{-N}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{N=40\quad\text{for bracket width at most }2^{-40}.}
$$
The midpoint of a bracket has an exact-arithmetic error bound of half its width. If tolerance is defined instead as that midpoint error bound, $39$ updates suffice to make it at most $2^{-40}$. Stating the convention avoids an off-by-one difference; once the endpoints are adjacent <floating-point numbers>, their exact midpoint need not itself be representable.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the <mean value theorem>, the first-order case of <Taylor's theorem>, to $f'$ between $0$ and $x>1$. It yields
$$
f'(x)=3+xf''(\xi)\geq3+2x>5.
$$
Also $f(3/2)\geq-5+9/2+9/4=7/4>0$, so the initial point is to the right of the root $x^*$. For an increasing <strictly convex function>, its tangent at a point to the right of a root meets the axis between that root and the point. Hence the exact <Newton root-finding iteration> remains in $[x^*,3/2]$ and decreases towards $x^*$.

On every relevant <interval>, $|f''|<3$ and $f'>5$, so the stated <quadratic convergence> bound gives, with $e_n=|x_n-x^*|$,
$$
e_{n+1}\leq\tfrac3{10}e_n^2,\qquad e_0<\tfrac12.
$$
Iterating the bound gives
$$
e_1<0.075,\quad e_2<0.0016875,\quad
 e_3<8.543\times10^{-7},\quad e_4<2.190\times10^{-13}<2^{-40}.
$$
Thus **four Newton corrections suffice to reduce the exact-iteration error bound below the floating-point spacing scale**. The <quadratic convergence> doubles the approximate number of correct digits at each late step. Beyond this point further corrections cannot reliably improve a generic floating-point answer. This is a bound on useful accuracy under the usual stable-evaluation model, not a claim that the data specify the exact iteration at which every implementation must stagnate.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For negative $x$, the <mean value theorem> gives $f'(x)=3+xf''(\xi_x)$. In particular,
$$
f'(-2)\leq-1<0,\qquad f'(-1)>0.
$$
Since $f'$ is <continuous> and strictly increasing, it vanishes at exactly one point $x_m\in(-2,-1)$. The positive <second derivative> makes $f$ a <strictly convex function> on the entire real line. Its <derivative> changes from negative to positive at $x_m$, so **$x_m$ is the unique <global minimum>**. One may also use $f(x)\geq x^2+3x-5\to\infty$ to verify <coercivity>.

In the endpoint-bracketing form of <golden-section search>, begin with **$[-2,-1]$**, a width-one <interval> known to contain this unique minimum, and place the two standard interior probes. The attainable position accuracy is different from that for a simple root. Around $x_m$, <Taylor's theorem> gives
$$
f(x_m+h)-f(x_m)=\tfrac12f''(\xi)h^2.
$$
Here the minimum value is nonzero and of order one: the lower quadratic bound gives $f(x_m)\geq-29/4$, while $f(x_m)\leq f(-1)<-13/2$. Ordinary relative <round-off error> in these <function> values therefore hides changes of order $\epsilon$, once $h^2$ has that size. The <function-value resolution near a nondegenerate minimum> is consequently of order
$$
\boxed{|h|\sim\sqrt\epsilon=2^{-20}\approx9.54\times10^{-7}.}
$$
Each <golden-section search> contraction multiplies the bracket width by $\rho=(\sqrt5-1)/2$. Using $2^{-20}$ as the nominal target width gives
$$
N\geq\frac{20\log2}{-\log\rho}=28.81\ldots,
\qquad\boxed{N=29\text{ contractions}.}
$$
Unknown evaluation-error constants can change the actual stopping point; $29$ is the standard scale-based count. Requiring $2^{-40}$ position accuracy from these <function>-value comparisons would disregard the quadratic flatness of the minimum. The same precision effect is documented for one-dimensional numerical minimization.<One dimensional minimization in the GNU Scientific Library manual>[Caveats: Taylor expansion near the minimizer and the square-root precision limit; Minimization Algorithms: golden-section contraction]{ref}

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Separate inactive and active <optimization constraints>. At an interior feasible point with $h>0$, a differentiable <local minimum> must satisfy $\nabla f=0$. Retain only solutions lying in the interior.

On the boundary $h=0$, if $\nabla h\ne0$, apply the <Lagrange multiplier> condition. Using the <optimization Lagrangian> $L=f-\lambda h$, the <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> are
$$
\nabla f=\lambda\nabla h,\qquad h\geq0,\qquad
\lambda\geq0,\qquad\lambda h=0.
$$
The sign follows from feasible motion into $h>0$: the directional <derivative> along $\nabla h$ at a minimum cannot be negative. Thus a boundary candidate has $h=0$ and a nonnegative <Lagrange multiplier>. Boundary points with $\nabla h=0$ must be examined separately, because the usual constraint qualification fails there.

Solve the interior and boundary systems, check membership in the <feasible set>, and establish which candidates are minima using second-order tests or a direct global comparison. The <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> alone are not sufficient for general nonconvex <functions> or <feasible sets>. On an unbounded <feasible set>, also check whether an infimum is attained and whether escaping <sequences> can improve the objective.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Complete the square to write the objective as
$$
x^2-2x+y^2-4y=\|(x,y)-(1,2)\|^2-5.
$$
Let $z=(x,y)$ and $a=(1,2)$, so $\|a\|=\sqrt5$ while feasibility requires $\|z\|\geq\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5$. The <reverse triangle inequality> gives
$$
\|z-a\|\geq\|z\|-\|a\|\geq\sqrt5.
$$
The objective is therefore at least zero. Equality is attained by $z=2a=(2,4)$, which lies on the boundary. Equality requires the <vectors> to have the same direction and $\|z\|=2\sqrt5$, so this minimizer is unique. Hence
$$
\boxed{(x_*,y_*)=(2,4),\qquad f_*=0.}
$$
It also satisfies the <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> with $\lambda=1/2$. The direct distance argument proves global optimality even though the exterior of a disk is not a <convex set>; this is <nearest-point minimization outside a Euclidean ball>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The objective is <continuous> on the compact disk, so a <global maximum> exists. Its <gradient> is $(e^x,2e^{2y})$, which is never zero, so a maximum cannot lie in the interior. It must have $x^2+y^2=1$.

Both coordinates of a maximizing point are positive. Reflecting a negative coordinate to its positive counterpart strictly increases the objective without changing the constraint; at an endpoint with one coordinate zero, a small move along the circle into the positive quadrant also increases the objective to first order. Thus it suffices to consider $x>0,y>0$.

The boundary <Lagrange multiplier> equations are $e^x=2\lambda x$ and $2e^{2y}=2\lambda y$. Eliminate $\lambda$ to obtain the two-dimensional nonlinear <system of equations>
$$
\boxed{F(x,y)=
\begin{pmatrix}x^2+y^2-1\\ y e^x-2x e^{2y}\end{pmatrix}=0,
\qquad x>0,\ y>0.}
$$
Keeping this quadrant restriction excludes the boundary minimum that an unconstrained elimination could also produce. Under the stated uniqueness assumption for the admissible solution, the <global maximum> must be that solution. Approximate it with <multivariable Newton iteration>, solving $J_F(z_n)\Delta_n=-F(z_n)$ and setting $z_{n+1}=z_n+\Delta_n$, with a suitable safeguard if needed. For example its explicit <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J_F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}
2x&2y\\
y e^x-2e^{2y}&e^x-4x e^{2y}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
No numerical solution of the system is required.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <optimization Lagrangian>
$$
L=x^TAx+y^TBy-\lambda(\mathbf1^Tx+\mathbf1^Ty-1).
$$
Symmetry gives the <gradient> equations $2Ax=\lambda\mathbf1$ and $2By=\lambda\mathbf1$. The <positive-definite matrices> are invertible, so define
$$
D=\mathbf1^TA^{-1}\mathbf1+\mathbf1^TB^{-1}\mathbf1>0.
$$
The affine constraint gives $\lambda=2/D$, hence
$$
\boxed{x_* =\frac{A^{-1}\mathbf1}{D},\qquad
 y_* =\frac{B^{-1}\mathbf1}{D},\qquad f_* =\frac1D.}
$$
For a global certificate, write any other feasible point as $(x_*+u,y_*+v)$. Feasibility implies $\mathbf1^Tu+\mathbf1^Tv=0$. Expansion cancels the cross terms and leaves
$$
f(x_*+u,y_*+v)-f(x_*,y_*)=u^TAu+v^TBv.
$$
By positive definiteness this is positive unless $u=v=0$. Thus the solution is the **unique <global minimum>**, not merely a <stationary point>. This is a two-block instance of <positive quadratic minimization under one affine constraint>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $r=x-a$. The squared <Euclidean norm> is $r^Tr$, whose <gradient> is $2r$. The <chain rule> for the <exponential function> therefore gives the column <gradient>
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x)=2e^{\|x-a\|^2}(x-a).}
$$
The row <derivative>, if that convention is used for $df/dx$, is its transpose.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiate the column <gradient> $2e^{r^Tr}r$. The <Jacobian matrix> product rule gives one term from differentiating the scalar <exponential function> and one from differentiating $r$, whose <Jacobian matrix> is the <identity matrix>:
$$
\boxed{H_f(x)=e^{\|r\|^2}(4rr^T+2I).}
$$
In particular, for any nonzero <vector> $v$,
$$
v^TH_f(x)v=e^{\|r\|^2}\bigl(4(r^Tv)^2+2\|v\|^2\bigr)>0.
$$
Thus the <Hessian matrix> is positive definite everywhere. This calculation proves strict <convexity> and supplies the <derivatives> needed for sums of these terms.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A practical choice is a <BFGS method>, using exact <gradients> and a <line search>. At dimension $d=100$, its $d$-by-$d$ inverse-<Hessian matrix> approximation is small, whereas forming a million rank-one contributions to the exact <Hessian matrix> is expensive. If $F(x)=\sum_i e^{\|x-a_i\|^2}$, the explicit <derivatives> are
$$
\nabla F=2\sum_i e^{\|x-a_i\|^2}(x-a_i),\qquad
H_F=\sum_i e^{\|x-a_i\|^2}\bigl(4(x-a_i)(x-a_i)^T+2I\bigr).
$$
The positive-definite <Hessian matrix> and <coercivity> show that the objective has one global minimizer.

For numerical stability one can minimize $\Phi=\log F$ instead, which has exactly the same minimizer. Put $r_i=\|x-a_i\|^2$, $w_i=e^{r_i}/\sum_j e^{r_j}$, and $\bar a=\sum_iw_i a_i$. Then the <logarithmic gradient of an exponential squared-distance sum> gives
$$
g(x):=\nabla\Phi(x)=2(x-\bar a),\qquad
\nabla^2\Phi=2I+4\sum_iw_i(a_i-\bar a)(a_i-\bar a)^T\succeq2I.
$$
Evaluate the weights as $e^{r_i-r_{\max}}/\sum_j e^{r_j-r_{\max}}$ to avoid <floating-point overflow>. A <quasi-Newton method> step is
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha_nH_n g(x_n),
$$
where $H_n$ is the positive-definite inverse-<Hessian matrix> approximation updated by <BFGS>, and $\alpha_n$ is chosen by a <line search>, for example one satisfying the <Wolfe conditions>. Under the standard smoothness and nondegeneracy assumptions at this strongly convex minimizer, full-memory <BFGS> has **local <superlinear convergence>**. An exact <Newton's method in optimization> would have local <quadratic convergence>, but pays for those much more expensive exact second <derivatives>.<Quasi-Newton Methods by NEOS Guide>[Opening construction and BFGS update: approximate Hessians from <gradients>; dense update cost of order d squared]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Here a dense $100000$-by-$100000$ <matrix> has $10^{10}$ entries, about $80$ GB even with eight-byte storage per entry. Use <limited-memory BFGS> on the negative of the objective, or equivalently its positive-definite approximate-metric form of <gradient ascent>. It stores only a fixed small number $\ell$ of <vector> pairs, costing $O(\ell d)$ memory rather than $O(d^2)$.

Let $G(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{100}e^{e^{-\|x-a_i\|^2}}$ and put $c_i(x)=e^{e^{-\|x-a_i\|^2}}e^{-\|x-a_i\|^2}$. Direct use of the <chain rule> gives
$$
\nabla G(x)=-2\sum_{i=1}^{100}c_i(x)(x-a_i).
$$
If $H_n$ denotes the positive-definite inverse-<Hessian matrix> approximation for $-G$, the iteration is
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+\alpha_nH_n\nabla G(x_n),
$$
with a <line search> increasing $G$ and the usual safeguarded limited-memory updates. No exact <Hessian matrix> is required. With fixed memory and the usual bounded-metric and <line search> hypotheses, expect **local <linear convergence> near a nondegenerate strict maximum**; fixed-memory <limited-memory BFGS> does not generally inherit the superlinear theorem for full-memory <BFGS>.

Unlike the preceding objective, $G$ is not generally a <concave function>. Thus this is a method for finding a <local maximum>; comparison of different converged candidates or a global search is needed to certify a <global maximum> for arbitrary centres. The symbolic problem supplies no assumption that would justify an unconditional global-convergence claim.<Quasi-Newton Methods by NEOS Guide>[Final paragraphs: storing only recent secant <vectors>, linear-in-dimension memory, and the L-BFGS implementation]{ref}

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $T(x)$ be the weighted average on the right, and define $F$ and $\Phi=\log F$ as in part (i). Their explicit <gradient> identity is
$$
\nabla\Phi(x)=2(x-T(x)).
$$
Therefore the displayed equation is exactly the <stationary point> equation for the strongly convex $\Phi$. It has one solution, which is the unique minimizer from part (i). **Reuse the BFGS iteration**, with
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-2\alpha_nH_n(x_n-T(x_n)).
$$
The <gradient> is explicit, the weights can again be scaled by their largest exponent, and the usual full-memory <BFGS> assumptions give **local <superlinear convergence>**.

Simply setting $x_{n+1}=T(x_n)$ is not automatically a convergent <fixed-point iteration>. Differentiating the normalized weights gives
$$
J_T(x)=-2\sum_iw_i(a_i-T(x))(a_i-T(x))^T,
$$
whose <operator norm> need not be below $1$. For example, in one dimension with centres $-1,1$, $T(x)=-\tanh(2x)$ and $T'(0)=-2$: the desired fixed point is locally repelling for that naive iteration. If exact second <derivatives> are affordable, <multivariable Newton iteration> on $x-T(x)=0$ is another valid choice, with <Jacobian matrix> $I-J_T$ as given above and local <quadratic convergence>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

A general-purpose high-dimensional choice is <Monte Carlo integration>. Draw <independent> <vectors> $W_r$ uniformly from the unit cube and evaluate $H(W_r)=\sum_{i=1}^{100}e^{\|W_r-a_i\|^2}$. The cube has volume $1$, so its <integral> is the <expected value> of $H(W_1)$. The iterative estimator is
$$
y_N=\frac1N\sum_{r=1}^NH(W_r),\qquad
y_{N+1}=y_N+\frac{H(W_{N+1})-y_N}{N+1}.
$$
The integrand is <continuous> on a compact <set>, hence bounded with finite <variance>. The <strong law of large numbers> gives almost sure convergence, and the square root of the <mean squared error> is exactly $\sigma/\sqrt N$, where $\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(H(W_1))$. Thus the standard stochastic accuracy rate is **$O(N^{-1/2})$**; the dimension affects the <variance> and evaluation cost, not this exponent.

There is also an important structure-specific improvement here: $e^{\|x-a_i\|^2}=\prod_{j=1}^{100}e^{(x_j-a_{ij})^2}$ is a <separable integrand>. The <Fubini theorem> reduces the requested value exactly to
$$
y=\sum_{i=1}^{100}\prod_{j=1}^{100}\int_0^1e^{(t-a_{ij})^2}\,dt.
$$
One can therefore replace high-dimensional sampling by $10000$ one-dimensional <composite Simpson's rule> calculations. For each factor use even $n$, $h=1/n$, and
$$
Q_n(g)=\frac h3\left[g(0)+g(1)+4\sum_{r=1,\ r\text{ odd}}^{n-1}g(rh)+2\sum_{r=2,\ r\text{ even}}^{n-2}g(rh)\right],
$$
then iterate $n\leftarrow2n$ and form the sum of the products. These smooth factors give **$O(n^{-4})$ deterministic discretization error** for fixed centres. Thus <Monte Carlo integration> is a valid generic answer, while exploiting separability is the more efficient specialized alternative; a large dimension alone does not force a tensor-product grid.

=== a

==== Solution

Let $A_i$ be the <event> that candidate $i$ succeeds. The <countable additivity> axiom adds <probabilities> only for mutually disjoint <events>. Successes of different candidates are not mutually exclusive, so replacing the <probability> of their <union> by $\sum_i\mathbb P(A_i)=1$ misuses that axiom. Without disjointness one obtains only the <union bound>, not certainty. The same sum is the <expected value> of the number of successes, which also need not imply at least one.

If the ten outcomes are mutually <independent events>, with each success <probability> $0.1$, all fail with <probability> $0.9^{10}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{at least one success})=1-0.9^{10}
=0.6513215599\ldots.}
$$
The <independence> assumption is essential. The marginal <probabilities> alone allow any <union> <probability> from $0.1$ up to $1$; perfectly overlapping success <events> give the lower endpoint and disjoint successes give the upper endpoint.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <independent random variables> have a product <joint probability mass function>. For <integers> $r,t\geq0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=r,Y=t)=e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\frac{\lambda^r\mu^t}{r!\,t!}.}
$$
It is zero for all other pairs. The factors are the respective <Poisson distribution> masses, with the product justified by <independence>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

By the definition of a <probability generating function> and the power series for the <exponential function>,
$$
G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]
=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda s)^r}{r!}
=\boxed{e^{\lambda(s-1)}}.
$$
The <series> is absolutely convergent for every finite real $s$; in particular the expression is finite for all $s>1$, as needed for the later tail estimate.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $Z=2(X+Y)+1$, <independence> gives
$$
G_Z(s)=\mathbb E[s^{2(X+Y)+1}]
=sG_X(s^2)G_Y(s^2)
=\boxed{s\exp\bigl((\lambda+\mu)(s^2-1)\bigr)}.
$$
The initial factor $s$ accounts for the shift by one; substituting $s^2$ accounts for doubling the <random variable>. Its support consists of positive odd <integers>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Conditioning on $Z=z$ has positive <probability> exactly when $z=2r+1$ for an <integer> $r\geq0$. This is the same <event> as $X+Y=r$. The <addition of independent Poisson random variables> gives a <Poisson distribution> with parameter $\lambda+\mu$ for the sum. Therefore, for $0\leq t\leq r$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X=t\mid Z=2r+1)
&=\frac{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\lambda^t\mu^{r-t}/[t!(r-t)!]}
{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}(\lambda+\mu)^r/r!}\\
&=\binom rt\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}\right)^t
\left(\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\right)^{r-t}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **$X\mid\{Z=2r+1\}\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(r,\lambda/(\lambda+\mu))$**. The case $r=0$ is a constant zero <random variable>. For even, nonpositive, or noninteger $z$, the conditioning <event> has zero <probability>, so an event-conditional <probability distribution> is not specified by the quotient formula. This is a two-component case of <Poisson-multinomial conditioning>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $s>1$, the <function> $t\mapsto s^t$ is increasing and nonnegative. Thus $\{X\geq x\}=\{s^X\geq s^x\}$. Applying <Markov's inequality> to the nonnegative <random variable> $s^X$, whose <expected value> is finite by assumption, yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\geq x)\leq\frac{\mathbb E[s^X]}{s^x}=s^{-x}G_X(s).}
$$
Under the usual <probability generating function> convention $X$ takes nonnegative <integer> values. The argument itself works for any real <random variable> if $G_X(s)$ is understood as the finite <expected value> $\mathbb E[s^X]$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Insert the <Poisson distribution> <probability generating function> into the preceding bound:
$$
\mathbb P(X\geq x)\leq\exp\bigl(\lambda(s-1)-x\log s\bigr),\qquad s>1.
$$
For fixed $x>\lambda$, differentiate the exponent with respect to $s$. Its <derivative> is $\lambda-x/s$, and its <second derivative> is $x/s^2>0$, so its unique minimum occurs at $s=x/\lambda>1$. At that value,
$$
\lambda(s-1)-x\log s=x-\lambda-x\log(x/\lambda).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\geq x)\leq e^{-\lambda}\left(\frac{e\lambda}{x}\right)^x.}
$$
This <Chernoff bound> remains valid for noninteger positive thresholds $x>\lambda$; the <event> is then the same as exceeding or equalling its <integer> ceiling.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $N=m+n$. In a uniformly shuffled deck, each labelled card is equally likely to occupy the first position, and exactly $m$ cards are red. Therefore **$\mathbb P(\text{first red})=m/(m+n)$**. Equivalently, the red positions are a uniformly chosen $m$-element <subset> of the $N$ positions.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Every position in the random <permutation> has the same marginal colour <probability>, so **$\mathbb P(\text{second red})=m/(m+n)$**. One can verify this with the <law of total probability>, conditioning on the first card:
$$
\frac mN\frac{m-1}{N-1}+\frac nN\frac m{N-1}
=\frac{m(m-1+n)}{N(N-1)}=\frac mN.
$$
Here $N\geq2$ because both colour counts are positive.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $R_1,R_2$ be the two red-card <events>. Sampling without replacement gives
$$
\mathbb P(R_1\cap R_2)=\frac mN\frac{m-1}{N-1}.
$$
This differs from the product $m^2/N^2$, since
$$
\mathbb P(R_1\cap R_2)-\mathbb P(R_1)\mathbb P(R_2)
=-\frac{mn}{N^2(N-1)}<0.
$$
Thus **the events are not independent**, for every positive $m,n$. A first red card reduces the remaining red fraction, a basic instance of how <sampling without replacement need not be independent>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The $m$ red positions form a uniformly chosen <subset> of size $m$, so there are $\binom{m+n}{m}$ equally likely colour patterns. A contiguous red block can start at positions $1,2,\ldots,n+1$, giving exactly $n+1$ favourable patterns. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{all reds together})=\frac{n+1}{\binom{m+n}{m}}.}
$$
If the cards are regarded as individually labelled, each colour pattern corresponds to $m!n!$ <permutations>, so the same counting argument still applies. In the edge case $m=1$, the formula correctly gives <probability> one.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

Conditional on a contiguous red block, its $n+1$ possible starting positions remain equally likely because their original colour patterns had equal <probabilities>. The first card is red exactly when that block starts at position $1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{first red}\mid\text{all reds together})=\frac1{n+1}.}
$$
This also follows by dividing the <probability> of the single favourable block position, $1/\binom{m+n}{m}$, by the <probability> of all $n+1$ block positions.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the rate parameter convention for the <exponential distribution>, <independence> gives the <joint probability density function>
$$
\boxed{f_{U,V}(u,v)=2e^{-u-2v}\mathbf1_{\{u\geq0,\ v\geq0\}}.}
$$
For $z\geq0$, the survival <probability> of the minimum is
$$
\mathbb P(Z>z)=\mathbb P(U>z,V>z)=e^{-z}e^{-2z}=e^{-3z}.
$$
Thus **$Z$ has exponential distribution with rate $3$**, and
$$
f_Z(z)=3e^{-3z}\mathbf1_{\{z\geq0\}}.
$$
Values of a <probability density function> at the single endpoint $0$ do not affect the <probability distribution>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Compute all three <probabilities>. From the preceding part, $\mathbb P(A)=e^{-6}$. Integrating the <joint probability density function> over $u>v$ gives
$$
\mathbb P(B)=\int_0^\infty\int_v^\infty2e^{-u-2v}\,du\,dv
=\int_0^\infty2e^{-3v}\,dv=\frac23.
$$
On $A\cap B$ one has $v>2$ and $u>v$, so
$$
\mathbb P(A\cap B)=\int_2^\infty\int_v^\infty2e^{-u-2v}\,du\,dv
=\frac23e^{-6}=\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B).
$$
Therefore **$A$ and $B$ are independent events**. The minimum waiting time and the identity of the first clock are independent for <competing exponential clocks>; unequal rates change the winner <probabilities> but do not destroy that <independence>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose **$g(t)=e^t$**. If $W$ has an <exponential distribution> with rate $r$, then for $c<r$,
$$
\mathbb E[e^{cW}]=\int_0^\infty r e^{-(r-c)t}\,dt=\frac r{r-c},
$$
while the <integral> diverges to $+\infty$ when $c\geq r$. Since $Z$ has rate $3$,
$$
\mathbb E[g(Z)]=\frac32,\qquad
\mathbb E[g(Z)^2]=3,\qquad
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(g(Z))=\frac34.}
$$
For the other two rates, $\mathbb E[g(V)]=2/(2-1)=2$, whereas $\mathbb E[g(U)]=\int_0^\infty1\,dt=+\infty$. These computations establish exactly the required finite <variance>, finite <expected value>, and infinite <expected value>.

==== Solution

Condition on the fair coin choosing the initial chamber. The <law of total probability> gives $\mathbb P(\text{reach }k)=(p_1+p_2)/2$. With $r=(1-p)/p$, substituting the <biased gambler's ruin probability> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{reach }k)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{2-r-r^2}{2(1-r^k)},&p\ne\tfrac12,\\[5pt]
\displaystyle\frac3{2k},&p=\tfrac12.
\end{cases}}
$$
Both numerator and denominator in the first case have the same sign. Its limit as $r\to1$ is the fair-walk value, since the ratio of their first <derivatives> is $3/(2k)$. The averaging is over initial locations, not over the transition <probabilities>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <expected value> of an <indicator random variable> is the <probability> of its <event>. For a <simple symmetric random walk> absorbed at $0,k$, the <gambler's ruin> success <probability> from $i$ is $i/k$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_i[J]=\frac ik.}
$$
The boundary values are $0$ at the left exit and $1$ at chamber $k$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $t_i=\mathbb E_i[X]$. Time is accumulated in each transient chamber before the next move; arrival at either terminal state ends the trial immediately. Thus $t_0=t_k=0$. A block of $k$ identical rightward choices forces absorption, so the <geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability> gives finite <expected value> of $X$.

<First-step analysis> now gives
$$
t_i=1+\tfrac12t_{i-1}+\tfrac12t_{i+1},\qquad1\leq i\leq k-1.
$$
The quadratic $t_i=i(k-i)$ has the correct boundary values and second difference $t_{i+1}-2t_i+t_{i-1}=-2$, so it solves this system. The difference of two solutions would be linear in $i$ with zero values at both endpoints, hence zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_i[X]=i(k-i)\text{ minutes}.}
$$
This is the <expected duration of symmetric gambler's ruin>. It includes the first minute in the starting transient chamber and does not add a minute after absorption.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $h_i=\mathbb E_i[J]=i/k$ and $v_i=\mathbb E_i[JX]$. Since $0\leq JX\leq X$, the latter <expected value> is finite. Conditional on the first move to $i-1$ or $i+1$, total time is one plus the remaining time, while the terminal success <indicator random variable> is unchanged. The <Markov property> and <linearity of expectation> give
$$
v_i=\tfrac12(v_{i-1}+h_{i-1})+\tfrac12(v_{i+1}+h_{i+1})
=\tfrac12v_{i-1}+\tfrac12v_{i+1}+\frac ik.
$$
At either absorbing boundary the remaining time is zero, so $v_0=v_k=0$.

Let $w_i=i(k^2-i^2)/(3k)$. The linear term has zero second difference and $i^3$ has second difference $6i$. Consequently
$$
w_{i+1}-2w_i+w_{i-1}=-\frac{2i}{k},
$$
which is exactly the required recurrence, and $w_0=w_k=0$. Uniqueness follows as before: the difference of two solutions has zero second difference and zero endpoint values. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_i[Y]=v_i=\frac{i(k^2-i^2)}{3k}.}
$$
This is the success-weighted duration underlying the <duration of gambler's ruin conditioned on a chosen boundary>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For $1\leq i\leq k$, the success <probability> is $i/k>0$, and the definition of <conditional expectation> on an <event> gives
$$
\mathbb E_i[X\mid J=1]
=\frac{\mathbb E_i[X\mathbf1_{\{J=1\}}]}{\mathbb P_i(J=1)}
=\frac{i(k^2-i^2)/(3k)}{i/k}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_i[X\mid\text{reach }k]=\frac{k^2-i^2}{3}\text{ minutes}.}
$$
At $i=k$ the value is zero because the trial has already ended. At the left absorbing state $i=0$, conditioning on success would be undefined because it has zero <probability>. The formula concerns the successful trajectories, so it need not equal the unconditional mean $i(k-i)$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat one visit to a level as a whole <gambler's ruin> excursion, starting at its chamber $1$ and ending at local chamber $0$ or $4$. The room choices are fair, so the chance of reaching $4$ before $0$ is $1/4$, and the chance of reaching $0$ first is $3/4$. The one-way hatches reset the next excursion to chamber $1$ of the adjacent level. Thus the <embedded Markov chain at level exits> moves upward with <probability> $1/4$ and downward with <probability> $3/4$, even though individual room choices are unbiased.

Let $u$ be the food <probability> from the lower starting chamber, and $v$ that from the upper chamber $1$. The lower level either exits unsuccessfully or reaches the upper level, while the upper level either finds food or returns to the lower start. <First-step analysis> at these level exits gives
$$
u=\tfrac14v,\qquad v=\tfrac14+\tfrac34u.
$$
Substituting yields $u=1/16+3u/16$, so
$$
\boxed{u=\frac1{13}.}
$$
This also follows from <biased gambler's ruin probability> on levels $0,1,2,3$, starting at $1$, with ratio $(1-p)/p=3$: $(1-3)/(1-3^3)=1/13$. Absorption occurs <almost surely>, since from each active level a fixed positive chance of terminal absorption occurs within two excursions; the original chamber excursions are also <almost surely> finite.
