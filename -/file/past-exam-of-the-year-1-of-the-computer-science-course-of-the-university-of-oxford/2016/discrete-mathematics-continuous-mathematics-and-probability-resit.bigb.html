=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $C^c=\mathcal U\setminus C$. The rules for <set difference>, <set intersection>, and <complement of a set> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\cap B)\setminus(A\cap C)
&=(A\cap B)\cap(A\cap C)^c\\
&=(A\cap B)\cap(A^c\cup C^c)\\
&=(A\cap B\cap A^c)\cup(A\cap B\cap C^c)\\
&=\varnothing\cup\bigl(A\cap(B\cap C^c)\bigr)\\
&=\boxed{A\cap(B\setminus C)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second step is <De Morgan's laws>; the third distributes <set intersection> over <set union>. The fourth uses $A\cap A^c=\varnothing$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Using <set difference> as intersection with a <complement of a set>, then <De Morgan's laws> and distribution, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\cup B)\setminus(C\setminus A)
&=(A\cup B)\cap(C\cap A^c)^c\\
&=(A\cup B)\cap(C^c\cup A)\\
&=A\cup(B\cap C^c)\\
&=\boxed{A\cup(B\setminus C)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For the third equality, expand the two <set unions>: the terms $A\cap C^c$ and $A\cap B$ are absorbed by $A$, and $A\cap A=A$. This proves the required identity entirely by <set> algebra.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

By definition the <symmetric difference> is $A\oplus B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$. Put $D=A\oplus B$ and $E=A\cap B$. Distribution of <set intersection> over <set union> gives
$$
D\cap E=(A\cap B^c\cap A\cap B)\cup(B\cap A^c\cap A\cap B)=\varnothing.
$$
For disjoint <sets>, <set difference> removes nothing, so $D\oplus E=(D\setminus E)\cup(E\setminus D)=D\cup E$. Consequently
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\oplus B)\oplus(A\cap B)
&=(A\cap B^c)\cup(B\cap A^c)\cup(A\cap B)\\
&=A\cup(B\cap A^c)\\
&=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup A^c)\\
&=\boxed{A\cup B}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the second equality combines $A\cap B^c$ with $A\cap B$; the last uses $A\cup A^c=\mathcal U$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <function> $f:A\to B$ is **onto**, or a <surjective function>, when every $b\in B$ has a preimage: $\forall b\in B\ \exists a\in A:\ f(a)=b$. It is **one-to-one**, or an <injective function>, when $f(a)=f(a')$ implies $a=a'$ for all $a,a'\in A$. It is **bijective**, a <bijection>, when both properties hold.

An <inverse function> is a <function> $f^{-1}:B\to A$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{f^{-1}\circ f=\operatorname{id}_A,\qquad f\circ f^{-1}=\operatorname{id}_B}.
$$
For a <bijection>, $f^{-1}(b)$ is the unique $a$ with $f(a)=b$: existence follows from <surjectivity> and uniqueness from <injectivity>. This is a two-sided <inverse function>, rather than just a left or right inverse.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <function composition> $g\circ f:A\to C$ is defined by $(g\circ f)(a)=g(f(a))$. Suppose $f(a)=f(a')$. Applying $g$ gives $(g\circ f)(a)=(g\circ f)(a')$. Since the composite is an <injective function>, $a=a'$. Thus **$f$ is an <injective function>**.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For each $c\in C$, <surjectivity> of the <function composition> gives $a\in A$ such that $g(f(a))=c$. Taking $b=f(a)\in B$ supplies a preimage of $c$ under $g$. Thus **$g$ is a <surjective function>**.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $h:B\to A$ be a two-sided <inverse function> of $f$. If $f(a)=f(a')$, apply $h$ and use $h\circ f=\operatorname{id}_A$ to obtain $a=a'$, proving <injectivity>. For any $b\in B$, the other identity gives $b=f(h(b))$, proving <surjectivity>. Therefore **$f$ is a <bijection>**. Both inverse identities matter: a one-sided inverse alone would establish only the corresponding one of these two properties.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Replace $x$ by $1/x$, which stays in the domain of the <functional equation>. Writing $u=f(x)$ and $v=f(1/x)$ gives the <linear system>
$$
u+2v=x,\qquad 2u+v=\frac1x.
$$
Twice the second equation minus the first gives $3u=2/x-x$, so the only possible <function> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac{2/x-x}{3}\quad(x>0)}.
$$
To check existence as well as uniqueness, substitute this expression: $f(x)+2f(1/x)=(2/x-x+4x-2/x)/3=x$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The algebraic identity
$$
x^3+x^{-3}=(x+x^{-1})^3-3(x+x^{-1})
$$
suggests **$f(t)=t^3-3t$ for every $t>0$**, and substitution verifies the <functional equation>.

In fact $x+1/x\geq2$, since $(x-1)^2/x\geq0$. Conversely, for each $t\geq2$, the <quadratic equation> $x^2-tx+1=0$ has a positive root $(t+\sqrt{t^2-4})/2$. Thus the <functional equation> forces $f(t)=t^3-3t$ exactly on $[2,\infty)$; its values on $(0,2)$ are arbitrary. The displayed <function> is one convenient choice on the full required domain.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is an <equivalence relation> exactly when it is <reflexive>, <symmetric>, and <transitive>:
$$
\begin{gathered}
aRa\quad(a\in A),\\
aRb\Longrightarrow bRa\quad(a,b\in A),\\
aRb\text{ and }bRc\Longrightarrow aRc\quad(a,b,c\in A).
\end{gathered}
$$
The <equivalence class> of $a\in A$ is **$[a]_R=\{b\in A:aRb\}$**. The <equivalence classes> partition $A$: every element belongs to its own class, and two classes are either equal or disjoint.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive integer pairs, <reflexivity> is $ab=ba$. If $ad=bc$, reversing the equality gives $cb=da$, establishing <symmetry>. For <transitivity>, suppose $(a,b)R(c,d)$ and $(c,d)R(e,f)$. Then $ad=bc$ and $cf=de$, whence
$$
adf=bcf=bde.
$$
Since $d>0$, cancellation gives $af=be$. Thus $(a,b)R(e,f)$, and $R$ is an <equivalence relation>.

Membership in the <equivalence class> of $(4,6)$ means $4b=6a$, or $2b=3a$. Since $2$ and $3$ are <coprime>, $a=2r$ and $b=3r$ for a positive integer $r$. Conversely every such pair satisfies the equality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[(4,6)]_R=\{(2r,3r):r\in\mathbb Z_+\}}.
$$
These pairs are precisely the positive numerator-denominator representations of the <rational number> $2/3$.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <mathematical induction>, starting at $n=1$. Since $F_0=0$ and $F_1=1$, the sum is $-1=(-1)^1F_0-1$.

Suppose the formula holds at $n\geq1$. Adding the next <Fibonacci number> with its sign and using the <Fibonacci recurrence> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(-1)^kF_k
&=(-1)^nF_{n-1}-1+(-1)^{n+1}F_{n+1}\\
&=(-1)^{n+1}(F_{n+1}-F_{n-1})-1\\
&=(-1)^{n+1}F_n-1.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the required successor case. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^kF_k=(-1)^nF_{n-1}-1\quad(n\geq1)}.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix $n\geq1$ and use <mathematical induction> on $m$ with two initial cases, as appropriate for the second-order <Fibonacci recurrence>. At $m=0$, the right side is $F_{n-1}F_0+F_nF_1=F_n$. At $m=1$, it is $F_{n-1}+F_n=F_{n+1}$.

If the identity holds at $m$ and $m+1$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{n+m+2}
&=F_{n+m+1}+F_{n+m}\\
&=F_{n-1}(F_{m+1}+F_m)+F_n(F_{m+2}+F_{m+1})\\
&=F_{n-1}F_{m+2}+F_nF_{m+3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence the <induction> establishes
$$
\boxed{F_{n+m}=F_{n-1}F_m+F_nF_{m+1}\quad(n\geq1,\ m\geq0)}.
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix $m\geq0$, using the convention for <binomial coefficients> that $\binom{k}{m}=0$ when $m>k$. At $n=0$, both $\binom0m$ and $\binom1{m+1}$ equal $1$ if $m=0$ and $0$ otherwise.

For the successor step, apply the <induction hypothesis>, then <Pascal's rule>:
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\binom{k}{m}
=\binom{n+1}{m+1}+\binom{n+1}{m}
=\binom{n+2}{m+1}.
$$
Thus <mathematical induction> proves the <hockey-stick identity>
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{k}{m}=\binom{n+1}{m+1}\quad(m,n\geq0)}.
$$
The zero convention also covers the case $n<m$, without a separate starting index.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The stated identity $k\binom nk=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ has an even right side because $n$ is even. Its left side is an odd integer $k$ times the integer <binomial coefficient> $\binom nk$. An odd multiplier preserves <integer parity>, so that coefficient must be even. Equivalently, reducing the identity in <modular arithmetic> gives
$$
\boxed{\binom nk\equiv0\pmod2}.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $n=2r$ and $k=2s$. Partition an $n$-element <set> into $r$ distinguished pairs, and let $\tau$ interchange the two elements of every pair. It induces an <involution> on the $k$-element subsets. Every nonfixed subset is paired with its distinct image, so nonfixed subsets contribute an even number to the <binomial coefficient> $\binom{2r}{2s}$.

A fixed subset contains either both or neither of the elements of each distinguished pair. Since it contains $2s$ elements, it consists of exactly $s$ whole pairs. There are $\binom rs$ such fixed subsets. Counting modulo $2$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\binom nk\equiv\binom{n/2}{k/2}\pmod2}.
$$
The same <involution> explains part (i): a fixed subset cannot have odd size.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <preorder> is a <binary relation> that is <reflexive> and <transitive>: $a\preceq a$, and $a\preceq b\preceq c$ implies $a\preceq c$.

A <partial order> is a <preorder> that is also an <antisymmetric relation>: $a\preceq b$ and $b\preceq a$ together imply $a=b$.

A <linear order>, also called a <total order>, is a <partial order> in which every two elements are comparable: **$a\preceq b$ or $b\preceq a$ for all $a,b\in A$**.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

This is the <pointwise order> on a <function space>. Each of the three <partial order> axioms follows from the corresponding axiom in $B$.

For <reflexivity>, $f(x)\preceq_B f(x)$ for every $x$, so $f\preceq_{\mathcal F}f$. For <antisymmetry>, if $f\preceq_{\mathcal F}g$ and $g\preceq_{\mathcal F}f$, then $f(x)=g(x)$ for every $x\in A$; equality of <functions> therefore gives $f=g$. For <transitivity>, if $f\preceq_{\mathcal F}g\preceq_{\mathcal F}h$, then $f(x)\preceq_B g(x)\preceq_B h(x)$ for every $x$, so $f\preceq_{\mathcal F}h$.

Thus **$(\mathcal F,\preceq_{\mathcal F})$ is a <partially ordered set>**. The argument also covers an empty domain $A$, since then there is exactly one <function> $A\to B$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret $\mathbb R_+$ as the strictly positive reals, as in the domain $x>0$ in question 1(e). A bound valid for every $n$ is immediately an eventual bound, by taking $m=0$ (or the least natural number under the other convention).

Conversely, suppose the eventual <big O notation> bound holds with constants $m,c$. Since $g(j)>0$, all the finitely many ratios $|f(j)|/|g(j)|$ with $j<m$ are finite. Choose
$$
C=\max\left(1,c,\left\{\frac{|f(j)|}{|g(j)|}:j<m\right\}\right),
$$
omitting the set of ratios when it is empty. For $n<m$ the definition of $C$ gives $|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|$, and for $n\geq m$ the original bound does so. Hence **the two definitions are equivalent for the stated strictly positive codomain**.

Strict positivity matters. If $\mathbb R_+$ were instead intended to include zero, an eventual bound need not extend to all indices: $g(0)=0$, $f(0)=1$, and $f(n)=g(n)=1$ for $n\geq1$ give a counterexample. More generally, extension is possible exactly when each initial zero of $g$ is also a zero of $f$.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <natural logarithms>; the same argument works for any fixed base greater than $1$. The <function> $\log\log x$ is increasing for $x>1$, since its <derivative> is $1/(x\log x)>0$. With natural logs the sole negative summand is at $i=2$. Put $C_0=|\log\log2|$.

For all sufficiently large $n$ with $\log\log n\geq1$, monotonicity and the <triangle inequality> give
$$
\left|\sum_{i=2}^n\log\log i\right|
\leq C_0+\sum_{i=3}^n\log\log i
\leq C_0+n\log\log n
\leq(1+C_0)n\log\log n.
$$
These are fixed constants and an eventual bound, proving
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=2}^n\log\log i=O(n\log\log n)}.
$$
For another fixed logarithm base there are still only finitely many negative initial terms, which are absorbed in $C_0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For <natural logarithms> and sufficiently large $n$, take the <logarithm> of the positive expression:
$$
\log\left((\log n)^{\log\log n}\right)=(\log\log n)^2.
$$
Set $t=\log\log n$. Then $\log n=e^t$. The <exponential function> grows faster than $t^2$: for example, its power series gives $e^t\geq t^3/6$ for $t>0$, so $t^2/e^t\leq6/t\to0$. In particular, eventually $(\log\log n)^2\leq\log n$. Exponentiating yields
$$
\boxed{(\log n)^{\log\log n}\leq n\text{ eventually},\qquad (\log n)^{\log\log n}=O(n)}.
$$
For logarithms to a fixed base $b>1$, the natural logarithm of the left side is $(\log\log n-\log\log b)^2/\log b$, still smaller than $\log n$ eventually. The <big O notation> conclusion is therefore independent of that base choice.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Unroll the <recurrence relation> down to its <initial condition>:
$$
T(n)=T(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}j=1+\frac{n(n+1)}2.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{T(n)=1+\frac{n(n+1)}2=O(n^2)}.
$$
Indeed $T(n)/n^2\to1/2$, so the sharper growth classification is $\Theta(n^2)$ in <big theta notation>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Multiplying the <recurrence relation> by $n$ removes the average:
$$
nT(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}T(i)+n^2.
$$
For $n\geq2$, subtract the same identity at $n-1$:
$$
nT(n)-(n-1)T(n-1)=T(n-1)+n^2-(n-1)^2.
$$
Rearranging gives $T(n)=T(n-1)+2-1/n$. This also holds at $n=1$, because the original <recurrence relation> gives $T(1)=1$ and $T(0)=0$. Summing the differences therefore yields
$$
\boxed{T(n)=2n-H_n=O(n)},\qquad H_n=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1j,\quad H_0=0.
$$
Here $H_n$ is a <harmonic number>. Since $H_n\leq n$, we have $n\leq T(n)\leq2n$ for $n\geq1$, also establishing $\Theta(n)$ in <big theta notation>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $Y(t)=y(e^t)$, so $x=e^t>0$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
Y'(t)=xy'(x),\qquad Y''(t)=xy'(x)+x^2y''(x).
$$
Hence $x^2y''=Y''-Y'$ and the <Cauchy-Euler equation> becomes
$$
\boxed{Y''+3Y'+2Y=0}.
$$
Its <characteristic equation> is $r^2+3r+2=(r+1)(r+2)=0$. The distinct roots give $Y(t)=Ae^{-t}+Be^{-2t}$. Returning to the original independent variable gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac A x+\frac B{x^2}\quad(x>0)}.
$$
The same form holds on any negative interval avoiding $0$, using $t=\log|x|$; the stated substitution covers the positive interval. At $x=0$ the <differential equation> is singular, so crossing it is a separate extension problem.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Seek a <particular solution> $y_p=ax+b$. Substitution into the <linear ordinary differential equation> gives $6ax+2b=6x+2$, so $a=b=1$. Add the <complementary solution> from part (a):
$$
y=x+1+\frac A x+\frac B{x^2}.
$$
The two prescribed values yield $A+B=5$ and $A/2+B/4=3/2$. Thus $2A+B=6$, so $A=1$ and $B=4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x+1+\frac1x+\frac4{x^2}\quad(x>0)}.
$$
Direct substitution gives $y(1)=7$ and $y(2)=9/2$, and the <homogeneous solution> contributes zero to the left-hand differential operator.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

On an interval avoiding $x=0$, divide by $x$ to obtain the first-order <linear ordinary differential equation>
$$
y'-\frac2x y=x^3\cos x.
$$
An <integrating factor> is $\mu(x)=\exp\!\left(\int-2/x\,dx\right)=x^{-2}$. Multiplying through and using the <product rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}(x^{-2}y)=x\cos x.
$$
By <integration by parts>, $\int x\cos x\,dx=x\sin x+\cos x+C$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x^2\bigl(x\sin x+\cos x+C\bigr)}.
$$
The formula differentiates back to the original equation. Although division excluded $x=0$, this formula has a smooth extension there with $y(0)=0$, satisfying the original equation at zero as well.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The substitution $y=xv$ gives $y'=v+xv'$. Cancelling $x^3v$ on both sides of the <differential equation>, for $x>0$, gives
$$
\boxed{xv'=v^3}.
$$
The <initial condition> is $v(1)=1$. This selects a positive solution locally, so <separation of variables> gives
$$
\int v^{-3}\,dv=\int x^{-1}\,dx,
\qquad -\frac1{2v^2}=\log x+C.
$$
Using $v(1)=1$ gives $C=-1/2$, and continuity selects the positive square root:
$$
v=\frac1{\sqrt{1-2\log x}},\qquad
\boxed{y=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-2\log x}}=\sqrt{\frac{x^2}{1-\log(x^2)}},\quad m=2}.
$$
The real solution containing $x=1$ has maximal positive domain **$0<x<\sqrt e$**. Its denominator tends to zero and $y\to+\infty$ as $x\uparrow\sqrt e$, a <finite-time blow-up of an ordinary differential equation> in the independent variable $x$. Thus the source's wording “for $x>0$” needs this domain restriction; the prescribed initial value admits no real solution on the whole positive half-line. The division by $v^3$ loses the separate solution $v=0$, but that solution does not meet the <initial condition>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Divide $[a,b]$ into $N$ equal subintervals of width $h=(b-a)/N$, with $x_j=a+jh$. Linear interpolation between adjacent values of $f$ gives a trapezoid of signed area $h(f(x_{j-1})+f(x_j))/2$. Adding these areas produces the <composite trapezoidal rule>
$$
\boxed{T_N=h\left(\frac{f(a)+f(b)}2+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}f(x_j)\right)\approx\int_a^bf(x)\,dx}.
$$
For continuous $f$, this tends to the <integral> as $h\to0$; if $f$ has a bounded continuous second <derivative>, the error is at most $(b-a)h^2\max|f''|/12$.

The original PDF prints $n\to\infty$ while using $n$ as the bound summation index and $N$ as the upper limit. Literally this does not specify a varying upper limit. Interpreting the intended limit as **$N\to\infty$**, and relabelling the summation index $j$, gives the <right Riemann sum>
$$
S_N=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\left(\frac jN\right)^4=\frac1{N^5}\sum_{j=1}^Nj^4.
$$
For $f(x)=x^4$, $a=0$, and $b=1$, the only difference from the <composite trapezoidal rule> is the endpoint weighting: $S_N=T_N+1/(2N)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{N^5}\sum_{j=1}^Nj^4=\int_0^1x^4\,dx=\frac15}.
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The given <square wave> agrees almost everywhere with an <odd function> on $(-\pi,\pi)$; its assigned value at the single point $0$ does not affect any <Fourier coefficient>. Thus $a_0=a_n=0$, while
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin(nx)\,dx
=\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{\pi n}
=\begin{cases}4/(\pi n),&n\text{ odd},\\0,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}
$$
The <Fourier series> is therefore
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\frac4\pi\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2r+1)x)}{2r+1}\quad(-\pi<x<0\text{ or }0<x<\pi)}.
$$
The <Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series> applies because the periodic extension is piecewise smooth. It gives $f(x)$ at each continuity point and the midpoint $0$ at the jumps, including $x=0$; it does not reproduce the assigned value $f(0)=-1$ there.

At the continuity point $x=\pi/2$, $\sin((2r+1)\pi/2)=(-1)^r$ and $f(\pi/2)=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots=\frac\pi4}.
$$
No evaluation at a discontinuity or rearrangement of the conditionally convergent series is involved.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <Fourier sine series> automatically meets the zero-temperature <boundary conditions> because $\sin0=\sin(n\pi)=0$. Equivalently, extend $\Theta_0$ oddly from $(0,L)$ to $(-L,L)$ and then periodically with period $2L$. Its <Fourier series> has only sine terms, with
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad b_n=\frac2L\int_0^L\Theta_0(x)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx\quad(n\geq1)}.
$$
The coefficient formula follows from the <orthogonality> identity
$$
\int_0^L\sin(k_nx)\sin(k_mx)\,dx=\frac L2\delta_{nm}.
$$
For piecewise smooth initial data the series equals $\Theta_0$ at interior continuity points and the average of its one-sided values at interior jumps. More general square-integrable data have convergence in <L2 norm convergence>. A classical solution continuous at the initial corners additionally requires $\Theta_0(0)=\Theta_0(L)=0$, in agreement with the prescribed <boundary conditions>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Substitute the proposed <Fourier sine series> into the <heat equation>:
$$
\sum_{n\geq1}b_nT_n'(t)\sin(k_nx)
=-D\sum_{n\geq1}b_nk_n^2T_n(t)\sin(k_nx).
$$
Projecting onto each sine using <orthogonality> gives $T_n'=-Dk_n^2T_n$ for every mode with $b_n\ne0$. The <initial condition> fixes $T_n(0)=1$, since $b_n$ already describes the initial amplitude. Hence
$$
\boxed{T_n(t)=e^{-Dk_n^2t},\qquad
\Theta(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n e^{-D(n\pi/L)^2t}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)}.
$$
For a mode with $b_n=0$, the value of $T_n$ is immaterial and the same convention is convenient. The positive <thermal diffusivity> $D$ damps every mode, with higher frequencies decaying faster. For square-integrable initial data the damping justifies termwise differentiation for $t>0$ and recovery of the initial data in <L2 norm convergence> as $t\downarrow0$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <critical points> satisfy both first <partial derivatives> equal to zero:
$$
h_x=2x+y=0,\qquad h_y=x+3y^2=0.
$$
Substitute $x=-y/2$ into the second equation to get $y(3y-1/2)=0$. Thus the candidates are $(0,0)$ and $(-1/12,1/6)$.

The <Hessian matrix> and its determinant are
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&6y\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det H=12y-1.
$$
At $(0,0)$ the determinant is $-1$, so the <Hessian matrix> is indefinite and the point is a **<saddle point>**. At $(-1/12,1/6)$ the determinant is $1$ and $h_{xx}=2>0$, so the <Hessian matrix> is positive definite and the point is a **strict <local minimum>**, with value
$$
\boxed{h(-1/12,1/6)=-\frac1{432}}.
$$
There is no local maximum. Along $x=0$, $h(0,y)=y^3$ is unbounded above and below, so there are no global extrema either.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The constraint is a <compact set>, so the continuous objective attains both extrema by the <extreme value theorem>. Use <Lagrange multipliers> with $q(x,y)=9x^2+y^2$:
$$
(4,1)=\lambda(18x,2y).
$$
Since the second component makes $\lambda\ne0$, the two equations give $4/(18x)=1/(2y)$, or $y=9x/4$. Substitution into the constraint yields
$$
\frac{225}{16}x^2=36,\qquad x=\pm\frac85,\quad y=\pm\frac{18}5,
$$
with matching signs. The objective values are therefore
$$
\boxed{\max(4x+y)=10\text{ at }(8/5,18/5),\qquad
\min(4x+y)=-10\text{ at }(-8/5,-18/5)}.
$$
The constraint gradient never vanishes on the ellipse, so these are all the possible extrema. As a direct check, set $u=3x$, $v=y$; then $u^2+v^2=36$ and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|4u/3+v|\leq6\sqrt{16/9+1}=10$, with equality at the same points.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Rewrite the <rational function> as
$$
g(x)=\frac12\left(1-\frac1{x-1}\right).
$$
If $x>2$, then $x-1>1$, so $0<1/(x-1)<1$. Subtracting from $1$ and dividing by $2$ proves
$$
\boxed{0<g(x)<\frac12\quad(x>2)}.
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The tangent line to $f$ at $x_n$ is $y=f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(x-x_n)$. Its intersection with the horizontal axis gives <Newton's method>:
$$
\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}.
$$
Each step requires $f'(x_n)\ne0$. Convergence to a <zero of a function> additionally depends on the starting point and the local behavior of $f$; the update formula alone is not a convergence guarantee.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $f'(x)=2x-2$, <Newton's method> becomes
$$
\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{x_n(x_n-2)}{2(x_n-1)}=\frac{x_n^2}{2(x_n-1)}}\qquad(x_n\ne1).
$$
For $x_n>2$, subtraction from $2$ gives
$$
x_{n+1}-2=\frac{(x_n-2)^2}{2(x_n-1)}>0.
$$
Also $x_n-x_{n+1}=x_n(x_n-2)/(2(x_n-1))>0$. Hence **$2<x_{n+1}<x_n$**. Equivalently, the positive excess above $2$ is multiplied by the <function> from part (c): $x_{n+1}-2=g(x_n)(x_n-2)$, with $0<g(x_n)<1/2$.

Thus the iterates form a decreasing <sequence> bounded below by $2$. By the <monotone convergence theorem for sequences> they have a limit $\ell\geq2$. Passing to the limit in the continuous update gives $2\ell(\ell-1)=\ell^2$, or $\ell(\ell-2)=0$. Therefore **$x_n\to2$**. The exact error formula also shows <quadratic convergence> near this root.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

For $1<x_0<2$, the same <Newton's method> formula gives
$$
x_1-2=\frac{(x_0-2)^2}{2(x_0-1)}>0.
$$
Thus the first step **overshoots the root to $x_1>2$**. Part (e) then applies from index $1$: the remaining <sequence> decreases to $2$. The first step can be very large when $x_0$ is close to $1$, where the derivative vanishes, but every starting value strictly between $1$ and $2$ still gives **convergence to $2$**. The entire sequence is not decreasing, since $x_1>2>x_0$.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable>, its <expectation> is the probability-weighted sum
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\,\mathbb P(X=j)}.
$$
All terms are nonnegative, so this is always defined as an extended nonnegative number, possibly $+\infty$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Because the $B_i$ partition the <sample space>, the events $A\cap B_i$ are disjoint and their union is $A$. Finite additivity and the definition of <conditional probability> give the <law of total probability>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i)}.
$$
The assumption $\mathbb P(B_i)>0$ ensures that each conditional probability is defined.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <conditional expectation> given an event of positive probability is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X\mid B_i)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\,\mathbb P(X=j\mid B_i)}.
$$
Apply the <law of total probability> to each event $\{X=j\}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E(X)
&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb P(X=j\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb P(B_i)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\,\mathbb P(X=j\mid B_i)\\
&=\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb P(B_i)\mathbb E(X\mid B_i)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <law of total expectation>. Interchanging the sums is justified by nonnegative terms, for example by <Tonelli theorem>, so it remains valid when the expectation is infinite.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $q=(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)$, the probability of surviving a full day, and $p=1-q=\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta$. These lie in $(0,1)$. Let $M$ be the event that the first breakdown is in the morning. By <independence>, surviving $d-1$ complete days and then failing in the morning of day $d$ has probability $q^{d-1}\alpha$.

These events are disjoint, so summing the <geometric series> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M)=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\alpha q^{d-1}=\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta}}.
$$
There is no missing probability of never breaking down: survival for $d$ days has probability $q^d\to0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $J$ count successful journeys before the first breakdown. The probability of surviving the first $r$ journeys is the <tail probability> $\mathbb P(J\geq r)$. For $d\geq0$, <independence> gives
$$
\mathbb P(J\geq2d+1)=q^d(1-\alpha),\qquad
\mathbb P(J\geq2d+2)=q^{d+1},
$$
where again $q=(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)$. For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable>, the <tail-sum formula> follows by writing $J=\sum_{r\geq1}\mathbf1_{\{J\geq r\}}$ and interchanging nonnegative sums. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E(J)
&=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left(q^d(1-\alpha)+q^{d+1}\right)\\
&=\boxed{\frac{(1-\alpha)+q}{1-q}=\frac{(1-\alpha)(2-\beta)}{\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
As a separate first-day check using the <law of total expectation>, if $E=\mathbb E(J)$, then $E=\alpha\cdot0+(1-\alpha)\beta\cdot1+q(2+E)$. Solving gives the same finite value. This counts only successful journeys, excluding the failed journey.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $D$ be the number of days up to and including the first breakdown, and $M$ the event of a morning first breakdown. For $d=1,2,\ldots$, <conditional probability> gives
$$
\mathbb P(D=d\mid M)=\frac{\alpha q^{d-1}}{\alpha/(1-q)}=(1-q)q^{d-1}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{D\mid M\sim\operatorname{Geom}(\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta)\text{ on }\{1,2,\ldots\}}.
$$
This <geometric distribution> counts the day on which the first breakdown occurs. Its parameter is the probability of at least one breakdown during a day, rather than the morning breakdown probability $\alpha$ alone.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <exponential series> gives $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^n/n!=e^\lambda$. Thus the normalization constant for the <Poisson distribution> is
$$
\boxed{c_\lambda=e^{-\lambda}}.
$$
Every value $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^n/n!$ is nonnegative, and their sum is $e^{-\lambda}e^\lambda=1$. Hence this is a valid <probability mass function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

By definition, the <probability generating function> is
$$
G_X(s)=\mathbb E(s^X)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s^n\mathbb P(X=n).
$$
Substituting the <Poisson distribution> and evaluating the <exponential series> gives
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda s)^n}{n!}=e^{\lambda(s-1)}}.
$$
The series converges for every finite complex $s$; in particular it gives the <probability generating function> on $0\leq s\leq1$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For <independent random variables>, the <probability generating function of an independent sum> is the product of the individual <probability generating functions>. Thus
$$
G_{X+Y}(s)=e^{\lambda(s-1)}e^{\mu(s-1)}=e^{(\lambda+\mu)(s-1)}.
$$
Expanding this last expression as $e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\sum_{r\geq0}(\lambda+\mu)^rs^r/r!$ identifies each probability coefficient. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X+Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda+\mu)}.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq k\leq n$, the event $\{X=k,X+Y=n\}$ is $\{X=k,Y=n-k\}$. Use <independence> in its numerator and the <Poisson distribution> of the sum in its denominator:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X=k\mid X+Y=n)
&=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\;e^{-\mu}\mu^{n-k}/(n-k)!}{e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}(\lambda+\mu)^n/n!}\\
&=\boxed{\binom nk\left(\frac\lambda{\lambda+\mu}\right)^k\left(\frac\mu{\lambda+\mu}\right)^{n-k}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Outside $0\leq k\leq n$ the conditional probability is zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{X\mid(X+Y=n)\sim\operatorname{Bin}\!\left(n,\frac\lambda{\lambda+\mu}\right)}.
$$
This <binomial distribution> includes $n=0$, when it is concentrated at zero. Conditioning on the total converts the independent counts into an allocation of that total between the two rates.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n\geq2$, $X_2+\cdots+X_n$ is independent of $X_1$ and has a <Poisson distribution> with parameter $(n-1)\lambda$. Part (ii) therefore gives
$$
X_1\mid(X_1+\cdots+X_n=n)\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/n).
$$
Its probability at zero is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_1=0\mid X_1+\cdots+X_n=n)=\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\quad(n\geq1)}.
$$
For $n=1$ the event being conditioned on is $X_1=1$, so the answer is $0$, also matching the displayed expression. The cancellation of $\lambda$ reflects the equal rates of all $n$ counts, not an approximation or large-$n$ limit.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x)}.
$$
For an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>, the <probability density function> is a nonnegative integrable <function> $f_X$ with
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(u)\,du,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(u)\,du=1}.
$$
Thus interval probabilities are <integrals> of $f_X$, and $F_X'=f_X$ almost everywhere, in particular at continuity points of $f_X$.

The word “continuous” in the source is understood in this density sense. A merely continuous <cumulative distribution function> need not possess a <probability density function>; it can instead describe a <singular continuous distribution>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>, its <expectation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx}.
$$
This is finite when $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|f_X(x)\,dx<\infty$. More generally it is an extended real value if only one of the positive and negative parts diverges; it is undefined if both diverge. For nonnegative $X$ it always exists in $[0,\infty]$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $S(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)=1-F_X(x)$ for $x\geq0$. Since $f_X=0$ on the negative half-line, $X\geq0$ almost surely. We have $S'=-f_X$ almost everywhere. On a finite interval, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_0^R x f_X(x)\,dx
=-\int_0^R xS'(x)\,dx
=-RS(R)+\int_0^R S(x)\,dx.
$$
The lower endpoint contributes zero. The stated hypothesis gives $RS(R)\to0$ as $R\to\infty$. Taking the increasing limits of the two nonnegative <integrals> proves the <tail-integral identity>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X)=\int_0^{\infty}\mathbb P(X>x)\,dx}.
$$
The equality allows the value $+\infty$; the boundary hypothesis alone does not force a finite expectation. In fact the formula for nonnegative $X$ also follows directly from <Tonelli theorem>, without that additional boundary hypothesis, by integrating $x=\int_0^{\infty}\mathbf1_{\{t<x\}}\,dt$ against the <probability density function>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Translate the inequality inside the <cumulative distribution function>:
$$
F_Y(y)=\mathbb P(1+X\leq y)=\mathbb P(X\leq y-1)=\boxed{F_X(y-1)}.
$$
Differentiating with the <chain rule> gives the <probability density function>
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=f_X(y-1)}
$$
almost everywhere. Translation shifts the <probability distribution> without changing its shape or its total probability.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Reversing sign reverses the inequality. Since $X$ has a <probability density function>, it has no point masses, so
$$
F_Y(y)=\mathbb P(X\geq-y)=1-\mathbb P(X<-y)=\boxed{1-F_X(-y)}.
$$
Differentiating the <cumulative distribution function> gives
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=f_X(-y)}
$$
almost everywhere: the minus sign outside $F_X$ cancels the minus sign from the <chain rule>. If point masses were allowed, the <cumulative distribution function> would instead require the left limit $1-F_X((-y)^-)$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Since a square is nonnegative, $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y<0$. For $y\geq0$, the event $X^2\leq y$ is $-\sqrt y\leq X\leq\sqrt y$. As $X$ has no point masses,
$$
\boxed{F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
0,&y<0,\\
F_X(\sqrt y)-F_X(-\sqrt y),&y\geq0.
\end{cases}}
$$
Differentiating for $y>0$ gives the contributions of the two inverse branches:
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{f_X(\sqrt y)+f_X(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y},&y>0,\\
0,&y<0.
\end{cases}}
$$
The value assigned to the <probability density function> at $y=0$ is arbitrary. It can be unbounded near $0$, but there is no atom there because $\mathbb P(X=0)=0$. Both positive and negative preimages must be included; keeping just one would generally lose probability.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For any real $y$, put $k=\lfloor y\rfloor$. Since $Y$ is integer-valued,
$$
\{Y\leq y\}=\{\lfloor X\rfloor\leq k\}=\{X<k+1\}.
$$
Continuity of the <cumulative distribution function> of $X$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F_Y(y)=F_X(\lfloor y\rfloor+1)}.
$$
The strict inequality $X<k+1$ matters before using the absence of point masses. The resulting <probability distribution> is discrete, with <probability mass function>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y=k)=F_X(k+1)-F_X(k)\quad(k\in\mathbb Z)}.
$$
Thus **$Y$ has no ordinary <probability density function>** with respect to length on the real line. Its <cumulative distribution function> is a step function; its derivative is zero almost everywhere and does not recover its probabilities. The <floor function> has converted a density into masses on the integers.
