===== Solution

We prove both <set inclusions>, as required by a <double inclusion proof>. If $x\in A\setminus(B\cup C)$, then $x\in A$ and $x\notin B\cup C$. Thus $x\notin B$ and $x\notin C$, so $x\in A\setminus B$ and $x\in A\setminus C$. This proves the forward <set inclusion>.

Conversely, if $x\in(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C)$, then $x\in A$, $x\notin B$, and $x\notin C$. Hence $x\notin B\cup C$, giving $x\in A\setminus(B\cup C)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A\setminus(B\cup C)=(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the first <set inclusion>, take $x\in(A\cap B)\setminus C$. Membership in the <intersection> gives $x\in A$ and $x\in B$, while the <set difference> gives $x\notin C$. Consequently $x\in(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$.

For the reverse <set inclusion>, an element of $(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C)$ belongs to both $A$ and $B$ and does not belong to $C$. It therefore belongs to $(A\cap B)\setminus C$. By <double inclusion proof>,
$$
\boxed{(A\cap B)\setminus C=(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C).}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Complements are relative to the same <universal set> $\mathcal U$. Suppose $A\subseteq B$ and $x\in\overline B$. Then $x\in\mathcal U$ and $x\notin B$. If $x$ belonged to $A$, the assumed <set inclusion> would imply $x\in B$, a contradiction. Thus $x\notin A$ and $x\in\overline A$, proving
$$
\boxed{A\subseteq B\ \Longrightarrow\ \overline B\subseteq\overline A.}
$$
There is only one <set inclusion> in this conclusion; a reverse inclusion is neither needed nor generally true. The request for double inclusion applies to the equalities in (i) and (ii).

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the definition $A\oplus B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$ of <symmetric difference>. The algebraic laws for <set difference> and the <empty set> give
$$
A\oplus\varnothing=(A\setminus\varnothing)\cup(\varnothing\setminus A)=A\cup\varnothing=\boxed A,
$$
and
$$
A\oplus A=(A\setminus A)\cup(A\setminus A)=\varnothing\cup\varnothing=\boxed\varnothing.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $A\subseteq\mathcal U$, the <set difference> $A\setminus\mathcal U$ is empty and $\mathcal U\setminus A=\overline A$. Therefore the <symmetric difference> satisfies
$$
A\oplus\mathcal U=(A\setminus\mathcal U)\cup(\mathcal U\setminus A)=\varnothing\cup\overline A=\boxed{\overline A}.
$$
The <disjoint sets> $A$ and $\overline A$ have <union> $\mathcal U$, so
$$
A\oplus\overline A=(A\setminus\overline A)\cup(\overline A\setminus A)=A\cup\overline A=\boxed{\mathcal U}.
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply <symmetric difference> with $C$ to both sides, then use its <associativity> and the identities in (b)(i):
$$
(A\oplus C)\oplus C=(B\oplus C)\oplus C
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A\oplus(C\oplus C)=B\oplus(C\oplus C)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A\oplus\varnothing=B\oplus\varnothing.
$$
The final identity gives **$A=B$**. This is cancellation by applying the same operation twice.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix one distinguished element $a$ of the $n$-element <set>. Every <partition of a set> into $l$ blocks belongs to exactly one of two cases. If $\{a\}$ is a block, removing it leaves an $(l-1)$-block <partition of a set> of the other $n-1$ elements, giving $S(n-1,l-1)$ possibilities. Otherwise remove $a$ from its block; that block remains nonempty, so the result has $l$ blocks. Conversely choose any of the $S(n-1,l)$ such partitions and insert $a$ in any one of its $l$ blocks. This gives $lS(n-1,l)$ possibilities.

These cases are disjoint and exhaustive, proving the <Stirling numbers of the second kind> recurrence
$$
\boxed{S(n,l)=lS(n-1,l)+S(n-1,l-1).}
$$
Use $S(0,0)=1$, $S(n,0)=0$ for $n>0$, and $S(n,l)=0$ for $l>n$; these conventions include the boundary cases $n=1$ and $l=1$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Starting from $n$ singleton blocks, a <partition of a set> into $n-1$ nonempty blocks has exactly one block of size two and all other blocks of size one: the sum of the block sizes exceeds the number of blocks by exactly one. Choosing that pair determines the partition uniquely. Hence
$$
\boxed{S(n,n-1)=\binom n2,\qquad k=2.}
$$
This fixed value of $k$ works for every $n\geq2$. It is forced already by $n=2$: $S(2,1)=1$, whereas for positive $k$ the only $\binom2k$ equal to one is $\binom22$.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Each of the $m$ elements of $A$ independently receives one of $n$ images in $B$. The <multiplication principle> therefore gives **$n^m$ functions**. In particular an <empty set> domain has exactly one function to any $B$, so here $0^0$ is interpreted as $1$; a nonempty domain has no function to an <empty set> codomain.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <bijection> between <finite sets> requires equal <cardinality>. When $m=n$, list the elements of $A$ and choose their distinct images in $n,n-1,\ldots,1$ ways. Thus
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{bijections }A\to B\}=\begin{cases}n!&m=n,\\0&m\ne n.\end{cases}}
$$
The <factorial> convention $0!=1$ includes the unique <bijection> from the <empty set> to itself.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <surjection>, the nonempty <fibres> $f^{-1}(b)$ form a <partition of a set> $A$ into exactly $n$ blocks. Choose these unlabelled blocks in $S(m,n)$ ways, then assign the $n$ distinct elements of $B$ to them in $n!$ ways. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{surjections }A\to B\}=n!S(m,n).}
$$
It is zero when $m<n$. With $S(0,0)=1$ and $S(m,0)=0$ for $m>0$, the same formula also handles an <empty set> codomain.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose the <image of a function> as a $j$-element <subset> of $B$, then choose a <surjection> onto that selected image as in (iii). Therefore
$$
\boxed{\#\{f:A\to B:|\operatorname{Im}f|=j\}=\binom nj j!S(m,j)\quad(0\leq j\leq\min(m,n)).}
$$
The answer is zero when $j>n$. If $j=0$, it is one for $m=0$ and zero for $m>0$, as the <Stirling numbers of the second kind> conventions require.

==== Solution

Count pairs $(M,K)$ of <subsets> of an $n$-element candidate <set> with $K\subseteq M$, $|M|=m$, and $|K|=k$. Choosing the whole team $M$ first and then the special-role players $K$ gives $\binom nm\binom mk$. Alternatively choose $K$ first, then choose the other $m-k$ players from the $n-k$ candidates outside $K$, giving $\binom nk\binom{n-k}{m-k}$. Each construction specifies the same pair uniquely. Thus <double counting> proves
$$
\boxed{\binom nm\binom mk=\binom nk\binom{n-k}{m-k}.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the wiki convention that paths follow the written order of the <binary relations>, <composition of relations> is
$$
\boxed{R\circ S=\{(x,z)\in A\times A:\exists y\in A,\ (x,y)\in R\text{ and }(y,z)\in S\}.}
$$
Some treatments reverse the naming convention; stating it explicitly avoids ambiguity. The powers of the single relation $R$ below are the same under either convention.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A pair belongs to $R\circ R$ exactly when two successive $R$ steps connect its endpoints. The chain $a\to b\to c\to d$ has two such length-two paths, $a\to b\to c$ and $b\to c\to d$. Hence
$$
\boxed{R\circ R=\{(a,c),(b,d)\}.}
$$
This applies <composition of relations> directly; no other middle element gives a pair.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <relation powers> are $R^2=\{(a,c),(b,d)\}$, $R^3=\{(a,d)\}$, and $R^i=\varnothing$ for $i\geq4$. For $k=1$, $(a,b),(b,c)\in T$ but $(a,c)\notin T$, so <transitivity> fails. For $k=2$, $(a,c),(c,d)\in T$ but $(a,d)\notin T$, so it still fails.

For $k=3$, $T$ consists of every pair in which the first endpoint precedes the second in $a,b,c,d$. Composing any two such pairs again gives such a pair, so $T$ is <transitive>. Thus **the smallest $k$ is $3$**; this $T$ is the <transitive closure of a relation> $R$.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <partially ordered set> $(A,\preceq)$, the assertion $m=\operatorname{lub}(S)$ means both
$$
\boxed{(\forall s\in S,\ s\preceq m)\quad\text{and}\quad
(\forall u\in A,\ [\forall s\in S,\ s\preceq u]\Rightarrow m\preceq u).}
$$
The first condition makes $m$ an <upper bound>; the second makes it the <least upper bound>. Both conditions are necessary, and <antisymmetry> makes such an element unique.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose $m$ is the <greatest lower bound> of the <set> $X$ of all <upper bounds> of $S$. Every $s\in S$ satisfies $s\preceq x$ for every $x\in X$, so each such $s$ is a <lower bound> of $X$. Since $m$ is the greatest of those <lower bounds>, $s\preceq m$ for every $s\in S$. Thus $m$ itself is an <upper bound> of $S$, meaning $m\in X$.

Also $m$, being a <lower bound> of $X$, satisfies $m\preceq x$ for every $x\in X$. It is therefore an <upper bound> of $S$ below every other <upper bound>, which proves **$m=\operatorname{lub}(S)$**. The argument also covers empty $S$ or $X$ whenever the assumed <greatest lower bound> exists.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $H_n=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-k}{k}$; the other summands in the printed sum vanish. The initial values are $H_0=1=F_1$ and $H_1=1=F_2$. For $n\geq2$, <Pascal's identity> gives
$$
H_n=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-1-k}{k}
+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}
=H_{n-1}+H_{n-2}.
$$
For the first sum, when $n$ is even its final term is zero; otherwise its range already agrees with $H_{n-1}$. In the second sum put $j=k-1$, obtaining $\binom{n-2-j}{j}$ for $0\leq j\leq\lfloor(n-2)/2\rfloor$. Thus both range identifications hold without negative upper arguments in a <binomial coefficient>.

Now <strong induction> gives $H_n=F_n+F_{n-1}=F_{n+1}$ from the two preceding cases and the <Fibonacci recurrence>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^n\binom{n-k}{k}=F_{n+1}\quad(n\geq0).}
$$

=== a

==== Solution

We prove the <contrapositive>. If $3\nmid a$, <Euclidean division> gives $a=3q+1$ or $a=3q+2$ for an <integer> $q$. Squaring gives respectively $a^2=3(3q^2+2q)+1$ or $a^2=3(3q^2+4q+1)+1$. Both have remainder $1$ upon division by $3$, so $3\nmid a^2$. Taking the <contrapositive> yields **$3\mid a^2\Rightarrow3\mid a$**.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <proof by contradiction>, suppose $\sqrt3=a/b$ for positive <integers> $a,b$ in lowest terms, so $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Squaring gives $a^2=3b^2$, and part (a) implies $3\mid a$. Write $a=3c$; then $9c^2=3b^2$, hence $b^2=3c^2$, and part (a) again implies $3\mid b$. This contradicts the coprimality of $a,b$. Therefore **$\sqrt3$ is an <irrational number>**.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The fifty <disjoint sets> $\{1,100\},\{2,99\},\ldots,\{50,51\}$ form a <partition of a set> $\{1,\ldots,100\}$. If a chosen <subset> met each pair at most once, it would contain at most fifty elements. The <pigeonhole principle> therefore forces a $51$-element <subset> to contain both elements of at least one pair. **Those two distinct elements sum to $101$.**

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume $n\geq1$ and list the elements of $X$ as $a_1,\ldots,a_n$. Define the <partial sums> $s_j=a_1+\cdots+a_j$ for $1\leq j\leq n$. If one is divisible by $n$, its corresponding nonempty prefix is a suitable $Y$.

Otherwise the $n$ <partial sums> have remainders in the $n-1$ nonzero <residue classes>. The <pigeonhole principle> gives $i<j$ with $s_i\equiv s_j\pmod n$. Thus $s_j-s_i=a_{i+1}+\cdots+a_j$ is divisible by $n$, giving a nonempty <subset> $Y=\{a_{i+1},\ldots,a_j\}$.

Therefore **a nonempty $Y$ exists**. This strengthens the literal assertion, which would otherwise allow the trivial <empty set> with sum zero.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $h(n)=\max\{f(n),g(n)\}$, both $f(n)\leq h(n)$ and $g(n)\leq h(n)$. Therefore $f(n)+g(n)\leq2h(n)$ for every positive $n$. The definition of <big O notation> is satisfied with constant $2$ and threshold $1$. **True: $f+g=O(h)$.**

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Positivity gives $\max\{f(n),g(n)\}\leq f(n)+g(n)$. Thus $h(n)\leq f(n)+g(n)$ for every positive $n$, meeting <big O notation> with constant $1$ and threshold $1$. **True: $h=O(f+g)$.**

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take $f(n)=g(n)=n^2$, so $h(n)=n^2$ but $(f\circ g)(n)=n^4$. The ratio $(f\circ g)(n)/h(n)=n^2$ is unbounded, precluding any eventual constant upper bound in <big O notation>. **False: in general $f\circ g\ne O(h)$.** This is a <counterexample> involving <function composition>, not pointwise addition.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For each fixed positive <integer> $k$, part (i) gives $(f(n)+g(n))^k\leq(2h(n))^k=2^kh(n)^k$. This meets <big O notation> with constant $2^k$ and threshold $1$. **True: $(f+g)^k=O(h^k)$ for fixed $k$.** The constant may depend on $k$; no claim uniform in a varying $k$ is required.

=== f
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Prove the explicit bound $n!\leq2^{n^2}$ by <mathematical induction>. At $n=1$, $1\leq2$. For the step, note that $n+1\leq2^n$ for $n\geq1$: this auxiliary inequality has base $2=2$ and follows at the next index from $n+2\leq2(n+1)$. Hence, using the <induction hypothesis>,
$$
(n+1)!\leq(n+1)2^{n^2}\leq2^{n^2+n}\leq2^{(n+1)^2}.
$$
Thus **$n!=O((2^n)^n)$**, with constant $1$ and threshold $1$ in <big O notation>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

We prove the stronger explicit bound $n!\leq n^n/2^{n-1}$ by <mathematical induction>. The case $n=1$ is equality. The <binomial theorem> gives $(1+1/n)^n\geq1+n/n=2$, so $(n+1)^n\geq2n^n$. Consequently the <induction hypothesis> yields
$$
(n+1)!\leq\frac{(n+1)n^n}{2^{n-1}}\leq\frac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{2^n}.
$$
Therefore **$n!=O(n^n/2^n)$**, with constant $2$ and threshold $1$.

The second bound is **asymptotically tighter**. Using $n\leq2^n$,
$$
\frac{n^n/2^n}{2^{n^2}}=\left(\frac{n}{2^{n+1}}\right)^n\leq2^{-n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus its bounding function is negligible relative to the first in <little o notation>; this compares the bounds without needing <Stirling's formula>.

==== Solution

Use <partial fraction decomposition>:
$$
\frac1{x^2(x^2-4)}=-\frac1{4x^2}+\frac1{4(x^2-4)}
=-\frac1{4x^2}+\frac1{16}\left(\frac1{x-2}-\frac1{x+2}\right).
$$
Integrating each term gives the <antiderivative>
$$
\boxed{\int\frac{dx}{x^2(x^2-4)}=\frac1{4x}+\frac1{16}\log\left|\frac{x-2}{x+2}\right|+C.}
$$
This holds on any interval avoiding $-2,0,2$; the constant may differ between these intervals. <Differentiation> of the logarithm recovers $1/[4(x^2-4)]$, providing a direct check.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <characteristic equation of a constant-coefficient differential equation> here is $r^2-3r+2=(r-1)(r-2)=0$. Its <homogeneous solution> is $C_1e^x+C_2e^{2x}$. Because the forcing $e^x$ is already a homogeneous mode, the <method of undetermined coefficients> requires $y_p=Axe^x$ rather than $Ae^x$.

Here $y_p'=A(x+1)e^x$ and $y_p''=A(x+2)e^x$, so $y_p''-3y_p'+2y_p=-Ae^x$. Thus $A=-1$, and the <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1e^x+C_2e^{2x}-xe^x.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <gradient> vanishes when $4x+3y-1=0$ and $3x+2y-1=0$. Subtracting these equations gives $x+y=0$, so the unique <critical point> is $\boxed{(x,y)=(1,-1)}$, with $f(1,-1)=0$.

The <Hessian matrix> is
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\3&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det H=8-9=-1<0.
$$
It is indefinite, so the <critical point> is a **<saddle point>**. Explicitly, around that point the change is exactly $2u^2+3uv+v^2$: it is positive for $v=0,u\ne0$ and negative for $v=-3u/2,u\ne0$. Thus arbitrarily close points have both larger and smaller values.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

On the unit circle the constraint <gradient> $(2x,2y)$ never vanishes. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations are
$$
8x-3y=2\lambda x,\qquad -3x=2\lambda y,\qquad x^2+y^2=1.
$$
A nonzero solution of the first two equations requires
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}8-2\lambda&-3\\-3&-2\lambda\end{pmatrix}
=4\lambda^2-16\lambda-9=0,
$$
so $\lambda=9/2$ or $\lambda=-1/2$. Multiplying the first equation by $x$, the second by $y$, and adding shows $2f=2\lambda(x^2+y^2)=2\lambda$. Thus each candidate value of $f$ equals its multiplier.

For $\lambda=9/2$, $y=-x/3$, giving $(x,y)=\pm(3,-1)/\sqrt{10}$. For $\lambda=-1/2$, $y=3x$, giving $(x,y)=\pm(1,3)/\sqrt{10}$. Since a continuous function attains its extrema on the <compact set> given by the <unit circle> and these exhaust the <Lagrange multiplier> candidates,
$$
\boxed{f_{\max}=\frac92\text{ at }\pm\frac{(3,-1)}{\sqrt{10}},\qquad
f_{\min}=-\frac12\text{ at }\pm\frac{(1,3)}{\sqrt{10}}.}
$$
Each $\pm$ changes both coordinates together.

The <chain rule> gives $\frac{d}{dx}\cos(\mu x)=-\mu\sin(\mu x)$. Dividing by the nonzero constant $-\mu$ gives the <antiderivative>
$$
\boxed{\int\sin(\mu x)\,dx=-\frac{\cos(\mu x)}\mu+C.}

===== Solution

Apply <integration by parts> with $u=x$ and $dv=\sin(\mu x)\,dx$. Part (i) gives $v=-\cos(\mu x)/\mu$, so
$$
\int x\sin(\mu x)\,dx=-\frac{x\cos(\mu x)}\mu+\frac1\mu\int\cos(\mu x)\,dx
=\boxed{-\frac{x\cos(\mu x)}\mu+\frac{\sin(\mu x)}{\mu^2}+C.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <Fourier sine series> write $u_0(x)=\sum_{n\geq1}b_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$. <Orthogonality of trigonometric functions> gives
$$
b_n=\frac2L\int_0^L u_0(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx.
$$
In the second half of the integral put $z=L-x$; then $u_0(L-z)=\rho z$ and $\sin[n\pi(L-z)/L]=(-1)^{n+1}\sin(n\pi z/L)$. Hence
$$
b_n=\frac{2\rho}L[1+(-1)^{n+1}]\int_0^{L/2}x\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx.
$$
Every even coefficient is zero. For odd $n$, part (a) or the supplied integral yields
$$
b_n=\frac{4L\rho}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi}2.
$$
Putting $n=2m+1$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{u_0(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}F_m\sin\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}L,\qquad
F_m=(-1)^m\frac{4L\rho}{(2m+1)^2\pi^2}.}
$$
This is the <Fourier sine series of a symmetric tent>. The absolute values of its terms are uniformly bounded by a summable sequence, giving <absolute convergence> and <uniform convergence>; the piecewise smooth odd extension and the <Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series> identify its value with the continuous $u_0$, including the zero endpoints and the peak.

**The printed lower limit $m=1$ is an error.** The original PDF visibly has this lower limit. It omits the nonzero fundamental coefficient $F_0=4L\rho/\pi^2$. In particular, the printed series equals $u_0(x)-F_0\sin(\pi x/L)$, rather than $u_0(x)$. At $x=L/2$ it gives $L\rho/2-4L\rho/\pi^2$ instead of $L\rho/2$. The corrected lower limit is displayed explicitly above.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply <separation of variables> to each mode of the <damped wave equation> $u_{tt}+2k^2u_t=c^2u_{xx}$, assuming $L,c>0$. The spatial <ordinary differential equation> is $X_n''+(\lambda_n/c)^2X_n=0$. Its general solution is $C\sin(\lambda_n x/c)+D\cos(\lambda_n x/c)$. The boundary condition at $0$ gives $D=0$; a nonzero mode at $L$ requires
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=\frac{n\pi c}L,\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\qquad X_n(x)=\sin\frac{n\pi x}L.}
$$
Only odd $n=2m+1$ occur in the initial <Fourier sine series>. Put $\omega_m=(2m+1)\pi c/L$ and $d_m=\sqrt{k^4-\omega_m^2}$. The temporal equation $T_m''+2k^2T_m'+\omega_m^2T_m=0$ has roots $-k^2\pm d_m$. Define
$$
\boxed{\alpha_m=k^2-d_m,\qquad\beta_m=k^2+d_m.}
$$
For distinct roots, let the full amplitude be $q_m(t)=A_me^{-\alpha_mt}+B_me^{-\beta_mt}$. The initial displacement and zero initial velocity impose $A_m+B_m=F_m$ and $\alpha_mA_m+\beta_mB_m=0$. Solving gives
$$
\boxed{A_m=\frac{\beta_mF_m}{\beta_m-\alpha_m},\qquad
B_m=-\frac{\alpha_mF_m}{\beta_m-\alpha_m}.}
$$
Thus the physically consistent superposition is
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\sum_{m=0}^\infty q_m(t)\sin\frac{(2m+1)\pi x}L.}
$$
For $\omega_m<k^2$ there is <overdamping>, with two real decaying exponentials. For $\omega_m>k^2$ there is <underdamping>; set $\nu_m=\sqrt{\omega_m^2-k^4}$; the equivalent real expression is
$$
q_m(t)=F_me^{-k^2t}\left(\cos(\nu_mt)+\frac{k^2}{\nu_m}\sin(\nu_mt)\right).
$$
At <critical damping>, the repeated-root case $\omega_m=k^2$, take the limit, giving $q_m(t)=F_m(1+k^2t)e^{-k^2t}$. These formulas all satisfy $q_m(0)=F_m$ and $q_m'(0)=0$.

Three source qualifications matter. First, the sum must start at $m=0$, for the same reason as in (b). Second, the printed $X(x)=u_0(x)$ cannot hold for a single separated mode: the tent has $X''=0$ on each open linear segment but $X\ne0$, contradicting $X''=-(\lambda_n/c)^2X$ for positive $\lambda_n$, and its peak is not twice differentiable. Separation applies mode by mode, with the initial tent recovered by superposition. Third, no finite “large” $k$ makes every mode overdamped, because $\omega_m\to\infty$. The two-exponential expression permits complex conjugate roots, while a repeated root requires its displayed limiting form. The tent initial data are naturally interpreted through the convergent modal solution, rather than as a single smooth separated product.

==== i

===== Solution

Write the derivative equation as $f'(x)=\Phi(x,f(x))$ with initial approximation $y_0$. The <forward Euler method> uses the slope at the start of the step:
$$
\boxed{y_{i+1}=y_i+h\Phi(x_i,y_i),\qquad0\leq i<n.}
$$
If the derivative is a known function of $x$ alone, this is $y_{i+1}=y_i+hf'(x_i)$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $f'=\Phi(x,f)$, the <backward Euler method> uses the slope at the end of the step:
$$
\boxed{y_{i+1}=y_i+h\Phi(x_{i+1},y_{i+1}).}
$$
This generally requires solving an implicit equation for $y_{i+1}$. For a derivative depending only on $x$, it reduces to $y_{i+1}=y_i+hf'(x_{i+1})$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $f'=\Phi(x,f)$, the standard explicit <midpoint method> is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde y_{i+1/2}=y_i+\frac h2\Phi(x_i,y_i),\qquad
y_{i+1}=y_i+h\Phi(x_i+h/2,\widetilde y_{i+1/2}).}
$$
The <implicit midpoint rule> instead uses $y_{i+1}=y_i+h\Phi(x_i+h/2,(y_i+y_{i+1})/2)$. When the derivative depends only on $x$, both give $y_{i+1}=y_i+hf'(x_i+h/2)$.

The endpoint-only request in (e) suggests another possible convention: averaging the endpoint slopes, $y_{i+1}=y_i+\tfrac h2[\Phi(x_i,y_i)+\Phi(x_{i+1},y_{i+1})]$. This is the <trapezoidal rule>, and generally differs from the <implicit midpoint rule>. A further use of “midpoint” is the two-step <explicit midpoint linear multistep method>, $y_{i+1}=y_{i-1}+2h\Phi(x_i,y_i)$, which needs a second starting value. The printed question supplies no convention to distinguish these meanings; (e) spells out the resulting alternatives.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $h=(b-a)/n$, $g_i=g(x_i)$, and $z_0=0$. Under the endpoint-slope convention suggested by the requested connection, the recurrence is $z_{i+1}=z_i+\tfrac h2(g_i+g_{i+1})$. Summing telescopes, so
$$
\boxed{z_n=\frac{b-a}{n}\left[\frac{g_0+g_n}2+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}g_i\right]=T_n(g).}
$$
Since $G(b)=\int_a^b g(x)\,dx$ by the <fundamental theorem of calculus>, this recurrence gives exactly the <composite trapezoidal rule>. It replaces the average derivative over each strip by the arithmetic mean of its endpoint derivatives.

**For the standard <midpoint method>, the literal endpoint-only request is not valid for general $g$.** Both the explicit and implicit one-step midpoint formulas here give instead
$$
\boxed{z_n=h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g(x_i+h/2)=M_n(g),}
$$
the <composite midpoint quadrature rule>. It requires half-grid samples. These samples cannot be recovered exactly from $g_0,\ldots,g_n$ for arbitrary smooth $g$: the zero function and $g(x)=\prod_{j=0}^n(x-x_j)^2$ have identical grid values but different midpoint sums. For a smooth $g$, replacing each half-grid sample by $(g_i+g_{i+1})/2$ is an additional approximation, giving $T_n$ with an $O(h^2)$ total difference, rather than an identity. A precise useful relation, obtained by splitting the finer trapezoidal sum into old nodes and midpoints, is
$$
\boxed{T_{2n}(g)=\tfrac12[T_n(g)+M_n(g)],\qquad M_n(g)=2T_{2n}(g)-T_n(g).}
$$
Here $T_{2n}$ uses the finer half-grid, so it does not eliminate the missing data.

If “Mid-Point Method” means the two-step <explicit midpoint linear multistep method>, the recurrence is $z_{i+1}=z_{i-1}+2hg_i$. It gives $z_{2r}=2h\sum_{j=1}^{r}g_{2j-1}$ and $z_{2r+1}=z_1+2h\sum_{j=1}^{r}g_{2j}$, so an odd final index additionally needs a starting value $z_1$. For even $n=2r$, the result is the midpoint quadrature on $r$ double-width strips; equivalently $z_n=2T_n-T_{n/2}$. The question specifies neither even $n$ nor a starter. Thus the trapezoidal expression above is the natural endpoint-average interpretation, while the other common midpoint meanings require the qualifications just given. The ambiguity is present in the original PDF.

===== Solution

The $X_i$ are independent <Bernoulli random variables> with success parameter $p$. Their sum has the **<binomial distribution> $S_n\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$**, whose <probability mass function> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(S_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n.}
$$
Choose the $k$ successful events in $\binom nk$ ways; <independence> gives the same product probability for each choice. At $p=0$ or $p=1$, interpret the formula by its degenerate distribution or by continuity.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

An <indicator random variable> equals $1$ with probability $p$ and $0$ with probability $1-p$, so $\mathbb E(X_i)=1\cdot p+0\cdot(1-p)=p$. Also $X_i^2=X_i$ pointwise, whence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X_i)=\mathbb E(X_i^2)=p.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

By <linearity of expectation>, $\mathbb E(S_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n p=np$. Part (ii) gives $\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\mathbb E(X_i^2)-[\mathbb E(X_i)]^2=p-p^2$. <Independent random variables> have zero pairwise <covariance>, so the <variance> of their sum is the sum of their <variances>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(S_n)=np,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=np(1-p).}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $0<p<1$, <independence> of the remaining indicators gives
$$
\mathbb P(E_1\cap\{S_n=k\})=\binom{n-1}{k-1}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
=\frac kn\mathbb P(S_n=k).
$$
Because $0<k<n$, the latter probability is positive for interior $p$. The two events are <independent events> exactly when their joint probability equals $p\mathbb P(S_n=k)$, which is equivalent to $p=k/n$.

At $p=0$ or $p=1$, the event $\{S_n=k\}$ is null, and a null event is independent of every event. Therefore the full answer, including the degenerate endpoints, is
$$
\boxed{p\in\left\{0,\frac kn,1\right\}.}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Summing the <probability mass function> of the <binomial distribution> up to the largest permitted integer gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{0\leq k\leq\alpha n}\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}
=\mathbb P(S_n\leq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor)
=\mathbb P(S_n/n\leq\alpha).}
$$
The floor is implicit in the original integer-indexed sum.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <weak law of large numbers> gives $S_n/n\to p$ in probability. If $\alpha<p$, the event $S_n/n\leq\alpha$ implies $|S_n/n-p|\geq p-\alpha$, whose probability tends to zero. If $\alpha>p$, its complement $S_n/n>\alpha$ implies $|S_n/n-p|>\alpha-p$, whose probability also tends to zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(S_n/n\leq\alpha)=\begin{cases}0&\alpha<p,\\1&\alpha>p.\end{cases}}
$$
For an explicit independent bound, <Chebyshev's inequality> bounds either unwanted probability by $p(1-p)/[n(\alpha-p)^2]$. The boundary $\alpha=p$ is outside the two requested cases and is not decided by the <weak law of large numbers> alone.

==== i

===== Solution

A <partition of a set> $\Omega$ is a collection of nonempty <subsets> $B_i$ such that $B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing$ for $i\ne j$ and $\bigcup_{i\in I}B_i=\Omega$. Thus **every outcome lies in exactly one cell**. For a probability-space partition the cells must also be measurable <events>. Nonempty cells may nevertheless have probability zero.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For an <integrable random variable> $X$, the <law of total expectation> on the finite or countable measurable partition is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X)=\sum_{i:\,\mathbb P(B_i)>0}\mathbb P(B_i)\mathbb E(X\mid B_i).}
$$
Here $\mathbb E(X\mid B_i)=\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{B_i}]/\mathbb P(B_i)$ for positive-probability cells; null cells contribute zero and need no conditional value. The series is absolutely convergent since its absolute terms sum to at most $\mathbb E|X|$. For a nonnegative $X$, the same identity is valid with possible value $+\infty$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable> $X$, its <probability generating function> is
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E(s^X)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\mathbb P(X=j)s^j,\qquad0\leq s\leq1.}
$$
At $s=0$, use $0^0=1$ so $G_X(0)=\mathbb P(X=0)$. The series also converges absolutely for complex $|s|\leq1$; for $s$ near zero, its coefficients recover the <probability mass function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Summing a <geometric series> gives the <probability generating function> of the zero-based <geometric distribution>:
$$
\boxed{G_N(s)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty p(1-p)^js^j=\frac p{1-(1-p)s}.}
$$
The series converges for $|(1-p)s|<1$, in particular on $[0,1]$. At $p=1/2$, $G_N(s)=1/(2-s)$, $G_N'(s)=(2-s)^{-2}$, and $G_N''(s)=2(2-s)^{-3}$. The <factorial moment> identities give $\mathbb E(N)=G_N'(1)=1$ and $\mathbb E[N(N-1)]=G_N''(1)=2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(N)=1,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(N)=G_N''(1)+G_N'(1)-[G_N'(1)]^2=2.}
$$

==== i

===== Solution

Use the measurable partition $B_n=\{N=n\}$ for $n\geq0$, omitting any null cells. For $0\leq s\leq1$, the <random variable> $s^X$ is bounded, so the <law of total expectation> applies without moment assumptions. Conditional on $N=n$, the sum contains exactly $n$ terms. Independence of $N$ from the whole sequence preserves their joint law under this conditioning; independence of the $Y_k$ then gives
$$
\mathbb E(s^X\mid N=n)=\mathbb E\left(\prod_{k=1}^n s^{Y_k}\right)
=\prod_{k=1}^n\mathbb E(s^{Y_k})=[G_Y(s)]^n.
$$
For $n=0$ the <empty sum> is zero and the <empty product> is one, in agreement with the formula. Therefore the <probability generating function of an independent random sum> is
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\mathbb P(N=n)[G_Y(s)]^n=G_N(G_Y(s)).}
$$

===== Solution

For the <continuous uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$, the <probability density function> is
$$
\boxed{f_X(x)=\begin{cases}1&0\leq x\leq1,\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Changing the values at the endpoints does not change any probabilities, because individual points have zero probability.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <law of the unconscious statistician> gives the improper integral
$$
\mathbb E(X^p)=\int_0^1x^p\,dx
=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\frac{1-\varepsilon^{p+1}}{p+1}
=\boxed{\frac1{p+1}}\qquad(p>-1).
$$
In particular $\mathbb E(X)=1/2$ and $\mathbb E(X^2)=1/3$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac13-\frac14=\frac1{12}.}
$$
For $p=-1$, $\int_\varepsilon^1x^{-1}dx=-\log\varepsilon\to+\infty$; for $p<-1$, the power integral also tends to $+\infty$. Thus **$\mathbb E(X^p)=+\infty$ for $p\leq-1$**, so there is no finite <expected value>. Negative powers need only be defined almost surely on $X>0$; the null event $X=0$ has no effect on this conclusion.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <covariance> identity and the <moments> from (ii):
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,X^2)=\mathbb E(X^3)-\mathbb E(X)\mathbb E(X^2)
=\frac14-\frac12\cdot\frac13=\boxed{\frac1{12}}.
$$
The positive value is consistent with both $X$ and $X^2$ increasing together on $[0,1]$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

First $\mu>0$. If a nonnegative <random variable> had zero <expected value>, it would equal zero almost surely, which is impossible for a distribution having a <probability density function> on $[0,\infty)$: a singleton has probability zero. Thus the given division by $\mu$ is legitimate.

Because $x\geq0$ and $f(x)\geq0$, $g(x)=xf(x)/\mu\geq0$. Moreover
$$
\int_0^\infty g(x)\,dx=\frac1\mu\int_0^\infty xf(x)\,dx=\frac\mu\mu=1.
$$
Extend $g$ by zero for negative $x$. **It is a <probability density function>**, defining the <size-biased distribution> of $X$: larger observations receive weight proportional to their value.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <law of the unconscious statistician> and the finite <variance> give
$$
\mathbb E(Y)=\int_0^\infty xg(x)\,dx
=\frac1\mu\int_0^\infty x^2f(x)\,dx
=\frac{\mathbb E(X^2)}\mu
=\boxed{\mu+\frac{\sigma^2}\mu}.
$$
Thus <size bias> increases the <mean> by $\sigma^2/\mu$, in accord with preferentially selecting larger values.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x)$ and $F_Y(x)=\mathbb P(Y\leq x)$. For $0\leq x\leq\mu$, the factor $t/\mu$ is at most one throughout $0\leq t\leq x$, so
$$
F_Y(x)=\int_0^x\frac t\mu f(t)\,dt\leq\int_0^x f(t)\,dt=F_X(x).
$$
For $x>\mu$, compare the tails instead: $t/\mu\geq1$ throughout $t\geq x$, giving
$$
1-F_Y(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac t\mu f(t)\,dt\geq\int_x^\infty f(t)\,dt=1-F_X(x).
$$
Rearranging proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y\leq x)\leq\mathbb P(X\leq x)\quad(x\geq0).}
$$
This is <stochastic domination> of $X$ by its <size-biased distribution>. The proof needs only the finite positive <mean>, not the finite <variance> assumed in (ii).
