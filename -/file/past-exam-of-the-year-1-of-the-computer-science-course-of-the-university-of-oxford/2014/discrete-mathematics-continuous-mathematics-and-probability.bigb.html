= Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics and probability

Solutions to all nine questions of the examination dated 26 June 2014.<Discrete Mathematics, Continuous Mathematics and Probability examination paper by the University of Oxford (2014)>[Printed cover, paper 4223; questions on printed pp. 2–10]{ref}

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

By <De Morgan's laws>, taking a <set difference> from a <union> requires exclusion from both <sets>, while taking a <set difference> from an <intersection> requires exclusion from at least one:
$$
\boxed{A\setminus(B\cup C)=(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C),\qquad A\setminus(B\cap C)=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\setminus C).}
$$
For example, $x$ belongs to the first left-hand side precisely when $x\in A$, $x\notin B$ and $x\notin C$; these are exactly the membership conditions on the first right-hand side. For the second identity, replace the two exclusions by their <logical disjunction>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Prove both identities by <mathematical induction>. The base case $n=2$ is the two-set form of <De Morgan's laws>. For the <union> identity, suppose the result holds for $n$. Applying the two-set identity and then the <inductive hypothesis> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1}B_i
&=\left(A\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_i\right)\cap(A\setminus B_{n+1})\\
&=\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(A\setminus B_i)\right)\cap(A\setminus B_{n+1})
=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1}(A\setminus B_i).
\end{aligned}
$$
For the <intersection> identity, the corresponding step is
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\setminus\bigcap_{i=1}^{n+1}B_i
&=\left(A\setminus\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}B_i\right)\cup(A\setminus B_{n+1})\\
&=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(A\setminus B_i)\right)\cup(A\setminus B_{n+1})
=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n+1}(A\setminus B_i).
\end{aligned}
$$
These close the two inductions.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the two identities already proved with $A=\mathcal U$. Every $B_i$ is a <subset> of the common universe, and a <complement> is the <set difference> from that universe. Therefore
$$
\mathcal U\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^nB_i
=\bigcap_{i=1}^n(\mathcal U\setminus B_i),\qquad
\mathcal U\setminus\bigcap_{i=1}^nB_i
=\bigcup_{i=1}^n(\mathcal U\setminus B_i).
$$
Replacing each <set difference> from $\mathcal U$ by its <complement> notation gives the claimed finite forms of <De Morgan's laws>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**Function composition is not always commutative.** The reversed <function composition> may be undefined when the necessary <domain of a function> and <codomain> do not match. A counterexample using <functions> $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ avoids that issue: let $f(x)=x+1$ and $g(x)=2x$. Then
$$
(g\circ f)(x)=2x+2,\qquad(f\circ g)(x)=2x+1.
$$
Both <function compositions> exist, and their values differ at every real $x$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the definition of <function composition> gives
$$
\bigl(h\circ(g\circ f)\bigr)(x)
=h\bigl((g\circ f)(x)\bigr)
=h(g(f(x)))
=\bigl((h\circ g)\circ f\bigr)(x).
$$
The <functions> agree at every argument, so <function extensionality> proves the <associative property> of <function composition>.

=== f
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The real <cube root> in the denominator vanishes at $x=-1$, so the printed rule needs the <domain of a function> $D_1=\mathbb R\setminus\{-1\}$. On its actual <domain of a function>, the second <iterated function> simplifies to
$$
f(f(x))=
\frac{x/\sqrt[3]{1+x^3}}{\sqrt[3]{1+x^3/(1+x^3)}}
=\frac{x}{\sqrt[3]{1+2x^3}}.
$$
This suggests
$$
\boxed{f^{(n)}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt[3]{1+nx^3}},\qquad
D_n=\mathbb R\setminus\{-j^{-1/3}:1\leq j\leq n\}.}
$$
For the induction step, assume the formula at $n$ and restrict to arguments for which its value is not $-1$. Its cube is $x^3/(1+nx^3)$, so another application gives
$$
f(f^{(n)}(x))
=\frac{x/\sqrt[3]{1+nx^3}}{\sqrt[3]{(1+(n+1)x^3)/(1+nx^3)}}
=\frac{x}{\sqrt[3]{1+(n+1)x^3}}.
$$
Real <cube roots> are multiplicative, including for negative arguments. The newly excluded point satisfies $x^3/(1+nx^3)=-1$, hence $x^3=-1/(n+1)$; all earlier exclusions remain. This proves both the formula and the <domain of a function> by <mathematical induction>. The inherited exclusions are essential for an <iteration of a partial function>: cancellation in the final expression cannot repair an undefined intermediate application.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $y=f(x)$ and cube the equation. Solving for $x^3$ gives
$$
y^3=\frac{x^3}{1+x^3},\qquad
x^3=\frac{y^3}{1-y^3}.
$$
Consequently the <inverse function> is
$$
\boxed{f^{-1}(y)=\frac{y}{\sqrt[3]{1-y^3}},\qquad y\ne1.}
$$
The value $y=1$ is impossible in the original equation. Conversely, every real $y\ne1$ gives exactly one real $x$ by the displayed <cube root>, and this $x$ cannot equal $-1$. Direct substitution verifies both inverse identities. Thus the corrected <bijection> is $f:\mathbb R\setminus\{-1\}\to\mathbb R\setminus\{1\}$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

List the $n$ elements of the <finite set> $A$. A <subset> is determined by $n$ membership decisions, each with two possibilities. This is a <bijection> from the <power set> to the <set> $\{0,1\}^n$, so
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal P(A)|=2^n.}
$$
The empty <set> case is included: its <power set> contains the single empty <subset>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

There are $n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=n!/(n-k)!$ ordered selections of $k$ distinct elements. Every size-$k$ <subset> appears in exactly $k!$ orders, giving the <binomial coefficient>
$$
\boxed{\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!},\qquad0\leq k\leq n.}
$$
For $k=0$, both the empty selection and $0!$ contribute one.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Partition the <power set> into disjoint classes according to <subset> size. The class of size-$i$ <subsets> has <cardinality> $\binom ni$, while the whole <power set> has <cardinality> $2^n$. Counting every <subset> once gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^n\binom ni=2^n.}
$$
This is also the <binomial theorem> evaluated at $1+1$, but the counting proof follows directly from the earlier parts.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <mathematical induction> with the defining <Fibonacci recurrence>. Both identities hold at $n=0$: their two sides are zero. If the first holds at $n$, then
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}F_i
=F_{n+2}-1+F_{n+1}=F_{n+3}-1.
$$
If the second holds at $n$, then
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}F_i^2
=F_nF_{n+1}+F_{n+1}^2
=F_{n+1}(F_n+F_{n+1})
=F_{n+1}F_{n+2}.
$$
Each <inductive hypothesis> therefore advances to $n+1$.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n\geq2$ and $1\leq k\leq n-1$, the <factorial> formula for a <binomial coefficient> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\binom{n-1}k+\binom{n-1}{k-1}
&=\frac{(n-1)!(n-k)}{k!(n-k)!}
+\frac{(n-1)!k}{k!(n-k)!}\\
&=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}=\binom nk.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves <Pascal's identity>. The boundary cases $k=0,n$ follow for $n\geq1$ using the usual extension $\binom mj=0$ for $j<0$ or $j>m$. The recurrence itself requires $n\geq1$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For fixed $n\geq0$, use <mathematical induction> on $k$. At $k=0$, both sides equal one. If the <hockey-stick identity> holds at $k$, then <Pascal's identity> gives
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}\binom{n+i}i
=\binom{n+k+1}k+\binom{n+k+1}{k+1}
=\binom{n+k+2}{k+1}.
$$
This is the required formula with $k$ replaced by $k+1$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The relation is <reflexive> because $a-a=2\cdot0$. It is <symmetric> because $a-b=2k$ implies $b-a=2(-k)$. It is <transitive> because $a-b=2k$ and $b-c=2\ell$ imply $a-c=2(k+\ell)$. It is therefore an <equivalence relation>, namely <congruence modulo an integer> for modulus two. Its two <equivalence classes> consist of the <even integers> and the <odd integers>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The notation means that $n$ divides $a_1-a_2$:
$$
\boxed{a_1\equiv a_2\pmod n\ \Longleftrightarrow\
\text{there exists }k\in\mathbb Z\text{ with }a_1-a_2=kn.}
$$
This is <congruence modulo an integer>; equivalently, the two <integers> have the same remainder on division by the positive <integer> $n$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $a_1-a_2=kn$ with $k\in\mathbb Z$. The <difference of two squares> factors as
$$
a_1^2-a_2^2=(a_1-a_2)(a_1+a_2)=n\,k(a_1+a_2).
$$
The final factor is an <integer>, so $n$ divides the difference. By <congruence modulo an integer>, $\boxed{a_1^2\equiv a_2^2\pmod n}$.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Dividing by $n^2$ gives $10^5/n+2\to2$. Thus the quadratic term controls the growth:
$$
\boxed{10^5n+2n^2=\Theta(n^2),\quad\text{in particular }O(n^2).}
$$
The sharper <Big Theta notation> also supplies the requested <Big O notation> bound.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n>0$,
$$
\frac{\sqrt{n^6+n^2}}{n^3}=\sqrt{1+n^{-4}}\longrightarrow1.
$$
Consequently $\boxed{\sqrt{n^6+n^2}=\Theta(n^3),\quad\text{in particular }O(n^3)}$ in <Big Theta notation> and <Big O notation>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Here $a,b\geq2$ are fixed <integers>. The term $ab^n$ has <exponential growth>, whereas $an^b$ has fixed-degree <polynomial growth> and $nb^a$ is linear. Since a fixed-base exponential eventually dominates every fixed-degree polynomial,
$$
\frac{an^b+ab^n+nb^a}{b^n}\longrightarrow a.
$$
Hence the requested <Big O notation> bound, with its matching lower order, is $\boxed{\Theta(b^n),\quad\text{in particular }O(b^n)}$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For sufficiently large positive $n$, the <logarithm> identities give
$$
n\log_2(5n)+\log_2(n^2)+4n
=n\log_2n+(\log_25+4)n+2\log_2n.
$$
After division by $n\log_2n$, the right-hand side tends to one. Thus $\boxed{\Theta(n\log n),\quad\text{in particular }O(n\log n)}$ gives the asymptotic order in <Big Theta notation> and <Big O notation>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The number $K$ is fixed. By <Big O notation>, for each $i$ there are constants $C_i>0$ and $N_i$ such that $f_i(n)\leq C_i g(n)$ when $n\geq N_i$. Take $N=\max_iN_i$. For $n\geq N$, positivity gives
$$
h(n)\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^K C_i\right)g(n),
\qquad
l(n)\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}C_iC_{i+1}\right)g(n)^2.
$$
The constants in parentheses are independent of $n$, proving $\boxed{h=O(g),\quad l=O(g^2)}$. If $K=1$, the second sum is empty and $l=0$, which satisfies the same upper bound.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The printed fractional arguments require a size convention. To make the <recurrence relation> precise on positive <integers>, take a bounded base case $T(1)=t>0$ and use $m=\lceil n/4\rceil$, $n-m$ for $n\geq2$. This <integer rounding in a divide-and-conquer recurrence> gives $1/4\leq m/n\leq1/2$. The unrounded calculation is the special split fraction $1/4$.

Prove $T(n)\leq A n\log_2n+B n$ by <strong induction>. Put $h_0=H_2(1/4)>0$, choose $A\geq1/h_0$, and choose $B\geq t$. The base case follows. The <entropy potential for a divide-and-conquer recurrence> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
T(n)
&\leq A\{m\log_2m+(n-m)\log_2(n-m)\}+Bn+n\\
&=A n\log_2n+Bn+n\{1-AH_2(m/n)\}\\
&\leq A n\log_2n+Bn.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last step uses $H_2(m/n)\geq h_0$. This closes the <inductive hypothesis> and proves $\boxed{T(n)=O(n\log n)}$ in <Big O notation>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the same <integer rounding in a divide-and-conquer recurrence>, now with root cost $n^2$. Since $1/4\leq m/n\leq1/2$,
$$
m^2+(n-m)^2\leq\frac58n^2.
$$
Choose $C\geq\max\{T(1),8/3\}$ and use <strong induction> with <inductive hypothesis> $T(j)\leq Cj^2$. Both child sizes are positive and less than $n$, so
$$
T(n)\leq C\{m^2+(n-m)^2\}+n^2
\leq\left(\frac58C+1\right)n^2
\leq Cn^2.
$$
The same factor $5/8$ occurs for the exact fractional split. Therefore $\boxed{T(n)=O(n^2)}$ in <Big O notation>.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $I=\int e^x\sin x\,dx$. Two applications of <integration by parts> give
$$
I=e^x\sin x-\int e^x\cos x\,dx,\qquad
\int e^x\cos x\,dx=e^x\cos x+I,
$$
where additive constants can be absorbed at the end. Solving for $I$ yields
$$
\boxed{\int e^x\sin x\,dx=\frac{e^x}{2}(\sin x-\cos x)+C.}
$$
Differentiating the answer gives $e^x\sin x$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

After division by $x>0$, the equation is linear with coefficient $-1/x$ on $y$. Its <integrating factor> is $1/x$, and the <product rule> gives
$$
\left(\frac yx\right)'=\frac{xy'-y}{x^2}=e^x\sin x.
$$
Using the <antiderivative> from the preceding part,
$$
\boxed{y(x)=Cx+\frac{xe^x}{2}(\sin x-\cos x),\qquad x>0.}
$$
The arbitrary constant multiplies the homogeneous solution $x$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The homogeneous <characteristic equation> is $r^2-4r+3=(r-1)(r-3)=0$, giving $e^x$ and $e^{3x}$. Since the forcing $e^{3x}$ already appears among these solutions, try a <particular solution> $y_p=Ax e^{3x}$. Differentiation and substitution give
$$
y_p''-4y_p'+3y_p=2Ae^{3x},
$$
so $A=1/2$. The <general solution> is
$$
\boxed{y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{3x}+\frac{x}{2}e^{3x}.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $Y(t)=y(e^t)$, so $t=\log x$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
y'(x)=\frac{Y'(t)}x,\qquad
y''(x)=\frac{Y''(t)-Y'(t)}{x^2}.
$$
Substitution into the <Cauchy-Euler equation> therefore gives $Y''+Y'-2Y=0$. Its <characteristic equation> factors as $(r-1)(r+2)=0$, so $Y=C_1e^t+C_2e^{-2t}$. Returning to $x$ gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1x+C_2x^{-2},\qquad x>0.}
$$

== 5
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> for the positive numbers $x$ and $a/x$ gives
$$
g(x)=\frac12\left(x+\frac ax\right)\geq\sqrt a.
$$
Equality holds precisely when $x=a/x$, hence at $x=\sqrt a$. Thus $\boxed{\min_{x>0}g(x)=\sqrt a}$, attained only at $\boxed{x=\sqrt a}$. Equivalently, the exact identity $g(x)-\sqrt a=(x-\sqrt a)^2/(2x)$ proves the same global bound.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $f(x)=x^2-a$, its <derivative> is $f'(x)=2x$. Substitution into <Newton's method> gives
$$
\boxed{x_n=x_{n-1}-\frac{x_{n-1}^2-a}{2x_{n-1}}
=\frac12\left(x_{n-1}+\frac a{x_{n-1}}\right).}
$$
Every positive input gives another positive input, so a starting value $x_0>0$ keeps the denominator nonzero at every step.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The update in <Newton's method> is exactly $x_n=g(x_{n-1})$. Positivity is preserved, and the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> from part (a) applies to every update. Consequently
$$
\boxed{x_n\geq\sqrt a\quad\text{for every }n\geq1.}
$$
This holds even when $0<x_0<\sqrt a$: the first update moves to or above the positive <square root>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Subtract the previous iterate in <Newton's method>:
$$
\boxed{x_n-x_{n-1}=\frac{a-x_{n-1}^2}{2x_{n-1}}<0
\quad\text{if }x_{n-1}>\sqrt a.}
$$
The denominator is positive and the numerator is negative. If $x_{n-1}=\sqrt a$, the difference is zero and all subsequent iterates equal the root.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

After the first update, the <sequence> lies in $[\sqrt a,x_1]$ and is nonincreasing. The <monotone convergence theorem for sequences> gives a <limit> $L\geq\sqrt a$. The update is <continuous> on this interval, so passing to the <limit> gives
$$
L=\frac12\left(L+\frac aL\right),\qquad L^2=a.
$$
Positivity forces $\boxed{x_n\to\sqrt a\text{ for every }x_0>0}$. If an iterate reaches the root exactly, the constant tail gives the same conclusion. The <exact Newton error identity for a quadratic> here is $x_n-\sqrt a=(x_{n-1}-\sqrt a)^2/(2x_{n-1})$, also showing <quadratic convergence> once the error is small.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The constraint implies $x^2=z^2+1$, so $x\ne0$ and its <gradient> $(2x,0,-2z)$ never vanishes on the feasible set. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ give
$$
2x=2\lambda x,\qquad2y=0,\qquad1=-2\lambda z.
$$
Thus $\lambda=1$, $y=0$, $z=-1/2$, and feasibility gives $x=\pm\sqrt5/2$. The two stationary points and their values are
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\sqrt5}{2},0,-\frac12\right),\
\left(-\frac{\sqrt5}{2},0,-\frac12\right),\qquad f=\frac34.}
$$
They are both <global minima>. Indeed, on the constraint, <completing the square> gives
$$
f=1+z^2+y^2+z=\left(z+\frac12\right)^2+y^2+\frac34.
$$
Equality holds only at the two displayed points. There are no other constrained stationary points, and the objective is unbounded above as $|y|\to\infty$.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiating the polynomial coordinate map gives
$$
\boxed{x_u=u,\quad x_v=-v,\quad y_u=u,\quad y_v=v.}
$$
Its <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
=\begin{pmatrix}u&-v\\u&v\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=2uv.
$$
The determinant identifies where the <inverse function theorem> applies.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

On a branch with fixed signs of $u,v$ and $uv\ne0$, use $u^2=x+y$ and $v^2=y-x$. Differentiating these identities, or inverting the <Jacobian matrix>, gives
$$
\boxed{u_x=\frac1{2u},\quad u_y=\frac1{2u},\quad
v_x=-\frac1{2v},\quad v_y=\frac1{2v}.}
$$
The inverse <Jacobian matrix> is therefore
$$
\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}
=\begin{pmatrix}1/(2u)&1/(2u)\\-1/(2v)&1/(2v)\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These are local <partial derivatives>. Choosing the signs specifies an <inverse function> on $y>|x|$; the original map has four preimages there and has no single global inverse. At $u=0$ or $v=0$, the displayed inverse derivatives are not finite.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The requested <partial derivatives> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f_x&=3x^2+2x+2\alpha y+2\alpha,&
f_y&=2\alpha x+2y+2,\\
f_{xx}&=6x+2,&
f_{xy}&=2\alpha,&
f_{yy}&=2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The last printed derivative has a misplaced superscript on $f$; the second-derivative list and subsequent <Taylor expansion> use $f_{yy}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $h=x-1$ and $k=y-1$. At $(1,1)$, the value is $5+4\alpha$, the <gradient> is $(5+4\alpha,4+2\alpha)$, and the <Hessian matrix> is $\begin{pmatrix}8&2\alpha\\2\alpha&2\end{pmatrix}$. Thus the quadratic <Taylor expansion> is
$$
\boxed{f(1+h,1+k)
=5+4\alpha+(5+4\alpha)h+(4+2\alpha)k
+4h^2+2\alpha hk+k^2+O((|h|+|k|)^3).}
$$
The exact remainder is $h^3$, since the original <polynomial> has only the cubic term $x^3$ beyond degree two.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Solving $f_y=0$ gives $y=-1-\alpha x$. Substitution into $f_x=0$ gives $x\{3x+2(1-\alpha^2)\}=0$. Therefore $(0,-1)$ is always a <critical point>. When $\alpha\ne-1$ as well as the printed restriction $\alpha\ne1$, there is a second <critical point>
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac23(\alpha^2-1),\,
-1-\frac{2\alpha}{3}(\alpha^2-1)\right).}
$$
At $(0,-1)$, the <Hessian matrix> is $\begin{pmatrix}2&2\alpha\\2\alpha&2\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $4(1-\alpha^2)$. The <second-derivative test> gives a strict <local minimum> when $|\alpha|<1$ and a <saddle point of a scalar function> when $|\alpha|>1$.

The allowed value $\alpha=-1$ requires higher-order terms. With $v=y+1+\alpha x$, <completing the square> gives the exact identity
$$
f(x,y)+1=x^3+(1-\alpha^2)x^2+v^2.
$$
The coordinate change is invertible, so the <Hessian at a critical point under a change of coordinates> preserves the classification. At $\alpha=-1$, this is a <cubic degenerate saddle>: along $v=0$, the difference from the critical value is $x^3$ and takes both signs. Thus, throughout the printed parameter range,
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\text{strict local minimum},&-1<\alpha<1,\\
\text{saddle point},&\alpha\leq-1\ \text{or}\ \alpha>1.
\end{cases}}
$$
There is no <local maximum>. When the second point is distinct, it is a <local minimum> for $|\alpha|>1$ and a <saddle point of a scalar function> for $|\alpha|<1$: in the $(x,v)$ coordinates its <Hessian matrix> is $\operatorname{diag}(-2(1-\alpha^2),2)$. The printed instruction to split only at $\alpha=1$ misses the negative-parameter cases.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $(B_i)$ be a finite or countable <partition of a sample space> into <events> of positive <probability>. The <events> $A\cap B_i$ are disjoint and their <union> is $A$. <Countable additivity> and the definition of <conditional probability> therefore give the <law of total probability>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\sum_i\mathbb P(A\cap B_i)
=\sum_i\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i).}
$$
Cells of zero <probability> contribute zero through $\mathbb P(A\cap B_i)$ and can be omitted rather than assigning an undefined <conditional probability>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The two definitions of <conditional probability> give
$$
\mathbb P(B\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(A)},
\qquad
\mathbb P(A\mid B)\mathbb P(B)=\mathbb P(A\cap B).
$$
Substitution proves <Bayes' theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\mid B)\mathbb P(B)}{\mathbb P(A)}.}
$$
The positive <probabilities> assumed in the question make both conditional expressions defined.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

<Mutual independence> means that for every nonempty $I\subseteq\{1,2,3\}$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right)=\prod_{i\in I}\mathbb P(A_i).}
$$
Beyond the trivial single-event identities, this requires the three pairwise equalities and the triple equality $\mathbb P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3)=\mathbb P(A_1)\mathbb P(A_2)\mathbb P(A_3)$. <Pairwise independence> alone omits that last requirement.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Each position is equally likely to receive any coin, so the <law of total probability> gives $\mathbb P(A_i)=(1/3+1/2+2/3)/3=1/2$. For two specified positions, the ordered pair of distinct coin biases is uniform over six possibilities. Assuming independent tosses conditional on the order,
$$
\mathbb P(A_1\cap A_2)
=\frac13\left(\frac13\frac12+\frac13\frac23+\frac12\frac23\right)
=\frac{13}{54}\ne\frac14.
$$
Thus **the events are not independent**, even pairwise. The <covariance of draws without replacement> explains the difference: the head <indicator random variables> have <covariance> $13/54-1/4=-1/108$. Independent physical tosses given the assigned coins do not imply <independence> after that hidden assignment is averaged out.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $C_j$ identify the selected coin and let $H$ be the event of two heads. The prior coin <probabilities> are equal, and the <conditional probabilities> of $H$ are $1/9,1/4,4/9$. By <Bayes' theorem>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(C_1\mid H)=
\frac{(1/3)(1/9)}{(1/3)(1/9+1/4+4/9)}
=\frac4{29}.}
$$
The full <posterior distribution> over the three biases is $(4,9,16)/29$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Given the coin, the next toss is independent of its earlier tosses and has that coin's head <probability>. Averaging using the <posterior distribution> gives the <posterior predictive probability>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{next head}\mid H)
=\frac4{29}\frac13+\frac9{29}\frac12+\frac{16}{29}\frac23
=\frac{33}{58}.}
$$
Observing two heads raises the <probability> of having chosen a high-bias coin.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

If the selected coin has bias $p$, the other two biases sum to $3/2-p$. A uniform choice between those two coins therefore has conditional head <probability> $3/4-p/2$. Average over the <posterior distribution>, using the previous <posterior predictive probability>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{head on another coin}\mid H)
=\frac34-\frac12\frac{33}{58}
=\frac{27}{58}.}
$$
The observation makes a high-bias coin more likely to have been removed from the remaining pair.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $q=1-p$. Summing the <geometric series> in the definition of the <probability generating function> gives
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]
=p\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(qs)^k
=\frac{p}{1-qs},\qquad|s|<\frac1q.}
$$
This is the zero-based <geometric distribution>, counting failures before the first success.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiate the <probability generating function> to obtain
$$
G_X'(1)=\frac qp,\qquad G_X''(1)=\frac{2q^2}{p^2}.
$$
The first derivative is the <expected value>, and the second gives the <factorial moment> $\mathbb E[X(X-1)]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\frac{1-p}{p},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=G_X''(1)+G_X'(1)-G_X'(1)^2
=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.}
$$
In particular, $\mathbb E[X^2]=q(2-p)/p^2$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Given $X=k$, the score $Y$ has a <binomial distribution> with success <probability> $1/2$. Its conditional <expected value> and <variance> are $k/2$ and $k/4$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Y\mid X=k]=\frac k2,\qquad
\mathbb E[Y^2\mid X=k]=\frac k4+\frac{k^2}{4}.}
$$
This is <binomial thinning> of the first-round count; the second formula uses the identity $\mathbb E[Y^2\mid X]=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)+\mathbb E[Y\mid X]^2$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <law of total expectation> gives $\mathbb E Y=\mathbb E[X/2]=q/(2p)$. The <law of total variance> separates the two sources of randomness:
$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y)
=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)]
+\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb E[Y\mid X])
=\frac14\mathbb E X+\frac14\operatorname{Var}(X).
$$
Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E Y=\frac{1-p}{2p},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\frac{q(p+1)}{4p^2}
=\frac{1-p^2}{4p^2}.}
$$
The <binomial thinning of a geometric random variable> identity supplies a further check: $Y$ itself has a zero-based <geometric distribution> with success <probability> $2p/(1+p)$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $S=X+Y$. Given $X$, its conditional <expected value> is $3X/2$ and its conditional <variance> is $X/4$, since $X$ is then fixed. The <law of total expectation> and <law of total variance> give
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E S=\frac{3(1-p)}{2p},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(S)=\frac14\mathbb E X+\frac94\operatorname{Var}(X)
=\frac{(1-p)(p+9)}{4p^2}.}
$$
The two scores are dependent: the <law of total covariance> gives $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Cov}(X,X/2)=q/(2p^2)$. Adding their two <variances> without that <covariance> term would give an incorrect total.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <probability density function> must be nonnegative and integrate to one. For $c>0$,
$$
\int_1^\infty c x^{-c-1}\,dx
=\left[-x^{-c}\right]_1^\infty=1.
$$
At $c=0$ the function is identically zero, while $c<0$ makes it negative on $x>1$. Thus $\boxed{c>0}$ is necessary and sufficient. The resulting law is a <Pareto distribution> with scale one and shape $c$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Integrating the <probability density function> from the lower endpoint gives the <cumulative distribution function>
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x\leq1,\\
1-x^{-c},&x>1.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <cumulative distribution function> is continuous at $x=1$ and tends to one at infinity.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $c>1$, the <expected value> is the convergent improper <integral>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\int_1^\infty x\frac{c}{x^{c+1}}\,dx
=c\int_1^\infty x^{-c}\,dx=\frac{c}{c-1}.}
$$
For completeness, when $0<c\leq1$ the same positive <integral> diverges and the <expected value> is infinite.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The <logarithm> is strictly increasing, so $\mathbb P(Y\leq y)=\mathbb P(X\leq e^y)$. Since $X>1$ almost surely,
$$
\boxed{F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0,&y\leq0,\\1-e^{-cy},&y>0,\end{cases}
\qquad
f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}ce^{-cy},&y>0,\\0,&y\leq0.\end{cases}}
$$
Thus the <logarithm of a Pareto random variable> has an <exponential distribution> with rate $c$. Its <probability density function> value at the single endpoint can be chosen arbitrarily without changing the law.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

By <linearity of expectation> and <independence>,
$$
\mathbb E W=\mu,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(W)=
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$
For $\sigma>0$, apply <Chebyshev's inequality> at threshold $h\sigma$ and take the complementary event:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|W-\mu|\leq h\sigma)
=1-\mathbb P(|W-\mu|>h\sigma)
\geq1-\frac{1}{nh^2}.}
$$
If $\sigma=0$, every $X_i=\mu$ almost surely, so $W=\mu$ almost surely and the bound holds directly. This handles the zero-threshold case without applying <Chebyshev's inequality> at an inadmissible threshold.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $X_i$ be the head <indicator random variable> for toss $i$. These are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with a <Bernoulli distribution> of parameter $1/2$, so $\mu=1/2$, $\sigma=1/2$, and $W=N/n$. Taking $h=1/5$ makes $h\sigma=1/10$. The preceding <Chebyshev's inequality> bound gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(0.4\leq\frac Nn\leq0.6\right)
\geq1-\frac{25}{n}\geq\frac34,\qquad n\geq100.}
$$
The endpoints are included because this event is exactly $|W-1/2|\leq1/10$.
