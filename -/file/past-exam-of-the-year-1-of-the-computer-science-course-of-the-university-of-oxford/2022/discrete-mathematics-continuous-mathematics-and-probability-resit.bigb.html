=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $A$ and $B$ be the finite <sets> of objects satisfying the two properties. The <rule of sum> says that mutually exclusive alternatives have additive counts: if $A\cap B=\varnothing$, then $|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|$. Without disjointness, the corresponding <inclusion-exclusion principle> is
$$
|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|.
$$
The <rule of subtraction> counts objects satisfying the first property but not the second:
$$
\boxed{|A\setminus B|=|A|-|A\cap B|.}
$$
In particular, if the objects with the second property form a <subset> of those with the first, the count is $|A|-|B|$. These conditions prevent either double counting an overlap or subtracting objects that were not counted initially.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <rule of product> says that if a construction has $r$ stages, with exactly $a_j$ choices at stage $j$ after every permitted earlier history, then it has $\prod_{j=1}^r a_j$ possible outcomes. The later choices need not be identical <sets> after different histories; their numbers must have the stated values.

For a <permutation> of $n$ distinct objects, choose the first object in $n$ ways, the second in $n-1$ ways, and continue until only one remains. Thus the number is
$$
\boxed{n(n-1)\cdots1=n!.}
$$
Every ordered arrangement is produced exactly once by its successive choices, so this counts <permutations> without omissions or repetitions. For $n=0$, the unique empty arrangement agrees with $0!=1$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

An allocation is a <tuple> $(x_1,\ldots,x_k)$ of nonnegative <integers> with $\sum_jx_j=n$, where $x_j$ is the number in labelled box $j$. Empty boxes are allowed. Encode the allocation by $n$ stars and $k-1$ bars: put $x_1$ stars before the first bar, $x_2$ between the first two bars, and so on. Adjacent bars represent an empty intermediate box; a bar at an end represents an empty end box.

Reading the group sizes recovers exactly one allocation from every such string, so this is a <bijection>. There are $n+k-1$ positions and we choose the $k-1$ bar positions. Hence <stars and bars> gives
$$
\boxed{\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}.}
$$
No extra factor for ordering the objects is present, because the objects are indistinguishable. For $k=1$ the string has no bars and gives the single allocation of all objects to that box; for $n=0$ all boxes are empty, also a single allocation.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Pad every <integer> from $0$ through $999999$ with leading zeros to obtain a unique six-digit decimal string. If its digits sum to $5$, each digit is automatically at most $5$, so the usual digit <upper bound> $9$ adds no restriction. We therefore count the nonnegative <integer> solutions of
$$
d_1+d_2+\cdots+d_6=5.
$$
By <stars and bars>, there are $\binom{5+6-1}{6-1}=\binom{10}{5}$. The all-zero string is not counted because its <digit sum> is zero, so every counted <integer> is positive. Thus the answer is **$252$**.

=== a

==== Solution

For real-valued <functions>, use magnitude comparisons. The constants below are independent of $n$.

The statement $f(n)=O(g(n))$ in <Big O notation> means that some $C>0$ and <integer> $N$ satisfy $|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|$ for all $n\geq N$. The statement $f(n)=\Omega(g(n))$ in <big omega notation> means that some $c>0$ and $N$ satisfy $|f(n)|\geq c|g(n)|$ for all $n\geq N$.

Finally, $f(n)=\Theta(g(n))$ in <big theta notation> means that some $c,C>0$ and $N$ satisfy
$$
\boxed{c|g(n)|\leq|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|\qquad(n\geq N).}
$$
Equivalently it is both an eventual <upper bound> and an eventual <lower bound>. These definitions ignore a finite initial segment; they do not assert pointwise inequalities with constant one.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Suppose first that $f=O(g)$. Choose $C>0$ and $N$ so that $|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|$ for $n\geq N$. There are only finitely many natural numbers below $N$. Because $g(n)\ne0$ at each of them, all ratios $|f(n)|/|g(n)|$ in this finite prefix are finite. Let
$$
c=\max\left(\{C\}\cup\left\{\frac{|f(n)|}{|g(n)|}:n<N\right\}\right).
$$
Then $|f(n)|\leq c|g(n)|$ both in the prefix and in the tail, hence on the entire <domain of a function>. This proves the <finite-prefix extension of a big O bound>.

Conversely, a bound valid for every natural number is certainly valid eventually. The purported real constant cannot be negative because $g$ never vanishes; if it is zero, enlarge it to a positive constant. The definition of <Big O notation> then applies. The nonvanishing assumption is what permits the finite exceptions to be absorbed into one constant.

===== Solution

**False.** Put $r_n=3^n/n^3$ for $n\geq1$. For $n\geq4$,
$$
\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n}=3\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^3

==== iii

===== Solution

**True.** Rationalization gives, for $n\geq1$,
$$
\sqrt{4n^2+n}-2n
=\frac n{\sqrt{4n^2+n}+2n}

$$
This lies between zero and $1/4$, so it is $O(1)$ in <Big O notation>. In fact it converges to $1/4$, rather than to zero. The rationalized form also avoids the <catastrophic cancellation> of subtracting two nearby large quantities.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**True for every fixed $k\geq0$.** Extract $e^n$ from inside the <logarithm>:
$$
\log(e^n+n^k)-n=\log(1+n^ke^{-n}).
$$
Choose an <integer> $m>k$. The nonnegative terms of the <exponential series> give $e^n\geq n^m/m!$ for $n>0$, whence
$$
0\leq n^ke^{-n}\leq m!n^{k-m}\longrightarrow0.

Since $0\leq\log(1+u)\leq u$ for $u\geq0$, the displayed expression tends to zero and is therefore $O(1)$. The constant and cutoff may depend on the fixed $k$; no uniform assertion over unbounded $k$ is being made.

==== Solution

The <integer congruence> $x\equiv y\pmod n$ means $n\mid(x-y)$: the difference is an <integer> multiple of the positive modulus $n$. Write $x_1-x_2=na$ and $y_1-y_2=nb$ for <integers> $a,b$. Then
$$
(x_1+y_1)-(x_2+y_2)=n(a+b),
$$
so <addition> preserves <integer congruence>. For <multiplication>,
$$
x_1y_1-x_2y_2=y_1(x_1-x_2)+x_2(y_1-y_2)=n(ay_1+bx_2).
$$
The final coefficient is an <integer>, so <multiplication> also preserves <integer congruence>. These properties make <addition> and <multiplication> well defined on <residue classes>.

The <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
2392&=1748+644,\\
1748&=2\cdot644+460,\\
644&=460+184,\\
460&=2\cdot184+92,\\
184&=2\cdot92.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last nonzero remainder is **$g=92$**. Back-substitution produces its <Bézout coefficients>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
92&=460-2\cdot184\\
&=3\cdot460-2\cdot644\\
&=3\cdot1748-8\cdot644\\
&=-8\cdot2392+11\cdot1748.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **$(m,n)=(-8,11)$** is a requested pair. The final equality independently checks the calculation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <pigeonhole principle> states that if more than $r$ objects are placed in $r$ boxes, with $r\geq1$, at least one box contains two or more objects. Equivalently, a <function> from a finite <set> of more than $r$ elements to a <set> of $r$ elements cannot be injective: if every box held at most one object, there would be at most $r$ objects altogether.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For five points, partition the <unit square> into four <squares> of side $1/2$. Assign boundary points to just one neighbouring piece. By the <pigeonhole principle>, two of the points lie in one piece, whose <diameter> is its diagonal, $\sqrt{(1/2)^2+(1/2)^2}=\sqrt2/2$.

For eight points, use seven smaller-<diameter> pieces rather than eight <rectangles>. Start with the convex <pentagon>
$$
P=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1/2,0),(1/2,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,1/4)\}.
$$
Take its four reflected copies at the corners of the <square>. The remaining pieces are the left <triangle> with vertices $(0,1/4),(1/4,1/2),(0,3/4)$, its right-hand <reflection>, and the central diamond with vertices $(1/4,1/2),(1/2,1/4),(3/4,1/2),(1/2,3/4)$. These seven <polygons> cover the <square> with disjoint interiors. To verify coverage directly, for $0\leq y\leq1/4$ the two bottom pentagons split the horizontal section at $x=1/2$. For $1/4\leq y\leq1/2$, the five successive sections have endpoints $0,y-1/4,3/4-y,y+1/4,5/4-y,1$ and belong respectively to the left triangle, bottom-left pentagon, diamond, bottom-right pentagon and right triangle. Reflection gives the top half.

The <diameter of a finite convex hull> is the greatest <distance> between its vertices: if two points are <convex combinations> of vertices, their difference is a <convex combination> of <vertex> differences, and the <triangle inequality> bounds its <norm> by the largest such <distance>. Checking the five vertices of $P$ gives <diameter> $\sqrt{(1/2)^2+(1/4)^2}=\sqrt5/4$; <reflections> preserve that value. Each remaining <triangle> and the diamond has <diameter> $1/2$, which is smaller. After assigning shared boundaries to one piece, eight points in these seven pieces force a pair in a common piece. Hence **some pair is at <distance> at most $\sqrt5/4$**.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Prove the stronger statement with a nonempty <subset>. Order the $n\geq1$ <integers> as $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, and form the <prefix sums>
$$
s_0=0,\qquad s_j=\sum_{k=1}^ja_k\quad(1\leq j\leq n).
$$
There are $n+1$ such sums but only $n$ <residue classes> modulo $n$. The <pigeonhole principle> gives indices $0\leq i<j\leq n$ with $s_i\equiv s_j\pmod n$. Their difference satisfies
$$
s_j-s_i=a_{i+1}+\cdots+a_j\equiv0\pmod n.
$$
The selected elements form a nonempty <subset> of $S$, proving the claim. Neither positivity nor distinct values in a general ordered <sequence> are needed for the <consecutive sum divisible by the sequence length> argument; distinctness merely makes the present input a <set>.

==== Solution

Let $e_n=x_n-x_*$ for the <limit> $x_*$. <Quadratic convergence> means that eventually $|e_{n+1}|\leq C|e_n|^2$ for a fixed constant $C$; exact asymptotic order two has $|e_{n+1}|/|e_n|^2\to c$ with $0<c<\infty$.

For this iteration and $x_*=3$, direct algebra gives
$$
x_{n+1}-3=x_n-6+\frac9{x_n}=\frac{(x_n-3)^2}{x_n}.
$$
Using the stipulated convergence $x_n\to3$, the ratio of successive squared errors is
$$
\boxed{\frac{|e_{n+1}|}{|e_n|^2}=\frac1{|x_n|}\longrightarrow\frac13.}
$$
Thus the convergence is quadratic. The iteration is well defined here: $x_1=7/2$, and thereafter $x_n>3$ implies $3<x_{n+1}<x_n$. The initial point is not already the <limit>, so the error ratio is meaningful.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume $f$ and $g$ are continuously differentiable near the point. At a regular <feasible point>, meaning $g(x)=0$ and $\nabla g(x)\ne0$, a differentiable constrained local extremum satisfies the <Lagrange multiplier> equations
$$
\nabla f(x)=\lambda\nabla g(x),\qquad g(x)=0.
$$
They arise by differentiating the <optimization Lagrangian> $L(x,\lambda)=f(x)-\lambda g(x)$ with respect to all coordinates and the multiplier. Geometrically, the objective has zero <derivative> along the <tangent space> of the <constraint> surface, so its <gradient> must lie in the normal direction.

Solve these equations for candidates, examine <feasible points> where the regularity condition fails separately, and compare objective values or use a second-order test restricted to feasible directions. The equations locate <stationary points>; they do not by themselves establish which are minima or whether a <global minimum> is attained.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $F=x^6+\tfrac32y^4$ and $G=x^4-2y^2-3$. Every <feasible point> has $x\ne0$, so $\nabla G=(4x^3,-4y)$ cannot vanish. The <Lagrange multiplier> equations are
$$
6x^5=4\lambda x^3,\qquad6y^3=-4\lambda y.
$$
The first gives $\lambda=\tfrac32x^2>0$. If $y\ne0$, the second would give $\lambda=-\tfrac32y^2<0$, a contradiction. Therefore $y=0$, and the <constraint> gives $x^4=3$. The only constrained <stationary points> are $(\pm3^{1/4},0)$.

For a global certificate, feasibility implies $x^4\geq3$, and hence
$$
F=(x^4)^{3/2}+\tfrac32y^4\geq3^{3/2}.
$$
Equality requires $y=0$ and $x^4=3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\min F=3\sqrt3,\qquad (x,y)=(\pm3^{1/4},0).}
$$
The case split is essential: dividing by $y$ would discard precisely the minimizers.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Here the <constraint> surface $x^4+2y^2=3$ is compact and regular, so a <global minimum> exists and satisfies the <Lagrange multiplier> equations
$$
x^3(6x^2-4\lambda)=0,\qquad y(6y^2-4\lambda)=0.
$$
Keep the zero-coordinate cases. If $y=0$, the two points are $(\pm3^{1/4},0)$, with value $3\sqrt3$. If $x=0$, the two points are $(0,\pm\sqrt{3/2})$, with value $27/8$. If both coordinates are nonzero, the equations give $x^2=y^2=t>0$. The <constraint> becomes $t^2+2t=3$, whose only positive solution is $t=1$. These are the four points $(\pm1,\pm1)$, all with value $5/2$.

These cases exhaust the stated eight constrained <stationary points>. Comparing their values gives
$$
\boxed{\min F=\frac52,\qquad (x,y)\in\{(1,1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(-1,-1)\}.}
$$
For additional classification, substituting $u=x^2\in[0,\sqrt3]$ gives $F(u)=u^3+\tfrac38(3-u^2)^2$, with
$$
F'(u)=\tfrac32u(u-1)(u+3).
$$
It decreases from $u=0$ to $u=1$ and increases from $u=1$ to $u=\sqrt3$. Thus the two $y$-axis points are constrained local maxima, the two $x$-axis points are global maxima, and the four mixed-coordinate points are global minima.

Both terms $x^6$ and $\tfrac32y^4$ are nonnegative, and they vanish simultaneously only at $(0,0)$. That point satisfies $x^4+2y^2\leq3$. Consequently

==== i

===== Solution

Use compatible dimensions $A\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ and $b,x\in\mathbb R^d$; the printed “b-by-b” size is read consistently with the displayed products and domain. Take <derivatives> as column <gradients>. <Symmetry> of $A$ gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x)=2Ax+b,\qquad H_f(x)=2A.}
$$
For example, the directional <differential> of $x^TAx$ is $dx^TAx+x^TAdx=2(Ax)^Tdx$. The linear term contributes $b$ to the <gradient> and zero to the <Hessian matrix>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <eigenvector> identity $Ab=\tfrac12b$ gives $\nabla f(-b)=-2Ab+b=0$. Since $H_f=2A$ is a <positive-definite matrix>, this is a strict <local minimum>. A global certificate follows by completing the <quadratic form>:
$$
f(x)=(x+b)^TA(x+b)-b^TAb=(x+b)^TA(x+b)-\tfrac12.
$$
<Positive definiteness> makes the first term nonnegative, with equality only at $x=-b$. Thus **$-b$ is the unique <global minimum>**, with value $-1/2$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The full, undamped <Newton's method in optimization> step is
$$
x_1=x_0-H_f(x_0)^{-1}\nabla f(x_0)
=x_0-(2A)^{-1}(2Ax_0+b)
=-\tfrac12A^{-1}b.
$$
The <eigenvector> identity gives $A^{-1}b=2b$. Consequently **$x_1=-b$ for every starting point**. This is a <one-step Newton solution of a quadratic stationary equation>: its <gradient> is an <affine function>, so a single exact Newton solve resolves it completely.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $(x^Tb)^2=x^Tbb^Tx$ and put $C=A-bb^T$, which is symmetric. Differentiating this <quadratic form> gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla g(x)=2Cx=2Ax-2(x^Tb)b,\qquad H_g(x)=2(A-bb^T).}
$$
Unlike $A$, $C$ need not be a <positive-definite matrix>. Its behaviour must be checked before classifying the <stationary point>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <gradient> vanishes at $0$. Decompose any <vector> as $x=tb+w$, with $w\perp b$. <Symmetry> of $A$ and $Ab=\tfrac12b$ imply $b^TAw=(Ab)^Tw=0$. Since $\|b\|=1$,
$$
g(tb+w)=-\tfrac12t^2+w^TAw.
$$
If $d\geq2$, choose a nonzero $w\perp b$. Arbitrarily close to zero, the direction $b$ gives negative values and the direction $w$ gives positive values. Thus **$0$ is a <saddle point> for $d\geq2$**. Equivalently, the <Hessian matrix> has one negative <eigenvalue> and all its other <eigenvalues> positive.

The dimension matters: if $d=1$, the <orthogonal complement> is zero-dimensional and $g(tb)=-t^2/2$. In that case **$0$ is the unique <global maximum>**. This edge case is not excluded by the stated hypotheses. The decomposition is a <rank-one downdate along an eigenvector>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <matrix> $C=A-bb^T$ is an <invertible matrix>. Indeed, on the line spanned by $b$ it has <eigenvalue> $-1/2$, and on $b^\perp$ it agrees with the positive-definite restriction of $A$. Therefore the full <Newton step> is well defined and gives
$$
\boxed{x_1=x_0-(2C)^{-1}(2Cx_0)=0.}
$$
This is the unique <stationary point> because $C$ is an <invertible matrix>. The algebra applies to every starting point, but it does not make the answer a minimum: for $d\geq2$ it is the <saddle point> just classified, and for $d=1$ it is a maximum. A safeguarded minimization algorithm that replaces an <indefinite matrix> used as its <Hessian matrix> or damps the step need not take this exact step; the assertion concerns the ordinary full <Newton step> formula.

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $q(x)=x^TAx$ and $t(x)=x^Tb$. The <product rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla h(x)=2t(x)Ax+q(x)b.}
$$
Differentiate each term. The <derivative> of $2tAx$ is $2(Ax)b^T+2tA$, while that of $qb$ is $2b(Ax)^T$. Hence
$$
\boxed{H_h(x)=2(x^Tb)A+2(Ax)b^T+2b(Ax)^T.}
$$
The last two terms are transposes of each other, so this <Hessian matrix> is symmetric, as expected.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At $x=0$, the <gradient> from the preceding part vanishes and the <Hessian matrix> is zero. The second-order test is therefore inconclusive. Instead inspect the <eigenline>:
$$
h(tb)=(t^2b^TAb)(t\|b\|^2)=\tfrac12t^3.
$$
For arbitrarily small positive and negative $t$, these values have opposite signs. Thus **$0$ is neither a <local maximum> nor a <local minimum>**. In dimension at least two it is a degenerate <saddle point>; in one dimension it is a stationary <inflection point>. Looking only at the zero <Hessian matrix> would not determine this classification.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <mathematical induction> on the iteration. Initially $x_0=b=1\cdot b$. If $x_n=\lambda_nb$, then $Ax_n=\lambda_nb/2$, $x_n^Tb=\lambda_n$, and $x_n^TAx_n=\lambda_n^2/2$. Therefore
$$
\nabla h(x_n)=\tfrac32\lambda_n^2b.
$$
A <gradient descent> step with any scalar step length $\alpha_n$ consequently has the form
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\alpha_n\nabla h(x_n)
=\left(\lambda_n-\tfrac32\alpha_n\lambda_n^2\right)b.
$$
Thus every iterate remains on the same <eigenline>, with
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0=1,\qquad
\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_n-\tfrac32\alpha_n\lambda_n^2.}
$$
This proves that <gradient descent preserves an eigenline for a cubic product>. The statement is invariance, not convergence to a minimum: along the negative direction of this line the <cubic polynomial> objective is unbounded below.

==== Solution

The displayed rational expression has domain $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$; its value at zero is undefined despite the printed $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ notation. On that domain, the <power rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f'(x)&=2x-4+\frac4{x^2}-\frac2{x^3},\\
f''(x)&=2-\frac8{x^3}+\frac6{x^4},\\
f^{(3)}(x)&=\frac{24}{x^4}-\frac{24}{x^5},\\
f^{(4)}(x)&=-\frac{96}{x^5}+\frac{120}{x^6},\\
f^{(5)}(x)&=\frac{480}{x^6}-\frac{720}{x^7}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
These <derivatives> are smooth on every interval avoiding zero, including all intervals used below.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Substitution gives
$$
f(1)=-6,\qquad f'(1)=f''(1)=f^{(3)}(1)=0,\qquad f^{(4)}(1)=24.
$$
In particular, $1$ is a <stationary point>. For $x$ sufficiently close to $1$, <Taylor's theorem> with fifth-order remainder gives
$$
\boxed{f(x)=-6+(x-1)^4+\frac{f^{(5)}(\xi)}{120}(x-1)^5,}
$$
where $\xi$ lies between $1$ and $x$. The fifth <derivative> is bounded near $1$, so the remainder is $O((x-1)^5)$ and is smaller in magnitude than half the positive quartic term for sufficiently small nonzero $x-1$. Therefore **$x=1$ is a strict <local minimum>**, despite the vanishing <second derivative>.

An exact algebraic check gives the stronger global conclusion:
$$
f(x)+6=\frac{(x-1)^4}{x^2}\geq0\qquad(x\ne0).
$$
Equality holds only at $x=1$, so it is the unique <global minimum> on the actual domain.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $h=(b-a)/n$ and let $M_4=\max_{x\in[a,b]}|f^{(4)}(x)|$. A <composite Simpson's rule> panel covers two strips, so it has width $2h$ and absolute error at most
$$
\frac{(2h)^5}{2880}M_4=\frac{h^5}{90}M_4.
$$
There are $n/2$ panels. By the <triangle inequality>, the absolute error of their sum is at most the sum of their absolute errors. Hence
$$
\boxed{|I-S_n|\leq\frac n2\frac{h^5}{90}M_4
=\frac{(b-a)^5}{180n^4}M_4.}
$$
This assumes even $n$ and, for the usual Simpson remainder theorem, $f\in C^4[a,b]$. In particular its fourth <derivative> is bounded on the interval. It is the <error bound for composite Simpson's rule>, expressed in strip count rather than panel count.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/3.5 Quadrature, subsection (ii) Simpson’s Rule>[NIST DLMF §3.5(ii), equations 3.5.6–3.5.8: elementary and composite Simpson error terms]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $f^{(4)}(x)=24(5-4x)/x^6$. Its <derivative> is $f^{(5)}(x)=240(2x-3)/x^7$, so on $[1,2]$ it decreases to $x=3/2$ and then increases. Its endpoint and turning-point values are
$$
f^{(4)}(1)=24,\qquad
f^{(4)}(3/2)=-\frac{512}{243},\qquad
f^{(4)}(2)=-\frac98.
$$
Thus $M_4=24$, and the preceding bound becomes
$$
\boxed{|I-S_n|\leq\frac{2}{15n^4}\qquad(n\text{ even}).}
$$
For the <rounding> estimate, standard binary64 has spacing $\epsilon=2^{-52}$ above one. Balancing this nominal absolute accuracy with the bound gives
$$
n\approx\left(\frac{2}{15\epsilon}\right)^{1/4}\approx4.95\times10^3.
$$
Thus **a few thousand even strips, roughly $5000$, reach the double-precision scale according to this bound**. A scale-aware estimate leads to the same order. Indeed,
$$
I=-\frac{19}{6}-4\log2\approx-5.9392553889
$$
lies in magnitude between $4$ and $8$, where the binary64 spacing is $2^{-50}$. Balancing the error bound against half that spacing gives about $4.16\times10^3$ strips.

These are saturation estimates from <balancing quadrature truncation and round-off error>, not sharp guarantees of the actual <rounding> plateau. The error bound can be conservative, and evaluation and summation errors can limit accuracy earlier. Merely storing the final result does not specify every source of <rounding error>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Not near the minimum at $1$.** The <derivative> being set to zero factors as
$$
f'(x)=\frac{2(x-1)^3(x+1)}{x^3},
$$
so $1$ has <multiplicity of a root> equal to three. The ordinary <Newton iteration at a multiple root> is generally only linearly convergent, with error ratio $(3-1)/3=2/3$ here.

In fact, cancellation in $x-f'(x)/f''(x)$ gives the exact error identity, away from the original undefined quotient at the root,
$$
x_{n+1}-1=(x_n-1)\frac{x_n+3}{x_n^2+2x_n+3}.
$$
For iterates approaching $1$, the factor tends to $2/3$, proving <linear convergence>. The other zero of $f'$ at $-1$ is simple and could instead give quadratic local convergence; the failure at $1$ is due to its multiplicity, not to every <stationary point> of this <rational function>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For each <integer> $x$, the <events> $\{X=x,Y=y\}$, indexed by the <integers> $y$, are disjoint and their union is $\{X=x\}$. Therefore <countable additivity> gives the marginal <probability mass function>
$$
\boxed{p_X(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb Z}p_{X,Y}(x,y).}
$$
The nonnegative terms make this sum well-defined, even when the <support> is infinite.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <random variables> are <independent random variables> if the <events> determined by one are independent of the <events> determined by the other. For <integer>-valued variables, this is equivalent to the factorization
$$
\boxed{p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\quad\hbox{for every }x,y\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Indeed, <independence> gives the factorization by taking singleton <events>. Conversely, summing it over $x\in A$ and $y\in B$, for any <subsets> $A,B\subseteq\mathbb Z$, gives $\mathbb P(X\in A,Y\in B)=\mathbb P(X\in A)\mathbb P(Y\in B)$. Thus the singleton condition implies <independence> of all <events> in question.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Interpret existence of the <expectations> as finite <expected values>, equivalently $\mathbb E|X|<\infty$ and $\mathbb E|Y|<\infty$. First justify the summations rather than assuming that a signed double series can be rearranged. <Independence> and <Tonelli theorem> give
$$
\sum_{x,y}|xy|p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\sum_{x,y}|x|p_X(x)|y|p_Y(y)=\mathbb E|X|\,\mathbb E|Y|<\infty.
$$
Consequently $XY$ is an <integrable random variable>, and <Fubini theorem> permits summing the signed terms in either order:
$$
\mathbb E[XY]=\sum_x\sum_yxy\,p_X(x)p_Y(y)=\left(\sum_xxp_X(x)\right)\left(\sum_yyp_Y(y)\right)=\boxed{\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y].}
$$
If extended infinite <expectations> are allowed instead, the phrase “exist” needs additional care: expressions such as $0\cdot\infty$ do not define an <expectation>. The finite-<expectation> convention avoids that ambiguity.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Summing the two nonzero entries with $X=1$ gives
$$
\mathbb P(X=1)=p(1-e^{-p})+pe^{-p}=p.
$$
The remaining <probability> is $\mathbb P(X=0)=1-p$, so **$X$ has the <Bernoulli distribution> with parameter $p$**.

For the other marginal, the two entries at $Y=0$ add to $e^{-p}$, the entry at $Y=1$ is $pe^{-p}$, and for $k\geqslant2$ the only nonzero entry is already $e^{-p}p^k/k!$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y=k)=e^{-p}\frac{p^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots,}
$$
and **$Y$ has the <Poisson distribution> with parameter $p$**. This also checks normalization of the <joint probability mass function> by summing the marginal.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Equality can occur only at the two pairs $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Their combined <probability> is
$$
e^{-p}-p(1-e^{-p})+pe^{-p}=(1+2p)e^{-p}-p.
$$
Taking the complementary <probability> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\ne Y)=1+p-(1+2p)e^{-p}.}
$$
Since $1+2p>0$, the supplied exponential bound implies
$$
\mathbb P(X\ne Y)\leqslant1+p-(1+2p)(1-p)=\boxed{2p^2}.
$$
This is a bound on the mismatch <probability> for this particular <coupling> of the two <marginal distributions>; the variables are not presumed independent.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <marginal distributions> have <means> $\mathbb E[X]=p$ and $\mathbb E[Y]=p$. In the joint distribution, $XY$ is nonzero only at $(1,1)$: when $X=1$, the only possible values of $Y$ are $0$ and $1$. Hence
$$
\mathbb E[XY]=pe^{-p},\qquad\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=pe^{-p}-p^2=p(e^{-p}-p).}
$$
These quantities are finite; for example, $0\leqslant XY\leqslant1$. The <covariance> changes sign when $p=e^{-p}$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**There is no such value of $p$ in the specified interval.** Indeed, $\mathbb P(Y=1)=pe^{-p}>0$, and the <joint probability mass function> gives
$$
\mathbb P(X=1\mid Y=1)=1\ne p=\mathbb P(X=1).
$$
This contradicts <independence> for every $0<p<0.8$. Equivalently, $(1,2)$ has joint <probability> zero but the product of its marginal <probabilities> is $p\,e^{-p}p^2/2>0$.

At the suggested value $r=e^{-r}$, the <covariance> is zero, but the same <conditional probability> contradiction still applies. This is an explicit example of how <uncorrelated Bernoulli and Poisson variables need not be independent>. Zero <covariance> controls one mixed <moment>, whereas <independence> requires the entire <joint probability mass function> to factor.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $(B_j)$ be a finite or countable <partition of a set> of the <sample space> into disjoint <events>, with $\mathbb P(B_j)>0$. For any <event> $A$, the <law of total probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\sum_j\mathbb P(A\mid B_j)\mathbb P(B_j).}
$$
To prove it, the intersections $A\cap B_j$ are disjoint and have union $A$. <Countable additivity> and the definition of <conditional probability> therefore give
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\sum_j\mathbb P(A\cap B_j)=\sum_j\mathbb P(A\mid B_j)\mathbb P(B_j).
$$
It is enough that the union of the partition <events> has <probability> one. Parts of <probability> zero contribute zero in the intersection formula and can be omitted before writing <conditional probabilities>; conditioning by division on a zero-<probability> part would be undefined.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $X=m$, the first $m-1$ digits must all differ from $3$ and the next digit must be $3$. <Independence> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=m)=\left(\frac23\right)^{m-1}\frac13,\qquad m=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Thus $X$ has the <geometric distribution> counting trials up to and including the first success, with success <probability> $1/3$. For completeness, its <mean> follows from the <tail-sum formula>:
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb P(X>n)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac23\right)^n=\boxed{3}.
$$
The sum of the <geometric series> also proves finiteness of this <expectation>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

There is no forbidden pair in a <sequence> of length zero or one, so $p_0=p_1=1$. For $n\geqslant2$, divide acceptable <sequences> into two disjoint cases. If the first digit differs from $3$, its <probability> is $2/3$, and the remaining $n-1$ independent digits must avoid the pair. If the first digit is $3$, the next must differ from $3$, which has combined <probability> $2/9$, and the remaining $n-2$ digits must avoid the pair. The boundary between this prefix and the remaining digits cannot create a forbidden pair, because the prefix ends in a digit other than $3$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{p_n=\frac23p_{n-1}+\frac29p_{n-2},\qquad p_0=p_1=1.}
$$
The <characteristic polynomial of a linear recurrence> is $r^2-\frac23r-\frac29$, with distinct roots
$$
r_+=\frac{1+\sqrt3}{3},\qquad r_-=\frac{1-\sqrt3}{3}.
$$
Writing $p_n=Ar_+^n+Br_-^n$, the <initial conditions> give $A+B=1$ and $Ar_++Br_-=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_n=\frac{2+\sqrt3}{2\sqrt3}\left(\frac{1+\sqrt3}{3}\right)^n+\frac{\sqrt3-2}{2\sqrt3}\left(\frac{1-\sqrt3}{3}\right)^n,\qquad n\geqslant0.}
$$
For example, $p_2=8/9$, since only one of the nine equally likely pairs is forbidden. This is the case with success <probability> $1/3$ of the <avoidance recurrence for two consecutive successes>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $T_2$ be the time of the second occurrence of $3$, whether or not it is adjacent to the first. On every digit <sequence>, completion of a consecutive pair requires at least two occurrences of $3$, so $Y\geqslant T_2$.

The waiting time for the first $3$ has <mean> $3$. Once that digit has appeared, the fresh independent digits give another waiting time with the same <mean> for the next $3$. Thus $\mathbb E[T_2]=3+3=6$, the <mean> of the corresponding <negative binomial distribution>. Monotonicity of <expectation> now proves **$\mathbb E[Y]\geqslant6$**, without computing the waiting time for the consecutive pair itself. The argument remains valid even before establishing that $Y$ has finite mean, since these waiting times are nonnegative.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <probability density function> convention used here, a <random variable> $X$ has a nonnegative <probability density function> $f$, with $\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)\,\mathrm dx=1$, such that for every <Borel set> $A$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X\in A)=\int_A f(x)\,\mathrm dx.}
$$
In particular, its <cumulative distribution function> is $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,\mathrm dt$ and individual points have <probability> zero. More precisely, this property is the statement that the distribution is an <absolutely continuous probability distribution>; <continuity> of the <cumulative distribution function> alone is a weaker property and need not imply a <probability density function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <probability density function> $f$, the finite <expected value> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f(x)\,\mathrm dx,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|f(x)\,\mathrm dx<\infty.}
$$
The <absolute integrability> condition makes the signed integral unambiguous. One can also allow an extended <expectation> when exactly one of the positive and negative parts has infinite integral, obtaining $+\infty$ or $-\infty$. If both parts have infinite integral, the <expectation> is undefined; subtracting two infinities is not a definition.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Multiply the <probability density function> by $x$ and integrate over its <support>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X]&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_0^1x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\,\mathrm dx\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The middle equality is the <beta function> integral with parameters $\alpha+1$ and $\beta$; the last uses the recurrence for the <gamma function>. Both parameters are positive, so the integral converges. Equivalently, multiplying the <beta distribution> <probability density function> by $x$ converts its kernel to that of a <beta distribution> <probability density function> with its first parameter increased by one.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/5.12 Beta Function>[NIST DLMF §5.12, equation 5.12.1]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The same integral works with both powers shifted:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X^s(1-X)^t]&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_0^1x^{\alpha+s-1}(1-x)^{\beta+t-1}\,\mathrm dx\\
&=\boxed{\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\Gamma(\alpha+s)\Gamma(\beta+t)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+s+t)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The assumptions $s>-\alpha$ and $t>-\beta$ say precisely that the exponents at each endpoint are greater than $-1$, ensuring <integrability> there. Values of a negative power at the endpoints themselves can be assigned arbitrarily, because those endpoints have <probability> zero. This establishes the <mixed power moments of a beta distribution> without requiring $s$ and $t$ to be <integers>.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/5.12 Beta Function>[NIST DLMF §5.12, equation 5.12.1]{ref}

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $Z=U^a$. Since $a>0$, the map is increasing on $[0,1]$, and for $0\leqslant z\leqslant1$,
$$
\mathbb P(Z\leqslant z)=\mathbb P(U\leqslant z^{1/a})=z^{1/a}.
$$
<Differentiation> gives
$$
f_Z(z)=\frac1a z^{1/a-1},\qquad0<z<1,
$$
and zero outside this interval. Since $B(1/a,1)=a$, this is exactly the <beta distribution> <probability density function> with **parameters $(1/a,1)$**. The <power transform of a uniform random variable> therefore satisfies $U^a\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1/a,1)$; the second parameter does not depend on $a$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $W_i=U_i^2$. Applying the same <measurable function> separately to <independent and identically distributed> <random variables> preserves those two properties, and
$$
\mathbb E[W_i]=\int_0^1u^2\,\mathrm du=\frac13.
$$
In particular, these bounded <random variables> are <integrable random variables>. The <weak law of large numbers> gives
$$
\overline W_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nW_i\xrightarrow{\mathbb P}\frac13.
$$
If $c>1/3$, then $\{\overline W_n>c\}\subseteq\{|\overline W_n-1/3|\geqslant c-1/3\}$, whose <probability> tends to zero. If $0\leqslant c<1/3$, then $\{\overline W_n\leqslant c\}\subseteq\{|\overline W_n-1/3|\geqslant1/3-c\}$, whose <probability> also tends to zero. Taking a complement in the second case proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^nU_i^2>cn\right)\longrightarrow\begin{cases}0,&c>1/3,\\1,&0\leqslant c<1/3.\end{cases}}
$$
The positive gap from $1/3$ is essential to this argument. <Convergence in probability> alone does not determine the answer at the excluded threshold $c=1/3$.
