== Discrete mathematics
{scope}

=== 1
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

For pairwise <disjoint> <finite sets>, the <rule of sum> gives
$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right|=\sum_{i=1}^n|A_i|.
$$
The password sets of different lengths are <disjoint>, so the required number is **$P_4+P_6+P_8$**.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

The <rule of subtraction> is
$$
\boxed{|A\setminus B|=|A|-|A\cap B|.}
$$
In particular, if $B\subseteq A$ are <finite sets>, the <set difference> has <cardinality> $|A|-|B|$.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

The <rule of product> says that a sequence of $k$ choices, with $n_i$ possibilities at stage $i$ regardless of earlier choices, has **$\prod_{i=1}^k n_i$** possible outcomes. Applied to the <Cartesian product> of <finite sets>, it gives $|A\times B|=|A||B|$. Here independence means that the available choices do not restrict one another; no <probability> assumption is needed.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

The <rule of product> counts $7^4$ unrestricted passwords and $6^4$ passwords containing no A. By the <rule of subtraction>, the answer is
$$
\boxed{7^4-6^4=2401-1296=1105.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <power set> is $\mathcal P(A)=\{B:B\subseteq A\}$, the <set> of all <subsets> of $A$. In this case,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal P(A)=\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b\},\{c\},\{a,b\},\{a,c\},\{b,c\},\{a,b,c\}\}.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Each of the $n$ elements is either included or excluded, so the <rule of product> gives $|\mathcal P(\{1,\ldots,n\})|=2^n$. Alternatively, partition the <power set> into <disjoint> families according to <cardinality>. The family of $k$-element <subsets> has size equal to the <binomial coefficient> $\binom nk$. The <rule of sum> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{2^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk.}
$$

==== c
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

For <finite sets>, the <inclusion-exclusion principle> states
$$
\boxed{\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right|=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|.}
$$
Thus single-set <cardinalities> are added, pairwise-intersection <cardinalities> subtracted, triple-intersection <cardinalities> added, and so on.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $A_d$ contain the multiples of $d$ among $1,\ldots,999$. Its <cardinality> is $\lfloor999/d\rfloor$, using the <floor function>. Because $3,7,11$ are pairwise <coprime>, their pairwise intersections correspond to divisibility by $21,33,77$, and their triple intersection to divisibility by $231$. The <inclusion-exclusion principle> gives
$$
\boxed{333+142+90-47-30-12+4=480.}
$$

{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <pigeonhole principle> states that **if $N$ objects are placed in $k$ boxes and $N>k$, at least one box contains at least two objects**.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Set $m=\lceil N/k\rceil$, using the <ceiling function>. If no box contains at least $m$ objects, all boxes contain at most $m-1$, so the total number of objects is at most $k(m-1)$. Taking the <contrapositive>, a total greater than $k(m-1)$ forces some box to contain at least $m$ objects. But $m-1<N/k$, hence $k(m-1)<N$. This proves the <generalized pigeonhole principle>: **some box contains at least $\lceil N/k\rceil$ objects**.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

By the <generalized pigeonhole principle>, **13 cards** guarantee $\lceil13/4\rceil=4$ cards in one suit. The guarantee is sharp: 12 cards can consist of three cards from each suit, with no four cards of a common suit.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Use <mathematical induction> on $n\ge0$. For $n=0$, the sum is $0!\,0=0=1!-1$. Suppose it is $(n+1)!-1$ at $n$. Then
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}i!\,i=(n+1)!-1+(n+1)!(n+1)=(n+2)!-1.
$$
The induction is complete, proving **$\sum_{i=0}^n i!\,i=(n+1)!-1$**. The <factorial> identity $i!\,i=(i+1)!-i!$ also explains the cancellation directly.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

In <big O notation>, $f(n)=O(g(n))$ means that there exist $C>0$ and $n_0$ such that $|f(n)|\le C|g(n)|$ for every integer $n\ge n_0$. For the required <polynomial versus exponential growth> comparison, $3^3=3^3$. If $n^3\le3^n$ and $n\ge3$, then
$$
(n+1)^3=n^3\left(1+\frac1n\right)^3\le3^n\left(\frac43\right)^3<3^{n+1}.
$$
By <mathematical induction>, $n^3\le3^n$ for all $n\ge3$. Thus **$n^3=O(3^n)$**, with $C=1$ and $n_0=3$.

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is a <reflexive relation> precisely when **$aRa$ for every $a\in A$**.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is a <symmetric relation> precisely when **$aRb\Rightarrow bRa$ for all $a,b\in A$**.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is an <antisymmetric relation> precisely when **$(aRb\ \text{and}\ bRa)\Rightarrow a=b$ for all $a,b\in A$**.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is a <transitive relation> precisely when **$(aRb\ \text{and}\ bRc)\Rightarrow aRc$ for all $a,b,c\in A$**.

===== v
{scope}

====== Solution

A <binary relation> is an <equivalence relation> precisely when it is **<reflexive>, <symmetric>, and <transitive>**.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <counterexample> to closure under <union of antisymmetric relations>, take $A=\{0,1\}$, $R=\{(0,1)\}$, and $S=\{(1,0)\}$. Each is an <antisymmetric relation>: neither contains an off-diagonal pair together with its reverse. But $R\cup S$ contains both $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ while $0\ne1$. Therefore **$R\cup S$ is not an <antisymmetric relation>**.

==== c
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Write $q(x,y)=x^2+y^2$. Equality of $q$-values defines an <equivalence relation induced by a function>: equality is <reflexive>, <symmetric>, and <transitive>, so all three properties hold for $R$. The <equivalence class> of $(x,y)$ is
$$
\boxed{[(x,y)]=\{(u,v):u^2+v^2=x^2+y^2\}.}
$$
For $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ this is the <circle> centred at the origin with radius $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$; the remaining <equivalence class> is the singleton $\{(0,0)\}$.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

This <binary relation> is **not an <equivalence relation>**, because it is not <symmetric>: $(0,0)R(0,1)$ since $0\le1$, but $(0,1)R(0,0)$ is false. In fact the injective encoding $(a,b)\mapsto10a+b$ gives a <total order>. There are no <equivalence classes> for this <binary relation>.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

Every nonzero integer $a$ has $a^2>0$, proving <reflexivity>. Because $ab=ba$, the relation is <symmetric>. If $ab>0$ and $bc>0$, then $ac=(ab)(bc)/b^2>0$, proving <transitivity>. Thus this is an <equivalence relation>, whose **two <equivalence classes> are the positive integers and the negative integers**:
$$
\{1,2,3,\ldots\},\qquad\{-1,-2,-3,\ldots\}.
$$

== Continuous mathematics
{scope}

=== 4
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <second-derivative criterion for convexity> is
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is convex on }\mathbb R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f''(x)\ge0\text{ for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The non-strict inequality is necessary and sufficient; the stronger condition $f''>0$ is sufficient for <strict convexity>.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Expand before taking the <second derivative>:
$$
f(x)=e^{3x}+3e^x+3e^{-x}+e^{-3x},\qquad f''(x)=9e^{3x}+3e^x+3e^{-x}+9e^{-3x}>0.
$$
The <second-derivative criterion for convexity> proves that **$f$ is <strictly convex>**, hence <convex>.

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <Hessian criterion for convexity>, in the usual twice-differentiable multivariate setting, is
$$
\boxed{g\text{ is convex on }\mathbb R^d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x)v\ge0\text{ for all }x,v\in\mathbb R^d.}
$$
Thus the <Hessian matrix> must be a <positive semidefinite matrix> everywhere. The criterion follows by applying the <second-derivative criterion for convexity> to each line restriction $t\mapsto g(x+tv)$, whose <second derivative> is $v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x+tv)v$. Continuous second partials are a sufficient regularity hypothesis for this line-restriction argument.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Writing $g=e^{x^{\mathsf T}x}$, the <gradient> and <Hessian matrix> are
$$
\nabla g(x)=2gx,\qquad H(g)(x)=g(2I+4xx^{\mathsf T}).
$$
Consequently, for any nonzero $v$,
$$
v^{\mathsf T}H(g)(x)v=g\left(2\|v\|^2+4(x^{\mathsf T}v)^2\right)>0.
$$
In particular the <Hessian matrix> is a <positive semidefinite matrix> everywhere, so the <Hessian criterion for convexity> proves **$g$ is <convex>**. The strict inequality along every nonconstant line also proves <strict convexity>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The map $u\mapsto u^2$ is <strictly increasing> on $[0,\infty)$: for $u,v\ge0$, $u\le v$ if and only if $u^2\le v^2$. Therefore a point has the smallest $f$-value if and only if it has the smallest $f^2$-value, and the same applies to $g$. By <minimizers under strictly increasing transformations>, **both asserted <argmin> equalities hold**, including when the <argmin> sets are empty. The differentiability assumptions are unnecessary for this order argument.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Minimize the square of the <Euclidean distance>, using <minimizers under strictly increasing transformations>. For $s=x+y>0$,
$$
x^2+y^2+\ln^2(x+y)=\frac{s^2}{2}+\frac{(x-y)^2}{2}+\ln^2s.
$$
For fixed $s$ the unique minimum has $x=y=s/2$. Put $x=y=\alpha>0$ and $J(\alpha)=2\alpha^2+\ln^2(2\alpha)$. Its <derivative> is
$$
J'(\alpha)=\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(2\alpha^2+\ln(2\alpha)\right).
$$
Let $h(\alpha)=2\alpha^2+\ln(2\alpha)$. Since $h'(\alpha)=4\alpha+1/\alpha>0$, it is <strictly increasing>, with limits $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ at the two ends of $(0,\infty)$. It therefore has exactly one zero. The sign of $J'$ changes there from negative to positive, proving the unique <global minimum>. The nearest point is **$P=(\alpha,\alpha,\ln(2\alpha))$** at that zero.<Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics, and probability examination paper A10100W1 (2022)>[Question 4(d), p. 4: the logarithm is interpreted as ln(2α)]{ref}

The <Newton root-finding iteration> is
$$
\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_k-\frac{2\alpha_k^2+\ln(2\alpha_k)}{4\alpha_k+1/\alpha_k}.
$$
Starting at $0.4$ gives $0.376376476$, $0.376544572$, and $0.376544582$. Moreover $h(0.3765)<0<h(0.3766)$ certifies the rounding. Thus **$\alpha=0.377$ to three significant figures**; more accurately $P\approx(0.3765445825,0.3765445825,-0.2835716452)$.

=== 5
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Use <Simpson's rule>: the <cubic exactness of Simpson's rule> includes every quadratic integrand, so three function evaluations already give the exact answer. Thus **the error is zero**. For a general smooth integrand the corresponding <composite Simpson's rule> has error $O(N^{-4})$ in <big O notation>, but that bound is unnecessarily weak here.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Use <composite Simpson's rule> on this smooth, bounded one-dimensional interval. Its fourth <derivative> is bounded on the interval, so the <error bound for composite Simpson's rule> gives **$O(N^{-4})$** in <big O notation> as the number of function evaluations increases.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

Use <tensor-product quadrature> with <composite Simpson's rule> in each coordinate. If there are $m$ subintervals in each direction, then $N=(m+1)^2$ and the <quadrature rule> error is $O(m^{-4})$: apply the one-dimensional <error bound for composite Simpson's rule> successively, using bounded fourth partial <derivatives>. Hence the error is **$O(N^{-2})$** in <big O notation>. The rectangular domain makes the product grid convenient.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

A convenient choice for this irregular region is <Monte Carlo integration>. The region is contained in $[-1,1]^2$. For independent uniform points $U_j$ in that square, let
$$
\widehat I_N=\frac4N\sum_{j=1}^N\mathbf1_{\{U_j\in R\}}\exp\!\left(e^{(U_j)_1}+e^{(U_j)_2}+e^{(U_j)_1(U_j)_2}\right).
$$
The <indicator random variable> incorporates the curved boundary directly. This estimator is unbiased; the integrand is bounded on the square, so its <variance> is finite. The <variance of a sum> of independent terms gives <root mean square> error **$O(N^{-1/2})$** in <big O notation>. This is a stochastic error rate. Applying a rectangular <Simpson's rule> to the indicator-weighted integrand would lose the smoothness required for its fourth-order error bound.

===== v
{scope}

====== Solution

For a generic integrand in 20 dimensions, <Monte Carlo integration> gives <root mean square> error $O(N^{-1/2})$, whereas <tensor-product quadrature> with <composite Simpson's rule> gives only $O(N^{-4/20})=O(N^{-1/5})$. One can sample uniformly in the <Euclidean unit ball>, for example by an independent uniform direction and radius $U^{1/20}$ with $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$.

For this particular integrand, the most efficient elementary choice uses <radial integration over a ball> first. The area of the unit 19-sphere is $2\pi^{10}/9!$, as follows from the <volume of an n-ball>. Hence
$$
I=\frac{2\pi^{10}}{9!}\int_0^1 e^{r^2}r^{19}\,dr=\frac{\pi^{10}}{9!}\int_0^1 e^t t^9\,dt.
$$
The reduced integrand is smooth. Apply <composite Simpson's rule> to obtain **$O(N^{-4})$** deterministic error in <big O notation>, with one original radial integrand evaluation per node. If the selection is restricted to direct multidimensional methods, **<Monte Carlo integration> with $O(N^{-1/2})$ <root mean square> error** is the appropriate choice.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

By <Taylor's theorem> with <Lagrange remainder>, for an increment $t$,
$$
\boxed{f(x+t)=f(x)+tf'(x)+\frac{t^2}{2}f''(x)+R_x(t),\qquad R_x(t)=\frac{t^3}{6}f'''(\xi_t),}
$$
where $\xi_t$ lies between $x$ and $x+t$. The given third <derivative> bound implies $|R_x(t)|\le |t|^3/6$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the preceding <Taylor's theorem> at $t=h$ and $t=-h$, with $h>0$. The even-order terms cancel in the <central finite difference>, giving
$$
d(x,h)-f'(x)=\frac{R_x(h)-R_x(-h)}{2h}.
$$
Both <Lagrange remainders> have magnitude at most $h^3/6$, so the <triangle inequality> gives the <sharp central-secant derivative error>
$$
\boxed{|d(x,h)-f'(x)|\le\frac{h^2}{6}.}
$$

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Write the computed function values as $f(x\pm h)+e_\pm$, where $|e_\pm|\le\delta$. The computed <central finite difference> $\widetilde d$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{|\widetilde d-d|=\left|\frac{e_+-e_-}{2h}\right|\le\frac\delta h,\qquad |\widetilde d-f'(x)|\le\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac\delta h.}
$$
The two contributions are respectively evaluation <absolute error> and <truncation error>. For $\delta>0$, <step-size choice for a noisy central derivative> minimizes their sum: its <derivative> is $h/3-\delta/h^2$, negative before and positive after
$$
\boxed{h=(3\delta)^{1/3},\qquad \min_{h>0}\left(\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac\delta h\right)=\frac{(3\delta)^{2/3}}2.}
$$
If $\delta=0$, the bound tends to zero as $h\downarrow0$ and has no positive minimizing step.

=== 6
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

The <derivative> is represented by the <gradient>, and the <Hessian matrix> contains the second partial <derivatives>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla f(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}4x^3-4y\\4y-4x\end{pmatrix},\qquad H(f)(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}12x^2&-4\\-4&4\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Equivalently the <derivative> as a linear map is $Df(x,y)(u,v)=(4x^3-4y)u+(4y-4x)v$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <stationary point> equations give $y=x$ and $x^3=x$, hence $(0,0),(1,1),(-1,-1)$. At $(0,0)$ the <Hessian matrix> has negative <determinant> $-16$, so **$(0,0)$ is a <saddle point>**. At each of $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, the <Hessian matrix> has positive leading minors $12$ and $32$, proving a strict <local minimum>. More strongly,
$$
f(x,y)=(x^2-1)^2+2(y-x)^2+3\ge3.
$$
Thus **$(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$ are exactly the <global minima>, with value $3$**. There are no <local maxima>, since any differentiable interior <local maximum> would be a <stationary point>, and all three have been classified.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For the equality-constrained <minimization problem>, $y=8/x$ and $x\ne0$. Put $t=x^2>0$. The restricted objective is
$$
f(x,8/x)=t^2+\frac{128}{t}-28=:F(t),\qquad F'(t)=2t-\frac{128}{t^2}.
$$
The only zero has $t^3=64$, so $t=4$. Since $F''(t)=2+256/t^3>0$, the <second-derivative criterion for convexity> proves a unique minimizing $t$. Consequently **the equality constraint has minimizers $(2,4)$ and $(-2,-4)$, with minimum $20$**.

For the inequality-constrained <minimization problem>, the identity $f=(x^2-1)^2+2(y-x)^2+3$ supplies the universal lower bound $3$. Both equality points for that bound, $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$, satisfy $xy=1\le8$. Hence **the inequality constraint has exactly these two minimizers, with minimum $3$**.

==== d
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

For <Newton's method in optimization>, let $\Delta=H(f)^{-1}\nabla f$. Its second row equation is $-4\Delta_x+4\Delta_y=4y-4x$, so $\Delta_y=\Delta_x+y-x$. Therefore $y_{n+1}=y_n-\Delta_y=x_n-\Delta_x=x_{n+1}$. The given first coordinate yields
$$
\boxed{\varphi(x,y)=\frac{2x^3}{3x^2-1}.}
$$
The <Newton step> is defined where $\det H(f)=16(3x^2-1)\ne0$; both coordinates after any defined step are equal.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

By the <diagonal reduction in a two-variable Newton iteration>, $y_n=x_n$ for $n\ge1$. Put $e_n=x_n-1$. Direct factorization gives the exact <Newton map in optimization> error identity
$$
e_{n+1}=\frac{2x_n^3-(3x_n^2-1)}{3x_n^2-1}=e_n^2\frac{2x_n+1}{3x_n^2-1}.
$$
The assumed convergence $x_n\to1$ makes the final factor tend to $3/2$, so it is bounded for all sufficiently large $n$. Hence $|e_{n+1}|\le C|e_n|^2$. For the <Euclidean norm> error $E_n=\|(x_n,y_n)-(1,1)\|=\sqrt2|e_n|$, this becomes **$E_{n+1}\le(C/\sqrt2)E_n^2$**, proving <quadratic convergence>. Unless the exact limit is reached in finitely many steps, $E_{n+1}/E_n^2\to3/(2\sqrt2)$.

== Probability
{scope}

=== 7
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Assume finite second moments and set $Y_i=X_i-\mathbb E X_i$. By <linearity of expectation>, centring $X_1+X_2$ gives $Y_1+Y_2$. Expanding its square and using the definitions of <variance> and <covariance>,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X_1+X_2)=\mathbb E(Y_1+Y_2)^2=\operatorname{var}(X_1)+\operatorname{var}(X_2)+2\operatorname{cov}(X_1,X_2).}
$$
No independence assumption is required for this <variance of a sum> identity.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

For finite second moments, the <variance of a sum> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{var}(X_i)+2\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\sum_{i,j=1}^n\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j).}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

In the <uniform balls-in-bins allocation>, each ball enters box 1 independently with <probability> $1/n$. Thus **$M_1$ has the <binomial distribution> $\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/n)$**, with <probability mass function>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_1=k)=\binom nk\left(\frac1n\right)^k\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\ldots,n,}
$$
and zero outside that range.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

For both boxes to be empty, each ball must avoid two specified boxes. The individual avoidance <probability> is $(n-2)/n$, and the choices of different balls are independent. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_1=0,M_2=0)=\left(1-\frac2n\right)^n.}
$$
This also covers $n=2$, when the answer is zero.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

Write $p_n=(1-1/n)^n$, the <probability> that all balls avoid a specified box. Each of the <empty-box indicators in uniform allocation> is an <indicator random variable> with success <probability> $p_n$. Since $I_i^2=I_i$, its <expectation> and <variance> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E I_i=p_n,\qquad\operatorname{var}(I_i)=p_n(1-p_n).}
$$

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $q_n=(1-2/n)^n$. The product of two distinct <empty-box indicators in uniform allocation> is the <indicator random variable> of both boxes being empty, so $\mathbb E(I_iI_j)=q_n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cov}(I_i,I_j)=q_n-p_n^2=\left(1-\frac2n\right)^n-\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{2n}<0.}
$$
Indeed $0\le1-2/n<(1-1/n)^2$ for $n\ge2$, and taking the positive integer power $n$ preserves this strict inequality. This proves the negative <covariance> directly.

===== v
{scope}

====== Solution

By <linearity of expectation>, $\mathbb E N_n=np_n$. The <variance of a sum> and the negative <covariance> just proved give
$$
\operatorname{var}(N_n)=np_n(1-p_n)+n(n-1)(q_n-p_n^2)\le np_n(1-p_n)\le\frac n4.
$$
Apply <Chebyshev's inequality> to the <empty-box indicators in uniform allocation>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(\left|\frac{N_n}{n}-p_n\right|>\epsilon\right)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(N_n)}{n^2\epsilon^2}\le\frac1{4n\epsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This proves the required <concentration of the empty-box fraction>. Since $p_n\to e^{-1}$, it also yields <convergence in probability> of $N_n/n$ to $e^{-1}$.

=== 8
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable>, its <probability generating function> is
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbb P(X=k)s^k.}
$$
The series converges absolutely at least for $|s|\le1$, with $0^0=1$ in the $k=0$ term; it may converge on a larger domain.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

This is the <geometric distribution> on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with success parameter $1/2$. Its <probability generating function> is a <geometric series>:
$$
\boxed{G_N(s)=\frac12\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac s2\right)^k=\frac1{2-s},\qquad |s|<2.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The ancestor contributes one individual. Given its offspring count $N$, the descendant family rooted at each child is an independent <branching process> with the same <offspring distribution> as the original one. Its <total progeny> therefore has the same distribution as $Z$. The independent reproduction choices let these descendant <total progeny> variables be represented by independent copies $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$, independent of $N$. Hence, on this family-tree construction,
$$
\boxed{Z=1+\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i,}
$$
where the sum is zero when $N=0$.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

For $0\le s\le1$, condition on $N=k$. Independence of the descendant <total progeny> variables gives
$$
\mathbb E\!\left[s^{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i}\mid N=k\right]=\prod_{i=1}^k\mathbb E[s^{Z_i}]=G_Z(s)^k,
$$
including the empty product $1$ when $k=0$. Taking <expectation> over $N$ proves the <probability generating function of an independent random sum> formula
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\!\left[s^{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathbb P(N=k)G_Z(s)^k=G_N(G_Z(s)).}
$$
The same identity holds for complex $|s|<1$ by absolute convergence.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

The extra ancestor multiplies the <probability generating function> by $s$. The preceding <probability generating function of an independent random sum> formula therefore yields
$$
G_Z(s)=sG_N(G_Z(s))=\frac{s}{2-G_Z(s)},\qquad G_Z(s)^2-2G_Z(s)+s=0.
$$
For $0\le s<1$ the two roots are $1\pm\sqrt{1-s}$. A <probability generating function> lies in $[0,1]$ there, so the plus root is impossible. Also $Z\ge1$ gives $G_Z(0)=0$. Consequently the <total progeny with critical geometric offspring> has
$$
\boxed{G_Z(s)=1-\sqrt{1-s},\qquad 0\le s\le1.}
$$
At $s=1$ the value is $1$, consistent with almost-sure finiteness. For complex $|s|<1$, the same expression uses the analytic square root that equals $1$ at $s=0$.

=== 9
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <cumulative distribution function> is **$F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\le x)$**. In the absolutely continuous setting of a <probability density function>, the <probability density function> is a nonnegative integrable function $f_X$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\,dt,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(t)\,dt=1.}
$$
Thus $\mathbb P(a<X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(t)\,dt$ and $F_X'=f_X$ almost everywhere. A merely continuous <cumulative distribution function> need not possess a <probability density function>; the density assumption means absolute continuity.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Every <cumulative distribution function> is **nondecreasing, right-continuous, valued in $[0,1]$, and has limits $0$ at $-\infty$ and $1$ at $+\infty$**. For a continuous <random variable> it is continuous. When a <probability density function> exists, it is in fact absolutely continuous, with nonnegative <derivative> almost everywhere.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

The proposed <cumulative distribution function> satisfies $0<F(x)<1$ and is smooth. Its <derivative> is
$$
F'(x)=e^{-x}\exp(-e^{-x})>0,
$$
so it is increasing and continuous, hence right-continuous. As $x\to-\infty$, $e^{-x}\to\infty$ and $F(x)\to0$; as $x\to\infty$, $e^{-x}\to0$ and $F(x)\to1$. Thus **it has all the required <cumulative distribution function> properties**, and defines the standard <Gumbel distribution>.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

Because $Z=e^{-Y}>0$, its <cumulative distribution function> is zero for $z\le0$. For $z>0$, the transformation is decreasing, and $Y$ has a continuous <cumulative distribution function>, so
$$
\mathbb P(Z\le z)=\mathbb P(Y\ge-\ln z)=1-F(-\ln z)=1-e^{-z}.
$$
Thus the <exponential transform of a standard Gumbel variable> gives
$$
\boxed{F_Z(z)=\begin{cases}0,&z\le0,\\1-e^{-z},&z>0,\end{cases}\qquad Z\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).}
$$
This is the <exponential distribution> with rate $1$.

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Write $M_n=\max_iE_i$. Each <exponential distribution> has <cumulative distribution function> $1-e^{-x}$ for $x\ge0$. Independence therefore gives the <maximum of independent exponential random variables> formula
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_n\le x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\(1-e^{-x})^n,&x\ge0.\end{cases}}
$$
For any fixed $y$, the threshold $y+\ln n$ is nonnegative for all sufficiently large $n$. Then
$$
\mathbb P(M_n-\ln n\le y)=\left(1-\frac{e^{-y}}n\right)^n\longrightarrow\boxed{\exp(-e^{-y})}.
$$
This is <convergence in distribution> to the standard <Gumbel distribution>.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

First $0\le M_n\le\sum_iE_i$, so <linearity of expectation> gives $\mathbb E M_n\le n<\infty$. The <tail integral formula for expectation>, followed by the finite <binomial theorem> expansion, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E M_n&=\int_0^\infty\left[1-(1-e^{-x})^n\right]dx\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^{k+1}\binom nk\int_0^\infty e^{-kx}dx\\
&=\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^n\binom nk\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}k.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The exchange involves only a finite sum. As a useful check, substitution $t=e^{-x}$ rewrites the integral as $\int_0^1[1-(1-t)^n]/t\,dt=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_0^1(1-t)^jdt=\sum_{k=1}^n1/k$, the <harmonic number> $H_n$.
