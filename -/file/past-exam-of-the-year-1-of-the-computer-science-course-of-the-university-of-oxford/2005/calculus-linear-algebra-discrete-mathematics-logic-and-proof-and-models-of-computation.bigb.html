== 1

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <image of a linear map> and <kernel of a linear map> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}\subseteq W,\qquad\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}\subseteq V.}
$$
A <linear map> sends zero to zero, since $T(0)=T(0+0)=T(0)+T(0)$. Thus both displayed <sets> contain zero. If $w_1=T(v_1)$ and $w_2=T(v_2)$ belong to the <image of a linear map>, then for any real <scalars> $a,b$,
$$
aw_1+bw_2=T(av_1+bv_2)\in\operatorname{im}T.
$$
If $v_1,v_2$ belong to the <kernel of a linear map>, then
$$
T(av_1+bv_2)=aT(v_1)+bT(v_2)=0,
$$
so $av_1+bv_2\in\ker T$. Containing zero and being closed under <linear combinations> is the <vector subspace> criterion. Hence the <image of a linear map> is a <vector subspace> of $W$, and the <kernel of a linear map> is a <vector subspace> of $V$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <rank-nullity theorem> says that for a <linear map> $T:V\to W$ with finite-dimensional <domain of a function> $V$,
$$
\boxed{\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T=\operatorname{nullity}T+\operatorname{rank}T.}
$$
Take a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_r$ of the <kernel of a linear map> and use <basis extension> to obtain a <basis> $k_1,\ldots,k_r,u_1,\ldots,u_s$ of $V$. We show that $T(u_1),\ldots,T(u_s)$ is a <basis> of the <image of a linear map>.

Every $v\in V$ is a <linear combination> of the extended <basis>, and its $k_i$ terms vanish under $T$. Therefore $T(v)$ is a <linear combination> of the $T(u_j)$, proving the required <linear span> property. For <linear independence>, suppose $\sum_j b_jT(u_j)=0$. Then $\sum_j b_ju_j\in\ker T$, so $\sum_j b_ju_j=\sum_i a_ik_i$ for some real <scalars> $a_i$. The extended <basis> is <linearly independent>, forcing every $a_i$ and $b_j$ to be zero. Thus the <image of a linear map> has <dimension> $s$, the <kernel of a linear map> has <dimension> $r$, and $\dim V=r+s$ proves the result.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <basis> provides unique coordinates. Define
$$
\boxed{\alpha(a_1e_1+a_2e_2+a_3e_3)=a_1v_1+a_2v_2+a_3v_3.}
$$
The uniqueness of the coordinates makes this a well-defined <function>. Adding coordinates and multiplying them by a real <scalar> shows directly that $\alpha(au+bw)=a\alpha(u)+b\alpha(w)$, so it is a <linear map>. Substituting $e_i$ gives $\alpha(e_i)=v_i$.

Conversely, every <linear map> with those three values must send $\sum_i a_ie_i$ to $\sum_i a_iv_i$, by preservation of <linear combinations>. This determines its value on every element of $V$, proving uniqueness. No <linear independence> of $v_1,v_2,v_3$ is needed.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Here $v_3=2v_1-v_2$, so the <image of a linear map> is the <linear span> of $v_1,v_2$. These two <vectors> are <linearly independent>: if $av_1+bv_2=0$, the $e_1$ and $e_2$ coordinates give $a+b=0$ and $a-b=0$, hence $a=b=0$. Consequently the <rank of a linear map> is two. By the <rank-nullity theorem>, the <nullity> is $3-2=1$:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}\alpha=2,\qquad\operatorname{nullity}\alpha=1.}
$$
More explicitly, the relation $-2v_1+v_2+v_3=0$ gives the <kernel of a linear map>
$$
\ker\alpha=\operatorname{span}\{-2e_1+e_2+e_3\}.
$$

==== Solution

The printed word “eigenvalues” must read “eigenvectors”: <eigenvalues> are real numbers, whereas a <basis> of $\mathbb R^n$ consists of <vectors>. The intended assertion is
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is diagonalizable over }\mathbb R\iff\mathbb R^n\text{ has a basis of eigenvectors of }A.}
$$
This wording error is present in the original scan.<Calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2005)>[Printed p. 2, Q2(a), “basis consisting of eigenvalues”]{ref}

If $X^{-1}AX=D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$ for an <invertible matrix> $X$, write $x_i$ for its columns. The <matrix> identity $AX=XD$ says $Ax_i=\lambda_ix_i$. The columns of an <invertible matrix> are a <basis>, hence they give the required <basis> of <eigenvectors>.

Conversely, put a <basis> of real <eigenvectors> $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ into the columns of $X$, with corresponding <eigenvalues> $\lambda_i$. The <basis> property makes $X$ an <invertible matrix>. The equations $Ax_i=\lambda_ix_i$ give $AX=XD$, so $X^{-1}AX=D$. This is precisely <diagonalizability> over the real numbers.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the first <matrix>, the <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(tI-A)=(t-3)\big((t-2)(t-1)-6\big)=(t-3)(t-4)(t+1).
$$
Thus the real <eigenvalues> are $4,-1,3$. Corresponding <eigenvectors> are $(3,1,0)^\mathsf T$, $(-2,1,0)^\mathsf T$ and $(0,0,1)^\mathsf T$. They are <linearly independent>; the <determinant> of the <matrix> with these columns is $5$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad X^{-1}AX=\operatorname{diag}(4,-1,3).}
$$
For example, multiplying the first column by $A$ gives $(12,4,0)^\mathsf T$, four times that column; multiplying the second gives $(2,-1,0)^\mathsf T$, its negative. These also check the chosen <eigenvectors> directly.

For the second <matrix>,
$$
\det(tI-A)=t^3-1=(t-1)(t^2+t+1).
$$
The quadratic factor has negative <discriminant> $-3$, so $1$ is the only real <eigenvalue>. Solving $Av=v$ gives $v=(a,a,a)^\mathsf T$, a one-dimensional <eigenspace>. It cannot provide a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$, so
$$
\boxed{\text{the only real eigenvalue is }1;\quad A\text{ is not diagonalizable over }\mathbb R.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\lambda$ be the single <eigenvalue>. By <diagonalizability>, there is a <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of <eigenvectors>, and every corresponding <eigenvalue> is $\lambda$. For any $v=\sum_i a_iv_i$, <linearity> gives
$$
T(v)=\sum_i a_iT(v_i)=\sum_i a_i\lambda v_i=\lambda v.
$$
Thus $\boxed{T=\lambda I}$, a <scalar multiple> of the <identity map>.

A counterexample to the conclusion without <diagonalizability> is
$$
\boxed{J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is $(t-2)^2$, but $(J-2I)(a,b)^\mathsf T=(b,0)^\mathsf T$. The <eigenspace> for $2$ is therefore $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)^\mathsf T\}$, of <dimension> one. There is no <basis> of <eigenvectors> in <dimension> two, so $J$ is not <diagonalizable>.

===== Solution

The <homogeneous linear differential equation> has solutions $\cos x$ and $\sin x$. Since the forcing is already a homogeneous solution, seek a <particular solution> proportional to $x\cos x$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\left(-\tfrac12x\cos x\right)''-\tfrac12x\cos x=\sin x.
$$
Thus the general solution of the <linear ordinary differential equation> is
$$
y=A\cos x+B\sin x-\tfrac12x\cos x.
$$
The first <initial condition> gives $A=0$. Also
$$
y'=-A\sin x+B\cos x-\tfrac12\cos x+\tfrac12x\sin x,
$$
so the second <initial condition> gives $B-\tfrac12=-\tfrac12$, hence $B=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-\tfrac12x\cos x.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $y_1=e^x/x$, on an <interval> avoiding zero,
$$
y_1'=e^x\left(\frac1x-\frac1{x^2}\right),\qquad y_1''=e^x\left(\frac1x-\frac2{x^2}+\frac2{x^3}\right).
$$
Substitution into the <ordinary differential equation> gives
$$
xy_1''+2y_1'-xy_1=e^x\left(1-\frac2x+\frac2{x^2}+\frac2x-\frac2{x^2}-1\right)=0.
$$
To find an independent solution, put $v=xy$. The <product rule> gives $v''=xy''+2y'$, so the <ordinary differential equation> becomes $v''-v=0$. Its independent solutions are $e^x$ and $e^{-x}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y_2(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x,\qquad y(x)=\frac{Ae^x+Be^{-x}}x\quad(x\ne0).}
$$
The two displayed solutions are <linearly independent>, since their ratio $e^{2x}$ is nonconstant on any open <interval>. Constants can be chosen separately on the two sides of the singular point $x=0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <logarithm> requires $y>0$, and $x>0$ is given. Set $z=\log(y/x)$, so $y=xe^z$ and the <chain rule> yields $xy'/y=1+xz'$. Dividing the <ordinary differential equation> by $y$ therefore gives
$$
1+xz'=1+z,\qquad xz'=z.
$$
Since $(z/x)'=(xz'-z)/x^2=0$, we obtain $z=Cx$, including the case $z=0$. Thus the general positive solution is $y=xe^{Cx}$. The <initial condition> $y(1)=e$ gives $C=1$:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=xe^x.}
$$
For the separate decay requirement, $xe^{Cx}\to0$ precisely when $C<0$: if $C=0$, the result is $x$, and if $C>0$, it grows. Consequently the full family with the stated <limit> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=xe^{-ax},\qquad a>0,\quad x>0.}
$$
This latter family does not also satisfy $y(1)=e$; the solution selected by that <initial condition> grows. The identically zero <function> is excluded by the original <logarithm>.

==== i

===== Solution

For $r>0$, the <chain rule> gives $r_x=x/r$ and $r_y=y/r$. Applying it twice to the <radial function> $u=f(r)$ gives
$$
u_{xx}=f''\frac{x^2}{r^2}+f'\left(\frac1r-\frac{x^2}{r^3}\right),\qquad u_{yy}=f''\frac{y^2}{r^2}+f'\left(\frac1r-\frac{y^2}{r^3}\right).
$$
Using $x^2+y^2=r^2$, <Laplace's equation> becomes
$$
f''+\frac1rf'=0,\qquad (rf')'=0.
$$
Two <integrations> now give
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=A\log r+B,\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0.}
$$
These are the general solutions of the specified <radial function> form on an <annulus> or a punctured plane. If the solution must extend continuously to the origin, the <logarithm> term forces $A=0$, leaving only a constant <function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <product rule> for the <Laplace operator>:
$$
\Delta(xg(r))=x\Delta g(r)+2\partial_xg(r)=x\left(g''+\frac{g'}r\right)+2\frac{x}{r}g'=x\left(g''+\frac{3g'}r\right).
$$
Thus <Laplace's equation>, away from the origin, reduces to $g''+3g'/r=0$. On the line $x=0$ the displayed expression vanishes automatically; requiring the equation on an open region gives the same radial equation by continuity from $x\ne0$. Multiplying by $r^3$ gives $(r^3g')'=0$, so $g'=Cr^{-3}$ and $g=A+Br^{-2}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=Ax+B\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\qquad r>0.}
$$
If extension through the origin is required, $B=0$: along the horizontal axis the second term is $B/x$. The remaining <harmonic function> is $Ax$.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <wave equation> with wave speed one, <d'Alembert's formula> with arbitrary twice continuously differentiable initial displacement $h(x)=u(x,0)$ is
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{h(x+t)+h(x-t)}2+\frac12\int_{x-t}^{x+t}g(s)\,ds.
$$
The arbitrary <function> $h$ is essential: only the initial velocity is prescribed. For $g=0$, this gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{h(x+t)+h(x-t)}2.}
$$
Both travelling terms solve the <wave equation>, and their <time derivatives> cancel at $t=0$. Conversely every classical solution has this form for its initial displacement $h$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The velocity contribution in <d'Alembert's formula> is $\tfrac12\int_{x-t}^{x+t}1\,ds=t$. Hence, with arbitrary twice continuously differentiable $h$,
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{h(x+t)+h(x-t)}2+t.}
$$
The added term has both second <partial derivatives> zero and has <time derivative> one, so it supplies precisely the required <initial condition> while preserving the <wave equation>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Here the velocity contribution in <d'Alembert's formula> is
$$
\frac12\int_{x-t}^{x+t}e^s\,ds=\frac{e^{x+t}-e^{x-t}}2=e^x\sinh t.
$$
Thus, with arbitrary twice continuously differentiable $h$,
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac{h(x+t)+h(x-t)}2+e^x\sinh t.}
$$
For the added term, $u_{tt}=e^x\sinh t=u_{xx}$ and $u_t(x,0)=e^x\cosh0=e^x$. Its displacement at $t=0$ is zero, leaving $h$ free as required.

The <domain of a function> is fixed by its declared type, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{dom}f=(0,\infty).}
$$
Although the rational expression also has values at some negative arguments, they are not in the specified <domain of a function>. Part (a) of the printed question only supplies the definition used in these four subparts.<Calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2005)>[Printed p. 4, Q5(a)–(b)]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $r>0$, solve $f(s)=r$ with $s>0$. Multiplying by the positive denominator gives $s^2+s=1/r$, so the <quadratic formula> gives
$$
s=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4/r}}2.
$$
The minus sign gives a negative number and is inadmissible. The plus sign gives a positive number for every $r>0$, and it is the unique positive solution. Hence the <inverse function> is
$$
\boxed{g(r)=\frac{\sqrt{1+4/r}-1}{2},\qquad r>0.}
$$
Indeed $g(r)(g(r)+1)=1/r$, so $f(g(r))=r$. Conversely, for $s>0$, $\sqrt{1+4/f(s)}=\sqrt{(2s+1)^2}=2s+1$, giving $g(f(s))=s$. These verify both <inverse function> identities.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $s>0$, the denominator $s(s+1)$ is positive, so every value of $f$ is positive. Conversely, for every $r>0$, the positive number $s=(\sqrt{1+4/r}-1)/2$ satisfies $f(s)=r$. Thus its <image of a function>, or range, is
$$
\boxed{f((0,\infty))=(0,\infty).}
$$
One can also see the full range from <continuity> and <monotonicity>: $f$ is a <continuous function> with $f'(s)=-(2s+1)/(s^2+s)^2<0$, and its endpoint <limits> are $+\infty$ as $s\to0^+$ and $0$ as $s\to\infty$. The endpoints themselves are not attained.

===== Solution

The <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
f(i)=\frac1{i(i+1)}=\frac1i-\frac1{i+1}
$$
makes the sum a <telescoping sum>. All interior terms cancel:
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^nf(i)=\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)=1-\frac1{n+1}=\frac n{n+1}.}
$$

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <two-chain noncontinuous complete partial order>
$$
\boxed{P=\{\bot,\top\}\cup\{a_n:n\geq1\}\cup\{b_n:n\geq1\},}
$$
where $\bot<a_1<a_2<\cdots<\top$ and $\bot<b_1<b_2<\cdots<\top$, with no comparisons between the two interiors. In this solution $\sup D$ denotes the <least upper bound> printed as $\sqcap D$ in the question.<Calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2005)>[Printed p. 4, Q6, definitions preceding (a)]{ref}

Every nonempty <directed set> containing $\top$ has <least upper bound> $\top$. A <directed set> omitting $\top$ cannot contain both an $a_i$ and a $b_j$, because their only common <upper bound> in $P$ is $\top$, which would have to belong to that <directed set>. It therefore lies in one <chain>, possibly with $\bot$. If its indices are bounded it has a greatest element, which is its <least upper bound>; if they are unbounded its <least upper bound> is $\top$. Thus $P$ is a <directed-complete partial order>.

No element other than $\bot$ is <way-below> $\top$. For $a_i$, the <directed set> $\{b_n:n\geq1\}$ has <least upper bound> $\top$, but none of its members is above $a_i$. Interchanging the two <chains> excludes every $b_i$. Either <chain> excludes $\top$ itself. On the other hand, $\bot\ll\top$: every <directed set> whose <least upper bound> is above $\top$ is nonempty, and any member is above $\bot$. Hence
$$
\{y:y\ll\top\}=\{\bot\},\qquad\sup\{y:y\ll\top\}=\bot\ne\top.
$$
So $P$ is **not a <continuous directed-complete partial order>**.

There is a small convention issue in the printed definitions. Our usual <directed set> is nonempty, but the question's pairwise condition also allows the empty <set>. Taken literally, the empty <set> has <least upper bound> $\bot$, and the preceding example and failure of continuity still work. Under this literal convention $\bot\not\ll\bot$, because the empty <set> supplies no witness. We retain this qualification in the next parts.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <continuous nonalgebraic unit interval> is
$$
\boxed{P=[0,1]\text{ with its usual order}.}
$$
Every nonempty <directed set> has a <least upper bound> by completeness of the real numbers, and that <least upper bound> lies in $[0,1]$. Thus this is a <directed-complete partial order>.

For $0\leq a<b\leq1$, we have $a\ll b$. Indeed, if $b\leq\sup D$ and no $d\in D$ were at least $a$, then $a$ would be an <upper bound> for $D$, implying $\sup D\leq a<b$, a contradiction. If $a>b$, the singleton <directed set> $\{b\}$ excludes $a\ll b$. If $a=b>0$, the increasing <sequence> $d_n=b(1-1/n)$, $n\geq1$, has <least upper bound> $b$ and has no member at least $b$, so $b\not\ll b$.

Under the usual nonempty convention, $0\ll0$. Therefore the elements <way-below> $b>0$ form $[0,b)$, and those <way-below> $0$ form $\{0\}$. These are <directed sets> with <least upper bounds> $b$ and $0$, respectively, proving that $[0,1]$ is a <continuous directed-complete partial order>. Its only <compact element> is $0$. For any $b>0$, the <compact elements> below $b$ have <least upper bound> $0$, rather than $b$, so it is not an <algebraic partially ordered set>.

Under the literal empty-set convention of the paper, $0\not\ll0$ as well. The elements <way-below> zero then form the empty <set>, which is directed in that convention and has <least upper bound> zero. There are no <compact elements>, and their empty <set> still fails to approximate any positive $b$. Thus both conventions give the same requested classification:
$$
\boxed{[0,1]\text{ is continuous but not algebraic}.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

We prove the result <algebraic partially ordered sets are continuous> directly from the definitions. First, $a\ll b$ implies $a\leq b$, by applying the <way-below relation> to the singleton <directed set> $\{b\}$. Also,
$$
a\ll b\leq c\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a\ll c:
$$
if $c\leq\sup D$, then $b\leq\sup D$, so the witness required for $a\ll b$ also works for $a\ll c$.

Fix $x$ and put
$$
K_x=\{k:k\leq x,\ k\ll k\},\qquad A_x=\{a:a\ll x\}.
$$
By algebraicity, $K_x$ is a <directed set> with <least upper bound> $x$. Every $k\in K_x$ satisfies $k\ll k\leq x$, hence $k\ll x$. Thus $K_x\subseteq A_x\subseteq\{a:a\leq x\}$.

To prove that $A_x$ is a <directed set>, take $a,b\in A_x$. Applying $a\ll x$ and $b\ll x$ to $x=\sup K_x$ gives $k_a,k_b\in K_x$ such that $a\leq k_a$ and $b\leq k_b$. Directedness of $K_x$ gives $k\in K_x$ above both. Since $k\in A_x$, it is the required common <upper bound> inside $A_x$. With the usual nonempty convention, $A_x$ is nonempty since it contains $K_x$.

By directed completeness $A_x$ has a <least upper bound>. The inclusions above give
$$
x=\sup K_x\leq\sup A_x\leq x,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{A_x\text{ is directed and }x=\sup A_x;\text{ hence the order is continuous}.}
$$
The same proof handles the paper's literal empty-set convention. If $K_x=\varnothing$, then $x=\sup\varnothing=\bot$, and the defining implication for $a\ll x$, applied to $D=K_x$, shows that $A_x$ must also be empty. Its <least upper bound> is again $x$. If $K_x$ is nonempty, the argument already given applies without change.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In <first-order logic> with equality, the three <first-order sentences> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{reflexivity: }&\forall x\,R(x,x),\\
\text{antisymmetry: }&\forall x\forall y\,((R(x,y)\land R(y,x))\to x=y),\\
\text{transitivity: }&\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((R(x,y)\land R(y,z))\to R(x,z)).
\end{aligned}}
$$
A <reflexive relation> relates each element to itself. An <antisymmetric relation> forbids distinct elements from relating in both directions. A <transitive relation> contains every composite of two successive related pairs. Together these axioms define a <partially ordered set>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The requested examples are <binary relations> on <sets>, rather than <partially ordered sets>: a <partially ordered set> necessarily has all three properties. This corrects the wording of the source.<Calculus, linear algebra, discrete mathematics, logic and proof, and models of computation examination paper by the University of Oxford (2005)>[Printed p. 5, Q7(b), requesting a “partially ordered set” without the third axiom]{ref}

For an <antisymmetric relation> and <transitive relation> that is not a <reflexive relation>, take $S=\{0,1\}$ and $R=\{(0,1)\}$. No distinct pair relates both ways, so the <antisymmetric relation> condition holds. There are no composable pairs, so the <transitive relation> condition holds vacuously. Neither $(0,0)$ nor $(1,1)$ belongs to $R$, so the <reflexive relation> condition fails.

For a <reflexive relation> and <transitive relation> that is not an <antisymmetric relation>, take $S=\{0,1\}$ and $R=S\times S$. All diagonal pairs and all composite pairs belong to $R$, but $R(0,1)$ and $R(1,0)$ both hold with $0\ne1$.

For a <reflexive relation> and <antisymmetric relation> that is not a <transitive relation>, take
$$
S=\{0,1,2\},\qquad R=\{(0,0),(1,1),(2,2),(0,1),(1,2)\}.
$$
All diagonal pairs are present, and no off-diagonal pair has its reverse present. However $R(0,1)$ and $R(1,2)$ hold while $R(0,2)$ fails. Thus each pair of axioms can hold without the third.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Totality of the <binary relation> is expressed by
$$
\boxed{\tau=\forall x\forall y\,(R(x,y)\lor R(y,x)).}
$$
When $R$ is already an order, this adds comparability and makes it a <total order>. In fact this <first-order sentence> alone entails $\rho=\forall xR(x,x)$.

By the definition of <semantic entailment>, $\tau\models\rho$ means that every <first-order structure> satisfying $\tau$ also satisfies $\rho$. Let $\mathcal M$ be any such <first-order structure>, and let $a$ be any element of its <domain of a first-order structure>. The instance of $\tau$ with $x=y=a$ gives $R(a,a)\lor R(a,a)$, which is true only if $R(a,a)$ is true. Since $a$ was arbitrary, $\mathcal M\models\forall xR(x,x)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\tau\models\rho.}
$$
There is no exception for $x=y$ in the displayed totality axiom; that case is exactly what forces the <reflexive relation> property.

{scope}

==== Solution

In this <first-order structure>, $R(x,z)\land R(y,z)$ says $x\leq z$ and $y\leq z$. Thus $z$ is an <upper bound> of $x,y$. The universally quantified clause says that if $w$ is any other <upper bound>, then $z\leq w$. Therefore $z$ is their <least upper bound>. In the usual <total order> on the <natural numbers>, this is their <maximum>:
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y,z)\iff z=\max\{x,y\}.}
$$
This describes a condition on the values assigned to the free variables $x,y,z$; $\phi$ itself is not a closed <first-order sentence>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Every pair of <natural numbers> has a <maximum>, itself a <natural number>. Given arbitrary $x,y$, choose $z=\max\{x,y\}$. Then $x,y\leq z$, and any $w$ satisfying $x,y\leq w$ also satisfies $z\leq w$. All three clauses of $\phi$ hold. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf N\models\psi.}
$$
This explicitly supplies the witness required by the <existential quantifier> for every pair chosen by the two <universal quantifiers>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Here the direction of the <divisibility> relation matters: $R(x,z)$ means $z\mid x$. The first two clauses say that $z$ is a common <divisor> of $x,y$. The last clause says that every common <divisor> $w$ divides $z$. These are precisely the defining properties of the <greatest common divisor>. Choose $z=\gcd(x,y)$; then
$$
z\mid x,\quad z\mid y,\qquad (w\mid x\land w\mid y)\Longrightarrow w\mid z.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf N'\models\psi.}
$$
This also holds if the <natural numbers> include zero: use $\gcd(x,0)=x$ and $\gcd(0,0)=0$. Every <natural number> divides zero, so for $x=y=z=0$ the last clause remains true. In the order obtained by reversing <divisibility>, the <greatest common divisor> is the <least upper bound>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The first two clauses now require $x=2z$ and $y=2z$. Thus they already imply $x=y$. Choose, for example, $x=1,y=2$; no <natural number> $z$ can satisfy both equations. Consequently the <existential quantifier> fails for this pair, irrespective of the universally quantified final clause, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf N''\not\models\psi.}
$$
In fact even $x=y=1$ fails, since $1=2z$ has no solution among the <natural numbers>.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <regular language> over a finite <alphabet> is a <formal language> recognized by a <deterministic finite automaton>; equivalently it is described by a <regular expression>. A <nondeterministic finite automaton> accepts an input <string> if at least one permitted computation consumes that <string> and reaches an accepting state. The connection is the equivalence
$$
\boxed{L\text{ is regular}\iff L\text{ is recognized by some nondeterministic finite automaton}.}
$$
Every <deterministic finite automaton> is a special case of a <nondeterministic finite automaton>. Conversely, the <subset construction> makes a <deterministic finite automaton> whose states record the <set> of all possible current states of a <nondeterministic finite automaton>. After each input symbol, update that <set> by taking all permitted transitions, also including any <epsilon closure>. A new state is accepting exactly when it contains an old accepting state. A finite state <set> has finitely many <subsets>, so this really is a <deterministic finite automaton> recognizing the same <formal language>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Every finite <formal language> is a <regular language>. A single <string> $a_1\cdots a_k$ is described by the <regular expression> formed by concatenating its symbols; the empty <string> is described by $\varepsilon$. A finite <union> of these <regular expressions> describes any specified finite collection of <strings>. The empty collection is described by $\varnothing$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{every finite subset of }\{0,1\}^*\text{ is regular}.}
$$
Equivalently, a <deterministic finite automaton> can remember which <prefix> of one of the finitely many accepted <strings> has been read, moving to a rejecting dead state when no such <prefix> remains possible.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

These <strings> are the even-length <palindromes> $uu^R$. Suppose this were a <regular language>, and let $p$ be its length from the <pumping lemma for regular languages>. The <string>
$$
w=0^p110^p
$$
belongs to the <formal language>, with $u=0^p1$. In any allowed decomposition $w=abc$, where $|ab|\leq p$ and $|b|>0$, the pumped block is $b=0^k$ with $1\leq k\leq p$. Pumping it away gives
$$
ac=0^{p-k}110^p.
$$
This is not a <palindrome>: its initial and final runs of zeros have different lengths. Hence it cannot have the form $uu^R$, contradicting the <pumping lemma for regular languages>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{uu^R:u\in\{0,1\}^*\}\text{ is not regular}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

This is a <regular language>. A <deterministic finite automaton> needs only to remember the number of consecutive trailing ones, up to three, and whether a forbidden run of four has already occurred. Use states $q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,q_\times$, start at $q_0$, and accept in $q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3$. Its <transition function of a finite automaton> is:

|| State
|| On $0$
|| On $1$

| $q_0$
| $q_0$
| $q_1$

| $q_1$
| $q_0$
| $q_2$

| $q_2$
| $q_0$
| $q_3$

| $q_3$
| $q_0$
| $q_\times$

| $q_\times$
| $q_\times$
| $q_\times$

Reading a zero resets the trailing count unless rejection has already occurred. A fourth successive one enters the permanent rejecting state $q_\times$. Thus the accepted <strings> are exactly those with no substring $1111$, and
$$
\boxed{\text{the bounded-run language is regular}.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $L=\{0^m1^n:m,n\geq0,\ m\ne n\}$, and suppose it were a <regular language>. The <formal language> $B=0^*1^*$ is a <regular language>. By closure of <regular languages> under <set difference>,
$$
B\setminus L=\{0^n1^n:n\geq0\}
$$
would be a <regular language>. But the <pumping lemma for regular languages> rules this out: for pumping length $p$, every permitted pumped block in $0^p1^p$ consists of $k>0$ zeros, and deleting it produces $0^{p-k}1^p$, with unequal counts. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{\{0^m1^n:m\ne n\}\text{ is not regular}.}
$$
The closure step can also be realized by a <product automaton> accepting when the automaton for $B$ accepts and the automaton for $L$ rejects. If the exponents are instead required to be positive, use $B=0^+1^+$; the same argument applies.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <context-free grammar> is a quadruple $(N,\Sigma,P,S)$: $N$ is a finite <set> of nonterminal symbols, $\Sigma$ is a disjoint finite <alphabet> of terminal symbols, $S\in N$ is the start symbol, and $P$ is a finite <set> of productions $A\to\alpha$, with $A\in N$ and $\alpha\in(N\cup\Sigma)^*$. Its <formal language> consists of the terminal <strings> derivable from $S$.

For the even-length <palindromes> in (b)(ii), take terminal <alphabet> $\{0,1\}$ and the <context-free grammar>
$$
\boxed{S\to0S0\mid1S1\mid\varepsilon.}
$$
Every production adds identical symbols at the two ends, so every generated <string> is an even-length <palindrome>. Conversely, remove the matching outer pair of any nonempty even-length <palindrome> and repeat; this constructs its derivation, ending with $\varepsilon$.

For the unequal-block <formal language> in (b)(iv), use
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
S&\to A\mid B,\\
A&\to0A1\mid Z,& Z&\to0Z\mid0,\\
B&\to0B1\mid O,& O&\to1O\mid1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
After $k$ uses of $A\to0A1$, and $j\geq1$ zeros from $Z$, the resulting <string> is $0^{k+j}1^k$, so its zero count is larger. Every such unequal pair with $m>n$ occurs by choosing $k=n,j=m-n$. Similarly $B$ generates exactly $0^k1^{k+j}$ with $j\geq1$, covering precisely $m<n$. The start alternatives cover both possibilities and never equal counts. If positive exponents are required, replace the start productions by $S\to0A1\mid0B1$, which adds one matched pair and ensures both counts are positive.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Turing machine> has a finite control, an unbounded tape divided into cells over a finite tape <alphabet> including a blank symbol, and a head that reads one cell at a time. For a deterministic <Turing machine>, the current state and scanned symbol determine the replacement symbol, a head move one cell left or right, and the next state. The input occupies finitely many cells initially; all other cells are blank. Distinguished halting states report acceptance or rejection.

A <formal language> is a <decidable language> if some <Turing machine> halts on every input <string>, accepting exactly the members and rejecting all nonmembers:
$$
\boxed{L\text{ is decidable}\iff\exists M\ \forall w,\ M(w)\text{ halts and accepts exactly when }w\in L.}
$$
Halting on the nonmembers is part of the requirement; a machine that merely runs forever on some nonmembers need not decide the <formal language>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Cancel one unary symbol from each block at each round. Mark the first, middle and last delimiters by $L,C,E$, respectively; use $X$ for a cancelled symbol in the left block and $Y$ for one in the right block. These are extra tape symbols of the <Turing machine>, all distinct from $0,1$ and blank. No numerical counter is needed in the finite control.

Starting on the first zero, replace it by $L$, scan right through the first block of ones to replace the next zero by $C$, then scan right through the second block and replace its terminal zero by $E$. Move left to $C$. If the initial head is on a blank immediately before the first zero, first move right to that zero. The cancellation loop always starts with the head at $C$:

* Move right past the $Y$ symbols. If the next symbol is $E$, all right-hand ones have been cancelled; enter the successful cleanup below.
* Otherwise that symbol is $1$. Replace it by $Y$, move left past right-block symbols to $C$, and continue left past any $X$ symbols.
* If the next symbol is $1$, replace it by $X$. Move right past the $X$ symbols back to $C$ and repeat the loop.
* If instead the next symbol is $L$, the left block is exhausted while the right block still needed a cancellation. Enter the zero-result cleanup below.

After $k$ completed rounds, the invariant is
$$
L1^{m-k}X^kCY^k1^{n-k}E,\qquad0\leq k\leq\min\{m,n\}.
$$
It holds initially with $k=0$, and each completed round changes exactly one remaining $1$ in each block into the corresponding marker. This proves the cancellation count and preserves the locations of all delimiters.

For successful cleanup, the right block is entirely $Y$ symbols. From $C$, sweep right, erasing all $Y$ symbols and finally $E$, then move left across the erased blanks back to the still-present $C$. Erase $C$ and continue left, erasing each $X$, until reaching either the last surviving $1$ or $L$. Move one cell right and write the new terminal zero there. Move left through the surviving ones to $L$, replace $L$ by $0$, and halt. The remaining nonblank tape is exactly
$$
\boxed{01^{m-n}0\quad\text{if }m>n.}
$$
For example, $0111110110$ represents $m=5,n=2$ and becomes $01110$, representing three.

For zero-result cleanup, the head has reached $L$. Sweep right, erasing everything through $E$ while retaining $L$; after erasing $E$, move left across the blanks to $L$. Replace $L$ by $0$, write a second zero in the next cell, and halt. Thus if $m<n$ the machine returns $00$. If $m=n$, successful cleanup also leaves $00$. With this explicit choice of behaviour,
$$
\boxed{\text{the machine always halts and returns }01^{\max\{m-n,0\}}0.}
$$
Each sweep seeks a retained delimiter or an end marker a finite distance away. Each completed round removes one right-block $1$; after at most $n$ completed rounds either cleanup or left-block exhaustion occurs. The stated scans, writes and single-cell moves are implementable by finitely many states, so this is an implementation-level <Turing machine> construction. The output $00$ represents zero; a negative integer cannot be represented in the unsigned <unary numeral system> specified here.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <halting problem> asks, given the encoding $\langle M,w\rangle$ of a <Turing machine> and an input <string>, whether that machine eventually halts on that input. Its <formal language> is
$$
\operatorname{HALT}=\{\langle M,w\rangle:M\text{ halts on }w\}.
$$
Suppose a <Turing machine> $H$ decided this <formal language>. Construct another <Turing machine> $D$ that, on an input encoding $\langle M\rangle$, runs $H$ on $\langle M,\langle M\rangle\rangle$. If $H$ answers that $M$ halts on its own encoding, $D$ enters an infinite loop. If $H$ answers that $M$ does not halt, $D$ halts. Handling malformed encodings by immediate rejection makes $D$ well-defined on arbitrary <strings>.

Now run $D$ on its own encoding. If $H$ says that $D$ halts, the construction makes $D$ loop; if $H$ says that $D$ does not halt, the construction makes $D$ halt. Either answer contradicts the assumed correctness of $H$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{HALT}\text{ is not a decidable language}.}
$$
The contradiction concerns a decider that must give a correct terminating answer for every encoded machine and input. Simulating $M$ does detect a halt when one occurs, but cannot in general certify that a running computation will continue forever.
