==== Solution

We prove both <set inclusions>. If $x\in A\setminus(A\setminus B)$, then $x\in A$ and $x\notin A\setminus B$. Since $x\in A$, the latter condition forces $x\in B$, giving $x\in A\cap B$. Conversely, if $x\in A\cap B$, then $x\in A$ and $x\notin A\setminus B$, so $x\in A\setminus(A\setminus B)$. By <set extensionality>, the two <sets> are equal. This is a <double inclusion proof>; the implication involving the <set difference> uses membership in $A$ explicitly.

**True.** The two operations retain complementary membership information. For $x\in C$, equality of the <intersections> makes $x\in A$ equivalent to $x\in B$. For $x\notin C$, equality of the <unions> makes $x\in A$ equivalent to $x\in B$. These cases exhaust every possible element. <Set extensionality> therefore gives $A=B$. Equivalently, reconstruct either <set> from
$$
A=(A\cap C)\cup\bigl((A\cup C)\setminus C\bigr).
$$
Substituting the two given equalities into this expression gives $B$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $x_i$ be the number in box $i$ and put $y_i=x_i-1$. We count <integer compositions> satisfying $y_1+\cdots+y_4=6$ and $0\leq y_i\leq4$. Without the upper bounds, <stars and bars> gives $\binom93=84$. If a specified $y_i\geq5$, subtract five from it; the remaining <nonnegative integer> variables sum to one, giving $\binom43=4$ possibilities. Two violations cannot occur because they would require a sum at least ten. The <inclusion-exclusion principle> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\binom93-4\binom43=84-16=68.}
$$
The boxes are distinguished by their positions in the composition; individual objects are not distinguished.

==== Solution

Use <modular exponentiation> by <repeated squaring>:
$$
27\equiv6\pmod7,\quad27^2\equiv6^2\equiv1\pmod7,\quad27^4\equiv27^8\equiv27^{16}\equiv1\pmod7.

Since $17=16+1$, multiply the indicated powers to obtain **$27^{17}\equiv6\pmod7$**. Only the <binary expansion> of the exponent determines which squared powers are multiplied.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the representative $1\leq a\leq p-1$. Since $p$ is <prime> and does not divide $a$, their <greatest common divisor> is one. The <extended Euclidean algorithm> gives the <Bézout identity>
$$
ax+py=1
$$
for some <integers> $x,y$. Reducing modulo $p$ gives $ax\equiv1\pmod p$, so **the residue class of $x$ is a <multiplicative inverse> of $a$**. The argument uses the nonzero condition to exclude the only residue with <greatest common divisor> $p$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First prove $a^p\equiv a\pmod p$ for every <nonnegative integer> $a$ by <mathematical induction>. The case $a=0$ is immediate. If it holds at $a$, the <binomial theorem> and the stated divisibility of the middle <binomial coefficients> give
$$
(a+1)^p\equiv a^p+1\equiv a+1\pmod p.
$$
This establishes the induction. For an arbitrary <integer> $a$, take a nonnegative representative $r$ of its <residue class>; then $a^p\equiv r^p\equiv r\equiv a\pmod p$. When $a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, part (b) supplies a <multiplicative inverse> modulo $p$. Multiplying the congruence by that inverse proves <Fermat's little theorem>:
$$
\boxed{a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Absorb the finitely many exceptional initial terms in the <Big O notation> assumption into a constant $C>0$, so that $|a_n|\leq Cn$ for every $n\geq1$. This is possible because each initial term is finite and each index is positive. We prove the stronger bound $|{}^kb_n|\leq Cn$ for every $k,n\geq1$.

The case $k=1$ is the chosen bound. If it holds at $k-1$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
|{}^kb_n|\leq\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n|{}^{k-1}b_j|\leq\frac Cn\sum_{j=1}^n j=\frac{C(n+1)}2\leq Cn.
$$
<Mathematical induction> completes the proof. Thus **${}^kb_n=O(n)$ for every fixed $k$**; in fact the same constant works for all the iterated <Cesàro means>. This is the <linear-growth bound for iterated Cesàro means>.

==== Solution

For these real-valued <functions>, **$f(n)=O(g(n))$** means that there exist constants $C>0$ and $N\in\mathbb N$ such that
$$
|f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|\qquad\text{for every }n\geq N.

The absolute values matter because neither <function> is assumed nonnegative. The constants are independent of $n$; finitely many earlier values do not affect <Big O notation>.

{scope}

===== Solution

**True.** For every <integer> $n\geq1$, $n^{3/2}\leq n^2$ and $1\leq n^2$, so
$$
0\leq3n^2+5n^{3/2}+6\leq(3+5+6)n^2=14n^2.
$$
Thus $C=14$ and $N=1$ witness the required <Big O notation> bound.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**False.** Set $r_n=n!/2^n$. The ratio of successive terms is
$$
\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n}=\frac{n+1}{2}\geq2\qquad(n\geq3).
$$
Consequently $r_n\geq r_3\,2^{n-3}$ for $n\geq3$, which is unbounded. No constant can bound $n!$ by that constant times $2^n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. The <factorial> therefore fails the proposed <Big O notation> bound, without needing <Stirling's formula>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**True.** Every term has $k^2\leq n^2$ when $1\leq k\leq n$. Adding these $n$ inequalities gives
$$
0\leq\sum_{k=1}^n k^2\leq n\,n^2=n^3.
$$
The constants $C=1$ and $N=1$ suffice in the definition of <Big O notation>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**False.** The <sum of squares of the first n positive integers> gives
$$
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{k=1}^n k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\geq\frac n3.
$$
This ratio is unbounded, so it cannot satisfy the constant bound required by <Big O notation>. In fact the <sum> has order $n^3$, as its leading term also shows.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

An element $u\in A$ is a <least upper bound> of $S$ if it is an <upper bound>, meaning $s\leq u$ for every $s\in S$, and **$u\leq v$ for every <upper bound> $v\in A$ of $S$**. The candidate need not belong to $S$. Because a <partial order> is an <antisymmetric relation>, two <least upper bounds> must be equal. A <least upper bound> is also called the <supremum>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

An element $m$ is a <maximum> of $S$ if **$m\in S$ and $s\leq m$ for every $s\in S$**. Thus it is an <upper bound> that is itself a member. Every <maximum> is the <least upper bound>: any <upper bound> must lie above $m$ because $m\in S$. A <supremum> outside $S$ need not be a <maximum>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Under the <product order>, every point of the open <unit disc> has both coordinates below one, so $(1,1)$ is an <upper bound>. Any <upper bound> $(u,v)$ must have $u\geq1$ and $v\geq1$, since the two coordinate projections of the open <unit disc> are $(-1,1)$. Therefore $(1,1)\leq_P(u,v)$, proving **$\sup_{\leq_P}S=(1,1)$**.

Under the <lexicographic order>, every point $(1,v)$, with arbitrary real $v$, is an <upper bound>, because every point of $S$ has first coordinate strictly below one. Conversely, an <upper bound> cannot have first coordinate $u<1$: a point $(x,0)\in S$ with $x>u$ would exceed it. If an <upper bound> has $u>1$, the <upper bound> $(1,0)$ is strictly smaller. If it is $(1,v)$, the <upper bound> $(1,v-1)$ is strictly smaller. Hence **there is no <least upper bound> under the <lexicographic order>**. This illustrates the <supremum of an open disc under product and lexicographic orders>.

=== a

==== Solution

The <function> is even, so every sine coefficient is zero. Its mean coefficient is
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin x\,\mathrm dx=\frac4\pi.
$$
For $n\geq1$, compute $a_n=(2/\pi)\int_0^\pi\sin x\cos(nx)\,\mathrm dx$. The product-to-sum identity makes this integral zero for odd $n$ and gives $-4/(\pi(n^2-1))$ for even $n$. Thus the missing <Fourier coefficients> are **$a_n=0$ for odd $n$ and $b_n=0$ for every $n$**, with $a_0=4/\pi$. Equivalently,
$$
|\sin x|=\frac2\pi-\frac4\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(2mx)}{4m^2-1}.
$$
The <Fourier series> converges to this continuous periodic <function>; there is no jump value to average.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $x>0$, substitute $v=y/x$. Then $y'=v+xv'$, and cancellation reduces the <differential equation> to $v'+2v=e^{-2x}$. Multiplication by the <integrating factor> $e^{2x}$ gives $(e^{2x}v)'=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x(x+C)e^{-2x},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Direct substitution gives $y'+2y-y/x=xe^{-2x}$ and verifies the result. Dividing by $x$ is legitimate on the stated positive domain.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <integrating factor> is $e^{2x}$, so the <differential equation> becomes $(e^{2x}y)'=1$. Integration gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=(x+C)e^{-2x},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The factor $x$ in the <particular solution> accounts for the forcing term being proportional to the <homogeneous solution>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Here the <integrating factor> is $e^{2\sin x}$, since its <logarithmic derivative> is $2\cos x$. Multiplying the <differential equation> by it cancels the right-hand exponential and yields $(e^{2\sin x}y)'=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=(x+C)e^{-2\sin x},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This formula is actually defined for every real $x$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Seek a periodic <particular solution> with <Fourier coefficients> $A_n,B_n$. Comparing constant terms gives $A_0=a_0/2$. Comparing the cosine and sine terms for $n\geq1$ gives
$$
2A_n+nB_n=a_n,\qquad -nA_n+2B_n=0,
$$
so $A_n=2a_n/(n^2+4)$ and $B_n=na_n/(n^2+4)$. Insert the coefficients from part (a) and add the <homogeneous solution>:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=Ce^{-2x}+\frac1\pi-\frac2\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(2mx)+m\sin(2mx)}{(4m^2-1)(m^2+1)}.}
$$
The differentiated series converges absolutely and uniformly, because its largest coefficient terms are $O(m^{-2})$. This justifies the term-by-term first <derivative> used in substituting into the <differential equation>. Any two solutions differ by $Ce^{-2x}$, proving that the displayed family is the general solution. The computation is an instance of <Fourier response of a first-order linear differential equation>.

The coordinate sum is $2\sin t$, while the sum of their cubes is $6\sin t-4\sin^3t$. Along the <parametrized curve>, the composite <function> is therefore $-3\sin t/2-\sin^3t/3$. Differentiate, or apply the multivariable <chain rule>, to obtain

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply <Newton's method> to $F(t)=1-3\sin t/2-\sin^3t/3$. The derivative in part (a) gives
$$
\boxed{t_{k+1}=t_k+\frac{1-\frac32\sin t_k-\frac13\sin^3t_k}{\cos t_k\left(\frac32+\sin^2t_k\right)}.}
$$
Choose a starting point near a desired root, such as $t_0=0.6$ for the root in $(0,\pi/2)$; do not divide when $\cos t_k=0$. On $(0,\pi/2)$, $F$ is strictly decreasing and changes sign between its endpoints, so the <intermediate value theorem> gives exactly one root there. Its derivative is nonzero, giving the usual local convergence of <Newton's method>. The <periodic function> has other roots; the iteration does not impose global uniqueness or convergence from every starting value.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <gradient> vanishes exactly when $x^2/4-1=0$ and $y^2/4-1=0$, giving the four points $(\pm2,\pm2)$. The <Hessian matrix> is $\operatorname{diag}(x/2,y/2)$. It is positive definite at $(2,2)$, negative definite at $(-2,-2)$, and indefinite at the two mixed-sign points. Thus **$(2,2)$ is a strict local minimum, $(-2,-2)$ a strict local maximum, and $(2,-2),(-2,2)$ are <saddle points>** by the <second derivative test>. Their respective <function> values are $-8/3$, $8/3$, and zero at both <saddle points>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Substituting $y=-x$ cancels both the cubic and linear terms, leaving $f(x,-x)=0$ for every real $x$. Thus **the constrained maximum and minimum are both zero**, attained everywhere on the constraint line. The feasible <set> is unbounded, but the restricted <function> is constant; compactness is not needed here.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $f(x,y)=g(x)+g(y)$ with $g(s)=s^3/12-s$. On $[0,4]$, its <derivative> is $g'(s)=s^2/4-1$, negative below two and positive above two. Compare the critical and endpoint values:
$$
g(0)=0,\qquad g(2)=-\frac43,\qquad g(4)=\frac43.
$$
Each coordinate can be chosen independently. Therefore **the global minimum is $-8/3$ at $(2,2)$, and the global maximum is $8/3$ at $(4,4)$**. This comparison includes the boundary of the rectangle, rather than classifying only interior <stationary points>.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

The continuous <function> attains a global maximum and minimum on the closed disc by the <extreme value theorem>. All four unconstrained <stationary points> have squared distance eight, so none lies in its interior. The extrema must therefore lie on $x^2+y^2=6$.

The constraint <gradient> is nonzero on that circle, so <Lagrange multipliers> apply:
$$
\frac{x^2}4-1=2\lambda x,\qquad\frac{y^2}4-1=2\lambda y.
$$
Subtracting gives $(x-y)((x+y)/4-2\lambda)=0$. If $x=y$, the constraint gives $x=y=\pm\sqrt3$. Otherwise $2\lambda=(x+y)/4$; substitution into either equation gives $xy=-4$. This is impossible, since $|xy|\leq(x^2+y^2)/2=3$ on the circle. Thus the diagonal points are the only candidates. Comparing their values gives
$$
\boxed{\min f=-\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}\text{ at }(\sqrt3,\sqrt3),\qquad\max f=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}\text{ at }(-\sqrt3,-\sqrt3).}
$$

==== Solution

Repeated use of the <product rule> and <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=\frac{-2x}{(1+x^2)^2},\quad f''(x)=\frac{2(3x^2-1)}{(1+x^2)^3},\quad f'''(x)=\frac{24x(1-x^2)}{(1+x^2)^4}.}
$$
The denominators are strictly positive on the whole real line, making the signs in the next parts easy to read.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The critical points of $f''$ occur where $f'''=0$, namely $-1,0,1$. Its sign on the four intervals separated by these points is, in order, positive, negative, positive, negative. Thus $f''$ has local maxima at $\pm1$ and a local minimum at zero. Their values are
$$
f''(-1)=f''(1)=\frac12,\qquad f''(0)=-2.
$$
Since $f''(x)\to0$ at both ends of the real line, these are also **the global maximum $1/2$ and the global minimum $-2$**. The sign analysis establishes extremality; merely solving $f'''=0$ would not.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Taylor polynomial> uses $f(1)=1/2$, $f'(1)=-1/2$ and $f''(1)=1/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f_2(x)=\frac12-\frac12(x-1)+\frac14(x-1)^2=\frac{x^2-4x+5}{4}.}
$$
The quadratic coefficient is $f''(1)/2!$, not $f''(1)$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**The printed inequality has its sign reversed: $f(x)<f_2(x)$ for $x>1$.** This is present in the original scan, rather than arising in transcription. For $x>1$, <Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder> gives
$$
f(x)-f_2(x)=\frac{f'''(\xi)}{3!}(x-1)^3,\qquad1<\xi<x.
$$
Part (a) has $f'''(\xi)<0$, so the remainder is strictly negative. As an independent check, direct algebra gives the <quadratic Taylor majorant of a reciprocal quadratic>:
$$
\boxed{f_2(x)-f(x)=\frac{(x-1)^4}{4(1+x^2)}>0\quad(x>1).}
$$
For example, $f(2)=1/5<1/4=f_2(2)$ is already a counterexample to the requested direction. The exact identity also shows that this polynomial majorizes $f$ on the entire real line, with equality only at one.

With unit strip width, the <composite trapezoidal rule> gives

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

For $n$ equal strips and a uniform bound $|f''(x)|\leq M$ on $[a,b]$, the <composite trapezoidal error bound> is
$$
|I-T_n|\leq\frac{(b-a)^3M}{12n^2}.
$$
On $[1,25]$, part (b) gives $M=1/2$: $f''$ is positive and decreases from $1/2$ after $x=1$. Hence
$$
|I-T_n|\leq\frac{24^3}{24n^2}=\frac{576}{n^2}.
$$
To certify error at most $10^{-4}$, choose **$n\geq2400$**; equality gives exactly $10^{-4}$ in this bound. This is a sufficient certificate, not a claim that every smaller $n$ fails in actual numerical error.

Strictly, the maximum specified over the open interval $(1,25)$ is not attained; its <supremum> is $1/2$. Using the maximum on the closed interval, or simply the uniform bound $M=1/2$, gives the valid error estimate above.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <probability measure> $\mathbb P$ maps the <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal F$ of events into $[0,1]$. Its <probability axioms> are nonnegativity, normalization $\mathbb P(\Omega)=1$, and <countable additivity>: for every pairwise disjoint sequence of events $A_1,A_2,\ldots$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbb P(A_n).
$$
The <sample space> is $\Omega$, and $\mathcal F$ specifies which subsets are events. Requiring <countable additivity>, rather than only its finite version, is part of the <probability space> definition.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <probability axioms> first give $\mathbb P(\varnothing)=0$: apply <countable additivity> to $\Omega,\varnothing,\varnothing,\ldots$ and use normalization and nonnegativity. If $A\subseteq B$, then $B=A\mathbin{\dot\cup}(B\setminus A)$ is a disjoint <union> of events. Finite additivity, as a special case of <countable additivity>, gives
$$
\mathbb P(B)=\mathbb P(A)+\mathbb P(B\setminus A)\geq\mathbb P(A).
$$
Thus **a <probability measure> is monotone with respect to <set inclusion>**. The difference event is measurable because the events belong to a <sigma-algebra>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

By the even <probability density> of the <standard normal distribution>,
$$
\mathbb E|Z|=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^\infty z e^{-z^2/2}\,\mathrm dz=\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[-e^{-z^2/2}\right]_0^\infty=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac2\pi}}.
$$
The integral is finite. Equivalently, $|Z|$ has the <half-normal distribution> with unit scale.

{scope}

===== Solution

The all-red event $B$ is a <subset> of the same-colour event $A$. Its probability is $(1/2)^3=1/8$, while the three disjoint monochromatic cases give
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\left(\frac12\right)^3+\left(\frac13\right)^3+\left(\frac16\right)^3=\frac16.
$$
Thus the <conditional probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb P(B)}{\mathbb P(A)}=\frac34.}
$$
The monochromatic outcomes are not equally likely, so counting colours alone would give the wrong conditional answer.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $I_c$ be the <indicator random variable> that colour $c$ appears at least once. Then $X=I_R+I_B+I_G$. For a colour of single-roll probability $p_c$, independence of rolls gives $\mathbb P(I_c=0)=(1-p_c)^n$. By <linearity of expectation>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\sum_c\bigl(1-(1-p_c)^n\bigr)=3-\left(\frac12\right)^n-\left(\frac23\right)^n-\left(\frac56\right)^n.}
$$
The three indicators are not independent, but <linearity of expectation> does not require them to be. This is the <expected number of observed categories>.

==== i

===== Solution

For $x>1$, write the proposed <density> as $x^{-\alpha}(c-1/x)$. Since the first factor is positive, nonnegativity for all such $x$ requires $c\geq1$; if $c<1$, values sufficiently close to one make it negative. With $c\geq1$, the positive tail is integrable only if $\alpha>1$. In that case normalization requires
$$
\frac c{\alpha-1}-\frac1\alpha=1,\qquad c=\alpha-\frac1\alpha.
$$
Imposing $c\geq1$ now gives $\alpha^2-\alpha-1\geq0$. Combining this with $\alpha>1$ yields
$$
\boxed{\alpha\geq\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad c=\alpha-\frac1\alpha.}
$$
Conversely, these conditions ensure a nonnegative, integrable <function> with integral one, so they are sufficient as well as necessary. The endpoint is allowed: the <density> vanishes at the left-hand boundary limit, not on its whole support.

===== Solution

Normalization gives $c=8/3$. Integrating the <probability density> from one to two gives
$$
\mathbb P(X\leq2)=\frac83\int_1^2x^{-3}\,\mathrm dx-\int_1^2x^{-4}\,\mathrm dx=\frac83\frac38-\frac7{24}=\boxed{\frac{17}{24}}.
$$
The <expected value> exists, since the first-moment integrals converge, and equals
$$
\mathbb E X=\frac83\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,\mathrm dx-\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,\mathrm dx=\frac83-\frac12=\boxed{\frac{13}{6}}.
$$

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable>, its <probability generating function> is $G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k\geq0}\mathbb P(X=k)s^k$, at least for $|s|\leq1$. Insert the <Poisson distribution> masses and sum the <exponential series>:
$$
G_X(s)=e^{-\mu}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(\mu s)^k}{k!}=\boxed{e^{\mu(s-1)}}.
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Independence gives the <probability generating function> of the sum as the product:
$$
G_{X+Y}(s)=\mathbb E[s^Xs^Y]=G_X(s)G_Y(s)=e^{(\mu+\lambda)(s-1)}.
$$
This is the <probability generating function> of the <Poisson distribution> with parameter $\mu+\lambda$. Uniqueness of the coefficients of a <power series> therefore gives **$X+Y\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu+\lambda)$**. The product rule for these generating functions requires the stated independence.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Condition first on $X$. A <Poisson distribution> with parameter $k$ has conditional <probability generating function> $e^{k(s-1)}$, including the degenerate zero case when $k=0$. The <law of total expectation> gives
$$
G_Z(s)=\mathbb E\bigl[e^{X(s-1)}\bigr]=G_X(e^{s-1})=\boxed{\exp\!\left(\mu\bigl(e^{s-1}-1\bigr)\right)}.
$$
For $0\leq s\leq1$ the inner argument is in $(0,1]$, so this use of the generating function is justified directly. The result is a <compound Poisson distribution> with a <Poisson distribution> of unit parameter for each jump, also called a <Neyman Type A distribution> in this case. It is generally not an ordinary <Poisson distribution>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The conditional mean of a <Poisson distribution> equals its parameter, so $\mathbb E[Z\mid X]=X$. The <law of total expectation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E Z=\mathbb E X=\mu.}
$$
As a check, differentiating the <probability generating function> in part (iii) at one also gives $G'_Z(1)=\mu$.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $R$ count red balls and let $h_r$ be the probability of eventual absorption at $R=0$ starting from $R=r$. The <birth-death chain> moves to $r-1$ with probability $r/4$ and to $r+1$ with probability $(4-r)/4$. Its absorbing states have $h_0=1$, $h_4=0$. <First-step analysis> gives
$$
h_1=\frac14+\frac34h_2,\qquad h_2=\frac12h_1+\frac12h_3,\qquad h_3=\frac34h_2.
$$
Substitution yields $h_2=1/2$, $h_1=5/8$ and $h_3=3/8$. The initial state has one red ball, so **the probability that all balls end white is $5/8$**.

Absorption occurs almost surely: from each interior state there is probability at least $1/4$ of absorption within the next two draws. The chance of surviving $2m$ draws is therefore at most $(3/4)^m$. This also justifies the finite expected times used in part (ii). The process is the <absorbing four-ball Ehrenfest chain>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $t_r$ be the <expected hitting time> of either absorbing state. The boundary values are $t_0=t_4=0$. The colour symmetry gives $t_1=t_3$, and <first-step analysis> gives
$$
t_1=1+\frac34t_2,\qquad t_2=1+\frac12t_1+\frac12t_3=1+t_1.
$$
Solving gives $t_1=t_3=7$ and $t_2=8$. Thus **the expected total number of draws from the stated initial condition is seven**. The additive one counts the current draw. Finiteness follows from the uniform two-step absorption bound in part (i), so the first-step equations do not hide an assumption of finite expectation.
