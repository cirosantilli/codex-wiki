= Discrete mathematics, continuous mathematics and probability

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <relative complement> consists of the elements of the first set absent from the second, while the <symmetric difference> consists of elements in exactly one set:
$$
\boxed{A\setminus B=\{x:x\in A,\ x\notin B\},\qquad A\oplus B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A).}
$$
The two parts of the symmetric difference are <disjoint sets>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For the first <set inclusion>, take $x\in A\setminus(A\setminus B)$. Then $x\in A$ but $x\notin A\setminus B$. Since membership in $A$ is already known, failure to lie in that <relative complement> forces $x\in B$. Hence $x\in A\cap B$.

Conversely, if $x\in A\cap B$, then $x\in A$ and its membership in $B$ excludes it from $A\setminus B$. Therefore $x\in A\setminus(A\setminus B)$. The two inclusions prove the stated equality with the <set intersection>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $x\in A\oplus B$, the definition of <symmetric difference> puts it in exactly one of $A,B$. It is therefore in their <set union> and outside their <set intersection>, giving $x\in(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)$.

Conversely, an element of $(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)$ lies in at least one of the two sets but not both. It consequently belongs to $A\setminus B$ or to $B\setminus A$, and hence to $A\oplus B$. Both <set inclusions> hold.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The outermost braces form a <singleton set>, regardless of how its one element is nested. Its <cardinality> is therefore **$1$**. A set containing the <empty set> is different from the empty set itself.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <Cartesian product> with the <empty set> is empty: there is no possible second coordinate. Thus the requested <cardinality> is **$0$**, independently of the first factor's <power set>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <singleton set> $\{\varnothing\}$ has cardinality one, so its <power set> has cardinality two. Taking the <Cartesian product> with the singleton $\{\varnothing\}$ leaves that cardinality equal to two. The outer power set therefore has **$2^2=4$** elements.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

One example is **the identity function**, $f(n)=n$. A second is the <permutation>
$$
\boxed{f(1)=2,\quad f(2)=3,\quad f(3)=1,\quad f(n)=n\ \text{otherwise}.}
$$
Both are <bijections> of $\mathbb N$, whichever convention includes or excludes zero. For the second, applying $f$ three times returns every input to itself. Thus $f^3=\operatorname{id}$, and composing with the <inverse function> gives $f^2=f^{-1}$. This <permutation cube identity criterion> also shows why three-cycles work whereas a nontrivial two-cycle would not.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the convention that $R\circ S$ applies $S$ first. Reflexivity inserts an identity step: if $aRb$, use $aSa$ to obtain $(a,b)\in R\circ S$; if $aSb$, use $bRb$ for the same conclusion. Thus $R\cup S\subseteq R\circ S$. By <monotonicity of relation composition>,
$$
(R\cup S)\circ(R\cup S)\subseteq(R\circ S)\circ(R\circ S).
$$
For strictness, take $A=\{0,1,2,3\}$ and let $R=S$ consist of the identity pairs together with $(0,1),(1,2),(2,3)$. Both are <reflexive relations>. The left side permits at most two forward chain steps, so does not contain $(0,3)$. The right side permits four steps, including $0\to1\to2\to3\to3$, so does contain it. Equality therefore need not hold.

Use <modular arithmetic> modulo eight: $801\equiv1$, $32\cdot19\equiv0$, and $29\cdot13\equiv5\cdot5\equiv1$. The squared difference is consequently congruent to one, giving the remainder
$$
\boxed{2.}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Modulo seven, $19\equiv-2$ and $(-2)^3=-8\equiv-1$. Therefore $19^9\equiv(-1)^3\equiv-1\equiv6\pmod7$. The remainder is **$6$**, using <modular exponentiation>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For integers $a,b$ not both zero, replace them first by their <absolute values>, then repeat <integer division>:

```
while b != 0:
    (a,b) := (b, a mod b)
return a
```

This is the <Euclidean algorithm>. Its <loop invariant> is that $\gcd(a,b)$ equals the original <greatest common divisor>. When $b>0$, the new remainder lies in $[0,b)$, so $b$ is a strictly decreasing nonnegative <loop variant>. At termination, $\gcd(a,0)=a$, proving correctness, including the case where one original input is zero.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
1394=4(306)+170,\quad306=170+136,\quad170=136+34,\quad136=4(34).
$$
Thus the <greatest common divisor> is $34$. Back-substitution, as in the <extended Euclidean algorithm>, gives
$$
34=170-136=2(170)-306=2(1394)-9(306).
$$
Hence **$\gcd(306,1394)=34$, with $x=2$ and $y=-9$**.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For one colour, the box occupancies form a <weak composition> of $k$ into $n$ nonnegative parts. For $n\geq1$, <stars and bars> gives $\binom{k+n-1}{n-1}$ choices. The three colours' occupancies can be selected independently, so the number is
$$
\boxed{\binom{k+n-1}{n-1}^{\!3}.}
$$
Boxes are labelled, while equal-colour balls contribute only occupancy counts; no extra factorial for permuting such balls is appropriate.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

There are $6^3=216$ unrestricted placements, since one colour has $\binom42=6$ occupancy vectors. For any specified empty box, the two remaining boxes admit $3^3=27$ placements. For any specified pair of empty boxes, the one remaining box admits one placement. All three boxes cannot be empty.

The <inclusion-exclusion principle> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{216-3(27)+\binom32(1)=138.}
$$
The empty-box events are not <disjoint events>, so subtracting their counts without restoring their pairwise overlaps would be incorrect.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The base cases are $13=3(2)+7(1)$, $14=3(0)+7(2)$ and $15=3(5)+7(0)$. Suppose a counterexample exists and choose the least integer $n\geq13$ failing the claim. It is not one of these base cases, so $n\geq16$ and $n-3\geq13$.

Minimality supplies nonnegative integers $x,y$ with $n-3=3x+7y$. Then $n=3(x+1)+7y$, a contradiction. This <proof by minimal counterexample> establishes the claim. Three consecutive base cases are needed because the reduction subtracts three.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

As $x\to+\infty$, <big O notation> means
$$
\boxed{f=O(g)\iff\exists C>0\ \exists x_0\ \forall x\geq x_0:\ |f(x)|\leq C|g(x)|.}
$$
The same constant must work on the entire sufficiently large tail. Absolute values accommodate signed functions. A two-sided convention at infinity would replace $x\geq x_0$ by $|x|\geq x_0$; the positive-integer arguments in the later parts use the positive-tail convention.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose constants $C_1,C_2>0$ and a common threshold beyond which $f_1\leq C_1g_1$ and $f_2\leq C_2g_2$. Positivity permits multiplication:
$$
|f_1f_2|=f_1f_2\leq C_1C_2g_1g_2.
$$
The fixed constant $C_1C_2$ proves **$f_1f_2=O(g_1g_2)$** by the definition of <big O notation>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Beyond the same common threshold, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
|f_1-f_2|\leq f_1+f_2\leq C_1g_1+C_2g_2\leq\max(C_1,C_2)(g_1+g_2).
$$
Thus **$f_1-f_2=O(g_1+g_2)$**. The absolute value matters because the difference need not itself be positive, even though the four original functions are.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take
$$
f_1(n)=2n,\quad g_1(n)=n,\quad f_2(n)=g_2(n)=2^n.
$$
The premises hold with constants two and one. But
$$
\frac{f_2(f_1(n))}{g_2(g_1(n))}=\frac{2^{2n}}{2^n}=2^n\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
No fixed bounding constant can satisfy the proposed conclusion. This proves the <failure of unrestricted Big O composition>: a constant factor in an inner argument can become an unbounded factor after applying an exponential.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A stronger exact formula makes the <induction> explicit:
$$
\boxed{S_n=1+m\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor\bigr),\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
For $n=1$, the recurrence gives $S_1=S_0+m=1+m$. For $n\geq2$, set $j=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$, so $1\leq j<n$. In the relevant binary interval, $\lfloor\log_2j\rfloor=\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor-1$. The <strong induction> hypothesis gives
$$
S_n=S_j+m=1+m\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2j\rfloor\bigr)+m
=1+m\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor\bigr).
$$
For $n\geq2$, $S_n\leq1+m+m\log_2n\leq(1+2m)\log_2n$. Hence $S_n=O(\log_2n)$. This is an <integer-halving recurrence with constant toll>; the separately defined $n=0$ case does not require evaluating $\log0$.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <preorder> is **reflexive and transitive**: $xRx$ for every element, and $xRy,yRz$ imply $xRz$. A <partial order> additionally requires **<antisymmetry>**: $xRy$ and $yRx$ imply $x=y$. It need not compare every pair of distinct elements.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**No.** On $\{0,1,2\}$ take $R=\{(0,1),(1,2),(2,0)\}$. This is an <antisymmetric relation> because it contains no opposite pair of distinct elements. Its <reflexive transitive closure> contains every ordered pair: following the directed three-cycle reaches any vertex from any other. In particular it contains both $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$, violating antisymmetry.

This illustrates the <reachability preorder>: collapsing mutually reachable vertices into <strongly connected components> yields a partial order, but retaining all original vertices need not.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <gradient> equations are
$$
f_x=3x^2+2xy-10x-9=0,\qquad f_y=x^2-9=0.
$$
Thus $x=3$ gives $y=2$, while $x=-3$ gives $y=8$. The <Hessian matrix> is
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}6x+2y-10&2x\\2x&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det H=-4x^2.
$$
At both critical points the determinant is $-36<0$, so the Hessian is indefinite and the <second derivative test> classifies both as <saddle points>. Direct substitution gives
$$
\boxed{(3,2),\ (-3,8)\text{ are saddle points, and }f(3,2)=f(-3,8)=-30.}
$$
The factorization $f+30=(x^2-9)(x+y-5)$ also shows the crossing level-set branches through each saddle.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $\dot x=dx/du$, $\ddot x=d^2x/du^2$, $A_v=dA/dv$, $A_{vv}=d^2A/dv^2$, and $y_w=dy/dw$, $y_{ww}=d^2y/dw^2$. Put $S=A+A^T$. The <product rule> and <chain rule> give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f_u&=\dot x^TSx,\\
f_v&=x^TA_vx+w' y_w^Tb,\\
f_{uu}&=\ddot x^TSx+\dot x^TS\dot x,\\
f_{uv}&=\dot x^T(A_v+A_v^T)x,\\
f_{vv}&=x^TA_{vv}x+\bigl((w')^2y_{ww}+w''y_w\bigr)^Tb.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $w',w''$ are derivatives with respect to $v$. In the first derivative, $x^TA\dot x=\dot x^TA^Tx$ because the expression is scalar. Likewise $\dot x^TS\dot x=2\dot x^TA\dot x$ if desired. This <differentiation of a parameter-dependent quadratic form> does not assume that $A$ is symmetric. The positive linear term $y^Tb$ contributes the positive chain-rule terms in the $v$ derivatives; it is independent of $u$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Complete the square:
$$
h=(x^2+2y)^2-6.
$$
The <minimum locus of a squared function> occurs where its residual vanishes. Hence the minimum is $-6$ on $y=-x^2/2$, provided these points lie in the ellipse. Setting $t=x^2$ in the constraint gives $t+t^2/2\leq9$, or $0\leq t\leq\sqrt{19}-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\min_R h=-6,\quad\text{at }\left\{(x,-x^2/2):|x|\leq\sqrt{\sqrt{19}-1}\right\}.}
$$
For the maximum, let $q=x^2+2y$. The ellipse gives
$$
q\leq9-2y^2+2y=\frac{19}{2}-2\left(y-\frac12\right)^2\leq\frac{19}{2}.
$$
Also $q\geq2y\geq-3\sqrt2$, whose absolute value is less than $19/2$. Therefore $q^2\leq(19/2)^2$. Equality is attained at $y=1/2$, $x^2=17/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\max_R h=\frac{337}{4}=84\frac14,\quad\text{at }(x,y)=\left(\pm\sqrt{17/2},\frac12\right).}
$$
These attained bounds are global, not merely stationary-point tests. The minimizing locus is a parabola segment.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <partial fraction decomposition>:
$$
\frac1{4-x^2}=\frac14\left(\frac1{2+x}+\frac1{2-x}\right).
$$
Integrating gives
$$
\boxed{I(x)=\frac14\log\left|\frac{2+x}{2-x}\right|+C.}
$$
This real <antiderivative> is valid on each interval avoiding $x=\pm2$, with its own integration constant. In particular, $I(1)-I(0)=\frac14\log3$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive bounds, <integration by parts> with $u=\log x$ and $dv=x^2dx$ gives
$$
\boxed{F(a,b)=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\log x-\frac{x^3}{9}\right]_{a}^{b}.}
$$
For negative bounds, specify the <complex logarithm on the negative real axis>. With argument $\pi$, namely the upper-bank boundary value of the <principal logarithm>, $\log x=\log(-x)+i\pi$. Substitute $t=-x$ in the real part:
$$
\boxed{\int_c^d x^2\log x\,dx=F(-d,-c)+\frac{i\pi}{3}(d^3-c^3).}
$$
More generally, a continuous branch taking argument $(2k+1)\pi$ replaces $i\pi$ by $(2k+1)i\pi$. The imaginary part depends on that choice. The analytic principal branch excludes the negative axis itself, so the boundary-value convention is part of this interpretation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A horizontal strip at height $y$ has width $4-2\sin(\pi y/2)$. The sine term is odd, so the <area> is
$$
\boxed{|A|=\int_{-2}^{2}\left(4-2\sin\frac{\pi y}{2}\right)dy=16.}
$$
Reflection symmetry in the $y$-axis gives $\bar x=0$. For uniform density, the constant density cancels from the <center of mass> ratio. Its first moment in the vertical coordinate is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_A y\,dA
&=-4\int_0^2 y\sin\frac{\pi y}{2}\,dy\\
&=-4\left[-\frac{2y}{\pi}\cos\frac{\pi y}{2}+\frac4{\pi^2}\sin\frac{\pi y}{2}\right]_0^2
=-\frac{16}{\pi}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{(\bar x,\bar y)=\left(0,-\frac1\pi\right).}
$$
The wider part below the horizontal axis accounts for the negative vertical coordinate.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For $h=1/N$, the <composite trapezoidal rule> is
$$
T_N(f)=\frac1N\left(\frac{f(0)}2+\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}f(m/N)+\frac{f(1)}2\right).
$$
With $f(x)=1/(4-x^2)$ this becomes
$$
T_N=\frac{7}{24N}+\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}\frac{N}{4N^2-m^2}.
$$
Write the requested right <Riemann sum> as $S_N$. The <trapezoidal endpoint correction for a right Riemann sum> gives
$$
S_N-T_N=\frac{f(1)-f(0)}{2N}=\frac1{24N}.
$$
The integrand is continuous on $[0,1]$, so the trapezoidal values converge to its definite integral; the endpoint correction tends to zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{m=1}^N\frac{N}{4N^2-m^2}=\frac14\log3.}
$$

=== a

==== Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> of this <constant-coefficient differential equation> is $r^2-r-2=(r-2)(r+1)$. Its distinct roots give
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-x}.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the forcing $8e^{-2x}$, the <method of undetermined coefficients> gives the particular term $2e^{-2x}$. The term $e^{-x}$ is resonant with a homogeneous solution, so use $axe^{-x}$ for its forcing; substitution gives $a=1$.

Thus $y=C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-x}+2e^{-2x}+xe^{-x}$. The two initial conditions reduce to $C_1+C_2=3$ and $2C_1-C_2=0$, so $C_1=1$, $C_2=2$. The solution of the <initial value problem> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{2x}+(x+2)e^{-x}+2e^{-2x}.}
$$
The extra factor $x$ in the resonant particular solution is essential.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Forward Euler method> uses the current slope:
$$
\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n+h,\qquad y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(x_n,y_n).}
$$
Here $x_n=x_0+nh$ and the initial approximation normally equals the given initial value. Each step takes a tangent-line increment of width $h$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Define the one-step <local truncation error> as the defect obtained by inserting the exact solution into an update:
$$
\delta_{n+1}=y(x_n+h)-y(x_n)-h f(x_n,y(x_n)).
$$
For a smooth solution, <Taylor's theorem> and $y'=f(x,y)$ give
$$
\boxed{\delta_{n+1}=\frac{h^2}{2}y''(x_n)+O(h^3)=O(h^2).}
$$
Thus **$m=2$ for the one-step defect convention**. The leading derivative can also be written $y''=f_x+f_yf$ by the chain rule.

Some conventions instead call $\tau_{n+1}=\delta_{n+1}/h$ the truncation error. That <normalized local truncation error> is $O(h)$, so **$m=1$ under the normalized convention**. Both describe the same <Euler one-step defect>; neither should be confused with an unqualified global error claim.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Factor the right side as $(x-1)^2(x-3)$. On $-1<x<1$, division by $x^2-1$ gives a <first-order linear differential equation> with <integrating factor>
$$
\mu(x)=\frac{1+x}{1-x},\qquad \frac{\mu'}\mu=-\frac2{x^2-1}.
$$
Multiplication makes the equation $(\mu y)'=3-x$, so $\mu y=3x-x^2/2+C$. At zero, $\mu(0)y(0)=1$, hence $C=1$. Simplifying gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{1-x}{1+x}\left(1+3x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)=\frac{x^2}{2}-4x+6-\frac5{x+1}.}
$$
Its derivative is
$$
y'(x)=x-4+\frac5{(x+1)^2},\qquad\boxed{y'(0)=1.}
$$
The derivation solves the initial-value problem on $(-1,1)$. The simplified expression also extends smoothly through $x=1$ and satisfies the original undivided equation there, giving a solution on all $x>-1$; its pole prevents continuation through $x=-1$.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The variable has positive-integer support, so $\mathbb P(X>0)=1$. Subtract consecutive values of the <survival function>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=k)=\mathbb P(X>k-1)-\mathbb P(X>k)=p(1-p)^{k-1},\quad k\geq1.}
$$
This is the positive-support convention for the <geometric distribution>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Summing a <geometric series> gives the <probability generating function>
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\sum_{k\geq1}p(1-p)^{k-1}s^k=\frac{ps}{1-(1-p)s}.}
$$
The series converges absolutely for $|s|<1/(1-p)$. Differentiating the rational expression yields
$$
\boxed{G_X'(s)=\frac{p}{(1-(1-p)s)^2}=\frac p{(1-s+ps)^2}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <expected value> is the derivative of the <probability generating function> at one. The expression from part (ii) gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EX=G_X'(1)=\frac p{p^2}=\frac1p.}
$$
Its finiteness also follows directly from the exponentially decaying geometric probabilities.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

By <independence>, for $k\geq0$,
$$
\mathbb P(W>k)=\mathbb P(X_1>k,X_2>k)=(1-p)^{2k}.
$$
This is the <geometric minimum law>, giving
$$
\boxed{W\sim\operatorname{Geom}\bigl(1-(1-p)^2\bigr)=\operatorname{Geom}(2p-p^2).}
$$
The support remains the positive integers; this parameter is a success probability, not a mean.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $Y\geq1$, its finite <expected value> satisfies $1\leq\mu<\infty$. Every $q_k=kp_k/\mu$ is nonnegative and
$$
\sum_{k\geq1}q_k=\frac1\mu\sum_{k\geq1}kp_k=\frac{\mathbb EY}{\mu}=1.
$$
Thus these values are a <probability mass function>. They define the <size-biased distribution> of $Y$: larger values receive proportionally more weight.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq s<1$, differentiate the <probability generating function> term by term and multiply by $s/\mu$:
$$
\boxed{G_{\widetilde Y}(s)=\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{kp_k}{\mu}s^k
=\frac s\mu\sum_{k\geq1}kp_ks^{k-1}=\frac s\mu G_Y'(s).}
$$
This is the <size-biased probability generating function> identity. At $s=1$, the finite-mean assumption gives the limiting value one on both sides; at zero, positive support gives value zero.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $Y\sim\operatorname{Geom}(p)$, its mean is $1/p$, so the <size-biased probability generating function> is
$$
G_{\widetilde Y}(s)=\frac{p^2s}{(1-(1-p)s)^2}.
$$
Choose **$X_1,X_2$ independent with distribution $\operatorname{Geom}(p)$**. Their shifted sum has generating function, initially for $s>0$,
$$
G_{X_1+X_2-1}(s)=\frac1s\left(\frac{ps}{1-(1-p)s}\right)^2=G_{\widetilde Y}(s),
$$
with both sides continuous and zero at $s=0$. Equality of <probability generating functions> proves
$$
\boxed{\widetilde Y\overset d=X_1+X_2-1.}
$$
Equivalently, $X_1+X_2=k+1$ has $k$ possible positive summand pairs, each of probability $p^2(1-p)^{k-1}$, matching the <size-biased geometric distribution> probabilities.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <partition of a set> consists of nonempty subsets that are pairwise disjoint and whose union is the whole set. Here
$$
\boxed{B_i\ne\varnothing,\quad B_i\cap B_j=\varnothing\ (i\ne j),\quad\bigcup_{i\in I}B_i=\Omega.}
$$
Thus every outcome belongs to exactly one cell. The probability calculation additionally requires the cells to be measurable events.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a finite or countable measurable partition, the <law of total probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\sum_{i:\,\mathbb P(B_i)>0}\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i).}
$$
Indeed, $A$ is the disjoint union of $A\cap B_i$, and the <conditional probability> formula gives each positive-probability term. Zero-probability cells contribute zero; their conditionals need not be defined by a ratio. If all cells have positive probability, the sum simply runs over every $i\in I$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Starting at $X$, the two possible two-step returns are $X\to X\to X$ and $X\to Y\to X$. They are <disjoint events>, with probabilities $1/4$ and $3/16$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_2=\frac14+\frac3{16}=\frac7{16}.}
$$
Each path probability uses the specified <Markov property> to multiply its successive transition probabilities.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $q_j=\mathbb P(M_j=Y)$. By the <law of total probability>,
$$
p_n=\frac12p_{n-1}+\frac38q_{n-1}.
$$
There is no return contribution from the <absorbing state> $Z$. Since $Y$ can be entered only from $X$, $q_{n-1}=\frac12p_{n-2}$ for $n\geq2$. Substitution yields
$$
\boxed{16p_n=8p_{n-1}+3p_{n-2},\qquad n\geq2.}
$$
No independence between successive positions is assumed; the conditional transitions give the recurrence.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The initial values are $p_0=1$ and $p_1=1/2$. The <characteristic polynomial> of the <linear recurrence> is
$$
16r^2-8r-3=(4r-3)(4r+1),
$$
with roots $3/4$ and $-1/4$. Hence $p_n=a(3/4)^n+b(-1/4)^n$. The initial conditions give $a+b=1$, $3a-b=2$, so $a=3/4$, $b=1/4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_n=\frac34\left(\frac34\right)^n+\frac14\left(-\frac14\right)^n
=\frac{3^{n+1}+(-1)^n}{4^{n+1}},\qquad n\geq0.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Markov property> and time homogeneity give
$$
\mathbb P(M_n=X,M_{2n}=X)=p_n\mathbb P(M_{2n}=X\mid M_n=X)=p_n^2.
$$
Since $p_{2n}>0$, <conditional probability> yields the <Markov chain return bridge> expression
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_n=X\mid M_{2n}=X)=\frac{p_n^2}{p_{2n}}
=\frac{(3^{n+1}+(-1)^n)^2}{4(3^{2n+1}+1)}.}
$$
The explicit formula gives $p_n\sim\frac34(3/4)^n$, so
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(M_n=X\mid M_{2n}=X)=\frac34.}
$$
The conditioning event has positive probability at each finite time even though those return probabilities tend to zero. Taking an undefined conditional on an infinite-time zero-probability event is not needed.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The rate-one <exponential distribution> has density $e^{-t}$ for $t\geq0$. <Integration by parts> gives $\mathbb ET=1$ and $\mathbb E[T^2]=2$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb ET=1,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(T)=2-1^2=1.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $T$ and $U$ are <independent random variables>, their product's <expected value> factors:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[TU]=\mathbb ET\,\mathbb EU=1.}
$$
The original independence assumption is essential to this factorization.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the overlapping products, factor using the independence of $T,U,V$:
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(TU,UV)=\mathbb E[TU^2V]-\mathbb E[TU]\mathbb E[UV]
=1\cdot2\cdot1-1\cdot1=1.
$$
This is the <covariance of overlapping independent products>: the shared $U$ contributes its second moment. In contrast, $TU$ and $VW$ use disjoint independent factors and are themselves independent, giving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(TU,UV)=1,\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(TU,VW)=0.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume finite second moments and write $Z_i=X_i-\mathbb EX_i$. Expanding the squared centered sum gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X_1+X_2)
&=\mathbb E[(Z_1+Z_2)^2]\\
&=\mathbb E[Z_1^2]+\mathbb E[Z_2^2]+2\mathbb E[Z_1Z_2]\\
&=\operatorname{Var}(X_1)+\operatorname{Var}(X_2)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2).
\end{aligned}
$$
This <variance of a sum> identity does not require independence; the <covariance> term records the contribution that would otherwise be lost.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For finite second moments, the general <variance of a sum> formula is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right)
=\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).}
$$
Each distinct unordered pair appears once in the second sum and twice when the full square is expanded. No independence assumption is needed.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $Z_i=Y_{i-1}Y_i$ for $1\leq i\leq n$. Independence of the underlying exponentials gives $\mathbb EZ_i=1$ and
$$
\operatorname{Var}(Z_i)=\mathbb E[Y_{i-1}^2]\mathbb E[Y_i^2]-1=4-1=3.
$$
By the <covariance of overlapping independent products>, adjacent terms have covariance one. Terms separated by at least two indices use disjoint independent factors, so their covariance is zero. There are $n-1$ adjacent pairs. <Linearity of expectation> and the <variance of a sum> formula therefore yield
$$
\boxed{\mathbb ES_n=n,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=3n+2(n-1)=5n-2,\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
In particular, the products themselves are not independent merely because the original $Y_i$ are.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The normalized sum has mean one and variance $(5n-2)/n^2$. For every fixed $\epsilon>0$, the <Chebyshev inequality> gives
$$
0\leq\mathbb P\left(\frac{S_n}{n}>1+\epsilon\right)
\leq\mathbb P\left(\left|\frac{S_n}{n}-1\right|>\epsilon\right)
\leq\frac{5n-2}{n^2\epsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
This proves the requested limit, and in fact proves <convergence in probability> of $S_n/n$ to one. It is a <finite covariance range weak law>: adjacent dependence is harmless here because only linearly many covariance terms survive.
