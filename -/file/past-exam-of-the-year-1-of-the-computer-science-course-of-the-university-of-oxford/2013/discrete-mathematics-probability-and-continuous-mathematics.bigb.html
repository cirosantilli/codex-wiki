= Discrete mathematics, probability and continuous mathematics
{scope}
{title2=4223}

Solutions to the nine questions in the original paper.<Discrete mathematics, probability and continuous mathematics examination paper by the University of Oxford (2013)>[Printed pp. 2–9, Questions 1–9]{ref}

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Write each <set difference> as an <intersection> with a <complement of a set>. Applying <De Morgan's laws> and then the <distributive property> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\setminus(B\cup C)&=A\cap\overline{B\cup C}=A\cap(\overline B\cap\overline C)=(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C),\\
A\setminus(B\cap C)&=A\cap\overline{B\cap C}=A\cap(\overline B\cup\overline C)=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\setminus C).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <complements> turn the inner <union> into an <intersection> and vice versa. Thus
$$
\boxed{A\setminus(B\cup C)=(A\setminus B)\cap(A\setminus C),\quad A\setminus(B\cap C)=(A\setminus B)\cup(A\setminus C).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply the <distributive property> to the <intersection> with $\overline C$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\cap B)\setminus C&=(A\cap B)\cap\overline C=(A\cap\overline C)\cap(B\cap\overline C),\\
(A\cup B)\setminus C&=(A\cup B)\cap\overline C=(A\cap\overline C)\cup(B\cap\overline C).
\end{aligned}
$$
In the first line the repeated factor $\overline C$ changes nothing. Rewriting in terms of <set differences> yields
$$
\boxed{(A\cap B)\setminus C=(A\setminus C)\cap(B\setminus C),\quad(A\cup B)\setminus C=(A\setminus C)\cup(B\setminus C).}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <De Morgan's laws> to remove the <complement of a set>, then distribute the two <unions>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)
&=(A\cup B)\cap(\overline A\cup\overline B)\\
&=(A\cap\overline A)\cup(A\cap\overline B)\cup(B\cap\overline A)\cup(B\cap\overline B)\\
&=(A\cap\overline B)\cup(B\cap\overline A).
\end{aligned}
$$
The first and last <intersections> in the middle line are the <empty set>. The remaining <union> is precisely the collection of elements belonging to exactly one of the original <sets>. Therefore both expressions define the same <symmetric difference>:
$$
\boxed{(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)=A\oplus B.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

An algebraic way to prove the <set identity> is to show that $D=\overline A\cup\overline B$ is a <complement of a set> for $A\cap B$. By the <distributive property>,
$$
(A\cap B)\cap D=(A\cap B\cap\overline A)\cup(A\cap B\cap\overline B)=\varnothing,
$$
and
$$
(A\cap B)\cup D=(A\cup D)\cap(B\cup D)=\mathcal U\cap\mathcal U=\mathcal U.
$$
A <complement of a set> is unique: if $D,E$ both complement $S$, then $D=D\cap(S\cup E)=D\cap E$, and similarly $E=D\cap E$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\overline{A\cap B}=\overline A\cup\overline B.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write the <set difference> as an <intersection> and use the fact that taking the <complement of a set> twice returns that <set>. The <commutativity> of <intersection> gives the reversible calculation
$$
\boxed{\overline A\setminus\overline B=\overline A\cap\overline{\overline B}=\overline A\cap B=B\cap\overline A=B\setminus A.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <symmetric difference> formula from part (b), followed by the <set difference> identity from part (c)(ii):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline A\oplus\overline B
&=(\overline A\setminus\overline B)\cup(\overline B\setminus\overline A)\\
&=(B\setminus A)\cup(A\setminus B)
=A\oplus B.
\end{aligned}
$$
Taking both <complements> swaps the two ways to belong to exactly one <set>, leaving their <union> unchanged:
$$
\boxed{\overline A\oplus\overline B=A\oplus B.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <image of a function> is the <set> of values actually attained as its argument ranges over its <domain of a function>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}f=f(A)=\{f(a):a\in A\}=\{b\in B:\exists a\in A,\ f(a)=b\}.}
$$
It is a <subset> of the <codomain> $B$, and need not equal $B$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <function> is <surjective>, also called onto, when every element of its <codomain> has at least one <preimage>:
$$
\boxed{f(A)=B\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\forall b\in B\ \exists a\in A:\ f(a)=b.}
$$
The condition concerns the specified <codomain>, not just the formula defining the <function>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <function> is <injective>, also called one-to-one, when equal outputs force equal inputs:
$$
\boxed{\forall a,a'\in A,\quad f(a)=f(a')\Longrightarrow a=a'.}
$$
Equivalently, different elements of the <domain of a function> always have different images.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

A <bijection> is a <function> that is both <injective> and <surjective>. Each element of the <codomain> then has exactly one <preimage>:
$$
\boxed{\forall b\in B\ \exists!a\in A:\ f(a)=b.}
$$
This unique-preimage property allows the <inverse function> $f^{-1}:B\to A$ to be defined on all of $B$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

If $n$ is positive and odd, $n^2$ is odd. The <function> $x\mapsto x^{n^2}$ is a <strictly increasing function> on the <real numbers>, so it is <injective>. Every real $y$ has the real odd root $\operatorname{sgn}(y)|y|^{1/n^2}$, so the <function> is also <surjective>.

If $n$ is positive and even, the exponent is positive and even. Then $f(1)=f(-1)$, while $f(x)\geq0$ for all real $x$. These facts disprove <injectivity> and <surjectivity>, respectively. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is onto iff }n\text{ is odd};\qquad f\text{ is one-to-one iff }n\text{ is odd}.}
$$
If the convention $0\in\mathbb N$ is used, $n=0$ gives exponent zero. Interpreting $x^0$ as the constant <polynomial> $1$ makes the <function> neither <injective> nor <surjective>. If $0^0$ is left undefined, that case does not define the stated <function> on all the <real numbers>. Neither convention adds a qualifying value of $n$.

=== f
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <function composition> of two <bijections> is a <bijection>. Its <inverse function> is two-sided, so
$$
\boxed{\text{True: }(f\circ g)\circ(f\circ g)^{-1}=\operatorname{id}.}
$$
This is exactly the defining cancellation property of an <inverse function>, with the whole <function composition> regarded as one <function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The inverse factors are generally in the wrong order. For a counterexample, take the <bijections> $f(x)=x+1$ and $g(x)=2x$, with <inverse functions> $f^{-1}(x)=x-1$ and $g^{-1}(x)=x/2$. Then
$$
((f\circ g)\circ(f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}))(x)=2(x/2-1)+1=x-1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{False in general; the displayed composition need not be }\operatorname{id}.}
$$
For particular <bijections> that commute the equality can hold; the claim fails as a universal assertion.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <associativity> of <function composition> to cancel adjacent <inverse functions> in the right order:
$$
f\circ g\circ g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}=f\circ\operatorname{id}\circ f^{-1}=\operatorname{id}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{True: }(f\circ g)\circ(g^{-1}\circ f^{-1})=\operatorname{id}.}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <bijections> $f(x)=x+1$ and $g(x)=2x$ used above, $f\circ g$ sends $x$ to $2x+1$. Its <inverse function> sends $x$ to $(x-1)/2$, whereas $f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$ sends $x$ to $x/2-1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{False in general: }(f\circ g)^{-1}\ne f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}.}
$$

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

The proposed <inverse function> cancels on both sides:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(f\circ g)\circ(g^{-1}\circ f^{-1})&=\operatorname{id},\\
(g^{-1}\circ f^{-1})\circ(f\circ g)&=g^{-1}\circ(f^{-1}\circ f)\circ g=\operatorname{id}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The uniqueness of an <inverse function> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\text{True: }(f\circ g)^{-1}=g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}.}
$$
The operation applied last must be undone first.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Big O notation> asserts an eventual uniform bound on the size of $f$ by a constant multiple of the size of $g$:
$$
\boxed{f(n)=O(g(n))\iff\exists C>0\ \exists n_0\ \forall n\geq n_0:\ |f(n)|\leq C|g(n)|.}
$$
The constants $C,n_0$ may depend on the two <functions> but cannot depend on $n$. Absolute values matter because the stated <functions> can be real-valued with either sign.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <logarithm> identity $a^{\log_b n}=n^{\log_b a}$ rewrites the expression as $n^{\log_2 3}$. Since $3<4=2^2$, we have $\log_2 3<2$, and for $n\geq1$,
$$
3^{\log_2 n}=n^{\log_2 3}\leq n^2.
$$
Thus the <Big O notation> bound holds with $C=1$:
$$
\boxed{\text{True: }3^{\log_2 n}=O(n^2).}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <logarithm> laws give
$$
n\log_2(5n)+\log_2(n^2)=n\log_2 n+n\log_2 5+2\log_2 n.
$$
For $n\geq2$, both $n\leq n\log_2 n$ and $\log_2 n\leq n\log_2 n$. The expression is therefore at most $(3+\log_2 5)n\log_2 n$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{True: }n\log_2(5n)+\log_2(n^2)=O(n\log_2 n).}
$$
The <Big O notation> concerns eventual behaviour; the zero value of $n\log_2 n$ at $n=1$ is irrelevant.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Divide by the proposed comparison <function> $2^n$:
$$
\frac{n^2+n2^n}{2^n}=\frac{n^2}{2^n}+n\geq n.
$$
The ratio is unbounded, so no constant in the definition of <Big O notation> can work:
$$
\boxed{\text{False: }n^2+n2^n\ne O(2^n).}
$$

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The <exponential function> eventually grows faster than the fixed <polynomial>. A direct justification uses the ratio $r_n=4^n/n^4$:
$$
\frac{r_{n+1}}{r_n}=4\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^4\longrightarrow4.
$$
For all sufficiently large $n$, this ratio exceeds $2$, so $r_n$ grows without bound. The proposed <Big O notation> bound is therefore impossible:
$$
\boxed{\text{False: }4^n\ne O(n^4).}
$$

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

Treat $a,b$ as fixed positive <integers>. For $n\geq1$, $n^2+a\leq(1+a)n^2$. Raising this inequality to the positive power $b$ gives
$$
(n^2+a)^b\leq(1+a)^b n^{2b}.
$$
The factor $(1+a)^b$ is independent of $n$, as required by <Big O notation>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{True for fixed }a,b:\quad(n^2+a)^b=O(n^{2b}).}
$$
A bound uniform over varying $a,b$ is not what the notation here asserts.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

By <Big O notation>, there are $C_1,C_2>0$ and thresholds after which $f_1(n)\leq C_1g_1(n)$ and $f_2(n)\leq C_2g_2(n)$. Choose a common threshold and set $C=\max(C_1,C_2)$. The positivity assumption gives
$$
0\leq f_1(n)+f_2(n)\leq C(g_1(n)+g_2(n)).
$$
Squaring preserves this inequality, so
$$
\boxed{(f_1+f_2)^2\leq C^2(g_1+g_2)^2\text{ eventually},\qquad(f_1+f_2)^2=O((g_1+g_2)^2).}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

First consider powers of four, with a fixed finite base value. For a <mathematical induction> hypothesis $x_{n/4}\leq C(n/4)^2$, the <recurrence relation> gives
$$
x_n\leq4C(n/4)^2+n^2=(C/4+1)n^2\leq Cn^2
$$
provided $C\geq4/3$ and $C$ also covers the base value.

The allowed integer rounding needs a little more care. Suppose the <recurrence relation> is applied above fixed base cases, as usual; applying $x_1=4x_1+1$ with a ceiling would not define a positive sequence. For either rounding write $m=\lceil n/4\rceil$ or $m=\lfloor n/4\rfloor$. For $n\geq6$,
$$
m\leq\frac{n+3}{4},\qquad\frac{4m^2}{n^2}\leq\frac14\left(1+\frac3n\right)^2\leq\frac9{16},\qquad m<n.
$$
Choose $C\geq16/7$ large enough to bound all finitely many positive-index base values below $6$. By <strong induction>,
$$
x_n=4x_m+n^2\leq4Cm^2+n^2\leq(9C/16+1)n^2\leq Cn^2.
$$
This argument starts at $n=6$, so the floor convention's index zero, if used in an earlier base calculation, is absorbed into those fixed initial values. Thus, for either convention with a well-defined finite base,
$$
\boxed{x_n=O(n^2).}
$$

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

In <modular arithmetic>, calculations may be performed after replacing each input by its remainder. For a positive modulus $m$, write $[a]_m$ for the remainder in $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$. Then
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
[a+b]_m&=[[a]_m+[b]_m]_m,\\
[ab]_m&=[[a]_m[b]_m]_m,\\
[a^k]_m&=[[a]_m^k]_m\qquad(k\geq0).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Equivalently, if $a\equiv a'\pmod m$ and $b\equiv b'\pmod m$, addition and multiplication give $a+b\equiv a'+b'$ and $ab\equiv a'b'$, and a fixed nonnegative integer exponent gives $a^k\equiv(a')^k$. One cannot in general reduce the exponent modulo $m$ by this rule.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <modular arithmetic> to keep all intermediate numbers small:
$$
34\equiv2,\quad45\equiv5,\quad55\equiv7,\quad89\equiv1\pmod8.
$$
Thus $34\cdot45\cdot55+89\equiv2\cdot5\cdot7+1=71\equiv7\equiv-1\pmod8$. Cubing gives
$$
\boxed{(34\cdot45\cdot55+89)^3\equiv(-1)^3\equiv7\pmod8;\quad\text{remainder }7.}
$$

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

<Repeated squaring> computes $a,a^2,a^4,a^8,\ldots$ by successively squaring. The <binary representation> of the exponent specifies which of these values to multiply. For <modular exponentiation>, reduce after every multiplication; only a number of steps proportional to the <logarithm> of the exponent is needed.

Here $13\equiv2\pmod{11}$ and $17=16+1$. Successive squares give
$$
2^2\equiv4,\quad2^4\equiv16\equiv5,\quad2^8\equiv25\equiv3,\quad2^{16}\equiv9\pmod{11}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{13^{17}\equiv2^{16}2\equiv9\cdot2\equiv7\pmod{11};\quad\text{remainder }7.}
$$

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Mathematical induction> has two obligations. Establish the base case $S(0)$ if the <natural numbers> start at zero, or $S(1)$ if they start at one. Then prove the induction step: for an arbitrary natural $k$ in the relevant range, assume $S(k)$ and deduce $S(k+1)$.

The base case starts the chain, and the step propagates truth to every subsequent <natural number>:
$$
\boxed{S(n_0)\ \land\ \bigl(\forall k\geq n_0,\ S(k)\Rightarrow S(k+1)\bigr)\ \Longrightarrow\ \forall n\geq n_0,\ S(n).}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The base case is $F_5=5$, so $5\mid F_5$. To relate successive blocks of five, use the <Fibonacci addition formula>:
$$
F_{k+5}=F_5F_{k+1}+F_4F_k=5F_{k+1}+3F_k.
$$
This identity can also be obtained simply by applying the <Fibonacci recurrence> five times. If the <mathematical induction> hypothesis is $5\mid F_{5n}$, then
$$
F_{5(n+1)}=5F_{5n+1}+3F_{5n}
$$
is a sum of two multiples of five. Hence
$$
\boxed{F_5\mid F_{5n}\quad\text{for every integer }n>0.}
$$
The generalisation is <Fibonacci multiple-index divisibility>:
$$
\boxed{F_m\mid F_{mn}\quad(m,n\geq1).}
$$
Indeed, $F_{mn+m}=F_mF_{mn+1}+F_{m-1}F_{mn}$, so precisely the same <mathematical induction> works with $5$ replaced by $m$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For finite <sets>, the <inclusion-exclusion principle> states
$$
\boxed{\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\right|=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|.}
$$
An element present in $r$ of the <sets> is counted $\sum_{k=1}^r(-1)^{k+1}\binom rk=1$ times, which explains the alternating corrections.

There are $\lfloor999/d\rfloor$ positive <integers> strictly below $1000$ divisible by $d$. For the divisors $2,5,7$, the single counts are $499,199,142$. Their pairwise <intersections> correspond to divisibility by $10,14,35$, giving $99,71,28$. The triple <intersection> has $\lfloor999/70\rfloor=14$ elements. Therefore
$$
\boxed{499+199+142-99-71-28+14=656.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A <binary relation> $R$ on $A$ is a <subset> of $A\times A$. Its properties are quantified over elements of $A$:

* A <reflexive relation> satisfies $\forall a\in A,\ aRa$.
* A <symmetric relation> satisfies $\forall a,b\in A,\ aRb\Rightarrow bRa$.
* An <antisymmetric relation> satisfies $\forall a,b\in A,\ (aRb\land bRa)\Rightarrow a=b$.
* A <transitive relation> satisfies $\forall a,b,c\in A,\ (aRb\land bRc)\Rightarrow aRc$.
* An <irreflexive relation> satisfies $\forall a\in A,\ \neg(aRa)$.
* A <serial relation> satisfies $\forall a\in A\ \exists b\in A,\ aRb$.

Thus an <equivalence relation> is a <binary relation> that is simultaneously reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The <equivalence class> of $a$ is the <set> of all elements related to it:
$$
\boxed{R\text{ is an equivalence relation}\iff R\text{ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive};\qquad[a]_R=\{b\in A:aRb\}.}
$$
<Antisymmetry> permits diagonal pairs and does not mean that a <binary relation> fails to be a <symmetric relation>.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <binary relation> $\leq$ is reflexive and transitive but is not a <symmetric relation>. For example, $1\leq2$ while $2\nleq1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\leq\text{ is not an equivalence relation on }\mathbb N.}
$$
There are consequently no <equivalence classes> for this <binary relation> in the requested sense.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

This is equality of remainders in <modular arithmetic>. It is reflexive because $3\mid(n-n)=0$, symmetric because $3\mid(n-m)$ implies $3\mid(m-n)$, and transitive because $3\mid(n-m)$ and $3\mid(k-n)$ imply $3\mid(k-m)$ by addition. Thus
$$
\boxed{m\sim n\iff m\equiv n\pmod3\text{ is an equivalence relation}.}
$$
Its three <equivalence classes> are
$$
\boxed{C_r=\{n\in\mathbb N:n\equiv r\pmod3\},\qquad r=0,1,2.}
$$
If zero is included in $\mathbb N$, it belongs to $C_0$; otherwise that class starts at three.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

In a <symmetric relation>, each of the $n$ diagonal pairs $(a,a)$ can be chosen independently. For each of the $\binom n2$ unordered pairs of distinct elements, either both ordered pairs $(a,b),(b,a)$ occur or neither occurs. Every such choice is free and produces a distinct <symmetric relation>. Hence
$$
\boxed{2^n2^{\binom n2}=2^{n(n+1)/2}.}
$$
For the <empty set> this correctly gives one <binary relation>, the empty one.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take each <partial derivative> while holding the other coordinate fixed. Differentiating the <polynomial> term by term gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f_x&=2x(y^4+y^2+1),\\
f_y&=4x^2y^3+3y^2+2x^2y-3,\\
f_{xx}&=2(y^4+y^2+1),\\
f_{xy}&=8xy^3+4xy,\\
f_{yy}&=12x^2y^2+6y+2x^2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Since the <function> is a <polynomial>, both orders of the <mixed partial derivative> agree everywhere.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <multivariable chain rule> follows the change of both coordinates along the <parametric curve>:
$$
\frac{df}{ds}=f_x\frac{dx}{ds}+f_y\frac{dy}{ds},\qquad\frac{dx}{ds}=2s,\quad\frac{dy}{ds}=e^s.
$$
Substituting the <partial derivatives> from part (a), with $x=1+s^2$ and $y=e^s$, yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{df}{ds}=4s(1+s^2)(e^{4s}+e^{2s}+1)+4(1+s^2)^2e^{4s}+3e^{3s}+2(1+s^2)^2e^{2s}-3e^s.}
$$
The first term comes from changing $x$, and the remaining terms from changing $y$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At the origin the needed <partial derivatives> are
$$
f(0,0)=1,\quad f_x(0,0)=0,\quad f_y(0,0)=-3,\quad f_{xx}(0,0)=2,\quad f_{xy}(0,0)=f_{yy}(0,0)=0.
$$
The <Taylor series> through total degree two is therefore
$$
f(0,0)+f_x(0,0)x+f_y(0,0)y+\tfrac12\bigl(f_{xx}(0,0)x^2+2f_{xy}(0,0)xy+f_{yy}(0,0)y^2\bigr),
$$
which gives
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=1-3y+x^2+O(\|(x,y)\|^3)\quad\text{as }(x,y)\to(0,0).}
$$
Here total degree is what matters: $x^2y^2$ is quartic, not quadratic. The exact omitted <polynomial> is $y^3+x^2y^2+x^2y^4$, which verifies the stated <Big O notation> remainder directly.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A <critical point> requires $f_x=f_y=0$. Since $y^4+y^2+1>0$ for every real $y$, the first <partial derivative> forces $x=0$. The second then reduces to $3y^2-3=0$, so the only <critical points> are $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$.

At these points the <Hessian matrix> is diagonal:
$$
H_f(0,1)=\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\0&6\end{pmatrix},\qquad H_f(0,-1)=\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\0&-6\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The first is a <positive-definite matrix>, giving a strict local minimum; the second is an <indefinite matrix>, giving a <saddle point>. Their values are $f(0,1)=-1$ and $f(0,-1)=3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(0,1):\text{ strict local minimum, value }-1;\qquad(0,-1):\text{ saddle point, value }3;\qquad\text{no local maxima}.}
$$
The minimum is local: along $x=0$, $f(0,y)=y^3-3y+1$ is unbounded below as $y\to-\infty$.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Differentiate the product $4(x-1)^{-2}(x+1)^{-1}$ using the <product rule> and <chain rule>:
$$
f'(x)=-8(x-1)^{-3}(x+1)^{-1}-4(x-1)^{-2}(x+1)^{-2}.
$$
Combining terms over a common denominator gives
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{4(3x+1)}{(x-1)^3(x+1)^2},\qquad x\ne\pm1.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <partial fraction decomposition>, write
$$
\frac4{(x-1)^2(x+1)}=\frac A{x-1}+\frac B{(x-1)^2}+\frac C{x+1}.
$$
Multiplying by $(x-1)^2(x+1)$ gives $4=A(x^2-1)+B(x+1)+C(x-1)^2$. Setting $x=1,-1$ gives $B=2,C=1$, and comparing the coefficient of $x^2$ gives $A=-1$. Thus
$$
f(x)=-\frac1{x-1}+\frac2{(x-1)^2}+\frac1{x+1}.
$$

The <constant of integration> can be chosen independently on each interval separated by $-1$ and $1$.

===== Solution

With step size $h=1/4$, the <composite trapezoidal rule> gives half weight to the two endpoints:
$$
T_4=\frac14\left[\frac{0^2+1^2}{2}+\left(\frac14\right)^2+\left(\frac12\right)^2+\left(\frac34\right)^2\right]
=\frac14\left(\frac12+\frac{1+4+9}{16}\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_4=\frac{11}{32}=0.34375.}

It exceeds the exact <definite integral> by $1/96$, as expected because $x^2$ is a <convex function> and each trapezoid lies above the curve.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $N$ equal intervals, the <composite trapezoidal rule> is
$$
T_N=\frac1N\left[\frac12+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\frac nN\right)^2\right]
=\frac1{N^3}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n^2+\frac1{2N}.
$$
Adding the missing $N^2/N^3$ term therefore gives the exact relation
$$
\frac1{N^3}\sum_{n=1}^N n^2=T_N+\frac1{2N}.
$$
Since $x^2$ is continuous, its <Riemann sums>, and hence its endpoint-averaged trapezoidal sums, converge to its <definite integral>. Thus $T_N\to1/3$ and $1/(2N)\to0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{N^3}\sum_{n=1}^N n^2=\frac13.}
$$
No pre-existing formula for the sum of squares is needed.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <discrete random variable> taking nonnegative integer values, its <probability generating function> is
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb P(X=k)s^k.}
$$
It is defined at least for $|s|\leq1$. The usual <probability generating function> is intended for nonnegative integer-valued <random variables>; a general discrete real-valued <random variable> need not have this form. The coefficient of $s^k$ recovers $\mathbb P(X=k)$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <binomial distribution>, the <binomial theorem> gives
$$
G_X(s)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}s^k=(1-p+ps)^n.
$$
Differentiation of the <probability generating function> recovers the first <expected value> and second <factorial moment>:
$$
G_X'(1)=np,\qquad G_X''(1)=n(n-1)p^2=\mathbb E[X(X-1)].
$$
Since $X^2=X(X-1)+X$, the <variance> is
$$
G_X''(1)+G_X'(1)-G_X'(1)^2=n(n-1)p^2+np-n^2p^2=np(1-p).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{G_X(s)=(1-p+ps)^n,\qquad\mathbb E[X]=np,\qquad\operatorname{var}(X)=np(1-p).}
$$
The formulas also hold for $n=0,1$ and the degenerate choices $p=0,1$, with derivatives read directly from the resulting <polynomial>.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <conditional probability> rescales the <probability> of the part of $A$ lying in $B$ by the <probability> of $B$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)},\qquad\mathbb P(B)>0.}
$$
The denominator makes $B$ itself have <conditional probability> one.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <events> $A\cap B$ and $A\cap B^c$ are disjoint and their <union> is $A$. Additivity of <probability> gives
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\mathbb P(A\cap B)+\mathbb P(A\cap B^c).
$$
Both conditioning <events> have positive <probability> because $0<\mathbb P(B)<1$. Apply the definition of <conditional probability> to each term to obtain the <law of total probability>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\mathbb P(A\mid B)\mathbb P(B)+\mathbb P(A\mid B^c)\mathbb P(B^c).}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $H$ mean the added ball was red, and $D$ mean the first draw was red. Their relevant <probabilities> are $\mathbb P(H)=\mathbb P(H^c)=1/2$, $\mathbb P(D\mid H)=1$, and $\mathbb P(D\mid H^c)=1/2$. The <law of total probability> gives $\mathbb P(D)=3/4$.

Given the observed red draw, the remaining ball is red precisely when $H$ occurred. <Bayes' theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{remaining ball red}\mid D)=\mathbb P(H\mid D)=\frac{1\cdot(1/2)}{3/4}=\frac23.}
$$
The observation increases the weight of the two-red-ball box, because that box is more likely to yield a red first draw.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $B$ count the red balls among the $n$ added balls. By <independence of random variables>, $B$ has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $n,1/2$. The total red count is $R=1+B$, and the box has $n+1$ balls. Conditional on $R=r$, <sampling without replacement> gives
$$
\mathbb P(D_1\mid R=r)=\frac r{n+1},\qquad\mathbb P(D_1\cap D_2\mid R=r)=\frac{r(r-1)}{n(n+1)},
$$
where $D_1,D_2$ denote red first and second draws. Averaging these <conditional probabilities> and taking their ratio gives
$$
\mathbb P(D_2\mid D_1)=\frac{\mathbb E[R(R-1)]}{n\mathbb E[R]}.
$$
The <binomial distribution> moments give $\mathbb E[B]=n/2$ and $\mathbb E[B^2]=n/4+n^2/4$. Consequently
$$
\mathbb E[R]=\frac{n+2}{2},\qquad\mathbb E[R(R-1)]=\mathbb E[B^2+B]=\frac{n(n+3)}4.
$$
Therefore the <red-ball prediction with one certain red ball> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(D_2\mid D_1)=\frac{n+3}{2(n+2)},\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
It reduces to $2/3$ at $n=1$ and approaches $1/2$ as the number of randomly coloured extra balls grows. Taking an unweighted average of the remaining red fractions would miss the change in weights caused by taking a <conditional probability> given the first red draw.

For the <discrete uniform distribution>, each value has <probability> $1/n$. Using the given finite sums,
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\frac1n\sum_{r=1}^n r=\frac{n+1}{2},\qquad\mathbb E[X^2]=\frac1n\sum_{r=1}^n r^2=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6.

Subtracting the square of the <expected value> gives the <variance>:
$$
\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}6-\frac{(n+1)^2}4=\frac{(n+1)(n-1)}{12}.
$$
Hence
$$

$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

<Independence of random variables> factors the <joint probability mass function> as $\mathbb P(X=x,Y=y)=\mathbb P(X=x)\mathbb P(Y=y)$. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[f(X)g(Y)]
&=\sum_x\sum_y f(x)g(y)\mathbb P(X=x,Y=y)\\
&=\sum_x\sum_y f(x)\mathbb P(X=x)g(y)\mathbb P(Y=y)\\
&=\left(\sum_x f(x)\mathbb P(X=x)\right)\left(\sum_y g(y)\mathbb P(Y=y)\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
With finite <expected values>, these rearrangements are justified by absolute convergence: the analogous sum with absolute values equals $\mathbb E[|f(X)|]\mathbb E[|g(Y)|]<\infty$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[f(X)g(Y)]=\mathbb E[f(X)]\mathbb E[g(Y)].}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume the <variances> are finite. Expand the square and apply <linearity of expectation>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{var}(X+Y)
&=\mathbb E[X^2]+2\mathbb E[XY]+\mathbb E[Y^2]-(\mathbb E[X]+\mathbb E[Y])^2\\
&=\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)+2\bigl(\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Part (i), applied to the identity <functions>, makes the bracketed <covariance> zero by <independence of random variables>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y).}
$$

===== Solution

This numbered item specifies the model used below. A convenient <sample space> is the collection of $36$ ordered pairs $(s_1,s_2)\in\{1,\ldots,6\}^2$, each with <probability> $1/36$. The corresponding <random variables> are determined by the sum and difference. Inverting the two equations gives
$$
\boxed{S_1=\frac{X+Y}{2},\qquad S_2=\frac{X-Y}{2}.}
$$
Thus a candidate pair $(X,Y)=(i,j)$ is possible exactly when $(i+j)/2$ and $(i-j)/2$ are both <integers> between $1$ and $6$. In particular, $i$ and $j$ must have the same parity. This inverse description will determine the <joint probability mass function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Part (a) with $n=6$ gives $\mathbb E[S_1]=\mathbb E[S_2]=7/2$ and $\operatorname{var}(S_1)=\operatorname{var}(S_2)=35/12$. By <linearity of expectation>,
$$
\mathbb E[X]=7,\qquad\mathbb E[Y]=0.
$$
<Independence of random variables> and $\operatorname{var}(-S_2)=\operatorname{var}(S_2)$ give the same <variance> for the sum and difference:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=7,\quad\mathbb E[Y]=0,\qquad\operatorname{var}(X)=\operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac{35}{6}.}
$$

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The identity $XY=(S_1+S_2)(S_1-S_2)=S_1^2-S_2^2$ gives $\mathbb E[XY]=0$, since the two rolls have the same <probability distribution>. Also $\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]=7\cdot0=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]=0.}
$$
The two <random variables> are <uncorrelated random variables>, but that alone does not establish <independence of random variables>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Count the ordered roll pairs producing each value. The <probability mass functions> are
$$
\mathbb P(X=i)=\frac{6-|i-7|}{36}\quad(i=2,\ldots,12),\qquad\mathbb P(Y=j)=\frac{6-|j|}{36}\quad(j=-5,\ldots,5),
$$
and are zero outside these ranges. Both counts have a unique peak:
$$
\boxed{i=7,\quad j=0;\qquad\mathbb P(X=7)=\mathbb P(Y=0)=\frac16.}
$$
However, $Y=0$ forces equal rolls, whose sum is even. Hence $\mathbb P(X=7,Y=0)=0$, whereas the product of the two marginal <probabilities> is $1/36>0$. This disproves <independence of random variables>:
$$
\boxed{X\text{ and }Y\text{ are not independent}.}
$$

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

First restrict to <integers> in the marginal supports: $2\leq i\leq12$ and $-5\leq j\leq5$. Their marginal <probabilities> are positive. By part (i), a feasible joint pair identifies a unique ordered pair of rolls, so its joint <probability> is $1/36$; an infeasible pair has joint <probability> zero.

Put $a_i=6-|i-7|$ and $b_j=6-|j|$. If the two <events> were independent, a feasible pair would require
$$
\frac1{36}=\frac{a_i}{36}\frac{b_j}{36},\qquad\text{so }a_ib_j=36.
$$
Since $1\leq a_i,b_j\leq6$, equality forces $a_i=b_j=6$, hence $(i,j)=(7,0)$. But that pair is infeasible. An infeasible pair cannot give independence either, since its joint <probability> is zero while the product of its positive marginal <probabilities> is positive. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{There are no independent pairs of these events within the two marginal supports}.}
$$
If all real values of $i,j$ are allowed literally, an impossible <event> is independent of every <event>. The complete answer without a support restriction is therefore
$$
\boxed{\{X=i\}\text{ and }\{Y=j\}\text{ are independent iff }i\notin\{2,\ldots,12\}\text{ or }j\notin\{-5,\ldots,5\}.}
$$

===== Solution

On $0<x<2$, $2x-x^2=x(2-x)>0$. A valid <probability density function> must integrate to one, so
$$
1=\alpha\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,dx=\alpha\left[x^2-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\frac43\alpha.
$$
Thus $\alpha=3/4$, which is positive and ensures nonnegativity. Its <expected value> is
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\frac34\int_0^2(2x^2-x^3)\,dx=\frac34\left[\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2=1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Valid with }\alpha=\frac34,\qquad\mathbb E[X]=1.}
$$
The <probability density function> is symmetric around $1$, providing a second check on the <expected value>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The factor $2x-x^3=x(2-x^2)$ is positive on $(0,\sqrt2)$ and negative on $(\sqrt2,2)$. For every nonzero $\alpha$, the proposed <probability density function> is therefore negative on an interval. The choice $\alpha=0$ has total integral zero. In fact,
$$
\int_0^2(2x-x^3)\,dx=\left[x^2-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2=0,
$$
so no scaling could normalise it even without the sign obstruction. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{No value of }\alpha\text{ gives a probability density function; no corresponding expectation exists}.}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume $a<b$. An increasing <affine map> sends the two endpoints to zero and one when $ca+d=0$ and $cb+d=1$. Solving gives
$$
\boxed{c=\frac1{b-a},\qquad d=-\frac a{b-a}.}
$$
Indeed, for $0\leq t\leq1$, the <cumulative distribution function> of the transformed <random variable> is
$$
\mathbb P\left(\frac{V-a}{b-a}\leq t\right)=\mathbb P(V\leq a+(b-a)t)=\frac{a+(b-a)t-a}{b-a}=t,
$$
which is the <cumulative distribution function> of the <continuous uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$.

A decreasing <affine map> works too, by swapping the endpoints:
$$
\boxed{c=-\frac1{b-a},\qquad d=\frac b{b-a}.}
$$
These are the two possible choices, since the image interval must have endpoints zero and one. The case $a=b$ cannot be transformed into a nondegenerate <continuous uniform distribution> by deterministic constants.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $W=U^2$. On $0\leq U\leq1$, squaring preserves order. Therefore for $0\leq w\leq1$,
$$
F_W(w)=\mathbb P(U^2\leq w)=\mathbb P(U\leq\sqrt w)=\sqrt w.
$$
Including the tails gives the <cumulative distribution function>
$$
\boxed{F_W(w)=\begin{cases}0&w<0,\\\sqrt w&0\leq w\leq1,\\1&w>1.\end{cases}}
$$
Differentiating on the interior of the support gives the <probability density function>
$$
\boxed{f_W(w)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1{2\sqrt w}&0<w<1,\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
The divergence near zero is integrable: $\int_0^1(2\sqrt w)^{-1}\,dw=1$. There is no point mass at zero, and the assigned value of a <probability density function> at a single endpoint does not affect the <probability distribution>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Complete the square in the transformation:
$$
Y=X(2-X)=1-(X-1)^2.
$$
Since $0<X<2$ almost surely, $0<Y\leq1$. For $0\leq y<1$, put $r=\sqrt{1-y}$. The <event> $Y>y$ is equivalent to $1-r<X<1+r$. Integrating the given <probability density function> over both branches at once gives the <survival function>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(Y>y)&=\int_{1-r}^{1+r}\frac x2\,dx
=\frac{(1+r)^2-(1-r)^2}{4}=r.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, including the tails,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y>y)=\begin{cases}1&y<0,\\\sqrt{1-y}&0\leq y<1,\\0&y\geq1.\end{cases}}
$$
The <cumulative distribution function> on $(0,1)$ is $1-\sqrt{1-y}$. Differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1{2\sqrt{1-y}}&0<y<1,\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Both inverse branches matter: $x=1\pm\sqrt{1-y}$. The endpoint divergence is integrable, and $\mathbb P(Y=1)=\mathbb P(X=1)=0$, so no endpoint atom is needed.
