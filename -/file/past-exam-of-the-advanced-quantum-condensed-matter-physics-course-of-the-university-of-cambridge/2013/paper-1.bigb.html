= Solution
{parent=1}

The <static Lindhard dielectric function> describes the real, zero-frequency <linear response> of a uniform <free electron gas>. Here $\epsilon_1=\operatorname{Re}\epsilon$, $\mathbf r$ is the position operator, and $\mathbf q$ is the perturbation <wavevector>, $q=|\mathbf q|$, $V$ the normalization volume, $e>0$ the elementary charge, and $\epsilon_0$ the vacuum <permittivity>. The normalized one-electron states are $\psi_{\mathbf k}=V^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ with $E_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e)$; $f(E)$ is their <Fermi-Dirac distribution> occupation. Both momentum sums exclude spin; the prefactor two counts the two equal <spin> populations. The density-operator matrix element is $\delta_{\mathbf k',\mathbf k+\mathbf q}$. Coincident energy denominators are interpreted by the static limit or <Cauchy principal value>.

A weak total <electric potential> $\phi_{\rm tot}$ perturbs an electron by $-e\phi_{\rm tot}$. First-order state mixing produces $\delta n=e\Pi_0(q)\phi_{\rm tot}$, where
$$
\Pi_0(q)=\frac2V\sum_{\mathbf k}\frac{f(E_{\mathbf k})-f(E_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q})}{E_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-E_{\mathbf k}}.
$$
The induced charge is $-e\delta n$. Self-consistent <Poisson equation> screening then gives $\phi_{\rm ext}=\epsilon\phi_{\rm tot}$, with $\epsilon=1+e^2\Pi_0/(\epsilon_0q^2)$. This is the <random phase approximation>: use the independent-electron response to the self-consistent total field.<Lecture Notes for Quantum Matter by Steven H. Simon>[Chapter8,§8.3, printed pp.123–126: density response and the Lindhard function]{ref}

At zero temperature $f(E_{\mathbf k})=\Theta(k_F-k)$. Shifting the integration variable in the second occupation term gives
$$
\epsilon-1=\frac{4m_ee^2}{\epsilon_0\hbar^2q^2}\int_{k<k_F}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\left(\frac1{q^2+2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf q}+\frac1{q^2-2\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf q}\right).
$$
The two angular integrals are equal. Using the supplied integral, with $x=q/(2k_F)$, gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon(q,0)=1+\frac{\kappa^2}{q^2}F(x),\qquad
\kappa^2=\frac{m_ee^2k_F}{\epsilon_0\pi^2\hbar^2},\qquad
F(x)=\frac12+\frac{1-x^2}{4x}\log\left|\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right|.}
$$
The limits are $F(0)=1$, $F(1)=1/2$ and $F(x)\sim1/(3x^2)$ for large $x$. The first recovers <Thomas-Fermi screening>; the logarithm produces the <Kohn singularity of static Lindhard screening> at $q=2k_F$.

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\rho(\mathbf q)=\int d^3r\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\rho(\mathbf r)$. A point charge $Ze$ has total screened charge $Ze/\epsilon(q)$ and induced charge $-Ze[1-\epsilon(q)^{-1}]$. Angular inversion therefore gives the <radial induced-charge screening kernel>
$$
\rho_{\rm ind}(r)=-\frac{Ze}{r}\int_0^\infty dq\,g(q)\sin(qr),\qquad
\boxed{g(q)=\frac{q}{2\pi^2}\left[1-\frac1{\epsilon(q)}\right]=\frac{q\kappa^2F(q/2k_F)}{2\pi^2[q^2+\kappa^2F(q/2k_F)]}.}
$$
In particular, $\rho_{\rm ind}(\mathbf q=0)=-Ze$: the total screening charge is negative although its local density can oscillate in sign.

Integrate the sine transform by parts twice; $g(0)=g(\infty)=0$ removes the boundary terms. For the <Friedel density tail from a derivative singularity>, write $Q=2k_F$ and $g''(q)\sim C/(q-Q)$. Then
$$
\rho_{\rm ind}(r)=\frac{Ze}{r^3}\int_0^\infty g''(q)\sin(qr)\,dq
\sim\frac{ZeC\pi}{r^3}\cos(Qr).
$$
Indeed, near $Q$ the <principal value> transform is $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin[(Q+s)r]\,ds/s=\pi\cos(Qr)$ for $r>0$. Smooth terms do not remove this leading oscillatory long-range contribution. Thus **$\rho_{\rm ind}\propto\cos(2k_Fr)/r^3$**.

For a three-dimensional, single-valley gas with two <spin> states, the <Friedel oscillation wavelength from carrier density> is
$$
\boxed{k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=3.90\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{\pi}{k_F}=8.06\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Low-temperature <scanning tunneling spectroscopy> near exposed dopants can map the oscillatory <local density of states>, with a Fourier feature near $2k_F$ at the <Fermi energy>. An energy-resolved tunnelling image measures local spectral weight, not directly the bulk energy-integrated charge: comparing occupied-bias maps and accounting for the surface geometry is necessary. In actual <GaAs>, the background <relative permittivity> and band <effective mass> change screening strengths; they do not change this wavelength at the stated density.

The <Mott two-current model> treats the two <spin> populations as parallel conduction channels when <spin-flip scattering> is weak. Thus $\sigma=\sigma_\uparrow+\sigma_\downarrow$ and $\rho^{-1}=\rho_\uparrow^{-1}+\rho_\downarrow^{-1}$. Light, broad-band carriers conduct efficiently; scattering into narrow $d$-like states relaxes their current. In a simple spin-conserving $s$–$d$ picture,
$$
\tau_\sigma^{-1}\propto|V_{sd,\sigma}|^2D_{d,\sigma}(E_F),\qquad
\sigma_\sigma\simeq\frac{e^2}{3}D_\sigma(E_F)\langle v_\sigma^2\tau_\sigma\rangle.
$$
In <copper>, the filled $d$ bands supply little final-state phase space at $E_F$, so both spin channels have low resistivity. In <cobalt>, the nearly filled majority $d$ band suppresses majority-channel scattering, while minority $d$ states remain available. The stated <iron> reversal corresponds to stronger scattering-weighted majority-channel phase space: its majority $d$ band is not closed as in cobalt. Band filling, exchange splitting, velocities and scattering matrix elements set this <scattering-weighted spin-channel asymmetry>; majority population alone does not select the better conductor. **The given iron ordering is a material/scattering input, not a universal consequence of the two-current postulate.** Different impurities and interfaces can change the asymmetry.<Spin dependent transport: GMR & TMR by Alain Schuhl and Daniel Lacour>[§2.1, printed p.947: intrinsic transport weights and impurity-dependent asymmetry]{ref}

= Solution

The spacer electrons mediate <interlayer exchange coupling>. Spin-dependent reflection at the two magnetic interfaces changes their electronic energy as the spacer thickness varies. In the <oscillatory spacer-mediated exchange> picture, Fermi-surface spanning wavevectors produce terms such as $J(t_2)\propto\cos(Qt_2+\varphi)$ with a decaying envelope; real spacers can have several periods.

Define the coupling energy per area by $E_{\rm ex}=-J(t_2)\hat{\mathbf m}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf m}_2$. **$J>0$ favours parallel moments and $J<0$ antiparallel moments.** The oscillation period and phase depend on spacer band structure and interface reflection; there is no universal monotone rule or numerical crossover thickness from $t_2$ alone. Roughness and nonuniform thickness average and weaken the coupling. This is conduction-electron-mediated exchange, not a requirement that the nonmagnetic spacer itself be ferromagnetic.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $D=t_1+t_2$, $a=t_1\rho_{\rm FM}^L+t_2\rho_{\rm NM}$ and $b=t_1\rho_{\rm FM}^H+t_2\rho_{\rm NM}$. In the <CIP thickness-averaged two-current model>, assume spin is conserved and electron trajectories sample the layers, so each channel's effective resistivity is the thickness average: $\bar\rho_L=a/D$, $\bar\rho_H=b/D$. This uses averaged relaxation rates with comparable transport weights, neglecting additional interface resistance. Here $\rho_{\rm NM}$ is the per-spin-channel resistivity, twice the measured nonmagnetic bulk resistivity. The stated long <mean free path> motivates sampling more than one layer.

A four-layer period has thickness $2D$. For an in-plane square, $R_\sigma=\bar\rho_\sigma L/(2DL)=\bar\rho_\sigma/(2D)$, hence the two parallel spin conductances add:
$$
\boxed{G_P=\frac1{R_{\uparrow,\uparrow}}=2D^2\left(\frac1a+\frac1b\right).}
$$
The square's side $L$ cancels. The layers are not being treated as four macroscopic resistors in series along the in-plane current; the averaging is over the traversing carriers' scattering histories.

With opposite magnetic moments, a fixed global <spin> is the low-resistivity species in one ferromagnetic layer and the high-resistivity species in the other. Thus both spin channels have the same four-layer averaged resistivity
$$
\bar\rho_{AP}=\frac{t_1\rho_{\rm FM}^L+t_1\rho_{\rm FM}^H+2t_2\rho_{\rm NM}}{2D}=\frac{a+b}{2D}.
$$
Each channel's square resistance is $(a+b)/(4D^2)$, and the two channels run in parallel. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac1{R_{\uparrow,\downarrow}}=\frac{8D^2}{a+b}.}
$$
Both channels encounter the strongly scattering magnetic layer; neither retains the uniformly favourable path available for parallel magnetizations.

An in-plane <magnetic field> lowers the <Zeeman energy> when moments align with it. It competes with antiferromagnetic <interlayer exchange coupling>; the layers generally cant or switch toward a parallel configuration and ultimately saturate if the field overcomes coupling and <magnetic anisotropy>. **The high-resistance antiparallel state evolves toward the lower-resistance parallel state.** The switching path and field scale depend on anisotropy, pinning and coupling strength, which are not numerically specified.

= Solution
{parent=2}
{disambiguate=magnetoresistance}

Combining the two results gives the <GMR ratio for identical thickness-averaged layers>:
$$
R_P=\frac{ab}{2D^2(a+b)},\qquad R_{AP}=\frac{a+b}{8D^2},\qquad
R_{AP}-R_P=\frac{(a-b)^2}{8D^2(a+b)}\ge0.
$$
Thus a field that aligns initially antiparallel moments produces **a resistance decrease**. One spin channel conducts well throughout the aligned stack; in the antiparallel stack both channels sample strong scattering. For the low-resistance-state normalization,
$$
\boxed{\frac{R_{AP}-R_P}{R_P}=\frac{(a-b)^2}{4ab}.}
$$
Normalizing the field-induced drop by $R_{AP}$ instead gives $(a-b)^2/(a+b)^2$. Large channel contrast can produce a large <giant magnetoresistance>; equal channels give none, and common nonmagnetic scattering dilutes the contrast. This is the spin-dependent alignment effect, distinct from <anisotropic magnetoresistance> of an individual magnetic layer.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <organic semiconductor> has conjugated molecular units with strong intramolecular bonding and comparatively weak intermolecular electronic overlap. Optical excitation commonly promotes a $\pi$ electron from an occupied molecular orbital toward an unoccupied one. Weak dielectric screening and narrow bands favour bound <Frenkel excitons>, sometimes mixed with intermolecular charge-transfer states, rather than immediate production of free carriers. The optical excitation energy is below the unbound electron–hole threshold by the <exciton binding energy>, with relaxation conventions specified when comparing measured gaps.

The <electric-dipole interaction> selects bright states through their transition dipoles, producing polarization-dependent absorption in oriented samples. From a singlet ground state, strong electric-dipole absorption normally preserves spin and reaches singlet excitons; direct triplet excitation is weak without spin mixing. The <Franck-Condon principle> treats excitation as vertical on the nuclear-motion timescale. For one displaced harmonic vibration at low temperature, the <displaced-oscillator Franck-Condon progression> has relative strengths
$$
\boxed{I_\nu\propto e^{-S}\frac{S^\nu}{\nu!},\qquad
\hbar\omega_\nu=E_{00}+\nu\hbar\omega_v.}
$$
Here the <Huang-Rhys factor> $S$ measures the displacement and $E_{00}$ is the zero-vibration transition energy. Vibrational relaxation before emission produces a <Stokes shift>. Molecular packing and excitonic coupling shift or split bright features and change their intensities; disorder and vibrations broaden them. Consequently a universal parabolic-band absorption edge is inappropriate for a localized molecular excitation.<Organic Optoelectronics lecture notes by Vladimir Bulović>[Lectures4–7: molecular levels, Franck–Condon shifts, aggregates and Frenkel/charge-transfer excitons]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In <quasi-one-dimensional quantum transport>, transverse confinement makes discrete subbands while motion along a <quantum wire> remains continuous. Only occupied propagating subbands conduct. For elastic scattering between equilibrated reservoirs, the <Landauer formula> is
$$
\boxed{G=\frac{e^2}{h}\sum_{n,\sigma}\int dE\,[-f'(E)]T_{n\sigma}(E).}
$$
The sum explicitly counts spin. A perfectly transmitting spin-degenerate subband gives $2e^2/h$ at low temperature; a <quantum point contact> shows steps as a gate opens additional subbands. The one-dimensional <density of states> cancels the carrier velocity in the current, giving a conductance quantum rather than a material-dependent velocity factor. Finite two-terminal <contact resistance> remains even without internal scattering.

Transport is <ballistic transport> for length $L\ll\ell$, where $\ell$ is the <mean free path>. In a multichannel wire with $\ell\ll L$ below its localization length, scattering reduces transmission and gives approximately Ohmic behaviour. If the <phase coherence length> exceeds the sample length, amplitudes interfere; <weak localization> and sample-dependent <universal conductance fluctuations> can appear. At greater lengths, generic coherent disordered wires undergo <Anderson localization>, with localization length of order the channel number times $\ell$, up to symmetry-dependent factors. Thermal averaging and dephasing smear the quantum features.

Strong interactions require more than independent-electron transmission. A clean single-channel interacting wire is described at low energy by a <Luttinger liquid>, with collective modes and possible <spin-charge separation>; impurities can generate temperature-dependent power laws. Ideal contacts to noninteracting leads can still fix the two-terminal conductance of a clean wire, so it must not automatically be multiplied by a bulk Luttinger parameter.

<Density functional theory> replaces the interacting many-electron <ground state> <wavefunction> by its spatial density $n(\mathbf r)$. For fixed interaction and a nondegenerate ground state, the <Hohenberg-Kohn theorem> fixes the external potential up to an additive constant. If two distinct potentials gave the same density, their strict variational energy inequalities would sum to the contradiction $E_0+E'_0<E_0+E'_0$.

The <density functional variational principle> minimizes
$$
E_v[n]=F[n]+\int v(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r,\qquad
F[n]=\min_{\Psi\to n}\langle\Psi|T+W|\Psi\rangle,
$$
over admissible densities of the correct particle number. The universal $F$ contains the kinetic and interaction energies. This is exact in principle; an explicit useful functional is the practical difficulty.<Inhomogeneous electron gas by Pierre Hohenberg and Walter Kohn>[1964: density-potential uniqueness and density variational principle]{ref}

The <Kohn-Sham equations> introduce noninteracting orbitals with the same density and decompose $F=T_s+E_H+E_{\rm xc}$. With $n=\sum_i f_i|\phi_i|^2$,
$$
\left[-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m_e}+v+v_H[n]+v_{\rm xc}[n]\right]\phi_i=\epsilon_i\phi_i,\qquad
v_H=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r',\quad
v_{\rm xc}=\frac{\delta E_{\rm xc}}{\delta n}.
$$
The <exchange-correlation functional> includes exchange, correlation and the interacting kinetic-energy correction relative to $T_s$. Guess a density, construct the potentials, solve and occupy orbitals, then mix and iterate to <self-consistent field> convergence. The <local-density approximation> uses $E_{\rm xc}^{\rm LDA}=\int n\epsilon_{\rm xc}^{\rm uniform}(n)\,d^3r$; a <generalized gradient approximation> also depends on density gradients.

**Kohn–Sham orbital energies are not generally excitation energies**, and their sum double counts interactions. The <Kohn-Sham energy double-counting correction> is
$$
\boxed{E=\sum_i f_i\epsilon_i-E_H+E_{\rm xc}-\int n v_{\rm xc}\,d^3r,}
$$
with ion–ion energy added for a total crystal energy. Approximate functionals can miss self-interaction cancellation, band gaps and strong correlation; convergence alone does not prove the global ground state.<Self-Consistent Equations Including Exchange and Correlation Effects by Walter Kohn and Lu Jeu Sham>[1965: auxiliary noninteracting system and effective density-dependent equations]{ref}
