= Solution

The <Hartree-Fock method> minimizes the expectation of an interacting-electron <Hamiltonian> over normalized <Slater determinants> of orthonormal one-particle <spin> orbitals. It is a variational, self-consistent mean-field approximation. The determinant enforces fermionic antisymmetry exactly within that class, while each orbital responds to the mean charge <density> and a nonlocal exchange operator generated by the occupied orbitals.

For an <electron gas>, this accounts for kinetic pressure, the average electrostatic field, same-spin exchange and the associated exchange <hole>, and possible <spin polarization>. It does not account for general dynamical <electron correlation>, such as the correlated avoidance of opposite-spin <electrons>, and cannot capture all screening or collective fluctuations merely by optimizing one determinant. Uniform plane waves are an additional restriction, not the definition of Hartree-Fock itself.

In the <energy> functional, the one-body term is the sum of <electron> <kinetic energy> and ionic-potential <energy>. The direct two-body term is the <Hartree energy>: the classical interaction of the mean <electron> <density> with itself. Its factor $1/2$ avoids counting every pair twice. The negative Fock term comes from exchanging the two indistinguishable <electron> labels in the antisymmetric state. For a spin-independent interaction, <spin> <orthogonality> makes this exchange act only between equal <spin> components. For an orbital interacting with itself, direct and exchange contributions cancel exactly; the exchange term removes the spurious one-electron self-interaction. Ionic background terms and their prescribed cancellation must be included consistently with the direct term.

Use normalized plane-wave <spin> orbitals $V^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\chi_\sigma$ and fill a separate <Fermi sphere> for each <spin> species. Each <spin> state counts once, so
$$
n_\sigma=\int_{|\mathbf k|<k_{F\sigma}}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}
=\frac{k_{F\sigma}^3}{6\pi^2}.
$$
Its <kinetic energy> per <volume> is
$$
\frac{T_\sigma}{V}=\int_{|\mathbf k|<k_{F\sigma}}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_e}
=\frac{\hbar^2 k_{F\sigma}^5}{20\pi^2m_e}
=A n_\sigma^{5/3},
\qquad
\boxed{A=\frac{3\hbar^2}{10m_e}(6\pi^2)^{2/3}.}
$$
The spin-independent contact interaction has uncancelled direct <energy> $E_H/V=\gamma n^2/2$, with $n=n_\uparrow+n_\downarrow$. By the imposed background assumption this direct contribution cancels. For the Fock term the contact delta function sets the two spatial positions equal; its <spin> factors then select equal spins. The local one-body <density> matrix for each <spin> has diagonal value $n_\sigma$, giving
$$
\frac{E_X}{V}=-\frac\gamma2\sum_\sigma n_\sigma^2.
$$
Combining these results gives the <contact-interaction Hartree-Fock spin energy>
$$
\boxed{\frac{E_0}{V}=A(n_\uparrow^{5/3}+n_\downarrow^{5/3})
-\frac\gamma2(n_\uparrow^2+n_\downarrow^2).}
$$
Here $\gamma$ has units of <energy> times <volume>. The result is the model with the stated background subtraction; an isolated contact-interacting gas without that subtraction retains its direct contribution.

For the unpolarized gas write $x=n/2$. The positive direct <Hartree energy> before background cancellation and the <kinetic energy> are
$$
\frac{E_H}{V}=\frac\gamma2(2x)^2=2\gamma x^2,
\qquad \frac TV=2Ax^{5/3}.
$$
Their ratio is therefore
$$
\boxed{r_s=\frac{E_H}{T}=\frac\gamma A\left(\frac n2\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
This compares the separate direct and kinetic terms. The net Hartree-plus-background contribution after the stipulated cancellation is zero, so it would be incorrect to interpret $r_s$ as that residual contribution divided by $T$. The exchange-energy magnitude in the unpolarized state is $E_H/2$, not $E_H$.

At fixed total <density>, the unpolarized and fully polarized candidate energies are
$$
\frac{E_u}{V}=A2^{-2/3}n^{5/3}-\frac{\gamma n^2}{4},\qquad
\frac{E_p}{V}=An^{5/3}-\frac{\gamma n^2}{2}.
$$
Polarization increases the <kinetic energy> because one <spin> species must occupy a larger <Fermi sphere>, but gains negative exchange <energy>. The fully polarized candidate wins this endpoint comparison if
$$
A n^{5/3}(1-2^{-2/3})-\frac{\gamma n^2}{4}<0.
$$
Using the defined $r_s$ gives
$$
\boxed{r_s>4\,2^{-1/3}(1-2^{-2/3})=2^{5/3}-2=1.174802\ldots.}
$$
Thus the specified two-state comparison indeed predicts a polarized state below the unpolarized state's <energy> for $r_s>1.175$.

The <endpoint and continuous polarization thresholds of a contact electron gas> are different. To check the actual onset within the same uniform Hartree-Fock model, allow $P=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/n$ to vary continuously. In units $2A(n/2)^{5/3}$,
$$
f(P)=\frac{(1+P)^{5/3}+(1-P)^{5/3}}2-\frac{r_s}{2}(1+P^2),
$$
$$
f(P)-f(0)=\left(\frac59-\frac{r_s}{2}\right)P^2+\frac5{243}P^4+O(P^6).
$$
The unpolarized state loses local stability already at **$r_s=10/9\simeq1.111$**. Full polarization becomes the minimizing endpoint only when $f'(1)\le0$, or $r_s\ge(5/6)2^{2/3}\simeq1.323$. Between those values the optimum is partly polarized. Consequently $1.175$ is the requested endpoint crossing, not the exact onset of spontaneous polarization after all <spin> populations are allowed.

The central limitation is that a <Hartree-Fock electron gas> includes exchange but omits the correlation <energy> that is not representable by one determinant. Opposite-spin <electrons> can lower their repulsion by avoiding one another in a correlated unpolarized state. Hartree-Fock misses that avoidance while retaining the exchange advantage of aligned spins, so it need not place a magnetic instability at a reliable interaction strength. The relevant <energy> differences between competing <spin> states can be much smaller than their total energies. Correlation calculations of the <electron> gas explicitly address this competition.<Ground State of the Electron Gas by a Stochastic Method by D. M. Ceperley and B. J. Alder>[Author manuscript, physical PDF pages 4–8: competing phases, correlated trial functions and <energy> differences]{ref}

There are further model restrictions. A constant contact coupling discards the <momentum> dependence of the <Coulomb interaction> and the resulting nontrivial screening. Real materials have a <lattice>, band structure and material-dependent effective interactions. Spatial magnetic textures or other competing states are excluded by a comparison restricted to uniform Fermi spheres. Even before those changes, part c's two endpoints do not find the continuous polarization onset.

The symbol $r_s$ also needs care. Here it is an <energy> ratio proportional to $\gamma n^{1/3}$. The usual <dimensionless Wigner-Seitz radius> of a three-dimensional Coulomb <electron> gas is $r_s^{\rm WS}=[3/(4\pi n)]^{1/3}/a_B$, proportional to $n^{-1/3}$. They are not the same <density> parameter. A quoted experimental value of the latter cannot be compared numerically to $1.175$ without a model-specific mapping. **The contact interaction, missing correlations, restricted variational class and parameter convention prevent the number $1.175$ from being a quantitative experimental prediction.** The paper supplies no particular experimental threshold, so none is invented here.

<Landau quasiparticles> describe the long-lived low-energy excitations of an interacting normal <Fermi liquid>. Starting from a free <Fermi gas>, imagine switching on interactions without changing the phase or destroying the one-to-one labelling of low-energy states. The resulting excitation carries the quantum numbers of a dressed particle but has a renormalized <energy> and interactions with other excitations. Its microscopic <wavefunction> need not resemble a bare <electron> state. The occupied momenta define a <Fermi surface>, and the quasiparticle <energy> measured from the <chemical potential> vanishes on that surface.

The <Landau quasiparticle energy functional> expresses the change near equilibrium in terms of occupation changes:
$$
\delta(E-\mu N)=\sum_{\mathbf p\sigma}\xi^*_{\mathbf p}\delta n_{\mathbf p\sigma}
+\frac1{2V}\sum_{\mathbf p\sigma,\mathbf p'\sigma'}
f_{\sigma\sigma'}(\mathbf p,\mathbf p')\delta n_{\mathbf p\sigma}\delta n_{\mathbf p'\sigma'}.
$$
Differentiation gives the <energy> of one quasiparticle in the distorted distribution. This is a phenomenological expansion around the true interacting ground state, not the assertion that its total <energy> equals that of independent particles. For an isotropic system, spin-symmetric and spin-antisymmetric components of $f$ are expanded in Legendre polynomials. Multiplication by the appropriate Fermi-surface <density of states> defines dimensionless <Landau parameters> $F_l^s,F_l^a$. The original formulation relates a change in the distribution to the <energy> shift.<On the Theory of the Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Page 70, quasiparticle interaction and distribution variation]{ref}

<Pauli blocking> makes the low-energy excitations sharp. The phase space for a quasiparticle to scatter while creating another particle–<hole> pair shrinks as the <Fermi surface> is approached, giving the regular three-dimensional <low-energy Fermi-liquid quasiparticle lifetime>
$$
\tau^{-1}\propto(\epsilon-\mu)^2+(\pi k_BT)^2.
$$
Thus at zero <temperature> its width is small compared with its excitation <energy>. The single-particle spectral function has a pole with <quasiparticle residue> $Z$, generally less than one, and an incoherent remainder. In a conventional <Fermi liquid> the occupation distribution has a discontinuity of height $Z$ at the <Fermi surface>, rather than a unit free-particle step.

For an isotropic three-dimensional system, define $N^*(0)$ to include both spins. The quasiparticle <density of states> determines the low-temperature electronic <heat capacity>, $C_V/V=(\pi^2/3)k_B^2N^*(0)T$. In the usual Landau-parameter normalization, the <spin> response is modified by $1/(1+F_0^a)$ and the <compressibility> by $1/(1+F_0^s)$. In a Galilean-invariant continuum, $m^*/m=1+F_1^s/3$; that particular relation must not be imposed unchanged on a crystal band. A denominator approaching zero signals a possible instability of the assumed normal state.

The same interaction functional and a kinetic equation describe collective <zero sound> when the excitation frequency exceeds the collision rate. Ordinary <first sound> is the collision-dominated hydrodynamic limit. **The theory organizes thermodynamics, response and transport through a small set of measured effective parameters while explaining why interactions coexist with long-lived fermionic excitations.** It is not applicable without qualification in phases where the Fermi-liquid quasiparticle pole disappears or a gap opens over the relevant <Fermi surface>.

A <Wannier-Mott exciton> is a bound conduction-band <electron> and valence-band <hole> whose envelope extends over many <lattice> cells. That large radius permits an <effective mass> description and dielectric screening of the <electron>–<hole> attraction. With <electron> and <hole> masses $m_e^*,m_h^*$, define reduced mass $\mu=m_e^*m_h^*/(m_e^*+m_h^*)$ and center-of-mass mass $M=m_e^*+m_h^*$. The bulk relative-motion <Hamiltonian> is hydrogenic with an appropriate <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r$:
$$
H_{\rm rel}=-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2\mu}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r},\qquad
a_X=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\hbar^2}{\mu e^2},\qquad
R_X=\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)^2\hbar^2}.
$$
A <semiconductor> <quantum well> confines the <electron> and <hole> perpendicular to the plane while leaving approximately free in-plane center-of-mass motion. If the well width is small compared with the bulk <exciton Bohr radius>, their relative motion becomes quasi-two-dimensional. The ideal zero-width, same-dielectric limit is
$$
H_\rho=-\frac{\hbar^2\nabla_\rho^2}{2\mu}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\rho},\qquad
\boxed{E_n=-\frac{R_X}{(n-1/2)^2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Its ground-state binding is $4R_X$, compared with $R_X$ in three dimensions, and the ground envelope decays on the scale $a_X/2$. Stronger binding follows because confinement prevents the pair from separating in the third direction.

For a <finite-width quantum-well exciton>, average the Coulomb attraction over the normalized <electron> and <hole> confinement envelopes:
$$
V_{\rm eff}(\rho)=-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}
\int dz_e\,dz_h\,
\frac{|\chi_e(z_e)|^2|\chi_h(z_h)|^2}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z_e-z_h)^2}}.
$$
This smooths the strict planar singularity and changes the binding. The ideal factor four is a useful reference, not a universal value for every real well. Finite barriers, dielectric mismatch, several confinement subbands and nonparabolic valence bands can require a coupled treatment.

Optical absorption creates a nearly zero in-plane center-of-mass wavevector <exciton>. For the lowest confinement subbands, its line lies approximately at
$$
\hbar\omega=E_g+E_{e,z}+E_{h,z}-E_b+\frac{\hbar^2K_\parallel^2}{2M}.
$$
The discrete bound-state peaks occur below the free <electron>–<hole> continuum. Dipole-allowed envelope states have nonzero relative <wavefunction> at zero separation; the absorption strength also contains the confinement overlap $|\int\chi_e^*(z)\chi_h(z)\,dz|^2$. Confinement can increase the overlap and the binding, making excitonic optical structure more prominent. A perpendicular electric field shifts and separates the confinement envelopes, changing the transition <energy> and usually reducing their optical overlap: this is the <quantum-confined Stark effect>.

\Image[paper-1-exciton-series.png]

**<Quantum wells> tune both the continuum threshold and the bound-state <energy>; an optical line shift is not automatically a change in binding alone.** The hydrogenic envelope model requires a Wannier-sized state, rather than an <exciton> localized to one <lattice> cell.

Experimental tests of <BCS theory> compare several properties of the same superconducting state. In the conventional weak-coupling setting, phonon-mediated attraction pairs opposite momenta and spins, and the <BCS quasiparticle spectrum> is $E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+\Delta^2(T)}$. Agreement with a single <energy> scale and its <temperature> dependence is more discriminating than observing zero resistance alone.

The <superconducting isotope effect> tests the <lattice>'s role. Replacing one <isotope> by another changes ionic masses while leaving the chemical valence nearly unchanged. <Phonon> frequencies scale approximately as $M^{-1/2}$, so a weak-coupling <phonon> mechanism predicts $T_c\propto M^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha\simeq1/2$ when other parameters stay fixed. The shift was measured in mercury before the microscopic pairing theory was developed.<Isotope Effect in the Superconductivity of Mercury by Emanuel Maxwell>[1950 primary report, transition-temperature comparison of mercury <isotopes>]{ref} Deviations from $1/2$ can result from Coulomb, anharmonic and strong-coupling effects; the <isotope> observation supports lattice-mediated pairing without uniquely proving every assumption of the simplest model.

Thermodynamic measurements test the gapped excitations and condensation <energy>. An isotropic fully gapped <superconductor> has exponentially small low-temperature electronic <heat capacity>, rather than the normal linear law. At the transition the <weak-coupling BCS thermodynamic ratios> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{2\Delta(0)}{k_BT_c}=2\pi e^{-\gamma_E}\simeq3.528,
\qquad \frac{C_s(T_c^-)-C_n(T_c)}{C_n(T_c)}
=\frac{12}{7\zeta(3)}\simeq1.426.}
$$
Here $C_n$ means the electronic contribution after subtracting the <lattice> background. The gap closes at $T_c$, and the same framework predicts the <temperature> dependence of penetration depth and thermodynamic critical field. These quantities were quantitative comparisons in the original theory.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[Abstract and thermodynamic discussion: <energy> gap, specific heat and penetration depth]{ref}

<Electron> tunnelling measures the excitation spectrum more directly. A normal-metal–insulator–<superconductor> junction has, at sufficiently low <temperature> and nearly constant tunnelling matrix element, $dI/dV$ proportional to the superconducting <density of states> at $E=eV$:
$$
\frac{N_s(E)}{N_n(0)}=
\operatorname{Re}\frac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}}.
$$
The suppression below the gap and enhancement at its edges test the <BCS electronic density of states>. Giaever's thin oxide-barrier junction measurements established such energy-gap structure.<Energy Gap in Superconductors Measured by Electron Tunneling by Ivar Giaever>[Physical Review Letters 5, 147–148; tunnel-current and differential-conductance measurements]{ref} For a junction between two <superconductors> the threshold involves the sum of their gaps, so it must not be read as a normal-metal single-gap spectrum without accounting for the two electrodes.

\Image[paper-1-bcs-density-of-states.png]
{title=Ideal BCS density of states illustrating gap-edge spectroscopy; model prediction, not experimental measurements}

Nuclear spin-lattice relaxation supplies a phase-coherence-sensitive test. In the conventional s-wave case, the <Hebel-Slichter coherence peak> just below $T_c$ is followed by a falling rate at lower <temperature>. <Density of states> alone is not enough: the <BCS coherence factors> enter the transition matrix elements. The contrast between nuclear relaxation and ultrasonic attenuation was explained using the paired state, unlike a simple two-fluid population model.<Nuclear Spin Relaxation in Normal and Superconducting Aluminum by L. C. Hebel and Charles Pence Slichter>[Abstract: field-cycling method and comparison of nuclear relaxation with ultrasonic absorption]{ref}

Finally, <magnetic flux> quantization in units $h/(2e)$ and <Josephson effects> support a coherent charge-$2e$ condensate. Those observations establish pairing and phase coherence more broadly; their existence alone does not specify a <phonon> mechanism or an isotropic weak-coupling gap. **The converging <isotope>, spectroscopic, thermodynamic and coherence-factor tests support conventional BCS <superconductivity>, with explicit limits to its simplest universal ratios.**

The first term assigns <energy> $\epsilon_i$ to occupying the localized orbital at site $i$. The hopping term moves an <electron> between neighbouring sites, producing kinetic delocalization and band dispersion; <hermitian> conjugate hops make the <Hamiltonian> <Hermitian>. The final term costs $U>0$ only when both <spin> states occupy the same local orbital. This is a <Hubbard model> with an on-site Coulomb penalty, not a classical interaction between magnetic dipoles.

At one <electron> per site and large $U$, a real hop is energetically costly, but a virtual hop into a doubly occupied intermediate state is possible. Opposite-spin states can exploit these virtual processes and lower their <energy>, while parallel spins cannot occupy the same one-orbital site. The resulting singlet–triplet splitting produces antiferromagnetic <Hubbard kinetic exchange>.

There is a terminology qualification. The paper calls this overlap-mediated process “<direct exchange>” because the hopping is directly between neighbouring magnetic orbitals. In the stricter distinction between <direct Coulomb exchange versus kinetic exchange>, the displayed <Hamiltonian> omits an explicit intersite Coulomb exchange integral. The antiferromagnetic $t^2/U$ mechanism derived below is kinetic exchange; a genuine intersite <direct exchange> term is separate and can compete with it. This distinction also separates the model from ligand-mediated <superexchange> in part f.

Expand the field operator in orthonormal localized orbitals, $\hat\psi(\mathbf r)=\sum_i\phi_i(\mathbf r)c_i$. For a one-electron continuum <Hamiltonian> $h_0=\hat p^2/(2m^*)+V(\mathbf r)$, projection gives
$$
H_0=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}c_i^\dagger c_j,\qquad
h_{12}=\int d^3r\,\phi_1^*(\mathbf r)
\left[\frac{\hat p^2}{2m^*}+V(\mathbf r)\right]\phi_2(\mathbf r).
$$
The coefficient in the displayed <lattice> <Hamiltonian> is $-t_{12}$. Thus the <projected hopping matrix element and sign convention> require
$$
\boxed{-t_{12}=h_{12}=K_{12}+V_{12},\qquad
K_{12}=\int d^3r\,\phi_1^*\frac{\hat p^2}{2m^*}\phi_2.}
$$
<Orthogonality> states $\int\phi_1^*\phi_2=0$; it does not imply $\int\phi_1^*V\phi_2=0$ for a spatially varying potential. If the off-diagonal potential is assumed zero, for example when it is a constant, the hopping coefficient is $h_{12}=K_{12}$. This is the kinetic integral requested by the paper, but it equals $-t_{12}$ under the paper's own leading-minus convention. A convention writing the hopping term with a plus sign would instead call $h_{12}$ itself $t_{12}$.

**The printed positive equality is not a consequence of the stated <orthogonality> assumption and the displayed sign convention.** <Orthogonality does not diagonalize a varying potential> gives an explicit counterexample to the underlying inference. The general projected relation above states the supported result; setting $V_{12}=0$ still leaves $-t_{12}=K_{12}$ in the question's convention. Rephasing an orbital changes both $h_{12}$ and the coefficient of the corresponding operator together; it does not remove this algebraic sign mismatch in a fixed convention. The spectrum in the remaining two-site calculation depends on $|t|^2$, so those parts are unaffected.

Fix canonical single-particle order $(1\uparrow,1\downarrow,2\uparrow,2\downarrow)$ and write the occupation ket as $|n_1n_2n_3n_4\rangle$. The six states in the printed order are $|1010\rangle$, $|1001\rangle$, $|0110\rangle$, $|0101\rangle$, $|1100\rangle$, $|0011\rangle$. The last state is <double occupancy> on site 2.

For a <fermionic raising and lowering operator> acting on mode $j$, the sign is $(-1)^{\sum_{k<j}n_k}$, and annihilation requires that mode occupied while creation requires it empty. Starting from the second basis state, the hop that reaches the last state is
$$
c_{2\uparrow}^\dagger c_{1\uparrow}|1001\rangle
=c_{2\uparrow}^\dagger|0001\rangle=|0011\rangle.
$$
It therefore contributes **$H_{6,2}=-t$**. Starting from the third state instead,
$$
c_{2\downarrow}^\dagger c_{1\downarrow}|0110\rangle
=c_{2\downarrow}^\dagger|0010\rangle=-|0011\rangle,
$$
because the occupied $2\uparrow$ mode precedes $2\downarrow$. The <Hamiltonian>'s leading $-t$ makes **$H_{6,3}=+t$**. Finally $n_{2\uparrow}n_{2\downarrow}|0011\rangle=|0011\rangle$, so **$H_{6,6}=U$**. No other displayed basis state reaches $|0011\rangle$ in one allowed hop. These signs verify the three nonzero entries without assigning distinguishable-particle hopping signs to fermions.

Use $|j\rangle$ for the six printed basis vectors. The three singly occupied triplets are
$$
|T_+\rangle=|1\rangle,\qquad
|T_0\rangle=\frac{|2\rangle+|3\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad
|T_-\rangle=|4\rangle.
$$
Each has **<energy> zero**: either Pauli exclusion prevents hopping to <double occupancy> or the two contributions cancel. Define
$$
|S\rangle=\frac{|2\rangle-|3\rangle}{\sqrt2},\quad
|D_+\rangle=\frac{|5\rangle+|6\rangle}{\sqrt2},\quad
|D_-\rangle=\frac{|5\rangle-|6\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The state $D_-$ has <energy> $U$. In the $S,D_+$ subspace the <Hamiltonian> is $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-2t\\-2t&U\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, with
$$
E_\pm=\frac{U\pm\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}}2.
$$
For $U\gg|t|>0$, the fourth low-energy eigenstate is the normalized singlet
$$
\boxed{|\psi_-\rangle=\mathcal N\left(|S\rangle-\frac{E_-}{2t}|D_+\rangle\right)
\simeq\mathcal N\left(|S\rangle+\frac{2t}{U}|D_+\rangle\right),}
$$
where $\mathcal N=[1+(E_-/(2t))^2]^{-1/2}$. Its <energy> is
$$
\boxed{E_-=-\frac{4t^2}{U}+O(t^4/U^3).}
$$
The **four lowest energies are $-4t^2/U,0,0,0$ to leading order**, with the singlet and triplets just given. The upper pair lie near $U$ and $U+4t^2/U$.

The low-energy <Hamiltonian> can be written
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm eff}=J\left(\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2-\frac14\right),\qquad
J=\frac{4t^2}{U}>0,}
$$
using dimensionless spin-half operators. It places the singlet below the triplet by $J$, hence favors antiferromagnetic alignment. A <lattice> of such bonds can support <antiferromagnetism>, although its ordering pattern also depends on geometry and competing couplings. The physical <energy> gain comes from virtual charge fluctuations, not from a permanent <double occupancy> in the large-$U$ limit.

An overlap estimate must use nearest magnetic-ion separations. For the <face-centered cubic lattice> of Mn, $d_{\rm Mn}=a_{\rm Mn}/\sqrt2=3.147\ \mathrm{\mathring A}$; for the <body-centered cubic lattice> of Fe, $d_{\rm Fe}=\sqrt3a_{\rm Fe}/2=2.477\ \mathrm{\mathring A}$. The supplied radial functions have inverse decay lengths $\kappa_Z=Z/(3a_B)$. Their intersite overlap therefore contains approximately $e^{-\kappa_Zd}$, up to polynomial and angular factors. The decisive exponent difference is
$$
\kappa_{\rm Mn}d_{\rm Mn}-\kappa_{\rm Fe}d_{\rm Fe}
=\frac{25(4.45/\sqrt2)-26(\sqrt3\,2.86/2)}{3(0.529)}
\simeq8.99.
$$
The <exponential orbital-tail estimate of exchange> thus gives a hopping suppression approximately $e^{-8.99}\simeq1.25\times10^{-4}$ before polynomial corrections. Keeping the supplied $Z^{7/2}r^2$ radial prefactor in a midpoint overlap proxy gives
$$
\frac{\phi_{\rm Mn}(d_{\rm Mn}/2)^2}{\phi_{\rm Fe}(d_{\rm Fe}/2)^2}
\sim\left(\frac{25}{26}\right)^7
\left(\frac{d_{\rm Mn}}{d_{\rm Fe}}\right)^4e^{-8.99}
\simeq2.5\times10^{-4}.
$$
This proxy is not an evaluated <hopping integral>, but confirms that the polynomial factors do not undo the exponential loss. If comparable Coulomb denominators and nonexponential prefactors are assumed, a kinetic-exchange scale proportional to $t^2/U$ is suppressed by roughly $10^{-8}$–$10^{-7}$. Direct intersite Coulomb exchange likewise contains products of the small tails. Coordination-number differences cannot compensate for many orders of magnitude.

By contrast, the measured ordering-temperature ratio supplied in the question is **$122/1043\simeq0.117$**, not an exponentially tiny number. **Within this overlap model, direct Mn–Mn exchange cannot explain MnO's substantial magnetic ordering scale by extrapolating the Fe mechanism.** Another exchange path is required.

This is an order-of-magnitude argument, not an exact relation between an itinerant <Curie temperature> and a localized-spin <Néel temperature>. The hydrogenic functions use the printed bare $Z$ values; actual solid-state orbitals are screened and hybridized. Their literal absolute exchange scales should not be treated as quantitative predictions for Fe or MnO.

= Solution
{parent=f}

Use an oxygen-mediated <superexchange> path between two magnetic metal orbitals. A minimal one-active-orbital <three-site charge-transfer superexchange> <Hamiltonian> is clearest in a <hole> representation:
$$
H=\Delta\sum_\sigma n_{p\sigma}
+U_d\sum_{i=1,2}n_{di\uparrow}n_{di\downarrow}
+U_p n_{p\uparrow}n_{p\downarrow}
-t_{pd}\sum_{i=1,2;\sigma}(d_{i\sigma}^\dagger p_\sigma+p_\sigma^\dagger d_{i\sigma}).
$$
Here $d_i$ describes a metal <hole>, $p$ a ligand <hole>, and $\Delta>0$ is the <energy> needed to create the virtual ligand <hole>. The low-energy configuration has one <hole> on each metal orbital and no ligand <hole>, corresponding to a filled bridging ligand orbital in the <electron> picture. $U_d$ and $U_p$ penalize double <hole> occupancy on the corresponding sites.

Virtual metal–ligand hopping transfers charge through oxygen without producing a real mobile carrier. Eliminating the ligand gives an effective metal–metal hopping $t_{dd}^{\rm eff}\sim t_{pd}^2/\Delta$. Combining it with the large metal repulsion gives
$$
\boxed{J_{\rm AF}\sim\frac{4t_{pd}^4}{\Delta^2U_d}>0.}
$$
A ligand double-occupancy channel adds a contribution of the same sign for the simple half-filled straight-bond model, often organized as $J\sim(4t_{pd}^4/\Delta^2)[1/U_d+2/(2\Delta+U_p)]$. The precise coefficients depend on the orbitals and hopping conventions; the essential mechanism is virtual charge transfer plus spin-selective access to intermediate states. The effective <spin> interaction favors antiparallel alignment while the material can remain insulating.

For MnO's <rock salt structure>, the strong approximately $180^\circ$ Mn–O–Mn path joins next-nearest Mn sites. It is not the nearest fcc Mn–Mn separation used in the direct-overlap estimate of part e. The oxygen-mediated path avoids relying on the tiny direct overlap of the two distant 3d tails. A realistic model includes several Mn 3d orbitals, Hund coupling and the high-spin Mn ion; the single-orbital <Hamiltonian> above isolates the antiferromagnetic exchange channel. Approximately $90^\circ$ paths and different orbital occupancies need not have the same sign.
