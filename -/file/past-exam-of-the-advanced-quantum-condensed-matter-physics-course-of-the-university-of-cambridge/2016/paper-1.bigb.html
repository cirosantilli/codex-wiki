= Solution
{parent=1}

The absorptive <dielectric function> counts optically accessible <electron-hole pairs>. In the electric dipole approximation the perturbation is proportional to $\mathbf a\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}$, with $\mathbf a$ the <light> <polarization> and $\hat{\mathbf p}$ the electronic <momentum operator>. The squared <matrix element> between occupied valence and empty conduction states is the transition strength; it includes <polarization> and band-symmetry selection rules. The sums enumerate initial and final subbands. <photon> <momentum> is negligible on the crystal-momentum scale, so the transition is approximately vertical in the in-plane wavevector $\mathbf k$.

For each subband pair, $C$ is the constant-transition-energy contour in the two-dimensional <Brillouin zone>. Its line element is $dl$, and the reciprocal gradient weights the states satisfying <energy> conservation. Specifically, the <level-set representation of the joint density of states> is
$$
\int d^2k\,\delta\!\left(E_{n'}(\mathbf k)-E_n(\mathbf k)-\hbar\omega\right)=\int_C\frac{dl}{|\nabla_{\mathbf k}(E_{n'}-E_n)|}.
$$
A small <energy> gradient means many nearly resonant states; a vanishing gradient can produce a <Van Hove singularity> or an edge. The displayed expression suppresses overall optical prefactors and assumes the <momentum> <matrix element> is approximately constant along each contour. More generally it stays inside that integral. Occupation factors $f_v-f_c$ are also implicit; at low <temperature> in an undoped well the relevant valence states are full and the conduction states empty. This is the independent-particle continuum contribution from <Fermi's golden rule>. Bound <excitons> add optical resonances below the corresponding free-pair thresholds.

The <GaAs four-band model> is rooted in bonding and <spin-orbit coupling>. Near the zone centre, the conduction edge is predominantly s-like and antibonding, while the upper valence manifold is predominantly p-like. Combining <orbital angular momentum> $l=1$ with <spin> $s=1/2$ splits the six p-derived states into a $j=3/2$ quartet and a lower $j=1/2$ doublet. The latter is the <split-off valence band>. Within the upper quartet, the $m_j=\pm3/2$ and $m_j=\pm1/2$ branches, defined relative to a propagation or quantization direction, have different curvatures and are called <heavy holes> and <light holes>. They coincide at the unstrained bulk zone centre but need not remain degenerate in a well. The conduction edge is a <spin> doublet. Thus “four bands” counts conduction, heavy-hole, light-hole and split-off branches, including their <spin> multiplicities; it does not count four individual states. Positive hole masses describe the downward valence curvature, whereas the conduction curvature is upward.

For the final <exciton> comparison, define the <reduced mass> $\mu_j$ and screened <exciton> Rydberg $R_j^*$ by
$$
\frac1{\mu_j}=\frac1{m_e^*}+\frac1{m_{h,j}^*},\qquad R_j^*=\frac{\mu_j}{m_e}\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{12.8^2}.
$$
The resulting $(\mu_j/m_e,R_j^*)$ are approximately $(0.05948,4.937\,\mathrm{meV})$ for <heavy holes>, $(0.03646,3.027\,\mathrm{meV})$ for <light holes> and $(0.04631,3.844\,\mathrm{meV})$ for split-off holes. In the ideal <two-dimensional exciton> model, the first two optically active internal states lie $4R_j^*$ and $4R_j^*/9$ below their continuum edge. Their separation is $32R_j^*/9$, independent of a common threshold offset. The corresponding three-dimensional separation would be only $3R_j^*/4$. The listed peak spacings specified for this theoretical spectrum give
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{subband family}&\text{listed successive pair spacings / meV}&\text{2D prediction / meV}&\text{3D prediction / meV}\\\hline
\mathrm{hh},\ n=1,2,3&18,\ 18,\ 17&17.56&3.70\\
\mathrm{lh},\ n=1,2,3&11,\ 11,\ 10&10.76&2.27
\end{array}
$$
Here each pair comprises the stronger lower-energy peak and its weaker nearby companion, in the order associated with the continuum edges calculated in part (c). **The approximately 18-meV and 11-meV pair splittings support the two-dimensional series and are incompatible with the three-dimensional series at the supplied masses and permittivity.** Equivalently, for a two-dimensional pair with energies $E_1,E_2$, the inferred edge is $E_2+(E_2-E_1)/8$; the observed pair splittings predict ground-state bindings around 20 and 12 meV, close to $4R_j^*$.

Absolute agreement should not be overstated. For example, the stated square-well parameters give the first heavy-hole edge $1.56390$ eV and the first light-hole edge $1.59161$ eV. Their ideal two-dimensional peak predictions are approximately $(1.54415,1.56170)$ eV and $(1.57950,1.59027)$ eV, respectively. The listed peaks are lower by about 1–2 meV across the families; rounded parameters and this simple effective-mass model do not reproduce every absolute <energy> exactly. No gap, width or mass has been silently adjusted to fit them. The peak-spacing comparison remains useful because it avoids a common edge offset. The split-off transitions predicted by the four-band data are discussed separately in part (c), rather than assigned fictitious extra peaks.

At 300 K, $k_BT\simeq25.9$ meV, whereas the ideal ground-state binding energies are only about $19.7$, $12.1$ and $15.4$ meV; excited states are still more weakly bound. **None of these series is expected to remain a clearly resolved, robust bound-exciton peak at room <temperature> in this simple estimate.** Thermal ionization and <phonon> broadening work against their visibility. This is a scale argument, not a theorem that the <exciton> population or all Coulomb-enhanced absorption becomes exactly zero: actual observability also requires temperature-dependent gaps, linewidths, screening and carrier density, which are not supplied.

Choose the growth direction as $z$ and the well as $0<z<L$. In the <envelope function approximation>, write a subband state as $A^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k_\parallel\cdot\mathbf r_\parallel}F_n(z)u_{j0}(\mathbf r)$, where $u_{j0}$ is the band-edge cell-periodic Bloch factor. The <infinite square well> has
$$
F_n(z)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi z}{L},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The envelope has the same shape for <electron> and hole states: changing their <effective masses> changes the eigenenergies, but not these infinite-well <eigenfunctions>. With in-plane <polarization>, the leading interband <momentum> <matrix element> acts on the Bloch factors, while the slow envelopes give
$$
\langle c,n',\mathbf k'|\hat p_x|v,j,n,\mathbf k\rangle\simeq\delta_{\mathbf k'\mathbf k}\langle u_c|\hat p_x|u_{v,j}\rangle\int_0^L F_{n'}^*(z)F_n(z)\,dz.
$$
<orthonormality> therefore yields **the <infinite-well interband selection rule> $n'=n$**. A nonzero band-level <matrix element> is still required; the overlap rule does not replace <polarization> selection rules. Finite barriers can make <electron> and hole envelopes different, and interface asymmetry or band mixing can relax this ideal same-index rule.

For a fixed allowed band/subband pair, parabolic dispersions give $E_c(\mathbf k)-E_v(\mathbf k)=E_{j,n}^{\rm edge}+\hbar^2k_\parallel^2/(2\mu_j)$. If the <photon> <energy> exceeds the edge, the contour is a circle of radius $k_\parallel$ and the gradient magnitude is $\hbar^2k_\parallel/\mu_j$. Thus
$$
\int_C\frac{dl}{|\nabla_{\mathbf k}(E_c-E_v)|}=\frac{2\pi k_\parallel}{\hbar^2k_\parallel/\mu_j}=\frac{2\pi\mu_j}{\hbar^2}.
$$
Below threshold the contour is absent. With the conventional $d^2k/(2\pi)^2$ normalization, the <two-dimensional joint density of states> per area and independent channel is
$$
\boxed{J_{j,n}(\hbar\omega)=\frac{\mu_j}{2\pi\hbar^2}\Theta(\hbar\omega-E_{j,n}^{\rm edge}).}
$$
With a locally energy-independent optical <matrix element>, **each allowed pair therefore adds a constant step** to the continuum <dielectric function> in the approximation of the supplied equation. Different hole families need not have equal step heights: <reduced masses>, <spin> multiplicities and Bloch-state <matrix elements> differ. Frequency-dependent overall optical prefactors, nonparabolicity, broadening and excitonic Coulomb enhancement can modify the literal step shape outside this approximation.

<electron> confinement raises the conduction edge and hole confinement lowers the corresponding valence edge, so their positive confinement energies add. With $L=120\,\mathring{\mathrm A}=12$ nm, the common bare-mass scale is
$$
A_L=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m_eL^2}\simeq0.0026111\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Writing $\eta_e=0.067$ and $\eta_j=m_{h,j}^*/m_e$, the <quantum-well interband thresholds> are
$$
\boxed{E_{j,n}^{\rm edge}=E_{g,j}+A_Ln^2(\eta_e^{-1}+\eta_j^{-1}).}
$$
Use $E_{g,\rm hh}=E_{g,\rm lh}=1.52$ eV and $E_{g,\rm so}=1.86$ eV. The thresholds in the requested window, using the printed masses without fitting, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|r}
\text{hole branch}&n& E_{j,n}^{\rm edge}/\mathrm{eV}\\\hline
\mathrm{hh}&1&1.56390\\
\mathrm{lh}&1&1.59161\\
\mathrm{hh}&2&1.69560\\
\mathrm{lh}&2&1.80645\\
\mathrm{hh}&3&1.91509\\
\mathrm{so}&1&1.91638\\
\mathrm{so}&2&2.08552\\
\mathrm{lh}&3&2.16450
\end{array}
$$
The next heavy-hole and split-off thresholds are about $2.2224$ and $2.3674$ eV, outside the interval. Each threshold marks a continuum onset or shoulder, not the stronger bound-exciton peak below it. The six heavy- and light-hole onsets follow the six paired peak groups in the supplied diagram. Their first members lie roughly 20 and 12 meV below their respective onsets, as the common solution above explains.

A qualification is necessary for the split-off branch. Its p-like $j=1/2$ <bloch states> have nonzero $p_x$ <matrix elements> to the s-like conduction states, so in-plane <polarization> does not generally forbid them. For example, a $j=1/2$ state has a nonzero $p_x\pm ip_y$ orbital component with the appropriate <spin>, which couples to an s-like state through $p_x$. The same-envelope rule therefore allows the two split-off thresholds in the table. The first nearly coincides with the third heavy-hole edge, while the second would give an additional onset near 2.086 eV. **The supplied figure and twelve peak energies appear to retain only the heavy- and light-hole series; they do not specify a split-off-series oscillator strength or identify its extra features.** Omitting the split-off transitions would require an additional approximation beyond the four-band information and <polarization> given. Their expected ideal two-dimensional first-state peaks would be near 1.9010 and 2.0701 eV; these are predictions of the stated model, not additional listed data.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Start from <electrons> moving in the periodic equilibrium ionic potential. Displace ion $l$ by $\mathbf u_l$ and expand its contribution as $V(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_l-\mathbf u_l)\simeq V(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_l)-\mathbf u_l\cdot\nabla V(\mathbf r-\mathbf R_l)$. Quantizing the harmonic <normal modes> expresses $\mathbf u_l$ as a sum of creation and <annihilation operators> with displacement scale $\sqrt{\hbar/(2\rho_{\rm ion}V\omega_{\mathbf q})}$ for a single simple branch. Expanding the electronic field in <bloch states> then gives a band-projected <Fröhlich Hamiltonian>
$$
H=H_0+V_{e\text{-}ph},\qquad H_0=\sum_{\mathbf k\sigma}\epsilon_{\mathbf k}c_{\mathbf k\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf k\sigma}+\sum_{\mathbf q}\Omega_{\mathbf q}b_{\mathbf q}^{\dagger}b_{\mathbf q},
$$
$$
V_{e\text{-}ph}=\sum_{\mathbf k\mathbf q\sigma}M_{\mathbf q}c_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf k\sigma}(b_{\mathbf q}+b_{-\mathbf q}^{\dagger}),\qquad \Omega_{\mathbf q}=\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q}.
$$
Here $c,c^{\dagger}$ obey <canonical anticommutation relations> and remove/create an <electron> with <crystal momentum> $\mathbf k$ and <spin> $\sigma$; $b,b^{\dagger}$ are <boson> operators for lattice modes. The <matrix element> $M_{\mathbf q}$ contains the derivative of the ionic potential, <phonon polarization>, displacement normalization and electronic <wavefunction> overlaps. With phases chosen appropriately, $M_{-\mathbf q}=M_{\mathbf q}^*$ ensures a <Hermitian> Hamiltonian. The displayed one-band, one-branch notation suppresses additional branch and band labels. “<Fröhlich Hamiltonian>” here means the linear electron-phonon model used in the question; it is not a requirement to use specifically the polar-optical-phonon coupling of a polar <semiconductor>. A harmonic lattice, small displacements and a weak enough coupling to organize <perturbation theory> are the underlying approximations.

The <Fröhlich canonical transformation> removes real linear <phonon> operators from the electron-only effective problem to first order. Choose an <anti-Hermitian> $S$ with $[S,H_0]=-V_{e\text{-}ph}$. For $A_{\mathbf k\mathbf q\sigma}=c_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf k\sigma}$ and $\Delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf q}=\epsilon_{\mathbf k+\mathbf q}-\epsilon_{\mathbf k}$, one possible generator is
$$
S=\sum_{\mathbf k\mathbf q\sigma}M_{\mathbf q}A_{\mathbf k\mathbf q\sigma}\left(\frac{b_{\mathbf q}}{\Delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf q}-\Omega_{\mathbf q}}+\frac{b_{-\mathbf q}^{\dagger}}{\Delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf q}+\Omega_{\mathbf q}}\right).
$$
Its <Hermitian> conjugate is its negative after relabelling momenta. The <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> gives
$$
e^SHe^{-S}=H_0+\frac12[S,V_{e\text{-}ph}]+O(M^3).
$$
The <commutator> describes virtual emission and absorption of a <phonon>. <normal ordering> yields an electron-electron term as well as one-electron <energy> renormalizations and <phonon> terms. Retain the former, absorb single-particle shifts into $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}$ and eliminate the explicit <phonon> sector. For elastic pair scattering the two virtual time orderings have the combined amplitude
$$
V_{\rm eff}(\mathbf q,\Delta)=|M_{\mathbf q}|^2\left(\frac1{\Delta-\Omega_{\mathbf q}}-\frac1{\Delta+\Omega_{\mathbf q}}\right)=\frac{2|M_{\mathbf q}|^2\Omega_{\mathbf q}}{\Delta^2-\Omega_{\mathbf q}^2}.
$$
Using the usual factor one-half to count unordered pairs gives the coefficient in the printed Hamiltonian, with $\Delta=\epsilon_{\mathbf k}-\epsilon_{\mathbf k-\mathbf q}$. The two outgoing momenta are $\mathbf k-\mathbf q$ and $\mathbf k'+\mathbf q$, so total <momentum> and the <spins> are conserved. A fully off-shell effective operator can instead use symmetrized denominators for both external legs; the displayed expression is the usual elastic-scattering reduction. Eliminating nearly resonant real <phonons> requires more care than this second-order construction.

For $|\Delta|<\Omega_{\mathbf q}$ the denominator is negative: **virtual <phonon> exchange is attractive in this low-energy channel**. Physically, an <electron> changes the ionic density and a second <electron> can gain <energy> by responding to the delayed distortion. This is a retarded interaction, not an unscreened instantaneous attraction at every <energy> transfer. At large transfer the sign changes, and screened Coulomb repulsion must also be included when deciding the net low-energy pairing strength. The connection between lattice dynamics and <superconductivity> is supported by the isotope effect, but the mathematical attraction alone does not determine a measured transition <temperature>.<Electron-Phonon Interactions and Superconductivity by John Bardeen>[pp. 55–57, lattice-coupling mechanism and isotope-effect motivation]{ref}

For the pair calculation, make the model restrictions explicit. The filled <Fermi sea> is inert, and $|0\rangle$ denotes that sea with no added particles, not an empty electronic vacuum. Project the interaction onto the previously empty shell $0\le\xi_{\mathbf k}\le D$, where $\xi_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)-E_F$ and $D=\hbar\omega_D$. Measure the added-pair <energy> relative to $2E_F$; this is the negative $E$ in the final formula. If absolute one-electron energies are retained in the preceding <eigenvalue> equation, its <eigenvalue> must instead be $E_{\rm abs}=2E_F+E$. A shell-restricted state would not in general be an exact eigenstate of the completely unprojected, all-momentum contact Hamiltonian.

Write $g=\gamma^2>0$ and $|\mathbf k\rangle=c_{-\mathbf k\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf k\uparrow}^{\dagger}|0\rangle$. Within the zero-total-momentum pair subspace the two opposite-spin terms cancel the interaction's factor one-half, while same-spin s-wave terms cancel by fermionic antisymmetry. Thus the projected Hamiltonian acts as
$$
H'_{\rm pair}|\mathbf k\rangle=2\xi_{\mathbf k}|\mathbf k\rangle-g\sum_{\langle\mathbf p\rangle}|\mathbf p\rangle.
$$
For $|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\langle\mathbf k\rangle}\varphi_{\mathbf k}|\mathbf k\rangle$ and $C=\sum_{\langle\mathbf k\rangle}\varphi_{\mathbf k}$, matching each basis coefficient gives
$$
(E-2\xi_{\mathbf k})\varphi_{\mathbf k}=-gC,\qquad \boxed{\varphi_{\mathbf k}=-\frac{\gamma^2C}{E-2\xi_{\mathbf k}}.}
$$
This is the stated coefficient formula in the consistent Fermi-level <energy> convention. Its even dependence on $\mathbf k$ produces an even orbital state with a <spin> singlet. The constant $C$ is fixed up to an overall phase by normalization; for a <bound state>, $|C|^{-2}=g^2\sum_{\langle\mathbf k\rangle}(E-2\xi_{\mathbf k})^{-2}$. It cannot be set to zero for the nontrivial bound solution.

Put $E=-B$ with $B>0$ and impose the sum defining $C$:
$$
1=g\sum_{\langle\mathbf k\rangle}\frac1{2\xi_{\mathbf k}+B}\simeq g\rho(E_F)\int_0^D\frac{d\xi}{2\xi+B}=\frac{g\rho(E_F)}2\log\frac{2D+B}{B}.
$$
Here $\rho(E_F)$ counts orbital states for one <spin> species, as appropriate to the unrestricted $\mathbf k$ sum in this pair basis, and is approximately constant over the thin shell. If a two-spin total <density of states> is used, it must be divided by two in this integral. State-counting and the volume convention in $g$ and $\rho$ must be consistent. With $\xi=g\rho(E_F)$ as the paper's dimensionless coupling, inversion yields
$$
\boxed{E=-\frac{2\hbar\omega_D}{e^{2/\xi}-1}\simeq-2\hbar\omega_D e^{-2/\xi}\quad(\xi\ll1).}
$$
The first approximation is the constant-density-of-states, restricted-shell model; the final exponential additionally assumes weak attraction. The binding <energy> is nonanalytic at zero coupling, so a finite-order power expansion in $g$ cannot produce it.

Cooper's problem asks whether two <electrons> added above an inert <Fermi sea> bind despite Pauli exclusion of lower states. **An arbitrarily weak net attraction binds a zero-momentum singlet below the free-pair threshold**, because the near-Fermi-surface integral diverges logarithmically as $B\downarrow0$. Its <wavefunction> draws on many time-reversed <momentum> states and becomes dominated by a narrow region near the <Fermi surface> at weak coupling; the corresponding pair is spatially extended. This establishes a <Cooper instability> of the unpaired reference state.<Bound Electron Pairs in a Degenerate Fermi Gas by Leon Neil Cooper>[pp. 1189–1190, two added particles above the filled sea and the bound-state criterion]{ref} The full superconducting <ground state> needs a coherent many-electron treatment in which overlapping pairs and the reorganized sea are included. <BCS theory> supplies that step; the two-electron binding scale alone is neither a calculation of the many-body gap nor a derivation of zero resistance.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[pp. 1175–1177, paired many-electron ground state and excitation gap]{ref}

A <Bravais lattice> is the translation set $\Lambda=\{n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3:n_i\in\mathbb Z\}$ generated by <primitive lattice translation vectors>. Translating by any member of $\Lambda$ leaves its surroundings equivalent. A <crystal structure> requires both this lattice and an atomic basis attached to every lattice point. The basis can lower the symmetry of the bare point lattice; a familiar-looking geometrical arrangement of atoms is not necessarily itself a <Bravais lattice>. For example, the <diamond cubic structure> is a face-centred cubic lattice with an identical-atom basis at fractional positions $(0,0,0)$ and $(1/4,1/4,1/4)$ in conventional cubic coordinates. The displacement between those two basis atoms is not itself a <lattice translation>.

The seven three-dimensional <crystal systems> are triclinic, monoclinic, orthorhombic, tetragonal, trigonal, hexagonal and cubic; the distinct translation types give 14 <Bravais lattices>. A conventional cell can contain several lattice points, while a <primitive unit cell> contains one. Simple cubic <primitive lattice translation vectors> can be chosen along the cube edges; for face-centred cubic they can be $(a/2)(0,1,1)$, $(a/2)(1,0,1)$ and $(a/2)(1,1,0)$. Their primitive volume is $a^3/4$, explaining the four lattice points per conventional face-centred cell.

A <crystallographic point group> comprises the orthogonal parts of the <crystal>'s spatial symmetry operations. It constrains possible rotations, reflections, inversion and their combinations, while forgetting translations. Lattice preservation restricts proper rotational orders in three dimensions to $1,2,3,4,6$; a fivefold axis cannot be a symmetry of a periodic three-dimensional lattice. One quick argument restricts a rotation's integral lattice representation through $1+2\cos\theta$, an integer trace, producing these orders. There are 32 crystallographic point-group types. Examples are the cubic full octahedral group $O_h$ with Hermann–Mauguin symbol $m\bar3m$, the tetrahedral class $T_d$ with symbol $\bar43m$, and the polar tetragonal class $C_{4v}$ with symbol $4mm$.

The <space group> retains the full <isometry>. In <Seitz notation> $\{R\mid\mathbf t\}$ acts by $\mathbf x\mapsto R\mathbf x+\mathbf t$, and multiplication is
$$
\{R\mid\mathbf t\}\{R'\mid\mathbf t'\}=\{RR'\mid R\mathbf t'+\mathbf t\}.
$$
Its <translation subgroup> is normal and the quotient is the <crystallographic point group>. A <screw axis> combines rotation with translation along the axis; for example, applying a $2_1$ screw twice gives one whole <lattice translation>. A <glide plane> combines reflection with a translation parallel to the plane; applying the glide twice likewise gives a translation. These operations can preserve the complete <crystal> without their rotation or reflection alone preserving it.

A <symmorphic space group> admits an origin and representatives for which the point-group operations have no translation component. A <nonsymmorphic space group> has essential fractional translations that cannot all be removed by moving the origin. There are 230 three-dimensional space-group types, distinguished more finely than their <point groups>. These counts refer to ordinary, nonmagnetic periodic <space groups>; magnetic and aperiodic generalizations are different classifications.<International Tables for Crystallography, Volume A: Space-group symmetry>[2016 edition, overview and Part 1 introduction to crystallographic and space-group symmetry]{ref}

For concrete comparisons, a monatomic simple-cubic <crystal> has <space group> $Pm\bar3m$ and <point group> $m\bar3m$. Rock salt has a face-centred cubic lattice, a two-species basis and <symmorphic space group> $Fm\bar3m$. Diamond has the same cubic <point group> and translation-lattice type, but its basis leads to nonsymmorphic $Fd\bar3m$. Replacing the two diamond basis sites by distinct species gives the <zinc blende structure>, exemplified by <GaAs>, with $F\bar43m$ and <point group> $\bar43m$; inversion that would interchange the species is no longer a symmetry. **The lattice specifies translations, the <point group> specifies the linear symmetry classes, and the <space group> specifies their complete spatial implementation.** These examples show why none alone uniquely describes an atomic structure.

= Solution

A <quantum dot> confines electronic motion in all three directions. A <semiconductor> dot can be thin enough that only one transverse subband is occupied, with discrete lateral states controlled by gates. In a rectangular hard-wall model,
$$
\epsilon_{n_xn_yn_z}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m^*}\left(\frac{n_x^2}{L_x^2}+\frac{n_y^2}{L_y^2}+\frac{n_z^2}{L_z^2}\right).
$$
A smooth circular lateral confinement is often better approximated by $m^*\omega_0^2r^2/2$, giving $\epsilon_{n_xn_y}=\hbar\omega_0(n_x+n_y+1)$ above the transverse ground <energy>. Shell $q=n_x+n_y$ contains $q+1$ orbitals, twice that many <electrons> before interactions split the <spin degeneracy>. Hence closed-shell <electron> numbers are $2,6,12,20,\ldots$. These are single-particle <quantum numbers>; an interacting dot's states are many-electron eigenstates, not simply one orbital occupied by a whole charged island.

A perpendicular <magnetic field> changes the harmonic spectrum to the <Fock-Darwin spectrum>. For an <electron> of charge $-e$, take $\omega_c=eB/m^*>0$ and $L_z=\ell\hbar$ in the usual symmetric gauge. Then
$$
\epsilon_{n\ell s}=\hbar\Omega(2n+|\ell|+1)+\frac{\ell\hbar\omega_c}{2}+g^*\mu_BBs,\qquad \Omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\frac{\omega_c^2}{4}},\quad s=\pm\frac12.
$$
Reversing the definition of the angular label changes the displayed sign of its orbital term, not the set of energies. The Zeeman term lifts <spin degeneracy>, while exchange can favor parallel-spin filling in partially filled shells. These effects are distinguished by how peaks evolve with field rather than by identifying every irregular peak spacing with one-particle confinement.

For electrostatics, let $C_\Sigma=C_s+C_d+C_g$ and $Q_g=C_sV_s+C_dV_d+C_gV_g+Q_0$, where $Q_0$ represents a fixed offset charge in the chosen convention. The <constant interaction model> gives
$$
U(N)=\sum_{i=1}^N\epsilon_i+\frac{(Ne-Q_g)^2}{2C_\Sigma},\qquad \mu_N=U(N)-U(N-1)=\epsilon_N+\frac{(N-1/2)e^2-eQ_g}{C_\Sigma}.
$$
The <charging energy> is conventionally $E_C=e^2/(2C_\Sigma)$, whereas adjacent charge-state potentials differ by
$$
\boxed{\mu_{N+1}-\mu_N=\frac{e^2}{C_\Sigma}+\epsilon_{N+1}-\epsilon_N.}
$$
This <addition energy> separates a capacitive contribution from orbital spacing. Exchange, nonuniform screening and correlations are corrections to the <constant interaction model>; a change in dot shape or effective <capacitance> can also alter spacings.

Connect the island weakly to source and drain reservoirs through tunnel barriers. <sequential tunnelling> is suppressed in a charge-stable region when neither adding nor removing an <electron> is energetically accessible. Sweeping the gate brings a charge <electrochemical potential> through the reservoir Fermi level, giving a Coulomb-blockade conductance peak. The gate lever arm is $\alpha_g=C_g/C_\Sigma$, so $\Delta V_g=E_{\rm add}/(e\alpha_g)$ locally if the <capacitances> and level spectrum do not vary appreciably. At finite source-drain bias, alignment with either reservoir defines the boundaries of a <Coulomb diamond>. Its slopes constrain <capacitance> ratios, and lines parallel to its edges can reveal excited-state transport. The conversion from a diamond's bias height to an <energy> depends on bias convention; a full-height/half-height factor must not be assumed without declaring the circuit.

A clear charging spectrum requires $k_BT$ and <tunnel broadening> $\hbar\Gamma$ smaller than the relevant <addition energy>; resolving individual orbital levels additionally requires them smaller than the orbital spacing. Tunnel resistances large compared with $h/e^2$ suppress strong charge fluctuations in the usual weak-junction regime. Blockade is not an assertion of mathematically zero current: higher-order <cotunnelling> can remain, and strong coupling or low-temperature <spin> correlations can invalidate the sequential model. An experimental check therefore combines gate sweeps with bias spectroscopy, <temperature> dependence and barrier tuning to separate level structure from broadening or uncontrolled charge traps.

In a primary vertical-dot experiment, gate-controlled current oscillations were converted into <addition energies> using finite-bias diamond spectroscopy; varying <magnetic field> then followed shell and <spin> filling. Reproducing enhanced <addition energies> at <electron> numbers 2 and 6 in several devices strengthened the shell assignment. The <electron> <temperature> was independently estimated above the refrigerator base <temperature>, an essential distinction when judging spectral resolution. Field-dependent reordering also revealed exchange effects beyond a purely spin-degenerate orbital model.<Shell Filling and Spin Effects in a Few Electron Quantum Dot by S. Tarucha and others>[pp. 3613–3616, gate control, finite-bias calibration, sample comparisons, electron-temperature estimate and field-dependent spin filling]{ref}

= Solution

Use a three-dimensional <electron gas> with a uniform positive background, the <jellium model>. The <Hartree-Fock method> minimizes the expectation of the interacting Hamiltonian over normalized <Slater determinants>. Antisymmetry is exact within that trial class, but correlations requiring a superposition of determinants are omitted. For orthonormal occupied <spin> orbitals $\phi_i$, the <energy> is
$$
E_{\rm HF}=\sum_i\langle i|h_0|i\rangle+\frac12\sum_{ij}(J_{ij}-K_{ij})+E_{\rm background},
$$
where $J_{ij}$ is the direct <Coulomb integral> and $K_{ij}$ the <exchange integral>; the <spin> overlap makes exchange vanish between opposite-spin orbitals. The self-interaction terms $J_{ii}$ and $K_{ii}$ cancel. Variation gives a self-consistent <Fock operator> $\hat f=h_0+\sum_j(\hat J_j-\hat K_j)$; exchange is nonlocal. Its <eigenfunctions> define occupied orbitals of a stationary determinant, and the determinant must be compared with competing self-consistent solutions to select a variational <ground state>.

The <eigenvalues> are orbital Lagrange multipliers. Their sum counts each interaction twice:
$$
E_{\rm HF}=\sum_{i\in\rm occ}\epsilon_i-\frac12\sum_{ij}(J_{ij}-K_{ij})+E_{\rm background}.
$$
Frozen-orbital removal energies may be estimated using <Koopmans' theorem>, but measured quasiparticle energies can contain relaxation and correlations absent from this approximation. Unoccupied orbital energies are not automatically an exact excitation spectrum. In particular, unscreened exchange in a uniform three-dimensional gas produces an unphysical logarithmic slope singularity at its <Fermi surface>; a one-determinant mean field does not reproduce the interacting <Fermi liquid>'s screened response.

For a spatially uniform plane-wave determinant, electron-background and background-background terms cancel the uniform direct Coulomb contribution. Retain the background when making this cancellation: an isolated infinite <electron> charge distribution would have a divergent direct <energy>. The surviving contributions per particle are kinetic <energy> and negative same-spin exchange <energy>. Let $(3/(4\pi n))^{1/3}=r_sa_B^*$ define the <Dimensionless Wigner-Seitz radius>, with $a_B^*=4\pi\varepsilon\hbar^2/(m^*e^2)$, $E_H^*=\hbar^2/(m^*a_B^{*2})$ and $\zeta=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/n$. Then
$$
\frac{E_{\rm HF}(r_s,\zeta)}{NE_H^*}=\frac{3(9\pi/4)^{2/3}}{10r_s^2}\frac{(1+\zeta)^{5/3}+(1-\zeta)^{5/3}}2-\frac{3(9\pi/4)^{1/3}}{4\pi r_s}\frac{(1+\zeta)^{4/3}+(1-\zeta)^{4/3}}2.
$$
For the unpolarized state this is approximately $1.105/r_s^2-0.4582/r_s$. The kinetic contribution penalizes <spin> <polarization> because it enlarges an occupied Fermi sphere; exchange favors <polarization> because equal-spin occupation gains more exchange <energy>. As density falls, the $r_s^{-1}$ exchange scale overtakes the $r_s^{-2}$ kinetic scale. The uniform fully polarized and unpolarized energies cross at
$$
r_s=\frac{[3(9\pi/4)^{2/3}/10](2^{2/3}-1)}{[3(9\pi/4)^{1/3}/(4\pi)](2^{1/3}-1)}\simeq5.45.
$$
This is a variational comparison within the homogeneous <Hartree-Fock electron gas>, not an exact physical ferromagnetic phase boundary. It demonstrates how Pauli kinetic <energy> and exchange compete and why the allowed trial states matter.

Allowing spatial symmetry breaking changes the result: the <Overhauser instability> lowers the paramagnetic determinant's Hartree-Fock <energy> through a <spin density wave>, even in the high-density limit. A calculation restricted to uniform Fermi spheres therefore does not give the unrestricted Hartree-Fock <ground state>.<Spin Density Waves in an Electron Gas by A. W. Overhauser>[abstract and p. 1437, instability of the uniform paramagnetic state to spin-density waves]{ref} At still lower density, localization and a <Wigner crystal> compete with fluid states. <electron-gas correlation energy> beyond a single determinant is important in assessing their competition. <Quantum Monte Carlo> provides a concrete route to comparing correlated fluid and <crystal> energies; the original stochastic calculation demonstrates why exchange-only <energy> differences are insufficient.<Ground State of the Electron Gas by a Stochastic Method by D. M. Ceperley and B. J. Alder>[abstract and pp. 566–569, correlation energies and competing fluid/crystal states]{ref}

**Hartree-Fock is a useful variational baseline with exact exchange, not a controlled complete low-density phase diagram.** At high density the kinetic scale dominates, but even a small symmetry-breaking <energy> gain can invalidate a restricted determinant as the exact Hartree-Fock minimizer. At low density neglected correlations, screening and localization can change both transition locations and the ordering of candidate phases. A numerical phase boundary should therefore state dimension, <spin> constraints, background model and whether correlation and spatially modulated states were included.
