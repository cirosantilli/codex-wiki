= Thermodynamics
{wiki}

Thermodynamics studies <energy> transfers and the constraints on macroscopic changes. Its vocabulary connects <internal energy>, <entropy>, <temperature>, <pressure> and <volume> to <heat> and <work>. The <first law of thermodynamics> balances <energy>; the <second law of thermodynamics> decides which changes can occur spontaneously and limits how much <work> a <heat engine> can deliver. These principles let us bound performance before choosing a material or a detailed machine design. Applications include power generation, propulsion and <refrigerators>. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node10.html][MIT, What thermodynamics describes]{ref}

Start by choosing a system boundary. A <closed system> can exchange <energy> but no matter; an <isolated system> exchanges neither. A <state function>, such as <internal energy> or <entropy>, depends on the equilibrium state. A process specifies how that state changes. Thus a body's <temperature> is a property it has, whereas <heat> records <energy> crossing its boundary during a process. A body contains <internal energy>, rather than a stored quantity of <heat>.

Two insulated bodies initially at different positive <temperatures> approach <thermal equilibrium> when brought into thermal contact. Their combined <energy> is conserved, but their combined <entropy> increases. They do not spontaneously separate again into a hotter body and a colder one. This familiar asymmetry makes <thermodynamics> interesting: conserving <energy> leaves many imaginable changes available, and <entropy> supplies a further constraint. The same distinction underlies both <entropy maximization under thermal contact> and the <thermal efficiency> limit in <Carnot's theorem>.

= Thermodynamic system
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A thermodynamic system is the part of the physical world selected for an energy and matter balance. Its boundary determines which transfers count as <heat>, <work>, or transported matter.

= Closed system
{parent=Thermodynamic system}

A closed thermodynamic system exchanges no matter with its surroundings, but can exchange <energy> through <heat> or <work>. A sealed cylinder with a moving piston is a typical idealized example.

= Isolated system
{parent=Thermodynamic system}

An isolated thermodynamic system exchanges neither matter nor <energy> with its surroundings. Its total <internal energy> is fixed, while the <second law of thermodynamics> constrains its total <entropy>.

= State function
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A state function depends on the current equilibrium state, rather than the path used to reach it. <Internal energy> and <entropy> are state functions; transferred <heat> and <work> depend on the process.

= Heat engine
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A heat engine operates cyclically, taking in <heat>, delivering net <work>, and rejecting the remaining <heat>. For two <thermal reservoirs>, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $W=Q_h-Q_c$ and <Carnot's theorem> bounds its <thermal efficiency>.

= Refrigerator
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A refrigerator consumes <work> to transfer <heat> from a colder region to warmer surroundings. Its useful output is the <heat> extracted from the colder region; this transfer obeys the <second law of thermodynamics> because the device requires an additional input of <energy>.

= Heat pump
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A heat pump consumes <work> to transfer <heat> to a warmer region. Its useful output is the <heat> delivered to that region; the same thermodynamic cycle can be described as a <refrigerator> when the cold-side extraction is the objective.

= Free expansion
{parent=Thermodynamics}

Free expansion lets a gas occupy an initially empty region without pushing against an external load. In an insulated <isolated system>, transferred <heat> and external <work> are both zero, so the <internal energy> is unchanged; the process is generally irreversible and can increase <entropy>.

= Thermodynamic phase
{parent=Thermodynamics}

A thermodynamic phase is a form of macroscopic equilibrium characterized by its structure, symmetries and thermodynamic response. Away from <phase transitions>, its <free-energy density> varies smoothly with the control parameters. Different phases can share a <phase coexistence> boundary. An ordered magnetic phase is distinguished from a disordered phase by its spontaneous <order parameter>; positive and negative ordered states are different <pure thermodynamic phases> related by <spin inversion symmetry>.

= Laws of thermodynamics
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

The laws organize the equilibrium and process constraints of <thermodynamics>: the <zeroth law of thermodynamics> makes thermal equilibrium transitive, the <first law of thermodynamics> expresses energy balance, the <second law of thermodynamics> restricts entropy changes, and the <third law of thermodynamics> describes the zero-temperature limit. Their analogy with horizon mechanics becomes quantitative through <Hawking temperature> and <Bekenstein-Hawking entropy>.

= Zeroth law of thermodynamics
{parent=Laws of thermodynamics}
{wiki}

If two systems are each in <thermal equilibrium> with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Equilibrium classes can therefore be labelled by a common <thermodynamic temperature>. The corresponding <zeroth law of black-hole mechanics> states constancy of <surface gravity> over an equilibrium horizon; <Hawking radiation> identifies that quantity with physical temperature up to its universal conversion factor.

= Compressibility
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{title2=$\kappa_X=-V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_X$}
{wiki}

Compressibility measures fractional volume reduction per increase in <pressure>, with another thermodynamic variable held fixed. Holding <temperature> fixed gives isothermal compressibility; holding <entropy> fixed gives adiabatic compressibility for a reversible process. Its reciprocal is the bulk modulus. The constraint matters: these responses need not agree.

= Isothermal compressibility
{parent=Compressibility}
{title2=$\kappa_T=-V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial p)_T$}

The <compressibility> measured at fixed <temperature>. For a mechanically stable equilibrium it is positive; its reciprocal is the isothermal bulk modulus.

= Maxwell relation
{c}
{synonym}

A Maxwell relation equates mixed derivatives of a thermodynamic potential. For $dF=-S\,dT-P\,dV$, equality of mixed derivatives gives $(\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial P/\partial T)_V$. For $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP$, it gives $(\partial S/\partial P)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_P$. These identities connect experimentally accessible response functions to <heat capacities> and <compressibility>.

= Diffusion
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{title2=$\mathbf j=-D\nabla C$}

Diffusion is the spreading of heat or material by microscopic random motion. A <concentration> gradient produces flux down that gradient; with uniform <diffusion coefficient> $D$, conservation gives the <diffusion equation> $C_t=D\nabla^2C$. In a moving carrier, <advection> must also be included. Physical diffusion is a transport mechanism, distinct from the general probabilistic class of <Markov diffusion>.

= Molecular diffusion
{parent=Diffusion}

Molecular diffusion transports material through random molecular motion rather than coherent fluid <advection>. Its continuum limit gives a concentration flux $-\kappa\nabla\chi$, with molecular <diffusivity> $\kappa$, and a <diffusion equation> when no bulk motion is present. Molecular diffusion acts on the small scalar gradients produced by stretching, even when the macroscopic effective transport is much faster than molecular transport.

= Rotational diffusion
{parent=Diffusion}
{wiki}
{title2=$D_r$}

Rotational diffusion randomizes orientation. For a unit orientation vector on a sphere, isotropic rotational diffusion contributes $D_r\nabla_p^2f$ to the <Fokker-Planck equation>, where $\nabla_p^2$ is the <Laplace-Beltrami operator>. Its coefficient has units inverse time. It differs from the translational <diffusion coefficient>, whose units are length squared per time.

= Coefficient of thermal expansion
{parent=Thermal expansion}
{title2=$\alpha_T=-\rho^{-1}(\partial\rho/\partial T)_p$}

The fractional <mass density> decrease per <temperature> increase at fixed <pressure>. In the <Boussinesq approximation>, a <temperature> excess $\theta$ gives <reduced gravity> $g'=g\alpha_T\theta$. It converts a <heat> budget into a buoyancy budget in <natural ventilation>.

= Thermal expansion coefficient
{parent=Thermal expansion}
{title2=$\alpha=-\rho^{-1}(\partial\rho/\partial T)_p$}

For a fluid at fixed <pressure>, the volumetric thermal expansion coefficient obeys $\rho(T_0+\theta)\simeq\rho(T_0)(1-\alpha\theta)$. Under the <Boussinesq approximation> it determines the <buoyancy> proportional to $g\alpha\theta$, while <mass density> is otherwise held constant.

= Stochastic thermodynamics
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

Stochastic thermodynamics assigns work, heat, and entropy production to individual fluctuating trajectories while recovering ordinary thermodynamics after ensemble averaging.

= Local detailed balance
{parent=Stochastic thermodynamics}

Local detailed balance states that the logarithm of the probability ratio between a trajectory and its time reverse equals the entropy delivered to the environment in units of $k_B$. For one heat bath, $\log(P_F/P_B)=\Delta Q/(k_BT)$ when positive $\Delta Q$ flows into the bath.

= Microscopic reversibility
{parent=Local detailed balance}
{wiki}

For an equilibrium process, a path and its physically time-reversed path have equal probability after including their equilibrium starting weights. Conditional on their endpoints, this means
$$
\frac{P_F}{P_B}=\exp[-(F_2-F_1)/(k_BT)].
$$
This is the path version of <detailed balance>. A time-even <order parameter> is read backward without changing sign, while a transport flux changes sign. The <Onsager--Machlup path probability> implements this reversal in a coarse-grained stochastic description.

= Entropy production
{title2=$\Delta S_{\rm tot}$}
{parent=Stochastic thermodynamics}
{wiki}

Entropy production is the sum of the entropy changes of a system and its environment. Under local detailed balance it is represented by a forward-to-reversed path-probability logarithm and has nonnegative expectation.

= Thermodynamic entropy flux
{parent=Entropy production}
{title2=$j_\eta=q/\theta$}

Local equilibrium associates the outgoing <heat flux> $q$ with outgoing <entropy> flux $q/\theta$ at positive <temperature> $\theta$. Its <divergence> appears in the <Lagrangian entropy inequality>, with the appropriate nominal flux in reference coordinates.

= Fluctuation theorem
{parent=Entropy production}
{wiki}

A fluctuation theorem relates probabilities of positive and negative entropy production. In a steady-state large-deviation form, the rate function often obeys $I(s)-I(-s)=-s$ in units where $s$ is the entropy-production rate.

= Fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle
{title2=$\sigma^2=2\zeta k_BT$}
{parent=Stochastic thermodynamics}

For a Langevin particle with drag coefficient $\zeta$ and white-force covariance amplitude $\sigma^2$, detailed balance at temperature $T$ requires $\sigma^2=2\zeta k_BT$.

= Vector noise normalization in inertial Langevin dynamics
{parent=Fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle}
{title2=$\langle\mathbf A(t)\cdot\mathbf A(s)\rangle=(6\gamma k_BT/m^2)\delta(t-s)$}

Divide the inertial <Langevin equation> by mass and let $\zeta=\gamma/m$. An isotropic three-dimensional equilibrium velocity has $\langle|\mathbf u|^2\rangle=3k_BT/m$ by the <equipartition theorem>. Its white acceleration dot-covariance amplitude is therefore $6\zeta k_BT/m$, while the amplitude per Cartesian component is $2\zeta k_BT/m$. A scalar moment formula must use the component variance rather than the trace.

At thermal equilibrium, the Boltzmann distribution assigns a state of energy $E_i$ probability $P_i=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta E_i}$, where $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$ and the partition function $Z$ normalizes the probabilities.

= Temperature
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

Temperature is the intensive variable thermodynamically conjugate to entropy; at equilibrium it determines the direction of heat transfer.

= Kelvin
{parent=Temperature}
{title2=$\mathrm K$}

The kelvin is the SI unit of <thermodynamic temperature>, with symbol $\mathrm K$. Its definition fixes the numerical value of the <Boltzmann constant>; it is written without a degree sign.\a[https://www.bipm.org/en/si-base-units/kelvin][BIPM, SI unit kelvin]{ref}

= Degree Celsius
{parent=Temperature}
{title2=${}^\circ\mathrm C$}

The degree Celsius has the same size as the <kelvin>, with a shifted temperature origin: $t/{}^\circ\mathrm C=T/\mathrm K-273.15$. Thus a change of one degree Celsius is a change of one <kelvin>, but temperature ratios in <Carnot's theorem> must use absolute <thermodynamic temperature>.\a[https://www.bipm.org/documents/d/guest/si-brochure-9-2_01][BIPM, SI Brochure, temperature units]{ref}

= Inverse temperature
{parent=Temperature}
{title2=$\beta=1/(k_BT)$}

<Inverse temperature> is $\beta=1/(k_BT)$, where $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $T$ is the absolute <temperature>. A <canonical ensemble> weights an energy $E$ by its <Boltzmann factor> $e^{-\beta E}$. Calling couplings dimensionless means absorbing this factor $\beta$ into their energy coefficients. The alternative convention $k_B=1$ makes <inverse temperature> $1/T$; physical and dimensionless couplings must not be mixed.

In a thermal <Euclidean worldline path integral> with $\hbar=1$, $\beta$ is also the circumference of the imaginary-time circle.

= Tolman temperature law
{c}
{synonym}

In static <thermal equilibrium>, the local temperature times the stationary lapse is constant. Energy measured locally redshifts to conserved Killing energy by that same lapse; requiring a single equilibrium Boltzmann exponent gives the relation. The <Killing vector field> normalization fixes the constant but not the ratio of temperatures at different locations.

= Boltzmann suppression
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}
{c}
{title2=$e^{-m/T}$}

<Boltzmann suppression> is the exponential reduction of an equilibrium population when creating it costs energy much greater than the temperature. For a nonrelativistic species with negligible <chemical potential>, the leading factor is $e^{-m/T}$, multiplied by its momentum phase-space volume. It concerns the equilibrium abundance; freeze-out can preserve a larger population than the instantaneous equilibrium value at later times.

= Boltzmann suppression of a nonrelativistic relic
{parent=Boltzmann suppression}
{c}
{title2=$n_{\rm eq}\simeq g(mT/2\pi)^{3/2}e^{-m/T}$}

A nonrelativistic species with $g$ states and negligible <chemical potential> has equilibrium density $g(mT/(2\pi))^{3/2}e^{-m/T}$. If particle-antiparticle annihilation maintains equilibrium after the species becomes massive, its eventual freeze-out population can be much smaller than a relativistically decoupled population. The nonrelativistic annihilation rate and any asymmetry determine the final abundance.

= Kinetic equilibrium
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}

Momentum-exchange collisions maintain a common <temperature> and an equilibrium-shaped momentum <probability distribution>. Particle abundances and <chemical potentials> need not have their <chemical equilibrium> values: elastic scattering alone conserves particle number. Thus kinetic equilibrium does not by itself imply either zero <chemical potential> or conserved comoving <entropy>.

A heat bath, or thermal reservoir, is an idealized system so large that exchanging a finite amount of <heat> does not appreciably change its <temperature>.

= Entropy maximization under thermal contact
{parent=Thermal equilibrium}

If two otherwise isolated systems freely exchange energy while $E_1+E_2$ remains fixed, their total entropy is
$$
S_{\mathrm{tot}}(E_1)=S_1(E_1)+S_2(E-E_1).
$$
At an interior maximum, $\partial S_1/\partial E_1=\partial S_2/\partial E_2$. Since <thermodynamic temperature> satisfies $1/T=\partial S/\partial E$, the systems have equal temperatures at <thermal equilibrium>.

= Entropy
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{title2=$S$}
{wiki}

Entropy is an <extensive quantity> and a <state function> of an equilibrium system, measured in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Differences between equilibrium states are calculated along a <reversible thermodynamic process>:
$$
S_2-S_1=\int_1^2\frac{\delta Q_{\rm rev}}{T}.
$$
Here <heat> entering the system is positive and $T$ is its absolute <thermodynamic temperature>. The reversible path used for calculation need not be the path actually taken. Dividing the actual <heat> by a single <temperature> generally fails for an irreversible process.

For example, put two rigid bodies, each with constant <heat capacity> $C=100\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, at $600\,\mathrm K$ and $300\,\mathrm K$ in an otherwise <isolated system>. Assume each body has a uniform internal <temperature>, no <phase transition> occurs, and no <work> is extracted. Their final <thermal equilibrium> is at $450\,\mathrm K$, because the <internal energy> lost by one equals that gained by the other. Using <entropy from heat capacity>,
$$
\Delta S_h=C\log(450/600)=-28.77\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta S_c=C\log(450/300)=40.55\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}},
$$
so $\Delta S_{\rm tot}=C\log(9/8)=11.78\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. One body's <entropy> decreases while the total increases. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node41.html][MIT, Entropy changes and the hot-brick problem]{ref}

\Image[heat-exchange-entropy.png]
{title=Thermal equilibration of two finite bodies conserves energy and increases total entropy}

In <statistical mechanics>, <Boltzmann's entropy formula> counts equally likely <microstates> compatible with a <macrostate>; <Gibbs entropy> handles a specified <probability distribution> over <microstates>. If the number of equally likely <microstates> doubles, <entropy> increases by $k_B\log2$. The <logarithm> makes multiplicative counts additive for independent systems. These are precise statements about states and probabilities, not a universal measure of visible messiness. Calling <entropy> merely "disorder" obscures the thermal calculation above and hides which <microstates> are being counted. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node57.html][MIT, Statistical entropy and randomness]{ref}

The usefulness of <entropy> is that measurable <heat capacities> determine its changes, and the <second law of thermodynamics> turns those changes into constraints on processes. It also connects macroscopic measurements to microscopic descriptions without requiring us to track every particle.

= Specific entropy
{title2=$s=S/M$}
{parent=Entropy}

Specific entropy is <entropy> per unit mass. For a <perfect gas> with constant <specific heat capacity> $c_v$ at constant volume and <specific-heat ratio> $\gamma$, differences obey
$$
s_2-s_1=c_v\log\left[\frac{p_2}{p_1}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^\gamma\right],
$$
where $p$ is <pressure> and $\rho$ is <mass density>. Thus a <homentropic flow> has constant $p/\rho^\gamma$.

= Entropy density
{title2=$s$}
{parent=Entropy}
{wiki}

For a homogeneous equilibrium system with <energy density> $\rho$, <pressure> $P$, <chemical potential> $\mu$, and <number density> $n$, the entropy density is
$$
s=\frac{\rho+P-\mu n}{T}.
$$

= Comoving entropy
{parent=Entropy density}
{title2=$S_c=a^3s$}

Comoving entropy is the <entropy density> multiplied by the physical volume factor $a^3$ for a fixed comoving region in an expanding homogeneous universe. In adiabatic expansion it is conserved. Separate decoupled radiation sectors conserve their own comoving entropy, so disappearance of massive species can heat the still-equilibrated sector relative to a decoupled species.

= Entropy density at zero chemical potential
{parent=Entropy density}
{title2=$s=(\rho+P)/T$}

For an extensive equilibrium system, the <thermodynamic Euler relation> gives $E=TS-PV+\mu N$. At zero <chemical potential>, divide by $V$ to obtain $s=(\rho+P)/T$. Equivalently, $dP=(\rho+P)dT/T$ makes
$$
d\left(\frac{(\rho+P)V}{T}\right)=\frac{d(\rho V)+P\,dV}{T}.
$$
The <first law of thermodynamics> identifies this as $dS$; the <thermodynamic Euler relation> fixes the entropy normalization. For adiabatic expansion satisfying the <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation>, the numerator on the right vanishes, proving <cosmological entropy conservation>.

= Nonrelativistic entropy at zero chemical potential
{parent=Entropy density at zero chemical potential}
{title2=$s\sim g m^3(2\pi)^{-3/2}x^{-1/2}e^{-x}$}

For a dilute nonrelativistic gas with $x=m/T\gg1$ and zero <chemical potential>, the <Bose-Einstein distribution> approaches the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. Expanding $E=m+p^2/(2m)+\cdots$ in the <number density> integral gives $n\sim g(mT/(2\pi))^{3/2}e^{-x}$. Since $\rho=mn+3nT/2+\cdots$ and $P=nT$, the <entropy density at zero chemical potential> gives $s=n(x+5/2+\cdots)$ and hence the displayed leading expression. This includes the internal multiplicity $g$; a single real <scalar field> has $g=1$.

= Second law of thermodynamics
{c}
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

For an <isolated system>, the second law requires $\Delta S\ge0$. More generally, include all affected surroundings to obtain
$$
\Delta S_{\rm tot}=\Delta S_{\rm system}+\Delta S_{\rm surroundings}\ge0.
$$
Equality describes the reversible limit; irreversible macroscopic processes produce <entropy>. A <closed system> can lose <entropy> by exporting <heat>, so nondecrease does not apply to that system alone. The <Clausius statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is transferring <heat> from cold to hot. The <Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law> forbids a cyclic device whose sole effect is converting <heat> from one <thermal reservoir> completely into <work>. "Sole effect" matters: a <refrigerator> moves <heat> from cold to hot by consuming <work>. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node37.html][MIT, Statements of the second law]{ref}

For direct transfer of $Q=1000\,\mathrm J$ from a <thermal reservoir> at $600\,\mathrm K$ to one at $300\,\mathrm K$, with no <work> extracted,
$$
\Delta S_{\rm tot}=-\frac{1000}{600}+\frac{1000}{300}
=\frac53\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}>0.
$$
The reverse transfer alone would have negative total <entropy> change. Both directions conserve <energy>, but only the hot-to-cold direction is spontaneous. The finite-body example under <entropy> replaces these constant <temperatures> by changing ones.

<Friction>, <free expansion> and <heat transfer> across a finite <temperature> difference all introduce irreversibility. Moving arbitrarily slowly is insufficient if <friction> remains. A <reversible thermodynamic process> must permit restoration of both the system and surroundings. This gives engineers a useful ideal benchmark: irreversibility spends an opportunity to obtain <work>, even though <energy> itself is conserved. \a[https://www.ocw.mit.edu/ans7870/16/16.unified/thermoF03/chapter_7.htm][MIT, Irreversibility and lost opportunities for work]{ref}

= Reversible thermodynamic process
{parent=Second law of thermodynamics}
{title2=$dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T$}

A thermodynamically reversible process can be reversed without a net change of the system and surroundings. Its entropy change equals reversible heat transfer divided by temperature. A reversible <adiabatic process> conserves <entropy>; adiabaticity alone does not forbid internal entropy production in an irreversible evolution.

= Carnot theorem
{c}
{synonym}

No cyclic <heat engine> exchanging <heat> only with two fixed <thermal reservoirs> at positive absolute <temperatures> $T_h>T_c$ can exceed the <thermal efficiency> of an engine whose cycle is a <reversible thermodynamic process> between them. All such reversible engines have the same <thermal efficiency>, regardless of their working substance. Let $Q_h>0$ be <heat> absorbed from the hot <thermal reservoir> and $Q_c>0$ the magnitude rejected to the cold one. Then
$$
W=Q_h-Q_c,\qquad
\eta=\frac W{Q_h}\le\eta_C=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.
$$
The <work> convention is the one in the <first law of thermodynamics>: $W>0$ is net output by the engine. Use absolute <thermodynamic temperatures>, such as values in <kelvin>; ratios of readings in <degree Celsius> are invalid because their zero is shifted.

For $T_h=600\,\mathrm K$, $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$ and $Q_h=1000\,\mathrm J$, the maximum <thermal efficiency> is $50\%$. A reversible engine delivers $W=500\,\mathrm J$ and rejects $Q_c=500\,\mathrm J$. The <thermal reservoirs> have <entropy> changes $-1000/600$ and $+500/300\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, which cancel. The engine's own <entropy> change is zero over its <thermodynamic cycle>.

\Image[carnot-engine-efficiency.png]
{title=Carnot engine energy transfers and the reversible cycle between 600 K and 300 K}

A short derivation now becomes transparent: the <second law of thermodynamics> gives $-Q_h/T_h+Q_c/T_c\ge0$, and the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $W=Q_h-Q_c$. Together they yield the bound and, more precisely,
$$
W=Q_h\left(1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}\right)-T_c\Delta S_{\rm tot}.
$$
At fixed $Q_h,T_h,T_c$, every extra unit of total <entropy production> reduces output by $T_c$ units of <energy>. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node44.html][MIT, Maximum heat-engine work]{ref}

Fixed <thermal reservoirs> are essential. Finite bodies cool and warm, so their initial <temperature> ratio cannot be used throughout an entire extraction process. As a computed contrast, the bodies with equal constant <heat capacity> in the <entropy> example would reach $T_f=\sqrt{600\times300}=424.26\,\mathrm K$ if a reversible engine extracted <work> until their <temperatures> matched. Zero total <entropy> change gives this geometric mean; the <first law of thermodynamics> then gives $W_{\max}=C(600+300-2T_f)=5147.19\,\mathrm J$. Direct contact reaches $450\,\mathrm K$ and extracts no <work>.

Carnot's 1824 book asked whether the useful output of machines driven by <heat> was fundamentally limited and whether changing the working substance could improve that limit. Its reversible comparisons are the historical setting of <Carnot's theorem>. Carnot used the then-current caloric picture, treating <heat> as conserved in its passage through an engine; the modern balance $Q_h-Q_c=W$ should not be read back into that text. \a[https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-9118][Carnot, Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu, 1824, pp. 6–7 and 20–23]{ref}

= Thermodynamic temperature
{parent=Carnot's theorem}
{wiki}

Absolute thermodynamic temperature can be defined so that a reversible engine exchanging heats $Q_h$ and $Q_c$ with reservoirs at $T_h$ and $T_c$ satisfies
$$
\frac{Q_c}{Q_h}=\frac{T_c}{T_h},
\qquad
\eta=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.
$$
A choice of one reference temperature fixes the scale.

= Carnot cycle
{c}
{parent=Carnot's theorem}
{title2=$W=(T_h-T_c)\Delta S$}
{wiki}

A Carnot cycle realizes the reversible bound in <Carnot's theorem>. Its four steps for an <ideal gas> working substance are:

* Reversible <isothermal expansion> at $T_h$, absorbing $Q_h$ and increasing <entropy> by $\Delta S$.
* Reversible <adiabatic expansion>, doing <work> while the <temperature> falls from $T_h$ to $T_c$ and <entropy> stays fixed.
* Reversible <isothermal compression> at $T_c$, rejecting $Q_c$ and decreasing <entropy> by $\Delta S$.
* Reversible <adiabatic compression>, requiring <work> to restore $T_h$ at fixed <entropy>.

The <temperature> versus <entropy> plot in the <Carnot's theorem> figure is therefore a rectangle traversed clockwise. Because $\delta Q_{\rm rev}=T\,dS$, its width is $\Delta S=Q_h/T_h$. For the illustrated engine, $\Delta S=5/3\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$: the upper step absorbs $600\times5/3=1000\,\mathrm J$, the lower step rejects $300\times5/3=500\,\mathrm J$, and the enclosed area is the $500\,\mathrm J$ net <work>. Geometry displays the <thermal efficiency> without solving the gas motion. The two vertical steps are <isentropic processes>, not constant-<temperature> steps. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node24.html][MIT, Four steps of a Carnot cycle]{ref} \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node46.html][MIT, Temperature-entropy diagrams]{ref}

Reversible <heat transfer> is a limit of vanishing <temperature> differences; ordinary finite-rate transfer introduces <entropy production>. The <Carnot cycle> is a performance benchmark rather than a guarantee of useful <power>. Running it backward gives the ideal operation of a <refrigerator> or <heat pump>, with <work> supplied to move <heat> toward the hotter <thermal reservoir>. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node25.html][MIT, Reversed cycles and refrigeration]{ref}

<Sensible heat> changes <temperature> without changing the phase. For constant <specific heat capacity>, its change per unit mass is $c_p\Delta T$, whereas <latent heat> accompanies a <phase transition>.

= Nusselt number
{c}
{parent=Heat transfer}
{title2=$\mathrm{Nu}$}
{wiki}

The <Nusselt number> is the ratio of total <heat transfer> to conductive <heat transfer> across the same length scale: $\mathrm{Nu}=qh/(k_T\Delta T)$, where $q$ is heat flux and $k_T$ is <thermal conductivity>.

= Thermal inertia
{parent=Heat transfer}
{title2=$\Gamma=\sqrt{k\rho c_p}$}

For a homogeneous material, thermal inertia is $\Gamma=\sqrt{k\rho c_p}$, where $k$ is <thermal conductivity>, $\rho$ is density and $c_p$ is specific <heat capacity>. It measures the resistance of the surface temperature to periodic heating and controls the phase lag relevant to the <Yarkovsky effect>.

= Radiative cooling
{parent=Heat transfer}
{wiki}

A body loses <internal energy> by emitting <thermal radiation> faster than it absorbs radiation or receives other heating. In a gravitating gas this can remove pressure support and allow further contraction.

= Compton cooling by the cosmic microwave background
{parent=Radiative cooling}
{title2=$t_C\propto(1+z)^{-4}/x_e$}

Hot electrons can transfer energy to <cosmic microwave background> photons through <Compton scattering>. For a fixed electron fraction $x_e$ and electron temperature well above the radiation temperature, the <optically thin gas cooling time> scales approximately as $(1+z)^{-4}/x_e$. Its volume loss scales with electron density, not with $n_H^2$, because the radiation density is independent of local gas density. The process tends toward the radiation temperature; it heats rather than cools electrons colder than that radiation.

= Dust thermal emission
{parent=Radiative cooling}

Dust grains emit thermal radiation after absorbing energy from radiation or collisions. Collisions can transfer thermal energy from dense gas to grains, whose radiation then cools the gas. This channel requires dust and does not operate in strictly pristine hydrogen-helium material without prior enrichment.

= Inverse Compton cooling
{parent=Radiative cooling}
{title2=$P_e=4\sigma_TcU_\gamma k_B(T_e-T_\gamma)/(m_ec^2)$}

Thermal electrons can transfer energy to lower-energy radiation photons through <Compton scattering>. In the nonrelativistic Thomson regime, the net cooling power per electron is $4\sigma_TcU_\gamma k_B(T_e-T_\gamma)/(m_ec^2)$ for a blackbody photon bath. A sufficiently hot electron population therefore loses energy; a colder population is heated instead. The <cosmic microwave background> radiation energy density grows as $(1+z)^4$, making this channel more important for high-redshift diffuse ionized gas.

= Astrophysical cooling function
{parent=Radiative cooling}
{title2=$\mathcal C=n_H^2\Lambda(T)$}

= Hydrogen-density-squared cooling function
{synonym}

An <astrophysical cooling function> packages radiative losses of a plasma into a temperature-dependent coefficient. In the hydrogen-density-squared convention, the volume energy loss is $n_H^2\Lambda(T)$, so the coefficient has units $\mathrm{erg\,cm^3\,s^{-1}}$. Its dependence on <ionization> state, chemical abundances and radiation field must be specified; a low-density collisional-equilibrium curve is not universal.

= Molecular line cooling
{parent=Astrophysical cooling function}

Radiation from rotational and vibrational transitions of molecules removes gas thermal energy. In primordial gas below the atomic threshold, <molecular hydrogen> is a principal coolant; <hydrogen deuteride> can matter in suitable chemical conditions. Molecular formation, photodissociation, optical depth and density-dependent level populations control the rate. This allows some small systems below the atomic <virial temperature> threshold to form stars.

= Cooling suppression by photoionization
{parent=Astrophysical cooling function}

<Photoionization> changes the ion population, often removing bound-state coolants at temperatures where collisional equilibrium would retain them. It can therefore suppress primordial line-cooling peaks. It also supplies <photoionization heating>. The net loss is cooling minus heating and depends on radiation spectrum, intensity, shielding and gas density. A universal density-independent function of temperature alone is not generally sufficient for irradiated gas.

= Optically thin gas cooling time
{parent=Astrophysical cooling function}
{title2=$t_{\rm cool}=3\chi k_BT/(2n_H\Lambda)$}

= Radiative gas cooling time
{synonym}

For a nonrelativistic ideal gas with $n_{\rm tot}$ thermal particles per volume, divide its thermal energy $3n_{\rm tot}k_BT/2$ by the optically thin volume radiation loss. In the $n_H^2$ cooling convention this becomes $3\chi k_BT/(2n_H\Lambda)$, where $\chi=n_{\rm tot}/n_H$. Density scaling and ionization-dependent particle count must be kept consistent.

= Cooling-to-free-fall equality curve
{parent=Optically thin gas cooling time}
{title2=$n_{H,\rm crit}=\frac{32Gm_p}{3\pi Xf_g}\left(\frac{3\chi k_BT}{2\Lambda}\right)^2$}

Equating the optically thin cooling time to the uniform-sphere free-fall time gives a critical gas density proportional to $T^2/\Lambda^2$. With <hydrogen mass fraction> $X$, particle ratio $\chi$ and gravitating gas fraction $f_g$, it is $[32Gm_p/(3\pi Xf_g)][3\chi k_BT/(2\Lambda)]^2$. At fixed temperature, gas above this density cools faster than free fall. A smaller gravitating gas fraction moves the boundary upward because gravity is faster at a fixed gas density.

= Primordial atomic cooling curve
{parent=Astrophysical cooling function}
{title2=$\Lambda_{\rm H,He}(T)$}

The <primordial atomic cooling curve> describes hydrogen-helium gas without heavy elements. Near the <hydrogen> excitation threshold it rises sharply, then shows <hydrogen> and <helium> excitation/<ionization> features. Bound-state losses weaken when both elements become highly ionized. At sufficiently high temperatures <thermal bremsstrahlung> gives a slowly rising tail. Composition, density convention and <ionization> assumptions fix the numerical normalization.

= Atomic line cooling
{parent=Astrophysical cooling function}
{title2=$\mathcal C_{\rm line}=\sum n_en_iq_{ij}(T)\,E_{ij}$}

<Atomic line cooling> removes gas kinetic energy by <collisional excitation> followed by photon escape. It is strongest where atoms or ions have bound <electrons> and thermally accessible transitions. If photons are trapped, or if the relevant atoms are fully ionized, the simple optically thin line-cooling rate no longer gives the same loss.

= Metal-line cooling
{parent=Atomic line cooling}
{title2=$\Lambda(T,Z)$}

Heavy elements provide many ionic transitions at temperatures where <hydrogen> and <helium> have lost their most effective line emitters. <Metal-line cooling> can therefore increase the plasma cooling function substantially and broaden its temperature features. Low-energy fine-structure transitions also permit cooling below the <hydrogen> atomic threshold when the relevant ions and <electrons> are present.

Heat flux density is the rate of <heat> transfer through unit area. Its SI unit is watts per square metre, and <Fourier's law> gives its conductive contribution.

{synonym}

Thermal conduction transfers <heat> down a <temperature> gradient through microscopic interactions, without bulk transport of matter.

= Temperature dipole around an insulating sphere
{parent=Thermal conduction}
{title2=$T=T_0+(\mathbf H\cdot\mathbf r)(1+a^3/(2r^3))$}

In the absence of heat advection, an insulating <sphere> of radius $a$ in a uniform remote <temperature> gradient $\mathbf H$ has the displayed exterior <harmonic function> as its temperature field. The reflected dipole term makes $\partial_rT=0$ at $r=a$. The surface temperature is $T_0+(3/2)a\mathbf H\cdot\mathbf n$, so the tangential gradient is amplified by $3/2$. If the sphere has <thermal conductivity> ratio $K$ relative to its surroundings, temperature and heat-flux continuity instead give surface gradient factor $3/(2+K)$.

= Isobaric conductive cooling in mass coordinates
{parent=Thermal conduction}
{title2=$T_\tau+\Lambda(T)/T-\lambda_0\partial_m(T^{\alpha-1}T_m)=0$}

With fixed <pressure> $p$, let $A=\mu p/\mathcal R$, so $\rho=A/T$. A material mass coordinate has $\partial_x=\rho\partial_m$ and the material time derivative becomes $\partial_t|_m$. The energy and continuity equations then give the displayed equation with $\tau=Ct$ and $C=(\gamma-1)(\mu/\mathcal R)^2p/\gamma$. A mass integral from a fixed origin is material only when the <mass flux> there vanishes; otherwise subtract its time-integrated flux to label parcels. Uniform <pressure> is a low-Mach approximation when sound crossing is faster than thermal evolution.

= Error-function isobaric cooling front
{parent=Isobaric conductive cooling in mass coordinates}
{title2=$T=T_0\operatorname{erf}\!\left(m/(2\sqrt{\lambda_0\tau})\right)$}

For conductivity $\lambda=\lambda_0T$, no cooling in the hot phase and a zero-<temperature> boundary at $m=0$, the mass-coordinate equation is linear diffusion. Its similarity profile solves $f''+\xi f'/2=0$, $f(0)=0$, $f(\infty)=1$, giving the displayed <error function>. The thermal layer broadens as the square root of time. Conducted energy enters the cold radiating reservoir at rate per area $L=\lambda_0A T_m(0)=A T_0\sqrt{\lambda_0}/\sqrt{\pi\tau}$, hence $L\propto t^{-1/2}$.

The temperature gradient is the <gradient> $\nabla T$ of the <temperature> field. It points in the direction of fastest temperature increase.

The thermal conductivity tensor maps a temperature gradient to the negative heat-flux density. In passive reciprocal conduction it is symmetric positive definite. This permits direction-dependent transport while ensuring positive dissipated energy.

= Thermal diffusivity

Thermal diffusivity is $\kappa=k/(\rho c_p)$, the ratio of <thermal conductivity> to volumetric <heat capacity>. It sets the diffusion length $\sqrt{\kappa t}$ over time $t$.

= Prandtl number

The <Prandtl number> is the ratio of <kinematic viscosity> to <thermal diffusivity>. It compares the rates at which momentum and <temperature> variations diffuse. In thermal-diffusion units the <Boussinesq equations> have momentum inertia multiplied by $\operatorname{Pr}^{-1}$; large, rather than small, <Prandtl number> supplies the usual instantaneous <Stokes flow> limit.

= Thermal diffusion time

The thermal diffusion time across a distance $L$ is $L^2/\kappa$, where $\kappa$ is the <thermal diffusivity>. It is the time required for a temperature disturbance to spread over that distance by <thermal conduction>.

= Thermal diffusion rate of a Fourier mode

With <thermal diffusivity> $\xi$, a <Fourier mode> of <wavenumber> $k$ decays by diffusion at this rate.

= Stefan–Boltzmann law

The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives the thermal-radiation flux emitted by a surface as $F=\epsilon\sigma T^4$, where $\epsilon$ is its <emissivity> and $\sigma$ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant.

= Stefan-Boltzmann constant
{parent=Stefan–Boltzmann law}
{c}
{title2=$\sigma$}

The <Stefan-Boltzmann constant> $\sigma$ is the proportionality constant in the blackbody flux $F=\sigma T^4$. The blackbody <radiation pressure> is $p_{\rm rad}=4\sigma T^4/(3c)$.

= Emissivity

Emissivity is the ratio of the thermal radiation emitted by a surface to that emitted by an ideal black body at the same <temperature>.

= Radiative equilibrium
{parent=Stefan–Boltzmann law}
{wiki}

A body is in radiative equilibrium when absorbed radiative power equals emitted radiative power. A surface absorbing flux $F$ and emitting as a grey body has equilibrium temperature $T=(F/(\epsilon\sigma))^{1/4}$.

= Heat capacity
{parent=Heat}
{wiki}

Heat capacity is the heat required per unit temperature change under a specified constraint.

= Entropy from heat capacity
{parent=Heat capacity}

Along a reversible path at fixed volume, $dS=C_V(T)dT/T$. Hence entropy differences can be measured macroscopically from
$$
S(T_2)-S(T_1)=\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{C_V(T)}T\,dT,
$$
with an additional $L/T_c$ across a first-order transition of <latent heat> $L$ at $T_c$.

= Entropy concavity and positive heat capacity
{parent=Heat capacity}

For a thermodynamically stable system, the <entropy> is concave in energy, so
$$
\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial E^2}
=-\frac1{T^2}\frac{\partial T}{\partial E}<0.
$$
Consequently $\partial E/\partial T>0$: energy increases with <temperature>, and the corresponding <heat capacity> is positive.

= Specific heat capacity
{title2=$c_p$}
{parent=Heat capacity}
{wiki}

Specific heat capacity is <heat capacity> per unit mass. The volumetric heat capacity is $\rho c_p$, and $k/(\rho c_p)$ is the <thermal diffusivity> when $k$ is the <thermal conductivity>.

= Specific heat capacity at constant volume
{parent=Specific heat capacity}
{title2=$c_V=(\partial u/\partial T)_
ho$}

The specific heat capacity at constant volume is the temperature derivative of <specific internal energy> at fixed density and composition: $c_V=(\partial u/\partial T)_\rho$. It is the corresponding extensive <heat capacity at constant volume> divided by material mass.

= Specific heat capacity at constant pressure
{title2=$c_P$}
{parent=Specific heat capacity}

The specific heat capacity at constant pressure is $c_P=(\partial h/\partial T)_P$, where $h$ is specific enthalpy. It includes expansion work as well as the change in internal energy.

= Pure-radiation constant-pressure heat-capacity singularity
{parent=Specific heat capacity at constant pressure}

For pure <blackbody radiation>, $P=aT^4/3$ depends only on <temperature>. Constant pressure therefore fixes temperature, so a usual finite constant-pressure temperature derivative is unavailable. In the material gas-radiation limit the <specific-heat ratio> diverges, although all three <stellar adiabatic exponents> approach $4/3$. The radiation index $4/3$ is an adiabatic pressure-volume response, not a finite heat-capacity ratio.

= Heat capacity at constant volume
{title2=$C_V$}
{parent=Heat capacity}

The heat capacity at constant volume is $C_V=(\partial E/\partial T)_V$ when particle number and other conserved quantities are also fixed.

= Dulong-Petit law
{c}
{parent=Heat capacity at constant volume}
{wiki=Dulong–Petit_law}

In the classical high-temperature limit, each independent harmonic mode contributes $k_B$ to the constant-volume heat capacity.

A classical crystalline solid has three vibrational modes per atom, giving $C_V\simeq3Nk_B$, or $3R$ per mole.

= Heat capacity at constant area
{title2=$C_A$}
{parent=Heat capacity}

For a two-dimensional system of fixed area $A$, the heat capacity at constant area is $C_A=(\partial E/\partial T)_{N,A}$ when the particle number and other conserved quantities are also fixed. It is the two-dimensional analogue of <heat capacity at constant volume>.

= Third law of thermodynamics
{c}

{wiki}

The third law implies that the entropy of a system with a nondegenerate ground state approaches zero as its temperature approaches absolute zero. In particular, ordinary equilibrium heat capacities approach zero.

= Pressure

Dividing a <pressure> disturbance by the constant reference <mass density> $\rho_0$ gives a quantity whose <gradient> enters the <Boussinesq equations> directly as an <acceleration>. Its units are squared <velocity>. Distinguish this convention from physical <pressure> when calculating <energy flux> or comparing <wave polarization> formulae.

= Pressure tensor

In the local rest frame of a gas, the <pressure tensor> is the directional <momentum flux> $P_{ij}=\int p_iv_jf(\mathbf p)\,d^3p$, where $f$ is the <phase-space distribution function> per unit <momentum> volume and $\mathbf v$ is particle velocity. For an isotropic distribution, $P_{ij}=P\delta_{ij}$, recovering the <kinetic pressure of an isotropic gas>. An anisotropic distribution can exert different normal <pressures> along different directions.

= Gas pressure

Gas pressure is the mechanical pressure contributed by material particles. For a classical ideal gas, $p_{\rm gas}=nk_BT$.

= Radiation pressure

Radiation pressure is momentum flux carried by electromagnetic radiation. Isotropic blackbody radiation has $p_{\rm rad}=a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$.

An isotropic ideal gas whose particles obey the ultrarelativistic dispersion $\varepsilon=pc$ satisfies

pV=\frac E3.

= Chemical potential

{wiki}

The chemical potential $\mu=(\partial E/\partial N)_{S,V}$ is the energy cost of adding a particle under fixed entropy and volume.

= Chemical potential of a composition field
{parent=Chemical potential}

For a composition <free energy> $F=\int[f(\phi)+\kappa|\nabla\phi|^2/2]$, the local chemical potential is the <functional derivative>
$$
\mu=f'(\phi)-\kappa\nabla^2\phi.
$$
At <phase coexistence>, exchanges between the bulk phases require equal <chemical potentials>. Mechanical equilibrium additionally requires equal <pressure> for a flat interface, or a <Young–Laplace equation> pressure jump for a curved interface.

= Chemical equilibrium

At chemical equilibrium, the sum of chemical potentials weighted by each reaction's stoichiometric coefficients vanishes.

= Chemical disequilibrium
{parent=Chemical equilibrium}

A chemical system is out of local <chemical equilibrium> when reaction adjustment cannot keep pace with transport, imposed changes or external driving. Comparing the <chemical relaxation time> with transport and irradiation times identifies plausible departures. <Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere> combines this principle with mixing, horizontal advection and <atmospheric photochemistry>.

= Zero chemical potential from number-changing reactions

{title2=$\mu_\phi=0$}

For one particle species in <chemical equilibrium> under $2\phi\leftrightarrow3\phi$, chemical balance gives $2\mu_\phi=3\mu_\phi$, hence $\mu_\phi=0$. Elastic $2\phi\leftrightarrow2\phi$ imposes no such constraint. In an expanding universe the number-changing reaction must remain fast compared with the <Hubble parameter>; after chemical freeze-out a nonzero <chemical potential> can develop even if elastic collisions maintain <kinetic equilibrium>.

= Chemical relaxation time
{title2=$\tau_{\rm chem}$}

The chemical relaxation time estimates how rapidly a perturbed abundance returns toward <chemical equilibrium>. Comparing it with the <eddy mixing time> identifies a <chemical quench level>.

= Photon chemical potential

= Ideal gas
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

= Perfect gas
{synonym}

An ideal gas obeys $pV=Nk_BT$ and neglects intermolecular interactions except during elastic collisions.

= Ideal gas law
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$PV=Nk_BT$}
{wiki}

The <ideal gas> equation of state relates <pressure>, volume, particle number and <temperature>. For a stellar mixture with fixed <mean molecular weight> it can be written $P=\mathcal R\rho T/\mu$. It assumes negligible interaction corrections and nondegenerate particles; excitation or ionization can change the particle count and thermodynamic response.

= Monatomic gas
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\gamma=5/3$}

A monatomic gas consists of individual atoms or ions rather than molecules. In the classical dilute ideal-gas regime, its three translational degrees of freedom give $U=3Nk_BT/2$ by <equipartition theorem>. Thus $C_V=3Nk_B/2$, while $C_P-C_V=Nk_B$ follows by differentiating the ideal-gas enthalpy $H=U+PV=5Nk_BT/2$. Consequently the <adiabatic index> is $\gamma=C_P/C_V=5/3$ and the <specific internal energy> is $e=3P/(2\rho)$. The same translational result applies to a fully ionized mixture when the particle numbers are fixed. This excludes additional excitation, ionization and radiation contributions to the heat capacity.

= Mean free path
{parent=Ideal gas}
{title2=$\ell$}

The mean free path is the average distance travelled between <collisions>. For dilute targets with <number density> $n$ and effective <collision cross-section> $\sigma_c$, $\ell\sim1/(n\sigma_c)$; identical-particle conventions introduce factors such as $\sqrt2$.

= Mean free path through randomly oriented disc absorbers
{parent=Mean free path}
{title2=$\ell^{-1}=n_{\rm com}(1+z)^3\pi R_d^2/2$}

For an unclustered population of fully covering circular disc absorbers, the inverse <mean free path> is proper number density times mean projected area. A comoving number density must first be multiplied by $(1+z)^3$. The incidence then constrains the proper radius; covering factor and multiplicity of detectable lines can change the inference.

= Mean molecular weight
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}

The mean molecular weight is the average mass per freely moving gas particle in units of the atomic mass unit. A fully ionized hydrogen-helium gas with mass fractions $X,Y$ has $\mu^{-1}=2X+3Y/4$.

= Stellar gas constant
{parent=Mean molecular weight}
{title2=$\mathcal R=k_B/m_u$}

In stellar <ideal gas> equations, the <stellar gas constant> $\mathcal R=k_B/m_u$ converts a dimensionless <mean molecular weight> $\mu$ into the specific thermal relation $P=\mathcal R\rho T/\mu$. Here $m_u$ is the reference atomic mass unit, commonly approximated by the proton mass in simplified hydrogen-helium models. It has units of energy per mass per temperature. It is the molar gas constant divided by the molar mass corresponding to $m_u$, rather than the molar gas constant used with a dimensional molar molecular weight.

= Mean molecular weight per ion
{parent=Mean molecular weight}
{title2=$\mu_i^{-1}=\sum_jX_j/A_j$}

= Ion mean molecular weight
{synonym}

The <mean molecular weight> per ion counts nuclei rather than nuclei plus free electrons: $\rho/(\mu_i m_u)$ is the ion number density. For species with mass fractions $X_j$ and mass numbers $A_j$, $\mu_i^{-1}=\sum_jX_j/A_j$. It is useful when ions provide a thermal <ideal gas> pressure but <electron degeneracy pressure> supplies a separate contribution.

= Mean molecular weight per electron
{title2=$\mu_e=\rho/(m_un_e)$}
{parent=Mean molecular weight}

The mean molecular weight per electron is the mass carried per free electron in units of atomic mass $m_u$. Fully ionized hydrogen has $\mu_e=1$, while helium, carbon, and oxygen have approximately $\mu_e=2$. It converts <electron number density> to bulk <mass density> in the <equation of state of a cold electron gas>.

= Heat capacity ratio
{title2=$\gamma$}
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}

= Specific-heat ratio
{synonym}

= Adiabatic exponent
{synonym}

= Adiabatic index
{synonym}

The specific-heat ratio of an ideal gas is $\gamma=C_p/C_V>1$.

= Nondegenerate gas
{parent=Ideal gas}

A gas is nondegenerate when its occupation numbers are low enough that <quantum statistics> reduce to the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. A common criterion is $n\lambda_{\rm th}^3\ll1$, where $\lambda_{\rm th}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength.

For an <ideal gas>, this is the classical statistical regime; nondegeneracy alone does not assert the absence of interactions.

= Internal energy of an ideal gas
{parent=Ideal gas}

For a fixed amount of ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature. If $C_V$ is constant, $dE=C_V\,dT$ and hence $E=C_VT$ after a choice of energy zero.

= Mayer's relation
{c}
{parent=Ideal gas}
{wiki}

= Mayer relation
{c}
{synonym}

For an ideal gas of $N$ particles,
$$
C_p-C_V=Nk_B.
$$
Thus, with $\gamma=C_p/C_V$, one has $Nk_B/C_V=\gamma-1$.

= Real gas
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

A <real gas> departs from the <ideal gas> law because interactions and molecular volume matter. Nonideal <equations of state>, including the <Dieterici equation>, model such departures.

= Dieterici equation
{parent=Real gas}
{c}

The Dieterici equation of state
$$
p=\frac{k_BT}{v-b}\exp\left(-\frac{a}{k_BTv}\right)
$$
models excluded volume through $b$ and attraction through $a$. Its critical point has $v_c=2b$ and $T_c=a/(4bk_B)$.

= Intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities
{parent=Thermodynamics}

An extensive quantity scales in proportion to the amount of material, while an intensive quantity is unchanged when the system is replicated. Energy, entropy, volume, and particle number are extensive; temperature, pressure, and chemical potential are intensive.

= Extensive quantity
{parent=Intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities}

= Extensive variable
{synonym}

An extensive quantity scales in proportion to the size of a thermodynamic system: combining $n$ independent identical copies multiplies it by $n$. <Energy>, <entropy> and <volume> are examples.

= Euler relation in thermodynamics
{c}
{parent=Extensive quantity}
{title2=$E=TS-PV+\mu N$}

= Thermodynamic Euler relation
{synonym}

For a single-component equilibrium system with extensive <internal energy> $E(S,V,N)$, scaling its <entropy>, <volume> and particle number together gives $E(\lambda S,\lambda V,\lambda N)=\lambda E(S,V,N)$. The <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> therefore gives $E=S\partial_SE+V\partial_VE+N\partial_NE$. The <first law of thermodynamics> identifies these derivatives as $T,-P,\mu$, respectively, proving $E=TS-PV+\mu N$. Dividing by volume yields the <entropy density> formula $s=(\rho+P-\mu n)/T$. This step fixes the entropy normalization rather than merely identifying its differential. Differentiating the Euler relation and subtracting $dE=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN$ gives the <Gibbs-Duhem equation> $S\,dT-V\,dP+N\,d\mu=0$. The argument requires extensivity; it is not an assumption about arbitrary nonadditive systems.

= Gibbs-Duhem equation
{c}
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki=Gibbs–Duhem_equation}

The <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> applied to an <extensive quantity>, together with $dE=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN$ give
$$
E=TS-pV+\mu N,
\qquad
S\,dT-V\,dp+N\,d\mu=0.
$$

= Gibbs free energy
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{c}
{wiki}

The Gibbs free energy is $G=E-TS+pV$. For a simple system of fixed composition,
$$
dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp.
$$

= Gibbs free energy of an ideal-gas mixture
{c}
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

For a single-phase <ideal gas> mixture with molar amounts $N_i$, common <temperature> $T$, and <pressure> $P$, extensivity and ideal mixing give
$$
G=\sum_iN_i\mu_i=N\sum_i x_i\left[\mu_i^\circ(T)+\mathcal RT\log\left(\frac{x_iP}{P^\circ}\right)\right].
$$
Here $N=\sum_iN_i$, $x_i$ is the <mole fraction>, $\mathcal R$ is the molar gas constant, and $P^\circ$ is the standard <pressure>. Integrating $\partial\mu_i/\partial P=\mathcal RT/P$ and including the ideal mixing <entropy> proves $\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+\mathcal RT\log(x_iP/P^\circ)$. Reactions can change $N$, so fractions alone do not set the extensive <Gibbs free energy>. Its composition Hessian is $\mathcal RT(\delta_{ij}/N_i-1/N)$; it is positive semidefinite by the weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

= Gibbs free-energy Maxwell relation
{c}
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

Equality of mixed derivatives of $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP$ gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T
=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P.
$$

= Chemical-potential balance for a reaction
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

For a reaction with stoichiometric changes $\nu_i$, varying the reaction extent at fixed temperature and pressure gives
$$
dG=\left(\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i\right)d\xi.
$$
Equilibrium requires $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=0$.

= Thermochemical equilibrium
{parent=Chemical-potential balance for a reaction}

Thermochemical equilibrium requires every chemical reaction to have zero Gibbs free-energy change. For ideal reactants, the equilibrium constant is $K=\exp[-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)]$ after standard-state activity factors are included.

= Constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium
{parent=Thermochemical equilibrium}

At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, a closed reacting system reaches <thermochemical equilibrium> by minimizing <Gibbs free energy> over nonnegative species amounts subject to conserved elemental abundances. For an allowed <stoichiometric vector> $\nu$, an interior minimum has $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=0$. More generally the <chemical potential> vector lies in the row span of the elemental conservation matrix at positive concentrations, with the appropriate one-sided inequalities for absent species. The <first law of thermodynamics> gives the thermodynamic differential, while the <second law of thermodynamics> supplies the minimization direction.

= Reverse reaction rate from equilibrium thermodynamics
{parent=Thermochemical equilibrium}

For an elementary reversible reaction, detailed balance gives $k_f\prod_r n_r^{\nu_r}=k_r\prod_p n_p^{\nu_p}$ at equilibrium. Hence the reverse rate coefficient is the forward coefficient divided by the equilibrium constant, with units fixed by the reaction stoichiometry.

= Phase coexistence curve
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

Two phases coexist where their molar Gibbs free energies agree. Differentiating that equality along the coexistence curve relates its slope to the entropy and volume jumps.

= Maxwell construction
{c}
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}

= Maxwell equal-area construction
{c}
{synonym}

At fixed <temperature>, choose a coexistence <pressure> $p_*$ such that the gas and liquid have equal <chemical potentials>. Because $d\mu=v\,dp$, this gives $\int_{v_l}^{v_g}v\,dp=0$, or equivalently $\int_{v_l}^{v_g}[p(v,T)-p_*]\,dv=0$. The equal areas replace the unstable part of an isotherm by a horizontal coexistence segment.

= Phase diagram
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}
{wiki}

A phase diagram partitions a space of thermodynamic variables into regions occupied by different equilibrium phases and records the coexistence curves between them.

= Solidus
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$T_S(C)$}

The solidus bounds the entirely solid equilibrium region of a <phase diagram>. For a linear <liquidus> $T_L(C_l)=-mC_l$ and a constant <segregation coefficient> $C_s=k_D C_l$, the corresponding solidus is $T_S(C_s)=-mC_s/k_D$. The <concentration> argument is the solid <concentration>, not the neighboring liquid <concentration>.

= Lever rule
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$\phi=(\overline C-C_s)/(C_l-C_s)$}
{wiki}

The lever rule finds phase fractions from a horizontal tie line on a <phase diagram>. For solid and liquid <concentrations> $C_s,C_l$ and bulk <concentration> $\overline C$, conservation gives $\overline C=(1-\phi)C_s+\phi C_l$. Thus $\phi=(\overline C-C_s)/(C_l-C_s)$ is the <liquid fraction>, provided $C_s\le\overline C\le C_l$. For pure solid, $\phi=\overline C/C_l$.

= Bicritical point
{parent=Phase diagram}

A bicritical point is where two continuous transition lines and a first-order line meet, typically separating a disordered phase from two competing ordered phases.

= Liquidus
{parent=Phase diagram}
{wiki}

The liquidus is the locus in a <phase diagram> above which the equilibrium state is entirely liquid. In a dilute binary solution it is often approximated locally by a linear relation such as $T_L=T_0-mC$.

= Freezing-point depression
{parent=Liquidus}
{wiki}

Freezing-point depression is the lowering of a solvent's equilibrium freezing temperature by dissolved solute. In the linear dilute approximation, $T_L=T_0-mC$ with $m>0$.

= Eutectic system
{parent=Phase diagram}
{wiki}

An eutectic system has a composition and temperature at which a liquid is in equilibrium with two distinct solid phases. Cooling liquid of that composition through the eutectic temperature produces both solid phases together.

= Eutectic composition
{parent=Eutectic system}
{title2=$C_E$}

The eutectic composition is the liquid <concentration> at the <eutectic temperature>, where liquid and two solid phases coexist. It is a composition coordinate on a <phase diagram>, not the overall <concentration> of every sample in an <eutectic system>.

= Eutectic temperature
{title2=$T_E$}
{parent=Eutectic system}

The eutectic temperature is the temperature of simultaneous liquid–two-solid <phase coexistence> in an <eutectic system>. In directional freezing of a solution away from the eutectic composition, a <mushy layer> can end at this temperature, where its remaining liquid solidifies.

= First-order phase transition
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}

At a first-order phase transition, the Gibbs free energy is continuous while at least one first derivative, such as entropy or volume, jumps. A nonzero entropy jump produces latent heat $L=T\Delta S$.

= Two-phase scalar coexistence endpoint conditions
{parent=First-order phase transition}
{title2=$V'=V''=V^{(3)}=0,\quad V^{(4)}>0$}

A smooth scalar <Landau free energy> can end a two-phase <phase coexistence curve> by merging two minima and their intervening maximum. At a generic stable endpoint, its first three derivatives vanish and its fourth derivative is positive. A field shift and two mixed control parameters reduce the local form to a quartic <Landau free energy>; the order-parameter jump then vanishes and the <magnetic susceptibility> diverges. This conclusion assumes there is no competing third phase and that the endpoint lies inside the control-parameter domain. It is not a general theorem about every first-order line: a sextic potential with fixed negative quartic coefficient has a symmetry-related ordered coexistence line that ends at a triple point with a finite jump.

{parent=First-order phase transition}

= Classical nucleation theory
{parent=First-order phase transition}
{wiki}

Classical nucleation theory balances the positive surface free energy of a droplet against its negative bulk free-energy gain. In three dimensions,
$$
F(R)=4\pi\sigma R^2-\frac{4\pi}{3}\Delta R^3
$$
has critical radius $R^*=2\sigma/\Delta$ and barrier $F^*=16\pi\sigma^3/(3\Delta^2)$.

= Critical nucleus
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

A critical nucleus is the unstable droplet at the maximum of the nucleation free energy. Smaller droplets shrink, while larger droplets lower their free energy by growing.

= Arrhenius nucleation time
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

Thermal crossing of a nucleation barrier $F^*$ has rate proportional to $e^{-F^*/(k_BT)}$ and mean waiting time proportional to $e^{F^*/(k_BT)}$, up to a kinetic prefactor.

= Latent heat
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}
{wiki}

The latent heat from phase $\alpha$ to phase $\beta$ is
$$
L=T(S_\beta-S_\alpha)=H_\beta-H_\alpha
$$
per mole or per particle, according to the normalization used.

= Stefan number
{c}
{title2=$\mathrm{Ste}=c_p\Delta T/L$}
{parent=Latent heat}
{wiki}

The Stefan number compares sensible heat $c_p\Delta T$ with <latent heat> $L$ per unit mass. Some solidification literature uses its reciprocal as the Stefan parameter, so the defining formula must accompany the convention.

= Latent-to-sensible heat ratio
{title2=$S=L/(c_p\Delta T)$}
{parent=Stefan number}

= Inverse Stefan number
{synonym}

The latent-to-sensible heat ratio is the reciprocal of the <Stefan number> defined by $\mathrm{Ste}=c_p\Delta T/L$. Large $S$ means that phase change uses much more energy than changing temperature by $\Delta T$.

= Clausius-Clapeyron relation
{parent=Latent heat}
{c}
{wiki=Clausius–Clapeyron_relation}

Along a first-order coexistence curve,
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}
=\frac{S_\beta-S_\alpha}{V_\beta-V_\alpha}
=\frac{L}{T(V_\beta-V_\alpha)}.
$$
At a critical point the two phases merge, their entropy jump vanishes, and the latent heat tends to zero.

= Common-pressure melting-point slope
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$dT_m/dp=-T_m(1/\rho_s-1/\rho_l)/L$}

When the solid and liquid have the same <pressure>, equality of <chemical potentials> implies $-(s_l-s_s)dT+(1/\rho_l-1/\rho_s)dp=0$. With <latent heat of fusion> $L=T_m(s_l-s_s)$, this gives the displayed slope. If solid is less dense than liquid, raising the common pressure depresses the <melting> temperature. $T_m$ is absolute <temperature>. This coexistence-curve derivative differs from a thermomolecular pressure difference between equal-density phases.

= Conjugate variables

{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

= Thermodynamic conjugate variable
{synonym}

Two thermodynamic variables are conjugate when their product has units of energy and they occur as a paired term in a thermodynamic differential, such as pressure and volume or tension and length.

= First law of thermodynamics
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{title2=$dU=\delta Q-\delta W$}
{wiki}

The first law applies <conservation of energy> to a thermodynamic system. For a <closed system> with unchanged bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, write
$$
dU=\delta Q-\delta W,\qquad \Delta U=Q-W.
$$
Here $U$ is <internal energy>, $Q>0$ is <heat> entering the system and $W>0$ is <work> done by the system on its surroundings. Compression therefore gives negative $W$. Some references define <work> positive when done on the system; their plus sign represents the same balance with a different convention. If bulk <kinetic energy> or <potential energy> changes, include it in the total <energy> balance. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node15.html][MIT, First law and work sign convention]{ref}

Supplying $1000\,\mathrm J$ of <heat> while a gas does $300\,\mathrm J$ of <work> increases its <internal energy> by $700\,\mathrm J$. Compressing it with $200\,\mathrm J$ of input <work> in an <adiabatic process> instead gives $Q=0$, $W=-200\,\mathrm J$ and $\Delta U=200\,\mathrm J$.

The distinction between $d$ and $\delta$ records state versus path: <internal energy> is a <state function>, whereas <heat> and <work> depend on the process. For an <ideal gas> expanding reversibly at constant <temperature>, $W=Nk_BT\log(V_2/V_1)$ and $Q=W$, since <internal energy of an ideal gas> depends only on <temperature>. Choose $Nk_BT=1000\,\mathrm J$ and $V_2=2V_1$: then $Q=W=693.15\,\mathrm J$. An insulated <free expansion> into vacuum between the same equilibrium endpoints has $Q=W=0$. Both have $\Delta U=0$, yet their transfers differ. Their common <entropy> increase is $Nk_B\log2$. \a[https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node40.html][MIT, Ideal-gas entropy differences]{ref}

For a simple compressible equilibrium system of fixed composition, combining the laws gives
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV.
$$
Here $p$ is <pressure> and $V$ is <volume>. This identity relates neighboring equilibrium states. Identifying $T\,dS$ with actual <heat> and $p\,dV$ with actual <work> requires a suitable <reversible thermodynamic process>; an irreversible piston calculation uses the external resisting <pressure> for boundary <work>. This distinction prevents applying a <reversible ideal-gas adiabat> to an <irreversible adiabatic piston compression>.

= Adiabatic process
{parent=First law of thermodynamics}
{wiki}

An adiabatic process exchanges no heat with its surroundings, so $\delta Q=0$. A reversible adiabatic process is isentropic.

= Adiabatic compression
{parent=Adiabatic process}

= Adiabatic expansion
{parent=Adiabatic process}

= Isentropic process
{parent=Adiabatic process}
{wiki}

An isentropic process keeps the <entropy> constant. Every reversible <adiabatic process> is isentropic, while an irreversible adiabatic process can increase entropy.

= Reversible ideal-gas adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic process}

For a fixed amount of ideal gas with constant heat capacities, the first law and $\delta Q=0$ give
$$
TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant},
\qquad
pV^\gamma=\text{constant},
$$
where $\gamma=C_p/C_V$.

= Enthalpy
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

Enthalpy is $H=E+pV$ and satisfies $dH=T\,dS+V\,dp$ for a simple compressible system.

= Joule-Thomson coefficient
{c}
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$\mu=(\partial T/\partial p)_H$}

The temperature change per pressure change at fixed enthalpy is $\mu=(\partial T/\partial p)_H=[T(\partial V/\partial T)_p-V]/C_p$. A positive coefficient means cooling under an adiabatic throttle pressure drop.

= Virial criterion for Joule-Thomson cooling
{parent=Joule-Thomson coefficient}

For $p=k_BT[n+B_2(T)n^2]$, the coefficient is $\mu=N[TB_2'-B_2]/[C_p(1+2B_2n)]$. On the stable dilute branch it is positive precisely when $d(B_2/T)/dT>0$.

= Specific enthalpy
{title2=$h$}
{parent=Enthalpy}

Specific enthalpy is <enthalpy> per unit mass. For a simple compressible fluid of fixed composition, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
dh=T\,ds+\frac{dp}{\rho},
$$
where $T$ is <temperature>, $s$ is <specific entropy>, $p$ is <pressure>, and $\rho$ is <mass density>. Along an <isentropic flow>, $dh=dp/\rho$. For a <perfect gas> with constant <specific-heat ratio> $\gamma$, $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$.

= Polytropic enthalpy
{parent=Specific enthalpy}
{title2=$Q=(n+1)p/\rho$}

For $p=K\rho^{1+1/n}$ with $K,n>0$, the specific <enthalpy> relative to vacuum is $Q=(n+1)K\rho^{1/n}=(n+1)p/\rho$. It obeys $\nabla Q=\nabla p/\rho$ and $nD_tQ=-Q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$. The vacuum reference is fixed when these equations are combined with the <polytropic equation of state>.

= Enthalpy Maxwell relation
{parent=Enthalpy}

Since $T=(\partial H/\partial S)_p$ and $V=(\partial H/\partial p)_S$, equality of mixed derivatives gives $(\partial T/\partial p)_S=(\partial V/\partial S)_p$.

= Heat capacity at constant pressure
{parent=Enthalpy}

At fixed pressure, supplied heat equals enthalpy change; for constant $C_p$, $Q=C_p\Delta T$.

= Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities
{title2=$C_P-C_V$}
{parent=Heat capacity at constant pressure}

For a simple compressible system,
$$
C_P-C_V
=T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P
\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$
This follows by expressing the entropy as a function of temperature and volume and using a Maxwell relation.

= Diatomic ideal-gas enthalpy
{parent=Thermodynamics}

With translational and rotational modes active but vibration frozen, a diatomic ideal gas has $E=5Nk_BT/2$ and $H=7Nk_BT/2$.

= Irreversible adiabatic piston compression
{parent=Thermodynamics}

After a sudden increase to constant external pressure $p_1$, an insulated gas obeys $\Delta E=p_1(V_0-V_1)$ rather than a reversible adiabatic power law.

= Constant-pressure heating of an ideal gas
{parent=Thermodynamics}

At fixed pressure, $Q=\Delta H$ and the expansion work is $p\Delta V=Nk_B\Delta T$.

= Thermodynamic cycle
{parent=Thermodynamics}
{wiki}

A thermodynamic cycle returns a working substance to its initial state. Its net internal-energy change is zero, so the net work output equals net heat input.

= Thermal efficiency
{title2=$\eta=W/Q_{\rm in}$}
{parent=Thermodynamic cycle}
{wiki}

The thermal efficiency of a heat engine is the net work output divided by heat input. For a cycle absorbing $Q_{\rm in}$ and rejecting the positive amount $Q_{\rm out}$,
$$
\eta=1-\frac{Q_{\rm out}}{Q_{\rm in}}.
$$

= Otto cycle
{c}
{parent=Thermodynamic cycle}
{wiki}

The ideal Otto cycle has adiabatic compression, constant-volume heat addition, adiabatic expansion, and constant-volume heat rejection. For an ideal gas with compression ratio $r=V_1/V_2$ and specific-heat ratio $\gamma$,
$$
\eta=1-\frac1{r^{\gamma-1}}.
$$

= Diesel cycle
{c}
{parent=Thermodynamic cycle}
{wiki}

The ideal Diesel cycle consists of reversible adiabatic compression, constant-pressure heat addition, reversible adiabatic expansion, and constant-volume heat rejection.
