**<cetaceans> illustrate a sequence of inherited mammalian features, terrestrial specializations and subsequent aquatic transformations, followed by divergence in feeding and sensory <anatomy>.** This is a branching <phylogenetic tree>, not a procession of named <fossils> that can all be identified as direct ancestors. Here <Cetacea> includes living whales, dolphins and porpoises; <mammals> means the <crown group> containing living <monotremes> and <therians>, whereas <mammaliaforms> also includes close extinct relatives outside that <crown group>. Dates below are approximate millions of years ago, abbreviated Ma.

The starting condition is a terrestrial, non-mammaliaform <cynodont>, itself a <therapsid> within <Synapsida>. Along the mammalian <stem group>, enlargement of the <dentary> and reduction of the other lower-jaw bones accompanied more differentiated <teeth>, increasingly precise <dental occlusion> and elaboration of a <secondary palate>. These changes belong to a feeding and respiratory complex: a larger tooth-bearing bone carries the <mastication> load, differentiated <teeth> process food, and a bony partition separates nasal and oral passages. They were acquired in a mosaic; neither a full modern mammalian <jaw> nor the aquatic body plan appeared at the initial divergence from other <cynodonts>.

Early <mammaliaforms> show how <jaw> support and hearing became reorganized. <Morganucodon>, around the <Triassic>–<Jurassic> transition and Early <Jurassic>, retained the old <articular>–<quadrate> <jaw> joint alongside a <dentary>–<squamosal> joint. In the mammalian condition the <dentary>–<squamosal> articulation supports the <jaw>, while the <articular> and <quadrate> correspond to the <malleus> and <incus>; the <angular bone> contributes to the <ectotympanic>. This is <biological homology> supported by comparative <anatomy> and <biological development>, not merely similarity in function. <Hadrocodium>, about 195 Ma, records further changes in <jaw> attachment and the <braincase> consistent with increased separation of <mastication> and hearing. Its tiny <skull> does not preserve an entire living-style ear apparatus; the reconstruction depends partly on bony attachment evidence. <A new mammaliaform from the early Jurassic and evolution of mammalian characteristics by Zhe-Xi Luo and others>[Science 292, pp. 1535–1540; Hadrocodium skull and jaw comparisons]{ref}

The inherited mammalian package also includes <diphyodonty>, <hair> and <lactation>, although the latter two leave much poorer evidence in these particular <fossils> than do <teeth> and <jaws>. Precise adult <dental occlusion> is easier to maintain when repeated replacement ceases. The lineage subsequently passed through <Theria>, <Eutheria> and the ancestors of living <placental mammals>. These nested relationships matter: a <Cretaceous> <eutherian> is not automatically a member of the living placental <crown group>, and neither the first known <fossil> nor the date of a <tooth> innovation fixes the origin of an entire group.

The immediate terrestrial context of whale evolution is within <Artiodactyla>, broadly circumscribed to include <Cetacea>. Living <hippopotamuses> are the closest living relatives of <cetaceans>, not their ancestors. Some early whale <teeth> suggested relationships with extinct <mesonychians>, but dental resemblance alone was misleading. Early whale skeletons preserve the characteristic artiodactyl double-pulley <astragalus>, providing a powerful independent anatomical test of the relationship inferred from molecular evidence. The relevant comparison is the detailed construction of the ankle, not simply whether an animal has hooves or eats meat. <Skeletons of terrestrial cetaceans and the relationship of whales to artiodactyls by J. G. M. Thewissen and others>[Nature 413, pp. 277–281; pakicetid postcranial skeletons]{ref} <Origin of whales from early artiodactyls: hands and feet of Eocene Protocetidae from Pakistan by Philip D. Gingerich and others>[Science 293, pp. 2239–2242; protocetid ankle anatomy]{ref}

Early <Eocene> <pakicetids>, roughly 50 Ma, combine a largely terrestrial, weight-bearing skeleton with diagnostic whale ear-region <anatomy>, notably the thickened medial wall of the <auditory bulla> called the <involucrum>. Thus identification as a whale does not imply an obligately marine animal. <Ambulocetus>, roughly 48 Ma, has a different combination: robust limbs still useful around shore, large feet and an axial skeleton suited to substantial movement in water. Reconstruction of amphibious locomotion uses limb proportions, joint surfaces and vertebral <anatomy> together; it does not require assuming that its swimming exactly resembled an otter's. These are successive grades represented by branching relatives, not proof that a particular <pakicetid> gave birth to the known <Ambulocetus> specimen. <From Land to Water: the Origin of Whales, Dolphins, and Porpoises by J. G. M. Thewissen and others>[sections on pakicetids, ambulocetids, protocetids and basilosaurids]{ref}

Middle <Eocene> <protocetids> further document reduction of terrestrial support and increasing commitment to swimming. Changes in the connection between <pelvis> and <vertebral column>, limb proportions and the position of external <nostrils> occurred in different combinations. The ankle of <Rodhocetus> is especially informative about ancestry even while other parts of its skeleton document aquatic specialization. It would be unjustified to draw a preserved <tail fluke> onto <Rodhocetus>: a reconstructed swimming mode must distinguish actual preserved <anatomy> from inferred soft parts. <Origin of whales from early artiodactyls: hands and feet of Eocene Protocetidae from Pakistan by Philip D. Gingerich and others>[Science 293, pp. 2239–2242; protocetid ankle anatomy]{ref}

Aquatic hearing changed alongside locomotion. Enlargement of the mandibular sound-reception region and changes in the middle ear support a transition towards receiving underwater sound through the lower <jaw>, with increasing acoustic separation of the ears from the <skull>. Early whale lineages display different intermediate combinations, so the presence of an <involucrum> alone does not imply a modern dolphin hearing system. Reconstructing soft sound-conducting tissues requires comparison with living <anatomy>; the <fossil> preserves their bony context rather than the entire pathway. <Eocene evolution of whale hearing by Sirpa Nummela and others>[Nature 430, pp. 776–778; sound transmission reconstructed from early whale ear and jaw anatomy]{ref}

By the later <Eocene>, <basilosaurids> were fully aquatic. The <forelimbs> functioned as <flippers>, the <vertebral column> and tail supplied propulsion, and the <hind limbs> were far too small to support terrestrial walking. Nevertheless, the reduced limbs of <Basilosaurus> retain recognizable limb and foot elements. Loss of a locomotor role is not equivalent to absence of all function; reproductive or muscular roles are compatible with reduction, although a specific behaviour cannot be read directly from a bone. The transition therefore involved changing proportions and connections as well as losses. <Hind limbs of Eocene Basilosaurus: evidence of feet in whales by Philip D. Gingerich and others>[Science 249, pp. 154–157; reduced pelvis and hind limbs]{ref}

Finally, the living branches acquired different specializations. <Odontoceti> retain <teeth> and evolved specialized sound production and <echolocation>; <Mysticeti> evolved <baleen> and lack functional adult <teeth>. <fossil> toothed <mysticetes> and degraded enamel-related <genes> show that modern toothlessness is derived. Palatal vascular features have been used to infer an intermediate combination of <teeth> and <baleen>, but such foramina do not preserve the <baleen> itself, and the exact sequence of feeding behaviours is less secure than the transition from toothed ancestry. <Morphological and molecular evidence for a stepwise evolutionary transition from teeth to baleen in mysticete whales by Thomas A. Deméré and others>[Systematic Biology 57, pp. 15–37; palatal foramina, tooth characters and enamel-gene pseudogenes]{ref} The resulting history explains why a whale can have a mammalian <jaw> and reproductive system, an artiodactyl ancestry, a radically modified swimming skeleton, and a feeding apparatus that evolved after the aquatic transition. **The strongest reconstruction combines nested anatomical characters with independently dated <fossils>, while treating soft-tissue and behavioural reconstructions as hypotheses.**

**Repeated dietary adaptations reflect recurring mechanical and digestive demands, but a similar <diet> need not produce identical <anatomy>, and anatomical resemblance need not represent an independent evolutionary origin.** A useful comparison places each example on a <phylogenetic tree>, identifies the inherited structure being modified, and separates food acquisition, oral processing and digestion.

Among dental adaptations, shearing blades recur in meat-eating <mammals>. In <Carnivora>, a placental order within <Laurasiatheria>, the main <carnassial> pair comprises the upper fourth <premolar> and lower first <molar>. Their opposing blades slice flesh, while relative development of crushing surfaces varies with <diet>. Predatory <marsupials>, including the extinct <thylacine> within <Dasyuromorphia>, also emphasize shearing, but do not reproduce precisely the same specialized <tooth> pair. The <jaw> and basic <tooth> classes are inherited mammalian structures; the highly blade-like feeding apparatus represents <convergent evolution> of performance in separate <therian> branches. Within <Carnivora>, resemblance between close relatives may instead reflect shared ancestry, and specialized insect-eating or plant-eating members demonstrate that membership of the order does not establish a carnivorous <diet>.

Repeated elaboration of grinding surfaces provides a complementary case. <lophodonty> joins <tooth> cusps into ridges, and <selenodonty> produces crescentic ridges, maintaining effective cutting edges as the surface wears. Herbivorous <perissodactyls>, including <horses>, contrast with <ruminants> such as bovids and cervids in <Artiodactyla>; both are within <Laurasiatheria>. Complex grinding <teeth> also evolved in <proboscideans> within <Afrotheria> and in <macropods>, a <marsupial> lineage within <Diprotodontia>. These positions rule out treating every complex herbivore <tooth> as one recent innovation inherited from a common grazing ancestor. Three-dimensional measurements of occlusal surface complexity in <carnivorans> and <rodents> show that dietary demands can produce comparable patterns across very different <tooth> constructions. The useful prediction concerns functional surface geometry, not identical cusp names in every group. <High-level similarity of dentitions in carnivorans and rodents by Alistair R. Evans and others>[Nature 445, pp. 78–81; three-dimensional occlusal complexity and diet]{ref}

Resistance to abrasion repeatedly modifies <tooth> growth as well as <tooth> shape. <hypsodonty> supplies a tall reserve crown that is progressively exposed by wear; it occurs in several ungulate groups and in some <marsupials>. It differs from <hypselodonty>, in which <teeth> continue growing, as in rodent <incisors>. Self-sharpening <incisors>, with uneven distribution of <tooth enamel> and differential wear of dental tissues, facilitate gnawing. <Rodentia> and <Lagomorpha> are both within <Glires>, so their <incisor> similarities must be analysed against that relationship rather than automatically counted as two independent origins of the whole dental system.

A high crown is a wear-management adaptation, not an infallible grass detector. Abrasive particles can come from vegetation or ingested external dust and grit; food toughness and abrasiveness are also distinct. North American <horse> <fossils> show substantial variation in <dental mesowear> within populations and temporal relationships between dietary abrasion and crown-height changes that are more complicated than an instantaneous switch from browsing to grazing. <Dietary Change and Evolution of Horses in North America by Matthew C. Mihlbachler and others>[Science 331, pp. 1178–1181; mesowear compared with crown-height evolution]{ref} A defensible palaeodiet reconstruction combines <tooth> form with <dental microwear>, <dental mesowear>, <stable isotope analysis> where suitable, and environmental evidence. <dental microwear> emphasizes relatively recent meals, whereas crown form reflects inherited <morphology> across generations. Disagreement can therefore be informative rather than an analytical failure.

A major non-dental recurrence is an enlarged gut compartment supporting microbial <fermentation>. <mammals> do not obtain the full energy value of structural plant carbohydrates through their own <enzymes> alone; microbial partners release usable <fermentation> products, notably <short-chain fatty acids>. <foregut fermentation> evolved in <ruminants>, <colobine monkeys> within <Primates>, and <macropods>. The forestomach compartments are not identical organs merely because each houses <microbial digestion>: the relevant independent changes concern enlargement, compartmentalization, retention and mixing in phylogenetically separated lineages. In <ruminants>, <rumination> additionally returns food for further <mastication>, increasing the surface available to <microorganisms>.

<hindgut fermentation> provides another solution. <horses>, within <Perissodactyla>, use enlarged caecal and colonic compartments; <rabbits>, within <Lagomorpha>, combine caecal processing with <cecotrophy>, reingesting selected soft material and thereby recovering microbial nutrients through a second passage. <foregut fermentation> allows microbial products and microbial biomass to encounter later digestive regions, whereas <hindgut fermentation> can accommodate high intake and rapid passage of some food fractions. Neither arrangement is universally superior: retention time, food quality, <body size>, intake rate and the ability to select particles affect the balance. The comparative explanation of equid success therefore requires throughput as well as percentage extraction from a single meal. <The evolutionary strategy of the Equidae and the origins of rumen and cecal digestion by Christine M. Janis>[Evolution 30, pp. 757–774; retention, throughput and digestive strategies]{ref}

Food acquisition supplies a further non-dental example. <anteaters>, within <Xenarthra>, and <pangolins>, within <Pholidota> in <Laurasiatheria>, independently combine reduction or loss of <teeth> with an elongated, protrusible <tongue> and a feeding apparatus suited to taking small social insects. Powerful <forelimbs> and <claws> can open nests, but digging also serves many other functions; <claws> alone do not identify ant feeding. Conversely, absence of grinding <teeth> need not signify nutritional simplicity: prey capture, protection from prey defences and <microbial digestion> can remain specialized.

Comparative dissection of primate digestive tracts supplies an independent anatomical test: gut compartments and their relative dimensions can be compared with <diet> rather than guessed from the <teeth>. Such comparisons must account for <body size> and the non-independence of related taxa. <Morphology of the gastrointestinal tract in primates: comparisons with other mammals in relation to diet by David J. Chivers and others>[Journal of Morphology 166, pp. 337–386; comparative gut measurements and dietary categories]{ref} Testing these interpretations requires more than a gallery of look-alikes. Record <diet> in wild animals, compare anatomical performance under relevant loads, measure gut retention or digestive products, and include relatives with different <diets>. Control for <body size>, age, sex, <tooth> wear stage and shared ancestry, since ten related grazers are not ten independent evolutionary experiments. For <fossils>, preserve the distinction between direct anatomical observation and an inferred gut or behaviour. **The repeated solutions are cutting or grinding surfaces, mechanisms that sustain those surfaces under wear, specialized acquisition structures and microbial digestive chambers; their phylogenetic distribution reveals both convergence and inherited constraints.**

**Southern <fossils> have changed the geography, timing and anatomical character of mammaliaform evolution, while exposing how strongly a northern <fossil> record had shaped earlier expectations.** Their importance is not that every southern lineage arose in the south. A dated occurrence establishes presence and a minimum age; an evolutionary origin requires an argument from <phylogenetics> and <biogeography>. Nor do modern continental outlines reproduce the connections of <Gondwana>.

A particularly consequential example is <Ambondro mahabo>, represented by a <jaw> fragment with lower <teeth> from Middle <Jurassic> Madagascar, approximately $167\pm2$ Ma. The <teeth> possess a basined heel associated with the crushing component of <tribosphenic molars>, which combine shearing and crushing in a single occlusal system. Its age extended the then-known record of this dental construction substantially backwards and challenged a simple northern origin inferred from better-sampled northern localities. The material nevertheless consists of a very restricted part of the animal, and the matching upper <dentition> was not directly observed. Assigning the entire skeleton, physiology or reproductive mode from these <teeth> would go beyond the evidence. <A Middle Jurassic mammal from Madagascar by John J. Flynn and others>[Nature 401, pp. 57–60; Ambondro lower jaw, approximately 167 ± 2 million years old]{ref}

The discovery also changed the character of the phylogenetic question. One hypothesis groups several southern forms in <Australosphenida>, associated with the ancestry of <monotremes>, and proposes that a tribosphenic-like construction arose independently of northern <Boreosphenida>, including <therians>. In that interpretation a highly functional <tooth> arrangement is <convergent evolution>, not a unique certificate of therian ancestry. A competing analysis supports a single origin of <tribospheny> and rearranges the relationships of the relevant southern taxa. <Dual origin of tribosphenic mammals by Zhe-Xi Luo and others>[Nature 409, pp. 53–57; alternative placements of southern tribosphenic mammals]{ref} <The evolution of tribospheny and the antiquity of mammalian clades by Michael O. Woodburne and others>[phylogenetic analysis supporting a single origin of tribospheny]{ref} The important lesson is not to declare one historical topology established by the striking <tooth> alone. The choice depends on which cusps and basins are judged instances of <biological homology>, character coding, inclusion of other anatomical regions, and the taxon sample. Nor does a Middle <Jurassic> tribosphenic <tooth> by itself date the split between living <marsupials> and <placental mammals>.

South American <monotreme> material further undermined the equation of present distribution with evolutionary birthplace. Early <Paleocene> <Monotrematum>, approximately 60–65 Ma, has <teeth> with affinities to the <platypus> lineage. Living <monotremes> are restricted to Australasia, but that restriction cannot be projected unchanged into the past. <First discovery of monotremes in South America by Rosendo Pascual and others>[Nature 356, pp. 704–706; Paleocene Monotrematum teeth]{ref} South America, Antarctica and Australia offered changing opportunities for connection, fragmentation and movement. A South American occurrence is compatible with a geographically extensive southern history; it does not uniquely establish the direction of dispersal or prove that <Monotrematum> was the direct ancestor of the living <platypus>. The distinction between a <crown group> and related members of its <stem group> is essential when attaching such <fossils> to divergence-time estimates.

Latest <Cretaceous> Madagascar has contributed more than isolated <tooth> surprises. The cranium of <Vintana>, around 70 Ma, supplied extensive new characters for the formerly tooth-dominated <Gondwanatheria>. Its robust feeding apparatus and unusual cranial combination permit tests of relationships and ecology that were unavailable from a few <teeth>. Yet a mosaic of familiar and unusual features is precisely the situation in which a single apparently diagnostic character can mislead. The placement of <gondwanatherians> therefore needs broad anatomical and taxonomic comparison, including other extinct groups, rather than assignment to a living order by superficial resemblance. <First cranial remains of a gondwanatherian mammal reveal remarkable mosaicism by David W. Krause and others>[Nature 515, pp. 512–517; Vintana cranium and phylogenetic character sampling]{ref}

The approximately 66 Ma skeleton of <Adalatherium> expanded that evidence into the postcranial skeleton. It documents a relatively large, several-kilogram non-therian mammal with an unusual combination of cranial, dental, vertebral and limb features. This contradicts the caricature that all <Mesozoic> <mammaliaforms> were anatomically interchangeable tiny insect-eaters. Madagascar's long isolation provides a plausible context for its evolutionary distinctiveness, but the claim that isolation caused a particular feature remains an explanatory hypothesis. One needs comparative island and continental samples to distinguish an island effect from inherited lineage-specific <anatomy>. <Skeleton of a Cretaceous mammal from Madagascar reflects long-term insularity by David W. Krause and others>[Nature 581, pp. 421–427; Adalatherium skeleton and insularity hypothesis]{ref}

Australia's early <Eocene> <Djarthia>, approximately 55 Ma, addresses a different issue: the relationships and movement of <marsupials>. Its ankle material supports a position close to the base of <Australidelphia>, a group containing the living Australian orders and the South American <Microbiotheria>. This is stronger evidence than assuming that every small Australian <jaw> belongs inside a modern Australian family. <Australia's Oldest Marsupial Fossils and their Biogeographical Implications by Robin M. D. Beck and others>[PLoS ONE 3, e1858; Djarthia tarsals and australidelphian relationships]{ref} It is compatible with a history involving South America and Antarctica, but a branching order is not a map of a single migration. An extinct distribution can be extensive, intermediate populations can disappear, and the oldest sampled <fossil> need not occur near the place where a lineage originated. Also, the older and more inclusive <Metatheria> must not be confused with the living marsupial <crown group>.

Together these discoveries change how large-scale history is tested. First, expanding southern sampling can overturn a supposed absence that arose from collecting intensity, exposure of suitable rocks or preservation. <taphonomy> and the concentration of many early records on durable <teeth> must be modelled before comparing continental diversity. Second, new skeletons can change the inferred <phylogenetic tree>, and therefore whether a <tooth> adaptation counts as one origin or several. Third, <fossil calibration> constrains the minimum age of a lineage, with explicit uncertainty in both geological age and phylogenetic assignment. Fourth, <continental drift> changes which dispersal routes were physically plausible. Shared distributions across former <Gondwana> may reflect <vicariance>, later dispersal, or a mixture; the geological separation must be compared with a defensible lineage age rather than used as its automatic explanation.

From the <Jurassic> through the <Palaeogene>, these independent discoveries have cumulatively changed both the anatomical evidence and its interpretation. **They reveal a more diverse southern mammaliaform history and force explicit tests of convergence, missing data and geographic sampling, rather than replacing an assumed northern centre with an equally assumed southern centre.**

**Repeated movements out of Africa are compatible with a predominant shared source for the ancestry of living non-Africans.** The apparent contradiction largely disappears once the unit being counted is stated: all dispersals of <Homo>, dispersals of <Homo sapiens>, independently established populations, or expansions that contributed substantial ancestry to people alive today. These are different historical claims.

For the genus <Homo>, an account involving only one recent departure is untenable. Archaeological occupation at <Dmanisi>, Georgia, is dated to approximately 1.85–1.78 Ma, long before the late <Pleistocene> expansion of <Homo sapiens>. The earliest occupation levels must be distinguished from the somewhat younger hominin-bearing deposits: the site is not a single instantaneous event. Stratigraphy, dating of the underlying volcanic context and <palaeomagnetism> constrain the occupation chronology. <Earliest human occupations at Dmanisi (Georgian Caucasus) dated to 1.85–1.78 Ma by Reid Ferring and others>[Results, stratigraphy and palaeomagnetic chronology; distinguish earliest occupation from hominin-bearing beds]{ref} Later Eurasian archaic lineages, including <Neanderthals> and <Denisovans>, cannot simply be folded into the recent expansion of modern humans as though all were the same population moving at the same time.

Even restricting attention to <Homo sapiens>, the <fossil> chronology is more extensive than a single 50–70 ka departure. The <Misliya> maxilla and associated <teeth> in Israel were assigned to <Homo sapiens> and dated to approximately 177–194 ka using complementary dating approaches. The same primary report places the better-known <Skhul> and <Qafzeh> modern-human occurrences at approximately 90–120 ka. <The earliest modern humans outside Africa by Israel Hershkovitz and others>[Science 359, pp. 456–459; maxillary morphology and 177–194 ka dating interval]{ref} These occurrences demonstrate presence outside Africa before the dominant later expansion if the taxonomic and chronological assignments hold. They do not by themselves demonstrate uninterrupted occupation, a particular migration route through Arabia, or a large surviving genetic contribution to present-day Eurasians.

<Apidima> adds an important but less secure claim. Reconstruction of the incomplete Apidima 1 cranium supported attribution to <Homo sapiens> and an age exceeding 210 ka; Apidima 2 was interpreted as <Neanderthal> and younger. <Apidima Cave fossils provide earliest evidence of Homo sapiens in Eurasia by Katerina Harvati and others>[Nature 571, pp. 500–504; reconstruction and uranium-series dating of Apidima 1 and 2]{ref} The very fragmentary <anatomy>, reconstruction choices and complicated depositional context require scrutiny. An assigned minimum age is not an exact date of arrival, and a different reconstruction or taxonomic diagnosis can alter the biogeographic inference. The sensible argument for early departures therefore uses the converging set of dated remains and its uncertainty, rather than treating the oldest proposed specimen as a decisive, uncontested fact.

The main evidence favouring a single principal source is genetic. Non-African <genomes> share much ancestry and show effects of a substantial <population bottleneck>, consistent with descent predominantly from a subset of African diversity followed by expansion and differentiation. Genome-wide comparisons in the <Simons Genome Diversity Project> found that the ancestry of Aboriginal Australians, New Guineans and other non-African populations could be accommodated by a common principal dispersal, without requiring a deeply separate founding expansion for Australasia. <The Simons Genome Diversity Project: 300 genomes from 142 diverse populations by Swapan Mallick and others>[population history analyses comparing Australo-Melanesian and other non-African ancestry]{ref} Such a result favours a parsimonious demographic model for the bulk of surviving ancestry. It does not observe a unique group crossing a border on one date, nor rule out movements whose descendants disappeared.

Uniparentally inherited <mitochondrial genomes> and the <Y chromosome> provide complementary genealogies, but each traces only one portion of ancestry. Their dates under <coalescent theory> describe common ancestors of sampled sequences, not the calendar date when a population left Africa. <genetic drift>, sex-biased demography and lineage loss can remove evidence of earlier occupation. Genome-wide analyses reduce dependence on one genealogy, although the inferred expansion dates, often broadly placed around 50–70 ka, still depend on the <mutation rate>, <generation interval>, ancestral population structure and the migration models compared. A prolonged connected expansion can resemble one founder event in a simplified model.

There is also genetic evidence that complicates an absolute single-expansion claim. One analysis inferred a small contribution to Papuan ancestry from an earlier expansion, while still attributing the great majority of non-African ancestry to a shared later expansion. <Genomic analyses inform on migration events during the peopling of Eurasia by Luca Pagani and others>[analyses of a predominant shared expansion and proposed earlier ancestry in Papuans]{ref} The size and necessity of such a component are model-dependent: unsampled ancestral structure or archaic <introgression> can mimic some signals. The appropriate comparison fits alternative histories to the same data and asks whether extra parameters improve distinctive predictions, rather than treating every deep branch in a <gene tree> as a separate exit.

Archaic <admixture> makes the ancestry history explicitly reticulate. Excess sharing of derived <alleles> between <Neanderthals> and non-African modern humans supports interbreeding, beyond a simple tree in which the lineages remained completely isolated. <A draft sequence of the Neandertal genome by Richard E. Green and others>[Science 328, pp. 710–722; excess allele sharing and evidence of admixture]{ref} Thus a model in which most modern-human ancestry comes from one late expansion can still contain genetic contributions from populations whose ancestors left Africa much earlier. <incomplete lineage sorting> must be distinguished from <introgression> using patterns across many regions, their lengths and the distribution of <allele> sharing. Back-migration into Africa further prevents “African” and “non-African” from being permanently sealed categories.

Africa itself should not be represented as a homogeneous ancestral village. Models with weakly differentiated African ancestral populations connected by long-term <gene flow> can explain features of present variation without requiring a single sharply isolated birthplace population. <A weakly structured stem for human origins in Africa by Aaron P. Ragsdale and others>[Nature 617, pp. 755–763; model comparison with long-term gene flow among ancestral African populations]{ref} That result concerns ancestry within Africa; it neither erases the African origin of <Homo sapiens> nor directly counts subsequent exits. Geographically, northern routes through the Levant and southern routes through Arabia remain testable alternatives or complements. <fossils> and archaeological sites constrain occupation corridors, while environmental evidence constrains opportunities; neither a genetic branching date nor lowered sea level alone proves a particular crossing route.

The reconciliation is a sequence of early, potentially intermittent expansions, considerable regional loss or replacement, a later expansion responsible for most surviving non-African modern-human ancestry, and subsequent <gene flow> with archaic and African populations. Testing it requires directly dated remains, secure taxonomic comparisons, independent genetic samples, explicit demographic alternatives and allowance for unsampled populations. **“One dominant ancestry expansion” is a much narrower and better-supported proposition than “only one movement out of Africa”; multiple movements need not imply several equally large surviving founding ancestries.**

= Solution

**Living <great apes> are indispensable comparative evidence, but none is a preserved version of the Pan–Homo last common ancestor.** The ancestor was a population with its own combination of traits, and every living descendant has subsequently evolved. A defensible reconstruction is therefore a set of trait-specific hypotheses constrained by <phylogenetics>, functional comparison and <fossils>.

The comparison starts with relationships. <Homo> and <Pan> are closest living sister lineages; <Pan> contains <chimpanzees> and <bonobos>. <Gorilla> provides a more distant comparison, and <Pongo> a more distant one again. Humans are themselves <great apes>, rather than a group standing outside them. A character shared by <Pan> and <Homo> may have been inherited from their <last common ancestor>, but it can also have changed in either lineage or arisen in parallel. A shared feature of <Pan> and <Gorilla> does not automatically reconstruct the ancestor either: it may be ancestral, convergent, or retained in some branches and lost in others.

Living <great apes> are especially valuable because their soft tissues, growth, behaviour and mechanical performance can be studied. Muscle paths, joint motion and hand loading can make competing functional interpretations of a <fossil> <wrist> testable. Observing climbing and terrestrial movement establishes which combinations are mechanically possible; it also exposes the inadequacy of labelling a whole animal simply “arboreal” or “terrestrial”. Appropriate controls compare individuals of different ages and sexes, adjust for <body size>, record substrate and loading, and distinguish performance in captivity from behaviour in the wild. A surface that resembles another surface is weak functional evidence unless its position in the complete joint and the range of loads are considered.

The same accessibility creates temptations. <chimpanzee> locomotion, male coalitions or territorial behaviour cannot be transferred wholesale to the common ancestor. <bonobos> demonstrate variation within <Pan>, not a second unchanged ancestral template. Ecology, group composition, developmental plasticity and histories of selection differ among living populations. Current behaviour can generate hypotheses about feasible ancestral social systems, but proving that a particular ancestral behaviour existed requires additional evidence; most social behaviours do not fossilize. Treating the contrast as a choice between a uniformly aggressive <chimpanzee> ancestor and a uniformly peaceful <bonobo> ancestor replaces biology with caricature.

<knuckle-walking> illustrates the genuine scientific alternatives. One reconstruction takes the shared use of knuckle support in <Pan> and <Gorilla> as evidence for an ancestral condition, with later loss or modification in the <hominin> lineage. <wrist> features in early <hominins> have been interpreted as retained support for that hypothesis. <Evidence that humans evolved from a knuckle-walking ancestor by Brian G. Richmond and others>[Nature 404, pp. 382–385; wrist characters interpreted as retained knuckle-walking adaptations]{ref} A competing study emphasized differences in the development and functional organization of <chimpanzee> and <Gorilla> <wrists>, consistent with independent origins of their knuckle-walking systems. <Independent evolution of knuckle-walking in African apes shows that humans did not evolve from a knuckle-walking ancestor by Tracy L. Kivell and others>[PNAS 106, pp. 14241–14246; contrasts between chimpanzee and gorilla wrist development and function]{ref} Similar external posture can therefore conceal distinct load-bearing arrangements. Neither simply counting two living knuckle-walking genera nor quoting the conclusion of one comparative study settles the ancestral condition. The inference depends on <biological homology>, functional interpretation and inclusion of relevant extinct forms.

<Ardipithecus ramidus>, approximately 4.4 Ma, is particularly important because its skeleton combines a grasping great toe and arboreal capabilities with <anatomy> interpreted as supporting terrestrial <bipedalism>. The authors argued that its hands and feet did not represent an unchanged modern <chimpanzee> locomotor package. <Ardipithecus ramidus and the paleobiology of early hominids by Tim D. White and others>[Science 326, pp. 75–86; 4.4 Ma skeleton and locomotor mosaic]{ref} This weakens a reconstruction that derives early <hominins> by merely standing a modern <chimpanzee> upright. However, <Ardipithecus> lived after the inferred <Pan>–<Homo> divergence; its features may include specializations of its own. Its proposed <hominin> position and functional reconstruction must also be evaluated against incomplete preservation and distortion, especially in the <pelvis>. It is evidence about a nearby branch, not direct observation of the common ancestor.

Older <Miocene> <apes> extend the comparison beyond surviving lineages. <Pierolapithecus>, approximately 12.5–13 Ma, combines an upright-trunk construction (<orthogrady>) with hand <anatomy> lacking the complete suspensory specialization of some living <apes>. <Pierolapithecus catalaunicus, a new Middle Miocene great ape from Spain by Salvador Moyà-Solà and others>[Science 306, pp. 1339–1344; orthograde trunk and contrasting hand morphology]{ref} The example shows that trunk posture, climbing and <suspensory locomotion> can evolve as a mosaic. Its exact position is debated, and it is not thereby identified as the <Pan>–<Homo> ancestor. Rather, extinct combinations expand the set of plausible ancestral forms and can reveal that a feature widespread among living <apes> was assembled in stages.

Genetic evidence establishes relationships and helps constrain timing, but a molecular divergence estimate is not a <fossil> anatomical reconstruction. Estimates are sensitive to <mutation rate>, calibration and <generation interval>. Pedigree observations of wild <chimpanzees> and <Gorilla> demonstrate why substituting one universal generation length can shift the inferred dates of great-ape divergences. <Generation times in wild chimpanzees and gorillas suggest earlier divergence times in great ape and human evolution by Kevin E. Langergraber and others>[PNAS 109, pp. 15716–15721; pedigrees, generation intervals and divergence-time sensitivity]{ref} A <gene tree> can also coalesce before a population split, and <incomplete lineage sorting> means that not every genomic region follows the species branching order. There is consequently no reason to force every candidate <fossil> into an overly precise split date selected from one molecular estimate.

The best procedure is to specify competing ancestral states and their predictions: for example, whether a particular <wrist> character should accompany habitual knuckle support, or whether an inferred climbing apparatus requires suspensory finger proportions. Score those characters across <Pan>, <Homo>, <Gorilla>, <Pongo> and well-preserved <fossil> taxa; examine sensitivity to uncertain character coding and alternative <fossil> placements. Related features must not be counted as a large number of independent confirmations when they are mechanically or developmentally coupled. Extant-only <ancestral state reconstruction> is especially vulnerable to the <extinction> of lineages that possessed combinations absent today.

**Living <great apes> supply the comparative mechanics, development and diversity needed to interpret <fossils>; <fossils> prevent the living sample from dictating a false ancestral stereotype.** A cautious composite reconstruction, with separate uncertainty for posture, hand use, <diet> and behaviour, is more defensible than choosing one living species as the ancestor's complete anatomical and social model.

**The Holocene history combines natural climatic and geographic filtering with increasingly strong human restructuring through hunting, habitat change, <domestication>, introductions and conservation.** The balance varies through time and between Britain and Ireland. The <Holocene> begins approximately 11.7 thousand calibrated years before present; for <radiocarbon dating>, “present” conventionally means 1950. Uncalibrated radiocarbon ages must not be silently treated as calendar ages.

At the beginning, warming following the last glaciation changed vegetation and the distribution of food and cover. Open, cold-adapted assemblages gave way to a changing mixture of woodland, wetland and open-habitat <mammals>. Rising seas progressively restricted natural colonization; Britain retained connections to continental Europe later than Ireland's faunal assembly can simply be assumed to have done. The modern Irish fauna is therefore not a complete British fauna subsequently pruned by identical extinctions. A missing Irish species may never have colonized, rather than having been exterminated there. <pollen analysis>, stratigraphy and directly dated bones help distinguish habitat change from collecting gaps.

Chronology prevents an equally misleading division into wholly “Pleistocene” losses and wholly modern survivors. Some conspicuous cold-stage <mammals>, such as the woolly rhinoceros, disappeared from Britain before the <Holocene> and should not automatically be included in a Holocene hunting-extinction list. Conversely, directly dated <giant deer> from the northern Irish Sea basin demonstrate early <Holocene> survival of a lineage once thought to have disappeared regionally earlier. <Survival of the Irish elk into the Holocene by Silvia Gonzalez and others>[Nature 405, pp. 753–754; directly dated giant deer from the northern Irish Sea basin]{ref} A date from one part of this region does not establish persistence everywhere in Ireland or Britain. Local disappearance, regional disappearance and global <extinction> are different endpoints; environmental deterioration and human activity need to be evaluated at the appropriate geographic and chronological scale.

Later prehistory saw a profound change in mammalian abundance as farming spread. Domestic <cattle>, <sheep>, <goats> and <pigs> became major components of archaeological assemblages and living biomass. Grazing, clearance and woodland management altered habitat for wild <herbivores> and their <predators>; settlement and stored food also favoured commensal <rodents>. This was more than adding several species to an unchanged ecosystem. Distinguishing domestic from wild forms requires skeletal comparisons, context and, where available, <Ancient DNA> and <stable isotope analysis>. A large number of livestock bones in a settlement midden is evidence of human use, not a direct census of every mammal in the surrounding landscape.

Losses of large native species were staggered. British <aurochs>, <wild boar>, <lynx>, <brown bears>, <beavers> and <wolves> disappeared at different times through combinations of hunting, persecution, habitat transformation and <habitat fragmentation>. The list cannot be transferred intact to Ireland: a secure native record must be established for each taxon, and a late imported specimen does not demonstrate an earlier indigenous population. Small and elusive species are also less visible in many archaeological deposits, so the absence of their remains should not be equated with a known <extinction> date.

The dated British <lynx> record is especially instructive. Survival into the early medieval period makes an explanation based solely on warming at the start of the <Holocene> inadequate. Human habitat change and direct or indirect persecution become more plausible proximate causes, although the last dated specimen cannot identify which pressure killed the final population. <New evidence for the occurrence of Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) in medieval Britain by David A. Hetherington and others>[Journal of Quaternary Science 21, pp. 3–8; directly dated lynx bone and implications for extinction causes]{ref} <brown bear> history is less tidy: review of the British evidence supports either late Neolithic/early Bronze Age or early medieval <extinction>, and later archaeological <bear> remains can represent imported captive animals rather than surviving wild populations. <The presence of the brown bear Ursus arctos in Holocene Britain: a review of the evidence by Hannah J. O'Regan>[assessment of native survival versus imported animals and alternative extinction chronologies]{ref} Traditional stories of the “last wolf” likewise require caution; historically documented persecution is much stronger evidence of human pressure than an unverified exact last-kill year.

Human transport makes apparent continuity equally uncertain. Combined skeletal, <Ancient DNA> and <phylogeography> evidence indicates a complex Irish <red deer> history involving prehistoric and recent introductions, rather than simple uninterrupted survival of every modern population from the last glaciation. Neolithic-era records and the contrast between older Kerry-associated ancestry and many recent stocks must be interpreted with sampling gaps in mind. <Phylogeographic, ancient DNA, fossil and morphometric analyses reveal ancient and modern introductions of a large mammal: the complex case of red deer (Cervus elaphus) in Ireland by Ruth F. Carden and others>[Quaternary Science Reviews 42, pp. 74–84; Neolithic record, ancient DNA and recent introductions]{ref} Modern distribution alone cannot distinguish an ancient native population, an early <species translocation>, and a recent estate introduction. Each may require a different conservation interpretation even if all animals bear the same species name.

<fallow deer> provide a clearer example of deliberate <species translocation>. Integrated archaeological and biomolecular research traces Roman movements and later medieval redistribution, including the British history of introduction and reintroduction. <The 10,000-year biocultural history of fallow deer and its implications for conservation policy by Karis H. Baker and others>[British Roman introductions and medieval translocations; combined biomolecular and archaeological evidence]{ref} Managed <rabbits> and later introductions of <sika deer>, <muntjac>, <grey squirrels> and <American mink> further altered assemblages. Some escaped from breeding or management; others were deliberately released. Historical records establish routes and timing that cannot be deduced from climatic suitability alone. Modern invasions are thus a continuation of human-mediated assembly, with potentially stronger effects because transport crosses barriers that once limited colonization.

The replacement of <red squirrels> by <grey squirrels> illustrates multiple interacting mechanisms. <interspecific competition> for resources can matter, but experimental <infection> demonstrated severe <infectious disease> in <red squirrels> with little detectable effect on <grey squirrels>, supporting a role for <squirrelpox> in some declines. <Parapoxvirus causes a deleterious disease in red squirrels associated with UK population declines by Daniel M. Tompkins and others>[Proceedings of the Royal Society B 269, pp. 529–533; contrasting experimental susceptibility of red and grey squirrels]{ref} That experiment establishes differential susceptibility; it does not show that every field decline is caused by the virus. A field test must establish <infection> exposure as well as squirrel abundance and account for habitat and competitor density. Likewise, recovery of <pine martens> in Ireland has been associated with declines of <grey squirrels> and recovery opportunities for <red squirrels>; the evidence is informative but a spatial association alone is not a controlled demonstration that <predation> explains every local change. <Population crash in an invasive species following the recovery of a native predator: the case of the American grey squirrel and the European pine marten in Ireland by Emma Sheehy and others>[Biodiversity and Conservation 23, pp. 753–774; distributional changes associated with pine marten recovery]{ref}

Recent protection, woodland recovery and reintroductions also allow people to reverse some earlier losses. Such recoveries do not recreate a unique pristine baseline: a “native” reference chosen at the beginning of the <Holocene>, before farming, or before modern introductions gives different assemblages. The same applies to interpreting recovering coastal populations; marine and terrestrial records need their own detection and exploitation histories. Reliable attribution combines directly dated remains, habitat evidence, historical documentation and demographic or experimental tests, with explicit allowance for preservation and survey effort.

**Climate and access strongly shaped early <Holocene> assembly; later changes increasingly reflect farming, persecution and transport, with modern <infectious disease> and conservation adding further layers.** Human influence is therefore pervasive, but neither every absence nor every change in abundance can be assigned to humanity without establishing when and where the change occurred.
