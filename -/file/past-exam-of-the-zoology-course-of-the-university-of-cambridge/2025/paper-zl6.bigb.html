**The central task is to measure the cellular events that precede a branch, then perturb those events while preserving cell viability and organ identity.** Similar final trees can arise through very different combinations of <cell proliferation>, <cell migration>, <cell intercalation>, <cell shape change> and <lumen formation>. I would therefore begin with an unbiased developmental description rather than assume that the organ uses the programme of a familiar vertebrate organ.

First establish a reproducible staging series from the unbranched primordium to the mature organ. Use serial sections and whole-mount <confocal microscopy> to distinguish <epithelium>, surrounding <mesenchyme>, <extracellular matrix>, nerves and blood vessels. Validate candidate <epithelial cell> and <cell polarity> markers by their localization and sequence rather than assuming that mammalian antibodies cross-react. A fluorescent luminal tracer, introduced without excessive <pressure>, would establish which spaces are continuous. Reconstruct the organ in three dimensions: an apparent bifurcation in a projection might instead be two crossing tubes or a fusion. Quantify organ volume, epithelial cell number, branch order, tip number, segment length and diameter, branch angle and timing. Distinguish lateral budding, tip bifurcation and branch fusion. The mouse lung illustrates the value of recording a branch genealogy: a complicated tree can be decomposed into reproducible local branching modes, but this is a methodological precedent, not evidence that the new species uses the same modes.<The branching programme of mouse lung development by Ross J. Metzger and others>[Developmental reconstruction and identification of local branching modes]{ref}

Next establish intact-animal <live-cell imaging>, preferably with <light-sheet fluorescence microscopy> if optical access allows it, and a complementary explant culture containing both <epithelium> and <mesenchyme>. Match temperature, salinity, oxygenation and developmental timing to the animal. Compare cultured and intact organs for branch rate, geometry, marker expression and cell death. Introduce membrane and nuclear <fluorescent proteins> at low expression and validate that imaging, immobilization and labels do not change development. Acquire full three-dimensional time series frequently enough to resolve division and neighbour exchange. Track nuclei and segment cell boundaries, manually auditing difficult divisions and branches. Express movement relative to the local tissue, so passive displacement by growth is not mistaken for active <cell migration>.

Measure separately the sources of tissue expansion. Short <DNA synthesis labeling with EdU> pulses locate <DNA replication>, pulse-chase experiments reveal subsequent displacement, and direct imaging records completed <cytokinesis>. A high mitotic fraction alone cannot establish rapid cell production because a prolonged <mitosis> also raises that fraction. Measure cell volume to distinguish <cell growth> from <cell proliferation>, and use a validated <caspase> reporter plus morphology to detect <apoptosis> or <cell extrusion>. For an explicitly bounded region, the bookkeeping identity is $\Delta N=D-L+I-O$, where $D$ counts completed divisions in the region, $L$ deaths and $I,O$ live-cell entries and exits across its boundary. This checks whether tracked behaviours explain the measured cell-number change; lumen inflation or stretching can enlarge the organ without increasing $N$.

Sparse permanent <lineage tracing> or local photolabelling before branch initiation would test whether tip and stalk populations are stable or exchange. Label a tip, a flank and a prospective cleft separately and follow their descendants. Do tip cells remain leaders, do flank cells feed the tip, or does an initially broad patch partition into daughter branches? Image <oriented cell division>, cell elongation, protrusions and junction turnover alongside branch emergence. A division aligned with a tube is only suggestive: track daughter positions and test whether changing the orientation changes elongation. Quantify neighbour exchanges to identify <cell intercalation>. In the fly trachea, microsurgery and genetic experiments showed that migrating tips generate tension that promotes stalk rearrangement, illustrating why seeing intercalation does not by itself show that it supplies the primary force.<Tip-cell migration controls stalk-cell intercalation during Drosophila tracheal tube elongation by Emmanuel Caussinus and others>[Genetic and microsurgical tests of tip-generated tension]{ref}

Then make acute, staged causal tests. Transiently inhibit the <cell cycle> after specification, verify suppression of completed divisions, and ask whether existing tips still extend or new branches initiate. Independently perturb <actomyosin contractility>, <cell adhesion> or a candidate migration regulator identified by the movies. Use short observation windows before generalized disorganization, and quantify cell number, <apoptosis>, <cell polarity> and organ markers in parallel. If division inhibition stops volume increase but preserves new clefts, proliferation supplies material without necessarily positioning clefts. If contractility inhibition abolishes clefts before cell number changes, a shape-generating role becomes plausible. Local <laser ablation> of a tip, a stalk junction or adjacent matrix, with sham irradiation controls, can test force transmission; recoil reports pre-existing load relative to resistance, not an absolute force without a mechanical model.

Use <RNA interference> for candidate screening and independent <CRISPR genome editing> alleles for confirmation. Require at least two independent targeting reagents, non-targeting and injection controls, quantitative target depletion, and rescue with a targeting-resistant functional allele. Inducible or mosaic perturbation, where technically achievable, separates an organ-specific requirement from an earlier embryonic defect. Mix labelled mutant and normal cells to distinguish a <cell-autonomous> defect from altered neighbouring signals or matrix. Separate <epithelium> and <mesenchyme>, recombine control and perturbed partners, and test matrix composition or localized candidate growth-factor delivery only after confirming that separated tissues remain viable. Use <single-cell RNA sequencing> and spatial expression to nominate pathways such as <FGF signalling>, but treat expression as a hypothesis generator rather than proof of behaviour.

Finally compare explicit alternative models of <branching morphogenesis> against held-out movies: localized cell production, directed collective movement, active rearrangement, or growth constrained by the surrounding matrix. Each should predict both branch geometry and the observed cell trajectories, and its response to a spatially restricted perturbation. Randomize treatment, blind segmentation and use animals from independent clutches as biological replicates; many cells within one animal do not replace independent animals. The strongest outcome is a causal chain from molecular perturbation to altered cell behaviour to altered branch geometry, supported by rescue and by measurements showing that general developmental delay cannot explain it.

**Intestinal development constructs a patterned renewing organ; intestinal homeostasis continually replaces its cells while preserving that organization.** Both depend on reciprocal signalling between <endoderm>-derived <epithelium> and <mesenchyme>, but they solve different problems. Development expands and regionalizes the tissue, establishes architecture and produces its first mature <cell types>. Adult <tissue homeostasis> balances cell production, <cell differentiation>, movement and loss within an already established architecture. The mammalian small intestine provides the main comparison; its villi and crypts should not be indiscriminately attributed to the colon or to all vertebrates.

Early <intestinal development> begins with a broadly proliferative epithelial tube whose regional identity is established in its embryonic context. Factors including <CDX2> help impose intestinal identity, while local signals distinguish the future small intestine and colon. Growth is coordinated with the formation of <smooth muscle>, connective tissue, the <enteric nervous system> and vasculature. The emergence of <intestinal villi> transforms a relatively simple luminal surface into an absorptive one. In mouse, epithelial <Hedgehog signalling> promotes underlying mesenchymal clusters, and <BMP signalling> helps space those clusters. In explants, increased BMP activity suppresses clusters whereas BMP inhibition produces merged clusters and abnormal villus patterns. This supports a role in pattern generation, not simply in the terminal differentiation of enterocytes.<Villification in the mouse: Bmp signals control intestinal villus patterning by Katherine D. Walton and others>[Explant BMP perturbations and the comparison with chick villification]{ref}

The mechanics and signalling must be distinguished across species. In chick, sequential <smooth muscle> differentiation constrains growth, generating ridges, zigzags and villi through <buckling>. In mouse, patterned mesenchymal clusters arise before equivalent surface deformation, so the chick sequence is not a general account of mammalian villus initiation. The common principle is reciprocal communication between layers; the ordering of mechanical and chemical patterning differs. In development, therefore, an abnormal villus pattern could arise from defective mesenchymal organization, epithelial growth or mechanical coupling, even if the final epithelial <cell differentiation> markers are present.

As villi emerge, proliferative cells become concentrated in intervillus regions, and <intestinal crypts> develop chiefly around the perinatal and postnatal transition in mouse. The fetal proliferative <epithelium> is consequently not simply a miniature adult crypt. <Paneth cells> and other components of the mature niche also appear on their own developmental schedules. Demonstrating an adult stem-cell marker in an embryonic cell would not alone establish that the embryonic lineage hierarchy or niche requirements are identical to the adult ones.

In adult small-intestinal <tissue homeostasis>, cycling <Lgr5> positive <intestinal stem cells> at crypt bases generate renewed <stem cells> and <transit-amplifying cells>. Descendants differentiate into absorptive <enterocytes> and secretory populations including <goblet cells>, <enteroendocrine cells>, <tuft cells> and <Paneth cells>. Most differentiated descendants move toward the villus tip and are shed, whereas <Paneth cells> occupy the crypt base. <lineage tracing> from the <Lgr5> locus generated persistent contributions to all epithelial lineages, providing functional evidence for <self-renewal> and <multipotency> rather than relying on marker expression alone.<Identification of stem cells in small intestine and colon by marker gene Lgr5 by Nick Barker and others>[Inducible lineage tracing and persistence of labelled epithelial lineages]{ref}

The adult niche spatially restricts developmental pathways. High local <Wnt signalling> and <R-spondin> support crypt stemness and growth, while <BMP signalling> restrains crypt-like programmes outside their appropriate domain. <Notch signalling> favours absorptive over secretory lineage allocation partly by opposing <ATOH1>-dependent secretory specification. Thus a single pathway can control both population maintenance and cell composition: stimulating proliferation is not sufficient if the resulting <cell differentiation> balance is wrong. Stromal signals are essential in vivo, and <Paneth cells> provide additional local support in small intestine; the colon maintains <stem cells> without a small-intestinal Paneth-cell arrangement. Niche dependence cannot therefore be equated with dependence on one invariant supporting <cell type>.

The adult organ is dynamically stable rather than cellularly static. A stem-cell clone can expand while neighbouring stem-cell clones disappear through <neutral drift>, with the total compartment maintained.<Intestinal crypt homeostasis results from neutral competition between symmetrically dividing Lgr5 stem cells by Hugo J. Snippert and others>[Multicolour clonal tracing and neutral-drift analysis]{ref} At the organ level, sustained excess production generates expansion or neoplasia; excess loss causes barrier failure. Development deliberately changes total cell number, surface area and proportions over time, whereas adult maintenance constrains their long-term averages. Nevertheless, adult <tissue homeostasis> remains responsive to feeding, microbes, immune signals and injury, and is not a completely fixed endpoint.

Experimental models expose both continuity and differences. A single sorted <Lgr5> positive cell can produce a self-renewing <intestinal organoid> containing organized differentiated progeny in a suitable matrix and supplied growth factors. This establishes epithelial self-organization under defined conditions, not niche independence in the absence of extracellular signals: the medium replaces important niche functions.<Single Lgr5 stem cells build crypt-villus structures in vitro without a mesenchymal niche by Toshiro Sato and others>[Single-cell cultures, lineage organization and defined culture requirements]{ref} Embryonic explants retain the <mesenchyme> needed to study villus patterning, while adult <intestinal organoids> are especially useful for stem-cell maintenance and lineage allocation. Neither alone reproduces an intact organ's circulation, innervation, immune environment and longitudinal regionalization.

Finally, injury reveals an important bridge between development and maintenance. <cell fate plasticity> can restore missing <stem cells>, so the normal adult lineage flow is not an irreversible restriction of all descendants. Committed <progenitor cells> can recover stem-cell function under particular injury conditions, but this does not establish that all mature villus cells routinely do so. A decisive comparison uses stage-specific <lineage tracing>, matched marker analysis and timed pathway perturbation, measuring architecture and functional output as well as <cell proliferation>. Development and adult renewal reuse molecular machinery while differing in tissue geometry, developmental competence and the direction of net population change.

**Epithelial <cell fate plasticity> is a context-dependent change in developmental potential or differentiated identity.** It includes <dedifferentiation> toward a <progenitor cell> state, <transdifferentiation> between differentiated identities, and reversible intermediate states. It is distinct from selecting a pre-existing rare <stem cell>, and from ordinary progression down a lineage. <epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity> is one important form, but changing epithelial subtype without losing epithelial organization is also <cell fate plasticity>.

The strongest physiological rationale is that a tissue needs both stable specialization and reserve routes for replacement. During ordinary adult <tissue homeostasis>, a stem-cell hierarchy can efficiently supply predictable cell types. After injury removes a component of that hierarchy, local signals and newly available <stem-cell niche> space may permit a committed descendant to reacquire <self-renewal>. In mouse intestine, <Alpi> marked enterocyte-lineage <progenitor cells> normally generate short-lived absorptive descendants. After targeted ablation of <Lgr5> positive <intestinal stem cells>, labelled <progenitor cells> can generate long-lived crypt-villus lineages with renewed stem-cell output. Comparing the same labelled population before and after ablation distinguishes injury-dependent <cell fate plasticity> from its normal fate. The experiment concerns <progenitor cells> in the enterocyte lineage; it should not be generalized to every terminally mature villus enterocyte.<Replacement of Lost Lgr5-Positive Stem Cells through Plasticity of Their Enterocyte-Lineage Daughters by Paul W. Tetteh and others>[Alpi-CreER fate mapping with targeted Lgr5-cell ablation]{ref}

A complementary model is the mouse airway. Genetic depletion of basal <stem cells>, combined with prior <lineage tracing> of luminal secretory cells, revealed secretory-cell descendants that acquired basal-cell properties and sustained subsequent epithelial production. Experiments with isolated cells and cocultures further showed that contact with basal cells inhibits this conversion. <cell fate plasticity> is therefore regulated by the tissue environment, not simply an intrinsic capacity unmasked whenever a marker changes. Its interpretation requires controls for residual basal cells and for whether the secretory-lineage driver labelled basal cells before injury.<Dedifferentiation of committed epithelial cells into stem cells in vivo by Purushothama Rao Tata and others>[Basal-cell ablation, secretory-cell lineage tracing and contact-dependent suppression]{ref}

Development provides additional contexts. Embryonic epithelia can undergo <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> during processes such as <gastrulation>, and <mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition> can subsequently assemble epithelial structures. These are regulated changes in adhesion, <cell polarity>, motility and <gene expression>, rather than a simple binary switch read from <E-cadherin> alone. In wound repair, transient relaxation of epithelial organization can facilitate migration while retaining intercellular contacts. A partially migratory repair state need not entail a complete mesenchymal conversion or a permanent change of lineage. Following wound closure, restoration of barrier function and polarized architecture is as important as the initial ability to move.

The most convincing experimental design labels a defined starting population irreversibly before the inducing event, allows the inducer to clear, then follows labelled descendants through the transition. Check immediate labelling specificity, include no-inducer and uninjured controls, quantify labelling efficiency and rule out ectopic driver activation after injury. Demonstrate the new state with multiple markers, morphology and function, and persistence after the inducing signal is removed. Serial epithelial production or a second injury challenge tests stable <self-renewal>; a transient appearance of a basal marker does not. Sparse labelling and continuous <live-cell imaging> help distinguish conversion from contamination, <cell fusion> or selective expansion of a rare initially unrecognized <progenitor cell>.

<single-cell RNA sequencing> can identify intermediate <cell states> and candidate regulators, but inferred trajectories are not lineage histories. Combine it with genetic barcodes, spatial localization and timed perturbation of candidate <transcription factors> or niche signals. <Organoids> offer defined media, prospective cell isolation and manipulation of matrix stiffness. A sorted population's ability to make a structure in culture establishes potential under those conditions; dissociation and a permissive medium may induce a capacity rarely used in vivo. Compare culture results with intact-tissue fate mapping before claiming a normal physiological contribution.

The evolutionary argument is a trade-off, rather than a claim that every plastic response is an adaptation. Conditional access to alternative <cell fates> could buffer environmental injury and permit repair without maintaining a separate reserve stem-cell pool for every possible lesion. Conserved signalling modules can also be redeployed in new developmental contexts. These advantages must be weighed against loss of specialized function, energetic costs and the danger of inappropriate persistent growth. Comparative experiments should ask whether homologous lineages undergo similar transitions under comparable injuries; shared expression of a few regulators is insufficient to prove that an ancestral <cell fate plasticity> programme has been conserved. At the tissue level, <cell fate plasticity> supplies alternative states on which selection can act, but it does not itself establish heritable evolutionary change.

Clinically, controlled <cell fate plasticity> may improve epithelial repair, provide transplantable cells and make patient-derived disease models. The same capacity can undermine treatment: a tumour may switch to a less drug-sensitive lineage or regenerate a stem-like population after therapy. In <carcinoma>, <epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity> can contribute to invasion and treatment resistance, but neither a single EMT marker nor its absence establishes the mechanism of metastasis. In a lineage-traced mouse lung-cancer model, the tested EMT programme was dispensable for lung metastasis while contributing to chemoresistance; the conclusion is specific to that model and reporter coverage.<Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition is not required for lung metastasis but contributes to chemoresistance by Kari R. Fischer and others>[Lineage-tracing and chemotherapy experiments in mouse tumours]{ref} The useful clinical aim is therefore a defined, reversible transition that restores function, with evidence that it does not leave a persistently proliferative or malignant population.

**<reactive oxygen species> can alter competitive fitness, but neither high ROS nor an antioxidant response universally identifies a loser.** <cell competition> is relative: cells that survive in one neighbourhood are preferentially lost in another, with fitter neighbours occupying their place. An experiment showing oxidant-induced death in a homogeneous tissue establishes toxicity, not necessarily <cell competition>. In epithelia, loss can occur through <apoptosis>, extrusion or differentiation followed by departure from a proliferative layer.

<reactive oxygen species> include <superoxide> and <hydrogen peroxide>, which differ in reactivity, localization and transport. They can arise from mitochondrial metabolism or regulated oxidase activity. At controlled levels they participate in <redox signalling>; excess or poorly buffered production damages <DNA>, proteins and membranes. Competitive fitness can therefore change through altered damage, stress responses, growth or differentiation. <NRF2> and its fly counterpart <CncC> regulate antioxidant and detoxification programmes. Their activation is an integrated response to cellular conditions, not a direct quantitative measurement of one ROS species.

An instructive fly study compared prospective losers carrying a ribosomal-protein defect or loss of <Mahjong protein>. Both activated antioxidant-response genes before elimination. In ribosomal-protein-deficient cells, two ROS dyes did not reveal a uniform increase, whereas reduced-thiol staining was lower. Mild <CncC> activation was tolerated across an entire disc but caused boundary-enriched death in clones surrounded by normal cells; those clones survived better among less-fit neighbours. Conversely, reducing <CncC> impaired stressed-cell survival. Thus a protective response can also confer relative loser status. The study separated this module from <JNK signalling> effects on growth and from <JAK-STAT signalling> effects on tissue expansion; it did not establish a compulsory linear chain from ROS through JNK to elimination.<chronic-activation-of-jnk-jak-stat-and-oxidative-stress-signalling-causes-the-loser-cell-status-by-iwo-kucinski-and-others>[Figures 5–7, redox measurements and neighbourhood-dependent CncC experiments]{ref}

This distinction prevents two errors. First, a high antioxidant-response reporter can coexist with successful buffering and relatively low measured oxidant levels. Second, knocking down a protective factor may kill cells autonomously, making their apparent competitive loss worse even if that factor also participates in neighbour-dependent recognition. To identify a competitive mechanism, compare the same genotype in a homogeneous tissue, in a mosaic with normal neighbours and in a mosaic with an alternative neighbour genotype. Quantify clone growth, death at boundaries and in centres, and replacement by neighbours, normalized to the corresponding cell numbers or areas. A clone that is small because its cells divide slowly has not necessarily been actively eliminated.

The adult mammalian oesophagus supplies a striking environmental example. In transgenic mouse <epithelium>, low-dose <ionizing radiation> induced an oxidative response that shifted normal basal cells toward differentiation. Cells carrying mutant <p53> were relatively insensitive and expanded at their neighbours' expense. Combining antioxidant treatment with irradiation reversed the population outcome, promoting normal-cell proliferation and mutant-cell differentiation. The selective advantage was consequently conditional on the redox environment, not a fixed property of the mutation. This also shows that competitive replacement need not be executed primarily by <apoptosis>.<Outcompeting p53-Mutant Cells in the Normal Esophagus by Redox Manipulation by David Fernandez-Antoran and others>[Mouse lineage tracing, irradiation and antioxidant-intervention experiments]{ref}

A separate mouse experiment generated a mosaic of <NRF2>-deficient and NRF2-intact oesophageal cells. Deficient cells persisted under unstressed conditions but accumulated damage and were preferentially lost during exposure to the carcinogen <4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide>. Tumours predominantly arose from the surviving NRF2-intact population. This supports a context-dependent role for stress protection in population selection, while not proving that every disappearing cell was directly recognized and killed by a neighbour. Carcinogen detoxification and <DNA damage> are additional variables, so the experiment is not a chemically pure ROS titration.<Cellular Nrf2 Levels Determine Cell Fate during Chemical Carcinogenesis in Esophageal Epithelium by Makoto Horiuchi and others>[Mosaic deletion, unstressed controls and 4NQO exposure experiments]{ref}

To test a proposed ROS mechanism in an epithelial mosaic, image a calibrated, compartment-targeted <redox biosensor> before clone loss, along with cell boundaries, a proliferation readout and a death or differentiation reporter. Include sensor controls for <pH>, expression level and dynamic range; general oxidant dyes have loading, localization and chemical-specificity limitations. Perturb the source and scavenging machinery independently and restrict the perturbation to prospective losers or winners. For example, a validated <catalase> manipulation tests peroxide buffering, while a source-specific perturbation tests whether that peroxide is required. A matched rescue should restore both the measured redox change and the competitive outcome. Monitor cellular energy and survival in homogeneous controls because a mitochondrial perturbation can affect fitness independently of ROS. Perturb downstream stress signalling separately to distinguish oxidant production, stress detection and execution of cell loss.

The tissue consequences can be beneficial or harmful. Elimination of damaged cells can preserve a barrier, but a stress-resistant mutant can also exploit environmental oxidants to displace normal cells. Persistent stress signals from damaged or dying cells may alter neighbour proliferation and inflammation, producing further changes in fitness. The experimentally supported conclusion is that redox state reshapes the conditions under which cells persist, differentiate or are eliminated. A universal prescription to raise ROS or administer antioxidants does not follow: the relevant species, tissue compartment, neighbour genotype and exposure history determine the direction of selection.

**<positional information> allows cells to choose an appropriate developmental response for their location; <positional memory> allows a location-dependent identity to persist after the original signal has changed.** Neither is identical to <cell differentiation>. Two connective-tissue cells can have similar current functions but different information about where they belong in a limb. Development must establish these relationships, while regeneration must reconstruct missing relationships from an incomplete stump.

In the classical <positional information> framework, a tissue first establishes coordinates and cells interpret them according to their competence and developmental history. A <morphogen gradient> is one possible coordinate system. Different thresholds can elicit different <gene expression> states, but concentration alone is not the entire signal: exposure duration, feedback, changing tissue geometry and the response network also matter. Thus the same positional signal can produce different differentiated structures in different competent tissues.<Positional information and the spatial pattern of cellular differentiation by Lewis Wolpert>[The separation of positional specification from cellular interpretation]{ref}

The vertebrate <limb bud> is patterned along three interacting axes: proximal-distal, anterior-posterior and dorsal-ventral. The <apical ectodermal ridge> supplies <FGF signalling> that supports distal outgrowth and survival. Its early removal causes distal truncation, but this does not uniquely demonstrate that cells measure time in a <progress zone>: cell loss, altered growth and loss of signals can also change the structures that remain.<Positional Information in Chick Limb Morphogenesis by Dennis Summerbell and others>[Classical progress-zone formulation and limb-bud experiments]{ref} Distal FGF signals and proximal influences interact with <Hox genes> and other regulators to organize <stylopod>, <zeugopod> and <autopod> territories. One must distinguish a positional identity defect from a failure to supply enough cells to execute that identity.

Anterior-posterior patterning provides a more direct signal-relocation test. Grafting posterior <zone of polarizing activity> tissue to the anterior of a chick <limb bud> produces duplicated digit patterns. <Sonic hedgehog> is expressed in the posterior region, and anterior implantation of cells expressing it reproduces polarizing activity. This connects a localized molecular signal to a change in spatial identity, rather than merely correlating expression with posterior development. Digit pattern still depends on signal duration, growth and downstream regulation; a static concentration-to-digit lookup table is inadequate.<Sonic hedgehog mediates the polarizing activity of the ZPA by Robert D. Riddle and others>[Expression analysis and anterior implantation of SHH-expressing cells]{ref} Along the dorsal-ventral axis, dorsal <Wnt7a> promotes <Lmx1b> in <mesenchyme>, while ventral <Engrailed-1> helps restrict dorsal signalling. Interactions among these systems allow outgrowth and coordinate specification to remain aligned.

Regeneration adds the problem of interpreting an amputation plane. An <axolotl> limb usually replaces structures distal to the cut: a wrist-level stump does not routinely add a new upper arm before rebuilding the hand. This implies that regenerative outgrowth retains or reconstructs level-specific information. A <blastema> contains proliferative <progenitor cells> beneath a specialized wound <epithelium>, but it is not a uniformly pluripotent mass. Transgenic graft and lineage experiments showed substantial memory of tissue origin and differences among <progenitor cell> populations. The distinction between tissue identity and spatial identity is essential: producing cartilage does not alone show that its proximal-distal address is correct.<Cells keep a memory of their tissue origin during axolotl limb regeneration by Martin Kragl and others>[Labelled tissue grafts and lineage-restricted contributions to the blastema]{ref}

Retinoid experiments show that positional identity can be reprogrammed. Treatment of distal <axolotl> amputations with <vitamin A> or related <retinoids> can generate proximal structures before distal ones, sometimes producing a much more complete limb from a distal stump. This is difficult to explain as simple acceleration of growth, because the identities and sequence of regenerated structures change. It supports proximalization of the responding tissue. Nevertheless, pharmacological reprogramming does not by itself prove that an endogenous retinoid gradient is the sole normal coordinate system; dose-dependent toxicity and effects on growth must be assessed separately.<Vitamin A and pattern formation in the regenerating limb by Malcolm Maden>[Proximal-distal duplications after retinoid treatment of axolotl amputations]{ref}

Spatial confrontation supplies another test. In an accessory-limb assay, a wound with appropriate nerve support can be combined with tissue of a different circumferential identity. Productive anterior-posterior interactions can support a patterned extra outgrowth, whereas wound healing and proliferation alone need not generate a complete limb. In <axolotl> experiments, anterior <FGF8> and posterior <Sonic hedgehog> activities were identified as interacting signals, and manipulating them could replace aspects of the normally required tissue confrontation. This connects positional disparity to a signalling circuit that promotes regeneration, without implying that <FGF8> and SHH encode every spatial identity by themselves.<FGF8 and SHH substitute for anterior–posterior tissue interactions to induce limb regeneration by Eugen Nacu and others>[Signal localization and substitution in regenerative assays]{ref}

A rigorous molecular test would transplant permanently labelled connective-tissue cells between defined limb positions, score their contribution and spatial identity, and perturb a candidate positional regulator specifically in those cells. Compare position-matched grafts, rotated or mismatched grafts, sham operations and equal nerve support; measure survival and proliferation so that graft failure is not mistaken for lost positional identity. Assess skeletal pattern and lineage output as well as positional markers, with rescue of the candidate perturbation. Such experiments separate a stable address, responsiveness to local signals and a generic requirement for outgrowth. Development and regeneration reuse signalling pathways, but regeneration additionally depends on retained information, wound-specific competence and communication between cells carrying different positional histories.

**The developing mammalian heart grows by a changing combination of <progenitor cell> recruitment and division of differentiated <cardiomyocytes>.** Differentiation is therefore not synonymous with permanent cell-cycle exit. Premature differentiation can exhaust a <progenitor cell> pool, while delayed differentiation can leave many cells unable to build a functional heart. The correct balance is spatial and temporal: different cardiac populations must be supplied, expand and mature in the right order.

Early cardiac <mesoderm> is specified through combinations of extracellular signals and <transcription factors> including <GATA4>, <NKX2-5> and <TBX5>. The first heart field differentiates relatively early and contributes prominently to the initial heart tube and future left ventricle. The <second heart field> retains proliferative <progenitor cells> outside the tube for longer and adds cells at its poles, with major contributions to the right ventricle and outflow tract as well as other regions. These are useful developmental populations rather than perfectly isolated compartments with one exclusive marker. The key growth mechanism is delayed recruitment: maintaining a <progenitor cell> reservoir allows the heart tube to lengthen after its first contractile cells have differentiated.<Isl1 identifies a cardiac progenitor population that proliferates prior to differentiation and contributes a majority of cells to the heart by Chen-Leng Cai and others>[Isl1 expression, lineage tracing and loss-of-function analysis]{ref}

<FGF signalling> helps support <progenitor cell> expansion, while <BMP signalling> contributes to specification and the transition toward differentiation. Their effects depend on developmental stage and location, so a global instruction to activate either pathway is inadequate. In mouse, an <NKX2-5>-dependent negative-feedback circuit limits Bmp2/Smad1 activity in heart-field <progenitor cells>. Nkx2-5 mutants initially overexpressed specification and <progenitor cell> programmes but subsequently failed to sustain normal second-heart-field proliferation and outflow-tract development. Reducing Smad1 activity rescued important aspects of these defects. The genetic rescue is stronger mechanistic evidence than an expression correlation: excessive specification-associated signalling can compromise the growth needed to construct the outflow tract.<an-nkx2-5-bmp2-smad1-negative-feedback-loop-controls-heart-progenitor-specification-and-proliferation-by-owen-w-j-prall-and-others>[Nkx2-5 and Smad1 mutant analysis and genetic rescue]{ref}

Once incorporated into the heart, embryonic <cardiomyocytes> assemble <sarcomeres> and contribute to contraction while retaining substantial proliferative capacity. <cell proliferation> in this differentiated population thickens chamber walls and helps produce the tissue needed for septation. Chamber myocardium and non-chamber regions follow different regulatory programmes, allowing rapid chamber expansion alongside specialized conduction and junctional territories. The goal is not simply maximum cardiomyocyte number: the arrangement, coupling and regional identity of those cells are necessary for a coordinated pump.

Signals between cardiac layers couple growth to architecture. <endocardium>-derived <neuregulin 1> acts through myocardial <ErbB receptors>, including ErbB2 and ErbB4, in <ventricular trabeculation>. Mouse loss-of-function experiments disrupting this ligand-receptor system produce severe defects in trabecular development. The phenotype demonstrates an essential tissue interaction, but absent trabeculae must not automatically be described as a pure proliferation defect: differentiation, adhesion, cell rearrangement and survival can also contribute. Separate measurements of these processes are needed.<Multiple essential functions of neuregulin in development by Dirk Meyer and Carmen Birchmeier>[Targeted neuregulin mutation and embryonic cardiac phenotype]{ref} The <epicardium> and developing coronary system add further supporting signals and structural requirements as the ventricular wall grows. Proliferation must remain matched to oxygen delivery and tissue organization.

<Hippo signalling> provides a direct brake on myocardial expansion. When active, the pathway restricts nuclear growth-promoting activity of <YAP> and its cooperation with <TEAD> factors. Conditional disruption of Hippo components in developing mouse hearts increased cardiomyocyte proliferation and enlarged the heart. Reducing <beta-catenin> dosage suppressed overgrowth in the tested mutants, supporting functional interaction with <Wnt signalling>. This demonstrates that differentiated myocardium contains an actively regulated growth programme; its number of divisions is not merely the residue of an earlier <progenitor cell> state.<Hippo pathway inhibits Wnt signaling to restrain cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart size by Todd Heallen and others>[Cardiac conditional mutants, proliferation measurements and beta-catenin genetic interaction]{ref} Conversely, unrestricted proliferation is not necessarily desirable, because chamber geometry, septation and function must mature together.

Around birth, <cardiomyocytes> undergo substantial changes in metabolism, sarcomere organization, electrical coupling and loading. Many mouse <cardiomyocytes> become binucleate or acquire <polyploidy>, while growth shifts increasingly toward <cardiomyocyte hypertrophy>. This transition is gradual and species-dependent, not a universal instantaneous switch at birth. A nucleus incorporating a nucleotide analogue may have undergone <DNA replication> without completing a new cell: <endoreduplication> and failed <cytokinesis> can mimic proliferation in a simple staining assay. Counts of total <cardiomyocytes>, nuclear number, DNA content and directly observed division are therefore needed alongside EdU and mitotic markers.

The early postnatal regeneration window illustrates the importance of developmental timing. Partial apex resection in one-day-old mice was followed by substantial myocardial restoration, and fate mapping implicated pre-existing <cardiomyocytes>; the reported capacity was greatly reduced by seven days of age. This is evidence that early differentiated <cardiomyocytes> retain regenerative potential under the tested injury conditions, rather than evidence for an indefinitely active adult cardiac stem-cell reservoir.<Transient regenerative potential of the neonatal mouse heart by Enzo R. Porrello and others>[Age comparison, injury experiments and cardiomyocyte fate mapping]{ref} Injury severity and the method of measuring tissue restoration matter when comparing regenerative outcomes.

To distinguish mechanisms experimentally, combine inducible <lineage tracing> with stage- and lineage-specific perturbation. Deleting a regulator before heart-field specification tests a different question from deleting it after cardiomyocyte differentiation. Follow <progenitor cell> number, recruitment, differentiated-cell number, completed division, <apoptosis> and chamber structure, then test pump function. Use littermate controls, verify recombination timing and specificity, and seek pathway-specific genetic rescue. A small heart with few marker-positive cells could reflect failed specification, reduced proliferation, delayed deployment or excess death. The developmental solution is an ordered handover: expand competent <progenitor cells>, recruit them into differentiated tissue, permit controlled division of working myocardium, and progressively constrain division as mature structure and function are established.

**Polarity proteins generate tissue form by controlling where cells build surfaces, place junctions and divide.** Their morphogenetic roles can be separated experimentally from a wholesale loss of epithelial identity. Three examples illustrate different outputs: positioning a single lumen, orienting neighbour exchange, and limiting tube dimensions.

The first example is <Cdc42>-dependent <lumen formation> in three-dimensional <Caco-2> epithelial cysts. Cells in a normal cyst coordinate their <apical-basal polarity> so that apical surfaces face one central lumen. During growth, a correctly oriented <spindle apparatus> places daughter cells into the epithelial wall rather than establishing inappropriate internal cell arrangements. <Cdc42> depletion disrupts spindle orientation and produces abnormal, often multiple, luminal spaces. Importantly, the cells can still establish apical membrane identity: in this model the crucial defect is the coordination and positioning of apical surfaces during division, rather than an absolute inability to make any apical surface. This distinguishes tissue-level organization from polarity within an isolated cell.<Cdc42 controls spindle orientation to position the apical surface during epithelial morphogenesis by Aron B. Jaffe and others>[Caco-2 three-dimensional cysts, Cdc42 depletion and spindle-orientation analysis]{ref}

Mechanistically, <Cdc42> connects cortical organization to the <PAR polarity complex>, polarized trafficking and spindle-positioning machinery. However, the relative contributions depend on the model: a <Cdc42> trafficking phenotype in another epithelial line cannot simply be substituted for the <Caco-2> spindle result. A convincing test combines <live-cell imaging> of division and the first ectopic lumen with apical and basal markers, verifies target depletion and rescues with functional <Cdc42>. Compare the total number and viability of cells, because a dying cell population can also produce abnormal cavities. A perturbation that restores spindle positioning while leaving other outputs of <Cdc42> impaired would help isolate the causal route. These controls make the single-lumen phenotype an interpretable morphogenetic output.

The second example is <Bazooka>, the fly <PAR-3> protein, during <Drosophila germband extension>. This uses <planar cell polarity> within the epithelial sheet as well as apical-basal organization. <Bazooka> and junctional components become enriched at a complementary set of cell interfaces to <myosin II>. Spatially patterned gene activity organizes this planar asymmetry, and loss of <Bazooka> disrupts normal polarized cell behaviour and extension. Thus a protein associated with epithelial junction assembly is redeployed to make different junctions behave differently in the same sheet.<Patterned gene expression directs bipolar planar polarity in Drosophila by Jennifer A. Zallen and Eric Wieschaus>[Planar distributions of Bazooka and myosin and dependence on patterned gene expression]{ref}

Oriented junction shrinkage and new-junction formation permit <cell intercalation> while maintaining epithelial continuity. The important geometry is that junctions separating anterior and posterior neighbours shrink and resolve into interfaces with the complementary orientation, producing extension along the body axis. Junction names can be confusing because they may describe either the neighbours or the line itself; tracking vertices and neighbour exchange avoids this ambiguity. <Bazooka> is a spatial organizer, not itself the force-generating motor. To test its contribution, quantify its localization, myosin localization, junction lifetimes, cell rearrangements and tissue strain after an acute or mosaic perturbation that leaves enough epithelial structure for movement to occur. A general epithelial collapse would establish an essential structural role but would not isolate its role in planar coordination.

The third example is control of fly tracheal tube size by the apical determinant <Crumbs> and basolateral regulators including <Yurt> and <Coracle>. <Crumbs> promotes the apical domain, so restricting its activity helps constrain the amount of luminal cell surface and hence tube dimensions. Mutations in yurt produce overlong tracheal tubes, and reducing <Crumbs> dosage suppresses important length defects; <Crumbs> overexpression has the opposite tendency. This genetic interaction connects the balance of apical and basolateral regulators to organ geometry rather than simply to the presence of an epithelial sheet.<Epithelial polarity proteins regulate Drosophila tracheal tube size in parallel to the luminal matrix pathway by Patrick Laprise and others>[Tube measurements, Crumbs dosage suppression and <Vermiform> secretion controls]{ref}

The luminal <extracellular matrix> is a crucial control in this example. Septate-junction-associated proteins can also affect delivery of the matrix-modifying protein <Vermiform>, potentially changing tube shape indirectly. Yet yurt mutants retain <Vermiform> secretion, and reducing <Crumbs> can suppress coracle length defects without restoring the secretion defect. <Scribble> mutants also show long tubes but are not equivalently rescued by the tested <Crumbs> reduction. The result supports parallel and partly distinct routes, rather than a universal linear pathway in which every basolateral protein merely inhibits <Crumbs>. Compare cell number, apical area, tube length and diameter independently: a longer tube need not result from extra divisions. Quantitative dosage interactions and rescue are particularly informative because they can reveal a size-control function while preserving much of epithelial organization.

Together, these examples link polarity to three separable operations. <Cdc42> coordinates the location of new apical surfaces during division; <Bazooka> contributes to directional junction remodelling within a sheet; and the Crumbs-regulator balance sets the extent of a luminal surface. Conversely, changing forces and geometry can alter junctional organization, so the relationship is reciprocal. The common principle is that epithelial polarity supplies spatial constraints on growth and rearrangement. It is a dynamic component of <morphogenesis>, not merely a static property to be established before morphogenesis starts.

**Mechanics shapes tissues by generating active forces, directing rearrangements and converting constrained growth into deformation.** Three experimentally distinct examples are folding of the fly ventral <epithelium>, elongation of the fly germband and formation of chick intestinal villi. They respectively emphasize changes in cell shape, changes in cell neighbours and a growth-driven instability. In each case, a satisfactory explanation needs both a force-generating process and the material or boundary conditions that convert it into a particular shape.

First, <apical constriction> drives <Drosophila ventral furrow formation>. Ventral cells accumulate a contractile <actomyosin> network near their apical surfaces. Reducing apical area while retaining tissue continuity changes cell geometry and contributes to inward bending of the epithelial sheet. This is not adequately described as uniform contraction of a permanent circumferential belt. Time-resolved imaging showed pulses of medioapical <myosin II> accumulation associated with discrete contractions, and stabilization between pulses permits net constriction. Regulation by <Snail> and <Twist> helps coordinate contraction and the retention of the resulting shape.<Pulsed contractions of an actin-myosin network drive apical constriction by Adam C. Martin and others>[Live imaging, pulsatile apical contraction and transcription-factor perturbations]{ref}

The force must be transmitted to neighbouring cells through <adherens junctions>; a detached contracting cortex can deform internally without folding a coherent sheet. Geometry is therefore a collective property. A useful causal test compares acute inhibition of myosin activation with perturbation of junctional coupling, scoring apical area, contraction pulses, cell length and tissue curvature separately. <laser ablation> can reveal the direction of tissue load, and localized activation of contractility can test whether the site of force generation predicts bending. Recoil velocity alone is not tension: in an overdamped approximation $v_0\sim T/\zeta$, where $T$ is released tension and $\zeta$ an effective resistance. Comparing recoil between conditions requires considering possible changes in resistance as well as load. External pressure, basal constraints and neighbouring tissue movement also influence invagination, so apical contraction need not be sufficient under every boundary condition.

Second, <Drosophila germband extension> changes tissue aspect ratio through oriented <cell intercalation>. Selected cell junctions shrink, cells briefly share a vertex or multicellular rosette, and new junctions appear in the complementary orientation. Repeated local neighbour exchanges extend the tissue along one axis while narrowing it along the other. <myosin II> is enriched at the shrinking interfaces, and interfering with its activity disrupts polarized junction remodelling. This supplies a mechanism for <convergent extension> without requiring that every cell grow longer or that an increased number of divisions provide the extension.<Myosin-dependent junction remodelling controls planar cell intercalation and axis elongation by Claire Bertet and others>[Time-lapse junction analysis and perturbation of polarized myosin activity]{ref}

Here the crucial distinction is between active rearrangement and passive stretching. Track all vertices and neighbours in a patch and decompose tissue deformation into cell-shape change, neighbour exchange and cell-number change. A tissue can lengthen while individual cells remain similar in shape if topology changes; alternatively, pulling from a neighbouring tissue can elongate cells without local intercalation supplying the force. Test whether local myosin inhibition suppresses the junction transitions before global tissue movement is lost, and compare spatial patterns of load by <laser ablation>. Rescue must restore directional transitions as well as the final length. <planar cell polarity> provides the orientation, and junctional adhesion permits force transmission while contacts are remodelled. Thus mechanics can translate a molecular anisotropy into coordinated movement of an epithelial sheet.

Third, chick <intestinal villi> illustrate <differential growth> constrained by surrounding layers. The growing inner tissues are enclosed by sequentially differentiating <smooth muscle> layers. Mechanical constraints drive a progression from longitudinal ridges through zigzags toward a villous surface. The important process is not simply muscle contraction pushing each villus into place: the muscle acts as a restricting layer against which continued tissue growth builds compressive <stress>. In the primary experiments, blocking muscle differentiation prevented the expected folding, while an artificial constraining silk tube could restore folding in the inhibited tissue. This rescue is particularly informative because it replaces a living layer's mechanical contribution without restoring all its possible signals.<Villification: how the gut gets its villi by Amy E. Shyer and others>[Muscle perturbation, artificial confinement and mechanical modelling]{ref}

The physical principle is a <buckling> instability: a sufficiently compressed growing layer can lower its mechanical cost by bending. The wavelength and mode depend on geometry, thickness and stiffness, so equal amounts of growth need not yield equal shapes in different environments. An ideal thin-plate model has bending rigidity $B=Eh^3/[12(1-\nu^2)]$, with <Young's modulus> $E$, thickness $h$ and <Poisson's ratio> $\nu$. It predicts strong thickness sensitivity of resistance to bending, but this formula alone does not compute the wavelength of a living multilayer gut. That requires measured geometry, growth and an appropriate constitutive model. Compare proliferation, muscle differentiation, material properties and folding after each perturbation to distinguish mechanical confinement from secondary effects on growth.

The chick example should not be imposed unmodified on mouse villus development, where mesenchymal cluster patterning precedes the corresponding epithelial deformation. Nor is any of the three mechanisms independent of chemical signalling: signals position contractility, specify muscle and regulate adhesion, while mechanical deformation changes the subsequent signalling environment. Their distinct causal signatures are nonetheless clear. A contractile apical network folds a coupled sheet; anisotropic junctional tension changes neighbours and tissue aspect ratio; constrained growth generates an instability whose form depends on material properties. Testing these signatures through timed perturbation and mechanical rescue explains organ shape more precisely than simply observing that a cytoskeletal protein is required.
