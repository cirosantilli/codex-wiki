**<behavioural plasticity> can slow inherited change by buffering selection, or accelerate it by permitting persistence and exposing organisms to new selection. There is no general ranking without specifying the trait, environment and timescale.** The immediate adjustment of an individual's <animal behaviour> is a change in expression. <evolution> of that response requires a change in inherited properties of a <biological population>. A learned innovation spreading through <cultural inheritance> is another form of transmitted change, which should be distinguished from a change in <allele frequencies>.

A <reaction norm> makes this distinction explicit. Suppose a behavioural measurement in environment $E$ is $z=a+bE$, with inherited variation in baseline $a$ and responsiveness $b$. For illustration, let the favoured value be $E$, and let relative <genotype fitness> be:
$$
w(a,b,E)=\exp\{-k[a+bE-E]^2-cb^2\},\qquad k>0,\quad c\geq0.
$$
The first term penalizes mismatch; the second represents a possible cost of responsiveness. This is a deliberately simple model, not a claim that all <animal behaviour> has one optimum. Its <phenotypic selection gradient> on baseline, holding $b$ fixed, is:
$$
\frac{\partial\log w}{\partial a}=2k[(1-b)E-a].
$$
Immediately after a change to $E=1$, individuals with $a=0,b=1$ match the optimum and experience no mismatch selection on $a$; otherwise identical individuals with $b=0$ experience a gradient $2k$. Thus <phenotypic buffering> can reduce selection for inherited baseline change even when the response is highly beneficial. If $c>0$, selection on responsiveness can nevertheless persist. Buffering one dimension of the <phenotype> does not stop all <evolution>.

This impeding effect is biologically plausible when animals can avoid adverse conditions. Moving to a cooler microhabitat can reduce exposure to heat and therefore weaken selection on heat tolerance. Switching food can similarly reduce selection on digestive performance for an avoided resource. These are conditional mechanisms: the relevant evidence would be reduced differences in survival or <reproduction> among inherited physiological variants, rather than mere observation that animals move or switch foods. <learning> can also allow several inherited variants to reach similar behavioural performance, weakening selection among them. Such buffering may preserve <genetic variation>, which can become consequential after later environmental change; slow immediate change can therefore coexist with greater long-term evolutionary potential.

Acceleration begins with the demographic problem. An inherited advantage cannot spread in a lineage that disappears before it is expressed or transmitted. A flexible response can allow individuals to survive and reproduce after an environmental shift, providing time for subsequent inherited <adaptation> and possibly <evolutionary rescue>. This need not increase the selection coefficient of a particular variant. Increasing the number of surviving individuals or generations available for selection can matter even while the per-generation gradient becomes smaller. A comparison of change per generation and total <adaptation> over a fixed number of years can consequently give different answers.

<behavioural plasticity> can also change which environments impose selection. A newly learned <foraging> technique may expose animals to a new resource, making inherited differences in handling, digestion or sensory discrimination important. <habitat preference> can alter encounters with competitors and mates; repeated use of a new host can influence <gene flow> when mating occurs there. The <host fidelity> of <Rhagoletis pomonella> provides evidence that host-associated actions can constitute a premating barrier. It demonstrates a route from <animal behaviour> to evolutionary consequences, but does not by itself establish that the initiating host shift was a plastic response. <Host fidelity is an effective premating barrier between sympatric races of the apple maggot fly by Jeffrey L. Feder and others>[PNAS 91, pp. 7990–7994; three mark-and-recapture studies and host-associated gene flow]{ref} Establishing that historical sequence is necessary before counting the case as <plasticity-led evolution>.

In the <Baldwin effect>, acquired adjustments change survival and the selection subsequently experienced by inherited variants. <genetic accommodation> can then refine the threshold, specificity or efficiency of an environmentally induced response. Its outcome may be greater <phenotypic plasticity>, a differently tuned response, or <genetic assimilation>, in which expression becomes less dependent on the original inducing condition. No step requires a learned action to be copied directly into <DNA>.

Experiments on <spadefoot toad> tadpoles provide relevant, though not exclusively behavioural, evidence. Populations that expressed the carnivore ecomorph more frequently showed refinement of associated resource-use performance and morphology under laboratory comparison. Such correspondence is predicted if recurrent induction exposes the expressed <phenotype> to selection. The comparison supports inherited refinement following environmental responsiveness, but contemporary populations are not a direct film of the ancestral transition, and their prior ecological histories could differ. <Plasticity-led evolution: evaluating the key prediction of frequency-dependent adaptation by Nicholas A. Levis and David W. Pfennig>[laboratory competition and expression of the carnivore ecomorph in <spadefoot toad> tadpoles]{ref} Classic selection on heat-induced crossveinless wings in <Drosophila melanogaster> also showed that a previously induced <phenotype> could eventually appear without the inducing treatment. That is evidence for <genetic assimilation>, not direct evidence that behavioural innovations generally accelerate <evolution>. <Genetic assimilation of an acquired character by Conrad Hal Waddington>[Evolution 7, pp. 118–126; heat-induced crossveinless wings and selection across generations]{ref}

The balance depends on cue reliability, environmental persistence and <costs of phenotypic plasticity>. A response based on a formerly reliable cue can become maladaptive after environmental change. A persistent new environment may favour a cheaper constitutive solution, whereas recurring variation can favour continued flexibility. Response delays, limited sensory discrimination and trade-offs between alternative activities can leave substantial residual selection. Conversely, sufficiently effective and inexpensive flexibility can remove much of the benefit of further inherited change. The availability of relevant <genetic variation> and its <genetic covariance> with other traits also constrain the response.

A decisive study would separate immediate adjustment from inherited divergence. Replicated populations could experience stable and fluctuating environments, with variation in access to a learned response or environmental cue. Descendants should then be assayed in a <common garden experiment>, both with and without that cue. Repeated measures estimate within-individual change; <pedigrees> or crosses estimate inherited <reaction norm> differences. Survival, <reproduction>, population persistence and changes in other traits must all be measured. Manipulating responsiveness requires controls for sensory damage, <physiological stress>[stress] and general incapacity: disabling a neural process and then observing extinction would not isolate an evolutionary benefit of flexibility. Comparisons across <species> additionally require control for <phylogenetic nonindependence> and ecological opportunity.

**The strongest conclusion is conditional: effective adjustment often weakens immediate selection on the buffered trait, while persistence, new ecological exposure and inherited refinement can increase cumulative evolutionary change.** Those mechanisms can operate together within the same lineage; their relative importance is an empirical question rather than an intrinsic property of flexible <animal behaviour>.

**<reinforcement> is the strengthening of reproductive barriers by selection against costly hybridization. Its importance must be demonstrated causally, rather than inferred solely from greater mating discrimination where <species> meet.** Typically, partially isolated lineages encounter one another, produce low-fitness hybrids, and experience selection favouring discrimination that reduces such matings. <secondary contact> after divergence is a common setting, but the definition concerns the selective mechanism rather than one obligatory geographical history.

Consider a rare inherited preference that reduces the probability of producing a hybrid by $\delta$, when hybrid offspring lose a proportion $d$ of <reproductive success> relative to within-population offspring. In a simplified accounting with a common offspring-equivalent denominator, the benefit is $\delta d$. If stronger discrimination costs $c$ through delayed mating or rejection of suitable mates, it is favoured when:
$$
\delta d>c.
$$
This expresses the logic of <reinforcement>, not a complete <population genetics> model. Encounter rates determine $\delta$, sex-specific costs can differ, and <gene flow>, <recombination> and associations between preference and ancestry determine whether discrimination can evolve and persist. The mere existence of low-fitness hybrids does not guarantee that an effective preference variant is available or that its costs are small.

The first empirical requirement is evidence for contact and opportunity to hybridize. Maps should establish coexistence at the scale of mating, not merely occurrence within the same region. Field observations and parentage or genomic ancestry estimates can establish heterospecific mating and recent <gene flow>. Historical contact may also be relevant if successful <reinforcement> has already made contemporary hybrids rare. Complete present-day separation therefore does not disprove a past role, but it makes that historical role harder to identify. Models fitted to genomic data should compare alternative demographic histories rather than equating shared <alleles> automatically with recent <introgression>.

The second requirement is a reproductive cost. Reciprocal crosses should measure fertilization, survival, fertility and subsequent <reproductive success>, including later-generation and backcross offspring where feasible. Reduced performance may be intrinsic, such as <hybrid sterility>, or ecological, such as failure to use either parental habitat effectively. Laboratory survival alone can miss the latter. Reciprocal environmental comparisons with parental controls test whether hybrid disadvantages persist under natural conditions. Handling effects, parental condition and rearing density should be standardized. Selection against hybrids must be distinguished from lower mating rates caused solely by ecological separation.

The third requirement is variation in barriers consistent with selection against those costs. A classic prediction is <reproductive character displacement>: stronger discrimination in <sympatry> than in populations outside the contact zone. Female <Drosophila pseudoobscura> from regions shared with <Drosophila persimilis> showed greater sexual isolation than females from outside that overlap, providing an influential test of this prediction. <Speciation driven by natural selection in Drosophila by Mohamed A. Noor>[Nature 375, pp. 674–675; geographic variation in female discrimination against D. persimilis]{ref} Comparisons should use several independent populations, matched environments and standardized male stimuli. Both choice and no-choice assays are useful: the former measures relative preference among available partners, whereas the latter asks whether rejection persists when an alternative partner is absent. Neither alone estimates realized <gene flow> in the field.

The geographic pattern has important alternatives. Different environments can independently favour different signals or sensory responses; <sexual selection> within each lineage can generate divergence; existing discrimination can permit coexistence while poorly discriminating populations disappear. This last process can sort populations without increasing discrimination within them. Costly heterospecific courtship can also favour signal divergence without selection against hybrid offspring. Such reproductive interference is relevant to isolation, but should not automatically be called <reinforcement> in its strict hybrid-cost sense. <Common garden experiments> help distinguish inherited differences from immediate effects of environment or experience, but do not on their own identify the historical selective cause.

Crosses and mapping provide a further line of evidence. In the <Drosophila pseudoobscura> system, crosses between populations with stronger and weaker discrimination showed inherited variation in enhanced discrimination, with high discrimination expressed in first-generation crosses. Chromosome substitutions and finer mapping implicated multiple regions, and their contributions differed among sampled populations. Controls measured male mating attempts, helping separate female rejection from altered male effort. Importantly, candidate regions enriched for olfactory functions were suggestive rather than proof of a particular sensory mechanism; mapping an interval is not identifying every causal <gene>. <The Genetics of Speciation by Reinforcement by Daniel Ortiz-Barrientos and others>[PLoS Biology 2, e416; crosses, mate discrimination and the genetic basis of enhanced isolation]{ref} This evidence makes an evolutionary response plausible and helps distinguish basal isolation from its subsequent enhancement.

The most direct prospective test would use replicated <experimental evolution>. Start with the same partially isolated populations, allow contact in treatment lines, and vary the <biological fitness> consequences of hybrid offspring while retaining appropriately matched controls. Across generations, test discrimination in a common environment, measure actual hybrid production and follow parental and hybrid <reproductive success>. Lines must be independent <experimental units>. Artificially removing hybrids can demonstrate a possible response to selection, but complete elimination of all hybrid-mediated <gene flow> simplifies the problem that the natural hypothesis seeks to explain. Changes due to crowding, <learning> or laboratory <adaptation> must also be separated from inherited changes in mate discrimination. A field intervention changing encounter rates could be informative, but ethical and ecological constraints often make longitudinal natural contact zones preferable.

Finally, importance in <speciation> is a broader question than existence in one pair of <species>. Comparative data should estimate how often hybrid costs, geographical overlap and enhanced barriers coincide, controlling for divergence time, ecology and <phylogenetic nonindependence>. Ascertainment matters: conspicuous surviving hybrid zones may omit cases where contact caused fusion, exclusion or near-complete isolation. A stronger preference is evolutionarily consequential only if it reduces effective exchange or helps maintain distinct lineages. Its effect should be measured alongside habitat separation, seasonal timing and <postzygotic isolation>, because sequential barriers act on the individuals that reach each stage.

**Convincing evidence combines hybrid costs, opportunity for contact, inherited enhancement of isolation and exclusion of competing selective explanations.** <reinforcement> can complete or stabilize a separation already under way, but geographic differences in signals alone establish neither its mechanism nor its frequency as a cause of <speciation>.

**Knowing the molecular basis of a behaviour is not a prerequisite for understanding its <evolution> or many of its evolutionary consequences. Evidence about transmission is essential for claims of inherited change, while detailed <genetic architecture> becomes essential for particular mechanistic predictions.** The claim therefore depends on what is meant by a genetic basis and on the explanatory question being asked.

<Tinbergen's four questions> distinguish mechanism, development, adaptive significance and evolutionary history. Identifying a neural or molecular mechanism helps explain how a response is produced; it does not automatically show why a variant spread, whether it currently increases <reproductive success>, or whether the same mechanism generated the ancestral transition. Conversely, a well-supported evolutionary explanation can begin with experimentally demonstrated <biological fitness> consequences and transmission without a complete molecular account. These explanatory levels are complementary rather than substitutes. <On aims and methods of Ethology by Nikolaas Tinbergen>[Zeitschrift für Tierpsychologie 20, pp. 410–433; causation, development, survival value and evolution]{ref}

The phrase genetic basis can refer to several different achievements: identifying inherited variation; estimating the number and effects of contributing <genetic loci>; finding a causal nucleotide change; or describing every <gene> required for development of the relevant sensory and motor systems. These are not equivalent. Many <genes> can be necessary for an animal to perform an action while none explains variation in that action within the studied population. Knocking out a broadly required neural <gene> and eliminating courtship does not identify an evolutionary substitution for a naturally varying <mate preference>.

<quantitative genetics> can predict a response without naming the causal molecular variants. Under the appropriate additive, generational and environmental assumptions, the <multivariate breeder's equation> gives:
$$
\Delta\overline{\mathbf z}=\mathbf G\boldsymbol\beta,
$$
where $\mathbf G$ is the <additive genetic covariance matrix> and $\boldsymbol\beta$ the vector of <phenotypic selection gradients>. Behavioural measurements, <pedigrees>, repeated observations and <reproductive success> can therefore estimate whether and in what direction a response is expected. The covariances matter: selection favouring exploration can produce a correlated response in aggression or constrain the response when their <biological fitness> effects conflict. Naming an exploration-related <gene> would not replace this population-level information.

These estimates require strong controls. <behavioural repeatability> is consistency among observations of the same individual; it can reflect persistent environment as well as inherited differences and is not equivalent to <heritability>. Parent–offspring resemblance can also reflect teaching, maternal provisioning or shared habitat. <cross-fostering>, <common garden experiments> and appropriately structured breeding designs help separate these alternatives. <heritability> is conditional on the sampled population and environment, not a measure of immutability. A response measured in one environment may fail elsewhere because of <genotype-environment interaction> or altered selection. Thus ignorance of exact molecular variants is compatible with informative prediction, but ignorance of transmission and its confounders is not.

Molecular work adds power when it establishes a causal link to naturally occurring variation. In <Drosophila melanogaster>, rover and sitter food-search variants were connected to the <foraging gene>, which encodes a <cGMP-dependent protein kinase>. Mapping, enzyme activity and transgenic rescue linked the molecular difference to behavioural performance; comparable locomotion without food helped exclude a general activity explanation. <Natural Behavior Polymorphism Due to a cGMP-Dependent Protein Kinase of Drosophila by K. A. Osborne and others>[Science 277, pp. 834–836, Tables 1–3; foraging variants, kinase activity and transgenic rescue]{ref} This is much stronger than finding a statistical association between a <gene> and a behavioural score. Nevertheless, the experiment alone does not establish which ecological conditions historically maintained the polymorphism. That requires <biological fitness> comparisons across resources, densities and frequencies, with genetic-background controls.

<genetic architecture> is particularly informative when <animal behaviour> changes <gene flow>. In <Heliconius melpomene> and <Heliconius cydno>, mapping identified several major regions associated with male mating preferences; one lay close to the wing-pattern regulator <Optix>. Tight <genetic linkage> can help maintain associations between a mating cue and preference despite exchange between lineages. It does not establish <pleiotropy>, and courtship assays must distinguish attraction from the female's response and subsequent mating. <Genetic dissection of assortative mating behavior by Richard M. Merrill and others>[PLoS Biology 17, e2005902; preference QTLs, linkage to optix, and limitations of courtship assays]{ref} Here molecular localization helps evaluate whether a proposed route to <reproductive isolation> is genetically feasible. A preference could still have been shown to reduce heterospecific mating before its contributing regions were mapped.

The influence of one individual's <animal behaviour> on another's development further complicates a simple own-genotype explanation. Maternal provisioning in <Onthophagus taurus> was studied using a breeding design that separated direct and maternal genetic contributions to offspring traits. <The genetics of maternal care: direct and indirect genetic effects on phenotype in the dung beetle Onthophagus taurus by John Hunt and others>[PNAS 99, pp. 6828–6832; paternal half-sib design, brood mass and maternal contributions]{ref} An <indirect genetic effect> occurs when another individual's inherited properties alter the focal individual's <phenotype>. Such effects change the environment in which selection acts and can alter expected evolutionary responses. Identifying only the offspring's molecular variants would miss an important part of the causal system. Conversely, an observed maternal effect is not automatically a maternal genetic effect; provisioning can vary for environmental reasons.

Transmission need not follow genetic parentage. In wild <great tits>, experimenters trained demonstrators to use alternative puzzle-box techniques and followed the spread of the introduced variants. Persistent local traditions and preferential adoption of the common local technique supported <social learning> and conformist transmission. <Experimentally induced innovations lead to persistent culture via conformity in wild birds by Lucy M. Aplin and others>[Nature 518, pp. 538–541; seeded puzzle-box techniques and social diffusion in wild great tits]{ref} The experiment established a route of behavioural inheritance without identifying a molecular difference between the techniques' users. It did not prove that <learning> ability has no inherited basis, or that the new tradition changed <allele frequencies>. Rather, it shows why an account of behavioural change needs to identify the relevant transmission process instead of assuming it.

Even a learned response with no demonstrated inherited differences can influence subsequent <evolution>. A new resource use can alter selection on digestion; a socially learned mating signal can alter <assortative mating>; habitat choice can change contact with other populations. The immediate causal effect can be tested by manipulating the action or its social transmission and measuring <biological fitness> or exchange. To claim ensuing genetic <adaptation>, one must additionally show inherited change. To explain exactly which variants can respond, how quickly, and with what correlated effects, information about <genetic architecture> becomes increasingly valuable.

The most productive programme therefore combines behavioural intervention, transmission studies, <biological fitness> measurement and molecular analysis when it addresses an unresolved mechanism. Candidate variants should be tested across natural genetic backgrounds and environments, preferably with targeted perturbation and rescue; historical claims also need comparative or temporal evidence. **Exact molecular knowledge is neither sufficient nor universally necessary: the necessary evidence is the evidence that connects the particular behavioural difference, its transmission and its measured evolutionary consequence.**

**Apparent resemblance generates competing hypotheses, not a diagnosis of <mimicry>. Establishing receiver-relevant similarity, evolutionary history and the <biological fitness> effect of resemblance are separate tasks.** Shared ancestry, shared developmental responses and similar ecological demands may all contribute, and the origin of a pattern need not be the same as the process currently maintaining it.

First establish that the sampled individuals represent distinct <species> and comparable life stages and sexes. Voucher records, repeated observations, morphology and independent genetic evidence help distinguish <species> from geographical forms or sexually different members of one <species>. Sampling should span localities, seasons and within-species variation, since choosing only strikingly similar specimens exaggerates community-wide resemblance. For each individual, record dorsal and ventral patterns, size, resting posture, flight behaviour, microhabitat and activity time. An identical-looking patch in a photograph may rarely be exposed to the same receiver in nature.

Similarity must then be measured for plausible receivers. Predatory <birds> and butterfly mates need not share human <colour vision>. Calibrated <reflectance> measurements across visible and ultraviolet wavelengths, natural illumination and relevant backgrounds can support a <colour vision model>. A simplified relative <receptor quantum catch> is:
$$
Q_i\propto\int L(\lambda)R(\lambda)S_i(\lambda)\,d\lambda,
$$
where $L$ is illumination, $R$ surface <reflectance> and $S_i$ the effective <spectral sensitivity> of receptor class $i$. The comparison should include <receptor noise> and spatial resolution, because two spectrally distinguishable patches may be unresolved at attack distance. An apparent human match may conceal an ultraviolet or spatial difference. Conversely, experimentally demonstrated <predator> generalization can establish functional similarity despite a difference obvious to a human. A model predicts discrimination; behavioural tests verify whether the animal actually uses it.

One hypothesis is retention of an ancestral pattern through <biological homology>. Infer relationships from many independent genomic regions, then reconstruct pattern changes with uncertainty on the resulting <phylogenetic tree>. Resemblance concentrated among close relatives is compatible with retention. Similarity among distant relatives, especially matching different local patterns in different places, supports repeated change, although uncertainty in ancestral states must be retained. A single wing-pattern <gene tree> cannot serve as the independent <species> history when that very region might have crossed <species> boundaries.

A second hypothesis is <Müllerian mimicry>. If several <species> are defended and share <predators>, a common warning pattern can reduce the number of individuals attacked while <predators> learn avoidance. A local <mimicry ring> is predicted, with newcomers to an unfamiliar pattern suffering more attacks. Measure defence rather than infer it from colour: chemical assays, controlled feeding responses and subsequent <predator> behaviour address different components of <palatability>. <predators> from different localities may have different experience, so both naive and experienced receivers are informative. Several defended <species> can differ in defence strength; their interaction need not fit a perfectly symmetric mutual benefit.

A third hypothesis is <Batesian mimicry>, in which a relatively undefended <species> gains protection by resembling a defended model. This predicts protection dependent on the model's presence, abundance and the receiver's experience. A resemblance experiment should therefore vary both visual matching and the frequency or density of defended and undefended <prey>. A palatable mimic that becomes too common can weaken learned avoidance, whereas sharing a well-defended signal can reinforce it. These are ecological predictions to test, not a rule that each natural assemblage falls into one simple category. Defence varies with diet, age and the <predator> used in the assay.

The critical causal experiment changes the signal while controlling the rest of the <prey>. Artificial <prey> or wing models can independently vary colour and geometry against a standardized food reward. Randomized placement and position changes control background and location; handling and paint controls are needed with live butterflies. Record detection, attack and rejection separately: fewer missing <prey> can reflect poor detection rather than avoidance after recognition. Camera records help identify actual <predators> and distinguish attack from weather damage or removal by other animals. Artificial models lack movement and some chemical information, so their results should be checked against observations of live <prey> where feasible.

A field precedent is the release of different <Heliconius cydno> colour morphs into communities with different abundant co-models. Survival patterns supported an advantage to local matching, and the experiment also examined the prediction that this disadvantage should decrease with increased release density. <Three-butterfly system provides a field test of müllerian mimicry by Durrell D. Kapan>[Nature 409, pp. 338–340; H. cydno release experiments, local co-models and release density]{ref} This is more informative than simply mapping similar butterflies in the same forest. However, disappearance after release can include dispersal and unequal detectability; recapture effort, movement observations and attack evidence are needed when adapting such a design. Replication belongs at the site or independently manipulated community level, rather than treating every observation within one site as an independent community comparison.

Other ecological explanations deserve distinct predictions. Shared <crypsis> could make <species> match the same leaves, bark or light environment without resembling one another as defended models. Test detection against matched and mismatched backgrounds, separating the visibility of the <prey> from a <predator>'s willingness to consume it. Shared <thermoregulation>[thermoregulatory] demands could favour similar absorption properties; temperature and performance measurements would then be relevant. Similar mate-detection environments could favour similar signals, but <mate preference> experiments must establish the relevant receiver and show how <species> avoid reproductive confusion. These mechanisms can coexist with defensive <mimicry>.

A common developmental or environmental cause is another possibility. Rear offspring from different populations and <species> in standardized and experimentally varied conditions, controlling larval diet, temperature and age. Persistence of differences under common rearing supports inherited contributions; condition-dependent convergence supports <phenotypic plasticity>. Crosses and <quantitative trait locus mapping> can then test the <genetic architecture> of patterning. Work on <Optix> in <Heliconius> combined mapping, expression and population comparisons to identify a shared regulator of red wing patterning. <optix Drives the Repeated Convergent Evolution of Butterfly Wing Pattern Mimicry by Robert D. Reed and others>[Science 333, pp. 1137–1141; mapping, expression and population genetics of red wing patterning]{ref} Use of the same developmental <gene> does not by itself prove a single recent origin of the pattern, nor prove that resemblance is adaptive.

Finally, adaptive <introgression> could transfer a pattern-associated region between <species>. Genome-wide ancestry should be compared with local ancestry around pattern regions, testing alternatives such as <incomplete lineage sorting>. The <Heliconius> genome study found evidence of exchange associated with mimetic adaptations among lineages. <Butterfly genome reveals promiscuous exchange of mimicry adaptations among species by The Heliconius Genome Consortium>[Nature 487, pp. 94–98; genomic comparisons and introgression around mimicry loci]{ref} This concerns the route by which a useful pattern is acquired; selection by <predators> can still explain its maintenance. **The strongest explanation combines receiver-specific similarity, a tested ecological benefit and an independently supported developmental and evolutionary history; neither human resemblance nor a shared pattern-associated <gene> supplies all three.**

**The proposed asymmetry is not generally defensible: <animal behaviour> and <immunity> form a reciprocal system, and “more” has no common meaning without an outcome, scale and intervention.** Avoiding <infection>, controlling <pathogen> growth, tolerating tissue damage and changing social contact are different outcomes. Their causal effect sizes cannot be ranked by counting examples in either direction.

The definition of <immunity> matters first. In its narrower physiological sense, it concerns cellular and molecular defences. A <behavioural immune system> describes actions that reduce exposure before those defences are challenged, while <social immunity> includes protection generated by interactions among group members. If avoidance is defined as part of <immunity>, a statement that <animal behaviour> influences <immunity> becomes partly true by definition. A useful analysis instead specifies whether an action changes exposure, immune activity, <pathogen> burden or the damage associated with that burden. <disease resistance> reduces burden; <disease tolerance> reduces damage at a given burden. Lower illness alone does not distinguish these routes.

One strong route from <animal behaviour> to defence is the management of exposure. Choice of food, mates and social partners determines contact with infectious material. <grooming> can remove external <parasites>, and <hygienic behaviour> can remove contaminated material from a nest. Conversely, aggregation and some forms of close social contact can increase opportunities for transmission. These effects depend on the <pathogen>'s transmission route: removing one kind of contact need not reduce <infection> spread by an environmental reservoir. An observed association between sociability and <infection> can also arise because <infection> changes sociability, making temporal or experimental evidence essential.

<honeybee> colonies selected for <hygienic behaviour> provide a causal evolutionary and experimental example. Selection based on brood-removal assays was combined with exposure to <American foulbrood> <disease>, allowing <disease> outcomes in selected and comparison colonies to be assessed. Removal of infected brood can reduce a colony's infectious reservoir and protect other individuals. <Resistance to American foulbrood disease by honey bee colonies Apis mellifera bred for hygienic behavior by Marla Spivak and Gary S. Reuter>[Apidologie 32, pp. 555–565; selected hygienic colonies, brood assays and pathogen challenge]{ref} The result concerns colony protection, not a demonstration that each worker's cellular <immunity> is stronger. Selection can change correlated traits as well as brood removal, so a mechanistic follow-up should match colony size, brood age, exposure and background, and manipulate the opportunity for removal. Colonies are independent <experimental units>; scoring many cells within one colony does not create many independently selected colonies.

Choice of an offspring’s food plant can also change the chemical environment encountered by <parasites>. Infected <monarch butterflies> preferentially laid eggs on plants that reduced <parasite> growth and <disease> in their offspring. This supported <transgenerational medication>: neither a simple cure of the mother nor merely a demonstration that a toxic plant can inhibit a <parasite>. <Evidence for trans-generational medication in nature by Thierry Lefèvre and others>[Ecology Letters 13, pp. 1485–1493; infected monarch mothers, oviposition choice and offspring parasite growth]{ref} The inference needs both the infection-dependent change in <oviposition> and the offspring benefit. Tests should include infected and uninfected mothers offered the same plant alternatives, standardized <parasite> exposure of offspring, and measurements of larval growth and survival as well as <parasite> burden. Nutritional quality and general plant preference are competing explanations. A cost of the medicinal plant in parasite-free conditions would additionally help explain why every individual does not always choose it.

<animal behaviour> can influence physiological <immunity> as well as exposure. Activity, feeding and <sleep> affect resource acquisition and allocation, while social interactions can alter endocrine and neural signals. These are plausible routes, but a study must measure the relevant immune process and not infer it from survival alone. An immune assay can increase because the animal is more infected, rather than because it has greater protective capacity. <randomization>, controlled nutrition and repeated observations can help separate these interpretations. Any claim of a trade-off should identify the limiting resource and the competing <biological fitness> demands; a change in one circulating marker is insufficient.

The reverse direction is equally substantive. <Immune challenges> can change activity, appetite, social investigation and priorities, producing <sickness behaviour>. Such changes may conserve resources, reduce exposure to further hazards or alter transmission, but some can instead reflect damaging <inflammation> or a <pathogen>'s manipulation. Reduced activity after <infection> is therefore not automatically adaptive host defence. Comparing host <biological fitness> with and without the response, and separating immune activation from direct <pathogen> action, is necessary for an evolutionary interpretation.

<lipopolysaccharide> provides one experimental way to induce an inflammatory response without a replicating <pathogen>. Work on lactating <house mice> showed that the resulting effects depended on the activity and environmental context: pup retrieval could be maintained, and cold conditions elicited nest building despite the challenge. <Differential Effects of Lipopolysaccharide on Pup Retrieving and Nest Building in Lactating Mice by Arnaud Aubert and others>[Brain, Behavior, and Immunity 11, pp. 107–118; immune challenge, maternal behaviour and ambient temperature]{ref} This supports a reorganization of competing motivations rather than simple global paralysis. It does not show that all illness-related inactivity is strategic. Controls should match handling and injection, assess motor capacity and temperature, and distinguish loss of appetite from inability to acquire food. Dose and timing also matter: an artificial inflammatory challenge need not reproduce a natural <infection>'s trajectory. Blocking a candidate immune mediator, followed by a rescue intervention, would provide stronger evidence for a specific immune-to-neural pathway than a challenge alone.

Immune influences are not confined to overt sickness. In <house mice>, work on <interferon-gamma> linked immune deficiency and altered social preference to changes in neural connectivity and inhibitory physiology. Immune restoration and targeted signalling interventions strengthened a mechanistic interpretation beyond a correlation between illness and social withdrawal. <Unexpected role of interferon-γ in regulating neuronal connectivity and social behaviour by Anthony J. Filiano and others>[Nature 535, pp. 425–429, Figs. 1–2; immune deficiency, IFN-γ interventions, neural physiology and social preference]{ref} The experiments support a role for immune signalling in the tested neural and behavioural context; they do not establish a universal explanation of sociability or justify extrapolating a <house mouse> social-preference assay directly to human psychiatric conditions. Sensory ability, general activity, anxiety-related responses, housing and genetic background remain important controls when interpreting such assays.

The two directions can form the same feedback loop. An <immune response> changes social contact; changed contact alters subsequent exposure and <infection>; the new <infection> state changes the <immune response> again. The medication example similarly links infection-dependent action to offspring defence. Neither direction is an independent input fixed while the other acts. A useful factorial design would manipulate a specific action or opportunity for action and a specific <immune challenge>, then measure both behavioural and physiological outcomes over time. Baseline health, age, sex and reproductive state should be controlled, and the manipulation of action should avoid introducing injury or <physiological stress>[stress] that independently changes <immunity>.

A claim that one direction is larger would require comparable interventions and a shared endpoint, such as lifetime <reproductive success> or <pathogen> transmission, with uncertainty over an explicitly chosen time horizon. Different answers may follow for individual survival, colony persistence and epidemic spread. **The evidence supports bidirectional, context-dependent regulation: actions govern exposure and defence, while immune signals govern behavioural priorities and sometimes normal neural function.** A universal ranking conceals precisely the causal distinctions needed to understand this system.

**The <mirror-mark test> can provide evidence that an animal uses reflected information to identify and act on its own body. It is a narrower assay than a general test of <self-awareness>, and failure is especially difficult to interpret.** The core inference rests on directing an action to the otherwise unseen marked body part, rather than merely responding to something unusual in a mirror.

In a suitable design, an animal first has opportunities to inspect a mirror without a mark. A visible mark is then placed where it cannot be seen directly, using a procedure that avoids an informative tactile, olfactory or handling cue. After recovery from any necessary handling, investigators compare responses with the mirror available and unavailable, and with a matched invisible <sham treatment>. The predicted response is mark-directed investigation of the animal's own body specifically when the visible change can be seen in reflection. Looking at the mirror, attacking it or searching behind it are interesting observations, but do not alone establish <bodily self-recognition>.

The classic <chimpanzee> study used prior mirror exposure and otherwise unseen dye marks to demonstrate self-directed investigation. <Chimpanzees: Self-Recognition by Gordon G. Gallup Jr.>[Science 167, pp. 86–87; mirror exposure, hidden dye marks and self-directed touching]{ref} Its conceptual advance was to turn a claim about self-recognition into a behavioural prediction that could be compared with alternative responses. A mark on the reflected forehead corresponds to a location on the subject's own forehead. Reaching there uses information about the relation between the image and body; trying to touch the glass instead supports a different interpretation. An odourless mark that is imperceptible by touch makes this distinction stronger.

A rigorous modern design makes those alternatives explicit. In a factorial comparison, let $T_{VM}$ and $T_{SM}$ denote rates of appropriately localized body-directed actions for visible and sham marks with a mirror, and let $T_{VC}$ and $T_{SC}$ denote the corresponding rates with the mirror covered. The interaction contrast:
$$
D=(T_{VM}-T_{SM})-(T_{VC}-T_{SC})
$$
asks whether the visible mark's effect specifically depends on reflective information. A positive $D$ is useful only if the actions are correctly localized, opportunities to act are comparable and uncertainty is estimated across animals. It does not acquire a cognitive interpretation just because its statistical test is significant. General increases in <grooming>, attention or agitation need to be coded separately.

Controls should match application pressure, odour, texture and handling, vary mark side or location, and counterbalance condition order. A covered mirror controls its physical presence; a live or recorded other animal helps characterize social responses. Noncontingent or delayed self-images can test the importance of immediate <sensorimotor contingency>, though they also increase task difficulty. Separate trials with directly visible marks help establish that a subject notices such a change and has an available response, without training the critical hidden-mark action in advance. Coders should be <blinding>[blinded] where feasible, and the criteria for a pass specified before viewing the outcome. Repeated touches by one animal are not independent evidence from many animals.

A positive result supports at least a learned mapping between reflected information and the subject's body. How much more it supports is a problem of <construct validity>. An animal might combine visual contingencies, <proprioception> and a <body schema> without representing itself as a named, temporally continuous individual. Conversely, <learning> the mapping does not make the result meaningless: learned information about one's own body can itself be a form of <self-representation>. The question is which alternative mechanisms the design distinguishes. A pass does not by itself demonstrate <theory of mind>, <autobiographical memory>, moral understanding or a particular <subjective experience>.

Negative results are weaker because successful performance requires several capacities together: noticing the mark, attending to reflection, relating the image to the body, being motivated to investigate, and possessing a suitable action. Fear, social attraction to the image, poor visual access or indifference to marks can prevent the predicted response even when some self-related information is available. <ecological validity> is therefore central. Mirror dimensions, access, viewing geometry and prior experience must suit the <species>. Uniformly presenting a small mirror to animals of very different sizes does not create a biologically equivalent test.

The elephant study illustrates this dependence. Three <asian elephants> were exposed to a large accessible mirror and visible and sham marks. One passed the mark criterion, while the others also showed informative mirror-oriented exploration. <Self-recognition in an Asian elephant by Joshua M. Plotnik and others>[PNAS 103, pp. 17053–17057; large accessible mirror, visible and sham marks, one of three subjects passing]{ref} This is evidence about the observed individuals and assay, not a species-wide estimate based on one success, nor proof that the nonpassing individuals lacked every form of <self-representation>. The apparatus enabled close physical exploration that smaller or inaccessible mirrors would not permit.

The required action must also reflect anatomy. <Bottlenose dolphins> cannot use a hand to touch a facial mark. Investigators therefore examined orientations and movements exposing marked regions to a reflective surface, comparing marked and sham-marked conditions. <Mirror self-recognition in the bottlenose dolphin: A case of cognitive convergence by Diana Reiss and Lori Marino>[PNAS 98, pp. 5937–5942; marked and sham-marked dolphins and mirror-oriented body inspection]{ref} Such an <adaptation> avoids treating hand use as a cognitive prerequisite, but creates a greater interpretive burden: body inspection must be distinguished from ordinary swimming, attention to handling or generalized interest in the mirror. Spatially specific changes and <blinding>[blinded] coding are particularly valuable.

<cleaner wrasse> challenge assumptions about which animals should count as passing. Following mirror exposure, <fish> with coloured marks on otherwise unseen regions scraped their bodies in the mirror condition; transparent marks and coloured marks without a mirror did not elicit the same pattern. <If a fish can pass the mark test, what are the implications for consciousness and self-awareness testing in animals? by Masanori Kohda and others>[PLoS Biology 17, e3000021; cleaner wrasse, coloured versus transparent marks and mirror-dependent scraping]{ref} The ecological relevance of parasite-like marks helps explain motivation and sharpens the test. It also raises mechanistic questions about whether the animal identifies a persistent visual self or uses learned reflected cues to localize a possible <parasite>. Those alternatives require further controls and transfer tests; accepting a behavioural criterion in <mammals> but rejecting identical criteria solely because the animal is a <fish> would be inconsistent.

Tests in other modalities can help identify capacities missed by a visual assay. <dogs> investigated modified own odours more than unmodified own odours, with comparisons involving other dogs and the added odour alone. <Smelling themselves: Dogs investigate their own odours longer when modified in an “olfactory mirror” test by Alexandra Horowitz>[Behavioural Processes 143, pp. 17–24; own, modified-own, other-dog and modifier-only odour comparisons]{ref} The modifier-only comparison addresses simple interest in a novel smell, but sniffing longer does not require locating a change on one's own body. It consequently supports odour discrimination and a possible self-related comparison more directly than it establishes the same representation tested by hidden-mark localization. Ecologically appropriate tasks should be compared in terms of their information and inferential demands, rather than all being declared interchangeable mirror tests.

A stronger account would combine spontaneous mirror-guided inspection, transfer to new untrained body locations or viewing arrangements, and independent tasks probing self-related information. Training should be reported explicitly: success after reinforcement demonstrates a learned capacity under those conditions, while spontaneous generalization supports a different claim. Samples should include individual and developmental variation, and comparative analyses should retain uncertainty rather than coding entire <species> as cognitively present or absent after a handful of observations.

**A well-controlled pass is evidence for mirror-guided bodily identification; it is not a complete measure of a self-concept. A failure is compatible with absent capacity, unsuitable task demands or insufficient motivation.** The <mirror-mark test> is most useful as one component of a comparative research programme that separates those possibilities.
