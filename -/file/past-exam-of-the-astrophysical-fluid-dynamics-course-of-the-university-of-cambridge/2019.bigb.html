= Solution
{parent=1}

Write the initial <mass density> as $\rho=mn$. The <ideal gas law> gives <pressure> $p=nk_BT$, and the sphere has $M=4\pi\rho R^3/3$. Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $G$ is the <gravitational constant>. First obtain the <gravitational potential energy> by adding concentric shells. The mass inside radius $r$ is $M(r)=Mr^3/R^3$, so
$$
W=-\int_0^R\frac{GM(r)}r\,dM(r)
=-\int_0^R\frac{GMr^3}{R^3r}\frac{3Mr^2}{R^3}\,dr
=-\frac{3GM^2}{5R}.
$$
In a small contraction $dR<0$ at fixed total <mass>, the gravitational <energy> released is $-dW=3GM^2|dR|/(5R^2)$. The positive <work> required against the gas <pressure> is $-p\,dV=4\pi R^2p|dR|$. Equating these gives
$$
\frac{3GM^2}{5R^2}=4\pi R^2nk_BT
=\frac{3Mk_BT}{mR},\qquad
\frac{GMm}{R}=5k_BT.
$$
Use $R=(3M/(4\pi mn))^{1/3}$ to eliminate the radius. The <uniform-sphere pressure-work collapse criterion> is
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm crit}=\left(\frac{5k_BT}{Gm}\right)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{3}{4\pi mn}\right)^{1/2}
=\sqrt{\frac{375}{4\pi}}\frac{(k_BT)^{3/2}}{G^{3/2}m^2\sqrt n}.}
$$
The units are kilograms: $(k_BT/(Gm))^{3/2}$ has units $\mathrm{kg^{3/2}\,m^{-3/2}}$, and $\rho^{-1/2}$ supplies $\mathrm{kg^{-1/2}\,m^{3/2}}$. At fixed initial $n$ and $T$, $M>M_{\rm crit}$ makes gravity stronger than the pressure-work requirement in this model, allowing contraction.

This is a <Jeans mass> estimate for homologous contraction of an initially uniform sphere. It is not an exact stability limit for a hydrostatic gas sphere: uniform <mass density> and uniform <temperature> would give uniform <pressure>, rather than the gradient required by <hydrostatic equilibrium>. The numerical coefficient therefore differs from conventions based on a <Jeans instability> wavelength or a <pressure-confined isothermal sphere>. Subsequent contraction also depends on heat exchange; <isothermal pressure support during gravitational collapse> weakens relative to gravity as the radius decreases.

= Solution
{parent=2}

The exponential factor removes the first spatial derivative. Apply the <chain rule> explicitly:
$$
\partial_z\Delta\rho=e^{-z/(2H)}\left(\Psi_z-\frac{\Psi}{2H}\right),\qquad
\partial_z^2\Delta\rho=e^{-z/(2H)}\left(\Psi_{zz}-\frac{\Psi_z}{H}+\frac{\Psi}{4H^2}\right),
$$
while $\partial_t^2\Delta\rho=e^{-z/(2H)}\Psi_{tt}$. Substituting into the supplied density equation, the terms proportional to $\Psi_z$ cancel, leaving
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{tt}-c^2\Psi_{zz}+\frac{c^2}{4H^2}\Psi=0,
\qquad \omega_0=\frac{c}{2H}.}
$$
This is the <atmospheric acoustic cutoff transformation>. There is a sign error in the target equation in the original PDF, as well as in the transcription: the last term must be $+\omega_0^2\Psi$ for real positive $\omega_0$. If the displayed minus sign is retained literally, coefficient matching would require $\omega_0^2=-c^2/(4H^2)$, so it would not define a real characteristic oscillation period.

For the corrected equation, a <plane wave> $\Psi\propto e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$ has <dispersion relation>
$$
\omega^2=c^2k^2+\omega_0^2.
$$
Thus $\omega_0$ is the <acoustic cutoff frequency>: vertically propagating <acoustic waves> have $|\omega|\ge\omega_0$. The <scale height> and <sound speed> have units metres and metres per second, respectively, so $\omega_0$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

For an order-of-magnitude estimate in the lower <terrestrial atmosphere>, take $H\simeq8.0\ \mathrm{km}$ and $c\simeq340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. These are representative atmospheric values, not additional data specified by the paper. They give
$$
\omega_0\simeq2.1\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}=\frac{4\pi H}{c}\simeq3.0\times10^2\ \mathrm s\simeq5\ \mathrm{min}.}
$$
The <isothermal atmosphere> approximation is local: a real atmospheric <temperature> profile and the thermodynamic response of the disturbance modify the precise cutoff and period.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For an inverted <free surface>, <gravity> promotes a displacement instead of restoring it. The <surface tension> term still resists curvature. Hence the <gravity-capillary wave> relation becomes, with $q=|k|$,
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=q\left(\frac{\sigma q^2}{\rho}-g\right).}
$$
For $0<q<k_c$, where $k_c=\sqrt{\rho g/\sigma}$, $\omega^2<0$. Writing the displacement as $\eta\propto e^{st+ikx}$ gives the <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> growth rate
$$
s=\sqrt{gq-\frac{\sigma q^3}{\rho}},\qquad 0<q<k_c.
$$
Shorter <wavelengths>, $q>k_c$, remain oscillatory because <surface tension> dominates. The marginal scale is determined by the <capillary length>,
$$
\ell_c=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho g}}
=\sqrt{\frac{7.2\times10^{-2}}{1000(9.81)}}\ \mathrm m
\simeq2.71\ \mathrm{mm},
\qquad
\boxed{\lambda_c=2\pi\ell_c\simeq1.70\ \mathrm{cm}.}
$$
A sufficiently small aperture excludes the long unstable modes: this is <finite-aperture stabilization of an inverted liquid surface>. The estimate requested is therefore **a container opening of order one centimetre**, with $1.7\ \mathrm{cm}$ the marginal full wavelength rather than a geometry-independent maximum diameter.

The exact largest opening cannot be fixed from the stated infinite-surface <dispersion relation> alone. It requires the aperture shape and contact-line <boundary conditions>. For example, a one-dimensional span of width $L$ whose <contact line> is pinned at both ends has a half-sine lowest mode with $q_{\min}=\pi/L$. In that simple model,
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm crit}=\pi\ell_c\simeq8.5\ \mathrm{mm},\qquad
L<L_{\rm crit}\ \text{excludes growing modes}.}
$$
Different geometries change the numerical factor. This is a small-disturbance stability estimate with negligible air inertia and no leakage or contact-line failure; a guarantee that a real inverted container retains its water needs those additional boundary details.

= Solution
{parent=4}

**Following a fluid element.** Let $\mathbf X(t)$ be a <pathline>, satisfying $\dot{\mathbf X}=\mathbf u(\mathbf X,t)$. The <material derivative>, also called the Lagrangian time derivative, is the rate observed while following that element. The <multivariable chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\phi}{Dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\phi(\mathbf X(t),t)
=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\phi.}
$$
The <partial derivative> holds position fixed; the <advection> term accounts for motion through a spatially varying field. For a <vector field>, the same formula applies component by component in fixed Cartesian axes.

**Momentum balance.** For an <inviscid flow>, <pressure> is the only surface stress. A small material volume $dV$ has net pressure <force> $-\nabla p\,dV$, obtained by the <divergence theorem>. Its <mass> is $\rho\,dV$ and its <material acceleration> is $D\mathbf u/Dt$. Applying <Newton's second law> gives the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>,
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}
=\rho\left[\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right]
=-\nabla p.}
$$
This is the local <momentum conservation> equation when viscosity and physical <body forces> are absent. A prescribed <body force> per unit mass would add $\rho\mathbf f$ on the right.

**Changing to rotating coordinates.** In a <rotating frame> with constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$, the lab acceleration is the rotating-frame <material acceleration> plus $2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u+\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)$. Therefore
$$
\rho_0\left[\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u\right]
=-\nabla p-\rho_0\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
For uniform rotation,
$$
\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)
=-\nabla\!\left(\frac12|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2\right).
$$
With constant $\rho_0$, this gives the <Euler equation in a uniformly rotating frame>,
$$
\boxed{\rho_0\left[\partial_t\mathbf u+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u
+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right]
=-\nabla\!\left[p-\frac{\rho_0}{2}|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2\right].}
$$
The term subtracted from $p$ is the equilibrium centrifugal contribution. Dividing the bracket by $\rho_0$ defines a reduced pressure $\Pi=p/\rho_0-|\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r|^2/2$, with units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$. The <Coriolis acceleration> remains a velocity-dependent term.

**Linear disturbances.** Linearize about rest in the rotating coordinates, with the equilibrium part of $\Pi$ removed. The advective acceleration is quadratic in disturbance amplitude, so the <linearized Euler equations> and <incompressible flow> condition are
$$
\partial_t\mathbf u+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u=-\nabla\Pi',\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Taking the <curl> removes the pressure <gradient>. The identity
$$
\nabla\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf u)
=\boldsymbol\Omega(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)-(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=-(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
$$
gives the <inertial-wave vorticity equation>,
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\nabla\times\mathbf u)-2(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=0,
\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.}
$$

**Plane-wave frequencies.** Let $\mathbf k\ne0$ and use the given <plane wave> convention. <Incompressible flow> requires $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_0=0$, and the <vorticity> equation gives
$$
\omega\,\mathbf k\times\mathbf u_0
=2i(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\mathbf k)\mathbf u_0.
$$
For a nonzero frequency, apply $\mathbf k\times$ again, using $\mathbf k\times(\mathbf k\times\mathbf u_0)=-k^2\mathbf u_0$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{4(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\mathbf k)^2}{k^2}
=4\Omega^2\frac{k_z^2}{k^2},\qquad \omega=\pm2\Omega\frac{k_z}{k}.}
$$
This is the <inertial-wave dispersion relation>. If $\theta$ is the angle of the <wavevector> to the rotation axis, the positive <angular frequency> is $2|\Omega\cos\theta|$. It is largest, $2|\Omega|$, for a parallel <wavevector>, decreases to zero for a perpendicular one, and is independent of the magnitude of the <wavenumber> at fixed direction. The two signs describe the two oscillatory polarization branches. When $k_z=0$, the zero-frequency result is obtained directly from the linear equations rather than by dividing by $\omega$; it describes the nonoscillating balanced limit.

= Solution
{parent=5}

**The local conservation laws.** The first equation is the radial <Euler equation>: the <material acceleration> $Du/Dt=\partial_tu+u\partial_ru$ is caused by the <pressure gradient> force per unit mass, $-\partial_rp/\rho$. It neglects gravity and viscosity. The second equation is <mass conservation> in spherical coordinates,
$$
\partial_t\rho+\frac1{r^2}\partial_r(r^2\rho u)=0.
$$
The term $2\rho u/r$ appears because the area of a spherical surface grows as $r^2$.

To obtain the third equation, let $e=p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ be the <specific internal energy> of an <ideal gas> with constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma$. The <first law of thermodynamics> for a smooth adiabatically moving element is
$$
\frac{De}{Dt}+p\frac{D(1/\rho)}{Dt}=0,
\qquad
\frac{De}{Dt}=\frac p{\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}.
$$
Differentiating $e$ and collecting terms gives
$$
\frac{1}{(\gamma-1)\rho}\frac{Dp}{Dt}
-\frac{p}{(\gamma-1)\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt}
=\frac p{\rho^2}\frac{D\rho}{Dt},
$$
and hence
$$
\frac{Dp}{Dt}=\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}\frac{D\rho}{Dt},\qquad
\boxed{\left(\partial_t+u\partial_r\right)(p\rho^{-\gamma})=0.}
$$
Thus the entropy parameter $p/\rho^\gamma$ is conserved along each smooth <pathline>; for fixed composition, <specific entropy> differs from $c_v\ln(p/\rho^\gamma)$ only by a constant. This is local <isentropic flow> behind the shock. A physical <shock wave> itself generates <entropy> and must be treated with jump conditions, rather than by applying this smooth equation across the discontinuity.

**The front-radius scaling.** In the <self-similar blast wave> approximation there is no scale set by ambient pressure, cooling, gravity, or the initial source radius. Write $R\propto E^a\rho_0^bt^d$. Using $[E]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-2}$ and $[\rho_0]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-3}$, <dimensional analysis> gives
$$
a+b=0,\qquad 2a-3b=1,\qquad -2a+d=0.
$$
Thus $a=1/5$, $b=-1/5$, $d=2/5$ and
$$
\boxed{R=\xi_0\left(\frac E{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}t^{2/5}.}
$$
The dimensionless coefficient $\xi_0$ depends on $\gamma$ and the resolved <similarity profiles>; dimensions alone do not fix it. Define the length scale $\ell(t)=(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/5}$, so $\xi=r/\ell$ and the front is at the fixed coordinate $\xi=\xi_0$.

**Kinetic energy.** Substitute $\rho=\rho_0f_\rho(\xi)$ and $u=(r/t)f_u(\xi)$ into the volume integral. Since $r=\ell\xi$ and $dr=\ell\,d\xi$,
$$
E_{\rm kin}=\frac{2\pi\rho_0}{t^2}\int_0^Rr^4f_\rho f_u^2\,dr
=\frac{2\pi\rho_0\ell^5}{t^2}\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^4f_\rho f_u^2\,d\xi.
$$
But $\rho_0\ell^5/t^2=E$, so the first of the <spherical blast-wave similarity energy integrals> is
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm kin}=2\pi E\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^4f_\rho(\xi)f_u(\xi)^2\,d\xi
=\text{constant}.}
$$
The conclusion requires a fixed, integrable <similarity profile>, as in the finite-energy blast solution. Expansion increases the swept-up mass while reducing the characteristic squared speed, leaving this product constant.

**Pressure and internal energy.** The derivatives of the <similarity variable> are
$$
\left.\partial_t\xi\right|_r=-\frac{2\xi}{5t},\qquad
\left.\partial_r\xi\right|_t=\frac1\ell.
$$
Writing a prime for differentiation with respect to $\xi$, the <material acceleration> becomes
$$
\partial_tu=\frac r{t^2}\left[-f_u-\frac25\xi f_u'\right],\qquad
u\partial_ru=\frac r{t^2}\left[f_u^2+\xi f_uf_u'\right],
$$
$$
\frac{Du}{Dt}=\frac r{t^2}\left[f_u^2-f_u+\xi\left(f_u-\frac25\right)f_u'\right].
$$
The radial momentum equation therefore requires a <pressure gradient> on the scale $\rho_0\ell/t^2$. Integrating over radius gives <pressure similarity in a spherical blast wave>:
$$
\boxed{p(r,t)=\rho_0\frac{\ell^2}{t^2}f_p(\xi)
=E^{2/5}\rho_0^{3/5}t^{-6/5}f_p(\xi),}
$$
where
$$
\boxed{f_p'=-\xi f_\rho\left[f_u^2-f_u+\xi\left(f_u-\frac25\right)f_u'\right].}
$$
The prefactor has units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, as required for <pressure>. Strictly, the momentum equation alone permits an additional pressure uniform in space and arbitrary in time. In the scale-free strong-blast solution, shock matching and the remaining thermodynamic equation fix that freedom with the same similarity scaling; any allowed constant of integration is included in $f_p$. An independently imposed external pressure would introduce another scale and invalidate the simple dimensional ansatz.

For constant $\gamma>1$, the <internal energy> is consequently
$$
E_{\rm int}=\frac{4\pi}{\gamma-1}\frac{\rho_0\ell^5}{t^2}
\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^2f_p(\xi)\,d\xi,
$$
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm int}=\frac{4\pi E}{\gamma-1}\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^2f_p(\xi)\,d\xi
=\text{constant}.}
$$
If $E$ is the total excess explosion <energy>, its normalization is
$$
1=2\pi\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^4f_\rho f_u^2\,d\xi
+\frac{4\pi}{\gamma-1}\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^2f_p\,d\xi.
$$
The two constant fractions need not be equal. No explicit solution for $f_p$ is needed to establish their time independence.

= Solution
{parent=6}

**Hydrostatic equilibrium with self-gravity.** A resting <fluid> obeys $\nabla p=-\rho\nabla\Psi$. Spherical symmetry and the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> give
$$
\frac{dp}{dr}=-\rho\frac{d\Psi}{dr},\qquad
\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\Psi}{dr}\right)=4\pi G\rho.
$$
Eliminate the <Newtonian gravitational potential> derivative to obtain
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^2}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dr}\right)
=-4\pi Gr^2\rho.}
$$
Equivalently, the <shell theorem> gives $\Psi'=GM(r)/r^2$ and $M'(r)=4\pi r^2\rho$, which leads to the same <hydrostatic equilibrium> equation.

**A uniform-density liquid.** With constant $\rho$, $M(r)=4\pi\rho r^3/3$. There is no central point mass, so the interior <gravitational acceleration> magnitude is $4\pi G\rho r/3$. Thus
$$
\frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{4\pi G\rho^2}{3}r.
$$
Use $p(R)=0$ at a vacuum boundary, neglecting surface tension. The <uniform-density planetary pressure profile> is
$$
\boxed{p(r)=\frac{2\pi G\rho^2}{3}(R^2-r^2),\quad 0\le r\le R.}
$$
The central value can also be written
$$
\boxed{p_c=\frac{2\pi G\rho^2R^2}{3}=\frac{3GM^2}{8\pi R^4}.}
$$
It has units pascals and decreases smoothly from its maximum at the centre to zero at the surface.

**An isothermal gas.** Here $c$ is the constant <isothermal sound speed>, with $p=c^2\rho$. Try $p=A/r^2$, so $\rho=A/(c^2r^2)$. The two sides of the equilibrium equation become
$$
\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^2}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dr}\right)
=\frac{d}{dr}(-2c^2r)=-2c^2,
\qquad -4\pi Gr^2\rho=-\frac{4\pi GA}{c^2}.
$$
Thus $A=c^4/(2\pi G)$, giving the <singular isothermal sphere>,
$$
\boxed{p(r)=\frac{c^4}{2\pi Gr^2},\qquad
\rho(r)=\frac{c^2}{2\pi Gr^2},\qquad
M(r)=\frac{2c^2r}{G}.}
$$
This does **not describe a finite gaseous sphere in vacuum**. Both <pressure> and <mass density> diverge at the centre, and $p(R)>0$ for every finite outer radius $R$, so it cannot meet $p=0$ there. Extending it to infinity removes the finite-radius pressure jump but makes the total mass infinite. It is useful as an idealized profile over an intermediate radial range, or as a truncated model with nonzero external pressure. The <vacuum boundary obstruction for an isothermal sphere> also applies to the regular, nonsingular isothermal solution; removing the central singularity does not produce a finite vacuum surface.

**Steady outflow.** In the wind calculation, take the central mass $M$ to be fixed and neglect the wind's own <self-gravity>. Constant <mass flow rate> and the <isothermal equation of state> give
$$
4\pi r^2\rho u=\dot M,\qquad
\frac1\rho\frac{d\rho}{dr}=-\frac2r-\frac1u\frac{du}{dr},\qquad p=c^2\rho.
$$
The steady radial <Euler equation> is
$$
u\frac{du}{dr}=-c^2\frac1\rho\frac{d\rho}{dr}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
Substituting the density derivative gives
$$
\boxed{\left(u-\frac{c^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}
=\frac{2c^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.}
$$
This is the <isothermal Parker wind> equation. A smooth <sonic point> requires both sides to vanish when $u=c$, so
$$
\boxed{r_s=\frac{GM}{2c^2},\qquad u(r_s)=c.}
$$
Maintaining constant <temperature> during expansion generally requires heat input; it is an isothermal model, not the adiabatic blast-wave model of Question 5.

**The escaping branch at large radius.** Integrate the wind equation, using dimensionless logarithm arguments:
$$
\frac{u^2}{2}-c^2\ln\frac uc
=2c^2\ln\frac r{r_s}+\frac{GM}{r}+C_E.
$$
Set $x=r/r_s$ and $w=u^2/c^2$. Then
$$
w-\ln w=4\ln x+\frac4x+C_0.
$$
For the accelerating <transonic branch>, $w(1)=1$, which fixes $C_0=-3$. At $x\gg1$ on its supersonic branch, $w\to\infty$ and $\ln w$ is smaller than $w$. Consequently
$$
w\sim4\ln x,\qquad
\boxed{u(r)\sim2c\sqrt{\ln(r/r_s)}.}
$$
A first correction makes the ordering explicit:
$$
w=4\ln x+\ln(4\ln x)-3+o(1).
$$
This is the <large-radius asymptotics of an isothermal Parker wind>. The leading expression also holds for other supersonic branches with finite $C_0$.

The source implicitly selects an escaping wind. Positive steady outward flow alone does not determine that branch: a <subsonic isothermal breeze> also satisfies the differential equation. Its $w\ll1$ limit gives $w\sim e^{-C_0}x^{-4}$, hence $u\propto r^{-2}$ and $\rho\to\rho_\infty>0$. Such a breeze needs nonzero pressure at infinity. Selecting a regular outflow from a subsonic base into vanishing ambient pressure instead gives the accelerating transonic wind used above.
