= Solution
{parent=1}

The first argument mixes a material <fluid element> with a fixed spatial volume. For a <material volume element> following the fluid, its volume is generally time dependent:
$$
\frac D{Dt}\delta V=(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\delta V.
$$
Therefore the <material derivative> cannot be taken outside the product with $\delta V$ as in the proposed cancellation. Instead, <mass conservation> states
$$
\frac D{Dt}(\rho\delta V)=0,\qquad
\frac{D\rho}{Dt}=-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u.
$$
The <momentum balance for a material fluid element>, using <Newton's second law>, is consequently
$$
\frac D{Dt}(\rho\delta V\,\mathbf u)
=(\rho\delta V)\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}
=-\nabla p\,\delta V,
\qquad
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla p.}
$$
For a fixed <control volume>, fluid crosses its boundary and carries <momentum>; a <momentum flux> term must be included. Its local conservative form is $\partial_t(\rho\mathbf u)+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u)=-\nabla p$, equivalent to the displayed <Euler equations> after using the <continuity equation>. The erroneous equation omits the term $\rho\mathbf u\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$ needed to convert a material derivative of momentum density into this conservative form.

For the second task, take a homogeneous fluid with constant <mass density> $\rho$, prescribed gravitational <acceleration> $\mathbf g$, <incompressible flow> and <inviscid flow>. Its equations are
$$
\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla\left(\frac p\rho\right)+\mathbf g,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Under the replacement $\rho\mapsto\widetilde\rho=C\rho$, the same <velocity> solves these equations if $p\mapsto\widetilde p=Cp$, up to a spatially constant pressure offset. This is <density scaling invariance of incompressible Euler flow>:
$$
\boxed{\text{The velocity field alone cannot determine the uniform fluid density.}}
$$
The comparison assumes the same geometry and compatible velocity or gravitational <free surface> conditions; an independently specified nonuniform boundary <pressure> would supply additional information. The <gravitational field> is taken as prescribed. If the fluid's own <self-gravity> must be included, its field changes with <mass density>, so the invariance need not hold. A single freely moving particle's density-independent gravitational acceleration does not justify neglecting that distinction.

= Solution

A highly conducting <solar wind> carries the <Sun>'s <magnetic field> outward by <magnetic flux freezing>. This can open <magnetic field lines> that would form closed loops in a static dipole model. Beyond an opening radius $r_o$, approximate the field as radial. Constancy of the <magnetic flux> in a fixed solid-angle <flux tube> gives the <radial solar-wind magnetic flux> law
$$
B_r(r)r^2\simeq\text{constant},\qquad B_r\propto r^{-2},
$$
which decays more slowly than the vacuum <magnetic dipole field>, proportional to $r^{-3}$.

For example, if the field becomes radial already near the solar radius $R_\odot$, normalizing both estimates to the same field there gives
$$
\frac{B_{\rm wind}(r_E)}{B_{\rm dip}(r_E)}\sim\frac{r_E}{R_\odot}
=\frac1{0.005}=200.
$$
Thus a dipole estimate $10^{-11}\ \mathrm T$ becomes of order $2\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm T$, comparable to the stated hundredfold enhancement. More generally, matching dipolar decay inside $r_o$ to radial decay outside gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{B_{\rm wind}(r_E)}{B_{\rm dip}(r_E)}\sim\frac{r_E}{r_o};
\quad r_o\sim2R_\odot\ \Rightarrow\ B(r_E)\sim10^{-9}\ \mathrm T.}
$$
This is a plausible scaling explanation, not a determination of the actual opening radius. The magnetic field need not be outward everywhere: opposite-polarity sectors can preserve zero net <magnetic flux> through a closed <surface>, consistent with <Gauss's law for magnetism>. The argument concerns the changed geometry of the field in a flowing <plasma>, rather than changing the dipole exponent by assumption.

= Solution

Take the outward <wavenumber> $k>0$; the absolute value in the source makes the two propagation directions equivalent. The <phase velocity> of the <gravity-capillary wave> obeys
$$
c_p^2=\frac{\omega^2}{k^2}=\frac gk+\frac\sigma\rho k.
$$
Differentiate with respect to $k$:
$$
\frac{d(c_p^2)}{dk}=-\frac g{k^2}+\frac\sigma\rho=0
\quad\Rightarrow\quad k_*=\sqrt{\frac{\rho g}{\sigma}}.
$$
The second derivative is positive, and $c_p\to\infty$ at both ends of the positive-$k$ range, so this is the global minimum. The <minimum phase speed of a gravity-capillary wave> is
$$
\boxed{c_{p,\min}=\left(\frac{4g\sigma}{\rho}\right)^{1/4}.}
$$
For $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and the supplied <surface tension> and <mass density>,
$$
\boxed{c_{p,\min}=0.231\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding <wavenumber> is $k_*\simeq369\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, giving <wavelength> $\lambda_*=2\pi/k_*\simeq1.70\ \mathrm{cm}$. At this minimum, $c_g=d\omega/dk=c_p+k\,dc_p/dk=c_p$. Thus the <group velocity> and <phase velocity> agree for this particular ripple, although they differ for general <gravity-capillary waves>.

= Solution

A <shock wave> forms when a compressive flow steepens or when gas must be decelerated or turned faster than upstream <acoustic waves> can communicate the change. Astrophysical examples include a <supernova remnant> sweeping up surrounding gas, a fast <stellar wind> colliding with ambient material, and supersonic accretion onto an obstacle or onto a dense gas layer. A large <Mach number> by itself is not sufficient: a uniform supersonic stream need not contain a <shock wave>.

Choose the <shock frame>, and denote upstream and downstream normal <velocities> by $u_1,u_2$. Integrating the local <conservation laws> over a thin control volume spanning a steady <normal shock wave> gives equal fluxes on both sides. <Mass conservation> gives constant mass flux per unit area,
$$
\boxed{j=\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2.}
$$
<Pressure> and advected <momentum> together give the normal <momentum flux>,
$$
\boxed{p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2.}
$$
For a thin non-radiating layer, with negligible gravitational-potential change across it, <conservation of energy> gives equal fluxes $j(h+u^2/2)$. A perfect gas has <specific enthalpy> $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac\gamma{\gamma-1}\frac{p_1}{\rho_1}+\frac12u_1^2
=\frac\gamma{\gamma-1}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2}+\frac12u_2^2.}
$$
These are the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas>. Dissipation inside the thin layer produces an <entropy> increase and selects the physical compressive branch. Efficient radiative cooling would change the energy relation and may instead lead to an <isothermal shock>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $p_1\ll\rho_1u_1^2$. Let $q=u_2/u_1$. <Mass conservation> gives $\rho_2=\rho_1/q$, and normal <momentum conservation> gives
$$
p_2\simeq\rho_1u_1^2(1-q),\qquad
\frac{p_2}{\rho_2}=q(1-q)u_1^2.
$$
Insert this into the energy condition from part (a):
$$
\frac\gamma{\gamma-1}q(1-q)+\frac12q^2=\frac12.
$$
Rearranging and factoring,
$$
(\gamma+1)q^2-2\gamma q+(\gamma-1)
=(q-1)[(\gamma+1)q-(\gamma-1)]=0.
$$
The root $q=1$ is the unshocked state. The compressive <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> instead give
$$
\boxed{u_2=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}u_1.}
$$
Correspondingly, $\rho_2/\rho_1=(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$ and $p_2=2\rho_1u_1^2/(\gamma+1)$. These relations require $\gamma>1$ and negligible upstream thermal <pressure>.

= Solution

Let $U_1,U_2$ denote the total flow speeds, keeping the normal components distinct from them. The original diagram measures $\beta$ from the initial wall direction to the <shock wave> itself. The outgoing <velocity> is turned by $\theta$, so it makes angle $\beta-\theta$ with the shock.

Resolve each <velocity> tangent and normal to the <oblique shock>. Tangential <momentum conservation>, with nonzero mass flux and no tangential surface force, makes the tangential <velocity> continuous:
$$
U_1\cos\beta=U_2\cos(\beta-\theta).
$$
The normal flow obeys the strong <normal shock wave> result from part (b):
$$
U_2\sin(\beta-\theta)=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}U_1\sin\beta.
$$
Divide the second relation by the first to obtain
$$
\boxed{\tan\beta=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\tan(\beta-\theta).}
$$
The approximation is controlled by the upstream normal <Mach number> $M\sin\beta\gg1$, not just by $M\gg1$. This distinction matters on the small-angle weak branch in part (d). The angle convention is also important: formulas using the angle to the shock normal instead would interchange sine and cosine.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Rewrite the relation from part (c) as
$$
(\gamma-1)\sin\beta\cos(\beta-\theta)
=(\gamma+1)\cos\beta\sin(\beta-\theta).
$$
The angle-addition identities reduce this to
$$
\sin(2\beta-\theta)=\gamma\sin\theta.
$$
For $0<\theta<\arcsin(1/\gamma)$, the right side lies strictly between zero and one. There are two physically admissible <strong oblique-shock angle branches>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\beta_{\rm weak}&=\frac12\left[\theta+\arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta)\right],\\
\beta_{\rm strong}&=\frac12\left[\theta+\pi-\arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta)\right].
\end{aligned}}
$$
Both lie between $\theta$ and $\pi/2$. They merge when $\gamma\sin\theta=1$, proving the <maximum deflection through a strong perfect-gas shock>,
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\max}=\arcsin(1/\gamma),\qquad
\beta_* =\frac\pi4+\frac{\theta_{\max}}2.}
$$
For small positive $\theta$, the formal strong-shock-limit asymptotics are
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm weak}\sim\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\theta,\qquad
\beta_{\rm strong}\sim\frac\pi2-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\theta.}
$$
The weak branch is normally observed for an unconfined supersonic stream turning through a modest compression corner. It has a smaller normal component of upstream <velocity>, hence a smaller downstream <pressure> increase; for small turns the downstream flow remains supersonic. The nearly normal strong branch can be selected by suitable downstream <pressure> conditions, so it is not mathematically forbidden.

The two limits must be ordered honestly. At finite $M$, the weak <oblique shock> approaches the <Mach angle> $\arcsin(1/M)$ as $\theta\to0$, rather than zero. The displayed weak-branch asymptotic applies when the large-$M$ limit is taken first, or in the overlapping range $1/M\ll\theta\ll1$, where $M\sin\beta\gg1$ remains valid.

Let $M(r)$ be the enclosed mass. For <spherical symmetry>, <hydrostatic equilibrium> and <mass conservation> give
$$
\frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{GM(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},\qquad
\frac{dM}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho.
$$
The isothermal <equation of state> $p=K\rho$ therefore implies
$$
K\frac{d\ln\rho}{dr}=-\frac{GM(r)}{r^2}.
$$
Multiply by $r^2$, differentiate and eliminate $M$:
$$
\boxed{\frac1{r^2}\frac d{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\ln\rho}{dr}\right)
=-\frac{4\pi G}{K}\rho,}
$$
or equivalently $r^{-2}d[r^2\rho^{-1}\rho']/dr=-4\pi G\rho/K$. This is the density equation for an <isothermal sphere>. Here $K$ has units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$ and equals the squared <isothermal sound speed>.

A regular <isothermal sphere> does not reach zero density at a finite radius. The finite body discussed later must therefore be truncated by a nonzero external <pressure>, as in a <pressure-confined isothermal sphere>. A finite vacuum boundary would not be compatible with this <equation of state>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $\rho=\rho_c e^{-\Psi(\xi)}$ and $r=a\xi$. Then $d\ln\rho/dr=-a^{-1}\Psi'$ and the equation from part (a) becomes
$$
-\frac1{a^2\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}(\xi^2\Psi')
=-\frac{4\pi G\rho_c}{K}e^{-\Psi}.
$$
With $a^2=K/(4\pi G\rho_c)$, the coefficients cancel, giving the <isothermal Lane-Emden equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}(\xi^2\Psi')=e^{-\Psi}.}
$$
The regular central <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{\Psi(0)=0,\qquad\Psi'(0)=0.}
$$
The first makes the central <mass density> equal to $\rho_c$. The second follows from smooth <spherical symmetry> and the vanishing central <gravitational acceleration>; a preferred radial slope at the origin would be singular. Substitution of a regular <power series> gives
$$
\Psi(\xi)=\frac{\xi^2}{6}-\frac{\xi^4}{120}+O(\xi^6),
$$
which supplies nonsingular starting data for numerical integration. The central conditions select the regular solution, not the <singular isothermal sphere>.

= Solution

The particle count is the enclosed mass divided by the particle mass $m$:
$$
N(\xi)=\frac{4\pi\rho_ca^3}{m}\int_0^\xi s^2e^{-\Psi(s)}\,ds.
$$
Integrating the <isothermal Lane-Emden equation> gives
$$
\int_0^\xi s^2e^{-\Psi(s)}\,ds
=\left[s^2\Psi'(s)\right]_0^\xi=\xi^2\Psi'(\xi),
$$
where regularity makes the lower endpoint vanish. Thus
$$
\boxed{N(\xi)=\frac{4\pi\rho_c}{m}
\left(\frac{K}{4\pi G\rho_c}\right)^{3/2}\xi^2\Psi'(\xi).}
$$
The prefactor is a mass divided by a mass, and the remaining factor is dimensionless. In particular, at fixed $K$ the dependence on central <mass density> is $N\propto\rho_c^{-1/2}f(\xi)$, with $f(\xi)=\xi^2\Psi'(\xi)$. Both changes must be retained when comparing equilibria at fixed $N$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Along a family of equilibria at fixed temperature, $K$ and the dimensionless function $\Psi$ are fixed. The boundary <mass density> changes through both $\rho_c$ and the boundary coordinate $\xi$:
$$
\boxed{\delta\rho=e^{-\Psi}\delta\rho_c-\rho_ce^{-\Psi}\Psi'\delta\xi
=\rho\left(\frac{\delta\rho_c}{\rho_c}-\Psi'\delta\xi\right).}
$$
From part (c), $N=C\rho_c^{-1/2}f(\xi)$, where $C$ is constant at fixed temperature and $f=\xi^2\Psi'$. Set its logarithmic variation to zero:
$$
0=\frac{\delta N}{N}=-\frac12\frac{\delta\rho_c}{\rho_c}
+\frac{f'}f\delta\xi.
$$
The <isothermal Lane-Emden equation> gives $f'=\xi^2e^{-\Psi}$, so the fixed-$N$ relation is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\delta\rho_c}{\rho_c}=\frac{2e^{-\Psi}}{\Psi'}\delta\xi.}
$$
Substitute back into the boundary-density variation:
$$
\delta\rho\big|_N
=\rho\left(\frac{2e^{-\Psi}}{\Psi'}-\Psi'\right)\delta\xi.
$$
For a nontrivial variation at a finite positive radius, its vanishing gives the <fixed-mass turning point of an isothermal sphere>:
$$
\boxed{[\Psi'(\xi)]^2=2e^{-\Psi(\xi)}.}
$$
This derivative follows a fixed-mass sequence of equilibria; it is not merely the spatial derivative of density inside one sphere. The corresponding radial variation is
$$
\frac{\delta r}{r}=\left(\frac1\xi-\frac{e^{-\Psi}}{\Psi'}\right)\delta\xi.
$$
At small $\xi$, $\Psi'\simeq\xi/3$: the boundary density increases while the radius decreases along increasing $\xi$, giving the stated negative $(\partial p/\partial V)_{N,T}$ of the stable branch. The first density turning point marks its first loss of radial <stability>; it need not be confused with a turning point of radius itself.

= Solution

Using the supplied first critical value $\xi_c\simeq6.5$, the <critical radius of a pressure-confined isothermal sphere> is
$$
\boxed{r_c=6.5\sqrt{\frac{K}{4\pi G\rho_c}}
\simeq1.83\frac{c_s}{\sqrt{G\rho_c}},\qquad c_s^2=K.}
$$
No radius in metres can be specified without $K$ and a central <mass density>. Here $c_s$ is the <isothermal sound speed>, consistent with the fixed-temperature variations in part (d). At a given central <mass density>, equilibria truncated at $\xi<\xi_c$ lie on the first stable branch, while increasing the dimensionless outer radius beyond this first threshold gives an unstable configuration. Numerical integration gives $\xi_c\simeq6.451$, consistent with the paper's rounded $6.5$.

For a transparent comparison with the given <Jeans length>, set the uniform comparison density $\rho_0=\rho_c$ and use the same <speed of sound>. Then
$$
\boxed{\frac{r_c}{\lambda_J}=\frac{6.5}{2\pi}\simeq1.03.}
$$
Both scales express the competition between <pressure> support and <self-gravity>, with characteristic length $c_s/\sqrt{G\rho}$. Their numerical closeness under this density choice does not mean that the models impose the same threshold: $r_c$ is a boundary radius, whereas $\lambda_J$ is the wavelength of a perturbation. Comparing $r_c$ with $\lambda_J/2$ would instead give a factor about $2.07$. Using the sphere's mean or boundary density instead of its central density changes the coefficient again, so the reference density must always be stated.

The <pressure-confined isothermal sphere> gives an actual finite, centrally concentrated <hydrostatic equilibrium> and an explicit confining <pressure>, making its radial stability threshold more self-consistent for a roughly spherical isothermal cloud. The uniform <Jeans instability> model gives a simpler local criterion, includes perturbations at different <wavelengths>, and does not require constructing a spherical equilibrium. Its ideal uniform static infinite background requires the <Jeans swindle> or an explicitly specified treatment of background gravity. Neither model includes rotation, <magnetic fields>, turbulent support or a realistic temperature evolution, so neither is a universal collapse criterion.

= Solution

For a steady axisymmetric flow $\mathbf u_0=r\Omega(r)\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, <incompressible flow> is automatic. The only material <acceleration> is centripetal, $-r\Omega^2\widehat{\mathbf r}$. The radial <Euler equations> therefore require
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp_0}{dr}=\rho r\Omega(r)^2.}
$$
With no <viscosity> there is no tangential shear <stress> to redistribute <angular momentum>. Any sufficiently regular $\Omega(r)$ can therefore be a steady inviscid profile, with the radial <pressure> adjusted by integrating this equation. This is a statement of equilibrium, not of stability: some such profiles are centrifugally unstable.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a homogeneous <Newtonian fluid> with constant <kinematic viscosity> $\nu>0$, assume the same steady purely azimuthal flow. In <cylindrical coordinates>, the azimuthal <Navier-Stokes equation> has no pressure forcing or convective term in this state, and becomes
$$
0=\nu\left[\frac{d^2v_\phi}{dr^2}+\frac1r\frac{dv_\phi}{dr}-\frac{v_\phi}{r^2}\right],\qquad v_\phi=r\Omega.
$$
Equivalently, $\Omega''+3\Omega'/r=0$, or $(r^3\Omega')'=0$. Two integrations give the <steady circular Couette profile>,
$$
\boxed{\Omega(r)=A+\frac B{r^2},\qquad v_\phi(r)=Ar+\frac Br.}
$$
This is the basic <Taylor-Couette flow> between rotating cylinders. The constants depend on boundary <angular velocities>; for radii $R_<,R_>$ and prescribed <angular velocities> $\Omega_<,\Omega_>$,
$$
A=\frac{\Omega_>R_>^2-\Omega_<R_<^2}{R_>^2-R_<^2},\qquad
B=\frac{R_<^2R_>^2(\Omega_<-\Omega_>)}{R_>^2-R_<^2}.
$$
If the domain includes $r=0$, regularity requires $B=0$, leaving <solid-body rotation>. On an annulus a nonzero $B$ is allowed and wall torques maintain the shear.

The restriction concerns a steady profile. An arbitrary initial <angular velocity> does not cease to exist when <viscosity> is introduced; within the purely azimuthal ansatz it evolves according to $\partial_t\Omega=\nu(\Omega''+3\Omega'/r)$ until compatible boundary conditions and angular-momentum constraints determine its long-time state.

= Solution

Expand the axisymmetric <Euler equations> about the rotating equilibrium, retaining only terms linear in $u_r,u_\phi,u_z$ and in the <pressure> perturbation. The symbol in this question is the <kinematic pressure> perturbation,
$$
\boxed{\pi(r,z,t)=\frac{p(r,z,t)-p_0(r)}\rho,\qquad[\pi]=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}.}
$$
The linearized centripetal term in the radial equation is $-2\Omega u_\phi$, after subtracting the equilibrium term $-r\Omega^2$. Radial advection of the background azimuthal <velocity>, together with the cylindrical-coordinate term $u_rv_\phi/r$, gives
$$
\left[\frac d{dr}(r\Omega)+\Omega\right]u_r
=(2\Omega+r\Omega')u_r
=\frac1r\frac d{dr}(r^2\Omega)u_r.
$$
This is the azimuthal coupling in the second equation; it also expresses conservation of <specific angular momentum> $j=r^2\Omega$ for a radially displaced element. The axial equation is acceleration balanced by $-\partial_z\pi$. Finally, the axisymmetric <incompressible flow> condition in <cylindrical coordinates> is
$$
\frac1r\partial_r(ru_r)+\partial_zu_z=0,
$$
which expands to the last supplied equation. Background azimuthal advection contributes no $\Omega\partial_\phi$ term because the perturbations are axisymmetric.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use the thin-annulus approximation stated in the source, so $u_r'+iku_z=0$. Let
$$
A(r)=2\Omega+r\Omega'=\frac1r(r^2\Omega)',\qquad
\kappa_{\rm ep}^2=2\Omega A=\frac1{r^3}(r^4\Omega^2)'.
$$
Here $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2$ is the conventional <Rayleigh discriminant>, also the squared local <radial epicyclic frequency> when positive. For $k\ne0$ and $\omega\ne0$, the azimuthal and axial mode equations give
$$
u_\phi=-\frac{iA}{\omega}u_r,\qquad u_z=\frac k\omega\pi.
$$
The thin-gap <incompressible flow> condition then gives $\pi=i\omega u_r'/k^2$. Substitute these into the radial equation:
$$
-i\omega u_r+\frac{2i\Omega A}{\omega}u_r=-\pi',\qquad
\frac{i\omega}{k^2}u_r''=\frac{i(\omega^2-\kappa_{\rm ep}^2)}\omega u_r.
$$
Thus
$$
u_r''=k^2\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{\rm ep}^2}{\omega^2}\right)u_r.
$$
To put this into exactly the form printed in the paper, define
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r)=-\kappa_{\rm ep}^2
=-\frac{2\Omega}{r}(r^2\Omega)'
=-\frac1{r^3}(r^4\Omega^2)'.}
$$
The <narrow-gap centrifugal stability equation> is consequently
$$
\boxed{-u_r''+\frac{k^2}{\omega^2}\Phi(r)u_r=-k^2u_r.}
$$
The two rigid cylinders are impermeable, so the <no-penetration boundary condition> gives
$$
\boxed{u_r(R_<)=u_r(R_>)=0.}
$$
There is no inviscid no-slip condition on the tangential or axial components.

The sign deserves emphasis: the source's printed plus sign makes its $\Phi$ the negative of the conventional <Rayleigh discriminant>. Using the conventional positive discriminant instead requires a minus sign in front of that potential term. These are equivalent conventions; replacing one without adjusting the other would falsely predict instability of <solid-body rotation>. The $k=0$ and marginal $\omega=0$ cases, excluded by these eliminations, must be treated separately or by limits.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Let $u_r$ be a nonzero admissible mode, multiply the equation of part (d) by its complex conjugate and integrate between the cylinders. The endpoint conditions remove the boundary term in <integration by parts>, giving the <Rayleigh stability integral identity>
$$
\int|u_r'|^2\,dr+\frac{k^2}{\omega^2}\int\Phi|u_r|^2\,dr
=-k^2\int|u_r|^2\,dr.
$$
Rearrange to obtain a real <Rayleigh quotient>:
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=-\frac{k^2\int\Phi(r)|u_r|^2\,dr}
{\int|u_r'|^2\,dr+k^2\int|u_r|^2\,dr}.}
$$
The denominator is strictly positive. Consequently, with the paper's convention,
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r)<0\ \text{throughout the gap guarantees oscillatory normal-mode stability}.}
$$
The weak inequality $\Phi\le0$ excludes exponentially growing axisymmetric modes, but permits marginal cases and possible algebraic growth when the restoring effect vanishes. Strict negativity avoids that degeneracy. Equivalently, the conventional <Rayleigh discriminant> is positive, or the squared <specific angular momentum> increases outward:
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dr}(r^2\Omega)^2>0.}
$$
This is the <Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion>. In the suggested <time-independent Schrödinger equation> analogy, a growing mode has $\omega^2<0$; if $\Phi\le0$, its effective potential $k^2\Phi/\omega^2$ is nonnegative. The nonnegative kinetic term and that potential cannot have the required negative eigenvalue $-k^2$, which gives the same exclusion.

As a sign check, for <solid-body rotation> $\kappa_{\rm ep}^2=4\Omega^2$ and $\Phi=-4\Omega^2$. In a gap of width $d=R_>-R_<$, the radial modes $u_r\propto\sin[n\pi(r-R_<)/d]$ have
$$
\omega_n^2=\frac{4\Omega^2k^2}{k^2+(n\pi/d)^2}>0,
$$
as expected for <inertial waves>. The argument establishes axisymmetric inviscid stability within the stated thin-gap model, rather than a claim about all perturbations of a viscous flow.
