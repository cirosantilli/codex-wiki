= Solution
{parent=a}

The equation describes small plane-wave <perturbations> of a uniform, stationary <plasma> in <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>, with propagation **parallel to the background <magnetic field>**. The nonzero field defines $\widehat{\mathbf b}=\mathbf v_A/v_A$; $c_s$ is the <sound speed> for the assumed thermodynamic closure. Neglect <viscosity>, <electrical resistivity>, <gravity> and microscopic dispersive effects. A uniform background flow would instead introduce its Doppler-shifted <frequency>.

Decompose $\mathbf u=\mathbf u_\perp+u_\parallel\widehat{\mathbf b}$. The displayed matrix equation then becomes
$$
(k^2v_A^2-\omega^2)\mathbf u_\perp=0,\qquad
(k^2c_s^2-\omega^2)u_\parallel=0.
$$
Thus there are two independent transverse <Alfvén wave> <polarizations> with $\omega^2=k^2v_A^2$, restored by <magnetic tension>, and a longitudinal acoustic mode with $\omega^2=k^2c_s^2$, restored by <pressure>. The transverse modes have no first-order <density> compression; a strictly longitudinal displacement along the field does not bend it. These are the <parallel-propagating ideal magnetohydrodynamic wave> branches. In the general oblique problem, fast and slow magnetosonic modes mix compression and magnetic response; their parallel limits coincide with the faster and slower of these speeds. If $c_s=v_A$, all three <velocity> <polarizations> share the same <frequency> magnitude. The <velocity> equation alone does not enumerate nonpropagating <entropy> <perturbations>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take positive $\dot Q$ to mean net cooling per unit <mass>. At fixed <pressure> for an <ideal gas> of unchanged composition, $\rho\propto T^{-1}$, so $\dot Q=C T^{\alpha-1}-H$ with $C>0$. At <thermal equilibrium>, $CT_0^{\alpha-1}=H$. Because expansion work is included at fixed <pressure>, the perturbed <temperature> obeys
$$
c_p\frac{d\delta T}{dt}=-\left(\frac{\partial\dot Q}{\partial T}\right)_p\delta T
=-\frac{(\alpha-1)H}{T_0}\delta T.
$$
This <isobaric stability criterion for density-dependent power-law cooling> gives stability for $\alpha>1$ and instability for $\alpha<1$. For optically thin <bremsstrahlung>, $\alpha=1/2$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\delta T}{dt}=\frac{H}{2c_pT_0}\delta T,\qquad\text{isobarically unstable}.}
$$
A slightly cooler parcel becomes denser and cools faster; a slightly hotter parcel has reduced cooling and heats further. The conclusion assumes the <sound-crossing time> is short enough to maintain nearly common <pressure>, with conduction and other stabilizing processes omitted.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Using a center-to-edge <sound-crossing time> $t_s=R/c_s$ and the pressureless uniform-sphere <free-fall time of a uniform sphere> $t_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{3\pi/(32G\rho)}$, equality gives
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm cross}=c_s\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho}}\sim\frac{c_s}{\sqrt{G\rho}}.}
$$
On scales larger than this estimate, <pressure> information cannot cross the region before its gravitational dynamical evolution. It therefore identifies the order of the <Jeans length> and separates gravity-dominated from pressure-supported behavior in the simple comparison. It is not an exact normal-mode stability calculation: with the standard Jeans <wavelength> $\lambda_J=c_s\sqrt{\pi/(G\rho)}$, this radius is $\sqrt{3/32}\lambda_J$. Using the diameter rather than the radius as the crossing distance changes the numerical coefficient. <Boundary conditions>, <density> structure, <turbulence> and magnetic support also matter for an actual cloud.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <stellar wind> removes <mass> and carries <energy> and <angular momentum> from a star into its surroundings. Hot gas can escape through thermal <pressure>; radiation transfers <momentum> through continuum or spectral-line <absorption>, and radiation acting on dust can drive surrounding gas. Magnetic stresses can extract rotational <energy> and <angular momentum>. These mechanisms have different dependencies on heating, opacity, stellar <luminosity> and field geometry.

For a steady spherical isothermal pressure-driven model, <mass> conservation gives $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u$. With $p=a^2\rho$ and constant <isothermal sound speed> $a$, the radial <Euler equation> becomes the <Parker wind equation>
$$
\left(u-\frac{a^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}=\frac{2a^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
A smooth <transonic flow> solution crosses $u=a$ at $r_s=GM/(2a^2)$. Its inner subsonic atmosphere connects to a supersonic outflow; continuing heat input is needed to keep that expanding wind isothermal. Other boundary <pressures> can select a breeze rather than this escaping branch, and the mass-loss rate needs boundary/heating information. A polytropic outflow instead converts finite <enthalpy> into escape <energy> and has a different terminal budget.

\Image[paper-1-pressure-driven-wind.png]
{title=Pressure-driven isothermal wind: a regular sonic crossing selects the transonic branch}

The <solar wind> supplies a nearby <plasma> example. Spectral-line <Doppler shifts> and profiles diagnose more distant winds; blueshifted <absorption> against stellar light indicates material moving toward the observer. Inference of a mass-loss rate additionally needs <density>, <ionization>, geometry and <velocity> information, rather than a line shift alone.

= Solution

The <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> occurs when a denser fluid is supported against an effective <acceleration> by a lighter fluid. Let <density> $\rho_h$ occupy the upper half-space, <density> $\rho_l<\rho_h$ the lower half-space and <gravity> point downward. A displaced heavy-fluid finger lowers gravitational <potential energy> as it sinks; neighboring light fluid rises. For incompressible inviscid half-spaces without <surface tension>, a disturbance $\eta\propto e^{\sigma t+ikx}$ has
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2=gk\frac{\rho_h-\rho_l}{\rho_h+\rho_l}.}
$$
The <density> ratio in this expression is the <Atwood number>. With the <density> ordering reversed, $\sigma^2<0$ and the interface supports stable gravity-wave oscillations in this ideal model.

\Image[paper-1-rayleigh-taylor-interface.png]
{title=Rayleigh-Taylor growth: a heavy layer above a light layer develops growing fingers}

<Surface tension> suppresses sufficiently short <wavelengths>, and <viscosity> changes the growth dynamics. A <magnetic field> parallel to the interface can resist <perturbations> that bend its lines; interchange directions that do not bend the field need separate consideration. In astrophysics, effective deceleration can make dense ejecta or a swept-up shell supported by a lower-density hot interior unstable. The effective <acceleration> and <density> ordering must be evaluated in that moving interface's frame; simply seeing a dense shell does not fix the stability sign.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a steady inviscid <barotropic fluid> in a conservative <gravitational potential>, define $h(\rho)=\int dp/\rho$. Dotting the <Euler equation> with the <velocity> gives
$$
\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{u^2}{2}+h+\phi\right)=0.
$$
The <Bernoulli function> is therefore constant along a <streamline>. It balances <kinetic energy>, <pressure> work or <enthalpy>, and gravitational <potential energy> per unit <mass>. Different <streamlines> can have different constants; for an <irrotational flow>, the identity $\nabla H=\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$ makes the constant common throughout a connected region.

For an incompressible fluid, $h=p/\rho$ up to a constant, giving $u^2/2+p/\rho+\phi=C$. For a constant-entropy perfect-gas <streamline>, $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$. Together with <mass> conservation, this explains conversion of <enthalpy> into <kinetic energy> in a nozzle and the need to include <gravity> in an escaping wind. Viscous losses, heating and <entropy> production require their corresponding <energy> balance. Across an adiabatic shock the total <stagnation enthalpy> can still be conserved, but the upstream isentropic pressure-density law cannot simply be continued through the <entropy> jump.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Interstellar shocks arise when relative flow is supersonic: stellar winds, <supernova> ejecta, colliding clouds and gas moving through strong disturbances can drive them. Neighboring compressive <characteristics> steepen or a fast flow encounters material it cannot decelerate through upstream sound communication. A thin dissipative front then converts directed <kinetic energy> into thermal <energy> and increases <entropy>.

Integrate <mass>, normal <momentum> and total <energy> conservation through a planar steady shock in its rest frame. With no <mass> source, negligible external impulse across the thin layer and no radiative <energy> loss, their normal fluxes are respectively
$$
j=\rho u,\qquad p+\rho u^2,\qquad
j\left(h+\frac{u^2}{2}\right).
$$
For a <perfect gas> of constant <adiabatic index>, $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$. Equality of the fluxes on the two sides yields the three <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> in the question. Adiabatic means no <energy> escapes this control <volume>; it does not make the shock isentropic.

In the strong-shock limit, let $r=\rho_2/\rho_1=u_1/u_2$ and neglect $p_1$. <Momentum> conservation gives $p_2=\rho_1u_1^2(1-1/r)$. Substitution into <energy> conservation gives
$$
\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}(r-1)+1=r^2,
\qquad
(r-1)\left(r-\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\right)=0.
$$
The nontrivial compressive root is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{u_1}{u_2}=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}.}
$$
The $r=1$ root describes no shock. A <monatomic gas> has the familiar compression four.

For identical colliding clouds in their center-of-momentum frame, <reflection symmetry> fixes the contact plane and the uniform compressed material on either side at rest. The unshocked clouds still approach at speeds $\pm V$; their supersonic upstream flow has not received a smooth <pressure> adjustment ahead of the shock. Let the right shock move to the right at speed $S$. In its own frame the upstream and downstream normal speed magnitudes are $V+S$ and $S$. The same <shock compression ratio> therefore gives
$$
\frac{V+S}{S}=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1},\qquad
\boxed{S=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}V.}
$$
This is the <shock speed in a symmetric cloud collision>. For $\gamma=5/3$, the shocks move outward at $V/3$, not at the upstream speed or the relative cloud speed $2V$. As a check, the post-shock <pressure> is $p_2=(\gamma+1)\rho_1V^2/2$; in the monatomic case $p_2/\rho_2=V^2/3$, giving specific internal <energy> $V^2/2$, exactly the initial bulk <kinetic energy> per unit cloud <mass> when brought to rest.

\Image[paper-1-symmetric-cloud-shocks.png]
{title=Symmetric cloud collision: stationary compressed gas and two outward-moving shocks}

When a shock reaches a cloud's rear boundary, its supply of cold upstream gas ends. If the surrounding <pressure> is low, the hot compressed gas expands outward and a rarefaction travels back into it, converting thermal <energy> into motion and cooling the gas. A sustained uniform stationary shocked region is no longer the solution. Interaction with an appreciable exterior medium can instead drive another outward disturbance; the boundary <pressure> determines that part of the evolution. The stated adiabatic model provides no radiative mechanism that would keep a permanently cold dense sheet at the contact.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Follow a fluid element with position $\mathbf X(t)$ satisfying $\dot{\mathbf X}=\mathbf u(\mathbf X,t)$. The <chain rule> gives the <material derivative>
$$
\boxed{\frac{DQ}{Dt}=\frac d{dt}Q(\mathbf X(t),t)=\partial_tQ+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla Q.}
$$
Apply <Newton's second law> to a material element of <volume> $dV$ and <mass> $\rho dV$. The net <pressure> <force> is $-\nabla p\,dV$, and <gravity> gives $-\rho\nabla\phi\,dV$. Dividing by the element <mass>, neglecting <viscosity> and using the <chain rule> componentwise gives the <Euler equation>
$$
\partial_t\mathbf u+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u=-\frac1\rho\nabla p-\nabla\phi.
$$

For a static spherical isothermal gas, $p=c_s^2\rho$ and $dp/dr=-GM(r)\rho/r^2$. Trial <density> $\rho=A/r^2$ gives enclosed <mass> $M(r)=4\pi Ar$, so
$$
-\frac{2Ac_s^2}{r^3}=-\frac{4\pi GA^2}{r^3},\qquad
\boxed{A=\frac{c_s^2}{2\pi G}.}
$$
This is the <singular isothermal sphere>. Its central <density> diverges, although the enclosed <mass> tends to zero linearly and its total <mass> inside a finite radius is finite. It is not the finite-central-density regular isothermal solution.

Truncate at $r_0$ with boundary <pressure> $p_0$. Then $M=4\pi Ar_0=2c_s^2r_0/G$ and $p_0=c_s^2\rho(r_0)$, giving
$$
\boxed{r_0=\frac{GM}{2c_s^2},\qquad p_0=\frac{c_s^4}{2\pi G r_0^2}.}
$$
The external medium is being treated as a prescribed <pressure> boundary; its own gravitational field is excluded from the cloud self-energy calculation.

For a monatomic <perfect gas>, the equilibrium is isothermal but the internal-energy relation still uses its <adiabatic index> $\gamma=5/3$:
$$
U=\int\frac{p}{\gamma-1}\,dV=\frac32Mc_s^2.
$$
The gravitational self-energy follows by assembling shells:
$$
\Omega=-\int_0^{r_0}\frac{GM(r)}r\,dM(r)
=-16\pi^2GA^2r_0=-\frac{GM^2}{r_0}=-2Mc_s^2.
$$
Both <integrals> remain finite at the singular center. Consequently
$$
\boxed{2U+\Omega=Mc_s^2=4\pi r_0^3p_0=3p_0\mathcal V,\qquad\mathcal V=4\pi r_0^3/3.}
$$
The <pressure-confined singular isothermal gas sphere> therefore satisfies the <virial theorem> including the work of the confining surface <pressure>. One can see its origin directly by integrating $\mathbf r\cdot\nabla p$: <integration by parts> gives $\oint p\,\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf S-3\int p\,dV$, whose surface term is $4\pi r_0^3p_0$. An isolated vacuum-boundary version would omit that term and could not truncate this same positive-temperature isothermal profile at finite radius. The equality expresses equilibrium <force> and <energy> balance, rather than proving <dynamical stability>.
