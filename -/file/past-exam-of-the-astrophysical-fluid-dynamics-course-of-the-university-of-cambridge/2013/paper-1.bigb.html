= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $x$ measure distance down the incline and $y$ distance perpendicular to it, with the wall at $y=0$ and the <free surface> at $y=h$. A uniform steady <falling film flow> has $\mathbf u=u(y)\mathbf e_x$. Its <convective acceleration> vanishes because $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla=u\partial_x$ and $\partial_xu=0$. A flat surface exposed to constant atmospheric <pressure> has
$$
p(y)=p_{\rm atm}+\rho g\cos\alpha\,(h-y).
$$
Thus $\partial_xp=0$, and the downslope component of the <Navier-Stokes equations> reduces to $0=g\sin\alpha+(\eta/\rho)u''$. The printed pressure-free equation must be understood as this **downslope projection**: its full vector version would incorrectly omit the normal <hydrostatic pressure> gradient that balances $g\cos\alpha$.

The <no-slip boundary condition> gives $u(0)=0$. Neglecting air <shear stress> makes the surface shear-free, so $u'(h)=0$. Two integrations give the <uniform gravity-driven Newtonian film> profile
$$
u'(y)=\frac{\rho g\sin\alpha}{\eta}(h-y),\qquad
u(y)=\frac{\rho g\sin\alpha}{\eta}\left(hy-\frac{y^2}{2}\right).
$$
The <velocity> increases up to the <free surface>, where
$$
\boxed{u_{\max}=\frac{\rho gh^2\sin\alpha}{2\eta}.}
$$
This assumes a uniform laminar <Newtonian fluid> film, a stationary wall and negligible surface shear; a varying thickness or externally imposed downslope <pressure gradient> would alter the balance.

= Solution
{parent=b}

By reflection symmetry the vertical <gravitational field> of the <self-gravitating incompressible slab> vanishes at its mid-plane. Inside the slab, the gravitational <Poisson equation> therefore integrates to $d\psi/dz=4\pi G\rho z$. Treat the <star> as a test particle, so its own <mass> does not alter that field. Its <vertical stellar orbit in a uniform slab> obeys
$$
\ddot z=-4\pi G\rho z,\qquad z(0)=a,\qquad\dot z(0)=0.
$$
Hence $z(t)=a\cos(\omega t)$ with $\omega=\sqrt{4\pi G\rho}$. The first mid-plane crossing occurs after one quarter of this <simple harmonic motion> period:
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm cross}=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{4\pi G\rho}}=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4\sqrt{G\rho}}.}
$$
Its independence of $a$ follows from the linear interior <restoring force>. The calculation neglects drag and changes to the slab, and it uses the infinite planar approximation rather than the field of a finite Galactic disk.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Work in the rest frame of the <shock>. The normal <momentum flux> is conserved, so $p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2$. In the strong-shock limit $p_1\ll\rho_1u_1^2$, while <mass conservation> gives $\rho_2u_2=\rho_1u_1$. The given <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> therefore yield
$$
p_2\simeq\rho_1u_1^2\left(1-\frac{u_2}{u_1}\right)
=\frac{2\rho_1u_1^2}{\gamma+1},\qquad
\rho_2=\rho_1\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}.
$$
Using the downstream <ideal gas law> $p_2/\rho_2=k_BT_2/m$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{k_BT_2}{m}=\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}u_1^2.}
$$
For a monatomic <perfect gas>, $\gamma=5/3$ and the coefficient is $3/16$. This is the immediate post-shock <temperature>, before substantial <radiative cooling>; ionization, dissociation or unequal <electron> and ion <temperatures> require additional physical modeling.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <Alfvén wave> is a transverse disturbance restored by <magnetic tension>. In a uniform conducting <fluid> with background $\mathbf B_0=B_0\mathbf e_z$, a small transverse displacement $\boldsymbol\xi_\perp(z,t)$ bends the <magnetic field lines>. <Magnetic flux freezing> gives $\mathbf b_\perp=B_0\partial_z\boldsymbol\xi_\perp$, and the linear transverse <Lorentz force density> is $(B_0/\mu_0)\partial_z\mathbf b_\perp$. Thus the <linear Alfvén wave equations> reduce to
$$
\rho\,\partial_t^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp
=\frac{B_0^2}{\mu_0}\partial_z^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp,\qquad
\boxed{v_A=\frac{B_0}{\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}},\quad\omega^2=k_\parallel^2v_A^2.}
$$
For this shear <polarization>, $\delta\rho=\delta p=0$ at linear order. The disturbance transports <energy> along the background <magnetic field>; a single traveling wave has equal perturbation <magnetic energy> and <kinetic energy>, with $\mathbf b_\perp=\mp\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}\,\mathbf u_\perp$ for propagation along $\pm\mathbf B_0$. These properties distinguish it from compressive <magnetosonic waves>.

\Image[paper-1-alfven-wave.png]
{title=Transverse displacement of field lines in a traveling Alfvén wave}

The <ideal magnetohydrodynamics> approximation requires strong electrical coupling and wavelengths large compared with microscopic scales. Finite <electrical resistivity> and <viscosity> dissipate the wave's <energy>. In <stellar winds> and other astrophysical <plasmas>, <Alfvén waves> carry disturbances and can contribute to heating when dissipated. The <Lundquist liquid-metal Alfvén wave experiment> provides a laboratory counterpart: a conducting liquid in a strong applied <magnetic field> transmits a transverse magnetic disturbance, making the field's mechanical restoring action measurable.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A geometrically thin <accretion disk> around a central <mass> $M$ has nearly <Keplerian orbits>, with $\Omega^2\simeq GM/r^3$. Its vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives $H\sim c_s/\Omega\ll r$. The <specific angular momentum> $\ell=r^2\Omega\propto r^{1/2}$ decreases inward, so accreting matter must transfer <angular momentum> to other matter. A <viscous stress> couples neighboring annuli rotating at different <angular velocities>.

Writing $\nu$ for effective <kinematic viscosity> and $\Sigma$ for surface <mass density>, the outward <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is
$$
\mathcal G=-2\pi r^3\nu\Sigma\frac{d\Omega}{dr}>0.
$$
Steady <mass conservation> defines $\dot M=-2\pi r\Sigma u_r>0$. If the inner radius $r_{\rm in}$ has negligible applied torque, <angular momentum conservation> gives $\mathcal G=\dot M(\ell-\ell_{\rm in})$, hence
$$
\boxed{\nu\Sigma=\frac{\dot M}{3\pi}\left[1-\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}r\right)^{1/2}\right].}
$$
Well outside that boundary, $u_r\simeq-3\nu/(2r)$ and the inward drift time is of order $r^2/\nu$, much longer than an <orbital period> in a thin disk with modest effective stress. Dissipation of orbital <energy> heats the disk. If that heat escapes locally from two faces, the radiative flux from either face is
$$
F(r)=\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi r^3}\left[1-\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}r\right)^{1/2}\right].
$$
Integration over both faces gives $L=GM\dot M/(2r_{\rm in})$ in this Newtonian zero-torque model. Material can lose <angular momentum> while the disk's outer parts spread outward: outward transport does not imply that most of its <mass> moves outward. In real ionized disks, <magnetorotational instability> and magnetic stresses often provide the effective transport; molecular <viscosity> alone is generally insufficient.

= Solution
{parent=c}

In a <radiative shock>, a narrow dissipative jump first converts upstream <kinetic energy> into internal <energy>. The immediate strong adiabatic compression is $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, and the immediate <temperature> is the result of part 1(c). Downstream gas then radiates, loses <specific enthalpy> and compresses further. Its <shock cooling length> is of order $u_2t_{\rm cool}$, with the immediate downstream speed used only as an estimate because the gas slows as it cools.

For the idealization of equal far-upstream and cooled downstream <temperatures>, unchanged composition and negligible magnetic stress, write $p=c_T^2\rho$. Conserved <mass> and normal <momentum flux> across the whole layer give
$$
\rho_1u_1=\rho_fu_f,\qquad
c_T^2\rho_1+\rho_1u_1^2=c_T^2\rho_f+\rho_fu_f^2.
$$
With $r=\rho_f/\rho_1=u_1/u_f$, the nontrivial <isothermal shock> root is
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{u_1^2}{c_T^2}=\mathcal M_{T,1}^2.}
$$
It can greatly exceed the adiabatic strong-shock compression. An upstream <adiabatic sound speed> instead defines $\mathcal M_{{\rm ad},1}=u_1/(\sqrt\gamma c_T)$, so the same result is $r=\gamma\mathcal M_{{\rm ad},1}^2$; the two <Mach numbers> must not be confused. The radiated <energy> flux is $\rho_1u_1(u_1^2-u_f^2)/2$ when the two endpoint <specific enthalpies> agree. The gas's <specific entropy> rises at the initial jump and falls during cooling, while the escaping radiation carries <entropy> away. A transverse frozen-in <magnetic field>, or an imposed <temperature> floor, can limit the compression. Strongly cooling shocks can therefore collect thin dense shells without violating total <conservation of energy>.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For a <barotropic fluid>, define $h(\rho)=\int dp/\rho$. Dotting the steady <Euler equation> with $\mathbf u$ gives
$$
\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{u^2}{2}+h+\psi\right)=0.
$$
Thus the <Bernoulli function> $H=u^2/2+h+\psi$ is constant along each <streamline>. Different streamlines can have different constants; steady flow alone does not imply one spatially uniform $H$.

In a <quasi-one-dimensional flow> through area $A(z)$ with negligible <gravity>, the axial <momentum> equation and $dp=c_s^2d\rho$ give
$$
u\frac{du}{dz}=-\frac{c_s^2}{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{dz},\qquad
u^2\frac{d\ln u}{dz}=-c_s^2\frac{d\ln\rho}{dz}.
$$
Meanwhile the <mass flow rate> $\dot M=\rho uA$ is constant, so $d\ln\rho=-d\ln u-d\ln A$. Substitution proves the <nozzle area-velocity relation>
$$
\boxed{(u^2-c_s^2)\,d\ln u=c_s^2\,d\ln A.}
$$
A <subsonic flow> accelerates where $A$ decreases, whereas a <supersonic flow> accelerates where $A$ increases. A smooth accelerating <transonic branch> in a <de Laval nozzle> therefore crosses $u=c_s$ at a minimum-area throat. Suitable reservoir and downstream <pressures> are also required: a converging-diverging shape by itself does not exclude a wholly subsonic solution or a downstream <shock>.

\Image[paper-1-de-laval-nozzle.png]
{title=Choked adiabatic nozzle: minimum area, sonic crossing and continued supersonic acceleration}

For the stipulated constant-$K$ <polytropic flow>, $c_s^2=\gamma K\rho^{\gamma-1}$. At the throat $u_m=c_{s,m}$, so
$$
\dot M=A_m\rho_m\sqrt{\gamma K\rho_m^{\gamma-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\rho_m=\left(\frac{\dot M}{A_m\sqrt{\gamma K}}\right)^{2/(\gamma+1)}.}
$$
The <specific enthalpy> is $h=\gamma K\rho^{\gamma-1}/(\gamma-1)=c_s^2/(\gamma-1)$. Evaluating $H$ at the throat and substituting $\rho=\dot M/(Au)$ elsewhere gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma K}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{\dot M}{Au}\right)^{\gamma-1}
=\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}c_{s,m}^2.}
$$
On the outward supersonic branch, $A\sim Cr^2$ lets the gas expand and its <specific enthalpy> tend to zero for $\gamma>1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{u_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\,c_{s,m},\qquad
\rho\sim\frac{\dot M}{Cu_\infty}\,r^{-2}.}
$$
For $\gamma=5/3$, $u_\infty=2c_{s,m}$. Also $T\propto\rho^{\gamma-1}\propto r^{-2(\gamma-1)}$. These are the <adiabatic nozzle terminal speed> and expansion laws for a lossless escaping branch; a subsonic branch could instead approach finite <pressure> with $u\propto A^{-1}$. A pressure-shaped <astrophysical jet> is being approximated by a nozzle here: the assumed geometry does not by itself explain the confinement or account for magnetic <forces> or relativistic motion in an actual <active galactic nucleus>.

= Solution
{parent=4}

In a spherical self-gravitating equilibrium with positive <mass density>, $d\psi/dr=GM(r)/r^2>0$ away from the center. Thus $r$ can locally be replaced by $\psi$, so both $p(r)$ and $\rho(r)$ can be expressed as functions of $\psi$. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> further gives
$$
\frac{dp}{d\psi}=-\rho.
$$
Since $p$ decreases outward wherever $\rho>0$, eliminating $\psi$ gives a <barotropic equation of state> along this equilibrium. This is a relation describing its structure; it does not require all thermodynamic perturbations of an arbitrary material to be barotropic.

For $p=K\rho^{1+1/n}$, integrate the <polytropic enthalpy> balance $dp/\rho=-d\psi$:
$$
(n+1)K\rho^{1/n}+\psi=\psi_T,\qquad
\boxed{\rho=\left[\frac{\psi_T-\psi}{(n+1)K}\right]^n.}
$$
For a finite-radius zero-pressure boundary with $K>0$ and $n>0$, $\rho(R)=0$ and therefore $\psi_T=\psi(R)$. With the gravitational potential zero at infinity, an isolated sphere has $\psi_T=-GM/R$. It is an integration constant tied to that boundary, not necessarily a zero of the <gravitational potential>.

Put $\theta=(\rho/\rho_c)^{1/n}$ and $\xi=\alpha r$, with $\alpha^2=4\pi G\rho_c^{1-1/n}/[(n+1)K]$. The preceding relation gives $\psi=\psi_T-(n+1)K\rho_c^{1/n}\theta$. Substitution into the spherical gravitational <Poisson equation> yields the <Lane-Emden equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right)=-\theta^n,\qquad
\theta(0)=1,\quad\theta'(0)=0.}
$$
Let $\xi_1$ be the first zero of the regular <Lane-Emden function> and $Y_n=-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)>0$. Then the <Lane-Emden mass formula> and radius are
$$
R=\frac{\xi_1}{\alpha},\qquad
M=\frac{4\pi\rho_c}{\alpha^3}Y_n.
$$
For a common fixed <polytropic index> with a finite first zero, their scalings are
$$
\boxed{M\propto K^{3/2}\rho_c^{3/(2n)-1/2},\qquad
R\propto K^{1/2}\rho_c^{1/(2n)-1/2}.}
$$
The usual regular zero-pressure models have a finite first zero for $0<n<5$; the $n=5$ model has infinite radius, so a finite-radius formula cannot simply be applied to it.

For an <ideal gas> of fixed composition, $T_c\propto K\rho_c^{1/n}$. Consequently <homologous polytropes at fixed central temperature> have $K\propto\rho_c^{-1/n}$ and both $M,R\propto\rho_c^{-1/2}$, recovering $M\propto R$. The common central <temperature>, composition and dimensionless structure are the relevant assumptions. Keeping $K$ fixed instead gives the different <polytropic mass-radius relation>, and no finite index makes its generic fixed-$K$ exponent exactly one.

For adiabatic monatomic <hydrogen>, $\gamma=5/3$ and $n=3/2$. At a common fixed <specific entropy>, hence common $K$, the same relations give
$$
\boxed{R\propto KM^{-1/3},\qquad M\propto R^{-3},\qquad\bar\rho\propto M^2\quad(K\text{ fixed}).}
$$
Here $\bar\rho=3M/(4\pi R^3)$, and the omitted constant in the last relation depends on $K$. A population with different cloud entropies need not follow that <mass> sequence.

Efficient <radiative cooling> in <molecular clouds> prevents compression from forcing the <temperature> upward as steeply as in an adiabatic monatomic gas. Rotational transitions of molecules and radiation by dust provide low-temperature cooling channels; which dominates depends on composition, opacity and <temperature>. It is therefore possible to assemble dense massive cold clouds while disposing of compressional <heat>. This explains the direction of the idealized comparison, rather than proving a universal cloud-mass cutoff.

For a common <isothermal sound speed> $c_T$, a spherical isothermal equilibrium has $GM/R$ equal to $c_T^2$ times a dimensionless structural factor. Holding that factor fixed gives the <homologous isothermal mass-radius relation>
$$
\boxed{M\propto R\quad(T\text{ and dimensionless structure fixed}).}
$$
For example, the <singular isothermal sphere> has $M(R)=2c_T^2R/G$. A regular finite cloud requires a <pressure-confined isothermal sphere> or another outer boundary: an unconfined <isothermal sphere> does not end at a finite vacuum radius. Equal <temperature> alone, while allowing arbitrary truncation or density contrast, does not make the coefficient universal.
