= Solution
{parent=1}

A fluid particle follows a <Lagrangian trajectory>, so its position satisfies $\dot x=u(x,t)$ rather than the <velocity> evaluated permanently at its initial position. Assume $\omega\ne0$, and write
$$
x(t)=X_0+u_0a(t)+u_0^2b(t)+O(u_0^3),\qquad a(0)=b(0)=0.
$$
Set $\alpha=kX_0$ and $\theta=\alpha-\omega t$. A <Taylor expansion> of the <velocity> about $X_0$ gives
$$
u_0\dot a+u_0^2\dot b=u_0\sin\theta+ku_0^2a\cos\theta+O(u_0^3).
$$
Thus $\dot a=\sin\theta$, and
$$
a(t)=\frac{\cos\theta-\cos\alpha}{\omega}.
$$
At the next order,
$$
\dot b=\frac k\omega(\cos^2\theta-\cos\alpha\cos\theta),
$$
so the required <second-order trajectory in a sinusoidal velocity field> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x(t)={}&X_0+\frac{u_0}{\omega}(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha)\\
&+\frac{ku_0^2}{\omega}\left[\frac t2+
\frac{\sin2\alpha-\sin2\theta}{4\omega}
-\frac{\cos\alpha(\sin\alpha-\sin\theta)}{\omega}\right]+O(u_0^3).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Both corrections vanish at $t=0$. All retained terms have units of length. The secular contribution gives the <Stokes drift>
$$
\boxed{\overline{\dot x}=\frac{ku_0^2}{2\omega}+O(u_0^3),}
$$
although the <Eulerian time average> of the given <velocity> is zero. The particle samples the <wave> at its displaced position, producing this second-order drift. The expansion is in the dimensionless parameter $|ku_0/\omega|\ll1$, for fixed times; it ceases to be uniform when the accumulated drift changes the <wave> phase by order unity.

Interpret “reach” first as remaining an energetic, supersonic <blast wave>, rather than as the arrival of any <acoustic wave>. An <atmospheric blast-wave range> estimate must account for the thin atmosphere: the distance $D=8.5\times10^6\ \mathrm m$ is much greater than its height $H=10^4\ \mathrm m$.

For an optimistic estimate, suppose the deposited <energy> $E$ remains in a horizontally expanding atmospheric layer. If its representative <mass density> is $\rho_a$, the swept mass is of order $\pi D^2H\rho_a$. <Conservation of energy> gives the <self-similar blast wave> scaling
$$
E\sim\pi\rho_aHD^2U^2,\qquad
U(D)\sim\left(\frac E{\pi\rho_aHD^2}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
Using ordinary atmospheric estimates $\rho_a\sim1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and <speed of sound> $c_a\sim300\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, this gives
$$
\pi D^2H\rho_a\simeq2.3\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{kg},\qquad
U(D)\sim66\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad U/c_a\sim0.22.
$$
These are scaling estimates, not an exact <shock wave> solution. The strong <shock wave> approximation would already have failed at
$$
D_s\sim\sqrt{\frac E{\pi\rho_aHc_a^2}}\simeq1.9\times10^6\ \mathrm m.
$$
Equivalently, keeping the disturbance strongly supersonic out to $D$ would require substantially more than the sonic-transition <energy> scale
$$
E_s\sim\pi\rho_aHD^2c_a^2\simeq2.0\times10^{23}\ \mathrm J,
$$
which exceeds $10^{22}\ \mathrm J$ by about a factor of twenty. Reasonable atmospheric averages and order-unity geometrical coefficients do not reverse the comparison. Escape of <energy> through the top of the atmosphere makes the fully confined estimate optimistic; <Earth>'s curvature changes its area estimate by an order-unity factor.

**The strong atmospheric blast will not survive to Lansing in this model.** A weakened <acoustic wave> can nevertheless travel there. The supplied <energy> and atmospheric height do not determine its detectable amplitude, detailed attenuation or damage: those require an atmospheric propagation model. Thus “no disturbance of any kind reaches Lansing” would be an unjustified stronger conclusion.

This is <magnetic flux freezing> in <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>. In addition to the supplied <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation>, a physical <magnetic field> obeys <Gauss's law for magnetism>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$.

Parameterize the <material surface> by two particle labels $a,b$, so $d\mathbf S=(\partial_a\mathbf x\times\partial_b\mathbf x)\,da\,db$ and $D\mathbf x/Dt=\mathbf u$. Differentiating these tangents gives the <material surface element evolution> law
$$
\frac D{Dt}d\mathbf S=
\left[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)\mathsf I-(\nabla\mathbf u)^{\mathsf T}\right]d\mathbf S,
$$
where $(\nabla\mathbf u)_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$. Thus the <material magnetic-flux transport theorem> is
$$
\frac D{Dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S
=\int_{S(t)}\left[\frac{D\mathbf B}{Dt}
+\mathbf B(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)-(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right]\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$
Expand the <curl> in the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation>:
$$
\partial_t\mathbf B=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B
-\mathbf B(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mathbf u(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B).
$$
Since $D/Dt=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$ is the <material derivative>, the first three terms cancel the corresponding terms in the surface transport formula. The remaining integrand is $\mathbf u\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac D{Dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S=0.}
$$
No <incompressible flow> assumption is needed: the changing <surface> area cancels the compression term. This proof assumes smooth <velocity> and <magnetic field> and a surface carried with the fluid; the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation> by itself, for an arbitrary non-solenoidal <vector field>, would leave the final divergence term.

The <buoyancy frequency>, also called the <Brunt–Väisälä frequency>, is the <angular frequency> of a small vertical displacement restored by <buoyancy> in a stably stratified fluid. Take $z$ upward, <gravitational acceleration> $-g\widehat{\mathbf z}$, and a background in <hydrostatic equilibrium>,
$$
\frac{dp}{dz}=-\rho g.
$$
Displace a small gas element upward by $\xi$, sufficiently slowly for its <pressure> to match its surroundings but without heat exchange. For this <adiabatic process>, $p/\rho^\gamma$ is constant along the element's motion, so its <mass density> changes by $\rho\gamma^{-1}(d\ln p/dz)\xi$. The surrounding <mass density> changes by $(d\rho/dz)\xi$. The element's excess <mass density> is consequently
$$
\Delta\rho=\rho\left(\frac1\gamma\frac{d\ln p}{dz}-\frac{d\ln\rho}{dz}\right)\xi.
$$
Its acceleration is $\ddot\xi=-g\Delta\rho/\rho=-N^2\xi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{N^2=g\left(\frac1\gamma\frac{d\ln p}{dz}-\frac{d\ln\rho}{dz}\right).}
$$
For a gas of fixed composition obeying the <ideal gas law>, $\rho\propto p/T$. The <adiabatic buoyancy frequency of an ideal gas> is therefore
$$
\boxed{N^2=g\left[\frac1T\frac{dT}{dz}-\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma p}\frac{dp}{dz}\right]
=\frac gT\left(\frac{dT}{dz}+\frac g{c_p}\right),}
$$
where $c_p=\gamma\mathcal R/(\gamma-1)$ is the <specific heat capacity> at constant <pressure>, and $\mathcal R=p/(\rho T)$ is the specific gas constant. Here $N^2$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$; $N$ is an <angular frequency>, not a cyclic <frequency>. Positive $N^2$ gives <simple harmonic motion>, negative $N^2$ gives <convective instability>, and $N^2=0$ is marginal. The temperature form assumes fixed composition; the preceding density form is more general.

Write the background <mass density> as $\bar\rho(z)=\rho_0+\rho'_0z$ and its <hydrostatic pressure> as $\bar p(z)$, where
$$
\bar p'(z)=-g\bar\rho(z),\qquad
\bar p(z)=p_* -g\rho_0z-\frac12g\rho'_0z^2.
$$
Apply an <Eulerian perturbation of a fluid variable> to the background fields:
$$
\rho_{\rm tot}=\bar\rho+\varrho,\qquad p_{\rm tot}=\bar p+\pi.
$$
Thus $\pi$ is the Eulerian <pressure> perturbation, in pascals; it is not the background <hydrostatic pressure>.

For <incompressible flow>, <mass conservation> becomes $D\rho_{\rm tot}/Dt=0$. Linearizing about rest gives
$$
\partial_t\varrho=-u_z\rho'_0.
$$
The <Euler equations> are $\rho_{\rm tot}D\mathbf u/Dt=-\nabla p_{\rm tot}-\rho_{\rm tot}g\widehat{\mathbf z}$. The background <pressure gradient> and weight cancel. Dropping products of perturbations gives
$$
\bar\rho\,\partial_t\mathbf u=-\nabla\pi-\varrho g\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Under $|\rho'_0z|\ll\rho_0$, replace $\bar\rho$ by $\rho_0$ in inertia but retain the background <mass density> gradient in the displacement-induced <buoyancy>. This is the <Boussinesq approximation>. Its linear <Boussinesq equations> here are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\partial_xu_x+\partial_zu_z&=0,\\
\rho_0\partial_tu_x&=-\partial_x\pi,\\
\rho_0\partial_tu_z&=-\partial_z\pi-g\varrho,\\
\partial_t\varrho&=-\rho'_0u_z.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The absolute value makes explicit that the small-density-variation condition concerns magnitude, including the usual negative $\rho'_0$ of stable <density stratification>.

Use <plane wave> perturbations proportional to $e^{i(k_xx+k_zz-\omega t)}$, with amplitudes $\widehat u,\widehat w,\widehat\pi,\widehat\varrho$. The linear equations become
$$
k_x\widehat u+k_z\widehat w=0,\qquad
\omega\rho_0\widehat u=k_x\widehat\pi,\qquad
-i\omega\rho_0\widehat w=-ik_z\widehat\pi-g\widehat\varrho,\qquad
-i\omega\widehat\varrho=-\rho'_0\widehat w.
$$
To eliminate <pressure> without dividing by $k_x$, multiply the vertical momentum equation by $k_x$ and subtract $k_z$ times the horizontal momentum equation:
$$
-i\omega\rho_0(k_x\widehat w-k_z\widehat u)=-gk_x\widehat\varrho.
$$
Use <incompressible flow> and the equation for <mass density> to obtain $\omega^2\rho_0(k_x^2+k_z^2)\widehat w=-g\rho'_0k_x^2\widehat w$. Hence, for a nontrivial <internal gravity wave>,
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=N^2\frac{k_x^2}{k_x^2+k_z^2},\qquad
N^2=-\frac{g\rho'_0}{\rho_0}.}
$$
For stable <density stratification>, $\rho'_0<0$ and $N=\sqrt{-g\rho'_0/\rho_0}$ is the <buoyancy frequency>. Then $0\le\omega^2\le N^2$ and the <normal modes> oscillate rather than grow. For $\rho'_0>0$, a mode with $k_x\ne0$ has $\omega^2<0$ and grows exponentially: denser fluid lies above lighter fluid. Uniform density, $\rho'_0=0$, is marginal for this restoring mechanism.

The case $k_x=0$, $k_z\ne0$, has zero <frequency> in the displayed relation. <Incompressible flow> then requires $\widehat w=0$, so it is a degenerate non-propagating case rather than a finite-frequency vertical oscillation. These equations concern the incompressible model of part (b); its $N^2$ does not contain the adiabatic compression correction needed in part (a).

For $N^2>0$, write $K=\sqrt{k_x^2+k_z^2}$ and choose either signed branch $\omega=sNk_x/K$, with $s=\pm1$. These two branches cover the roots of the squared <dispersion relation>. The <group velocity> of an <internal gravity wave> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c_g=\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega
=\frac{sN}{K^3}(k_z^2,\,0,\,-k_xk_z).}
$$
If instead the positive-frequency root is chosen for both signs of $k_x$, set $s=+1$ and multiply the displayed components by $\operatorname{sgn}(k_x)$, away from the cusp $k_x=0$.

Taking the <dot product> with the <wave vector> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf c_g=0.}
$$
Thus the <group velocity>, which describes the travel of a narrow <wave packet> and its <energy>, is perpendicular to the <wave vector>, and hence perpendicular to the <phase velocity> $\mathbf c_p=\omega\mathbf k/K^2$. This is the <internal-wave phase and group velocity> geometry. It also follows from <phase-group orthogonality for degree-zero dispersion>: the <frequency> depends only on the direction of $\mathbf k$, not its magnitude. For $k_z=0$, the <group velocity> is zero, consistent with the stationary extremum $|\omega|=N$.

= Solution

The <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> follow by integrating <conservation laws> across a thin moving layer, then letting its macroscopic thickness tend to zero. For example, normal <momentum conservation> in one dimension has the conservative form
$$
\partial_t(\rho u)+\partial_x(\rho u^2+p)=0.
$$
For a <shock wave> moving at speed $V$, integrate over a control interval whose endpoints move with the discontinuity. The <Reynolds transport theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{V[\rho u]=[\rho u^2+p],}
$$
where $[f]=f_R-f_L$ denotes the difference of limiting states. <Mass conservation> similarly gives $V[\rho]=[\rho u]$. If $v=u-V$ is the normal <velocity> in the <shock frame>, combining the two relations yields
$$
\boxed{[\rho v]=0,\qquad[p+\rho v^2]=0.}
$$
The same construction applied to <conservation of energy>, for a non-radiating <normal shock wave> with nonzero mass flux, gives $[h+v^2/2]=0$, where $h$ is the <specific enthalpy>. An <equation of state> closes these relations, and the physical compression branch has increasing <entropy>.

Finite <dynamic viscosity> replaces the ideal discontinuity by a thin smooth transition. A viscous <stress> contributes a derivative to the momentum flux inside the layer and converts directed <kinetic energy> into internal <energy>. If its gradients vanish in the uniform far states, the same limiting <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> still hold there. Balancing nonlinear transport with viscous diffusion gives a thickness of order $\nu/|u_L-u_R|$ in the <viscous Burgers equation>, where $\nu$ is the <kinematic viscosity>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The pressure-free equation has the <scalar conservation law> form
$$
\partial_tu+\partial_xf(u)=0,\qquad f(u)=\frac12u^2.
$$
A smooth travelling ansatz $u=U(x-Vt)$ obeys $(U-V)U'=0$, so it cannot continuously connect two distinct constant states. The inviscid <shock wave> must be interpreted as a discontinuous <weak solution>. Its <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> is
$$
V(u_2-u_1)=f(u_2)-f(u_1)=\frac12(u_2^2-u_1^2).
$$
For $u_1\ne u_2$,
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{u_1+u_2}{2}.}
$$
The result is independent of which state is called upstream. For a physical compressive <entropy shock> of the <inviscid Burgers equation>, the left and right states satisfy $u_L>u_R$ and therefore $u_L>V>u_R$: <characteristic curves> run into the <shock wave> from both sides. The ordering $u_L<u_R$ instead produces a <rarefaction wave>.

= Solution

For $\nu>0$ and positive $\Psi$, set $f=\log\Psi$, so the <Cole-Hopf transformation> is $u=-2\nu f_x$. Substitution into the <viscous Burgers equation> gives
$$
u_t+uu_x-\nu u_{xx}
=-2\nu\partial_x\left(f_t-\nu f_{xx}-\nu f_x^2\right)
=-2\nu\partial_x\left(\frac{\Psi_t-\nu\Psi_{xx}}{\Psi}\right)=0.
$$
Hence the most general equation implied by this substitution is
$$
\Psi_t-\nu\Psi_{xx}=C(t)\Psi.
$$
The <time normalization in the Cole-Hopf transformation> accounts for the arbitrary function $C(t)$: multiplying $\Psi$ by a nonzero function of time leaves $\partial_x\log\Psi$ and therefore $u$ unchanged. Replacing $\Psi$ by $e^{-\int^tC(s)ds}\Psi$ sets $C=0$. With this conventional normalization,
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\Psi=\nu\partial_x^2\Psi,}
$$
the <heat equation>.

For each $j=1,2$, the function $\Psi_j=e^{\nu\kappa_j^2t+\kappa_jx}$ obeys $\partial_t\Psi_j=\nu\kappa_j^2\Psi_j=\nu\partial_x^2\Psi_j$. By the <superposition principle> for this linear <partial differential equation>,
$$
\boxed{\Psi=e^{\nu\kappa_1^2t+\kappa_1x}+e^{\nu\kappa_2^2t+\kappa_2x}}
$$
is a solution, positive for all real $x,t,\kappa_1,\kappa_2$. Here $\kappa_j$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ so both exponents are dimensionless. The positivity ensures that its logarithm and the resulting <velocity> are regular.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $a_j=\nu\kappa_j^2t+\kappa_jx$. Differentiating the logarithm gives
$$
u=-2\nu\frac{\kappa_1e^{a_1}+\kappa_2e^{a_2}}{e^{a_1}+e^{a_2}}.
$$
For definiteness order $\kappa_1<\kappa_2$. Then $e^{a_1}$ dominates as $x\to-\infty$, while $e^{a_2}$ dominates as $x\to+\infty$. The limiting states are
$$
\boxed{u_L=-2\nu\kappa_1,\qquad u_R=-2\nu\kappa_2,\qquad u_L>u_R.}
$$
Without this ordering convention, $u(-\infty)=-2\nu\min(\kappa_1,\kappa_2)$ and $u(+\infty)=-2\nu\max(\kappa_1,\kappa_2)$. Thus the states $u_{1,2}$ correspond to $-2\nu\kappa_{1,2}$ after matching each label to its dominant exponential; the propagation speed does not depend on the naming of the two states.

Their exponent difference is
$$
a_2-a_1=(\kappa_2-\kappa_1)\left[x+\nu(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)t\right].
$$
Consequently, the <viscous Burgers travelling shock> moves at
$$
\boxed{V=-\nu(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)=\frac{u_L+u_R}{2}=\frac{u_1+u_2}{2}.}
$$
Set $\Delta u=u_L-u_R=2\nu(\kappa_2-\kappa_1)>0$ and $\xi=x-Vt$. Factoring out the mean exponential gives the <hyperbolic tangent> profile
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=V-\frac{\Delta u}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\Delta u\,\xi}{4\nu}\right).}
$$
A precise measure of the width must be stated. The natural length in the <hyperbolic tangent> argument is
$$
\boxed{\ell=\frac{4\nu}{\Delta u}=\frac{2}{|\kappa_2-\kappa_1|}.}
$$
Thus the layer has thickness of order $\nu/\Delta u$. A different common convention, jump divided by maximum gradient, gives $\delta=\Delta u/|u_x(0)|=8\nu/\Delta u=2\ell$; the far-tail exponential e-folding length is $\ell/2$. These differ only by their stated definitions, rather than by different solutions.

Two distinct limits require $\kappa_1\ne\kappa_2$. The source permits arbitrary real constants, but in the degenerate case $\kappa_1=\kappa_2$ the solution is uniform, $u=-2\nu\kappa_1$, and there is no <shock wave> or finite shock width.

Treat the cylinder as infinitely long, with a vacuum exterior and no <surface tension>. Let $\Phi_0$ be its <gravitational potential>. Integrating the gravitational <Poisson equation> over a coaxial cylinder of radius $r<R$ and length $L$, then applying the <divergence theorem>, gives
$$
2\pi rL\frac{d\Phi_0}{dr}=4\pi G(\pi r^2L\rho),\qquad
\frac{d\Phi_0}{dr}=2\pi G\rho r.
$$
Thus the inward <gravitational acceleration> is $-2\pi G\rho r\widehat{\mathbf r}$. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> requires
$$
\frac{dp_0}{dr}=-\rho\frac{d\Phi_0}{dr}=-2\pi G\rho^2r.
$$
Impose zero <pressure> on the <free surface>, $p_0(R)=0$, and integrate inward:
$$
\boxed{p_0(r)=\pi G\rho^2(R^2-r^2),\qquad 0\le r<R.}
$$
This is the <hydrostatic self-gravitating incompressible cylinder>. Its central <pressure> is $\pi G\rho^2R^2$, and the units of the expression are $\mathrm{Pa}$. A nonzero external <pressure> would add that constant to $p_0(r)$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write total <pressure> and <gravitational potential> as
$$
p=p_0(r)+\pi(r,z,t),\qquad \Phi=\Phi_0(r)+\psi(r,z,t).
$$
Here $\pi$ is the <Eulerian perturbation of a fluid variable> applied to <pressure>, while $\psi$ is the corresponding <gravitational potential> perturbation. They have units $\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$, respectively. The constant interior <mass density> is $\rho$.

The <Euler equations> are
$$
\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=-\frac1\rho\nabla(p_0+\pi)-\nabla(\Phi_0+\psi).
$$
Subtract the <hydrostatic equilibrium> relation $\nabla p_0/\rho+\nabla\Phi_0=0$ and discard terms quadratic in the perturbation. <Mass conservation> gives <incompressible flow>, and the gravitational <Poisson equation> has no bulk density perturbation. Thus, strictly inside the fluid,
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf u=-\frac{\nabla\pi}{\rho}-\nabla\psi,\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad\nabla^2\psi=0.}
$$
The last equation is <Laplace equation>, not a statement that self-gravity has disappeared. The displaced surface carries a first-order <surface mass density> $\rho\eta$, where $\eta$ is its radial displacement, and this supplies the source and matching conditions for $\psi$ at the moving interface. The <axisymmetric gravitational potential of a displaced cylindrical interface>, expressed using <modified Bessel functions>, incorporates that boundary source.

It is useful to define $W=\psi+\pi/\rho$. Taking the <divergence> of the momentum equation gives $\nabla^2W=0$. For an axisymmetric <Fourier mode> with axial <wavenumber> $k>0$, regularity at $r=0$ selects radial dependence $I_0(kr)$, the <modified Bessel function of the first kind>, since $K_0(kr)$ is singular there. In what follows $k=|k_z|$ can always be chosen nonnegative.

Use complex amplitudes and take their <real parts> at the end. Write the radial surface displacement as $\eta=\epsilon e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$ and the combined potential as
$$
W=\epsilon\Pi_0I_0(kr)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}.
$$
The linear <kinematic boundary condition> is $u_r(R)=\partial_t\eta$. Differentiate it once more in time and use the radial momentum equation, $\partial_tu_r=-\partial_rW$, evaluated at the unperturbed radius $R$:
$$
\partial_t^2\eta=-\left.\partial_rW\right|_R.
$$
With $\kappa=kR$, the mode amplitudes therefore satisfy
$$
-\omega^2\epsilon=-\epsilon\Pi_0kI'_0(\kappa),
\qquad
\boxed{\Pi_0=\frac{\omega^2}{kI'_0(\kappa)}.}
$$
The prime differentiates the <modified Bessel function of the first kind> with respect to its dimensionless argument, rather than with respect to $r$. The result has units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, as required because $\epsilon\Pi_0$ is a potential. It applies to $k>0$; the uniform $k=0$ limit requires separate treatment.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The vanishing total <pressure> must be imposed at the displaced <free surface>, not at fixed $r=R$. The first-order <Taylor expansion> is
$$
0=p_0(R+\eta)+\pi(R+\eta,z,t)
=p_0(R)+\eta p'_0(R)+\pi(R,z,t)+O(\eta^2).
$$
Since $p_0(R)=0$ and $p'_0(R)=-2\pi G\rho^2R$, the <Lagrangian pressure perturbation> condition is
$$
\pi(R,z,t)=2\pi G\rho^2R\eta.
$$
The supplied gravitational perturbation and the combined potential, evaluated at $R$, then give
$$
\eta\Pi_0I_0(\kappa)=W(R)
=-4\pi G\rho R K_0(\kappa)I_0(\kappa)\eta+2\pi G\rho R\eta.
$$
Here $K_0$ is the <modified Bessel function of the second kind>. Equating mode amplitudes and using part (c) yields
$$
\frac{\omega^2I_0(\kappa)}{kI'_0(\kappa)}
=4\pi G\rho R\left[\frac12-K_0(\kappa)I_0(\kappa)\right].
$$
Rearrange to obtain the <dispersion relation> for the <axisymmetric gravitational instability of an incompressible cylinder>:
$$
\boxed{-\frac{\omega^2}{4\pi G\rho}
=\kappa\frac{I'_0(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)}
\left[K_0(\kappa)I_0(\kappa)-\frac12\right].}
$$
Every quantity on the right is dimensionless, consistent with $G\rho$ having units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$. The stabilizing term $1/2$ comes from displacing the background <hydrostatic pressure> profile; omitting the <Lagrangian pressure perturbation> would lose it.

Define the dimensionless right-hand side of part (d) by $F(\kappa)$. Then $\omega^2=-4\pi G\rho F(\kappa)$. Because $I'_0(\kappa)=I_1(\kappa)>0$ and $I_0(\kappa)>0$ for $\kappa>0$, its sign is the sign of $I_0(\kappa)K_0(\kappa)-1/2$. The original plot is positive for sufficiently long <wavelengths> and negative at short <wavelengths>; the nonzero crossing is approximately $\kappa_c\simeq1.07$. Numerically solving the displayed equation gives $\kappa_c\simeq1.0667$.

For $0<\kappa<\kappa_c$, $\omega^2<0$. Write $\omega=i\sigma$ with $\sigma=\sqrt{4\pi G\rho F}>0$. The time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ then contains an exponentially growing solution. For $\kappa>\kappa_c$, $\omega^2>0$ and the ideal inviscid modes oscillate stably, without damping. At the crossing the <frequency> is zero and the mode is marginal. In terms of the axial <wavelength> $\lambda_z=2\pi/k$,
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\lambda_z>\frac{2\pi R}{\kappa_c}\simeq5.89R&:\quad\text{gravitationally unstable},\\
\lambda_z<5.89R&:\quad\text{oscillatory and stable}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <growth rate> tends to zero as $kR\to0^+$, so the very longest <wavelengths> are not the fastest growing. The source plot has its maximum near $kR\simeq0.58$, corresponding to $\lambda_z\simeq11R$ and $\sigma\simeq0.25\sqrt{4\pi G\rho}$. Thus a characteristic fragmentation spacing is several cylinder diameters; a quantitative nonlinear fragment mass is beyond this <linear stability> calculation.

For unstable modes the physical displacement must be interpreted as $\operatorname{Re}[\epsilon e^{i(kz-\omega t)}]$ with complex amplitudes handled consistently throughout. The paper's real-part notation is harmless for the squared <dispersion relation>, but it should not be read as imposing real $\omega$ in a <gravitational instability> calculation. The exactly uniform mode $k=0$ is excluded from the finite-$k$ derivation; the limit of the curve alone does not establish a separate volume-preserving uniform-radius mode.
