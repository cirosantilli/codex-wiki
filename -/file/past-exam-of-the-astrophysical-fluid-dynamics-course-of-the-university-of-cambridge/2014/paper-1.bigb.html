= Solution
{parent=a}

For an <ideal gas> of fixed composition, the central <temperature> satisfies $T_c\propto p_c/\rho_c=K\rho_c^{1/n}$. Keeping $T_c$ fixed therefore requires $K\propto\rho_c^{-1/n}$, rather than keeping the <polytropic constant> fixed. Substitution in the <Lane-Emden equation> scalings gives
$$
M\propto\rho_c^{(3/n-1)/2}\rho_c^{-3/(2n)}=\rho_c^{-1/2},\qquad
R\propto\rho_c^{(1/n-1)/2}\rho_c^{-1/(2n)}=\rho_c^{-1/2}.
$$
Consequently the <homologous polytropes at fixed central temperature> obey
$$
\boxed{M\propto R.}
$$
The <polytropic index> and mean particle <mass> must be common to this homologous sequence: varying either changes the dimensionless structural coefficient. The fixed-central-temperature assumption is the idealization being used here, rather than a universal property of real <stars>.

= Solution

In an adiabatic outflow without additional heating, the reservoir's <specific enthalpy> supplies both escaping <kinetic energy> and the work against <gravity>. For a monatomic <ideal gas>, $\gamma=5/3$ and $h=c_s^2/(\gamma-1)=3c_s^2/2$, where $c_s$ is the reservoir's <adiabatic sound speed>. The <Bernoulli equation>, measured from a reservoir initially at rest at radius $r$, gives
$$
\frac{v_\infty^2}{2}+h_\infty
=\frac{c_s^2}{\gamma-1}-\frac{GM}{r}.
$$
The maximum terminal speed uses all available <specific enthalpy>, so $h_\infty\to0$ and
$$
\boxed{v_{\infty,\max}=\sqrt{3c_s^2-\frac{2GM}{r}}.}
$$
A freely escaping adiabatic flow needs a nonnegative radicand; equality is marginal escape. If the radicand is negative, this reservoir cannot supply an unheated escaping outflow. The initial rest condition is a stagnation-reservoir approximation: an exactly stationary finite-density surface cannot simultaneously carry a nonzero steady <mass flow rate>. Further heating, radiation <forces> or an imposed confining <pressure> would change the terminal-energy budget.

Once swept-up matter dominates the ejecta and <radiative cooling> is negligible, the <Sedov-Taylor blast wave> has its <shock> radius fixed by <dimensional analysis>:
$$
R_s=\xi\left(\frac{Et^2}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/5}.
$$
Here $\xi$ is a dimensionless constant of order unity fixed by the <adiabatic index>. Treating the ambient matter as <hydrogen> gives $\rho_0\simeq10^6m_H\simeq1.67\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, while $t\simeq3.16\times10^{10}\,\mathrm s$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R_s\simeq1.43\times10^{17}\,\mathrm m\simeq4.6\,\mathrm{pc}\quad(\xi\simeq1).}
$$
For a monatomic gas, the usual similarity normalization $\xi\simeq1.15$ instead gives about $5.3\,\mathrm{pc}$. An estimate of a few <parsecs> is therefore appropriate. Negligible ejecta <mass> does not by itself guarantee this energy-conserving stage: substantial radiation losses or a strongly nonuniform ambient <mass density> would invalidate the assumed similarity law.

= Solution

A <stellar wind> carries matter away from a <star>, altering its <mass>, <angular momentum> and surroundings. Hot coronal gas can escape through pressure-driven expansion; radiation transfers <momentum> to gas through continuum or <spectral line> absorption, or to dust that then drags gas. A <magnetic field> can transfer rotational <energy> and <angular momentum> to an outflow. These mechanisms need not give the same terminal speed or <mass loss rate>.

For a steady spherical pressure-driven model, <conservation of mass> gives $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u$. Combining the radial <Euler equation> with an <isothermal equation of state> $p=\rho a^2$ gives the <Parker wind equation>
$$
\left(u-\frac{a^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}
=\frac{2a^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
A smooth <transonic branch> passes through $u=a$ at $r_s=GM/(2a^2)$, connecting a subsonic inner atmosphere to an escaping supersonic wind. The <isothermal Parker wind> requires continuing heat input as the gas expands; a <polytropic stellar wind> instead tracks a changing <temperature> and must satisfy its own terminal-energy condition. A <subsonic isothermal breeze> generally tends to a nonzero external <pressure>, so outward velocity alone does not select the escaping solution.

\Image[paper-1-parker-wind.png]
{title=Transonic isothermal Parker wind and a pressure-confined breeze}

The <solar wind> supplies a nearby example of an outward <plasma> flow carrying a <magnetic field>. Profiles of <spectral lines>, especially absorption shifted by the <Doppler effect> together with emission, diagnose more distant <stellar winds>. The critical-point condition and boundary heating constrain $\dot M$; it is not determined by an escape-speed estimate alone.

An <accretion disk> forms when inward-moving gas retains enough <angular momentum> to orbit a compact object. In a geometrically thin <Keplerian disk>, radial <force> balance gives $\Omega\simeq\sqrt{GM/r^3}$ and vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives a thickness $H\sim c_s/\Omega\ll r$. Matter cannot fall far inward while keeping its original <specific angular momentum> $r^2\Omega\propto r^{1/2}$: stresses must transport <angular momentum> outward.

The <viscous torque in an accretion disk> has outward magnitude
$$
\mathcal G=-2\pi r^3\nu\Sigma\frac{d\Omega}{dr}
=3\pi\nu\Sigma\sqrt{GMr},
$$
where $\nu$ is the effective <kinematic viscosity> and $\Sigma$ the <surface density>. With constant inward <accretion rate> $\dot M>0$ and zero inner torque at $r_{\rm in}$, <conservation of angular momentum> gives
$$
\nu\Sigma=\frac{\dot M}{3\pi}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{r_{\rm in}}r}\right).
$$
The associated <standard thin-disk dissipation flux> from each face is
$$
F(r)=\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi r^3}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{r_{\rm in}}r}\right).
$$
Orbital <gravitational potential energy> becomes <heat> and, in a radiatively efficient disk, escaping radiation. Integrating both faces gives the <standard thin-disk luminosity> $L=GM\dot M/(2r_{\rm in})$ under this boundary condition. A central stellar surface or an inner torque can release or redistribute additional <energy>.

The <alpha disk> prescription $\nu\sim\alpha c_sH$ summarizes an effective stress rather than a claim that molecular <viscosity> alone explains observed rates. In an ionized disk, <magnetorotational instability> can produce magnetic and fluid stresses. The inward evolution occurs on the <viscous timescale> $t_\nu\sim r^2/\nu$, much longer than an orbit for a thin disk with $\alpha\lesssim1$. Hot inner annuli and cooler outer annuli naturally give a range of emitted <temperatures>; the <blackbody> approximation additionally requires sufficient <optical depth>.

= Solution

A local thermal imbalance can amplify a gas perturbation even without an initial displacement of an interface. Let $\mathcal L(\rho,T)$ denote net cooling per unit <mass>, including heating with a negative sign, and suppose $\mathcal L(\rho_0,T_0)=0$. When the <sound-crossing time> is short compared with the thermal evolution time, <pressure> equilibrates and an <ideal gas> perturbation is approximately isobaric. The linearized thermal equation is
$$
c_p\frac{d\delta T}{dt}
=-\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial T}\right)_p\delta T.
$$
Thus a negative constant-pressure derivative produces <isobaric thermal instability>: a cooler parcel experiences a net loss that cools it further, while a warmer parcel heats further. For example, <isobaric thermal stability of a heated ideal gas> with $\mathcal L=A\rho T^\alpha-H$ gives $\sigma=(1-\alpha)H/(c_pT_0)$ and instability for $\alpha<1$. Different heating laws and fixed-density perturbations give different criteria; <thermal conduction> damps sufficiently short-scale <temperature> contrasts. Such feedback helps a gas separate into denser cool and rarer warm regions when stable thermal branches exist.

The <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> instead releases <gravitational potential energy> when a heavier fluid lies above a lighter fluid in a downward gravitational field. For two inviscid incompressible half-spaces with no <surface tension> or <magnetic field>, a horizontal perturbation of <wavenumber> $k$ has
$$
\sigma^2=\mathcal A gk,\qquad
\mathcal A=\frac{\rho_h-\rho_l}{\rho_h+\rho_l}>0.
$$
The <Atwood number> measures the density contrast. A descending heavy-fluid finger and an ascending light-fluid bubble both lower the total <gravitational potential energy>, increasing the original displacement. Interchanging the layers gives an oscillatory stable interface. An accelerating or decelerating astrophysical shell has an analogous effective acceleration, allowing mixing at suitable ejecta interfaces in a <supernova remnant>. Finite interface width, <viscosity>, <surface tension> and <magnetic tension> modify the short-wavelength behavior; the ideal sharp-interface formula does not select a finite fastest-growing wavelength.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For small isentropic perturbations of a fixed-composition <gas>, linearizing its <equation of state> gives $p_1=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\rho_1=c_s^2\rho_1$. The <adiabatic sound speed> is evaluated at the uniform background, so it is spatially constant at this order. If fast heat exchange instead enforces constant <temperature>, use the <isothermal sound speed> consistently. A completely arbitrary <entropy> perturbation would require an additional thermodynamic variable.

The uniform static infinite medium requires the <Jeans swindle>: its background gravitational field is subtracted or externally balanced, and only the perturbation sources the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>. Linearizing the <continuity equation>, <Euler equation> and gravitational equation then gives
$$
\partial_t\rho_1+\rho_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf u_1=0,\qquad
\partial_t\mathbf u_1=-\frac{c_s^2}{\rho_0}\nabla\rho_1-\nabla\Psi_1,\qquad
\nabla^2\Psi_1=4\pi G\rho_1.
$$
Taking one more time derivative of the first equation and substituting the other two yields
$$
\partial_t^2\rho_1=c_s^2\nabla^2\rho_1+4\pi G\rho_0\rho_1.
$$
A <Fourier mode> proportional to $e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}$ therefore obeys the <Jeans instability> relation
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=c_s^2k^2-4\pi G\rho_0
=c_s^2(k^2-k_J^2),\qquad k_J=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_0}}{c_s}.}
$$
For $k>k_J$, the mode oscillates as a <sound wave>. For $0<k<k_J$, $\omega=\pm i\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_0-c_s^2k^2}$ gives a growing and a decaying mode; the <static Jeans growth-rate spectrum> has no preferred finite fastest-growing wavelength in the infinite-medium approximation. The threshold $k=k_J$ is marginal at linear order. The <Jeans length> is $\lambda_J=2\pi/k_J=c_s\sqrt{\pi/(G\rho_0)}$. Defining the <Jeans mass> as a sphere whose diameter is this wavelength gives
$$
\boxed{M_J=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_0\left(\frac{\lambda_J}{2}\right)^3
=\frac{\pi^{5/2}}6\frac{c_s^3}{G^{3/2}\rho_0^{1/2}}.}
$$
Other radius conventions alter its numerical coefficient; the <mass> scale compares <self-gravity> with <pressure> communication.

For the pressureless finite cloud, the <shell theorem> gives an edge acceleration $\ddot r=-GM/r^2$, with fixed enclosed $M=4\pi r_0^3\rho_0/3$. Multiplication by $\dot r$ and integration from rest gives
$$
\frac{\dot r^2}{2}=GM\left(\frac1r-\frac1{r_0}\right),\qquad
\dot r=-\sqrt{2GM}\left(\frac1r-\frac1{r_0}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
The negative root describes collapse. Substituting $r=r_0\sin^2\theta$ gives the <free-fall time of a uniform sphere>
$$
t_{\rm ff}=\frac{2r_0^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2GM}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi r_0^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2GM}}
=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho_0}}}.
$$
The surface and inner shells reach the origin together in <pressureless homologous spherical collapse>; <pressure>, rotation or shell crossing would change this ideal solution.

Equating this time to the specified radius-based <sound-crossing time> $r_0/c_s$ gives $r_0=c_s\sqrt{3\pi/(32G\rho_0)}$. The resulting <crossing-time cloud support mass> is
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm cross}=C_{\rm cross}\frac{c_s^3}{G^{3/2}\rho_0^{1/2}},\qquad
C_{\rm cross}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\frac{3\pi}{32}\right)^{3/2}.}
$$
This is the same dimensional <mass> scale as $M_J$, with a different coefficient because the adopted length and time criteria differ. In particular, $t_{\rm ff}=r_0/c_s$ is not the exact marginal dispersion-relation condition.

With the requested isotropic support approximation, replace $c_s$ by the <Alfvén speed> $v_A=B/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho_0}$. In the crossing-time convention the <magnetic Jeans mass> is
$$
\boxed{M_{B,{\rm cross}}=C_{\rm cross}\frac{v_A^3}{G^{3/2}\rho_0^{1/2}}
=C_{\rm cross}\frac{B^3}{(\mu_0G)^{3/2}\rho_0^2}.}
$$
Using the wavelength-based <Jeans mass> convention instead replaces $C_{\rm cross}$ by $\pi^{5/2}/6$. The scaling, rather than a convention-independent coefficient, is the shared prediction.

For a fixed cloud <mass> undergoing isothermal homologous contraction, $\rho/\rho_0=(r_0/r)^3$ and $c_s$ is constant. Its number of thermal <Jeans masses> therefore grows as
$$
\boxed{N_J(\rho)=N_{J,0}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_0}\right)^{1/2}
=N_{J,0}\left(\frac{r_0}{r}\right)^{3/2}.}
$$
A cloud initially close to the support threshold has $N_{J,0}$ of order one, so smaller pieces can subsequently become independently unstable: this is <isothermal fragmentation>.

For approximately isotropic contraction with <magnetic flux freezing>, $Br^2$ stays constant, so $B\propto\rho^{2/3}$. Hence $M_B\propto B^3/\rho^2$ stays constant and
$$
\boxed{N_B(\rho)=N_{B,0}.}
$$
This ideal magnetic support therefore does not create progressively more unstable masses in the way thermal isothermal support does. The same conclusion follows from the conserved <mass-to-flux ratio>. A magnetically subcritical cloud cannot initiate unrestricted cross-field collapse under ideal flux freezing alone; flux loss or initially supercritical conditions are needed. A real <magnetic field> also gives anisotropic support: motion and fragmentation along its field lines are not excluded by this scalar-speed estimate.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <Mach number> is $\mathcal M=u/c_s$, comparing the flow speed relative to a disturbance with the local <sound speed>. For a body moving supersonically, spherical acoustic disturbances emitted earlier have a tangent envelope with <Mach cone> half-angle $\sin\theta=1/\mathcal M$. Signals cannot propagate arbitrarily far upstream. Compressive characteristics can converge and steepen into a <shock wave>, within which microscopic dissipation prevents a multivalued fluid state; supersonic motion alone does not require every flow to contain a shock.

Work in the <shock frame> with steady normal velocities $u_1,u_2>0$. Integrating <conservation of mass>, normal <momentum> and total <energy> over a thin pillbox, neglecting gravity across the thin layer, gives the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas>:
$$
j=\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2,\qquad
p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2,
$$
$$
j\left(h_1+\frac{u_1^2}{2}\right)
=j\left(h_2+\frac{u_2^2}{2}\right),\qquad
h=\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}.
$$
The <energy> equation includes <pressure> work through <specific enthalpy>. The shock is adiabatic if negligible <heat> escapes the layer, but its irreversible dissipation means it is not isentropic.

Put $\chi=\rho_2/\rho_1=u_1/u_2$ and $\mathcal M_1=u_1/\sqrt{\gamma p_1/\rho_1}$. <Momentum> conservation gives $p_2=p_1+\rho_1u_1^2(1-1/\chi)$. Substitution in the <energy> equation produces the unshocked root $\chi=1$ and the compressive root
$$
\chi=\frac{(\gamma+1)\mathcal M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)\mathcal M_1^2+2},\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1}=\frac{2\gamma\mathcal M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}.
$$
Since $\rho_1u_1^2/p_1=\gamma\mathcal M_1^2$, the strong-shock limit has negligible upstream thermal <pressure> compared with ram <pressure>. The <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{u_1}{u_2}\longrightarrow\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1},\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1}\simeq\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}\mathcal M_1^2.}
$$
For unchanged composition, the <ideal gas law> implies $T_2/T_1=(p_2/p_1)/\chi$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_2}{T_1}\simeq\frac{2\gamma(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}\mathcal M_1^2.}
$$
For $\gamma=5/3$, the compression tends to four and $T_2\simeq3\bar m u_1^2/(16k_B)$, where $\bar m$ is mean particle <mass>.

With constant specific heat $c_v$, <entropy production in a perfect-gas shock> is measured by the change in <specific entropy>
$$
\Delta s=c_v\ln\left[\frac{p_2}{p_1}\left(\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right)^\gamma\right].
$$
The strong-shock expression is therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta s\simeq c_v\ln\left[\frac{2\gamma\mathcal M_1^2}{\gamma+1}
\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\right)^\gamma\right].}
$$
The exact jump is positive for every compressive shock with $\mathcal M_1>1$, in accordance with the <second law of thermodynamics>; the asymptotic expression is only for $\mathcal M_1\gg1$. There is a normalization issue in the printed description: this logarithmic formula with constant $c_v$ describes <entropy> per unit <mass>. The true <entropy density> is $\rho s$, so its difference is $\rho_2s_2-\rho_1s_1$, rather than the same logarithmic jump with a constant <heat capacity> per unit <volume>. A material parcel's irreversible <entropy> increase should be assessed using $\Delta s$.

Downstream gas is denser and hotter. For optically thin collisional emission the loss per unit <volume> is typically $n_en_i\Lambda(T)$, whereas stored thermal <energy> scales approximately as $nT$. Compression can therefore reduce the <radiative gas cooling time>; excitation and ionization at the higher <temperature> can open additional cooling channels. The actual efficiency still depends on the <astrophysical cooling function>, composition and <optical depth>. In a radiative shock with separated scales, the <temperature> first rises across a narrow dissipative layer, then decreases through a much broader cooling region of characteristic length $\ell_{\rm cool}\sim u_2t_{\rm cool}$. Subsonic cooling usually brings further compression with approximately constant <pressure>. The immediate microscopic shock thickness and the macroscopic cooling length are distinct.

Let state $f$ be the fully cooled gas, with $T_f=T_1$ and the same composition. Set $a^2=p_1/\rho_1$, the <isothermal sound speed> squared. Across the entire shock plus cooling layer, <mass> and <momentum> are still conserved, but gas <energy> alone is not. With $\chi_f=\rho_f/\rho_1$,
$$
a^2+u_1^2=a^2\chi_f+\frac{u_1^2}{\chi_f},\qquad
(\chi_f-1)(a^2\chi_f-u_1^2)=0.
$$
The nontrivial <isothermal shock> therefore has
$$
\boxed{\rho_f=\rho_1\frac{u_1^2}{a^2}
=\gamma\mathcal M_1^2\rho_1.}
$$
If the upstream Mach number were defined with $a$ instead, this would be $\rho_f/\rho_1=\mathcal M_{\rm iso}^2$. Retaining the distinction avoids losing the factor $\gamma$.

The gas's <specific entropy> rises irreversibly at the hot shock and then decreases as escaping radiation removes heat. Since the final <temperature> equals the initial <temperature>,
$$
\boxed{s_f-s_1=-(\gamma-1)c_v\ln\chi_f<0.}
$$
This decrease for the gas is consistent with the <second law of thermodynamics>: the emitted radiation and its eventual surroundings carry <entropy> away. It does not make the microscopic compressive shock reversible. The figure illustrates both the hot <entropy> jump and the subsequent cooling; its spatial cooling profile is schematic because no cooling law has been specified.
