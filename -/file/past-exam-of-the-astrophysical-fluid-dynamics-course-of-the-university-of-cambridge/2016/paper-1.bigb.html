= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $z$ increase upwards and let $g>0$ denote the downward <gravitational acceleration>. The environment obeys <hydrostatic equilibrium>, $dp/dz=-\rho g$. Displace a small parcel by $\zeta$ while it remains in <pressure> balance with its surroundings and exchanges negligible heat. For fixed composition and <adiabatic index> $\gamma$, its density change at constant <specific entropy> satisfies $d\rho_{\rm par}/dp=\rho/(\gamma p)$. At the displaced position, its density excess over the environment is therefore
$$
\Delta\rho=\left(\frac{\rho}{\gamma p}\frac{dp}{dz}-\frac{d\rho}{dz}\right)\zeta+O(\zeta^2).
$$
The <buoyancy> acceleration is $\ddot\zeta\simeq-g\Delta\rho/\rho$. Hence
$$
\ddot\zeta=-N^2\zeta,\qquad N^2=\frac g\rho\left(\frac{\rho}{\gamma p}\frac{dp}{dz}-\frac{d\rho}{dz}\right).
$$
**Stability requires $N^2>0$**, equivalently
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho}{p\gamma}\frac{dp}{dz}>\frac{d\rho}{dz}}.
$$
An upward-displaced parcel is then denser than its surroundings and falls back; a downward-displaced parcel is lighter and rises. Equality gives neutral stability in this parcel model. For a <perfect gas> with constant composition, $ds=c_v\,d\ln(p/\rho^\gamma)$ gives $N^2=(g/c_p)\,ds/dz$, so a rising <specific entropy> is stable. This is the parcel form of the <Schwarzschild criterion>. Rapid <pressure> equilibration, negligible heat diffusion during displacement and no composition-driven <buoyancy> are the assumptions behind this test.

For a Newtonian <circular orbit>, $v_\phi^2=GM/r$, so the specific orbital <energy> is $e=v_\phi^2/2-GM/r=-GM/(2r)$. Half of the released potential <energy> remains in circular orbital motion. The binding-energy estimate between the specified edges is therefore
$$
L_{\rm bind}\simeq\frac{GM\dot m}{2}\left(\frac1{r_{\rm in}}-\frac1{r_{\rm out}}\right)=\frac{GM\dot m}{2R_s}\left(\frac16-\frac1{30}\right)=\frac{\dot m c^2}{30}.
$$
Using $M_\odot=1.98847\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and one Julian year $=3.15576\times10^7\,\mathrm s$ gives $\dot m=6.30\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. Thus **the radiative <luminosity> scale is $L\sim1.9\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W$**, approximately $1.9\times10^{45}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}$ or $4.9\times10^{11}L_\odot$. The black-hole <mass> cancels because both edge radii scale with $M$. The specified inner edge is $6R_s$; it must not be silently replaced with a different relativistic orbit radius.

For a finite viscous disc, the precise radiated power also depends on boundary work: <torque transport of energy in a steady accretion disk> means that orbital binding-energy release need not all be radiated inside the two stated edges. If the intended model is a radiatively efficient steady <Keplerian accretion disk> with a <zero-torque inner boundary condition>, integrate the one-face flux $F=3GM\dot m[1-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}/r}]/(8\pi r^3)$ over both faces. It gives
$$
L_{\rm diss}=\frac{GM\dot m}{2r_{\rm in}}\left[1-3\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm out}}+2\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm out}}\right)^{3/2}\right]\simeq\boxed{1.37\times10^{38}\,\mathrm W}.
$$
The difference from $L_{\rm bind}$ is the outward <torque> power at the outer edge. The question supplies neither boundary <torque> nor the external material-supply <energy> budget, so the two calculations state their assumptions explicitly: about $2\times10^{38}$ W is the binding-energy estimate, while $1.37\times10^{38}$ W is the finite-disc zero-inner-torque dissipation result. Both neglect <radiation> advection, winds and relativistic corrections as required for this model.<Dynamics of Astrophysical Discs, Lecture 5: Boundary Conditions and Steady Accretion by Gordon I. Ogilvie>[§5.2, printed pp.3–4: steady-disc surface flux and outward torque transport of energy]{ref}

Immediately upstream, free fall from rest at infinity gives $u_1^2=2GM_0/r_0$. Treat the surface shock as locally planar and stationary, with negligible upstream <pressure>. The <Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> for a <monatomic gas>, $\gamma=5/3$, give $\rho_2/\rho_1=4$ and $u_2=u_1/4$. <momentum conservation> then gives
$$
p_2=\rho_1u_1^2-\rho_2u_2^2=\frac34\rho_1u_1^2,\qquad\frac{p_2}{\rho_2}=\frac3{16}u_1^2.
$$
Use the source's gas-constant convention $p=\rho(R^*/\mu)T$. The immediate postshock <temperature> is
$$
\boxed{T_2=\frac\mu{R^*}\frac3{16}\frac{2GM_0}{r_0}=\frac\mu{R^*}\frac38\frac{GM_0}{r_0}}.
$$
Here adiabatic means that the jump does not lose appreciable heat to the surroundings; the shock is nevertheless irreversible and raises <entropy>. The gas still has <velocity> $u_1/4$ immediately downstream, so setting all upstream <kinetic energy> equal to downstream internal <energy> would give the wrong coefficient. Later settling, <radiative cooling> or changing ionization can alter the <temperature>; they are outside this immediate, fixed-composition perfect-gas jump.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> extracts <gravitational potential energy> when a heavy fluid is supported above a lighter one. A small downward excursion of heavy material and upward excursion of light material can grow instead of experiencing restoring <buoyancy>. The <Kelvin-Helmholtz instability> instead extracts <kinetic energy> from a <velocity> difference across an interface or <shear layer>. The interface displacement and its induced flow reinforce one another by tapping the background shear; a reversed density ordering is not required.

A useful common model has two inviscid, incompressible, semi-infinite fluids separated at $z=0$, with lower density/<velocity> $\rho_1,U_1$, upper density/<velocity> $\rho_2,U_2$, downward <gravity> $g$, and <surface tension> $\sigma$. For a disturbance proportional to $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, with $k>0$, the <two-fluid interfacial gravity-shear dispersion relation> is
$$
\left(\omega-k\frac{\rho_1U_1+\rho_2U_2}{\rho_1+\rho_2}\right)^2=\frac{gk(\rho_1-\rho_2)+\sigma k^3}{\rho_1+\rho_2}-\frac{\rho_1\rho_2}{(\rho_1+\rho_2)^2}k^2(U_1-U_2)^2.
$$
A negative right side gives exponential growth. With no shear or <surface tension> and $\rho_2>\rho_1$, the <growth rate> of the <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> is $s=\sqrt{\mathcal A gk}$, where $\mathcal A=(\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_2+\rho_1)$ is the <Atwood number>. With equal densities and no restoring force, the <growth rate> of the <Kelvin-Helmholtz instability> is $s=k|U_1-U_2|/2$. **Heavy-over-light stratification drives Rayleigh-Taylor growth; <velocity> shear drives Kelvin-Helmholtz growth.** A heavy lower fluid supplies gravitational stabilization, and <surface tension> stabilizes sufficiently short waves in this ideal interface model.

Finite shear-layer thickness, <viscosity>, compressibility and <magnetic tension> can change the <growth rates> and unstable <wavelength> range. In particular, unlimited growth with increasing $k$ is a limitation of the zero-thickness, zero-surface-tension idealization. Density-interface overturning and jet-boundary mixing are appropriate physical applications of the two mechanisms, but the nonlinear mixed state is not determined by the linear <growth rate> alone.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A finite-amplitude compression can steepen because different parts of the wave travel at different characteristic speeds. For a right-moving <simple wave> in a <perfect gas>, one may write $dx/dt=c_0+(\gamma+1)u/2$ in terms of the local <velocity> perturbation $u$. Faster compressive characteristics catch slower ones. When the smooth inviscid description would become multivalued, a physical compression shock replaces it. A rarefaction normally spreads rather than forming an entropy-violating expansion shock.<Lecture notes: Astrophysical fluid dynamics by Gordon I. Ogilvie>[§§6.1.3–6.2, simple-wave reduction and characteristic crossing]{ref}

Across a locally planar shock, use <velocities> relative to its moving front. The <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> conserve <mass>, momentum and total <energy> flux:
$$
\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2=j,\qquad p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2,\qquad h_1+\frac12u_1^2=h_2+\frac12u_2^2,
$$
where $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ is the <specific enthalpy>. For upstream normal <Mach number> $M_1>1$,
$$
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{(\gamma+1)M_1^2}{2+(\gamma-1)M_1^2},\quad\frac{p_2}{p_1}=\frac{2\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1},\quad M_2^2=\frac{1+(\gamma-1)M_1^2/2}{\gamma M_1^2-(\gamma-1)/2}.
$$
Thus a normal compression shock increases <pressure>, density and <temperature>, reduces the normal <velocity>, and changes a supersonic upstream flow to subsonic downstream flow in the <shock frame>. In the strong-shock limit, compression approaches $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which is four for a <monatomic gas>. **A shock conserves total <energy> but generates <entropy>; an adiabatic shock is not an isentropic compression.** For a calorically <perfect gas>, <stagnation temperature> is conserved while <stagnation pressure> falls.<Normal Shock Wave Equations by NASA Glenn Research Center>[normal-shock property ratios, total-temperature conservation and total-pressure loss]{ref}

Real dissipative processes resolve the narrow front; the jump conditions do not require its detailed internal profile. In a continuum-gas experiment a <shock tube> can produce a moving shock by releasing a <pressure> discontinuity. <pressure> probes separated along the tube measure arrival speed and the <pressure> jump; distinguishing the shock from the following contact surface is essential. A contact surface can have a density/<entropy> jump with continuous <pressure> and normal <velocity>, whereas a shock carries nonzero <mass> flux through its front.

A small <pressure> disturbance propagates because compression raises the <pressure>, the <pressure gradient> accelerates nearby fluid, and the induced convergence compresses the next region. For a uniform fluid at rest, linearizing <mass> and <momentum conservation> and using the adiabatic pressure-density response gives
$$
\frac{\partial\rho'}{\partial t}+\rho_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf u'=0,\qquad\rho_0\frac{\partial\mathbf u'}{\partial t}=-\nabla p',\qquad p'=c_s^2\rho',\qquad c_s^2=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\right)_s.
$$
Eliminating $\rho'$ and $\mathbf u'$ gives $\partial_t^2p'=c_s^2\nabla^2p'$. A <plane wave> has $\omega^2=c_s^2|\mathbf k|^2$ and longitudinal <velocity> parallel to $\mathbf k$. In a <perfect gas>,
$$
\boxed{c_s=\sqrt{\gamma p_0/\rho_0}=\sqrt{\gamma(R^*/\mu)T_0}}.
$$
The adiabatic response is appropriate when heat cannot diffuse appreciably out of each compressed region during a period; an isothermal response would give a different derivative. In a moving uniform medium, the acoustic characteristics travel at $U\pm c_s$ along the flow, rather than at $\pm c_s$ in the laboratory.

For a progressive wave, $p'=\rho_0c_su'_\parallel$; <pressure>, density and longitudinal <velocity> are in phase. The <acoustic energy density> is the sum of kinetic and compression energies,
$$
E_{\rm ac}=\frac12\rho_0|\mathbf u'|^2+\frac{p'^2}{2\rho_0c_s^2},\qquad\langle I\rangle=\frac{p_{\rm rms}^2}{\rho_0c_s}.
$$
**Sound transports <energy> while fluid elements execute small oscillations about their mean positions.** The ideal uniform-fluid acoustic mode is nondispersive. Spherical spreading reduces <pressure> amplitude as $1/r$ when total power is conserved; <viscosity> and <heat conduction> attenuate waves, while sufficiently large compressions can steepen into shocks. In a stratified astrophysical gas the uniform result is local: <wavelengths> short compared with background scale heights justify it, whereas long waves can couple to <buoyancy> and acoustic-cutoff behavior.

Let $e=c_vT$ be the <specific internal energy> of a calorically <perfect gas> and define its <specific gas constant> $\mathcal R=R^*/\mu$, so $p=\mathcal R\rho T$. A reversible adiabatic change has no heat input and obeys
$$
de+p\,d(1/\rho)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_v\,dT-\frac{\mathcal RT}{\rho}\,d\rho=0.
$$
Using $c_p-c_v=\mathcal R$ and $\gamma=c_p/c_v$ gives
$$
\frac{dT}{T}=(\gamma-1)\frac{d\rho}{\rho},\qquad d\ln p=d\ln\rho+d\ln T=\gamma\,d\ln\rho.
$$
Integration yields **$p/\rho^\gamma=K$, or $\boxed{p=K\rho^\gamma}$**, with $K$ fixed on that isentrope. Equivalently $s-s_{\rm ref}=c_v\ln[(p/\rho^\gamma)/K_{\rm ref}]$, so $K$ specifies <entropy> as well as composition. In a smooth nondissipative flow this means $D(p\rho^{-\gamma})/Dt=0$ along each parcel. A single common $K$ throughout the fluid additionally requires a <homentropic flow>. No heat transfer alone does not forbid <entropy> production: different sides of an adiabatic shock generally have different $K$ values. Constant composition and heat capacities are needed for the fixed exponent used here.

Write $\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf u$ and use the vector identity $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$. Taking the <curl> of the momentum equation eliminates all pure <gradients>, including <gravity> and the $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)$ viscous contribution. Before invoking barotropy, the <pressure> term gives
$$
\nabla\times\left(-\frac1\rho\nabla p\right)=-\nabla(1/\rho)\times\nabla p=\frac{\nabla\rho\times\nabla p}{\rho^2}.
$$
This is <baroclinic vorticity generation>. For a <barotropic fluid>, $p=p(\rho)$, so $\nabla p$ is parallel to $\nabla\rho$ and this source vanishes. Constant $\nu$ permits the <curl> and <Laplacian> to commute. The result is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol\omega}{\partial t}=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega)+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega}.
$$
No incompressibility assumption was used. Since $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=0$, an equivalent form is
$$
\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-\boldsymbol\omega\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf u+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
The terms represent <vortex stretching>/tilting, compression/dilatation and viscous diffusion. In particular, removing the baroclinic source does not make the <vorticity> of a three-dimensional parcel constant. A spatially varying entropy-dependent $K$ in $p=K\rho^\gamma$ need not define a single barotropic law; the common-pressure-law assumption matters.

= Solution

Use the idealized fully developed, laminar cylindrical-flow model implied by the fixed cross-section and zero <velocity> at its sides. Take the jet length to be $R$, as for a central source reaching the cloud boundary; if the source is off-centre, the actual path length must replace $R$. Let $s$ denote distance from the jet axis and write the axial <velocity> as $u(s)$. Then $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=0$, the <pressure gradient> is $dp/dz=-p_0/R$, and the axial <Navier-Stokes equation> reduces to
$$
0=\frac{p_0}{R}+\rho\nu\frac1s\frac d{ds}\left(s\frac{du}{ds}\right).
$$
Regularity at $s=0$ excludes a logarithmic term. The imposed <no-slip boundary condition> $u(r_0)=0$ gives the <Hagen-Poiseuille flow> profile
$$
u(s)=\frac{p_0}{4\rho\nu R}(r_0^2-s^2)=U_{\max}\left(1-\frac{s^2}{r_0^2}\right),\qquad U_{\max}=\frac{p_0r_0^2}{4\rho\nu R}.
$$
The <kinetic energy> crossing each area element per unit time is $(\rho u^2/2)u\,dA$, so the <velocity> must be cubed before integration. Thus
$$
P=\frac12\rho\int u^3\,dA=\pi\rho U_{\max}^3\int_0^{r_0}\left(1-\frac{s^2}{r_0^2}\right)^3s\,ds=\frac{\pi\rho U_{\max}^3r_0^2}{8}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{P=\frac{\pi p_0^3}{512\rho^2\nu^3R^3}\,r_0^8,\qquad A=\frac{\pi p_0^3}{512\rho^2\nu^3R^3}}.
$$
The <mean velocity> is $\bar u=U_{\max}/2$. Replacing the profile by $\bar u$ in $\tfrac12\rho\pi r_0^2\bar u^3$ underestimates the flux by two; the <kinetic energy correction factor> is two for this profile. The radius dependence follows from $u\propto r_0^2$ and cross-sectional area $\propto r_0^2$.

The specified $P$ is outgoing bulk kinetic-energy flux, not the pressure-work rate $p_0Q$, which scales as $r_0^4$. In a fully developed section the profile is independent of $z$ and <pressure> work is dissipated as heat; entrance acceleration and <energy> supply establish the profile. A turbulent or freely entraining astrophysical jet need not have this no-slip parabolic structure. The model assumes a long enough, laminar confined region and neglects entrance losses and <gravity>.

Write the polytropic law as $p=K\rho^{1+1/n}$, with constant $K$ and $n>0$. Its <pressure> potential, or <polytropic enthalpy> relative to zero density, is
$$
H(\rho)=\int_0^\rho\frac1{\widetilde\rho}\frac{dp}{d\widetilde\rho}\,d\widetilde\rho=(n+1)K\rho^{1/n}.
$$
Hydrostatic balance is $dH/dr=-d\psi/dr$. If the surface has zero density and potential $\psi_0$, integration gives $H=\psi_0-\psi$. At the centre, $\Delta\psi\equiv\psi_0-\psi_c=(n+1)K\rho_c^{1/n}>0$. Therefore
$$
\theta=\frac H{\Delta\psi}=\left(\frac\rho{\rho_c}\right)^{1/n},\qquad r=\alpha\xi,\qquad\alpha^2=\frac{\Delta\psi}{4\pi G\rho_c}.
$$
Insert $\psi=\psi_0-\Delta\psi\theta$ and $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$ into the spherical <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>:
$$
\frac1{r^2}\frac d{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\psi}{dr}\right)=4\pi G\rho\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right)=-\theta^n}.
$$
The central conditions are $\theta(0)=1$ and $\theta'(0)=0$; the latter follows from a regular spherically symmetric centre. This reproduces the supplied coordinate and potential scaling rather than introducing an unrelated normalization.

For the sound-speed task, the <adiabatic sound speed> of a perfect polytropic gas is
$$
c_s^2=\frac{dp}{d\rho}=\gamma K\rho^{\gamma-1}=(\gamma-1)H=(\gamma-1)(\psi_0-\psi),\qquad n=\frac1{\gamma-1}.
$$
A <diatomic gas> with active rotational but frozen vibrational degrees of freedom has $\gamma=7/5$ and $n=5/2$. With the requested surface-potential gauge $\psi_0=0$,
$$
\boxed{c_s=\sqrt{-\frac25\psi}}.
$$
The interior potential is then negative. Choosing zero potential at the surface is legitimate; for a finite isolated sphere it is a different additive gauge from setting potential to zero at infinity. Both choices cannot generally be imposed simultaneously.

For the mass-radius task, let $\xi_1$ be the first zero of the regular <Lane-Emden function> and $Y_n=-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)$. A finite-radius <polytrope> has
$$
R=\alpha\xi_1,\qquad M=4\pi\alpha^3\rho_cY_n,\qquad\frac{GM}{R}=\Delta\psi\frac{Y_n}{\xi_1}=(n+1)\frac{Y_n}{\xi_1}\frac{R^*T_c}{\mu}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{M=\frac{(n+1)Y_n}{\xi_1}\frac{R^*T_c}{G\mu}\,R}.
$$
**At fixed central <temperature>, fixed composition and fixed <polytropic index>, $M\propto R$.** These are <homologous polytropes at fixed central temperature>. They do not in general have constant $K$; imposing constant <entropy> instead gives a different polytropic mass-radius sequence. If $n$ or $\mu$ varies between stars, the displayed dimensionless structure factor and composition factor must be retained: equal $T_c$ alone is not a universal exact proportionality for arbitrary stellar models.

= Solution

Treat the gas as a smooth <quasi-one-dimensional flow> through a rigid passage, neglecting axial body forces and heat input. It is the confining medium that is incompressible; the flowing gas must remain compressible. With area $\mathcal A(x)$ and axial speed $u$, continuity and inviscid momentum balance give
$$
\rho u\mathcal A=\dot M,\qquad u\,du=-\frac{dp}{\rho},\qquad dp=c_s^2\,d\rho.
$$
Combining their logarithmic differentials yields the <nozzle area-velocity relation>
$$
\frac{d\mathcal A}{\mathcal A}=(\mathcal M^2-1)\frac{du}{u},\qquad\mathcal M=\frac u{c_s}.
$$
A smooth crossing of $\mathcal M=1$ requires an area extremum. The given radius is positive and has derivative $r'(x)=16r_0x\exp[-16x^2/L^2]/L^2$, which vanishes only at $x=0$ within the slab and changes from negative to positive there. Thus this station is the unique throat, with $r_s=r_0/2$ and $\mathcal A_s=\pi r_0^2/4$. The specified subsonic inlet and supersonic outlet select the smooth accelerating branch, so
$$
\boxed{x_s=0}.
$$
A throat by itself does not guarantee transonic flow for arbitrary reservoir and back <pressures>; that branch is specified here. Before the throat a converging section accelerates <subsonic flow>; beyond it a diverging section accelerates <supersonic flow>.<Nozzle Design by NASA Glenn Research Center>[Supersonic and Subsonic Flow, continuity/momentum derivation of the area-velocity relation and choked throat]{ref}

For the <temperature> task, evaluate the <mass> flux and <sound speed> at the throat using $p=K\rho^\gamma$:
$$
\dot M=\mathcal A_s\rho_sc_s=\mathcal A_s\sqrt{\gamma K}\,\rho_s^{(\gamma+1)/2},\qquad\rho_s=\left(\frac{\dot M}{\mathcal A_s\sqrt{\gamma K}}\right)^{2/(\gamma+1)}.
$$
The ideal-gas relation then gives
$$
\boxed{T_{x_s}=\frac{\mu K}{R^*}\left(\frac{4\dot M}{\pi r_0^2\sqrt{\gamma K}}\right)^{2(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)}}.
$$
This expresses the sonic <temperature> in terms of the <entropy> constant, geometry, <mass> flux and composition; no numerical value of $K$ or $\dot M$ is provided. Equivalently $c_s^{\gamma+1}=\gamma K(\dot M/\mathcal A_s)^{\gamma-1}$ and $T_s=\mu c_s^2/(\gamma R^*)$. The slab length drops out because the sonic thermodynamic state depends on the minimum area, not on the axial distance over which a lossless smooth <nozzle> contracts. As a check, conserved stagnation enthalpy gives $T_s=2T_{\rm stag}/(\gamma+1)$.<Mass Flow Choking by NASA Glenn Research Center>[equations 1–10 and 19, sonic mass-flow maximum and temperature/Mach relation]{ref}

The result assumes cross-sectional thermodynamic uniformity and negligible transverse <kinetic energy>. Exact multidimensional flow through a sharply varying passage can have a curved sonic surface; the radius profile alone does not solve that boundary-value problem.
