Assume a constant downward <gravitational acceleration> $g$, constant composition, and an <ideal gas law> $p=\rho\mathcal R T$, where $\mathcal R=k_B/(\mu m_u)$ is the gas constant per unit mass. Here $m_u$ is the <unified atomic mass unit>; this convention makes the specified <mean molecular weight> dimensionless. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dz}=-\rho g=-\frac{pg}{\mathcal R(T_0-\alpha z)},\qquad
\frac{d\ln p}{dz}=-\frac{g}{\mathcal R(T_0-\alpha z)}.
$$
Integrating from sea level yields
$$
\frac{p(z)}{p(0)}=\left(\frac{T_0-\alpha z}{T_0}\right)^{g/(\mathcal R\alpha)}.
$$
Dividing by the <temperature> ratio gives the <hydrostatic atmosphere with a constant temperature lapse rate>:
$$
\boxed{\rho(z)=\rho(0)\left(1-\frac{\alpha z}{T_0}\right)^{\mu m_ug/(k_B\alpha)-1}.}
$$
The model requires $T_0-\alpha z>0$ and is used here only within the troposphere.

Convert the <atmospheric lapse rate> to $\alpha=6.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{K\,m^{-1}}$. At $11\ \mathrm{km}$, $T=228.5\ \mathrm K$. With $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, $m_u\simeq1.66054\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $\mu=28$, the density exponent is $4.0825$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho(11\ \mathrm{km})}{\rho(0)}
=\left(\frac{228.5}{300}\right)^{4.0825}\simeq0.329.}
$$
The <mass density> is therefore approximately one third of its sea-level value in this model.

The original PDF has **absolute-value bars around both gradients**; they are missing from the transcription. The stability argument must respect the sign of the upward coordinate.

Displace a small parcel upward by $\xi$. Assume it remains in <pressure equilibrium> with its surroundings but exchanges negligible heat, so it follows an <adiabatic process>. For a perfect gas with <adiabatic index> $\gamma$, $T\propto p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}$ along that parcel's adiabat. Its <temperature> gradient is therefore
$$
T'_{\rm ad}=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\frac Tp\frac{dp}{dz}
=-\frac{g}{c_p},\qquad c_p=\frac{\gamma\mathcal R}{\gamma-1}.
$$
The last step uses <hydrostatic equilibrium>, and $c_p$ is the <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>. At its new height, the <temperature> difference between the parcel and the environment is $(T'_{\rm ad}-T')\xi$. At equal <pressure>, its relative density excess is the negative of its relative <temperature> excess. The upward <buoyancy> acceleration is thus
$$
\ddot\xi=\frac gT(T'_{\rm ad}-T')\xi=-N^2\xi,\qquad
N^2=\frac gT\left(\frac{dT}{dz}+\frac g{c_p}\right).
$$
This is the squared <Brunt–Väisälä frequency>. <Convective stability> requires $N^2>0$, or, in signed-gradient form,
$$
\boxed{\frac{dT}{dz}>\left(1-\frac1\gamma\right)\frac Tp\frac{dp}{dz}=-\frac g{c_p}.}
$$
For a <temperature> decreasing upward, this is exactly the PDF's <Schwarzschild criterion>
$$
\boxed{\left|\frac{dT}{dz}\right|<\left(1-\frac1\gamma\right)\frac Tp
\left|\frac{dp}{dz}\right|.}
$$
The absolute-value condition is also sufficient more generally, although a sufficiently steep <temperature> inversion remains stable even when that stronger condition fails. Reversing the signed inequality, as the bar-free transcription does, would instead describe an unstable <temperature> decrease.

Take incidence from A into B and first specify **<pressure> amplitude**. For a plane <acoustic wave> traveling normally to the interface, the <acoustic impedance> is $Z=\rho c_s$. A right-moving wave has <pressure> amplitude $P=ZU$, where $U$ is its fluid-velocity amplitude; a left-moving reflected wave has $P=-ZU$.

Let $P_i,P_r,P_t$ denote incident, reflected, and transmitted <pressure> amplitudes. Continuity of <pressure> and normal <velocity> at the interface gives
$$
P_i+P_r=P_t,\qquad \frac{P_i-P_r}{Z_A}=\frac{P_t}{Z_B}.
$$
Solving the two linear equations gives the <acoustic reflection and transmission at an interface> coefficients
$$
\frac{P_t}{P_i}=\frac{2Z_B}{Z_A+Z_B},\qquad
\frac{P_r}{P_i}=\frac{Z_B-Z_A}{Z_A+Z_B}.
$$
For the same perfect gas, take a common <adiabatic index> $\gamma$. The <adiabatic sound speed> satisfies $c_s^2=\gamma p/\rho$. The equal equilibrium <pressure> therefore implies $Z_B/Z_A=\sqrt{\rho_B/\rho_A}=\sqrt\delta$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{P_t}{P_i}=\frac{2\sqrt\delta}{1+\sqrt\delta}.}
$$
The word amplitude is otherwise ambiguous. For fluid <velocity>, or <displacement> at the same frequency, the corresponding answer is
$$
\boxed{\frac{U_t}{U_i}=\frac{\xi_t}{\xi_i}=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt\delta}.}
$$
These are consistent because <pressure> and <velocity> amplitudes differ by the impedance. As an energy check, the time-averaged <acoustic intensity> is $|P|^2/(2Z)$, so its transmission coefficient is $\mathcal T=4\sqrt\delta/(1+\sqrt\delta)^2\leq1$, even when the transmitted <pressure> amplitude exceeds the incident one.

Equal <mean molecular weight> alone does not logically guarantee equal $\gamma$. If the gases have different values of the <adiabatic index>, replace $\sqrt\delta$ in these formulas by $\sqrt{\gamma_B\delta/\gamma_A}$; a density-only answer requires the common-$\gamma$ assumption.

= Solution

A <plasma> contains mobile charged species whose collective electromagnetic response matters. On scales large compared with the <Debye length>, positive and negative charge densities nearly cancel: this is <quasineutrality>, not the absence of <electric currents> or <electric fields>. For electrons of <temperature> $T_e$ and number density $n_e$, the characteristic <plasma screening> and oscillation scales are
$$
\lambda_D=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0k_BT_e}{n_ee^2}},\qquad
\omega_{pe}=\sqrt{\frac{n_ee^2}{\epsilon_0m_e}}.
$$
A collective <plasma> description requires many particles within a screening volume; long-wavelength, sufficiently slow behavior can often be described by <magnetohydrodynamics> with an appropriate fluid closure.

An individual particle feels the <Lorentz force> $q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$. In a uniform <magnetic field>, its transverse motion gyrates while its parallel motion remains free, giving a helical path when both components are present. The nonrelativistic <cyclotron frequency> and <gyroradius> are
$$
\Omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad r_L=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B}.
$$
When the system scale is much larger than $r_L$ and the relevant time scale longer than $\Omega_c^{-1}$, the field strongly organizes transport across it, provided <collisions> do not interrupt the gyromotion. A perpendicular <electric field> also gives the common guiding-centre drift $\mathbf v_E=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$.

For a conducting fluid, the macroscopic <Lorentz force density> has the useful decomposition
$$
\mathbf J\times\mathbf B=-\boldsymbol\nabla\left(\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)
+\frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\mathbf B}{\mu_0}.
$$
These terms are <magnetic pressure> and <magnetic tension>. Magnetic tension resists bending, while magnetic-pressure gradients resist nonuniform compression. The <plasma beta> $\beta=2\mu_0p/B^2$ compares thermal and magnetic stresses.

With uniform <magnetic diffusivity> $\eta_m$, the <resistive induction equation> is
$$
\partial_t\mathbf B=\boldsymbol\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)+\eta_m\nabla^2\mathbf B.
$$
If the <magnetic Reynolds number> $R_m=UL/\eta_m\gg1$, <ideal magnetohydrodynamics> applies approximately and <magnetic flux freezing> links the field geometry to fluid motion. Finite diffusivity permits field slippage and <magnetic reconnection>. Field tension supports <Alfvén waves> with $v_A=B/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}$; coupled magnetic and gas compression also supports <magnetosonic waves>. These effects allow a field to transmit forces and <angular momentum> between distant parts of an astrophysical <plasma>.

An <accretion disk> forms when infalling material has enough <angular momentum> to orbit before it can reach the central object. In a thin disc dominated by a central <mass> $M$, the orbital motion is approximately <Keplerian rotation>:
$$
\Omega_K=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R^3}},\qquad
\ell=R^2\Omega_K=\sqrt{GMR}.
$$
Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives the <disk scale height> $H\sim c_s/\Omega_K$; a geometrically thin disc has $H/R\ll1$. The <surface density> is $\Sigma=\int\rho\,dz$, and a steady inward <mass flow rate> is $\dot M=-2\pi R\Sigma v_R>0$.

Moving inward requires losing specific <angular momentum>. Internal stresses transport <angular momentum> outward; much of the mass can then accrete while an outer fraction spreads to larger radius. Molecular <viscosity> alone is generally inadequate for rapid astrophysical accretion. Turbulent and magnetic stresses, including those generated by the <magnetorotational instability>, provide effective transport. A common parametrization is $\nu=\alpha c_sH$ for an effective <kinematic viscosity>, with $\alpha$ a dimensionless stress parameter.

For a thin <Keplerian accretion disk>, the evolution equation is
$$
\frac{\partial\Sigma}{\partial t}
=\frac3R\frac\partial{\partial R}\left[R^{1/2}\frac\partial{\partial R}
\left(\nu\Sigma R^{1/2}\right)\right].
$$
This <viscous evolution of an accretion disk> occurs on the <viscous timescale> $t_\nu\sim R^2/\nu$, much longer than an orbital period in a thin disc with moderate effective stress.

The orbital specific <energy> is $-GM/(2R)$, so inward motion releases binding energy. In a steady, optically thick, radiatively efficient disc with zero torque at its inner edge, the emergent flux from **each face** is
$$
\boxed{F(R)=\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm eff}^4
=\frac{3GM\dot M}{8\pi R^3}\left[1-\left(\frac{R_{\rm in}}R\right)^{1/2}\right].}
$$
The <Stefan–Boltzmann law> identifies the local effective <temperature>. Far from the inner boundary, $T_{\rm eff}\propto R^{-3/4}$, so the emitted spectrum combines hotter inner and cooler outer annuli. Integrating both faces gives $L_{\rm disc}=GM\dot M/(2R_{\rm in})$ under these assumptions. This is not a universal spectrum or efficiency: optical depth, inner torque, energy advection and outflows can change the radiation. The essential connection is **outward angular-momentum transport, inward accretion, and release of binding energy**.

= Solution

A <stellar polytrope> approximates a spherical self-gravitating star by the structural relation
$$
p=K\rho^{1+1/n},
$$
where $K$ is constant and $n$ is the <polytropic index>. Combining <hydrostatic equilibrium> with <mass conservation> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{Gm(r)\rho}{r^2},\qquad
\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho.
$$
The <Lane-Emden variables for a stellar polytrope> separate the dimensional scales from the profile:
$$
\rho=\rho_c\theta^n,\quad r=a\xi,\qquad
 a^2=\frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G}\rho_c^{1/n-1}.
$$
For $n>0$ these substitutions give the <Lane-Emden equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\frac{d\theta}{d\xi}\right)=-\theta^n,\qquad
\theta(0)=1,\quad\theta'(0)=0.}
$$
The central conditions express finite central <mass density> and spherical symmetry. If $\xi_1$ is the first zero, a zero-pressure surface has
$$
R=a\xi_1,\qquad
M=4\pi a^3\rho_c[-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)],
$$
the <Lane-Emden mass formula>. For $0<n<5$ this surface is at finite radius. For example, the <polytrope of index one> has $\theta=\sin\xi/\xi$ and $\xi_1=\pi$, giving the density profile shown in the overview figure.

An isentropic ideal monatomic gas has $p\propto\rho^{5/3}$ and hence $n=3/2$, a useful approximation for a fully convective ideal-gas star. Nonrelativistic <electron degeneracy pressure> has the same density exponent. The exponent $4/3$, giving $n=3$, occurs for ultrarelativistic electron degeneracy and in appropriate radiation-dominated stellar models. The structural index should not be confused with the <adiabatic index> controlling a perturbation unless the model is actually isentropic.

Polytropes give useful mass-radius scalings and central concentration with very few parameters. They do not by themselves specify <nuclear fusion>, energy transport, or the star's thermal evolution. Nor does a structural profile alone establish dynamical stability; that requires the response of <pressure> to the perturbation.

Work in the rest frame of a thin planar <shock wave>, with normal upstream and downstream velocities $u_1,u_2>0$. Assume a steady flow, negligible gravitational change across its thickness, no radiative energy loss, and negligible viscous stress and heat flux at the two remote faces of a control volume. Dissipation within the shock is permitted and is essential to the physical discontinuity.

Integrating <mass conservation> through that volume gives equal <mass flux> per unit area:
$$
\boxed{\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2=j.}
$$
The dimensions of $j$ are $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$. The normal momentum entering minus that leaving must balance the <pressure> forces. Equivalently, the conserved normal <momentum flux> is $p+\rho u^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2.}
$$
For <conservation of energy>, matter advects kinetic and internal energy while <pressure> performs work at rate $pu$ per unit area. Writing $\epsilon$ for <specific internal energy>, the <energy flux> is $\rho u(u^2/2+\epsilon)+pu$. With <specific enthalpy> $h=\epsilon+p/\rho$, its conservation gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{u_1^2}{2}+h_1=\frac{u_2^2}{2}+h_2.}
$$
The division by $j$ is valid for a genuine through-flow shock. Together these are the three <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions>. The conserved <energy flux> has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, and both momentum fluxes have units of <pressure>.

For a calorically perfect <ideal gas>, $\epsilon=p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ and $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$. The three jump conditions can be combined explicitly. Put $r=\rho_2/\rho_1$, so $u_2=u_1/r$ and
$$
p_2=p_1+\rho_1u_1^2(1-r^{-1}).
$$
Substitute these into the enthalpy equation and use $\mathcal M_1^2=\rho_1u_1^2/(\gamma p_1)$, with the upstream <adiabatic sound speed>. Besides the trivial no-shock root $r=1$, the physical compression root is
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{(\gamma+1)\mathcal M_1^2}{(\gamma-1)\mathcal M_1^2+2},\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1}=\frac{2\gamma\mathcal M_1^2-(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}.}
$$
For unchanged composition, $T_2/T_1=(p_2/p_1)/r$. A compressive shock has $\mathcal M_1>1$ and increases the <specific entropy>. Thus an adiabatic shock conserves total energy but is not an <isentropic process>.

= Solution

Interpret $v$ as the normal upstream speed in the shock rest frame and $\rho$ as its upstream <mass density>. If the quoted jet speed is in another frame, the shock speed must first be supplied to obtain this relative <velocity>. Use a calorically perfect gas of mean particle <mass> $\bar m=\mu m_u$.

In the strong-shock limit, $\mathcal M_1\gg1$. The <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give
$$
\rho_2=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\rho,\qquad
u_2=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}v,\qquad
p_2\simeq\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\rho v^2.
$$
The <pressure> expression also follows from the supplied ratio by inserting $\mathcal M_1^2=\rho v^2/(\gamma p_1)$. The <ideal gas law> then gives the immediate postshock <temperature>
$$
\boxed{T_2\simeq\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma+1)^2}\frac{\mu m_u}{k_B}v^2.}
$$
The upstream thermal contribution is negligible at this order. This is a <temperature> in kelvin, rather than a <temperature> change per unit time.

To turn the postshock thermal energy into a rate, the processed mass per unit time is $\dot m=\rho vS$. Its downstream <specific internal energy> is
$$
\epsilon_2=\frac{k_BT_2}{(\gamma-1)\mu m_u}
=\frac{2v^2}{(\gamma+1)^2}.
$$
Thus the **thermal internal energy carried into the shocked gas per unit time** is
$$
\boxed{\dot E_{\rm int}=\dot m\epsilon_2
=\frac{2}{(\gamma+1)^2}\rho Sv^3.}
$$
Per unit area, divide this expression by $S$. Its dimensions are watts; the <mean molecular weight> cancels because the heat capacity per unit mass is inversely proportional to it, while $T_2$ is proportional to it.

There is a second common convention for shock heating power: the loss of directed bulk <kinetic energy> flux. The <energy fluxes of a strong adiabatic shock> give
$$
\boxed{\dot E_{\rm kin,loss}=\frac{\dot m}{2}(v^2-u_2^2)
=\frac{2\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^2}\rho Sv^3
=\dot m h_2.}
$$
This equals the downstream <enthalpy> flux, and includes <pressure> work as well as the advected internal energy. For $\gamma=5/3$, the compression ratio is $4$, $T_2=3\mu m_uv^2/(16k_B)$, $\dot E_{\rm int}=9\rho Sv^3/32$, and $\dot E_{\rm kin,loss}=15\rho Sv^3/32$. Specifying the energy convention avoids conflating these rates. A rate $dT/dt$ for a fixed slab cannot be inferred without its participating mass and thermal evolution; the supplied data fix the postshock <temperature> and the processed energy fluxes.

Let $a_T^2=k_BT/(\mu m_u)$ be the constant <isothermal sound speed> squared. The stationary galactic gas obeys <hydrostatic equilibrium> in the specified <gravitational potential>:
$$
a_T^2\frac{d\rho}{dr}=-\rho\frac{d\psi}{dr},\qquad
\frac{d\ln\rho}{dr}=-\frac{1}{a_T^2}\frac{d\psi}{dr}.
$$
Integrating from the pressure-matching radius $r_0$ gives $\rho(r)=\rho(r_0)\exp[-(\psi(r)-\psi(r_0))/a_T^2]$. Since $\rho(r_0)=p_0/a_T^2$ and $\psi=-Ar^{-1/2}$,
$$
\boxed{\rho(r)=\frac{\mu m_up_0}{k_BT}
\exp\!\left[\frac{A\mu m_u}{k_BT}
\left(r^{-1/2}-r_0^{-1/2}\right)\right],\qquad0<r\leq r_0.}
$$
The exponent is dimensionless: the quantity in parentheses times $A$ is a difference of <gravitational potential>, of units $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}$. Density increases inward, as expected. The idealized potential is singular at the centre, so this formal profile should not be extrapolated into a region where the prescribed potential or the neglect of gas self-gravity fails.

= Solution

The smaller galaxy is treated as a <test particle> released from rest at $r_0$. <Conservation of energy> in the fixed <gravitational potential> gives
$$
\frac12\dot d^2+\psi(d)=\psi(r_0),\qquad
\dot d^2=2A\left(d^{-1/2}-r_0^{-1/2}\right).
$$
The galactic gas is stationary in its hydrostatic model, so this is the relative speed squared. Write $\Delta(d)=d^{-1/2}-r_0^{-1/2}\geq0$ on the inward path. The astrophysical <ram pressure> is the incident normal momentum flux $\rho(d)\dot d^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm ram}(d)=\frac{2A\mu m_up_0}{k_BT}\,\Delta(d)
\exp\!\left[\frac{A\mu m_u}{k_BT}\Delta(d)\right],\qquad0<d\leq r_0.}
$$
It vanishes at release and increases during infall. This <ram pressure> convention is $\rho v^2$, not the $\rho v^2/2$ sometimes called dynamic <pressure>. Gas drag and the object's response are neglected consistently with the prescribed ballistic motion.

Assume an inviscid <barotropic fluid> acted on by a conservative body force $-\boldsymbol\nabla\psi$. Define its <barotropic enthalpy function> by
$$
H(\rho)=\int^{\rho}\frac{p'(s)}s\,ds,\qquad
\boldsymbol\nabla H=\frac{\boldsymbol\nabla p}{\rho}.
$$
The <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> are then $\partial_t\mathbf v+(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\mathbf v=-\boldsymbol\nabla(H+\psi)$. Using the <vorticity> $\boldsymbol\omega=\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf v$ and the vector identity
$$
(\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\mathbf v
=\boldsymbol\nabla(v^2/2)-\mathbf v\times\boldsymbol\omega
$$
gives $\partial_t\mathbf v+\boldsymbol\nabla B=\mathbf v\times\boldsymbol\omega$, where $B=v^2/2+H+\psi$.

For **steady flow**, dotting this equation with $\mathbf v$ yields $\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla B=0$. Thus <Bernoulli's equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{v^2}{2}+\int^{\rho}\frac{p'(s)}s\,ds+\psi
=\text{constant along each streamline}.}
$$
The constant need not agree between different <streamlines>. If the flow is also <irrotational>, the vector equation gives $\boldsymbol\nabla B=0$, so one common constant applies throughout a connected flow region.

For an <isentropic process> with $p=K\rho^\gamma$, $H=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ up to an additive constant, equal to the thermodynamic <specific enthalpy>. For an externally maintained isothermal gas, $H=a^2\ln(\rho/\rho_{\rm ref})$; this <pressure> potential is not the constant thermodynamic enthalpy of a fixed-temperature ideal gas.

If steady flow is not assumed, a useful additional case is unsteady <potential flow> $\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\nabla\phi$. The same vector equation integrates spatially to
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\phi+\frac{v^2}{2}+H+\psi=C(t).}
$$
General unsteady barotropic flow does not possess the steady streamline constant without further conditions.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The surface must be a **material surface $S(t)$**, moving with the fluid; its boundary moves with it as well. The stated <barotropic vorticity transport> law, with $\boldsymbol\omega=\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf v$, gives $\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=0$. Expanding its curl therefore yields
$$
\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}
=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\mathbf v
-\boldsymbol\omega(\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v).
$$
A material tangent vector $\mathbf t$ obeys $D\mathbf t/Dt=(\boldsymbol\nabla\mathbf v)\mathbf t$. Apply the <product rule> to the <cross product> of two such tangents to obtain <material surface element evolution>:
$$
\frac{D(d\mathbf S)}{Dt}
=\left[(\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)I-(\boldsymbol\nabla\mathbf v)^{\mathsf T}\right]d\mathbf S.
$$
Here $(\boldsymbol\nabla\mathbf v)_{ij}=\partial_jv_i$. The stretching and area-change terms cancel exactly in the transported flux element:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac D{Dt}(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot d\mathbf S)
&=\big[(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\mathbf v
-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\big]\cdot d\mathbf S\\
&\quad+\boldsymbol\omega\cdot
\big[(\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf v)I-(\boldsymbol\nabla\mathbf v)^{\mathsf T}\big]d\mathbf S
=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Integrate over the fixed particle labels parameterizing the material surface. This proves the <conservation of vorticity flux through a material surface>:
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\int_{S(t)}\boldsymbol\omega\cdot d\mathbf S=0.}
$$
The conserved flux is also the <circulation> $\oint_{\partial S(t)}\mathbf v\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$ by the <Stokes theorem>, so this agrees with <Kelvin's circulation theorem>. The assumptions are smooth inviscid barotropic flow and conservative body forces. A fixed open surface generally exchanges vorticity flux with its surroundings and does not satisfy this conservation statement; compressibility by itself does not invalidate the material-surface result.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Assume a steady spherical outflow of fixed composition. Let $\bar m$ be its mean particle <mass>, and define the constant <isothermal sound speed> by $a^2=k_BT_0/\bar m$. Subsonic here refers to this isothermal closure, so $0<u<a$. <Mass conservation> and radial momentum balance give
$$
\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u,\qquad
u\frac{du}{dr}=-a^2\frac{d\ln\rho}{dr}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
Logarithmically differentiating the first equation yields $d\ln\rho/dr=-2/r-u'/u$. Substitution eliminates the density gradient and gives the <isothermal Parker wind> equation
$$
\left(u-\frac{a^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}
=\frac{2a^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
At a smooth speed maximum, $u'=0$, while the left prefactor is finite and negative. The right side must vanish, hence
$$
\boxed{r_M=\frac{GM}{2a^2}=\frac{GM\bar m}{2k_BT_0}.}
$$
For $r<r_M$ both the right side and the prefactor are negative, so $u'>0$; for $r>r_M$, $u'<0$. This is a true maximum on a <subsonic isothermal breeze>, not the accelerating <transonic branch>.

There is an underdetermination in the requested density estimate. Introduce the peak <Mach number> $\mathcal M_M=u_M/a$, where $0<\mathcal M_M<1$. The <density at the maximum speed of an isothermal breeze> is exactly
$$
\boxed{\rho_M=\frac{\dot M}{4\pi r_M^2u_M}
=\frac{\dot M a^3}{\pi G^2M^2\mathcal M_M}
=\frac{\dot M}{\pi G^2M^2\mathcal M_M}
\left(\frac{k_BT_0}{\bar m}\right)^{3/2}.}
$$
The hypotheses do not fix $\mathcal M_M$. A boundary density or <pressure>, or an equivalent wind integration constant, is needed. The definite statement following from subsonicity is the lower bound
$$
\boxed{\rho_M>\rho_{\rm char},\qquad
\rho_{\rm char}=\frac{\dot M}{\pi G^2M^2}
\left(\frac{k_BT_0}{\bar m}\right)^{3/2}.}
$$
If a maximum speed of order $a$ is intended as an extra estimate, then $\rho_M\sim\rho_{\rm char}\propto\dot M T_0^{3/2}M^{-2}$. A very slow breeze can have a much larger density, so setting $u_M=a$ as an exact equality would contradict the stated always-subsonic assumption. The mean particle mass is another implicit fixed-composition input needed to express a <temperature> as a squared sound speed.

One can see the free parameter directly by integrating the wind equation:
$$
\frac{u^2}{2}-a^2\ln\frac ua
=2a^2\ln\frac r{r_M}+\frac{GM}{r}+C.
$$
Different $C$ give different subsonic maxima at the same radius. The original plot illustrates this nonuniqueness. These breeze branches require nonzero ambient <pressure> at large radius; they are not the usual transonic escape into a low-pressure exterior.

For an unattenuated isotropic point source, the radiation flux is $L_*/(4\pi r^2)$. The <radiation pressure> force per particle divided by its mass $\bar m$ gives the outward acceleration
$$
a_{\rm rad}=\frac{\sigma L_*}{4\pi\bar m c\,r^2}.
$$
Thus the effective inward inverse-square coefficient is $GM-\sigma L_*/(4\pi\bar m c)$. Insert it into the preceding wind equation. The <radiative shift of an isothermal breeze maximum> is
$$
\boxed{r'_M=\frac{GM-\sigma L_*/(4\pi\bar m c)}{2a^2}
=\frac{GM\bar m-\sigma L_*/(4\pi c)}{2k_BT_0}.}
$$
Equivalently, with <opacity> $\kappa=\sigma/\bar m$ and an <Eddington luminosity> $L_{\rm Edd}=4\pi GMc/\kappa$, this is $r'_M=r_M(1-L_*/L_{\rm Edd})$. It is an exterior speed maximum only if the radius is positive and lies within the modeled wind region. For $L_*\geq L_{\rm Edd}$, the wind equation's right side is positive at every $r>0$, so an always-subsonic outflow decreases in speed and has no such interior maximum.

Because $\sigma$ is a cross section **per particle**, its conversion to acceleration requires $\bar m$; that mass cannot be omitted dimensionally. The requested dependence on the four stated variables therefore assumes a fixed particle species. For the atomic hydrogen of part (e), set $\bar m\simeq m_H$ before molecule formation.

Retain the preceding **isothermal** assumption, so the gas <temperature> remains $T_0$. When two neutral <hydrogen> atoms become one <molecular hydrogen> molecule, the mean particle mass doubles. At fixed <mass density> and <temperature> the <ideal gas law> would halve the <pressure>, but the flowing gas is free to compress and change speed. It cannot in general keep density, <pressure>, and <velocity> all unchanged.

To describe a thin steady transition, label the atomic and molecular sides by $1$ and $2$ and neglect the integrated external force and radiative recoil across its small thickness. Local <mass conservation> and <momentum conservation> require
$$
j=\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2,\qquad
p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2.
$$
The <isothermal sound speed> changes from $a_1^2=k_BT_0/m_H$ to $a_2^2=a_1^2/2$. Dividing the momentum flux by $j$ gives $u_2+a_2^2/u_2=J$, with $J=u_1+a_1^2/u_1$. The subsonic continuation is therefore
$$
u_2=\frac{J-\sqrt{J^2-2a_1^2}}2,\qquad
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}=\frac{u_1}{u_2},\qquad
p_2-p_1=j(u_1-u_2)>0.
$$
Thus the gas becomes denser and slower. In the well-subsonic limit, where ram stresses are small compared with thermal <pressure>, the useful leading description of <isothermal flow through molecular hydrogen formation> is
$$
\boxed{T_2=T_1,\qquad \rho_2\simeq2\rho_1,\qquad
u_2\simeq\frac{u_1}{2},\qquad p_2\simeq p_1.}
$$
The exact momentum condition gives a small <pressure> increase at low Mach number, rather than an imposed discontinuous factor-of-two <pressure> drop. The full expressions above apply when the subsonic speed is not asymptotically small.

Radiation of the molecular binding energy prevents that released energy from automatically heating the gas. It does **not**, by itself, determine the <temperature>: the constant-temperature result uses the inherited isothermal closure. If that assumption is relaxed, an energy equation including cooling and molecular degrees of freedom is needed; the wording about emitted binding energy alone does not uniquely determine all four changes.
