= Solution
{parent=i}

The <image charge for a grounded conducting sphere> is obtained by the <Kelvin transform>:
$$
\boxed{q^\star=-\frac{a}{r_0}q,\qquad \mathbf r^\star=\frac{a^2}{r_0^2}\mathbf r_0=\left(0,0,\frac{a^2}{r_0}\right).}
$$
It lies inside the <electrical conductor>, outside the region where it represents the physical <electric field>. The <electric potential> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf r)=\begin{cases}
0,&r\leq a,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac q{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|}-\frac{qa/r_0}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^\star|}\right),&r>a,\quad\mathbf r\ne\mathbf r_0.
\end{cases}}
$$
At the physical <point charge>, the <electric potential> has its usual <Coulomb's law> singularity. There is no physical singularity at the <image charge>: the interior expression is identically zero.

For $|\mathbf r|=a$, direct expansion of squared distances gives
$$
|\mathbf r-\mathbf r^\star|^2
=a^2+\frac{a^4}{r_0^2}-\frac{2a^2}{r_0^2}\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r_0
=\frac{a^2}{r_0^2}|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^2.
$$
Consequently the two terms cancel exactly, proving the zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> on the sphere. At infinity each term is $O(r^{-1})$, and more explicitly
$$
\phi(\mathbf r)=\frac{q(1-a/r_0)}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}+O(r^{-2})\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the <Dirichlet boundary condition> at infinity also holds. The candidate solves the <Poisson equation> with the one physical <point charge>; <uniqueness of Poisson equation>, with these <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, identifies it as the required <electric potential>.

Let $\mathbf n=\mathbf r/a$ be the <unit normal> from the <electrical conductor> into the <vacuum>. The <electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor>, together with the vanishing interior <electric field>, give
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm out}=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\mathbf n,\qquad
\sigma=\epsilon_0\mathbf E_{\rm out}\cdot\mathbf n=-\epsilon_0\left.\partial_r\phi\right|_{a^+}.}
$$
Write $u=\cos\theta$ in <spherical coordinates>, $D=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$ on $r=a$, and $z^\star=a^2/r_0$. Differentiating the <electric potential> at fixed angles gives
$$
\left.\partial_r\phi\right|_{a^+}
=\frac q{4\pi\epsilon_0D^3}\left[-(a-r_0u)+\frac{r_0^2}{a^2}\left(a-\frac{a^2}{r_0}u\right)\right]
=\frac q{4\pi\epsilon_0D^3}\left(\frac{r_0^2}{a}-a\right).
$$
Here the distance identity in the preceding answer was used before simplifying. Hence the induced <surface charge density> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(\mathbf r)=\frac{q(a-r_0^2/a)}{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^3},\qquad |\mathbf r|=a.}
$$
For positive $q$ this <surface charge density> is negative everywhere, with largest magnitude nearest the <point charge>.

Integrate the <surface charge density> using the <surface integral> in <spherical coordinates>:
$$
Q=2\pi a^2\frac{q(a-r_0^2/a)}{4\pi}
\int_{-1}^1\frac{du}{(a^2+r_0^2-2ar_0u)^{3/2}}.
$$
Since $r_0>a$, the elementary integral is
$$
\frac1{ar_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{a^2+r_0^2-2ar_0u}}\right]_{-1}^1
=\frac1{ar_0}\left(\frac1{r_0-a}-\frac1{r_0+a}\right)
=\frac2{r_0(r_0^2-a^2)}.
$$
Substitution yields the total induced <electric charge>
$$
\boxed{Q=-\frac{qa}{r_0}=q^\star.}
$$
The equality with the <image charge> also follows by applying <Gauss's law> to the exterior <electric field>: the physical and fictitious descriptions give the same <electric flux> through a sphere enclosing the conductor but excluding the physical <point charge>.

Put $k=b/a>1$ and $\mathbf e_z=(0,0,1)$. The complete construction of <image charges between concentric grounded spheres> consists of two families, indexed by every integer:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
q_n&=qk^n,&\mathbf r_n&=r_0k^{2n}\mathbf e_z,\\
\widetilde q_n&=-\frac{qa}{r_0}k^n,&\widetilde{\mathbf r}_n&=\frac{a^2}{r_0}k^{2n}\mathbf e_z,
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The term $(q_0,\mathbf r_0)$ is the physical <point charge>; every other term is an <image charge>. In the first family $n\leq-1$ lies inside $r=a$ and $n\geq1$ lies outside $r=b$. In the second family $n\leq0$ lies inside $r=a$ and $n\geq1$ lies outside $r=b$. Thus no <image charge> lies in the physical gap.

To derive and verify these families, reflection of an axial <point charge> $(Q,z)$ in a grounded sphere of radius $R$ replaces it by the <image charge> $(-RQ/z,R^2/z)$. Reflection in $a$ sends $(q_n,\mathbf r_n)$ to $(\widetilde q_{-n},\widetilde{\mathbf r}_{-n})$; reflection in $b$ sends it to $(\widetilde q_{1-n},\widetilde{\mathbf r}_{1-n})$. Successive reflections therefore generate exactly the listed families. The <method of images> gives
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf r)=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}
\left(\frac{qk^n}{|\mathbf r-r_0k^{2n}\mathbf e_z|}
-\frac{(qa/r_0)k^n}{|\mathbf r-(a^2/r_0)k^{2n}\mathbf e_z|}\right),\qquad a<r<b.}
$$
The physical <point charge> itself is excluded when evaluating this formula. On $r=a$, pair the first-family term $n$ with the second-family term $-n$; their <electric potentials> cancel by the single-sphere distance identity. On $r=b$, pair $n$ with $1-n$ instead. These reindexings are justified by <absolute convergence>, as follows.

For $n\to-\infty$, both source positions tend to the origin while their <electric charges> have size $O(k^n)$. On the closed gap their distances are eventually at least $a/2$, so both <electric potential> terms are $O(k^n)$. For $n\to+\infty$, both distances are at least a positive constant times $k^{2n}$ and the <electric charges> are $O(k^n)$, giving $O(k^{-n})$ terms. Both tails are bounded by convergent <geometric series>. This proves <absolute convergence> and <uniform convergence> of the tails on the closed gap. On any compact set avoiding the physical <point charge>, the differentiated tails obey the same type of bounds: for example, the large positive tail of the <electric field> is $O(k^{-3n})$. Thus termwise differentiation is valid and the sum is a <harmonic function> except at the physical <point charge>, where it has the correct <Coulomb's law> singularity. The boundary cancellations and <uniqueness of Poisson equation> complete the construction.

Choose a spherical <Gaussian surface> of radius $s$ with $a<s<r_0$. Its physical enclosed <electric charge> is the total induced <electric charge> $Q_a$ on the inner sphere. The <method of images> produces the same <electric field> on that <Gaussian surface>, so <Gauss's law> identifies $Q_a$ with the sum of the enclosed <image charges>. These are the first family with $n\leq-1$ and the second family with $n\leq0$:
$$
Q_a=q\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}k^n-\frac{qa}{r_0}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{0}k^n
=q\frac{k^{-1}-a/r_0}{1-k^{-1}}.
$$
Summing these <geometric series> gives
$$
\boxed{Q_a=-q\frac{a(b-r_0)}{r_0(b-a)}.}
$$
The <electric field> in the empty central cavity vanishes by <electrostatic shielding>, so it contributes no additional inner-face <surface charge density>. The formula tends to $-q$ as $r_0\downarrow a$ and to zero as $r_0\uparrow b$, as expected when the <point charge> approaches one grounded boundary or the other.

Let $r_0=|\mathbf r_0|$ and let $\Omega_0=(\theta_0,\varphi_0)$ describe its direction in <spherical coordinates>; write $\Omega=(\theta,\varphi)$. The given <Dirichlet Green function between concentric spheres> has normalization $\nabla^2G=-4\pi\delta^{(3)}$, so the <electric potential> is $\phi=qG/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. On the inner sphere the <unit normal> from metal into the gap is $+\mathbf e_r$, hence its induced <surface charge density> is
$$
\sigma(a,\Omega)=-\frac q{4\pi}\left.\partial_rG(r,\Omega;\mathbf r_0)\right|_{a^+}.
$$
Near this boundary $r_<=r$ and $r_>=r_0$. The needed radial <derivative> is
$$
\left.\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^\ell-\frac{a^{2\ell+1}}{r^{\ell+1}}\right)\right|_{r=a}
=\ell a^{\ell-1}+(\ell+1)a^{\ell-1}=(2\ell+1)a^{\ell-1}.
$$
It cancels the factor $2\ell+1$ in the <Dirichlet Green function>. Therefore the answer in <spherical harmonics> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(a,\Omega)=-q\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}
\frac{a^{\ell-1}\overline{Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega_0)}{1-(a/b)^{2\ell+1}}
\left(\frac1{r_0^{\ell+1}}-\frac{r_0^\ell}{b^{2\ell+1}}\right).}
$$
This <spherical harmonic> expansion converges on the boundary: after the angular sum, the two radial factors decay geometrically with ratios $a/r_0<1$ and $ar_0/b^2<1$, while the angular factors grow at most polynomially. This justifies the boundary <derivative> and the subsequent <surface integral>.

Write the constant <spherical harmonic> $Y_{00}=C$. Its <orthonormality> gives $4\pi|C|^2=1$, independently of its phase convention. <Orthogonality> to $Y_{00}$ gives
$$
\int_{S^2}\overline{Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)}\,d\Omega=0\quad(\ell>0),\qquad
\int_{S^2}\overline{Y_{00}(\Omega)}Y_{00}(\Omega_0)\,d\Omega=1.
$$
Only the $\ell=m=0$ term contributes to the <surface integral> of the <surface charge density>. Multiplying by the surface-area factor $a^2$ gives
$$
Q_a=-qa^2\frac{a^{-1}}{1-a/b}\left(\frac1{r_0}-\frac1b\right).
$$
Thus the <spherical harmonic> calculation reproduces the total induced <electric charge>:
$$
\boxed{Q_a=-q\frac{a(b-r_0)}{r_0(b-a)}.}
$$

= Solution

Translate the concentric spheres so that their centre is $(0,0,-a)$, take $b=a+d$, and keep the <point charge> fixed at $(0,0,h)$. Its distance from the centre is $r_0=a+h$. As $a\to\infty$, the upper portions of the inner and outer spheres approach the planes $z=0$ and $z=d$. The preceding total induced <electric charge> becomes
$$
Q_a=-q\frac{a(d-h)}{(a+h)d}\longrightarrow-\frac{q(d-h)}d.
$$
The outer sphere has total induced <electric charge> $Q_b=-q-Q_a$: a <Gaussian surface> within its conducting material has zero <electric flux>, and the grounded exterior is field-free by <uniqueness of Poisson equation>. Therefore $Q_b\to-qh/d$.

One can justify the total-charge limit without assuming that local convergence of <electric fields> preserves their <surface integrals>. Use <induced charge as harmonic measure>: if a <harmonic function> $w$ is one on the selected grounded boundary and zero on the other, <Green's second identity> with the <electric potential> gives
$$
Q_{\rm selected}=-q\,w(\mathbf r_0).
$$
Indeed, $\nabla^2\phi=-q\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)/\epsilon_0$, $\phi=0$ on the boundaries, and their <unit normal> outward from the gap satisfies $\sigma=\epsilon_0\partial_n\phi$. For the spheres the weighting <harmonic function> is $w_a(r)=a(b-r)/(r(b-a))$, exactly the factor already obtained. For the slab the two weighting <harmonic functions> are $w_0=1-z/d$ and $w_d=z/d$. Applying the same identity to the decaying grounded-slab <electric potential>, whose boundary term at lateral infinity vanishes, establishes directly
$$
\boxed{Q_0=-\frac{q(d-h)}d,\qquad Q_d=-\frac{qh}d.}
$$
Their sum is $-q$, and their relative sizes measure how close the <point charge> lies to each grounded plane.

Use the <constitutive relations> $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu$, where $\mathbf D$ is the <electric displacement field>, $\mathbf H$ the <magnetic field strength>, $\epsilon$ the <permittivity>, and $\mu$ the <magnetic permeability>. Let $\rho_f$ be the <charge density> of <free charge> and $\mathbf J_f$ its <current density>. In this homogeneous medium, <Gauss's law> and the <magnetostatic Ampère law> are, respectively,
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\qquad
\oint_{\partial W}\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=Q_f(W)=\int_W\rho_f\,d^3r,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f,\qquad
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=I_f(S)=\int_S\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Here $W$ is a volume with closed boundary $\partial W$, and $S$ is an oriented spanning surface whose boundary orientation follows the right-hand rule. Thus the <electric field> form is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_f/\epsilon$ and $\oint_{\partial W}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=Q_f/\epsilon$; the <magnetic field> form is $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf J_f$ and $\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu I_f$.

The remaining static <Maxwell equations> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad\oint_{\partial W}\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=0,
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=0,\qquad\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=0.
$$
These express <Gauss's law for magnetism> and the static limit of <Faraday's law>. The volume and circulation forms follow from the <divergence theorem> and <Stokes theorem>. The <electric charge> and <electric current> on the right of the macroscopic laws are free sources; material <electric polarization> and <magnetization> are already included in $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf H$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Allow time dependence while keeping the <constitutive relations> linear, isotropic, nondispersive, and independent of time. The <Maxwell equations in matter> give
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D.
$$
For the <Poynting vector> $\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$, the <divergence> identity yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}
&=\mathbf H\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H)\\
&=-\frac1\mu\mathbf B\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B
-\epsilon\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf E-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J_f.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first two terms are exact time <derivatives>. Taking the zero-field <energy density> as zero gives the <electromagnetic energy density>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\frac12\epsilon|\mathbf E|^2+\frac1{2\mu}|\mathbf B|^2
=\frac12(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H).}
$$
Consequently the <Poynting theorem> is the <continuity equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathcal E+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E.}
$$
In integral form,
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_W\mathcal E\,d^3r+
\oint_{\partial W}\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=-\int_W\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E\,d^3r.
$$
The left side is the rate of increase of stored <electromagnetic energy> plus outward <Poynting flux>. The right side accounts for work delivered to the free <electric current>; in an Ohmic medium $\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E$ is the local rate of <Joule heating>.

= Solution

Work in <cylindrical coordinates> and use the assumed independence of $\varphi$ and $z$. In each material region, the zero <curl> of the static <electric field> gives
$$
\frac{dE_z}{d\rho}=0,\qquad
\frac{d(\rho E_\varphi)}{d\rho}=0.
$$
The tangential <electric field> is zero at a <perfect conductor>. The <dielectric-interface boundary conditions> make its tangential components continuous at each material interface, so $E_z=E_\varphi=0$ throughout the gap. They also vanish in the empty interior and exterior for the regular solution with no applied external <electric field>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=E_\rho(\rho)\mathbf e_\rho.}
$$
This specifies an axis of direction: the sign of $E_\rho$ depends on the sign of $V$.

For the static <magnetic field>, <Gauss's law for magnetism> implies
$$
\frac1\rho\frac{d(\rho B_\rho)}{d\rho}=0.
$$
Thus $\rho B_\rho$ is constant in each layer. Continuity of the normal <magnetic field> across every interface makes that constant global, and regularity at the axis makes it zero. Next use $\nabla\times\mathbf H=0$ away from the conducting surfaces. Its $\varphi$ component gives $dH_z/d\rho=0$. There is no azimuthal <surface current density>, so $H_z$ is continuous across the conducting surfaces and the material interface. The decay of the exterior <magnetic field> at infinity therefore forces this global constant to zero. The only remaining component is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=B_\varphi(\rho)\mathbf e_\varphi.}
$$
The <magnetostatic Ampère law> fixes its sign: positive inner <electric current> in the $+z$ direction produces positive $B_\varphi$ for positive <magnetic permeability>.

For this <radially layered coaxial cable>, define
$$
L_1=\ln(c/a),\qquad L_2=\ln(b/c),\qquad
\Delta=\epsilon'L_1+\epsilon L_2,\qquad
C=\frac{V\epsilon\epsilon'}{\Delta}.
$$
Use the solution regular at the axis with bounded exterior <electric potential>, so that the empty interior and exterior have the constant <electric potentials> fixed by their adjacent conducting cylinders.

There is no <free charge> in either <electrical insulator> or on their interface. <Gauss's law> and continuity of normal <electric displacement field> give $D_\rho=C/\rho$ in both layers. The <electric potential difference> fixes the constant because
$$
V=\int_a^bE_\rho\,d\rho=C\left(\frac{L_1}{\epsilon}+\frac{L_2}{\epsilon'}\right).
$$
The <electric potential>, including the empty regions bounded by the conducting cylinders, is therefore
$$
\boxed{\phi(\rho)=\begin{cases}
V,&0\leq\rho\leq a,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac V\Delta\left[\epsilon'\ln(c/\rho)+\epsilon\ln(b/c)\right],&a<\rho<c,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{V\epsilon}{\Delta}\ln(b/\rho),&c\leq\rho<b,\\[2pt]
0,&\rho\geq b.
\end{cases}}
$$
Both inner and outer <Dirichlet boundary conditions> hold, and the <electric potential> is continuous at the material interface, where
$$
\boxed{\phi(c)=\frac{V\epsilon\ln(b/c)}{\epsilon'\ln(c/a)+\epsilon\ln(b/c)}.}
$$
Taking the negative <gradient> of the <electric potential> yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\rho)=\begin{cases}
0,&\rho<a\ \text{or}\ \rho>b,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{V\epsilon'}{\Delta\rho}\mathbf e_\rho,&a<\rho<c,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{V\epsilon}{\Delta\rho}\mathbf e_\rho,&c<\rho<b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The radial <electric field> generally jumps at $\rho=c$, but $\epsilon E_\rho(c^-)=\epsilon'E_\rho(c^+)$, as required by the absence of free <surface charge density> there. The conducting cylinders carry opposite <linear charge densities> $\lambda=2\pi C$ and $-\lambda$.

For an <Amperian loop> of radius $\rho$, the enclosed <electric current> is zero, $I$, or zero according as $\rho<a$, $a<\rho<b$, or $\rho>b$. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> therefore gives $H_\varphi=I/(2\pi\rho)$ in the gap, and zero outside. Using the two <magnetic permeabilities>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\rho)=\begin{cases}
0,&\rho<a\ \text{or}\ \rho>b,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\mu I}{2\pi\rho}\mathbf e_\varphi,&a<\rho<c,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\mu'I}{2\pi\rho}\mathbf e_\varphi,&c<\rho<b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The tangential <magnetic field strength> is continuous at the material interface, while the tangential <magnetic field> need not be. At each ideal conducting surface and at the material interface, the displayed <electric field> and <magnetic field> formulas specify one-sided limits; a single interface value is unnecessary when the limiting values differ.

Since $\mathbf e_\rho\times\mathbf e_\varphi=\mathbf e_z$, the <Poynting vector> is axial. The <magnetic permeability> cancels from $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/\mu$ in each material layer:
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}(\rho)=\begin{cases}
0,&\rho<a\ \text{or}\ \rho>b,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{VI\epsilon'}{2\pi\Delta\rho^2}\mathbf e_z,&a<\rho<c,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{VI\epsilon}{2\pi\Delta\rho^2}\mathbf e_z,&c<\rho<b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The signed <Poynting flux> through the plane with <unit normal> $+\mathbf e_z$ is its <surface integral> with area element $\rho\,d\rho\,d\varphi$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=2\pi\int_a^b\mathcal P_z(\rho)\rho\,d\rho\\
&=\frac{VI}{\Delta}\left[\epsilon'\ln(c/a)+\epsilon\ln(b/c)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{P=VI.}
$$
The transmitted <electromagnetic energy> per unit time is the circuit's terminal power. For $VI>0$, the <Poynting flux> points in the direction of the inner <electric current>. The <electromagnetic energy> travels through the insulating gap and reaches a load at the end of the <coaxial cable>. The radii and material constants redistribute the <electromagnetic energy density> and the local <Poynting vector>, while their integrated <Poynting flux> depends only on the terminal <electric potential difference> and <electric current>.

Orient the <unit normal> $\mathbf n$ from $R_1$ to $R_2$ and write $\mathbf H$ for the <magnetic field strength>. For static <electric fields>, $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$; the static <Ampère's law> gives $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f$. The <curl> product rule therefore gives the local <Poynting flux and terminal power> identity
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}=-\nabla\phi\times\mathbf H
=\phi\mathbf J_f-\nabla\times(\phi\mathbf H).
$$
Integrate over an oriented piece $\Sigma_R$ of the separating surface and apply <Stokes theorem>:
$$
\int_{\Sigma_R}\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_{\Sigma_R}\phi\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
-\oint_{\partial\Sigma_R}\phi\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell.
$$
Once all the wires are included, the first <surface integral> is $\sum_k\phi_kI_k$: the <electric potential> is constant on each <perfect conductor> and the integral of its normal <current density> is its signed <electric current>. Equivalently, remove the wire cross-sections and apply <Stokes theorem> in the surrounding insulator. Each resulting hole has negative boundary orientation, and the <magnetostatic Ampère law> around it gives the positive contribution $\phi_kI_k$.

For a closed separating surface the boundary term is absent. For a noncompact separating surface, take $R\to\infty$ with the usual isolated-circuit condition $\oint_{\partial\Sigma_R}\phi\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\to0$. In either case,
$$
\boxed{\int_\Sigma\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\sum_{k=1}^N\phi_kI_k.}
$$
For a steady closed circuit the signed <electric currents> sum to zero, so shifting the reference of <electric potential> does not change the terminal power.

For a fully general noncompact surface, the hypotheses as printed need an additional condition at infinity. The exact result before imposing it is
$$
\boxed{P_{1\to2}=\sum_{k=1}^N\phi_kI_k-
\lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{\partial\Sigma_R}\phi\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell.}
$$
Time independence alone does not remove this boundary term or ensure a finite <Poynting flux>; for example, spatially constant crossed <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> carry a constant <Poynting vector> through an infinite plane. The stated terminal-power formula holds with the boundary condition specified above.

With <charge density> $\rho$, <current density> $\mathbf J$, and <speed of light> $c=(\epsilon_0\mu_0)^{-1/2}$, the four <Maxwell equations> in <vacuum> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho/\epsilon_0,&\nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\
\nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf B,&
\nabla\times\mathbf B&=\mu_0\mathbf J+c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> determine the <electric field> and <magnetic field> by
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A,\qquad
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.}
$$
These definitions satisfy <Gauss's law for magnetism> and <Faraday's law> identically. The <Lorenz gauge condition> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0.}
$$
Substituting into <Gauss's law> and using the <Lorenz gauge condition> gives
$$
\frac\rho{\epsilon_0}=-\nabla^2\phi-\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)
=-\nabla^2\phi+c^{-2}\partial_t^2\phi.
$$
For the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>, the double-<curl> identity gives
$$
\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A
=\mu_0\mathbf J-c^{-2}\nabla\partial_t\phi-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf A.
$$
The two <gradient> terms cancel by the <Lorenz gauge condition>. Defining the <d'Alembert operator> with the sign convention $\square=\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2$, the remaining equations are
$$
\boxed{\square\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0,\qquad
\square\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J.}
$$
Conversely, if the <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> satisfy these <wave equations> and the <Lorenz gauge condition>, the same substitutions in reverse give <Gauss's law> and the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>. The other two <Maxwell equations> follow identically from the definitions, proving equivalence within the <Lorenz gauge>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The moving <point charge> has distributional <charge density> and <current density>
$$
\rho(\mathbf r',t')=q\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r'-\mathbf r_0(t')),\qquad
\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t')=q\mathbf v_0(t')\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r'-\mathbf r_0(t')).
$$
Let
$$
K(\mathbf r,t;\mathbf r',t')=
\frac{\delta(t-t'-|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|/c)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}.
$$
The given <retarded fundamental solution> identity immediately implies that the <retarded potentials>
$$
\phi=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int K\rho\,d^3r'\,dt',\qquad
\mathbf A=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int K\mathbf J\,d^3r'\,dt'
$$
solve $\square\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$ and $\square\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J$ in the sense of <distributions>. Integrating the spatial <Dirac delta function> first gives
$$
\phi=\frac q{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\delta(g(t'))}{R(t')}\,dt',\qquad
g(t')=t-t'-R(t')/c,
$$
and the analogous integral for $\mathbf A$ has numerator $\mu_0q\mathbf v_0(t')/(4\pi)$.

Assume the physical trajectory has <subluminal speed> and the <retarded time> exists. At its root $t_r$, with $\mathbf n_r=\mathbf R(t_r)/R(t_r)$,
$$
\frac{dR}{dt'}=-\frac{\mathbf R\cdot\mathbf v_0}{R},\qquad
g'(t_r)=-\left(1-\frac{\mathbf n_r\cdot\mathbf v_0(t_r)}c\right).
$$
The positive factor $\kappa=1-\mathbf n_r\cdot\mathbf v_0/c$ also proves <uniqueness of retarded time for a subluminal source>. The <Dirac delta function> substitution formula gives $\delta(g(t'))=\delta(t'-t_r)/\kappa$, so
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf r,t)=\frac q{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac1{R(t_r)-\mathbf R(t_r)\cdot\mathbf v_0(t_r)/c},\qquad
\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{\mathbf v_0(t_r)}{c^2}\phi(\mathbf r,t).}
$$
These are the <Liénard–Wiechert potentials>. The denominator includes the time-substitution factor, rather than just the retarded distance. Away from the trajectory the <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> are ordinary smooth functions; on the trajectory their <wave equations> have the indicated distributional sources.

For completeness, the <charge continuity equation> $\partial_{t'}\rho+\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J=0$ also verifies the <Lorenz gauge>. Using $\nabla K=-\nabla'K$ and $\partial_tK=-\partial_{t'}K$, <integration by parts> in the retarded integrals gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int K(\nabla'\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_{t'}\rho)\,d^3r'\,dt'=0,
$$
with the retarded boundary prescription. Thus the construction also has the required <Lorenz gauge condition>.

Take the ideal <electric dipole> limit with a fixed smooth <electric dipole moment> $\mathbf p(t)$, so that $\mathbf r_0(t)=\mathbf p(t)/(2q)$; its required time <derivatives> are bounded on the time interval under consideration. Fix $r>0$ and put $\mathbf n=\mathbf r/r$, $t_0=t-r/c$. For $s=+1$ or $-1$, the source position is $s\mathbf p(t)/(2q)$. A <Taylor expansion> of the distance gives
$$
R_s(t_r^s)=\left|\mathbf r-\frac{s\mathbf p(t_r^s)}{2q}\right|
=r-\frac{s\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p(t_r^s)}{2q}+O(q^{-2}).
$$
Substitute this into the defining equation for <retarded time>:
$$
t_r^s=t_0+\frac{s\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p(t_r^s)}{2qc}+O(q^{-2}).
$$
Boundedness first implies $t_r^s-t_0=O(q^{-1})$. The <Taylor expansion> $\mathbf p(t_r^s)=\mathbf p(t_0)+O(q^{-1})$ then changes the displayed correction only by $O(q^{-2})$. Hence
$$
\boxed{t_r^+=t_0+\frac{\mathbf p(t_0)\cdot\mathbf r}{2qrc}+O(q^{-2}),\qquad
t_r^-=t_0-\frac{\mathbf p(t_0)\cdot\mathbf r}{2qrc}+O(q^{-2}).}
$$
The nearer positive displacement reduces the propagation distance and consequently increases the <retarded time>. The <Big O notation> here is for $q\to\infty$ at a fixed observation event away from the origin. Keeping a fixed smooth $\mathbf p$ is essential: bounded amplitude alone, with arbitrarily increasing time frequency as $q$ varies, would not justify this <Taylor expansion>.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the sign $s=\pm1$ to label a <point charge> $sq$ at $s\mathbf p/(2q)$, with <velocity> $s\mathbf p'/(2q)$. All quantities on the right below are evaluated at $t_0=t-r/c$. To first order,
$$
\mathbf R_s=\mathbf r-\frac{s\mathbf p}{2q}+O(q^{-2}),\qquad
R_s=r-\frac{s\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p}{2q}+O(q^{-2}),\qquad
\mathbf v_s=\frac{s\mathbf p'}{2q}+O(q^{-2}).
$$
The denominator in each <Liénard–Wiechert potential> therefore expands as
$$
D_s=R_s-\mathbf R_s\cdot\mathbf v_s/c
=r-\frac{s}{2q}\left(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p+\frac{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p'}c\right)+O(q^{-2}),
$$
so
$$
\frac1{D_s}=\frac1r+\frac{s}{2qr^2}
\left(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p+\frac{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p'}c\right)+O(q^{-2}).
$$
In the sum of the two <electric potentials>, the $q/r$ terms cancel and the finite terms add. This gives the <retarded potentials of a point electric dipole>:
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf r,t)=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(\frac{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p(t_0)}{r^3}
+\frac{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p'(t_0)}{cr^2}\right),\qquad r>0.}
$$
For the <magnetic vector potential>, the signed <electric charge> and signed <velocity> multiply to give the same sign for both contributions:
$$
\mathbf A=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0c^2}\sum_{s=\pm1}\frac{sq\,\mathbf v_s}{D_s}
\longrightarrow\frac{\mathbf p'}{4\pi\epsilon_0c^2r}.
$$
Since $\mu_0\epsilon_0c^2=1$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\mathbf p'(t_0).}
$$
The $\mathbf p'$ term in the <electric potential> arises from the moving-source denominator and remains finite in the point-<electric dipole> limit; discarding it would fail to give the full retarded <electric field>.

= Solution

Set $K=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, $t_0=t-r/c$ and $\mathbf n=\mathbf r/r$, and evaluate $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf p'$, $\mathbf p''$ at $t_0$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\nabla t_0=-\frac{\mathbf n}c,\qquad
\partial_i p_j=-\frac{n_i}c p'_j,\qquad
\partial_i n_j=\frac{\delta_{ij}-n_in_j}r.
$$
In particular, for $\mathbf u(t_0)$ and any positive integer $m$,
$$
\nabla\left(\frac{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf u}{r^m}\right)
=\frac{\mathbf u-(m+1)\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf u)}{r^{m+1}}
-\frac{\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf u')}{cr^m}.
$$
Apply this <gradient> identity to the <electric potential> $\phi=K[(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p)/r^2+(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p')/(cr)]$:
$$
\nabla\phi=K\left[
\frac{\mathbf p-3\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p)}{r^3}
+\frac{\mathbf p'-3\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p')}{cr^2}
-\frac{\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p'')}{c^2r}
\right].
$$
The <magnetic vector potential> is $\mathbf A=K\mathbf p'/(c^2r)$, with time <derivative> $\partial_t\mathbf A=K\mathbf p''/(c^2r)$. Thus the <electric field> $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[
\frac{3\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p)-\mathbf p}{r^3}
+\frac{3\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p')-\mathbf p'}{cr^2}
+\frac{\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p'')-\mathbf p''}{c^2r}
\right].}
$$
For the <magnetic field>, use the <curl> product rule and the <chain rule>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\times\left(\frac{\mathbf p'}r\right)
&=\nabla(r^{-1})\times\mathbf p'+r^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf p'\\
&=-\frac{\mathbf n\times\mathbf p'}{r^2}
-\frac{\mathbf n\times\mathbf p''}{cr}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently the <fields of a time-dependent electric dipole> include
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left(\frac{\mathbf p'}{r^2}
+\frac{\mathbf p''}{cr}\right)\times\mathbf n.}
$$
These formulas hold for $r>0$. The $r^{-3}$ <electric field> is the retarded <electric dipole field>, the $r^{-2}$ terms are induction contributions, and the $r^{-1}$ terms describe <electric dipole radiation>. Retaining the dependence of $t_0$ on position in every spatial <derivative> is what produces both the induction and radiation contributions.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The leading <electric dipole radiation> fields are
$$
\mathbf E_{\rm rad}=\frac{\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p'')-\mathbf p''}{4\pi\epsilon_0c^2r},\qquad
\mathbf B_{\rm rad}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi cr}\mathbf p''\times\mathbf n
=\frac1c\mathbf n\times\mathbf E_{\rm rad}.
$$
The radiation <electric field> is transverse, $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf E_{\rm rad}=0$. The <vector triple product> therefore gives the leading <Poynting vector>
$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}=\frac1{\mu_0c}\mathbf E_{\rm rad}\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf E_{\rm rad})+O(r^{-3})
=\frac{|\mathbf E_{\rm rad}|^2}{\mu_0c}\mathbf n+O(r^{-3}).
$$
If $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf p''(t-r/c)$ and $\mathbf n$, then
$$
|\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p'')-\mathbf p''|^2
=|\mathbf p''|^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p'')^2
=|\mathbf p''|^2\sin^2\theta.
$$
Using $\mu_0\epsilon_0c^2=1$, the final result is
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}(\mathbf r,t)=
\frac{|\mathbf p''(t-r/c)|^2\sin^2\theta}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3r^2}\mathbf n+O(r^{-3}),\qquad
|\boldsymbol{\mathcal P}|=\frac{|\mathbf p''(t-r/c)|^2\sin^2\theta}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3r^2}+O(r^{-3}).}
$$
The leading <Poynting flux> is outward, vanishes along the instantaneous retarded $\mathbf p''$ axis, and is maximal in its perpendicular plane. If $\mathbf p''=0$, the radiation term is zero and no angle needs to be assigned. The large-$r$ expansion assumes bounded retarded <electric dipole moment> and time <derivatives>; it may also be read at fixed <retarded time>.
