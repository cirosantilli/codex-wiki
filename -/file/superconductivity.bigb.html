= Superconductivity
{wiki}

<Superconductivity> is a phase of matter that supports steady <electric current> with zero bulk <electrical resistivity> and exhibits the <Meissner effect>: equilibrium screening of a sufficiently weak, static <magnetic field> from the interior. It occurs below a material-dependent <superconducting transition temperature>, provided the <electric current> and applied <magnetic field> are also sufficiently small. https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0409402[Milind N. Kunchur (2004), limits on dissipation-free superconducting current]{ref} Bulk expulsion means that a sample much thicker than its <London penetration depth> has an almost field-free interior; the <magnetic field> need not vanish at its surface. Conventional <BCS theory> explains <superconductivity> through correlated <electrons> forming <Cooper pairs>. https://hoffman.physics.harvard.edu/materials/SCintro.php[Hoffman laboratory, introduction to superconductivity]{ref}

The two observations answer different questions. A <resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity> asks whether a steady <electric current> requires a measurable <electric potential difference>. A <field-cooled Meissner experiment> asks whether a <magnetic field> already present in the material is expelled when it enters <superconductivity>. An ideal <perfect conductor> with zero <electrical resistivity> can preserve that pre-existing <magnetic field>; zero <electrical resistance> alone therefore does not establish the <Meissner effect>.

The usefulness follows directly from <Joule heating>, $P=I^2R$: maintaining a steady <electric current> through a segment with zero <electrical resistance> produces no resistive heating in that segment. This makes persistent <electric currents> attractive for producing steady <magnetic fields>. The beauty is that one phase has both a transport property and an equilibrium magnetic response, and the second cannot be inferred just by making an ordinary <electrical resistance> very small. The <London equations> encode this extra magnetic response. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1935.0048[F. and H. London (1935), The electromagnetic equations of the supraconductor]{ref}

= Unconventional superconductivity
{parent=Superconductivity}

Superconducting pairing outside the simple conventional isotropic spin-singlet scenario, with the precise classification depending on the context and evidence. Gap symmetry, pairing mechanism, spin structure and time-reversal behavior are separate questions; a nonstandard gap does not establish every other proposed broken symmetry.

= D-wave superconductivity
{parent=Unconventional superconductivity}

Pairing with d-wave orbital structure, such as a crystal gap proportional to $k_x^2-k_y^2$. Its sign changes under a quarter-turn rotation, but a compensating overall phase change can leave gauge-invariant observables fourfold symmetric. Gap nodes and phase signs therefore do not by themselves establish nematicity or time-reversal breaking. A <phase-sensitive test of d-wave pairing> probes relative signs through <interference>.

= Nodal quasiparticle power laws
{parent=D-wave superconductivity}

Near clean two-dimensional d-wave nodes, linearized quasiparticle energies form anisotropic cones. Their low-energy density of states is proportional to $|E|$, so electronic heat capacity scales as $T^2$ rather than the activated low-temperature form of a full gap. The clean in-plane penetration-depth increment is linear in T. Disorder, dimensional effects, vortex fields and gap changes can alter these laws, so the assumptions should accompany any proposed diagnostic.

= Volovik effect
{c}
{parent=D-wave superconductivity}
{title2=$\gamma(B)\propto\sqrt B$}

<Vortex> superflow Doppler-shifts low-energy <quasiparticles> in a nodal superconductor. For a circular cell of radius $R\propto B^{-1/2}$ and flow magnitude proportional to $1/r$, its spatially averaged magnitude is proportional to $1/R$. A linearly vanishing nodal <density of states> then acquires a zero-energy contribution proportional to $\sqrt B$, producing a low-temperature linear heat-capacity coefficient with that scaling. This is a clean semiclassical mixed-state estimate; the vector flow average can be zero, and disorder, screening, core states and other field effects change its range of validity.

= Nodal quasiparticle density of states in a two-dimensional d-wave superconductor
{parent=D-wave superconductivity}
{title2=$g_{\rm qp}(E)/\mathcal A=4E/(\pi\alpha\beta)$}

For four equivalent nodes, two spin <quasiparticles> and local dispersion $E^2=\alpha^2q_1^2+\beta^2q_2^2$, one node encloses momentum area $\pi E^2/(\alpha\beta)$. Counting states with measure $d^2q/(2\pi)^2$ gives the displayed positive-excitation density. The associated clean low-temperature <heat capacity> is $C/\mathcal A=18\zeta(3)k_B^3T^2/(\pi\alpha\beta)$. Electron-addition spectral density alone has a different count because its coherence weights do not count all particle and hole thermal excitations.

= D-wave superconductor
{parent=D-wave superconductivity}

A <superconductor> whose pairing <order parameter> has the angular or crystal-symmetry structure of <d-wave superconductivity>. The sign of the gap depends on momentum direction and nodes can permit low-energy <fermionic Bogoliubov quasiparticles>.

= D-wave bond order
{parent=D-wave superconductivity}
{title2=$\Delta_x=-\Delta_y,\quad\Delta_{\mathbf k}\propto\cos(k_xa)-\cos(k_ya)$}

For nearest-neighbor spin-singlet pairing on a square lattice, opposite signs of the pairing amplitude on the two bond directions give the displayed momentum-dependent <superconducting gap function>. It changes sign under a quarter-turn and has nodes on the zone diagonals. This sign structure is distinct from a nonnegative gap whose magnitude is merely anisotropic. A real pure $d_{x^2-y^2}$ pattern does not by itself require broken time reversal.

= Superconducting magnet
{parent=Superconductivity}
{wiki}

An electromagnet whose winding uses a superconductor, allowing large currents without the normal winding's steady resistive loss in its superconducting state. Refrigeration, strain, field-dependent critical current and <superconducting quench> protection remain practical constraints. Magnetic resonance imaging, nuclear magnetic resonance and particle accelerators are applications.

= Superconducting quench
{parent=Superconducting magnet}

A transition of part of a current-carrying superconducting system into a resistive state. Joule heating can spread the transition, and the stored magnetic <energy> must be handled safely by the system's protection design. Zero steady winding resistance does not remove the finite magnetic <energy> or the refrigeration requirement.

= Condensation energy of a superconductor
{parent=Superconductivity}
{title2=$F_s-F_n=-a^2/(2b)$}

The <condensation energy of a superconductor> is the free-energy lowering on entering the superconducting state, with sign convention specified. For a local Landau density $a|\Delta|^2+b|\Delta|^4/2$ and $a<0$, its minimum is $-a^2/(2b)$ relative to the normal-state value. The positive <energy> required to destroy the condensate is the opposite sign.

= Anderson-Higgs mechanism in a superconductor
{parent=Superconductivity}
{c}

Coupling a charged condensate's phase to electromagnetism changes the neutral Goldstone response into electromagnetic collective dynamics. In a bulk long-wavelength regime, the longitudinal phase-density mode has a plasma gap. Its dispersion and damping require a dynamical model; a static Ginzburg-Landau functional alone does not fix the entire excitation spectrum.

= Superconducting phase-density action
{parent=Anderson-Higgs mechanism in a superconductor}
{title2=$\mathcal L_\theta=\chi_p(\hbar\dot\theta+q\phi)^2/2-\rho_0(\hbar\nabla\theta-q\mathbf A)^2/(2m_*)$}

A lossless low-energy charged-condensate <action> has a positive compressibility coefficient $\chi_p$, effective pair density $\rho_0$, mass $m_*$ and signed charge $q$. Coupling its phase to the electromagnetic potentials through the displayed combinations preserves <gauge symmetry>. Adding the Maxwell action and eliminating the longitudinal electric potential gives $\omega^2=q^2\rho_0/(\epsilon_0m_*)+[\rho_0/(m_*\chi_p)]k^2$ in a simple homogeneous bulk model. Removing the electromagnetic coupling leaves neutral sound. Static <Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity> alone cannot determine these frequencies or their damping. <Physics 215C: Quantum Field Theory, Part 3 by John McGreevy>[§2.3, phase effective theory and coupling to electromagnetism]{ref}

= Superconducting persistent current
{synonym}

A <superconducting persistent current> is an <electric current> circulating in a closed path with <superconductivity> after the external driving <electromotive force> has ceased. Its associated <magnetic field> makes the circulation observable without inserting a measuring instrument into the path. This article concerns macroscopic circulation in <superconductivity>. A finite experiment establishes persistence over its observation window with a stated <measurement error>, rather than directly observing an infinite lifetime.

The ideal circuit connection is <flux conservation in a superconducting loop>. In a fixed loop with <self-inductance> $L$, a single circulating <electric current> $I$, and externally imposed linked <magnetic flux> $\Phi_{\rm ext}$, the lumped circuit approximation gives
$$
L\frac{dI}{dt}+RI=-\frac{d\Phi_{\rm ext}}{dt}.
$$
When the <electrical resistance> $R$ vanishes, integrating gives $LI+\Phi_{\rm ext}=\text{constant}$. Removing the external linked <magnetic flux> therefore changes the circulating <electric current>; holding it fixed afterwards preserves that <electric current>. This is a circuit approximation with a specified <self-inductance>, not a derivation of the microscopic state of <superconductivity>. For thin paths where the carrier contribution matters, <fluxoid and flux quantization in a superconductor> supplies the more careful description.

For constant $L>0$, constant effective <electrical resistance> $R\geq0$, and no changing external linked <magnetic flux>, the <RL circuit> instead predicts
$$
I(t)=I_0e^{-Rt/L},\qquad \tau=\frac{L}{R}\quad(R>0).
$$
This <exponential decay> follows by separating $dI/I=-(R/L)dt$. A long relaxation time tests extremely small <electrical resistance> without trying to resolve an equally tiny steady <electric potential difference>. The stored <magnetic energy> is $U=LI^2/2$, so the same model gives $dU/dt=-RI^2$, exactly the <Joule heating> loss. The finite <energy> stored in the loop supplies the magnetic readout; the circulating <electric current> is not a continuously powered source of useful work.

= Persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment
{parent=Superconductivity}

The <persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment> prepares a closed cold path, changes the externally linked <magnetic flux>, and follows the resulting <superconducting persistent current> through its <magnetic field>. The essential measurement starts after the inducing <magnetic field> has stopped changing.

\Image[persistent-superconducting-current-geometry.png]
{title=Preparing a closed superconducting loop and sensing its magnetic field with a compass}
{description=Original schematic of field preparation and magnetic compensation. Shapes, winding counts and distances are illustrative. The <magnetic compass> senses a static <magnetic field>; it is not a stationary voltage-reading <pickup coil>.}

<Heike Kamerlingh Onnes>'s 1914 Communication 140c describes a <lead> coil closed by fused <lead> connections. Cooling in a <magnetic field> and removing the inducing magnet prepared the circulation. A <magnetic compass> sensed it without contacts; a separate <copper> coil, cooled in liquid air, supplied a measured balancing <magnetic field>. Reversing orientation and compensation reduced offsets. <Further Experiments with Liquid Helium, J (Continued) by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes>[Communication 140c (1914), §5, pp. 278–280; §7, pp. 280–281]{ref}

The liquid-<helium> <cryostat> maintained low <temperature>, but refilling temporarily warmed the apparatus and changed the inferred <electric current>. A later run used a smaller initial <electric current>. Apparatus deformation and needle-zero drift limited comparison; cutting the closure still left a smaller magnetic signal, warning against assigning every signal to circulation around the whole loop. The report consequently gave an upper bound under a residual <Ohm's law> hypothesis. <Further Experiments with Liquid Helium, J (Continued) by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes>[Communication 140c, §5, pp. 279–280; §6, p. 280; §8, pp. 281–283]{ref}

For interpreting such a measurement, write a locally calibrated magnetic signal as
$$
S(t)=k(t)I(t)+b(t).
$$
Here $k$ is the coupling and gain, while $b$ contains the background <magnetic field> and readout offset. Only after establishing stable $k$ and correcting $b$ does a ratio of signals determine a ratio of <electric currents>. A gradual change in geometry, a drifting background <magnetic field>, or a change of <temperature> can imitate or conceal a decay. A <pickup coil> connected only to a voltage meter obeys <Faraday's law of induction> and detects a changing linked <magnetic flux>; its zero steady output cannot demonstrate that the underlying static <magnetic field> has disappeared. A <magnetic compass> can respond to that static <magnetic field>.

Opening the path, reversing the preparation, and repeating with smaller initial <electric current> are useful controls for separating whole-loop circulation, magnetic background and a <superconducting critical current>. Keep the <temperature> and external <magnetic field> stable during the interval used to infer <electrical resistance>. A sudden reduction near a <superconducting critical current> is not evidence for the constant-$R$ <exponential decay> model at smaller <electric current>. Persistence also does not replace the <field-cooled Meissner experiment>: transport around a hole and magnetic expulsion from the material test different properties.

= Resistance upper bound from persistent-current decay
{parent=Persistent current in a superconducting loop experiment}
{title2=$R\leq-(L/T)\ln(1-\epsilon)$}

Suppose a calibrated measurement bounds the fractional loss of a nonzero <superconducting persistent current> by $\epsilon$, with $0<\epsilon<1$, over a duration $T>0$. Take $I_0>0$ by choosing the circulation direction. Under the constant-$L$, constant-$R$, undriven <RL circuit> model,
$$
\frac{I(T)}{I_0}\geq1-\epsilon
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
0\leq R\leq-\frac{L}{T}\ln(1-\epsilon)
\simeq\frac{L\epsilon}{T}.
$$
The inequality follows because the <logarithm> is increasing. The final approximation requires $\epsilon\ll1$ and slightly understates the exact upper bound. If $L$ is uncertain, an independently established upper bound on $L$ gives the conservative <electrical resistance> bound. The uncertainty in the initial reading, the final reading, background subtraction and drift must all be included in $\epsilon$; a display resolution alone does not provide it.

\Image[persistent-superconducting-current-decay-bound.png]
{title=An illustrative exponential decay, its logarithmic slope and the resulting resistance bound}
{description=Chosen numerical model, not historical data: fixed <self-inductance> $L=1\,\mathrm{\mu H}$ and no external drive. The final panel assumes a calibrated fractional-loss bound of $0.001$ at each chosen observation duration. Longer observation tightens the bound only while that fractional uncertainty remains controlled.}

For an original illustrative example, let $L=1\,\mathrm{\mu H}$ and suppose the corrected <electric current> ratio after $T=3\,\mathrm h=10800\,\mathrm s$ is at least $0.999$. Then
$$
R\leq9.264\times10^{-14}\,\Omega,\qquad
\tau\geq1.07946\times10^7\,\mathrm s\simeq124.94\,\mathrm{days}.
$$
These are conditional bounds for stipulated parameters, not values attributed to the 1914 apparatus. With the same $L$ but $R=10^{-11}\,\Omega$, the model instead gives $\tau=10^5\,\mathrm s$ and $I(24\,\mathrm h)/I_0=e^{-0.864}\simeq0.42147$: a readily visible loss on the chosen scale.

A resolved decay can be checked using its <logarithm>:
$$
\ln\frac{I(t)}{I_0}=-\frac{R}{L}t,\qquad
R=-L\frac{d\ln|I|}{dt}.
$$
A straight line supports this constant-parameter model; curvature or jumps demand a different account. If an effective <electrical resistance> varies with time while $L$ remains fixed and the loop remains undriven, only its time average is constrained:
$$
I(T)=I_0\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{L}\int_0^T R(t)\,dt\right],\qquad
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T R(t)\,dt\leq-\frac{L}{T}\ln(1-\epsilon).
$$
This does not bound every instantaneous value of $R(t)$. Changing <self-inductance> or external linked <magnetic flux> adds terms to the circuit equation, so an uncorrected magnetic signal cannot simply be substituted for $I(t)$. Nonobservation of decay leaves arbitrarily small positive constant <electrical resistance> compatible with a finite observation: it is not a logical proof that $R=0$.

= Superconducting critical current
{parent=Superconductivity}

A <superconducting critical current> $I_c$ is the limiting <electric current>, for a specified sample, <temperature>, external <magnetic field> and measurement criterion, beyond which the chosen state of <superconductivity> no longer sustains dissipation-free transport. A weak connection can limit a loop before the rest of its material does. A <magnetic field> generated by the <electric current> itself also contributes to the sample's conditions. A limit on the initial <superconducting persistent current> and a bound on its subsequent <electrical resistance> answer different experimental questions.

{synonym}

A periodic array of <Abrikosov vortices>. = Flux-lattice spacing
{parent=Abrikosov vortex lattice}
{title2=$a=\sqrt{2\Phi_0/(\sqrt3\,\overline B)}$}

Assume a triangular array of straight, singly quantized <Abrikosov vortices> with nearest-neighbor distance $a$. Here $\overline B>0$ is the magnitude of the **spatially averaged internal** <magnetic field>, and $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$ is the <superconducting flux quantum>; $e>0$ is the <elementary charge>. Two lattice vectors of <length> $a$ meeting at $60^\circ$ span <area> $A_c=\sqrt3a^2/2$. One flux quantum per cell gives
$$
\overline B A_c=\Phi_0,\qquad
n_v=\frac{\overline B}{\Phi_0},\qquad
a=\sqrt{\frac{2\Phi_0}{\sqrt3\,\overline B}}.
$$
Thus multiplying $\overline B$ by five decreases $a$ by $\sqrt5$; it does not multiply the flux of each vortex by five. Using the exact SI <Planck constant> and <elementary charge>, $\Phi_0\simeq2.06783\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{Wb}$.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA table, exact Planck constant and elementary charge]{ref}

For chosen internal fields $0.20\,\mathrm T$ and $1.0\,\mathrm T$, the spacings are approximately **$109\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $48.9\,\mathrm{nm}$**. These are geometric model calculations, not measurements from a particular sample. The local <magnetic field> varies within a cell; replacing $\overline B$ by the field at a core would not give this counting relation.

= Abrikosov parameter
{c}
{parent=Abrikosov vortex lattice}
{title2=$\beta_A=\langle|\psi|^4\rangle/\langle|\psi|^2\rangle^2$}

A measure of spatial nonuniformity in the <order parameter>, with averages taken over one lattice cell. Set $X=|\psi|^2$. Then
$$
\beta_A=1+\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\langle X\rangle^2}\geq1.
$$
Equality requires uniform $|\psi|^2$, so a nonconstant core pattern costs more in the quartic term than a uniform amplitude with the same mean squared amplitude. This identity alone does not compare two different vortex arrays: their admissible profiles must also satisfy the field equations.

= Pair formation and phase ordering
{parent=Superconducting transition temperature}

Pairing can establish a local binding or gap scale before a system develops phase-coherent transport. An actual <superconducting transition temperature> depends on both the pair scale and the <phase stiffness>. Low carrier density or weak interlayer coupling can make phase ordering the lower scale even when the pairing amplitude is appreciable. A minimum or smooth interpolation of two estimated scales is a qualitative guide, not a substitute for a self-consistent theory of the crossover.

= Resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity
{parent=Superconductivity}

The motivating observation is an abrupt disappearance of detectable <electrical resistance> on cooling, rather than a smooth improvement in conduction. The diagram pairs the measuring arrangement with an idealized transition. Its curves are deliberately synthetic; neither their scales nor their abrupt step are a reconstruction of measured mercury data.

\Image[superconductivity-resistance-experiment.png]
{title=Separate current and voltage leads reveal a synthetic superconducting resistance transition}
{description=The left panel shows <four-terminal sensing>; the right compares an idealized zero-<electrical resistance> branch with a stipulated normal-state continuation. All curves and the resolution band are illustrative, not measured data. The dashed vertical segment marks an idealized jump, not a fitted transition width.}

In a report communicated in November 1911, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes described a mercury thread in seven connected glass U-tubes immersed in a stirred liquid-helium bath. Expansion spaces kept freezing mercury from breaking the glass or interrupting the thread. Separate leads supplied <electric current> and sensed <electric potential difference>. He compensated a troublesome <thermoelectric effect> and reversed the main <electric current> to extract the resistive signal. He reported a rapid fall between $4.21$ and $4.19\,\mathrm K$ on his stated historical <temperature> scale, with <electrical resistance> disappearing at the lower value. The apparatus and control of spurious <electric potential differences> are part of the discovery, not incidental details. https://dwc.knaw.nl/DL/publications/PU00013242.pdf[Kamerlingh Onnes, Communication 124c (1911), §§2–3, pp. 819–821]{ref}

A modern version places a contacted sample and a calibrated <thermometer> close together in a cooling environment. Use <four-terminal sensing>, record the small applied <electric current> $I$, the sensed <electric potential difference> $V$, and the sample <temperature> $T$, then plot $R(T)=V/I$. Repeat on warming as well as cooling, and allow readings to stabilize near <thermal equilibrium>. Disagreement between warming and cooling is a reason to check sample–<thermometer> lag and reproducibility before quoting a <superconducting transition temperature>. Mikhailov and Kordosky's undergraduate experiment uses this strategy with liquid-nitrogen cooling. https://physics.wm.edu/~evmik/classes/2021_fall_Experimental_Atomic_Physics_251/lab_descriptions/superconductivity/[Mikhailov and Kordosky, Physics 251 superconductivity lab, measurement procedure and discussion]{ref}

Choose the measuring <electric current> small enough that <Joule heating> does not appreciably change the sample <temperature>, and repeat with a smaller <electric current> to check that the transition persists. Cancel stable offsets using the reversal described in <four-terminal sensing>. A reading indistinguishable from zero supplies an experimental upper bound, not a proof of exact zero <electrical resistance>. If $\delta V$ bounds the unresolved resistive <electric potential difference>, then
$$
|R|\lesssim\frac{\delta V}{|I|}.
$$
For a stipulated sensitivity $\delta V=20\,\mathrm{nV}$ and $I=1\,\mathrm{mA}$, this is $20\,\mathrm{\mu\Omega}$. The bound applies between the sensing contacts; it is not the <electrical resistance> of the complete wiring. A conducting path through part of an inhomogeneous sample can also make $V$ negligible, so the <field-cooled Meissner experiment> adds independent evidence about the material's magnetic response.

= Four-terminal resistance measurement
{synonym}

<Four-terminal sensing> uses one pair of contacts to carry <electric current> and a separate pair to sense <electric potential difference>. The sensing instrument draws negligible <electric current>, so the voltage drops along its own leads are negligible, and the current-contact drops outside the sensing interval are excluded. A measurement using just two shared leads includes their <electrical resistance> and contact drops, which can overwhelm the small sample signal in a <resistance-versus-temperature experiment for superconductivity>. Four contacts do not automatically remove offsets from the <thermoelectric effect>. https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-article/low-voltage-measurement-techniques[Tektronix, Low-Voltage Measurement Techniques, four-lead arrangement and thermoelectric offsets]{ref}

For a stable offset $V_0$, let $I>0$ denote the magnitude of the reversed <electric current>. = Field-cooled Meissner experiment
{parent=Superconductivity}
{c}

The <field-cooled Meissner experiment> starts above the <superconducting transition temperature> with a weak, steady applied <magnetic field> already penetrating a solid sample. Cooling while keeping the applied <magnetic field> fixed tests whether the interior <magnetic flux> changes. This is the decisive contrast with cooling in zero <magnetic field> and switching the <magnetic field> on afterwards. The diagram shows the two histories for the same final applied <magnetic field>.

Meissner and Ochsenfeld reported magnetic expulsion in 1933, showing that the transition involves more than vanishing <electrical resistance>. A modern motivating version appears in MIT's lead-sample experiment: surround the sample with a <solenoid> supplying a steady <magnetic field> and a separate pickup coil connected to a recording instrument. Apply the <magnetic field> while the lead is still normal, then cool through its transition without changing the <solenoid> <electric current>. Expulsion produces a transient pickup-coil <electric potential difference>; warming through the transition produces the opposite sign as <magnetic flux> returns. MIT also checks the signal direction by switching off the applied <magnetic field> while the sample is normal. https://hoffman.physics.harvard.edu/materials/SCintro.php[Hoffman laboratory, magnetic-expulsion discovery]{ref} https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-13-14-experimental-physics-i-ii-junior-lab-fall-2016-spring-2017/ec45c3030c7ec2ba0f0537ab84d09c17_MIT8_13-14F16-S17exp39.pdf#page=10[MIT Junior Lab, §5.2.2: Meissner effect in lead]{ref}

<Faraday's law of induction> explains the readout. For a stationary $N$-turn pickup coil, with the same linked <magnetic flux> $\Phi$ per turn and a fixed orientation,
$$
V_{\rm pick}(t)=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt},\qquad
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}V_{\rm pick}(t)\,dt=-N[\Phi(t_2)-\Phi(t_1)].
$$
Thus a transient measures a change in linked <magnetic flux>, not directly the static <magnetic field> at every point in the sample. For stipulated $N=100$ and $\Delta\Phi=-2\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{Wb}$ per turn, the signed pulse area is $+2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{V\,s}$. The apparatus geometry determines how a change in interior <magnetic field> contributes to that linked <magnetic flux>. Keeping the applied <magnetic field> fixed, repeating on warming, and checking a normal-state control make the expulsion interpretation testable; a stationary pickup coil has no sustained signal after the <magnetic flux> has stopped changing.

Why does the preparation history matter? In the stationary, ideal <perfect conductor> model, the interior <electric field> is zero. The differential form of <Faraday's law of induction> then gives
$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}=-\nabla\times\mathbf E=0.
$$
If the interior <magnetic field> was $\mathbf B_0$ when perfect conduction began, it remains $\mathbf B_0$; if it was zero, it stays zero when an external <magnetic field> is subsequently applied. Both a <perfect conductor> prepared in zero <magnetic field> and a sample exhibiting the <Meissner effect> can therefore exclude a newly applied weak <magnetic field>. Only the field-cooled comparison tests expulsion of an already present <magnetic field>. This argument concerns a stationary sample and the ideal zero-<electric field> model, not a moving conductor.

The equilibrium <London equations> supply the additional constraint. In a homogeneous region without penetrating flux tubes, they imply $\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2$. For a half-space $z\geq0$ with a <magnetic field> parallel to its flat boundary, fixed surface value $B_s$, and a bounded solution deep inside, this gives
$$
\mathbf B(z)=B_s e^{-z/\lambda_L}\,\widehat{\mathbf x}.
$$
At $z=5\lambda_L$ only $e^{-5}\simeq0.00674$ of the surface <magnetic field> remains. The screening <electric currents> near the surface produce this reduction. Unlike the frozen interior <magnetic field> of a <perfect conductor>, this equilibrium profile does not retain an arbitrary initial $\mathbf B_0$. The extra constitutive relation, and not zero <electrical resistance> by itself, selects the screened state. Expulsion occurs during the transition, when transient <electric fields> can be present; the steady-state zero-<electric field> argument does not describe the process of establishing <superconductivity>. The diagram gives equilibrium end states, not transition kinetics. https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1935.0048[F. and H. London (1935), magnetic screening beyond the acceleration equation]{ref}

These ideal comparisons concern a solid sample in equilibrium and a sufficiently weak <magnetic field>. Stronger <magnetic fields> can destroy <superconductivity> or permit <magnetic flux> to penetrate parts of a sample, so complete bulk expulsion is not a claim about every superconducting state. https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-763-applied-superconductivity-fall-2005/41b2882232b1b43823ec488969c21b6d_lecture1.pdf#page=6[Terry P. Orlando, Applied Superconductivity, Lecture 1 (2005), mixed-state magnetic penetration]{ref} A hole in a superconducting loop can retain <magnetic flux> even while the material surrounding it screens its own interior; see <flux conservation in a superconducting loop>. The hole is not superconducting material, so its retained <magnetic flux> does not contradict the <Meissner effect>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/III_21.html#Ch21-S7[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, III.21.7: field cooling a superconducting ring]{ref}

= Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity
{c}
{parent=Superconductivity}

<Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg> and <Lev Landau> applied an <order parameter> description to <superconductivity> in 1950. A spatially varying complex amplitude extends the fixed-amplitude description of the <London equations>. Their initial charge assumption preceded the later identification of the universal pair charge.<Nobel Lecture: On superconductivity and superfluidity (what I have and have not managed to do) as well as on the physical minimum at the beginning of the XXI century by Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg>[pp.983–984, complex order parameter and the charge question]{ref}

Consider the following isotropic, local SI <Helmholtz free energy> model near a continuous <superconducting transition temperature>:
$$
F[\psi,\mathbf A]=\int d^3x\left[
\alpha|\psi|^2+\frac{\beta}{2}|\psi|^4+
\frac{|(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A)\psi|^2}{2m_*}+
\frac{|\mathbf B|^2}{2\mu_0}\right],\qquad
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.
$$
Here $\beta>0$, $m_*>0$ is a phenomenological mass parameter, and $q=-2e$ fixes the electron-pair convention. The uniform superconducting minimum for $\alpha<0$ has $|\psi_0|^2=-\alpha/\beta$. The gradient term penalizes changes of the amplitude and the gauge-invariant <quantum phase>; the magnetic term penalizes <magnetic field> penetration. The <gauge-coupling sign in Ginzburg-Landau superconductivity> keeps the <electric current> and field equation consistent. This model omits material anisotropy, microscopic nonlocality and critical fluctuations.

= Time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau equation
{parent=Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity}
{title2=$\Gamma(\partial_t+iq\phi/\hbar)\Psi=-\delta F/\delta\Psi^*$}

A phenomenological near-transition relaxation equation for a charged complex <order parameter>, with a positive real relaxation coefficient in its simplest dissipative form. Under $\Psi\mapsto e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\Psi$, $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi$, the displayed time derivative transforms covariantly, as does the spatial derivative in the <Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity>. Microscopic applicability depends on regime and scattering processes; this equation is distinct from a universal conservative condensate wave equation.

= Pair-field effective action of a superconductor
{parent=Microscopic derivation of Ginzburg-Landau theory}
{title2=$S_{\rm eff}[\Delta]=\int |\Delta|^2/g-\operatorname{Tr}\ln G^{-1}[\Delta]$}

A pairing-channel <Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation> replaces an attractive fermion interaction by a complex <Cooper pair> field coupled to fermion pairs. = Microscopic Ginzburg-Landau coefficients from BCS theory
{parent=Microscopic derivation of Ginzburg-Landau theory}
{title2=$\beta_\Delta=7\zeta(3)N(0)/(8\pi^2(k_BT_c)^2),\quad K_\Delta=\beta_\Delta\hbar^2v_F^2/6$}

For a clean isotropic three-dimensional weak-coupling s-wave model, use the energy-valued gap $\Delta$, a <density of states> $N(0)$ per spin, and free-energy density $\alpha_\Delta|\Delta|^2+\beta_\Delta|\Delta|^4/2+K_\Delta|D\Delta|^2$. Near $T_c$, $\alpha_\Delta\simeq N(0)(T-T_c)/T_c$. The quartic coefficient follows from
$$
\beta_\Delta=N(0)k_BT_c\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{d\xi}{(\omega_n^2+\xi^2)^2},\qquad\omega_n=(2n+1)\pi k_BT_c.
$$
The integral is $\pi/(2|\omega_n|^3)$ and the positive odd-integer sum is $7\zeta(3)/8$, yielding the displayed coefficient. The isotropic Fermi-velocity average gives $K_\Delta=\beta_\Delta\hbar^2v_F^2/6$. The pair charge is $q=-2e$ in $D=\nabla-iq\mathbf A/\hbar$. These values change under a rescaling of $\Delta$, different density-of-states convention or different material model; the local expansion is a near-transition approximation.<Microscopic Derivation of the Ginzburg-Landau Equations in the Theory of Superconductivity by Lev Gor'kov>[Microscopic near-transition expansion; pair charge]{ref}

= Superconducting coherence length

= Josephson junction
{c}
{parent=Superconductivity}

A <Josephson junction> couples two regions with <superconductivity> through a weak link. A tunnel barrier is one implementation.

The remarkable possibility is a steady <electric current> across an insulating barrier at zero <electric potential difference>. This is the <DC Josephson effect>, carried by coherent transfer of <Cooper pairs>. It differs from <single-electron superconducting tunneling>, which probes the <quasiparticle> excitation spectrum and produces a dissipative <current-voltage characteristic>. Both contributions can occur in the same superconducting tunnel device. <Brian D. Josephson>[Josephson (1962), pp. 251–252, opening paragraph and equation (3)]{ref}

The <Josephson effect> turns a difference of <quantum phases> into a measurable <electric current>, and an applied <electric potential difference> into a rate of phase advance. The <Giaever gap experiment> instead turns an <electric potential difference> into an excitation <energy> scale. These complementary measurements expose two aspects of <superconductivity>: its coherent <order parameter> and its gapped <quasiparticle> spectrum. The microscopic pairing calculation belongs to <BCS theory> and the <BCS gap equation>. A <tunnel diode> uses a different device, a semiconductor <p-n junction>.

Adding <capacitance> and an external bias makes the <gauge-invariant Josephson phase> dynamical. <Current-biased Josephson phase dynamics> explains how its trapped <energy levels> and escape can be measured electrically.

= Gauge-invariant Josephson phase
{parent=Josephson effect}
{c}
{title2=$\phi=\theta_2-\theta_1-(q/\hbar)\int_1^2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$}

Write the superconducting <order parameters> as $\psi_j=|\psi_j|e^{i\theta_j}$, using a field of pair <electric charge> $q=-2e$. For a specified path across the <superconducting weak link>, define
$$
\phi=\theta_2-\theta_1-\frac{q}{\hbar}\int_1^2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l
=\theta_2-\theta_1+\frac{2e}{\hbar}\int_1^2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l.
$$
Here $\mathbf A$ is the magnetic <vector potential>. To check electromagnetic <gauge invariance>, use a scalar function $\chi$ and transform
$$
\mathbf A'=\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\qquad
\psi_j'=e^{iq\chi_j/\hbar}\psi_j.
$$
The change of $\theta_2-\theta_1$ is $q(\chi_2-\chi_1)/\hbar$, exactly cancelled by the change of the <line integral>. Thus $\phi$ is unchanged although each local <quantum phase> changes. This is the same charge/sign bookkeeping discussed in <gauge-coupling sign in Ginzburg-Landau superconductivity>. Different physical paths can enclose <magnetic flux>, so fixing the path matters.

For stationary reservoirs with equal internal pair <chemical potentials> and a time-independent <vector potential>, the local <Schrödinger equation> gives $\dot\theta_j=-qU_j/\hbar$ after dropping the common internal term. With <electric potentials> $U_j$ and $V=U_2-U_1$, subtraction gives $\dot\phi=-qV/\hbar=2eV/\hbar$. For a general circuit, $V$ denotes the corresponding measured electrochemical voltage and the electromagnetic contribution must be treated consistently. A bare difference of phases without the <vector potential> term is insufficient in an arbitrary gauge.

= Josephson coupling energy
{parent=Josephson effect}
{c}
{title2=$U_J=-E_J\cos\phi,\quad E_J=\hbar I_c/(2e)$}

The ideal <Josephson junction> has a phase-dependent <energy>
$$
U_J(\phi)=-E_J\cos\phi,\qquad E_J=\frac{\hbar I_c}{2e}.
$$
Differentiating and using the voltage–phase relation yields
$$
\frac{dU_J}{dt}=E_J\sin\phi\,\dot\phi=VI_s.
$$
Thus the ideal pair element stores and returns <energy>. At $V=0$ it carries a static <electric current> without electrical power loss. Under a nonzero periodic drive its instantaneous power need not vanish, but its <energy> returns after a full phase cycle. Real junctions also have a <capacitance>, <quasiparticle> transport and an external circuit; their total losses cannot be inferred from this conservative pair term alone.

= DC Josephson effect
{parent=Josephson effect}
{c}
{title2=$V=0,\quad |I_s|\leq I_c$}

At zero <electric potential difference>, $\dot\phi=0$ permits a nonzero constant <quantum phase> and hence a steady pair <electric current>. Under an imposed <electric current> $I_b$ with $|I_b|<I_c$, a static solution satisfies $\sin\phi=I_b/I_c$. The low-energy branch has $\cos\phi>0$. For example, a stipulated $I_b=I_c/2$ gives $\phi=\pi/6$ modulo $2\pi$; it does not require a voltage drop. In the ideal current-biased <energy> $-E_J\cos\phi-(\hbar I_b/2e)\phi$, the positive second <derivative> on that branch explains its local stability.

The <superconducting critical current> $I_c$ is a maximum available static pair <electric current>, not the inevitable <electric current> under a voltage bias. At $V=0$ the initial state and circuit select a phase and a current. Above $I_c$ this sinusoidal model has no static phase solution, but its two relations alone do not predict the complete resistive <current-voltage characteristic>, switching dynamics or hysteresis. Those require the circuit and dissipative channels.

= AC Josephson effect
{parent=Josephson effect}

= Current-biased Josephson phase dynamics
{parent=Josephson junction}
{title2=$M_\phi=C(\hbar/2e)^2$}

A <Josephson junction> can make a collective <quantum phase> behave as a measurable dynamical coordinate. In <current-biased Josephson phase dynamics>, the <gauge-invariant Josephson phase> $\phi$ moves in a <tilted Josephson washboard potential>; the junction's <capacitance> supplies its effective inertia. A trapped phase gives zero mean <electric potential difference>, while sustained phase advance produces a nonzero mean <electric potential difference>. <Quantized phase levels in a Josephson junction> and <quantum escape of a current-biased Josephson junction> test <quantum mechanics> for this collective coordinate.

Begin with a stipulated parallel <electrical circuit>: an ideal sinusoidal <Josephson junction>, <capacitance> $C>0$, and effective <electrical resistance> $R>0$. The <Kirchhoff node law> and <Josephson effect> give
$$
I_b+I_\mu(t)+I_N(t)=I_c\sin\phi+\frac{\hbar}{2eR}\dot\phi+\frac{\hbar C}{2e}\ddot\phi.
$$
Here $I_b$ is the constant bias <electric current>, $I_\mu$ is a deliberately applied <microwave> <electric current>, and $I_N$ represents unwanted fluctuating <electric current>. Multiplication by $\hbar/(2e)$ puts the undriven equation into the mechanical form
$$
M_\phi\ddot\phi+\eta\dot\phi+U'(\phi)=0,\qquad
M_\phi=C\left(\frac{\hbar}{2e}\right)^2,\qquad
\eta=\frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2e}\right)^2.
$$
The effective inertia is a coefficient for a dimensionless <quantum phase>, not a literal material <mass> moving through the insulating layer. The capacitor's <energy>, $CV^2/2=M_\phi\dot\phi^2/2$, provides a direct check of that identification. The effective $R$ includes loading by the external <electrical circuit>; a constant parallel $R$ is an approximation over the relevant <frequency> range.

The useful design freedom is that $I_b$ changes the confinement while $C$ changes its inertia. The same manufactured device can therefore connect a classical oscillation, discrete <energy levels>, and escape to an electrical readout. The <Martinis-Devoret-Clarke circuit experiments> separate these questions experimentally. <John Clarke>, <Michel H. Devoret> and <John M. Martinis> shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics for circuit experiments establishing macroscopic <quantum tunnelling> and discrete <energy levels>.<The Nobel Prize in Physics 2025 — Press release by Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences>[7 October 2025, p.1, award decision]{ref}

= Tilted Josephson washboard potential
{parent=Current-biased Josephson phase dynamics}
{title2=$U(\phi)=-E_J(\cos\phi+s\phi)$}

At fixed positive bias ratio $s=I_b/I_c$ with $0<s<1$, the <Josephson coupling energy> and work of the ideal bias source combine into
$$
U(\phi)=-E_J(\cos\phi+s\phi),\qquad E_J=\frac{\hbar I_c}{2e}.
$$
This is the effective <potential energy> for an unwrapped <gauge-invariant Josephson phase>; it includes the bias source and decreases by $2\pi sE_J$ on advancing one period. A local minimum and the adjacent downhill barrier are
$$
\phi_m=\arcsin s,\qquad \phi_b=\pi-\arcsin s,\qquad
\Delta U=U(\phi_b)-U(\phi_m)=2E_J\left[\sqrt{1-s^2}-s\arccos s\right].
$$
To verify the barrier, solve $U'=E_J(\sin\phi-s)=0$, select $U''(\phi_m)>0$ and $U''(\phi_b)<0$, and subtract their <potential energies>. The small-oscillation angular <frequency> is fixed by the curvature:
$$
U''(\phi_m)=E_J\sqrt{1-s^2},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{U''(\phi_m)}{M_\phi},\qquad
\omega_p=\sqrt{\frac{2eI_c}{\hbar C}}(1-s^2)^{1/4}.
$$
Thus the barrier and oscillation <frequency> both vanish as $s\to1^-$, but with different powers. For $\epsilon=1-s\ll1$, expanding $\arccos(1-\epsilon)$ and $\sqrt{2\epsilon-\epsilon^2}$ gives
$$
\Delta U\simeq\frac{4\sqrt2}{3}E_J\epsilon^{3/2},\qquad
\omega_p\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2eI_c}{\hbar C}}(2\epsilon)^{1/4}.
$$
The first approximation is for a nearly critical bias. At $s=0$ the exact downhill barrier is $2E_J$; at $s\geq1$ there is no confining local minimum in this model.

= Quantized phase levels in a Josephson junction
{parent=Current-biased Josephson phase dynamics}
{title2=$H_\phi=-\hbar^2\partial_\phi^2/(2M_\phi)+U(\phi)$}

For a weakly damped well, quantizing the conservative part of <current-biased Josephson phase dynamics> gives the local <Hamiltonian operator>
$$
H_\phi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_\phi}\frac{d^2}{d\phi^2}+U(\phi).
$$
The tilted <potential energy> is unbounded downhill. Its trapped <energy levels> are therefore long-lived resonances with escape lifetimes, rather than globally bound stationary states. Close to $\phi_m$, set $x=\phi-\phi_m$ and expand
$$
U(\phi_m+x)=U(\phi_m)+\frac12M_\phi\omega_p^2x^2-\frac{E_Js}{6}x^3+O(x^4).
$$
Keeping only the quadratic term gives the <quantum harmonic oscillator> result
$$
E_n-U(\phi_m)\simeq\hbar\omega_p\left(n+\frac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
It is useful for states localized near the minimum, with <energy> well below $\Delta U$. It does not count all the resonances accurately near escape. The omitted terms make successive spacings unequal. For the chosen example, the three plotted reference <energies> divided by $h$ are $1.44804$, $4.34412$ and $7.24020\,\mathrm{GHz}$ above the minimum.

A small <microwave> bias contributes $\delta U=-(\hbar/2e)I_\mu(t)\phi$. A one-<photon> transition between <energy levels> is resonant when $hf=E_{n+1}-E_n$. Exciting a higher <energy level> can strongly increase its escape probability. Measuring <microwave> absorption indirectly through escape therefore connects an invisible <quantum phase> state to a visible <electric potential difference>. A classical oscillation can also show resonance, so the mere existence of one peak is insufficient: the bias dependence and resolved unequal spacings are the discriminating predictions.

= Quantum escape of a current-biased Josephson junction
{parent=Current-biased Josephson phase dynamics}
{title2=$\Gamma$}

<Quantum escape of a current-biased Josephson junction> is decay of a trapped collective <quantum phase> state through a barrier in the <tilted Josephson washboard potential>. This barrier lies in the <potential energy> of an <electrical circuit> coordinate. Transfer of <Cooper pairs> across the physical insulating layer already supplies the <Josephson coupling energy>; it does not by itself demonstrate this further collective escape. <Single-electron superconducting tunneling> is a third, distinct process.

At higher <temperature> $T$, <thermal activation> instead lifts the coordinate over the barrier. In the rare-escape regime its characteristic form is the <Kramers escape rate>
$$
\Gamma_{\rm th}\simeq a_{\rm th}\frac{\omega_p}{2\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta U}{k_BT}\right),
$$
where $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and the prefactor depends on <damping> and thermal equilibration. Cooling suppresses this route. For a quasistationary <energy level> at <energy> $E$, the leading conservative <WKB approximation> instead has
$$
\Gamma_E\ \propto\ \exp\left[-\frac{2}{\hbar}\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2}\sqrt{2M_\phi\left(U(\phi)-E\right)}\,d\phi\right],
$$
with the two turning points bracketing the forbidden barrier. This is an exponential estimate requiring a large action compared with $\hbar$, slow escape compared with intrawell motion, and weak environmental corrections. It is not a complete rate prediction near the barrier top or with substantial <damping>.

The familiar cubic-barrier exponent can be checked independently. Choose $V(x)=M_\phi\omega_p^2x^2(1-x/a)/2$, with $a>0$. Its top is at $2a/3$ and $\Delta U=2M_\phi\omega_p^2a^2/27$. At the well-bottom reference <energy> $E=0$,
$$
B=\frac{2M_\phi\omega_p}{\hbar}\int_0^a x\sqrt{1-x/a}\,dx
=\frac{8M_\phi\omega_pa^2}{15\hbar}
=\frac{36\Delta U}{5\hbar\omega_p}.
$$
Substitution $u=x/a$ reduces the dimensionless <integral> to $\int_0^1u\sqrt{1-u}\,du=4/15$. This leading semiclassical action uses a cubic potential and a well-bottom reference; actual ground-state escape also needs the zero-point correction, prefactor and environment. In the chosen washboard example it gives $B\simeq74.0$ after matching the exact barrier and curvature. This matching is an illustrative approximation, not an exact action integral for the cosine potential.

Escape need not yield a sustained voltage: the coordinate can become trapped again. The running-state readout assumes sufficient <underdamping> and bias to avoid such retrapping. The parallel model has <quality factor> $Q=\omega_pRC$; large $Q$ makes energy loss per oscillation small. Low-temperature rate saturation alone cannot distinguish <quantum tunnelling> from unwanted heating or external noise.

= Martinis-Devoret-Clarke circuit experiments
{c}

The 1985 spectroscopy experiment used <niobium>-based tunnel <Josephson junctions> on <silicon>, thermally anchored with final bias <low-pass filters> to a <dilution refrigerator>. Calibrated <germanium> and <cobalt> thermometers measured <temperature>. A filtered <coaxial cable> injected <microwaves>; repeated switches to nonzero <electric potential difference> measured escape with irradiation on and off. Bias scans at fixed <microwave> <frequency> resolved separate enhancements matching calculated unequal <energy level> spacings. Raising <temperature> merged the structure toward classical response. Junction parameters were calibrated in the classical regime; reducing the <superconducting critical current> with a <magnetic field> checked thermal readout. Parameter uncertainties limited absolute resonance positions.<Energy-Level Quantization in the Zero-Voltage State of a Current-Biased Josephson Junction by John M. Martinis and others>[Original paper, pp.1543–1545, apparatus and Figs.2–4; p.1546, numerical boundary condition]{ref}

The companion escape experiment mounted a tunnel <Josephson junction> on an attenuating <coaxial cable>, cold-anchored its bias filters, ramped the <electric current>, and counted switches to nonzero <electric potential difference>. Rapid current shutoff limited heating. Classical escape and <microwave> resonance calibrated the <superconducting critical current>, <capacitance> and environmental loading. Low-temperature escape agreed with the quantum prediction using these calibrated parameters. A <magnetic field> reduced the <superconducting critical current> to keep the same apparatus thermally activated at lower <temperature>, testing excess noise. Together these tests connect spectral structure and escape of the same kind of collective coordinate. They do not constitute a demonstration of coherent gate operations on a <qubit>, or prove that every macroscopic coordinate retains quantum behavior under arbitrary environmental coupling. The attraction is more concrete: a fabricated <electrical circuit> admits both an independently measurable classical model and quantitative quantum tests of its phase dynamics.

= Switching-current distribution of a Josephson junction
{parent=Martinis-Devoret-Clarke circuit experiments}
{title2=$p(I)=\Gamma(I)S(I)/r$}

A <switching-current distribution of a Josephson junction> records the bias <electric current> at the first detected transition to a sustained nonzero <electric potential difference>. Interpret it using a stipulated monotone ramp $dI/dt=r>0$, a reproducible initial trapped state, and an instantaneous escape <hazard function> $\Gamma(I)$. Neglect retrapping and readout delay. The <survival function> $S$ obeys $dS/dt=-\Gamma S$, so changing variable from time to <electric current> gives
$$
S(I)=\exp\left[-\frac1r\int_{I_0}^I\Gamma(u)\,du\right],\qquad
p(I)=-\frac{dS}{dI}=\frac{\Gamma(I)}rS(I),\qquad
\Gamma(I)=\frac{rp(I)}{1-\int_{I_0}^Ip(u)\,du}.
$$
Here $p$ is a <probability density function> per unit <electric current>, not a rate per unit time. Division by the surviving population is essential: only trials still trapped can escape. A typical switching current is consequently not identical to the noiseless <superconducting critical current>.

\Image[josephson-escape-switching-readout.png]
{title=From trapped phase to a voltage event and an escape probability}
{description=Original functional readout model and chosen constant-rate calculation. Boxes represent functions rather than historical wiring. The plotted <survival function> and switching <probability density function> assume $\Gamma=1000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $r=1\,\mathrm{\mu A\,s^{-1}}$ with perfect event detection and no retrapping; they are not experimental traces.}

For an independent illustrative check, choose constant $\Gamma_0=1000\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $r=1\,\mathrm{\mu A\,s^{-1}}$. Then
$$
S(I)=e^{-\Gamma_0(I-I_0)/r},\qquad
\langle I-I_0\rangle=\frac r{\Gamma_0}=1\,\mathrm{nA}.
$$
Half the trials have switched after $\ln2/\Gamma_0=0.693147\,\mathrm{ms}$, corresponding to $0.693147\,\mathrm{nA}$ of ramp. Doubling $r$ doubles the mean increment without changing the underlying escape rate. Real junction rates vary sharply with bias; this example isolates the statistical conversion.

Repeating the preparation, measuring the ramp, resolving the voltage onset, and allowing cooling after each dissipative event are prerequisites for this interpretation. Missed events, finite detection delay, changing <temperature> and retrapping alter the measured distribution. Even a correctly reconstructed rate needs the independent thermal and spectral checks before identifying its escape mechanism.

= Single-electron superconducting tunneling
{parent=Superconductivity}

<Single-electron superconducting tunneling> transfers individual <electrons> across a barrier into or out of <quasiparticle> states of a material with <superconductivity>. In a normal-metal–insulator–superconductor junction, abbreviated NIS, only one electrode has <superconductivity>. Such a junction can reveal a <superconducting energy gap> without supporting a <DC Josephson effect> between two superconducting reservoirs.

For an elementary interpretation, assume an opaque barrier, approximately constant normal-electrode <density of states>, and an energy-independent tunneling matrix element over the range probed. Write the superconducting <density of states> divided by its normal-state value as $n_s(E)$, with $E$ measured from its <chemical potential>. A signed voltage convention chosen for positive normal-electrode injection gives
$$
I_{\rm qp}(V)=\frac{1}{eR_N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}n_s(E)\,[f(E-eV)-f(E)]\,dE,
$$
where $f$ is the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> at a common <temperature> and $R_N$ is the normal-state junction <electrical resistance>. This current convention is local to the NIS measurement. The expression counts occupied initial and empty final states; it is a quasiparticle transport model, not a <current-phase relation of a superconducting weak link>.

An original analytic illustration uses the ideal fully gapped <BCS theory> spectrum at zero <temperature>, with positive gap edge $\Delta$:
$$
n_s(E)=\begin{cases}0,&|E|<\Delta,\\ |E|/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2},&|E|>\Delta.\end{cases}
$$
For positive $V$, the occupation difference restricts the <integral> to $0<E<eV$. Since $d\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}/dE=E/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$, integration and odd extension give
$$
I_{\rm qp}(V)=\begin{cases}0,& |eV|\leq\Delta,\\ \displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(V)}{eR_N}\sqrt{(eV)^2-\Delta^2},& |eV|>\Delta.\end{cases}
$$
The threshold is $\Delta/e$ for this NIS model. In the corresponding zero-temperature single-electron process between two fully gapped superconductors, creating excitations on both sides instead requires $|eV|\geq\Delta_1+\Delta_2$. That quasiparticle threshold does not forbid coherent pair <electric current> below it. At finite <temperature>, thermally populated <quasiparticles> soften the simple threshold argument.

At zero <temperature> in the NIS approximation, $R_N\,dI_{\rm qp}/dV=n_s(eV)$ away from the singular gap edge. Thus a voltage sweep probes the excitation <density of states>. The ideal divergence is rounded in real measurements, and at nonzero <temperature> the <derivative> samples a thermally broadened range of <energies>. For $|eV|>\Delta$, the model has $VI_{\rm qp}>0$: electrical work creates excitations and is dissipated on relaxation. This distinguishes its steady transport from the conservative <Josephson coupling energy>.

= Parallel-path model of a superconducting ceramic
{parent=Transport and magnetic tests of superconducting ceramics}
{title2=$R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_n r/(R_n+r)$}

Consider an original <electrical circuit> model between two equipotential cross-sections. A normal path has <electrical resistance> $R_n>0$. Let $b\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether an additional path connects those same cross-sections; when connected it has <electrical resistance> $r\geq0$. Ideal voltage contacts measure those cross-sections. This collapses a spatial network to two parallel branches; it is not a fit to the <Bednorz-Müller experiment>.

For $r>0$, both branches have the same <electric potential difference> $V$. <Ohm's law> and the <Kirchhoff node law> give $I=V/R_n+bV/r$, hence
$$
R_{\mathrm{eff}}=\begin{cases}
R_n,&b=0,\\
\dfrac{R_n r}{R_n+r},&b=1,\ r>0,\\
0,&b=1,\ r=0.
\end{cases}
$$
The last case follows directly from the ideal short's constraint $V=0$, provided it can carry the imposed <electric current> below its <superconducting critical current>. It does not follow by substituting $r=0$ into $V/r$. A normal bridge in series with otherwise superconducting pieces belongs in $r$; superconducting grains alone need not short the measured interval.
