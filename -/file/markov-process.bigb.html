= Markov process
{c}
{wiki}

A Markov process is a <stochastic process> whose present state contains all the information in its observed past needed to predict its future. Precisely, if $\mathcal F_s$ is the <natural filtration> generated by observations up to time $s$, then, for $t>s$ and any <measurable set> $A$ of states,
$$
\mathbb P(X_t\in A\mid\mathcal F_s)=\mathbb P(X_t\in A\mid X_s).
$$
The equality is between <conditional probabilities> and holds <almost surely>. It expresses the <Markov property>: the past and future have <conditional independence> given the present. It does not assert <independence> of consecutive states. In the sunny/rainy example below, tomorrow's sunny <probability> is $0.8$ after sunshine but $0.4$ after rain, so knowing today's state matters.

The memory assumption concerns the chosen <state space>. If an equipment model's failure <probability> depends on its age, recording only “working” or “failed” may lose relevant information; recording age as part of the state can retain it. Similarly, a dependence on two previous observations can sometimes be represented by using their ordered pair as the current state. Whether such a description fits observations is a modeling question.

Transition <Markov kernels> describe the <conditional distribution> of a state at a later time. Successive kernels and the initial <probability distribution> determine every <finite-dimensional distribution>, through repeated <conditional probability>. The <time-homogeneous Markov property> is an additional assumption: the kernel depends on elapsed time rather than calendar time. The attraction is compression: prediction can proceed from a current state instead of an entire recorded history. <Brownian motion> and <continuous-time Markov chains> illustrate that a <Markov process> need not evolve in discrete time.

= Random affine recursion
{parent=Markov process}
{title2=$X_n=A_nX_{n-1}+B_n$}

A random affine recursion updates a real <random variable> by $X_n=A_nX_{n-1}+B_n$. Assume the coefficient pairs $(A_n,B_n)$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables>, independent of the initial value. The two coordinates within a pair may be dependent. Fresh coefficients make the recursion a time-homogeneous <Markov process>: its next <conditional distribution> depends on the current value alone.

The competition between the random multiplier and the fresh input determines whether initial conditions are forgotten. A <synchronous coupling> makes this particularly visible by using the same coefficient sequence for two initial values.

= Exponential invariant law of a random affine recursion
{parent=Random affine recursion}
{title2=$X_n=U_n(X_{n-1}+E_n),\quad X_\infty\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$}

Let $U_n$ have the <uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$ and let $E_n$ have an <exponential distribution> of rate $\lambda>0$, with all these inputs independent. The <random affine recursion> $X_n=U_n(X_{n-1}+E_n)$ has an invariant exponential law of the same rate. Indeed, for independent exponential $X,E$, <beta-gamma independence> says that $V=X/(X+E)$ is uniform and independent of $W=X+E$. Thus an independent uniform $U$ gives $UW$ the same law as $VW=X$.

For any deterministic real initial value $c$, couple the recursion to a stationary copy. <Synchronous contraction of a random affine recursion> gives an expected difference $2^{-n}\mathbb E|c-X_0|\leq2^{-n}(|c|+\lambda^{-1})$. Hence the one-time laws converge to $\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$, independently of $c$. An invariant initialization keeps every one-time law unchanged while the chain continues to fluctuate.

= Synchronous contraction of a random affine recursion
{parent=Random affine recursion}
{title2=$\mathbb E|X_n-Y_n|=\rho^n\mathbb E|X_0-Y_0|$}

Drive two copies of a <random affine recursion> with the same coefficient pairs, independent of their initial <coupling>. Then $X_n-Y_n=A_n(X_{n-1}-Y_{n-1})$. If $\rho=\mathbb E|A_1|<1$ and $\mathbb E|X_0-Y_0|<\infty$, <independence of random variables> gives
$$
\mathbb E|X_n-Y_n|=\rho^n\mathbb E|X_0-Y_0|\longrightarrow0.
$$
This proves <convergence in L1> and hence <convergence in probability> of the difference. If one copy starts in a <stationary distribution>, <Slutsky's theorem> shows that the other copy converges to that distribution. The argument compares paths before taking their one-time <probability distributions>; it does not require the paths ever to become exactly equal.

= Piecewise-deterministic Markov process
{parent=Markov process}
{title2=$L_tf=a(t,\cdot)f'+\alpha(K_tf-f)$}

A <Markov process> following a deterministic <flow of a vector field> between random jumps, with jump <hazard function> $\alpha(X_{t-})$ and destination <transition kernel> $K_t(X_{t-},dy)$. The integrated rate along the flowing path determines the next jump time. Retaining instead the holding times of a different piecewise-constant process need not yield a <Markov process> in the flowing state. Assume <nonexplosion> when describing paths at all finite times.

= Forward equation of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process
{parent=Piecewise-deterministic Markov process}
{title2=$\partial_tp=-\partial_x(ap)-\alpha p+\int\alpha(y)m_t(y,x)p(y)dy$}

If the destination <transition kernel> has <probability density function> $m_t(x,y)$, the formal <adjoint operator> of $L_tf=af'+\alpha(K_tf-f)$ is $(L_t^*p)(x)=-\partial_x(a(t,x)p(x))-\alpha(x)p(x)+\int\alpha(y)m_t(y,x)p(y)dy$. Multiply by a smooth compactly supported <test function>, use <integration by parts> on transport and <Fubini's theorem> on the jump gain to prove the formula when the integrals are finite. The general formulation is $\frac d{dt}\int f\,d\nu_t=\int L_tf\,d\nu_t$ for <probability measures> $\nu_t$. A deterministic initial value leaves positive no-jump atomic mass at finite times, so a full smooth density cannot be assumed for that initialization.

= Killed deterministic flow
{parent=Piecewise-deterministic Markov process}
{title2=$P^\alpha_{s,t}f(x)=f(\varphi(s,x,t))e^{-\int_s^t\alpha(\varphi(s,x,u))du}$}

The no-jump part of a <piecewise-deterministic Markov process>. The <chain rule> gives $\partial_tP^\alpha_{s,t}f=P^\alpha_{s,t}L_t^\alpha f$, where $L_t^\alpha f=a(t,\cdot)f'-\alpha f$. The time subscript is essential for a time-dependent <vector field>.

= First-jump decomposition of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process
{parent=Piecewise-deterministic Markov process}
{title2=$P_{s,t}=P^\alpha_{s,t}+\int_s^tP^\alpha_{s,u}\alpha K_uP_{u,t}\,du$}

For a <piecewise-deterministic Markov process>, conditional on no jump before $u$, the state is $z(u)=\varphi(s,x,u)$ and the <survival probability> is $S(u)=\exp[-\int_s^u\alpha(z(r))dr]$. The first jump has <probability density function> $\alpha(z(u))S(u)$; conditioning on its time and its destination gives $P_{s,t}f(x)=S(t)f(z(t))+\int_s^t\alpha(z(u))S(u)\int P_{u,t}f(y)K_u(z(u),dy)du$. This proves the identity for bounded <Borel measurable functions> under <nonexplosion>.

= Markov operator
{parent=Markov process}
{c}
{wiki}

A positive <linear operator> on bounded <measurable functions> that preserves the constant function one. A <Markov semigroup> is a time-indexed family of such operators with the semigroup law.

{title2=$P_tg(x)=\mathbb E_xg(X_t)$}

= Transition semigroup
{synonym}

The transition operators of a time-homogeneous <Markov process> satisfy $P_{t+s}=P_tP_s$ by the <Markov property>. They preserve constants and positivity. When the generator and function domain permit differentiation, $\partial_tP_tg=L(P_tg)$ is the <Kolmogorov backward equation>. <Transition semigroup of a continuous-time Markov chain> is the discrete-state special case.

= Invariant probability law of a Markov process
{parent=Markov process}
{title2=$\nu P_t=\nu\quad(t\ge0)$}

= Invariant probability measure of a Markov process
{synonym}

A <probability measure> is invariant for a <Markov process> with transition semigroup $P_t$ when starting with that law gives the same law at every later time. Equivalently, $\int P_tf\,d\nu=\int f\,d\nu$ for every bounded measurable function. Invariant laws may be constructed by <weak convergence of probability measures> of evolved laws when the <Feller semigroup> property permits passing the semigroup through the limit, as for the <extremal invariant measures of the contact process>.

= Affine process
{parent=Markov process}
{title2=$\log\mathbb E_x e^{\theta X_1}=A(\theta)x+B(\theta)$}

An affine process is a time-homogeneous <Markov process> whose conditional <moment-generating function> is exponential-affine in its starting state. In discrete time one writes $\log\mathbb E_x e^{\theta X_1}=A(\theta)x+B(\theta)$ on the finite <exponential moment> domain. Continuous-time definitions allow the functions to depend also on elapsed time.

= Affine autoregressive process
{parent=Affine process}
{title2=$X_t=aX_{t-1}+b+\xi_t$}

An <autoregressive process of order one> with <independent and identically distributed random variables> as innovations is an <affine process> when the innovation <exponential moments> are finite. Its one-step coefficients are $A(\theta)=a\theta$ and $B(\theta)=b\theta+\psi(\theta)$, where $\psi$ is the innovation <cumulant-generating function>.

= Joint affine transform
{parent=Affine process}

For a scalar discrete-time <affine process>, the conditional <cumulant-generating function> of $\sum_{j=1}^t\theta_jX_j$ is affine in $X_0$. Backward effective parameters $q_t=\theta_t$, $q_j=\theta_j+A(q_{j+1})$ give coefficients $A(q_1)$ and $\sum_jB(q_j)$.

= Doob h-transform
{c}
{parent=Markov process}
{title2=$P_t^hf(x)=h(x)^{-1}P_t(hf)(x)$}

For a killed <Markov process> and a positive harmonic function $h$, its Doob h-transform weights the path law through a stopping time $\tau$ by $h(X_\tau)/h(X_0)$, with localization when necessary. It describes conditioning on a boundary behaviour of probability zero. In the <complex upper half-plane>, $h(z)=\operatorname{Im}z$ conditions killed <planar Brownian motion> to remain in that half-plane.

= Brownian change of measure by a positive space-time harmonic function
{c}
{parent=Doob h-transform}
{title2=$dX_t=dB_t^{\mathbb Q}+\partial_x\log h(X_t,t)dt$}

If $h>0$ is smooth and $Z_t=h(X_t,t)$ is the unit-mean density martingale of a locally absolutely continuous probability measure relative to Wiener measure, then $h_t+\frac12h_{xx}=0$. The <Itô formula> gives $Z=\mathcal E(\int(h_x/h)(X_s,s)dX_s)$ when $Z_0=1$. The true-martingale hypothesis is already supplied by the density process. On each finite horizon the <Girsanov theorem> makes $X-\int(h_x/h)(X_s,s)ds$ Brownian under the new measure. Positivity provides local equivalence; global infinite-horizon equivalence and the Novikov condition are not additional requirements.

= Brownian motion conditioned to exit at a boundary point
{c}
{parent=Doob h-transform}
{title2=$h(z)=P_D(z,a)/P_D(z_0,a)$}

Killed <planar Brownian motion> conditioned to exit at a smooth boundary point $a$ is the <Doob h-transform> by its positive <Poisson kernel>, normalized at the starting point. Its generator is $\tfrac12\Delta+\nabla\log h\cdot\nabla$. Conditioning on shrinking boundary arcs yields the same localized laws and specifies a canonical version of an otherwise probability-zero conditioning event.

= Radial restriction for a conditioned Brownian path
{parent=Brownian motion conditioned to exit at a boundary point}
{title2=$\mathbb P(B\subset U)=|\Phi_U'(1)|$}

For a Brownian path in the unit disc from $0$ conditioned to exit at $1$, let $\Phi_U:U\to D$ fix $0$ and $1$, and suppose $U$ agrees with the disc near $1$. The probability of avoiding $D\setminus U$ is the ratio of the boundary Poisson-kernel densities, hence $|\Phi_U'(1)|$. Given avoidance, the conformally mapped path has the original law up to the <conformal Brownian clock>. There is no conformal-radius factor at the interior starting point.

= Hidden Markov model
{c}
{parent=Markov process}
{wiki}

A hidden Markov model is a <Markov process> whose state is latent and observed only through random emissions whose conditional distribution depends on the current hidden state.

= Emission probability
{parent=Hidden Markov model}
{title2=$b_i(x)=P(O_t=x\mid Z_t=i)$}

An <emission probability> in a <Hidden Markov model> is the probability of an observation conditional on its hidden state. For a finite observation alphabet, each state's emission probabilities sum to one. A <match state> can therefore emit several different characters, each with its own probability.

= Backward algorithm
{parent=Hidden Markov model}
{title2=$\beta_t(i)$}

The <backward algorithm> computes suffix likelihoods in a <Hidden Markov model>. With $\beta_t(i)=P(o_{t+1},\ldots,o_T\mid Z_t=i)$, its recurrence is $\beta_t(i)=\sum_j a_{ij}b_j(o_{t+1})\beta_{t+1}(j)$. Together with the <forward algorithm>, it gives posterior state probabilities proportional to $\alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)$. The terminal condition must include any explicit End-state transition.

= Forward algorithm
{parent=Hidden Markov model}
{title2=$\alpha_t(i)$}

The <forward algorithm> computes sequence likelihoods by summing over all hidden paths through a <Hidden Markov model>. For emitting states, $\alpha_t(i)=P(o_1,\ldots,o_t,Z_t=i)$ obeys $\alpha_{t+1}(j)=b_j(o_{t+1})\sum_i\alpha_t(i)a_{ij}$. It reuses partial sums rather than enumerating exponentially many state sequences. Silent states require additional transitions without consuming an observation. Scaling or <log-sum-exp> prevents <floating-point underflow>.

= Insertion state
{parent=Hidden Markov model}

An <insertion state> in a <profile hidden Markov model> emits characters between successive <match states>. A bypass allows zero inserted characters and a self-transition allows several. Under a fixed loop probability $r<1$, the length of an insertion run conditional on entering has a <geometric distribution> on positive integers with exit probability $1-r$.

= Match state
{parent=Hidden Markov model}

A <match state> in a <profile hidden Markov model> emits one aligned character at a particular profile position whenever it is visited. “Match” names its alignment role, not a requirement to emit one deterministic character or to match another sequence exactly.

= Profile hidden Markov model
{parent=Hidden Markov model}

A <profile hidden Markov model> represents the position-dependent structure of a <sequence alignment> using an ordered series of <match states> and optional <insertion states>, commonly with silent deletion states as well. An emitting state chooses one character from its <probability mass function>; transitions describe which profile position comes next. If deletion states are forbidden, an alignment column containing gaps cannot be a compulsory <match state>.

= Screening misclassification model
{parent=Hidden Markov model}

In a <screening misclassification model>, true disease states are latent and screen results are emissions. A cancer-free state may emit a positive result with a <false-positive rate>, while a perfectly sensitive pre-clinical state always emits a positive result. Conditional independence of emissions given latent states is an additional model assumption, not a consequence of a marginal false-positive rate.

= Chapman-Kolmogorov equation
{c}
{parent=Markov process}
{wiki}

For a time-homogeneous Markov process with transition kernel $P_t(x,dy)$,
$$
P_{s+t}(x,A)=\int P_s(x,dy)P_t(y,A).
$$
When transition densities exist, this becomes
$$
p(s+t,x,z)=\int p(s,x,y)p(t,y,z)dy.
$$
