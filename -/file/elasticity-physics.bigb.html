= Elasticity
{synonym}

Elasticity studies reversible deformation and the stresses produced by it.

= Elastic modulus
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}
{wiki}

A coefficient relating an appropriate stress and strain in the linear elastic regime. <Young's modulus>, <shear modulus> and <bulk modulus> describe different deformation modes.

= Buckling
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

Loss of stability of a loaded configuration toward a bent or folded form. The threshold and deformation mode depend on geometry, material properties and boundary conditions.

= Buckling of a synthetic film
{parent=Buckling}
{title2=$\kappa q^4+\sigma q^2<0$}

A flat, unsupported film's bending and <tension> energies give modal stiffness $\kappa q^4+\sigma q^2$. Negative <tension> represents compression, destabilizing sufficiently long waves. A finite sheet restricts allowed <wave numbers>, while a supporting foundation adds stiffness and can select a wrinkle wavelength. This is generic continuum mechanics, independent of biological membrane structure.

= Eigenstrain
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}
{title2=$\varepsilon^*$}

An <eigenstrain> is a local preferred deformation in the absence of <stress>. Constraints or incompatibility can make the actual <strain> differ from it, generating <stress>. <Magnetostrictive strain> is one example.

= Magnetoelastic coupling
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

<Magnetoelastic coupling> links a material's magnetic state to its <strain> and <stress>. <Magnetostriction> describes a resulting deformation; mechanical loading can also change the magnetic state.

= Elastic deformation
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

An elastic deformation is recoverable when the deforming loads are removed. In a local elastic constitutive model the <stress> depends on the present deformation. When this response also derives from a stored <elastic energy>, the material is a <hyperelastic material>. In the small-deformation approximation, the relevant kinematic variable is the <infinitesimal strain tensor>.

= Perfectly bonded elastic interface
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}
{title2=$[u]=0,\quad[\sigma]n=0$}

A <perfectly bonded elastic interface> has continuous <displacement field> and continuous <traction>, with the same chosen normal on both sides. For piecewise affine <displacement fields>, the difference of their gradients annihilates every tangential vector, and hence has the form $a\otimes n$. Their <infinitesimal strain tensors> differ by $\operatorname{sym}(a\otimes n)$.

= Torsion and tilt stiffness of a bonded elastic disk
{parent=Perfectly bonded elastic interface}
{title2=$K_V=\pi Ga^4/(2h),\quad K_H=\pi Ma^4/(4h)$}

A circular elastic layer of thickness $h\ll a$ bonded between rigid faces develops <shear strain> $r\phi/h$ under a relative twist $\phi$. Integrating the <shear stress> times its moment arm gives $K_V=GJ_p/h$. A small relative tilt produces longitudinal <strain> $y\theta/h$; when lateral deformation is constrained, the corresponding normal traction gives $K_H=MI/h$. The <polar second moment of area> is $J_p=\pi a^4/2$ and the diametric <second moment of area> is $I=\pi a^4/4$. Edge relaxation limits this thin-layer approximation, especially in a nearly incompressible material.

= Static elastic equilibrium
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}
{title2=$\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$}

A symmetric <stress tensor> is in <static elastic equilibrium> when its divergence balances the prescribed <body force>. Its boundary <traction> is $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$. With zero <body force>, <integration by parts> gives $\int_\Omega\sigma:\operatorname{sym}\nabla v=\int_{\partial\Omega}t\cdot v$. This is the weak <principle of virtual work> for a static elastic body.

= Elastic beam
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

An <elastic beam> is a slender body that supports bending moments and transverse loads. In the small-slope Euler-Bernoulli approximation its bending moment is $M=EIy_{xx}$, and transverse momentum balance gives $\rho Ay_{tt}+EIy_{xxxx}=q$ for a uniform beam under distributed force per length $q$. Here $I$ is the <second moment of area>; boundary conditions specify the end constraints and applied moments and forces.

= Euler strut
{parent=Elastic beam}
{c}

An <Euler strut> is an initially straight slender elastic column loaded in axial compression. Its lateral stability depends on <bending stiffness>, <length> and endpoint constraints. <Euler buckling> predicts a loss of straight-configuration stability before material failure only in the appropriate slender elastic regime; it is distinct from crushing or plastic yielding of a short column.

= Euler elastica
{c}
{parent=Elastic beam}
{title2=$EI\theta''+F\sin\theta=0$}

The planar equilibrium of an inextensible slender beam can have large tangent <angle> while retaining small elastic <strain> across its thickness. For constant horizontal compressive <force> $F$, moment balance and <curvature> $\theta'=d\theta/ds$ give the displayed nonlinear equation. Multiplication by $\theta'$ gives the first integral $EI\theta'^2/2-F\cos\theta=C$. A tensile load has $F<0$ in this convention.

= Tensile elastica loop
{parent=Euler elastica}
{title2=$\theta=4\arctan e^{s\sqrt{T/(EI)}}$}

An ideal zero-thickness planar loop under tension $T$ is a separatrix of the elastica equation. Its tangent turns from zero to $2\pi$, the same physical orientation at both ends. With $\alpha=\sqrt{T/(EI)}$, integration gives $x=s-2\tanh(\alpha s)/\alpha$ and $y=2\operatorname{sech}(\alpha s)/\alpha$. Its height is $2\sqrt{EI/T}$. Self-contact and finite beam thickness are absent from this ideal crossing solution.

= Cantilever
{parent=Elastic beam}
{wiki}

A beam fixed at one end and free at the other. A transverse end force $F$ gives bending moment $M(x)=F(L-x)$ and, for a rectangular section, maximum bending stress $6FL/(wh^2)$ at the fixed end. Longitudinal tension and compression are greatest on opposite outer fibres.

= Self-weight deflection of a square cantilever
{parent=Cantilever}
{title2=$y(l)=3\rho g l^4/(2Ea^2)$}

A uniform square section has <second moment of area> $a^4/12$ and gravitational load per length $\rho g a^2$. Euler-Bernoulli bending therefore gives $y''''=12\rho g/(Ea^2)$, with zero displacement and slope at the clamp and zero moment and shear at the free end. Integrating gives the displayed tip deflection and $y(x)=\rho g x^2(x^2-4lx+6l^2)/(2Ea^2)$. Small slope and <strain> are required.

= Dead loading
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

= Dead load
{synonym}

In an elastic <boundary value problem>, a dead load is a prescribed force independent of the incremental displacement. A dead reference <traction> has $\delta t^0=0$, even when the current face changes orientation. This differs from a follower load, whose direction follows the moving body, and from the structural-engineering use of the same phrase for permanent self-weight. Under pure dead <tractions>, a constant incremental translation remains a zero-frequency mode unless a displacement condition fixes it.

= Magnetostriction
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

<Magnetostriction> is a change of a material's shape or dimensions associated with its <magnetization>. A <magnetic field> can therefore produce <strain> without a contact <force>. The resulting <magnetoelastic coupling> lets a <ferromagnet> convert changes of magnetic state into mechanical motion; a constraint can instead turn the preferred deformation into <stress>. <James Prescott Joule> published his study of <iron> and <steel> bars in 1847.<On the effects of magnetism upon the dimensions of iron and steel bars by James Prescott Joule>[1847, volume 30, pp. 76–87]{ref}

The surprising point is that reversing the <magnetization> need not reverse the deformation. A material can prefer a longer axis along either $+\mathbf m$ or $-\mathbf m$: an axis has no arrow. <Isotropic linear magnetostriction vanishes> excludes one particular <linear> constitutive term, while <saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain> supplies a nonzero response with a <quadratic dependence> on the magnetic direction. The <pulsed-field optical magnetostriction experiment> shows how an actual length change can be resolved, and <quadratic magnetostriction and frequency doubling> separates an illustrative response law from a measured material curve.

= Strain-mediated magnetoelectric coupling
{parent=Magnetostriction}

Coupling in which a magnetic material's preferred <strain> transfers mechanical <stress> to a piezoelectric constituent, producing an electrical response. It depends on interfaces, elastic load sharing and electrical boundary conditions; a composite's measured <voltage> is not by itself an intrinsic single-phase magnetoelectric coefficient.

= Magnetostrictive actuator
{parent=Magnetostriction}

A <magnetostrictive actuator> uses <magnetostriction> to produce displacement or <force>. Its free motion and its blocked <stress> are different boundary conditions, as shown in <free strain and blocked magnetostrictive stress>.

= Magnetostrictive strain
{parent=Magnetostriction}
{title2=$\varepsilon^{\rm mag}_{ij}$}

<Magnetostrictive strain> is the preferred <strain> associated with a specified magnetic state relative to a stated reference. The measured <strain> also depends on mechanical constraints.

= Volume magnetostriction
{parent=Magnetostrictive strain}
{title2=$\Delta V/V$}

<Volume magnetostriction> is the relative <volume> change associated with a magnetic-state change. To first order it is the <trace> of the <magnetostrictive strain>. Zero <trace> is an additional restriction, not a consequence of <isotropy> alone.

= Saturation magnetostriction
{parent=Magnetostrictive strain}
{title2=$\lambda_s$}

In the <saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain> convention, <saturation magnetostriction> is the longitudinal relative length change from a random-domain reference to alignment. A parallel-to-perpendicular length difference instead equals $3\lambda_s/2$ in that model.

= Isotropic linear magnetostriction vanishes
{parent=Magnetostriction}
{title2=$A_{ijk}=b\epsilon_{ijk},\quad\sigma_{ij}=A_{ijk}B_k=0$}

Under proper rotations a third-rank <isotropic tensor> is proportional to the <Levi-Civita symbol>. Its contraction with a field vector is antisymmetric in the two stress indices. A physical symmetric stress therefore vanishes in this linear constitutive model. Under the full orthogonal <group> an ordinary isotropic third-rank <tensor> is already zero; the orientation-sensitive Levi-Civita <tensor> requires the usual pseudotensor distinction.

= Saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain
{parent=Magnetostriction}
{title2=$\varepsilon^{\rm mag}_{ij}=\frac{3\lambda_s}{2}(m_i m_j-\delta_{ij}/3)$}

Consider a mechanically free <isotropic> model of a saturated <ferromagnet>, at fixed <temperature> and fixed magnitude of <magnetization>. Let $\mathbf m=\mathbf M/M_s$ be a <unit vector>, where $M_s$ is the <saturation magnetization>. The <magnetostrictive strain> is an <infinitesimal strain tensor>, measured relative to a reference with randomly oriented <magnetic domains>. This reference convention matters: it sets the orientational average of the preferred <strain> to zero. Define $\lambda_s$, the <saturation magnetostriction>, as the longitudinal relative length change in this model.

<Rotational symmetry> permits a symmetric second-rank <tensor> made from $\mathbf m$ of the form $a\delta_{ij}+b m_i m_j$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. If we restrict attention to a change of shape with zero first-order <volume> change, its <trace> gives $3a+b=0$. Requiring the longitudinal component to equal $\lambda_s$ gives $a+b=\lambda_s$. Hence
$$
\varepsilon^{\rm mag}_{ij}=\frac{3\lambda_s}{2}\left(m_i m_j-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{3}\right),\qquad
\varepsilon_\parallel=\lambda_s,\qquad
\varepsilon_\perp=-\frac{\lambda_s}{2}.
$$
The <tensor product> $\mathbf m\otimes\mathbf m$ is symmetric and unchanged under $\mathbf m\mapsto-\mathbf m$. It uses two copies of the magnetic direction, so the third-rank <isotropic tensor> obstruction in <isotropic linear magnetostriction vanishes> does not apply. More generally, a small-<magnetization> expansion can contain $a M^2\delta_{ij}+b M_iM_j$; <isotropy> alone does not require its <trace> to vanish. The displayed saturated formula isolates the volume-preserving orientational contribution, rather than asserting that every material has zero <volume magnetostriction>.

Along a measuring <unit vector> $\mathbf n$, with $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m=\cos\theta$, the fractional length change is
$$
\frac{\Delta L_{\mathbf n}}{L}=n_i\varepsilon^{\rm mag}_{ij}n_j
=\frac{\lambda_s}{2}(3\cos^2\theta-1).
$$
For a stipulated $\lambda_s=20\times10^{-6}$ and reference length $L=100\,\mathrm{mm}$, alignment gives $+2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and a perpendicular orientation gives $-1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Rotating from perpendicular to parallel therefore changes the length by $3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, even though the saturated longitudinal value relative to the random reference is $2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. A negative $\lambda_s$ reverses these elongation and contraction signs.

The <eigenvalues> of this <infinitesimal strain tensor> are $\lambda_s,-\lambda_s/2,-\lambda_s/2$, so $\Delta V/V=\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon^{\rm mag}=0$ to first order. This is not exact finite <incompressibility>: using the illustrative <deformation gradient> $F=I+\varepsilon^{\rm mag}$ gives
$$
\det F=(1+\lambda_s)(1-\lambda_s/2)^2
=1-\frac34\lambda_s^2+\frac14\lambda_s^3.
$$
A finite-deformation law would need higher-order corrections if exact <volume> conservation were required.

= Magnetic reversal and domain averaging
{parent=Saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain}
{title2=$\varepsilon^{\rm mag}(-\mathbf m)=\varepsilon^{\rm mag}(\mathbf m)$}

A full $180^\circ$ reversal leaves <saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain> unchanged. A $90^\circ$ rotation generally changes it. In the simplest unconstrained orientation average over <magnetic domains>, replace $m_i m_j$ by $\langle m_i m_j\rangle$, not by $\langle m_i\rangle\langle m_j\rangle$. Equal populations pointing along $+z$ and $-z$ have zero net <magnetization> but the same preferred <strain> as a single domain along $z$. An isotropic orientation distribution instead has $\langle m_i m_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}/3$ and zero average preferred <strain>. Elastic compatibility between real <magnetic domains> can modify the macroscopic response.

Reversing an applied <magnetic field> does not guarantee that all microscopic orientations retrace the same path. <Hysteresis>, <magnetic domain-wall pinning>, and changes in domain populations can alter the measured curve. The reversal identity concerns the same pair of magnetic states with opposite local <magnetization>; it is not a universal prediction that an arbitrary experimental sweep is a single-valued even function of applied <magnetic field>.

= Pulsed-field optical magnetostriction experiment
{parent=Magnetostriction}

A $3\times1.5\times1.5\,\mathrm{mm^3}$ <single crystal> of <gadolinium antimonide> carried a $2\,\mathrm{mm}$ <fibre Bragg grating> cyanoacrylate-bonded along its $a$ axis. The pulsed <magnetic field> paralleled that axis. Broadband light, a circulator and a spectrometer resolved reflected-wavelength shifts at $5.4\,\mathrm K$. Pre-pulse readings measured noise; low fibre <thermal expansion> reduced thermal background. A residual post-pulse shift indicated possible heating. Spectral distortion checked bond uniformity, while the fibre's smaller cross-section limited loading; bonding was not assumed force-free. Longitudinal <strain> showed a kink near $32.6\,\mathrm T$.<High resolution magnetostriction measurements in pulsed magnetic fields using Fibre Bragg Gratings by Ramzy Daou and others>[PDF pp. 2–4, Figs. 1–2]{ref}

= Quadratic magnetostriction and frequency doubling
{parent=Magnetostriction}
{title2=$\varepsilon_{zz}=\kappa H^2$}

As an original stipulated low-field model, take the axial <magnetic field intensity> $H$ in $\mathrm{A/m}$ and prescribe
$$
q(H)=\kappa H^2,\qquad
\varepsilon_{zz}=q(H),\qquad
\varepsilon_{xx}=\varepsilon_{yy}=-\frac{q(H)}2,
\qquad \kappa=5\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m^2/A^2}.
$$
All <strain> components are dimensionless, and their <trace> vanishes. This is an even, quasistatic, unsaturated toy law with a fixed measuring axis. It is distinct from the fixed-magnitude directional model in <saturated isotropic magnetostrictive strain>. In particular, one must not replace local unit <magnetization> by normalized net <magnetization> in a many-domain specimen and assume this law follows.

For $H(t)=H_0\sin(2\pi ft)$, $H_0=20\,\mathrm{kA/m}$, and $f=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$, a <trigonometric identity> gives
$$
q(t)=\frac{\kappa H_0^2}{2}[1-\cos(4\pi ft)]
=10^{-5}[1-\cos(2\pi(100\,\mathrm{Hz})t)].
$$
The axial <strain> ranges from zero to $20\times10^{-6}$, with mean $10\times10^{-6}$ and a $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ component of amplitude $10\times10^{-6}$. The <cosine> coefficient is negative with the chosen time origin. For $L=100\,\mathrm{mm}$ the length ranges from zero to $2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ relative to $H=0$. There is no $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ term in this unbiased model.

A static bias $H_b$ changes the conclusion. Expanding $\kappa[H_b+H_0\sin(2\pi ft)]^2$ produces a fundamental-frequency term $2\kappa H_bH_0\sin(2\pi ft)$ as well as the doubled-frequency term. This is an incremental <linear response> about a state with a chosen magnetic axis; it does not contradict <isotropic linear magnetostriction vanishes> at an unbiased isotropic reference. Real <hysteresis>, <thermal expansion> from <Joule heating>, and mechanical <resonance> can add further components or change their phases. Frequency alone cannot identify the cause of motion: <Joule heating> also contains an even-in-<electric-current> contribution.

= Free strain and blocked magnetostrictive stress
{parent=Magnetostriction}
{title2=$\sigma=E(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\rm mag})$}

An <eigenstrain> is the deformation a material would prefer locally without applied <stress>. In a one-dimensional <linear elasticity> model, let $E$ be <Young's modulus>, take tension as positive <stress>, and write $\sigma=E(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\rm mag})$. A free rod has $\sigma=0$ and follows $\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{\rm mag}$. A perfectly blocked rod has $\varepsilon=0$ and $\sigma=-E\varepsilon^{\rm mag}$. Thus positive preferred elongation produces compressive <stress> when blocked.

For stipulated $E=100\,\mathrm{GPa}$, cross-sectional area $A=1\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, and $\varepsilon^{\rm mag}=20\times10^{-6}$, blocking gives $\sigma=-2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and axial <force> $F=\sigma A=-2\,\mathrm N$. This idealization ignores changes of <magnetization> caused by the load itself. A real <magnetostrictive actuator> therefore needs both magnetic and mechanical constitutive information: a free-length measurement alone does not determine its loaded motion.

= Betti identity for elastodynamic fields
{c}
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}
{title2=$\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\sigma^a\mathbf u^b-\boldsymbol\sigma^b\mathbf u^a)=\rho(\omega_b^2-\omega_a^2)\mathbf u^a\cdot\mathbf u^b+\mathbf u^a\cdot\mathbf f^b-\mathbf f^a\cdot\mathbf u^b$}

For two Fourier <elastic wave> fields with the same real <elastic stiffness tensor>, its major symmetry cancels the cross-strain terms in the <divergence> displayed above. The force-balance equations supply the remaining volume terms, and the <divergence theorem> gives their boundary integral of reciprocal <tractions>. Taking one field to be a <complex conjugate> gives energy-work identities and weighted modal <orthogonality>. The bilinear identity uses no <complex conjugation> until that field is chosen explicitly.

= Inflation and extension of an incompressible tube
{parent=Finite elasticity}
{title2=$r^2=a^2+(R^2-a_0^2)/z$}

For reference radii $a_0,b_0$, current inner radius $a$ and uniform axial stretch $z>0$, <incompressibility> integrates to $r^2=a^2+(R^2-a_0^2)/z$. The <principal stretches> are $r_{,R}=1/(z\lambda)$, $\lambda=r/R$ and $z$. Write $\widehat W(\lambda,z)=W((z\lambda)^{-1},\lambda,z)$ and $\mathcal E=2\pi\int_{a_0}^{b_0}\widehat W R\,dR$ per reference length. <Virtual work> gives $p=\mathcal E_{,a}/(2\pi az)$ and the total axial wall load $N_{\rm wall}=\mathcal E_{,z}$, both derivatives holding the other displayed parameter fixed. For pressurized closed ends the independently applied axial load is $N_{\rm ext}=N_{\rm wall}-\pi a^2p$. The distinction follows from the axial <pressure> power $\pi a^2p\dot z$.

= Constrained second-variation stability in elasticity
{parent=Finite elasticity}

For an equilibrium with $P_{Ii}=W_{,F_{iI}}+q\phi_{,F_{iI}}$ and dead loads, the constrained <second variation> on $\phi_{,F}:\delta F=0$ is
$$
\mathcal Q[\delta F]=\int_\Omega(W_{,FF}+q\phi_{,FF})[\delta F,\delta F]\,dX.
$$
Indeed, a constraint-preserving perturbation path has $\phi_{,F}:\delta^2F=-\phi_{,FF}[\delta F,\delta F]$, and stationarity of the total potential supplies the multiplier term. Linearized kinetic energy plus $\mathcal Q/2$ is conserved. Positivity gives the usual energy-norm linear stability; control in a specified displacement norm additionally requires the corresponding coercivity and treatment of rigid-motion modes.

= Torque and axial force of a twisted incompressible cylinder
{parent=Finite elasticity}

For axial stretch $\lambda$ and twist per reference length $\alpha$, the deformation in local cylindrical orthonormal bases has
$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{-1/2}&0&0\\0&\lambda^{-1/2}&\alpha R\lambda^{-1/2}\\0&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <principal stretches> follow from the eigenvalues of $F^TF$. For a cylinder of reference radius $A$, stored energy per reference length is $\mathcal E=2\pi\int_0^A W(F)R\,dR$. Reversible power $M\dot\alpha+N\dot\lambda=\dot{\mathcal E}$ gives torque $M=\mathcal E_{,\alpha}$ and axial force $N=\mathcal E_{,\lambda}$. The relevant derivative holds the reference twist fixed, not the twist per current length.

= Nominal end traction of a twisted neo-Hookean cylinder
{parent=Torque and axial force of a twisted incompressible cylinder}
{title2=$t=(-\mu\alpha x_2,\mu\alpha x_1,\mu\lambda+q/\lambda)$}

For axial stretch $\lambda$ and twist $\alpha$ per reference length, an incompressible <neo-Hookean solid> has current radius $a=R/\sqrt\lambda$. In local cylindrical orthonormal bases the <deformation gradient> is $A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{-1/2}&0&0\\0&\lambda^{-1/2}&\alpha r\\0&0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$. The <Cauchy stress tensor> is $\mu AA^T+qI$. Radial equilibrium gives $q'=\mu\alpha^2r$, and a traction-free curved surface gives $q=-\mu/\lambda+\mu\alpha^2(r^2-a^2)/2$. The nominal end <traction> is the displayed <vector>, measured per reference area. Its moment about the axis is $\int(x_1t_2-x_2t_1)dS_0=\mu\alpha\int r^2dS_0=\mu\pi R^4\alpha/(2\lambda)$. This calculation uses current moment arms with reference-area <tractions>.

= Twist and inflation of an incompressible tube
{parent=Finite elasticity}
{title2=$r^2=\rho^2+c,\quad\theta=\Theta+\alpha Z,\quad z=Z$}

In cylindrical orthonormal bases the <deformation gradient> is $F=\begin{pmatrix}\rho/r&0&0\\0&r/\rho&\alpha r\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and has unit <determinant>. One <principal stretch> is $\rho/r$; the squares of the other two are the roots of $z^2-[(r/\rho)^2+\alpha^2r^2+1]z+(r/\rho)^2=0$. The reference-volume energy per height is $E=2\pi\int\rho W\,d\rho$. <Virtual work> gives end <torque> $M=\partial_\alpha E$ and inner pressure $p=(2\pi a)^{-1}\partial_aE$ at fixed reference radii and height.

= Neo-Hookean tube pressure under twist and inflation
{parent=Twist and inflation of an incompressible tube}
{title2=$p=\mu\log\frac{b_0a}{a_0b}+\frac{\mu}2\left(\frac{b_0^2}{b^2}-\frac{a_0^2}{a^2}\right)+\frac{\mu\alpha^2}2(b_0^2-a_0^2)$}

Here $b^2=b_0^2+a^2-a_0^2$. Substitute the <neo-Hookean solid> energy into the <twist and inflation of an incompressible tube> work identity. Differentiating with respect to $a$ gives the integrand $\mu\rho[\rho^{-2}-\rho^2/(\rho^2+c)^2+\alpha^2]$, with $c=a^2-a_0^2$. Integrating between the two reference radii proves the displayed pressure when the outer surface is traction free.

= Inextensible fibre constraint
{parent=Finite elasticity}
{title2=$|Fm_0|=1$}

A unit reference fibre direction $m_0$ retains its length under this constraint on the <deformation gradient>. Its reaction contributes an undetermined tension along the current fibre direction, independently of the pressure reaction enforcing <incompressibility>.

= Two-family inextensible-fibre pure stretch
{parent=Inextensible fibre constraint}
{title2=$\alpha^2=\frac{\lambda\pm\sqrt{\lambda^2-\sin^22\theta}}{2\cos^2\theta}$}

For two distinct reference fibre directions $l_\pm=(\cos\theta,\pm\sin\theta,0)$, with $0<\theta<\pi/2$, consider the volume-preserving deformation with <right stretch tensor> $U=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda^{-1/2}\alpha,\lambda^{-1/2}\alpha^{-1},\lambda)$, with positive $\lambda,\alpha$. The <inextensible fibre constraint> gives $\alpha^2\cos^2\theta+\alpha^{-2}\sin^2\theta=\lambda$. Setting $b=\alpha^2$ gives a quadratic with the displayed roots. There are two distinct positive branches for $\lambda>\sin2\theta$. The smallest possible axial stretch is $\lambda=\sin2\theta$, at which $\alpha^2=\tan\theta$ and $(Ul_+)\cdot(Ul_-)=0$. Since both image fibres have unit length, their mutual area cannot exceed one; their area is $\sin2\theta/\lambda$, which gives the same lower bound. Coincident or antiparallel fibres at the angle endpoints do not give the two-family orthogonality conclusion.

= Incompressible inextensible-fibre plane strain
{parent=Inextensible fibre constraint}
{title2=$F=R(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$}

Let $e=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ be the current fibre direction and $n=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$. <Inextensibility> fixes the first column of the <deformation gradient> to $e$, and <incompressibility> fixes the second to $\gamma e+n$. Commuting mixed derivatives gives $\gamma_{,1}=\theta_{,1}$ and $\theta_{,2}=\gamma\theta_{,1}$. Thus $\gamma=\theta+f(X_2)$, and curves tangent to $(-\gamma,1)$ map to straight transverse curves with constant $\theta$.

= Triangular equilibrium construction for inextensible-fibre plane strain
{parent=Incompressible inextensible-fibre plane strain}

Write the <Cauchy stress tensor> in the local fibre frame as $\sigma=s\,e\otimes e+h(e\otimes n+n\otimes e)+t\,n\otimes n$. The compatible geometry has $\nabla\theta=\kappa e$, $\nabla\cdot e=0$ and $\nabla\cdot n=-\kappa$. With zero <body force>, <force balance> becomes $e\cdot\nabla s=2\kappa h-n\cdot\nabla h$ and $n\cdot\nabla t-\kappa t=-e\cdot\nabla h-\kappa s$. For specified <shear stress> $h$, the first equation determines $s$ along fibres, then the second determines $t$ along transverse curves. Pressure and fibre tension supply these two normal constraint reactions. This is a local stress construction; boundary tractions must be chosen consistently with its integration data.

= Hyperelastic material
{parent=Finite elasticity}
{wiki}

= Green-elastic material
{c}
{synonym}

A hyperelastic material derives its elastic <stress> from a <strain energy density>. In reference-volume units its <first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor> is the derivative of that density with respect to the <deformation gradient>.

= Compatibility condition for pure nonlinear transverse waves
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$\partial_qP_{11}(1,q,0)=0$}

For a plane material-coordinate <elastic wave> with $u=(0,w(X_1,t),0)$, set $q=w_{,X_1}$. The longitudinal equation is $0=\partial_{X_1}P_{11}(1,q,0)$. Arbitrary nonuniform pure transverse profiles thus require the displayed constitutive compatibility, which does not follow from <isotropy> alone. If it holds and $\tau'(q)>0$, the shear equation closes as $\rho_0w_{tt}=\partial_{X_1}\tau(q)$ with speed $c(q)^2=\tau'(q)/\rho_0$.

= Compatible nonlinear shear energy
{parent=Compatibility condition for pure nonlinear transverse waves}
{title2=$\tau(q)=\mu q+hq^3$}

An explicit objective <isotropic> example is $\mathcal W=\mu(S-1)/2+h(S-1)^2/4+(\lambda+\mu)(J-1)^2/2$, with $J=\det A$, $S=\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf T}A)-2J$, and $\lambda,\mu>0$, $h\ge0$. On $A=[(a,q,0)^{\mathsf T},e_2,e_3]$, $S-1=(a-1)^2+q^2$ and $J=a$, so $P_{11}=0$ at $a=1$ while the shear <nominal stress> is the displayed cubic. The natural-state quadratic energy has <Lamé parameters> $\lambda,\mu$, and the shear speed is $\sqrt{(\mu+3hq^2)/\rho_0}$.

= Saint Venant–Kirchhoff hyperelastic energy
{c}
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$\mathcal W=\lambda(\operatorname{tr}E)^2/2+\mu\operatorname{tr}(E^2)$}

This objective <isotropic> constitutive model uses the <Green-Lagrange strain tensor> $E=(A^{\mathsf T}A-I)/2$. It reproduces <linear elasticity> at small strain. Under simple shear $A=[(1,q,0)^{\mathsf T},e_2,e_3]$, its longitudinal <nominal stress> is $(\lambda+2\mu)q^2/2$. Nonuniform finite shear therefore generates longitudinal acceleration, even though the corresponding infinitesimal transverse mode is uncoupled.

= Lamé parameters from hyperelastic energy Hessian
{c}
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$\lambda=\rho_0W_{A_{11}A_{22}}(I),\quad\mu=\rho_0W_{A_{12}A_{12}}(I)$}

At a stress-free natural reference state, an objective <isotropic> <hyperelastic material> with energy $W$ per unit mass has $\rho_0[W(I+H)-W(I)]=\lambda(\operatorname{tr}e)^2/2+\mu e:e+O(|H|^3)$, where $e=\operatorname{sym}H$. Twice differentiating gives the displayed <Lamé parameters> and the usual <elastic stiffness tensor>. A prestressed reference requires initial-stress corrections.

= Inflation pressure of a thin incompressible elastic balloon
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$p=\frac{h_0}{a_0\lambda^2}\frac d{d\lambda}W(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda^{-2})$}

At leading thin-shell order, a spherical balloon with reference radius $a_0$, thickness $h_0$ and current radius $a=\lambda a_0$ has reference <elastic energy> $4\pi a_0^2h_0W(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda^{-2})$. Equating its differential to pressure work $p\,4\pi a^2da$ gives the displayed pressure difference. Its thickness becomes $h_0\lambda^{-2}$ by <incompressibility>. The same result follows from the spherical membrane <force> balance $p=2h\sigma_t/a$, with current tangential <Cauchy stress tensor> component $\sigma_t=\lambda N$ and the <equibiaxial nominal tension of an incompressible sheet>. Thin-shell validity requires $h/a\ll1$.

= Incremental elastic moduli
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$c_{\alpha i\beta j}=W_{,A_{i\alpha}A_{j\beta}}$}

= Incremental elastic modulus
{synonym}

For unconstrained <hyperelastic material>, the <Hessian> of the <strain energy density> gives $\delta N_{\alpha i}=c_{\alpha i\beta j}\delta A_{j\beta}$. Commuting the second derivatives gives major symmetry $c_{\alpha i\beta j}=c_{\beta j\alpha i}$. These material-coordinate moduli linearize about a finite base deformation; they need not have the minor symmetries of a small-strain <elastic stiffness tensor>. Material constraints require their additional reaction increments and admissibility equations.

= Elastic normal-mode energy identity
{parent=Incremental elastic moduli}
{title2=$\omega^2\int\rho_0|u|^2=\int\overline{u_{i,\alpha}}c_{\alpha i\beta j}u_{j,\beta}$}

For a static base state with zero incremental <body force>, homogeneous incremental <tractions> or homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> eliminate the boundary term in <integration by parts>. The displayed <Rayleigh quotient> then gives real squared frequencies because of major symmetry. Positive moduli give strictly positive squared frequencies for modes with nonzero displacement gradient. Pure <dead loading> leaves constant translations at zero; fixed displacement conditions remove those modes.

= Strong ellipticity in elasticity
{parent=Incremental elastic moduli}
{title2=$c_{\alpha i\beta j}m_i\nu_\alpha m_j\nu_\beta>0$}

Strong ellipticity requires the displayed inequality for every nonzero displacement vector $m$ and material direction $\nu$. Equivalently, every <acoustic tensor> is <positive-definite>. It is weaker than positivity of the moduli on all matrices. A <quadratic minor null Lagrangian> vanishes on rank-one matrices, so adding such a term does not change the acoustic tensor or this condition.

= Acoustic tensor
{parent=Incremental elastic moduli}
{title2=$Q_{ij}(\nu)=c_{\alpha i\beta j}\nu_\alpha\nu_\beta$}

= Elastic acoustic tensor
{synonym}

The acoustic tensor determines the <plane waves> of a locally uniform incremental elastic medium. For a unit material direction $\nu$, its <eigenvalues> are $\rho_0c^2$ and its <eigenvectors> are displacement polarizations. Major symmetry of the <incremental elastic moduli> makes $Q$ a real <symmetric matrix>. Positivity on every <rank-one matrix> $m\otimes\nu$ makes all its wave speeds real and nonzero.

= Strong ellipticity
{parent=Acoustic tensor}

The elastic stiffness satisfies $c_{ijkl}a_ip_ja_kp_l>0$ for every nonzero <polarization> $a$ and propagation <vector> $p$. It makes the <elastic acoustic tensor> positive definite, ensuring positive squared wave <speeds>. Positive <strain> <energy> is a sufficient stronger condition in ordinary <linear elasticity>.

= Constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$N_{\alpha i}=W_{,A_{i\alpha}}+qF_{,A_{i\alpha}}$}

For a regular material constraint $F(A)=0$, admissible rates of the <deformation gradient> lie in the kernel of $F_{,A}$. The difference between the <nominal stress> and the derivative of the <strain energy density> annihilates that tangent space by <virtual work>, so it is a <Lagrange multiplier> times $F_{,A}$. For <incompressibility>, the derivative of the <determinant> is its <cofactor matrix>, and the material-first reaction is $qA^{-1}$ when $\det A=1$.

= Equibiaxial nominal tension of an incompressible sheet
{parent=Constraint reaction in hyperelastic stress}
{title2=$N=\tfrac12\frac d{d\lambda}W(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda^{-2})$}

A thin isotropic <hyperelastic material> subject to <incompressibility>, with equal in-plane <principal stretches> $\lambda$ has transverse stretch $\lambda^{-2}$. With unloaded transverse faces, $N_{33}=W_3+q\lambda^2=0$. Hence $N_{11}=N_{22}=W_1-\lambda^{-3}W_3$, using isotropy to set $W_1=W_2$. The derivative along the constrained path is $W_1+W_2-2\lambda^{-3}W_3$, proving the displayed formula. The two equal nominal tensions do work $2N\,d\lambda$ per reference volume, exactly the change in <strain energy density>.

= Mooney-Rivlin solid
{c}
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{wiki=Mooney–Rivlin_solid}

An incompressible <Mooney-Rivlin solid> has <strain energy density> $W=C_1(I_1-3)+C_2(I_2-3)$, with <right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor> $C=F^TF$, $I_1=\operatorname{tr}C$ and $I_2=\operatorname{tr}C^{-1}$ when $\det C=1$. It extends the <neo-Hookean solid> by a second invariant. A convention $W=\mu_1(I_1-3)/2-\mu_2(I_2-3)/2$ means $C_1=\mu_1/2$, $C_2=-\mu_2/2$; the minus sign must be retained when comparing material constants.

= Neo-Hookean solid
{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{wiki}

For an incompressible neo-Hookean solid, a convenient <strain energy density> is $W=\mu(\operatorname{tr}C-3)/2$, where $C$ is the <right Cauchy-Green deformation tensor>. An additive constant in $W$ does not affect the <stress>. The <shear modulus> $\mu$ fixes the small-strain stiffness.

= Homogeneous tensile bifurcation of a neo-Hookean cube
{parent=Neo-Hookean solid}

For the volume-preserving family $F=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1/2},\lambda^{-1/2})$, equal dead nominal tension $T$ gives potential
$$
\Pi(\lambda)=\frac\mu2(\lambda^2+2\lambda^{-1}-3)-T(\lambda+2\lambda^{-1/2}-3).
$$
Its derivative factors as $\Pi'=(1-\lambda^{-3/2})[\mu(\lambda+\lambda^{-1/2})-T]$. The nontrivial branch has a fold at $\lambda=2^{-2/3}$, $T/\mu=3\,2^{-2/3}$, and meets the trivial branch at $T/\mu=2$. Stability in this one-parameter family is determined by $\Pi''$, not by branch count alone. At $T/\mu=2$ there is only one nontrivial positive solution, $\lambda=(3-\sqrt5)/2$; the other branch value is $\lambda=1$.

{parent=Hyperelastic material}
{title2=$W$}

= Nonnegative isotropic elastic strain energy
{parent=Strain energy density}
{title2=$\mu\ge0,\quad3\lambda+2\mu\ge0$}

In three-dimensional isotropic <linear elasticity>, decompose the symmetric strain as $e=pI+d$ with $p=\operatorname{tr}(e)/3$ and $\operatorname{tr}d=0$. The stored energy is $E=\mu\sum_{ij}d_{ij}^2+\tfrac32(3\lambda+2\mu)p^2$. Its nonnegativity for every strain is equivalent to the displayed inequalities: the traceless and purely volumetric test strains prove necessity, and the sum of squares proves sufficiency.

= Material isotropy
{parent=Finite elasticity}

Material isotropy makes the elastic response independent of a rotation of reference material directions: $\sigma(FR)=\sigma(F)$ for proper rotations $R$. Combined with <material frame indifference>, it yields <coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress>.

= Coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress
{parent=Material isotropy}
{title2=$\sigma B=B\sigma,\quad B=FF^T$}

The <polar decomposition in continuum mechanics> and <material isotropy> remove the reference rotation from an elastic <Cauchy stress tensor>. Half-turns around principal stretch axes leave the diagonal <left stretch tensor> fixed, so <material frame indifference> forces the stress's off-diagonal entries to vanish in those axes. Rotations within repeated-stretch eigenspaces also force isotropic stress there. Consequently stress and the <left Cauchy-Green deformation tensor> are <coaxial symmetric tensors>.

= Equivariance of isotropic principal stresses
{parent=Coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress}
{title2=$\sigma_i(\lambda_{\pi(1)},\lambda_{\pi(2)},\lambda_{\pi(3)})=\sigma_{\pi(i)}(\lambda)$}

For an objective <material isotropy> law, permutation of the <principal stretches> permutes the labelled <principal stresses>. This follows by conjugating the <left stretch tensor> with signed permutation <rotation matrices>. Each principal stress is symmetric in the other two stretches, but need not be symmetric in all three. The law $\sigma=V^2$ provides the example $\sigma_i=\lambda_i^2$. In an invariant representation $\sigma=\beta_0I+\beta_1V+\beta_2V^2$, the scalar coefficients are symmetric functions of the unordered stretches.

= Universal simple-shear normal-stress identity
{parent=Coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress}
{title2=$\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22}=\gamma\sigma_{12}$}

For <simple shear>, $B=\begin{pmatrix}1+\gamma^2&\gamma\\\gamma&1\end{pmatrix}$. The off-diagonal entry of $\sigma B-B\sigma$ is $\gamma(\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22})-\gamma^2\sigma_{12}$. <Coaxiality of isotropic elastic stress> therefore proves the identity for $\gamma\ne0$. At $\gamma=0$, <material isotropy> makes the stress isotropic, proving the same identity.

= Elastic filament
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

An elastic filament is a slender elastic body modeled by a centerline and bending rigidities. Its <elastic energy> penalizes curvature. In a small-deflection representation $(x,y(x),z(x))$, the bending energy is quadratic in $y^{\prime\prime}$ and $z^{\prime\prime}$. Compression can cause <Euler buckling of an elastic filament>.

= Filament torsional stiffness
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$C=G_sJ_p$}

For an isotropic elastic rod with shear modulus $G_s$, polar second moment of area $J_p$, and twist rate $\Omega$, the torque is $C\Omega$ and the energy per length is $C\Omega^2/2$. For a circular section of radius $r$, $J_p=\pi r^4/2$. This material twist stiffness is distinct from the energy of bending filament centerlines into a helix.

= Intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$\kappa_0(s)$}

= Intrinsic filament curvature
{synonym}

The intrinsic <signed curvature> is the curvature of a stress-free filament, measured along material <arc length>. An <elastic filament> with actual curvature $\kappa$ stores bending energy $\frac B2\int(\kappa-\kappa_0)^2ds$, with <filament bending modulus> $B$. In a planar small-slope <Monge representation>, $\kappa\simeq\zeta''$ and the leading energy is quadratic in $\zeta''-\kappa_0$.

= Free-end curvature boundary layer
{parent=Intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament}
{title2=$\ell=\sqrt{B/F}$}

Moment-free ends require $\zeta''=\kappa_0$ there, so a highly stretched finite filament can retain curvature in boundary layers of width $\ell=\sqrt{B/F}$. For constant intrinsic curvature $\kappa$, zero transverse end forces give slope $\zeta'(x)=\kappa\ell\sinh[(x-L/2)/\ell]/\cosh[L/(2\ell)]$. Direct integration yields
$$
\mathcal L-L=\frac{\kappa^2\ell^3}2\left[\tanh a-a\operatorname{sech}^2a\right],\qquad a=L/(2\ell).
$$
Its large-force deficit is $\kappa^2\ell^3/2$, of order $F^{-3/2}$, even though the smooth bulk curvature derivative vanishes. This shows why endpoint assumptions matter in <force-extension of an intrinsically curved filament>.

= Force-extension of an intrinsically curved filament
{parent=Intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament}
{title2=$\widehat\zeta(q)=-B\widehat\kappa_0(q)/(F+Bq^2)$}

For a planar <elastic filament> under positive constant extension force $F$, the quadratic force-controlled energy is
$$
\mathcal H=\frac12\int\left[B(\zeta''-\kappa_0)^2+F(\zeta')^2\right]dx.
$$
The <higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation> is $B\zeta''''-F\zeta''=B\kappa_0''$. On a periodic interval or in a bulk <Fourier transform> treatment, every nonzero mode obeys the displayed transfer relation. The length deficit is $\frac12\int(\zeta')^2dx$, so its ensemble value is determined by the <power spectrum> of the <quenched intrinsic curvature>. Boundary conditions must be specified for a finite filament.

= Smooth-curvature high-force extension law
{parent=Force-extension of an intrinsically curved filament}
{title2=$\langle\mathcal L-L\rangle/L\sim B^2\langle(\kappa_0')^2\rangle/(2F^2)$}

For a stationary <quenched intrinsic curvature> with <power spectrum> $S_\kappa(q)$ and finite second spectral moment, the bulk deficit satisfies
$$
\frac{\langle\mathcal L-L\rangle}{L}=\frac{B^2}2\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{q^2S_\kappa(q)}{(F+Bq^2)^2}
\sim\frac{B^2}{2F^2}\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}q^2S_\kappa(q).
$$
The moment is $\langle(\kappa_0')^2\rangle$ for mean-square differentiable curvature. Infinite-moment disorder and finite endpoint boundary layers can change the exponent, so this is a smooth bulk law rather than a universal law for all random polymers.

= Quenched intrinsic curvature
{parent=Intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament}

Quenched <intrinsic curvature of an elastic filament> is fixed during a mechanical experiment, although different filaments can have different random intrinsic shapes. Ensemble averaging is then over these shapes after mechanical minimization. It differs from averaging over time-dependent thermal bending fluctuations in a <worm-like chain>.

= Spatially varying tension in filament bending
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$K_\sigma=A\partial_x^4-\partial_x(\sigma\partial_x)$}

Adding $\frac12\int\sigma(x)(h')^2dx$ to the quadratic bending energy of an <elastic filament> contributes $-(\sigma h')'$ to its <variational derivative>. The endpoint term is $Ah''\eta'+(\sigma h'-Ah''')\eta$. If the real <filament tension> vanishes at both ends, the usual <self-adjoint endpoint conditions for filament bending> remain valid. A complete <eigenfunction expansion> diagonalizes the energy even when the <eigenfunctions> have no explicit formula. Thermal <equipartition theorem> arguments require positive eigenvalues after fixing any <kernel>; strong compression can violate this requirement.

= Thermal covariance of an elastic filament
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$C(x,y)=k_BT K^{-1}(x,y)$}

For a positive quadratic bending operator $K$ with a real <orthonormal basis> of <eigenfunctions>, the <equipartition theorem> gives $C(x,y)=k_BT\sum_nW_n(x)W_n(y)/\mu_n$, where $KW_n=\mu_nW_n$. This is the inverse-operator <Green function> multiplied by <Boltzmann constant> and <temperature>. Unconstrained <zero-energy filament modes> prevent a normalizable <canonical ensemble>, and negative modes signal an unstable quadratic model.

For both ends clamped and $K=A\partial_x^4$, the diagonal is $C(x,x)=k_BT x^3(L-x)^3/(3AL^3)$. This differs from a clamped-free tip <variance>. The center <variance> is $k_BT L^3/(192A)$.

= Rigid-motion-projected thermal covariance of a free filament
{parent=Thermal covariance of an elastic filament}
{title2=$C(x,y)=\frac{k_BT}{A}\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{W_n(x)W_n(y)}{k_n^4}$}

Free bending has two <zero-energy filament modes>, so it does not define an unrestricted normalizable thermal height distribution. Fix the mean displacement and linear tilt, or project out that affine kernel. On the positive complement, the <equipartition theorem> gives independent Gaussian mode <variances> $k_BT/(Ak_n^4)$ for normalized modes. The resulting <covariance> is the bending operator pseudoinverse multiplied by $k_BT$. It depends on how the rigid motion is fixed.

= Curvature-noise representation of a free filament
{parent=Rigid-motion-projected thermal covariance of a free filament}
{title2=$\langle\xi(s)\xi(t)\rangle=(k_BT/A)\delta(s-t)$}

The quadratic bending energy makes curvature a <white noise> field in the generalized thermal description. Integrate it twice, then subtract the affine least-squares projection to fix translation and tilt. With $u=x/L$ and $t=s/L$, the dimensionless kernel is $(u-t)_+-(1-t)^2/2-(1-3t^2+2t^3)(u-1/2)$. Its second spatial derivative is the source delta and its mean and first moment are zero. Its squared integral yields the <free-filament variance with fixed translation and tilt>.

= Free-filament variance with fixed translation and tilt
{parent=Rigid-motion-projected thermal covariance of a free filament}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}h(x)=\frac{k_BTL^3}{A}P(x/L)$}

For the frame fixed by zero mean height and zero first height moment, the <variance> profile is $P(u)=1/105-11u/105+13u^2/35-u^3/3-u^4/3+3u^5/5-u^6/5$. It obeys $P(1-u)=P(u)$, $P(0)=1/105$ and $P(1/2)=1/320$. Integrating a <white noise> curvature field twice, subtracting its affine projection and integrating the squared kernel proves the profile. These free-end conditions must not be confused with physically clamping the ends.

= Zero-energy filament mode
{parent=Thermal covariance of an elastic filament}
{title2=$K\psi=0$}

A zero-energy mode lies in the <kernel> of the quadratic <elastic filament> operator. For pure bending, zero curvature makes the displacement affine. Free-free endpoints therefore leave translation and tilt modes, while slope-constrained, force-free endpoints leave only translation. Both clamped-clamped and hinged-hinged endpoints remove these modes. An unrestricted modal amplitude has constant <Boltzmann distribution> weight, so no normalizable <canonical ensemble> or finite displacement <variance> exists. Fixing the rigid degrees of freedom allows the <equipartition theorem> on the positive complement.

= Clamped--clamped bending mode
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$\cos\beta\cosh\beta=1$}

A <normal mode> of a uniformly bending <elastic filament> fixed in position and slope at both ends satisfies $W''''=k^4W$. With $\beta=kL>0$, the allowed <wavenumbers> solve $\cos\beta\cosh\beta=1$. The first root is $4.730040745$ and $\beta_n=(n+\tfrac12)\pi+O(e^{-(n+1/2)\pi})$ for $n\geq1$. The apparent root at zero is not an <eigenfunction>: the zero-eigenvalue cubic polynomial satisfying all four <clamped boundary conditions> is identically zero. This spectrum differs from that of a filament clamped only at one end.

= Self-adjoint endpoint conditions for filament bending
{parent=Elastic filament}

For the <elastic filament> operator $K=A\partial_x^4$, two <integration by parts> operations give boundary form $A[\overline u v'''-\overline{u'}v''+\overline{u''}v'-\overline{u'''}v]$. Four elementary homogeneous choices at each endpoint are $h=h'=0$ (clamped), $h=h''=0$ (hinged), $h''=h'''=0$ (free), and $h'=h'''=0$ (slope-constrained and transverse-force-free). Each defines a <self-adjoint operator> on a finite interval when imposed at both ends. More general real <Robin boundary conditions> also give <self-adjointness>; the four choices are not an exhaustive classification of all boundary subspaces.

= Free-end bending boundary conditions
{parent=Self-adjoint endpoint conditions for filament bending}
{title2=$h^{(2)}=h^{(3)}=0$}

Variation of the small-slope bending energy of an <elastic filament> gives endpoint work $A[h^{(2)}\delta h^{(1)}-h^{(3)}\delta h]$. Arbitrary endpoint displacement and slope variations force the bending moment and transverse force to vanish. These are $h^{(2)}=h^{(3)}=0$. They define a <self-adjoint> nonnegative fourth-order bending operator on a finite interval; its kernel contains rigid translation and tilt.

= Free-free bending spectrum
{parent=Free-end bending boundary conditions}
{title2=$\cos q_n\cosh q_n=1,\quad k_n=q_n/L$}

Nonzero bending modes have the <sine>, <cosine>, <hyperbolic sine> and <hyperbolic cosine> form. Free conditions at the first end identify the <cosine> and <hyperbolic cosine> coefficients, and likewise the <sine> coefficients. The remaining boundary determinant is $2(1-\cos q\cosh q)$. Its first positive roots are about $4.73004,7.85320,10.99561$. The complete basis additionally includes two <zero-energy filament modes>, constant translation and affine tilt; these are absent from the finite-coefficient formula at $k=0$.

= Stable characteristic equation for free-free bending modes
{parent=Free-free bending spectrum}
{title2=$\cos q=\operatorname{sech}q$}

Writing the characteristic equation with reciprocal <hyperbolic cosine> avoids exponentially large products. For the positive roots indexed from one, $q_n=(n+1/2)\pi+2(-1)^{n+1}e^{-(n+1/2)\pi}+O(e^{-2(n+1/2)\pi})$. The apparent root at zero represents the separate affine kernel, not another positive bending mode. Evaluating hyperbolic pieces of high modes with decaying exponential forms also avoids cancellation.

= Tip-force compliance of a cantilever
{parent=Elastic filament}
{title2=$h(L)/f=L^3/(3A)$}

For a clamped <elastic filament> of <filament bending modulus> $A$, a transverse tip force $f$ gives $h(x)=fx^2(3L-x)/(6A)$ in the small-slope model. Thus $h(L)/f=L^3/(3A)$. This differs from the effective tip stiffness under a distributed load. The zero-load Gaussian response reproduces the <thermal bending fluctuations of a clamped filament>, $\langle h(L)^2\rangle=k_BTL^3/(3A)$ for one transverse coordinate.

= Minimum potential energy principle in elasticity
{parent=Elastic energy}

Let a convex <strain energy density> have derivative equal to the actual <stress tensor>. Under mixed displacement and <traction> data, any equilibrated <stress> $\sigma_0$ with the prescribed <body force> and boundary <tractions> represents the external work. For any compatible trial <strain> $\varepsilon'$, the convex supporting-plane inequality gives $W(\varepsilon')-W(\varepsilon)\ge\sigma:(\varepsilon'-\varepsilon)$. The integral of $(\sigma-\sigma_0):(\varepsilon'-\varepsilon)$ vanishes by <integration by parts>. Thus the actual <strain> minimizes $\int(W-\sigma_0:\varepsilon)$.

Linear elasticity approximates strain and stress to first order in a small displacement field.

The <elasticity tensor> relates <stress> to infinitesimal <strain> by $\sigma_{ij}=C_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl}$. In conservative <linear> elasticity, the minor symmetries reflect symmetric <stress> and <strain>, while the major symmetry $C_{ijkl}=C_{klij}$ expresses the existence of an elastic energy <density>.

= Uniaxial strain modulus

The <uniaxial strain modulus> relates longitudinal <normal stress> to longitudinal <strain> when both transverse normal strains vanish. For isotropic <linear elasticity>, solving the transverse compliance equations gives the transverse stresses $\nu\tau_1/(1-\nu)$ and hence the displayed modulus in terms of <Young's modulus> and <Poisson's ratio>. = Area expansion under uniaxial compression

For a thin sheet of initial face <area> $A$, compressed normally by <force> $F$ between frictionless plates, each lateral <strain> is $\nu F/(EA)$. To first order the <area> of either broad face increases by $2\nu F/E$; total two-face <area> changes twice as much.

= Radial equilibrium of a thick elastic cylinder
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\tau_\phi=(r\tau_r)',\quad r^2R''+rR'-R=0$}

An axisymmetric small radial displacement $R(r)$ gives radial <strain> $R'$ and hoop <strain> $R/r$. Radial force balance on an annular wedge gives $\tau_r'+(\tau_r-\tau_\phi)/r=0$. Under <plane stress>, isotropic <Hooke's law> gives $\tau_r=E(R'+\nu R/r)/(1-\nu^2)$ and $\tau_\phi=E(R/r+\nu R')/(1-\nu^2)$. Substitution into equilibrium gives the displayed displacement equation. Its power-law exponents are $+1$ and $-1$, so $R=c_1r+c_2/r$. The hoop-stress solution is not based on treating a thick wall as a membrane.

{c}
{parent=Radial equilibrium of a thick elastic cylinder}
{title2=$\tau_r=P-Q/r^2,\quad\tau_\phi=P+Q/r^2$}

For inner and outer radii $a,b$, with internal and external <pressures> $p_1,p_0$, the boundary tractions are $\tau_r(a)=-p_1$ and $\tau_r(b)=-p_0$. The <radial equilibrium of a thick elastic cylinder> gives $P=(p_1a^2-p_0b^2)/(b^2-a^2)$ and $Q=(p_1-p_0)a^2b^2/(b^2-a^2)$. The radial compressive pressure is $p(r)=-P+Q/r^2$. Since hoop stress is monotone in $r$ when $Q\neq0$, its maximum absolute value is the larger of $|P+Q/a^2|$ and $|P+Q/b^2|$. With negligible external pressure, its maximum is the tensile inner-wall value $p_1(a^2+b^2)/(b^2-a^2)$. The stress distribution itself is independent of elastic moduli in this linear homogeneous axisymmetric model; its associated displacement depends on them.

= Plane stress
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\tau_{zz}=\tau_{xz}=\tau_{yz}=0$}

A <stress tensor> state with all traction components involving one normal direction zero. In an isotropic solid, its two remaining normal stresses obey $\tau_x=E(e_x+\nu e_y)/(1-\nu^2)$ and $\tau_y=E(e_y+\nu e_x)/(1-\nu^2)$. The out-of-plane <strain> need not vanish: $e_z=-\nu(\tau_x+\tau_y)/E$. Constraining the out-of-plane deformation instead would be a different boundary condition.

= Isotropic elastic modulus relation
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$E=3B(1-2\nu)=2G_s(1+\nu)$}

Under uniform compressive <pressure> $p$, isotropic <Hooke's law> gives each normal <strain> as $-p(1-2\nu)/E$. The volume strain is three times this, so the <bulk modulus> is $B=E/[3(1-2\nu)]$, where $E$ is <Young's modulus> and $\nu$ is <Poisson's ratio>. Positivity of $E$ and finite $B$ gives $\nu<1/2$. Positive <shear modulus> $G_s=E/[2(1+\nu)]$ adds the lower bound $\nu>-1$. Negative <Poisson's ratio> is allowed; the bulk relation alone does not supply the lower bound.

= Proportional limit
{parent=Linear elasticity}

The <proportional limit> bounds the loading interval over which <stress> is proportional to <strain>. It does not by itself establish the last load that permits complete recovery of <elastic deformation>.

= Mean stress and strain boundary identities
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$V\overline\sigma_{ij}=\int_Sx_it_j\,dS+\int_V\rho x_i(b_j-a_j)\,dV$}

Apply the <divergence theorem> to $\partial_k(x_i\sigma_{jk})$ and use momentum balance $\sigma_{jk,k}=\rho(a_j-b_j)$ and stress symmetry to obtain the displayed mean <stress> identity. Applying the same theorem to the <infinitesimal strain tensor> gives $V\overline e_{ij}=\frac12\int_S(u_in_j+u_jn_i)\,dS$. The acceleration term cannot be dropped during a transient.

= Equilibrium contraction of a self-gravitating elastic sphere
{parent=Mean stress and strain boundary identities}
{title2=$\delta a=\rho ga^2/(15\kappa)$}

For a uniform traction-free sphere, first-order self-gravity is $b=-gx/a$. Static averaging gives $\overline\sigma=-\rho gaI/5$. Homogeneous <linear elasticity> then gives $\overline e=-\rho gaI/(15\kappa)$. The boundary strain identity identifies $\overline e=u(a)I/a$, proving the displayed positive radius decrease. The local strain is not uniform, and the cancellation of the <shear modulus> concerns only the mean contraction.

= Elastic strain Green operator
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\varepsilon=E-\Gamma\tau$}

For a homogeneous comparison <elastic stiffness tensor> $C^0$, the <elastic strain Green operator> maps an <elastic polarization> to minus the induced <infinitesimal strain tensor>. In a bounded domain the induced <displacement field> has zero boundary data; in an infinite medium it is the decaying perturbation. The defining <static elastic equilibrium> equation is $\nabla\cdot(C^0\operatorname{sym}\nabla u+\tau)=0$. The bounded-domain operator is self-adjoint, annihilates constant <elastic polarizations>, has zero mean output, and satisfies $\Gamma C^0\Gamma=\Gamma$.

= Isotropic elastic strain Green kernel
{parent=Elastic strain Green operator}
{title2=$\Gamma_{ijkk}(x)=-(4\pi L)^{-1}\partial_i\partial_j|x|^{-1},\quad L=\kappa+4\mu/3$}

For a three-dimensional isotropic comparison material, the contraction of its <elastic strain Green operator> is the displayed <distributional derivative>. Its double trace is $\Gamma_{iikk}=\delta/L$, because $\Delta|x|^{-1}=-4\pi\delta$. Away from zero the contraction is $(\delta_{ij}|x|^2-3x_ix_j)/(4\pi L|x|^5)$; the full <distribution> also includes $\delta_{ij}\delta/(3L)$. This contact term must not be discarded when computing hydrostatic <strain>.

= Constant-shear bulk-modulus inclusion
{parent=Isotropic elastic strain Green kernel}
{title2=$\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon=\frac{3\kappa^0+4\mu}{3\kappa(x)+4\mu}\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon^0$}

When only the <bulk modulus> differs from an isotropic matrix, the <elastic polarization> is spherical: $\tau=(\kappa-\kappa^0)\operatorname{tr}(\varepsilon)I$. The double trace of the <isotropic elastic strain Green kernel> makes the trace equation local, giving the displayed formula. Individual <strain> components still depend on a nonlocal potential of the spherical polarization, so the complete <infinitesimal strain tensor> is not generally a local multiple of its reference value.

= Directional elastic strain Green operator
{parent=Elastic strain Green operator}
{title2=$\widetilde\Gamma_C(n)=B_n(B_n^TCB_n)^{-1}B_n^T$}

Let $B_na=\operatorname{sym}(a\otimes n)$; its adjoint on symmetric <stress tensors> is $B_n^T\tau=\tau n$. Then $B_n^TCB_n$ is the <acoustic tensor>. The <directional elastic strain Green operator> satisfies the resolvent identity $\widetilde\Gamma_r(C^r-C^s)\widetilde\Gamma_s=\widetilde\Gamma_s-\widetilde\Gamma_r$, by the elementary inverse identity $K_r^{-1}(K_r-K_s)K_s^{-1}=K_s^{-1}-K_r^{-1}$.

= Elastic polarization
{parent=Elastic strain Green operator}
{title2=$\tau=\sigma-C^0\varepsilon$}

The <elastic polarization> is the excess <stress> relative to a homogeneous comparison <elastic stiffness tensor>. In heterogeneous <linear elasticity>, $\tau=(C-C^0)\varepsilon$. The comparison <static elastic equilibrium> equation gives the integral equation $\varepsilon=\varepsilon^0-\Gamma\tau$, where $\varepsilon^0$ is the reference <strain> under the same external loading.

= Bulk modulus
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\kappa$}

The <bulk modulus> relates hydrostatic compressive pressure to fractional volume compression. In <isotropic> three-dimensional <linear elasticity>, the spherical part of the <stress tensor> is $\kappa\operatorname{tr}(\varepsilon)I$, so uniform dilation has <strain energy density> $\kappa(\operatorname{tr}\varepsilon)^2/2$. Positive <bulk modulus> and positive <shear modulus> make the isotropic <elastic stiffness tensor> positive on nonzero symmetric <strains>.

= Hydrostatic volume strain from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio
{parent=Bulk modulus}
{title2=$\Delta V/V=-3(1-2\nu)p/E$}

For an isotropic linearly elastic solid, equal compressive principal <stresses> $-p$ produce three equal normal <strains> $-(1-2\nu)p/E$. Adding them gives the displayed <volume> <strain>, or <bulk modulus> $K=E/[3(1-2\nu)]$. It is a small-strain relation using the <pressure> change, not an arbitrary absolute <pressure> reference.

= Adiabatic bulk modulus
{parent=Bulk modulus}
{title2=$K_S$}

The <pressure> response to compression at fixed entropy, $K_S=\rho(\partial P/\partial\rho)_S$. Seismic compressions are commonly approximately adiabatic; a background <density> gradient follows the corresponding derivative only when composition and entropy assumptions also apply.

= Antiplane shear
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{wiki}
{title2=$u=(0,0,w(x_1,x_2))$}

Antiplane shear has displacement normal to a plane and independent of that normal coordinate. In an isotropic linear elastic body, $\sigma_{13}=\mu w_{,1}$ and $\sigma_{23}=\mu w_{,2}$, together with their symmetric partners, are the only nonzero <stress tensor> entries. Static balance without <body force> reduces to the <Laplace equation> for $w$.

= Steadily moving antiplane shear
{parent=Antiplane shear}
{title2=$y=\beta x_2,\quad\beta=\sqrt{1-V^2/c^2}$}

For subsonic motion $|V|<c$, an <antiplane shear> <displacement> $w(x_1-Vt,x_2)$ satisfies $\beta^2w_{xx}+w_{x_2x_2}=0$. The displayed rescaling makes it a <harmonic function> of $(x,y)$, so locally $w=\operatorname{Re}H(x+iy)$ for a <holomorphic function> $H$. Set $F=\beta H$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> then give $\mu F'=\beta\sigma_{13}-i\sigma_{23}$. Keeping this potential normalization explicit avoids losing a factor of $\beta$ in the dynamic <stress> field. At sonic or supersonic speeds the elliptic harmonic reduction does not apply.

= Complex stress potential for antiplane shear
{parent=Antiplane shear}
{title2=$\sigma_{23}+i\sigma_{13}=\mu F'(z)$}

A <harmonic conjugate> $\phi$ satisfying $\phi_{,1}=w_{,2}$ and $\phi_{,2}=-w_{,1}$ makes $F=\phi+iw$ a <holomorphic function>. Its derivative packages the two <antiplane shear> stresses into the displayed complex field. On multiply connected domains a global potential additionally requires the appropriate periods to vanish; prescribed displacement jumps can instead be represented by branches of the potential.

= Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack
{c}
{parent=Complex stress potential for antiplane shear}
{title2=$G^++G^-=-2p$}

For a crack on the real axis under applied shear traction $p$, write $G=\mu F'$ for the additional <complex stress potential for antiplane shear> derivative. Reflection of the induced displacement gives $G^- =\overline{G^+}$. Traction-free crack faces therefore require $G^++G^-=-2p$; intact portions have $G^+=G^-$. This is a scalar <Riemann-Hilbert problem>. Endpoint regularity, single-valued displacement and the absence of additional remote loads fix its homogeneous terms.

= Cauchy solution for a steadily moving semi-infinite antiplane crack
{parent=Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack}
{c}
{title2=$F'(z)=-\frac{i}{\pi\mu\sqrt z}\int_{-\infty}^0\frac{\sqrt{-s}\,f(s)}{z-s}\,ds$}

For the moving semi-infinite crack, take the <branch cut> of $\sqrt z$ on the negative real axis and $\sqrt x>0$ ahead of the tip. With <steadily moving antiplane shear>, face <tractions> $\sigma_{23}^{\pm}=-f(x)$ imply $\operatorname{Im}F'^{\pm}=f/\mu$. The antisymmetric <displacement> response gives $F'^-=-\overline{F'^+}$. Hence $G=\sqrt zF'$ has jump $G^+-G^-=-2\sqrt{-x}f(x)/\mu$. The <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem> gives the displayed <Cauchy integral>, assuming convergence and no extra remote homogeneous loading. For $x>0$ it is purely imaginary, so $\sigma_{23}=\int_{-\infty}^0\sqrt{-s}f(s)/(x-s)\,ds/(\pi\sqrt x)$. At fixed co-moving loading this ahead-of-tip <stress> is independent of speed; the <displacement> still has the factor $1/\beta$.

= Homogeneous stress-intensity field of a semi-infinite antiplane crack
{parent=Cauchy solution for a steadily moving semi-infinite antiplane crack}
{title2=$F'_h(z)=iC/\sqrt z$}

For real $C$, this analytic homogeneous field has real boundary values on both faces of the negative-axis crack, so it adds no face shear <traction>. Ahead of the tip it adds <stress> $-\mu C/\sqrt x$. Its <stress> tends to zero at infinity, but its primitive grows like $\sqrt z$, producing unbounded remote <displacement> and infinite total <elastic energy>. Thus merely requiring vanishing far <stress> does not uniquely fix the face-loaded crack solution. Excluding an added remote stress-intensity field, for example through the localized-load bounded-displacement far condition, removes this homogeneous ambiguity. Ordinary inverse-square-root tip <stress> remains allowed and has finite energy in any bounded neighbourhood of the tip.

= Antiplane ligament Cauchy solution
{parent=Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack}
{title2=$G(z)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{a^2-z^2}}\left(\int_a^\infty-\int_{-\infty}^{-a}\right)\frac{\sqrt{t^2-a^2}\,p(t)}{t-z}\,dt$}

For two semi-infinite cracks separated by a ligament, choose $\chi(z)=\sqrt{a^2-z^2}>0$ on the ligament. Its upper crack values are $-i\sqrt{t^2-a^2}$ on the right and $+i\sqrt{t^2-a^2}$ on the left. Multiplication by $\chi$ converts the <Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack> to an additive jump; the <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem> gives the displayed solution, assuming convergence. A homogeneous term $C/\chi$ transfers an extra resultant $\pi C$ through the ligament and has logarithmic displacement at infinity. The usual correction with no additional remote resultant excludes it. The far-field normalization is essential to uniqueness.

= Finite antiplane crack Cauchy solution
{parent=Hilbert problem for an antiplane crack}
{title2=$G(z)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{z^2-a^2}}\int_{-a}^a\frac{\sqrt{a^2-t^2}\,p(t)}{z-t}\,dt$}

Choose $\chi(z)=\sqrt{z^2-a^2}\sim z$ at infinity, cut on the finite crack. Its upper crack value is $i\sqrt{a^2-t^2}$, so $H=\chi G$ has jump $-2i\sqrt{a^2-t^2}p(t)$. The <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem> gives the displayed <Cauchy integral> solution. It decays as $z^{-2}$ for integrable weighted traction. A possible homogeneous term $C/\chi$ would give a logarithmic displacement period; it is removed for a dislocation-free finite crack with no extra remote loading.

= Elastic stiffness tensor
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\sigma_{ij}=c_{ijpq}\partial_pu_q$}

An <elastic stiffness tensor> maps a small <displacement gradient tensor> to the <stress tensor>. Minor symmetries in each index pair ensure symmetric <stress> and dependence only on symmetric <strain>; major symmetry $c_{ijpq}=c_{pqij}$ follows from an <elastic energy> and underlies reciprocity. Stability requires positive <elastic energy> on nonzero strains. In an isotropic material, $c_{ijpq}=\lambda\delta_{ij}\delta_{pq}+\mu(\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}+\delta_{iq}\delta_{jp})$, with <Lamé parameters> $\lambda,\mu$.

= Independent elastic stiffness counts
{parent=Elastic stiffness tensor}
{title2=$21=6\cdot7/2$}

The two minor symmetries of the <elastic stiffness tensor> make it a map between six-dimensional spaces of symmetric <strain> and <stress>. An <elastic energy> gives major symmetry, so the matrix of this map is symmetric and has 21 independent entries. For a symmetry operation acting on strains with parity spaces of dimensions $m$ and $6-m$, invariance eliminates the mixed block and leaves $m(m+1)/2+(6-m)(7-m)/2$ independent coefficients.

= Twofold elastic symmetry
{parent=Independent elastic stiffness counts}
{title2=$N=13$}

A rotation through $\pi$ about the third coordinate axis preserves the <strain> components $e_{11},e_{22},e_{33},e_{12}$ and reverses $e_{13},e_{23}$. A symmetry-invariant <elastic stiffness tensor> cannot couple the two parity spaces. Its independent symmetric blocks therefore contain $4\cdot5/2+2\cdot3/2=13$ coefficients. A twofold axis alone does not impose invariance under every rotation about that axis.

= Bending moment
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$M$}
{wiki}

A bending moment is an internal <torque> transmitted across a cut through a beam or <elastic plate>. For a slender linear elastic beam, $M=EI\kappa$ up to sign convention, where $E$ is <Young's modulus>, $I$ the <second moment of area>, and $\kappa$ the centreline <curvature>. The corresponding bending response of an <elastic plate> is described by its <bending stiffness> and curvature tensor.

= Neutral axis
{parent=Bending moment}
{wiki}

The <neutral axis> is the line in the cross-section of an <elastic beam> where longitudinal bending <strain> vanishes. For a homogeneous beam in pure bending, zero resultant longitudinal <force> gives $\int_Az\,dA=0$, so the <neutral axis> passes through the <centroid>. Fibres on its opposite sides are stretched and compressed. The <second moment of area> about this axis controls the relation between <bending moment> and <curvature>.

= Elastic membrane
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\eta$}

An elastic membrane is a thin deformable sheet whose transverse restoring force is supplied by in-plane tension. For uniform tension $T$ and mass per area $m$, a two-dimensional small-displacement model has $m\eta_{tt}=T\eta_{xx}-[p]$, where the pressure jump is defined from the lower fluid to the upper fluid. Fluid loading modifies its traveling modes through the <acoustic wave on a tensioned massive membrane>.

= Equal-phase two-wave membrane modes
{parent=Elastic membrane}
{title2=$k_y=(2j+1)\pi/a,\quad k_z^2=(\omega/c)^2-k_y^2$}

Two equal <complex amplitudes> with opposite transverse <wavevector> components sum to a <cosine> across a strip with edges $y=\pm a/2$. Identical phases satisfy the edge condition only for the odd transverse indices displayed. Equal magnitudes with opposite phase give the <sine> family; together the families form all Dirichlet strip modes $n\pi/a$, $n\ge1$. The lowest axial <phase velocity> exceeds the unrestricted membrane wave <speed>, while its <group velocity> is smaller; neither should be substituted for the other.

= Evanescent membrane response below cutoff
{parent=Equal-phase two-wave membrane modes}
{title2=$\kappa=\sqrt{(\pi/a)^2-(\omega/c)^2}$}

Below the lowest strip cutoff, the axial <wavevector> is imaginary. A localized harmonic drive produces a displacement decaying as $e^{-\kappa|z-z_0|}$ away from the source in the dominant lowest mode. Cross-sections still oscillate in time, but there is no far-field travelling axial wave. Several more rapidly decaying transverse modes can contribute near the source. For a symmetric central point drive, transverse modes vanishing at the centre are not excited.

= Euler-Bernoulli beam equation
{c}
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$u_{tt}+c_b^2u_{xxxx}=0$}

The Euler-Bernoulli beam equation models transverse linear bending waves in a slender beam. The normalized equation has $c_b=1$ and may be reduced by the <Schrodinger factorization of the elastic beam equation>. Prescribing displacement and curvature at an endpoint supplies two boundary traces for its fourth spatial derivative.

= Simply supported beam normal modes
{parent=Euler-Bernoulli beam equation}
{title2=$\omega_n=(n\pi/L)^2\sqrt{EI/\rho_\ell}$}

A uniform <elastic beam> with endpoint conditions $y=y_{xx}=0$ has <normal modes> $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ for positive integers $n$. Substitute $y=Xe^{-i\omega t}$ into $EIy_{xxxx}+\rho_\ell y_{tt}=0$ and impose the four endpoint conditions to obtain the displayed spectrum. Here $\rho_\ell$ is <mass> per unit <length>, $I$ is a <second moment of area>, and $EI$ is <bending stiffness>. <frequencies> proportional to $n^2$ distinguish <flexural waves> from the nondispersive ideal stretched string.

= Initial curvature incompatibility at a moment-free beam end
{parent=Simply supported beam normal modes}

A prescribed initial beam profile may vanish at a pivot while having nonzero endpoint <curvature>. If the post-release pivot is moment-free, its <bending moment> condition is $EIy_{xx}=0$. The data then cannot have a classical solution with continuous <curvature> at the initial boundary corners. A finite-energy <weak solution> can still represent release from preparing end moments. For example $y_0=ax(x-L)$ has odd <sine> coefficients proportional to $n^{-3}$; $\sum n^4|A_n|^2$ converges, giving finite bending <energy>. The <displacement> series takes the prescribed initial shape, but termwise endpoint second <derivatives> cannot be identified with its interior <curvature> limit. This conservative beam evolution does not have the immediate smoothing of a <heat equation>.

= Clamped-beam Green function
{parent=Euler-Bernoulli beam equation}
{title2=$G(x,\xi)=x_<^2(1-x_>)^2[3x_>-(1+2x_>)x_<]/6$}

For a unit-length <elastic beam> with unit bending stiffness, the <Green function> of $D_x^4$ with $G=G_x=0$ at both endpoints is the displayed kernel. It is piecewise cubic and continuous through its second <derivative>, while $G_{xxx}(\xi+,\xi)-G_{xxx}(\xi-,\xi)=1$. A constructive form is $G=ax^2+bx^3+(x-\xi)_+^3/6$, with $a=\xi(1-\xi)^2/2$ and $b=-(1-\xi)^2(1+2\xi)/6$. The right endpoint conditions determine these coefficients. Thus $y(x)=\int_0^1G(x,\xi)f(\xi)d\xi$ solves the clamped static beam problem.

= Reciprocity of the clamped-beam Green function
{parent=Clamped-beam Green function}
{title2=$G(x,\xi)=G(\xi,x)$}

The <clamped-beam Green function> is symmetric. Its explicit formula depends only on $x_<=\min(x,\xi)$ and $x_>=\max(x,\xi)$. More generally, two <integrations by parts> give $\int uv''''dx=\int u''v''dx=\int u''''v\,dx$ when $u=u'=v=v'=0$ at both endpoints. Applying this identity to two point-load responses shows that the displacement at one point caused by a unit force at a second equals the displacement at the second caused by a unit force at the first.

= Euler's critical load
{c}
{parent=Euler-Bernoulli beam equation}
{wiki}
{title2=$P_{\rm cr}=\pi^2EI_A/(KL)^2$}

= Euler buckling
{synonym}
{c}

A straight slender <elastic beam> under axial compression loses stability when bending can decrease its total <potential energy>. With pinned ends a distance $L$ apart, its quadratic potential is $\tfrac12\int_0^L[EI_A(y'')^2-P(y')^2]dx$. Stationarity gives $EI_Ay''''+Py''=0$, with $y=y''=0$ at each end. The first nontrivial mode is $\sin(\pi x/L)$, at $P_{\rm cr}=\pi^2EI_A/L^2$. More generally $P_{\rm cr}=\pi^2EI_A/(K L)^2$, with an effective-length factor $K$ determined by endpoint <constraints>: $K=1$ for pinned-pinned, $2$ for fixed-free and $1/2$ for fixed-fixed. Real imperfections remove the perfectly sharp onset, and short columns can yield before this elastic instability.

= Initially curved compressed beam
{parent=Euler's critical load}

For a pin-ended slender beam with unstressed profile $y_0$, <bending moment> depends on <curvature> change: $M=EI(y-y_0)$. An axial dead load gives $EI(y-y_0)=-Fy$ in the small-slope model.

= Sinusoidal imperfection amplification of a column
{parent=Initially curved compressed beam}
{title2=$y_{\max}=Y/(1-F/F_c)$}

With $y_0=Y\sin(\pi x/L)$ and $F_c=\pi^2EI/L^2$, the equilibrium branch connected to zero load has $y=Y\sin(\pi x/L)/(1-F/F_c)$. The pole as $F$ approaches $F_c$ marks breakdown of the small-deflection model, not a physically infinite beam deflection.

= Compressed Euler-Bernoulli beam and its endpoint-dependent buckling threshold
{parent=Euler-Bernoulli beam equation}
{title2=$\rho\phi_{tt}+\alpha\phi_{xx}+\beta\phi_{xxxx}=0$}

Positive compression $\alpha$ contributes negative quadratic <potential energy> $-\alpha\phi_x^2/2$, while positive <bending stiffness> $\beta$ contributes $\beta\phi_{xx}^2/2$. A <plane wave> has $\omega^2=(\beta k^4-\alpha k^2)/\rho$, so sufficiently long waves grow exponentially. For displacement-fixed, moment-free endpoints at $0,l$, the <natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint> on the slope give $\phi_{xx}=0$ as well as $\phi=0$. The modes are $\sin(n\pi x/l)$, and the first <Euler buckling of an elastic filament> threshold is $\alpha_c=\beta\pi^2/l^2$. For truly clamped ends, $\phi=\phi_x=0$, the threshold is instead $4\pi^2\beta/l^2$, with first static mode $1-\cos(2\pi x/l)$. More generally,
$$
\alpha_c=\beta\inf_{\phi\ne0}\frac{\int_0^l\phi_{xx}^2dx}{\int_0^l\phi_x^2dx},
$$
with the infimum taken over the specified endpoint class. At threshold there is a static zero mode and a marginal linear-in-time partner in the linear dynamics; above it the straight configuration is unstable.

= Schrodinger factorization of the elastic beam equation
{c}
{parent=Euler-Bernoulli beam equation}
{title2=$u_t=-v_{xx},\quad v_t=u_{xx}$}

Writing $q=u+iv$ in the <free Schrodinger equation> gives $u_t=-v_{xx}$ and $v_t=u_{xx}$, hence $u_{tt}+u_{xxxx}=0$. To encode initial velocity $u_1$, choose the decaying primitive $v_0(x)=-\int_x^\infty(s-x)u_1(s)ds$. A prescribed endpoint curvature becomes the time derivative of the imaginary boundary trace. This turns the normalized <Euler-Bernoulli beam equation> into a complex second-order boundary problem.

= Second moment of area
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$I$}
{wiki}

The second moment of area of a cross-section about an axis is $I=\int y^2\,dA$, where $y$ is the perpendicular distance from that axis. Its dimensions are length to the fourth power. For a circular section of radius $a$, polar integration gives $I=\int_0^a r^3dr\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta d\theta=\pi a^4/4$. A slender rod with <Young's modulus> $E$ has <filament bending modulus> $B=EI$: the axial <strain> caused by <curvature> $\kappa$ is $-y\kappa$, and integrating the <linear elasticity> energy density $E y^2\kappa^2/2$ over the section gives $B\kappa^2/2$ per unit length.

= Polar second moment of area
{parent=Second moment of area}
{title2=$J_p=\int_A r^2\,dA$}

The polar second moment of a planar area about a perpendicular axis is the integral of squared distance from that axis over area. It has units $\mathrm{m^4}$ and equals the sum of second moments about two perpendicular in-plane axes through the same origin. For a disk of radius $a$, $J_p=\int_0^a2\pi r^3\,dr=\pi a^4/2$. It is geometric, not a mass <moment of inertia>; a uniform cylinder has axial mass moment $\rho LJ_p$.

= Rectangular section second moment of area
{parent=Second moment of area}
{title2=$I=ab^3/12$}

For a rectangular cross-section of horizontal width $a$ and vertical thickness $b$, the <second moment of area> about its horizontal <centroid> axis is $I=a\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}z^2\,dz=ab^3/12$. Thus increasing vertical thickness is especially effective at reducing the <curvature> caused by a given <bending moment>.

= Poisson's ratio
{c}
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$\nu$}
{wiki}

Poisson's ratio is minus the transverse strain divided by longitudinal strain under uniaxial loading in isotropic <linear elasticity>.

= Young's modulus
{c}
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$E$}
{wiki}

Young's modulus is the ratio of axial <stress> to axial <strain> in uniaxial <linear elasticity>. For an isotropic plate, the bending rigidity is $E t^3/[12(1-\nu^2)]$, with thickness $t$ and <Poisson's ratio> $\nu$.

A <tensile modulus experiment> asks how much a specimen stretches under an axial <force>, while allowing its sides to contract freely. For initial cross-sectional <area> $A_0$ and an <extensometer> spanning initial <gauge length> $L_g$, define <engineering stress> $\sigma=F/A_0$ and <engineering strain> $\varepsilon=\Delta L_g/L_g$. In the proportional range,
$$
E=\frac{d\sigma}{d\varepsilon}=\frac{L_g}{A_0}\frac{dF}{d(\Delta L_g)}.
$$
The ratio $\sigma/\varepsilon$ agrees with this <derivative> when the line passes through the unloaded origin. A fitted offset should not be mistaken for a material property. <Young's modulus> has units of <pressure>: $1\ \mathrm{Pa}=1\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. By contrast, the <spring constant> of a uniform tensile specimen of length $L$ is $k_s=EA_0/L$, with units $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Two rods made of the same material can therefore have very different <spring constants> while sharing <Young's modulus>.

The normalization removes a useful geometrical ambiguity: doubling $L$ doubles the extension at a given <force>, whereas doubling $A_0$ halves it. For a round specimen $A_0=\pi d^2/4$; for a rectangular one $A_0=wt$, using initial diameter $d$, width $w$ and thickness $t$. Diameter error matters twice at first order: $\delta A_0/A_0\simeq2\delta d/d$. Specify material direction, <temperature> and loading rate when comparing <Young's modulus>. Lateral restraint changes the <stress> state and can prevent this experiment from measuring the uniaxial modulus.

The name commemorates <Thomas Young>, whose formulation of a measure of <elasticity> is recorded by the Royal Society. This attribution alone does not establish priority for every modern definition or experimental method.<Young; Thomas (1773–1829); physician, physicist and Egyptologist by Royal Society>[Royal Society person record NA7947, Activity: formulation of a measure of elasticity called Young's Modulus]{ref}

= Centrifugal strain of a thin elastic hoop
{parent=Young's modulus}
{title2=$\Delta r/r\simeq\rho\omega^2r^2/E$}

A small arc of a rotating thin hoop needs inward <force> per length $\rho A\omega^2r$. Its circumferential <tension> supplies this through curvature, giving <tension> $\rho A\omega^2r^2$ and tensile <stress> $\rho\omega^2r^2$. <Hooke's law> converts that <stress> to circumferential <strain>, which equals fractional radius change. Small <strain>, a slender cross-section and uniaxial <linear elasticity> are required; the cross-sectional area cancels at leading order.

= Tensile modulus experiment
{parent=Young's modulus}

MIT's Spring 2004 laboratory assignment Properties of Heat Treated Steel gives a concrete <tensile testing> protocol. Record transducer channels and calibration factors; measure specimen diameter and length, and the <extensometer> <gauge length> with its locking pin inserted. Zero the <load cell>, pin the threaded grips into their adapters, and attach the <extensometer> centrally. Apply the specified seating load, record actuator position, then acquire readings every second while moving at about $0.05\ \mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$. The handout derives <engineering stress> from <load cell> <force>, <engineering strain> from <extensometer> deformation, and <Young's modulus> by <linear regression> below the <proportional limit>; it warns that actuator-derived <strain> includes apparatus deformation.<Properties of Heat Treated Steel by Massachusetts Institute of Technology>[Laboratory assignment 10, printed pp. 2–3: Procedure for Tension Tests; Calculations]{ref}

For a modulus-focused measurement, select a load window after seating, hold <temperature> and loading rate consistent, and record <force>, local extension and <crosshead displacement> together. Fit $F=F_0+k_g\Delta L_g$ within that window and calculate $E=k_gL_g/A_0$. Inspect the <regression residuals> for curvature; repeat at a lower maximum load and include unloading to check <elastic deformation>. These are diagnostic additions to the cited protocol, whose full test continues through failure. The handout's seating load is specific to its specimens and apparatus, rather than a universal preload.

An <extensometer> measures the change between its contact points; <crosshead displacement> measures travel of the machine's moving member. The manufacturer Instron explicitly distinguishes local <strain> from travel that includes machine deformation and <machine compliance>.<Static Axial Clip-On Extensometers by Instron>[FAQs: How does a clip-on extensometer work?; When should I use a clip-on extensometer instead of crosshead displacement?]{ref} The <gauge length> $L_g$ can be shorter than the uniform length $L$ between grips. Thus even a perfectly rigid machine generally has $\Delta L_g\ne x$: the local instrument sees only its own segment. Do not divide one channel's extension by the other channel's reference length.

Grips must transmit axial <force> without slip. Misalignment adds <bending moment> and makes opposite sides experience different axial <strains>; an instrument on just one side may then report a biased modulus. A changing extension offset, hysteresis between loading and unloading, or load-dependent fitted slope is evidence to investigate seating, slip or material response. A straight line over a short interval alone does not identify which is responsible.

= Machine compliance
{parent=Tensile modulus experiment}
{title2=$C_m$}

<Machine compliance> is the extra load-path <displacement> per applied <force> attributed to the apparatus in a specified measurement model. Its units are $\mathrm{m\,N^{-1}}$, the reciprocal units of a <spring constant>. Here $C_m$ includes the frame, <load cell>, adapters and grips insofar as their deformation contributes to the recorded <crosshead displacement> $x$. It is a property of that configuration and readout, rather than a universal constant of the testing machine.

Assume a uniform specimen with initial length $L$, constant $A_0$ and <Young's modulus> $E$, small axial <elastic deformation>, quasistatic loading, no slip, and a linear, length-independent apparatus response over the chosen <force> window. Equilibrium makes the same <force> act through specimen and apparatus, while their extensions add. The two <springs> in series therefore give
$$
x=x_0+\frac{FL}{EA_0}+C_mF,\qquad
q(L)=\frac{dx}{dF}=\frac{L}{EA_0}+C_m,
\qquad k_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac1{q(L)}.
$$
The offset $x_0$ describes the displacement origin after seating; the slope $q$ describes total compliance. Fitting an intercept avoids forcing an arbitrary zero through the origin, but cannot repair slip or a curved response.

If all of $x-x_0$ is incorrectly attributed to specimen extension, the inferred modulus is
$$
E_{\mathrm{app}}=\frac{L}{A_0q(L)}
=\frac{E}{1+EA_0C_m/L}.
$$
For positive $C_m$, this is too small. The fractional bias is most serious for a short, thick, stiff specimen because its own compliance $L/(EA_0)$ is small. A local <extensometer> instead has $d(\Delta L_g)/dF=L_g/(EA_0)$ in this ideal model, excluding deformation outside its contacts.

A shaped specimen requires more care. With varying initial cross-sectional <area> $A(s)$ and uniform $E$, its axial compliance is $\int ds/[EA(s)]$ in the one-dimensional approximation. Shoulder and end deformation must be included explicitly or remain identical between tests and be absorbed into an effective intercept. Changing only the <extensometer> <gauge length> on an unchanged specimen does not change its total compliance and cannot perform a <two-length machine compliance separation>.

= Two-length machine compliance separation
{parent=Machine compliance}
{title2=$E=\frac{L_2-L_1}{A_0(q_2-q_1)}$}

Prepare two uniform specimens with the same material, direction and $A_0$, but different deforming lengths $L_1,L_2$. Keep grip engagement, adapters, loading window and measurement configuration equivalent. Determine $q_i=dx_i/dF$ separately by <linear regression> with its own displacement intercept. Subtracting the two series equations eliminates the common <machine compliance>:
$$
q_2-q_1=\frac{L_2-L_1}{EA_0},\qquad
E=\frac{L_2-L_1}{A_0(q_2-q_1)},\qquad
C_m=\frac{L_2q_1-L_1q_2}{L_2-L_1}.
$$
This is an <inverse problem>: one length supplies only one equation for two unknowns, while two distinct lengths separate the length-dependent specimen contribution from the constant contribution. In a graph of compliance against length, the slope is $1/(EA_0)$ and the vertical intercept is $C_m$. It is the compliance, not $k_{\mathrm{obs}}$, that is linear in $L$.

The <dimensional analysis> is revealing: $[q_2-q_1]=\mathrm{m/N}$, so $[(L_2-L_1)/(A_0(q_2-q_1))]=\mathrm{N/m^2}$. For $L_2>L_1$, the ideal model requires $q_2>q_1$. A negative fitted $C_m$ challenges the common-intercept assumptions or the measurements. Two lengths determine two parameters exactly, so they cannot test the assumed straight line; three or more lengths allow <regression residuals> to reveal departures. Nearly equal lengths amplify slope errors through the small difference $q_2-q_1$. If the actual apparatus compliances differ by $\Delta C_m$, the inferred length slope becomes $1/(EA_0)+\Delta C_m/(L_2-L_1)$.

= Worked two-length tensile compliance example
{parent=Two-length machine compliance separation}

The following values are stipulated arithmetic inputs, not measurements of a material. Take $A_0=2.00\ \mathrm{mm^2}$ and lengths $L_1=50.0\ \mathrm{mm}$, $L_2=100.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. At <forces> $F=(0,100,200,300,400)\ \mathrm{N}$, stipulate <crosshead displacements>
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1&=(0.005,0.040,0.075,0.110,0.145)\ \mathrm{mm},\\
x_2&=(0.008,0.068,0.128,0.188,0.248)\ \mathrm{mm}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The two different initial offsets deliberately test the use of intercepts. <Linear regression> of $x_i=a_i+q_iF$ gives $a_1=0.005\ \mathrm{mm}$, $a_2=0.008\ \mathrm{mm}$ and
$$
q_1=3.50\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mm/N},\qquad
q_2=6.00\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mm/N}.
$$
Their reciprocal <spring constants> are $k_{\mathrm{obs},1}=2.85714\ \mathrm{kN/mm}$ and $k_{\mathrm{obs},2}=1.66667\ \mathrm{kN/mm}$. The compliance-versus-length slope is
$$
m=\frac{6.00\times10^{-4}-3.50\times10^{-4}}{100.0-50.0}\ \mathrm{N^{-1}}
=5.00\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{N^{-1}},
$$
so the recovered <Young's modulus> and <machine compliance> are
$$
E=\frac1{A_0m}=100000\ \mathrm{N/mm^2}=100\ \mathrm{GPa},\qquad
C_m=q_1-mL_1=1.00\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mm/N}.
$$
The corresponding apparatus <spring constant> is $1/C_m=10.0\ \mathrm{kN/mm}$.

= Linear reversibility and yield in tensile tests
{parent=Tensile modulus experiment}

<Linearity> and reversibility answer different questions. The <proportional limit> ends the interval in which axial <stress> is proportional to axial <strain>. <Elastic deformation> means that deformation is recovered after unloading; a nonlinear response can still be elastic. Conversely, a short straight portion of a loading curve does not by itself demonstrate recovery.

To check recovery, unload and examine the local <extensometer> <strain> relative to its initial value, allowing for instrument resolution, drift and any time dependence. A residual extension can indicate <plasticity in continuum mechanics>, but grip slip can produce a residual in <crosshead displacement> without the same local residual. Compare the channels before assigning the effect to the specimen. <Yield point> concerns the onset of irreversible deformation; a specified offset <proof stress> is an operational convention rather than <Young's modulus>. For example, a $0.2\%$ offset <proof stress> is the intersection of the loading curve with $\sigma=E(\varepsilon-0.002)$, using the correctly referenced <strain>. Report the fitted <stress>/<strain> window and the unloading evidence alongside the modulus, so that apparatus seating, departure from <linearity> and onset of <plasticity in continuum mechanics> remain distinguishable.

= Fracture toughness
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{title2=$K_c$}
{wiki}

Fracture toughness measures a material's resistance to crack advance. In a thin <elastic plate> model, the threshold for advance can be represented by a prescribed front <curvature> $\kappa_f$. Such a curvature parameter is not numerically interchangeable with a bulk fracture-toughness parameter without specifying the fracture model.

= Griffith fracture criterion
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{c}

Crack growth becomes energetically possible when the <energy release rate> reaches its critical value. For fixed crack geometry this gives a brittle-failure <stress> scaling as $\sigma_f\propto\sqrt{EG_c}$.

= Energy release rate
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{title2=$G$}

The decrease in potential energy per increment of crack <area> under specified loading constraints. Its critical value $G_c$ is a crack-growth threshold, not energy stored per specimen <volume>.

= Critical energy release rate
{parent=Energy release rate}
{title2=$G_c$}

The threshold <energy release rate> for propagation of a specified crack under specified environmental and loading conditions.

= Leak-before-break criterion
{parent=Fracture toughness}

A requirement that a crack penetrate a pressure boundary and produce a detectable leak before unstable fracture. A simple screening comparison uses critical crack size versus wall thickness, but the result depends on crack geometry and on whether <stress> or pressure is held fixed.

= Yield-limited leak-before-break thickness
{parent=Leak-before-break criterion}
{title2=$t_{\max}=K_{IC}^2/(\pi Y^2\sigma_y^2)$}

A conservative material-screening upper bound obtained by requiring wall penetration before fast fracture even at a stated yield-limited <stress>. Substituting a thickness-dependent pressure <stress> instead yields a different fixed-pressure inequality; the two comparisons must not be conflated.

= Strain energy release rate
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{title2=$G$}

The reduction in a mechanical system's potential energy per incremental crack area under specified loading conditions. In small-scale-yielding linear elasticity, mode-I <plane strain> gives $G=K_I^2/E'$ with $E'=E/(1-\nu^2)$. The critical value includes the dissipative processes associated with crack advance.

= Crack-tip plastic zone
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{title2=$r_p\sim(K_I/\sigma_y)^2$}

A region of irreversible deformation near a crack tip where an elastic singular-field extrapolation would exceed the material's local yielding response. Its size depends on loading and constraint. Plastic work and blunting can raise resistance to crack extension.

= Stress intensity factor
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{title2=$K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}$}

An amplitude characterizing a near-tip linear-elastic crack field for a specified loading mode. The mode-I opening factor scales as the displayed expression for a suitable crack geometry factor $Y$. It is not itself the local <stress>, and large-scale plasticity can invalidate a linear-elastic characterization.

= Intergranular fracture
{parent=Fracture toughness}

Crack propagation predominantly along <grain boundaries>. Boundary cavities, chemical segregation and environmental attack are possible contributors; fracture appearance alone does not establish which caused failure.

= Cleavage fracture
{parent=Fracture toughness}

Brittle crack propagation associated with separation along particular crystallographic planes. Cleavage facets and river patterns can help identify the mechanism, while establishing its cause requires material, loading and exposure evidence.

= Ductile-to-brittle transition
{parent=Fracture toughness}
{title2=$T_{\rm DBTT}$}

= Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature
{synonym}

A change from predominantly ductile deformation and fracture to predominantly brittle fracture as <temperature> decreases under specified loading and specimen conditions. A quoted transition <temperature> depends on its criterion: absorbed impact energy, fracture appearance and <fracture toughness> need not give the same number. In ferritic <steel>, <irradiation hardening> can raise the transition <temperature>.

= Hooke's law
{c}
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{wiki}

Hooke's law states that the restoring force or stress is proportional to a sufficiently small displacement or strain. For a one-dimensional spring, $f=kx$ in magnitude.

= Transverse stiffness of a stretched spring
{parent=Hooke's law}
{title2=$k_\perp=T_0/L$}

A taut <spring> of equilibrium <length> $L$ and initial <tension> $T_0$ whose ends acquire a small relative transverse displacement $y$ tilts through $y/L$. Its leading transverse <force> is therefore $-T_0y/L$. The <length> change is quadratic in $y$, so using only incremental axial extension misses this first-order <force>. A Hooke <spring> with natural <length> $l_0$ has $T_0=k(L-l_0)$ and $k_\perp=k(1-l_0/L)$. The unstretched limit has no linear transverse stiffness under this geometry.

= Zero-natural-length spring
{parent=Hooke's law}
{title2=$V=\tfrac12k|\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|^2$}

An ideal spring with stiffness $k>0$ and zero natural length has extension equal to the distance between its endpoints. Its <elastic potential energy> is exactly $k|\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|^2/2$, and its force on endpoint 2 is $-k(\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1)$. Thus its Cartesian stiffness is isotropic and constant. Coupled systems of such springs have exactly linear displacement equations after a constant force, such as gravity, is absorbed into the <equilibrium> position. This special model differs from an ordinary finite-natural-length spring, whose transverse small-motion stiffness depends on its tension and equilibrium length.

= Spring
{parent=Hooke's law}
{title2=$F=kx$}

A linear <spring> stores <elastic energy> $kx^2/2$ when displaced by $x$ from its relaxed position. The external <force> maintaining that <displacement> is $kx$; its <restoring force> on the moving attachment has the opposite sign. Its <spring constant> $k$ has units <force> per length.

= Zero-rest-length spring
{parent=Spring}
{title2=$V=\kappa s^2/2$}

A <zero-rest-length spring> has quadratic <potential energy> in its actual length $s$, with no subtracted natural length. It is a useful idealization, not an ordinary unstretched <spring> of arbitrary length. For an end-to-end <spring> on a two-link pendulum, changing the natural length changes the quadratic bending stiffness about the straight configuration.

= Series and parallel spring stiffness
{parent=Spring}
{title2=$k_{\rm eff}$}

Identical linear <springs> of stiffness $k$ have equivalent stiffness $2k$ in parallel and $k/2$ in series. Parallel <springs> have equal <displacement> and add <forces>; series <springs> carry equal <force> and add extensions. A <mass> $m$ attached to an equivalent massless <spring> has <angular frequency> $\sqrt{k_{\rm eff}/m}$.

= Central spring stiffness matrix
{parent=Spring}
{title2=$V=\tfrac12q^TKq,\quad K_{ij}=-k_{ij}n_{ij}n_{ij}^T\ (i\ne j)$}

For point masses joined by unstretched central <springs> obeying <Hooke's law>, let $n_{ij}$ be the equilibrium <unit vector> along an edge and $u_i$ the small <displacement> of its endpoint. The leading extension is $n_{ij}\cdot(u_j-u_i)$, so its quadratic <elastic potential energy> is $k_{ij}[n_{ij}\cdot(u_j-u_i)]^2/2$. Consequently the block <stiffness matrix> has off-diagonal blocks $K_{ij}=-k_{ij}n_{ij}n_{ij}^T$ and diagonal blocks $K_{ii}=\sum_jk_{ij}n_{ij}n_{ij}^T$, with absent edges contributing zero. It is <symmetric> and <positive semidefinite>. The <normal modes> solve $Kq=\omega^2Mq$, where $M$ is the <mass matrix>. Rigid translations and infinitesimal rotations are <rigid-motion zero modes of a spring framework>. A prestressed spring can add transverse quadratic terms, so the unstretched assumption matters.

= Torsion spring
{parent=Spring}
{wiki}
{title2=$\tau=-\kappa\Delta\theta,\quad U=\tfrac12\kappa(\Delta\theta)^2$}

= Torsional spring
{synonym}

An ideal linear torsion spring exerts a restoring <torque> proportional to the relative <angle> between its two ends. Integrating the torque gives its <elastic potential energy>. A spring connecting two rotating bodies contributes $\kappa(\theta_2-\theta_1)^2/2$ to the <potential energy>; a spring connected to a fixed frame is obtained by setting one angle to zero. The coefficient $\kappa$ is the <torsional spring constant>.

= Torsion of a solid circular shaft
{parent=Torsion spring}
{title2=$C=GJ_p\phi/L,\quad J_p=\pi a^4/2$}

A circular shaft twisted uniformly through small end angle $\phi$ over length $L$ has local engineering <shear strain> $r\phi/L$ at radius $r$. Its <shear stress> is $Gr\phi/L$, so the integrated <torque> is $C=\int_A r(Gr\phi/L)\,dA=GJ_p\phi/L$, using the <polar second moment of area>. The restoring torque has the opposite sign to the imposed twist. The stored elastic <energy> is $C\phi/2=GJ_p\phi^2/(2L)$. The circular cross-section is important: general noncircular sections can warp and have a different torsion constant.

= Spring constant
{parent=Spring}
{title2=$k$}

The spring constant is the positive proportionality coefficient in the ideal <spring> <restoring force> $F=-kx$. Its SI unit is newtons per metre. With a constant attached <mass> $m$, it sets the <angular frequency> $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ of <simple harmonic motion>. Doubling $k$ doubles the restoring <force> at a fixed <displacement>, but increases that <angular frequency> only by a factor $\sqrt2$.

= Elastic plate
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{wiki}

An elastic plate is a thin elastic body whose leading deformation is bending of its middle surface.

= Line-forced fluid-loaded bending plate
{parent=Elastic plate}
{title2=$D(k)=\gamma(\beta k^4-m\omega^2)-\rho_0\omega^2$}

An <elastic plate> coupled to one compressible fluid half-space has an outgoing <Fourier transform> kernel $D$, where $\gamma^2=k^2-\omega^2/c_0^2$. A harmonic line <force> $F_0$ produces displacement transform $F_0\gamma/D$. Propagating acoustic components have $\gamma=-i\sqrt{\omega^2/c_0^2-k^2}$, whereas guided components decay normally to the plate. The outgoing prescription separates <acoustic energy flux> from guided-wave energy transport.

= Flexural wave
{parent=Elastic plate}
{title2=$\omega^2=(B/m)k^4$}

For a thin plate with areal mass $m$ and <bending stiffness> $B$, small free transverse disturbances satisfy $m\eta_{tt}+B\nabla^4\eta=0$. A <plane wave> of tangential <wavenumber> $k$ therefore satisfies
$$
\omega^2=(B/m)k^4.
$$
The frequency is quadratic in <wavenumber>, so these waves are dispersive. Matching this free-wave relation cancels the plate's inertial and bending response in an acoustic transmission problem.

= Flexural-gravity wave
{parent=Flexural wave}
{title2=$\omega^2=(gk+\beta k^5)/(1+\alpha k)$}

A flexural-gravity wave bends a floating <elastic plate> while displacing the underlying water. For a uniform plate with areal mass $m$ and <bending stiffness> $D$ above inviscid deep water of <mass density> $\rho_w$, put $\alpha=m/\rho_w$ and $\beta=D/\rho_w$. Combining the plate equation with the water's <kinematic boundary condition> gives the displayed <dispersion relation>. Both <gravitational acceleration> and plate bending restore displacement; water and plate inertia resist acceleration. It assumes small amplitude, no prestress and negligible shear deformation of the plate.

= Elastic-cover frequency ratio for water waves
{parent=Flexural-gravity wave}
{title2=$F(k)^2=[1+Bk^4/(\rho g+\sigma k^2)]/[1+(a\rho_s/\rho)k]$}

In the specified thin-sheet deep-water model, bending stiffness adds restoring force while sheet <mass> adds inertia. Their ratio to an uncovered <gravity-capillary wave> gives the displayed expression. Added inertia first lowers <frequency> at small <wavenumber>; at formally large <wavenumber> bending produces a <frequency> ratio proportional to $k^{1/2}$. That formal asymptote must not be extrapolated past the range where a thin-sheet continuum description is valid.

= Minimum phase speed of a flexural-gravity wave
{parent=Flexural-gravity wave}
{title2=$c_{p,\min}=\min_{k>0}\omega(k)/k$}

Write $\beta=D/\rho_w$ and $\alpha=m/\rho_w$ in the <dispersion relation of a floating elastic plate>. A stationary point of squared <phase velocity> satisfies $\beta k^4(3+2\alpha k)=g(1+2\alpha k)$. If plate inertia is neglected, its solution is $k=(g/(3\beta))^{1/4}$. A steadily translating load can resonate only when its speed matches a <phase velocity>; this threshold differs from the <group-velocity minimum of a flexural-gravity wave>.

= Dispersion relation of a floating elastic plate
{parent=Flexural-gravity wave}
{title2=$\omega^2=\frac{\rho_wgk+Dk^5}{\rho_w+mk}$}

For <bending stiffness> $D$ and mass per area $m$, a <plane wave> of surface displacement $\eta$ satisfies $m\eta_{tt}+D\nabla_H^4\eta=p$. An inviscid deep-water <velocity potential> has vertical dependence $e^{kz}$; the <kinematic boundary condition> and linear <Bernoulli equation> give $p=(\rho_w\omega^2/k-\rho_wg)\eta$. Eliminating $p$ gives the displayed <dispersion relation>. With water depth $d$, replace it by $\omega^2=(gk+Dk^5/\rho_w)\tanh(kd)/[1+(m/\rho_w)k\tanh(kd)]$.

= Group-velocity minimum of a flexural-gravity wave
{parent=Flexural-gravity wave}
{title2=$c_g=d\omega/dk$}

The <group velocity> of a <flexural-gravity wave> diverges on the long-wave gravity branch and on the short-wave bending branch, and therefore has a positive minimum between them when the <bending stiffness> is positive. This minimum bounds the propagation speed of narrow <wave packets> within the ideal model. It does not by itself determine a wind-generation threshold: steady forcing requires matching <phase velocity>, while dissipation and coupling determine growth.

= Viscous peeling of an elastic plate
{parent=Elastic plate}
{title2=$U_p\sim Bh_f^3/(\mu l_p^5)$}

A fluid advancing under an <elastic plate> can be resisted mainly by <lubrication theory> flow near its front. For front gap $h_f$ and peeling length $l_p$, the elastic pressure gradient is of order $Bh_f/l_p^5$, while the gap <volume flux> is of order $Bh_f^4/(\mu l_p^5)$. Mass balance therefore gives peeling speed $U_p\sim Bh_f^3/(\mu l_p^5)$.

= Axisymmetric clamped-plate deflection
{parent=Elastic plate}
{title2=$h=p(R^2-r^2)^2/(64B)$}

A circular <elastic plate> with <bending stiffness> $B$, constant excess pressure $p$ and <clamped boundary conditions> at radius $R$ obeys $B\nabla_r^4h=p$, where $\nabla_r^2=r^{-1}\partial_r(r\partial_r)$. Its regular solution is $h=p(R^2-r^2)^2/(64B)$. Consequently its enclosed volume is $V=\pi pR^6/(192B)$, and its edge <curvature> is $h_{rr}(R)=pR^2/(8B)=24V/(\pi R^4)$.

= Bending stiffness
{title2=$B$}
{parent=Elastic plate}
{wiki}

= Flexural rigidity
{synonym}

Bending stiffness is the coefficient relating plate curvature to bending moment. In linear thin-plate theory a transverse displacement $\zeta$ produces a restoring load $B\nabla^4\zeta$.

= Nondegenerate one-dimensional elastic material
{parent=Linear elasticity}

For the one-dimensional reduction of isotropic <linear elasticity>, nondegeneracy means that the effective longitudinal modulus $\lambda+2\mu$ is nonzero. The static <Navier-Cauchy equation> then forces the displacement to be affine.

= Displacement field

{disambiguate=mechanics}
{wiki}

= Displacement field
{synonym}

A displacement field $\mathbf u(\mathbf x)$ gives the change in position of each material point from a reference configuration.

= Lagrangian fluid displacement
{parent=Displacement field (mechanics)}
{c}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\xi$}

The Lagrangian fluid displacement $\boldsymbol\xi$ compares neighbouring fluid trajectories. Linear <velocity> <perturbations> about a steady flow $\mathbf U$ obey $\delta\mathbf u=(\partial_t+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\xi-(\boldsymbol\xi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U$. For a background scalar $f_0$, its material <perturbation> is $\Delta f=\delta f+\boldsymbol\xi\cdot\nabla f_0$. In an axisymmetric <normal mode> about cylindrical rotation, the radial and vertical <velocities> are $-i\omega\xi_r$ and $-i\omega\xi_z$. The displacement of a <free surface> is needed to impose the <Lagrangian pressure perturbation> condition.

= Displacement gradient tensor
{parent=Displacement field (mechanics)}

The displacement gradient has components $(\nabla\mathbf u)_{ij}=\partial_i u_j$ and records local changes of the displacement field.

This tensor is a local derivative of the <displacement field>; it enters the deformation gradient and strain measures.

= Lamé parameter
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{c}

The two Lamé parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$ determine the linear isotropic relation between stress and strain; $\mu$ is the shear modulus.

= Poisson solid
{parent=Lamé parameter}
{c}
{title2=$\lambda=\mu,\quad\nu=1/4,\quad\alpha^2=3\beta^2$}

An isotropic <linear elasticity> material with equal positive <Lamé parameters> has <Poisson's ratio> $1/4$ and the displayed compressional/shear speed ratio. This simplifying model fixes the relative amplitudes in the <tensile-crack radiation pattern>; it does not describe every stable isotropic solid.

= Isotropic linear-elastic energy density
{parent=Linear elasticity}

For the two-dimensional convention $\nabla\mathbf u=(\partial_i u_j)$, the energy density
$$
W=\frac\mu2\nabla\mathbf u:\nabla\mathbf u^T
+\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)^2
$$
produces the static isotropic elastic equations by variation.

= Navier-Cauchy equation
{parent=Linear elasticity}
{c}

In a homogeneous isotropic elastic body without body forces, static equilibrium of the displacement field is
$$
\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u
+(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)=0.
$$

This is the static, homogeneous-isotropic displacement form of <linear elasticity>.

= Uniform extension of a one-dimensional elastic body
{parent=Navier-Cauchy equation}

For a one-dimensional body with endpoints displaced by zero and $\Delta$, the static Navier-Cauchy equation reduces to $u''=0$ and gives the uniform strain solution $u(x)=\Delta x/L$.

= Elastic wave in an isotropic solid
{parent=Linear elasticity}

For density $\rho$ and <Lamé parameters> $\lambda,\mu$, the displacement satisfies
$$
\rho\mathbf u_{tt}
=(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u.
$$
Its longitudinal and transverse wave speeds are
$$
c_P=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho}},
\qquad
c_S=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}}.
$$

= Shear-horizontal wave
{title2=SH wave}
{parent=Elastic wave in an isotropic solid}

A shear-horizontal wave propagating in the $x$ direction has displacement perpendicular to the sagittal $xy$ plane, for example $\mathbf u=w(x,y,t)\mathbf e_z$. It is divergence-free and obeys the scalar shear-wave equation $w_{tt}=c_S^2(w_{xx}+w_{yy})$.

= SH input impedance
{c}
{parent=Shear-horizontal wave}
{title2=$Z=-T/V$}

The <SH input impedance> is the negative ratio of vertical <traction> to particle <velocity> for a <shear-horizontal wave> field, with positive depth and tension-positive <traction>. A superposition of upward and downward waves generally has a depth-dependent complex ratio. A downward progressive wave in a uniform medium has $Z=\mu p_\beta$, while the upward wave has $Z=-\mu p_\beta$. The input ratio includes reflection from deeper structure and can have poles at <velocity> nodes.

= SH impedance Riccati equation
{c}
{parent=SH input impedance}
{title2=$Z'=-i\omega(Z^2/\mu-\mu p_\beta^2)$}

With $e^{ikx-i\omega t}$ phasors, positive depth $z$, <shear modulus> $\mu=\rho\beta^2$, vertical <traction> $T$ and particle <velocity> $V$, the shear-wave equations give $V'=-i\omega T/\mu$ and $T'=-i\omega\mu p_\beta^2V$, where $p_\beta^2=\beta^{-2}-k^2/\omega^2$. Define $Z=-T/V$ wherever $V\ne0$. Differentiation gives the displayed <Riccati equation> and $V'=i\omega ZV/\mu$. A pole of $Z$ can be an ordinary zero of $V$, not a singular physical wavefield.

= Uniform-layer SH impedance update
{parent=SH impedance Riccati equation}
{title2=$Z(z)=\frac{Z_r\cos\kappa d+iq\sin\kappa d}{\cos\kappa d+i(Z_r/q)\sin\kappa d}$}

In a uniform layer, put $q=\mu p_\beta$, $\kappa=\omega p_\beta$ and $d=z-z_r$. Initial impedance $Z_r$ gives the displayed fractional-linear solution and $V(z)=V_r[\cos\kappa d+i(Z_r/q)\sin\kappa d]$. Expanding into exponentials yields forward and backward <shear-horizontal waves>. Negative $d$ propagates a bottom load upward. If the denominator vanishes, the nonsingular pair $(V,T)$ must be propagated through the impedance pole.

= Reflection from a layered SH impedance
{parent=Uniform-layer SH impedance update}
{title2=$R=(q_a-Z_{\rm in})/(q_a+Z_{\rm in})$}

Set the lower <half-space> load to $q_b=\mu_bp_{\beta,b}$ using the <causal vertical wavenumber branch>, which excludes a wave arriving from depth infinity. Apply the <uniform-layer SH impedance update> upwards through every layer, keeping particle <velocity> and <traction> continuous. For upper <half-space> impedance $q_a$, incident <velocity> $V_0$ and reflected <velocity> $RV_0$, the top values are $V=V_0(1+R)$ and $T=-q_aV_0(1-R)$. Their ratio gives the displayed <reflection coefficient>, incorporating every underlying interface and multiple reflection.

= Complex-frequency SH flux monotonicity
{parent=SH impedance Riccati equation}
{title2=$S_z'=-\tfrac12\operatorname{Im}\omega[|T|^2/\mu+(\rho+\mu k^2/|\omega|^2)|V|^2]$}

For positive real <mass density> and <shear modulus>, real $k$ and $\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$, the displayed <derivative> of $S_z=-\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(V^*T)$ follows from the two first-order shear equations. Every nontrivial solution has strictly decreasing $S_z$. Since $S_z=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}Z|V|^2$, positive-real impedance at a deeper point remains positive at all shallower points; negative-real impedance at a shallower point remains negative deeper down. A zero field is the necessary exception to strictness. At complex <frequency> this is a spectral flux diagnostic, reducing to a physical cycle average on the real axis.

= Causal vertical wavenumber branch
{parent=Shear-horizontal wave}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}k_\beta>0\iff\operatorname{Re}(k_\beta/\omega)>0$}

For real horizontal <wavenumber> $k$, positive real <wave speed> $\beta$ and $\omega=u+iv$ with $v>0$, write $k_\beta=a+ib$ with $k_\beta^2=\omega^2/\beta^2-k^2$. Its imaginary part gives $ab=uv/\beta^2$. The number $b$ cannot vanish, and $\operatorname{Re}(k_\beta/\omega)=v[b+u^2/(\beta^2b)]/|\omega|^2$ has the sign of $b$. The branch $b>0$ makes $e^{ik_\beta z}$ decay towards increasing depth and analytically continues the outgoing propagating or evanescent branch to the upper <frequency> half-plane.

= Guided shear-horizontal mode
{title2=SH mode}
{parent=Shear-horizontal wave}

Between parallel boundaries, a guided shear-horizontal mode has a discrete transverse wavenumber $q_n$ and dispersion relation $\omega_n^2=c_S^2(k^2+q_n^2)$. Its cutoff frequency is $c_Sq_n$.

= Mode conversion at a planar elastic interface
{parent=Elastic wave in an isotropic solid}

An obliquely incident in-plane P- or SV-wave generally produces reflected and transmitted P- and SV-waves. Continuity of displacement and traction supplies four scalar conditions for their four amplitudes, while frequency and tangential wavenumber are shared.

= Solid-fluid P-wave transmission coefficient
{parent=Mode conversion at a planar elastic interface}
{title2=$T_P=\frac{2\rho\alpha_f\cos2\theta_S\cos\theta_P}{\rho_f\alpha_f\cos\theta_P+\rho\alpha E\cos\theta_f}$}

For incidence from a solid with compressional/shear speeds $\alpha,\beta$ into a fluid of sound speed $\alpha_f$, let $E=\cos^22\theta_S+(\beta^2/\alpha^2)\sin2\theta_P\sin2\theta_S$. The displayed coefficient multiplies the transmitted <displacement field> potential relative to the incident <P-wave> potential, using the same retarded-time waveform. <Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves> matches the tangential slowness. Continuity of normal <displacement field>, zero tangential <traction>, and continuity of normal <traction> give the coefficient. It is not a displacement-amplitude coefficient: longitudinal <displacement field> acquires an additional ratio $\alpha/\alpha_f$.

= Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves
{parent=Mode conversion at a planar elastic interface}
{c}

Phase matching at a planar interface preserves frequency and tangential wavenumber. Thus waves of speeds $c_j$ and angles $\theta_j$ from the normal satisfy
$$
\frac{\sin\theta_j}{c_j}=\text{constant}.
$$

= Acoustic impedance
{title2=$Z=\rho c$}
{parent=Elastic wave in an isotropic solid}
{wiki}

The acoustic impedance of a fluid is $Z=\rho c$. For a progressive plane acoustic wave, pressure amplitude $P$ and velocity amplitude $U$ in its propagation direction satisfy $P=ZU$. At an oblique planar interface, zero reflection requires a matching condition involving the normal impedances $Z/\cos\theta$ or, equivalently for displacement amplitudes, $Z\cos\theta$.

= Normal acoustic impedance
{parent=Acoustic impedance}
{title2=$Z_n$}

The normal acoustic impedance of a progressive <acoustic wave> at a planar boundary is the pressure amplitude divided by the normal-velocity amplitude. For propagation angle $\theta$ from the normal,
$$
Z_n=\frac{\rho c}{\cos\theta}=\frac{\rho\omega}{k_n}.
$$
The distinction from the <acoustic impedance> $\rho c$ matters at oblique incidence: interface reflection depends on the ratio of normal impedances. For an evanescent outgoing wave $k_n$ is imaginary and the normal impedance is reactive, giving no mean normal <acoustic energy flux>.

= Surface acoustic impedance
{parent=Normal acoustic impedance}
{title2=$Z=P/V$}

At an acoustic boundary, the <surface acoustic impedance> is the <pressure> amplitude divided by the <velocity> amplitude directed into that boundary. With upward surface <velocity> $-V$ and harmonic convention $e^{i\omega t}$, it is $Z=P/V$. Reflection compares this impedance with the incident fluid's <normal acoustic impedance>. The <velocity> orientation and harmonic convention must be fixed before interpreting signs of inertial, elastic, and radiation terms.

= Passive acoustic impedance
{parent=Surface acoustic impedance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}Z\geq0$}

With <pressure> amplitude $P=ZV$ and <velocity> $V$ directed into a boundary, the mean absorbed <acoustic energy flux> is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(PV^*)=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}Z|V|^2$. A passive boundary cannot supply net energy, so $\operatorname{Re}Z\geq0$ at a real frequency. Its reflection from a propagating <acoustic plane wave> has magnitude at most one. A pole continued to a complex incidence angle or frequency must be interpreted separately from real-frequency passive scattering.

= Acoustic transmission through a uniform layer
{parent=Acoustic impedance}

A layer of impedance $Z_1$, wavenumber $k_1$, and thickness $L$ between identical media of impedance $Z_0$ has pressure-amplitude transmission coefficient
$$
|T|=\left[\cos^2(k_1L)+\frac14\left(\frac{Z_1}{Z_0}+\frac{Z_0}{Z_1}\right)^2\sin^2(k_1L)\right]^{-1/2}.
$$
It transmits perfectly at the layer resonances $k_1L=n\pi$, irrespective of the impedance mismatch.

= Coherent acoustic layer above a rough reflector
{parent=Acoustic transmission through a uniform layer}
{title2=$D=t_{21}I/(1-r_{12}r_be^{2i\beta d})$}

In a coherent specular model, let $r_b$ be the effective lower pressure reflection coefficient referenced to $z=0$, and let the planar upper interface lie at $z=d$. With layer and upper normal impedances $Z_1,Z_2$, $r_{12}=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$ and $t_{21}=2Z_1/(Z_1+Z_2)$. Summing repeated round trips gives the displayed downward amplitude at the upper interface. The mean layer field is $De^{i\alpha x}[e^{-i\beta(z-d)}+r_be^{2i\beta d}e^{i\beta(z-d)}]$. Feedback through diffuse roughness channels must be neglected in this scalar model or included in a context-dependent effective reflection coefficient.

= Displacement reflection from an interface between two inviscid elastic liquids
{parent=Acoustic impedance}

For a P-wave incident at angle $\theta$ from a liquid $(\rho,c,\lambda)$ onto $(\rho',c',\lambda')$, with transmitted angle $\theta'$ obeying <Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves>,
$$
R=\frac{\lambda'\sin2\theta-\lambda\sin2\theta'}
{\lambda'\sin2\theta+\lambda\sin2\theta'}.
$$
No reflection is equivalent to $\rho'c'\cos\theta=\rho c\cos\theta'$.

= Elastic-wave energy flux
{parent=Elasticity (physics)}

For displacement velocity $\dot u$ and stress $\sigma$, the instantaneous elastic-energy flux is
$$
P_i=-\sigma_{ij}\dot u_j.
$$
For complex harmonic amplitudes, its average over one temporal period is
$$
\langle P_i\rangle=-\frac12\operatorname{Re}(\widehat\sigma_{ij}\widehat{\dot u}_j^*).
$$

= P-SV directional impedance matrix
{c}
{parent=Elastic-wave energy flux}
{title2=$Z_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}\pm A&C\\-C&\pm B\end{pmatrix}$}

For real <frequency> $\omega>0$ and both vertical wavenumbers $p=k_\alpha>0$, $s=k_\beta>0$, let $D=k^2+ps$, $A=\rho\omega p/D$, $B=\rho\omega s/D$, and $C=k(2\mu D-\rho\omega^2)/(\omega D)$. With <traction> $\mathbf t=(\sigma_{xz},\sigma_{zz})$ and particle <velocity> $\mathbf v=(v_x,v_z)$, each directional <P-wave>/<SV-wave> field obeys $\mathbf t=-Z_\pm\mathbf v$. Direct displacement-potential substitution yields $Z_-=-Z_+^\dagger$. The <Hermitian part> of $Z_+$ is positive diagonal; thus forward/backward fluxes have opposite signs and mixed directional flux cross terms cancel.

= P-SV characteristic reconstruction
{c}
{parent=P-SV directional impedance matrix}
{title2=$(Z_+-Z_-)^{-1}=D(2\rho\omega)^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(1/p,1/s)$}

For a mixture of forward and backward <elastic waves>, $\mathbf v=\mathbf v^++\mathbf v^-$ and $\mathbf t=-Z_+\mathbf v^+-Z_-\mathbf v^-$. The diagonal inverse displayed above gives $\mathbf v^+=-(Z_+-Z_-)^{-1}(\mathbf t+Z_-\mathbf v)$ and $\mathbf v^-=(Z_+-Z_-)^{-1}(\mathbf t+Z_+\mathbf v)$. It is well-defined when both modes propagate strictly away from grazing. At a vanishing vertical <wavenumber> its singularity signals a failure of this strict directional split.

= Reflection of an SV-wave from a rigid plane
{parent=Elastic-wave energy flux}
{c}

An incident SV-wave generally reflects as both an SV-wave and a P-wave. A rigid boundary determines their amplitudes by requiring both displacement components to vanish, while phase matching preserves frequency and tangential wavenumber.

= Evanescent reflected P-wave at a rigid plane
{parent=Reflection of an SV-wave from a rigid plane}
{c}

For incident SV angle $\theta$, tangential phase matching gives $\sin\phi=(c_P/c_S)\sin\theta$ for the reflected P-wave. It is evanescent when $\sin\theta>c_S/c_P$.

= Unit-modulus SV reflection with evanescent P conversion
{parent=Evanescent reflected P-wave at a rigid plane}
{c}

When the converted P-wave is evanescent, its amplitude decays away from the boundary and the reflected SV amplitude has modulus one. The P field stores reactive energy near the boundary but carries zero mean normal power.

= Rigid boundary condition for an elastic wave
{parent=Reflection of an SV-wave from a rigid plane}

At a perfectly rigid plane, every component of the displacement field vanishes.

A wavevector points in the direction of phase propagation; its magnitude is the wavenumber and its scalar product with position gives the spatial phase.

= Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations
{c}
{parent=Wavevector}

For a slowly varying local dispersion relation $\omega=\Omega(\mathbf k;\mathbf x,t)$, wave-packet rays obey
$$
\dot x_i=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial k_i},
\qquad
\dot k_i=-\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial x_i},
\qquad
\dot\omega=\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial t}.
$$

= WKB method
{c}
{parent=Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations}

The WKB method seeks a rapidly oscillating field in the form
$$
\phi(x,t)=A(x,t;\varepsilon)e^{i\theta(x,t)/\varepsilon},
\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll1,
$$
and determines the phase and slowly varying amplitude order by order in $\varepsilon$.

= Eikonal equation
{parent=WKB method}
{wiki}

The leading WKB phase obeys an eikonal equation. For a local dispersion relation $\omega=\Omega(k;x,t)$ and $k=\nabla\theta$, it is
$$
-\partial_t\theta=\Omega(\nabla\theta;x,t).
$$

= Total derivative along a ray
{parent=Hamiltonian ray-tracing equations}

Along a ray $\mathbf x(t)$, the derivative of a field $f(\mathbf x,t)$ is
$$
\frac{df}{dt}
=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
+\dot{\mathbf x}\cdot\nabla f.
$$

An evanescent wave has an imaginary component of its wavevector and therefore decays exponentially in that direction instead of transporting energy away as a propagating wave.
