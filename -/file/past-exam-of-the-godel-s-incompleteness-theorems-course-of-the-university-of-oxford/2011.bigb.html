= Solution

Write $BA=P(\ulcorner A\urcorner)$, with the corner notation understood as the numeral for the <Gödel number>. An <arithmetical provability predicate> numerates the theorems of $S$ in the standard natural numbers and satisfies the <Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
& S\vdash A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\vdash BA,\qquad\text{an external rule;}\\
& S\vdash B(A\to C)\to(BA\to BC);\\
& S\vdash BA\to B(BA).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last two conditions are internal theorems, not rules authorizing reflection from $BA$ to $A$.

Here $P(x)=\exists y\,\operatorname{Prov}(x,y)$ numerates the theorem set by the given assumption. If $S\vdash A$, its proof supplies a genuine natural-number witness, making $BA$ a true <Sigma-1 formula>. <Sigma-1 completeness> gives $S\vdash BA$, proving the first condition. The second condition is supplied explicitly. Since $BA$ is itself a <Sigma-1 formula>, the given formalized <Sigma-1 completeness> yields $S\vdash BA\to B(BA)$, the third condition. Thus **the specified existential proof predicate is an <arithmetical provability predicate>**.

= Solution

Write $BA=\exists y\,\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner A\urcorner,y)$ and fix a refutable <first-order sentence> $\bot$, so $S\vdash\neg\bot$ and $\operatorname{Con}_S=\neg B\bot$. First prove the internal equivalence
$$
\boxed{S\vdash G\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Con}_S.}
$$
Because $\bot\to G$ is provable, the <derivability conditions> give $B\bot\to BG$. The fixed-point equivalence $G\leftrightarrow\neg BG$ therefore yields $G\to\neg B\bot$.

For the reverse implication, necessitating $G\to\neg BG$ and applying distribution gives $BG\to B(\neg BG)$. <positive introspection> gives $BG\to B(BG)$. Finally the provable implication $BG\to(\neg BG\to\bot)$, necessitated and distributed, gives
$$
B(BG)\to\bigl(B(\neg BG)\to B\bot\bigr).
$$
Consequently $S\vdash BG\to B\bot$, so $\neg B\bot\to\neg BG\to G$. The same argument works with any chosen refutable <first-order sentence> in place of $\bot$; their formal consistency statements are provably equivalent.

By the classical <deduction theorem for propositional logic> applied to adjoining a <first-order sentence>, $S+\{G\}$ is consistent exactly when $S\nvdash\neg G$. The displayed equivalence makes $S+\{G\}$ and $S+\{\operatorname{Con}_S\}$ deductively equivalent. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S\nvdash\neg G\iff S+\{\operatorname{Con}_S\}\text{ is consistent}.}
$$
No soundness assumption on that extension is being substituted for consistency.

If $S$ is inconsistent, it proves $\neg R$ by <explosion>. Conversely, suppose $S$ is consistent and, towards a contradiction, has a proof of $\neg R$ with standard code $n$. Consistency excludes any proof of $R$. In particular, for each $k\leq n$, the closed bounded statement $\neg\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner R\urcorner,\overline k)$ is true and is provable in $S$ by <Sigma-1 completeness>. The true bounded statement $\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner,\overline n)$ is also provable.

Using the given finite-numeral exhaustion below $\overline n$, these finitely many nonproof facts give
$$
S\vdash\forall y\leq\overline n\,\neg\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner R\urcorner,y).
$$
For arbitrary $y$, the given comparison splits into $y\leq\overline n$ or $\overline n\leq y$. In the first case the antecedent $\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner R\urcorner,y)$ is false. In the second case the known negative proof at code $\overline n$ supplies the required witness $z\leq y$. Hence $S$ proves
$$
\forall y\left(\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner R\urcorner,y)\to\exists z\leq y\,\operatorname{Prov}(\ulcorner\neg R\urcorner,z)\right).
$$
The <Rosser sentence> equivalence now gives $S\vdash R$, contradicting consistency. Thus
$$
\boxed{S\nvdash\neg R\iff S\text{ is consistent}.}
$$
Only a fixed finite list of closed bounded facts was used; no assumption that the <first-order theory> is omega-consistent is needed.

The preceding <Gödel sentence> argument gives $S\vdash G\to\operatorname{Con}_S$. The permitted arithmetized positive half of the <Rosser sentence> argument is
$$
S\vdash\operatorname{Con}_S\to\neg BR.
$$
Pure first-order logic gives $\neg BR\to R$: if there is no proof of $R$, each antecedent in its defining universal implication is false. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S\vdash G\to\operatorname{Con}_S\to\neg BR\to R,\qquad S\vdash G\to R.}
$$
This uses the supplied internal Rosser implication, rather than replacing an external nonprovability fact by an unproved internal <first-order sentence>.

Let $T=S+\{\operatorname{Con}_S\}$ and assume $T$ is consistent. The allowed arithmetizations of the previous equivalences give
$$
S\vdash\neg B(\neg R)\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Con}_S,\qquad S\vdash\neg B(\neg G)\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Con}(T).
$$
Suppose $S\vdash R\to G$. Its contrapositive is $\neg G\to\neg R$, and the <Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions> then give
$$
S\vdash B(\neg G)\to B(\neg R),\qquad S\vdash\neg B(\neg R)\to\neg B(\neg G).
$$
Combining with the two internal equivalences yields $S\vdash\operatorname{Con}_S\to\operatorname{Con}(T)$. Since $T$ contains $\operatorname{Con}_S$, it would prove $\operatorname{Con}(T)$, contrary to the given <Gödel second incompleteness theorem> for this consistent extension. Hence
$$
\boxed{S\nvdash R\to G\quad\text{if }S+\{\operatorname{Con}_S\}\text{ is consistent}.}
$$
Together with the preceding implication, this is the <strict Gödel-to-Rosser implication> under the stated consistency hypothesis.

<Löb's theorem> states that, for a <first-order theory> with the stated provability and diagonalization properties,
$$
\boxed{S\vdash(BA\to A)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\vdash A,}
$$
where $BA=P_S(\ulcorner A\urcorner)$. It does not require an extra consistency assumption.

By the <diagonal lemma>, choose $D$ with $S\vdash D\leftrightarrow(BD\to A)$. <necessitation rule> and distribution applied to $D\to(BD\to A)$ give
$$
S\vdash BD\to B(BD\to A),\qquad S\vdash BD\to(B(BD)\to BA).
$$
Using <positive introspection> $BD\to B(BD)$ gives the unconditional theorem $S\vdash BD\to BA$. If $S\vdash BA\to A$, it follows that $S\vdash BD\to A$, and the reverse fixed-point implication gives $S\vdash D$. Necessitating gives $S\vdash BD$, hence $S\vdash BA$ and finally $S\vdash A$.

The same derivation also gives the <internal form of Löb's theorem>, needed for modal soundness. Put $H=(BA\to A)$. From the unconditional $BD\to BA$, propositional reasoning and the fixed point give $S\vdash H\to D$. <necessitation rule> and distribution imply $S\vdash BH\to BD$, and hence
$$
\boxed{S\vdash B(BA\to A)\to BA.}
$$
This internal statement was derived without necessitating a temporary assumption.

The <Gödel second incompleteness theorem> says that a consistent, effectively axiomatized <first-order theory> with enough arithmetic and its standard derivability and diagonalization properties cannot prove its <formal consistency statement> $\operatorname{Con}_S=\neg B\bot$.

If a consistent $S$ proved $\operatorname{Con}_S$, it would prove $B\bot\to\bot$ by <classical propositional logic>. Apply <Löb's theorem> to $A=\bot$. This would give $S\vdash\bot$, contradicting consistency. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S\text{ consistent}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\nvdash\operatorname{Con}_S.}
$$
For an inconsistent <first-order theory> the conclusion need not hold, since it proves every <first-order sentence>.

Assume $S\vdash\neg X$ and $S\vdash BX\to X$. Combining these theorems gives $S\vdash\neg BX$. Because $X$ is refutable, $\neg BX$ is a legitimate <formal consistency statement> for $S$: the standard contradiction <first-order sentence> and $X$ are provably interderivable, and the <derivability conditions> transfer those implications to their provability statements.

Thus if $S$ were consistent, the <Gödel second incompleteness theorem> would contradict $S\vdash\neg BX$. Consequently the assumed reflection theorem forces $S$ to be inconsistent; <explosion> then gives $S\vdash X$. This establishes the instance of <Löb's theorem>
$$
\boxed{S\vdash BX\to X\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\vdash X}
$$
for every $X$ refutable in $S$, using only the second incompleteness theorem.

Assume $S\vdash B_SX\to X$ and put $T=S+\{\neg X\}$. Then $T\vdash\neg B_SX$. The given extension-provability implication, with $Y=\bot$, gives
$$
S\vdash B_T\bot\to B_S(\neg X\to\bot).
$$
The provable classical implication $(\neg X\to\bot)\to X$, together with distribution, gives $S\vdash B_S(\neg X\to\bot)\to B_SX$. Hence
$$
T\vdash B_T\bot\to B_SX,\qquad T\vdash\neg B_T\bot=\operatorname{Con}(T).
$$
If $T$ were consistent, this would violate its <Gödel second incompleteness theorem>. Thus $T$ is inconsistent. The <deduction theorem for propositional logic> and double-negation elimination give $S\vdash\neg\neg X$, hence $S\vdash X$. Therefore **the second incompleteness theorem for $S+\{\neg X\}$ yields <Löb's theorem> for arbitrary $X$**.

Put $T=S+\{X\}$ and choose its <Gödel sentence> $Z$, so $T\vdash Z\leftrightarrow\neg B_TZ$. The assumed reflection scheme gives the particular theorem
$$
T\vdash B_S(X\to Z)\to(X\to Z).
$$
The given extension-provability implication is $S\vdash B_TZ\to B_S(X\to Z)$. The fixed-point equivalence gives $T\vdash\neg Z\to B_TZ$, so these implications compose to
$$
T\vdash\neg Z\to(X\to Z).
$$
The supplied propositional <tautology> $((\neg Z\to(X\to Z))\to(X\to Z))$ gives $T\vdash X\to Z$, and $T\vdash X$ then yields $T\vdash Z$. The first of the <derivability conditions> for $T$ gives $T\vdash B_TZ$, while its Gödel equivalence gives $T\vdash\neg B_TZ$. Hence $T$ is inconsistent. By the <deduction theorem for propositional logic>,
$$
\boxed{S\vdash\neg X.}
$$
This proof uses diagonalization and proof transfer for the extension, and invokes neither <Löb's theorem> nor the second incompleteness theorem.

The <Gödel-Löb logic> $\mathrm{GL}$ uses propositional variables, $\bot$, implication, and a unary <modal operator> $\Box$. Other Boolean connectives are abbreviations; for example $\neg A$ means $A\to\bot$ and $\top$ means $\bot\to\bot$.

Its axioms are all classical propositional tautologies and the two schemes
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathrm K:&\quad\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B),\\
\mathrm L:&\quad\Box(\Box A\to A)\to\Box A.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Its rules are the <modus ponens rule> and the <necessitation rule>, $\mathrm{GL}\vdash A\Rightarrow\mathrm{GL}\vdash\Box A$. Uniform <propositional substitution> is allowed, or equivalently built into the axiom schemes. <necessitation rule> applies to theorems; a temporary assumption cannot simply be boxed. In <provability logic>, $\Box A$ is read as the formal assertion that $A$ is provable.

= Solution

Induct on a derivation in <Gödel-Löb logic>. Propositional axiom instances translate to theorems of <Peano arithmetic>. The image of $\bot$ is a refutable sentence, so it behaves as falsity in those translated propositional arguments.

The translation of axiom $\mathrm K$ is the distribution clause of the <Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions>. The translation of axiom $\mathrm L$ is
$$
\operatorname{PA}\vdash B(BA\to A)\to BA,
$$
the <internal form of Löb's theorem>, whose derivation was given above and applies to the stipulated <arithmetical provability predicate> for <Peano arithmetic>. Thus every axiom has a provable translation.

The <modus ponens rule> is preserved because the interpretation commutes with implication. The <necessitation rule> is preserved by the first derivability condition: if $\operatorname{PA}\vdash\mathcal I_i(A)$, then
$$
\operatorname{PA}\vdash Pr(\ulcorner\mathcal I_i(A)\urcorner)=\mathcal I_i(\Box A).
$$
Here the equality denotes the definition of the translated formula, not an arithmetic equality of truth values. A propositional substitution merely changes the arithmetic sentences assigned to the variables. The induction proves the <arithmetical soundness of GL>:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{GL}\vdash A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{PA}\vdash\mathcal I_i(A).}
$$

Take $Y(p)=p\to q$ and put $\top=(\bot\to\bot)$, $H=Y(\top)$ and $F_0=Y(\Box H)=\Box H\to q$. The first supplied substitution identity reads
$$
\mathrm{GL}\vdash\Box H\leftrightarrow\Box Y(\Box H)=\Box F_0.
$$
Use the supplied replacement of equivalents in the context $D(p)=p\to q$. It gives
$$
\mathrm{GL}\vdash F_0\leftrightarrow(\Box F_0\to q).
$$
But $H=(\top\to q)$ is equivalent to $q$. Replacement under a box gives $\Box H\leftrightarrow\Box q$, so $F_0$ is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{F=(\Box q\to q).}
$$
Replacing equivalents once more transfers the fixed-point equation to $F$, proving $\mathrm{GL}\vdash F\leftrightarrow(\Box F\to q)$.

A direct check of this <GL fixed point for a provability implication> is useful: the Löb axiom gives $\Box F\to\Box q$, and necessitating the <tautology> $q\to F$ gives $\Box q\to\Box F$ by $\mathrm K$. Thus $\Box F\leftrightarrow\Box q$, and replacing the antecedent in $\Box F\to q$ recovers $F$.

Use the preceding fixed point with $q=\bot$. If the chosen arithmetic interpretation sends $\bot$ to a PA-refutable sentence $Z$, it gives the arithmetic sentence
$$
C=(BZ\to Z),\qquad\operatorname{PA}\vdash C\leftrightarrow(BC\to Z).
$$
This follows from <arithmetical soundness of GL>. Since <Peano arithmetic> proves $\neg Z$, the two implications are equivalent to negated provability:
$$
\operatorname{PA}\vdash C\leftrightarrow\neg BZ,\qquad\operatorname{PA}\vdash C\leftrightarrow\neg BC.
$$
Thus $C$ expresses the <formal consistency statement> for <Peano arithmetic> and is also a negative provability fixed point, like its <Gödel sentence> $G$.

Apply the supplied modal uniqueness formula to the context with a <modalized occurrence> $X(p)=(\Box p\to\bot)$, with two fresh variables interpreted as $G$ and $C$. A <modalized occurrence> means that each indicated variable occurrence lies inside a box. <Peano arithmetic> proves both arithmetic fixed-point equations. The first derivability condition boxes those equations, so the translated uniqueness formula gives
$$
\operatorname{PA}\vdash B(G\leftrightarrow C).
$$
One must not infer $G\leftrightarrow C$ by reflection. Instead, distribution and the provability of the two propositional projections give $\operatorname{PA}\vdash BG\leftrightarrow BC$. Negating and using the two fixed-point equations now yields
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{PA}\vdash G\leftrightarrow C\leftrightarrow\neg BZ=\operatorname{Con}(\operatorname{PA}).}
$$
This proves the <equivalence of a Gödel sentence and a consistency statement> using the required modal soundness, fixed point and uniqueness results.
