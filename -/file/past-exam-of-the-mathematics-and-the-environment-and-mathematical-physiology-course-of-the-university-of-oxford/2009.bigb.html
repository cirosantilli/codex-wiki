= Solution
{parent=i}

For <Röthlisberger channel drainage>, $S$ is the water-filled cross-sectional <area> of the subglacial channel, $Q$ its <volume> discharge, $m$ the <mass> of <ice> melted per unit channel <length> per unit <time>, and $N=p_i-p_w$ the <effective pressure in subglacial drainage>[effective pressure], namely <ice> overburden <pressure> minus <water> <pressure>. The first balance converts melt <mass> to <area> growth through division by <ice> <density> $\rho_i$, and subtracts <material creep> closure $KSN^3$. The cubic <pressure> dependence comes from the adopted ice-flow law; $K$ includes its material and geometric factors.

The second relation balances the downslope hydraulic driving gradient $\rho_wg\sin\alpha$ against the adopted frictional <pressure> loss. It is an instantaneous discharge-area law, giving $Q=(\sin\alpha/f)^{1/2}S^{4/3}$ for positive downstream flow. The final balance equates <latent heat> $mL$ needed to melt the channel walls with the gravitational <power> dissipated per unit <length>, $\rho_wg\sin\alpha\,Q$. It assumes that this dissipated <power> is available for melting rather than substantial sensible heating or other <energy> losses. The model thus couples **melt opening, pressure-driven <material creep> closure and hydraulic discharge**.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

With constant lake surface <area>, raising its level by $dh$ changes its <volume> by $S_L\,dh$. The symbol $M_L$ in the proposed balance is an effective filling rate measured as lake-level rise per unit <time>, so the <volume> inflow is $S_LM_L$. <Volume> conservation gives
$$
S_L\frac{dh}{dt}=S_LM_L-Q,\qquad\boxed{h_t=M_L-Q/S_L.}
$$
Thus the lake rises when its inflow exceeds channel discharge and falls in the opposite case. If an inflow were instead specified directly as a <volume> rate $I_L$, its contribution would be $I_L/S_L$; keeping these units distinct is necessary for the interpretation of $M_L$.

= Solution

<Hydrostatic pressure> at the entrance is $p_w=p_{\rm ref}+\rho_wgh$, with a constant reference <pressure>. Therefore $N=p_i-p_{\rm ref}-\rho_wgh$, and constant overburden <pressure> gives
$$
\boxed{N_t=-\rho_wg\,h_t=-\rho_wg(M_L-Q/S_L).}
$$
A filling lake raises <water> <pressure> and lowers <effective pressure in subglacial drainage>[effective pressure], reducing <material creep> closure. A draining lake has the reverse effect. These signs supply the <pressure> feedback in the coupled drainage model.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Write dimensional variables as their scales <times> dimensionless variables, with positive scales $S_0,Q_0,m_0,N_0,t_0$. For compactness define
$$
a=\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{f}\right)^{1/2},\qquad B=\frac{\rho_wg\sin\alpha}{L},\qquad D=\frac{\rho_iL}{S_L\sin\alpha}.
$$
A consistent explicit choice is
$$
S_0=\left(\frac{Ba}{\rho_iKD^3}\right)^{3/8},\quad Q_0=aS_0^{4/3},\quad m_0=BQ_0,\quad t_0=\frac{\rho_iS_0}{m_0},\quad N_0=DS_0.
$$
These identities give $t_0m_0/(\rho_iS_0)=1$, $Kt_0N_0^3=1$, $fQ_0^2/(\sin\alpha S_0^{8/3})=1$ and $\rho_wg\,t_0Q_0/(S_LN_0)=1$. Substitution therefore leaves the unit coefficients in all four balances, and the sole supply parameter is $\mu=M_LS_L/Q_0$. Expanding its scales yields
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{M_L\rho_i^2L^2\sqrt K\,f^{3/4}}{\sqrt{\rho_wgS_L}\,(\sin\alpha)^{11/4}}.}
$$
After dropping scale hats, eliminate the instantaneous quantities to obtain the useful two-dimensional system
$$
\boxed{S_t=S^{4/3}-SN^3,\qquad N_t=S^{4/3}-\mu,\qquad Q=m=S^{4/3}.}
$$
These scales assume a positive slope, positive <friction> coefficient and nonzero lake <area>; they nondimensionalize exactly the hydraulic law printed in the source.

= v
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

For $\mu>0$, the positive <equilibrium> is
$$
\boxed{Q_*=m_*=\mu,\qquad S_*=\mu^{3/4},\qquad N_*=\mu^{1/12}.}
$$
Set $S=S_*+\varepsilon S_1$ and $N=N_*+\varepsilon N_1$ and retain first-order terms. The linear system is
$$
\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}S_1\\N_1\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{1/4}/3&-3\mu^{11/12}\\4\mu^{1/4}/3&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}S_1\\N_1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Differentiating its first row and using the second gives
$$
\boxed{S_1''=\frac13\mu^{1/4}S_1'-4\mu^{7/6}S_1.}
$$
In particular the <matrix trace>[trace] is positive and <determinant> is $4\mu^{7/6}>0$. The steady drainage state is <linear stability>[linearly unstable] for every positive supply, whether its instability is oscillatory or nonoscillatory.

= vi
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vi}

The <characteristic> roots satisfy
$$
\lambda^2-\frac13\mu^{1/4}\lambda+4\mu^{7/6}=0.
$$
They are a nonreal <complex conjugate>[conjugate pair] precisely when $\mu^{1/2}/9-16\mu^{7/6}<0$, or $\mu^{2/3}>1/144$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu_c=144^{-3/2}=\frac1{1728}.}
$$
In <lake-coupled Röthlisberger drainage instability>, above this value the <real part> is $\mu^{1/4}/6>0$, so nonzero linear perturbations are sinusoidal <oscillations> with an exponentially growing envelope. Their <angular frequency> is $[4\mu^{7/6}-\mu^{1/2}/36]^{1/2}$.

Physically the feedback represents repeated lake filling and <pressure> increase, channel enlargement by melting, rapid drainage, <pressure> reduction and <material creep> closure. Large drainage events are glacial lake outburst floods, or jökulhlaups. **The unbounded <amplitude> is a conclusion of the linearized <equations>**, not a proof of infinite physical discharge or an indefinitely growing nonlinear solution. Once perturbations are comparable with the <equilibrium> scales, nonlinear dynamics and constraints such as lake depletion must be retained. At the threshold the two real roots coincide; below it the instability is nonoscillatory.

The cross-sectional <area> $A(s,t)$ multiplied by the mean downstream <speed> $u(s,t)$ is the <volume flux> $Q=Au$. The term $A_t$ is local storage change per unit river <length>, $(Au)_s$ is net downstream export per unit <length>, and $M>0$ is lateral <volume> input per unit <length> per unit <time>, for example distributed tributary or runoff supply.

For a fixed reach $[s_1,s_2]$, <incompressibility> and constant <density> give
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{s_1}^{s_2}A\,ds=Q(s_1,t)-Q(s_2,t)+\int_{s_1}^{s_2}M\,ds.
$$
Apply the <fundamental theorem of calculus> and localize the resulting <integral> identity to obtain $A_t+Q_s=M$. The <density> cancels because every term transports the same <water>; no sediment source is included in this balance.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

To obtain a <power-law river kinematic wave>, let $P_b$ be the wetted perimeter and $I$ the small bed slope. A uniform-flow <force> balance per unit river <length> is $\rho gAI=\tau_bP_b$. With a <Chezy friction law> $\tau_b=\rho f u^2$, it gives $u=(gI/f)^{1/2}(A/P_b)^{1/2}$. Thus the <hydraulic radius> determines the <power> of <area>.

For a channel family with $P_b=P_0A^q$, where $q<1$, we have $u=C A^{(1-q)/2}$, with $C=(gI/(fP_0))^{1/2}$. Set $m=(1-q)/2>0$ and $c=(m+1)C$. Then
$$
\boxed{Q=Au=\frac c{m+1}A^{m+1}.}
$$
For example a broad fixed-width rectangular channel has approximately constant wetted perimeter, giving $m=1/2$; a geometrically similar channel family has $P_b\propto A^{1/2}$, giving $m=1/4$. The exponent is therefore a geometry-dependent consequence of the <friction> balance, not a universal arbitrary value fixed by Chezy's law alone.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

At <equilibrium> $Q_s=M$, so $Q=Q_{\rm in}+Ms$ for an upstream boundary <flux> $Q_{\rm in}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A_{\rm eq}(s)=\left[\frac{m+1}{c}(Q_{\rm in}+Ms)\right]^{1/(m+1)}.}
$$
Without a specified upstream condition this <integration> constant must be retained. On a region of positive <area>, the <characteristic> <speed> is $Q'(A)=cA^m>0$, increasing downstream along the <equilibrium>. Small disturbances move downstream, spread in space as this <speed> increases, and decrease in <area> <amplitude> in the manner derived next. They are transported away from a fixed location; the model has no <diffusion> giving instantaneous smoothing. Finite-amplitude compressive disturbances can steepen into shocks because the <flux> is <convex>, $Q''(A)=cmA^{m-1}>0$, so the small-disturbance picture is not a global no-shock assertion.

= Solution

<Taylor expansion> gives $Q(A_{\rm eq}+a)=Q(A_{\rm eq})+cA_{\rm eq}^m a+O(a^2)$. Subtract the <equilibrium> <equation> and write $V(s)=cA_{\rm eq}^m(s)$ to obtain $a_t+(Va)_s=0$. Set $b=Va$. Since $V$ is <time> independent, $b_t+Vb_s=0$. The <travel coordinate for a conservative transport perturbation> $\xi(s)=\int_0^sV(r)^{-1}\,dr$ transforms this into $b_t+b_\xi=0$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{a(s,t)=\frac{\phi(\xi(s)-t)}{cA_{\rm eq}^m(s)}.}
$$
The conserved quantity along a <characteristic> is the <flux> perturbation $Va$, explaining the diminishing <area> <amplitude> as $V$ grows.

Differentiate the <equilibrium> relation to get $A_{\rm eq}'=M/(cA_{\rm eq}^m)=M/V$. Thus the arrival <time> is explicitly
$$
\boxed{t_l=\xi(l)=\frac{A_{\rm eq}(l)-A_{\rm eq}(0)}M
=\frac1M\left(\frac{m+1}{c}\right)^{1/(m+1)}\left[(Q_{\rm in}+Ml)^{1/(m+1)}-Q_{\rm in}^{1/(m+1)}\right].}
$$
For zero upstream <flux> the <integral> remains finite, giving the limiting formula with its second term zero. However $V(0)=0$ then makes the displayed <amplitude> formula singular at the head; a uniformly small arbitrary <area> disturbance imposed exactly there is outside this <linear stability analysis>[linearization]. A positive upstream <flux> avoids that degeneracy.

Here $h$ is <water> depth, $u$ the depth-averaged downstream <speed>, $s$ bed elevation relative to the reference slope, and $\eta=s+h$ free-surface elevation relative to that reference. The constants $\rho_w,\rho_s$ are <water> and grain densities, $S$ is the reference bed slope, $\tau$ bed <shear stress>, $\phi$ bed <porosity>, $c$ <suspended sediment> <mass> <concentration>, $q_b$ bedload solid <volume flux> per unit width, and $v_E,v_D$ erosion and deposition velocities. The settling <speed> is denoted by $v_s$ in the constitutive laws.

Depth <integration> of incompressible <water> conservation gives the depth-storage plus discharge-divergence balance. Depth-averaged <momentum> has local and advective acceleration on the left, and <gravity> driven by the actual surface gradient together with bed drag on the right. <Hydrostatic pressure> and a <shallow-water approximation> underlie this <shallow water equations>[Saint-Venant] description.

<Suspended sediment> conservation first has the conservative form $(hc)_t+(huc)_x=\rho_s(v_E-v_D)$; the <water> balance reduces it to the material-derivative form printed in the source. Solid bed <volume> per unit horizontal <area> is $(1-\phi)s$. Its storage plus bedload divergence equals deposition minus erosion, giving the Exner bed balance. Adding the suspended and bed balances after converting bed <volume> to grain <mass> cancels exchange terms: **erosion and deposition transfer sediment between the two reservoirs rather than create it**.

= Solution

Take an arbitrary reference depth $h_0>0$, and select $u_0=(gSh_0/f)^{1/2}$ so the uniform reference flow balances drag and slope. Work with positive downstream <velocity>, so $u_*=\sqrt f\,u$ in the dimensional constitutive laws. Introduce the erosion and bedload scales
$$
E_0=\frac{bf^{3/2}u_0^3}{v_s^2},\qquad q_{b0}=\frac{\rho_wKf^{3/2}u_0^3}{\Delta\rho g}.
$$
Matching erosion with deposition, suspended <advection> with erosion, and bed storage with erosion gives respectively
$$
c_0=\frac{\rho_sbf^2u_0^4}{\gamma v_s^4},\qquad x_0=\frac{h_0c_0u_0}{\rho_sE_0}=\frac{h_0\sqrt f\,u_0^2}{\gamma v_s^2},\qquad t_0=\frac{(1-\phi)h_0}{E_0}.
$$
Use $h_0$ for the scales of $\eta$ and $s$ too, so $h=\eta-s$ survives unchanged. After dividing the balances by their matched scales, the parameters are
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=\frac{x_0}{u_0t_0}=\frac{c_0}{\rho_s(1-\phi)},\quad F=\frac{u_0}{\sqrt{gh_0}},\quad\delta=\frac{Sx_0}{h_0},\quad\beta=\frac{q_{b0}}{E_0x_0}=\frac{\rho_wK v_s^2}{\Delta\rho g b x_0}.}
$$
For example the <momentum> balance divided by $gh_0/x_0$ has inertial prefactor $F^2$, surface-gradient term $-\eta_x$, and equal slope/drag prefactors $Sx_0/h_0=\delta$, because $fu_0^2=gSh_0$. The suspended balance has source $u^3-c/u$, and the bed balance has its negative with unit bed-time coefficient by the definition of $t_0$. These substitutions give the entire stated dimensionless system; $q_b=u^3$ is dimensionless bedload, with its dimensional scale already absorbed into $\beta$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For <quasistatic bed stability with suspended sediment>, in the leading approximation, $(hu)_x=0$ and $\eta_x=0$. For spatially varying perturbations at fixed unit <water> <flux> and fixed surface level, write $u=1+U$, $h=1+H$, $c=1+C$, $s=s_*+S_1$. The constraints give $H=-U$ and $S_1=-H=U$. Linearizing the suspended and bed <equations> yields
$$
C_x=4U-C,\qquad U_t+3\beta U_x=-4U+C.
$$
For a <Fourier mode> $e^{\sigma t+i\kappa x}$, the first relation gives $C=4U/(1+i\kappa)$; substitution into the second gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma=-\frac{4\kappa^2}{1+\kappa^2}-i\kappa\left(3\beta+\frac4{1+\kappa^2}\right).}
$$
Thus every nonzero spatial <frequency> has negative growth rate, independently of $\beta$. The parameter changes propagation, not damping. The spatially uniform mode has $\sigma=0$ and is neutral, representing a change among uniform reference states compatible with the leading constraints. Hence **there is no growing linear mode in this approximation**; the claim is stability, not strict decay of every homogeneous perturbation. Restoring the small discarded terms or changing <boundary conditions> requires a separate calculation.

In <alternating-access carrier transport>, the four reversible reaction pairs are $S_i+C_i\rightleftharpoons P_i$, $S_e+C_e\rightleftharpoons P_e$, $C_i\rightleftharpoons C_e$ and $P_i\rightleftharpoons P_e$. Binding and unbinding use $k_+,k_-$; both conformational pairs use $k$ in each direction. Define the net binding rates $B_i=k_+s_ic_i-k_-p_i$ and $B_e=k_+s_ec_e-k_-p_e$. <law of mass action>[Mass action] and the change of each species in each reaction give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\dot s_i&=-B_i,&\dot s_e&=-B_e,\\
\dot p_i&=B_i+k(p_e-p_i),&\dot p_e&=B_e+k(p_i-p_e),\\
\dot c_i&=-B_i+k(c_e-c_i),&\dot c_e&=-B_e+k(c_i-c_e).
\end{aligned}}
$$
For example binding consumes one free interior <substrate> and one free interior carrier, and produces one interior complex; loaded-carrier transformation then moves that bound <substrate> across the <cell membrane>[membrane]. A bare-carrier transformation alone does not transport <substrate>. The <concentration> convention implicitly uses compatible compartment-volume <normalization>, as required for the stated unweighted <conservation laws>.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Adding the two complex and two free-carrier <equations> cancels binding and conformational exchange, giving
$$
\boxed{p_i+p_e+c_i+c_e=c_0.}
$$
Each free or bound carrier contains one <carrier protein>, and the model neither synthesizes nor degrades it. Adding the two free-substrate and two bound-substrate <equations> likewise gives
$$
\boxed{s_i+s_e+p_i+p_e=s_0.}
$$
Binding stores <substrate> temporarily and loaded conformational exchange relocates it; neither changes total <substrate>. The two invariants count distinct conserved chemical moieties. The constants $c_0$ and $s_0$ should not be interchanged: <protein> and <substrate> need not be present in equal amounts.

= Solution

Replace only the free-substrate <equations> by
$$
\boxed{\dot s_i=-B_i-J,\qquad\dot s_e=-B_e+J.}
$$
The other four <equations> remain as above. The carrier invariant remains unchanged because no <protein> is added or removed. The two external <substrate> terms cancel in the total-substrate balance, so the second invariant also remains unchanged. This cancellation describes a balanced source and sink, even though there is a nonzero throughput. A constant sink is physically meaningful only while <substrate> <concentrations> remain nonnegative; it is not a state-dependent removal law valid after an interior reservoir is exhausted.

At <steady state> the interior free-substrate balance gives $B_i=-J$. Substituting this into the interior free-carrier balance gives $0=J+k(c_e-c_i)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c_i=c_e+J/k.}

For positive inward throughput there are more free carriers facing inward: release of transported <substrate> must be followed by outward return of empty carriers at net rate $k(c_i-c_e)=J$.

= Solution

The relation $B_i=-J$ means $k_-p_i=k_+s_ic_i+J$. Put $K=k_-/k_+$ and substitute the preceding expression for $c_i$:
$$
\boxed{p_i=\frac{s_ic_e}{K}+J\left(\frac1{k_-}+\frac{s_i}{Kk}\right).}
$$
The first term is the <equilibrium> binding contribution from the outward free-carrier pool; the additional terms account for the maintained current and the excess inward-facing free carriers. This is a steady throughput relation, not a detailed-balance assumption.

= Solution

The exterior free-substrate <equation> gives $B_e=J$ at <steady state>. Thus $k_+s_ec_e-k_-p_e=J$, and
$$
\boxed{p_e=\frac{s_ec_e}{K}-\frac J{k_-}.}
$$
The sign of the current term is opposite to the interior relation because <substrate> is supplied to the exterior and removed from the interior. Nonnegative occupancies are selected by the complete steady transport solution and compatible <substrate> totals.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The loaded-carrier balance gives $J=k(p_e-p_i)$. Substitute the preceding two complex <concentrations> and collect the current terms:
$$
J\left(1+\frac{2k}{k_-}+\frac{s_i}{K}\right)=\frac kK(s_e-s_i)c_e,
\qquad\boxed{J=\frac{kk_+(s_e-s_i)c_e}{2k+k_-+k_+s_i}.}
$$
Next carrier conservation gives
$$
c_0=\frac{s_i+s_e+2K}{K}c_e+\frac Jk\left(1+\frac{s_i}K\right),
\qquad\boxed{c_e=\frac{c_0K-(J/k)(s_i+K)}{s_i+s_e+2K}.}
$$
Eliminate $c_e$ between these relations and set $K_d=k/k_+$. Simplification yields
$$
\boxed{J=\frac{K_dKk_+c_0}{2}\frac{s_e-s_i}{(s_i+K+K_d)(s_e+K+K_d)-K_d^2}.}
$$
Here $c_0$ must be the **total <carrier protein>**, as in the conservation <equation>. Calling it total <substrate> in the final line of the scan is a typographical inconsistency; total <substrate> is the different invariant $s_0$.

For <carrier-turnover saturation of passive transport> at fixed $s_i$, write $A=s_i+K+K_d$, $D=A(K+K_d)-K_d^2>0$ and $C=K_dKk_+c_0/2>0$. Then $J=C(s_e-s_i)/(As_e+D)$, so
$$
J'=\frac{C(D+As_i)}{(As_e+D)^2}>0,\qquad J''=-\frac{2AC(D+As_i)}{(As_e+D)^3}<0,
$$
$$
\boxed{J(s_i)=0,\qquad \lim_{s_e\to\infty}J=\frac{K_dKk_+c_0}{2(s_i+K+K_d)}.}
$$
The <flux> is outward when the <substrate> gradient is reversed and inward when $s_e>s_i$. Saturation occurs because there is a finite carrier population and finite binding-release/conformational turnover: arbitrarily abundant exterior <substrate> cannot make carriers cycle arbitrarily quickly. This is passive facilitated transport, so no maintained <flux> remains when the two free <concentrations> agree. Varying $s_e$ at fixed $s_i$ compares reservoir-controlled steady states or systems with different total <substrate>; it is not a trajectory preserving one fixed $s_0$.

The cytoplasmic <concentration> $c$ gains a constant influx $r$ from outside the cell and loses <calcium> through a first-order extrusion term $kc$. The pump <flux> $J_+$ moves <calcium> from <cytoplasm> into the <sarcoplasmic reticulum>, <Hill function>[saturating] with cytoplasmic <concentration> through a <Hill function> with scale $K_1$, maximal rate $V_1$ and exponent $n$. The release <flux> $J_-$ returns stored <calcium> to the <cytoplasm>. It requires both <calcium> in the store and calcium-dependent activation of release; its two <Hill function>[saturating] factors have scales $K_2,K_3$ and exponents $m,p$. This represents <calcium-induced calcium release>. The leak $k_sc_s$ is passive return from the store.

Every internal transfer appears with opposite signs in the two balances, so $d(c+c_s)/dt=r-kc$ in the model's compatible <volume> <normalization>. Only influx and extrusion change the total. The nonlinear pump and activated release can redistribute this total rapidly and generate <oscillations> even with constant external input.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the <three-region calcium nullcline>, define
$$
J(u)=\frac{\beta u^2}{1+u^2},\qquad K(u)=\frac{u^4}{1+u^4},\qquad f=J(u)-K(u)\frac{v^2}{1+v^2}-\delta v.
$$
For $u>0$, $f(u,0)>0$, $f\to-\infty$ as $v\to\infty$, and $f_v=-2K(u)v/(1+v^2)^2-\delta<0$. Thus there is **exactly one positive root $v=V(u)$ for every positive $u$**. The curve starts at the origin and initially satisfies $v\sim\beta u^2/\delta$.

For $0<\beta<1$, let $u_c>0$ solve $J(u_c)=K(u_c)$ and put $K_c=K(u_c)$, $J_c'=J'(u_c)$, $K_c'=K'(u_c)$. Explicitly, with $z_c=u_c^2$,
$$
z_c=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4\beta(1-\beta)}}{2(1-\beta)},\qquad J'(u)=\frac{2\beta u}{(1+u^2)^2},\quad K'(u)=\frac{4u^3}{(1+u^4)^2}.
$$
In particular $u_c\sim\sqrt\beta$ and $K_c'-J_c'>0$ for small $\beta$.

On the large-$v$ branch away from the crossing, replace $v^2/(1+v^2)$ by one. This gives $v\sim[J(u)-K(u)]/\delta$ where this is large and positive. Near $u_c$ retain the next term $1-v^{-2}$ and linearize $J-K$:
$$
0\simeq-(K_c'-J_c')(u-u_c)+K_c/v^2-\delta v,
\qquad\boxed{u-u_c\simeq\frac{K_c/v^2-\delta v}{K_c'-J_c'}.}
$$
Finally where the leak is negligible compared with the other terms, $v^2/(1+v^2)\simeq J(u)/K(u)$, so $v\simeq[J/(K-J)]^{1/2}$ on the side $J<K$. These are overlapping asymptotic descriptions with different validity ranges, not three exact disconnected curves. The curve rises to a large peak, falls sharply near $u_c$, then approaches a small finite level as $u\to\infty$. At leading small-$\delta$ order that last level is $[\beta/(1-\beta)]^{1/2}$.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At every <equilibrium> the store balance gives $f=0$, and adding $\gamma$ <times> that balance to the cytoplasmic one gives $u=\mu$. The unique-root result therefore gives a single positive <equilibrium> $(\mu,V(\mu))$. Its <Jacobian matrix>[Jacobian] is
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}-1-\gamma f_u/\epsilon&-\gamma f_v/\epsilon\\f_u/\epsilon&f_v/\epsilon\end{pmatrix},\quad
\boxed{\det A=-f_v/\epsilon>0,\qquad\operatorname{tr}A=-1+(f_v-\gamma f_u)/\epsilon,}
$$
with <derivatives> evaluated at that <equilibrium>. At small $\mu$, $f_u>0$ and the <matrix trace>[trace] is negative; sufficiently large $\mu$ also has $f_u>0$, since $J'$ is of order $u^{-3}$ and $K'$ of order $u^{-5}$. Thus **both extreme regimes have a <linearly stable> unique <equilibrium>**.

The fast variable is not $v$ alone. Using <total-calcium fast fibres>, introduce total scaled <calcium> $w=u+\gamma v$. Then $\dot w=\mu-u$, while on fast <time> $\sigma=\tau/\epsilon$ the leading <equations> give $du/d\sigma=-\gamma f$, $dv/d\sigma=f$ and $w$ constant. <total-calcium fast fibres>[Fast paths] are therefore straight lines of slope $-1/\gamma$ in the $(u,v)$ plane. Attracting portions of the <critical manifold> satisfy $f_v-\gamma f_u<0$, rather than merely $f_v<0$. Along $v=V(u)$, slow motion obeys
$$
\boxed{\frac{du}{d\tau}=\frac{\mu-u}{1+\gamma V'(u)},\qquad V'=-f_u/f_v.}
$$
Starting with large $u$ and $v=0$, $f>0$ drives a rapid move upward and left, loading the store until the trajectory approaches the right attracting branch. For large $\mu$ in the right stable regime, slow motion on that branch carries it toward $u=\mu$; the direction reverses appropriately if the starting value lies below that <equilibrium>. For sufficiently small $\mu$, slow motion travels left along the right branch until its fold, then a fast approximately constant-$w$ jump carries the trajectory to the left attracting branch. Subsequent slow motion reaches the small-$u$ <equilibrium>. At a fold the slow approximation is singular, so the full <equations> resolve the transition. At very small $u$ an additional loss of time-scale separation is possible because the rates themselves vanish; the fast/slow drawing describes the separated portions, not a uniform estimate arbitrarily near the origin.

For <Hopf thresholds in a two-pool calcium model>, with the unique <equilibrium> parametrized by $u=\mu$, locate changes of stability by solving the two <equations>
$$
\boxed{f(\mu,v)=0,\qquad f_v(\mu,v)-\gamma f_u(\mu,v)=\epsilon.}
$$
If there are two simple roots $\mu_-<\mu_+$ with positive <matrix trace>[trace] between them, the <equilibrium> is a <unstable equilibrium>[repeller] throughout that interval, since its <determinant> stays positive. At a nondegenerate crossing a <Hopf bifurcation> is possible, with linear <frequency> $[-f_v/\epsilon]^{1/2}$. A complete classification of criticality requires a nonlinear coefficient, not merely the <matrix trace>[trace] calculation. At leading singular order the thresholds approach the folds determined by $f=0$ and $f_v-\gamma f_u=0$, equivalently $V'=-1/\gamma$.

Periodic solutions are also supported globally in the positive quadrant. The axes point inward for positive input, $v'\le(\beta-\delta v)/\epsilon$ bounds $v$, and the $u$ <equation> then bounds $u$ by a linear restoring term plus a bounded source. A large rectangle can thus be chosen <invariant set>[forward invariant]. For an initial point distinct from the <unstable equilibrium>[repelling] unique <equilibrium>, its <omega-limit set> cannot contain that <equilibrium> or a boundary <equilibrium>. The <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> supplies a <periodic orbit>. In the strong separation regime it follows the two attracting branches and jumps between them, a <relaxation oscillation>.

The interval requires the <maximum> of $f_v-\gamma f_u$ along the <critical manifold> to exceed $\epsilon$; the listed scale hierarchy alone is not a substitute for that test for arbitrary remaining constants. As an explicit example, $\beta=0.1$, $\delta=10^{-5}$, $\epsilon=5\times10^{-4}$ and $\gamma=0.1$ give approximately $\mu_-=0.22618$ and $\mu_+=0.37350$. At $\mu=0.3$ the <matrix trace>[trace] is positive, whereas at $0.03$ and $1$ it is negative. The figures use this example and label their singular-limit portions accordingly.

= Solution

In a relaxation cycle, cytoplasmic <calcium> rises slowly on the left attracting branch while total <calcium> is accumulated, then rises rapidly during store discharge along a constant-$w$ <total-calcium fast fibres>[fast path] to the right branch. It subsequently decreases on the right slow branch as extrusion dominates input, and finally drops rapidly during store uptake back to the left branch. The result is **a repeated pulse with slow sections joined by sharp transitions**, rather than a sinusoid except near a small-amplitude <Hopf bifurcation>.

For explicit <time> labelling, the compatible scaling $u=c/K_1$, $v=c_s/K_2$ gives $\tau=kt$, $\gamma=K_2/K_1$, $\epsilon=kK_2/V_2$, $\mu=r/(kK_1)$, $\beta=V_1/V_2$ and $\delta=k_sK_2/V_2$. Hence an interval $\Delta\tau$ corresponds to physical duration $\Delta t=\Delta\tau/k$. Slow-branch <times> can be computed by integrating
$$
\Delta\tau=\int\frac{1+\gamma V'(u)}{\mu-u}\,du
$$
in the direction of motion; fast-jump durations are omitted at leading order. The plot uses that <quadrature>, with vertical lines representing the limiting rapid transitions. Finite $\epsilon$ rounds those lines and changes the precise <amplitude> and <period>, especially near folds.

In <delayed carbon-dioxide ventilation feedback>, $P$ is the effective <carbon dioxide>[carbon-dioxide] <partial pressure>[partial pressure] of the lumped body compartment. Its stored <carbon dioxide>[carbon-dioxide] amount is proportional to $P$, with effective capacitance $K$, so $K\,dP/dT$ is its accumulation rate. Metabolism produces <carbon dioxide> at rate $M$. <pulmonary ventilation>[Ventilation] removes it at a rate proportional to <partial pressure>[partial pressure] <times> <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation], $P\dot V$, with gas-conversion constants absorbed into the model coefficients. The <equation> is therefore a one-compartment <carbon dioxide>[carbon-dioxide] balance: **production minus respiratory removal equals accumulation**. It idealizes mixing and treats metabolic production as constant over the <time> scale of interest.

= Solution

Chemoreceptors respond to <carbon dioxide>[carbon-dioxide] levels, but feedback is delayed by blood transport to sensing sites, neural processing and the response of respiratory muscles. A fixed positive delay $\tau$ approximates their combined effect. The <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation] at the present instant therefore depends on the earlier <pressure>, $\dot V(t)=\mathcal V(P(t-\tau))$, not just on $P(t)$. This delayed <negative feedback> can overcorrect after the <pressure> has already changed, providing a mechanism for oscillatory breathing. The delay <equation> requires a <pressure> history over an interval of <length> $\tau$, rather than a single initial value.

The <positive part>[positive-part] law $\mathcal V(P)=G[P-P_0]_+$ is a threshold-linear approximation to the ventilatory response. Below the apnoeic threshold $P_0$, this model assigns zero <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation]; above it, each <pressure> increment produces a proportional <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation] increase, with gain $G>0$. Taking the <positive part> ensures a nonnegative <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation] rate rather than the unphysical negative rate that unrestricted linear extrapolation would give. It is a local idealization of the response, not a claim that actual physiology lacks other respiratory drives or saturates nowhere. In the delayed model this response is evaluated at the delayed <pressure>.

= Solution

Distinguish physical <time> $t$ from scaled <time> $T=t/\tau$. Put
$$
P=P_0+P_s p,\qquad P_s=\frac M{GP_0},\qquad \dot V=\frac M{P_0}v,\qquad v(T)=[p(T-1)]_+.
$$
Then $K(P_s/\tau)p'=M-[P_0+P_sp](M/P_0)v$, giving
$$
\boxed{p'=\alpha[1-(1+\mu p)v],\quad\alpha=\frac{\tau GP_0}{K},\quad\mu=\frac M{GP_0^2},\quad p_1=p(T-1).}
$$
A <steady state> must be positive, since otherwise <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation] vanishes while production remains positive. It solves $\mu p_*^2+p_*-1=0$, so
$$
\boxed{p_*=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4\mu}}=1-\mu+2\mu^2+O(\mu^3).}
$$
The value $1/(1+\mu)=1-\mu+\mu^2+O(\mu^3)$ printed in the question agrees only through first order. Thus it is a small-$\mu$ approximation with error of order $\mu^2$, not an exact <equilibrium> for nonzero $\mu$. The other quadratic root is negative and inadmissible for this steady <pulmonary ventilation>[ventilation] balance.

= ii
{parent=d}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since the <steady state> is strictly positive, the <positive part>[positive-part] <function> is differentiable in a neighbourhood of it. Write $p=p_*+q$. Retaining first-order terms gives
$$
q'(T)=-\alpha\mu p_*q(T)-\alpha(1+\mu p_*)q(T-1).
$$
Substitute the exponential mode $q=e^{\lambda T}$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-\beta-\gamma e^{-\lambda},\qquad\beta=\alpha\mu p_*,\quad\gamma=\alpha(1+\mu p_*)=\alpha/p_*.}
$$
Here the last equality uses the exact steady-state <equation>. For small $\mu$, $\beta=\alpha\mu+O(\alpha\mu^2)$ and $\gamma=\alpha(1+\mu)+O(\alpha\mu^2)$. Linearizing at the exact root avoids carrying a spurious constant residual from the printed approximate <equilibrium>.

{parent=d}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For real $\lambda\ge0$, draw $y=\lambda+\beta$ and $y=-\gamma e^{-\lambda}$. The first is nonnegative and increasing, while the second is negative and approaches zero from below. They cannot intersect on the nonnegative real axis. At zero the <characteristic> <function> equals $\beta+\gamma>0$, so a stationary zero-root crossing is excluded as well.

Consequently a loss of stability from a stable regime cannot occur by a real <eigenvalue> crossing through zero. It must involve a <complex conjugate>[conjugate pair] crossing the <imaginary axis> at nonzero <frequency>, yielding **oscillatory instability**. This does not mean that every <characteristic> root is nonreal: negative real roots may exist and are harmless for stability. The graph and imaginary-axis conditions are shown together below.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For the <first Hopf boundary of scalar delayed negative feedback>, first, if a root has nonnegative <real part> then $|\lambda+\beta|=\gamma e^{-\operatorname{Re}\lambda}\le\gamma$. For $\beta\ge\gamma$ this is impossible, apart from the potential equality case $\lambda=0$, already excluded. Thus $\beta<\gamma$ is necessary for instability, although not sufficient by itself.

At an imaginary root $\lambda=i\omega$ with $\omega>0$, separate real and imaginary parts:
$$
\beta+\gamma\cos\omega=0,\qquad\omega=\gamma\sin\omega.
$$
The first crossing uses $\omega_c\in(\pi/2,\pi)$ for $\beta>0$, and satisfies
$$
\boxed{\beta=-\omega_c\cot\omega_c,\qquad\gamma_c=\frac{\omega_c}{\sin\omega_c}=\sqrt{\beta^2+\omega_c^2},\qquad\mathcal T_c=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_c}.}
$$
The <period> is in scaled <time>; its physical value is $\tau\mathcal T_c$. Higher-frequency branches have larger critical gains. For fixed $\beta$, stability at zero delayed gain and absence of any earlier imaginary or zero-root crossing identify this lowest branch as the first stability boundary. Its crossing is transverse: implicit differentiation of $\lambda+\beta+\gamma e^{-\lambda}=0$ gives at the imaginary root
$$
\operatorname{Re}\frac{d\lambda}{d\gamma}=\frac{\beta(1+\beta)+\omega_c^2}{\gamma[(1+\beta)^2+\omega_c^2]}>0.
$$
For very small $\beta$, $\omega_c=\pi/2+2\beta/\pi+O(\beta^2)$, so $\mathcal T_c\to4$ and $\gamma_c=\pi/2+2\beta/\pi+O(\beta^2)$. Therefore **$\gamma_c=\pi/2$ is exact in the limit $\beta=0$ and is the leading approximation for small positive $\beta$**. At that limit $\lambda e^\lambda=-\gamma$ first has an imaginary pair at $\lambda=\pm i\pi/2$; subsequent pairs occur at $\pi/2+2n\pi$ and cannot be the first loss of stability. A nonlinear Hopf theorem, including its nondegeneracy conditions, is needed to turn the linear imaginary mode into a claim about a particular finite-amplitude periodic branch.
