== 1
{scope}

*Section A: Earthquakes & Faulting*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A **<normal fault>** is the plausible interpretation. The photograph shows a steep, exposed planar rock face with lower ground on its open side; this geometry is consistent with an exhumed <footwall> and a down-dropped <hanging wall>. A steep dip alone is not a unique indicator of <shear sense>, and the photograph does not supply a displaced marker proving the sense independently. Under an <Andersonian faulting regime>, a plausible maximum compression $\sigma_1$ is approximately vertical, with $\sigma_3$ horizontal in the extension direction and $\sigma_2$ approximately along strike.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A numerical answer needs an assumption about fault orientation. For a cohesionless fault optimally oriented for <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion>, with vertical $\sigma_1$, write $\varphi$ for the <internal friction angle> and $\delta$ for the <fault dip>. The angle between the plane and $\sigma_1$ is $90^\circ-\delta$, so the <optimal normal-fault dip relation> gives
$$
90^\circ-\delta=45^\circ-\frac\varphi2,\qquad
\varphi=2\delta-90^\circ=40^\circ.
$$
Hence **$\mu_f=\tan40^\circ=0.839\simeq0.84$** under that model.

For the <Mohr circle>, plot effective compression $\sigma_n'$ horizontally and <shear stress> $\tau$ vertically. Draw a circle with diameter $[\sigma_3',\sigma_1']$ tangent to $\tau=\mu_f\sigma_n'$. Its centre and radius obey $R/C=\sin\varphi$. One illustrative normalization is $\sigma_3'=1$, $\sigma_1'=4.59891$, with tangency at the resolved stresses on the $65^\circ$ plane. <Pore fluid pressure> shifts the total-stress circle horizontally; the friction law uses <effective stress>.

\Image[paper-1-fault-friction.png]
{title=Original normalized <Mohr circles> for optimally oriented normal faults: different dips imply different tangent slopes only under the stated failure model}

**The dip does not determine the actual sliding <coefficient of friction> without the optimal-orientation assumption.** A reactivated inherited plane may be nonoptimal, <cohesion> may matter, and the stress ratio and <pore fluid pressure> are not measured. For an arbitrary compression-positive stress state,
$$
\mu_f=\frac{(\sigma_1'-\sigma_3')\sin\delta\cos\delta}
{\sigma_1'\cos^2\delta+\sigma_3'\sin^2\delta}
$$
at cohesionless sliding. Thus the requested single value is a conditional inference, not a friction measurement.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The visible surface can be an **<exhumed fault surface>**, rather than an unrelated hillslope that happens to intersect a buried plane. Downward motion of the <hanging wall> exposes the <footwall> face; repeated displacement and removal of loose material can reveal a substantial length of the same <fault plane>. Where exposure is renewed faster than <weathering>, <erosion> and collapse erase it, that planar surface remains part of the hillside. The less weathered lower face and rougher, vegetated upper ground are consistent with differential exposure, though the photograph alone does not date individual events.

At greater distance or over longer times, <erosion> modifies the slope and the hillside will cross or obscure the <fault plane>. The correspondence is therefore a transient competition between <fault slip>, exhumation and surface processes, not a requirement that all hillslopes follow faults.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Assuming pure <dip-slip faulting>, use an east–west striking, $65^\circ$ north-dipping <normal fault>, with the northern <hanging wall> moving down dip. In a right-hand-rule strike convention this is **strike $270^\circ$, dip $65^\circ$, rake $-90^\circ$**. The auxiliary <nodal plane of a double couple>[nodal plane] strikes east–west and dips $25^\circ$ south. The <focal mechanism> has a predominantly vertical P axis and a predominantly north–south horizontal T axis; the steep central direction is dilatational.

\Image[paper-1-normal-focal-mechanism.png]
{title=Original lower-hemisphere normal-fault <focal mechanism>: black compression, white dilatation, and two alternative <nodal plane of a double couple>[nodal planes]}

The sketch is a double-couple <seismic moment tensor> calculation, not a claim that first motions uniquely identify which <nodal plane of a double couple>[nodal plane] slipped. The mapped north-dipping surface supplies that additional geological choice.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The along-strike rupture time is $L/v_r$, where $v_r$ is <rupture velocity>. Taking a representative crustal $v_r\simeq3\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ gives **about $2$ s** for a $6$ km unilateral <finite-source rupture>. A different assumed <rupture velocity> changes this estimate. The local slip rise time at one patch is not specified and need not equal the whole-fault rupture time.

For comparable distance and ground conditions, the eastern observer lies in the forward <rupture directivity> direction. Radiation from successive patches arrives closer together, producing a shorter, more concentrated and generally larger-amplitude ground-velocity pulse. The western observer receives the contributions spread over a longer interval, generally with less concentrated shaking. This concerns pulse duration and <amplitude>; it does not imply an equally large permanent displacement at the eastern site.<What is Directivity by United States Geological Survey>[Explanation of constructive interference and the relation of rupture speed to S-wave speed]{ref}

For distant observers approximately along strike, an elementary <seismic travel time> construction gives
$$
\Delta t_E\simeq L\left(\frac1{v_r}-\frac1\beta\right),\qquad
\Delta t_W\simeq L\left(\frac1{v_r}+\frac1\beta\right),
$$
where $\beta$ is the <shear-wave velocity>. With $v_r=3$ and $\beta=3.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, these propagation contributions are about $0.29$ and $3.71$ s, respectively; finite local rise time broadens them. <Double-couple radiation pattern>, receiver geometry and local site response can change the observed <amplitudes>, so location east or west alone is not an unconditional intensity prediction.

==== Solution

For an <Andersonian faulting regime> appropriate to the stated <normal fault>, take **$\sigma_1$ vertical, $\sigma_2$ north–south along strike, and $\sigma_3$ east–west**. Thus the minimum horizontal compression is normal to the fault’s north–south strike. In an east–up cross-section, draw $\sigma_1$ vertically and $\sigma_3$ horizontally, with $\sigma_2$ perpendicular to the page. These are plausible regional <principal stress> directions, rather than measured directions uniquely recoverable from one photograph.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the same conditional <optimal normal-fault dip relation> as in question 1, with $\delta=55^\circ$:
$$
\varphi=2(55^\circ)-90^\circ=20^\circ,\qquad
\boxed{\mu_f=\tan20^\circ=0.364.}
$$
The second panel of the original <Mohr circle> figure has $\sigma_3'=1$ and $\sigma_1'=2.03961$; its tangent from the origin has slope $0.364$. Resolving the stresses onto a $55^\circ$ plane places its stress point at that tangency. The diagram assumes negligible <cohesion>, a vertical maximum <principal stress> and an optimally oriented new or equivalently oriented sliding plane. Without those assumptions, a dip and a statement that sliding began do not uniquely determine the <coefficient of friction>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose coordinates $(x,y,z)=(\mathrm{east},\mathrm{north},\mathrm{up})$ and use compression-positive <effective stress>. One possible tensor, in MPa, is
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\sigma'=\begin{pmatrix}10&0&0\\0&15&0\\0&0&20.3961\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its ordered <principal stresses> are vertical $\sigma_1'$, north–south $\sigma_2'$ and east–west $\sigma_3'$. On the $55^\circ$ east-dipping plane,
$$
\sigma_n'=\sigma_1'\cos^255^\circ+\sigma_3'\sin^255^\circ,
\qquad
|\tau|=(\sigma_1'-\sigma_3')\sin55^\circ\cos55^\circ,
$$
so $|\tau|/\sigma_n'=0.36397$, as required by the conditional friction estimate. The scale is illustrative: no absolute <stress> magnitude is supplied. A corresponding total <stress tensor> is $\boldsymbol\sigma^c=\boldsymbol\sigma'+P_f\mathbf I$ for any specified <isotropic> <pore fluid pressure> $P_f$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The thin, pale, steep feature beside the fingers is plausibly a **mineral-filled opening fracture, a <mineral vein>**, rather than the whole coarse <fault breccia> band. <Calcite> is a plausible filling in this appearance, but its mineral identity would need a field or laboratory test. Opening normal to its approximately vertical north–south plane requires the smallest <principal stress> to be approximately east–west, consistent with the extension direction above.

For example, a compression-positive <effective stress> tensor
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma'=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&4&0\\0&0&9.3961\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{MPa}
$$
has east–west tension and can open a weak fracture whose <tensile strength> is at most $1$ MPa. More generally opening requires $\sigma_3-P_f<-T_0$, where $T_0$ is <tensile strength>. This illustrates <hydraulic opening of a mineral vein>: raising $P_f$ by $11$ MPa at the illustrative total stresses in part c translates all effective <principal stresses> by $-11$ MPa, producing this tensor without rotating its axes.

The vein and <fault slip> need not have formed simultaneously at those exact magnitudes: <pressure> transients, local stress changes and slip can alternate. Their orientations can nevertheless reflect the same normal-faulting stress regime. **The source’s reference to “part d” is circular; the meaningful comparison is with part c.**

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The coarse angular fragments in the light-coloured band identify a **<fault breccia>**, probably cemented and accompanied by finer comminuted material. Repeated brittle fracture, clast rotation and crushing during <fault slip> produce the fragments; fluid flow can later fill pores and fractures with mineral cement. If the finer material is cohesive it can be termed <cataclasite>; a loose, very fine seam is <fault gouge>. The photograph alone does not establish all those <cohesion> and grain-size distinctions.

Slip is commonly concentrated on a narrow principal slip surface or fine-grained seam at a margin or inside a broader damaged band. Thus the coarsest, widest breccia is not automatically the material that accommodated the largest shear displacement. In this image the sharp, finer-looking boundaries of the coarse band are the best candidates for localized slip. Identifying the uniquely most displaced seam would require displaced markers, thin sections or additional exposure; the coarse band itself records substantial distributed damage.

*Section B: Remote Sensing & Active Tectonics*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For regional coverage, use **<stereo photogrammetry> from overlapping satellite or airborne imagery**, or a radar-derived <digital elevation model> where suitable data exist. Match the same terrain features in images taken from different known viewing positions, infer their three-dimensional positions by triangulation, and georeference and grid the result. Satellite stereo or interferometric radar can cover $10^4\,\mathrm{km^2}$ efficiently; choose pixel size and vertical precision fine enough for the <fault scarps> of interest, rather than assuming every regional model resolves small scarps.

<InSAR> topographic mapping uses the phase difference from a known viewing baseline; repeat-pass deformation work must separate that height contribution from actual motion. Vegetation, cloud, radar shadow and steep slopes can leave gaps or represent the canopy rather than bare ground.<Interferometry by NASA>[Topographic mapping with separated viewing positions and the known baseline; DEM construction]{ref} Ground control and independent elevation checks are needed before interpreting small relief as active <faulting>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use **<airborne lidar> with sufficient pulse density and ground-return classification**. Laser travel times give ranges; the aircraft’s surveyed position and orientation place returns in three dimensions. Multiple returns include canopy and, where gaps permit, the ground. Classify ground points, remove vegetation returns and interpolate a bare-ground <digital elevation model> over the $20\,\mathrm{km^2}$ strip.

This specifically addresses the forest-obscured <fault scarp>, unlike ordinary optical stereo, which primarily models the visible canopy. Leaf-off acquisition can improve ground sampling where relevant. Dense understorey and a complete lack of ground returns still cause uncertainty; interpolation does not create observed ground heights. Inspect ground-point coverage and compare hillshades under several illumination directions.<What is the difference between lidar data and a digital elevation model by United States Geological Survey>[Point clouds, vegetation removal and the bare-earth product]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use **low-altitude <structure-from-motion photogrammetry>**, with strongly overlapping aerial images and well-distributed surveyed <ground control points>. Bundle adjustment estimates camera geometry and a dense surface reconstruction; independent check points test its accuracy. A small unmanned aircraft or carefully controlled close-range aerial platform can cover the $0.05\,\mathrm{km^2}$ site with centimetre ground sampling. Surveyed targets and stable camera calibration are essential: centimetre pixels alone do not establish centimetre vertical accuracy.

For exposed trench walls or especially demanding local geometry, terrestrial <lidar> is an alternative or complement. Remove vegetation where permissible, retain the trench’s sharp topographic breaks and state whether the output is a visible surface model or a bare-ground <digital elevation model>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

After removing orbital, topographic and atmospheric contributions, an earthquake <InSAR> <radar interferogram> would show elongated displacement lobes and generally closed or nested fringes associated with uplift and horizontal shortening of the <hanging wall>. Each full phase cycle represents $\lambda_r/2$ of <line-of-sight displacement>, where $\lambda_r$ is radar <wavelength>; equivalently $\Delta\phi=\pm4\pi d_{\mathrm{LOS}}/\lambda_r$, with sign fixed by processing convention.<Interferometry by NASA>[Phase differencing, nuisance contributions and half-wavelength displacement per fringe]{ref}

If primary slip reaches the surface, expect a displacement discontinuity across the surface trace: fringes can terminate or change abruptly there, and large near-fault gradients or disturbed ground may decorrelate. For a <blind reverse fault>, the elastic displacement field is continuous at the ground surface and spread over a broader zone, giving smoother gradients and fringes over the uplifted <fold>. A blind rupture can still create sharp local gradients or secondary faulting.

**The exact colour order, number of fringes and sign of the displacement lobes cannot be fixed from this shaded relief alone.** They depend on slip, depth and radar look direction; uplift is only one contribution to <line-of-sight displacement>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <blind reverse fault> can shorten and bend its cover into a <fault-propagation fold> or other growing <anticline>. At the <fold> crest, bending can create shallow extension and **secondary <normal faults> or <tension fissures>**, even though the regional deformation is shortening. Bedding-parallel <flexural slip>, local <backthrusts> and <fold scarps> can also break the surface.

Thus a shallow surface break need not be the upward continuation of the main seismogenic <thrust fault>. The expected association is distributed deformation related to the uplifted <fold>; its detailed pattern depends on cover layering, <fold> geometry and shallow strength. The broad frontal relief in Attachment A is compatible with this possibility, but the image alone does not establish a unique earthquake rupture history.

{scope}

*Section B: Remote Sensing & Active Tectonics*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Many optical land-mapping missions acquire daylight images in the **midmorning, roughly 10–11 local solar time**, using a <sun-synchronous orbit>. This supplies substantial solar illumination while often avoiding the stronger afternoon convective cloud development. It also gives useful relief shadows without the very long shadows and weak illumination of sunrise. Repeated local-time viewing reduces illumination changes when comparing scenes.<Catalog of Earth satellite orbits by NASA Earth Observatory>[Low Earth Orbit: repeated local solar time and the approximately 10:30 morning example]{ref}

The time is a mission design choice, not a law applying to every optical satellite; afternoon acquisitions also exist. Sun-synchrony approximately fixes local solar time, not the same civil clock time everywhere, and seasonal solar-angle changes remain.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A **<band ratio image>** emphasizes differences in <spectral reflectance> rather than overall brightness. If two corrected band signals are $L_1=k\rho_1$ and $L_2=k\rho_2$, with the same multiplicative illumination factor $k$, their ratio is $\rho_1/\rho_2$: common shading or exposure factors cancel. Materials with different spectral slopes or absorption features can therefore separate more clearly than in either band alone.

The cancellation is approximate. Additive atmospheric path radiance, different angular reflectance responses and different band noise do not cancel, and division by a small denominator amplifies errors. Use calibrated, co-registered bands and mask cloud, deep shadow and invalid pixels before interpreting a ratio as a material or vegetation contrast.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a **near-infrared/red ratio**, or its normalized form, the <normalized difference vegetation index>:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{NDVI}=\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}-\rho_{\mathrm{red}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{red}}}.}
$$
Useful bands are approximately $0.63$–$0.69\,\mu\mathrm m$ in red and $0.75$–$0.90\,\mu\mathrm m$ in near infrared, with exact ranges depending on the sensor. <Chlorophyll> absorbs strongly in red, whereas leaf structure scatters near infrared efficiently; healthy green vegetation therefore has a large near-infrared/red ratio and positive <NDVI>. Soil and water have different spectral contrasts.<Measuring vegetation by NASA Earth Observatory>[Normalized Difference Vegetation Index: visible absorption, near-infrared scattering and the normalized ratio]{ref}

A single value is not a direct census of vegetation biomass or species. Soil background, canopy saturation, season, atmospheric effects and mixed pixels need consideration when mapping the distribution of vegetation.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If a restored channel or fan boundary has accumulated displacement $d$ since it formed, a constant <fault slip rate> $v=1.6\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ gives the <geomorphic offset-age relation>
$$
\boxed{t=\frac d v=625\,d_{\mathrm{metres}}\ \mathrm{yr}.}
$$
The right-hand channel jog is of order one to two of the printed $100$ m scale bars, so apparent offsets of order $100$–$200$ m give **about $62.5$–$125$ ka**. These are scale-based order-of-magnitude estimates, not surveyed piercing-point offsets: the amount of deflection along an eroding channel is not automatically equal to the tectonic displacement. For example, a reliably restored $150$ m marker would imply $93.75$ ka.

These times lie in the late <Pleistocene>, when global glacial–interglacial changes could alter vegetation, <sediment> supply and runoff and thereby promote <alluvial fan> deposition or abandonment. The wide interval spans different climatic phases, so it does not identify one event or prove a climatic cause. More importantly, a channel cut into a fan usually dates channel establishment or abandonment; the fan <sediment> can be older. Independent <optically stimulated luminescence dating>, stratigraphic correlation and several matched offsets are needed to assign a depositional age and test a climatic correlation.

*Section C: Geophysical Methods*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A **<gravity anomaly>** is measured gravity minus a specified reference gravity after the stated reductions. A <Bouguer gravity anomaly> aims to remove the predictable effects of latitude, observation elevation and surrounding topographic mass, so residual variations can be related to subsurface <geophysical density contrasts>.

Measure relative gravity with a calibrated <gravimeter> at stations across and beyond the crater, and survey station positions and elevations accurately, preferably with <GNSS> tied to a reference datum. Repeat a base station through the day to estimate instrument drift and tie the traverse to a gravity datum. Record station times, because tidal and drift corrections are time dependent.

Apply instrument scale and drift corrections, solid-Earth and relevant ocean-loading tidal corrections, latitude/reference-ellipsoid gravity subtraction, the <free-air correction>, the <Bouguer slab> correction for material between station and datum, and a <terrain correction> for departures from a flat slab. The crater’s strong relief makes the last correction important. A local regional-field/background estimate is also needed if the target is the crater’s residual anomaly. State the reduction density; it is not the inferred breccia density.<Geophysical Data Grids for the Conterminous United States by United States Geological Survey>[Gravity-anomaly discussion: latitude, free-air, simple Bouguer and terrain reductions]{ref} For a fixed land traverse there is no moving-platform <Eötvös correction> to add.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat the quoted anomaly as the response of a slab of thickness $h$ replacing host rock. The <Bouguer slab> formula applies to the <geophysical density contrast>, not the absolute density:
$$
\Delta g=2\pi G(\rho_b-\rho_h)h.
$$
With $\Delta g=-2.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $h=400$ m and $\rho_h=2750\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$,
$$
\rho_b=2750+\frac{-2.5\times10^{-5}}{2\pi(6.67430\times10^{-11})(400)}
=2750-149.04.
$$
Thus **$\rho_b\simeq2601\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$** in the slab approximation. The negative anomaly requires less dense infill. A finite bowl has a different geometric response, and water, post-impact <sediment> and the regional baseline can matter; the stated calculation is the requested slab estimate. The plotted minimum appears closer to $-2$ mGal, but the numerical instruction explicitly supplies $-2.5$ mGal and controls this calculation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Collect a **<magnetic survey>** along the traverse with a calibrated total-field magnetometer, recording time, accurately surveyed position and sensor height. Extend observations beyond the crater to establish the background, repeat stations or tie lines to test consistency, and use a stationary <base-station magnetometer> to monitor temporal field changes. Keep the instrument away from vehicles, fences and magnetic equipment; map unavoidable cultural sources rather than treating them as geological signals.

Correct instrument offsets and heading effects where applicable, remove measured diurnal variation, reject or separately treat magnetic-storm intervals, and subtract the appropriate large-scale <geomagnetic field> at the survey epoch. Level overlapping traverses and state whether a local regional trend has also been removed. Unlike gravity, there is no universal rock-independent Bouguer correction for magnetic topography: a <magnetic topographic effect> needs a model of terrain and <magnetization>.

Measure representative <magnetic susceptibility> and <remanent magnetization> on oriented rock samples to interpret the profile. Total field alone does not tell whether a contrast comes from induced or <remanent magnetization>, crater excavation, or strongly magnetic material in the infill.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

At low northern latitude, a simple induced <total-field magnetic anomaly> is generally **asymmetric and bipolar**, not a symmetric central positive peak. A compact body more magnetic than its surroundings, magnetized parallel to a northward, downward ambient field, gives its principal positive lobe on the southern side and a negative lobe toward the north. At exactly zero <magnetic inclination> its central anomaly reverses relative to the pole; latitude and survey orientation must therefore be included.<Magnetic line profiles by UBC Geophysical Inversion Facility>[Line-profile examples 1–5: latitude, profile orientation and remanence effects]{ref}

For an original explicit sketch, put a dipole at depth $d$ and let $x$ increase northward. In a north–south vertical section with inducing inclination $I$, an <induced magnetization> parallel to the ambient field gives
$$
\Delta B(x)\ \propto\ \Delta M\,
\frac{3(x\cos I-d\sin I)^2-(x^2+d^2)}{(x^2+d^2)^{5/2}}.
$$
A geocentric-dipole approximation at latitude $20^\circ$ gives $I\simeq\arctan(2\tan20^\circ)=36.1^\circ$, only an illustrative field direction, not a measured local value.

\Image[paper-1-magnetic-contrast.png]
{title=Original induced-dipole profiles: a positive magnetic body and an excavated or weakly magnetized replacement give opposite signs at the same field inclination}

For the actual crater, breccia, water and <sediment> may replace more magnetic <basalt>, giving **negative <magnetization contrast>**. In that case reverse the simple positive-body sketch: a dominant southern low and a northern high are possible even with purely induced contrasts. The magnetic sign cannot be inferred from density or from the word “crater” alone.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The supplied south–north profile has a large southern/interior negative trough and a sharp positive peak toward the northern rim. That is opposite to the simplest positive, presently induced magnetic body in question 5(d). It therefore does **not** justify interpreting the crater as a concentration of strongly magnetic meteorite material.

A negative <magnetization contrast> from replacing magnetic <basalt> already reverses the simple induced-body response. In addition, the target <Deccan Traps> can retain strong <remanent magnetization> whose direction differs from the present <geomagnetic field>; crater excavation and rim geometry then produce a strong <magnetic topographic effect>. Rock-magnetic measurements and forward modelling distinguish these contributions. A primary study of this crater attributes most of its anomaly to magnetized target <basalts> and topography, with a comparatively small contribution from the breccia and post-impact <sediment>.<Magnetic Anomaly and Model of the Lonar Meteorite Impact Crater in Maharashtra, India by Kalle Kiik and others>[Sections 4.2, 5 and 6: target-rock remanence, terrain modelling and weak infill contribution]{ref}

**The curve alone is not a unique diagnosis of reversed polarity, impact-induced <magnetization> or a buried iron mass.** The physical conclusion supported by the comparison is that a positive induced-body model is inadequate; inherited target-rock <magnetization>, its removal and geometry must be modelled.

*Section C: Geophysical Methods*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an approximately <isotropic> elastic solid,
$$
V_P=\sqrt{\frac{K+4\mu/3}{\rho}},\qquad V_S=\sqrt{\frac\mu\rho},
$$
where $K$ is <bulk modulus>, $\mu$ is <shear modulus> and $\rho$ is <mass density>. Five relevant controls are:

* **Mineral composition and texture:** different elastic moduli and densities give different <seismic velocities>; compliant clay-rich material generally differs from crystalline igneous framework rock.
* **<Porosity>, cracks and fractures:** compliant voids reduce the effective moduli and usually reduce both speeds; aligned fractures produce <seismic anisotropy>.
* **Effective confinement:** increasing <effective stress> closes compliant cracks and tends to raise speeds. Higher <pore fluid pressure> at fixed total confinement can reopen or preserve cracks and lower the effective stiffness.
* **Fluid saturation and phase:** replacing gas by liquid commonly increases the saturated <bulk modulus> and $V_P$; fluids have no static shear rigidity, so $V_S$ changes through the frame, density and frequency-dependent interactions rather than a direct fluid <shear modulus>.
* **<Temperature> and alteration:** heating commonly reduces elastic moduli and may create thermal cracks; <hydrothermal alteration> can replace strong minerals by clay or other weaker phases. The sign depends on alteration products and crack sealing, so reservoir-specific measurements are needed.

These controls interact: a dense rock is not automatically faster if its moduli are also different.

===== Solution

An **<explosive seismic source>** gives an impulsive, potentially high-energy broad-band signal and can couple effectively to competent material. It can be useful where strong penetration is needed and heavy vibrating vehicles cannot reach the site. Disadvantages include destructive ground disturbance, permitting and specialist handling, variable source coupling and signature, and the cost of repeated source preparation. The comparison does not require a prescription for charge size or emplacement.

**<Vibroseis>** spreads controlled energy over a longer <frequency> sweep. It is repeatable, permits tailored bandwidth and repeated sweeps, and its known signal supports correlation processing and rejection of unrelated noise. It avoids the impulsive ground disruption of an explosive source. Disadvantages include vehicle access and terrain restrictions, dependence on baseplate coupling, limited force and low-frequency performance, possible harmonic distortion, and strong near-surface waves. For a fractured shallow geothermal site, test which source produces usable coherent reflections or arrivals in the desired band; nominal source energy alone does not establish data quality.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At each source position, couple the vibrator to the ground, transmit a known <frequency> sweep $s(t)$ and record the ground response on synchronized <geophones>. Record a pilot or measured source-force reference and sufficient listening time for delayed arrivals. Repeat sweeps where beneficial and retain geometry and timing information.

If the Earth’s impulse response is $h(t)$, a linear model is $d=s*h+n$. <Vibroseis correlation> cross-correlates the record with the known sweep:
$$
c(\tau)=\int d(t)s(t-\tau)\,dt=(h*R_{ss})(\tau)+R_{ns}(\tau).
$$
The sweep’s <autocorrelation> $R_{ss}$ is a compact, approximately zero-phase wavelet, so the long chirp is compressed into identifiable delayed events. In the <frequency> domain this multiplies $D(\omega)$ by $\overline{S(\omega)}$, yielding $H(\omega)|S(\omega)|^2$ plus noise. It is **pulse compression, not an exact recovery of $h$ at <frequencies> never transmitted**. Spectral shaping or stabilized deconvolution can further compensate the finite source bandwidth and sidelobes.<Geophysical Survey Summary Report, Section 3: Geophysical Testing Methodology>[Vibratory sources: known input sweep correlated with geophone records]{ref}

The <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> gives a **Nyquist rate of $200$ samples per second** for a $100$ Hz upper <frequency>. ===== Solution

===== Solution

d(15)&=15.5+(8.4-15.5)\frac{15-11.6}{15.3-11.6}=8.97568.
\end{aligned}
$$
The estimated displacements are **$7.00$, $12.99$ and $8.98\,\mu\mathrm m$**. Interpolation assumes approximately linear behavior within each interval; it does not recover any unobserved high-frequency motion.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Regular timing supplies a common interval and a well-defined <frequency> grid for a standard <discrete Fourier transform> and <fast Fourier transform>. It also makes sample-index lags correspond to fixed physical delays in <cross-correlation>, <autocorrelation> and digital filtering. These methods estimate <frequency> content and characteristic periods, separate instrument or environmental noise from signals, identify arrivals and time delays, and compare ground-motion spectra.

A uniform interval establishes a <Nyquist frequency>; <frequencies> above it alias unless filtered before acquisition. Interpolating irregular data onto a regular grid does not undo prior <aliasing> or fill large gaps with new measurements, and it can alter spectra and create correlated errors. Methods that directly use irregular times are possible, but the ordinary FFT and fixed-lag filter formulas cannot simply treat the original irregular observations as evenly spaced.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Successive jumps occur at times separated by **$T=6$ s**. Between $-3$ and $3$ s the plotted function is $f(t)=2-t$, periodically extended, with maximum $5$ and minimum $-1$. Its mean is therefore **$2$ m**, and $f(t)-2=-t$ is an <odd function> over that centered period.

For the <Fourier series> convention
$$
f(t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}
\left[a_r\cos\frac{2\pi rt}{T}+b_r\sin\frac{2\pi rt}{T}\right],
$$
**$a_0=4$ m and $a_r=0$ for every $r\ge1$**; the nonconstant content consists of <sine> terms. If $a_0$ denotes the mean directly in the alternative convention $f=a_0+\sum\cdots$, its value is $2$ m. Stating the convention resolves the otherwise ambiguous notation.

For completeness, integrating $-t$ against the <sine> basis gives **$b_r=6(-1)^r/(\pi r)$ m**. At a jump the <Fourier series> converges to the midpoint $2$ m. The nonzero mean does not destroy the oddness argument for the mean-subtracted function; declaring the original function itself odd would incorrectly force its mean to vanish.

== 11
{scope}

*Section F: Evolution*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Extinction selectivity> is systematic variation in the <probability> or rate of <extinction> among lineages with different traits**, rather than trait-independent loss. Traits may be biological, ecological or geographical: for example, survival may differ between narrow-ranging and widespread taxa within the same <extinction> interval.

A test needs an explicit denominator of taxa at risk, a defined taxonomic level and time window, and a specified trait measurement. Regional disappearance is not automatically global <extinction>. Nor is an association necessarily a causal mechanism: traits covary with habitat, abundance and preservation, and their effects can change between background intervals and <mass extinctions>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Four documented examples, each tied to its actual context, are:

* **Geographic range:** widespread marine <bivalve> genera survived the end-<Cretaceous> event better than geographically restricted genera. Spatial spread can reduce exposure to some geographically heterogeneous stresses, although this does not guarantee survival of a global perturbation.<Selectivity of end-Cretaceous marine bivalve extinctions by David Jablonski and others>[Abstract: lower extinction intensity in geographically widespread genera]{ref}
* **<Body size>:** large-bodied <mammals> were disproportionately lost during the late <Quaternary>. The global analysis documents size selectivity across continents and trophic groups; this is not evidence that large <body size> always raises <extinction> risk in every geological interval.<Body size downgrading of mammals over the late Quaternary by Felisa Smith and others>[Abstract: size-selective losses over the studied late-Quaternary intervals]{ref}
* **Physiological buffering and respiratory capacity:** poor capacity to tolerate elevated <carbon dioxide> and associated <oxygen> stress has been linked to higher vulnerability among end-<Permian> marine groups. This is a paleophysiological interpretation informed by living relatives and <fossil> traits, rather than a direct measurement of a <fossil> animal’s tolerance.<Paleophysiology and end-Permian mass extinction by Andrew H. Knoll and others>[Abstract and section Extinction in the oceans: hypercapnia vulnerability and physiological selectivity]{ref}
* **<Sexual dimorphism> as a proxy for male reproductive investment:** greater male–female carapace differences in <fossil> cytheroid <ostracods> were associated with higher estimated <extinction> rates. The measured <fossil> trait is dimorphism; interpreting it as investment requires the biological correspondence observed in living relatives.<High male sexual investment as a driver of extinction in fossil ostracods by Maria João Fernandes Martins and others>[Abstract: dimorphic fossil carapaces and association with estimated extinction rates]{ref}

These examples demonstrate event- and group-dependent selectivity. They do not define one universal vulnerability ranking: the <bivalve> study, for instance, did not find body-size selectivity at the same end-Cretaceous genus-level event.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

One fossil-record study is **<Selectivity of end-Cretaceous marine bivalve extinctions by David Jablonski and others>**, which compared survival of marine <bivalve> genera across the end-<Cretaceous> <mass extinction> against their pre-event attributes. Geographically widespread genera had lower <extinction> intensity than narrowly distributed ones. In the same analysis, <body size>, burrowing versus exposed life position, and shelf bathymetric position were not significant selectors; the apparent feeding-mode contrast was concentrated in particular groups rather than establishing a universal feeding rule.<Selectivity of end-Cretaceous marine bivalve extinctions by David Jablonski and others>[Science 268, pp. 389–391, abstract: tested traits, range result and qualifications to the feeding-mode result]{ref}

The logic of the test is to define the pre-event genera at risk, classify their geographic ranges using pre-event occurrences, then compare the fractions becoming extinct. Using pre-event rather than surviving ranges avoids measuring the predictor after the outcome. A two-by-two <contingency table> gives an elementary broad/narrow-range comparison; a <logistic regression> can instead retain a continuous range measure and include correlated covariates. These are explanations of the inferential design, not a claim that this paper used that exact regression specification.

A persuasive interpretation must address unequal rock exposure, collecting intensity, taxonomic revision, incomplete occurrence records and the difference between local loss and global <extinction>. The important result is selective survival in a real <fossil> dataset and the failure of several plausible alternative predictors in that setting. It is observational evidence: geographic range can correlate with unmeasured ecological properties, so the association alone does not prove a unique kill mechanism.

== 12
{scope}

*Section F: Evolution*.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In **<allopatric speciation>**, geographically separated <biological populations> experience sufficiently reduced <gene flow> for divergence to accumulate through <natural selection>, <genetic drift> and other evolutionary processes. A barrier can split a former <biological population>, or a colonizing <biological population> can become isolated. The eventual species distinction requires persistent <reproductive isolation>, not merely physical separation at one instant.

In **<sympatric speciation>**, reproductive divergence originates without the initial geographic separation required by the allopatric model, at the spatial scale relevant to mating and dispersal. Divergent ecological selection and assortative mating, or some forms of <polyploidy>, can reduce gene exchange despite geographic overlap. Present-day <sympatry> alone does not establish this history: previously isolated species can meet through secondary contact. The difference concerns the geography of the origin of isolation, not whether the resulting species currently occupy different places.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Examples of **<prezygotic reproductive isolation>**, all acting before a zygote forms, include:

* **Habitat isolation:** potential mates occupy different habitats or hosts and seldom encounter each other at mating sites.
* **Temporal isolation:** breeding seasons, flowering times or daily mating periods differ, reducing opportunities for mating or pollen transfer.
* **Behavioral isolation:** courtship signals, calls or chemical mating signals elicit responses mainly from the same species, preventing cross-group mating.
* **Mechanical isolation:** incompatible mating structures or flower–pollinator fit prevent successful transfer of gametes.
* **Gametic incompatibility:** gametes meet but cannot recognize, fuse or complete <fertilization> successfully.

The first four primarily reduce mating or gamete transfer; the last can occur after mating while still preceding <fertilization>. These differ from <postzygotic isolation>, such as hybrid inviability or hybrid sterility, which acts after the zygote has formed. Distinguishing these stages prevents “premating” from being used as a synonym for every prezygotic barrier.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Allopatric speciation> is the predominant classical model and is generally regarded as especially important in animals.** Geographic separation directly reduces <gene flow>, making it easier for divergent adaptation and incompatibilities to accumulate. The exact <frequency> across all organisms and historical events is not directly observable; a universal numerical fraction is not established by this statement.

Evidence combines the geographic arrangement of close relatives, <phylogenetic trees>, genetic divergence and independently reconstructed barrier histories. Sister lineages on opposite sides of barriers, island radiations founded by colonization, and differentiated <biological populations> that remain reproductively isolated after contact are mutually reinforcing observations. They are stronger when separation is independently dated and the origin of reproductive barriers is distinguished from later range changes.

A concrete primary example is the comparison of 15 trans-isthmian snapping-shrimp pairs using allozymes and mitochondrial COI. Concordant genetic differences support separation of lineages by the developing Isthmus of Panama, but divergence varied substantially between pairs. This cautions against assigning every pair to one simultaneous final closure event.<New dates and new rates for divergence across the Isthmus of Panama by Nancy Knowlton and others>[Abstract: 15 Alpheus pairs, concordant markers, variable divergence and different cessation times of gene flow]{ref}

The inference is not exclusive. The island-palm study of <Howea forsteriana> and <Howea belmoreana> used sister relationships, island history, soil association and flowering-time differences to support a sympatric origin.<Sympatric speciation in palms on an oceanic island by Vincent Savolainen and others>[Abstract: endemic sister taxa, dated island history and flowering-time disjunction correlated with soil preference]{ref} Such cases show that <sympatric speciation> is possible. Conversely, sister-species allopatry by itself cannot prove historical allopatry, because <extinction> and range shifts can change their present distributions. The case for the common mode rests on combined historical evidence and the widespread effect of barriers on <gene flow>.
