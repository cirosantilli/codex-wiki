=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $S_g$ be the fraction of the <pore space> occupied by <gas>, and let $\phi$ be the <porosity>. At fixed <porosity> and unchanged <rock> framework, the bulk <density> is
$$
\rho=(1-\phi)\rho_s+\phi\bigl[(1-S_g)\rho_w+S_g\rho_g\bigr].
$$
Replacing <water> with much less dense <gas> therefore decreases the bulk <density> approximately linearly, as in Figure 1. The liquid and gas <densities> here are those at reservoir conditions.

A <pore fluid> has negligible static <shear modulus>, so the <rock> framework chiefly controls the effective <shear modulus> $\mu$. If $\mu$ changes little, $V_s=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}$ increases gently as the <density> falls. This does **not** imply that <S-waves> travel through the pore <gas> itself: their restoring force comes from the connected solid framework.

The <P-wave> speed depends on both <bulk modulus> and <shear modulus>:
$$
V_p=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa+4\mu/3}{\rho}}.
$$
<Gas> is much more compressible than <water>. Even a small connected gas fraction can strongly reduce the effective fluid <bulk modulus>, approximately through the compliance average $\kappa_f^{-1}\simeq(1-S_g)/\kappa_w+S_g/\kappa_g$ for a pressure-equilibrated mixture. The saturated <rock>'s effective <bulk modulus> consequently falls rapidly at first, overwhelming the smaller <density> reduction and causing the sharp initial fall in $V_p$. Once the fluid is already very compliant, further gas replacement changes the stiffness less; the continuing <density> decrease can then make $V_p$ rise again. Figure 1 assumes a particular saturation response: patchy fluids, changing effective <pressure>, and frequency-dependent fluid motion can alter it. The plotted $V_s$ is only nearly constant, so the plot should not be interpreted as an exact fixed-$\mu$ constitutive model.

=== b
{scope}

For incidence from cap rock (1) into reservoir (2), use the conventional normal-incidence stress-amplitude <reflection coefficient>
$$
R_j=\frac{Z_{j2}-Z_{j1}}{Z_{j2}+Z_{j1}},\qquad Z_{pj}=\rho_jV_{pj},\quad Z_{sj}=\rho_jV_{sj}.
$$
Here <acoustic impedance> is the <P-wave> impedance; $\rho V_s$ is the corresponding <shear-wave impedance>. Positive $R$ preserves the chosen stress-wave <seismic reflection polarity>; negative $R$ reverses it. These are amplitude coefficients, not reflected <energy> fractions. At normal incidence in the ideal isotropic model there is no P-to-S <mode conversion>. The cap-rock impedances are
$$
Z_{p1}=2.25\times10^6,\qquad Z_{s1}=0.900\times10^6\quad\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}.
$$

The following are **approximate readings of the supplied Figure 1 PNG**, checked against the matching source PDF, rather than exact tabulated parameters. At zero gas, use $\rho\simeq1.75\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $V_p\simeq1.75\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and $V_s\simeq0.60\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$; at full gas use $\rho\simeq1.25\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $V_p\simeq1.15\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and $V_s\simeq0.65\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The left-hand $V_p$ endpoint is crowded against the axis and the <density> curve; readings differing by roughly $0.05$ in the plotted units are reasonable. The resulting coefficients deserve about two significant figures, not the apparent precision of the intermediate arithmetic.

Both end members change <seismic reflection polarity>, but the <P-wave> change is much larger: approximately $+0.15$ to $-0.22$, compared with $+0.08$ to $-0.05$ for <S-waves>. Thus a calibrated <P-wave> survey is better for distinguishing these two saturation end members and should give a stronger reflection from the fully gas-filled reservoir. A negative <P-wave> reflection relative to the water-filled prediction is a useful gas indicator in this stipulated model. In a real <seismic reflection survey>, lithology, <porosity>, effective <pressure>, and <seismic wavelet> phase also affect the response, so a bright or reversed reflection alone does not prove the presence of <gas>.

===== Solution

For the fully water-filled reservoir, the approximate <densities> and <wave speeds> give
$$
Z_{p2}\simeq3.06\times10^6,\quad Z_{s2}\simeq1.05\times10^6\quad\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}},
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{R_p\simeq\frac{3.0625-2.25}{3.0625+2.25}\simeq+0.15,\qquad R_s\simeq\frac{1.05-0.900}{1.05+0.900}\simeq+0.08.}
$$
Both are positive: the reservoir has larger <P-wave> and <shear-wave impedances> than the cap rock, and neither reflection reverses the chosen stress-wave <seismic reflection polarity>.

{scope}

===== Solution

For the fully gas-filled reservoir,
$$
Z_{p2}\simeq1.44\times10^6,\quad Z_{s2}\simeq0.81\times10^6\quad\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{R_p\simeq\frac{1.4375-2.25}{1.4375+2.25}\simeq-0.22,\qquad R_s\simeq\frac{0.8125-0.900}{0.8125+0.900}\simeq-0.05.}
$$
Both impedances are now smaller than those of the cap rock. Both reflections reverse <seismic reflection polarity>, with a substantially larger amplitude for the <P-wave> reflection. Reading the right-hand $V_p$ endpoint instead as $1.20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ would give $R_p\simeq-0.20$, preserving the interpretation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**The <P-wave> source is normally the more useful depth source for the end members and for the stated approximately 50% gas case**, because the predicted $|R_p|$ exceeds $|R_s|$, making the reservoir-top arrival easier to identify, assuming comparable source and recording quality. For a horizontal reflector and vertical two-way <travel time> $t$ entirely through the homogeneous cap rock,
$$
h=\frac{V_{p1}t_p}{2}=750t_p\ \mathrm m,
$$
or $h=V_{s1}t_s/2=300t_s\ \mathrm m$ for an identified <S-wave> arrival, with times in seconds. The reservoir's own <wave speed> is not the overburden <wave speed> used in this conversion. Nonzero-offset data require <moveout correction> and an appropriate overburden <velocity model>; no numerical depth can be obtained without a measured time.

At approximately 50% gas, Figure 1 gives $\rho\simeq1.5\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $V_p\simeq1.05\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, and $V_s\simeq0.62$–$0.63\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Thus
$$
Z_{p2}\simeq1.58\times10^6,\quad R_p\simeq-0.18,\qquad Z_{s2}\simeq(0.93\text{–}0.945)\times10^6,\quad R_s\simeq+0.02.
$$
The <S-wave> reflector is then weak because its impedance is nearly matched to the cap rock. An exact <impedance match>, $\rho_2V_{s2}=\rho_1V_{s1}$, gives $R_s=0$: a reservoir top can exist without producing an observable normal-incidence <S-wave> reflection. Rounding the 50% $V_s$ to $0.60$ would suggest exactly that cancellation, but the plotted line sits slightly higher. The continuous $Z_s$ curve crosses the cap-rock value at a somewhat greater saturation; the coarse figure does not justify a precise percentage.

There is also a smaller-saturation <P-wave> impedance match: $R_p$ changes sign between zero and modest gas saturation, so it must pass through zero. Thus neither source guarantees a detectable reflector at every saturation. Joint <P-wave> and <S-wave> observations help distinguish a weak impedance contrast from an absent reservoir; a slower <S-wave> speed alone is no reason to prefer it when its reflected amplitude is near zero.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <proton-precession magnetometer> measures the scalar magnitude $F=|\mathbf B|$ of the local <magnetic field>. Its sensor contains a proton-rich liquid, such as a hydrocarbon or suitable water-based fluid. A strong current through a coil briefly polarizes the nuclear <magnetic moments>, producing a small net <magnetization>. The polarizing field is directed sufficiently across the local <magnetic field> to leave a transverse component when the current is switched off.

The <protons> then undergo <Larmor precession> about the ambient <magnetic field>. Their rotating net <magnetization> induces a decaying alternating <voltage> in a receiving coil. Measuring the <frequency> determines the field:
$$
\omega=\gamma_pF,\qquad f=\frac{\gamma_p}{2\pi}F,\qquad \frac{\gamma_p}{2\pi}\simeq42.58\ \mathrm{MHz\,T^{-1}}.
$$
For example, $50\ \mathrm{\mu T}$ gives about $2.13\ \mathrm{kHz}$. The known proton <gyromagnetic ratio> provides an absolute frequency-to-field scale; this instrument does not measure the field's direction. Strong spatial <gradients> cause different parts of the liquid to precess at different rates and shorten the usable signal, which matters very close to a buried steel object.<G-857 Memory-Mag operation manual by Geometrics>[Introduction, “What is a total field magnetometer?”, and “Magnetic Environment”; scalar intensity and sensitivity to gradients.]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Lay out a surveyed grid extending beyond the possible burial area into unaffected ground. Use closely spaced parallel traverses with measurements along each line; spacing should resolve a car-scale <magnetic anomaly>, with a finer grid over any candidate. For a shallow car, a pilot survey with roughly metre-scale spacing is a sensible starting point, refined according to anomaly width rather than treated as a universal prescription. Record position, time, sensor height, repeat readings and any nearby interference. Cross-lines and repeat stations check reproducibility. Maintain a constant sensor height on a nonmagnetic staff and remove magnetic items from the operator; map fences, gates, pipes, buildings and vehicles before interpreting an anomaly.

Operate a synchronized stationary <base-station magnetometer> in quiet ground while the rover surveys, or make frequent base returns if a second instrument is unavailable. A <total-field magnetic anomaly> is the corrected field magnitude minus a specified background:
$$
\Delta T(\mathbf x)=F_{\mathrm{corrected}}(\mathbf x)-F_{\mathrm{background}}(\mathbf x).
$$
For a field-sized target, a local background estimated away from the car is often sufficient. A larger regional survey may subtract the <International Geomagnetic Reference Field> and an independently justified regional trend. Do not remove the car's broad anomaly by overfitting that trend.

Correct rover observations for time-varying <geomagnetic field> using the base record, for example $F_c(\mathbf x,t)=F_r(\mathbf x,t)-[F_b(t)-F_b(t_0)]$. Check instrument offsets and tie-line differences, reject disturbed or poor-quality readings, and allow for differing sensor heights. Preserve localized target signals when interpolating a contour map or comparing with a <magnetic dipole> model. There are no compulsory gravity-style free-air or <Bouguer gravity anomaly> reductions for this magnetic search.

Postpone surveying during a <geomagnetic storm> or rapid irregular magnetic variation, when base correction may be inadequate. Avoid periods of moving farm machinery or electrical interference, and recognize that strong permanent cultural interference or highly magnetic host <rock> may make this particular method unsuitable. Ordinary cloud cover or darkness does not prevent a <magnetic survey>.<G-857 Memory-Mag operation manual by Geometrics>[“Magnetic Environment” and survey/base-station operation: magnetic cleanliness, spatial gradients, and time variation.]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Strong geological <magnetic anomalies> generally occur in <rocks> containing abundant strongly magnetic iron oxides, especially <magnetite> and <titanomagnetite>. Many <mafic rocks>, including <basalt> and <gabbro>, and some <ultramafic rocks> have large signatures; magnetite-rich iron formations can be exceptionally strong, while <serpentinization> can create additional <magnetite>. Actual <magnetic susceptibility> and <remanent magnetization>, rather than the rock name alone, determine the response. Sedimentary and felsic <rocks> are often weaker but have important exceptions.

<Magnetite>, <titanomagnetite> and <maghemite> exhibit strong <ferrimagnetism>; some <pyrrhotite> is also strongly magnetic. <Hematite> is generally much weaker in induced <magnetic susceptibility>, although it can carry stable <remanent magnetization>. <Quartz> and <calcite> have very small, usually negative, susceptibilities, and many ordinary silicates have only weak <paramagnetism>. A steel car exhibits strong <ferromagnetism> and can therefore stand out against a weakly magnetic host, but its shape and pre-existing <remanent magnetization> complicate the signal.<Applications manual for portable magnetometers by Geometrics>[Rock-susceptibility discussion and the section “Remanent or Permanent Magnetization”.]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

The sketches assume the car can be approximated by a compact <magnetic dipole> whose **induced** <magnetic moment> is parallel to the local <geomagnetic field>. The host contributes negligible local contrast. Real steel also has <remanent magnetization> and shape-dependent magnetization, so the car's actual profile need not follow this idealization. A <proton-precession magnetometer> observes
$$
\Delta T=|\mathbf B_0+\mathbf b|-|\mathbf B_0|\simeq\widehat{\mathbf B}_0\cdot\mathbf b
$$
when $|\mathbf b|\ll|\mathbf B_0|$. This scalar <total-field magnetic anomaly> must not be confused with a fixed vertical field component. For induced $\mathbf m=m\widehat{\mathbf B}_0$, the <magnetic dipole> field gives
$$
\Delta T\simeq\frac{\mu_0m}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\widehat{\mathbf B}_0\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})^2-1\right].
$$
Reversing both the inducing field and induced moment leaves this <total-field magnetic anomaly> unchanged, exactly for the magnitude definition as well as in the weak-anomaly approximation.<Magnetic surveying: induced magnetization by UBC Geophysical Inversion Facility>[Buried-dipole examples at the magnetic equator and magnetic pole; total-field projection and traverse orientation.]{ref}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Near the **north magnetic pole**, take the inducing <magnetic field> and induced <magnetic moment> as vertically downward. Directly above the car, its anomalous field reinforces the main field: a positive central <total-field magnetic anomaly> results. Along a horizontal traverse, the vertical-dipole approximation gives a symmetric central high flanked by weaker negative side lobes:
$$
\Delta T(x)\propto\frac{2h^2-x^2}{(x^2+h^2)^{5/2}},
$$
where $h$ is the depth from the sensor to the effective dipole centre. The anomaly width scales with $h$; no absolute amplitude can be calculated without the car's <magnetic moment>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At the **magnetic equator**, the inducing <magnetic field> and induced <magnetic moment> are horizontal. Let $x$ point along this field, approximately magnetic north, and $y$ across it. Directly over the car, the dipole field opposes the main field, producing a negative central <total-field magnetic anomaly>. A north–south traverse through the car gives
$$
\Delta T(x)\propto\frac{2x^2-h^2}{(x^2+h^2)^{5/2}},
$$
a symmetric central low with positive shoulders. An east–west traverse through the car instead has $\Delta T(y)\propto-(y^2+h^2)^{-3/2}$, a single negative trough. The equatorial anomaly therefore needs a stated traverse orientation; there is no universal single equatorial line profile.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Near the **south magnetic pole**, the inducing <magnetic field> and induced <magnetic moment> are vertically upward. Directly over the car its dipole field is also upward, again reinforcing the main field. The <total-field magnetic anomaly> is therefore the **same positive central high and weak negative side lobes as at the north magnetic pole** under the same induced-dipole assumptions. A fixed signed vertical field component would reverse, but the scalar field strength measured by the <proton-precession magnetometer> does not. If the car's <remanent magnetization> dominates, neither this symmetry nor these predicted signs is assured.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use compression-positive <principal stresses> $\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3$. The two-dimensional <Mohr's circle> shown is the $\sigma_1$–$\sigma_3$ circle: its horizontal axis is compressive <normal stress> $\sigma_n$, its vertical axis signed <shear stress> $\tau$, its centre is $C=(\sigma_1+\sigma_3)/2$, and its radius is $R=(\sigma_1-\sigma_3)/2$. The intercepts $(\sigma_1,0)$ and $(\sigma_3,0)$ represent <principal stress planes>, on which <shear stress> vanishes. The top and bottom points have $|\tau|=R$, the maximum <shear stress>.

In the <rock> sketch, $\theta$ is the counterclockwise angle from the $\sigma_1$ direction to the plane's <unit normal vector> $\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Choose the clockwise unit tangent $\mathbf t=(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)$ and define $\tau=\mathbf t\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma^c\mathbf n$, with $\boldsymbol\sigma^c=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\sigma_3)$ compression positive. Thus a positive $\theta$ produces a positive $2\theta$ rotation on the plotted circle. The actual <traction> on the positive-normal face is $-\boldsymbol\sigma^c\mathbf n=-\sigma_n\mathbf n-\tau\mathbf t$. Reversing the tangent convention reverses the plotted shear sign, not the physical prediction.

In three dimensions there are circles for each pair of <principal stresses>. The largest is the $\sigma_1$–$\sigma_3$ circle; a general three-dimensional plane requires all three <principal stresses> and its normal, rather than a single angle on this circle.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a plane whose normal lies in the $\sigma_1$–$\sigma_3$ plane, rotate through $2\theta$ from $(\sigma_1,0)$ in the stated sign convention. The circle point gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_n=C+R\cos2\theta=\sigma_1\cos^2\theta+\sigma_3\sin^2\theta,\qquad\tau=R\sin2\theta.}
$$
The normal-to-plane angle is essential: if the angle supplied is between the **plane itself** and $\sigma_1$, use its complement for $\theta$. The <principal stress planes> have zero <shear stress>, and planes at $45^\circ$ to those <principal stress planes> have maximum $|\tau|$.

For an arbitrary plane in a fully three-dimensional <stress> field, let $n_i$ be its direction cosines in the <principal stress> basis. Then
$$
\sigma_n=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_i n_i^2,\qquad |\tau|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_i^2n_i^2-\sigma_n^2}.
$$
These follow by resolving the <traction> into its normal and tangential components; the compression-positive tensor is the negative of the usual tension-positive <Cauchy stress tensor>. The family of <Mohr circles> visualizes the possible combinations, but the two-dimensional $2\theta$ rule alone does not specify an arbitrary three-dimensional orientation.<Stress and strength lecture notes by MIT OpenCourseWare>[Lecture 10, stress tensor, traction resolution and Mohr construction; signs here are explicitly compression positive.]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Draw a <failure envelope> in the $(\sigma_n',\tau)$ plane. A useful brittle shear-failure approximation is the <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion>
$$
|\tau|=c+\mu_f\sigma_n',\qquad\mu_f=\tan\varphi,
$$
where $c$ is <cohesion>, $\varphi$ the <internal friction angle>, and $\sigma_n'$ the effective compressive <normal stress>. A circle wholly inside the envelope is below this ideal failure threshold. Increasing the <differential stress> expands it until it first touches the envelope: the tangent points identify the failing plane orientations. For a linear envelope, tangency implies
$$
R=C'\sin\varphi+c\cos\varphi,\qquad \sigma_1'=\sigma_3'\frac{1+\sin\varphi}{1-\sin\varphi}+\frac{2c\cos\varphi}{1-\sin\varphi}.
$$
With the angle convention above, the upper tangent point is at $2\theta=90^\circ+\varphi$. Its plane therefore makes $45^\circ-\varphi/2$ with the maximum-compression direction; there is a conjugate plane on the other side. For $\varphi=30^\circ$, this is $30^\circ$, rather than the $45^\circ$ plane of maximum <shear stress>. An existing weak <fault> can reactivate first, with its own lower <cohesion> and friction, and need not have this optimum new-fracture orientation.

For tensile <brittle fracture>, add a <tensile strength> cutoff: failure occurs when $\sigma_3'\leq-T_0$, where $T_0>0$. The opening fracture is approximately perpendicular to the minimum-compression direction. Real <rock> envelopes curve, especially at small confinement; a straight shear envelope must not be extrapolated as an exact tensile law. High confinement can instead favor distributed or ductile deformation, so merely drawing a larger circle does not establish that brittle failure is the active mechanism.<Stress and strength lecture notes by MIT OpenCourseWare>[Lecture 10, frictional strength and Mohr construction; the tensile cutoff is a separate condition.]{ref}

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

<Pore fluid pressure> $P$ reduces the compressive load carried across grain contacts or an accessible <fault>. In the usual elementary approximation,
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma'=\boldsymbol\sigma^c-P\mathbf I,\qquad \sigma_i'=\sigma_i-P,\quad\sigma_n'=\sigma_n-P,\quad\tau'=\tau.
$$
At fixed total <stress>, increasing $P$ translates the entire <Mohr circle> left by $P$ without changing its radius or the <differential stress>. The circle therefore approaches the frictional <failure envelope> even though the applied shear has not increased. Equivalently, in total-stress coordinates the strength on an existing plane is $c+\mu_f(\sigma_n-P)$, reduced by $\mu_f P$. For a known orientation and constant frictional parameters, the ideal critical pressure is
$$
P_{\mathrm{crit}}=\sigma_n-\frac{|\tau|-c}{\mu_f},
$$
provided the plane is in contact and sliding failure is reached before tensile opening.

For the diagram's illustrative $\sigma_1=80$, $\sigma_3=20$, $c=10\ \mathrm{MPa}$ and $\mu_f=0.6$, the dry circle is below failure. The freely oriented tangency threshold is about $P=8.4\ \mathrm{MPa}$; the $15\ \mathrm{MPa}$ circle is already beyond that threshold and shows the loading tendency, not a stable post-failure state. If $P$ grows enough that $\sigma_3-P\leq-T_0$, fluid pressure can instead open a tensile fracture. A porous-continuum <effective stress> law may use $\sigma_i'=\sigma_i-\alpha P$ with a coefficient $\alpha$ different from one. Also, changing fluid pressure can change total <stress> through constrained deformation; pure horizontal translation assumes those total stresses remain fixed.<Stress and strength lecture notes by MIT OpenCourseWare>[Lecture 10, frictional strength modified by pore-fluid pressure.]{ref}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Fault slip> must taper toward zero at the tips of an isolated finite <fault>, because its two sides are still joined beyond the tips. Accumulating more central displacement at a fixed short length steepens the displacement gradient and loads the surrounding <rock>, especially near the tips. In a simple elastic scaling, a fault of length $L$ and characteristic displacement $D$ requires a characteristic driving shear <stress> of order
$$
\Delta\tau\sim\mu\frac{D}{L},
$$
where $\mu$ is the <shear modulus> and the omitted coefficient depends on fault geometry. The host's finite strength prevents this gradient increasing indefinitely. Tip damage, new cracking and linkage with neighboring segments allow the fault system to lengthen and accommodate more displacement without unbounded tip loading. Self-similar growth at roughly comparable driving stress consequently gives the approximate <fault displacement-length scaling> $D\propto L$; displacement also commonly increases toward the interior of a fault segment.<Fault displacement-length scaling by United States Geological Survey>[2008 study, abstract: approximately linear scaling for shearing discontinuities under constant driving stress, with near-tip stress limited by rock strength.]{ref}

The word “must” describes this mechanical limitation over sustained growth. It is not a rule that every <earthquake> or every increment of accumulated <fault slip> lengthens the mapped trace: strong barriers, interactions and early establishment of fault length can permit substantial later displacement at nearly constant length.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <blind reverse fault> can transfer displacement into <folding> above its buried tip. Look for an asymmetric growing <anticline>, often with a steep forelimb and gentler backlimb, aligned with the inferred <fault strike>. A <fault-propagation fold> is one possible geometry; the fold alone does not uniquely establish the fault's depth or shape. Exposed older <geological strata> may be more steeply tilted than younger deposits, while <growth strata> thin and onlap toward an uplifting crest or fan in dip as deformation accumulates. Progressive <unconformities> and warped young deposits help distinguish active folding from an inherited fold.

In the landscape, seek an elongate uplifted ridge, warped or tilted <river terraces> and <alluvial fans>, localized <fold scarps>, incision across the rising structure, and drainage deflection or abandoned channels. Compare landforms of known relative ages: increasing deformation with age records repeated motion. Opposite fold limbs may show contrasting uplift and tilting, while active axial surfaces can concentrate deformation into narrow belts even without the main <fault> reaching the surface. Combine geological mapping and dating with <seismic reflection> or evidence from <geodesy> where available; an erosional ridge alone is not proof of a buried active reverse fault.<Blind-thrust mapping report by United States Geological Survey>[Sections “Active fold scarp” and “Syncline axis corresponding to the base of a fold forelimb”: surface folding and scarps can locate deformation associated with buried thrust tips.]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar> compares the <phase> of coherent radar returns acquired before and after the <earthquake> from closely repeated viewing geometry. After image registration and removal of orbital and topographic phase, the residual <interferometric phase> constrains relative ground displacement along the radar <line of sight>. Defining $u_{\mathrm{LOS}}$ positive toward the satellite and choosing the interferogram's phase order accordingly,
$$
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{def}}=\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}u_{\mathrm{LOS}}\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
One complete colour cycle therefore represents $\lambda/2$ of relative <line-of-sight displacement>. Reversing the image order reverses the displayed phase sign. This observes a projection of both horizontal and vertical motion, not uplift alone.<Interferometry by NASA>[NISAR mission explanation: remove orbit/topography contributions and interpret each complete fringe as half a radar wavelength of line-of-sight range change.]{ref}

\Image[paper-3-q4-interferograms.png]
{title=Qualitative interferograms for surface-breaking and blind reverse rupture}
{description=Original wrapped-phase cartoons and corresponding unwrapped line profiles. They illustrate a displacement discontinuity versus a continuous deformation lobe, rather than numerical elastic fault models. The chosen phase sign is positive toward the radar. Every complete colour cycle represents half a radar wavelength of line-of-sight displacement; real fringe shape depends on viewing and rupture geometry.}

If the main reverse rupture reaches the surface, coherent areas on opposite sides can show a displacement step: fringes become offset, truncated or abruptly juxtaposed along a trace. A narrow belt can lose <interferometric coherence> where cracking, intense gradients or changed scatterers prevent reliable phase comparison. If the rupture remains blind, a simple elastic model instead predicts a continuous surface displacement field, often a broad asymmetric lobe with continuous curved or closed fringes and a smooth displacement gradient across the projected tip.

These are diagnostic tendencies, not a unique test. An ordinary wrapped-phase boundary is just a $2\pi$ colour reset and is not itself a rupture. A step of an integer number of cycles can be hidden in wrapped phase; loss of <interferometric coherence> can hide either kind of near-field deformation. Vegetation, atmosphere and viewing direction also affect the pattern, and a displacement perpendicular to the radar <line of sight> is poorly sensed. Inspect coherence and unwrapped displacement, compare ascending and descending views where possible, and test a <fault> model against field observations. A sharp <fold scarp> above a blind rupture or a secondary shallow fault can also create localized gradients, so smooth fringes do not prove a buried tip and broken fringes alone do not prove a surface-breaking main rupture.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a surface-breaking reverse <earthquake>, expect a <fault scarp> or disrupted belt with the <hanging wall> displaced upward and commonly over the <footwall>. Small <thrust faults>, splays and <backthrusts> may repeat or override shallow deposits. Shortened soil, roads or other markers may buckle, while soft sediment can form pressure ridges and compression folds. Near the scarp, gravitational collapse and local bending can also produce tensile fissures and small <normal faults>; a reverse event need not create compression everywhere at the surface.

For a blind reverse rupture, expect coherent uplift and warping, newly tilted surfaces, or a <fold scarp> without a throughgoing primary fault offset. Small flexural-slip surfaces along bedding, secondary <thrust faults> or <backthrusts>, and opening cracks or small <normal faults> over the bent outer arc of the <anticline> can accompany the folding. Deformation may be distributed or localized at fold hinges. Thus a blind main fault does not imply an entirely unbroken ground surface, and secondary cracking must be distinguished from the principal rupture by its geometry and displacement pattern. Shaking-triggered <landslides> or <soil liquefaction> can accompany either case and should not automatically be mapped as primary tectonic rupture.<Blind-thrust mapping report by United States Geological Survey>[Active fold scarps can arise through localized coseismic uplift and tilting without the principal thrust reaching the ground surface.]{ref}

==== i

===== Solution

A <sun-synchronous orbit> maintains approximately constant orientation relative to the mean Sun, so corresponding passes over a location occur at nearly the same local solar time. It is usually a nearly polar, slightly retrograde <low Earth orbit>. Earth rotates beneath the successive tracks, allowing broad coverage; the satellite is not stationary over a location and does not necessarily revisit that location every day.

It is attained by selecting altitude, <orbital eccentricity> and <orbital inclination> so that <nodal precession> caused by Earth's equatorial bulge matches the mean apparent annual motion of the Sun, about $360^\circ$ per year eastward. To leading order,
$$
\dot\Omega=-\frac32J_2 n\left(\frac{R_E}{a(1-e^2)}\right)^2\cos i.
$$
Here $a$ is the <semi-major axis>, $e$ the <orbital eccentricity>, $i$ the <orbital inclination>, $n=\sqrt{GM_E/a^3}$ the <mean motion>, and $J_2$ Earth's dimensionless quadrupole coefficient. A retrograde inclination has $\cos i<0$, giving the required positive precession; typical imaging altitudes yield inclinations around $97^\circ$–$99^\circ$. The launch establishes the required orbital plane, and subsequent orbit maintenance limits drift.

This geometry suits repeated optical and <multispectral imaging> of land, vegetation and oceans, because comparable local time reduces illumination and shadow differences. <Landsat> and <Sentinel-2> are examples. Radar missions such as <Sentinel-1> also use this orbit for regular global sampling even though their illumination is supplied by the radar. Constant local time helps comparison but does not remove seasonal changes in solar elevation.<Catalog of Earth satellite orbits by NASA Earth Observatory>[“Polar Orbits” and “Sun-Synchronous Orbits”: near-polar global sampling and controlled precession maintaining local solar time.]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <geostationary orbit> is a circular, prograde, equatorial orbit with period equal to one <sidereal day>, approximately $23\ \mathrm h\ 56\ \mathrm{min}$. Its satellite therefore remains above one longitude on the equator. Merely having a one-day period gives a <geosynchronous orbit>; an inclined or eccentric version still moves across the ground observer's sky.

Balancing <gravitational acceleration> and <centripetal acceleration> gives
$$
r=\left(\frac{GM_ET^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}\simeq42164\ \mathrm{km},\qquad h=r-R_E\simeq35786\ \mathrm{km}.
$$
One route is injection into an elliptical <geostationary transfer orbit>, followed by a burn near apogee to circularize and remove the remaining inclination; direct or low-thrust transfers are also possible. Subsequent <station keeping> controls longitude, inclination and eccentricity.

The fixed wide view permits frequent observations of weather, clouds and rapidly changing storms, as in <Meteosat> and <GOES>. The tradeoffs are large range, generally coarser spatial sampling for comparable optics, oblique views at high latitudes and poor polar coverage. It is valuable for following change through the day rather than obtaining global coverage with one spacecraft.<Catalog of Earth satellite orbits by NASA Earth Observatory>[“High Earth Orbit”: geosynchronous radius, the circular/equatorial conditions for geostationarity, and weather-monitoring applications.]{ref}

==== Solution

Many open-ocean <chlorophyll retrieval> algorithms infer <chlorophyll a> from the blue-to-green <remote-sensing reflectance> ratio. Increasing pigment generally depresses blue reflectance relative to green. In coastal waters, however, <coloured dissolved organic matter> and <non-algal particles> can independently absorb blue light, as in the sketches, and therefore mimic an increase in pigment. $$
R_{rs}(\lambda)\propto\frac{b_b(\lambda)}{a(\lambda)+b_b(\lambda)},
$$
where $b_b$ is the <backscattering coefficient>; this is an explanatory approximation, not a complete retrieval formula. ==== Solution

<Chlorophyll a> occurs in nearly all oxygen-producing <phytoplankton> groups and is central to their <photosynthesis>. Its concentration can be measured reproducibly by pigment extraction or calibrated optical methods, and its absorption affects <ocean colour>, making it observable over large areas. It is therefore a practical common index of photosynthetic standing stock across diverse communities, unlike a pigment limited to one group.

Dead pigment and methodological differences can also affect interpretation, and nonphotosynthetic plankton are not represented. ==== Solution

The <photic zone> is the illuminated layer supporting <photosynthesis>; its <euphotic depth> is conventionally associated with about 1% of surface <photosynthetically active radiation>, integrated over approximately $400$–$700\ \mathrm{nm}$. This is a useful convention rather than a universal organism-specific compensation depth. Spectral filtering can be represented locally by
$$
E_d(z,\lambda)=E_d(0,\lambda)\exp\!\left[-\int_0^zK_d(\zeta,\lambda)\,d\zeta\right],
$$
where $K_d$ is the wavelength-dependent <diffuse attenuation coefficient>. It includes effects of absorption and scattering and is not simply equal to the absorption coefficient $a$.

In clear, low-chlorophyll water, pure <water> removes red light strongly, while weak blue pigment and CDOM absorption permits blue light, typically around $450$–$490\ \mathrm{nm}$, to penetrate furthest. The deepest usable light is consequently dim and blue-rich, and the <euphotic zone> can extend relatively deep. At high pigment concentrations, <phytoplankton pigments> preferentially remove blue and red light; associated detritus or CDOM can remove still more blue. The remaining transmission window commonly shifts toward blue-green or green wavelengths, roughly $500$–$570\ \mathrm{nm}$, and the total light falls off faster, so the <euphotic depth> is shallower. Exact window and depth depend on the full particle/dissolved mixture, not chlorophyll alone. The diagram normalizes the two deep spectra separately to show their different shapes, not equal absolute light at a common depth.

This confines photosynthetic growth to a thinner illuminated interval in high-chlorophyll water and allows a deeper potential habitat in clear low-chlorophyll water, subject also to nutrient supply, mixing and the organism's compensation requirement. Cells near the base benefit from low-light acclimation and pigment systems that capture the **remaining** wavelengths. In blue-rich deep ocean water, blue-absorbing pigments and some low-light-adapted picoplankton can be favored; where the surviving light is greener, groups with effective green-absorbing <accessory pigments>, such as certain <phycobilins>, gain an advantage. A pigment appears coloured by light it fails to absorb, so green-absorbing accessory pigments need not themselves look green. There is no universal deep species determined by chlorophyll concentration alone. A deep chlorophyll maximum can partly reflect increased pigment per cell rather than a proportional maximum in carbon <biomass>.<Phytopia's light and color by NASA>[Photic-zone discussion: the 1% convention, deep penetration in clear blue waters, and shallower illuminated habitat in productive turbid waters; pigment-based community selection here follows the surviving wavelengths and is conditional.]{ref}

==== Solution

The <wave equation> is second order in time. Even one <normal mode> obeys a second-order <ordinary differential equation>, so its initial displacement and initial velocity are independent data. Physically they specify stored elastic deformation and the initial motion.

Use <separation of variables> with a nontrivial product $\eta=XT$. Substitution into the <wave equation on a string> gives $\tau_0X''T=\lambda_0XT''$. Wherever the factors are nonzero,
$$
\frac{X''(x)}{X(x)}=\frac{\lambda_0}{\tau_0}\frac{T''(t)}{T(t)}.
$$
The left side depends only on $x$ and the right only on $t$, so both are a constant. For nontrivial fixed-end solutions this constant is negative: multiplying $X''=\sigma X$ by $X$ and integrating, using the homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, gives
$$
\sigma\int_0^LX^2\,dx=-\int_0^L(X')^2\,dx<0.

$$
\boxed{X''+k^2X=0,\qquad T''+c^2k^2T=0.}

These equations extend across the zeros of $X$ and $T$; the division is a derivation device, not a restriction excluding their nodes.

$$
The last term already accounts for the gas-pressure contribution; adding another gas-volume correction would double count it. The pressure ratio inside the logarithm is dimensionless.

With pressure expressed in bars, the solid correction is $-2.28(4500-1)=-10257.72\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{130862.28}{170.7-8.3145\ln4500}
=1298.755\,\mathrm K\simeq1025.605\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.}
$$
Using $4500$ rather than $4499$ for the solid pressure increment would silently shift the specified one-bar reference.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The compositions alone do not specify a unique <thermodynamic activity> model. Adopt the usual ideal, independent <mixing on crystallographic sites> approximation: three Mg–Fe positions per formula unit, with the other sites fixed at their stated endmember compositions. Then the <mineral endmember> activities are
$$
a_{\rm phlogopite}=0.85^3,\qquad a_{\rm pyrope}=0.23^3.
$$
The exponent counts the three mixing cations; treating each whole mineral formula as a single mixing site would be a different model. In particular, the magnesian <biotite> and <garnet> components here are <phlogopite> and <pyrope>.

For the written reaction, the <reaction quotient> is
$$
Q=\frac{a_{\rm pyrope}}{a_{\rm phlogopite}}\frac{P}{P_0},
$$
with the other solid activities equal to one. The <Gibbs energy of reaction> balance therefore gives
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{130862.28}
{170.7-8.3145\ln\!\left[4500(0.23/0.85)^3\right]}
=981.235\,\mathrm K\simeq708.085\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.}
$$
The smaller product-endmember activity lowers the reaction <chemical potential> and lowers the equilibrium temperature in this model. Nonideal <activity coefficients> or unequal occupancies of crystallographic sites would require further information rather than a revised universal numerical answer.<Geochemistry by William M. White>[Online teaching draft, chapter 3, Example 3.4: pyrope activity as a product of site fractions, $a_{\rm pyrope}=X_{\rm Mg}^3X_{\rm Al}^2$. Here the aluminium sites are fixed at their endmember occupancy.]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <fugacity coefficient> gives $f_{\mathrm{H_2O}}=\phi P=0.82\times4500=3690\,\mathrm{bar}$. Keep the solid pressure correction at the actual $4500\,\mathrm{bar}$; only the <water activity> uses the fugacity. Thus
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{130862.28}
{170.7-8.3145\ln\!\left[3690(0.23/0.85)^3\right]}
=969.243\,\mathrm K\simeq696.093\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.}
$$
The decrease from part (c) follows because $\phi<1$ lowers the chemical potential of the water product. All logarithms in these thermodynamic formulae are natural <logarithms>.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take base-ten <logarithms> and use dimensionless <thermodynamic activities>. The supplied concentration notation is interpreted in the ideal dilute-solution approximation, with a consistent reference concentration. Put
$$
x=\log_{10}a_{\mathrm{H_4SiO_4}},\qquad
y=\log_{10}(a_{\mathrm{Na^+}}/a_{\mathrm{H^+}}).
$$
With pure solids and unit water activity, the <reaction quotients> are $Q_I=10^{2y+2x}$ and $Q_{II}=10^{2y+6x}$. At <chemical equilibrium>, $Q=K$, so the two lines on the <mineral activity diagram> are
$$
\boxed{I:\ y=3.44-x,\qquad II:\ y=-7.59-3x.}
$$
They have slopes $-1$ and $-3$. Restricting the plotted silica activity to $x<0$ is compatible with their intersection,
$$
x_*=-5.515,\qquad y_*=8.955.
$$
Their full straight extensions are equilibrium relations for the respective phase pairs; not every extension is a stable boundary when the third mineral is available.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Subtract reaction (I) from reaction (II), cancelling the common aqueous ions and <corundum>, and divide by two. Choose the albite-to-nepheline direction:
$$
\mathrm{NaAlSi_3O_8}+4\mathrm{H_2O}
\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{NaAlSiO_4}+2\mathrm{H_4SiO_4}.
$$
This is <albite> plus water converting to <nepheline> and dissolved silica. <Standard Gibbs energies of reaction> add with their <stoichiometric coefficients>, so logarithms of <equilibrium constants> do too. For this single-formula normalization,
$$
\boxed{\log_{10}K_{III}=\frac{-15.18-6.88}{2}=-11.03.}
$$
The doubled reaction has $\log_{10}K=-22.06$, while reversing either reaction changes the sign. Specifying the direction and normalization removes that apparent ambiguity.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For pure <albite>, pure <nepheline> and unit water activity, $Q_{III}=a_{\mathrm{H_4SiO_4}}^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{x=\tfrac12\log_{10}K_{III}=-5.515,}
$$
a vertical line through the intersection of (I) and (II). It does not depend on $y$ because the net reaction has no hydrogen or sodium ions.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <stability boundary in a mineral activity diagram> requires the two coexisting minerals to be stable against the third. For the forward dissolution directions of (I) and (II),
$$
\frac{\Delta_rG_I}{2RT\ln10}=y-(3.44-x),\qquad
\frac{\Delta_rG_{II}}{2RT\ln10}=y-(-7.59-3x).
$$
A negative value favours <corundum> over the corresponding <silicate mineral>. For (III),
$$
\frac{\Delta_rG_{III}}{RT\ln10}=2x+11.03,
$$
so low silica activity favours <nepheline> over <albite>, and high silica activity favours <albite>. Combining these comparisons gives the stable portions:

* **(I): $x<-5.515$**, the nepheline–corundum boundary to the left of the intersection.
* **(II): $x>-5.515$**, the albite–corundum boundary to its right.
* **(III): $x=-5.515$, $y>8.955$**, the nepheline–albite boundary above the intersection.

The opposite extension of each line is <metastable> because the third mineral has lower thermodynamic potential there. All three minerals can coexist at the intersection in this fixed-temperature, fixed-pressure activity model.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The three stable fields are:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\text{nepheline: }&x<-5.515,\quad y>3.44-x,\\
\text{albite: }&x>-5.515,\quad y>-7.59-3x,\\
\text{corundum: }&y<\min(3.44-x,-7.59-3x).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Equalities give the stable coexistence boundaries described above. In particular, a point between the two descending line extensions is not automatically a fourth field: only the lower line borders the stable <corundum> region on each side of the intersection. The free-energy comparisons, rather than the appearance of the complete straight-line sketch, select the stable <mineral>.
