=== a

==== Solution

For an approximately <isotropic> <rock>, the <P-wave> and <S-wave> speeds are
$$
V_p=\sqrt{\frac{K+4\mu/3}{\rho}},\qquad V_s=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}},
$$
where $K$ is <bulk modulus>, $\mu$ is <shear modulus>, and $\rho$ is <density>. Five important controls are:

* **Mineralogy and cementation.** Stiff, well-cemented <mineral> frameworks usually have higher <elastic modulus> values and faster <seismic velocities> than poorly consolidated <sediments>. <Density> alone is insufficient: both stiffness and <density> enter the equations.
* **Porosity and cracks.** Larger <porosity>, especially compliant cracks, usually lowers effective <elastic modulus> values and <seismic velocities>. Crack orientation can make the <seismic velocities> direction-dependent through <seismic anisotropy>.
* **Pore-fluid composition and saturation.** Replacing liquid <water> by <gas> generally lowers the effective <bulk modulus> sharply and reduces $V_p$. <Pore fluids> have negligible static <shear modulus>; $V_s$ responds chiefly through the solid framework and the changed bulk <density>, rather than through a shear restoring force in the fluid.
* **Effective confining pressure.** Increasing <effective stress> closes cracks and improves grain contacts, usually increasing both <seismic velocities>. Increasing <pore fluid pressure> at fixed total <stress> reduces <effective stress> and can reverse this trend.
* **Temperature.** At otherwise comparable conditions, increasing <temperature> generally softens <minerals> and lowers <seismic velocities>; thermal cracking or partial <melting> can lower them further. Increasing burial commonly increases both <temperature> and <pressure>, so their separate effects need not have the same sign.

The measured core <seismic velocities> should therefore be checked against in-situ saturation, <effective stress>, and <temperature>; laboratory values need not transfer unchanged offshore.

The normal-incidence <acoustic impedance> is $Z=\rho V_p$, with units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Z_1=1.50\times10^6,\qquad Z_2=2.25\times10^6,\qquad Z_3=5.00\times10^6.}
$$
Define the <pressure-amplitude reflection coefficient> and <pressure-amplitude transmission coefficient> for incidence downwards from layer $i$ into layer $j$ by
$$
r_p=\frac{p_r}{p_i}=\frac{Z_j-Z_i}{Z_j+Z_i},\qquad t_p=\frac{p_t}{p_i}=\frac{2Z_j}{Z_j+Z_i}=1+r_p.
$$
Here $p$ denotes compressional normal-stress amplitude; in the <water> it is the acoustic <pressure>. Continuity of normal <traction> and normal particle <velocity> gives these expressions. They describe each interface separately, before any layer <layer reverberation>.

At normal incidence between these ideal <isotropic> layers there is no P-to-S <seismic mode conversion>.

{scope}

==== Solution

Use a **high-resolution multichannel <seismic reflection survey>**, with closely spaced crossing lines or a suitable small three-dimensional survey over the turbine locations. It should map the seabed, the <sediment>–<sandstone> boundary, local <faults>, buried channels, and lateral changes relevant to foundations. <Seismic reflection> responds to <acoustic impedance> contrasts and can image an irregular boundary without requiring it to be a first-arrival refractor. Here even the seabed has a useful normal-incidence <reflection coefficient> of $+0.20$ despite its zero P-velocity contrast.

A <seismic refraction survey> can provide complementary <seismic velocities> and geotechnical information, particularly using seabed multicomponent receivers for <S-waves>. Its limitations as the principal imaging method here include the missing water–sediment P refractor, required offsets of hundreds of metres to obtain useful basement first arrivals, and weaker constraints on thin layers or velocity inversions. Variable <bathymetry> and <sediment> thickness further complicate simple refraction interpretations.

A shallow-towed **<boomer seismic source> or small <sparker seismic source>** is a sensible initial <seismic source> for penetrating about 20 m of <sediment> beneath a 50 m water column. A <chirp sub-bottom profiler> can add finer imaging of the upper cover where penetration permits; penetration depends on grain size, consolidation, <gas>, and <seismic attenuation>. A pilot line should establish the minimum useful source energy and <bandwidth>. Large low-frequency air-gun arrays are unnecessary for this shallow target. Small coastal vessels, sea state, tow stability and acoustic background constrain usable <frequencies> and operations. Marine mammals and other local receptors motivate a low-energy effective source, monitoring and appropriate operational mitigation.<High-resolution multichannel seismic system by United States Geological Survey>[The institutional instrument description lists configurable boomer, sparker, hydrophone-streamer and complementary chirp equipment for shallow subsurface mapping.]{ref}

The expected normal-incidence two-way <travel times> are $2(50)/1500\simeq0.0667\ \mathrm s$ for the seabed and $2(50+20)/1500\simeq0.0933\ \mathrm s$ for basement, separated by $26.7\ \mathrm{ms}$. Source duration, recording sample interval and <bandwidth> must distinguish them. As an initial <vertical seismic resolution> estimate, $\Delta z\sim\lambda/4=V_p/(4f)$: $f=1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$ gives about $0.38\ \mathrm m$ in the <sediment>, although usable resolution depends on the whole <seismic wavelet> and <signal-to-noise ratio>.

Choose shot and receiver spacing from the shortest useful <wavelength>, target size, maximum <reflector dip>, <seismic migration> needs and achievable navigation accuracy. For a plane event of horizontal <seismic ray parameter> $p$, avoiding spatial <aliasing> requires approximately $\Delta x\leq1/(2f_{\max}|p|)$. Source spacing and receiver spacing also set midpoint coverage and <seismic acquisition fold>; adequate offsets enable <moveout correction> and velocity estimation. For nearly horizontal shallow reflectors a few-metre shot interval can be practical, whereas steep events at high frequency require denser spatial sampling. These values must be designed together rather than imposing one spacing for every target. Cross-line spacing must resolve lateral features between turbine sites, not merely along each track.

At a vessel speed of $2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, one shot per second gives 2 m shot spacing and ample separation relative to a roughly $0.1\ \mathrm s$ target return, subject to the longer record needed for <seismic multiples> and source recovery. Shallow towing limits <seismic source ghosts> and receiver ghosts, but requires stable tow depth. Accurate positioning, tides, measured water sound speed, <bathymetry> and checks for seabed <seismic multiples> are essential for correct target depths.

==== Solution

A <gravity anomaly> is the difference between observed gravitational acceleration and a specified reference prediction after defined reductions. A <free-air gravity anomaly>, a <Bouguer gravity anomaly>, and a residual local <gravity anomaly> differ because they remove different reference contributions. State the reference <ellipsoid>, datum, reference <density>, and regional trend before interpreting an anomaly as a subsurface <geophysical density contrast>.

On land, tie <gravimeter> observations to a gravity base, calibrate them, correct instrumental drift and Earth tides, and determine station <latitude> and elevation accurately. Subtract latitude-dependent normal gravity $\gamma(\varphi)$. Reduce to the reference elevation using a positive <free-air correction> for a station above that datum: about $+0.3086h\ \mathrm{mGal}$ for $h$ in metres. A simple <Bouguer gravity anomaly> then subtracts attraction of the intervening rock slab and adds the conventional <terrain correction>:
$$
\Delta g_B\simeq g_{\mathrm{obs,corr}}-\gamma(\varphi)+0.3086h-2\pi G\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}h+C_{\mathrm{terrain}},
$$
with all acceleration terms expressed consistently. In SI units $1\ \mathrm{mGal}=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.

At sea, also correct vessel-motion effects, especially the <Eötvös correction> from east–west vessel motion and Earth rotation, and vertical ship acceleration; average or filter the moving-platform data appropriately. GPS navigation and tide information specify position and measurement height. Sea-surface measurements give a marine <free-air gravity anomaly> after motion, latitude, tide and height reductions. A marine <Bouguer gravity anomaly> additionally allows for measured <bathymetry> and the replacement of low-density <water> by reference <rock>; the simple water-column replacement correction is positive, $2\pi G(\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}-\rho_w)H$, relative to the sea-surface free-air value. A consistent three-dimensional bathymetric correction is preferable near irregular coasts. Remove a justified broad regional field if the intended quantity is a residual anomaly of the local body.

==== Solution

For a wide horizontal <Bouguer slab> replacing surrounding basement, use the **density contrast** $\Delta\rho=\rho_{\mathrm{sed}}-\rho_{\mathrm{base}}$, not the absolute <sediment> <density>:
$$
\Delta g=2\pi G\Delta\rho\,h.
$$
Take downward attraction as positive. The negative anomaly therefore requires $\Delta\rho<0$. With $\Delta g=-40\ \mathrm{mGal}=-4.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $h=3000\ \mathrm m$, and $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$,
$$
\Delta\rho=\frac{-4.0\times10^{-4}}{2\pi(6.67430\times10^{-11})(3000)}=-317.95\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\mathrm{base}}=2300-(-317.95)\simeq2618\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\simeq2.62\ \mathrm{Mg\,m^{-3}}.}
$$
This is the requested <Bouguer slab> approximation: a flat observation surface, laterally infinite constant-thickness material, uniform average <geophysical density contrast>, and a regional anomaly already removed. The actual basin in Figure 1 narrows downwards and is finite. For a fixed maximum thickness, finite width reduces the central anomaly magnitude, so this calculation can underestimate the true magnitude of the average <geophysical density contrast>. It does not establish a unique basement <density> without the geometrical assumption.

Use compression-positive <principal stresses>, $\sigma_1\geq\sigma_2\geq\sigma_3$. For the intended <thrust fault> regime during approximately north–south <tectonic shortening>, the likely orientations are
$$
\boxed{\sigma_1:\ \text{horizontal N--S};\quad\sigma_2:\ \text{horizontal E--W};\quad\sigma_3:\ \text{vertical}.}

A locally horizontal traction-free surface motivates taking one principal direction as vertical, while the strong north–south compression selects the maximum horizontal <principal stress>. The expected <fault strike> is then approximately east–west.

This ordering assumes the two horizontal compressions exceed the vertical <principal stress>. North–south shortening by itself does not establish the magnitudes of all three: if the vertical <principal stress> were intermediate, <strike-slip faulting> would instead be possible. The answer above is the simple compressional end member used in the following parts.

A **reverse or <thrust fault> earthquake** is the expected end member: a <hanging wall> moves upwards relative to its <footwall>, accommodating north–south <tectonic shortening>. A plausible <double-couple seismic source> <focal mechanism> has an approximately east–west <fault strike>, a moderately north- or south-dipping <fault plane>, and up-dip <fault slip>. For example, right-hand-rule strike $090^\circ$, southward <fault dip> about $32^\circ$, and <fault rake> $+90^\circ$ specify a pure thrust mechanism.

In a lower-hemisphere equal-area plot, with shaded regions denoting compressional P first motions, this <focal mechanism> has a shaded central region and two unshaded outer lobes. The P axis is approximately horizontal north–south and the T axis approximately vertical for a symmetric idealization; for a single $32^\circ$ plane their axes are slightly tilted. The P and T axes of a <double-couple seismic source> need not exactly equal the regional <principal stress> axes. ==== Solution

Assume an initially unfractured homogeneous <isotropic> material described by a linear <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion>, with the given <coefficient of friction> $\mu_f=0.5$ representing the relevant <internal friction angle>. Use spatially uniform <pore fluid pressure>, <effective stresses> $\sigma_i'=\sigma_i-P_f$, and compression-positive signs. Then
$$
|\tau|=c+\mu_f\sigma_n',\qquad\varphi=\arctan\mu_f=26.565^\circ.
$$
The <Mohr circle> in the $\sigma_1',\sigma_3'$ plane has centre $C=(\sigma_1'+\sigma_3')/2$ and radius $R=(\sigma_1'-\sigma_3')/2$. At first failure it is tangent to the <failure envelope>. The radius to the positive-shear tangent point is perpendicular to the line of slope $\tan\varphi$, hence makes angle $90^\circ+\varphi$ with the positive normal-stress axis.

If $\theta$ is the angle between the **fault normal** and $\sigma_1$, the <stress transformation> is
$$
\sigma_n'=C+R\cos2\theta,\qquad\tau=R\sin2\theta.
$$
Thus $2\theta=90^\circ+\varphi$, $\theta=58.2825^\circ$, and the angle of the fault itself to horizontal $\sigma_1$ is
$$
\boxed{\delta=90^\circ-\theta=45^\circ-\frac\varphi2=31.7175^\circ.}
$$
The two conjugate <thrust faults> therefore strike approximately east–west and dip about **$32^\circ$ north and $32^\circ$ south**. This is the <fault dip>, not the $58.3^\circ$ fault-normal angle. Both <sediments> and basement have the same optimum orientation under these assumptions because their stipulated friction slopes are equal; their <cohesion> and absolute failure <stresses> could still differ.

The first two panels in <3/b/solution> give the labeled construction. Constant <cohesion> shifts the straight <failure envelope> without changing its slope, and uniform <pore fluid pressure> translates the <Mohr circle> in normal-stress coordinates; neither changes this tangency angle. In the illustrative cohesionless case, $R/C=\sin\varphi$, giving $\sigma_1'/\sigma_3'=(1+\sin\varphi)/(1-\sin\varphi)\simeq2.618$. Pre-existing weaknesses, a nonuniform <stress> field, anisotropic strength, or a nonlinear <failure envelope> invalidate the simple optimum-orientation prediction.<Stress and strength lecture notes by MIT OpenCourseWare>[Lecture 10 develops the traction, pore-pressure and Mohr-circle framework; the signs and angle definitions above specify the convention used here.]{ref}

==== Solution

The $31.7^\circ$ result predicts **initial failure in a uniform material under the assumed principal-stress orientations**. The actual Figure 3 is a present-day NW–SE cross section through a thick, heterogeneous Alpine stack, extending to about 50 km depth. It contains near-horizontal basal stretches, curved ramps, tightly bent contacts, and steep structures in the internal zones around Viso and the Dora Maira complex. It does not show a single undeformed conjugate pair newly formed in horizontal uniform <rock>.

Several processes explain the departures:

* **Mechanical layering and inherited weaknesses.** <Faults> can follow weak bedding, old <fault planes>, or contacts with lower <friction> or <cohesion> than the surrounding <rock>. These can form shallow <décollements> linked by steeper ramps. Figure 2 explicitly distinguishes the near-surface red “geophysical bedrock” boundary at a few hundred metres from the much deeper crystalline basement beneath the <Jurassic>, <Triassic> and <Permian> cover. An age label or a seismic contrast alone does not establish a friction coefficient; clay-rich or <evaporite> detachments are possibilities to test, not lithologies proved by that drawing.
* **Rotation after formation.** Continuing <tectonic shortening>, <folding>, stacking and <fault slip> can rotate an initially optimal <fault plane> to steeper or shallower present-day <fault dips>. A steep older structure may be inherited from an earlier deformation episode rather than created at its present orientation.
* **Curved faults and local stresses.** <Faults> bend through a layered <crust>; local <principal stress> axes rotate near topography, ramps and interacting faults. The optimum angle is measured relative to the local $\sigma_1$, not necessarily to the regional horizontal direction everywhere.
* **Changing strength and deformation mechanism.** Spatial changes in <pore fluid pressure>, <coefficient of friction>, <cohesion> and <temperature> alter which surfaces reactivate. At depth, <ductile deformation> can replace frictional brittle failure, so the same new-fracture construction need not apply throughout the section.

Finally, a section shows **apparent dip** unless it is perpendicular to <fault strike>. For an ideal plane, $\tan\delta_{\mathrm{app}}=\tan\delta_{\mathrm{true}}\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the section direction and <fault strike>. Obliquity can make a plane look shallower, but cannot on its own explain an apparent dip steeper than its true dip. Do not infer that each black curve is a currently active brittle <earthquake> fault.

The **northern station is closer**, under comparable <P-wave> and <S-wave> paths through the same <velocity model>. In the actual Figure 4 the northern P and S pulses are separated by about **6 s**, whereas the southern separation is roughly **8–8.5 s**. The <S–P time> removes the unknown <earthquake origin time>:
$$
\Delta t=t_S-t_P=D\left(\frac1{V_s}-\frac1{V_p}\right).

$$

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

The shorter, stronger northern pulses and broader southern pulses suggest propagation **from south to north**, towards the northern station. The useful evidence is the difference in **pulse widths**, not just the earlier northern arrivals: the latter chiefly reflect station distance.

As rupture reaches successively more northerly patches, each patch radiates a new <P-wave> and <S-wave> pulse. Towards the north, the later emission time is partly cancelled by a shorter propagation path; those contributions arrive closer together. Towards the south, later emission and a longer path add, stretching the signal. This is <rupture directivity>. It refers to the advance of the slipping region along the <fault>, not necessarily the direction of the local <fault slip> vector. A thrust can slip up-dip while its rupture front advances in a direction with a northward component.

More generally, emission from a patch at distance $s$ along the rupture arrives at
$$
t_{\mathrm{arr}}(s)=t_{\mathrm{start}}+\frac{s}{V_r}+\frac{R(s)}c,\qquad \frac{dt_{\mathrm{arr}}}{ds}\simeq\frac1{V_r}-\frac{\cos\psi}{c}.
$$
The forward direction has a smaller arrival-time spread. This is the same travel-time compression captured by isochrone-based <rupture directivity> calculations.<A formulation of directivity for earthquake sources using isochrone theory>[The primary report relates directivity to rupture geometry, seismic speed and the arrival-time distribution of radiation from successive fault patches.]{ref} Larger northern amplitude supports the interpretation but is not decisive on its own because <geometrical spreading>, <seismic attenuation>, <seismic radiation patterns> and site response also affect amplitude. With only two traces, propagation, filtering and site effects remain alternatives to test; the idealized exercise is most naturally explained by northward directivity.

==== Solution

The <geomorphology> of active <normal faulting> records a competition between accumulated tectonic displacement and the ability of <erosion>, transport and deposition to modify it. A <hanging wall> moves down relative to a <footwall>, producing a <fault scarp>, mountain-front relief, and accommodation space for a <sedimentary basin>. Repeated earthquakes can build a large front even though each individual surface break is much smaller.<Normal-fault geomorphic features by United States Geological Survey>[The primary mapping report's table of geomorphic features lists scarps, faceted spurs, oversteepened mountain fronts and rejuvenated valley floors as common normal-slip indicators.]{ref}

\Image[paper-3-q4-geomorphology.png]
{title=Original sketches of tectonic, erosional and depositional controls on normal-fault landforms}
{description=The panels distinguish cumulative block displacement, progressive scarp rounding, drainage and faceted mountain fronts, and burial of a scarp by sediment. Geometries and relative scales are illustrative.}

The principal controls and their observable effects are:

* **Displacement, rate and timing.** Larger single-event <fault slip> creates a higher fresh scarp; repeated slip builds cumulative offsets of fans and <river terraces>. The interval since the latest rupture controls degradation. For pure <dip-slip faulting> on a plane dipping $\delta$, vertical <fault throw> is $D\sin\delta$ and horizontal <fault heave> is $D\cos\delta$. Scarp height on an inclined eroded surface need not equal either the slip or the original vertical throw.
* **Geometry and structural history.** <Fault dip>, strike, curvature, segmentation, splays and along-strike variations in <fault slip> control scarp orientation and continuity. Linked faults can create a stepped front; synthetic and antithetic faults can create a <graben>. Cumulative deformation includes broad footwall uplift and hanging-wall tilting as well as a sharp break.
* **Lithology and cover.** Strong exposed <bedrock> can preserve steep scarps and fresh fault surfaces. Weak fractured <rock>, thick <regolith> and loose <alluvium> weather and slump more rapidly, spread deformation, and produce debris at the scarp base. Differential <erosion> can make a lithological front without recent tectonic movement, so a straight cliff alone is not sufficient evidence of activity.
* **Climate and surface transport.** Rainfall, runoff, frost action, vegetation and slope processes control scarp rounding and <colluvium> production. Steep new faces collapse towards stable slopes; later hillslope transport rounds the crest and fills the toe. A simple <scarp diffusion model> broadens a step over a length proportional to $\sqrt{\kappa t}$, but only if a suitable transport law and comparable material and climate apply.
* **Drainage and sediment supply.** Footwall uplift can incise channels and produce <river terraces>, knickpoints and triangular <faceted spurs>. Hanging-wall subsidence can pond drainage, deflect streams and promote <alluvial fans> and basin filling. Rapid <aggradation> can bury an active scarp, while stream incision can remove or exaggerate local relief.
* **Preservation and initial landscape.** Pre-existing slopes, relief, surface ages, glacial modification and human disturbance determine where offsets can be recognized. A fan abandoned long before the latest earthquake can accumulate more offset than a younger nearby surface. Compare like-age markers across a fault, and distinguish a mountain front's long history from a single event.

Useful observations therefore include scarp profiles, repeated offsets of dated surfaces, drainage organization, and continuity along strike. A high rugged front is not by itself evidence of a high present <fault slip rate>; conversely, a muted or buried scarp need not mark an inactive <fault>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A surface-reaching <normal fault> earthquake commonly produces a **down-facing step towards the <hanging wall>**, accompanied by extension across the break. A fresh free face can expose <bedrock>, polish or striations, roots or offset soil. The actual rupture is usually irregular: <fault slip> changes along strike, separate strands overlap or step, and <tension fissures>, small <grabens> and secondary cracks distribute part of the extension.

\Image[paper-3-q4-surface-rupture.png]
{title=Original cross sections showing how surficial cover changes normal-fault surface rupture}
{description=Competent exposed rock localizes a sharp free face; thin loose cover permits splays and a small graben; thick weak cover can accommodate a buried fault tip by broad flexure and distributed extension.}

**Exposed competent rock or very thin cover:** a relatively narrow, sharp break can closely follow the principal <fault plane>. A steep free face can be recognizable immediately, then weather and shed debris. The face need not be vertical or maintain the same height along strike.

**Loose granular or poorly consolidated cover:** the main shear can splay upwards through <alluvium> or <regolith>. Open fissures, step-like scarps, rotated blocks, synthetic and antithetic <normal faults>, and a small intervening <graben> can form across a wider zone. Collapse and raveling quickly build <colluvium> at the toe. A single rupture event can therefore create several parallel breaks; counting them does not count earthquakes.

**Thick weak or cohesive cover:** deeper <fault slip> can be transferred into distributed <shear strain>, tilted beds, broad warping or <monoclines>, with tension cracks above a propagating tip. A discrete break may be attenuated or absent if the <fault> remains blind. Cover thickness alone is not enough to predict the response: cohesion, grain size, layering, saturation, rupture displacement and fault depth also matter. Saturated loose <sediment> can additionally develop <soil liquefaction>, <sand blows> or lateral spreading; these are secondary shaking effects and do not independently locate the tectonic rupture.

The observed vertical step, crack aperture and total width of disturbed ground should be measured separately. Reconstruct the total displacement across all strands and account for the original ground slope. <Landslides> or gravitational spreading can imitate normal-fault scarps, so map connections to the main <fault> and displaced markers rather than assuming every open crack is primary tectonic slip.

Excavate across the mapped <fault scarp>, approximately perpendicular to its strike, at a site where young <sediments> record and preserve deformation. Look for **event horizons**, not merely a stack of sediments:

* A <fault> or fissure cuts older layers but terminates below an undeformed sealing deposit. The event occurred after deposition of the youngest demonstrably displaced material and before deposition of the oldest demonstrably sealing material.
* A fresh free face sheds coarse angular debris, often thickest against the scarp and thinning into the <hanging wall>; later finer slopewash can overlie it. A buried <soil horizon> between successive <colluvial wedges> can indicate a period of stability separating events. Fissures filled from above can preserve collapsed soil blocks or distinctive younger sediment.
* Older markers may have larger offsets than younger markers. A lower wedge or soil cut and displaced by a younger shear, then overlain by a new wedge and an intact seal, gives a cross-cutting basis for at least two events. Changes in displacement should be reconstructed across all fault strands.
* Rapid tilting, growth of accommodation space, abrupt changes in deposit thickness, and locally ponded beds can corroborate the structural evidence. <Soil liquefaction> structures or shaking-induced <landslides> may support a chronology, but do not alone prove surface rupture on the trenched fault.

In the drawing, A was faulted in event 1; W1 accumulated; B buried and stabilized that surface. Event 2 displaced A, W1 and B, after which W2 accumulated and C sealed the fault tip. Thus A records two events, B one and C none. This combination of offset and sealing relationships is stronger evidence than identifying two wedge-shaped units alone.

Bracket event ages with multiple samples just below and above the event horizons. Suitable short-lived organic material can be dated by <radiocarbon dating>; <optically stimulated luminescence dating> can constrain deposition of appropriate mineral grains. Reworked old charcoal, younger roots, incomplete bleaching, erosional gaps and time elapsed between rupture and wedge deposition must enter the age interpretation. For calendar dates, $t_{\mathrm{youngest\ cut}}<t_{\mathrm{event}}<t_{\mathrm{oldest\ seal}}$ only when those deposits genuinely bracket the rupture; a radiocarbon age in years before present reverses the numerical ordering.<Introduction to paleoseismology by United States Geological Survey>[“Finding Paleoearthquakes” explains disrupted layers followed by unbroken cover; “Determining the Age of a Paleoearthquake” explains dating deposits above and below the event horizon.]{ref}

One earthquake can generate several debris pulses and wedges; repeated earthquakes may reuse one strand without leaving separable deposits. <Erosion>, <bioturbation>, nondeposition, lateral facies changes and upward-dying splays can hide events or mimic terminations. Confirm relationships on both trench walls and, when possible, in adjacent exposures. The result is a minimum supported event sequence with age intervals, not an assumption of perfectly periodic recurrence or one earthquake per wedge.

Put $h=\Delta x$ and suppose $L=(n+1)h$, so there are $n$ interior grid points $x_j=jh$, $j=1,\ldots,n$. Write $u_j(t)\simeq u(x_j,t)$. The fixed ends impose homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>,
$$
u_0(t)=u_{n+1}(t)=0.
$$
For a sufficiently smooth <displacement> field, the two <Taylor series> at fixed time give
$$
\begin{aligned}
u(x_j+h,t)&=u(x_j,t)+hu_x+\frac{h^2}{2}u_{xx}+\frac{h^3}{6}u_{xxx}+O(h^4),\\
u(x_j-h,t)&=u(x_j,t)-hu_x+\frac{h^2}{2}u_{xx}-\frac{h^3}{6}u_{xxx}+O(h^4),
\end{aligned}
$$
where all displayed <derivatives> on the right are evaluated at $(x_j,t)$. Adding cancels the odd terms, leaving the <second-order central difference>
$$
u_{xx}(x_j,t)=\frac{u(x_j-h,t)-2u(x_j,t)+u(x_j+h,t)}{h^2}+O(h^2).
$$
Substitute this <finite difference> into the <wave equation> and retain continuous time. The resulting <method of lines> gives coupled second-order <ordinary differential equations>,
$$
\ddot u_j=\frac{c^2}{h^2}(u_{j-1}-2u_j+u_{j+1}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
$$
In particular, the first and last equations are $\ddot u_1=(c^2/h^2)(-2u_1+u_2)$ and $\ddot u_n=(c^2/h^2)(u_{n-1}-2u_n)$ for $n\ge2$. With the interior <displacement> <vector> only,
$$
\boxed{\ddot{\mathbf u}=A\mathbf u,\qquad
\mathbf u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)^T,\qquad
A=\frac{c^2}{h^2}\begin{pmatrix}
-2&1&0&\cdots&0\\
1&-2&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&1&-2&1\\
0&\cdots&0&1&-2
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Equivalently, $A_{jj}=-2c^2/h^2$, $A_{j,j+1}=A_{j+1,j}=c^2/h^2$, and all other entries vanish. This entrywise definition also covers small $n$, including $A=(-2c^2/h^2)$ when $n=1$. The <matrix> is a <symmetric matrix> and a <tridiagonal matrix>. The spatial approximation has error $O(h^2)$ in <Big O notation>; time has not yet been discretized.

In this particular <matrix>, the row sums and column sums both vanish. Put $\mathbf1=(1,1,1,1)^T$. Then $A\mathbf1=0$ and $\mathbf1^TA=0$, and the two three-dimensional <vector subspaces> coincide:
$$
\operatorname{Col} A=\operatorname{Row} A
=\{v\in\mathbb R^4:\mathbf1^Tv=0\}.
$$
This equality follows here from both sum identities and the <dimensions of a vector space>; it does not make $A$ a <symmetric matrix> and is not true for every <square matrix>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <null space> consists of <concentration> patterns on which transport produces no change. Here it is the family
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c=\alpha\mathbf1.}
$$
Every box has the same <concentration>. <Ocean circulation> may continue, but inflow and outflow of the <chemical tracer> balance in each box. With $\mathbf b=0$ these are exactly the <steady states>; physically admissible members have $\alpha\ge0$. With nonzero $\mathbf b$, adding $\alpha\mathbf1$ to any particular <steady state> leaves its transport balance unchanged, but a <null space> <vector> alone need not balance the sources and sinks.

To identify the tracer inventory, apply <conserved inventory from a left null vector>. Directly summing each column gives
$$
\mathbf1^TA=(-2+2,\ 1-2+1,\ -2+2,\ 1+1-2)=(0,0,0,0).
$$
Consequently, for the supplied <ordinary differential equations>,
$$
M(t)=\mathbf1^T\mathbf c(t),\qquad
\frac{dM}{dt}=-\mathbf1^T\mathbf b.
$$
For $\mathbf b=0$, $M$ is a <conserved quantity>. The connected <box model> equalizes <concentrations>, approaching the unique member of the <null space> with the initial inventory:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c(t)\longrightarrow\frac{\mathbf1^T\mathbf c(0)}4\mathbf1.}
$$
The <arithmetic mean> is appropriate here because the supplied <matrix> has zero column sums. In a general <box model> the conserved quantity is $w^T\mathbf c$, where $w$ is a positive <left null space> <vector>; for physical <concentrations> expressed per unit <volume>, these weights represent the box <volumes> up to a common factor. One must establish the weights from the equations, rather than infer equal <volumes> from a schematic drawing.

Nonnegative initial <concentrations> remain nonnegative under transport alone. Indeed $A+2I$ is a <nonnegative matrix>, so
$$
e^{tA}=e^{-2t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^k(A+2I)^k}{k!}
$$
has nonnegative entries for $t\ge0$: it is a <positive matrix semigroup>. Its column sums are one by $\mathbf1^TA=0$. To see why equalization occurs, set $d=\mathbf c-(M/4)\mathbf1$ for $\mathbf b=0$; then $\mathbf1^Td=0$ and
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\|d\|_2^2}{2}
=d^TAd=-\frac12\bigl[3(d_1-d_2)^2+(d_1-d_4)^2+(d_2-d_3)^2+3(d_3-d_4)^2\bigr].
$$
The right side vanishes only for constant $d$, which in the zero-sum <vector subspace> means $d=0$. Its strict negative bound on the <unit sphere> in that <vector subspace> gives decay to zero. Thus the <matrix exponential> redistributes inventory without creating negative <concentrations>; arbitrary prescribed sinks can still exhaust the tracer if they are allowed to act indefinitely.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

A <steady state> $\mathbf c_*$ satisfies $0=A\mathbf c_*-\mathbf b$, hence
$$
A\mathbf c_*=\mathbf b.
$$
The sign is positive on the right after rearranging the supplied equation. A solution exists exactly when $\mathbf b$ lies in the <column space> of $A$, which above was found to be the zero-sum <vector subspace>. Equivalently, multiply by the <left null space> <vector> $\mathbf1^T$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf1^T\mathbf b=b_1+b_2+b_3+b_4=0.}
$$
This condition is necessary and sufficient for an algebraic <steady state>, since both the <column space> and that zero-sum <vector subspace> have <dimension> $3$. It expresses global <conservation of mass>: in the closed <box model>, total tracer production must balance total tracer removal. With the paper's convention $-\mathbf b$, a positive $b_i$ is a sink and a negative $b_i$ is a source. If the sum is nonzero, $dM/dt=-\sum_i b_i$ and the inventory changes linearly in time, preventing a <steady state>.

When the condition holds, all algebraic <steady states> form the <affine solution space of a linear equation> $\mathbf c_p+\alpha\mathbf1$, for any particular solution $A\mathbf c_p=\mathbf b$. The initial inventory selects one member through $\mathbf1^T\mathbf c_* =\mathbf1^T\mathbf c(0)$. For a physically admissible solution, its four <concentrations> must additionally be nonnegative; the compatibility condition alone imposes no such inequalities.

{scope}

==== Solution

Choose a fixed time increment $h=\Delta t>0$, let $t_n=nh$, and approximate $\mathbf c(t_n)$ by $\mathbf c_n$. The <backward Euler method> evaluates the transport term at the new time:
$$
\frac{\mathbf c_{n+1}-\mathbf c_n}{h}=A\mathbf c_{n+1}-\mathbf b.
$$
Rearrange to the <linear system>
$$
\boxed{B\mathbf c_{n+1}=\mathbf c_n-h\mathbf b,\qquad
B=I-hA=\begin{pmatrix}
1+2h&-h&0&-h\\
-2h&1+2h&0&0\\
0&-h&1+2h&-h\\
0&0&-2h&1+2h
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Although $A$ is a <singular matrix>, $B$ is an <invertible matrix> for every $h>0$. In each row, its diagonal entry is $1+2h$ and the sum of the <absolute values> of its off-diagonal entries is $2h$; hence it is a <strictly diagonally dominant matrix>. Its <Gershgorin discs> have centre $1+2h$ and radius $2h$, excluding zero. In particular, the zero <eigenvalue> of $A$ becomes an <eigenvalue> $1$ of $B$.

The algorithm uses <reusing LU factors for multiple right-hand sides>:

* Form $B$ once. Compute a <pivoted LU decomposition> $PB=L_B U_B$, storing the <permutation matrix> $P$, the unit <lower triangular matrix> $L_B$, and the <upper triangular matrix> $U_B$.
* Set $\mathbf c_0=\mathbf c(0)$.
* At each step form $r_n=\mathbf c_n-h\mathbf b$. Solve $L_B y=Pr_n$ by <forward substitution>, then solve $U_B\mathbf c_{n+1}=y$ by <back substitution>.
* Advance $n$ and repeat until the desired final time. Choose the fixed increment so the final time is an integer multiple of $h$, or refactor $B$ if a shorter final step is used.

Both triangular solves use the factors of **$I-hA$**. The <LU factorization> of the singular transport <matrix> $A$ from part (a) is not a solver for this new <linear system>. No <matrix inverse> needs to be formed. Since $A$ and $h$ are fixed, the same <LU factorization> serves every step; only the right-hand side changes. A varying $\mathbf b(t)$ would give $r_n=\mathbf c_n-h\mathbf b(t_{n+1})$ without changing these factors.

As a check, multiplying the update by $\mathbf1^T$ gives
$$
\mathbf1^T\mathbf c_{n+1}=\mathbf1^T\mathbf c_n-h\mathbf1^T\mathbf b,
$$
exactly reproducing the inventory balance at the sampled times, apart from <roundoff error>. The <backward Euler method> has temporal <global error> $O(h)$ for smooth solutions, so its <A-stability> does not remove the need to select $h$ for accuracy.

The bounding equalities give the stable two-mineral coexistences. In particular, the stable <corundum> region lies below the lower of the two oblique lines; treating every line extension as stable would mislabel parts of the <mineral activity diagram>.
