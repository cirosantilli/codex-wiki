Section A: Earthquakes and Faulting.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**The <fault dips> are approximately $90^\circ$.** The mapped senses of <fault slip> are predominantly <strike-slip faulting>. In an <Andersonian faulting regime>, the intermediate <principal stress> $\sigma_2$ is vertical and the maximum and minimum <principal stresses> $\sigma_1,\sigma_3$ are horizontal, so the preferred <fault planes> are vertical. Local bends can introduce <oblique-slip faulting> without changing this first-order interpretation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat the two strands as newly formed <conjugate faults> obeying the same <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion>, rather than merely inherited <faults> reactivated in a later <stress> field. A <fault strike> is an unoriented line, so $296^\circ$ is equivalent to $116^\circ$. The acute angle between the <fault strikes> is therefore $116^\circ-60^\circ=56^\circ$. The formal compressive acute bisector puts $\sigma_1$ at azimuth $88^\circ$ and $\sigma_3$ at $178^\circ$. This orientation must also be checked against the mapped senses of <strike-slip faulting>, as discussed below.

Let $\beta=28^\circ$ be the angle between either <fault plane> and $\sigma_1$. Tangency of <Mohr's circle> to $|\tau|=c+\mu\sigma'_n$, where $\sigma'_n$ is <effective stress> normal to the plane and $c$ is <cohesion>, gives $\beta=45^\circ-\phi/2$ and $\mu=\tan\phi$. Thus
$$
\phi=90^\circ-56^\circ=34^\circ,\qquad \boxed{\mu=\tan34^\circ\simeq0.675}.
$$
In the <Mohr circle>, the <fault planes> are $112^\circ$ apart around the circle, because a physical plane rotation is doubled. The figure uses $c=0$ to display both tangencies; a common nonzero <cohesion> changes the intercept, not the inferred <friction angle>. The inference of $\mu$ requires optimally oriented <conjugate faults> at formation: the two <fault strikes> alone cannot measure a friction coefficient for arbitrarily reactivated <faults>.

**Consistency limitation:** the mapped westward motion of the Anatolian block corresponds to <left-lateral strike-slip faulting> on the East Anatolian strand and <right-lateral strike-slip faulting> on the North Anatolian strand. East–west maximum compression for the acute-bisector construction instead predicts the opposite pair. The stress orientation compatible with the mapped senses lies near the north–south, obtuse bisector; the planes are then about $62^\circ$ from $\sigma_1$, which would give $\phi=90^\circ-2(62^\circ)=-34^\circ$ in the optimal-formation formula. A negative friction angle is inadmissible for ordinary frictional strength. Thus **$0.675$ is the formal magnitude inferred from unoriented angles, while the full map does not support a self-consistent positive-friction, optimally formed conjugate pair**. The common-stress assumption can still describe inherited or anisotropically controlled <faults>, but then their orientations no longer determine a unique coefficient.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a heterogeneous <fault-slip> distribution, <seismic moment> is
$$
M_0=G\int_A D\,dA=GA\overline D,
$$
where $G=30\times10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}$ is the <shear modulus>, $A$ the <fault-plane> area, and $\overline D$ the area-averaged <fault slip>. The peak colour cannot be used as $\overline D$: most of the illustrated <fault plane> has much smaller <fault slip>.

One reproducible graphical estimate divides the approximately $190\ \mathrm{km}$ long and $40\ \mathrm{km}$ down-dip panel into seven $25\ \mathrm{km}$ strips and a final $15\ \mathrm{km}$ strip. Reading area averages from the colour scale, from southwest to northeast, gives roughly $1.7,2.7,3.1,1.8,1.9,1.7,1.0,0.3\ \mathrm m$. This includes the pale, nearly unslipped lower part of each strip. Hence
$$
\int_A D\,dA\simeq40\{25(1.7+2.7+3.1+1.8+1.9+1.7+1.0)+15(0.3)\}\ \mathrm{km^2\,m}\simeq1.4\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^3},
$$
and **$M_0\simeq4.2\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$**. A reasonable graphical range is approximately $3$–$6\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{N\,m}$, dominated by colour averaging and the down-dip extent of small <fault slip>. The corresponding <moment magnitude> is about $M_w=7.7$, using $M_w=\frac23(\log_{10}(M_0/\mathrm{N\,m})-9.1)$. This is an estimate from the supplied distribution, rather than a substitution of a catalogued <earthquake> magnitude.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Effective stress> and frictional strength generally increase with depth through the shallow crust undergoing <brittle fracture>. At $1\ \mathrm{km}$, small overburden and often weak, fractured near-surface materials limit the <shear stress> and <elastic energy> that can accumulate. Some shallow materials also undergo stable sliding rather than unstable <earthquake> nucleation. Near $15\ \mathrm{km}$, a locked <fault> prone to <brittle fracture> can sustain a much larger <differential stress> before frictional instability releases stored <elastic energy>.

This depth is plausibly near the lower, strong part of the seismogenic layer: below it, increasing <temperature> eventually promotes <material creep> across the <brittle-ductile transition>. Once nucleated at depth, a dynamic <finite-source rupture> can propagate into shallow regions that could not independently nucleate the same event. Depth alone does not determine the nucleation point: local <pore pressure>, frictional stability and <stress> heterogeneity also matter.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The star is near the southwestern end of the main <fault-slip> patch, so a much longer part of the <finite-source rupture> propagates northeastward. For comparable distances, ground conditions and buildings, **the northeastern site receives the stronger, shorter direct pulse; the southwestern site receives the weaker, longer direct shaking**. This is <rupture directivity>.<What is Directivity by United States Geological Survey>[Explanation of focusing by successive rupture elements]{ref}

For a segment of length $\ell$, <rupture velocity> $v_r$ and <seismic wave> speed $c>v_r$, successive arrival times span approximately
$$
\Delta t_{\rm forward}=\ell(1/v_r-1/c),\qquad \Delta t_{\rm backward}=\ell(1/v_r+1/c).
$$
Forward arrivals overlap and concentrate <energy> into a shorter pulse. Backward arrivals spread out. The supplied <finite-source rupture> is not perfectly unilateral, so the prediction concerns the dominant contribution. <Seismic radiation patterns>, <seismic attenuation>, sediment amplification and building response can alter the ranking of actual damage or total coda duration.

These magnitudes illustrate one possible <stress tensor>, rather than a measurement from the photograph. With $\mu=1/\sqrt3$, the $\sigma_1$–$\sigma_3$ <Mohr circle> is tangent to the <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion> at a plane $30^\circ$ from horizontal. === b
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret the vertical, apparently unoffset fractures as opening <geological joints>. Their normals are east–west, so the least <principal stress> $\sigma_3$ must be east–west and sufficiently tensile in <effective stress>. The other two <principal stresses> lie within the vertical north–south <geological joint> plane; their ordering cannot be recovered from this fracture orientation alone.

One example, with compression positive and coordinates east, north, up, is
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma'=\begin{pmatrix}-3&0&0\\0&5&0\\0&0&10\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{MPa}.
$$
Here $\sigma_1$ is vertical, $\sigma_2$ north–south, and $\sigma_3=-3\ \mathrm{MPa}$ east–west. The <geological joint> opens east–west when its <tensile strength> is $3\ \mathrm{MPa}$. An isotropic <pore pressure> can make the east–west <effective stress> tensile even while the total <normal stress> remains compressive. Draw opening arrows pointing away from the vertical fracture, rather than sliding arrows along it.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use compression-positive <effective stress> normal to the plane $\sigma'_n$ on the horizontal axis and <shear stress> $\tau$ on the vertical axis. For any chosen pair of <principal stresses>, <Mohr's circle> has centre $C=(\sigma'_1+\sigma'_3)/2$ and radius $R=(\sigma'_1-\sigma'_3)/2$.

For the <reverse fault> example, $C=60\ \mathrm{MPa}$ and $R=30\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Draw the two <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion> lines $\tau=\pm\sigma'_n/\sqrt3$. The tangencies are
$$
(\sigma'_n,\tau)=(45,\pm15\sqrt3)\ \mathrm{MPa}.
$$
These are <shear failure> points with nonzero <shear stress>. The two smaller three-dimensional <Mohr circles> use $(\sigma'_1,\sigma'_2)$ and $(\sigma'_2,\sigma'_3)$; they lie inside the outer circle and do not touch the envelope in this example.

For the opening <geological joints>, use the <tensile failure criterion> $\sigma'_n=-T_0$. The example gives $C=3.5\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $R=6.5\ \mathrm{MPa}$ and $T_0=3\ \mathrm{MPa}$. The circle first touches this vertical cutoff at **$(\sigma'_n,\tau)=(-3,0)\ \mathrm{MPa}$**. Thus the <geological joints> form on planes normal to $\sigma_3$ and have zero resolved <shear stress>. A complete brittle envelope can combine this tensile cutoff with compressive <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion>, using sufficient <cohesion> that shear failure does not occur first. The failure-mechanics figure displays these two distinct constructions.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <reverse fault> requires strong east–west compression; the vertical opening <geological joints> require the least <principal stress> in the <effective stress> tensor east–west. **They need not have formed at the same time or under the same local <stress> field.** Compression can form the <reverse fault> during burial and <crustal shortening>; subsequent <tectonic exhumation>, unloading, relaxation, cooling or another tectonic episode can rotate the <principal stress> ordering and permit opening <geological joints>.

Furthermore, local <stress> near <faults>, layer interfaces and topography can differ from the regional <stress>. A high <pore pressure> shifts every <Mohr circle> left by $p$, helping <tensile failure>, but isotropic <pore pressure> alone cannot exchange the east–west and vertical <principal stress> ordering. Determine relative timing using cross-cutting relations, offsets of <geological joints>, and any generations of fracture-filling <minerals>. Spatial coexistence is therefore evidence of a deformation history, rather than a requirement that the two failure diagrams describe one simultaneous homogeneous <stress> state.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Within a shallow <fault zone> undergoing <brittle fracture>, repeated sliding crushes and grinds grains. Expect <fault gouge>, potentially rich in fine <clay minerals>, and coarser angular <fault breccia>; a cohesive, cemented crushed rock is a <cataclasite>. <Slickensides> or displaced grains can record the sliding direction.

An opening <geological joint> need not contain any <fault rock>: it may be an open crack. If fluids circulated, it can contain a <mineral vein>, for example <calcite> or <quartz>, grown from the fracture walls. Thus **comminuted <fault rock> supports shear, while open space or a crack-filling <mineral vein> supports opening**. Mineral filling is conditional on fluid chemistry and timing; its precise composition cannot be inferred from the photograph.

Section B: Remote Sensing and Active Tectonics.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use calibrated <multispectral imaging> of reflected sunlight, with visible, near-infrared and short-wave-infrared bands. A satellite records <radiance> in several passbands for each ground pixel. Perform geometric registration and <atmospheric correction> to estimate <spectral reflectance>, then account for illumination differences on slopes and mask cloud, water and dense vegetation. A <false-colour composite>, diagnostic band ratios or a trained <statistical classification> can separate surface <lithologies>; field observations and representative spectra validate any mapped contact.

Different <minerals> have different absorption features: iron-bearing materials affect visible/near-infrared <spectral reflectance>, while hydroxyl-bearing <clay minerals> and carbonate-bearing rocks can differ in short-wave-infrared bands. Adjacent patches with reproducibly different spectra and coherent geological boundaries are candidates for <lithological contacts>. This is particularly useful in dry, exposed terrain. Vegetation, soil cover, similar rock compositions, weathering and topographic shadow can hide or mimic a contact; a colour boundary alone is insufficient.

Choose <spatial resolution> appreciably finer than the narrowest unit being mapped, with several pixels across that unit. Approximately $10$–$30\ \mathrm m$ is suitable for regional contacts separated by hundreds of metres; metre-scale units require finer imagery or field work. <Sentinel-2> provides $10$–$20\ \mathrm m$ bands useful for this purpose, while the reflected-light multispectral bands of <Landsat> are typically $30\ \mathrm m$. Broad multispectral passbands distinguish many rock assemblages but cannot resolve every narrow <mineral> absorption feature; <hyperspectral imaging> is preferable when those features are essential.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use repeat-pass <interferometric synthetic-aperture radar>. An orbiting radar transmits coherent microwave pulses and records the amplitude and phase of the returning field. Form an <interferogram> from registered complex images acquired before and after the <earthquake>, remove the orbital contribution and the topographic phase predicted by a <digital elevation model>, and correct atmospheric and other long-wavelength phase delays. Where <interferometric coherence> is adequate, unwrap the residual phase to estimate <line-of-sight displacement>:
$$
\Delta d_{\rm LOS}=-\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\Delta\phi,
$$
with sign set by the instrument's displacement convention. Each $2\pi$ phase cycle represents $\lambda/2$ of range change.

This maps a distributed <earthquake> displacement field day or night and through cloud, including areas inaccessible on foot. Roughly $10$–$30\ \mathrm m$ ground sampling and a scene spanning many kilometres on both sides of the <fault> can resolve regional deformation; a narrow surface break benefits from finer pixels or complementary sub-metre optical surveys. Sensitivity to displacement can be millimetres to centimetres in coherent areas, but that does not equal <spatial resolution>. A single viewing direction measures one component; ascending and descending tracks and <GNSS> constrain more components. At the rupture itself, large gradients and changed ground cover can destroy <interferometric coherence>; radar pixel-offset tracking or before/after optical correlation can then recover metre-scale <fault offsets>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**The rate of surface modification** controls how quickly a <fault scarp> is erased. Strong rainfall, runoff, slope failure and easily eroded <lithologies> can round or dissect a scarp; dry conditions and resistant rock often preserve it longer. <Alluvial-fan> deposition and <aggradation> can bury a scarp even if erosion is slow. A young marker surface can preserve an offset that an older, strongly modified surface no longer records.

**The balance between <fault-slip rate>, recurrence interval and marker age** controls whether renewed displacement outruns this modification. Rapid or repeated <normal faulting> can maintain a clear front, whereas a long interval after a small displacement permits smoothing. A very young fan deposited after the event may have no offset, while an older surface may retain cumulative rather than single-event <fault throw>. These two controls explain why absence of an obvious scarp is not proof of an inactive <fault>.

Section C: Geophysical Methods.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <gravity anomaly> is measured <gravitational acceleration>, reduced to a stated reference surface and compared with a reference <gravity> field. A local geological interpretation further removes or models the regional background. An absolute negative <Bouguer gravity anomaly> is therefore not itself a local negative <geophysical density contrast>.

Measure relative <gravity> with a calibrated <gravimeter> at stations across the profile, tie it to a base station, repeat base readings to estimate instrumental drift, and measure station coordinates and accurate elevations, for example by <GNSS> and levelling. Record observation times for tide corrections. Subtract the latitude-dependent reference <gravity>, correct instrument drift and Earth tides, add the <free-air correction> for station height, and subtract the attraction of the reference-density <Bouguer slab>. A terrain correction accounts for relief missing from or exceeding that slab. Use a consistent <density> such as $2670\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ for the topographic correction.<Geophysical Data Grids for the Conterminous United States by United States Geological Survey>[Gravity anomaly reduction: free-air, simple-Bouguer and complete-Bouguer corrections]{ref}

The question's reference to the anomaly in panel b is a panel-label slip: the upper plot contains <gravity>, while the lower plot supplies geometry and elevations. A geological slab thickness is the vertical thickness of the anomalous body, not simply its height above sea level.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a laterally extensive <Bouguer slab> of thickness $h$ and <geophysical density contrast> $\Delta\rho$,
$$
\Delta g=2\pi G\Delta\rho h,\qquad \rho_{\rm gs}=2670+\frac{\Delta g}{2\pi Gh}.
$$
The <greenstone belt> reaches from roughly $+1.0$ to $-0.5\ \mathrm{km}$ near its deepest central part, so use $h\simeq1500\ \mathrm m$. The <gravity> maximum is approximately $-100\ \mathrm{mGal}$; the exposed host-rock background in the south is approximately $-115\ \mathrm{mGal}$. Taking this as a constant regional reference gives a local excess $\Delta g\simeq15\ \mathrm{mGal}=1.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\rm gs}\simeq2670+\frac{1.5\times10^{-4}}{2\pi(6.6743\times10^{-11})(1500)}\simeq2.91\times10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}.
$$
This is an approximate <density> under a constant-background, infinite-slab model. The finite width, variable thickness and neighboring <pluton> require a fuller <gravity inversion> for a precise value. In particular, the absolute value $-100\ \mathrm{mGal}$ must not be inserted as the local excess.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Retain the same regional-background assumption as in part (b). The northern <pluton> is about $2.0\ \mathrm{km}$ thick beneath the station near the northern trough, while its <gravity anomaly> is about $-114\ \mathrm{mGal}$. Its anomaly relative to the greenstone maximum is approximately $-14\ \mathrm{mGal}$. Therefore the contrast equation is
$$
-14\times10^{-5}=2\pi G\{(\rho_{\rm gr}-2670)(2000)-(\rho_{\rm gs}-2670)(1500)\}.
$$
Using the result of part (b), the second term contributes $15\ \mathrm{mGal}$, leaving a net pluton anomaly of about $+1\ \mathrm{mGal}$. Hence **$\rho_{\rm gr}\simeq2680\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, effectively close to the host-rock <density> at the precision of this diagram**.

The apparent trough is strongly negative relative to the <greenstone belt>, but hardly negative relative to the southern host-rock background. If a northward regional trend is intended instead, it must be stated: in general,
$$
\rho_{\rm gr}\simeq2670+\frac{[-114-g_{\rm bg,N}]\,10^{-5}}{2\pi G(2000)},
$$
with both <gravity> values in <milligals>. For example, a northern background of $-109\ \mathrm{mGal}$ would give $\rho_{\rm gr}\simeq2610\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, but the same reference would also change part (b). No regional curve is explicitly identified in the supplied figure, so the numerical result must retain its reference-field assumption. This is the geological <nonuniqueness of geophysical inversion> of potential-field interpretation, rather than a reason to force a presumed textbook <granite> density onto the plot.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Measure total <magnetic field> along the same south–north line with a calibrated <magnetometer>, recording position, time and sensor height. A simultaneous <base-station magnetometer> measures temporal variation so that diurnal changes can be removed from the moving survey. Avoid disturbed periods and magnetic contamination from vehicles, fences, power lines and equipment. Choose station spacing much smaller than the expected anomaly width, for example tens to hundreds of metres for a kilometre-scale target.

Subtract the epoch-appropriate <International Geomagnetic Reference Field> or another stated regional main-field model to obtain a <total-field magnetic anomaly>. Level overlapping traverses and remove offsets or drift where appropriate. Accurate terrain/sensor geometry is needed for comparison with a geological model, but there is no universal magnetic analogue of a constant-density <Bouguer slab> correction: the effect depends on the distribution and direction of <magnetization>. Measure <magnetic susceptibility> and <remanent magnetization> on representative <greenstones>, <granite> and host rock to test the assumed magnetic sources.

=== e
{scope}

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Four groups have $\binom42=6$ pairwise comparisons. Testing each at <test size> $\alpha=0.05$ without adjustment generally increases the <familywise error rate>, the chance of at least one <type I error>. If the six rejection events were independent, that chance would be $1-(1-0.05)^6\simeq0.265$; shared samples make the actual comparisons dependent, so this expression is illustrative rather than exact.

A single omnibus <ANOVA> tests equality of all four means with one stated <test size>, avoiding that six-test inflation for the global question. If the aim is to identify differing pairs, follow with suitably adjusted comparisons, such as <Bonferroni correction> or a simultaneous pairwise method. A significant omnibus test does not automatically license six unadjusted claims. The benefit concerns control of <type I error>, rather than a guarantee that the model's independence and common-variance assumptions hold.

Match the initial uniform interior <temperature> $T_0$ using the <orthogonality> of the <Fourier sine series>:

b_n=\frac2L\int_0^LT_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=\frac{2T_0}{n\pi}\{1-(-1)^n\}.

Thus **$b_n=0$ for <even integers> $n$ and $b_n=4T_0/(n\pi)$ for <odd integers> $n$**, giving

For every $t>0$, the exponential factor makes the series and the derivatives needed in the <heat equation> converge uniformly on later time intervals. At $t=0$, the <Fourier sine series> equals $T_0$ at interior points but zero at the endpoints: the instantaneous cooling boundary condition is incompatible with the pre-immersion endpoint values. ==== Solution

After <bath cooling followed by insulated equilibration>, the remaining mean <temperature> is conserved: integrating the insulated <heat equation> gives
$$
\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_0^L U(x,\tau)\,dx=\kappa[U_x(L,\tau)-U_x(0,\tau)]=0.
$$
For constant <density>, specific <heat capacity> and cross-section, this is <conservation of energy>. Its <Fourier cosine series> has a constant mode equal to the initial <integral average>; every nonconstant mode decays. Therefore the eventual uniform <temperature> is
$$
T_f=\frac1L\int_0^LT(x,t_f)\,dx.
$$
For each mode with an <odd integer> index in part (c), $L^{-1}\int_0^L\sin((2j+1)\pi x/L)dx=2/[(2j+1)\pi]$. Integrating the bath-cooled series yields
$$
\boxed{T_f=\frac{8T_0}{\pi^2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2j+1)^2}\exp\left[-\frac{(2j+1)^2\pi^2\kappa t_f}{L^2}\right]}.
$$
For $t_f>0$, <uniform convergence> justifies termwise <integration>; the $t_f=0$ result also follows by dominated convergence of the summable positive coefficients. As a check, $\sum_{j\geq0}(2j+1)^{-2}=\pi^2/8$ gives $T_f=T_0$ for zero bath time, and $T_f\to0$ as $t_f\to\infty$. === a

==== Solution

<Gregor Mendel>'s first law is **<Mendelian segregation>**: the two <alleles> at a <genetic locus> in a <diploid> individual separate into different <gametes>, so each gamete receives one. In ordinary <meiosis>, separation of <homologous chromosomes> at <meiosis I> provides the chromosomal mechanism; their sister chromatids separate at <meiosis II>.

The second law is **<independent assortment>**: segregation at one <genetic locus> is independent of segregation at another when the loci are unlinked. Random orientation of different pairs of <homologous chromosomes> at <meiosis I> supplies the mechanism for genes on different chromosomes. <Genetic linkage> is an essential qualification: nearby loci on one <chromosome> do not generally assort independently, although sufficiently frequent <genetic recombination> can make their observed combinations approach independence. <Genetic dominance> concerns the <phenotype> of a <heterozygote> and is not one of these two segregation laws.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The **<biological species concept>** groups natural populations that actually or potentially interbreed and are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Its central criterion is <reproductive isolation> and <gene flow>, rather than visible similarity. It is hard to apply directly to <fossils>, organisms reproducing strictly by <asexual reproduction>, and geographically separated populations whose potential interbreeding cannot be tested.

The **<phenetic species concept>** groups organisms by overall similarity in measured <phenotypic traits>. A practical <morphological species concept> uses anatomical form as its main evidence; strict phenetic classification can combine many kinds of measured traits. This makes such approaches usable for preserved specimens and much of the <fossil record>, but the decision depends on which traits are measured and how similarities are weighted.

At least four difficulties arise. <Cryptic species> may be reproductively isolated while looking nearly identical, so similarity can lump separate lineages. Conversely, <sexual dimorphism>, juvenile/adult differences and <phenotypic plasticity> can make one interbreeding <species> look like several, causing over-splitting. <Convergent evolution> can make distantly related organisms resemble one another in ecologically selected traits. Finally, continuous geographic or temporal variation supplies no unique gap at which to place a boundary: character choice, sample size, preservation and similarity thresholds can alter the inferred <species delimitation>. These are distinct from the biological concept's practical difficulty of testing actual interbreeding.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Allopatric speciation>** begins when a geographic barrier divides a population and reduces <gene flow>. Independent <mutation>, <genetic drift> and divergent <natural selection> can then build <reproductive isolation>; if populations later meet, they remain distinct when isolation is sufficiently strong. Sister lineages of snapping shrimp in the genus <Alpheus> on opposite sides of the Isthmus of Panama illustrate the role of separated marine populations. Their divergences are not all synchronous with the final closure: genetic data indicate several separation histories, so one barrier date should not be assigned indiscriminately to every pair.<New dates and new rates for divergence across the Isthmus of Panama by Nancy Knowlton and others>[Abstract: comparisons of 15 trans-isthmian pairs and heterogeneous divergence]{ref}

**<Sympatric speciation>** develops without a geographic barrier separating the initial population. Divergent ecological selection combined with <assortative mating>, habitat-associated mating, different reproductive times or a chromosomal change can reduce <gene flow> within a shared area. The palms <Howea forsteriana> and <Howea belmoreana> on Lord Howe Island provide a documented candidate: different soil associations and substantially different flowering times contribute to <reproductive isolation>, and their sister relationship and timing support divergence on the island.<Sympatric speciation in palms on an oceanic island by Vincent Savolainen and others>[Abstract and Figures 2–3: flowering-time and soil associations]{ref}

The distinction concerns the geography of divergence, not whether present-day ranges overlap. Present coexistence alone cannot distinguish <sympatric speciation> from earlier <allopatric speciation> followed by range expansion. <Parapatric speciation> describes divergence between adjacent populations with limited exchange and provides an intermediate geographical category; the two main modes requested are the allopatric and sympatric endpoints.

==== Solution

A **<mass extinction>** is a geologically brief, geographically widespread loss of a large fraction of <species> across multiple major groups, substantially exceeding background <extinction> rates. Severity, global extent and rapidity distinguish it from the disappearance of a local population or the ordinary loss of individual lineages. A threshold near $75\%$ of species is often used operationally, but the qualitative definition does not depend on one universal numerical cutoff. The duration is short on geological timescales, and need not mean that every species disappeared simultaneously in a single day.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <mass extinction> is driven by changes that overwhelm organisms' physiological tolerances or destroy their food and habitat systems. Distinguish the initiating forcing from the linked environmental mechanisms: one event can generate several contributors.

* **Large extraterrestrial impact.** At the <Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary>, the <Chicxulub impact> injected dust, aerosols and soot that reduced incoming sunlight and <photosynthesis>, disrupting <food webs>; rapid heating, fires and subsequent cooling added stresses. The impact is the trigger, while darkness and loss of primary production are proximate mechanisms.
* **Large-scale <volcanism>.** Eruption and intrusion of <large igneous provinces>, notably the <Siberian Traps> around the end-<Permian> crisis, release or mobilize gases and can produce rapid <climate change>. Carbon dioxide favors warming; sulfur-bearing emissions can cause shorter cooling and acid deposition. The outcome depends on rate, duration and interacting reservoirs, rather than eruption volume alone.
* **Ocean <anoxia> and <euxinia>.** Warming lowers oxygen solubility and can strengthen stratification; altered nutrient supply increases oxygen demand. Oxygen loss removes habitable marine volume, while sulfide in anoxic waters can be toxic. These processes contributed to the end-<Permian> marine crisis and can reinforce warming-driven mortality.
* **Ocean <ocean acidification> and elevated carbon dioxide.** Rapid addition of carbon dioxide changes seawater carbonate chemistry and can impair <biomineralization>, while high carbon dioxide also challenges physiological acid–base regulation. Susceptibility varies with physiology; this is a linked mechanism rather than proof that every calcifying group responds identically.
* **Rapid cooling and <eustatic sea level change>.** Late <Ordovician> glaciation and regression reduced shallow-marine habitat and changed temperatures and circulation; subsequent warming and oxygen loss introduced a different set of stresses. Habitat loss and climate change can therefore contribute in successive phases of one crisis.

These examples supply more than four drivers or contributors. The historical effects of <volcanism>, warming, oxygen loss and elevated carbon dioxide can act together rather than compete as mutually exclusive explanations.<Paleophysiology and end-Permian mass extinction by Andrew H. Knoll and others>[Abstract and introduction: linked physiological effects of warming, anoxia, sulfide and hypercapnia]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Extinction selectivity>** means that the probability of <extinction> depends systematically on biological or ecological traits, rather than being equal across all <species>. An empirical association needs a specified event, group and taxonomic level; a trait protective in ordinary background turnover may not protect during every <mass extinction>.

**Geographic range:** at the end-<Cretaceous> crisis, geographically widespread marine <bivalve> genera had lower extinction intensity than narrowly distributed genera. This makes range breadth a demonstrated selective trait for that dataset. The same analysis found no clear selectivity by body size, so that result should not be generalized to every trait or event.<Selectivity of end-Cretaceous marine bivalve extinctions by David Jablonski and others>[Abstract: geographic-range selectivity and absence of body-size selectivity]{ref}

**<Body size>:** large-bodied <mammals> suffered disproportionately during late-<Pleistocene> and later Quaternary losses, including the loss of many giant herbivores and carnivores. A global analysis over the last $125000$ years found strong size-selective losses across continents and trophic modes. Large size can correlate with slow reproduction, high resource demand and exposure to human hunting, but the observed size pattern and its possible mechanisms should be distinguished.<Body size downgrading of mammals over the late Quaternary by Felisa Smith and others>[Abstract: size-selective extinction across continents, trophic modes and intervals]{ref}

**<Sexual dimorphism> and reproductive investment:** a study of fossil cytheroid <ostracods> found much higher estimated extinction rates in <species> with stronger male–female carapace dimorphism, interpreted as greater male investment in reproduction. This supplies a third empirical trait association; the observed relationship is not a universal claim that <sexual selection> is always harmful.<High male sexual investment as a driver of extinction in fossil ostracods by Maria João Fernandes Martins and others>[Abstract and main result: approximately tenfold rate contrast between high- and low-investment species]{ref}

The examples concern different time intervals and levels, including genera in the first example and species in the third. They demonstrate <extinction selectivity> without assuming one set of trait effects is universal across all crises.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Extinction debt>** is future <extinction> committed by past environmental change but not yet realized because populations persist temporarily. After habitat loss or fragmentation, surviving adults and small isolated populations can remain visible even when successful recruitment and recolonization are too low for long-term persistence. Long generation times can delay the observed species loss.<Habitat destruction and the extinction debt by David Tilman and others>[Abstract: extinctions delayed for generations following habitat fragmentation]{ref}

Thus current <species richness> can overstate the diversity sustainable under present habitat conditions. Restoration or renewed connectivity before the delayed losses occur may avert part of the debt; the concept is a lagged consequence of past change, rather than a prediction that every threatened population is irreversibly doomed.
