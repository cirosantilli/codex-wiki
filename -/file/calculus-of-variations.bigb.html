= Calculus of variations
{wiki}

Calculus of variations finds <stationary points> of <functionals>, often among <functions> or <regular curves>.

= Log-cosh energy on a Sobolev space
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{title2=$I(v)=\int_\Omega[|\nabla v|^2/2+\log\cosh v]$}

The inequalities $0\le\log\cosh t\le t^2/2$ make this energy finite on $H^1$. Since the second <derivative> of its <scalar> potential is $\operatorname{sech}^2t\in(0,1]$, its <Fréchet derivative> is $DI(v)[h]=\int(\nabla v\cdot\nabla h+\tanh(v)h)$, with <Taylor remainder> between zero and half the squared $H^1$ <norm> of $h$. The <derivative> operator is strictly monotone because the <hyperbolic tangent> is strictly increasing.

= Weierstrass excess function
{c}
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{title2=$E(x,y,p,q)=f(x,y,q)-f(x,y,p)-f_v(x,y,p)(q-p)$}

This excess measures the height of the slope-dependent integrand above its tangent line at the reference slope $p$, evaluated at a trial slope $q$. A <convex function> of slope has nonnegative excess. At a smooth strong local minimizer, localized needle variations force nonnegative excess along the minimizing curve for every trial slope. It detects fast-gradient competitors that the <second variation> around a fixed derivative can miss.

= Weierstrass necessary condition
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass excess function}
{title2=$E(x,u,u',v)\geq0$}

At a <strong local minimizer of a variational integral>, almost every reference slope $u'(x)$ is a <point of convexity> of $v\mapsto f(x,u(x),v)$. Equivalently its <Weierstrass excess function> is nonnegative for every trial slope. Uniformly small variations can have large derivatives, which is why this condition tests all trial slopes rather than only nearby ones. It is a necessary condition along the minimizing curve; the <strengthened Weierstrass condition> additionally controls a neighborhood of that curve and its slopes.

= Bounded slope integrands force strong minima to be global
{parent=Weierstrass necessary condition}
{title2=$I(u)=\int g(u'),\quad g\in C^1(\mathbb R)\text{ bounded}$}

Suppose the <Weierstrass necessary condition> applies to a strong local minimum of this fixed-endpoint integral. It requires the tangent to $g$ at $u'(x)$ to lie below the entire graph for almost every $x$. A line of nonzero slope is unbounded above in one direction, while $g$ is bounded; hence this supporting line has slope zero. Its contact value is then a <global minimum> of $g$. The resulting <function> minimizes the integral globally, since its integrand is almost everywhere equal to the global lower bound. Thus for a bounded differentiable slope-only integrand a strong local minimum can exist only at global energy level. Weak local minima can behave differently because their competitors have nearby derivatives.

= Strengthened Weierstrass condition
{parent=Weierstrass excess function}
{title2=$E(x,y,p,q)\geq0$}

For a $C^1$ curve $u$, some $\delta>0$ must make the <Weierstrass excess function> nonnegative for every $x\in[a,b]$, every trial slope $q\in\mathbb R$, and all $y,p$ satisfying $|y-u(x)|<\delta$, $|p-u'(x)|<\delta$. Thus the supporting-line property persists in a tube of nearby heights and reference slopes. The term “strengthened” here refers to this neighborhood requirement; strict excess for $q\ne p$ is a further condition.

= Weierstrass sufficient condition
{c}
{parent=Strengthened Weierstrass condition}

Under the usual $C^2$ integrand regularity, an admissible $C^1$ curve satisfying the weak <Euler-Lagrange equation>, a coercively positive <second variation>, and the <strengthened Weierstrass condition> is a <strong local minimizer of a variational integral>. Strict excess away from the reference slope gives strict minimality. Coercive positivity means $\delta^2I(u)[\eta,\eta]\geq c\|\eta\|_{W^{1,2}}^2$ for every zero-trace variation and some $c>0$.

The <second variation> provides a neighboring field of extremals with slope $p(x,y)$. The associated endpoint-invariant Hilbert integral yields $I(v)-I(u)=\int E(x,v,p(x,v),v')\,dx$ for uniformly close admissible $v$. The tube condition makes this integral nonnegative even when $v'$ is large, explaining the passage from weak to strong minimality.

= Strong local minimizer of a variational integral
{parent=Calculus of variations}

For a fixed-endpoint variational integral, an admissible function $u$ is a strong local minimizer if $I(v)\geq I(u)$ for all admissible $v$ whose <uniform norm> distance from $u$ is sufficiently small. Derivatives can be far apart. Hence strong local minimality implies <weak local minimizer of a variational integral>[weak local minimality], while a global minimizer implies both. Small narrow bumps and rapid low-amplitude oscillations explain why the reverse implication can fail.

= Coercivity transfers strong minimality to L1 minimality
{parent=Strong local minimizer of a variational integral}
{title2=$f(x,u,v)\geq c|v|^p-C,\quad p>1$}

With fixed endpoint values on a finite interval, the displayed <derivative> <coercivity> makes a finite-energy <strong local minimizer of a variational integral> also a local minimizer in $L^1$. Otherwise choose better competitors converging in $L^1$. The upper energy bound controls their derivatives in $L^p$, and a common endpoint controls their function norms. Compact <Sobolev embedding> gives a uniformly convergent subsequence; its limit is the $L^1$ limit. This contradicts strong local minimality. <Convexity> of the integrand is unnecessary for this neighborhood-transfer argument.

= Weak local minimizer of a variational integral
{parent=Calculus of variations}

For a fixed-endpoint integral on $W^{1,\infty}(a,b)$, an admissible function $u$ is a weak local minimizer if $I(v)\geq I(u)$ whenever the <Sobolev norm> $\|v-u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}$ is sufficiently small. Both function and derivative must be close. “Weak” here labels the variational neighborhood convention, not the <weak topology> of a <Banach space>.

= Periodic slopes give weak minima without strong minima
{parent=Weak local minimizer of a variational integral}
{title2=$g(v)=-\cos(\pi v/2),\quad v_1=\delta,\ v_2=\delta-4$}

For $0<\delta<1$, choose the zero-trace piecewise affine curve with slope $\delta$ up to $1-\delta/4$ and slope $\delta-4$ thereafter. Periodicity makes $g'$ equal to the same nonzero constant at both slopes, and $g''>0$ on small neighborhoods of both. For sufficiently small Lipschitz perturbations, local <strict convexity> bounds the energy difference below by $c\int\phi'^2$, since the common first-variation coefficient multiplies $\int\phi'=0$. This proves a strict weak minimum. The global supporting-tangent condition fails because its nonzero slope cannot stay below bounded $g$, so it is not a strong minimum. By contrast, tents of slopes $4n,-4n$ attain $g=-1$ everywhere and provide infinitely many distinct global minimizers.

= Length extremals from a constant quadratic energy
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{title2=$L=\dot q^Tg(q)\dot q,\quad\ell=\sqrt L$}

For an autonomous smooth <Lagrangian> homogeneous of degree two in velocities, the <Beltrami identity> gives $\dot q\cdot L_{\dot q}-L=L$ constant on its <Euler-Lagrange equations>[Euler-Lagrange] extremals. On a regular segment with this constant positive, the Euler-Lagrange expression for $\sqrt L$ equals that for $L$ divided by $2\sqrt L$, since the extra term is proportional to $\dot L$. Hence an energy extremal has the same path as a length extremal. Length is invariant under regular reparametrization: in two coordinates, on a graph $v=v(u)$, its density is $\sqrt{E+2Fv_u+Gv_u^2}\,|du|$. If a nonnegative semidefinite energy vanishes along a curve, the curve instead minimizes length at zero; division by $\sqrt L$ is not permitted there. For a positive definite metric, regular nonconstant curves have positive energy.

= Euler-Lagrange equation for a second-derivative functional
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{c}
{title2=$F_y-(F_{y'})'+(F_{y''})''=0$}

For a sufficiently smooth <functional> $I[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y',y'')\,dx$, the <first variation> in direction $\eta$ is $\int(F_y\eta+F_{y'}\eta'+F_{y''}\eta'')dx$. Twice applying <integration by parts> gives the interior coefficient $F_y-(F_{y'})'+(F_{y''})''$ and boundary expression $[(F_{y'}-(F_{y''})')\eta+F_{y''}\eta']_a^b$. Variations supported in the interior and the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> force the displayed equation. The remaining boundary terms give the <natural boundary conditions for second-derivative functionals>.

= Clamped fourth-order variational extremal on a quarter period
{parent=Euler-Lagrange equation for a second-derivative functional}
{title2=$J[y]=\int_0^{\pi/2}(y''^2-y^2+x^2)dx$}

With values $y(0)=y(\pi/2)=y'(\pi/2)=0$ and $y'(0)=1$, the <higher-order Euler-Lagrange equation> is $y^{(4)}-y=0$. Write $c=\cosh(\pi/2)$ and $s=\sinh(\pi/2)$. Solving the four linear boundary equations gives $y=[c(\cos x-\cosh x)+(1-s)\sin x+(1+s)\sinh x]/2$. The added function $x^2$ does not affect the <first variation>.

= Beltrami identity for a second-derivative functional
{parent=Euler-Lagrange equation for a second-derivative functional}
{c}
{title2=$H=y''F_{y''}+y'(F_{y'}-(F_{y''})')-F,\qquad H'=-F_x$}

For a smooth <functional> $\int F(x,y,y',y'')\,dx$, let $P=F_{y'}$ and $Q=F_{y''}$. Along an <extremal>, the <Euler-Lagrange equation for a second-derivative functional> is $F_y-P'+Q''=0$. Differentiating $H=y''Q+y'(P-Q')-F$ by the <chain rule> gives
$$
H'=y'(P'-Q''-F_y)-F_x=-F_x.
$$
Consequently $H$ is constant whenever $F$ has no explicit dependence on $x$. This extends the <Beltrami identity> to a second-derivative variational problem. If $F$ is independent of $y''$, the expression reduces to $y'F_{y'}-F$.

= Natural boundary conditions for second-derivative functionals
{parent=Euler-Lagrange equation for a second-derivative functional}
{title2=$F_{y'}-(F_{y''})'=0,\quad F_{y''}=0$}

At an endpoint of a second-derivative variational problem, a free value $y$ gives the first displayed condition, while a free slope $y'$ gives the second. Each condition is required only when its corresponding endpoint variation is free; a prescribed value or slope instead forces that variation to vanish. For $F=K(y'')^2/2+qy$, the two conditions become $y'''=0$ and $y''=0$, respectively. A clamped beam has both value and slope fixed, a pinned beam fixes value but leaves slope free, and a free end leaves both free.

= Extremal
{parent=Calculus of variations}

A sufficiently regular admissible <function> at which the <first variation> of a <functional> vanishes for every permitted <variation>. An <extremal> is a stationary candidate; it need not be a local maximum or minimum. For a first-order integral <functional> with fixed endpoint values, it satisfies the <Euler-Lagrange equation> under the usual regularity assumptions.

= Variational principle
{parent=Calculus of variations}

A variational principle specifies a <function>, path or field by requiring a <functional> to be stationary under an allowed class of <variations>. The admissible class includes the prescribed <boundary conditions> and <constraints>. A stationary value need not be a <minimum>: the <Euler-Lagrange equation> expresses the first-order stationarity condition, while a minimum requires additional analysis. <Fermat principle> applies this idea to optical path length, and the <Rayleigh-Ritz method> approximates a variational problem in a chosen family of <trial functions>.

= Variational projection onto an involutive symmetry
{parent=Variational principle}
{title2=$\mathcal E_\pm=(a\pm c)/(1\pm b)$}

Let $H$ be a lower-bounded self-adjoint <Hamiltonian operator> and $I$ a commuting self-adjoint operator with $I^2=1$. For a normalized trial vector $\phi$, define $a=\langle H\rangle_\phi$, $b=\langle I\rangle_\phi$, $c=\langle HI\rangle_\phi$. The <Rayleigh quotient> on $\phi+tI\phi$ is $[(1+t^2)a+2tc]/[1+t^2+2tb]$, with derivative $2(c-ab)(1-t^2)/(1+t^2+2tb)^2$. Unless $c=ab$, its extrema are the normalized nonzero vectors $\phi\pm I\phi$, with energies $(a\pm c)/(1\pm b)$. Their difference is $2(c-ab)/(1-b^2)$, identifying the maximum and minimum.

The orthogonal projections $(1\pm I)/2$ split any trial energy into a weighted mean of the two sector energies, so one nonzero sector projection never raises the trial energy. If $|b|=1$, one projected vector is zero and its quotient is undefined; the remaining family has constant energy. Exchange symmetry makes these sectors bosonic and fermionic spatial wavefunctions. Stationarity within this two-dimensional trial space does not generally make a vector an exact eigenvector of $H$.

= Quadratic minor null Lagrangian
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{title2=$K_{k\gamma}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\epsilon_{ijk}u_{i,\alpha}u_{j,\beta}$}

For constant $K$, this quadratic combination of two-by-two gradient minors is a <divergence>. Indeed,
$$
K_{k\gamma}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\epsilon_{ijk}\overline{u_{i,\alpha}}u_{j,\beta}=\partial_\alpha\left(K_{k\gamma}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\epsilon_{ijk}\overline{u_i}u_{j,\beta}\right),
$$
because the term containing $u_{j,\beta\alpha}$ vanishes by antisymmetry of the <Levi-Civita symbol>. Its volume integral vanishes for homogeneous displacement boundary data. It also vanishes pointwise on <rank-one matrices>, since $\nu_\alpha\nu_\beta$ is symmetric. Consequently one can strengthen an elastic energy density by such a term without changing fixed-boundary incremental energies or <acoustic tensors>.

= Brachistochrone problem
{parent=Calculus of variations}

The brachistochrone problem asks for the curve along which a particle released from rest reaches a specified target fastest under a uniform gravitational field, without friction. With downward depth $u$, conservation of energy gives speed $\sqrt{2gu}$ and travel time $\int\sqrt{(1+u'^2)/(2gu)}\,dx$. The <Beltrami identity> makes $u(1+u'^2)$ constant, leading to a <cycloid>. If only the horizontal endpoint coordinate $x_0>0$ is fixed, the <natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint> select the half arch ending at its deepest point, with depth $2x_0/\pi$ and minimum time $\sqrt{\pi x_0/g}$.

= Fixed-length brachistochrone between equal-height endpoints
{parent=Brachistochrone problem}
{title2=$L>1,\quad b<1,\quad c>0$}

For horizontal separation one, let $y>0$ be downward depth and constrain the <arc length> to $L$. <Conservation of energy> makes the <travel time> proportional to $\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}/\sqrt y\,dx$. An <isoperimetric constraint> adds a <Lagrange multiplier> $\lambda$, and the <Beltrami identity> gives $(y^{-1/2}+\lambda)/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=H>0$. With $b=\lambda/H$, $c=H^2$ and $s=\sin(\phi/2)$, the stationary arch is $y=s^2/[c(1-bs)^2]$, $dx/d\phi=s^2/[c(1-bs)^3]$, for $0\le\phi\le2\pi$. The endpoint and length integrals fix $b,c$ uniquely for $L>1$; symmetry gives $x(2\pi-\phi)=1-x(\phi)$. At $L=1$ the only curve is horizontal and a particle released from rest never completes the journey. The finite-time stationary arches have vertical endpoint tangents, so the graph is smooth only on the open interval. These first-variation equations alone do not establish global minimality in a larger admissible class.

= Brachistochrone on a hemispherical ramp
{parent=Brachistochrone problem}
{title2=$\phi'^2=C^2\cos\theta/[\sin^2\theta(\sin^2\theta-C^2\cos\theta)]$}

Measure polar angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical on the lower hemisphere of radius $R$. A particle released from rest at the rim and sliding without energy loss has speed $\sqrt{2gR\cos\theta}$. The spherical <arc length> along $\phi=\phi(\theta)$ is $R\sqrt{1+\sin^2\theta\,\phi'^2}|d\theta|$. Thus its <travel time> has integrand proportional to $\sqrt{(1+\sin^2\theta\,\phi'^2)/\cos\theta}$. Since the azimuth is a <cyclic coordinate>, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives
$$
\frac{\sin^2\theta\,\phi'}{\sqrt{\cos\theta}\sqrt{1+\sin^2\theta\,\phi'^2}}=C.
$$
Solving for $\phi'^2$ gives the displayed <first integral> on a segment where $\theta$ is a valid path parameter. For finite $C$, approaching the rim $\theta\uparrow\pi/2$ gives $\phi'\to0$. Thus a regular stationary path begins along the meridian toward the bottom. This is an initial direction condition; a path joining other rim points can turn before reaching the bottom. Monotone segments use positive $|d\theta|$ in the time integral, and a turning point requires another path parameter or a split into segments.

= Maximum-depth bound for a brachistochrone
{parent=Brachistochrone problem}
{title2=$T\geq[l/\sqrt h+\pi\sqrt h]/\sqrt{2g}\geq\sqrt{2\pi l/g}$}

Consider a frictionless track from rest between equal-height endpoints separated horizontally by $l>0$. Let depth $y$ be positive downward and let the maximum depth be $h>0$. <Conservation of energy> gives speed $\sqrt{2gy}$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, applied to the tangent displacement, gives
$$
\frac{ds}{\sqrt y}\geq\frac{dx}{\sqrt h}+\sqrt{\frac{h-y}{hy}}\,|dy|.
$$
The squared norm of the coefficient vector is $1/h+(h-y)/(hy)=1/y$. The track goes from depth zero to $h$ and returns to zero, so the second term integrates to at least $2\int_0^h\sqrt{(h-y)/(hy)}dy=\pi\sqrt h$. Integrating the first term gives $l/\sqrt h$, even if the track backtracks horizontally. Division by $\sqrt{2g}$ proves the first bound. The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> proves the second.

Equality requires $h=l/\pi$ and tangent slope $|dy/dx|=\sqrt{(h-y)/y}$, on the descending and ascending halves respectively. Substitution $y=h\sin^2\theta$ integrates to the <cycloid> $x=h(\theta-\frac12\sin2\theta)$, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$. It attains the bound, proving global minimality of the single-arch <brachistochrone problem> between these endpoints. Endpoint singularities are handled as limits of the integrals; the cycloid has finite travel time.

= Brachistochrone on a horizontal half-cylinder
{parent=Brachistochrone problem}
{title2=$z'(\theta)=RK\sqrt{\cos\theta}/\sqrt{1-K^2\cos\theta}$}

Measure $\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ from the vertically downward direction on the lower half of a cylinder of radius $R$, and let $z$ be axial distance. From rest at an edge, <conservation of energy> gives speed $\sqrt{2gR\cos\theta}$. The <travel time> is $T=(2gR)^{-1/2}\int\sqrt{R^2+z'^2}/\sqrt{\cos\theta}\,d\theta$. Since $z$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, its conjugate quantity $z'/[\sqrt{\cos\theta}\sqrt{R^2+z'^2}]=K$ is constant, giving the displayed formula. For finite positive axial separation, $0<K<1$ is fixed uniquely by integrating $z'$ between the edges: the separation grows strictly from zero to infinity as $K$ increases from zero to one. The time integrand is strictly convex in $z'$, so its supporting-line inequality proves global minimality for the fixed endpoints. As the starting edge is approached, $z'\to0$: the initial direction is directly down the curved surface, with no axial component. This differs from the constant-pitch shortest <geodesic> on the unrolled cylinder.

= Travel-time minimization with speed proportional to squared radius
{parent=Calculus of variations}

For speed $v=kr^2$ in the punctured plane, the travel-time element is $ds/(kr^2)$. Under the <inversion in a circle> with radial coordinate $u=1/r$, this becomes $k^{-1}\sqrt{du^2+u^2d\theta^2}$, the <arc length> in <Euclidean space> divided by $k$. Thus a shortest travel-time route corresponds to a straight <line segment> in the inverted plane, whenever that segment avoids the origin and lies in the allowed region. Between $(a,0)$ and $(0,a)$ the inverted segment has equation $u(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)=1/a$. The route is $r=a(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)$, and its minimum travel time is $\sqrt2/(ka)$. The <triangle inequality> for <arc length> proves global minimality.

= Gamma-convergence
{c}
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{title2=$F_n\xrightarrow{\Gamma}F$}

A sequence of functionals Gamma-converges in a specified <topology> when every convergent $u_n\to u$ satisfies $F(u)\le\liminf_nF_n(u_n)$, and each $u$ has a recovery sequence $u_n\to u$ with $F_n(u_n)\to F(u)$. With appropriate compactness of energy sublevels, limits of approximate global <minimizers> minimize $F$, and the minimum values converge. This is suited to variational approximation such as the <Ambrosio–Tortorelli approximation>; it does not describe arbitrary local minima.

= Variational problem
{parent=Calculus of variations}

A variational problem seeks a stationary point or minimizer of a <functional> on a specified <function space>. For a symmetric <coercive bilinear form> $a$ and bounded <linear functional> $\ell$ on a real <Hilbert space>, minimizing $J(v)=a(v,v)/2-\ell(v)$ is equivalent to the <weak formulation> $a(u,v)=\ell(v)$ for every test vector. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> gives a unique solution, and $J(u+w)-J(u)=a(w,w)/2$ proves unique minimization. Specifying the admissible space and its boundary constraints is part of the problem.

= Direct method in the calculus of variations
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{wiki}

The direct method takes a minimizing sequence, extracts a subsequence using compactness, and uses lower semicontinuity to show that its limit attains the infimum.

= Lavrentiev phenomenon
{c}
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}
{title2=$\inf_{W^{1,1}}I<\inf_{W^{1,\infty}}I$}

A Lavrentiev gap is a strict change in an <infimum> when the admissible regularity class is restricted, despite retaining the same endpoint values. For example $I(u)=\int_0^1(u^3-x)^2u'^6dx$ with $u(0)=0,u(1)=1$ has value zero at the absolutely continuous function $u=x^{1/3}$. Every Lipschitz competitor has a uniform positive lower energy bound. Indeed its ratio to $x^{1/3}$ starts at zero, so let $x_0$ be its first crossing of $u(x)=x^{1/3}/2$. Before that point, $|u^3-x|\geq7x/8$. <Holder inequality> gives
$$
\left(\frac{x_0^{1/3}}2\right)^6\leq\left(\int_0^{x_0}x^2|u'|^6dx\right)\left(\frac53x_0^{3/5}\right)^5.
$$
Thus $I(u)\geq(49/4096)(3/5)^5/x_0\geq(49/4096)(3/5)^5>0$. This proves a gap directly. The example's integrand lacks a uniform superlinear lower bound along its zero set; it illustrates the admissible-class issue rather than by itself furnishing a counterexample under stronger coercivity assumptions.

= Power-law comparison for vanishing derivative regularization
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}
{title2=$\min I_\varepsilon=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$}

For $I_\varepsilon(u)=\int_0^1[(u^4-x)^2+\varepsilon|u'|^{4/3}]dx$ with endpoint values zero and one, the zero-penalty minimizer $x^{1/4}$ has infinite <derivative> penalty. Use $u=x^{1/4+\delta}$ instead. The mismatch is at most $(32/27)\delta^2$, while the <derivative> penalty is $(3\varepsilon/(4\delta))(1/4+\delta)^{4/3}$. Balancing $\delta^2$ and $\varepsilon/\delta$ by $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/3}$ gives an upper energy bound of order $\varepsilon^{2/3}$. Nonnegativity then proves convergence of minimum energies to zero. This is a recovery construction; no claim of a matching lower asymptotic constant is needed.

= Tonelli existence theorem
{c}
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}

For a scalar fixed-endpoint problem on a bounded interval, suppose the continuous integrand $f(x,y,p)$ is convex in $p$ and satisfies $f(x,y,p)\geq\Theta(|p|)-C$, where $\Theta$ is nonnegative convex and $\Theta(r)/r\to\infty$. If there is one admissible absolutely continuous function of finite energy, a global minimizer exists in $W^{1,1}$.

The lower bound gives uniform integrability of derivatives in a minimizing sequence. Fixed endpoint values then give boundedness and equicontinuity of the functions. Extract uniform convergence of the functions and weak $L^1$ convergence of their derivatives; the limit retains the endpoint values. Convexity in the derivative gives <weak lower semicontinuity of convex gradient energies>, so the limit attains the infimum. Strict convexity of the full integrand on a convex admissible class additionally gives uniqueness.

= Weak lower semicontinuity of convex gradient energies
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}
{title2=$u_k\rightharpoonup u\text{ in }H^1\Longrightarrow \int F(Du)\leq\liminf_k\int F(Du_k)$}

Suppose the domain has finite measure and $F\in C^1$ is convex, with $|F(p)|\leq C(1+|p|^2)$ and $|DF(p)|\leq C(1+|p|)$. The supporting inequality $F(p)\geq F(q)+DF(q)\cdot(p-q)$, integrated with $q=Du$, gives $\int F(Du_k)\geq\int F(Du)+\int DF(Du)\cdot(Du_k-Du)$. The last term tends to zero by weak convergence of the gradients and the fixed $L^2$ test field $DF(Du)$. This proves weak lower semicontinuity without requiring pointwise convergence of gradients.

= Symmetric elliptic Dirichlet energy with a nonpositive potential
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}
{title2=$J(u)=\tfrac12\int(A\nabla u\cdot\nabla u-qu^2)+\int fu$}

For a bounded domain, bounded symmetric uniformly elliptic $A$, $q\leq0$, and $f\in L^2$, minimizing $J$ on $\psi+H_0^1$ solves $\operatorname{div}(A\nabla u)+qu=f$. The nonnegative quadratic terms are weakly lower semicontinuous, and <Poincare inequality> makes the gradient norm coercive on the zero-boundary part. The first variation gives the weak equation with the forcing sign displayed above. If $q$ is positive, coercivity may fail at a Dirichlet eigenvalue; orthogonality to the homogeneous kernel is then needed for solvability.

= Screened sine-Gordon energy
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}
{title2=$E[u]=\int_{\mathbb R^3}(\tfrac12|\nabla u|^2+\tfrac12u^2+1-\cos u-fu)\,dx$}

For real $f\in L^2(\mathbb R^3)$, this <energy functional> on $H^1(\mathbb R^3)$ is coercive and attains its minimum by the <direct method in the calculus of variations>. The nonnegative potential term is weakly lower semicontinuous by <local Sobolev compactness gives lower semicontinuity of a nonnegative integral>. Its <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $-\Delta u+u+\sin u=f$ in the <weak solution> sense. The extra linear restoring term screens the <sine-Gordon equation> nonlinearity. The scalar potential $V(s)=s^2/2+1-\cos s$ is convex because $V''(s)=1+\cos s\geq0$; hence any weak critical point is a minimizer. The <elliptic regularity> estimate for $-\Delta+1$, together with $|\sin u|\leq|u|$, gives $u\in H^2$. The <Sobolev inequality> then puts $u$ in $W^{1,6}$, so <Morrey's inequality> and <uniformly continuous integrable functions vanish at infinity> give a continuous representative tending to zero.

= Failure of the source-size bound for the screened sine-Gordon equation
{parent=Screened sine-Gordon energy}
{title2=$\|u\|_\infty>\|f\|_\infty$}

Take $a=3\pi/2$, $L=10$ and $u(x)=a\exp(-|x|^2/L^2)$ on $\mathbb R^3$. Set $f=-\Delta u+u+\sin u$. Both functions are smooth and belong to <L2 space>, and $u\in H^1$. The strictly increasing reaction $h(s)=s+\sin s$ has $0\leq h(u)\leq h(a)=a-1$, while
$$
|\Delta u|=\frac{a}{L^2}|4q-6|e^{-q}\leq\frac{6a}{L^2},\qquad q=|x|^2/L^2.
$$
Consequently $\|f\|_\infty\leq a-1+6a/L^2<a=\|u\|_\infty$. This $u$ is a minimizer of the <screened sine-Gordon energy>: it solves the <Euler-Lagrange equation> and that energy is convex. Thus even the minimizing and smooth hypotheses do not justify the stronger source-size bound. The appropriate general estimate is <maximum bound for a monotone reaction term>.

= Minimizing sequence
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}

A <minimizing sequence> satisfies $E(u_n)\to\inf E$. <Coercivity> bounds an appropriate <sublevel set>, <Compactness> supplies a <convergent subsequence>, and <sequential lower semicontinuity> turns its limit into a <global minimizer>.

= Oscillating minimizing sequence with no minimizer
{parent=Minimizing sequence}
{title2=$u_N(x)=2\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z)$}

A <minimizing sequence> can converge uniformly while its <derivatives> keep oscillating between distinct energy wells. For the <functional> $I(u)=\int_0^1[u^4+(u'^4-16)^4]$, with zero endpoint values and <Lipschitz> admissible functions, the triangular waves displayed above have slopes $\pm2$ <almost everywhere> and height $1/N$. Direct integration gives $I(u_N)=1/(5N^4)$. Nonnegativity therefore makes the <infimum> zero. Attainment would require both $u=0$ and $|u'|=2$ <almost everywhere>, an impossibility. The uniform limit is zero but its energy is $16^4$; thus the energy fails to be <lower semicontinuous> for this convergence. The example explains why control of <function> values alone cannot replace suitable <convexity> in the <derivative> variable in the <direct method in the calculus of variations>.

= Bounded slope condition
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}

Boundary data $g$ satisfy the bounded slope condition with constant $K$ when every boundary point admits affine lower and upper supporting functions of Lipschitz constant at most $K$. Comparison with these barriers gives a global Lipschitz bound for convex variational problems.

= Comparison principle for convex variational integrals
{parent=Direct method in the calculus of variations}

For suitable convex integral functionals, ordered boundary values of two minimizers imply the same ordering in the domain. Affine minimizers can therefore serve as upper and lower barriers.

= Functional
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{wiki=Functional_(mathematics)}

A functional is a <function> whose inputs are themselves functions, curves, or other elements of a function space.

= Quadratic functional
{parent=Functional}
{title2=$I(v)=B(v,v)-2\ell(v)+c$}

A quadratic functional on a real vector space combines a <quadratic form> $B(v,v)$, a <linear functional> $\ell(v)$ and a constant. If $B$ is a symmetric <bounded bilinear form>, its <first variation> is $DI(v)[w]=2B(v,w)-2\ell(w)$. For a strictly positive $B$, an existing stationary point is the unique minimizer by the <quadratic variational principle for a symmetric positive operator>.

= Resonant sine modes of a quadratic variational integral
{parent=Quadratic functional}
{title2=$J[y]=\int_0^\pi(y'^2-ky^2)\,dx,\quad y(0)=y(\pi)=0$}

The <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $y''+ky=0$. For positive $k$ a nonzero stationary <function> exists exactly when $\sqrt k$ is a positive integer $n$, and all such <functions> are $B\sin(nx)$. These values are the <Dirichlet eigenvalues> of $-d^2/dx^2$ on the interval. At $k=0$ the solutions are affine and the endpoint conditions leave only zero; negative $k$ likewise leaves only zero. Stationarity alone does not assert minimization of the <quadratic functional>.

= Stationary values of a quadratic functional with a quadratic constraint
{parent=Quadratic functional}
{title2=$J[y]=\int(py'^2-qy^2),\quad I[y]=\int wy^2=1$}

Impose zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on an interval. The <isoperimetric constraint> $I[y]=1$ is regular because $DI_y[y]=2I[y]=2\ne0$. The <Lagrange multiplier> rule applied to $J-\lambda(I-1)$ gives the <Sturm-Liouville equation>
$$
(py')'+qy+\lambda wy=0.
$$
Multiplying by $y$ and using <integration by parts> gives $J[y]=\lambda I[y]=\lambda$. Thus the multiplier is the stationary value of the original functional, or its <Rayleigh quotient>. This calculation does not require $w$ to have one sign; positive-weight spectral conclusions require additional hypotheses.

If two stationary functions have different multipliers, multiplying their equations crosswise and subtracting gives $(\lambda_n-\lambda_m)\int wy_ny_m=0$. The endpoint terms vanish. This proves the weighted <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>. Higher stationary values need not be local minima of the constrained functional.

= Epigraph
{parent=Functional}
{title2=$\operatorname{epi}E$}
{wiki=Epigraph_(mathematics)}

The <epigraph> is $\{(u,t):E(u)\leq t\}$. It is a <convex set> exactly when $E$ is a <convex function>, and is a <sequentially closed set> in the <product topology> exactly when $E$ is <sequentially lower semicontinuous>.

= Functional derivative
{title2=$\delta F/\delta\phi$}
{parent=Functional}
{wiki}

The functional derivative is defined by the first variation
$$
F[\phi+\epsilon h]-F[\phi]
=\epsilon\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi(x)}h(x),dx+o(\epsilon).
$$

= Functional Taylor expansion
{parent=Functional derivative}
{title2=$S[\phi+\eta f]=S[\phi]+\eta\int f\,\delta S/\delta\phi+O(\eta^2)$}

The first variation of a differentiable <action functional> gives its change under a small field deformation. With $E=\delta S/\delta\phi$, the change $\phi\mapsto\phi-\eta f$ generates $-\eta\int fE$ to first order. This is the basic cancellation behind the <equivalence theorem for field redefinitions>; the <functional Jacobian> and transformed operator insertions must also be treated consistently.

= Functional chain rule
{parent=Functional derivative}

Along a differentiable path of fields $\phi(x,t)$, a differentiable functional obeys
$$
\frac{d}{dt}F[\phi(t)]
=\int dx\,
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi(x)}\,
\partial_t\phi(x,t).
$$
Integrating in time expresses the functional change as the line integral of its functional derivative along the path.

= Energy functional
{parent=Functional}

An energy functional assigns an integral energy to a function. Minimization of a suitable energy is the basis of the <Dirichlet principle>. For the modified Helmholtz equation, a natural example is
$$
E[u]=\int_V\left(|\nabla u|^2+m^2u^2\right)dV.
$$

= Critical point of an energy functional
{parent=Energy functional}
{title2=$\delta E=0$}

= Stationary field configuration
{synonym}

A field configuration is critical for an <energy functional> $E$ if its <first variation> vanishes for every admissible infinitesimal field variation. For $E[\phi]=\int(\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2+U(\phi))$, integration by parts against compactly supported variations gives $\delta E=\int(-\Delta\phi+U'(\phi))\delta\phi$, so <stationary field configurations> satisfy the <Euler-Lagrange equation> $\Delta\phi=U'(\phi)$. A constrained target instead allows only tangent variations, changing that field equation. Criticality is stationarity, not necessarily energetic stability or a local minimum.

= p-energy
{title2=$E_p[u]=\frac1p\int_\Omega|Du|^p$}
{c}
{parent=Energy functional}

The p-energy of a Sobolev function is
$$
E_p[u]=\frac1p\int_\Omega|Du|^p\,dx.
$$
Its stationary points under fixed boundary data are weak solutions of the <p-Laplacian equation>.

= Lagrangian
{parent=Functional}

A Lagrangian is a function or density whose stationary integral encodes an extremization problem. In <Lagrangian mechanics> its action gives the equations of motion; in geometry it describes geodesics and minimal surfaces.

In <Lagrangian field theory>, the action integrates such a density over spacetime.

A finite-dimensional <optimization Lagrangian> combines an <objective function> with <Lagrange multiplier> terms for its constraints.

= Variation
{parent=Functional}

A variation of a function $y$ is a one-parameter family $y+\varepsilon\eta$, where $\eta$ satisfies the required <boundary conditions>.

= First variation
{parent=Variation}
{wiki}

The first variation of a <functional> $L$ at $y$ in the direction $\eta$ is
$$
\delta L[y;\eta]
=\left.\frac d{d\varepsilon}L[y+\varepsilon\eta]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}.
$$

= Fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations
{parent=First variation}
{wiki}

If a continuous function $f$ satisfies
$$
\int_a^b f(x)\eta(x)\,dx=0
$$
for every smooth compactly supported test function $\eta$, then $f=0$ on $(a,b)$.

= Stationary point
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{wiki}

A point is stationary for a <differentiable function> or <functional> when its first <derivative> or <first variation> vanishes in every admissible direction.

= Stationary points of a cosine-modulated Gaussian
{parent=Stationary point}
{title2=$z(x,y)=e^{-x^2-y^2}\cos x$}

The <gradient> vanishes only when $y=0$ and $\tan x=-2x$. On each tangent branch, $\tan x+2x$ increases strictly from negative to positive infinity, so it has exactly one root $x_s$. With $z_s=e^{-x_s^2}\cos x_s$, the <Hessian matrix> is diagonal with entries $-(4x_s^2+3)z_s$ and $-2z_s$. Its determinant is positive, so every stationary point is a strict maximum when $z_s>0$ or a strict minimum when $z_s<0$. Signs alternate between successive tangent branches. This gives infinitely many nondegenerate extrema under a decaying <Gaussian function> envelope.

= Stationary points with real cube-root factors
{parent=Stationary point}
{title2=$[(x-a)\sqrt[3]{q(x)}]'=[3q+(x-a)q']/[3(\sqrt[3]q)^2]$}

Where $q(x)\ne0$, the <product rule> and <chain rule> give the displayed real derivative. Its stationary points therefore satisfy $3q+(x-a)q'=0$. The denominator uses the square of the real <cube root>, which stays positive for negative $q$. Points with $q=0$ must be checked in the original function: a simple zero of $q$ with $x\ne a$ creates a vertical tangent, not a stationary point. If the prefactor also vanishes, a cancellation can change differentiability and must be analyzed separately.

= Stationary points under an increasing outer function
{parent=Stationary point}
{title2=$\nabla(F\circ h)=F'(h)\nabla h$}

Suppose $h$ is differentiable and $F'>0$ on its range. The <multivariable chain rule> shows that $F\circ h$ has exactly the same <stationary points> as $h$. Since $F$ is strictly increasing, the type of every <local maximum>, <local minimum>, and <saddle point> is preserved. If both functions are twice differentiable, then at a stationary point
$$
\operatorname{Hess}(F\circ h)=F'(h)\operatorname{Hess}h,
$$
so the <Hessian matrix> signature is unchanged. The hypothesis $F'>0$ matters for the converse about stationary points: a strictly increasing function such as $F(s)=s^3$ can have a zero <derivative> and create new stationary points in a composition.

= Dirichlet principle
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{c}
{wiki}

Among functions with fixed boundary values, the solution of $\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\phi)=0$ with $\kappa>0$ uniquely minimizes the weighted energy
$$
\int\kappa|\nabla\phi|^2.
$$

= Quadratic energy minimization by the parallelogram law
{parent=Dirichlet principle}
{title2=$Q(v_j-v_k)=2Q(v_j)+2Q(v_k)-4Q((v_j+v_k)/2)$}

Let $V$ be a closed <linear subspace> of a real <Hilbert space>, and let $Q(v)=a(v,v)$ for a symmetric <bounded bilinear form> that is nonnegative on the ambient space and coercive on $V$. Then $Q$ has a unique minimizer on every closed <affine subspace> $g+V$. If $Q(v_j)\to m=\inf_{g+V}Q$, the midpoint belongs to $g+V$ and the <parallelogram law> gives $Q(v_j-v_k)\leq2Q(v_j)+2Q(v_k)-4m\to0$. Coercivity on $V$ makes the <minimizing sequence> Cauchy; completeness and continuity give a minimizer. The vanishing <first variation> is $a(u,v)=0$ for all $v\in V$. Two minimizers differ by $w\in V$ with $Q(w)=0$, so coercivity gives uniqueness. This proves the symmetric existence theorem directly, without invoking the <Lax-Milgram theorem>.

= Sharp radial Dirichlet energy inequality
{parent=Dirichlet principle}
{title2=$\int_0^1[rg'(r)^2+g(r)^2/r]\,dr\geq1$}

For $g(1)=1$ and finite displayed energy, the angular mode $g(r)\cos\theta$ has <Dirichlet energy> equal to $\pi$ times that integral. The <harmonic function> $r\cos\theta$ minimizes the energy with the same boundary data by the <Dirichlet principle>, giving the bound. Finite energy forces $g(r)\to0$ as $r\to0$: Cauchy–Schwarz bounds changes of $g^2$ by twice the product of the two energy tails, so $g^2$ has a limit, and a nonzero limit would make $\int g^2/r$ divergent. Consequently completing the square gives the exact identity $\int_0^1[rg'^2+g^2/r]=1+\int_0^1r(g'-g/r)^2$. Equality occurs exactly for $g(r)=r$. This identity also covers finite-energy functions whose polar expression is not classically smooth at the centre.

= Optical ray in cylindrical coordinates
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{wiki}

Fermat-type optical paths in cylindrical coordinates extremize refractive index times Euclidean arclength.

= Helical extremal
{parent=Optical ray in cylindrical coordinates}
{wiki}

A helical extremal has constant radius and a polar angle linear in axial position.

= Noether theorem
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{c}
{wiki}

= Noether's first theorem
{c}
{synonym}

Noether’s theorem associates a conserved quantity to each continuous variational symmetry.

= Mass-weighted oscillator symmetries
{parent=Noether theorem}

For independent coordinates with $L=\sum_i m_i(\dot x_i^2-\omega^2x_i^2)/2$, the variables $X_i=\sqrt{m_i}x_i$ give an isotropic oscillator even at unequal masses. Rotations of the $X_i$ are continuous symmetries. Their conserved <Noether theorem>[Noether charges] $X_iP_j-X_jP_i$ therefore predate any equality of physical masses. Equal masses alone cannot create new invariances of arbitrary additional interactions.

= Time-translation symmetry
{parent=Noether theorem}

Invariance under a shift of the time coordinate, with other required background structures held fixed. A continuous <time-translation symmetry> gives an <energy> <conservation law> through <Noether theorem>. A generic expanding cosmological background is not stationary and need not supply a corresponding globally conserved total <energy>.

= Noether second theorem
{parent=Noether theorem}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_a[\mathcal E_aR_a-\partial_\mu(\mathcal E_aR_a^\mu)]\equiv0$}

Suppose an action is invariant under a local transformation $\delta\Phi_a=R_a\alpha+R_a^\mu\partial_\mu\alpha$ for every compactly supported function $\alpha$. Variation and integration by parts give the off-shell identity
$$
\sum_a\left(\mathcal E_aR_a-\partial_\mu(\mathcal E_aR_a^\mu)\right)\equiv0.
$$
Thus arbitrary local symmetry parameters imply dependencies among the field equations. In <gauge theory>, these identities accompany constraints rather than independent physical charges for each function. This complements the conserved-current statement of <Noether theorem>.

= Noether identity for abelian scalar gauge symmetry
{parent=Noether second theorem}
{c}
{title2=$-\partial_\mu\mathcal E_A^\mu-ie\phi\mathcal E_\phi+ie\phi^*\mathcal E_{\phi^*}\equiv0$}

For $\delta A_\mu=\partial_\mu\alpha$, $\delta\phi=-ie\alpha\phi$ and $\delta\phi^*=ie\alpha\phi^*$, <Noether second theorem> gives
$$
-\partial_\mu\mathcal E_A^\mu-ie\phi\mathcal E_\phi+ie\phi^*\mathcal E_{\phi^*}\equiv0.
$$
This holds without imposing the field equations. On the matter equations it becomes a divergence identity for the electromagnetic equation, expressing compatibility with charge conservation. The Hamiltonian description carries the corresponding <Gauss law constraint in gauge theory>.

= Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry
{title2=$J=\sum_i p_i\xi_i$}
{c}
{parent=Noether theorem}

= Noether's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

If a time-independent <Lagrangian> $L(q,\dot q)$ is invariant under a one-parameter point transformation with infinitesimal generator $\xi_i=\partial_sQ_i|_{s=0}$, then every Euler-Lagrange trajectory conserves
$$
J=\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\xi_i.
$$

= Action symmetry
{parent=Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry}

An infinitesimal transformation that preserves the <action> up to endpoint terms. For $\delta t=\varepsilon\phi$ and $\delta q_i=\varepsilon\psi_i$, invariance of $L\,dt$ yields the <Noether charge> $\sum_ip_i\psi_i-H\phi$, less any boundary-term generator.

= Second variation
{parent=Calculus of variations}
{wiki}

The second variation is the quadratic term in a functional's expansion along $u+\varepsilon\eta$; positivity on admissible variations is a local-minimum test.

= Coercive positivity of a second variation
{parent=Second variation}
{title2=$q(\varphi)\geq c\int_a^b|\varphi'|^2$}

For fixed-endpoint variations, a <second variation> is coercively positive if the displayed estimate holds for one constant $c>0$ and every admissible variation. The endpoint <Poincaré inequality> makes this equivalent to a positive lower bound in the $W^{1,2}$ norm. It is stronger than strict positivity on each nonzero variation: $q(\varphi)=\int\varphi^2$ is strictly positive, but high-frequency variations make its ratio to $\int\varphi'^2$ tend to zero.

For $q=\int(p\varphi'^2+2r\varphi\varphi'+s\varphi^2)$ with <continuous> coefficients, coercive positivity implies $p\geq c$. Indeed, if $p-c<0$ on an interval, multiply a smooth <function> supported there by $\sin(nx)$. Its negative highest-<derivative> contribution has order $n^2$, while the mixed term has order at most $n$ and the remaining term is bounded. This contradicts the displayed inequality for large $n$. In particular coercive positivity implies the <strengthened Legendre condition>, whereas mere strict positivity does not.

= Legendre necessary condition
{c}
{parent=Second variation}
{title2=$f_{vv}(x,u,u')\geq0$}

A nonnegative <second variation> requires its highest-<derivative> coefficient $P=f_{vv}$ to be nonnegative. If it were negative on an interval, a smooth compactly supported factor times $\sin(nx)$ would have negative <derivative> energy of order $n^2$, while its zero-order energy stays bounded. Large $n$ would contradict nonnegativity. This is a local necessary condition; it does not rule out negative directions caused by the lower-order terms.

= Strengthened Legendre condition
{c}
{parent=Legendre necessary condition}
{title2=$P(x)=f_{vv}(x,u,u')>0$}

Strict positivity of the highest-<derivative> coefficient on the closed parameter interval is the strengthened Legendre condition. Continuity then gives a uniform positive lower bound and makes the <Jacobi equation> a regular second-order equation. It permits the <Jacobi positivity criterion>. It is not necessary for mere strict positivity of a <second variation>: $\int\varphi^2$ is strictly positive on nonzero variations even though its <derivative> coefficient vanishes.

= Jacobi equation
{parent=Second variation}
{c}

The Jacobi equation is the linearization of an <Euler-Lagrange equation> about a stationary path. Its solutions describe infinitesimal one-parameter families of stationary paths.

= Jacobi identity for the second variation
{c}
{parent=Jacobi equation}
{title2=$P\varphi'^2+Q\varphi^2=P(\varphi'-w'\varphi/w)^2+(Pw'\varphi^2/w)'$}

If $w$ is a nonvanishing solution of $-(Pw')'+Qw=0$, expanding the right-hand side gives the displayed identity. Integrating expresses a scalar <second variation> as a square plus a boundary term. A simple zero of $w$ at a fixed endpoint causes no difficulty for <Lipschitz> endpoint-vanishing variations, since $\varphi=O(x-a)$ makes the boundary term tend to zero. This identity is the scalar form of a <ground-state transform for a weighted Dirichlet energy>.

= Conjugate point
{parent=Second variation}

A conjugate point marks a nonzero endpoint-vanishing Jacobi variation and the loss of positive definiteness of the second variation.

For the energy functional of a <Riemannian manifold>, this variational condition yields <conjugate points along a geodesic>.

= Jacobi positivity criterion
{c}
{parent=Conjugate point}
{title2=$q>0\iff\text{no conjugate point in }(a,b]$}

Assume the <strengthened Legendre condition> $P>0$ and let $q(\varphi)=\int_a^b(P\varphi'^2+Q\varphi^2)$. Then $q$ is strictly positive on all nonzero fixed-endpoint <Lipschitz> variations exactly when the normalized <Jacobi equation> solution with $w(a)=0$, $P(a)w'(a)=1$ has no zero in $(a,b]$. A zero at $c$ gives a nonzero variation equal to $w$ up to $c$ and zero thereafter, with $q=0$. Conversely, if no zero occurs, the <Jacobi identity for the second variation> writes $q$ as a nonnegative square. Equality forces $\varphi=Cw$, and the endpoint at $b$ forces $C=0$.

= Regularized Jacobi criterion for a degenerate second variation
{c}
{parent=Jacobi positivity criterion}
{title2=$q_\eta=q+\eta\int|\varphi'|^2$}

For continuous $P\geq0,Q$, a scalar <second variation> $q=\int(P\varphi'^2+Q\varphi^2)$ is strictly positive on nonzero fixed-endpoint <Lipschitz> variations if and only if every regularized form $q_\eta$ for $\eta>0$ has no <conjugate point> in $(a,b]$, and the original weak Jacobi boundary problem has no nonzero <Lipschitz> solution. The <Jacobi positivity criterion> applies to each regularized coefficient $P+\eta>0$. Letting $\eta$ decrease to zero gives $q\geq0$. For a nonnegative <quadratic form>, $q(\varphi)=0$ forces its mixed <bilinear form> with every <test function> to vanish, by considering $q(\varphi+t\psi)$ for both signs of $t$. This is the weak <Jacobi equation>, so the kernel condition gives strict positivity. Necessity follows by the same argument in reverse. It distinguishes strict positivity from a uniform coercive estimate when the <derivative> coefficient degenerates.

= Multiplicity of a conjugate point
{parent=Conjugate point}
{title2=$\dim\{J:J(0)=J(t_0)=0\}$}

Along a nonconstant affinely parametrized <geodesic>, the multiplicity of a <conjugate point> at positive time $t_0$ is the dimension of the space of <Jacobi fields> vanishing at both endpoints. The tangential component of such a <Jacobi field> is affine and vanishes at both endpoints, hence is zero. Uniqueness for the <Jacobi field> equation embeds this space into the $(n-1)$-dimensional normal space by $J\mapsto D_tJ(0)$. Thus its dimension is at most $n-1$.

= Nonpositive sectional curvature excludes conjugate points
{parent=Conjugate point}
{title2=$(|J|^2)^{\prime\prime}\ge0$}

For a <Jacobi field> along a <geodesic> with nonpositive <sectional curvature>, the curvature convention with positive round-sphere curvature gives
$$
(|J|^2)''=2|D_tJ|^2-2\langle R(J,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma,J\rangle\ge0.
$$
Thus $|J|^2$ is nonnegative and convex. If it vanishes at both ends of a segment, convexity forces it to vanish everywhere. No nonzero <Jacobi field> can vanish at both ends, so the segment has no <conjugate points>. Only curvature of planes containing the <geodesic> tangent is needed, and completeness is unnecessary.

= Wirtinger inequality
{parent=Second variation}
{c}
{wiki}

For $f(0)=f(T)=0$,
$$\int_0^T|f'|^2dt\geq\frac{\pi^2}{T^2}\int_0^T|f|^2dt,$$
with equality for a multiple of $\sin(\pi t/T)$.

= Minimum Dirichlet energy at fixed L2 norm on an interval
{parent=Wirtinger inequality}
{title2=$\min_{y\in H_0^1(0,L),\ \|y\|_2=1}\int_0^L|y'|^2=\pi^2/L^2$}

Expand a real function with zero endpoint values in its <Fourier sine series>, $y=\sum_{n\geq1}a_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$. The <Parseval identity> gives $\int y^2=(L/2)\sum a_n^2$. For its <weak derivative>, the <Fourier cosine series> has coefficients $(n\pi/L)a_n$ and zero constant term, so
$$
\int_0^L|y'|^2\,dx=\frac L2\sum_{n\geq1}\left(\frac{n\pi}L\right)^2a_n^2
\geq\frac{\pi^2}{L^2}\int_0^Ly^2\,dx.
$$
The derivative coefficient identity follows by <integration by parts>, rather than by assuming pointwise differentiation of an arbitrary series. This proves the <Wirtinger inequality> and its equality case. Under unit <L2 norm>, the only real minimizers are $y=\pm\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi x/L)$.

= Periodic Wirtinger inequality
{parent=Wirtinger inequality}

If a continuously differentiable $L$-periodic function $f$ has mean zero, then
$$
\int_0^L f(s)^2\,ds
\leq\frac{L^2}{4\pi^2}\int_0^L f'(s)^2\,ds.
$$
Equality holds exactly for $f(s)=A\cos(2\pi s/L)+B\sin(2\pi s/L)$.
