= Quantum theory

Quantum theory supplies a common language for <quantum mechanics>, <measurement in quantum mechanics>, and <quantum information>: prepare a <quantum state>, transform it, and ask which experimental outcomes occur. In a finite-dimensional idealization, a <pure state> is a normalized <vector> of <complex numbers>. Its entries are <probability amplitudes>; the <Born rule> turns their squared magnitudes into <probabilities>. Between measurements, an isolated system evolves by a <unitary operator>, preserving its <norm> and <inner products>. A <quantum circuit> makes this evolution explicit as a sequence of <quantum gates>.

The extra information carried by <quantum phases> is essential. Relative <quantum phases> affect how <probability amplitudes> add when different contributions reach the same output. Their <interference> can enhance one outcome and cancel another. A <global phase>, multiplying the entire <quantum state> by the same complex factor of magnitude one, changes neither these relative phases nor observable predictions. The <Hadamard gate> below provides a small experiment in which this distinction becomes a deterministic change of output.

<Quantum coherence> describes this capacity to interfere relative to a specified <orthonormal basis>. In a <density matrix>, it appears in off-diagonal entries; a diagonal <mixed state> retains the populations but lacks that basis coherence. Thus matching the <probabilities> of one measurement need not match the predictions of a later experiment. Baumgratz, Cramer, and Plenio made this basis-dependent viewpoint precise in their resource framework for <quantum coherence>. https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.0275[Baumgratz, Cramer, and Plenio, Quantifying Coherence, 2014, pp. 1–2]{ref}.

This makes <quantum information> an operational question: which distinctions can a preparation, an available <unitary operator>, and <measurement in quantum mechanics> reveal? <Amplitude amplification> illustrates how carefully arranged <interference> converts a weakly represented answer into a likely observable result. Preserving the relevant <quantum coherence> throughout the computation is part of the physical task. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/introduction[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, introduction to single systems]{ref}.

= Hadamard transform
{parent=Quantum theory}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$H^{\otimes n}$}

The normalized <Hadamard transform> maps a <vector> indexed by <binary strings> through entries $2^{-n/2}(-1)^{x\cdot y}$, where the <dot product> is evaluated modulo two. It is its own inverse and an <orthogonal transformation>, hence also a <unitary operator> on complex input vectors. The <Hadamard gate> is the $n=1$ quantum case, and the <Hadamard basis> is the image of the <computational basis>. The <Walsh-Hadamard transform> gives the same <tensor product> construction.

For two <qubits>, applying one <Hadamard gate> to each prepares the <uniform quantum superposition>
$$
H^{\otimes2}|00\rangle=\tfrac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle).
$$
Each <probability amplitude> is $1/2$, so each <computational-basis measurement> outcome has <probability> $1/4$. Applying the <Hadamard transform> again reconstructs $|00\rangle$: signed contributions cancel at the other three outputs. Its usefulness lies in combining simple local <quantum gates> into an operation on exponentially many amplitudes. Extracting useful information still requires an appropriate subsequent transformation and measurement.

= Hadamard gate
{parent=Hadamard transform}
{c}
{title2=$H$}

The <Hadamard gate> is the <unitary operator>
$$
H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H^2=I,
$$
where $I$ is the <identity operator>. It maps the <computational basis> to the <Hadamard basis>:
$$
|0\rangle\mapsto\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
|1\rangle\mapsto\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$

These outputs have equal <computational-basis measurement> probabilities, but their relative <quantum phase> differs. Applying another <Hadamard gate> sends them to different definite outputs. Calling this gate a classical coin toss would discard precisely the information that makes this possible. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/quantum-information[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, unitary operations on qubits]{ref}.

Starting from $|0\rangle$, compare two <quantum circuits>, reading matrix products from right to left:
$$
HH|0\rangle=|0\rangle,\qquad HZH|0\rangle=|1\rangle.
$$
Here $Z$ is the <Pauli Z gate>. After the first <Hadamard gate>, the <probability amplitudes> are $(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)$. Inserting $Z$ changes them to $(1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2)$ without changing their squared magnitudes. At the final gate, the contributions to output zero cancel and those to output one add. Without $Z$, cancellation occurs at output one instead. This is controlled <interference>, with the <quantum phase> determining which answer emerges.

The same calculation works for any <phase gate> $P_\phi=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\phi})$:
$$
HP_\phi H|0\rangle=\frac{1+e^{i\phi}}2|0\rangle+\frac{1-e^{i\phi}}2|1\rangle,
\qquad P(1)=\sin^2(\phi/2),\quad P(0)=\cos^2(\phi/2).
$$
For example, $\phi=\pi/3$ gives $P(1)=1/4$, while $\phi=\pi/2$ gives $P(1)=1/2$. The output-one amplitude at $\phi=\pi/2$ is $(1-i)/2$: its two complex contributions add before the <Born rule> is applied. A <phase gate> alone leaves the populations unchanged; recombination converts its relative <quantum phase> into observable <probabilities>.

\Image[hadamard-interference.png]
{title=Hadamard interference: a relative phase changes output probabilities through complex amplitude addition; the dotted circle traces the output-one amplitude as the phase varies}

If <quantum decoherence> completely removes the <computational basis> coherence between the gates, the intermediate <density matrix> becomes $I/2$. Both the <phase gate> and final <Hadamard gate> leave $I/2$ unchanged, so $P(1)=1/2$ for every $\phi$. The disappearing fringe explains why preserving <quantum coherence> matters: it enables the gate sequence to carry phase information into the final measurement.

= Hadamard-phase-Hadamard qubit rotation
{parent=Hadamard gate}
{c}
{title2=$HP_\alpha H=e^{i\alpha/2}e^{-i\alpha\sigma_x/2}$}

For $P_\alpha=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\alpha})$, conjugation by the <Hadamard gate> turns a relative <quantum phase> into an $x$-axis <Bloch vector> <rotation>. In the convention $\rho=(I+\mathbf P\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$, the map is $(P_x,P_y,P_z)\mapsto(P_x,P_y\cos\alpha-P_z\sin\alpha,P_y\sin\alpha+P_z\cos\alpha)$. An $x$-polarized input is unchanged, whereas a $z$-polarized input gives a sinusoidal population difference. A maximally mixed input remains maximally mixed under every <unitary time evolution>.

= Photon-number reference for a Hadamard gate
{parent=Hadamard gate}
{title2=$r_k=1-1/k,\quad s_k=\max(k-2,0)/k$}

A flat reference over $k$ adjacent <Fock states> has overlap $r_k$ with a one-photon shift and $s_k$ with a two-photon shift. These overlaps, obtained by counting shared number states, determine the reduced <qubit> channel of a number-conserving Hadamard interaction. For $k\ge2$, its squared fidelity with the desired pure output is $1-(1+xz)/(2k)$ for input <Bloch vector> $(x,y,z)$. Its worst-case infidelity is $3/(4k)$ because $|xz|\le1/2$. At $k=1$ the two-shift overlap is zero, not $-1$, and the fidelity is $1/2+(y^2-xz)/4$. Broad coherent number support, rather than merely a large fixed <photon> number, suppresses distinguishability of the outgoing field states.

$$
H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle
=2^{-n/2}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle,

= Amplitude amplification theorem
{synonym}

<Amplitude amplification> increases the <probability> of obtaining an answer accepted by a specified <Boolean function>. It requires a coherent preparation $A$, its inverse, and a <marked-state phase oracle> that negates accepted components. Alternating that sign change with a <reflection operator> about the prepared state directs <interference> toward the accepted subspace. Brassard, Høyer, Mosca, and Tapp developed the general framework, extending <Grover's algorithm> beyond a <uniform quantum superposition>. https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0005055[Brassard et al., Quantum Amplitude Amplification and Estimation, 2000, §2]{ref}.

If a normalized <quantum state> has good amplitude $\sin\theta$, the product of the reflection in the bad axis and the reflection in the state line rotates its good-bad plane by $2\theta$. After $k$ iterations the good <probability> is
$$
\sin^2((2k+1)\theta),
$$
so $O(1/\sin\theta)$ iterations raise a small good amplitude close to one. Success subsequently oscillates, making the stopping time part of the algorithm.

Compute the <exact Grover search on four entries> directly. Order the <computational basis> as $00,01,10,11$ and mark $11$. The <Hadamard transform> prepares amplitudes $(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)$. The <marked-state phase oracle> changes these to $(1/2,1/2,1/2,-1/2)$, whose <arithmetic mean> is $\overline a=1/4$. The <Grover diffusion operator> performs <inversion about the mean>:
$$
a_x\longmapsto2\overline a-a_x,\qquad
\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right)\longmapsto(0,0,0,1).
$$
The three unmarked amplitudes become $1/2-1/2=0$, while the marked amplitude becomes $1/2-(-1/2)=1$. All three stages have squared <norm> one. Here $\sin\theta=1/2$, so $\theta=\pi/6$ and one iteration gives $\sin^2(3\theta)=1$, agreeing with the explicit calculation.

\Image[four-state-amplitude-amplification.png]
{title=One Grover iteration on four states: a phase flip followed by reflection about the mean concentrates all amplitude on the marked state}

Grover's original search paper was submitted on 29 May 1996; its algorithm combines selective sign changes with <inversion about the mean>. The example exposes the beauty of that construction: a change initially invisible to <computational-basis measurement> becomes complete cancellation of every wrong answer. https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9605043[Grover, A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search, 1996, §§3–4]{ref}.

The <marked-state phase oracle> is an assumed operation, whose implementation cost must be counted. <Computational-basis measurement> yields a candidate label, here $11$; using it to retrieve a record requires the relevant data access. Preparing a <quantum superposition> does not make all its entries available as classical outputs. The improvement concerns the number of oracle queries needed to obtain an accepted label.

= Nearest-integer stopping for amplitude amplification

Hence the success probability after <amplitude amplification> is at least $\cos^2\theta=1-p$. = Exact amplitude amplification
{parent=Amplitude amplification}

If the initial good amplitude $p=\sin\theta$ is known, an ancillary qubit can reduce it to $\sin\theta'=p c$ with $\theta'=\pi/(4k+2)$ and $0<c\leq1$. After $k$ ordinary amplitude-amplification iterations the good amplitude is $\sin((2k+1)\theta')=1$, so the target state is prepared exactly.

= Success dilution
{synonym}

For known good probability $p=\sin^2\theta$, choose $j=\lceil\pi/(4\theta)-1/2\rceil$. A known reversible preparation of one state with good probability $p_*$ permits $j$ ordinary <amplitude amplification> iterations to reach the good subspace exactly. With ancillary marking access, prepare a flag with one-probability $q=p_*/p$ and define joint success as good data and flag one. Reflection about the prepared product state is known, while the joint phase test requires a controlled marking oracle or a computed Boolean output. This changes the success projector and enlarges the state space; it does not evade the <obstruction to uniform probability lowering by a unitary>. Query costs of preparation, its inverse and marking must all be counted.

= Reflection operator

= Projector phase rotation
{parent=Reflection operator}
{title2=$e^{-i\theta P}=I+(e^{-i\theta}-1)P$}

For an <orthogonal projection>, $P^k=P$ for $k\geq1$. Summing the exponential series proves the displayed identity. At $\theta=\pi$, the operator is $I-2P$, which changes the sign on the range and fixes its orthogonal complement. In <Grover search algorithm>, the marked-state reflection and the uniform-state reflection compose to the search iterate up to an irrelevant <global phase>.

= Selective phase rotation
{parent=Reflection operator}

For a unit vector $|\psi\rangle$ and real $\phi$,
$$
R_\psi^\phi=I-(1-e^{i\phi})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
$$
multiplies the component parallel to $|\psi\rangle$ by $e^{i\phi}$ and fixes its orthogonal complement. The case $\phi=\pi$ is a <reflection operator>.

A reflection operator is a unitary involution that changes sign on one orthogonal subspace and fixes its orthogonal complement. For a unit vector $|\psi\rangle$, $I-2|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ reflects in the hyperplane perpendicular to $|\psi\rangle$.

= HHL controlled reciprocal rotation
{c}
{parent=HHL algorithm}
{title2=$\sin\theta_\lambda=c/\lambda$}

On a positive <eigenvalue> label $\lambda$ and a clean <quantum ancilla>, the <HHL algorithm> applies a <quantum variable rotation> with angle $\theta_\lambda=\arcsin(c/\lambda)$, where $0<c\leq\lambda_{\min}$:
$$
|\lambda\rangle|0\rangle\longmapsto|\lambda\rangle\left(\sqrt{1-c^2/\lambda^2}|0\rangle+\frac c\lambda|1\rangle\right).
$$
<Uncomputation> of the eigenvalue register followed by conditioning on flag one multiplies each input <eigenvector> amplitude by $c/\lambda$. If $|b\rangle$ is normalized, the success probability is $c^2\|A^{-1}|b\rangle\|^2$. Choosing $c=\lambda_{\max}/\kappa$ from a valid <condition number> bound gives success probability at least $1/\kappa^2$.

= Cost of exact phase estimation on a dyadic spectrum
{parent=HHL algorithm}

If the <eigenvalues> of $A$ are $\ell/2^n$ with $0\leq\ell<2^n$, <exact quantum phase estimation> on $U=e^{2\pi iA}$ uses $n$ controlled powers $U^{2^j}$. Their <Hamiltonian simulation> times reach $2\pi2^{n-1}$. Counting each supplied controlled power as one oracle query is valid in that oracle model, but does not prove a <polynomial-time> implementation from generic sparse-matrix access. In the ordinary <HHL algorithm>, one instead chooses finite precision appropriate to the output error and the <condition number>, or exploits additional structure giving efficient access to the needed powers.

= Remote preparation in conjugate bases
{parent=Quantum no-signalling}
{title2=$({}_A\langle a|\otimes I_B)|\Phi_+\rangle=|a^*\rangle_B/\sqrt2$}

A <projective measurement> on one half of a <Bell state> prepares the complex-conjugate basis on the other half, conditional on the outcome. Measuring Alice in the columns of $U^*$ prepares Bob in the columns of $U$. Ignoring the equally likely outcomes leaves Bob's <reduced density matrix> equal to $I/2$ in either basis.

= Perfect two-basis discrimination would allow superluminal signalling
{parent=Remote preparation in conjugate bases}

Suppose two orthonormal <qubit> bases have disjoint physical rays. Alice chooses which conjugate basis to measure on her half of a <Bell state>. A device that perfectly labels Bob's individual member of either basis would reveal Alice's choice without her outcome being communicated. Spacelike operation would therefore violate relativistic causality, even though ordinary <projective measurements> see the same <reduced density matrix> $I/2$.

= Exact pure-state overlap readout would allow superluminal signalling
{parent=Perfect two-basis discrimination would allow superluminal signalling}
{title2=$|\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2\in\{0,1\}\ \text{or}\ \{1/2\}$}

With a fixed reference $|0\rangle$, exact overlap readout on Bob's remotely prepared state distinguishes the computational basis from the diagonal basis in one use, by the displayed disjoint squared-overlap sets. This would reveal Alice's spacelike basis choice. The argument rules out even deterministic exact overlap magnitude, avoiding the additional unphysical global-phase dependence of a raw complex inner product.

= Relativistic causality constraint on an ideal nonlocal measurement
{parent=Quantum no-signalling}

The nonselective channel of a hypothetical spacelike nonlocal <quantum measurement> must not allow a distant input change to alter local output statistics. For a rank-one measurement in the rotated even and odd two-qubit bases with angle $\theta$, Alice's output satisfies
$$
\langle Z_A\rangle_{\rm out}=\cos^2(2\theta)\langle Z_A\rangle_{\rm in}+\sin2\theta\cos2\theta\langle X_AX_B\rangle_{\rm in}.
$$
Bob can change the last correlation using only his local <Pauli Z gate> while Alice keeps the input $|+\rangle$. The resulting signal excludes $0<\theta<\pi/4$. Degenerate observables that do not distinguish this basis are not covered by the rank-one argument. Locally recording a jointly encoded result, which is decoded later by <local operations and classical communication>, is compatible with <quantum no-signalling>.

= Controlled-basis measurement causality obstruction
{parent=Relativistic causality constraint on an ideal nonlocal measurement}
{title2=$p_B(1\mid A=1)=\tfrac12\sin^2(2\theta)$}

A rank-one <projective measurement> whose Bob basis is computational when Alice is zero and rotated by $\theta$ when Alice is one can signal. Bob starts in zero. Alice zero leaves Bob zero; Alice one dephases Bob in the rotated basis, giving the displayed nonzero flip probability for $0<\theta\leq\pi/4$. This violates <quantum no-signalling>. The conclusion applies to a measurement distinguishing the four product-basis states, rather than a degenerate observable that does not resolve them.

= Singlet-triplet measurement causality obstruction
{parent=Relativistic causality constraint on an ideal nonlocal measurement}
{title2=$\mathcal M(\rho)=P_s\rho P_s+(I-P_s)\rho(I-P_s)$}

The binary <Lüders rule> measurement of singlet versus triplet preserves the entire triplet subspace, but its nonselective channel permits signalling. Starting with $|00\rangle$, Bob retains $|0\rangle$. If Alice instead applies a local <Pauli X gate>, the input is $|10\rangle$ and the channel outputs the equal mixture of $|\Psi^+\rangle$ and $|\Psi^-\rangle$; Bob has <reduced density matrix> $I/2$. Thus no spacelike local implementation can realize this channel. A <Bell-state nondemolition measurement> has a finer triplet readout and a different nonselective channel, so this obstruction does not exclude it.

= Quantum cloning
{parent=Quantum theory}

A cloning operation for a family of pure states would map
$$
|\psi\rangle|0\rangle\longmapsto|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle
$$
for every state in that family. A single physical operation cannot clone every unknown quantum state.

The <no-cloning theorem> forbids a universal unknown-state cloning operation, while orthogonal families can be cloned.

= Cloning failure bound from trace-distance contraction
{parent=Quantum cloning}
{title2=$\sqrt{e_0}+\sqrt{e_1}\geq\sqrt{1-c^{2N}}-\sqrt{1-c^{2M}}$}

For two <pure states> of overlap $0<c<1$, a <quantum channel> attempts to turn $M$ copies into $N>M$ copies. Let $e_i$ be the failure probability of the all-copies projector test on input $i$. The <pure-state trace distance and fidelity identity>, <trace-distance contraction under quantum channels>, <pure-target upper bound on trace distance> and <triangle inequality> give the displayed strictly positive gap $\Delta$. Hence $\max_i e_i\geq\Delta^2/4$, and for positive priors $\pi_0+\pi_1=1$, weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $\pi_0e_0+\pi_1e_1\geq\pi_0\pi_1\Delta^2$. No positive state-by-state bound holds: preparing one candidate perfectly makes its own conditional failure probability zero.

= Copy-number trace-distance obstruction
{parent=Quantum cloning}
{title2=$\frac{\epsilon_u+\epsilon_v}{2}\geq\frac14\left(\sqrt{1-c^{2m}}-\sqrt{1-c^{2n}}\right)^2,\quad m>n$}

Let distinct nonorthogonal <pure states> have overlap magnitude $0<c<1$, and let one <quantum channel> take $n$ identical inputs to $m$ outputs. Let $\epsilon_u,\epsilon_v$ be the respective <probabilities> that a test for the ideal $m$-copy <pure state> fails. <Trace distance> contraction, its triangle inequality and the <pure-target upper bound on trace distance> give $\sqrt{1-c^{2m}}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_u}+\sqrt{1-c^{2n}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_v}$. Squaring with the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives the displayed strategy-independent positive bound. It establishes both a worst-case bound and an equal-prior average bound. Averaging rotated pairs establishes a positive Haar-average bound for unknown qubit inputs, without assuming independent clone errors.

= No-cloning theorem
{parent=Quantum cloning}
{wiki}

No deterministic quantum operation can make two perfect copies of every unknown <pure state>. For a proposed <unitary operator> acting with a fixed blank and environment, preservation of inner products gives $c=c^2 e$, where $c$ is the input overlap and $|e|\leq1$ is the overlap of final environment states. Nonorthogonal distinct states have $0<|c|<1$, contradicting this equality. The <no-cloning theorem for two pure states> is its elementary two-state form.

= No-cloning theorem for two pure states
{parent=Quantum cloning}

No <unitary operator> $U$ can satisfy
$$
U|c_j\rangle|0\rangle=|c_j\rangle|c_j\rangle,
\qquad j=0,1,
$$
for two distinct nonorthogonal pure states. Taking the inner product of these two equations would require
$$
\langle c_0|c_1\rangle
=\langle c_0|c_1\rangle^2,
$$
which is impossible when $0<|\langle c_0|c_1\rangle|<1$.

= Clone-assisted asymptotic state discrimination
{parent=Quantum cloning}

If a device produces arbitrarily many copies of either of two distinct pure-state rays, their $N$-copy overlap is
$$
|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^N\longrightarrow0.
$$
The Helstrom measurement on the copies therefore distinguishes the alternatives with success probability tending to one.

= Perfect discrimination implies cloning for a known state family
{parent=Quantum cloning}

If a device perfectly identifies which member of a known finite state family was supplied, its classical output can control state-preparation unitaries that prepare any requested number of fresh copies. The discrimination may destroy the supplied system.

= Environment-assisted two-state pure-state transformation
{parent=Quantum theory}

There are a unitary $U$ and normalized environment states $|e_i\rangle$ satisfying
$$
U|\phi_i\rangle|0\rangle=|\psi_i\rangle|e_i\rangle,
\qquad i=0,1,
$$
exactly when
$$
|\langle\phi_0|\phi_1\rangle|
\leq|\langle\psi_0|\psi_1\rangle|.
$$
Necessity follows from preservation of inner products. For sufficiency, choose $\langle e_0|e_1\rangle$ to make the input and output inner products equal; equal two-vector Gram matrices define an isometry that extends to a unitary.

= Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states
{parent=Helstrom measurement for two pure states}
{c}

Suppose one <qubit> is prepared in either of the known <pure states> $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with equal prior <probabilities>. Measuring in the <computational basis> seems natural, but a tilted <projective measurement> gives better <quantum state discrimination>. The two <density operators> and their difference are
$$
\rho_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho_+=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\Delta=\rho_0-\rho_+=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac12(\sigma_z-\sigma_x).
$$
The <Pauli matrices> give $\Delta^2=I/2$, and the <eigenvalues> of $\Delta$ are $\lambda_\pm=\pm1/\sqrt2$. Their absolute sum gives <trace distance> $D=1/\sqrt2$, agreeing with <trace distance between qubit states>.

The positive <spectral projector> points along the unit <Bloch vector> $\mathbf n=(-1,0,1)/\sqrt2$:
$$
P_+=\frac{I+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2}
=\frac12\left(I+\frac{\sigma_z-\sigma_x}{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad
P_-=I-P_+.
$$
The outcome labels $+$ and $-$ here refer to the sign of the <eigenvalue> of $\Delta$; they do not denote a <Hadamard basis> measurement. Guess $|0\rangle$ after $P_+$ and $|+\rangle$ after $P_-$. This sign choice is the equal-prior <Helstrom measurement for two pure states>, a case of the <Holevo–Helstrom theorem>. https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/TQI.3.pdf[Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, Theorem 3.4, equation (3.17)]{ref}

An explicit <orthonormal basis> for this <projective measurement> is
$$
|h_+\rangle=\cos(\pi/8)|0\rangle-\sin(\pi/8)|1\rangle,\qquad
|h_-\rangle=\sin(\pi/8)|0\rangle+\cos(\pi/8)|1\rangle,
$$
with $P_\pm=|h_\pm\rangle\langle h_\pm|$. Thus the real coefficient rotation angle is $\beta=-\pi/8$ relative to the <computational basis>, while the <Bloch vector> of $P_+$ is tilted by $\pi/4$ from the positive $z$ axis toward negative $x$. State-vector and <Bloch sphere> angles differ by a factor of two.

Write $a=(1+1/\sqrt2)/2$ and $b=(1-1/\sqrt2)/2$, so $a+b=1$. The <Born rule> gives the conditional outcome <probabilities>
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\text{input}&P_+&P_-\\\hline
\rho_0&a&b\\
\rho_+&b&a
\end{array}
\qquad
a\simeq0.853553,\quad b\simeq0.146447.
$$
The event $P_+$ has <probability> gap $a-b=1/\sqrt2=D$, attaining the <variational characterization of trace distance>. Both preparations are identified correctly with conditional <probability> $a$, so
$$
P_{\mathrm{success}}^{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{a+a}{2}
=\frac12\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)\simeq0.853553.
$$

For comparison, a $Z$ <projective measurement> in the <computational basis> has conditional outcome <probabilities> $(1,0)$ on $\rho_0$ and $(1/2,1/2)$ on $\rho_+$. Guess zero on outcome zero and plus on outcome one. The average success is
$$
P_{\mathrm{success}}^{Z}=\frac12\left(1+\frac12\right)=\frac34,
$$
and the measured <total variation distance> is only $1/2$. The optimal tilted <projective measurement> gains about $0.103553$ in absolute success <probability> by balancing the two conditional error rates instead of identifying $|0\rangle$ perfectly at the expense of $|+\rangle$.

{title2=$\Delta A\,\Delta B\geq\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$}

= Robertson uncertainty relation
{c}
{synonym}

The <Robertson uncertainty principle> bounds the product of the <standard deviations> of two <observables> in the same <quantum state>. For a normalized <pure state> $|\psi\rangle$ and <self-adjoint operators> $A,B$, write their <expectation values> and <variances> as
$$
\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle,\qquad (\Delta A)^2=\|(A-\langle A\rangle I)\psi\|^2=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2,
$$
and similarly for $B$, where $I$ is the <identity operator>. The <quantum uncertainties> $\Delta A,\Delta B$ then obey
$$
\Delta A\,\Delta B\geq\frac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|,\qquad [A,B]=AB-BA.
$$
Here $[A,B]$ is the <commutator>, and every <expectation value> is taken in the same $|\psi\rangle$. These <standard deviations> describe the outcome spreads when $A$ and $B$ are measured on separate ensembles prepared in that same <quantum state>. They are not instrument errors or the change caused by a preceding <measurement in quantum mechanics>. The definition and bound are developed in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-05-quantum-physics-ii-fall-2013/005979fa741c3ea2e0430456b70caf93_MIT8_05F13_Chap_05.pdf[Barton Zwiebach, Uncertainty Principle and Compatible Observables, 2013, §§1–2].

For <unbounded self-adjoint operators>, the <operator domains> matter. Assume $\psi\in D(A)\cap D(B)$, so that both second moments are finite; $\langle A^2\rangle$ here means the second moment of the measured <probability distribution>, equal to $\|A\psi\|^2$, even if $A^2\psi$ is undefined. To use the displayed operator <commutator>, also assume $\psi\in D(AB)\cap D(BA)$. Without these product-domain assumptions, the always meaningful version on $D(A)\cap D(B)$ uses $|\operatorname{Im}\langle A\psi|B\psi\rangle|$ as its right-hand side. These qualifications prevent a formal <commutator> identity from being applied to an inadmissible <quantum state>.

The brief proof is <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> applied to $u=(A-\langle A\rangle I)\psi$ and $v=(B-\langle B\rangle I)\psi$:
$$
\Delta A\,\Delta B=\|u\|\|v\|\geq|\langle u|v\rangle|\geq|\operatorname{Im}\langle u|v\rangle|=\frac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|.
$$
The final equality uses <self-adjointness> and the product-domain assumptions. Thus a geometric statement about <inner products> becomes a constraint on experimental spreads. For nonzero $\Delta A,\Delta B$, equality requires $u,v$ to be proportional with a purely imaginary proportionality factor; the <equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation> turns this condition into a <Gaussian wave packet>.

The bound is useful because computing a single <commutator> and its <expectation value> can constrain two entire outcome distributions. Its right-hand side depends on the <quantum state>: the <spin uncertainty state dependence> example gives both a positive saturated bound and a zero bound for the same noncommuting <observables>. Robertson's paper, titled "The Uncertainty Principle", appeared on 1 July 1929 in Physical Review, volume 34, starting at page 163. https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.34.163[H. P. Robertson, The Uncertainty Principle, 1929]{ref}.

= Minimum uncertainty state
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}

A state saturating the uncertainty bound for a specified pair of <quantum observables>. The ground state of the <quantum harmonic oscillator> satisfies $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$. Saturation is a property of the state and the chosen observables.

= Spin uncertainty state dependence
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}
{title2=$\Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_z\geq|\langle\sigma_y\rangle|$}

For a <qubit>, use the dimensionless <Pauli matrices> as <observables> $A=\sigma_x$, $B=\sigma_z$. Their <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives
$$
[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]=-2i\sigma_y,\qquad \sigma_x^2=\sigma_y^2=\sigma_z^2=I,\qquad \Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_z\geq|\langle\sigma_y\rangle|.
$$
These <observables> have <eigenvalues> $\pm1$; the physical <spin> components are $S_j=(\hbar/2)\sigma_j$, where $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>, so their <standard deviations> acquire a factor $\hbar/2$.

In the <computational basis>, prepare $|+y\rangle=(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, an <eigenstate> of $\sigma_y$ with <eigenvalue> $+1$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\langle\sigma_z\rangle=0,\qquad \langle\sigma_y\rangle=1,\qquad \Delta\sigma_x=\Delta\sigma_z=1.
$$
Both <Pauli measurements> return $+1$ and $-1$ with equal <probabilities>, and the <Robertson uncertainty principle> is saturated: $1\cdot1=1$.

Instead prepare $|+z\rangle=|0\rangle$. Now the <expectation values> are $\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\langle\sigma_y\rangle=0$, $\langle\sigma_z\rangle=1$, so $\Delta\sigma_x=1$, $\Delta\sigma_z=0$. The <Pauli measurement> of $\sigma_z$ is certain, and both sides of the bound vanish. The <commutator> is still nonzero as an operator: noncommuting <observables> do not guarantee a positive lower bound independent of the <quantum state>. This example is useful for distinguishing the algebra of the <observables> from the <expectation values> in a particular preparation.

= Heisenberg uncertainty relation
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\hbar/2$}

= Heisenberg uncertainty principle
{c}
{synonym}

The <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> constrains the <standard deviations> of <position> and <momentum> in one preparation:
$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}.
$$
On the real line, the <position operator> $x$ acts by multiplication, and the <momentum operator> is $p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$, with $\hbar$ the reduced <Planck constant>. Their <canonical commutation relation> $[x,p]=i\hbar I$ and the <Robertson uncertainty principle> give the bound on normalized <quantum states> satisfying the moment and product-domain assumptions above; <Schwartz functions> are a convenient sufficient class. Thus making a preparation more sharply localized in <position> forces a wider <momentum> distribution, even for ideal <measurement in quantum mechanics>.

An explicit saturating example is the normalized <Gaussian wave packet>, for $\sigma>0$,
$$
\psi_\sigma(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\sigma^2}\right),\qquad |\psi_\sigma(x)|^2=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$
The <wavefunction> is a <probability amplitude>; its squared magnitude is the <probability density function>, by the <Born rule>. That density is a <normal distribution> of mean zero and <standard deviation> $\Delta x=\sigma$, rather than a density whose width is read directly from the amplitude's exponent. With the unitary, negative-exponent convention for the <Fourier transform>, its <momentum> amplitude is
$$
\widetilde\psi_\sigma(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ipx/\hbar}\psi_\sigma(x)\,dx=\left(\frac{2\sigma^2}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\sigma^2p^2}{\hbar^2}\right).
$$
The <Gaussian integral> evaluates this <Fourier transform>. Consequently $|\widetilde\psi_\sigma(p)|^2$ is another <normal distribution>, with mean zero and <standard deviation>
$$
\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2\sigma},\qquad \Delta x\,\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2}.
$$
For example, in dimensionless units with $\hbar=1$, $\sigma=1/2,1,2$ give $(\Delta x,\Delta p)=(1/2,1),(1,1/2),(2,1/4)$. The <Gaussian wave packets> all saturate the same bound: narrowing one density broadens the other. More precisely, choose a reference length $L$ and set $X=x/L$, $P=pL/\hbar$; then $[X,P]=iI$, and the plotted widths are $\sigma/L$ and $L\Delta p/\hbar$.

\Image[gaussian-position-momentum-uncertainty.png]
{title=Position and momentum probability densities for three minimum-uncertainty Gaussian preparations: dimensionless widths 0.5, 1, and 2 in position give reciprocal momentum widths 1, 0.5, and 0.25}

The <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> connects localization to the cost in <kinetic energy>. In a <quantum harmonic oscillator> with <mass> $m>0$ and <angular frequency> $\omega>0$, the <variance> contribution to the <expectation value> of the <energy> is bounded using the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality>:
$$
\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2}{2}\geq\omega\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}.
$$
The mean-dependent contributions are nonnegative. The <Gaussian eigenstate for a quadratic potential> with $\sigma^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)$ and zero means reaches this <ground state> energy. This is why the tradeoff is more than a limitation on preparation: it predicts an unavoidable positive minimum <energy>.

Heisenberg's original article appeared in March 1927 as "Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik", Zeitschrift für Physik 43, pp. 172–198. Its abstract discusses simultaneous imprecision of canonically conjugate quantities and explanatory thought experiments. The precise <standard deviation> formulation here distinguishes preparation spreads from a slogan about measurement disturbance. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01397280[W. Heisenberg, original 1927 article and publisher metadata]{ref}.

= Confinement energy bound from position support
{parent=Heisenberg uncertainty relation}
{title2=$\langle K\rangle\geq d\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$}

Suppose each Cartesian coordinate of a particle of mass $m$ is supported in an interval of length $L$, in $d$ dimensions. <Popoviciu's inequality on variances> gives $\Delta x_i\leq L/2$, and the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> gives $\Delta p_i\geq\hbar/L$. Since $\langle p_i^2\rangle=(\Delta p_i)^2+\langle p_i\rangle^2$,
$$
\boxed{\langle K\rangle\geq\frac{d\hbar^2}{2mL^2}.}
$$
This is a rigorous but non-sharp confinement estimate. For a cubical <infinite square well>, separation of variables gives the stronger exact ground-state value $d\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$. One must not identify a support length with a uniquely prescribed <standard deviation> or claim that the uncertainty estimate is the exact ground-state energy.

= Preparation uncertainty
{parent=Heisenberg uncertainty relation}

<Preparation uncertainty> describes the outcome spread intrinsic to a specified <quantum state preparation>. Operationally, prepare many copies of the same <quantum state>, measure <position> on one group and <momentum> on another, and estimate the two <variances>. Ideal measurements still have the spreads predicted by the <Born rule>. No earlier <position> measurement need occur in the <momentum> group. The <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> constrains these two distributions belonging to the same preparation.

<Measurement backaction> concerns how a particular <measurement in quantum mechanics> changes a <quantum state>. Instrument resolution concerns how its reported outcomes relate to the intended <observable>. Those questions require a model of the instrument and its interaction with the system. Substituting an instrument error and an induced change for $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ in the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> does not follow from its proof. The practical value of <preparation uncertainty> is that it bounds achievable input spreads before any such instrument model is chosen.

= Heisenberg's microscope
{parent=Heisenberg uncertainty relation}
{c}
{wiki}

<Heisenberg's microscope> is a <thought experiment>, not a report of a performed measurement. In <Werner Heisenberg>'s Nobel lecture, The development of quantum mechanics, delivered on 11 December 1933, the imagined <electron> is illuminated with very short-wavelength light and observed through a high-resolution <microscope>. A scattered <photon> entering the observer's eye would, through <Compton scattering>, eject the <electron> from its original path. The passage challenges a classical <trajectory> picture; its microscope is hypothetical. The lecture date is 1933 even though the award is the <Nobel Prize in Physics in 1932>.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/The Development of Quantum Mechanics by Werner Heisenberg>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; Werner Heisenberg, The development of quantum mechanics, heading and printed pp. 291–292; text inspected in the Studyres transcription]{ref}

The following idealized geometry makes the tradeoff visible. Let the collected light have <wavelength> $\lambda$ and let the <microscope objective> accept outgoing directions within a half-angle $\alpha$ about the vertical axis. Take $x$ transverse to that axis and hold the incoming <photon> <momentum> fixed. A <diffraction>-limited estimate of transverse resolution has scale $\delta x\sim\lambda/\sin\alpha$. The unobserved outgoing direction admits transverse <photon> <momentum> components between approximately $-(h/\lambda)\sin\alpha$ and $+(h/\lambda)\sin\alpha$, neglecting the angular variation of the scattered <wavelength>. By <conservation of momentum>, uncertainty in that component leaves uncertainty in the transverse recoil of the <electron>:
$$
\delta p_x\sim\frac{h}{\lambda}\sin\alpha,
\qquad
\delta x\,\delta p_x\sim h.
$$
Here $h=2\pi\hbar$ is the <Planck constant>; the two $\delta$ symbols denote heuristic resolution and recoil scales, not the <standard deviations> in the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>. Increasing the acceptance angle improves the estimated resolution while broadening the allowed recoil range. Numerical factors depend on the resolution criterion and angular distribution; this scaling argument cannot supply the sharp constant $\hbar/2$.

= Chirped Gaussian wave packet
{parent=Heisenberg uncertainty relation}
{title2=$\psi_{\sigma,\kappa}(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-(1-i\kappa)x^2/(4\sigma^2)}$}

A <chirped Gaussian wave packet> adds a quadratic <quantum phase> to the zero-mean <Gaussian wave packet> already evaluated above. Here $\sigma>0$ and $\kappa$ is a real dimensionless parameter. Its <probability density function> $|\psi_{\sigma,\kappa}(x)|^2$ is unchanged for every $\kappa$, so $\langle x\rangle=0$ and $(\Delta x)^2=\sigma^2$. Nevertheless, applying the <momentum operator> gives
$$
p\psi_{\sigma,\kappa}=\frac{\hbar(\kappa+i)x}{2\sigma^2}\psi_{\sigma,\kappa},
\qquad \langle p\rangle=0,
\qquad (\Delta p)^2=\|p\psi_{\sigma,\kappa}\|^2=\frac{\hbar^2(1+\kappa^2)}{4\sigma^2}.
$$
The sign of the <quantum covariance> follows directly, without assigning a classical trajectory to the <wavefunction>:
$$
\langle xp\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2}(\kappa+i),
\qquad C_{xp}=\operatorname{Re}\langle xp\rangle=\frac{\hbar\kappa}{2},
\qquad r=\frac{C_{xp}}{\Delta x\,\Delta p}=\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{1+\kappa^2}}.
$$
Here $r$ is a normalized symmetrized <quantum covariance>, analogous to a <correlation coefficient>. Positive $\kappa$ gives positive $C_{xp}$, and reversing $\kappa$ reverses $C_{xp}$ while leaving both <variances> unchanged. In the negative-exponent <Fourier transform> convention used above, the <momentum> density is
$$
|\widetilde\psi_{\sigma,\kappa}(p)|^2
=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2}{\pi\hbar^2(1+\kappa^2)}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\sigma^2p^2}{\hbar^2(1+\kappa^2)}\right).
$$
Consequently the <quantum uncertainty> product and the <covariance matrix> <determinant> tell different stories:
$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{1+\kappa^2},
\qquad
\det\Sigma=\frac{\hbar^2(1+\kappa^2)}4-\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}4=\frac{\hbar^2}4.
$$
Every member of this <pure state> family saturates the <Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation>. Only $\kappa=0$ saturates the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> for this fixed pair of <position> and <momentum> axes. Thus being a <Gaussian wave packet> alone does not imply a minimum $\Delta x\,\Delta p$.

For a concrete comparison, choose a reference <length> $L$, put $X=x/L$, $P=pL/\hbar$, and set $\sigma=L$. The choices $\kappa=0,+2,-2$ all have $(\Delta X)^2=1$, but give $(\Delta P)^2=1/4,5/4,5/4$ and $C_{XP}=0,+1,-1$, respectively. The two nonzero cases have $r=\pm2/\sqrt5$ and product $\sqrt5/2$; all three <covariance matrices> have <determinant> $1/4$.

\Image[chirped-gaussian-covariance-ellipse.png]
{title=Quadratic phase preserves the position density but broadens the momentum density: opposite chirps tilt covariance ellipses in opposite directions while preserving their area}

The <phase space> curves in the figure satisfy $z^T\Sigma^{-1}z=1$ in the dimensionless coordinates, where $z=(X,P)^T$. They display second moments, not observed simultaneous <position> and <momentum> pairs. Their common area is $\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}=\pi/2$, although their coordinate projections differ.

This distinction is useful for predicting focusing. Under <free particle> evolution with <mass> $m$, $x(t)=x(0)+tp(0)/m$, so
$$
(\Delta x(t))^2=\sigma^2+\frac{2t}{m}C_{xp}+\frac{t^2}{m^2}(\Delta p)^2.
$$
A negative chirp initially narrows the <position> distribution; positive chirp initially broadens it. For $\kappa<0$, the minimum occurs at $t_*=-2m\sigma^2\kappa/[\hbar(1+\kappa^2)]>0$ and has <variance> $\sigma^2/(1+\kappa^2)$. At that time the <quantum covariance> vanishes, and the unchanged <momentum> spread gives $\Delta x(t_*)\,\Delta p=\hbar/2$. The extra initial product was a usable position-momentum correlation, not extra <measurement backaction>.

= Quadratic-norm proof of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}

For an admissible <quantum state> with zero <position> and <momentum> means, positivity of the squared <norm> $\lVert(p-isx)\psi\rVert^2$ for every real $s$ gives
$$
(\Delta p)^2+s^2(\Delta x)^2-s\hbar\geq0.
$$
The <discriminant> is nonpositive, hence $\Delta x\,\Delta p\geq\hbar/2$. Subtracting the <expectation values> from the <position operator> and <momentum operator> extends this outline to nonzero means.

= Equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}

Equality in the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> requires the centred <vectors> $x'\psi$ and $p'\psi$ to be imaginary <scalar multiples>, where $x'=x-x_0I$, $p'=p-p_0I$ and $x_0,p_0$ are the <expectation values>. Thus, for some $s>0$,
$$
(p-p_0)\psi=is(x-x_0)\psi.
$$
In the <position> representation, using the <momentum operator> turns this into a first-order <differential equation>, whose normalizable solutions are
$$
\psi(x)=C\exp\left(\frac{ip_0x}{\hbar}-\frac{s(x-x_0)^2}{2\hbar}\right).
$$
Hence the <quantum states> saturating the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> are these <Gaussian wave packets>, with arbitrary <position> and <momentum> means and <global phase>. Their width is $\sigma^2=\hbar/(2s)$. A <Gaussian wave packet> with an additional quadratic <quantum phase> need not saturate this product bound.

= Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation
{parent=Robertson uncertainty principle}
{c}
{title2=$(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2-C_{AB}^2\geq|\langle[A,B]\rangle|^2/4$}

The <Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation> keeps the real part discarded in the proof of the <Robertson uncertainty principle>. Write $A'=A-\langle A\rangle I$ and $B'=B-\langle B\rangle I$. Their <quantum covariance> is $C_{AB}=\operatorname{Re}\langle A'\psi|B'\psi\rangle$. On the same finite-moment and product <operator domains> used above, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\geq|\langle A'\psi|B'\psi\rangle|^2
=C_{AB}^2+\frac14|\langle[A,B]\rangle|^2.
$$
For <position> and <momentum>, form the real, symmetrized <covariance matrix>
$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}(\Delta x)^2&C_{xp}\\C_{xp}&(\Delta p)^2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det\Sigma\geq\frac{\hbar^2}{4}.
$$
A larger coordinatewise product can therefore encode a tilted <covariance ellipse>, rather than a larger <determinant>. This explains why the <equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation> is more restrictive than equality in the <Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation>.

= Quantum covariance
{parent=Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation}
{title2=$C_{AB}=\tfrac12\langle A'B'+B'A'\rangle$}

For <self-adjoint operators> and an admissible <pure state>, the <quantum covariance> is the real, symmetrized second moment of two centered <observables>. The displayed expression uses their <anticommutator>; equivalently $C_{AB}=\operatorname{Re}\langle A'\psi|B'\psi\rangle$, which requires only the individual finite second moments. It reduces to ordinary <covariance> for commuting <observables>. For noncommuting <observables>, its definition does not assert that both have simultaneous sharp values drawn from a joint classical <probability distribution>.

= Gaussian eigenstate for a quadratic potential
{parent=Quantum theory}

For $U(x)=kx^2$ and $k>0$, the ansatz $\psi=Ce^{-\alpha x^2}$ solves the stationary Schrödinger equation when
$$
\alpha=\frac1\hbar\sqrt{\frac{mk}{2}},
\qquad
E=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{k}{2m}},
\qquad
C=\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}.
$$
It has $\Delta x=1/(2\sqrt\alpha)$ and $\Delta p=\hbar\sqrt\alpha$, so it saturates the Heisenberg bound.

= Classical mixture of coherent states
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$\rho=\int P(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,d^2\alpha,\quad P\ge0$}

A normalized nonnegative probability measure over <coherent states> produces a classical mixture. The measure may contain <Dirac delta distributions>, so a pure <coherent state> is included. Conditioning on $\alpha$ gives <Poisson distribution> <photon statistics>; hence $\operatorname{Var}n=\langle n\rangle+\operatorname{Var}(|\alpha|^2)\ge\langle n\rangle$. Each <optical quadrature> has variance at least its vacuum value because mixing adds nonnegative variance of conditional means. These inequalities provide simple <nonclassicality witnesses>.

= Glauber-Sudarshan representation
{parent=Classical mixture of coherent states}
{c}
{title2=$\rho=\int P(\alpha)|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,d^2\alpha$}

The diagonal <coherent state> representation writes a single-mode <density matrix> in the form shown. Its weight may be a generalized distribution rather than an ordinary nonnegative probability density. A <nonclassical state of light> has no nonnegative measure of this form. This use of $P$ differs from the <Wigner distribution>; a nonnegative Wigner function does not by itself certify classicality.

= Coherent-field condensate variational state
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$|\psi\rangle=e^{-\bar N/2}\exp(\int\psi\widehat\Psi^\dagger)|0\rangle$}

A normalized bosonic field coherent state with $\bar N=\int|\psi|^2$ has $\langle\widehat\Psi(\mathbf r)\rangle=\psi(\mathbf r)$. For a normal-ordered contact-interacting Hamiltonian, its <energy> expectation is $\int[\hbar^2|\nabla\psi|^2/(2m)+V|\psi|^2+g|\psi|^4/2]d^3r$. Minimizing at fixed mean particle number gives the stationary <Gross-Pitaevskii equation>. = Poisson counting in a coherent state
{parent=Coherent state}

The normalized <coherent state> has number amplitude $e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!}$, hence the displayed Poisson law with mean and <variance> $|\alpha|^2$. A product of coherent modes has factorized number <probabilities>.

= Passive linear transformation of coherent states
{parent=Coherent state}

A lossless mode transformation maps a product of <coherent states> to another product of <coherent states> with transformed complex amplitudes. <unitarity> preserves the total mean particle number. It does not generally turn a fixed-number input into <coherent states>.

= Coherent-state overlap
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle=e^{-(|\beta|^2+|\alpha|^2)/2+\beta^*\alpha}$}

Expand both states in the <Fock basis> and sum the exponential series to obtain the displayed overlap. In particular $\langle\alpha|-\alpha\rangle=e^{-2|\alpha|^2}$. Distinct <coherent states> are nonorthogonal at finite separation; dropping this overlap can give incorrect normalization and small-amplitude limits.

= Displacement operator
{parent=Coherent state}
{wiki}
{title2=$D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha a^\dagger-\alpha^*a}$}

The displacement operator translates oscillator <phase space>. It is <unitary> and obeys $D(\alpha)^\dagger aD(\alpha)=a+\alpha$ and $D(\alpha)|0\rangle=|\alpha\rangle$, a <coherent state>. The <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> with a scalar <commutator> gives
$$
D(\alpha)=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}e^{\alpha a^\dagger}e^{-\alpha^*a},\qquad
D(\alpha)D(\beta)=e^{(\alpha\beta^*-\alpha^*\beta)/2}D(\alpha+\beta).
$$
The conjugation on $\alpha^*$ ensures that the exponent is anti-Hermitian for arbitrary complex displacement. For real $\alpha=\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}x_0$, this becomes the spatial translation $e^{-ix_0p/\hbar}$.

= Coherent state after shifting an oscillator equilibrium
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$\alpha_0=-\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}\,x_0$}

Suddenly shifting a <quantum harmonic oscillator> equilibrium from zero to a signed position $x_0$ leaves the old <ground state> unchanged at the instant of the <quantum quench>. The new <annihilation operator> is $a'=a-\lambda$, with $\lambda=\sqrt{m\omega/(2\hbar)}x_0$, so $a'|0\rangle=-\lambda|0\rangle$. In the translated <energy eigenstates> $|n'\rangle=e^{-ipx_0/\hbar}|n\rangle$, the expansion is
$$
|0\rangle=e^{-\lambda^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-\lambda)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n'\rangle.
$$
The sign follows by translating the old vacuum by $-x_0$ before expanding it in the old <Fock states>. A <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> with central <commutator> separates the resulting exponential into creation and annihilation parts. Evolution with the shifted <Hamiltonian> preserves this <coherent state>, with $\alpha(t)=\alpha_0e^{-i\omega t}$. Its mean occupation is $|\alpha_0|^2$ and its mean energy above the new potential minimum is $\hbar\omega(|\alpha_0|^2+1/2)$. Omitting $(-\lambda)^n$ in favour of $|\lambda|^n$ changes the state when $x_0>0$.

= Coherent-state oscillator expectation values
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$\langle x\rangle_t=\sqrt{2\hbar/(m\omega)}\operatorname{Re}(\alpha e^{-i\omega t})$}

A normalized oscillator <coherent state> obeys $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$, so its mean <occupation number> is $N=|\alpha|^2$. Under the <quantum harmonic oscillator> <Hamiltonian operator> $\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)$, its evolution is $|\alpha(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega t/2}|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle$. Inserting $x=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(a+a^\dagger)$ and $p=-i\sqrt{m\hbar\omega/2}(a-a^\dagger)$ gives
$$
\langle x\rangle_t=\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}}\operatorname{Re}(\alpha e^{-i\omega t}),\qquad
\langle p\rangle_t=\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}\operatorname{Im}(\alpha e^{-i\omega t}).
$$
These mean values obey the classical <harmonic oscillator> equations. The displacement amplitude is $\sqrt{2\hbar N/(m\omega)}$, whose classical orbit has <energy> $\hbar\omega N$. The full quantum mean <energy> has the additional <zero-point energy> $\hbar\omega/2$. Indeed the variances are $\hbar/(2m\omega)$ and $m\hbar\omega/2$, and their kinetic and potential contributions sum to $\hbar\omega/2$.

= Coherent-state resolution of identity
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$\int d[\bar\psi,\psi]e^{-\bar\psi\psi}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=I$}

For an <unnormalized bosonic coherent state>, the measure is $d^2\psi/\pi$ over the complex plane. Gaussian moments $\int d^2\psi\,e^{-|\psi|^2}\psi^n\bar\psi^m/\pi=n!\delta_{nm}$ recover the identity in the <Fock state> basis. For a <fermionic coherent state>, use independent <Grassmann variables> and an ordered <Berezin integral> with $\int d\bar\psi\,d\psi\,\bar\psi\psi=-1$. The coefficient of $-\bar\psi\psi$ in the weighted projector is the identity. Products of these one-mode measures give the finite-mode result.

= Unnormalized bosonic coherent state
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$|\psi\rangle=e^{\psi a^\dagger}|0\rangle$}

An <unnormalized bosonic coherent state> is $|\psi\rangle=e^{\psi a^\dagger}|0\rangle$ for a complex label $\psi$. The <canonical commutation relation> gives $a|\psi\rangle=\psi|\psi\rangle$ and overlap $\langle\psi|\psi'\rangle=e^{\bar\psi\psi'}$. Multiplication by $e^{-|\psi|^2/2}$ produces a normalized <coherent state>. The unnormalized form makes the <coherent-state resolution of identity> and thermal time slicing particularly simple.

= Fermionic coherent state
{parent=Coherent state}
{title2=$a|\theta\rangle=\theta|\theta\rangle$}

For a single <fermionic annihilation operator> $a$ and an independent odd <Grassmann variable> $\theta$, a <fermionic coherent state> is an extended state obeying $a|\theta\rangle=\theta|\theta\rangle$. With $\{a,\theta\}=0$ one may take $|\theta\rangle=\exp(-\theta a^\dagger)|0\rangle$. Its overlap is exponential in the Grassmann labels. A resolution of identity with a <Berezin integral> gives the time-slice construction of a <fermionic path integral>. These formal eigenstates use Grassmann-valued coefficients.

= Fermionic coherent-state trace
{parent=Fermionic coherent state}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}A=\int d[\bar\psi,\psi]e^{-\bar\psi\psi}\langle-\psi|A|\psi\rangle$}

The ordinary trace of an even fermionic operator requires $-\psi$ in the bra of its <fermionic coherent state> kernel. An untwisted bra gives $A_{00}-A_{11}$, the <supertrace>. This distinction produces the antiperiodic <coherent-state thermal boundary conditions>.

= First Born approximation
{synonym}

The Born approximation replaces the unknown total field inside a scattering integral by the known incident field, thereby retaining only single scattering. In nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, first-order Lippmann-Schwinger iteration gives
$$
f(\mathbf k',\mathbf k)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\widetilde V(\mathbf k'-\mathbf k).
$$

= Born amplitude for a Yukawa potential
{c}
{parent=Born approximation}

For $V(r)=Ae^{-\kappa r}/r$, with real $A$ and $\kappa>0$, the <first Born approximation> gives the <scattering amplitude>
$$
f_B(q)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int_{\mathbb R^3}e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(r)\,d^3r
=-\frac{2mA}{\hbar^2(q^2+\kappa^2)}.
$$
Indeed, orienting the polar axis along the <momentum transfer> gives the angular integral $4\pi\sin(qr)/(qr)$, and the remaining integral is $\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa r}\sin(qr)\,dr=q/(q^2+\kappa^2)$. The <differential scattering cross-section> is $|f_B(q)|^2$.

The limit $\kappa\to0^+$ at fixed nonzero $q$ produces the <Rutherford differential cross-section> for a <Coulomb potential>. The unscreened interaction has long range, so this limiting calculation by itself does not establish the validity of a naive <Born approximation> to the full <Rutherford scattering> state. The exact <Rutherford scattering> amplitude has an additional phase, while its modulus agrees with the <Rutherford differential cross-section>.

= Delta-shell Born amplitude
{parent=Born approximation}
{title2=$f_B(\theta)=-gR\,\operatorname{sinc}[2kR\sin(\theta/2)]$}

For a real shell potential $V(r)=V_0\delta(r-R)$, define $g=2mV_0R/\hbar^2$ and $\operatorname{sinc}u=\sin u/u$, with value one at zero. Angular integration of its three-dimensional <Fourier transform> gives $4\pi V_0R^2\operatorname{sinc}(qR)$, where the <momentum transfer> has wave-number magnitude $q=2k\sin(\theta/2)$. Substitution in the <first Born approximation> proves the displayed <scattering amplitude>. It is real. Its squared <modulus> is a nonzero order-$V_0^2$ <differential scattering cross-section>, whose <optical theorem> counterpart requires the order-$V_0^2$ imaginary part of the forward amplitude from the next Born term. Thus squaring the first-order amplitude does not supply a consistently unitary second-order amplitude.

The Toffoli, or controlled-controlled-NOT, gate maps $|x_1x_2y\rangle$ to $|x_1x_2,y\mathbin\oplus x_1x_2\rangle$.

= Modular-addition quantum oracle
{parent=Quantum theory}
{title2=$U_h$}

For $h:\mathbb Z_N\to\mathbb Z_M$, its modular-addition oracle is the <unitary operator>
$$
U_h|x,y\rangle=|x,y+h(x)\bmod M\rangle.
$$
For each fixed $x$, <addition> by $h(x)$ permutes the answer register's <computational basis>. This preserves <inner products>, even when $h$ is not injective.

= Modular-oracle inversion by negation
{parent=Modular-addition quantum oracle}

Let $S_M|y\rangle=|-y\bmod M\rangle$. The <modular-addition quantum oracle> obeys
$$
U_h^{-1}=(I\otimes S_M)U_h(I\otimes S_M).
$$
The three steps replace $y$ by $-y$, then $-y+h(x)$, then $y-h(x)$. Thus a query to the inverse costs one forward query and two known <unitary operators>. The negation operator is a <permutation matrix> with $S_M^2=I$.

For a two-variable <Boolean quantum oracle>, <quantum phase kickback> produces the phase $(-1)^{axy+bx+cy+d}$ on $|xy\rangle$. = Bernstein-Vazirani decoding controlled by a quantum register

= Lieb-Robinson bound
{c}
{parent=Quantum theory}
{wiki}

A Lieb-Robinson bound limits the commutator of initially separated local observables by $\lVert[A(t),B]\rVert\leq C\lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert e^{-\mu(d-v|t|)}$. It gives local lattice dynamics an effective causal cone with Lieb-Robinson velocity $v$.

= Full-evolution comparison for truncated dynamics
{parent=Lieb-Robinson bound}
{title2=$\|\tau_t^H(A)-\tau_t^{H_j}(A)\|\leq\sum_{Y\notin H_j}\int_0^{|t|}\|[\tau_\sigma^H(A),h_Y]\|d\sigma$}

A Duhamel comparison can bound truncation error using <commutators> evolved under the full Hamiltonian. This avoids assuming that a black-box <Lieb-Robinson bound> for $H$ also holds for every truncation. Polynomial shell weights and exponential distance suppression make the sum over omitted shells convergent. Comparing two truncations through the full evolution bounds their difference.

= Lieb-Robinson interaction-chain expansion
{c}
{parent=Lieb-Robinson bound}

Iterating the integral <commutator> inequality gives ordered interaction chains with factors $(2|t|)^n/n!$. A per-site interaction weight at most $se^{-\mu}$ bounds each successive chain extension by $ks e^{-\mu}$. No chain shorter than the <interaction distance> connects separated supports. Reversing chains supplies the smaller support prefactor, producing the <Lieb-Robinson bound>. Overlapping supports require retaining the equal-time <commutator> term.

= Interaction distance
{parent=Lieb-Robinson bound}
{title2=$d(X,Y)$}

The <interaction distance> between disjoint supports can count the minimum number of interacting hyperedges in a chain connecting them. Overlapping supports have distance zero in the usual support convention. Distances between interaction terms can instead assign shell zero only to the central term and positive shells to distinct terms. The convention must be specified when using a truncated neighborhood Hamiltonian: ordinary zero support distance includes distinct overlapping terms.

= Lieb-Robinson localization by Haar twirling
{parent=Lieb-Robinson bound}
{c}
{title2=$v=4ks/\mu$}

Apply <Haar twirling conditional expectation> to the complement of a neighbourhood around the initial operator support. A <Lieb-Robinson bound> on commutators with arbitrary complement-supported unitaries then bounds the approximation error without summing over sites. For a bound proportional to $e^{-\mu d}(e^{2kst}-1)$, choosing $v=4ks/\mu$ gives an error at most $\mu vt\,|X|\|A_X\|e^{-\mu l/2}$ outside radius $vt+l$.

= Finite-depth local quantum circuit
{parent=Lieb-Robinson bound}

A depth-$D$ circuit of gates with range at most $r$ expands the support of a local observable by at most $rD$. States connected by a depth bounded independently of system size are regarded as belonging to the same short-range-entangled phase when no required symmetry is violated.

= Backward light cone of a local quantum circuit
{parent=Finite-depth local quantum circuit}

The backward light cone of an output observable is the set of input degrees of freedom that can influence it. In a depth-$D$ circuit with gate range at most $r$, it lies within distance $rD$ of the observable's output support.

= GHZ-state circuit-depth lower bound
{c}
{parent=Finite-depth local quantum circuit}

The GHZ state has nonzero connected correlations between arbitrarily distant sites. A local circuit starting from a product state can create such a correlation only when the two backward light cones overlap, giving depth at least proportional to the system's linear size.

= Grover's algorithm
{c}
{parent=Quantum theory}
{wiki}

= Grover search algorithm
{c}
{synonym}

Grover search rotates a uniform state toward a marked subspace and finds one of $M$ marked entries using order $\sqrt{N/M}$ oracle calls.

= Grover iteration in a nonorthogonal basis
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{c}
{title2=$G=\begin{pmatrix}1&2a\\-2a&1-4a^2\end{pmatrix}$}

For normalized marked and uniform states $|w\rangle,|s\rangle$ with real overlap $a\in(0,1)$, the two reflections $(2|s\rangle\langle s|-I)(I-2|w\rangle\langle w|)$ have the displayed matrix in that ordered nonorthogonal basis. The coordinate <Gram matrix> is $S=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\a&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $G^\dagger SG=S$. With $a=\sin t$, its <eigenvalues> are $e^{\pm2it}$. Applied $r$ times to $|s\rangle$, it gives marked-state success $\sin^2((2r+1)t)$. In these coordinates the overlap with the marked state is the first coefficient plus $a$ times the second; it is not generally the first coefficient alone.

= First half-success time in Grover search
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{title2=$r_{1/2}=\left\lceil\frac{\pi}{8\chi}-\frac12\right\rceil$}

For both oracle phases equal to $\pi$, the good/bad matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\chi&\sin2\chi\\-\sin2\chi&\cos2\chi\end{pmatrix}$. Acting $r$ times on the initial vector $(\sin\chi,\cos\chi)$ yields success $\sin^2((2r+1)\chi)$. For $0<\chi\le\pi/4$, the first crossing of one half is the displayed integer, since the rounded angle stays between $\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$. With $\sin^2\chi=m/N\ll1$ this is asymptotic to $(\pi/8)\sqrt{N/m}$. Iterating indefinitely is incorrect: the success oscillates after its first maximum.

= Continuous-time quantum search
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{title2=$H=\Delta(P_s+P_w),\quad t_* =\pi/(2\Delta|\langle s|w\rangle|)$}

Take normalized states with real positive overlap $a\in(0,1)$. The eigenvectors proportional to $|s\rangle\pm|w\rangle$ have energies $\Delta(1\pm a)$. With $\hbar=1$, their relative phase changes by $\pi$ after the displayed time, giving $e^{-iHt_*}|s\rangle=-ie^{-i\Delta t_*}|w\rangle$. For a uniform search state and one marked basis vector in dimension $D$, $a=D^{-1/2}$, so the time is proportional to $\sqrt D$. Implementing the marked projector assumes access to a search oracle; the formula does not supply oracle information for free.

= Grover search with a nonzero reflection label
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{c}
{title2=$G_y=T_yGT_y,\quad T_y|x\rangle=(-1)^{x\cdot y}|x\rangle$}

Let $|s\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0^n\rangle$, let $U_f$ be the <marked-state phase oracle>, and replace reflection about $|0^n\rangle$ by reflection about a known <computational basis> vector $|y\rangle$. Define $T_y=\bigotimes_{j=0}^{n-1}Z_j^{y_j}$, so $T_y|x\rangle=(-1)^{x\cdot y}|x\rangle$ and $H^{\otimes n}|y\rangle=T_y|s\rangle$. The resulting Grover iterate obeys
$$
G_y=(2|s_y\rangle\langle s_y|-I)U_f=T_yGT_y,\qquad |s_y\rangle=T_y|s\rangle,
$$
because the two diagonal operators $T_y,U_f$ commute. Thus the good and bad uniform vectors are replaced by their signed vectors $T_y|g\rangle,T_y|b\rangle$, with exactly the same <Grover rotation angle>. Initializing in $H^{\otimes n}|y\rangle$ gives $G_y^r|s_y\rangle=T_yG^r|s\rangle$. Computational-basis measurement probabilities are unchanged because $T_y$ only multiplies amplitudes by signs.

= Verified Grover promise test
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{title2=$k=\lfloor\pi/(4\theta)\rfloor,\quad\sin\theta=N^{-1/2}$}

Under the promise of either no marked entry or one marked entry in an $N$-element <Boolean quantum oracle> search space, run the <Grover search algorithm> for the displayed number of iterations, measure a candidate, and make one additional oracle query to check it. Since $|(2k+1)\theta-\pi/2|\leq\theta$, the marked-entry success probability is at least $\cos^2\theta=1-1/N$. For $N\geq4$ this is at least $3/4$. If no entry is marked the verification never accepts, so there is no false positive. The query count is $k+1=O(\sqrt N)$ because $\theta\geq1/\sqrt N$. Measuring a candidate without verification would not determine whether a marked entry exists.

= Marked density under a permutation
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{title2=$p=K/N$}

For a <permutation> $f$ of an $N$-element set and a known marked output subset of size $K$, exactly $K$ inputs have marked outputs. The <uniform quantum superposition> thus has good probability $K/N$, independent of which permutation the oracle represents. This allows a known-angle <amplitude amplification> schedule without counting unknown preimages.

= Permutation-preimage quantum search
{parent=Marked density under a permutation}
{title2=$O(\sqrt{N/K})$}

To find an input of a <permutation> whose output lies in a known $K$-element subset, compute the output, apply its <marked-state phase oracle>, and use <uncomputation>. A <modular-addition quantum oracle> costs two forward queries per marked reflection, because <modular-oracle inversion by negation> supplies its inverse. <Amplitude amplification> then uses $O(\sqrt{N/K})$ queries for fixed success probability. Positive-square outputs have $K=\lfloor\sqrt{N-1}\rfloor$ and hence query cost $O(N^{1/4})$.

= Known-subset Grover search
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{title2=$O(\sqrt{|B|/k})$}

For a known finite set $B$, prepare the <uniform superposition state> $|s_B\rangle$ and implement its <reflection operator> $2|s_B\rangle\langle s_B|-I$. If exactly $k$ basis states in $B$ are marked, the same two-dimensional <Grover rotation angle> argument uses $O(\sqrt{|B|/k})$ phase queries. Preparation and reflection are known operations with no calls to the unknown predicate. Their gate cost must be accounted for separately. The success guarantee requires $k>0$, and choosing the optimal iteration number uses a known $k$.

= Clean subset superposition preparation
{parent=Known-subset Grover search}
{title2=$|0^n0^m\rangle\longmapsto |\psi_A\rangle|0^m\rangle$}

Suppose a known list of $2^m$ distinct $n$-bit strings $a_j$ is available and <computational basis> transpositions on $n+m$ <qubits> have polynomial-size implementations. Start with the <uniform superposition state> of index labels $j$, leaving the data zero. First swap $|0^n,j\rangle$ with $|a_j,j\rangle$ for each nonzero $a_j$. Next, for each $j\ne0$, swap $|a_j,j\rangle$ with $|a_j,0^m\rangle$. Distinct index labels separate the first-stage pairs, and distinct data strings separate the second-stage pairs. Thus no later transposition disturbs an earlier term. At most $2^{m+1}-1$ transpositions prepare the uniform data state and return the <quantum ancilla> to zero. For $m=O(\log n)$ the <quantum circuit> has polynomial size. This assumes the list is available; a membership predicate alone does not supply it.

= Grover diffusion operator
{parent=Grover's algorithm}
{c}

For $|s\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0^n\rangle$, the diffusion operator is $2|s\rangle\langle s|-I=-H^{\otimes n}I_0H^{\otimes n}$, where $I_0=I-2|0^n\rangle\langle0^n|$.

= Inversion about the mean
{parent=Grover diffusion operator}

The diffusion operator sends every computational-basis amplitude $a_x$ to $2\overline a-a_x$, where $\overline a$ is their arithmetic mean.

= Grover rotation angle
{parent=Grover's algorithm}

= Deutsch algorithm
{c}
{parent=Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm}

The one-input Deutsch algorithm determines whether a <Boolean function> is constant or balanced using one <Boolean quantum oracle> call. Initialize the input <qubit> in $|+\rangle$ and the answer <qubit> in $|-\rangle$. <Quantum phase kickback> changes the input to $(-1)^{f(0)}(|0\rangle+(-1)^{f(0)\oplus f(1)}|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. A <Hadamard gate> and <computational-basis measurement> therefore return the parity $f(0)\oplus f(1)$ with certainty.

= Deutsch algorithm with pure dephasing
{c}
{parent=Deutsch algorithm}
{title2=$P_{\mathrm{correct}}=\tfrac12(1+v\cos\alpha)$}

If the input <qubit> of the <Deutsch algorithm> acquires conditional environment states with overlap $\langle e_0|e_1\rangle=ve^{i\alpha}$ between its two <Hadamard gates>, its fixed zero-for-constant, one-for-balanced rule succeeds with probability $(1+v\cos\alpha)/2$. A known phase can be compensated before the last <Hadamard gate>, raising the success probability to $(1+v)/2$. An orthogonal pair of environment states gives $v=0$ and destroys all information in the input <qubit> about the answer; unit overlap modulus preserves coherence up to a known phase.

= Opposite-pair elimination for exact quantum balance testing
{parent=Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm}

= Opposite-pair elimination
{synonym}

For an even-length Boolean string, one phase query followed by a known <unitary extension> can produce a zero-pair outcome with amplitude proportional to the zero-minus-one imbalance, or an index pair with amplitude proportional to the difference of their phase signs. A zero-pair observation certifies nonbalance. Every nonzero pair has opposite bits, so deleting it preserves the imbalance and allows recursion on two fewer indices. = Variational method
{parent=Quantum theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$E_0\leq E_\psi$}

The <variational method> estimates the lowest <energy> of a <quantum Hamiltonian> by choosing a trial <wavefunction>, evaluating its <energy>, and optimizing the trial parameters. It uses the <quantum variational principle>, the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> expressed for <quantum states>. Let $H$ be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below, and define $E_0=\inf\sigma(H)$, the bottom of its <spectrum>. Every normalized admissible trial <quantum state> satisfies
$$
E_\psi\geq E_0,\qquad \|\psi\|=1.
$$
When $E_0$ is an <energy eigenvalue>, it is the <ground-state energy>; equality holds precisely for a <quantum state> in the <ground-state subspace>. Otherwise the bottom of the <spectrum> need not be attained by any normalizable <wavefunction>.

Admissible means finite form energy. Choose a real $b\leq E_0$ and use the shifted <quadratic form of a positive quantum Hamiltonian>:
$$
Q(H)=D((H-bI)^{1/2}),\qquad q_H[\psi]=\|(H-bI)^{1/2}\psi\|^2+b\|\psi\|^2,\qquad E_\psi=q_H[\psi].
$$
The form domain $Q(H)$ is independent of this choice of $b$ and can be larger than the <operator domain> $D(H)$. For $\psi\in D(H)$, $E_\psi=\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle$ is the usual <expectation value>; for nonzero unnormalized trials, divide $q_H[\psi]$ by $\|\psi\|^2$ to get the form version of the <Rayleigh quotient>. See the <quantum variational principle> for the general domain justification.

Thus each trial <energy> is an **upper bound** on $E_0$, and minimizing within a trial family improves that upper bound. It is not a lower bound on $E_0$. The <Gaussian variational energy for the harmonic oscillator> reaches $E_0=1/2$ because its family contains an exact <ground state>. The <exact variational solution in a two-level system> makes the equality condition visible as vanishing excited-state <probability amplitude>.

The method is useful when solving the <Schrödinger equation> exactly is difficult but integrals of a trial <wavefunction> are tractable. Enlarging a trial family cannot increase its optimal <energy>, although an optimum need not be attained. The beauty is that even an imperfect trial gives a rigorous bound, provided its form energy is computed exactly: numerical integration errors require separate control. The <Gaussian variational estimate for a solvable sextic potential> provides an existing example beyond a <quantum harmonic oscillator>.

A short proof explains the direction. With a complete <orthonormal eigenbasis> of <energy eigenstates>, write $\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n$, where $H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n$ and $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$. Then
$$
E_\psi-E_0=\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_0)\geq0.
$$
This is a <weighted mean> of nonnegative <energy> differences; equality requires every occupied <energy eigenstate> to have <energy> $E_0$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-05-quantum-physics-ii-fall-2013/61bc31b8d8bf0680c322733910a71aa0_MIT8_05F13_Chap_01.pdf[Barton Zwiebach, Wave Mechanics, MIT 8.05, 2013, §5, pp. 18–20]{ref}. For <continuous spectrum>, the <spectral theorem> replaces the sum by an integral against the <scalar spectral measure> of $\psi$, with the same nonnegative integrand. This proves the bound without assuming an <orthonormal eigenbasis> exists.

The historical setting predates <quantum mechanics>: Rayleigh's *The Theory of Sound*, volume I, appeared in 1877, and Ritz's paper on solving variational problems of mathematical physics appeared in volume 135 of Crelle's journal, catalogued by its publisher as 1909, pp. 1–61. These are original works associated with the <Rayleigh-Ritz method>; the dates here identify the publications rather than assert priority. https://archive.org/details/theorysound06raylgoog[Lord Rayleigh, The Theory of Sound, volume I, 1877]{ref}; https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1909.135.1[Walter Ritz, Über eine neue Methode zur Lösung gewisser Variationsprobleme der mathematischen Physik, original article and publisher metadata]{ref}.

= Gaussian variational bounds for a quartic oscillator
{c}
{parent=Variational method}
{title2=$E_0\leq(3\hbar^2/8m)(6mk/\hbar^2)^{1/3}$}

For $H=p^2/(2m)+kx^4$ with $k>0$, the normalized even trial <wavefunction> $f_\alpha=(\alpha/\pi)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$ has <Rayleigh quotient>
$$
\mathcal E_0(\alpha)=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{4m}+\frac{3k}{4\alpha^2}.
$$
The <Gaussian moment recurrence> gives the potential term, and <integration by parts> gives the kinetic term. Its minimum is at $\alpha^3=6mk/\hbar^2$, proving the displayed upper bound by the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>.

An odd trial family $g_\beta=(4\beta^3/\pi)^{1/4}x e^{-\beta x^2/2}$ is orthogonal to the exact even <ground state>. The <Sturm oscillation theorem> identifies the lowest odd state as the first excited state, so minimizing within this family bounds $E_1$:
$$
\mathcal E_1(\beta)=\frac{3\hbar^2\beta}{4m}+\frac{15k}{4\beta^2},\quad
\beta_*^3=\frac{10mk}{\hbar^2},\quad
E_1\leq\frac{9\hbar^2}{8m}\left(\frac{10mk}{\hbar^2}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
Orthogonality to an approximate ground trial alone would not justify this excited-state bound; odd parity ensures orthogonality to the exact ground state. Both bounds exhibit the characteristic quartic-oscillator <energy> scale $(\hbar^4k/m^2)^{1/3}$.

= Gaussian trial for a quadratic-plus-quartic oscillator
{parent=Gaussian variational bounds for a quartic oscillator}
{c}
{title2=$E_\epsilon(\alpha)=\alpha/2+1/(2\alpha)+3\epsilon/(4\alpha^2)$}

For $H=-d^2/dx^2+x^2+\epsilon x^4$, use the normalized real <wavefunction> $\psi_\alpha=(\alpha/\pi)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$, $\alpha>0$. The <Gaussian integral> gives $\langle x^2\rangle=1/(2\alpha)$ and $\langle x^4\rangle=3/(4\alpha^2)$, while the kinetic contribution is $\alpha/2$. Thus the <Rayleigh quotient> is the displayed expression. For positive $\epsilon$, its stationary width satisfies $\alpha^3-\alpha-3\epsilon=0$. At $\epsilon=2$ the unique positive solution is $\alpha=2$, giving $E_0\leq13/8$ by the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>. For $\epsilon<0$, taking $\alpha\downarrow0$ makes the negative quartic contribution dominate, so the energy is unbounded below even within this normalized family. The positive-coupling bound and negative-coupling instability concern different signs of the same variational expression.

= Gaussian variational energy for the harmonic oscillator
{parent=Variational method}
{c}
{title2=$E(\sigma)=1/(8\sigma^2)+\sigma^2/2$}

Use the <quantum harmonic oscillator> in units with reduced <Planck constant> $\hbar=1$, <mass> $m=1$, and <angular frequency> $\omega=1$. Its <Hamiltonian operator> and trial <Gaussian wave packet> are
$$
H=-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad \psi_\sigma(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4\sigma^2}\right),\qquad \sigma>0.
$$
This is the same <Gaussian wave packet> used for the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>: $|\psi_\sigma|^2$ is a <normal distribution> with mean zero and <variance> $\sigma^2$. The <Gaussian integral> gives <wavefunction normalization> and $\langle x^2\rangle=\sigma^2$. These smooth, rapidly decaying trials lie in the <one-dimensional harmonic oscillator form domain> and even in the <operator domain>.

Since $\psi_\sigma'=-x\psi_\sigma/(2\sigma^2)$, <integration by parts> with vanishing boundary terms gives the <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>:
$$
\langle T\rangle=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R}|\psi_\sigma'|^2\,dx=\frac{1}{8\sigma^2},\qquad \langle V\rangle=\frac12\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\sigma^2}{2},\qquad E(\sigma)=\frac{1}{8\sigma^2}+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}.
$$
Narrow trials cost <kinetic energy>, while broad trials cost <potential energy>. The exact difference
$$
E(\sigma)-\frac12=\frac{(2\sigma^2-1)^2}{8\sigma^2}\geq0
$$
shows that the unique optimal width is $\sigma^2=1/2$, with $E=1/2$. At $\sigma=1/2$ and $\sigma=1$, the trial <energies> are both $5/8=0.625$; the <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> contributions exchange places.

{description=The <kinetic energy> decreases and the <potential energy> increases with the width of the <Gaussian wave packet>. Their sum touches the exact <ground-state energy> only at $\sigma=1/\sqrt2$.}

Minimizing within this trial family alone establishes $E_0\leq1/2$. To establish exactness, use the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> to obtain $E_\psi\geq1/2$ for every admissible <quantum state>, as in its existing oscillator example. Together the bounds give $E_0=1/2$. Direct substitution also yields $H\psi_{1/\sqrt2}=\psi_{1/\sqrt2}/2$, agreeing with the <Gaussian eigenstate for a quadratic potential>. This is a useful distinction: the <variational method> supplies the upper bound, while the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation> supplies the complementary lower bound.

= Domain assumptions in singular-potential virial arguments
{parent=Fall to the centre for a supercritical inverse-power potential}

For the formal one-dimensional operator $H=p^2/(2m)-a|x|^{-p}$ with $a>0$ and $p>2$, take a smooth normalized test function supported away from the origin. Its normalized <dilation> $\psi_\lambda(x)=\sqrt\lambda\psi(\lambda x)$ has
$$
\mathcal E(\lambda)=\lambda^2T+\lambda^pU,\qquad T>0,\quad U<0.
$$
As $\lambda\to\infty$, this tends to minus infinity. Thus the unregularized expression has <fall to the centre for a supercritical inverse-power potential> and cannot define a semibounded <Hamiltonian operator> on a domain containing these test functions.

If a putative <bound state> has separately finite $T,U$ and admits norm-preserving dilation variations inside its <operator domain> or energy domain, stationarity gives $2T+pU=0$, hence $E=(1-2/p)T>0$, incompatible with a negative-energy bound state below the zero continuum threshold. For a putative ground state, the second scaling derivative is $2(2-p)T<0$, also incompatible with a minimum. The argument's finite-energy and domain assumptions are essential: it proves absence of a stable ground state and excludes bound states to which this ordinary virial calculation applies. An unqualified assertion about all formal square-integrable solutions at the singular origin needs a specified self-adjoint realization; adding a short-distance cutoff changes the problem.

= Exact variational solution in a two-level system
{parent=Variational method}
{title2=$E(\theta)=2\sin^2\theta$}

In a <two-level system>, let the <Hamiltonian operator> have <matrix> $H=\operatorname{diag}(0,2)$ in the <orthonormal basis> $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. The normalized real trial <quantum states> and their <Rayleigh quotient> are
$$
|\psi_\theta\rangle=\cos\theta\,|0\rangle+\sin\theta\,|1\rangle,\qquad E(\theta)=\langle\psi_\theta|H|\psi_\theta\rangle=2\sin^2\theta.
$$
The excited-state <probability amplitude> is $\sin\theta$, so its <probability> is $\sin^2\theta$. Thus $E(\theta)\geq0=E_0$, with equality if and only if the excited-state <probability amplitude> vanishes: $\theta\in\pi\mathbb Z$. The remaining <quantum state> is $|0\rangle$ up to a <global phase>. At $\theta=\pi/4$ the trial <energy> is $1$; at $\theta=\pi/2$ it is $2$. These are upper bounds on the <ground-state energy>, even though neither is a minimizing trial.

The real trial family contains the <ground state>, so it attains the exact minimum despite not covering every complex <quantum state>. More generally, a family covering all normalized <vectors> in a two-dimensional <Hilbert space> gives the exact lowest <eigenvalue> of any <Hermitian matrix> by minimizing its <Rayleigh quotient>. For a complex trial $c_0|0\rangle+c_1|1\rangle$ with $|c_0|^2+|c_1|^2=1$, the same calculation gives $E=2|c_1|^2$: a relative <quantum phase> cannot change this diagonal <Hamiltonian> energy. This finite example is the <weighted mean> proof of the <variational method> with just two <energy eigenstates>.

= Fermi-energy oscillations in a two-dimensional electron gas

= Filling factor
{synonym}

For a <two-dimensional electron gas> in a perpendicular <magnetic field> of magnitude $B>0$, the <filling factor> is the <electron> number per unit <area> $n_s$ divided by the orbital degeneracy per unit <area> $eB/h$ of one <spin> branch of a <Landau level>. = Carrier density from spin-degenerate quantum oscillations
{parent=Filling factor (quantum Hall effect)}

One resolved spin branch of a two-dimensional <Landau level> has degeneracy $eB/h$ per area. Thus the total <carrier density> is $n_s=\nu eB/h$, where the <filling factor> $\nu$ counts all occupied spin and other internal branches, including partial filling. If consecutive unsplit orbital peaks change filling by an unresolved degeneracy $g_d$,
$$
n_s=\frac{g_d e}{h\Delta(1/B)}.
$$
For only spin degeneracy, $g_d=2$. Once spin splitting is resolved, adjacent individual branches differ by one rather than two; counting split subpeaks as unsplit orbital peaks gives a factor-two error.

= Ground-state filling of two spin-degenerate Landau levels

= Cyclotron resonance
{parent=Cyclotron frequency}

<Cyclotron resonance> is resonant absorption by charged particles in a <magnetic field> when an applied oscillating electric field drives their cyclotron motion. For a parabolic band, the resonance frequency is $\omega_c=|q|B/m^*$, so measuring it determines the <cyclotron effective mass>. In the quantum description, transverse <electric dipole transitions> connect adjacent <Landau levels>, whose separation is $\hbar\omega_c$. For an electron and a field along $z$, only the circular polarization rotating in the electron's orbital sense drives the positive-frequency resonance. Scattering broadens the absorption peak; resolving it requires an orbital period short compared with the relaxation time.

= Localizing a Bell pair from a GHZ state
{parent=Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger state}
{title2=$|\mathrm{GHZ}\rangle_{ABC}\longrightarrow|\Psi^+\rangle_{AB}$}

= GHZ contradiction for N congruent to 3 modulo 4
{parent=GHZ theorem}
{c}
{title2=$N\equiv3\pmod4$}

For $|G_N^-\rangle=(|0\rangle^{\otimes N}-|1\rangle^{\otimes N})/\sqrt2$, let $O_j$ have one <Pauli X gate> at site $j$ and <Pauli Y gates> elsewhere, and let $X_N=X^{\otimes N}$. The relations $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$, $Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle$ show that $O_j|G_N^-\rangle$ is proportional to $|G_N^-\rangle$ exactly when $N$ is odd. In that case its <eigenvalue> is $\lambda_N=(-1)^{(N+1)/2}$, while $X_N$ always has <eigenvalue> $-1$.

Under the <EPR criterion of reality> and locality, each site's $X$ and $Y$ outcomes have predetermined values $x_j,y_j\in\{\pm1\}$. Multiplying the $N$ certain equations $x_j\prod_{k\ne j}y_k=\lambda_N$ gives $\prod_jx_j=\lambda_N$ when $N$ is odd, since every $y_k$ appears an even number of times. This contradicts the certain quantum value $-1$ precisely when $N\equiv3\pmod4$. Thus the displayed <GHZ state> gives an infinite family of perfect-correlation contradictions with <local hidden-variable theory>, without an inequality.

If $r$ is even, $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$, and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$, then $\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,N)$ is a nontrivial factor unless the order was not minimal.

= Probability of a useful unit in Shor factorization
{parent=Factor extraction from an even modular order}
{title2=$\Pr(r\text{ even},a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\bmod N)\geq1/2$}

For an odd integer $N$ having at least two distinct prime divisors, choose a uniform unit $a\bmod N$. The <Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups> makes its odd-prime-power components independent cyclic groups. Failure of <factor extraction from an even modular order> requires all components to have equal 2-primary order exponents. For any component each possible exponent has probability at most $1/2$, so conditioning on another component bounds failure by $1/2$. Excluding the identity unit only improves the success proportion.

= Quantum order finding
{parent=Shor's algorithm}

Quantum order finding Fourier-transforms a superposition of arguments having the same modular-exponentiation value, producing peaks near multiples of the reciprocal period.

= Modular multiplication eigenstates
{parent=Quantum order finding}
{title2=$|u_s\rangle=r^{-1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi isj/r}|x^j\bmod n\rangle$}

For $\gcd(x,n)=1$ and order $r$, multiplication by $x$ modulo $n$ is a <unitary operator>. On the orbit of one it cyclically permutes $r$ basis states. The displayed orthonormal <eigenstates> have <eigenvalues> $e^{2\pi is/r}$, and $|1\rangle=r^{-1/2}\sum_s|u_s\rangle$. Thus <quantum phase estimation> on this efficiently prepared state samples a uniform $s$ without knowing $r$. Controlled powers are implemented by <quantum modular exponentiation>. Sufficient phase resolution allows <continued-fraction recovery in quantum order finding>; the reduced denominator equals $r$ only when $s$ is coprime to $r$.

= Quantum Fourier sampling bound for order finding
{parent=Quantum order finding}
{title2=$\Pr(|j/Q-s/r|\leq1/(2Q)\mid s)\geq4/\pi^2$}

In <quantum order finding>, a phase $s/r$ produces amplitude $Q^{-1}\sum_{x=0}^{Q-1}e^{2\pi ix(s/r-j/Q)}$ after an inverse <quantum Fourier transform>. Its nearest grid point has probability at least $4/\pi^2$, by bounding the sine ratio of this <finite geometric Fourier amplitude>. The initial modular orbit state is an equal-weight superposition of the $r$ phase eigenstates, so the phase label is uniform.

= Quantum modular exponentiation
{parent=Quantum order finding}

Quantum modular exponentiation is the <reversible computation> that maps
$$
|x\rangle|0\rangle\longmapsto|x\rangle|a^x\bmod N\rangle.
$$
It can be implemented in <polynomial time> in the bit lengths of $x$ and $N$ by <repeated squaring> and controlled modular multiplication.

= Fourier transform of a finite periodic comb
{parent=Quantum order finding}
{c}

The Fourier amplitude of equally spaced basis states is a finite geometric sum with peaks where the Fourier phase increment is nearly one.

= Finite geometric Fourier amplitude
{parent=Fourier transform of a finite periodic comb}

For $A$ terms with phase ratio $q$, the amplitude factor is $(1-q^A)/(1-q)$, with limiting value $A$ when $q=1$.

= Continued-fraction recovery in quantum order finding
{parent=Quantum order finding}

If a measured ratio approximates a reduced $k/r$ within $1/(2r^2)$, continued-fraction convergents recover the candidate denominator $r$.

= Uniqueness of a rational approximation with bounded denominator
{parent=Continued-fraction recovery in quantum order finding}

Two distinct reduced fractions with denominators below $N$ differ by more than $1/N^2$, so an interval of radius $1/(2N^2)$ contains at most one.

= Environmental decoherence
{synonym}

= Decoherence
{synonym}

Quantum decoherence is the suppression of observable phase coherence when a system becomes entangled with unobserved environmental degrees of freedom. In a preferred basis, tracing out the environment damps off-diagonal entries of the system's <reduced density matrix> while leaving the corresponding populations unchanged.

= Single-qubit environment dephasing model
{parent=Quantum decoherence}
{title2=$z(t)=\cos(2gt/\hbar)$}

A detector qubit with $H=gZ_D\otimes X_E$ and environment initially $|0\rangle_E$ generates conditional states $|E_\pm\rangle=\cos A|0\rangle\pm i\sin A|1\rangle$, where $A=gt/\hbar$. Their overlap is $\cos2A$. Tracing out the environment multiplies detector coherences by this number while preserving the populations. Orthogonal records occur at $A=\pi/4$, but coherence returns later. For equal initial detector populations the reduced purity is $(1+\cos^22A)/2$. Thus complete reduced dephasing at one time does not imply irreversible global collapse.

= Pointer basis
{parent=Quantum decoherence}

A basis of apparatus records robust under the relevant environmental interaction. Its meaning is dynamical and depends on the coupling and the time scale, rather than on an arbitrary coordinate choice for the apparatus <Hilbert space>.

= Environment-induced pointer-basis selection
{parent=Pointer basis}

If record projectors commute with an apparatus-environment interaction, their populations are preserved while the corresponding environmental states can become distinguishable. Their <conditional environment overlap> then suppresses interference in the apparatus <reduced density matrix>. A nondegenerate record coupling can thereby select robust sectors; commutation alone does not select a unique basis in a degenerate or trivial interaction.<Pointer basis of quantum apparatus: Into what mixture does the wave packet collapse? by W. H. Zurek>[1981 abstract: recorded observables and the apparatus-environment interaction commutation condition]{ref}

= Zurek spin-bath model
{c}
{parent=Quantum decoherence}
{title2=$H=-Z_D\otimes\sum_k g_kZ_k$}

A device qubit couples diagonally to a finite bath of spins. Conditional bath states acquire opposite phase rotations, and their <conditional environment overlap> multiplies the device coherence. An initially aligned bath leaves the overlap's modulus at one. Initially equatorial spins yield $z_N(t)=\prod_k\cos(2g_kt)$. Global <unitary time evolution> remains reversible, and a <finite spin-bath coherence recurrence> prevents a strict zero long-time limit at finite bath size.

= Random-coupling spin-bath decoherence
{parent=Zurek spin-bath model}

Independent random couplings suppress typical coherence as the bath size increases. This conclusion concerns an ensemble or a specified large-bath limit, and must be distinguished from a pointwise long-time limit for one finite realization. <Ensemble spin-bath coherence> supplies exact mean and mean-square formulas for a uniform coupling distribution, while <short-time Gaussian spin-bath decoherence> gives the initial decay scale.

= Short-time Gaussian spin-bath decoherence
{parent=Random-coupling spin-bath decoherence}
{title2=$z_N(t)\simeq e^{-2Ng_*^2t^2/3}$}

For initially equatorial spins and uniform couplings on $[0,g_*]$, expand $\log\cos(2g_kt)=-2g_k^2t^2+O(g_k^4t^4)$. The <strong law of large numbers> then gives $\sum g_k^2\simeq Ng_*^2/3$. On the scale $t=O((g_*\sqrt N)^{-1})$, the total fourth-order remainder tends to zero, giving the Gaussian coherence envelope. This approximation concerns early times and does not remove <finite spin-bath coherence recurrence>.

= Ensemble spin-bath coherence
{parent=Random-coupling spin-bath decoherence}
{title2=$\mathbb E z_N(t)=\operatorname{sinc}(2g_*t)^N$}

For independent uniform couplings on $[0,g_*]$ and equatorial initial bath spins,
$$
\mathbb E z_N(t)=\left[\frac{\sin(2g_*t)}{2g_*t}\right]^N,\qquad
\mathbb E|z_N(t)|^2=\left[\frac12+\frac{\sin(4g_*t)}{8g_*t}\right]^N.
$$
At fixed finite $N$, the mean tends to zero and the mean square tends to $2^{-N}$ as time grows. At any fixed nonzero time, the mean-square bracket is strictly below one; the <Markov inequality> proves coherence tends to zero in probability as $N$ grows. Individual finite realizations still exhibit <finite spin-bath coherence recurrence>.

= Finite spin-bath coherence recurrence
{parent=Zurek spin-bath model}
{title2=$z_N(t_j)\to1\quad\text{for some }t_j\to\infty$}

For any finite set of couplings in $z_N(t)=\prod_k\cos(2g_kt)$, the simultaneous <Dirichlet approximation theorem> supplies arbitrarily late near-alignment of all phases, or an exact common period. Thus finite bath coherence does not tend to zero as time tends to infinity. This remains true for almost every realization of continuous random couplings; an ensemble mean can decay while each realization has recurrences.

= Decoherence factor
{parent=Quantum decoherence}

If a joint pure state is $\alpha|0\rangle|E_0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle|E_1\rangle$, the off-diagonal entry of the system's <reduced density matrix> is multiplied by the environmental overlap $\langle E_1|E_0\rangle$. Independent repeated interactions multiply these overlaps.

= Conditional environment overlap
{parent=Decoherence factor}
{title2=$z=\langle E_1|E_0\rangle$}

For a pure state $a|0\rangle|E_0\rangle+b|1\rangle|E_1\rangle$ with normalized environment states, the upper-right device <reduced density matrix> entry is $ab^*\langle E_1|E_0\rangle$. Distinguishability of the conditional environment states suppresses device coherence. States differing only by a global phase have unit-modulus overlap and do not cause <quantum decoherence>.

= Single-qubit interference with conditional environment states
{parent=Conditional environment overlap}
{title2=$P_0=\tfrac12[1+v\cos(\phi+\alpha)]$}

Prepare a <qubit> in $|+\rangle$, apply a <phase gate> $P(\phi)=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\phi})$, and correlate its <computational basis> states with normalized environment states $|e_0\rangle,|e_1\rangle$. Write $\langle e_0|e_1\rangle=ve^{i\alpha}$. After a final <Hadamard gate>, the environment amplitude accompanying output zero is $(|e_0\rangle+e^{i\phi}|e_1\rangle)/2$. Its squared <norm> is $P_0=[1+v\cos(\phi+\alpha)]/2$. Thus $v$ is the <interference visibility>, while $\alpha$ shifts the fringe. This <quantum decoherence> map commutes with the <phase gate>, so placing it immediately before that gate gives the same probabilities.

= Quantum recoherence
{parent=Quantum decoherence}

Quantum recoherence is a later restoration of phase coherence. It can occur when a finite environment revisits a state having substantial overlap with its initial conditional state.

A thought experiment placing a friend's physical measurement record inside a larger isolated quantum laboratory. Its contrast with a collapsed mixture is an instance of the <quantum measurement problem>; it does not require giving consciousness a special dynamical law. <Eugene Wigner> used the observer-within-the-system construction to examine such tensions.

{c}
{parent=Measurement problem}
{wiki}

A thought experiment correlating a microscopic quantum alternative with macroscopically distinct records, introduced by <Erwin Schrödinger> to sharpen the problem of applying unitary evolution to a measuring apparatus. The resulting entangled superposition and the mixture produced by selecting an outcome are different joint <density operators>. <Quantum decoherence> can make the records' reduced density operator approximately diagonal without selecting a single global component.

{parent=Measurement problem}

The problem of explaining which basis represents stable measurement records. A maximally entangled <quantum premeasurement> state can have several equally weighted <Schmidt decompositions>. Choosing a different decomposition does not alter that pure state, but declaring a projection in a different basis generally changes the resulting mixture. <Environment-induced pointer-basis selection> supplies a dynamical account of robust records, while leaving the further question of a single realized outcome distinct.

= Relative state
{synonym}

For a bipartite vector $|v\rangle\in\mathcal H_R\otimes\mathcal H_A$ and a chosen orthonormal basis of $R$, the generally unnormalized relative state on $A$ is obtained by the partial inner product with one basis vector. It gives the expansion $|v\rangle=\sum_j|j\rangle\otimes|\eta_j\rangle$. The squared norm of a relative state is the probability of its corresponding basis outcome when $|v\rangle$ is normalized. An index state is a chosen orthonormal basis vector in the indexing subsystem of a <relative state> decomposition. It labels the corresponding partial inner product; it need not be an eigenvector of the subsystem's <reduced density matrix>.

= Conjugate index vector
{parent=Index state}
{title2=$|\phi_B^*\rangle=\sum_jc_j^*|j\rangle_B$}

Fix paired orthonormal bases and let $|\phi\rangle_A=\sum_jc_j|j\rangle_A$. Its conjugate index vector in the other <Hilbert space> uses the conjugated coefficients. For the unnormalized <maximally entangled state> $|\widetilde\Psi\rangle=\sum_j|j\rangle_A|j\rangle_B$, the partial inner product is $(I\otimes\langle\phi_B^*|)|\widetilde\Psi\rangle=|\phi\rangle_A$. = Partial trace

The partial trace over $B$ is the linear operation characterized by
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[M_A\operatorname{Tr}_B(X_{AB})]
=\operatorname{Tr}[(M_A\otimes I_B)X_{AB}].
$$
Applied to a <density operator>, it produces a <reduced density matrix>.

= Partial trace positivity from product vectors
{parent=Partial trace}
{title2=$\langle u,\operatorname{Tr}_B(\rho)u\rangle=\sum_b\langle u\otimes e_b,\rho(u\otimes e_b)\rangle$}

The <partial trace> of a <positive operator> is positive by this sum of nonnegative expectations in an orthonormal basis of the traced factor. Its trace equals the original trace, using the product orthonormal basis. Basis independence follows from $\operatorname{Tr}[(\operatorname{Tr}_B\rho)A]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(A\otimes I)]$, which uniquely determines the reduced operator. Thus reductions of <density matrices> remain density matrices. For a positive trace-class operator the same argument uses convergent nonnegative basis sums.

= Haar twirling conditional expectation

For an operator on a tensor-product <Hilbert space>, averaging unitary conjugations on subsystem $B$ with normalized <Haar measure> gives $\mathcal E_A(O)=(\operatorname{Tr}_B O/d_B)\otimes I_B$. If a Hamiltonian changes sufficiently slowly while an eigenstate remains separated from the rest of the spectrum by a nonzero gap, evolution keeps the state in the corresponding instantaneous eigenspace up to a dynamical and geometric phase. = Instantaneous eigenstate coupling

Expanding a <quantum state> in instantaneous <energy eigenstates> separates <dynamical phases> from the coupling $A_{mn}$. <orthonormality> makes $A$ anti-Hermitian. A local Hamiltonian is a sum of terms, each acting nontrivially on only a bounded number of subsystems.

= Operator support
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}

The <operator support> is the set of subsystems on which an operator may act nontrivially; it acts as the identity outside that set. <Unitary time evolution> can enlarge the support, while a <Lieb-Robinson bound> controls the error of replacing the evolved operator by one with smaller support. = Stoquastic Hamiltonian
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}

A <stoquastic Hamiltonian> is a real Hermitian <Hamiltonian operator> with nonpositive off-diagonal entries in a specified basis, usually the <computational basis>. The property depends on that basis. <Permutation matrices> in a <quantum circuit>'s propagation terms and $|-\rangle\langle-|$ input penalties give a useful family of such operators.

= Spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}
{title2=$g_Z=\int w(t)e^{itH}h_Ze^{-itH}\,dt$}

A real nonnegative normalized filter $w$ defines $g_Z=\int w(t)e^{itH}h_Ze^{-itH}\,dt$. In an energy <eigenbasis>, it multiplies each matrix element by $\widehat w(E_i-E_j)$. A Fourier cutoff at the <spectral gap> removes couplings between a unique <ground state> and excitations. The filtered terms still sum to $H$, because the total Hamiltonian is unchanged by its own <unitary time evolution>. Each term commutes with the ground projector, but need not be minimized there.

= Almost-exponential locality of filtered Hamiltonian terms
{parent=Spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms}
{title2=$\|A^{(Z,d)}\|\leq O(\|h_Z\|d^{\alpha+1}e^{-cd/(\log(cd))^2})$}

In a <telescoping local-shell decomposition>, split the <spectral filter> integral at a time proportional to the shell distance. A <Lieb-Robinson bound> controls short times, while the two-sided almost-exponential <spectral filter> tail controls long times. Polynomial shell-weight growth then gives the displayed decay. The estimate is asymptotic at large distance; small shells retain the elementary operator-norm bound. The <evenization of a nonnegative bandlimited filter> can supply the two-sided tail from a one-sided existence statement.

= Filtering preserves existing frustration freeness
{parent=Spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms}

If each local term is already minimized by the global <ground state>, subtract its minimum $a_Z$ to obtain a <positive operator>. Nonnegative normalized <spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms> preserves positivity and leaves the ground eigenvalue $a_Z$ unchanged. Thus the same state minimizes every filtered term. This preserves <frustration freeness> but does not create it for an initially frustrated decomposition.

= Commuting with a ground projector does not imply frustration freeness
{parent=Spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms}

The condition $[g_Z,P_0]=0$ makes a unique global <ground state> an <eigenvector> of every filtered term, but its termwise eigenvalues can exceed the corresponding minima. For example $h_{12}=|1\rangle\langle1|_1+|0\rangle\langle0|_2$ and $h_{23}=|1\rangle\langle1|_3+2|1\rangle\langle1|_2$ commute with their sum. Their sum is uniquely minimized by $|000\rangle$, yet $h_{12}$ is lower on $|010\rangle$. Filtering leaves these commuting terms unchanged, so it cannot force <frustration freeness>.

= Telescoping local-shell decomposition
{parent=Spectral filtering of Hamiltonian terms}

Filter a fixed local term using a nested sequence of neighbourhood Hamiltonians $H_s$, and call the results $G_s$. The differences $G_s-G_{s-1}$ give a shell decomposition. When $H_0=h_Z$ and $H_D=H$, normalization gives $G_0=h_Z$ and telescoping gives $g_Z=h_Z+\sum_{s=1}^D(G_s-G_{s-1})$. Individual shell terms need not commute with the full ground projector.

= Local Hamiltonian problem
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}
{title2=$\lambda_{\min}(\sum_jh_j)\leq a\quad\text{or}\quad\lambda_{\min}(\sum_jh_j)\geq b$}

Given a polynomial list of fixed-locality Hermitian terms and thresholds $a<b$ separated by at least an inverse polynomial, distinguish whether the minimum <eigenvalue> of their sum is at most $a$ or at least $b$. Terms may be normalized to $0\leq h_j\leq I$ with polynomial rescaling. This <promise problem> is in <QMA> by <local-energy measurement verification>.

= Local-energy measurement verification
{parent=Local Hamiltonian problem}

Uniformly sample one positive local term and measure the effect $h_j$. The averaged energy-flag effect on a witness is $H/M$, where $M$ is the number of terms. Repetition with a threshold halfway between the promised flag probabilities uses $O(M^2/(b-a)^2)$ witness registers for constant error. A spectral tensor-product analysis, as in <QMA parallel repetition with entangled witnesses>, proves soundness against entangled witnesses. Constant-locality <positive operator-valued measures> are efficiently implementable using one <ancilla qubit>.

= Diagonal Hamiltonian
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}

A diagonal Hamiltonian is diagonal in a specified product basis, usually the computational basis. Every basis state is therefore an <energy eigenstate>, and optimization of its diagonal entries becomes minimization of the corresponding classical cost function.

= Frustration-free quantum Hamiltonian
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}

= Frustration-free Hamiltonian
{synonym}

A quantum Hamiltonian $H=\sum_jh_j$ with positive-semidefinite local terms is frustration free when one state minimizes every $h_j$ simultaneously. After shifting each local minimum to zero, that state lies in every kernel $\ker h_j$ and has zero total energy.

= Detectability lemma
{parent=Frustration-free quantum Hamiltonian}
{title2=$\|K|_{\operatorname{supp}H}\|\leq(1+\Delta/2)^{-1/3}$}

In a nearest-neighbour <frustration-free Hamiltonian> chain, multiply the bond <orthogonal projections> in two disjoint alternating layers. The resulting product $K$ fixes the common <ground state> space but contracts its orthogonal complement by a factor bounded away from one in terms of the <spectral gap>. A bound $\|K|_{\operatorname{supp}H}\|\leq(1+\Delta/2)^{-1/3}$ implies exponentially convergent powers. The product is generally neither Hermitian nor normal.

= Local projector cone in a frustration-free chain
{parent=Detectability lemma}

To evaluate a layered product on $A_x|\psi_0\rangle$, scan the bond <orthogonal projections> from right to left. A projection disjoint from the current support commutes through the accumulated operator and disappears against the common <ground state>. Retained projections expand the support by at most one site per layer. Their product $L$ gives a local operator $LA_x$ with both $K^rA_x|\psi_0\rangle=LA_x|\psi_0\rangle$ and $\langle\psi_0|LA_x=\langle\psi_0|A_x$.

= Exponential clustering from layered projectors
{parent=Local projector cone in a frustration-free chain}
{title2=$\alpha=\tfrac13\ln(1+\Delta/2)$}

Combine a <local projector cone in a frustration-free chain> with the contraction bound from the <detectability lemma>. If the localized operator misses a second observable's support, their commutation and the exact ground-state bra identity convert the <connected correlation function> into an approximation error. The two-projection power estimate yields spatial decay rate $\alpha=\frac13\ln(1+\Delta/2)$ for the stated detectability contraction.

= Frustration freeness
{parent=Frustration-free quantum Hamiltonian}
{title2=$E_0(H)=\sum_ZE_0(h_Z)$}

A decomposition $H=\sum_Zh_Z$ has <frustration freeness> when a global <ground state> minimizes every term simultaneously. For finite-dimensional Hermitian terms this implies $E_0(H)=\sum_ZE_0(h_Z)$. Shifting each term by its minimum produces <positive operators> with a common null vector. The property depends on the decomposition, not only the total operator.

= k-local Hamiltonian
{title2=$k$-local Hamiltonian}
{parent=Local Hamiltonian}

A $k$-local Hamiltonian is a sum of terms that each act nontrivially on at most $k$ subsystems. The integer $k$ remains fixed as the system size grows.

= Product-formula Hamiltonian simulation
{parent=Hamiltonian simulation}

Product-formula Hamiltonian simulation approximates $e^{-it(A+B)}$ by alternating exponentials of $A$ and $B$, which are assumed easier to implement separately.

= First-order two-local Hamiltonian simulation
{parent=Product-formula Hamiltonian simulation}
{title2=$O(M+M^3t^2/\epsilon)$}

For a sum of $M$ Hermitian two-local terms of norm below one, repeat a first-order <Lie-Trotter product formula> step $r$ times. The <first-order unitary product-formula error bound> gives total error at most $t^2\sum_{j<k}\|[H_j,H_k]\|/(2r)<M(M-1)t^2/(2r)$ for $M>1$. Taking $r=\max(1,\lceil M(M-1)t^2/(2\epsilon)\rceil)$ uses $Mr$ arbitrary two-qubit gates. At unit time and $M=O(n^2)$ this is a sufficient $O(n^6/\epsilon)$ bound; additional commutation structure can improve it.

= Second-order product formula
{parent=Product-formula Hamiltonian simulation}

The symmetric second-order formula, also called Strang splitting, is
$$
e^{-i\delta A/2}e^{-i\delta B}e^{-i\delta A/2}
=e^{-i\delta(A+B)}+O(\delta^3)
$$
for bounded operators, with the constant controlled by nested <commutators>.

= Simulation of a computable diagonal Hamiltonian
{parent=Hamiltonian simulation}

Let $A|x\rangle=a(x)|x\rangle$, where a <reversible circuit> $C$ computes $a(x)$ into a clean register. Then applying $e^{-ita(x)}$ as a phase on that register and performing <uncomputation> implements $e^{-itA}$ on the data register. For $a(x)=(-1)^{f(x)}$, a Boolean output qubit suffices: $C^\dagger(I\otimes e^{-itZ})C$ acts as $e^{-itA}$ when that qubit starts in $|0\rangle$. This exact construction avoids a <Lie-Trotter product formula> because the eigenvalue is computed directly.

= Pauli-string phase by parity computation
{c}
{parent=Simulation of a computable diagonal Hamiltonian}
{title2=$e^{itZ^{\otimes n}}$}

The <Pauli Z gates> give <eigenvalue> $(-1)^{\sum_jx_j}$ on $|x\rangle$. Use <parity computation by CNOT gates> to put the <parity bit> in a zero ancilla, apply $e^{itZ}$ to it, and uncompute. The data acquire exactly $e^{it(-1)^{\sum_jx_j}}$, and the ancillary line returns to zero. This <compute-phase-uncompute construction> needs $2n+1$ one- and <two-qubit gates>. Accumulating parity into the last data line instead uses $2n-1$ gates without an extra ancilla. Neither implementation drops the global phase.

= Block encoding
{parent=Quantum theory}
{wiki}

A block encoding of a matrix $A$ is a <unitary operator> whose designated block equals $A/\alpha$ for a known normalization factor $\alpha$. It gives a quantum circuit coherent access to matrices that need not themselves be unitary.
