= Temperature
{wiki}

Temperature labels <thermal equilibrium>: two internally equilibrated bodies have the same <temperature> when contact permitting only <heat> exchange produces no net <heat transfer>. The <zeroth law of thermodynamics> makes the comparison transitive. If bodies A and B separately reach <thermal equilibrium> with the same <thermometer> without appreciably disturbing either body, they agree with each other. This is what lets a <gas thermometer> compare a metal block with <water>, despite their different <internal energies> and <heat capacities>. Insulation alone makes no such comparison: it can prevent <heat transfer> between bodies at different <temperatures>. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/1.1 Temperature and Thermal Equilibrium>[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, section 1.1: temperature and the zeroth law]{ref}

The <zeroth law of thermodynamics> establishes equality comparisons, but does not choose a numerical scale. <Thermodynamic temperature> supplies an absolute scale consistent with <Carnot's theorem> and <entropy>. For a <simple compressible system> at <thermodynamic equilibrium> with fixed particle number $N$ and composition,
$$
T=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N},\qquad
\frac1T=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N}.
$$
Here $U$ is <internal energy>, $S$ is <entropy>, and $V$ is <volume (thermodynamics)>; the second equality assumes the derivative can be inverted. Temperature is a <thermodynamic conjugate variable> to <entropy> and belongs to the <intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities> as an intensive quantity. Its <partial derivative> tells us how much <internal energy> must change per unit change of <entropy> under the stated constraints. More general systems require their other mechanical and material variables to be held fixed too. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S1#S1.SS2.SSS2[David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 1.2.2: temperature from the entropy derivative]{ref}

For an infinitesimal quantity $\delta Q>0$ of <heat> transferred from A to B, with no <work> or matter exchange, the equilibrium <entropy> changes give
$$
dS_{\rm total}=\delta Q\left(\frac1{T_B}-\frac1{T_A}\right).
$$
For ordinary positive <temperatures>, spontaneous <heat transfer> from $T_A>T_B$ increases total <entropy>; <thermal equilibrium> has $T_A=T_B$. This comparison uses changes in <entropy>, rather than the total <internal energy> contained in either body. A small hot object can have less <internal energy> than a large cool one. See <entropy maximization under thermal contact> for the equilibrium criterion and <negative temperature> for the special case of bounded <energy> spectra.

Express absolute <thermodynamic temperature> $T$ in <kelvin>, and a Celsius reading $t_C$ in <degree Celsius>:
$$
\frac{t_C}{{}^\circ\mathrm C}=\frac{T}{\mathrm K}-273.15,\qquad
\frac{\Delta t_C}{{}^\circ\mathrm C}=\frac{\Delta T}{\mathrm K}.
$$
The offset is exact: $300\,\mathrm K$ corresponds to $26.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, and $400\,\mathrm K$ to $126.85\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Their absolute <temperature> ratio is $4/3$; dividing their Celsius readings gives a different number. Thus <gas thermometer> pressure ratios and <Carnot's theorem> require <thermodynamic temperature>. The modern <SI> definition of the <kelvin> fixes the <Boltzmann constant> $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$; a reference bath realizes or transfers the scale rather than defining that constant.<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[section 2.3.1: kelvin and Celsius temperature]{ref}

Temperature is not universally the <mean> <kinetic energy> of a particle. In a classical, nonrelativistic <ideal gas> at <thermal equilibrium>, the <equipartition theorem> gives the mean translational <kinetic energy> relative to the gas's bulk motion:
$$
\left\langle\frac12m|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|^2\right\rangle=\frac32k_BT.
$$
Here $m$ is particle <mass>, $\mathbf v$ its <velocity>, and $\mathbf u$ the mean flow <velocity>. In this regime, different particle <masses> at the same <temperature> have equal mean translational <kinetic energies> but different characteristic speeds. A moving gas can also carry bulk <kinetic energy> without being hotter in its rest frame. Internal molecular modes and <quantum statistics> require further care; the <equipartition theorem> has classical assumptions, whereas the equilibrium <entropy> definition applies more widely. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.SS2.SSS1[Tong, Statistical Physics, section 2.2.1: classical equipartition and its limits]{ref}

= Kinetic temperature
{parent=Temperature}
{title2=$T_k$}

For an isotropic nonrelativistic Maxwellian particle population, <kinetic temperature> describes random translational motion, with mean kinetic energy $3k_BT_k/2$ per particle after subtracting bulk motion. Different species can have different kinetic temperatures outside thermal equilibrium. A rotational excitation temperature equals the kinetic temperature only when the excitation is sufficiently thermalized; a radiation brightness temperature is another distinct quantity.

= Absolute zero
{parent=Temperature}
{title2=$0\,\mathrm K$}
{wiki}

<Absolute zero> is the zero of <thermodynamic temperature>, corresponding to $-273.15\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. For a system with a nondegenerate <ground state>, equilibrium <entropy> approaches zero as <temperature> approaches <absolute zero>. This does not mean that every quantum <degree of freedom> loses its <zero-point energy>. The <entropy> statement and the operational <unattainability of absolute zero> require their respective physical assumptions.

= Adiabatic demagnetization
{parent=Temperature}

A cooling method that reduces an applied <magnetic field> while suppressing <heat transfer> to or from the material. For a suitable material exhibiting <paramagnetism>, an approximately reversible field reduction can lower its <temperature> at approximately constant <entropy>.

The preparation matters as much as the field reduction. First establish <thermal equilibrium> with a cold <heat bath>, then raise the <magnetic field> while maintaining the bath <temperature>: this is <magnetization> in an <isothermal process>. The bath accepts <heat> and the material loses <magnetic entropy>. Next open a <heat switch>, thermally isolating the working material, and lower the <magnetic field>. If the sweep maintains internal <thermal equilibrium> with negligible <entropy production>, this second leg is an <isentropic process>. An <adiabatic process> alone does not guarantee constant <entropy>.

The attraction is a controllable <energy> scale: changing the <magnetic field> changes the separation of magnetic <energy levels>, even across a thermally insulating gap. This lets an already cold solid become colder without needing a colder contacting <heat bath>. A practical <refrigerator> also needs a load, a period of absorbing its <heat>, and a reset that rejects accumulated <entropy> to the bath. A single field sweep has finite cooling capacity.

= Demagnetization with a linear-heat-capacity load
{parent=Adiabatic demagnetization}
{title2=$T_f=T_i-(N\theta/M)\Delta s_{\rm mag}/k_B$}

Let $N$ independent spin-$J$ moments and a load with <heat capacity> $C_L=Mk_BT/\theta$ remain in equilibrium during reversible isolated demagnetization. Then $S_L(T)=S_L(0)+Mk_BT/\theta$. Fully removing the field raises each spin <entropy> by its initial <magnetic entropy deficit>. Total <entropy> conservation gives $N\Delta s_{\rm mag}=Mk_B(T_i-T_f)/\theta$, yielding the title result. The formula assumes positive final <temperature>, validity of the load law throughout the interval, negligible additional <entropy> contributions and an attainable equilibrated low-field endpoint. Interactions require an actual many-body <entropy> instead of the independent-spin expression.

= Magnetic-entropy demagnetization adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic demagnetization}

When the <entropy> of independent magnetic moments dominates, a reversible isolated change fixes $B/T$. Lowering the field therefore lowers <temperature> as $T_f/T_i=B_f/B_i$. Other <heat capacities>, internal interactions and residual fields limit this ideal relation.

= Magnetic entropy
{parent=Adiabatic demagnetization}
{title2=$S_{\rm mag}$}

<Magnetic entropy> counts the uncertainty in the occupied magnetic <microstates>. For independent localized <magnetic moments> with one-site <probabilities> $p_j$,
$$
S_{\rm mag}=-Nk_B\sum_j p_j\ln p_j,
$$
where $N$ counts sites and $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. This is the <Gibbs entropy> for the magnetic degrees of freedom. Equal occupation of two states gives $Nk_B\ln2$; certain occupation of one state gives zero. Calling this disorder is useful only if the relevant states and their <probabilities> are specified. In an interacting material, correlations generally prevent replacing the many-body <Gibbs entropy> by this sum of independent one-site contributions.

Increasing the <magnetic field> at fixed positive <temperature> favors lower magnetic <energy levels>, reducing <magnetic entropy>. During ideal isolated demagnetization, both the level separation and <temperature> fall together, leaving the occupation <probabilities> unchanged. Thus the independent-spin idealization cools without increasing spin disorder along the isolated leg. When a real crystal's vibrating lattice also cools, its lost <entropy> can instead be taken up by the magnetic degrees of freedom. The conserved quantity is the total <entropy> of the thermally isolated stage, including any attached load in <thermal equilibrium>.

= Magnetic entropy deficit
{parent=Magnetic entropy}
{title2=$\Delta s_{\rm mag}=s_{\max}-s(T,B)$}

The <magnetic entropy deficit> is the <entropy> increase still available to independent moments as their field <polarization> is removed. For a spin-$J$ multiplet, $s_{\max}=k_B\ln(2J+1)$ and the high-temperature deficit is $k_BJ(J+1)[g\mu_BB/(k_BT)]^2/6$. Isothermal <magnetization> creates this deficit while rejecting heat to a bath. Subsequent reversible demagnetization can absorb <entropy> from a cooling load. The deficit, not the initial absolute <magnetic entropy>, measures that capacity.

= Entropy of a finite magnetic multiplet
{parent=Magnetic entropy}
{title2=$s_J/k_B=\ln Z_J-x\partial_x\ln Z_J$}

For magnetic quantum numbers $m=-J,\ldots,J$, energies $E_m=-mg\mu_BB$ and $x=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)$, the <partition function> is $Z_J=\sum_m e^{mx}=\sinh[(2J+1)x/2]/\sinh(x/2)$. Its <entropy> is the title expression, a function of $B/T$ alone. At small $J|x|$, $s_J/k_B=\ln(2J+1)-J(J+1)x^2/6+O(x^4)$. The maximal <entropy> is $k_B\ln(2J+1)$ at zero splitting. A fixed independent-spin <entropy> consequently fixes $B/T$; an attached heat load changes the combined isentropic path.

= Two-level paramagnetic cooling model
{parent=Magnetic entropy}
{title2=$x=\mu B/(k_BT)$}

Use the <independent spin-one-half two-level system> with fixed $N$ distinguishable sites and <Hamiltonian> levels $E_\pm=\pm\mu B$, where $\mu>0$ is the magnitude of the field-axis <magnetic moment> and $B>0$ is a uniform externally controlled <magnetic field>. Assume positive <temperature>, no interactions between sites, and no appreciable lattice or load <entropy>. The two levels differ by $2\mu B$. With <inverse temperature> $\beta$, the <canonical ensemble> gives the one-site and total <partition functions>
$$
\beta=\frac1{k_BT},\qquad x=\beta\mu B,\qquad
z=e^x+e^{-x}=2\cosh x,\qquad Z=z^N,
\qquad p_-=\frac{e^x}{2\cosh x},\quad p_+=\frac{e^{-x}}{2\cosh x}.
$$
Here the minus subscript denotes the lower <energy> $-\mu B$. Factorization of the <partition function> requires additive <energy> and independent sites; there is no particle-exchange factor $N!$ for these fixed distinguishable sites.

Differentiate the <partition function>, holding $B$ fixed when differentiating with respect to $\beta$. The <internal energy>, <Helmholtz free energy>, total field-axis <magnetic moment> $m$, and <magnetic entropy> are
$$
U_{\rm mag}=-\partial_\beta\ln Z=-N\mu B\tanh x,\qquad
F=-Nk_BT\ln(2\cosh x),
$$
$$
m=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial B}\right)_T=N\mu\tanh x,\qquad
\frac{S_{\rm mag}}{Nk_B}=\frac{\ln Z+\beta U_{\rm mag}}N
=\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x=:s(x).
$$
The extensive moment $m$ is distinct from <magnetization>, the moment per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>. Direct substitution of $p_\pm$ into the <Gibbs entropy> gives the same $s(x)$ independently of the free-energy <derivative>.

At fixed $B$, the magnetic <heat capacity> is
$$
C_{B,{\rm mag}}=\left(\frac{\partial U_{\rm mag}}{\partial T}\right)_B
=Nk_Bx^2\operatorname{sech}^2x\geq0,\qquad
\frac{ds}{dx}=-x\operatorname{sech}^2x<0\quad(x>0).
$$
Strict decrease makes $s$ invertible on $x>0$. Constant <magnetic entropy> therefore fixes $x$, not $B$ or $T$ separately:
$$
\frac{B}{T}=\text{constant},\qquad
\frac{T_f}{T_i}=\frac{B_f}{B_i}\quad(B_i,B_f,T_i,T_f>0).
$$
The <partition function> may be used to label these equilibrium states even though the isolated cooling leg has no external <heat bath>. Actual thermalization of the lattice and load additionally requires a sufficiently fast <spin-lattice relaxation>; perfectly uncoupled spins by themselves cannot refrigerate an attached object.

The limiting cases are instructive. For $x\ll1$, $s(x)=\ln2-x^2/2+O(x^4)$, $m\simeq N\mu^2B/(k_BT)$, and $C_{B,{\rm mag}}\simeq Nk_Bx^2$. The moment response reproduces <Curie's law>. For $x\gg1$, $s(x)\simeq(1+2x)e^{-2x}$ and the upper-state occupation is exponentially small. Taking $B\to0$ at fixed $T>0$ gives $s\to\ln2$; taking $T\to0$ at fixed $B>0$ gives $s\to0$. The origin has a path-dependent limit. Extrapolating a line of constant $x$ to $B=T=0$ does not construct a usable zero-<temperature> state or prove that a real material retains independent degenerate spins there.

= Constant-entropy magnetic cooling example
{parent=Two-level paramagnetic cooling model}
{title2=$T_f=T_iB_f/B_i$}

Choose a fictional two-level refrigerant with $\mu/k_B=1\,\mathrm{K\,T^{-1}}$, where the upright $\mathrm T$ denotes <tesla (unit)>. This chosen moment is a convenient model parameter, not a fitted property of the historical salt. At bath <temperature> $T_b=1\,\mathrm K$, raise $B$ from $0.20\,\mathrm T$ to $1.00\,\mathrm T$. Then $x$ increases from $0.20$ to $1.00$, so
$$
s(0.20)=0.673540\ldots,\qquad s(1)=0.365334\ldots,
\qquad \frac{Q_{\rm in}}{Nk_BT_b}=s(1)-s(0.20)=-0.308206\ldots.
$$
The last equality assumes a reversible <isothermal process> of <magnetization>; negative $Q_{\rm in}$ means the refrigerant rejects <heat>. The bath gains $0.308206\ldots Nk_B$ of <entropy>, balancing the stage's loss in this reversible limit. An appreciable <temperature> difference during real <heat transfer> adds <entropy production>.

Now open the <heat switch> and reduce $B$ from $1.00\,\mathrm T$ to $0.10\,\mathrm T$. Constant $x=1$ gives $T_f=0.10\,\mathrm K$. Throughout this ideal cooling leg, $p_-=0.880797\ldots$, $p_+=0.119203\ldots$, and $S_{\rm mag}=0.365334\ldots Nk_B$. Neither state becomes more probable. Field reduction shrinks the <energy> gap by ten while preserving the <Boltzmann distribution>'s dimensionless ratio of gap to <temperature>.

There is a subtle <energy> check. For the spin <Hamiltonian> convention used here, external-field interaction <energy> belongs to $U_{\rm mag}$, so reversible <work> done on the spin subsystem is $\delta W_{\rm on}=-m\,dB$ and
$$
dU_{\rm mag}=T\,dS_{\rm mag}-m\,dB.
$$
On the isolated leg, $Q=0$ but $U_{\rm mag}$ increases from $-0.761594\ldots Nk_B\,\mathrm K$ to $-0.0761594\ldots Nk_B\,\mathrm K$, becoming less negative as the gap shrinks. Integrating $-m\,dB$ at constant $x=1$ supplies exactly $+0.685435\ldots Nk_B\,\mathrm K$. Cooling therefore need not decrease this chosen spin <internal energy>. An account of <work> exchanged with a magnet circuit must specify where field interaction <energy> is assigned before comparing signs. The occupation ratio and <temperature> prediction do not depend on that bookkeeping choice.

For the isothermal preparation, direct integration gives $W_{\rm on}/(Nk_BT_b)=-\ln[\cosh(1)/\cosh(0.20)]=-0.413913\ldots$. The endpoint <internal energy> difference is $-0.722119\ldots Nk_BT_b$, equal to $Q_{\rm in}+W_{\rm on}$. This independently checks both the rejected <heat> and the magnetic-work sign in the chosen convention.

= Giauque-MacDougall magnetic cooling experiment
{parent=Adiabatic demagnetization}
{c}
{title2=1933}

<William Francis Giauque>'s 1949 lecture, Some Consequences of Low Temperature Research in Chemical Thermodynamics, credits <Peter Debye> with an independent proposal. It describes <gadolinium sulfate octahydrate> in a jacket immersed in liquid <helium>, with an external <solenoid>. Exchange gas conducts away <heat> during <magnetization>; evacuation isolates the sample before field reduction. A liquid-<nitrogen> shield reduces radiative <heat transfer>. Its photograph identifies the 1933 apparatus operated with <Duncan Peck MacDougall>. An <inductance> bridge reads the sample's <magnetic susceptibility>; measuring coils remain outside the evacuated jacket in liquid <helium>. Increasing <magnetic susceptibility> indicates cooling, but converting it to <temperature> using <Curie's law> becomes approximate as that law fails.<Some Consequences of Low Temperature Research in Chemical Thermodynamics by William Francis Giauque>[Giauque, Nobel lecture, pp. 237–245, Figs. 16–20 and 23–25]{ref}

The measurement logic deserves a separate check. A changed <inductance> alone is not an absolute <temperature> calibration: the sample geometry, applied measuring <magnetic field>, and constitutive response enter the inference. A modern control would compare the susceptibility-based <thermometer> with an independently calibrated <thermometer> over their common range, check recovery after repeated sweeps, and measure subsequent warming to bound incoming <heat>. These are proposed controls derived from the readout principle, rather than additional procedures attributed to the 1933 experiment. The historical working salt has a richer magnetic level structure than the illustrative <two-level paramagnetic cooling model>.

= Limits of adiabatic demagnetization
{parent=Adiabatic demagnetization}

Three distinct effects spoil indefinite $T\propto B$ cooling: extra <entropy> outside the spin subsystem, additional magnetic <energy> scales, and irreversible <heat> or <work> input. They can be distinguished with a thermodynamic <derivative>. For a smooth equilibrium <Helmholtz free energy> obeying $dF=-S\,dT-m\,dB$, a <Maxwell relation> gives
$$
dS=\frac{C_B}{T}\,dT+\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\right)_B dB,\qquad
\left(\frac{dT}{dB}\right)_S=-\frac{T}{C_B}\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\right)_B.
$$
Here $C_B$ is the total <heat capacity> participating in the stage's <thermal equilibrium>. The ideal model has $(\partial m/\partial T)_B=-C_{B,{\rm mag}}/B$. If its spins share <temperature> with a field-independent lattice and load of combined <heat capacity> $C_{\rm other}>0$, then
$$
\left(\frac{dT}{dB}\right)_{S_{\rm total}}
=\frac{T}{B}\frac{C_{B,{\rm mag}}}{C_{B,{\rm mag}}+C_{\rm other}}.
$$
The positive slope still means cooling when $dB<0$, but its smaller magnitude gives less cooling per fractional field reduction. For example, a low-<temperature> <Debye model> gives a lattice <entropy> proportional to $T^3$. As that lattice <entropy> falls, the spins take up the released <entropy>; $x$ can no longer remain constant. A weak enough coupling to neglect its equilibrium interaction <energy> can still be strong enough to exchange <heat> on the sweep timescale.

Interactions between <magnetic moments>, including <exchange interaction> and the fields of neighboring <magnetic dipole moments>, introduce <energy> scales that do not vanish with the applied $B$. Once they compete with $k_BT$ or $\mu B$, the independent-site <partition function> fails. Correlations and possible magnetic ordering alter the available <magnetic entropy> and the cooling curve. Simply setting the external <magnetic field> to zero does not eliminate those interactions. Different refrigerants therefore need their own measured equilibrium properties; there is no universal final <temperature> from the applied-field ratio alone.

Finally, a real open <heat switch> has residual <thermal conductance>; supports, wires and radiation carry incoming <heat>. Field changes may also dissipate <energy>. Locally near <thermal equilibrium>, with incoming <heat> rate $\dot Q_{\rm leak}$ and internal <entropy production> rate $\dot S_{\rm prod}$, the stage balance becomes
$$
\frac{dS}{dt}=\frac{\dot Q_{\rm leak}}T+\dot S_{\rm prod},\qquad
C_B\dot T=-T\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\right)_B\dot B
+\dot Q_{\rm leak}+T\dot S_{\rm prod}.
$$
This uses a common stage <temperature>; a non-equilibrated load requires separate balances. The sweep must allow <spin-lattice relaxation> while remaining fast enough to limit accumulated parasitic <heat>. At fixed $B$, the same balance explains warming and finite hold time. Useful refrigeration is the controlled absorption of a load's <heat> during that finite interval, followed by a renewed <isothermal process> of <magnetization> to export its <entropy>.

= Thermometer
{parent=Temperature}
{wiki}

A thermometer measures <temperature> through a physical response whose relation to <temperature> is known. Examples include the length change from <thermal expansion>, a change in <electrical resistance>, and the <pressure> of a <gas thermometer>. If the response <function> $X=f(T)$ is an <injective function> over the working range, its measured value determines $T=f^{-1}(X)$ through the <inverse function>. <Thermometer calibration> establishes that relation for the particular instrument and measurement conditions. Two matching readings are meaningful only after calibration to a common scale and with the probes sampling comparable equilibrium states. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/1-2-thermometers-and-temperature-scales[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, section 1.2: physical responses used in thermometers]{ref}

For a contact thermometer, the sensing part must reach <thermal equilibrium> with the region being measured. That region may differ from the rest of the object: an immersed probe near a heater can read a local <temperature> while the surrounding <water> remains cooler. A stable reading can also be a <steady state> set by competing <heat transfer> paths to the sample and the room. Good contact, adequate immersion, mixing where appropriate, and checks for spatial differences help establish which <temperature> the reading represents. These are measurement controls inferred from the <thermal equilibrium> requirement, not a guarantee that every flat trace is equilibrium.

A thermometer also changes the sample it touches. Consider an original toy calculation with an initially uniform sample at $T_o=350\,\mathrm K$, a probe at $T_p=300\,\mathrm K$, and constant whole-body <heat capacities> $C_o=100\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $C_p=1\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Suppose together they form an <isolated system>, with no <work> or <phase transition> and negligible contact <energy>. The <first law of thermodynamics> and <thermal equilibrium> give
$$
T_f=\frac{C_oT_o+C_pT_p}{C_o+C_p}=349.50495\ldots\,\mathrm K,\qquad
T_f-T_o=\frac{C_p}{C_o+C_p}(T_p-T_o).
$$
The final <thermometer> reading underestimates the sample's original <temperature> by about $0.495\,\mathrm K$, although it correctly measures their new common <temperature>. The disturbance becomes small when the participating probe <heat capacity> is small compared with the sample's. A large controlled <heat bath> is a different limit: it supplies or removes <heat> while approximately maintaining its own <temperature>.

Waiting time is a separate issue from disturbance. Under a toy <Newton's law of cooling> model for a uniform probe in a maintained <heat bath>, a constant contact conductance $G$ gives
$$
C_p\frac{dT_p}{dt}=G(T_b-T_p),\qquad
T_p(t)=T_b+[T_p(0)-T_b]e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau=\frac{C_p}{G}.
$$
The <time constant> $\tau$ controls this <exponential decay>. For stipulated values $T_b=350\,\mathrm K$, $T_p(0)=300\,\mathrm K$ and $\tau=5\,\mathrm s$, the probe reads about $331.61\,\mathrm K$ after one <time constant> and $347.51\,\mathrm K$ after three. These are calculated values, not an instrument specification or measured data. Real probes may have multiple relaxation times and additional <heat transfer> paths; choose an equilibration criterion from the required accuracy, rather than assuming that a fixed waiting time works universally.

= Gas thermometer
{parent=Thermometer}
{title2=$P\propto T\quad(N,V\text{ fixed})$}

A gas thermometer infers <temperature> from the <equation of state> of a confined <gas>. The constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> version measures <pressure>; the constant-<pressure> version measures <volume (thermodynamics)>. For an <ideal gas>,
$$
PV=Nk_BT=nRT,\qquad
\frac{T}{T_0}=\frac{P}{P_0}\quad(N,V\text{ fixed}),\qquad
\frac{T}{T_0}=\frac{V}{V_0}\quad(N,P\text{ fixed}).
$$
Here $N$ is particle number, $n$ is <amount of substance>, $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, and $R$ is the <molar gas constant>. The reference and test states must be internally equilibrated, and $T_0$ must be known on the absolute <thermodynamic temperature> scale. A pressure ratio cancels the unknown gas inventory and bulb size in the ideal constant-volume limit. This cancellation is the method's appeal: a mechanical measurement transfers an absolute <temperature> scale between different materials. The <Maxwell speed distribution> and <ideal gas pressure from wall collisions> explain the microscopic origin of the response. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/2.1 Molecular Model of an Ideal Gas>[OpenStax, University Physics Volume 2, section 2.1: the ideal gas law]{ref}

For an original ideal-model calculation, choose $T_0=300\,\mathrm K$, $P_0=120\,\mathrm{kPa}$ and $P=160\,\mathrm{kPa}$. Then $T=400\,\mathrm K$. These are stipulated values, not observations. Both <pressures> must be absolute, referred to vacuum. If the surrounding <atmospheric pressure> were $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$, the corresponding <gauge pressures> would be $20$ and $60\,\mathrm{kPa}$; their ratio is three, which would incorrectly predict $900\,\mathrm K$. A pressure difference from the atmosphere cannot be substituted into the <ideal gas law>.

To turn this principle into a readable measurement protocol, use the following controls. These steps are a proposed teaching interpretation of the cited apparatus, rather than additional reported NBS observations:
* **Define the inventory.** Evacuate and clean the bulb and connecting tube, fill with a known pure <gas>, close the filling valve, and keep the same total particle number during each reference/test comparison. Monitor <pressure> at a maintained <temperature> before and after a run. A leak changes $N$; a slow drift can also reflect contamination, sorption, or imperfect equilibration, so pressure stability alone does not identify its cause.
* **Measure the reference.** Place the bulb in a controlled environment with independently assigned $T_0$. Record $P_0$ after thermalization, together with the independent <thermometer> readings and their spread. A reference near $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ has an absolute <temperature> near $273.15\,\mathrm K$, rather than zero <temperature>. Repeating reference readings tests whether the inventory or apparatus changed during the comparison.
* **Measure the test state.** Change the bath setting, allow the bulb and <gas> to settle, and record $P$ only when the surrounding <thermometers> and <pressure> are sufficiently stable for the intended accuracy. Compare readings at different positions and vary the settling time. Record the connecting tube's <temperature> profile too: uniform bath <temperature> does not make the entire connecting tube isothermal.
* **Measure the volume changes.** Determine the bulb's <thermal expansion> and its deformation from differences between inside and outside <pressure>. Measure the connecting tube's dimensions and the effective valve volume. “Constant volume” is the ideal model; a real vessel expands. These measurements supply corrections, even though the absolute bulb volume cancels in the simplest pressure ratio.
* **Measure pressure against a reference.** For a liquid-column pressure standard, measure the vertical column height, the liquid's <temperature>-dependent <mass density>, local gravitational acceleration, and the reference-side <pressure>. Include diaphragm offsets and the gas's <hydrostatic pressure> change between the sensor and bulb. The pressure needed in the <equation of state> is at the bulb, not automatically at the remote gauge.

The corresponding NBS apparatus used a protective case with an outer <helium> layer held close to the bulb <pressure>, reducing pressure deformation while aiding thermal contact. <Thermal expansion> was measured on bulb material. Gas in the capillary remained partly outside the hot region, requiring a measured tube <temperature> profile and geometry. Pure working <gas> also mattered: contaminants that sorb on surfaces can change the freely circulating inventory. These are physical effects to measure or bound, rather than scatter that disappears by averaging. https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir85-3133.pdf[J. F. Schooley, Thermometry, NBS IR 85-3133, chapter V, gas thermometry and the NBS experiment]{ref}

For illustration, neglect connecting-tube gas and describe a uniform bulb with a dilute <virial expansion> in particle <number density> $\rho_N=N/V$:
$$
PV=Nk_BT\left[1+B_2(T)\rho_N+O(\rho_N^2)\right],\qquad
\frac{T}{T_0}=\frac{P}{P_0}\frac{V(T)}{V(T_0)}\frac{Z(T_0)}{Z(T)}.
$$
Here $Z=PV/(Nk_BT)$ and $B_2$ is the <second virial coefficient> in the particle-density convention; a molar-density convention rescales the coefficient. The second relation follows exactly from the definition of $Z$ for the same $N$, and shows the signs of the corrections. If the bulb expands by $0.1\%$ and $Z=1$ at both states, the toy pressure ratio above gives $400.4\,\mathrm K$, rather than $400\,\mathrm K$. $$
\frac{P}{P_0}=\frac{T}{T_0}\left[1+\bigl(B_2(T)-B_2(T_0)\bigr)\rho_N+O(\rho_N^2)\right],\qquad
\lim_{\rho_N\to0}\frac{P}{P_0}=\frac{T}{T_0}.
$$

= Gas thermometer dead-space correction
{parent=Gas thermometer}

Gas outside the uniform-temperature bulb of a <gas thermometer> contributes to its conserved particle inventory. An idealized calculation divides the connecting tube into small regions with <volumes> $V_j$ and local <temperatures> $T_j$. Neglect <hydrostatic pressure> offsets and <thermal transpiration>, assume a common <pressure> $P$ and a local <ideal gas law> in each region, and let the bulb have <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_b$ and <temperature> $T_b$. Adding the particle inventories gives
$$
N=\frac{P}{k_B}\left(\frac{V_b}{T_b}+\sum_j\frac{V_j}{T_j}\right).
$$
Thus even an <ideal gas> in a perfectly rigid sealed apparatus need not obey $P\propto T_b$: some of its particles remain in regions with other <temperatures>. For a stipulated bulb of $100\,\mathrm{cm^3}$, a connecting volume of $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ maintained at $300\,\mathrm K$, and bulb reference and test <temperatures> of $300$ and $400\,\mathrm K$, conservation of $N$ gives
$$
\frac{P}{P_0}=\frac{100/300+1/300}{100/400+1/300}
=\frac{101}{76}\approx1.328947,\qquad
T_{\rm app}=300\,\mathrm K\frac{P}{P_0}\approx398.684\,\mathrm K.
$$
These are toy dimensions and a calculated bias, not measured data. Reducing $N$ reduces both <pressures> proportionally and leaves this ratio unchanged: extrapolating away gas nonideality does not eliminate the connecting-volume error. Actual apparatus also requires the tube's <thermal expansion>, a measured <temperature> profile, and the pressure corrections omitted in this model.

= Helium liquefaction

<Helium liquefaction> converts <helium> <gas> into a collectable <liquid>. It creates a bath for investigating material properties at very low <temperatures>, including <superconductivity>. The central question is how the incoming <specific enthalpy> compares with the <specific enthalpies> of the outgoing <liquid> and <vapor>. The <liquefaction enthalpy budget> makes that comparison explicit; a <temperature> decrease by itself does not specify a liquid yield.

In a <Joule-Thomson effect> cooling stage, a throttling <pressure> drop approximately preserves <enthalpy> under the assumptions of the <steady-flow energy balance>. For an <ideal gas>, <specific enthalpy> depends only on <temperature>: with positive <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>, equal <specific enthalpy> means equal <temperature>. Thus its <Joule-Thomson coefficient> is zero. An <ideal gas> also supplies no <liquid> branch. Cooling by throttling depends on <real gas> properties; expansion is not an automatic source of refrigeration.

For a local drop $dp<0$, $dT=\mu_{\rm JT}dp$ cools only where the <Joule-Thomson coefficient> is positive. Precooling must bring the <real gas> into a useful cooling region, with enough cooling to overcome unwanted <heat transfer>. A finite expansion can cross a <Joule-Thomson inversion curve>; its final state requires the full <enthalpy> balance, including <phase coexistence> if <liquid> forms. <Kamerlingh Onnes's 1908 helium liquefaction experiment> combined external precooling with regeneration to reach that regime. A constant-<volume (thermodynamics)> <helium> <gas thermometer> below the valve supplied the <temperature> indication. An <oxygen> <boiling> reference check disagreed with its expected reading; the <real gas> correction to <thermodynamic temperature> remained provisional. The measurement principles separate two questions. A visible <liquid meniscus> establishes an interface; a <gas thermometer> estimates <temperature> through an <equation of state>. A stationary <temperature> trace can also result from a <steady state> without <phase coexistence>, so it cannot replace the interface observation. Conversely, seeing a <liquid> does not calibrate a <thermometer>. <Thermometer calibration>, the <gas thermometer dead-space correction>, departures from the <ideal gas> law and incomplete <thermal equilibrium> all matter when assigning an absolute <temperature>. A check far from the intended measurement range tests the instrument but does not by itself bound every low-<temperature> error.

= Liquefaction enthalpy budget
{parent=Helium liquefaction}
{title2=$y=(h_g-h_{\rm in}-q)/(h_g-h_l)$}

Consider an original model of a cold section enclosing a throttle and collecting region, with the precooling <heat exchanger> outside its boundary. The inlet has <mass flow rate> $\dot m>0$ and <specific enthalpy> $h_{\rm in}$. The outlets are equilibrium <liquid> and <vapor> at one fixed <pressure>, with <specific enthalpies> $h_l$ and $h_g$. Let $y$ be the outlet <liquid> <mass fraction>, and let $q=\dot Q/\dot m\geq0$ be incoming unwanted <heat> per unit incoming <mass>. Assume <steady flow>, no shaft <work>, and negligible bulk <kinetic energy> and <gravitational potential energy> changes. The <steady-flow energy balance> gives
$$
h_{\rm in}+q=yh_l+(1-y)h_g,\qquad
L=h_g-h_l>0,\qquad
y=\frac{h_g-h_{\rm in}-q}{L}.
$$
Here $L$ is the <latent heat> per unit <mass> at the chosen outlet <pressure>. The <mass flow rates> of the outlets are $y\dot m$ and $(1-y)\dot m$. The formula describes a flowing cold section; a filling vessel with appreciable <energy> or <mass> accumulation requires storage terms.

For a deliberately dimensionless example, choose
$$
\eta=\frac{h_{\rm in}-h_l}{L}=0.70,\qquad
\ell=\frac qL=0.05,\qquad y=1-\eta-\ell=0.25.
$$
Per unit incoming <mass>, one quarter leaves as <liquid> and three quarters as <vapor>. With zero incoming <heat>, the same inlet would give $y=0.30$. Increasing $q/L$ to $0.20$ reduces $y$ to $0.10$, and $q/L=0.30$ reaches the no-<liquid> boundary. These are chosen model values, not <helium> property data or a historical yield. In normalized <enthalpy> units with $h_l=0$ and $h_g=1$, conservation checks directly: $0.70+0.05=0.25\times0+0.75\times1$.

The admissible interval is $h_l\leq h_{\rm in}+q\leq h_g$, or $0\leq y\leq1$. Outside it, the assumed equilibrium two-phase outlet fails: an <enthalpy> above $h_g$ requires a hotter single-<vapor> outlet under these assumptions, rather than a negative amount of <liquid>. Below $h_l$, a colder <liquid> or a different <thermodynamic phase> must be considered. The balance does not predict its own outlet <pressure>, <temperature> or <helium> property functions.

At fixed outlet <pressure>, hence fixed $L$, the <derivatives> are $\partial y/\partial h_{\rm in}=-1/L$ and $\partial y/\partial q=-1/L$. Lowering inlet <specific enthalpy> by regeneration and reducing incoming <heat> therefore buy the same increase in <liquid> <mass fraction> per unit <energy> per unit <mass> in this model. Adding any constant to all three <specific enthalpies> leaves $y$ unchanged. This independence of the arbitrary <enthalpy> reference makes the balance useful even when property tables choose different zero points.

= Liquefaction yield with separate warm-gas and liquid outlets
{parent=Liquefaction enthalpy budget}
{title2=$y=(h_g-h_{\rm in}-q)/(h_g-h_l)$}

Apply the <steady-flow energy balance> to an entire <liquefaction> apparatus, allowing a warm <gas> outlet and a separate cold <liquid> outlet. With incoming <heat> $q$ per unit feed <mass>, no shaft <work>, and negligible changes in bulk <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy>, <conservation of mass> and <conservation of energy> give $h_{\rm in}+q=yh_l+(1-y)h_g$. Solving this equation gives the displayed liquid <mass fraction>.

The two outlet streams need not share a <temperature>. For example, a <counterflow heat exchanger> can warm returning <gas> before it crosses the overall <control volume> boundary, while collected <liquid> leaves at the cold end. When the outlet temperatures differ, $h_g-h_l$ includes sensible heating as well as phase change; it is not simply a <latent heat>. A colder two-phase separator nested inside the apparatus has its own boundary states and a different local enthalpy balance.

= Thermal boundary resistance
{parent=Cryogenics}
{wiki=Interfacial_thermal_resistance}
{title2=$r_B=\Delta T/q$}

<Thermal boundary resistance> describes a <temperature> drop between the two sides of a material interface carrying <heat flux>. Efficient <thermal conduction> within each material does not guarantee efficient <heat transfer> between them. The solid–<helium> case is <Kapitza resistance>; the broader concept also includes solid–solid interfaces. <Thermal boundary resistance by E. T. Swartz and Robert Otto Pohl>[Swartz and Pohl (1989), abstract: helium–solid and solid–solid interfaces]{ref}

Let $T_s$ and $T_l$ be the <temperatures> extrapolated to the interface from its solid and liquid sides, each treated as internally in <thermal equilibrium>. For uniform normal <heat flux> $q>0$ through <area> $A$, define the finite-drop, area-specific <thermal resistance> and the resistance of the whole interface by
$$
\Delta T=T_s-T_l,\qquad \dot Q=Aq,\qquad
r_B=\frac{\Delta T}{q}\quad[\mathrm{K\,m^2\,W^{-1}}],\qquad
\mathcal R_B=\frac{\Delta T}{\dot Q}=\frac{r_B}{A}\quad[\mathrm{K\,W^{-1}}].
$$
The macroscopic jump represents an unresolved interfacial region; it is not a claim that one point has two equilibrium <temperatures>. At nonzero <heat flux>, the two sides are outside mutual <thermal equilibrium>. A measured difference between distant <thermometers> also contains bulk <temperature gradients>, which must be measured or bounded before assigning all of it to the interface.

An <energy> balance explains why a <temperature> jump can coexist with continuous <heat flux>. For a stationary interfacial region with no local <work> or <heat> source,
$$
\frac{dU_{\rm int}}{dt}=Aq_{\rm in}-Aq_{\rm out}.
$$
The <first law of thermodynamics> therefore requires $q_{\rm in}=q_{\rm out}=q$ in a <steady state>. It does not require $T_s=T_l$. For a heated sample with electrical <power> $P=VI$, an independently determined outward parasitic loss $P_{\rm leak}$ and no other inputs,
$$
\frac{dU_s}{dt}=P-P_{\rm leak}-Aq,\qquad
q=\frac{P-P_{\rm leak}}{A}\quad\text{in a steady state}.
$$
Here $V$ is the heater's <electric potential difference> and $I$ its <electric current>. Subtracting a lead loss means subtracting <power> before dividing by <area>; $q$ is not the total heating <power>. Unknown losses and stored <internal energy> prevent that simple steady inference.

For a one-dimensional comparison, put an interface between two slabs with the same <area> $A$, positive constant <thermal conductivities> $k_s,k_l$ and <lengths> $L_s,L_l$. Neglect side losses. Integrating <Fourier's law> across each slab gives
$$
T_H-T_s=q\frac{L_s}{k_s},\qquad
T_l-T_C=q\frac{L_l}{k_l},\qquad
T_H-T_C=q\left(\frac{L_s}{k_s}+\frac{L_l}{k_l}\right)+(T_s-T_l).
$$
Thus, for a linear interface with constant $r_B$, the series result is
$$
q=\frac{T_H-T_C}{L_s/k_s+r_B+L_l/k_l},\qquad
\mathcal R_{\rm total}=\frac1A\left(\frac{L_s}{k_s}+r_B+\frac{L_l}{k_l}\right).
$$
These slab formulas are a chosen <thermal conduction> model, not a bulk transport law for moving liquid <helium>. If the interface law is nonlinear, substitute its own relation between $q$ and $T_s-T_l$ into the preceding balance; a single constant resistance cannot describe every load. Increasing bulk <thermal conductivity> has diminishing benefit when the interfacial drop dominates. Increasing usable contact <area> reduces the whole-interface <thermal resistance> if the local interface law and the other conditions remain fixed.

= Kapitza resistance
{parent=Thermal boundary resistance}
{c}
{title2=$R_K$}

<Kapitza resistance> is the <thermal boundary resistance> encountered between a solid and <helium> in <cryogenics>. A cold <helium> <heat bath> can coexist with a warmer immersed component under continuous heating. The bath's <temperature> alone therefore does not establish the component's <temperature>. <Kapitza thermal boundary experiment> supplied an early direct demonstration of the obstacle at the solid–liquid boundary.

A microscopic route to interfacial <heat transfer> is transmission of <phonons>. Transmission depends on the two materials and their interface; scattering and the condition of the surface matter. <Thermal boundary resistance by E. T. Swartz and Robert Otto Pohl>[Swartz and Pohl (1989), abstract: phonon transmission, scattering and surface sensitivity]{ref} The <fourth-power thermal boundary model> isolates one useful low-<temperature> limiting law without assigning an empirical coefficient to a particular sample.

= Kapitza thermal boundary experiment
{parent=Kapitza resistance}
{c}
{title2=1941}

<Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitza>'s 1941 study suspended cleaned, annealed <copper> blocks in liquid <helium>. Sealed, gas-filled cavities held a heater and a <bronze> <resistance thermometer>. Electrical readout tracked the heating-induced <temperature> rise; the earlier readout method assumed a stable bath without a separate bath <thermometer>. A nearby unheated block warmed much less, localizing the drop near the heated surface. Gold plating and coatings tested surface effects; tight cavities prevented liquid ingress. These were solid-body readings, not probes resolving the microscopic interfacial layer. <The Study of Heat Transfer in Helium II by Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitza>[The Study of Heat Transfer in Helium II, chapter 38, §2 pp. 584–585; §6 pp. 616–621, Figs. 25–29]{ref}

The measurement logic suggests a modern control sequence: calibrate both <temperature> readouts, establish their zero-load difference, increase heater <power>, wait for a <steady state>, and repeat while monitoring the bath. Determine lead losses and the bulk <temperature gradients> before using $r_B=(T_s-T_l)/q$. These are proposed controls derived from the <energy> balance, rather than additional reported steps in the 1941 apparatus. A nearby unheated solid also has its own interface: its reading need not equal the liquid's local <temperature> exactly. The experiment supports a drop concentrated near the surface, while a microscopic thickness and a universal interfacial law require further evidence.

= Fourth-power thermal boundary model
{parent=Kapitza resistance}
{title2=$q=\Sigma(T_h^4-T_c^4)$}

Choose a positive constant $\Sigma$ and the illustrative interface law
$$
q=\Sigma(T_h^4-T_c^4),\qquad
[\Sigma]=\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}},\qquad T_h\geq T_c>0.
$$
Here $T_h$ and $T_c$ are the hot and cold interface-side <temperatures>, not distant <thermometer> readings. This is a low-<temperature> <phonon> model under restrictive assumptions, not a universal fit to Kapitza's measured exponent or a material-property table. Its coefficient $\Sigma$ is not the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>: the carriers in this model are <phonons>, rather than <photons> crossing a vacuum.

The fourth power has a simple counting explanation. In the three-dimensional low-<temperature> <Debye model>, a linear <phonon dispersion relation> gives a <density of states> proportional to $\omega^2$. A <phonon> has <energy> $\hbar\omega$ and <Bose-Einstein distribution> occupation $[\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1]^{-1}$. If propagation speeds and the angle-averaged transmission introduce no additional frequency scale over the occupied modes, the outgoing <energy> flux has the form
$$
J(T)\propto\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega^3\,d\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}
=\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}\right)^4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}\propto T^4.
$$
Extending the upper integration limit assumes occupied modes lie well below the cutoff. Balancing the two outgoing fluxes, with coefficients satisfying zero net <heat flux> at equal <temperatures>, gives the chosen difference of fourth powers. Frequency-dependent transmission, different excitations or additional transport channels can change this conclusion.

The finite-drop area-specific <thermal resistance> follows by factoring a difference of powers:
$$
r_{K,\rm sec}=\frac{T_h-T_c}{q}
=\frac{1}{\Sigma(T_h+T_c)(T_h^2+T_c^2)}.
$$
Near <thermal equilibrium>, write $T_h=T+\delta T$ and $T_c=T$. Expansion gives $q=4\Sigma T^3\delta T+O((\delta T)^2)$, so the differential area-specific <Kapitza resistance> is
$$
R_K(T)=\lim_{\delta T\to0}\frac{\delta T}{q(T+\delta T,T)}
=\frac{1}{4\Sigma T^3}\quad[\mathrm{K\,m^2\,W^{-1}}],\qquad
\mathcal R_K(T)=\frac{R_K(T)}{A}\quad[\mathrm{K\,W^{-1}}].
$$
At a finite bias, varying only the hot side gives $(\partial q/\partial T_h)^{-1}=1/(4\Sigma T_h^3)$; this is generally different from $r_{K,\rm sec}$. Stating what is held fixed makes a differential resistance reproducible.

For an original normalized example, choose reference scales $T_0>0$ and $A_0>0$, and set
$$
\theta_h=\frac{T_h}{T_0}=1.2,\qquad \theta_c=\frac{T_c}{T_0}=1,\qquad
j=\frac{q}{\Sigma T_0^4}=1.2^4-1=1.0736.
$$
For contact <area> $A=2A_0$, the total <heat transfer> rate is $\dot Q=2.1472A_0\Sigma T_0^4$. The finite-drop resistance satisfies $\Sigma T_0^3r_{K,\rm sec}=0.2/1.0736=125/671\approx0.18629$. Using the equilibrium tangent at $T_0$ instead would predict $j=4(0.2)=0.8$, illustrating why a substantial <temperature> rise requires the nonlinear law. Doubling the common equilibrium <temperature> reduces $R_K$ by eight in this chosen model.

A series check can be done without guessing any material properties. Stipulate a hot-side slab with dimensionless areal bulk resistance $b_s=\Sigma T_0^3L_s/k_s=0.10$, and neglect the cold-side bulk drop. At the same $j=1.0736$, the hot reservoir must have $T_H/T_0=1.2+b_sj=1.30736$. The imposed reservoir drop splits into $0.10736$ in the bulk and $0.20$ at the interface. Applying the fourth-power law directly to $T_H$ would assign the bulk drop to the interface and overestimate the <heat flux>.

Finally, transfer <heat> at rate $Aq$ between maintained interface-side <temperatures> $T_h>T_c>0$. The hot side loses <entropy> at rate $Aq/T_h$, and the cold side gains it at rate $Aq/T_c$. With no interface <entropy> accumulation, the <entropy production> per unit <area> is
$$
\frac{\dot S_{\rm prod}}{A}=q\left(\frac1{T_c}-\frac1{T_h}\right)
=\Sigma\frac{(T_h-T_c)^2(T_h+T_c)(T_h^2+T_c^2)}{T_hT_c}>0.
$$
For the normalized example it is $\dot S_{\rm prod}/(A\Sigma T_0^3)=1.0736(1-1/1.2)=0.178933\ldots$. The same <energy> crosses both sides in a <steady state>, while total <entropy> increases, consistently with the <second law of thermodynamics>. A finite <temperature> jump during <heat transfer> therefore expresses dissipation, not a failure of <energy> conservation.
