= Solution

At fixed <temperature> and <solvent> <permittivity>, the <Debye–Hückel screening length> satisfies $\lambda_D^{-2}\propto\sum_i c_i z_i^2=2I$, where $I=\tfrac12\sum_i c_i z_i^2$ is <ionic strength>. Fully dissociated <sodium chloride> has two monovalent <ion> species, giving $I_1=0.01$ M. Fully dissociated <magnesium sulfate> has two divalent species, giving
$$
I_2=\tfrac12[0.1(2)^2+0.1(-2)^2]=0.40\,\mathrm M.
$$
The <multivalent-electrolyte screening-length ratio> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{D,2}=3\,\mathrm{nm}\sqrt{\frac{0.01}{0.40}}=0.474\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq0.47\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The squared valences are essential: counting only the total <ion> <concentration> misses a factor of four in the screening strength per formula unit. This is the comparable linear-screening estimate, assuming unchanged dielectric response and full dissociation. <ion pairs> and concentrated-electrolyte nonideality can make a real divalent-salt solution depart from it; no such correction is specified.

For a long <ideal chain> of $N$ statistical segments of <length> $b$, $R_G^2=Nb^2/6$. At fixed repeat chemistry and statistical segment <length>, <molar mass> is proportional to $N$. The <molecular-weight scaling of an ideal-chain gyration radius> thus gives
$$
\boxed{R_G(10^6)=120\,\mathrm{nm}\sqrt{\frac{10^6}{10^5}}=379.5\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq3.8\times10^2\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The supplied size and ideal statistics are used as stipulated; an independently tabulated coil size would not replace the given baseline.

<solvent quality> changes the balance of segment–segment and segment–<solvent> contacts. In a <good solvent>, excluded-volume repulsion swells a sufficiently long isolated chain, with $R_G\propto N^\nu$, $\nu\simeq0.588$ in three dimensions; the <Flory approximation> gives $\nu=3/5$. At theta conditions the effective two-segment <excluded volume> vanishes and the ideal exponent $1/2$ is recovered for long chains. A <poor solvent> favors a compact <polymer globule>, whose large-chain size scales approximately as $N^{1/3}$ at fixed internal <density>. <solvent> and <temperature> can also change local <persistence length> and hence the prefactor. These different conformations need not have the same radius at the same <molar mass>.

The independent fluid-mechanical relation is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UL}{\eta}=\frac{UL}{\nu},}
$$
where $\rho$ is <fluid> <mass density>, $\eta$ <dynamic viscosity> and $\nu$ <kinematic viscosity>. For a generic rigid <sphere>, the convention must specify whether $L$ is its diameter or radius; their <Reynolds numbers> differ by a factor of two. The ratio compares advective <inertia> $\rho U^2/L$ with viscous <stress> gradients $\eta U/L^2$. Small <Reynolds number> supports a creeping-flow approximation when unsteady <fluid> <inertia> is also negligible. No organism-specific size, speed or propulsion calculation is made here.

The <Stokes–Einstein relation> connects thermal <diffusion> with dissipative <mobility>. For a dilute suspension of isolated rigid <spheres> of <hydrodynamic radius> $a$ in an unbounded <Newtonian fluid> of <viscosity> $\eta$,
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{k_BT}{\zeta}=\frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta a},}
$$
where $\zeta$ is translational <drag coefficient>, $D$ <diffusion coefficient> and $T$ absolute <temperature>. <Stokes drag> supplies $\zeta=6\pi\eta a$ under no-slip, continuum, low-Reynolds-number conditions. The <Einstein diffusion relation> $D=k_BT/\zeta$ is more general than that particular spherical <drag> law.

A short <equilibrium> argument explains the connection. Let a weak external potential $U$ exert <force> $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$, so drift <velocity> is $\mathbf F/\zeta$. The number <flux> is $\mathbf j=n\mathbf F/\zeta-D\nabla n$. A <Boltzmann distribution> $n\propto e^{-U/(k_BT)}$ has $\nabla n=n\mathbf F/(k_BT)$; requiring zero <equilibrium> <flux> fixes $D=k_BT/\zeta$. <thermal fluctuations> and viscous <dissipation> are therefore linked, rather than independent adjustable effects. The original theory also relates <diffusion> to measured random displacements.<On the Movement of Small Particles Suspended in a Stationary Liquid Demanded by the Molecular-Kinetic Theory of Heat by Albert Einstein>[Sections 3–5, printed pages 9–18 in the linked translation: equilibrium flux balance, spherical drag and displacement variance]{ref}

On times long compared with momentum relaxation, an isotropic diffusing <sphere> has
$$
\langle[\Delta x(t)]^2\rangle=2Dt,\qquad
\langle|\Delta\mathbf r(t)|^2\rangle=6Dt.
$$
<single-particle tracking> can measure the slope. Alternatively, dilute single-scattering <dynamic light scattering> measures a field correlation $g_1(q,t)=e^{-Dq^2t}$; the corresponding intensity-correlation excess is proportional to $e^{-2Dq^2t}$. The scattering <wavenumber> and the distinction between field and intensity decay must be retained.

A <Stokes-Einstein experimental consistency test> independently measures <temperature> and <solvent> <viscosity>, uses a narrow distribution of inert <spheres>, checks decay rate proportional to $q^2$ or displacement variance proportional to time, and extrapolates toward infinite dilution. Subtract drift, account for wall-enhanced <drag>, and verify that aggregation is absent. Tracking additionally needs spatial calibration and correction for finite exposure and <localization precision>. At finite <concentration>, collective <diffusion> is not automatically the single-particle <self-diffusion> in the equation. A <hydrodynamic radius> is not generally identical to a <polymer> <radius of gyration>; nonspherical particles, slip or viscoelastic <solvents> require a different <mobility> model.

<polymers at interfaces> balance favorable surface contacts against lost <conformational entropy> and interactions among neighboring chains. At an attractive solid–liquid interface, an <adsorbed polymer train> consists of consecutive bound segments. An <adsorbed polymer loop> leaves the surface and returns to it, while an <adsorbed polymer tail> extends to a free chain end. A chain can contain all three, so <adsorption> does not imply a completely flattened molecule. Surface affinity, <solvent quality>, <persistence length>, chain <length> and <concentration> control the balance and the resulting segment-density profile.

\Image[paper-1-polymer-interfaces.png]
{title=Original schematic of adsorbed trains, loops and tails, and the crossover from isolated grafted mushrooms to an overlapping polymer brush}

<adsorption> gains <energy> of order $\epsilon k_BT$ per bound segment but reduces the number of accessible chain configurations. Weak <adsorption> can be described through <adsorption blobs> whose thickness exceeds the statistical segment <length>; each blob loses an <energy> of order $k_BT$ through <confinement>. Stronger attraction generally creates more contacts and a more compact layer, while loops and tails can still dominate its outer extent. A measurement of adsorbed mass alone therefore does not determine layer thickness or conformation.

End-grafted chains are different: their attachment is fixed at one end. At <grafting density> $\sigma_g$, the mean lateral spacing is $D\sim\sigma_g^{-1/2}$. At low coverage the chains behave as isolated <polymer mushrooms>. The <polymer mushroom-to-brush crossover> occurs when $\sigma_gR_G^2\sim1$. At higher coverage in a <good solvent>, crowding makes chains extend away from the surface. A free-energy balance per chain,
$$
\frac{F}{k_BT}\sim\frac{H^2}{Nb^2}+\frac{vN^2\sigma_g}{H},
$$
combines stretching <entropy> with repulsive <excluded volume> $v>0$. Minimization gives $H^3\sim vN^3b^2\sigma_g$, or
$$
\boxed{H\sim Nb(\sigma_gb^2)^{1/3}\quad\text{when }v\sim b^3.}
$$
This is a scaling result with an undetermined order-one coefficient, valid for overlapping chains stretched below their contour <length>. In a compact poor-solvent layer, a roughly fixed internal <polymer> <volume fraction> instead gives $H\propto N\sigma_g$.

Small-angle neutron scattering on end-grafted polydimethylsiloxane layers on porous silica has distinguished the good- and poor-solvent <density> exponents. The chemical system and scattering contrast matter: changing <solvent quality> should be separated from changing graft coverage, chain <molar mass> or <adsorption> strength.<Characterization of the brush regime for grafted polymer layers at the solid-liquid interface by P. Auroy and others>[Physical Review Letters 66, 719–722 (1991), abstract: actual polymer/substrate system, neutron-scattering method and the two thickness scalings]{ref}

An interface can also localize chains of a <block copolymer> when its blocks prefer different media. Such layers change <interfacial tension> and can stabilize dispersed droplets or particles through <steric repulsion>. Overlapping layers in a <good solvent> cost osmotic and conformational <free energy>; incomplete <adsorption> can instead produce <polymer bridging> between particles. Nonadsorbing chains can cause depletion attraction. Thus the presence of a <polymer> does not by itself guarantee stabilization.

A viscoelastic material stores part of the <work> of deformation and dissipates part. In the linear regime its <stress> depends on the <strain> history, not just the instantaneous <strain> or <shear strain rate>. For simple shear, a causal <relaxation modulus> $G(t)$ gives $\sigma(t)=\int_{-\infty}^tG(t-t')\dot\gamma(t')dt'$. A sudden <strain> then probes <stress relaxation>, while a constant imposed <stress> probes <material creep>; these are different experiments.

For steady sinusoidal <strain> use the convention $\gamma(t)=\gamma_0\cos\omega t$ and $e^{i\omega t}$ complex amplitudes. The <complex viscoelastic modulus> is
$$
\boxed{G^*(\omega)=\frac{\widetilde\sigma}{\widetilde\gamma}
=G'(\omega)+iG''(\omega),}
$$
so $\sigma(t)=\gamma_0[G'\cos\omega t-G''\sin\omega t]$. The <storage modulus> $G'$ is the in-phase elastic component. The <loss modulus> $G''$ multiplies the component in phase with <shear strain rate>; <stress> leads <strain> by $\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(G'',G')$. The <loss tangent> is $\tan\delta=G''/G'$ when $G'>0$.

Multiplying <stress> by <shear strain rate> and averaging gives $\langle P\rangle/V=\tfrac12\omega G''\gamma_0^2$. Hence the <dissipated energy per oscillatory shear cycle> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{W_{\rm cycle}}{V}=\pi G''\gamma_0^2.}
$$
The elastic contribution returns its <work> over the cycle. A passive material has nonnegative <dissipation>, which fixes the sign of the loss term in the chosen Fourier convention. This <complex viscoelastic modulus> is a response function, not the mathematical absolute value of a complex number.

A <Maxwell model> places a <spring> of <shear modulus> $G$ in series with a <dashpot> of <viscosity> $\eta$, so the <strains> add at common <stress>:
$$
\dot\gamma=\frac{\dot\sigma}{G}+\frac{\sigma}{\eta},
\qquad\tau=\frac{\eta}{G}.
$$
The <Maxwell fluid dynamic modulus> is
$$
G^*=\frac{G\,i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},\qquad
\boxed{G'=\frac{G(\omega\tau)^2}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\quad
G''=\frac{G\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.}
$$
At $\omega\tau\ll1$ the response is predominantly viscous, $G''\simeq\eta\omega$ and $G'\simeq G(\omega\tau)^2$. At $\omega\tau\gg1$ it approaches the elastic plateau $G'=G$. The two moduli cross, and $G''$ reaches its maximum, at $\omega\tau=1$. A <Kelvin-Voigt model> instead has the <spring> and <dashpot> in parallel, giving $G^*=G+i\eta\omega$ and no fluid-like zero-frequency terminal flow. Real materials often need a spectrum of <relaxation times> rather than one Maxwell time.

\Image[paper-1-viscoelasticity.png]
{title=Original Maxwell storage and loss moduli, with a stress–strain cycle whose enclosed area measures dissipated work}

Oscillatory <rheometry> measures <stress> amplitude and phase while imposing a small <strain>. An <oscillatory-shear amplitude sweep> first identifies a linear regime; an <oscillatory-shear frequency sweep> then measures $G'$ and $G''$. Repeat at controlled <temperature>, suppress evaporation, check <wall slip> and sample aging, and correct instrument <inertia> at high <frequency>. Frequency-dependent structure and a nonlinear response cannot be inferred reliably by fitting one <spring> and <dashpot> to one point.

An <amphiphile> has a solvent-affine part and a solvent-averse part within one molecule. In water these are commonly called <hydrophilic> head and <hydrophobic> tail. Two nonbiological examples are <sodium dodecyl sulfate>, with a twelve-carbon tail and an anionic sulfate head, and <cetyltrimethylammonium bromide>, with a sixteen-carbon tail and a cationic quaternary-ammonium head. Their <counterions> balance the ionic head-group charge; the head and tail do not have interchangeable interactions with water.

Aggregation reduces unfavorable exposure of <hydrocarbon> tails to water but loses molecular translational <entropy>. Interfacial <free energy>, head-group <solvation> and electrostatic repulsion, chain packing and <conformational entropy> determine the preferred aggregate. The <hydrophobic effect> is a solvent-mediated free-energy balance, rather than a literal attractive bond between tails.

The <amphiphile packing parameter>
$$
P=\frac{v}{a_0\ell_c}
$$
compares tail <volume> $v$, effective head-group <area> $a_0$ and maximum tail <length> $\ell_c$. A spherical core of radius $R$ obeys $v/a_0=R/3$, and a long cylindrical core obeys $v/a_0=R/2$. The condition $R\leq\ell_c$ gives the conventional spherical window $P\lesssim1/3$ and cylindrical window $1/3\lesssim P\lesssim1/2$. Less strongly curved bilayers become compatible for $1/2\lesssim P\lesssim1$, with planar packing near one; values above one can favor inverse <curvature> in an appropriate nonpolar surrounding medium. These windows are geometric guides rather than exact phase boundaries.

A larger hydrated or electrostatically repelling head increases $a_0$ and favors positive <curvature>. Screening of charged heads or a smaller head can lower $a_0$ and favor rods or sheets. Multiple tails increase the tail <volume> relative to head <area> and can favor bilayers. Tail <length>, branching, <temperature> and <solvent> selectivity alter both geometry and interaction <free energy>, so one cannot predict a unique aggregate from hydrophobicity alone.<Theory of self-assembly of hydrocarbon amphiphiles by Jacob N. Israelachvili and others>[1976, pp. 1525–1568: coupled thermodynamics, molecular packing constraints and globular, cylindrical and bilayer aggregates]{ref}

In the supplied chemical-potential expression, $\mu$ is the <equilibrium> <chemical potential> per <amphiphile>, $\mu_\alpha^0$ the standard <free energy> per <amphiphile> within an aggregate of size $\alpha$, and $x_\alpha$ the dimensionless molecular fraction belonging to that size class. The <aggregate molecular fraction versus aggregate number fraction> distinction explains the logarithm: the aggregate count fraction is $x_\alpha/\alpha$, and dividing its translational <chemical potential> by $\alpha$ gives a per-molecule value. Thus
$$
\alpha\frac{\mu-\mu_\alpha^0}{k_BT}=\ln(x_\alpha/\alpha),
\qquad
\boxed{x_\alpha=\alpha\exp\!\left[\frac{\alpha(\mu-\mu_\alpha^0)}{k_BT}\right].}
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $T$ absolute <temperature>, and $\alpha$ a positive integer. The logarithm acts on a dimensionless ideal activity; a concentrated nonideal solution would require <activity coefficients>.

<equilibrium> exchange of <amphiphiles> gives the same per-molecule $\mu$ for every size. For monomers, $\mu=\mu_1^0+k_BT\ln x_1$. Substitution yields the <finite-micelle aggregation-number distribution>
$$
\boxed{x_\alpha=\alpha x_1^\alpha
\exp\!\left[\frac{\alpha(\mu_1^0-\mu_\alpha^0)}{k_BT}\right],\qquad
x_{\rm tot}=\sum_{\alpha\ge1}x_\alpha.}
$$
This formula by itself does not prescribe a preferred <micelle> size: the size dependence of $\mu_\alpha^0$ must include the competing packing and interaction terms. If their balance favors a finite size $\alpha_*$, a monomer population and a peak around that size can coexist. Dividing by $\alpha$ would instead plot the distribution of aggregate counts.

At very low $x_{\rm tot}$, the factors $x_1^\alpha$ suppress large aggregates and most molecules remain monomers. As $x_1$ rises, a favorable aggregate class grows extremely rapidly because of this high <power>. The <critical micelle concentration from standard chemical potentials> has the large-size estimate
$$
x_{1,*}\simeq\exp\!\left[\frac{\mu_{\alpha_*}^0-\mu_1^0}{k_BT}\right].
$$
More precisely, the illustrative crossover condition $x_{\alpha_*}=x_1$ gives
$$
\ln x_{1,*}=
\frac{\alpha_*(\mu_{\alpha_*}^0-\mu_1^0)/(k_BT)-\ln\alpha_*}{\alpha_*-1}.
$$
Added material above the crossover enters aggregates predominantly. Monomer <concentration> then increases slowly rather than being exactly pinned: its logarithm enters $\mu$, while aggregate populations respond with a large factor $\alpha_*$. For finite <micelles> this is a sharp association crossover, not an absolute prohibition on aggregates below one exact <concentration>.

\Image[paper-1-aggregate-distribution.png]
{title=Original illustrative molecular-fraction distributions over aggregation number below and above a micellization crossover}

The drawing uses a declared illustrative standard-free-energy model favoring aggregates near $\alpha=40$. It is not an inferred distribution or critical <concentration> for either named <surfactant>. The separate monomer peak is at $\alpha=1$; a distribution of molecular fractions weights each aggregate by its number of molecules.

For the general physics of a nearly planar <fluid> sheet, let $h(x,y)$ be a small-slope displacement, $\kappa$ <membrane bending rigidity> and $\sigma$ mechanical tension. At fixed topology and symmetric reference <curvature>, the quadratic <Helfrich energy> is
$$
F[h]=\frac12\int d^2x\,[\kappa(\nabla^2h)^2+\sigma|\nabla h|^2].
$$
The bending term is the small-slope curvature-elastic <energy>; additional spontaneous-curvature or boundary terms require extra information.<Elastic Properties of Lipid Bilayers: Theory and Possible Experiments by Wolfgang Helfrich>[Section II.C, p. 695, equation (12): curvature-elastic energy and coefficients with units of energy]{ref}

For Fourier amplitudes $h_{\mathbf q}=A^{-1}\int_Ah(\mathbf x)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}d^2x$, equipartition gives the <membrane fluctuation spectrum under tension>,
$$
\boxed{\langle|h_{\mathbf q}|^2\rangle=
\frac{k_BT}{A(\kappa q^4+\sigma q^2)}.}
$$
Higher <temperature> increases fluctuations, while greater <membrane bending rigidity> or tension suppresses them. Short-wavelength modes are predominantly bending-controlled; longer wavelengths can be tension-controlled. Finite sheet size, supports, <confinement> and imposed <area> set <boundary conditions> and <infrared cutoffs>; continuum elasticity also needs a short-distance cutoff. <solvent> <viscosity> affects relaxation rates, although it does not enter this <equilibrium> amplitude at fixed elastic parameters.

In a <regular solution>, ideal combinatorial <entropy> competes with a nonzero unlike-contact <energy>. For equal molecular <volumes> and <mole fraction> $\phi$ of species A, write mixing <free energy> per site as
$$
\frac{f(\phi)}{k_BT}
=\phi\ln\phi+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi),
$$
where
$$
\chi=\frac{z}{k_BT}\left[\epsilon_{AB}-\frac{\epsilon_{AA}+\epsilon_{BB}}2\right].
$$
Here $z$ is the <coordination number> and $\epsilon_{ij}$ a pair-contact <energy> counted consistently in the lattice model. A positive $\chi$ penalizes unlike contacts; a negative value favors them. The logarithmic terms lower <free energy> on mixing. The sign of the <enthalpy of mixing> alone therefore does not determine miscibility.

At fixed <temperature> and composition, a homogeneous phase is globally stable only if no division into two compositions lowers its <free energy>. The <common-tangent construction> selects coexisting compositions $\phi_a,\phi_b$, equivalent to equality of both species' <chemical potentials>. Local stability to infinitesimal fluctuations instead requires
$$
\frac{f''}{k_BT}=\frac1\phi+\frac1{1-\phi}-2\chi>0.
$$
Zero <curvature> gives the <spinodal>, while the coexistence boundary is the <binodal>. Between them a homogeneous state can be metastable and require <nucleation>; inside the <spinodal> its small composition fluctuations grow. The symmetric <regular solution> has
$$
\boxed{\phi_c=\tfrac12,\qquad\chi_c=2.}
$$
Below this contact penalty, the <free energy> is convex at every composition. Above it a miscibility gap is possible around the central composition. If $\chi=A/T$ with $A>0$, heating promotes mixing, but specific interactions or temperature-dependent noncombinatorial terms can produce other trends.

For two <polymers>, let $N_A,N_B$ count equal-volume segments per chain, and now let $\phi$ be the segment <volume fraction> of A. Connecting segments into chains reduces their independent translational <entropy>. The <binary Flory-Huggins polymer blend> has
$$
\frac{f}{k_BT}=\frac{\phi}{N_A}\ln\phi
+\frac{1-\phi}{N_B}\ln(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi).
$$
The energetic contact term remains extensive in the number of segments, while the favorable <entropy> per segment is reduced by $1/N_A$ and $1/N_B$. This is the key <long-chain limit of polymer-blend miscibility>: a small positive incompatibility can overcome the mixing <entropy>. The lattice treatment is a controlled model for this argument, rather than a claim that every real blend has composition-independent $\chi$.<Thermodynamics of High Polymer Solutions by Paul J. Flory>[1942, pp. 51–55: chain-connected lattice configurations and combinatorial mixing entropy; later sections add the energetic contribution]{ref}

The local stability condition and critical interaction parameter become
$$
\frac1{N_A\phi}+\frac1{N_B(1-\phi)}-2\chi>0,\qquad
\boxed{\chi_c=\frac12\left(N_A^{-1/2}+N_B^{-1/2}\right)^2.}
$$
For equal chain <lengths>, $\chi_c=2/N$. Similar segment chemistry and matched cohesive interactions can reduce the unlike-contact penalty; favorable specific interactions, such as complementary <hydrogen bonding>, can make mixing favorable even for long chains. Lower <molar mass> increases mixing <entropy>. <temperature> can help when it lowers the effective $\chi$, but heating is not universally sufficient. A suitable common <solvent> can facilitate processing or ternary mixing, and a block-copolymer compatibilizer can stabilize fine domains; neither guarantees that the final solvent-free binary material is one <equilibrium> molecular phase.

An oil–water <emulsion> illustrates <emulsion stability versus molecular miscibility>. A <surfactant> adsorbs with its water-affine head toward water and its oil-affine tail toward oil, lowering <interfacial tension> and the <work> $\gamma\,dA$ needed to make additional interface. Breaking a fixed oil <volume> $V_o$ into spherical droplets of radius $R$ gives total <area> $3V_o/R$, so creating small droplets still requires substantial <area> and usually mechanical <work>.

Adsorbed <surfactants> can oppose <droplet coalescence> through charged double-layer repulsion, hydrated-head interactions or <steric repulsion> from sufficiently extended groups. Adequate coverage, <adsorption> time, ionic conditions and <film drainage> affect the lifetime. A lower <interfacial tension> does not itself remove the bulk miscibility gap, and a protected <emulsion> is commonly kinetically stabilized. A thermodynamically stable <microemulsion> is a distinct situation requiring an appropriate composition and free-energy balance. The two liquids must not be called molecularly miscible simply because droplets disperse visibly.

For the capillary, balance upward contact-line <force> against hydrostatic column weight, neglecting meniscus <volume> and gas <density>. For inner radius $r$,
$$
2\pi r\gamma\cos\theta=\rho g\pi r^2h,
\qquad h=\frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho gr}.
$$
The <contact angle> is measured through the liquid. <complete wetting> means $\theta_2=0$, so the <contact-angle change of capillary rise> gives
$$
\boxed{h_2=\frac{0.050}{\cos35^\circ}=0.06104\,\mathrm m\simeq6.10\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
This follows from the stipulated <wetting> change, holding radius, bulk liquid <surface tension> and <density> fixed. One should not replace that premise with a general claim that grease always improves glass <wetting>; if contamination also changes the liquid <surface tension>, the original height and new angle alone would not determine the new height.

For two wide vertical plates separated by gap $d$, use unit width. There are two upward contact-line contributions and liquid cross-sectional <area> $d$, giving the <capillary rise between parallel plates>:
$$
2\gamma\cos\theta=\rho gdh_{\rm plates},
\qquad\boxed{h_{\rm plates}=\frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho gd}.}
$$
Only one principal meniscus <curvature> contributes between wide plates, whereas a circular tube has two. For the same <fluid> and angle,
$$
\boxed{\frac{h_{\rm plates}}{h_{\rm tube}}=\frac rd.}
$$
If the plate gap equals the tube diameter, $d=2r$, the rise is half as large: the initial and completely <wetting> heights would be $2.50$ cm and $3.05$ cm. If instead $d=r$, the heights are equal. In either geometry, changing only the angle from $35^\circ$ to zero multiplies the height by $1/\cos35^\circ$. Since the question specifies no plate spacing, the general gap-dependent relation is the unambiguous result; the half-height answer requires the equal-gap-to-diameter convention.
