Use the <amphiphile packing parameter> $P=v/(a_0\ell_c)$, where $v$ is tail <volume>, $a_0$ the effective head-group <area> and $\ell_c$ the maximum tail extension. The conventional cylindrical-<micelle> window is $1/3\lesssim P\lesssim1/2$. Its upper limit follows directly from <cylindrical micelle geometry>: a long core of radius $R$ has volume-to-area ratio $R/2$, so $v/a_0=R/2\leq\ell_c/2$. The spherical packing limit is $P=1/3$; above it a filled spherical core cannot accommodate the assumed head-group <area> and chain length.

Inverting both positive inequalities gives
$$
\frac{2v}{\ell_c}\lesssim a_0\lesssim\frac{3v}{\ell_c},\qquad
\boxed{0.246\,\mathrm{nm^2}\lesssim a_0\lesssim0.369\,\mathrm{nm^2}.}
$$
Thus the useful estimate is **a head-group area of approximately $0.25$–$0.37\,\mathrm{nm^2}$**. If phase-boundary configurations are excluded, use strict inequalities. These endpoints are geometric morphology crossovers, rather than exact thermodynamic phase boundaries: chain conformations and head-group interactions also affect which <micelle> minimizes the <free energy>. The supplied molecular <volume> is interpreted as the tail-core <volume> required by this packing model.<Theory of self-assembly of hydrocarbon amphiphiles by Jacob N. Israelachvili and others>[1976, aggregate packing theory; cylindrical and spherical core constraints]{ref}

For an equilibrium <contact angle> measured through the <liquid>, <Young's equation> balances the tangential interfacial forces:
$$
\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta.
$$
The pure-<ethanol> measurement therefore fixes the solid–vapour <surface energy> as
$$
\gamma_{SV}=(21.0+21.4\cos23^\circ)\times10^{-3}
=40.699\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.
$$
**The inferred surface energy is $40.7\,\mathrm{mJ\,m^{-2}}$**, equivalently $40.7\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$. The equality of these units follows because creating interfacial <area> costs <energy>.

For the equal-fraction <mixture>, the stated linear relations give $\gamma_{SL}=20.5\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ and $\gamma_{LV}=28.5\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$. Holding the solid–vapour <surface energy> fixed,
$$
\cos\theta_{1/2}=\frac{40.699-20.5}{28.5}=0.70873,
\qquad \boxed{\theta_{1/2}\simeq44.9^\circ.}
$$
The small decrease in $\gamma_{SL}$ increases the numerator of <Young's equation>, but the increase in $\gamma_{LV}$ has the larger relative effect and reduces $\cos\theta$. Consequently **the contact angle increases and the mixture wets the surface less well**. This inference uses equilibrium <wetting> on an unchanged smooth solid; a changing surface composition or <contact-angle hysteresis> would require additional measurements.

The ordinary physics is <Stokes settling velocity>. For a generic isolated rigid <sphere> of radius $a$, particle <mass density> $\rho_p$ and surrounding-fluid <mass density> $\rho_f$, balance the buoyancy-corrected body <force> against <Stokes law>:
$$
\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}(\rho_p-\rho_f)g_{\rm eff}=6\pi\eta aU,
\qquad U=\frac{2a^2(\rho_p-\rho_f)g_{\rm eff}}{9\eta}.
$$
Here $\eta$ is the <dynamic viscosity>. If the effective acceleration is approximately constant over the displacement and the terminal regime has been reached, $s=Ut$. In an actual centrifuge $g_{\rm eff}=\Omega^2r$, so a sufficiently large radial change requires integrating $dr/dt\propto r$ instead of assuming constant $U$. This generic relation does not resolve the deferred biological application.

The <Reynolds number>
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UL}{\eta}=\frac{UL}{\nu}}
$$
compares advective <inertia> with viscous <force> on a specified <length> scale $L$ and <velocity> scale $U$. Here $\rho$ is fluid <mass density>, $\eta$ is <dynamic viscosity> and $\nu=\eta/\rho$ is <kinematic viscosity>. In the <Navier-Stokes equation>, advective acceleration scales as $U^2/L$, while the viscous term per unit <mass> scales as $\nu U/L^2$; their ratio is <Reynolds number>.

When $\mathrm{Re}\ll1$ and unsteady fluid <inertia> is also negligible, the <incompressible flow> equations reduce to <Stokes flow>:
$$
-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u+\mathbf f=\mathbf0,
\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Thus <pressure>, applied body <forces> and viscous stresses balance at each instant. For a translating rigid <sphere>, <drag> is proportional to <velocity>, $F_D=6\pi\eta aU$, rather than the inertial scale $\rho a^2U^2$. Given the same instantaneous geometry, reversing the imposed boundary velocities reverses the <Stokes flow>; there is no fluid-inertial coasting in this approximation. This statement concerns the fluid limit and does not discard a separately significant particle <inertia>.

A low steady <Reynolds number> alone does not justify dropping rapid unsteady acceleration. For forcing frequency $\omega$, the additional dimensionless ratio is $\rho\omega L^2/\eta$. No organism-specific sizes, velocities or propulsion mechanism are assumed here.

The <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi$ measures the excess cost of unlike segment contacts. In a simple lattice with coordination number $z$, contact <energies> $\epsilon_{AA},\epsilon_{BB},\epsilon_{AB}$ and <temperature> $T$,
$$
\chi=\frac{z}{k_BT}\left[\epsilon_{AB}-\frac{\epsilon_{AA}+\epsilon_{BB}}2\right].
$$
A positive <Flory interaction parameter> penalizes unlike contacts; a negative value favors them. Mixing still depends on the balance with <entropy of mixing>, rather than the sign of $\chi$ alone.

For two <polymers> of degrees of polymerization $N_A,N_B$, let $\phi$ be the <volume fraction> of species $A$. The <binary Flory-Huggins polymer blend> has mixing <free energy> per lattice site
$$
\frac{f(\phi)}{k_BT}=\frac\phi{N_A}\ln\phi+
\frac{1-\phi}{N_B}\ln(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi).
$$
The logarithmic terms favor mixing. Their factors $1/N_A$ and $1/N_B$ arise because an entire chain translates as one object; connecting many segments greatly reduces the mixing <entropy> per segment. Hence long <polymers> can demix for a much smaller positive <Flory interaction parameter> than small <molecules>.

For composition-independent $\chi$, the local stability criterion is
$$
\frac{f''(\phi)}{k_BT}=
\frac1{N_A\phi}+\frac1{N_B(1-\phi)}-2\chi.
$$
Positive curvature means stability to small composition fluctuations, zero curvature locates the <spinodal>, and negative curvature means spontaneous growth of fluctuations. Actual two-phase coexistence is determined by the <common tangent construction>, or equality of both species' <chemical potentials>; its boundary is the <binodal>. A locally stable but globally unfavourable homogeneous <mixture> can lie between the <binodal> and <spinodal>.

Setting the second and third <derivatives> of $f$ to zero gives
$$
\phi_c=\frac{\sqrt{N_B}}{\sqrt{N_A}+\sqrt{N_B}},\qquad
\chi_c=\frac12\left(N_A^{-1/2}+N_B^{-1/2}\right)^2.
$$
For the <symmetric Flory-Huggins polymer blend>, **$\phi_c=1/2$ and $\chi_c=2/N$**. For a <polymer solution> with $N_B=1$ and long chains of species $A$, $\chi_c\to1/2$, consistent with the <theta solvent> limit. An effective <Flory interaction parameter> can depend on <temperature> and composition; in the simple energetic case $\chi\propto1/T$, heating encourages mixing, but that temperature trend is not universal.

The original illustration uses stipulated $N_A=N_B=100$. At $\chi=0.01$ the mixing <free energy> is convex, whereas at $\chi=0.03$ a central unstable region appears. The horizontal <common tangent construction> in the latter case joins the symmetric coexisting compositions; its contacts differ from the <spinodal> boundaries. These are model curves, not supplied experimental data.

The <critical micelle concentration> is the approximate <surfactant> <concentration> above which added <amphiphile> predominantly joins <micelles>. Isolated <amphiphiles> expose their tails to the surrounding <solvent>; aggregation can reduce the unfavorable exposure, while losing translational <entropy> and incurring head-group and chain-packing costs. The favored aggregate minimizes the total <free energy>.

A <mass-action model of micellization> makes the concentration dependence explicit. For aggregation of $q$ monomers into one <micelle>, equilibrium requires
$$
q\mu_1=\mu_q,\qquad
\mu_1=\mu_1^\circ+k_BT\ln a_1,\qquad
\mu_q=\mu_q^\circ+k_BT\ln a_q.
$$
The dimensionless <thermodynamic activities> therefore obey
$$
\boxed{a_q=K_q a_1^q,\qquad
K_q=\exp\!\left[-\frac{\mu_q^\circ-q\mu_1^\circ}{k_BT}\right].}
$$
For a large <aggregation number> $q$, a modest increase in monomer activity produces a steep increase in <micelle> abundance. In an ideal dilute approximation with the same reference <concentration> $c^\circ$ for both species, the amphiphile balance is
$$
\frac{c_{\rm tot}}{c^\circ}=a_1+qK_qa_1^q.
$$
Well below the crossover almost every <amphiphile> is isolated. Above it, most additional <amphiphile> enters <micelles>, so the free-monomer <concentration> rises only slowly. It is not exactly constant for a finite $q$, and a small aggregate population can exist below the nominal <critical micelle concentration>. **The experimentally sharp change is an association crossover, not an assertion that aggregation is absolutely absent below one concentration.**

\Image[paper-1-micelle-crossover.png]
{title=A finite-aggregation <mass-action model of micellization> produces a concentration crossover}

In the original illustrative model, $q=50$ and $qK_q=1$ after choosing the arbitrary reference <concentration>. The normalization is a teaching choice, not a measured <critical micelle concentration>. The monomer curve flattens relative to total <concentration>, while the aggregated fraction rises rapidly.

An experimental <critical micelle concentration> can be estimated from a change of slope in <surface tension> against the logarithm of total <surfactant> <concentration>, as interfacial coverage approaches saturation and bulk aggregation becomes important. Changes in <electrical conductivity> for ionic <surfactants>, or in <light scattering>, supply other probes. Different observables can give slightly different operational values because the crossover has finite width. Molecular structure, <temperature> and ionic conditions affect the balance of <chemical potentials>; screening charged head groups can reduce repulsion and favor aggregation, but no universal sign of the temperature dependence should be assumed.

A <colloid> has dispersed constituents whose relevant dimensions are mesoscopic: roughly $1\,\mathrm{nm}$ to $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in at least one direction. Interfaces and <Brownian motion> are consequently important. The size range is a useful convention, not a sharp boundary separating all physical behaviour. A <sol> of fine <silicon dioxide> particles in <water> illustrates solid in <liquid>; an oil-in-water <emulsion> illustrates <liquid> in another immiscible <liquid>; and a <foam> illustrates <gas> dispersed in a <liquid>, with thin intervening films supplying the small structural dimension. A <colloid> need not be a <thermodynamic equilibrium> state.<Definition and classification of colloids by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[Definition and classification of colloids: approximate size range, colloidal dispersions, sols, emulsions and foams]{ref}

Unprotected particles attract through <van der Waals forces>. For two equal <spheres> of radius $a$ with a small surface gap $H$, the <near-contact van der Waals attraction of spheres> is
$$
U_{\rm vdW}(H)\simeq-\frac{A_Ha}{12H},\qquad H\ll a,
$$
where the <Hamaker constant> $A_H$ is positive for the attractive case. The large number of interacting constituent <molecules> can give a well much deeper than $k_BT$, even though individual molecular attractions are weak. <Brownian motion> brings particles together, and a sufficiently deep attractive well makes separation unlikely. This gives <particle aggregation>; solid particles can remain distinct within a cluster rather than merging into one continuous solid. <Emulsion> droplets can additionally coalesce if the separating film ruptures.

<Charge stabilization of colloids> supplies a repulsive barrier. Like-charged surfaces carry compensating <counterions> and <electric double layers>; bringing the layers together costs <free energy>. <DLVO theory> combines this repulsion with <van der Waals forces>. A barrier large compared with $k_BT$ can make aggregation slow, even when sticking would lower the final <free energy>. Adding salt generally shortens the <Debye–Hückel screening length> and can lower the barrier; surface charge regulation and specific ion interactions also matter. A negatively charged particle and a positively charged particle need not repel.

<Steric stabilization> instead coats each particle with adsorbed or grafted <polymer> chains. In a <good solvent>, overlap and compression of these layers raise the <osmotic pressure> and reduce chain <entropy>, opposing close contact. <Electrosteric stabilization> combines the two mechanisms. Dense, securely bound layers matter: incomplete adsorption can allow <polymer bridging>, while nonadsorbing dissolved <polymers> can create an attractive <depletion force>. Poor solvent quality can collapse a protective layer. Reducing the density mismatch slows sedimentation but does not remove the interparticle attraction, so uniform suspension and resistance to aggregation are different requirements.

\Image[paper-1-colloid-growth.png]
{title=An illustrative interaction barrier and the radial concentration field around a growing <sphere>}

The left panel is an original qualitative potential with arbitrary coefficients: it illustrates a barrier and its reduction by shorter-ranged repulsion, without fitting an experimental <DLVO theory> curve or extrapolating the continuum attraction to molecular contact. The right panel plots the derived quasi-steady <concentration> profile in dimensionless variables.

In **diffusion-controlled growth**, attachment is rapid and delivery through the surrounding medium is the bottleneck. The growing surface depletes the neighboring <solute>, so a substantial <concentration gradient> develops. In **reaction-controlled growth**, arrival is fast compared with incorporation; many arrivals fail to attach, and the surface <concentration> stays near the reservoir value. The analogous distinction for <particle aggregation> is between near-unit sticking probability in <diffusion-limited cluster aggregation> and barrier-suppressed sticking in <reaction-limited cluster aggregation>. Here the subsequent calculation concerns accretion onto one growing particle, rather than a collision kernel for two mobile clusters.

The printed time-dependent equation is the **diffusion equation**, not the usual <Poisson equation>. For constant <diffusion coefficient> $D$, no bulk sources and negligible <advection>, <Fick's laws> give the number <flux> $\mathbf j=-D\nabla c$. Local <conservation of mass>, expressed here as conservation of the accreting constituent, then gives
$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=D\nabla^2c.
$$
An arbitrary time-independent exterior profile does not solve the full moving-boundary problem exactly. The required elementary result uses a quasi-steady approximation: <diffusion> establishes the exterior profile much faster than the particle radius changes. At each instantaneous radius $a$, set $\partial c/\partial t\simeq0$. Thus the exterior field solves the homogeneous <Laplace equation>,
$$
\nabla^2c=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dc}{dr}\right)=0,
\qquad c(a)=c_s,\quad c(\infty)=c_\infty.
$$
Two integrations give $c=A+B/r$. The two <boundary conditions> fix
$$
\boxed{c(r)=c_\infty-(c_\infty-c_s)\frac ar,\qquad r\geq a.}
$$
For growth, $c_\infty>c_s$, so the profile increases outwards. Its radial <flux> is negative, and the positive inward arrival rate is
$$
\dot n=4\pi a^2D\left.\frac{dc}{dr}\right|_{a^+}
=4\pi aD(c_\infty-c_s).
$$
A material density is needed to turn an arrival rate into a radial <velocity>. Introduce the constant constituent <number density> $c_p$ inside the particle, using the same units as $c_s,c_\infty$. The sharp-interface balance, neglecting exterior <advection>, is
$$
4\pi a^2(c_p-c_s)\dot a=4\pi aD(c_\infty-c_s).
$$
The difference $c_p-c_s$ accounts for material already present in the volume swept by the growing interface. In the usual dilute-feed approximation $c_s\ll c_p$, it can be replaced by $c_p$. With constant reservoir and surface <concentrations>, integration from radius $a_0$ gives the <constant-reservoir diffusion-controlled particle growth> law
$$
\boxed{a^2(t)=a_0^2+\frac{2D(c_\infty-c_s)}{c_p-c_s}\,t.}
$$
Consequently **$a\propto t^{1/2}$ when the growth contribution dominates $a_0^2$**. For a finite seed at the literal limit $t\to0$, the increment $a-a_0$ is instead initially proportional to $t$. Here “early time” means before appreciable reservoir depletion or interactions with other particles, while already beyond the rapid initial establishment of the <diffusion> field. Indeed, the ratio of diffusion-adjustment time $a^2/D$ to radial-growth time $a/\dot a$ is $(c_\infty-c_s)/(c_p-c_s)$; small supersaturation relative to the particle density makes the quasi-steady approximation self-consistent. No initial concentration field was specified, so an exact transient solution is not uniquely determined.

For comparison, let a finite surface-attachment coefficient be $k_s$ and the equilibrium surface <concentration> be $c_{\rm eq}$. In the dilute-feed approximation, the <Robin boundary condition> $Dc'(a)=k_s[c(a)-c_{\rm eq}]$ gives
$$
\dot a\simeq\frac{D(c_\infty-c_{\rm eq})}{c_p(a+D/k_s)}.
$$
For $k_sa/D\gg1$ this recovers transport control; for $k_sa/D\ll1$, the <surface-reaction-controlled particle growth> has approximately constant $\dot a=k_s(c_\infty-c_{\rm eq})/c_p$. Thus **reaction control gives $a-a_0\propto t$**, in contrast to the diffusion-controlled square-radius law. No numerical $c_p$ or $k_s$ is supplied or needed for these scaling results.

<Polydispersity> means that a sample contains chains of different <molar masses> or lengths. Let $n_i$ count chains with <molar mass> $M_i$. The <number-average molar mass> gives every chain equal weight, while the <weight-average molar mass> weights a chain population by its contribution to total <mass>:
$$
\boxed{M_n=\frac{\sum_i n_iM_i}{\sum_i n_i},\qquad
M_w=\frac{\sum_i n_iM_i^2}{\sum_i n_iM_i}.}
$$
Writing $w_i=n_iM_i/\sum_jn_jM_j$ makes the latter interpretation explicit: $M_w=\sum_iw_iM_i$. Large chains therefore influence $M_w$ more strongly. If brackets denote an average over chain number,
$$
\frac{M_w}{M_n}=\frac{\langle M^2\rangle}{\langle M\rangle^2}
=1+\frac{\operatorname{Var}(M)}{\langle M\rangle^2}\geq1.
$$
The ratio is <molar-mass dispersity>, conventionally written Đ. It equals one only when every chain has the same <molar mass>. Different distributions can share the same ratio, so it does not specify the full distribution.

For a <freely jointed chain>, write the end-to-end vector as the sum of $N$ independently oriented steps $\mathbf b_i$, each of fixed length $a$. <Isotropy> gives $\langle\mathbf b_i\rangle=\mathbf0$, and <independence> gives $\langle\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j\rangle=0$ for $i\ne j$. Hence
$$
\langle R^2\rangle=
\sum_{i,j=1}^N\langle\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j\rangle
=\sum_{i=1}^N a^2,
\qquad \boxed{\langle R^2\rangle=Na^2.}
$$
The root-mean-square <end-to-end distance of a polymer chain> is $a\sqrt N$, although the mean vector is zero. Its <polymer contour length> is $L=Na$, an upper bound on extension; it is not the typical unforced end-to-end length.

Stiffness correlates neighboring bond directions, so the discarded cross terms need not vanish. A <freely rotating chain> fixes the angle $\theta$ between successive forward bond vectors but leaves torsion uniformly distributed. Set $q=\cos\theta$, so $\langle\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_{i+s}\rangle=a^2q^s$. Then
$$
\langle R^2\rangle=Na^2+2a^2\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}(N-s)q^s
=Na^2\left[\frac{1+q}{1-q}
-\frac{2q(1-q^N)}{N(1-q)^2}\right],\qquad |q|<1.
$$
For a chain long compared with its orientational correlation range, the <characteristic ratio of a freely rotating chain> approaches $C_\infty=(1+q)/(1-q)$. Positive forward persistence increases the mean-square size and gives the <Kuhn length of a freely rotating chain> $b_K=aC_\infty$. A chemical internal bond angle is supplementary to the forward-vector angle used here, so substituting that internal angle requires $q=-\cos\theta_{\rm internal}$. For $q\to1$ at fixed $N$, the finite sum tends to $N^2a^2$, the rigid-rod result; taking the long-chain limit first would incorrectly predict an unbounded size. Real torsional energy preferences can be represented by a <rotational isomeric state model>, with weighted torsional states and correlations beyond independent free rotations.

A <worm-like chain> describes continuously varying local orientation. For unit tangent $\mathbf t(s)$ and bending stiffness $\kappa$, its elastic <energy> is $E_b=(\kappa/2)\int_0^L|d\mathbf t/ds|^2ds$. In three spatial dimensions the <persistence length> is $\ell_p=\kappa/(k_BT)$ and
$$
\langle\mathbf t(s)\cdot\mathbf t(s')\rangle=e^{-|s-s'|/\ell_p}.
$$
Since $\mathbf R=\int_0^L\mathbf t(s)\,ds$, integrating the tangent correlation gives the <end-to-end distance of a worm-like chain>:
$$
\boxed{\langle R^2\rangle=
2\ell_pL-2\ell_p^2(1-e^{-L/\ell_p}).}
$$
For $L\ll\ell_p$, expansion gives $\langle R^2\rangle=L^2-L^3/(3\ell_p)+\cdots$, approaching a straight rod. For $L\gg\ell_p$, it gives $\langle R^2\rangle\simeq2\ell_pL$, the long-chain <ideal chain> result with <Kuhn length> $2\ell_p$. These models isolate stiffness; long-range <excluded volume> can additionally swell an isolated chain in a <good solvent>. When coarse-graining, a chemical repeat count and a count of independent statistical steps must not be silently interchanged.

\Image[paper-1-chain-and-brush-scaling.png]
{title=Stiff-chain crossover and distinct <polymer brush> density scalings}

The first panel shows the exact <worm-like chain> crossover between a rod and a long flexible coil. The second compares original normalized scaling curves for a uniform <polymer brush>; their prefactors have been chosen for illustration and do not fit the exam's experimental points.

For a <good solvent>, consider long flexible <grafted polymer chains> with <grafting density> $\sigma$ and lateral spacing $D\sim\sigma^{-1/2}$. At high coverage, $D$ is smaller than the isolated coil size, and adjacent chains cannot retain their unperturbed conformations. A scaling model divides each chain into <concentration blobs> of diameter $D$. Inside one blob the chain retains good-solvent statistics,
$$
D\sim ag^{3/5},\qquad g\sim(D/a)^{5/3},
$$
using the <Flory approximation> exponent $3/5$. The $N/g$ blobs per chain form a stretched sequence approximately normal to the surface. The <good-solvent brush height> therefore obeys
$$
h\sim\frac NgD\sim Na^{5/3}D^{-2/3},\qquad
\boxed{h\sim Na(\sigma a^2)^{1/3}.}
$$
Here $a$ and $N$ refer to consistently chosen effective segments, whose microscopic properties are held fixed as chain length changes. The result is a scaling law with an undetermined order-one prefactor, not a fully specified height in nanometres.

A complementary free-energy balance per chain is
$$
\frac{F}{k_BT}\sim\frac{h^2}{Na^2}+\frac{vN^2\sigma}{h},
$$
where $v>0$ is effective segment <excluded volume>. The first term is the chain <entropy> cost of stretching and the second is the cost of crowding segments into a layer of volume $h/\sigma$ per chain. Minimization gives $h^3\sim vN^3a^2\sigma$; with $v\sim a^3$ it recovers the same height. The blob argument incorporates good-solvent correlations more directly. Both descriptions require overlapping chains, many segments per chain, and heights below the <polymer contour length>; the power law cannot be extrapolated to infinite grafting density.

In the printed experimental graph, the vertical axis is $hD^2/(10^6\,\mathrm{\mathring A^3})$ and the horizontal axis is $M/10^5$. The approximately straight trend through the origin therefore means **$hD^2\propto M$**. Since $D^{-2}\sim\sigma$ and $M$ is proportional to chain length for a fixed repeat chemistry,
$$
\boxed{h\propto M\sigma\propto N\sigma\qquad\text{(poor solvent).}}
$$
The dashed guide rises by roughly $1.8$–$1.9$ vertical units over six horizontal units, corresponding to $hD^2/M$ of order $3\,\mathrm{\mathring A^3}$ per unit of the plotted $M$. This is an approximate graphical slope, not a fitted material constant; the graph does not specify a numerical monomer count or a repeat-unit volume from which an absolute brush height could independently be calculated.

The <poor-solvent brush height> has a simple volume argument. Let $v_0$ be the volume per repeat and $\phi$ the roughly fixed polymer <volume fraction> within a dense uniform layer. There are $N\sigma$ repeats per substrate <area>, so
$$
\phi h=N\sigma v_0,\qquad
\boxed{h\simeq\frac{Nv_0\sigma}{\phi}.}
$$
For fixed poor-solvent conditions, $\phi$ is approximately independent of $N$ and $\sigma$, giving the observed linear grafting-density dependence. Polymer–polymer contacts are favored and the layer becomes relatively compact; in a <good solvent>, favorable solvent contact and segment repulsion instead produce a swollen layer whose stretching and osmotic costs balance at the weaker $\sigma^{1/3}$ dependence. Sparse isolated <polymer globules> or lateral segregation fall outside the uniform-brush model.
