= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Reynolds number> compares inertial and viscous effects:
$$
\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UL}{\eta},
$$
where $\rho$ is fluid <mass density>, $U$ a characteristic swimming <speed>, $L$ a characteristic body <length>, and $\eta$ the <dynamic viscosity>. Both swimmers are in the same fluid, so the fluid properties cancel from the ratio:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{bacterium}}}{\mathrm{Re}_{\mathrm{whale}}}
=\frac{U_bL_b}{U_wL_w}.
$$
For an order-of-magnitude estimate, take $L_b=2\,\mu\mathrm m$, $U_b=20\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $L_w=10\,\mathrm m$ and $U_w=5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. With water values $\rho\simeq10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\eta\simeq10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, these give
$$
\mathrm{Re}_b\simeq4\times10^{-5},\qquad
\mathrm{Re}_w\simeq5\times10^7,
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm{Re}_b}{\mathrm{Re}_w}\simeq8\times10^{-13}\sim10^{-12}}.
$$
The dimensions and swimming speeds are estimates, so this ratio is not a universal species-independent constant. Its physical significance is robust: <viscous forces> dominate the bacterium's motion, whereas fluid <inertia> is crucial for the whale.

There is a source error to resolve first. For a <drag coefficient> defined by $F_{\mathrm{drag}}=-\xi v$, the <velocity relaxation time> is $\tau=m/\xi$. The mass and drag coefficient in the PDF's exponential are interchanged: $\xi t/m$ is dimensionless, whereas $mt/\xi$ is not. The consistent mean relaxation law is therefore $\langle v(t)\rangle=v_0e^{-t/\tau}$.

A mean relaxation law alone does not determine a <variance>. Use the usual thermal <Langevin equation>, with a bath at <temperature> $T$ and the <fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle>:
$$
m\dot v=-\xi v+f(t),\qquad
\langle f(t)\rangle=0,\qquad
\langle f(t)f(t')\rangle=2\xi k_BT\,\delta(t-t').
$$
Here the calculation is for one component in <Cartesian coordinates>. With fixed initial position and velocity, integration gives the <mean displacement>
$$
\langle x(t)-x_0\rangle=v_0\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau}).
$$
Integrating the stochastic part of the velocity once more gives the centered displacement
$$
x(t)-\langle x(t)\rangle
=\frac1\xi\int_0^t\bigl[1-e^{-(t-s)/\tau}\bigr]f(s)\,ds.
$$
The delta correlation reduces its <variance> to a single <integral>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}x(t)
&=\frac{2k_BT}{\xi}\int_0^t\bigl[1-e^{-(t-s)/\tau}\bigr]^2ds\\
&=\boxed{2D\left[t-2\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})+\frac\tau2(1-e^{-2t/\tau})\right]},
\qquad \boxed{D=\frac{k_BT}{\xi}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <integrated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck displacement> for a prescribed initial velocity. Its short-time variance is $2Dt^3/(3\tau^2)+O(t^4)$; at long times it is $2Dt+O(1)$. Thus the <diffusion coefficient> is
$$
\boxed{D=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Var}x(t)}{2t}}.
$$
If the requested mean-squared fluctuation instead refers to the conventional equilibrium <mean squared displacement>, the initial velocity is also averaged over its thermal distribution, with $\langle v_0\rangle=0$ and $\langle v_0^2\rangle=k_BT/m$. Its contribution adds $D\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}=2D\left[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})\right]}.
$$
This has the ballistic limit $(k_BT/m)t^2$ and the same diffusive limit $2Dt$. For fixed nonzero $v_0$, the uncentered mean square instead equals the first boxed <variance> plus $v_0^2\tau^2(1-e^{-t/\tau})^2$. In three independent spatial dimensions, the centered variances add, and the long-time result is $\langle|\mathbf r-\langle\mathbf r\rangle|^2\rangle\sim6Dt$. Stating the initial-velocity convention is necessary to distinguish these finite-time formulas.

Two examples are <sodium dodecyl sulfate>, with a negatively charged sulfate head and a hydrocarbon tail, and <cetrimonium bromide>, with a positively charged ammonium head and a hydrocarbon tail. Both are <amphiphiles>: one region is <hydrophilic> and another is <hydrophobic>.

In <water>, the <hydrophobic effect> favors association of the tails into a core, reducing their exposed area while the hydrated heads remain outside. This can lower the total <Gibbs free energy> despite loss of translational <entropy>. Head-group repulsion, interfacial cost and chain-packing constraints oppose unlimited growth, so finite <micelles> are favored under suitable conditions above the <critical micelle concentration>.

The appropriate geometric measure is the <amphiphile packing parameter>:
$$
P=\frac{v}{a_0\ell}=\frac{0.12}{0.5\times0.9}=\boxed{0.267}.
$$
For a spherical <micelle> with $n$ tails and core radius $r_c$, the volume and area relations are $nv=4\pi r_c^3/3$ and $na_0=4\pi r_c^2$. Dividing gives $r_c=3v/a_0$, so a sphere fits without overextending a tail if $P\leq1/3$. Here $P<1/3$ and
$$
r_c=\frac{3v}{a_0}=0.72\,\mathrm{nm}<\ell.
$$
Thus the packing model predicts **spherical <micelles>**. This is a preferred aggregate geometry, not a claim that concentration, temperature or ionic conditions can never alter the morphology.

= Solution

**Diffusion and mobility.** For a dilute isolated sphere in a <Newtonian fluid>, <Stokes's law> gives the drag magnitude $F_{\mathrm{drag}}=6\pi\eta Rv=\xi v$, where $\eta$ is the <dynamic viscosity> and $R$ is the <hydrodynamic radius>. The <mechanical mobility> is therefore $1/\xi$; the translational <hydrodynamic mobility matrix> is $\xi^{-1}I$.

To relate mobility to <diffusion>, put the particles in a weak external <potential energy> $U(x)$. Their drift speed is $-U'(x)/\xi$. For <number density> $n(x)$, the sum of drift and <Fick's first law> fluxes is
$$
J=-\frac{nU'}\xi-D\frac{dn}{dx}.
$$
At <thermal equilibrium>, the <Boltzmann distribution> gives $n\propto e^{-U/(k_BT)}$, and the net flux is zero. Substituting $n'=-nU'/(k_BT)$ into $J=0$ yields $D=k_BT/\xi$. Thus the <Stokes–Einstein relation> is
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta R}}.
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $T$ is absolute <temperature>. The assumptions include a no-slip approximately spherical particle, a continuum fluid, a small particle <Reynolds number>, and negligible hydrodynamic interactions with other particles or boundaries.

**Gravitational sedimentation.** The <buoyant force> must be included, since the mass of displaced water is comparable to the protein mass. Using $1\,\mathrm{Da}=1.66054\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$ gives
$$
m=6.1108\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{kg},\qquad
V_p=\frac{4\pi R^3}{3}=4.2213\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{m^3},
$$
so the buoyant mass in the stated spherical model is
$$
m_b=m-\rho_wV_p=1.8894\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$
The drag coefficient is $\xi=6\pi(10^{-3})(2.16\times10^{-9})=4.0715\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. At terminal speed, $\xi v_g=m_bg$, so
$$
\boxed{v_g=\frac{(m-\rho_wV_p)g}{6\pi\eta R}=4.55\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\quad\text{downwards}}.
$$
Ignoring <buoyancy> would give $1.47\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and would not use the supplied water density. This calculation treats the stated radius as both the hydrodynamic radius and the radius determining displaced volume; a real hydrated <protein> can require a separately measured partial specific volume.

**Electrical drift.** In the specified simple <electrophoresis> model, terminal force balance is $Ze\mathbf E=\xi\mathbf v$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v=\frac{Ze}{6\pi\eta R}\mathbf E},\qquad
\boxed{\mu=\frac{Ze}{6\pi\eta R}},
$$
where $\mu$ is the <electrophoretic mobility> and $e$ the elementary <electric charge>. For the given positive <beta-lactoglobulin> charge,
$$
\boxed{v_{20}=3.94\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=39.4\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}},
$$
in the direction of the <electric field>. This exceeds the gravitational <sedimentation> speed by about $8.6\times10^6$.

**Separation time.** The two populations have equal drag coefficients because their radii are equal. Their difference in drift speed is therefore set by one elementary charge:
$$
\Delta v=v_{21}-v_{20}=\frac{eE}{6\pi\eta R}=1.97\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
Starting with coincident band centres, their separation is $\Delta v\,t$. Hence
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{10^{-3}}{\Delta v}=5.08\times10^2\,\mathrm s\simeq8.47\,\mathrm{min}}.
$$
At that time the original band has travelled $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ and the mutant $21\,\mathrm{mm}$. Their masses do not enter this ideal electrical force balance: equal-radius particles with different masses but the same charge would have equal <electrophoretic mobility> in this model.

**Practical discrimination.** A one-charge difference is enough for the stipulated separation, so the method is sensitive to mutations that change effective charge or friction. It cannot distinguish every mutation: substitutions that preserve charge and hydrodynamic size need not separate. Buffer <pH> changes protein ionization, and realistic <electrophoresis> also involves the ionic atmosphere, <electro-osmosis>, walls or gel interactions, and finite band widths.

For example, the <Stokes–Einstein relation> at $293.15\,\mathrm K$ gives $D=9.94\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. For an initially narrow band, <diffusive broadening of electrophoretic bands> gives
$$
\sigma_x=\sqrt{2Dt}=0.318\,\mathrm{mm}
$$
at the calculated separation time. Thus the centres are about $3.15$ diffusion standard deviations apart, rather than forming infinitely sharp bands. The stated $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ centre-separation criterion is achievable in the model, but actual resolution also depends on the loading width and other dispersion mechanisms.

**Equivalent centrifugal acceleration.** For the original protein to attain its electrical drift speed by <sedimentation>, its buoyant centrifugal force must equal $20eE$:
$$
m_ba_{\mathrm{eff}}=20eE.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{a_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{20eE}{m_b}=8.48\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\simeq8.64\times10^6g}.
$$
This is the acceleration at the protein's position in the rotating solution; no rotation rate can be assigned without a centrifuge radius. If the intended comparison is instead the relative separation speed of the two bands, use $\Delta m_b=600\,\mathrm{Da}$, since their displaced volumes are equal. Matching $|\Delta m_b|a_{\mathrm{eff}}=eE$ then gives $8.04\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. In that separation experiment the heavier original protein moves faster centrifugally, whereas the more highly charged mutant moves faster electrically.

= Solution

**Solvent quality.** The <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi$ measures the free-energy cost of polymer–solvent contacts relative to like contacts. In the simplest lattice model,
$$
\chi=\frac{z_c}{k_BT}\left[\epsilon_{ps}-\frac{\epsilon_{pp}+\epsilon_{ss}}2\right],
$$
where $z_c$ is the coordination number and the $\epsilon$ values are contact energies. For lattice-site volume $v_0$, the effective two-segment <excluded volume> is $v_{\mathrm{eff}}=v_0(1-2\chi)$. Its sign determines the leading tendency of a long dilute flexible chain.

For $\chi<1/2$, $v_{\mathrm{eff}}>0$: a <good solvent> favors a swollen coil because segment encounters are effectively repulsive. For $\chi=1/2$, the leading attraction and repulsion cancel, giving approximately <ideal chain> statistics. For $\chi>1/2$, effective attraction favors contraction in a <poor solvent>, eventually producing a compact <polymer globule> under sufficiently poor conditions. This does not mean that every finite-chain solution with $\chi>1/2$ is automatically phase-separated; that also depends on composition and chain length.

The condition $\chi=1/2$ is the <theta temperature> condition for that polymer–solvent pair. For an energetic model $\chi=A/T$, it occurs at $T=\Theta=2A$. More generally, if $\chi=A/T+B$, it occurs when $A/\Theta+B=1/2$. It is not the absence of all intermolecular interactions and does not require the polymer and solvent to be chemically identical.

**Chain dimensions.** Interpret $R$ as the root-mean-square <end-to-end distance of a polymer chain>. Let $a$ denote the supplied statistical step length, calibrated to the same count $N$ as the <degree of polymerization>. At the theta condition, random-walk statistics give
$$
\boxed{R_\theta=aN^{1/2}}.
$$
For a <good solvent>, the <Flory approximation> balances entropic stretching and two-segment interaction:
$$
\frac{F(R)}{k_BT}\sim\frac{R^2}{Na^2}+\frac{v_{\mathrm{eff}}N^2}{R^3}.
$$
Minimization gives $R^5\sim v_{\mathrm{eff}}a^2N^3$, hence
$$
\boxed{R_{\mathrm{good}}\sim a\left(\frac{v_{\mathrm{eff}}}{a^3}\right)^{1/5}N^{3/5}}.
$$
Thus the familiar good-solvent scaling is $R\sim aN^{3/5}$ when its nonuniversal solvent-quality amplitude is suppressed. With the additional lattice estimate $v_0\simeq a^3$, the more explicit form is $R\sim a(1-2\chi)^{1/5}N^{3/5}$. This swollen-coil estimate is used away from the narrow theta crossover; it must not be extrapolated to predict $R=0$ as $\chi\uparrow1/2$. The crossover occurs when $(1-2\chi)N^{1/2}$ is of order one. Nearer theta, the ideal-coil size supplies the appropriate limit.

**Numerical sizes.** The theta value is
$$
\boxed{R_\theta=(0.67\,\mathrm{nm})\sqrt{1500}=25.9\,\mathrm{nm}}.
$$
For $\chi=0.3$, the plain good-solvent scaling gives
$$
\boxed{R_{\mathrm{good}}\sim(0.67\,\mathrm{nm})(1500)^{3/5}=53.9\,\mathrm{nm}}.
$$
If the solvent-quality amplitude is retained with $v_0=a^3$, it instead gives
$$
\boxed{R_{\chi=0.3}\sim(0.67\,\mathrm{nm})(0.4)^{1/5}(1500)^{3/5}=44.9\,\mathrm{nm}}.
$$
The distinction is an explicitly stated modeling convention: the question does not give a microscopic site volume or an absolute swollen-coil prefactor. Both estimates describe a coil larger than the theta coil. Here $(1-2\chi)\sqrt N\simeq15.5$, so a swollen-coil estimate is reasonable. Neither result is an exact microscopic prediction from the supplied data alone.

**Overlap concentration.** The <polymer overlap concentration> corresponds to one chain per coil volume. Using the scaling convention $V_{\mathrm{coil}}\sim R_\theta^3$ gives
$$
n_p^*\sim R_\theta^{-3}=5.72\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
n_m^*\sim\frac{N}{R_\theta^3}=8.58\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
The first is chain <number density>; the second counts <repeat units>. Thus the repeat-unit <molar concentration> is approximately $0.143\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. If the lattice-site volume is taken as $v_0=a^3$, this is equivalently
$$
\boxed{\phi^*\sim\frac{Nv_0}{R_\theta^3}\simeq N^{-1/2}=2.58\times10^{-2}}.
$$
For a mass-concentration estimate, a <polystyrene> repeat unit $\mathrm{C_8H_8}$ has <molar mass> about $104.15\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, so this scaling convention gives
$$
\boxed{c^*\sim14.8\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}}.
$$
There is no sharp coil surface fixing an exact numerical prefactor. For example, using the often adopted geometric definition $V_{\mathrm{coil}}=4\pi R_g^3/3$, with the <ideal chain> <radius of gyration> $R_g=R_\theta/\sqrt6=10.6\,\mathrm{nm}$, gives $c^*\simeq52\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$. The invariant content is one chain per coil volume and the theta scaling $\phi^*\propto N^{-1/2}$; the radius and coil-volume convention must accompany a numerical value.

**Osmotic pressure.** In the given lattice expression, $\phi$ is a dimensionless polymer <volume fraction>, not a dimensional <number density>, and $f$ is the mixing <free energy> per lattice site. Introduce $v_0$ to obtain a physical <osmotic pressure>. If $M=N_s+NN_p$ is the number of sites and $\phi=NN_p/M$, differentiation of $F_{\mathrm{mix}}=Mf(\phi)$ with respect to the solvent count at fixed chain count gives
$$
\Delta\mu_s=f-\phi f'.
$$
Balancing this change in solvent <chemical potential> against the pressure contribution $v_0\Pi$ gives $\Pi=(\phi f'-f)/v_0$. For composition-independent $\chi$,
$$
\frac{f'}{k_BT}=\frac{\ln\phi+1}{N}-\ln(1-\phi)-1+\chi(1-2\phi),
$$
and therefore the <osmotic pressure in Flory-Huggins solution theory> is
$$
\boxed{\Pi=\frac{k_BT}{v_0}\left[\frac\phi N-\ln(1-\phi)-\phi-\chi\phi^2\right]}.
$$
Expanding the <logarithm> as $\phi\to0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Pi=\frac{k_BT}{v_0}\left[\frac\phi N+\left(\frac12-\chi\right)\phi^2+\frac{\phi^3}{3}+O(\phi^4)\right]}.
$$
The first term is $n_pk_BT$ with $n_p=\phi/(Nv_0)$ now denoting chain <number density>: a whole chain supplies one translational contribution. The sign of the quadratic correction reproduces the solvent-quality classification. It is positive for a <good solvent>, zero at the <theta temperature>, and negative for a <poor solvent>. This is the same two-segment interaction that swells or contracts a single coil. At theta, a positive third-order contribution remains; the chain is approximately ideal in its large-scale conformation, not a gas of segments with no interactions at all.

**Stabilization and screening.** <Charge stabilization of colloids> means suppressing aggregation through repulsion between similarly charged particles and their <electric double layers>. In <DLVO theory>, this repulsion competes with attractive <van der Waals forces>. A substantial repulsive barrier compared with thermal energy $k_BT$ can make aggregation slow, even when the dispersed state is only metastable.

The <Debye–Hückel screening length> $\lambda_D=\kappa^{-1}$ is the characteristic range over which a weak electric potential is attenuated by rearrangement of mobile <ions>. It measures the thickness of a diffuse screening cloud, not a molecular diameter. For a plane, a weak potential decays as $e^{-\kappa x}$ into the bulk.

**Derivation and the source convention.** Put a planar interface at $x=0$, with electrolyte in $x>0$, bulk potential zero, and absolute <permittivity> $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$. For a symmetric $z:z$ electrolyte, let $n_\infty$ be the bulk <number density> of each ionic species. The <Boltzmann distribution> gives
$$
n_\pm(x)=n_\infty\exp\left(\mp\frac{ze\psi(x)}{k_BT}\right),
$$
so the mobile <charge density> is
$$
\rho_e=ze(n_+-n_-)=-2zen_\infty\sinh\left(\frac{ze\psi}{k_BT}\right).
$$
Substitution into the SI <Poisson equation>, $\nabla^2\psi=-\rho_e/\varepsilon$, gives the <Poisson-Boltzmann equation>
$$
\nabla^2\psi=\frac{2zen_\infty}{\varepsilon}\sinh\left(\frac{ze\psi}{k_BT}\right).
$$
For $|ze\psi|\ll k_BT$, linearize the <hyperbolic sine>. The <Debye–Hückel approximation> is then
$$
\nabla^2\psi=\kappa^2\psi,\qquad
\boxed{\kappa^2=\frac{2z^2e^2n_\infty}{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rk_BT}\quad\text{(SI)}}.
$$
Here $z$ is the ionic valence magnitude, $e$ the elementary <electric charge>, $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>, $T$ absolute <temperature>, $\varepsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>, and $\varepsilon_r$ the solvent's <relative permittivity>. For several ionic species, the numerator $2z^2e^2n_\infty$ is replaced by $e^2\sum_i z_i^2n_{i,\infty}$.

The original PDF includes an additional $4\pi$ in the denominator. That factor does not follow from SI <Poisson equation> with number densities and the stated permittivities. The <Bjerrum length> provides a useful check: $\ell_B=e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon k_BT)$ and the consistent result is $\kappa^2=8\pi\ell_Bz^2n_\infty$. A Gaussian-unit convention moves factors of $4\pi$ together with the charge and permittivity conventions; it does not justify inserting this extra factor while retaining all the given SI quantities. Both numerical interpretations are reported below rather than silently changing the source.

**Conditions favoring stability.** Increasing the surface-potential or surface-charge magnitude usually strengthens the electrostatic barrier. Lower <ionic strength> increases $\lambda_D$ and generally makes repulsion longer ranged; added salt and especially multivalent <counterions> shorten it and can promote aggregation. Suitable <pH> can keep an ionizable surface charged, while conditions near an isoelectric state weaken charge repulsion. The solvent-dependent <van der Waals forces>, charge regulation and particle geometry also affect the barrier, so a long screening length is not by itself sufficient. At fixed density and valence, the linear formula gives $\lambda_D\propto\sqrt{\varepsilon T}$, but actual temperature changes can also alter the permittivity and surface chemistry.

**Surface charge at small potential.** Take the usual one-sided planar-interface model, with negligible electric displacement on the nonaqueous side. The solution decaying into the solution is
$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=\psi_0e^{-\kappa x}}.
$$
The <electric field> is $E_x=-\psi'=\kappa\psi_0e^{-\kappa x}$. The <electrostatic interface boundary conditions> then give the <surface charge density>
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\varepsilon E_x(0^+)=\varepsilon\kappa\psi_0}.
$$
One can also derive it by integrating the diffuse <charge density>:
$$
\int_0^\infty\rho_e(x)\,dx=-\varepsilon\kappa^2\psi_0\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa x}\,dx
=-\varepsilon\kappa\psi_0=-\sigma.
$$
Thus the diffuse layer neutralizes the surface charge. This is the <planar Debye-Hückel double layer>. If instead the object is an isolated sheet wetted by identical electrolyte on both sides, each side screens half its total charge and $\sigma_{\mathrm{sheet}}=2\varepsilon\kappa\psi_0$. The one-sided assumption is the conventional local model for the exterior of a large charged colloid; it must be stated because the question does not specify the material on the other side.

**Numerical ion density.** For $T=298.15\,\mathrm K$, $z=1$, $\kappa=10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $\varepsilon=80\varepsilon_0=7.0834\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, the consistent SI result is
$$
\boxed{n_\infty=\frac{\varepsilon k_BT\kappa^2}{2e^2}=5.68\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}}.
$$
This is $\boxed{0.0943\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}$ for each ion species, and hence also the salt concentration for a monovalent $1:1$ salt. The combined number density of both signs is $2n_\infty$.

Literal substitution into the formula printed in the PDF instead gives
$$
\boxed{n_{\infty,\mathrm{printed}}=4\pi n_{\infty,\mathrm{SI}}=7.14\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\simeq1.19\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}.
$$
These values are distinct consequences of the source's extra factor; they cannot both describe the same physical screening length in SI with the same definition of $n_\infty$.

**Surface potential and validity.** The requested linear estimate in the one-sided model is
$$
\boxed{\psi_{0,\mathrm{linear}}=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon\kappa}=\frac{0.25}{(7.0834\times10^{-10})(10^9)}=0.353\,\mathrm V}.
$$
However, the <thermal voltage> is $k_BT/e=25.7\,\mathrm{mV}$, so $e\psi_{0,\mathrm{linear}}/(k_BT)\simeq13.7$. Thus **the small-potential approximation is not self-consistent for the supplied surface charge**. The boxed value is its formal extrapolation, not a reliable prediction of the actual surface potential.

For a valid nonlinear comparison within the ideal one-sided planar <Poisson-Boltzmann equation>, multiply the differential equation by $\psi'$ and use zero potential and field in the bulk. This gives
$$
(\psi')^2=\frac{8n_\infty k_BT}{\varepsilon}\sinh^2\left(\frac{e\psi}{2k_BT}\right).
$$
Using the surface boundary condition yields the <Grahame equation> and its inverse:
$$
\sigma=\frac{2\varepsilon\kappa k_BT}{e}\sinh\left(\frac{e\psi_0}{2k_BT}\right),\qquad
\psi_0=\frac{2k_BT}{e}\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\frac{e\sigma}{2\varepsilon\kappa k_BT}\right).
$$
With the SI screening length, the latter gives $\boxed{\psi_{0,\mathrm{PB}}\simeq0.135\,\mathrm V}$. This is a nonlinear mean-field estimate under explicit assumptions, not a unique experimentally determined potential: compact interfacial layers, finite ion size and surface chemistry were not supplied. The source's normalization problem and its use of a linear formula far outside the small-potential regime are separate issues.
