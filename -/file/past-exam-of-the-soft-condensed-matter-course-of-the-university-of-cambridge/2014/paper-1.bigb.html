<Young's equation> gives $\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta$. The <interfacial-energy difference from two contact angles> follows by subtracting the two equilibrium contact-angle equations, which eliminates the common solid–air energy:
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{S,\mathrm{water}}-\gamma_{S,\mathrm{decane}}
=\gamma_{\mathrm{decane},V}\cos25^\circ-\gamma_{\mathrm{water},V}\cos102^\circ
\simeq3.67\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}.}
$$
The water–solid interface has the larger energy. This assumes the same clean solid surface and equilibrium angles, without swelling or strong <contact-angle hysteresis>.

Use a <freely rotating chain>: fixed bond length $b$, fixed angle $\vartheta$ between forward bond vectors, and independently random torsion angles. Then $\langle\hat t_0\cdot\hat t_n\rangle=(\cos\vartheta)^n=e^{-nb/\ell_p}$, giving the <orientational persistence length of a freely rotating chain>
$$
\boxed{\ell_p=-\frac b{\ln\cos\vartheta}\simeq3.48\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The equivalent long-chain <Kuhn length> divided by two, $b(1+\cos\vartheta)/[2(1-\cos\vartheta)]\simeq3.48\,\mathrm{nm}$, is nearly equal here but is a distinct discrete-chain convention. A chemical internal bond angle is supplementary to the forward-vector angle used above.

Synthetic examples are relatively stiff <poly(p-phenylene terephthalamide)> and flexible <polydimethylsiloxane>. Light scattering and viscometry established rodlike solution conformations for the former; single-chain mechanical measurements support the latter's flexibility. Persistence is solvent- and model-dependent, so no universal numerical value is inferred for either example.<Properties of some rodlike polymers in solution by C.-P. Wong and others>[publisher abstract, dilute-solution light scattering and viscometry]{ref}<Understanding the Extraordinary Flexibility of Polydimethylsiloxane through Single-Molecule Mechanics by Song Lu and others>[abstract, single-chain atomic-force measurements and flexibility]{ref}

A <Couette rheometer> measures the <torque> required to rotate one coaxial cylinder relative to another. With inner radius $R_i$, outer radius $R_o$, wetted length $L$, inner <angular velocity> $\Omega$ and stationary outer wall, laminar Newtonian flow has
$$
v_\theta(r)=\frac{\Omega R_i^2}{R_o^2-R_i^2}\left(\frac{R_o^2}{r}-r\right),\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal T=\frac{4\pi\eta L\Omega R_i^2R_o^2}{R_o^2-R_i^2}.}
$$
The <shear stress> at the inner wall is $\tau_i=\mathcal T/(2\pi LR_i^2)$. In a narrow gap $d=R_o-R_i\ll R_i$, $\dot\gamma\simeq\Omega R_i/d$, so $\eta\simeq\mathcal T d/(2\pi L\Omega R_i^3)$.

Measuring <torque> over a range of speeds gives a flow curve: constant <viscosity> identifies a Newtonian regime; decreasing or increasing apparent <viscosity> indicates <shear thinning> or thickening. A yield-stress fit needs a genuine low-rate regime and must not be confused with bearing drag or <wall slip>. Small oscillatory strain also probes <viscoelasticity>, with $\tau=\gamma_0[G'\cos\omega t-G''\sin\omega t]$ for imposed $\gamma=\gamma_0\cos\omega t$.

Control <temperature>, calibrate instrument <torque> and end effects, and check gap dependence or roughened surfaces for slip. Keep inertia small enough to avoid Taylor vortices. The exact Newtonian profile is a calibration model; a non-Newtonian material needs its own stress–rate relation.

An <amphiphile> contains a solvent-compatible head and a solvent-avoiding tail. In water, aggregation hides hydrocarbon tails while leaving heads exposed. This reduces the free-energy cost of tail–water contact, competing with loss of <translational entropy>, headgroup repulsion and chain packing. A <micelle> is a finite equilibrium aggregate, not necessarily a permanently bound particle.

Equality of molecular <chemical potentials> controls aggregation. For a large preferred aggregation number, $\mu_1=\mu_1^0+k_BT\ln x_1$ approaches an aggregate value $\mu_*^0$, giving
$$
\boxed{x_{\rm CMC}\simeq\exp[(\mu_*^0-\mu_1^0)/(k_BT)].}
$$
Above the <critical micelle concentration>, added <surfactant> mainly increases aggregate population while <monomer> activity changes relatively little. Finite aggregation numbers give a smooth crossover rather than an exact bulk phase transition. The <amphiphile packing parameter> $p=v/(a_0l_c)$ compares tail volume, preferred head area and chain length; small $p$ favours spherical <micelles>, while larger values favour less curved aggregates. These geometric criteria are approximate, not universal shape phase boundaries.<Theory of self-assembly of hydrocarbon amphiphiles by Jacob N. Israelachvili and others>[1976, packing/free-energy model of generic hydrocarbon surfactant aggregates]{ref}

Surface-tension or conductivity changes with <concentration> locate an operational CMC; scattering probes size and aggregation number. <temperature>, impurities and salt must be controlled, and independent methods help distinguish aggregation from precipitation. In an ionic <surfactant>, salt screens headgroup repulsion and can change both the CMC and aggregate shape. This discussion concerns generic synthetic <surfactants> only.

Interpret the stated <free energy> as per lattice site of volume $v_0$. <Flory–Huggins solution theory> counts <polymer> chains, giving the <entropy> term $(\varphi/N)\ln\varphi$, and <solvent> molecules, giving $(1-\varphi)\ln(1-\varphi)$. The interaction term penalizes unlike contacts when $\chi>0$; a simple contact model has $\chi=z[\epsilon_{ps}-(\epsilon_{pp}+\epsilon_{ss})/2]/(k_BT)$. Connectivity reduces <polymer> <translational entropy> relative to independent <monomers>. This is an incompressible mean-field model with composition-independent $\chi$ for the calculations below.<Thermodynamics of High Polymer Solutions by Paul J. Flory>[1942, connected-chain combinatorial entropy and mixing free energy]{ref}

Expanding the dilute <osmotic pressure> identifies the effective second virial coefficient. A <good solvent> has $\chi<1/2$ and positive <excluded volume>, promoting swollen chains. A <poor solvent> has $\chi>1/2$, favouring contraction and potentially phase separation. The theta-solvent condition is $\chi=1/2$ in this long-chain mean-field description: the second virial term vanishes, but higher terms remain. For finite $N$, this condition is not the exact critical point, which instead has $\chi_c=(1+N^{-1/2})^2/2$.

Let $g=f_{\rm mix}/(k_BT)$. The <osmotic pressure in Flory-Huggins solution theory> is
$$
\boxed{\Pi=\frac{k_BT}{v_0}(\varphi g'-g)
=-\frac{k_BT}{v_0}\left[\ln(1-\varphi)+(1-1/N)\varphi+\chi\varphi^2\right].}
$$
Thus $\Pi=(k_BT/v_0)[\varphi/N+(1/2-\chi)\varphi^2+\varphi^3/3+\cdots]$. For <mass> <concentration> $c=\rho_p\varphi$ and chain molar <mass> $M_n=N\rho_pv_0N_A$,
$$
\frac\Pi c=\frac{RT}{M_n}+B c+O(c^2).
$$
<osmometry> uses a membrane permeable to <solvent> but retaining every <polymer> chain, at known uniform <temperature>. Extrapolate $\Pi/c$ to zero <concentration> to remove interactions; the intercept gives the <number-average molar mass>. Aggregation, leakage of small chains, <ions> with a Donnan contribution, or a poor-solvent two-phase sample invalidate the simple molecular-weight interpretation.

The measured ratios give an exactly linear reduced-pressure fit:
$$
\boxed{\Pi/c=185+750c\quad\text{in the stated SI concentration units},\qquad
M_n=\frac{RT}{185}.}
$$
The <reduced osmotic-pressure intercept> is $185\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^3\,kg^{-1}}$. The data determine **$M_n/T=0.04494\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$**; the measurement <temperature> is needed to convert this into an absolute <molar mass>. For an explicitly assumed room <temperature> $T=300\,\mathrm K$, **$M_n\simeq13.5\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}=1.35\times10^4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$**. The boxed expression is the general answer for an arbitrary experimental <temperature>.

Balancing buoyant weight with <Stokes drag> gives
$$
\frac{4\pi r^3}{3}\Delta\rho g=6\pi\eta rU_s,\qquad
\boxed{U_s=\frac{2\Delta\rho gr^2}{9\eta}.}
$$
This needs isolated spherical particles, low <Reynolds number> and negligible wall corrections. The supplied settling measurement gives $\Delta\rho\simeq99.1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, hence **$\rho_p\simeq1.10\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$**. Its <Reynolds number> is about $7\times10^{-5}$, consistent with the approximation.

Compare gravitational work over one radius with <thermal energy>, or use $\mathrm{Pe}_g=U_sr/D$ with $D=k_BT/(6\pi\eta r)$. The <gravitational Péclet number> gives
$$
\mathrm{Pe}_g=\frac{4\pi\Delta\rho gr^4}{3k_BT},\qquad
\boxed{r_c\sim\left(\frac{3k_BT}{4\pi\Delta\rho g}\right)^{1/4}\simeq1.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
Below this <sedimentation-Brownian crossover radius> <Brownian motion> dominates settling on the particle-size scale. It does not eliminate gravity: the <sedimentation scale height> is $\ell_g=k_BT/[(4\pi r^3/3)\Delta\rho g]$. For negligible <concentration> gradients across a sample height $H$, the stronger condition is $\ell_g\gg H$. No universal permanently nonsedimenting size follows without a length/time criterion.

Brownian encounters bring small particles into contact; van der Waals attraction can then bind them. Surface charge can create a repulsive barrier through <Debye screening of colloid repulsion>. For a monovalent salt reservoir with <number density> $n$ of each ionic species, Poisson–Boltzmann theory has
$$
n_\pm=n e^{\mp e\psi/(k_BT)},\qquad
\nabla^2\psi=\frac{2en}{\epsilon}\sinh\frac{e\psi}{k_BT}.
$$
For $|e\psi|\ll k_BT$, linearization gives $\nabla^2\psi=\kappa^2\psi$, with **$\kappa^{-1}=\sqrt{\epsilon k_BT/(2ne^2)}$**. If $n$ instead means total <ion> <density>, the factor two is absent. The planar potential decays as $e^{-\kappa h}$; a sphere has an additional $1/r$ factor. This mean-field approximation neglects specific adsorption, <ion> correlations and finite <ion> size.

<DLVO theory> adds screened repulsion to dispersion attraction. At small surface separation $h$ for equal spheres of radius $r$, a useful schematic form is
$$
U(h)\sim C e^{-\kappa h}-\frac{A_Hr}{12h},
$$
with a short-range repulsive core needed outside the continuum model. Low salt can leave a barrier many times $k_BT$, giving kinetic stability. Higher salt shortens the repulsion and can lower or remove the barrier, allowing coagulation into a deep primary minimum. The <primary and secondary DLVO minima> have different consequences: a shallow secondary minimum permits looser, sometimes reversible aggregation. The surface boundary condition affects $C$; screening length alone is not a complete measured interaction law. Non-DLVO hydration or steric forces can matter at contact.
