= Solution

In the weak-field <reptation> regime of <agarose gel electrophoresis>, the <electrophoretic mobility> of a long linear <DNA> chain scales as $\mu\propto M^{-1}$, where $M$ is its number of <base pairs>. One way to see the scaling is that the constrained-chain <diffusion coefficient> scales as $D\propto M^{-2}$, whereas its effective <electric charge> scales as $M$; the drift response is proportional to $DM$. With the same <electric field> and running time, the migration distance is therefore $x=\mu Et\propto M^{-1}$. The time cancels from the ratio, giving
$$
\frac{M_2}{M_1}=\frac{x_1}{x_2},\qquad
\boxed{M_2=10\frac{4}{4.5}\,\mathrm{kbp}\simeq8.9\,\mathrm{kbp}.}
$$
Here “kbp” means a thousand <base pairs>, not a rate. The second <gel band> has traveled farther and corresponds to the shorter chain under this migration model. The inverse-length law applies only over an appropriate migration regime.https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/gold/pdfs/teaching/140911-ufk-2-corr.pdf[Electrokinetic Phenomena, gel electrophoresis slides 27–28]{ref}.

At very low <agarose> concentration the mesh becomes too open to sieve these chains effectively. In the free-solution limit the chain's <electric charge> and friction are both approximately proportional to its length, so both chains have nearly the same <electrophoretic mobility> and their <gel bands> cease to separate well. At very high <agarose> concentration the pores become small and transport is strongly hindered; over a fixed experimental time both chains migrate very little, so their absolute spatial separation again becomes small. Increasing gel concentration can improve resolution over an intermediate range, but not indefinitely.

The numerical result is conditional on the inverse-length <reptation> law. Actual <gel electrophoresis size calibration> often uses an empirical approximately linear relation between distance and $\log M$ over a specified range. One known band alone cannot fix that relation's slope. The PDF supplies neither gel concentration nor a calibration curve, so $8.9\,\mathrm{kbp}$ is the standard model estimate rather than a uniquely calibrated experimental size.

Choose a coordinate $z$ measured from the sphere centre towards increasing distance from the block, so an atom in the sphere is at height $L+z$. Summing the atom–block <potential energy> with the sphere's <number density> gives
$$
U(L)=-\frac{B\pi n^2}{6}\int_{|\boldsymbol x|<R}\frac{d^3x}{(L+z)^3}.
$$
For $R/L\ll1$, a uniformly valid <Taylor series> over the sphere is
$$
\frac1{(L+z)^3}=\frac1{L^3}\left[1-\frac{3z}{L}+\frac{6z^2}{L^2}-\frac{10z^3}{L^3}+O\!\left(\frac{z^4}{L^4}\right)\right].
$$
The odd moments vanish by reflection symmetry. The sphere volume is $V_s=4\pi R^3/3$, and its second moment is
$$
\int z^2d^3x=\pi\int_{-R}^{R}z^2(R^2-z^2)dz=\frac{4\pi R^5}{15}=\frac{V_sR^2}{5}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{U(L)=-\frac{2\pi^2Bn^2R^3}{9L^3}
\left[1+\frac65\frac{R^2}{L^2}+O\!\left(\frac{R^4}{L^4}\right)\right].}
$$
The first finite-radius correction is therefore $-4\pi^2Bn^2R^5/(15L^5)$. There is no relative $R/L$ correction because the first moment about the centre is zero. The leading term is just the number of sphere atoms times the potential at its centre; the correction increases the attraction because the nearer atoms contribute more strongly than the farther atoms lose.

This is the <far-field sphere-plane dispersion energy>, with <Hamaker constant> $A_H=\pi^2Bn^2$. It must be distinguished from the near-contact <sphere-plane dispersion force>, whose small parameter is the surface gap divided by the radius. Here $L$ is explicitly the centre-to-plane distance.

Let $z$ increase down the pipe, $a=0.015\,\mathrm m$ be its radius and $r$ the distance from its axis. For steady, fully developed <laminar flow> of a <Newtonian fluid>, the axial <Navier–Stokes equations> reduce to
$$
0=-\frac{dp}{dz}+\eta\frac1r\frac d{dr}\left(r\frac{du}{dr}\right)+\rho g\sin20^\circ.
$$
Write $K=-dp/dz+\rho g\sin20^\circ$. Integrating twice, regularity at $r=0$ excludes a logarithmic term, and the <no-slip boundary condition> $u(a)=0$ gives the <Hagen-Poiseuille flow> profile
$$
\boxed{u(r)=\frac{K}{4\eta}(a^2-r^2).}
$$
The <volumetric flow rate> follows by integrating over the circular cross-section:
$$
Q=2\pi\int_0^a u(r)r\,dr
=\frac{\pi Ka^4}{8\eta},\qquad
\overline u=\frac{Q}{\pi a^2}=\frac{Ka^2}{8\eta}=\frac{u(0)}2.
$$
For the intended gravity-driven case with no imposed axial <pressure gradient>, $dp/dz=0$. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{u(r)\simeq0.2095\left[1-\left(\frac{r}{0.015\,\mathrm m}\right)^2\right]\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{Q\simeq7.40\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}
=74.0\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}\simeq4.44\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding mean speed is about $0.1047\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and the diameter-based <Reynolds number> is $\rho\overline u(2a)/\eta\simeq3.49$, consistent with <laminar flow>. The original PDF does not specify inlet and outlet pressures. If they impose an additional <pressure gradient>, the general result with $K$ above applies; gravity alone does not determine that extra contribution.

= Solution

Use a <rheometer>, for example a <cone-and-plate rheometer> or a <parallel-plate rheometer>, to impose a small deformation and measure the required torque, or impose a torque and measure the deformation. The apparatus geometry converts these into <shear stress> and <strain>. First check that reducing the imposed amplitude does not change the normalized response; this identifies the linear regime and avoids yielding or structural rearrangement.

Three complementary protocols characterize the material's memory. In a <stress relaxation> experiment impose a small step $\epsilon_0H(t)$ and hold the deformation fixed. The output is the <relaxation modulus> $G(t)=\sigma(t)/\epsilon_0$, revealing how stored <stress> decays and whether an elastic plateau remains. In a <creep compliance> measurement impose a small <stress> step $\sigma_0H(t)$ and record $J(t)=\epsilon(t)/\sigma_0$. Removing the load also distinguishes recoverable deformation from permanent flow.

In <small-amplitude oscillatory shear>, impose sinusoidal <strain> over a range of frequencies and measure both <stress> amplitude and phase. With $\epsilon=\epsilon_0\cos\omega t$, fit
$$
\sigma(t)=\epsilon_0[G'(\omega)\cos\omega t-G''(\omega)\sin\omega t].
$$
The outputs are the <storage modulus> $G'$ and <loss modulus> $G''$, or equivalently amplitude ratio and <loss tangent> $\tan\delta=G''/G'$. These measure elastic storage and dissipative response, respectively. The step experiments give time-domain information and the oscillatory experiment gives the corresponding frequency-domain response. All comparisons must use the same deformation convention and controlled <temperature>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <dynamic modulus>, or <complex viscoelastic modulus>, is the ratio of complex <stress> amplitude to complex <strain> amplitude in the periodic steady response:
$$
\sigma(t)=\Re[\widehat\sigma e^{i\omega t}],\qquad
\epsilon(t)=\Re[\widehat\epsilon e^{i\omega t}],\qquad
\boxed{G^*(\omega)=\frac{\widehat\sigma}{\widehat\epsilon}=G'+iG''.}
$$
This defines the sign convention; for passive response the <loss modulus> is nonnegative at positive frequency. A <Newtonian fluid> obeys $\sigma=\eta\dot\epsilon$, while a <Hooke's law> solid obeys $\sigma=G_0\epsilon$. Hence
$$
\boxed{G^*_{\mathrm{Newtonian}}=i\omega\eta,\qquad
G^*_{\mathrm{Hookean}}=G_0.}
$$
The first is purely dissipative and the second purely elastic. Here $\epsilon$ denotes the scalar deformation used in the problem, with any shear-versus-extension geometric factors already included in its modulus and viscosity.

= Solution

Parallel elements have the same <strain>, while their <stresses> add. The spring supplies $G_0\epsilon$ and the viscous element supplies $\eta_0\dot\epsilon$, so the <Kelvin-Voigt model> has <constitutive equation>
$$
\boxed{\sigma(t)=G_0\epsilon(t)+\eta_0\dot\epsilon(t).}
$$
For the oscillatory deformation this gives
$$
\sigma(t)=(G_0+i\omega\eta_0)\epsilon_0e^{i\omega t},\qquad
\boxed{G^*(\omega)=G_0+i\omega\eta_0.}
$$
Physical <stress> and <strain> are the real parts of these expressions. Thus the <storage modulus> is $G_0$, independent of frequency, and the <loss modulus> is $\omega\eta_0$. The equation is the quasistatic material law; an inertial equation for an attached mass would require additional mechanical data.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For elements in series the <stress> is common and the <strains> add. If $\widehat\epsilon_K$ and $\widehat\epsilon_1$ are the Kelvin element and added spring amplitudes, then
$$
\widehat\epsilon=\widehat\epsilon_K+\widehat\epsilon_1
=\frac{\widehat\sigma}{G_0+i\omega\eta_0}+\frac{\widehat\sigma}{G_1}.
$$
The compliances therefore add, yielding
$$
\boxed{G^*(\omega)=\left[\frac1{G_0+i\omega\eta_0}+\frac1{G_1}\right]^{-1}
=\frac{G_1(G_0+i\omega\eta_0)}{G_0+G_1+i\omega\eta_0}.}
$$
This is a <standard linear solid model>. Define
$$
G_L=\frac{G_0G_1}{G_0+G_1},\qquad G_H=G_1,\qquad
\Delta G=G_H-G_L=\frac{G_1^2}{G_0+G_1},\qquad
\tau=\frac{\eta_0}{G_0+G_1}.
$$
Writing $x=\omega\tau$, its <storage modulus> and <loss modulus> are
$$
\boxed{G'=G_L+\Delta G\frac{x^2}{1+x^2},\qquad
G''=\Delta G\frac{x}{1+x^2}.}
$$
Thus $G'$ increases from the low-frequency plateau $G_L$ to the high-frequency plateau $G_H$. The <loss modulus> has low-frequency slope $+1$ and high-frequency slope $-1$ on a <log-log plot>, with maximum $\Delta G/2$ at $\omega=1/\tau$. At that frequency $G'=(G_L+G_H)/2$. There need not be a crossing of the two curves for arbitrary spring constants.

= Solution
{parent=e}

A <Kelvin-Voigt model> has no finite instantaneous elastic compliance: the viscous element prevents a sudden deformation under a finite <stress> step. Its <creep compliance> is
$$
J_K(t)=\frac1{G_0}\left(1-e^{-G_0t/\eta_0}\right),\qquad J_K(0^+)=0.
$$
Conversely an ideal <strain> step requires an impulsive <stress>,
$$
\sigma(t)=\epsilon_0[G_0H(t)+\eta_0\delta(t)].
$$
After the impulse the held-strain response is simply constant. There is no finite stress-decay interval, and the <loss modulus> grows without bound as $\omega\to\infty$. This fails to represent a material with both a finite instantaneous stiffness and delayed <stress relaxation>.

The added series spring resolves this particular defect. At the instant of loading the Kelvin element cannot move but the added spring can. The <standard linear solid model> consequently has
$$
\boxed{G(t)=G_L+(G_H-G_L)e^{-t/\tau},\qquad t\geq0,}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{J(t)=\frac1{G_1}+\frac1{G_0}\left(1-e^{-G_0t/\eta_0}\right).}
$$
Its instantaneous stiffness is $G_H=G_1$, its long-time stiffness is $G_L$, and it exhibits genuine <stress relaxation> as well as delayed creep. The creep retardation time $\eta_0/G_0$ differs from the stress-relaxation time $\tau=\eta_0/(G_0+G_1)$. These properties also follow directly from its constitutive equation,
$$
\eta_0\dot\sigma+(G_0+G_1)\sigma=G_1\eta_0\dot\epsilon+G_0G_1\epsilon.
$$
It remains an idealized solid with one relaxation mode: it cannot describe unrestricted long-time viscous flow, nonlinear response, or a broad spectrum of relaxation times.

A <hydrophobic> surface is poorly wetted by <water>: on a smooth surface its equilibrium <contact angle>, measured through the liquid, exceeds $90^\circ$. A drop therefore tends to bead up, limiting its contact area. Hydrophobicity does not mean that the surface exerts a mysterious repulsive force on every water <molecule>.

The waxy coating has a largely nonpolar exterior. <Water> <molecules> form favorable <hydrogen bonds> with one another, whereas a hydrocarbon-rich wax surface offers few suitable <hydrogen bond donors> or <hydrogen bond acceptors>. Water–surface adhesion is weak relative to water's cohesion. The drop shape minimizes the combined solid–air, solid–water and water–air interfacial <free energies>, not merely the liquid's exposed area.

In terms of <Young's equation>, $\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta$. A <contact angle> greater than $90^\circ$ corresponds to $\gamma_{SL}>\gamma_{SV}$: replacing dry surface by wetted surface has an interfacial cost. The balancing liquid interface then favors a small wetted patch. Microscopic leaf texture can amplify the apparent <hydrophobicity>, especially when it traps air under the drop as a <Cassie–Baxter state>; the chemistry of a smooth wax layer alone does not guarantee an extremely large apparent angle.

On <Teflon>, the <water> drop has an obtuse <contact angle> and beads up, whereas the oil drop has an acute <contact angle> and spreads into a shallower patch. For small drops with negligible <Bond number>, both can be drawn as <spherical caps>.

To compare shape at equal volume $V$, let $R$ denote the generating sphere radius. The cap height and footprint radius are
$$
h=R(1-\cos\theta),\qquad s=R\sin\theta,\qquad
V=\frac\pi3 R^3(2-3\cos\theta+\cos^3\theta).
$$
For a fixed $V$, these relations give a taller, narrower drop for the larger <contact angle>. The angles are measured inside the liquid, including the obtuse angle of the water drop.

For an ideal smooth homogeneous substrate in <thermodynamic equilibrium>, <Young's equation> is
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{SA}=\gamma_{SL}+\gamma_{LA}\cos\theta,}
$$
where $A$ denotes air, $S$ the solid, and $L$ the chosen liquid. It follows either by balancing tangential interfacial forces at the <contact line> or by varying the wetted area at fixed drop volume.

The dry Teflon–air <surface energy> is the same in the two measurements, so subtracting the two equations eliminates it:
$$
\gamma_{SW}-\gamma_{SO}
=\gamma_{OA}\cos\theta_O-\gamma_{WA}\cos\theta_W.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{SW}-\gamma_{SO}
=23.8\cos25^\circ-72.8\cos102^\circ
\simeq36.7\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}=36.7\,\mathrm{mJ\,m^{-2}}.}
$$
The water–Teflon interface has the higher interfacial <free energy> per unit area. Neither individual solid–liquid <surface energy> is determined without the solid–air value; the difference is determined.

<Colloidal> particles experience attractive <van der Waals forces>, arising from correlated molecular charge fluctuations and other dipolar interactions. Integrating many weak molecular attractions over two particles can give a deep near-contact well. For identical spheres of radius $a$ and surface gap $h\ll a$, a positive <Hamaker constant> gives approximately
$$
U_{\mathrm{vdW}}(h)\simeq-\frac{A_Ha}{12h}.
$$
<Brownian motion> brings particles into encounters. Without a repulsive barrier, they can reach the attractive primary minimum; if the binding <energy> is much larger than $k_BT$, thermal motion rarely separates them again. Repeated sticking produces aggregates. This is a kinetic stability statement: thermal agitation causes encounters but does not itself supply a stabilizing interparticle repulsion. The molecular cutoff and <hard sphere> exclusion prevent the unphysical $h\to0$ divergence of the continuum attraction.

= Solution
{parent=e}

One method is to give every particle a sufficiently large <surface charge density> of the same sign. For example, attach ionizable sulfonate groups covalently to the surface. Dissociation leaves negatively charged surface groups and releases mobile positive <counterions> into the water. A <surfactant> with a strongly bound head group can provide another charging route, provided it does not desorb during the experiment.

Each charged particle is surrounded by an <electric double layer>. As two such particles approach, overlap of these ionic clouds raises the electrostatic and ionic <free energy>, producing repulsion. In a dilute electrolyte, the range is set by the <Debye–Hückel screening length>,
$$
\lambda_D=\kappa^{-1},\qquad
\kappa^2=\frac{e^2}{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r k_BT}\sum_i z_i^2 n_i.
$$
For identical weakly charged spheres a representative <screened Coulomb pair potential> is
$$
U_{\mathrm{el}}(R)\simeq\frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r(1+\kappa a)^2R}
\,e^{-\kappa(R-2a)},\qquad R\geq2a.
$$
Its sum with the <van der Waals force> attraction is the <DLVO theory> pair potential. Choose the charging level so that the repulsive barrier is many $k_BT$ high; particles then rarely reach the primary attractive well during the observation time. This is kinetic stabilization rather than removal of all attraction.

Deionized water is useful because low added <ionic strength> gives a long-ranged repulsion. It does not mean that the charged dispersion contains no <ions>: the surface's own <counterions> are unavoidable and contribute to screening. Surface dissociation, contamination and particle concentration therefore matter even without added salt. An alternative valid route is a dense, firmly attached layer of water-soluble <polyethylene glycol>, producing <steric stabilization> without relying on a large electrostatic barrier.

Without stabilization, the <potential energy> is predominantly attractive outside the molecular core and drops into a deep primary minimum. With the charging treatment, the <DLVO theory> sum develops a positive barrier between distant particles and that primary minimum. The barrier must be large compared with $k_BT$ to inhibit aggregation on the experimental timescale. A shallow secondary minimum can remain at larger separation, depending on parameters.

The <Flory–Huggins solution theory> treats a <polymer solution> as an incompressible lattice mixture, with one <solvent> <molecule> occupying one site and one chain occupying $N$ connected sites. Thus the supplied $f$ is naturally the mixing <free energy> per lattice site; if a site has volume $v_0$, the free-energy density is $f/v_0$.

The term $k_BT(\phi/N)\ln\phi$ is the <polymer> translational <entropy of mixing> contribution. There are only $1/N$ as many independently translating chains as <monomers>, which explains the factor $1/N$. The term $k_BT(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)$ is the <solvent> <entropy of mixing> contribution. Both lower the mixing <free energy> for $0<\phi<1$.

The term $k_BT\chi\phi(1-\phi)$ is the mean-field excess interaction contribution from replacing like contacts with <polymer>–<solvent> contacts. In the simplest energetic lattice model,
$$
\chi=\frac{z}{k_BT}\left[\epsilon_{ps}-\frac{\epsilon_{pp}+\epsilon_{ss}}2\right],
$$
where $z$ counts neighboring contacts and the $\epsilon_{ij}$ are pair contact energies. Positive $\chi$ penalizes unlike contacts and can favor demixing. Because chain connectivity suppresses the <polymer>'s translational mixing <entropy>, relatively modest unfavorable interactions can cause separation of long chains. More general effective $\chi$ can also include entropic effects.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Local compositional stability requires positive <curvature> of the mixing <free energy>. Differentiating twice at fixed <temperature>, with $\chi$ independent of composition as in the given model, gives
$$
\frac1{k_BT}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\phi^2}
=\frac1{N\phi}+\frac1{1-\phi}-2\chi.
$$
The <spinodal> occurs where this vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\chi_s(\phi)=\frac12\left[\frac1{N\phi}+\frac1{1-\phi}\right].}
$$
Between the two spinodal compositions at sufficiently large $\chi$, $f''<0$ and a homogeneous mixture is unstable to infinitesimal composition fluctuations. The <binodal> is instead fixed by the <common-tangent construction>, and encloses a larger coexistence region. Between binodal and spinodal the mixture is locally stable but <metastable>.

A <phase diagram> with a conventional upper critical <temperature> additionally assumes that $\chi$ decreases with $T$, for example $\chi=C/T$ with $C>0$. Then high <temperature> gives a uniform mixture; cooling produces an asymmetric coexistence dome whose critical composition is on the dilute-polymer side. The spinodal lies inside the binodal dome. An arbitrary $\chi(T)$ need not give this orientation, so the free-energy formula alone does not specify a unique <temperature> axis.

= Solution
{parent=c}

At the <thermodynamic critical point>, the two spinodal branches merge: $f''=0$ and $f^{(3)}=0$. The third <derivative> is
$$
\frac{f^{(3)}}{k_BT}=-\frac1{N\phi^2}+\frac1{(1-\phi)^2}.
$$
For $0<\phi<1$, its zero gives $1-\phi_c=\sqrt N\,\phi_c$. Substitution in the spinodal relation yields
$$
\boxed{\phi_c=\frac1{1+\sqrt N}\sim N^{-1/2},\qquad
\chi_c=\frac12\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt N}\right)^2
=\frac12+N^{-1/2}+\frac1{2N}.}
$$
An absolute <critical temperature> requires an interaction–<temperature> relation. Under the usual energetic approximation $\chi=C/T$, define the <theta temperature> $\Theta=2C$, at which an infinite chain has $\chi=1/2$. Then
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac{\Theta}{(1+N^{-1/2})^2}
=\Theta\left[1-2N^{-1/2}+3N^{-1}+O(N^{-3/2})\right].}
$$
Thus $T_c$ tends to a nonzero constant, $T_c\sim N^0$, while $\Theta-T_c\sim N^{-1/2}$. It is the <temperature> deficit, not $T_c$ itself, that scales as $N^{-1/2}$.

More generally, if a smooth $\chi(T)$ obeys $\chi(\Theta)=1/2$ and $\chi'(\Theta)\ne0$, then
$$
T_c-\Theta=\frac{N^{-1/2}}{\chi'(\Theta)}+O(N^{-1}).
$$
This is the <critical point of a Flory–Huggins polymer solution>. The same leading chain-length exponent follows for a smooth <temperature> law, while its coefficient depends on the omitted <solvent> thermodynamics. The lattice interpretation connects the chain-length dependence of mixing <entropy> to the theta condition $\chi=1/2$.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-063-polymer-physics-spring-2007/resources/lec3_07/[MIT Polymer Physics, lecture 3: mean-field lattice theory]{ref}.

= Solution

For $N=300$, the critical <polymer> <volume fraction> is
$$
\phi_c=\frac1{1+\sqrt{300}}\simeq0.05458.
$$
If $v_0$ is the volume assigned to one <amino acid> in the lattice model, the critical <number densities> are exactly
$$
\boxed{n_{m,c}=\frac{\phi_c}{v_0},\qquad
n_{p,c}=\frac{\phi_c}{Nv_0}=\frac{n_{m,c}}{300}.}
$$
The extra physical approximation needed to use the given <radius of gyration> is that a chain in a <poor solvent> is a compact <polymer globule>. Its size then scales as $R_g\sim bN^{1/3}$ with $v_0\sim b^3$, so $Nv_0\sim R_g^3$. This gives the standard scaling estimates
$$
n_{m,c}\sim\frac{N\phi_c}{R_g^3},\qquad
n_{p,c}\sim\frac{\phi_c}{R_g^3}.
$$
With $R_g=3\,\mathrm{nm}$, numerical substitution gives
$$
\boxed{n_{m,c}\sim6\times10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
n_{p,c}\sim2\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$
Using only the leading large-$N$ value $\phi_c\sim N^{-1/2}$ gives $6.4\times10^{26}$ and $2.1\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, respectively. These are scaling estimates, not geometrically exact densities.

For comparison, a specifically *uniform spherical* compact protein has the <radius of gyration of a uniform sphere>, $R_g^2=3R^2/5$, so its physical volume is $V_p=(4\pi/3)(5/3)^{3/2}R_g^3$. Taking $Nv_0=V_p$ instead gives $n_{m,c}\simeq6.7\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and $n_{p,c}\simeq2.2\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. The factor between these conventions is geometric. The PDF does not give a <monomer> volume, globule density or shape model, and a <radius of gyration> alone does not determine an exact molecular volume. The model-independent conclusions are the formulas in terms of $v_0$ and the 300:1 ratio of <monomer> to protein number density.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Interpret the stated transition as an upper critical miscibility <temperature>: cooling increases the effective $\chi$ through $\chi_c$. Above $T_c$, the homogeneous mixing <free energy> is convex throughout $0<\phi<1$, with no two-phase common tangent. Below $T_c$, it acquires a region with $f''<0$, bordered by the <spinodal>. A lower straight line touches the curve at two binodal compositions $\phi_-$ and $\phi_+$; this <common-tangent construction> gives the equilibrium polymer-poor and polymer-rich phases.

The precise raw curve need not have two equal minima: subtracting an <affine function> of $\phi$ changes reference chemical potentials without changing <curvature> or coexistence compositions. The free-energy panels above subtract the tangent at $\phi_c$ from the upper-temperature curve and the common tangent from the lower-temperature curve. This makes the common tangent and the convex-versus-nonconvex distinction visible.

If the mean initial composition $\phi_0$ lies inside the low-temperature spinodal region, an arbitrarily small separation into equal-volume regions with compositions $\phi_0\pm\delta\phi$ changes the mean free <energy> by
$$
\frac{f(\phi_0+\delta\phi)+f(\phi_0-\delta\phi)}2-f(\phi_0)
=\frac12f''(\phi_0)(\delta\phi)^2+O((\delta\phi)^4)<0.
$$
Small fluctuations therefore grow spontaneously: this is <spinodal decomposition>. Interfacial costs limit very short-wavelength fluctuations, and the polymer-poor and polymer-rich domains subsequently coarsen towards their binodal compositions.

If $\phi_0$ lies just outside a spinodal branch but still inside the binodal, then $f''(\phi_0)>0$. Small fluctuations increase <free energy>, so the uniform mixture is <metastable>. Separation requires a sufficiently large nucleus to overcome the interfacial barrier, followed by <nucleation> and growth. There is no guarantee that this happens rapidly. If the initial composition is outside the binodal as well, it remains a stable uniform phase after cooling.

For a mean composition in the coexistence interval, conservation of <polymer> fixes the volume fraction $\alpha$ of the rich phase by the <lever rule>:
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\phi_0-\phi_-}{\phi_+-\phi_-},\qquad
1-\alpha=\frac{\phi_+-\phi_0}{\phi_+-\phi_-}.}
$$
The temperatures alone do not determine which mechanism occurs without the initial composition. Numerical free-energy curves additionally require $\chi(T)$; the plotted example uses $\chi(T)=\chi_cT_c/T$ with absolute temperatures $T_c=298.15\,\mathrm K$, $T_1=303.15\,\mathrm K$ and $T_2=293.15\,\mathrm K$.
