**Entropic <elasticity>.** A rubber contains flexible <polymer> strands tied into a network by <cross-links>. Stretching biases the strands toward relatively extended configurations, reducing their configurational <entropy>. The restoring <entropic force> follows from the increase in <Helmholtz free energy> $F=U-TS$. For a Gaussian strand with $N_s$ statistical steps of length $b$, its deformation-dependent <free energy> is approximately $3k_BTR^2/(2N_sb^2)$. Its stiffness therefore increases with absolute <temperature>, even if the underlying bond stiffness changes little.

In the ideal affine-network model of <rubber elasticity>, the <shear modulus> is
$$
\boxed{G=\nu_s k_BT,}
$$
where $\nu_s$ is the <number density> of elastically active network strands. Thus heating increases the modulus approximately in proportion to $T$, provided the network density and rubbery state remain approximately unchanged. <Thermal expansion>, changing relaxation times or damage can modify that simple trend.

Examples are **lightly crosslinked <natural rubber>**, based on cis-rich <polyisoprene>, and **crosslinked <polydimethylsiloxane>**. The crosslinks prevent the long-time macroscopic flow of an unconnected chain solution.

**Density estimate and the unspecified modulus convention.** With room <temperature> $T\simeq298\,\mathrm K$, the <Boltzmann constant> gives $k_BT\simeq4.11\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm J$. If the stated modulus is $G=2\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$, then
$$
\boxed{\nu_s=\frac{G}{k_BT}\simeq4.9\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
\simeq81\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}.}
$$
The molar value counts moles of active strands. If “modulus” instead means <Young's modulus> $E$, a nearly incompressible isotropic rubber has $E\simeq3G$, and the inferred value is
$$
\boxed{\nu_s\simeq\frac{E}{3k_BT}=1.6\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
\simeq27\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}.}
$$
Thus the robust order-of-magnitude estimate is $10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. The <cross-link density inferred from rubber elasticity> is usually an effective strand density; the actual junction density is $\nu_j=2\nu_s/f$ for junction functionality $f$ in an ideal network without inactive loops or dangling ends. For four-functional junctions this would be half the strand density. The paper supplies neither modulus type nor junction functionality, so a unique chemical junction count cannot be extracted.

For an ideally dissociated <electrolyte>, the <Debye–Hückel screening length> is
$$
\lambda_D=\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r k_BT}
{e^2\sum_i n_i z_i^2}\right)^{1/2},
\qquad I=\frac12\sum_i c_i z_i^2.
$$
Here $n_i$ is ionic <number density>, $z_i$ the charge in units of the <elementary charge>, and $c_i$ is molar concentration. Conversion between $I$ and <number density> cancels in a ratio. At fixed <relative permittivity>, $\lambda_D\propto\sqrt{T/I}$.

In $0.01\,\mathrm M$ <sodium chloride>, $c_{\rm Na^+}=c_{\rm Cl^-}=0.01\,\mathrm M$, giving $I_1=0.01\,\mathrm M$. Complete dissociation of $0.1\,\mathrm M$ <aluminium chloride> gives $c_{\rm Al^{3+}}=0.1\,\mathrm M$ and $c_{\rm Cl^-}=0.3\,\mathrm M$, so
$$
I_2=\frac12[0.1(3)^2+0.3(1)^2]=0.60\,\mathrm M=60I_1.
$$
The factor is sixty, not ten: the squared charge and the three counterions per formula unit both matter. Using the given $5\,\mathrm{nm}$ reference length directly, and $T_1\simeq298\,\mathrm K$, $T_2=323\,\mathrm K$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{D,2}=5\,\mathrm{nm}\sqrt{\frac{323}{298}\frac{0.01}{0.60}}
\simeq0.67\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The modest <temperature> increase raises the screening length by only about $4\%$ at fixed concentration; the large increase in <ionic strength> shortens it by almost an order of magnitude overall.

Under <charge stabilization of colloids>, the electrostatic double-layer repulsion now has a much shorter range. Attractive <van der Waals forces> can then dominate at closer separations, reducing the barrier to <particle aggregation> in <DLVO theory>. Thus **an electrostatically stabilized <colloid> is likely to be destabilized**, with aggregation more likely than in the original dispersion.

This is a conditional physical prediction, not a proof of instability from a length alone. No particle radius, surface potential, attractive interaction strength or steric coating is specified. <Steric stabilization> could preserve stability, and trivalent-ion <adsorption> can alter the surface charge. Moreover, ideal screening theory is only an estimate at $I\simeq0.6\,\mathrm M$, where ion association, <hydrolysis> and activities can matter. The numerical result uses the supplied reference length and the stipulated unchanged permittivity, rather than replacing either with an unprovided calibration.

Write the membrane height above its mean plane as a sum of <Fourier modes>, $h(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf q}h_{\mathbf q}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}$. The supplied average is a thermal mean-squared modal height; for complex Fourier coefficients it is understood as $\langle|h_{\mathbf q}|^2\rangle$, with $h_{-\mathbf q}=h_{\mathbf q}^*$ for a real height field. The terms are:

* $k_BT$ is the thermal energy, with <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$ and absolute <temperature> $T$.
* $A$ is the projected membrane <area> used in the Fourier normalization.
* $q=|\mathbf q|$ is the in-plane <wavenumber>; the corresponding <wavelength> is $2\pi/q$.
* $\sigma$ is the <membrane tension>, in $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}=\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$.
* $\kappa$ is the <membrane bending modulus>, in joules.

In the small-slope approximation, <Helfrich membrane energy> contains
$$
F=\frac12\int_A[\sigma|\nabla h|^2+\kappa(\nabla^2h)^2],d^2r
=\frac A2\sum_{\mathbf q}(\sigma q^2+\kappa q^4)|h_{\mathbf q}|^2.
$$
The <equipartition theorem>, with the independent real modes counted consistently, gives the stated <membrane fluctuation spectrum under tension>. Its units are length squared: $\sigma q^2$ and $\kappa q^4$ both have units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-4}}$, so $k_BT/[A(\sigma q^2+\kappa q^4)]$ has units $\mathrm{m^2}$.

At small length scales, $q$ is large and the bending term dominates:
$$
\boxed{\langle|h_{\mathbf q}|^2\rangle\simeq\frac{k_BT}{A\kappa q^4}
\quad\text{for }q\gg\sqrt{\sigma/\kappa}.}
$$
The fluctuation <amplitude> is controlled mainly by the <membrane bending modulus> and thermal energy, rather than tension. The crossover occurs when $\sigma q_c^2=\kappa q_c^4$, hence
$$
\boxed{q_c=\sqrt{\frac\sigma\kappa},\qquad
\ell_c\equiv q_c^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac\kappa\sigma},\qquad
\lambda_c=2\pi\sqrt{\frac\kappa\sigma}.}
$$
The bending-dominated regime extends up to the <membrane elastic length> when scale means $1/q$, or up to $\lambda_c$ when it means <wavelength>. At still larger scales tension gives a $q^{-2}$ spectrum. At the opposite end, the continuum formula requires <wavelengths> larger than the microscopic membrane thickness or molecular cutoff. Without numerical $\kappa$ and $\sigma$, no numerical crossover scale is determined.

The <Reynolds number> compares inertial and viscous terms in the momentum equation:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UL}{\eta}.}
$$
Here $\rho$ is fluid <mass density>, $U$ a characteristic speed, $L$ a swimmer or flow length, and $\eta$ the <dynamic viscosity>. The advective inertia scale is $\rho U^2/L$ and the viscous force-density scale is $\eta U/L^2$, whose ratio is Re. For an illustrative micron-scale swimmer with $L=2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $U=20\,\mathrm{\mu m\,s^{-1}}$ in water, taking $\rho=10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\eta=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ gives $\mathrm{Re}\sim4\times10^{-5}$.

At such low Re, <Stokes flow> is the useful approximation. For an incompressible <Newtonian fluid> it obeys $-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, with negligible inertia. Swimming is not a sequence of propulsive impulses followed by coasting: drag balances propulsive <forces> almost instantaneously. For example, the drag on a slowly translating sphere is $6\pi\eta aU$. A freely swimming organism has approximately zero total external <force> and <torque>; its body motion balances the <forces> and <torques> generated by its appendages.

The linear equations have <kinematic reversibility of Stokes flow>: reversing the boundary velocities reverses the fluid motion. A reciprocal stroke that retraces exactly the same sequence of shapes therefore retraces its displacement. This is the <scallop theorem>. A single hinge opening and closing along the same shape path cannot propel an ideal swimmer through an otherwise quiescent Newtonian Stokes fluid, regardless of merely changing how quickly it opens relative to closing.

\Image[2017-q2a-microswimming.png]
{title=A three-bead swimmer can use a nonreciprocal loop in its two-length shape space, unlike a retraced one-parameter stroke}

A useful design has at least two shape coordinates driven out of phase, so the stroke follows a loop rather than a retraced line in shape space. In the three-bead example the two link lengths change in a cycle; hydrodynamic interactions differ at different points of the cycle, allowing net translation. The drawing illustrates this mechanism without assigning a particular swimming speed.

Biological routes include continuous rotation of a chiral helical flagellum, nonreciprocal ciliary strokes, and traveling bending waves along a flagellum. An elongated filament generally has greater drag for motion normal to itself than along itself; this drag anisotropy helps convert those motions into propulsion. The body may counterrotate to balance appendage <torque>. **Micro-organisms must exploit nonreciprocal shape changes, rotation or another mechanism consistent with viscous <force> balance; inertia-based swimming intuition fails.** Viscoelastic surroundings or appreciable inertia can invalidate the particular Newtonian assumptions behind the simple scallop result.

A <grafted polymer chain> is tethered to a surface at one end. Let its isolated coil size be $R\sim bN^\nu$, where $N$ is the number of statistical units and $b$ their <statistical segment length>. For a <good solvent>, the <Flory approximation> gives $\nu\simeq3/5$; an <ideal chain> has $\nu=1/2$. Let $\sigma_g$ be the number of tethers per unit <area>, distinct from the <membrane tension> in Question 1. Their typical lateral separation is $D\sim\sigma_g^{-1/2}$.

At low coverage, $D\gg R$, the chains hardly overlap. Each forms a <polymer mushroom>, retaining roughly the isolated-coil conformation apart from the constraint of the solid surface. Raising coverage until $D\sim R$ makes neighboring coils overlap. The crossover criterion is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_gR^2\sim1,\qquad
\sigma_g^*\sim R^{-2}\sim b^{-2}N^{-2\nu}.}
$$
For $D<R$ in a <good solvent>, maintaining isolated coil shapes would crowd segments into the same lateral regions, increasing excluded-volume and osmotic <free energy>. Chains stretch predominantly away from the surface to relieve that crowding, at the cost of their conformational <entropy>. The resulting dense tethered layer is a <polymer brush>.

\Image[2017-q2b-grafted-chains.png]
{title=Sparse grafted coils form mushrooms; overlap at higher grafting density stretches chains into a brush}

A simple scaling estimate makes the balance explicit. For brush height $H$, the monomer concentration is of order $N\sigma_g/H$. The <free energy> per chain in units of $k_BT$ is
$$
\frac{F_{\rm chain}}{k_BT}\sim
\frac{H^2}{Nb^2}+\frac{vN^2\sigma_g}{H},
$$
where $v>0$ is the effective segment <excluded volume>. The first term is Gaussian stretching; the second is the cost of segment crowding in a <good solvent>. Minimizing with respect to height gives
$$
\boxed{H\sim Nb\left(\frac{v\sigma_g}{b}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
When $v\sim b^3$, this is $H\sim Nb(\sigma_gb^2)^{1/3}$. Height increases with chain length and grafting density, while the lateral spacing is fixed by the attachment points. The estimate assumes stretching strong compared with an isolated coil but still much smaller than contour length $Nb$; it does not describe fully extended chains. Poor-solvent attraction can instead collapse the layer, so solvent quality must be specified before using the good-solvent formula.

The mushroom-to-brush change is a crowding crossover for finite chains, rather than automatically a sharp thermodynamic <phase transition>. A swollen brush can provide <steric stabilization>: bringing two coated surfaces together further compresses and crowds their chains, producing a repulsive free-energy cost. **The controlling comparison is tether separation versus isolated coil size, and the brush height follows from competition between chain stretching and segment interactions.**

The requested model is the <standard linear solid model>: a <spring> of modulus $E_0>0$ in parallel with a Maxwell branch consisting of a <spring> $E_1>0$ in series with a <dashpot> of <viscosity> $\eta>0$. Both parallel branches have the same <strain>; their stresses add. In the Maxwell branch the <spring> and <dashpot> have the same <stress> and their strains add. The equilibrium <spring> prevents indefinite flow at long times.

For harmonic <strain> $\epsilon(t)=\operatorname{Re}[\epsilon_0e^{i\omega t}]$, define the <dynamic modulus> by $\widetilde\sigma=E^*(\omega)\widetilde\epsilon$. The Maxwell branch has compliance $1/E_1+1/(i\omega\eta)$. Defining $\tau=\eta/E_1$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{E^*(\omega)=E_0+\frac{i\omega\eta E_1}{E_1+i\omega\eta}
=E_0+E_1\frac{i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}.}
$$
The same mechanical model can represent a <shear modulus> if the appropriate shear stresses and strains are used consistently.

\Image[2017-q2c-zener-response.png]
{title=A spring parallel to a Maxwell element gives finite low- and high-frequency storage moduli and one loss-modulus peak}

Writing $E^*=E'+iE''$, its <storage modulus> and <loss modulus> are
$$
\boxed{E'=E_0+E_1\frac{(\omega\tau)^2}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},
\qquad E''=E_1\frac{\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.}
$$
With this time convention the physical <stress> is $\epsilon_0[E'\cos\omega t-E''\sin\omega t]$ and leads the <strain> by the loss angle. The dissipated energy per unit volume per cycle is $\pi E''\epsilon_0^2\ge0$. Thus $E'$ describes elastic storage and $E''$ describes dissipation.

At low <frequency>, $\omega\tau\ll1$, the <dashpot> flows during a cycle and relaxes the Maxwell <spring>, leaving $E'\to E_0$, $E''\sim E_1\omega\tau$. At high <frequency>, $\omega\tau\gg1$, the <dashpot> hardly deforms within a cycle, so both springs carry elastic load: $E'\to E_0+E_1$, while $E''\sim E_1/(\omega\tau)$. The <loss modulus> is maximal at $\omega\tau=1$, with peak value $E_1/2$.

The equivalent constitutive equation is
$$
\sigma+\tau\dot\sigma=E_0\epsilon+\tau(E_0+E_1)\dot\epsilon.
$$
After a sudden <strain> $\epsilon_s$ is imposed and held, the <stress> relaxes as $\sigma(t)=\epsilon_s[E_0+E_1e^{-t/\tau}]$. It falls from the instantaneous modulus $E_0+E_1$ to the nonzero relaxed modulus $E_0$. This single-time relaxation is the simplest improvement over a purely elastic solid or a Maxwell fluid with zero long-time modulus.

The <loss tangent> is $\tan\delta=E''/E'$. Its maximum need not coincide with the loss-modulus maximum: the <peak frequencies in a standard linear solid> give $\omega\tau=\sqrt{E_0/(E_0+E_1)}$ for the <loss tangent>. **The model captures one viscoelastic crossover between two finite elastic plateaux, with dissipation concentrated near the relaxation <frequency>.**

**Molecular <area> from the <adsorption> slope.** The supplied <Gibbs adsorption equation> identifies the <surface excess> <number density> $\Gamma=1/\Sigma$ through
$$
\Gamma=-\frac1{k_BT}\frac{d\gamma}{d\ln c},\qquad
\Sigma=-\frac{k_BT}{d\gamma/d\ln c}.
$$
The logarithm is understood relative to a fixed concentration unit or reference concentration; only concentration ratios enter its differences. The pure-water point $c=0$ supplies a baseline <surface tension>, but cannot be included in $\ln c$.

An estimate near the later working concentration $0.055\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ uses its two bracketing points:
$$
\frac{d\gamma}{d\ln c}\simeq
\frac{(45.2-48.5)\times10^{-3}}{\ln(0.06/0.05)}
=-1.810\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.
$$
Taking room <temperature> as $298\,\mathrm K$,
$$
\boxed{\Sigma\simeq\frac{4.11\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm J}
{1.810\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}}
=2.27\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{m^2}\simeq0.23\,\mathrm{nm^2}.}
$$
This corresponds to about $4.4$ adsorbed molecules per $\mathrm{nm^2}$. The data are not perfectly linear in $\ln c$, so the inferred molecular <area> depends somewhat on the concentration range: a fit to all five positive-concentration points gives about $0.26\,\mathrm{nm^2}$, while the higher-concentration region gives roughly $0.22\,\mathrm{nm^2}$. An effective <area> of order **$0.2$–$0.3\,\mathrm{nm^2}$** is justified; a concentration-independent exact value is not.

\Image[2017-q3a-surface-tension.png]
{title=Surface-tension interpolation and extrapolated wetting threshold, alongside the local Gibbs adsorption slope}

**<Contact angle>.** Linear interpolation between $0.05$ and $0.06\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ gives $\gamma_{LV}(0.055)=(48.5+45.2)/2=46.85\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$. For an ideal equilibrium <contact angle> measured through the liquid, <Young's equation> is
$$
\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}=\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta.
$$
The supplied solid-vapour and solid-liquid values have difference $40\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\theta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{300-260}{46.85}\right)
=31.4^\circ.}
$$
This assumes clean homogeneous equilibrium interfaces, negligible contact-angle hysteresis, and the stated solid interfacial energies applicable to the solution.

**Complete wetting.** The <spreading coefficient> is $S=\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL}-\gamma_{LV}=40\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}-\gamma_{LV}$. The onset of <complete wetting> occurs at $S=0$, or $\gamma_{LV}=40\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$, if the solid interfacial energies remain unchanged. The last measured value is still $42.0\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$, so this requires extrapolation beyond the table.

Continuing the last local slope in concentration, $(42.0-45.2)/(0.07-0.06)=-320\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ per $\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$, gives
$$
\boxed{c_{\rm wet}\simeq0.07+\frac{42.0-40.0}{320}
=0.0763\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}.}
$$
Continuing a constant local Gibbs slope in $\ln c$ instead gives approximately $0.078\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$. Thus the data suggest a threshold near **$0.08\,\mathrm M$ under continued-trend assumptions**, rather than fixing one exactly. A surface-tension plateau caused by micellization, or a change in $\gamma_{SL}$, could prevent or shift this threshold; no such extra data are given.

**Capillary rise.** For a circular capillary of radius $a$, vertical surface-tension <force> $2\pi a\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta$ balances the weight $\rho g\pi a^2h$. Thus <capillary action> gives
$$
h=\frac{2\gamma_{LV}\cos\theta}{\rho ga}
=\frac{2(\gamma_{SV}-\gamma_{SL})}{\rho ga}.
$$
Using $a=0.5\,\mathrm{mm}=5.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$, $\rho=10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$,
$$
\boxed{h=\frac{2(0.040)}{(10^3)(9.81)(5.0\times10^{-4})}
=1.63\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m\simeq16\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
This is the height above the reservoir level in the ideal static capillary approximation; meniscus-volume corrections and contact-angle hysteresis are neglected. The positive $\cos\theta$ gives a rise, rather than a depression.

**Diffuse-interface <free energy>.** A sharp boundary assigns one mathematical surface to two uniform fluids. Molecular mixing instead changes composition over a finite distance. In <square-gradient theory of a liquid interface>, let $\phi(\mathbf r)$ be a dimensionless local composition and write
$$
F[\phi]=\int\left[f_b(\phi,T)+\frac K2|\nabla\phi|^2\right]dV,
\qquad K>0.
$$
The bulk free-energy density $f_b$ favors the equilibrium phase compositions, while the <gradient> term penalizes changing composition too abruptly. Since $f_b$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$ and $\phi$ is dimensionless, $K$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}$. The theory balances bulk mixing cost against this smoothness cost, rather than imposing a zero-width surface from the start.

At coexistence the two compositions $\phi_-,\phi_+$ have a <common-tangent construction for phase coexistence>. Subtract this tangent from $f_b$ to define an excess grand-potential density $\Delta\omega(\phi)$, zero at both bulk compositions. For a planar profile varying along $z$, minimizing the excess <free energy> gives
$$
K\phi''=\frac{d\Delta\omega}{d\phi}.
$$
Multiply by $\phi'$ and integrate using $\phi'\to0$ in the bulk:
$$
\boxed{\frac K2(\phi')^2=\Delta\omega(\phi).}
$$
It follows that the interfacial <surface tension> is
$$
\gamma=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\left[\Delta\omega+\frac K2(\phi')^2\right]dz
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K(\phi')^2dz
=\int_{\phi_-}^{\phi_+}\sqrt{2K\Delta\omega(\phi)}\,d\phi.
$$
This connects a continuous composition profile to a positive interfacial energy per <area>.

**Broadening near criticality.** Close to a symmetric mean-field <thermodynamic critical point>, an <order parameter> $m$ measuring departure from the critical composition has bulk density
$$
f_b(m)=\frac r2m^2+\frac u4m^4,\qquad
r\propto T-T_c<0,\quad u>0.
$$
The coexistence values are $m=\pm m_0$, $m_0=\sqrt{|r|/u}$. The <scalar quartic domain wall> profile is
$$
\boxed{m(z)=m_0\tanh(z/w),\qquad
w=\sqrt{\frac{2K}{|r|}}.}
$$
The curvature at either bulk minimum is $f_b''(m_0)=2|r|$, so the bulk <correlation length> is $\xi=\sqrt{K/(2|r|)}$ and this convention gives $w=2\xi$. As $T\to T_c^-$, the bulk distinction between phases weakens, its free-energy curvature and barrier decrease, and composition can vary gradually over an increasing distance at little bulk cost. The <gradient> penalty then favors a wider profile:
$$
\boxed{w\propto(T_c-T)^{-1/2}\quad\text{in mean-field theory}.}
$$
The same model gives $\gamma=2\sqrt{2K}|r|^{3/2}/(3u)$, which vanishes at criticality. The exponents here are mean-field results, not universal exact values for every real mixture.

\Image[2017-q3b-diffuse-interface.png]
{title=Approaching criticality reduces the composition contrast and broadens the equilibrium diffuse interface}

The figure uses two illustrative parameter values in this quartic model, not experimental parameters supplied by the paper. In addition to the intrinsic gradient-theory width, thermal <capillary waves> move and roughen an interface. Their increasing <amplitude> as <surface tension> decreases can broaden the spatially averaged measured profile further. Thus a measured broad interface can include both intrinsic composition smoothing and long-wavelength interface-position fluctuations.

**Meaning of the mixing <free energy>.** The supplied expression is the <Flory–Huggins solution theory> mixing <free energy> *per lattice site*, or per solvent-sized reference cell. Although the question calls it “per particle,” it cannot mean per <polymer> molecule: one chain occupies $N$ sites. Let the site volume be $v_0$, the total number of sites be $M=V/v_0$, and the chain and solvent counts be $n_p,n_s$, so
$$
M=Nn_p+n_s,\qquad\phi=\frac{Nn_p}{M},\qquad F_{\rm mix}=Mk_BT\,g(\phi),
$$
where
$$
g(\phi)=\frac\phi N\ln\phi+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi).
$$
The first term is the <polymer>'s translational <entropy of mixing>. Its factor $1/N$ expresses that $N$ connected segments translate as one molecule. The second is the solvent's translational mixing <entropy>. Both are negative in the mixing <free energy> for $0<\phi<1$, favoring mixing.

The final term is a mean-field excess contact contribution. The <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi$ measures the relative preference for unlike polymer-solvent contacts compared with like contacts. In the simplest lattice model with coordination number $z$ and pair energies $\epsilon_{ps},\epsilon_{pp},\epsilon_{ss}$,
$$
\chi=\frac z{k_BT}\left[\epsilon_{ps}-\frac{\epsilon_{pp}+\epsilon_{ss}}2\right].
$$
Positive $\chi$ penalizes unlike contacts and promotes separation; negative $\chi$ favors them. Effective real-solution values can also include non-enthalpic effects, so their <temperature> dependence need not be exactly a constant divided by $T$. The subsequent derivatives assume composition-independent $\chi$ as in the supplied model.

**<Osmotic pressure> and the special value one half.** At fixed chain count, $\partial\phi/\partial V=-\phi/V$. Differentiating $F_{\rm mix}=(V/v_0)k_BTg(\phi)$ with respect to volume, relative to the pure-solvent reference, gives
$$
\pi=\frac{k_BT}{v_0}[\phi g'(\phi)-g(\phi)].
$$
The resulting <osmotic pressure in Flory-Huggins solution theory> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\pi v_0}{k_BT}
=-\ln(1-\phi)-\left(1-\frac1N\right)\phi-\chi\phi^2
=\frac\phi N+\left(\frac12-\chi\right)\phi^2
+\frac{\phi^3}{3}+O(\phi^4).}
$$
The leading term is the ideal <osmotic pressure> of chains, since their <number density> is $\phi/(Nv_0)$. At $\chi=1/2$ the second-virial term vanishes: effective two-segment attraction and excluded-volume repulsion balance. This is the <theta solvent> condition for a sufficiently long dilute chain; the positive third-order contribution remains, so a finite-concentration solution is not a completely noninteracting gas. The site-volume factor is necessary to give physical <pressure> units.

For $\chi<1/2$, the effective pair <excluded volume> is positive. In a <good solvent>, the isolated chain expands relative to a Gaussian coil, with $R\sim bN^\nu$, $\nu\simeq0.588$ or $3/5$ in the <Flory approximation>. For $\chi>1/2$, attraction wins in a <poor solvent> and a sufficiently long chain collapses toward a dense <polymer globule>, with asymptotic size $R\propto N^{1/3}$. Close to the theta condition, finite-chain crossover effects matter. At $\chi=1/2$, $R\sim bN^{1/2}$ to leading scaling order. This theta criterion is not the exact finite-$N$ phase-separation critical point; the lattice model has $\phi_c=1/(1+\sqrt N)$ and $\chi_c=(1+1/\sqrt N)^2/2$.

**Overlap concentration.** The <polymer overlap concentration> is reached when there is roughly one isolated coil in its own pervaded volume. If the coil size is $R$ and a <repeat unit> occupies volume $v_0$,
$$
\phi^*\sim\frac{Nv_0}{R^3}.
$$
With $R\sim bN^\nu$, the general scaling law is
$$
\boxed{\phi^*\sim\frac{v_0}{b^3}N^{1-3\nu}.}
$$
The usual single-segment-volume estimate $v_0\sim b^3$ gives $\phi^*\sim N^{1-3\nu}$: $N^{-1/2}$ in a <theta solvent>, approximately $N^{-4/5}$ in a <good solvent>, and order one for compact globules. A coil has no sharp surface, so choosing a <radius of gyration> or a geometric coil volume changes numerical prefactors.

For the stated <polystyrene> at $N=1500$ and $\chi=1/2$, the conventional scaling estimate is
$$
\boxed{\phi^*\sim N^{-1/2}=1500^{-1/2}=0.0258\simeq2.6\%.}
$$
This is a volume-fraction estimate, not a molar concentration. It uses the implicit site-volume approximation; the question does not provide an independent repeat-unit volume to determine a more precise prefactor.

**Semidilute length scale.** Beyond overlap, a <semidilute polymer solution> is characterized locally by a <concentration blob> size $\xi$. Suppose one blob contains $g$ statistical units. At the theta condition the subchain is Gaussian, so $\xi\sim b\sqrt g$, while matching its internal concentration to the bulk gives $\phi\sim gv_0/\xi^3$. Eliminating $g$ gives the <concentration-blob size in a theta solvent>:
$$
\boxed{\xi\sim\frac{v_0}{b^2\phi}\sim\frac b\phi
\quad\text{when }v_0\sim b^3.}
$$
Using the supplied <statistical step length> $b=0.67\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\phi=0.05$,
$$
\boxed{\xi\sim\frac{0.67}{0.05}\,\mathrm{nm}=13.4\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq13\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The same convention gives $g\sim\phi^{-2}=400<N=1500$, and the whole <ideal chain> has $R\sim b\sqrt N\simeq26\,\mathrm{nm}$. Thus the blob is smaller than the chain. Because $\phi/\phi^*\simeq1.94$ is only modestly above overlap, the value is a scaling estimate in a crossover region, not a precision asymptotic measurement. A <statistical step length> is not automatically a physical monomer diameter: if $v_0$ differs from $b^3$, the more general estimates are $\phi^*\sim(v_0/b^3)N^{-1/2}$ and $\xi\sim v_0/(b^2\phi)$, with the missing prefactor left explicit.
