The tension is supplied by <Stokes drag> on the free bead. With bead <radius> $b=0.5\,\mu\mathrm m$, the stipulated isolated-sphere approximation gives
$$
\boxed{f=6\pi\eta bU=2.83\,\mathrm{pN}.}
$$
The trap also balances drag on the trapped bead, but that bead's drag is not an additional <force> transmitted through the chain. The <polymer contour length> is $L=Na=6\,\mu\mathrm m$. Use a room-<temperature> estimate $T\simeq300\,\mathrm K$, consistent with the water-viscosity approximation imported from part (a); the expressions below retain the dependence on $T$.

For a <theta solvent>, <Gaussian chain> <elasticity> gives $F_{\rm el}=3k_BTR^2/(2Na^2)$ and hence
$$
\boxed{R_\theta=\frac{fNa^2}{3k_BT}
=0.819\left(\frac{300\,\mathrm K}{T}\right)\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
This is the mean extension along the <force>, rather than the unforced <end-to-end distance of a polymer chain> $a\sqrt N=60\,\mathrm{nm}$. At $300\,\mathrm K$, $fa/(k_BT)=0.410$; the finite-link <freely jointed chain> result $R=L[\coth(fa/k_BT)-k_BT/(fa)]$ is $0.810\,\mu\mathrm m$, close to the Gaussian estimate.

The <good-solvent force-extension scaling> changes the <elasticity>. In the <tensile blob> regime, a blob has size $\xi_t=k_BT/f$, contains $g\sim(\xi_t/a)^{5/3}$ segments, and the aligned string of $N/g$ blobs has <length>
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm good}\sim\frac Ng\xi_t
=Na\left(\frac{fa}{k_BT}\right)^{2/3}
\simeq3.31\left(\frac{300\,\mathrm K}{T}\right)^{2/3}\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
Only the scaling and order of magnitude of the good-solvent value are determined: solvent-dependent swelling amplitudes are not supplied. The nonlinear <force> law is the essential correction to extrapolating a weak-force coil <spring>.<Excluded Volume Effects and Stretched Polymer Chains by Philip Alan Pincus>[abstract and section I, p. 386: strongly elongated good-solvent chain and restoring force proportional to extension to the power 3/2]{ref}

To expose the unrealistic extrapolation, the unforced good-solvent size estimate is $R_0\sim aN^{3/5}=151\,\mathrm{nm}$. Treating this coil as a linear <spring> with <spring constant> $3k_BT/R_0^2$ predicts $R\simeq5.17\,\mu\mathrm m$ at $300\,\mathrm K$, although its required condition $fR_0\ll k_BT$ fails by a factor of about $103$. Dropping the order-one factor three, as in the rough estimate $k\sim k_BT/R_0^2$, even gives $R\sim15.5\,\mu\mathrm m>L$. **An extension beyond the <polymer contour length> is an artefact of extending the coil's <linear response> outside its range.** The <tensile blob> estimate avoids that violation but here $\xi_t\simeq1.46\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $g\sim4.4$, so it is only marginally within a many-segment-blob regime. <finite extensibility of a polymer chain>, chain drag and flow disturbance by both large beads further limit quantitative realism. The specified neglect of chain drag is a model assumption, not evidence that it is physically negligible.

For a <symmetric Flory-Huggins polymer blend>, the mixing <free energy> per lattice site is
$$
\frac{f}{k_BT}=\frac{\phi\ln\phi+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)}N+\chi\phi(1-\phi).
$$
The critical conditions $f''=0$ and $d^3f/d\phi^3=0$ give $\phi_c=1/2$ and $\chi_c=2/N=0.002$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac{48.2}{0.032+0.002}=1.418\times10^3\,\mathrm K.}
$$
Since $d\chi/dT<0$, heating improves miscibility: this is an **<upper critical solution temperature>** in the stipulated model. The very high value is an extrapolation of the supplied interaction-parameter fit, not evidence that an intact experimental blend can be brought reversibly to this <temperature>; chemical stability and the fitted <temperature> range must be checked before interpreting it as an accessible transition.

At $293.15\,\mathrm K$, $\chi=0.132421$ and $N\chi\simeq132$, well into strong segregation. For the <polymer-blend interface width>, state the convention explicitly. In the equal-segment, long-chain square-gradient approximation,
$$
\frac{F}{k_BT\rho_0}=\int dz\left[\chi\phi(1-\phi)+\frac{a^2(\phi')^2}{24\phi(1-\phi)}\right].
$$
Its first integral gives $\phi'=\sqrt{24\chi}\,\phi(1-\phi)/a$, so
$$
\phi(z)=\frac{1+\tanh(z/\ell)}2,\qquad
\ell=\frac{a}{\sqrt{6\chi}}=0.752\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
The inverse-central-slope width is consequently
$$
\boxed{w\equiv\frac1{\phi'(0)}=2\ell=1.50\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
A convention calling the hyperbolic-tangent scale itself the width instead quotes $0.752\,\mathrm{nm}$; these describe the same profile. The predicted interface spans only a few statistical segments, so the <continuum approximation> for gradients is quantitatively marginal. Intrinsic composition width and experimentally broadened width also differ because roughness and finite spatial resolution can broaden a measured profile.

A viscoelastic material stores part of the deformation <energy> and dissipates part of it. In <linear viscoelasticity>, a small imposed shear <strain> produces a <stress> determined by its history, $\tau(t)=\int_{-\infty}^tG(t-t')\dot\gamma(t')\,dt'$. A shear step probes <stress relaxation>; a <stress> step probes <creep compliance>. A purely elastic solid has persistent <stress> after a held <strain>, while a <Newtonian fluid> relaxes that <stress> and creeps continuously under constant <stress>.

For a <Maxwell model>, a <spring> of <shear modulus> $G_0$ and a <dashpot> of <viscosity> $\eta$ are in series:
$$
\dot\gamma=\frac{\dot\tau}{G_0}+\frac\tau\eta,\qquad
G(t)=G_0e^{-t/\tau_M},\qquad \tau_M=\eta/G_0.
$$
For the convention $\gamma\propto e^{i\omega t}$,
$$
\boxed{G'=G_0\frac{(\omega\tau_M)^2}{1+(\omega\tau_M)^2},\qquad
G''=G_0\frac{\omega\tau_M}{1+(\omega\tau_M)^2}.}
$$
The <storage modulus> dominates at short times or high frequencies; the <loss modulus> dominates at long times or low frequencies. The <energy> dissipated per cycle per <volume> is $\pi G''\gamma_0^2$. A <Kelvin-Voigt model> places <spring> and <dashpot> in parallel and instead gives bounded creep $J(t)=(1-e^{-G_0t/\eta})/G_0$: a simple illustration of a viscoelastic solid. Real materials can require a spectrum of relaxation times rather than one element.

A small-amplitude oscillatory <rheometer> measures <stress> amplitude and phase relative to imposed <strain>, giving $G'=|\tau_0/\gamma_0|\cos\delta$ and $G''=|\tau_0/\gamma_0|\sin\delta$. First sweep <strain> amplitude to identify the linear range; control <temperature>, sample ageing, <wall slip> and instrument inertia. Frequency-dependent phase lag alone is insufficient if these apparatus effects are unresolved.

<DLVO theory> adds attractive <Van der Waals forces> and <electric double layer> repulsion to obtain an effective <colloid> pair potential. For two identical spheres of <radius> $b$ at a surface gap $h\ll b$, a useful schematic form is
$$
U(h)\simeq-\frac{A_Hb}{12h}+B e^{-\kappa_Dh},\qquad B>0.
$$
Here $A_H$ is the <Hamaker constant>. For a symmetric monovalent electrolyte with <number density> $n$ of each ionic species, $\kappa_D^2=2ne^2/(\epsilon k_BT)$. The exponential is a simplified screened repulsion; its amplitude depends on <electric potential> at the surface, <charge regulation> and geometry. Constant-charge and constant-potential <boundary conditions> need not produce the same close-approach law.

A primary attractive <minimum> near contact may be separated from dispersed configurations by an <energy> barrier; a shallower secondary <minimum> can produce loose reversible aggregation. A barrier much larger than $k_BT$ can give long-lived kinetic stabilization without making the dispersed state the global free-energy <minimum>. Adding salt shortens the <Debye–Hückel screening length> and can lower the barrier, allowing <colloidal coagulation>. Changing <pH> can alter surface ionization, so screening alone does not determine stability.

One can vary salt <concentration> while holding particle <concentration>, <temperature> and <pH> fixed, then follow <particle aggregation> using <light scattering> or <microscopy>. A solvent-only control and an independently measured surface-charge indicator help distinguish a change of screening from <adsorption> or <charge reversal>. Classical <DLVO theory> omits <steric stabilization>, <hydration forces> and strong ionic correlations; the formal attraction diverging at contact is cut off by molecular-scale physics.

The <coil-globule transition> is the contraction of an isolated flexible <polymer> as <solvent quality> worsens. A swollen chain in a <good solvent> has size $R\sim aN^{3/5}$ in the <Flory approximation>. At the <theta temperature> the effective two-segment <excluded volume> vanishes and $R\sim aN^{1/2}$. A <poor solvent> favors a dense <polymer globule>, with $R\propto N^{1/3}$ at fixed internal <density>.

The competition is visible in the scaling <free energy>
$$
\frac{F}{k_BT}\sim\frac{R^2}{Na^2}+\frac{vN^2}{R^3}+\frac{uN^3}{R^6},\qquad u>0.
$$
For $v>0$, elastic cost balances two-segment repulsion to give the swollen-coil law. At $v=0$, three-segment repulsion stabilizes ideal-chain scaling; for $v<0$, attraction balanced by the positive three-segment term gives $R^3\sim uN/|v|$. The dense <polymer globule> also has an interfacial <free energy>. The scaling expression explains regimes, not all fluctuation corrections or a detailed finite-chain transition curve. A finite chain crosses over smoothly; the sharp theta-point limit refers to indefinitely long chains.

A dilute-solution light-scattering experiment can follow <radius of gyration> while changing <temperature> or <solvent> composition. Measure at several concentrations and extrapolate toward isolated chains; check molecular-weight distribution, reversibility and the absence of growing multi-chain aggregates. <dynamic light scattering> measures <hydrodynamic size>, which is related to but distinct from <radius of gyration>. A falling scattering-derived size accompanied by a rising aggregate population cannot by itself establish single-chain collapse. **<solvent quality> controls conformation through the balance of segment interactions and <conformational entropy>.**

Write $s$ for the number of grafted chains per unit <area> and $\Sigma=sa^2$ for its dimensionless form; this separates the physical <grafting density> from a dimensionless coverage. A free dilute chain has size $R_0\sim aN^\nu$, with $\nu=1/2$ in a <theta solvent> and approximately $3/5$ in a <good solvent>. An isolated end-grafted chain loses translations and configurations that cross the substrate, but retains the same leading size exponent if the surface is nonadsorbing. When the lateral graft spacing $D\sim s^{-1/2}$ exceeds $R_0$, the layer consists of <polymer mushrooms>. When coils overlap, crowding stretches the chains away from the surface into a <polymer brush>.

The <polymer mushroom-to-brush crossover> is
$$
\boxed{s_*\sim R_0^{-2},\qquad \Sigma_*\sim N^{-2\nu}.}
$$
For the stipulated statistical-segment interpretation of $N$ and $a$, the scaling estimates are
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 &R_0&D_*&s_*\;(\mathrm{nm^{-2}})\\\hline
\theta&14\,\mathrm{nm}&14\,\mathrm{nm}&5.10\times10^{-3}\\
\text{good}&25.5\,\mathrm{nm}&25.5\,\mathrm{nm}&1.54\times10^{-3}
\end{array}
$$
Thus $\Sigma_*\simeq2.50\times10^{-3}$ and $7.54\times10^{-4}$ respectively. The numerical constants are scaling conventions: using a <radius of gyration> rather than an end-to-end size changes order-one amplitudes, not the powers of $N$.

In the <Alexander-de Gennes brush model>, take blobs of lateral size $D$. Within a blob, $D\sim ag^\nu$; beyond it, the $N/g$ blobs are aligned away from the wall. Thus
$$
g\sim(D/a)^{1/\nu},\qquad
\boxed{H\sim ND/g=Na\Sigma^{(1-\nu)/(2\nu)}.}
$$
With coarse-grained segment <volume> $a^3$, conservation gives the <polymer-brush volume fraction> through $\phi H\sim Na^3s$, hence
$$
\boxed{\phi\sim\Sigma^{(3\nu-1)/(2\nu)},\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&H&\phi\\\hline
\theta&Na\Sigma^{1/2}&\Sigma^{1/2}\\
\text{good}&Na\Sigma^{1/3}&\Sigma^{2/3}
\end{array}}
$$
The <theta-solvent brush height> includes three-segment repulsion even though the effective two-segment interaction vanishes. These are scaling estimates for a roughly uniform brush, not an assertion that every chain end lies at the same height in an actual brush.

The many-segment-blob requirement $g\gg1$ gives $D\gg a$, or $\Sigma\ll1$. Together with overlap, the regime is **$N^{-2\nu}\ll\Sigma\ll1$**. At the <dense-brush packing limit>, $\phi\sim1$, $g\sim1$ and $H\sim Na$. Here $s\sim a^{-2}=2.04\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}$ and $H\sim280\,\mathrm{nm}$. If one formally continues a dense incompressible layer above this boundary, conservation would instead give
$$
\boxed{\phi\simeq1,\qquad H\simeq Na^3s=Na\Sigma.}
$$
This is the requested formal dense-layer expression, but for $\Sigma>1$ it gives $H>Na$, which violates <finite extensibility of a polymer chain>. **There is no physically admissible continuation above this limit in the stated segment-volume model.** A <Kuhn length> segment is not necessarily a cube of material: with an independently specified material <volume> $v_0$ per counted segment, the dense-layer law is $H=Nv_0s$, and the packing bound is $s\leq a/v_0$. The data do not determine $v_0$ separately. The simple model therefore establishes its own breakdown, rather than a universal microscopic maximum <grafting density>.

<grafting-to> uses preformed chains whose <lengths> and dispersities can be characterized before attachment. As coverage rises, each incoming coil must penetrate a crowded brush and lose <conformational entropy> to bring its end to the surface; the growing insertion barrier limits achievable coverage. <grafting-from> supplies small <monomers> to surface-bound growing chains and avoids insertion of a whole coil. **Choose <grafting-from> for high <grafting density>.** Initiator coverage and reaction control still matter, and inaccessible active sites, termination and unequal growth can broaden the resulting chain-length distribution. Neither route removes the elastic and packing costs of the final brush.

A generic application is <steric stabilization> of synthetic <colloids> in a <good solvent>. Bringing coated particles together compresses and interpenetrates their brushes, increasing <osmotic pressure> and conformational free-energy cost. This repulsion can oppose aggregation. The chains must remain attached and sufficiently cover the surface; poor-solvent collapse, sparse coverage or interparticle bridging can defeat stabilization.

Use the <Monge representation> $\mathbf r(x,y)=(x,y,h(x,y))$ for a single-valued surface without overhangs. Its <area> element is $dA=\sqrt{1+|\nabla h|^2}\,dx\,dy$; at small slopes, $dA\simeq[1+|\nabla h|^2/2]dx\,dy$ and twice the <mean curvature> is $2\mathcal H\simeq\nabla^2h$, up to orientation sign. For zero spontaneous curvature, the quadratic excess <Helfrich energy> is
$$
\boxed{F=\frac12\int dx\,dy\,[\kappa(\nabla^2h)^2+\sigma|\nabla h|^2].}
$$
The <fluid membrane shear response> has no equilibrium static in-plane <shear modulus>: constituents can rearrange to relax shear. Bending and isotropic tension still cost <energy>; viscous <shear stresses> can matter during motion. A <Gaussian curvature> term is omitted under the stated approximation; for fixed closed-surface <topology> its integral is constant, but boundaries and <topology> changes require care. The small-slope Monge expression describes weak deformations, not an entire fully wrapped sphere.<Elastic Properties of Lipid Bilayers: Theory and Possible Experiments by Wolfgang Helfrich>[section II.C, equation (12), p. 695: curvature-elastic energy; only the generic continuum mechanics is used here]{ref}

For <spherical-particle wrapping by a fluid membrane>, integrate the spherical <area> element from polar angle $0$ to $\alpha$:
$$
A=\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\alpha r_0^2\sin\theta\,d\theta
=2\pi r_0^2(1-\cos\alpha)=2\pi r_0^2\xi.
$$
Its footprint <radius> is $r_\parallel=r_0\sin\alpha$, so
$$
A_\parallel=\pi r_0^2(2\xi-\xi^2),\qquad
\boxed{\Delta A=\pi r_0^2\xi^2.}
$$
For a spherical surface $2\mathcal H=2/r_0$, making the bending cost $\kappa(2/r_0)^2A/2=4\pi\kappa\xi$. <adhesion> contributes $-2\pi wr_0^2\xi$, and tension contributes $\sigma\Delta A$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{E}{\pi\kappa}=(4-2\bar w)\xi+\bar\sigma\xi^2,\qquad
\bar w=\frac{wr_0^2}{\kappa},\quad\bar\sigma=\frac{\sigma r_0^2}{\kappa}.}
$$
All three contributions are energies; $\bar w$ and $\bar\sigma$ are dimensionless. This is the specified bound-patch model: deformation of the free membrane and the neck is omitted.

The allowed interval $0\leq\xi\leq2$ corresponds to no <adhesion>, partial wrapping and full wrapping. At $\xi=2$, the cap covers a whole sphere and its footprint shrinks to zero. This is a closed neck in the geometric limit. It does not by itself prove membrane scission: creating a detached enclosure changes <topology> and involves neck physics absent from this <energy>.

For positive <membrane tension>, $E''/(\pi\kappa)=2\bar\sigma>0$. Projecting the unconstrained <minimum> $(\bar w-2)/\bar\sigma$ onto the allowed interval gives
$$
\boxed{\xi_{\min}=\begin{cases}
0,&\bar w\leq2,\\
(\bar w-2)/\bar\sigma,&2<\bar w<2+2\bar\sigma,\\
2,&\bar w\geq2+2\bar\sigma.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <tension-dependent wrapping phase diagram> boundaries are $\bar w=2$ and $\bar w=2+2\bar\sigma$. At zero <membrane tension>, the <energy> is linear: the <minimum> jumps from $0$ to $2$ as $\bar w$ crosses $2$, while all indentation values are degenerate at $\bar w=2$. Negative <membrane tension> is outside the stable-flat-membrane interpretation used here. Increasing <membrane tension> makes the excess <area> required for wrapping more expensive, broadening the partially wrapped region.
