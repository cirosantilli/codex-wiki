Use the squared three-dimensional endpoint <displacements>, not their arithmetic <mean>. The <mean squared displacement> estimate and corresponding <diffusion coefficient> are
$$
\widehat{\langle\Delta r^2\rangle}=\frac{31^2+2^2+50^2+65^2+14^2}{5}=1577.2\,\mu\mathrm m^2,
\qquad \widehat D=\frac{1577.2}{6\times60}=4.38\,\mu\mathrm m^2\,\mathrm s^{-1}.
$$
The factor six is for three-dimensional <Brownian motion>. For an isolated no-slip sphere, the <Stokes–Einstein relation> gives the <Brownian radius estimate from endpoint displacements>:
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{k_BT}{6\pi\eta\widehat D}\simeq5.6\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m=56\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Here room <temperature> is taken as $298\,\mathrm K$; choosing $293\,\mathrm K$ gives $55\,\mathrm{nm}$, a much smaller difference than the sampling uncertainty. Under independent increments with a <normal distribution>, $\operatorname{sd}(\widehat D)/D=\sqrt{2/(3n)}\simeq0.37$ for $n=5$. The <sampling uncertainty of an endpoint diffusivity estimate> therefore makes this a rough <radius> estimate. It assumes negligible drift and hydrodynamic coupling between probes, the supplied bulk <viscosity>, and actual three-dimensional tracking. Localization noise adds to the squared <displacements> and would bias this inferred <radius> downward if uncorrected.

For the three-dimensional <worm-like chain>, define the bending coefficient $\kappa_b$ by $F_b=(\kappa_b/2)\int|\partial_s\mathbf t|^2ds$. Its <persistence length> is $l_p=\kappa_b/(k_BT)$. Holding that coefficient and the <polymer contour length> $L$ fixed gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{l_p(T_2)}{l_p(T_1)}=\frac{T_1}{T_2},\qquad
\frac{\Delta l_p}{l_p(T_1)}=\frac{T_1}{T_2}-1=-\frac{T_2-T_1}{T_2}.}
$$
The <temperatures> must be absolute. The <end-to-end distance of a worm-like chain> obeys
$$
\langle R^2\rangle=2Ll_p-2l_p^2(1-e^{-L/l_p}).
$$
For a long flexible chain, $L\gg l_p$, the typical size is $R_{\rm rms}\simeq\sqrt{2Ll_p}$, so **warming contracts the chain**, with $R_{\rm rms}(T_2)/R_{\rm rms}(T_1)\simeq\sqrt{T_1/T_2}$. The finite-length formula also decreases as $l_p$ decreases, but the rod limit $L\ll l_p$ changes very little. A large segment count alone does not guarantee $L\gg l_p$. If the material's bending coefficient itself depends on <temperature>, replace the first ratio by $[\kappa_b(T_2)/\kappa_b(T_1)]T_1/T_2$; the sign then needs that additional material information. This calculation concerns a generic physical filament, with no biomolecular application.

For monovalent mobile ions, the linearized <Debye–Hückel screening length> is
$$
\lambda^2=\frac{\epsilon_0\epsilon_r k_BT}{e^2\sum_i n_i z_i^2}.
$$
Here $n_i$ is a <number density> in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, not a <molarity>. Use the question's idealized neutral-water convention, $c_+=c_-=10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, and $T=293.15\,\mathrm K$. Both ion species contribute:
$$
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{80\epsilon_0k_BT}{2e^2N_A\,10^3\times10^{-7}}},
\qquad \boxed{\lambda_{\rm water}\simeq9.63\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m\simeq0.96\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
For added salt concentration $c_s$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, the <ionic strength> in this model is $I=c_s+10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. With the numerical <molarity> $I_M=I/(1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})$,
$$
\lambda=\frac{0.3045\,\mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{I_M}},\qquad
\boxed{c_s\simeq0.0927\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\simeq0.093\,\mathrm M\quad(\lambda=1\,\mathrm{nm}).}
$$
The water contribution is negligible at that salt level. The factor $10^3$ converts molarity into $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$; omitting it or counting only one ion species gives a wrong screening <length>. Exact water autoionization and activities vary with <temperature>, so the first estimate uses the simplified neutral $10^{-7}\,\mathrm M$ concentrations implied by the question. At the higher salt level, ion-size effects, nonideal activities and changes in <permittivity> limit the numerical accuracy of this continuum estimate.

A <synthetic fluid membrane> is a thin, laterally fluid sheet whose components can rearrange in its plane while the sheet resists changes of <area> and <curvature>. Synthetic <amphiphiles> can assemble into two opposed layers with solvent-compatible groups facing outward. Their fluid in-plane response gives no equilibrium static <shear modulus>, but does not imply zero <membrane bending rigidity> or zero area-compression modulus. A thin membrane can therefore bend easily while remaining difficult to stretch. These notes cover generic synthetic interfaces.

TODO: biological, biofluid and biomolecular membrane applications are user-deferred.

A convenient continuum description is the <Helfrich energy>, augmented by <spontaneous curvature> and the Gaussian term:
$$
F=\int\left[\frac\kappa2(2H-C_0)^2+\bar\kappa K+\sigma\right]dA.
$$
Here $H$ and $K$ are <mean curvature> and <Gaussian curvature>, $C_0$ is preferred <curvature>, $\kappa$ and $\bar\kappa$ have <energy> units, and $\sigma$ is <membrane tension> with units $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Leaflet asymmetry can favor nonzero $C_0$. At fixed topology the integrated Gaussian term is constant for a closed surface by the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem>. A symmetric tensionless spherical sheet has mean-curvature bending <energy> $8\pi\kappa$, independent of its <radius> in this ideal model. Edge <energy> can favor closure, while fixed <area> and enclosed <volume> constrain the resulting shapes.

For a nearly flat sheet, its <small-slope elastic membrane energy> becomes
$$
F=\frac12\int[\kappa(\nabla^2h)^2+\sigma|\nabla h|^2]d^2x.
$$
With $h_{\mathbf q}=A^{-1}\int h(\mathbf x)e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}d^2x$, the <equipartition theorem> gives the <membrane fluctuation spectrum under tension>:
$$
\boxed{\langle|h_{\mathbf q}|^2\rangle=\frac{k_BT}{A(\kappa q^4+\sigma q^2)}.}
$$
Bending suppresses short wavelengths, whereas positive tension suppresses the longest undulations. Their crossover is $q\sim\sqrt{\sigma/\kappa}$. Measuring the spectrum can separate these two contributions, provided finite image resolution, projected versus true <area>, and external confinement are accounted for. The drawn modes below are synthetic calculations, not an image of a sample.

For a clean liquid interface at fixed <temperature> and composition, <surface tension> is the reversible free-energy cost per added <area>, $\gamma=(\partial F/\partial A)_{T,V,N}$, so $dF=\gamma\,dA$. Its units are both $\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}$ and $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. Molecules at an interface have a different environment from those in the bulk; creating more interface changes their interactions and <entropy>. For constant $\gamma$, the interfacial <free energy> is $\gamma A$ up to a reference constant. This is not generally identical to the internal <surface energy>: the interfacial <entropy> density is $-d\gamma/dT$, giving internal-energy density $\gamma-T\,d\gamma/dT$.

A line of <length> $\ell$ in one interface transmits a tangential <force> $\gamma\ell$, perpendicular to the line. A soap film has two interfaces, giving <force> $2\gamma\ell$ on a sliding rim. Quasistatic extension by $dx$ creates total <area> $2\ell dx$, recovering that <force> from $dF=2\gamma\ell dx$. Uniform positive <surface tension> favors minimum <area> at fixed <volume>, hence an isolated spherical droplet when gravity is negligible. <curvature> gives the <Young–Laplace equation>, with outward normal chosen consistently:
$$
\boxed{\Delta p=\gamma\left(\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}\right).}
$$
A spherical liquid drop has $\Delta p=2\gamma/R$; a thin soap bubble with two similar interfaces has approximately $4\gamma/R$. The <capillary length> $\sqrt{\gamma/(\Delta\rho g)}$ compares gravity with capillarity. <surface tension> also restores <capillary waves>, rather than providing a bulk static <shear modulus>.

Interfacial contamination and added <surfactants> can change $\gamma$ substantially; a nonuniform value produces tangential <Marangoni stress>. A rim-force measurement should therefore control <temperature>, cleanliness and the number of interfaces. Capillary-rise methods additionally need the <contact angle>, because the vertical component is proportional to $\gamma\cos\theta$. An independent frame-force measurement can distinguish a contact-angle change from a true change of <surface tension>.

A moving or externally forced colloid disturbs the surrounding fluid, changing the <velocity> of other colloids even without direct contact. These <hydrodynamic interactions> are long-ranged at small <Reynolds number>. In an unbounded incompressible Newtonian liquid, the <Stokes flow> response to a point <force> is the <Stokeslet>:
$$
-\nabla p+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf u+\mathbf F\,\delta(\mathbf r)=0,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,
\qquad u_i(\mathbf r)=\mathsf G_{ij}(\mathbf r)F_j.
$$
Its <Oseen tensor> is
$$
\boxed{\mathsf G_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\frac1{8\pi\eta r}
\left(\delta_{ij}+\frac{r_ir_j}{r^2}\right).}
$$
This is a mobility tensor, with units <velocity> per <force>, rather than a <stress> tensor. At separation $r$ the response parallel to the line of centres is $1/(4\pi\eta r)$; the perpendicular response is $1/(8\pi\eta r)$. Flow is not isotropic even though the solvent is. The disturbance decays as $1/r$, so treating all drag <forces> as independent can miss correlated motion.

For well-separated equal spheres of <radius> $a$, the leading <hydrodynamic mobility matrix> gives
$$
\mathbf V_i=\frac{\mathbf F_i}{6\pi\eta a}
+\sum_{j\ne i}\mathsf G(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j)\mathbf F_j.
$$
The first term is the isolated-sphere self mobility; the remaining terms couple distinct particles. At thermal equilibrium the <fluctuation-dissipation theorem> also predicts correlated short-time increments, $\langle\Delta r_{i\alpha}\Delta r_{j\beta}\rangle\simeq2k_BT\mu_{i\alpha,j\beta}\Delta t$ with the configuration locally fixed. Thus correlated Brownian <displacements> can reveal hydrodynamic coupling without an applied <force>.

The point-force approximation requires $r\gg a$ and negligible inertia. It diverges at the origin and cannot replace the finite self mobility. Near-contact lubrication, walls, many-body corrections, and a viscoelastic solvent require more complete mobility laws. A force-free particle can instead have a faster-decaying leading disturbance, so the $1/r$ claim specifically concerns a net-force-driven object.

The three solvent conditions describe effective interactions between segments of a generic <polymer>. In a <good solvent>, solvent contacts favor a swollen, self-avoiding coil with positive <excluded volume>. At a <theta solvent> condition, leading pair repulsion and attraction cancel; the chain has approximately ideal random-walk statistics. In a <poor solvent>, effective attraction contracts the chain, eventually producing a dense <polymer globule> for a sufficiently long chain.

For $N$ statistical segments of <length> $a$, the <radius of gyration> has the scaling
$$
\boxed{R_g\sim\begin{cases}
aN^{3/5},&\text{good solvent},\\
aN^{1/2},&\text{theta/ideal solvent},\\
aN^{1/3},&\text{dense poor-solvent globule}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The good-solvent power $3/5$ is the <Flory approximation>; the asymptotic three-dimensional exponent is closer to $0.588$. In the ideal <freely jointed chain>, the more precise result is $R_g^2\simeq Na^2/6$, whereas the end-to-end <mean> square is $Na^2$. Scaling notation suppresses those geometry-dependent factors. The globule power follows from a <volume> proportional to $N$, and presumes a dense collapsed state away from the theta crossover. The dilute condition excludes overlap with other chains; it does not eliminate contacts between distant segments of the same chain.

Use <polymer> <volume fraction> $\phi$ horizontally and the <Flory interaction parameter> $\chi$ vertically. Lower $\chi$ gives better solvent quality. The simplest <Flory–Huggins solution theory> assigns the dimensionless mixing <free energy> per lattice site
$$
f(\phi)=\frac\phi N\ln\phi+(1-\phi)\ln(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi),
\qquad v_{\rm eff}\simeq a^3(1-2\chi).
$$
Thus $\chi<1/2$ gives positive <excluded volume> and a good-solvent coil, $\chi=1/2$ is the long-chain theta condition, and $\chi>1/2$ is the poor-solvent side. It is the net two-segment interaction that vanishes at theta, not every molecular <force>.

At fixed good-solvent quality, the <polymer overlap volume fraction> $\phi^*\sim Na^3/R_g^3$ separates isolated coils from a <semidilute polymer solution>. Above overlap, many chains interpenetrate, but there is still solvent on the segment scale. As $\phi$ approaches order unity the solution is concentrated; these concentration-regime boundaries are crossovers, not first-order phase transitions. The overlap fraction increases from order $N^{-4/5}$ in a strong <good solvent> toward order $N^{-1/2}$ at theta. The crossover can be expressed as $\phi^*\sim N^{-4/5}(1-2\chi)^{-3/5}$ while $1-2\chi\gg N^{-1/2}$; it saturates at the theta scaling when that condition fails.

On the poor-solvent side there can instead be coexistence of polymer-poor and polymer-rich phases. The <binodal> follows a common tangent of $f$; the <spinodal> follows
$$
f''(\phi)=\frac1{N\phi}+\frac1{1-\phi}-2\chi=0.
$$
The <critical point of a Flory–Huggins polymer solution> is
$$
\boxed{\phi_c=\frac1{1+\sqrt N},\qquad
\chi_c=\frac12(1+N^{-1/2})^2.}
$$
For finite $N$, $\chi_c$ lies above $1/2$. Therefore <poor solvent> does not automatically mean demixing at every concentration, and an overlap line must not be mistaken for a coexistence boundary. The original figure uses the stated ideal model for an illustrative chain <length>; it does not assert a measured phase boundary. A vertical <temperature> axis would additionally require a material-specific relation $\chi(T)$.

Overlap means roughly one coil per coil <volume>. With segment <volume> $a^3$, the corresponding <polymer overlap volume fraction> is
$$
\phi^*\sim\frac{Na^3}{R_g^3}.
$$
For a strong <good solvent>, $R_g\sim aN^{3/5}$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\phi^*\sim N^{-4/5}=(10^4)^{-4/5}\simeq6.3\times10^{-4},}
$$
or about **0.063% by <volume>** in the unit-prefactor scaling convention. The segment <length> cancels. A coil has no sharply defined boundary, so using $4\pi R_g^3/3$ or retaining a size amplitude changes an order-one prefactor. This calculation is a scaling estimate, not an exact volume-fraction measurement.

The <correlation length> in a semidilute solution is the <concentration blob> size. Below it a chain retains isolated good-solvent statistics; beyond it concentration fluctuations are screened and many chains interpenetrate. It is a local scale, not the total chain <radius>. If a blob contains $g$ segments,
$$
\xi\sim ag^{3/5},\qquad \phi\sim\frac{ga^3}{\xi^3}.
$$
Eliminating $g$ gives the <good-solvent concentration-blob scaling>
$$
\boxed{g\sim\phi^{-5/4},\qquad\xi\sim a\phi^{-3/4}.}
$$
Using the supplied segment size and concentration,
$$
\boxed{\xi\simeq0.5\,\mathrm{nm}\,(0.01)^{-3/4}\simeq15.8\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Here $g\simeq316$ and $N/g\simeq32$, so there are many segments within each blob and several dozen blobs per chain. Also $\phi/\phi^*\simeq16$, consistent with a semidilute estimate rather than isolated coils. The cancellation of $N$ from $\xi$ is physical: once chains are long enough, the local concentration sets the mesh scale. As throughout the scaling argument, numerical amplitudes are not determined by the given data.

In a dilute solution, separate coils diffuse through solvent with long-ranged hydrodynamic coupling among their segments. The <Zimm model> gives whole-chain diffusion
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm dilute}\sim\frac{k_BT}{\eta_sR_H}\propto N^{-\nu},\qquad\nu\simeq3/5,}
$$
where $R_H$ is a <hydrodynamic radius> and $\eta_s$ the solvent <viscosity>. The coil continually changes shape while its <centre of mass> performs long-time <Brownian motion>; treating it as a rigid sphere is only a scaling analogy.

At high concentration, solvent-mediated <hydrodynamic interactions> are screened on sufficiently large scales and <times>. If chains are not entangled, additive local or blob friction gives <Rouse model> behaviour, $D\propto N^{-1}$ at fixed concentration. For long entangled chains, neighboring chains prevent rapid transverse escape. In the <reptation> tube picture the chain moves mainly along its contour until it renews the surrounding tube. Contour diffusion scales as $N^{-1}$, the tube-renewal <time> as $N^3$, and the random-coil size squared as $N$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm entangled}\sim\frac{R^2}{\tau_d}\propto N^{-2}.}
$$
Both cases ultimately give diffusive centre-of-mass motion, but entangled concentrated chains move much more slowly and retain constraints for longer. The inequality $\phi\gg\phi^*$ establishes strong overlap, not by itself entanglement: the latter also requires sufficiently many segments relative to an entanglement <length>. No numerical entanglement scale or concentrated-solution <diffusion coefficient> is supplied, so only the conditional regimes and scaling exponents can be fixed here.

In <linear viscoelasticity>, <stress> is a linear functional of past <strain> increments: scaling the deformation scales the response, and responses to small deformations add. For a time-translation-invariant material, <Boltzmann superposition> writes $\sigma(t)=\int_{-\infty}^tG(t-s)\dot\epsilon(s)ds$, where $G$ is a <relaxation modulus>. The material combines reversible storage with delayed or dissipative response; the description is restricted to small deformations about the chosen state.

The <Maxwell model> places an elastic <spring> and viscous <dashpot> in series. They carry the same <shear stress> $\sigma$, and their <shear strains> add:
$$
\epsilon=\epsilon_e+\epsilon_v,\qquad
\epsilon_e=\frac\sigma{G_0},\qquad
\dot\epsilon_v=\frac\sigma\eta.
$$
Differentiating gives
$$
\boxed{\dot\epsilon=\frac{\dot\sigma}{G_0}+\frac\sigma\eta,\qquad\tau=\frac\eta{G_0}.}
$$
Here $\epsilon$ is dimensionless <shear strain>, $\sigma$ <shear stress> in pascals, $G_0$ the <spring> <shear modulus>, $\eta$ <dashpot> <viscosity> in $\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, and $\tau$ the <relaxation time>. The first term describes changing elastic <strain>; the second is viscous flow. In this model the material flows at long <times> and has zero static <shear modulus>.

A <relaxation modulus> is measured by imposing a step of <strain> and then holding that <strain> fixed. Let its amplitude be $\epsilon_0$, so the initial <stress> jump is $\sigma_0=G_0\epsilon_0$. For $t>0$, $\dot\epsilon=0$ and the <Maxwell model> gives
$$
\dot\sigma=-\frac{G_0}{\eta}\sigma,
\qquad\sigma(t)=\sigma_0e^{-t/\tau},\qquad
\boxed{G(t)=\frac{\sigma(t)}{\epsilon_0}=G_0e^{-t/\tau},\quad\tau=\eta/G_0.}
$$
The initial <stress> in the wording must be understood as that produced by the initial deformation. If an external apparatus instead maintains $\sigma=\sigma_0$, the <stress> does not decay: the experiment is creep, considered below. Merely specifying an initial <stress> without a subsequent constraint would not uniquely prescribe the relaxation experiment.

Choose complex amplitudes proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$ and define $\widetilde\sigma=G^*(\omega)\widetilde\epsilon$. The <Maxwell model> gives
$$
i\omega\widetilde\epsilon=\left(\frac{i\omega}{G_0}+\frac1\eta\right)\widetilde\sigma,
\qquad G^*=G_0\frac{i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}.
$$
Rationalizing the denominator yields the <storage modulus> and <loss modulus>:
$$
\boxed{G'=G_0\frac{(\omega\tau)^2}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\qquad
G''=G_0\frac{\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.}
$$
For $\omega\tau\ll1$, $G''\simeq\eta\omega$ dominates and $G'\simeq G_0\omega^2\tau^2$ is small: the material has <time> to flow. For $\omega\tau\gg1$, $G'\to G_0$ and $G''\simeq G_0/(\omega\tau)$: the <dashpot> scarcely deforms during a cycle and the response is mainly elastic. At $\omega\tau=1$, $G'=G''=G_0/2$, and $G''$ is maximal. The <stress> leads <strain> by $\delta=\arctan(G''/G')$, ranging from nearly $\pi/2$ to nearly zero.

For a fixed <strain> amplitude $\epsilon_a$, dissipation per cycle is $\pi G''\epsilon_a^2$. A peak in $G''$ therefore concerns that normalization. At fixed <stress> amplitude $\sigma_a$, it is instead $\pi\sigma_a^2/(\eta\omega)$, so it has no finite-frequency maximum in this ideal model. Reversing the Fourier <time> convention reverses the imaginary-part sign, while the physical dissipated <energy> remains positive.

A creep experiment applies a small step of <stress> and holds it constant while recording <strain>. For $\sigma=\sigma_0H(t)$, the series <spring> jumps by $\sigma_0/G_0$ and the <dashpot> subsequently flows at constant rate $\sigma_0/\eta$. The <creep compliance> is thus
$$
J(t)=\frac{\epsilon(t)}{\sigma_0}=\frac1{G_0}+\frac t\eta,\qquad t>0.
$$
Using the stipulated effective <stress> and <strain> substitutions gives
$$
\frac{\langle r^2(t)\rangle}{a^2}
=\frac{k_BT}{\pi a^3}\left(\frac1{G_0}+\frac t\eta\right),
\qquad
\boxed{\langle r^2(t)\rangle_{\rm model}=\frac{k_BT}{\pi aG_0}+\frac{k_BT}{\pi a\eta}\,t.}
$$
On linear axes the model is a straight line of positive intercept $A=k_BT/(\pi aG_0)$ and slope $A/\tau$. On logarithmic axes it has an approximate elastic plateau for $t\ll\tau$ and slope one for $t\gg\tau$, within the <time> window where the continuum approximation applies. The long-time slope is $6D$ with $D=k_BT/(6\pi\eta a)$, agreeing with the <Stokes–Einstein relation>.

There is an essential zero-lag convention. A literal lag <displacement> $\Delta\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r(t_0+t)-\mathbf r(t_0)$ has $\langle|\Delta\mathbf r(0)|^2\rangle=0$. The nonzero intercept above is the spring-compliance extrapolation of the idealized <microrheology of a Maxwell fluid>, after unresolved solvent or inertial transients. It must not be interpreted as an instantaneous finite physical <displacement> at exactly zero lag. The stated mesh condition motivates a continuum probe response; quantitative use is more reliable for $a\gg m$ than merely $a>m$.

For a thermally elastic network, estimate one elastically active element per mesh <volume>. Each contributes an elastic free-energy scale of order $k_BT$, giving the <entropic network modulus>
$$
\boxed{G_0\sim\frac{k_BT}{m^3}\simeq3.3\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{Pa}}
$$
at $T=298\,\mathrm K$. Mesh size alone would not determine the modulus of an arbitrary bending-dominated or athermal network; this is the entropic scaling assumption needed for the estimate.

The formal elastic intercept in the given model is consequently
$$
\boxed{\langle r^2\rangle_{\rm plateau}\sim\frac{m^3}{\pi a}
=3.98\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m^2}=0.0398\,\mu\mathrm m^2.}
$$
The corresponding three-dimensional root-mean-square <displacement> is about $0.20\,\mu\mathrm m$. The <temperature> cancels from this estimate because the assumed modulus is itself proportional to $T$. This is the extrapolated plateau from the preceding part, not a nonzero exact zero-lag <displacement>. Here $m/a=1/2$, so a continuum estimate is only approximate.

In <passive microrheology>, track dilute inert probe spheres by video microscopy and average squared lag <displacements> over starting <times> and probes. An isotropic two-dimensional image gives $\langle\Delta r^2\rangle_{3d}=(3/2)\langle\Delta r^2\rangle_{2d}$. Alternatively, <dynamic light scattering> probes ensemble <displacement> statistics; the original optical <microrheology> study compared inferred storage and loss moduli with mechanical measurements.<Optical Measurements of Frequency-Dependent Linear Viscoelastic Moduli of Complex Fluids by T. G. Mason and D. A. Weitz>[Physical Review Letters 74 (1995), p. 1250, abstract: dynamic light scattering and comparison with mechanical moduli]{ref}

Remove drift, calibrate spatial scale and frame timing, estimate localization noise using an immobilized-bead control, and account for motion blur. Keep probes away from walls and check that changing probe <radius> or tracer concentration does not change the inferred bulk response unexpectedly. Noise alone can generate a false short-time intercept, while adsorption or confinement can mimic <elasticity>. No numerical long-time slope is fixed because a numerical <viscosity> is not supplied.
