=== 1

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Ramsey number> $R(s,t)$ is the least <integer> $N$ such that every <graph> on $N$ <vertices> contains either a <clique> of size $s$ or an <independent set> of size $t$. Equivalently, every red-blue <colouring> of the <edges> of $K_N$ contains a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_t$.

Put $a=R(s-1,t)$, $b=R(s,t-1)$ and consider a <graph> on $a+b$ <vertices>. Fix a <vertex> $v$. If it has at least $a$ <neighbours>, their induced <graph> contains either a <clique> of size $s-1$, which together with $v$ gives a <clique> of size $s$, or an <independent set> of size $t$. Otherwise $v$ has at least $b$ nonneighbours. Their induced <graph> contains either a <clique> of size $s$, or an <independent set> of size $t-1$, which can be enlarged by $v$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R(s,t)\leq R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1).}
$$
Since $R(2,3)=R(3,2)=3$, this gives $R(3,3)\leq6$. For the reverse bound, the <cycle graph>[five-cycle] has no <triangle in a graph>[triangle] and no <independent set> of size three: its <complement> is again a <cycle graph>[five-cycle], so it too is triangle-free. Hence the property fails on five <vertices> and
$$
\boxed{R(3,3)=6.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <vertex> version of <Menger's theorem> states that for two distinct nonadjacent <vertices> $s,t$ of a finite <graph>, the <maximum> number of internally vertex-disjoint $s$-$t$ <graph paths> equals the <minimum> size of a <set> $S\subseteq V\setminus\{s,t\}$ whose deletion separates $s$ from $t$.
$$
\boxed{\max\{\text{internally disjoint }s\text{-}t\text{ paths}\}=\min\{|S|:s,t\text{ disconnected in }G-S\}.}
$$
Internally disjoint <graph paths> may share their endpoints but no other <vertex>. The nonadjacency hypothesis is needed in this separator formulation, since an <edge> $st$ cannot be destroyed by deleting other <vertices>.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Let the <bipartition> be $L\cup R$. Form an auxiliary <graph> by adding a new <vertex> $s$ adjacent to every <vertex> of $L$ and a new <vertex> $t$ adjacent to every <vertex> of $R$. The <vertices> $s,t$ are nonadjacent, so <Menger's theorem> applies.

A <matching> of size $m$ gives $m$ internally disjoint <graph paths> $s\ell rt$, one for each <matching> <edge> $\ell r$. Conversely, every $s$-$t$ <graph path> must use an <edge> of the original <bipartite graph>. Choose one such <edge> from each member of an internally disjoint family. Their endpoints are all distinct, so the chosen <edges> form a <matching>. Thus the <maximum> number of those <graph paths> is the <maximum matching> size.

An $s$-$t$ separator contained in $L\cup R$ is exactly a <vertex cover> of the original <graph>. Indeed, an uncovered <edge> $\ell r$ would leave the <graph path> $s\ell rt$, while removing a <vertex cover> removes every <edge> between $L$ and $R$ and hence every $s$-$t$ <graph path>. The <minimum> separator size is therefore the <minimum> <vertex cover> size. Menger gives
$$
\boxed{\nu(G)=\tau(G)\quad\text{for bipartite }G.}
$$
This is <Kőnig's theorem for bipartite matching>. The auxiliary construction gives the required deduction from <vertex-disjoint paths> rather than assuming a <matching> theorem in advance.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

<Hall's marriage theorem> says that a <bipartite graph> with parts $L,R$ has a <matching> saturating $L$ if and only if
$$
\boxed{|N(S)|\geq|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq L.}
$$
Necessity follows because the matched <neighbours> of <vertices> of $S$ are distinct and belong to $N(S)$.

For sufficiency, assume these <inequalities>. Let $K=U\cup W$ be any <vertex cover>, with $U\subseteq L$ and $W\subseteq R$. Every <neighbour> of $S=L\setminus U$ must belong to $W$, since otherwise its <edge> from $S$ is uncovered. Therefore $|W|\geq|N(S)|\geq|S|$, and $|K|\geq|U|+|S|=|L|$. The whole part $L$ is itself a <vertex cover> of size $|L|$. Part (ii) now gives a <matching> of size exactly $|L|$, so it saturates $L$.

===== i

====== Solution

Choose a <spanning tree> of the <connectivity (graph theory)>[connected] <graph> and <root> it at $x$. List <vertices> in nonincreasing order of their <distance> from $x$ in this <tree>, breaking ties arbitrarily. The <root> has <distance> zero and is last, so $v_n=x$.

Every other <vertex> has a parent one step closer to the <root>. That parent is adjacent to it and appears later in the ordering. Hence
$$
\boxed{\forall i<n\ \exists j>i:\ v_iv_j\in E(H).}
$$
For a <graph> of one <vertex> the condition is vacuous and the same ordering works.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Write $\Delta=\Delta(G)$, which is at least two because $x$ has the two specified <neighbours>. Give $y$ and $z$ the same colour, say colour one; this is proper because they are nonadjacent.

Apply part (i) to the <connectivity (graph theory)>[connected] <graph> $G\setminus\{y,z\}$, with $x$ last. Colour its <vertices> greedily in the resulting order using a palette of $\Delta$ colours. Every <vertex> before $x$ has a <neighbour> later in this order, so at most $\deg(v)-1\leq\Delta-1$ of its <neighbours> have already received colours. At least one palette colour is available.

When $x$ is reached, all its <neighbours> are coloured, but the two <neighbours> $y,z$ have the same colour. Thus there are at most $\deg(x)-1\leq\Delta-1$ distinct forbidden colours. Colour $x$ with a remaining one. This constructs a proper <colouring> with
$$
\boxed{\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G).}
$$
The common colour on the two initial <vertices> replaces the usual need for an uncoloured <neighbour> at the last greedy step.

This is <greedy colouring with two precoloured neighbours>.

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Take a proper <colouring> $c:V(G)\to\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and orient every <edge> from its lower-coloured endpoint towards its higher-coloured endpoint. Along a <directed path> the colour strictly increases, so such a <graph path> has at most $k$ <vertices>. A <directed cycle> is impossible for the same reason. Thus
$$
\boxed{D\text{ is acyclic and }\lambda(D)\leq k.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

For each <vertex> $v$, let $c(v)$ be the <maximum> number of <vertices> in a <directed path> ending at $v$. These numbers lie between one and $\ell$.

If $u\to v$ is an <edge>, a longest <graph path> ending at $u$ cannot already contain $v$, since that would give a <directed cycle> on appending $u\to v$. Appending it therefore creates a <graph path> ending at $v$, so $c(v)\geq c(u)+1$. Adjacent <vertices> consequently receive different colours. Hence
$$
\boxed{c\text{ is a proper colouring using at most }\ell\text{ colours}.}
$$
Acyclicity is exactly what justifies extending the <graph path> without repeating a <vertex>.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

Part (ii) shows that $\chi(G)\leq\lambda(D)$ for every <acyclic orientation> $D$, so $\chi(G)$ is at most their <minimum>. Part (i), applied to an optimal <colouring>, supplies an <acyclic orientation> with $\lambda(D)\leq\chi(G)$. Combining the <inequalities> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi(G)=\min_{D\text{ acyclic orientation of }G}\lambda(D).}
$$
The parameter counts <vertices> on a <graph path>; if <graph path> length were instead counted in <edges>, the corresponding <minimum> would be $\chi(G)-1$.

These constructions prove <chromatic number from acyclic longest paths>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix a proper <colouring> with exactly $k$ nonempty <colour classes>. For any class $C_i$, suppose every <vertex> in it misses at least one of the other $k-1$ colours in its <neighbourhood>. Recolour each of these <vertices> by one of its missing colours. No <edge> joins two <vertices> of $C_i$, so these simultaneous recolourings cannot conflict with one another, and the chosen colours do not conflict with <vertices> outside $C_i$.

This would eliminate colour $i$ and produce a $(k-1)$-colouring, contradicting optimality. Therefore each <colour class> contains a <vertex> adjacent to at least one <vertex> of every other colour. That <vertex> has degree at least $k-1$. Choosing one from each class gives $k$ distinct <vertices>, proving
$$
\boxed{\bigl|\{v:\deg(v)\geq k-1\}\bigr|\geq k.}
$$
For $k=1$, a nonempty <graph> already has a <vertex> of degree at least zero, so the conclusion holds as well.

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

For disjoint nonempty <sets> $X,Y$, let $e(X,Y)$ count <edges> between them. Their <edge> density is
$$
\boxed{d(X,Y)=\frac{e(X,Y)}{|X||Y|}.}
$$
The pair is $\varepsilon$-regular if every $A\subseteq X$ and $B\subseteq Y$ with $|A|\geq\varepsilon|X|$, $|B|\geq\varepsilon|Y|$ satisfies $|d(A,B)-d(X,Y)|\leq\varepsilon$.

An $\varepsilon$-regular partition has disjoint classes $V_0,V_1,\ldots,V_k$ covering $V(G)$, with $|V_0|\leq\varepsilon|V(G)|$, equal positive sizes $|V_1|=\cdots=|V_k|$, and at most $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered pairs $(V_i,V_j)$ with $1\leq i<j\leq k$ that are not $\varepsilon$-regular. The <set> $V_0$ is exceptional and its pairs need not satisfy regularity.

<Szemerédi regularity lemma> states that for every $\varepsilon>0$ and <integer> $m_0\geq1$, there are <integers> $M,n_0$ such that every <graph> with $n\geq n_0$ <vertices> has such a partition with
$$
\boxed{m_0\leq k\leq M.}
$$
The bounds depend on $\varepsilon,m_0$, not on the <graph> order. Using a strict regularity <inequality> or a slightly different <constant> in the allowed pair count gives an equivalent conventional version; the displayed convention will be used below.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $B\subseteq X$ be the <vertices> with fewer than $(d/2)|Y|$ <neighbours> in $Y$. If $|B|\geq\varepsilon|X|$, regularity applies to the pair $(B,Y)$ and gives
$$
d(B,Y)\geq d(X,Y)-\varepsilon\geq d-\varepsilon\geq d/2.
$$
But averaging the defining strict degree <inequality> over $B$ gives $d(B,Y)<d/2$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|B|<\varepsilon|X|.}
$$
Regularity controls the <neighbourhood> of almost every <vertex> because a large <set> of exceptional low-degree <vertices> would itself witness an irregular density.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Apply part (b) to $(X_1,X_2)$ and $(X_1,X_3)$. Fewer than $2\varepsilon|X_1|$ <vertices> fail at least one of the degree bounds. For any remaining <vertex> $v$, put $N_2=N(v)\cap X_2$ and $N_3=N(v)\cap X_3$. Then
$$
|N_2|\geq\frac d2|X_2|\geq\varepsilon|X_2|,\qquad
|N_3|\geq\frac d2|X_3|\geq\varepsilon|X_3|.
$$
Regularity of $(X_2,X_3)$ gives $d(N_2,N_3)\geq d-\varepsilon\geq d/2$. Every <edge> between $N_2$ and $N_3$ makes a distinct <triangle in a graph>[triangle] containing $v$. Hence $v$ belongs to at least
$$
\frac d2|N_2||N_3|\geq\frac{d^3}8|X_2||X_3|
$$
such <triangle in a graph>[triangles]. This proves the asserted bound on exceptional <vertices>.

There are at least $(1-2\varepsilon)|X_1|\geq|X_1|/2$ good <vertices>. Summing their <triangle in a graph>[triangle] counts counts each <triangle in a graph>[triangle] with one <vertex> in each cluster exactly once, at its $X_1$ <vertex>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{triangles in }G\}\geq\frac{d^3}{16}|X_1||X_2||X_3|.}
$$
There is no factor of three to divide out in this cross-cluster count.

This is the <regular triangle counting lemma> with explicit coarse constants.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a small density threshold $d>0$, then $\varepsilon\leq\min(d/2,1/4)$ sufficiently small and a lower partition bound $m_0$ sufficiently large that
$$
2\varepsilon+\frac1{2m_0}+\frac d2<\eta.
$$
Apply the regularity lemma. Delete all <edges> incident with $V_0$, all <edges> within a class $V_i$, all <edges> between <irregular pairs>, and all <edges> between <regular pairs> of density below $d$. Their total is at most
$$
\varepsilon n^2+\frac{n^2}{2k}+\varepsilon n^2+\frac d2 n^2<\eta n^2.
$$
If a <triangle in a graph>[triangle] remains, its <vertices> lie in three distinct nonexceptional classes, all whose pairs were regular with original density at least $d$. Part (c) then forces at least $(d^3/16)t^3$ <triangle in a graph>[triangles] in the original <graph>, where $t$ is the common class size. Since $k\leq M$ and $\varepsilon\leq1/4$, we have $t\geq n/(2M)$, so this is at least $d^3n^3/(128M^3)$.

Choose $c=d^3/(256M^3)$ and take $n_0$ large enough for the regularity lemma. An original <graph> with at most $cn^3$ <triangle in a graph>[triangles] cannot leave any <triangle in a graph>[triangle] after these deletions. Thus **deleting at most $\eta n^2$ <edges> makes the <graph> triangle-free**. This is the <triangle removal lemma>; boundedly many large regular clusters turn any surviving <triangle in a graph>[triangle] into a positive-density family of original <triangle in a graph>[triangles].

==== a

===== Solution

<Markov's inequality> states that for $Y\geq0$ and $t>0$, $\mathbb P(Y\geq t)\leq\mathbb E Y/t$. It follows by taking expectations in $Y\geq t\mathbf1_{\{Y\geq t\}}$.

If $X$ has finite <mean> $\mu$ and <variance> $\sigma^2$, apply Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$ to obtain <Chebyshev's inequality>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|X-\mu|\geq t)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{t^2}\qquad(t>0).}
$$
For the <first moment method>, assume $\mu_n=\mathbb E X_n\to0$. Nonnegative <integer> values give $\{X_n>0\}=\{X_n\geq1\}$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_n>0)\leq\mathbb E X_n\longrightarrow0.}
$$
For a standard <second moment method>, suppose $\mu_n>0$ eventually, the <second moments> are finite, and $\mathbb E X_n^2/\mu_n^2\to1$. Equivalently, $\operatorname{Var}(X_n)/\mu_n^2\to0$. The zero <event> is contained in $\{|X_n-\mu_n|\geq\mu_n\}$, so
$$
\mathbb P(X_n=0)\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X_n)}{\mu_n^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence $\boxed{\mathbb P(X_n>0)\to1}$. The second-moment condition controls the fluctuations relative to the <mean>; a large <mean> alone is insufficient.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $X$ count the four-vertex <sets> spanning all four of their possible triples. For each such <set> $S$, write $I_S$ for its <indicator>. <Independence> of the four <hyperedges> gives
$$
\mu=\mathbb E X=\binom n4p^4.
$$
If $np\to0$, then $\mu\leq (np)^4/24\to0$, and the <first moment method> shows that the <probability> of a four-clique tends to zero.

For the other direction, consider two distinct four-sets. If they meet in at most two <vertices>, they have no possible triple in common, so their indicators are <independent>. If they meet in three <vertices>, they share exactly one triple, so $\mathbb E(I_SI_T)=p^7$ and their <covariance> is at most $p^7$. There are $O(n^5)$ ordered pairs of this latter type. The diagonal variances sum to at most $\mu$, hence
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)\leq\mu+O(n^5p^7).
$$
For large $n$, $\mu$ is a <constant> multiple of $n^4p^4$, and consequently
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\mu^2}
=O\left(\frac1{n^4p^4}+\frac1{n^3p}\right).
$$
If $np\to\infty$, the first term tends to zero, and so does the second since $n^3p=n^2(np)\to\infty$. The <second moment method> proves that a four-clique exists with <probability> tending to one. Therefore a <threshold function> is
$$
\boxed{p^*(n)=n^{-1}.}
$$
The shared-triple overlap, rather than overlaps in one or two <vertices>, is the only off-diagonal dependence that affects this computation.

This is the <four-clique threshold in a binomial 3-uniform hypergraph>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The requested scale is $\boxed{k=\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})}$. To see its first-moment origin, the number $X_k$ of $k$-cliques has <expectation>
$$
\mathbb E X_k=\binom nk2^{-\binom k3}.
$$
For $k$ on the proposed scale, taking base-two <logarithms> gives
$$
\log_2\mathbb E X_k=k\log_2 n-\frac{k^3}6+O(k^2+k\log k).
$$
The leading terms balance at $k\approx\sqrt{6\log_2 n}$. In particular, for any fixed $c>\sqrt6$, taking $k$ near $c\sqrt{\log_2 n}$ makes the <expectation> tend to zero, so such <cliques> are absent with <probability> tending to one.

Thus the first-moment estimate places the largest-clique scale at a <constant> multiple of $\sqrt{\log n}$, as requested. A diverging <expectation> below that balance does not itself prove existence; a corresponding probabilistic lower-bound proof would require an additional argument and is not part of the requested first-moment estimate.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A <independence digraph of events>[dependency digraph] has <vertices> $1,\ldots,n$ and no loops. For each $i$, the <event> $A_i$ must be <independent> of the <sigma-algebra> generated jointly by all $A_j$ with $j\neq i$ for which there is no <edge> $i\to j$. Joint <independence> from that collection is required; <pairwise independence> alone does not suffice.

Write $\Gamma^+(i)$ for the outneighbours of $i$, and $B_S=\bigcap_{j\in S}A_j^c$. We prove by <induction> on $|S|$ that $B_S$ has positive <probability> and, when $i\notin S$,
$$
\mathbb P(A_i\mid B_S)\leq x_i.
$$
For $S=\varnothing$, the asserted conditional bound follows from the hypothesis, since its product factors are at most one. For the <induction> step, positivity for a <set> of size $r$ follows by writing its avoidance <probability> as a chain product: all preceding conditioning <sets> have size below $r$, so each factor is at least $1-x_j>0$.

Now take $|S|=r$, split $S_1=S\cap\Gamma^+(i)$ and $S_2=S\setminus\Gamma^+(i)$, and condition first on $B_{S_2}$. <Independence> gives $\mathbb P(A_i\mid B_{S_2})=\mathbb P(A_i)$. Moreover, order the elements of $S_1$ and use the inductive conditional bounds on the successive avoidance factors. Their conditioning <sets> have size at most $r-1$, so
$$
\mathbb P(B_{S_1}\mid B_{S_2})\geq\prod_{j\in S_1}(1-x_j).
$$
This remains true when $S_1$ is empty, with both sides one. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(A_i\mid B_S)
&=\frac{\mathbb P(A_i\cap B_{S_1}\mid B_{S_2})}{\mathbb P(B_{S_1}\mid B_{S_2})}\\
&\leq\frac{\mathbb P(A_i)}{\prod_{j\in S_1}(1-x_j)}\\
&\leq x_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma^+(i)\setminus S_1}(1-x_j)\leq x_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <induction> is complete. Finally expand the <probability> of avoiding all <events> in the order $1,\ldots,n$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^nA_i^c\right)
=\prod_{i=1}^n\left(1-\mathbb P\left(A_i\,\middle|\,\bigcap_{j<i}A_j^c\right)\right)
\geq\prod_{i=1}^n(1-x_i)>0.}
$$
This proves the asymmetric <Lovász local lemma>, including the positivity needed to make every conditioning step legitimate.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The symmetric <Lovász local lemma> states: if each bad <event> has <probability> at most $p$, each <event> is <independent> of the collection of all but at most $d$ other <events>, and
$$
\boxed{ep(d+1)\leq1,}
$$
then the <probability> that none of the bad <events> occurs is positive. <Independence> is from the <sigma-algebra> generated by the indicated nonneighbours, as in part (a). A sufficient sharper parameter condition is $p\leq x(1-x)^d$ for some $x\in(0,1)$; taking $x=1/(d+1)$ gives the displayed standard bound when $d\geq1$. When $d=0$, mutual <independence> directly gives the conclusion under the displayed bound.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

If there are no <triangle in a graph>[triangles], any <edge colouring> works. Otherwise $\Delta\geq2$. Put $q=\lceil3\sqrt\Delta\rceil$ and colour each <edge> independently and uniformly from $q$ colours. For each <triangle in a graph>[triangle] $T$, let $A_T$ be the <event> that its three <edges> have one colour. Then
$$
\mathbb P(A_T)=q\left(\frac1q\right)^3=\frac1{q^2}.
$$
<Events> for <triangle in a graph>[triangles] sharing no <edge> depend on disjoint edge-colour variables, so each <event> is jointly <independent> of the collection of all such nonneighbours. Each <edge> of a <triangle in a graph>[triangle] lies in at most $\Delta-2$ other <triangle in a graph>[triangles]: a common <neighbour> of its endpoints must be one of at most $\Delta-1$ <neighbours> besides the other endpoint, and one of these is the third <vertex> of $T$ itself. Thus the dependency degree is at most $d=3(\Delta-2)$.

Because $q^2\geq9\Delta$,
$$
e\,\frac1{q^2}(d+1)\leq\frac{e(3\Delta-5)}{9\Delta}<\frac e3<1.
$$
The symmetric local lemma gives positive <probability> of avoiding every monochromatic <triangle in a graph>[triangle]. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lceil3\sqrt\Delta\rceil\text{ colours suffice}.}
$$
For an edgeless <graph> the empty <colouring> handles even the zero-colour case $\Delta=0$.

=== 6
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A <Galton-Watson process> starts with one ancestor, $X_0=1$. Given generation size $X_r$, define
$$
X_{r+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{X_r}Z_{r,j},
$$
where all offspring variables are <independent> with common distribution $Z$. The empty sum is zero, so zero is absorbing. Its <extinction> <probability> is
$$
\eta=\mathbb P(\exists r:X_r=0)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\mathbb P(X_r=0).
$$
For the <probability generating function> $f(s)=\mathbb E[s^Z]$, the allowed fixed-point characterization is
$$
\boxed{\eta\text{ is the smallest solution in }[0,1]\text{ of }f(s)=s.}
$$
For binomial offspring, $f(s)=(1-p+ps)^k$, with $f(1)=1$, $f'(1)=kp$, and $f$ <convex> on $[0,1]$.

First assume $0<p<1$. If $k=0$, <extinction> occurs immediately. For $k=1$, $f(s)-s=(1-p)(1-s)>0$ for $s<1$, so the only <fixed point> is one. For $k\geq2$, <strict convexity> gives
$$
f(s)>f(1)+f'(1)(s-1)=1+kp(s-1)\qquad(0\leq s<1).
$$
If $kp\leq1$, the right-hand side is at least $s$, so no <fixed point> below one exists and $\eta=1$. If $kp>1$, $f(s)-s<0$ for $s$ just below one, whereas $f(0)>0$. <Continuity> supplies a <fixed point> in $(0,1)$; hence $\eta<1$. Thus in the nondegenerate parameter range,
$$
\boxed{0<p<1:\quad\eta<1\iff kp>1.}
$$
For completeness, if binomial endpoint parameters are allowed, $p=0$ or $k=0$ gives $\eta=1$, while $p=1$ and $k\geq1$ gives deterministic positive offspring and $\eta=0$. In particular $(k,p)=(1,1)$ survives forever despite <mean> one. Over $k\in\{0,1,\ldots\}$ and $p\in[0,1]$, the full condition is
$$
\boxed{\eta<1\iff kp>1\text{ or }(k,p)=(1,1).}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Explore the family <tree> one individual at a time, maintaining a <queue> of individuals not yet explored. Initially its size is $Y_0=1$. Exploring individual $t$ removes it and adds its <independent> offspring $Z_t$, so before the <queue> empties,
$$
Y_t=1+\sum_{j=1}^t(Z_j-1).
$$
One may define the <independent increments> indefinitely, but the <tree> exploration stops at $T=\inf\{t:Y_t=0\}$. This <hitting time> is the <total progeny>, including the ancestor. Since each increment is at least $-1$, a <queue> cannot jump below zero before hitting zero. If it never hits zero, the family has infinitely many individuals.

Suppose $kp<1$. A <Chernoff bound> in moment-generating-function form says that for <independent> $W_j$ and $\theta>0$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\sum_{j=1}^nW_j\geq0\right)\leq\prod_{j=1}^n\mathbb E e^{\theta W_j}.
$$
Here take $W_j=Z_j-1$, with
$$
M(\theta)=\mathbb E e^{\theta(Z-1)}=e^{-\theta}(1-p+pe^\theta)^k.
$$
We have $M(0)=1$ and $M'(0)=kp-1<0$, so some $\theta>0$ has $M(\theta)<1$. Let $c=-\log M(\theta)>0$. If $T>n$, the <queue> is still positive after $n$ explorations; its <integer> value is at least one, giving $\sum_{j=1}^n(Z_j-1)\geq0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(T>n)\leq M(\theta)^n=e^{-cn},\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
This proves the claimed bound without appealing to a general branching-process tail theorem.

The argument proves the more general <exponential total progeny tail for bounded subcritical offspring>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

First take $0<p<1$ and $kp>1$. Part (a) gives an <extinction> <probability> $\eta\in(0,1)$. Write $p_j=\mathbb P(Z=j)$. Conditional on the ancestor having $j$ children, <extinction> requires <extinction> of all $j$ <independent> descendant trees and has <probability> $\eta^j$. Bayes' rule therefore gives the conditional offspring law
$$
\widetilde p_j=\mathbb P(Z=j\mid\text{extinction})=p_j\eta^{j-1}.
$$
Its sum is $f(\eta)/\eta=1$. Conditional on <extinction> of all descendant trees, those trees remain <independent>, each with its own extinction-conditioned law. Thus the entire conditioned process is a Galton–Watson process with offspring generating function
$$
\widetilde f(s)=\frac{f(\eta s)}\eta.
$$
For binomial offspring, using $\eta=(1-p+p\eta)^k$ gives
$$
\boxed{\widetilde Z\sim\operatorname{Bi}(k,\widetilde p),\qquad
\widetilde p=\frac{p\eta}{1-p+p\eta}.}
$$
Its <mean> is $k\widetilde p=f'(\eta)<1$. Indeed, $f(s)-s$ is <strictly convex> and has its two zeros at $\eta$ and one, so its <derivative> at the first zero is strictly negative. This proves subcriticality directly from the generating function.

Let $T$ be <total progeny>, taking value infinity on survival. Apply part (b) to the conditioned process. For some $c>0$,
$$
\boxed{1-\eta\leq\mathbb P(T>n)
=(1-\eta)+\eta\,\mathbb P(\widetilde T>n)
\leq1-\eta+\eta e^{-cn}.}
$$
Thus the unconditional tail tends to the positive survival <probability> $1-\eta$, with an exponentially small finite-extinction contribution. It does not decay to zero.

If $p=1$ and $kp>1$, <extinction> has <probability> zero and conditioning on it does not specify a <probability> law. In that deterministic case <total progeny> is infinite and $\mathbb P(T>n)=1$ for every finite $n$.

The nondegenerate law above is the <binomial branching process conditioned on extinction>.
