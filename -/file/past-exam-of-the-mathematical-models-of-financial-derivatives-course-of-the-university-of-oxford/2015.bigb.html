= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Write $R=e^r$, $u=4/3$ and $d=2/3$. Since both moves have positive physical <probability>, the <binomial options pricing model> is free of <arbitrage> exactly when
$$
\boxed{\frac23<e^r<\frac43.}
$$
If $R\leq d$, borrowing the share price and buying one share gives terminal gain $S_t(u-R)$ or $S_t(d-R)$, both nonnegative and at least one positive. If $R\geq u$, shorting one share and investing its proceeds gives the opposite comparison, with gain $S_t(R-u)$ or $S_t(R-d)$. Equality at either endpoint still permits <arbitrage> because the other outcome has positive <probability>.

Conversely, strict inequalities give a <risk-neutral probability> $q=(R-d)/(u-d)\in(0,1)$, for which the discounted share is a <martingale>. Any zero-cost <self-financing portfolio> with nonnegative terminal value has zero discounted <expectation> under this equivalent positive-probability law and hence cannot have a strictly positive payoff in either state. Applying this argument conditionally at each node proves <no arbitrage> over the tree.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

A <replicating strategy> holds $\Delta$ shares and a current cash amount $b$ in the <bank account>. Matching the two next values gives
$$
\Delta S^u_{t+1}+Rb=V^u_{t+1},\qquad
\Delta S^d_{t+1}+Rb=V^d_{t+1}.
$$
Solving this <linear system>,
$$
\Delta=\frac{V^u_{t+1}-V^d_{t+1}}{S^u_{t+1}-S^d_{t+1}},\qquad
b=\frac{S^u_{t+1}V^d_{t+1}-S^d_{t+1}V^u_{t+1}}{R(S^u_{t+1}-S^d_{t+1})}.
$$
By <no arbitrage>, the claim costs $V_t=\Delta S_t+b$. Collecting the coefficients of its two future values yields
$$
\boxed{V_t=R^{-1}\bigl[qV^u_{t+1}+(1-q)V^d_{t+1}\bigr],\qquad
q=\frac{RS_t-S^d_{t+1}}{S^u_{t+1}-S^d_{t+1}}.}
$$
The physical <probability> $p$ does not appear: replication fixes the price of this <contingent claim> from traded prices.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <risk-neutral probability> obeys $R=qu+(1-q)d$. Thus
$$
R=\frac23\frac43+\frac13\frac23=\frac{10}{9},
\qquad R^{-1}=\frac9{10}.
$$
Substitution into the <risk-neutral pricing> recursion gives
$$
\boxed{V_t=\frac9{10}\left(\frac23V^u_{t+1}+\frac13V^d_{t+1}\right)
=\frac3{10}\left(2V^u_{t+1}+V^d_{t+1}\right).}
$$

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Using the <risk-neutral probability> $q=2/3$ from part <1/c>, the <stock> tree has values $9$ initially, $12,6$ after one step, and $16,8,4$ after two. The <American put option> has terminal <financial payoffs> $0,1,5$. Work backwards, comparing the immediate <exercise payoff> with its discounted continuation value.

At $S_1=12$, continuation is $(3/10)(2\cdot0+1)=3/10$, exceeding the zero exercise payoff. At $S_1=6$, continuation is $(3/10)(2\cdot1+5)=21/10$, while exercise pays three. Therefore **exercise at the down node $S_1=6$ and continue at $S_1=12$**. The initial exercise payoff is zero and the continuation value is
$$
\boxed{P_0=\frac3{10}\left(2\cdot\frac3{10}+3\right)=\frac{27}{25}=1.08.}
$$
The <Snell envelope> gives this backwards maximum recursion. For comparison, restricting exercise to expiry would give $81/100$, so early exercise genuinely adds value.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=e}
{c}

For the <forward-start call option>, the strike is fixed by the intermediate <stock> price. The four path payoffs are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{path}&S_1&S_2&(S_2-S_1)^+\\\hline
uu&12&16&4\\
ud&12&8&0\\
du&6&8&2\\
dd&6&4&0
\end{array}
$$
The same final price eight has different payoffs on the two middle paths, so one must retain the history at expiry. At time one, <risk-neutral pricing> gives $C_1^u=(3/10)(2\cdot4)=12/5$ and $C_1^d=(3/10)(2\cdot2)=6/5$. Thus
$$
\boxed{C_0=\frac3{10}\left(2\cdot\frac{12}{5}+\frac65\right)=\frac95=1.8.}
$$
Equivalently, its value at the strike-fixing date is $S_1/5$ by homogeneity, so the <martingale> property of discounted <stock> prices gives the initial value $S_0/5$.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Let $\tau=T-t$. The <product rule> and <Itô's formula> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
d\bigl(e^{r(T-t)}V(S_t,t)\bigr)
&=e^{r\tau}\left[V_t+\frac12\sigma^2S_t^2V_{SS}+rS_tV_S-rV\right]dt
+e^{r\tau}\sigma S_tV_S\,dW_t\\
&=e^{r\tau}\sigma S_tV_S\,dW_t,
\end{aligned}
$$
where the <Black-Scholes equation> cancels the drift. Under the usual pricing growth and integrability conditions this <local martingale> is a <martingale>. Its terminal value is $f(S_T)$, since the exponential factor becomes one at $T$. Taking a <conditional expectation> gives
$$
e^{r(T-t)}V(S_t,t)=\mathbb E[f(S_T)\mid\mathcal F_t],\qquad
\boxed{V(S,t)=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb E[f(S_T)\mid S_t=S].}
$$
The dynamics already use the <risk-neutral measure>. This is the <Feynman-Kac formula> for the given pricing problem; a differential equation alone, without the relevant growth condition, would not justify upgrading a local martingale or uniqueness.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Apply <Itô's formula> to the <logarithm> of the positive <geometric Brownian motion>. The quadratic-variation correction gives
$$
d\log S_t=(r-\sigma^2/2)dt+\sigma dW_t.
$$
Integrating yields
$$
\boxed{S_T=S_t\exp\!\left[(r-\sigma^2/2)(T-t)+\sigma(W_T-W_t)\right].}
$$
The increment is a <Gaussian random variable> with <variance> $T-t$, independent of current information. Writing it as $W_\tau$ means a fresh future <Brownian motion> $\widetilde W_v=W_{t+v}-W_t$, evaluated at $\tau$; it need not equal the original process at time $T-t$.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

Take the terminal <financial payoff> $f(S)=S$. The function $V(S,t)=S$ satisfies the <Black-Scholes equation>: its time derivative and second stock derivative vanish, while $rSV_S-rV=rS-rS=0$. It also has the correct terminal value. Part <2/a> therefore gives
$$
S_t=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb E[S_T\mid\mathcal F_t],
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb E[e^{-rT}S_T\mid\mathcal F_t]=e^{-rt}S_t.}
$$
Thus the discounted <stock> is a <martingale>. The explicit <lognormal distribution> in part <2/b> supplies the necessary finite <expectation>, so here this is a true martingale identity.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Under the <risk-neutral measure>, set $a=\sigma\sqrt\tau$ and $m=\log(S/K)+(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau$. Then $\log(S_T/K)=m+aZ$ with $Z$ a <standard normal random variable>, and $d_-=m/a$. The <log-call option> is the discounted <Gaussian positive-part expectation>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
V(S,t)&=e^{-r\tau}\int_{-d_-}^{\infty}(m+az)\phi(z)\,dz\\
&=e^{-r\tau}\bigl[m\Phi(d_-)+a\phi(d_-)\bigr],
\end{aligned}
$$
since $\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=e^{-r\tau}\sigma\sqrt\tau
\left[d_-\operatorname N(d_-)+\frac{e^{-d_-^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right].}
$$
Here $\phi$ and $\Phi=\operatorname N$ are the <standard normal density> and <standard normal distribution function>. At expiry the continuous terminal <limit> is $(\log(S/K))^+$.

Let $a=\sigma\sqrt\tau$, so $d_+=d_-+a$. Squaring gives
$$
d_+^2-d_-^2=2ad_-+a^2=2\log(S/K)+2r\tau.
$$
Exponentiation proves
$$
\boxed{Se^{-d_+^2/2}=Ke^{-r\tau}e^{-d_-^2/2}.}
$$
At fixed $S,t,T$, only $\log(S/K)$ depends on $K$, so the <partial derivatives> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial d_+}{\partial K}=\frac{\partial d_-}{\partial K}
=-\frac1{K\sigma\sqrt\tau}.}
$$

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Differentiate the <Black-Scholes formula> for the <European put option> with respect to its strike. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{\partial P}{\partial K}
=e^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_-)
-Ke^{-r\tau}\phi(d_-)\frac{\partial d_-}{\partial K}
+S\phi(d_+)\frac{\partial d_+}{\partial K}.
$$
The final two terms cancel by both identities in part <3/a/i>. Hence the <put-price strike derivative> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial P}{\partial K}=e^{-r\tau}\operatorname N(-d_-).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

For a terminal price distinct from $K$, differentiating the put's <financial payoff> gives $\partial_K(K-S_T)^+=\mathbf1_{\{S_T<K\}}$. The difference quotients are bounded by one, and the continuous <lognormal distribution> has zero <probability> at the strike. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore permits differentiation inside its discounted <expectation>. The <put-price strike derivative> is exactly the price of the <digital put option>:
$$
\boxed{P_d(S,t)=\frac{\partial P}{\partial K}=e^{-r\tau}\operatorname N(-d_-).}
$$
Equivalently, <risk-neutral pricing> discounts the <probability> $\mathbb Q(S_T<K\mid S_t=S)=\Phi(-d_-)$.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

While the <up-and-out digital put> is alive, its pricing domain is $0<S<B$, $t<T$. Its <Black-Scholes equation>, terminal <financial payoff>, and absorbing <boundary condition> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
H_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2H_{SS}+rSH_S-rH&=0,&0<S<B,\ t<T,\\
H(S,T)&=\mathbf1_{\{S<K\}},&0<S<B,\\
H(B,t)&=0,&t\leq T.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For completeness, the natural limiting condition is $\lim_{S\downarrow0}H(S,t)=e^{-r(T-t)}$, with $0\leq H(S,t)\leq e^{-r(T-t)}$. A positive <geometric Brownian motion> cannot hit zero in finite time, and from a very small starting value the survival and below-strike terminal events have probability tending to one. If the barrier has already been hit, its value is zero regardless of the current stock price.

For $K>B$, the terminal payoff tends to one from inside at $S=B$, while an already hit barrier pays zero. Thus there is a terminal-boundary corner discontinuity; the terminal condition is imposed only for $S<B$, not as a requirement of continuous extension at $(B,T)$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Let $D_K(S,t)=P_d(S,t)$ and $\gamma=1-2r/\sigma^2$. The <Black-Scholes reflection symmetry> gives another solution $(S/B)^\gamma D_K(B^2/S,t)$. Subtracting it enforces absorption:
$$
\boxed{H(S,t)=D_K(S,t)-(S/B)^\gamma D_K(B^2/S,t),\qquad0<S<B,\quad B>K.}
$$
At $S=B$ the terms agree. At expiry the reflected argument satisfies $B^2/S>B>K$, so its digital-put payoff vanishes; the first term supplies the required terminal payoff. These facts and the bounded pricing solution's uniqueness prove the formula. The <up-and-out digital put> price is zero once knocked out.

= iv
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

If $0<B<K$, every surviving terminal price is already below the strike, so this is a <barrier digital put> paying one at expiry exactly when the upper barrier has been avoided. To use the <Black-Scholes reflection symmetry>, cap the free digital-put strike at $B$, rather than continuing to use $K$:
$$
\boxed{H(S,t)=D_B(S,t)-(S/B)^\gamma D_B(B^2/S,t),\qquad0<S<B<K.}
$$
Indeed, $D_B$ has terminal payoff one throughout the alive region, while its reflected argument exceeds $B$ and hence has zero terminal payoff. The barrier values again cancel. Using $D_K$ in both terms would generally leave an unwanted reflected terminal payoff near the barrier.

Writing $b=r-\sigma^2/2$ makes the discounted survival <probability> explicit:
$$
\boxed{H(S,t)=e^{-r\tau}\left[
\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log(B/S)-b\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau}\right)
-(S/B)^\gamma\Phi\!\left(\frac{\log(S/B)-b\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau}\right)
\right].}
$$
The price is independent of $K$ in this case. Both cases combine by replacing the free strike with $\min(K,B)$ in the <up-and-out digital put> formula.
