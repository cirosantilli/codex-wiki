Solutions to all three questions. Write $\tau=T-t$, $\Phi$ for the <standard normal distribution function>, and $\phi$ for the <standard normal density>. Formulas with $\tau$ in a denominator are for $t<T$; terminal values are specified separately. Barrier formulas refer to a contract whose barrier has not previously been hit, unless stated otherwise.

= Solution

{c}

The portfolio value is $\Pi_t=\phi_tS_t+\psi_tB_t$. The <self-financing condition> means that changes in holdings are financed by trading within the portfolio, with no external infusion or withdrawal. Thus
$$
\boxed{d\Pi_t=\phi_t\,dS_t+\psi_t\,dB_t
=(\mu\phi_tS_t+r\psi_tB_t)\,dt+\sigma\phi_t\,dW_t.}
$$
The holdings are predictable and satisfy the usual stochastic-integrability and admissibility conditions; without admissibility, doubling strategies would spoil the intended <no-arbitrage> interpretation.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For $V\in C^{1,2}$, <Itô's formula> gives
$$
dV=\left(V_t+\mu sV_s+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}V_{ss}\right)dt+\sigma V_s\,dW.
$$
Match the Brownian coefficient with the <self-financing portfolio> by choosing the <option delta> $\phi=V_s$ and bond value $\psi B=V-sV_s$. Equating drifts cancels the physical drift $\mu$ and gives
$$
\boxed{V_t+rsV_s+\frac{\sigma^2}{2}V_{ss}-rV=0,
\quad (t,s)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb R,\qquad V(T,s)=F(s).}
$$
This is the <pricing equation for a linear asset with additive noise>. Its spatial <domain> is the whole <real line>, because the asset can be negative. In particular the diffusion term is $\sigma^2V_{ss}/2$, not $\sigma^2s^2V_{ss}/2$. The derivation is <delta hedging> and does not impose a boundary condition at $s=0$.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Set $\theta_t=(\mu-r)S_t/\sigma$. The <Girsanov theorem> uses the density
$$
Z_T=\exp\left(-\int_0^T\theta_u\,dW_u-\frac12\int_0^T\theta_u^2\,du\right),\qquad
W_t^{\mathbb Q}=W_t+\int_0^t\theta_u\,du.
$$
For these nonexplosive <linear stochastic differential equations>, the <linear drift change of measure> ensures that $Z$ is a true <martingale> on each finite horizon. This matters: a stochastic exponential being a local <martingale> alone would not prove that its terminal expectation is one. Its strict positivity gives an equivalent <risk-neutral measure> $\mathbb Q$, under which
$$
\boxed{dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t^{\mathbb Q}.}
$$
The discounted asset is then a <martingale>. The <Feynman-Kac formula>, equivalently <risk-neutral valuation> of the replicable claim, gives
$$
\boxed{V(t,s)=e^{-r\tau}\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[F(S_T)\mid S_t=s].}
$$
Here the payoff must meet the usual integrability and replication assumptions; the bounded digital payoff below does.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The discounted stock over the remaining horizon satisfies
$$
e^{-r\tau}S_T=s+\sigma\int_t^T e^{-r(u-t)}\,dW_u^{\mathbb Q}.
$$
The <Itô integral> on the right is a centered <normal random variable> with <variance> $a_\tau=(1-e^{-2r\tau})/(2r)$. Therefore $\mathbb Q(S_T\geq0\mid S_t=s)=\Phi(d)$, where $d=s/(\sigma\sqrt{a_\tau})$. The <digital option> price and its <option delta> are
$$
\boxed{V(t,s)=e^{-r\tau}\Phi(d),\qquad
\Delta(t,s)=\frac{e^{-r\tau}\phi(d)}{\sigma\sqrt{a_\tau}}.}
$$
The formula also shows $V(t,0)=e^{-r\tau}/2$ for every $t<T$. At maturity use the specified step payoff, including its chosen value one at zero; the terminal discontinuity is not removed by the earlier-time smoothing.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Since $a_\tau$ does not depend on $\sigma$, differentiation of the <digital option> value gives its <vega>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial V}{\partial\sigma}=-\frac{e^{-r\tau}}\sigma\,d\phi(d).}
$$
Thus <vega> is negative for $s>0$, zero at $s=0$, and positive for $s<0$. Increasing volatility spreads the conditional <normal distribution> around its mean: when the current asset value is positive this reduces the chance of ending positive, while for a negative starting value it increases that chance. At zero the distribution stays symmetric and the probability remains one half. A digital payoff need not have the positive <vega> familiar from a <European call option>.

= Solution

A <self-financing replicating portfolio> for a terminal payoff $F(S_T)$ has the same terminal value on every relevant path, with all earlier rebalancing funded internally. In a <complete market> its initial cost is unique under <no-arbitrage>: otherwise buy the cheaper payoff and sell the more expensive identical payoff.

In the <Black-Scholes model>, the portfolio holds $V_s$ shares and bond value $V-SV_s$, where $V$ solves the <Black-Scholes equation> with terminal data $F$. <Itô's formula> verifies the <self-financing condition>. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{V(t,s)=e^{-r\tau}\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[F(S_T)\mid S_t=s],\qquad dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^{\mathbb Q}.}
$$
This relates <claim replication>, the <Black-Scholes equation>, and <risk-neutral valuation>.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Buy $1/F_0^T$ shares and take a short <forward contract> for $1/F_0^T$ shares at delivery price $F_0^T$. The forward has zero initial value, and the combined terminal payoff is
$$
\frac{S_T}{F_0^T}+\frac{F_0^T-S_T}{F_0^T}=1.
$$
Therefore the <synthetic zero-coupon bond> has initial price
$$
\boxed{P(0,T)=\frac{S_0}{F_0^T}.}
$$
Its implied continuously compounded maturity yield is $r_T=T^{-1}\log(F_0^T/S_0)>0$.

To replicate a constant-rate <risk-free asset> throughout the interval, the market must support dynamically coherent discounting, not just this one terminal payment. A sufficient condition is that for all $0\leq t\leq u\leq T$ the fair forward prices satisfy $F_t^u=S_te^{r(u-t)}$ for the same deterministic $r>0$, with the required stock/forward positions tradable and no intervening payments. Then $P(t,u)=S_t/F_t^u=e^{-r(u-t)}$, and a suitably scaled fixed-maturity <zero-coupon bond> grows deterministically as $e^{rt}$. A single initial forward quote does not establish these intermediate-time conditions; even a flat initial yield curve need not imply deterministic future rates.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

Under the <risk-neutral measure>, write
$$
S_T/s=\exp\left((r-\sigma^2/2)\tau+\sigma\sqrt\tau Z\right),\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$
Complete the square in the <normal density> after multiplying by the power of $S_T/s$. The <truncated lognormal moment> gives, for $K>0$,
$$
\boxed{V_p(t,s,K)=M_p(\tau)\Phi(d_p),\quad
M_p(\tau)=e^{[pr+p(p-1)\sigma^2/2]\tau},\quad
d_p=\frac{\log(s/K)+[r+(p-1/2)\sigma^2]\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau}.}
$$
All real powers have finite moments in this <lognormal distribution>. If $K=0$, the indicator is identically one and $V_p=M_p$. The function $V_p$ is an undiscounted conditional moment; the option prices below additionally include the <discount factor>.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

If $p>0$ and $K>0$, the payoff is positive exactly when $S_T>k:=K^{1/p}$. Splitting it into two truncated moments gives
$$
\boxed{e^{-r\tau}\left[s^pV_p(t,s,K^{1/p})-K V_0(t,s,K^{1/p})\right].}
$$
For $p=0$, the payoff is the constant $(1-K)^+$, so its price is $e^{-r\tau}(1-K)^+$. For $K=0$ and $p>0$, its price is $e^{-r\tau}s^pM_p(\tau)$. These boundary cases avoid taking $K^{1/p}$ when $p=0$.

= Solution
{parent=e}
{c}

The payoff is $(S_0S_T-S_T^2)\mathbf1_{\{S_T<S_0\}}$. Subtract the upper-tail <truncated lognormal moments> from the full moments in part <2/c>. With current spot $s=S_t$, the price is
$$
\boxed{e^{-r\tau}\left[S_0s\{M_1(\tau)-V_1(t,s,S_0)\}
-s^2\{M_2(\tau)-V_2(t,s,S_0)\}\right],}
$$
where $M_1=e^{r\tau}$ and $M_2=e^{(2r+\sigma^2)\tau}$. The threshold is the original $S_0$, not the current spot $s$. Since the conditional <lognormal distribution> has no atoms, the strict endpoint convention does not affect the price for $t<T$.

= Solution

The <logarithm> of the geometric average is
$$
\log\overline S_T=\log S_0+\frac12(r-\sigma^2/2)T+\frac\sigma T\int_0^T W_u^{\mathbb Q}\,du.
$$
By <stochastic Fubini theorem>, $\int_0^TW_u\,du=\int_0^T(T-v)\,dW_v$, a centered <Gaussian random variable> with <variance> $T^3/3$. Therefore
$$
\log((\overline S_T)^p)\sim N(m,v),\qquad
m=p\left[\log S_0+\frac12(r-\sigma^2/2)T\right],\quad v=\frac{p^2\sigma^2T}{3}.
$$
The <geometric Asian option> is consequently a call on this <lognormal random variable>. Completing the square as in part <2/c> gives
$$
\boxed{e^{-rT}\left[e^{m+v/2}\Phi(d_1)-K\Phi(d_2)\right],\qquad
d_2=\frac{m-\log K}{\sqrt v},\quad d_1=d_2+\sqrt v.}
$$
The averaging changes both the log-mean and log-variance; replacing $S_T$ by an ordinary stock price at a shorter maturity without these adjustments would give the wrong formula.

For a long position in the claim, hold $-V_S$ shares and bank value $SV_S-V$ so that claim plus hedge has zero initial value. The <Black-Scholes equation> and <Itô's formula> show that the total position is <self-financing> and its Brownian and drift terms cancel. Equivalently, the positive replicating portfolio holds $V_S$ shares and bond value $V-SV_S$.

Under the usual growth, integrability and admissibility conditions, <no-arbitrage> forces the claim price to be $V$: a different price could be traded against the <replicating portfolio>. The qualification matters because an arbitrary formal PDE solution without a suitable financial class need not be a valid price.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Let $\tau_B=\inf\{u\geq t:S_u\leq B\}$. While the contract is alive, the <down-and-out put> pays $(K-S_T)^+\mathbf1_{\{\tau_B>T\}}$ at maturity; a prior touch has already knocked it out.

The truncated terminal payoff can be represented by the ordinary European-price combination
$$
F(S,t):=C(S,t;K)-C(S,t;B)+(K-B)C_d(S,t;B).
$$
At maturity its three regions are
$$
\boxed{F(S,T)=(K-S)^+\mathbf1_{\{S\geq B\}}.}
$$
Indeed it is zero below $B$, equals $K-S$ between $B$ and $K$, and is zero above $K$. This supplies the terminal building block for reflection.

The zero-below-barrier property is a property of the terminal payoff. Before maturity, $F(S,t)$ need not vanish for $S<B$, because the ordinary European contracts can still finish above $B$. The killed <barrier option> price, rather than this unrestricted European combination, is zero after a touch.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Put $q=1-2r/\sigma^2$. The <Black-Scholes reflection symmetry> maps $F$ to $(S/B)^qF(B^2/S,t)$, which solves the same PDE. Their difference vanishes at $S=B$. For $S>B$ at maturity the reflected argument is below $B$, where the terminal function in part <3/a/ii> is zero. Thus the boundary and terminal data of the alive <down-and-out put> are satisfied:
$$
\boxed{G(S,t)=F(S,t)-\left(\frac SB\right)^qF(B^2/S,t),\qquad S>B.}
$$
The killed-domain <Feynman-Kac formula> identifies this solution with the discounted payoff conditional on survival. For $S\leq B$, or after any prior barrier hit, the actual price is **zero**. The reflected difference is a formula on the alive domain $S>B$; extending that algebraic expression to $S<B$ generally gives a nonzero, possibly negative function and is not a valid barrier-option price.

A <down-and-out digital call> with strike at the barrier pays one exactly when the barrier is avoided until expiry. Reflection therefore gives its alive price
$$
D_{\mathrm{out}}(S,t)=C_d(S,t;B)-\left(\frac SB\right)^qC_d(B^2/S,t;B),\qquad S>B.
$$
The complementary contract pays one at expiry when the barrier has been hit. Its price is
$$
\boxed{H(t,S)=e^{-r\tau}-D_{\mathrm{out}}(S,t),\qquad S>B\text{ and no previous hit}.}
$$
This is a cash-at-expiry <one-touch option>, not cash paid at the first touch. After the barrier has already been hit, its price is $e^{-r\tau}$ regardless of the current spot. In particular this branch applies immediately for $S\leq B$. Current spot alone does not encode the full path history.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

If the barrier is reached, path <continuity> gives $S_{\tau_B}=B$ and the early payment is $K-B$. If it is not reached, the terminal payment is exactly the <down-and-out put> payoff. When $r=0$, moving a fixed payment from the hitting time to expiry does not change its value. Hence the <barrier-triggered put> price is
$$
\boxed{E_B(S,t)=G(S,t)+(K-B)H(t,S),\qquad r=0.}
$$
Here $G,H$ are evaluated with zero interest rate. If the barrier has already been hit, the exploding put has been exercised and has no remaining value after its payment; the displayed replication concerns an initially alive contract and its cash flows.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <barrier-triggered put> exercises at the particular <stopping time> $\tau_B\wedge T$. The <American put option> optimizes over all allowed exercise times, so
$$
\boxed{E_B(S,t)\leq A(S,t;K).}
$$
It can be strictly smaller, but can never exceed the American value. Equality is possible if immediate exercise is already triggered and optimal; a fixed barrier is not generally the optimal finite-maturity exercise rule.

The comparison with the <European put option> can go in either direction. By the <early exercise premium for a fixed barrier>,
$$
E_B(S,t)-P(S,t;K)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}\!\left[e^{-r(\tau_B-t)}
\{K-B-P(B,\tau_B;K)\}\mathbf1_{\{\tau_B\leq T\}}\mid S_t=S\right].
$$
Thus an early payment gains interest but sacrifices remaining put time value; the expression inside braces need not have a fixed sign for arbitrary barriers.

For a concrete general construction take current spot $S\geq K$, $r>0$, $\sigma>0$, and $t<T$. Set $\gamma=2r/\sigma^2$ and $b_\infty=K\gamma/(1+\gamma)$, the <perpetual put option> exercise boundary. For $0<B<b_\infty$, the perpetual value at $B$ equals $K-B$ and strictly dominates the finite-maturity European value. The premium formula and positive probability of hitting $B$ therefore give **$E_B>P$**. Strictness follows from the perpetual verification argument: before a fixed positive expiry, its discounted value loses drift $rK$ whenever the stock is below $b_\infty$, with positive expected occupation time when started at $B<b_\infty$.

Conversely, for $B<K$ sufficiently close to $K$, every possible payment of the exploding put is at most $K-B$, whether paid at touch or on surviving to expiry. Hence $0\leq E_B\leq K-B\to0$, while $P(S,t;K)>0$. This gives **$E_B<P$** for such barriers, and also strict inequality below the American value.

These examples establish both possibilities in general, not a claim that every fixed initial state admits both. If $S<b_\infty$ and only alive barriers $B<S$ are allowed, all those barriers are in the same perpetual stopping region and the premium comparison is nonnegative.
