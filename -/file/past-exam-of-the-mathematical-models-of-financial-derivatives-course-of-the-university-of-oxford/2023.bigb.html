Solutions follow the numbering of the 6 June 2023 paper. Throughout, volatility is positive and the stated <Black-Scholes model> assumptions apply. The finite perpetual values in 2(b) additionally require $r>0$; the other interest-rate cases are explained below. Question 3's conditional expectation is evaluated under the measure stipulated there, with its financial interpretation qualified in 3(d).

= 1
{parent=2023}
{scope}

= a
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Use a <self-financing portfolio> which holds $\Delta=V_S$ shares and $V-SV_S$ in the <bank account>, withdrawing the claim's cash flow $C\,dt$. A share's total gain includes the <continuous dividend yield>, so its gain is $dS+DS\,dt$. Matching the claim's total gain gives
$$
dV+C\,dt=V_S(dS+DS\,dt)+r(V-SV_S)\,dt.
$$
By the <Itô formula>,
$$
dV=\left[V_t+(\mu-D)SV_S+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\right]dt+\sigma SV_S\,dW.
$$
The <delta hedge> cancels the <Brownian motion> term. Comparing the remaining drifts yields the <Black-Scholes equation with claim dividends>, with the stock's own dividend yield included:
$$
\boxed{V_t+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}+(r-D)SV_S-rV=-C(S,t),\qquad V(S,T)=S}.
$$
This gain calculation accounts for rebalancing through the <self-financing portfolio> condition. The physical expected-return parameter $\mu$ cancels, as required by <risk-neutral pricing>.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For <claim dividends proportional to the underlying stock>, put $V(S,t)=S\phi(t)$. Then $V_S=\phi$, $V_{SS}=0$ and $V_t=S\phi'$, so the <partial differential equation> reduces to the <linear ordinary differential equation>
$$
\phi'-D\phi=-f(t),\qquad\phi(T)=1.
$$
The <integrating factor> $e^{-Dt}$ gives $(e^{-Dt}\phi)'=-e^{-Dt}f(t)$. Integrating backwards from the terminal value,
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=S\left[e^{-D(T-t)}+\int_t^T e^{-D(u-t)}f(u)\,du\right]}.
$$
The formula assumes the displayed <integral> is finite. Under the <risk-neutral measure>, $\mathbb E_t[S_u]=S e^{(r-D)(u-t)}$. Discounting each cash payment at its own payment time gives the same result:
$$
V=\mathbb E_t\!\left[e^{-r(T-t)}S_T+\int_t^T e^{-r(u-t)}f(u)S_u\,du\right].
$$
The <interest rate> disappears because every payment is proportional to the <stock> price.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Setting $f=0$ in <1/b> gives
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=S e^{-D(T-t)}}.
$$
This is a <prepaid forward contract>: the holder pays now and receives one share at $T$, with no interim dividend payments. Buying $e^{-D(T-t)}$ shares now and reinvesting their <continuous dividend yield> grows the shareholding to one at expiry. Thus the displayed initial cost replicates the terminal delivery. When $D=0$, holding one share already does so and $V=S$.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}

For the non-dividend-paying <stock>, a <self-financing portfolio> replicating a smooth <European option> has $\Delta=V_S$ shares and $V-SV_S$ in the <bank account>. Its gain satisfies
$$
dV=V_S\,dS+r(V-SV_S)\,dt.
$$
The <Itô formula> gives $dV=(V_t+\mu SV_S+\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}/2)dt+\sigma SV_SdW$. Matching its diffusion and drift with the portfolio gains cancels $\mu$, yielding
$$
\boxed{V_t+\frac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}+rSV_S-rV=0}.
$$
The <financial payoff> supplies the terminal value. This is the <Black-Scholes equation>; the associated <risk-neutral measure> replaces the physical stock drift by $r$.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= i
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write $\tau=T-t>0$ and $g_n(s)=(K-s)_+^n$. The <European power put formula> is
$$
P_n(S,t)=e^{-r\tau}\mathbb E\!\left[(K-SY)_+^n\right],\qquad Y=e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau+\sigma\sqrt\tau Z},\quad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$
Here $Y>0$ has a <lognormal distribution>. Since $g_n$ is decreasing and is a <convex function>, its discounted <expected value> is also decreasing and convex as a function of $S$. For $S>0$ it is smooth and strictly positive: the <normal distribution> assigns positive probability to $SY<K$ whatever the current positive spot price. Its endpoints are
$$
\boxed{P_n(0,t)=K^n e^{-r\tau},\qquad \lim_{S\to\infty}P_n(S,t)=0}.
$$
The <dominated convergence theorem> justifies the second <limit>, because $0\leq(K-SY)_+^n\leq K^n$. For a quantitative sketch, the <binomial theorem> and <truncated lognormal moment> give
$$
P_n(S,t)=e^{-r\tau}\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\binom nj K^{n-j}S^j e^{jr\tau+j(j-1)\sigma^2\tau/2}\Phi(-d_j),\qquad d_j=\frac{\log(S/K)+[r+(j-\tfrac12)\sigma^2]\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau},
$$
where $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution function>. This <finite sum> is useful for drawing the curve; its positive <expectation> form makes its qualitative shape clearer.

\Image[2023-power-put-prices.png]
{title=European power-put prices before expiry for powers one, two and three}

The solid curves use $K=1$, $r=0.05$, $\sigma=0.30$ and $\tau=1$; the dashed curves show their terminal <financial payoffs> for comparison. Each price remains positive at $S=K$ and tends smoothly towards zero beyond it. At small $S$, discounting gives a price below the undiscounted <financial payoff>; European exercise is permitted only at expiry.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Assume $r>0$, put $b=S^\star\in(0,K)$ and let $\tau_b=\inf\{u\geq0:S_u\leq b\}$. In the continuation region $S>b$, the time-independent <Black-Scholes equation> is a <Cauchy-Euler equation>. Substituting $V=S^m$ gives
$$
\frac12\sigma^2m(m-1)+rm-r=(m-1)\left(\frac12\sigma^2m+r\right)=0.
$$
Set $\gamma=2r/\sigma^2>0$. The two powers are $S$ and $S^{-\gamma}$. The <boundary condition> $V(S)\to0$ as $S\to\infty$ removes the growing solution; value matching at $b$ sets the coefficient. Hence the fixed-trigger value is
$$
\boxed{V_b(S)=(K-b)^n(S/b)^{-\gamma},\qquad S>b}.
$$
For $0\leq S\leq b$ the policy exercises immediately, giving $V_b(S)=(K-S)^n$. The quoted continuation expression alone does not apply below the exercise boundary. Equivalently, the <discounted boundary-hitting representation> gives $\mathbb E_S[e^{-r\tau_b};\tau_b<\infty]=(S/b)^{-\gamma}$, which multiplies the exercise <financial payoff>.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $S>b$, maximize the coefficient $H(b)=b^\gamma(K-b)^n$ of $S^{-\gamma}$. Its logarithmic <derivative> is
$$
\frac{H'(b)}{H(b)}=\frac\gamma b-\frac n{K-b}.
$$
It changes sign once, at $b=\gamma K/(\gamma+n)$. The <perpetual power put option> therefore has candidate value
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{2rK}{2r+n\sigma^2},\qquad V_n(S)=\begin{cases}(K-S)^n,&0\leq S\leq b,\\(K-b)^n(S/b)^{-2r/\sigma^2},&S>b.\end{cases}}
$$
The same boundary follows from <smooth fit>, $-\gamma(K-b)^n/b=-n(K-b)^{n-1}$. To establish optimality among all <stopping times>, rather than only fixed barriers, we verify the candidate. Let $\mathcal L=rS\partial_S+\sigma^2S^2\partial_{SS}/2$ be the <infinitesimal generator>. The maximum property of $H$ gives $V_n\geq g_n$ everywhere, and $(\mathcal L-r)V_n=0$ for $S>b$. For $S\leq b$, set $y=S/(K-S)\leq\gamma/n$. Then
$$
\frac{(\mathcal L-r)g_n}{g_n}=\frac12\sigma^2\big[n(n-1)y^2-\gamma ny-\gamma\big]\leq-r(1+y)<0.
$$
Thus the discounted candidate is a nonnegative <supermartingale>. The <smooth fit> removes the derivative-jump <local time of a semimartingale> term in the <Itô formula>. The <optional stopping theorem> and localization bound every exercise policy's discounted <expected value> by $V_n$. Immediate exercise attains it below $b$; the first hitting time of $b$ attains it above $b$. The terminal remainder vanishes since $0\leq e^{-ru}V_n(S_u)\leq K^n e^{-ru}\to0$. This proves the displayed value.

For $n=1$ it reduces to the ordinary <perpetual put option>. Larger $n$ lowers the optimal trigger, because the more strongly curved <financial payoff> makes further downward moves more valuable. If $r=0$, under the <risk-neutral measure> the stock tends to zero <almost surely>, so the supremum is $K^n$, approached as the exercise time tends to infinity and not attained at a finite time when $S>0$. If $r<0$, the values at deterministic exercise times grow without bound; the perpetual value is infinite. These cases do not admit the positive-rate formula above.

= a
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

For $x_t=\log S_t$, the <Itô formula> uses $(\log S)'=1/S$ and $(\log S)''=-1/S^2$. Consequently
$$
dx_t=\frac{dS_t}{S_t}-\frac12\frac{d\langle S\rangle_t}{S_t^2}=\left[\alpha(\mu-x_t)-\frac12\sigma^2\right]dt+\sigma\,dZ_t.
$$
The <quadratic variation> term shifts the mean level. Thus the <log-mean-reverting price> has logarithm following the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process>
$$
\boxed{dx_t=\alpha(\widetilde\mu-x_t)dt+\sigma\,dZ_t,\qquad \widetilde\mu=\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}}.
$$
It is the logarithm, rather than the price itself, which reverts linearly to a constant mean level.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Apply the <Itô product rule> with deterministic <integrating factor> $e^{\alpha u}$:
$$
d(e^{\alpha u}x_u)=\alpha\widetilde\mu e^{\alpha u}du+\sigma e^{\alpha u}dZ_u.
$$
Integrate from $t$ to $T$ and multiply by $e^{-\alpha T}$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{x_T=x_t e^{-\alpha(T-t)}+\widetilde\mu(1-e^{-\alpha(T-t)})+\sigma e^{-\alpha T}\int_t^T e^{\alpha u}\,dZ_u}.
$$
This is the explicit solution behind the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck transition distribution>.

= c
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $\tau=T-t$ and $a=e^{-\alpha\tau}$. The <stochastic integral> in <3/b> has a deterministic integrand, so it is a <Gaussian random variable> independent of the information available at $t$. Its <variance>, by the <Itô isometry>, is
$$
v=\sigma^2 e^{-2\alpha T}\int_t^T e^{2\alpha u}du=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}(1-a^2).
$$
Thus $x_T$ conditionally has a <normal distribution> with <expected value> $m=ax_t+(1-a)\widetilde\mu$ and <variance> $v$. The <moment-generating function of a normal distribution> gives $\mathbb E_t[e^{x_T}]=e^{m+v/2}$, so the <conditional mean of a log-mean-reverting price> is
$$
\boxed{F(t,T)=\exp\!\left[e^{-\alpha(T-t)}\log S_t+\widetilde\mu(1-e^{-\alpha(T-t)})+\frac{\sigma^2}{4\alpha}(1-e^{-2\alpha(T-t)})\right]}.
$$
The correction $v/2$ is essential: exponentiating the conditional mean of the logarithm alone would omit the dispersion of the <lognormal distribution>.

= d
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Keep the maturity $T$ fixed and write $F(t,T)=\exp L(t,x_t)$, where
$$
L(t,x)=ax+(1-a)\widetilde\mu+\frac{\sigma^2}{4\alpha}(1-a^2),\qquad a=e^{-\alpha(T-t)}.
$$
As calendar time increases, $\partial_ta=\alpha a$, so $L_x=a$, $L_{xx}=0$ and $L_t=\alpha a(x-\widetilde\mu)-\sigma^2a^2/2$. Applying the <Itô formula> to $L$ and then to its exponential gives
$$
dL=-\frac12\sigma^2a^2dt+\sigma a\,dZ_t,\qquad \boxed{\frac{dF(t,T)}{F(t,T)}=\sigma e^{-\alpha(T-t)}\,dZ_t}.
$$
The <Itô formula>'s exponential correction cancels the displayed drift of $L$. The zero drift also follows from the <tower property of conditional expectation>: for $t\leq u\leq T$, $\mathbb E_t[F(u,T)]=\mathbb E_t[S_T]=F(t,T)$. In particular $F(T,T)=S_T$, while the quote's instantaneous <spot volatility> approaches $\sigma$ as expiry approaches.

This is a <martingale> under the same measure used in the stipulated <conditional expectation>. To interpret it as <futures pricing>, that measure must be a suitable pricing measure. For a non-dividend-paying traded <stock> at a deterministic <interest rate>, the absence of <arbitrage> instead gives a <forward contract> delivery price $S_t e^{r(T-t)}$. A physical-measure expectation under the given mean-reverting drift is not automatically that price; the question's expectation convention is sufficient for the calculation above, but not for this additional identification.
