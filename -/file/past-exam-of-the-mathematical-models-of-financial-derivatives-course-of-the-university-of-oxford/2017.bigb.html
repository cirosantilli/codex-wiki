= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Apply <Itô's formula> to $f(u,s)=e^{-\alpha u}s$. Its second stock derivative is zero, and the two drift terms cancel, giving
$$
d(e^{-\alpha u}S_u)=\sigma e^{-\alpha u}\,dW_u.
$$
Integrating and multiplying by $e^{\alpha T}$ solves the <linear stochastic differential equation>:
$$
\boxed{S_T=e^{\alpha(T-t)}S_t+\sigma\int_t^T e^{\alpha(T-u)}\,dW_u.}
$$
The <Itô integral> has a deterministic integrand, so it is a centered <Gaussian random variable> independent of the current <filtration> $\mathcal F_t$. Its <variance> follows from the <Itô isometry>. With $\tau=T-t$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[S_T\mid\mathcal F_t]=e^{\alpha\tau}S_t,\qquad
\operatorname{var}(S_T\mid\mathcal F_t)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\alpha}(e^{2\alpha\tau}-1),&\alpha\ne0,\\
\sigma^2\tau,&\alpha=0.
\end{cases}}
$$
The second line is also the <limit> as $\alpha\to0$. The normal law asserted here is conditional on $\mathcal F_t$; an arbitrary random initial value need not give an unconditional <normal distribution>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Use a <self-financing portfolio> with $\Delta_t$ units of the traded asset and the remainder in the <continuous-time bank account>. If its value equals $V(S_t,t)$, its <self-financing condition> is
$$
dV=\Delta_t\,dS_t+r(V-\Delta_tS_t)\,dt.
$$
Meanwhile <Itô's formula> gives
$$
dV=(V_t+\alpha S_tV_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2V_{SS})\,dt+\sigma V_S\,dW_t.
$$
Matching the <Brownian motion> coefficients gives the <delta hedge> $\Delta_t=V_S$. Matching drifts then cancels the physical drift $\alpha$, leaving the <pricing equation for a linear asset with additive noise>:
$$
\boxed{V_t+\frac12\sigma^2V_{SS}+rSV_S-rV=0,\qquad V(S,T)=P_{\mathrm o}(S).}
$$
The domain is the full <real line>: additive noise can take the asset through zero. The <diffusion coefficient> is $\sigma$, so the second-derivative term has no factor $S^2$. As usual, the replication argument assumes suitable differentiability and an <admissible trading strategy>.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The differential operator in part <1/b> is the <infinitesimal generator> of the auxiliary <diffusion process> $dS_u=rS_u\,du+\sigma\,dW_u$, with killing rate $r$. The <Feynman-Kac formula> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[P_{\mathrm o}(S_T)\mid S_t=S].}
$$
Equivalently, <Itô's formula> makes $e^{-r(u-t)}V(S_u,u)$ a <local martingale> under these dynamics; under appropriate growth and integrability assumptions it is a <martingale>, whose terminal <conditional expectation> yields the same formula. The expectation uses the drift-$r$ law, rather than the physical drift-$\alpha$ law in part <1/a>. This is <risk-neutral pricing>.

{parent=1}

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Apply part <1/a> with $\alpha=r$. For $\tau>0$, the <transition density> is the <normal distribution> with mean $m=Se^{r\tau}$ and positive <variance> $v=\sigma^2(e^{2r\tau}-1)/(2r)$. Integrating the <financial payoff> against this <probability density function> gives
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=\frac{e^{-r\tau}}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{\mathrm o}(x)
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-Se^{r\tau})^2}{2v}\right]dx,\qquad
v=\frac{\sigma^2}{2r}(e^{2r\tau}-1).}
$$
Thus $d_*=(x-Se^{r\tau})/\sqrt v$, as required. The <variance> is positive even if $r<0$, because numerator and denominator have the same sign. For $r=0$, replace $m$ by $S$ and $v$ by $\sigma^2\tau$; at expiry use the terminal value directly. The <integral> presumes an integrable <financial payoff>.

= Solution

{c}

For a positive starting value, <Itô's formula> applied to $\log S_u$ gives $d\log S_u=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)du+\sigma dW_u$. Hence the <geometric Brownian motion> is
$$
\boxed{S_T=S\exp\!\left[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\tau+\sigma(W_T-W_t)\right].}
$$
The future <Brownian increment> $W_T-W_t$ is independent of $\mathcal F_t$ and has <normal distribution> $N(0,\tau)$. If $\widetilde W_v=W_{t+v}-W_t$, then $\widetilde W$ is a fresh <Brownian motion> and the increment is exactly $\widetilde W_\tau$. This specifies the meaning of the paper's abbreviated $W_\tau$; the original process value $W_{T-t}$ is not generally that future increment. The <stochastic exponential> solution also verifies positivity for $S>0$.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Under the <risk-neutral measure>, write $Z\sim N(0,1)$, $a=\sigma\sqrt\tau$, and $m=\log(S/K)+(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau$. Then $\log(S_T/K)=m+aZ$ and $d_-=m/a$. By <risk-neutral pricing>, this <log-call option> has value $e^{-r\tau}\mathbb E[(m+aZ)^+]$.

Let $\phi$ and $\Phi$ denote the <standard normal density> and <standard normal distribution function>. The <Gaussian positive-part expectation> follows by integration:
$$
\mathbb E[(m+aZ)^+]
=\int_{-d_-}^{\infty}(m+az)\phi(z)\,dz
=m\Phi(d_-)+a\phi(d_-),
$$
because $\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V(S,t)=e^{-r\tau}\sigma\sqrt\tau
\left[d_-\Phi(d_-)+\frac{e^{-d_-^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right],\qquad
d_-=\frac{\log(S/K)+(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt\tau}.}
$$
For $\tau=0$ take the continuous terminal <limit> $(\log(S/K))^+$.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The <Black-Scholes reflection symmetry> becomes a weighted reflection after taking a <logarithm>. Put $x=\log(S/B)$ and $v(x,t)=V(Be^x,t)$. The <multivariable chain rule> transforms the equation into
$$
v_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2v_{xx}+bv_x-rv=0,\qquad b=r-\sigma^2/2.
$$
For $w(x,t)=e^{\gamma x}v(-x,t)$, its left-hand side, after removing the factor $e^{\gamma x}$, is
$$
v_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2v_{xx}-(\sigma^2\gamma+b)v_x
+(\tfrac12\sigma^2\gamma^2+b\gamma-r)v,
$$
evaluated at $(-x,t)$. Taking $\gamma=-2b/\sigma^2$ makes the derivative coefficient $b$ and the additional zeroth-order term zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{W(S,t)=(S/B)^{1-2r/\sigma^2}V(B^2/S,t)}
$$
satisfies the same <Black-Scholes equation>.

Subtract the reflected solution from part <2/c>. On the alive side of the <down-and-out barrier option>,
$$
\boxed{V_B(S,t)=V(S,t)-(S/B)^{1-2r/\sigma^2}V(B^2/S,t),\qquad S>B.}
$$
Both terms satisfy the <Black-Scholes equation>, and at $S=B$ they cancel. At expiry, $S\geq B$ gives $B^2/S\leq B<K$, so the reflected <log-call option> has zero terminal <financial payoff>; the first term gives the required payoff. With the usual growth condition, uniqueness for the killed pricing problem identifies the expression as the <risk-neutral price>. After the barrier has been hit the contract value is zero. The algebraic image expression on $S<B$ is not its financial value.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

A <European call option> has nonnegative <financial payoff>, so its price is nonnegative under <no arbitrage>. A second <arbitrage> comparison buys one call and a <zero-coupon bond> paying $K$, and shorts one share. Its terminal value is $(S_T-K)^++K-S_T=(K-S_T)^+\geq0$, so its initial cost cannot be negative. Consequently
$$
\boxed{C_t^e\geq\max(S_t-Ke^{-r(T-t)},0).}
$$
The holder of an <American call option> can choose to wait until expiry, so $C_t^a\geq C_t^e$. Since $r>0$ and $t<T$, an in-the-money call has $C_t^a\geq S_t-Ke^{-r(T-t)}>S_t-K$: immediate exercise sacrifices value. An out-of-the-money exercise pays zero and cannot improve the holder's position. This proves <no early exercise of a call without dividends>: waiting is always at least as good, and is strictly better for positive immediate exercise payoff. In a degenerate market with zero continuation value an out-of-the-money holder can be indifferent; the <Black-Scholes model> with positive volatility gives strictly positive continuation value before expiry.

= Solution

For the <perpetual American call option>, substitute a power $S^q$ into the stationary <Black-Scholes equation with continuous stock dividends>. The <characteristic equation> is
$$
F(q)=\tfrac12\sigma^2q(q-1)+(r-y)q-r=0.
$$
Its two <roots> have negative product $-2r/\sigma^2$, so one is negative and one positive. The condition at zero excludes the negative power. Since $F(1)=-y<0$ and $F(q)\to\infty$ as $q\to\infty$, the positive root satisfies $p>1$; explicitly,
$$
p=\frac{-(r-y-\sigma^2/2)+\sqrt{(r-y-\sigma^2/2)^2+2r\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}.
$$
Value matching gives $C(S)=(\hat S-K)(S/\hat S)^p$. The <smooth fit> condition gives $p(\hat S-K)/\hat S=1$, hence
$$
\boxed{\hat S=\frac{pK}{p-1},\qquad
C(S)=\begin{cases}(\hat S-K)(S/\hat S)^p,&0\leq S<\hat S,\\S-K,&S\geq\hat S.\end{cases}}
$$
As $y\downarrow0$, the positive root tends to $1$ from above, so $\boxed{p\to1,\ \hat S\to\infty}$. For fixed $S$, the value tends to $S$. In the dividend-free perpetual limit no finite threshold attains that supremum; this agrees with the finite-maturity <no early exercise of a call without dividends>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For a fixed <exercise threshold> $\bar S>K$, the continuation value solves the same stationary equation but is matched to $\bar S-K$ at that threshold. Boundedness at zero removes the negative power. Thus the <fixed-threshold value of a perpetual call> is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde C(S)=(\bar S-K)(S/\bar S)^p,\qquad 0\leq S\leq\bar S.}
$$
It is also $(\bar S-K)\mathbb E_S^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-r\tau_{\bar S}};\tau_{\bar S}<\infty]$, where $\tau_{\bar S}$ is the first up-crossing <stopping time>. Differentiating while keeping the current $S$ fixed gives
$$
\frac{\partial\widetilde C}{\partial\bar S}
=S^p\bar S^{-p-1}\bigl[pK-(p-1)\bar S\bigr].
$$
This is positive for $K<\bar S<\hat S=pK/(p-1)$, so increasing such an early threshold increases its continuation value. The comparison assumes $S<\bar S$; a policy already exercised has no remaining continuation value to vary.

If $\bar S>\hat S$, then
$$
\widetilde C'(\bar S-)=p\left(1-\frac K{\bar S}\right)>1.
$$
Since $\widetilde C(\bar S)=\bar S-K$, the <derivative> comparison gives $\widetilde C(S)<S-K$ for $S$ just below $\bar S$. Therefore **quoting this policy value as the price of an unrestricted American call permits arbitrage**: buy the option below its immediate exercise value and exercise it at once. A contract that actually commits its holder to the threshold is a different claim; its lower value is a suboptimal policy value, not by itself an arbitrage. The <perpetual American call option> optimizes over all exercise policies and remains above its exercise payoff.
