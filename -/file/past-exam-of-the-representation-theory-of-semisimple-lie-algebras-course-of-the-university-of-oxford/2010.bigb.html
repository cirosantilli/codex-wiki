= Solution
{parent=a}

Put $e=E_+$ and $f=E_-$. The supplied identities give
$$
Hf^kv=(\chi-2k)f^kv,\qquad
ef^kv=k(\chi-k+1)f^{k-1}v.
$$
If no $f^kv$ vanishes, this is the first alternative. Otherwise choose the least $N\ge1$ with $f^Nv=0$. Applying $e$ gives $N(\chi-N+1)f^{N-1}v=0$. Since $f^{N-1}v\ne0$ and the field has characteristic zero, $\chi=N-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},\quad f^kv\ne0\ (0\le k\le\chi),\quad f^kv=0\ (k>\chi).}
$$
The endpoint must include zero: the trivial one-dimensional <module> has $\chi=0$ and $fv=0$, so the printed claim of strict positivity has that counterexample. All nonzero vectors in the displayed chain have distinct $H$-weights, and hence are linearly independent.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the intended <module>, index a <basis> by $v_k=a_{l-2k}$ for $0\le k\le l$, and use
$$
Hv_k=(l-2k)v_k,\qquad fv_k=v_{k+1},\qquad ev_k=k(l+1-k)v_{k-1},
$$
with $v_{-1}=v_{l+1}=0$. This makes the <basis> endpoints distinct, identifies the lowering operator, and includes its missing raising action at $k=l$. These are the necessary indexing corrections to the printed display. The same definition for $l=0$ supplies the trivial <module> needed for classification.

Directly on $v_k$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
[H,f]v_k&=-2fv_k,\\
[H,e]v_k&=2ev_k,\\
[e,f]v_k&=((k+1)(l-k)-k(l+1-k))v_k=(l-2k)v_k.
\end{aligned}
$$
These hold at the endpoints too, proving the three defining $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ relations. Finally
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta v_k
&=\bigl(4k(l+1-k)+(l-2k)^2+2(l-2k)\bigr)v_k\\
&=\boxed{l(l+2)v_k.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <Casimir element> acts by that scalar on the whole <module>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $V\ne0$ be finite-dimensional. Choose an $H$-<eigenvector> and apply $e$ repeatedly until the last nonzero vector $v$ in that chain. Such a last vector exists because $e$ raises the $H$-<eigenvalue> by two and $H$ has only finitely many eigenvalues. Then $ev=0$ and $Hv=lv$ for some $l$.

The nonzero $f$-chain cannot continue indefinitely, since its weights are distinct. Part (a), with its zero endpoint included, gives $l\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ and the <basis> $v,fv,\ldots,f^lv$. The raising formula in part (a) shows its span is a <submodule> isomorphic to $A_l$.

To prove $A_l$ <irreducible>, a nonzero <submodule> stable under diagonal $H$ contains a <weight vector>: <polynomial interpolation> in the distinct eigenvalues projects onto individual <representation weight> lines. Repeated raising reaches $v_0$, and repeated lowering then reaches every $v_k$. Thus the <submodule> is all of $A_l$. Distinct $l$ give different dimensions $l+1$, so the modules are nonisomorphic. Every finite-dimensional <irreducible> <module> contains one of these nonzero simple submodules and hence equals it. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{A_l:l\ge0\}\text{ is the complete list of finite-dimensional simple modules}.}
$$
The zero <module> has no nonzero simple <submodule> and is dealt with as the empty <direct sum> in part (d).

= Solution
{parent=d}

We first prove that every extension of two simple modules splits. Consider
$$
0\longrightarrow A_n\longrightarrow V\longrightarrow A_m\longrightarrow0.
$$
If $n\ne m$, the central <Casimir element> has distinct scalars $n(n+2)$ and $m(m+2)$. On $V$ its <minimal polynomial> divides the product of the corresponding two distinct linear factors. Its eigenspaces are submodules, and the <eigenspace> for $m(m+2)$ maps isomorphically onto the <quotient>. It is the required complement.

Now suppose $n=m=l$. Use <generalized weight spaces> for $H$; their possible weights are $l,l-2,\ldots,-l$, because the <characteristic polynomial> on an extension is the product of those on its two factors. Lift the <quotient>'s highest vector to $w$ in the <generalized weight space> of <representation weight> $l$. Then $ew=0$ and $f^{l+1}w=0$, since these would lie in <generalized weight spaces> of weights $l+2$ and $-l-2$, which are absent. Also $(H-l)w=av_0$ for a highest vector $v_0$ in the <submodule> $A_l$. The commutator identity gives
$$
0=ef^{l+1}w=(l+1)f^l(H-l)w=(l+1)a f^lv_0.
$$
Thus $a=0$. The vector $w$ is an actual highest-<weight vector>. Its lowering chain maps to the full <basis> of the <quotient>, so it generates an $A_l$ complementary to the original <submodule>. This proves splitting also when the two simple types coincide, including $l=0$.

The same splitting holds for a semisimple <kernel> and simple <quotient> by induction on the number of <kernel> summands. Write the <kernel> as $U_1\oplus U_2$ with $U_1$ simple. Modulo $U_2$, split the extension by the simple <quotient>; the inverse image of that chosen <quotient> copy is an extension with <kernel> $U_2$. Split it inductively, obtaining a <submodule> in $V$ meeting the entire original <kernel> trivially.

Finally induct on $\dim V$. A maximal proper <submodule> has smaller <dimension> and hence is semisimple by induction; the <quotient> is simple by maximality. The just-proved splitting completes the induction. Hence
$$
\boxed{V\cong\bigoplus_{l\ge0}A_l^{\oplus m_l},\qquad m_l\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},}
$$
with only finitely many nonzero multiplicities. This proof does not assume the complete-reducibility theorem it is establishing.

= Solution

Use a <triangular decomposition of a Lie algebra> $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak n_-\oplus\mathfrak h\oplus\mathfrak n_+$. The <highest weight> space of $M^\chi$ has <dimension> one. If $z$ is central, $zm_\chi$ is still of <representation weight> $\chi-\rho$ and is killed by $\mathfrak n_+$, so
$$
zm_\chi=\theta_\chi(z)m_\chi.
$$
Since the <module> is generated by $m_\chi$ and $z$ commutes with $U(\mathfrak g)$, the same scalar acts on every vector. Addition, multiplication and the unit show that $z\mapsto\theta_\chi(z)$ is an <algebra homomorphism> to $\mathbb C$.

For the <polynomial> assertion, the relevant variable for an element of $S(\mathfrak h)$ is $\chi$. The dependence on $z$ itself is linear, as already follows from the scalar action. Apply the <Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> ordering $U(\mathfrak n_-)U(\mathfrak h)U(\mathfrak n_+)$. Because $z$ has adjoint <representation weight> zero, its surviving action on the highest vector is precisely the pure $U(\mathfrak h)$ component $p_z$; terms with a <positive root> factor kill that vector, and a term with only a nontrivial negative <root> factor could not have <representation weight> zero. Since $U(\mathfrak h)=S(\mathfrak h)$,
$$
\boxed{\theta_\chi(z)=p_z(\chi-\rho),\qquad j_z(\chi):=p_z(\chi-\rho)\in S(\mathfrak h).}
$$
This specifies the intended <polynomial> despite the printed variable mismatch. The unadorned <module> symbol in that sentence is understood as $M^\chi$.

= Solution

Write $h_\alpha$ for the simple <coroot> and choose the corresponding normalized $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triple. The order condition means
$$
\chi-s_\alpha\chi=k\alpha\in Q_+,\qquad
k=\langle\chi,\alpha^\vee\rangle\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.
$$
The highest vector has $h_\alpha$-<representation weight> $\langle\chi-\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle=k-1$. The raising-lowering identity gives
$$
E_\alpha E_{-\alpha}^km_\chi
=k((k-1)-k+1)E_{-\alpha}^{k-1}m_\chi=0.
$$
Other simple <positive root> vectors commute with $E_{-\alpha}$ and also kill this vector. Thus it is a highest vector of <representation weight>
$$
\chi-\rho-k\alpha=s_\alpha\chi-\rho.
$$
The universal property of the <Verma module> gives the required homomorphism. It is injective: under the <Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> identification of each <Verma module> with the free rank-one $U(\mathfrak n_-)$ <module>, it is right multiplication by $E_{-\alpha}^k$. The algebra $U(\mathfrak n_-)$ has no zero divisors, because its associated graded algebra is the <polynomial> domain $S(\mathfrak n_-)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{M^{s_\alpha\chi}\hookrightarrow M^\chi,\qquad
m_{s_\alpha\chi}\mapsto E_{-\alpha}^km_\chi.}
$$
For $k=0$ this is the identity map, so the equality case of the order is covered.

= Solution

For every $\chi$ on a hyperplane $\langle\chi,\alpha^\vee\rangle=k$, with $k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$, part (b) embeds one <Verma module> into the other. A central element acts on the nonzero image by both scalars, so $j_z(\chi)=j_z(s_\alpha\chi)$ there.

The difference of these two expressions is a <polynomial> on $\mathfrak h^*$. Fix all coordinates parallel to those hyperplanes and vary the remaining coordinate. It vanishes at every nonnegative integer, so as a <polynomial> in that coordinate it is identically zero. Its coefficient polynomials therefore vanish as well. Consequently the equality holds for all $\chi$. Since the simple reflections generate the <Weyl group>,
$$
\boxed{j_z(w\chi)=j_z(\chi)\qquad(w\in W).}
$$

In the shifted normalization of part (a), the <Harish-Chandra isomorphism> is the algebra isomorphism
$$
\boxed{Z(U(\mathfrak g))\overset\sim\longrightarrow S(\mathfrak h)^W,\qquad z\longmapsto j_z.}
$$
Thus the centre is identified with the Weyl-invariant polynomials on $\mathfrak h^*$. Central characters satisfy $\theta_\chi=\theta_\xi$ exactly when $\chi$ and $\xi$ belong to the same Weyl orbit. The $\rho$ shift is already incorporated in $M^\chi$ having <highest weight> $\chi-\rho$; without that shift the corresponding Weyl action is the dot action.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The centre commutes with $\mathfrak h$, so every nonzero <representation weight> component of the common <eigenvector> $m$ has the same <central character> $\theta$. Pick such a component $m_\lambda$. In <category O>, its $U(\mathfrak n_+)$-span is finite-dimensional and has finitely many weights. Choose a maximal <representation weight> in that span and a nonzero vector $v$ of that <representation weight>, say $\nu$. Every <positive root> operator kills $v$, since it would raise the <representation weight> within the same finite span. Centrality ensures $zv=\theta(z)v$.

The universal property supplies a nonzero map $M^{\nu+\rho}\to M$ taking its highest vector to $v$. The centre acts on that image both by $\theta$ and by $\theta_{\nu+\rho}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\theta=\theta_\xi,\qquad \xi=\nu+\rho,\quad\nu\in\operatorname{wt}(M).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=f}

Use the standard defining properties of <category O>: finite generation over $U(\mathfrak g)$, a <direct sum> of finite-dimensional <weight spaces>, and locally finite <positive root> action. In particular the weights lie in a finite union of downward <root> cones. Submodules and quotients retain the <representation weight> bounds and finite <weight spaces>; finite generation of submodules follows from the <Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> and the noetherian property of $U(\mathfrak g)$.

Choose finitely many <weight vector> generators. The sum $T$ of their full <weight spaces> is finite-dimensional and preserved by the centre. The <kernel> of its central action has finite codimension in $Z(U(\mathfrak g))$ and kills the generators, hence, by centrality, all of $M$. Thus only finitely many central characters $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_r$ can occur in any subquotient: they are the characters of a fixed finite-dimensional commutative <quotient> algebra of the centre.

By the <Harish-Chandra isomorphism> each $\theta_i$ determines one finite Weyl orbit of shifted weights. Let $S$ be the finite union of these orbits after subtracting $\rho$. Every nonzero subquotient has a nonzero highest-<weight vector>, using the bounded-above weights. Within its finite-dimensional <highest weight> space, the commuting central operators have a common <eigenvector>. Part (e) and the central-character restriction show that its <highest weight> belongs to $S$.

Define for any subquotient $N$
$$
d(N)=\sum_{\nu\in S}\dim N_\nu.
$$
It is finite, additive in short exact sequences, and positive whenever $N\ne0$. Hence each strict step of a <submodule> chain consumes at least one unit of $d(M)$. Refining a nonsimple factor into a proper <submodule> and <quotient> cannot continue more than $d(M)$ times. It ends with simple factors, proving
$$
\boxed{M\text{ has a composition series of finite length, at most }d(M).}
$$
The argument proves the assertion for every object of $\mathcal O$, rather than just for a cyclic <highest weight> object.

For $f,g\in\mathcal E$, define their <convolution> by
$$
(f*g)(\lambda)=\sum_{\mu+\nu=\lambda}f(\mu)g(\nu).
$$
The support restrictions make each sum finite: for fixed upper-cone representatives, the <simple root> coefficients in the two downward displacements are bounded nonnegative integers. This also proves closure in $\mathcal E$ and associativity by finite reordering at each coefficient. The unit is $\delta_0$, and $\delta_\mu*\delta_\nu=\delta_{\mu+\nu}$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

With the source's negative <root> partition function, interpret $Q_+=\sum_{\alpha\in B}\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\alpha$ and allow zero coefficients in the partitions. Write $e^\lambda$ for $\delta_\lambda$, with multiplication meaning <convolution>. Then
$$
K=\prod_{\gamma>0}\left(\sum_{n\ge0}e^{-n\gamma}\right).
$$
The denominator needed for the asserted character identities is
$$
D=\prod_{\gamma>0}(e^{\gamma/2}-e^{-\gamma/2})
=e^\rho\prod_{\gamma>0}(1-e^{-\gamma}).
$$
The repeated identical subscripts in the printed denominator would instead make it zero, so those literal factors cannot satisfy an inverse identity. Also the required inverse uses the negative $\rho$ shift. Geometric-series cancellation gives
$$
\boxed{K*\delta_{-\rho}*D=\delta_0.}
$$
With a positive shift the left side would be $\delta_{2\rho}$, which displays the second printed sign problem even after the denominator is repaired.

<Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> gives $\pi_{M^\xi}=\delta_{\xi-\rho}*K$, since each ordered negative <root> monomial lowers the <highest weight> by its corresponding partition. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D*\pi_{M^\xi}=\delta_\xi.}
$$
The remaining parts use $D$ for the intended <Weyl denominator>, while the private TeX preserves the printed signs.

For a finite-support <formal character>, use the same pullback rule $(wu)(\xi)=u(w^{-1}\xi)$; equivalently $w(e^\lambda)=e^{w\lambda}$. This specifies the action on the denominator, which is a finite delta sum rather than a <polynomial> function. The whole infinite-support completion need not be Weyl-stable, so no such claim is required.

A simple <reflection> $s_\alpha$ permutes the positive roots other than $\alpha$ and sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$. In the product defining $D$, all factors are therefore permuted except the $\alpha$ factor, whose sign reverses. Thus $s_\alpha D=-D$. Simple reflections generate $W$, and each has <determinant> $-1$, so
$$
\boxed{wD=(\det w)D.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the intended finite-dimensional <irreducible> <highest weight> <module> $A^\phi$, with dominant integral <highest weight> $\phi$. Its <highest weight> multiplicity is one. An arbitrary <direct sum> having that same largest <representation weight> would not in general satisfy this formula. Set $f=\pi_{A^\phi}$ and use the corrected denominator $D$ of part (a).

Restriction to each <simple root> $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ decomposes into symmetric finite <representation weight> strings, so $f$ is Weyl-invariant. Consequently $Df$ is Weyl-antisymmetric. We prove that no lower Weyl alternants occur, rather than assuming the desired character formula.

First establish the denominator identity itself. The finite sum $D$ has support in $\rho-Q_+$ and coefficient one at $\rho$. Antisymmetry kills all singular <representation weight> coefficients, since a <reflection> fixing the <representation weight> reverses their sign. Every remaining Weyl orbit has a unique regular dominant representative $\eta$. If it occurs in $D$, then $\eta\le\rho$. Since $\eta$ is integral and regular dominant, $\eta-\rho$ is dominant integral; it also belongs to $-Q_+$. A dominant vector $v=-\sum n_i\alpha_i$ satisfies $(v,v)=-\sum n_i(v,\alpha_i)\le0$, so it must be zero. Thus the only possible orbit is $W\rho$. The leading coefficient gives
$$
D=\sum_{w\in W}(\det w)e^{w\rho}.
$$
For the formal <Laplacian> $\mathscr D(e^\lambda)=\|\lambda\|^2e^\lambda$, this implies $\mathscr D D=\|\rho\|^2D$.

Now choose <root> vectors dual under an invariant form, so $[E_\alpha,E_{-\alpha}]=H_\alpha$ with $H_\alpha$ corresponding to the vector $\alpha$, and take the quadratic Casimir
$$
C=\sum_iH_i^2+\sum_{\alpha>0}(E_\alpha E_{-\alpha}+E_{-\alpha}E_\alpha)
$$
for an orthonormal Cartan <basis>. On a highest vector its scalar is $c_\phi=\|\phi+\rho\|^2-\|\rho\|^2$, hence it has this scalar throughout the cyclic <highest weight> <module>.

Here is the computation of the weighted <trace> of its radial identity. For a <positive root>, let $X$ be the formal weighted <trace> of $E_{-\alpha}E_\alpha$ and $Y$ that of $E_\alpha E_{-\alpha}$. Cyclicity of <trace> and <representation weight> shifting give $Y=e^\alpha X$, while their difference is the weighted <trace> of $H_\alpha$, namely $(\alpha,\nabla f)$. Thus
$$
X+Y=\frac{1+e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}(\alpha,\nabla f).
$$
Taking the <trace> of $C$ yields
$$
c_\phi f=\mathscr D f+\sum_{\alpha>0}\frac{1+e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}(\alpha,\nabla f).
$$
The product expression for $D$ gives
$$
\nabla\log D=\rho+\sum_{\alpha>0}\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{1-e^{-\alpha}}\alpha.
$$
Using $2\rho=\sum_{\alpha>0}\alpha$ and the <product rule> for the formal <Laplacian> therefore gives
$$
\mathscr D(Df)=c_\phi Df+f\mathscr DD
=\|\phi+\rho\|^2Df.
$$
Hence every <representation weight> in the support of $Df$ has <norm> $\|\phi+\rho\|$. Its support is also contained in $\phi+\rho-Q_+$, and it is antisymmetric. Any occurring regular <dominant weight> $\eta$ thus satisfies $\eta\le\phi+\rho$. If the inequality were strict, then
$$
\|\phi+\rho\|^2-\|\eta\|^2
=(\phi+\rho-\eta,\phi+\rho+\eta)>0,
$$
because the first factor is a nonzero nonnegative sum of simple roots and the second is strictly dominant. This contradicts the equal-<norm> condition. Only the orbit $W(\phi+\rho)$ remains. Its leading coefficient is one, proving
$$
\boxed{D*\pi_{A^\phi}=\sum_{w\in W}(\det w)\delta_{w(\phi+\rho)}.}
$$

= Solution

Convolve the identity of part (c) with the inverse denominator $K*\delta_{-\rho}$ found in part (a). Associativity gives
$$
\pi_{A^\phi}=\sum_{w\in W}(\det w)\,K*\delta_{w(\phi+\rho)-\rho}.
$$
Translation by a <Kronecker delta> obeys $(K*\delta_\lambda)(\psi)=K(\psi-\lambda)$. Taking the coefficient at $\psi$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\pi_{A^\phi}(\psi)=\sum_{w\in W}(\det w)
K\bigl(\psi+\rho-w(\phi+\rho)\bigr).}
$$
This is the <Kostant multiplicity formula> in the paper's convention that $K$ counts negative <root> partitions. With a partition function counting <positive root> sums, each argument would have the opposite sign.
