= Solution
{parent=a}

Distinct <gamma matrices> anticommute, and $(\gamma^0)^2=I$, $(\gamma^j)^2=-I$ for $j=1,2,3$. Moving a particular $\gamma^\mu$ through the other three factors in $\gamma^5$ gives three minus signs. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{\gamma^5,\gamma^\mu\}=0.}
$$
Pairing equal factors in the square involves $3+2+1=6$ interchanges. The <Clifford algebra> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{(\gamma^5)^2=(-1)^6\prod_{\mu=0}^3(\gamma^\mu)^2=-I.}
$$
In particular $(\gamma^5)^{-1}=-\gamma^5$; replacing it by the conventional <chirality> matrix would change this answer.

The <trace> is unchanged by a <similarity transformation>. Since $(\gamma^0)^{-1}=\gamma^0$ and $\gamma^0\gamma^5\gamma^0=-\gamma^5$, <cyclic property of the trace> gives $\operatorname{tr}\gamma^5=-\operatorname{tr}\gamma^5$. Also
$$
\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu)=\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^5)=-\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}\gamma^5=\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu)=0.}
$$

The <mass dimensions> are $[\phi]=1$, $[\psi]=3/2$, $[g]=0$. Up to <total derivatives>, <power counting> allows local <counterterms> of <mass dimension> at most four. A convenient basis for the <counterterm Lagrangian> compatible with the actual <parity symmetry in quantum field theory> and <Dirac fermion number conservation> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\rm ct}^{(+)}={}&-\delta\Lambda+\delta Z_\psi\bar\psi i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi-\delta M\bar\psi\psi
+\tfrac12\delta Z_\phi\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi-\tfrac12\delta m^2\phi^2\\
&-\delta g\,\phi\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi-\frac{\delta\lambda}{4!}\phi^4.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The optional constant $\delta\Lambda$ renormalizes the <vacuum energy density>. The <wavefunction renormalization>, <mass counterterms> and <Yukawa coupling> renormalization correct the terms already present. The <quartic interaction> must also be included: the <fermion loop> with four external bosonic legs is logarithmically divergent, even if the <renormalized> quartic coupling is set to zero at one chosen <renormalization scale>. The displayed original interactions alone do not form a closed set of <counterterms>.

If “most general” is interpreted before imposing the <parity symmetry in quantum field theory> examined in <1/d>, <Lorentz invariance>, a <Hermitian> <action> and <Dirac fermion number conservation> also allow
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\rm ct}^{(-)}={}&-\delta t\,\phi-\frac{\delta\kappa}{3!}\phi^3-\delta y\,\phi\bar\psi\psi-\delta M_5\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi
+\delta Z_5\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\partial_\mu\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here all coefficients can be real in the paper's <gamma matrix> convention; the last term is Hermitian in the <action> up to a <total derivative>. It is an axial kinetic normalization, which could alternatively be removed by a suitable perturbative field redefinition. Every term in $\mathcal L_{\rm ct}^{(-)}$ is odd under the actual <parity symmetry in quantum field theory>, so its coefficient vanishes in a symmetry-preserving <renormalization scheme>. Listing these terms makes explicit which assumption excludes a <tadpole counterterm>, a cubic scalar term, a scalar <Yukawa interaction>, a pseudoscalar <Dirac mass term> and an axial kinetic term.

Expanding $e^{iS_{\rm int}}$ gives the <Feynman vertex>
$$
\boxed{V_{ab}=-ig(\gamma^5)_{ab}.}
$$
There is one scalar leg and a continuous oriented fermion line; no extra factor $i$ is absorbed into $\gamma^5$. The <Dirac propagator> is $S_F(k)=i(\not{k}+M)/(k^2-M^2+i0)$, where $\not{k}=\gamma^\mu k_\mu$. Each closed <fermion loop> supplies a minus sign.

\Image[2025-pseudoscalar-loops.png]
{title=Pseudoscalar Yukawa vertex and the vanishing one-loop bosonic one-point and three-point diagrams}

The <tadpole diagram> is proportional to
$$
-\int\frac{d^4k}{(2\pi)^4}\operatorname{tr}\left[(-ig\gamma^5)\frac{i(\not{k}+M)}{k^2-M^2+i0}\right]=0,
$$
because both numerator <traces> vanish by <1/a>. This is zero before the <loop momentum> integration.

For either orientation of the <triangle Feynman diagram>, let $a,b,c$ be its three internal <four-momenta>. Its numerator <trace> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
N&=\operatorname{tr}\left[\gamma^5(\not{a}+M)\gamma^5(\not{b}+M)\gamma^5(\not{c}+M)\right]\\
&=\operatorname{tr}\left[(\not{a}-M)(\not{b}+M)\gamma^5(\not{c}+M)\right]=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
After using <cyclic property of the trace>, each expanded term is a <trace> of $\gamma^5$ times at most three ordinary <gamma matrices>. Such <traces> vanish in four dimensions. For an odd number of ordinary <gamma matrices>, conjugation by $\gamma^5$ changes the sign. For two ordinary <gamma matrices>, the contracted <Clifford algebra> identity $\gamma_\rho\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho=4\eta^{\mu\nu}I$ gives
$$
4\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=-4\eta^{\mu\nu}\operatorname{tr}\gamma^5=0.
$$
The zero-matrix case is <1/a>. Hence **both the one-point and three-point one-loop bosonic vertices vanish**, independently of their external <momenta>. No cancellation between different loop orientations or delicate shift of a divergent <integral> is needed.

Writing $x_P=(t,-\mathbf x)$, the standard <parity transformation of a Dirac field> is
$$
\boxed{\psi'(x)=\eta_P\gamma^0\psi(x_P),\qquad \bar\psi'(x)=\eta_P^*\bar\psi(x_P)\gamma^0,\qquad |\eta_P|=1.}
$$
Since $\gamma^0\gamma^5\gamma^0=-\gamma^5$, the <fermion bilinear> $\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi$ changes sign. Invariance of the <pseudoscalar Yukawa interaction> therefore requires
$$
\boxed{\phi'(x)=-\phi(x_P).}
$$
Thus $\phi$ is a <pseudoscalar>, despite having the usual scalar-form <kinetic term> and <mass term>. In this convention $(\gamma^5)^\dagger=-\gamma^5$, but $\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi$ is Hermitian because of the additional $\gamma^0$ in the <Dirac adjoint>. A real $g$ is consequently consistent with a Hermitian <Lagrangian density>.

In <perturbation theory> about the parity-invariant <vacuum state>, <renormalization> can preserve this non-anomalous <parity symmetry in quantum field theory>. Every local <counterterm> must then be parity even, at every <loop order>. The allowed terms are precisely $\mathcal L_{\rm ct}^{(+)}$ in <1/b>; all coefficients in $\mathcal L_{\rm ct}^{(-)}$ are zero. In particular, **no $\phi$ or $\phi^3$ counterterm is generated**, whereas $\phi^4$ is allowed and required. The one-point and three-point pure-boson vertices also vanish to all orders: a parity-odd <Lorentz scalar> cannot be formed from their at most two independent external <four-momenta>. This last reasoning is specific to these low-point vertices; it does not assert that <parity symmetry in quantum field theory> is an independent $\phi\mapsto-\phi$ internal symmetry with the fermions held fixed.

= Solution

Independent <global phase symmetries> would rotate $\phi\mapsto e^{-i\alpha}\phi$ and $\Phi\mapsto e^{-i\beta}\Phi$. The cubic <interaction vertex> preserves only $2\alpha+\beta=0$ modulo $2\pi$. The continuous <internal symmetry of a classical field theory> is therefore the <circle group>
$$
\boxed{U(1):\quad\phi\mapsto e^{-i\alpha}\phi,\qquad\Phi\mapsto e^{2i\alpha}\Phi.}
$$
Normalize the <Noether charge> so that $q_\phi=1$. Then $q_\Phi=-2$, $q_{\bar\phi}=-1$ and $q_{\bar\Phi}=2$. The <Noether current> is
$$
\boxed{j^\mu=i\phi^\dagger\overleftrightarrow{\partial^\mu}\phi-2i\Phi^\dagger\overleftrightarrow{\partial^\mu}\Phi,\qquad\partial_\mu j^\mu=0,}
$$
where $A\overleftrightarrow\partial B=A\partial B-(\partial A)B$. For example, the <Euler-Lagrange field equations> give
$$
\partial_\mu(i\phi^\dagger\overleftrightarrow{\partial^\mu}\phi)=ih(\phi^2\Phi-\phi^{\dagger2}\Phi^\dagger),\qquad
\partial_\mu(i\Phi^\dagger\overleftrightarrow{\partial^\mu}\Phi)=\frac{ih}{2}(\phi^2\Phi-\phi^{\dagger2}\Phi^\dagger),
$$
which cancel with the relative coefficient $-2$. The quartic <interaction vertex> separately preserves both original phase rotations.

There is also a discrete <charge conjugation> symmetry $(\phi,\Phi)\mapsto(\phi^\dagger,\Phi^\dagger)$ for these real couplings. Including it, the internal symmetry is $U(1)\rtimes\mathbb Z_2\cong O(2)$, with <charge conjugation> reversing the <Noether charge>. The connected symmetry and its <conserved current> are the $U(1)$ above.

The nonzero free <scalar propagators> join a field to its adjoint:
$$
\boxed{\langle T\phi\phi^\dagger\rangle(p)=\frac{i}{p^2-m^2+i0},\qquad \langle T\Phi\Phi^\dagger\rangle(p)=\frac{i}{p^2-M^2+i0}.}
$$
Contractions $\langle T\phi\phi\rangle$, $\langle T\Phi\Phi\rangle$ and mixed-species contractions vanish. With all <momenta> incoming, the <Feynman vertices> are
$$
\boxed{V_{\phi\phi^\dagger\Phi\Phi^\dagger}=-ig,\qquad V_{\phi\phi\Phi}=-ih,\qquad V_{\phi^\dagger\phi^\dagger\Phi^\dagger}=-ih.}
$$
The two identical light legs cancel the $1/2!$ in each cubic <interaction vertex>; there is no remaining factor $1/2$ in its <Feynman rule>. Multiply each <Feynman vertex> by $(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(\sum p)$, and integrate an internal unconstrained <four-momentum> with $d^4p/(2\pi)^4$.

\Image[2025-charged-scalar-rules.png]
{title=Charged scalar propagators and vertices, with arrows following positive conserved charge and heavy arrows carrying two units}

The arrows in the figure follow positive <Noether charge>: a light arrow carries one unit and a heavy arrow two. Consequently a heavy <particle> $\Phi$ travels opposite its charge arrow, whereas its <antiparticle> $\bar\Phi$ travels along it. At a $\phi^2\Phi$ <Feynman vertex>, two unit-charge arrows enter and a two-unit arrow leaves; the conjugate <Feynman vertex> reverses every arrow. The quartic <Feynman vertex> has one incoming and one outgoing arrow of each weight. These rules prevent attaching a <propagator> between two unconjugated fields.

Let $p_1,p_2$ be the incoming and $p_3,p_4$ the outgoing <four-momenta>, and set $s=(p_1+p_2)^2$, $t=(p_1-p_3)^2$, $u=(p_1-p_4)^2$, the <Mandelstam variables>. The only connected <tree-level Feynman diagram> is the $s$-channel exchange of $\bar\Phi$, with <Noether charge> $+2$. The two incoming light <particles> attach to $\phi^2\Phi$, and the two outgoing light <particles> to its conjugate. There is no four-light contact <Feynman vertex>, nor a charge-allowed $t$ or $u$ exchange with these external labels.

\Image[2025-light-scalar-scattering.png]
{title=Light-particle scattering through a heavy antiparticle in the s channel}

Define the <invariant scattering amplitude> by $\langle f|S-1|i\rangle=i(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(P_f-P_i)\mathcal M$. Then
$$
i\mathcal M=(-ih)\frac{i}{s-M^2+i0}(-ih),\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal M(\phi\phi\to\phi\phi)=-\frac{h^2}{s-M^2+i0}.}
$$
The <Feynman vertices> already contain the identical-leg combinatorics. No extra factor of two multiplies this <invariant scattering amplitude>; the distinct <identical final-state symmetry factor> enters a <cross-section> integral.

Keep the <particle> labels specified under <2>. There are two connected <tree-level Feynman diagrams>: the quartic contact <interaction vertex> and an $s$-channel light <antiparticle> exchange. The initial <Noether charge> is $1-2=-1$, exactly the charge of $\bar\phi$. At the first cubic <Feynman vertex>, the incoming $\phi$ and $\Phi$ leave the second $\phi$ leg internal; the conjugate <Feynman vertex> supplies both outgoing <particles>.

\Image[2025-mixed-scalar-scattering.png]
{title=Scattering of a light particle and a heavy particle, with contact and s-channel light-antiparticle exchange diagrams}

Thus
$$
i\mathcal M=-ig+(-ih)\frac{i}{s-m^2+i0}(-ih),\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal M(\phi\Phi\to\phi\Phi)=-g-\frac{h^2}{s-m^2+i0}.}
$$
Here $s=(p_1+p_2)^2$ for incoming $\phi(p_1),\Phi(p_2)$. Both terms have the same sign in the chosen <S-matrix> convention.

The distinction from <antiparticles> matters. If the paper's heavy “$\Phi$ particle” is instead intended to mean the charge-$+2$ state $\bar\Phi$, so that its printed decay into $\phi\phi$ is allowed, the exchange diagram is crossed. For that alternative physical process,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal M(\phi\bar\Phi\to\phi\bar\Phi)=-g-\frac{h^2}{u-m^2+i0},\qquad u=(p_1-p_4)^2.}
$$
This is a consistent alternative naming convention only if it is also applied to the decay in <2/c/iii>; one cannot use the same heavy <particle> label for both charge assignments.

With the conventional <particle> labels, the allowed cubic <decay channels> are
$$
\boxed{\Phi\to\bar\phi\bar\phi,\qquad\bar\Phi\to\phi\phi,\qquad\mathcal M=-h\quad\hbox{for either channel}.}
$$
Each has one <tree-level Feynman diagram>, consisting of one cubic <Feynman vertex>. The final identical-leg contractions supply $2!$ and cancel the $1/2!$ in the <Lagrangian density>.

\Image[2025-heavy-scalar-decay.png]
{title=The two charge-conjugate heavy-scalar decays, with explicit antiparticle labels and conserved charge arrows}

Taken literally with these labels, **the printed $\Phi\to\phi\phi$ channel is forbidden**: its initial <Noether charge> is $-2$ and its final <Noether charge> is $+2$. Its <decay amplitude> is zero, at every order preserving the <global phase symmetry>. If the source uses “$\Phi$” to name the heavy <antiparticle> instead, its intended answer is the nonzero <decay amplitude> $-h$ for $\bar\Phi\to\phi\phi$. The following <decay width> calculation concerns either allowed channel, whose widths are equal by <charge conjugation>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For an allowed <decay channel> in <2/c/iii>, $|\mathcal M|^2=h^2$. Integrating the spatial <Dirac delta distribution> in the printed <Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure> sets $\mathbf p_2=-\mathbf p_1$. With $p=|\mathbf p_1|$ and $E_p=\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$, its remaining value is
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_2&=\int d\Phi_2=\frac1{4(2\pi)^2}\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{E_p^2}\,\delta(2E_p-M)\\
&=\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{p^2\,dp}{E_p^2}\,\delta(2E_p-M).
\end{aligned}
$$
The positive root is $p_*=(M/2)\beta$, where $\beta=\sqrt{1-4m^2/M^2}$, and the <Dirac delta distribution> contributes the <Jacobian determinant> $E_*/(2p_*)$. Hence
$$
I_2=\frac{p_*}{8\pi E_*}=\frac{\beta}{8\pi},\qquad E_*=\frac M2.
$$
It follows that the **literal printed integral** evaluates to
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{\rm printed}=\frac{h^2}{16\pi M}\sqrt{1-\frac{4m^2}{M^2}}.}
$$
The printed integration ranges count each final configuration twice, once as $(\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2)$ and once in the opposite order. For two identical final <bosons>, the physical <decay width> therefore requires an additional <identical final-state symmetry factor> $1/2!$:
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{\rm phys}=\frac{h^2}{32\pi M}\sqrt{1-\frac{4m^2}{M^2}}\simeq\frac{h^2}{32\pi M}\quad(M\gg m).}
$$
There is one allowed <decay channel> per heavy <particle>, not an extra factor of two from summing the two charge-conjugate heavy species. Both expressions vanish below the <two-body decay threshold> $M=2m$, and the limiting value at threshold is zero. For the literally forbidden labels $\Phi\to\phi\phi$, the <decay width> is instead zero because its <decay amplitude> vanishes, as explained in <2/c/iii>.

= Solution

For $g=0$, the <invariant scattering amplitude> from the <tree-level Feynman diagram> in <2/c/i> has a <pole> at $s=M^2$. The intermediate heavy <antiparticle> can go on shell and live for a time of order its <mean lifetime>. Near this <scattering resonance>, inserting a single <self-energy> is no longer a uniformly small correction. <Dyson resummation> gives, to leading accuracy in a narrow <decay width>,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal M(s)\simeq-\frac{h^2}{s-M^2+iM\Gamma_{\rm phys}}.}
$$
Here $M$ is understood as the <renormalized> resonance mass; the real <self-energy> shifts the original pole position. The imaginary term removes the unphysical divergence of the fixed-order <invariant scattering amplitude>.

For equal incoming and outgoing light masses, the two-body <momentum> ratio cancels in the <cross-section>. The angle-independent <invariant scattering amplitude>, integrated over the full solid angle with the <identical final-state symmetry factor> $1/2!$, gives $\sigma=|\mathcal M|^2/(32\pi s)$. Thus the <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(s)\simeq\frac{h^4}{32\pi s\left[(s-M^2)^2+M^2\Gamma_{\rm phys}^2\right]},\qquad
\Gamma_{\rm phys}=\frac{h^2\beta}{32\pi M}.}
$$
At $s\gg M^2$ it reproduces $h^4/(32\pi s^3)$. At the resonance peak,
$$
\boxed{\sigma(M^2)\simeq\frac{32\pi}{M^2\beta^2}\simeq\frac{32\pi}{M^2}\quad(M\gg m).}
$$
This is also $8\pi/k_*^2$ for $k_*=M\beta/2$, the elastic identical-boson <S-wave> <partial-wave unitarity> limit when this is the only open <decay channel>. The approximation assumes $\Gamma_{\rm phys}\ll M$ and a resonance sufficiently far from threshold for the width to vary slowly across it.

If one mechanically uses the unsymmetrized $\Gamma_{\rm printed}=2\Gamma_{\rm phys}$ from <2/d>, the same displayed <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section> would have that doubled width and predict a peak $8\pi/(M^2\beta^2)$. This is the literal-width substitution, rather than the consistent physical result: the given high-energy <cross-section> already includes the identical-final-particle factor that the printed <decay width> integral omits.

For three odd <fermionic operators>, the identity
$$
[A,BC]=\{A,B\}C-B\{A,C\}
$$
converts the <commutator> with a quadratic <Hamiltonian> into the given <canonical anticommutation relations>. Writing a mode label as $i=(\mathbf p,s)$, this yields
$$
[a_i,a_j^\dagger a_j]=\delta_{ij}a_j,\qquad
[b_i^\dagger,b_j^\dagger b_j]=-\delta_{ij}b_j^\dagger.
$$
The opposite-species number terms commute with each <fermionic creation operator> or <fermionic annihilation operator>, because two anticommutations give a positive sign. Restoring the continuous normalization, the <Dirac delta distribution> in the mode brackets cancels $d^3\mathbf q/(2\pi)^3$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[a^s_{\mathbf p},H]=E_{\mathbf p}a^s_{\mathbf p},\qquad[b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p},H]=-E_{\mathbf p}b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}.}
$$
Substitute these into the <mode expansion of a Dirac field>. The result is
$$
[\psi(x),H]=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s E_{\mathbf p}
\left[e^{-ip\cdot x}a^s_{\mathbf p}u^s(\mathbf p)-e^{ip\cdot x}b^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}v^s(\mathbf p)\right].
$$
Since $i\partial_t e^{-ip\cdot x}=E_{\mathbf p}e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and $i\partial_t e^{ip\cdot x}=-E_{\mathbf p}e^{ip\cdot x}$, this is exactly
$$
\boxed{i\partial_t\psi(t,\mathbf x)=[\psi(t,\mathbf x),H].}
$$
The <Heisenberg equation of motion> uses an ordinary <commutator> with the even <Hamiltonian>, despite the odd <fermionic operators> obeying <anticommutators> among themselves.

The <canonical anticommutation relations> give $\{a_1^\dagger,a_2^\dagger\}=0$, where each label includes both <momentum> and <spin>. Therefore
$$
|2,1\rangle=a_2^\dagger a_1^\dagger|0\rangle=-a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger|0\rangle=-|1,2\rangle.
$$
Thus **exchanging the complete labels of the two identical <fermions> changes the sign of their state**. If the labels coincide, $(a_1^\dagger)^2=0$; the state vanishes, which is the <Pauli exclusion principle>. A negative state vector describes the same isolated physical ray, but its relative sign matters when exchange alternatives interfere.

= Solution

For $i<j$, interchange the entries in slots $i,j$ while preserving the order of all intermediate <fermionic creation operators>. Move $a_j^\dagger$ left through $j-i$ factors, then move $a_i^\dagger$ right through the remaining $j-i-1$ intermediate factors. The <canonical anticommutation relations> give the overall <fermionic sign>
$$
(-1)^{(j-i)+(j-i-1)}=(-1)^{2(j-i)-1}=-1.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{|1,\ldots,j,\ldots,i,\ldots,n\rangle=-|1,\ldots,i,\ldots,j,\ldots,n\rangle.}
$$
More generally any <permutation> $\pi$ gives
$$
\boxed{|\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(n)\rangle=\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)|1,\ldots,n\rangle.}
$$
The <sign of a permutation> is independent of its decomposition into adjacent interchanges. Thus the $n$-<particle> sector is an <exterior power> of the one-<particle> state space, the defining antisymmetry of <fermionic Fock space>. Repeated complete mode labels make the state zero, so they satisfy the same identity trivially.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Put $z=x-y$ and $d\Pi_p=d^3\mathbf p/[(2\pi)^3 2E_{\mathbf p}]$. The <Dirac adjoint> of the printed <mode expansion of a Dirac field> is
$$
\bar\psi_a(x)=\int\frac{d^3\mathbf p}{(2\pi)^3\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}\sum_s
\left[e^{ip\cdot x}a^{s\dagger}_{\mathbf p}\bar u_a^s(\mathbf p)+e^{-ip\cdot x}b^s_{\mathbf p}\bar v_a^s(\mathbf p)\right].
$$
The <Fock vacuum> is annihilated by $a,b$. The <canonical anticommutation relations> therefore give the two non-time-ordered <vacuum expectation values>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle0|\bar\psi_a(x)\psi_b(y)|0\rangle&=\int d\Pi_p\,e^{-ip\cdot z}\sum_s\bar v_a^s(\mathbf p)v_b^s(\mathbf p),\\
\langle0|\psi_b(y)\bar\psi_a(x)|0\rangle&=\int d\Pi_p\,e^{ip\cdot z}\sum_s\bar u_a^s(\mathbf p)u_b^s(\mathbf p).
\end{aligned}
$$
In the first line, the only surviving contraction is $\langle b b^\dagger\rangle$; in the second it is $\langle a a^\dagger\rangle$. Both of these unordered contractions have positive coefficients.

However, <fermionic time ordering> includes a minus sign when it interchanges two odd fields:
$$
T\bar\psi_a(x)\psi_b(y)=\theta(z^0)\bar\psi_a(x)\psi_b(y)-\theta(-z^0)\psi_b(y)\bar\psi_a(x).
$$
Thus the result following from the stated <canonical anticommutation relations> and standard <time ordering> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
C_{ab}(x,y)&\equiv\langle0|T\bar\psi_a(x)\psi_b(y)|0\rangle\\
&=\int d\Pi_p\left[\theta(z^0)e^{-ip\cdot z}\sum_s\bar v_a^s v_b^s
-\theta(-z^0)e^{ip\cdot z}\sum_s\bar u_a^s u_b^s\right].
\end{aligned}}
$$
**The second term printed with a plus sign is not correct for standard <fermionic time ordering>.** Its plus-plus expression would be an ungraded chronological product. The missing minus cannot be absorbed by a phase choice for $u$ or $v$, since each <spin sum> contains a spinor and its adjoint.

For comparison with the conventional <Dirac propagator>, define $S_F(w)_{ba}=\langle0|T\psi_b(w)\bar\psi_a(0)|0\rangle$ and let $\mu$ denote this free <Dirac field>'s mass. Then
$$
\boxed{C_{ab}(x,y)=-S_F(y-x)_{ba}

= Solution

The corrected expression in <3/d> is a <vacuum two-point function>, or <Wick contraction>, of the free <Dirac field>. It equals the negative spinor transpose of the usual <Dirac propagator> with reversed spacetime arguments. It supplies the internal fermion lines in <Feynman diagrams>, rather than a position-space detection probability.

For $x^0>y^0$, $\psi(y)$ creates an <antiparticle> and $\bar\psi(x)$ annihilates it. The $v\bar v$ term describes positive-energy <antiparticle> propagation from $y$ to $x$. For $y^0>x^0$, $\bar\psi(x)$ creates a <particle> and $\psi(y)$ annihilates it. The $u\bar u$ term describes positive-energy <particle> propagation from $x$ to $y$, with the minus sign required by exchanging the odd operators in <fermionic time ordering>. Neither term describes a physical negative-energy excitation.

The <Heaviside step functions> select these chronological alternatives. The factors $e^{\mp ip\cdot z}$ are on-shell plane waves with $p^0=E_{\mathbf p}>0$, and $d\Pi_p$ is the positive-energy <Lorentz-invariant phase-space measure>. The sum over $s$ includes the two <spin> states. With standard relativistic spinor normalization, the <fermion spin sums> are
$$
\sum_s u^s(\mathbf p)\bar u^s(\mathbf p)=\not{p}+\mu,\qquad
\sum_s v^s(\mathbf p)\bar v^s(\mathbf p)=\not{p}-\mu.
$$
They encode the <spinor> structure and solve the corresponding on-shell <Dirac equations>. In particular, the corrected answer can also be written
$$
C_{ab}(x,y)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i(\not{p}-\mu)_{ba}}{p^2-\mu^2+i0}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}.
$$
The <Feynman i-epsilon prescription> specifies the temporal boundary conditions of the <Green function>; its positive- and negative-frequency residues reproduce the two terms and their relative minus sign. With $S_F$ defined in <3/d>,
$$
(i\not{\partial}-\mu)S_F(w)=i\delta^{(4)}(w)I.
$$
The contact term is fixed by the equal-time <canonical anticommutation relations>. Choosing a value for $\theta(0)$ does not change the unequal-time sign demonstrated above.

Finally, <microcausality> requires field <anticommutators> to vanish at spacelike separation; the time-ordered <vacuum two-point function> itself need not vanish there. These facts are compatible with relativistic causality. The plus-plus expression printed in the paper lacks the <fermionic sign> needed for this standard <Dirac propagator> interpretation.
