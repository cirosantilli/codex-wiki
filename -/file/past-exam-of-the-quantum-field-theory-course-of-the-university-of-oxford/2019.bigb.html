== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <real scalar field> suffices to describe one neutral species of bosonic <quasiparticle>. Let $\pi(\mathbf x)$ be its <canonical momentum>. In three spatial dimensions, choose the positive classical <Hamiltonian>
$$
\boxed{H[\phi,\pi]=\frac12\int d^3x\left[\pi^2+\kappa^2(\nabla^2\phi)^2+m^2\phi^2\right].}
$$
The same construction works in any number of spatial dimensions by replacing $d^3x$ appropriately. Under a spatial <Fourier transform>, $\nabla^2\phi$ becomes $-|\mathbf p|^2\widetilde\phi$, so the <Hamiltonian> is a collection of independent <quantum harmonic oscillators> with squared <angular frequency> $\omega_{\mathbf p}^2=\kappa^2|\mathbf p|^4+m^2$. The usual reality condition pairs opposite <momenta>; it does not introduce a second species.

The canonical <action> is $S=\int dt\,[\int d^3x\,\pi\dot\phi-H]$. Varying $\pi$ gives $\pi=\dot\phi$; eliminating it by the <Legendre transform> gives
$$
\boxed{S[\phi]=\frac12\int dt\,d^3x\left[(\partial_t\phi)^2-\kappa^2(\nabla^2\phi)^2-m^2\phi^2\right].}
$$
This <Lagrangian density> is local: only the field and finitely many derivatives at the same point occur. It contains second spatial derivatives but only first time derivatives, so no extra canonical time data are needed. A square root of a differential operator in the classical <Hamiltonian> is unnecessary. The fourth-order spatial term breaks <Lorentz invariance> while preserving spatial rotations and translations. This is a <free scalar field with quartic spatial dispersion>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <Lagrangian density> depending on $\phi$, $\partial_t\phi$, $\partial_i\phi$ and $\partial_i\partial_j\phi$, direct variation gives
$$
\delta S=\int dt\,d^3x\left[
\mathcal L_\phi\delta\phi+
\mathcal L_{\dot\phi}\partial_t\delta\phi+
\mathcal L_{\partial_i\phi}\partial_i\delta\phi+
\mathcal L_{\partial_i\partial_j\phi}\partial_i\partial_j\delta\phi\right].
$$
Take $\delta\phi$ to have compact support, so every boundary term vanishes. One <integration by parts> for the first-derivative terms and two for the second-derivative term yield the <Euler-Lagrange field equation with second derivatives>
$$
\mathcal L_\phi-\partial_t\mathcal L_{\dot\phi}
-\partial_i\mathcal L_{\partial_i\phi}
+\partial_i\partial_j\mathcal L_{\partial_i\partial_j\phi}=0.
$$
The plus sign on the last term is essential. For the <action> in <1/a>, it is particularly transparent to vary the <Laplacian> directly:
$$
\delta S=\int dt\,d^3x\left[
\dot\phi\,\partial_t\delta\phi-\kappa^2\nabla^2\phi\,\nabla^2\delta\phi-m^2\phi\,\delta\phi\right]
=-\int dt\,d^3x\left[\ddot\phi+\kappa^2\nabla^4\phi+m^2\phi\right]\delta\phi.
$$
Thus the classical <equation of motion> is
$$
\boxed{\partial_t^2\phi+\kappa^2\nabla^4\phi+m^2\phi=0.}
$$
Here $\nabla^4=(\nabla^2)^2$ is the <biharmonic operator>. Substitution of the <plane wave> $\phi=Ae^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$ gives
$$
[-\omega^2+\kappa^2|\mathbf p|^4+m^2]A=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\omega=\pm\sqrt{\kappa^2|\mathbf p|^4+m^2}.}
$$
A real classical solution combines complex-conjugate <plane waves>. Quantization assigns the positive branch to the excitation <energy>, giving the required <dispersion relation>. The negative-frequency branch supplies the <creation operator> part of the field, rather than negative excitation <energy>. Independently, the <Hamiltonian> in <1/a> gives $\dot\phi=\pi$, $\dot\pi=-\kappa^2\nabla^4\phi-m^2\phi$, confirming the same sign.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Define $E_{\mathbf p}=\sqrt{\kappa^2|\mathbf p|^4+m^2}$ and $\int_{\mathbf p}=\int d^3p/(2\pi)^3$. The Hermitian <quantum field> has the <mode expansion of a free field>
$$
\boxed{\phi(t,\mathbf x)=\int_{\mathbf p}\frac1{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}
\left[a_{\mathbf p}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}
+a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\right],}
$$
where the <annihilation operators> and <creation operators> obey the bosonic <canonical commutation relations>
$$
\boxed{[a_{\mathbf p},a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger]=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q),\qquad
[a_{\mathbf p},a_{\mathbf q}]=[a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger,a_{\mathbf q}^\dagger]=0.}
$$
Because $\pi=\dot\phi$, its <mode expansion of a free field> is
$$
\pi(t,\mathbf x)=-i\int_{\mathbf p}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathbf p}}2}
\left[a_{\mathbf p}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}
-a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger e^{iE_{\mathbf p}t-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\right].
$$
The two nonzero cross <commutators> then give
$$
[\phi(t,\mathbf x),\pi(t,\mathbf y)]
=\frac i2\int_{\mathbf p}\left[e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}+e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}\right]
=i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y),
$$
and the equal-time field-field and momentum-momentum <commutators> vanish. This verifies the factor $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$ without requiring <Lorentz invariance>.

Substitution into the <Hamiltonian>, followed by <normal ordering>, yields
$$
\boxed{{:H:}=\int_{\mathbf p}E_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger a_{\mathbf p}.}
$$
Consequently $[{:H:},a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger]=E_{\mathbf p}a_{\mathbf p}^\dagger$: one application of a <creation operator> adds precisely the required <quasiparticle> <energy>. Before <normal ordering> there is the usual constant zero-point <vacuum energy>. If $m=0$, the exactly zero-<momentum> mode has $E_{\mathbf0}=0$ and is a free canonical coordinate, not an oscillator with a normalizable oscillator <vacuum state>; in a finite box handle that mode separately or introduce a small positive <mass>. The formula applies directly to all modes with $E_{\mathbf p}>0$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\phi(x)=\int d^4p/(2\pi)^4\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\widetilde\phi(p)$, and similarly for $\Phi$. All <four-momenta> at an <interaction vertex> are incoming. The quadratic terms are diagonal, so the <scalar propagators> are
$$
\boxed{D_\phi(p)=\frac{i}{p^2-m^2+i0},\qquad
D_\Phi(p)=\frac{i}{p^2-M^2+i0},\qquad D_{\phi\Phi}=0.}
$$
The <field interaction terms> are $\lambda\phi\Phi^2/2$ and $g\Phi\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi/2$. The two identical field slots cancel the factor $1/2$ at either cubic <interaction vertex>. Expanding $\exp(iS_{\rm int})$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\phi(p)\Phi(k)\Phi(\ell):&\quad i\lambda,\qquad p+k+\ell=0,\\
\Phi(k)\phi(p)\phi(q):&\quad -ig\,p\cdot q,\qquad k+p+q=0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
In the second rule $p,q$ are specifically the two $\phi$ <four-momenta>; there is no derivative acting on the $\Phi$ leg. Indeed the two derivatives give $(-ip_\mu)(-iq^\mu)=-p\cdot q$, fixing the minus sign. This is a <mixed scalar derivative cubic vertex>. For arbitrary off-shell legs one may equivalently write $-ig(k^2-p^2-q^2)/2$, but may not replace the squared <four-momenta> by squared <masses> unless those legs are on their <mass shells>.

Each <interaction vertex> also carries $(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(\sum p)$ for <momentum conservation>. Integrate independent <loop momenta> with $d^4p/(2\pi)^4$ and divide by the <Feynman-diagram symmetry factor>. An amputated external scalar leg has factor 1. The <Feynman i-epsilon prescription> fixes the propagator poles. As a dimensional check in four spacetime dimensions, $[\phi]=[\Phi]=1$, $[\lambda]=1$ and $[g]=-1$ in <mass dimensions>; the two powers of <momentum> in the derivative rule restore <mass dimension> one for its cubic <interaction vertex>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $p_1,p_2$ denote the incoming $\phi,\Phi$ <four-momenta> and $p_3,p_4$ the outgoing $\phi,\Phi$ <four-momenta>. Thus $p_1+p_2=p_3+p_4$, $p_1^2=p_3^2=m^2$ and $p_2^2=p_4^2=M^2$. Define the <Mandelstam variables> and exchanged <four-momenta> by
$$
s=(p_1+p_2)^2=q_s^2,\qquad u=(p_1-p_4)^2=q_u^2,
\qquad q_s=p_1+p_2,\quad q_u=p_1-p_4=p_3-p_2.
$$
The leading connected four-point <scattering amplitude> uses two cubic <interaction vertices>. There are four <tree-level Feynman diagrams>: $s$ and $u$ exchange of $\Phi$ with two $\lambda$ vertices, and $s$ and $u$ exchange of $\phi$ with two $g$ vertices.

\Image[2019-mixed-scalar-tree-channels.png]
{title=The four tree channels for mixed-scalar scattering. Blue solid lines represent $\phi$ and orange dashed lines $\Phi$. External momenta are incoming on the left and outgoing on the right; crossings have no vertex.}

There is no term proportional to $\lambda g$: one vertex of each kind has three field slots of each species altogether, whereas the external state uses two of each. This leaves one $\phi$ and one $\Phi$ to contract internally, and their mixed <scalar propagator> is zero. There is also no $t$ channel: grouping both external $\phi$ legs at one vertex would require a $g$ vertex with an internal $\Phi$, while grouping both external $\Phi$ legs at the other would require a $\lambda$ vertex with an internal $\phi$.

Define $i\mathcal M$ after removing the overall <momentum conservation> delta function. The nonderivative exchange gives
$$
i\mathcal M_{\lambda^2}=(i\lambda)^2
\left[\frac{i}{s-M^2+i0}+\frac{i}{u-M^2+i0}\right].
$$
For the derivative $s$ channel, the incoming $\phi$ <four-momenta> at the first vertex are $p_1,-q_s$, and at the second they are $q_s,-p_3$. The two <interaction vertices> are therefore $ig\,p_1\cdot q_s$ and $ig\,p_3\cdot q_s$. The same reasoning with $q_u$ applies to the $u$ channel, giving
$$
i\mathcal M_{g^2}=-ig^2\left[
\frac{(p_1\cdot q_s)(p_3\cdot q_s)}{s-m^2+i0}
+\frac{(p_1\cdot q_u)(p_3\cdot q_u)}{u-m^2+i0}\right].
$$
The <mass shell> conditions and <momentum conservation> imply
$$
p_1\cdot q_s=p_3\cdot q_s=\frac{s+m^2-M^2}{2},\qquad
p_1\cdot q_u=p_3\cdot q_u=\frac{u+m^2-M^2}{2}.
$$
Combining the four <Feynman diagrams> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&-\lambda^2\left[\frac1{s-M^2+i0}+\frac1{u-M^2+i0}\right]\\
&-\frac{g^2}{4}\left[
\frac{(s+m^2-M^2)^2}{s-m^2+i0}
+\frac{(u+m^2-M^2)^2}{u-m^2+i0}\right].
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each displayed <tree-level Feynman diagram> has <Feynman-diagram symmetry factor> 1 for fixed external species and labels. The result is of order $\lambda^2,g^2$; if both couplings are scaled by a common parameter $\varepsilon$, the next connected corrections begin at order $\varepsilon^4$. A four-point cubic <Feynman diagram> has $3V=2I+4$, so its vertex count $V$ is even. The internal lines remain off shell throughout: imposing an on-shell relation on an exchanged <four-momentum> would incorrectly change the derivative vertices.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, the <Clifford algebra> gives $(\gamma^0)^2=1$, $(\gamma^1)^2=-1$, and $\gamma^0\gamma^1=-\gamma^1\gamma^0$. Hence
$$
\gamma^5\gamma^0=-\gamma^1,\quad \gamma^0\gamma^5=\gamma^1,\qquad
\gamma^5\gamma^1=-\gamma^0,\quad \gamma^1\gamma^5=\gamma^0,
$$
so $\{\gamma^5,\gamma^0\}=\{\gamma^5,\gamma^1\}=0$. Its square is
$$
(\gamma^5)^2=\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^0\gamma^1
=-(\gamma^0)^2(\gamma^1)^2=1.
$$
Choose the following <gamma matrices>, expressed using the <Pauli matrices>:
$$
\boxed{\gamma^0=\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\gamma^1=-i\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\gamma^5=\sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
These satisfy $(\gamma^0)^\dagger=\gamma^0$, $(\gamma^1)^\dagger=-\gamma^1$ and $(\gamma^5)^\dagger=\gamma^5$. The <chirality> <eigenvalues> are therefore $\boxed{+1,-1}$, and we order the <spinor> as $\psi=(\psi_R,\psi_L)^{\mathsf T}$ throughout. Its <Dirac adjoint> is $\bar\psi=(\psi_L^\dagger,\psi_R^\dagger)$. No additional factor of $i$ multiplies $\gamma^0\gamma^1$ in this spacetime dimension. This realizes a <Dirac field in two spacetime dimensions>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Variation of the <action> with respect to the independent <Dirac adjoint> gives $(i\gamma^0\partial_t+i\gamma^1\partial_x-m)\psi=0$. The <gamma matrices> chosen in <3/a> make the <Dirac equation>
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}
-m&i(\partial_t-\partial_x)\\
i(\partial_t+\partial_x)&-m
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_R\\\psi_L\end{pmatrix}=0.}
$$
Multiplication by $\gamma^0$ gives the equivalent <Dirac Hamiltonian> form
$$
i\partial_t\psi=(-i\sigma_3\partial_x+m\sigma_1)\psi.
$$
For a <plane wave> $\psi=u\,e^{-iEt+ipx}$, where $p$ is the signed one-dimensional <momentum>, the equation is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}E-p&-m\\-m&E+p\end{pmatrix}u=0.
$$
A nonzero <spinor> exists precisely when the <determinant> vanishes:
$$
\boxed{E^2-p^2-m^2=0,\qquad E=\pm\sqrt{p^2+m^2}.}
$$
For $m\ne0$, one possible unnormalized <spinor> solution is $u=(E+p,m)^{\mathsf T}$. Alternatively, $(p\sigma_3+m\sigma_1)^2=(p^2+m^2)1$ follows from the <Pauli matrix multiplication law>, proving the same <relativistic energy-momentum relation>. Acting on the <Dirac equation> with $i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu+m$ also shows that each component satisfies $(\partial_t^2-\partial_x^2+m^2)\psi=0$, the <Klein-Gordon equation>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <chirality> basis of <3/a>, the two rows of the <Dirac Hamiltonian> equation give
$$
\boxed{i(\partial_t+\partial_x)\psi_R=m\psi_L,\qquad
i(\partial_t-\partial_x)\psi_L=m\psi_R.}
$$
The <Dirac mass term> thus couples the two opposite <chirality> components. In particular, the <Lagrangian density> is
$$
\mathcal L=i\psi_R^\dagger(\partial_t+\partial_x)\psi_R
+i\psi_L^\dagger(\partial_t-\partial_x)\psi_L
-m(\psi_L^\dagger\psi_R+\psi_R^\dagger\psi_L).
$$
For $m=0$, the coupling disappears and the <massless Dirac equation> splits into the independent transport equations
$$
\boxed{(\partial_t+\partial_x)\psi_R=0,\qquad
(\partial_t-\partial_x)\psi_L=0.}
$$
These are the two <chiral transport equations in two spacetime dimensions>. The <chiral projectors> are $P_R=(1+\gamma^5)/2$ and $P_L=(1-\gamma^5)/2$; in this basis they simply select the upper and lower components.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Continue with $m=0$. Along a characteristic $x=x_0+t$, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\psi_R(t,x_0+t)=(\partial_t+\partial_x)\psi_R=0.
$$
Thus the initial <wave packet> $\psi_R(0,x)=f(x)$ evolves as $\psi_R(t,x)=f(x-t)$. Similarly, along $x=x_0-t$, the left component is constant, giving
$$
\boxed{\psi_R(t,x)=f(x-t),\qquad \psi_L(t,x)=h(x+t).}
$$
A feature initially at $x_0$ therefore appears at $x_0+t$ for the right component and $x_0-t$ for the left component. The profiles translate without changing shape at the <speed of light>, which is 1 in these units. For positive-<energy> <plane waves>, the right component has $E=p>0$ and the left component has $E=-p>0$, another way to identify their propagation directions.

The conclusion concerns the <massless Dirac equation>. For $m\ne0$, the components couple as in <3/c>, and a positive-<energy> packet has <group velocity> $p/\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$, rather than a universal speed $\pm1$. For example, a massive positive-<energy> rest solution has $p=0$ and $\psi_R=\psi_L$, so neither component alone describes an independently moving light-speed packet.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

A continuous <Lorentz transformation> in one spatial dimension must preserve $t^2-x^2$. In the component connected to the identity, take the image of the time axis to have velocity $v$, with $|v|<1$, and write $B_v(1,0)^{\mathsf T}=\gamma(1,v)^{\mathsf T}$. Preservation of the <Minkowski norm> gives $\gamma^2(1-v^2)=1$, so $\gamma=(1-v^2)^{-1/2}$. The spacelike column must be orthogonal to the timelike column and have squared <Minkowski norm> $-1$; continuity fixes it to $\gamma(v,1)^{\mathsf T}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x'=B_vx,\qquad
B_v=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&v\\v&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Here $x=(t,x)^{\mathsf T}$ denotes the coordinate pair. This is the positive off-diagonal convention in the source; describing a passive frame moving at $+v$ instead reverses the sign of $v$.

Define the <rapidity> $\alpha=\operatorname{artanh}v$. The <hyperbolic function> identities give
$$
\cosh\alpha=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\qquad
\sinh\alpha=\frac v{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\qquad
\boxed{B(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\alpha&\sinh\alpha\\\sinh\alpha&\cosh\alpha\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In <light-cone coordinates>, $t'+x'=e^\alpha(t+x)$ and $t'-x'=e^{-\alpha}(t-x)$. Therefore the derivatives transform oppositely:
$$
\partial_{t'}+\partial_{x'}=e^{-\alpha}(\partial_t+\partial_x),\qquad
\partial_{t'}-\partial_{x'}=e^{\alpha}(\partial_t-\partial_x).
$$
Use the field-transformation convention $\psi'(x')=S(\alpha)\psi(x)$. The component <action> in <3/c> is invariant under
$$
\boxed{\psi_R'(x')=e^{\alpha/2}\psi_R(x),\qquad
\psi_L'(x')=e^{-\alpha/2}\psi_L(x),\qquad
\Lambda_R(\alpha)=e^{\alpha/2},\quad\Lambda_L(\alpha)=e^{-\alpha/2}.}
$$
Indeed, the right-component <kinetic term> gains $e^{\alpha/2}e^{-\alpha}e^{\alpha/2}=1$, and the left-component <kinetic term> gains $e^{-\alpha/2}e^{\alpha}e^{-\alpha/2}=1$. The two factors in each <Dirac mass term> cancel as well. Finally $\det B=1$, so $dt'\,dx'=dt\,dx$ and the whole <action> is unchanged. These are the <chiral boost weights in two spacetime dimensions>.

In <spinor> notation, $S(\alpha)=\exp(\alpha\gamma^5/2)$ satisfies $S^{-1}\gamma^\mu S=B^\mu{}_{\nu}\gamma^\nu$ and $S^\dagger\gamma^0S=\gamma^0$, so $\bar\psi'=\bar\psi S^{-1}$. These identities directly verify covariance of the <Dirac equation> and the <Lorentz invariance> of its <action>. The matrices $S$ are not unitary with respect to the ordinary component norm; the <Dirac adjoint> supplies the invariant bilinear.

The statement about there being only <Lorentz boosts> applies to the identity component of the <one-dimensional Lorentz group>. The full <Lorentz group> also has discrete transformations: the concrete <spatial reflection> $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ preserves the <Minkowski metric> but has <determinant> $-1$, so it cannot equal any displayed $B(\alpha)$, whose <determinant> is $+1$.
