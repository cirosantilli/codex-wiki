=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <neutron> has valence content $udd$ and total <spin> $J=1/2$; its ground-state parity is positive. The two <down quarks> contribute charge $-2/3$ and the <up quark> contributes $+2/3$, giving net charge zero. “Three valence <quarks>” specifies the net flavor quantum numbers, rather than excluding <gluons> or additional sea <quark>–<antiquark> components from the full <quantum chromodynamics> state.

In the approximate light-flavor SU(3) classification, the <neutron> belongs to the ground-state <baryon octet>, containing eight <hadrons>:
$$
\boxed{p,n;\quad\Sigma^+,\Sigma^0,\Sigma^-;\quad\Lambda^0;\quad\Xi^0,\Xi^-.}
$$
These are eight particle species, without counting their <antiparticles> again as extra members of the same octet. The corresponding <antibaryons> form the conjugate particle set. Within the smaller SU(2) <isospin> classification, the <neutron> instead belongs to the two-member nucleon doublet $(p,n)$. Stating the symmetry distinguishes the usual eight-member flavor multiplet answer from the also valid two-member <isospin> multiplet. The <baryon decuplet> has different ground-state spin and is not the <neutron>'s octet.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Uniform probability per volume does not give a uniform radial distribution. A shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ occupies volume $4\pi r^2dr$, so the radial <probability density function> is
$$
f(r)=\frac{3r^2}{R^3},\qquad0\leq r\leq R.
$$
It integrates to one. The second radial moment is
$$
\langle r^2\rangle=\int_0^R r^2\frac{3r^2}{R^3}dr=\frac35R^2,
\qquad\boxed{r_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\frac35}R=1.162\ \mathrm{fm}\quad(R=1.5\ \mathrm{fm}).}
$$
The <radial uncertainty scale in a uniform sphere> supplies the position-uncertainty scale **as instructed by the toy model**. It is not literally the <standard deviation> of the scalar radius: $\langle r\rangle=3R/4$ and $\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle-\langle r\rangle^2}=R\sqrt{3/80}$. Nor is a uniform spatial probability density sufficient to determine a unique <momentum space> <wavefunction>. This explicit distinction keeps the order-of-magnitude assumption separate from a fully specified quantum bound-state calculation. Replacing the radial density by a uniform density in $r$ would give a different second moment because it omits the spherical volume element.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Adopt the lower-bound scale of the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>, $\Delta r\Delta p\sim\hbar/2$, with the prescribed radial scale from <1/b/a>. Using $\hbar c=197.327$ MeV fm,
$$
\boxed{\Delta p\,c\sim\frac{\hbar c}{2r_{\rm rms}}=84.92\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
For a one-sided <exponential distribution> of a positive momentum magnitude, $f(p)=p_0^{-1}e^{-p/p_0}$, both its mean and its <standard deviation> are $p_0$. Under the additional identification made in this estimate, set the characteristic momentum to $\Delta p$. This identification concerns a positive magnitude; the mean of a signed momentum component in an isotropic stationary state would instead vanish.

Use the <relativistic energy-momentum relation>, not a nonrelativistic kinetic-energy approximation, because the momentum-energy scale is much larger than the stipulated <quark> rest energy:
$$
\boxed{E_q\sim\sqrt{(\Delta p\,c)^2+(m_qc^2)^2}
=\sqrt{(84.92)^2+5^2}\ \mathrm{MeV}=85.06\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
This is a characteristic energy estimated at the characteristic momentum. It is not an exact evaluation of $\langle\sqrt{p^2c^2+m_q^2c^4}\rangle$ over a fully specified three-dimensional wavefunction. An order-of-magnitude convention $\Delta p\sim\hbar/\Delta r$ would give an answer about twice as large; the uncertainty principle by itself specifies a bound, not a unique confinement energy.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Adding the three characteristic <quark> energies from <1/b/b> gives
$$
\boxed{m_n^{\rm estimate}c^2\sim3E_q=255.2\ \mathrm{MeV},\qquad
\frac{m_n^{\rm true}}{m_n^{\rm estimate}}\sim\frac{940}{255.2}=3.68.}
$$
The estimate recovers a sub-GeV scale but underestimates the stated <neutron> mass by a factor of a few. This is consistent with its deliberately crude uncertainty and geometry assumptions; it is not a precision mass prediction.

A better model needs the actual spatial and momentum correlations, a confining interaction, and the energy in the <quark> and <gluon> fields. In <quantum chromodynamics>, hadron masses are not simply the sum of three freely moving particles with tiny masses. The supplied $5$ MeV rest-energy scale resembles a light current-quark scale; a phenomenological constituent-quark mass includes substantial dressing and is a different parameter. Color, spin correlations, relativistic dynamics and sea components all matter when defining a more realistic bound-state <Hamiltonian operator> or performing a <lattice QCD> calculation. The prescribed uniform-sphere model supplies none of those extra dynamical inputs, so its discrepancy should not be repaired by an unannounced change of the given radius or mass.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The charged weak current changes one <neutron> <down quark> into an <up quark>:
$$
d\to u+W^{-*},\qquad W^{-*}\to e^-+\bar\nu_e,
\qquad\boxed{n(udd)\to p(uud)+e^-+\bar\nu_e.}
$$
The other two valence <quarks> are spectators in this tree-level <Feynman diagram>. The intermediate <W boson> is highly virtual; the available <neutron> decay energy is far below its on-shell rest energy. Charge is conserved at each vertex, and the <electron>'s lepton number is balanced by the <electron antineutrino>.

The reversed quark-level charged current is allowed as an interaction, but a **free** <proton> cannot decay as $p\to n+e^++\nu_e$ because $m_p<m_n+m_e$. <Four-momentum conservation> leaves no accessible final-state <Lorentz-invariant phase space>. The absence of this free-proton beta-plus decay is therefore kinematic, not an absence of a $u\to d$ weak vertex. Binding-energy differences in a nucleus, or incoming energy in a collision, can change whether the corresponding conversion is possible. Exotic baryon-number-violating <proton> decay is a separate question and is not generated by this ordinary charged-current diagram.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Work first in units $c=1$. In the <neutron> rest frame, let $s_{p\nu}=(p_p+p_\nu)^2$. From $p_n-p_e=p_p+p_\nu$,
$$
s_{p\nu}=m_n^2+m_e^2-2m_nE_e,
\qquad E_e=\frac{m_n^2+m_e^2-s_{p\nu}}{2m_n}.
$$
The <electron>'s minimum possible total energy is its rest energy. This configuration has the <electron> at rest while the <proton> and massless <antineutrino> move back to back with equal momentum magnitudes. Their common momentum satisfies a two-body energy balance for the remaining mass $m_n-m_e$:
$$
\boxed{E_{e,\min}=m_ec^2,\qquad
|\mathbf p_p|=|\mathbf p_\nu|=
\frac{(m_n-m_e)^2-m_p^2}{2(m_n-m_e)}c,\quad\mathbf p_p=-\mathbf p_\nu.}
$$
For the maximum <electron> energy, minimize $s_{p\nu}$. Since the massless <antineutrino> has nonnegative energy, $s_{p\nu}\geq m_p^2$, with the boundary reached as its energy tends to zero. Then
$$
\boxed{E_{e,\max}=\frac{m_n^2+m_e^2-m_p^2}{2m_n}c^2.}
$$
At this limiting endpoint the <electron> and <proton> have equal and opposite momenta. The <proton> recoils; it is **not** at rest. A massless <neutrino> has no rest frame, so “<neutrino> at rest” must be replaced by the precise soft-energy limit $E_\nu\to0$. The <electron>'s maximum <kinetic energy> is the displayed total energy minus $m_ec^2$, rather than the same number. These endpoint configurations lie on phase-space boundaries; their kinematic existence does not imply a nonzero event density exactly at the boundary.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For exponential decay with <mean lifetime> $\tau$, the state amplitude evolves as $\exp[-i(E_0-i\Gamma/2)t/\hbar]$ and its survival probability is $e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$. Hence the energy <decay width> is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=\frac\hbar\tau=
\frac{6.58212\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{eV\,s}}{15\times60\ \mathrm s}
=7.31\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The exceptionally small energy width expresses the long lifetime of this weak decay. Use $\hbar$, not $h$, because $\Gamma$ is an energy width in the complex-energy convention; changing to a frequency convention introduces the appropriate factor of $2\pi$. The quoted lifetime is treated as the <mean lifetime>. If a half-life were supplied instead, one would first use $\tau=t_{1/2}/\ln2$. No extra logarithm is needed for the given mean-lifetime interpretation. This calculation uses the source's approximate 15-minute value rather than silently replacing it with a more precise contemporary neutron-lifetime measurement.

==== Solution

The light <pseudoscalar meson nonet> has $J^P=0^-$ and flavor structure $3\otimes\bar3=8\oplus1$. Its members are the three <pions>, four <kaons>, and the two neutral isospin-zero states <eta meson> and <eta prime meson>. For mesons the <baryon number> is zero, so the <Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula> reduces to
$$
\boxed{Q=I_3+S/2.}
$$
Here $S$ is <strangeness>, with an $s$ <quark> carrying $-1$ and an <antiquark> $\bar s$ carrying $+1$. Thus the upper row is $K^0=d\bar s$ at $(I_3,S)=(-1/2,1)$ and $K^+=u\bar s$ at $(1/2,1)$. The lower row is $K^-=s\bar u$ at $(-1/2,-1)$ and $\bar K^0=s\bar d$ at $(1/2,-1)$. The charged <pions> lie at $(\pm1,0)$, while $\pi^0,\eta,\eta'$ share $(0,0)$ as flavor-weight coordinates.

The neutral <pion> direction is $(u\bar u-d\bar d)/\sqrt2$. The octet and singlet isoscalar directions mix to make the physical eta and eta-prime states; counting three central states is essential to obtaining nine rather than seven particles. Horizontal lines in the diagram have constant $S$, while lines $I_3+S/2=Q$ mark constant charge. Meson exchange describes part of the residual nuclear force; the underlying quark-level <strong interaction> is mediated by <gluons>, not by treating <pions> as elementary gauge bosons.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The charged meson decays proceed through a virtual $W^+$:
$$
\pi^+(u\bar d)\to W^{+*}\to\mu^+\nu_\mu,\qquad
K^+(u\bar s)\to W^{+*}\to\mu^+\nu_\mu.
$$
The first <quark> vertex has CKM factor $V_{ud}$, while the second has $V_{us}$. In the simple Cabibbo description these are approximately $\cos\theta_C$ and $\sin\theta_C$, so the strange amplitude carries a suppression $\tan\theta_C$ relative to the nonstrange one, or $\tan^2\theta_C\simeq0.053$ in their corresponding squared weak-coupling factors.

For a charged pseudoscalar and a negligibly massive <neutrino>, the leading leptonic energy width has the form
$$
\Gamma(P^+\to\mu^+\nu_\mu)=
\frac{G_F^2f_P^2|V_{qq'}|^2}{8\pi}m_\mu^2m_P
\left(1-\frac{m_\mu^2}{m_P^2}\right)^2
$$
in natural units. The helicity factor $m_\mu^2$ is common to these two <muon> channels, while the threshold factor is much less favorable for the <pion>. Using the usual masses, its value is about $0.182$ for the <pion> and $0.910$ for the charged <kaon>: the <pion>'s threshold factor alone is only about one fifth as large. Including the explicit $m_P$ factor gives a kinematic ratio about $0.0564$. The <pseudoscalar decay constants> $f_P$ also matter, so <Lorentz-invariant phase space> and CKM factors alone do not determine the full lifetime ratio.<Leptonic Decays of Charged Pseudoscalar Mesons by Particle Data Group>[leading pseudoscalar leptonic-width formula and <pion>/<kaon> discussion]{ref}

For $K^+\to\pi^+\pi^0$, one tree topology has the spectator $u$ unchanged and $\bar s\to\bar u+W^{+*}$ followed by $W^{+*}\to u\bar d$. The spectator $u$ can combine with $\bar d$ to form $\pi^+$, and the $u\bar u$ component supplies $\pi^0$. This carries the strangeness-changing weak factor $V_{us}^*V_{ud}$. <Hadronization>, other <quark> rearrangements and strong final-state interactions determine the hadronic amplitude; the diagram is not a complete precision width calculation. The small <pion>–<muon> energy release suppresses the <pion> leptonic channel kinematically, whereas the strange weak vertex suppresses the <kaon> channels through <Cabibbo suppression>.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Use $c=1$ and let $E$ denote the **total** center-of-momentum energy, not one beam's energy. Opposite equal momenta give $E=2E_{\rm beam}=498$ MeV, so the <kaon> is at its stipulated resonance mass $m_K=498$ MeV. Write the unspecified selected exit <branching fractions> as $b_\rho$ and $b_K$. The supplied line shape gives
$$
\mathcal R(E)\equiv\frac{\sigma_\rho}{\sigma_K}
=\frac{3b_\rho}{0.69b_K}\frac{\Gamma_\rho^2}{\Gamma_K^2}
\frac{(E^2-m_K^2)^2+E^2\Gamma_K^2}
{(E^2-m_\rho^2)^2+E^2\Gamma_\rho^2}.
$$
The distinction between <inclusive and exclusive resonance branching factors> matters here. For inclusive resonance formation, sum over all decays, so $b_\rho=b_K=1$. At $E=m_K$ the small <kaon> width cancels from the peak value:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R_{\rm inclusive}(498)=\frac3{0.69}
\frac{498^2\,147^2}{(498^2-769^2)^2+498^2\,147^2}
=0.1890.}
$$
If instead the measured cross section is specifically resonant elastic $\pi^+\pi^-\to\pi^+\pi^-$, its exit <branching fractions> equal the entrance ones: $b_\rho=1$, $b_K=0.69$. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R_{\pi\pi}(498)=0.1890/0.69=0.2740.}
$$
The source says production without specifying an observed decay channel, so both conventions are made explicit rather than silently squaring a <branching fraction> for an inclusive measurement. The quoted <kaon> width and two-pion <branching fraction> identify the short-lived propagation state $K_S$; a pure flavor state $K^0$ is a superposition of short- and long-lived states. This exercise uses the stipulated $K_S$ resonance model and equal omitted proportionality factors.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Raising each beam energy to 250 MeV makes $E=500$ MeV. The <kaon> denominator is now dominated by
$$
(E^2-m_K^2)^2=(500^2-498^2)^2=(1996\ \mathrm{MeV^2})^2,
$$
rather than by $E^2\Gamma_K^2$. Substituting into <2/c/a> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R_{\rm inclusive}(500)=5.606\times10^{22},\qquad
\mathcal R_{\pi\pi}(500)=8.125\times10^{22}.}
$$
The enormous change is a tiny-width effect, not a comparably abrupt change in the broad rho contribution. The center-of-momentum energy has moved about $2/(7.4\times10^{-12})\simeq2.7\times10^{11}$ <kaon> widths from the peak. A width of $7.4\times10^{-12}$ MeV is only $7.4\times10^{-6}$ eV, requiring an energy-selection scale of order $10^{-14}$ relative to the several-hundred-MeV beam energy.

Real beams have vastly broader energy distributions. Averaging over such a distribution suppresses the useful narrow-resonance contribution: the finite peak value does not imply a large energy-integrated formation rate, whose resonant area shrinks with the width at fixed <branching fractions>. The weak inverse-decay entrance coupling and the broad strong-interaction rho background therefore make this a poor way to study <kaon> resonant formation. The supplied broad-resonance formula is used algebraically as requested; a practical calculation would convolve an appropriate narrow line shape with the actual beam distribution.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The beam energy is a **total** <pion> energy, so the momentum follows the relativistic relation $p^2c^2=E_\pi^2-m_\pi^2c^4$. Taking the standard charged-pion rest energy $m_\pi c^2=139.57$ MeV,<Charged Pion Particle Listing by Particle Data Group>[page 1, charged-pion mass table]{ref}
$$
pc=\sqrt{249^2-139.57^2}\ \mathrm{MeV}=206.21\ \mathrm{MeV}.
$$
For motion perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field, the <Lorentz force> supplies the transverse momentum change and gives $r=p/(qB)$. With the stipulated $qB=1$ eV s m$^{-2}$,
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{206.21\times10^6\ \mathrm{eV}/c}{1\ \mathrm{eV\,s\,m^{-2}}}
\simeq0.688\ \mathrm m.}
$$
The unit cancellation leaves length after dividing by $c$ in metres per second. Equivalently, the usual magnetic-rigidity conversion is $p[\mathrm{GeV}/c]\simeq0.2998\,B[\mathrm T]r[\mathrm m]$ for unit charge. The sign of the <pion> charge chooses the bending direction, while the radius uses its magnitude. Treating the <pion> as massless would give about $0.831$ m and is not a good approximation at this modest total energy. The production target does not determine the downstream orbit once the <pion> momentum and magnetic field are specified.

==== Solution

The first step of the <proton-proton chain> is
$$
\boxed{p+p\longrightarrow d+e^++\nu_e,\qquad Q\simeq0.420\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
Here $d$ is the <deuteron>. A <weak charged current> converts one <proton> into a <neutron>, while the resulting <proton>–<neutron> pair becomes bound. The <Q value> follows from the difference of initial and final rest energies:
$$
Q=(2m_p-m_d-m_e)c^2
=B_d-(m_n-m_p)c^2-m_ec^2
\simeq2.2246-1.2933-0.5110=0.4203\ \mathrm{MeV}.
$$
This includes the emitted <positron>'s rest energy in the final-state mass and excludes its subsequent <electron-positron annihilation>. The <nuclear binding energy> of the <deuteron> makes this reaction possible although the isolated conversion $p\to n+e^++\nu_e$ is energetically forbidden. The released energy is shared by final-state kinetic energies, including the escaping <neutrino>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The radiation registered in the stated <solar luminosity> includes two <positron> annihilations but excludes the two escaping <neutrinos>. Thus the visible energy per completed helium-producing chain is
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm vis}=24.68+2(1.02)-2(0.26)=26.20\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The signs in this energy ledger matter: adding the <neutrino> energy would overestimate the energy available to sustain the measured radiation. Using $1\ \mathrm{MeV}=1.602176634\times10^{-13}$ J, the chain rate and hydrogen-consumption rate are
$$
\dot N_{\rm chain}=\frac{3.86\times10^{26}}{26.20(1.602176634\times10^{-13})}
=9.196\times10^{37}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\dot N_H=4\dot N_{\rm chain}=3.678\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$
With a year taken as $365.25$ days, the stated elapsed time is $1.4516496\times10^{17}$ s. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{N_{H,\rm used}=5.34\times10^{55},\qquad
N_{H,\rm remaining}=8.47\times10^{56}.}
$$
At the same fixed <luminosity>, the remaining time would be
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm remaining}=\frac{9\times10^{56}-5.3395\times10^{55}}{3.6782\times10^{38}}
=2.302\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{s}=7.29\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Only about $5.93\%$ of the stipulated initial reservoir has been consumed under these assumptions. This is a calculation for an entirely accessible hydrogen reservoir with constant <luminosity>. It is not a prediction of the Sun's remaining <main-sequence lifetime>: only part of the hydrogen participates in core burning, and stellar structure and <luminosity> evolve.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret the displayed <semi-empirical mass formula> as an equation for mass energy, using $c=1$ for the mass terms. Its seven contributions are as follows.

The term $Z(m_p+m_e)$ is the rest energy of the $Z$ <protons> and $Z$ atomic <electrons>; $(A-Z)m_n$ is the rest energy of the <neutrons>. Including $Zm_e$ makes this an atomic-mass approximation. Small atomic electronic binding energies are neglected.

The volume contribution $-a_vA$ represents the short-range attractive <strong interaction>. Saturation gives each nucleon roughly a fixed number of neighbors, so the <nuclear binding energy> is approximately proportional to $A$; binding lowers the mass. The surface contribution $+a_sA^{2/3}$ restores the binding missing from <nucleons> near the surface. Approximately constant nuclear density gives radius proportional to $A^{1/3}$ and area proportional to $A^{2/3}$, explaining that power and the positive mass correction.

The <Coulomb potential energy> contribution $+a_cZ^2/A^{1/3}$ is positive because <proton> charges repel. A sphere's electrostatic self-energy scales as $Z^2/R$, and $R\propto A^{1/3}$. The more refined proton-pair count $Z(Z-1)$ differs from the $Z^2$ approximation explicitly used here.

The <nuclear symmetry energy> contribution $+a_a(Z-A/2)^2/A$ penalizes unequal <neutron> and <proton> populations. By the <Pauli exclusion principle>, filling one species' higher-momentum states is energetically more expensive than balancing the two <Fermi seas>; nuclear interactions also contribute to the empirical coefficient. The stated convention is equivalent to $23.285(N-Z)^2/A$ MeV, since $Z-A/2=(Z-N)/2$. It must not be combined with the usual coefficient near $23$ MeV without this factor of four.

Finally, <nuclear pairing> favors paired <protons> and paired <neutrons>. In the mass formula,
$$
\boxed{\Delta M_{\rm pair}c^2=
\begin{cases}
-a_pA^{-1/2}&Z,N\text{ both even},\\
0&A\text{ odd},\\
+a_pA^{-1/2}&Z,N\text{ both odd}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The signs reverse when this correction is written as a contribution to positive <nuclear binding energy>. This smooth <liquid-drop model> omits detailed nuclear shell corrections.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The bookkeeping reaction is
$$
n+{}^{235}_{92}\mathrm U\longrightarrow{}^{92}_{37}\mathrm{Rb}
+{}^{140}_{55}\mathrm{Cs}+4n.
$$
Both <mass number> and <electric charge> are conserved: $236=92+140+4$ and $92=37+55$. Express each atomic mass as the sum of its constituent rest masses minus its <nuclear binding energy>. The total constituent masses, including the atomic-electron masses, cancel between the two sides. Neglecting incident-neutron <kinetic energy>, the <Q value> is therefore
$$
\boxed{Q=B(37,92)+B(55,140)-B(92,235).}
$$
The relevant initial binding is that of uranium-235. Introducing uranium-236 as an intermediate state is possible only if the neutron-capture energy is also accounted for.

In the stated coefficient convention,
$$
B(Z,A)=15.56A-17.23A^{2/3}-0.697\frac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}}
-93.14\frac{(Z-A/2)^2}{A}+\delta_B.
$$
Uranium-235 has odd $A$, hence $\delta_B=0$. Both products are odd–odd: rubidium has $(Z,N)=(37,55)$ and caesium has $(55,85)$. Each therefore has $\delta_B=-12/\sqrt A$ MeV. Direct substitution gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
B(92,235)&=1786.751\ \mathrm{MeV},\\
B(37,92)&=785.759\ \mathrm{MeV},\\
B(55,140)&=1157.089\ \mathrm{MeV},\\
\boxed{Q}&\boxed{=156.098\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the estimate from the supplied smooth mass formula, rather than a value imported from measured isotope masses.

For $100$ MW recovered as the stated fission energy, the annual energy output is $10^8(365.25)(86400)$ J. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm fission/yr}=\frac{10^8(31557600)}{156.0976(1.602176634\times10^{-13})}
=1.262\times10^{26}.}
$$
Each event consumes one uranium-235 atom. With <Avogadro constant> $N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$ and molar mass approximated as $235$ g mol$^{-1}$,
$$
\boxed{m=\frac{235N_{\rm fission/yr}}{N_A}=4.92\times10^4\ \mathrm g\simeq49.2\ \mathrm{kg}.}
$$
This uses the stated power directly as recovered fission power; an unspecified conversion efficiency or additional heat-loss correction cannot be inferred from it.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The supplied average retained-energy fraction in one <elastic collision> is
$$
f=\frac{11.18^2+0.94^2}{(11.18+0.94)^2}=0.8569149.
$$
The <mean-energy neutron moderation count> follows by iterating the stated mean-energy model for <neutron moderation>, giving $E_n=f^nE_0$. Equivalently, take logarithms to solve the geometric decrease:
$$
n=\frac{\ln(E_n/E_0)}{\ln f}
=\frac{\ln[0.025/(2\times10^6)]}{\ln(0.8569149)}
=117.847.
$$
Hence the estimated integer count is
$$
\boxed{n=118\ \text{collisions}.}
$$
After $117$ collisions the predicted mean energy is about $0.0285$ eV; after $118$ it is about $0.0244$ eV, bracketing the target. This is a mean-energy estimate using the given average retention factor. Individual scattering angles and energy losses fluctuate, so a specified single <neutron> is not guaranteed to reach the target on exactly its $118$th collision. An estimate based on the average logarithmic energy decrement is a different statistical convention.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For the first generation the flavor-diagonal <weak neutral current> permits
$$
Z\longrightarrow e^-e^+,\quad\nu_e\bar\nu_e,\quad u\bar u,\quad d\bar d.
$$
Each <quark> channel has three possible matching <color charges>. The <weak charged current> permits
$$
W^+\longrightarrow e^+\nu_e,\ u\bar d;\qquad
W^-\longrightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e,\ d\bar u.
$$
The two charges have equal <partial decay widths> to charge-conjugate channels to the accuracy needed here. <Lepton universality> and <color multiplicity in a decay width> give one unit of width for a massless lepton pair and three for a <quark> pair, apart from <quark> mixing and radiative corrections.

**If only the first generation is included**, the <branching fractions> are $1/(1+3)=1/4$ to the lepton channel and $3/4$ to <quarks>. For the actual accessible <Standard Model> channels, there are three lepton units and two accessible up-type <quark> rows, $u$ and $c$, each with three colors. The top <quark> is too heavy. Unitarity of the <CKM matrix> sums over accessible down-type partners, giving nine units in total:
$$
\boxed{B(W\to\ell\nu)\simeq\frac19\ \text{for one lepton flavor},\quad
B(W\to\text{any leptons})\simeq\frac13,\quad
B(W\to\text{any quarks})\simeq\frac23.}
$$
These fractions concern a charged lepton together with its appropriate <neutrino>; “lepton pair” does not mean two charged leptons in a $W$ decay. The <Z boson> has different vector and axial couplings for different fermions, so the same simple equal-unit rule cannot be transferred to all its <partial decay widths>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Using the measured single-flavor leptonic <partial decay width> as one unit,
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm eff}=\frac{\Gamma_W}{\Gamma(W\to e\nu)}
=\frac{2085}{232}=8.987\simeq9.}
$$
This agrees with three leptonic channels plus six color-distinguishable <quark> channels in the leading massless approximation. The six <quark> units are two accessible up-type flavors times three <color charges>, with the sums of squared <CKM matrix> elements over down-type partners approximately unity. They are not nine generations or nine new flavors. Small mass and <quantum chromodynamics> radiative corrections prevent the ratio from being an exactly integer channel count.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

An extra active generation with the same <weak neutral current> couplings would increase the <Z boson> <total decay width> if any new particle–antiparticle pair were light enough. A simple sufficient kinematic condition for closing each such two-body channel is
$$
\boxed{m_T,m_B,m_\sigma,m_N\gtrsim\frac{m_Z}{2}\simeq45.6\ \mathrm{GeV}/c^2.}
$$
The corresponding within-generation <W boson> channels close when
$$
\boxed{m_T+m_B\gtrsim m_W,\qquad m_\sigma+m_N\gtrsim m_W,\qquad
m_W\simeq80.4\ \mathrm{GeV}/c^2.}
$$
If both members of each doublet already exceed $m_Z/2$, these two sum constraints follow automatically. Mixing with lighter generations can also open charged-current channels involving one heavy and one light particle, depending on the mixing amplitudes; that information is not specified.

These are threshold estimates, not precise confidence limits. Close to threshold, <two-body decay phase space> suppresses a new <partial decay width>, and a quantitative lower mass bound requires the experimental width uncertainties and the fermion's couplings. The given observations yield no upper mass bound. A sterile <neutrino> with no standard active coupling would not obey the stated $Z$-width inference, whereas the question specifies the same quantum numbers as the existing generations.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Under the stipulated peak-energy approximation, set $E=m_V$ separately for $V=W,Z$ in the supplied <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section>. For a spin-one resonance,
$$
\sigma_V(m_V)\propto\frac{3B_{\rm in}^VB_{\rm out}^V}{m_V^2}
=\frac{3\Gamma_{\rm in}^V\Gamma_{\rm out}^V}{m_V^2\Gamma_V^2}.
$$
Define the measured ratio as $R=\sigma(W\to\ell\nu)/\sigma(Z\to\ell\ell)$. With the known production prefactor ratio neglected as instructed, the spin factors cancel and
$$
R=\frac{m_Z^2}{m_W^2}\frac{\Gamma_{\rm in}^W\Gamma_{\rm out}^W}
{\Gamma_{\rm in}^Z\Gamma_{\rm out}^Z}\frac{\Gamma_Z^2}{\Gamma_W^2}.
$$
Solving for the positive physical width gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_W=\Gamma_Z\frac{m_Z}{m_W}
\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{\rm in}^W\Gamma_{\rm out}^W}
{R\,\Gamma_{\rm in}^Z\Gamma_{\rm out}^Z}}.}
$$
If the production prefactors are retained as $C_W,C_Z$, multiply the quantity under the square root by $C_W/C_Z$. The <partial decay widths> in this extraction are calculated inputs, while the <total decay width> being inferred need not be fixed to its <Standard Model> value.

The <peak and integrated resonance width dependence> explains why the square root is essential to the specified **peak** approximation. An energy-integrated narrow-width cross section contains one inverse power of the <total decay width>, because integrating over the resonance supplies a factor proportional to its width. That different observable leads to a different extraction formula. Real hadron-collider analyses also include <parton distribution functions> and the energy convolution; the present result follows the explicit approximation in the source.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The new light active leptons open one additional $W\to\sigma N$ channel and two $Z$ channels. At the stated equal-partial-width approximation,
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_W'=2085+232=2317\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
**The quoted $501$ MeV is the total invisible width into the three known <neutrino> flavors**, not the width into one flavor. The <invisible Z width per active neutrino> fixes this interpretation. The notation in the numerical input is ambiguous; the <Standard Model> normalization fixes the new active-neutrino contribution to $501/3=167$ MeV. Adding the new charged-lepton width therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_Z'=2495+167+84=2746\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
This interpretation is consistent with the usual determination of the effective number of active <neutrinos> from the invisible width divided by the predicted width of a single species.<Number of Neutrino Types by Particle Data Group>[definition of the effective <neutrino> count from the total invisible width and the single-species <neutrino> width]{ref}

Keep the original light-fermion production and observed-decay <partial decay widths>, masses and prefactors fixed. New leptonic channels alter their <branching fractions> through the total widths. From the peak formula in <4/d>,
$$
\frac{R'}{R}=\left(\frac{\Gamma_Z'}{\Gamma_Z}\frac{\Gamma_W}{\Gamma_W'}\right)^2
=\left(\frac{2746}{2495}\frac{2085}{2317}\right)^2
=0.9808893.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\frac{R'-R}{R}=-0.0191107\simeq-1.91\%.}
$$
The relatively small change is a cancellation between the increase in the two total widths; each separately changes substantially more. This assumes that the new <quarks> do not contribute and that adding leptons leaves the old <partial decay widths> unchanged.

If the source's $501$ MeV were instead imposed literally as the width of a single <neutrino> flavor, the algebra would give $\Gamma_Z'=3080$ MeV and $R'/R=1.23401$. That would be a different numerical premise, inconsistent with the <Standard Model> interpretation of the supplied invisible-width value. It is recorded to make the normalization issue explicit, rather than silently treating the three-flavor sum as a one-flavor <partial decay width>.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

For a measurement of the cross-section ratio with <standard deviation> $0.019R$, the separation of the fourth-lepton prediction from the ordinary prediction is
$$
\boxed{n_{\rm ratio}=\frac{|R'-R|}{0.019R}
=\frac{0.0191107}{0.019}=1.006\simeq1.0.}
$$
Thus the peak-ratio model predicts only about a one-standard-deviation shift in this observable. In contrast, the direct <Z boson> width changes by $167+84=251$ MeV, giving
$$
\boxed{n_{\Gamma_Z}=\frac{251}{2.3}=109.1.}
$$
Under these assumptions the direct width measurement is overwhelmingly more sensitive. These are separate expected displacements, using the supplied experimental uncertainties and fixed theoretical inputs; they are not a combined statistical significance. The $501/3$ single-neutrino normalization and the peak-energy assumption are the same as in <4/e>. If the alternative literal $501$ MeV per <neutrino> premise were used, the corresponding displacements would instead be $12.3$ and $254$ standard deviations, illustrating why that normalization must be stated.
