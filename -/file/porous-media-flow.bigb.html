= Porous-media flow
{wiki}

<Porous-media flow> is <fluid> motion through the interconnected voids of a <porous medium>, such as a sand bed, a rock or a filter. Individual passages can be tiny and tortuous, yet their combined discharge can be predicted without resolving every passage. <Darcy law> supplies this bulk description when viscous resistance dominates pore-scale inertia; <porosity> measures how much space stores <fluid>, while <permeability of a porous medium> measures how readily that space transmits it.

The basic fully saturated model assumes one <Newtonian fluid> fills the connected pore space of a stationary solid framework, with <incompressible flow>, a small pore-scale <Reynolds number>, and averaging volumes large compared with individual pores but small compared with macroscopic variations. These are assumptions of the model developed below, rather than a claim that <Darcy law> describes every <porous-media flow>. <Two-phase porous-media flow> and <poroelastic aquifers> require additional constitutive and storage information.

For a first calculation, see <Darcy flow through a homogeneous slab>: a pressure drop determines the discharge, but <porosity> determines the corresponding <pore velocity>. <Layered porous permeability> then shows how geometry alone can make the bulk response <anisotropic>.

= Soil infiltration
{parent=Porous-media flow}

Entry of surface water into soil pore space. The <soil infiltration>[infiltration] rate depends on water availability, soil permeability, capillary suction and the existing moisture profile. In a shallow overland-flow model it is a sink from the surface layer, distinct from <evaporation> into the atmosphere. An imposed <soil infiltration>[infiltration] capacity cannot remove more liquid than is locally available.

= Pore pressure
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$P_f$}

<Pressure> of fluid occupying pore space, which can reduce effective normal stress on a <fault>.

= Aquifer
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{wiki}

An aquifer is a permeable geological formation containing and transmitting groundwater. A confined aquifer lies beneath a confining layer; an <unconfined aquifer> instead has a free water table.

= Saline aquifer
{parent=Aquifer}

An <aquifer> containing saline rather than fresh water. A deep <saline aquifer> can provide pore space for <carbon capture and storage>, but safe retention depends on reservoir connectivity, continuous <caprock> and pressure management.

= Aquifer drainage hydrograph
{parent=Aquifer}
{title2=$Q_0(t)$}

An <aquifer drainage hydrograph> plots the time-dependent discharge from an <aquifer>. In the shallow constant-recharge model, early discharge grows linearly according to the <rain-fed aquifer similarity solution>. Sustained recharge $R$ on a finite length $L$ gives eventual steady discharge $RL$ when the other end is impermeable. After recharge stops, a <separable aquifer drawdown> has height proportional to $1/(t+t_0)$ and discharge proportional to $1/(t+t_0)^2$; this is generally a late-time description rather than an exact solution for arbitrary inherited height profiles.

= Unconfined aquifer
{parent=Aquifer}

An unconfined aquifer has a free groundwater table. In a shallow aquifer of thickness $h(x,t)$, hydrostatic pressure and <Darcy's law> give horizontal flux proportional to $-hh_x$.

= Water table
{parent=Unconfined aquifer}
{wiki}
{title2=$z=h(x,t)$}

The <water table> is the upper surface of the saturated zone in an <unconfined aquifer>, where liquid <pressure> equals <atmospheric pressure> in the simplest drainage model. Under the <Dupuit approximation>, its height determines <hydrostatic pressure> throughout the saturated region and therefore the lateral <Darcy flux>.

= Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer
{parent=Unconfined aquifer}

For pore fraction $n$, permeability $k$, liquid density $\rho$, viscosity $\mu$, rainfall recharge $R$, and a shallow groundwater thickness $h$,
$$
n h_t=\frac{k\rho g}{\mu}(hh_x)_x+R.
$$
This is a nonlinear diffusion equation with diffusivity proportional to the local thickness.

= Square-root drainage boundary
{parent=Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer}
{title2=$h\sim\sqrt{2Jx},\quad hh_x\to J$}

A zero-height outlet can carry finite nonzero <volume flux per unit width> in a nonlinear <Darcy flow> model with $q=-Khh_x$. If $hh_x\to J>0$ as $x\downarrow0$, then $(h^2)'\to2J$, and <integration> gives $h\sim\sqrt{2Jx}$ and $h_x\sim\sqrt{J/(2x)}$. Thus the height vanishes while its slope diverges; the flux must be evaluated as a limit of the product. The divergent slope also identifies a region where the outer <Dupuit approximation> eventually needs a local correction.

= Rain-fed aquifer similarity solution
{parent=Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer}
{title2=$h=t f(x/t)$}

For the dimensionless rainfall-driven <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer>, $h_t=(hh_x)_x+1$, balancing every time power in $h=t^af(x/t^b)$ gives $a=b=1$. The <similarity profile> obeys $(ff')'+\eta f'-f+1=0$. A river drained to base level gives $f(0)=0$, while undrained storage far away gives $f(\infty)=1$. The solution approximates a finite <aquifer> before its drainage-affected length reaches the far boundary. If $J=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}ff'>0$, the dimensional drainage flux is $\sqrt K R^{3/2}Jt/\phi$.

= Conserved first moment of a draining porous current
{parent=Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer}
{title2=$D=\int_0^\infty xh\,dx$}

For $h_t=\kappa(hh_x)_x$ with a drained boundary $h(0,t)=0$ and a finite current vanishing at its nose, <integration by parts> gives $\dot D=-\kappa[h^2]_0^\infty/2=0$. The outlet can have finite <volume flux> because its contribution to the first-moment flux is multiplied by $x=0$. A positive prescribed boundary height instead gives $\dot D=\kappa h(0,t)^2/2$, so drainage is essential to this invariant.

= Dipole similarity solution of a draining porous current
{parent=Conserved first moment of a draining porous current}
{title2=$h\sim t^{-1/2},\quad x_f\sim t^{1/4}$}

The <conserved first moment of a draining porous current> fixes $\alpha+2\beta=0$ in a <self-similar solution> $h=a t^\alpha f(x/(dt^\beta))$. The <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer> supplies $\alpha=2\beta-1$, giving $\alpha=-1/2$, $\beta=1/4$. Normalize $\kappa a=d^2$ and the nose at $\eta=1$. Then $-f/2-\eta f'/4=(ff')'$ is solved by
$$
f(\eta)=\frac{\sqrt\eta-\eta^2}{6}\quad(0<\eta<1),\qquad
D=\frac{ad^2}{40},\quad d=(40\kappa D)^{1/4},\quad a=d^2/\kappa.
$$
The square-root outlet has finite drainage flux and the nose has zero flux. This is a particular similarity solution and its scaling family, not an exact description of arbitrary initial data.

= Drainage volume decay in a dipole porous current
{parent=Dipole similarity solution of a draining porous current}
{title2=$V\propto t^{-1/4}$}

For the <dipole similarity solution of a draining porous current>, $V=\phi\int h\,dx=\phi ad\,t^{-1/4}/18$. Thus $\dot V/V=-1/(4t)$. A shifted similarity origin replaces $t$ by $t+t_0$. Neither a positive rate nor a rate $-1/(2t)$ is compatible with this two-dimensional first-moment-conserving profile.

= Separable aquifer drawdown
{parent=Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer}

After recharge stops on a finite aquifer connected to a river, the late solution of the <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer> has the form $h\sim t^{-1}F(x/L)$. The profile solves a nonlinear eigenvalue problem, and river discharge decays as $t^{-2}$.

= Dupuit approximation
{c}
{parent=Unconfined aquifer}

The Dupuit approximation treats flow in a shallow <unconfined aquifer> as predominantly horizontal, with <hydrostatic pressure> $p=\rho g(h-z)$ and horizontal pressure gradient independent of depth. Integrating <Darcy's law> over the saturated depth then gives a <volume flux per unit width> depending only on the groundwater height and its slope.

= Dupuit flow in a channel with cubic wetted area
{c}
{parent=Dupuit approximation}
{title2=$Q=\alpha K(H^4-\eta^4)/(12L)$}

For saturated <Darcy flow> through a long dam occupying a channel of width $\alpha z^2$, let hydraulic conductivity be $K=k\rho g/\mu$ and free-surface height $h(x)$. The <Dupuit approximation> gives $Q=-K\alpha h^3h_x/3$. Fixed heads $h(0)=H$ and $h(L)=\eta$ give $h^4=H^4-(H^4-\eta^4)x/L$ and the displayed steady discharge. <Porosity> relates pore velocity to superficial <Darcy velocity> but does not independently multiply this discharge when $k$ is intrinsic permeability. For depth-dependent permeability, integrate the horizontal conductivity over the saturated cross-section first.

= Unconfined aquifer with depth-dependent permeability
{parent=Unconfined aquifer}

With <permeability of a porous medium> $k(z)=k_0(1+\beta z)$, the <Dupuit approximation> gives
$$
q=-u_b\left(h+\frac\beta2h^2\right)h_x,\qquad \phi h_t+q_x=R,\qquad u_b=\frac{k_0\rho g}{\mu}.
$$
Here $\phi$ is <porosity> and $R$ is recharge. The low-height limit has mobility proportional to $h$, whereas large heights have mobility proportional to $h^2$. This changes both the <forced filling similarity for power-law diffusion> and the <separable draining profile for power-law diffusion>.

= Outlet layer in a deep unconfined aquifer
{parent=Unconfined aquifer with depth-dependent permeability}

Even when most of an <unconfined aquifer with depth-dependent permeability> is deep, its height vanishes at an absorbing outlet. Near that outlet, finite outward <volume flux per unit width> $Q$ gives $u_bhh_x\simeq Q$ and hence $h\simeq(2Qx/u_b)^{1/2}$. The high-depth outer approximation instead has $h\simeq[6Qx/(u_b\beta)]^{1/3}$. Their crossover lies at height of order $1/\beta$ and distance of order $u_b/(\beta^2Q)$; the thin inner <boundary layer> transmits the same leading discharge.

= Poroelastic aquifer
{parent=Unconfined aquifer}

A poroelastic aquifer couples pore-fluid storage and flow to elastic compaction of its solid skeleton. Changes in gravity, surface loading, or pore pressure can therefore change groundwater discharge even without changing rainfall.

= Poroelastic compaction length
{title2=$\ell_c$}
{parent=Poroelastic aquifer}

For a skeleton with Young modulus $E$, undeformed solid fraction $\phi_0$, grain-fluid density difference $\rho_s-\rho$, and reference gravity $g_0$, the hydrostatic poroelastic compaction length is
$$
\ell_c=\frac{E}{\phi_0(\rho_s-\rho)g_0}.
$$
It is the depth over which self-weight changes the solid fraction appreciably.

= Delayed polymer diversion in parallel porous layers
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$q_i=k_i\Delta P/[\mu(L+(\lambda-1)\ell_i)]$}

For a fixed pressure difference across two noncommunicating layers, an activated viscous polymer segment of length $\ell_i$ adds hydraulic resistance in series with the remaining fluid. The <Darcy flux> is the displayed expression when polymer raises <dynamic viscosity> by a factor $\lambda$. Tracking particle entry time determines activation and exit, while $\dot X_i=q_i/\phi$ gives each displacement front. Preferentially occupying the more permeable layer shifts the relative inflow toward the other layer, but cannot increase that layer's absolute flux at fixed pressure. It can delay its breakthrough and leave original reservoir fluid flowing after the unmodified reservoir would be fully swept.

= Thermal front in a porous medium
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$V_T=\Gamma U,\quad\Gamma=C_f/C_{\rm eff}$}

= Porous thermal front
{synonym}

A sharp advected temperature transition in a fluid-filled <porous medium>. If fluid and solid equilibrate locally in temperature, their effective heat capacity per total volume is $C_{\rm eff}=\phi C_f+(1-\phi)C_s$, while the advected heat flux is $C_fUT$ with $U$ the <Darcy velocity>. Neglecting conduction gives $C_{\rm eff}T_t+C_fUT_x=0$, so the thermal front speed is $\Gamma U$ with $\Gamma=C_f/C_{\rm eff}$. The material <pore velocity> is $U/\phi$, so thermal retardation relative to that speed is $\Gamma\phi$. Temperature-dependent viscosity couples thermal-front perturbations back to the <Darcy flux>.

= Porous medium
{parent=Porous-media flow}

A porous medium is a solid framework containing connected voids in which a <fluid> can reside and flow. Its <porosity> is the void fraction; its <permeability of a porous medium> measures the ease of pressure-driven flow through the connected pores. The averaged <Darcy flux> is measured per total cross-sectional area, whereas the pore velocity is that flux divided by porosity. Not every material with voids has connected permeable pores.

= Resident-weighted transit time in a layered flow
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$\langle\tau\rangle_{\rm resident}=\langle L/v\rangle$}

Without transverse exchange, a particle chosen uniformly from pore volume samples each streamline's transit time with resident-volume weight. This differs from the <flux-weighted residence time in a porous layer>, which samples at the inlet proportionally to $v$. For constant <porosity> and a linear positive <velocity> $v=v_0+(v_1-v_0)y/H$, the two means are $L\log(v_1/v_0)/(v_1-v_0)$ and $2L/(v_0+v_1)$, respectively. Their limits agree for uniform <velocity>.

= Capillary imbibition
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$\phi\dot z=k\Delta P/(\mu z)$}

= Imbibition
{synonym}

A wetting liquid penetrates pore space under a pressure difference that includes capillary suction. With a planar front, fixed pressure drop and negligible gravity/inertia, <Darcy's law> and pore-volume storage give $z\propto t^{1/2}$. The front pressure must be defined relative to the gas phase consistently; adding a positive suction to the inlet pressure is different from prescribing a positive liquid front pressure.

= Hemispherical capillary imbibition
{parent=Capillary imbibition}
{title2=$t=\frac{\mu\phi}{k\Delta P R_s}(R^3/3-R_sR^2/2+R_s^3/6)$}

Radial <capillary imbibition> into a half-space has hemispherical flow area $2\pi r^2$. Its radial hydraulic resistance is proportional to $1/R_s-1/R$, so its inlet <volume flux> approaches $2\pi k\Delta P R_s/\mu$. The growing front area gives late-time $R\propto t^{1/3}$, unlike the planar $t^{1/2}$ law. Both results assume capillary-dominated geometry with negligible directional gravity effects.

= Evaporation-limited hemispherical imbibition
{parent=Hemispherical capillary imbibition}
{title2=$R_*=(R_s+\sqrt{R_s^2+4k\Delta P R_s/(\mu F_e)})/2$}

When a hemispherical wetted front loses liquid at fixed volumetric flux per unit area $F_e$, equilibrium balances inlet flow with $2\pi R^2F_e$. The displayed radius exceeds $R_s$ and is stable because inlet flow decreases while evaporating area increases with radius. <Porosity> affects the adjustment time but cancels from the equilibrium radius.

= Two-phase porous-media flow
{parent=Porous-media flow}

Immiscible fluids share pore space, with each phase's <Darcy flux> determined by its pressure, viscosity and <relative permeability>. <Fluid saturation> specifies how much pore space each phase occupies. A <capillary pressure> couples their pressures, while their individual <mass conservation> laws determine displacement and saturation evolution.

= Capillary residual trapping
{parent=Two-phase porous-media flow}
{title2=$s\in[0,1]$}

When a nonwetting phase recedes from pores, <surface tension> and pore geometry can retain a residual saturation $s$. If $h$ is the mobile invaded thickness and $M$ its maximum past value, stored phase volume per plan area is $\phi[(1-s)h+sM]$. Newly invading pores have $M=h$; during recession $M$ remains fixed. With mobile <Darcy velocity> $u$, conservation gives advance speed $u/\phi$ and recession speed $u/[\phi(1-s)]$. The latter is faster for $0<s<1$.

= Residual-trapping attenuation of a porous current
{parent=Capillary residual trapping}
{title2=$h=h_0\exp[-t/\tau+\phi(1-s)x/(u\tau)]$}

When a thinning <porous gravity current> leaves fraction $s$ behind, receding storage changes by only $\phi(1-s)\,dh$ after accounting for immobile fluid. Thus its advective thinning equation is $\phi(1-s)h_t+uh_x=0$. Advancing into previously unwetted pores still requires the full storage $\phi h$, so the nose moves at $u/\phi$. With exponentially decreasing injection the mobile volume per unit well length is $V=Q\tau(e^{-st/\tau}-e^{-t/\tau})/(1-s)$. It obeys $V'=Qe^{-t/\tau}-sV/\tau$; the second term becomes immobile retained volume.

= Mobile-volume peak of an exponentially forced porous current
{parent=Residual-trapping attenuation of a porous current}
{title2=$t_{\max}=\tau\log(1/s)/(1-s)$}

For residual fraction $0<s<1$, differentiation of $V=Q\tau(e^{-st/\tau}-e^{-t/\tau})/(1-s)$ gives its unique positive maximum at the displayed time. The maximum volume is $Q\tau s^{s/(1-s)}$. When $s=0$ the mobile volume approaches $Q\tau$ monotonically, with no finite maximum. The limiting $s\to1$ expression is $V=Qt e^{-t/\tau}$ with peak at $t=\tau$.

= Triangular current with capillary retention
{parent=Capillary residual trapping}
{title2=$h=a\max\{0,\min(x-v_Rt,2L+v_At-x)\}$}

For <capillary residual trapping> of an initial triangular current, the <method of characteristics> translates its leading and trailing faces at $v_A=u/\phi$ and $v_R=u/[\phi(1-s)]$. Their intersection has height $a[L-(v_R-v_A)t/2]$. Extinction occurs at $t_*=2L/(v_R-v_A)$ and position $x_*=2L/s$. These formulas require $0<s<1$; at zero residual saturation the triangle translates indefinitely.

= Maximum-invasion envelope of a retained current
{parent=Triangular current with capillary retention}
{title2=$M_\infty(x)=\max_{t\geq0}h(x,t)$}

For the <triangular current with capillary retention>, the initial trailing face is already the maximum for $0<x<L$. Further upslope, the maximum occurs when the moving crest passes. Hence
$$
M_\infty(x)=\begin{cases}ax,&0<x<L,\\a(2L-sx)/(2-s),&L<x<2L/s,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
$$
The final trapped phase has saturation $s$ below this envelope. Its volume is $\phi s\int M_\infty\,dx=\phi aL^2$, exactly the initial mobile volume. Maximum invaded thickness is distinct from residual saturation: $s$ multiplies occupied pore volume rather than shrinking the geometrical envelope.

= Waterflooding
{parent=Two-phase porous-media flow}

Injecting <water> to displace oil through a porous reservoir. Spatial permeability contrasts cause preferential flow and early water breakthrough, while unswept slower regions retain oil. The recovery also depends on <fractional flow>, <capillary pressure> and phase mixing; a passive streamline model alone does not capture all two-phase effects.

= Buckley-Leverett equation
{c}
{parent=Two-phase porous-media flow}
{title2=$\phi s_t+Q\partial_xF(s)=0$}

The one-dimensional <mass conservation> equation for saturation when total <Darcy flux> is constant and capillary pressure and gravity are neglected. It is a <scalar conservation law> with dimensional characteristic speed $(Q/\phi)F'(s)$. Shock speeds obey a <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>; physically admissible solutions satisfy an entropy selection. <Capillary diffusion> regularizes the saturation transition.

= Capillary diffusion
{parent=Buckley-Leverett equation}
{title2=$D_c=-K\lambda_w\lambda_n p_c'/ (\lambda_w+\lambda_n)$}

Eliminating phase pressures with $p_c=p_n-p_w$ gives $\phi s_t+QF(s)_x=(D_cs_x)_x$. For decreasing <capillary pressure>, $D_c\geq0$. This nonlinear diffusion broadens a saturation shock; it can degenerate where a <phase mobility> vanishes. Endpoint conservation retains the limiting <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>.

= Fractional-flow tangent construction
{parent=Buckley-Leverett equation}
{title2=$F'(s_s)=[F(s_s)-F(s_0)]/(s_s-s_0)$}

When a <rarefaction wave> attaches to a saturation shock, its terminal <characteristic speed> must match the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> speed. This gives a tangent from the initial state $(s_0,F(s_0))$ to the fractional-flow curve at $s_s$. It selects the upstream shock saturation; the actual dimensional shock speed still includes total flux divided by <porosity>.

= Fractional flow
{parent=Two-phase porous-media flow}
{title2=$F_w=\lambda_w/(\lambda_w+\lambda_n)$}

The fraction of total advective <Darcy flux> carried by one phase when capillary and gravity corrections are omitted. For two phases it is $\lambda_w/(\lambda_w+\lambda_n)$. This quantity lies between zero and one for nonnegative <phase mobilities>. Its nonlinear dependence on <fluid saturation> determines <characteristic speeds> and shocks in the <Buckley-Leverett equation>.

= Fluid saturation
{parent=Two-phase porous-media flow}
{title2=$s_i=V_i/V_{\rm pore}$}

The fraction of pore volume occupied by a fluid phase. For a completely fluid-filled two-phase pore space the saturations sum to one. Residual saturation refers to fluid remaining immobile under the modeled displacement; it can impose zero relative mobility at the endpoint.

= Flux-weighted residence time in a porous layer
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$\langle\tau\rangle_Q=V_{\rm pore}/Q$}

For steady incompressible flow with no stagnant volume, partition the layer into streamtubes of <volume flux> $dQ$. The pore volume in a streamtube is $\tau\,dQ$, so $\int\tau\,dQ=V_{\rm pore}$. This gives the mean residence time $V_{\rm pore}/Q$ even when individual path times vary widely. <Streamfunction> labels measure flux in two-dimensional flow, so they supply the correct weighting.

= Vertical imbibition under a draining liquid layer
{parent=Porous-media flow}
{title2=$\phi\dot l=(gk/\nu)(1+h/l)$}

A liquid layer of depth $h$ above a dry deep porous substrate drives a <Darcy flux> $w=(gk/\nu)(1+h/l)$ through its saturated depth $l$, assuming negligible <capillary pressure> and negligible resistance from the displaced phase. The pressure head contributes $h/l$ and gravity contributes one. <Volume conservation> gives $\phi\dot l=w$. For a finite column, $h+\phi l=h_0$ and initially
$$
l(t)\sim\left(\frac{2gkh_0t}{\phi\nu}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
The singular initial flux is an idealization; the continuum model requires penetration beyond the pore scale.

= Darcy's law
{c}
{synonym}

<Darcy law> is a linear relation between the bulk <Darcy flux> and the net driving force per unit volume. For an <isotropic> <porous medium> containing one fully saturating <Newtonian fluid>,
$$
\mathbf q=-\frac{k}{\mu}\bigl(\nabla p-\rho\mathbf g\bigr).
$$
Here $\mathbf q$ is the <Darcy velocity> relative to the solid framework, $k$ is the intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium>, $\mu>0$ is <dynamic viscosity>, $p$ is averaged <fluid pressure>, $\rho$ is <mass density>, and $\mathbf g$ is the downward <gravitational acceleration> vector. The <pressure gradient> exerts force density $-\nabla p$, while <gravity> contributes $\rho\mathbf g$; the minus sign makes the discharge follow their sum. The symbols $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf q$ are both commonly used for <Darcy velocity>; neither denotes <pore velocity> here.

With upward coordinate $z$ and positive <gravitational acceleration> magnitude $g$, $\mathbf g=-g\mathbf e_z$. Constant $\rho$ and $g$ therefore give
$$
\nabla p-\rho\mathbf g=\nabla p+\rho g\mathbf e_z=\rho g\nabla\left(z+\frac{p}{\rho g}\right),\qquad \mathbf q=-K\nabla h,\qquad K=\frac{k\rho g}{\mu}.
$$
The <hydraulic head> $h=z+p/(\rho g)$ combines elevation and <fluid pressure>, and <hydraulic conductivity> $K$ combines the medium and <fluid> properties. <Hydrostatic pressure> satisfies $\nabla p=\rho\mathbf g$, so it produces zero <Darcy flux> even though <fluid pressure> increases downward. Conversely, equal pressures at the two ends of a vertical column do not imply zero flow: their elevations give different <hydraulic heads>.

The appeal of <Darcy law> is that a complicated connected pore geometry enters a macroscopic calculation through a measurable coefficient. It predicts filter discharge and <aquifer> flow; combined with <mass conservation>, it gives an equation for the evolving <fluid pressure>. Its simple linear form requires a small pore-scale <Reynolds number>, slow adjustment compared with pore-scale inertial relaxation, and a valid <continuum approximation>. For a characteristic pore size $d$ and mean <pore velocity> $v$, one possible convention is $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm pore}=\rho|v|d/\mu\ll1$; conventions using grain size or <Darcy velocity> have different numerical thresholds. Merely calling flow laminar does not guarantee negligible inertia. Unsaturated pores, several <fluid> phases, appreciable inertia, or a <fluid> whose stress is not Newtonian require further modeling. Woessner and Poeter discuss these limits in their authored hydrology text.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/applicability-of-darcys-law/{ref}

Henry Darcy's 1856 book *Les fontaines publiques de la ville de Dijon* was published by Victor Dalmont; the University Grenoble Alpes digital collection verifies this bibliographic record and supplies a digitized copy.https://bibnum-patrimoniale.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/items/show/1643{ref} Woessner and Poeter's account connects <Darcy law> to experiments on water flowing through sand filters, relating discharge to cross-sectional area, <hydraulic head> difference and sample length.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/darcys-law/{ref} The modern intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> notation above should not be read as a quotation of Darcy's original notation.

= Spherical Darcy injection
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$q_r=\Pi a\Delta p/(\mu r^2)$}

For a sphere of radius $a$ injecting an <incompressible flow> into an infinite homogeneous isotropic <porous medium>, <Darcy's law> and <conservation of mass> give $r^2q_r=C$. Integrating $p_r=-\mu C/(\Pi r^2)$ with $p(a)-p(\infty)=\Delta p$ gives $C=\Pi a\Delta p/\mu$. The <hydraulic resistance> is $\Delta p/Q=\mu/(4\pi\Pi a)$. A common background <hydrostatic pressure> cancels <gravity> if the imposed excess <pressure> is measured relative to that background.

= Hydraulic head
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$h=z+p/(\rho g)$}
{wiki}

<Hydraulic head> is the sum of elevation and pressure head, measured in metres relative to a chosen height datum. For constant <mass density> $\rho$ and <gravitational acceleration> magnitude $g$, the pressure head $p/(\rho g)$ is the height of a column of the same <fluid> supported by <fluid pressure> $p$. A water-filled open measuring tube records <hydraulic head> as the elevation of its water level when $p$ is gauge <fluid pressure>. Changing the common pressure reference or height datum adds a constant to <hydraulic head> and leaves its driving gradient unchanged.

For <water> with $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, a point at $z=2\,\mathrm m$ with gauge <fluid pressure> $p=9810\,\mathrm{Pa}$ has $h=3\,\mathrm m$. A second point at $z=0$ with gauge <fluid pressure> $29430\,\mathrm{Pa}$ also has $h=3\,\mathrm m$: these values are compatible with a stationary column under <hydrostatic pressure>. It is a difference in <hydraulic head>, rather than a difference in <fluid pressure> alone, that drives saturated <Darcy flow>. This form uses constant <mass density>; one cannot generally pull spatially varying $\rho$ through the gradient.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/hydraulic-head/{ref}

= Hydraulic conductivity
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$K=k\rho g/\mu$}
{wiki}

<Hydraulic conductivity> is the coefficient in $\mathbf q=-K\nabla h$, with units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Because the gradient of <hydraulic head> is dimensionless, <hydraulic conductivity> has the dimensions of <velocity>, but it is not the actual <Darcy velocity> unless the driving gradient has magnitude one. Intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> $k$ has units $\mathrm{m^2}$ and describes pore geometry; <hydraulic conductivity> also depends on <mass density>, <dynamic viscosity> and <gravitational acceleration>.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/hydraulic-conductivity/{ref}

For $k=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and $\mu=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, <hydraulic conductivity> is $K=9.81\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Doubling <dynamic viscosity> while keeping pore geometry, <mass density> and <gravitational acceleration> fixed halves <hydraulic conductivity> without changing intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium>. Thus a value quoted as a material's <hydraulic conductivity> needs its <fluid> conditions.

= Darcy flow through a homogeneous slab
{c}
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$Q=Ak\Delta p/(\mu L)$}

Consider a horizontal slab of fully saturated sand with uniform intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> $k=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, length $L=0.50\,\mathrm m$, total cross-sectional area $A=0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$, and <porosity> $\phi=0.25$. Use one <Newtonian fluid> with <mass density> $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Prescribe inlet and outlet gauge <fluid pressures> $10000\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $0\,\mathrm{Pa}$, with no sources or leaks and steady <incompressible flow>. The two ends have the same elevation, so <gravity> has no component along the slab. <Mass conservation> makes the axial <Darcy flux> constant, and <Darcy law> then gives a linear <fluid pressure> profile:
$$
p(x)=10000\left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right)\mathrm{Pa},\qquad \frac{dp}{dx}=-20000\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}},\qquad q=-\frac{10^{-12}}{10^{-3}}(-20000)=2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
The measured <volume flux> and mean <pore velocity> are
$$
Q=Aq=4.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}=0.40\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}},\qquad v=\frac q\phi=8.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
The slab's total volume is $AL=0.010\,\mathrm{m^3}$, of which $\phi AL=0.0025\,\mathrm{m^3}=2.5\,\mathrm L$ is pore volume. In the ideal uniform advective model, a nonreacting tracer has transit time $L/v=6250\,\mathrm s\simeq1.74\,\mathrm h$, consistent with pore volume divided by <volume flux>, $\phi AL/Q$. The incorrect substitution of <Darcy velocity> for <pore velocity> would give $25000\,\mathrm s$, four times too long. Real passages have different path lengths and local speeds; these numbers describe a bulk mean rather than every particle's journey.

As an independent check, <gravitational acceleration> magnitude $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ gives <hydraulic conductivity> $K=9.81\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, <hydraulic head> drop $\Delta h=10000/(1000\times9.81)\,\mathrm m\simeq1.01937\,\mathrm m$, and $q=K\Delta h/L=2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ again. If a representative pore size is $d=10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$, the <Reynolds number> convention based on <pore velocity> gives $\rho vd/\mu=0.008$, supporting neglect of pore inertia. This size is an illustrative extra measurement, not something determined by <porosity> and intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> alone.

= Darcy flow
{c}
{parent=Darcy law}

Darcy flow is slow flow through a <porous medium> whose averaged flux obeys the <Darcy law>. The <Darcy velocity> is the fluid volume flux per total cross-sectional area; it differs from the velocity averaged only over pore space.

= Conformal map equation for planar Darcy growth
{parent=Darcy flow}
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}(\zeta F_\zeta\overline{F_t})=Q\quad(|\zeta|=1)$}

Let $F(\zeta,t)$ map the <unit disk> conformally to a planar <Darcy flow> region, with $F(0,t)=0$ at a point source of <flux> $2\pi Q$. With zero <velocity potential> on the <boundary>, the logarithmic source potential becomes $\phi=Q\log|\zeta|$. Differentiating the inverse map at fixed physical position gives $\phi_t=-Q\operatorname{Re}(F_t/(\zeta F_\zeta))$, while $|\nabla\phi|^2=Q^2/|\zeta F_\zeta|^2$. The <boundary> law $\phi_t+|\nabla\phi|^2=0$ therefore gives the displayed equation, for nonzero $Q$.

The vector $\zeta F_\zeta\,d\theta$ is outward normal times arclength on $\zeta=e^{i\theta}$. Consequently the equation gives the <area> growth law $d|D|/dt=2\pi Q$. For a circular map $F=R(t)\zeta$, it reduces to $R\dot R=Q$, and hence $R(t)^2=R(0)^2+2Qt$.

= Lubrication boundary condition for a crack in a Darcy medium
{parent=Darcy flow}
{title2=$\partial_x(h^3p_x/12)+k[p_y]^+_-=0$}

In a thin fluid-filled crack, <lubrication theory> gives flux per unit depth $q=-h^3p_x/(12\mu)$. The surrounding <Darcy flow> has normal velocity $-kp_y/\mu$. Conservation in the crack gives $q_x=-[u_y]^+_-$, yielding the displayed condition. Pressure is continuous across the crack. This reduced model neglects transverse pressure variation and assumes wall slip lengths are small compared with $h$.

= Crack pressure in elliptic coordinates
{parent=Lubrication boundary condition for a crack in a Darcy medium}
{title2=$p=\frac{\mu Ua}{k}\left[-\cosh\xi+\frac\alpha{1+\alpha}e^{-\xi}\right]\cos\eta,\quad\alpha=\frac{H_0^3}{24ka}$}

For $h=H_0(1-x^2/a^2)^{1/6}$, <elliptic coordinates> make $h^3/\sin\eta$ constant on the upper crack face. Pressure continuity and harmonicity reduce the boundary condition to $F'(0)=\alpha F(0)$ for the cosine mode, while uniform far flow gives $F\sim-e^\xi/2$. Solving $F''=F$ yields the displayed pressure. The midpoint crack flux is $2aU\alpha/(1+\alpha)$; small $\alpha$ leaves the background pressure gradient nearly unchanged, while large $\alpha$ makes the crack nearly equipotential.

= Elliptic crack pressure dipole
{parent=Crack pressure in elliptic coordinates}
{title2=$\delta p\sim\frac{\alpha\mu Ua^2}{2(1+\alpha)k}\frac{\cos\theta}{r}$}

Subtracting the uniform-flow pressure $-\mu Ux/k$ leaves $(\mu Ua/k)\alpha e^{-\xi}\cos\eta/(1+\alpha)$. The coordinate map has $r\sim ae^\xi/2$, so $e^{-\xi}\sim a/(2r)$, giving the displayed dipole coefficient. The one-half is essential. In the perfect-conductor limit the independent expression $p=-(\mu U/k)\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{z^2-a^2}$ has the same far-field expansion.

= Fixed-throughflow dissipation reduction of a conductive crack
{parent=Elliptic crack pressure dipole}
{title2=$\Delta\mathcal D=-\frac{\pi\mu U^2a^2}{k}\frac\alpha{1+\alpha}$}

For dipole pressure $\delta p\sim Cx/(x^2+y^2)$, choose a rectangular <control volume> whose transverse size tends to infinity faster than its streamwise size. Each end face contributes $\pi C$ to $\int\delta p\,n_xds$, so fixed-throughflow boundary work gives $\Delta\mathcal D=-2\pi UC$, the displayed result. This control-surface choice removes the integrated far-field velocity correction. A circular cutoff of the pressure-only functional gives $-\pi UC$ instead and does not implement the same throughflow normalization.

= Uniformly heated vertical plate in a porous medium
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$f'''+\tfrac23ff''-\tfrac13(f')^2=0$}

For constant wall heat-flux density $F>0$, effective <thermal conductivity> $k_e$, buoyancy mobility $B=\rho_0g\alpha K/\mu$ and thermal transport coefficient $\kappa=k_e/(\rho_0c_f)$, a slender <Darcy flow> boundary layer has width $\delta=(\kappa k_e z/(BF))^{1/3}$. Writing $\eta=y/\delta$, $\psi=\kappa zf(\eta)/\delta$ and $T-T_a=(F\delta/k_e)f'(\eta)$ gives the displayed equation with $f(0)=0$, $f''(0)=-1$ and $f'(\infty)=0$. The exact integral is $\int_0^\infty(f')^2d\eta=1$. Consequently its kinematic Darcy momentum flux is $\int w^2dy=B\kappa Fz/k_e$ and its buoyancy flux is $g\alpha\kappa Fz/k_e$. These are per span on one heated face.

= Integral exponential profile for porous wall convection
{parent=Uniformly heated vertical plate in a porous medium}
{title2=$f(\eta)\simeq2^{2/3}(1-e^{-\eta/2^{1/3}})$}

Assume an exponentially decreasing <temperature> profile while imposing wall heat flux, impermeability and the exact integrated heat budget. For $f=C(1-e^{-\eta/d})$, the wall condition gives $C=d^2$ and $\int(f')^2d\eta=1$ gives $d^3=2$. This yields the displayed integral approximation and discharge $\int wdy\simeq2^{2/3}\kappa z/\delta$. It satisfies the boundary conditions and exact flux budget, but not the differential equation pointwise; its mass-flux coefficient is approximate.

= Fixed-pressure planar Darcy displacement
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$\mu_hLX+\tfrac12(\mu-\mu_h)X^2=k\Delta P\,t/\phi$}

For two incompressible fluids separated by a planar material front $X(t)$, the <Darcy flux> is uniform and the pressure drop sums the resistances of the two layers: $U=k\Delta P/[\mu X+\mu_h(L-X)]$. The front moves at the <pore velocity> $X'=U/\phi$. Integrating from $X(0)=0$ yields the displayed relation. The formula holds up to breakthrough $X=L$, with either sign of the viscosity difference; equal viscosities give constant speed.

= Thermal-front retardation in Darcy flow
{parent=Fixed-pressure planar Darcy displacement}
{title2=$Y=\Gamma\phi X$}

If a thermal front has speed $Y'=\Gamma U$ with $U$ the <Darcy velocity>, while a material front has speed $X'=U/\phi$, their position ratio from coincident initial locations is $Y/X=\Gamma\phi$. Calling a retardation factor the ratio to pore velocity instead would give $Y/X=\Gamma$; these conventions must not be mixed. With cold viscosity $\mu$ and heated viscosity $b\mu$, the displacement's effective viscosity is $\mu_{\rm eff}=\mu[b-(b-1)\Gamma\phi]$. In the pressure resistance the cold layer has length $Y$, the hot injected layer has length $X-Y$, and the displaced layer has length $L-X$.

= Porous thermal plume
{parent=Darcy law}
{title2=$w=\beta(T-T_0)$}

A slender planar plume in a saturated porous medium has buoyancy velocity $w=\beta(T-T_0)$, with $\beta=\rho_0g\alpha\Pi/\mu$. The leading heat equation retains both <advection> components and lateral <thermal conduction>: $uT_x+wT_z=\kappa T_{xx}$. Its convective heat input per span is conserved, $F_h=C_h\int w(T-T_0)dx$. Defining $Q=\beta F_h/C_h$ gives width proportional to $z^{2/3}$ and vertical speed/temperature excess proportional to $z^{-1/3}$.

= Conserved momentum flux of a porous thermal plume
{parent=Porous thermal plume}
{title2=$M=\beta F_h/C_h,\quad Q=(36\kappa Mz)^{1/3},\quad B=g\alpha F_h/C_h$}

For a slender <porous thermal plume>, <Darcy law> gives $w=\beta(T-T_0)$, with $\beta=Kg\alpha/\nu$. If $C_h=\rho_0c_p$ and $F_h=C_h\int w(T-T_0)dx$ is the conserved excess convective <heat flux> per span, then the kinematic <momentum flux> is $M=\int w^2dx=\beta F_h/C_h$, and the <buoyancy flux> is $B=\int g\alpha(T-T_0)w dx=g\alpha F_h/C_h$. The <hyperbolic-secant porous plume profile> has $Q=(36\kappa Mz)^{1/3}$. Multiplication of $Q$ and $M$ by $\rho_0$ gives physical <mass flux> and the density-weighted Darcy second moment. Intrinsic pore-velocity momentum flux instead has an additional inverse-porosity factor. Momentum conservation here follows from the heat/velocity proportionality, not from an inertia balance: porous drag balances buoyancy locally.

= Hyperbolic-secant porous plume profile
{parent=Porous thermal plume}
{title2=$w=W\operatorname{sech}^2(x/b)$}

The symmetric steady <porous thermal plume> has $w=W\operatorname{sech}^2(x/b)$, $T-T_0=w/\beta$ and $\psi=-Wb\tanh(x/b)$. Its exact normalizations are $Wb^2=6\kappa z$ and $(4/3)W^2b=Q$, so $b=(48\kappa^2z^2/Q)^{1/3}$ and $W=(3Q^2/(32\kappa z))^{1/3}$. The <streamfunction> supplies lateral entrainment required by continuity.

= Heat-weighted head speed of a porous plume
{parent=Hyperbolic-secant porous plume profile}
{title2=$\dot Z_h\simeq\tfrac23W(Z_h)$}

Truncating the steady <porous thermal plume> profile below a head height and conserving supplied heat gives $\dot Z_h\simeq\int w^2dx/\int wdx=(2/3)W(Z_h)$. Thus $Z_h\propto t^{3/4}$. This is an integral bulk-head estimate, while fastest centreline parcels have characteristic speed $W$; the approximation does not resolve the transient nose.

= Darcy dissipation
{parent=Darcy law}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal D=\mu|\mathbf u|^2/\Pi$}

A <Darcy velocity> $\mathbf u$ driven by pressure in a stationary porous skeleton dissipates mechanical power $\mu|\mathbf u|^2/\Pi$ per bulk volume. It follows by dotting <Darcy law> with the discharge: $-\mathbf u\cdot\nabla p=\mu|\mathbf u|^2/\Pi$. Its contribution to a <heat equation> can be neglected only when it is small relative to the relevant thermal-energy transfer.

= Specific discharge
{synonym}

<Darcy velocity>, also called <Darcy flux> or <specific discharge>, is <fluid> <volume flux> relative to the solid framework per unit total cross-sectional area. For a uniform axial discharge $Q$ through bulk area $A$,
$$
q=\frac QA.
$$
The area includes both solid and voids. Hence <Darcy velocity> is a superficial flux with units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, even though no <fluid> travels through the solid part. For a nonuniform discharge, $Q=\int_A\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,dA$, with $\mathbf n$ the unit normal; $Q/A$ is the area average of the normal <Darcy flux>.

If <porosity> is $\epsilon$, the pore-volume-averaged liquid <velocity> is $\mathbf v_l$, and the local solid framework <velocity> is $\mathbf v_s$, the convention used here is $\mathbf u=\epsilon(\mathbf v_l-\mathbf v_s)$. In a stationary, fully saturated framework with all pore volume participating, $\mathbf v_l=\mathbf q/\epsilon$. The notation $\phi=\epsilon$ for <porosity> is equally common. <Darcy flow through a homogeneous slab> gives $q=2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ but <pore velocity> $v=8.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ at $\phi=0.25$. Keeping these quantities separate lets <Darcy law> predict filter discharge while <pore velocity> predicts ideal advective travel time.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/darcys-law/{ref}

= Pore velocity
{parent=Darcy velocity}
{title2=$v=q/\phi$}

= Interstitial velocity
{synonym}

<Pore velocity> is the <fluid> <velocity> averaged over pore volume relative to the solid framework. For uniform <porosity> $\phi$, full saturation and all pores participating, $\mathbf v=\mathbf q/\phi$, with <Darcy velocity> $\mathbf q$ defined per bulk area. The identity follows from volume averaging: extending the relative <fluid> <velocity> by zero in the solid gives a bulk average equal to the pore fraction times the pore-volume average. It does not assert that every local passage has open area $\phi A$ or identical speed.

In a uniform advective model, travel over length $L$ takes $L/v=\phi L/q$. If some pore volume is inaccessible to transport, the corresponding estimate uses <effective porosity> $\phi_e$ and $v=q/\phi_e$. A connected but nearly stagnant passage can exchange solute slowly, so even this mean does not describe all arrival times. <Flux-weighted residence time in a porous layer> explains one precise averaging convention.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/darcys-law/{ref}

{c}
{parent=Darcy law}

= Rayleigh-Darcy convection
{c}
{synonym}

Darcy-Bénard convection is <thermal convection> in a porous layer heated from below, with velocity governed by <Darcy's law>. With fixed temperatures and impermeable boundaries, a normalized two-dimensional model uses <streamfunction> $\psi$ and temperature $\theta$:
$$
\nabla^2\psi=-R\theta_x,\qquad \theta_t+\psi_z\theta_x-\psi_x\theta_z=\nabla^2\theta.
$$
The control parameter is a porous-medium <Rayleigh number>. On $0<z<1$ with temperature values $0,-1$, the conductive state is $\psi=0$, $\theta=-z$.

= Insulated square-column Darcy convection onset
{parent=Darcy-Bénard convection}
{title2=$\Gamma_c=\pi^2\kappa/(Ba^2)$}

A tall porous column with square width $a$, insulated impermeable sidewalls and a maintained upward temperature decrease $\Gamma$ admits nearly vertical, zero-net-flux overturning modes. Let $B=\rho_0g\alpha K/\mu$ be buoyancy mobility and $\kappa=k_e/(\rho_0c_f)$ the thermal transport coefficient accompanying <Darcy flux>. Neumann transverse modes have $k_\perp^2=\pi^2(m^2+n^2)/a^2$. In the long-column limit the lowest nonconstant modes have growth rate proportional to $B\Gamma-\kappa\pi^2/a^2$. They are warm ascending and cold descending columns with return flow at distant ends. The two lowest modes, varying along either side direction, are degenerate; linear theory alone does not select their nonlinear superposition.

= Conducting-square Darcy convection
{parent=Darcy-Bénard convection}
{title2=$R_c=8\pi^2$}

In a unit square with prescribed conductive boundary <temperatures> and impermeable boundaries, the <stream function> convention $\mathbf u=(-\psi_z,0,\psi_x)$ gives linear equations $\Delta\psi=R\theta_x$, $\theta_t=\psi_x+\Delta\theta$, with $\psi=\theta=0$ on all sides. The <velocity> equation follows by taking the vertical-plane <curl> of <Darcy law>, and the <temperature> equation by linearizing the <heat equation> about $1-z$. Unlike a laterally unbounded Darcy layer, the conducting side boundaries produce a two-dimensional real marginal <eigenspace> at $R_c=8\pi^2$.

= Growth-rate solvability for conducting-square Darcy convection
{parent=Conducting-square Darcy convection}
{title2=$\lambda=(2q_1/q_c)\|\nabla\theta_0\|_2^2/\|\theta_0\|_2^2$}

For $q=q_c+\epsilon q_1$, $\theta_t=\epsilon\lambda\theta$, the first-order equations are $\Delta\psi_1-q_c^2\theta_{1x}=2q_cq_1\theta_{0x}$ and $\psi_{1x}+\Delta\theta_1=\lambda\theta_0$. Multiply them by $\psi_0,q_c^2\theta_0$ and integrate. <Integration by parts> and the leading equations cancel the homogeneous correction terms, giving $2q_cq_1\langle\psi_0\theta_{0x}\rangle+q_c^2\lambda\langle\theta_0^2\rangle=0$. Since $\langle\psi_0\theta_{0x}\rangle=-\langle|\nabla\theta_0|^2\rangle$, the displayed title formula follows. The reciprocal norm ratio is incompatible with this <solvability condition>. Reflection parity diagonalizes the first-order splitting of the two marginal modes.

= Gauge transformation for conducting-square Darcy onset
{parent=Conducting-square Darcy convection}
{title2=$P=e^{-iq(x-1/2)/2}F$}

At zero growth rate put $P=\psi+iq\theta$, $q=\sqrt R$. The coupled Darcy equations become $\Delta P+iqP_x=0$. Substituting $P=e^{-iq(x-1/2)/2}F$ gives $\Delta F+(q^2/4)F=0$ with homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. The square <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalues> give $q^2=4\pi^2(m^2+n^2)$, $m,n\geq1$, whose minimum is $8\pi^2$. For $S=\sin\pi x\sin\pi z$, $a=q_c/2$, the complex multiples $F=S,iS$ give real pairs $(S\cos(a\xi),-S\sin(a\xi)/q_c)$ and $(S\sin(a\xi),S\cos(a\xi)/q_c)$, $\xi=x-1/2$. Their reflection parities are opposite, yielding two independent physical modes.

= Darcy thermal-time Rayleigh number
{c}
{parent=Darcy-Bénard convection}
{title2=$R=g\alpha\Delta T Kd/(\nu\kappa_T)$}

With permeability $K$, depth $d$, <kinematic viscosity> $\nu$ and the relevant effective <thermal diffusivity> $\kappa_T$, the Darcy <Rayleigh number> compares <buoyancy>-driven Darcy <advection> to thermal diffusion. It differs from the clear-fluid <Rayleigh number> by replacing $d^3$ with $Kd$. Effective heat capacity and porosity factors may be absorbed into the definition of $\kappa_T$ and the thermal-time scale, and must be kept consistent with the chosen <Darcy law> convention.

= Onset of convection in a horizontal Darcy layer
{parent=Darcy-Bénard convection}
{title2=$R_c=4\pi^2$}

A perturbation proportional to $\sin(n\pi z)e^{st+ikx}$ has <growth rate>
$$
s=\frac{Rk^2}{k^2+n^2\pi^2}-(k^2+n^2\pi^2).
$$
Minimizing the neutral curve $(k^2+n^2\pi^2)^2/k^2$ gives $n=1$, $k=\pi$ and $R_c=4\pi^2$. Thus the first convection rolls have horizontal wavelength twice the layer depth.

= Supercritical saturation of Darcy convection rolls
{parent=Onset of convection in a horizontal Darcy layer}

For $R=4\pi^2+\epsilon^2$, fixed roll phase, $T=\epsilon^2t$ and normalization $\psi=\epsilon A(T)\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi z)+\cdots$, the <Landau amplitude equation> is
$$
A_T=\frac12A-\frac{\pi^2}{8}A^3.
$$
The <mean-temperature correction in weakly nonlinear Darcy convection> supplies the cubic negative feedback. Projection of the third-order equations onto the critical <eigenfunction> via the <Fredholm solvability condition for a self-adjoint operator> fixes both coefficients. The stable nonzero amplitudes are $\pm2/\pi$ within the chosen phase, giving a supercritical <pitchfork bifurcation> in this real-amplitude reduction.

= Mean-temperature correction in weakly nonlinear Darcy convection
{parent=Supercritical saturation of Darcy convection rolls}

For $\psi_1=\cos(\pi x)\sin(\pi z)$, the first temperature mode is $\theta_1=(2\pi)^{-1}\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi z)$. The <streamfunction advection bracket> is $J(\psi_1,\theta_1)=(\pi/4)\sin(2\pi z)$, independent of $x$. The second-order equations therefore have $\psi_2=0$ and
$$
\theta_2=-\frac1{16\pi}\sin(2\pi z).
$$
This modifies the vertical temperature gradient. Its interaction with the critical roll produces the cubic term in the <amplitude equation>.

= Saffman–Taylor instability
{c}
{parent=Darcy law}
{wiki}

The Saffman–Taylor instability occurs when a more mobile fluid displaces a less mobile fluid in a porous medium or Hele–Shaw cell. Viscosity contrast amplifies interface corrugations, while <surface tension> suppresses sufficiently short wavelengths.

= Planar viscous-fingering dispersion relation
{parent=Saffman–Taylor instability}
{title2=$\sigma(\alpha)$}

For two fluids with <dynamic viscosities> $\mu_1<\mu_2$, uniform <permeability of a porous medium> $k$, <porosity> $\phi$, and imposed <Darcy velocity> $U$, the <Saffman–Taylor instability> of a planar interface has growth rate $\sigma=\alpha U(\mu_2-\mu_1)/[\phi(\mu_2+\mu_1)]$ without capillarity. With constant interfacial tension $\gamma$ and the stabilizing pressure-jump convention, a perturbation of transverse <wavenumber> $\alpha>0$ instead has
$$
\sigma=\frac{\alpha}{\phi(\mu_1+\mu_2)}[(\mu_2-\mu_1)U-k\gamma\alpha^2].
$$
The fastest-growing mode satisfies $\alpha_m^2=(\mu_2-\mu_1)U/(3k\gamma)$.

= Thermal-front viscous-fingering dispersion relation
{parent=Planar viscous-fingering dispersion relation}
{title2=$\sigma=\Gamma U|a|(b-1)/(b+1)$}

For a sharp thermal front moving at fraction $\Gamma$ of the <Darcy velocity> $U$, a cold fluid of viscosity $\mu$ displacing its hot counterpart of viscosity $b\mu$ has the displayed growth rate in the local, unbounded planar approximation without <thermal conduction>. The harmonic pressure perturbations decay on either side. <Pressure continuity> and continuity of perturbed <Darcy flux> give the velocity response $\delta U=U|a|(b-1)\eta/(b+1)$; the thermal front's kinematic law is $\eta_t=\Gamma\delta U$. The prefactor is the thermal-front speed, not the material <pore velocity>. Increasing viscosity with heating therefore makes the trailing thermal interface unstable even when the leading displacement of the original fluid is stable.

= Buoyancy-modified Darcy fingering dispersion relation
{parent=Planar viscous-fingering dispersion relation}
{title2=$\sigma(\alpha)$}

Let fluid 1 lie above fluid 2, with <mass densities> $\rho_1<\rho_2$ and <dynamic viscosities> $\mu_1<\mu_2$. Define $\Delta\rho=\rho_2-\rho_1>0$, $\Delta\mu=\mu_2-\mu_1>0$, <porosity> $\phi$, <permeability of a porous medium> $k$, and downward interface speed $W$. With <Darcy flux> $\phi W$, no capillarity, and two unbounded layers, <Darcy's law> gives
$$
\sigma(\alpha)=\frac{|\alpha|[\phi W\Delta\mu-kg\Delta\rho]}{\phi(\mu_1+\mu_2)}.
$$
For a <Fourier mode> of interface displacement, the two perturbation <pressures> decay exponentially into the respective layers. The <kinematic boundary condition> makes their amplitudes proportional to $\phi\mu_1\sigma/(k|\alpha|)$ and $-\phi\mu_2\sigma/(k|\alpha|)$. Linearized <pressure continuity> adds the base-gradient difference $\phi W\Delta\mu/k-g\Delta\rho$. Eliminating the amplitudes proves the formula. The critical interface speed is $kg\Delta\rho/(\phi\Delta\mu)$; the critical <Darcy velocity> is $kg\Delta\rho/\Delta\mu$. Without <surface tension> this model has unbounded short-wave <growth rate> on its unstable side.

= Surface-tension-gradient stabilization of viscous fingering
{parent=Planar viscous-fingering dispersion relation}

Suppose the apparent <capillary pressure> is $p_1-p_2=\gamma(1+\beta x)(\kappa_0+\nabla\cdot\mathbf n)$, with normal towards the displaced fluid and positive apparent tension at the flat front $x=X$. Linearizing the <Darcy law> interface problem gives the local growth rate
$$
\sigma(\alpha;X)=\frac{\alpha}{\phi(\mu_1+\mu_2)}[(\mu_2-\mu_1)U-k\gamma\kappa_0\beta-k\gamma(1+\beta X)\alpha^2].
$$
The pressure increase experienced by a forward protrusion is stabilizing when $\kappa_0\beta>0$. It suppresses every positive <wavenumber> when $U\le k\gamma\kappa_0\beta/(\mu_2-\mu_1)$. For a fixed spatial gradient, $X=Ut/\phi$ makes the rate time dependent; constant exponential growth is a frozen-position approximation.

= Intrinsic permeability
{synonym}

<Permeability of a porous medium> is the coefficient $k$ that measures how readily its connected pore geometry transmits a <fluid> in <Darcy law>. Its units are $\mathrm{m^2}$, since $k/\mu$ converts a <pressure gradient> in $\mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}}$ into a <Darcy velocity> in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Large open, well-connected passages generally give larger <permeability of a porous medium> than narrow or winding passages. <Porosity> alone cannot determine it: two samples can contain the same void volume but have different passage sizes and connectivity.

For an unchanged framework and the basic no-slip <Newtonian fluid> model, intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> does not contain <dynamic viscosity> or <mass density>. These properties instead enter <hydraulic conductivity> $K=k\rho g/\mu$. At the same horizontal <pressure gradient>, replacing a liquid with another of twice the <dynamic viscosity> halves <Darcy flux> even though $k$ stays fixed. Structural changes, such as swelling or clogging, can change $k$; the independence claim assumes the pore geometry stays fixed.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/hydraulic-conductivity/{ref}

An <isotropic> sample has scalar <permeability of a porous medium>. An <anisotropic> sample requires a <tensor> $\boldsymbol{k}$ in $\mathbf q=-\boldsymbol{k}(\nabla p-\rho\mathbf g)/\mu$, so discharge need not be parallel to the net driving force. <Layered porous permeability> gives a concrete example: arranging individually <isotropic> materials in horizontal layers produces different vertical and horizontal <effective permeabilities>.

= Relative permeability
{parent=Permeability of a porous medium}
{title2=$k_{ri}(s)$}

A dimensionless reduction of a porous formation's intrinsic <permeability of a porous medium> for one fluid phase in multiphase flow. Its effective phase permeability is $Kk_{ri}$, and its <phase mobility> is $k_{ri}/\mu_i$. It depends on saturation and pore-scale phase configuration; it is not the magnetic use of relative permeability.

= Phase mobility
{parent=Relative permeability}
{title2=$\lambda_i=k_{ri}/\mu_i$}

Relative permeability divided by the phase's <dynamic viscosity>. In the phase <Darcy law>, $q_i=-K\lambda_i\nabla p_i$ without body forces. Ratios of mobilities determine <fractional flow> when the phases have a common pressure gradient.

= Effective permeability
{parent=Permeability of a porous medium}
{title2=$k_{\rm eff}$}

<Effective permeability> is a bulk <permeability of a porous medium> that reproduces the total <Darcy flux> through a heterogeneous sample under specified boundary conditions. For a horizontal sample with length $L$, <dynamic viscosity> $\mu$, and positive inlet-to-outlet <fluid pressure> drop $\Delta p$, define $k_{\rm eff}$ by $Q/A=k_{\rm eff}\Delta p/(\mu L)$. The description is useful when individual layers or passages are smaller than the scale of the calculation. It depends on direction, geometry and boundary conditions; it is not generally the <arithmetic mean> of local <intrinsic permeabilities>.

<Layered porous permeability> illustrates the mechanism: when every path must cross a resistant layer, successive <hydraulic resistances> add; when more permeable paths span the whole sample alongside less permeable ones, their discharges add. This is the same organizing idea as series and parallel <resistors>. For fixed <fluid> properties the corresponding <hydraulic conductivities> average in the same way.

= Layered porous permeability
{parent=Effective permeability}
{title2=$k_\perp=(\sum_i f_i/k_i)^{-1},\quad k_\parallel=\sum_i f_i k_i$}

Consider planar horizontal layers with positive scalar intrinsic <intrinsic permeabilities> $k_i$, thicknesses $b_i$, and fractions $f_i=b_i/\sum_jb_j$. Assume each layer is uniform and <isotropic>, fully saturated by the same <Newtonian fluid> of constant <mass density> and <dynamic viscosity>, with perfect hydraulic contact and steady <incompressible flow>. Use boundaries that impose pure flow normal to the layers or a common gradient of <hydraulic head> along them; additional interface resistance and finite-edge effects are excluded.

For vertical flow normal to the layers, every layer carries the same normal <Darcy flux> by <mass conservation>. The <hydraulic head> drops add. Writing $B=\sum_i b_i$ and taking positive $\Delta h_i$ as the drop along the flow direction gives
$$
q=\frac{\rho g}{\mu}k_i\frac{\Delta h_i}{b_i},\qquad \Delta h=\sum_i\Delta h_i=\frac{\mu q}{\rho g}\sum_i\frac{b_i}{k_i},\qquad k_\perp=\frac{B}{\sum_i b_i/k_i}=\left(\sum_i\frac{f_i}{k_i}\right)^{-1}.
$$
This is the thickness-weighted <harmonic mean>. It is <hydraulic head>, rather than <fluid pressure> alone, that accounts correctly for <gravity> in the vertical calculation. A thin layer of very small <permeability of a porous medium> can control the whole discharge because all flow must pass through it.

For horizontal flow parallel to the layers, each layer experiences the same gradient of <hydraulic head>. For width $W$, each area is $A_i=Wb_i$, and the individual <volume fluxes> add:
$$
Q=\sum_i Q_i=-\frac{\rho g}{\mu}\frac{dh}{dx}\sum_i k_i A_i,\qquad \frac QA=-\frac{\rho g}{\mu}\frac{dh}{dx}\sum_i f_i k_i,\qquad k_\parallel=\sum_i f_i k_i.
$$
This is the thickness-weighted <arithmetic mean>. A highly permeable layer can carry most of the horizontal flow because it supplies a continuous route from inlet to outlet. Woessner and Poeter derive both cases for <hydraulic conductivity>; identical <fluid> properties allow the common factor $\rho g/\mu$ to cancel.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/equation-derivation-for-equivalent-k-and-a-4-layer-application/{ref}

For two equally thick layers, take $k_1=k_0$ and $k_2=9k_0$, where $k_0=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The values 1 and 9 are therefore permeabilities in units of $k_0$, rather than dimensionless intrinsic <intrinsic permeabilities>. Then
$$
k_\perp=\frac{2}{1/k_0+1/(9k_0)}=1.8k_0,\qquad k_\parallel=\frac{k_0+9k_0}{2}=5k_0.
$$
Across the layers, the less permeable half takes $90\%$ of the total <hydraulic head> drop and the more permeable half only $10\%$. Along the layers, the more permeable half carries $90\%$ of the total <volume flux>. At the same magnitude of imposed gradient of <hydraulic head>, horizontal <Darcy flux> is $5/1.8=25/9\simeq2.78$ times the vertical <Darcy flux>.

The arrangement thus creates physical <anisotropy> from locally <isotropic> ingredients. In coordinates with $x,y$ along the layers and $z$ normal to them, the effective <permeability of a porous medium> <tensor> is $\boldsymbol{k}_{\rm eff}=\operatorname{diag}(5,5,1.8)k_0$. A driving gradient of <hydraulic head> with both horizontal and vertical components generally produces a discharge in a different direction. This distinction matters when deciding whether a geological bed conducts <aquifer> flow along itself or acts as a barrier to flow across it.

= Dilute permeability enhancement by aligned cracks
{parent=Effective permeability}
{title2=$k_{xx}^*=k\left[1+\pi\phi a^2\frac\alpha{1+\alpha}+O((\phi a^2)^2)\right]$}

At small areal number density $\phi$, identical aligned cracks contribute independently the <fixed-throughflow dissipation reduction of a conductive crack>. Equating the mean dissipation density to $\mu U^2/k_{xx}^*$ gives the displayed first-order enhancement. Writing $k/[1-\pi\phi a^2\alpha/(1+\alpha)]$ is an algebraic noninteraction estimate, not a claim of accuracy at higher density. The enhancement saturates as crack conductance becomes large compared with transport through the surrounding porous matrix.

= Effective permeability of complementary wedges
{parent=Effective permeability}
{title2=$k_{\rm eff}=(k_1-k_2)/\log(k_1/k_2)$}

In a slender layer, two parallel sublayers of fractions $x/L$ and $1-x/L$ have local effective permeability $K(x)=k_1x/L+k_2(1-x/L)$. Integrating their successive hydraulic resistance gives the logarithmic mean displayed above. A leading <pressure gradient> uniform across the thickness does not make parcel paths horizontal; a small transverse velocity preserves <mass conservation> as the cross-sectional conductance changes.

= Porosity
{title2=$\epsilon$}
{parent=Permeability of a porous medium}
{wiki}

<Porosity> is the fraction of a representative bulk material volume occupied by voids:
$$
\phi=\epsilon=\frac{V_{\rm pore}}{V_{\rm bulk}},\qquad 0\leq\phi\leq1.
$$
It is dimensionless and measures storage space. A sample with bulk volume $1.0\,\mathrm L$ and pore volume $0.25\,\mathrm L$ has <porosity> $\phi=0.25=25\%$. If all its pores are saturated by one liquid, the liquid occupies $0.25\,\mathrm L$; partial <fluid saturation> $s$ instead gives liquid volume $s\phi V_{\rm bulk}$.

<Porosity> and <permeability of a porous medium> answer different questions: how much <fluid> fits, and how easily it flows. Isolated bubbles or cavities can contribute to total <porosity> while providing no throughgoing path. Even connected passages may be narrow enough to transmit very little discharge. In the simple fully participating saturated model, <Darcy velocity> is $\phi$ times <pore velocity>; changing <porosity> at a stipulated fixed <Darcy flux> changes ideal advective travel time, but <porosity> alone cannot predict the <Darcy flux>.

= Pore space
{parent=Porosity}

The void volume within a <porous medium>. <Porosity> is its fraction of bulk volume; saturation specifies which fluids occupy that void volume.

= Pore fluid
{parent=Pore space}

A <liquid> or <gas> occupying connected or isolated <pore space>. Its <pressure> and compressibility affect the mechanical and seismic response of porous <rock>.

= Pore fluid pressure
{parent=Pore fluid}
{title2=$P$}

The <pressure> of fluid in <pore space>. On fluid-accessible contacts, it reduces effective compression and can promote frictional sliding or tensile opening at fixed total <stress>.

= Effective porosity
{parent=Porosity}
{title2=$\phi_e$}

<Effective porosity> is the pore-volume fraction accessible to connected transport under the specified measurement or model. It excludes isolated pore volume and may also exclude poorly exchanging regions on the transport timescale, so $0\leq\phi_e\leq\phi$ for total <porosity> $\phi$. In a simple mobile-volume model the mean <pore velocity> is $q/\phi_e$; the assumptions behind that model matter when interpreting tracer arrival times. A sample with total <porosity> $0.25$ but mobile pore fraction $0.20$ and <Darcy flux> $2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ has mobile mean <pore velocity> $1.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Woessner and Poeter discuss the role of connectivity and slowly exchanging pores.https://books.gw-project.org/hydrogeologic-properties-of-earth-materials-and-principles-of-groundwater-flow/chapter/effective-porosity/{ref}

= Pressure-dependent Darcy drainage
{parent=Darcy law}

Suppose a porous layer has thickness $h(p)$ and <permeability of a porous medium> $k(p)$, and the pressure drop $p$ acts across its thickness. <Darcy's law> gives the drainage <volume flux>
$$
w=\frac{k(p)}{\mu h(p)}p.
$$
If $k/h=Kp^{-\beta}$, then $w=(K/\mu)p^{1-\beta}$.

= Reactive infiltration instability
{parent=Porous-media flow}

Reactive infiltration instability is the positive feedback in which reaction or melting increases <permeability of a porous medium>, focuses more reacting fluid into that region, and further increases permeability. Finite reaction-zone thickness, transport, and pore geometry can change the short-wave behavior; https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.5034[Interacting length scales in the reactive-infiltration instability] analyzes transport and reaction length scales in a dissolution model.

= Melting-front instability due to permeability contrast
{parent=Reactive infiltration instability}

A planar <advection-driven melting front in a porous matrix> with permeabilities $k$ ahead and $k+\Delta k$ behind has the ideal sharp-front <growth rate>
$$
\sigma=|\alpha|V\frac{\Delta k}{2k+\Delta k}.
$$
This follows by matching <harmonic> pressure perturbations and normal <Darcy flux>, then converting flux to front speed by the <enthalpy> balance. It predicts no finite fastest-growing wavelength when $\Delta k>0$.

= Porous gravity current
{parent=Porous-media flow}

A porous gravity current spreads because a density difference creates a lateral hydrostatic-pressure gradient. For a two-dimensional current of thickness $h$, density deficit $\Delta\rho$, permeability $k$, viscosity $\mu$, and porosity $\phi$,
$$
\phi h_t=\frac{k\Delta\rho g}{\mu}(h h_x)_x.
$$

= Inclined porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current}
{title2=$\phi h_t+\partial_x[uh-u\cot\theta\,hh_x]=0$}

For a thin buoyant layer beneath an inclined impermeable roof, the <hydrostatic approximation> and <Darcy's law> give $u=k g\Delta\rho\sin\theta/\mu$ and the <volume flux per unit width> $F=uh-u\cot\theta\,hh_x$. The first term transports fluid upslope; the second spreads the layer through its hydrostatic pressure gradient. The <porosity> multiplies the stored fluid volume, so the advection speed is $u/\phi$, not the <Darcy velocity> $u$.

= Volume Jacobian for downslope stretched coordinates
{parent=Inclined porous gravity current}
{title2=$V=P\cot^2\theta\iint h\,d\xi\,d\eta$}

With constant <porosity> $P$ and $X=x\tan\theta$, $Y=y\tan\theta$, a translating frame $\xi=X-T$, $\eta=Y$ has area <Jacobian determinant> $dx\,dy=\cot^2\theta\,d\xi\,d\eta$. Actual fluid volume is $P\iint h\,dx\,dy$, proving the displayed normalization. Replacing the cotangent factor by a tangent factor is incompatible with these coordinates except at a slope of $\pi/4$.

= Depth-dependent inclined porous-current equation
{parent=Inclined porous gravity current}
{title2=$\partial_T h^\alpha+\partial_Xh^\beta=\nabla\cdot(h^\beta\nabla h)$}

For <porosity> $Pz^{\alpha-1}$ and <permeability of a porous medium> $Kz^{\beta-1}$ above an inclined impermeable plane, stored liquid volume per area is $Ph^\alpha/\alpha$. The depth-integrated <Darcy flux> is $\rho gKh^\beta(\sin\theta-\cos\theta\,h_x,-\cos\theta\,h_y)/(\beta\mu)$. <Conservation of mass> yields the displayed equation with $X=x\tan\theta$, $Y=y\tan\theta$ and $T=t\alpha\rho gK\sin\theta\tan\theta/(\beta\mu P)$.

= Far-downslope width of a constant-flux porous current
{parent=Depth-dependent inclined porous-current equation}
{title2=$Y_N\propto X^{\beta/(2\beta+1)}$}

In a slender steady <porous gravity current>, the dominant balance is $(h^\beta)_X=(h^\beta h_Y)_Y$. If its width and height scales are $W,H$, fixed downslope <volume flux> gives $H^\beta W=\text{constant}$, and transverse spreading gives $W^2\sim HX$. Eliminating $H$ yields the displayed width exponent. The porosity exponent affects transient storage but not this steady balance.

= Advection limit of an inclined porous current
{parent=Inclined porous gravity current}
{title2=$h=h_0\exp[-(t-\phi x/u)/\tau]$}

With exponentially decreasing injection, the advective approximation $\phi h_t+uh_x=0$ and inlet condition $uh(0,t)=Qe^{-t/\tau}$ give $h_0=Q/u$ and the displayed solution along <characteristic curves>. It applies behind the advective nose $X=ut/\phi$. The relative size of the discarded nonlinear spreading term for this profile is $2\phi h\cot\theta/(u\tau)$. Smallness of that quantity, and matching through a narrow leading layer, are necessary qualifications: elapsed time alone does not make the approximation uniform at the nose. In a translating frame the full equation remains a <porous medium equation> with coefficient $u\cot\theta/\phi$.

= One-sided constant-volume porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current}
{title2=$L^3=9\lambda Vt/\phi$}

A localized release of actual fluid volume $V$ per unit width on a reflecting half-line $x\geq0$ has the <Barenblatt solution> $h=[L^2-x^2]_+/(6\lambda t)$, where $L^3=9\lambda Vt/\phi$ and $\lambda=k\Delta\rho g/(\phi\mu)$. The <mass conservation> normalization is $\phi\int_0^Lh\,dx=V$. Zero slope at the reflecting boundary gives zero <Darcy flux>. For symmetric spreading on the full line, the same formula has $L^3=9\lambda Vt/(2\phi)$ when $V$ denotes the total released volume. A finite initial patch is not determined by volume alone; this exact source profile has a concentrated initial release.

= Slope-driven porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current}
{title2=$u=K\Delta\rho g\sin\theta/\mu$}

A buoyant phase beneath a sloping impermeable cap has a slope-driven <Darcy velocity> proportional to density deficit and slope. Far from thickness-adjustment regions, its uniform plateau speed is $K\Delta\rho g\sin\theta/\mu$. With thickness measured normal to the slope, buoyant cap overpressure is $\Delta\rho g h\cos\theta$. A complete depth model also includes the along-current gradient of thickness.

= Caprock leakage threshold
{parent=Slope-driven porous gravity current}
{title2=$\Delta\rho g h\cos\theta=\Delta p_{\rm entry}$}

A buoyant current beneath an overlying low-permeability cap can enter it when current overpressure exceeds the capillary entry threshold. The plateau criterion compares $\Delta\rho g h\cos\theta$ to that threshold. Cold and warm portions can have different depths and mobilities, giving distinct onset injection rates. These onset criteria do not determine the depleted current after leakage begins.

= Moving thermal front in a porous current
{parent=Slope-driven porous gravity current}
{title2=$\rho_h(u_h-\phi V_T)h_h=\rho_c(u_c-\phi V_T)h_c$}

A localized temperature adjustment separates regions with different fluid density, viscosity and Darcy speed. <Mass conservation> in the moving frame gives the displayed plateau-depth relation. A speed stated as $V_T=\Gamma u_c$ with $u_c$ a <Darcy velocity> has effective retardation parameter $\phi\Gamma$. Setting both laboratory-frame mass fluxes equal neglects storage associated with passage of the temperature front.

= Porous gravity current with background flow
{parent=Porous gravity current}
{title2=$\phi h_t+(Uh-K_bhh_x)_x=0$}

A shallow buoyant <porous gravity current> in a deep aquifer with uniform imposed <Darcy velocity> $U$ obeys $\phi h_t+(Uh-K_bhh_x)_x=0$ away from sources, where $K_b=k\Delta\rho g/\mu$. Here $k$ is <permeability of a porous medium>, $\phi$ is <porosity>, and $\mu$ is <dynamic viscosity>. The pore-fluid drift speed is $u=U/\phi$, and the gravity <diffusion> coefficient multiplying $(hh_x)_x$ is $a=K_b/\phi$. The sharp-interface model neglects capillary trapping and dissolution.

= Advected constant-volume porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current with background flow}
{title2=$h=[R^2-(x-ut)^2]_+/(6at)$}

An instantaneous point release of actual volume $V$ per unit width in a <porous gravity current with background flow> has the <Barenblatt solution> $h=[R(t)^2-(x-ut)^2]_+/(6at)$, where $R^3=9aVt/(2\phi)$. Its centre translates at pore speed $u=U/\phi$ and its radius grows as $t^{1/3}$. The conserved physical volume is $\phi\int h\,dx=V$.

= Constant-flux porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current with background flow}
{title2=$t_c=\phi K_bQ/U^3$}

Constant actual volume input $Q$ into a <porous gravity current with background flow> gives early symmetric scales $h\sim[(Q/\phi)^2t/a]^{1/3}$ and $x\sim[a(Q/\phi)t^2]^{1/3}$. For $U>0$, drift dominates after $t_c=\phi K_bQ/U^3$. The late interior depth is $Q/U$ and the steady upstream edge is $x_-=-K_bQ/U^2$, with upstream linear profile $h=U(x-x_-)/K_b$. Upstream <volume flux> is zero because background drift balances gravity spreading.

= Diffusive nose of an advected porous gravity current
{parent=Constant-flux porous gravity current}
{title2=$w\sim\sqrt{a h_{\mathrm{int}}t}$}

The downstream edge of a continuously fed <porous gravity current with background flow> has a transition width proportional to $\sqrt{a h_{\mathrm{int}}t}$ around drift position $ut$. Its <similarity solution> satisfies $(FF\prime)\prime+\eta F\prime/2=0$, $F(-\infty)=1$, and $F(\eta_N)=0$. A finite front has $F\sim(\eta_N/2)(\eta_N-\eta)$. A clipped linear ramp is a useful mass-preserving approximation, but not an exact solution of this nonlinear transition equation.

= Leaky porous gravity current
{parent=Porous gravity current}

A leaky porous gravity current loses flux through a localized fracture or a distributed permeable boundary. At a point fracture, <mass conservation> makes the horizontal Darcy flux discontinuous by exactly the leakage rate.

= Hydrostatic leakage through a basal seal
{parent=Leaky porous gravity current}
{title2=$\Omega=\lambda\Delta\rho g/(\phi\mu b)$}

A dense <porous gravity current> of height $h$ places excess <hydrostatic pressure> $\Delta\rho gh$ on a basal seal. For seal <permeability of a porous medium> $\lambda$ and thickness $b$, <Darcy law> gives downward flux $\lambda\Delta\rho gh/(\mu b)$. Dividing the stored height-volume balance by <porosity> $\phi$ gives a linear drainage term $-\Omega h$. The coefficient has inverse-time units and does not contain an extra factor of the aquifer permeability.

= Exponential drainage transform for porous-medium diffusion
{parent=Leaky porous gravity current}
{title2=$h=e^{-\Gamma t}H(x,\tau),\quad\tau=(1-e^{-\Gamma t})/\Gamma$}

The <porous medium equation> $h_t=\lambda(hh_x)_x-\Gamma h$ with constant $\Gamma>0$ becomes $H_\tau=\lambda(HH_x)_x$ under the displayed transformation. Substitution gives $h_t+\Gamma h=e^{-\Gamma t}\tau'H_\tau$ and $\lambda(hh_x)_x=\lambda e^{-2\Gamma t}(HH_x)_x$, so $\tau'=e^{-\Gamma t}$. This transformation applies to every solution with appropriately transformed initial and boundary conditions, not just a <similarity solution>. A <one-sided constant-volume porous gravity current> therefore has remaining physical volume $Ve^{-\Gamma t}$ and a limiting front $[9\lambda V/(\phi\Gamma)]^{1/3}$. The finite transformed time $\tau\to1/\Gamma$ means that arbitrary initial profiles need not reach the universal large-time source shape before drainage removes them.

= Invasion envelope of an exponentially draining porous current
{parent=Exponential drainage transform for porous-medium diffusion}
{title2=$x_*^2=R^2(1+2\Omega s)/3$}

For an impulsive <leaky porous gravity current>, write $s=(1-e^{-\Omega t})/\Omega$ and $h=(1-\Omega s)[R^2-x^2]_+/(6Ds)$, where $R=C s^{1/3}$. At a fixed lateral location, $h_t=0$ when $x^2=R^2(1+2\Omega s)/3$. The maximum invaded thickness is the envelope $x_*(s)=C s^{1/3}\sqrt{(1+2\Omega s)/3}$, $h_*(s)=C^2(1-\Omega s)^2/(9D s^{1/3})$. The source solution is singular at the initial line release; finite source size and aquifer depth limit its near-source validity.
