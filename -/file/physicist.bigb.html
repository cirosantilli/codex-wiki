= James Prescott Joule
{c}
{parent=List of physicists}
{title2=1818–1889}
{wiki}

James Prescott Joule was an English <physicist> whose private experiments connected <electricity>, <heat> and mechanical <work>. His family ran a brewery in Salford; he developed an experimental laboratory while working in the family business.<James Prescott Joule by Science Museum Group>[Biographical account: brewing family, private laboratory and experimental interests]{ref} The Royal Society's archive identifies his occupation as <physicist> and records his life as 1818–1889.<James Prescott Joule by Royal Society>[Person record NA7041: occupation and life dates]{ref}

\Image[https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6a/James_Prescott_Joule_by_John_Collier%2C_1882.jpg]
{width=250}
{title=Portrait of <James Prescott Joule>, 1882}
{source=https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:James_Prescott_Joule_by_John_Collier,_1882.jpg}

His electrical heating experiments placed metal coils in water and measured the <temperature> change. Later, he used the brewery cellar to reduce ambient <temperature> fluctuations during friction experiments and took particular care over <thermometer calibration>.<James Prescott Joule by Science Museum Group>[Electrical heating experiments; brewery-cellar friction measurements and precision thermometers]{ref} These practical choices address a central difficulty of <calorimetry>: a small <temperature> rise can reflect both the intended input of <energy> and unwanted <heat transfer> to the surroundings. A quiet baseline and a known <heat capacity> are part of the measurement, not decoration around the formula.

The contrast between <Joule heating> and <Joule's paddle-wheel experiment> makes the connection especially clear. One supplies electrical <work>; the other supplies mechanical <work>. In each case, <calorimetry> tracks the resulting change of <internal energy>. Comparing quantitatively controlled inputs with thermal changes gives an experimental test of <conservation of energy>, rather than merely showing that stirring or an <electric current> can warm water. The paddle-wheel account describes the descending weights, vanes, thermal controls and corrections needed for that comparison.

<Joule's free-expansion experiment> asks a different question: how does a gas's <temperature> change during expansion without external <work>? The <Joule-Thomson effect> instead concerns flow through a restriction and a balance involving <enthalpy>. Keeping the two experiments distinct prevents a shared historical name from hiding different system boundaries and different conserved quantities.

= A. Einstein
{c}
{synonym}

A <physicist> whose work connects <special relativity>, <general relativity>, <Brownian motion> and the <photoelectric effect>. He worked at the Institute for Advanced Study from 1933 until his death in 1955.<Albert Einstein (institutional profile) by Institute for Advanced Study>[Institutional profile: physicist, life dates and Institute appointment]{ref}

In 1905, <Albert Einstein> used the <photoelectric effect> to explore quantized <light>, related <Brownian motion> to molecular agitation, formulated <special relativity>, and connected changes in <mass> and <energy>. These problems link visible measurements to otherwise inaccessible structure: emitted <electron> energies, suspended-particle displacements and comparisons of clocks.<Albert Einstein: The Great Works by Center for History of Physics>[March, May, June and September 1905: the four papers and their physical questions]{ref}

The <Millikan photoelectric experiment> follows one of those predictions into the laboratory. Its stopping-voltage measurements test a quantitative relation; the experiment's apparatus, surface preparation and electrical corrections matter to that inference. The <Einstein field equations> and <general relativity> lead to a different set of tests involving <gravity> and <spacetime>.

The <Nobel Prize in Physics in 1921> recognized his theoretical work, particularly the law of the <photoelectric effect>; the award was presented in 1922.<Albert Einstein, Facts by Nobel Prize Outreach>[Prize motivation and the note that Einstein received the 1921 award in 1922]{ref}

= Lord Rayleigh
{c}
{synonym}

A <physicist> whose research joined <waves>, <optics>, <acoustics> and precise measurement. He maintained a laboratory at Terling and served as Cavendish professor of experimental <physics> from 1879 to 1884. At Cambridge he developed laboratory courses; later he donated his Nobel award proceeds toward a Cavendish laboratory extension.<John William Strutt (Lord Rayleigh) by John Joseph O'Connor and Edmund Frederick Robertson>[O'Connor and Robertson, biography: Terling laboratory; Cavendish professorship and laboratory courses; donation of Nobel proceeds]{ref}

The <Rayleigh-Ramsay argon density experiment>, pursued with <William Ramsay>, connects a small discrepancy in gas weighing to chemical separation. Its lesson is practical: agreement within one preparation method does not ensure that a second method produces the same <chemical substance>. The <gas density> model explains how composition and filling conditions both affect the readout.
