= Arithmetic function
{title2=$f:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb C$}
{wiki}

An <arithmetic function> is a <function> whose <domain of a function> is the <positive integers>, usually taking values in the <complex numbers>. Thus $f(1),f(2),\ldots$ are its values; $f(0)$ is outside this <domain of a function>. Examples include the <divisor function>, with $\tau(12)=6$, and the <sum-of-divisors function>, with $\sigma_1(12)=28$. Studying an <arithmetic function> packages a question about each <positive integer> into one object whose values can be combined using <Dirichlet convolution>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Section 2.1: arithmetic functions]{ref}

The Liouville function is $\lambda(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)}$, where $\Omega(n)$ is the <total number of prime factors> counted with multiplicity. It is a <completely multiplicative function>: multiplication adds prime-factor multiplicities, so $\lambda(ab)=\lambda(a)\lambda(b)$.

= Integer radical
{synonym}

The <radical of an integer> $n>0$ is the <product> of its distinct <prime factors>, each included exactly once. If the <prime factorization> is $n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$ with distinct <prime numbers> $p_i$ and positive <integer> exponents $a_i$, then
$$
\operatorname{rad}(n)=p_1\cdots p_r=\prod_{p\mid n}p.
$$
Thus the <radical of an integer> remembers which <prime factors> occur and forgets their <prime valuations>. At $n=1$, the <empty product> gives $\operatorname{rad}(1)=1$. The <domain of a function> here is the <positive integers>; zero is excluded. https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~evertsejh/dio12-ex4.pdf[Jan-Hendrik Evertse, Diophantine approximation, Homework IV, Exercise 20: definition of the integer radical]{ref}

For example, $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ has distinct <prime factors> $2,3$, so $\operatorname{rad}(72)=2\cdot3=6$. Similarly,
$$
\operatorname{rad}(30)=30,\qquad
\operatorname{rad}(32)=2,\qquad
\operatorname{rad}(36)=6,
$$
using the <prime factorizations> $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$, $32=2^5$ and $36=2^2\cdot3^2$. The <prime omega function> counts the retained <prime factors>, while the <total number of prime factors> counts their occurrences before repetitions are removed.

The <radical of an integer> is its largest <squarefree divisor>. Indeed, a <squarefree divisor> chooses at most one copy of each <prime factor>, so it divides $\operatorname{rad}(n)$; the <radical of an integer> itself is a <squarefree divisor>. Consequently,
$$
\operatorname{rad}(n)\mid n,\qquad
\operatorname{rad}(n)\le n,\qquad
\operatorname{rad}(n)=n\ \Longleftrightarrow\ n\text{ is squarefree}.
$$
The last statement follows because every nonzero <prime valuation> is then one. It includes $n=1$, a <squarefree integer>. Applying the <radical of an integer> twice also changes nothing: $\operatorname{rad}(\operatorname{rad}(n))=\operatorname{rad}(n)$.

{title=The <radical of an integer> for $1\le n\le60$: <squarefree integers> lie on the diagonal, and horizontal families share their <prime support of an integer>}

For any <positive integers> $a,b$, the exact <product> rule is
$$
\operatorname{rad}(ab)=\frac{\operatorname{rad}(a)\operatorname{rad}(b)}{\operatorname{rad}(\gcd(a,b))}.
$$
To prove it, a <prime factor> dividing just one input occurs once in the numerator and not in the denominator. A <prime factor> dividing both occurs twice in the numerator and once in the denominator, since it divides their <greatest common divisor>. Each <prime factor> of $ab$ therefore remains exactly once. For <coprime integers>, the denominator is $\operatorname{rad}(1)=1$, proving the <radical of an integer> is a <multiplicative function>. It is not a <completely multiplicative function>: $\operatorname{rad}(4)=2\ne4=\operatorname{rad}(2)^2$.

For a worked example with shared <prime factors>, take $a=12$ and $b=18$. Their <greatest common divisor> is $6$, and $ab=216=2^3\cdot3^3$, so
$$
\operatorname{rad}(216)=\frac{6\cdot6}{6}=6.
$$
This explains why <prime support of an integer> is useful: questions about the presence of <prime factors> can discard their multiplicities. For example, the <squarefree divisors> of $72$ are exactly the <integer divisors> of $\operatorname{rad}(72)=6$, namely $1,2,3,6$. Also $\gcd(m,n)=1$ if and only if $\gcd(m,\operatorname{rad}(n))=1$, because <coprimality> depends only on shared <prime factors>.

A further characterization uses mutual <divisibility> of powers: for <positive integers> $a,b$,
$$
\operatorname{rad}(a)=\operatorname{rad}(b)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{there exist }k,\ell\ge1\text{ with }a\mid b^k\text{ and }b\mid a^\ell.
$$
If their <prime supports of an integer> agree, sufficiently large $k,\ell$ make all the required <prime valuations> large enough. Conversely, these two <divisibility> relations force each <prime factor> of either input to divide the other. For $72$ and $36$, one can take $k=2$ and $\ell=1$: $72\mid36^2$ and $36\mid72$.

= Prime supports of an integer
{synonym}

The <prime support of an integer> $n>0$ is the <finite set> of its distinct <prime factors>. For example, $P(72)=P(36)=\{2,3\}$, $P(30)=\{2,3,5\}$ and $P(1)$ is the <empty set>. Its <cardinality> is the <prime omega function> $\omega(n)$, and the <product> of its elements is the <radical of an integer> $\operatorname{rad}(n)$. These are two different ways to summarize the same <finite set>: a count and a <product>.

= Total number of prime factors
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{title2=$\Omega(n)$}

= Prime omega function with multiplicity
{synonym}

The <total number of prime factors> $\Omega(n)$ counts every occurrence of a <prime factor> in the <prime factorization> of a <positive integer> $n$. Equivalently, it adds the <prime valuations>:
$$
n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Omega(n)=a_1+\cdots+a_r=\sum_{p\mid n}v_p(n).
$$
One may also write $\Omega(n)=\sum_p v_p(n)$ over all <prime numbers>, since only finitely many <prime valuations> are nonzero. The <empty sum> gives $\Omega(1)=0$. By contrast, the <prime omega function> has $\omega(n)=r$ and counts distinct <prime factors>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781, Floor and arithmetic functions, Definition 2.1.2: the two prime-factor counts]{ref}

Expanding $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ into $2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot3$ shows $\Omega(72)=3+2=5$, whereas $\omega(72)=2$ and $\operatorname{rad}(72)=6$. The same calculation for the comparison examples gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
n&\text{prime factorization}&\operatorname{rad}(n)&\omega(n)&\Omega(n)\\\hline
1&\text{empty product}&1&0&0\\
72&2^3\cdot3^2&6&2&5\\
30&2\cdot3\cdot5&30&3&3\\
32&2^5&2&1&5\\
36&2^2\cdot3^2&6&2&4
\end{array}
$$
The <total number of prime factors> agrees for $72$ and $32$, although their <prime supports of an integer> differ. Meanwhile $72$ and $36$ have the same <radical of an integer> and <prime omega function>, but different values of the <total number of prime factors>. These examples show exactly which information each <arithmetic function> forgets.

The <total number of prime factors> is a <completely additive arithmetic function>:
$$
\Omega(ab)=\Omega(a)+\Omega(b)\qquad(a,b\ge1).
$$
The proof is that <multiplication> adds each <prime valuation>, $v_p(ab)=v_p(a)+v_p(b)$, and summing preserves that equality. No <coprimality> condition is needed. For example, $12=2^2\cdot3$ and $18=2\cdot3^2$ both have <total number of prime factors> three; their <product> $216=2^3\cdot3^3$ has six. By comparison, the <prime omega function> is only an <additive arithmetic function>: here $\omega(216)=2\ne4=\omega(12)+\omega(18)$.

There is a useful way to see the <total number of prime factors> as a count of elementary building blocks: any expression of $n$ as a <product> of <prime numbers> has exactly $\Omega(n)$ factors, by the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>. Equivalently, every <factorization tree> for $n>1$ has $\Omega(n)$ leaves. Repeated <prime factors> increase this count without introducing new kinds of <prime number>. The difference is the existing <excess prime-factor multiplicity>:
$$
\Omega(n)-\omega(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}\bigl(v_p(n)-1\bigr)\ge0.
$$
Every summand is nonnegative, so $\Omega(n)=\omega(n)$ if and only if $n$ is a <squarefree integer>, including $n=1$. In particular, a <prime power> $p^k$ with $k\ge1$ has $\Omega(p^k)=k$, $\omega(p^k)=1$ and $\operatorname{rad}(p^k)=p$.

Historically, Hardy and Ramanujan's 1917 paper explicitly separated the <prime omega function> from the <total number of prime factors>, writing them as $f(n)$ and $F(n)$, respectively. Their example $2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$ has counts three and six. That distinction let them compare two measures of how an <integer> is built from <prime factors>. https://ramanujan.sirinudi.org/Volumes/published/ram35.html[G. H. Hardy and S. Ramanujan, The normal number of prime factors of a number n, Quarterly Journal of Mathematics 48 (1917), 76–92, Section 1.2, transcribed original text]{ref}

= Multiplicative arithmetic function
{synonym}

= Multiplicative
{synonym}

An <arithmetic function> $f$ is a <multiplicative function> when $f(1)=1$ and $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ for all <coprime integers> $m,n>0$, meaning their <greatest common divisor> is $1$. The condition $f(1)=1$ excludes the identically zero <function>. This definition makes no assertion about a pair whose <greatest common divisor> exceeds $1$. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Definition 2.2.1]{ref}

For the <divisor function>, the <integer divisors> of $2$ are $1,2$, those of $3$ are $1,3$, and those of $6$ are $1,2,3,6$. Hence
$$
\tau(6)=4=\tau(2)\tau(3)=2\cdot2.
$$
Here $2$ and $3$ are <coprime integers>. In contrast, $\tau(4)=3\ne4=\tau(2)^2$, because the <integer divisors> of $4$ are $1,2,4$. Thus the <divisor function> is a <multiplicative function> but not a <completely multiplicative function>.

The practical gain is that <prime factorization> reduces all values to values at <prime powers>. For distinct <prime numbers> $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ and positive exponents $a_1,\ldots,a_r$,
$$
n=\prod_{k=1}^r p_k^{a_k}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(n)=\prod_{k=1}^r f(p_k^{a_k}).
$$
For instance, $f(12)=f(4)f(3)$ since $4$ and $3$ are <coprime integers>; replacing $f(4)$ by $f(2)^2$ additionally requires a <completely multiplicative function>. The short proof repeatedly applies the defining rule to <prime powers> belonging to different <prime numbers>, which are <coprime integers>.

For an <arithmetic function>, <multiplicativity> means $f(1)=1$ and $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ whenever $m,n$ are positive <coprime integers>. It allows values of an <arithmetic function> to be assembled from its values on <prime powers>, as explained under <multiplicative function>.

= Completely multiplicative
{synonym}

An <arithmetic function> $f$ is a <completely multiplicative function> when $f(1)=1$ and $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ for every pair of <positive integers> $m,n$. In particular $f(p^a)=f(p)^a$ for every <prime number> $p$ and every nonnegative exponent $a$. Both the <constant-one arithmetic function> and the <identity arithmetic function> are <completely multiplicative functions>: $\mathbf1(mn)=1=1\cdot1$ and $\operatorname{id}(mn)=mn=\operatorname{id}(m)\operatorname{id}(n)$. The <divisor function> fails this stronger condition at $m=n=2$.

= Divisor sum
{parent=Multiplicative function}
{title2=$F(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)$}

The <divisor sum> of an <arithmetic function> $f$ assigns to each <positive integer> $n$ the <sum>
$$
F(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d).
$$
Only positive <integer divisors> are included, so $F(1)=f(1)$. The weights $f(d)$ can count the <integer divisors>, measure their sizes, or record other arithmetic information: the <constant-one arithmetic function> gives the <divisor function>, whereas the <identity arithmetic function> gives the <sum-of-divisors function>.

Equivalently, $F=f*\mathbf1$, where $*$ is <Dirichlet convolution> and $\mathbf1$ is the <constant-one arithmetic function>. If $f$ is a <multiplicative function>, then $F$ is a <multiplicative function> by <multiplicativity of Dirichlet convolution>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 notes on floor and arithmetic functions, Proposition 2.4.2 and Theorem 2.6.1]{ref}

Here is the mechanism. For positive <coprime integers> $m,n$, every positive <integer divisor> of $mn$ has a unique form $ab$ with $a\mid m$ and $b\mid n$; moreover, $a,b$ are <coprime integers>. Thus a <multiplicative function> satisfies
$$
F(mn)=\sum_{a\mid m}\sum_{b\mid n}f(a)f(b)=F(m)F(n).
$$
At a <prime power>, the <divisor sum> becomes $F(p^a)=f(1)+f(p)+\cdots+f(p^a)$. This turns a calculation over many <integer divisors> into short local calculations at <prime powers>.

= Sum-of-divisors function
{title2=$\sigma_1(n)$}
{parent=Divisor sum}

= Divisor-sum function
{synonym}

The <sum-of-divisors function> adds all positive <integer divisors> of a <positive integer>, including the number itself:
$$
\sigma(n)=\sigma_1(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d,\qquad \sigma(1)=1.
$$
It is the <divisor sum> of the <identity arithmetic function> $\operatorname{id}(n)=n$, so $\sigma=\operatorname{id}*\mathbf1$ under <Dirichlet convolution>. Both factors are <multiplicative functions>, hence so is the <sum-of-divisors function>. The notation $\sigma_1$ distinguishes it from other weighted <divisor sums>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 notes, Definition 2.1.2 and Proposition 2.2.2(e)]{ref}

For a <prime number> $p$ and a nonnegative exponent $a$, the positive <integer divisors> of the <prime power> $p^a$ are precisely $1,p,\ldots,p^a$. Their <finite geometric series> gives
$$
\sigma(p^a)=1+p+\cdots+p^a=\frac{p^{a+1}-1}{p-1}.
$$
For example, $\sigma(8)=1+2+4+8=15=(2^4-1)/(2-1)$. For a <prime factorization> $n=\prod_{k=1}^r p_k^{a_k}$ with distinct <prime numbers> $p_k$ and positive exponents $a_k$, <multiplicativity> gives
$$
\sigma(n)=\prod_{k=1}^r(1+p_k+\cdots+p_k^{a_k})=\prod_{k=1}^r\frac{p_k^{a_k+1}-1}{p_k-1}.
$$
For $n=1$, this is an <empty product>, equal to $1$. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 notes, Theorem 2.1.8 with k equal to one]{ref}

The <distributive property> makes the formula especially beautiful: choosing one term from each factor produces each positive <integer divisor> exactly once, by the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>. For $12=4\cdot3=2^2\cdot3$, the choices form a three-by-two grid with entries $2^i3^j$, where $0\le i\le2$ and $0\le j\le1$. Adding its rows gives
$$
\sigma(12)=(1+2+4)+(3+6+12)=(1+2+4)(1+3)=7\cdot4=28.
$$
The <divisor function> counts six cells; the <sum-of-divisors function> adds their six values. Once the <prime factorization> is known, the product formula avoids testing every candidate <integer divisor>. Shared <prime factors> require care: $\sigma(4)=7\ne9=\sigma(2)^2$, so the <sum-of-divisors function> is not a <completely multiplicative function>.

Subtracting the number itself gives the <proper-divisor sum> $s(n)=\sigma(n)-n$. Comparing $s(n)$ with $n$ distinguishes <perfect numbers>, <deficient numbers>, and <abundant numbers>. For $6$, $s(6)=1+2+3=6$; for $28$, $s(28)=1+2+4+7+14=28$. Both are <perfect numbers>. The calculation $s(8)=1+2+4=7<8$ makes $8$ a <deficient number>, while $s(12)=1+2+3+4+6=16>12$ makes $12$ an <abundant number>. These comparisons turn the sizes of <integer divisors> into a classification of <positive integers>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/ec-s01-internet-technology-in-local-and-global-communities-spring-2005-summer-2005/aac0b504648aff57a83daec957da3a58_MITEC_S01S05_perf_numb.pdf[Western Carolina University, Challenge Problem: Perfect Numbers, hosted by MIT OpenCourseWare]{ref}

This connection is ancient: <Euclid's Elements>, Book IX, Proposition 36, constructs a <perfect number> by multiplying the last term of $1,2,\ldots,2^{k-1}$ by their <sum>, when that <sum> is a <prime number>. In modern notation, put $q=2^k-1$ and $N=2^{k-1}q$, with $k\ge2$ and $q$ a <prime number>. The <sum-of-divisors function> proves the construction immediately:
$$
\sigma(N)=(2^k-1)(1+q)=q\,2^k=2N.
$$
Consequently the <proper-divisor sum> is $s(N)=N$. Taking $k=2,3$ produces the <perfect numbers> $6,28$. <Euclid's Elements by Euclid/Book IX, Proposition 36>[Euclid, Elements IX.36, translated text and commentary by David Joyce]{ref}

= Granville-Soundararajan distance
{synonym}
{c}

For multiplicative functions bounded by one, the pretentious distance up to $x$ is defined by
$$
\mathbb D(f,g;x)^2
=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1-\Re(f(p)\overline{g(p)})}{p}.
$$
It measures how similarly $f$ and $g$ behave on primes.

= Large Fourier coefficient criterion for pretentiousness
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$|\widehat\nu(t)|\leq C\log X\,e^{-D(f,n^{it};X)^2}$}

Let $X\geq3$, and let $f$ be a normalized <multiplicative arithmetic function> supported on <squarefree integers>, with $|f(p)|\leq1$. Form the <finite measure> $\nu=\sum_n f(n)n^{-1-1/\log X}\delta_{\log n}$ using <Dirac measures>. Under the transform convention $\widehat\nu(t)=\int e^{-itu}\,d\nu(u)$, its <Euler product> is $\prod_p(1+f(p)p^{-1-1/\log X-it})$. Expanding its logarithmic modulus, and replacing the damped <prime> weights by $1/p$ up to $X$ with <Mertens first theorem>, gives the displayed absolute bound, uniformly in real $t$. Hence $|\widehat\nu(t)|\geq\delta\log X$ forces $D(f,n^{it};X)^2\leq\log(C/\delta)$. The distance expression still defines a nonnegative pretentious score for <prime> values bounded by one; its actual <metric> interpretation uses unit-modulus values.

= Pretentious distance between one and the Möbius function
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$D(1,\mu;X)\sim\sqrt{2\log\log X}$}

Because $\mu(p)=-1$, the squared <pretentious distance> from one to the <Möbius function> is twice the reciprocal-prime sum. The <Mertens second theorem> therefore gives $D(1,\mu;X)^2=2\log\log X+2B_1+o(1)$. In particular the distance diverges, illustrating that a multiplicative function can be far from the constant function even though both have unit-modulus <prime> values.

= Prime-restriction metric for pretentious distance
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$D(f,g;X)^2=\tfrac12\sum_{p\leq X}|f(p)-g(p)|^2/p$}

For unit-modulus <prime> values, <pretentious distance> is the Euclidean <metric> on the finite vectors $(f(p)/\sqrt{2p})_{p\leq X}$. On whole <arithmetic functions> it is only a <pseudometric>: it sees neither values on <prime powers> nor <primes> above the cutoff. Even for every cutoff, the functions one and $\mu^2$ have distance zero but differ on nonsquarefree integers. The <norm> representation proves the <triangle inequality> and identifies exactly the quotient on which separation holds.

= Strong aperiodicity of the Möbius function

The Möbius function stays far in <pretentious distance> from every <Archimedean character>. One useful uniform form is
$$
\inf_{|t|\leq2(\log x)^{1/10}}

This follows by estimating the prime sum $\sum_{p\leq x}(1+\cos(t\log p))/p$.

= Pointwise product of arithmetic functions

The <pointwise product of arithmetic functions> multiplies two <arithmetic functions> at the same input: $(f\cdot g)(n)=f(n)g(n)$. = Dirichlet convolution
{c}
{title2=$*$}

{wiki}

The <Dirichlet convolution> of <arithmetic functions> $f,g$ is the <arithmetic function>
$$
(f*g)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)g(n/d)=\sum_{\substack{a,b>0\\ab=n}}f(a)g(b),\qquad n>0.
$$
In the first sum, $d$ runs over the positive <integer divisors> of $n$; the second uses <ordered pairs> of <positive integers> whose product is $n$. Both sums are finite. Each pair contributes one weight from $f$ and one from $g$, so <Dirichlet convolution> combines information attached to complementary factors. It turns a <divisor sum> into an operation on <arithmetic functions>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Sections 2.3 and 2.4: Dirichlet convolution and examples]{ref}

For $n=12$ the six <ordered pairs> are $(1,12),(2,6),(3,4),(4,3),(6,2),(12,1)$. The <constant-one arithmetic function> gives one per pair, while the <identity arithmetic function> weights the first factor by its size:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau&=\mathbf1*\mathbf1,&\tau(12)&=1+1+1+1+1+1=6,\\
\sigma_1&=\operatorname{id}*\mathbf1,&\sigma_1(12)&=1+2+3+4+6+12=28.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\tau$ is the <divisor function> and $\sigma_1$ is the <sum-of-divisors function>. By comparison, the <pointwise product of arithmetic functions> gives $(\mathbf1\cdot\mathbf1)(12)=1$ and $(\operatorname{id}\cdot\mathbf1)(12)=12$.

<Commutativity> follows by swapping the two entries of every <ordered pair>. <Associativity> follows by reindexing either grouping over the same ordered <tuples> of three <positive integers>:
$$
((f*g)*h)(n)=\sum_{\substack{a,b,c>0\\abc=n}}f(a)g(b)h(c)=(f*(g*h))(n).
$$
For example, $((\mathbf1*\mathbf1)*\mathbf1)(12)$ counts ordered <tuples> of three <positive integers> with product $12$. Distribute the two occurrences of the <prime factor> $2$ among three entries in $\binom{2+2}{2}=6$ ways, and the one occurrence of the <prime factor> $3$ in $\binom{1+2}{2}=3$ ways. These <binomial coefficients> are the <stars and bars (combinatorics)> counts of exponent choices in the <nonnegative integers>; <prime factorization> therefore gives $6\cdot3=18$ such <tuples>. Directly, the same <Dirichlet convolution> is $\sum_{d\mid12}\tau(d)=1+2+2+3+4+6=18$.

The <identity for Dirichlet convolution> is $\varepsilon$, and the <Möbius function> reverses a <divisor sum> because $\mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon$. This makes <Dirichlet convolution> useful both for counting factorizations and for recovering an <arithmetic function> from its <divisor sum>.

= Multiplicativity of Dirichlet convolution
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}
{title2=$f,g\ \text{multiplicative}\ \Longrightarrow\ f*g\ \text{multiplicative}$}

The <Dirichlet convolution> of two <multiplicative functions> is a <multiplicative function>. For positive <coprime integers> $m,n$, every positive <integer divisor> of $mn$ has a unique form $de$ with $d\mid m$ and $e\mid n$, by <prime factorization>. The pairs $d,e$ and $m/d,n/e$ are each <coprime integers>, so the defining rules for the two <multiplicative functions> separate the sum:
$$
(f*g)(mn)=\sum_{d\mid m}\sum_{e\mid n}f(d)f(e)g(m/d)g(n/e)=(f*g)(m)(f*g)(n).
$$
Also $(f*g)(1)=f(1)g(1)=1$. This short argument proves <multiplicativity> of both the <divisor function> $\mathbf1*\mathbf1$ and the <sum-of-divisors function> $\operatorname{id}*\mathbf1$. The result need not be a <completely multiplicative function>, even when both inputs are: $\mathbf1*\mathbf1=\tau$ fails at $2\cdot2$. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Theorem 2.6.1]{ref}

= Constant-one arithmetic function
{title2=$\mathbf 1$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The <constant-one arithmetic function> has $\mathbf1(n)=1$ for every <positive integer> $n$, including $\mathbf1(1)=\mathbf1(12)=1$. It is a <completely multiplicative function>. Its <Dirichlet convolution> with an <arithmetic function> $f$ is the <divisor sum> $(\mathbf1*f)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)$. Thus $\mathbf1*\mathbf1=\tau$, the <divisor function>. Its <pointwise product of arithmetic functions> with $f$ is $f$ itself; its <Dirichlet convolution> with $f$ generally is not. The <identity for Dirichlet convolution> is instead $\varepsilon$.

= Identity arithmetic function
{title2=$\operatorname{id}$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The <identity arithmetic function> is $\operatorname{id}(n)=n$ on the <positive integers>, for example $\operatorname{id}(1)=1$ and $\operatorname{id}(12)=12$. It is a <completely multiplicative function> and gives the <sum-of-divisors function> through $\operatorname{id}*\mathbf1=\sigma_1$ under <Dirichlet convolution>. It is distinct from the <identity for Dirichlet convolution> $\varepsilon$. The identity $\operatorname{id}=\mathbf1*\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is <Euler's totient function>, is equivalent to $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$.

= Identity for Dirichlet convolution
{c}
{title2=$\varepsilon$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The <identity for Dirichlet convolution> is the <arithmetic function>
$$
\varepsilon(n)=\begin{cases}1&n=1,\\0&n>1.\end{cases}
$$
For every <arithmetic function> $f$, it satisfies $f*\varepsilon=\varepsilon*f=f$. Indeed, in $(f*\varepsilon)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)\varepsilon(n/d)$, only $d=n$ survives. For example,
$$
(f*\varepsilon)(12)=f(1)0+f(2)0+f(3)0+f(4)0+f(6)0+f(12)1=f(12).
$$
At $n=1$ the sole term is $f(1)\varepsilon(1)=f(1)$. This gives a useful way to check <Dirichlet convolution> calculations: inserting $\varepsilon$ leaves every value unchanged. The three <arithmetic functions> $\mathbf1$, $\operatorname{id}$, and $\varepsilon$ all take value $1$ at $1$, but at $12$ the <constant-one arithmetic function>, the <identity arithmetic function>, and the <identity for Dirichlet convolution> take values $1$, $12$, and $0$, respectively. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Theorem 2.3.4]{ref}

The <Möbius function> is the inverse of the <constant-one arithmetic function> under <Dirichlet convolution>, meaning $\mu*\mathbf1=\mathbf1*\mu=\varepsilon$. The <Möbius divisor-sum identity> gives a short proof: for $n>1$ the nonzero terms expand $\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1)=0$, while at $1$ the sum is $\mu(1)=1$. At $12$, the computation is
$$
(\mu*\mathbf1)(12)=\mu(1)+\mu(2)+\mu(3)+\mu(4)+\mu(6)+\mu(12)=1-1-1+0+1+0=0=\varepsilon(12).
$$
The zeros occur because $4$ and $12$ are divisible by the square of the <prime number> $2$. Consequently, if $F=f*\mathbf1$ is a <divisor sum>, then <associativity> gives $F*\mu=f*(\mathbf1*\mu)=f*\varepsilon=f$: this is <Möbius inversion>.

= Dirichlet hyperbola method
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}
{wiki}

The <Dirichlet hyperbola method> turns a long <summation> over a <Dirichlet convolution> into two short ones by covering the <lattice points> below a <hyperbola> with two overlapping strips. Its simplest application is an exact count: for a <positive integer> $N$, every positive <integer divisor> $a$ of $n\le N$ corresponds to the <ordered pair> $(a,n/a)$. Thus the <divisor summatory function> counts precisely the <ordered pairs> of <positive integers> with $ab\le N$:
$$
D(N)=\sum_{n=1}^N\tau(n)
=\#\{(a,b)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^2:ab\le N\}
=\sum_{a=1}^N\left\lfloor\frac Na\right\rfloor.
$$
Here $\tau$ is the <divisor function>, and the <floor function> gives the number of allowed values of $b$ for each $a$. The axes are excluded, while points on $ab=N$ are included.

Set $r=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, using the <floor function> and the positive <square root>. **Every counted pair has $a\le r$ or $b\le r$.** Otherwise $ab\ge(r+1)^2>N$. The first strip contains $\sum_{a=1}^r\lfloor N/a\rfloor$ points; swapping the two coordinates gives the same count for the second. Their overlap contains all $r^2$ pairs with $1\le a,b\le r$, since $r^2\le N$. The <inclusion-exclusion principle> therefore gives the exact identity
$$
\boxed{D(N)=2\sum_{a=1}^r\left\lfloor\frac Na\right\rfloor-r^2,
\qquad r=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor.}
$$
The subtraction is for the whole overlap square, rather than only the diagonal. The same <Dirichlet hyperbola method> identity holds for real $x\ge1$, with $D(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)$ and $r=\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor$. <Multiplicative number theory: The pretentious approach by Andrew Granville and K. Soundararajan>[Andrew Granville and K. Soundararajan, Multiplicative number theory: The pretentious approach, §1.1.4, pp. 7–8: divisor pairing and the hyperbola split]{ref}

= Divisor summatory function
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}
{title2=$D(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)$}
{wiki}

The <divisor summatory function> adds the <divisor function> up to a cutoff $x\ge1$. It counts all <ordered pairs> of <positive integers> whose product is at most $x$. For $N=12$, the twelve individual <divisor function> values are
$$
(\tau(1),\ldots,\tau(12))=(1,2,2,3,2,4,2,4,3,4,2,6),
\qquad D(12)=35.
$$
Counting columns of <lattice points> instead gives $12+6+4+3+2+2+1+1+1+1+1+1=35$. The <Dirichlet hyperbola method> needs only the first three column counts:
$$
D(12)=2(12+6+4)-3^2=44-9=35.
$$
The <arithmetic work of the Dirichlet hyperbola method> is much smaller even though the answer is identical.

This is useful for elementary averages: individual <divisor function> values fluctuate, but their <summation> has the regular behavior given by the <divisor summatory asymptotic>. Dividing that estimate by a <positive integer> $N$ gives
$$
\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\tau(n)
=\log N+2\gamma-1+O(N^{-1/2})\qquad(N\longrightarrow\infty),
$$
where $\log$ is the <natural logarithm>, $\gamma$ is the <Euler-Mascheroni constant>, and $O$ uses <big O notation>. This describes an average, rather than an estimate for every individual <divisor function> value. <Multiplicative number theory: The pretentious approach by Andrew Granville and K. Soundararajan>[§1.1.4, p. 8, equation (1.1.13): the divisor summatory asymptotic]{ref}

= Asymmetric Dirichlet hyperbola split
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}
{title2=$AB=N$}

A balanced <Dirichlet hyperbola method> split takes real thresholds $A=B=\sqrt N$. An asymmetric split takes $1\le A\le N$ and $B=N/A$. Every <lattice point> with $ab\le N$ then has $a\le A$ or $b\le B$, and every point in their overlap rectangle is allowed because $AB=N$. For <arithmetic functions> $f,g$, define their <partial sums> by $F(t)=\sum_{a\le t}f(a)$ and $G(t)=\sum_{b\le t}g(b)$, always over <positive integers>. The same <inclusion-exclusion principle>, now with weight $f(a)g(b)$, yields
$$
\sum_{n\le N}(f*g)(n)
=\sum_{a\le A}f(a)G(N/a)
+\sum_{b\le B}g(b)F(N/b)-F(A)G(B),
\qquad AB=N.
$$
No <multiplicativity> assumption is needed: this is a finite rearrangement of <Dirichlet convolution>. The <constant-one arithmetic function> in both positions recovers the <divisor summatory function>. For $N=12$, $A=2$, $B=6$ gives
$$
D(12)=(12+6)+(12+6+4+3+2+2)-2\cdot6=18+29-12=35.
$$
It uses two columns and six rows, with a twelve-point overlap.

For general positive real thresholds, **$AB\ge N$ is sufficient for coverage**, since $a>A$ and $b>B$ would imply $ab>AB\ge N$. If $AB>N$, however, the overlap can include points above the <hyperbola>. The correct subtraction is then $\sum_{a\le A,\ b\le B,\ ab\le N}f(a)g(b)$; the product $F(A)G(B)$ is justified only when the whole integer rectangle lies below it. For example, $N=12$, $A=B=4$ covers every allowed pair, but its rectangle contains the forbidden pair $(4,4)$, so subtracting all sixteen points would give $34$ instead of $35$.

Conversely, the balanced integer thresholds $r,r$ have $r^2\le N$ and still cover every allowed pair because $(r+1)^2>N$. More generally, positive integer thresholds $U,V$ give coverage exactly when $(U+1)(V+1)>N$, and their overlap is a full rectangle exactly when $UV\le N$. These discrete conditions explain why the square-root identity works even when $r^2<N$.

= Arithmetic work of the Dirichlet hyperbola method
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}

The direct <divisor summatory function> formula evaluates $N$ floor divisions. Its balanced <Dirichlet hyperbola method> formula evaluates $r=\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ floor divisions and reuses their <sum> for the other strip, followed by doubling and square-overlap subtraction. Thus it uses $O(\sqrt N)$ <arithmetic operations>, with <big O notation>, in a model that counts each division, addition and multiplication as one operation. At $N=10^6$, the division counts for these two formulas are $10^6$ and $10^3$.

\Image[dirichlet-hyperbola-work-and-partial-sums.png]
{title=The balanced <Dirichlet hyperbola method> reduces floor divisions from $N$ to $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$; for $N=12$, two sequences of <partial sums> reach the same exact answer 35}

For a general <Dirichlet convolution>, the asymmetric formula has $\lfloor A\rfloor+\lfloor B\rfloor$ outer terms; evaluating $F$ and $G$ has an additional cost. Under $AB=N$, equal thresholds minimize $A+B$, but an asymmetric split can be useful if the two <partial sums> have different evaluation costs. The balanced <divisor summatory function> gains the further reuse because its two <arithmetic functions> are identical.

These are <arithmetic operation> counts, rather than counts of elementary operations on <binary digits>: the <integers> grow with $N$, and division of longer <integers> costs more in that model of <computational complexity theory>. Computing $r$ also requires an exact integer <square root>, or checking and correcting a numerical <square root> until $r^2\le N<(r+1)^2$ holds.

= Divisor summatory asymptotic
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}
{title2=$\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt x)$}

The <Dirichlet hyperbola method> gives $D(x)=2\sum_{a\le r}\lfloor x/a\rfloor-r^2$, where $r=\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor$. The <harmonic number> estimate $H_r=\log r+\gamma+O(1/r)$, with $\gamma$ the <Euler's constant>, yields $D(x)=2x(\log r+\gamma)-r^2+O(x/r+r)$. Substitution of $r=\sqrt x+O(1)$ gives the displayed <asymptotic expansion>. Comparison of $\sum_{n\le x}\log n$ with its integral consequently gives $\sum_{n\le N}(\tau(n)-\log n-2\gamma)=O(\sqrt N)$ for integer $N$.

= Harmonic divisor sum
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}

Write $B(t)=\sum_{n\leq t}\tau(n)/n$ and $r=\lfloor\sqrt t\rfloor$. The weighted <Dirichlet hyperbola method> gives
$$
B(t)=2\sum_{a\leq r}\frac{H_{\lfloor t/a\rfloor}}a-H_r^2
=\tfrac12\log^2t+2\gamma\log t+c+O\left(\frac{\log(2t)}{\sqrt t}\right).
$$
Indeed, use $H_{\lfloor u\rfloor}=\log u+\gamma+O(1/u)$ and $\sum_{a\leq r}(\log a)/a=\tfrac12\log^2r+c\prime+O(\log(2r)/r)$. The latter follows by comparing each summand with the integral on its unit interval: the derivative of $(\log t)/t$ has an integrable tail. Since $r=\sqrt t+O(1)$, substitution yields the expansion. Here $H_n$ is the <harmonic number> and $\gamma$ the <Euler-Mascheroni constant>.

= Divisor-counting function

The <divisor function> $\tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1$ counts the positive <integer divisors> of a <positive integer> $n$. In particular $\tau(1)=1$, because $1$ has just one positive <integer divisor>. More generally the notation $\sigma_k(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^k$ gives $\tau=\sigma_0$ and the <sum-of-divisors function> $\sigma_1$. Thus the <divisor function> counts the positive <integer divisors>, while $\sigma_1$ adds their sizes. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Definition 2.1.2 and Theorem 2.1.6]{ref}

If the <prime factorization> is $n=\prod_{k=1}^r p_k^{a_k}$ with distinct <prime numbers> $p_k$ and positive exponents $a_k$, then
$$
\tau(n)=\prod_{k=1}^r(a_k+1).
$$
The short proof is a count: each positive <integer divisor> has a unique form $\prod_{k=1}^r p_k^{b_k}$, where $0\le b_k\le a_k$. There are $a_k+1$ choices at each <prime number>, and choosing them gives every positive <integer divisor> exactly once. For $n=1$ there are no <prime factors> and the product is $1$.

For $12=2^2\cdot3$, choose $i\in\{0,1,2\}$ and $j\in\{0,1\}$ to obtain $2^i3^j$. The six values are $1,2,4,3,6,12$; sorted, the positive <integer divisors> are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Therefore $\tau(12)=(2+1)(1+1)=6$. The diagram makes the independent exponent choices visible.

This count is useful because a known <prime factorization> gives $\tau(n)$ without testing every smaller <positive integer> for <divisibility>. It also proves the <divisor function> is a <multiplicative function>: <coprime integers> have disjoint <prime factors>, so their exponent choices combine. For example $\tau(6)=4=\tau(2)\tau(3)$, but $\tau(4)=3\ne4=\tau(2)^2$, so it is not a <completely multiplicative function>. Equivalently, $\tau=\mathbf1*\mathbf1$ under <Dirichlet convolution>, counting <ordered pairs> of <positive integers> whose product is $n$.

= Von Mangoldt function
{c}
{title2=$\Lambda(n)$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The <Von Mangoldt function> assigns a <natural logarithm> weight to each positive power of a <prime number>. For a <positive integer> $n$,
$$
\Lambda(n)=\begin{cases}
\log p,&n=p^k,\quad p\text{ prime},\ k\geq1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
Here and throughout the <Chebyshev functions>, **$\log$ means the <natural logarithm>**. In particular, $\Lambda(1)=0$: the exponent must be positive, even though the convention in <prime power> allows exponent zero. The <support of a function> of $\Lambda$ therefore consists precisely of the <prime powers> greater than one. <254A – analytic prime number theory/254A, Notes 1: Elementary multiplicative number theory>{ref}

For example, $\Lambda(2)=\Lambda(4)=\Lambda(8)=\log2$ and $\Lambda(3)=\Lambda(9)=\log3$, whereas $\Lambda(6)=\Lambda(12)=0$. A <composite number> can have a nonzero weight: $8$ has just one distinct <prime factor>. But $12=2^2\cdot3$ has two distinct <prime factors>, so its weight is zero. At $p^k$ the weight is **$\log p$, not $\log(p^k)=k\log p$**. This choice lets successive powers record successive copies of the same <prime factor> in the <Von Mangoldt divisor identity>.

The <Von Mangoldt function> is useful because it connects two views of the <prime numbers>: the <prime factorization> of an individual <integer>, through a <divisor sum>, and the accumulated weights below a cutoff, through the <second Chebyshev function>. The latter turns questions about the distribution of <prime numbers> into questions about a <partial sum> of an <arithmetic function>.

= Selberg symmetry formula
{parent=Von Mangoldt function}
{c}

The elementary Selberg symmetry formula can be written
$$
\psi(x)+\frac1{\log x}\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\psi(x/n)=2x+O\left(\frac{x}{\log x}\right).
$$
Use the weights $\lambda_d=\mu(d)\log(x/d)/\log x$. Their <divisor sum> is $1_{n=1}+\Lambda(n)/\log x$. Apply them to the <integrated Chebyshev sum>, then use the <Möbius harmonic logarithmic moments>. The <Chebyshev estimate> shows this is equivalent, with an $O(x)$ error, to $\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\log n+\sum_{mn\leq x}\Lambda(m)\Lambda(n)=2x\log x+O(x)$.

= Von Mangoldt divisor identity
{c}
{parent=Von Mangoldt function}
{title2=$\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d)=\log n$}

For every <positive integer> $n$, the <divisor sum> of its <Von Mangoldt function> weights recovers its <natural logarithm>:
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d)=\log n.
$$
The sum runs over the positive <integer divisors> of $n$. For $n=1$, its only term is $\Lambda(1)=0=\log1$. Equivalently, if $L(n)=\log n$ and $\mathbf1$ is the <constant-one arithmetic function>, then $\mathbf1*\Lambda=L$, where $*$ is <Dirichlet convolution>. <254A – analytic prime number theory/254A, Notes 1: Elementary multiplicative number theory>{ref}

For $12=2^2\cdot3$, the positive <integer divisors> are $1,2,3,4,6,12$. Only $2,3,4$ contribute:
$$
\Lambda(1)+\Lambda(2)+\Lambda(3)+\Lambda(4)+\Lambda(6)+\Lambda(12)
=0+\log2+\log3+\log2+0+0
=\log12.
$$
Thus the vanishing of $\Lambda(12)$ loses no information here: the <divisor sum> retrieves both copies of the <prime factor> $2$ from the <integer divisors> $2$ and $4$.

Proof outline: by the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>, write $n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}$ with distinct <prime numbers> $p_i$ and positive exponents $a_i$. Among the positive <integer divisors>, the ones with nonzero <Von Mangoldt function> weights are exactly $p_i,p_i^2,\ldots,p_i^{a_i}$. Consequently
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d)=\sum_{i=1}^r\sum_{j=1}^{a_i}\log p_i
=\sum_{i=1}^r a_i\log p_i
=\log\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}\right)=\log n.
$$
The <natural logarithm> turns the <prime factorization> product into a sum, and the <prime powers> greater than one supply exactly the required multiplicities.

= Chebyshev function
{c}
{parent=Von Mangoldt function}
{wiki}
{title2=$\vartheta(x),\ \psi(x)$}

The two <Chebyshev functions> measure the total <natural logarithm> weight of <prime numbers> or of <prime powers> greater than one below a real cutoff $x\geq0$:
$$
\vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p,
\qquad
\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)
=\sum_{\substack{p^k\leq x\\k\geq1}}\log p.
$$
Here $p$ ranges over <prime numbers>, $n$ over <positive integers>, and $k$ over <positive integers>. The <Chebyshev theta function> $\vartheta$, also written $\theta$, weights each <prime number> once. The <second Chebyshev function> $\psi$ weights every positive power of each <prime number> separately. Both vanish for $0\leq x<2$; the inclusive cutoff makes them <right-continuous functions> and <nondecreasing functions>, constant between their jumps. <Elementary Number Theory/7.2: Chebyshev's Functions>{ref}

At $x=10$, the <prime numbers> are $2,3,5,7$, while the extra <prime powers> greater than one are $4,8,9$. Exactly,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\vartheta(10)&=\log2+\log3+\log5+\log7=\log210,\\
\psi(10)&=3\log2+2\log3+\log5+\log7=\log2520,\\
\psi(10)-\vartheta(10)&=2\log2+\log3=\log12.
\end{aligned}
$$
At each <prime number> $p$, both <Chebyshev functions> jump by $\log p$. At a <prime power> $p^k$ with $k\geq2$, only the <second Chebyshev function> jumps, by $\log p$. At $6$ and $10$ neither jumps. Notice that the gap is already positive at $5$ and $7$: a shared jump does not remove the earlier contribution from $4$.

Grouping the <prime powers> by their exponent gives, for $x\geq2$,
$$
\psi(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log x/\log2\rfloor}\vartheta(x^{1/k}),
\qquad
\psi(x)-\vartheta(x)=\sum_{k=2}^{\lfloor\log x/\log2\rfloor}\vartheta(x^{1/k}).
$$
The <floor function> cutoff is exact: $p\geq2$ forces $2^k\leq x$ whenever $p^k\leq x$. For each exponent, $p^k\leq x$ is equivalent to $p\leq x^{1/k}$. An upper cutoff smaller than the lower one means an empty sum. <Elementary Number Theory/7.2: Chebyshev's Functions>{ref}

= Second Chebyshev function
{title2=$\psi(x)$}
{c}
{parent=Chebyshev function}

The <second Chebyshev function> is the <partial sum> of the <Von Mangoldt function> up to a real cutoff $x\geq0$:
$$
\psi(x)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\Lambda(n).
$$
For example, $\psi(3)=\log6$, $\psi(4)=\log12$, and $\psi(4.5)=\log12$. At $4=2^2$ the jump has size $\log2$; between $4$ and $5$ no new <prime power> greater than one enters the sum. In contrast, the <Chebyshev theta function> stays at $\vartheta(4)=\log6$.

There is also an exact connection with the <least common multiple>. For $N=\lfloor x\rfloor\geq1$, each <prime number> $p\leq N$ occurs to the highest exponent $k$ for which $p^k\leq N$, both in that <least common multiple> and in the accumulated <Von Mangoldt function> weights. Thus
$$
\psi(x)=\log\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,N).
$$
This explains the $\log2520$ value at $x=10$: the <least common multiple> is $2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5\cdot7$. The <Chebyshev theta function> instead gives the <natural logarithm> of the <primorial> $2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=210$. The additional <prime powers> retain the highest required multiplicities.

Why is this useful for the distribution of <prime numbers>? The <prime number theorem> has the equivalent forms
$$
\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x},\qquad\vartheta(x)\sim x,\qquad\psi(x)\sim x
\quad(x\longrightarrow\infty),
$$
where $\pi$ is the <prime-counting function> and $\sim$ denotes <asymptotic equivalence>. Thus an <arithmetic function> defined using <prime factorization> has average weight tending to one over long initial intervals of the <positive integers>. These are substantive <asymptotic equivalences>, not consequences of the definitions or the <Von Mangoldt divisor identity> alone. <254A – analytic prime number theory/254A, Notes 2: Complex-analytic multiplicative number theory>{ref}

The elementary reason the two <Chebyshev functions> have the same leading behavior is that their difference is small compared with $x$. Every additional <prime power> has base $p\leq\sqrt x$, and for each such <prime number> its total extra weight is at most $\log x$. There are at most $\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor$ such bases, so
$$
0\leq\psi(x)-\vartheta(x)\leq\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor\log x,
\qquad
\frac{\psi(x)-\vartheta(x)}x\longrightarrow0.
$$
This transfers $\psi(x)\sim x$ to $\vartheta(x)\sim x$, or conversely, once either is established. <Partial summation> then relates the <Chebyshev theta function> to the <prime-counting function>. The estimate controls the extra <prime powers>; proving the leading value itself is the harder <prime number theorem> problem.

The extra denominator makes this smoothing useful: the resulting sum over <nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function> is absolutely convergent. This integral differs from the <integrated Chebyshev sum>, which denotes a sum of the values $\psi(x/n)$.

{parent=First integral of the Chebyshev function}

Zeros are included with multiplicity. The <Riemann–von Mangoldt formula> makes the zero sum absolutely convergent. {c}

= Prime omega function
{title2=$\omega(n)$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The <prime omega function> $\omega(n)$ counts the distinct <prime factors> of a <positive integer> $n$, so it is the <cardinality> of the <prime support of an integer> $P(n)$. For a <prime factorization> with distinct <prime numbers> $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ and positive exponents,
$$
n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\omega(n)=r=\sum_{p\mid n}1.
$$
At $n=1$ there are no <prime factors>, so the <empty sum> gives $\omega(1)=0$. The <total number of prime factors> instead gives $\Omega(n)=\sum_i a_i$. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781, Floor and arithmetic functions, Definition 2.1.2: distinct and repeated prime factors]{ref}

For $72=2^3\cdot3^2$, the <prime support of an integer> is $\{2,3\}$ and $\omega(72)=2$. The comparison examples give $\omega(30)=3$, $\omega(32)=1$ and $\omega(36)=2$. Raising an existing <prime factor> to a higher power does not affect the <prime omega function>: $\omega(p^k)=1$ for a <prime number> $p$ and $k\ge1$. Thus the <prime omega function> is a <strongly additive arithmetic function>, with
$$
\omega(ab)=\omega(a)+\omega(b)\quad\text{if }\gcd(a,b)=1.
$$
The reason is that <coprime integers> have disjoint <prime supports of an integer>. For arbitrary <positive integers>, their <prime support of an integer> combines by <set union>, and the shared <prime factors> are exactly those of the <greatest common divisor>. Counting this <set union> gives the more general identity
$$
\omega(ab)=\omega(a)+\omega(b)-\omega(\gcd(a,b)).
$$
For $a=12$, $b=18$, both <prime supports of an integer> are $\{2,3\}$ and $\gcd(a,b)=6$, so $\omega(216)=2+2-2=2$. In particular $\omega(4)=1\ne2=\omega(2)+\omega(2)$: the <prime omega function> is not a <completely additive arithmetic function>.

The <prime omega function> is useful for counting choices involving distinct <prime factors>. Every <squarefree divisor> of $n$ chooses a <subset> of its <prime support of an integer>, so there are $2^{\omega(n)}$ <squarefree divisors>. For $72$, this gives $2^2=4$, namely $1,2,3,6$. Also $\omega(\operatorname{rad}(n))=\omega(n)$, because the <radical of an integer> keeps exactly that <prime support of an integer>. The <total number of prime factors> records what this count discards, and the two counts agree exactly on <squarefree integers>.

The <Euler product> and <Mertens second theorem> give $\sum_{n\leq D}3^{\omega(n)}/n\ll\log^3D$. The <log-weighted convolution bound for three to the prime omega>, with the <Chebyshev estimate> $\psi(y)\ll y$, then gives $\sum_{\sqrt D\leq n\leq D}3^{\omega(n)}\ll D\log^2D$. $$

If $p^a\Vert n$, its contribution to $(f*\Lambda)(n)$ is $f(n)(a-1+1/3)\log p$. Since $3(a-1+1/3)\geq a$, summing over the <prime factors> proves the bound. Here $*$ is <Dirichlet convolution> and $\Lambda$ is the <Von Mangoldt function>.

= Möbius function
{c}
{title2=$\mu(n)$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The <Möbius function> assigns a cancellation weight to each <positive integer>:
$$
\mu(n)=\begin{cases}
1&n=1,\\
(-1)^r&n=p_1\cdots p_r\text{ with distinct primes }p_1,\ldots,p_r,\\
0&p^2\mid n\text{ for some prime }p.
\end{cases}
$$
Thus a <squarefree integer> contributes a sign determined by its number of distinct <prime factors>; a repeated <prime factor> makes the weight zero. This packages the alternating signs of <inclusion-exclusion> into an <arithmetic function>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Definition 2.1.2]{ref}

For example, $\mu(2)=\mu(3)=-1$, $\mu(6)=1$, and $\mu(30)=(-1)^3=-1$, since $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$. In contrast, $\mu(4)=\mu(12)=\mu(60)=0$: all three have the square of the <prime number> $2$ as a <divisor>. On <prime powers>, $\mu(p)=-1$ and $\mu(p^a)=0$ for $a\geq2$.

The <Möbius function> is a <multiplicative function>: for <coprime integers>, their <prime factors> are disjoint, so either a squared <prime factor> forces both sides of $\mu(mn)=\mu(m)\mu(n)$ to vanish, or the counts of distinct <prime factors> add and their signs multiply. It is not a <completely multiplicative function>, since $\mu(4)=0\ne1=\mu(2)^2$. Its <Möbius divisor-sum identity> makes it the inverse of the <constant-one arithmetic function> under <Dirichlet convolution>: $\mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon$, the <identity for Dirichlet convolution>. This is why the small set of weights $-1,0,1$ can undo an entire <divisor sum>.

= Möbius divisor-sum identity
{c}
{parent=Möbius function}
{title2=$\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=1_{n=1}$}

For every <positive integer> $n$, the <divisor sum> of the <Möbius function> is
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\begin{cases}1&n=1,\\0&n>1.\end{cases}
$$
The sum includes all positive <integer divisors>, but only <squarefree divisors> contribute. Equivalently, $\mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon$ under <Dirichlet convolution>, where $\mathbf1$ is the <constant-one arithmetic function> and $\varepsilon$ is the <identity for Dirichlet convolution>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Propositions 2.4.6 and 2.4.7]{ref}

Each <squarefree divisor> chooses a <subset> of the distinct <prime factors> of $n$, contributing $(-1)^r$ when that <subset> has $r$ elements. Expanding a product therefore gives
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1).
$$
For $n>1$ at least one <prime factor> occurs, so the product is zero. At $n=1$ it is an <empty product>, equal to one. This is the same alternating cancellation that drives <inclusion-exclusion>.

For $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$, all eight positive <integer divisors> are <squarefree integers>, giving
$$
\mu(1)+\mu(2)+\mu(3)+\mu(5)+\mu(6)+\mu(10)+\mu(15)+\mu(30)
=1-1-1-1+1+1+1-1=0.
$$
Pair each <subset> not containing $2$ with the <subset> obtained by inserting $2$: $(1,2),(3,6),(5,10),(15,30)$. Each pair cancels. The same nonzero terms occur for $60$, because its <squarefree divisors> are still the <integer divisors> of $30$; its other <integer divisors> have <Möbius function> value zero.

= Möbius inversion
{c}
{synonym}

<Möbius inversion> recovers an <arithmetic function> from its <divisor sum>. For <arithmetic functions> $f,g$, the following statements are equivalent, with each equality required for every <positive integer> $n$:
$$
g(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)g(n/d).
$$
Here $d$ ranges over positive <integer divisors> of $n$ and $\mu$ is the <Möbius function>. No <multiplicativity> assumption is needed, and both sums are finite. The right side can also be written $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)g(d)$ by exchanging complementary <integer divisors>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Theorem 2.5.1 and Proposition 2.5.2]{ref}

The <divisor sum> $g(n)$ bundles $f(n)$ together with values at smaller <integer divisors>. <Möbius inversion> subtracts aggregates indexed by one distinct <prime factor>, adds those indexed by two, and continues with the alternating signs of <inclusion-exclusion>. A <divisor> containing a squared <prime factor> receives weight zero. The <Möbius divisor-sum identity> ensures that every unwanted contribution cancels exactly.

In <Dirichlet convolution> notation, $g=f*\mathbf1$ and $\mu*\mathbf1=\varepsilon$, where $\mathbf1$ is the <constant-one arithmetic function> and $\varepsilon$ is the <identity for Dirichlet convolution>. <Associativity> and <commutativity> give the short proof:
$$
\mu*g=\mu*(\mathbf1*f)=(\mu*\mathbf1)*f=\varepsilon*f=f.
$$
Convolving $f=\mu*g$ with $\mathbf1$ proves the reverse implication. At $n=1$, the formula reduces to $f(1)=g(1)$ because $\mu(1)=1$.

For a worked example, take $f(n)=\varphi(n)$, <Euler's totient function>. The <totient divisor-sum identity> says that $g(n)=n$, the <identity arithmetic function>:
$$
g(12)=\varphi(1)+\varphi(2)+\varphi(3)+\varphi(4)+\varphi(6)+\varphi(12)
=1+1+2+2+2+4=12.
$$
This identity comes from classifying the <integers> $1,\ldots,n$ by their <greatest common divisor> with $n$: the group with $\gcd(j,n)=h$ has $\varphi(n/h)$ members, since $j/h$ and $n/h$ are <coprime integers>. Summing the group sizes gives $n$, as detailed under <totient divisor-sum identity>.

Now undo this aggregation with the <Möbius function>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(12)&=\mu(1)12+\mu(2)6+\mu(3)4+\mu(4)3+\mu(6)2+\mu(12)1\\
&=12-6-4+0+2+0=4.
\end{aligned}
$$
The nonzero terms use $d=1,2,3,6$; $d=4,12$ vanish because they contain the square of the <prime number> $2$. The four surviving <integers> <coprime> to $12$ are $1,5,7,11$.

\Image[mobius-inversion-totient.png]
{title=A <divisor sum> aggregates <Euler's totient function> values to 12, while <Möbius inversion> recovers the four <integers> <coprime> to 12}

This example also reveals why <Möbius inversion> is useful: it turns an easy count of all objects into a count satisfying a <coprimality> restriction. Expanding over <subsets> of distinct <prime factors> gives
$$
\varphi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\frac nd
=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right).
$$
The product is <inclusion-exclusion> over multiples of the <prime factors>. For $12$, it gives $12(1-1/2)(1-1/3)=4$. More generally, whenever a counting problem produces a <divisor sum>, <Möbius inversion> lets that aggregate be reversed without solving a new system of <equations>.

= Prime number asymptotic from Mertens cancellation
{parent=Mertens function}
{title2=$M(x)=o(x)\Longrightarrow\psi(x)=x+o(x)$}

Let $\Delta(N)=\sum_{n\le N}(\tau(n)-\log n-2\gamma)$. The <Möbius divisor-sum identity> and the <Von Mangoldt divisor identity> give $\mu*\tau=\mathbf1$ and $\mu*\log=\Lambda$, so
$$
\psi(N)=N-2\gamma-\sum_{d\le N}\mu(d)\Delta(\lfloor N/d\rfloor).
$$
Here $*$ is <Dirichlet convolution>. The <divisor summatory asymptotic> bounds the terms with $d\le N/K$ in absolute value by $O(N/\sqrt K)$. For $d>N/K$, group by $j=\lfloor N/d\rfloor<K$: the remaining sum is $\sum_{j<K}\Delta(j)(M(N/j)-M(N/(j+1)))=o_K(N)$. First let $N$ tend to infinity, then $K$ tend to infinity. This proves the <second Chebyshev function> asymptotic from cancellation of the <Mertens function>.

= Mertens bound from a log-integrable Chebyshev error
{c}
{parent=Mertens function}
{title2=$M(x)\ll x/\log x$}

Suppose $\Psi(x)=x+O(xE(x))$ and $\sum_{j\geq0}\sup_{2^j\leq u<2^{j+1}}|E(u)|<\infty$. The <Möbius divisor-sum identity> gives $\sum_{d\leq x}\mu(d)/d=O(1)$. The exact <Dirichlet convolution> identity $\mu\log=-\mu*\Lambda$ then gives $\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)\log n=O(x)$, because its error is a convergent dyadic harmonic sum. Since $\sum_{n\leq x}\log(x/n)=O(x)$, the displayed <Mertens function> bound follows. The classical <prime number theorem> error $E(x)=e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}$ meets the summability condition. This is a deduction from the Chebyshev error itself, without a separate estimate for the reciprocal <zeta function>.

= Mertens function bound from the classical zeta zero-free region
{c}
{parent=Mertens function}

The classical <zero-free region of the Riemann zeta function> and a <truncated Perron formula> give
$$
M(x)\ll x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})
$$
for some absolute constant $c>0$. Take contour height $T=\exp(\sqrt{\log x})$ and move the Perron contour to $\Re s=1-c_1/\log T$.
