== 1

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $\partial_t\mathbf u=0$ and $\nu=0$ in the <Navier-Stokes equation>. Dotting the resulting <Euler equation> with a displacement $d\mathbf r$ tangent to a <streamline> gives
$$
d\left(\frac{|\mathbf u|^2}{2}\right)+\frac{dp}{\rho}+g\,dz=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{|\mathbf u|^2}{2}+\frac p\rho+gz=B.}
$$
Here <mass density> $\rho$ is constant, and $B$ is constant along each <streamline>. The terms are <kinetic energy> per unit mass, the <pressure work> per unit mass available from pushing a parcel through the flow, and <gravitational potential energy> per unit mass. Their sum is conserved mechanical energy per unit mass; $p/\rho$ is not an additional independently conserved parcel energy. Different <streamlines> can have different $B$ unless, for example, the flow is also <irrotational> in a connected region.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

For planar <incompressible flow>, $u_x+w_z=0$. Locally this permits a <streamfunction> with $u=\psi_z$, $w=-\psi_x$, because equality of the mixed derivatives enforces <incompressibility>. The remaining component of the <curl> is $u_z-w_x=\psi_{zz}+\psi_{xx}$, so <irrotational flow> requires <Laplace's equation> for $\psi$.

Put $K=\pi/b$. The sinusoidal contribution to the supplied <streamfunction> has $\psi_{xx}=U_0aK^2\cos(Kx)e^{-Kz}$ and $\psi_{zz}=-U_0aK^2\cos(Kx)e^{-Kz}$; the linear term is also harmonic. Hence
$$
u=U_0[1+aK\cos(Kx)e^{-Kz}],\qquad
w=-U_0aK\sin(Kx)e^{-Kz}.
$$
As $z\to\infty$, these approach the specified <uniform flow>, $(u,w)=(U_0,0)$.

The impermeability <kinematic boundary condition> is $w=u\,z_s'(x)$ on $z=z_s(x)$, equivalently $\psi$ is constant on the wall. For $aK\ll1$, evaluating the perturbed velocities at $z=0$ gives $w=-U_0aK\sin(Kx)=U_0z_s'$ to first order. The residual is of order $U_0(aK)^2$. Equivalently $\psi(x,z_s)=U_0a\cos(Kx)[1-e^{-Ka\cos(Kx)}]=O(U_0Ka^2)$. The surface velocity is
$$
\boxed{u_s=U_0[1+aK\cos(Kx)e^{-Ka\cos(Kx)}]
=U_0[1+aK\cos(Kx)]+O(U_0(aK)^2).}
$$
The first expression is the supplied approximate field evaluated exactly at the wall; its accuracy as a wall-flow solution is still only first order in $a/b$.

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <rest frame> of the <hydrofoil>, with undisturbed velocity $U_0\mathbf e_x$. Let $p_*$ be the undisturbed <pressure> at the lower surface level $z=0$, so the undisturbed <hydrostatic pressure> is $p_*-\rho gz$. The <Bernoulli equation> gives, with $h=a\cos(\pi x/b)$ and $\epsilon=\pi a/b$,
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm lower}=p_*,\qquad
p_{\rm upper}=p_*-\rho gh+
\frac{\rho U_0^2}{2}\left[1-\{1+\epsilon\cos(\pi x/b)\}^2\right].}
$$
These formulas implement the prescribed neglect of the other velocity components. To the controlled first order in $a/b$, $p_{\rm lower}-p_{\rm upper}=\rho gh+\rho U_0^2\epsilon\cos(\pi x/b)$. The vertical component of <pressure> acting on an inclined surface is integrated over its horizontal projection, so
$$
\boxed{L'_{\rm dynamic}=2\rho U_0^2a,\qquad
B'=\rho g\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}h\,dx=\frac{2\rho gab}{\pi}.}
$$
The total upward fluid <force> per unit span is $L'_{\rm dynamic}+B'$. The <lift> due to motion is $L'_{\rm dynamic}$; $B'$ is the separate <buoyancy>. If the given approximate speed is squared without truncation, the formal additional dynamic term is $\rho U_0^2\pi^2a^2/(4b)$, but a controlled second-order prediction would require the omitted second-order flow and transverse-velocity contributions too.

=== 4
{scope}

==== Solution

Take positive <circulation> to mean counterclockwise in the $(x,z)$ diagram, with $x$ right and $z$ up. The upper surface is then traversed right to left. To first order in $a/b$,
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=\oint\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf l
=\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}(U_0-u_s)\,dx=-2U_0a.}
$$
Thus the bound <circulation> is clockwise. Any exterior contour enclosing the <hydrofoil> has the same <circulation> in an <irrotational flow>; a globally completed two-dimensional flow must contain a circulation component decaying as $|\Gamma|/(2\pi r)$ at large distance. The simple local upper/lower velocity prescription cannot describe the entire exterior. For a start from rest, <Kelvin's circulation theorem> requires compensating shed <vorticity>, represented by a starting vortex in a physical wake.

Numerically,
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=-0.40\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}},\qquad
L'_{\rm dynamic}=8.0\times10^2\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
If the question's fluid <force> includes <buoyancy>, $B'\simeq1.25\times10^3\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ and the total upward fluid <force> is about $2.05\times10^3\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, using $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Weight of the <hydrofoil> is not specified and is not part of this fluid <force>. The motion-induced <lift> also agrees with the <Kutta–Joukowski theorem>, $L'=-\rho U_0\Gamma$ in this orientation.

The chord-based <Reynolds number> is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{U_0b}{\nu}\simeq2.7\times10^6.}
$$
At this large <Reynolds number>, smooth <laminar flow> over the whole body is unlikely in ordinary conditions; wall layers may undergo transition and separate. A thin-layer <inviscid flow> approximation outside them can remain useful, but does not justify globally <laminar flow>.

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $\mu$ for <dynamic viscosity> and $\nu=\mu/\rho$ for <kinematic viscosity>, consistently with the introductory <Navier-Stokes equation>. The phrase “dynamic viscosity $\nu$” in this request mixes these conventions. A fully developed <plane Poiseuille flow>, $\mathbf u=(u(z),0,0)$, has zero <convective acceleration>. The <Navier-Stokes equation> gives $\mu u''=-f$ and $p_z=-\rho g$. Applying the <no-slip boundary condition> at both stationary plates gives
$$
\boxed{u(z)=\frac{f}{2\mu}z(b-z)=\frac{f}{2\rho\nu}z(b-z).}
$$
The material coefficients must be effectively constant and positive. The profile is an exact parallel-flow solution for every $\mu>0$; experimental validity additionally requires that the realized flow remain in the <laminar flow> regime. A sufficient conservative condition is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\bar u b}{\nu}=\frac{fb^3}{12\rho\nu^2}\ll1,
\qquad \bar u=\frac{fb^2}{12\mu}.}
$$
Equivalently $\nu\gg\sqrt{fb^3/(12\rho)}$, or $\mu\gg\sqrt{\rho fb^3/12}$. This sufficient bound is stronger than necessary; there is no need to discard <convective acceleration> by an approximation here because it vanishes identically for the assumed profile.

Here $k$ is a <mass absorption coefficient>, because $k\rho$ has units of inverse length. The <Beer-Lambert law> is $dF/dz=-k\rho F$, giving $F(z)=F_0e^{-k\rho z}$. A horizontal slab of area $A$ and thickness $dz$ absorbs <power> $A[F(z)-F(z+dz)]$. Dividing by its volume and taking the limit gives

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

A parcel at fixed $z$ spends <time> $a/u(z)$ in the light. With no <thermal conduction> or <thermal expansion>, its energy balance is $\rho c\,dT/dt=q_R$. Equivalently the steady <advection equation> is $\rho c\,u(z)T_x=q_R(z)$ with $T(0,z)=T_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T(a,z)=T_0+\frac{aF_0k}{c\,u(z)}e^{-k\rho z}
=T_0+\frac{2\mu aF_0k}{cf}\frac{e^{-k\rho z}}{z(b-z)}.}
$$
The <no-slip boundary condition> makes residence <time> diverge as either wall is approached. The resulting unbounded <temperature> is a failure of the approximations, rather than a physically steady wall prediction. Even small <thermal conductivity> transfers heat away from these layers; conduction into real perspex adds another heat sink. <Thermal expansion> can alter <mass density>, create <buoyancy>-driven motion and change the background profile, while temperature-dependent <dynamic viscosity> changes the assumed residence <time>. With exactly insulating walls, finite fluid <thermal conductivity> still regularizes the transverse profile; an entrance region and, sufficiently near a stagnant wall, longitudinal conduction also matter.

=== 4
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $A=2\mu aF_0k/(cf)$. In the specified near-wall limit the outer <temperature> is $T-T_0\simeq A/(bz)$, so
$$
q_C\simeq\frac{2\kappa A}{bz^3},\qquad
\frac{|q_C|}{q_R}\simeq\frac{4\kappa\mu a}{\rho cfbz^3}
=\frac{4\kappa\nu a}{cfbz^3},\qquad
\chi=\frac{\kappa}{\rho c}.
$$
Thus the <near-wall radiative heating layer> has scale
$$
\boxed{0<z\lesssim\delta_C,\qquad
\delta_C=\left(\frac{4\kappa\mu a}{\rho cfb}\right)^{1/3}
=\left(\frac{4\kappa\nu a}{cfb}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
The comparison is an estimate of the breakdown of the outer solution, not a new inner <temperature> solution. It requires $\delta_C\ll b$ and $k\rho\delta_C\ll1$; neglecting longitudinal relative to transverse <thermal conduction> additionally requires $\delta_C\ll a$ in the region to which the estimate is applied.

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

An <equilibrium point of a dynamical system> is a state $\mathbf x_*$ with $\mathbf f(\mathbf x_*)=0$, so the trajectory stays there. In a planar <phase portrait>, an <unstable spiral> has a complex-conjugate pair of <Jacobian matrix> <eigenvalues> with positive real part: nearby trajectories rotate while moving outward. An <attractor> is an invariant set approached by trajectories from a neighborhood, its basin of attraction. It can be an <equilibrium point>, a <limit cycle>, or a more complicated set; outward spiralling near one <equilibrium point> does not preclude attraction to a larger set.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

At an <equilibrium point> of the <Rössler system>, $y=-z$, $x=az$, and $az^2-cz+a=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{z_\pm=\frac{c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4a^2}}{2a},\qquad
(x_\pm,y_\pm,z_\pm)=(az_\pm,-z_\pm,z_\pm).}
$$
There are two distinct real <equilibrium points> only if $c>2a$; they merge if $c=2a$ and there are none if $c<2a$. Positivity of $a,c$ alone does not guarantee two. Under the later limits, the nearer one is $(x_-,y_-,z_-)\simeq(a^2/c,-a/c,a/c)$, while $(x_+,y_+,z_+)\simeq(c,-c/a,c/a)$.

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Jacobian matrix> of the <Rössler system> is
$$
\boxed{J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&-1\\1&a&0\\z&0&x-c\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
At the origin the vertical homogeneous perturbation obeys $\delta\dot z=-c\,\delta z$, so it decays as $e^{-ct}$. Nearby, $\dot z\simeq a-cz$ also relaxes the trajectory toward $z\simeq a/c$, close to the $x$–$y$ plane. The small fixed-point value of $z$ weakly couples planar errors back into $z$; this is neglected in the requested approximation.

The planar block has <trace> $a$ and <determinant> $1$, with
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{1,2}=\frac a2\pm i\sqrt{1-\frac{a^2}{4}}.}
$$
For $0<a\ll1$, this is an <unstable spiral>. Writing $X=x-x_-$ and $Y=y-y_-$, its linearized trajectories have an envelope proportional to $e^{at/2}$ and angular frequency approximately $1$. They rotate counterclockwise: at $X>0,Y=0$, $\dot Y=X>0$. Fast vertical contraction occurs on scale $1/c$, much shorter than the planar growth scale $2/a$.

\Image[2011-rossler-spiral.png]
{title=Counterclockwise outward spiral near the inner Rössler equilibrium}

Confinement weakens as $x$ approaches $c$ from below; the approximate vertical relaxation time is $1/(c-x)$, and the quasi-steady value $a/(c-x)$ ceases to be small. For $x>c$, the coefficient of $z$ becomes positive, so vertical departures are amplified. The plane $z=0$ is never exactly invariant because $\dot z=a$ there.

=== 4
{scope}

==== Solution

For an infinitesimal perturbation $\boldsymbol\eta$, $\dot{\boldsymbol\eta}=J\boldsymbol\eta$, and
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log|\boldsymbol\eta|=\frac{\boldsymbol\eta^TS\boldsymbol\eta}{|\boldsymbol\eta|^2},\qquad
S=\frac{J+J^T}{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&(z-1)/2\\0&a&0\\(z-1)/2&0&x-c\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus the principal <instantaneous stretching rates>, called local <Lyapunov exponents> in the question, are
$$
\boxed{\lambda_y=a,\qquad
\lambda_\pm=\frac{x-c\pm\sqrt{(x-c)^2+(z-1)^2}}{2}.}
$$
For $z\ne1$, $\lambda_+>0$ and $\lambda_-<0$, while $a>0$. There are two instantaneously expanding orthogonal directions and one contracting direction. These are instantaneous norm-growth rates; the directions rotate along a trajectory, so this does not imply two positive long-time <Lyapunov exponents> or indefinitely exponential growth along fixed axes.

At $x=c$, put $s=\operatorname{sgn}(z-1)$. The <eigenvectors> and rates are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf e_+=\frac{(1,0,s)}{\sqrt2},\quad\lambda_+=\frac{|z-1|}{2};\qquad
\mathbf e_-=\frac{(1,0,-s)}{\sqrt2},\quad\lambda_-=-\frac{|z-1|}{2}.}
$$
The condition $|z-1|>2a$ makes $\mathbf e_+$ the fastest-growing direction, faster than $\mathbf e_y$; $\mathbf e_-$ is the fastest-contracting direction. Both lie at $45^\circ$ to the $x,z$ axes.

=== 1

==== Solution

Take $u>0$. Despite the source's terminology, $P(x)$ is a dimensionless conditional bound-state <probability> at phase $x$, not a spatially normalized <probability density>; the phase-average bound fraction is $\Delta^{-1}\int_0^\Delta P\,dx$. The given equation has <advection> and binding reactions but no diffusion term. Large $N$ permits the prescribed steady $u$ approximation.

For <spatially gated myosin attachment>, define $A=e^{-k_1x_1/u}$, $B=e^{-k_2(\Delta-x_2)/u}$, $p_0=P(0)$ and $p_b=P(x_1)$. Continuity and periodicity give $p_b=1-(1-p_0)A$, $p_0=Bp_b$, hence $p_b=(1-A)/(1-AB)$. The full periodic steady solution is
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm ss}(x)=\begin{cases}
1-(1-p_0)e^{-k_1x/u},&0\le x<x_1,\\
p_b,&x_1\le x<x_2,\\
p_b e^{-k_2(x-x_2)/u},&x_2\le x<\Delta,
\end{cases}\qquad
p_0=\frac{B(1-A)}{1-AB}.}
$$
The requested limit makes $B\ll1$, so $p_0\simeq0$ and $p_b\simeq1-e^{-k_1x_1/u}$. The curve rises exponentially over the binding window, remains flat to $x_2$, then decays over length $u/k_2$ to an exponentially small value before the next period. The piecewise expression is continuous at both switching points and at the period boundary.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

For a head sampling phases uniformly during steady sliding, its mean <force> is $f_0$ times the phase-average bound <probability>. In the $B\ll1$ limit of <spatially gated myosin attachment>, integrating the three intervals gives
$$
\frac{\Delta\langle f\rangle}{f_0}\simeq x_1-\frac{u}{k_1}(1-A)
+(x_2-x_1)(1-A)+\frac{u}{k_2}(1-A)(1-B).
$$
The first integral is bounded by $x_1p_b$, and the last by $(u/k_2)p_b$. Relative to the plateau contribution, they are negligible when $x_1\ll x_2$ and $u/k_2\ll x_2$, respectively. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle f\rangle\simeq f_0\frac{x_2}{\Delta}\left(1-e^{-k_1x_1/u}\right).}
$$
This is a <constant-force spatially gated motor> approximation: complete detachment between binding windows, a narrow attachment window, a short detachment tail and essentially fixed sliding <velocity>. In particular, $k_1x_1/u$ need not be small.

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $V=k_1x_1$ and $F_*=Nf_0x_2/\Delta$. The <constant-force spatially gated motor> produces
$$
F_m(u)=F_*\left(1-e^{-V/u}\right),\qquad
\boxed{F_{\max}=\lim_{u\to0^+}F_m(u)=\frac{Nf_0x_2}{\Delta}.}
$$
The limit is approached after the periodic steady state is established; at exactly $u=0$ arbitrary initially bound heads in inactive phase intervals need not equilibrate. The curve decreases from $F_{\max}$ and has formal large-$u$ tail $F_{\max}V/u$. Extrapolation of that tail beyond the small-$u$ detachment assumptions is not controlled.

**Sign inconsistency in the printed force balance.** For a positive <viscous drag coefficient> $\gamma$, the signed <drag force> is $-\gamma u$. The printed $F_{\rm ext}+F_m+\gamma u=0$ cannot give the requested positive no-load contraction speed: both last terms are positive when $u>0$. With $F_{\rm ext}=-F_{\max}/2$, the left side starts at $F_{\max}/2>0$ and may have zero, one or two positive roots depending on parameters; the requested usual load construction is not implied by that equation.

The physical <linear-drag load curve for a spatially gated motor> uses
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm ext}+F_m(u)-\gamma u=0.}
$$
On the same axes plot the positive drag magnitude $D(u)=\gamma u$ and the decreasing motor <force> $F_m(u)$. The no-load speed $u_0$ is their intersection. For opposing load $F_{\max}/2$, intersect $F_m(u)$ with the parallel raised line $D(u)+F_{\max}/2$; equivalently intersect $D(u)$ with $F_m(u)-F_{\max}/2$. Drop verticals to the speed axis. The loaded speed obeys $0<u_{1/2}<u_0$. Each intersection is unique because $F_m$ decreases and $D$ increases. The signed drag is also indicated below the axis in the sketch.

The notes stop at “no change”; the experimental implication is that this is a poorly conditioned <neutrino mass> measurement. In <relativistic two-body decay>, the corrections to the rest-frame energy and momentum enter through the square of the daughter <rest mass>, while the <Lorentz transformation> boosts both the massless value and its small correction. A measured laboratory energy or angle must therefore resolve a very small change against beam-energy spread, decay-angle uncertainty and detector resolution. **No visible change at the quoted precision does not establish zero neutrino mass.**

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

The official notes give only “Part 1: as in textbooks” (PDF p. 13, handwritten p. 8). The requested derivation is supplied here.

Put a light source at the rod midpoint in its <rest frame> $S$ and emit two pulses simultaneously toward its ends, each a distance $\ell$ away. In $S$, the reflection events are $(t,x)=(\ell/c,\pm\ell)$. Invariance of light speed, the <Einstein's principle of relativity> and linearity give a <Lorentz transformation> $x'=A(x-vt)$, $t'=A(t-vx/c^2)$. Applying the inverse transformation with speed $-v$ gives $A^2(1-v^2/c^2)=1$, hence $A=\gamma$.

The transformed reflection events have
$$
x'_+=\gamma\ell(1-\beta),\quad t'_+=\frac{\gamma\ell}{c}(1-\beta),\qquad
x'_-=-\gamma\ell(1+\beta),\quad t'_- =\frac{\gamma\ell}{c}(1+\beta).
$$
These events are not simultaneous in $S'$. Both rod ends move at $-v$ there, so extrapolating each endpoint to $t'=0$ gives $x'_\pm(0)=x'_\pm+vt'_\pm=\pm\ell/\gamma$. Measuring simultaneous endpoints therefore yields <length contraction>:
$$
\boxed{L'=x'_+(0)-x'_-(0)=\frac{2\ell}{\gamma}=2\ell\sqrt{1-\beta^2}.}
$$

===== Solution

The orientation-dependent results in the official notes show that the measured <spring constant> is **not a Lorentz scalar**. A <Lorentz transformation> changes both the simultaneous extension and the measured three-force; their ratio depends on the orientation. This does not make the spring intrinsically anisotropic in its own <rest frame>, or require a preferred inertial frame. It is a quasistatic effective stiffness comparison between frames; an extended real spring also has finite internal signal speeds.

==== Solution

**Covered by the official notes:** definitions of four-force and four-velocity and the vanishing antisymmetric contraction (PDF p. 15; handwritten p. 10).<Symmetry and Relativity 2011 Exam Solution Notes>[PDF p. 15; handwritten p. 10: definitions of four-force and four-velocity and the vanishing antisymmetric contraction]{ref}

To make the necessity statement precise, mass preservation must hold **for every allowed timelike four-velocity**, with the same field <tensor> and nonzero charge. It is not necessary for one prescribed trajectory alone. Use <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$, so $U_aF^a=-c^2dm/d\tau$. Put $H^{ab}=(\mathcal F^{ab}+\mathcal F^{ba})/2$. The mass-preservation condition requires the <quadratic form> $H^{ab}U_aU_b=0$ for every timelike $U$. Every timelike vector can be rescaled to a physical <four-velocity>; by homogeneity the quadratic form vanishes on the open timelike cone. A polynomial vanishing on an open set is identically zero, so $H^{ab}=0$. Thus the <electromagnetic field tensor> must be antisymmetric under this universal quantifier. For a single velocity, the scalar contraction can vanish even for a nonzero symmetric part, which is why the quantifier is essential.

==== Solution

The official notes state only $\mathcal F'=\Lambda\mathcal F\Lambda^T$ (PDF p. 15, handwritten p. 10); they do not evaluate the requested coefficients. For a boost along positive $x$, use $\Lambda^0{}_0=\Lambda^1{}_1=\gamma$, $\Lambda^0{}_1=\Lambda^1{}_0=-\gamma v/c$, with the transverse diagonal entries equal to one. Multiplying the specified <electromagnetic field tensor> gives
$$
\mathcal F'^{01}=\mathcal F^{01},\qquad
\mathcal F'^{02}=\gamma(\mathcal F^{02}-\beta\mathcal F^{12}),\qquad
\mathcal F'^{23}=\mathcal F^{23},\qquad
\mathcal F'^{31}=\gamma(\mathcal F^{31}-\beta\mathcal F^{30}).
$$
The corresponding <Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields> is
$$
\boxed{E_x'=E_x,\qquad E_y'=\gamma(E_y-vB_z),\qquad
B_x'=B_x,\qquad B_y'=\gamma\left(B_y+\frac{vE_z}{c^2}\right).}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{a_1=\gamma,\qquad a_2=0,\qquad a_3=-\gamma v.}
$$
These coefficients depend on the frame speed, but are constant for the fixed boost.
