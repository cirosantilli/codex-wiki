= Solution
{parent=a}

**The viral <DNA> is copied by <semiconservative replication> and its labelling kinetics broadly follow those of host <DNA> during <viral latency>.** At the earliest time the newly labelled material is mainly hybrid: an old light strand has acquired a heavy complementary strand. Fully heavy duplexes accumulate later, after heavy strands themselves serve as templates. Conservative copying would instead produce a separate fully heavy population from the first copying round, while dispersive copying would produce progressively shifting intermediate densities rather than these three discrete classes.

For an ideal synchronous population starting entirely light, one complete round gives only hybrid duplexes; after $n\geq1$ rounds the hybrid fraction is $2^{1-n}$ and the fully heavy fraction is $1-2^{1-n}$. The actual culture is asynchronous, so these integer-generation proportions cannot simply be imposed at $t/23$ hours. The similar viral and cellular bars support replication coordinated with <cell proliferation> rather than rapid autonomous amplification. They do not establish that every <episome> initiates exactly once in every individual <biological cell>, or prove an initiation time within <S phase>. Persistent light material also warns against treating the population as a perfectly uniform set of cycling <biological cells>. The strand-accounting principle is the one tested in <The replication of DNA in Escherichia coli by Matthew Meselson and Franklin W. Stahl>[Figures 3–4: changes in banding over successive copying rounds]{ref}.

**Base composition changes both the initial <buoyant density> and the size of the BUdR-induced density increment.** The fraction-number axis runs toward lower density. The unlabelled viral peak lies at a lower fraction number than the corresponding host peak, consistent with viral <DNA> having a higher <GC content>. <BUdR> replaces <thymidine> in newly synthesized strands, so relatively AT-rich <DNA> incorporates more brominated <nucleoside> per unit length and undergoes a larger density increase.

Consequently the more AT-rich host <DNA> can overtake the viral <DNA> in the fully substituted class: the host fully heavy peak is slightly farther toward the dense end, although host unlabelled <DNA> was less dense. The hybrid peaks are intermediate. A useful qualitative model is $\rho_{LL}=\rho_0(g)$, $\rho_{LH}=\rho_0(g)+\Delta\rho(g)/2$ and $\rho_{HH}=\rho_0(g)+\Delta\rho(g)$, where $g$ is GC fraction, $\rho_0$ increases with $g$, and $\Delta\rho$ decreases as the <thymidine> fraction decreases. Unequal substitution efficiencies could contribute, but different replication mechanisms are unnecessary. These are equilibrium density positions, not molecular-length measurements.

**The <DNA-binding domain> supports site occupancy, while the region for <homotypic protein interaction> permits much larger assemblies.** With the truncated <protein>, the faster complex 4 appears at low <protein>:<DNA> ratios and gives way to the slower complex 3 as <protein> increases. A natural assignment is occupancy of one and then both recognition repeats by functional binding units. Full-length <protein> instead produces the very slow complex 1 already at relatively low ratios, followed by more of complex 2 at high ratios. <homotypic protein interactions> can make cooperative higher-order assemblies and can bridge different probe molecules. At low <concentration>, the array with more and higher-affinity binding sites is preferentially occupied, giving one visible <protein> cluster on an otherwise extended <DNA> molecule. At higher <concentration> the weaker origin array also becomes occupied. **<homotypic protein interactions> between <proteins> at the two arrays can bridge them, trapping the intervening <DNA> through <DNA looping>.** This explains why the electron-dense <protein> cluster lies at the loop junction rather than uniformly coating the entire molecule.

The high-affinity array can stabilize weak-site occupancy through tethering: once one partner is anchored, the second site experiences a high effective local <concentration> and several contacts can contribute <avidity>. This reconciles stable looped assemblies with the vulnerability of the isolated weak site to <nonspecific competitor DNA>. The images support intramolecular <DNA bridging>, but selected micrographs do not measure its frequency or exclude some intermolecular associations. A high local density of <protein> need not imply <biomolecular phase separation>. **Latent viral <DNA> has regularly spaced <nucleosome> protection, whereas much of the nuclear viral <DNA> during productive infection lacks that regular protection.** In the latent sample, increasing digestion resolves a ladder of protected fragments, as expected when <micrococcal nuclease> cuts exposed linker <DNA> between <nucleosomes>. In the productive sample, viral signal is lost rapidly into heterogeneous small material rather than accumulating as a comparable ladder. That is consistent with a large pool of newly replicated or otherwise differently packaged viral <DNA> being more accessible.

The total-DNA ladders show that host <chromatin> remains nucleosomal and that the assay can recover protected fragments in each cellular condition. The virus-specific <Southern blot> distinguishes viral <DNA> from this abundant host background, and the uninfected lane controls probe background. The conclusion concerns the dominant detectable viral population: productive cultures can contain both chromatinized templates in <viral latency> and accessible replication products. Absence of a ladder does not establish absence of every <histone> molecule, and differences in <DNA> recovery or <nuclease> accessibility should be considered before assigning a precise fraction of chromatinized <genomes>.

**Sequestration of assembly-competent <G-actin> explains both inhibition of <polymer> formation and loss of pre-existing cellular structures.** In a simple model, free <actin monomer> $G$ binds drug $D$ to form an assembly-incompetent complex $GD$. The available endpoints do not uniquely distinguish these possibilities. In particular, they provide no positive evidence for direct <actin filament severing> or <actin filament capping> by ToxB. **Test whether cellular drug effects persist when <actin> is protected by a <drug-resistant allele>.** Compare otherwise matched wild-type and act1-117 <biological cells> across a drug-concentration range, measuring growth or colony formation together with <actin> organization and a relevant cellular phenotype. Each strain needs a vehicle-only baseline because the mutant already has abnormal intrinsic assembly. If the resistant strain maintains its <actin> network and the normal actin-dependent functions but still develops another reproducible defect, that residual effect is a candidate <off-target drug effect>. Comparable intracellular exposure is important: resistance caused by lower uptake would not protect only the intended target.

Conversely, coordinated rescue of the <actin> and growth phenotypes supports an on-target explanation over that dose range, without proving the absence of every other molecular interaction. Reintroducing wild-type <actin> or using another independently resistant allele helps separate the <actin> substitution from unrelated <genetic background> effects. A candidate secondary target would need its own binding or functional assay and a selective rescue test. A second <actin> inhibitor giving the same phenotype is supportive but cannot alone exclude shared secondary effects.

**Experiment 2:** relative to untreated wild-type <actin>, <actin monomer sequestration> predicts a longer apparent <actin nucleation> lag, a smaller elongation slope, and a lower steady-state <polymer> mass. At sufficiently strong binding the trace could remain nearly flat rather than displaying clearly separated phases. The wild-type <sedimentation> data support a lower endpoint, but do not establish the exact kinetic changes or whether the time to plateau is longer or shorter.

**Experiment 3:** the halo result does not determine the drug-free <actin polymerization> curve of act1-129. A <mutation> that mainly raises drug affinity could have a nearly wild-type curve without drug. A <mutation> that intrinsically destabilizes <actin filaments> or impairs <actin nucleation> could instead prolong the lag, reduce growth and lower the plateau. Thus a wild-type-like untreated curve is a useful conditional reference prediction, not a result that follows from cellular hypersensitivity.

**Experiment 4:** act1-129 is about sixfold more sensitive in the cellular assay. If that reflects a stronger direct effect of ToxB on its <actin monomers>, predict greater suppression than in experiment 2: a longer or effectively absent <actin nucleation> phase, slower or undetectable elongation, and a still lower plateau relative to its own drug-free control. It could be almost flat. However, cellular hypersensitivity could also arise from reduced functional reserve or altered interactions with other <proteins>; it does not guarantee stronger inhibition of purified <actin>. These alternatives are exactly what the comparison with experiment 3 resolves. No numerical lag, slope or plateau can be calculated from halo sizes alone.

**Efficient and timely budding requires the normal <actin> network, but some initial <polarized cell growth> remains possible when visible <actin filaments> are greatly depleted.** Around 40% of control <biological cells> have new buds by two hours, rising to nearly 60% by six hours. Drug-treated <biological cells> show almost no budding at two hours but roughly 15–20% at four to six hours, and the phase images confirm small buds despite loss of normal bright <actin> structures.

The result therefore supports a large delay and reduction, rather than an absolute block to every initiation event. Polarity cues or localized membrane-fusion machinery could remain spatially organized without normal long <actin cables>. The images and bud counts do not establish completion of a daughter-cell cycle, normal <cytokinesis> or indefinite proliferation. Residual <actin filaments> below detection remain an alternative contributor. **The new bud contains newly deposited wall rather than simply inheriting a stretched patch of the mother's old labelled wall.** <concanavalin A> conjugated to <FITC> was applied before growth resumed, so it marks the pre-existing accessible <biological cell> surface. The bright mother outline persists while the emerging bud is weakly labelled or unlabelled. That spatial contrast places substantial new, unlabelled wall material at the growth site. Drug-treated <biological cells> that eventually bud show that at least some localized new wall deposition still occurs under treatment.

The pulse label does not mark all wall made subsequently, and it does not prove that absolutely no new material is inserted elsewhere in the mother. Signal dilution, shedding and accessibility can affect brightness. A second distinguishable wall label applied after refeeding would more directly map new deposition. If the original <lectin> remained free in the medium, it would also bind newly exposed wall, so the interpretation assumes the stated prelabelling step functions as a pulse rather than continuous staining.

**Some <cell membrane> delivery and fusion must continue in the <biological cells> whose buds actually grow, but it need not use normal long-range cable transport.** <secretory vesicles> can move by <molecular diffusion> or other non-directed motion and be preferentially captured, tethered and fused where a surviving polarity cue concentrates the relevant <vesicle docking> machinery. Spatial selectivity can therefore reside in the destination rather than in directed travel over the entire route. Less efficient delivery is consistent with delayed budding and small buds.

Short residual <actin> tracks could also contribute; the experiment does not prove that every <actin filament> is absent. Neither bud shape nor <Con-A> staining directly measures vesicle trajectories or fusion events. Tracking <secretory vesicles> together with a membrane-fusion reporter and a polarity marker would distinguish directed runs from <molecular diffusion> followed by localized capture, with expression and imaging controls for reporter effects. Random arrival with selective <vesicle docking> is a published mechanistic proposal for this physiological context, rather than a demonstrated trajectory in the present images.<Polarized growth in the absence of F-actin in Saccharomyces cerevisiae exiting quiescence by Annelise Sahin and others>[Discussion: vesicle delivery and preferential docking during polarized growth]{ref}

**The weak <phalloidin> signal could include sparse or very short residual <actin filaments>, together with nonspecific background; it is not a direct image of <G-actin>.** <phalloidin> preferentially binds <filamentous actin>, so genuinely specific residual signal would indicate some polymeric <actin>. Long exposure also amplifies <autofluorescence>, out-of-focus light and nonspecific staining. Vehicle controls, unstained <biological cells>, matched acquisition settings and background subtraction are needed before assigning a small residual signal to <actin filaments>.

For wavelength $\lambda$ and <numerical aperture> $\mathrm{NA}$, the <Rayleigh criterion> gives a conventional lateral resolution of roughly $d\approx0.61\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$, typically a few hundred nanometres under these conditions, far larger than the roughly 7-nm <actin filament> diameter. It cannot resolve every short <actin filament>, distinguish all closely spaced <actin filaments>, or infer <polymer> mass directly from the displayed intensity. The tenfold exposure change is not itself a calibrated tenfold decrease in <F-actin>. Fixation, dye accessibility and detector saturation introduce further uncertainty.

Super-resolution <fluorescence microscopy> could localize remaining labelled structures more precisely, while <electron microscopy> or <cryo-electron tomography> could test ultrastructure, subject to preservation and sampling limits. Biochemical separation of polymeric and soluble <actin>, with the mass-balance and aggregation controls already discussed, supplies an independent population measurement. A live <actin> reporter can test dynamics but must be checked for perturbation of assembly. Agreement between these approaches would be stronger than concluding complete <actin filament> absence from weak conventional staining alone.
