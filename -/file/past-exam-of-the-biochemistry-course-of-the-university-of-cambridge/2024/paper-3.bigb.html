**I would establish whether the alteration drives <cancer>, whether the established <cancer> depends on it, and whether that dependence offers a selective intervention point.** These are different questions. A recurrent alteration can be a passenger; a genuine initiating driver can become dispensable after progression; and an essential <protein> can be equally essential in normal tissue.

First validate the genomic observation with an independent assay and matched normal <DNA>. Distinguish a somatic <mutation> from inherited variation, sequencing error and contamination. For <gene amplification>, resolve the altered interval, copy number and <tumour purity>, and determine whether the candidate is the focus of a recurrent, narrowly amplified segment or simply accompanies a neighbouring driver in a broad gain. Measure <RNA>, <protein> and relevant activity: increased <DNA> dosage need not increase active <protein>. For <mutations>, inspect the spectrum and allelic configuration. Recurrent clustered missense changes can suggest altered activity, whereas dispersed truncations and loss of the remaining allele can suggest tumour-suppressor inactivation. Neither pattern is conclusive without accounting for sequence context, background <mutation> rate and selection. Large <genes>, highly mutable sites and poorly repaired regions can recur without being selected; analyses of mutational heterogeneity and somatic selection show why a simple recurrence ranking is insufficient.<Mutational heterogeneity in cancer and the search for new cancer-associated genes by Michael S. Lawrence and others>[Background heterogeneity and false candidate-driver discovery]{ref}<Universal patterns of selection in cancer and somatic tissues by Iñigo Martincorena and others>[Selection estimates accounting for mutation opportunity]{ref}

Relate the alteration to <cancer> type, stage, co-occurring lesions and clonal architecture. Single-cell or spatial assays can establish whether it is in the malignant compartment and whether every <tumour> <biological cell> carries it. Correct variant fractions for purity and copy number before inferring clonality. A clonal lesion may be early, but clonality alone does not prove causality; a passenger can hitchhike in an expanding clone. Association with outcome should be assessed within appropriate clinical and molecular strata rather than interpreted from a mixture of unrelated <tumour> types.

For functional necessity, use several independently altered <cell lines> and patient-derived <organoids>, together with lineage-matched unaltered <cancers> and nonmalignant controls. Reduce the candidate with orthogonal perturbations and rescue with a perturbation-resistant allele at a near-physiological level. Measure proliferation, cell-cycle distribution, <apoptosis>, differentiation and the proposed pathway output separately. In an amplified region, a particularly important control is to avoid equating CRISPR cutting toxicity with <gene> dependence: many <DNA> breaks at multiple copies can suppress growth independently of the encoded <gene>. Aguirre and colleagues demonstrated this copy-number-dependent response.<Copy-number-dependent CRISPR toxicity study by Andrew J. Aguirre and others>[Amplified targets and gene-independent antiproliferative effects]{ref} <CRISPR interference> or <RNA interference>, combined with rescue and appropriately matched cutting controls, helps distinguish <protein> dependence from this artefact. Guide-specific off-target effects and incomplete depletion also require direct checks.

Test sufficiency and allele specificity by introducing the observed allele into a relevant nonmalignant background, preferably through endogenous editing, or by increasing expression to the measured <tumour> level. Compare wild type, each recurrent mutant and appropriate inactive variants at matched abundance. For a suspected <tumour> suppressor, recreate the observed heterozygous or biallelic loss rather than assuming every <mutation> is equivalent to a knockout. A kinase-domain mutant might activate signalling, a truncation might remove negative regulation, and another allele might abolish function. Physiological assays of acinar organization, growth-factor dependence or invasion can reveal transformation phenotypes missed by a two-dimensional growth assay. Failure to transform one <biological cell> type alone does not exclude a driver requiring cooperating lesions or the correct developmental state.

Then connect genotype to mechanism. Identify affected enzymatic activity, binding partners, localization and downstream signalling, using time-resolved perturbation to separate primary changes from consequences of dying <biological cells>. Restore a downstream effector to test whether it bypasses loss of the candidate, or inhibit that effector to test whether it is required for the mutant phenotype. Rescue with an inhibitor-resistant target allele is strong evidence for a drug's intended mechanism. A correlation between <mutation> and a distant transcriptional signature is weaker. Genetic and pharmacological results should agree at exposures that establish <target engagement> rather than general cytotoxicity.

Finally test initiation and maintenance in suitable in vivo settings: tissue-appropriate engineered mice, orthotopic <organoid> transplantation or <patient-derived xenografts>, with the immune context chosen for the hypothesis. Inducing an allele before <tumour> development tests initiation; turning it off after established growth tests maintenance. Quantify <tumour latency>, burden, invasion and metastatic outcome with random allocation, blinded assessment and independent <biological replicates>. Measure effects on normal tissue to define a <therapeutic window>, and examine resistant outgrowth for bypass mechanisms. **The strongest conclusion combines statistically credible human selection, allele-specific causal perturbation, mechanistic rescue and disease-selective dependence across appropriate models.**

**<cancer> largely reuses normal cellular machinery, but its organization and evolutionary behaviour are not adequately described as one homeostatic control simply being turned up or down.** Growth, survival, metabolism, plasticity and tissue remodelling all have normal functions. Malignancy arises when their coordination with the needs of the organism is lost, and selection can then generate new combinations, altered molecular activities and pathological ecological interactions.

Normal proliferation depends on extracellular signals and internal cell-cycle controls. Constitutive receptor or <Ras> signalling, increased cyclin activity or weakened <RB>–<E2F> restraint can make a <biological cell> divide under conditions in which its neighbours would arrest. These changes exploit existing <DNA replication> and mitotic machinery rather than inventing a new way to duplicate a <genome>. Equally, normal <apoptosis> removes damaged or inappropriate <biological cells>; a <tumour> can survive by suppressing this response. Loss of <p53> can compromise a network linking stress to arrest, repair, death and other restraints. Mice lacking <p53> develop spontaneous <tumours> despite largely normal development, directly demonstrating the importance of an existing protective programme.<Mice deficient for p53 are developmentally normal but susceptible to spontaneous tumours by L. A. Donehower and others>[Tumour susceptibility after p53 loss]{ref} This does not mean that every <cancer> must eliminate every <p53> output or that all <p53> <mutations> have the same effect.

Many other properties have similarly recognizable antecedents. <glycolysis>, nutrient uptake and <autophagy> support normal growth and stress adaptation; their deployment can help <cancer> <biological cells> tolerate fluctuating resources. Angiogenic signals normally support development and repair, but sustained production can recruit vessels into a growing <tumour>. Motility, matrix remodelling and reversible changes in adhesion participate in development and wound repair. <cancer> can recruit them for invasion, with surrounding <fibroblasts> and immune <biological cells> contributing signals and physical routes. Collective breast-cancer invasion, for example, can depend on a conserved basal epithelial programme rather than a completely new locomotor apparatus.<Collective invasion in breast cancer requires a conserved basal epithelial program by Kevin J. Cheung and others>[Basal-marker leader cells and functional invasion experiments]{ref} Immune suppression also uses normal regulatory pathways, although suppressing antitumour immunity conflicts with organismal protection.

The homeostatic interpretation is most useful when it emphasizes constraints and feedback. Normal tissue renewal couples production to <biological cell> loss and spatial organization. A <mutation> increasing division, reducing death or changing competitive interactions can unbalance this system even without maximal activation of every pathway. Selection acts on net clonal success, not only on instantaneous proliferation. A slowly cycling population that survives therapy or colonizes a new site may be favoured under particular conditions. Conversely, an excessively strong oncogenic signal can trigger <senescence> or <apoptosis>, so successful <cancers> often combine activating changes with escape from compensating restraints. Different combinations can converge on similar phenotypes.

However, “nothing new” becomes misleading at the molecular level. Some alterations generate a qualitatively changed activity. Cancer-associated <IDH1> mutants acquire the ability to reduce <alpha-ketoglutarate> to R-2-hydroxyglutarate, rather than simply increasing the <enzyme>'s ordinary reaction. <metabolite> profiling and biochemical assays established this altered catalytic activity.<Cancer-associated IDH1 mutations produce 2-hydroxyglutarate by Lenny Dang and others>[Tumour-associated metabolite and mutant-enzyme activity]{ref} <gene fusions> can join recognition and signalling domains in new regulatory arrangements, while new enhancer contacts or altered splicing can change which molecular combinations are expressed. These innovations still obey familiar chemistry and use inherited components, but that is true of biological innovation generally; it does not make them equivalent to a reversible normal homeostatic response.

<cancer> is also an evolving population within a changing environment. Genetic and epigenetic variation permit selection for survival during <hypoxia>, immune attack or treatment. The selected phenotype can depend on the surrounding stroma and on interactions among <tumour> subclones. Normal wound-healing responses may stop when repair is complete, whereas a <tumour> can maintain the signals or continually regenerate the damage that recruits them. Altered metabolism, inflammation and genomic instability can create feedback that changes the environment itself. This is not a single <biological cell> merely ignoring one stop signal, and one successful clone need not benefit either neighbouring <cancer> <biological cells> or the host.

Experimentally, the distinction matters. If an existing normal programme is misregulated, correcting the upstream input or restoring feedback may normalize behaviour, but normal tissues using the same programme may also be affected. If an altered <protein> has a tumour-specific pocket or catalytic dependence, that difference may provide selectivity. Genotype-matched perturbations, measurements of the normal counterpart and tests in tissue context discriminate these cases. A list of cancer-associated phenotypes alone does not determine whether the causal change is quantitative, qualitative or ecological. **<cancer> usually repurposes normal processes, but malignancy emerges from altered control, molecular changes and selection among <biological cells>; “simply deregulation” understates that causal complexity.**

**Invasion is a crucial enabling step for the natural dissemination of most solid <cancers>, but it is neither synonymous with <metastasis> nor sufficient to complete it.** A carcinoma must escape its original epithelial compartment and negotiate surrounding tissue before disseminated <biological cells> can form a distant lesion. An invasive primary <tumour> can nevertheless fail at circulation survival, distant tissue entry or colonization.

The <metastatic cascade> is a sequence of conditional transitions: local escape, access to blood or lymph, survival during transport, arrest and entry at a distant site, and successful outgrowth. Schematically,
$$
P(\mathrm{distant\ colony})=P(E)P(T\mid E)P(S\mid E,T)P(C\mid E,T,S),
$$
where $E$ is escape, $T$ transport, $S$ establishment of a surviving disseminated <biological cell> and $C$ sustained colonization. This is a chain of conditional probabilities, not an assumption that the stages are independent. An intervention can strongly affect one factor while leaving another limiting step dominant. Consequently, invasiveness and overall metastatic efficiency need not rank <tumour> populations identically.

Local invasion requires altered relationships with neighbouring <biological cells> and <extracellular matrix>. <cancer> <biological cells> may remodel matrix, traverse pre-existing paths or recruit stromal <biological cells> that facilitate movement. Adhesion changes, <actomyosin> forces, polarity and protease activity can contribute in different proportions depending on tissue geometry. Histological breach of a <basement membrane> is important evidence of invasive carcinoma, but a static section does not reveal whether the <tumour> <biological cell> itself degraded the barrier or exploited a route created by another <biological cell>. Intravital imaging, three-dimensional culture and perturbation of both <tumour> and stromal compartments help identify the mechanism.

Invasion does not require every <biological cell> to become an isolated mesenchymal migrant. <biological cells> can move collectively while maintaining junctions, with leader and follower <biological cells> performing different tasks. <organoid> and <tumour> experiments showed that basal-marker-positive leader <biological cells> can drive collective breast-cancer invasion, and functional perturbation of the programme impaired that behaviour.<Collective invasion in breast cancer requires a conserved basal epithelial program by Kevin J. Cheung and others>[Collective strands, leader-cell programme and perturbation]{ref} Such evidence links a specific invasion mechanism to tissue behaviour, while leaving open which additional properties allow those invading <biological cells> to form distant colonies.

An <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> can assist dissemination in some contexts, but <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> and invasion should not be treated as interchangeable variables. EMT-associated changes can alter motility, survival and therapy response; partial states can retain epithelial cohesion. In a breast-cancer lineage-tracing model, metastases occurred without passage through the reporter-defined <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> state, while that state was associated with chemoresistance.<Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition is not required for lung metastasis but contributes to chemoresistance by Kari R. Fischer and others>[Lineage tracing, lung metastasis and treatment response]{ref} The inference is limited by the state the reporter detects: failure to mark a <biological cell> is not proof that no transient or partial transition occurred. Other experimental work found that the timing of <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> activation and reversal affected dissemination and metastatic growth, emphasizing that a useful escape programme can differ from a useful colonization programme.<Spatiotemporal regulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition is essential for squamous cell carcinoma metastasis by Jeff H. Tsai and others>[Temporal manipulation of EMT and metastatic progression]{ref} These findings challenge a universal <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> requirement, not the requirement to cross relevant anatomical barriers.

A direct test of invasion's importance must preserve the stages being investigated. Start with an orthotopic primary <tumour> and inducibly perturb a proposed invasion factor after comparable <tumours> have formed. Quantify primary burden, local boundary crossing, circulating <biological cells>, early distant seeds and later colonies separately. A reduction in metastases accompanied by much smaller primaries could simply reflect fewer <biological cells> available to disseminate. Conversely, a perturbation that leaves growth unchanged but reduces escape and subsequent seeds supports an invasion-specific contribution. Rescue and independent perturbations help exclude nonspecific loss of viability or adhesion.

Intravenous injection is a complementary bypass experiment: it tests transport survival and later establishment while omitting much primary-site invasion and <intravasation>. If a mutant fails from a primary <tumour> but succeeds after injection, that supports an early-stage defect. If it fails in both assays, it may affect a later step or several processes. Injection is not a demonstration that invasion is dispensable for spontaneous <metastasis>. The route also changes the first capillary bed encountered and introduces an artificial bolus. Where feasible, temporally restricted perturbation, primary-tumour resection and tracking of individually labelled disseminated clones can further distinguish escape from dormancy and outgrowth.

The anatomical context matters. Blood <cancers> already occupy migratory compartments, so their dissemination cannot be forced into the same basement-membrane-escape model as an epithelial carcinoma. Within solid <cancers>, the rate-limiting step can also differ by organ and <tumour> state. **Invasion provides access to the metastatic route in most solid <tumours>; distant colonization is an additional, demanding phenotype. Its importance is established by stage-specific causal measurements, not by equating an invasion assay, an <epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition> marker or a vascular injection result with the whole metastatic process.**

**Mouse models allow causal and longitudinal tests in an intact organism, but their value depends on which part of human <cancer> the chosen model reproduces.** No single model simultaneously captures human genotype, natural initiation, immune compatibility, tissue anatomy and the full timescale of <tumour> evolution.

A major strength is experimental control. Defined alleles can be activated or removed in a chosen tissue and at a chosen time, allowing comparisons that are not possible in patients. Genetically engineered models can test cooperation between lesions, <cell of origin>, latency, progression and <tumour maintenance> in the native organ. Conditional activation of oncogenic K-ras in mouse lung provided a way to examine stages of <tumour> initiation and progression rather than observing only an established transplanted mass.<Analysis of lung tumor initiation and progression using conditional expression of oncogenic K-ras by Erica L. Jackson and others>[Conditional activation and staged lung pathology]{ref} A reversible or inducible intervention after <tumour> formation can then distinguish an initiation requirement from a maintenance dependency. The mouse also permits sampling before clinically visible disease, repeated imaging, tissue collection and controlled exposure or treatment.

The host environment is another advantage. <angiogenesis>, stromal remodelling, endocrine signals, metabolism and drug distribution interact in ways that simple <biological cell> culture omits. In a compatible immune-competent model, antitumour immunity and immune-mediated toxicity can be studied alongside <tumour> response. Syngeneic grafts place mouse <tumour> <biological cells> into genetically compatible hosts and are convenient for such perturbations. Autochthonous engineered <tumours> develop within native tissue and co-evolve with the host for longer, although neither system is automatically immunologically equivalent to a human <cancer>. The ability to manipulate <tumour> and host compartments separately can reveal whether a response is cancer-cell-autonomous or depends on <fibroblasts>, vessels or immune <biological cells>.

Human <xenografts> provide a different advantage. Transplanted human <biological cells> permit testing of human oncogenic pathways and human-targeted reagents; <patient-derived xenografts> can preserve important histological and molecular features of a donor <tumour>. Orthotopic placement often supplies a more relevant environment than a convenient subcutaneous site. Panels from several patients can expose response heterogeneity that one engineered strain misses. Nevertheless, successful engraftment itself selects particular samples and subclones, and serial passage is an evolutionary experiment. Ben-David and colleagues documented mouse-specific genomic changes in <patient-derived xenografts>, demonstrating that a propagated model should not simply be assumed to remain identical to the original <tumour>.<Patient-derived xenografts undergo mouse-specific tumor evolution by Uri Ben-David and others>[Longitudinal copy-number changes during xenograft propagation]{ref} Compare early and late passages with the donor, and use the passage appropriate to the question.

The greatest <xenograft> limitation for many questions is the host. Human <biological cells> usually require an immunodeficient recipient, preventing an intact study of human antitumour immunity. Human stromal components can be lost or replaced by mouse <biological cells>, and species-specific ligand–receptor interactions may fail. Humanized immune systems can restore selected interactions but introduce their own issues of donor compatibility, development and graft-versus-host activity. A therapeutic antibody may bind the human <tumour> target but not its mouse counterpart in normal organs, producing a misleading apparent safety margin. Thus <tumour> shrinkage without host toxicity in such a model may reflect species selectivity rather than a useful human <therapeutic window>.

Engineered and syngeneic models also have limitations. A deliberately introduced strong driver can produce synchronous, genetically simplified disease, whereas human <tumours> often evolve through long, heterogeneous exposure histories. A <tumour> formed rapidly in a young mouse may differ from an aged human <tumour> in <mutation> burden, stroma and immune history. <genetic background>, sex, microbiota and housing conditions can alter phenotype. Species differences in lifespan, organ structure, metabolism, telomere regulation and drug <pharmacokinetics> further limit direct extrapolation. The <tumour> spectrum after <p53> loss, for example, is informative about <tumour> suppression but does not by itself reproduce the distribution of human TP53-mutant <cancers>.<Mice deficient for p53 are developmentally normal but susceptible to spontaneous tumours by L. A. Donehower and others>[Spontaneous-tumour model and its tissue context]{ref}

The endpoint and intervention must fit the biological question. Subcutaneous growth measures a local mass under an artificial geometry; tail-vein injection measures selected post-entry metastatic steps. Neither establishes a treatment effect on natural invasion or initial dissemination. Drug tests should establish exposure and <target engagement> in both <tumour> and relevant normal tissue, use clinically motivated schedules where possible, and measure survival or durable disease control as well as short-term volume. Rapidly growing implants may make some treatments look more effective than they are against slow, heterogeneous human disease.

Good design uses random allocation after a defined eligibility point, blinded measurement, prespecified endpoints and justified numbers of independent animals. Multiple <tumours> within one mouse are not automatically independent treatment replicates. Match or record relevant background variables and validate the intended allelic change and molecular phenotype. Ethical constraints favour well-chosen questions and informative longitudinal measurements rather than duplicating a weak model at larger scale. Combine mouse results with human genomics, patient-derived cultures or <organoids> and, ultimately, human evidence. **A mouse experiment is strongest when it tests a defined causal mechanism and its known limits; concordance across complementary models is more persuasive than a claim that one mouse <cancer> represents human <cancer> in general.**

**Direction arises from switching between mutually incompatible states, with feedback providing memory and regulated destruction resetting the system.** Different transitions use related logic, but a steep response, a bistable switch and an irreversible molecular event are not identical. The <cell cycle> also requires checkpoints and once-per-cycle licensing so that the ordered biochemical states produce a correct sequence of physical events.

At the G1/S transition, growth signals promote <cyclin-dependent kinase> activity and relieve RB-mediated restraint on <E2F>. E2F-dependent expression of additional cell-cycle components can reinforce the transition. More generally, activation of a kinase together with removal of its inhibitor creates <positive feedback>. In yeast, multisite <phosphorylation> of Sic1 permits recognition by the Cdc4 ubiquitin-ligase system and degradation of an inhibitor of S-phase kinase. This supplies a threshold that prevents weak or premature signals from launching the next programme.<Multisite phosphorylation of a CDK inhibitor sets a threshold for the onset of DNA replication by Piers Nash and others>[Phosphorylation-dependent Sic1 recognition and timing]{ref} The detailed regulators vary across organisms and <biological cell> types; the conserved point is a coordinated transition from an inhibited state to one that reinforces its own activation.

Mitotic entry gives an experimentally accessible example of <bistability>. <cyclin B> binding increases the available <CDK1> complex, but Wee1-dependent inhibitory <phosphorylation> can keep it inactive. <Cdc25> removes the inhibitory phosphate. Active <CDK1> promotes <Cdc25> activity and suppresses <Wee1>, giving a positive-feedback loop and a double-negative loop. Over a suitable input range, both a low-activity and a high-activity state can be stable. If cyclin input rises from interphase, activation occurs at a higher threshold than the input required to maintain a pre-existing mitotic state. These distinct thresholds constitute <hysteresis> and prevent small fluctuations from repeatedly reversing a committed transition.

In egg extracts, nondegradable cyclin could be supplied while CDK activity was approached from different initial states. The same cyclin input then supported different persistent activities depending on history, directly distinguishing <hysteresis> from a single steep but history-independent response.<Building a cell cycle oscillator: hysteresis and bistability in the activation of Cdc2 by Joseph R. Pomerening and others>[History-dependent activity at matched nondegradable cyclin input]{ref} These are extract measurements, not universal numerical thresholds for every intact <biological cell>. A useful conceptual representation is
$$
u_{\mathrm{off}}<u_{\mathrm{on}},\qquad u_{\mathrm{off}}<u<u_{\mathrm{on}}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{state depends on prior history},
$$
where $u$ represents effective activating input. <hysteresis> supplies memory within this interval; it does not mean that the switch can never be reset.

\Image[paper-3-cell-cycle-switch.png]
{title=Hysteretic activation preserves state until a separate reset crosses the lower threshold}

Reset is achieved by coupling activation to delayed <negative feedback> and <proteolysis>. Mitotic kinase activity helps prepare activation of the <anaphase-promoting complex> with <Cdc20>, while the <spindle checkpoint> prevents productive progression until attachment conditions are met. Once this restraint is satisfied, APC/C-dependent <ubiquitination> promotes destruction of <securin> and <cyclin B>. <securin> loss allows <separase> to cleave <cohesin>, making sister-chromatid separation possible. Experiments identifying <cohesin> cleavage connected regulated <proteolysis> to a physical transition that simple reversal of a <phosphorylation> state cannot undo.<Sister-chromatid separation at anaphase onset is promoted by cleavage of the cohesin subunit Scc1 by Frank Uhlmann and others>[Cohesin cleavage and anaphase onset]{ref} Chromatids cannot be returned to a pre-anaphase <chromosome> merely by reducing <separase> activity.

<cyclin B> destruction lowers <CDK1> activity, allowing phosphatases to reverse mitotic <phosphorylation> and promoting an interphase state. Activation of <anaphase-promoting complex> with <Cdh1> at low kinase activity can help keep cyclins low, while high kinase activity opposes that state. Thus low-CDK and high-CDK programmes are mutually antagonistic. Cyclin synthesis eventually provides a new activating input. Early experiments in extracts showed the importance of cyclin synthesis and destruction, including failure of normal exit with nondegradable cyclin.<The role of cyclin synthesis and degradation in the control of maturation promoting factor activity by Andrew W. Murray and others>[Cyclin synthesis, degradation and nondegradable variants]{ref} A bistable activation module combined with an appropriate delayed reset can generate an ordered oscillation; a bistable module held at constant input need not oscillate on its own.

<replication licensing> enforces another essential directionality. At low CDK activity, <origin recognition complex> with <Cdc6> and <Cdt1> loads <MCM2-7> double hexamers, establishing competence to replicate. In <S phase>, activating kinases promote origin firing while high CDK activity and additional controls prevent renewed licensing. In animal <biological cells>, <geminin> inhibition of <Cdt1> and regulated degradation help separate these states. Purified-protein reconstitution demonstrated loading of the replication-helicase precursor as a distinct biochemical event.<Concerted loading of Mcm2-7 double hexamers around DNA during DNA replication origin licensing by Dirk Remus and others>[Origin licensing by double-hexamer loading]{ref} Separating licensing from firing allows many origins to be prepared but prevents fired regions from being copied repeatedly during the same cycle. Lowering mitotic kinase activity must eventually restore licensing competence for the next cycle, rather than permit uncontrolled rereplication during the current <S phase>.

Checkpoint pathways modify or hold these switches when <DNA> is unreplicated, damaged or <chromosomes> are improperly attached; they do not supply the entire oscillator. Energy-consuming <phosphorylation> cycles, <ubiquitination>, <proteolysis> and resynthesis make the system a driven process, while feedback stabilizes distinct states. **Thresholds provide commitment, <hysteresis> provides memory, destruction and cleavage enforce selected transitions, and licensing/checkpoints couple those transitions to one correct round of replication and segregation.**

**Replication fidelity uses successive discrimination and repair systems, because no single <enzyme> detects every error.** The important distinctions are between an incorrect <base pair>, a slipped sequence register, the wrong sugar incorporated into <DNA>, a damaged template and an incompletely or incorrectly restored <chromosome>. Repair must also identify which information should be retained: recognizing a mismatch without strand discrimination could fix the wrong sequence.

A polymerase can select an incorrect <deoxynucleoside triphosphate> and insert the wrong base through imperfect discrimination, transient base configurations or an altered <active site> environment. If the mismatch is extended and survives into another replication round, it can become a stable <base substitution>. Many replicative polymerases possess a separate 3′→5′ <exonuclease> proofreading site. A mismatched primer end tends to slow extension and transfer toward this site; removal of the terminal <nucleotide> permits another attempt at correct synthesis. This is distinct from the polymerase's forward synthetic reaction and from proofreading in <biological translation>. In mice, selective disruption of <DNA polymerase> δ proofreading increased <mutation> and <tumour> susceptibility, demonstrating an organismal consequence of unrepaired copying errors.<High incidence of epithelial cancers in mice deficient for DNA polymerase δ proofreading by Robert E. Goldsby and others>[Proofreading-domain mutation, mutator phenotype and tumour development]{ref}

Post-replicative <DNA mismatch repair> corrects many errors that escape the polymerase. In <eukaryotes>, <MutSα>, formed by MSH2 and MSH6, recognizes base mismatches and many short insertion–deletion loops; <MutSβ>, containing MSH2 and MSH3, has an important role in larger loops. Recruitment of MutL complexes, strand incision, excision, resynthesis and ligation replaces a stretch of the newly synthesized strand. Nascent-strand discontinuities and replication-associated factors including <PCNA> help direct the process. Purified human activities reconstituted nick-directed repair from defined substrates, showing how a mismatch and a strand-break signal can support directional replacement.<Human mismatch repair: reconstitution of a nick-directed bidirectional reaction by Nicoleta Constantin and others>[Reconstitution using defined nicked substrates and human factors]{ref} The classic <Escherichia coli> <MutH> system instead uses transiently undermethylated daughter <DNA> at suitable sites; that methyl-directed mechanism is not a universal description of eukaryotic <DNA mismatch repair> or even of every bacterium.

<replication slippage> creates a different substrate. In repetitive sequence, transient misalignment can leave an extra loop in the nascent or template strand. A retained nascent-strand loop can produce an insertion; copying past a looped-out template segment can produce a deletion. Short repeats are therefore vulnerable to length changes, and coding-region insertions or deletions can cause a frameshift if their length is not a multiple of three. <DNA mismatch repair> recognizes many such loops, although substrate size and structure affect efficiency. Yeast mismatch-repair mutants destabilized simple repetitive tracts, linking repair defects with <microsatellite instability>.<Destabilization of tracts of simple repetitive DNA in yeast by mutations affecting DNA mismatch repair by Micheline Strand and others>[Repeat-length instability in repair mutants]{ref} This is a DNA-sequence frameshift mechanism, distinct from a ribosome changing <reading frame> during <biological translation>.

The layers can be represented by conditional escape probabilities,
$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{final}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{insert}}q_{\mathrm{proof}}q_{\mathrm{MMR}},
$$
where the second and third factors refer to escape among errors reaching the corresponding stage. As a purely illustrative calculation, $10^{-5}\times10^{-2}\times10^{-2}=10^{-9}$ per copied base. These are not asserted universal measured rates: mismatch identity, sequence, polymerase and repair state all matter. The multiplicative logic explains why disabling a later layer can reveal errors that had previously escaped detection without eliminating <DNA> synthesis itself.

Polymerases can also incorporate a <ribonucleotide> instead of a deoxyribonucleotide. <base pairing> may be correct, so ordinary mismatch recognition is insufficient. <RNase H2> can initiate <ribonucleotide excision repair> by incising near an embedded <ribonucleotide>, followed by <DNA> synthesis, removal of the displaced segment and ligation. Reconstitution established this dedicated pathway.<RNase H2-initiated ribonucleotide excision repair by Justin L. Sparks and others>[RNase H2-initiated incision and downstream repair reactions]{ref} <RNase H1> processing of an RNA–DNA hybrid should not be equated with removal of a single isolated <ribonucleotide>. Separately, normal <RNA> primers on <Okazaki fragments> must be processed and the remaining nicks sealed; persistent primers, flaps or unligated junctions threaten <chromosome> integrity even without an incorrect base.

A chemically damaged template can miscode or stall a fork. Small base lesions can be addressed by <base excision repair>, whereas <nucleotide excision repair> removes a damaged oligonucleotide around many bulky, helix-distorting lesions. These pathways repair chemical damage, not every mismatch made on an undamaged template. Replication across an oxidized base can require coordinated recognition of the lesion and the incorrectly paired partner so that repair does not convert damage into a fixed <mutation>. <translesion synthesis> permits passage across some lesions with specialized polymerases; it is damage tolerance, not necessarily lesion removal or error-free copying. <template switching> can provide another route around a block.

Stalled or collapsed forks can leave gaps, broken <DNA> and structural rearrangements. <homologous recombination> can use a sister chromatid to restore information and restart appropriate substrates; inappropriate pairing or <template switching> can instead change sequence organization. <non-homologous end joining> can restore continuity after a break but need not preserve every base, particularly when end processing is required. Replication checkpoints help stabilize forks and delay progression, while under-replication or rereplication requires control of origin use as well as lesion processing. Numerical chromosome-segregation errors are a related genome-stability problem but are not corrected by simply excising one mismatched base. **Base selectivity and proofreading act during synthesis, mismatch and <ribonucleotide> repair address distinct residual substrates, and damage/fork-repair pathways restore templates and continuity with different fidelity limits.**

= Solution
{parent=7}

**Yes: a <microRNA> can suppress <tumour> formation or progression by restraining an oncogenic programme, but that role must be demonstrated for a defined <microRNA>, tissue and genetic context.** Reduced expression in a <tumour> or growth inhibition after massive <microRNA mimic> transfection is suggestive rather than sufficient evidence of an endogenous tumour-suppressor function.

<microRNAs> are short regulatory RNAs whose guide strands associate with <Argonaute>. Recognition through sequence complementarity, often involving a <microRNA seed region> and a target's <3′ untranslated region>, can recruit translational repression and <RNA> decay machinery. Canonical precursor processing involves <Drosha> and <Dicer>, although not every <microRNA> follows every canonical step. A <microRNA> can affect many transcripts, and a transcript can receive input from several <microRNAs>. Therefore modest coordinated repression of several growth or survival factors can have a substantial phenotype even without complete elimination of one target. Suppression is a property of the regulatory network, not of an <RNA> being universally “anticancer”.

The <let-7> family supplies a mechanistic example. Experiments connecting let-7-responsive sequences in <Ras> transcripts with regulation of <Ras> expression linked a developmental regulatory <RNA> to a conserved growth pathway.<RAS is regulated by the let-7 microRNA family by Steven M. Johnson and others>[Target-site reporters, developmental genetics and human RAS regulation]{ref} Reduced <let-7> in lung <cancer> is compatible with loss of this restraint, but the inverse expression relationship alone would not show causation. Functional experiments restoring <let-7> in relevant lung-cancer systems suppressed <tumour> development, providing a stronger test of tumour-suppressive capacity.<Suppression of non-small cell lung tumor development by the let-7 microRNA family by Manish S. Kumar and others>[Functional let-7 manipulation in lung-tumour models]{ref} Expression should still be compared with a physiological range, and one must distinguish preventing <tumour> initiation from treating an established <tumour>.

Genetic loss gives a complementary direction of evidence. The <miR-15a> and <miR-16-1> cluster lies within a recurrently deleted region associated with <chronic lymphocytic leukaemia>. Deletion of the corresponding mouse regulatory region or the <microRNA> cluster increased B-cell proliferative abnormalities and produced clonal lymphoproliferative disease in a subset of animals.<DLEU2 microRNA cluster deletion study by Ulf Klein and others>[Separate deletions of the broader region and the miRNA cluster; B-cell proliferation and disease]{ref} Comparing the smaller cluster deletion with a larger deletion matters because neighbouring <genes> can contribute to a human copy-number association. Disease after specific <microRNA> loss supports endogenous restraint more directly than an expression signature does; incomplete penetrance and latency imply cooperation and context rather than an automatic one-step conversion of every <b cell>.

The p53–miR-34 connection illustrates both strength and limitation of a proposed pathway. <p53> activation can induce miR-34-family <transcription>, and ectopic <miR-34 family> can restrain proliferation or promote death in suitable <biological cells>, connecting a transcriptional <tumour> suppressor with post-transcriptional repression.<A microRNA component of the p53 tumour suppressor network by Lin He and others>[p53-dependent miR-34 induction and growth-related responses]{ref} Yet mice lacking all three <miR-34 family> members retained substantial p53-dependent arrest and <apoptosis> and did not show the predicted general increase in <tumour> susceptibility in the tested settings.<Intact p53-dependent responses in miR-34-deficient mice by Carla P. Concepcion and others>[Triple-family deletion and in vivo tumour-suppression tests]{ref} This does not prove that <miR-34 family> cannot suppress a <tumour> in any setting. It shows that a gain-of-function phenotype and placement downstream of <p53> do not establish that the <microRNA> family is an indispensable general mediator of <p53> <tumour> suppression.

A convincing analysis combines several evidence types. Measure mature guide <RNA> in defined malignant and normal <biological cell> populations, distinguishing it from precursor abundance and changes in stromal composition. Determine whether deletion, promoter silencing or altered processing explains loss. Reduce or delete the endogenous <microRNA> and test initiation or progression in a suitable model, with restoration from a controlled expression system. Compare related family members to expose redundancy. Use seed-mutant controls and multiple reagents to distinguish sequence-specific regulation from delivery toxicity or innate immune activation; deleting an entire processing <enzyme> is not a specific test of one <microRNA> because it affects many regulatory RNAs.

To identify consequential targets, combine binding evidence, early <RNA>/<protein> responses and target-site tests. A 3′-region reporter whose seed-complementary site is mutated supports direct sequence-dependent regulation, but the decisive endogenous test is to edit the site or express a suitably controlled <microRNA-resistant target> and ask whether it rescues the phenotype. Failure of one target to rescue can reflect regulation of several cooperating targets rather than absence of specificity. Conversely, rescuing only bulk proliferation does not establish which target controls invasion or <metastasis>. Measure relevant tissue phenotypes and target <protein> levels, not merely a pathway enrichment score from predicted targets.

Tumour-suppressive activity also does not guarantee an effective therapy. Delivery must reach the relevant <biological cells> and release the <RNA> into the correct compartment without excessive exposure of normal tissues. A clinical miR-34a <microRNA mimic> study encountered serious immune-mediated toxicity, illustrating that an endogenous regulatory rationale does not establish safe systemic replacement.<Phase 1 study of MRX34, a liposomal miR-34a mimic, in patients with advanced solid tumours by David S. Hong and others>[Clinical safety findings and limits of replacement therapy]{ref} Target abundance, isoform-specific 3′ regions and competing pathways can change the effect of the same <microRNA> between <tumours>. **<microRNAs> can be genuine <tumour> suppressors; the strongest evidence is specific endogenous loss, controlled restoration and mechanistic target tests, interpreted alongside genetic redundancy and tissue context.**
