=== Solution

Insert <hydrogen> <energy eigenstates> on either side of the <commutator>. Because $L_z$ is <Hermitian> and has <eigenvalue> $\hbar m$,
$$
0=\langle nlm|[L_z,z]|nl'm'\rangle=\hbar(m-m')\langle nlm|z|nl'm'\rangle.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle nlm|z|nl'm'\rangle\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ m=m'.}
$$
This is a **necessary <electric-dipole selection rule>**, not a sufficient condition for a nonzero <matrix element>. For example, $\langle200|z|200\rangle=0$ by <parity> even though $m=m'=0$. The angular factor $z=r\cos\theta$ additionally requires $l'=l\pm1$: multiplication of a <spherical harmonic> by $\cos\theta$ gives only harmonics with the same $m$ and adjacent $l$. The <commutator> alone establishes only the <magnetic quantum number> restriction; radial <integrals> and the remaining <electric-dipole selection rules for hydrogen> must still be considered.

{scope}

=== Solution

<Separation of variables> in the central <Coulomb potential> gives $\psi_{nlm}=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$. The <radial wavefunction> depends on **$n$ and $l$**, independently of $m$. For the <hydrogen radial wavefunction in the first excited shell>, put $\rho=r/a_0$. The <Bohr radius> is $a_0=4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2/(\mu e^2)$, where $\mu$ is the <electron>–<proton> <reduced mass>. Choose the real <phases>
$$
R_{20}=\frac{(2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}}{2\sqrt2\,a_0^{3/2}},\qquad R_{21}=\frac{\rho e^{-\rho/2}}{\sqrt{24}\,a_0^{3/2}}.
$$
Near the origin, $R_{20}\to1/(\sqrt2a_0^{3/2})$, whereas $R_{21}\sim r/(\sqrt{24}a_0^{5/2})$. More generally, regular <hydrogen> <radial wavefunctions> behave as $r^l$. At large $r$, both displayed functions have a <polynomial> factor proportional to $r$ multiplying $e^{-r/(2a_0)}$; $R_{20}$ approaches zero from below and $R_{21}$ from above with these <phase> choices.

The $2s$ <radial wavefunction> crosses zero once, at $r=2a_0$, and has its negative minimum at $4a_0$. The $2p$ <radial wavefunction> has no <radial node> at positive $r$, and reaches its maximum at $2a_0$. The count is $n-l-1$; neither the origin nor the limit at infinity is counted as a <radial node>. These are sketches of $R$, rather than of $rR$ or the <radial probability density> $r^2|R|^2$.

=== Solution

For the <Schrödinger equation> with a real <scalar potential> and no <vector potential>, the <probability current> is
$$
\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi-\Psi\nabla\Psi^*)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi).
$$
Together with the <probability density> $\rho=|\Psi|^2$, it satisfies the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0$. Substituting $\partial_x\Psi=ik\Psi$ gives
$$
\boxed{j=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2,\qquad \rho(x)=|A|^2,\qquad dP=|A|^2\,dx.}
$$
A complex <wavefunction> requires both its real and <imaginary parts> in the sketch. Choosing the irrelevant constant <phase> so that $A$ is real, they are $A\cos kx$ and $A\sin kx$, while the <probability density> is horizontal. It is the **<spatial phase gradient of a Schrodinger wavefunction>** $\partial_x\arg\Psi=k$ that determines the sign of the <probability current>. In particular, $e^{ikx}$ and $e^{-ikx}$ have the same <real part> but opposite <probability currents>, so the slope of the <real part> cannot determine the direction.

A nonzero <plane wave> on the whole real line is not square integrable. The displayed density is understood with box <normalization>, generalized <normalization>, or as a local description of a broad <wave packet>; it does not define a normalized <probability distribution> on the whole line.

{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <orthonormal> <energy eigenstates> of the <infinite square well>,
$$
\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad E_n=n^2E_1,\qquad E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}.
$$
The identity $\sin u\cos u=\tfrac12\sin2u$ resolves the initial <wavefunction> into $\Psi(0)=(2\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt5$. Each <energy eigenstate> acquires its own <dynamical phase>, so
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x,t_0)=\frac{2\phi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t_0/\hbar}+\phi_2(x)e^{-4iE_1t_0/\hbar}}{\sqrt5}}
$$
inside the well, and the <wavefunction> vanishes outside. The <energy> probabilities are $4/5$ and $1/5$ at every time. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\langle H\rangle_0=\langle H\rangle_{t_0}=\frac85E_1=\frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{5ma^2}.}
$$
For the left half, each diagonal <integral> $\int_0^{a/2}|\phi_n|^2dx$ is $1/2$, while
$$
\int_0^{a/2}\phi_1\phi_2\,dx=\frac4{3\pi}.
$$
The <interference> term therefore yields
$$
\boxed{P_{\mathrm{left}}(t_0)=\frac12+\frac{16}{15\pi}\cos\frac{3E_1t_0}{\hbar}.}
$$
This <probability> lies between approximately $0.1605$ and $0.8395$, with <period> $2\pi\hbar/(3E_1)$. A conserved <energy> distribution is compatible with time-dependent position probabilities because the relative <quantum phase> keeps evolving.

{scope}

=== Solution

Identify the nonrelativistic <mass> with the <rest mass>, $m=m_0$. Expanding the <relativistic kinetic energy> in $p^2/(m^2c^2)$ gives
$$
T=\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}+O(c^{-4}),\qquad H'=-\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}.
$$
First-order <perturbation theory> requires $\Delta E_0=\langle0|H'|0\rangle$. Put $\alpha=\sqrt{m\hbar\omega/2}$, so $p=i\alpha(a^\dagger-a)$. The annihilation relations are $a|0\rangle=0$ and $\langle0|a^\dagger=0$. In particular, $a^\dagger|0\rangle$ does **not** vanish. A short calculation retaining the operator ordering gives
$$
p^2|0\rangle=\alpha^2\bigl(|0\rangle-\sqrt2|2\rangle\bigr),\qquad
\langle0|p^4|0\rangle=\|p^2|0\rangle\|^2=3\alpha^4.
$$
Equivalently, of the ordered words in $(a^\dagger-a)^4$, only $aa^\dagger aa^\dagger$ and $aa a^\dagger a^\dagger$ have nonzero ground-state expectation values, respectively $1$ and $2$. Words with a <creation operator> on the far left or an <annihilation operator> on the far right vanish; the remaining unbalanced words also vanish. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_0=-\frac{3\hbar^2\omega^2}{32mc^2},\qquad E_0=\frac12\hbar\omega-\frac{3\hbar^2\omega^2}{32mc^2}+O(c^{-4}).}
$$
The correction is negative because the <relativistic kinetic energy> is smaller than $p^2/(2m)$ at fixed nonzero <momentum>. This <relativistic oscillator ground-state kinetic correction> requires $\hbar\omega\ll mc^2$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For two <spin one-half> particles, $s_1^2=s_2^2=3\hbar^2/4$. Squaring $\mathbf S=\mathbf s_1+\mathbf s_2$ gives
$$
\boxed{f=a+\frac b2\left(S^2-\frac32\hbar^2\right).}
$$
Take $a,b$ real so that $f$ is <Hermitian>. Since $[S^2,S_z]=0$, the displayed <polynomial> implies $[f,S^2]=[f,S_z]=0$. There is a common <orthonormal> <eigenbasis>, so all three observables can be simultaneously certain **in a common <eigenstate>**. Commutation does not mean that every <quantum superposition> has definite values of all three.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Suppress the fixed individual <quantum numbers> $s_1=s_2=1/2$. In the coupled <basis>, ordered on both rows and columns as $|1,1\rangle,|1,0\rangle,|1,-1\rangle,|0,0\rangle$, the total-spin <eigenvalues> give
$$
\boxed{[f]_{\mathrm{coupled}}=\operatorname{diag}\left(a+\frac{b\hbar^2}4,a+\frac{b\hbar^2}4,a+\frac{b\hbar^2}4,a-\frac{3b\hbar^2}4\right).}
$$
Here the first three entries form the <triplet state> sector and the last is the <singlet state>. For the product <basis>, use
$$
\mathbf s_1\cdot\mathbf s_2=s_{1z}s_{2z}+\frac12(s_{1+}s_{2-}+s_{1-}s_{2+}).
$$
The first term has diagonal entries $\hbar^2(1,-1,-1,1)/4$. Each nonzero exchange term flips $|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$ to $|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$ or back, with coefficient $\hbar^2/2$. With rows and columns ordered as $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$,
$$
\boxed{[f]_{\mathrm{product}}=aI+\frac{b\hbar^2}4\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\0&-1&2&0\\0&2&-1&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <change of basis> is made explicit by $|1,0\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|0,0\rangle=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with $|1,\pm1\rangle=|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$. The resulting <eigenvalues> reproduce the diagonal coupled <matrix>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <spin> <Hilbert space> is a <tensor product> of two two-dimensional spaces and therefore has <dimension> four. <Angular momentum addition> decomposes it as
$$
\boxed{S=1:\ M_S=1,0,-1;\qquad S=0:\ M_S=0.}
$$
These are three <orthogonal> <triplet states> and one <singlet state>. For identical <electrons> the **complete** <wavefunction> must also satisfy <fermionic exchange symmetry>: a symmetric triplet <spin> factor accompanies an antisymmetric spatial factor, whereas the antisymmetric singlet <spin> factor accompanies a symmetric spatial factor. If both <electrons> occupy the same <spatial orbital>, only the singlet <spin> factor is allowed. The four-dimensional <spin> space does not assert four allowed <spin> states for that particular spatial occupation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Expanding the total <angular momentum> and replacing transverse components by <ladder operators> gives
$$
\boxed{S_z=s_{1z}+s_{2z},\qquad S^2=s_1^2+s_2^2+2s_{1z}s_{2z}+s_{1+}s_{2-}+s_{1-}s_{2+}.}
$$
The <particle exchange operator> obeys $P_{12}^2=I$ and $P_{12}s_{1\alpha}P_{12}=s_{2\alpha}$, with the reverse relation for index $2$. These statements also apply to the <ladder operators>. Exchanging the indices preserves $s_1^2+s_2^2$ and $s_{1z}+s_{2z}$; it interchanges the last two terms in $S^2$. Operators on different tensor factors commute. Hence
$$
P_{12}S^2P_{12}=S^2,\qquad P_{12}S_zP_{12}=S_z,
\qquad\boxed{[P_{12},S^2]=[P_{12},S_z]=0.}
$$
The full particle exchange includes the <spin> coordinates as well as the spatial coordinates.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $\Psi=(|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle+|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Each term has cancelling $z$ components, so $S_z\Psi=0$. In the formula for $S^2$, the first three terms contribute $(3/2-1/2)\hbar^2\Psi=\hbar^2\Psi$. The two ladder products exchange the two terms and contribute another $\hbar^2\Psi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S^2\Psi=2\hbar^2\Psi,\quad S=1,\quad M_S=0,\quad P_{12}\Psi=+\Psi.}
$$
This <spin> state is symmetric; the accompanying spatial state must be antisymmetric for two <electrons>. A labelled product $|\downarrow_1\uparrow_2\rangle$ alone is not an <eigenstate> of <spin> exchange: its exchanged <vector> is <orthogonal> to it. Thus it cannot supply a definite exchange symmetry when multiplied by a fixed symmetric spatial factor.

This does **not** prohibit opposite <spins> in different <spatial orbitals>. For example, with <orthonormal> orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$, the <Slater determinant>
$$
\frac1{\sqrt2}\left[\phi_a(\mathbf r_1)\phi_b(\mathbf r_2)|\downarrow_1\uparrow_2\rangle-\phi_b(\mathbf r_1)\phi_a(\mathbf r_2)|\uparrow_1\downarrow_2\rangle\right]
$$
is a valid antisymmetric two-electron state. What fails is treating persistent particle labels as physically distinguishable identities without the required antisymmetrization.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <conditional spin measurement of identical fermions> addressing the $z$ component in one spatial mode, the <Born rule> gives
$$
\boxed{P(\uparrow)=\frac12.}
$$
Conditioned on this result, the mode <spin> state is $|\uparrow_a\downarrow_b\rangle$; the other outcome produces $|\downarrow_a\uparrow_b\rangle$. The <spin> product is not a spin-exchange <eigenstate>. The <measurement> has selected an addressed mode and recorded an outcome, so this conditional <spin> description distinguishes the measured subsystem.

The full <electron> <wavefunction> still obeys <fermionic exchange symmetry>. For example, the conditional spin-up outcome in spatial mode $a$ is represented in particle coordinates by
$$
\frac1{\sqrt2}\left[\phi_a(\mathbf r_1)\phi_b(\mathbf r_2)|\uparrow_1\downarrow_2\rangle-\phi_b(\mathbf r_1)\phi_a(\mathbf r_2)|\downarrow_1\uparrow_2\rangle\right].
$$
It is antisymmetric under full particle exchange. Thus the loss of a definite symmetry of the **conditional <spin> product** does not mean that <measurement> turns identical <electrons> into distinguishable particles. If the outcome is discarded, the mode <spin> state is instead the equal <mixture> of the two opposite-spin products. The <measurement> must specify which spatial mode is addressed for the conditional statement to be operationally meaningful.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Hermiticity of the Cartesian <angular momentum> components gives
$$
\boxed{L_+^\dagger=L_-,\qquad L_-^\dagger=L_+.}
$$
The <angular momentum commutation relations> $[L_i,L_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$ imply
$$
\boxed{[L^2,L_\pm]=0,\qquad [L_z,L_\pm]=\pm\hbar L_\pm.}
$$
For example, $[L_z,L_x+iL_y]=i\hbar L_y+\hbar L_x=\hbar L_+$. The two terms in $[L_x^2+L_y^2+L_z^2,L_i]$ cancel in pairs, giving the first <commutator>. Also $[L_+,L_-]=2\hbar L_z$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <spherical harmonics> satisfy
$$
L^2Y_l^m=\hbar^2l(l+1)Y_l^m,\qquad L_zY_l^m=\hbar mY_l^m,
\quad l=0,1,\ldots,\quad -l\le m\le l.
$$
Commuting $L^2$ or $L_z$ past $L_\pm$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{L^2(L_\pm Y_l^m)=\hbar^2l(l+1)(L_\pm Y_l^m),\qquad L_z(L_\pm Y_l^m)=\hbar(m\pm1)(L_\pm Y_l^m).}
$$
For a nonzero result these are the <eigenvalue equations> with unchanged $l$ and shifted <magnetic quantum number>. Taking the squared <norm>, using $L_\mp L_\pm=L^2-L_z^2\mp\hbar L_z$, fixes the conventional coefficient:
$$
L_\pm Y_l^m=\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,Y_l^{m\pm1}.
$$
At the appropriate endpoint the coefficient is zero. The zero <vector> is not itself an <eigenstate>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

There is no allowed <spherical harmonic> with $m=l+1$, so $L_+Y_l^l=0$. The equation $-i\hbar\partial_\phi Y=\hbar lY$ first gives $Y=f(\theta)e^{il\phi}$. Combining the differential expressions for $L_x,L_y$ yields
$$
L_+=\hbar e^{i\phi}(\partial_\theta+i\cot\theta\,\partial_\phi).
$$
Its annihilation of the highest-weight state becomes
$$
f'(\theta)-l\cot\theta\,f(\theta)=0.
$$
Integrating $f'/f=l\cot\theta$ gives $f=C\sin^l\theta$, with the constant solution included for $l=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Y_l^l(\theta,\phi)=C\sin^l\theta\,e^{il\phi}.}
$$
The <phase> $e^{il\phi}$ is single valued for <integral> orbital <quantum number> $l$; <normalization> fixes only $|C|$, leaving a conventional constant <phase>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Direct <integration> over <solid angle> gives
$$
1=2\pi|C|^2\int_0^\pi\sin^{2l+1}\theta\,d\theta
=4\pi|C|^2\frac{(2^l l!)^2}{(2l+1)!}.
$$
Thus $|C|=\sqrt{(2l+1)!/(4\pi)}/(2^l l!)$. Choosing the usual alternating sign convention,
$$
\boxed{Y_l^l=(-1)^l\frac{\sqrt{(2l+1)!}}{2^l l!\sqrt{4\pi}}\sin^l\theta\,e^{il\phi}.}
$$
Apply $L_-$ successively and divide by its ladder coefficient at each step. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{Y_l^m=\sqrt{\frac{(l+m)!}{(2l)!(l-m)!}}\,\hbar^{-(l-m)}(L_-)^{l-m}Y_l^l.}
$$
The <normalization> follows because the product of the squared ladder coefficients is $\hbar^{2(l-m)}(2l)!(l-m)!/(l+m)!$. This constructs every <magnetic sublevel>, including negative $m$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Separate the <center-of-mass motion> from the <relative motion>. Two equal <quark> masses give <reduced mass> $\mu=m_q/2$. Replacing the <hydrogen> Coulomb strength by $a>0$ gives the Coulomb binding energies and length scale
$$
\boxed{E_n^{(0)}=-\frac{m_q a^2}{4\hbar^2n^2},\qquad a_q=\frac{\hbar^2}{\mu a}=\frac{2\hbar^2}{m_q a}.}
$$
The factor of two in the <reduced mass> matters: using $m_q$ directly would double the <binding energy>. These are relative binding energies measured from the free <quark>–<antiquark> threshold; the rest <energy> $2m_qc^2$ is excluded.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Ignoring <spin> and working in the fixed <quarkonium> internal sector, the orbital degeneracy is $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{g_1=1\quad(1s),\qquad g_2=4\quad(2s\text{ and three }2p\text{ states}).}
$$
No extra <spin> or colour multiplicity is being counted. The degeneracy between different $l$ at fixed $n$ is special to the <Coulomb potential>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <linear confinement correction to Coulomb bound states> uses $H'=br$, and its <first-order energy correction> in a normalized Coulomb <eigenstate> is $b\langle r\rangle$. Replace $a_0$ by $a_q$ in the <hydrogen> <wavefunctions>. The normalized <spherical harmonics> remove the angular <integral>, leaving
$$
\langle r\rangle_{nl}=\int_0^\infty r^3|R_{nl}(r)|^2\,dr.
$$
For $1s$, the <integral> of $r^3e^{-2r/a_q}$ yields
$$
\langle r\rangle_{1s}=\frac4{a_q^3}\frac{3!}{(2/a_q)^4}=\frac32a_q.
$$
For $2s$, put $u=r/a_q$ and square the full radial <polynomial>, including its cross term:
$$
\langle r\rangle_{2s}=\frac{a_q}{2}\int_0^\infty u^3\left(1-u+\frac{u^2}4\right)e^{-u}du
=\frac{a_q}{2}\left(3!-4!+\frac{5!}4\right)=6a_q.
$$
For $2p$, the same calculation gives
$$
\langle r\rangle_{2p}=\frac{a_q}{24}\int_0^\infty u^5e^{-u}du=5a_q.
$$
Consequently the first two Coulomb levels become, to first order,
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
E_{1s}&=-\frac{m_qa^2}{4\hbar^2}+\frac32ba_q+O(b^2),\\
E_{2s}&=-\frac{m_qa^2}{16\hbar^2}+6ba_q+O(b^2),\\
E_{2p,m}&=-\frac{m_qa^2}{16\hbar^2}+5ba_q+O(b^2),\qquad m=-1,0,1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Thus the second level splits into a singlet orbital level and a triply degenerate orbital level, with $E_{2s}-E_{2p}=ba_q+O(b^2)$. For the confining sign $b>0$, $2p$ lies lower than $2s$: its smaller mean radius gives the smaller positive correction. The expansion assumes these corrections and the induced couplings to other principal shells are small compared with the relevant Coulomb <energy> gaps; $ba_q^2/a\ll1$ is the governing small parameter for fixed low $n$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The unperturbed $n=2$ level **is degenerate**. The useful point is that a central perturbation is already diagonal within its usual orbital <eigenbasis>:
$$
\langle nlm|br|nl'm'\rangle
=b\left(\int_0^\infty r^3R_{nl}^*R_{nl'}\,dr\right)\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}.
$$
Angular <orthogonality> removes the possible mixing of $2s$ and $2p$, and also the mixing of different <magnetic sublevels>. Therefore the <matrix> required by <degenerate perturbation theory> needs no further diagonalization: its diagonal entries are precisely the expectation values above. It would be incorrect to justify the calculation by claiming that the unperturbed spectrum was nondegenerate.

For $b=0$ the <energy> needs only $n$. For a nonzero central confinement term the <energy> generally needs **$n,l$**, but remains independent of $m$. The <magnetic quantum number> is still needed to specify an individual orbital <eigenstate>, rather than just its <energy>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With $\mathbf B=B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $S_z=(\hbar/2)\sigma_z$,
$$
H_0=\mu_BB_0\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{E_+=\mu_BB_0,\quad |+\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix};\qquad E_-=-\mu_BB_0,\quad |-\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For $B_0>0$, <spin> down is the lower <energy eigenstate>. The negative <electron magnetic moment> is already accounted for in the positive coefficient multiplying $\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf B$ in the given <Hamiltonian>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the ordered <basis> $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$, the transverse <magnetic field> produces
$$
V(t)=\mu_BB_1(\sigma_x\cos\omega t+\sigma_y\sin\omega t)
=\boxed{\hbar\gamma\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\omega t}\\e^{i\omega t}&0\end{pmatrix}},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\mu_BB_1}{\hbar}.
$$
The off-diagonal complex exponentials follow directly from $\cos\omega t\mp i\sin\omega t$. This <matrix> is <Hermitian> at every time.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Insert the expansion with amplitudes $c_1,c_2$ and the two unperturbed <dynamical phases> into $i\hbar\dot\Psi=(H_0+V)\Psi$. Differentiation of the explicit <phases> cancels $H_0$, leaving
$$
i\dot c_1=\gamma e^{-i(\omega-\omega_0)t}c_2,\qquad
i\dot c_2=\gamma e^{i(\omega-\omega_0)t}c_1,
\qquad\omega_0=\frac{2\mu_BB_0}{\hbar}.
$$
Equivalently, with $\Omega=(\omega-\omega_0)/2$,
$$
\boxed{\dot c_1=-i\gamma e^{-2i\Omega t}c_2,\qquad\dot c_2=-i\gamma e^{2i\Omega t}c_1.}
$$
These interaction-picture equations conserve $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2$. They are exact for the <circularly rotating magnetic drive>; a <rotating-wave approximation> has not been used.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Differentiate $\dot c_2=-i\gamma e^{2i\Omega t}c_1$ and substitute the equation for $\dot c_1$. The result is
$$
\boxed{\ddot c_2-2i\Omega\dot c_2+\gamma^2c_2=0.}
$$
Putting $c_2=e^{i\Omega t}u$ removes the first <derivative> and gives $\ddot u+\kappa^2u=0$, where $\kappa=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\gamma^2}$. For $\kappa>0$ the general solution is
$$
c_2=e^{i\Omega t}(A\cos\kappa t+B\sin\kappa t).
$$
The initial <spinor> fixes $c_1(0)=1,c_2(0)=0$, and the **first-order coupled equation** fixes the second initial datum $\dot c_2(0)=-i\gamma$. Thus $A=0$, $B=-i\gamma/\kappa$, and
$$
\boxed{c_2(t)=-i\frac\gamma\kappa e^{i\Omega t}\sin\kappa t,\qquad c_1(t)=e^{-i\Omega t}\left(\cos\kappa t+i\frac\Omega\kappa\sin\kappa t\right).}
$$
Their squared magnitudes sum to one. If both $\Omega$ and $\gamma$ vanish, the initial amplitudes simply remain $(1,0)$; the formulas have that continuous limit.

**Source convention correction.** For positive $\gamma$, the printed expression for $c_2$ lacks the factor $-i$ required by the stated <basis> and initial <vector>. A concrete test is its <derivative> at $t=0$: the printed expression gives $+\gamma$, whereas the <Schrödinger equation> gives $-i\gamma$. Multiplying only $c_2$ by a <phase> changes the relative <quantum phase>. One may change the overall <phase> of the entire <spinor>, but must then transform $c_1$ and its initial amplitude as well. The printed modulus nevertheless gives the correct <transition probability>.

==== a

===== Solution

On Rabi resonance, $\omega=\omega_0$ and $\Omega=0$, so
$$
\boxed{P_-(t)=|c_2(t)|^2=\sin^2(\gamma t).}
$$
For nonzero drive the <probability> starts at zero, reaches unity at $t=\pi/(2|\gamma|)$, and repeats with <period> $\pi/|\gamma|$. The population oscillation therefore has <angular frequency> $2|\gamma|$. This is complete resonant Rabi transfer; a drive of zero strength gives no transition.

===== Solution

For nonzero <Rabi detuning>,
$$
\boxed{P_-(t)=\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma^2+\Omega^2}\sin^2\left(\sqrt{\gamma^2+\Omega^2}\,t\right).}
$$
The sketch starts at zero and oscillates between zero and $\gamma^2/(\gamma^2+\Omega^2)<1$, with <period> $\pi/\sqrt{\gamma^2+\Omega^2}$ when the coupling is nonzero. Relative to resonance, the oscillations have a smaller maximum and a shorter <period> at fixed coupling. The sign of the <Rabi detuning> changes the amplitudes' <phases> but leaves this <transition probability> unchanged. For $|\Omega|\gg|\gamma|$, its maximum is approximately $\gamma^2/\Omega^2$, so population transfer is strongly suppressed. The plotted detuned example has $|\Omega|=|\gamma|$, giving maximum $1/2$.
