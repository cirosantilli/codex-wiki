Apply the surjectivity of $f(Y)$ to the <identity morphism> $1_Y$. It gives a <morphism of schemes> $g:Y\to X$ satisfying $f\circ g=1_Y$. To obtain the other identity, compare $g\circ f$ and $1_X$ in $X(X)$. Their images under $f(X)$ agree, since
$$
f\circ(g\circ f)=(f\circ g)\circ f=f=f\circ1_X.
$$
The injectivity of $f(X)$ therefore gives $g\circ f=1_X$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is an isomorphism of schemes, with inverse }g.}
$$
Only surjectivity for the test <scheme> $Y$ and injectivity for the test <scheme> $X$ were needed. This is the isomorphism-detection argument behind the <Yoneda lemma> for the <functor of points>.

**No.** Let $k=\mathbb F_2$ be the <field with two elements>, put $A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, and take the <closed immersion>
$$
\boxed{f:\operatorname{Spec}k\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A}
$$
induced by the <ring homomorphism> $q:A\twoheadrightarrow k$ with $q(\varepsilon)=0$.

For every <field> $K$, a <ring homomorphism> $\alpha:A\to K$ must send $\varepsilon$ to zero: $\alpha(\varepsilon)^2=0$, and a <field> has no nonzero <nilpotent elements>. Consequently $\alpha$ factors uniquely through $q$. Passing from <ring homomorphisms> to <morphisms of schemes> between <affine schemes> gives a <bijection>
$$
f(\operatorname{Spec}K):\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(\operatorname{Spec}K,\operatorname{Spec}k)
\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Sch}}(\operatorname{Spec}K,\operatorname{Spec}A).
$$
This concerns all <morphisms of schemes>, without requiring them to be over $k$: if $K$ does not have <characteristic> two, both sets are empty; otherwise each contains exactly one element.

Nevertheless, $f$ is not an <isomorphism of schemes>. Its map on <global sections> is $q$, whose <kernel of a ring homomorphism> contains the nonzero element $\varepsilon$, so $q$ is not a <ring isomorphism>. The target is the <nonreduced scheme> defined by the <dual numbers>, whereas the source is a <reduced scheme>. This illustrates why <field-valued points do not detect nilpotents>.

**Yes.** First, the hypothesis implies that $f(T)$ is injective for every <scheme> $T$, even when $T$ is not an <affine scheme>. Indeed, suppose $a,b:T\to X$ satisfy $f\circ a=f\circ b$. Choose an <affine open cover> $T=\bigcup_\lambda V_\lambda$. On each $V_\lambda$, injectivity of $f(V_\lambda)$ gives $a|_{V_\lambda}=b|_{V_\lambda}$. By <gluing morphisms of schemes>, $a=b$.

Now choose an <affine open cover> $Y=\bigcup_i U_i$, and denote its inclusion maps by $j_i:U_i\hookrightarrow Y$. Surjectivity of $f(U_i)$ gives <morphisms of schemes> $g_i:U_i\to X$ with $f\circ g_i=j_i$. On $U_i\cap U_j$, the two restrictions have the same composite with $f$. By the injectivity just established for arbitrary test <schemes>,
$$
g_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}=g_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}.
$$
In particular, the overlaps need not be <affine schemes>. <Gluing morphisms of schemes> supplies $g:Y\to X$ with $g|_{U_i}=g_i$, and $f\circ g=1_Y$ because this holds on every $U_i$.

Finally,
$$
f\circ(g\circ f)=f=f\circ1_X.
$$
Injectivity of $f(X)$, already proved without an affineness assumption, gives $g\circ f=1_X$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is an isomorphism of schemes}.}
$$
This proves the relevant instance of <affine test schemes determine scheme morphisms>: the restricted <functor of points> still retains the information needed to construct the inverse.

The <localization of a ring> $R_r$ is generated over $R$ by the single element $1/r$, since every fraction can be written as
$$
\frac{a}{r^n}=\phi(a)(1/r)^n.
$$
The <presentation of a localization at one element> is
$$
\boxed{R_r\cong R[t]/(rt-1),\qquad t\longmapsto1/r.}
$$
To verify the presentation even when $R$ has <zero divisors>, observe that $r$ is invertible in $R[t]/(rt-1)$ with inverse $t$. Hence the map from $R$ to this <quotient ring> extends uniquely to $R_r$. This extension and the displayed map are inverse <ring homomorphisms>: they agree on $R$ and send the designated inverse of $r$ to the designated inverse of $r$.

Thus $R_r$ is a <finitely generated algebra over a ring>, and the criterion for a <morphism of finite type> between <affine schemes> applies to $u:\operatorname{Spec}R_r\to\operatorname{Spec}R$. There is just one <affine scheme> in the required cover of the source, with one algebra generator. Therefore **$u$ is of finite type**. If $r$ is <nilpotent>, the same presentation is the <zero ring>, and the source is the empty <scheme>; the conclusion still holds.

Put $C=\overline{\{z\}}$, where the <closure> is taken in the underlying <topological space> of $X$. It is nonempty because it contains $z$.

Suppose $C=A\cup B$ for two <closed subsets> of $C$ in the <subspace topology>. Since $C$ is a <closed subset> of $X$, both $A$ and $B$ are also <closed subsets> of $X$. The point $z$ lies in one of them, say $A$. By the defining minimality of the <closure>, every <closed subset> of $X$ containing $z$ contains $C$. Hence $C\subseteq A$, so $A=C$.

Thus $C$ cannot be expressed as the union of two proper <closed subsets>, proving that **$C$ is an irreducible closed subset**. This proves <closure of a point is irreducible> in every <topological space>; the <scheme> assumption is unnecessary. Equivalently, $z$ is a <generic point> of its own <closure>.

**Yes: every scheme is a Kolmogorov space.** Choose an <affine open subscheme> $V=\operatorname{Spec}A$ containing $x$. If $y\notin V$, take $U=V$.

Otherwise, the distinct points $x,y\in V$ correspond to distinct <prime ideals> $\mathfrak p_x,\mathfrak p_y\subseteq A$. There is an element in one of these <prime ideals> and not the other. After interchanging the names of the two points if necessary, choose
$$
a\in\mathfrak p_x\setminus\mathfrak p_y.
$$
The <principal open subscheme>
$$
\boxed{U=D(a)=\{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A:a\notin\mathfrak p\}}
$$
contains $y$ and does not contain $x$. It is an <open subset> of $V$, hence an <open subset> of $X$ because $V$ is open in $X$. This establishes $(*)$ in every case, proving <schemes are Kolmogorov spaces>.

Write $\mathbb P^r=\mathbb P^r_k$ over its ground <field>; the argument also works over any <Noetherian> affine base. Since $\iota$ is a <closed immersion>, $X$ is a <noetherian scheme> and a <quasi-compact scheme>. Let
$$
L=\iota^*\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^r}(1),\qquad F(n)=F\otimes_{\mathcal O_X}L^{\otimes n}.
$$
Here the <pullback of a sheaf of modules> makes $L$ an <invertible sheaf>, the restriction of the <twisting sheaf on projective space>. It is an <ample line bundle>.

By <global generation by sufficiently positive twists>, choose $n\geq0$ such that $F(n)$ is a <globally generated sheaf>. Finitely many <global sections> suffice: at each point, finite generation of the <stalk> of the <coherent sheaf> $F(n)$ lets us select finitely many generating germs of <global sections>. Those sections generate on a neighbourhood, because their <cokernel sheaf> is a <coherent sheaf> with zero <stalk> at that point. A finite subcover, using that $X$ is a <quasi-compact scheme>, supplies finitely many <global sections> generating everywhere. This is <finite global generation on a quasi-compact scheme>, and gives a surjective <sheaf morphism>
$$
\mathcal O_X^{\oplus m}\twoheadrightarrow F(n).
$$

Tensor with $(L^\vee)^{\otimes n}$, where $L^\vee$ is the <dual of a sheaf>. Here <tensoring with an invertible sheaf is exact>: locally the <tensor product of sheaves> is tensoring with a free module of rank one. Using the permitted identity $L\otimes L^\vee\cong\mathcal O_X$, we obtain
$$
\boxed{G=\bigl((L^\vee)^{\otimes n}\bigr)^{\oplus m}=\mathcal O_X(-n)^{\oplus m},\qquad G\twoheadrightarrow F\longrightarrow0.}
$$
A finite <direct sum of sheaves> of <invertible sheaves> is a <locally free sheaf> of finite rank, and it is a <coherent sheaf> on the <noetherian scheme> $X$. This constructs the required $G$. No regularity or smoothness assumption on $X$ is needed.

Use homogeneous coordinates $x_0,\ldots,x_r$ on <projective space> and let
$$
U_i=X\cap D_+(x_i),\qquad 0\leq i\leq r.
$$
These $r+1$ <open subschemes> cover $X$. Each is a <closed subscheme> of the <affine scheme> $D_+(x_i)$, hence is an <affine scheme>.

Every nonempty finite intersection is affine as well. Indeed, inside $D_+(x_{i_0})$ the intersection with $D_+(x_{i_1}),\ldots,D_+(x_{i_p})$ is the <principal open subscheme> obtained by inverting
$$
\prod_{j=1}^p\frac{x_{i_j}}{x_{i_0}}.
$$
Intersecting it with the <closed subscheme> $X$ gives $U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}$, again an <affine scheme>.

For any <quasi-coherent sheaf> $F$, <vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme> makes every such intersection acyclic for $F$. The <acyclic cover theorem> therefore identifies the <sheaf cohomology> of $F$ with the <Čech cohomology> of this cover. Its alternating <Čech cochain complex> has terms
$$
\check C^p(\mathcal U,F)=\prod_{0\leq i_0<\cdots<i_p\leq r}\Gamma(U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p},F),
$$
which vanish for $p>r$: one cannot choose $p+1$ distinct indices from $r+1$ indices. Consequently
$$
\boxed{H^k(X,F)=0\quad(k>r),\qquad N=\max\{1,r\}.}
$$
The choice $N=\max\{1,r\}$ meets the strict requirement $N>0$ even when $r=0$. It is independent of $F$, and applies to all <quasi-coherent sheaves>, including those which are not <coherent sheaves>. This is the <cohomological dimension bound from an affine cover>.

Fix a <coherent sheaf> $F$ and an integer $k>M$. Set $K_0=F$. Repeatedly apply <3/a> to construct <short exact sequences of sheaves>
$$
0\longrightarrow K_{j+1}\longrightarrow G_j\longrightarrow K_j\longrightarrow0,
$$
where each $G_j$ is a <coherent sheaf> and a <locally free sheaf>. Each <kernel sheaf> $K_{j+1}$ is a <coherent sheaf> because $X$ is a <noetherian scheme>. Thus the construction can be repeated as many times as needed; it does not require the <kernel sheaves> themselves to be <locally free sheaves>.

For every integer $q>M$, the <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology> contains
$$
H^q(X,G_j)\longrightarrow H^q(X,K_j)\xrightarrow{\ \delta_j\ }H^{q+1}(X,K_{j+1})\longrightarrow H^{q+1}(X,G_j).
$$
Both outer groups vanish by the hypothesis, since $q$ and $q+1$ exceed $M$. Hence $\delta_j$ is an <isomorphism>. Iterating this <dimension shifting in sheaf cohomology> gives
$$
H^k(X,F)\cong H^{k+1}(X,K_1)\cong\cdots\cong H^{k+s}(X,K_s).
$$

Choose $s\geq0$ with $k+s>N$, where $N$ is the uniform bound from <3/b>. The <coherent sheaf> $K_s$ is a <quasi-coherent sheaf>, so <3/b> makes the last group zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H^k(X,F)=0\quad\text{for every coherent sheaf }F\text{ and every }k>M.}
$$
The argument uses a finite initial segment of a <locally free resolution>. There is no need to assume that every <coherent sheaf> has a finite <locally free resolution>: the uniform bound from <3/b> is what terminates the argument.
