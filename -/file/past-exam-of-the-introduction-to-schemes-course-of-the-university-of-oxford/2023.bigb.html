Write $h_T(S)=\operatorname{Hom}(S,T)$ for the <functor of points> of a <scheme> $T$. A <subfunctor> $H\subseteq F$ is an <open subfunctor> if every <natural transformation> $h_T\to F$ has a <pullback> represented by an <open subscheme> $U\subseteq T$:
$$
\boxed{h_T\times_F H\cong h_U,\qquad U\hookrightarrow T\text{ an open immersion}.}
$$
The displayed <natural isomorphism> must identify the projection to $h_T$ with the <natural transformation> induced by $U\hookrightarrow T$. Equivalently, for each $\xi\in F(T)$, there is an <open subscheme> $U\subseteq T$ such that a <morphism of schemes> $a:S\to T$ factors through $U$ exactly when $F(a)(\xi)\in H(S)$. The <pullback> is taken in the <functor category>, so this tests arbitrary families, rather than just underlying points.

A <Zariski cover> of a <scheme> $T$ is a family of <open immersions> $(j_i:U_i\hookrightarrow T)_{i\in I}$ whose images cover its underlying <topological space>:
$$
\boxed{T=\bigcup_{i\in I}j_i(U_i).}
$$
There is no finiteness requirement. Intersections used in the <sheaf on a site> condition are the <pullbacks> $U_i\times_TU_j$, identified with the corresponding <open subschemes> $U_i\cap U_j$.

A <presheaf> $F:\mathsf{Sch}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Set}$ is a <Zariski sheaf> when every <Zariski cover> $(U_i\to T)$ gives an <equalizer> diagram
$$
\boxed{F(T)\longrightarrow\prod_iF(U_i)\rightrightarrows\prod_{i,j}F(U_i\times_TU_j).}
$$
Thus compatible local elements glue to a unique element of $F(T)$. Compatibility means that the two restrictions agree on every intersection. This includes the empty <Zariski cover> of the empty <scheme>, so $F(\varnothing)$ is a singleton.

The <open affine representability criterion> says that $F$ is a <functor represented by a scheme> exactly when $F$ is a <Zariski sheaf> and admits a family of <open subfunctors> $F_i\subseteq F$, each represented by an <affine scheme>, which covers $F$ in the following sense: for every <scheme> $T$ and every $\xi\in F(T)$, the <open subschemes> representing $h_T\times_FF_i$ cover $T$. In particular, the covering condition does not demand that every $\xi$ lie in one $F_i(T)$.

In symbols, for <affine schemes> $X_i$,
$$
\boxed{F_i\cong h_{X_i},\quad F_i\subseteq F\text{ open},\quad \bigcup_i(T\times_FF_i)=T,\quad F\text{ a Zariski sheaf}.}
$$
The family is indexed by a set. For necessity, take an <affine open cover> of a representing <scheme>; <morphisms of schemes> glue over a <Zariski cover>. For sufficiency, $F_i\times_FF_j$ is represented by an <open subscheme> of each $X_i,X_j$. The two descriptions identify these overlaps and satisfy the compatibility on triple overlaps because all maps come from the inclusions into $F$. Apply <gluing of schemes along open subschemes> to obtain $X$. Local identification gives $h_X\to F$, and the <Zariski sheaf> condition makes this a <natural isomorphism>: its elements can be constructed and compared on the pulled-back <open cover>.

A <morphism of schemes> $f:X\to Y$ defines a <natural transformation> $g_f:g_X\to g_Y$ by $u\mapsto f\circ u$. We construct the inverse correspondence.

Let $\alpha:g_X\to g_Y$ be a <natural transformation>, and choose an <affine open cover> $(j_i:U_i\hookrightarrow X)$. Regard $j_i$ as an element of $g_X(U_i)$ and set
$$
f_i=\alpha_{U_i}(j_i):U_i\longrightarrow Y.
$$
Here and below the <equivalence of categories> in the question lets us write the restricted <functor of points> on <affine schemes>, contravariantly, rather than on <commutative rings>, covariantly. If $W\subseteq U_i\cap U_j$ is an <affine open subscheme>, <naturality> for its two inclusions gives
$$
f_i|_W=\alpha_W(W\hookrightarrow X)=f_j|_W.
$$
Such $W$ cover the overlap even when that overlap is not an <affine scheme>. Consequently the $f_i$ agree and glue to a <morphism of schemes> $f:X\to Y$.

It remains to check all elements of the restricted <functor of points>, not only the chosen inclusions. Given an <affine scheme> $T$ and $u:T\to X$, cover each $u^{-1}(U_i)$ by <affine open subschemes> $V$. On such a $V$, factor $u|_V=j_i\circ v$. Applying <naturality> first to $v:V\to U_i$ and then to $V\hookrightarrow T$ gives
$$
\alpha_T(u)|_V=\alpha_V(u|_V)=\alpha_{U_i}(j_i)\circ v=f\circ u|_V.
$$
These local equalities prove $\alpha_T(u)=f\circ u$. The glued $f$ is unique, since its restrictions to all $U_i$ are prescribed. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathsf{Sch}}(X,Y)\cong\operatorname{Nat}(g_X,g_Y),}
$$
so the assignment is <fully faithful>.

If $g_X\cong g_Y$, the <natural isomorphism> and its inverse correspond to <morphisms of schemes> $f:X\to Y$ and $h:Y\to X$. Their compositions induce the identity <natural transformations>, and faithfulness implies $hf=\operatorname{id}_X$, $fh=\operatorname{id}_Y$. Conversely, an <isomorphism of schemes> induces a <natural isomorphism>. Thus
$$
\boxed{X\cong Y\iff g_X\cong g_Y.}
$$

For an <idempotent> $e\in R$, define the <principal open subset> $D(e)\subseteq\operatorname{Spec}R$. Since $e(1-e)=0$, a <prime ideal> contains at least one of $e,1-e$, and it cannot contain both. Therefore
$$
D(e)=V(1-e),\qquad \operatorname{Spec}R=D(e)\sqcup D(1-e).
$$
Thus $D(e)$ is a <clopen set>.

Conversely, let $C$ be a <clopen set> in $T=\operatorname{Spec}R$. The <structure sheaf> glues the section $1$ on $C$ and the section $0$ on its complement to a unique <global section> $e\in\Gamma(T,\mathcal O_T)=R$. Checking on these two <open subsets> gives $e^2=e$, and $D(e)=C$. Conversely, the restriction of an <idempotent> $e$ to $D(e)$ is $1$ because $e$ is a <unit> there, and its restriction to $D(1-e)$ is $0$. Thus the two constructions are inverse, including for the zero <ring>:
$$
\boxed{\{e\in R:e^2=e\}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \{C\subseteq\operatorname{Spec}R:C\text{ clopen}\},\qquad e\longmapsto D(e).}
$$

Label the two charts of the <affine line with doubled origin> by $A,B$, and use $t$ for their common coordinate. Let $u:T=\operatorname{Spec}R\to X$ be an $R$-valued element of the <functor of points>. Its composite with $\phi$ corresponds to a unique $r\in R$ under the <affine-target adjunction for schemes>:
$$
\phi u:T\longrightarrow\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb Z},\qquad t\longmapsto r.
$$
The <closed subscheme> $Z=V(r)=\operatorname{Spec}(R/(r))$ maps into the disjoint union of the two zero sections,
$$
X\times_{\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb Z}}\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z
=A_0\sqcup B_0,\qquad A_0\cong B_0\cong\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z.
$$
The inverse image $Z_A$ of $A_0$ is a <clopen set> in $Z$. By the <idempotent–clopen correspondence>, it determines a unique <idempotent> $e\in R/(r)$, equal to $1$ on $Z_A$ and $0$ on the inverse image $Z_B$ of $B_0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u\longmapsto(r,e)\in F(R),\qquad D_Z(e)=Z_A.}
$$
Over $\mathbb Z$, the two “origins” are entire zero sections, rather than single closed points; this construction uses their full <scheme-theoretic fibers>.

Let $T=\operatorname{Spec}R$ and $Z=\operatorname{Spec}(R/(r))$. The <idempotent> $e$ determines the disjoint <clopen sets> $Z_A=D_Z(e)$ and $Z_B=D_Z(1-e)$. Each is closed in $T$. Define the <open subsets>
$$
T_A=T\setminus Z_B,\qquad T_B=T\setminus Z_A.
$$
They cover $T$ and satisfy $T_A\cap T_B=T\setminus Z=D(r)$. The <global section> $r$ defines <morphisms of schemes> $T_A\to A$ and $T_B\to B$, both sending the coordinate $t$ to $r$. On their intersection $r$ is a <unit>, so both maps land in the common <open subscheme> $A\cap B=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[t,t^{-1}]$, where they agree. They therefore glue uniquely:
$$
\boxed{u_{r,e}:T\longrightarrow X.}
$$
For an entirely explicit description, choose any lift $a\in R$ of $e$. Then $a(1-a)\in(r)$ and
$$
T_A=D(r)\cup D(a),\qquad T_B=D(r)\cup D(1-a).
$$
The equality $a+(1-a)=1$ proves that these <open subsets> cover. The inclusion $D(a(1-a))\subseteq D(r)$ proves that their intersection is exactly $D(r)$. Their description as complements of $Z_A,Z_B$ proves independence of the chosen lift. In particular, no lift of $e$ to an <idempotent> of $R$ is required.

Starting from $(r,e)$, the construction in (iii) preserves the composite to the <affine line> and sends $Z_A$ to the $A$ zero section and $Z_B$ to the $B$ zero section. Part (ii) therefore recovers $(r,e)$.

Conversely, start with $u:T\to X$ and construct $(r,e)$. The only points of $X$ outside $A$ lie on $B_0$, and the only points outside $B$ lie on $A_0$. Consequently $u^{-1}(A)=T\setminus Z_B=T_A$ and $u^{-1}(B)=T\setminus Z_A=T_B$. On each of these <open subsets>, $u$ is the unique <morphism of schemes> to the appropriate <affine line> with coordinate $r$. Thus part (iii) recovers $u$.

Finally, a <ring homomorphism> $R\to R'$ sends $r$ to $r'$ and induces $R/(r)\to R'/(r')$, sending $e$ to an <idempotent> $e'$. Inverse images of the <clopen sets> $Z_A,Z_B$ are precisely those determined by $e'$. The constructions therefore commute with this <base change>. We have a <natural bijection>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathsf{Sch}}(\operatorname{Spec}R,X)\cong F(R),}
$$
so $F$ is a <functor represented by a scheme>, represented by the <affine line with doubled origin>. The representing <scheme> is not required to be an <affine scheme>.

For every <valuation ring> $D$ with <fraction field> $K$, consider compatible <morphisms of schemes>
$$
u:\operatorname{Spec}K\to X,\qquad v:\operatorname{Spec}D\to S,\qquad fu=v|_{\operatorname{Spec}K}.
$$
An extension is a <morphism of schemes> $\ell:\operatorname{Spec}D\to X$ satisfying $\ell|_{\operatorname{Spec}K}=u$ and $f\ell=v$. The <valuative criterion for universal closedness> states
$$
\boxed{f\text{ universally closed}\iff f\text{ quasi-compact and every such pair admits an extension}.}
$$
Equivalently, among <quasi-compact morphisms>, the existence condition characterizes <universal closedness>. A <universally closed morphism> is automatically a <quasi-compact morphism>; the converse direction of the criterion needs this compactness condition. All <valuation rings> are allowed, with arbitrary <fraction fields>; there is no <Noetherian ring> hypothesis and one cannot replace them by <discrete valuation rings> without additional assumptions.https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01KF[Stacks Project, valuative criterion of universal closedness]{ref}https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/04XU[Stacks Project, universally closed morphisms are quasi-compact]{ref}

Use the compatible pair $u,v$ and the notion of extension from (i). The <valuative criterion for separatedness> is
$$
\boxed{f\text{ separated}\iff f\text{ quasi-separated and every such pair has at most one extension}.}

With the same compatible pair $u,v$, the <valuative criterion for properness> is
$$
\boxed{f\text{ proper}\iff f\text{ is of finite type and quasi-separated, and every pair has exactly one extension}.}
$$
Equivalently, one can replace <finite type> by being a <quasi-compact morphism> and <locally of finite type>. Necessity follows from the definition of a <proper morphism> and the existence and uniqueness criteria in (i) and (ii). Conversely, <finite type> supplies the compactness needed in (i), while being a <quasi-separated morphism> supplies the compactness of the <diagonal morphism> needed in (ii). Thus the extension condition gives both <universal closedness> and <separatedness>, and these together with <finite type> give a <proper morphism>. These hypotheses are part of the criteria, even though the question is phrased for unrestricted <morphisms of schemes>.https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0BX5[Stacks Project, valuative criterion for properness]{ref}

Let $S'\to S$ be a <morphism of schemes> and set $X'=X\times_SS'$, with projection $f':X'\to S'$. Given compatible <morphisms of schemes> $u':\operatorname{Spec}K\to X'$ and $v':\operatorname{Spec}D\to S'$, compose them with the projections to obtain a valuative problem for $f$. An extension $\ell:\operatorname{Spec}D\to X$ for that problem and the prescribed $v'$ determine a unique <morphism of schemes>
$$
\ell'=(\ell,v'):\operatorname{Spec}D\longrightarrow X\times_SS'.
$$
The <universal property of a fibre product> shows that $\ell'$ extends $u'$ and lies over $v'$. Conversely, every extension $\ell'$ has such a first component $\ell$. Thus the sets of extensions in the two problems are in <bijection>.

If $f$ is a <universally closed morphism>, it is a <quasi-compact morphism> and satisfies existence. The allowed stability of <quasi-compact morphisms> under <base change> and (a)(i) prove that $f'$ is a <universally closed morphism>. If $f$ is a <separated morphism>, it is a <quasi-separated morphism> and satisfies uniqueness. Stability of <quasi-separated morphisms> under <base change> and (a)(ii) prove that $f'$ is a <separated morphism>.

If $f$ is a <proper morphism>, the same <bijection> supplies existence and uniqueness. The permitted stability under <base change> of <quasi-compact morphisms>, <quasi-separated morphisms> and <locally of finite type> supplies all remaining hypotheses of (a)(iii). Hence
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f\text{ universally closed}&\Longrightarrow f'\text{ universally closed},\\
f\text{ separated}&\Longrightarrow f'\text{ separated},\\
f\text{ proper}&\Longrightarrow f'\text{ proper}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Let $X\xrightarrow fY\xrightarrow gS$ be <morphisms of schemes>. Start with $u:\operatorname{Spec}K\to X$ and $v:\operatorname{Spec}D\to S$ satisfying $gfu=v|_{\operatorname{Spec}K}$.

For existence, first apply the extension condition for $g$ to $fu$ and $v$, obtaining $w:\operatorname{Spec}D\to Y$. Then apply the extension condition for $f$ to $u,w$, obtaining $\ell:\operatorname{Spec}D\to X$. This $\ell$ solves the problem for $gf$. If both $f,g$ are <universally closed morphisms>, both are <quasi-compact morphisms>; their composite is a <quasi-compact morphism> by the allowed stability fact. Part (a)(i) therefore proves that $gf$ is a <universally closed morphism>.

For uniqueness, suppose $\ell_1,\ell_2$ are extensions for $gf$. Uniqueness for $g$ gives $f\ell_1=f\ell_2=w$, and uniqueness for $f$, now over this same $w$, gives $\ell_1=\ell_2$. If $f,g$ are <separated morphisms>, their composite is a <quasi-separated morphism> by the allowed stability fact. Part (a)(ii) proves that $gf$ is a <separated morphism>.

Finally, if $f,g$ are <proper morphisms>, these arguments give both existence and uniqueness. Stability under composition of <quasi-compact morphisms>, <quasi-separated morphisms> and <locally of finite type> gives the other hypotheses of (a)(iii). Consequently
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f,g\text{ universally closed}&\Longrightarrow gf\text{ universally closed},\\
f,g\text{ separated}&\Longrightarrow gf\text{ separated},\\
f,g\text{ proper}&\Longrightarrow gf\text{ proper}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Let $x\in\operatorname{Frac}(D)$ satisfy a <monic polynomial> equation
$$
x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=0,\qquad a_i\in D.
$$
If $x=0$, there is nothing to prove. Suppose $x\notin D$. The defining property of a <valuation ring> gives $y=x^{-1}\in D$. Multiplying the equation by $y^{n-1}$ yields
$$
x=-a_{n-1}-a_{n-2}y-\cdots-a_0y^{n-1}\in D,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D\text{ is an integrally closed domain}.}
$$
This proof uses only the comparison of an element with its inverse; no discreteness or finite generation of the <valuation> is needed.

Let $D$ be a <valuation ring>, $K$ its <fraction field>, and let $u:\operatorname{Spec}K\to X$, $v:\operatorname{Spec}D\to S$ be a compatible pair. Since $D$ is a <local ring>, every <open subset> of $\operatorname{Spec}D$ containing its unique <closed point> is the whole space: a <principal open subset> containing the <maximal ideal> is defined by a <unit>. Choose an <affine open subscheme> $V=\operatorname{Spec}A\subseteq S$ containing the image under $v$ of that <closed point>. The entire $v$ factors through $V$.

Because $f$ is a <finite morphism>, $f^{-1}(V)=\operatorname{Spec}B$ with $B$ a finite $A$-<module>. The valuative problem is the compatible <ring homomorphisms>
$$
A\longrightarrow D\subseteq K,\qquad \psi:B\longrightarrow K.
$$
Every $b\in B$ is an <integral element> over $A$. Mapping its <monic polynomial> equation to $K$ proves that $\psi(b)$ is an <integral element> over $D$. Part (c) gives $\psi(b)\in D$. Thus $\psi$ factors uniquely as a <ring homomorphism> $B\to D\hookrightarrow K$, producing the unique extension $\operatorname{Spec}D\to X$.

For completeness, a <finite morphism> is a <quasi-compact morphism> and of <finite type>: over $V$ its inverse image is an <affine scheme>, and finite $A$-<module> generators also generate it as an $A$-<algebra>. It is also a <separated morphism>. Affine-locally its <diagonal morphism> corresponds to the surjective multiplication <ring homomorphism>
$$
B\otimes_AB\longrightarrow B,\qquad b\otimes c\longmapsto bc,
$$
and hence is a <closed immersion>. In particular it is a <quasi-separated morphism>. The <valuative criterion for properness> now applies, giving
$$
\boxed{f\text{ finite}\Longrightarrow f\text{ proper}.}
$$

Write $X=\operatorname{Spec}B$, $S=\operatorname{Spec}A$ with $A,B$ <integral domains>, let $\alpha:A\to B$ be the <ring homomorphism>, and put $K=\operatorname{Frac}(B)$. We do not assume that $\alpha$ is injective. Write $A'=\alpha(A)\subseteq B\subseteq K$.

Let $D\subseteq K$ be any <valuation ring> containing $A'$, with <fraction field> $K$. The canonical <morphism of schemes> $\operatorname{Spec}K\to\operatorname{Spec}B$ and the <ring homomorphism> $A\to A'\hookrightarrow D$ form a valuative problem for $f$. Since $f$ is a <proper morphism>, there is an extension. On <coordinate rings> it is a <ring homomorphism> $\beta:B\to D$ such that
$$
B\xrightarrow{\beta}D\hookrightarrow K
$$
is the original inclusion of $B$ into $K$. Therefore every element of $B$ lies in $D$. Since $D$ was arbitrary, the stated description of <integral closure as an intersection of valuation rings> gives
$$
B\subseteq\bigcap_{\substack{D\subseteq K\text{ valuation ring}\\ A'\subseteq D}}D
=\overline{A'}^{\,K}.
$$
Thus every element of $B$ is an <integral element> over $A'$, and hence over $A$: lift the coefficients of its <monic polynomial> along the surjection $A\to A'$. The <ring homomorphism> $\alpha$ is therefore an <integral extension> in the sense of the question.

A <proper morphism> is of <finite type>, so in this affine situation $B=A[b_1,\ldots,b_s]$. If $b_i$ satisfies a <monic polynomial> of degree $n_i$, repeatedly reducing its powers shows that the finitely many <monomials>
$$
b_1^{e_1}\cdots b_s^{e_s},\qquad 0\leq e_i<n_i,
$$
generate $B$ as an $A$-<module>. Equivalently, apply the allowed equivalence between finite and integral plus <finite type>. Hence
$$
\boxed{f\text{ proper between affine integral schemes}\Longrightarrow f\text{ finite}.}
$$

For a <quasi-compact scheme> $X$, the <Serre criterion for affineness> gives the equivalences
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
X\text{ affine}
&\iff H^1(X,\mathcal F)=0\text{ for every quasi-coherent }\mathcal O_X\text{-module }\mathcal F\\
&\iff H^q(X,\mathcal F)=0\text{ for every such }\mathcal F\text{ and every }q>0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Indeed, an <affine scheme> has <vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme>. Conversely, vanishing of the first <sheaf cohomology> for every <quasi-coherent sheaf> includes every <quasi-coherent sheaf> of ideals, which suffices for the criterion. No <separated scheme> hypothesis is needed in this formulation. Nor may one test only the <structure sheaf>.https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01XF[Stacks Project, Serre's criterion for a quasi-compact scheme, using quasi-coherent ideals]{ref}

Suppose, to the contrary, that $H^1(X,\mathcal F)=0$ for every <quasi-coherent sheaf> $\mathcal F$. Since $X$ is a <quasi-compact scheme>, part (a) makes it an <affine scheme>.

Every <affine scheme> $X=\operatorname{Spec}A$ is a <separated scheme>: its <diagonal morphism> over $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z$ is induced by the surjective <ring homomorphism> $A\otimes_{\mathbb Z}A\to A$, $a\otimes b\mapsto ab$, so it is a <closed immersion>. This contradicts the assumption that $X$ is not a <separated scheme>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{There exists a quasi-coherent }\mathcal F\text{ with }H^1(X,\mathcal F)\ne0.}
$$
In fact, the version of the <Serre criterion for affineness> using ideals shows that $\mathcal F$ may be chosen to be a <quasi-coherent sheaf> of ideals. This argument does not use a comparison with <Čech cohomology> on the nonseparated <scheme>.

Let $U=\operatorname{Spec}A$ and $V=\operatorname{Spec}B$ be <affine open subschemes> of the <separated scheme> $X$. Its <diagonal morphism> is a <closed immersion>
$$
\Delta:X\hookrightarrow X\times_{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z}X.

Restricting this <closed immersion> to the <open subscheme> $U\times_{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z}V$ identifies its inverse image with $U\cap V$. Hence
$$
U\cap V\hookrightarrow U\times_{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z}V
=\operatorname{Spec}(A\otimes_{\mathbb Z}B)
$$
is a <closed immersion>. A <closed subscheme> of an <affine scheme> is an <affine scheme>, so
$$
\boxed{U\cap V\text{ is an affine open subscheme of }X.}
$$
The argument iterates to every finite intersection of <affine open subschemes>. The empty intersection of two disjoint charts causes no exception: the empty <scheme> is $\operatorname{Spec}(0)$.

Suppose $X=U_1\cup\cdots\cup U_n$ with each $U_i$ an <affine open subscheme>. Every <affine scheme> is a <quasi-compact topological space>: a cover by <principal open subsets> $D(f_\lambda)$ means that the $f_\lambda$ generate the <unit ideal>; an expression $1=\sum_{j=1}^r a_jf_{\lambda_j}$ gives a finite subcover. Every <open cover> has a refinement by <principal open subsets>, so this proves the general covering property.

Restrict an arbitrary <open cover> of $X$ to each $U_i$ and choose a finite subcover there. The union of these finitely many finite families covers $X$. Thus $X$ is a <quasi-compact scheme>.

Now assume $X$ is a <separated scheme>. By (c), every finite intersection of the $U_i$ is an <affine scheme>, and hence has vanishing positive <sheaf cohomology> for the <structure sheaf>. This <affine open cover> therefore computes <sheaf cohomology> using its <Čech cochain complex>. Its degree $p$ term is
$$
\check C^p=\prod_{1\leq i_0<\cdots<i_p\leq n}\Gamma(U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p},\mathcal O_X).
$$
There are no strictly increasing $(p+1)$-tuples when $p\geq n$, so those <Čech cochain groups> vanish. Consequently
$$
\boxed{H^m(X,\mathcal O_X)=0\qquad(m\geq n).}
$$
The same argument works for every <quasi-coherent sheaf>. The relevant comparison requires the acyclicity of the finite intersections; the bare assertion that arbitrary <Čech cohomology> equals <sheaf cohomology> would be too broad.

**Interpretation of the removed locus.** Over $\mathbb Z$, the notation $[1:0:0]$ specifies a section $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z\to\mathbb P^2_{\mathbb Z}$, with image $V_+(x_1,x_2)\cong\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z$. We remove this entire <closed subscheme>. This is the interpretation forced by the asserted <open cover> $X=U_1\cup U_2$. Removing one closed point in a particular fiber would not give that <open cover>. The native PDF uses the same notation as the transcription.

Put $a=x_0/x_1$, $b=x_2/x_1$ on $U_1$. On $U_2$ put $c=x_0/x_2$, $d=x_1/x_2$. On their intersection $c=a/b$ and $d=b^{-1}$, giving <coordinate rings>
$$
\Gamma(U_1,\mathcal O_X)=\mathbb Z[a,b],\qquad
\Gamma(U_2,\mathcal O_X)=\mathbb Z[a/b,b^{-1}],\qquad
\Gamma(U_1\cap U_2,\mathcal O_X)=L:=\mathbb Z[a,b,b^{-1}].
$$
The <Čech cochain complex> for the <structure sheaf>, using increasing distinct indices, is
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z[a,b]\oplus\mathbb Z[a/b,b^{-1}]
\xrightarrow{\delta}L\longrightarrow0,\qquad
\delta(f,g)=g-f.
$$
Thus $\check H^0$ is the intersection of the two subrings of $L$, while $\check H^1$ is the <quotient module> of $L$ by their additive sum.

The <Laurent polynomial ring> $L$ is a free <abelian group> on the <monomials> $a^ib^j$ with $i\geq0$, $j\in\mathbb Z$. The first subring has the <basis> $j\geq0$. The second has the <basis>
$$
c^id^k=a^ib^{-i-k},\qquad i,k\geq0,
$$
so its allowed exponents satisfy $j\leq-i$. A <monomial> belongs to both precisely when $i=j=0$. The <monomials> belonging to neither satisfy $i\geq2$ and $-i<j<0$. Since the subgroups are spanned by subsets of the same <basis>, their sum introduces no other relations. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\check H^0(\{U_1,U_2\},\mathcal O_X)&=\mathbb Z,\\
\check H^1(\{U_1,U_2\},\mathcal O_X)&=\bigoplus_{i\geq2}\ \bigoplus_{k=1}^{i-1}\mathbb Z\,[a^ib^{-k}]\\
&=\bigoplus_{m,n\geq1}\mathbb Z\!\left[\frac{x_0^{m+n}}{x_1^mx_2^n}\right],\\
\check H^q(\{U_1,U_2\},\mathcal O_X)&=0\qquad(q\geq2).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here the square brackets denote <cohomology classes>, and the <direct sums> are as <abelian groups>. In particular, $[a^2b^{-1}]=[x_0^2/(x_1x_2)]$ is a nonzero <cohomology class>. This <scheme> is a <separated scheme>, being an <open subscheme> of <projective space> over $\mathbb Z$, and is a <quasi-compact scheme> by (d). Both charts and their intersection are <affine schemes>, so this <Čech cohomology> equals <sheaf cohomology>. The nonzero first <sheaf cohomology> of the <quasi-coherent> <structure sheaf> rules out an <affine scheme>:
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is not affine}.}
$$

A <Noetherian scheme> is a <quasi-compact scheme>, so choose a finite <affine open cover> $X=\bigcup_{i=1}^s\operatorname{Spec}A_i$ with all $A_i$ <Noetherian rings>. The <nilradical sheaf> restricts on this chart to the <quasi-coherent sheaf> associated to $\operatorname{Nil}(A_i)$: the <nilradical> commutes with <localization>.

By the allowed fact, choose $d_i\geq1$ such that $\operatorname{Nil}(A_i)^{d_i}=0$ and put $m=\max_i d_i$. On every chart, the <ideal sheaf> $\mathcal N^m$ is zero, and hence it is zero globally. Higher powers are also zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal N^d=0\qquad\text{for every }d\geq m.}
$$
If $X$ is empty, take $m=1$. The finite <affine open cover> is essential for choosing one bound valid everywhere.

Let $i:X_{\mathrm{red}}\hookrightarrow X$ be the <reduced induced subscheme>, with <structure sheaf> $\mathcal O_{X_{\mathrm{red}}}=\mathcal O_X/\mathcal N$. Its underlying map of <topological spaces> is a <homeomorphism>, since every <prime ideal> contains the <nilradical>.

Put $\mathcal G=\mathcal F/\mathcal N\mathcal F$. Multiplication by $\mathcal N$ annihilates $\mathcal G$, so its action by $\mathcal O_X$ factors through $\mathcal O_X/\mathcal N$. Thus $\mathcal G$ is naturally a <sheaf of modules> over $\mathcal O_{X_{\mathrm{red}}}$. On an <affine open subscheme> $\operatorname{Spec}A$, with $\mathcal F=\widetilde M$, it corresponds to $M/\operatorname{Nil}(A)M$ over $A/\operatorname{Nil}(A)$, so it remains a <quasi-coherent sheaf>.

The <sheaf cohomology> of a <sheaf of modules> is the <sheaf cohomology> of its underlying <sheaf of abelian groups>. Under the above <homeomorphism>, that underlying sheaf is identical on the two spaces: the <global sections functor> and its <flasque resolutions> are identical. Equivalently, use <sheaf cohomology under a closed inclusion> with $i_*\mathcal G$. Therefore there are canonical <isomorphisms>, in every degree,
$$
\boxed{H^q(X,\mathcal F/\mathcal N\mathcal F)\cong H^q(X_{\mathrm{red}},\mathcal F/\mathcal N\mathcal F)\qquad(q\geq0).}
$$
In particular, this gives the requested equality in degree one. Equality of the underlying <topological spaces> alone would not compare arbitrary coefficient sheaves; the factorization of the action explains why the indicated coefficients are the same.

Let $\mathcal F$ be any <quasi-coherent sheaf> on $X$, and choose $m$ from (i). Filter it by the powers of the <nilradical sheaf>:
$$
\mathcal F\supseteq\mathcal N\mathcal F\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal N^m\mathcal F=0.
$$
Each subquotient
$$
\mathcal Q_j=\mathcal N^j\mathcal F/\mathcal N^{j+1}\mathcal F,\qquad 0\leq j<m,
$$
is a <quasi-coherent sheaf> annihilated by $\mathcal N$. Apply (ii) to the <quasi-coherent sheaf> $\mathcal N^j\mathcal F$. Since $X_{\mathrm{red}}$ is an <affine scheme>, <vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme> gives
$$
H^1(X,\mathcal Q_j)\cong H^1(X_{\mathrm{red}},\mathcal Q_j)=0.
$$
The <short exact sequences>
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal N^{j+1}\mathcal F\longrightarrow\mathcal N^j\mathcal F\longrightarrow\mathcal Q_j\longrightarrow0
$$
yield the segments of the <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology>
$$
H^1(X,\mathcal N^{j+1}\mathcal F)\longrightarrow H^1(X,\mathcal N^j\mathcal F)\longrightarrow H^1(X,\mathcal Q_j)=0.
$$
Starting from $\mathcal N^m\mathcal F=0$, descending <mathematical induction> makes every middle group zero, finally giving $H^1(X,\mathcal F)=0$.

This holds for every <quasi-coherent sheaf> $\mathcal F$. Since a <Noetherian scheme> is a <quasi-compact scheme>, the <Serre criterion for affineness> in (a) applies and proves
$$
\boxed{X_{\mathrm{red}}\text{ affine}\Longrightarrow X\text{ affine}.}
$$
This is an instance of the general fact that <affineness is invariant under nilpotent thickening>.
