= Transport equation
{title2=$u_t+b\mathbin\cdot\nabla u=0$}
{wiki}

= Advection equation
{synonym}

A <transport equation> carries a quantity along the flow of a <velocity field> $b$. For a smooth solution of $u_t+b\mathbin\cdot\nabla u=0$, the value of $u$ is constant along each <characteristic curve>. With constant speed $c$, the <chain rule> gives $\frac{d}{dt}u(x_0+ct,t)=u_t+c u_x=0$, hence $u(x,t)=g(x-ct)$. This is exact <translation>: it preserves the shape rather than smoothing it, as the <periodic heat and transport comparison> shows.

= Propagation of a derivative jump along a characteristic
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$u=A+g(q),\quad Dq=0$}

Suppose $D=a\partial_x+b\partial_y$, $DA=f$, $Dq=0$, and $A,q$ are <smooth functions>. Then $u=A+g(q)$ solves $Du=f$ wherever $g$ is differentiable. If $g$ is <continuous> and piecewise continuously differentiable with a jump in $g'$ at $q=q_0$, the one-sided <gradients> of $u$ differ by $[g']\nabla q$ across that <characteristic curve>. Thus a nonzero derivative jump in the data persists along the characteristic unless the relevant component of $\nabla q$ vanishes. A locally <Lipschitz continuous> $g$ also gives a <weak solution> across the curve.

= Periodic heat and transport comparison
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$u_H=1+e^{-t}\cos x+0.4e^{-9t}\cos(3x),\quad u_T=g(x-t)$}

Use dimensionless $x$ and $t$, the <periodic domain> $[0,2\pi]$, and the same <initial condition>
$$
g(x)=1+\cos x+0.4\cos(3x).
$$
With zero <PDE source term>, compare the <heat equation> $u_t=u_{xx}$ and the <transport equation> $u_t+u_x=0$. Their exact solutions are
$$
u_H(x,t)=1+e^{-t}\cos x+0.4e^{-9t}\cos(3x),\qquad u_T(x,t)=1+\cos(x-t)+0.4\cos(3(x-t)).
$$
Each <cosine> satisfies $\partial_x^2\cos(nx)=-n^2\cos(nx)$, so the <heat equation> reduces its amplitude according to $A_n'=-n^2A_n$. The <superposition principle> combines the constant and two <Fourier modes>. The <transport equation> shifts the argument of the entire profile. Both formulas meet the <initial condition> and matching <periodic boundary conditions> exactly.https://web.stanford.edu/class/math220b/handouts/heateqn.pdf[Stanford Math 220B heat-equation notes, periodic boundary conditions]{ref}

At $t=1$, the heat amplitudes are approximately $0.368$ and $0.0000494$: the small ripples disappear much sooner than the broad variation. The <transport equation> retains amplitudes $1$ and $0.4$, shifting the profile right by one unit with wraparound. Both preserve spatial average $1$, but only the <heat equation> approaches the uniform profile. This gives <diffusion> its usefulness for smoothing uneven <temperatures>, while the <transport equation> models a profile carried by a flow. The signed profile can represent a scaled <temperature> deviation.

\Image[pde-transport-vs-diffusion.png]

To restore units, let physical position be $X=\ell x$ and time be $T=\tau t$. For $U_T=D U_{XX}$, the dimensionless coefficient is $D\tau/\ell^2$, with $D$ measured in length squared per time. For $U_T+cU_X=0$, it is $c\tau/\ell$, with $c$ measured in length per time. Both plotted coefficients equal one when $D=\ell^2/\tau$ and $c=\ell/\tau$. Thus the example compares specified models on the same scales, rather than identifying <diffusivity> with <velocity>.

= Compact third-order advection stencil
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$K(\mu)=\mu(\mu+1)(\mu-1)(\mu-2)$}

Let $a=\mu(1+\mu)/2$, $b=(1+\mu)(2-\mu)$, $c=(1-\mu)(2-\mu)/2$, $d=2-\mu$, and $e=1+\mu$. The implicit stencil $aU_{j-1}^{n+1}+bU_j^{n+1}+cU_{j+1}^{n+1}=dU_j^n+eU_{j+1}^n$ for $u_t=u_x$ has centered characteristic moments zero through degree three and fourth moment $K(\mu)$. Its Fourier numerator and denominator satisfy $|L(\theta)|^2-|N(\theta)|^2=K(\mu)(1-\cos\theta)^2$. The marching operator is a contraction exactly for nonnegative <Courant numbers> in $[0,1]\cup[2,\infty)$; with signed Courant numbers, also include $(-\infty,-1]$. At the four roots of $K$ the method becomes an exact integer grid translation rather than merely third order.

= Hamiltonian transport equation
{c}
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$f_t+H_v\cdot f_x-H_x\cdot f_v=0$}

Transport along a <Hamiltonian flow>. The solution is $f(\cdot,t)=f_I\circ\Phi_{-t}$ wherever the inverse characteristic exists. Equality of mixed derivatives makes the transport vector field divergence-free, so its flow preserves phase-space volume. Smoothness of the Hamiltonian alone gives local characteristics, not a complete flow on all of phase space.

= Casimir invariant of a transport equation
{c}
{parent=Hamiltonian transport equation}
{title2=$C_F(f)=\int F(f)\,dx\,dv$}

For an incompressible transport flow, every integrable function of the transported scalar has constant integral: the scalar is unchanged along characteristics and the flow's Jacobian determinant equals one. For a finite integral on infinite phase space, require integrability; $F(0)=0$ and compactly supported data are one common choice. For a whole-space conservation statement also require no escape or boundary flux at infinity.

= Commuting velocity derivative for kinetic transport
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$Z_j=\partial_{v_j}+t\sum_i(\partial_{v_j}a_i)\partial_{x_i}$}

The differential operators $Z_j$ commute with $D=\partial_t+a(v)\cdot\nabla_x$. Indeed $[D,\partial_{v_j}]=-\sum_i(\partial_{v_j}a_i)\partial_{x_i}$, canceled by differentiating the explicit time factor in $Z_j$. Hence $Z_jf$ satisfies the same <transport equation> as $f$, and obeys the same <L2 norm> conservation when boundary fluxes vanish. For the standard <Jacobian matrix> $(Da)_{ij}=\partial_{v_j}a_i$, the vector of these derivatives is $\nabla_vf+t(Da)^T\nabla_xf$.

= Dispersion with a nonlinear velocity map
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$\|f(t)\|_{L_x^\infty L_v^1}\leq|t|^{-d}\|m_a\|_\infty\|f_0\|_{L_x^1L_v^\infty}$}

For $f(t,x,v)=f_0(x-ta(v),v)$, apply the <area formula> to the velocity map and bound the initial data by their velocity <essential supremum>. This gives the displayed <mixed Lebesgue norm> estimate whenever the <weighted inverse multiplicity> is essentially bounded. If $a$ is injective and $|\det Da|\geq\alpha>0$, the constant is at most $1/\alpha$. In dimension one a derivative bounded away from zero gives a global <diffeomorphism>; in higher dimensions determinant bounds alone do not.

= Free transport equation
{parent=Transport equation}
{title2=$\partial_tf+v\cdot\nabla_xf=0$}

Particles keep their velocity and move along straight trajectories. The <method of characteristics> gives $f(t,x,v)=f_0(x-tv,v)$. The shear preserves <Lebesgue measure> and every <Lp norm>. Compact initial <support> remains compact at each finite time, but need not remain in one compact set for all times.

= Free transport dispersion
{parent=Free transport equation}
{title2=$\|f(t)\|_{L_x^\infty L_v^1}\leq|t|^{-d}\|f_0\|_{L_x^1L_v^\infty}$}

For the <free transport equation>, substitute $y=x-tv$ into $\int|f_0(x-tv,v)|\,dv$. The resulting factor $|t|^{-d}$ and the bound by $\sup_v|f_0(y,v)|$ prove this <mixed Lebesgue norm> estimate. The right side contains initial data. The same-time mixed norm is not a conserved replacement: prescribing a product $\psi(x)\chi(v)$ at a later time and dilating $\chi$ disproves such a claim.

= Absolutely continuous representative along free characteristics
{parent=Free transport equation}
{title2=$F(t)=C+\int_0^tG(s)\,ds$}

A <distributional weak solution> of $\partial_tf+v\cdot\nabla_xf=g$, with locally integrable $f,g$, becomes $\partial_tF=G$ after the shear $F(t,x,v)=f(t,x+tv,v)$. For almost every characteristic label, there is an <absolutely continuous function> representing $F$, and its increments are the <integrals> of $G$ for every pair of times. Arbitrary modifications on time slices can destroy this every-time identity without changing the distributional solution.

= Free-transport semigroup
{parent=Free transport equation}
{title2=$U_tg(x,v)=g(x-tv,v)$}

For $1\leq p<\infty$, these operators are isometries on the phase-space <Lp space>, with $U_tU_s=U_{t+s}$. They form a <strongly continuous semigroup> for $t\geq0$ and extend to a group for all real times. Strong continuity follows by smooth compactly supported approximation and measure preservation. They preserve the <essential supremum> as well, but strong continuity is not claimed on the whole $L^\infty$ space.

= Characteristic curve
{parent=Transport equation}

For the transport field $b(t,x)$, a characteristic curve solves
$$
\dot X(t)=b(t,X(t)).
$$
The chain rule converts differentiation of $u(t,X(t))$ into the transport operator applied to $u$.

= Characteristic reachability for radial transport
{parent=Characteristic curve}
{title2=$x u_x+y u_y=ku,\quad(x,y)=(a,1-a)e^s$}

The radial <transport equation> has projected <characteristic curves> $(x,y)=e^s(x_0,y_0)$, which remain on positive rays. Data on $x+y=1$ reach precisely the half-plane $x+y>0$, since $x+y=e^s$. A datum $u(a,1-a)=h(a)$ therefore gives $u(x,y)=(x+y)^k h(x/(x+y))$ in that half-plane. The opposite half-plane is not determined by these data. Extending a characteristic line through the origin would join distinct flow orbits across a point where the projected vector field vanishes.

= Characteristic vector field
{parent=Characteristic curve}
{title2=$a\partial_x+b\partial_y$}

For $au_x+bu_y=c$, the projected <vector field> $(a,b)$ has the <characteristic projections> as its integral curves. The lifted <vector field> is $(a,b,c)$ in $(x,y,u)$ coordinates. A vanishing projected <vector field> can obstruct a <classical solution> even when the lifted <vector field> does not vanish.

= Characteristic projection
{parent=Characteristic curve}

For the first-order <partial differential equation> $a(x,y,z)z_x+b(x,y,z)z_y=c(x,y,z)$, a lifted <characteristic curve> satisfies $(x',y',z')=(a,b,c)$. Its characteristic projection is the image $(x(s),y(s))$ in the plane of independent variables. The projection need not be a regular curve: if $a=b=0$, it can be stationary while $z$ varies. In that situation the lifted curve cannot lie in the graph of a single-valued classical solution unless the required compatibility holds. <Noncharacteristic Cauchy data> require the tangent to the initial curve and the projected characteristic vector to be linearly independent.

= Characteristic flow map
{title2=$Z_{s,t}$}
{parent=Characteristic curve}

The characteristic flow map $Z_{s,t}(x)$ is the point reached at time $t$ by the <characteristic curve> that passes through $x$ at time $s$. Whenever the flow is invertible, transport solutions pull their initial data back by $Z_{0,t}^{-1}$.

= Characteristic representation of a measure-valued continuity equation
{parent=Characteristic flow map}
{title2=$\rho_t=X(t;0,\cdot)_\#\rho_0$}

Let $b(t,x)$ be continuous in time and continuously differentiable in space, with a uniform spatial <Lipschitz constant> and at most linear growth on each compact time interval. Its <characteristic flow map> exists forward and backward over every bounded time interval. For each initial <probability measure> with finite first <absolute moment>, the displayed <pushforward measure> is the unique <weak solution> of the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}(b\rho)=0$ continuous in the first <Wasserstein distance>.

The <Gronwall inequality> bounds the flow by $C_T(1+|x|)$ and therefore preserves finite first <absolute moments>. The <chain rule> establishes the weak equation. For uniqueness, transport a compactly supported terminal <test function> backward along the flow; it solves $\partial_t\varphi+b\cdot\nabla\varphi=0$ and remains compactly supported on a bounded time interval. Inserting it into the weak identity identifies every terminal integral with that of the claimed <pushforward measure>. A zero <divergence> makes the corresponding scalar <transport equation> and the <continuity equation> coincide.

= Phase-space flow Jacobian
{parent=Characteristic flow map}
{title2=$J(t,z)=\exp\int_0^t\operatorname{div}b(s,\Phi_s(z))\,ds$}

The <Jacobian determinant> of a differentiable <characteristic flow map> measures its change of volume. The variational equation and the <Liouville formula for a fundamental matrix> give the displayed formula for a complete smooth field $b$. For kinetic advection with $b=(v,F)$, its <divergence> is $\nabla_v\cdot F$. A zero <divergence> preserves phase-space <Lebesgue measure>.

= Global characteristic flow under linear growth
{parent=Characteristic flow map}

If $F\in C^1(\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^d)$ and $|F(t,x)|\le K(1+|x|)$, every <characteristic curve> is defined for all real time. The <Gronwall inequality> bounds $1+|X(t)|$ on each bounded time interval, so local <ordinary differential equation> existence cannot terminate through escape to infinity. Uniqueness and the variational equation give a global $C^1$ spatial flow diffeomorphism, with Jacobian $J>0$ satisfying $J_t=(\operatorname{div}F)(t,X)J$.

= Method of characteristics
{parent=Characteristic curve}
{wiki}

The method of characteristics solves a first-order partial differential equation by reducing it to ordinary differential equations along <characteristic curves>.

= Characteristic fold for sinusoidal data in a forced Burgers equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$y_\eta=1+x\cos\eta,\quad u_y=\frac{\cos\eta}{1+x\cos\eta}$}

For the <partial differential equation> $u_x+u u_y=x$ with initial profile $u(0,\eta)=1+\sin\eta$, the <method of characteristics> gives the parametrized surface
$$
u=1+\sin\eta+\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad y=\eta+x(1+\sin\eta)+\frac{x^3}{6}.
$$
The projection to the $(x,y)$ plane has <Jacobian determinant> $1+x\cos\eta$. It is positive for $|x|<1$, so the <inverse function theorem> makes the surface a unique local solution graph there. For $\eta\ge0$, strict monotonicity and divergence to infinity give a global inverse onto $y\ge x+x^3/6$ at each such $x$.

The <derivative> $u_y=\cos\eta/(1+x\cos\eta)$ becomes singular when that projection first degenerates, although $u$ itself stays finite. At $x=1$ the degenerate labels are odd multiples of $\pi$, and at $x=-1$ they are even multiples. At either boundary the limiting map remains strictly increasing, with isolated zero <derivatives>, so fixed image values determine convergent labels. Consequently the gradient diverges at fixed $y=(2k+1)\pi+7/6$ as $x\uparrow1$, and at fixed $y=2k\pi-7/6$ as $x\downarrow-1$, using $k\ge0$ and $k\ge1$, respectively, to stay in the half-line initial domain. At these first singularities $y_{\eta\eta}=0$ and $y_{\eta\eta\eta}\ne0$; this is the onset of loss of invertibility, rather than an ordinary nondegenerate fold at the initial singular time.

= Parabolic characteristic strip from a finite initial segment
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$yu_x+u_y=u,\quad u(x,0)=x\ (1\leq x\leq2)$}

The equations of the <method of characteristics> give $(x,y,u)=(s+t^2/2,t,se^t)$. Their parameter map to the plane has <Jacobian determinant> one, so it is globally invertible and $u=(x-y^2/2)e^y$ is uniquely determined exactly where $1\leq x-y^2/2\leq2$. Both positive and negative $y$ occur. Outside this closed parabolic strip, $u=e^yF(x-y^2/2)$ allows arbitrary smooth changes of $F$ outside the initial parameter interval. The boundary <characteristic curves> carry the data from its endpoints.

= Quadrant uniqueness for unequal dilation characteristics
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$xz_x+2yz_y=xy,\quad z(s,s)=s^2$}

The <method of characteristics> from $s\neq0$ are $(x,y,z)=(se^t,se^{2t},s^2(2+e^{3t})/3)$. Their projections are half-parabolas $y=x^2/s$ with $x/s>0$. The inverse parameters $s=x^2/y$, $t=\log(y/x)$ exist exactly on the first and third quadrants, giving the unique solution $z=2x^4/(3y^2)+xy/3$ there. The projection Jacobian $se^{3t}$ is nonzero, so every allowed initial point is noncharacteristic.

= Fold boundary of a characteristic graph
{parent=Method of characteristics}

A smooth characteristic surface in three-dimensional $(x,y,u)$ space need not remain the graph of a <differentiable function> after <projection> to the $(x,y)$ plane. Locally, if its implicit equation is $F(x,y,u)=0$, the <implicit function theorem> produces a smooth graph wherever $F_u\ne0$. At a point where $F_u=0$, the <projection> can fold and two graph branches can meet. A real radical formula may continue to that boundary even though its <partial derivatives> diverge there. Thus the closed set on which the radical is real can be larger than the open domain of a <classical solution>.

For example,
$$
F=16u^2-(30x+24y)u+9y^2-40xy
$$
has two branches $u=(15x+12y\pm5\sqrt{9x^2+40xy})/16$. On them $F_u=\pm10\sqrt{9x^2+40xy}$, so the graph <projection> folds on the nonzero boundary points of $9x^2+40xy\ge0$. Its gradient in the base variables does not vanish at those points, and <differentiation> of the radical gives the transverse <derivative> singularity. The characteristic surface itself can be smooth there: its graph coordinate is the part which fails. Boundary values may still be defined continuously, but they do not create an open classical-solution neighborhood.

= Glacier characteristic admissibility
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$(x-\xi)(1-x-\xi)>0$}

For $H_t+H^{n+1}H_x=1-2x$ with $H(\xi,0)=0$, <method of characteristics> conserves $H^{n+2}/(n+2)-(x-x^2)$. A positive downstream branch therefore requires $0\leq\xi<x<1-\xi$. Initial points in the negative-accumulation region cannot create nonnegative <ice> from a bare bed. <Characteristic> intersections and missing dry-bed physics prevent using the formal initial/boundary branches as an unrestricted global solution.

= Charpit's equations
{c}
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$\dot x=F_p,\ \dot y=F_q,\ \dot u=pF_p+qF_q$}

For a first-order nonlinear <partial differential equation> $F(x,y,u,p,q)=0$, with $p=u_x,q=u_y$, Charpit's equations extend the <method of characteristics> to the five-dimensional space of position, value and gradient:
$$
\dot x=F_p,\quad\dot y=F_q,\quad\dot u=pF_p+qF_q,\quad\dot p=-F_x-pF_u,\quad\dot q=-F_y-qF_u.
$$
The chain rule gives $dF/dt=0$ by direct cancellation, so a trajectory starting on $F=0$ remains there. Initial data $u_0(s)$ on $(x_0(s),y_0(s))$ require both $F(x_0,y_0,u_0,p_0,q_0)=0$ and $p_0x_0'+q_0y_0'=u_0'$. The <non-characteristic> condition is $x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0$. It makes the projection onto $(x,y)$ locally invertible. Compatible initial contact data then propagate as $du=p\,dx+q\,dy$, reconstructing a classical solution. Multiple permitted initial normal derivatives can give different local branches; loss of the projection <Jacobian determinant> marks a <characteristic caustic>.

= Fold caustic for a cubic Charpit equation
{parent=Charpit's equations}
{c}
{title2=$xu_x+u_y^3+u=1$}

For initial data $u(x,0)=1+x^3/4$ on $0\leq x\leq1$, the <characteristic> surface has $x=se^r$, $y=3s^2(1-e^{-2r})/2$, $u=1+s^3(e^{-3r}-3e^{-r}/4)$. Its projection determinant is $3s^2e^r(2e^{-2r}-1)$, so the sheet connected to the initial data loses local invertibility at $r=\log2/2$. The regular sheet has $r<\log2/2$ and projects to $0<x<\sqrt2$, $3(1-x^{-2})/2<y<3x^2/8$. Its upper boundary is a <fold point of a critical manifold> <caustic>. Solving the inverse yields $s^2=[x^2+\sqrt{x^4-(8/3)x^2y}]/2$; the other square-root branch lies on the folded sheet and cannot simply be joined as a unique smooth solution. The initial point $s=0$ is already <characteristic>, illustrating the need to inspect endpoints as well as the interior of a Cauchy curve.

= Cones generated by rotation and dilation characteristics
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$\dot r=r-k\times r$}

For a fixed real unit <vector> $k$, the linear <ordinary differential equation> $\dot r=r-k\times r$ combines <dilation> with <rotation> about the axis $\mathbb Rk$. Its solution from a line $s\ell$, with $|\ell|=1$, is
$$
r(s,t)=se^t\bigl(\kappa k+\cos t\,v-\sin t\,(k\times v)\bigr),\qquad\kappa=k\cdot\ell,\quad v=\ell-\kappa k.
$$
Since $k\cdot r=se^t\kappa$ and $|r|=|s|e^t$, the union of these <characteristic curves> is given by $|k\cdot r|=|\kappa||r|$. For $0<|\kappa|<1$ it is a circular <cone> of semi-angle $\arccos|\kappa|$: varying $t$ supplies every azimuth and varying $s$ supplies every radius on both halves. For $\kappa=0$ it is a <plane>; for $|\kappa|=1$ it is only the axis. In the last case the line is itself a <characteristic curve>, so the <initial conditions>[initial data] do not generate a two-dimensional <surface>. The cone is a <smooth surface> away from its vertex, and it is a <graph of a function> only on suitable patches. Invariance of a graph patch under <characteristic curves> holds only while the curve stays in its domain; global flow invariance requires continuation of that patch.

= Characteristic caustic
{parent=Method of characteristics}

A characteristic caustic is a locus where the coordinate map from initial-data and evolution parameters to physical coordinates has zero <Jacobian determinant>. The <inverse function theorem> then fails, and different characteristic branches can reach the same point. A classical solution constructed from initial data must be restricted to its appropriate invertible branch before such a fold. In <geometrical optics> the corresponding ray-envelope phenomenon is a <caustic>; in nonlinear first-order equations a fold can signal the onset of multiple gradients or <shock formation by characteristic intersection>.

= Domain determined by a finite segment under a radial characteristic flow
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$\dot x=x-a,\ \dot y=y,\ x(0)=b<a,\ 0\leq y(0)\leq c\implies x<a,\ 0\leq y\leq c(a-x)/(a-b)$}

For a first-order <partial differential equation> with the displayed projected <characteristic> field, label the initial segment by $r=y(0)\in[0,c]$. The <characteristic curves> are $x=a-(a-b)e^s$, $y=re^s$. They are the branches of straight lines through $(a,0)$ on the side $x<a$. The inverse coordinates are
$$
e^s=\frac{a-x}{a-b},\qquad r=\frac{(a-b)y}{a-x}.
$$
They are unique exactly in the displayed sector. The <Jacobian determinant> is $-(a-b)e^{2s}\ne0$, so the initial segment is noncharacteristic and its transported data determine a unique classical solution on the sector's interior whenever the characteristic equation for the dependent variable has a unique solution. The bounding rays inherit data from the two endpoints. The point $(a,0)$ is approached only at infinite parameter time, and characteristics on the opposite side cannot reach the initial segment.

= Characteristic equations for a first-order quasilinear equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$x_s=P(x,y,z),\quad y_s=Q(x,y,z),\quad z_s=R(x,y,z)$}

= Characteristic equations for a quasilinear equation
{synonym}

For the first-order <quasilinear partial differential equation> $P(x,y,z)z_x+Q(x,y,z)z_y=R(x,y,z)$, the displayed <ordinary differential equations> trace curves tangent to every classical <solution surface>. Indeed, if $z=u(x,y)$ solves the equation and $x_s=P$, $y_s=Q$, then the <chain rule> gives $z_s=u_xP+u_yQ=R$. Conversely a surface swept out by these curves solves the equation wherever its projection to $(x,y)$ is invertible and hence represents a differentiable graph. The <coefficients> can depend on the unknown $z$, which distinguishes these equations from characteristic equations for a prescribed linear <transport equation>.

= Fold caustic
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$\det D_{(s,t)}(x,y)=0$}

A characteristic surface may remain smooth while its projection to the independent-variable plane fails to be locally invertible. At a generic fold, smooth changes of parameter and plane coordinates reduce this projection to $(u,v)\mapsto(u,v^2)$. There are two sheets on one side of the image of $v=0$, no sheet on the other, and one merged sheet on the fold itself. The image is a fold caustic. A characteristic graph can terminate there even though the space curves defining the surface continue smoothly. Vanishing of the <Jacobian determinant> detects a candidate singular locus; verifying the fold requires a nonzero quadratic term in its null direction, as in $v^2$.

= Elliptic characteristics of a planar transport equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}

For the <linear partial differential equation> $2y u_x-xu_y=0$, the <method of characteristics> gives $\dot x=2y$, $\dot y=-x$, $\dot u=0$. Thus $\phi=x^2+2y^2$ is constant on each <characteristic curve>, whose nonzero level sets are <ellipses>. On a domain containing entire such <ellipses>, the solutions have the form $u=F(\phi)$, subject to the desired regularity at the origin.

For the inhomogeneous equation $2yu_x-xu_y=xy(2y^2-x^2)$, a <particular solution> is $u_p=x^2y^2/2$, so the <general solution> is $u=x^2y^2/2+F(x^2+2y^2)$. Prescribing $u(x,1)=x^2$ fixes $F(s)=(s-2)/2$ only for $s\geq2$. The inner <ellipses> $s<2$ never intersect that data line, so the boundary values leave their solution values undetermined. Smooth extensions must match the prescribed $F$ at $s=2$; choosing the same linear formula everywhere gives one polynomial extension.

= Characteristic components of a hyperbolic first-order equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$yf_x+xf_y=0$}

The characteristic <ordinary differential equations> $\dot x=y$, $\dot y=x$ preserve $u=x^2-y^2$. Away from the origin, $u=x^2-y^2$, $v=2xy$ have nonzero <Jacobian determinant> $4(x^2+y^2)$, and the <chain rule> reduces the displayed equation to $2(x^2+y^2)f_v=0$. Hence locally $f=F(u)$.

Globally, a <level set> of $u$ can have disconnected characteristic components, permitting different profiles on its different branches. A smooth counterexample on the whole plane is $f=\exp[-1/(x^2-y^2)^2]$ where $x>0$ and $x^2>y^2$, and $f=0$ elsewhere. The exponential is flat on the wedge boundary, so this extension is smooth and satisfies the equation. Nevertheless the points $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$ have equal $u$ and different function values. One global profile $F(u)$ therefore needs an additional branch-identification condition, rather than following from the local change of variables alone.

= Weighted Euler first-order equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}

For $x f_x+\beta y f_y=\gamma f$, the curves in the <method of characteristics> are $x=x_0e^s$, $y=y_0e^{\beta s}$ and $f=f_0e^{\gamma s}$. On either component $x\ne0$, the invariant is $y|x|^{-\beta}$, giving $f=|x|^\gamma F(y|x|^{-\beta})$. A coordinate change that includes $y/x$ may be singular on an axis, so smooth continuation there must be checked against the original <partial differential equation>.

= Fold tangency obstruction for characteristic data
{parent=Method of characteristics}

If initial data are parameterized by $s$ and their characteristic invariant is $D(s)$, a solution constant on <characteristics> requires $f(s)=F(D(s))$. At a fold $D'(s_0)=0$, equal invariant values must have equal data, and $f'(s)/D'(s)$ must extend continuously if $F$ is <continuously differentiable>. The condition $f'(s_0)=0$ alone is insufficient: for $D(s)=1/4-(s-1/2)^2$, the data $|s-1/2|^{3/2}$ would require a derivative of $F$ blowing up at $1/4$.

= Characteristic equations for a transport equation
{parent=Method of characteristics}
{title2=$\dot z=b(t,z),\quad\dot f=h(t,z)$}

For a <transport equation> $\partial_tf+b(t,z)\cdot\nabla_zf=h$, solve $\dot z=b(t,z)$. The <chain rule> then gives $d[f(t,z(t))]/dt=h(t,z(t))$. Initial data and the time <integral> of this source determine the solution along each <characteristic curve>.

= Linear transport equation
{parent=Transport equation}

A linear transport equation has a prescribed velocity field and is linear in the transported unknown.

= Travel coordinate for a conservative transport perturbation
{parent=Linear transport equation}
{title2=$a(s,t)=\phi(\xi(s)-t)/V(s),\quad\xi(s)=\int_0^sV(r)^{-1}\,dr$}

For $a_t+(V(s)a)_s=0$ with $V>0$, the <flux> perturbation $b=Va$ solves $b_t+Vb_s=0$. The travel <coordinate> transforms it into unit-speed transport. Thus the <flux> perturbation is constant on <characteristics> while <area> <amplitude> changes as $1/V$. In a supplied power-law river, $A_{\rm eq}'=M/V$, giving travel <time> $[A_{\rm eq}(l)-A_{\rm eq}(0)]/M$. At a zero-speed head, this <time> can remain integrable even though the <amplitude> formula is not uniform.

= Transport of a Dirac point mass

{title2=$u(x,t)=\delta_0(x-ct)$}

= Moving Dirac point mass
{synonym}

For the constant-velocity <linear transport equation> $u_t+c\cdot\nabla_xu=0$, a <Dirac delta distribution> initially at zero moves along $x=ct$. Its spacetime <distribution> pairs by $\langle u,\varphi\rangle=\int\varphi(ct,t)\,dt$, and its initial trace is obtained by <weak convergence of distributions>. The <Fourier representation of the Dirac delta function> writes it as an <oscillatory integral> with <phase function> $(x-ct)\cdot\theta$. Its <singular support> is precisely the trajectory; it has <order-zero distribution> regularity as a measure, without becoming a <smooth function>.

= Coercive energy estimate for kinetic transport
{parent=Linear transport equation}
{title2=$\|f(t_2)\|_2\leq e^{-\delta(t_2-t_1)}\|f(t_1)\|_2$}

If a velocity operator satisfies $\operatorname{Re}\langle Lh,h\rangle\geq\delta\|h\|_2^2$, then sufficiently regular solutions of $\partial_tf+v\cdot\nabla_xf+Lf=0$ obey the displayed estimate when spatial boundary fluxes vanish. The free-transport part contributes zero to the energy <derivative>. Integrating the resulting differential inequality avoids dividing by a possibly zero <norm>.

= Lp conservation for incompressible transport
{c}
{parent=Linear transport equation}
{title2=$\|f_0\circ\Phi_t^{-1}\|_p=\|f_0\|_p$}

Scalar advection preserves every finite <Lp norm> if its complete differentiable characteristic flow preserves volume and the initial datum belongs to the corresponding <Lp space>. In general the $p$th power of the <norm> is the initial $|f_0|^p$ weighted by the <phase-space flow Jacobian>. Completeness alone preserves the <essential supremum>, but does not preserve finite-$p$ <norms> for compressible transport.

= Inflow transport boundary condition
{parent=Linear transport equation}
{title2=$u(t,0)=f(t)$}

For a <transport equation> on a domain, prescribe boundary data only where the velocity points into the domain. On $x>0$ with speed $a>0$, a bounded <weak solution> with initial data $u_0$ and inflow data $f$ satisfies
$$
\int u(\varphi_t+a\varphi_x)+\int u_0\varphi(0,x)+a\int f(t)\varphi(t,0)=0.
$$
The integrals are over the quadrant and its two boundary rays. Backtracking <characteristic curves> gives $u_0(x-at)$ when $x\ge at$ and $f(t-x/a)$ otherwise. Corner values need not match for a bounded <weak solution>.

= Corner compatibility for constant-speed transport
{parent=Inflow transport boundary condition}
{title2=$f^{(j)}(0)=(-a)^j u_0^{(j)}(0)$}

For the <transport equation> $u_t+a u_x=0$ on the quadrant with $a>0$, smooth initial and inflow data glue across $x=at$ to order $m$ precisely when $f^{(j)}(0)=(-a)^j u_0^{(j)}(0)$ for $0\le j\le m$. Matching all orders yields a smooth solution; matching orders zero and one yields a <classical solution> up to the two boundaries.

= Weak transport solution under characteristic coordinates
{parent=Linear transport equation}
{title2=$u(t,X(t,a))=u_0(a)$}

For a complete $C^1$ velocity flow, the bounded <weak solution> of $u_t+F\cdot\nabla u=0$ is its initial value pulled back by the inverse flow. Its weak identity is $\int u(\varphi_t+\operatorname{div}(F\varphi))+\int u_0\varphi(0)=0$; the divergence term is essential. Changing to flow coordinates gives $\int w\partial_t(J\zeta)+\int u_0\zeta(0)=0$. Approximation in the spatial parameter permits $\zeta=\eta/J$, since $J$ and $J_t$ are continuous and $J>0$ on compact sets. Hence $\partial_tw=0$ distributionally, proving uniqueness without requiring second <derivatives> of $F$.

= Adjoint transport equation
{parent=Linear transport equation}

An adjoint transport equation transfers the transport operator from a weak solution to a test function. Solving the terminal-value adjoint equation allows arbitrary compactly supported source terms to be used in uniqueness proofs.

= System of conservation laws
{title2=$U_t+\partial_xF(U)=0$}
{parent=Transport equation}

A system of conservation laws evolves a vector of conserved variables $U$ through a flux vector $F(U)$. For a smooth solution it has quasilinear form $U_t+A(U)U_x=0$, where $A(U)=DF(U)$ is the <flux Jacobian>.

= Weak conservation laws depend on conserved variables
{parent=System of conservation laws}

Two smooth differential systems related by a nonlinear change of dependent variables need not define the same <weak solutions>. If $U_t+F(U)_x=0$, a smooth function $V=g(U)$ satisfies $V_t+g'(U)F'(U)U_x=0$ for smooth $U$; even when this can be written as another divergence, its <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> need not be equivalent to those in $U$. The smooth chain rule does not justify multiplying jump distributions by discontinuous coefficients.

For example the <shallow water equations> can be written smoothly as $h_t+(hv)_x=0$ and $v_t+(h+v^2/2)_x=0$. Mechanical mass and <momentum conservation> instead select $h_t+(hv)_x=0$ and $(hv)_t+(hv^2+h^2/2)_x=0$. These systems agree for smooth positive $h$, but their jump laws differ. The states $(h,v)=(2,\sqrt3/2)$ and $(1,0)$ satisfy the mass/momentum jump laws with speed $\sqrt3$, while the velocity-form jump equation would demand $11/(4\sqrt3)$. A model for discontinuities must specify the conservative variables or an equivalent admissible regularization, rather than using smooth equivalence alone.

= Lax shock admissibility conditions
{c}
{parent=System of conservation laws}

For a strictly hyperbolic <system of conservation laws> $U_t+F(U)_x=0$ with ordered <characteristic speeds> $\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_n$, a Lax family-$j$ shock has $\lambda_j(U_R)<s<\lambda_j(U_L)$. The remaining families must stay on the same side of $s$: $\lambda_{j-1}(U_L)<s$ and $s<\lambda_{j+1}(U_R)$ where those neighbors exist. These inequalities imply that the lower families have speed below $s$ on both sides and the higher families speed above $s$ on both sides. There are $n+1$ incoming characteristic directions: two from the compressed family and one from each other family. The <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> supply the jump balance, while these inequalities supply admissibility. For a convex scalar flux this reduces to the <entropy shock> condition.

= Riemann problem
{parent=System of conservation laws}
{c}
{title2=$U(x,t)=U(x/t)$}

A Riemann problem for a one-dimensional <system of conservation laws> $\partial_tU+\partial_xf(U)=0$ has two constant initial states, $U_L$ for $x<0$ and $U_R$ for $x>0$. The data and equation are invariant under $(x,t)\mapsto(\lambda x,\lambda t)$. When the physically admissible <entropy solution> is unique, this invariance makes it a <self-similar solution> depending on $x/t$. For ideal-gas <compressible flow>, the characteristic speeds are $u-c,u,u+c$. The two acoustic families carry <shock waves> or <rarefaction waves>, while the middle family carries a <contact discontinuity>. Sufficiently strong separating velocities can instead create a vacuum region.

= Single-rarefaction matching conditions for an ideal-gas Riemann problem
{parent=Riemann problem}
{title2=$\Delta u=2\sigma\Delta c/(\gamma-1)$}

Two positive uniform ideal-gas states can be joined by one nontrivial <rarefaction wave> precisely when their <specific entropy> agrees, $p_L/\rho_L^\gamma=p_R/\rho_R^\gamma$, and for one $\sigma\in\{\pm1\}$ they satisfy $u_R-u_L=2\sigma(c_R-c_L)/(\gamma-1)$ with $\sigma(c_R-c_L)>0$. These equations are the constant-entropy and <Riemann invariant> conditions of the <self-similar ideal-gas rarefaction>. They are sufficient because its affine $u,c$ profiles match the two states at $\xi_L=u_L+\sigma c_L$ and $\xi_R=u_R+\sigma c_R$, and $\xi_R>\xi_L$. The left family has $c_R<c_L$, while the right family has $c_R>c_L$. The inequality $u_R>u_L$ alone does not impose the two matching equalities.

= Contact discontinuity
{parent=Riemann problem}
{title2=$[u]=[p]=0$}

A contact discontinuity in one-dimensional gas dynamics moves with the common fluid <velocity> $s=u_L=u_R$ and has continuous <pressure>, while <mass density> and <specific entropy> can jump. The mass jump condition $s[\rho]=[\rho u]$ is automatic with common $u=s$; momentum conservation $s[\rho u]=[\rho u^2+p]$ then requires $[p]=0$. The energy jump condition is also satisfied. Thus a contact is a material interface, not a compression shock. A smooth self-similar positive-density flow cannot spread this zero-speed-relative-to-the-fluid family into a fan: $u=x/t$ on an interval would require both $du/d(x/t)=1$ and, from continuity, $du/d(x/t)=0$.

= Conservation law flux
{parent=System of conservation laws}
{title2=$F(U)$}

= Flux of a conservation law
{synonym}

A <conservation law flux> $F(U)$ transports the conserved density $U$ across a surface. In one dimension $U_t+\partial_xF(U)=0$; a moving discontinuity satisfies $s[U]=[F]$. For a stationary <shock wave>, each conserved flux has the same value on both sides.

= Quasilinear system
{parent=System of conservation laws}

A first-order quasilinear system has the form $U_t+A(U,x,t)U_x=S(U,x,t)$: derivatives of the unknown enter linearly, while their coefficient matrix may depend on the unknown itself.

= Hyperbolic system
{parent=Quasilinear system}

A quasilinear system is hyperbolic where its principal coefficient matrix has real eigenvalues and enough independent eigenvectors. The eigenvalues are its <characteristic speeds>.

= Flux Jacobian

{parent=System of conservation laws}

The flux Jacobian is the <Jacobian matrix> of the flux in a <system of conservation laws>. Its <eigenvalues> are the local <characteristic speeds>, and its left and right <eigenvectors> select the corresponding wave variables and directions in state space.

= Characteristic speed

{parent=Flux Jacobian}

A characteristic speed is an <eigenvalue> of the <flux Jacobian>. Along a corresponding <characteristic curve>, an associated wave variable obeys an <ordinary differential equation> obtained by projecting the governing system onto the matching left <eigenvector>.

= Kinematic wave
{parent=Characteristic speed}
{wiki}

A kinematic wave is a disturbance whose propagation speed is determined by the local relation between a conserved density and its flux. In a <scalar conservation law> $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, smooth values travel at speed $f'(u)$.

= Drying front in a rainfall-driven Manning kinematic wave
{parent=Kinematic wave}
{title2=$h_t+(h^{5/3})_s=E(t),\quad h\geq0$}

Let $H(t)=\int_0^tE(r)\,dr$, increasing until $t^*$ and decreasing afterwards. For dry initial data and zero upstream depth, a <characteristic> emitted at time $\tau$ has $h=H(t)-H(\tau)$ and position $s=\frac53\int_\tau^t[H(r)-H(\tau)]^{2/3}\,dr$, for as long as its depth stays nonnegative. The height follows by integrating the source along a <characteristic>, and the position follows from the <derivative> of the Manning flux.

When $E<0$ and $H(t)>0$, define the earlier emission time $\tau_d(t)\leq t^*$ by $H(\tau_d)=H(t)$. The front where this water is just exhausted has
$$
s_d(t)=\frac53\int_{\tau_d(t)}^t[H(r)-H(t)]^{2/3}\,dr.
$$
It moves downstream because $s_d'=-(10/9)E(t)\int_{\tau_d}^t[H(r)-H(t)]^{-1/3}\,dr>0$. Behind it depth is zero; ahead, unexhausted <characteristic> labels determine the wet profile. This construction presumes removal limited by water availability: continuing a negative source unchanged at zero depth would violate nonnegativity. If $H$ reaches zero, the remaining initially generated water has also vanished.

= Power-law river kinematic wave
{parent=Kinematic wave}
{title2=$A_t+[cA^{m+1}/(m+1)]_s=M$}

With constant lateral supply $M$, a discharge law of the displayed form gives <equilibrium> <area> $[(m+1)(Q_{\rm in}+Ms)/c]^{1/(m+1)}$ and <characteristic> <speed> $cA^m$. Under a <Chezy friction law>[Chezy law], a wetted-perimeter relation $P_b\propto A^q$ gives $m=(1-q)/2$. Small <area> perturbations are transported downstream with <amplitude> inversely proportional to this <characteristic> <speed>; finite compressive disturbances can still form shocks because the <flux> is <convex>.

= Scalar conservation law
{title2=$u_t+\partial_xf(u)=0$}
{parent=Transport equation}

A scalar conservation law in one space dimension has the form $u_t+\partial_xf(u)=0$. While a classical solution exists, it obeys the quasilinear transport equation $u_t+f'(u)u_x=0$.

= Time reparametrization for a separable conservation-law flux
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$u_t+a(t)F(u)_x=0,\quad T=\int_0^ta(s)\,ds$}

Suppose $a(t)>0$ for positive times. The strictly increasing new time $T(t)=\int_0^t a(s)\,ds$ converts the <scalar conservation law> $u_t+a(t)\partial_xF(u)=0$ into $U_T+\partial_xF(U)=0$, with $u(x,t)=U(x,T(t))$. The <chain rule> proves the claim for classical solutions; a change of variables in the weak formulation proves it for <weak solutions> and preserves the time orientation of the <Kruzhkov entropy inequality>. An isolated zero of $a$ at the initial endpoint does not obstruct the positive-time transformation.

The <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> becomes $dx_s/dt=a(t)[F(u)]/[u]$, while <characteristic curves> obey $dx/dt=a(t)F'(u)$. Thus a straight <shock> in the new time may accelerate in physical time. For $a(t)=t$, a constant-speed trajectory in $T=t^2/2$ is a parabola in $(x,t)$. This separates genuine changes in wave structure from a common time-dependent speed factor.

= Unit-mass source solution for a quartic flux
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$\rho=t^{-1/4}f(x/t^{1/4})$}

The nonnegative <scalar conservation law> $\rho_t+(\rho^4/4)_x=0$ has a unit-mass source-type <entropy solution> with $f(\eta)=\eta^{1/3}$ on $0<\eta<(4/3)^{3/4}$ and zero elsewhere. Mass conservation gives the scaling exponents $\alpha+\beta=0$, while flux balance gives $\alpha-1=4\alpha-\beta$, so $\alpha=-1/4$, $\beta=1/4$. The reduced ODE integrates to $f^4-\eta f=0$ on the positive branch adjoining vacuum. The integral $\int_0^{\eta_F}\eta^{1/3}\,d\eta=1$ fixes the front. Its speed $\eta_Ft^{-3/4}/4$ equals the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>[Rankine-Hugoniot] speed $\rho_L^3/4$, between the two limiting <characteristic speeds>, so the front is compressive. The initial trace is a unit <Dirac delta distribution>, not a smooth finite-width density.

= Sonic point of a scalar flux
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$f\prime(s_*)=0$}

= Stagnation point of a scalar flux
{synonym}

For a differentiable <scalar conservation law> $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, a <sonic point of a scalar flux> is a state $s$ where its <characteristic speed> $f'(s)$ vanishes. If $f$ is convex and this zero is unique, $f'<0$ on the left and $f'>0$ on the right, so $s$ is the unique minimum of the flux. The definition refers to a vanishing transport speed in a <scalar conservation law>. In compressible <fluid mechanics>, a <sonic point> instead refers to flow at the local sound speed.

= Uniformly convex scalar flux
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$F''\geq\kappa>0$}

In the common PDE convention, a uniformly convex scalar flux is a $C^2$ flux satisfying $F''(z)\geq\kappa>0$ throughout the specified state interval, with one constant $\kappa$. Equivalently, $F(z)-\kappa z^2/2$ is a <convex function>. This guarantees strict inequality below nontrivial chords and strict ordering of the two endpoint <derivatives> around the chord slope. On the whole real line it is incompatible with a globally bounded first <derivative>.

= Viscous scalar conservation law
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$u_t+F(u)_x=\varepsilon u_{xx}$}

Positive $\varepsilon$ adds diffusion to a one-dimensional <scalar conservation law>. For a sufficiently decaying smooth solution, the divergence structure gives $\frac12\frac d{dt}\|u\|_2^2+\varepsilon\|u_x\|_2^2=0$, so the $L^2$ <energy estimate> is uniform in $\varepsilon$. If $|F'|\leq M$, testing against $-u_{xx}$ also yields $\|u_x(t)\|_2^2\leq e^{M^2t/\varepsilon}\|u_x(0)\|_2^2$. Thin <travelling waves> explain why a uniform <gradient> bound generally fails.

= Vanishing viscosity approximation
{parent=Viscous scalar conservation law}
{title2=$\varepsilon\downarrow0$}

This approximation studies the limit of a <viscous scalar conservation law> as its positive diffusion coefficient tends to zero. Under hypotheses giving compactness and appropriate convergence, the viscous entropy dissipation selects an <entropy solution> of the inviscid <scalar conservation law>. Fixed-phase decreasing <travelling waves> for a strictly convex flux converge to an <entropy shock>. Translating each profile differently can change or destroy the limit, so phase or initial data must specify the family.

= Maximum representation for a concave conservation law
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$U(t,x)=\max_y\{U_0(y)+tg((x-y)/t)\}$}

Let $f$ be smooth and strictly concave, with $f(0)=0$ and $f'$ bijective, and let $u_0$ be smooth with compact support. For $0<t<t_*$ in the smooth <concave-flux characteristic lifespan>, the primitive $U_x=u$ solves the <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> $U_t+f(U_x)=0$ and obeys
$$
U(t,x)=\max_y\left\{U(0,y)+t g\left(\frac{x-y}{t}\right)\right\},
$$
where $g$ is the <concave Legendre dual>. The tangent inequality for a <concave function> bounds every candidate by $U(t,x)$, and the backward <characteristic curve> attains equality. Its foot $x_0$ is the unique maximizer, and $u(t,x)=h((x-x_0)/t)$ for $h=(f')^{-1}$. This statement concerns the smooth solution, without asserting a post-crossing continuation.

= Square-root decay before characteristic crossing
{parent=Maximum representation for a concave conservation law}
{title2=$\|u(t)\|_\infty\le Kt^{-1/2}$}

Under the <inverse-flux quadratic bounds>, smooth compactly supported data in a <scalar conservation law> satisfy
$$
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_\infty\le-2k_-\sqrt{\frac{2\|u_0\|_1}{-k_+}}\,t^{-1/2}\qquad(0<t<t_*).
$$
At the maximizing foot $x_0$, the <maximum representation for a concave conservation law> bounds $U$ below by $-\|u_0\|_1$ and above by $\|u_0\|_1+k_+(x-x_0-ct)^2/t$. This bounds the deviation of the <characteristic speed> from $c$; the linear inverse-flux bound then controls $u$. The time interval ends at the first <characteristic crossing>.

= Characteristic solution of a scalar conservation law
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$u(t,X_t(\xi))=u_0(\xi)$}

Before <characteristic crossing>, the <scalar conservation law> $u_t+f(u)_x=0$ has solution $u(t,X_t(\xi))=u_0(\xi)$ with $X_t(\xi)=\xi+t f'(u_0(\xi))$. When this map is a <diffeomorphism>, the solution is $u_0\circ X_t^{-1}$. Its spatial derivative is $u_0'(\xi)/(1+t f''(u_0(\xi))u_0'(\xi))$.

= Concave-flux characteristic lifespan
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$t_*$}

For smooth compactly supported data in the <scalar conservation law> $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, put $m=\min_\xi f''(u_0(\xi))u_0'(\xi)$. Its smooth <characteristic solution of a scalar conservation law> exists until $t_*=-1/m$ if $m<0$, and for all times if $m\ge0$. The <characteristic flow map> $X_t(\xi)=\xi+t f'(u_0(\xi))$ has derivative $1+t f''(u_0)u_0'$; its first zero produces gradient blowup. The formula applies to smooth fluxes of either concavity, although it is particularly useful for a <concave function> flux.

= Bounded-derivative Burgers flux
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{title2=$f(z)=z^2/2\ (|z|\le1),\quad f(z)=|z|-1/2\ (|z|\ge1)$}

This $C^1$ flux has $|f'|\le1$ globally and agrees with the <inviscid Burgers equation> flux on $[-1,1]$. It allows Burgers examples to satisfy a global bounded-speed hypothesis. For Riemann data $-1$ to the left and $1$ to the right, both the stationary expansion discontinuity and the fan $u=x/t$ on $|x|<t$, with exterior values $\pm1$, are bounded weak solutions. Only the fan is an <entropy solution>.

= Shock wave
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{wiki}

= Shock
{synonym}

A shock wave is a moving discontinuity of a <weak solution> to a conservation law. Its speed is fixed by the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>, while an entropy condition selects the physically admissible direction of compression.

= Viscous shock
{parent=Shock wave}

A <viscous shock> is a smooth travelling transition produced when positive <diffusion> resolves a <shock> of a <conservation law>. For $u_t+q(u)_x=\epsilon[D(u)u_x]_x$ with $D>0$, an inner coordinate $X=(x-ct)/\epsilon$ gives $D(U)U'=q(U)-q(u_-)-c(U-u_-)$. The endpoint conditions impose the Rankine-Hugoniot speed. If $q$ is strictly concave, the right side is positive between its roots, so the admissible profile increases from its rear state to its front state. This supplies the entropy selection directly from a regularizing mechanism.

= Shock speed in a symmetric cloud collision
{parent=Shock wave}
{title2=$S=(\gamma-1)V/2$}

Two identical cold clouds approach at speeds $\pm V$ in their center-of-momentum frame. <Reflection symmetry> makes the uniformly compressed gas stationary. A shock moving outward at $S$ sees upstream speed $V+S$ and downstream speed $S$; their ratio equals the strong adiabatic compression $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. Solving gives the displayed speed. For a <monatomic gas> $S=V/3$. Finite-cloud breakout ends the steady upstream supply and requires a subsequent boundary-release calculation.

= Radiative shock
{parent=Shock wave}

A <shock wave> with significant radiation losses in its downstream evolution. Often a narrow dissipative jump initially heats the gas according to adiabatic <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions>, followed by a much longer <radiative cooling> layer. The gas's <specific entropy> increases at the jump and may then fall as radiation carries <entropy> away. If both distant states have equal <temperature>, the combined <mass> and <momentum> balances give an <isothermal shock>; the gas's <energy> flux is not conserved across the whole radiating region.

= Shock cooling length
{parent=Radiative shock}
{title2=$\ell_{\rm cool}\sim u_2t_{\rm cool}$}

The distance a downstream fluid element travels while losing a substantial fraction of its thermal <energy>. Multiplying the immediate downstream <velocity> by a <radiative gas cooling time> gives an order-of-magnitude length. A resolved layer instead uses $\ell=\int u(t)\,dt$, because <mass density>, <temperature> and speed change during cooling. It is distinct from the microscopic dissipative thickness of the <shock wave>.

= Blast wave
{parent=Shock wave}
{wiki}

A <blast wave> is the expanding disturbance produced by localized rapid release of <energy>, initially led by a strong <shock wave>. An energy-conserving <self-similar blast wave> ties front speed to the mass swept up. Once the front is only weakly supersonic, a strong-shock approximation ceases to apply; a subsequent <acoustic wave> is not excluded by that transition.

= Atmospheric blast-wave range
{parent=Blast wave}
{title2=$D_s\sim[E/(\pi\Sigma c_a^2)]^{1/2}$}

For an explosion confined approximately to a thin atmospheric layer with column <mass density> $\Sigma=\int\rho\,dz$, an optimistic conserved-energy scaling is $E\sim\pi D^2\Sigma U^2$. The strong <blast wave> approximation becomes invalid near front speed $U\sim c_a$, where $c_a$ is the ambient <speed of sound>, giving the displayed distance scale. Its numerical coefficient depends on the flow profiles, geometry and <heat capacity ratio>. Vertical energy escape makes full confinement an optimistic approximation. The criterion estimates survival of a strong <shock wave>, rather than whether any later <acoustic wave> is detectable.

= Rankine-Hugoniot conditions
{c}
{parent=Shock wave}
{wiki=Rankine–Hugoniot_conditions}
{title2=$\sigma[u]=[f(u)]$}

= Rankine-Hugoniot condition
{c}
{synonym}

The <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> expresses <conservation laws> across a moving discontinuity of a <weak solution>. Across a discontinuity $x=\sigma t$ in $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, with left and right states $u_-$ and $u_+$, conservation requires
$$
\sigma(u_+-u_-)=f(u_+)-f(u_-).
$$
Writing $[u]=u_+-u_-$ and $[f(u)]=f(u_+)-f(u_-)$ gives $\sigma[u]=[f(u)]$. The same relation holds componentwise for a <system of conservation laws>; in <compressible flow>, conservation of <mass>, <momentum> and <energy> supplies the jump conditions for a <shock wave>. Integrating the <weak solution> identity over a thin space-time region crossing the discontinuity yields this balance between the swept-up conserved quantity and the difference of fluxes.

= Entropy shock
{parent=Shock wave}
{title2=$F'(u_r)<\sigma<F'(u_l)$}

For a scalar equation with strictly convex flux and states $u_l>u_r$, the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> determines a discontinuity speed strictly between the right and left <characteristic speeds>. <Characteristic curves> enter this compressive discontinuity from both sides. Its <entropy flux for a scalar conservation law> inequalities make it an <entropy solution>. It is the limit of a phase-normalized decreasing <travelling wave> in the <vanishing viscosity approximation>.

= Rarefaction wave
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{wiki}

A rarefaction wave is a continuous expanding fan of characteristics that connects two constant states of a hyperbolic conservation law.

= Shallow-water rarefaction from a receding wall
{parent=Rarefaction wave}
{title2=$u=\frac23(x/t-c_0),\quad c=\frac13(x/t+2c_0)$}

A resting <shallow water> layer has depth $h_0$ and <wave speed> $c_0=\sqrt{gh_0}$. A vertical wall receding into negative $x$ at speed $0<V<2c_0$ creates a <simple wave> with invariant $u-2c=-2c_0$. Inside the expansion fan $x/t=u+c$, proving the displayed profiles. The wall-adjacent state is $u=-V$, $c=c_0-V/2$; the fan occupies $c_0-3V/2<x/t<c_0$, with the resting state ahead. The wall lies at $x/t=-V$, strictly to the left of the fan tail because $c_0-V/2>0$. Thus the assumption on wall speed is exactly the condition for positive depth and continued contact between fluid and wall. The depth follows from $h=c^2/g$.

= Self-similar ideal-gas rarefaction
{parent=Rarefaction wave}
{title2=$\frac{du}{d\xi}=\frac2{\gamma+1}$}

For $\gamma>1$ and positive <mass density> and <pressure>, let $\xi=x/t$ in one-dimensional adiabatic <compressible flow>. The smooth equations are $(u-\xi)\rho'+\rho u'=0$, $(u-\xi)u'+p'/\rho=0$ and $(u-\xi)p'+\gamma p u'=0$. Away from a constant branch, their coefficient determinant forces $\xi=u+\sigma c$ with $\sigma=\pm1$ and <adiabatic sound speed> $c=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$. The entropy equation gives $p=K\rho^\gamma$, and combining continuity with $c'/c=(\gamma-1)\rho'/(2\rho)$ gives
$$
u'=\frac2{\gamma+1},\qquad c'=\sigma\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1},\qquad u-\frac{2\sigma c}{\gamma-1}=J.
$$
Thus $u=[2\xi+(\gamma-1)J]/(\gamma+1)$, $c=\sigma(\gamma-1)(\xi-J)/(\gamma+1)$, $\rho=(c^2/(\gamma K))^{1/(\gamma-1)}$ and $p=K\rho^\gamma$, on intervals where $c>0$. The <specific entropy> is constant. Together with uniform branches, these exhaust smooth positive-gas self-similar solutions; a <contact discontinuity> is not a smooth fan.

= Inviscid Burgers equation
{title2=$u_t+uu_x=0$}
{c}
{parent=Scalar conservation law}
{wiki}

The <inviscid Burgers equation> is the <scalar conservation law>
$$
u_t+\left(\frac{u^2}{2}\right)_x=0.
$$
For a <classical solution>, the <chain rule> makes this equivalent to $u_t+uu_x=0$, a nonlinear <transport equation> with <characteristic speed> $u$. The conservative form remains meaningful for a discontinuous <weak solution>, whereas the product $uu_x$ cannot simply be evaluated pointwise at a jump. Its discontinuities obey the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> $s=(u_L+u_R)/2$; this condition alone permits inadmissible expansion jumps. For bounded <initial conditions> on the infinite line, imposing the <Kruzhkov entropy inequalities> and the initial trace selects a unique <entropy solution>. For step data, incoming <characteristic curves> select $u_L>s>u_R$, while $u_L<u_R$ produces a <rarefaction wave>.<Riemann Problems and Jupyter Solutions by David I. Ketcheson, Randall J. LeVeque, and Mauricio J. del Razo/Burgers' equation>[Shock solution; Rarefaction wave; The entropy condition]{ref}

= Periodic square-wave relaxation for the inviscid Burgers equation
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{title2=$u(x,t)=\tfrac12+t^{-1}(\{x-t/2\}-\tfrac12),\quad t\geq1$}

Take period-one data equal to one on $[k-1/2,k]$ and zero on $(k,k+1/2)$. Before $t=1$, the separate <rectangular pulse solutions of the inviscid Burgers equation> do not overlap in their interiors. At $t=1$ each shock meets the two neighboring fans. Afterward, on the interval between shocks $s_{k-1}$ and $s_k$, the solution remains $(x-k+1/2)/t$. The <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives $s_k'=(s_k-k)/t$ and $s_k(1)=k+1/2$, hence $s_k=k+t/2$.

The title formula uses the <fractional part> and chooses right-hand shock values. Its left and right states are $1/2+1/(2t)$ and $1/2-1/(2t)$, making every shock a causal <entropy shock> of speed $1/2$. Therefore $\sup_x|u(x,t)-1/2|\leq1/(2t)$: the periodic <entropy solution> converges uniformly to its conserved mean even though it has a jump at every finite time. Periodizing a nonlinear solution is valid here only before the supports interact; after interaction the matching shock calculation is essential.

= Rectangular pulse solution of the inviscid Burgers equation
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{title2=$t_*=2L/a,\quad s(t)=x_L+\sqrt{2aLt}\quad(t\geq t_*)$}

= Rectangular pulse solutions of the inviscid Burgers equation
{synonym}

Start the <inviscid Burgers equation> with height $a>0$ on $[x_L,x_L+L]$, $L>0$, and zero elsewhere. The upward left jump gives a <rarefaction wave> $u=(x-x_L)/t$, while the downward right jump gives an <entropy shock> at $x_L+L+at/2$. They meet at $t_*=2L/a$. Afterward the entire nonzero profile is the triangular fan on $x_L<x<s(t)$, with $s(t)=x_L+\sqrt{2aLt}$ and zero outside. To prove the continuation, the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives $s'=(s-x_L)/(2t)$, and matching at $t_*$ fixes the integration constant. The triangle has conserved mass $aL$, and its left shock state $(s-x_L)/t$ is twice the shock speed, certifying causality.

= Burgers characteristic breaking
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{c}
{title2=$x=\xi+t u_0(\xi),\quad u_x=\frac{u_0'(\xi)}{1+t u_0'(\xi)}$}

Before <characteristic crossing>, the <method of characteristics> for the <inviscid Burgers equation> transports each initial value without changing it:
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=u,\qquad \frac{du}{dt}=0,\qquad x=\xi+t u_0(\xi),\qquad u(x,t)=u_0(\xi).
$$
Here $\xi$ labels the starting position. Suppose $u_0$ is a bounded <continuously differentiable function> with bounded <derivative>. As long as $1+t u_0'(\xi)>0$ for every label, the <characteristic flow map> is invertible and differentiating it gives the displayed formula for $u_x$.<Partial Differential Equations by Victor Ivrii/First order PDEs>[§2.1, Quasilinear equations, Example 7, (2.1.8)–(2.1.9)]{ref}

If the negative minimum $m=\min_\xi u_0'(\xi)$ is attained, the first breaking time is $t_*=-1/m$. Two labels $\xi_1<\xi_2$ with $u_0(\xi_1)>u_0(\xi_2)$ have formal intersection time
$$
t=\frac{\xi_2-\xi_1}{u_0(\xi_1)-u_0(\xi_2)}>0.
$$
Their neighboring-label limit explains why the most negative initial slope breaks first. As $t\uparrow t_*$, the amplitude remains bounded but $u_x$ diverges: this is <gradient blow-up>. Continuing intersecting lines would assign incompatible values at one position; the post-breaking <entropy solution> instead uses <shock waves>. Nondecreasing data have no forward <characteristic crossing> by this mechanism.

For a concrete calculation in <dimensionless quantities> on the infinite line, choose $u_0(\xi)=1-\tanh\xi$, with limiting states $2$ and $0$. Then $u_0'(\xi)=-(1-\tanh^2\xi)$ has minimum $-1$ at $\xi=0$, so $t_*=1$ and the breaking point is $(x,t)=(1,1)$. Along the central <characteristic curve> $x=t$, $u=1$ and $u_x=-1/(1-t)$ before breaking. The labels $\xi=\pm1$ give intersecting formal lines at $t=1/\tanh1\approx1.313$, after the <classical solution> has already broken.

= Burgers Riemann problem with positive flux
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{c}
{title2=$f(u)=u^2/2,\quad s=(u_L+u_R)/2$}

Consider the <Riemann problem> on $\mathbb R$ with <initial condition> $u_0(x)=u_L$ for $x<0$ and $u_0(x)=u_R$ for $x>0$. No endpoint <boundary conditions> are imposed. When $u_L>u_R$, its <entropy solution> is a <shock wave> with constant speed $s=(u_L+u_R)/2$. In the original example $u_L=2$, $u_R=0$, the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives
$$
s=\frac{f(u_R)-f(u_L)}{u_R-u_L}=\frac{0-2}{0-2}=1,\qquad u(x,t)=\begin{cases}2,&x<t,\\0,&x>t.\end{cases}
$$
The left <characteristic speed> is $2$ and the right <characteristic speed> is $0$, so $2>1>0$: both sides enter the <entropy shock>. The <viscous Burgers travelling shock> gives an exact smooth family tending to this solution.

Reversing the states to $u_L=0$, $u_R=2$ still gives a <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> speed of $1$, but now <characteristic curves> move away from the discontinuity. That expansion jump is a <weak solution> and fails entropy admissibility. For general $u_L<u_R$, the selected <entropy solution> is the continuous <self-similar solution>
$$
u(x,t)=\begin{cases}u_L,&x\leq u_Lt,\\x/t,&u_Lt<x<u_Rt,\\u_R,&x\geq u_Rt,\end{cases}\qquad t>0.
$$
This <rarefaction wave> fills the expanding gap between the two exterior <characteristic curves>. Inside its fan, $u_t=-x/t^2$ and $uu_x=x/t^2$, verifying the <inviscid Burgers equation>; its values agree continuously with both exterior states. For $0$ to $2$, the fan occupies $0<x<2t$. If $u_L=u_R$, the solution is constant.<Riemann Problems and Jupyter Solutions by David I. Ketcheson, Randall J. LeVeque, and Mauricio J. del Razo/Burgers' equation>[Rarefaction wave, full piecewise solution; expansion-shock example; The entropy condition]{ref}

= Periodic backward-sawtooth Burgers solution
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}

For the negative-flux equation $f_Z-ff_\theta=0$, a period-two increasing initial ramp develops rarefaction fans at odd points and steepens at even points. Before $Z=1$, its central ramp is $f=(\theta-2m)/(1-Z)$ on $|\theta-2m|<1-Z$, and its odd-point fans are $f=-(\theta-(2m+1))/Z$. After $Z=1$, stationary compressive shocks occupy the even points and $f=(2m+1-\theta)/Z$ between consecutive shocks. The amplitude decays as $1/Z$. The <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> and incoming <characteristic speeds> certify the post-breaking <entropy solution>.

= Burgers Riemann problem with negative flux
{c}
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{title2=$F(f)=-f^2/2$}

For left state zero and right state $U$, negative-flux <inviscid Burgers equation> gives a shock at $\theta=-UZ/2$ when $U>0$, and a <rarefaction wave> $f=-\theta/Z$ on $0<\theta<-UZ$ when $U<0$. This is the standard positive-flux equation after setting $u=-f$; <entropy solution> selection reverses which step direction is compressive.

= Entropy solution
{parent=Inviscid Burgers equation}
{wiki}

An entropy solution is a <weak solution> of a nonlinear <scalar conservation law> that also satisfies entropy inequalities selecting physically admissible shocks. For convex fluxes, this excludes expansion shocks and restores uniqueness for bounded initial data.

= Kruzhkov entropy inequality
{parent=Entropy solution}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_t|u-c|+\partial_xq_c(u)\le0$}

For a scalar <conservation law> law $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, the Kruzhkov entropy inequalities require
$$
\partial_t|u-c|+\partial_x\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(u-c)[f(u)-f(c)]\bigr)\le0
$$
in distributions for every real constant $c$, together with the initial trace. This means the integral against every nonnegative compactly supported test function is nonpositive after applying the distributional derivatives. For a smooth solution the entropy balance is an equality away from $u=c$. For a viscous approximation $u_t+f(u)_x=\varepsilon u_{xx}$, smooth convex approximations $\eta$ of $|u-c|$ give $\partial_t\eta(u)+\partial_xq(u)=\varepsilon\partial_{xx}\eta(u)-\varepsilon\eta''(u)u_x^2\le\varepsilon\partial_{xx}\eta(u)$, with $q'=\eta'f'$. Letting the entropy smoothing vanish and then taking a bounded, strongly locally convergent zero-viscosity limit proves the inequality. The discrete entropy inequality of a monotone conservative scheme is its numerical analogue.

= Discrete entropy inequality
{parent=Entropy solution}

For a scalar conservative monotone update with flux $F$, define $Q(a,b;c)=F(a\vee c,b\vee c)-F(a\wedge c,b\wedge c)$. Monotonicity and preservation of constant states give $|U_j^{n+1}-c|\leq|U_j^n-c|-(k/d)(Q_{j+1/2}-Q_{j-1/2})$. At equal states $Q(u,u;c)=\operatorname{sgn}(u-c)(f(u)-f(c))$, the <Kruzhkov entropy flux>. Such discrete inequalities connect stability of a <monotone conservative scheme> with the admissibility of its limiting <entropy solution>.

= Averaged initial trace of an entropy solution
{parent=Entropy solution}
{title2=$\delta^{-1}\int_0^\delta\|u(t)-u_0\|_{L^1(K)}dt\longrightarrow0$}

For a bounded <entropy solution> with its initial entropy inequalities, the displayed limit holds on every compact spatial set. Test a constant-level entropy with $(1-t/\delta)_+\rho(x)$. The flux remainder is $O(\delta)$, giving a bound by $\int\rho|u_0-k|$. A finite smooth partition and constant levels approximate the initial function in local $L^1$; the triangle inequality makes the limsup arbitrarily small. This averaged trace suffices for one-sided time mollifiers supported in $[\delta,2\delta]$.

= Entropy flux for a scalar conservation law
{parent=Entropy solution}
{title2=$q'=\eta'f'$}

A convex entropy $\eta$ is paired with any flux antiderivative satisfying $q'(z)=\eta'(z)f'(z)$ almost everywhere. Its admissibility inequality is $\partial_t\eta(u)+\partial_xq(u)\le0$ distributionally, with the matching initial entropy trace. Affine entropies $\eta(z)=\pm z$ recover the <weak formulation>, while bounded classical solutions satisfy equality for smooth entropies and then by approximation for piecewise smooth convex entropies.

= Kruzhkov entropy flux
{c}
{parent=Entropy flux for a scalar conservation law}
{title2=$q_k(z)=\operatorname{sgn}(z-k)(f(z)-f(k))$}

For $\eta_k(z)=|z-k|$, the displayed flux is continuous at $z=k$ and has <derivative> $\operatorname{sgn}(z-k)f'(z)$ almost everywhere. On a bounded state range with $|f'|\le M$, its symmetric two-state version $Q(a,b)$ obeys $|Q(a,b)|\le M|a-b|$ and is Lipschitz in both arguments. The vanishing factor at $a=b$ removes any sign-function jump contribution.

= Doubling of variables for scalar conservation laws
{parent=Kruzhkov entropy flux}

Apply the <entropy solution> inequality for $u(t,x)$ with level $v(s,y)$ and for $v(s,y)$ with level $u(t,x)$, integrate both in the other pair of variables, and add them. Symmetry of the <Kruzhkov entropy flux> produces <derivatives> $\partial_t+\partial_s$ and $\partial_x+\partial_y$. A nonnegative mollifier concentrated near the diagonal then yields the <Kato inequality for scalar conservation laws>. Boundary-time terms require an initial trace argument, not merely interior translation continuity.

= Kato inequality for scalar conservation laws
{c}
{parent=Doubling of variables for scalar conservation laws}
{title2=$\partial_t|u-v|+\partial_xQ(u,v)\le0$}

For bounded <entropy solutions> of the same scalar law, $Q(u,v)=\operatorname{sgn}(u-v)(f(u)-f(v))$ satisfies the displayed distributional inequality, with initial value $|u_0-v_0|$ in its test-function form. The <doubling of variables for scalar conservation laws>, local translation continuity and the <averaged initial trace of an entropy solution> prove it. Its significance is comparison between two solutions rather than an inequality against a constant state.

= Local L1 contraction for scalar conservation laws
{parent=Kato inequality for scalar conservation laws}
{title2=$\int_{a-M(t-s)}^{b+M(t-s)}|u(s)-v(s)|\le\int_{a-Mt}^{b+Mt}|u_0-v_0|$}

If $|f'|\le M$ on the solutions' state range, the <Kato inequality for scalar conservation laws> implies the displayed estimate for almost every $s\in[0,t]$. Approximate the shrinking interval by smooth cutoffs $W$ satisfying $W_s+M|W_x|\le0$ and multiply by a temporal cutoff ending at $s$. Since $|Q|\le M|u-v|$, the lateral flux cannot increase the integral. The estimate gives uniqueness and a <finite propagation speed> without global $L^1$ assumptions on the data.

= Order preservation for scalar entropy solutions
{parent=Local L1 contraction for scalar conservation laws}

Subtract the weak equation for $u-v$ from its <Kato inequality for scalar conservation laws> and divide by two. The result is an inequality for $(v-u)_+$ with a flux bounded in magnitude by $M(v-u)_+$. The same shrinking-interval argument proves $u_0\ge v_0\Rightarrow u\ge v$ almost everywhere. Choosing the constant solution $v=0$ proves preservation of nonnegativity even when $f(0)\ne0$. Two-sided absolute-value contraction alone should not be mistaken for this one-sided comparison proof.

= Characteristic crossing
{parent=Scalar conservation law}

Characteristic crossing occurs when the map from an initial point to its position at time $t$ loses injectivity. For $u_t+f(u)_x=0$, its Jacobian is $1+t f''(u_0)u_0'$ in the autonomous case, so a negative value of $f''(u_0)u_0'$ causes finite-time crossing.

= Gradient blow-up
{parent=Characteristic crossing}

Gradient blow-up means that a solution stays bounded while the norm of a spatial derivative tends to infinity. In a scalar conservation law this happens as characteristics meet, because the derivative contains the reciprocal of the characteristic-map Jacobian.
