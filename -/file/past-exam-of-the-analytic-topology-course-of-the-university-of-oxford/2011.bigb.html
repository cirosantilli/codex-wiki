= Solution
{parent=1}

**The extension.** <Urysohn's lemma> states that disjoint <closed subsets> $A,B$ of a <normal topological space> can be separated by a <continuous function> $u:X\to[0,1]$ with $u|_A=0$ and $u|_B=1$.

For the bounded <Tietze extension theorem>, first suppose $g:C\to[-M,M]$ is continuous. The subsets $A=\{g\leq-M/3\}$ and $B=\{g\geq M/3\}$ are closed in $X$, because $C$ is closed. <Urysohn's lemma> supplies a <continuous function> $h:X\to[-M/3,M/3]$ with $h=-M/3$ on $A$ and $h=M/3$ on $B$. Directly checking the two outer ranges and the middle range of $g$ gives
$$
\|h\|_\infty\leq M/3,\qquad\|g-h|_C\|_\infty\leq2M/3.
$$
Empty level sets cause no difficulty: take the appropriate constant, or zero if both are empty. This is the <Urysohn two-thirds approximation>.

Apply this successively to the residual on $C$, beginning with $f$ and $M_0=1$. We get $h_n$ with $\|h_n\|_\infty\leq\tfrac13(\tfrac23)^n$ and residual after the first $n$ terms at most $(2/3)^n$. The <Weierstrass M-test> gives <uniform convergence> of $F=\sum_{n\geq0}h_n$. By the <uniform limit theorem>, $F$ is continuous; the residual bound proves $F|_C=f$, and the sum of the norm bounds is one. Thus
$$
\boxed{F:X\to[-1,1]\text{ is continuous and }F|_C=f.}
$$

**Closed sets as exact zero sets.** If $X$ is a <perfectly normal space>, write a closed $C$ as $\bigcap_{n\geq0}U_n$ with each $U_n$ open. Apply <Urysohn's lemma> to $C$ and $X\setminus U_n$ to obtain $f_n:X\to[0,1]$ vanishing on $C$ and equal to one on $X\setminus U_n$. The uniformly convergent series
$$
\boxed{f=\sum_{n\geq0}2^{-n-1}f_n\quad\text{satisfies}\quad f^{-1}(0)=C.}
$$
Indeed all summands vanish on $C$; outside $C$, at least one summand is strictly positive. The <uniform limit theorem> proves continuity. This realizes the <closed G-delta set as a zero set> construction.

Conversely, suppose each closed set is the <zero set of a continuous function>. For disjoint closed $C,D$, select $f,g:X\to[0,1]$ with zero sets $C,D$. Their sum never vanishes, and
$$
\boxed{h=\frac{f}{f+g}:X\to[0,1],\qquad h^{-1}(0)=C,\quad h^{-1}(1)=D.}
$$
The quotient is continuous. This also proves normality by inverse images of disjoint endpoint neighborhoods. Each zero set is a <G-delta set>, since $f^{-1}(0)=\bigcap_{n\geq1}f^{-1}([0,1/n))$; thus this property characterizes <perfectly normal spaces> among <T1 spaces>.

**Subspaces.** Let $Y\subseteq X$ and let $A,B$ be disjoint <closed subsets> of $Y$. Put $C=\overline A^{\,X}$ and $D=\overline B^{\,X}$. Since $C\cap Y=A$ and $D\cap Y=B$, the intersection $C\cap D$ misses $Y$. Choose <continuous functions> $f,g$ on $X$ whose zero sets are $C,D$. On the <open neighborhood> $W=X\setminus(C\cap D)$ of $Y$, the same quotient $f/(f+g)$ is continuous and is zero on $A$, one on $B$. Its inverse images of $[0,1/3)$ and $(2/3,1]$, restricted to $Y$, are disjoint <open neighborhoods>. Therefore **every subspace is a <normal topological space>**: <perfect normality implies hereditary normality>.

**A strict counterexample to the converse.** Let $D$ be an uncountable <discrete space>, and let $X=D\cup\{\infty\}$ be its <one-point compactification>. Its neighborhoods of $\infty$ have finite complement in $D$, because compact subsets of a <discrete space> are finite. The space is compact Hausdorff, hence a <T1 space> and normal. A subspace missing $\infty$ is discrete. A subspace containing $\infty$ is closed in $X$, since its complement is a subset of the open discrete part; it is therefore compact Hausdorff and normal. Thus all subspaces are normal.

But $\{\infty\}$ is not a <G-delta set>. A countable intersection of its <open neighborhoods> omits at most countably many elements of $D$, and cannot omit all of uncountable $D$. Hence $X$ is not a <perfectly normal space>. This is the <uncountable discrete one-point compactification counterexample>.

**Compactifications.** A <Hausdorff compactification> of $X$ is a compact <Hausdorff space> $K$ together with a <topological embedding> $j:X\to K$ having dense image. We identify $X$ with that image.

For noncompact locally compact Hausdorff $X$, adjoin $\infty$ and give $X^+=X\cup\{\infty\}$ its <one-point compactification> topology: retain the open sets of $X$, and take $\{\infty\}\cup(X\setminus C)$, with $C$ compact, as a neighborhood base at $\infty$. Compact subsets are closed, making this a topology with the original <subspace topology> on $X$.

An open cover contains a neighborhood of $\infty$, leaving a compact subset of $X$ to cover by finitely many more members. Hence $X^+$ is compact. Points of $X$ are separated using its Hausdorff property; to separate $x\in X$ from $\infty$, use local compactness to choose an open $U\ni x$ with compact closure and take $X^+\setminus\overline U$ at $\infty$. Finally every neighborhood of $\infty$ meets $X$, because $X$ itself is not compact. Thus $X$ is dense and **$X^+$ is a <Hausdorff compactification> with one-point remainder**.

**Comparison maps.** The <Stone-Čech compactification> of a <Tychonoff space> has the universal property that every <continuous map> from $X$ to a compact <Hausdorff space> extends continuously to $\beta X$. Applying it to any <Hausdorff compactification> $K$ gives a continuous surjection $\pi:\beta X\to K$ fixing $X$. Surjectivity follows because its compact image is closed and contains the dense set $X$.

We need the <fibers of compactification comparison maps> property
$$
\boxed{\pi^{-1}(X)=X.}
$$
Suppose $\pi(z)=x\in X$ with $z\ne x$. By regularity of compact Hausdorff $\beta X$, choose an <open neighborhood> $U$ of $x$ with $z\notin\overline U$. The embedding $X\subseteq K$ supplies open $V\subseteq K$ containing $x$ and satisfying $V\cap X\subseteq U\cap X$. The open set $\pi^{-1}(V)$ contains $z$. Density of $X$ implies $z\in\overline{\pi^{-1}(V)\cap X}=\overline{V\cap X}\subseteq\overline U$, a contradiction. Thus the displayed identity holds.

**Equivalence of (a), (b), (c).** Clearly (b) implies (c). If (c) holds in $K$, inverse images under $\pi$ and $\pi^{-1}(X)=X$ show that $X$ is a <G-delta set> in $\beta X$, giving (a). Conversely assume (a) and write $X=\bigcap_n U_n$ in $\beta X$. The sets $F_n=\beta X\setminus U_n$ are compact and miss $X$. Their images $\pi(F_n)$ are closed in $K$ and miss $X$ by the fiber identity. Since $\pi$ is onto, they exhaust $K\setminus X$. Hence
$$
\boxed{X=\bigcap_n\bigl(K\setminus\pi(F_n)\bigr).}
$$
This proves (b), for every $K$. This <G-delta compactification invariance> defines a <Čech-complete space>.

**The Baire property.** Suppose $X=\bigcap_n G_n$ in a compact Hausdorff $K$, with $X$ dense and all $G_n$ open. Each $G_n$ is dense. For dense open subsets $U_n$ of $X$, choose open $O_n\subseteq K$ with $U_n=X\cap O_n$. Every nonempty open subset of $K$ meets $X$ in a nonempty relatively open set and hence meets $U_n$, so each $O_n$ is dense in $K$.

The assumed <Baire category theorem> in $K$ makes $\bigcap_n(G_n\cap O_n)$ dense in $K$. This set lies in $X$ and equals $\bigcap_n U_n$, so it is also dense in $X$. Therefore **<Čech-complete spaces are Baire spaces>**.

Enumerate $\mathbb Q=\{q_n:n\in\omega\}$. Each $\mathbb Q\setminus\{q_n\}$ is open and dense in $\mathbb Q$, while their intersection is empty. Thus $\mathbb Q$ is not a <Baire space>, and consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ is not a }G_\delta\text{ in any Hausdorff compactification of }\mathbb Q.}
$$
If $\mathbb Q$ were a <G-delta set> in $\mathbb R$, it would also be one in $\mathbb R^+$: the subspace $\mathbb R$ is open in its <one-point compactification>, and adjoining that open condition to a countable intersection preserves the <G-delta set> property. Since $\mathbb Q$ is dense in $\mathbb R$ and hence in $\mathbb R^+$, this would be a <Hausdorff compactification> of $\mathbb Q$ contradicting the preceding conclusion. Therefore $\boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ is not }G_\delta\text{ in }\mathbb R}$.

{parent=3}

A <zero-dimensional space> has a base of <clopen sets>. A <totally disconnected space> has no <connected subset> with more than one point, equivalently all its <connected components> are singletons. We use these definitions in the stated Hausdorff setting.

If a <Hausdorff space> has a clopen base, any two different points can be separated by a <clopen set>. A <connected subset> containing both would be split into two nonempty disjoint relatively open sets. Thus **zero-dimensional implies totally disconnected** here.

**The compact converse, including the component lemma.** For a point $x$ of a compact Hausdorff $K$, put
$$
Q_x=\bigcap\{N:x\in N,\ N\text{ clopen in }K\}.
$$
This is its <quasicomponent>, a closed compact set. We prove the <Šura-Bura component lemma> that $Q_x$ equals the <connected component> of $x$.

Suppose $Q_x=A\cup B$ were a separation into nonempty disjoint relatively closed sets, with $x\in A$. Both are compact, hence closed in $K$. Normality of <compact Hausdorff spaces> gives disjoint <open neighborhoods> $U\supseteq A$, $V\supseteq B$. The compact set $H=K\setminus(U\cup V)$ misses $Q_x$. For each $y\in H$, choose a clopen neighborhood $N_y$ of $x$ missing $y$; these exist by the definition of $Q_x$. The open complements cover $H$, so finitely many suffice. Their intersection $N$ is clopen, contains $Q_x$, and lies in $U\cup V$. If $H$ is empty take $N=K$.

Now $N\cap U$ is clopen in $K$: it is open, and its complement is $(K\setminus N)\cup(N\cap V)$, also open. It contains $x$ and misses $B$, contradicting that $B\subseteq Q_x$ lies in every clopen neighborhood of $x$. Hence $Q_x$ is connected. Conversely every <connected subset> containing $x$ lies in every clopen neighborhood of $x$, so the <connected component> is contained in $Q_x$. Equality follows.

If $K$ is <totally disconnected>, then $Q_x=\{x\}$. Given an <open neighborhood> $O$ of $x$, for each $y\in K\setminus O$ choose a clopen neighborhood of $x$ missing $y$. Compactness again gives a finite intersection $N$ with $x\in N\subseteq O$. These $N$ form a clopen base. Therefore
$$
\boxed{K\text{ compact Hausdorff}:\qquad K\text{ zero-dimensional}\iff K\text{ totally disconnected}.}
$$

**The Stone-Čech characterization.** The stated clopen separation property is <strong zero-dimensionality> in this normal Hausdorff setting. A normal <T1 space> is a <Tychonoff space>, so its <Stone-Čech compactification> exists. We use its extension property for maps into <compact Hausdorff spaces> and the density of $X$.

First assume $\beta X$ is a <zero-dimensional space>, and let $C,D$ be disjoint <closed subsets> of $X$. <Urysohn's lemma> gives $f:X\to[0,1]$ equal to zero on $C$ and one on $D$. Its continuous extension to $\beta X$ puts $\overline C^{\,\beta X}$ and $\overline D^{\,\beta X}$ in disjoint zero and one level sets. Using a clopen base, cover the first compact set by finitely many <clopen sets> missing the second; their union $H$ is clopen and separates them. Thus $U=H\cap X$ and $V=X\setminus U$ are disjoint <clopen sets> containing $C,D$.

Conversely, assume the stated clopen separation property on $X$. Let $A,B$ be disjoint <closed subsets> of $\beta X$. Normality supplies a <continuous function> $f:\beta X\to[0,1]$ with $f|_A=0$, $f|_B=1$. The sets
$$
C=X\cap f^{-1}([0,1/3]),\qquad D=X\cap f^{-1}([2/3,1])
$$
are disjoint and closed in $X$. Select clopen $U\subseteq X$ containing $C$ and missing $D$; the given disjoint pair of clopen neighborhoods supplies such a $U$. Its <characteristic function>, one on $U$ and zero outside, extends from $X$ to a <continuous map> $\beta X\to\{0,1\}$. Let $H$ be its one level set, a <clopen set>.

Every $a\in A$ is in $\overline C^{\,\beta X}$: any neighborhood of $a$, intersected with $f^{-1}([0,1/3))$, is an <open neighborhood> meeting the dense set $X$. Likewise $B\subseteq\overline D^{\,\beta X}$. Since $C\subseteq H$ and $D\subseteq\beta X\setminus H$, it follows that $A\subseteq H$ and $B\cap H=\varnothing$.

Apply this to $A=\{z\}$ and $B=\beta X\setminus O$, for any point $z$ and <open neighborhood> $O$. It yields a clopen $H$ with $z\in H\subseteq O$, proving that $\beta X$ has a clopen base. Hence the <strong zero-dimensionality and Stone-Čech compactification> criterion is
$$
\boxed{X\text{ has the stated clopen separation property}\iff\beta X\text{ is zero-dimensional}.}
$$
