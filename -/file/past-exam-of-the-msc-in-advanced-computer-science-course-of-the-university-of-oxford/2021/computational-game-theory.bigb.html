== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**$\ell_2\succ\ell_1$.** The distinction is small but strict: their normalized <expected utilities> are $18/25$ and $7/10$, respectively.

By the <Von Neumann-Morgenstern utility theorem>, use a representing <utility function> and apply a positive <affine function> to set $u(\omega_1)=1$ and $u(\omega_4)=0$. This is possible because the given <strict preference> implies $u(\omega_1)>u(\omega_4)$. The two specified <indifference relations> then give
$$
u(\omega_3)=\frac35,\qquad u(\omega_2)=\frac34+\frac14\frac35=\frac9{10}.
$$
Taking the <expected utility> of each <lottery> yields
$$
U(\ell_1)=\frac25+\frac15\frac9{10}+\frac15\frac35=\frac7{10},\qquad
U(\ell_2)=\frac25\frac9{10}+\frac35\frac35=\frac{18}{25}.
$$
Their difference is $1/50>0$, establishing the stated <strict preference>. These numbers measure <utility>, not monetary reward or an assumed ordering of the subscripts.

Here is the underlying argument directly in terms of the axioms. The <independence axiom for lottery preferences> permits <substitution of indifferent lotteries> inside a common mixture. Replacing $\omega_3$ by its given mixture of $\omega_1,\omega_4$, and then replacing $\omega_2$, followed by <reduction of compound lotteries>, gives
$$
\omega_2\sim\frac9{10}\omega_1+\frac1{10}\omega_4,
$$
and consequently
$$
\ell_1\sim\frac7{10}\omega_1+\frac3{10}\omega_4,
\qquad
\ell_2\sim\frac{18}{25}\omega_1+\frac7{25}\omega_4.
$$
The <transitive relation> axiom combines the successive substitutions. Finally, <mixture monotonicity> says that among mixtures of a strictly better and a strictly worse outcome, the greater <probability> of the better outcome is strictly preferred; this follows from the <independence axiom for lottery preferences> and <transitivity>. Since $18/25>7/10$ and $\omega_1\succ\omega_4$, the comparison follows. <Complete lottery preferences> and <continuity of lottery preferences> are part of the representation theorem's hypotheses; the direct substitution calculation uses the already supplied indifferences and does not need to invoke <continuity of lottery preferences> again.<Expected utility theory by EconPort>[“The Expected Utility Property” and the preceding preference axioms]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**No: these two strict comparisons violate the <independence axiom for lottery preferences>.** Let $z$ denote the certain zero-money <lottery>. <Reduction of compound lotteries> gives
$$
\ell_1=\frac14\ell_3+\frac34z,\qquad
\ell_2=\frac14\ell_4+\frac34z.
$$
For example, the second expression assigns <probability> $(1/4)(4/5)=1/5$ to the nonzero reward and $4/5$ to zero. The claimed $\ell_3\succ\ell_4$ must therefore imply
$$
\frac14\ell_3+\frac34z\succ\frac14\ell_4+\frac34z,
$$
which is $\ell_1\succ\ell_2$, contradicting the other claimed <strict preference>.

The same obstruction is visible in any proposed <expected utility> representation. Subtract $u(0)$ from the <utility function>, and write $a=u(3000)-u(0)$ and $b=u(4000)-u(0)$. The first comparison requires $(1/5)b>(1/4)a$, hence $(4/5)b>a$, whereas the second requires $a>(4/5)b$. No real $a,b$ satisfy both. This argument requires neither <risk neutrality> nor an assumption that the <utility function> is increasing in money.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $p=\Pr(T)$ and $q=\Pr(L)$, so the complementary <probabilities> are $1-p$ and $1-q$. All four expressions are the row player's <expected payoff>, including those indexed by a column action. Direct <expected value> calculations give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\pi_1(p,L)&=2p-(1-p)=3p-1,\\
\pi_1(p,R)&=-3p+(1-p)=1-4p,\\
\pi_1(T,q)&=2q-3(1-q)=5q-3,\\
\pi_1(B,q)&=-q+(1-q)=1-2q.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The column player's <expected payoff> is the negative of the row player's because this is a <zero-sum game>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The bold black curve is the <pointwise minimum> of the two opposing-column <expected payoff> functions. It is the row player's <security level payoff> at the specified <mixed strategy>:
$$
g(p)=\min(3p-1,1-4p)=
\begin{cases}3p-1,&0\le p\le2/7,\\1-4p,&2/7\le p\le1.\end{cases}
$$
The lines meet where $3p-1=1-4p$, hence at $p=2/7$. The first branch increases with slope $3$ and the second decreases with slope $-4$, so their intersection is the unique global <maximum> of the lower envelope:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{0\le p\le1}g(p)=\frac27,\qquad \max g=-\frac17.}
$$
The endpoint values are $g(0)=-1$ and $g(1)=-3$, both smaller. Since an opposing <mixed strategy> forms a <convex combination> of the two pure-column <expected payoffs>, it cannot make the row player's <expected payoff> lower than this envelope.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The bold black curve is now the <pointwise maximum> of the two pure-row <expected payoff> functions:
$$
h(q)=\max(5q-3,1-2q)=
\begin{cases}1-2q,&0\le q\le4/7,\\5q-3,&4/7\le q\le1.\end{cases}
$$
It measures the largest <expected payoff> that the row player can achieve against column <mixed strategy> $q$. The column player minimizes this quantity because its own <payoff> has the opposite sign. Equality of the two lines gives $5q-3=1-2q$, or $q=4/7$. The envelope decreases before that point and increases afterwards, giving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{0\le q\le1}h(q)=\frac47,\qquad \min h=-\frac17.}
$$
The endpoint values $h(0)=1$ and $h(1)=2$ are both larger. A row <mixed strategy> cannot exceed the greatest pure-row <expected payoff>, because its <expected payoff> is a <convex combination> of those two values.

{title=The column player's upper payoff envelope and its minimum at q = 4/7}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**The unique <Nash equilibrium> uses row <probabilities> $(2/7,5/7)$ on $(T,B)$ and column <probabilities> $(4/7,3/7)$ on $(L,R)$. The <value of a zero-sum game> to the row player is $-1/7$; the column player receives $1/7$.**

The first graph solves the row player's max–min <optimization>: choose a <mixed strategy> guaranteeing the greatest possible worst-case <expected payoff>. The second solves the column player's min–max <optimization>: choose a <mixed strategy> placing the smallest upper bound on the row player's <expected payoff>. Their optimal heights agree, illustrating the <minimax theorem>.

There is also a direct <Nash equilibrium> certificate, so the conclusion does not depend on reading coordinates from a plot. At $p^*=2/7$, both opposing pure columns give row <expected payoff> $-1/7$; at $q^*=4/7$, both pure rows give row <expected payoff> $-1/7$. By linearity, every <unilateral deviation>, including an arbitrary <mixed strategy>, leaves the deviator's <expected payoff> unchanged against the fixed opposing equilibrium mixture. Neither player can improve it. This is a <mixed-strategy optimality certificate for a matrix game>.

The equilibrium outcome is a <probability distribution> over cells, rather than one deterministic cell. <Independent> randomization gives
$$
\boxed{\Pr(TL)=\frac8{49},\quad \Pr(TR)=\frac6{49},\quad
\Pr(BL)=\frac{20}{49},\quad \Pr(BR)=\frac{15}{49}.}
$$
The resulting row <expected payoff> is $(16-18-20+15)/49=-1/7$. Finally, each envelope has a unique optimum, so any <Nash equilibrium> must use the displayed <optimal mixed strategies>. In particular there is no <pure-strategy> <Nash equilibrium>.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**Exactly $(T,L)$, $(M,C)$ and $(B,R)$ can be sustained as constant <stage game> outcomes. Their <long-run average payoffs> are $(6,6)$, $(4,4)$ and $(0,0)$, respectively.** We assume the usual <repeated game> monitoring convention: both players observe each completed joint action before the next round. The source's colour convention puts the blue lower-left row <payoff> first and the red upper-right column <payoff> second.

For necessity, row action $B$ earns $(8,5,0)$ against $(L,C,R)$, so playing it forever guarantees a nonnegative <long-run average payoff> against every opposing <pure strategy>. Similarly, column action $R$ earns $(8,5,0)$ against $(T,M,B)$ and guarantees zero or more. Conversely, column action $R$ holds the row player's best possible stage <payoff> to $0$, and row action $B$ holds the column player's to $0$. Thus each player's <minmax payoff> is exactly $0$.

Any <Nash equilibrium> must give both players at least zero, an <individually rational payoff in a repeated game>. Every off-diagonal cell has a negative <payoff> for one player: $(T,C)$ and $(T,R)$ hurt row, $(M,L)$ and $(B,L)$ hurt column, $(M,R)$ hurts row and $(B,C)$ hurts column. Repeating any such cell forever therefore permits a profitable <unilateral deviation> to the zero-guaranteeing action. The three diagonal cells pass this necessary test.

For sufficiency, fix target $a^*=(T,L)$ or $(M,C)$. Each player's <finite-state strategy> has initial state $F$ and an absorbing state $P$. In $F$, output the player's component of $a^*$; remain in $F$ if the completed joint action equals $a^*$, and otherwise move to $P$. In $P$, row always outputs $B$ and column always outputs $R$, regardless of further observations. These are two-state <grim trigger strategies>; the separate machines are drawn below. The input is the observed joint action, so after any off-path history the prescribed continuation states agree.

\Image[computational-game-theory-punishment-machines.png]
{title=Separate finite-state punishment strategies sustaining the two positive-payoff diagonal outcomes}

If neither player deviates, the target is repeated forever. If a player first deviates at a finite round $\tau$, the opponent enters $P$ for all later rounds. Against column action $R$, every row action earns at most zero; against row action $B$, every column action earns at most zero. All <payoffs> are bounded, so the total reward before and at $\tau$, divided by the horizon, tends to zero. The deviator's upper limiting average is therefore at most $0$, below either target <payoff> $6$ or $4$. This proof tests arbitrary future deviations, not merely a single changed action. It also rules out improvement by deviations whose averages fail to converge, if the criterion is extended by an upper or lower limit.

For target $(B,R)$, one-state machines outputting $B$ and $R$ forever suffice: each is a <best response> to the other in every round. Their <long-run average payoffs> are zero and no <unilateral deviation> can improve them. In fact, the punishment is credible because $(B,R)$ is a stage <Nash equilibrium>, so the two positive-target constructions are also <subgame perfect equilibria> under the stated monitoring assumption. The general punishment mechanism is described in <Lecture 15: Repeated Games by Asu Ozdaglar>[slides 12–15; those slides use discounting, while the finite-prefix argument here uses long-run averages]{ref}.

“Outcomes of the stage game” here means a fixed action pair repeated forever. Allowing time sharing also sustains some average <payoff> vectors that are not stage cells: for example, a deterministic alternating cycle $(T,L),(M,C)$ gives $(5,5)$, with a two-phase <finite-state strategy> and the same permanent punishment. An off-diagonal cell may occur in a sustainable cycle; the impossibility above concerns its constant repetition, not every appearance on an equilibrium path. With finite-state <pure strategies>, an undisturbed path is eventually periodic, so its limiting cell frequencies are <rational numbers>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the standard <repeated game> convention that the first round's joint action is observed before round two. The following original <game tree> represents simultaneous moves by making row move first in each round while hiding that current move from column. Terminal labels are **total <payoffs> over both rounds**, ordered row then column.

\Image[computational-game-theory-two-round-tree.png]
{title=The full two-round extensive-form game, with all sixteen terminal payoff pairs and five column information sets}

Formally, round one starts at a row decision with actions $T,B$. The two ensuing column nodes form one <information set> $I_2^0$, with available actions $L,R$; column cannot tell which current row action occurred. The four completed first-round histories are $H=\{TL,TR,BL,BR\}$. At each $h\in H$, round two begins at a separate row node. Its two successors, after row's current $T$ or $B$, form a column <information set> $I_2^h$. Distinct $h$ have distinct <information sets>: both players know the whole first-round outcome. There are sixteen terminal histories $(h,k)\in H^2$, whose total <payoff> is $u(h)+u(k)$, as displayed in the tree.

The row player's five <information sets> are the initial singleton and the four singleton round-two nodes, one per $h$. The column player's five <information sets> are $I_2^0$ and the four two-node sets $I_2^h$. Thus each player must specify one binary choice at each of five <information sets>, including histories that its own first choice might prevent:
$$
\boxed{|S_1|=|S_2|=2^5=32.}
$$
Counting only the four possible two-round action sequences would miss the distinct contingent responses to the opponent's first action.

For a concrete pair of complete <pure strategies>, order the second-round histories as $(TL,TR,BL,BR)$ and use
$$
s_1=(T;B,T,T,T),\qquad s_2=(L;R,L,L,L).
$$
The first entry is the initial action, and the four remaining entries prescribe the action after each first-round history. This induces the unique path $TL$ in round one followed by $BR$ in round two. Hence the total <payoff> is
$$
\boxed{(1,5)+(2,1)=(3,6).}
$$
The off-path entries are part of each <pure strategy>, even though this pair does not reach them.

For randomization, a <mixed strategy> is a <probability distribution> over the $32$ complete plans, with $31$ free <probabilities> in a general distribution. A <behavioural strategy> instead gives one binary-action <probability> at each of five <information sets>, requiring five parameters. This game has <perfect recall>: a player never forgets a previous own action or a first-round observation, and the only concealed action is the opponent's current simultaneous choice. Consequently **neither kind of randomization has a greater attainable-payoff advantage**: <Kuhn's theorem> gives <realization-equivalent strategies> against every fixed opposing strategy.<Extensive games and the problem of information by Harold W. Kuhn>[original article, pp. 193–216, equivalence under perfect recall]{ref}

The equivalence can be verified directly here. Given a <behavioural strategy>, sample all five contingent actions independently before play; the resulting product <probability distribution> over complete plans is an equivalent <mixed strategy>. Conversely, let $\mu$ be a <mixed strategy> for player $i$. Use its marginal <probability> for the initial action. At history $h$ containing own first action $a_i$, choose second action $b_i$ with <conditional probability>
$$
\beta_i(b_i\mid h)=
\frac{\mu\{s:s^0=a_i,\ s^h=b_i\}}
{\mu\{s:s^0=a_i\}},
$$
whenever the denominator is positive; if it is zero, choose arbitrarily at that unreachable history. The opponent's first-round action is simultaneous and cannot depend on the sampled hidden plan beyond the chosen first action. Thus conditioning on $h$ preserves exactly these <conditional probabilities> of actions, and multiplying the first- and second-round factors reproduces each terminal <probability>. Correlations between prescriptions at mutually exclusive second-round histories have no further observable effect. <Behavioural strategies> offer the more compact representation, while <mixed strategies> describe a single random choice of a full plan. Imperfect information here does not mean imperfect recall.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**If the <core> is nonempty, it is exactly the singleton allocation $p_j=v(\{j\})$ for every $j$.** Take any $p$ in the <core of a cooperative game> and write $p(C)=\sum_{j\in C}p_j$. Core membership gives efficiency of the <imputation>, $p(N)=v(N)$, and the <coalition> inequalities
$$
p(C)\ge v(C),\qquad p(N\setminus C)\ge v(N\setminus C).
$$
Using the given complementary-coalition equality in this <constant-sum cooperative game>, the second inequality yields
$$
p(C)=v(N)-p(N\setminus C)
\le v(N)-v(N\setminus C)=v(C).
$$
Combining upper and lower bounds gives $p(C)=v(C)$ for every <coalition> $C$. In particular,
$$
\boxed{p_j=p(\{j\})=v(\{j\})\quad\text{for every }j\in N.}
$$
Since this holds for any <core> member and at least one exists by hypothesis, the displayed allocation is in the <core> and is its only member. The argument also shows why nonemptiness is restrictive: it forces $v(C)=\sum_{j\in C}v(\{j\})$ for every <coalition>, so the <characteristic function of a coalitional game> must be additive. No <superadditive coalitional game> or monotonicity assumption is needed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**$G_1$ cannot have an <induced subgraph game> representation. $G_2$ is represented by a weighted triangle with edge weights $w_{12}=2$, $w_{13}=3$, $w_{23}=3$, and has <Shapley value> vector $(5/2,5/2,3)$.**

In an <induced subgraph game>, players are <graph vertices> and a <coalition> receives the sum of the weights of <graph edges> whose two endpoints lie in it. Therefore each two-player <coalition> determines the corresponding edge weight. For $G_1$ these weights would have to be $2,6,3$, making the grand <coalition> worth $2+6+3=11$, whereas its actual value is $15$. No other edge weights can repair that discrepancy without changing a two-player <coalition>'s value. Even a convention allowing vertex weights would not help: its singleton values force all such weights to zero.

For $G_2$, the stated weighted <graph triangle> has grand <coalition> value $2+3+3=8$ and reproduces all three pair values. Empty and singleton <coalitions> contain no <graph edges>, so their values are zero. This verifies all eight <coalitions> and supplies the complete representation.

To compute the <Shapley value>, reveal players in a uniformly random <permutation>. An edge's weight contributes to an endpoint's <marginal contribution> precisely when the other endpoint appeared earlier. Each endpoint is later with <probability> $1/2$, independently of where the third player appears. Linearity of the <expected value> therefore splits each edge weight equally between its two endpoints:
$$
\boxed{\phi_1=\frac{2+3}{2}=\frac52,\quad
\phi_2=\frac{2+3}{2}=\frac52,\quad
\phi_3=\frac{3+3}{2}=3.}
$$
The values sum to $8=v_2(N)$, as required by the <Shapley value>'s efficiency property. This is the general <Shapley value of an induced subgraph game> formula, not an assumption that all three players are symmetric: player $3$ has a different incident-weight total.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

**The <core> is the singleton $\{(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)\}$.** Here the <characteristic function of a coalitional game> is $v(C)=\lfloor |C|/2\rfloor$, the two-person instance of the <core of the miners game> calculation.

Let $x$ be in the <core>. Efficiency requires $x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=2$, and every pair <coalition> requires $x_i+x_j\ge1$. Summing the six pair inequalities counts each coordinate three times, giving $3\sum_i x_i\ge6$. Efficiency makes this an equality; since every individual slack is nonnegative, all six pair inequalities must be equalities. In particular $x_1+x_2=x_1+x_3=x_1+x_4=1$ forces $x_2=x_3=x_4$, and comparison with $x_2+x_3=1$ gives every $x_i=1/2$.

Conversely, for the uniform allocation every <coalition> satisfies
$$
\sum_{i\in C}x_i=\frac{|C|}{2}\ge\left\lfloor\frac{|C|}{2}\right\rfloor=v(C),
$$
and its total is $2=v(N)$. It is therefore a <core> member, completing both existence and uniqueness. In particular, a three-player <coalition> receives $3/2\ge1$; the pair constraints are the tight ones.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**The <core> is empty.** Efficiency would require $x_1+x_2+x_3=v(N)=1$. Each two-player <coalition> has value $1$, so <core> membership would also require
$$
x_1+x_2\ge1,\qquad x_1+x_3\ge1,\qquad x_2+x_3\ge1.
$$
Adding gives $2(x_1+x_2+x_3)\ge3$, contradicting efficiency, which makes the left side $2$. Thus no allocation can meet all <coalition> demands. The obstruction uses only pair <coalitions> and the grand <coalition>; imposing individual rationality cannot remove it. The equal allocation $(1/3,1/3,1/3)$ is efficient but gives each pair only $2/3<1$, so symmetry does not supply a <core> allocation.
