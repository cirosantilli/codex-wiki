= Computational game theory

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $X$ denote the monetary reward. Its <expected value> and <expected utility> are different quantities:
$$
\mathbb E X=\frac{89}{100}+\frac{10}{10}=\boxed{1.89},\qquad
\mathbb E[u(X)]=\frac{89}{100}+\frac{100}{10}=\frac{1089}{100}=10.89.
$$
Thus indifference to a sure nonnegative reward requires $k^2=10.89$, giving **$k=3.30$ dollars**. This is the <certainty equivalent>, hence the <lottery>'s monetary value in the question's comparison with a sure reward. The <utility function> $u(x)=x^2$ is increasing and <strictly convex> on nonnegative monetary amounts. Its <certainty equivalent> exceeds its <expected value>, $3.30>1.89$, demonstrating **<risk seeking>**; the same conclusion follows from the <Jensen inequality>.

The wording about paying an entry fee needs a qualification. A <certainty equivalent> compares a gross <lottery> with a sure gross reward; a literal <utility indifference price> also accounts for the fee's effect on wealth. If $u$ instead describes final wealth, with initial wealth $w$ and fee $c$, the comparison is $\mathbb E[(w-c+X)^2]=w^2$, on a domain where the <utility function> is increasing. Here $\operatorname{Var}(X)=7.3179$, so the nonnegative-wealth branch gives $c=w+1.89-\sqrt{w^2-7.3179}$ whenever that branch is admissible. An initial wealth and an admissible payment domain are not specified. Therefore **$3.30$ is the determined <certainty equivalent>**, while a literal wealth-dependent maximum fee is not uniquely determined. This is the distinction in <certainty equivalent and willingness to pay>. If negative rewards were allowed without requiring an increasing <utility function>, the equation for $k$ also has the algebraic root $-3.30$; the usual nonnegative-reward interpretation selects the positive root.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $A$ and $B$ for the two deterministic outcomes. Under the <expected utility hypothesis>, indifference between them implies $u(A)=u(B)=c$. The <expected utility> of the fair <lottery> is consequently
$$
U\left(\tfrac12 A+\tfrac12 B\right)=\tfrac12u(A)+\tfrac12u(B)=c.
$$
It must therefore be indifferent to each deterministic outcome, contradicting the asserted strict preference. **These <preferences> do not admit an <expected utility> representation over the stated outcomes.** Equivalently, <affine utility on probability measures> preserves this indifference under mixing, as required by the <independence axiom for lottery preferences>.

A concern for the fairness of the procedure can be perfectly intelligible. It would require enlarging the outcome description to include the procedure; it cannot be represented by an <expected utility> over only the two final gift recipients while retaining the stated strict preference.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First evaluate the inner <lottery>: $U(\ell_1)=12p+3(1-p)=3+9p$. By <reduction of compound lotteries>, the outer <expected utility> is
$$
U(\ell_2)=2p+(1-p)(3+9p)=3+8p-9p^2
=\frac{43}{9}-9\left(p-\frac49\right)^2.
$$
Since $4/9$ lies in the permitted <real interval>, the square is minimized there. The unique maximizer and maximum <expected utility> are **$p=4/9$ and $U(\ell_2)=43/9$**. Directly flattening the <lottery> gives <probabilities> $p(1-p)$, $(1-p)^2$ and $p$ for the three outcomes; these sum to one and give the same expression.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Reading the lower-left number in each source cell as player 1's <payoff>, against an opponent playing $A$ the player's own <payoffs> from $A,B$ are $y,0$; against $B$ they are $x,x/2$. For $x>0$, identify $A$ with aggressive defection and $B$ with yielding cooperation. The standard <payoffs> then have values
$$
T=x,\qquad R=x/2,\qquad S=0,\qquad P=y.
$$
The <prisoner's dilemma> ordering $T>R>P>S$ holds precisely for **$x>0$ and $0<y<x/2$** with this labeling. For example, $x=4,y=1$ gives $T=4,R=2,P=1,S=0$. Each player then has the <strictly dominant strategy> $A$, although $(B,B)$ gives both a larger <payoff> than $(A,A)$. Here $2R=T+S$ as well, so the optional weak aggregate-payoff condition is met with equality.

For the <Chicken payoff ordering>, the collision outcome must be worse than yielding to an aggressive opponent: $T>R>S>P$. This holds for **$x>0$ and $y<0$**, for example $x=4,y=-1$. Against $B$, the <best response> is $A$ because $x>x/2$; against $A$, it is $B$ because $0>y$. Consequently $(A,B)$ and $(B,A)$ are the two <pure strategy> <Nash equilibria>, and mutual aggression is the worst outcome for both.

If action names may be exchanged, there is another <prisoner's dilemma> region: take $A$ as cooperation and $B$ as defection. Then $(T,R,P,S)=(0,y,x/2,x)$, so **$x<0$ and $x/2<y<0$** also work, for example $x=-4,y=-1$. This relabeling explains why the first displayed region is not a necessary condition without an action convention. Equality at any of these boundaries destroys the strict ordering.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Represent satisfied and unsatisfied goals by <payoffs> one and zero. The second player's goal is an <exclusive OR>: it is true precisely when $q\ne r$, independently of $p$. The first goal is false only when all three <Boolean variables> are false. The resulting <payoff matrix> is below; each boxed entry marks that player's <best response> to the opposing <pure strategy>, including ties:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
 &pq&p\bar q&\bar p q&\bar p\bar q\\\hline
r&(\boxed{1},0)&(\boxed{1},\boxed{1})&(\boxed{1},0)&(\boxed{1},\boxed{1})\\
\bar r&(\boxed{1},\boxed{1})&(\boxed{1},0)&(\boxed{1},\boxed{1})&(0,0)
\end{array}
$$
For player 1, choosing $r$ satisfies the goal against every opposing <pure strategy>. Choosing $\bar r$ does equally well except against $\bar p\bar q$, where it does worse. Thus **$r$ is player 1's unique <dominant strategy> in the weak sense**, and there is no <strictly dominant strategy>. The ties are why most first-player entries in both rows are boxed.

For player 2, either <pure strategy> with $q=0$ is a <best response> against $r$, while either with $q=1$ is a <best response> against $\bar r$. No one choice of $q$ works against both rows, so **player 2 has no <dominant strategy>**, and **there is no <dominant-strategy equilibrium>**. The value of $p$ does not affect player 2's own <payoff>.

The cells with both entries boxed give exactly four <pure strategy> <Nash equilibria>:
$$
\boxed{(r,p\bar q),\quad(r,\bar p\bar q),\quad(\bar r,pq),\quad(\bar r,\bar p q).}
$$
All four give <payoffs> $(1,1)$. In particular, the <weakly dominated> choice $\bar r$ can occur in a <Nash equilibrium> because player 1 is already satisfied there and cannot strictly improve.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The succinct input is the players' variable-control sets, their goal <propositional formulas>, and a complete candidate <Boolean valuation>; an exponentially expanded <payoff matrix> is not part of the input. This representation is essential to <candidate equilibrium checking in a Boolean game>.

For membership in <co-NP>, consider the complement: the candidate is not a <Nash equilibrium>. A <complexity certificate> consists of a player $i$ and an alternative <Boolean valuation> of that player's controlled <Boolean variables>. Evaluate the current goal and the goal after this <unilateral deviation>, keeping every other player's variables fixed. A profitable <unilateral deviation> exists precisely when the current goal is false and the new goal is true. The certificate has at most the number of input variables plus the player index, and both <propositional formula> evaluations take <polynomial time>. Thus the complement belongs to <NP>, and the original <decision problem> belongs to <co-NP>.

For hardness, start with a <propositional formula> $F(X)$ and introduce a fresh <Boolean variable> $z$. Construct a one-player <Boolean game> in which that player controls $X\cup\{z\}$ and has goal
$$
\gamma=z\land F(X).
$$
Choose the candidate <pure strategy> assigning every variable false. Its <payoff> is zero because $z$ is false, regardless of $F$. If $F$ has a <satisfying assignment>, changing $X$ to that assignment and $z$ to true is a profitable <unilateral deviation>. If $F$ is an <unsatisfiable Boolean formula>, no <unilateral deviation> can satisfy the goal. Therefore
$$
F\text{ is unsatisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{the constructed candidate is a Nash equilibrium}.
$$
The construction increases the formula length by only a constant and writes a candidate of linear length, so it is a <polynomial-time many-one reduction>. Unsatisfiability is <co-NP>-complete; combining hardness and membership proves that **candidate <Nash equilibrium> checking for <Boolean games> is <co-NP>-complete**. If a convention requires at least two players, add a player controlling a fresh unused variable and having the always-true goal; this changes neither direction of the proof.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

There is **no <perfect recall>**. Player 1's later <information set> joins histories following both of her initial actions, so she has forgotten whether she chose $L$ or $R$. It also hides player 2's intervening action, but failure to observe another player's action is <imperfect information>, whereas forgetting one's own earlier action is the particular violation of <perfect recall>. Player 2's two nodes form a single <information set> because player 2 does not observe the initial choice.

For a <behavioural strategy>, let $p$ be player 1's initial <probability> of $L$ and $r$ her later <probability> of $L$. These are separate <information sets>, so $p$ and $r$ need not be equal; their random draws are <independent>. Name player 2's unlabeled branches left and right, and let $q$ be the <probability> of taking the left branch. The only unit-<payoff> paths are left–left–left and right–right–right. Consequently
$$
\Pr(u_1=1)=qpr+(1-q)(1-p)(1-r).
$$
Because this is a <zero-sum game>, the natural meaning of the claimed best <behavioural strategy> is its maximum guaranteed <expected payoff>. Player 2 can choose either branch, so the guarantee at $(p,r)$ is
$$
g(p,r)=\min\{pr,(1-p)(1-r)\}.
$$
For nonnegative numbers, their minimum is at most their <geometric mean>. Also $p(1-p)\le1/4$ and $r(1-r)\le1/4$, hence
$$
g(p,r)\le\sqrt{p(1-p)r(1-r)}\le\frac14.
$$
Taking $p=r=1/2$ makes both possible branch-conditioned success <probabilities> $1/4$, so this upper bound is attained against every $q$. Thus **the optimal guaranteed <behavioural strategy> success <probability> is $1/4$**.

A <mixed strategy> can instead select the complete plan $LL$ with <probability> $1/2$ and the complete plan $RR$ with <probability> $1/2$. The two choices within a sampled plan are correlated. If player 2 chooses left, exactly the sampled plan $LL$ succeeds; if player 2 chooses right, exactly $RR$ succeeds. Therefore the success <probability> is
$$
\tfrac12q+\tfrac12(1-q)=\boxed{\tfrac12}
$$
against every opposing <mixed strategy>. It is optimal among <mixed strategies> too: player 2's uniform mixture gives success <probability> $1/2$ to each of $LL,RR$ and zero to $LR,RL$, so no mixture of the four plans can exceed $1/2$ against that opponent. This establishes <mixed and behavioural security levels without perfect recall> directly.

\Image[computational-game-theory-imperfect-recall-security.png]
{title=Independent behavioural choices guarantee at most $1/4$, whereas a mixed plan correlates the choices and guarantees $1/2$}

The guarantee qualification matters: if player 2's branch were known to be left, the deterministic plan $LL$ would succeed with <probability> one. The number $1/4$ is not an upper bound against every fixed opposing choice separately.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Against <Tit for tat>, let $a_t$ be an arbitrary deviator's action at round $t$, and set $a_{-1}=C$ to encode the opponent's initial cooperation. The opponent then plays $a_{t-1}$. Define $h(C)=0$ and $h(D)=3$. The four possible transitions give
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
a_{t-1}&a_t&u(a_t,a_{t-1})&3+h(a_t)-h(a_{t-1})\\\hline
C&C&3&3\\
C&D&6&6\\
D&C&0&0\\
D&D&1&3
\end{array}
$$
Thus every stage <payoff> is at most $3+h(a_t)-h(a_{t-1})$. The <telescoping sum> yields the <uniform payoff bound against Tit for tat>:
$$
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}u(a_t,a_{t-1})\le3T+h(a_{T-1})-h(C)\le3T+3.
$$
After division by $T$, no <long-run average payoff> can exceed three. The bound applies to every action <sequence>, so in particular it covers every <finite-state machine> representing a <unilateral deviation>. Mutual <Tit for tat> produces $C,C$ in every round and gives each player three. Neither player can improve, proving **$(\mathrm{TFT},\mathrm{TFT})$ is a <Nash equilibrium>**.

Another pair is **$(\mathrm{TFT},\text{grim trigger})$**. Here <grim trigger> initially cooperates and continues cooperating until the opponent first defects, after which it defects forever. Both strategies are two-state <finite-state machines>: <Tit for tat> records the opponent's previous action, while <grim trigger> records whether defection has ever occurred. Their prescribed play again consists entirely of cooperation and gives $(3,3)$.

The player using <grim trigger> faces <Tit for tat>, so every <unilateral deviation> is bounded by the same <telescoping sum> and cannot improve on three. The player using <Tit for tat> faces <grim trigger>. If that player never defects, the <payoff> remains three. If the first defection occurs at a finite round $\tau$, it gives a one-time <payoff> six; thereafter the opponent always defects, against which each subsequent <payoff> is at most one. Any finite initial prefix has zero contribution to the <long-run average payoff>, so such a <unilateral deviation> has limiting upper average at most one. It cannot improve on three either. Both players are therefore <best responses>, proving the second claimed <Nash equilibrium>.

For deterministic <finite-state machines>, the joint state path is eventually periodic, so the displayed averages have a <limit>. The first bound also controls the limiting upper average for arbitrary action <sequences>, making the no-improvement argument stronger than an enumeration of finite-state deviations. Notice that equality in the bound permits alternating exploitation to average three: equilibrium requires absence of a strict improvement, not uniqueness of a <best response>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $x(S)=\sum_{i\in S}x_i$. The <core of a cooperative game> is
$$
C(v)=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:x(N)=v(N),\quad x(S)\ge v(S)\text{ for every }S\subseteq N\}.
$$
Take any $x,y\in C(v)$ and $\alpha\in[0,1]$, and form the <convex combination> $z=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)y$. Its total <payoff> is $z(N)=\alpha v(N)+(1-\alpha)v(N)=v(N)$. For every <coalition> $S$,
$$
z(S)=\alpha x(S)+(1-\alpha)y(S)\ge\alpha v(S)+(1-\alpha)v(S)=v(S).
$$
Hence $z$ remains in the <core>. **The <core> is a <convex set>**, even when the <transferable utility game> is not a <convex cooperative game>. An empty <core> is a <convex set> vacuously; the argument does not claim nonemptiness. Geometrically, the same conclusion follows because the constraints intersect an <affine hyperplane> with <closed half-spaces>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume the standard <empty-coalition normalization> $v(\varnothing)=0$. A <convex cooperative game> has increasing <marginal contributions>: whenever $A\subseteq B$ and $i\notin B$,
$$
v(A\cup\{i\})-v(A)\le v(B\cup\{i\})-v(B).
$$
This follows from the <supermodular> inequality applied to $A\cup\{i\}$ and $B$. Fix a <permutation> $\pi$ of all players, let $P_i$ be the players preceding $i$, and form its <marginal contribution vector>
$$
m_i^\pi=v(P_i\cup\{i\})-v(P_i).
$$
Summing along the full <permutation> is a <telescoping sum>, so $\sum_{i\in N}m_i^\pi=v(N)$. For an arbitrary <coalition> $S$, list its members in their induced order $i_1,\ldots,i_k$, and put $S_j=\{i_1,\ldots,i_j\}$. Since $S_{j-1}\subseteq P_{i_j}$, increasing <marginal contributions> give
$$
\sum_{i\in S}m_i^\pi\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\bigl(v(S_j)-v(S_{j-1})\bigr)=v(S).
$$
Thus every <marginal contribution vector> belongs to the <core>. The vector of <Shapley values> is exactly their uniform average over the $n!$ <permutations>, hence a <convex combination>. By the preceding part, that average is in the <core>. This proves that **the <Shapley value> allocation belongs to the <core> of a <convex cooperative game>**, including every <coalition> constraint. It is the result <Shapley value belongs to the core of a convex game>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

**Yes.** Use the <simple cooperative game> in which a <coalition> wins exactly when it contains at least two players. In every <permutation>, precisely the second arrival changes a losing <coalition> into a winning one. Each player is second in two of the six <permutations>, so each <Shapley value> is $2/6=1/3$. This constructs the required vector while verifying that singleton <coalitions> lose and the grand <coalition> wins.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**No.** In a <simple cooperative game>, a player's <marginal contribution> along any <permutation> is zero or one. With three players there are six equally likely <permutations>, so every <Shapley value> is an integer multiple of $1/6$, by the <Shapley value denominator in a simple game>. But $6(5/12)=5/2$ and $6(3/12)=3/2$ are not integers. The vector sums to one, but fails this necessary denominator condition.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**Yes.** Give players $1,2,3$ weights $1,1,2$, respectively, and let the quota be three. This <weighted voting game> is a <simple cooperative game> whose minimal winning <coalitions> are $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2,3\}$. In orders $123,132,213,231$, player 3 supplies the winning <marginal contribution>. In order $312$ player 1 does, and in order $321$ player 2 does. Averaging the six <marginal contribution vectors> gives exactly **$(1/6,1/6,4/6)$** as the vector of <Shapley values>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**No.** The denominator test from part ii is satisfied here, so a stronger obstruction is needed. Put $a_i=v(\{i\})$ and $b_{ij}=v(\{i,j\})$. In a three-player <simple cooperative game>, the <Shapley value> formula gives, for distinct $i,j,k$,
$$
\phi_i=\frac13a_i+\frac16(b_{ij}-a_j)+\frac16(b_{ik}-a_k)+\frac13(1-b_{jk}).
$$
Subtract the corresponding expression for $\phi_j$:
$$
\phi_i-\phi_j=\frac12\bigl(a_i-a_j+b_{ik}-b_{jk}\bigr).
$$
Each <coalition> value is zero or one, so this difference must be an integer multiple of $1/2$. This is the obstruction from <pairwise Shapley differences in a three-player simple game>. The proposed first two coordinates differ by $1/6$, which is not an integer multiple of $1/2$. Therefore the vector cannot occur, even though each coordinate separately passes the $1/6$ denominator test.
