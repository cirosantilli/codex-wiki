= Solution
{parent=1}

Let $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ and write $\psi_S(t)=U(t)\psi_0$ in the <Schrodinger picture>, with <observables> $A_S$ constant unless they have an explicit time dependence. In the <Heisenberg picture> the state is $\psi_H=\psi_0$ and
$$
A_H(t)=U(t)^*A_SU(t),\qquad\boxed{\dot A_H=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,A_H]+U^*(\partial_tA_S)U.}
$$
Differentiate the two <unitary> factors to obtain the equation. <Unitarity> gives $\langle\psi_S,A_S\psi_S\rangle=\langle\psi_0,A_H\psi_0\rangle$.

For the <interaction picture> choose a reference split $H=H_0+V$, put $U_0=e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}$, and define $\psi_I=U_0^*\psi_S$, $A_I=U_0^*A_SU_0$. Then $i\hbar\dot\psi_I=V_I\psi_I$, $V_I=U_0^*VU_0$, while $A_I$ evolves with $H_0$ (and its explicit time dependence). Again
$$
\boxed{\langle\psi_I,A_I\psi_I\rangle=\langle\psi_S,A_S\psi_S\rangle=\langle\psi_0,A_H\psi_0\rangle.}
$$
These are different <unitary> allocations of the same dynamics between states and <observables>, so measured <expectations> agree.

The <canonical commutation relation> now gives
$$
\boxed{\dot X_t=P_t/m,\quad\dot P_t=-m\omega^2X_t-\mathcal E,\quad\dot A_t=-i\omega A_t-\mathcal E.}
$$
Multiplication of the last equation by $e^{i\omega t}$ and integration gives
$$
\boxed{A_t=e^{-i\omega t}A_0-\frac{i\mathcal E}{\omega}(e^{-i\omega t}-1).}
$$
Since $A_t^*-A_t=2im\omega X_t$ and $A_0\psi_0=0$, both initial <expectations> of $A_0,A_0^*$ vanish. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle X_t\rangle=-\frac{\mathcal E}{m\omega^2}(1-\cos\omega t).}
$$
**The printed positive displacement endpoint has the wrong sign** for the <Hamiltonian>'s positive $\mathcal EX$ term. The two turning values are $0$ and $-2\mathcal E/(m\omega^2)$; their ordering depends on the sign of $\mathcal E$. The result oscillates about the displaced equilibrium $-\mathcal E/(m\omega^2)$, exactly as the force $-\mathcal E$ predicts.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Choose an <orthonormal> <eigenbasis> $H_0|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$ and the <phase> convention $\langle0|\psi'\rangle=0$. Comparing coefficients of the <eigenvalue> equation at first order yields
$$
(H_0-E_0)\psi'=-(H'-E')|0\rangle.
$$
Projecting onto $|0\rangle$ and the other <eigenvectors> gives
$$
\boxed{E'=\langle0|H'|0\rangle,\qquad\psi'=\sum_{n\ne0}\frac{\langle n|H'|0\rangle}{E_0-E_n}|n\rangle.}
$$
Projection of the next-order equation onto $|0\rangle$ gives
$$
\boxed{E''=\langle0|H'|\psi'\rangle=\sum_{n\ne0}\frac{|\langle n|H'|0\rangle|^2}{E_0-E_n}.}
$$
These are coefficients of the printed series, so $E''$ denotes the quadratic coefficient rather than the second derivative itself. Without a <phase> convention an arbitrary purely imaginary multiple of $\psi_0$ can be added to the first vector correction. Continuous spectrum, if present, requires the corresponding <spectral theorem>[spectral integral]; the oscillator has a discrete <complete> <eigenbasis>.

Put $K=m\omega^2/2$ and let $g(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ denote the ground-state density. Then $H'=K(a^2-x^2)\mathbf1_{[-a,a]}$ and $p=\int_{-a}^ag(x)\,dx$. Because $g'=-xg/\sigma^2$, <integration by parts> yields
$$
I_2:=\int_{-a}^ax^2g\,dx=\sigma^2p-2a\sigma^2g(a),\qquad
I_4:=\int_{-a}^ax^4g\,dx=3\sigma^4p-2a\sigma^2(a^2+3\sigma^2)g(a).
$$
Thus the two first-order shifts are
$$
\boxed{E'_0=K[(a^2-\sigma^2)p+2a\sigma^2g(a)],\qquad
E'_1=K[(a^2-3\sigma^2)p+6a\sigma^2g(a)].}
$$
The first is the supplied ground-state expression; the second follows because the excited-state density is $x^2g/\sigma^2$, so $E'_1=K(a^2I_2-I_4)/\sigma^2$.

Subtracting, and using $K\sigma^2=\hbar\omega/4$, gives
$$
\boxed{E'_1-E'_0=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}[p(a)-2ag(a)].}
$$
The bracket is zero at $a=0$, tends to one at infinity, and has derivative $2a^2g(a)/\sigma^2\geq0$. Therefore the first-order predicted level gap is
$$
\boxed{E_1^{\rm app}-E_0^{\rm app}=\hbar\omega\left[1-\frac\lambda2\{p(a)-2ag(a)\}\right].}
$$
For every $\lambda>2$ it becomes negative for all sufficiently large $a$, crossing when $p-2ag=2/\lambda$. It is still positive at $a=0$: the stated conclusion must be read with the large-$a$ regime, rather than for every interval width.

For $\lambda>1$ the inner potential curves downward, placing a central barrier between two symmetric minima at $\pm a$. The <even> unperturbed <ground state> has its greatest density at the central barrier, while the <odd> first <excited state> has a node there and is less penalized. This explains the relative first-order shifts. **A negative predicted gap signals failure of that perturbation approximation**, not inversion of the exact lowest two states: the true one-dimensional <ground state> remains <nodeless> and <even>, with the <odd> state above it. At a high barrier the actual low levels instead form a close double-well pair. The <apparent level crossing from a finite-interval oscillator perturbation> is useful precisely as a diagnostic of the approximation's range.

= Solution
{parent=3}

A <density operator> is a positive self-adjoint <trace-class operator> with $\boxed{\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1}$. A finite <expectation> is $\boxed{\langle A\rangle_\rho=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)}$. It represents a <pure state> exactly when $\rho=|v\rangle\langle v|$ for a unit vector, equivalently $\rho^2=\rho$ or $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=1$.

For a <finite-dimensional vector space>[finite-dimensional] <spectral decomposition> $\rho=\sum_jp_j|v_j\rangle\langle v_j|$, introduce an auxiliary space with <orthonormal> vectors $f_j$ and use the enlarged space $\mathcal K=\mathcal H\otimes\mathcal E$. Define
$$
\Omega_\rho=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}\,v_j\otimes f_j,\qquad\widetilde A=A\otimes I.
$$
Then $\|\Omega_\rho\|^2=\sum p_j=1$ and $\langle\Omega_\rho,\widetilde A\Omega_\rho\rangle=\sum p_j\langle v_j,Av_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho A)$. This <purification> shows that subsystem mixedness can arise from <entanglement> with an unobserved environment <even> when the total state is pure. Subsystem measurements alone cannot distinguish these two descriptions.

A nonzero pure bipartite vector is entangled when it cannot be written as a <tensor product> of two vectors; equivalently its <Schmidt rank> exceeds one. For mixed bipartite states, <entanglement> means absence of a convex decomposition into product <density operators>.

For the nonorthogonal expansion in the question, the unnormalized reduced operator is
$$
\boxed{S_1=\sum_{j,k}\langle\eta_k,\eta_j\rangle\,|\xi_j\rangle\langle\xi_k|.}
$$
Indeed $\langle\Psi,(A\otimes I)\Psi\rangle=\sum_{j,k}\langle\xi_k,A\xi_j\rangle\langle\eta_k,\eta_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(AS_1)$. Positivity follows because $\langle v,S_1v\rangle=\|\sum_j\langle v,\xi_j\rangle\eta_j\|^2\geq0$, and $\operatorname{Tr}S_1=\|\Psi\|^2=:Z$. Thus $\boxed{\rho_1=S_1/Z}$ is a <density operator>; if $\Psi$ is already <normalization>[normalized], $Z=1$. The Gram factors cannot be dropped merely because the vectors are called bases. For unnormalized $\Psi$, physical <expectations> likewise divide its raw quadratic form by $Z$.

For the <Schmidt decomposition> diagonalize $S_1$ in an <orthonormal> <basis> $\xi_j$ with its positive <eigenvalues> $c_j^2$. Expand $\Psi=\sum_j\xi_j\otimes w_j$ with $w_j=(\langle\xi_j|\otimes I)\Psi$. The reduced <matrix> gives $\langle w_k,w_j\rangle=c_j^2\delta_{jk}$. Therefore $\eta_j=w_j/c_j$ are <orthonormal> and
$$
\boxed{\Psi=\sum_{j:c_j>0}c_j\xi_j\otimes\eta_j.}
$$
Zero-<eigenvalue> components vanish because their squared <norms> are zero. This proves the decomposition for an arbitrary nonzero vector; the zero vector uses the empty sum. For a unit vector $\sum c_j^2=1$.

In the numerical example the coefficient <matrix> in the two product bases is $C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Taking the first-subsystem <trace> yields
$$
\boxed{S_2=C^{\mathsf T}C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&5&8\\3&8&13\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The vector $(1,-2,1)^{\mathsf T}$ is in its <nullspace>, so <rank> is at most two; its leading $2\times2$ minor has <determinant> one, so $\boxed{\operatorname{rank}S_2=2}$. This is the <rank> of $C$ and the <Schmidt rank>, proving <entanglement>. **The displayed source ket has <norm> squared $19$**, and the displayed reduced <matrix> has <trace> $19$. The physical <density operator> is $\boxed{\rho_2=S_2/19}$; its nonzero <eigenvalues> are $(19\pm\sqrt{337})/38$, since $CC^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}14&8\\8&5\end{pmatrix}$. The raw <matrix> is correct for the unnormalized quadratic form actually printed.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Put $d=2^N$ and $\lambda=e^{2\pi i/d}$, with nonnegative integer $N$ so the register exists. The <geometric sum> satisfies
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\lambda^{j(k-l)}=\begin{cases}d,&k=l\pmod d,\\0,&k\ne l\pmod d.\end{cases}
$$
Consequently the <matrix> $F_+$ with entries $d^{-1/2}\lambda^{jk}$ has $F_+^*F_+=I$. This proves <unitarity> and
$$
\boxed{f(j)=d^{-1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\lambda^{-jk}\widehat f(k).}
$$
For a binary output string $j=\sum_{r=1}^Nj_r2^{N-r}$, expansion of the printed <tensor product> gives coefficient
$$
d^{-1/2}\prod_{r=1}^N\lambda^{2^{N-r}kj_r}=d^{-1/2}\lambda^{kj}.
$$
Thus the printed product is exactly $F_+|k\rangle$. It is a correct <product decomposition of a Fourier phase state>, but the printed recovery identity has inconsistent signs:
$$
\boxed{\sum_k\widehat f(k)F_+|k\rangle=\sum_jf(-j\bmod d)|j\rangle,}
$$
by the <square of the quantum Fourier transform>. To recover $f(j)$ instead, use $F_-=F_+^*$, replacing every product <phase> by its complex conjugate. Equivalently one may reverse the sign in the definition of $\widehat f$. The discrepancy is visible for $d\geq4$ by taking $f$ concentrated at $1$: the printed expression returns the <basis> vector at $d-1$. The recovery display also uses $k$ as the summation variable on its right while its summand uses $j$; that dummy variable should be $j$.

For factorization first check $\gcd(3,91)=1$. Successive residues are
$$
3^0,3^1,\ldots,3^6\equiv1,3,9,27,81,61,1\pmod{91}.
$$
The first return is $\boxed{r=6}$. Its half power is $3^3=27$, which is neither $1$ nor $-1$ modulo $91$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\gcd(27-1,91)=13,\qquad\gcd(27+1,91)=7,\qquad91=7\cdot13.}
$$

In <Shor's algorithm> prepare a uniform exponent register of size $Q=2^n$, conveniently $91^2\leq Q<2\cdot91^2$, and reversibly compute $3^x\bmod91$ into a second register. Measuring that second register, or tracing it out, leaves periodic exponent correlations of period $r$. Conditional on one measured residue, the first register is a superposition over an arithmetic progression of exponents separated by $r$. The <quantum Fourier transform> converts this periodic structure into peaks near $k/Q=s/r$. <Continued fractions> recover $s/r$; when $s$ is coprime to $r$, its denominator is $r$. Verify by <modular exponentiation> and repeat when the reduced denominator is insufficient. For this example $Q=16384$ is suitable, and useful peaks include those near $Q/6$ and $5Q/6$. Either transform sign gives the same period information, so the source's inverse-sign error does not invalidate the order-finding mechanism.

The Part C inverse-transform display also prints $j$ as its summation index while the summand uses $k$; that sum must be over $k$, as in the corrected inverse above.
