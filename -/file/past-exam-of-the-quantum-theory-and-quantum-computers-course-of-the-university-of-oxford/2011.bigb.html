Its three questions match questions 4–6 of section B in the combined Part B paper B7.1/C7.1, formal code `DMAB 2686`. = Solution
{parent=1}

In the scalar two-particle model, the <fermionic exchange symmetry> and <bosonic exchange symmetry> conditions are
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x_2,x_1)=-\Psi(x_1,x_2)\text{ for fermions},\qquad
\Psi(x_2,x_1)=\Psi(x_1,x_2)\text{ for bosons}.}
$$
For particles with spin, exchange acts on all their labels; the stated coordinate-only model fixes the spatial symmetry sector. Define $S\Psi(x_1,x_2)=\Psi(x_2,x_1)$. Equal masses make the kinetic operator invariant under exchange, and the assumed potential is invariant too, so $HS=SH$. Therefore $H\Psi=E\Psi$ implies $H(S\Psi)=E(S\Psi)$. The components
$$
\Psi_{\mathrm B}=\tfrac12(\Psi+S\Psi),\qquad
\Psi_{\mathrm F}=\tfrac12(\Psi-S\Psi)
$$
are respectively symmetric and antisymmetric eigenfunctions whenever nonzero. Their sum is the original nonzero <quantum state>, so at least one is nonzero. An eigenspace may contain both sectors if the energy is degenerate.

The <normal modes of two harmonically coupled identical particles> provide the separation. The <coordinate transformation> to $y_+,y_-$ is <orthogonal>, with absolute Jacobian one. Exchanging the particles leaves $y_+$ fixed and changes $y_-$ to $-y_-$. Hence **bosonic <quantum states> are even and fermionic <quantum states> odd in the relative coordinate**. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_{x_1}=\frac{\partial_{y_+}+\partial_{y_-}}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\partial_{x_2}=\frac{\partial_{y_+}-\partial_{y_-}}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\partial_{x_1}^2+\partial_{x_2}^2=\partial_{y_+}^2+\partial_{y_-}^2.
$$
Also $x_1^2+x_2^2=y_+^2+y_-^2$ and $(x_1-x_2)^2=2y_-^2$. Combining the two trapping potentials with the interaction therefore gives the separated <Hamiltonian operator>
$$
H=\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_{y_+}^2+\frac12m\omega^2y_+^2\right]
+\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_{y_-}^2+\frac12m\omega^2\cos^2\alpha\,y_-^2\right].
$$
These are independent oscillators of frequencies $\omega$ and $\omega\cos\alpha$. Both frequencies are positive in the stated range. Their product <eigenstates> have energies
$$
\boxed{E_{n_+,n_-}=\hbar\omega\left[n_++\tfrac12+(n_-+\tfrac12)\cos\alpha\right],\qquad n_+,n_-=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The <parity> of an oscillator level is $(-1)^n$. Consequently <bosons> allow even $n_-$ and <fermions> odd $n_-$, while $n_+$ is unrestricted.

The bosonic <ground state> uses $n_+=n_-=0$, and the fermionic <ground state> uses $n_+=0,n_-=1$. The oscillator length in the relative mode is
$$
\boxed{\tau^2=\frac{\hbar}{m\omega\cos\alpha}=\frac{\hbar\sec\alpha}{m\omega}.}
$$
Using the normalized <Gaussian> for each mode gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\mathrm B}(y_+,y_-)=\psi_\sigma(y_+)\psi_\tau(y_-),\qquad
\Phi_{\mathrm F}(y_+,y_-)=\psi_\sigma(y_+)\phi_\tau(y_-).}
$$
Indeed $\phi_\tau(y)=\sqrt2\,y\psi_\tau(y)/\tau$ by differentiating the <Gaussian>, so it is the normalized odd first <excited state>. The ground energies are $E_{\mathrm B}=\hbar\omega(1+\cos\alpha)/2$ and $E_{\mathrm F}=\hbar\omega(1+3\cos\alpha)/2$.

Choose normalized factors for a normalized product <quantum state>; this can always be done by reciprocal rescaling of its factors. Since the coordinate Jacobian has absolute value one,
$$
\mathbb E[f(y_-)]=\iint f(y_-)\,|\psi(y_+)|^2|\phi(y_-)|^2\,dy_+dy_-
=\boxed{\int f(y)|\phi(y)|^2\,dy.}
$$
If the supplied factors are not individually normalized, the general expression has an additional factor $\|\psi\|^2$; choosing normalized factors is the convention in the displayed formula.

For the <ground states>, put $\rho(y)=|\psi_\tau(y)|^2=e^{-y^2/\tau^2}/(\sqrt\pi\tau)$. Then $|\phi_\tau(y)|^2=2y^2\rho(y)/\tau^2$ and $\rho'=-2y\rho/\tau^2$. Thus, for differentiable $f$ with suitable <Gaussian> integrability and vanishing boundary terms,
$$
\mathbb E_{\mathrm F}[f]=\frac2{\tau^2}\int y^2f(y)\rho(y)\,dy
=-\int yf(y)\rho'(y)\,dy
=\boxed{\int\bigl(f(y)+yf'(y)\bigr)\rho(y)\,dy.}
$$
This is <integration by parts>, and every polynomial satisfies the required endpoint conditions. The <relative-coordinate moments in even and odd oscillator ground states> follow by substituting $f(y)=y^n$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_{\mathrm F}[y_-^n]=(n+1)\mathbb E_{\mathrm B}[y_-^n].}
$$
For odd $n$, both expectations vanish by <parity>, so this is an equality of zeros rather than an increase in signed mean separation. The even moments show the physical difference. In particular $\mathbb E_{\mathrm B}[y_-^2]=\tau^2/2$ and $\mathbb E_{\mathrm F}[y_-^2]=3\tau^2/2$, so the fermionic root-mean-square particle separation is $\sqrt3$ times the bosonic value. The fermionic <probability density> vanishes at coincidence $y_-=0$, expressing <Pauli exclusion principle> in this spatial symmetry sector. This is an exchange constraint on the <wave function>, not an additional classical force. As $\alpha$ approaches $\pi/2$, the relative oscillator softens and its width diverges; the excluded endpoint would no longer have this normalizable oscillator <ground state>.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Use an expansion for the <eigenstate> continuing the nondegenerate level, with intermediate <normalization> $\langle\psi_0,\psi(\epsilon)\rangle=1$:
$$
E(\epsilon)=E_0+\epsilon E^{(1)}+\epsilon^2E^{(2)}+O(\epsilon^3),\qquad
\psi(\epsilon)=\psi_0+\epsilon\chi+\epsilon^2\eta+\cdots,\qquad\langle\psi_0,\chi\rangle=0.
$$
Insert these expressions into the <eigenvalue> equation. At first order,
$$
(H_0-E_0)\chi=-(H'-E^{(1)})\psi_0.
$$
Taking the <inner product> with $\psi_0$ eliminates the left side by self-adjointness. Thus
$$
\boxed{E^{(1)}=\langle\psi_0,H'\psi_0\rangle,\qquad
E(\epsilon)=\langle\psi_0,H\psi_0\rangle+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
For $j\ne0$, projection onto $\psi_j$ instead gives
$$
\langle\psi_j,\chi\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi_j,H'\psi_0\rangle}{E_0-E_j}.
$$
At second order, projecting the <eigenvalue> equation onto $\psi_0$ gives $E^{(2)}=\langle\psi_0,H'\chi\rangle$. Expand $\chi$ in the orthonormal energy <basis> and use self-adjointness of $H'$:
$$
\boxed{\epsilon^2E^{(2)}=\epsilon^2\sum_{j\ne0}\frac{|\langle\psi_j,H'\psi_0\rangle|^2}{E_0-E_j}.}
$$
Nondegeneracy ensures the denominators for this level do not vanish; convergence and validity of the expansion are among the assumed perturbation-theory hypotheses.

For the <cosine perturbation of the ground level in a one-dimensional box>, write $u=\pi x/a$. The product-to-sum identity gives
$$
\cos(2u)\sin u=\tfrac12\bigl(\sin(3u)-\sin u\bigr),
$$
so, with the given common <normalization> of the sine eigenfunctions,
$$
\boxed{V\phi_1=\frac\epsilon2(\phi_3-\phi_1).}
$$
The diagonal <matrix> element is $-\epsilon/2$, and the only nonzero off-diagonal <matrix> element out of the <ground state> is $\langle\phi_3,V\phi_1\rangle=\epsilon/2$. Let $E_{\mathrm{box}}=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2ma^2)$. Since $\mathcal E_3=9E_{\mathrm{box}}$, the ground energy is
$$
E_{\mathrm{ground}}=E_{\mathrm{box}}-\frac\epsilon2+\frac{\epsilon^2/4}{E_{\mathrm{box}}-9E_{\mathrm{box}}}+O(\epsilon^3)
=\boxed{\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}-\frac\epsilon2-\frac{ma^2\epsilon^2}{16\pi^2\hbar^2}+O(\epsilon^3).}
$$
The second-order term is negative, as expected for a nondegenerate ground level: every excited-<quantum state> denominator is negative. The first-order shift is also negative for positive $\epsilon$, because the ground-<quantum state> density is largest near the center, where this perturbing potential is negative.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use the <Dirac notation> convention that the <inner product> is linear in the ket. Orthonormality of the computational <basis> gives
$$
\langle k|f\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z_N}f(\ell)\langle k|\ell\rangle=f(k).
$$
The finite geometric sum satisfies $\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\lambda^{rk}=N$ if $r=0$ in $\mathbb Z_N$, and zero otherwise: in the second case it equals $(1-\lambda^{rN})/(1-\lambda^r)=0$. Therefore
$$
\langle\widehat m|\widehat n\rangle=\frac1N\sum_k\lambda^{(n-m)k}=\delta_{mn}.
$$
There are $N$ such orthonormal <vectors> in an $N$-dimensional space, so they form a <basis>. The inverse expansion is verified by the same sum:
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_j\lambda^{-jk}|\widehat j\rangle
=\frac1N\sum_{j,\ell}\lambda^{j(\ell-k)}|\ell\rangle=|k\rangle.}
$$
This is the <unitary> <discrete Fourier transform> and its inverse.

For the <basis-copying isometry>, the images $|k\rangle|k\rangle$ are orthonormal, so $C$ is a linear <isometry>, with $C^*C=I$. Its <matrix> elements give
$$
\langle Ck|\,|f\rangle|g\rangle
=\langle k|f\rangle\langle k|g\rangle=f(k)g(k)=\langle k|f.g\rangle.
$$
Applying the definition of the <adjoint operator>, the coefficient of $|k\rangle$ in $C^*|f\rangle|g\rangle$ is exactly this expression. Thus
$$
\boxed{C^*|f\rangle|g\rangle=|f.g\rangle.}
$$
Equivalently $C^*|k\rangle|\ell\rangle=\delta_{k\ell}|k\rangle$. The adjoint of <basis> copying acts as pointwise multiplication of coefficient <functions>.

For the <Fourier transform of a basis-copying isometry>, the <normalization> matters:
$$
f_m(k)f_n(k)=\frac{\lambda^{(m+n)k}}N=\frac1{\sqrt N}f_{m+n}(k).
$$
Hence $C^*|\widehat m\rangle|\widehat n\rangle=N^{-1/2}|\widehat{m+n}\rangle$. Taking adjoint <matrix> elements, or expanding both output factors in the inverse Fourier <basis>, yields
$$
\boxed{C|\widehat j\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{m+n=j}|\widehat m\rangle|\widehat n\rangle.}
$$
**The printed formula is missing the factor $1/\sqrt N$.** There are $N$ mutually <orthogonal> terms in its sum, so without that factor its right side has <norm> $\sqrt N$, whereas its left side has <norm> one because $C$ is an <isometry>. The formula above is the normalized identity consistent with all the preceding definitions. For $N=1$ the distinction disappears, but the no-cloning assertion is substantive for $N\geq2$.

The normalized output is not $|\widehat j\rangle|\widehat j\rangle$. Its coefficient <matrix> pairs every Fourier label $m$ with $j-m$; tracing out either factor gives $I/N$. Thus it is a <maximally entangled state>, while two clones of a pure Fourier <quantum state> would have pure reduced <quantum states>. A copier for the computational <basis> therefore does not copy the Fourier <basis>.

More generally, suppose a linear <isometry> with a fixed blank input could clone every normalized <quantum state>. Its restriction to $|\psi\rangle|\mathrm{blank}\rangle$ would define a linear map $D$ with $D|k\rangle=|k\rangle|k\rangle$ on every computational <basis> <quantum state>. Linearity forces $D=C$, which has just been shown to fail on Fourier <quantum states>. This proves the <no-cloning theorem> in the pure-<quantum state> isometric setting.

The inner-product proof makes the obstruction independent of the chosen <basis>. If an <isometry> cloned two <quantum states> with overlap $s=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$, preservation of inner products would give $s=s^2$, or, allowing irrelevant output phases, $|s|=|s|^2$. Distinct nonorthogonal rays have $0<|s|<1$, which is impossible. Even allowing an output environment does not help: <unitary> evolution would give $|s|=|s|^2|\langle e_\psi|e_\phi\rangle|\leq|s|^2$, the same contradiction. **Arbitrary unknown <quantum states> cannot be cloned**, although any fixed <orthonormal basis> can be copied and a one-dimensional <quantum state> space is trivial.
