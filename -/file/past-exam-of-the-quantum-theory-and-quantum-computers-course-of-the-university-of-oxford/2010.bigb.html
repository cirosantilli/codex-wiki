= Solution
{parent=1}

A <permutation> $\pi$ of <identical particles> multiplies a fermionic state by $\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)$; a bosonic state is invariant. Particle exchange keeps $y_+$ fixed and sends $y_-$ to $-y_-$. Consequently the spatial <wave function> is even in $y_-$ for the stated bosonic model and odd for the stated fermionic model. This assumes no additional spin variables in the supplied model; exchange of full particle states is the underlying rule.

The coordinate change is orthogonal, with unit <Jacobian determinant>, so the <kinetic energy> becomes
$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\partial_{y_+}^2+\partial_{y_-}^2)
$$
on the square $|y_+|,|y_-|<a$ with zero boundary values. Product states $\psi_r(y_+)\psi_s(y_-)$ have energy
$$
E_{r,s}=K(r^2+s^2),\qquad K=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}.
$$
The one-dimensional <eigenfunction> is even for odd $s$ and odd for even $s$. <Bosons> therefore allow odd $s$, whereas <fermions> require even $s$. The lowest bosonic pair is $(r,s)=(1,1)$; its first excited pair is $(2,1)$. The lowest fermionic pair is $(1,2)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_{B,0}=2K=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{4ma^2},\qquad
E_{B,1}=E_{F,0}=5K=\frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}.}
$$
The printed bosonic ground-energy claim omits the factor $\pi^2/4$; the value above follows directly from the supplied one-dimensional spectrum and <kinetic energy>.

The separation is $|x_1-x_2|=\sqrt2|y_-|$. The normalized $y_+$ factor integrates out. In the fermionic <ground state>, the relative-coordinate density is $a^{-1}\sin^2(\pi y_-/a)$, giving
$$
\langle|y_-|\rangle_F=\frac2a\int_0^a y\sin^2(\pi y/a)\,dy=\frac a2.
$$
For the bosonic <ground state> it is $a^{-1}\cos^2(\pi y_-/(2a))$, giving
$$
\langle|y_-|\rangle_B=\frac2a\int_0^a y\cos^2(\pi y/(2a))\,dy
=a\left(\frac12-\frac2{\pi^2}\right).
$$
Both <integrals> follow by replacing squared trigonometric functions by their constant and cosine parts and integrating once by parts. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle|x_1-x_2|\rangle_F=\frac a{\sqrt2},\quad
\langle|x_1-x_2|\rangle_B=\sqrt2a\left(\frac12-\frac2{\pi^2}\right),}
$$
and the difference is $\boxed{2\sqrt2a/\pi^2}$. The odd relative state vanishes at coincidence, which increases its expected separation.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Let $E=f_H(\psi)$. For any admissible variation $\eta$, differentiation with respect to a real parameter gives
$$
\left.\frac d{dt}f_H(\psi+t\eta)\right|_{t=0}
=\frac{2\operatorname{Re}\langle\eta|(H-E)\psi\rangle}{\|\psi\|^2}.
$$
Stationarity for all variations, including their imaginary multiples, forces $(H-E)\psi=0$; conversely this <eigenvector> equation makes the <derivative> vanish. Thus **stationary <Rayleigh quotients> are exactly <eigenvectors>, with their <eigenvalues> as the quotient values**.

Put $\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ and assume $\alpha>0$. The squared <norm> of the radial trial function is $4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-2\alpha r}\,dr=\pi/\alpha^3$. Its kinetic expectation, computed through $\int|\nabla\psi|^2$, is $\hbar^2\alpha^2/(2m)=\kappa a\alpha^2/2$. Splitting the potential <integral> at $R$ gives
$$
\langle V\rangle=-4\kappa\alpha^3
\left(\frac1R\int_0^Rr^2e^{-2\alpha r}\,dr
+\int_R^\infty re^{-2\alpha r}\,dr\right)
=-\frac\kappa R\left[1-(1+\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}\right].
$$
For example, the first <integral> is $[1-e^{-2s}(1+2s+2s^2)]/(4\alpha^3)$ and the second is $e^{-2s}(R/(2\alpha)+1/(4\alpha^2))$, where $s=\alpha R$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f_H(\psi_\alpha)=\kappa\left[\frac{a\alpha^2}{2}
-\frac{1-(1+\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}}R\right].}
$$
Its <derivative> is $\kappa[a\alpha-(1+2\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}]$. The actual stationary condition is consequently
$$
\boxed{a\alpha=(1+2\alpha R)e^{-2\alpha R}.}
$$
The extra Coulomb factor in the printed stationary equation is an error: the common prefactor cancels on differentiation. The corrected equation is dimensionless on both sides. Its left side increases from zero, while its right side decreases from one, so it has one positive solution and gives the minimum in this trial family.

Let $\varepsilon=R/a$ and $y=a\alpha$. Expansion of $(1+2s)e^{-2s}$ gives $1-2s^2+O(s^3)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a\alpha=1-2\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^3),\qquad
\alpha=\frac1a\left[1-2(R/a)^2+O((R/a)^3)\right].}
$$
There is no linear correction in $R/a$. By the <quantum variational principle>, any normalized trial state gives an upper bound on the true ground energy. Choosing $\alpha=1/a$ already gives the exact bound
$$
\boxed{E_0(R)\leq\frac\kappa a\left[\frac12-
\frac{1-(1+\varepsilon)e^{-2\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right].}
$$
Its small-radius expansion is
$$
E_0(R)\leq\frac\kappa a
\left[-\frac12+\frac23\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^3)\right].
$$
Using the optimized $\alpha$ improves this bound starting at order $\varepsilon^4$, and hence gives the same leading finite-size correction. The true energy is also at least the point-nucleus value $-\kappa/(2a)$, since the finite-radius potential is nowhere more attractive.

= Solution
{parent=3}

A <density operator> is a positive self-adjoint trace-class operator with trace one. In finite dimensions this is a positive semidefinite <Hermitian matrix> with $\operatorname{tr}\rho=1$. Its expectation of an observable is $\boxed{\langle A\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A\rho)}$ whenever the expectation is defined.

In the source's square-root notation, take the unique positive square root $W=\sqrt\rho$. It has Hilbert-Schmidt <norm> one and satisfies $WW^*=\rho$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(A\rho)=\operatorname{tr}(W^*AW).}
$$
The standard vector <purification> is the associated vector $|W\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{r,s}W_{rs}|\xi_r\rangle|\eta_s\rangle$ on a system and a chosen copy. Tracing out the copy gives $\rho$; measuring $A\otimes I$ gives the same trace formula. Thus the operator square root and the purified vector are two related objects, distinguished by this vectorization.

For a general coefficient matrix $C$, expansion of the expectation gives
$$
\langle\Psi|A\otimes I|\Psi\rangle
=\sum_{r,t,s}\overline{c_{rs}}A_{rt}c_{ts}
=\operatorname{tr}(ACC^*).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\rho_1=CC^*}$. Positivity is immediate from $v^*CC^*v=\|C^*v\|^2$, and its trace is one by <normalization> of $\Psi$.

If $C$ is nonsingular, it is square and $\rho_1$ is positive definite. Define
$$
\boxed{U=C^{-1}\sqrt{\rho_1}.}
$$
Then $UU^*=C^{-1}\rho_1(C^*)^{-1}=I$, which for a square matrix also implies $U^*U=I$. Thus $U$ is unitary, $\sqrt{\rho_1}=CU$, and $C=\sqrt{\rho_1}U^*$.

In an eigenbasis of $\rho_1$, its square root is $\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{p_r})$. Hence $c_{rs}=\sqrt{p_r}\,\overline{U_{sr}}$. Define
$$
\boxed{|\zeta_r\rangle=\sum_s\overline{U_{sr}}|\eta_s\rangle.}
$$
Unitarity gives $\langle\zeta_r|\zeta_t\rangle=\delta_{rt}$, and substitution gives the <Schmidt decomposition>
$$
\boxed{|\Psi\rangle=\sum_r\sqrt{p_r}|\xi_r\rangle|\zeta_r\rangle.}
$$
For singular or rectangular $C$, the same conclusion follows on its support by a <singular value decomposition>; the stated nonsingular case needs only the unitary calculation above.

For the supplied <Bell state>, the requested matrices are
$$
\boxed{C=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\rho_1=\frac12I,\quad\sqrt{\rho_1}=\frac1{\sqrt2}I,\quad
U=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The Schmidt vectors can be chosen as $\zeta_1=\eta_2$, $\zeta_2=-\eta_1$, and the two coefficients are $1/\sqrt2$. The square root's canonical vector <purification> is $(|\xi_1\eta_1\rangle+|\xi_2\eta_2\rangle)/\sqrt2$; the original Bell vector is a different <purification> of the same reduced state, related by a unitary on the second factor. This illustrates the unitary freedom of <purification>.
