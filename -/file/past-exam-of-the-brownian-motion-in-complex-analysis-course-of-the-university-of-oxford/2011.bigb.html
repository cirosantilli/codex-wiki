= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Write $Z_t=z+B_t^1+iB_t^2$, where the coordinates are independent standard <Brownian motions>, and write $f=u+iv$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> imply $\Delta u=\Delta v=0$. <Itô's formula> therefore makes
$$
M_t=u(Z_t)-u(z),\qquad N_t=v(Z_t)-v(z)
$$
<continuous local martingales>. Their <quadratic variations> and <quadratic covariation> are
$$
\langle M\rangle_t=\langle N\rangle_t=A_t:=\int_0^t|f'(Z_s)|^2ds,\qquad
\langle M,N\rangle_t=0.
$$
Indeed the two gradient norms are equal to $|f'|$, and their dot product vanishes by the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

The clock is finite at every finite time, since a continuous Brownian path has compact range there and $f'$ is continuous. To show its lifetime is infinite, choose a small disc on which $|f'|^2\geq c>0$; such a disc exists because the <entire function> is nonconstant. A supporting recurrence result is that planar Brownian motion spends infinite total time in every nonempty open disc almost surely. One proof combines recurrent returns to a smaller disc with the strong Markov property: each visit has a uniformly positive chance of lasting a fixed positive time in the larger disc. Equivalently, use the <divergence of a positive planar Brownian occupation integral>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A_t\longrightarrow\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Finally the <common-clock time change of orthogonal local martingales> states that two continuous local martingales starting at zero with common bracket $A$, zero cross bracket, and $A_\infty=\infty$ are the coordinates of a planar Brownian motion evaluated at $A_t$. Applying it gives a <planar Brownian motion> $\widetilde Z$ starting at zero with
$$
\boxed{f(Z_t)=f(z)+\widetilde Z_{A_t}.}
$$
This proves <conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion> in the entire-function setting, including the <divergence of the conformal Brownian clock for an entire function>.

Apply part (a) to the entire function $f(z)=e^{i\theta}z$. Its derivative has modulus one, so its <conformal Brownian clock> is $A_t=t$. Thus
$$
e^{i\theta}Z_t=e^{i\theta}Z_0+\widetilde Z_t,
$$
which is a <planar Brownian motion> starting from the rotated initial point. In particular, if the initial point is zero, its law is unchanged. This is <rotational invariance of planar Brownian motion>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Start a <planar Brownian motion> $B$ at zero and apply part (a) to the entire function $f(z)=e^z$. Then
$$
e^{B_t}=1+\widetilde Z_{A_t},\qquad
A_t=\int_0^t e^{2\operatorname{Re}B_s}\,ds.
$$
The left side never vanishes. The clock is continuous, starts at zero, and tends to infinity almost surely, so its range is all nonnegative times. Consequently the complex Brownian motion $1+\widetilde Z_s$ never reaches zero at any time $s$. Every <planar Brownian motion> started at one has that same law, hence
$$
\boxed{P(T<\infty)=0.}
$$
This is the <complex exponential construction of planar Brownian motion> and proves that a fixed point is a <polar point for planar Brownian motion>. The divergence of the clock is essential: it makes this a conclusion about all Brownian times.

Let $\beta(\theta)=w-e^{i\theta}=|\beta(\theta)|e^{ig(\theta)}$. It is never zero, since $|w|<1$. On the <unit circle>,
$$
e^{i\theta}-w=-\beta(\theta),\qquad
1-\overline w e^{i\theta}=-e^{i\theta}\overline{\beta(\theta)}.
$$
Dividing these identities gives
$$
\boxed{f(e^{i\theta})=e^{-i\theta}\frac{\beta(\theta)}{\overline{\beta(\theta)}}
=\exp\bigl(i(-\theta+2g(\theta))\bigr).}
$$
Any other continuous lift of the argument differs by an integer multiple of $2\pi$, which leaves the exponential unchanged.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

Differentiate the argument through the nonvanishing curve, using $g'=\operatorname{Im}(\beta'/\beta)$. Since $\beta'=-ie^{i\theta}$,
$$
g'(\theta)=\operatorname{Im}\frac{-ie^{i\theta}(\overline w-e^{-i\theta})}{|w-e^{i\theta}|^2}
=\frac{1-w_1\cos\theta-w_2\sin\theta}{|w-e^{i\theta}|^2}.
$$
Also $|w-e^{i\theta}|^2=1+|w|^2-2(w_1\cos\theta+w_2\sin\theta)$. Subtracting one therefore gives
$$
\boxed{-1+2g'(\theta)=\frac{1-|w|^2}{|w-e^{i\theta}|^2}>0.}
$$
Thus the lifted boundary angle $\phi(\theta)=-\theta+2g(\theta)$ is strictly increasing. Its total change around the circle is $2\pi$, because the <disk automorphism> has boundary degree one. The same total change follows directly from the <winding number> one of $w-e^{i\theta}$. This is the <boundary-angle derivative of a disk automorphism>.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The <disk automorphism> satisfies $f(w)=0$ and maps the unit circle bijectively to itself. Its derivative $(1-|w|^2)/(1-\overline wz)^2$ is bounded above and away from zero on the closed disk. By <conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion>, the image path up to the exit time is a time-changed planar Brownian motion started at zero. Its exit point is $f(Z_T)$; the clock changes when it exits, but not where.

The original exit time is finite almost surely. For example, $|Z_{t\wedge T}|^2-2(t\wedge T)$ is a martingale, so $\mathbb E(t\wedge T)\leq(1-|w|^2)/2$. Boundedness of $f'$ makes the image exit clock finite too. By <rotational invariance of planar Brownian motion>, the image exit angle is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$.

Let $\Theta=\arg Z_T$. Parts (a) and (b) show that $\phi(\Theta)$ modulo $2\pi$ is uniform, where $\phi$ is strictly increasing and increases by exactly $2\pi$ in one circuit. The one-dimensional change-of-variables formula therefore gives
$$
\boxed{h(\theta)=\frac{\phi'(\theta)}{2\pi}
=\frac1{2\pi}\frac{1-|w|^2}{|w-e^{i\theta}|^2}.}
$$
Its integral is one because the total angle change is $2\pi$. This <harmonic measure> density is the <Poisson kernel for the unit disk> divided by $2\pi$. It is uniform for $w=0$ and concentrates near the boundary point closest to $w$ as $|w|$ increases.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

A bounded <holomorphic function> of planar Brownian motion is a local martingale up to exit, because its real and imaginary parts are harmonic and <Itô's formula> has zero drift. Stop before the given $S<T_D$. Localization inside compact subdisks gives the local martingale $f(Z_{t\wedge S}^z)$, and $|f|<1$ makes it bounded. The <bounded local martingale criterion> therefore gives a true martingale.

Exit from the unit disk is finite almost surely, so $S<T_D$ is finite too. Taking $t\to\infty$ and using the <dominated convergence theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[f(Z_S^z)]=f(z).}
$$
The same proof works for any bounded <holomorphic function>, after rescaling if needed; it does not require the stopping time itself to be bounded.

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Since $f(0)=0$, the function $g(z)=f(z)/z$ extends analytically to zero by $g(0)=f'(0)$. It is bounded on the disk: outside $|z|<1/2$, $|g(z)|\leq2$, while on the closed half-radius disk its analytic extension is continuous and hence bounded.

Fix $z\ne0$ and choose $|z|<r<1$. Let $T_r$ be the first exit time of Brownian motion started at $z$ from the radius-$r$ disk. The bounded analytic martingale identity in part (a) gives
$$
g(z)=\mathbb E_z[g(Z_{T_r})].
$$
On this exit circle, $|g(Z_{T_r})|=|f(Z_{T_r})|/r\leq1/r$. The triangle inequality gives $|g(z)|\leq1/r$. Letting $r\uparrow1$ proves $|g(z)|\leq1$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{|f(z)|\leq|z|\quad\text{for all }z\in D.}
$$
At zero the claim holds by the assumed zero value. This is the <Schwarz lemma>, obtained from Brownian optional stopping rather than an analytic maximum principle.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Let $z_0\ne0$ be an equality point and set $c=g(z_0)$, so $|c|=1$. For any $|z_0|<r<1$, the same Brownian exit identity gives
$$
1=\operatorname{Re}(\overline c\,g(z_0))
=\mathbb E_{z_0}\operatorname{Re}(\overline c\,g(Z_{T_r})).
$$
Part (i) gives $|g|\leq1$, so the real random variable in the expectation is at most one. Equality of its mean to one forces $g(Z_{T_r})=c$ almost surely. By the scaled version of the exit density from Question 2, every nonempty arc of the exit circle has positive probability. Continuity of $g$ on that circle therefore implies $g=c$ at every boundary point of the radius-$r$ disk.

For any $w$ inside that circle, the Brownian exit identity now gives $g(w)=\mathbb E_wg(Z_{T_r})=c$. Letting $r$ increase to one makes this true throughout $D$. Writing $c=e^{i\theta}$ yields
$$
\boxed{f(w)=we^{i\theta}\quad\text{for all }w\in D.}
$$
This completes the equality case of the <Brownian proof of the Schwarz lemma>.
