= Solution
{parent=a}

For the supporting inequality, assume first that $F$ is convex and fix $z,z_0$. For $0<t\leq1$,
$$
F(z_0+t(z-z_0))\leq(1-t)F(z_0)+tF(z).
$$
Subtract $F(z_0)$, divide by $t$ and let $t\downarrow0$. <Differentiability> gives
$$
\boxed{F(z)\geq F(z_0)+DF(z_0)\cdot(z-z_0).}
$$
Conversely assume this inequality for every pair. Put $w=(1-t)z_0+tz$ and apply it with base point $w$ to both $z_0$ and $z$. Multiply those two inequalities by $1-t$ and $t$ and add. The <gradient> terms cancel, proving $F(w)\leq(1-t)F(z_0)+tF(z)$. Thus the supporting inequality characterizes a differentiable <convex function>.

For the <integral> inequality let $\bar z=|\Omega|^{-1}\int_\Omega u$. Apply the supporting inequality at $\bar z$ to $u(x)$ and integrate. Its affine term has <integral> $DF(\bar z)\cdot(\int_\Omega u-|\Omega|\bar z)=0$, yielding
$$
\boxed{F\left(\frac1{|\Omega|}\int_\Omega u\right)
\leq\frac1{|\Omega|}\int_\Omega F(u).}
$$
This is <Jensen's inequality>. Since $u$ is integrable, the supporting affine lower bound has integrable negative part, so the right side is well-defined as an extended real <integral>. If its positive part is infinite, the inequality remains valid.

A <convex function> satisfies
$$
\boxed{F((1-t)x+ty)\leq(1-t)F(x)+tF(y)
\quad(x,y\in\mathbb R^n,\ 0\leq t\leq1).}
$$
Its value on a line segment never lies above the chord joining its endpoint values.

A <strictly convex function> satisfies the strict inequality
$$
\boxed{F((1-t)x+ty)<(1-t)F(x)+tF(y)
\quad\text{if }x\ne y,\ 0<t<1.}
$$
Endpoint and coincident-point cases are excluded because they necessarily give equality.

A <global minimum>[global minimizer] is an admissible function $\bar u$ for which
$$
\boxed{I(\bar u)\leq I(v)\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathcal A.}
$$
Equivalently it attains the <infimum> over the whole admissible class. Its endpoint traces must equal the prescribed boundary values; comparing only with nearby functions would define a local rather than a global notion.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The integrand is nonnegative, so $\inf I\geq0$. For each positive integer $n$, set
$$
u_n(x)=2\operatorname{dist}\left(x,\frac1n\mathbb Z\right),\qquad0\leq x\leq1.
$$
This is a continuous piecewise affine sawtooth with zero endpoints, $0\leq u_n\leq1/n$, and $u_n'=\pm2$ <almost everywhere>. It belongs to $W^{1,\infty}$, and $(u_n')^6=64$ <almost everywhere>. Therefore
$$
I(u_n)=\int_0^1\bigl(u_n^{2011}+4096u_n^4\bigr)\,dx
\leq n^{-2011}+4096n^{-4}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Together with nonnegativity this proves $\boxed{\inf_{\mathcal A}I=0}$. The functions converge uniformly to zero while their derivatives retain magnitude two; small amplitude does not force small <gradient>.

= Solution

If an admissible $u$ attained the <infimum> zero, each nonnegative term of its integrand would vanish <almost everywhere>. In particular $|u|^{2011}=0$ <almost everywhere>, so $u=0$ in its <Sobolev space> and its <weak derivative> is zero. But then the remaining term is $(-64)^2=4096$ <almost everywhere>, giving $I(u)=4096$, a contradiction. Thus **the <infimum> is zero but no global minimizer exists**. The oscillatory sequence has no convergence of derivatives compatible with substitution into this nonconvex energy.

= Solution

The variational terminology refers to the size of a neighborhood, rather than weak versus strong convergence in a <Banach space>. The definitions in the two numbered children give
$$
\boxed{\text{global minimizer}\Longrightarrow
\text{strong local minimizer}\Longrightarrow\text{weak local minimizer}.}
$$
The second implication holds because smallness in $W^{1,\infty}$ implies smallness in the <uniform norm>. Reverse implications need not hold.

For differentiable $f$ in its slope variable, define the <Weierstrass excess function>
$$
E(x,y,p,q)=f(x,y,q)-f(x,y,p)-f_v(x,y,p)(q-p).
$$
The <strengthened Weierstrass condition> means that some $\delta>0$ gives
$$
\boxed{E(x,y,p,q)\geq0\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b],\ q\in\mathbb R,
\quad |y-u(x)|<\delta,\quad|p-u'(x)|<\delta.}
$$
It requires support at nearby heights and nearby reference slopes, while the trial slope $q$ is arbitrary. Requiring this only exactly on the curve is the unstrengthened necessary condition.

A standard <Weierstrass sufficient condition> assumes the usual $C^2$ smoothness of $f$, $u\in\mathcal A\cap C^1$, the weak <Euler-Lagrange equation>, a coercively positive <second variation>, and the strengthened condition above. Explicitly, for some $c>0$,
$$
\delta^2I(u)[\eta,\eta]=\int_a^b
\bigl(f_{uu}\eta^2+2f_{uv}\eta\eta'+f_{vv}(\eta')^2\bigr)\,dx
\geq c\|\eta\|_{W^{1,2}}^2
$$
for zero-endpoint variations. Then **$u$ is a strong local minimizer**. If the excess is strictly positive for $q\ne p$ throughout the tube, the strong minimum is strict. The second-variation hypothesis supplies a neighboring field of extremals. Its slope $p(x,y)$ gives the comparison identity
$$
I(v)-I(u)=\int_a^b E(x,v(x),p(x,v(x)),v'(x))\,dx\geq0
$$
for uniformly close admissible $v$. This explains how the condition controls competitors with large derivatives.

A <weak local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{whenever }v\in\mathcal A,
\ \|v-\bar u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\varepsilon.}
$$
Both the function and its first derivative must remain uniformly close, up to the almost-everywhere convention for the derivative.

A <strong local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{whenever }v\in\mathcal A,
\ \|v-\bar u\|_\infty<\varepsilon.}
$$
The competitor's derivative need not be close. This compares with a larger family of competitors, which is why it is the stronger minimality property.

For any zero-endpoint admissible $u$, use $|\sin u|\leq|u|$ and the sharp <Poincaré inequality> on $(0,1)$:
$$
I(u)\geq\frac12\int_0^1(u')^2-\int_0^1u^2
\geq\left(\frac12-\frac1{\pi^2}\right)\int_0^1(u')^2\geq0.
$$
The coefficient is positive, and equality forces $u'=0$, hence $u=0$ by its endpoint values. Thus
$$
\boxed{u=0\text{ is the unique strict global minimizer}.}
$$
It is therefore **both a strict strong and a strict weak local minimizer**.

Here $I(0)=1/2$. First restrict $\|u'\|_\infty\leq\pi/12$. On that slope interval, the second derivative of $h(p)=1-\cos(\pi/3+p)$ is $\cos(\pi/3+p)\geq\cos(5\pi/12)>1/4$. <Taylor's theorem> gives
$$
h(p)\geq h(0)+h'(0)p+\tfrac18p^2.
$$
The linear term integrates to zero because $\int_0^1u'=0$. Since $x^3\leq1$, the <Poincaré inequality> yields
$$
I(u)-I(0)\geq\frac18\int_0^1(u')^2-\int_0^1u^2
\geq\left(\frac18-\frac1{\pi^2}\right)\int_0^1(u')^2.
$$
The coefficient is positive. Thus **zero is a strict weak local minimizer**.

For failure of strong minimality, take a triangular bump supported on $[1/2-\varepsilon,1/2+\varepsilon]$, with slopes $+2\pi$ and $-2\pi$, and zero outside. Its height is $2\pi\varepsilon\to0$. <Almost everywhere> its slope is $0$ or $\pm2\pi$, so the cosine term has exactly its zero-function value everywhere. The potential term is strictly negative on the bump. Therefore
$$
I(u_\varepsilon)-I(0)=-\int_0^1x^3u_\varepsilon^2\,dx<0,
\qquad\|u_\varepsilon\|_\infty\to0.
$$
Hence **it is not a strong local minimizer**. The derivatives of these bumps stay large, so they do not contradict weak local minimality.

= Solution

A scalar <Tonelli existence theorem> states: if $f(x,y,p)$ is continuous, convex in $p$, and has a uniform superlinear lower bound $f(x,y,p)\geq\Theta(|p|)-C$ with $\Theta(r)/r\to\infty$, and at least one fixed-endpoint admissible <absolutely continuous function> has finite energy, then the energy attains its <infimum> in the fixed-endpoint $W^{1,1}$ class. One may take $\Theta$ nonnegative and convex. The lower bound makes derivatives uniformly integrable; endpoint control then gives uniform compactness of a minimizing sequence, and <convexity> supplies the required <lower semicontinuity>.

For the specified functional, the integrand is continuous, nonnegative, and convex in the derivative, with
$$
f(x,y,p)\geq\frac{|p|^{52}}{52},\qquad
\frac{|p|^{52}/52}{|p|}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
The affine function $u_0(x)=2x-1$ has the required endpoints and finite energy. Thus **a global minimizer exists**. In fact any finite-energy minimizer belongs to $W^{1,52}$, since its derivative's 52nd power is integrable.

It is **unique**. The admissible class is convex, and the integrand is strictly convex in $(y,p)$: $|p|^{52}$ and $|y|^{2011}$ are strictly convex, with the latter multiplied by $e^x>0$. If two distinct admissible finite-energy functions minimized the energy, their midpoint would have strictly smaller energy. Distinct continuous representatives differ on a set of positive measure, so the strict <integral> inequality really applies. This contradiction proves <uniqueness>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

In one dimension, $u\in W^{1,2}$ has an absolutely continuous bounded representative. For any smooth zero-endpoint <test function> $\eta$, the functions $u+t\eta$ are admissible and arbitrarily close to $u$ for small $t$. Local minimality forces the <first variation> to vanish. Differentiation under the <integral> is valid because $u,\eta$ are bounded and the derivative terms are integrable, giving the weak <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1\bigl(2u'\eta'+4xu^3\eta\bigr)\,dx=0.}
$$
In distributional form this says $(2u')'=4xu^3$. The fundamental theorem for <weak derivatives>, or the fundamental lemma applied after subtracting an antiderivative, gives a constant $C$ with
$$
\boxed{u'(x)=C+2\int_0^x t\,u(t)^3\,dt\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$
This is the integrated <Euler-Lagrange equation>. The boundary difference $u(1)-u(0)=2$ determines
$$
C=2-2\int_0^1(1-t)t\,u(t)^3\,dt.
$$
The weak identity extends to all zero-trace $W^{1,2}$ <test functions> by <density>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The continuous representative of $u$ makes $x\mapsto2xu(x)^3$ continuous on the closed interval. The integrated formula therefore gives a continuously differentiable representative of $u'$, and hence $u\in C^2([0,1])$ with
$$
u''(x)=2xu(x)^3.
$$
Differentiate once more. The right side is continuously differentiable because $u\in C^2$, and
$$
\boxed{u^{(3)}(x)=2u(x)^3+6xu(x)^2u'(x).}
$$
Thus $\boxed{u\in C^3([0,1])}$. These formulas give continuous one-sided derivatives at the endpoints as well. Repeated bootstrapping would actually give smoothness of every order.
