= Solution
{parent=i}

A <convex function> satisfies
$$
\boxed{F((1-t)x+ty)\leq(1-t)F(x)+tF(y)\quad(x,y\in\mathbb R^n,\ 0\leq t\leq1).}
$$
Thus its value on every <line segment> lies below the chord joining the endpoint values.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <strictly convex function> satisfies the same inequality strictly whenever $x\ne y$ and $0<t<1$.

For the unlabelled Hessian criterion, <convexity> implies $F(z_0+th)+F(z_0-th)-2F(z_0)\geq0$. Divide by $t^2$ and let $t\to0$ to obtain $h^TD^2F(z_0)h\geq0$ for every $h$. Thus the <Hessian matrix> is <positive semidefinite>.

Conversely, if every Hessian is <positive semidefinite>, Taylor's <integral> formula with $h=z-z_0$ gives
$$
F(z)-F(z_0)-DF(z_0)\cdot h=\int_0^1(1-t)h^TD^2F(z_0+th)h\,dt\geq0.
$$
The allowed supporting-plane characterization then proves
$$
\boxed{F\text{ convex}\iff D^2F(z)\text{ positive semidefinite for all }z.}
$$
For the <integral> inequality put $m=(b-a)^{-1}\int_a^bu$. The supporting tangent at $m$ gives $F(u(x))\geq F(m)+F'(m)(u(x)-m)$ <almost everywhere>. Integrating cancels the linear term, so
$$
\boxed{F\left(\frac1{b-a}\int_a^bu\right)\leq\frac1{b-a}\int_a^bF(u).}
$$
This is <Jensen's inequality>. It remains valid with value $+\infty$ on the right: the affine lower bound has <integrable> negative part, so the extended <integral> is well-defined.

An admissible $\bar u$ is a <global minimizer> if
$$
\boxed{I(\bar u)\leq I(v)\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathcal A.}
$$
In particular it attains the <infimum>; no closeness condition on competing admissible functions is imposed.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The integrand is nonnegative. Define the <Lipschitz> triangular waves
$$
u_N(x)=2\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z),\qquad0\leq x\leq1.
$$
They have zero endpoint values, slope $\pm2$ <almost everywhere>, and height $1/N$. Hence $(u_N'^4-16)^4=0$ <almost everywhere>. On each of the $N$ equal cells, direct integration of the fourth power of the two affine pieces gives $1/(5N^5)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I(u_N)=\frac1{5N^4}\longrightarrow0,\qquad\inf_{\mathcal A}I=0.}
$$
This is an <oscillating minimizing sequence with no minimizer>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If an admissible $u$ attained the <infimum>, nonnegativity and $I(u)=0$ would force $u^4=0$ <almost everywhere>, so its <continuous> Sobolev representative would be identically zero. Its <derivative> would then vanish <almost everywhere>, but the other integrand term would equal $16^4>0$. This is impossible. Equivalently, zero energy would simultaneously require $u=0$ and $|u'|=2$ <almost everywhere>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{The infimum zero is not attained}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

For fixed-endpoint variations, the <second variation> is
$$
q(\varphi)=\delta^2I(\bar u)(\varphi,\varphi)=\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}I(\bar u+t\varphi)\right|_{t=0}=\int_a^b(p\varphi'^2+2r\varphi\varphi'+s\varphi^2)dx,
$$
with $p=f_{vv}$, $r=f_{uv}$, $s=f_{uu}$ evaluated along $(x,\bar u,\bar u')$. The <Jacobi equation>, written without differentiating $r$ separately, is
$$
\boxed{-(pz'+rz)'+rz'+sz=0.}
$$
When $r$ is differentiable this is $-(pz')'+(s-r')z=0$.

<Positive definiteness> means $q(\varphi)>0$ for every nonzero admissible variation. Uniform or <coercive positivity of a second variation> is the stronger assertion that $q(\varphi)\geq c\int\varphi'^2$ for some fixed $c>0$. On a fixed-endpoint interval this is equivalent to a positive lower bound in the $W^{1,2}$ norm.

**The strict Legendre implication uses the uniform interpretation of positivity.** If $q\geq c\int\varphi'^2$, localized rapidly oscillating variations show $p(x)\geq c$: otherwise support them in an interval where $p-c<0$, so the <derivative> term of $q-c\int\varphi'^2$ is negative of order frequency squared, overwhelming its mixed and zero-order terms. Continuity extends the bound to the endpoints, giving $p>0$ on $[a,b]$. Under mere <positive definiteness> this strict conclusion is false: $f=u^2$ at $\bar u=0$ gives $q=2\int\varphi^2>0$ but $p=0$. In that weaker convention only the ordinary <Legendre necessary condition> $p\geq0$ follows. This distinction makes the implication precise.

Under the stated regular condition $p>0$, a <conjugate point> $c\in(a,b]$ to $a$ is an endpoint admitting a nonzero Jacobi solution with $z(a)=z(c)=0$. The disconjugacy form of the <Jacobi positivity criterion> says that no such point in $(a,b]$ exists if and only if there is a Jacobi solution strictly positive on all of $[a,b]$.

For the required identity, let that positive solution be $z$ and write $\varphi=zh$. Set $A=pzz'+rz^2$. The <Jacobi equation> gives
$$
A'=pz'^2+2rzz'+sz^2.
$$
Expanding the second-variation integrand therefore yields
$$
p\varphi'^2+2r\varphi\varphi'+s\varphi^2=pz^2h'^2+(Ah^2)'.
$$
For compactly supported $\varphi$, the <boundary term> vanishes, proving
$$
\boxed{\delta^2I(\bar u)(\varphi,\varphi)=\int_a^bpz^2\left[\left(\frac\varphi z\right)'\right]^2dx.}
$$
This is the <Jacobi identity for the second variation>. The divergence form is also appropriate under the original $C^1$ regularity; with $p>0$, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives the usual further regularity along the extremal.

= Solution
{parent=i}

At zero, the sixth-power term has zero second <derivative>, so
$$
\delta^2I_\lambda(0)(\varphi,\varphi)=2Q_\lambda(\varphi),\qquad Q_\lambda(u)=\int_0^\pi(u'^2-\lambda^2u^2)dx.
$$
For $0<\lambda<1$, the Jacobi solution $z(x)=\cos(\lambda(x-\pi/2))$ is strictly positive on $[0,\pi]$. The preceding identity gives $Q_\lambda(u)=\int z^2[(u/z)']^2>0$ for nonzero zero-trace $u$.

To control the nonlinear remainder, a uniform lower bound follows directly. Let $m=\min z>0$, $K=\max|z'|$, and $v=u/z$. Since $\max z=1$ and $\int v^2\leq(\pi^2/2)\int v'^2$ by the elementary endpoint estimate,
$$
\int u'^2\leq2K^2\int v^2+2\int v'^2\leq(K^2\pi^2+2)\int v'^2.
$$
Thus $Q_\lambda(u)\geq c_\lambda\int u'^2$ with $c_\lambda=m^2/(K^2\pi^2+2)>0$. Also $\int u^6\leq\|u\|_\infty^4(\pi^2/2)\int u'^2$, so
$$
I_\lambda(u)\geq\left(c_\lambda-\frac{\pi^2}{4018}\|u\|_\infty^4\right)\int u'^2.
$$
This is positive for every nonzero admissible $u$ sufficiently close to zero in $W^{1,\infty}$. Therefore **zero has positive <second variation> and is a strict weak local minimizer**. No sharp <Poincaré inequality> has been assumed in this proof.

For every $0<\lambda<1$, positivity in the preceding part gives $\int u'^2\geq\lambda^2\int u^2$ for each fixed admissible $u$. Let $\lambda\uparrow1$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\pi u'^2dx\geq\int_0^\pi u^2dx.}
$$
The sharp constant is attained by $u(x)=\sin x$, consistently with the first <conjugate point> occurring at the endpoint for $\lambda=1$.

Choose any nonzero admissible $u_0$ and set $u=\gamma u_0$. Then
$$
I_\lambda(\gamma u_0)=\gamma^2\int_0^\pi(u_0'^2-\lambda^2u_0^2)dx-\frac{\gamma^6}{2009}\int_0^\pi u_0^6dx.

The coefficient of $-\gamma^6$ is strictly positive, so the energy tends to minus infinity as $|\gamma|\to\infty$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\inf_{\mathcal A}I_\lambda=-\infty,\qquad0\text{ is not a global minimizer}.}
$$

A <weak local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which some $\rho>0$ makes $I(v)\geq I(\bar u)$ whenever $v\in\mathcal A$ and $\|v-\bar u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\rho$. Both the <function> and its <derivative> are required to be close.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <strong local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which the same conclusion holds whenever $\|v-\bar u\|_\infty<\rho$. Every strong local minimizer is weak, because Sobolev closeness implies uniform closeness. A <global minimizer> is both, and the converse implication from weak to strong is not generally valid.

If the integrand is independent of $u$, a weak local minimizer has vanishing <first variation>:
$$
\int_a^bf_v(x,\bar u'(x))\varphi'(x)dx=0\quad(\varphi\in C_0^\infty(a,b)).
$$
The bounded <function> $h(x)=f_v(x,\bar u'(x))$ therefore has zero <distributional derivative>, so is constant <almost everywhere>. For an elementary test-<function> argument, any smooth compactly supported $\psi$ of <integral> zero is the <derivative> of a compactly supported smooth $\varphi$. Hence $\int h\psi=0$; subtracting a fixed unit-<integral> <test function> from arbitrary tests shows that $h$ agrees with one constant against every test. The fundamental lemma then gives
$$
\boxed{f_v(x,\bar u'(x))=c\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$
This is the <first integral for a variational integrand independent of the function value>.

A <point of convexity> $v_0$ of a differentiable real <function> $g$ has a globally supporting tangent: $g(v)\geq g(v_0)+g'(v_0)(v-v_0)$ for every $v$. The <Weierstrass necessary condition> for a strong minimum says that $\bar u'(x)$ is such a point for $v\mapsto f(x,\bar u(x),v)$ for almost every $x$. Equivalently, the <Weierstrass excess function> is nonnegative at every trial slope.

**Zero is neither a weak nor a strong local minimizer.** Take $u_\varepsilon(x)=-\varepsilon\sin(\pi x)$. It tends to zero in $W^{1,\infty}$ and has zero endpoint values. Since $\sqrt{1+t}\leq1+t/2$ for $t\geq0$,
$$
I(u_\varepsilon)-I(0)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^2\pi^2}{4}-\frac{4018}{\pi}\varepsilon<0
$$
for sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$. Thus even weakly close admissible competitors decrease the energy.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**Zero is neither a weak nor a strong local minimizer.** For any nonzero smooth zero-trace $\varphi$, <Taylor expansion> gives
$$
I(\varepsilon\varphi)-I(0)=\varepsilon^2\left[-\frac12\int(\varphi+\varphi')^2+\frac18\int\varphi'^2+\int\varphi^2\right]+O(\varepsilon^4).
$$
The mixed <integral> $\int\varphi\varphi'=0$ by the endpoint values. Writing $D=\int\varphi'^2$ and $U=\int\varphi^2$, the quadratic coefficient is $-3D/8+U/2$. The elementary estimate $U\leq D/2$ on $[0,1]$ makes this at most $-D/8<0$. Thus arbitrarily small variations decrease the energy. The separate coefficient one on $u^2$ is retained in this calculation.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

**Zero is a strict weak local minimizer, but is not a strong local minimizer.** The <derivative> integrand has expansion $\cosh v\cos(v^2)=1+v^2/2+O(v^4)$, so for small $|v|$ it is at least $1+3v^2/8$. <Concavity> gives $(1+u^2)^{1/4}\leq1+u^2/4$ for every real $u$. Therefore, when $\|u'\|_\infty$ is small,
$$
I(u)-I(0)\geq\frac38\int_0^1u'^2-\frac12\int_0^1u^2\geq\frac18\int_0^1u'^2,
$$
using $\int u^2\leq\tfrac12\int u'^2$. This proves strict weak minimality.

For the strong claim, let $v_N(x)=\sqrt\pi\,\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z)$. Its amplitude tends to zero and its <derivative> is $\pm\sqrt\pi$ <almost everywhere>, so the <derivative> integrand is the constant $-\cosh\sqrt\pi$. The other integrand term is at most $-2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{I(v_N)\leq-\cosh\sqrt\pi-2<-1=I(0),\qquad\|v_N\|_\infty\to0.}
$$
This disproves strong minimality. The same obstruction is detected by the <Weierstrass necessary condition>, since the supporting-tangent inequality at slope zero fails at $v=\sqrt\pi$.
