= Solution

A <weak local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which there is $\rho>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{whenever }v\in\mathcal A,\ \|v-\bar u\|_{W^{1,\infty}}<\rho.}
$$
The <Sobolev norm> controls both the function and its first <weak derivative>. The boundary values remain fixed because both functions are admissible.

A <strong local minimizer of a variational integral> is an admissible $\bar u$ for which some $\rho>0$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{I(v)\geq I(\bar u)\quad\text{for }v\in\mathcal A\text{ with }\|v-\bar u\|_\infty<\rho.}
$$
Only the function values must be close; admissible derivatives may be large. Every strong local minimizer is therefore also a <weak local minimizer of a variational integral>.

A <point of convexity> of $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ is a point $v_0$ where its tangent line supports the entire graph from below:
$$
g(v)\geq g(v_0)+g'(v_0)(v-v_0)\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathbb R.
$$
The <Weierstrass necessary condition> says that, at almost every $x$, $\bar u'(x)$ is a <point of convexity> of $v\mapsto f(x,\bar u(x),v)$. In terms of the <Weierstrass excess function>,
$$
E(x,\bar u,\bar u',v)=f(x,\bar u,v)-f(x,\bar u,\bar u')-f_v(x,\bar u,\bar u')(v-\bar u')\geq0\quad(v\in\mathbb R).
$$

Now use the stated <derivative> <coercivity>. If $\bar u$ were not a local minimizer in $L^1$, there would be $v_n\in\mathcal A$ with $\|v_n-\bar u\|_1\to0$ and $I(v_n)<I(\bar u)$. The energy bound gives
$$
c\int_a^b|v_n'|^{2010}\,dx\leq I(\bar u)+d(b-a).
$$
The common value $v_n(a)=\alpha$ controls their function norms as well, by $|v_n(x)|\leq|\alpha|+\int_a^b|v_n'|$. Thus $(v_n)$ is bounded in $W^{1,2010}(a,b)$. The one-dimensional compact <Sobolev embedding> into $C([a,b])$ supplies a uniformly convergent subsequence. Its limit must be $\bar u$, since the same subsequence converges to $\bar u$ in $L^1$. Eventually $\|v_n-\bar u\|_\infty<\rho$, contradicting strong local minimality. Hence **$\bar u$ is also a local minimizer in $L^1$**. This is <coercivity transfers strong minimality to L1 minimality>.

**Zero is a strict weak local minimizer, but is not a strong local minimizer.** Put $D=\int_0^1|u'|^2\,dx$. Since $u(0)=0$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|u(x)|^2\leq xD$, and therefore
$$
\int_0^1x^{2010}u(x)^2\,dx\leq\frac{D}{2012}.
$$
For sufficiently small $\|u'\|_\infty$, $\sin(u'^2)\geq\tfrac12u'^2$ <pointwise>. Hence
$$
I(u)\geq\left(\frac12-\frac1{2012}\right)D\geq0,
$$
with equality only when $u=0$. Thus zero is a strict <weak local minimizer of a variational integral>.

For the strong claim, define a <Lipschitz> triangular wave
$$
u_N(x)=\sqrt\pi\,\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z).
$$
It has zero endpoint values, $\|u_N\|_\infty=\sqrt\pi/(2N)\to0$, and $u_N'=\pm\sqrt\pi$ almost everywhere. Thus $\sin(u_N'^2)=\sin\pi=0$, while $u_N$ is nonzero on a set of positive measure. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I(u_N)=-\int_0^1x^{2010}u_N^2\,dx<0=I(0).}
$$
These uniformly small competitors disprove the <strong local minimizer of a variational integral> property.

**Zero is a strict strong local minimizer, and therefore also a strict weak local minimizer.** Here $I(0)=-2/2010$, and
$$
I(u)-I(0)=\int_0^1u'^2\,dx-\frac2{2010}\int_0^1(\cosh u-1)\,dx.
$$
For $|u|\leq1$, <Taylor's theorem> gives $\cosh u-1\leq\tfrac12\cosh(1)u^2$. Also $u(0)=0$ and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $\int_0^1u^2\,dx\leq\tfrac12\int_0^1u'^2\,dx$. Therefore
$$
I(u)-I(0)\geq\left(1-\frac{\cosh1}{4020}\right)\int_0^1u'^2\,dx.
$$
The coefficient is positive. Thus every nonzero admissible $u$ with $\|u\|_\infty\leq1$ has strictly greater energy, proving that zero is a strict <strong local minimizer of a variational integral>.

= Solution

For a fixed-endpoint variation $\varphi\in W^{1,\infty}_0(a,b)$, the <second variation> is
$$
\delta^2I(\bar u)(\varphi,\varphi)=\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}I(\bar u+t\varphi)\right|_{t=0}=\int_a^b\left(f_{vv}\varphi'^2+2f_{uv}\varphi\varphi'+f_{uu}\varphi^2\right)dx,
$$
where all coefficients are evaluated at $(x,\bar u(x),\bar u'(x))$. The notation $\delta^2I(\bar u)>0$ means this is strictly positive for every nonzero admissible variation. This is <pointwise> positive definiteness of the <quadratic form>, not an assertion of a prescribed uniform lower bound.

Put
$$
P=f_{vv},\qquad Q=f_{uu}-\frac d{dx}f_{uv}.
$$
After <integration by parts>, the form is $q(\varphi)=\int_a^b(P\varphi'^2+Q\varphi^2)\,dx$. Its <Jacobi equation> is
$$
\boxed{-(Pw')'+Qw=0.}
$$
In the regular case $P>0$ on $[a,b]$, a <conjugate point> $c\in(a,b]$ to $a$ is a point for which a nonzero solution satisfies $w(a)=w(c)=0$. Equivalently it is a zero of the normalized solution with $w(a)=0$, $P(a)w'(a)=1$.

Under this regular <strengthened Legendre condition>, the <Jacobi positivity criterion> is
$$
\boxed{q>0\iff\text{there is no conjugate point to }a\text{ in }(a,b].}
$$
The endpoint $b$ must be excluded as a <conjugate point> too. To prove necessity, suppose a Jacobi solution vanishes at $a$ and $c$. Define $\varphi=w$ on $[a,c]$ and zero on $[c,b]$. It is a nonzero <Lipschitz> admissible variation, and
$$
q(\varphi)=\int_a^c(Pw'^2+Qw^2)dx=[Pww']_a^c=0,
$$
a contradiction. For completeness, the converse follows from the <Jacobi identity for the second variation>: when the normalized $w$ has no further zero,
$$
q(\varphi)=\int_a^bP\left(\varphi'-\frac{w'}w\varphi\right)^2dx.
$$
The boundary contribution $[P(w'/w)\varphi^2]_a^b$ vanishes, including the limit at $a$ since $w$ has a simple zero and $\varphi=O(x-a)$. Equality forces $\varphi=Cw$, and $\varphi(b)=0$ with $w(b)\ne0$ forces $C=0$.

Independently of regularity, positivity necessarily implies the ordinary <Legendre necessary condition> $P\geq0$. If $P<0$ on an interval, localized rapidly oscillating test functions have a negative <derivative> contribution of order frequency squared, dominating the bounded zero-order term. This contradicts positivity.

The assumptions stated here do not themselves impose $P>0$. That strengthened condition is a regularity hypothesis for the familiar Jacobi criterion, and is not necessary for strict positivity: the integrand $f=u^2$ gives $q(\varphi)=2\int\varphi^2>0$ with $P=0$. For a criterion covering the full possibly degenerate class, use <regularized Jacobi criterion for a degenerate second variation>. The following conditions are necessary and sufficient:

* $P\geq0$;
* for every $\eta>0$, the regular equation $-((P+\eta)w')'+Qw=0$ has no <conjugate point> to $a$ in $(a,b]$;
* the original weak Jacobi boundary problem has no nonzero <Lipschitz> solution with zero endpoint values.

These conditions are necessary because $q>0$ gives $q_\eta(\varphi)=q(\varphi)+\eta\int\varphi'^2>0$, so the regular criterion just proved applies; a weak Jacobi solution tested against itself would give $q=0$. They are sufficient as well: the regular criterion gives $q_\eta>0$ for every $\eta>0$, hence $q\geq0$ by letting $\eta\downarrow0$. If $q(\varphi)=0$, nonnegativity of $q(\varphi+t\psi)$ for every real $t$ forces its mixed bilinear term to vanish for every test $\psi$. This is precisely the weak <Jacobi equation>. The third condition then forces $\varphi=0$. Thus the criterion treats degeneracy without silently assuming the strengthened condition.

Here $P=2$, $Q=-2\lambda^2$ and the <Jacobi equation> is $w''+\lambda^2w=0$. With $w(0)=0$ and $w'(0)=1$, its solution is $w(x)=\sin(|\lambda|x)/|\lambda|$ for $\lambda\ne0$, and $w(x)=x$ for $\lambda=0$. Its first positive zero is $\pi/|\lambda|$ when $\lambda\ne0$.

If $\lambda^2<\pi^2/b^2$, no <conjugate point> occurs in $(0,b]$. The <Jacobi positivity criterion> gives
$$
\boxed{\delta^2I_\lambda(0)(\varphi,\varphi)=2\int_0^b(\varphi'^2-\lambda^2\varphi^2)dx>0\quad(\varphi\ne0).}
$$
Since $I_\lambda$ itself is this <quadratic form> divided by two, $I_\lambda(u)>0=I_\lambda(0)$ for every nonzero admissible $u$. Thus **zero is the unique <global minimizer>**. This argument establishes the result before using the sharp <Poincaré inequality>.

= Solution

Fix an admissible $u$. The preceding result gives $\int_0^bu'^2\,dx\geq\lambda^2\int_0^bu^2\,dx$ for every $\lambda^2<\pi^2/b^2$. Letting $\lambda^2$ increase to $\pi^2/b^2$ proves
$$
\boxed{\int_0^bu'^2dx\geq\frac{\pi^2}{b^2}\int_0^bu^2dx.}
$$
Equality is attained by
$$
\boxed{u(x)=A\sin(\pi x/b),\qquad A\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Indeed the <derivative> and function squared integrals are $A^2\pi^2/(2b)$ and $A^2b/2$. These are all equality cases: an equality function minimizes the nonnegative threshold <quadratic form>, so its <first variation> vanishes. The resulting weak equation $u''+(\pi/b)^2u=0$ makes $u$ smooth and, with the endpoint conditions, yields precisely these multiples. This is the <Wirtinger inequality>.

The function $u_*(x)=x^{1/4}$ has <weak derivative> $u_*'(x)=\tfrac14x^{-3/4}\in L^1(0,1)$ and the required endpoint values, so it belongs to the admissible <Sobolev space>. Its fourth power is $x$, and hence
$$
\boxed{I_0(u_*)=0.}
$$
Since the integrand $(u^4-x)^2$ is nonnegative, zero is a global lower bound. Thus **$u_*$ is a <global minimizer>**. Its <derivative> does not belong to $L^{4/3}$, since its $4/3$ power is proportional to $x^{-1}$; positive regularization therefore needs different competitors.

= Solution

A convenient form of the <Tonelli existence theorem> is: on a finite interval with prescribed endpoint values, if a continuous integrand $F(x,z,v)$ is <convex> in $v$ and satisfies $F(x,z,v)\geq c|v|^p-C$ for some $p>1$, then, provided a finite-energy admissible competitor exists, its integral attains a minimum in the corresponding $W^{1,p}$ class. The lower bound may more generally subtract an integrable function of $x$. Compact one-dimensional <Sobolev embedding> controls $z$, while <convexity> gives <weak lower semicontinuity> in the <derivative>.

Here $p=4/3$ and $F_\varepsilon=(z^4-x)^2+\varepsilon|v|^{4/3}$ is continuous, <convex> in $v$, and bounded below by $\varepsilon|v|^{4/3}$. The competitor $u(x)=x$ has finite energy. Thus for every $\varepsilon>0$ the <Tonelli existence theorem> gives an admissible minimizer in $W^{1,4/3}(0,1)$.

Any finite-energy member of the larger stated $W^{1,1}$ class necessarily has <derivative> in $L^{4/3}$ and bounded function values by <absolute continuity>; it therefore belongs to $W^{1,4/3}$. All other competitors have infinite energy. Hence **the same function $u_\varepsilon$ is a <global minimizer> in the full stated class**.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The finite-energy comparison $I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)\leq I_\varepsilon(x)<\infty$ gives $\varepsilon\int|u_\varepsilon'|^{4/3}<\infty$. Together with the endpoint value and <absolute continuity>, this proves $u_\varepsilon\in W^{1,4/3}(0,1)$.

For $\alpha>1/4$, the power competitor $u_\alpha=x^\alpha$ is admissible and has finite <derivative> energy. Direct integration gives
$$
I_\varepsilon(u_\alpha)=\frac1{8\alpha+1}-\frac2{4\alpha+2}+\frac13+\frac{3\varepsilon\alpha^{4/3}}{4\alpha-1}.
$$
Choose $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/3}$ and $\alpha=1/4+\delta$. The first three terms tend to zero as $\alpha\downarrow1/4$, while the last equals $\tfrac34\varepsilon^{2/3}(1/4+\varepsilon^{1/3})^{4/3}\to0$. By minimality and nonnegativity,
$$
\boxed{0\leq I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)\leq I_\varepsilon(u_{1/4+\varepsilon^{1/3}})\longrightarrow0.}
$$
One also obtains the quantitative bound $I_\varepsilon(u_\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$: for $\alpha=1/4+\delta$, the <mean value theorem> gives $|x^{4\alpha}-x|\leq4\delta x|\log x|$, and $\int_0^1x^2\log^2x\,dx=2/27$. Thus the squared mismatch is at most $32\delta^2/27$, matching the order of the <derivative> penalty with this choice. This is a <power-law comparison for vanishing derivative regularization>.

Use the <direct method in the calculus of variations>. The affine function $1-x$ is admissible with finite energy, and the energy is nonnegative since $2010$ is even. Thus $m=\inf I$ is finite. Choose a <minimizing sequence> $u_n$ with uniformly bounded energy. Then
$$
\frac12\int_0^1|u_n'|^2dx\leq I(u_n)\leq C.
$$
The fixed value $u_n(0)=1$ and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $\|u_n\|_\infty\leq1+\|u_n'\|_2$ and $|u_n(x)-u_n(y)|\leq\|u_n'\|_2|x-y|^{1/2}$. Hence the sequence is bounded in $W^{1,2}$ and uniformly bounded and equicontinuous.

By <reflexivity> and the <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem>, pass to a subsequence with $u_n\rightharpoonup u$ in $W^{1,2}$ and $u_n\to u$ uniformly. The two limits coincide; <uniform convergence> also preserves $u(0)=1$, $u(1)=0$. Since all functions stay in a common bounded interval, <uniform convergence> gives $u_n^{2010}\to u^{2010}$ uniformly, and therefore convergence of the potential integrals. The <derivative> term is lower semicontinuous for <weak convergence>, so
$$
I(u)\leq\liminf_n\frac12\int|u_n'|^2+\lim_n\int u_n^{2010}=\liminf_nI(u_n)=m.
$$
Admissibility gives $I(u)\geq m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I(u)=m,\quad\text{so the global minimum is attained}.}
$$
