== 1

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <minimal polynomial> of a <linear transformation> $T$ is the unique <monic polynomial> of least <degree> among the nonzero <polynomials> $p\in F[x]$ satisfying $p(T)=0$:
$$
\boxed{m_T\text{ monic},\quad m_T(T)=0,\quad\deg m_T\text{ least possible}.}
$$
Such a <polynomial> exists because the <endomorphism> space is finite-dimensional, so sufficiently many <powers> $I,T,T^2,\ldots$ are <linearly dependent>. Monic normalization is possible over the <field> $F$. Two <monic polynomials> annihilating $T$ of the same least <degree> have a difference of smaller <degree> annihilating $T$, so their difference is zero. For the zero <vector space> the convention is $m_T=1$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply <polynomial division> in $F[x]$:
$$
f=q m_T+r,\qquad r=0\text{ or }\deg r<\deg m_T.
$$
Evaluation at $T$ gives $r(T)=f(T)-q(T)m_T(T)=0$. A nonzero remainder could be rescaled to a monic <polynomial> annihilating $T$ of smaller <degree>, contradicting the defining minimality. Hence $r=0$ and
$$
\boxed{m_T\mid f.}
$$
In particular, the annihilating <polynomials> form the <principal ideal> $(m_T)$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $p$ and $q$ are coprime, <Bézout's identity for polynomials> gives $a,b\in F[x]$ with $ap+bq=1$. For every $v\in V$,
$$
v=b(T)q(T)v+a(T)p(T)v.
$$
The first summand belongs to $U$: applying $p(T)$ gives $b(T)p(T)q(T)v=0$. The second belongs to $W$ by the same calculation. Here all <polynomials> in $T$ commute, and $p(T)q(T)=f(T)=0$. Thus $V=U+W$.

If $v\in U\cap W$, both terms on the right of the identity vanish, so $v=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V=U\oplus W.}
$$
Finally, $p(T)Tv=Tp(T)v=0$ whenever $v\in U$; hence $T(U)\subseteq U$. Replacing $p$ by $q$ proves $T(W)\subseteq W$. These are <invariant subspaces>; this is the coprime-factor case of the <primary decomposition theorem for linear maps>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $m_1,m_2$ for the <minimal polynomials> of the two <restrictions>. Since $p(T)$ vanishes on $U$ and $q(T)$ vanishes on $W$, part (b) gives
$$
m_1\mid p,\qquad m_2\mid q.
$$
Hence $m_1,m_2$ are coprime. By the direct-sum decomposition, a <polynomial> annihilates $T$ exactly when it annihilates both <restrictions>, which happens exactly when it is divisible by both $m_1$ and $m_2$. Consequently
$$
m_T=\operatorname{lcm}(m_1,m_2)=m_1m_2.
$$
If $m_1=p$ and $m_2=q$, this immediately gives $m_T=pq=f$. Conversely, if $m_T=pq$, the <monic polynomials> $p/m_1$ and $q/m_2$ have product one, so each is one. Thus
$$
\boxed{m_T=pq\iff m_{T|_U}=p\text{ and }m_{T|_W}=q.}
$$
This also handles a zero summand, whose <minimal polynomial> is one; a nonconstant factor cannot be its <minimal polynomial>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Part (b) requires $m_T$ to be a monic <divisor> of $x^2(x-1)$. The constant <divisor> one cannot annihilate a nonzero <vector space>. Every remaining <divisor> can occur in dimension three:
$$
\boxed{m_T\in\{x,\ x^2,\ x-1,\ x(x-1),\ x^2(x-1)\}.}
$$
Here are corresponding <matrices>, all satisfying $T^3-T^2=0$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
m_T&\text{matrix of }T\\\hline
x&0_{3\times3}\\
x^2&\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\\
x-1&I_3\\
x(x-1)&\operatorname{diag}(0,0,1)\\
x^2(x-1)&\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\end{array}
$$
The last <matrix> contains a size-two <nilpotent> <Jordan block>, requiring the factor $x^2$, and an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> one, requiring $x-1$. Thus its <minimal polynomial> is exactly $f$. The <zero matrix> has <minimal polynomial> $x\neq f$, providing the other requested example.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $p=\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle v_j$. <Orthonormality> gives $\langle u-p,v_k\rangle=0$ for every $k$, so $u-p$ is <orthogonal> to $p$. The <Pythagorean theorem for inner product spaces> gives
$$
\|u\|^2=\|u-p\|^2+\|p\|^2
=\|u-p\|^2+\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle^2.
$$
Since a squared <norm> is nonnegative,
$$
\boxed{\langle u,u\rangle\geq\sum_{j=1}^m\langle u,v_j\rangle^2.}
$$
This is <Bessel's inequality>, with equality exactly when $u$ belongs to the <span> of the <orthonormal set>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <Euclidean inner product> on $\mathbb R^n$, with $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and $b=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$. If $b=0$, both sides of the desired inequality are zero. Otherwise take the single <unit vector> $v=b/\|b\|$ in part (a). Then
$$
\|a\|^2\geq\frac{\langle a,b\rangle^2}{\|b\|^2},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2\right)\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i\right)^2.}
$$
This is the real <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Equality holds when the two <vectors> are <linearly dependent>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

In part (i), set every $b_i=1$. Its squared <norm> is $n$, giving
$$
\boxed{n\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^2.}
$$
The equality condition is $a_1=\cdots=a_n$, because $a$ must be a <scalar multiple> of $(1,\ldots,1)$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

In $\mathbb R^{2n}$, take the two <unit vectors>
$$
v_{\mathrm{even}}=\frac1{\sqrt n}(0,1,0,1,\ldots,0,1),\qquad
v_{\mathrm{odd}}=\frac1{\sqrt n}(1,0,1,0,\ldots,1,0).
$$
Their supports are disjoint, so they are <orthonormal>. Apply part (a) to $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_{2n})$:
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2\geq\frac1n\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i}\right)^2+\frac1n\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i-1}\right)^2.
$$
Multiplying by $n$ gives
$$
\boxed{n\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i}\right)^2+\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_{2i-1}\right)^2.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Equality in part (a) requires $a$ to lie in the <span> of the two chosen <unit vectors>. Equivalently, there are real constants $r,s$ such that
$$
\boxed{a_{2i-1}=r,\qquad a_{2i}=s\qquad(1\leq i\leq n).}
$$
One can also see the necessity directly by writing the nonnegative deficit as
$$
n\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_i^2-\left(\sum_{i=1}^na_{2i}\right)^2-\left(\sum_{i=1}^na_{2i-1}\right)^2
=n\sum_{i=1}^n(a_{2i}-\bar a_e)^2+n\sum_{i=1}^n(a_{2i-1}-\bar a_o)^2,
$$
where $\bar a_e$ and $\bar a_o$ are the means of the even and odd entries. The deficit vanishes exactly when each group is constant; the two group constants need not be equal.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <first isomorphism theorem for rings> states that the map
$$
\boxed{R/\ker f\longrightarrow\operatorname{im}f,\qquad r+\ker f\longmapsto f(r)}
$$
is a <ring isomorphism>. It is well defined because two representatives differ by an element of the <kernel> exactly when their images agree. It preserves addition and multiplication, is onto its stated codomain, and has trivial <kernel>. In particular, if $f$ is <surjective> then $R/\ker f\cong S$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Define $\Phi:R\to R/I\times R/J$ by $\Phi(r)=(r+I,r+J)$. It is a <ring homomorphism> with
$$
\ker\Phi=I\cap J.
$$
Since $I+J=R$, choose $i\in I,j\in J$ such that $i+j=1$. For arbitrary $a,b\in R$, the element $r=aj+bi$ satisfies $r-a=i(b-a)\in I$ and $r-b=j(a-b)\in J$. Hence $\Phi(r)=(a+I,b+J)$, proving <surjectivity>. The first <isomorphism> theorem now gives
$$
\boxed{R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J.}
$$
This is the two-ideal <Chinese remainder theorem for ideals>. The hypothesis is comaximality of the two <ideals>; no assertion that every pair of <ideals> has this property is needed.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Coprimality> gives <polynomials> $a,b\in\mathbb Q[x]$ with $ag+bh=1$, so the <ideals> $(g)$ and $(h)$ are comaximal. Also
$$
(g)\cap(h)=(gh).
$$
Indeed, if $u=gr=hs$, multiplying $ag+bh=1$ by $u$ writes $u=aghs+bhgr$, a multiple of $gh$; the reverse inclusion is immediate. Part (b), with $f=gh$, gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q[x]/(f)\cong\mathbb Q[x]/(g)\times\mathbb Q[x]/(h).}
$$
The explicit <isomorphism> takes the class of a <polynomial> $r$ to its classes modulo $g$ and $h$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Because $g,h$ are nonconstant, their classes in $\mathbb Q[x]/(gh)$ are nonzero: each has smaller <degree> than $gh$, so neither is a multiple of $gh$. Their product is zero. Thus **the <quotient> has <zero divisors>**.

If both factors have <degree> one, each <quotient> by one factor is <isomorphic> to $\mathbb Q$, by evaluation at its rational <root>. <Coprimality> ensures the two roots are distinct. Part (c) therefore identifies the ring with $\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q$. If $(r,s)^2=(0,0)$, then $r^2=s^2=0$ in a <field>, so $r=s=0$.
$$
\boxed{\text{There is no nonzero square-zero element when both factors have degree one.}}
$$
A ring may therefore have <zero divisors> without having nonzero <nilpotent> elements.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Factorization over $\mathbb Q$ gives
$$
R_1\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q,\qquad
R_2\cong\mathbb Q(i),\qquad
R_3\cong\mathbb Q[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),\quad\varepsilon=x-1.
$$
For $R_1$, the factors $x-1$ and $x+1$ are coprime. The nonzero elements $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ multiply to zero, so the ring is neither an <integral domain> nor a <field>. Squaring coordinatewise shows it has no nonzero square-zero element.

For $R_2$, the <polynomial> $x^2+1$ has no rational <root> and is <irreducible> of <degree> two over $\mathbb Q$. The <quotient> is consequently a <field>, hence an <integral domain>, and has no nonzero square-zero element.

For $R_3$, the class $\varepsilon$ is nonzero by its <degree>, but $\varepsilon^2=0$. Thus this ring is neither an <integral domain> nor a <field>, and does have a nonzero square-zero element.
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccc}&\text{integral domain}&\text{field}&\exists u\neq0:\ u^2=0\\\hline
R_1&\text{no}&\text{no}&\text{no}\\
R_2&\text{yes}&\text{yes}&\text{no}\\
R_3&\text{no}&\text{no}&\text{yes}
\end{array}}
$$
**No two are <isomorphic>.** Being a <field> separates $R_2$ from the other two; existence of a nonzero square-zero element separates $R_1$ from $R_3$. Both properties are preserved by <ring isomorphisms>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A function is <holomorphic> on an <open set> when it is <complex differentiable> at every point of that set. Explicitly, for every $z\in U$, the limit
$$
\boxed{f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0,\ h\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}}\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h}
$$
exists as a finite <complex number>. The limit is independent of the direction in which the complex increment approaches zero; existence of <derivatives> only along the real axis would not suffice.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

<Cauchy's integral theorem> says that if $f$ is <holomorphic> on an <open set> $U$ and a closed, <piecewise smooth> <contour> $\gamma$ is <null-homotopic> in $U$, then
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0.}
$$
In particular, this holds for every closed <contour> when $U$ is <simply connected>, and for a simple closed <contour> whose boundary and enclosed region lie in $U$. For the later arguments it suffices to use the triangular version: the <integral> around a <triangle> is zero if the <triangle> and its interior lie in $U$ and $f$ is <holomorphic> there.

Both the holomorphicity condition and a suitable condition excluding holes inside the <contour> matter. <Continuity> alone, which is the starting hypothesis for this question, does not imply the theorem's conclusion; nor does holomorphicity on an arbitrary punctured <open set> imply zero <integral> around every loop.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Morera's theorem> states that a <continuous> function on an <open subset> of $\mathbb C$ is <holomorphic> if its <integral> around every triangular <contour>, with <triangle> and interior contained in that <open set>, is zero.

To prove this, work in any open disk $D$ whose closure lies in $U$, and fix $z_0\in D$. Define
$$
F(z)=\int_{[z_0,z]}f(w)\,dw,
$$
where the segment lies in $D$ by <convexity>. The zero <integral> on the <triangle> with vertices $z_0,z,z+h$ gives
$$
F(z+h)-F(z)=\int_{[z,z+h]}f(w)\,dw
=h\int_0^1f(z+th)\,dt.
$$
<Continuity> at $z$ implies that the last <integral> tends to $f(z)$ as $h\to0$: its difference from $f(z)$ is <bounded> by $\sup_{0\leq t\leq1}|f(z+th)-f(z)|$. Therefore $F'=f$ on $D$. The function $F$ is <holomorphic>, and the allowed theorem that a <holomorphic> <derivative> is <holomorphic> implies that $f=F'$ is <holomorphic> on $D$.

Every point of $U$ lies in such a disk, proving the result throughout $U$:
$$
\boxed{\text{continuity plus vanishing triangle integrals implies holomorphicity}.}
$$
The proof is local and does not require $U$ to be <simply connected>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For two moving vertices $p(s),q(s)$, the <integral> along their joining segment is
$$
J_{p,q}(s)=\bigl(q(s)-p(s)\bigr)\int_0^1 f\bigl((1-t)p(s)+tq(s)\bigr)\,dt.
$$
The <integral> factor is <continuous> in $s$ by the allowed fact, and the other factor is <continuous> because the vertices are <continuous>. Thus $J_{p,q}$ is <continuous>, including when its two endpoints coincide.

The <integral> around the <triangle> is exactly
$$
I(s)=J_{a,b}(s)+J_{b,c}(s)+J_{c,a}(s).
$$
Each term is <continuous>, so their sum is <continuous> at zero and
$$
\boxed{\lim_{s\to0}\int_{\Delta_s}f(z)\,dz=\int_{\Delta_0}f(z)\,dz.}
$$
No holomorphicity is used here. <Compactness> of the fixed containing <triangle> also gives a direct proof of the allowed fact through <uniform continuity> of $f$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

First take $z\in U_-$ and put $w=\bar z\in U_+$. Reflection symmetry of the function gives
$$
\frac{f(z+h)-f(z)}h
=\overline{\frac{f(w+\bar h)-f(w)}{\bar h}}
\longrightarrow\overline{f'(w)}.
$$
Hence $f$ is <holomorphic> on $U_-$, with $f'(z)=\overline{f'(\bar z)}$ there.

It remains to cross the real axis. Fix a real point $p\in U$ and a small disk about $p$ with closure in $U$. Consider a <triangle> in a still smaller disk. Intersect its filled interior with the closed upper and lower half-planes and triangulate the resulting <convex> polygons. This gives finitely many <triangles> lying on one side of the real axis, possibly with edges on that axis. Degenerate pieces contribute zero.

For an upper piece, translate its three vertices by $i\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon>0$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ the translated <triangle> lies entirely in $U_+$ and remains in $U$, so its boundary <integral> is zero by Cauchy's theorem. Part (d), or its segment-integral proof, shows that the <integral> converges to that around the untranslated piece as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Thus the original piece has zero <integral>. Translate lower pieces by $-i\varepsilon$ to obtain the same conclusion in $U_-$.

When all the oriented <triangle> integrals are added, their internal edges cancel, including the segments on the real axis. The original <triangle> therefore has zero boundary <integral>. <Continuity> of $f$ on $U$ now allows <Morera's theorem> in the disk, proving holomorphicity near $p$. Together with the two half-plane arguments this gives
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is holomorphic throughout }U.}
$$
This is a <Morera gluing lemma across a straight line>; the original <continuity> hypothesis is essential to the limiting step.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an <isolated singularity> at $a$, write the <Laurent expansion> in a punctured disk as $f(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_k(z-a)^k$. Its <residue> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}(f;a)=c_{-1}.}
$$
The <sine> in the denominator has simple zeros precisely at the integers, and the numerator is nonzero there. Consequently $g(z)=\pi\cot(\pi z)$ has <simple poles> exactly at $n\in\mathbb Z$, with
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}(g;n)=\frac{\pi\cos(\pi n)}{\pi\cos(\pi n)}=1.}
$$
There are no other finite <singularities>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <residue theorem> states that if a function is <holomorphic> on and inside a positively oriented simple closed <contour> except at finitely many isolated <singularities> $a_1,\ldots,a_N$ in its interior, then
$$
\boxed{\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^N\operatorname{res}(f;a_j).}
$$
No <singularity> may lie on the <contour>. More generally, a closed <contour> has a winding-number-weighted sum of <residues>, provided it and its enclosed winding regions lie in the domain on which the isolated <singularities> are specified.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A subset $B\subseteq\mathbb C$, with its <subspace topology>, is <compact> when every <open cover> of $B$ has a <finite subcover>. The <Heine-Borel theorem> in the <complex plane>, identified with $\mathbb R^2$, says
$$
\boxed{B\text{ compact}\iff B\text{ closed and bounded}.}
$$
Closedness is in the ambient <complex plane>. For a <continuous> real-valued function on a nonempty <compact> set, the <supremum> and <infimum> are finite and attained.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Periodicity> reduces all the indicated vertical segments to the <compact> segment $1/2+i[-1,1]$, which misses every pole. <Continuity> therefore gives the first uniform bound. In fact an explicit identity gives a stronger estimate for every real $y$:
$$
g(m+1/2+iy)=-i\pi\tanh(\pi y),\qquad |g(m+1/2+iy)|\leq\pi.
$$
Thus we can take $D_1=\pi$.

Write $L=m+1/2$. On the vertical sides of the square the same bound holds, since both real coordinates are half-integers. On either horizontal side $|\operatorname{Im}z|=L\geq1/2$. For $\operatorname{Im}z=y>0$, put $w=e^{2\pi iz}$, so $|w|=e^{-2\pi y}$. Using
$$
\cot(\pi z)=i\frac{w+1}{w-1}
$$
gives $|g(z)|\leq\pi(1+e^{-2\pi y})/(1-e^{-2\pi y})\leq\pi\coth(\pi/2)$. <Conjugation> gives the same estimate below the real axis. Hence an explicit bound valid on every square is
$$
\boxed{D_2=\pi\coth(\pi/2).}
$$
For $L>|b|$, the <contour> has length $8L$ and every point on it has $|z|\geq L$. The <reverse triangle inequality> yields $|z^2-b^2|\geq L^2-b^2$, so the <ML inequality> gives
$$
\left|\int_{\gamma_m}\frac{g(z)}{z^2-b^2}\,dz\right|
\leq\frac{8LD_2}{L^2-b^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Orient the square positively. Its enclosed poles at the integers $-m,\ldots,m$ have <residues> $1/(n^2-b^2)$. Since $b$ is nonintegral, the additional poles $b$ and $-b$ are distinct from those integers and from one another. Their <residues> are $g(b)/(2b)$ and $g(-b)/(-2b)$, both equal to $g(b)/(2b)$ because $g$ is odd. Their combined contribution is therefore $\pi\cot(\pi b)/b$.

The <residue theorem> gives
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_m}\frac{g(z)}{z^2-b^2}\,dz
=-\frac1{b^2}+2\sum_{n=1}^m\frac1{n^2-b^2}+\frac{\pi\cot(\pi b)}b.
$$
The series is <absolutely convergent>, since its terms are $O(n^{-2})$. Taking the limit, then including the $n=0$ term, yields
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2-b^2}=-\frac1{2b^2}-\frac{\pi\cot(\pi b)}{2b},\qquad b\in\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Z.}
$$
This is <cotangent contour summation>. Keeping the $n=0$ term separate until the last step determines the sign of the $1/(2b^2)$ contribution.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <conformal map> preserves oriented <angles> between regular smooth curves through each point. For a <holomorphic> map with nonzero <derivative>, the <derivative> also describes the local scale and <rotation>.

Indeed, if two curves $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ meet at $z$ and have nonzero tangent <vectors> $v_1,v_2$, the real <chain rule> and <complex differentiability> give
$$
(f\circ\gamma_j)'=f'(z)v_j.
$$
Because $f'(z)\neq0$, the <image> <tangents> are nonzero and
$$
\boxed{\frac{(f\circ\gamma_2)'}{(f\circ\gamma_1)'}=\frac{v_2}{v_1}.}
$$
Taking arguments proves preservation of oriented <angles>. Multiplication by $f'(z)$ rotates every tangent by $\arg f'(z)$ and scales every length by $|f'(z)|$. Thus the hypothesis gives <conformality> at every point of $U$; global <injectivity> is not needed for this local conclusion.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Möbius transformation> maps generalized <circles> to generalized <circles> on the <Riemann sphere>. A <generalized circle> is an ordinary <circle> or a <straight line> together with the point at infinity. Its <image> is a <straight line> precisely when it contains infinity, equivalently when the <preimage> of infinity lies on the extended real axis.

If $c=0$, the pole is infinity itself and the transformation is the <affine map> $(a/d)z+b/d$, taking the extended real axis to a line. If $c\neq0$, the pole is $-d/c$, which is real exactly when $d/c$ is real. Thus the full condition is
$$
\boxed{c=0\quad\text{or}\quad c\neq0\text{ and }d/c\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Equivalently, it is $\operatorname{Im}(d\bar c)=0$, a condition also valid when $c=0$. If it fails, the <image> misses infinity and is therefore **an ordinary <circle>**. The nonzero <determinant> ensures that none of these generalized <circles> collapses to a point.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Liouville's theorem> states that every <bounded> <entire function> is constant:
$$
\boxed{f\text{ holomorphic on }\mathbb C,\quad\sup_{z\in\mathbb C}|f(z)|<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f\text{ constant}.}
$$
For example, the Cauchy <derivative> estimate on a <circle> of radius $R$ gives $|f'(z)|\leq M/R$. Letting $R\to\infty$ gives $f'=0$ everywhere, proving the conclusion.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

It suffices to construct an <injective> <holomorphic> map $\Phi_j:U_j\to\mathbb D$ for each domain. Then $\Phi_j\circ f$ is a <bounded> <entire function> and is constant by <Liouville's theorem>; <injectivity> of $\Phi_j$ forces $f$ to be constant.

For the <upper half-plane>, use the <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk>
$$
\Phi_1(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i}.
$$
Its denominator is nonzero there, and $|z+i|^2-|z-i|^2=4\operatorname{Im}z>0$, so its <image> lies in the <unit disk>. The <determinant> is nonzero, hence it is <injective> and <conformal>.

On the plane slit along the nonnegative real axis, choose the branch
$$
S(z)=\sqrt{|z|}\,e^{i\operatorname{Arg}z/2},\qquad0<\operatorname{Arg}z<2\pi.
$$
This is the <holomorphic square root> obtained from the corresponding <logarithm branch>. Its <argument> lies in $(0,\pi)$, so it maps $U_2$ bijectively onto the <upper half-plane>, with inverse $w\mapsto w^2$. Its <derivative> is $1/(2S(z))\neq0$. Therefore
$$
\Phi_2(z)=\frac{S(z)-i}{S(z)+i}
$$
is an <injective> <conformal map> of $U_2$ onto the <unit disk>.

For the complement of the closed interval, first use
$$
M(z)=\frac{z}{1-z}.
$$
Its only finite pole is the excluded point one, and it is <injective> with <derivative> $1/(1-z)^2\neq0$. If $M(z)=t\geq0$, solving gives $z=t/(1+t)\in[0,1)$, which is excluded. Hence $M(U_3)\subset U_2$, and
$$
\Phi_3(z)=\Phi_2\left(\frac{z}{1-z}\right)
$$
is the required <injective> <conformal map> into the disk. More precisely, $M(U_3)=U_2\setminus\{-1\}$ and $\Phi_3(U_3)=\mathbb D\setminus\{0\}$, since $S(-1)=i$. No claim that the interval complement is conformally equivalent to the whole disk is required.

Applying Liouville to the three <bounded> compositions gives
$$
\boxed{f:\mathbb C\to U_j\text{ holomorphic}\Longrightarrow f\text{ constant},\qquad j=1,2,3.}
$$
This illustrates <Liouville rigidity from a bounded holomorphic embedding>: an explicit <injective> <bounded> coordinate is enough.
