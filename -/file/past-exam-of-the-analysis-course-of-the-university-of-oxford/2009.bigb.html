= Solution
{parent=i}

A <normed vector space> is a real or complex <vector space> $X$ with a <norm> satisfying positivity, definiteness, absolute homogeneity and the <triangle inequality>. It is <separable> if it has a countable <dense subset> for the <norm> distance.

First prove the unlabelled general assertions. If $(x_n)$ is dense in $X$, choose $y_{n,m}\in Y\cap B(x_n,1/m)$ whenever that intersection is nonempty. These choices form a countable subset of $Y$. For any $y\in Y$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $m$ with $2/m<\varepsilon$ and $n$ with $\|x_n-y\|<1/m$. The corresponding intersection is nonempty and $\|y-y_{n,m}\|<2/m<\varepsilon$. Thus **every subspace of a <separable> <normed vector space> is <separable>**.

If a countable $S$ has dense <linear span>, take all finite linear combinations of its elements with rational coefficients in the real case, or coefficients in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ in the complex case. This set is countable and dense in the <linear span>, by approximating the finitely many coefficients of each combination. Its closure is therefore $X$, proving separability. Conversely, a countable dense set already has dense <linear span>.

For $c_0$, use all <finitely supported sequences> with coordinates in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$. Truncate a sequence tending to zero to control its supremum tail, then approximate the finite initial coordinates. This produces a countable <dense subset>, so
$$
\boxed{c_0\text{ is separable}.}
$$

= Solution

Map each bounded sequence $a=(a_n)$ to the continuous function that takes value $a_n$ at the integer $n\geq1$ and is affine on every interval $[n,n+1]$. Convexity of absolute value gives $|F_a(t)|\leq\sup_n|a_n|$, and evaluation at integers gives equality of suprema. Thus $a\mapsto F_a$ is an <isometric embedding> of the <l-infinity sequence space> into $C^b([1,\infty))$.

The former is not <separable> by the allowed fact. If the latter were <separable>, the subspace result just proved would make its isometric copy of $\ell_\infty$ <separable>, a contradiction. Hence
$$
\boxed{C^b([1,\infty))\text{ is not separable}.}
$$

For $1<p<\infty$, truncation makes the $\ell^p$ tail arbitrarily small, since $\sum_j|a_j|^p$ converges. Approximate the finite remaining coordinates by numbers in $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$. The resulting countable set of <finitely supported sequences> is dense. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ell^p\text{ is separable for }1<p<\infty.}
$$
The same construction also works for $p=1$, though that endpoint is not needed here.

The <norm>-preserving form of the <Hahn-Banach theorem> states: if $M$ is a <linear subspace> of a real or complex <normed vector space> $X$ and $f:M\to\mathbb K$ is a bounded <linear functional>, there exists $F\in X^*$ with $F|_M=f$ and $\|F\|=\|f\|$.

For a real <separable> $X$, put $C=\|f\|$. The case $C=0$ is immediate. To extend over one vector $x_0\notin M$, choose a real number $c$ between
$$
\sup_{m\in M}\bigl(f(m)-C\|m-x_0\|\bigr)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\inf_{n\in M}\bigl(C\|n+x_0\|-f(n)\bigr).
$$
The lower endpoint is no greater than the upper: for any $m,n\in M$,
$$
f(m)+f(n)=f(m+n)\leq C\|m+n\|\leq C\|m-x_0\|+C\|n+x_0\|.
$$
The endpoints are finite because testing zero bounds them between finite numbers. Define $F(m+tx_0)=f(m)+tc$. The upper inequality ensures $F(z)\leq C\|z\|$ for vectors with positive $x_0$ coefficient, and the lower inequality gives it for negative coefficient; it already holds on $M$. Applying the same bound to $-z$ gives $|F(z)|\leq C\|z\|$. Thus the extension preserves the <norm>.

Now add a countable dense sequence of vectors one at a time. The consistent extensions define a bounded functional of <norm> $C$ on the union of the increasing domains. That union is dense in $X$, so extension by <continuity> gives the desired functional on all of $X$. This proves the real <separable> case without a maximality argument.

For the density application, suppose the <closed linear span> $M$ were proper. The <Hahn-Banach theorem> supplies a nonzero continuous <linear functional> annihilating $M$. By <duality of sequence spaces>, it has the form $f(z)=\sum_{j\geq1}a_jz_j$ with $a\in\ell_1$, not identically zero. Let $m$ be the first index with $a_m\ne0$. Writing $t_n=(n+1)^{-1}$, annihilation of $x_n$ gives
$$
0=t_n^{-m}f(x_n)=a_m+\sum_{j>m}a_jt_n^{j-m}.
$$
The tail has absolute value at most $t_n\sum_{j>m}|a_j|\to0$. Letting $n\to\infty$ contradicts $a_m\ne0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{x_n:n\geq1\}=c_0.}
$$
This is <geometric sequences with accumulating ratios span c0>.

First give the general definitions and radius facts. The <spectrum of a bounded operator> is $\sigma(A)=\{\lambda:A-\lambda I\text{ has no bounded inverse}\}$, and the <point spectrum> is $p\sigma(A)=\{\lambda:\ker(A-\lambda I)\ne0\}$. If $|\lambda|>\|A\|$, the <Neumann series> inverts $I-A/\lambda$, hence also $A-\lambda I=-\lambda(I-A/\lambda)$. Thus $\sigma(A)$ is contained in the closed disc of radius $\|A\|$ and is bounded.

The <spectral radius> is $\nu(A)=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|$. The <spectral radius formula> is
$$
\boxed{\nu(A)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\|A^m\|^{1/m}=\inf_{m\geq1}\|A^m\|^{1/m}.}
$$
For the left shift $R$, an eigenvector satisfies $a_{j+1}=\lambda a_j$, so $a_j=\lambda^{j-1}a_1$. A nonzero such sequence is in $\ell_1$ exactly when $|\lambda|<1$. Since $\|R\|=1$, its spectrum is contained in the closed unit disc. It contains the open unit disc as <point spectrum> and therefore its closure by closedness of the spectrum. Hence
$$
\boxed{p\sigma(R)=\{\lambda:|\lambda|<1\},\qquad\sigma(R)=\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq1\}.}
$$

For the diagonal operator $S$, the equation $Sa=\lambda a$ gives $(j^{-1}-\lambda)a_j=0$ for every $j$. Thus a nonzero eigenvector exists exactly for $\lambda=1/j$, with eigenvector $e_j$; zero is not an eigenvalue because all diagonal entries are nonzero.

If $\lambda$ lies outside $\{0\}\cup\{1/j:j\geq1\}$, its distance from this closed set is positive. Dividing each coordinate by $j^{-1}-\lambda$ gives a <bounded inverse> of $S-\lambda I$. Zero lies in the spectrum: $\|Se_j\|_1=1/j\to0$, excluding a <bounded inverse>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p\sigma(S)=\{1/j:j\geq1\},\qquad\sigma(S)=\{0\}\cup\{1/j:j\geq1\}.}
$$

= Solution

The weighted right shift $T$ is injective, so zero is not an eigenvalue. If $Ta=\lambda a$ with $\lambda\ne0$, the first coordinate forces $a_1=0$ and subsequent coordinates force every $a_j=0$. Thus its <point spectrum> is empty.

For $m\geq1$,
$$
T^me_j=\frac{e_{j+m}}{j(j+1)\cdots(j+m-1)},\qquad\|T^m\|=\frac1{m!},
$$
since the largest column sum is attained at $j=1$. Therefore the <spectral radius formula> gives $\nu(T)=\lim_m(m!)^{-1/m}=0$. The limit follows, for example, from $m!\geq(m/2)^{m/2}$ for large $m$. Zero is in the spectrum because the output first coordinate is always zero, so $T$ is not onto. Hence
$$
\boxed{p\sigma(T)=\varnothing,\qquad\sigma(T)=\{0\}.}
$$
This is a nonzero <quasinilpotent element>, specifically the <factorial weighted right shift>.

A complete <orthonormal sequence> $(e_n)$ has $\langle e_n,e_m\rangle=\delta_{nm}$ and dense <linear span>; equivalently, the only vector orthogonal to every $e_n$ is zero. The <Parseval identity> says $\|x\|^2=\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$, with expansion $x=\sum_n\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$ in the Hilbert <norm>.

Put $G(t,z)=(1-2zt+z^2)^{-1/2}$ and $D=1-2zt+z^2$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
G_t=zD^{-3/2},\quad G_{tt}=3z^2D^{-5/2},\quad G_z=(t-z)D^{-3/2},\quad G_{zz}=-D^{-3/2}+3(t-z)^2D^{-5/2}.
$$
Substitution yields $(1-t^2)G_{tt}-2tG_t+z^2G_{zz}+2zG_z=0$. Extracting the coefficient of $z^n$ in the <generating function> gives
$$
\boxed{(1-t^2)P_n''-2tP_n'+n(n+1)P_n=0,}
$$
the <Legendre differential equation>.

The <generating function> gives $P_n(-t)=(-1)^nP_n(t)$, so the odd function $f$ is orthogonal to every even-degree <Legendre polynomial>. For $n\geq1$, integrate the self-adjoint form of the <Legendre differential equation> from zero to one:
$$
[(1-t^2)P_n'(t)]_0^1+n(n+1)\int_0^1P_n(t)dt=0.
$$
Thus $\int_0^1P_n=P_n'(0)/(n(n+1))$. Differentiating the <generating function> in $t$ and then setting $t=0$ gives
$$
\sum_nP_n'(0)z^n=z(1+z^2)^{-3/2},\qquad P_{2m+1}'(0)=(-1)^m\frac{(2m+1)!}{2^{2m}(m!)^2}.
$$
Combining these identities with the normalization of $p_n$ yields
$$
\boxed{\langle f,p_{2m}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle f,p_{2m+1}\rangle=(-1)^m\sqrt{\frac{4m+3}{2}}\frac{(2m)!}{2^{2m}m!(m+1)!}.}
$$
For the <Legendre expansion of the sign function>, $\|f\|_2^2=2$, so the <Parseval identity> gives
$$
2=\sum_{m\geq0}\frac{4m+3}{2}\frac{((2m)!)^2}{2^{4m}(m!(m+1)!)^2}.
$$
Therefore the requested sum equals
$$
\boxed{4.}
$$

The <series> form alone already proves the required dependence.

With $\widehat f(k)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(s)e^{-iks}ds$, the classical <Fourier partial sum> is $s_N^f(t)=\sum_{|k|\leq N}\widehat f(k)e^{ikt}$. Its <Cesàro mean> is
$$
\sigma_N^f=\frac1{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^Ns_n^f=\sum_{|k|\leq N}\left(1-\frac{|k|}{N+1}\right)\widehat f(k)e^{ikt}.
$$
For the uniform <Fejér theorem>, extend the continuous endpoint-matching $f$ periodically. The given <Fejér kernel> is nonnegative, even and has total integral $\pi$. Thus
$$
\sigma_N^f(t)-f(t)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}[f(t-u)-f(t)]F_N(u)du.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, uniform <continuity> supplies $\delta>0$ so the bracket has modulus less than $\varepsilon$ for $|u|<\delta$, uniformly in $t$. That part contributes at most $\varepsilon$. On the remainder, $F_N(u)\leq[2(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)]^{-1}$, and the bracket is at most $2\|f\|_\infty$. Hence
$$
\|\sigma_N^f-f\|_\infty\leq\varepsilon+\frac{2\|f\|_\infty}{(N+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)}\longrightarrow\varepsilon.
$$
Letting $\varepsilon$ decrease to zero proves **<uniform convergence> of the <Fejér sums>**.

The later assertions attached to the printed factorial-frequency example are inconsistent. The following calculations resolve them while keeping the Fourier definition just given. Put $a_j=e^{i\sqrt{j+1}}-e^{i\sqrt j}$ and consider its formal trigonometric <series>. For $t=\pi p/q$, there is $J_0$ such that $\cos(j!t)=1$ for every $j\geq J_0$, because $2q$ divides $j!$. At $N_J=(J+1)!-1$, its ordinary partial sum therefore telescopes to
$$
s_{N_J}(t)=C(t)+e^{i\sqrt{J+1}}.
$$
The exponential has subsequences tending to $1$ and $-1$: take $J+1$ to be integers nearest $(2\pi m)^2$ and $((2m+1)\pi)^2$. Thus **the ordinary sums diverge at every $t\in\pi\mathbb Q$**.

But their genuine Fourier <Cesàro means> also diverge there. Indeed,
$$
\sigma_{N_J}(t)-s_{N_J}(t)=-\frac1{(J+1)!}\sum_{j=0}^Jj!a_j\cos(j!t).
$$
Since $|a_j|\leq2$ and $\sum_{j=0}^Jj!\leq2J!$ for $J\geq3$, its absolute value is at most $4/(J+1)\to0$, uniformly in $t$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{N_J}(t)=C(t)+e^{i\sqrt{J+1}}+o(1),}
$$
which has the same incompatible limit points. This is an instance of <Cesàro sums at factorial cutoffs track ordinary sums>. **The printed Cesàro-convergence claim is false.** Averaging the sparse <series> by its index $j$ would be a different operation from Fourier Cesàro summation by frequency.

There is a further issue: **no $L^1$ function has exactly this <Fourier series>**. Here is a direct proof using <strongly lacunary polynomial L1-L2 bound>. For a <trigonometric polynomial> $p$ supported on $\{\pm j!:j\geq3\}$, consecutive positive frequencies have ratio at least four. Every zero sum of four signed frequencies pairs into cancelling equal frequencies: a largest unpaired frequency would exceed the sum of the other three. Expanding the fourth moment with normalized measure $dt/(2\pi)$ and bounding the three possible pairings gives
$$
\|p\|_4^4\leq3\|p\|_2^4.
$$
The <Hölder inequality> gives $\|p\|_2\leq\|p\|_1^{1/3}\|p\|_4^{2/3}$, hence $\|p\|_2\leq\sqrt3\|p\|_1$.

If the asserted $f\in L^1$ existed, positivity and unit mass of the summation kernel would bound $\|\sigma_N^f\|_1\leq\|f\|_1$. Subtract the finitely many modes from $j<3$; their norms stay uniformly bounded, and the remainder is a <polynomial> of the type above. Its $L^2$ norms would therefore be uniformly bounded. <Orthogonality> then forces $\sum_{j\geq3}|a_j|^2<\infty$, by fixing any finite coefficient block and letting $N\to\infty$. However,
$$
|a_j|^2=4\sin^2\left(\frac{\sqrt{j+1}-\sqrt j}{2}\right)\sim\frac1{4j},
$$
so that sum diverges. This contradiction disproves the stated existence premise.

Even considered only formally, these coefficients rule out an $L^2$ function by <Bessel's inequality>, and hence rule out a bounded function on the finite interval. Thus a bounded continuous representative cannot satisfy the asserted <series> either. **The asserted $L^1$ function does not exist.**

The <Hilbert projection theorem> states that every vector $x$ has a unique decomposition $x=m+n$ relative to a closed <linear subspace> $M$, with $m\in M$ and $n\in M^\perp$. The vector $m$ is the unique nearest point in $M$.

The <Riesz representation theorem> states that every bounded complex-<linear functional> $F$ on a <Hilbert space> has a unique representation $F(x)=\langle x,y\rangle$, with $\|F\|=\|y\|$, using the convention of linearity in the first argument. If $F=0$, use $y=0$. Otherwise $M=\ker F$ is closed. Project a vector outside $M$ to obtain $z\in M^\perp$ with $F(z)\ne0$, and rescale it so $F(z)=1$. Then $x-F(x)z\in M$, so
$$
\langle x,z\rangle=F(x)\|z\|^2,\qquad y=z/\|z\|^2.
$$
This gives the representation. Uniqueness follows because a vector orthogonal to every $x$ is zero. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $\|F\|\leq\|y\|$, and evaluation at $y/\|y\|$ gives equality when $y\ne0$.

For the adjoint, fix $v$. The functional $u\mapsto\langle Tu,v\rangle$ is bounded with <norm> at most $\|T\|\|v\|$. By the <Riesz representation theorem>, there is a unique vector $T^*v$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{\langle u,T^*v\rangle=\langle Tu,v\rangle\quad(u\in V).}
$$
For scalars $a,b$, conjugate-linearity in the second argument shows that $T^*(av+bw)=aT^*v+bT^*w$, by uniqueness of the representing vector. The same <norm> estimate gives $\|T^*v\|\leq\|T\|\|v\|$. Thus $T^*$ is a unique <bounded linear operator>, the <adjoint operator>.

For the <adjoint of an integration operator anchored at zero>, use the <inner product> $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f\bar g$. On the positive half-interval, reversing the triangular integration order gives
$$
\int_0^\pi\left(\int_0^tf(s)ds\right)\overline{g(t)}dt=\int_0^\pi f(s)\overline{\left(\int_s^\pi g(t)dt\right)}ds.
$$
On the negative half-interval the corresponding calculation gives
$$
\int_{-\pi}^0\left(-\int_t^0f(s)ds\right)\overline{g(t)}dt=\int_{-\pi}^0f(s)\overline{\left(-\int_{-\pi}^sg(t)dt\right)}ds.
$$
These exchanges are justified by the <Fubini theorem>, since on a finite interval $L^2$ functions belong to $L^1$ and the absolute double integral is at most $\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. Therefore
$$
(T^*g)(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_s^\pi g(t)dt,&s>0,\\\displaystyle-\int_{-\pi}^sg(t)dt,&s<0.\end{cases}
$$
Adding $Tg$ gives $\int_0^\pi g$ on the positive half and $-\int_{-\pi}^0g$ on the negative half, exactly the prescribed $Ug$. Values at zero do not matter in $L^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{T+T^*=U.}
$$

Since $f(0)=0$, the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives $f=Tf'$, with the same oriented integral on the negative half-interval. Set $h(t)=t^2$. The adjoint calculation gives the real-valued function
$$
\boxed{g(t)=(T^*h)(t)=\begin{cases}(\pi^3-t^3)/3,&0<t\leq\pi,\\-(\pi^3+t^3)/3,&-\pi<t<0.\end{cases}}
$$
Choose any value at zero. This function is bounded and hence belongs to $L^2(-\pi,\pi]$. Since it is real, the <adjoint operator> identity yields
$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}t^2f(t)dt=\langle Tf',h\rangle=\langle f',T^*h\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(t)f'(t)dt.
$$
One can also check this by <integration by parts> separately on each half-interval: $g'=-t^2$, $g(\pm\pi)=0$, and the two possible jump terms at zero vanish because $f(0)=0$.
