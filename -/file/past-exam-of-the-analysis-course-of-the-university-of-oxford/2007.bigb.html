= Solution
{parent=1}

A <norm> on a <complex vector space> is a map $\|\cdot\|:X\to[0,\infty)$ that vanishes only at zero, satisfies $\|\lambda x\|=|\lambda|\|x\|$, and obeys the <triangle inequality>. The space is Banach when every <Cauchy sequence> in the resulting <metric> converges in $X$.

If $X$ is Banach and $\sum_j\|x_j\|<\infty$, the estimate $\|\sum_{j=m}^nx_j\|\leq\sum_{j=m}^n\|x_j\|$ shows that its <partial sums> are Cauchy, hence converge. Conversely let every such <series> converge and take a <Cauchy sequence> $(y_n)$. Choose a subsequence with $\|y_{n_{k+1}}-y_{n_k}\|<2^{-k}$. The <series> consisting of $y_{n_1}$ followed by these successive differences is absolutely summable in <norm>, so converges by hypothesis; its <partial sums> are exactly this subsequence. A <Cauchy sequence> with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit, proving **the <absolute-series criterion for completeness>**.

On the <absolutely summable sequence space> $\ell^1$, coordinatewise addition and scalar multiplication preserve membership, and summing the scalar <triangle inequality> verifies its <norm> axioms. To prove <completeness> using the criterion, let $x^{(k)}=(x_j^{(k)})\in\ell^1$ with $\sum_k\|x^{(k)}\|_1<\infty$. For each $j$ the sum $x_j=\sum_kx_j^{(k)}$ converges absolutely, and the permitted interchange of nonnegative double sums gives
$$
\sum_j|x_j|\leq\sum_j\sum_k|x_j^{(k)}|=\sum_k\|x^{(k)}\|_1<\infty.
$$
Thus $x\in\ell^1$. Moreover
$$
\left\|x-\sum_{k=1}^Nx^{(k)}\right\|_1\leq\sum_{k>N}\|x^{(k)}\|_1\to0.
$$
Every absolutely <norm>-summable <series> therefore converges in $\ell^1$, proving **$\ell^1$ is a <Banach space>**.

The <continuous dual space> $X'$ consists of all bounded complex-linear <functionals> $f:X\to\mathbb C$, with pointwise vector operations and <norm> $\|f\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|f(x)|$. This is finite by boundedness. The scalar <norm> identity and <triangle inequality> follow under the supremum; if the <norm> is zero, scaling every nonzero vector into the unit ball shows that $f$ vanishes. Hence $X'$ is a normed <complex vector space>.

For the <dual of the convergent sequence space>, take $\beta\in\ell^1$. The displayed formula is a <bounded linear functional>, since the <series> is absolutely convergent and
$$
|\phi(\beta)(\alpha)|\leq\left(|\beta_1|+\sum_{j\geq1}|\beta_{j+1}|\right)\|\alpha\|_\infty.
$$
It is linear in $\beta$, so $\|\phi(\beta)\|\leq\|\beta\|_1$. For equality fix $N$, choose $\alpha_j$ for $j\leq N$ of modulus at most one so that $\alpha_j\beta_{j+1}=|\beta_{j+1}|$, and make all later terms equal a constant $c$ of modulus at most one with $c\beta_1=|\beta_1|$. The choices for a zero coefficient can be arbitrary. This sequence lies in the unit ball of $c$ and converges to $c$. The <reverse triangle inequality> gives
$$
|\phi(\beta)(\alpha)|\geq|\beta_1|+\sum_{j=1}^N|\beta_{j+1}|-\sum_{j>N}|\beta_{j+1}|.
$$
Letting $N\to\infty$ proves **$\|\phi(\beta)\|=\|\beta\|_1$**, including injectivity.

For surjectivity let $F\in c'$. By the permitted description of $c_0'$, there is $\gamma\in\ell^1$ with $F(z)=\sum_j\gamma_jz_j$ on $c_0$. Every $\alpha\in c$ decomposes as $\alpha-\alpha_\infty\mathbf1\in c_0$ plus $\alpha_\infty\mathbf1$. Consequently
$$
F(\alpha)=\sum_j\gamma_j\alpha_j+\alpha_\infty\left(F(\mathbf1)-\sum_j\gamma_j\right).
$$
Take $\beta_{j+1}=\gamma_j$ and $\beta_1=F(\mathbf1)-\sum_j\gamma_j$. This is an $\ell^1$ sequence and $F=\phi(\beta)$, proving **the stated isometric linear isomorphism onto $c'$**. No complex conjugation is inserted, since these are complex-linear dual <functionals>.

The <complex Hahn-Banach theorem> extends every bounded complex-<linear functional> on a subspace to the whole normed space with exactly the same <norm>. Let $d=\operatorname{dist}(x_0,M)>0$ and define on $M+\mathbb Cx_0$
$$
g_0(y+\lambda x_0)=\lambda d.
$$
The representation is unique. The distance inequality $\|y+\lambda x_0\|\geq|\lambda|d$ gives $\|g_0\|\leq1$. Taking $y=-m$ with $\|x_0-m\|\downarrow d$ proves the reverse <norm> bound. Extend by the <Hahn-Banach theorem> to $g\in X'$; then **$g|_M=0$, $\|g\|=1$, and $g(x_0)=d\ne0$**. The labels (a)-(c) specify these simultaneous properties of this one functional.

Let $Y=\overline{\operatorname{span}F}$. Every functional vanishing on $F$ vanishes on $Y$ by linearity and continuity, so $Y\subseteq(F^\circ)_\circ$. If $x\notin Y$, the separation just proved gives a bounded functional zero on $Y$ and nonzero at $x$. It belongs to $F^\circ$ and excludes $x$ from its preannihilator. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(F^\circ)_\circ=\overline{\operatorname{span}F}.}
$$
For the dual-side counterexample take $X=\ell^1$, $X'=\ell^\infty$, and $G=c_0$. It is <norm> closed, since a uniform limit of sequences tending to zero still tends to zero. Every coordinate <unit vector> belongs to $G$, so the only $x\in\ell^1$ annihilated by every element of $G$ has all coordinates zero. Thus $G_\circ=\{0\}$ and
$$
\boxed{(G_\circ)^\circ=\ell^\infty\ne c_0=G.}
$$
The dual-side double annihilator corresponds to weak-star <closure>, which need not equal <norm> <closure>.

The <spectrum of a bounded operator> consists of $\lambda$ for which $T-\lambda I$ has no bounded two-sided inverse. The <point spectrum> consists of <eigenvalues>, so $\ker(T-\lambda I)\ne0$. The <approximate point spectrum> consists of $\lambda$ admitting <unit vectors> $x_n$ with $\|(T-\lambda I)x_n\|\to0$; equivalently $T-\lambda I$ is not bounded below.

If $T-\lambda I$ is invertible, its inverse bounds it below and its <Banach-space adjoint> is invertible. Thus neither alternative on the right of the claimed union occurs. Conversely suppose $\lambda$ belongs to neither term and write $S=T-\lambda I$. There is $c>0$ with $\|Sx\|\geq c\|x\|$, so $S$ is injective and has closed range: convergence of $Sx_n$ makes $x_n$ Cauchy, and <completeness> supplies a preimage of its limit. Also $\ker S'=0$. The allowed annihilator identity makes the range dense, hence equal to $X$. Its inverse has <norm> at most $1/c$. This proves the <spectrum decomposition through the Banach-space adjoint>:
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\operatorname{ap}\sigma(T)\cup\operatorname p\sigma(T').}
$$
For the right shift on $\ell^1$, $\|T\|=1$, so the allowed <norm> bound puts its spectrum in the closed unit disc. Under the linear pairing with $\ell^\infty$, its <Banach-space adjoint> is the left shift $T'(b_1,b_2,\ldots)=(b_2,b_3,\ldots)$. For each $|\lambda|\leq1$ the bounded nonzero sequence $(1,\lambda,\lambda^2,\ldots)$ is an <eigenvector> of $T'$ with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$. The union identity gives the reverse inclusion. Hence **$\sigma(T)=\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq1\}$**. This <Banach-space adjoint> uses the complex-linear dual, so its <eigenvalue> is $\lambda$ itself.

Put $Q(u)=\|u\|^2$ and $B(u,w)=\tfrac14[Q(u+w)-Q(u-w)]$. Then $B(0,w)=0$ and the <parallelogram identity>, applied to $u+w,v$ and to $u-w,v$ and subtracted, gives
$$
B(u+v,w)+B(u-v,w)=2B(u,w).
$$
Taking $v=u$ shows $B(2u,w)=2B(u,w)$. Replacing $u,v$ in the identity by $(u+v)/2,(u-v)/2$ now gives
$$
B(u,w)+B(v,w)=2B((u+v)/2,w)=B(u+v,w).
$$
The proposed complex pairing is $P(u,w)=B(u,w)+iB(u,iw)$. Applying these same identities with $w$ and $iw$ proves **both asserted midpoint and additivity equalities for $P$**.

= Solution

Repeated additivity gives $P(mu,v)=mP(u,v)$ for every positive integer $m$. Since $u$ is the sum of $n$ copies of $u/n$, $P(u/n,v)=P(u,v)/n$. Combining these proves
$$
\boxed{P((m/n)u,v)=(m/n)P(u,v).}
$$
The case $m=0$ also holds by additivity at zero.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Additivity applied to $u+(-u)=0$ gives $P(-u,v)=-P(u,v)$. Multiplication by $i$ preserves the <norm>, so
$$
B(iu,v)=-B(u,iv),\qquad B(iu,iv)=B(u,v).
$$
For example the first follows by multiplying $iu\pm v$ by $-i$ inside each <norm>. Consequently
$$
P(iu,v)=-B(u,iv)+iB(u,v)=iP(u,v).
$$
This proves **the two required identities**, with the convention of linearity in the first argument.

= Solution

For a fixed second argument the <norm> formula makes $P$ continuous in its first argument. Rational homogeneity therefore extends to real homogeneity by rational approximation; additivity and $P(iu,v)=iP(u,v)$ then give full complex linearity in that argument.

The real pairing $B$ is symmetric, and $B(v,iu)=B(iu,v)=-B(u,iv)$. Hence $P(v,u)=\overline{P(u,v)}$, giving conjugate linearity in the second argument. Finally $B(u,u)=Q(u)$ and $B(u,iu)=0$, because $\|(1+i)u\|=\|(1-i)u\|$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{P(u,u)=\|u\|^2,}
$$
which is positive for $u\ne0$. This proves **$P$ is a complex <inner product> inducing exactly the original <norm>**. The assumed Banach <completeness> makes the resulting <inner product> space Hilbert. This is the <Jordan-von Neumann theorem>.

Differentiating the Rodrigues identity gives $H_0=1$ and $H_{n+1}=2tH_n-H_n'$. Induct with the proposed finite sum. The coefficient at $t^{n+1-2j}$ in this recurrence is the contribution from $2tH_n$ minus the derivative contribution with previous index $j-1$:
$$
\frac{n!(-1)^j2^{n+1-2j}}{j!(n-2j)!}-\frac{n!(-1)^{j-1}2^{n-2j+2}}{(j-1)!(n-2j+1)!}.
$$
Using a common denominator, this equals $(n+1)!(-1)^j2^{n+1-2j}/[j!(n+1-2j)!]$. At an endpoint omit terms whose factorial indices would be negative; the same formula then covers the sole remaining term. Thus
$$
\boxed{H_n(t)=n!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^j2^{n-2j}}{j!(n-2j)!}t^{n-2j}.}
$$
The initial value and recurrence identify these polynomials with the given Rodrigues definition.

Complete the square to obtain, initially for real $y,z$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2}G(t,y)G(t,z)dt=e^{-y^2-z^2}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2+2t(y+z)}dt=\sqrt\pi e^{2yz}.
$$
Compare coefficients of $y^mz^n$ on the two sides. The left coefficient is $(m!n!)^{-1}\int e^{-t^2}H_m(t)H_n(t)dt$, and the right is zero unless $m=n$, in which case it is $\sqrt\pi2^n/n!$. The polynomials are real on the real line, so these are the complex $L^2$ <inner products> of the $\phi_n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle\phi_m,\phi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\sqrt\pi\,2^nn!,\qquad\|\phi_n\|_2=\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{2^nn!}.}
$$
Polynomial times Gaussian decay also directly shows each $\phi_n$ lies in $L^2$.

The classical <Fourier partial sums> are
$$
s_n^f(t)=\frac{a_0}2+\sum_{j=1}^n[a_j\cos jt+b_j\sin jt],\quad a_j=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\cos jt\,dt,\quad b_j=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)\sin jt\,dt.
$$
Here $a_0=\pi^{-1}\int f$, and functions are extended periodically. One version of the <Dini test for Fourier series> states that if one-sided limits $L_+,L_-$ at $t$ exist and
$$
\int_0^\delta\frac{|f(t+s)+f(t-s)-L_+-L_-|}{s}\,ds<\infty,
$$
then $s_n^f(t)\to(L_++L_-)/2$. A <step function> on a finite interval is constant on each side of any given point for sufficiently small distance, so its displayed numerator is zero there. <Dini test for Fourier series> gives exactly the asserted midpoint of the two one-sided values, including at jumps.

Let $P_n=s_n$ on $L^2$. It is the <orthogonal projection> onto the span of the displayed trigonometric functions through frequency $n$, hence $\|P_n\|\leq1$. For a step-function approximation $\psi$,
$$
\|P_nf-f\|_2\leq\|P_n(f-\psi)\|_2+\|P_n\psi-\psi\|_2+\|\psi-f\|_2\leq2\|f-\psi\|_2+\|P_n\psi-\psi\|_2.
$$
The <convergence on a dense set under a uniform operator bound>, here applied using the allowed convergence for $\psi$ and density of <step functions>, proves **$P_nf\to f$ in $L^2$**. Consequently the union of these finite trigonometric spans is dense, which is precisely <completeness> of the <orthonormal system>. It also proves Parseval's equality by passing to the limit in the Pythagorean projection identity.

For $f(t)=t^2$, oddness gives $b_j=0$, direct integration gives $a_0=2\pi^2/3$, and two integrations by parts give $a_j=4(-1)^j/j^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{t^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{(-1)^j\cos jt}{j^2}.}
$$
<Dini test for Fourier series> justifies this pointwise on the interval, or absolute coefficient summability gives its continuous extension after the $L^2$ identification. At zero it yields **$\sum_{j\geq1}(-1)^{j-1}/j^2=\pi^2/12$**. Parseval gives
$$
\frac{2\pi^5}5=\int_{-\pi}^\pi t^4dt=\frac\pi2\left(\frac{2\pi^2}3\right)^2+16\pi\sum_{j\geq1}\frac1{j^4}.
$$
Therefore **$\sum_{j\geq1}j^{-4}=\pi^4/90$**.

= Solution

<Anti-self-adjoint> means $T^*=-T$. Unitary means $T^*T=TT^*=I$, so $T^{-1}=T^*$. For a <unitary operator> $\|Tu\|^2=\langle u,T^*Tu\rangle=\|u\|^2$, and the space is nonzero. Thus **$\|T\|=1$**.

The <series> for the exponential converges absolutely in the <Banach algebra> $B(V)$, since $\sum_n\|T^n/n!\|\leq e^{\|T\|}$. The adjoint is <norm> continuous and conjugate linear, and its action on these real scalar coefficients gives $(e^T)^*=e^{T^*}$. The absolutely convergent Cauchy product gives
$$
e^Te^{-T}=e^{-T}e^T=I,
$$
because its degree-$n$ coefficient for $n>0$ is $T^n\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j/[j!(n-j)!]=0$. If $T^*=-T$, these identities prove **$e^T$ is unitary**.

If $T$ is invertible, zero is outside the spectrum of $T^{-1}$. For $\mu\ne0$ the factorization
$$
T^{-1}-\mu I=-\mu T^{-1}(T-\mu^{-1}I)
$$
shows that the left side is invertible exactly when the final factor is. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T^{-1})=\{\lambda^{-1}:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}.}
$$
For unitary $T$, both $T$ and $T^{-1}=T^*$ have <norm> one. The <norm> bound on each spectrum gives $|\lambda|\leq1$ and $|\lambda^{-1}|\leq1$, forcing **$\sigma(T)$ to lie on the unit circle**.

The converse fails: on $\mathbb C^2$ with its usual <inner product> take
$$
\boxed{T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its spectrum is $\{1\}$, but $T^*T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\ne I$, so it is not unitary.
