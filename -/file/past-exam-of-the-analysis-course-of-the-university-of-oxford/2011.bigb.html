A <normed vector space> over $\mathbb R$ is a real <vector space> $X$ with a function $\|\cdot\|:X\to[0,\infty)$ satisfying
$$
\|x\|=0\iff x=0,\qquad\|ax\|=|a|\|x\|,
\qquad\|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|.
$$
Its <distance> is $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$. It is a <Banach space> if this <metric> is complete: **every <Cauchy sequence> converges to an element of $X$**.

= a
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <series> $\sum_{n\geq1}x_n$ converges in $X$ when its <partial sums> $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nx_n$ converge in <norm> to an element of $X$. It is absolutely convergent when $\sum_n\|x_n\|<\infty$.

If $X$ is complete, then
$$
\|s_M-s_N\|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^M\|x_n\|\longrightarrow0
$$
for an absolutely convergent <series>. Its <partial sums> are Cauchy and therefore converge.

Conversely suppose every absolutely convergent <series> converges. Given a <Cauchy sequence> $(z_n)$, choose a subsequence $(z_{n_k})$ with $\|z_{n_{k+1}}-z_{n_k}\|\leq2^{-k}$. The <series>
$$
z_{n_1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(z_{n_{k+1}}-z_{n_k})
$$
is absolutely convergent. Its <partial sums> are precisely the subsequence, which therefore converges to some $z\in X$. A <Cauchy sequence> with a convergent subsequence converges to that same limit: estimate $\|z_n-z\|$ by $\|z_n-z_{n_k}\|+\|z_{n_k}-z\|$. Hence
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is Banach}\iff\text{every absolutely convergent series in }X\text{ converges}.}
$$

Show that $c$ is a closed <linear subspace> of $\ell^\infty$. Suppose $a^{(j)}\in c$ and $\|a^{(j)}-a\|_\infty\to0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $j$ with uniform error below $\varepsilon/3$. Since $a^{(j)}$ converges, choose $N$ such that $|a_m^{(j)}-a_n^{(j)}|<\varepsilon/3$ for $m,n\geq N$. Then
$$
|a_m-a_n|\leq|a_m-a_m^{(j)}|+|a_m^{(j)}-a_n^{(j)}|+|a_n^{(j)}-a_n|<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus $(a_n)$ is a real <Cauchy sequence> and converges, so $a\in c$. A closed <linear subspace> of a <Banach space> is complete in the inherited <norm>. Therefore $\boxed{(c,\|\cdot\|_\infty)\text{ is Banach}}$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $e_j$ be the <coordinate vectors> and define finitely supported vectors
$$
v_k=2^{-k}\sum_{j=2^k}^{2^{k+1}-1}e_j,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots.
$$
Each $v_k$ belongs to $\ell^1$, and $\|v_k\|_\infty=2^{-k}$. Hence $\sum_kv_k$ is absolutely convergent for the <supremum norm>. Its <partial sums> converge uniformly in $\ell^\infty$ to the sequence $v$ whose entries on the $k$th block all equal $2^{-k}$. But every block contributes one to its $\ell^1$ sum:
$$
\sum_j|v_j|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^k2^{-k}=\infty.
$$
Thus $v\notin\ell^1$. Any supremum-<norm> limit in $\ell^1$ would have the same coordinates as $v$, an impossibility. The <partial sums> are Cauchy and have no limit in the space. Therefore **$(\ell^1,\|\cdot\|_\infty)$ is not Banach**, and the displayed <series> is the required absolutely convergent but nonconvergent <series> in that space.

Write $q(x)=\sup_k|\sum_{n=1}^kx_n|$. This is indeed a <norm>: the <triangle inequality> and homogeneity follow from the scalar absolute value, and vanishing of every <partial sum> forces every coordinate to vanish. It is finite on $\ell^1$ because $q(x)\leq\|x\|_1$.

Let $a_n=(-1)^{n+1}/n$ and let $a^{(N)}$ be its first $N$ entries followed by zeros. The alternating-<series> bound gives, for $M>N$,
$$
q(a^{(M)}-a^{(N)})\leq\frac1{N+1}.
$$
Thus these truncations are Cauchy for $q$. But $|x_n|\leq2q(x)$ for every coordinate, since $x_n$ is the difference of two consecutive <partial sums>. Any $q$-limit would consequently have coordinates $a_n$. This sequence is not in $\ell^1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(\ell^1,q)\text{ is not a Banach space}.}
$$

= Solution

The <continuous dual space> $X'$ is the <vector space> of bounded scalar-valued <linear functionals>, with <operator norm>
$$
\boxed{\|f\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|f(x)|.}
$$
This makes it a normed space, and in fact a <Banach space> whether or not $X$ is complete.

The <norm>-preserving <Hahn-Banach theorem> states that a <bounded linear functional> $f_0$ on any <linear subspace> $M\subseteq X$ extends to a <bounded linear functional> $f$ on all of $X$ with $f|_M=f_0$ and $\|f\|=\|f_0\|$. The subspace need not be closed. This holds over both the real and complex scalar fields, using the complex version when necessary.

= Solution

Put $d=\operatorname{dist}(x,Z)$. If $d=0$, the zero functional works. If $d>0$, then $x\notin\overline Z$, and on $M=Z+\operatorname{span}\{x\}$ define
$$
f_0(z+ax)=ad.
$$
The representation is unique, so this is linear. For $a\ne0$,
$$
|f_0(z+ax)|=|a|d=\operatorname{dist}(ax,Z)\leq\|z+ax\|,
$$
and the bound also holds for $a=0$. Thus $\|f_0\|\leq1$. The <Hahn-Banach theorem> extends it to $f\in X'$ with the same bound, proving
$$
\boxed{f|_Z=0,\qquad f(x)=d,\qquad\|f\|\leq1.}
$$
No closedness assumption on $Z$ was required; <distance> is unchanged on taking its <closure>.

= Solution

For the centered ball, the <Hahn-Banach theorem> supplies a <norm>-one real functional with $f(y)=\|y\|>r$. Hence $f(z)\leq r$ on the ball, and any $t\in(r,\|y\|)$ separates it strictly from $y$.

For the general center, apply the same construction to $y-x$: choose $\|f\|=1$ and $f(y-x)=\|y-x\|$. Then
$$
f(z)\leq f(x)+r\quad(z\in B),\qquad
f(y)=f(x)+\|y-x\|>f(x)+r.
$$
For example,
$$
\boxed{t=f(x)+\frac{r+\|y-x\|}{2}}
$$
gives $f(z)<t<f(y)$ for every $z\in B$. The strict gap uses exactly the assumption that $y$ lies outside the closed ball.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $Z=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{x_n:n\geq1\}$. If $Z\ne X$, part (a) provides a nonzero bounded functional annihilating $Z$; normalize it to have <norm> one. Density of the sequence in the dual unit ball gives an index $n$ with $\|f_n-f\|<1/4$. Since $f(x_n)=0$ and $\|x_n\|\leq1$,
$$
|f_n(x_n)|\leq\|f_n-f\|<\frac14.
$$
But the hypothesis and reverse <triangle inequality> give
$$
|f_n(x_n)|\geq\frac12\|f_n\|>\frac12\left(1-\frac14\right)=\frac38,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore **the span is dense**.

If $X'$ is <separable>, its unit ball has a countable <dense set>. For each nonzero $f_n$, the definition of its <norm> permits choosing $x_n$ in the unit ball with $|f_n(x_n)|\geq\|f_n\|/2$; choose zero for a zero functional. The preceding result gives a dense countable span over $\mathbb Q$, or over $\mathbb Q+i\mathbb Q$ in the complex case. Thus **$X$ is <separable>**.

The converse fails: $\ell^1$ is <separable>, since finite rational-coordinate sequences are dense, but $(\ell^1)'\cong\ell^\infty$ is not <separable>. The dual identification sends a bounded sequence $a$ to $x\mapsto\sum_na_nx_n$, isometrically; conversely a bounded functional is determined by its values on the <coordinate vectors> and hence has this form by truncation. The uncountable family of zero-one sequences in $\ell^\infty$ has pairwise <distance> one, which no countable <dense set> can approximate within a radius smaller than $1/2$.

For a <bounded linear operator> on a complex <Banach space>, its <spectrum> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:\lambda I-T
\text{ has no bounded two-sided inverse}\}.}
$$
The complement is the <resolvent set>. For a real space, one may use its complexification when speaking of the complex <spectrum>; the algebraic arguments below apply over the underlying scalar field as well.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The bounded operators on a <Banach space> form a <Banach algebra> in the <operator norm>. If $\|T\|<1$, the <Neumann series> $A=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T^k$ converges absolutely in that <norm>. Its <partial sums> satisfy
$$
(I-T)\sum_{k=0}^NT^k=I-T^{N+1}
=\left(\sum_{k=0}^NT^k\right)(I-T).
$$
Taking limits proves $\boxed{(I-T)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq0}T^k}$.

If $|\lambda|>\|T\|$, factor $\lambda I-T=\lambda(I-T/\lambda)$ and apply the same argument. Thus such $\lambda$ are in the <resolvent set> and
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)\subseteq\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq\|T\|\}.}
$$

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If $T$ has a bounded inverse, it is onto and $\|x\|\leq\|T^{-1}\|\,\|Tx\|$, giving the claimed lower bound. Conversely a positive lower bound makes $T$ injective. Together with surjectivity it gives an everywhere-defined linear inverse with <norm> at most $1/\varepsilon$. Hence it is bounded, proving the equivalence.

If $R,S$ are individually invertible, their product has inverse $S^{-1}R^{-1}$. Conversely assume $RS=SR$ and put $A=RS$, with $A$ invertible. Both factors commute with $A$ and therefore with $A^{-1}$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
R(SA^{-1})=I=(SA^{-1})R,qquad
S(RA^{-1})=I=(RA^{-1})S.
$$
These are bounded two-sided inverses, so **an invertible commuting product has both factors invertible**. The commutation assumption is used when verifying the second side of each inverse.

The factorization
$$
T^n-\lambda^nI=(T-\lambda I)
\left(T^{n-1}+\lambda T^{n-2}+\cdots+\lambda^{n-1}I\right)
$$
has commuting factors because both are polynomials in $T$. If $\lambda^n\notin\sigma(T^n)$, their product is invertible. The preceding part would then make $T-\lambda I$ invertible, contradicting $\lambda\in\sigma(T)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda\in\sigma(T)\Longrightarrow\lambda^n\in\sigma(T^n).}
$$
This proves the required inclusion without assuming the full <spectral mapping theorem>.

= Solution

Linearity and $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq2\pi\|f\|_\infty$ show that the <integral> defines a <bounded linear operator>. Inductively,
$$
\boxed{|T^kf(t)|\leq\frac{t^k}{k!}\|f\|_\infty\qquad(0\leq t\leq2\pi).}
$$
For $k=0$ this is immediate. If it holds for $k$, then $|\sin s|\leq1$ gives $|T^{k+1}f(t)|\leq\int_0^t s^k\|f\|_\infty/k!\,ds$, which proves the next case. Thus $\|T^k\|\leq(2\pi)^k/k!$.

For every $\lambda\ne0$, the <series> $A_\lambda=\sum_{k\geq0}\lambda^{-k-1}T^k$ converges in <operator norm>, dominated by $|\lambda|^{-1}e^{2\pi/|\lambda|}$. Multiplying its <partial sums> by $\lambda I-T$ on either side gives the identity minus a remainder $\lambda^{-N-1}T^{N+1}$, which tends to zero by the factorial estimate. Therefore $A_\lambda$ is the bounded inverse of $\lambda I-T$.

Finally every function in the <image> of $T$ vanishes at zero, so $T$ is not onto the <continuous functions>. Hence $0$ belongs to its <spectrum> and
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)=\{0\}.}
$$

The graph $G(T)=\{(x,Tx):x\in X\}$ is closed if it is a closed subset of $X\times Y$. Equivalently, $x_n\to x$ and $Tx_n\to y$ imply $Tx=y$.

The <closed graph theorem> states that **an everywhere-defined <linear map> between <Banach spaces> is bounded if and only if its graph is closed**. Completeness of both spaces is part of the theorem. A bounded map has a closed graph by continuity.

Conversely, if the graph is closed, it is a <Banach space> in the product <norm> $\|(x,y)\|=\|x\|+\|y\|$. The coordinate projection $P:G(T)\to X$, $P(x,Tx)=x$, is a bounded linear bijection. The <open mapping theorem> makes $P^{-1}$ bounded. The other coordinate projection $Q:G(T)\to Y$ is bounded, and $T=QP^{-1}$ is therefore bounded. This proves the theorem from the permitted result.

For scalars $a,b$ and $f,h\in X$, linearity of the <Fourier coefficients> gives
$$
\widehat{aTf+bTh}(n)=\alpha_n\bigl(a\widehat f(n)+b\widehat h(n)\bigr)

For each fixed $n$, the coefficient functional is bounded since
$$
|\widehat f(n)|\leq\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(t)|\,dt
\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\|f\|_\infty.
$$
Suppose $f_j\to f$ and $Tf_j\to g$ uniformly. Taking coefficients and passing to the limit gives
$$
\widehat g(n)=\lim_j\widehat{Tf_j}(n)
=\alpha_n\lim_j\widehat f_j(n)=\alpha_n\widehat f(n)
$$
for every fixed $n$. No uniform bound on $\alpha_n$ is needed for this passage. Uniqueness gives $g=Tf$, so $T$ has a closed graph. The space $X$ is Banach, being the closed subspace defined by $f(-\pi)=f(\pi)$ inside the <continuous functions> with the uniform <norm>. The <closed graph theorem> therefore proves **$T$ is bounded**.

The <orthogonal complement> is
$$
\boxed{Y^\perp=\{w\in X:\langle w,y\rangle=0\text{ for every }y\in Y\}.}
$$
It is a <linear subspace> and is closed, since it is an intersection of <kernels> of the continuous <linear functionals> $w\mapsto\langle w,y\rangle$. Continuity follows from the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

The minimizing point and orthogonality proved below write every $x$ as $x=y_0+(x-y_0)\in Y+Y^\perp$. If $w\in Y\cap Y^\perp$, then $\|w\|^2=\langle w,w\rangle=0$, so the intersection is zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{X=Y\oplus Y^\perp.}
$$
In particular the minimizing point is unique: an alternative orthogonal decomposition would have a difference in this zero intersection.

Let $d=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|$ and choose $y_n\in Y$ with $\|x-y_n\|\to d$. The <parallelogram law> gives
$$
\|y_n-y_m\|^2
=2\|x-y_n\|^2+2\|x-y_m\|^2
-4\left\|x-\frac{y_n+y_m}{2}\right\|^2
\leq2\|x-y_n\|^2+2\|x-y_m\|^2-4d^2.
$$
The right side tends to zero. Thus $(y_n)$ is Cauchy. The closed subspace $Y$ of a <Hilbert space> is complete, so $y_n\to y_0\in Y$. Continuity of the <norm> gives $\|x-y_0\|=d$, proving **the <distance> is attained**.

Put $w=x-y_0$. For any $y\in Y$ and any real $t$, minimality gives
$$
\|w-ty\|^2-\|w\|^2=-2t\operatorname{Re}\langle w,y\rangle+t^2\|y\|^2\geq0.

The derivative at zero must vanish, so $\operatorname{Re}\langle w,y\rangle=0$. In a complex <Hilbert space>, apply the same argument to $iy$ to get the imaginary part zero as well. In the real case the first conclusion is already enough. Hence
$$
\boxed{x-y_0\in Y^\perp.}
$$

= Solution

For $h,g\in Y$, their product $hg$ is holomorphic and integrable on the disk by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Its angular mean on every centered circle is $h(0)g(0)$ by the <mean-value property>. Polar integration therefore gives
$$
\langle h,\overline g\rangle=\int_Dh(z)g(z)\,dA(z)
=\pi h(0)g(0).
$$
If $g(0)=0$, this vanishes for every $h\in Y$, proving $\boxed{Z\subseteq Y^\perp}$. Integrability of the product justifies integration over the full disk, even when the individual <holomorphic functions> grow near its boundary.

The inclusion is **strict**. Set $w(z)=|z|^2-1/2$, a nonzero bounded function in $L^2(D)$. For every $h\in Y$,
$$
\langle w,h\rangle
=2\pi\overline{h(0)}\int_0^1(r^2-1/2)r\,dr=0.
$$
Thus $w\in Y^\perp$. It cannot equal $\overline g$ for holomorphic $g$: equality almost everywhere between these <continuous functions> would give equality everywhere, making $g=w$ a real-valued nonconstant <holomorphic function>, which is impossible. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Z\ne Y^\perp.}
$$

Use the convention that the <inner product> is linear in its first argument. The precise <polarization identity> is
$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14\left(
\|x+y\|^2-\|x-y\|^2+i\|x+iy\|^2-i\|x-iy\|^2\right).}
$$
If (1) holds, apply this formula before and after $U$ to get preservation of all <inner products>, proving (2). If (2) holds, the identity $\langle Ux,Uy\rangle=\langle x,U^*Uy\rangle$ gives $U^*U=I$. For $z=Ux$, surjectivity then gives $UU^*z=UU^*Ux=Ux=z$, proving (3).

From (3), $\|Ux\|^2=\langle x,U^*Ux\rangle=\|x\|^2$ and likewise $\|U^*x\|=\|x\|$, proving (4). Finally, if (4) holds, apply the same <polarization identity> to $U^*$ to obtain $UU^*=I$. This makes $U$ onto; the other <norm> identity supplies the remaining requirement of (1). Thus
$$
\boxed{(1)\iff(2)\iff(3)\iff(4).}
$$
These four statements are conditions in a single equivalence proof, rather than four independent source questions.

The four claims below show that $I+T$ has a bounded inverse and that the <Cayley transform of a bounded accretive operator> is contractive. For an arbitrary $z\in X$, write $z=(I+T)x$. Then
$$
\|Sz\|=\|(I-T)x\|\leq\|(I+T)x\|=\|z\|.
$$
Taking the supremum over the unit ball gives
$$
\boxed{S=(I-T)(I+T)^{-1},\qquad\|S\|\leq1.}
$$
