= Solution
{parent=a}

For $\alpha,\beta\in\ell_{1,2}$ and real scalars $c,d$, the scalar <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\sum_{j\geq1}j^2|c\alpha_j+d\beta_j|\leq|c|\sum_jj^2|\alpha_j|+|d|\sum_jj^2|\beta_j|<\infty.
$$
The zero sequence belongs to the space, so it is a real <vector space>. The weighted sum is nonnegative and homogeneous, and the same inequality with $c=d=1$ proves its <triangle inequality>. Since every weight $j^2$ is strictly positive, zero weighted sum forces every coordinate to vanish. Therefore **$\|\cdot\|_{1,2}$ is a <norm>**.

Define $U:\ell_{1,2}\to\ell_1$ by $(U\alpha)_j=j^2\alpha_j$. This is a linear bijection, with inverse $(U^{-1}\gamma)_j=j^{-2}\gamma_j$, and
$$
\boxed{\|U\alpha\|_1=\|\alpha\|_{1,2}.}
$$
Thus $U$ is an <isometric isomorphism of normed spaces> onto the <absolutely summable sequence space>, which is a <Banach space>. Explicitly, a <Cauchy sequence> $\alpha^{(n)}$ maps to a <Cauchy sequence> $U\alpha^{(n)}\to\gamma\in\ell_1$; setting $\alpha=U^{-1}\gamma$ gives <convergence> in the weighted <norm>. Hence **$\ell_{1,2}$ is a <Banach space>**, an instance of <weighted summable sequence space isometry>.

Let $D$ be the set of <finitely supported sequences> with rational coordinates. It is countable: it is the union over $N$ of copies of $\mathbb Q^N$. Given $\alpha\in\ell_{1,2}$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\sum_{j>N}j^2|\alpha_j|<\varepsilon/2$. Choose rational numbers $q_j$ for $1\leq j\leq N$ so that $\sum_{j=1}^Nj^2|q_j-\alpha_j|<\varepsilon/2$, and put $q_j=0$ thereafter. Then $q\in D$ and $\|q-\alpha\|_{1,2}<\varepsilon$. Thus **$D$ is countable and dense, so the space is <separable>**.

For the first <linear map>, take $(\alpha,\beta)=(0,e_n)$. Its input <norm> is one, while $T_1(0,e_n)=(e_n,0)$ has <norm> $n^2$. Thus **$T_1$ is unbounded**.

For the second map, since $j^2\geq1$,
$$
\|T_2(\alpha,\beta)\|_X=\|\alpha+\beta\|_1\leq\|\alpha\|_1+\|\beta\|_1\leq\|\alpha\|_{1,2}+\|\beta\|_1=\|(\alpha,\beta)\|_X.
$$
Equality holds at $(0,e_1)$, so
$$
\boxed{T_2\text{ is bounded and }\|T_2\|=1.}
$$
Both formulas map $c_{00}\times c_{00}$ into itself because adding two <finitely supported sequences> preserves finite support.

Equip the <continuous dual space> $X'$ with the <operator norm>
$$
\|f\|=\sup_{\|x\|\leq1}|f(x)|.
$$
<Continuity> of a <linear functional> makes this finite; homogeneity, definiteness and the <triangle inequality> follow directly from the supremum formula.

For <completeness of the dual space>, let $(f_n)$ be Cauchy in this <norm>. For each $x\in X$, $|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\leq\|f_n-f_m\|\|x\|$, so $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb R$. Define $f(x)=\lim_nf_n(x)$. Passing to the limit in the linearity identities makes $f$ linear. Since a <Cauchy sequence> is bounded, $\sup_n\|f_n\|\leq C$ and $|f(x)|\leq C\|x\|$, so $f\in X'$.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\|f_n-f_m\|\leq\varepsilon$ for $n,m\geq N$. Let $m\to\infty$ <pointwise>; then $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\leq\varepsilon\|x\|$ and therefore $\|f_n-f\|\leq\varepsilon$. Thus **$X'$ is a <Banach space>**. This proof in fact works even if $X$ itself is not complete.

= Solution

If a <bilinear map> is continuous, <continuity> at $(0,0)$ gives $\delta>0$ such that $\|F(x,y)\|\leq1$ whenever $\|x\|,\|y\|<\delta$. For nonzero $x,y$, apply this to $\delta x/(2\|x\|)$ and $\delta y/(2\|y\|)$. Bilinearity gives
$$
\boxed{\|F(x,y)\|\leq\frac4{\delta^2}\|x\|\|y\|.}
$$
If either input is zero, the inequality follows from bilinearity directly.

Conversely, suppose the product bound holds with $C\geq0$. At a fixed point $(x,y)$,
$$
F(x+h,y+k)-F(x,y)=F(h,y)+F(x,k)+F(h,k).
$$
Its <norm> is at most $C(\|h\|\|y\|+\|x\|\|k\|+\|h\|\|k\|)$, which tends to zero as $(h,k)\to(0,0)$. Thus **the product bound is equivalent to joint <continuity>**. This is <boundedness criterion for a bilinear map>.

= Solution

Put $\rho=r-\sqrt{r^2-1}$. Since $r>1$, $0<\rho<1$ and $\rho^2-2r\rho+1=0$. The nonzero square-summable sequence $\beta_j=\rho^{j-1}$ defines a <continuous linear functional> on $\ell_2$ by
$$
f(\alpha)=\sum_{j\geq1}\alpha_j\rho^{j-1}.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> proves <continuity>. For every $k$,
$$
f(x_k)=\rho^{k-1}(1-2r\rho+\rho^2)=0.
$$
Hence $f$ vanishes on the <closed linear span> $M$, but $f(e_1)=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{M\ne\ell_2.}
$$
More precisely, any vector in the <orthogonal complement> of $M$ satisfies the recurrence $\beta_{k+2}-2r\beta_{k+1}+\beta_k=0$. Its roots are $r\pm\sqrt{r^2-1}$, one less than one and the other greater than one. Square summability eliminates the growing solution, so $M^\perp$ is exactly the span of $(\rho^{j-1})$. Thus $M=\ker f$ is a closed hyperplane of codimension one. This is <annihilator of a second-difference span in l2>.

The <spectrum of a bounded operator> is $\sigma(T)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:T-\lambda I\text{ has no bounded inverse}\}$. The <point spectrum> consists of those $\lambda$ for which $\ker(T-\lambda I)\ne0$, namely the <eigenvalues>. The <spectral radius> is $r(T)=\sup_{\lambda\in\sigma(T)}|\lambda|$.

If $\|T\|<1$, the <Neumann series> $S=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-T)^n$ converges in the <Banach space of bounded linear operators>, since its term norms are bounded by a convergent <geometric series>. Multiplying the finite sums on either side by $I+T$ gives $I-(-T)^{N+1}$, which tends in <operator norm> to $I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(I+T)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-T)^n.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=b}

If $|\lambda|>\|T\|$, write $T-\lambda I=-\lambda(I-T/\lambda)$. The <Neumann series> applies to $T/\lambda$, so $\lambda$ is outside the <spectrum of a bounded operator>. Thus $\sigma(T)\subseteq\{\lambda:|\lambda|\leq\|T\|\}$ and $r(T)\leq\|T\|$.

The polynomial <spectral mapping theorem> gives $\sigma(T^m)=\{\lambda^m:\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}$, and hence
$$
r(T)^m=r(T^m)\leq\|T^m\|.
$$
Taking $m$th roots and then the limit, whose existence is allowed, proves
$$
\boxed{r(T)\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\|T^m\|^{1/m}.}
$$
Only this inequality is required; the reverse inequality is the additional content of the full <spectral radius formula>.

= Solution

For the <multiplication operator>, $\|Tf\|_\infty\leq\|g\|_\infty\|f\|_\infty=\|f\|_\infty$. Taking $f=1$ gives equality, so $\|T\|=1$.

An <eigenfunction> obeys $(g(t)-\lambda)f(t)=0$ at every $t$. If $\lambda\in[0,1)$, its level set is the single point $t=\lambda$, and a <continuous function> supported at a single point is zero. If $\lambda\notin[0,1]$, that level set is empty. For $\lambda=1$, every <continuous function> vanishing on $[0,1]$ is an <eigenfunction>, and nonzero examples exist, such as $f(t)=\max\{t-1,0\}$. Thus $\sigma_p(T)=\{1\}$.

If $\lambda\notin[0,1]$, multiplication by $(g-\lambda)^{-1}$ is a bounded inverse of $T-\lambda I$. If $\lambda\in[0,1]$, choose $t_0$ with $g(t_0)=\lambda$. Every function in the range of $T-\lambda I$ vanishes at $t_0$, so the range omits the constant function one. Hence it is not onto. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|T\|=1,\qquad\sigma_p(T)=\{1\},\qquad\sigma(T)=[0,1].}
$$

= Solution

The <unilateral shift operator> $R$ is not onto, since its outputs have first coordinate zero. The <left shift operator> $L$ is not injective, since $Le_1=0$. Both have <operator norm> one, and $LR=I$. Consequently
$$
L(R+A)=LR+LA=I+LA.
$$
If $\|A\|<1$, then $\|LA\|<1$, so the <Neumann series> makes $I+LA$ invertible. If $R+A$ were invertible too, the identity above would give
$$
L=(I+LA)(R+A)^{-1},
$$
a product of invertible operators, contradicting the nontrivial <kernel> of $L$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\|A\|<1\Longrightarrow R+A\text{ is not invertible}.}
$$
This is <norm stability of noninvertibility for the unilateral right shift>.

= Solution

The <Baire category theorem> states that a nonempty complete <metric space> cannot be a countable union of closed <nowhere dense sets>. The <uniform boundedness principle> says that if $V$ is a <Banach space>, $W$ a <normed vector space>, and $\mathcal T\subseteq B(V,W)$ is <pointwise> bounded, meaning $\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|<\infty$ for every $x\in V$, then $\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|T\|<\infty$.

To prove it, let $E_n=\{x:\sup_{T\in\mathcal T}\|Tx\|\leq n\}$. Each set is closed by <continuity> of the individual operators, and their union is $V$. The <Baire category theorem> gives a ball $B(x_0,r)$ inside some $E_N$. For $\|h\|<r$, both $x_0+h$ and $x_0$ are in $E_N$, so $\|Th\|\leq\|T(x_0+h)\|+\|Tx_0\|\leq2N$ for every $T$. Scaling gives $\|T\|\leq2N/r$ uniformly. The zero-space and empty-family cases are immediate.

For the first family on a <Hilbert space>, fix $x$. The functionals $y\mapsto\langle y,Tx\rangle$ have <operator norm> $\|Tx\|$ by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and equality on the direction of $Tx$. Their values are <pointwise> bounded by the hypothesis, after taking complex conjugates if necessary. Apply the <uniform boundedness principle> to obtain $\sup_{T\in\mathcal E}\|Tx\|<\infty$. Apply it a second time to the operator family itself to conclude **$\sup_{T\in\mathcal E}\|T\|<\infty$**.

For the diagonal hypothesis, the answer depends on the scalar <field>. Over $\mathbb C$, let $q_T(x)=\langle Tx,x\rangle$, using the convention that the <inner product> is linear in its first variable. The complex <polarization identity> gives
$$
4\langle Tx,y\rangle=q_T(x+y)-q_T(x-y)+i q_T(x+iy)-i q_T(x-iy).
$$
The assumed bounds at these four fixed vectors make every matrix coefficient uniformly bounded, so the preceding argument applies. Thus **the complex-space answer is yes**, even without assuming self-adjointness.

Over $\mathbb R$, take $X=\mathbb R^2$ and
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal F=\{nJ:n\geq1\}.
$$
Every diagonal value $\langle nJx,x\rangle$ is zero, whereas $\|nJ\|=n$. Thus **the real-space answer is no in general**. Real diagonal values do not detect the skew-symmetric part of an operator. This is <diagonal boundedness of operator families over real and complex Hilbert spaces>.

A <bounded linear operator> on a complex <Hilbert space> is <self-adjoint> if $T=T^*$, equivalently $\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle$ for every $x,y$.

If $Tx=0$, then $\langle x,Ty\rangle=\langle Tx,y\rangle=0$ for all $y$, so $x\in(\operatorname{Ran}T)^\perp$. Conversely, membership in that <orthogonal complement> gives $\langle Tx,y\rangle=0$ for every $y$, and choosing $y=Tx$ yields $Tx=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ker T=(\operatorname{Ran}T)^\perp.}
$$

Self-adjointness and conjugate symmetry of the <inner product> give
$$
\langle Tx,x\rangle=\langle x,Tx\rangle=\overline{\langle Tx,x\rangle}.
$$
A complex number equal to its conjugate is real, so **$\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb R$** for every $x$.

= Solution

For a <positive operator>, $\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq0$. We show directly that every nonreal or negative real $\lambda$ lies in the <resolvent set>.

If $\operatorname{Im}\lambda\ne0$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and reality of $\langle Tx,x\rangle$ give
$$
\|(T-\lambda I)x\|\|x\|\geq|\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x\rangle|\geq|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\|x\|^2.
$$
Thus $T-\lambda I$ is bounded below, hence injective with closed range: a convergent sequence of outputs has Cauchy preimages, whose limit maps to that output. Its adjoint is $T-\bar\lambda I$, which has trivial <kernel> by the same estimate. The identity $\operatorname{Ran}(A)^\perp=\ker A^*$, proved by the same inner-product calculation as in (i), makes its range dense. Being closed and dense, it is all of $X$, and the lower bound makes the inverse bounded.

If $\lambda<0$ is real, positivity gives $\langle(T-\lambda I)x,x\rangle\geq(-\lambda)\|x\|^2$, so the same argument applies with lower bound $-\lambda$. Here the operator is <self-adjoint>, so its range is dense because its own <kernel> is zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sigma(T)\subseteq[0,\infty).}
$$

= Solution

Set $B(x,y)=\langle Tx,y\rangle$. It is a positive semidefinite <Hermitian form>. Write $A=B(x,x)$, $C=B(y,y)$ and $c=B(x,y)$. For every complex $t$,
$$
0\leq B(x+ty,x+ty)=A+\bar t c+t\bar c+|t|^2C.
$$
If $C>0$, choosing $t=-c/C$ gives $A-|c|^2/C\geq0$. If $C=0$ and $c\ne0$, choosing $t=-sc$ for large positive $s$ makes the expression negative, a contradiction. Thus $c=0$ in that case. In all cases,
$$
\boxed{|\langle Tx,y\rangle|^2\leq\langle Tx,x\rangle\langle Ty,y\rangle.}
$$
This is the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for a positive operator>.

By the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for a positive operator>, for every $y$,
$$
|\langle Tx,y\rangle|^2\leq\langle Tx,x\rangle\langle Ty,y\rangle=0.
$$
Taking $y=Tx$ gives $Tx=0$, and $(S+T)x=0$ then gives $Sx=0$. Thus

\boxed{\ker(S+T)=\ker S\cap\ker T.}
$$
The reverse inclusion is immediate. This is the <kernel of a sum of positive operators> identity.

Commutativity and equality of squares give
$$
(S-T)(S+T)=S^2-T^2=0.
$$
Thus $S-T$ vanishes on $\operatorname{Ran}(S+T)$, and by <continuity> on its closure. The preceding <kernel of a sum of positive operators> identity shows that it also vanishes on $\ker(S+T)$. Since $S+T$ is <self-adjoint>, part (i) and the <Hilbert projection theorem> give the <orthogonal direct sum>
$$
X=\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(S+T)}\oplus\ker(S+T).
$$
Therefore $S-T$ vanishes on all of $X$, proving
$$
\boxed{S=T.}
$$
This proves <commuting positive operators with equal squares are equal> without assuming a square-root uniqueness theorem.

For finite subsets $F\subset\mathbb Z$, orthonormality gives
$$
\left\|\sum_{n\in F}\alpha_ne_n\right\|^2=\sum_{n\in F}|\alpha_n|^2.
$$
For $S_N=\sum_{|n|\leq N}\alpha_ne_n$, the squared <norm> of $S_N-S_M$ is the corresponding finite tail sum. Since $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|\alpha_n|^2<\infty$, these tails tend to zero. Thus $(S_N)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> in the complete <Hilbert space> and converges.

The same estimate for arbitrary finite tails proves that the sum is unconditional, so **the bilateral orthogonal series converges in $X$ independently of its ordering**. This is the <Riesz-Fischer theorem for an orthonormal system>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $a_n=\langle x,e_n\rangle$, the finite <orthogonal projection> $P_Nx=\sum_{|n|\leq N}a_ne_n$ satisfies
$$
0\leq\|x-P_Nx\|^2=\|x\|^2-\sum_{|n|\leq N}|a_n|^2.
$$
This is <Bessel's inequality>, so the coefficients are square summable. The preceding result gives a limit $s=\sum_na_ne_n$. For each fixed $k$, <continuity> of the <inner product> gives $\langle x-s,e_k\rangle=0$. The <completeness> assumption on the <orthonormal system> forces $x-s=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n.}
$$
Taking squared norms also gives <Parseval's identity>, $\|x\|^2=\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2$.

= Solution

Apply <continuity> of the <inner product> to the convergent expansion of $x$:
$$
\boxed{\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle x,e_n\rangle\langle e_n,y\rangle.}
$$
The series is absolutely convergent because the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for scalar sequences and <Bessel's inequality> give
$$
\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle\langle e_n,y\rangle|\leq\left(\sum_n|\langle x,e_n\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_n|\langle y,e_n\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}\leq\|x\|\|y\|.
$$
This is the polarized <Parseval identity>.

Use the <inner product> convention $\langle f,h\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\bar h$. For $h=\mathbf1_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}$, integration gives
$$
\langle e_0,h\rangle=\sqrt{\pi/2},\qquad\langle e_k,h\rangle=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\frac{\sin(k\pi/2)}k\quad(k\ne0).
$$
The nonzero even coefficients vanish, while $\sin((2n+1)\pi/2)=(-1)^n$. The preceding polarized <Parseval identity> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f(t)dt=\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\langle f,e_0\rangle+\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(-1)^n\frac{\langle f,e_{2n+1}\rangle}{2n+1}.}
$$
Absolute <convergence> follows from square summability of the <Fourier coefficients> and of $((2n+1)^{-1})_{n\in\mathbb Z}$.

For the convolution identity, fix any real $t$ and define $h_t(s)=\overline{g(t-s)}$. Periodicity makes $h_t\in L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ with $\|h_t\|_2=\|g\|_2$. A change of variable and integration over one full period give
$$
\langle e_n,h_t\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ins}g(t-s)ds=e^{int}\langle g,e_n\rangle.
$$
Applying polarized <Parseval identity> to $f,h_t$ gives, for every fixed $t$,
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(s)g(t-s)ds=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\langle f,e_n\rangle\langle g,e_n\rangle e^{int}.}
$$
The integral exists by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. The same inequality gives $\sum_n|\langle f,e_n\rangle\langle g,e_n\rangle|\leq\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$, so the displayed series converges absolutely and uniformly in $t$. In particular the equality holds at every $t$, without requiring <pointwise> <convergence> of either original <Fourier series>. This is <Fourier convolution of two periodic L2 functions>.
