topological-group.bigb
= Topological group
{wiki}

A topological group is a group whose multiplication and inversion maps are continuous.

= Locally profinite group
{parent=Topological group}

A locally profinite group is a Hausdorff, locally compact, totally disconnected <topological group>. It has a neighbourhood basis at the identity consisting of <compact-open subgroups>. In smooth representation theory this is also called an $\ell$-group; that terminology is unrelated to a lattice-ordered group.

= Compact-open subgroup
{parent=Locally profinite group}

A <compact-open subgroup> is both a <compact subset> and an <open subgroup>. Its coset space is discrete, and compact subsets meet only finitely many cosets. Finite intersections of <compact-open subgroups> are again compact-open, making them useful common stabilizers for finitely many vectors.

= Continuous unitary character
{parent=Topological group}
{title2=$\chi:G\longrightarrow\mathbb T$}

= Group character
{synonym}

A continuous unitary character is a continuous <group homomorphism> from a <topological group> to the <unit circle> with multiplication. For Abelian groups, multiplication of characters is pointwise. Such characters must be distinguished from the traces of higher-dimensional group representations and from <characters of an algebra>.

= Right-invariant group metric
{parent=Topological group}
{title2=$d(xa,ya)=d(x,y)$}

Right translations are isometries for a right-invariant group metric. Given a compatible <left-invariant group metric> $d$, the formula $d_R(x,y)=d(x^{-1},y^{-1})$ defines a compatible right-invariant group metric, since inversion is a homeomorphism and $(xa)^{-1}=a^{-1}x^{-1}$. Compatible invariant metrics need not be Lipschitz equivalent; on the additive group $\mathbb R$, both $|x-y|$ and $\sqrt{|x-y|}$ are compatible invariant distances.

= Left-invariant group metric
{parent=Topological group}
{title2=$d(ax,ay)=d(x,y)$}

This is a distance function satisfying the displayed identity, rather than a <Riemannian metric> tensor. When it induces the group's given topology, it is a compatible left-invariant group metric. Left translations are isometries.

= Bi-invariant group metric
{parent=Left-invariant group metric}
{title2=$d(axb,ayb)=d(x,y)$}

A group distance is bi-invariant when both left and right translations are <isometries>. <Conjugation> then fixes the <identity element> and is an <isometry>, so each <metric> ball there is invariant under every inner automorphism. A compatible <bi-invariant group metric> therefore requires arbitrarily small conjugation-invariant <neighbourhoods>. This concerns a distance function, not merely a <bi-invariant Riemannian metric> tensor.

= Affine conjugation obstruction to a bi-invariant group metric
{parent=Bi-invariant group metric}
{title2=$D_aT_bD_a^{-1}=T_{ab}$}

In the <orientation-preserving affine group of the real line>, translations $T_{1/n}$ converge to the <identity element>, but <conjugation> by dilation $D_n$ sends each one to $T_1$. A compatible <bi-invariant group metric> would give $d(e,T_{1/n})=d(e,T_1)>0$ for every $n$, contradicting convergence. The group is nonetheless metrizable in its ordinary two real coordinates.

= Birkhoff-Kakutani theorem
{c}
{parent=Left-invariant group metric}
{title2=$G\text{ metrizable}\iff G\text{ first countable}$}

A Hausdorff <topological group> admits a compatible <left-invariant group metric> exactly when it has a countable <neighbourhood basis> at the <identity element>. Choose symmetric <neighbourhoods> of the <identity element> $U_n$ with $U_{n+1}^3\subset U_n$ and trivial intersection. Assign a step in $U_n$ cost $2^{-n}$, and take the infimum of total costs of chains joining two points. The <dyadic product estimate for group neighbourhoods> makes this a genuine compatible <metric>. The Hausdorff assumption separates distinct points; without it the same construction can give only a pseudometric.

= Dyadic product estimate for group neighbourhoods
{parent=Birkhoff-Kakutani theorem}
{title2=$\sum_j2^{-n_j}\le2^{-n}\Longrightarrow\prod_jg_j\in U_{n-1}$}

Let $U_0=G$, and let the $U_n$ be decreasing symmetric sets containing the <identity element> with $U_n^3\subset U_{n-1}$. If $g_j\in U_{n_j}$ and their dyadic costs sum to at most $2^{-n}$ for $n\ge1$, split the ordered word at an edge crossing its half-cost point. The words on either side each have cost at most $2^{-n-1}$. Induction on word length puts both side words in $U_n$, while the middle edge also belongs to $U_n$. Their threefold product lies in $U_{n-1}$. This controls the short-chain collapse that would otherwise make a path-distance construction degenerate.

= Closed subgroup
{parent=Topological group}

A closed subgroup is a <subgroup> which is closed in the ambient topology. It is a <topological group> in its <subspace topology>, but need not be an <open subgroup>.

= Open subgroup
{parent=Topological group}

An open <subgroup> is also closed, since its complement is the union of its other open <cosets>. Conversely a closed subgroup need not be open, as $\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb R$ shows.

= Open normal subgroup
{parent=Open subgroup}

An <open normal subgroup> $N$ of a <topological group> is simultaneously open in its <topology> and invariant under <conjugation>. For a <profinite group>, the quotient $G/N$ is a finite discrete <group>: its <cosets> form a disjoint <open cover>, so <compactness> gives finitely many. Conversely the <group homomorphism kernel> of a continuous <homomorphism> to a finite discrete <group> is open and normal. Such <subgroups> form an identity-neighbourhood <basis> of every <profinite group>.

= Compactly generated group
{parent=Topological group}
{wiki}

A topological group is compactly generated in the algebraic sense when a compact subset generates its underlying group. Adjoining its inverse and the identity gives a compact symmetric generating set $K$, and $G=\bigcup_{n\geq1}K^n$ is a <sigma-compact space>. Some conventions restrict the term to locally compact groups or require a compact generating neighbourhood; those stronger conventions supply local compactness. Compact generation by an arbitrary compact subset alone does not.

= Compact generation does not imply local compactness
{parent=Compactly generated group}

The compact set $K=\{0\}\cup\{1/n!:n\geq1\}$ generates the additive group $\mathbb Q$, since every positive integer denominator divides some factorial. With its usual topology, $\mathbb Q$ is metrizable and is not locally compact. Nor can it have a nonzero <Haar measure> finite on compact sets: invariance gives equal masses to all singletons; a positive singleton mass makes $K$ have infinite mass, whereas zero singleton masses make the countable group have zero total mass.

= Locally compact group
{parent=Topological group}
{wiki}

A locally compact group is a <topological group> whose underlying space is a <locally compact space>. In <Haar measure> and <Fourier transform> theory one normally also assumes it is a <Hausdorff space>. Local compactness supplies compact neighbourhoods, not just compact sets which algebraically generate the group.

= Sigma-compact open subgroup of a locally compact group
{parent=Locally compact group}

A compact identity neighbourhood $K$ produces a symmetric compact neighbourhood $S=K\cup K^{-1}\cup\{e\}$. The subgroup $\bigcup_{n\geq1}S^n$ is open because it contains an identity neighbourhood, and is sigma-compact because each finite product $S^n$ is compact. This construction does not require the original group itself to be sigma-compact.

= Neighbourhood criterion for a topological group
{parent=Topological group}

= Neighborhood criterion for a topological group
{synonym}

A topology on a <group> makes it a <topological group> precisely when its <neighbourhood bases> satisfy two local tests: every neighbourhood $U$ of $xy$ contains $VW$ for some neighbourhoods $V$ of $x$ and $W$ of $y$, and every neighbourhood of $x^{-1}$ contains $V^{-1}$ for some neighbourhood $V$ of $x$. These are exactly continuity of multiplication in the <product topology> and continuity of inversion. Equivalently, bases can be chosen as left translates of a basis at the identity, with arbitrarily small squares, inverses and conjugates inside each prescribed identity neighbourhood.

= Discrete subgroup
{parent=Topological group}
{title2=$\Gamma\leq G$}

A subgroup $G$ of a <topological group> is discrete when the identity has a neighbourhood containing no other element of $G$. Translation then isolates every group element. In a Hausdorff <topological group>, such a subgroup is closed: any accumulation at a point would make differences of distinct nearby group elements approach the identity. Consequently its intersection with a <compact set> is finite. For isometries of <hyperbolic space>, this converts compactness of the set of isometries moving one point a bounded distance into a <properly discontinuous group action>.

= Discrete additive subgroup of a real vector space
{parent=Discrete subgroup}
{title2=$\Lambda=\mathbb Zv_1+\cdots+\mathbb Zv_r$}

A discrete additive <subgroup> of $\mathbb R^n$ is generated by real-linearly independent vectors. Indeed, isolation of zero gives uniform separation, so compact sets meet the <subgroup> finitely. Choose a real basis from the <subgroup> for its real span. Its <integer> span has finite index, since reduction into a compact fundamental parallelepiped gives finitely many <coset> representatives. The <structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups> then gives a free <integer> basis, whose size is the dimension of the real span. Thus the <subgroup> is a <Euclidean lattice> in its span, although it need not have full rank in the ambient space.

= Planar discrete-subgroup basis
{parent=Discrete subgroup}

A nonzero discrete additive subgroup of $\mathbb R^2$ has a basis of one or two real-linearly independent vectors. Choose a shortest nonzero vector $w_1$. If the subgroup is not contained in its line, reduce parallel components modulo $w_1$ and choose a vector $w_2$ of least positive perpendicular height. Such a height exists: heights tending to zero would give infinitely many reduced points in a bounded region, contrary to discreteness. Reducing any group vector modulo $w_2$ leaves height smaller than this minimum, hence height zero, and reducing along $w_1$ then gives an integer multiple of $w_1$.

= Kleinian group
{c}
{parent=Discrete subgroup}
{title2=$\Gamma\leq\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb C)$}
{wiki}

A discrete subgroup of $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb C)$, acting by <Möbius transformations> on the <Riemann sphere> or orientation-preserving isometries of <hyperbolic space> $\mathbb H^3$. A group may be Kleinian even when some elements fix points; freeness and absence of torsion are additional conditions.

= Gaussian Bianchi group
{c}
{parent=Kleinian group}
{title2=$PSL_2(\mathbb Z[i])$}

The Gaussian Bianchi group is the <Kleinian group> of determinant-one two-by-two matrices over the <Gaussian integers>, modulo $\{\pm I\}$. Its matrix entries lie in a discrete subring of $\mathbb C$, so it is a discrete subgroup of $PSL_2(\mathbb C)$. The <Borromean link group> is a torsion-free subgroup of index 24.

= Jørgensen's inequality
{c}
{parent=Kleinian group}
{wiki}
{title2=$|\operatorname{tr}^2A-4|+|\operatorname{tr}[A,B]-2|\ge1$}

= Jorgensen inequality
{c}
{synonym}

For a non-elementary <discrete subgroup> generated by $A,B\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$, the displayed inequality is a necessary trace constraint. Failure of it proves nondiscreteness when non-elementarity has independently been checked. It is not a sufficient condition for discreteness and must not be applied to an elementary pair without its hypothesis.

= Schottky group
{c}
{parent=Kleinian group}
{wiki}

A <Schottky group> is a free, discrete group of <Mobius transformations> constructed by pairing disjoint Jordan curves on the <Riemann sphere>, taking the exterior of one bounded disk to the interior of its partner. For round circles it is classical. Strictly separated defining disks give a <Cantor set> as the <limit set of a Kleinian group>. The tangent-disk extension permits parabolic elements and is often called a noded or T-Schottky group; its <Kleinian limit set> need not be a <Cantor set>.

= Tangent-disk Schottky group
{c}
{parent=Schottky group}

A <tangent-disk Schottky group> uses side-pairing disks with disjoint interiors but permits boundaries to meet. This convention must be distinguished from the strictly separated, purely loxodromic <Schottky group> convention. For example, the maps $A(z)=z+2$ and $B(z)=z/(iz+1)$ pair the half-plane disks $\Re z<-1,\Re z>1$ and the disks $|z-i|<1,|z+i|<1$. The <ping-pong lemma> gives a free discrete group. With $C=ABA^{-1}B^{-1}$, the subgroup generated by $B,C$ is conjugate by $M(z)=iz/(2-z)$ to the principal level-two modular group, generated by $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Thus its <Kleinian limit set> contains the circle $|z-1|=1$ and its translate $|z-3|=1$. Its <Hausdorff dimension> is at least one and it cannot be <homeomorphic> to a single circle.

= Rank-one convergence of divergent Möbius transformations
{parent=Kleinian group}

A divergent sequence of <Möbius transformations> has a <subsequence> converging uniformly on compact subsets of the <Riemann sphere> outside one exceptional point to a constant. Represent the transformations by determinant-one $2\times2$ <matrices> and divide each by its matrix norm. A convergent subsequence has a nonzero rank-one limit: its determinant tends to zero because the original norms tend to infinity. The kernel line is the exceptional point, and the image line is the constant limit. This converts the <limit set of a Kleinian group> into a tractable boundary dynamics problem.

= Elliptic stabilizer obstruction to a hyperbolic manifold quotient
{parent=Kleinian group}

A nontrivial finite-order <elliptic Möbius transformation> fixes an axis in <hyperbolic space>. The quotient metric has cone angle $2\pi/m$ along that axis for a stabilizer of order $m$, so it is locally an <orbifold> metric rather than a smooth hyperbolic-manifold metric. The underlying topological quotient may nevertheless be a manifold.

= Non-elementary Kleinian group
{parent=Kleinian group}

A <Kleinian group> whose <Kleinian limit set> contains at least three points. A discrete group fixing a common boundary point has at most two limit points and is therefore elementary.

= Limit set of a Kleinian group
{parent=Kleinian group}
{title2=$\Lambda(\Gamma)=\overline{\Gamma o}\cap S^2_\infty$}

= Kleinian limit set
{c}
{synonym}

For a discrete isometry group of <hyperbolic space>, its limit set is the boundary accumulation set of one interior orbit. All interior base points give the same set: corresponding orbit points stay a fixed hyperbolic distance apart, and bounded-distance sequences approaching the boundary have the same endpoint. This definition does not require finite generation.

= Minimality of the Kleinian limit set
{parent=Limit set of a Kleinian group}

Every nonempty closed boundary subset invariant under a <non-elementary Kleinian group> contains the <Kleinian limit set>. Such a subset cannot be a singleton. Its hyperbolic convex hull contains an interior point, whose orbit lies in the hull; in the Klein ball model the boundary of this hull is contained in the original closed boundary subset.

= Limit set of an infinite normal Kleinian subgroup
{parent=Minimality of the Kleinian limit set}
{title2=$N\mathrel{\triangleleft}\Gamma,\quad |N|=\infty\implies\Lambda(N)=\Lambda(\Gamma)$}

The <Kleinian limit set> of an infinite subgroup is nonempty. If it is normal, its limit set is invariant under the ambient group by base-point independence and conjugation. <Minimality of the Kleinian limit set> gives one inclusion, and subgroup containment gives the other. Neither properness nor finite generation of the subgroup is needed.

= Gaussian-integer parabolic construction
{parent=Kleinian group}
{title2=$\begin{pmatrix}1+rn&-n^2\\r^2&1-rn\end{pmatrix}$}

For $n\in\mathbb Z[i]$ and a nonzero integer $r$, the displayed determinant-one matrix has trace two and represents a nonidentity <parabolic Möbius transformation> fixing $n/r$. Its fixed-point equation is $(rz-n)^2=0$. These parabolic fixed points are dense in the <Riemann sphere>, proving that the Gaussian-integer determinant-one <Kleinian group> has full limit set.

= A free Kleinian group generated by two parabolic transformations
{c}
{parent=Kleinian group}
{title2=$\langle z\mapsto z+2,\ z\mapsto z/(2z+1)\rangle\cong F_2$}

The integer matrices $A=(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{smallmatrix})$ and $B=(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{smallmatrix})$ generate a discrete subgroup. For every nonzero integer $m$, $A^m$ sends $|x|<1$ into $|x|>1$, while $B^m$ sends $|x|>1$, including infinity, into $|x|<1$. The <ping-pong lemma> identifies their subgroup as the <free product> of two infinite cyclic groups. This separates the proof of discreteness from the proof of freeness.

= Compact group
{parent=Topological group}
{wiki}

= Compact topological group
{synonym}

A <compact group> is a <topological group> whose underlying <topological space> is compact and Hausdorff.

= Compact groups with a descending chain condition are Lie groups
{parent=Compact group}

The <Peter-Weyl theorem> makes finite-dimensional <unitary representations> separate elements of a <compact group>. Intersecting their closed kernels gives the identity <subgroup>. If the <descending chain condition> holds for closed <subgroups>, choose finitely many representations whose kernel intersection is already trivial: otherwise repeatedly exclude a surviving nonidentity element to obtain an infinite strict chain. Their direct sum is a <faithful representation> into a finite-dimensional <unitary group>. Compactness makes its image closed, and the <closed-subgroup theorem> gives the original <group> a compatible <Lie group> structure.

= Closed subsemigroup of a compact group
{parent=Compact group}

A nonempty closed <subsemigroup> $S$ of a <compact group> is a subgroup. For $x\in S$ and a neighbourhood $U$ of the identity, choose $V$ with $V^{-1}V\subseteq U$ and cover the compact group by finitely many translates of $V$. Two of sufficiently many powers $1,x,x^2,\ldots$ lie in the same translate, so some positive power of $x$ belongs to $U$. Hence the identity is a limit of positive powers and lies in $S$. The preceding powers converge to $x^{-1}$, which also lies in $S$. Applied to the closure of a positive-word monoid, this proves that it equals the closed subgroup generated by its generators, without assuming they already include their inverses.

= Profinite group
{parent=Topological group}
{wiki}

A profinite group is an <inverse limit> of finite discrete groups. Equivalently, it is a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected <topological group>.

= Just-infinite profinite group
{parent=Profinite group}

= Just infinite
{synonym}

An infinite <profinite group> is just infinite if every nontrivial closed <normal subgroup> has finite index, equivalently is open. Requiring closedness is part of the topological convention. This property says that every proper <Hausdorff> quotient is finite.

= Hereditarily just-infinite profinite group
{parent=Just-infinite profinite group}

= Hereditarily just infinite
{synonym}

An infinite <profinite group> is <hereditarily just infinite> if every <open subgroup> is a <just-infinite profinite group>. To prove this, it suffices to show that every nonidentity element of every <open subgroup> has open closed <normal closure>: any nontrivial closed <normal subgroup> then contains that <normal closure>. The <Nottingham group> is an example. In odd characteristic its depth-commutator formula constructs nonzero elements of each sufficiently high layer inside any such <normal subgroup>, and successive coefficient correction fills the entire tail.

= Rank of a profinite group
{parent=Profinite group}
{title2=$\operatorname{rk}(G)=\sup_{K\le_cG}d(K)$}

The rank of a <profinite group> is $\operatorname{rk}(G)=\sup_{K\le_cG}d(K)$, where $K$ runs through closed <subgroups> and $d(K)$ is the least number of topological generators. If an <open subgroup> $H$ has rank $r$, its <normal core> $C$ is open and has rank at most $r$. Every closed $K\le G$ has a <normal subgroup> $K\cap C$ generated by at most $r$ elements, while $K/(K\cap C)$ has order at most $[G:C]$. A <finite group> of order at most $M$ is generated by at most $\lfloor\log_2M\rfloor$ elements, since each necessary new generator at least doubles the generated <subgroup>. Lifting generators yields $d(K)\le r+\lfloor\log_2[G:C]\rfloor$. Thus finite rank is preserved in both directions under passage to an <open subgroup>.

= Procyclic group
{parent=Profinite group}

= Procyclic
{synonym}

A <profinite group> $G$ is procyclic if every quotient by an <open normal subgroup> is cyclic. Equivalently $G=\overline{\langle g\rangle}$ for one element $g$. For the forward implication, the sets of lifts of generators of $G/N$ are nonempty <clopen> sets. Any finite intersection contains a lift of a generator of the cyclic quotient by the intersection of those <open normal subgroups>. <Compactness> supplies one element generating every finite quotient, and hence generating densely. The reverse implication follows by projecting that generator. For a <pro-p group>, two distinct maximal <open subgroups> would give a quotient $C_p^2$, so a nontrivial <procyclic group> has a unique maximal <open subgroup> $M$. Its <profinite Frattini subgroup> is $M$, and any $g\notin M$ generates topologically: otherwise the proper <closed subgroup> it generates lies in a maximal <open subgroup>. The additive <group> of <p-adic integers> is an infinite example, generated topologically by $1$.

= Open normal subgroup basis of a profinite group
{parent=Profinite group}

Every identity neighbourhood of a compact Hausdorff totally disconnected <topological group> contains an open <normal subgroup>. Choose a <clopen set> $U$ containing the identity inside that neighbourhood. Compactness and continuity give a symmetric identity neighbourhood $V$ with $VU\subseteq U$, hence $VU=U$. The setwise stabilizer $H=\{g:gU=U\}$ is an open <subgroup> contained in $U$. Its index is finite, so its <normal core> is an open <normal subgroup> contained in $U$. These subgroups recover the <profinite group> as the <inverse limit> of its finite quotients.

= Profinite Frattini subgroup
{parent=Profinite group}
{title2=$\Phi(G)$}

The intersection of the maximal proper open <subgroups> of a <profinite group>. A subset topologically generates the <profinite group> exactly when its image topologically generates its quotient by this intersection: a proper closed generated <subgroup> is contained in a maximal open <subgroup>, obtained from a suitable finite quotient. For a <pro-p group>, $\Phi(G)=\overline{G^p[G,G]}$, where the bar denotes <closure> of the algebraically generated power and <commutator subgroups>. Closedness cannot simply be discarded in infinite rank.

= Profinite abelian group
{parent=Profinite group}

A profinite abelian group is an inverse limit of finite <abelian groups>. Its topology is compact and totally disconnected. An abelian <pro-p group> acquires scalar multiplication by <p-adic integers> by taking limits of integer multiples.

= Pro-p group
{parent=Profinite group}
{title2=$P=\varprojlim P_i$}

= Pro-l group
{synonym}

A pro-$p$ group is an inverse limit of finite $p$-groups. Every continuous action on a finite set or finite module factors through a finite $p$-group. In particular its action on $\mu_p$ is trivial when the field characteristic differs from $p$, because $\operatorname{Aut}(\mu_p)$ has order $p-1$.

= Pro-p subgroup
{parent=Pro-p group}

A closed <subgroup> of a <profinite group> that is a <pro-p group> with the induced topology. Its images in finite continuous quotients are <p-groups>.

= R-analytic pro-p group
{parent=Pro-p group}
{c}

For a <pro-p ring> $R$, an $R$-analytic <pro-p group> is a <pro-p group> equipped with an analytic manifold structure locally modelled on open subsets of a finite free $R$-module, such that multiplication and inversion are given locally by convergent <power series>. The number of local coordinates is its analytic <dimension>. The <Nottingham group> admits no such structure over any pro-$p$ ring: its hereditary just infiniteness, unbounded finite cyclic torsion and sub-base-three derived-series growth exclude both alternatives in the analytic just-infinite structure theorem.

= Nottingham group
{parent=Pro-p group}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathcal N(\mathbb F_p)$}

Over $k=\mathbb F_p$, the <Nottingham group> is $\mathcal N(k)=\{f(t)=t+\sum_{i\ge2}a_it^i:a_i\in k\}$ with composition and the $t$-adic <topology>. Equivalently it is the <group> of continuous $k$-automorphisms of the <Laurent series field> $k((t))$ that act trivially on $(t)/(t^2)$. With ordinary series composition, the <isomorphism> sends $f$ to the automorphism $h(t)\mapsto h(f^{-1}(t))$; this inverse avoids reversing the <group> product. It is a <pro-p group>, recovered from its finite truncations, and contains elements of arbitrarily large finite $p$-power order.

= Finite p-group embedding in the Nottingham group
{parent=Nottingham group}

Realize a <finite p-group> $P$ as the <Galois group> of a <totally ramified extension> $E/\mathbb F_p((t))$, using <Artin-Schreier embedding problems over a characteristic-p field>. A <uniformizer> $u$ of $E$ gives $E\cong\mathbb F_p((u))$: every element has a unique convergent Laurent expansion with coefficients in the <residue field>. An automorphism has $u\mapsto a_1u+a_2u^2+\cdots$, and $a_1$ defines a <homomorphism> $P\to\mathbb F_p^\times$. Its image is trivial because $|P|$ is a $p$-power and $p\nmid|\mathbb F_p^\times|$. Thus the faithful Galois action lies in the <Nottingham group>, embedding $P$.

= Depth filtration of the Nottingham group
{parent=Nottingham group}
{title2=$\mathcal N_m=\{f:f(t)\equiv t\pmod{t^{m+1}}\}$}

Put $\mathcal N_m=\{f:f(t)\equiv t\pmod{t^{m+1}}\}$ for $m\ge1$. These are <open normal subgroups> and $\mathcal N_m/\mathcal N_{m+1}\cong(\mathbb F_p,+)$ via the coefficient of $t^{m+1}$. Thus $[\mathcal N:\mathcal N_m]=p^{m-1}$. The depth of a nonidentity series is $\operatorname{ord}_t(f(t)-t)-1$. If $f=t+at^{r+1}+\cdots$ and $g=t+bt^{s+1}+\cdots$, expansion of substitution gives
$$
[f,g](t)=t+ab(r-s)t^{r+s+1}+O(t^{r+s+2}).
$$
Here $[f,g]=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}\circ f\circ g$. This formula controls <commutator subgroups> and <normal subgroups>.

= Derived-series growth of the Nottingham group
{parent=Depth filtration of the Nottingham group}
{title2=$\log_p[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]=O(\lambda^n),\quad\lambda<3$}

For odd $p$, <group commutator> collection in the depth filtration gives $[\mathcal N_m,\mathcal N_m]=\mathcal N_{2m+1}$. Every degree $k\ge2m+1$ occurs as a leading <group commutator> degree by choosing $r,s\ge m$, $r+s=k$, and $r\not\equiv s\pmod p$; changing $r$ by one changes $r-s$ by two. Closed successive coefficient correction then fills the tail. Therefore $\mathcal N^{(n)}=\mathcal N_{2^{n+1}-1}$ and $\log_p[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]=2^{n+1}-2$. In characteristic two the sharper collection theorem gives $\log_2[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]\le C\kappa^n$ for a constant $C$, with $\kappa=(1+\sqrt{17})/2<3$. In both cases the exponential base is strictly smaller than three.

= Golod-Shafarevich infinitude criterion for pro-p groups
{c}
{parent=Pro-p group}

If a pro-$p$ presentation on $d$ generators and possibly infinitely many relators $r_i$ satisfies
$$
1-d\tau+\sum_i\tau^{D(r_i)}<0\qquad(0<\tau<1),
$$
with the sum convergent and $D$ the <Magnus degree of a free pro-p group element>, then the presented <pro-p group> is infinite. The Golod-Shafarevich inequality forces its completed group algebra to be infinite-dimensional, which would be impossible for a finite group. This formulation applies to relators whose degrees tend to infinity and is suitable for enumerated power-relation constructions.

= Powerful pro-p group
{parent=Pro-p group}
{title2=$[G,G]\subseteq G^{p^\epsilon}$}

A <pro-p group> whose closed <commutator subgroup> lies in its closed power subgroup $G^{p^\epsilon}$, with $\epsilon=1$ for odd $p$ and $\epsilon=2$ for $p=2$. This is the profinite analogue of a <powerful p-group>. Finitely generated torsion-free examples are exactly the <uniform pro-p groups>. The additive <group> of a finite product of <p-adic integers> is an abelian example.

= Pro-p completion
{parent=Pro-p group}
{title2=$\widehat G_p$}

The <inverse limit> of the quotients of an abstract <group> by its <normal subgroups> of finite $p$-power index. Maps to <pro-p groups> factor uniquely through the canonical dense image in this completion. Unlike <profinite completion>, only <finite p-groups> are used.

= Free pro-p group
{parent=Pro-p group}
{title2=$F_p(X)$}

For finite $X$, this is the <pro-p completion> of the <free group> on $X$. Every function from $X$ to a <pro-p group> extends uniquely to a continuous <group homomorphism>. = Magnus degree of a free pro-p group element

Embed a <free pro-p group> on $x_1,\ldots,x_d$ into the units of the noncommutative power-series algebra $\mathbb F_p\langle\!\langle X_1,\ldots,X_d\rangle\!\rangle$ by $x_i\mapsto1+X_i$. = Finite extensions preserve finite pro-p presentations
{parent=Finite pro-p presentation}

Suppose $K$ is an open <normal subgroup> of a <pro-p group> $G$ and has a <finite pro-p presentation>. Choose lifts of generators of the finite quotient $G/K$. The resulting finite <finite pro-p presentation> maps onto $G$. = Generator number and first cohomology of a finitely generated pro-p group

For a finitely generated <pro-p group>, its <profinite Frattini subgroup> has finite <elementary abelian> quotient. = Infinite-rank failure of the first-cohomology generator formula
{parent=Generator number and first cohomology of a finitely generated pro-p group}

Let $I$ be the set of infinite binary sequences and $G=\mathbb F_p^I$ with its <product topology>. Index the finite binary strings by the positive integers. For each $n$, take the element whose $r$ coordinate is one exactly when the $n$th string is a prefix of $r$. These countably many elements generate a dense <subgroup>: given finitely many distinct sequences, sufficiently long prefixes distinguish them individually. No finite family generates $G$, so its topological generator number is countable. Every continuous linear functional depends on finitely many coordinates, however, so $H^1(G,\mathbb F_p)$ has a basis of size $|I|=2^{\aleph_0}$. Thus the arbitrary-rank identity is false for unrestricted topological generating sets.

= Uniform pro-p group
{parent=Pro-p group}

A finitely generated <powerful p-group> in the category of <pro-p groups> is uniform if all successive indices in its <lower p-series> are equal. Equivalently it is torsion-free. If its generator number is $d$, the successive indices are $p^d$, the power map carries $G^{p^n}$ bijectively onto $G^{p^{n+1}}$, and an ordered minimal generating set gives unique coordinates $g_1^{\lambda_1}\cdots g_d^{\lambda_d}$ with $\lambda_i$ in the <p-adic integers>. For $p=2$, powerfulness means $[G,G]\leq G^4$.

= Odd-prime principal p-adic congruence subgroup is uniform
{parent=Uniform pro-p group}
{title2=$U_m=1+p^mM_d(\mathbb Z_p),\quad[U_m:U_{m+1}]=p^{d^2}$}

For an odd prime $p$, the <matrix logarithm> and <matrix exponential> are inverse maps between $1+pM_d(\mathbb Z_p)$ and $pM_d(\mathbb Z_p)$. They imply that the $p$th-power map carries $U_m$ bijectively onto $U_{m+1}$. Direct multiplication gives $[U_i,U_j]\subseteq U_{i+j}$, so the <group> is a <powerful pro-p group>. The quotient $U_m/U_{m+1}$ is the additive <matrix> space over $\mathbb F_p$, with constant index $p^{d^2}$. The $d^2$ <matrices> $1+pE_{ij}$ topologically generate, by successive approximation through these quotients. The <lower p-series> is exactly $U_m$, so the <group> is a <uniform pro-p group> and a <torsion-free group>. At $p=2$ the first congruence subgroup contains $-I$ and is not uniform.

= Root correction criterion for uniformity
{parent=Uniform pro-p group}
{title2=$u\mapsto u^p:U_j/U_{j+1}\longrightarrow U_{j+1}/U_{j+2}$}

For odd $p$ and a finitely generated <powerful pro-p group>, put $U_j=G^{p^j}$. The displayed <group homomorphisms> are surjective. If one has a nonzero <kernel>, choose $x\in U_j\setminus U_{j+1}$ with $x^p\in U_{j+2}$. Successive corrections $x\mapsto xu_r$, with $u_r\in U_r$, cancel its $p$-th power one layer at a time, using $(xu_r)^p\equiv x^pu_r^p\pmod {U_{r+2}}$. <Compactness> and the separated power filtration give a nonidentity limit of order $p$. Thus a <torsion-free group> has constant layer indices and is a <uniform pro-p group>. Conversely constant indices make every displayed map injective; comparison at the first differing power layer proves that $x\mapsto x^p$ is injective, so there is no nontrivial torsion.

= Linearity of a uniform pro-p group
{parent=Uniform pro-p group}

Every <uniform pro-p group> embeds continuously in $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb Z_p)$ for some finite $n$. Apply the <Ado theorem> to $\mathbb Q_p\otimes L_G$ and choose a deep power lattice whose representing <matrices> lie in $p\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$, or $4\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$ at $p=2$. The <matrix exponential> and <logarithm> give a <faithful representation> of the corresponding open normal power <subgroup>. Induce that representation to the whole <group> using its finitely many <cosets>. An element outside the <open subgroup> permutes the <coset> blocks nontrivially; an element inside it is detected by the original representation. Thus the <induced representation> is faithful. All block <matrices> preserve a finite free $\mathbb Z_p$-module, giving the integral embedding.

= Power congruences in a uniform pro-p group
{parent=Uniform pro-p group}
{title2=$x^{p^a}\equiv y^{p^a}\pmod{G^{p^{a+b}}}$}

For a <uniform pro-p group> with $U_i=G^{p^i}$, the map $x\mapsto x^{p^a}$ transports congruences by
$$
x\equiv y\pmod{U_b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^{p^a}\equiv y^{p^a}\pmod{U_{a+b}}.
$$
<Commutator> collection gives $(yu)^p\equiv y^pu^p\pmod{U_{b+2}}$ for $u\in U_b$, and the power map is an <isomorphism> $U_b/U_{b+1}\to U_{b+1}/U_{b+2}$. Thus a nontrivial first difference moves down exactly one layer when taking a $p$th power. Iterating gives the equivalence; completeness supplies unique roots when the congruence is read in reverse.

= Transported addition on a uniform pro-p group
{parent=Uniform pro-p group}
{title2=$x+_ny=(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})^{p^{-n}}$}

For a <uniform pro-p group>, the power map $x\mapsto x^{p^n}$ is a <homeomorphism> onto $G^{p^n}$. Transport multiplication back to $G$ by
$$
x+_ny=(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})^{p^{-n}}.
$$
Unique roots make this an associative <group> operation. For odd $p$, <group commutator> collection gives $x+_ny\equiv y+_nx\pmod{G^{p^{n+1}}}$ and $x+_ny\equiv x+_{n-1}y\pmod{G^{p^n}}$. The operations converge to addition in the <Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group>.

= Strong completeness of finitely generated pro-p groups
{parent=Pro-p group}

Every abstract <subgroup> of finite index in a finitely generated <pro-p group> is open. <Fixed-generator commutator collection in nilpotent groups> makes the abstract <commutator subgroup> closed. The resulting abelian quotient is a quotient of a finite power of the <p-adic integers>, so its subgroup of $p$th powers is closed and of finite index. Thus the algebraic $G^p[G,G]$ is open and every abstract index-$p$ <subgroup> is open. An arbitrary finite quotient is a <finite p-group>; induction on its order, using a normal subgroup of index $p$ and the <Schreier lemma>, gives the assertion. = Profinite topology

= Profinite topology on the integers is not compact
{parent=Profinite topology}

The <profinite topology> on $\mathbb Z$ has basis the <residue classes> $a+k\mathbb Z$, $k\geq1$. These classes are both open and closed, since their complements are finite unions of other <residue classes>. Set $F_n=(4^n-1)/3+4^n\mathbb Z$ for $n\geq1$. Then $F_{n+1}\subseteq F_n$ and every $F_n$ is nonempty. An integer $x$ in their intersection would make $3x+1$ divisible by every $4^n$, forcing $3x+1=0$, impossible. Thus the open complements $\mathbb Z\setminus F_n$ form an increasing cover with no finite subcover. The <topology> is <Hausdorff> because distinct integers are distinct modulo a sufficiently large integer, but is not compact.

= Determinant detects the order of a unitary loop

The determinant $U(n)\to S^1$ sends a unitary loop to a circle-valued loop. A nonzero determinant winding number proves that the original loop class has infinite order.
