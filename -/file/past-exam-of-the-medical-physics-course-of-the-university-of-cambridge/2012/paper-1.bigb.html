For the specified energy-loss model, multiply $dE/dx=-k/E$ by $2E$ and integrate from the entrance:
$$
\frac{d(E^2)}{dx}=-2k,\qquad
\boxed{E(x)^2=E_0^2-2kx.}
$$
The positive <stopping power> is $S=-dE/dx=k/E$. The model reaches zero <kinetic energy> at
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{E_0^2}{2k}=\frac{200^2}{2(770)}\,\mathrm{cm}=25.97\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
At the entrance,
$$
\boxed{S(0)=\frac{770}{200}=3.85\,\mathrm{MeV\,cm^{-1}}.}
$$
Half a centimeter before the formal endpoint, $E^2=2k(0.5\,\mathrm{cm})=770\,\mathrm{MeV^2}$, so
$$
\boxed{E=27.75\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad S=27.75\,\mathrm{MeV\,cm^{-1}}.}
$$
The signed derivatives are the negatives of the quoted <stopping powers>. This <inverse-energy stopping-power model> deposits <energy> more strongly toward the endpoint. Its divergent endpoint derivative is integrable, giving finite total deposited <energy> $E_0$; a real stopping curve includes finite <energy> spread, range straggling, scattering and a more accurate low-energy stopping law. These numbers are consequences of the supplied ideal equation.

The generic physics of <magnetic resonance imaging> combines a main static magnet, radiofrequency transmit and receive coils, controlled <magnetic-field gradients>, signal electronics and reconstruction. A transverse radiofrequency pulse excites <nuclear spins> near their Larmor <frequency>, $\omega_0=\gamma B_0$. The evolving transverse magnetization induces a received signal; <phase> coherence and relaxation determine its time dependence.

Gradients encode <position> into <phase>. In a simplified homogeneous receive-sensitivity model,
$$
s(t)=\int\rho(\mathbf r)e^{-2\pi i\mathbf k(t)\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3\mathbf r,
\qquad \mathbf k(t)=\frac{\gamma}{2\pi}\int^t\mathbf G(t')\,dt'.
$$
Here $\mathbf k$ is in cycles per unit length and $\gamma$ is in radians per second per tesla. Echo sign changes, pulse sequence timing and receive sensitivity belong in a more complete signal model. Sampling this <MRI k-space> and reconstructing its data produces an image; the measured samples are not already real-space pixel values.

The physical signal depends on <spin> density, longitudinal recovery and transverse dephasing. Timing changes their weighting. Static field inhomogeneity adds reversible dephasing beyond intrinsic <transverse relaxation>, motivating echo techniques and distinguishing $T_2^*$ from $T_2$. Those are physical signal mechanisms; their biological interpretation remains deferred.

Generic <image registration> estimates a coordinate transformation aligning corresponding features in two images. A rigid transform permits translation and rotation, an affine transform additionally permits linear deformation, and a nonrigid model permits spatially varying displacement. The transformation class expresses assumptions about what changes between the acquisitions.

One can minimize a discrepancy between $I_1(T(\mathbf x))$ and $I_2(\mathbf x)$, using interpolated image values and an explicit <optimization> procedure. Squared intensity differences suit some comparable acquisitions; <mutual information> can compare statistical dependence when image contrasts differ. Landmarks, feature descriptors or additional geometric constraints provide other information. Smoothness or other <regularization> controls overly flexible transformations. A favorable objective value does not by itself prove correct feature correspondence.

In <pulse-echo ultrasound>, a transducer emits a pulse and records returning echoes. For a straight round-trip path in a medium of assumed <sound speed> $c$, an echo delay $t$ gives depth $z=ct/2$. <Reflections> depend on acoustic-impedance changes, while attenuation, scattering and propagation geometry affect the received amplitude.

An <A-mode ultrasound scan> displays echo amplitude against inferred depth along one beam. A <B-mode ultrasound scan> maps received amplitude to brightness and assembles many beam directions or translated beam lines into a spatial image. An <M-mode ultrasound scan> repeats one beam line and displays its depth profile versus time, making motion along that line visible. Thus the display axes and acquisition pattern differ even when the pulse-echo principle is shared.

For a pulse lasting $\tau_p$, a simple axial-separation estimate is $c\tau_p/2$. Lateral resolution depends on aperture, wavelength, focusing and depth. Shorter pulses and broader bandwidth can improve axial resolution; an assumed <sound speed> or a multiple-reflection path can instead misplace an echo. Brightness is not a direct universal map of composition.

<Doppler ultrasound> uses <frequency> or pulse-to-pulse <phase> changes from moving scatterers. In a simple backscatter geometry, $\Delta f\simeq2f_0v\cos\theta/c$. Inferring <speed> requires the beam-to-motion angle; pulsed sampling can alias frequencies, and an unknown angle leaves a velocity-projection ambiguity. This describes generic acoustic acquisition rather than a clinical diagnosis.

The generic physics of <positron emission tomography> uses <electron-positron annihilation>. A slowly moving electron-positron pair predominantly produces two nearly opposite <photons> with <energy> close to $m_ec^2=511\,\mathrm{keV}$ each. A ring of detectors records approximately simultaneous interactions; a coincidence identifies a <coincidence line of response> along which an annihilation could have occurred. Finite <positron> travel before annihilation and nonzero pair <momentum> limit how directly this line identifies the original emission location.

Many such measurements constrain an emission distribution through a <tomography> model. Attenuation, scattered coincidences, unrelated random coincidences, finite detector resolution and dead time alter the observed counts and require <calibration> or modeling. With useful time-of-flight information, the arrival-time difference further localizes <position> along the line: a timing uncertainty $\delta t$ corresponds to a <position> scale $c\delta t/2$. The coincidence data therefore provide spatial constraints, rather than each event directly supplying a complete three-dimensional image point.
