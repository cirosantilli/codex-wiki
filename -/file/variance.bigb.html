= Variance
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(X)$}
{wiki}

The <variance> measures the mean squared distance of a <random variable> from its <expected value>. For a real <square-integrable> <random variable> $X$,
$$
\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)^2]
=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb EX)^2.
$$
The square prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling. It also gives the <variance> squared units: if $X$ measures a <length> in metres, its <variance> has units of square metres. Its nonnegative square root is the <standard deviation>, which has the same units as $X$.

For example, let $X$ take $-1$, $0$ and $1$ with equal <probabilities>. Its <expected value> is zero, but its <variance> is $[(-1)^2+0^2+1^2]/3=2/3$, and its <standard deviation> is $\sqrt{2/3}$. The constant <random variable> zero has the same <expected value> and zero <variance>: a <mean> alone does not describe the spread. The <variance> is zero exactly when $X=\mathbb E X$ <almost surely>.

The <variance> is useful because squared deviations interact neatly with <linear combinations>. The <covariance> supplies the cross terms when combining different <random variables>, and a <covariance matrix> organizes those terms for many variables. The characterization of <variance as the minimum mean squared error of a constant> also explains why the <expected value> is the best constant prediction under squared error.

= Fano factor
{c}
{parent=Variance}
{title2=$F=\operatorname{Var}(X)/\mathbb E[X]$}
{wiki}

For a nonnegative count with positive mean, the Fano factor compares its <variance> to its mean. A <Poisson distribution> has factor one. Correlated production bursts or weak restoring feedback can give factors larger than one even at large molecular copy number.

= Coefficient of variation
{title2=$\operatorname{CV}(X)$}
{parent=Variance}
{wiki}

For a random variable with nonzero mean, the coefficient of variation is the dimensionless ratio
$$
\operatorname{CV}(X)=\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}}{|\mathbb E X|}.
$$

= Variance as the minimum mean squared error of a constant
{parent=Variance}

For a square-integrable real random variable $X$,
$$
\mathbb E[(X-a)^2]
=\operatorname{Var}(X)+(\mathbb EX-a)^2.
$$
Thus the unique best constant predictor is $a=\mathbb EX$, and the minimum mean squared error is $\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

= Conditional variance
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)$}
{parent=Variance}
{wiki}

The conditional variance measures the remaining squared uncertainty after specified information has been used to form a <conditional mean>. For a real <square-integrable> <random variable> $X$ and a sub-<sigma-algebra> $\mathcal G$, let $m=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$. Then
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)
=\mathbb E[(X-m)^2\mid\mathcal G]
=\mathbb E[X^2\mid\mathcal G]-m^2.
$$
It is a $\mathcal G$-measurable <random variable>, nonnegative <almost surely>, rather than necessarily a single number. With the information $\mathcal G=\sigma(Y)$, its value is the <variance> in the group identified by $Y$. Its <expected value> is the <mean squared error> of predicting $X$ by its <conditional mean>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett/4.1 Conditional Expectation>[Theorem 4.1.15, p. 213]{ref}

In the exact <mixture distribution> in <law of total expectation>, group $A$ contains $0,4$ around mean two, and group $B$ contains $6,10$ around mean eight. Both groups have <conditional variance> four because their deviations from their own means are $-2,2$ with equal probabilities. Therefore $\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)]=4$. Meanwhile, the two group means have <variance> $\frac14(2-13/2)^2+\frac34(8-13/2)^2=27/4$. The <law of total variance> gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=4+\frac{27}{4}=\frac{43}{4}.}
$$
Direct calculation gives $\mathbb E[X^2]=53$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=53-(13/2)^2=43/4$, checking the decomposition.

\Image[conditional-variance-within-between-groups.png]
{title=Total variance separates remaining within-group error from variation of group means}
{description=Left: in the stipulated finite <mixture distribution>, each group's values deviate by $\pm2$ from its <conditional mean>. Right: the total <variance> $43/4$ separates into expected <conditional variance> four and <variance> of the <conditional mean> $27/4$. Knowing the group removes the latter part from the average squared prediction error. Values and weights are exact model choices, not measured data.}

For any square-integrable $\mathcal G$-measurable predictor $Z$, the error identity is
$$
\mathbb E[(X-Z)^2]=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)]+\mathbb E[(m-Z)^2].
$$
The cross term vanishes because $\mathbb E[X-m\mid\mathcal G]=0$. Thus using the <conditional mean> minimizes <mean squared error>, while <conditional variance> quantifies what this information still cannot predict. This is the <orthogonal projection> interpretation in <L2 space>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett/4.1 Conditional Expectation>{ref}

= Average conditional variance decreases with more information
{parent=Conditional variance}
{title2=$\mathbb E\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal H)-\mathbb E\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)=\mathbb E[(m_{\mathcal G}-m_{\mathcal H})^2]\geq0$}

For <square-integrable> $X$ and nested information $\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$, let $m_{\mathcal H}=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal H]$ and $m_{\mathcal G}=\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]$. The predictor $m_{\mathcal H}$ is also $\mathcal G$-measurable, so the <conditional variance> error identity gives
$$
\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal H)]-\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\mathcal G)]=\mathbb E[(m_{\mathcal G}-m_{\mathcal H})^2]\geq0.
$$
More information cannot increase the average best squared prediction error. Equality holds exactly when the two <conditional means> agree <almost surely>; additional information can change the <conditional distribution> without changing its mean.

This is an averaged statement. Take $X=-4,0,4$ with equal probabilities, and learn only whether $X$ is zero. The unconditional <variance> is $32/3$. In the nonzero group, the <conditional variance> is $16$, which is larger; in the zero group it is zero. Their weighted average remains $(2/3)16+(1/3)0=32/3$. Both groups have <conditional mean> zero, so the extra information has not improved squared-error prediction, even though it has revealed whether the outcome is extreme or central. An individual group's <conditional variance> need not decrease.

= Variance additivity for independent random variables
{parent=Variance}

For independent square-integrable random variables,
$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_iX_i\right)
=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i).
$$

= L2 convergence of averages of independent variables with bounded second moments
{title2=$L^2$ convergence of averages of independent variables with bounded second moments}
{parent=Variance additivity for independent random variables}

For independent random variables with $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|^2<\infty$, write
$$
Y_N=\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^NX_k,
\qquad
m_N=\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N\mathbb EX_k.
$$
Then $\lVert Y_N-m_N\rVert_2^2=N^{-2}\sum_{k\leq N}\operatorname{Var}(X_k)=O(N^{-1})$. Hence $Y_N$ converges in $L^2$ exactly when the scalar <Cesaro means> $m_N$ converge.

= Variance of a sum
{parent=Variance}

For square-integrable random variables,
$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_iX_i\right)
=\sum_i\sum_j\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
$$

= Dependent uniform sampling variance lower bound
{parent=Variance}

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ each be uniform on $[0,1]$, with arbitrary dependence. For centered unit-norm $f\in L^2[0,1]$, put
$$
D(f)=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf(Z_i)\right).
$$
Then
$$
\sup_fD(f)\ge\frac1n.
$$
Partition $[0,1]$ into $k$ equal intervals and test normalized differences of two interval indicators. Averaging over the two intervals reduces the claim to $\sum_pN_p^2\ge\sum_pN_p=n$ for their occupancy counts, and then let $k\to\infty$.

The standard deviation is the nonnegative square root $\sigma_X=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ of the <variance>.

= Covariance
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$}
{parent=Variance}
{wiki}

The <covariance> measures the average product of two centered deviations. For real <square-integrable> <random variables> $X$ and $Y$,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)(Y-\mathbb EY)]
=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E X\,\mathbb E Y.
$$
Deviations with the same sign contribute positively; deviations with opposite signs contribute negatively. Their magnitudes and <probabilities> determine the net result. Taking $Y=X$ recovers the <variance>. The definitions and algebraic properties are presented in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_class07-prep-b.pdf[Orloff and Bloom, MIT 18.05, Covariance and Correlation, sections 2–2.1].

For a worked example, let $X,Z$ be <independent random variables>, each equally likely to be $-1$, $0$ or $1$, and put $Y=2X+Z$. All nine pairs $(X,Z)$ have <probability> $1/9$. The <expected values> are zero, while <linearity of covariance> and the vanishing <covariance> of <independent random variables> give
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\frac23,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=2\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac43,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Y)=4\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\frac{10}{3}.
$$
The shared $X$ term makes large $X$ accompany large $Y$ on average. Replacing $2X$ by $-2X$ reverses the <covariance> to $-4/3$ without changing either <variance>.

\Image[covariance-and-dependence.png]
{title=Positive, negative, independent, and nonlinear relationships have different covariance behavior}
{description=In the first three panels, $X$ and $Z$ independently take $-1$, $0$ and $1$ with equal <probabilities>; each point has <probability> $1/9$. In the last panel, $Y=X^2$, and the three marked outcomes each have <probability> $1/3$. The thin curve shows that <quadratic function>. Its zero <covariance> leaves strong nonlinear <statistical dependence> visible.}

The <covariance> is useful whenever deviations are added or subtracted. Expanding the square gives
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)
=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)
=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)-2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).
$$
For the worked model, these are $20/3$ and $4/3$. Positive <covariance> increases variation in the sum while reducing variation in the difference: the same shared fluctuation reinforces one combination and partly cancels in the other. The identity applies beyond <independent random variables>.

The size of a <covariance> depends on the measurement scale. For constants $a,b,c,d$, <linearity of covariance> gives $\operatorname{Cov}(aX+b,cY+d)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$. Adding offsets has no effect; rescaling either measurement rescales the <covariance>. The <Pearson correlation coefficient> divides by both <standard deviations> to describe the standardized linear relationship.

= Law of total covariance
{parent=Covariance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y\mid\Lambda)+\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[X\mid\Lambda],\mathbb E[Y\mid\Lambda])$}
{wiki}

For square-integrable random variables, expand $X$ and $Y$ into their <conditional expectations> and their centered conditional residuals. The cross terms vanish by the <law of total expectation>, giving the displayed <covariance> decomposition. Conditional independence can therefore coexist with positive unconditional covariance from a shared <latent variable>.

= Strict positive covariance with an increasing payoff
{parent=Covariance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(g(Z),Z)>0$}

If $Z$ is nondegenerate, $g$ is strictly increasing and the relevant products are <integrable>, an independent copy $Z'$ gives $2\operatorname{Cov}(g(Z),Z)=\mathbb E[(g(Z)-g(Z'))(Z-Z')]$. The integrand is positive exactly when $Z\ne Z'$, so the <covariance> is strictly positive. The same argument applies within each conditional law and yields a positive terminal <stock> hedge for an attainable increasing payoff.

= Conditional covariance
{parent=Covariance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z\mid\mathcal G)$}

For square-integrable random variables, $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z\mid\mathcal G)=\mathbb E[XZ\mid\mathcal G]-\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal G]$. It is a $\mathcal G$-measurable random variable. Replacing $X$ by its <conditional expectation> given a larger sigma-field leaves this <covariance> unchanged when $Z$ is measurable with respect to that larger sigma-field. This observation underlies <recovery of a martingale-transform integrand by conditional covariance>.

= Sample correlation coefficient
{parent=Covariance}
{title2=$r_{XY}$}

= Sample correlation
{synonym}

For paired observations $(x_i,y_i)$, the <sample correlation coefficient> is
$$
r_{XY}=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)(y_i-\overline y)}
{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)^2\sum_i(y_i-\overline y)^2}}.
$$
It is the normalized <inner product> of the centered data <vectors>, or the <sample covariance> divided by the product of the corresponding sample <standard deviations>. The choice of divisor $n$ or $n-1$ cancels when used consistently in all three quantities. It is undefined if either centered data <vector> is zero, which occurs when that variable is constant in the observed <statistical sample>.

For $x=(1,2,3,4)$ and $y=(2,1,4,3)$, both <sample means> are $2.5$. The sum of centered products is $3$, and each sum of centered squares is $5$, hence **the sample correlation coefficient is $3/5=0.6$**. This is a summary of these four observed pairs; it is not automatically the population <Pearson correlation coefficient>. https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~stark/SticiGui/Text/computeR.htm[Philip Stark, Computing the correlation coefficient] explains the centered and standardized calculation.

= Sample correlation matrix
{parent=Sample correlation coefficient}
{title2=$R_{jk}=S_{jk}/\sqrt{S_{jj}S_{kk}}$}

A <sample correlation matrix> rescales a <sample covariance matrix> $S$ to unit diagonal, for variables with positive sample variance. Its off-diagonal entries are <sample correlation coefficients>. Strong correlations between predictors can indicate <multicollinearity>, but pairwise correlations alone do not exclude higher-order linear dependence.

= Complex covariance
{parent=Covariance}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb C}(Z,W)$}

= Hermitian covariance
{synonym}

For square-integrable complex <random variables>, the Hermitian covariance is $\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb C}(Z,W)=\mathbb E[(Z-\mathbb EZ)\overline{(W-\mathbb EW)}]$. Its diagonal is nonnegative and equals $\mathbb E|Z-\mathbb EZ|^2$. The conjugation distinguishes it from the <pseudo-covariance>.

= Pseudo-covariance
{parent=Complex covariance}
{title2=$P=\mathbb E(Z-\mathbb EZ)^2$}

= Pseudo-covariance of a complex random variable
{synonym}

For a centered complex <random variable> $Z=A+iB$, the pseudo-covariance is $P=\mathbb EZ^2$. Together with $V=\mathbb E|Z|^2$ it determines the real <covariance matrix>: $\mathbb EA^2=(V+\operatorname{Re}P)/2$, $\mathbb EB^2=(V-\operatorname{Re}P)/2$, and $\mathbb EAB=\operatorname{Im}P/2$.

= Linearity of covariance
{parent=Covariance}

Covariance is bilinear: for constants $a_i,b_j$ and square-integrable random variables,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\sum_i a_iX_i,\sum_jb_jY_j\right)
=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_j).
$$

= Uncorrelated random variables
{parent=Covariance}

Two <square-integrable> <random variables> $X,Y$ are <uncorrelated random variables> when $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$. <Independent random variables> with finite <second moments> are <uncorrelated random variables>, but zero <covariance> does not generally imply <independence>.

For an explicit counterexample, let $X$ take $-1$, $0$, $1$ with equal <probabilities>, and let $Y=X^2$. Then $\mathbb E X=\mathbb E X^3=0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E X^3-\mathbb E X\,\mathbb E X^2=0$. Nevertheless, $Y$ is completely determined by $X$: $\mathbb P(Y=0\mid X=0)=1$, while $\mathbb P(Y=0)=1/3$. Thus these are <uncorrelated random variables> with <statistical dependence>. In this example, opposite contributions from the <odd function> $x^3$ cancel; the <even function> $x^2$ still retains information about $|X|$. The last panel of the <covariance> diagram shows the relationship.

There are useful special cases. <Uncorrelated jointly normal variables are independent>, and the <Bernoulli independence from zero covariance> result supplies another setting where the converse holds. Their distribution assumptions do the extra work; zero <covariance> alone does not.

= Bernoulli independence from zero covariance
{c}
{parent=Uncorrelated random variables}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=ad-bc$}

For two <Bernoulli random variables> with joint probabilities $a,b,c,d$ in the order $(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)$, their <covariance> is $d-(c+d)(b+d)=ad-bc$. Zero covariance fixes the $(1,1)$ probability to the product of its marginals; subtracting this from each marginal fixes all three remaining joint probabilities to their products. Thus <uncorrelated random variables> taking only zero and one are independent, including degenerate marginals. The implication is special to this two-point setting and fails for general distributions.

= Covariance matrix
{parent=Covariance}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Sigma=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)(X-\mathbb EX)^T]$}

= Variance-covariance matrix
{synonym}

For a real <random vector> $X=(X_1,\ldots,X_p)^T$ with finite <second moments>, its <covariance matrix> collects all pairwise <covariances>:
$$
\Sigma=\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb EX)(X-\mathbb EX)^T],\qquad
\Sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
$$
The diagonal entries are <variances>. The <covariance matrix> is a <symmetric matrix> and a <positive semidefinite matrix>, because for every real <vector> $a$,
$$
a^T\Sigma a=\operatorname{Var}(a^TX)\geq0.
$$
Thus one <matrix> describes the <variance> of every <linear combination> of the coordinates. It is a <positive definite matrix> exactly when no nonzero <linear combination> is constant <almost surely>. A deterministic linear relation produces a zero <eigenvalue>.

For example,
$$
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.8\\0.8&1\end{pmatrix}
$$
has unit coordinate <variances>, <covariance> $0.8$, and <eigenvalues> $1.8$ and $0.2$. Its unit <eigenvectors> $(1,1)^T/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)^T/\sqrt2$ describe directions of greatest and least projected <variance>. For a unit <vector> $a=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$, direct multiplication gives $a^T\Sigma a=1+0.8\sin(2\theta)$: the variation in the sum direction is nine times that in the difference direction.

\Image[covariance-projection-geometry.png]
{title=A covariance matrix encodes projected variance in every direction}
{description=Left: the <covariance ellipse> has a <semi-major axis> of <length> $\sqrt{1.8}$ and a <semi-minor axis> of <length> $\sqrt{0.2}$ along the <eigenvectors> of $\Sigma$. Right: the <variance> of the unit-direction projection is $1+0.8\sin(2\theta)$. This is exact <matrix> geometry for the stipulated <covariance matrix>, with no sampled observations or claimed probability coverage.}

The <spectral theorem> supplies an <orthonormal basis> of these directions. This connects <covariance matrices> to <principal component analysis>: its leading <eigenvector> identifies the unit-direction projection with the largest <variance>. For a <linear transformation> $A$, the transformed <random vector> has <covariance matrix> $A\Sigma A^T$, so uncertainties propagate through a linear model by <matrix multiplication>.

= Covariance ellipse
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$x^T\Sigma^{-1}x=1$}

For a two-dimensional <positive definite> <covariance matrix> $\Sigma$, the centered <covariance ellipse> is the image of the <unit circle> under its positive definite <square root of a matrix>. Equivalently, it satisfies $x^T\Sigma^{-1}x=1$. Its <semi-major and semi-minor axes> have <lengths> given by the square roots of the larger and smaller <eigenvalues>, along the corresponding <eigenvectors>. The <ellipse> encodes a second-moment geometry; a probability-coverage interpretation needs additional information about the <probability distribution> and the chosen scale.

= Correlation matrix
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$C=D^{-1/2}\Sigma D^{-1/2}$}

For a random vector with <covariance> $\Sigma$ and positive coordinate <variances>, let $D=\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma)$. Its <correlation matrix> is the <covariance> of $D^{-1/2}(X-\mathbb EX)$, so it is positive semidefinite with unit diagonal. Each entry is the <correlation> of the corresponding pair of variables. In particular $\operatorname{tr}(C)=p$, explaining why an <eigenvalue> in correlation-based <principal component analysis> represents that <eigenvalue> divided by $p$ of the total <variance>. The empirical version is the <sample correlation matrix>.

= Equicorrelation covariance matrix
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$\Sigma=(1-r)I_p+r\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$}

This unit-variance <covariance matrix> has <eigenvalue> $1+(p-1)r$ on the constant-vector direction and <eigenvalue> $1-r$ on its orthogonal complement. It is positive semidefinite exactly when $-1/(p-1)\le r\le1$. For positive $r$, the first <population principal component> is the normalized sum of the centered variables.

= Mahalanobis distance
{c}
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$d_M(x,y)=\sqrt{(x-y)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-y)}$}

For a <positive-definite> <covariance matrix>, this is <Euclidean distance> after whitening. It accounts for both scales and correlations and is invariant under consistent nonsingular linear changes of coordinates. Estimating the covariance can be unstable with small samples, and a singular covariance requires restricting to a nonsingular subspace rather than claiming an ordinary <metric> on the full space.

= Compound-symmetry covariance
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$\Sigma=aI+cJ$}

A <compound-symmetry covariance> matrix has a common diagonal entry and a common off-diagonal entry: $\Sigma=aI+cJ$. For a group of size $k$, its within-group <eigenvalue> is $a$ and its constant-direction <eigenvalue> is $a+kc$. Thus $a\geq0$ and $a+kc\geq0$ characterize <positive semidefiniteness>. When $c\geq0$, an independent shared <random intercept> plus individual noise realizes this covariance. A group mean has <variance> $c+a/k$; a difference of two disjoint subgroup means within the same group cancels $c$.

= Rank-one covariance spike
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{title2=$\Sigma=I+\theta uu^\top$}

For a unit vector $u$ and $\theta>0$, a rank-one covariance spike is the <covariance matrix> $I+\theta uu^\top$. Its <eigenvalue> is $1+\theta$ in the direction $u$ and one on the <orthogonal complement>. It arises by adding an independent scalar <normal distribution> signal $Yu$ to a standard <Gaussian random vector>.

= Sample mean and covariance
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{wiki}

The sample mean and covariance estimate the first two moments of a <random vector> from repeated observations.

= Uncentered empirical second-moment matrix
{parent=Sample mean and covariance}
{title2=$\widehat Q=n^{-1}\sum_iX_iX_i^\top$}

The uncentered empirical second-moment matrix averages the outer products of the observations. Its <expected value> is $\operatorname{Cov}(X)+\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[X]^\top$. It is an <unbiased estimator> of the <covariance matrix> when the population <expected value> is known to be zero. It differs from a <sample covariance matrix> centered at the sample mean.

= Sample mean
{title2=$\overline X$}
{parent=Sample mean and covariance}
{wiki}

For observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the sample mean is $\overline X=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$.

= Sample covariance matrix
{title2=$S$}
{parent=Sample mean and covariance}

For observations $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the sample covariance matrix is $n^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)^T$, or the same sum divided by $n-1$ under the unbiased convention.

= Rank of a centered sample covariance matrix
{parent=Sample covariance matrix}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}S=\operatorname{rank}(HX)\leq\min(p,n-1)$}

If $H=I_n-\mathbf1\mathbf1^T/n$ and $S=X^THX/n$, then $\operatorname{rank}S=\operatorname{rank}(HX)\leq\min(p,n-1)$. In particular <full column rank> of raw $X$ does not ensure a nonsingular centered <sample covariance matrix>. When $S$ is singular, its Gaussian precision <profile likelihood> has no finite maximizer: increase precision along a nonzero null vector.

= Sample covariance
{parent=Sample covariance matrix}

For paired scalar observations $(x_i,y_i)$, their sample covariance is $n^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$, or the same sum divided by $n-1$ under the unbiased convention. It is an off-diagonal entry of the <sample covariance matrix> of the paired vectors.

= Effective rank of a covariance matrix
{title2=$r(\Sigma)=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)/\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$}
{parent=Sample covariance matrix}

The effective rank measures the total variance of a covariance matrix relative to its largest directional variance.

= Gaussian sample-covariance operator-norm bound
{parent=Effective rank of a covariance matrix}

For independent centered Gaussian vectors with covariance $\Sigma$ and uncentered sample covariance $\widehat\Sigma=n^{-1}\sum_ix_ix_i^T$, with probability at least $1-e^{-t}$,
$$
\lVert\widehat\Sigma-\Sigma\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
\leq C\lVert\Sigma\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
\left(\sqrt{\frac{r(\Sigma)+t}{n}}+\frac{r(\Sigma)+t}{n}\right).
$$

= Precision matrix
{title2=$\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}$}
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{wiki}

The precision matrix is the inverse of a nonsingular <covariance matrix>. For a <multivariate normal distribution>, a zero off-diagonal precision entry encodes conditional independence of the corresponding coordinates given all remaining coordinates.

= Gaussian conditional variance from precision
{c}
{parent=Precision matrix}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(X_i\mid X_{-i})=1/\Omega_{ii}$}

For a nonsingular <multivariate normal distribution> with <precision matrix> $\Omega$, fixing all coordinates except $X_i$ leaves a density proportional to $\exp[-\Omega_{ii}(x_i-m_i)^2/2]$ after completing the square. Thus its <conditional variance> is the reciprocal diagonal precision entry. This is conditioning on every other coordinate, not the marginal <variance> $(\Omega^{-1})_{ii}$.

= Conditional regression coefficients from a Gaussian precision matrix
{parent=Precision matrix}
{title2=$\theta_{jk}=-\Omega_{jk}/\Omega_{kk}$}

In a <Gaussian graphical model>, regressing component $k$ on the others gives the displayed coefficients, residual variance $1/\Omega_{kk}$ and intercept $\mu_k-\mu_{-k}^{\mathsf T}\theta_{-k,k}$. Opposite directions have identical zero patterns and signs because $\Omega$ is symmetric with positive diagonal, but generally unequal coefficient magnitudes.

= Profile likelihood for a Gaussian precision matrix
{parent=Precision matrix}
{title2=$-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)$}

For independent <multivariate normal> observations with unknown mean and nonsingular covariance, profiling the mean at its sample value gives negative log-likelihood proportional to $-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{tr}(S\Omega)$ over $\Omega\succ0$. The <sample covariance matrix> uses divisor $n$. Its minimizer exists exactly when $S\succ0$, and is then $S^{-1}$.

= Gaussian sampling from a precision Cholesky factor
{parent=Precision matrix}
{c}

If a <positive definite matrix> $Q=LL^T$ is the <precision matrix> of a <multivariate normal distribution>, draw independent $\xi\sim N(0,I)$ and solve $L^T\eta=\xi$. Then $m+\eta$ has covariance $L^{-T}L^{-1}=Q^{-1}$. This avoids forming the inverse and exploits a banded <Cholesky decomposition> when $Q$ is banded.

= CLIME precision-matrix estimator
{c}
{parent=Precision matrix}

The constrained $\ell^1$ minimization estimator minimizes $\lVert\Omega\rVert_1$ subject to
$$
\lVert\widehat\Sigma\Omega-I\rVert_{\max}\leq\lambda.
$$
Its constraints and objective separate by columns. Feasibility of the true precision matrix and elementary matrix-norm inequalities yield entrywise error bounds under an entrywise covariance-estimation bound.

= Graphical Lasso
{c}
{parent=Precision matrix}
{wiki}

The graphical Lasso estimates a sparse <precision matrix> by minimizing a Gaussian negative log-likelihood plus an entrywise $\ell^1$ penalty.

= Graphical Lasso column regression
{parent=Graphical Lasso}
{c}
{title2=$b=-\widehat\Omega_{-j,j}/\widehat\Omega_{jj}$}

For a <graphical Lasso> solution with inverse $\widehat\Sigma$, the unique minimizer of $\tfrac12\|Wb-W^{-1}S_{-j,j}\|_2^2+\lambda\|b\|_1$, $W^2=\widehat\Sigma_{-j,-j}\succ0$, is $b=-\widehat\Omega_{-j,j}/\widehat\Omega_{jj}$. The inverse identity gives $\widehat\Sigma_{-j,j}=\widehat\Sigma_{-j,-j}b$, and the off-diagonal <graphical-Lasso Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> become the column <Lasso> conditions because the corresponding signs are opposite.

= Graphical-Lasso Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions
{c}

For objective $-\log\det\Omega+\operatorname{Tr}(S\Omega)+\lambda\lVert\Omega\rVert_{1,\mathrm{entry}}$, the KKT conditions are $-\Omega^{-1}+S+\lambda Z=0$ with $Z_{ij}\in\partial|\Omega_{ij}|$.

= Pearson correlation coefficient
{c}
{title2=$\rho_{X,Y}$}
{parent=Covariance matrix}
{wiki}

= Correlation coefficient
{synonym}

= Correlation
{synonym}

The <Pearson correlation coefficient> is the <covariance> divided by the product of the <standard deviations>. For real <random variables> with finite, strictly positive <variances>,
$$
\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}.
$$
It is the <expected value> of the product of the two centered variables after dividing each by its <standard deviation>. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $-1\leq\rho_{X,Y}\leq1$. This removes measurement units, while retaining the direction and strength of the linear relationship. Constant <random variables> have zero <standard deviation>, so their <Pearson correlation coefficient> is undefined.

For the <covariance> model $Y=2X+Z$, with $X,Z$ independently uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$, the calculation gives
$$
\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{4/3}{\sqrt{(2/3)(10/3)}}=\frac2{\sqrt5}\approx0.894427.
$$
The positive <correlation> is less than one because the independent $Z$ term introduces extra variation. For $Y=-2X+Z$ the <correlation> is $-2/\sqrt5$. Both examples appear in the <covariance> diagram.

For nonzero real $a,c$, the <correlation> of $aX+b$ and $cY+d$ is $\operatorname{sgn}(ac)\rho_{X,Y}$. Positive rescaling and added offsets therefore leave the <correlation> unchanged; reversing one scale reverses its sign. Equality $|\rho_{X,Y}|=1$ means that one <random variable> is an <affine function> of the other <almost surely>, with a nonzero slope of the corresponding sign. This is the equality condition in the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, and includes <perfect positive correlation>.

Zero <correlation> says that the centered variables are <orthogonal> in their square-integrable <inner product>. It permits nonlinear <statistical dependence>, as the example $Y=X^2$ in <uncorrelated random variables> demonstrates. More generally, a <correlation> is a statement about a <joint probability distribution>; identifying effects of interventions is the separate task of <causal inference>. This distinction keeps an observed association from being mistaken for a demonstrated mechanism.

An early source for standardized association is <Francis Galton>'s 1888 paper “Co-relations and their Measurement, chiefly from Anthropometric Data”. He compared stature and forearm measurements and changed each measurement to units of its own variability so the two regression directions could be compared. His scale used <probable error>, so it should not be identified word for word with the modern <standard deviation> formula above. https://galton.org/essays/1880-1889/galton-1888-co-relations-royal-soc/galton_corr.html[Galton's original paper, Proceedings of the Royal Society 45, 135–145]{ref}.

= Conditional correlation
{parent=Pearson correlation coefficient}
{title2=$\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y\mid Z)$}

The <correlation coefficient> computed under a <conditional distribution>, using its <conditional covariance> and positive <conditional variances>. It can depend on the observed conditioning value. For a <multivariate normal distribution>, conditioning on other coordinates gives the corresponding <partial correlation>.

= Partial correlation
{parent=Pearson correlation coefficient}
{title2=$\rho_{12\cdot3}=(\rho_{12}-\rho_{13}\rho_{23})/\sqrt{(1-\rho_{13}^2)(1-\rho_{23}^2)}$}

The partial <correlation> is the <correlation> of residuals after linearly projecting each of two variables on the conditioning variables and an intercept. With nonsingular <covariance matrix>, subtracting the two least-squares predictors gives residual <covariance> $\sigma_{12}-\sigma_{13}\sigma_{23}/\sigma_{33}$ and residual <variances> $\sigma_{ii}-\sigma_{i3}^2/\sigma_{33}$. Dividing gives the displayed formula. For a <multivariate normal distribution>, it is also the <conditional correlation>, independent of the conditioning value; this equality need not hold for general distributions.

= Perfect positive correlation
{parent=Pearson correlation coefficient}
{title2=$\rho(X,Y)=1$}

For square-integrable variables of positive <variance>, correlation one is equivalent to $X-\mathbb EX=c(Y-\mathbb EY)$ with $c>0$. The squared norm of the difference between the two standardized centered variables is $2(1-\rho)$, proving the equivalence. For uncentered variables the relationship can have a nonzero intercept; it need not be proportionality.

= Terminal proportionality identifies bounded stock volatility
{parent=Perfect positive correlation}

For two strictly positive zero-rate stock <martingales> with bounded volatilities and the same initial value, proportional terminal prices must be equal because their <expectations> coincide. <Conditional expectation> then makes the whole paths equal. Comparing their stochastic integrals by the <Itô isometry> gives equality of the volatility coefficients almost everywhere in time and probability. <Perfect positive correlation> alone does not provide the required proportionality.

= Terminal correlation does not identify adapted stock volatility
{parent=Perfect positive correlation}

The stocks $S_t=e^{B_t-t/2}$ and $S'_t=(1+S_t)/2$ start at one, are true square-integrable zero-rate <martingales>, and have <perfect positive correlation> at each positive time. Their bounded multiplicative volatilities are $1$ and $S_t/(1+S_t)$, which differ throughout. The additive intercept is what allows the same correlation but different stochastic-exponential coefficients.

= Serial correlation
{parent=Pearson correlation coefficient}
{title2=$\operatorname{Corr}(Y_s,Y_t)$}

Responses observed on the same process or individual at different times may have nonzero <correlation coefficients>. Such serial correlation can arise from a shared <random effect> or from history-dependent conditional means. A scalar <dispersion parameter> adjustment alone does not represent this temporal dependence.

= Intraclass correlation coefficient
{title2=ICC}
{parent=Pearson correlation coefficient}
{wiki}

= Intraclass correlation
{synonym}

The intraclass correlation coefficient measures the correlation between observations in the same group. In a random-intercept model with group variance $\sigma_G^2$ and individual variance $\sigma_I^2$, it is $\sigma_G^2/(\sigma_G^2+\sigma_I^2)$.

= Popoviciu inequality on variances
{c}
{synonym}

If a <random variable> $X$ takes values in $[m,M]$, then
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)\leq\frac{(M-m)^2}{4}.
$$
