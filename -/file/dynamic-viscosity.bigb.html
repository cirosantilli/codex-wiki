dynamic-viscosity.bigb
= Dynamic viscosity
{title2=$\mu$}

= Viscosity
{synonym}

= Shear viscosity
{synonym}

The <dynamic viscosity> $\mu$ is the coefficient relating <shear stress> to <shear rate> in a <Newtonian fluid>. For a <simple shear flow> $\mathbf u=(u(y),0,0)$,
$$
\tau_{xy}=\mu\frac{du}{dy}=\mu\dot\gamma,\qquad
[\mu]=\mathrm{Pa\,s}=\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.
$$
Here $\tau_{xy}$ is the $x$ component of viscous <traction> on a plane with <normal vector> $+\mathbf e_y$. The <shear rate> $\dot\gamma=du/dy$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Thus <dynamic viscosity> measures the <stress> required to sustain a given rate of deformation. It is the shear coefficient; the separate coefficient for <volume> change is <volume viscosity>. https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~uceseug/Fluids2/Slides_1.pdf[UCL, Fluid Mechanics II, Forces in Fluids, slides 4–5]{ref}

For a worked <Couette flow> example, place a <Newtonian fluid> of constant <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=0.10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ between parallel plates separated by $h=0.0010\,\mathrm m$. Hold the lower plate fixed and move the upper plate at $U=0.020\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Assume <steady flow>, no streamwise <pressure gradient>, and the <no-slip boundary condition>. Then
$$
u(y)=\frac{Uy}{h},\qquad
\dot\gamma=\frac Uh=20\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\tau_{xy}=\frac{\mu U}{h}=2.0\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
The fluid pulls backward on the moving plate with <force> magnitude $F=\tau_{xy}A$, where $A$ is its wetted <area>. For $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$ this is $0.020\,\mathrm N$. Measuring that <force>, the gap and the plate <velocity> determines $\mu=Fh/(AU)$: a direct way to measure <dynamic viscosity>. The linear profile and opposing wall <tractions> are the standard plane <Couette flow> solution. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-25-advanced-fluid-mechanics-fall-2013/1a114d602956fa0dd328155f9b45f93d_MIT2_25F13_Couet_and_Pois.pdf[MIT, Couette and Poiseuille Flows, p. 1]{ref}

\Image[viscosity-couette-shear.png]
{title=Couette flow: a constant velocity gradient produces a constant shear stress}
{description=Blue arrows show the linear velocity profile between the plates. Orange arrows show viscous traction on the upper and lower faces of a fluid slice; reversing the surface normal reverses the traction. The slice has zero net viscous force although each face carries stress.}

The <velocity gradient> matters, rather than the <velocity> itself. Adding the same constant <velocity> to both plates and the fluid leaves $du/dy$, and hence <shear stress>, unchanged. In <uniform flow>, every layer translates together and $du/dy=0$. More generally, even a nonzero <velocity gradient> need not generate <viscous stress>: <solid-body rotation> has zero <strain-rate tensor>, as the <viscous stress tensor> example shows. Thin gaps amplify <shear stress> for a fixed plate-speed difference, making <dynamic viscosity> useful for predicting the <force> needed to move lubricated surfaces.

= Viscous drag coefficient
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{title2=$\mathbf F_{\rm drag}=-\gamma\mathbf v$}

The proportionality between drag <force> and relative <velocity> in a linear low-speed regime. Its translational units are $\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$; thermal equilibrium gives the <Einstein diffusion relation> $D=k_BT/\gamma$.

= Lateral viscosity
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{title2=$\nu_h$}

An effective horizontal transport coefficient for <momentum>, often representing unresolved <eddies> in ocean models. A simple constant-coefficient closure adds $\nu_h\nabla_h^2\mathbf u$ to the <Cauchy momentum equation> and $\nu_h\nabla_h^2\xi$ to the <vorticity equation>. Its balance with the beta effect sets the width of a <Munk boundary layer>.

= Zero-shear viscosity
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{title2=$\mu=\int_0^\infty G(s)\,ds$}

For an integrable <relaxation modulus>, a small constant shear rate sustained from the remote past gives stress equal to that rate times the displayed integral, including any instantaneous solvent mass. For the linear <Oldroyd-B model> it is $\mu_0+G_0\tau$. A nondecaying elastic modulus would make this integral infinite rather than describe a fluid with finite steady viscosity.

= Kinematic viscosity
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{title2=$\nu=\mu/\rho$}

The <kinematic viscosity> is <dynamic viscosity> divided by <mass density>:
$$
\nu=\frac\mu\rho,\qquad [\nu]=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.
$$
It measures the rate at which viscous transport smooths differences in <velocity>. For constant $\mu$ and $\rho$ in <incompressible flow>, dividing the <Navier-Stokes equation> by <mass density> makes the viscous acceleration $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u$, where $\nabla^2$ is the <Laplacian>. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/fluids/fluids.pdf[David Tong, Fluid Mechanics, section 3.1.1, p. 61]{ref}

For example, stipulate two <Newtonian fluids> with the same <dynamic viscosity> $0.10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ but different <mass densities>. In the same steady <Couette flow> as above, both have <shear stress> $2.0\,\mathrm{Pa}$, but
$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_1&=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},&\nu_1&=1.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}},\\
\rho_2&=500\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},&\nu_2&=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The same <viscous stress> transports <momentum> through less <mass> in the second fluid, so its <velocity> responds faster to a given nonuniform viscous <force>.

To see the <diffusion equation> directly, consider an unsteady parallel <velocity field> $\mathbf u=(u(y,t),0,0)$ with constant material coefficients and no streamwise <pressure gradient> or body <force>. Its <convective acceleration> vanishes, and the <Navier-Stokes equation> reduces to
$$
\partial_tu=\nu\partial_y^2u.
$$
Balancing $u/t$ against $\nu u/h^2$ gives the <viscous diffusion time> $t_\nu\sim h^2/\nu$. For the $1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ gap, the two fluids have scales $0.010\,\mathrm s$ and $0.0050\,\mathrm s$. This is a characteristic scale, not an exact settling time. For example, a perturbation of the <Couette flow> profile satisfying zero perturbation <velocity> at both plates is
$$
\delta u(y,t)=V\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{h}\right)
\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^2\nu t}{h^2}\right).
$$
Substituting verifies the <diffusion equation>; its amplitude falls by a factor $e$ in $h^2/(\pi^2\nu)$. <Kinematic viscosity> is therefore useful for predicting how quickly a moving wall influences the interior, while <dynamic viscosity> gives the <shear stress> at a prescribed <shear rate>.

= Momentum diffusion
{parent=Kinematic viscosity}
{title2=$\partial_t u=\nu\partial_z^2u$}

Viscous transfer of tangential <momentum> smooths gradients of <velocity>. For unidirectional incompressible flow without a <pressure gradient>, the <Navier-Stokes equation> reduces to the displayed <diffusion equation>, whose diffusion coefficient is the <kinematic viscosity> $\nu=\eta/\rho$.

= Density-weighted mean kinematic viscosity
{parent=Kinematic viscosity}
{title2=$\bar\nu=\Sigma^{-1}\int\rho\nu\,dz$}

For a vertically stratified <accretion disk> with <surface density> $\Sigma=\int\rho\,dz$, the density-weighted mean <kinematic viscosity> is $\bar\nu=\Sigma^{-1}\int\rho\nu\,dz$. In an <alpha disk> with $\nu=\alpha p/(\rho\Omega)$, this becomes $\bar\nu=\alpha(\Sigma\Omega)^{-1}\int p\,dz$.

= Density-weighted viscosity of a disk
{parent=Kinematic viscosity}
{title2=$\bar\nu=\Sigma^{-1}\int\rho\nu\,dz$}

If angular <velocity> is independent of height, integrating the viscous stress $\rho\nu r\Omega'$ gives $\Sigma\bar\nu r\Omega'$. Thus the <surface density> weighted average, rather than an unweighted height average, enters <vertically averaged viscous disk equations>.

= Viscous stress tensor
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}=2\mu D$}

= Viscous stress
{synonym}

The <viscous stress tensor> is the part of the <Cauchy stress tensor> associated with deformation rates. For an isotropic <Newtonian fluid> in <incompressible flow>,
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma=-pI+\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}},\qquad
\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}=2\mu D,\qquad
D=\frac12\left[\nabla\mathbf u+(\nabla\mathbf u)^T\right],\qquad
\operatorname{tr}D=\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Here $p$ is <pressure>, $I$ is the <identity matrix>, $\mu$ is <dynamic viscosity>, and $D$ is the <strain-rate tensor>. The convention is $(\nabla\mathbf u)_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$; $T$ denotes <transpose> and $\operatorname{tr}$ denotes <trace>. Across a plane with unit <normal vector> $\mathbf n$, the viscous <traction> is $\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}\mathbf n$. The <tensor> makes this rule work for any plane orientation. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/fluids/fluids.pdf[David Tong, Fluid Mechanics, section 3.1.1, equations (3.5)–(3.6)]{ref}

In the worked <Couette flow>, $\mathbf u=(Uy/h,0,0)$, so
$$
D=\frac{U}{2h}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&0\\2&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
In particular, $D_{xy}=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ while the <shear rate> is $20\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; the factor $2$ in $2\mu D$ recovers $\tau_{xy}=2\,\mathrm{Pa}$. On the upper face of a fluid slice, $\mathbf n=\mathbf e_y$ gives viscous <traction> $+2\mathbf e_x\,\mathrm{Pa}$; on the lower face it is $-2\mathbf e_x\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Constant <stress> can therefore transmit a <force> between the plates even though its <divergence>, and the net viscous <force> on each slice, vanish.

Compare planar extension $\mathbf u=(ax,-ay,0)$ with <solid-body rotation> $\mathbf u=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)$:
$$
D_{\mathrm{extension}}=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&0\\0&-a&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\nabla\mathbf u_{\mathrm{rotation}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Omega&0\\\Omega&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D_{\mathrm{rotation}}=0.
$$
Extension changes lengths and produces viscous <normal stresses> $\pm2\mu a$. Rotation preserves distances and gives zero <viscous stress>, despite its nonzero <velocity gradient> and <vorticity> $2\Omega\mathbf e_z$. For $a=\Omega=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\mu=0.10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, extension gives $\sigma_{\mathrm{visc},xx}=0.20\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{visc},yy}=-0.20\,\mathrm{Pa}$; rotation gives zero. A rotating fluid may still have a <pressure gradient> supplying its centripetal <acceleration>.

{title=Deformation produces Newtonian viscous stress; rigid rotation does not}
{description=Blue arrows show instantaneous velocity. The dashed square is a material patch at $t=0$; its orange outline at $t=0.65\,\mathrm s$ is obtained from the exact trajectories of planar extension and solid-body rotation, with $a=\Omega=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The marked corner makes rotation visible. Both motions preserve area, but only extension changes the patch's shape.}

For an isotropic <Newtonian fluid> in three-dimensional <compressible flow>, separate shape change from <volume> change:
$$
\boldsymbol\sigma_{\mathrm{visc}}
=2\mu\left(D-\frac{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u}{3}I\right)
+\zeta(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)I.
$$
The first term is <deviatoric stress>, with zero <trace>; $\zeta$ is <volume viscosity>, also called <bulk viscosity>. For uniform expansion $\mathbf u=b\mathbf x$, $D=bI$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=3b$, so the shear contribution vanishes and the viscous <stress> is $3\zeta bI$. Thus <dynamic viscosity> and <volume viscosity> describe distinct responses, and <incompressible flow> removes only the volume-change term. https://notes.shreyasmandre.com/fluid-dynamics-notes/Ch2.html[Shreyas Mandre, Fluid Dynamics Lecture Notes, section 2.11.3, equation (2.49)]{ref}

= Viscous force
{parent=Viscous stress tensor}
{title2=$\mathbf F_{\mathrm{visc}}=\int_{\partial V}\boldsymbol\tau\mathbf n\,dS$}

The viscous force on a fluid region is the resultant of the <traction> supplied by the <viscous stress tensor> $\boldsymbol\tau$. With $\mathbf n$ the outward <unit normal> of the region, its surface contribution is the displayed <surface integral>. For an incompressible <Newtonian fluid>, $\boldsymbol\tau=\eta[\nabla\mathbf u+(\nabla\mathbf u)^T]$, where $\eta$ is <dynamic viscosity> and $\mathbf u$ is <velocity>. A force scale is $\eta UL$ for a body of size $L$ moving at speed $U$, while the inertial force scale is $\rho U^2L^2$; their ratio is the <Reynolds number>. Total hydrodynamic <drag> on a body includes both viscous traction and the pressure traction induced by the flow. For example, the full <Stokes drag law> gives $6\pi\eta RU$ for a no-slip sphere, rather than just the viscous-traction part alone.

= Reynolds number
{parent=Dynamic viscosity}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu=UL/\nu$}

The <Reynolds number> $\operatorname{Re}=UL/\nu$ compares inertial and viscous effects. Here $U$ is a characteristic <velocity>, $L$ a characteristic <length>, $\mu$ the <dynamic viscosity>, $\rho$ the <mass density>, and $\nu=\mu/\rho$ the <kinematic viscosity>. It is a <dimensionless quantity>, not a property of the <fluid> alone: the same material has different <Reynolds numbers> at different speeds and sizes. Specify the chosen scales when reporting it.

The comparison comes from the <Navier-Stokes equation>. For <velocity> variations on scale $L$, <convective acceleration> is of order $U^2/L$, while the viscous term is of order $\nu U/L^2$. Their ratio is $UL/\nu$. Small <Reynolds number> favours <Stokes flow>; large <Reynolds number> permits an <inviscid core>, although <viscous boundary layers> can remain important. Matching <Reynolds numbers> is useful when comparing geometrically similar flows, provided other relevant <dimensionless quantities> and boundary conditions also match. https://uclnatsci.github.io/2021/Electromagnetism-Fluids-and-Waves/fluids/scaling.html[UCL, Scaling arguments, section 34.1.1]{ref}

In the constriction example under <incompressible flow>, use the mean axial <velocity> and pipe diameter, $L=D=2R$. Taking $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and a stipulated <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=0.001\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ gives
$$
\mathrm{Re}_1=\frac{1000(0.20)(0.020)}{0.001}=4000,\qquad
\mathrm{Re}_2=\frac{1000(0.80)(0.010)}{0.001}=8000.
$$
Halving the radius at fixed <volumetric flow rate> doubles this diameter-based <Reynolds number>, because mean <velocity> increases fourfold. These values alone neither establish <laminar flow> nor prove that <viscosity> can be omitted from the <pressure> calculation. Increasing $\mu$ to $0.10\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$ with the same imposed flow gives $40$ and $80$, but strengthens viscous effects; small <Reynolds number> does not justify <Bernoulli's principle>.

The name recalls Osborne Reynolds's 1883 experimental investigation of water motion and resistance in pipes. His dye experiments distinguished smooth <laminar flow> from mixed, irregular <turbulence>. https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1362544419765899904[Reynolds, 1883, original paper abstract and bibliographic record]{ref} https://uclnatsci.github.io/2021/Electromagnetism-Fluids-and-Waves/fluids/scaling.html[UCL, Scaling arguments, section 34.1.4]{ref} There is no universal transition value applicable to every geometry and disturbance environment. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/hkm2/PDFs/Moffatt_1977_GordonandBreach_Slogfdatohdt_149.pdf[H. K. Moffatt, transition discussion, p. 208]{ref}
