= Solution

Assume $A$ is nonempty, as is necessary for an approximating point to exist. The <reverse triangle inequality> gives
$$
|d(u,q)-d(u,r)|\leq d(q,r).
$$
Thus the distance from the fixed point $u$ is a continuous, indeed <Lipschitz continuous>, real-valued function on $A$. A <continuous function> on a nonempty <compact set> attains its <minimum>, so some $p\in A$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{d(u,p)=\min_{q\in A}d(u,q)=\inf_{q\in A}d(u,q).}
$$
If empty compact sets are allowed, the printed statement needs this nonemptiness qualification.

= Solution

The <vector subspace> contains zero, whose approximation error is $\|u\|$. For any $q$ with $\|q\|>2\|u\|$, the <triangle inequality> gives $\|u-q\|\geq\|q\|-\|u\|>\|u\|$, so such points cannot improve on zero.

Restrict to $K=\{q\in A:\|q\|\leq2\|u\|+1\}$. This is nonempty, closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional <normed space>, hence compact. The <continuous function> $q\mapsto\|u-q\|$ attains a <minimum> on $K$ by part (a). Outside $K$ its value exceeds the error at zero, so that minimizer is best on all of $A$:
$$
\boxed{\|u-p\|\leq\|u-q\|\quad(q\in A).}
$$
Completeness of the ambient space is unnecessary; <compactness> is used only in the finite-dimensional <vector subspace>.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The geometry of a strictly convex <normed space> proves <uniqueness> whenever a <best approximation> exists. If distinct $p_1,p_2$ both have best error $E>0$, then their two residuals have <norm> $E$. The defining midpoint inequality gives
$$
\left\|u-\frac{p_1+p_2}{2}\right\|
=\left\|\frac{(u-p_1)+(u-p_2)}2\right\|<E,
$$
contradicting <best approximation> because the midpoint lies in $A$. If $E=0$, both approximants equal $u$.

The printed <existence> assertion is false for an arbitrary <vector subspace>. For a concrete counterexample take $B=\ell^2$ with its strictly convex Hilbert <norm>, let $A=c_{00}$ be its finite-support sequences, and take $u=(2^{-j})_{j\geq1}$. Truncations in $A$ tend to $u$, so the best-error <infimum> is zero, but $u\notin A$ and that <infimum> is not attained. If the finite-dimensional assumption of part (b) is retained, it supplies <existence> and the argument above supplies <uniqueness>.

For the estimate, suppose $Y$ is a <projection operator> onto $A$, as needed for a finite <operator norm>. Since $Yp=p$,
$$
u-Yu=(u-p)-Y(u-p).
$$
The <triangle inequality> and <operator norm> give
$$
\boxed{\|u-Yu\|\leq(1+\|Y\|)\|u-p\|
=(1+\|Y\|)E.}
$$
The estimate actually holds without attainment: apply it with any $q\in A$ instead of $p$ and take the <infimum>. Its proof applies in every <normed space>. This is <strict convexity separates uniqueness from existence in best approximation>.

Write the <Fourier coefficients> as $c_k=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u(t)e^{-ikt}dt$. The partial sum through frequencies $-n,\ldots,n$ is therefore a <convolution> with
$$
D_n(\theta)=\sum_{k=-n}^ne^{ik\theta}
=\frac{\sin((n+1/2)\theta)}{\sin(\theta/2)}.
$$
The last expression follows by summing a finite <geometric series>, with its removable value $2n+1$ at zero. Periodicity and the evenness of $D_n$ give
$$
\boxed{s_n(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
u(x+\theta)D_n(\theta)d\theta.}
$$
This also proves linearity and that the image lies in $F_n$. We use the continuous <periodic function> space, consistent with the premise and the displayed maximum <norm>.

The <convolution> formula gives the upper bound
$$
\|S_nu\|_\infty\leq\frac{\|u\|_\infty}{2\pi}
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|D_n(\theta)|d\theta.
$$
To prove sharpness within continuous functions, take
$$
u_\epsilon(\theta)=\frac{D_n(\theta)}
{\sqrt{D_n(\theta)^2+\epsilon^2}}.
$$
It is continuous and periodic, with <norm> at most one. At $x=0$, dominated convergence gives $(S_nu_\epsilon)(0)\to(2\pi)^{-1}\int|D_n|$. Hence that upper bound is the actual <operator norm>. Evenness yields
$$
\boxed{\|S_n\|=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi
\left|\frac{\sin((n+1/2)\theta)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\right|d\theta.}
$$
The approximating continuous signs avoid assuming that the discontinuous sign of the kernel belongs to the domain space.

Use the usual approximation-space convention $P_n=\{p:\deg p\leq n\}$. The source's phrase “degree $n$” must include lower degrees for its later space and <interpolation> statements; the consequence of a literal exact-degree reading is addressed in part (d).

Order the alternating points as $x_0<\cdots<x_{n+1}$. If $q$ had strictly smaller error than $E_n$, the values
$$
(q-p)(x_j)=(u-p)(x_j)-(u-q)(x_j)
$$
would have the alternating signs of $(u-p)(x_j)$: the second term has magnitude strictly less than $E_n$. The <intermediate value theorem> would then give at least $n+1$ distinct zeros of $q-p$, one in each gap. A nonzero <polynomial> of degree at most $n$ cannot have that many zeros. Thus no such $q$ exists, proving
$$
\boxed{\|u-p\|_\infty\leq\|u-q\|_\infty\quad(q\in P_n).}
$$
The remainder of the equioscillation theorem says that a best uniform approximating <polynomial> exists, is unique, and is characterized by this $n+2$-point alternating extreme-error condition. For positive error, alternation is necessary as well as sufficient. If $u$ itself lies in $P_n$, the unique best <polynomial> is $u$ with zero error.

Let $\omega(t)=\prod_{r=0}^n(t-x_r)$. For $x$ not one of the nodes, put $c=(u(x)-p_n(x))/\omega(x)$ and define
$$
F(t)=u(t)-p_n(t)-c\omega(t).
$$
This function has zeros at the $n+1$ <interpolation> nodes and at the additional point $x$. Repeated <Rolle's theorem> gives a point $\eta$ between the outermost of these points such that $F^{(n+1)}(\eta)=0$. Since $p_n^{(n+1)}=0$ and $\omega^{(n+1)}=(n+1)!$,
$$
c=\frac{u^{(n+1)}(\eta)}{(n+1)!}.
$$
Substitution yields
$$
\boxed{u(x)-p_n(x)=
\frac{u^{(n+1)}(\eta)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{r=0}^n(x-x_r).}
$$
At an <interpolation> node both sides are zero, so any $\eta\in[-1,1]$ gives the identity there. The assumed $C^{n+1}$ regularity permits all applications of <Rolle's theorem>.

Choose the nodes as the $n+1$ roots of the <Chebyshev polynomial> $T_{n+1}$. Its leading coefficient is $2^n$, so the monic nodal product is $\omega=2^{-n}T_{n+1}$. Since $|T_{n+1}|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$, part (b) bounds this particular interpolant by
$$
\|u-p_n\|_\infty\leq
\frac{\|u^{(n+1)}\|_\infty}{2^n(n+1)!}.
$$
The best error cannot exceed the error of a particular member of $P_n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_n\leq
\frac{\|u^{(n+1)}\|_\infty}{2^n(n+1)!}.}
$$
The choice of Chebyshev roots controls the maximum of the nodal product, not the size of any individual <interpolation> coefficient.

The <polynomial> $T_6$ is
$$
T_6(x)=32x^6-48x^4+18x^2-1.
$$
Subtract its monic version from $x^6$:
$$
\boxed{p(x)=x^6-\frac1{32}T_6(x)
=\frac32x^4-\frac9{16}x^2+\frac1{32}.}
$$
This has degree four and hence belongs to the usual space $P_5$. The error is $T_6/32$, with <norm> $1/32$ and alternating extrema at the seven points $\cos(j\pi/6)$, $j=0,\ldots,6$. Part (a), followed by <uniqueness>, proves
$$
\boxed{\|x^6-p\|_\infty=\frac1{32}}
$$
and that this is the unique <best approximation> of degree at most five.

If “degree five” is taken literally to exclude degree four, no <best approximation> exists in that nonclosed class: $p+\epsilon x^5$ approaches the error $1/32$ as $\epsilon\to0$, but <uniqueness> in $P_5$ excludes attainment by any degree-five <polynomial>. This is why the standard at-most-degree convention matters here.

If $(u-p,q)=0$ for all $q\in A$, then for any $a\in A$ the difference $p-a$ lies in $A$ and <Pythagorean theorem> gives
$$
\|u-a\|_2^2=\|(u-p)+(p-a)\|_2^2
=\|u-p\|_2^2+\|p-a\|_2^2\geq\|u-p\|_2^2.
$$
Thus $p$ is best.

Conversely, if $p$ is best, then for every $q\in A$ the real quadratic
$$
\|u-p-tq\|_2^2
=\|u-p\|_2^2-2t(u-p,q)+t^2\|q\|_2^2
$$
has its <minimum> at $t=0$. Its linear coefficient must vanish, giving $(u-p,q)=0$. Hence **<best approximation> is equivalent to <orthogonality> of the residual to the approximation space**. The weight is positive almost everywhere, so this is a genuine positive-definite <inner product> on continuous functions.

Let $\beta(q)$ be the centered nondimensional cubic <B-spline>, even and supported on $[-2,2]$. The outer cubic must be $b(2-|q|)^3$ to make the function and its first two <derivatives> vanish at the support edge. Write its inner part as $A+Bq^2+C|q|^3$. Matching the value and first two <derivatives> at $|q|=1$ gives $A=4b$, $B=-6b$, $C=3b$. Its nonzero knot values are then $4b,b,b$, so requiring their sum to be one fixes $b=1/6$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\beta(q)=
\begin{cases}
(4-6q^2+3|q|^3)/6,&|q|\leq1,\\
(2-|q|)^3/6,&1\leq|q|\leq2,\\
0,&|q|\geq2.
\end{cases}}
$$
In particular $\beta(0)=2/3$, $\beta(\pm1)=1/6$.

With $h=1/m$, represent the <spline> on $[0,1]$ as $s(x)=\sum_{j=-1}^{m+1}c_j\beta(x/h-j)$. There are $m+3$ unknown coefficients. <interpolation> at $x_i=ih$, $0\leq i\leq m$, gives
$$
c_{i-1}+4c_i+c_{i+1}=6\sqrt{1-ih}.
$$
The natural endpoint conditions, using $\beta''(0)=-2$ and $\beta''(\pm1)=1$, give
$$
c_{-1}-2c_0+c_1=0,\qquad
c_{m-1}-2c_m+c_{m+1}=0.
$$
These $m+3$ equations are the required <matrix> system: the $m+1$ <interpolation> rows have coefficients $(1,4,1)$ at consecutive positions and right sides $6u(x_i)$; the two endpoint rows have $(1,-2,1)$ and zero right sides. No solution of that system is required. The scaling uses $\beta(x/h-j)$, without an extra $1/h$, because normalization is by knot values rather than by integral.

The <Sobolev space> and <norm> are
$$
H^1(a,b)=\{v\in L^2(a,b):v'\text{ exists weakly and belongs to }L^2(a,b)\},\qquad
\|v\|_{H^1}^2=\|v\|_2^2+\|v'\|_2^2.
$$
In one dimension these functions have continuous representatives and well-defined endpoint traces. <integration by parts> gives the weak problem: find $u\in H^1(a,b)$ such that $a(u,v)=\ell(v)$ for all $v\in H^1(a,b)$, where
$$
a(u,v)=\int_a^b(u'v'+x^2uv)\,dx+u(a)v(a)+u(b)v(b),\qquad
\ell(v)=\int_a^bfv\,dx+A v(a)+B v(b).
$$
Indeed the integration boundary term is $-u'(b)v(b)+u'(a)v(a)$; substituting $u'(a)=u(a)-A$ and $u'(b)=B-u(b)$ gives the two positive boundary terms on the left.

If $0\notin[a,b]$, put $m=\min_{[a,b]}x^2>0$. Then $a(v,v)\geq\|v'\|_2^2+m\|v\|_2^2\geq\min(1,m)\|v\|_{H^1}^2$. If $0\in[a,b]$, the supplied <trace> inequality gives
$$
\|v\|_{H^1}^2\leq(1+\widetilde c)\bigl(\|v'\|_2^2+v(a)^2+v(b)^2\bigr)\leq(1+\widetilde c)a(v,v).
$$
Thus the form is **coercive in both cases**. The <trace> theorem and boundedness of $x^2$ give continuity of $a$ and $\ell$, and the <Lax-Milgram theorem> gives <existence> and <uniqueness>.

Let $V_h$ be the continuous piecewise linear space including both endpoint <hat functions>. Robin conditions are natural conditions and do not restrict the endpoint values of this space. The <finite element> problem is $u_h\in V_h$, $a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)$ for all $v_h\in V_h$. <coercivity> restricted to $V_h$ gives <existence> and <uniqueness>.

Expanding $u_h=\sum_{j=0}^NU_j\phi_j$ gives $\mathsf A U=F$, where
$$
\boxed{\mathsf A_{ij}=\int_a^b(\phi_j'\phi_i'+x^2\phi_j\phi_i)\,dx+\delta_{i0}\delta_{j0}+\delta_{iN}\delta_{jN},\quad
F_i=\int_a^bf\phi_i\,dx+A\delta_{i0}+B\delta_{iN}.}
$$
The stiffness contribution has diagonal $2/h$ at interior nodes, $1/h$ at endpoints, and adjacent entries $-1/h$. For completeness, the weighted mass contribution has interior diagonal $2hx_i^2/3+h^3/15$, left endpoint diagonal $ha^2/3+ah^2/6+h^3/30$, right endpoint diagonal $hb^2/3-bh^2/6+h^3/30$, and adjacent entry $hx_i^2/6+h^2x_i/6+h^3/20$. All more distant entries vanish. The Robin contribution adds one to each endpoint diagonal.

Symmetry follows from symmetry of the <bilinear form>. If $V\neq0$, nodal independence makes $v_h=\sum_iV_i\phi_i$ nonzero, and $V^T\mathsf AV=a(v_h,v_h)\geq\gamma\|v_h\|_{H^1}^2>0$. Thus the <matrix> is **symmetric positive definite**.

The weak equation implies $u''=x^2u-f\in L^2(a,b)$ in <distributions>, hence $u\in H^2(a,b)$. Its nodal interpolant satisfies
$$
\|(u-I_hu)'\|_2\leq\frac h\pi\|u''\|_2,\qquad
\|u-I_hu\|_2\leq\frac{h^2}{\pi^2}\|u''\|_2.
$$
These follow interval by interval: the <interpolation> error vanishes at both endpoints, and its <derivative> has mean zero, so two Poincaré inequalities apply. If $M$ and $\gamma$ are the continuity and <coercivity> constants, <Céa's lemma> gives
$$
\|u-u_h\|_{H^1}\leq\frac M\gamma\|u-I_hu\|_{H^1}
\leq\frac M\gamma\sqrt{\frac{h^2}{\pi^2}+\frac{h^4}{\pi^4}}\,\|u''\|_2
=\boxed{O(h)}.
$$
All constants are independent of the mesh size.

\leq\frac{e+1}{\pi^2}\eta_h\|\psi\|_2.
$$
Here $\eta_h^2=\sum_i h_i^4\|R(u_h)\|_{2,I_i}^2$. Taking $\psi=u-u_h$ and cancelling its <norm>, unless it is zero already, yields
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_2\leq\frac{e+1}{\pi^2}\eta_h.}
$$
For adaptive refinement, solve the discrete problem, compute $\eta_i^2=h_i^4\|R(u_h)\|_{2,I_i}^2$, and stop when $(e+1)\eta_h/\pi^2\leq\mathrm{TOL}$. Otherwise mark intervals carrying a fixed positive fraction of $\eta_h^2$, bisect them, and repeat. Residual integration must be exact or have a certified upper bound if the stopping test is to certify the requested tolerance.

Use <residual adaptation with a uniform-refinement safeguard>, for example global bisection every second refinement, to ensure eventual termination. Discrete energy stability gives $\|u_h\|_{H^1}\leq\|f\|_2$, so $\|R(u_h)\|_2\leq(1+e)\|f\|_2$ independently of the mesh. Thus $\eta_h\leq h_{\max}^2(1+e)\|f\|_2$ tends to zero under the safeguard. Other iterations concentrate work where the residual is large.

Use $V=\{v\in H^1(0,1):v(0)=0\}$ and its continuous piecewise <linear subspace> $V_h=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N\}$. The condition at $x=1$ is natural, so the last <hat function> remains free. Set $u_h^0$ to the $L^2$ <orthogonal projection> of $u_0$ onto $V_h$: the stated $L^2$ initial data need not have nodal values.

For each $m$, find $u_h^{m+1}\in V_h$ such that
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{u_h^{m+1}-u_h^m}{\Delta t},v_h\right)
+((u_h^{m+1})',v_h')+(u_h^{m+1},v_h)=0\quad(v_h\in V_h).}
$$
This applies <backward Euler method> to diffusion and reaction together. The step <bilinear form> is coercive, so each step has a unique solution.

Test with $v_h=u_h^{m+1}$ and apply $2(a-b,a)=\|a\|^2-\|b\|^2+\|a-b\|^2$. The exact energy identity is
$$
\frac{\|u_h^{m+1}\|_2^2-\|u_h^m\|_2^2+\|u_h^{m+1}-u_h^m\|_2^2}{2\Delta t}
+\|(u_h^{m+1})'\|_2^2+\|u_h^{m+1}\|_2^2=0.
$$
The printed identity omits the last reaction contribution while explicitly describing its last term as the $H^1$ seminorm. **It requires the full $H^1$ <norm> instead of that seminorm.** With the printed seminorm its left side equals $-\|u_h^{m+1}\|_2^2$, rather than zero. The source reaction term forces this correction.

All terms other than the difference of successive squared <norms> are nonnegative. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|u_h^m\|_2\leq\|u_h^0\|_2\leq\|u_0\|_2.}
$$
The second inequality uses contractivity of the initial <orthogonal projection>. No restriction relates $\Delta t$ to $h$, giving unconditional stability. The retained reaction term strengthens dissipation.

Define the <mass matrix> $M_{ij}=\int_0^1\phi_j\phi_i$ and <stiffness matrix> $K_{ij}=\int_0^1\phi_j'\phi_i'$ for $1\leq i,j\leq N$. With coefficient vectors $U^m$, the step system is
$$
\boxed{\mathsf A U^{m+1}=M U^m,\qquad\mathsf A=M+\Delta t(K+M).}
$$
The matrices are symmetric, and $V^T\mathsf AV=\|v_h\|_2^2+\Delta t(\|v_h'\|_2^2+\|v_h\|_2^2)>0$ for nonzero $V$, proving positive definiteness.

For $h=1/2$, the first <basis> function is the full tent at $1/2$, and the last is the half tent ending at $1$. Integration gives
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/12\\1/12&1/6\end{pmatrix},\qquad
K=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since $\Delta t=2/2=1$, the requested <matrix> in this normalization is
$$
\boxed{\mathsf A=2M+K=\begin{pmatrix}14/3&-11/6\\-11/6&7/3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Here the <matrix> symbol $M$ is distinct from the printed integer number of time steps.
