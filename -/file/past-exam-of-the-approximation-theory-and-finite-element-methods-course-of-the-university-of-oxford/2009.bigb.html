= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Define the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>
$$
\ell_r(x)=\frac{\pi_{n,r}(x)}{\pi_{n,r}(x_r)}
=\prod_{s\ne r}\frac{x-x_s}{x_r-x_s}.
$$
Distinct nodes make every denominator nonzero. At $x_s$ these polynomials satisfy $\ell_r(x_s)=\delta_{rs}$, so the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{r=0}^nu_r\ell_r(x).}
$$
It has degree at most $n$ and takes the prescribed data values. If two such polynomials existed, their difference would have $n+1$ distinct roots but degree at most $n$, forcing that difference to be zero. This also proves uniqueness.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

With $w_s=1/\pi_{n,s}(x_s)$, the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> can be written, away from its nodes,
$$
p_n(x)=\pi_n(x)\sum_{s=0}^n\frac{w_su_s}{x-x_s}.
$$
Apply the same formula to the constant data $u_s=1$. Uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation> gives $\sum_s\ell_s=1$, and hence
$$
1=\pi_n(x)\sum_{s=0}^n\frac{w_s}{x-x_s}.
$$
Divide the two identities to obtain the <barycentric interpolation formula>
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^nw_su_s/(x-x_s)}
{\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^nw_s/(x-x_s)}.}
$$
At $x=x_r$, use the continuous limiting value $u_r$. Multiplying every weight by the same nonzero constant cancels between numerator and denominator, so only relative weights are needed in computation.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For uniformly spaced nodes,
$$
\pi_{n,s}(x_s)=h^n\prod_{j\ne s}(s-j)
=(-1)^{n-s}h^ns!(n-s)!.
$$
There are $n-s$ negative factors, coming from $j>s$. Thus the weights fixed by part (b) satisfy this formula exactly. The printed sign $(-1)^s$ agrees for even $n$; for odd $n$ it differs by the common factor $-1$. Rescaling all weights by $(-1)^n$ gives the printed convention without changing the <barycentric interpolation formula>.

For even $n$, the required magnitude is
$$
\left|\frac{w_0}{w_{n/2}}\right|
=\frac{((n/2)!)^2}{n!}
=\binom n{n/2}^{-1}.
$$
<Stirling's formula> gives
$$
\frac{((n/2)!)^2}{n!}
\sim\frac{\pi n(n/(2e))^n}{\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n}
=\boxed{\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2^{n+1/2}}.}
$$
Thus central weights are exponentially larger in magnitude than endpoint weights. Their alternating signs can cause severe cancellation in polynomial interpolation at equispaced nodes; they reflect the unfavorable high-degree interpolation geometry associated with <Runge's phenomenon>. A barycentric evaluation formula improves the implementation but does not remove the intrinsic sensitivity or guarantee convergence for every smooth function. The comparison with the bounded relative weights at <Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes> is decisive.

= Solution

{c}

Use the <Chebyshev polynomial> identities $T_0=1$, $T_1=x$, and $T_{j+1}=2xT_j-T_{j-1}$. Induction shows that $T_j$ has degree $j$ with leading coefficient $2^{j-1}$ for $j\geq1$. The trigonometric definition also gives
$$
T_n'(\cos\theta)=n\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{\sin\theta}.
$$
Therefore
$$
f_n(x)=\frac{2^{1-n}}n(x^2-1)T_n'(x).
$$
The leading coefficient is $2^{1-n}n^{-1}\cdot n2^{n-1}=1$, and the degree is $n+1$. The endpoints are roots through $x^2-1$; the other $n-1$ points $\cos(s\pi/n)$ are roots through $T_n'$. They are distinct, so this monic polynomial is exactly $\pi_n$.

Differentiating the product $\pi_n(x)=(x-x_s)\pi_{n,s}(x)$ at $x_s$ gives $\pi_n'(x_s)=\pi_{n,s}(x_s)=w_s^{-1}$. For an interior node, differentiate the trigonometric form using $d/dx=-(\sin\theta)^{-1}d/d\theta$:
$$
\pi_n'(\cos\theta)=2^{1-n}
\left[\cot\theta\sin(n\theta)+n\cos(n\theta)\right].
$$
At $\theta_s=s\pi/n$, this is $2^{1-n}n(-1)^s$. At either endpoint, taking the limit makes the two terms equal, doubling the value: $\pi_n'(x_s)=2^{2-n}n(-1)^s$ for $s=0,n$.

After removing the common factor $2^{n-1}/n$, the <barycentric interpolation formula> therefore uses
$$
\boxed{w_s=(-1)^s\delta_s,\qquad
\delta_s=1\ (1\leq s<n),\quad \delta_0=\delta_n=\tfrac12.}
$$
These <Chebyshev-Lobatto node> weights differ in magnitude by only a factor of two.

Let $t_1,\ldots,t_m$ be the distinct roots in $(0,1)$ at which $\phi_r$ changes sign, and put $q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^m(x-t_j)$. Both $\phi_r$ and $q$ change sign at each such point, while roots of even multiplicity do not change sign. Consequently $\phi_rq$ has one constant sign on $(0,1)$ except at its zeros, and is not identically zero. Its integral cannot vanish.

If $m<r$, however, orthogonality of the <monic orthogonal polynomial> $\phi_r$ to every polynomial of lower degree gives $(\phi_r,q)=0$, a contradiction. Hence $m\geq r$. Since $\deg\phi_r=r$, there must be exactly $r$ such roots, each of multiplicity one. They exhaust the degree, leaving no complex, exterior, or endpoint roots. Thus **all roots are simple and lie strictly in $(0,1)$**, which proves the requested weaker inclusion in $[0,1]$. This is the sign-change proof of the <zeros of orthogonal polynomials> theorem.

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

The <monic orthogonal polynomials> $\phi_0,\ldots,\phi_{r+1}$ form a basis of $P_{r+1}$, ordered by degree. Since $x\phi_r$ is monic of degree $r+1$,
$$
x\phi_r=\phi_{r+1}+\sum_{j=0}^ra_j\phi_j.
$$
For $j\leq r-2$, symmetry of multiplication by $x$ gives $(x\phi_r,\phi_j)=(\phi_r,x\phi_j)=0$, because $\deg(x\phi_j)<r$. Only $a_r$ and $a_{r-1}$ remain. Taking inner products with $\phi_r$ yields $a_r=(\phi_r,x\phi_r)/(\phi_r,\phi_r)$.

Similarly,
$$
a_{r-1}=\frac{(x\phi_r,\phi_{r-1})}{(\phi_{r-1},\phi_{r-1})}
=\frac{(\phi_r,x\phi_{r-1})}{(\phi_{r-1},\phi_{r-1})}
=\frac{(\phi_r,\phi_r)}{(\phi_{r-1},\phi_{r-1})},
$$
since $x\phi_{r-1}-\phi_r$ has degree at most $r-1$. Rearranging proves the <three-term recurrence for monic orthogonal polynomials>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_{r+1}=(x-a_r)\phi_r-
\frac{\|\phi_r\|_2^2}{\|\phi_{r-1}\|_2^2}\phi_{r-1}.}
$$

The monic degree-zero polynomial is $\phi_0=1$. Orthogonality of $x-a$ to one gives $a=\int_0^1x\,dx=1/2$, so $\phi_1=x-1/2$.

The relevant integrals are
$$
\|\phi_0\|_2^2=1,\qquad \|\phi_1\|_2^2=\frac1{12},\qquad
(\phi_1,x\phi_1)=\frac1{24}.
$$
Insert these into the <three-term recurrence for monic orthogonal polynomials>:
$$
\phi_2=(x-\tfrac12)(x-\tfrac12)-\frac1{12}
=x^2-x+\frac16.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi_0=1,\qquad\phi_1=x-\tfrac12,\qquad
\phi_2=x^2-x+\tfrac16.}
$$
Their mutual orthogonality can also be checked directly by integrating on $[0,1]$.

The best approximation in the $L^2$ norm is the <orthogonal projection> onto $\operatorname{span}\{\phi_0,\phi_1\}$. Therefore
$$
p_1=\frac{(x^k,\phi_0)}{\|\phi_0\|_2^2}\phi_0
+\frac{(x^k,\phi_1)}{\|\phi_1\|_2^2}\phi_1.
$$
Using $\int_0^1x^k\,dx=1/(k+1)$,
$$
(x^k,\phi_1)=\frac1{k+2}-\frac1{2(k+1)}
=\frac{k}{2(k+1)(k+2)}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{p_1(x)=\frac{2(1-k)+6kx}{(k+1)(k+2)}.}
$$
The error is orthogonal to every linear polynomial, so the Pythagorean identity proves optimality and uniqueness. At $k=0$, this gives $p_1=1$; at $k=1$, it gives $p_1=x$. Both are exact because the target itself then belongs to $P_1$.

Put $x=1-t$. For $0<k\leq1$,
$$
(1-t)^k=1-kt+\frac{k(k-1)}2t^2+O(t^3),
$$
whereas the constrained <rational interpolant> expands as
$$
\frac{a(1-t)}{1+b-bt}
=\frac a{1+b}\left[1-\frac{t}{1+b}+O(t^2)\right].
$$
Matching the constant term gives $a=1+b$, and matching the coefficient of $t$ gives $1/(1+b)=k$. Thus $a=1/k$, $b=(1-k)/k$, and
$$
\boxed{r(x)=\frac{x}{k+(1-k)x}.}
$$
This is an <endpoint-constrained rational approximation to a power>: it matches value and derivative at $x=1$ and also enforces $r(0)=0$ for $k>0$.

There is a terminology qualification. A standard degree-$(1,1)$ <Padé approximant> about $t=0$ matches through $t^2$, but the displayed constrained expression has quadratic coefficient $-k(1-k)$ rather than $-k(1-k)/2$. The unconstrained Padé approximant is instead
$$
R(x)=\frac{(1-k)+(1+k)x}{(1+k)+(1-k)x}.
$$
The source's requested formula is obtained by the two stated endpoint matching conditions within its restricted family, rather than by full Padé matching. At $k=1$ both reduce to $x$. At $k=0$, the requested expression is one for $x>0$ with a removable value at zero; finite $a,b$ in the original restricted form do not represent that constant function. The endpoint is understood by this limit.

= Solution

{c}

By definition, a <best uniform approximation> $p^*\in P_n$ minimizes $\|u-p\|_\infty$ over $p\in P_n$. Write $E=\|u-p^*\|_\infty$. The <equioscillation theorem> says that, if $E>0$, optimality is equivalent to the existence of $n+2$ ordered points with signed errors
$$
u(\xi_j)-p^*(\xi_j)=\sigma(-1)^jE,\qquad \sigma\in\{1,-1\}.
$$
The absolute-value bars on the left of the printed formula must be omitted for this alternating-sign statement. A nonnegative absolute value cannot equal a negative $-E$.

For sufficiency, suppose a polynomial $p$ had strictly smaller error. At the alternating points, $p-p^*$ must have the same sign as $u-p^*$, since otherwise the new error would have magnitude at least $E$. The difference polynomial therefore changes sign in all $n+1$ successive gaps and has at least $n+1$ distinct roots, impossible for a nonzero element of $P_n$.

For necessity, suppose that no $n+2$ alternating extremal points exist. On the compact extremal set $K=\{x:|u(x)-p^*(x)|=E\}$, group the signs of the error into consecutive blocks. The positive and negative extremal sets are disjoint compact sets, so sign changes are separated by positive distances; the number of blocks is finite. The absence of an $n+2$ alternating selection means there are at most $n+1$ blocks. Place a root in each gap between successive blocks and choose the overall sign of their product $q$ to agree with the error on $K$. Then $\deg q\leq n$ and $q$ is nonzero throughout $K$.

By compactness and continuity, on a neighborhood of $K$ the signs still agree and $|q|$ is bounded below. A sufficiently small positive $\eta$ reduces the error there when $p^*$ is replaced by $p^*+\eta q$. On the compact complement of this neighborhood the old error is below $E$ by a fixed positive margin, and choosing $\eta$ smaller preserves that margin. This gives a strictly better approximation, a contradiction. If $E=0$, the target already lies in $P_n$ and optimality is immediate.

For $u(x)=x^{1/3}$, the chord is $x$. Concavity gives $x^{1/3}-x\geq0$, with maximum at $x_*=1/(3\sqrt3)$ and maximum value $2/(3\sqrt3)$. Shift the chord up by half that amount. The errors at $0,x_*,1$ are respectively $-E,+E,-E$, so
$$
\boxed{p_1^*(x)=x+\frac1{3\sqrt3},\qquad E=\frac1{3\sqrt3}.}
$$

Use the standard meaning of a <cubic spline>: a $C^2$ function that is cubic on each unit interval. If it vanishes on both exterior intervals, continuity of the function and its first two derivatives gives zero endpoint jets at zero and at $k$.

Such a spline can be represented through its third-derivative jumps as
$$
\phi(x)=\sum_{j=0}^kc_j(x-j)_+^3.
$$
For $x\geq k$, this must be the zero polynomial. Expanding in powers of $x$ yields the four moment conditions
$$
\sum_{j=0}^kc_jj^m=0,\qquad m=0,1,2,3.
$$
If $k\leq3$, the columns of this Vandermonde system are linearly independent, so every $c_j=0$. No nonzero spline has such short support.

For $k=4$, a nonzero null vector is $(1,-4,6,-4,1)$, since the fourth finite difference annihilates every cubic. Thus the <cardinal cubic B-spline> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(x)=\frac16\left[x_+^3-4(x-1)_+^3+6(x-2)_+^3
-4(x-3)_+^3+(x-4)_+^3\right].}
$$
Its knot values at $0,1,2,3,4$ are $0,1/6,2/3,1/6,0$, whose sum is one. It is nonzero and supported on $[0,4]$, proving the minimal value **$k=4$**. Equivalently, on $[0,1]$ it is $x^3/6$, on $[1,2]$ it is $[-3x^3+12x^2-12x+4]/6$, and the other half follows by symmetry $\phi(4-x)=\phi(x)$.

= Solution
{parent=4}
{c}

Work in the usual bounded Lipschitz, polygonally triangulated setting in which Sobolev traces and conforming meshes are defined. Choose an $H^1$ lift $G$ of the smooth boundary data. Multiplying the Poisson equation by $v\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ and integrating by parts gives the <weak formulation>
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=\int_\Omega fv,\qquad
u\in G+H_0^1(\Omega).
$$
The test function has zero trace, so the boundary flux term vanishes. This formulation only needs first weak derivatives.

Let $V_h$ be the <conforming finite element space> of continuous functions affine on each triangle, and $V_h^0$ its zero-boundary subspace. Choose a nodal interpolation $G_h$ of the boundary lift and seek $u_h\in G_h+V_h^0$ satisfying the same identity for every $v_h\in V_h^0$. General smooth boundary data need not be exactly representable by boundary piecewise linear functions; this is the usual discrete boundary approximation.

With nodal <shape functions> $\varphi_j$, interior coefficients $U_j$ satisfy
$$
\sum_{j\in I}A_{ij}U_j=F_i-\sum_{j\in B}A_{ij}g_j,\qquad
A_{ij}=\int_\Omega\nabla\varphi_i\cdot\nabla\varphi_j,\quad
F_i=\int_\Omega f\varphi_i.
$$
Here $I$ and $B$ index interior and boundary vertices. <Finite element assembly> sums the triangle contributions.

Take a translated lower triangle with local vertices $(0,0),(h,0),(h,h)$. Its local functions are $N_1=1-x/h$, $N_2=(x-y)/h$, $N_3=y/h$. Their gradients are constant and the area is $h^2/2$, giving the <stiffness matrix>
$$
\boxed{A^{(T)}=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&0\\
-1&2&-1\\
0&-1&1
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Translations preserve this matrix, while rotations or a different local vertex order permute its entries. The coupling across the hypotenuse is zero.

Each interior grid vertex belongs to six triangles. Summation gives diagonal value 4 and coupling $-1$ to each of its four horizontal and vertical neighbors. Both positive-diagonal edge couplings vanish. Thus, before prescribed boundary values are removed, an interior equation has the five-point stencil
$$
\boxed{4U_{ij}-U_{i-1,j}-U_{i+1,j}-U_{i,j-1}-U_{i,j+1}=F_{ij}.}
$$
There is no $h^{-2}$ factor in the assembled variational matrix; that factor belongs to a differently normalized finite-difference equation.

\Image[2009-triangle-stencil.png]
{title=Triangular mesh edges along diagonals have zero stiffness coupling}

If $h=1/N$ and $m=N-1$, lexicographic ordering makes the Dirichlet-reduced matrix block tridiagonal with diagonal blocks $\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,4,-1)$ and neighboring blocks $-I$. A row with all four neighboring nodes unknown has five nonzeros: 4 once and $-1$ four times. Rows adjacent to the boundary have fewer unknown-neighbor entries; their exact count is $1+1_{i>1}+1_{i<m}+1_{j>1}+1_{j<m}$. Removed boundary contributions appear on the right, rather than as new nonzeros.

= Solution
{parent=5}
{c}

The <Sobolev space> $H^1(b,c)$ consists of $L^2$ functions with a weak first derivative in $L^2$, with norm
$$
\|v\|_{H^1}^2=\int_b^c(|v|^2+|v'|^2)\,dx.
$$
In one dimension these functions have absolutely continuous representatives and well-defined endpoint <Sobolev traces>. Put
$$
V=\{v\in H^1(b,c):v(b)=0\}.
$$
This is a closed subspace, hence a Hilbert space. Both trial and test functions belong to $V$; one does not impose a derivative boundary condition in this space.

Integrate the differential equation against $v\in V$:
$$
\int_b^cfv=\int_b^ce^xu'v'\,dx-e^cu'(c)v(c).
$$
The printed condition uses the ordinary derivative, $u'(c)=-u(c)$, so the weak form is
$$
\boxed{a(u,v)=\int_b^ce^xu'v'\,dx+e^cu(c)v(c),\qquad
\ell(v)=\int_b^cfv\,dx.}
$$
Seek $u\in V$ with $a(u,v)=\ell(v)$ for all $v\in V$. The coefficient $e^c$ in the endpoint term must be retained: replacing the ordinary derivative with the conormal flux would change the boundary problem.

Let $L=c-b$. The <Poincaré inequality> on $V$ can be stated as $\|v\|_{L^2}\leq L\|v'\|_{L^2}$. Also $|v(c)|\leq\sqrt L\|v'\|_{L^2}$. Consequently
$$
a(v,v)\geq e^b\|v'\|_{L^2}^2
\geq\alpha\|v\|_{H^1}^2,\qquad
\alpha=\frac{e^b}{1+L^2}>0,
$$
proving coercivity. By <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and the trace bound,
$$
|a(u,v)|\leq e^c(1+L)\|u\|_{H^1}\|v\|_{H^1}.
$$
Thus continuity holds with $M=e^c(1+L)$. The load is continuous if $f\in L^2$, and the <Lax-Milgram theorem> gives a unique weak solution.

Choose a finite-dimensional conforming subspace $V_h\subset V$, such as continuous piecewise linear functions vanishing at $b$, and solve $a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)$ for every $v_h\in V_h$. Subtracting the weak equations gives <Galerkin orthogonality> $a(u-u_h,v_h)=0$.

<Céa's lemma> states, for a continuous coercive bilinear form with constants $M,\alpha$ on a Hilbert space,
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_{H^1}\leq\frac M\alpha
\inf_{v_h\in V_h}\|u-v_h\|_{H^1}.}
$$
To prove it, put $e=u-u_h$ and use orthogonality to write $a(e,e)=a(e,u-v_h)$ for any $v_h\in V_h$. Coercivity and continuity give $\alpha\|e\|^2\leq M\|e\|\|u-v_h\|$. If $e=0$ the claim is immediate; otherwise cancel its norm and take the infimum. Any approximation theorem making the infimum tend to zero under mesh refinement therefore gives strong convergence $u_h\to u$ in $H^1$.

Finally, $a$ is symmetric and positive definite on $V$. It is an inner product, so $\|v\|_a=\sqrt{a(v,v)}$ is an <energy norm> on all of $V$: homogeneity and definiteness are immediate, and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for this inner product gives the triangle inequality. No smaller space is required. In this norm the continuity and coercivity constants are one. More precisely, <Galerkin orthogonality> gives
$$
\|u-v_h\|_a^2=\|u-u_h\|_a^2+\|u_h-v_h\|_a^2,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_a=\min_{v_h\in V_h}\|u-v_h\|_a.}
$$
The finite element solution is the exact best energy-norm approximation.

= Solution
{parent=6}
{c}

Take linearly independent basis functions $\varphi_i\in H_0^1(0,1)$ and write $u_h(x,t)=\sum_jU_j(t)\varphi_j(x)$. The <weak formulation> of the heat equation, tested against $\varphi_i$, is
$$
\int_0^1\partial_tu_h\,\varphi_i=-\int_0^1u_h'\varphi_i'.
$$
Thus the <semidiscrete finite element heat equation> is
$$
\boxed{M\dot U=KU,\qquad
M_{ij}=\int_0^1\varphi_i\varphi_j,\quad
K_{ij}=-\int_0^1\varphi_i'\varphi_j'.}
$$
The vector $U$ contains the basis coefficients. A natural initial vector $G$ is the $L^2$ projection of the initial data: $MG=b$, where $b_i=\int_0^1g\varphi_i$, and $U(0)=G$. Other consistent initial approximations can be specified, but exact representation of an arbitrary $g$ in the trial space is not implicit.

For a nonzero coefficient vector $a$, the function $v_h=\sum_ia_i\varphi_i$ is nonzero by basis independence. Hence $a^TMa=\|v_h\|_{L^2}^2>0$, proving that the <mass matrix> is symmetric positive definite. Also $a^TKa=-\|v_h'\|_{L^2}^2<0$: if the derivative vanished, $v_h$ would be a constant, and its zero endpoint traces would make it zero. Therefore **$K$ is symmetric negative definite**, not merely semidefinite, for these homogeneous Dirichlet conditions.

The squared spatial norm is $U^TMU$. Differentiating and using the semidiscrete system gives the <energy identity>
$$
\frac d{dt}\|u_h(t)\|_{L^2}^2
=2U^TM\dot U=2U^TKU=-2\|u_h'(t)\|_{L^2}^2\leq0.
$$
Using the <trapezoidal rule> in time gives the <Crank-Nicolson method>
$$
\boxed{(M-\tfrac12\Delta t K)U^{k+1}
=(M+\tfrac12\Delta t K)U^k.}
$$
The left matrix is positive definite for every $\Delta t>0$, so every step exists uniquely. To prove stability, set $d=U^{k+1}-U^k$ and $s=U^{k+1}+U^k$. The update says $Md=(\Delta t/2)Ks$. Multiplying by $s^T$ gives
$$
(U^{k+1})^TMU^{k+1}-(U^k)^TMU^k
=\frac{\Delta t}{2}s^TKs\leq0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|u_h^{k+1}\|_{L^2}\leq\|u_h^k\|_{L^2}\quad
\text{for every }\Delta t>0.}
$$
The same inequality applied to the difference of two discrete solutions proves nonamplification of initial perturbations. This is unconditional stability in the mass-matrix norm, exactly the spatial $L^2$ norm of the finite element functions.
