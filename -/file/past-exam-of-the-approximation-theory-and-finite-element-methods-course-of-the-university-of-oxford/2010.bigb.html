= Solution
{parent=a}

Direct evaluation gives $X[1]=X[x]=X[x^2]=0$ and $X[x^3]=-13/16$. Hence the <linear functional> vanishes on every <polynomial> of degree at most two and is nonzero on every genuine cubic <polynomial>.

The <Peano kernel theorem> with order three gives $X[u]=\int_{-1}^1K(t)u'''(t)\,dt$, where
$$
K(t)=\left(\frac14-t\right)_+-\frac38(1-t)_+^2+\frac14(-t)_+^2+\frac18(-1-t)_+^2.
$$
The first term comes from differentiating $(x-t)_+^2/2$ at $x=1/4$. On the integration interval this simplifies to
$$
\boxed{K(t)=\begin{cases}
-(t+1)^2/8,&-1\leq t\leq0,\\
-(1+2t+3t^2)/8,&0\leq t\leq1/4,\\
-3(1-t)^2/8,&1/4\leq t\leq1.
\end{cases}}
$$
In particular $K\leq0$. Its <L1 norm> is $-\int K=-X[x^3/6]=13/96$, so the sharp bound is
$$
\boxed{|X[u]|\leq\frac{13}{96}\|u'''\|_\infty.}
$$
**The printed constant $1/12$ is false for the printed functional.** The cubic $u=x^3/6$ has $\|u'''\|_\infty=1$ but $|X[u]|=13/96>1/12$, and it attains the corrected bound. This is an exact <Peano kernel norm> calculation, not an error from numerical quadrature.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A cubic <spline> that vanishes for $x\leq0$ and is twice continuously differentiable can be written using <truncated power functions> as $s(x)=\sum_{j=0}^k a_j(x-jh)_+^3$. Vanishing for $x\geq kh$ imposes the four moment equations $\sum_j a_jj^r=0$ for $r=0,1,2,3$. Their <Vandermonde matrix> has rank $\min(4,k+1)$, so a nonzero coefficient vector first exists at $\boxed{k=4}$.

A normalized compact <cubic cardinal B-spline> is
$$
\boxed{B_3(x/h)=\frac16\sum_{j=0}^4(-1)^j\binom4j(x/h-j)_+^3.}
$$
It has nodal values $0,1/6,2/3,1/6,0$ at $0,h,2h,3h,4h$, so their sum is one. The alternating <binomial coefficients> meet the four moment equations, and the factor $1/6$ fixes the requested normalization.

For interpolation on $[-1,1]$, centre the translates as $\beta_j(x)=B_3(x/h-j+2)$, with $j=-n-1,\ldots,n+1$. Their restrictions form a <basis> for the cubic $C^2$ <spline> space on these knots. Write $S=\sum_jc_j\beta_j$. The $2n+1$ interpolation equations are
$$
\boxed{\frac{c_{r-1}+4c_r+c_{r+1}}6=u(rh),\qquad r=-n,\ldots,n.}
$$
The recurrence determines all remaining coefficients after choosing $c_{-n-1},c_{-n}$, giving exactly two free parameters. The two <natural cubic spline> conditions become
$$
\boxed{c_{-n-1}-2c_{-n}+c_{-n+1}=0,\qquad c_{n-1}-2c_n+c_{n+1}=0,}
$$
because $S''(rh)=h^{-2}(c_{r-1}-2c_r+c_{r+1})$.

For uniqueness, take the difference $s$ of two candidates. It vanishes at every knot and has $s''(\pm1)=0$. Integrating by parts twice on each cubic piece gives $\int_{-1}^1(s'')^2=0$: terms involving $s$ vanish at the knots, and those involving $s''s'$ cancel at internal knots and vanish at the endpoints. Thus $s''=0$; the remaining affine <function> vanishes at both endpoints and is zero. The square linear system for the coefficients is therefore nonsingular, giving existence as well as uniqueness of the <natural cubic spline interpolant>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $e=u-p$. If $p$ minimizes the squared <L2 norm> error, then for any $q\in A$ the real quadratic $\|e-tq\|_2^2=\|e\|_2^2-2t(e,q)+t^2\|q\|_2^2$ has its minimum at $t=0$. Its <derivative> there is zero, giving $(e,q)=0$.

Conversely, if $e$ is <orthogonal> to $A$, then for every $q\in A$, $p-q\in A$ and the <Pythagorean theorem for inner product spaces> gives
$$
\boxed{\|u-q\|_2^2=\|u-p\|_2^2+\|p-q\|_2^2\geq\|u-p\|_2^2.}
$$
This proves the equivalence and identifies $p$ as an <orthogonal projection>. It characterizes a minimizer whenever one exists; a general nonclosed <vector subspace> need not admit a projection for every $u$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <orthogonal projection> is $p_n=\sum_{r=0}^n(u,\phi_r)\phi_r$. Subtract it from $u$ and use the <orthonormal basis> relations to obtain
$$
0\leq\|u-p_n\|_2^2=\|u\|_2^2-\sum_{r=0}^n(u,\phi_r)^2.
$$
This is the requested <Bessel inequality>.

For the infinite tail, use completeness of the orthonormal <polynomial> family in the usual weighted <L2 space>. The relevant density facts are that continuous functions are dense for the finite measure $w(x)\,dx$, and the <Weierstrass approximation theorem> makes <polynomials> uniformly dense in continuous functions on the compact interval. Since $\int w<\infty$, uniform approximation also gives weighted <L2 norm> approximation. <Parseval's identity> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\|u-p_n\|_2^2=\sum_{r=n+1}^\infty(u,\phi_r)^2.}
$$
Here finiteness of the weight integral is also necessary for the constant <polynomial> to belong to the stated Hilbert space. Without completeness of the polynomial family, an additional squared component orthogonal to every <polynomial> would remain; <Bessel inequality> alone does not justify equality.

The moments are $\int_0^1x^{j+2}\,dx=1/(j+3)$. The <Gram-Schmidt process> first gives the constant $\phi_0=\sqrt3$. Removing the constant component of $x$ gives $r_1=x-3/4$, with squared norm $1/80$, hence $\phi_1=4\sqrt5(x-3/4)$.

For the quadratic, imposing orthogonality of $r_2=x^2+ax+b$ to one and $x$ gives $1/5+a/4+b/3=0$ and $1/6+a/5+b/4=0$. Thus $r_2=x^2-4x/3+2/5$, whose squared norm is $1/1575$. Taking positive leading coefficients, the first three <orthonormal polynomials> are
$$
\boxed{\phi_0=\sqrt3,\qquad\phi_1=\sqrt5(4x-3),\qquad\phi_2=\sqrt7(15x^2-20x+6).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $n\geq1$, $\phi_n$ is <orthogonal> to every <polynomial> in $P_{n-1}$, so $(u,\phi_n)=(u-p_{n-1}^*,\phi_n)$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|(u,\phi_n)|\leq E_{n-1}\int_0^1x^2|\phi_n|\,dx
\leq E_{n-1}\left(\int_0^1x^2\,dx\right)^{1/2}\|\phi_n\|_2
=\boxed{E_{n-1}/\sqrt3}.
$$
Using the tail identity from part (b) and shifting the index,
$$
\boxed{\|u-p_n\|_2^2\leq\frac13\sum_{r=n}^\infty E_r^2.}
$$
For the stated power law and $n>1$, the decreasing-function <integral test> gives
$$
\sum_{r=n}^\infty r^{-2a}\leq\int_{n-1}^\infty x^{-2a}\,dx=\frac{(n-1)^{1-2a}}{2a-1}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|u-p_n\|_2\leq\frac K{\sqrt3}\frac{(n-1)^{-a+1/2}}{\sqrt{2a-1}}.}
$$
The assumption $a>1$ is sufficient; this summation estimate itself only needs $a>1/2$.

= Solution

Put $m=\min_r|u(x_r)-q(x_r)|$ and $M=\max_r|u(x_r)-q(x_r)|$. If the <best uniform approximation> had error below $m$, the values of $p_n^*-q$ would strictly alternate in sign at all $n+2$ reference points. The <intermediate value theorem> would give at least $n+1$ distinct <roots of a polynomial> of degree at most $n$, a contradiction. If $m=0$ the lower bound is immediate.

At least one reference point attains the global error maximum of $q$, so $M=\|u-q\|_\infty$. Optimality of $p_n^*$ gives the upper bound, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\min_r|u(x_r)-q(x_r)|\leq\|u-p_n^*\|_\infty\leq\max_r|u(x_r)-q(x_r)|.}
$$
This is the unequal-height version of the <alternating-reference error bound>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Here the exchange point lies between adjacent reference points and has the same, positive error sign as the point it replaces. Hence the new set remains spatially ordered, and the old error at its points alternates in sign. Its magnitudes are $|h_0|$ everywhere except at $\eta$, where $M=u(\eta)-q(\eta)>|h_0|$.

Set $w_r=\prod_{j\ne r}(z_r-z_j)^{-1}$. Apply the order-$(n+1)$ <divided difference> to $q$ and $v$; both vanish because their degrees are at most $n$. In the new alternation equations, all products $(-1)^rw_r$ have the same sign. Thus the <single-point exchange in polynomial minimax approximation> gives
$$
\boxed{|h_1|=|h_0|+\frac{|w_s|}{\sum_r|w_r|}(M-|h_0|)>|h_0|.}
$$
The <Remez algorithm> repeatedly solves for the reference polynomial and common signed height, locates a larger error extreme, and performs an order-preserving exchange. The reference height bounds the optimum from below and the global error maximum bounds it from above. Their gap provides an optimality stopping certificate. A general implementation also needs the endpoint exchange rule when the new extreme lies outside the reference set.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a line $p(x)=ax+b$, the error $e^x-ax-b$ is strictly convex. Its two endpoint maxima must agree, giving $a=e-1$. Its unique interior minimum is at $c=\log a\in(0,1)$. Equating its magnitude to the endpoint error gives
$$
\boxed{p_1^*(x)=ax+\frac{1+a-a\log a}{2},\qquad
E_1=\frac{1-a+a\log a}{2},\qquad a=e-1.}
$$
The errors at $0,c,1$ are respectively $E_1,-E_1,E_1$. The <equioscillation theorem> therefore certifies this <best uniform approximation> directly.

For degree above one, subtracting a general <polynomial> does not preserve convexity of the error. The required $n+2$ alternating extreme points include additional unknown interior points, and their derivative and equal-height equations form a coupled nonlinear problem. The simple endpoint-and-single-minimum calculation therefore does not extend as a direct higher-degree recipe.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <first-order Sobolev space> is $H^1(\Omega)=\{v\in L^2(\Omega):\partial_1v,\partial_2v\in L^2(\Omega)\}$, with derivatives understood as <weak derivatives> and norm squared $\int_\Omega(v^2+|\nabla v|^2)$.

**The printed normal boundary condition must be distinguished from a conormal condition.** Take $K$ symmetric positive definite, as required for the symmetric matrix requested later. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla v\cdot K\nabla u-\int_{\partial\Omega}v\,\mathbf n\cdot K\nabla u=\int_\Omega fv.
$$
The natural boundary term contains the <conormal derivative> $\mathbf n\cdot K\nabla u$, not the ordinary <normal derivative> $\partial_nu$. On a smooth boundary, writing $\mathbf t$ for its unit tangent, the literal condition $\partial_nu=0$ leaves the term $b\partial_{\mathbf t}u$, where $b=\mathbf n\cdot K\mathbf t$. Its weak form therefore retains a boundary pairing:
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla v\cdot K\nabla u-\left\langle b\,\partial_{\mathbf t}(\operatorname{Tr}u),\operatorname{Tr}v\right\rangle=\int_\Omega fv.
$$
Here the tangential derivative of the <Sobolev trace> can be interpreted in $H^{-1/2}$ and paired against $H^{1/2}$ on a smooth boundary. This form is generally nonsymmetric; the requested usual symmetric energy construction does not follow from the literal boundary condition.

For an explicit failure of the claimed mean-zero compatibility, take the unit disk, $K=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$ and $u=(x^2-y^2)[1-(x^2+y^2)/2]$. It has $\partial_nu=0$ on the circle, but
$$
f=-\nabla\cdot K\nabla u=-2+12x^2-6y^2,\qquad \int_\Omega f=-\frac\pi2\ne0.
$$
Thus replacing the printed normal condition by a zero conormal flux is not harmless.

For the intended zero-<conormal derivative> problem, the requested variational and finite element conclusions are as follows. Seek $u\in H^1(\Omega)$ with
$$
\boxed{a(u,v):=\int_\Omega\nabla v\cdot K\nabla u=\ell(v):=\int_\Omega fv\quad(v\in H^1(\Omega)).}
$$
Putting $v=1$ gives $\int_\Omega f=0$. On a connected domain this condition permits a solution unique modulo constants; the <Poincare-Wirtinger inequality> gives coercivity after fixing the mean.

The energy is $J(v)=\tfrac12a(v,v)-\ell(v)$. A minimizer satisfies $\frac d{dt}J(u+tv)|_{t=0}=a(u,v)-\ell(v)=0$. Conversely, a weak solution satisfies
$$
J(v)-J(u)=\frac12a(v-u,v-u)\geq0,
$$
so it is a minimizer. Testing by $u$ gives the minimum value
$$
\boxed{J_{\min}=-\frac12\ell(u)=-\frac12a(u,u).}
$$
This value is independent of the arbitrary additive constant when $\int f=0$. If the compatibility fails, constant shifts make $J$ unbounded below.

Write $u_h=\sum_jc_j\phi_j$. The <Galerkin method> gives
$$
\boxed{Ac=F,\qquad A_{ij}=\int_\Omega\nabla\phi_i\cdot K\nabla\phi_j,\qquad F_i=\int_\Omega f\phi_i.}
$$
Symmetry of $K$ gives $A^T=A$. For $v_h=\sum_jz_j\phi_j$, $z^TAz=\int_\Omega\nabla v_h\cdot K\nabla v_h\geq0$. Equality forces $v_h$ constant by positive definiteness and connectedness. Since the basis sums to one, the coefficient vector $\mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1)^T$ represents that constant, giving
$$
\boxed{\ker A=\operatorname{span}(\mathbf1),\qquad \mathbf1^TF=\int_\Omega f=0.}
$$
Thus the <stiffness matrix> is symmetric positive semidefinite and singular; the displayed compatibility condition is necessary and sufficient for the finite system. Positive definiteness of $K$ controls every gradient direction, preventing additional zero-energy directions and supplying ellipticity. A degenerate coefficient can introduce further kernel modes, while an indefinite one destroys the minimizing-energy argument.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Take the affine trial space $V_1=\{v\in H^1(a,b):v(a)=1\}$ and the test space $V_0=\{v\in H^1(a,b):v(a)=0\}$. Multiplication by $v\in V_0$ and <integration by parts> incorporate the right <Neumann boundary condition> as a load:
$$
\boxed{u\in V_1,\qquad\int_a^bu'v'\,dx=\int_a^bfv\,dx+2v(b)\quad(v\in V_0).}
$$
For the continuous piecewise-quadratic trial space, impose the left value exactly and use the same equation for every piecewise-quadratic test function vanishing at $a$. With a <basis> $(\psi_j)$ of that test space and $u_h=1+\sum_jc_j\psi_j$, the <finite element> equations are
$$
\boxed{\sum_j\left(\int_a^b\psi_j'\psi_i'\,dx\right)c_j=\int_a^bf\psi_i\,dx+2\psi_i(b).}
$$
The <endpoint Poincaré inequality>, reflected about the interval midpoint, makes the derivative norm a norm on $V_0$. <Galerkin orthogonality> then gives the exact best-approximation identity: for every admissible $v_h$,
$$
\|u'-v_h'\|_2^2=\|u'-u_h'\|_2^2+\|u_h'-v_h'\|_2^2.
$$
Therefore **$u_h$ minimizes the derivative error over all admissible piecewise-quadratic functions**.

To prove the requested error bound, assume $f\in H^1(a,b)$. Then $u\in H^3(a,b)$ and $u'''=-f'$. We need an elementary <Poincare-Wirtinger inequality> on each element $I$ of length $h_I$: if $w\in H^1(I)$ has mean zero, its continuous representative has a zero somewhere, and the <fundamental theorem of calculus> and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $\|w\|_{L^2(I)}\leq h_I\|w'\|_{L^2(I)}$.

Choose an affine function $q_I$ whose slope is the average of $u''$ on $I$ and whose average equals the average of $u'$. Then $w=u'-q_I$ and $w'=u''-q_I'$ both have mean zero. Applying the just-proved bound twice yields
$$
\|u'-q_I\|_{L^2(I)}\leq h_I^2\|u'''\|_{L^2(I)}.
$$
Define a quadratic interpolant on $I$ by integrating $q_I$ from the true left-endpoint value. The equal-average condition ensures that it reaches the true right-endpoint value, so these pieces join to an admissible continuous piecewise-quadratic function $I_hu$. Sum the squared estimates and use the best-approximation property:
$$
\boxed{\|u'-u_h'\|_2\leq\|u'-(I_hu)'\|_2\leq h^2\|u'''\|_2=h^2\|f'\|_2.}
$$
Only the maximum element length is used; equal spacing is unnecessary.

For the <Aubin–Nitsche duality argument>, let $e=u-u_h$ and solve $-z''=e$, $z(a)=0$, $z'(b)=0$. Explicitly $z'(x)=\int_x^be(t)\,dt$, so $\|z''\|_2=\|e\|_2$. Integration by parts and <Galerkin orthogonality> give
$$
\|e\|_2^2=\int_a^be'z'\,dx=\int_a^be'[z'-(J_hz)']\,dx,
$$
where $J_hz$ is its nodal piecewise-linear interpolant, which belongs to the quadratic test space. On each element $(J_hz)'$ is the average of $z'$, so the same mean-zero inequality gives $\|z'-(J_hz)'\|_2\leq h\|z''\|_2$. Canceling $\|e\|_2$ when nonzero proves the additional order:
$$
\boxed{\|u-u_h\|_{L^2(a,b)}\leq h\|u'-u_h'\|_2\leq h^3\|f'\|_2.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=6}

The <bilinear square finite element> contains the terms $1,x,y,xy$. In anticlockwise corner order beginning at $(0,0)$, its <shape functions> are
$$
N_1=(1-x/h)(1-y/h),\quad N_2=(x/h)(1-y/h),\quad
N_3=xy/h^2,\quad N_4=(1-x/h)(y/h).
$$
The <mass matrix> has entries $M_{ij}=\int_0^h\int_0^hN_iN_j\,dx\,dy$. Each product separates into one-dimensional factors, with $\int_0^1(1-s)^2\,ds=\int_0^1s^2\,ds=1/3$ and $\int_0^1s(1-s)\,ds=1/6$. Diagonal, adjacent-corner and opposite-corner entries are respectively $h^2/9$, $h^2/18$, $h^2/36$. Thus
$$
\boxed{M_E=\frac{h^2}{36}\begin{pmatrix}4&2&1&2\\2&4&2&1\\1&2&4&2\\2&1&2&4\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Number global grid nodes by $k=i+(n+1)j$, with $x_i=ih$, $y_j=jh$ and $0\leq i,j\leq n$. For an interior node, four elements contribute to its diagonal, two to each axial neighbour and one to each diagonal neighbour. Its global row therefore has the nine-point stencil
$$
\boxed{\frac{h^2}{36}\begin{pmatrix}1&4&1\\4&16&4\\1&4&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In the stated ordering the axial entries occur at columns $k\pm1$ and $k\pm(n+1)$; the four diagonal ones occur at $k\pm(n+1)\pm1$. No boundary degrees of freedom have been removed.

For general valid quadrilaterals, use an <isoparametric finite element>. Map the unit square to an element by $F(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^4\mathbf x_iN_i(\xi,\eta)$ and define physical <shape functions> $N_i\circ F^{-1}$. Require a one-to-one nondegenerate map and compatible common edges. On a shared edge the map and the trace of each <shape function> are linear between the same endpoints; the neighbouring traces therefore agree. The assembled functions are continuous and lie in $H^1$, giving a <conforming finite element space>. On a non-parallelogram the pulled-back functions are generally not bilinear polynomials in the physical coordinates themselves.
