Original solutions to all three questions of the <nonlinear systems course of the University of Oxford> Part B examination, held on 30 May 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=236[released examination PDF, bundle pages 236–238; paper pages 1–3]. Numbering follows the original paper. In Question 1 the displayed <centre manifold> dynamics are a truncated local approximation; the neglected terms are specified below. At Question 2's parameter value $\mu=-1$, the <fixed-point multipliers> have a <strong resonance of a planar map>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

At a <fixed point>, the first equation gives $y=0$, the second then gives $x=0$, and the third gives $z=-\mu$. Thus there is exactly one <equilibrium> for every real $\mu$:
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(0,0,-\mu).}
$$
The <Jacobian matrix> there is
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&5\epsilon\mu&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det(\lambda I-J)=(\lambda+1)(\lambda^2-5\epsilon\mu\lambda+1).
$$
One <eigenvalue> is $-1$. The other two have product one and sum $5\epsilon\mu$. For $\mu<0$ their real parts are negative, so the <equilibrium> is <asymptotically stable>; for $\mu>0$ both real parts are positive, leaving two unstable directions and one stable direction. At $\mu=0$ the <eigenvalues> are $-1,i,-i$, with
$$
\left.\frac{d}{d\mu}\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm\right|_{\mu=0}=\frac{5\epsilon}{2}>0.
$$
Therefore **the only local <bifurcation> of this <equilibrium> is a <Hopf bifurcation> at $\mu=0$**. A repeated real <eigenvalue> away from zero does not itself cause a <bifurcation>: the <equilibrium> remains <hyperbolic> there.

The cubic <centre manifold> calculation below gives the <Hopf normal form> radial coefficient $-9\epsilon/8<0$, so this is a <supercritical Hopf bifurcation>. Small attracting <periodic orbits> occur on the $\mu>0$ side. In particular, at $\mu=0$ the leading cubic radial drift is inward, although <linear stability> alone cannot decide that boundary case.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Adjoin $\dot\mu=0$ and put $w=z+\mu$. The <extended centre manifold> is a graph $w=H(x,y,\mu)$ tangent to $w=0$ at the origin. The equations become
$$
\dot x=y,\qquad \dot y=-x+5\epsilon\mu y-5\epsilon y w,\qquad \dot w=-w+x^2,\qquad \dot\mu=0.
$$
Here $\epsilon>0$ is fixed. The <centre-manifold invariance equation>, obtained by the <chain rule>, is
$$
H_x y+H_y(-x+5\epsilon\mu y-5\epsilon yH)=-H+x^2.
$$
Write $H=H_2+O(\rho^3)$, where $\rho=|x|+|y|+|\mu|$ and $H_2$ is a homogeneous quadratic <polynomial>. To quadratic order the <centre-manifold invariance equation> is
$$
y\partial_xH_2-x\partial_yH_2=-H_2+x^2.
$$
The terms $\mu x,\mu y,\mu^2$ have zero <coefficients>: their equations are homogeneous, and the operator $1+y\partial_x-x\partial_y$ is invertible on these quadratic <polynomials>. For $H_2=ax^2+bxy+cy^2$, comparison of <coefficients> gives
$$
-b=1-a,\qquad 2a-2c=-b,\qquad b=-c.
$$
Hence $a=3/5$, $b=-2/5$ and $c=2/5$, giving
$$
\boxed{z=-\mu+\frac35x^2-\frac25xy+\frac25y^2+O(\rho^3).}
$$
Substitution into the $y$ equation gives
$$
\boxed{\dot x=y,\qquad
\dot y=-x+\epsilon(5\mu y-3x^2y+2xy^2-2y^3)+O(\rho^4).}
$$
The constant in the <big O notation> may depend on fixed $\epsilon$. Thus the equations printed in part (b) are the retained terms of the local <Taylor series>, rather than an exact identity for the full reduced <vector field>.

For the <Hopf bifurcation> classification in part (a), set $\mu=0$ and use $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=-r\sin\theta$. The radial derivative is $\dot r=(x\dot x+y\dot y)/r$. Its cubic angular mean is
$$
\frac{\epsilon}{r}\left\langle-3x^2y^2+2xy^3-2y^4\right\rangle
=-\epsilon r^3\left(\frac38+\frac68\right)=-\frac{9\epsilon}{8}r^3.
$$
Here $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes the mean over one revolution. Removing the oscillatory cubic terms by a <near-identity transformation> gives this same cubic radial coefficient in the <Hopf normal form>. The linear radial coefficient is $5\epsilon\mu/2$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Apply the <Poincaré-Lindstedt method> to the truncated system from part (b). Since $y=\dot x$, its scalar equation is
$$
\ddot x+x=\epsilon\left(5\mu\dot x-3x^2\dot x+2x\dot x^2-2\dot x^3\right).
$$
Introduce the stretched time $\tau=\omega t$, with <asymptotic expansions>
$$
\omega=1+\epsilon\omega_1+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
x(t)=X(\tau)=A\cos\tau+\epsilon X_1(\tau)+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
Primes in this part mean <derivatives> with respect to $\tau$. At first order,
$$
X_1''+X_1=2\omega_1A\cos\tau-5\mu A\sin\tau
+3A^3\cos^2\tau\sin\tau+2A^3\cos\tau\sin^2\tau+2A^3\sin^3\tau.
$$
The <trigonometric identities>
$$
\cos^2\tau\sin\tau=\frac{\sin\tau+\sin3\tau}{4},\qquad
\cos\tau\sin^2\tau=\frac{\cos\tau-\cos3\tau}{4},\qquad
\sin^3\tau=\frac{3\sin\tau-\sin3\tau}{4}
$$
reduce this to
$$
X_1''+X_1=
\left(2\omega_1A+\frac{A^3}{2}\right)\cos\tau
+\left(-5\mu A+\frac{9A^3}{4}\right)\sin\tau
-\frac{A^3}{2}\cos3\tau+\frac{A^3}{4}\sin3\tau.
$$
A periodic $X_1$ cannot have a <secular term>, so both fundamental-harmonic <coefficients> must vanish. Taking $A>0$ gives
$$
\boxed{A^2=\frac{20\mu}{9},\qquad \omega_1=-\frac{A^2}{4}=-\frac{5\mu}{9}.}
$$
In particular $\mu>0$ is necessary. Once these conditions hold, a bounded particular correction is
$$
X_1=\frac{A^3}{16}\cos3\tau-\frac{A^3}{32}\sin3\tau;
$$
adding fundamental sine and cosine terms adjusts the <phase> and the first correction to the <amplitude>. Consequently the required leading <periodic orbit> is
$$
\boxed{x=A\cos(\omega t)+O(\epsilon),\quad
y=-A\omega\sin(\omega t)+O(\epsilon),\quad
\omega=1-\frac{5\mu\epsilon}{9}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
Here $A$ is the leading <amplitude>, not an assertion that the exact finite-$\epsilon$ <amplitude> is independent of $\epsilon$.

To justify existence beyond formal cancellation, the leading radial displacement in a <Poincaré return map> is $2\pi\epsilon B(r)$, where
$$
B(r)=\frac{5\mu}{2}r-\frac98r^3.
$$
At the positive root $A$, $B'(A)=-5\mu\ne0$. The <implicit function theorem>, applied after dividing the displacement by $\epsilon$, continues this root to a <periodic orbit> for sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$. Its radial <Floquet multiplier> is $1-10\pi\mu\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$, so it is attracting. These statements apply to the truncated system with fixed $\mu>0$; its interpretation as a local approximation to the original three-dimensional system also requires $\mu$ small, so that $A=O(\sqrt\mu)$ stays near the <Hopf bifurcation>.

The <fixed-point> equations give $x=\mu y$ and
$$
0=-\mu x+\mu y+y^3=y\bigl(y^2+\mu-\mu^2\bigr).
$$
Thus the origin exists for every real $\mu$. The nonzero <fixed points> are
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=\left(\pm\mu\sqrt{\mu(\mu-1)},\ \pm\sqrt{\mu(\mu-1)}\right),\qquad \mu<0\ \text{or}\ \mu>1,}
$$
with the same sign in both positions. At $\mu=0$ or $\mu=1$ these expressions coalesce with the origin and do not give additional <fixed points>. There are no nonzero real <fixed points> for $0<\mu<1$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Jacobian matrix> at the origin and its <characteristic polynomial> are
$$
M_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mu\\-\mu&1+\mu\end{pmatrix},\qquad
p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-(1+\mu)\lambda+\mu^2.
$$
The <fixed-point multipliers> are
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{1+\mu\pm\sqrt{(1-\mu)(1+3\mu)}}{2}.
$$
By the <Jury stability criterion>, both lie strictly inside the <unit circle> exactly when
$$
|\mu^2|<1,\qquad p(1)=\mu(\mu-1)>0,\qquad p(-1)=\mu^2+\mu+2>0.
$$
The last condition always holds, since $\mu^2+\mu+2=(\mu+1/2)^2+7/4$. Therefore **the origin is <asymptotically stable> exactly for $-1<\mu<0$ among the hyperbolic parameter values**.

For completeness, its <linear stability> throughout the parameter line is as follows. When $\mu<-1$, the complex-conjugate <Floquet multipliers> have <modulus> $|\mu|>1$, so the origin repels. When $-1<\mu<-1/3$, their <modulus> is $|\mu|<1$, so it attracts with oscillation. At $\mu=-1/3$ the repeated <Floquet multiplier> is $1/3$, still attracting. For $-1/3<\mu<0$, both <Floquet multipliers> are real and in $(0,1)$. For $0<\mu<1$, $p(1)<0$ and $p(0)>0$, so one <Floquet multiplier> lies in $(0,1)$ and the other exceeds one: the origin is a <saddle fixed point of a map>. For $\mu>1$, the complex-conjugate <Floquet multipliers> have <modulus> $\mu>1$, so it repels.

Possible changes at the <unit circle> are exhausted by $p(1)=0$, $p(-1)=0$, or a nonreal pair with product one. They give
$$
\boxed{\mu=-1,\quad0,\quad1.}
$$
At $\mu=0$ the <Floquet multipliers> are $0,1$; the nonzero <fixed-point> branches in part (a) meet the origin, and part (c) identifies a <subcritical pitchfork bifurcation>. Indeed at $\mu=0$ the exact restricted map is $y\mapsto y+y^3$, which makes the origin unstable despite its neutral linear direction.

At $\mu=-1$ the <Floquet multipliers> are $i,-i$: this is a 1:4 <strong resonance of a planar map>, coinciding with the change from repelling to attracting as $\mu$ increases. A generic nonresonant <Neimark–Sacker bifurcation> conclusion would be unjustified. At $\mu=1$ there is a double <Floquet multiplier> $+1$; the matrix has a nontrivial <Jordan block>, and the two further nonzero <fixed-point> branches meet the origin. <Linear stability> alone is inconclusive at these unit-modulus boundary values. The repeated <Floquet multiplier> at $\mu=-1/3$ is strictly inside the <unit circle> and gives no <bifurcation>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Adjoin $\mu_{n+1}=\mu_n$. At the origin the stable direction is the $x$ axis, with <Floquet multiplier> zero, while the $y$ and parameter directions have <Floquet multiplier> one. By the <centre manifold theorem for a discrete dynamical system>, write the <extended centre manifold> as
$$
x=h(y,\mu),\qquad h(0,0)=0,\qquad Dh(0,0)=0.
$$
On it the scalar map is $Y=(1+\mu)y-\mu h(y,\mu)+y^3$. Its discrete <centre-manifold invariance equation> is
$$
h(Y,\mu)=\mu y.
$$
Put $h=h_2+O(\rho^3)$ with $\rho=|y|+|\mu|$. Since $Y=y+O(\rho^2)$, the quadratic equation is simply $h_2(y,\mu)=\mu y$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x=\mu y+O(\rho^3).}
$$
In particular, setting the stable variable identically to zero would lose the leading parameter correction. The reduced map through total degree three is
$$
\boxed{y_{n+1}=y_n+\mu y_n-\mu^2y_n+y_n^3+O(\rho^4).}
$$
In the natural <pitchfork bifurcation> scaling $\mu=O(y^2)$ this has leading form $y_{n+1}=y_n+\mu y_n+y_n^3$. Its nonzero <fixed points> satisfy $y^2\sim-\mu$, and their reduced <fixed-point multipliers> satisfy $1-2\mu+O(\mu^2)>1$ for small $\mu<0$. The transverse <Floquet multiplier> remains inside the <unit circle>. Therefore **the <bifurcation> at $\mu=0$ is a <subcritical pitchfork bifurcation>: the origin attracts for small negative $\mu$, each nonzero branch is a <saddle fixed point of a map>, and the origin is a <saddle fixed point of a map> for small positive $\mu$**.

An exact check uses the full <Jacobian matrix> on either nonzero branch. Its <trace> is $T_*=1-2\mu+3\mu^2$ and its <determinant> is $D_*=\mu^2$. Thus
$$
p_*(1)=1-T_*+D_*=2\mu(1-\mu)<0
$$
whenever a nonzero branch exists. Also $p_*(0)=\mu^2>0$, so one real <Floquet multiplier> lies in $(0,1)$ and the other in $(1,\infty)$. This proves that each nonzero branch is a <saddle fixed point of a map> throughout $\mu<0$ and $\mu>1$, in particular near the requested <bifurcation>.

= Solution

Use $y$ vertically and $\mu$ horizontally in the <bifurcation diagram>; the other coordinate is recovered from $x=\mu y$. The origin is the horizontal branch $y=0$. Draw it solid on $-1<\mu<0$, where it is <asymptotically stable>, and dashed on the remaining <hyperbolic> intervals, where it is unstable. Its three <nonhyperbolic fixed points> in parameter space are marked at $\mu=-1,0,1$; open markers distinguish these boundary cases from the strict <linear stability> classifications on the intervening intervals.

The nonzero <fixed-point> branches are $y=\pm\sqrt{\mu(\mu-1)}$ for $\mu<0$ and $\mu>1$. Each is dashed because it is a <saddle fixed point of a map>, as proved in part (c). Near zero their shape is $y\sim\pm\sqrt{-\mu}$: the unstable branches lie on the same parameter side as the attracting origin, displaying the <subcritical pitchfork bifurcation>. The branches issuing from $\mu=1$ have $y\sim\pm\sqrt{\mu-1}$. At $\mu=-1$ no additional <fixed-point> branch appears; the <Floquet multipliers> cross the <unit circle> at $\pm i$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For the <left shift>, $\sigma^p(s)=s$ precisely when the <binary sequence> is obtained by repeating its first $p$ symbols. There are exactly $2^p$ such <sequences>, although some have smaller least period. Hence the set of all periodic <sequences> is a <countable union> of finite sets and is at most countable. Passing from periodic points to <periodic orbits> cannot increase this cardinality.

To prove infinitude, for every integer $p\geq1$ take the repeated word $(0^{p-1}1)^\infty$. Its least period is $p$, because its ones are separated by exactly $p$ positions; a smaller positive period would carry a one to a zero. Distinct least periods give distinct <periodic orbits>. Thus **there are countably infinitely many <periodic orbits>**.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The space $\Sigma$ of all infinite <binary sequences> is <uncountable> by the <Cantor diagonal argument>: given any list $s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\ldots$, the <sequence> $t_i=1-s_i^{(i)}$ differs from its $i$th entry at position $i$, so it is absent from the list. Part (i) shows that only countably many <sequences> are periodic. Their complement is therefore <uncountable>.

Each <forward orbit> $\{\sigma^n(s):n\geq0\}$ is countable. If there were only countably many nonperiodic <forward orbits>, their union together with the periodic points would be countable, contradicting the preceding argument. Hence **there are uncountably many nonperiodic <orbits>**, rather than merely uncountably many nonperiodic starting points.

One can also exclude eventual periodicity. An eventually periodic <sequence> is specified by a finite initial word and a finite repeating word, so only countably many exist. Removing all of them still leaves an <uncountable set>. This proves the assertion even if “nonperiodic” is understood to exclude <orbits> that eventually enter a <periodic orbit>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Construct one infinite <binary sequence> $u$ by concatenating every binary word of length one, then every word of length two, then every word of length three, and so on, in <lexicographic order> within each length. Every finite binary word occurs somewhere in $u$.

Given any target <sequence> $s$ and any $\eta>0$, choose $N$ such that $2^{-N}<\eta$. The word $s_1\cdots s_N$ occurs in $u$, beginning after some $k$ symbols. Thus $\sigma^ku$ agrees with $s$ in its first $N$ positions, and the prescribed <metric> gives
$$
d(\sigma^ku,s)\leq\sum_{i>N}2^{-i}=2^{-N}<\eta.
$$
Every open <open ball> about every point therefore meets the <forward orbit> of $u$. **This <forward orbit> is <dense> in $\Sigma$.** The argument identifies finite-prefix <cylinder sets> as arbitrarily small neighborhoods in this <metric>; no binary-expansion convention for real numbers is needed.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the real <logistic map>, $f(x)=x$ factors as
$$
x\bigl(\mu-1-\mu x\bigr)=0.
$$
There is always the <fixed point> $x_0=0$. If $\mu\ne0$ there is also $x_*=1-1/\mu$, distinct from zero unless $\mu=1$. At $\mu=0$ the map is constant and zero is its only <fixed point>. Negative $\mu$ must be retained because the paper specifies $x\in\mathbb R$.

Since $f'(x)=\mu(1-2x)$, the <fixed-point multipliers> are
$$
\lambda_0=\mu,\qquad \lambda_*=2-\mu.
$$
The <fixed point stability for an iteration> criterion gives **$x_0$ attracting for $-1<\mu<1$, and $x_*$ attracting for $1<\mu<3$**. The respective <fixed points> are repelling when $|\mu|>1$ and $|2-\mu|>1$. At the unit-modulus boundaries the linear criterion needs a nonlinear supplement.

At $\mu=1$, the two <fixed-point> branches meet and exchange stability: this is a <transcritical bifurcation>. Explicitly, with $\delta=\mu-1$,
$$
f(x)-x=\delta x-(1+\delta)x^2,
$$
which has the required nonzero parameter and quadratic terms. At the critical value itself, $f(x)=x-x^2$ attracts small positive points and pushes negative points away, so the merged <fixed point> is not stable from both sides.

At $\mu=-1$ the origin’s <fixed-point multiplier> crosses $-1$, while at $\mu=3$ the nonzero <fixed-point multiplier> crosses $-1$. Both are <period-doubling bifurcations>. To check existence and stability of the new <periodic orbit>, factor the second <iteration of a map>:
$$
f^2(x)-x=-x(\mu x-\mu+1)
\left(\mu^2x^2-\mu(\mu+1)x+\mu+1\right).
$$
The two non-fixed roots are
$$
q_\pm=\frac{\mu+1\pm\sqrt{(\mu-3)(\mu+1)}}{2\mu}.
$$
They form a real <period-two orbit> for $\mu<-1$ or $\mu>3$. Its <periodic-orbit multiplier> is
$$
f'(q_-)f'(q_+)=4+2\mu-\mu^2.
$$
It is less than one in <absolute value> just below $-1$ and just above $3$, respectively. Thus these are <supercritical bifurcations> in the direction where the parent <fixed point> loses stability. At the critical values the <fixed points> themselves are weakly attracting: at $\mu=-1$, $f^2(x)=x-2x^3+x^4$; at $\mu=3$, setting $u=x-2/3$ gives $u\mapsto u-18u^3-27u^4$ after two steps. Both shrink sufficiently small nonzero <absolute values>. The <bifurcation> values for finite <fixed points> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu=-1\text{ (period doubling)},\quad1\text{ (transcritical)},\quad3\text{ (period doubling)}.}
$$
The disappearance of the formula for $x_*$ at $\mu=0$ corresponds to that branch going to infinity, not a local <bifurcation> at a finite <fixed point>.

Solving $\mu x(1-x)=1$ by the <quadratic formula> gives
$$
x_\pm=\frac12\pm\sqrt{\frac14-\frac1\mu}.
$$
For $\mu>4$, $0<x_-<1/2<x_+<1$. On $L=[0,x_-]$ the <derivative> $f'(x)=\mu(1-2x)$ is strictly positive, while on $R=[x_+,1]$ it is strictly negative. Thus the restrictions are strictly <monotonic functions>. Their endpoint values are
$$
f(0)=0,\quad f(x_-)=1,\qquad f(x_+)=1,\quad f(1)=0.
$$
By <continuity> and strict <monotonicity>, **each restriction maps its interval bijectively onto $[0,1]$**, with opposite orientations. The corresponding <inverse functions> are
$$
\boxed{g_L(v)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4v/\mu}}2,\qquad
g_R(v)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4v/\mu}}2,\qquad 0\leq v\leq1.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $G=g_{s_1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{s_{n-1}}$. Because $g_{s_n}([0,1])\subseteq[0,1]$, taking the image under the same <function composition> gives
$$
\boxed{I_{s_1\cdots s_n}=G(g_{s_n}([0,1]))\subseteq G([0,1])=I_{s_1\cdots s_{n-1}}.}
$$
This uses preservation of set inclusion by every <function>, so decreasing <inverse branches> cause no difficulty. Since $f\circ g_L=f\circ g_R=\operatorname{id}_{[0,1]}$, cancellation of the first <inverse branch> gives the equality of images
$$
\boxed{f(I_{s_1\cdots s_n})=I_{s_2\cdots s_n}.}
$$
These compact <intervals> are <itinerary cylinders>: their symbols record successive positions under <iteration of a map>. In particular,
$$
\boxed{x\in I_{LLR}\ \Longrightarrow\ x\in L,\quad f(x)\in L,\quad f^2(x)\in R.}
$$
Conversely these three memberships allow successive substitution of the appropriate <inverse branches>, and therefore characterize $I_{LLR}$ exactly.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The set $I^n$ consists precisely of points whose first $n$ positions $x,f(x),\ldots,f^{n-1}(x)$ all lie in $L\cup R$. Hence
$$
\Lambda=\{x\in[0,1]: f^k(x)\in L\cup R\text{ for every }k\geq0\}.
$$
Deleting the first position preserves this condition, proving $f(\Lambda)\subseteq\Lambda$. For the reverse inclusion, if $y\in\Lambda$, take $x=g_L(y)$. Then $x\in L$ and $f^k(x)=f^{k-1}(y)\in L\cup R$ for every $k\geq1$. Thus $x\in\Lambda$ and $f(x)=y$. In fact both <inverse branches> give preimages in $\Lambda$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f(\Lambda)=\Lambda,}
$$
which proves full invariance, not merely forward invariance.

For any infinite symbolic <sequence> $s$, the <itinerary cylinders> $I_{s_1\cdots s_n}$ are nonempty compact <nested intervals>. The <nested interval theorem> gives at least one point in their intersection. Every $x\in\Lambda$ has a unique itinerary because $L\cap R=\varnothing$. To justify that the itinerary also determines a unique point, it suffices to show the <interval> lengths shrink. The fact that <logistic inverse branches contract in an angular coordinate> works for every $\mu>4$; a direct claim that $|g_L'|,|g_R'|<1$ in the original coordinate would fail for some $\mu$ just above four.

Use the <homeomorphism> $h(u)=\sin^2(\pi u/2)$ of $[0,1]$. The transformed <inverse branches> are
$$
G_L(u)=h^{-1}(g_L(h(u)))=\frac1\pi\arcsin\left(\frac2{\sqrt\mu}\sin\frac{\pi u}{2}\right),\qquad
G_R(u)=1-G_L(u).
$$
Their <derivatives> satisfy
$$
|G_L'(u)|=|G_R'(u)|=
\frac{\cos(\pi u/2)}{\sqrt\mu\sqrt{1-(4/\mu)\sin^2(\pi u/2)}}\leq\frac1{\sqrt\mu}<1.
$$
The inequality follows by squaring and using $4/\mu<1$. Thus every length-$n$ transformed <itinerary cylinder> has length at most $\mu^{-n/2}$. Since $|h'|\leq\pi/2$, its original <interval> has length at most $(\pi/2)\mu^{-n/2}$ and shrinks to a point. This gives the claimed one-to-one identification with the one-sided <full shift>.

Finally part (ii) gives
$$
f(x)\in\bigcap_{n\geq2} I_{s_2\cdots s_n},\qquad
\boxed{s_1s_2s_3\cdots\ \longmapsto\ s_2s_3\cdots.}
$$
Thus $f$ acts by the <left shift>. The identification is a <topological conjugacy>: matching long prefixes forces closeness by the shrinking estimate, while distinct finite <itinerary cylinders> are disjoint compact sets and so their inverse coding is <continuous>. In particular $f$ need not be one-to-one on $\Lambda$, just as a one-sided <left shift> has two possible first symbols before any given tail.
