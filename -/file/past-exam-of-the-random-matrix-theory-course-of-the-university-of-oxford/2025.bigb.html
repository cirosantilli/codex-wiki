= Solution

Taking the <transpose> gives $M^T=(X^T+X)/\sqrt2=M$, so $M$ is a <symmetric matrix>. For $i<j$, $M_{ij}=(X_{ij}+X_{ji})/\sqrt2$ has the <standard normal distribution>, whereas $M_{ii}=\sqrt2X_{ii}$ has the <normal distribution> $N(0,2)$. Distinct upper-triangular entries depend on disjoint sets of the original <independent random variables>, so they remain <independent>.

Consequently the <joint probability density function>, relative to $\prod_{i\leq j}dm_{ij}$, is
$$
\boxed{
f((m_{ij})_{i\leq j})
=(4\pi)^{-n/2}(2\pi)^{-n(n-1)/4}
\exp\!\left(-\frac14\sum_i m_{ii}^2-\frac12\sum_{i<j}m_{ij}^2\right)
=\frac{\exp[-\operatorname{tr}(m^2)/4]}
{(4\pi)^{n/2}(2\pi)^{n(n-1)/4}}.}
$$
Here $\operatorname{tr}(m^2)=\sum_i m_{ii}^2+2\sum_{i<j}m_{ij}^2$ because $m$ is a <symmetric matrix>. This is the **<Gaussian orthogonal ensemble>**, with off-diagonal <variance> one and diagonal <variance> two; the rescaled <matrix> $M/\sqrt n$ has the corresponding $1/n$ off-diagonal <variance>.

We will also use its entry <covariance>. Expanding in the <independent> entries of $X$, for which $\mathbb E[X_{ij}X_{kl}]=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}$, gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_{ij}M_{kl}]
=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}.}
$$
The symbols $\delta_{ab}$ are <Kronecker deltas>. This formula includes the diagonal case, where the two terms both contribute.

The <joint probability distribution> of all entries of $X$ is unchanged by $X\mapsto-X$, so $M$ and $-M$ have the same <probability distribution>. For the specified cyclic product $P=M_{i_1i_2}\cdots M_{i_ki_1}$, this transformation multiplies $P$ by $(-1)^k$. Every such product is an <integrable random variable>, since <normal random variables> have finite <moments> of all orders. Thus, for odd $k$,
$$
\mathbb EP=\mathbb E[(-1)^kP]=-\mathbb EP,
\qquad\boxed{\mathbb E[M_{i_1i_2}\cdots M_{i_ki_1}]=0.}
$$
Repeated indices cause no difficulty: all factors change sign simultaneously. It would be incorrect to assume that these factors are <independent>.

The probabilistic form of <Wick's theorem> is <Isserlis's theorem>. If $(Y_1,\ldots,Y_k)$ is a centered <random vector> with a <multivariate Gaussian distribution> and <covariance matrix> $C$, its odd product <moments> vanish, and, for even $k$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\prod_{a=1}^kY_a
=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal P_2(k)}\prod_{\{a,b\}\in\pi}C_{ab}.}
$$
Here $\mathcal P_2(k)$ is the set of <perfect matchings> of the $k$ labelled positions. The formula allows repeated coordinates and singular <covariance matrices>. To see the pairing rule, differentiate the <moment-generating function> $\exp(t^TCt/2)$ once in each coordinate and set $t=0$: only terms pairing all differentiated coordinates survive, each with the displayed product of <covariances>.

Take $Y_a=M_{i_ai_{a+1}}$, with $i_{k+1}=i_1$. These variables are linear combinations of the original <independent> <normal random variables>, hence are jointly <Gaussian>. They have zero <means>, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_{i_1i_2}\cdots M_{i_ki_1}]
=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal P_2(k)}\prod_{\{a,b\}\in\pi}
\left(\delta_{i_ai_b}\delta_{i_{a+1}i_{b+1}}
+\delta_{i_ai_{b+1}}\delta_{i_{a+1}i_b}\right).}
$$
Substituting the entry <covariance> from part (a)(i) identifies each parenthesis with $\mathbb E[M_{i_ai_{a+1}}M_{i_bi_{b+1}}]$, giving the requested identity.

The <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> identifies the sum of the $k$th powers of the <eigenvalues> with the <trace> of the $k$th <matrix> power. Therefore the <moment> of the <empirical spectral measure> is
$$
\boxed{m_k(n)=n^{-1-k/2}\operatorname{tr}(M^k)
=n^{-1-k/2}\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_k=1}^n
M_{i_1i_2}M_{i_2i_3}\cdots M_{i_ki_1}.}
$$
For odd $k$, every summand has zero <expectation> by part (a)(ii), so $\boxed{\mathbb E[m_k(n)]=0}$ for every $n$.

For the second <moment>, use $M_{ji}=M_{ij}$. There are $n$ diagonal entries with <variance> two and $n(n-1)$ ordered off-diagonal entries with <variance> one. Their <means> vanish, giving
$$
\mathbb E[m_2(n)]
=n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb E[M_{ij}^2]
=\frac{2n+n(n-1)}{n^2}
=1+\frac1n.
$$
Hence $\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[m_2(n)]=1}$.

Write the four cyclic factors as $A=M_{ij}$, $B=M_{jk}$, $C=M_{kl}$ and $D=M_{li}$. The <Gaussian moment pairing theorem> gives three terms, corresponding to $(AB)(CD)$, $(AC)(BD)$ and $(AD)(BC)$. We sum each term over $i,j,k,l$.

For $(AB)(CD)$, the entry <covariance> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i,j,k,l}\mathbb E[AB]\mathbb E[CD]
&=\sum_{i,j,k,l}(\delta_{ik}+\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk})
(\delta_{ik}+\delta_{kl}\delta_{li})\\
&=n^3+2n^2+n.
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed the four products leave respectively three, two, two and one free indices. A cyclic relabelling gives exactly the same sum for $(AD)(BC)$.

For the crossing pairing $(AC)(BD)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i,j,k,l}\mathbb E[AC]\mathbb E[BD]
&=\sum_{i,j,k,l}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})
(\delta_{jl}\delta_{ki}+\delta_{ji}\delta_{kl})\\
&=n^2+3n.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first product allows $i=k$ and $j=l$ freely, whereas each other product forces all four indices to coincide. Combining the three pairings and the <trace> normalization,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[m_4(n)]
=\frac{2(n^3+2n^2+n)+(n^2+3n)}{n^3}
=2+\frac5n+\frac5{n^2}\ \longrightarrow\ 2.}
$$

Let $g_i=(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{in})$ be row $i$ of $X$, viewed as a real <vector>. These are <independent> standard <Gaussian> <random vectors>, and $(XX^T)_{ij}=g_i\cdot g_j$. The <trace> expression for the <empirical spectral measure> gives
$$
\widetilde m_k(n)
=n^{-k-1}\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_k}
(g_{i_1}\cdot g_{i_2})\cdots(g_{i_k}\cdot g_{i_1}).
$$
We need the norm <moments>
$$
\mathbb E\lVert g_i\rVert^2=n,\qquad
\mathbb E\lVert g_i\rVert^4=n(n+2),\qquad
\mathbb E\lVert g_i\rVert^6=n(n+2)(n+4).
$$
For completeness, $S=\lVert g_i\rVert^2$ has the <chi-squared distribution> with $n$ degrees of freedom. By <independence> and the <Gaussian integral>, its <moment-generating function> is $\mathbb E e^{sS}=(1-2s)^{-n/2}$ for $s<1/2$. Differentiating at zero once, twice and three times proves these three identities.

For the first <moment>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\widetilde m_1(n)]
=n^{-2}\sum_i\mathbb E\lVert g_i\rVert^2=1.}
$$
For the second <moment>, if $i\ne j$, <conditional expectation> given $g_i$ gives $\mathbb E[(g_i\cdot g_j)^2\mid g_i]=\lVert g_i\rVert^2$, hence an unconditional value $n$. Separating equal and unequal row indices yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\widetilde m_2(n)]
=n^{-3}\{n\,n(n+2)+n(n-1)n\}=2+\frac1n.}
$$

For the third <moment>, the summand is $(g_i\cdot g_j)(g_j\cdot g_k)(g_k\cdot g_i)$. There are three disjoint cases.

* If $i,j,k$ are all different, average over $g_k$ first. Its <covariance matrix> is the identity, so the <conditional expectation> of the last two factors is $g_j\cdot g_i$. The summand's <expectation> is therefore $\mathbb E(g_i\cdot g_j)^2=n$. There are $n(n-1)(n-2)$ such triples.
* If exactly two indices agree, cyclic symmetry reduces the calculation to $i=j\ne k$. The product is $\lVert g_i\rVert^2(g_i\cdot g_k)^2$, whose <conditional expectation> given $g_i$ is $\lVert g_i\rVert^4$. Its <expectation> is $n(n+2)$, and there are $3n(n-1)$ such triples.
* If all three indices agree, the product is $\lVert g_i\rVert^6$, with <expectation> $n(n+2)(n+4)$. There are $n$ choices.

Consequently
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\widetilde m_3(n)]
&=n^{-4}\{n^2(n-1)(n-2)
+3n^2(n-1)(n+2)+n^2(n+2)(n+4)\}\\
&=5+\frac6n+\frac4{n^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The three limiting <expected values> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[\widetilde m_k(n)]
=\begin{cases}1,&k=1,\\2,&k=2,\\5,&k=3.\end{cases}}
$$

Let $P$ be the $3$-by-$3$ evaluation <matrix> $P_{i,\ell+1}=p_\ell(\lambda_i)$. The kernel evaluation <matrix> is the <Gram matrix> $PP^T$, so <multiplicativity of the determinant> gives
$$
\det[K_3(\lambda_i,\lambda_j)]_{i,j=1}^3
=\det(PP^T)=(\det P)^2.
$$
Because the <polynomials> are <monic> of consecutive degrees, their coefficient <matrix> relative to $1,x,x^2$ is a <triangular matrix> with unit diagonal. It has <determinant> one, so the <Vandermonde determinant invariance under monic basis change> gives
$$
\det P=\det\begin{pmatrix}
1&\lambda_1&\lambda_1^2\\
1&\lambda_2&\lambda_2^2\\
1&\lambda_3&\lambda_3^2
\end{pmatrix}
=\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_j-\lambda_i).
$$

$$
\boxed{\rho_3(\boldsymbol\lambda)
=c_3\det[K_3(\lambda_i,\lambda_j)]_{i,j=1}^3
\exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i^2\right).}
$$
No <orthogonality> of the <polynomials> is needed for this step.

<Gaudin's lemma> is the <projection kernel determinant integration> identity. Suppose an <integral kernel> $K$ satisfies
$$
\int K(x,y)K(y,z)\,d\mu(y)=K(x,z),
\qquad \int K(x,x)\,d\mu(x)=N,
$$
with the relevant integrals absolutely convergent. Define $D_k(x_1,\ldots,x_k)=\det[K(x_i,x_j)]_{i,j=1}^k$ and $D_0=1$. Then
$$
\boxed{\int D_k(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\,d\mu(x_k)
=(N-k+1)D_{k-1}(x_1,\ldots,x_{k-1}).}
$$
In particular, a <finite-rank projection kernel> of rank $N$ has $\int D_N\,d\mu^N=N!$.

One can see the factor directly from the <permutation> expansion of the <determinant>. Terms whose <permutation> fixes $k$ integrate to $ND_{k-1}$. Every <permutation> on the other $k-1$ indices admits $k-1$ insertions of $k$ into a cycle. Each insertion reverses its sign, and the reproducing identity contracts its two adjacent kernel factors into one. These terms integrate to $-(k-1)D_{k-1}$, proving the formula.

$$
\boxed{\kappa_0=\kappa_1=(2\pi)^{1/4},\qquad
\kappa_2=\sqrt2(2\pi)^{1/4}.}
$$
Then $\phi_0,\phi_1,\phi_2$ are an <orthonormal set> for <Lebesgue measure>. Expanding the finite sums gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\widetilde K_3(x,y)\widetilde K_3(y,z)\,dy
&=\sum_{\ell,m=0}^2\phi_\ell(x)\phi_m(z)
\int\phi_\ell(y)\phi_m(y)\,dy\\
&=\sum_{\ell=0}^2\phi_\ell(x)\phi_\ell(z)
=\widetilde K_3(x,z).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus this is an <orthogonal polynomial projection kernel>, explicitly
$$
\boxed{\widetilde K_3(x,y)
=\frac{e^{-(x^2+y^2)/4}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
\left[1+xy+\frac{(x^2-1)(y^2-1)}2\right].}
$$
The positive constants above are forced by the reproducing identity: before normalization, its convolution multiplies each independent term $\phi_\ell(x)\phi_\ell(z)$ by $\sqrt{2\pi}h_\ell/\kappa_\ell^2$. Each multiplier must equal one. Changing the sign of a real $\kappa_\ell$ leaves the kernel unchanged.

The <correlation functions of a point process> use factorial normalization:
$$
R_3^{(k)}(x_1,\ldots,x_k)
=\frac{3!}{(3-k)!}\int_{\mathbb R^{3-k}}
\rho_3(x_1,x_2,x_3)\,dx_{k+1}\cdots dx_3.
$$
Start with $\rho_3=D_3/6$. By <Gaudin's lemma>, integrating $D_3$ once gives $D_2$, and integrating $D_2$ once gives $2D_1$. The factorial factors cancel, so the <eigenvalues> form a <determinantal point process> with
$$
\boxed{R_3^{(1)}(x)=\widetilde K_3(x,x),}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{R_3^{(2)}(x,y)
=\det\begin{pmatrix}
\widetilde K_3(x,x)&\widetilde K_3(x,y)\\
\widetilde K_3(y,x)&\widetilde K_3(y,y)
\end{pmatrix}
=\widetilde K_3(x,x)\widetilde K_3(y,y)-\widetilde K_3(x,y)^2.}
$$
The last equality uses the real symmetry of this kernel. In particular, $\int R_3^{(1)}=3$ and $\iint R_3^{(2)}=6$, as required for three points and six ordered pairs of distinct points.

The <correlation function of a point process> is even and tends to zero as $|\lambda|\to\infty$. Its <derivative> factors as
$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}R_3^{(1)}(\lambda)
=-\frac{\lambda(\lambda^2-1)(\lambda^2-3)}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}
e^{-\lambda^2/2}.
$$
Consequently its local maxima occur at $0,\pm\sqrt3$, and its local minima at $\pm1$. The heights are
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_3^{(1)}(0)&=\frac3{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\simeq0.5984,\\
R_3^{(1)}(\pm1)&=\frac{2e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\simeq0.4839,\\
R_3^{(1)}(\pm\sqrt3)&=\frac{6e^{-3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\simeq0.5341.
\end{aligned}
$$

\Image[2025-one-point-intensity.png]
{title=The three peaks of the GUE one-point correlation function}
{description=Original plot of the exact <correlation function of a point process> calculated above. Filled markers indicate local maxima at $-\sqrt3,0,\sqrt3$; hollow markers indicate the local minima at $-1,1$. The total area is three.}

For an interval $I$, $\int_I R_3^{(1)}(\lambda)\,d\lambda$ is the <expected value> of the number of <eigenvalues> in $I$. Thus equally short intervals have the highest expected counts near
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-\sqrt3,\quad0,\quad\sqrt3,}
$$
with the central peak highest. Each exchangeably labelled <eigenvalue> has the same marginal <probability density function> $R_3^{(1)}/3$. The three peaks should not be interpreted as three separate marginal densities for ordered <eigenvalues>.

In this case the most likely joint configuration also lies at these three positions. On the ordered chamber, the logarithm of the <joint probability density function>, up to a constant, is
$$
L(\boldsymbol\lambda)=2\sum_{i<j}\log(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)
-\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i^2.
$$
Its stationary equations are $\lambda_i=2\sum_{j\ne i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^{-1}$, satisfied by $(-\sqrt3,0,\sqrt3)$. For every nonzero real <vector> $v$, the second directional <derivative> is
$$
D^2L[v,v]=-\sum_i v_i^2
-2\sum_{i<j}\frac{(v_i-v_j)^2}{(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2}<0.
$$
Thus $L$ is strictly <concave> on this convex chamber, proving that the stationary configuration is its unique global maximum. Permuting the labels gives the other maxima on $\mathbb R^3$.

The <characteristic polynomial> of the <rotation matrix> is
$$
\det(M-zI)=(\cos U-z)^2+\sin^2U
=z^2-2z\cos U+1.
$$
Its roots are $\cos U\pm i\sin U$, so
$$
\boxed{e^{i\Theta^+}=e^{iU},\qquad e^{i\Theta^-}=e^{-iU},
\qquad\Theta^\pm=\pm U\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
For example $(1,i)^T$ is an <eigenvector> for $e^{iU}$ and $(1,-i)^T$ for $e^{-iU}$. At $U=0$ or $\pi$ the two <eigenvalues> coincide; these events have <probability> zero under the <uniform distribution>.

The difference of the two <eigenvalues> is $2i\sin U$, hence $\Delta=\sin^2U$. Its <expectation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\Delta=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2u\,du=\frac12.}

For $0\leq x\leq1$, put $\alpha=\arcsin\sqrt{x}\in[0,\pi/2]$. The set of angles satisfying $|\sin u|\leq\sqrt{x}$ consists of $[0,\alpha]$, $[\pi-\alpha,\pi+\alpha]$ and $[2\pi-\alpha,2\pi]$, of total length $4\alpha$. Thus the full <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\boxed{F_\Delta(x)=
\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
\dfrac2\pi\arcsin\sqrt{x},&0\leq x\leq1,\\
1,&x>1.
\end{cases}}
$$
Differentiating on $(0,1)$ gives the <probability density function>
$$
\boxed{f_\Delta(x)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}},\qquad0<x<1.}
$$
This is the <arcsine distribution>, equivalently the <beta distribution> with both parameters $1/2$. It has no atoms at either endpoint.

The <characteristic function> of $B(t)\sim N(0,t)$ gives $\mathbb E e^{2iB(t)}=e^{-2t}$. Taking real parts and using $\sin^2b=(1-\cos2b)/2$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\Delta(t)=\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2,
\qquad \lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb E\Delta(t)=\frac12.}
$$
There is also a direct check from the <stochastic differential equation>. Its <Itô integral> has zero <expectation> because its integrand is bounded. Thus $m(t)=\mathbb E\Delta(t)$ satisfies $m'(t)=1-2m(t)$ and $m(0)=0$, whose solution is the same expression.

= Solution

Interpret the source's missing variable in the limit arrow as $t\to\infty$. We prove <convergence in distribution> by showing that the angle of the <Brownian motion> becomes uniform on the <circle group>.

Let $Z_t=e^{iB(t)}$. For every integer $m$, its angular <Fourier coefficients> satisfy
$$
\mathbb E[Z_t^m]=e^{-m^2t/2}
\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}1,&m=0,\\0,&m\ne0.\end{cases}
$$
These are precisely the <Fourier coefficients> of the <uniform distribution> on the <unit circle>. Thus <expectations> of every <trigonometric polynomial> converge to its uniform angular average. By the <Stone-Weierstrass theorem>, <trigonometric polynomials> approximate continuous functions on the <unit circle> uniformly. Given an approximation error $\varepsilon$, the discrepancy of <expectations> is at most the polynomial discrepancy plus $2\varepsilon$. Taking $t\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\to0$ proves <weak convergence of probability measures> to uniform angular measure.

The <function> $z\mapsto(\operatorname{Im}z)^2$ is continuous on the <unit circle>, so the <continuous mapping theorem> and part (a)(ii) imply
$$
\boxed{\Delta(t)\ \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathrm d}\ A,
\qquad A\sim\operatorname{Beta}\!\left(\frac12,\frac12\right).}
$$
Equivalently, the limiting <arcsine distribution> has <cumulative distribution function> $2\arcsin\sqrt{x}/\pi$ for $0\leq x\leq1$, and <probability density function> $1/(\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)})$ for $0<x<1$. This proves the limiting law rather than inferring it merely from the limiting <mean>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use <Haar sampling by Gaussian QR decomposition>:

* Generate an $n$-by-$n$ real <matrix> $G$ with <independent> entries having the <standard normal distribution>.
* Compute a <QR decomposition> $G=Q_0R_0$ with $Q_0$ an <orthogonal matrix> and $R_0$ an <upper triangular matrix>.
* Put $S=\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{sgn}(R_{0,11}),\ldots,\operatorname{sgn}(R_{0,nn}))$ and return $\boxed{M=Q_0S}$. Then $G=MR$ with $R=SR_0$ having strictly positive diagonal.

The <matrix> $G$ is invertible <almost surely>. Indeed, successively sampled <Gaussian> columns have <probability> zero of lying in the proper <linear subspace> spanned by the previous columns. Thus the diagonal signs used above exist with <probability> one. Equivalently, apply the <Gram-Schmidt process> to the columns, always dividing each residual by its positive <Euclidean norm>; this produces $M$ directly.

To prove uniformity, fix $H\in O(n)$. Each column of $G$ has density proportional to $e^{-\lVert x\rVert^2/2}$, so <orthogonal transformations> preserve its <probability distribution>. Column <independence> therefore gives $HG\overset{\mathrm d}=G$.

The <QR decomposition> with positive diagonal is unique: if $G=Q_1R_1=Q_2R_2$, then $Q_2^TQ_1=R_2R_1^{-1}$ is both an <orthogonal matrix> and an <upper triangular matrix>, hence diagonal with entries $\pm1$. Its diagonal entries are positive, so it is the identity. It follows that the orthogonal factor of $HG=HMR$ is $HM$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{HM\overset{\mathrm d}=M\quad\text{for every }H\in O(n).}
$$
By uniqueness of normalized <Haar measure> on the <compact group> $O(n)$, the returned <matrix> has exactly the required <probability distribution>. Reflection invariance gives each <determinant> sign <probability> $1/2$, so the algorithm samples both components of the <orthogonal group>. The positive-diagonal correction is essential when a numerical <QR decomposition> chooses its own signs.
