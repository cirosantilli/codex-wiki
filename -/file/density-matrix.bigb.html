= Density operator
{synonym}

A finite-dimensional <density operator> $\rho$ represents a <quantum state> on a <Hilbert space>. It is <Hermitian>, <positive semidefinite>, and has <trace> one:
$$
\rho=\rho^\dagger,\qquad \langle v|\rho|v\rangle\geq0\text{ for every }|v\rangle,\qquad \operatorname{Tr}\rho=1.
$$
The <density matrix> is its <matrix> in a chosen <orthonormal basis>. In <dimension> $N$ it has $N^2-1$ real parameters: a <Hermitian matrix> has $N^2$, and fixing its <trace> removes one; positivity restricts the allowed values.

A <quantum state ensemble> of normalized <pure states> $|\psi_i\rangle$ with positive <probabilities> $p_i$ gives
$$
\rho=\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|,\qquad p_i>0,\qquad \sum_i p_i=1.
$$
Zero-weight terms can be omitted. Conversely, the <spectral theorem> gives such an ensemble for every <density operator>, using its <orthonormal> <eigenvectors> and nonnegative <eigenvalues>, with zero <eigenvalues> omitted. A <pure state> has $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$; a <mixed state> needs at least two nonzero <eigenvalues>. https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/TQI.2.pdf[Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, chapter 2, section 2.1.2, pp. 62–64]{ref}

For a <projective measurement> with <orthogonal projections> $\Pi_a$, the <Born rule> is
$$
P(a)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\Pi_a)=\sum_i p_i\langle\psi_i|\Pi_a|\psi_i\rangle.
$$
This is the average of the component <pure-state> <probabilities>. Positivity makes each outcome <probability> nonnegative, and $\sum_a\Pi_a=I$ makes them sum to one. The same <density operator> therefore predicts every measurement on the system without requiring a unique preparation history. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, chapter 2, section 2.3.1, equations (2.51)–(2.59)]{ref}

For a closed system, <Schrödinger picture> evolution under a <unitary operator> is
$$
\rho_S(t)=U(t)\rho_HU(t)^\dagger
$$
when $\rho_H=\rho_S(0)$ denotes the time-independent <Heisenberg picture> state and $U(0)=I$. In the <Schrödinger picture> the <density operator> evolves; in the <Heisenberg picture> the <observables> evolve instead, with identical measurement predictions. This <unitary operator> preserves the <eigenvalues> and hence the <purity of a density operator>. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, chapter 2, section 2.3.4, equation (2.79)]{ref}

A historical source for the probabilistic formulation of <quantum mechanics> is von Neumann's 1927 paper Wahrscheinlichkeitstheoretischer Aufbau der Quantenmechanik. https://geodesic.mathdoc.fr/item/NGWGMP_1927__1927_59230/[von Neumann, Nachrichten von der Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften zu Göttingen, Mathematisch-Physikalische Klasse, 1927, pp. 245–272]{ref}

= Improper mixture
{parent=Density matrix}

The reduced <density matrix> obtained by taking the <partial trace> of a larger <entangled state>. It predicts the local measurement statistics, but its mixed form does not imply that the total state has collapsed into one of the displayed components. Different ensembles can represent the same <reduced density matrix>.

= Leading high-temperature density kernel
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\rho(x,x')\simeq Z^{-1}\sqrt{m/(2\pi\beta\hbar^2)}e^{-m(x-x')^2/(2\beta\hbar^2)-\beta V(x)}$}

Neglecting <commutators> between kinetic and <potential energy> permits a leading product approximation to the imaginary-time evolution operator. The kinetic factor is a Gaussian Fourier <integral>; the potential factor multiplies it in position space. The unsymmetrized product places the potential at one endpoint and need not be exactly <Hermitian> at the discarded order. Splitting the potential equally between both endpoints gives a <Hermitian> approximation with exponent $-\beta[V(x)+V(x')]/2$ and the same leading diagonal normalization.

= One-body density matrix
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$G^{(1)}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\langle a^\dagger(\mathbf r)a(\mathbf r')\rangle$}

The <one-body density matrix> contains first-order spatial coherence and mode occupations of a many-particle state. Its <trace> is the particle number in the convention displayed. A macroscopic <eigenvalue> identifies <Bose-Einstein condensation>; in a homogeneous condensate the long-distance <matrix> approaches a nonzero constant. A finite density or local coherence alone does not imply this long-distance limit.

= Thermal representation of a reduced coupled-oscillator ground state
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\nu=\sqrt{\omega_+\omega_-}$}

Tracing one oscillator out of a symmetric coordinate-coupled Gaussian <ground state> gives a thermal density operator for an effective oscillator of <frequency> $\nu$. Its geometric ratio is $q=[(\sqrt{\omega_+}-\sqrt{\omega_-})/(\sqrt{\omega_+}+\sqrt{\omega_-})]^2$. It is generally squeezed relative to the original bare-frequency number basis. The covariance ratio fixes the effective <frequency> rather than permitting an arbitrary thermal-basis identification.

= Quantum state population
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$p_k=\langle k|\rho|k\rangle$}

= Level population
{synonym}

For a chosen normalized level state $|k\rangle$, its population is the <probability> $\langle k|\rho|k\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(|k\rangle\langle k|\rho)$ of finding the system in that state. In an orthonormal level basis these diagonal entries are nonnegative and sum to one. Off-diagonal <quantum coherence> contains information absent from the population vector, so matching all level populations need not prepare a specified coherent superposition.

= Quantum coherence in a specified basis
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\rho_{jk}\ne0$, $j\ne k$}

= Quantum coherence
{synonym}

For a chosen <orthonormal basis>, the off-diagonal entries of a <density operator> encode quantum coherence between distinct basis states. A diagonal density operator is an incoherent mixture in that basis. This property is basis-dependent: diagonalizing the operator does not imply that it lacked coherence in the measurement or energy basis of interest. Relative phases affect these entries, while a global wavefunction phase cancels from the density operator. Dephasing damps the entries and can spoil <stimulated Raman adiabatic passage> by removing the ground-state interference that maintains the dark state.

= Electron phase coherence
{parent=Quantum coherence in a specified basis}

Preservation of relative electronic phase along alternative paths so they can interfere. Loss of coherence suppresses interference structure, but semiclassical magnetic focusing can persist if momentum trajectories remain sufficiently ballistic.

= Two-qubit Werner state
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\rho_p=pP_s+(1-p)(I-P_s)/3$}

This collective-unitary-invariant two-qubit state has singlet weight $p$ and equal weights on the three triplet <Bell states>. With $P_s$ the <spin singlet state> projector, its <partial transpose> has eigenvalues $(1-2p)/2$ once and $(1+2p)/6$ three times. It is entangled exactly when $p>1/2$. At $p=1/2$, it is the equally weighted mixture of six antiparallel product states along the three Pauli axes. In the interval $1/4\leq p\leq1/2$, convex mixing of this boundary state with $I/4$ explicitly proves separability. The weight $p$ here is the singlet probability, not the alternative coefficient multiplying a pure singlet in a white-noise mixture.

= Single-copy Werner filtering normalization bound
{parent=Two-qubit Werner state}
{title2=$p=\operatorname{Tr}[W_F(A^\dagger A\otimes B^\dagger B)]\geq|\det A\det B|$}

Write $W_F=(I\otimes I-w\sum_j\sigma_j\otimes\sigma_j)/4$, with $w=(4F-1)/3\in(1/3,1]$. For positive matrices $X=A^\dagger A=a_0I+a\cdot\sigma$ and $Y=B^\dagger B=b_0I+b\cdot\sigma$, the success probability is $p=a_0b_0-w a\cdot b$. It is at least $a_0b_0-|a||b|\geq\sqrt{(a_0^2-|a|^2)(b_0^2-|b|^2)}=|\det A\det B|$. Squaring the middle comparison leaves $(a_0|b|-b_0|a|)^2\geq0$.

= Single-copy Werner filtering cannot increase concurrence
{parent=Single-copy Werner filtering normalization bound}
{title2=$C(\rho_{\rm retained})\leq C(W_F)$}

Combine the <single-copy Werner filtering normalization bound> with <concurrence scaling under invertible local filters>. Every complete classical record of <local operations and classical communication> has a product filter $A\otimes B$; singular branches are separable. Thus every retained branch has <concurrence> at most that of the input. Coarse-graining successful records preserves the bound by convexity. Since <concurrence of a two-qubit Werner state> increases strictly with $F$, no successful single-copy protocol can output a Werner state with larger $F$. Several input copies permit collective distillation and are outside this statement.

= Concurrence of a two-qubit Werner state
{parent=Two-qubit Werner state}
{title2=$C(W_F)=2F-1,\quad 1/2<F\leq1$}

For any normalized pure two-<qubit> vector, its squared overlap with a <maximally entangled state> is at most $(1+C)/2$: in a <Schmidt decomposition> the overlap is bounded by $(\sqrt\lambda+\sqrt{1-\lambda})/\sqrt2$. Averaging gives $C(W_F)\geq2F-1$. Equality follows from the six-state ensemble $\sqrt F\,|\Psi_-\rangle\pm\sqrt{1-F}\,|t_j\rangle$, with equal weights, where $t_1=\Psi_+$, $t_2=\Phi_-$ and $t_3=i\Phi_+$. The coherences cancel and each coefficient determinant gives pure concurrence $2F-1$.

= Bell twirling followed by triplet symmetrization
{c}
{parent=Two-qubit Werner state}

Average the four correlated local Pauli operations to remove coherences in the <Bell states> basis. A one-party Pauli operation can permute the largest of the four resulting weights into the singlet sector; that weight is at least $1/4$. Correlated common local unitaries then permute the three triplet projectors, while <collective-unitary covariance of the two-qubit singlet> keeps the singlet projector unchanged. Averaging all six permutations makes the triplet weights equal, producing a <two-qubit Werner state>. Shared randomness or <local operations and classical communication> coordinates these local operations. Common unitaries alone cannot increase an initially small singlet weight, because that weight is invariant under them.

= Extreme points of the density-operator state space
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\rho\text{ extreme}\iff\operatorname{rank}\rho=1$}

The <density operators> on a finite-dimensional <Hilbert space> form a convex set. Its <extreme points> are exactly the rank-one projectors, hence exactly the <pure states>. To prove this, suppose $|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\sum_i a_i\rho_i$ with $a_i>0$ and $\rho_i$ <positive semidefinite>. For $w\perp\psi$, $0=\sum_i a_i\langle w|\rho_i|w\rangle$ is a sum of nonnegative numbers, so every term vanishes. Diagonalizing $\rho_i$ shows that $\langle w|\rho_i|w\rangle=0$ implies $\rho_iw=0$. Thus each $\rho_i$ is supported on the line spanned by $\psi$, and its unit <trace> forces $\rho_i=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$.

Conversely, a <density operator> of <rank> at least two has a <spectral decomposition> with at least two positive <eigenvalues>. If $\lambda$ is one of them, $0<\lambda<1$ and $\rho=\lambda|e\rangle\langle e|+(1-\lambda)\sigma$, where $\sigma$ is the normalized remainder. These are distinct <density operators>, proving that $\rho$ is not an <extreme point>.

= Unitary orbit of a density operator
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\mathcal O(\rho)=\{U\rho U^\dagger:U\in U(N)\}$}

The unitary orbit consists exactly of <density operators> with the same spectrum. If distinct eigenvalues have multiplicities $n_j$, its stabilizer is $\prod_jU(n_j)$ and its real dimension is $N^2-\sum_jn_j^2$. A reachable compact group with <Lie algebra> $\mathfrak g$ is transitive on this orbit exactly when $\dim\mathfrak g-\dim(\mathfrak g\cap\{X:[X,\rho]=0\})$ equals the orbit dimension. Global phases act trivially by conjugation.

= Quantum state ensemble
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\{p_i,|\psi_i\rangle\},\quad\rho=\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$}

= Quantum ensemble
{synonym}

A quantum state ensemble specifies a random preparation: normalized <pure states> $|\psi_i\rangle$ are selected with <probabilities> $p_i\geq0$, $\sum_i p_i=1$. Without an accessible preparation label, its measurement statistics are those of the <density operator> $\rho=\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$. Indeed every <POVM> effect $E$ has probability $\sum_i p_i\langle\psi_i|E|\psi_i\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(E\rho)$. Different ensembles can therefore have identical operational statistics: equal mixtures of computational-basis qubits or Hadamard-basis qubits both have density operator $I/2$. The ensemble decomposition contains information about the preparation, not additional information accessible from the system alone.

= Convex roof extension
{parent=Quantum state ensemble}
{title2=$F(\rho)=\inf_{\rho=\sum_jp_j\pi_j}\sum_jp_j\mu(\pi_j)$}

= Convex roof
{synonym}

A convex roof extension extends a function $\mu$ on <pure states> to arbitrary <density operators> by minimizing its ensemble average over all <pure-state> decompositions. It agrees with $\mu$ on <pure states>: every positive-weight state in a decomposition of a rank-one <density operator> has the same one-dimensional support. Concatenating nearly minimizing ensembles proves that $F$ is a <convex function>. Moreover, every <convex function> that is no larger than $\mu$ on <pure states> is no larger than $F$, by applying convexity to each decomposition. Infima need not be attained; an arbitrarily small additive error suffices when $F$ is finite.

= Monotonicity of a convex roof under a quantum instrument
{parent=Convex roof extension}
{title2=$F(\rho)\geq\sum_kP_kF(\rho_k)$}

If a <convex roof extension> is non-increasing on average for every pure input to a <quantum instrument>, then it is non-increasing on average for mixed inputs too. For an input ensemble $\{p_j,\pi_j\}$, let $q_{k|j}$ and $\sigma_{jk}$ be its conditional outcome probabilities and states. Linearity of each unnormalized outcome map gives $P_k\rho_k=\sum_jp_jq_{k|j}\sigma_{jk}$. Convexity gives $\sum_kP_kF(\rho_k)\leq\sum_{j,k}p_jq_{k|j}F(\sigma_{jk})\leq\sum_jp_j\mu(\pi_j)$. Taking the <infimum> over input ensembles proves the result, even when conditional outputs of pure inputs are mixed, provided the averages are well defined.

= Pauli correlation tensor
{parent=Density matrix}
{c}
{title2=$T_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\,\sigma_i\otimes\sigma_j)$}

For a two-qubit <density operator>, the real three-by-three <matrix> $T$ encodes the spin-product <expectation values>: unit measurement axes $\mathbf a,\mathbf b$ have $E(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\mathbf a^TT\mathbf b$. The tensor does not include local Bloch-vector data and alone need not specify the complete <density operator>. The <CHSH optimum from the correlation tensor> depends on the two largest <eigenvalues> of $T^TT$.

= Maximally mixed state
{parent=Density matrix}
{title2=$\rho_*=I_N/N$}

The maximally mixed <density operator> on an $N$-dimensional <Hilbert space> assigns equal weights to every vector of any <orthonormal basis>. It is invariant under every <unitary operator>. Its purity is $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_*^2)=1/N$, the minimum possible because the nonnegative <eigenvalues> sum to one and obey the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. For $N>1$ it is a <mixed state>; for $N=1$ it is the sole pure state. A rank-$k$ projector has probability $k/N$ in this state.

= Obstruction to uniform probability lowering by a unitary
{parent=Maximally mixed state}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}(PUI_NU^\dagger/N)=k/N$}

Let $P$ be a rank-$k$ projector with $0<k<N$. No <unitary operator> can map every pure state whose $P$ probability equals $k/N$ to a state with the same fixed smaller probability. Average $\sqrt{k/N}|g\rangle\pm\sqrt{1-k/N}|b\rangle$ over good and bad basis vectors and both signs. The average <density operator> is the <maximally mixed state>, so every unitary preserves its average $P$ probability. This contradicts a strictly smaller probability for all states in the ensemble. A preparation tailored to one known starting state is a different resource and is used in <exact amplitude amplification>.

= Mixed state
{parent=Density matrix}

= Mixed quantum state
{synonym}

A mixed state is a <density operator> that is not a rank-one <orthogonal projection>. In finite <dimension> it is characterized by $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)<1$, so its <purity of a density operator> distinguishes it from a <pure state>.

Compare a <pure-state> <quantum superposition> with an equal classical choice between two <computational-basis states> of a <qubit>:
$$
|+\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\rho_+=|+\rangle\langle+|=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}=\frac12|0\rangle\langle0|+\frac12|1\rangle\langle1|=\frac I2.
$$
A $Z$ <projective measurement> in the <computational basis> gives $P(0)=P(1)=1/2$ for both <density operators>. An $X$ <projective measurement> in the <Hadamard basis> $|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ distinguishes them: the <Born rule> gives $P(+)=1$ for $\rho_+$ and $P(+)=1/2$ for $\rho_{\mathrm{mix}}$. These comparisons use separate copies of each preparation; measuring $Z$ first changes the state tested by a subsequent $X$ measurement.

\Image[density-matrix-coherence-versus-mixture.png]
{title=Equal computational-basis populations can hide different coherence and X-measurement statistics}

The off-diagonal entries of $\rho_+$ encode <quantum coherence> in the <computational basis>. Such <quantum coherence> depends on the <basis>: $\rho_+$ is diagonal in the <Hadamard basis> but remains a <pure state>. Mixedness is independent of the <basis>, because a <unitary> change of <basis> preserves the <eigenvalues> and the <purity of a density operator>.

Even the <quantum state ensemble> is not unique:
$$
\frac I2=\frac12|0\rangle\langle0|+\frac12|1\rangle\langle1|
=\frac12|+\rangle\langle+|+\frac12|-\rangle\langle-|.
$$
The off-diagonal entries of the two <Hadamard-basis> projectors cancel. Thus the <density operator> retains precisely what measurements on the system can reveal, while an accessible preparation label carries additional information. A <mixed state> can also be the <reduced density matrix> of an <entangled state>, even when the whole system is in a <pure state>; classical ignorance about a selected component is not its only physical origin. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, chapter 2, section 2.3.1, equations (2.54)–(2.60)]{ref}

= Purification
{synonym}

A purification of a density operator $\rho_A$ is a pure state $|\Psi\rangle_{AE}$ on a larger system such that $\operatorname{Tr}_E|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|=\rho_A$. A <spectral decomposition> $\rho_A=\sum_jp_j|j\rangle\langle j|$ gives the canonical construction $|\Psi\rangle=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}|j\rangle_A|j\rangle_E$.

= Purity decouples a subsystem from its purification
{parent=Purification of a density operator}

If subsystem $S$ has <pure state> $|u\rangle\langle u|$ in a normalized joint pure vector $|\Omega\rangle_{SE}$, then $|\Omega\rangle_{SE}=|u\rangle_S\otimes|e\rangle_E$. Indeed, for an <orthonormal basis> $\{v_j\}$ of $u^\perp$, each squared norm $\|(\langle v_j|\otimes I)|\Omega\rangle\|^2=\langle v_j|\rho_S|v_j\rangle$ is zero. Therefore the joint vector has no component outside $\mathbb Cu\otimes\mathcal H_E$. In particular, if Alice and Bob share a known pure <entangled state>, an adversary holding a purifying system has no correlations with it. This does not make every pure bipartite state useful for key distribution: a pure <product state> has no shared random correlations.

= Flagged purification of a quantum ensemble
{parent=Purification of a density operator}
{title2=$|U\rangle=\sum_x\sqrt{p_x}|u_x\rangle|x\rangle$}

= Flagged purifications of a quantum ensemble
{synonym}

For $\rho=\sum_xp_x\rho_x$, choose component purifications $|u_x\rangle_{AR}$ and an orthonormal flag register. The vector $|U\rangle=\sum_x\sqrt{p_x}|u_x\rangle|x\rangle$ is normalized and reduces to $\rho$ after tracing out $R$ and the flag. Orthogonal labels remove the cross terms. This construction turns mixture identities into pure-state overlap arguments and proves <joint concavity of quantum fidelity>.

= Unitary freedom of purification
{parent=Purification of a density operator}

Any two purifications of the same density operator on sufficiently large reference spaces differ by an isometry on the reference system, and by a unitary when the reference spaces have the same dimension.

The Hughston--Jozsa--Wootters theorem says that two pure-state ensembles represent the same density operator exactly when their subnormalized state vectors are related by an isometry, or by a unitary after padding the shorter ensemble with zero vectors.

= Isometry parametrization of a density-matrix ensemble
{parent=Hughston–Jozsa–Wootters theorem}
{title2=$\sqrt{p_j}|\phi_j\rangle=\sum_{a=1}^r U_{ja}\sqrt{\lambda_a}|e_a\rangle,\quad U^\dagger U=I_r$}

For the nonzero spectral decomposition $\rho=\sum_{a=1}^r\lambda_a|e_a\rangle\langle e_a|$, every pure-state <quantum state ensemble> of $\rho$ is specified by the displayed column <linear isometry>. To prove this, write $w_j=\sqrt{p_j}\phi_j$. Positivity forces each $w_j$ into the support of $\rho$, and the coefficients $U_{ja}=\langle e_a|w_j\rangle/\sqrt{\lambda_a}$ have orthonormal columns because $\sum_j|w_j\rangle\langle w_j|=\rho$. Conversely this column identity reconstructs $\rho$; the squared vector norms are the <probabilities> and sum to one. Complete the columns to unitary bases after adding zero ensemble vectors to obtain the usual unitary-mixing formulation.

= Trace distance
{title2=$D(\rho,\sigma)$}
{parent=Density matrix}
{wiki}

How different can two preparations look to someone who can perform any <quantum measurement>? The <trace distance> answers this question for <density operators> $\rho,\sigma$ on the same <finite-dimensional vector space>:
$$
D(\rho,\sigma)=\frac12\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_1
=\frac12\operatorname{Tr}|\rho-\sigma|,\qquad
|A|=\sqrt{A^\dagger A}.
$$
Here $|A|$ is the <absolute value of an operator>, and $\lVert A\rVert_1$ is its <trace norm>. Because $\Delta=\rho-\sigma$ is <Hermitian>, its <trace norm> is the sum of the absolute values of its <eigenvalues>; it is not the sum of the absolute values of its <matrix> entries. The factor $1/2$ gives
$$
0\leq D(\rho,\sigma)\leq1.
$$
The <trace distance> is a <metric>: it is symmetric, vanishes exactly when $\rho=\sigma$, and obeys the <triangle inequality>. Identical preparations have <trace distance> zero; preparations with orthogonal occupied <eigenspaces> have <trace distance> one and can be identified perfectly in a single <quantum measurement>.

Operationally, the <trace distance> is the largest <total variation distance> between the outcome <probability distributions> obtainable by a <POVM>. Equivalently, the <variational characterization of trace distance> says it is the largest possible <probability> gap for one event in a binary <quantum measurement>. For one copy of either known preparation, each chosen with prior <probability> $1/2$, the <Holevo–Helstrom theorem> gives
$$
P_{\mathrm{success}}^{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1+D(\rho,\sigma)}2.
$$
Thus <trace distance> zero leaves only guessing, while <trace distance> one permits perfect <quantum state discrimination>. The optimum ranges over measurements; different measurement <bases> can expose different amounts of distinguishability. https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/TQI.3.pdf[Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, §3.1, Theorem 3.4 and Proposition 3.5]{ref}

For a worked example with commuting <density operators>, take a <qubit> with
$$
\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0\\0&0.2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0\\0&0.7\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Their difference has <eigenvalues> $0.5,-0.5$, so $D=(|0.5|+|-0.5|)/2=0.5$. Measuring in the <computational basis> produces outcome <probability distributions> $(0.8,0.2)$ and $(0.3,0.7)$, whose <total variation distance> is also $0.5$. Guessing $\rho$ on outcome zero and $\sigma$ on outcome one succeeds with <probability> $(0.8+0.7)/2=0.75$. A <projective measurement> in the <Hadamard basis> instead produces $(0.5,0.5)$ for both preparations and gives no discrimination advantage.

The <trace-distance contraction under quantum channels> ensures that a common <quantum channel>, including the <partial trace>, cannot increase the <trace distance>. This makes <trace distance> a useful error measure: if $D\leq\epsilon$, every event in every <quantum measurement> has a <probability> gap at most $\epsilon$. Losing access to a subsystem can hide distinguishability, but cannot create more of it.

= Trace distance between qubit states
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$D(\rho,\sigma)=|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|/2$}

= Qubit trace distance
{synonym}

For <qubit> <density operators>, the <trace distance> has a particularly simple geometry. Write their <Bloch vectors> as $\mathbf r,\mathbf s$ in the <Bloch ball>:
$$
\rho=\frac{I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},\qquad
\sigma=\frac{I+\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},\qquad
\Delta=\rho-\sigma=\frac{(\mathbf r-\mathbf s)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},
$$
where $I$ is the <identity operator> and $\boldsymbol\sigma=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)$ denotes the <Pauli matrices>. This <Bloch vector> representation includes both <pure states> on the <Bloch sphere> and <mixed states> in the interior of the <Bloch ball>. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, §2.3.2, equations (2.62)–(2.69)]{ref}

The <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives $[(\mathbf r-\mathbf s)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma]^2=|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|^2I$. Hence the two <eigenvalues> of $\Delta$ are $\pm|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|/2$, and their absolute sum gives
$$
D(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{|\mathbf r-\mathbf s|}{2}.
$$
The <trace distance> is half the straight-line <Euclidean distance> between the <Bloch vectors>, not the angular or surface distance along the <Bloch sphere>. Opposite <Bloch vectors> have <trace distance> one; the centre $I/2$ is at <trace distance> $1/2$ from every <pure state>.

For the <pure states> $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the <Bloch vectors> are $\mathbf r=(0,0,1)$ and $\mathbf s=(1,0,0)$. Their straight-line separation is $\sqrt2$, so
$$
D(|0\rangle\langle0|,|+\rangle\langle+|)=\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$
These <Bloch vectors> are perpendicular, but the <pure states> are not orthogonal: $|\langle0|+\rangle|=1/\sqrt2$. The <Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states> measures along the difference direction $\mathbf n=(\mathbf r-\mathbf s)/\sqrt2=(-1,0,1)/\sqrt2$, rather than along either input <Bloch vector>.

= Trace-distance contraction under quantum channels
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$D(\mathcal E(\rho),\mathcal E(\sigma))\leq D(\rho,\sigma)$}

By the <variational characterization of trace distance>, maximize $\operatorname{Tr}[P\mathcal E(\rho-\sigma)]$ over $0\leq P\leq I$. The adjoint of a <quantum channel> is positive and unital, so $0\leq\mathcal E^*(P)\leq I$. Moving the channel onto $P$ therefore restricts, rather than enlarges, the effects available in the input variational problem. This proves the displayed bound for every pair of <density operators>.

= Trace distance for qubit dephasing
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$d(\rho,\mathcal D_c(\rho))=|\alpha\beta|\,|1-c|$}

For the pure <qubit> $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$, subtracting the output of <dephasing channel> leaves a Hermitian matrix with zero diagonal and off-diagonal modulus $|\alpha\beta|\,|1-c|$. Its two <eigenvalues> are that number and its negative, giving the displayed <trace distance>. For real $c=1-2p$, this is $2p|\alpha\beta|$.

= Pure-target upper bound on trace distance
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$D(\rho,|v\rangle\langle v|)\leq\sqrt{1-\langle v|\rho|v\rangle}$}

For two normalized <pure states>, the difference of their projectors has two <eigenvalues> $\pm\sqrt{1-|\langle u|v\rangle|^2}$, giving that <trace distance>. Write a mixed <density operator> as $\rho=\sum_jq_j|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|$. Convexity of the <trace norm> and weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $D(\rho,|v\rangle\langle v|)\leq\sum_jq_j\sqrt{1-|\langle u_j|v\rangle|^2}\leq\sqrt{1-\langle v|\rho|v\rangle}$. Thus a small <probability> of failing the pure-state projector test forces trace-distance proximity, including for correlated multipartite outputs.

= Spectral-parts formula for trace distance
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$D(\rho,\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho-\sigma)_+$}

For the Hermitian difference $X=\rho-\sigma$, define its <positive part of a Hermitian operator> $Q=X_+$ and <negative part of a Hermitian operator> $R=X_-$. Their supports are orthogonal and $|X|=Q+R$. Equal traces give $\operatorname{Tr}Q=\operatorname{Tr}R$, so the <trace distance> is both $\tfrac12(\operatorname{Tr}Q+\operatorname{Tr}R)$ and $\operatorname{Tr}Q$. This explains why the positive spectral projector attains the <variational characterization of trace distance>.

= Pure-target lower bound on trace distance
{parent=Trace distance}
{title2=$D(\rho,P)\geq1-F(\rho,P)^2$}

For a <density operator> $\rho$ and <pure state> $P=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, the <diagonal absolute-sum bound for the trace norm> in a basis containing $|\psi\rangle$ gives $D(\rho,P)\geq1-\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle=1-F(\rho,P)^2$. The <quantum fidelity> is unsquared. Block-diagonal states with respect to the target and its complement attain equality.

= Variational characterization of trace distance
{parent=Trace distance}

The <variational characterization of trace distance> turns a <matrix> distance into the best possible binary experiment. For <density operators> $\rho,\sigma$ on the same <finite-dimensional vector space>,
$$
D(\rho,\sigma)=\max_{0\leq P\leq I}\operatorname{Tr}[P(\rho-\sigma)]
=\max_{0\leq P\leq I}|\operatorname{Tr}[P(\rho-\sigma)]|.
$$
An operator $P$ with $0\leq P\leq I$ is an effect of a <POVM>: both $P$ and $I-P$ are <positive operators>. The <Born rule> assigns the first outcome <probabilities> $\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(P\sigma)$. Their signed gap is $\operatorname{Tr}(P\Delta)$, where $\Delta=\rho-\sigma$. Since $\operatorname{Tr}\Delta=0$, replacing $P$ by $I-P$ reverses this gap, explaining the equality with the absolute-value maximum.

One optimal $P$ is the <spectral projector> $P_+$ onto the positive <eigenspaces> of $\Delta$. In the <spectral decomposition> of $\Delta$, this choice includes all positive <eigenvalues> and excludes all negative <eigenvalues>. Their positive sum equals the <trace distance> by the <spectral-parts formula for trace distance>. Any zero <eigenspace> can be assigned to either outcome without changing the gap. This is the effect selected by the equal-prior <Holevo–Helstrom theorem>. https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/TQI.3.pdf[Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, Theorem 3.4, equations (3.16)–(3.19)]{ref}

The same optimum has a classical description. For any finite-outcome <POVM> $\{E_i\}$, let $p_i=\operatorname{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ and $q_i=\operatorname{Tr}(E_i\sigma)$. Then
$$
D(\rho,\sigma)=\max_{\{E_i\}}\frac12\sum_i|p_i-q_i|
=\max_{\{E_i\}}\max_A\left|\sum_{i\in A}(p_i-q_i)\right|.
$$
The expression $\frac12\sum_i|p_i-q_i|$ is the <total variation distance> of the entire outcome <probability distributions>; the expression for $A$ is the gap for one event. Grouping outcomes in $A$ gives the single effect $P_A=\sum_{i\in A}E_i$. Conversely, an effect $P$ gives the binary <POVM> $\{P,I-P\}$, whose two <probability> gaps are opposites, so their <total variation distance> is $|\operatorname{Tr}(P\Delta)|$. Thus the optimum over all <POVMs> is already attained by one binary <projective measurement>. https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~watrous/TQI/TQI.3.pdf[Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, §3.1.1, Proposition 3.5 and the discussion following Theorem 3.4]{ref}

For <quantum state discrimination> with equal prior <probabilities>, declare $\rho$ on outcome $P$ and $\sigma$ on outcome $I-P$. Directly averaging the two conditional success <probabilities> gives
$$
P_{\mathrm{success}}(P)=\frac12\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)
+\frac12\operatorname{Tr}((I-P)\sigma)
=\frac12\left(1+\operatorname{Tr}(P\Delta)\right).
$$
Optimizing this expression gives $(1+D)/2$. For the diagonal example in <trace distance>, $P=|0\rangle\langle0|$ gives a gap $0.8-0.3=0.5$. For zero versus plus, that same effect gives only $1-1/2=1/2$, while the effect in <Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states> gives the larger gap $1/\sqrt2$. The <trace-distance-preserving binary measurement> depends on the pair of preparations; a fixed measurement <basis> does not generally attain the <trace distance> for every pair.

= Trace-distance-preserving binary measurement
{parent=Variational characterization of trace distance}

For two <density operators> $\rho,\sigma$, let $P_+$ project onto the positive spectral subspace of $\rho-\sigma$. The binary <POVM> $\{P_+,I-P_+\}$ has output probability difference $(D,-D)$, where $D=D(\rho,\sigma)$. Its <measurement channel> therefore preserves this pair's <trace distance> exactly. The optimizing <quantum measurement> depends on the pair; a single fixed measurement need not preserve every pair's distance.

= Purity of a density operator
{parent=Density matrix}

= Purity
{synonym}

The purity of a <density operator> is $\gamma=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)$. It equals one exactly for a <pure state>; in finite <dimension> $d$ it lies between $1/d$ and $1$. The minimum occurs exactly at the <maximally mixed state> $I/d$.

A worked <qubit> family interpolates between the <mixed state> $I/2$ and the <pure state> $\rho_+=|+\rangle\langle+|$, where $|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$:
$$
\rho_p=p\rho_++(1-p)\frac I2
=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&p\\p&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0\leq p\leq1.
$$
The <eigenvectors> are $|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with <eigenvalues> $\lambda_\pm=(1\pm p)/2$. Squaring and summing these <eigenvalues> gives
$$
\gamma(p)=\frac{(1+p)^2+(1-p)^2}{4}=\frac{1+p^2}{2},\qquad
P_X(+)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_p|+\rangle\langle+|)=\frac{1+p}{2}.
$$
Here $P_X(+)$ is the <Born rule> <probability> of the positive outcome in an $X$ <projective measurement>. The <Bloch vector> is $(p,0,0)$, since $\rho_p=(I+p\sigma_x)/2$ with $\sigma_x$ one of the <Pauli matrices>. At $p=0$ the purity is $1/2$; at $p=1/2$ it is $5/8$, with $P_X(+)=3/4$; at $p=1$ it is one. Within this family, $p=1$ is a <pure state> and every $p<1$ is a <mixed state>. Extending the formula to $p>1$ produces a negative <eigenvalue> $(1-p)/2$, so it ceases to be a <density operator> despite remaining <Hermitian> with <trace> one.

\Image[density-matrix-purity-and-polarization.png]
{title=Purity and X-measurement probability as a qubit moves from the Bloch-ball centre to a pure state}

The <Bloch vector> length measures polarization: for any <qubit> <density operator>, $\gamma=(1+|\mathbf r|^2)/2$. This connects the interior of the <Bloch ball> with <mixed states> and its boundary, the <Bloch sphere>, with <pure states>. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, chapter 2, section 2.3.2, equation (2.62)]{ref}

For a short proof of the general bound, use the <spectral theorem>: the nonnegative <eigenvalues> $\lambda_j$ sum to one, so $\gamma=\sum_j\lambda_j^2$. Then
$$
\gamma-\frac1d=\sum_{j=1}^d\left(\lambda_j-\frac1d\right)^2\geq0,\qquad
1-\gamma=2\sum_{j<k}\lambda_j\lambda_k\geq0.
$$
The lower bound is attained only when all <eigenvalues> equal $1/d$; the upper bound is attained only when exactly one <eigenvalue> is nonzero, and hence equals one. Purity is therefore a simple quantitative test of mixedness, invariant under a <unitary operator>. It does not specify the entire <density operator>: even distinct <pure states> all have purity one. https://www.preskill.caltech.edu/ph229/notes/chap2.pdf[Preskill, Physics 229 notes, chapter 2, section 2.3.1, equation (2.60) and the following purity criterion]{ref}

= Reduced density operator of a weakly coupled oscillator pair
{parent=Density matrix}

If a weak interaction changes $|0,0\rangle$ to a normalized state proportional to $|0,0\rangle-g|1,1\rangle$, tracing out either oscillator gives eigenvalues $1/(1+g^2)$ and $g^2/(1+g^2)$.

= Bloch vector
{c}
{parent=Density matrix}

Every qubit state has the unique form
$$
\rho=\frac12(I+r_x\sigma_x+r_y\sigma_y+r_z\sigma_z),
\qquad |r|\leq1.
$$
It is pure exactly when $|r|=1$, and
$r_i=2\langle S_i\rangle/\hbar$.

Unit-length Bloch vectors form the <Bloch sphere>; mixed-state vectors occupy the ball’s interior.

= Qubit polarization
{parent=Bloch vector}
{title2=$\mathbf P=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\boldsymbol\sigma)$}

The ensemble <Bloch vector> of a two-level <quantum state>. With $\rho=(I+\mathbf P\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$, full polarization has $|\mathbf P|=1$ and is pure, while zero polarization has $\rho=I/2$ and is maximally mixed. This internal-state ensemble polarization differs from the <polarization> of a light wave used to drive it.

= Bloch ball
{c}
{parent=Bloch vector}
{title2=$\{\mathbf s\in\mathbb R^3:|\mathbf s|\leq1\}$}

The qubit <density operators> $\rho=(I+\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$ correspond exactly to the closed unit ball: their <eigenvalues> are $(1\pm|\mathbf s|)/2$, so positivity is equivalent to $|\mathbf s|\leq1$. The boundary is the <Bloch sphere> of pure states, and the centre is $I/2$. A <depolarizing channel> scales this ball isotropically, possibly with a sign reversal in the larger completely positive parameter interval.

= Antipodal eigenprojectors of a qubit density matrix
{parent=Bloch ball}
{title2=$P_\pm=(I\pm\widehat n\cdot\sigma)/2$}

For a qubit <density matrix> $\rho=(I+n\cdot\sigma)/2$ with nonzero <Bloch vector>, $\widehat n=n/\|n\|$ and the multiplication rule for the <Pauli matrices> give these orthogonal rank-one <spectral projectors>, with eigenvalues $(1\pm\|n\|)/2$. Their <Bloch vectors> are opposite unit vectors $\pm\widehat n$. At $n=0$ the state is maximally mixed and the direction is undefined; any orthonormal eigenbasis gives an antipodal pair, but no pair is distinguished by the state.

= Generalized Bloch representation
{c}
{parent=Bloch vector}
{title2=$\rho=I/N+\sum_{k=1}^{N^2-1}s_k\sigma_k$}

Choose a <Hilbert-Schmidt inner product> orthonormal Hermitian operator basis with final element $I/\sqrt N$. A <density operator> has real coefficients $r_k=\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_k\rho)$ and fixed final coefficient $1/\sqrt N$. The remaining coefficients form its generalized <Bloch vector>. Positivity restricts these real vectors to a compact convex body; for $N>2$ this body is not the entire Euclidean ball allowed by a purity bound.

= Hamiltonian rotations of Bloch vectors
{c}
{parent=Generalized Bloch representation}
{title2=$\dot s=As,\quad A^T=-A$}

In a Hilbert-Schmidt orthonormal operator basis, the generator $-i[H,\cdot]$ is represented on the traceless Hermitian subspace by a real <skew-symmetric matrix>. Its propagator is therefore in the <special orthogonal group> and preserves the <purity of a density operator>. For $N>2$, these rotations form the adjoint image of the accessible unitary group, generally a proper subgroup of the full Euclidean rotation group; preservation of the full <spectrum> of the <density operator> imposes further constraints.

= Purity bound for generalized Bloch vectors
{parent=Generalized Bloch representation}
{title2=$\|s\|^2=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)-1/N\leq1-1/N$}

For a Hilbert-Schmidt orthonormal traceless Hermitian basis, the <generalized Bloch representation> gives the displayed identity by orthogonality. Nonnegative <eigenvalues> of a <density operator> sum to one, so their squared sum is at most one, with equality precisely for a rank-one projector. For $N>2$, the radius bound alone does not guarantee positivity: $(2/N)I-P$ for a rank-one projector $P$ has <trace> one and squared norm one, but one negative <eigenvalue>.

= Affine Bloch equation
{c}
{parent=Generalized Bloch representation}
{title2=$\dot s=As+c$}

A time-independent <Lindblad equation> becomes an affine linear equation for the <generalized Bloch representation>. If $\mathcal L(I)=\sum_d[V_d,V_d^\dagger]$, then $A_{mn}=\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_m\mathcal L(\sigma_n))$ and $c_m=\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_m\mathcal L(I))/N$ for traceless basis indices. The offset vanishes exactly when the dynamics are unital. Equilibria solve $As_*=-c$, and deviations evolve as $s(t)-s_*=e^{At}(s(0)-s_*)$.

= Transverse relaxation
{parent=Affine Bloch equation}
{title2=$T_2^{-1}=\Gamma=T_1^{-1}/2+\gamma_\phi$}

Decay of the off-diagonal coherence of a two-level <density operator>. Population relaxation contributes half its total rate; additional <dephasing> contributes $\gamma_\phi\geq0$ in the usual completely positive two-level model. Thus transverse relaxation should not automatically be identified with pure dephasing alone.

= Population relaxation
{parent=Affine Bloch equation}
{title2=$T_1^{-1}=\gamma_{12}+\gamma_{21}$}

For a two-level <density operator>, rates $\gamma_{12}$ from level one to two and $\gamma_{21}$ in the reverse direction give $\dot p_1=-\gamma_{12}p_1+\gamma_{21}p_2$. Their sum sets the longitudinal population relaxation rate, and their ratio fixes the stationary population imbalance. If both rates vanish, populations are conserved.

= Steady states of an affine Bloch equation
{parent=Affine Bloch equation}
{title2=$As_*=-c$}

An arbitrary real affine equation has <steady states> exactly when $\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}[A\mid-c]$. Its solution set is $s_*+\ker A$. For a finite-dimensional time-independent <Lindblad equation>, physical stationary states always exist by time averaging trajectories. If $A$ is invertible the <steady state> is unique. Conversely, a nonzero traceless Hermitian stationary direction can be decomposed into the difference of two equally weighted <density operators>; time averaging their trajectories yields two distinct stationary <density operators>. Thus singularity of the physical Bloch matrix also obstructs physical uniqueness.

= Bloch sphere
{c}
{parent=Bloch vector}
{title2=$S^2$}
{wiki}

The <Bloch sphere> is the unit sphere of <Bloch vectors>, corresponding to pure <qubit> states through $\rho=(I+\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2$. A local unitary change of qubit basis induces a rotation of this sphere, with its common phase physically irrelevant. Mixed states occupy the interior of the unit ball.

= Pure-qubit overlap identity
{parent=Bloch sphere}
{title2=$|\langle u|v\rangle|^2=\tfrac12(1+u\cdot v)$}

Write each pure <density matrix> as $(I+r\cdot\sigma)/2$. The <trace> identities of the <Pauli matrices>, $\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_i=0$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j)=2\delta_{ij}$, make the <trace> of their product equal to the displayed overlap. Antipodal unit vectors represent <orthogonal> states; vectors at right angles correspond to squared overlap one half. For mixed states the same <trace> formula gives Hilbert–Schmidt overlap, not in general quantum fidelity.

= Uniform pure-qubit average
{parent=Bloch sphere}
{title2=$d\mu=\frac{\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi}{4\pi}$}

= Haar-uniform qubit average
{c}
{synonym}

The rotation-invariant average of pure <qubit> states is uniform area measure on the <Bloch sphere>, induced by <Haar measure> on unitary transformations. For a uniformly distributed unit <Bloch vector> $r$, rotational symmetry gives $\mathbb Er_i=0$ and $\mathbb Er_ir_j=\delta_{ij}/3$. Uniform polar angle without the factor $\sin\theta$ is a different distribution.

= Informationally complete three-observable qubit tomography
{parent=Bloch vector}

Three Hermitian expectation values determine an arbitrary qubit state exactly when the traceless parts of the three observables span the three-dimensional space generated by the Pauli matrices. Mere linear independence as Hermitian matrices is insufficient: $I,\sigma_x,\sigma_z$ are independent but cannot detect the sign or magnitude of the Bloch $y$ component.
