= Classical mechanics
{wiki}

<Classical mechanics> describes the motion of bodies by assigning <positions> and <velocities> and specifying how their interactions change those quantities. A <point mass> is the simplest model: its size and internal structure are ignored, leaving a <mass> and a moving <position>. A <rigid body> retains orientation and <rotational kinetic energy>. Choosing a model means deciding which features affect the question being asked; a sliding block may be treated as a <point mass>, whereas predicting its <rolling without slipping> requires its <moment of inertia>.

Three complementary questions organize the subject. <Newton's second law> asks how the net <force> determines the instantaneous <acceleration>. The <work-energy theorem> asks how that <force>, acting through a <displacement>, changes <kinetic energy>. <Conservation of mechanical energy> asks which combinations of <position> and <velocity> are accessible when the interactions admit a time-independent <potential energy> and no other <force> supplies net <power>. These descriptions are compatible, but answer different questions: an <energy> calculation may find a maximum <speed> without first solving for <position> at every <time>.

The <harmonic oscillator> below makes this unity visible. A restoring <force> produces a repeating <position> trace; the corresponding <phase plane> has a closed <ellipse>; <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> exchange while their sum stays fixed. <Lagrangian mechanics> and <Hamiltonian mechanics> provide further ways to organize the same dynamics, especially when coordinates or constraints make individual <forces> awkward to track.

The scope of <classical mechanics> also matters. <Newtonian mechanics> approximates motion at <speeds> small compared with the <speed of light>; <special relativity> changes that description when this approximation fails. <Quantum mechanics> is needed for phenomena for which a classical assignment of simultaneous <position> and <momentum> is inadequate. These limitations do not prevent <classical mechanics> from being a useful predictive model within its regime. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter1.pdf][MIT mechanics, Chapter 1: scope and limitations]{ref}

= Variable-mass system
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{title2=$m\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf F+(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\dot m$}
{wiki}

A body exchanging <mass> with material of inertial <velocity> $\mathbf u$ satisfies the displayed <momentum conservation> law for one stream, with signed $\dot m$ positive for entry and negative for exit. The <force> $\mathbf F$ excludes the incoming or outgoing momentum flux. A short time interval gives $d(m\mathbf v)/dt=\mathbf F+\mathbf u\dot m$, proving the formula. For stationary incoming matter, $d(m\mathbf v)/dt=\mathbf F$; for matter leaving at the body's own <velocity>, $m\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf F$. The <rocket equation> and accreting-drop motion are applications.

= Accreting raindrop with quadratic drag
{parent=Variable-mass system}
{title2=$\dot a=cv/4,\quad\dot v=g-3(c+k)v^2/(4a)$}

For a spherical drop of constant <mass density> $\rho$, radius $a>0$ and downward <velocity> $v>0$, suppose its volume grows at rate $c\pi a^2v$ by collecting stationary mist and its upward <quadratic drag> is $k\rho\pi a^2v^2$, with $c,k>0$. The drop <mass> is $4\pi\rho a^3/3$. Its incoming <mass accretion rate> and the <variable-mass system> momentum law give the displayed equations. The accreted matter must be accelerated from rest, producing the additional $c$ term alongside drag.

= Speed-radius attractor of an accreting raindrop
{parent=Accreting raindrop with quadratic drag}
{title2=$v^2/a\longrightarrow8g/(7c+6k),\quad\dot v\longrightarrow cg/(7c+6k)$}

For the <accreting raindrop with quadratic drag>, set $q=v^2/a$ and $B=7c/8+3k/4$. Its exact equation is $\dot q=2(v/a)(g-Bq)$. Dividing by $\dot a=cv/4$ gives $q(a)=g/B+(q(a_0)-g/B)(a_0/a)^{7+6k/c}$. Thus $q$ stays between its positive initial value and positive equilibrium. Since $d\sqrt a/dt=c\sqrt q/8$, the radius grows without finite-time divergence and tends to infinity. The power correction then vanishes, proving both limits. Positive drag makes the limiting <acceleration> strictly less than $g/7$.

= Inertia
{parent=Classical mechanics}

<Inertia> is the resistance of a body's motion to change under applied force. For a particle, <Newton's second law> relates force to the rate of change of momentum; at constant mass it is $F=ma$. In an overdamped approximation the inertial acceleration term is small compared with drag over the time scales considered.

= Mechanical configuration space
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{title2=$Q$}

= Configuration space
{synonym}

The <configuration space> of a mechanical system is the space of its allowed positions or generalized coordinates. Its tangent bundle describes configurations and velocities, and its cotangent bundle describes configurations and conjugate momenta. For smooth holonomic constraints it is a manifold on which geometric Lagrangian and Hamiltonian descriptions can be built. Singular constraints or symmetry quotients can require strata rather than one global smooth manifold.

= Relational shape space
{parent=Classical mechanics}

The <relational shape space> of a particle configuration is obtained by removing collective translations and rotations; here scale is retained. For generic collision-free, noncollinear configurations of $N\ge3$ labelled particles in three dimensions, the resulting configuration dimension is $3N-6$. Collinear configurations have nontrivial rotational stabilizers and require singular strata. Removing position and orientation does not automatically remove conserved angular momentum or its centrifugal effect; a dynamically faithful reduction must retain the appropriate momentum-level data.

= Simple pendulum
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{title2=$\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0$}

A point mass constrained to a fixed-length massless arm under uniform gravitational <acceleration> is a simple pendulum. With no drag its angular equation is $\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0$. The <small-angle approximation> yields <simple harmonic motion>, whereas the full restoring force is nonlinear. Adding drag gives a <damped pendulum>.

For an ideal point mass $m$ swinging in a vertical plane, the <torque> about the pivot is $-mg\ell\sin\theta$. Dividing by its <moment of inertia> $m\ell^2$ gives the displayed equation. Both the inertia and the gravitational <torque> scale with $m$, explaining why the point mass cancels. This derivation and the small-amplitude period are given in \a[https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-4-pendulums][Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums]{ref}.

Released from rest at a small angular <amplitude> $\theta_0$, the linearized solution is $\theta(t)\simeq\theta_0\cos(\sqrt{g/\ell}\,t)$, with <oscillation period> $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$. For $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, lengths one and four metres give $T_0\approx2.0061\,\mathrm s$ and $4.0121\,\mathrm s$. This same scaling follows from <dimensional analysis>; the dynamical calculation supplies the coefficient $2\pi$.

Finite angular <amplitude> changes the exact ideal period. <Conservation of energy> gives $\dot\theta^2=(2g/\ell)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)$ for release from rest. Integrating a quarter oscillation and substituting $\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(\theta_0/2)\sin\phi$ gives
$$
T(\theta_0)=4\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\,K\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta_0}2\right),\qquad
\frac{T(\theta_0)}{T_0}=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2(\theta_0/2)\sin^2\phi}},\quad 0<\theta_0<\pi.
$$
Here $K$ is the <complete elliptic integral of the first kind>. The <small-angle approximation> gives a ratio tending to one; at $10^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ$, the ratios are approximately $1.00191,1.07318,1.18034$. Thus almost constant period at small <amplitude> has a quantitative limit. As $\theta_0\to\pi$, the period diverges, connecting to the <pendulum separatrix approach takes infinite time>.

{title=Length scaling and finite-amplitude period corrections for an ideal simple pendulum}

These predictions assume the specified ideal system. Drag changes the <mechanical energy>; a massive support changes the <moment of inertia>. The distinction is useful in experiments: a change in period can test an approximation rather than merely indicate an arithmetic mistake.

= Conical pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{wiki}

A conical pendulum moves at constant latitude around a vertical axis. For length $\ell$ and angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical, the <Euler–Lagrange equation> gives $\dot\phi^2\cos\theta=g/\ell$. Its horizontal speed satisfies $v^2=g\ell\sin^2\theta/\cos\theta$. It is a useful example of a relative equilibrium: the position changes while its shape relative to the symmetry axis is fixed.

= Damped pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$\ddot\theta+c\dot\theta+\sin\theta=0$}

In units where the small-amplitude natural frequency is one, linear velocity drag gives $\ddot\theta+c\dot\theta+\sin\theta=0$ with $c>0$. The even multiples of $\pi$ are attracting equilibria; the odd multiples are <saddle equilibria>. Small displacements obey a <damped harmonic oscillator>; the <energy dissipation of a damped pendulum> governs the global <phase portrait>.

= Energy dissipation of a damped pendulum
{parent=Damped pendulum}
{title2=$\dot E=-c\dot\theta^2$}

For the normalized <damped pendulum>, $E=\dot\theta^2/2+1-\cos\theta$ obeys $\dot E=-c\dot\theta^2\leq0$. Its <mechanical energy> is constant only along an equilibrium trajectory, and strictly decreases over every positive-length interval on a nonstationary trajectory. At an individual turning point its derivative vanishes. This excludes nonconstant periodic trajectories despite oscillatory approach to a <stable focus>.

{c}

= Eisenhart lift
{c}
{synonym}

= Eisenhart-Duval metric lift
{c}
{synonym}

A mechanical Lagrangian quadratic in velocities can be represented by <geodesics> of a <Lorentzian metric> with an extra null coordinate. For $L=\tfrac12|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2-2\mathbf A\cdot\dot{\mathbf x}-U$, use $g=d\mathbf x^2+2dt(du-2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x-Udt)$. The null <Killing vector field> $\partial_u$ gives the conserved momentum $p_u=t\prime$. Fixing $p_u=1$ projects the <geodesic equation> to the mechanical <Euler-Lagrange equations>. This is useful for relating mechanical forces to spacetime geometry.

= Gauge transformations of an Eisenhart-Duval lift
{parent=Eisenhart-Duval lift}
{title2=$\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\quad u\mapsto u+2\chi$}

A time-independent <gauge transformation> $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ in an <Eisenhart-Duval lift> is the coordinate change $u\mapsto u+2\chi$. It preserves the metric one-form $du-2\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x-Udt$ and adds only $-2d\chi/dt$ to the reduced Lagrangian.

= Null Kaluza-Klein reduction of a stationary force
{parent=Eisenhart-Duval lift}
{title2=$p_u=t\prime\ne0$}

For an <Eisenhart-Duval lift>, reduction along the null isometry fixes a nonzero conserved $p_u=t\prime$ and projects <geodesics> to $(t,\mathbf x)$. One never divides by $g_{uu}=0$. When $\mathbf E=-\nabla U$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf A=\mathbf B$, the reduced equation is $\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf E+2\mathbf B\times\dot{\mathbf x}$. Every trajectory admits a null lift by choosing $\dot u=U+2\mathbf A\cdot\dot{\mathbf x}-\tfrac12|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2$.

= Newtonian mechanics
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf F=m\mathbf a$}

Newtonian mechanics describes motion using forces, masses and acceleration in <inertial frames> with absolute time. It is the low-speed limit of <special relativity>; for constant <proper acceleration> with $|at|\ll c$, relativistic <hyperbolic motion> reduces to $x\simeq at^2/2$.

= Small-slope particle motion on a height graph
{parent=Newtonian mechanics}
{title2=$\ddot{\mathbf r}\simeq-g\nabla h$}

A particle constrained to the graph $z=h(x,y)$ has <kinetic energy> $m[|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2+(\nabla h\cdot\dot{\mathbf r})^2]/2$ and <potential energy> $mgh$. For a gently varying graph and velocities for which its geometric kinetic correction is higher order, the leading <Euler-Lagrange equations> are the displayed planar gradient-force equations. Small slope alone at arbitrarily large speed or curvature does not justify discarding the normal inertial acceleration. For a radial height, the leading planar <angular momentum> is conserved, allowing an <effective potential> reduction.

= Radial turning points for an inverse-square height well
{parent=Small-slope particle motion on a height graph}
{title2=$\dot r^2=2\mathcal E+2g/r-(L^2+2g)/r^2$}

For the height profile $h(r)=r^{-2}-r^{-1}$, set $A=L^2+2g$ and use the planar small-slope model. Its <effective potential> per unit mass is $V_{\rm eff}=A/(2r^2)-g/r$. Release with negligible radial speed at $r_0$ gives $\mathcal E=A/(2r_0^2)-g/r_0$ and the displayed radial-speed law. A bounded trajectory requires $\mathcal E<0$, equivalently $r_0>A/(2g)$. The two positive turning radii are $r_0$ and $Ar_0/(2gr_0-A)$; they coincide at the circular solution $r_0=A/g$. Zero energy is marginal escape, not bounded motion.

= Tangentially driven motion on an axisymmetric surface
{parent=Newtonian mechanics}
{title2=$\dot\phi^2=\frac{g h'(r)}{r+2r_\phi^2/r-r_{\phi\phi}}$}

Neglecting vertical acceleration and the vertical driving-force component, the radial normal reaction is $-Mg h'(r)$. A drive parallel to the horizontal velocity cancels when the radial acceleration equation is combined with $r_\phi/r$ times the azimuthal equation. For a prescribed path $r(\phi)$ this gives the displayed speed constraint. It describes the projected approximation, not arbitrary trajectories with substantial vertical motion.

= Friction
{parent=Newtonian mechanics}

Friction is a tangential contact interaction between surfaces that opposes their relative sliding or tendency to slide. <Kinetic friction> describes sliding contact; a static contact force can instead support <rolling without slipping>.

= Kinetic friction
{parent=Friction}
{title2=$|F_{\mathrm{fr}}|=\mu N$}

The Coulomb sliding-friction model gives a tangential <force> of magnitude $\mu N$, opposite the relative slip velocity at contact, where $N$ is the <normal force>. The transition to <rolling without slipping> occurs when contact slip vanishes; the sliding law is then replaced by the appropriate static contact condition.

= Two-stage stopping under linear drag and dry friction
{parent=Kinetic friction}
{title2=$t=\frac M\lambda\log\frac U{u_*}+\frac{u_*}{\mu g},\quad d=\frac M\lambda(U-u_*)+\frac{u_*^2}{2\mu g}$}

For a body of mass $M$ initially at speed $U$, suppose the total resistance is <linear drag> $\lambda u$ until speed $u_*>0$, followed by <kinetic friction> $\mu Mg$ until rest. The first stage solves $M\dot u=-\lambda u$, giving duration $(M/\lambda)\log(U/u_*)$ and distance $(M/\lambda)(U-u_*)$. Constant deceleration in the second stage gives duration $u_*/(\mu g)$ and distance $u_*^2/(2\mu g)$. The stopping distance is finite because the switch removes the infinite exponential stopping time of pure linear drag.

= Newton–Cartan theory
{parent=Newtonian mechanics}
{c}
{wiki}

Newton–Cartan theory describes <Newtonian mechanics> geometrically using absolute time, a degenerate spatial inverse metric, and a compatible torsion-free <affine connection>. It is not a theory with a nondegenerate Lorentzian metric. Gravitationally accelerated particles are geodesics of that connection, whose curvature measures tidal gravity.

= Newton–Cartan gravitational connection
{parent=Newton–Cartan theory}
{c}
{title2=$\Gamma^i{}_{00}=\partial_i\Phi$}

In Cartesian spatial coordinates and absolute time $t$, the <gravitational potential> $\Phi(t,x)$ determines an <affine connection> by the displayed coefficients, all others zero. The <geodesic equation> with affine time is $d^2x^i/dt^2=-\partial_i\Phi$. It preserves $dt$ and the spatial inverse metric $h^{ij}=\delta^{ij}$, reproducing freely falling <Newtonian mechanics> in a curved spacetime connection.

= Newtonian tidal curvature
{parent=Newton–Cartan gravitational connection}
{title2=$R^i{}_{0j0}=\partial_i\partial_j\Phi,\quad R_{00}=\nabla^2\Phi$}

Using $[\nabla_c,\nabla_d]V^a=R^a{}_{bcd}V^b$, the <Newton–Cartan gravitational connection> has only the displayed Hessian components and their antisymmetric partners in the last two indices. The <geodesic deviation> equation gives relative acceleration $-\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\,\delta x^j$. The <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> is therefore $R_{ab}=4\pi G\rho\,(dt)_a(dt)_b$. A spatially uniform gravitational acceleration has zero tidal curvature even if the connection coefficients do not vanish in the original coordinates.

= Equation of motion
{parent=Classical mechanics}

= Equations of motion
{synonym}

An equation of motion determines how a physical state evolves. A variational action gives <Euler-Lagrange equations>; a <phase-space action> instead gives canonical <Hamiltonian mechanics> equations together with any imposed <constraints>.

= Time reversal in classical mechanics
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{title2=$x(t)\mapsto x(-t)$}

<Time reversal in classical mechanics> reverses the order of a trajectory's states and the sign of its velocities. A coordinate is time-even, while its velocity is time-odd. <Microscopic reversibility> compares a path with this physical reversal, including the correct equilibrium initial weights.

= Newton's law of universal gravitation
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{c}
{wiki}

Newton's law of universal gravitation states that two point masses attract one another with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

= Free fall
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{wiki}

Motion under a <Newtonian gravitational field> without other forces. The Newtonian radial <free-fall speed> from rest at infinity is $v=\sqrt{2GM/r}$.

= Rotational deflection of a falling body
{parent=Free fall}

In an Earth-fixed <rotating reference frame>, the horizontal displacement of a freely falling body receives contributions from <Coriolis acceleration> and <centrifugal acceleration>. A short-fall approximation substitutes the unperturbed downward <velocity> into the Coriolis term and the Earth's surface <position> into the centrifugal term. If <gravitational acceleration> excludes centrifugal acceleration, both contributions must be included explicitly.

= Newtonian gravitational field
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf g$}

Outside a spherical mass $M$, the Newtonian gravitational field is radial and has magnitude
$$
g(r)=\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$

= Uniform gravitational field in an off-center spherical cavity
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$g=-4\pi G\rho c/3$}

Remove a spherical void centered at $c$ from a uniform sphere centered at the origin. At points inside the void, <superposition> combines the sphere's interior field $-4\pi G\rho x/3$ with the removed sphere's field $+4\pi G\rho(x-c)/3$. Their sum is the displayed constant <Newtonian gravitational field>. A second removed mass outside this void adds a nonuniform contribution.

= Cavity equilibrium destabilized by a second void
{parent=Uniform gravitational field in an off-center spherical cavity}

A uniform <Newtonian gravitational field> inside one spherical void can be cancelled at a point by the repulsive field representing another removed sphere. The latter field has a <Jacobian matrix> proportional to $I-3\hat r\hat r^T$: its axial eigenvalue is negative and both transverse eigenvalues positive. The equilibrium is restoring along the axis but unstable transversely. A zero force is thus not enough for stable rest.

= Newtonian gravitational stress tensor
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{c}
{title2=$T_{ij}$}

For a <Newtonian gravitational potential> obeying the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>, define $T_{ij}=[\partial_i\Phi\partial_j\Phi-\delta_{ij}|\nabla\Phi|^2/2]/(4\pi G)$. Commutation of mixed <partial derivatives> gives $\partial_jT_{ij}=\rho\partial_i\Phi$. Thus the gravitational force density can be put in conservative form as $-\partial_jT_{ij}$. The sign is chosen so that $T_{ij}$ is added to the momentum flux; reversing the stress convention reverses the divergence sign.

= Gravitational stress contribution to angular-momentum transport
{parent=Newtonian gravitational stress tensor}
{title2=$rT_{r\phi}$}

In cylindrical coordinates, the <Newtonian gravitational stress tensor> gives radial <angular-momentum flux> $rT_{r\phi}=r(\partial_r\Phi)(r^{-1}\partial_\phi\Phi)/(4\pi G)$. Nonaxisymmetric structure can correlate the field components and transport <angular momentum>. The isotropic negative-pressure part has zero off-diagonal component, so it gives no direct radial transport. This separates a shear flux from a contribution to normal force balance.

= Self-gravity
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

Self-gravity is the <gravitational acceleration> produced by a system's own <mass density>. It is obtained by solving the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> for that density, rather than treating the <Newtonian gravitational potential> as an externally fixed field. It competes with <pressure> and rotational support in <gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk>.

= Standard gravitational parameter
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$\mu$}
{wiki}

= Gravitational parameter
{synonym}

The product $\mu=GM$ of the <gravitational constant> and a central <mass>. In the exact relative <two-body problem>, the central parameter is $G(M_1+M_2)$. The appropriate conservative parameter can change when inverse-square <radiation pressure> is included in a dust <Kepler orbit>.

= Tidal tensor
{title2=$T_{jk}$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

The acceleration tidal tensor is $T_{jk}=\partial_k g_j=-\partial_j\partial_k\Phi$ for <gravitational acceleration> $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi$. It describes differential acceleration $\Delta\mathbf g\simeq T\Delta\mathbf r$. Some authors instead call the positive <Hessian matrix> $\partial_j\partial_k\Phi$ the tidal tensor, reversing this sign convention. For a point mass and the positive potential convention $\mathbf g=\nabla\psi$, the tensor is $Gm(3\mathbf R\mathbf R^{\mathsf T}/R^5-\mathbf1/R^3)$.

= Quadrupolar point-mass tidal potential
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{title2=$\psi_{\mathrm{tid}}=\tfrac12r_iT_{ij}r_j$}

Subtracting the constant and uniform-acceleration terms from the point-mass <relative potential> gives $\psi_{\mathrm{tid}}=Gm_p[3(\mathbf r\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}_p)^2-r^2]/(2r_p^3)$, with force convention $\nabla\psi$. Its <tidal tensor> is $Gm_p(3\widehat{\mathbf r}_p\widehat{\mathbf r}_p^T-I)/r_p^3$. This is the leading term for target size small compared with separation.

= Tidal disruption
{parent=Tidal tensor}

Disruption of a self-gravitating body when differential gravitational <acceleration> across it exceeds its binding <acceleration>. For a body of radius $R$, <mass> $m$ at distance $r$ from a much more massive body $M$, the competing scales are $GMR/r^3$ and $Gm/R^2$; their equality gives $r\sim R(M/m)^{1/3}$, up to geometry and internal-structure factors. The <tidal tensor> gives the directional differential <acceleration>. A <tidal disruption event> near a black hole is a specific astronomical application.

= Tidal force
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{wiki}

Differential gravitational <acceleration>, multiplied by the affected <mass>, across a spatially extended body. In the linear approximation the <acceleration> difference is $T\Delta\boldsymbol r$, where $T$ is the <tidal tensor>. Stellar <tidal forces> limit the domain of a <circumplanetary orbit>; sufficiently strong ones can produce <tidal disruption>.

= Love number
{c}
{title2=$k_2$}
{wiki}
{parent=Tidal force}

A Love number measures a body's tidal response to an applied potential. The degree-two potential response $k_2$ is sensitive to radial density structure and central concentration, and can complement mass and radius constraints on an <exoplanet interior>. Inferring it from orbital precession requires separating other contributions and assumptions about rotation and the tidal response.

= Quadrupolar tidal forcing
{parent=Tidal tensor}

For a point-mass companion at separation $a$, subtract the constant and linear terms of its <Newtonian gravitational potential> in a frame accelerating with the target's center. The leading residual is
$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{tide}}=-\frac{GM_2}{2a^3}\left[3(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)^2-r^2\right].
$$
It is degree two in <spherical harmonics>. In a circular orbit with angular frequency $\omega_o$, its time-dependent $m=\pm2$ components force at $2\omega_o$ in a frame with no spin; there is also a static $m=0$ component. This is the leading approximation in $r/a$, and the tidal amplitude may be absorbed into a harmonic-dependent constant.

= Gravitational acceleration
{title2=$g$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{wiki}

= Newtonian gravity
{synonym}
{c}

Gravitational acceleration is the acceleration of a freely falling test body in a <Newtonian gravitational field>[gravitational field]. Near Earth's surface its magnitude is approximately constant on shallow vertical scales, and is conventionally denoted by $g$.

= Newtonian central configuration
{c}
{parent=Gravitational acceleration}
{title2=$\nabla_iW=\Lambda m_i\mathbf a_i$}

A collision-free positive-mass configuration satisfies the displayed equation when every gravitational acceleration points proportionally toward a common centre. With $W=-\sum_{i<j}Gm_im_j/|\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j|$, homogeneity gives $\Lambda I=-W>0$, $I=\sum_i m_i|\mathbf a_i|^2$. Pairwise force cancellation implies $\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i=0$. Such a configuration generates a nonrotating <homothetic gravitational motion> by multiplying all position vectors by one positive scale factor.

= Homothetic gravitational motion
{parent=Newtonian central configuration}
{title2=$\ddot a=-\Lambda/a^2$}

For a <Newtonian central configuration>, set $\mathbf x_i(t)=a(t)\mathbf a_i$ with $a>0$. The gravitational force scales as $a^{-2}$, reducing all equations to the displayed scalar <ordinary differential equation>. Its first integral is $\dot a^2/2-\Lambda/a=k/2$. The total <energy> is $kI/2$, so the scalar energy constant exactly tracks the many-body energy for the fixed shape.

= Gravitational constant
{title2=$G$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{wiki}

The gravitational constant is the proportionality constant in <Newton's law of universal gravitation>.

= Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity
{c}
{title2=$\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

For <mass density> $\rho$, the <Newtonian gravitational potential> satisfies the <Poisson equation> $\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho$. The gravitational acceleration is $-\nabla\Phi$. An isolated system also requires the exterior vacuum solution and decay at infinity; solving the interior equation alone does not fix a globally physical field.

= Dyadic spherical shells with inverse-radius density scaling
{parent=Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity}
{title2=$M(2^{-N})=M(1)\,4^{-N}$}

Suppose the shell $2^{-n}<r<2^{1-n}$ has <mass density> $2^{n-1}\rho_0$. Its mass is $(14\pi\rho_0/3)4^{-n}$, by integrating $4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$. Summing the <geometric series> gives total mass $14\pi\rho_0/9$. At $r=2^{-N}$ the enclosed shells have indices $n\geq N+1$, so the enclosed mass is the total mass times $r^2$. The magnitude of the <Newtonian gravitational field> at every such boundary is therefore the same, $14\pi G\rho_0/9$. This does not assert that the field is constant throughout each shell.

= Newtonian gravitational potential
{title2=$\Phi$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{c}

The Newtonian gravitational potential is the potential energy per unit test mass. Outside a spherical mass $M$ it is $\Phi(r)=-GM/r$ after choosing zero at infinity, and the gravitational acceleration is $-\nabla\Phi$.

= Planetary quadrupole coefficient
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$J_2$}

The dimensionless coefficient $J_2$ describes the leading axisymmetric departure of a planet's external <Newtonian gravitational potential> from a monopole: $\Phi=-GM_p/r+[GM_pJ_2R_p^2/r^3]P_2(\cos\vartheta)$, where $\vartheta$ is colatitude from the spin axis. An oblate mass distribution has positive $J_2$. The <disturbing function> is minus the nonspherical potential term. On a circular inclined <Kepler orbit>, $\cos\vartheta=\sin I\sin M$, so <orbit averaging> gives $\overline{\mathcal R}_{J_2}=GM_pJ_2R_p^2a^{-3}(1/2-3\sin^2I/4)$. The exact circular nodal rate is $-3nJ_2(R_p/a)^2\cos I/2$; its small-tilt limit is $-3nJ_2(R_p/a)^2/2$.

= Oblate-quadrupole epicyclic precession
{parent=Planetary quadrupole coefficient}
{title2=$\kappa^2=n^2(1-3\alpha^2/R^2),\quad\nu^2=n^2(1+9\alpha^2/R^2)$}

For $\Phi=-\mu/r+\alpha^2\mu(3z^2-r^2)/r^5$, a nearly circular equatorial orbit has $n^2=\mu/R^3$ and azimuthal frequency $\Omega_c^2=n^2(1+3\alpha^2/R^2)$. The <radial epicyclic frequency> is $\kappa^2=R\,d\Omega_c^2/dR+4\Omega_c^2$, and the <vertical epicyclic frequency> is $\nu^2=\partial_z^2\Phi|_{z=0}$. Radial stability requires $R^2>3\alpha^2$. The <nodal precession> rate $\Omega_c-\nu$ is negative and the <apsidal precession> rate $\Omega_c-\kappa$ positive. These sign statements concern small eccentricity and inclination. For an arbitrary inclined orbit, the averaged <argument of periapsis> instead evolves as $(3/2)n\alpha^2[5\cos^2i-1]/[a^2(1-e^2)^2]$ and can regress.

= Axisymmetric logarithmic gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=\tfrac12V^2\log(R^2+z^2/q^2)$}

An axisymmetric logarithmic <Newtonian gravitational potential> has spheroidal equipotential surfaces and a <flat galaxy rotation curve>: $R\Phi_R(R,0)=V^2$. The singular model is defined away from the origin; a fixed reference length makes the logarithm dimensionless. Its central $r^{-2}$ <mass density> cusp is locally integrable, but its total <mass> diverges at large radius.

= Density positivity for an axisymmetric logarithmic potential
{parent=Axisymmetric logarithmic gravitational potential}
{title2=$q^2\geq1/2$}

The <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> gives
$$
\rho=\frac{V^2}{4\pi Gq^2}\frac{R^2+(2-q^{-2})z^2}{(R^2+q^{-2}z^2)^2}.
$$
For $V>0$, nonnegative <mass density> away from the origin is equivalent to $q^2\geq1/2$. Strict positivity requires $q^2>1/2$. This condition does not by itself guarantee a nonnegative <galactic distribution function>.

= Power-law gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=-Ar^{-\beta}$}

A radial <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi=-Ar^{-\beta}$ produces inward acceleration $A\beta r^{-\beta-1}$. The point-mass case has $\beta=1$ and $A=GM$. Treating the <power-law gravitational potential> as prescribed does not require it to be sourced by the flowing gas.

= Gravitational quadrupole potential of a point-mass binary
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi_2=-G\mu[3(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf r)^2-x^2r^2]/(2x^5)$}

For a <binary star> viewed far from its <centre of mass>, let $\mathbf r$ be the separation and $\mu$ the <reduced mass>, $M=M_1+M_2$ the total <mass>, $r=|\mathbf r|$ and $x=|\mathbf x|$. The <Taylor expansion> of the point-mass <Newtonian gravitational potential> has zero dipole because $\sum_A M_A\mathbf x_A=0$. Its second moment is $\sum_A M_Ax_{Ai}x_{Aj}=\mu r_i r_j$, so
$$
\Phi=-\frac{GM}{x}-\frac{G\mu}{2x^5}\left[3(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf r)^2-x^2r^2\right]+O(GMr^3/x^4).
$$
This holds for an instantaneous separation in any orbit, not only a <circular orbit>. The <mass quadrupole moment> convention $Q_{ij}=\mu(r_ir_j-r^2\delta_{ij}/3)$ gives $\Phi_2=-3GQ_{ij}x_ix_j/(2x^5)$.

= Flattened power-law gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=\Phi_0[R_0/(R^2+\lambda^2z^2)^{1/2}]^\beta$}

A flattened power-law <Newtonian gravitational potential> has equipotential spheroids $R^2+\lambda^2z^2=\text{constant}$. For $\Phi_0<0$ and $0<\beta<1$, its midplane <circular orbit> angular frequency satisfies $\Omega^2=-\beta\Phi_0R_0^\beta R^{-\beta-2}$.

= Density positivity for a flattened power-law potential
{parent=Flattened power-law gravitational potential}

Applying the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> to a <flattened power-law gravitational potential> yields
$$
\rho=-\frac{\beta\Phi_0R_0^\beta}{4\pi G}\frac{(\lambda^2-\beta)R^2+\lambda^2[2-(\beta+1)\lambda^2]z^2}{(R^2+\lambda^2z^2)^{(\beta+4)/2}}.
$$
For $0<\beta<1$, nonnegative <mass density> everywhere outside the origin requires and is ensured by $\beta\leq\lambda^2\leq2/(\beta+1)$. The central density singularity is locally integrable and carries no point mass, although the total mass diverges at large radius in this scale-free model.

= Rocket equation
{parent=Momentum}

= Tsiolkovsky rocket equation
{c}
{synonym}

For a body of varying mass $M$ ejecting material at fixed relative velocity, conservation of <momentum> gives a differential relation between $dM$ and the body's change $dv$. Integrating it produces a logarithm of the mass ratio.

= Constant-burn rocket with ground friction
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$q(t)\dot v=su-\mu gq(t)$}

For fuel mass rate $s>0$ and backward relative exhaust speed $u$, a rocket sliding on a horizontal plane has remaining mass $q(t)$ and satisfies the displayed <rocket equation> with <kinetic friction>. Integration over a sliding interval gives $v(t)-v(t_0)=u\log[q(t_0)/q(t)]-\mu g(t-t_0)$. From initial rest, immediate forward sliding additionally requires thrust to overcome <static friction>. If both friction coefficients equal $\mu$, motion starts only when $su>\mu gq(t)$. An algebraically negative sliding speed is not the physical solution at rest.

= Constant mass-loss propulsion with quadratic drag
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$m\dot u=QV-\alpha u^2$}

A body ejecting <mass> at constant rate $Q>0$ and relative exhaust <speed> $V>0$, against <quadratic drag> $\alpha u^2$, has remaining <mass> $m=m_0-Qt$. A short-interval <momentum> balance gives $m\dot u=QV-\alpha u^2$. From rest, <separation of variables> gives
$$
u(t)=\sqrt{\frac{QV}{\alpha}}\tanh\left(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha V}{Q}}\log\frac{m_0}{m_0-Qt}\right).
$$
For final <mass> $m_f>0$, this formula applies until $t=(m_0-m_f)/Q$. The zero-drag limit is the <rocket equation> $u_f=V\log(m_0/m_f)$. Two <dimensionless variables> are $\alpha V/Q$ and $m_0/m_f$.

= Zero-relative-velocity mass shedding
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$q\dot v=F_{\rm ext}$}

When matter leaves a moving body with zero instantaneous relative <velocity>, it carries the body's current <momentum> per unit mass but creates no additional recoil term. A short-interval <momentum> balance including the newly released material gives $q\dot v=F_{\rm ext}$ for the remaining body's mass $q(t)$. The expression $\frac d{dt}(qv)=F_{\rm ext}$ omits the outgoing momentum flux. Nonzero relative ejection <velocity> instead produces the thrust term of the <rocket equation>.

= Dust-collecting rocket
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$m\dot v=\alpha u-\beta v^2$}

A classical rocket ejecting fuel at rate $\alpha>0$ and relative exhaust <speed> $u>0$, while collecting stationary dust of <mass> $\beta\,dx$ per traveled distance, obeys
$$
\dot m=\beta v-\alpha,\qquad m\dot v=\alpha u-\beta v^2.
$$
These are <mass conservation> and <momentum conservation> for the rocket, incoming dust and outgoing gas. The dust-loading term supplies the <momentum> needed to accelerate incoming stationary material. Put $\lambda=\sqrt{\alpha/(\beta u)}$ and $V=\lambda u$. While the engines operate and $0\leq v<V$, an initially stationary rocket satisfies
$$
\frac m{m_0}=\left(\frac{V-v}{V}\right)^{(\lambda-1)/2}\left(\frac{V+v}{V}\right)^{-(\lambda+1)/2}.
$$
For $\lambda>1$, continuing to $V$ would drive total <mass> to zero. For $0<\lambda<1$, total <mass> instead grows without bound and $V$ is approached only after infinite time under indefinitely maintained thrust. In both cases a finite fuel supply runs out before that limiting <speed>. Total stored <mass> must not be confused with remaining fuel.

= Rocket equation with stationary mass accretion
{parent=Dust-collecting rocket}
{title2=$M\dot v=u\dot m-v\dot D$}

For a <rocket equation> with exhaust mass rate $\dot m$ and incoming stationary mass rate $\dot D$, mass balance is $\dot M=\dot D-\dot m$. A short-interval <momentum conservation> calculation gives $M\dot v=u\dot m-v\dot D$. Constant time rates $\dot D=\alpha$, $\dot m=\beta$ give $M\dot v+\alpha v=u\beta$, unlike a constant accretion rate per distance, whose incoming mass rate is proportional to speed. For unequal rates and initial rest, $v=(u\beta/\alpha)[1-(M/M_0)^{\alpha/(\beta-\alpha)}]$.

= Equal-rate rocket burning and accretion
{parent=Rocket equation with stationary mass accretion}
{title2=$v(t)=u(1-e^{-\alpha t/M_0})$}

If stationary mass arrives at the same constant time rate as fuel is ejected, total rocket mass stays fixed. The <rocket equation with stationary mass accretion> is then $M_0\dot v+\alpha v=\alpha u$. For initial rest, speed approaches the relative exhaust speed exponentially with relaxation time $M_0/\alpha$. This is the regular equal-rate limit of the unequal-rate mass-power formula.

= Relativistic rocket equation
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$m\gamma^2\,dv+u\,dm=0$}

For collinear motion without external forces, a rocket ejecting material backwards at constant speed $u$ in its instantaneous rest frame obeys $m\gamma^2\,dv+u\,dm=0$. Here $m$ is the rocket's <rest mass>, including stored <internal energy>, and $\gamma$ is its <Lorentz factor> in the chosen <inertial frame>. The equation follows from <momentum conservation>, <conservation of relativistic energy>, and the <relativistic velocity-addition formula>; expelled rest mass is not simply the decrease in rocket rest mass.

= Two-stage rocket with proportional fuel and body masses
{parent=Rocket equation}
{title2=$v_2=u\log\frac{M+m_0}{M+(1-k)m_0}+u\log\frac{M+m_0}{M}$}

For zero initial <velocity>, body <masses> $kM,(1-k)M$ and fuel <masses> $km_0,(1-k)m_0$, the <rocket equation> gives the displayed final second-stage <speed> after burning the first-stage fuel, discarding its body without impulse, and burning the second-stage fuel. It assumes $M>0$ and $0\le k<1$. The speed exceeds the single-stage value when $0<k<1$ and fuel is positive. Its limit as $k\to1^-$ concerns a surviving second stage of vanishing <mass>; at $k=1$ no such stage exists.

$$

= Magnetic axial angular-momentum invariant
{parent=Angular momentum}

= Angular momentum decay under uniform linear drag
{parent=Angular momentum}
{title2=$L(t)=L(0)e^{-kt/m}$}

Consider particles of the same constant mass $m$, interacting through equal-and-opposite central internal forces, with external forces $F_i=-k\dot r_i$ for constant $k$. About a fixed point $a$, internal pair <torques> cancel and the external <torque> is $G_a=-k\sum_i(r_i-a)\times\dot r_i=-(k/m)L_a$. Hence $\dot L_a=-(k/m)L_a$ and the displayed exponential decay follows. The same result holds for <angular momentum about the centre of mass>, because the moving-origin term $-\dot R\times P=-\dot R\times M\dot R$ vanishes. Unequal masses do not generally permit factoring out a single $k/m$.

= Generalized angular momentum in a radial monopole field
{parent=Angular momentum}
{title2=$\mathbf L=m\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}-\mu\widehat{\mathbf r}$}

For a radial conservative force plus the magnetic-type force $\dot{\mathbf r}\times(\mu\mathbf r/r^3)$, the torque equals $\mu\,d\widehat{\mathbf r}/dt$. Subtracting $\mu\widehat{\mathbf r}$ from ordinary <angular momentum> therefore produces a constant vector. Its dot product with $\widehat{\mathbf r}$ is $-\mu$, so motion lies on a cone about that vector. At $\mu=0$, ordinary planar <central force> motion is recovered. This is the classical radial <magnetic monopole> angular-momentum correction.

= Attractive and repulsive inverse-square closest approaches
{parent=Central-force radial turning point}

For unit <mass>, incoming <speed> $v>0$, <impact parameter> $b>0$, and <potential energy> $\mp\kappa/r$, put $q=\kappa/v^2>0$. = Conservation of mechanical energy
{parent=Mechanical energy}
{title2=$T+V=\mathrm{constant}$}

For constant masses, a time-independent <potential energy> $V$ and forces $\boldsymbol F_i=-\nabla_iV+\boldsymbol R_i$ with zero total extra power $\sum_i\boldsymbol R_i\cdot\boldsymbol v_i=0$, <Newton's second law> gives $dT/dt=-dV/dt$. Thus <mechanical energy> is conserved. Smooth stationary constraint reactions and a purely magnetic <Lorentz force> supply zero power. This is more restrictive than <conservation of energy> in general, which may include thermal or other internal forms.

= Work
{parent=Energy}
{wiki=Work_(physics)}

The work done by a force along a path is $W=\int F\mathbin{\cdot}dr$.

= Work-energy theorem
{parent=Work}
{title2=$W_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta T$}

For a <point mass> of constant <mass> $m$ observed in an <inertial frame>, the net <work> along its actual path equals its change in <kinetic energy>:
$$
W_{\mathrm{net}}=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}(t)\cdot\mathbf v(t)\,dt
=\int_a^b\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}\cdot d\mathbf r
=\frac12m|\mathbf v_b|^2-\frac12m|\mathbf v_a|^2.
$$
The middle expression is a <line integral>; the <dot product> selects the component of <force> along the <displacement>. Positive net <work> increases <speed>, negative net <work> decreases it, and zero net <work> leaves the endpoint <speeds> equal. Equal endpoint <speeds> do not imply equal <velocities>: a perpendicular <force> can continually change direction while doing zero <work>. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter13.pdf][MIT mechanics, Chapter 13, section 13.11]{ref}

The bridge to <Newton's second law> is short: take the <dot product> of $m\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}$ with <velocity>. The <chain rule> gives $d(m|\mathbf v|^2/2)/dt=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}\cdot\mathbf v$, equating the rate of change of <kinetic energy> to instantaneous <power>. Integrating gives the theorem. No <conservative force> assumption entered this argument; <friction> also contributes to the net <work>.

For the <harmonic oscillator> with <spring> stiffness $k=2\,\mathrm{N/m}$, release a <mass> $m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ from rest at $x=0.3\,\mathrm m$. The <spring> exerts $F=-kx$. Its <work> before the first passage through $x=0$ is
$$
W=\int_{0.3\,\mathrm m}^{0}(-kx)\,dx
=\frac12k(0.3\,\mathrm m)^2=0.09\,\mathrm J.
$$
Thus $mv^2/2=0.09\,\mathrm J$ gives <speed> $0.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ there. The <velocity> is negative on this passage because the <position> is decreasing. The <energy> calculation alone determines its magnitude, so the direction still requires information about the motion.

For a time-independent <potential energy> $V$, the <conservative force> contributes <work> $-\Delta V$. Separating all other <work> as $W_{\mathrm{other}}$ gives $\Delta(T+V)=W_{\mathrm{other}}$. Consequently <conservation of mechanical energy> requires zero net other <work>; the <work-energy theorem> itself does not require that conservation. With <damping>, <mechanical energy> decreases even though the theorem remains valid. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter14.pdf][MIT mechanics, Chapter 14: mechanical energy and nonconservative work]{ref}

= Kinetic energy
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

The nonrelativistic kinetic energy of a particle is $T=mv^2/2$.

= Kinetic energy decomposition about the center of mass
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$T=\frac12M|\dot R|^2+\frac12\sum_i m_i|\dot\rho_i|^2$}

= König theorem for kinetic energy
{c}
{synonym}

Writing particle positions relative to the <center of mass> as $\rho_i=r_i-R$ makes $\sum_i m_i\rho_i=0$, so the cross term in the squared <velocities> vanishes. The total <kinetic energy> splits into center-of-mass translation and relative motion as displayed. For a <rigid body>, the latter is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, where $I$ is the <moment of inertia> about the instantaneous rotation axis through the <center of mass>.

= Unit-direction kinetic-energy identity
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$v^2=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2-r^2\mathbf u\cdot\ddot{\mathbf u}$}

For a twice differentiable position away from the origin, write $\mathbf r=r\mathbf u$ with radial <unit vector> $\mathbf u$. Its unit-length constraint gives $\mathbf u\cdot\dot{\mathbf u}=0$ and $\mathbf u\cdot\ddot{\mathbf u}=-|\dot{\mathbf u}|^2$, separating radial and directional contributions to speed and <kinetic energy>. The identity is kinematic and does not require a particular force law.

= Kinetic energy density
{parent=Kinetic energy}

The kinetic energy density of a continuum with mass density $\rho$ and velocity $\mathbf v$ is $\rho|\mathbf v|^2/2$. For a displacement field, the material velocity is its time derivative.

= Rotational kinetic energy
{title2=$K=\frac12I\omega^2$}
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{wiki}

A rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with moment of inertia $I$ and angular speed $\omega$ has kinetic energy $K=I\omega^2/2$.

= Potential energy
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

A conservative force is the negative gradient of a potential energy: $F=-\nabla V$.

= Repulsive inverse-square potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$V(r)=c/r^2,\quad c>0$}

This <potential energy> generates a repulsive <central force> $F_r=-V'(r)=2c/r^3$. It is an inverse-square potential but an inverse-cube force; the powers must not be confused with the Coulomb or gravitational potential proportional to $1/r$.

= Scattering in a repulsive inverse-square potential
{parent=Repulsive inverse-square potential}
{title2=$r=r_{\min}\sec[\beta(\theta-\theta_0)]$}

For $V(r)=c/r^2$ with $c>0$, nonzero physical <angular momentum> $h$ and positive <energy> $E$, put $\beta=\sqrt{1+2mc/h^2}>1$ and $r_{\min}=\sqrt{(c+h^2/(2m))/E}$. Direct integration of the <effective potential> radial equation gives the displayed <orbit>. It has one radial minimum and two straight-line asymptotes separated by polar angle $\pi/\beta$. The repulsive scattering deflection is $\pi-\pi/\beta$; no bounded or circular orbit exists at finite positive energy.

= Gravitational energy
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$E_{\rm grav}$}
{wiki}

= Gravitational potential energy
{synonym}

The potential energy associated with gravitational interactions. For a self-gravitating spherical body with a fixed dimensionless density profile, $E_{\rm grav}=-C GM^2/R$ for a positive profile-dependent constant $C$; a uniform sphere has $C=3/5$. The $R^{-1}$ scaling competes with the same radius dependence of ultrarelativistic <electron degeneracy pressure> in a white dwarf, giving the <Chandrasekhar mass> scale.

= Available potential energy
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$A$}

The portion of a fluid system's <potential energy> that can be released by admissible rearrangement relative to a stable reference state. In a uniformly stratified incompressible fluid, the quadratic density is $A=\rho_0N^2\xi_z^2/2=\rho_0b^2/(2N^2)$, where $\xi_z$ is vertical <fluid displacement> and $b$ the buoyancy perturbation. It is positive for stable stratification and measures the restoring energy of <internal gravity waves>.

= Background potential energy
{parent=Available potential energy}
{title2=$P_b$}

The background potential energy is the <gravitational potential energy> of the statically stable state obtained by adiabatically sorting a fluid's parcels. The difference $A=P-P_b$ is <available potential energy>. Pure rearrangement changes $A$ but not $P_b$; irreversible density mixing can increase $P_b$. Molecular exchange with <internal energy> must be separated when defining an irreversible mixing rate.

= Potential function
{parent=Potential energy}

A potential function $V$ assigns a <scalar> to each point of a configuration or spatial domain. In mechanics it represents <potential energy>, and its negative <gradient> gives the corresponding <conservative force>.

= Central potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}

A central potential depends only on distance from a fixed centre, $V(\mathbf x)=V(|\mathbf x|)$. Its force is radial, and rotational symmetry implies conservation of <angular momentum>.

= Conservative force
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}

A force is conservative when its work depends only on the endpoints. On a simply connected domain this is equivalent to $F=-\nabla V$ for a <potential energy> $V$.

= Nonconservative force

A nonconservative force has path-dependent work. On a simply connected domain, a continuously differentiable force is nonconservative exactly when its curl does not vanish identically.

= Inverse-square potential

The potential $V(r)=-k/r$ generates the attractive inverse-square <central force> $F=-k r/r^3$.

= Exponential cusp potential
{parent=Potential energy}

The even potential
$$
V(x)=V_\infty(1-e^{-|x|/\lambda})
$$
has a cusp at the origin and approaches the finite value $V_\infty$. Motions below that value oscillate between symmetric turning points; motions above it escape.

= Escape velocity

Escape velocity is the least initial speed whose total energy permits motion to spatial infinity.

= Escape and circular speeds inside an isolated uniform sphere
{parent=Escape velocity}
{title2=$v_e^2=V_c^2(3r_0^2/r^2-1)$}

For a sphere of radius $r_0$ and uniform <mass density> $\rho_0$, with no surrounding <mass> and <gravitational potential> zero at infinity, the <spherical shell theorem> gives $V_c^2=4\pi G\rho_0r^2/3$ inside. Matching to the exterior point-mass potential gives $\Phi(r)=-(2\pi G\rho_0/3)(3r_0^2-r^2)$. Since $v_e^2=-2\Phi$, the displayed relation follows for $0<r\leq r_0$. Exterior shells leave the interior <circular speed> unchanged but deepen the potential and change the <escape velocity>; a uniform core alone is insufficient to determine escape.

= Equipotential surface

An equipotential surface is a level set of a scalar potential. Its gradient and the corresponding conservative force are normal to every smooth level surface.

An equipotential curve is a planar level curve of a scalar potential. Where the gradient is nonzero, its tangent is perpendicular to that gradient. In <potential flow>, it is therefore perpendicular to the <velocity field>; this distinguishes equipotential curves from <streamlines>.

= Force
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{wiki}

A force is an interaction that changes momentum; in Newtonian mechanics it satisfies $F=ma$ for constant mass.

= Restoring force
{parent=Force}
{wiki}
{title2=$F(x)=-kx$}

A restoring force acts toward an <equilibrium> when a body is displaced from it. In one dimension, taking <displacement> $x=0$ at <equilibrium>, this means $xF(x)<0$ for sufficiently small nonzero $x$. An ideal linear <spring> has $F(x)=-kx$ with positive <spring constant> $k$, producing <simple harmonic motion> for a constant <mass> without <damping>. More general restoring forces need not be linear.

= Tension
{parent=Force}
{title2=$T$}

Tension is a pulling <force> transmitted along a taut string, rope, or similar body. For an ideal uniform string with <linear mass density> $\mu$, constant tension $T$ sets the small transverse <wave> speed $c=\sqrt{T/\mu}$.

= Van der Waals force
{c}
{parent=Force}
{wiki}

The Van der Waals force is a distance-dependent nonbonded interaction between atoms or molecules, including dispersion and dipolar contributions. In the attractive nonretarded pair model, an interaction energy $U(r)=-\beta/r^6$ gives force magnitude $6\beta/r^7$. Macroscopic integration introduces a <Hamaker constant>; it does not replace the sign change and molecular cutoff needed at very short distances.

= Sphere-plane dispersion force
{parent=Van der Waals force}
{title2=$F\simeq Aa/(6d^2)$}

For a sphere of radius $a$ at separation $d\ll a$ from a half-space, pairwise integration of the attractive $r^{-6}$ molecular potential gives $F\simeq Aa/(6d^2)$. Equivalently, integrate the planar <disjoining pressure> magnitude $A/(6\pi h^3)$ over the local gap $h=d+r^2/(2a)$. Across a third medium the effective <Hamaker constant> can instead correspond to repulsion; state its sign convention explicitly.

The <static friction> force at impending sliding has magnitude $\mu_s$ times the normal contact <force>. In a simple grain model this coefficient represents resistance to downstream motion; the model excludes lift and detailed contact torques.

= Force balance
{parent=Force}

Force balance equates the rate of change of momentum to the sum of applied forces. A static continuum segment with internal resultant $\mathbf F(s)$ and force density $\mathbf f(s)$ obeys $\partial_s\mathbf F+\mathbf f=0$.

= Newton's laws of motion
{c}
{parent=Force}
{wiki}

<Newton's laws of motion> consist of inertia in an <inertial frame>, the relation between net <force> and change of <momentum>, and equal-and-opposite pair forces. The following three children state the individual laws.

= Newton's first law
{parent=Newton's laws of motion}
{c}

In an <inertial frame>, a body subject to no net force remains at rest or moves with constant velocity.

= Newton's second law
{parent=Newton's laws of motion}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=m\mathbf a$}

In an <inertial frame>, a <point mass> of constant <mass> $m>0$ obeys
$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=\sum_i\mathbf F_i
=\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=m\mathbf a,
\qquad \mathbf p=m\mathbf v,
\qquad \mathbf a=\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}.
$$
The net <force> is the sum of all <forces> acting on that body; <momentum>, <velocity> and <acceleration> refer to the same <reference frame>. A <force> changes <velocity>, rather than sustaining it: zero net <force> implies constant <velocity>, including a nonzero one. Doubling the net <force> at fixed <mass> doubles <acceleration>; doubling <mass> at fixed net <force> halves it. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf][MIT mechanics, Chapter 7, section 7.3]{ref}

For example, an otherwise balanced block of <mass> $0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ receives a constant horizontal net <force> $2\,\mathrm N$ for $0.5\,\mathrm s$. Its <acceleration> is $4\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, so its <velocity> changes by $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Equivalently, the <impulse> is $F\Delta t=1\,\mathrm{N\,s}$, and $\Delta v=\Delta p/m$. Starting from rest, its <displacement> during the pulse is $a(\Delta t)^2/2=0.5\,\mathrm m$. After the pulse, zero net <force> leaves it moving at constant <velocity>.

Applying the law therefore requires both a specification of the net <force> and <initial conditions>. In the <harmonic oscillator> example, the <force> is $-kx$, so <Newton's second law> becomes a <differential equation> for <position>. In an accelerating <reference frame>, the measured relative <acceleration> includes a contribution from the frame's own motion; the <equation of motion in a rotating frame> illustrates the extra terms required.

Constant <mass> is essential to the displayed $m\mathbf a$ form. A <variable-mass system> exchanging matter also exchanges <momentum>; its balance includes the entering or leaving matter's <velocity>. Simply differentiating $m\mathbf v$ and equating it to external <force> on that open body can omit this flux. The existing <variable-mass system> equation specifies the needed correction.

In the 1687 \i[Principia], <Newton's second law> was stated in terms of change of motion proportional to impressed <force>, directed along that <force>. Its original statement is verbal; the <derivative> equation above is modern notation. \a[https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/NATP00076][Newton, Axiomata sive leges motus (1687), Lex II]{ref}

= Avalanche front with linear mass entrainment
{parent=Newton's second law}
{title2=$\ddot x\longrightarrow g\sin\alpha/3$}

An idealized front at distance $x>0$ down a uniform slope entrains stationary material so that its moving mass is proportional to $x$. Its <linear momentum> balance is $d(x\dot x)/dt=Ax$, where $A=g\sin\alpha>0$. Multiplying by $x\dot x$ gives $x^2\dot x^2=2Ax^3/3+c$. Every forward-moving branch tends to $\dot x=\sqrt{2Ax/3}$ as $x\to\infty$, and $\ddot x=A/3-c/x^3\to A/3$. Acceleration is smaller than the constant-mass downslope value because some momentum accelerates newly entrained material.

= Equation of motion in a rotating frame
{parent=Classical mechanics}

= Equations of motion in a rotating frame
{synonym}

For a frame rotating with constant angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$,
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm inertial}
=\ddot{\mathbf x}+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}
+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x).
$$
The extra terms produce the Coriolis and centrifugal forces in the rotating-frame equation.

= Astroid motion of a harmonic oscillator in a rotating frame
{parent=Equation of motion in a rotating frame}
{title2=$(x',y')=(\cos^3\omega t,\sin^3\omega t)$}

For an isotropic <harmonic oscillator> with inertial frequency $2\omega$, viewed in a frame rotating at $\omega$, initial rotating coordinates $(1,0)$ and zero relative <velocity> produce the displayed <astroid> trajectory. The complex coordinate satisfies $\ddot\zeta+2i\omega\dot\zeta+3\omega^2\zeta=0$, with solution $\zeta=\tfrac34e^{i\omega t}+\tfrac14e^{-3i\omega t}$. The relative <speed> is $(3|\omega|/2)|\sin2\omega t|$, attaining its first maximum at $\pi/(4|\omega|)$ for nonzero $\omega$.

= Circular motion
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{wiki}

Uniform circular motion of radius $a$ and angular frequency $\omega$ has centripetal acceleration of magnitude $a\omega^2$ toward the centre.

= Centripetal acceleration
{parent=Circular motion}
{title2=$\mathbf a=-\omega^2\mathbf r$}

For uniform <circular motion> of radius $R$ and <angular speed> $\omega$, the <acceleration> is directed toward the centre and has magnitude $\omega^2R=v^2/R$. Writing $\mathbf r$ for the <position> relative to the centre gives $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\omega^2\mathbf r$. The required resultant <force> is $m\ddot{\mathbf r}$; it may be supplied by a <normal force>, gravity, or another physical interaction.

= Circular orbit
{parent=Circular motion}

A circular orbit maintains constant distance from its centre of attraction. Its inward radial acceleration has magnitude $v^2/r$, so the radial force must satisfy $F_r=-mv^2/r$.

= Circular-orbit energy gradient
{parent=Circular orbit}
{title2=$d\varepsilon=\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf h$}

In a spherically symmetric <gravitational potential> $\Phi(r)$, a <circular orbit> has $r\Omega^2=\Phi'(r)$, <specific orbital energy> $\varepsilon=\Phi+r^2\Omega^2/2$ and <specific angular momentum> $\mathbf h=r^2\Omega\mathbf n$, with $|\mathbf n|=1$. Differentiating gives $\varepsilon'=2r\Omega^2+r^2\Omega\Omega'=\Omega(r^2\Omega)'$. Changes in orbital orientation have $\mathbf n\cdot d\mathbf n=0$, so $\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf h=\Omega\,d(r^2\Omega)=d\varepsilon$. For a particle of variable <mass> $M$ and total <angular momentum> $\mathbf H=M\mathbf h$, this implies $d(M\varepsilon)=(\varepsilon-\Omega h)dM+\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf H$. Moreover $d(\varepsilon-\Omega h)/dr=-h\Omega'$, which is positive when the orbital <angular velocity> decreases outward.

= Circular orbits in an exponential central potential
{parent=Circular orbit}
{title2=$l^2=r^3e^{-r}$}

For unit mass with specific <angular momentum> $l$ in $V(r)=-e^{-r}$, the <effective potential> is $-e^{-r}+l^2/(2r^2)$. A positive-radius <circular orbit> satisfies the displayed equation. Since $r^3e^{-r}$ has its unique maximum $27/e^3$ at $r=3$, exactly two radii occur for $0<l^2<27/e^3$. Their radial stability follows from $V_{\rm eff}''=e^{-r}(3/r-1)$: the inner orbit is stable at fixed <angular momentum>, the outer unstable. The case $l=0$ has no finite-radius <circular orbit> despite satisfying the upper bound.

= Circular-orbit stability for a power-law central force
{parent=Circular orbit}
{title2=$\Omega_r^2=(n+3)ka^{n-1}$}

For the radial <acceleration> $-kr^n$ with $k>0$, a <circular orbit> of radius $a$ has squared <specific angular momentum> $h^2=ka^{n+3}$. At fixed $h$, its radial <small oscillations> have squared frequency $(n+3)ka^{n-1}$, so the orbit is stable for $n>-3$. This is a special case of the <effective potential stability criterion>.

= Circular orbit in a quadratic central potential
{parent=Circular orbit}
{title2=$r_0^4=h^2/(2km^2)$}

For $V(r)=kmr^2$, fixed nonzero <angular momentum> $h$ gives the <effective potential> $V_{\rm eff}=h^2/(2mr^2)+kmr^2$. A positive-radius circular orbit exists when $k>0$, at the displayed radius, and $V_{\rm eff}''(r_0)=8km>0$ proves radial stability by the <effective potential stability criterion>. Its angular speed has magnitude $\sqrt{2k}$ and its small radial oscillation frequency is $\sqrt{8k}$. If $k\le0$, no such orbit with nonzero angular momentum exists; for $h=0,k>0$, the sole minimum is at the origin, not a positive-radius circular orbit.

= Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector
{title2=$\mathbf A$}
{c}
{parent=Inverse-square force}
{wiki=Laplace–Runge–Lenz_vector}

= Runge-Lenz vector
{c}
{synonym}

For the signed <inverse-square force> $\boldsymbol F=-\kappa\boldsymbol r/r^3$ on a particle of <mass> $m$, with Hamiltonian $H=p^2/(2m)-\kappa/r$, the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> $\boldsymbol A=\boldsymbol p\times\boldsymbol L-m\kappa\widehat{\boldsymbol r}$ is conserved. Attraction has $\kappa>0$ and repulsion has $\kappa<0$. It obeys $\boldsymbol A\cdot\boldsymbol L=0$ and $A^2=m^2\kappa^2+2mHL^2$. For unit <mass> in a <Kepler orbit>, $\kappa=GM$.

= Kepler dynamical symmetry algebra
{c}
{parent=Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector}
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}(4),\quad\mathfrak e(3),\quad\mathfrak{so}(3,1)$}

For $H=p^2/2-\kappa/r$, the <angular momentum> and <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> obey $\{K_i,K_j\}=-2H\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$. On negative or positive energy regions use $K/\sqrt{|2H|}$ to obtain the compact or Lorentz <Lie algebra>, respectively. At zero energy the algebra on the characteristic orbit quotient is the Euclidean motion algebra. Global <group actions> require complete generators or collision regularization; simply replacing $H$ by a constant inside a Hamiltonian-vector-field derivative is not valid on the unreduced energy hypersurface.

= Polar equation of a repulsive inverse-square orbit
{parent=Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector}

Taking the dot product of the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> with $\widehat{\mathbf r}$ gives $A\cos\theta=L^2/r+mk$. Thus
$$
r=\frac{\lambda}{e\cos\theta-1},
\qquad
\lambda=\frac{L^2}{mk},
\qquad
e=\frac{A}{mk}.
$$

= Legendre transform in mechanics
{c}
{parent=Classical mechanics}

For a regular Lagrangian, $p=\partial L/\partial\dot q$ and $H=p\dot q-L$. Regularity means the velocity Hessian is invertible.

= Regular Lagrangian
{parent=Legendre transform in mechanics}
{title2=$\det(L_{v^iv^j})\ne0$}

A <Lagrangian> is regular when its velocity <Hessian matrix> is invertible. The <inverse function theorem> then makes the fiber derivative $p_i=L_{v^i}$ locally invertible, allowing a local <Legendre transform in mechanics> to a <Hamiltonian>. If that fiber derivative is globally invertible, the <Lagrangian> is called a <hyperregular Lagrangian>. Regularity alone does not assert global invertibility, and singular <Lagrangians> may give constrained rather than ordinary unconstrained <Hamilton's equations>.

= Hyperregular Lagrangian
{parent=Regular Lagrangian}
{title2=$\mathbb FL:TQ\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}T^*Q$}

A <Lagrangian> is hyperregular when its fiber derivative, sending a velocity to its conjugate momentum, is a global <diffeomorphism> from the <tangent bundle> to the <cotangent bundle>. This gives a globally defined <Legendre transform in mechanics> and a global equivalence between the <Euler-Lagrange equations> and <Hamilton's equations>. With time dependence this requirement is imposed at each time, with a smooth inverse depending on time.

= Singular Legendre transform of a degree-one velocity Lagrangian
{parent=Legendre transform in mechanics}

For $L=mc|\dot x|-V(x)$,
$$
p=mc\frac{\dot x}{|\dot x|},
\qquad |p|=mc,
\qquad H=V(x).
$$
The momentum loses the speed, so the velocity cannot be recovered from $(x,p)$ and the ordinary inverse Legendre transform does not exist.

= Hamiltonian of a charged particle

For $L=m\dot r^2/2-e\phi+e\dot r\cdot A$, $p=m\dot r+eA$ and $H=|p-eA|^2/(2m)+e\phi$.

= Virial theorem
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{wiki}

For a confined Newtonian gravitational system, $\dot G=2T+V$ for $G=\sum p_i\cdot r_i$, hence $2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle$.

= Virial mean escape speed
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$\langle v_{\rm esc}^2\rangle=4\langle v^2\rangle$}

For an isolated equilibrium of equal-mass points, the <virial theorem> gives $\langle v^2\rangle=-W/M$. A particle's instantaneous <escape velocity> satisfies $v_{\rm esc}^2=-2\Phi$ with zero potential at infinity. Since $W=\tfrac12\sum_i m\Phi_i$, averaging gives $\langle v_{\rm esc}^2\rangle=-4W/M$. These are three-dimensional mean-square speeds, not one-component <velocity dispersions>.

= Virial theorem for a homogeneous pair potential
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$2\langle K\rangle=q\langle W\rangle$}

For a differentiable total pair <potential energy> homogeneous of degree $q$ in all particle positions, the <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> gives $\sum_i\boldsymbol r_i\cdot\nabla_iW=qW$. Since $\boldsymbol F_i=-\nabla_iW$, the force virial is $-qW$. The bounded-motion time-averaged <virial theorem> consequently gives the displayed equation. For $q\ne0$, total energy is $E=(q+2)\langle K\rangle/q$. A positive kinetic-temperature coefficient makes its <heat capacity> negative exactly when $-2<q<0$. For a pair potential proportional to $r^{p+1}$ this means $-3<p<-1$.

= Negative heat capacity of a virialized gravitational system
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$dE/d\Theta=-3Nk_B/2$}

An isolated Newtonian system in <virial equilibrium> has total <energy> $E=K+W=-K$. Along an equilibrium sequence at fixed particle number, define the kinetic <temperature> by $K=3Nk_B\Theta/2$. Its <heat capacity> is therefore negative, as displayed: losing total energy increases the kinetic temperature. A <collisionless stellar system> need not have a thermodynamic temperature; this is a kinetic-temperature statement unless thermal equilibrium is additionally specified. Boundary pressure and external confinement can change the energy relation.

= Radiative energy of virialized gravitational collapse
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$E_{\rm rad}\simeq3M\sigma^2/2$}

For an isolated final <stellar population> with isotropic one-dimensional <velocity dispersion> $\sigma$, kinetic energy is $K=3M\sigma^2/2$. The <virial theorem> gives $W=-2K$ and total energy $E=-K$. Collapse from a diffuse nearly unbound gas reservoir therefore requires removal of approximately $K$ of energy. The radiated fraction can differ if there are outflows, appreciable initial binding energy, or boundary stresses.

= Virial equilibrium
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$2K+W=0$}

A bounded gravitational system in statistical equilibrium satisfies the time-averaged <virial theorem>. For inverse-distance gravity with negligible boundary stresses, $2K+W=0$. This relation does not require static particles or a homogeneous density profile, and reaching it does not erase the system's dynamical history.

= Positive-energy linear diameter growth
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$\liminf_{t\to\infty}R(t)/t\geq\sqrt{E/(2A_1)}$}

For an isolated Newtonian point-mass system, $\ddot I=T+E\geq E>0$. Twice integrating gives a quadratic lower bound on $I$. The <maximum-separation inertia bounds> then give at-least-linear asymptotic growth of the diameter $R$ for a trajectory existing for arbitrarily large times. The bound does not assert instantaneous monotonicity or escape of every particle.

= Pairwise moment-of-inertia identity
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$I=\frac1{2M}\sum_{j<k}m_jm_kr_{jk}^2$}

For positive <masses> in their <centre of mass> frame, $\sum_{j<k}m_jm_k|\boldsymbol r_j-\boldsymbol r_k|^2=M\sum_km_kr_k^2$. Thus the scalar inertia $I=\tfrac12\sum_km_kr_k^2$ equals $(2M)^{-1}\sum_{j<k}m_jm_kr_{jk}^2$. The identity compares total spatial extent with pairwise separations.

= Maximum-separation inertia bounds
{parent=Pairwise moment-of-inertia identity}
{title2=$B_1R^2\leq I\leq A_1R^2$}

Let $R$ be the actual largest pairwise separation of positive <masses> in their <centre of mass> frame. The <pairwise moment-of-inertia identity> gives $m_{(1)}m_{(2)}R^2/(2M)\leq I\leq R^2\sum_{j<k}m_jm_k/(2M)$. Consequently the scalar inertia is comparable to $R^2$, independently of configuration.

= Virial temperature
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$T_{\rm vir}$}

The <virial temperature> characterizes the thermal energy of gas supported in a gravitational potential. It is of order $\mu m_pGM/(k_BR)$, with a structural coefficient determined by the density profile. For a uniform self-gravitating gas sphere the <virial theorem> gives $k_BT=GM\mu m_p/(5R)$; a conventional halo circular-speed definition often uses $k_BT\simeq\mu m_pv_c^2/2$.

= Virial mass contours in a cooling diagram
{parent=Virial temperature}
{title2=$n_H\propto T^3/M^2$}

Combining a virial-temperature relation with the density of a uniform self-gravitating sphere gives $M\propto T^{3/2}n_H^{-1/2}$. Thus contours of constant mass in a density-temperature plane satisfy $n_H\propto T^3/M^2$. Structural coefficients, composition and the distinction between baryonic and total gravitating mass matter when giving numerical labels.

= Fixed-energy virial constraint between steady states
{parent=Virial theorem}
{title2=$T=-E,\quad W=2E$}

Two finite isolated steady gravitational systems at the same conserved energy obey $2T+W=0$ at each endpoint, so both have $T=-E$ and $W=2E$. Redistribution can increase central concentration without making the global potential energy more negative. Escaping matter or an external reservoir requires a different energy accounting.

= Gravitational radius
{title2=$R_g$}
{parent=Virial theorem}
{wiki}

For a self-gravitating system of mass $M$ and <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> $W<0$, its gravitational radius is defined by
$$
R_g=-\frac{GM^2}{W}.
$$
It converts the total binding energy into a characteristic size without assuming a particular density profile.

= Gravitational rms escape and collapse speeds
{parent=Gravitational radius}
{title2=$\langle v_{\rm esc}^2\rangle=4\langle v_{\rm eq}^2\rangle,\quad\langle v_{\rm coll}^2\rangle=2\langle v_{\rm eq}^2\rangle$}

For identical point masses of total mass $M$, let $W=-GM^2/R_g$ exclude self-interactions. The <virial theorem> gives $\langle v_{\rm eq}^2\rangle=GM/R_g$. Since the sum of individual potential energies counts each pair twice, the mass-weighted mean frozen-potential <escape velocity> squared is $-4W/M$. A dissipation-free collapse from zero total <energy> instead has kinetic energy $-W$, hence mean squared speed $-2W/M$. These are three-dimensional speeds relative to the <centre of mass>; a coherent collapse speed is not a local random <velocity dispersion>.

= Adiabatic invariant

{title2=$I$}

= Adiabatic invariance
{synonym}

A quantity conserved to leading order when a parameter varies slowly compared with the natural motion period. The <action variable> of a regular periodic Hamiltonian orbit is a standard example, as explained by <adiabatic invariance of the action>.

= Circular-shell adiabatic contraction
{parent=Adiabatic invariant}
{title2=$r_iM_i(r_i)=r_fM_f(r_f)$}

In a slowly changing spherical <gravitational potential>, <angular momentum> is exactly conserved and the <radial action> is an <adiabatic invariant>. An initially <circular orbit> remains circular in the adiabatic limit, so $J_r=0$ and $L^2=GrM(r)$ give the displayed shell relation. If dark-matter shells do not cross, their enclosed dark mass is also conserved. A density profile alone does not specify this orbital model: eccentric-orbit populations require their full action distribution.

= Inner NFW contraction to a flat rotation curve
{parent=Circular-shell adiabatic contraction}
{title2=$\rho'_D\propto r^{-5/3}$}

An inner <NFW profile> has $M_i(r_i)=Ar_i^2$, where $A=2\pi\rho_s r_s$. If the final total <circular speed> is $V$, <circular-shell adiabatic contraction> gives $r_i=(V^2/(GA))^{1/3}r^{2/3}$ and $M'_D=A^{1/3}(V^2/G)^{2/3}r^{4/3}$. Thus $\rho'_D=A^{1/3}(V^2/G)^{2/3}r^{-5/3}/(3\pi)$. The total density producing the flat curve is $V^2/(4\pi Gr^2)$, so the dark fraction tends to zero as $r^{1/3}$ despite its increase relative to the original cusp.

= Particle-speed invariant between slowly approaching walls
{parent=Adiabatic invariant}
{title2=$v\ell\approx\mathrm{constant}$}

An <elastic collision> with a wall moving toward the particle at speed $V$ changes the particle speed by approximately $2V$ when recoil is negligible. The collision rate is approximately $v/\ell$ for $v\gg V$, and $\dot\ell=-2V$, yielding $\dot v/v=-\dot\ell/\ell$. For a freely moving finite wall of mass $M$, the neglected recoil changes the increment by order $(m/M)v$, so relative accuracy also requires $(m/M)(v/V)\ll1$.

= Hamiltonian system
{c}
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}

A dynamical system on a <symplectic manifold> whose vector field is generated by a <Hamiltonian>. In <canonical variables> it obeys <Hamilton's equations>.

= Inverted quartic oscillator phase portrait
{parent=Hamiltonian system}
{title2=$K=x^2+y^2-y^4$}

The <autonomous planar system> $\dot x=y-2y^3$, $\dot y=-x$ preserves $K$. Its origin is a <center equilibrium> and $(0,\pm1/\sqrt2)$ are <saddle equilibria>. The level $K=1/4$ is $x=\pm(y^2-1/2)$: its two inner arcs are <heteroclinic orbits> enclosing periodic motion, while its outer branches connect saddles to escaping trajectories. Since $|\dot y|\sim y^2$ on escaping levels, infinity is reached in finite time. Adding $\alpha x/2$ to $\dot x$ gives $\dot K=\alpha x^2$, so positive $\alpha$ produces outward crossing of central levels and breaks the equal-energy heteroclinic connections.

= Conformal mechanics
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}

A one-dimensional particle with $H=p^2/2+g/x^2$ admits conserved time-translation, dilation and special-conformal charges. Their <Poisson brackets> form the <sl2R Lie algebra>. The homogeneity of the <inverse-square potential> is what allows the symmetry.

= Special conformal charge of inverse-square mechanics
{parent=Conformal mechanics}
{title2=$K=t^2E-txp+x^2/2$}

For the extended canonical variables with $\{t,E\}=-1$ and $\{x,p\}=1$, the charge $K=t^2E-txp+x^2/2$ generates a special conformal transformation. Its time derivative is $2te(E-H)$, so it is conserved on the <constraint surface>. With a time-dependent parameter $\beta$, the multiplier varies as $\delta e=-2te\beta$ and the action variation is $\dot\beta K-d(\beta x^2/2)/d\lambda$.

= Hamilton-Jacobi equation
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_tS+H(q,\partial_qS,t)=0$}

A principal function $S(q,t)$ obeys the <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> $\partial_tS+H(q,\partial_qS,t)=0$. A complete solution generates <canonical momentum> components $p_j=\partial_jS$ and local canonical coordinates constant along motion. For a time-independent <Hamiltonian>, $S=W(q)-Et$ reduces the equation to $H(q,\partial_qW)=E$. Additive separation of $W$ yields conserved separation constants, subject to local regularity and independence.

= Phase-space action
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}
{title2=$I=\int dt\,(p_I\dot q^I-H)$}

A phase-space <action> treats <canonical variables> as independent fields. Varying them gives <Hamilton's equations>; <Lagrange multipliers> can impose constraints.

= Relativistic particle phase-space action
{parent=Phase-space action}
{title2=$I=\int dt\,[p_m\dot x^m-\tfrac e2(p^2+M^2)]$}

The <worldline einbein> imposes the relativistic <mass-shell condition>. With the <Minkowski metric> of signature $(-,+,\ldots,+)$, its <first-class constraint> is $\varphi=(p^2+M^2)/2$.

= Pseudoclassical spinning particle
{parent=Relativistic particle phase-space action}
{title2=$\mathcal L=\dot x\cdot p+\tfrac i2\zeta\cdot\dot\zeta-\tfrac e2p^2-i\chi p\cdot\zeta$}

A pseudoclassical spinning particle supplements ordinary position and momentum by odd <Grassmann variables>. An even multiplier imposes $p^2=0$ and an odd multiplier imposes $p\cdot\zeta=0$. The odd symplectic term gives $\{\zeta^m,\zeta^n\}_{D}=-i\eta^{mn}$. Quantization represents these variables by a <Clifford algebra>, producing a spinor wavefunction.

= Spinning-particle Dirac quantization
{parent=Pseudoclassical spinning particle}
{title2=$\widehat\zeta^m=\gamma^m/\sqrt2$}

Quantizing the odd <Dirac brackets> yields $\{\widehat\zeta^m,\widehat\zeta^n\}_+=\eta^{mn}$. The <constraint> $p\cdot\zeta=0$ is then the <massless Dirac equation> on a spinor. Its square enforces $p^2=0$, matching the even <constraint>. The spin bilinear is represented by $-i[\gamma^m,\gamma^n]/4$ in this action convention.

= Spin from Grassmann bilinears
{parent=Pseudoclassical spinning particle}
{title2=$S^{mn}=-i\zeta^m\zeta^n$}

The odd particle variables form an even antisymmetric tensor through their bilinear. Adding it to orbital angular momentum gives a conserved Lorentz <Noether charge>. The particle and odd-variable equations make the two time derivatives cancel. After <spinning-particle Dirac quantization>, the bilinear becomes the spinor Lorentz generator rather than an ordinary commuting classical spin vector.

= Light-cone Hamiltonian
{parent=Relativistic particle phase-space action}
{title2=$H=(p_i^2+\mu^2)/(2p^+)$}

With $ds^2=-2dx^+dx^-+dx_i^2$, $x^+=t$, and $p_-\ne0$, the <mass-shell condition> gives $H=-p_+=-(p_i^2+\mu^2)/(2p_-)$. In the future-directed sector $p^+=-p_->0$, this is $(p_i^2+\mu^2)/(2p^+)$. Its <Schrodinger equation> is equivalent to the <Klein-Gordon equation> after multiplication by $2p_-$.

= Worldline einbein
{parent=Relativistic particle phase-space action}
{title2=$e(t)$}

An einbein specifies a one-dimensional <metric tensor> along a <worldline>. In the <relativistic particle phase-space action> it is the <Lagrange multiplier> for the <mass-shell condition> and transforms as $\delta e=\dot\epsilon$ under the corresponding canonical <gauge transformation>. After eliminating the <momentum>, its geometric interpretation depends on the normalization of the particle <action>.

= Proper-time gauge for a massive worldline einbein
{parent=Worldline einbein}
{title2=$ds=e\,d\lambda$}

For the <relativistic particle> action $S=(m/2)\int[g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu/e-e]d\lambda$, varying the <worldline einbein> gives $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu=-e^2$. On the positive-einbein branch the <proper time> satisfies $ds=e\,d\lambda$. A <reparametrization> with $\lambda'=s$ sets the transformed einbein to one, making the <geodesic equation> affinely parametrized. This gauge choice is made after retaining the einbein <mass shell> constraint; fixing the action first would discard that equation. If the parameter interval is held fixed at both ends, its integrated <proper length> is a modulus and cannot be arbitrarily changed by endpoint-preserving transformations.

= Proper-time modulus
{parent=Worldline einbein}
{title2=$s$}

The constant mode of the <worldline einbein> is not removed by <gauge transformations> whose parameters vanish at the ends of a fixed interval. This Schwinger proper-time parameter remains integrated over after <gauge fixing>. For $\varphi=(p^2+M^2)/2$, the geometric on-shell proper length is $M\int e\,dt$.

= Worldline gauge-orbit determinant
{parent=Proper-time modulus}
{title2=$\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}=\det{}^{\prime}\partial_t$}

On a unit interval with gauge parameters vanishing at its ends, $e=s+\dot\epsilon$ separates the <worldline einbein> into its invariant average $s$ and a <canonical gauge transformation>. The Jacobian for nonconstant modes is $\det\partial_t$ between the appropriate boundary-condition spaces. On a circle its prime excludes the constant gauge-parameter zero mode. This <Faddeev-Popov determinant> can be represented by anticommuting $b,c$ fields with an action proportional to $\int b\dot c\,dt$.

= Hamiltonian flow

Hamiltonian flow is the phase-space evolution generated by <Hamilton's equations> for a fixed <Hamiltonian>.

= Isotropic harmonic oscillator flow
{parent=Hamiltonian flow}
{title2=$S_t=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t\,I&(\sin\omega t/\omega)I\\-\omega\sin\omega t\,I&\cos\omega t\,I\end{pmatrix}$}

For $H_\omega=(|v|^2+\omega^2|x|^2)/2$, the displayed linear <Hamiltonian flow> solves $\dot X=V$, $\dot V=-\omega^2X$. Its inverse is $S_{-t}$, its determinant is one and it preserves $H_\omega$. The zero-frequency limit is the shear $(x,v)\mapsto(x+tv,v)$.

= Uniform support bound from a coercive conserved energy
{parent=Hamiltonian flow}
{title2=$\operatorname{supp}f_t\subseteq\{H\leq E_*\}$}

For homogeneous <Hamiltonian Liouville equation> transport with autonomous $H$, compact initial support remains in the fixed energy sublevel determined by its maximum initial energy. If these sublevels are compact, this proves uniform compact support without solving the flow. The harmonic energy $H_\omega=(|v|^2+\omega^2|x|^2)/2$ has this property exactly when $\omega\ne0$; at zero frequency spatial free streaming can escape every compact set.

= Global characteristic flow for a Hamiltonian with bounded Hessian
{parent=Hamiltonian flow}
{title2=$\|D_z^2H(t,z)\|\leq L_T$}

If $H$ is $C^2$ and its spatial <Hessian matrix> is uniformly bounded on every finite time interval, its <Hamiltonian vector field> is globally <Lipschitz continuous> in <phase space> with at most linear growth. The <Picard-Lindelof theorem> and <Gronwall inequality> give a unique <Hamiltonian flow> for all finite forward and backward times. Bounds on $\nabla_zH(t,0)$ follow from time continuity on compact intervals.

= Quadratic rotational Lie-Poisson dynamics
{parent=Hamiltonian flow}
{title2=$\{x_i,x_j\}=-\epsilon_{ijk}x_k,\quad H=Ax_1^2+Bx_2^2+Cx_3^2$}

With the displayed signed <Lie-Poisson bracket> and evolution $\dot F=\{F,H\}$, the equations are $\dot x_1=2(C-B)x_2x_3$, $\dot x_2=2(A-C)x_3x_1$, and $\dot x_3=2(B-A)x_1x_2$. The energy and the <Casimir function of a Poisson manifold> $x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2$ are conserved. For $A=1/(2I_1)$, $B=1/(2I_2)$, $C=1/(2I_3)$ with positive inertias, these are the <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body>. Reversing the bracket while fixing $H$ reverses time orientation.

= Integral of motion
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}
{wiki}

= Integrals of motion
{synonym}

An integral of motion is a function on <phase space> that remains constant along every trajectory. If it has no explicit time dependence, $I(q,p)$ is an integral exactly when its <Poisson bracket> with the <Hamiltonian> vanishes: $\{I,H\}=0$.

= Jacobi integral of a rigidly rotating potential
{c}
{parent=Integral of motion}
{title2=$E_J=E-\Omega L_z$}

For $\Phi(R,\phi-\Omega t,z)$ with constant pattern angular velocity $\Omega$, ordinary specific <energy> satisfies $\dot E=\partial_t\Phi=-\Omega\partial_\phi\Phi$ and specific axial <angular momentum> satisfies $\dot L_z=-\partial_\phi\Phi$. Their combination $E_J$ is conserved. This is the rotating-frame <integral of motion>; in the <circular restricted three-body problem>, the usual <Jacobi constant> is an affine rescaling of it. Thus time dependence in an inertial frame does not alone imply the absence of all integrals.

= Isolating integral
{parent=Integral of motion}

An isolating integral is a regular single-valued <integral of motion> whose fixed value restricts accessible <phase space> to a lower-dimensional level set. Functional independence of several such integrals means their differentials are linearly independent on the region considered. A central potential has <specific orbital energy> and the three components of <specific angular momentum> as four generically independent integrals. A <Kepler orbit> has a fifth independent integral supplied by its fixed apsidal direction, encoded by the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector>; its vector components obey two constraints with energy and angular momentum. Circular and radial orbits are degenerate cases of this count.

= Isolating integrals and orbital closure dimension
{parent=Isolating integral}
{title2=$N=6-d$}

In three-dimensional autonomous mechanics, a regular common level set of $N$ independent <isolating integrals> has dimension $6-N$ in <phase space>. If an <orbit> is dense in a $d$-dimensional <invariant torus> and the integrals characterize its closure, $N=6-d$. A generic three-frequency regular <orbit> has three integrals; a spherical nonclosed rosette has energy and three components of <specific angular momentum>, with two-dimensional closure and four integrals. A periodic nonstationary <orbit> has one-dimensional closure and can be isolated by five independent <orbit> labels on the invariant family. The maximum is five wherever the <Hamiltonian vector field> is nonzero, since all integral differentials annihilate that field. A single resonant <orbit> need not yield an additional smooth single-valued integral on an open neighborhood; global superintegrability is stronger than orbital closure.

= Canonical momentum sign in the affine particle action
{parent=Canonical momentum}
{title2=$P_\mu^c=-m g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu$}

For the affine action $S=-\tfrac m2\int g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu d\tau$ with signature $(-+++)$, differentiating with respect to velocity gives the displayed <canonical momentum>. Future-oriented proper time in a conformally flat metric yields $P_i^c=-a\gamma mv_i$, whereas the conventional future-directed mechanical momentum has $q_i=a\gamma mv_i$. = Boundary-term shift of canonical field momenta
{parent=Canonical momentum}

If the spatially integrated <Lagrangian> changes by $dB[\Phi]/dt$, where $B$ depends on fields and their spatial derivatives but not velocities, the <canonical momentum> components change by $\pi_a\mapsto\pi_a+\delta B/\delta\Phi_a$. This follows by varying $dB/dt$ and using spatial <integration by parts>. The canonical one-form changes by the exact variation $\delta B$, so the <symplectic form> and <canonical commutation relations> are preserved. Boundary flux must vanish for this spatial functional calculation.

= Dirac bracket

{c}

Second-class constraints $\chi_a$ with invertible bracket matrix can be eliminated with a modified bracket. \{F,\{G,H\}\}+\{G,\{H,F\}\}+\{H,\{F,G\}\}=0.
$$

= Poisson bracket of conserved quantities
{parent=Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket}

If $F$ and $G$ have no explicit time dependence and satisfy $\{F,H\}=\{G,H\}=0$, the Jacobi identity gives $\{\{F,G\},H\}=0$. Their Poisson bracket is therefore also conserved.

= Hamiltonian conservation law
{parent=Poisson bracket}

Along a trajectory generated by the <Hamiltonian> $H$,
$$
\frac{dF}{dt}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+\{F,H\}.
$$
Thus a time-independent observable is conserved exactly when its <Poisson bracket> with $H$ vanishes.

= Hamiltonian energy balance
{c}
{parent=Hamiltonian conservation law}
{title2=$\frac d{dt}H(t,X,V)=\partial_tH(t,X,V)$}

Along <Hamilton's equations>, spatial derivatives of the <Hamiltonian function> cancel in its total time derivative. Thus $dH/dt=\partial_tH$. An autonomous <Hamiltonian function> is conserved; explicit time dependence generally prevents conservation even though <phase space> is still preserved.

= Energy balance for an autonomous Lagrangian
{parent=Hamiltonian conservation law}
{title2=$\dot H=-\dot x\,\delta A/\delta x$}

For an autonomous <Lagrangian> $L(x,\dot x)$, define its <action> $A=\int L\,dt$ and <Hamiltonian> $H=\dot xL_{\dot x}-L$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\dot H=\dot x\left(\frac d{dt}L_{\dot x}-L_x\right)
=-\dot x\frac{\delta A}{\delta x}.
$$
Thus the identity holds along forced trajectories as well as solutions of the homogeneous <Euler-Lagrange equation>. Explicit time dependence adds $-\partial_tL$; stochastic work requires a consistent regularization or the <Stratonovich chain rule>.

= Phase-space dilation generator
{parent=Poisson bracket}

The function $D=q\cdot p$ generates the canonical scaling $(q,p)\mapsto(e^tq,e^{-t}p)$. An observable $F$ is invariant under this scaling exactly when
$$
\{F,D\}=q\cdot\nabla_qF-p\cdot\nabla_pF=0.
$$

= Poisson bracket in complex canonical coordinates
{parent=Poisson bracket}

For $z_j=q_j+ip_j$ and $\bar z_j=q_j-ip_j$,
$$
\{f,g\}
=-2i\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bar z_j}
+2i\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_j}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar z_j}.
$$
This follows from $\partial_{q_j}=\partial_{z_j}+\partial_{\bar z_j}$ and $\partial_{p_j}=i\partial_{z_j}-i\partial_{\bar z_j}$.

= Anti-Hermitian quadratic Hamiltonian
{parent=Poisson bracket in complex canonical coordinates}

For an anti-Hermitian matrix $A$, the real quadratic Hamiltonian
$$
I_A=\frac1{2i}\bar z^TAz
$$
satisfies
$$
\{I_A,I_B\}=-I_{[A,B]}.
$$
Thus $A\mapsto-I_A$ realizes the matrix commutator as a Poisson bracket; it is the quadratic moment-map construction for the unitary action.

= Variational derivative
{parent=Hamiltonian mechanics}

For a functional $H[u]$, its variational derivative is defined by
$$
\left.\frac d{d\epsilon}H[u+\epsilon v]\right|_{\epsilon=0}
=\int\frac{\delta H}{\delta u}v\,dx
$$
after integrating derivatives of $v$ by parts and discarding the boundary terms.

i\psi_t=\frac{\delta H}{\delta\overline\psi}
$$
is accompanied by its complex conjugate equation.

A canonical transformation preserves the symplectic form and hence all Poisson brackets. In one degree of freedom, an invertible map $(q,p)\mapsto(Q,P)$ is canonical exactly when $\{Q,P\}_{q,p}=1$.

= Exact quadratic normal form for a perturbed linear rotor
{parent=Canonical transformation}
{title2=$H\circ\Phi=E+\omega\cdot y'+\tfrac12Q+\epsilon^2\widetilde V$}

For $H=\omega\cdot y+\frac12|y|^2+\epsilon V(x)$ with a <Diophantine frequency vector>, solve $\mathcal D_\omega u=\langle V\rangle-V$ and apply <translated conjugacy of a Diophantine vector field> to $w=\epsilon\nabla u$. Set $\eta=\epsilon\nabla u+a$ and use the <symplectic cotangent lift with a closed momentum shift>. The transformed <Hamiltonian> is exactly $E+\omega\cdot y'+\frac12y'^T D\chi^{-1}D\chi^{-T}y'+\frac12|\eta\circ\chi|^2$, where $E=\epsilon\langle V\rangle+\omega\cdot a$. Since $a=O(\epsilon^2)$, the last term is an angular potential of order $\epsilon^2$ and the quadratic coefficient is identity plus $O(\epsilon)$. The construction is on a smaller angular strip and momentum ball.

= Canonical completion of independent commuting functions
{parent=Canonical transformation}
{title2=$\{Q_i,Q_j\}=0$}

Independent functions $Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ on a $2n$-dimensional <symplectic manifold> can locally form the position half of canonical coordinates exactly when $\{Q_i,Q_j\}=0$ throughout a neighbourhood. Their commuting <Hamiltonian vector fields> span the fibres of $Q$. Flow from a transverse section gives coordinates $(Q,t)$ with $\omega=\sum dt_i\wedge dQ_i+\beta(Q)$. Write $\beta=d\alpha$ locally and take $P_i=-t_i-\alpha_i(Q)$. Then $\omega=\sum dQ_i\wedge dP_i$.

= Superintegrable Hamiltonian system
{parent=Integrable Hamiltonian system}

A <superintegrable Hamiltonian system> with $n$ degrees of freedom has more than $n$ functionally independent <integrals of motion>, including an integrable set of $n$ in involution. At a regular nonzero <Hamiltonian vector field>, at most $2n-1$ independent integral differentials can annihilate the flow. A maximally superintegrable system attains that bound. The three-dimensional <Kepler orbit> has five independent integrals through energy, angular momentum and the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector>. = Integrals of an irrational two-frequency oscillator

On the regular set of two uncoupled oscillators with irrational frequency ratio, every continuous globally defined <integral of motion> is constant on each fixed-action <flat torus>, because every orbit is dense there. Local angle combinations can still give extra local integrals; they fail to be single-valued on the torus.

= Stäckel potential
{parent=Integrable Hamiltonian system}
{c}
{title2=$\psi=\frac{G(\lambda)-G(\mu)}{\lambda-\mu}$}

A <Stäckel potential> allows additive separation of the <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> in an orthogonal coordinate system. In axisymmetric <prolate spheroidal coordinates>, the relative-potential form is $\psi=[G(\lambda)-G(\mu)]/(\lambda-\mu)$. It gives <energy>, axial <angular momentum> and an independent third separation <integral>. Here $G$ is a one-variable generating function, not the gravitational constant. Generic bounded <orbits> are regular invariant-torus motions; resonance and coordinate degeneracy require separate treatment.

= Orbit volume in an axisymmetric Stäckel potential
{parent=Stäckel potential}
{title2=$p_\tau^2\geq0$}

For fixed $E,l,I$, separated motion obeys $p_\tau^2=[E\tau+G(\tau)-I+(\alpha-\beta)l^2/(2(\tau+\alpha))]/[2(\tau+\alpha)(\tau+\beta)]$. Allowed intervals are where this is nonnegative, with turning points and coordinate-fold endpoints treated separately. A generic nonresonant bound <orbit> with $l\ne0$ circulates azimuthally and densely samples a projected <volume> bounded by confocal spheroids and hyperboloids. It is not uniformly distributed in <volume>. Resonant or zero-angular-momentum <orbits> can project to lower-dimensional regions; integrability alone does not imply that every <orbit> fills a three-dimensional shell.

= Axisymmetric Stäckel third integral
{parent=Stäckel potential}
{title2=$I$}

For $H=\tfrac12\sum p_j^2/h_j^2-\psi$, $l=p_\phi$ and a <Stäckel potential>, multiplying the separated <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> by $\lambda-\mu$ gives $2(\tau+\alpha)(\tau+\beta)p_\tau^2-(\alpha-\beta)l^2/[2(\tau+\alpha)]-G(\tau)-E\tau=-I$ for both $\tau=\lambda,\mu$. Eliminating $E$ gives $I=\tfrac12[\mu v_\lambda^2+\lambda v_\mu^2+(\lambda+\mu+\alpha)v_\phi^2]-[\mu G(\lambda)-\lambda G(\mu)]/(\lambda-\mu)$. Physical components are $v_j=h_j\dot q_j$ and $p_j=h_jv_j$. Separation proves conservation locally; direct <Poisson brackets> extend it across regular coordinate patches.

= First integrals in involution
{parent=Integrable Hamiltonian system}

First integrals are in involution when every pair has zero <Poisson bracket>. An $n$-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system is <Liouville integrable> when it has $n$ almost-everywhere independent first integrals in involution.

= Invariant torus
{parent=Integrable Hamiltonian system}

An invariant torus is a subset homeomorphic to a <torus> and preserved by a <flow map>[flow]. In <action-angle variables>, an integrable Hamiltonian flow moves linearly in the angular coordinates of each regular invariant torus.

The torus is a particular <invariant manifold>; its toroidal topology is an extra condition beyond invariance.

A quasiperiodic orbit on a torus has angular coordinates $\theta(t)=\theta(0)+\omega t$ modulo $2\pi$. It is <periodic orbit>[periodic] exactly when the nonzero frequencies are commensurate; rationally independent frequencies produce a dense orbit.

= Incommensurate harmonic-oscillator orbit

For two uncoupled <harmonic oscillators> with nonzero amplitudes and irrational frequency ratio $\omega_1/\omega_2$, the orbit is bounded but not <periodic orbit>[periodic]. = Direct product of integrable Hamiltonian systems
{parent=Integrable Hamiltonian system}

The direct product of integrable Hamiltonian systems is integrable. Pull each family's commuting <integrals of motion> back to the product <phase space>. Integrals from different factors have zero <Poisson bracket> because they use disjoint canonical coordinates, and their <total differential>[differentials] are independent wherever the differentials within each factor are independent.

= Liouville integrability of the isotropic harmonic oscillator

For

the functions $I_j=(q_j^2+p_j^2)/2$ are independent almost everywhere, Poisson commute, and sum to $H$. A regular common level $I_j=c_j>0$ is the product of circles of radii $\sqrt{2c_j}$, hence an invariant $n$-torus. Allowing some $c_j=0$ gives lower-dimensional singular tori.

= Delaunay variables
{c}
{parent=Action-angle variables}
{title2=$(\mathcal L,\mathcal G,\mathcal H;\ell,\omega,\Omega)$}

For a bound noncollision <Kepler orbit> with gravitational parameter $\mu$, the canonical actions per unit mass are $\mathcal L=\sqrt{\mu a}$, $\mathcal G=\mathcal L\sqrt{1-e^2}$ and $\mathcal H=\mathcal G\cos i$. Their angles are <mean anomaly> $\ell$, <argument of periapsis> $\omega$ and <longitude of ascending node> $\Omega$. The <Hamiltonian> is $-\mu^2/(2\mathcal L^2)$, so only $\ell$ advances in the unperturbed problem. In the prograde spherical-action chart, $J_r=\mathcal L-\mathcal G$, $J_\theta=\mathcal G-\mathcal H$, $J_\phi=\mathcal H$; the type-two <canonical transformation> is generated by $F_2=(\mathcal L-\mathcal G)w_r+(\mathcal G-\mathcal H)w_\theta+\mathcal H w_\phi$. Retrograde spherical actions have $J_\theta=\mathcal G-|\mathcal H|$, so the absolute value cannot be dropped globally. Circular and coplanar limits are singular angle charts, not singular orbits.

= Action integral
{parent=Action-angle variables}
{title2=$I_j=\frac1{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_j}\lambda$}

Near a regular <Lagrangian torus>, choose a one-form $\lambda$ with $d\lambda=-\omega$ and a basis of fibre cycles $\gamma_j$. The action $I_j=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{\gamma_j}\lambda$ has <differential of a smooth map> $dI_j=(2\pi)^{-1}\sum_i t_i^{(j)}dF_i$, where $t^{(j)}$ is the corresponding joint-flow period. Nonsingularity of the period matrix makes these actions local coordinates transverse to the tori.

= Action variable
{parent=Action-angle variables}

With angle period $2\pi$, the action is the enclosed phase-space area divided by $2\pi$:
$$I=(2\pi)^{-1}\oint p\,dq.$$

This is the action component of <action-angle variables>, rather than the entire coordinate construction.

= Radial action of a bound Kepler orbit
{c}
{parent=Action variable}
{title2=$I_r=k/\sqrt{-2E}-|\ell|$}

For $H=p_r^2/2+\ell^2/(2r^2)-k/r$, a noncollision bound orbit has $\ell\ne0$ and $-k^2/(2\ell^2)\leq E<0$. Its radial action is $\sqrt{-2E}/\pi$ times $\int_\alpha^\beta\sqrt{(r-\alpha)(\beta-r)}\,dr/r$, where $\alpha,\beta=[k\mp\sqrt{k^2+2E\ell^2}]/(-2E)$. Evaluating the integral gives the displayed action and $H=-k^2/[2(I_r+|\ell|)^2]$. The angular and radial frequencies have equal magnitudes, explaining closed orbits rather than generic quasiperiodicity. The circular limit has $I_r=0$; zero angular momentum gives collision in the unregularized phase space.

= Adiabatic invariance of the action

When a Hamiltonian parameter varies slowly compared with the orbital period and no separatrix is crossed, the action changes only at higher adiabatic order.

The action is an <adiabatic invariant> under these assumptions; other slowly varying systems can have different adiabatic invariants.

= Adiabatic particle between moving parallel walls

For a one-dimensional particle of energy $E$ between walls separated by $L$, the unnormalized action is
$$

Adiabatic conservation gives $E(t)L(t)^2=\text{constant}$.

= Adiabatic invariance of an orbital action

When a gravitational potential changes slowly relative to an orbital period, the orbital actions remain constant provided no separatrix is crossed. In a spherical potential, angular momentum is conserved exactly and the radial action is adiabatically conserved.

= Adiabatic actions do not preserve orbital closure

<Adiabatic invariance of an orbital action> preserves action values during slow regular changes, but frequencies $\partial H/\partial J_i$ can vary. Closure requires rational frequency ratios, which need not persist. A slowly added harmonic component in a <Kepler orbit> potential produces generic apsidal precession.

= Simple harmonic oscillator
{synonym}

= Harmonic oscillator
{synonym}

<Simple harmonic motion> is motion in which the <acceleration> is proportional to the <displacement> from <equilibrium> and points back towards it:
$$
\ddot x=-\omega^2x,\qquad \omega>0.
$$
Here $x$ is measured from <equilibrium>, and $\omega$ is the constant <angular frequency>. For a <point mass> attached to an ideal <spring>, <Hooke's law> gives the restoring <force> $F=-kx$, with stiffness $k>0$, and <Newton's second law> yields $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. The minus sign matters: for $x>0$ the <force> is negative, and for $x<0$ it is positive. A restoring <force> need not oppose <velocity>; on the return towards <equilibrium> it increases <speed>.

The general solution is
$$
x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\varphi),\qquad
v(t)=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\varphi),\qquad
P=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}.
$$
The <amplitude> $A\geq0$ and <oscillation phase> $\varphi$ encode the <initial conditions>. We use $P$ for the <oscillation period> to distinguish it from <kinetic energy> $T$. For $A>0$, the <oscillation period> is independent of <amplitude>, the property of <isochronous harmonic motion>. At $A=0$ the body remains at <equilibrium>. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter23.pdf][MIT mechanics, Chapter 23, sections 23.2 and 23.3]{ref}

Choose $m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$, $k=2\,\mathrm{N/m}$, and release the <mass> from rest at <displacement> $A=0.3\,\mathrm m$. Then $\omega=2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $P=\pi\,\mathrm s$, and $\varphi=0$. Writing <time> $t$ in seconds,
$$
x(t)=0.3\cos(2t)\,\mathrm m,\qquad
v(t)=-0.6\sin(2t)\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$
At $t=0$ the <velocity> vanishes, but the <acceleration> is $-1.2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$: a <turning point> is not a state of rest that persists. At $t=\pi/4\,\mathrm s$, the body crosses <equilibrium> with <velocity> $-0.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and zero <acceleration>. At $t=\pi/2\,\mathrm s$ it reaches the opposite <turning point>, $x=-0.3\,\mathrm m$.

Taking <potential energy> zero at <equilibrium>, the same solution gives
$$
V(t)=\frac12kx(t)^2=0.09\cos^2(2t)\,\mathrm J,\qquad
T(t)=\frac12mv(t)^2=0.09\sin^2(2t)\,\mathrm J,
\qquad E=T+V=0.09\,\mathrm J.
$$
At each <turning point> all <mechanical energy> is <potential energy>; at each <equilibrium> crossing it is <kinetic energy>. Both repeat twice during one complete <position> cycle because squaring removes the sign. At $t=\pi/8\,\mathrm s$, for example, each contributes $0.045\,\mathrm J$.

\Image[mechanical-energy-oscillation.png]
{title=Position, velocity, phase ellipse and energy exchange for a mass of 0.5 kg on a spring of stiffness 2 N/m}

The physical <position> trace covers a line segment, $-0.3\leq x\leq0.3$ in metres. In the <phase plane>, the paired <position> and <velocity> instead trace the <ellipse>
$$
\frac{x^2}{(0.3\,\mathrm m)^2}+\frac{v^2}{(0.6\,\mathrm{m/s})^2}=1.
$$
This is precisely $mv^2/2+kx^2/2=E$, rewritten: the single conserved <mechanical energy> fixes the <amplitude> and the whole <ellipse>. It does not fix the starting <oscillation phase>. At $x=0$ both $v=-0.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $v=+0.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ lie on it; <position> alone cannot distinguish them. Replacing $v$ by <momentum> $p=mv$ gives the equivalent <phase-space trajectory>. The arrows show increasing <time>, clockwise in $(x,v)$ coordinates.

This closed <ellipse> assumes a time-independent <spring> and no <damping> or applied drive. Adding a resisting <force> $-bv$, $b>0$, gives $m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$ and $dE/dt=-bv^2\leq0$. An underdamped <damped harmonic oscillator> spirals inward in the <phase plane>, crossing successively smaller <energy> contours. The ideal <harmonic oscillator> is also useful beyond <springs>: near a smooth <potential energy> minimum with $V''(0)>0$, its <Taylor expansion> begins with $V(0)+V''(0)x^2/2$. For sufficiently small <displacements>, this gives the same restoring <force> to leading order, explaining why <small oscillations> share one solvable pattern.

= Radial orbit time transformation
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{title2=$d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$}

For a central-force radial <energy> equation, choose a monotone radius $q=q(r)$ and clock $\tau$ such that $d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$. Then $q'=(r/q)\dot r$, and multiplying the old <energy> equation by $r^2/q^2$ preserves the centrifugal form $L^2/(2q^2)$. A split of the remaining terms into constant <energy> and a new <Newtonian gravitational potential> produces a transformed radial orbit.

= Angular reconstruction of a radial orbit transformation
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$d\bar\theta/d\tau=L/q^2$}

A transformed radial equation containing $L^2/(2q^2)$ becomes a full planar central-force orbit by choosing $d\bar\theta/d\tau=L/q^2$. For $d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$ and old <angular velocity> $L/r^2$, this gives $d\bar\theta/d\theta=(r/q)dq/dr$. Keeping the old angle generally fails to preserve the stated transformed <angular momentum>.

= Kepler–isochrone radial transformation
{c}
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$r=\sqrt{\bar E/(\lambda E)}(\sqrt{\bar r^2+b^2}-b)$}

With $a=(1-\lambda)GM$, $h=\lambda E>0$ and $\bar E>0$, set $\bar E q^2=hr^2+ar$. If $a\geq0$, its positive-radius root is $r=\sqrt{\bar E/h}(\sqrt{q^2+b^2}-b)$, with $b=a/(2\sqrt{h\bar E})$. The transformed <Newtonian gravitational potential> is an additive constant plus $-G\bar M/(b+\sqrt{q^2+b^2})$, the <spherical isochrone model>. The <mass> sign and branch conditions must be checked.

= Attractive branch of the Kepler–isochrone transformation
{parent=Kepler–isochrone radial transformation}

For bound old <Kepler orbits>, $E<0$, $\lambda<0$ and $\bar E>0$ give a positive radius, positive isochrone scale $b$, and positive transformed <mass>. The shifted <energy> is $\bar E-k=\bar E/\lambda<0$. For unbound old orbits with $E>0$, $1/2<\lambda\leq1$ gives an attractive unbound isochrone branch. The case $\lambda=0$ is handled by the separate <Kepler–harmonic radial duality>.

= Kepler–harmonic radial duality
{c}
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$r=(\bar E/GM)\bar r^2$}

Taking $\bar E=GM\,r/q^2>0$ in a <radial orbit time transformation> gives $r=(\bar E/GM)q^2$ and $\bar\Phi(q)=-E(\bar E/GM)^2q^2$. Bound <Kepler orbits> with $E<0$ map to a confining <harmonic oscillator>; positive <energy> maps to an inverted oscillator. Full angular reconstruction halves the original orbital angle.

= Two-body problem
{parent=Celestial mechanics}

The two-body problem determines the motion of two particles interacting only with one another. For a central force it separates into uniform centre-of-mass motion and one relative-coordinate problem with the reduced mass.

= Equal-mass circular binary
{parent=Two-body problem}
{title2=$\omega^2=Gm/(4a^3)$}

Two equal point <masses> can move around the same fixed circle of radius $a$ in diametrically opposite positions. Their stationary <centre of mass> is the circle's center. A separation $2a$ gives gravitational <force> $Gm^2/(4a^2)$ on each <mass>, and <Newton's second law> equates it to $ma\omega^2$. The common <angular speed> is therefore fixed by the displayed relation. Opposite tangential <velocities> produce the same sense of angular rotation.

= Negative-energy gravitational collision
{parent=Two-body problem}
{title2=$t_{\mathrm{coll}}=\pi\sqrt{a^3/[8G(m_1+m_2)]}$}

Two point <masses> released from rest at separation $a$ have negative <mechanical energy> $-Gm_1m_2/a$ but collide in time $\pi\sqrt{a^3/[8G(m_1+m_2)]}$. Their separation approaches zero along a regular trajectory before collision. The <energy> sign alone therefore does not supply a positive lower separation bound.

= Kepler velocity hodograph
{c}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$\boldsymbol v=(\mu_g/h)(-\sin f,\cos f+e)$}

In axes directed along the <periapsis> and transverse to it, a <Kepler orbit> with gravitational parameter $\mu_g$ and <specific angular momentum> $h$ has the displayed <velocity>. The <velocity> tip traces a circle of radius $\mu_g/h$ centred at $(0,e\mu_g/h)$. To derive it, differentiate $r=h^2/[\mu_g(1+e\cos f)]$, use $r^2\dot f=h$, and resolve $\dot r\widehat{\boldsymbol r}+r\dot f\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}$. A rotation of the spatial axes rotates the entire hodograph by the same angle.

= Apsidal speed sum for an attractive inverse-square orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$v_{\min}+v_{\max}=2Q/h$}

For unit mass with <inverse-square potential> $-Q/r$, $Q>0$, and positive conserved <angular momentum> $h$, a bounded orbit has $-Q^2/(2h^2)\le E<0$. Its <periapsis distance> and <apoapsis distance> are roots of $|E|r^2-Qr+h^2/2=0$, so their sum is $Q/|E|$ and product $h^2/(2|E|)$. Speed is largest at the <periapsis> and smallest at the <apoapsis>, because $v^2=2(E+Q/r)$ decreases with $r$. Radial velocity is zero there, so the two speeds are $h/r_{\min}$ and $h/r_{\max}$. Their sum is $h(r_{\min}+r_{\max})/(r_{\min}r_{\max})=2Q/h$, independent of $E$, including the circular limit.

= Velocity kick on a circular Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$a'/a=(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)^{-1}$}

For an instantaneous in-plane change of <velocity> of magnitude $\gamma v_K$ on a <circular orbit>, with angle $\theta$ measured from the forward tangential direction, the new <specific orbital energy> gives $a'/a=(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)^{-1}$. The <specific angular momentum> gives $e'^2=1-(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)(1+\gamma\cos\theta)^2$. The signed <semi-major axis> becomes negative for an unbound <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>.

= Kick-orbit distribution
{parent=Velocity kick on a circular Kepler orbit}
{title2=$f_c(c)=1/(\pi\sqrt{1-c^2})$}

Uniform planar kick angle $\theta$ gives $c=\cos\theta$ with probability density $1/(\pi\sqrt{1-c^2})$. This maps onto a one-dimensional curve in <orbital eccentricity>–<semi-major axis> space. The two radial-kick signs share that curve but have opposite tangential components of the <eccentricity vector>. The resulting density is enhanced near tangential-kick endpoints.

= Kepler radial energy equation
{c}
{parent=Kepler orbit}

For unit <mass> in $\Phi=-GM/r$, <conservation of angular momentum> splits the <velocity> norm into $\dot r^2+L^2/r^2$. <Conservation of energy> then gives $\tfrac12\dot r^2+L^2/(2r^2)=E+GM/r$. It separates radial dynamics from the reconstruction of the orbital angle.

In the centre-of-mass frame of two Newtonian point masses, total orbital energy is reduced mass times <specific orbital energy>: $E=\mu[|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2-GM/r]$. The factor $\mu$ multiplies both kinetic and potential contributions, while $GM$ is the relative gravitational parameter.

= Polar equation of a Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}

For an attractive <inverse-square force>, $r=p/(1+e\cos f)$, where $f$ is <true anomaly>, $e$ is <orbital eccentricity>, and $p=h^2/\mu$ is the semilatus rectum. The <specific angular momentum> relation $\dot f=h/r^2$ converts this geometric equation into an angular evolution law.

= Circumplanetary orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}

An <orbit> about a <planet>. Stellar tides, <radiation pressure> and other perturbations can limit its stability; a sufficiently small <semi-major axis> compared with the <Hill sphere> permits a local approximately planet-centred <Kepler orbit>.

= Planet-synchronous orbit
{parent=Circumplanetary orbit}
{title2=$T_{\rm orb}=P_{\rm spin}$}

= Synchronous circumplanetary orbit
{synonym}

A <circumplanetary orbit> whose <orbital period> equals the central planet's spin period. Its <semi-major axis> follows from <Kepler's third law>. Matching periods alone does not fix the satellite above one ground location; that additionally requires a <planet-stationary orbit>.

= Planet-stationary orbit
{parent=Planet-synchronous orbit}

= Stationary circumplanetary orbit
{synonym}

A prograde, equatorial <circular orbit> with <orbital period> equal to the planet's spin period. In the ideal model its satellite remains above one equatorial ground location. Nonzero <orbital inclination> or <orbital eccentricity> destroys exact stationarity even when the periods agree.

= Radiation pressure on circumplanetary dust
{parent=Circumplanetary orbit}

The leading uniform stellar <radiation pressure> is a differential <acceleration> relative to a <planet>, of scale $\beta GM_\star/a_p^2$. Relative to planetocentric gravity at dust radius $a$, its magnitude is
$$
\eta_{\rm rad}=\beta\frac{M_\star}{M_p}\left(\frac a{a_p}\right)^2.
$$
Thus lying inside a <Hill sphere> alone does not guarantee small radiative perturbations. Approximately circular tightly bound dust requires this ratio sufficiently small, as well as weak tides; the uniform radiative <force> can otherwise excite <orbital eccentricity> or unbind grains.

= Shadowing of circumplanetary dust
{parent=Radiation pressure on circumplanetary dust}

A <planet> can block stellar radiation during part of a <circumplanetary orbit>. Then the full-orbit constant-flux averages for <radiation pressure> and <Poynting–Robertson drag> require modification. The detailed shadow duty cycle depends on the <orbital inclination>, stellar angular extent and dust radius.

= Circumstellar orbit

= Binet equation
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{c}
{wiki}

Binet’s equation rewrites a central-force orbit using $u(\theta)=1/r$. If $\ell=mr^2\dot\theta$ is angular momentum and the potential is $V(r)$, then
$$
u''+u=\frac{m}{\ell^2u^2}\frac{dV}{dr}\bigg|_{r=1/u}.
$$

= Circular orbit through an inverse-fifth-power singularity
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$c=8mh^2R^2,\quad T=2\pi R^2/|h|$}

For an attractive <central force> of magnitude $c/r^5$, a circle of radius $R$ through the force center has polar equation $r=2R\cos\theta$. With <specific angular momentum> $h$, substitution into the <Binet equation> gives $c=8mh^2R^2$. Consequently $R$ is inversely proportional to the magnitude of the <angular momentum>, and the angular-time integral gives $T=2\pi R^2/|h|\propto R^3$. This is the finite travel time along the punctured circle from one approach to the singular center to the next; the force law alone does not specify continuation through the collision.

= Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$u''+(1-B/l^2)u=A/l^2$}

For radial acceleration $-A/r^2-B/r^3$, conserved <specific angular momentum> $l$ and $u=1/r$ reduce the <Binet equation> to the displayed linear equation. If $l^2>B$, its angular oscillation frequency is $\sqrt{1-B/l^2}$; attraction proportional to $r^{-3}$ changes the apsidal angle. A zero of $u$ ends the physical positive-radius branch and corresponds to escape, not passage to a negative radius. A spatial self-intersection need not be a periodic return because <velocity> may differ.

= Apsidal invariant on an unbound central-force branch
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\psi_{\rm peri}=\psi-\operatorname{Arg}(u-u_c-iu'/\nu)/\nu$}

For $u=u_c[1+e\cos\nu(\psi-\psi_0)]$ with $\nu>0$ and $e\geq1$, the positive-radius scattering branch has $|\nu(\psi-\psi_0)|<\arccos(-1/e)\leq\pi$. Thus the principal complex argument in the displayed expression is continuous throughout that branch, unlike at an apocentre of a bound orbit. Its derivative is $\nu$, so the angle modulo $2\pi$, or the associated unit apsidal vector in the orbital plane, is a stationary <integral of motion> fixing the periapsis direction. Together with energy and three components of <specific angular momentum>, it gives five regular scattering labels even for irrational $\nu$. Periodicity is therefore necessary for five orbit labels in recurrent bound motion, not in general for escaping trajectories.

= Closed orbits with an inverse-cube perturbation
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\nu=\sqrt{1-2\mu a/L^2}\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$}

In the <gravitational potential> $-\mu(1/r+a/r^2)$, a regular noncircular bound <orbit> requires $L^2>2\mu a$ and has $u=\mu(L^2-2\mu a)^{-1}[1+e\cos\nu(\psi-\psi_0)]$ with $0<e<1$. Each radial period advances the angle by $2\pi/\nu$. The full position and <velocity> return after finitely many radial periods precisely when $\nu$ is rational. Irrational $\nu$ gives a dense rosette on the two-frequency <invariant torus>. Rationality permits a fifth <orbit> label fixing the apsidal phase on that resonant <angular momentum> family, but does not give global superintegrability for all nearby angular momenta. When $a=0$, every regular bound <Kepler orbit> is closed and the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> supplies the globally defined additional integral.

= Inverse-cube perturbation of Kepler apsidal motion
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\Delta\varpi\simeq-\pi C/[\mu a(1-e^2)]$}

For radial acceleration $-\mu/r^2+C/r^3$, the <Binet equation> gives $u''+(1+C/h^2)u=\mu/h^2$, where $h$ is <specific angular momentum>. A regular bound orbit has $h^2+C>0$, $a=-\mu/(2\mathcal E)$ and $e^2=1+2\mathcal E(h^2+C)/\mu^2$. Its radius is $a(1-e^2)/[1+e\cos(\alpha\theta)]$, with $\alpha=\sqrt{1+C/h^2}$. The <apsidal precession> per radial period is $2\pi(\alpha^{-1}-1)$, which reduces to the displayed expression for a small perturbation. Negative $C$ produces prograde precession; positive $C$ produces retrograde precession. The dimensionless coefficient is $C/(\mu a)$, since $C$ has units of length to the fourth power divided by time squared.

= Inferring an inverse-square force from Kepler laws
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$a_r=-l^2/(Ar^2)$}

Constant <areal velocity> gives constant specific <angular momentum> $l=r^2\dot\theta$, hence zero transverse acceleration and a <central force>. For a focus-based <ellipse> $u=1/r=(1+\varepsilon\cos\theta)/A$, one has $u_{\theta\theta}+u=1/A$. The <Binet equation> then gives the displayed radial acceleration, proving an attractive <inverse-square law>. An orbit's shape alone does not specify its acceleration without a time law.

= Damped Binet equation
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{k}{h}u^{\prime}+u=\frac{p}{h^2}$}

An attractive <inverse-square force> together with radial drag $-k\dot r/r^2$ preserves <angular momentum> $h=r^2\dot\theta$ but modifies the <Binet equation> for $u=1/r$ by a first derivative. For $k/|h|<2$ and $h\ne0$, the reciprocal radius is a decaying oscillation about $p/h^2$. Forward angular distance $\operatorname{sgn}(h)(\theta-\theta_0)$ makes its damping sign explicit for either orbital orientation.

= Circularization under radial inverse-square drag
{parent=Damped Binet equation}
{title2=$r\longrightarrow h^2/p,\quad\dot E=-k\dot r^2/r^2$}

A bounded trajectory in the <damped Binet equation> with nonzero <angular momentum> asymptotically becomes circular. Energy loss is nonnegative in magnitude and vanishes only for zero radial velocity. The centrifugal effective <potential energy> excludes collision; bounded radius forces indefinitely increasing forward angular distance, so the reciprocal-radius oscillation decays. Escaping trajectories are excluded by the boundedness hypothesis.

= Shifted inverse-radius potential
{parent=Binet equation}

For
$$
V(r)=-\frac{km}{r-r_0},
\qquad r>r_0,
$$
Binet’s equation is
$$
u''+u=\frac{K}{(1-r_0u)^2},
\qquad K=\frac{km^2}{\ell^2}.
$$
Its circular orbits solve $K=u(1-r_0u)^2$; the branch $u<1/(3r_0)$ is stable and the branch $u>1/(3r_0)$ is unstable.

= Eccentricity vector
{title2=$\mathbf e$}
{parent=Orbital eccentricity}
{wiki}

For an attractive <Kepler orbit> with gravitational parameter $GM$, <velocity> $\mathbf v$ and <specific angular momentum> $\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$, the eccentricity vector is
$$
\mathbf e=\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf h}{GM}-\widehat{\mathbf r}.
$$
Its magnitude is the <orbital eccentricity>, and it points toward <periapsis> for a noncircular orbit. It is a normalized attractive <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector>; it remains constant under the pure inverse-square force.

= Perturbed Kepler-orbit conservation laws
{parent=Eccentricity vector}

If the relative acceleration is $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-GM\mathbf r/r^3+\mathbf f$, with constant masses, then the <specific orbital energy> $\varepsilon$, <specific angular momentum> $\mathbf h$ and <eccentricity vector> obey
$$
\dot\varepsilon=\mathbf v\mathbin\cdot\mathbf f,\qquad
\dot{\mathbf h}=\mathbf r\times\mathbf f,\qquad
GM\dot{\mathbf e}=\mathbf f\times\mathbf h+\mathbf v\times(\mathbf r\times\mathbf f).
$$
The inverse-square-force terms cancel in each derivative. These identities express perturbing work and torque, and track the instantaneous change of the ellipse's shape and orientation.

= Thermal eccentricity distribution
{title2=$n(e)=2e$}
{parent=Orbital eccentricity}

An isotropic population of bound Kepler orbits has eccentricity probability density $n(e)=2e$ on $0\leq e<1$. It follows from the fixed-energy angular-momentum measure $n(L)dL\propto LdL$ and $L=L_c\sqrt{1-e^2}$.

= Delaunay phase-volume proof of the thermal eccentricity distribution
{c}
{parent=Thermal eccentricity distribution}
{title2=$P(e'<e)=e^2$}

For bound <Kepler orbits>, the <Delaunay variables> obey $0<G<L$, $-G<H<G$, and three periodic angles. A <canonical transformation> preserves <phase space> volume. At fixed $L$, integrating $H$ supplies the measure $2G\,dG$. Since $G=L\sqrt{1-e^2}$, the fraction with <orbital eccentricity> less than $e$ is $L^{-2}\int_{L\sqrt{1-e^2}}^L2G\,dG=e^2$. Thus the <probability density function> is $2e$, independently of any normalizable energy-only <phase-space distribution function>. A <Boltzmann distribution> is unnecessary. Even the time-dependent density $F(E(L))[1+\eta\cos(l-n(L)t)]$, $|\eta|<1$, has this same marginal because integrating the <mean anomaly> removes its oscillatory part; an eccentricity marginal alone does not imply equilibrium.

= Finite normalization of an energy-only Kepler distribution
{parent=Delaunay phase-volume proof of the thermal eccentricity distribution}
{title2=$N=(2\pi)^3\int_0^\infty L^2F(-\mu^2/(2L^2))\,dL$}

For bound <Kepler orbits> with gravitational parameter $\mu$, integrating the <Delaunay variables> over $0<G<L$, $-G<H<G$ and their three full angle periods gives the displayed population. A globally defined <thermal eccentricity distribution> requires this integral to converge, or an explicit finite action range. Merely having finite $F(E)$ at every energy does not suffice: $F=1$ yields an infinite integral at large $L$. At each fixed energy, or on every finite normalized action band, the cumulative eccentricity fraction remains $e^2$.

= Non-colliding gravitational velocity-kick bound
{parent=Gravitational scattering angle}
{title2=$|\Delta\boldsymbol v|_{\max}$}

For a local two-body hyperbolic encounter with central parameter $\mu$ and allowed centre separation $r_{\min}\geq r_0$, <conservation of energy> gives equal asymptotic speeds. The <gravitational scattering angle> therefore implies
$$
|\Delta\boldsymbol v|=\frac{2v_\infty}{1+r_{\min}v_\infty^2/\mu}
\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_0}}=\frac{v_{\rm esc}(r_0)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Put $z=v_\infty\sqrt{r_{\min}/\mu}$ and use $2z/(1+z^2)\leq1$. The optimizing grazing <orbit> has $z=1$, <orbital eccentricity> two and deflection $\pi/3$. For finite spherical bodies $r_0$ is the sum of their radii; excluding actual contact makes the upper bound a supremum. Without a finite exclusion radius the ideal point-mass problem has no finite bound.

= Barker equation
{parent=Parabolic trajectory}
{c}

For a <parabolic trajectory> with <periapsis> radius $r_p$, put $D=\tan(f/2)$, where $f$ is <true anomaly>. The Barker equation is $t-t_p=\sqrt{2r_p^3/(GM)}(D+D^3/3)$. Since $r=r_p(1+D^2)$, it is equivalent to the <radius-time relation for a parabolic Kepler orbit>. The sign of $D$ distinguishes approach from recession; $t_p$ is the <periapsis> time.

= Radius-time relation for a parabolic Kepler orbit
{parent=Barker equation}
{title2=$(r-r_p)(r+2r_p)^2=\tfrac92GM(t-t_p)^2$}

On a nonradial zero-<energy> <Kepler orbit>, conserved <specific angular momentum> $h$ gives periapsis $r_p=h^2/(2GM)$ and $\dot r^2=2GM(r-r_p)/r^2$. Integrating with $s=\sqrt{r-r_p}$ yields $t-t_p=\pm\sqrt{2/(GM)}(r+2r_p)\sqrt{r-r_p}/3$. Squaring gives the displayed version of the <Barker equation>, valid on both incoming and outgoing branches. The time origin $t_p$ is passage through <periapsis>, not an arbitrary point on the orbit.

= Pericentre-to-crossover flight time
{parent=Barker equation}
{title2=$n_pt_x\simeq2^{7/8}(q/a_p)^{3/8}/3$}

A <parabolic Kepler orbit> obeys $t=\sqrt{2q^3/(GM)}(D+D^3/3)$ with $D=\tan(f/2)$. The small time fraction explains why an eccentric orbit turns through almost $\pi$ of <true anomaly> while a distant perturber barely moves.

= True longitude
{parent=Orbital element}
{title2=$\ell_{\rm true}=\Omega+\omega+f$}

True longitude is the conventional orbital angle formed from the <longitude of ascending node>, argument of <periapsis> and <true anomaly>. For a coplanar <Kepler orbit> it is the actual polar longitude of the position vector. For an inclined <orbital plane> the sum of angles is generally not the projected azimuth. The <mean longitude> uses <mean anomaly> $M$ in place of $f$, so $\ell_{\rm true}-\lambda=f-M$. For small <orbital eccentricity>, <Kepler's equation> gives $E=M+e\sin M+O(e^2)$, while the eccentric-to-true anomaly relation gives $f=E+e\sin E+O(e^2)$; hence $f-M=2e\sin M+O(e^2)$. This periodic difference matters when separating a slow <resonant argument> from a rapidly varying geometric angle.

= Orbital plane
{parent=Orbital element}

The plane containing a central-force <Kepler orbit>, perpendicular to its conserved <specific angular momentum>. Its orientation can be specified by an <orbital inclination> and an <orbital node>. Perturbing <forces> can change that orientation.

= Osculating orbital element
{parent=Orbital element}

An <orbital element> of the <Kepler orbit> having the same instantaneous position and <velocity> as a perturbed trajectory. Its elements vary as the perturbing <force> acts. Their interpretation depends on the central <gravitational parameter> used, for example whether static <radiation pressure> is included in the unperturbed attraction.

= Astrocentric osculating-element oscillations
{parent=Osculating orbital element}
{title2=$\delta a/a=2\varepsilon\cos\Delta,\quad(e_r,e_\theta)=(2\varepsilon\cos\Delta,\varepsilon\sin\Delta)$}

A circular distant barycentric <Kepler orbit> around a circular star–planet binary need not have constant star-centred <osculating orbital elements>. Let $\mu$ be the planet's fraction of the binary <mass>, $\alpha=a_{\rm pl}/a\ll1$, and $\varepsilon=\mu/\sqrt\alpha\ll1$. Subtracting the stellar reflex <velocity> produces radial and tangential perturbations $(-v_0\varepsilon\sin\Delta,v_0\varepsilon\cos\Delta)$, where $\Delta$ is the planet's longitude minus the particle's longitude. The <vis-viva equation> gives the displayed <semi-major axis> variation. The <eccentricity vector> components follow from $e_r=rv_\theta^2/(GM_\star)-1$ and $e_\theta=-rv_rv_\theta/(GM_\star)$. The errors are $O(\mu+\varepsilon^2)$; the spatial translation itself is only $O(\mu\alpha)$. These are changes of instantaneous orbital description, even when the assumed barycentric trajectory is fixed.

= Cross-sectional-area current
{parent=Line density on a Kepler orbit}
{title2=$J_\sigma$}

The <geometric cross-section> passing a fixed point of a steady orbital wire per unit time is $J_\sigma=\lambda_\sigma v$, where $\lambda_\sigma=d\sigma/ds$. $$
J_\sigma=\frac{\sigma_{\rm tot}}P.
$$
With the <fractional luminosity of a phase-mixed eccentric wire>, this becomes $J_\sigma=2fh$. The current describes transported area, not the swept area enclosed by the <orbit>.

= Phase-mixed radial probability of a Kepler orbit
{parent=Phase-mixed orbit}

p_r(r)=\frac{r}{\pi a\sqrt{(r-q)(Q-r)}},\qquad q<r<Q.
$$
The two radial passages per <orbital period> are included, and $\int_q^Qp_r(r)\,dr=1$.

= Inclination to the invariable plane of two orbital planes

= Specific orbital energy
{title2=$\varepsilon$}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

The specific orbital energy is the mechanical energy per unit mass,
$$
\varepsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=-\frac{\mu}{2a}.
$$
Its sign distinguishes bound elliptic, marginal parabolic, and unbound hyperbolic Kepler orbits.

= Orbit-averaged drag work
{parent=Specific orbital energy}

A weak perturbing relative acceleration $\mathbf f$ changes <specific orbital energy> at rate $\dot\varepsilon=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f$. Since $\varepsilon=-\mu/(2a)$ for a <Kepler orbit>,
$$
\dot a=\frac{2a^2}{\mu}\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f.
$$
For a circular <Kepler orbit> under $\mathbf f=-\kappa\mathbf v+\mathbf g$ with constant $\mathbf g$, the constant-force term averages to zero over an unperturbed orbit. Thus $\langle\dot a\rangle=-2\kappa a$. This is a leading-order orbital average; a strong constant force can distort the orbit instead.

= Vis-viva equation
{parent=Specific orbital energy}
{wiki}

For a <Kepler orbit> with <semi-major axis> $a$, the <vis-viva equation> is
$$
v^2=\mu\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
$$
For the convention that a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit> has positive semi-major-axis magnitude $a$, its final term instead has a plus sign.

= Specific angular momentum
{title2=$h$}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

The specific angular momentum is angular momentum per unit mass, $\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$. For a planar Kepler orbit its magnitude is $h=r^2\dot\theta=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}$.

= High-eccentricity angular-speed crossover
{parent=Specific angular momentum}
{title2=$r_x=\sqrt{h/n_p}$}

For a nearly radial <Kepler orbit> with <pericentre> $q$, <specific angular momentum> is $h\simeq\sqrt{2GMq}$. Relative to a circular reference orbit of radius $a_p$ and <mean motion> $n_p$, the instantaneous azimuthal rate changes from faster to slower at $r_x=\sqrt{h/n_p}\simeq a_p(2q/a_p)^{1/4}$. In a <rotating reference frame> the polar angle has a turning point at this radius. The <true anomaly> there differs from $\pi$ by approximately $2\sqrt{q/r_x}$.

= Periapsis angular speed
{title2=$\dot\theta_{\rm peri}$}
{parent=Specific angular momentum}

The <specific angular momentum> of an elliptic <Kepler orbit> is $h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}$. At <periapsis>, $r=a(1-e)$, so
$$
\dot\theta_{\rm peri}=\frac h{r^2}=n\frac{\sqrt{1+e}}{(1-e)^{3/2}}.
$$
The <mean motion> understates instantaneous angular speed near <periapsis>. A particle in an <exterior mean-motion resonance> can briefly overtake the planet in angle despite completing full orbits more slowly.

= Circular Kepler orbit

A circular Kepler orbit at radius $r$ has speed $v_K=\sqrt{\mu/r}$ and mean motion $n=\sqrt{\mu/r^3}$.

= Synchronous circular orbit

Balancing gravitational acceleration and centripetal acceleration gives the displayed radius and speed $v=2\pi r/T$. The satellite's own mass cancels. A physically realizable external orbit additionally requires this radius to exceed the planet's radius.

= Hill equations

= Orbital resonance
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{wiki}

An orbital resonance occurs when orbital frequencies satisfy an approximately integer relation, allowing perturbations to accumulate coherently.

= Mean-motion resonance
{parent=Orbital resonance}
{wiki}

A mean-motion resonance has $jn_2-kn_1\simeq0$ for small integers $j,k$. Its slow resonant angle combines mean longitudes with apsidal or nodal longitudes.

= Interior eccentricity-type mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

For an eccentric inner body and circular outer perturber near the reduced $(m+n):m$ period ratio, the leading <resonant argument> is $\phi=(m+n)\lambda_{\rm out}-m\lambda_{\rm in}-n\varpi_{\rm in}$. Its <disturbing function> amplitude is of order $e_{\rm in}^n$. At a mean <conjunction>, $\phi=n(\Lambda-\varpi_{\rm in})$; at actual alignment the exact expression is $(m+n)x-my$ with $x=f_{\rm in}$ and $y=M_{\rm in}(x)$.

= Inclination-type mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

= Inclination resonance
{synonym}

An inclination-type mean-motion resonance has a leading <resonant argument> containing a <longitude of ascending node>. Reflection symmetry forces the total nodal coefficient in each disturbing-function harmonic to be even, so the leading resonant strength is an even power of $\sin(I/2)$.

= Exterior mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

In an exterior mean-motion resonance, the resonant small body orbits outside the perturbing planet and therefore has lower mean motion. A $(p+q):p$ exterior resonance has $n/n_p\simeq p/(p+q)$.

= Resonant semi-major axis
{parent=Exterior mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$a_r=a_p((p+q)/p)^{2/3}$}

The nominal <semi-major axis> of an exterior $(p+q):p$ <mean-motion resonance> follows from $(p+q)n_r=pn_p$ and <Kepler's third law>. Apsidal precession displaces the actual libration center because stationarity of the <resonant argument> requires $(p+q)n-pn_p-q\dot\varpi=0$.

= Radiation-pressure shift of a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Exterior mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$a/a_p=(1-\beta)^{1/3}((j+1)/j)^{2/3}$}

A grain with <radiation-pressure coefficient> $\beta<1$ feels effective central <gravitational parameter> $GM_\star(1-\beta)$. Combining its <mean motion> with the planet's <mean motion> at an exterior-type $(j+1):j$ frequency commensurability gives the displayed <semi-major axis>. The commensurability lies geometrically exterior only for $j<\sqrt{1-\beta}/(1-\sqrt{1-\beta})$. Radiation changes the grain's attraction, so the same frequency ratio need not imply the usual radial ordering.

= First-order mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

A first-order mean-motion resonance has $|j-k|=1$. For small eccentricity its leading eccentric resonant term is linear in eccentricity.

= Resonance-overlap encounter estimate
{parent=First-order mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$j_c\sim\mu^{-2/7}$}

A local weak <gravitational assist> estimate for a nonradiating circular grain gives $\delta\epsilon\simeq32\mu^2\epsilon^{-5}$, where $\mu=M_p/M_\star$. <Conjunction-longitude sensitivity to an encounter> then gives $|\delta\Lambda|\simeq128\pi\mu^2/(3\epsilon^7)$. Requiring the change to exceed one full turn and using $\epsilon\simeq2/(3j)$ gives $j_c\simeq(2/729)^{1/7}\mu^{-2/7}$. This motivates loss of coherent resonant conjunctions, rather than proving that every initial phase escapes. The coefficient depends on the encounter convention and the approximations; near-planet <Hill equations> require a consistent rotating-frame shear instead of mixing local inertial and rotating velocities.

= Resonant argument
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

= Resonant angle
{synonym}

A resonant argument is a slow angular combination whose libration diagnoses resonance. For an exterior $j:(j-1)$ eccentric resonance it can be $\phi=j\lambda-(j-1)\lambda_p-\varpi$.

A resonant argument is an integer combination of orbital angles whose slow variation can diagnose <mean-motion resonance>. Its libration, rather than circulation through every angle, characterizes trapping in the resonance.

= Resonant conjunction geometry
{parent=Resonant argument}

For an eccentric outer body and circular inner perturber, the exterior <resonant argument> is $\phi=(p+q)\lambda_{\rm out}-p\lambda_{\rm in}-q\varpi$. At equal <mean longitudes> $\Lambda$, <conjunction> phases are
$$
\Lambda_j=\varpi+\frac{\phi+2\pi j}{q},\qquad j=0,\ldots,q-1.
$$
The <synodic period> and coprime $p,q$ determine the visitation order, with advance $2\pi p/q$. For actual true alignment, write $x=f$ and $y=M(f)$ for the eccentric body's <true anomaly> and <mean anomaly>. Then $\phi=(p+q)y-px$ instead of $qx$. The evenly spaced directions are a leading-small-eccentricity approximation.

= Phase protection of interior integer resonances
{parent=Resonant conjunction geometry}
{title2=$\phi=k\lambda_p-\lambda-(k-1)\varpi$}

In a high-<orbital eccentricity> interior $k:1$ <mean-motion resonance>, the <resonant argument> $\phi=k\lambda_p-\lambda-(k-1)\varpi$ places successive rotating-frame <apocentres> at $\pi-[\phi+(2j+1)\pi]/k$. Avoiding <astronomical conjunction> at these slow outer excursions favors $\phi=0$ for $k=2$ and $\phi=\pi$ for $k=3$. This geometrical phase protection suggests stable <resonant-argument libration> centres; actual dynamical stability requires the resonant perturbation problem.

= Conjunction-pattern precession near a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Resonant conjunction geometry}
{title2=$\dot\Lambda$}

With constant <mean motions> and $P_c/P_b=(1+\delta)(p+q)/p$, mean <conjunctions> advance by $\Delta\Lambda=2\pi/(P_c/P_b-1)$ each <synodic period>. Tracking one branch every $q$ <conjunctions> and subtracting $2\pi p$ gives
$$
\dot\Lambda=\frac{q\Delta\Lambda-2\pi p}{qP_{\rm syn}}
=-\frac{2\pi p\delta}{qP_b(1+\delta)}.
$$
The usual formula $-2\pi p\delta/(qP_b)$ keeps only first order in $\delta$. A labelled branch takes $qP_b(1+\delta)/(p|\delta|)$ to turn once, whereas the full set of $q$ interchangeable directions repeats after $P_b(1+\delta)/(p|\delta|)$. Finite <orbital eccentricity> introduces true-conjunction corrections.

= Conjunction-longitude sensitivity to an encounter
{parent=Conjunction-pattern precession near a mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$\delta\Lambda=\Lambda'(\epsilon)\delta\epsilon$}

For an exterior circular grain with <radiation-pressure coefficient> $\beta$, set $q=\sqrt{1-\beta}$ and $\epsilon=a/a_p-1$. Its next <conjunction> occurs after the <synodic period>, during which the planet's unwrapped <mean longitude> advances by $\Lambda=2\pi/[1-q(1+\epsilon)^{-3/2}]$. A weak <planetary scattering> encounter changes this by $\delta\Lambda\simeq-3\pi q(1+\epsilon)^{-5/2}\delta\epsilon/[1-q(1+\epsilon)^{-3/2}]^2$. Unwrapped longitude counts turns; reducing it modulo $2\pi$ before comparison would erase this measure of timing sensitivity.

= Resonant-argument libration
{parent=Resonant argument}

Resonant-argument libration is bounded oscillation of a resonant argument about a stable equilibrium value instead of circulation through all angles.

= Pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}
{title2=$\ddot\phi=K\sin\phi$}

= Resonant pendulum approximation
{synonym}

Keeping one resonant harmonic and treating <orbital eccentricity> as fixed reduces <mean-motion resonance> dynamics to a <physical pendulum> equation. For the inner first-order term $\mathcal R=(GM_2/a_2)f(\alpha)e\cos\phi$, leading <Lagrange planetary equations> give $\ddot\phi\simeq3j^2\mu_2n^2\alpha f e\sin\phi$. Around its stable phase, the small-amplitude libration frequency is $jn\sqrt{3\mu_2\alpha|f|e}$. Evolution of eccentricity, other harmonics, and approach to a <separatrix> change this approximation.

= Resonant pendulum energy
{parent=Pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$E=\dot\phi^2/2+\omega_0^2(1+\cos\phi)$}

For $\ddot\phi=\omega_0^2\sin\phi$ with constant $\omega_0$, multiplication by $\dot\phi$ gives the conserved energy $E=\dot\phi^2/2+\omega_0^2(1+\cos\phi)$. The stable center is $\pi$; the <separatrix> has $E=2\omega_0^2$. Below it the <resonant argument> librates and above it the angle circulates. This is an invariant of the constant-coefficient <pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance>, not of arbitrary coupled <orbital element> evolution.

= Asymmetric resonant-argument libration
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}

Libration about a resonant phase distinct from the symmetric values zero and $\pi$. For example, in a negative-Kepler-curvature reduction with disturbing potential $R=A\cos\phi+B\cos2\phi$, $A>0$, $B>A/4$, stationary points obey $\sin\phi(A+4B\cos\phi)=0$. At $\pi$, $R''=A-4B<0$, so it is unstable, whereas at $\cos\phi=-A/(4B)$ one has $R''=4B-A^2/(4B)>0$, giving two stable asymmetric centres. Actual branch thresholds depend on the resonance coefficients and evolving <orbital elements>.

= Symmetric libration centres of an eccentricity resonance
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}

In the weak, non-crossing, leading-eccentricity planar model, exterior orders one to three have positive resonant <disturbing function> coefficients $B_q$. Interior eccentricity-type coefficients of these orders have sign $(-1)^q$. A local resonant <Hamiltonian> $H=-AJ^2/2-Be^q\cos\phi$, with $A>0$, gives $\ddot\phi=ABe^q\sin\phi$. <Linear stability analysis> therefore selects $\phi=\pi$ for $B>0$ and $\phi=0$ for $B<0$. Exterior symmetric centres are $\pi$; interior centres are $0,\pi,0$ for orders one, two and three. These are conditional leading-harmonic predictions, not universal centres at arbitrary <orbital eccentricity>; <asymmetric resonant-argument libration> changes the picture.

= Low-eccentricity correction to a first-order resonance center
{parent=Symmetric libration centres of an eccentricity resonance}
{title2=$3(p+1)^2ne^3-C_r$}

= Two hinged rods with both ends sliding on a floor
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{title2=$v_{\rm hinge}=\sqrt{6gl\sin\alpha}$}

Two identical uniform <rigid bodies> of rod shape, each of length $2l$ and mass $M$, form a symmetric arch with a free common hinge and both outer ends sliding without friction on a horizontal floor. At angle $\theta$, each center has <speed> $l|\dot\theta|$, giving total <kinetic energy> $4Ml^2\dot\theta^2/3$. The total gravitational <potential energy> is $2Mgl\sin\theta$. <Conservation of energy> from rest at $\alpha$ gives the displayed hinge impact <speed>. Unlike fixing one outer endpoint, allowing both endpoints to slide keeps the arch center horizontally fixed. The floor reaction on each rod is $Mg[1+9\sin^2\theta-6\sin\alpha\sin\theta]/4$, nonnegative throughout this fall.

= Endpoint collision of two freely rotating equal rods
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{title2=$J=\frac{mV(1+e)}2,\quad |\omega|=\frac{V(1+e)}{4\ell}$}

Each massless rod has endpoint masses $m$ at distance $\ell$ from its centre, hence total mass $2m$ and <moment of inertia> $2m\ell^2$. In a symmetric opposite-end collision with centres approaching at speed $V$, a horizontal <impulse> $J$ changes each centre speed by $J/(2m)$ and each contact-point speed by $J/m$. The <coefficient of restitution> gives $2J/m-2V=2eV$. Both rods rotate in the same sense for the staggered vertical configuration; their centre speeds after impact are $V(3-e)/4$.

= Two hinged rods sliding on a floor
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{title2=$\dot\theta^2=\frac{3g}{2l}\frac{\sin\alpha-\sin\theta}{1+3\sin^2\theta}$}

Two equal uniform rods of length $2l$ and mass $M$, one pivoted to the floor and the other endpoint sliding on it, have arch coordinates $B=(2l\cos\theta,2l\sin\theta)$ and $C=(4l\cos\theta,0)$. = Euler's equations
{c}
{disambiguate=rigid body dynamics}
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{title2=$I\dot\omega+\omega\times(I\omega)=\tau$}
{wiki}

In principal body coordinates, <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> express <angular momentum> balance as $I\dot\omega+\omega\times(I\omega)=\tau$, with applied <torque> $\tau$. Setting the torque to zero gives the following torque-free specialization.

= Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body
{parent=Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)}
{c}

In principal-axis body coordinates, torque-free angular velocity satisfies
$$
I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,
\quad
I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,
\quad
I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2.
$$

= Torque-free rigid-body invariants
{parent=Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body}

The <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body> conserve rotational kinetic energy and squared angular momentum,
$$
E=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^3I_i\omega_i^2,
\qquad
L^2=\sum_{i=1}^3I_i^2\omega_i^2.
$$
Their common level sets are the intersection of two ellipsoids in angular-velocity space.

= Quartic equation for one component of torque-free angular velocity
{parent=Torque-free rigid-body invariants}

Eliminating $\omega_1^2$ and $\omega_2^2$ between the conserved energy and squared angular momentum gives
$$
\dot\omega_3^2=-\frac{
\left[L^2-2EI_2+I_3(I_2-I_3)\omega_3^2\right]
\left[L^2-2EI_1+I_3(I_1-I_3)\omega_3^2\right]
}{I_1I_2I_3^2}.
$$
The right-hand side is a quartic polynomial in $\omega_3$; its real turning points delimit the nutation of the body-frame angular velocity.

= Coplanarity in a torque-free axisymmetric rigid body
{parent=Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body}

For principal moments $I_1=I_2=I$ and symmetry-axis unit vector $e_3$, angular momentum and angular velocity obey
$$
L=I\omega+(I_3-I)\omega_3e_3.
$$
Thus $L$, $\omega$, and the symmetry axis are coplanar at every instant.

= Intermediate axis theorem
{parent=Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body}
{wiki}

For $I_1<I_2<I_3$, steady torque-free rotation about the first or third principal axis is stable, while rotation about the intermediate principal axis is unstable.

= Linear stability of principal-axis rotation
{parent=Intermediate axis theorem}

Linearize the <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body> about rotation with angular speed $\Omega$ along the third principal axis. The transverse perturbations obey
$$
\dot\omega_1=\frac{I_2-I_3}{I_1}\Omega\omega_2,
\qquad
\dot\omega_2=\frac{I_3-I_1}{I_2}\Omega\omega_1,
$$
and hence
$$
\ddot\omega_1
=-\Omega^2\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_3-I_1)}{I_1I_2}\omega_1.
$$
Cyclic permutation gives the corresponding equations about the other two axes. For $I_1<I_2<I_3$, the squared frequency is positive about axes one and three, whereas the intermediate-axis equation has a positive exponential-growth coefficient.

= Intermediate-axis separatrix
{parent=Intermediate axis theorem}

On the invariant level $L^2=2EI_2$, the torque-free rigid-body orbit is a separatrix joining the two intermediate-axis rotations. With
$$
\mu=\sqrt{\frac{2E}{I_2}},
\qquad
\lambda=\mu\sqrt{\frac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}},
$$
one orientation has $\omega_2(t)=\mu\tanh(\lambda(t-t_0))$; the other components are constant multiples of $\operatorname{sech}(\lambda(t-t_0))$.

= Moment of inertia of a uniform solid hemisphere
{parent=Moment of inertia}
{title2=$I_G=83ma^2/320$}

The centroid of a uniform solid hemisphere of radius $a$ lies $3a/8$ from its flat face. About a diameter of the underlying sphere parallel to that face, its <moment of inertia> is $2ma^2/5$. The <parallel axis theorem> therefore gives $I_G=2ma^2/5-m(3a/8)^2=83ma^2/320$ about the parallel centroidal axis. A thin hemispherical shell has a different centroid and inertia.

= Moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder
{parent=Moment of inertia}
{title2=$I=ma^2/2$}

For a uniform solid cylinder of radius $a$, length $L$ and <mass density> $\rho$, a coaxial shell carries <mass> $dm=2\pi\rho Lr\,dr$. The axial <moment of inertia> is $I=\int_0^a r^2dm=\pi\rho La^4/2=ma^2/2$. The axial <moment of inertia> is independent of the length once <mass> and radius are fixed. A thin cylindrical shell instead has $I=ma^2$, demonstrating why the uniform solid assumption matters.

= Reflected inertia of a no-slip wheel and roller
{parent=Moment of inertia}
{title2=$K=I+J(R/S)^2$}

If a fixed-axis wheel of radius $R$ drives a fixed-axis roller of radius $S$ without slip, their angular-speed magnitudes satisfy $\sigma=(R/S)\omega$. Their <kinetic energy> is $\tfrac12[I+J(R/S)^2]\omega^2$, so the displayed reflected <moment of inertia> governs the wheel coordinate. A roller resisting torque of magnitude $\gamma\sigma^2$ consumes power $\gamma\sigma^3$ and therefore reflects to wheel torque $\gamma(R/S)^3\omega^2$. The squared ratio belongs to inertia; the cubed ratio belongs to this quadratic resisting torque.

= Moment of inertia of a uniform annulus
{parent=Moment of inertia}
{title2=$I=\frac M2(a^2+b^2)$}

A uniform planar annulus of outer radius $a$, inner radius $b<a$ and mass $M$ has surface density $M/[\pi(a^2-b^2)]$. Integrating $r^2$ over its mass gives the axial <moment of inertia> $I=M(a^2+b^2)/2$. Subtracting the inner disc from a complete disc gives the same result, provided their surface densities match.

= Moment of inertia of a uniform disc

For a uniform disc of radius $a$ and mass $M$, annuli of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ have mass $(M/\pi a^2)2\pi r\,dr$. Their axial <moment of inertia> sums to $I=\int_0^a r^2\,dm=Ma^2/2$.

= Moment of inertia of a uniform solid sphere

= Mass second-moment tensor
{title2=$I_{jk}$}
{parent=Moment of inertia}

The mass second-moment tensor about a <centre of mass> is $I_{jk}=\int y_jy_k\,dm$. It measures the shape of a mass distribution and appears in <tidal torque theory>. It differs from the mechanical <inertia tensor>, which is $\mathcal I_{jk}=\operatorname{tr}(I)\delta_{jk}-I_{jk}$. For a uniform <ellipsoid> of mass $M$ and semiaxes $a_1,a_2,a_3$, its principal values are $Ma_j^2/5$.

It maps <angular velocity> to <angular momentum>.

= Tensor parallel-axis theorem

If $G$ is the <center of mass> and $X=P-G$, expanding the defining <inertia tensor> about $P$ gives the displayed formula. The cross terms vanish because $\int r\,dm=0$ in coordinates centred at $G$. = Angular rocket equation
{parent=Moment of inertia}

For a uniformly filled, negligibly light disc of fixed radius $R$, remaining mass $m(t)$, angular velocity $\omega(t)$, and tangential exhaust speed $u$ relative to its rim, angular-momentum balance gives

m\dot\omega=\dot m\left(\omega+\frac{2u}{R}\right).
$$
At zero relative exhaust speed, $\omega/m$ is constant.

= Moment of inertia of a solid cone about its vertex
{parent=Moment of inertia}

For a uniform solid cone of height $l$, base radius $R$, and mass $M$, whose symmetry axis points from vertex to base, the centre of mass lies at $3l/4$ from the vertex. The principal moments at the vertex are
$$I_1=I_2=\frac{3M}{20}(R^2+4l^2),
\qquad I_3=\frac{3MR^2}{10}.$$

= Moment of inertia of a pointed cubic-profile solid of revolution

x_1^2+x_2^2=x_3^2-\frac{x_3^3}{h},

For total mass $M$, the center of mass lies a distance $3h/5$ from the pointed origin, and the principal moments about that origin are

= Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops
{c}
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{wiki}

The <Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops> are three integrable cases of fixed-point <rigid body dynamics>. The Euler top has no external torque; the Lagrange top has axial symmetry and its center of mass on the symmetry axis; the Kovalevskaya top has two equal principal moments twice the third and its center of mass in the corresponding principal plane.

= Euler angles
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$(\phi,\theta,\psi)$}

<Euler angles> describe the orientation of a <rigid body> through three successive angular rotations. Their convention specifies the axes and order of the rotations. For a symmetric top, a common convention takes $\theta$ as the inclination, $\phi$ as precession and $\psi$ as spin about the body axis.

= Euler angles for a symmetric top
{parent=Euler angles}
{c}

With the convention in which $\theta$ is inclination, $\phi$ is precession, and $\psi$ is body-axis spin, an axisymmetric body's kinetic energy is
$$T=\frac I2(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\dot\phi^2)
+\frac J2(\dot\psi+\cos\theta\dot\phi)^2.$$

= Heavy symmetric top Lagrangian
{parent=Euler angles for a symmetric top}

For a fixed point a distance $a$ from the centre of mass and inclination measured from the upward vertical,
$$L=T-Mga\cos\theta.$$
The spin and precession angles are cyclic.

This is the Lagrangian of a <Lagrange top>, one of the <Lagrange, Euler, and Kovalevskaya tops>.

= Lagrange top
{c}
{parent=Heavy symmetric top Lagrangian}

A Lagrange top is an axisymmetric rigid body with one point fixed on its symmetry axis, moving in a uniform gravitational field with its center of mass on that axis.

= Steady precession of a Lagrange top
{c}
{parent=Lagrange top}

For a Lagrange top whose inclination $\theta$ is measured from the upward vertical, steady precession at angular speed $\Omega$ obeys
$$
I_1\Omega^2\cos\theta-p_\psi\Omega+Mgl=0.
$$
Thus steady precession at a prescribed $0<\theta<\pi/2$ exists precisely when
$$
p_\psi^2\geq4MglI_1\cos\theta.
$$

= Heavy symmetric top Hamiltonian
{parent=Heavy symmetric top Lagrangian}

With transverse and axial moments $I,J$, the Hamiltonian is
$$H=\frac{p_\theta^2}{2I}
+\frac{(p_\phi-p_\psi\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}
+\frac{p_\psi^2}{2J}+Mga\cos\theta.$$

= Heavy symmetric top reduction
{parent=Heavy symmetric top Hamiltonian}

Fixing the cyclic momenta $p_\psi=\lambda$ and $p_\phi=\mu$ leaves the canonical pair $(\theta,p_\theta)$ and a one-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian with effective potential
$$V_{\lambda,\mu}(\theta)
=\frac{(\mu-\lambda\cos\theta)^2}{2I\sin^2\theta}
+\frac{\lambda^2}{2J}+Mga\cos\theta.$$

= Gyroscopic stabilization of an inverted symmetric top
{parent=Heavy symmetric top reduction}

At the upright spinning state, $p_\phi=p_\psi=\lambda$. The reduced potential is locally minimized at zero inclination when
$$\lambda^2>4IMga,$$
so sufficiently large axial angular momentum stabilizes the gravitationally inverted top.

= Rolling without slipping
{parent=Rigid body dynamics}
{wiki}

Rolling without slipping imposes that the contact point is instantaneously at rest, giving v=R omega.

= Rolling-time factor on a straight incline
{parent=Rolling without slipping}
{title2=$t_{\rm roll}/t_{\rm slide}=\sqrt{1+I/(MR^2)}$}

For a rigid body rolling from rest down a straight incline with fixed rolling radius $R$ and axial <moment of inertia> $I$, <translation> and <rotation> give $(M+I/R^2)\ddot s=Mg\sin\theta$. A smooth slide has $\ddot s=g\sin\theta$. Travel over the same distance from rest therefore gives the displayed time ratio. <Static friction> must suffice for <rolling without slipping>; its contact point is at rest, so this ideal friction does no work. A uniform solid cylinder has $I=MR^2/2$ and ratio $\sqrt{3/2}$.

= Rolling uniform solid hemisphere
{parent=Rolling without slipping}
{title2=$\dot\theta^2=15g\cos\theta/[a(28-15\cos\theta)]$}

Let the flat face make angle $\theta$ with horizontal while the rounded face contacts a horizontal plane. The centroid has height $a-(3a/8)\cos\theta$ and contact distance squared $b^2=a^2+(3a/8)^2-2a(3a/8)\cos\theta$. For <rolling without slipping>, its speed is $b|\dot\theta|$. Combining translation and rotation gives kinetic energy $\tfrac12(I_G+mb^2)\dot\theta^2$. Conservation from rest at $\theta=\pi/2$ gives the displayed result until the flat-face/contact geometry changes.

= Slipping solid cylinder on an incline
{parent=Rolling without slipping}
{title2=$\tan\alpha>3\mu$}

For a uniform solid cylinder, take $s$ positive uphill and $\omega$ positive for uphill <rolling without slipping>. The velocity of the contact point is $w=\dot s-a\omega$. Uphill <kinetic friction> gives $\ddot s=-g(\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha)$ and $a\dot\omega=-2\mu g\cos\alpha$. Starting from $\dot s(0)=a\omega(0)$, one obtains $w=-gt(\sin\alpha-3\mu\cos\alpha)$. Thus $\tan\alpha>3\mu$ makes the assumed uphill <kinetic friction> self-consistent for the entire ascent and descent. Without slip the required uphill <static friction> is $mg\sin\alpha/3$; it is available exactly when $\tan\alpha\leq3\mu$ under the same limiting coefficient.

= Rolling inside a fixed circular cylinder
{parent=Rolling without slipping}
{title2=$\Omega=-\frac{R_1-R_0}{R_0}\dot\theta$}

A round body of radius $R_0$ in a fixed cylinder of radius $R_1$ has <center of mass> path radius $a=R_1-R_0$. With orbital angle $\theta$ and spin <angular velocity> $\Omega$ both positive about the same axis, <rolling without slipping> gives $a\dot\theta+R_0\Omega=0$. For <moment of inertia> $I=\gamma mR_0^2$, its <kinetic energy> is $\frac12ma^2(1+\gamma)\dot\theta^2$. Release from rest at angle $\alpha$ gives, by <conservation of energy>, a descent time $\sqrt{1+\gamma}$ times that of frictionless sliding along the same <center of mass> path. The spin sign reverses if its positive direction is chosen opposite to the orbital angle.

= Lagrangian mechanics
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{c}
{wiki}

Lagrangian mechanics derives motion from the stationary action of $L=T-V$ through the Euler--Lagrange equations.

= Cartan form of a regular mechanical Lagrangian
{c}
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}
{title2=$\theta_L=L_{v^i}\,dq^i,\quad\omega_L=-d\theta_L$}

A <regular Lagrangian> $L:TQ\to\mathbb R$ defines a fibre derivative $\mathbb FL:TQ\to T^*Q$ and the Cartan one-form $\theta_L=(\mathbb FL)^*\theta$. Its two-form $\omega_L=-d\theta_L$ is nondegenerate when the velocity Hessian is invertible. The energy is $E_L=v^iL_{v^i}-L$. The equation $\iota_{\Gamma_L}\omega_L=dE_L$ determines the Euler–Lagrange vector field. A <hyperregular Lagrangian> makes the fibre derivative a global diffeomorphism and transports this dynamics to Hamiltonian phase space.

= Helical symmetry of a potential
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

A potential $V(r,\phi,z)=f(r,\phi-kz)$ is invariant under the screw transformation $(\phi,z)\mapsto(\phi+ks,z+s)$. The <Noether conserved quantity for a mechanical point symmetry> is
$$
p_z+kp_\phi,
$$
a linear combination of axial <linear momentum> and axial <angular momentum>.

= Cyclic coordinate

A generalized coordinate absent from the <Lagrangian> is cyclic. Its conjugate <generalized momentum> is conserved by the <Euler-Lagrange equations>.

= Action

The action of a path is the time integral of its <Lagrangian>. The <principle of stationary action> requires its first variation to vanish for every admissible fixed-endpoint variation.

The principle of stationary action says that the physical path between fixed endpoints is a <stationary point> of the <action>.

= Total-time-derivative invariance of a Lagrangian
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

Replacing a Lagrangian by
$$
L'(q,\dot q,t)=L(q,\dot q,t)+\frac d{dt}F(q,t)
$$
adds only the endpoint term $F(q(t_2),t_2)-F(q(t_1),t_1)$ to the action. Fixed-endpoint variations therefore give the same Euler--Lagrange equations.

= Charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

For electromagnetic scalar and vector potentials $\phi$ and $A$,
$$
L=\frac12m\dot r^2-q\phi+q\dot r\cdot A.
$$
With
$$
E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_tA,
\qquad
B=\nabla\times A,
$$
the Euler--Lagrange equations give the Lorentz force $m\ddot r=q(E+\dot r\times B)$, while the canonical momentum is $p=m\dot r+qA$.

= Hamiltonian of a charged particle in an electromagnetic field
{parent=Charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian}

The <Legendre transform> of the <charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian> is
$$
H(\mathbf x,\mathbf p,t)=\frac{[\mathbf p-q\mathbf A(\mathbf x,t)]^2}{2m}+q\phi(\mathbf x,t).
$$

= Gauge variation of the charged-particle Lagrangian
{parent=Charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian}

Under $\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_tf$ and $A\mapsto A+\nabla f$, the charged-particle Lagrangian changes by
$$
L' - L=q\bigl(\partial_tf+\dot r\cdot\nabla f\bigr)
=q\frac{df(r(t),t)}{dt}.
$$
It therefore has unchanged equations of motion by total-time-derivative invariance.

= Canonical momentum parallel to a uniform magnetic field
{parent=Charged-particle electromagnetic Lagrangian}

For a constant uniform magnetic field $B$, choose the symmetric gauge $A=\tfrac12B\times r$. The coordinate parallel to $B$ is cyclic, so its canonical momentum is conserved. Since $A\cdot B=0$, this component is also the mechanical momentum $m\dot r\cdot\widehat B$.

= Generalized momentum
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

The generalized, or conjugate, momentum associated with a coordinate $q_i$ is
$$
p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}.
$$

In <Lagrangian mechanics>, the coordinate variable describes the configuration; its conjugate momentum need not equal the Cartesian mechanical momentum.

= Ignorable coordinate
{parent=Generalized momentum}

A coordinate $q_i$ is ignorable, or cyclic, when $\partial L/\partial q_i=0$. The Euler--Lagrange equation then gives $\dot p_i=0$, so its generalized momentum is conserved.

= Conserved quantities from cyclic coordinates
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

If a Lagrangian is independent of a coordinate, the conjugate momentum to that coordinate is conserved by the Euler--Lagrange equations.

= Axially symmetric isotropic-mass Lagrangian
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

If $L=|\dot q|^2/2-V(q)$ and $V$ depends only on $|q|^2$ and $(q\cdot n)^2$, then rotations about the axis $n$ leave $L$ invariant. The quantity supplied by <Noether theorem> is the axial angular momentum
$$
J_n=n\cdot(q\times\dot q).
$$
If $L$ also has no explicit time dependence, the energy $|\dot q|^2/2+V(q)$ is conserved independently.

= Double pendulum
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}
{wiki}

A double pendulum consists of two pendula coupled because the second pivot moves with the first mass.

= Double pendulum Lagrangian
{parent=Double pendulum}

The kinetic energy contains the coupling $ml_1l_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$, while gravity supplies one cosine potential for each mass height.

= Double pendulum equations of motion
{parent=Double pendulum}

The nonlinear Euler--Lagrange equations couple both angular accelerations and contain centrifugal terms quadratic in angular velocity.

= Kinetic energy from Cartesian position vectors
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

For point masses with generalized coordinates, kinetic energy follows by differentiating their Cartesian position vectors and summing $m_i|\dot r_i|^2/2$.

= Small oscillation
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

Near a stable equilibrium, retaining quadratic Lagrangian terms gives a linear system $\mathsf M\ddot z+\mathsf Kz=0$.

The resulting linearized motion decomposes into <normal modes> near the stable equilibrium.

= Stable equilibrium in Lagrangian mechanics
{parent=Small oscillation}

At an equilibrium, a positive-definite <Hessian matrix> of the potential gives stable <small oscillations>. If a continuous symmetry supplies zero modes, positive definiteness on the directions transverse to the symmetry gives stability of the relative configuration.

= Angular frequency
{parent=Small oscillation}
{wiki}

Angular frequency measures phase advance per unit time. For $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$ it is $\omega=2\pi/T$, where $T$ is the period.

= Two vertically suspended springs
{parent=Small oscillation}

For two masses hung in series from springs of natural length $\ell$, the lower equilibrium spring tension is $m_2g$ and the upper tension is $(m_1+m_2)g$. Hence their equilibrium lengths are
$$
L_2=\ell+\frac{m_2g}{k_2},
\qquad
L_1=\ell+\frac{(m_1+m_2)g}{k_1}.
$$

= Vertical normal modes of two equal suspended masses
{parent=Two vertically suspended springs}

For equal masses $m$ and spring constants $k$, vertical displacements from equilibrium have stiffness and mass matrices
$$
K=k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad M=mI.
$$
The two squared frequencies and displacement ratios are
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\frac{k}{2m}(3\pm\sqrt5),
\qquad
\frac{\eta_2}{\eta_1}=\frac{1\mp\sqrt5}{2}.
$$
Gravity shifts the equilibrium but does not enter these vertical frequencies.

= Small-oscillation mass and stiffness matrices
{parent=Small oscillation}

The Hessians of kinetic and potential energy at equilibrium give the symmetric mass matrix $\mathsf M$ and stiffness matrix $\mathsf K$.

= Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations
{parent=Small oscillation}

Normal frequencies satisfy $\det(\mathsf K-\Omega^2\mathsf M)=0$, and each null vector gives the fixed amplitude ratio of a mode.

= Mass-matrix orthogonality of normal modes
{parent=Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations}

For symmetric stiffness matrix $K$ and positive-definite mass matrix $M$, eigenvectors $a_i,a_j$ of
$$
Ka=\omega^2Ma
$$
with distinct squared frequencies satisfy
$$
a_i^TMa_j=0.
$$
Thus normal modes are orthogonal in the kinetic-energy inner product.

= Normal modes of a double pendulum
{parent=Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations}

For equal lengths and mass ratio $\mu=m/M$, the squared frequencies are $\omega_0^2(1+\mu\pm\sqrt{\mu(1+\mu)})$.

= Square-root splitting of nearly degenerate frequencies
{parent=Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations}

A weakly coupled degenerate pair can split by order $\sqrt\mu$ when the small parameter enters a singular mass or stiffness matrix.

= Degenerate normal modes from permutation symmetry
{parent=Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations}

If the mass and stiffness matrices commute with a <permutation representation>, each symmetry subspace is invariant under the <generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations>. On an irreducible subspace, <Schur lemma> forces a commuting operator to be scalar, producing a degenerate <normal mode> multiplet.

= Normal modes of three equal masses with a symmetric gap potential
{parent=Degenerate normal modes from permutation symmetry}

Let three equal masses move on a circle of radius $r$, and let the potential be $U(\alpha)+U(\beta)+U(\gamma)$ with $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=2\pi$. At equal spacing $s_0=2\pi/3$, angular displacements have a zero-frequency rigid-rotation mode $(1,1,1)$ and a two-dimensional sum-zero eigenspace with
$$
\omega^2=\frac{3U''(s_0)}{mr^2}.
$$
The two vibrational modes are degenerate by the permutation symmetry.

= Normal modes of three symmetrically coupled trapped masses
{parent=Degenerate normal modes from permutation symmetry}

For three unit masses with unit springs between every pair and one unit spring anchoring each mass to the origin,
$$
M=I,\qquad K=4I-J,
$$
where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. The collective mode $(1,1,1)$ has angular frequency $1$, while the two-dimensional sum-zero subspace has angular frequency $2$.

= Normal modes of two equal masses between three springs
{parent=Generalized eigenvalue problem for small oscillations}

For equal masses $m$, wall-spring constants $k_1,k_2$, and coupling-spring constant $k_3$, the <small-oscillation mass and stiffness matrices> are
$$
\mathsf M=mI,
\qquad
\mathsf K=
\begin{pmatrix}
k_1+k_3&-k_3\\
-k_3&k_2+k_3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
If $k_1=k(1+\varepsilon\delta)$, $k_2=k(1-\varepsilon\delta)$, and $k_3=k\varepsilon$, the squared normal frequencies are
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\frac{k}{m}
\left[1+\varepsilon\left(1\pm\sqrt{1+\delta^2}\right)\right].
$$

= Conservation of energy from time-translation invariance
{parent=Lagrangian mechanics}

A Lagrangian with no explicit time dependence conserves $E=\sum_i\dot q_i\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_i-L$.

= Center of mass
{parent=Classical mechanics}
{wiki}

= Centres of mass
{synonym}

= Centre of mass
{synonym}

For masses $m_i$ at positions $\mathbf r_i$, the center of mass is $\mathbf R=(\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i)/(\sum_i m_i)$, with the analogous mass-density integral for a continuous body.

= Centre of mass after removing material
{parent=Center of mass}
{title2=$\mathbf R=(M_0\mathbf R_0-M_c\mathbf R_c)/(M_0-M_c)$}

Suppose a body of <mass> $M_0$ has <centre of mass> $\mathbf R_0$, and material of <mass> $M_c<M_0$ with <centre of mass> $\mathbf R_c$ is removed. Additivity of the first <mass> moment gives the remaining <centre of mass>
$$
\mathbf R=\frac{M_0\mathbf R_0-M_c\mathbf R_c}{M_0-M_c}.
$$
When the original <centre of mass> is at the origin, the remaining <centre of mass> points opposite the removed material's first <mass> moment. For a constrained <rigid body> rotating about a fixed axis, this displaced <centre of mass> undergoes <centripetal acceleration> even if the applied axial <torque> is zero.

= Center-of-mass motion
{parent=Center of mass}

The center of mass moves as if the total external force acted on the total mass; internal forces cancel in its equation.

= Center-of-mass reduction
{parent=Center-of-mass motion}

Center-of-mass reduction separates translational motion from internal coordinates by using the conserved or externally forced center coordinate.

= Kinetic-energy decomposition about the centre of mass
{parent=Center-of-mass reduction}
{title2=$T=\tfrac12M|\dot{\mathbf R}|^2+\tfrac12\sum_i m_i|\dot{\mathbf y}_i|^2$}

For fixed particle <masses> and positive total <mass>, write $\mathbf x_i=\mathbf R+\mathbf y_i$ with $\mathbf R$ the <centre of mass>. The weighted relative <velocities> sum to zero, so expanding the <kinetic energy> eliminates the mixed term and yields the displayed decomposition. For a <rigid body>, the relative contribution is the <rotational kinetic energy> $I\omega^2/2$ about the chosen centre-of-mass axis.

= Jacobi coordinates
{c}
{parent=Center-of-mass reduction}
{wiki}

For particles with masses $m_j$ and positions $r_j$, <Jacobi coordinates> consist of the total <center of mass> and successive relative coordinates $\rho_j=r_{j+1}-(\sum_{l=1}^j m_l r_l)/(\sum_{l=1}^j m_l)$. They separate translation from internal motion and diagonalize the <kinetic energy> with the corresponding reduced masses.

= Jacobi coordinates for three particles
{c}
{parent=Jacobi coordinates}

For three particles, choose the relative position of particles one and two, the position of their center of mass relative to particle three, and the total center of mass. The kinetic energy diagonalizes with masses
$$
\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2},
\qquad
\frac{(m_1+m_2)m_3}{m_1+m_2+m_3},
\qquad
m_1+m_2+m_3.
$$

= Angular momentum decomposition in three-body Jacobi coordinates
{parent=Jacobi coordinates for three particles}

For Jacobi coordinates $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$ and their corresponding reduced masses $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$, total angular momentum is
$$
\mathbf L
=\alpha\mathbf a\times\dot{\mathbf a}
+\beta\mathbf b\times\dot{\mathbf b}
+\gamma\mathbf c\times\dot{\mathbf c}.
$$

= Collective rotation and gap coordinates on a circle
{title2=$\Phi$}
{parent=Center-of-mass reduction}

For three ordered angular positions $q_1,q_2,q_3$, define consecutive gaps
$$
\alpha=q_2-q_1,
\qquad
\beta=q_3-q_2,
\qquad
\gamma=2\pi-q_3+q_1
$$
and the collective angle $\Phi=(q_1+q_2+q_3)/3$. Then $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=2\pi$. For three equal masses on a circle of radius $r$,
$$
T=\frac{3mr^2}{2}\dot\Phi^2
+\frac{mr^2}{3}
\left(\dot\alpha^2+\dot\alpha\dot\beta+\dot\beta^2\right).
$$
Thus the <ignorable coordinate> $\Phi$ decouples from the relative gap dynamics whenever the potential depends only on the gaps.

= Reduced mass
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Center of mass}
{wiki}

For masses $m_1,m_2$, the relative coordinate obeys a one-body equation with reduced mass
$$
\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.
$$

= Uniform gravitational field
{parent=Classical mechanics}

In a uniform gravitational field, every freely falling center of mass has constant acceleration $-g$.

= Normal coordinate
{parent=Classical mechanics}

A normal coordinate diagonalizes the linearized kinetic and potential forms so that its motion decouples as a harmonic oscillator.

= Antisymmetric molecular vibration
{parent=Normal coordinate}

In an antisymmetric vibration of a symmetric linear triatomic molecule, the outer atoms move oppositely and the central atom does not couple to that mode.

= Principle of virtual work
{synonym}

Virtual work is the work of applied forces through an infinitesimal displacement compatible with the system's instantaneous constraints. Equilibrium or overdamped force balance requires the total virtual work to vanish for every admissible virtual displacement.
