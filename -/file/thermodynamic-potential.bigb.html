= Thermodynamic potential
{wiki}

A <state function> used to describe a <thermodynamic system> with a chosen set of natural variables. <Internal energy>, <enthalpy>, <Helmholtz free energy>, and <Gibbs free energy> use different variables and serve different environmental constraints.

= Natural variables of a thermodynamic potential
{parent=Thermodynamic potential}

Natural variables are the independent variables appearing in the differential that directly identifies a potential's conjugate quantities. For $U(S,V,N)$, $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV+\mu\,dN$. <Legendre transforms> exchange a variable for its conjugate: $F=U-TS$ has natural variables $T,V,N$, and $G=F+pV$ has $T,p,N$. Equilibrium minimum principles require the corresponding environmental constraints and fixed conserved amounts.

= Exergy
{parent=Thermodynamic potential}
{wiki}
{title2=$A-A_{\mathrm{eq}}$}

= Availability
{synonym}

For a <closed system> with fixed composition in an environment at <temperature> $T_R>0$ and <pressure> $p_R$, define $A=U+p_RV-T_RS$, where $U$ is <internal energy>, $V$ is <volume (thermodynamics)> and $S$ is <entropy>. The <exergy> is this <availability> relative to the equilibrium reference state, $A-A_{\mathrm{eq}}$. When the combined system and environment are isolated except for the specified exchanges, $\Delta S_{\mathrm{tot}}=-\Delta A/T_R$, so the <second law of thermodynamics> makes $A$ decrease toward its constrained minimum. Away from equilibrium, the environmental $T_R,p_R$ must not be replaced by the system's instantaneous values.

= Availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations
{parent=Exergy}
{title2=$\langle(x-x_0)^2\rangle\simeq k_BT_R/A''(x_0)$}

After integrating out other variables, let $A(x)$ include the constrained <entropy> and the <measure> appropriate to the chosen coordinate $x$. Its equilibrium <probability density function> is proportional to $e^{-A(x)/(k_BT_R)}$. Near a stable interior minimum, $A(x)=A(x_0)+A''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2/2+\cdots$ with $A''(x_0)>0$. The resulting <normal distribution> has <variance> $k_BT_R/A''(x_0)$. This is the leading <Gaussian fluctuation approximation>; a coordinate-dependent <measure> contributes to the effective $A$, and higher-order terms matter near a vanishing curvature.

= Volume fluctuations of a small fluid parcel
{parent=Availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations}
{title2=$\langle\delta V^2\rangle=k_BT\kappa_T\bar V$}

For a material parcel of fixed amount inside a much larger thermal and mechanical environment, <availability> is $A(V)=F(V,T)+p_{\rm bath}V$. Since $F_V=-p$, its curvature is $A_{VV}=-p_V=1/(V\kappa_T)$. The <Gaussian fluctuation approximation> gives $\langle\delta V^2\rangle=k_BT\kappa_T\bar V$. This describes parcel-volume fluctuations, not the geometric <volume> of a fixed observation box or a globally rigid vessel. A fixed spatial box instead has number fluctuations related to the same compressibility.

= Thermodynamic free energy
{parent=Thermodynamic potential}
{wiki}

= Free energy
{synonym}

A <free energy> is a <thermodynamic potential> that compares possible states under specified environmental constraints. For a <closed system> of fixed composition, treated as a <simple compressible system> with only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>, stable <thermodynamic equilibrium> minimizes <Helmholtz free energy> at fixed <temperature> and <volume (thermodynamics)>, or <Gibbs free energy> at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>. The choice follows the experiment: a rigid sealed vessel and a sealed cylinder with a freely moving loaded piston impose different constraints. Both may exchange <heat> with a <thermal reservoir>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-00-thermodynamics-of-materials-fall-2002/14c95abd6fac023d84bd33c5d74d787d_lecture_02_oneside.PDF[W. C. Carter, MIT thermodynamics notes, sections 2-1.14 and 2-1.19].

\Image[thermodynamic-potential-constraints.png]
{title=Choose <Helmholtz free energy> for fixed <temperature> and <volume (thermodynamics)>, or <Gibbs free energy> for fixed <temperature> and <pressure>}
{description=Original schematic of two closed vessels in contact with a <thermal reservoir>. Composition is fixed, and only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work> is allowed. The right piston permits the <volume (thermodynamics)> to change.}

The useful insight is that <internal energy> alone cannot rank these alternatives: exchanging <heat> also changes the surroundings' <entropy>. Subtracting $TS$ packages that accounting into a property of the chosen system. Adding $pV$ additionally accounts for displacing the surroundings at fixed <pressure>. A <free energy> is a <state function>; supplied <heat> describes a transfer during a process. Under the stated constraints, supplied <heat> is $Q=\Delta U$ at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> or $Q=\Delta H$ at fixed <pressure>, whereas neither $\Delta F$ nor $\Delta G$ generally equals $Q$.

A lower <free energy> identifies a thermodynamic preference, without specifying a timescale. A barrier can trap a system in a <local minimum>, producing <metastability> although a lower <global minimum> exists. The <enzyme> example illustrates the corresponding distinction for <chemical reactions>: changing the route can change the <reaction rate> while preserving the endpoint <Gibbs free energy> difference and <chemical equilibrium>.

= Free-energy functional
{parent=Thermodynamic free energy}
{title2=$\mathcal A[m]$}

A <free-energy functional> assigns a <free energy> to an entire spatial <order parameter> configuration rather than only to one uniform value. In <Landau-Ginzburg theory>, a <local derivative expansion> gives $\mathcal A[m]=\int d^Dx\{\kappa(\nabla m)^2/2+V(m)\}$, with appropriate boundary terms and regular backgrounds. The <Landau approximation> minimizes this functional; a fluctuating <statistical field theory> instead integrates its <Boltzmann weight> over configurations. These procedures need not give identical <critical exponents>.

= Helmholtz energy
{c}
{synonym}

The <Helmholtz free energy> is
$$
F=U-TS,
$$
where $U$ is <internal energy>, also denoted $E$ elsewhere here, $T$ is absolute <temperature>, and $S$ is <entropy>. For the <closed system> of fixed composition and only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work> described above, spontaneous relaxation at fixed <temperature> and <volume (thermodynamics)> cannot increase $F$. Stable <thermodynamic equilibrium> is a <global minimum> over the permitted alternatives, including different <thermodynamic phases>. A rigid vessel fixes the total <volume (thermodynamics)>; it does not require each coexisting <thermodynamic phase> to retain its own <volume (thermodynamics)>.

For a constructed comparison at $300\,\mathrm K$, suppose one candidate has $1.5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ more <internal energy> but $6\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ more <entropy> than another at the same <temperature> and <volume (thermodynamics)>. Its difference is
$$
\Delta F=1500-300(6)=-300\,\mathrm J.
$$
Thus the candidate with higher <internal energy> has lower <Helmholtz free energy>. This is the practical reason for retaining the <entropy> term when selecting a state.

For equilibrium states within a smooth branch of fixed composition, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$, hence
$$
dF=-S\,dT-p\,dV,\qquad
S=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_V,\qquad
p=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_T.
$$
These <derivatives> extract <entropy> and <pressure>; the differential is not an evolution equation for relaxation. If <chemical species> amounts $n_i$ vary, add $\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i$, with molar <chemical potentials> $\mu_i$. A <closed system> can still change <chemical species> amounts through <chemical reactions>; the fixed-composition simplification then needs the conserved-inventory constraints of <chemical equilibrium>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-60-thermodynamics-kinetics-spring-2008/e763a06aa3b5de09cd6bfea6280f219c_5_60_lecture14.pdf[MIT 5.60, Lecture 14, fundamental equations and chemical potentials].

The minimum principle has a short explanation. At fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>, no boundary <work> occurs, so $Q=\Delta U$. A <thermal reservoir> at positive <temperature> $T$ loses <entropy> $Q/T$. Consequently
$$
\Delta S_{\rm total}=\Delta S-\frac{\Delta U}{T}=-\frac{\Delta F}{T}\geq0,
$$
by the <second law of thermodynamics>. Here both endpoint states share the imposed <temperature>. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-60-thermodynamics-kinetics-spring-2008/64efc6f359b20c43dab61fc77791d744_5_60_lecture12.pdf[MIT 5.60, Lecture 12, criteria for spontaneous change].

The microscopic counterpart is $F=-k_BT\ln Z$ in the <canonical ensemble>, with <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$ and <partition function> $Z$. The <partition function> examples compute the balance between <internal energy> and <entropy>; the <chemical equilibrium> example already displays an ideal-mixture <Helmholtz free energy> minimum.

= Gibbs energy
{c}
{synonym}

The <Gibbs free energy> is
$$
G=U-TS+pV=H-TS,\qquad H=U+pV,
$$
where $H$ is <enthalpy>. For a <closed system> of fixed composition, with only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>, spontaneous relaxation at fixed <temperature> and <pressure> cannot increase $G$; stable <thermodynamic equilibrium> minimizes it over the allowed states. A loaded piston can move while maintaining the imposed <pressure>, so the <enthalpy> term includes the displacement cost. Along a smooth equilibrium branch of fixed composition,
$$
dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp.
$$
Thus its <temperature> slope at fixed <pressure> is $-S$, while its <pressure> slope at fixed <temperature> is $V$. See https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-60-thermodynamics-kinetics-spring-2008/7bc33304ab1db561a64b4a6b12e25476_5_60_lecture13.pdf[MIT 5.60, Lecture 13, Gibbs free energy and its derivatives].

Consider two illustrative <thermodynamic phases>, A and B, of the same substance at one fixed <pressure>. Write B minus A, using quantities per <mole>:
$$
\Delta H=12\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta S=40\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}=0.040\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$
Treat these differences as approximately constant over a limited <temperature> window. Then
$$
\frac{\Delta G(T)}{\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}=12-0.040\frac{T}{\mathrm K},\qquad
T_* =\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}=300\,\mathrm K.
$$
At $280\,\mathrm K$, $\Delta G=+0.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, so A is preferred. At $320\,\mathrm K$, $\Delta G=-0.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, so B is preferred. At $300\,\mathrm K$, both have equal molar <Gibbs free energy>, permitting <phase coexistence> in this bulk model. B's greater <entropy> outweighs its greater <enthalpy> above the crossing.

\Image[free-energy-temperature-crossing.png]
{title=Illustrative molar <Gibbs free energy> difference crosses zero at $300\,\mathrm K$}
{description=Constructed B-minus-A values: $+0.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $280\,\mathrm K$ and $-0.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at $320\,\mathrm K$. The constants approximate a limited <temperature> window and represent no measured substance.}

Each plotted point compares alternatives at the same <temperature>; moving along the curve changes the imposed <temperature>. The model predicts preference, not instantaneous conversion: <nucleation> barriers can sustain <metastability>. Real <enthalpy> and <entropy> differences generally vary with <temperature> through <heat capacities>, bending this line outside the approximation.

At fixed <pressure>, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $Q=\Delta H$ under the stated <work> restriction, so the same <thermal reservoir> accounting yields $\Delta S_{\rm total}=-\Delta G/T\geq0$. Variable composition restores $\sum_i\mu_i\,dn_i$ to $dG$; <constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium> handles those permitted changes.

The historical foundation is Gibbs's https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Scientific_Papers_of_Josiah_Willard_Gibbs,_Volume_1/Chapter_IIIa[On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances, first part completed in 1876, continued in 1878]{ref}. Its equations (87) and (91) introduce the combinations now called <Helmholtz free energy> and <Gibbs free energy>, connecting <entropy> criteria, <chemical potentials> and <phase coexistence> within one framework.

= Single-component Gibbs Euler relation
{parent=Gibbs free energy}
{c}

For a homogeneous extensive simple-compressible phase, $U=TS-pV+\mu N$ implies $G=\mu N$. A mixture instead obeys $G=\sum_i\mu_iN_i$. Surface and other nonextensive contributions require extra terms.

= Fixed-particle-number Gibbs minimum
{parent=Gibbs free energy}
{c}

At imposed <temperature> and <pressure>, with fixed species amounts and only pressure-volume work, increasing total system-plus-reservoir <entropy> is equivalent to decreasing $G=U-TS+pV$. Unconstrained particle exchange requires the reservoir <chemical potentials> to be included; minimizing $G$ over arbitrary particle number is a different problem.

= Gibbs–Helmholtz equation
{parent=Gibbs free energy}
{c}
{title2=$\partial(G/T)/\partial T=-H/T^2$}

For fixed composition and pressure, $\partial(G/T)/\partial T=-H/T^2$. Applying the identity to standard reaction quantities connects equilibrium temperature dependence to reaction enthalpy.

= Transfer Gibbs energy
{parent=Gibbs free energy}
{title2=$\Delta G^\circ_{\mathrm{tr}}$}

The standard free-energy change for moving a species between specified phases or solvents. For a consistently standardized equilibrium distribution coefficient, $\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K$. Pure-solid lattice dissolution is a separate thermodynamic step.

= Gibbs free-energy change for a pressure-linear gas correction
{parent=Gibbs free energy}
{title2=$\Delta G=Nk_BT\ln(p_1/p_0)-NA(T)(p_1-p_0)$}

For the <equation of state> $V=Nk_BT/p-NA(T)$, integrate $(\partial G/\partial p)_{T,N}=V$ along an isotherm. This gives the displayed <Gibbs free energy> difference. Its negative <temperature> <derivative> at fixed endpoint pressures gives $\Delta S=-Nk_B\ln(p_1/p_0)+NA'(T)(p_1-p_0)$. The physically admissible branch must retain positive <volume (thermodynamics)>.

= Gibbs free energy of an ideal-gas mixture
{c}
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

For a single-phase <ideal gas> mixture with molar amounts $N_i$, common <temperature> $T$, and <pressure> $P$, extensivity and ideal mixing give
$$
G=\sum_iN_i\mu_i=N\sum_i x_i\left[\mu_i^\circ(T)+\mathcal RT\log\left(\frac{x_iP}{P^\circ}\right)\right].
$$
Here $N=\sum_iN_i$, $x_i$ is the <mole fraction>, $\mathcal R$ is the molar gas constant, and $P^\circ$ is the standard <pressure>. Integrating $\partial\mu_i/\partial P=\mathcal RT/P$ and including the ideal mixing <entropy> proves $\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+\mathcal RT\log(x_iP/P^\circ)$. Reactions can change $N$, so fractions alone do not set the extensive <Gibbs free energy>. Its composition Hessian is $\mathcal RT(\delta_{ij}/N_i-1/N)$; it is positive semidefinite by the weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

= Gibbs free-energy Maxwell relation
{c}
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

Equality of mixed derivatives of $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP$ gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T
=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P.
$$

= Chemical-potential balance for a reaction
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

For a reaction with stoichiometric changes $\nu_i$, varying the reaction extent at fixed temperature and pressure gives
$$
dG=\left(\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i\right)d\xi.
$$
Equilibrium requires $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=0$.

= Thermochemical equilibrium
{parent=Chemical-potential balance for a reaction}

Thermochemical equilibrium requires every chemical reaction to have zero Gibbs free-energy change. For ideal reactants, the equilibrium constant is $K=\exp[-\Delta_rG^\circ/(RT)]$ after standard-state activity factors are included.

= Constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium
{parent=Thermochemical equilibrium}

At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, a closed reacting system reaches <thermochemical equilibrium> by minimizing <Gibbs free energy> over nonnegative species amounts subject to conserved elemental abundances. For an allowed <stoichiometric vector> $\nu$, an interior minimum has $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=0$. More generally the <chemical potential> vector lies in the row span of the elemental conservation matrix at positive concentrations, with the appropriate one-sided inequalities for absent species. The <first law of thermodynamics> gives the thermodynamic differential, while the <second law of thermodynamics> supplies the minimization direction.

= Reverse reaction rate from equilibrium thermodynamics
{parent=Thermochemical equilibrium}

For an elementary reversible reaction, detailed balance gives $k_f\prod_r n_r^{\nu_r}=k_r\prod_p n_p^{\nu_p}$ at equilibrium. Hence the reverse rate coefficient is the forward coefficient divided by the equilibrium constant, with units fixed by the reaction stoichiometry.

= Phase coexistence curve
{parent=Gibbs free energy}

Two phases coexist where their molar Gibbs free energies agree. Differentiating that equality along the coexistence curve relates its slope to the entropy and volume jumps.

= Kelvin equation
{c}
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}
{wiki}
{title2=$\ln(p/p_{\rm sat})\simeq2\Gamma v_l/(Rk_BT)$}

In the ideal-vapor, incompressible-liquid capillarity approximation, a spherical droplet of radius $R$ has the displayed equilibrium vapor-pressure relation. Here $v_l$ is liquid volume per molecule, $\Gamma$ is <surface tension>, and $p_{\rm sat}$ is the flat-interface saturation pressure at the same <temperature>. The <Young-Laplace equation> raises liquid pressure by $2\Gamma/R$, increasing its <chemical potential> by approximately $2\Gamma v_l/R$. Matching this with the ideal-vapor chemical-potential increase $k_BT\ln(p/p_{\rm sat})$ proves the formula. The liquid contribution from the vapor-pressure change itself is neglected relative to its curvature pressure, and bulk properties are taken independent of radius. A convex small drop therefore requires a higher ambient vapor pressure than a flat interface.

= Capillary condensation
{parent=Kelvin equation}

Condensation in a <wetting> confined geometry at a <vapor pressure> below planar-interface saturation. A concave <liquid> meniscus has lower equilibrium <vapor pressure> in the capillarity approximation. Gap size, <contact angle> and surface <adsorption> affect the actual onset.

= Triple point
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}

A triple point is a <temperature>–<pressure> pair at which three distinct bulk <thermodynamic phases> of a one-component substance coexist in <thermodynamic equilibrium>. In the familiar <solid>–<liquid>–<gas> case, their three <phase coexistence curves> meet. Other combinations, such as two <crystal polymorphs> and a <liquid>, also qualify: “triple” counts <thermodynamic phases>, rather than states-of-matter categories.

For the same substance, the <chemical potential> in each <thermodynamic phase> must agree:
$$
\mu_s(T_t,p_t)=\mu_l(T_t,p_t)=\mu_g(T_t,p_t).
$$
There are two independent equality conditions for two controls, <temperature> and <pressure>. Generically they select an isolated pair $(T_t,p_t)$; the third pairwise equality follows from the first two. Equivalently, the <phase rule> gives $F=1-3+2=0$. This counts independent intensive controls, not <phase fractions>: changing the amounts of the three <thermodynamic phases> need not change the ideal bulk <temperature> and <pressure>, provided all three remain present. Different sets of <crystal polymorphs> can give different triple points for the same substance.<Gibbs Phase Rule (MIT 10.213 course material)>[Gibbs Phase Rule, three-phase count and pressure–temperature diagram discussion]{ref}

At a <liquid-gas critical point>, the two fluid <thermodynamic phases> instead merge, and their <mass density> difference and <latent heat> vanish. It is the endpoint of the <liquid>–<gas> <phase coexistence curve>, rather than a meeting of three distinct <thermodynamic phases>. The regular independent-equality count used for a triple point cannot explain this merger. A triple point can retain finite <mass density> and <entropy> differences between all three <thermodynamic phases>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.2, printed p. 141: vanishing liquid–gas discontinuity at the critical point; Figure 41 separates it from the three-phase junction]{ref}

= Triple-point cell
{parent=Triple point}

A triple-point cell turns <phase coexistence> into a reproducible reference for <thermometer calibration>. The NIST water-cell demonstration uses a sealed glass vessel containing <water> of specified <isotopic composition> and an open central thermometer well. Cooling this well with <solid> <carbon dioxide> and alcohol freezes an <ice> mantle while leaving <liquid> <water> and its vapor. The coolant is outside the sealed sample; the sample's vapor space does not communicate with room air.<Triple-Point Cell (NIST demonstration)>[Triple-Point Cell, preparation description, uploaded 21 December 2016]{ref}

In the actual NIST cell-comparison experiment, newly frozen mantles were compared with an aged reference mantle in a controlled maintenance bath. A warm aluminium rod melted a thin inner layer around the well, allowing the mantle to rotate freely and establishing an <ice>–<water> interface close to the sensor. A <standard platinum resistance thermometer> and a <resistance bridge> measured the <electrical resistance> ratio to a calibrated, temperature-controlled reference resistor. Readings at two currents permitted extrapolation to zero electrical <power>, correcting self-heating; measured immersion depths supplied <hydrostatic pressure> corrections. Comparing preparations over time tested stabilization rather than assuming freshly frozen <ice> was ready.<Effects of different methods of preparation of ice mantles of triple point of water cells on the temporal behaviour of the triple-point temperatures by G. T. Furukawa and others>[Furukawa, Mangum and Strouse, Effects of different methods of preparation of ice mantles of triple point of water cells on the temporal behaviour of the triple-point temperatures (1997), Abstract, §1 and §§3.2–3.5: aged reference, inner melt, bridge readout, zero-power and hydrostatic corrections]{ref}

The inference is a reproducible fixed-point response for the <thermometer>, not a direct reading of three <chemical potentials>. The reference cell anchors the comparison; a water point alone does not determine a <thermometer>'s response at every other <temperature>. The ideal $F=0$ argument excludes impurity and <isotope> variation, finite interface effects and unintended <heat transfer>; real cells also require adequate immersion and stable electrical references. Repeatability is evidence that these effects are controlled, not a reason to omit their uncertainty.

Since 20 May 2019 the <kelvin> has been defined through a fixed numerical value of the <Boltzmann constant>. The thermodynamic <temperature> of the <water> triple point is consequently a measured quantity with uncertainty, rather than an exact value imposed by the <kelvin> definition. Its continued use as an assigned reference on the <International Temperature Scale of 1990> is a separate convention.<On the revision of the International System of Units (SI), 26th CGPM Resolution 1 (2018)>[Resolution 1 of the 26th CGPM (2018), effective date; Appendix 2, third bullet; Appendix 3, kelvin definition]{ref}<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[§2.3.1: practical temperature scales and ITS-90]{ref}

= Saturation pressure
{synonym}

The equilibrium <pressure> of a vapor in contact with its <liquid> or <solid> at a specified <temperature>. For pure <liquid>–<gas> <phase coexistence> with negligible interface effects, it is the <pressure> on their <phase coexistence curve>. In a <gas> <mixture>, distinguish the component's <partial pressure> from the total <pressure>; the pure-substance <integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation> does not describe the total <pressure> of arbitrary humid air.

= Antoine equation
{parent=Vapor pressure}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\log_{10}p_{\rm sat}=A-B/(T+C)$}

The Antoine equation is an empirical <vapor pressure> fit whose coefficients depend on the pressure and temperature units and on a stated validity interval. Its slope is $d\ln p_{\rm sat}/dT=\ln(10)B/(T+C)^2$. In the <ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation>, this implies <latent heat> $L=R_v\ln(10)B[T/(T+C)]^2$. An accurate pressure fit need not give an equally accurate derivative; extrapolation to the <liquid-gas critical point> cannot replace the full <Clausius-Clapeyron relation>.

= Maxwell construction
{c}
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}

= Maxwell equal-area construction
{c}
{synonym}

At fixed <temperature>, choose a coexistence <pressure> $p_*$ such that the gas and liquid have equal <chemical potentials>. Because $d\mu=v\,dp$, this gives $\int_{v_l}^{v_g}v\,dp=0$, or equivalently $\int_{v_l}^{v_g}[p(v,T)-p_*]\,dv=0$. The equal areas replace the unstable part of an isotherm by a horizontal coexistence segment.

= Maxwell construction in density coordinates
{parent=Maxwell construction}
{c}

The equal-area condition is naturally $\int(p-p_{\rm coex})\,dv=0$ in volume per particle $v$. In number density $n=1/v$, it becomes $\int_{n_g}^{n_l}[p(n)-p_{\rm coex}]\,dn/n^2=0$. Unweighted area in a <pressure>-versus-density plot is not the <Maxwell construction>.

= Phase diagram
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}
{wiki}

A phase diagram partitions a space of thermodynamic variables into regions occupied by different equilibrium <thermodynamic phases> and records the <phase coexistence curves> between them. For a pure, one-component substance, a <pressure>–<temperature> <phase diagram> gives one point for each pair $(T,p)$. In a single-<thermodynamic phase> region both controls can vary independently. On a two-<thermodynamic phase> <phase coexistence curve>, they obey one relation; at a <triple point>, both are fixed. These statements assume bulk <thermodynamic equilibrium>, fixed composition and no additional applied fields.<Gibbs Phase Rule (MIT 10.213 course material)>[Gibbs Phase Rule, single-, two- and three-phase counts and final pressure–temperature diagram paragraphs]{ref}

At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, the stable bulk <thermodynamic phase> minimizes <Gibbs free energy> for a fixed <amount of substance>. For a pure substance this is a comparison of <chemical potentials> on a common normalization. Two candidate <thermodynamic phases> coexist where $\mu_\alpha(T,p)=\mu_\beta(T,p)$ and neither is exceeded in stability by a third <thermodynamic phase>. Differentiating their equality gives the <Clapeyron equation>. Thus a <phase coexistence curve> is a locus of equal stability, rather than a trajectory that every heated sample must follow.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.1, equations (5.3)–(5.4), printed p. 137: chemical-potential equality; §5.1.2, equation (5.6), printed pp. 140–141: coexistence slope]{ref}

The following original cartoon uses dimensionless positive coordinates $\theta=T/T_*$ and $\pi=p/p_*$, with arbitrary reference scales $T_*,p_*$. It represents no particular substance and contains no measured data. Stipulate a <triple point> at $(1,1)$, a <liquid-gas critical point> at $(3,5)$, and these drawing rules:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi_{\rm sub}(\theta)&=e^{\theta-1},&&0<\theta\le1,\\
\pi_{\rm vap}(\theta)&=2\theta-1,&&1\le\theta\le3,\\
\theta_{\rm melt}(\pi)&=1+0.1(\pi-1),&&\pi\ge1.
\end{aligned}
$$
They specify <sublimation>, <liquid>–<gas> <phase coexistence> and <melting> boundaries for the picture. They are not an <equation of state>, do not predict <latent heat> or <mass density>, and do not supply a quantitative critical theory. In particular, the <melting> slope is merely an illustrative choice.

{title=Generic dimensionless <pressure>–<temperature> <phase diagram>: curves, <triple point> and <liquid-gas critical point>}
{description=Original cartoon with stipulated drawing rules. Three distinct bulk <thermodynamic phases> coexist at $(1,1)$; the <liquid>–<gas> boundary ends at $(3,5)$. The fluid region beyond that endpoint is connected: no extra boundary is implied by the fading background. These coordinates are not <water> data.}

For example, at fixed $\pi=3$ the <melting> boundary is at $\theta=1.2$ and the <liquid>–<gas> boundary is at $\theta=2$. Heating from $(\theta,\pi)=(1.6,3)$ to $(3.6,3)$ therefore starts in <liquid>, crosses <phase coexistence> at $(2,3)$, and ends in <gas>. In an equilibrium conversion at that crossing, added <heat> can change the <phase fraction> at fixed <temperature> through <latent heat>. The <pressure>–<temperature> point alone does not specify how much of each <thermodynamic phase> is present: for that one needs an additional amount or <volume (thermodynamics)> constraint.

Now connect exactly the same endpoints by raising $\pi$ from $3$ to $6$ at $\theta=1.6$, moving to $\theta=3.6$ at $\pi=6$, then lowering $\pi$ to $3$. The first leg stays on the <liquid> side because $\theta_{\rm melt}$ ranges only from $1.2$ to $1.5$ and $\pi_{\rm vap}(1.6)=2.2<3$. The second leg lies above the entire <liquid>–<gas> boundary, whose largest drawn <pressure> coordinate is $5$; the third lies beyond its largest <temperature> coordinate, $3$. No leg crosses a <first-order phase transition> boundary. The middle leg passes through a <supercritical fluid> region, with $\theta>3$ and $\pi>5$, and the final leg returns to a gaslike state continuously.

Avoiding a <first-order phase transition> does not mean that no <heat> or <work> is exchanged. Along the detour, <mass density>, <enthalpy> and <heat capacity> may change substantially but continuously. The <liquid-gas critical point> itself has singular behavior, so the detour goes around it rather than through it. This connected fluid region is the striking lesson of a <phase diagram>: a familiar change from <liquid> to <gas> need not involve boiling across a sharp boundary.

An equilibrium <phase diagram> also does not predict how rapidly a conversion occurs. <Metastability> and a barrier described by <classical nucleation theory> can delay the observed <phase transition>. Nor should a binary-composition <phase diagram> be read as the same picture: its coexistence regions and <lever rule> encode composition and <phase fractions>, and can occupy an area even though pure-substance two-<thermodynamic phase> coexistence occupies a curve in the <pressure>–<temperature> plane.

= Mineral activity diagram
{parent=Phase diagram}

A diagram of stable <mineral> assemblages as functions of specified aqueous <thermodynamic activities>, at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>. The coordinates often use logarithmic activity ratios. Mass-action equations give pairwise coexistence lines; a line segment is stable only when no omitted mineral has a lower relevant thermodynamic potential under the same imposed chemical potentials.

= Upper-envelope stability for competing mineral dissolution reactions
{parent=Mineral activity diagram}
{title2=$y=\max(\ell_G(x),\ell_K(x))$}

Suppose a reference <mineral> $C$ can dissolve to either $G$ or $K$, with the same conserved solid component, and normalized <Gibbs energies of reaction> $\Delta_rG_G/A=y-\ell_G(x)$ and $\Delta_rG_K/A=y-\ell_K(x)$ for $A>0$. Choose relative solid potentials $\Phi_C=0$, $\Phi_G=y-\ell_G$ and $\Phi_K=y-\ell_K$. The stable state minimizes $\Phi$. Thus $C$ is stable above $y=\max(\ell_G,\ell_K)$; $G$ is stable below $\ell_G$ where $\ell_G>\ell_K$; and $K$ is stable below $\ell_K$ where $\ell_K>\ell_G$. The $G$–$K$ coexistence line is stable below their triple intersection and <metastable> above it. These inequalities follow directly from the sign of each forward dissolution reaction. Reversing reaction directions reverses the corresponding sign convention, so one must establish the reaction direction before choosing an upper or lower envelope.

= Stability boundary in a mineral activity diagram
{parent=Mineral activity diagram}

A pairwise equilibrium line restricted to conditions where both minerals are stable against the other allowed phases. Compute the <Gibbs energy of reaction> for independent conversions to compare them. A pairwise line can continue into a region where a third <mineral> is favoured; this continuation is a metastable equilibrium relation, rather than a boundary of stable fields.

= Common-potential test for a three-mineral activity diagram
{parent=Stability boundary in a mineral activity diagram}
{title2=$\Phi_C=0,\quad\Phi_1=\ell_1(x)-y,\quad\Phi_2=\ell_2(x)-y$}

Suppose two dissolution reactions to a reference <mineral> $C$ have been normalized to the same amount of a conserved reference component. After division by the same positive energy factor, let their <Gibbs energies of reaction> be $y-\ell_1(x)$ and $y-\ell_2(x)$. Their relative thermodynamic potentials can then be written as displayed. The stable <mineral> has the smallest potential.

It follows that $C$ is stable below $y=\min(\ell_1,\ell_2)$, mineral 1 is stable where $\ell_1<\ell_2$ and $y>\ell_1$, and mineral 2 is stable where $\ell_2<\ell_1$ and $y>\ell_2$. The two nonreference minerals coexist stably where $\ell_1=\ell_2<y$. Their equality below that height is a <metastable> continuation. This comparison proves the stable lower envelope and selects which portions of the pairwise mass-action lines belong on the <mineral activity diagram>.

= Solvus
{parent=Phase diagram}

A boundary giving the equilibrium limit of one <solid solution> against coexistence with another solid phase. Moving along a <solvus> during cooling changes equilibrium solid compositions even after all liquid has disappeared.

= Lower critical solution temperature
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$T_c$}

= LCST
{synonym}
{c}

The lower endpoint of a two-liquid coexistence region. In the lower-critical case, demixing can be induced by heating above the endpoint.

= Upper critical solution temperature
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$T_c$}

= UCST
{synonym}
{c}

The upper endpoint of a two-liquid coexistence region in a temperature-composition diagram. In the ordinary upper-critical case, heating above this temperature removes liquid-liquid demixing.

= Phase rule
{parent=Phase diagram}
{wiki}
{title2=$F=C-P+2$}

= Gibbs phase rule
{c}
{synonym}

The <phase rule> counts independent intensive controls compatible with coexistence of $P$ distinct bulk <thermodynamic phases> made from $C$ independent components:
$$
F=C-P+2.
$$
Here $P$ counts <thermodynamic phases>, not <pressure>, and $F$ does not count <phase fractions>. For the nonreacting one-component case, $C=1$ and $F=3-P$: a single <thermodynamic phase> occupies a region with $F=2$, two-<thermodynamic phase> coexistence a curve with $F=1$, and a <triple point> an isolated point with $F=0$. Fixing <pressure> externally removes one adjustable control wherever that constraint is independent.<Gibbs Phase Rule (MIT 10.213 course material)>[Gibbs Phase Rule, displayed nonreacting rule and pure-substance table]{ref}

For an elementary count without <chemical reactions>, take $C$ <chemical species>. Each <thermodynamic phase> has $C-1$ independent composition fractions, since they sum to one. Shared <temperature> and <pressure> add two controls, giving $2+P(C-1)$. Equality of each species' <chemical potential> between one reference <thermodynamic phase> and the other $P-1$ <thermodynamic phases> supplies $C(P-1)$ constraints. Subtraction gives $2+P(C-1)-C(P-1)=C-P+2$. This argument assumes the constraints are independent and treats interfaces as negligible; <chemical reactions>, external fields or critical mergers require appropriate qualifications.

= Solidus
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$T_S(C)$}

The solidus bounds the entirely solid equilibrium region of a <phase diagram>. For a linear <liquidus> $T_L(C_l)=-mC_l$ and a constant <segregation coefficient> $C_s=k_D C_l$, the corresponding solidus is $T_S(C_s)=-mC_s/k_D$. The <concentration> argument is the solid <concentration>, not the neighboring liquid <concentration>.

= Lever rule
{parent=Phase diagram}
{title2=$\phi=(\overline C-C_s)/(C_l-C_s)$}
{wiki}

The lever rule finds phase fractions from a horizontal tie line on a <phase diagram>. For solid and liquid <concentrations> $C_s,C_l$ and bulk <concentration> $\overline C$, conservation gives $\overline C=(1-\phi)C_s+\phi C_l$. Thus $\phi=(\overline C-C_s)/(C_l-C_s)$ is the <liquid fraction>, provided $C_s\le\overline C\le C_l$. For pure solid, $\phi=\overline C/C_l$.

= Bicritical point
{parent=Phase diagram}

A bicritical point is where two continuous transition lines and a first-order line meet, typically separating a disordered phase from two competing ordered phases.

= Liquidus
{parent=Phase diagram}
{wiki}

The liquidus is the locus in a <phase diagram> above which the equilibrium state is entirely liquid. In a dilute binary solution it is often approximated locally by a linear relation such as $T_L=T_0-mC$.

= Freezing-point depression
{parent=Liquidus}
{wiki}

Freezing-point depression is the lowering of a solvent's equilibrium freezing temperature by dissolved solute. In the linear dilute approximation, $T_L=T_0-mC$ with $m>0$.

= Eutectic system
{parent=Phase diagram}
{wiki}

An eutectic system has a composition and temperature at which a liquid is in equilibrium with two distinct solid phases. Cooling liquid of that composition through the eutectic temperature produces both solid phases together.

= Eutectic solidification
{parent=Eutectic system}

The transformation $L\rightarrow\alpha+\beta$ in which one liquid produces two solid phases at a binary <eutectic composition> and <temperature> under fixed <pressure>. The resulting two-phase constituent must be distinguished from either individual <thermodynamic phase>.

= Eutectic point
{parent=Eutectic system}

The invariant liquid-plus-two-solids equilibrium of a binary <eutectic system> at fixed <pressure>. It specifies both the <eutectic temperature> and the <eutectic composition>.

= Eutectic microstructure
{parent=Eutectic system}

A two-solid <microstructure> formed by the coupled transformation of liquid at a <eutectic point>. Lamellae and rods are common morphologies. A hypoeutectic alloy can contain both primary crystals and a <eutectic microstructure>; those <microconstituents> are not the same accounting categories as its total phase fractions.

= Eutectic constituent fraction
{parent=Eutectic microstructure}

The fraction of an alloy that solidifies through the eutectic reaction, equal to the remaining liquid fraction immediately before that reaction in a simple equilibrium <solidification> path. It differs from the total fraction of either solid phase, and later precipitation can change the phase inventory.

= Eutectic composition
{parent=Eutectic system}
{title2=$C_E$}

The eutectic composition is the liquid <concentration> at the <eutectic temperature>, where liquid and two solid phases coexist. It is a composition coordinate on a <phase diagram>, not the overall <concentration> of every sample in an <eutectic system>.

= Eutectic temperature
{title2=$T_E$}
{parent=Eutectic system}

The eutectic temperature is the temperature of simultaneous liquid–two-solid <phase coexistence> in an <eutectic system>. In directional freezing of a solution away from the eutectic composition, a <mushy layer> can end at this temperature, where its remaining liquid solidifies.

= First-order phase transition
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}

At a first-order phase transition, the Gibbs free energy is continuous while at least one first derivative, such as entropy or volume, jumps. A nonzero entropy jump produces latent heat $L=T\Delta S$.

= Two-phase scalar coexistence endpoint conditions
{parent=First-order phase transition}
{title2=$V'=V''=V^{(3)}=0,\quad V^{(4)}>0$}

A smooth scalar <Landau free energy> can end a two-phase <phase coexistence curve> by merging two minima and their intervening maximum. At a generic stable endpoint, its first three derivatives vanish and its fourth derivative is positive. A field shift and two mixed control parameters reduce the local form to a quartic <Landau free energy>; the order-parameter jump then vanishes and the <magnetic susceptibility> diverges. This conclusion assumes there is no competing third phase and that the endpoint lies inside the control-parameter domain. It is not a general theorem about every first-order line: a sextic potential with fixed negative quartic coefficient has a symmetry-related ordered coexistence line that ends at a triple point with a finite jump.

= Spin-flop transition
{parent=First-order phase transition}

= Classical nucleation theory
{parent=First-order phase transition}
{wiki}

Classical nucleation theory balances the positive surface free energy of a droplet against its negative bulk free-energy gain. In three dimensions,
$$
F(R)=4\pi\sigma R^2-\frac{4\pi}{3}\Delta R^3
$$
has critical radius $R^*=2\sigma/\Delta$ and barrier $F^*=16\pi\sigma^3/(3\Delta^2)$.

= Critical nucleus radius
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}
{title2=$r^*=-2\gamma/\Delta g_V$}

In the spherical capillarity approximation, the nonzero maximum of $\Delta G=4\pi r^2\gamma+(4\pi/3)r^3\Delta g_V$ lies at $r^*=-2\gamma/\Delta g_V$ when $\Delta g_V<0$. Smaller <nuclei> tend to shrink and larger ones tend to grow. Surface anisotropy and molecular-scale structure limit the approximation.

= Nucleation barrier
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

The maximum free-energy cost along a <nucleation> pathway, at its <critical nucleus>. A pathway with fixed shape is a constraint and need not coincide with an unconstrained saddle point.

= Disc-shaped nucleus
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

A cylindrical model nucleus of <diameter> $d$ and height $h$. Its <free energy> is $\pi d^2h\Delta g/4+\pi d^2\gamma_f/2+\pi dh\gamma_e$, where $\Delta g$ is the signed bulk free-energy change per <volume>.

= Critical nucleus
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

A critical nucleus is the unstable droplet at the maximum of the nucleation free energy. Smaller droplets shrink, while larger droplets lower their free energy by growing.

= Arrhenius nucleation time
{parent=Classical nucleation theory}

Thermal crossing of a nucleation barrier $F^*$ has rate proportional to $e^{-F^*/(k_BT)}$ and mean waiting time proportional to $e^{F^*/(k_BT)}$, up to a kinetic prefactor.

= Latent heat
{parent=Phase coexistence curve}
{title2=$L=T\Delta S=\Delta H$}
{wiki}

Latent heat is the <heat> absorbed or released in a <first-order phase transition> through its <entropy> jump. For an equilibrium conversion from <thermodynamic phase> $\alpha$ to <thermodynamic phase> $\beta$ at fixed <pressure>, evaluate both <thermodynamic phases> at the same <temperature> and <pressure> on their <phase coexistence curve>:
$$
L=T_{\rm tr}(S_\beta-S_\alpha)=H_\beta-H_\alpha.
$$
Use one normalization throughout: $L$, $S$ and $H$ may all be per <mole>, giving $L$ in $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$, or all per unit <mass>, giving $L$ in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. Here $S$ is <entropy> and $H$ is <enthalpy> on that basis. Equal <Gibbs free energies> of the coexisting <thermodynamic phases> give $\Delta H=T_{\rm tr}\Delta S$. Identifying this <enthalpy> difference with supplied <heat> assumes a <closed system> and an equilibrium conversion with only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>. <Statistical Physics by David Tong>[Tong, Statistical Physics, phase coexistence and latent heat]{ref}

For a pure substance with positive <latent heat> per unit <mass> $L$, let $x$ be the <phase fraction> of the higher-<enthalpy> <thermodynamic phase>, measured by <mass>. While both <thermodynamic phases> coexist at the fixed <pressure>,
$$
Q=mL(x_2-x_1),\qquad T=T_{\rm tr}.
$$
Adding <heat> changes $x$ rather than the <temperature>; this creates the horizontal portion of a <heating curve>. Once $x=1$, additional <heat> raises the <temperature> according to <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>. Reversing the conversion releases <heat>. The plateau represents changing <enthalpy>, rather than a finite single-<thermodynamic phase> <heat capacity> satisfying $Q=C_p\Delta T$. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/1.5 Phase Changes>{ref}

For a constructed fixed-<pressure> model, take $m=1\,\mathrm{kg}$, initial <temperature> $280\,\mathrm K$, transition <temperature> $T_{\rm tr}=300\,\mathrm K$, <solid> <specific heat capacity at constant pressure> $c_s=2\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, <latent heat> $L=100\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}}$, and <liquid> <specific heat capacity at constant pressure> $c_l=3\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. These are stipulated model parameters, not empirical properties of a named material. Heating the <solid> to $300\,\mathrm K$ takes $40\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of <sensible heat>; converting it fully to <liquid> takes another $100\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of <heat>; heating the <liquid> to $320\,\mathrm K$ takes $60\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The total is $200\,\mathrm{kJ}$. After a total <heat> input of $90\,\mathrm{kJ}$, $x=(90-40)/100=1/2$ and the <temperature> is still $300\,\mathrm K$. The corresponding per-unit-<mass> <entropy> jump is $L/T_{\rm tr}=\frac13\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

{title=<Sensible heat> raises <temperature>; <latent heat> changes <phase fraction>}
{description=Constructed $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ fixed-<pressure> model with total supplied <heat> starting at zero at $280\,\mathrm K$. The plateau runs from $40$ to $140\,\mathrm{kJ}$, while the <liquid> <phase fraction> by <mass> rises from zero to one. The horizontal axis measures $Q=\Delta H$, rather than absolute <internal energy>.}

A <continuous phase transition> has no <latent heat>. Its <heat capacity> can have a jump or a singularity without a finite interval in which added <heat> converts coexisting <thermodynamic phases> at a constant <temperature>. A heating plateau therefore requires the stated <phase coexistence> conditions; it is not a feature of every <phase transition>. <Statistical Physics by David Tong>[Tong, Statistical Physics, first-order and continuous transitions]{ref}

= Specific latent heat of vaporization
{parent=Latent heat}
{title2=$L_v$}

= Latent heat of vaporization
{synonym}

The <energy> per unit <mass> required to change a saturated <liquid> to vapour at a stated <pressure> and coexistence <temperature>. In a steam-condensation <calorimetry> experiment, sensible cooling of the condensate must be subtracted from the measured receiver heat gain.

= Stefan number
{c}
{title2=$\mathrm{Ste}=c_p\Delta T/L$}
{parent=Latent heat}
{wiki}

The Stefan number compares sensible heat $c_p\Delta T$ with <latent heat> $L$ per unit mass. Some solidification literature uses its reciprocal as the Stefan parameter, so the defining formula must accompany the convention.

= Latent-to-sensible heat ratio
{title2=$S=L/(c_p\Delta T)$}
{parent=Stefan number}

= Inverse Stefan number
{synonym}

The latent-to-sensible heat ratio is the reciprocal of the <Stefan number> defined by $\mathrm{Ste}=c_p\Delta T/L$. Large $S$ means that phase change uses much more energy than changing temperature by $\Delta T$.

= Clausius-Clapeyron relation
{parent=Latent heat}
{c}
{wiki=Clausius–Clapeyron_relation}
{title2=$dp/dT=L/(T\Delta v)$}

= Clapeyron equation
{c}
{synonym}

= Clausius-Clapeyron equation
{c}
{synonym}

The <Clapeyron equation> connects the slope of a <phase coexistence curve> to the <latent heat> and the change in <volume (thermodynamics)> during a <first-order phase transition>. A steep boundary can indicate a large <latent heat>, a small change in <volume (thermodynamics)>, or both. This lets a measured boundary constrain caloric properties and predicts how changing <pressure> shifts a <melting> <temperature>.

For a pure substance in bulk <phase coexistence>, label the initial and final <thermodynamic phases> $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Both have the same absolute <temperature> $T$, <pressure> $p$, and <chemical potential>. With $s$, $h$, and $v$ denoting <entropy>, <enthalpy>, and <volume (thermodynamics)> on one common basis, define $\Delta s=s_\beta-s_\alpha$, $\Delta h=h_\beta-h_\alpha$, and $\Delta v=v_\beta-v_\alpha$. Then
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp}{dT}=\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v}=\frac{L}{T\Delta v}},\qquad L=\Delta h=T\Delta s.
$$
The <derivative> follows the <phase coexistence curve>; it is not a <partial derivative> within one <thermodynamic phase>. Use <molar volume> $v_m$ with molar <latent heat> $L_m$ in $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$, or <specific volume> $v$ with <latent heat> $L$ in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. Both give $dp/dT$ in $\mathrm{Pa\,K^{-1}}$. For <molar mass> $M$, $L_m=ML$ and $\Delta v_m=M\Delta v$, so the predicted slope is independent of the chosen basis. This formulation assumes a <simple compressible system>, fixed composition, and negligible interface contributions; a curved interface can instead require the <Young–Laplace equation>.

For positive <latent heat> in the stated conversion direction, the sign of the slope is the sign of $\Delta v$. Converting <liquid> to <gas> normally increases <volume (thermodynamics)> and gives a positive slope. The <negative melting-point slope of water> illustrates the opposite sign. At a <thermodynamic critical point>, the coexisting <gas> and <liquid> merge: both $L$ and $\Delta v$ vanish, so their ratio needs a limiting analysis rather than substitution of zero into the <Clapeyron equation>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.2, equation (5.185) and the discussion of the critical endpoint]{ref}

\Image[clapeyron-coexistence-slopes-and-volume-jump.png]
{title=<Phase coexistence curve> tangents: positive vaporization slope and negative <water> <melting> slope}

A short derivation uses the molar <Gibbs free energy> $g_m$, equal to the <chemical potential> for a pure substance. Equality persists along the boundary, so $dg_{m,\alpha}=dg_{m,\beta}$ with $dg_m=-s_m\,dT+v_m\,dp$. Therefore $-\Delta s_m\,dT+\Delta v_m\,dp=0$. Equality of $g_m=h_m-Ts_m$ also gives $\Delta h_m=T\Delta s_m$. Eliminating $\Delta s_m$ yields the <Clapeyron equation>.

= Saturation vapor pressure
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$e_s(T)$}

The equilibrium partial <pressure> of a vapour in contact with its condensed phase at a specified <temperature>. With constant specific <latent heat> and negligible condensed-phase volume, $d\ln e_s/dT=L/(R_vT^2)$. The condensed phase and any metastability convention must be specified.

= Variable-latent-heat saturation curve
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$\ln(p/p_*)=\int_{T_*}^T L(\theta)/(R\theta^2)\,d\theta$}

For a dilute <ideal gas> <vapor> whose specific volume greatly exceeds that of its condensate, the <Clausius-Clapeyron relation> integrates to the displayed formula. If $L(T)=L_*(T_c-T)/(T_c-T_*)$, the result is $\ln(p/p_*)=L_*[T_c(1/T_*-1/T)-\ln(T/T_*)]/[R(T_c-T_*)]$. Near the <liquid-gas critical point>, both the <ideal gas> approximation and the neglect of condensed-phase volume fail; zero <latent heat> alone does not determine the limiting coexistence slope.

= Melting pressure inferred from flotation and latent heats
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$p-p_t\simeq[L_f/(v_l-v_s)]\ln(T/T_t)$}

Floating ice with submerged volume fraction $f$ has <density> $f\rho_l$ by <Archimedes' principle>. Its volume change on melting is therefore $v_l-v_s=(1-1/f)/\rho_l<0$. An <enthalpy> cycle gives $L_f=L_s-L_v$ for quantities consistently expressed per <mass> at the same <temperature>. Treating these <latent heats> and specific volumes as constant and integrating the <Clausius-Clapeyron relation> gives the displayed <pressure> estimate. Using a vaporization <latent heat> extrapolated from a different <temperature> carries that approximation into the inferred fusion <heat>.

= Solid-phase Clapeyron slope from cell volumes
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{c}
{title2=$dP/dT=\Delta S_m/\Delta V_m$}

Molar crystal volume follows from $V_m=N_AV_{\rm cell}/Z$ with $Z$ atoms per cell for an elemental solid. Combining two such values with their molar <entropy> difference gives $\mathrm dP/\mathrm dT=\Delta S_m/\Delta V_m$. Ambient values supply only an approximation to a high-pressure coexistence slope, not a phase-boundary intercept.

= Ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$d\ln p/dT\simeq L_m/(RT^2)$}

For <liquid>–<gas> <phase coexistence>, make two distinct approximations: the <gas> obeys the <ideal gas law>, $v_{m,g}=RT/p$, and its <molar volume> greatly exceeds the <liquid> <molar volume>, $v_{m,g}\gg v_{m,l}$. Here $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $p$ is the <saturation pressure> of the pure substance. Substitution into the <Clapeyron equation> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dT}\simeq\frac{L_m(T)p}{RT^2},\qquad \frac{d\ln(p/p_*)}{dT}\simeq\frac{L_m(T)}{RT^2},
$$
where $p_*>0$ is any fixed reference <pressure>, making the <logarithm> dimensionless. Constant <latent heat> is a further approximation, needed for the elementary <integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation> below.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.2, “An Approximate Solution to the Clausius-Clapeyron Equation”: the three assumptions and the exponential solution]{ref}

The neglected <liquid> <molar volume> has a transparent error estimate. If the <gas> is ideal but $\epsilon=v_{m,l}/v_{m,g}$ is retained, then
$$
\left(\frac{dp}{dT}\right)_{\rm full}=\frac{L_m p}{RT^2(1-\epsilon)},\qquad \frac{(dp/dT)_{\rm approx}}{(dp/dT)_{\rm full}}=1-\epsilon.
$$
Thus ignoring the <liquid> <molar volume> underestimates this slope by a relative amount $\epsilon$. This estimate does not bound errors from treating a <real gas> as ideal or from freezing $L_m(T)$. Near a <thermodynamic critical point>, the <gas> and <liquid> <molar volumes> approach each other and these dilute-vapor assumptions fail.

= Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation
{c}
{parent=Ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation}
{title2=$\ln(p_2/p_1)\simeq-(L_m/R)(1/T_2-1/T_1)$}

If the molar <latent heat> $L_m$ is approximately constant on the chosen <temperature> interval, the <ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation> integrates to
$$
\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}\simeq-\frac{L_m}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right),\qquad p(T)\simeq p_1\exp\left[\frac{L_m}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T}\right)\right].
$$
For $L_m>0$, higher absolute <temperature> gives higher <saturation pressure>. A plot of $\ln(p/p_1)$ against <inverse temperature> $1/T$ is a straight line of slope $-L_m/R$. One reference pair $(T_1,p_1)$ fixes its intercept. On a per-unit-<mass> basis replace $L_m/R$ by $L/R_s$, with $R_s=R/M$ and $M$ the <molar mass>; using $L$ in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ with the molar $R$ would give incompatible units.

If only the constant-<latent heat> approximation is relaxed, the same ideal-vapor model instead gives
$$
\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}\simeq\frac{1}{R}\int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{L_m(T)}{T^2}\,dT.
$$
Consequently an apparent straight-line fit across a broad <temperature> range need not represent a single material constant. The simple exponential also approaches a finite value as $T\to\infty$; this is a property of the stipulated formula, not a prediction that <liquid>–<gas> <phase coexistence> extends beyond a <thermodynamic critical point>.

= Stipulated vapor-pressure calculation
{parent=Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation}

Consider an unnamed model substance with molar <latent heat> $L_m=40.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and <saturation pressure> $p_1=10.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$ at $T_1=300\,\mathrm K$. Stipulate the <ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation> throughout $280\leq T\leq340\,\mathrm K$, constant $L_m$, and $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. These parameters define an illustrative model, not a measured dataset or a fit to <water>. At $T_2=330\,\mathrm K$,
$$
\ln\frac{p_2}{p_1}=\frac{40000}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{300}-\frac{1}{330}\right)\simeq1.458,\qquad p_2\simeq42.97\,\mathrm{kPa}.
$$
At the reference state the <gas> <molar volume> is $RT_1/p_1=0.24942\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$, and the local slope is $dp/dT\simeq0.5346\,\mathrm{kPa\,K^{-1}}$. The tangent estimate $p_2\approx10.0+30(0.5346)=26.04\,\mathrm{kPa}$ is much lower than the integrated result: a local <derivative> should not be extrapolated over this whole interval. By contrast, over a small <temperature> increment, the fractional sensitivity is $d\ln(p/p_1)/dT=L_m/(RT_1^2)\simeq0.05346\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$.

= Latent heat from a vapor-pressure slope
{parent=Integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$L_m\simeq-R\,d\ln(p/p_*)/d(1/T)$}

Within the <ideal-vapor Clausius-Clapeyron approximation>, a local slope of the <natural logarithm> of <saturation pressure> against <inverse temperature> estimates the molar <latent heat>:
$$
L_m(T)\simeq-R\frac{d\ln(p/p_*)}{d(1/T)}.
$$
In the <stipulated vapor-pressure calculation>, the slope is $-4811.16\,\mathrm K$, which recovers $40.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. For variable $L_m(T)$, a two-point estimate gives an average weighted by $T^{-2}$, rather than the <latent heat> at an arbitrary midpoint. Differentiating noisy <pressure> measurements amplifies their errors. Outside the ideal-vapor regime, use independently determined <molar volumes> in $L_m=T\Delta v_m\,dp/dT$; a straight-looking graph alone cannot establish that the <gas> is ideal.

= Saturation-pressure equilibrium measurement
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}

A concrete experiment turns the <phase coexistence curve> into pairs of <temperature> and <saturation pressure>. A 1969 paper reports <water> measurements made in 1942 and 1947–1949 using a boiler and precision <mercury (element)> <manometer>. <Platinum resistance thermometers> read the boiler <temperature> through a <resistance bridge>. The <pressure>-transfer line passed through an ice-water condenser and a dry-ice trap; <helium> filled connecting sections. Corrected <mercury (element)> column height gave the <pressure> at the thermometer coils, including <hydrostatic pressure> contributions from <water> vapor, <helium>, <mercury (element)>, and <mercury (element)> vapor.<Some Precise Measurements of the Vapor Pressure of Water in the Range From 25 to 100 °C by H. F. Stimson>[Stimson, §§1–2, pp. 493–495: boiler, bridge thermometry, and fluid-head corrections]{ref}

Controls included normal/reversed-lead readings, readings at two thermometer <electric currents> extrapolated to zero current to remove self-heating, repeated bridge calibration, and daily <triple point> <thermometer calibration> checks. Cellar <temperature> measurements corrected fluid-column and gauge-block <thermal expansion>. One measurement series was excluded because a trapped gas bubble was suspected of corrupting the <mercury (element)> head. The paper adjusted measurements to round <temperatures> using previously tabulated slopes; those adjusted values therefore are not independent measurements of those slopes.<Some Precise Measurements of the Vapor Pressure of Water in the Range From 25 to 100 °C by H. F. Stimson>[Stimson, §2, pp. 494–495, and §3, p. 495: corrections and suspected gas-bubble bias]{ref}

The thermodynamic inference requires <liquid> and <gas> in <phase coexistence>, a well-defined common <temperature>, and a <pressure> corrected to that same location. Leaks, contamination, or a <temperature gradient> can spoil that interpretation. A separate <calorimetry> measurement of <latent heat>, together with the two <specific volumes>, allows a test of the full <Clapeyron equation>. Converting a measured <phase coexistence curve> slope directly into <latent heat> instead assumes that equation; it is not an independent test.

= Common-pressure melting-point slope
{parent=Clausius-Clapeyron relation}
{title2=$dT_m/dp=-T_m(1/\rho_s-1/\rho_l)/L$}

When the solid and liquid have the same <pressure>, equality of <chemical potentials> implies $-(s_l-s_s)dT+(1/\rho_l-1/\rho_s)dp=0$. With <latent heat of fusion> $L=T_m(s_l-s_s)$, this gives the displayed slope. If solid is less dense than liquid, raising the common pressure depresses the <melting> temperature. $T_m$ is absolute <temperature>. This coexistence-curve derivative differs from a thermomolecular pressure difference between equal-density phases.

= Negative melting-point slope of water
{parent=Common-pressure melting-point slope}

For a local illustrative <ice>–<water> <melting> calculation, stipulate $T_m=273.15\,\mathrm K$, <latent heat of fusion> $L=334\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}}$, <solid> <mass density> $\rho_s=917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and <liquid> <mass density> $\rho_l=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. These are explicitly stipulated rounded inputs, not measurements made here. The <specific volume> change on <melting> is
$$
\Delta v=\frac{1}{\rho_l}-\frac{1}{\rho_s}=-9.0513\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}}.
$$
The <common-pressure melting-point slope> therefore gives
$$
\frac{dT_m}{dp}=\frac{T_m\Delta v}{L}=-7.4022\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{K\,Pa^{-1}}\simeq-0.07402\,\mathrm{K\,MPa^{-1}}.
$$
A small $1\,\mathrm{MPa}$ increase in the common <pressure> thus lowers the <melting> <temperature> by about $0.074\,\mathrm K$ in this local model. Positive <latent heat> does not force a positive <phase coexistence curve> slope: <ice> contracts on conversion to <liquid> <water>. Increasing <pressure> favors the smaller <specific volume>. This calculation compares equilibrium states of the same two <thermodynamic phases>; extending its tangent to large <pressures> would require updated <mass densities>, <latent heat of fusion>, and the relevant <ice> <phase diagram>.
