= Shortest path problem
{title2=$\min_{p:s\leadsto t}\sum_{e\in p}w_e$}

The <shortest path problem> asks for a <graph path> from a source <vertex> $s$ to a destination <vertex> $t$ minimizing its total <edge> weight. Here $w_e$ is the cost of traversing <edge> $e$, and costs add along the <graph path>. If no route exists, its shortest-path distance is $\infty$. With nonnegative <edge> weights, deleting <cycles in a graph> from a <walk> never increases its cost, so repetitions cannot improve the optimum.

Consider the <undirected graph> with <vertex set> $V=\{A,B,C,D\}$ and the six <edge> weights
$$
w_{AB}=4,\quad w_{AC}=1,\quad w_{CB}=2,\quad w_{BD}=1,\quad w_{CD}=5,\quad w_{AD}=10.
$$
Each <edge> can be traversed in either direction at the same cost. The direct <graph path> $A,D$ costs $10$; $A,B,D$ costs $4+1=5$; $A,C,D$ costs $1+5=6$. The cheapest <graph path> is $A,C,B,D$, costing $1+2+1=4$. The remaining <graph path> $A,B,C,D$ costs $11$, exhausting the possibilities without repeated <vertices>.

\Image[shortest-path-weights.png]
{title=The <shortest path problem>: three highlighted <edges> cost less than the direct connection}

Thus fewest <edges> and least total cost are different objectives. <Breadth-first search> minimizes the number of <edges>, which suffices when every <edge> has the same positive cost. Unequal nonnegative costs call for the <Dijkstra algorithm>. On a map, weights might be minutes rather than kilometres: a longer-looking route can arrive sooner. This is why a <weighted graph> separates the network's connections from what a particular journey costs.

= Floyd-Warshall algorithm
{c}
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$d_{ij}\leftarrow\min(d_{ij},d_{ik}+d_{kj})$}

The Floyd-Warshall algorithm computes all-pairs shortest <graph path> lengths in a finite weighted <directed graph> with no negative-length <cycles in a graph>. Initialize $d_{ii}=0$, use the arc cost for each existing directed <edge>, and use $+\infty$ when no arc exists. For $k=1,\ldots,n$, update every ordered pair $(i,j)$ by the displayed rule. The storage is $n^2$ lengths and the running time is $O(n^3)$.

For correctness, let $d_{ij}^{(k)}$ be the shortest simple <graph path> length whose intermediate <vertices> lie among the first $k$. A shortest allowed <graph path> either avoids $k$ or splits at its unique occurrence of $k$, giving the recurrence. Conversely, concatenate shortest paths through $k$ and remove any repeated-vertex sections; each such closed <walk> has nonnegative length, so removal cannot increase the length. This proves equality. The update can be performed in place because $d_{kk}=0$ and the $k$th row and column remain unchanged during stage $k$.

If negative-length <cycles in a graph> are present, a shortest <walk> may not exist and the recurrence need not compute shortest simple <graph paths>. Two optimal subpaths can concatenate into a repeated-vertex <walk> with a negative cycle; deleting that cycle increases its length. A negative diagonal entry in the algorithm's final array detects this obstruction to finite shortest-walk distances.

= Failure of uniform edge shifts to preserve shortest paths
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$w'(P)=w(P)+C|P|$}

Adding the same constant $C$ to every <edge weight> adds $C$ times the number of edges to each path's cost. Routes with different edge counts need not retain their order. For example, edges $s\to t$, $s\to u$, $u\to t$ of weights $1,-2,2$ make the two-edge path optimal with cost zero. Adding three changes the direct cost to four and the two-edge cost to six. The graph is a <directed acyclic graph> and all shifted weights are positive, yet the minimizing path changes.

= Single-source shortest-path problem
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$d_s(v)=d(s,v)$}

Compute <shortest-path distances> from one fixed source to every vertex, together with predecessor information when shortest paths exist. <Dijkstra's algorithm> handles nonnegative weights; the <Bellman-Ford algorithm> admits negative weights and detects reachable <negative cycles>. A <directed acyclic graph> instead permits one pass in <topological order>.

= DAG shortest-path algorithm
{c}
{parent=Single-source shortest-path problem}

Initialize source distance to zero and other distances to infinity, then process vertices in <topological order>, relaxing their outgoing edges once. Every predecessor on a path to a vertex appears earlier, so induction through that ordering proves that its distance is final when processed. Negative weights are allowed because there are no directed cycles. With <adjacency lists>, <topological sorting> and all <edge relaxations> take $O(|V|+|E|)$ time; predecessor pointers reconstruct paths.

= Shortest-path distance
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$d(s,t)=\inf_{P:s\leadsto t}\sum_{e\in P}w(e)$}

The infimum of total <edge weights> over allowed walks from a source to a target. The value is $+\infty$ when no route exists. A <negative cycle> reachable from the source and able to reach the target makes it $-\infty$. In a finite graph without such a cycle, a finite distance is attained by a <graph path>, after removing non-improving repetitions.

= Cycle erasure in minimum-weight walks
{parent=Shortest path problem}

If every nontrivial closed subwalk has strictly positive weight, removing any repeated-vertex segment strictly decreases a <walk>'s weight. Hence a minimum-weight walk must be a <graph path>, and it is a <shortest path>. Strictly positive edge weights are sufficient in an undirected graph. For a <trail>, positivity of every simple <cycle> is already sufficient: its first repeated-vertex segment is a cycle because no edge is repeated.

Under the usual unrestricted <walk> convention, positivity of simple cycles alone is insufficient. A graph consisting of one zero-weight edge $xy$ has no cycles, but the walk $x,y,x,y$ is a minimum-weight walk and is not a path. A zero-weight backtrack cannot be removed with a strict improvement. For nonnegative edge weights, one may erase such backtracks without increasing weight and obtain a shortest path, but cannot conclude that every minimum walk was already a path.

= Shortest cycle by repeated shortest-path trees
{parent=Shortest path problem}

In a positive-weight <directed graph>, run the <Dijkstra algorithm> from each source $s$ and minimize $d_s(u)+w(u,s)$ over closing <edges>. In an <undirected graph>, minimize $d_s(u)+w(u,v)+d_s(v)$ only over <edges> absent from the current <shortest-path tree>. A <tree> <edge> would otherwise give a spurious traversal out and back. A non-tree candidate contains a genuine cycle no longer than its score, even when the two <tree> routes share a prefix.

For the undirected optimum, choose a shortest cycle and a source on it. If an incident cycle <edge> is absent from the <tree>, its candidate is bounded by the cycle length. Otherwise its two neighbours lie in distinct <tree> branches, and some <edge> on the remaining cycle segment crosses branches. That <edge> is non-tree; shortest distances to its endpoints are bounded by the two complementary cycle segments, again giving a candidate no greater than the optimum. Thus the minimum candidate equals the shortest-cycle length. Repeated <Dijkstra algorithm> runs take $O((|V|^2+|V||E|)\log(|V|+1))$ <running time> with a <binary heap>.

= Hop-bounded shortest-route recurrence
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$D_r(v)=\min\{D_{r-1}(v),\min_{(u,v)\in E}(D_{r-1}(u)+w(u,v))\}$}

Let $D_r(v)$ be the minimum weight of a route from a fixed source to $v$ using at most $r$ <edges>; repeated <vertices> are permitted. Initialise $D_0$ to zero at the source and infinity elsewhere. Every feasible route either already uses at most $r-1$ <edges> or appends one <edge> to such a route, proving the displayed <dynamic programming> recurrence. Reading exclusively from the previous row enforces the <edge> budget. Negative cycles cause no unbounded objective because the budget is finite. Each round scans all <edges>, costing $O(m)$ when only edge-incident <vertices> and the specified endpoints are processed. With nonnegative weights, cycle removal gives an equally good or better simple path within the budget.

= Integer-weight shortest-path tie-breaking lemma
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$w'(e)=|V|w(e)+1$}

In a finite graph with positive integral <edge> weights, replacing $w(e)$ by $|V|w(e)+1$ makes every transformed <shortest path> minimise original weight first and <edge> count second. Positive weights imply that an optimal route is simple, with at most $|V|-1$ <edges>. Original integral path weights that differ have a gap of at least one; multiplication by $|V|$ outweighs every possible difference in simple-path <edge> counts. Among equal-weight paths, only the added <edge> count differs. Thus a single ordinary <shortest path> computation solves lexicographic weight-and-hop minimisation.

= Minimum-hop dominance for nearly unit weights
{parent=Shortest path problem}

For a graph on $n$ vertices with all edge weights in $[1,1+1/n]$, every minimum-weight <shortest path> has the smallest possible number of edges. Indeed, positivity permits a simple optimum, and a simple $r$-edge path weighs at most $r+r/n<r+1$ for $r\leq n-1$, while any path with more edges weighs at least $r+1$. A <breadth-first search> followed by weighted <dynamic programming> along increasing-hop edges therefore solves the single-source problem in $O(|V|+|E|)$ time. Choosing an arbitrary breadth-first tree alone is insufficient: equal-hop routes may still have different weights.

= Shortest-path pursuit distance bound
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$d_{t+1}\le d_t-a+b$}

Suppose two pieces occupy a common <connected component of a graph> in an unweighted <undirected graph>. For <integers> $a>b\ge0$, in each round an evader moves at most $b$ <edges>, then a pursuer moves up to $a$ <edges> along a <shortest path> to the new evader position, stopping upon capture. Let $d_t$ be their finite <graph distance> at a surviving round boundary.

The <triangle inequality> bounds the separation after the evader moves by $d_t+b$. If that separation is at most $a$, the pursuer captures it. Otherwise the pursuer reduces it by exactly $a$, giving
$$
1\le d_{t+1}\le d_t-(a-b).
$$
Hence every surviving history has at most $\lfloor(d_0-1)/(a-b)\rfloor$ rounds. With a finite <branching factor>, the surviving <search tree> is finite and <depth-first tree search> becomes a <complete search algorithm>. This does not make its first goal optimal for arbitrary rewards. Initial <reachability> is essential: an infinite <graph distance> supplies no decreasing finite bound.

= Bottleneck shortest path problem
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$\min_{P:x\leadsto y}\max_{e\in E(P)}\ell(e)$}

Unlike the sum in the usual <shortest path problem>, the bottleneck cost of a nonempty <graph path> is its largest <edge> weight. The optimum is the smallest threshold at which the endpoints become connected by edges of weight at most that threshold. Sorting edges and increasing the threshold, with a <disjoint-set data structure> maintaining components, finds this value. The <minimax path property of a minimum spanning tree> provides a single tree representing such optimal routes for every pair of vertices.

= Shortest-path tree
{parent=Shortest path problem}

A <shortest-path tree> rooted at a source vertex is a <spanning tree> whose unique tree route from the source to every <vertex> solves the <shortest path problem> in the original <weighted graph>. <Dijkstra's algorithm> constructs one for a finite connected <graph> with nonnegative <edge> lengths, recording a predecessor whenever a distance improves. It need not be a <minimum spanning tree>: one minimizes each source-route length, the other the total weight of the tree's edges.

= Positive-weight shortest-path predecessors form a tree
{parent=Shortest-path tree}
{title2=$d(u)=d(p(u))+w(p(u)u)>d(p(u))$}

In a finite connected graph with strictly positive edge weights, choose any <shortest path> from a root $v$ to every other vertex $u$, and let $p(u)$ be its penultimate vertex. Every prefix is shortest: a better prefix followed by the final edge would give a cheaper walk, whose positive-weight cycles can be erased to give a cheaper path. Thus the displayed distance identity holds.

Following predecessors strictly decreases the shortest-path distance, so no predecessor cycle is possible. Since every nonroot vertex has a predecessor, every chain reaches the root. Opposite predecessor choices cannot select the same undirected edge, again by strict distance decrease. The selected graph is connected and has exactly one edge per nonroot vertex, so is a <spanning tree>. Along its root paths the distance differences telescope, proving that it is a <shortest-path tree> even if the original shortest paths were chosen independently.

= Signed edge weights can prevent a shortest-path tree
{parent=Shortest-path tree}

In a triangle with root $v$, assign $w(va)=w(vb)=2$ and $w(ab)=-1$. Among <graph paths>, the unique <shortest path> from $v$ to $a$ is $v,b,a$, and that from $v$ to $b$ is $v,a,b$, both of weight one. A <shortest-path tree> would have to contain all three edges, impossible for a <tree>. The example uses shortest simple paths; arbitrary walks can have unboundedly negative weight by repeatedly traversing the negative edge.

= Shortest path
{parent=Shortest path problem}

A <graph path> of minimum total edge weight between its endpoints, among the permitted paths. In an unweighted graph, its weight is the number of edges. A shortest path need not be unique.

= Label-setting shortest-path algorithm
{parent=Shortest path problem}

An algorithm that makes each vertex's distance permanent once it is selected. With nonnegative <directed edge> lengths, the <Dijkstra algorithm> selects the unfinalized vertex with least tentative distance; a shorter route through another unfinalized vertex is impossible because its remaining route has at least that next tentative distance. For an all-to-one problem, incoming <directed edges> to the selected vertex are relaxed. Negative lengths can invalidate this finalization argument.

= Bellman inequalities
{c}
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{title2=$d_i\le c_{ij}+d_j$}

The Bellman inequalities turn local <directed edge> checks into a global certificate for the <shortest path problem>. Here the convention is *all-to-one*: every journey ends at a fixed <vertex> $r$, and $c_{ij}$ is the cost of the original <directed edge> $i\to j$. Work in a finite <directed graph> with finite real costs. Write $L_i$ for candidate labels, normalized by $L_r=0$, on the <vertices> that can reach $r$. A feasible potential means precisely that these finite labels satisfy
$$
L_i\le c_{ij}+L_j
$$
for every <directed edge> within that set. These labels give <lower bounds> on costs *to* $r$. A discovered <directed path> of cost $U_i$ instead gives an <upper bound>. The following constructed <graph> shows both bounds and the exact distances between them; its weights are illustrative costs, not measured travel data.

\Image[edge-relaxation-certificate.png]
{title=<Bellman inequalities> and explicit <directed paths> certify distances to $r$ from opposite sides}

The arrows in the picture point toward the destination, whereas <edge relaxation> propagates an improved label in the opposite direction: an improved $d_j$ can lower $d_i$. In the pictured <graph>, the <directed paths> from $a$ to $r$ cost $7$, $2+3=5$, and $2-2+4=4$. Thus $\delta(a,r)=4$. The candidate $L=(3,1,3,0)$ in <vertex> order $(a,b,c,r)$ is feasible, but underestimates the exact distances $(4,2,4,0)$. Merely satisfying the <Bellman inequalities> does not make a candidate exact.

Why do they give <lower bounds>? Along any <directed path> $i=v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_k=r$, successive substitution gives
$$
L_i\le\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}c_{v_\ell v_{\ell+1}}+L_r=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}c_{v_\ell v_{\ell+1}}.
$$
The same calculation works for a <directed walk>, with repeated <vertices> allowed. Taking the <infimum> of route costs gives $L_i\le\delta(i,r)$. A discovered route gives $\delta(i,r)\le U_i$. If the bounds meet, $L_i=U_i$, they certify an optimum without enumerating routes. Equivalently, check the <Bellman inequalities> for the proposed distances and exhibit <directed paths> realizing them. The authored <Bellman-Ford algorithm> implementation checks the corresponding single-source inequalities and equality on recorded route <edges>; reversing every <directed edge> gives this all-to-one convention.<Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others/BellmanFordSP.java>[Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others; Sedgewick and Wayne, BellmanFordSP.java, optimality checks]{ref}

For the exact labels in the picture, the slack $c_{ij}+d_j-d_i$ is respectively $0,3,1,0,0$ on $a\to b$, $a\to r$, $b\to r$, $b\to c$, $c\to r$. Zero slack means equality; the zero-slack <directed path> $a\to b\to c\to r$ realizes the cost $4$. This is the useful beauty of the certificate: a few local <inequalities> rule out every cheaper journey, while one explicit journey attains the bound.

Keep unreachable <vertices> separate: $\delta(i,r)=\infty$ when no <directed path> to $r$ exists, and a finite <lower bound> alone cannot certify that fact. Extended labels can use $\infty+c=\infty$ for finite $c$, but never compute a slack as $\infty-\infty$. If $i$ can reach a negative-cost <directed cycle> and then reach $r$, repeatedly traversing the <directed cycle> makes route costs unbounded below: their <infimum> is $-\infty$, with no finite shortest <directed walk>. A finite feasible potential on such a <directed cycle> is impossible, since summing its <Bellman inequalities> would give $0\le\sum c_{ij}<0$. A negative-cost <directed cycle> that cannot reach $r$ does not affect distances to $r$. These are statements about unrestricted <directed walks>; requiring a <directed path> with no repeated <vertices> is a different optimization problem when negative-cost <directed cycles> exist.<6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff/Lecture 17: Shortest Paths III: Bellman-Ford>[6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff; MIT 6.006, Lecture 17, pp. 1 and 5: relevant negative cycles and simple paths]{ref}

For the single-source convention, the same original <directed edge> $u\to v$ instead gives $L(v)\le L(u)+w_{uv}$ with $L(s)=0$, a <lower bound> on distance *from* $s$. The endpoint carrying the label on the left therefore changes with the direction of the problem.

= Label-correcting shortest-path algorithm
{parent=Shortest path problem}

An algorithm whose tentative distance labels may be revised repeatedly until all relevant <Bellman inequalities> hold. <Bellman-Ford algorithm> uses this approach: a label is an upper bound supplied by a discovered <directed path>, and relaxing an <directed edge> can replace it with a shorter discovered <directed path>. A finite label is not declared permanent merely because it has been processed.

= Bellman-Ford algorithm
{c}
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{wiki}
{title2=$\delta(s,v)$}

The <Bellman-Ford algorithm> solves the single-source <shortest path problem> in a finite <directed graph> with finite real <edge> weights, allowing negative weights. It repeatedly applies <edge relaxation>, so an improved <tentative distance> can improve its neighbours on a later visit. This makes it a <label-correcting shortest-path algorithm>.<Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others/BellmanFordSP.java>[Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others; Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, BellmanFordSP.java, signed-weight single-source solver and queue-based relaxation]{ref} If no negative-cost <directed cycle> is reachable from the source, at most $|V|-1$ complete passes suffice for all distances; a further check detects a reachable negative-cost <directed cycle>.<6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff/Lecture 17: Shortest Paths III: Bellman-Ford>[6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff; MIT 6.006, Lecture 17, pp. 3–4: complete-pass algorithm, proof and reachable-cycle corollary]{ref}

There are two orientations to keep distinct. The usual single-source problem asks for distances *from* $s$, extending routes along outgoing <directed edges>. The <Bellman inequalities> above instead concern distances *to* a sink $r$, prepending incoming <directed edges> to known routes. The <all-to-one Bellman-Ford recurrence> below preserves that convention. Reversing every <directed edge>, with its weight unchanged, converts one problem into the other.

Here is an original single-source example with source $S$, <vertex set> $V=\{S,A,B,C,T\}$, and precisely these <directed edges>:
$$
w_{SA}=4,\qquad w_{SB}=5,\qquad w_{AB}=-2,\qquad w_{BC}=3,\qquad w_{CT}=2,\qquad w_{AT}=8.
$$
The negative <edge> $A\to B$ is harmless: this <directed graph> has no <directed cycle>. Its best route to $T$ is $S\to A\to B\to C\to T$, of cost $4-2+3+2=7$. The other routes to $T$ cost $12$ through $S,A,T$ and $10$ through $S,B,C,T$.

\Image[bellman-ford-negative-edge-passes.png]
{title=Four in-place <Bellman-Ford algorithm> passes propagate an improvement across a negative-weight <edge>}

Initialize $d(S)=0$ and the other <tentative distances> to $\infty$. In each pass apply single-source <edge relaxation>, $d(v)\gets\min\{d(v),d(u)+w_{uv}\}$, scanning in the order $A\to T$, $C\to T$, $B\to C$, $A\to B$, $S\to B$, $S\to A$. Skip a candidate with $d(u)=\infty$. The figure records labels *after* each complete pass and uses current labels immediately: it is an in-place trace. In pass 2, $B\to C$ still uses $d(B)=5$, giving $8$; only later does $A\to B$ lower $B$ to $2$. Pass 3 carries that improvement to $C$, and pass 4 carries it to $T$. The final labels in order $(S,A,B,C,T)$ are $(0,4,2,5,7)$; the final <shortest-path predecessors> are $S,A,B,C$ for $A,B,C,T$ respectively.

The scan order explains the delay. Scanning instead as $S\to A$, $S\to B$, $A\to B$, $B\to C$, $C\to T$, $A\to T$ gives those same final labels in just one in-place pass. The <synchronous Bellman-Ford recurrence> has a precise bound on the number of <edges> used by a recorded route; an in-place pass can propagate along several <edges> and need not have that interpretation. Both implementations have the $|V|-1$ pass guarantee under the stated no-reachable-negative-cycle hypothesis.

With a stored list of <edges>, complete-pass <Bellman-Ford algorithm> has <time complexity> $O(|V||E|+|V|)$ and uses $O(|V|)$ extra space for labels and <shortest-path predecessors>, using <Big O notation> and constant-cost arithmetic. The extra $|V|$ accounts for initialization even when $E$ is empty. A pass with no strict decrease permits early termination. A queue variant instead revisits outgoing <edges> of <vertices> whose labels improved; the authored implementation provides this alternative and has $O(|V||E|)$ worst-case running time on its usual nontrivial instances.<Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others/BellmanFordSP.java>[Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others; Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, BellmanFordSP.java, class documentation, constructor and relax method]{ref} For nonnegative weights, the <Dijkstra algorithm> and its existing distance-frontier figure explain how permanent labels can avoid these repeated passes; the existing <edge relaxation> figure illustrates one update at a time.

= Synchronous Bellman-Ford recurrence
{c}
{parent=Bellman-Ford algorithm}
{title2=$D_k(v)=\min\{D_{k-1}(v),\min_{(u,v)\in E}(D_{k-1}(u)+w_{uv})\}$}

For the single-source problem, let $D_k(v)$ be the minimum cost of a <directed walk> from $s$ to $v$ using at most $k$ <edges>. Set $D_0(s)=0$ and $D_0(v)=\infty$ for $v\ne s$, use $\infty+c=\infty$ for finite $c$, and let a <minimum> over an empty <set> be $\infty$. Then
$$
D_k(v)=\min\left\{D_{k-1}(v),\ \min_{(u,v)\in E}\bigl(D_{k-1}(u)+w_{uv}\bigr)\right\}.
$$
Every entry on the right comes from the previous round, requiring separate old and new arrays. The proof is the last-<edge> decomposition: a permitted <directed walk> either already uses at most $k-1$ <edges>, or ends with some $u\to v$ after a prefix using at most $k-1$. Conversely, each candidate describes a permitted <directed walk>. This <dynamic programming> viewpoint gives an exact meaning to every intermediate label, even when negative-cost <directed cycles> exist. Apply the formula to the source too: its initial zero is not a permanent constraint.

If no negative-cost <directed cycle> is reachable from $s$, removing repeated-<vertex> portions of a <directed walk> cannot increase its cost. Whenever a destination is reachable, some shortest <directed path> therefore uses at most $|V|-1$ <edges>, and $D_{|V|-1}(v)=\delta(s,v)$. Unreachable <vertices> keep distance $\infty$. For in-place passes, every finite label still comes from a discovered <directed walk>; moreover, pass $k$ has offered every prefix of length $k$ of a shortest <directed path>, regardless of scan order. Its labels can improve faster than the synchronous labels, while the same $|V|-1$ upper bound on passes remains valid. This is why repeated local corrections eventually settle a global <shortest path problem>.

= Bellman-Ford negative-cycle detection
{c}
{parent=Bellman-Ford algorithm}

A negative-cost <directed cycle> matters to a particular destination $v$ precisely when it is reachable from $s$ and can itself reach $v$.<6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff/Lecture 17: Shortest Paths III: Bellman-Ford>[6.006 Introduction to Algorithms, Fall 2011 by Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 6.006 course staff; MIT 6.006, Lecture 17, p. 1: cycle between source and destination; p. 4: reachable-cycle corollary]{ref} Repeating it $m$ times adds $m$ times its negative total cost to a route. Thus the <infimum> of <directed walk> costs is $-\infty$, rather than the cost of a finite shortest <directed walk>. The distinction is about <reachability>, not simply the presence of a negative <edge> or a negative-cost <directed cycle> anywhere in the <graph>.

Add $C\to A$ of weight $-4$ to the worked example. Now $A\to B\to C\to A$ has total weight $-2+3-4=-3$. Add also an isolated <vertex> $U$ with a <self-loop> of weight $-1$. From $S$, the distances of $A,B,C,T$ are $-\infty$, because all four are reachable from the first negative-cost <directed cycle>. The source retains distance $0$: no <directed edge> leads back to $S$. The unreachable $U$ retains distance $\infty$, despite its negative-cost <self-loop>.

\Image[bellman-ford-negative-cycle-reachability.png]
{title=Relevant negative-cost <directed cycles>: descendants from a source become ancestors when every <directed edge> is reversed}

For example, $m$ extra laps before proceeding to $T$ give costs $7-3m$. This construction also shows why requiring a <directed path> with no repeated <vertices> would change the problem: only <directed walks> permit arbitrarily many laps. Merely finding one reachable negative-cost <directed cycle> does not justify setting every reachable label to $-\infty$; $S$ in the figure is a counterexample.

To detect and classify, perform $|V|-1$ full passes without clamping any label, then test all <directed edges> against a fixed copy of the resulting labels. Collect heads $v$ of <edges> $u\to v$ satisfying $d(u)<\infty$ and $d(u)+w_{uv}<d(v)$. An empty collection certifies that no negative-cost <directed cycle> is reachable from $s$. Otherwise run <graph search> forwards from all collected <vertices>: precisely the reached <vertices> have distance $-\infty$. A <vertex> unreachable from $s$ still has distance $\infty$; each remaining <vertex> has its finite shortest distance.

Why is this classification exact? Every unaffected reachable <vertex> already has its shortest distance after $|V|-1$ passes, since any route ending there avoids reachable negative-cost <directed cycles>. Thus it cannot be the head of a strict improvement, or be reached from such a head. Conversely, all <vertices> on a reachable negative-cost <directed cycle> have finite labels by that time. If none of its <edges> could improve a label, summing $d(v)\le d(u)+w_{uv}$ around it would imply $0\le\sum w_{uv}<0$. At least one of its heads is therefore collected, and the forward <graph search> reaches the entire <directed cycle> and every affected descendant. Detecting a <directed cycle> alone and marking its descendants are separate tasks; finite intermediate labels in the affected region are not answers.

= All-to-one Bellman-Ford recurrence
{c}
{parent=Bellman-Ford algorithm}
{title2=$d_i^{(k)}=\min\{d_i^{(k-1)},\min_{(i,j)\in E}(c_{ij}+d_j^{(k-1)})\}$}

Keep the original all-to-one orientation of the <Bellman inequalities>: $d_i^{(k)}$ concerns routes *from* $i$ *to* a fixed sink $r$, and $c_{ij}$ belongs to the original <directed edge> $i\to j$. Initialize $d_r^{(0)}=0$ and $d_i^{(0)}=\infty$ for $i\ne r$. The synchronous <dynamic programming> recurrence is
$$
d_i^{(k)}=\min\left\{d_i^{(k-1)},\ \min_{(i,j)\in E}\bigl(c_{ij}+d_j^{(k-1)}\bigr)\right\}.
$$
It prepends the first <edge> of a route instead of appending the last: $d_i^{(k)}$ is the minimum cost of a <directed walk> from $i$ to $r$ using at most $k$ <edges>. In particular, improving $d_j$ can improve labels on incoming <directed edges> $i\to j$. The same original <directed edge> therefore updates its tail in this convention, whereas single-source <edge relaxation> updates its head.

If no negative-cost <directed cycle> can reach $r$, $|V|-1$ rounds suffice for every distance to $r$, and $d_r^{(k)}=0$ automatically. This hypothesis concerns <directed paths> *from the cycle to the sink*, not paths from the sink to the cycle. In the base example with its original arrows and sink $T$, the distances in order $(S,A,B,C,T)$ are $(7,3,5,2,0)$. Once $C\to A$ of weight $-4$ is added, $S,A,B,C$ instead have distance $-\infty$ to $T$, while $T$ keeps distance $0$ and $U$ keeps $\infty$.

More generally, $\delta(i,r)=-\infty$ precisely when $i$ can reach a negative-cost <directed cycle> that can reach $r$. Thus the affected <vertices> are the ancestors of such a <directed cycle>. A negative-cost <directed cycle> unable to reach $r$ is irrelevant. Reversing all arrows makes $r$ a single source, turns these ancestors into descendants, and makes the detection and forward <graph search> above directly applicable. The right panel of the figure uses that reversal with sink $S$; its distances to $S$ equal the left panel's distances from $S$.

Do not force the root to remain zero outside the hypothesis. Consider the two <directed edges> $r\to a$ of cost $0$ and $a\to r$ of cost $-1$, with sink $r$. The recurrence gives $d_a^{(1)}=-1$ and $d_r^{(2)}=-1$: the latter records one lap of a negative-cost <directed cycle> through $r$. Repetition makes both unrestricted distances $-\infty$. Clamping $d_r$ at zero and omitting its update would instead leave the stationary labels $(d_r,d_a)=(0,-1)$, concealing that behaviour. Initialization specifies the cost of the empty <directed walk>; it does not forbid cheaper nonempty returns to the root. Cycle detection must test every relevant <directed edge>, including those whose relaxation updates the root.

= Dijkstra algorithm
{c}
{parent=Shortest path problem}
{wiki=Dijkstra's_algorithm}
{title2=$d(v)=\operatorname{dist}(s,v)$}

= Dijkstra's algorithm
{c}
{synonym}

The <Dijkstra algorithm> solves the single-source <shortest path problem> in a finite <graph> with nonnegative <edge> weights, finding distances to all reachable <vertices>. Start with <tentative distance> $d(A)=0$ and $d(v)=\infty$ elsewhere. Repeatedly choose the unfinalized <vertex> with least <tentative distance>, declare that label permanent, and perform <edge relaxation> on its outgoing <edges>. For an <undirected graph>, inspect both directions. A finite <tentative distance> always records the cost of a discovered <graph path>, but can improve before finalization.

In our example, finalizing $A$ discovers $C$ at $1$, $B$ at $4$, and $D$ at $10$. Choose $C$ next: <edge relaxation> through $C$ lowers $B$ to $1+2=3$ and $D$ to $1+5=6$. Choose $B$: <edge relaxation> through $B$ lowers $D$ to $3+1=4$. Finally choose $D$. The permanent distances are therefore $A:0$, $C:1$, $B:3$, $D:4$; the evolving <tentative distances> were $B:4\to3$ and $D:10\to6\to4$.

{title=<Tentative distances> fall until the <Dijkstra algorithm> makes each label permanent}

Following <shortest-path predecessors> backwards gives $D,B,C,A$; reversing the order gives $A,C,B,D$. If only this destination matters, stop when $D$ is finalized. Stopping when $D$ is first discovered would incorrectly return $10$. If the smallest remaining <tentative distance> is $\infty$, all remaining <vertices> are unreachable.

Why is the permanent choice safe? Suppose the selected <vertex> $u$ had a cheaper <graph path>. Let $y$ be the first unfinalized <vertex> on that <graph path>. Its preceding <vertex> was finalized, so <edge relaxation> has already offered $y$ a label no larger than that prefix's cost. Nonnegative remaining <edge> weights make the whole cost at least this prefix's cost; selection gives $d(u)\le d(y)$. A cheaper <graph path> would contradict these comparisons. Zero weights are allowed; ties can be resolved arbitrarily. This is the reason the <label-setting shortest-path algorithm> works, rather than a lucky feature of the drawing.

Negative weights break that reasoning. In a <directed graph> with $s\to u$ costing $2$, $s\to v$ costing $5$, and $v\to u$ costing $-4$, the <Dijkstra algorithm> would finalize $u$ at $2$ before discovering the cheaper cost $1$. The <Bellman-Ford algorithm> handles negative <edge> weights when no relevant negative <directed cycle> exists.

With an <adjacency list> and an indexed <binary heap> implementing a <priority queue>, the <time complexity> is $O((|V|+|E|)\log |V|)$ for $|V|\ge2$, using <Big O notation>. Each <vertex> is extracted at most once, each <edge> is inspected once per direction, and each successful <edge relaxation> decreases a heap key in $O(\log |V|)$ time. This assumes constant-cost arithmetic and comparisons; exact computation with very large weights adds their representation cost. The queue uses $O(|V|)$ space beyond the <graph>. Simply scanning all remaining <vertices> gives $O(|V|^2+|E|)$ instead.https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/44sp/DijkstraSP.java.html[Princeton, DijkstraSP implementation]{ref}https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/code/javadoc/edu/princeton/cs/algs4/IndexMinPQ.html[Princeton, indexed binary heap operations]{ref}

The 1959 paper presents the <shortest path problem> as its second problem, after the <minimum spanning tree> problem, and grows known shortest routes in increasing distance order.https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/lans/readings/routing/dijkstra-routing-1959.pdf[Dijkstra, A note on two problems in connexion with graphs, pp. 269–271]{ref} In his later recollection, Dijkstra dated the shortest-route discovery to 1956: a demonstration for the ARMAC computer used a simplified Dutch railway map and printed the route between towns. A practical request became an enduring <algorithm> because the same distance rule applies to any suitable <graph>.https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1983/A1983QA19900001.pdf[Dijkstra's recollection, published in 1983]{ref}

= Minimum-hop shortest paths
{parent=Dijkstra algorithm}
{title2=$\min_{\rm lex}(\text{weight},\text{edge count})$}

Among paths of minimum total weight, choose the fewest edges. Store a <lexicographic order> pair $(d,h)$ and extend it across weight $w$ as $(d+w,h+1)$. For positive edge weights, every extension strictly increases its first component, so <Dijkstra's algorithm> settles these pairs correctly when its <priority queue> and relaxation both compare the full pair. Equal total weight then selects the smaller hop count; arbitrary vertex-name tie-breaking does not express this objective.

= Tentative distance
{parent=Dijkstra algorithm}
{title2=$d(v)$}

A tentative distance $d(v)$ is the cheapest cost so far recorded by a <shortest path problem> <algorithm>. For the single-source convention, initialize $d(s)=0$ and $d(v)=\infty$ elsewhere. Every finite label is the cost of a discovered route from $s$ to $v$, so
$$
\delta(s,v)\le d(v).
$$
It is an <upper bound> supplied by an actual route, not a guess that might be too low. The <loop invariant> follows directly from <edge relaxation>: extending a discovered route to $u$ by $u\to v$ supplies another discovered route. The usual label initialization and this safety property are stated and proved in the authored MIT notes.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-006-introduction-to-algorithms-fall-2011/6277a1f06100c26a7ff21031af6757b5_MIT6_006F11_lec16.pdf[MIT 6.006, Lecture 16, p. 1: relaxation safety]{ref}

In the trace under <edge relaxation>, $d(t)=10$ first records the direct <directed path> $s\to t$. Later $d(t)=8$ records $s\to a\to t$, and finally $d(t)=3$ records $s\to b\to a\to t$. Each label is a usable route cost, but the first two are not shortest. An infinite <tentative distance> means no route has yet been discovered; only a completed search establishes unreachability. The isolated <vertex> $z$ in that example remains at $\infty$.

In a <directed graph>, the discovered route is formally a <directed walk>, which may repeat <vertices>; in the pictured acyclic <graph> it is always a <directed path>. Negative-cost <directed cycles> can keep supplying cheaper <directed walks> without a finite optimum. In the all-to-one convention, the same meaning applies to discovered routes *from* $i$ *to* $r$. Do not confuse either version with the <lower bounds> supplied by feasible potentials in the <Bellman inequalities>: one records something achievable, the other rules out anything cheaper.

= Edge relaxation
{parent=Dijkstra algorithm}
{title2=$d(v)\gets\min\{d(v),d(u)+w_{uv}\}$}

Edge relaxation tests whether extending a recorded route by one <directed edge> improves a <tentative distance>. For a single source $s$ and an original <directed edge> $u\to v$ of weight $w_{uv}$, form the candidate $q=d(u)+w_{uv}$. If $d(u)$ is finite and $q<d(v)$, replace $d(v)$ by $q$ and record the <shortest-path predecessor> $\pi(v)=u$. Otherwise leave both unchanged. The <minimum> formula in the header gives the resulting label in either case. The authored implementation uses this strict comparison and records the incoming <directed edge>.https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kevin-wayne/algs4/master/src/main/java/edu/princeton/cs/algs4/DijkstraSP.java[Sedgewick and Wayne, DijkstraSP.java, relax method]{ref}

\Image[edge-relaxation-trace.png]
{title=<Edge relaxation> lowers discovered-route costs, including across a negative-weight <directed edge>}

The table lists labels *after* each specified operation, keeping $d(s)=0$ throughout this constructed acyclic <graph>. Relaxing $a\to t$ first offers $6+2=8$. Relaxing $b\to a$ then offers $2-1=1$, lowering $a$ from $6$ to $1$; revisiting $a\to t$ now offers $1+2=3$. Finally $b\to t$ offers $2+7=9$, so the label $3$ stays. Changing a label can make a previously checked outgoing <directed edge> worth checking again. This trace illustrates the operation itself, with a deliberately specified order, rather than a run of the <Dijkstra algorithm>.

A single <edge relaxation> does not require nonnegative weights: its candidate is the cost of a real extended <directed walk>, even when the last <directed edge> has negative weight. Nonnegative weights are needed by the <Dijkstra algorithm> to make its selected minimum <tentative distance> permanent. The <Bellman-Ford algorithm> permits negative weights and revisits labels; its authored implementation also checks for reachable negative-cost <directed cycles>.https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kevin-wayne/algs4/master/src/main/java/edu/princeton/cs/algs4/DijkstraSP.java[Sedgewick and Wayne, DijkstraSP.java, nonnegative-weight precondition]{ref}<Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others/BellmanFordSP.java>[Algorithms, 4th edition by Robert Sedgewick and others; Sedgewick and Wayne, BellmanFordSP.java, signed weights and cycle detection]{ref}

For the all-to-one convention of the <Bellman inequalities>, relax the same original <directed edge> $i\to j$ by $d_i\gets\min\{d_i,c_{ij}+d_j\}$. This prepends $i\to j$ to a discovered route from $j$ to $r$, and improves the tail label $d_i$, not the head label $d_j$. Skip the operation when $d_j=\infty$. In exact arithmetic, a full pass over all relevant <directed edges> with no improvement establishes all their <Bellman inequalities>; combined with the discovered-route <upper bounds>, this certifies finite distances. One unsuccessful operation establishes only that this particular candidate did not improve its label at that moment.

= Shortest-path predecessor
{parent=Dijkstra algorithm}
{title2=$\pi(v)$}

A shortest-path predecessor $\pi(v)$ records the previous <vertex> on a chosen route from source $s$ to $v$. When <edge relaxation> strictly improves $d(v)$ through $u\to v$, set $\pi(v)=u$. For a <multigraph>, store the actual <directed edge>, since two <edges> with the same endpoints can have different weights. Initialize all <vertices>, including the source, without a <shortest-path predecessor>. The authored implementation reconstructs a route by following these incoming <edges> backwards, then reversing their order.https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kevin-wayne/algs4/master/src/main/java/edu/princeton/cs/algs4/DijkstraSP.java[Sedgewick and Wayne, DijkstraSP.java, edgeTo and pathTo]{ref}

At the end of the <edge relaxation> trace, $\pi(t)=a$, $\pi(a)=b$, and $\pi(b)=s$. Following them gives $t,a,b,s$, so the forward <directed path> is $s\to b\to a\to t$, of cost $2-1+2=3$. This makes the distance useful: a number tells us the best cost, whereas the <shortest-path predecessors> tell us which connections to take. In the <Dijkstra algorithm>, reconstruct a destination's shortest route after its label becomes permanent. In a <label-correcting shortest-path algorithm>, wait for convergence and the appropriate negative-cost <directed cycle> check. During correction, a predecessor's label may have changed since its child's last update: immediately after $b\to a$ in the trace, the current chain to $t$ costs $3$ although $d(t)$ still records the earlier discovered route of cost $8$.

Strict improvement is a useful guard against <directed cycles> in the recorded pointers. Consider $s\to a$ and $s\to b$ of cost zero, followed by $a\to b$ and $b\to a$, also of cost zero. After the first two <edge relaxations>, both labels are zero and both <shortest-path predecessors> are $s$. If equality also allows pointer replacement, the last two operations can set $\pi(b)=a$ and $\pi(a)=b$: following pointers loops forever despite correct distance labels. Keeping the old pointer on ties prevents this example; choosing among equal-cost routes requires a separate rule that preserves a route to the source. Strict improvement alone does not protect against reachable negative-cost <directed cycles>, which can force endless decreases. Equality on recorded <edges> plus the <Bellman inequalities> certifies route costs only when the pointer chains actually terminate at the source.

For all-to-one distances to $r$, record a next <vertex> $j$ whenever relaxing $i\to j$ improves $d_i$. Follow these successors forward in the original <directed graph> to reach $r$. They are <shortest-path predecessors> when viewed in the reversed <directed graph>; keeping the direction convention explicit prevents returning a route whose arrows point the wrong way.
