= Inner product
{title2=$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$}
{wiki}

An inner product assigns a scalar $\langle u,v\rangle$ to two <vectors> and supplies their lengths and <orthogonality>. Using the convention linear in the first argument, it satisfies
$$
\langle au+bv,w\rangle=a\langle u,w\rangle+b\langle v,w\rangle,\qquad
\langle u,v\rangle=\overline{\langle v,u\rangle},\qquad
\langle v,v\rangle>0\quad(v\ne0).
$$
The resulting <norm> is $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$. Over the <real numbers>, conjugation has no effect and the form is symmetric and <bilinear>. Over the <complex numbers>, it is <sesquilinear>; the convention linear in the second argument is also common, so formulas for <coefficients> must match the stated convention.

The Euclidean example is $\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_i u_i\overline{v_i}$. For real $u=(2,1)$ and $v=(1,3)$ this gives $\langle u,v\rangle=5$, while $\|u\|^2=5$ and $\|v\|^2=10$. If $r=(-1,2)$, then $\langle r,u\rangle=0$: perpendicularity becomes an algebraic equation. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\|v\|$, and in a real <inner product space> it makes the angle formula $\cos\theta=\langle u,v\rangle/(\|u\|\|v\|)$ meaningful for nonzero <vectors>.

Different <inner products> express different measurements. The weighted example $\langle u,v\rangle=4u_1v_1+u_2v_2$ on $\mathbb R^2$ gives $\|(x,y)\|^2=4x^2+y^2$, so its unit-length <vectors> lie on an ellipse. For <functions>, an <integral> such as $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f\overline g$ supplies the geometry of the <L2 space>. This lets <orthogonal projection> and <least-squares solutions> apply to curves as well as coordinate lists.

= Inner product space
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$}
{wiki}

An inner product space is a real or complex <vector space> equipped with a positive-definite <inner product>. Over the real numbers the form is symmetric and bilinear; over the complex numbers it is conjugate symmetric and sesquilinear. It defines the <norm> $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> proves the triangle inequality for this norm. A complete inner product space is a <Hilbert space>; every finite-dimensional inner product space is complete.

= Generalized parallelogram identity
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$2^{-n}\sum_{\epsilon_j=\pm1}\|\sum_j\epsilon_jv_j\|^2=\sum_j\|v_j\|^2$}

Averaging squared norms of all signed sums of finitely many vectors in an inner-product space cancels every mixed term. This equates the average to the sum of the individual squared norms, distinguishing Hilbert-space geometry from other sequence-space norms.

= Fischer inner product
{c}
{title2=$\langle x^\alpha,x^\beta\rangle_F=\alpha!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$}
{parent=Inner product}

The Fischer inner product on <polynomials> is $\langle x^\alpha,x^\beta\rangle_F=\alpha!\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. Multiplication by $x_i$ is adjoint to differentiation by $x_i$. Therefore multiplication by $|x|^2$ is adjoint to the ordinary <Laplacian>, yielding the <harmonic decomposition of homogeneous polynomials> by finite-dimensional orthogonality.

= Frobenius inner product
{c}
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$\langle A,B\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)$}

= Trace inner product
{synonym}

For complex <matrices>, the <inner product> $\langle A,B\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)$ is conjugate-linear in its first argument. On real <symmetric matrices> it becomes $\operatorname{tr}(AB)$. In particular $\langle A,xx^T\rangle_F=x^TAx$, identifying <quadratic forms> with linear tests on <matrices>.

= Trace orthogonality of symmetric and antisymmetric matrices
{parent=Frobenius inner product}
{title2=$\operatorname{tr}(SA)=0$}

Let $S$ be a real <symmetric matrix> and $A$ a real <antisymmetric matrix> of the same size. Invariance of the <trace> under <transpose> and the cyclic <trace> identity give
$$
\operatorname{tr}(SA)=\operatorname{tr}((SA)^T)=\operatorname{tr}(A^TS^T)=-\operatorname{tr}(AS)=-\operatorname{tr}(SA).
$$
Hence $\operatorname{tr}(SA)=0$. Equivalently these two <matrices> are <orthogonal> for the <Frobenius inner product>, since $\langle S,A\rangle_F=\operatorname{tr}(S^TA)=\operatorname{tr}(SA)$. Together with the unique <symmetric and antisymmetric parts of a matrix>, this is an <orthogonal decomposition> of the real square <matrices>.

= Discrete L2 inner product
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$\langle U,V\rangle_h=h^d\sum_j\overline{U_j}V_j$}

The discrete L2 inner product is the cell-volume-weighted <Hermitian inner product> on a uniform grid. Its associated <norm> is the <discrete L2 norm>. It provides the finite-dimensional analogue of spatial <integration by parts> and continuous <energy method> identities.

= Weighted inner product
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$\langle f,g\rangle_w$}

For a positive weight $w$, the weighted inner product of <functions> is $\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int\overline f g w$. It is the usual <inner product> in the <L2 space> for the measure $w(x)dx$. A <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> uses this weight to make its <eigenfunctions> <orthogonal>.

The weight specifies how different locations contribute to a squared error: $\|f-g\|_w^2=\int|f-g|^2w$. More weight makes an error in that region more costly. The <weighted Euclidean inner product> makes the same choice for a finite list of coordinates.

For an explicit real-function example on $[0,1]$, let $w(x)=1+x$ and approximate $f(x)=x$ by a <constant function>. The <orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace> spanned by the function $1$ gives
$$
c=\frac{\langle x,1\rangle_w}{\langle1,1\rangle_w}
=\frac{\int_0^1x(1+x)\,dx}{\int_0^1(1+x)\,dx}
=\frac{5/6}{3/2}=\frac59.
$$
The best unweighted constant is $1/2$. Giving greater weight to larger $x$ moves the best constant upward, so a weighting choice changes the approximation even though the allowed family of <functions> is unchanged.

= Weighted norm
{parent=Weighted inner product}
{title2=$\|f\|_w^2=\int |f(x)|^2w(x)\,dx$}

A positive measurable weight $w$ defines the displayed <norm> on the <L2 space> for the <measure> $w(x)\,dx$, with functions identified when equal almost everywhere. This is the <norm> induced by the <weighted inner product>. Changing $w$ changes the contribution of each region to approximation error. If $p_0,\ldots,p_N$ are nonzero mutually <orthogonal> real functions, completing squares shows that the unique best approximation to real $f$ in their <linear span> has coefficients $a_j=\langle f,p_j\rangle_w/\|p_j\|_w^2$.

= Weighted Euclidean inner product
{parent=Weighted inner product}
{title2=$\langle u,v\rangle_M=u^TMv$}

A <symmetric positive definite matrix> $M$ defines an <inner product> on real coordinate <vectors> by
$$
\langle u,v\rangle_M=u^TMv,\qquad \|u\|_M^2=u^TMu.
$$
For diagonal $M$, positive diagonal entries weight the squared coordinate errors. This is the finite-coordinate version of a <weighted inner product>; the positive-definite matrix example appears in https://people.tamu.edu/~f-narcowich/m641/m641_notes/inner_products.html[Francis Narcowich, Inner Products and Norms, standard inner products, example 8].

For $M=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$, the squared <norm> of $(a,b)^T$ is $4a^2+b^2$. A first-coordinate error of one costs as much as a second-coordinate error of two. The <unit sphere> is the <ellipse> $4a^2+b^2=1$, and <orthogonality> means $4u_1v_1+u_2v_2=0$. Thus “closest” and “perpendicular” depend on the chosen <inner product>. The <orthogonal projection residual> example shows how this changes a best approximation.

= Dot product
{title2=$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$}
{parent=Inner product}
{wiki}

= Scalar product
{synonym}

= Euclidean inner product
{synonym}

A <dot product> measures how much two <Euclidean vectors> point along each other. This article uses real <Euclidean vectors> in $\mathbb R^n$, with the usual <Cartesian basis>: multiply matching coordinates and add,
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i,\qquad
\|\mathbf a\|=\sqrt{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}.
$$
The result is a <scalar>, while $\|\mathbf a\|$ is the <Euclidean norm>. This real convention has no <complex conjugation>; for <vectors> over the <complex numbers>, use the convention specified in <inner product>.

For two nonzero <Euclidean vectors>, the same <dot product> records their <angle> $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$ through the <cosine>:
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|\cos\theta,\qquad
\theta=\arccos\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|}.
$$
Here $\arccos$ is an <inverse trigonometric function>. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> guarantees that the normalized ratio lies in $[-1,1]$. The <zero vector> has <dot product> zero with every <vector>, but its <angle> is undefined: the ratio would divide by zero. To outline the geometric identity, expand $\|\mathbf a-\mathbf b\|^2=\|\mathbf a\|^2+\|\mathbf b\|^2-2\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$ and compare with the <law of cosines>.https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/2-3-the-dot-product[OpenStax, Calculus Volume 3, §2.3: the dot product and angles]{ref}

Take $\mathbf a=(3,4)$ and $\mathbf c=(2,0)$. Their <Euclidean norms> are $5$ and $2$, and
$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c=3(2)+4(0)=6,\qquad
\cos\theta=\frac{6}{5\cdot2}=\frac35,\qquad
\theta=\arccos(3/5)\approx53.1301^\circ.
$$
The <unit vector> $\mathbf u=\mathbf c/\|\mathbf c\|=(1,0)$ selects the horizontal direction. The <scalar projection> $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf u=3$ says how far $\mathbf a$ reaches along that direction; its <vector projection> is $3\mathbf u=(3,0)$. Thus $\mathbf a=(3,0)+(0,4)$ splits into an aligned part and an <orthogonal projection residual>.

\Image[dot-product-projection-and-angle.png]
{title=The <vector projection> of $(3,4)$ onto the horizontal line is $(3,0)$; the <orthogonal projection residual> is $(0,4)$ and the <angle> is $53.1301^\circ$}

For $\mathbf b=(4,-3)$, the <dot product> $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=12-12=0$ proves <orthogonality>; both <Euclidean norms> are $5$. For $\mathbf d=(-2,0)$, $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf d=-6$: the <scalar projection> onto $\mathbf d/\|\mathbf d\|=(-1,0)$ is $-3$, because $\mathbf a$ reaches against that chosen direction. Its <angle> with $\mathbf d$ is $126.8699^\circ$.

Keeping $\mathbf a=(3,4)$ fixed and choosing $\mathbf b$ of <Euclidean norm> $2$ gives $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=10\cos\theta$. At <angles> $0^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ,120^\circ,180^\circ$, the <dot products> are $10,5,0,-5,-10$. Positive values mean partial alignment, zero means <orthogonality>, and negative values mean a component against the other direction.

\Image[dot-product-angle-sign.png]
{title=With <Euclidean norms> $5$ and $2$, the <dot product> follows $10\cos\theta$; equal-length arrows show positive, zero, and negative alignment with $(3,4)$}

This interpretation explains <work>: for a constant <force> $\mathbf F$ and a straight <displacement> $\mathbf s$, $W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf s$ counts the <force> component along the motion. A sideways <force> contributes zero <work>, and an opposing <force> contributes negative <work>.https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/2-3-the-dot-product[OpenStax, §2.3: work]{ref}

= Angle between vectors
{parent=Dot product}
{title2=$\cos\theta=\boldsymbol a\cdot\boldsymbol b/(\|\boldsymbol a\|\|\boldsymbol b\|)$}

For nonzero real <vectors> in a <Euclidean space>, their <angle> $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is determined by the displayed <dot product> formula. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> puts the quotient in $[-1,1]$, so the inverse cosine is defined. Perpendicular <vectors> have angle $\pi/2$, parallel vectors pointing the same way have angle zero, and those pointing in opposite directions have angle $\pi$. A zero vector has no uniquely defined direction and hence no angle by this definition.

= Scalar projection
{parent=Dot product}
{title2=$\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf c}\mathbf a=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c/\|\mathbf c\|$}
{wiki}

The <scalar projection> of a real <Euclidean vector> $\mathbf a$ onto a nonzero <Euclidean vector> $\mathbf c$ is its signed component along the <unit vector> $\mathbf c/\|\mathbf c\|$:
$$
\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf c}\mathbf a=\mathbf a\cdot\frac{\mathbf c}{\|\mathbf c\|}.
$$
For $\mathbf a=(3,4)$, the <scalar projection> onto $(2,0)$ is $3$, while that onto $(-2,0)$ is $-3$. Reversing the measuring direction reverses the sign; the <scalar projection> is not necessarily a nonnegative length. For nonzero $\mathbf a$, it also equals $\|\mathbf a\|\cos\theta$, using the <angle> with $\mathbf c$ and its <cosine>. For the <zero vector> it is zero, even though that <angle> is undefined.

= Vector projection
{parent=Dot product}
{title2=$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf c}\mathbf a=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf c/\|\mathbf c\|^2$}
{wiki}

The <vector projection> multiplies the <scalar projection> by the chosen <unit vector>, giving the <orthogonal projection> onto the <linear span> of a nonzero real <Euclidean vector> $\mathbf c$:
$$
\mathbf p=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf c}\mathbf a
=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c}{\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf c}\mathbf c,\qquad
\mathbf r=\mathbf a-\mathbf p,\qquad \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf c=0.
$$
For $\mathbf a=(3,4)$ and $\mathbf c=(2,0)$, this gives $\mathbf p=(6/4)(2,0)=(3,0)$ and $\mathbf r=(0,4)$; the <dot product> $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf c=0$ checks the <orthogonal projection residual>. Replacing $\mathbf c$ by $(-2,0)$ gives the same $\mathbf p=(-6/4)(-2,0)=(3,0)$: reversing direction changes the <scalar projection>, but preserves the <vector projection> onto the line.

For a short proof of the nearest-point property, any <vector> $\mathbf z$ on this line satisfies $\|\mathbf a-\mathbf z\|^2=\|\mathbf r\|^2+\|\mathbf p-\mathbf z\|^2$ by the <Pythagorean identity>. The second term is uniquely minimized at $\mathbf z=\mathbf p$. This connects a directional measurement to the <orthogonal projection> used in approximation.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-15-projections-onto-subspaces/[Strang, MIT 18.06, Lecture 15: projections onto subspaces]{ref}

For $x,y\in\mathbb F_2^n$, the binary inner product is $x\cdot y=\sum_i x_iy_i$ in $\mathbb F_2$. Its value is the parity of the coordinates at which both vectors are one.

= Resolution of the identity
{parent=Orthonormal basis}
{title2=$\sum_j|j\rangle\langle j|=I$}

= Completeness relation
{synonym}

A complete <orthonormal basis> in a <Hilbert space> resolves the identity as $\sum_j|j\rangle\langle j|=I$. In infinite <dimension> the partial sums converge strongly: their <action> on each fixed vector converges in norm. Generalized <position> and <momentum> eigenstates instead give distributional <integral> resolutions, such as $\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|=I$, with their normalization interpreted through the <Dirac delta function>.

= Resolution of the identity in an orthonormal basis
{parent=Orthonormal basis}
{title2=$I=\sum_{j=1}^nu_ju_j^T$}

For a real <orthonormal basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_n$, each <vector> has expansion $x=\sum_j u_j(u_j^Tx)$. Since this holds for every $x$, the sum of the rank-one <orthogonal projection matrices> $u_ju_j^T$ is the <identity matrix>. Their pairwise products vanish for distinct basis vectors. For a complex <inner product space>, replace the <transpose> by the <conjugate transpose>.

= Gram matrix
{parent=Inner product}
{c}
{wiki}

For vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_n$, the Gram matrix is
$$
G_{ij}=\langle v_i,v_j\rangle.
$$
Two finite vector families with the same Gram matrix are related by an isometry between their spans; in finite dimensions that isometry can be extended to a unitary map after completing orthonormal bases.

= Positive definiteness of a Gram matrix
{parent=Gram matrix}
{title2=$c^TGc=\|\sum_i c_ix_i\|^2$}

For real <vectors> $x_i$ in a Euclidean <inner product space>, their <Gram matrix> $G_{ij}=x_i^Tx_j$ satisfies the displayed identity by expanding the squared <norm>. Thus every <Gram matrix> is a <positive semidefinite matrix>, and it is a <positive-definite matrix> exactly when the vector family is <linearly independent>. The complex version replaces transposes by <conjugate transposes> and uses $c^*Gc=\|\sum_i c_ix_i\|^2$.

= Unit vectors with a common inner product
{parent=Gram matrix}
{title2=$G=(1-t)I+t\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$}

For $n\geq2$, the <Gram matrix> of $n$ unit <vectors> with common distinct-pair <inner product> $t$ is $G=(1-t)I+t\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$. Its <eigenvalues> are $1+(n-1)t$ on the all-ones direction and $1-t$ on its orthogonal complement. It is a <positive-definite symmetric matrix> exactly when $-1/(n-1)<t<1$, permitting a linearly independent realization in $\mathbb R^n$. An explicit construction takes the columns of
$$
B=\sqrt{1-t}(I-uu^T)+\sqrt{1+(n-1)t}\,uu^T,
\qquad u=\mathbf1/\sqrt n.
$$
The two orthogonal projections have product zero, so $B^TB=G$; positive <eigenvalues> also make $B$ invertible. At either endpoint the corresponding eigenspace becomes a linear dependence.

= Gram determinant
{c}
{parent=Gram matrix}
{title2=$\det(\langle v_i,v_j\rangle)_{i,j}$}

The determinant of a <Gram matrix> is nonnegative and vanishes precisely when its vectors are <linearly dependent>. In Euclidean three-dimensional space, three column vectors form a square <matrix> $V$, whose <Gram matrix> is $V^TV$. Its determinant is $(\det V)^2=[v_1\cdot(v_2\times v_3)]^2$, the square of the <scalar triple product> and of the parallelepiped volume.

= Riesz dual basis in an inner product space
{c}
{parent=Gram matrix}
{title2=$(u_i,\widehat u_k)=\delta_{ik}$}

For a basis $(u_i)$ of a finite-dimensional <inner product space> with conjugate-linear first argument, define $G_{ij}=(u_i,u_j)$ and $\widehat u_k=\sum_l(G^{-1})_{lk}u_l$. Then $(u_i,\widehat u_k)=\delta_{ik}$ and $(\widehat u_j,\widehat u_k)=(G^{-1})_{jk}$. This is the inner-product representation of the <dual basis>; requiring the dual vectors to lie in the given span is essential.

= Gaussian empirical Gram matrix
{parent=Gram matrix}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat\Sigma=G^TG/n$}

For a matrix $G$ with <independent> standard normal entries, $G^TG/n$ is a <Gaussian empirical Gram matrix> and has <expectation> equal to the identity. With mean known to be zero it is the raw empirical second moment. Subtracting a sample mean would give a different estimator. On any fixed unit vector its <quadratic form> is a chi-squared variable divided by $n$.

= Gaussian Gram matrix concentration on a fixed subspace
{parent=Gaussian empirical Gram matrix}
{c}
{title2=$P(\|G^TG/n-I_k\|_{\mathrm{op}}>1/2)\leq2e^{k\log B-n/1024}$}

For $G$ with $n$ <independent> standard Gaussian rows in dimension $k$, combine a constant-radius sphere <metric net>, the <quadratic form net bound>, the <chi-squared concentration inequality>, and a <union bound>. This gives $P(\|G^TG/n-I_k\|_{\mathrm{op}}>1/2)\leq2\exp(k\log B-n/1024)$ for a numerical $B$. Taking $n$ larger than a constant times $k\log p$ gives exponential decay in $k\log p$. The subspace is fixed, so no union over supports is needed.

= Sparse norm preservation from entrywise Gram control
{parent=Gram matrix}

If $E=A^TA/d-I$ has <entrywise maximum norm> at most $t$, every vector $z$ supported on at most $s$ coordinates satisfies $|z^TEz|\leq t\|z\|_1^2\leq st\|z\|_2^2$. Thus the squared norm distortion is at most $st$ on all such sparse vectors. Use the quadratic inequality at $z=0$, where a ratio would be undefined.

= Orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace
{parent=Inner product}

A finite-dimensional <linear subspace> $W$ of an <inner product space> has a unique closest <vector> to any given <vector> $x$. This is its <orthogonal projection> $P_Wx$. If $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ is an <orthogonal basis> of $W$, compute it one direction at a time:
$$
P_Wx=\sum_{j=1}^d\frac{\langle x,e_j\rangle}{\langle e_j,e_j\rangle}e_j.
$$
The <inner product> convention here is linear in its first argument, including over the <complex numbers>. For an <orthonormal basis>, each denominator is one. When $W$ consists only of the <zero vector>, the empty sum gives $P_Wx=0$. The finite-dimensional formula is described in https://people.tamu.edu/~f-narcowich/m641/m641_notes/inner_products.html[Francis Narcowich, Inner Products and Norms: minimization problems].

Take $W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)^T\}$ and $x=(3,1)^T$, using the <dot product>. With $e=(1,2)^T$,
$$
\frac{x\cdot e}{e\cdot e}=\frac{3+2}{1+4}=1,\qquad
P_Wx=(1,2)^T,\qquad r=x-P_Wx=(2,-1)^T.
$$
The <orthogonal projection residual> has $r\cdot e=2-2=0$. To verify the best approximation directly, every candidate in the line is $te$, and
$$
\|x-te\|_2^2=(3-t)^2+(1-2t)^2=5(t-1)^2+5.
$$
The smallest squared <Euclidean norm> error is therefore $5$, attained only at $t=1$. The diagram connects the perpendicular residual to this one-variable <quadratic polynomial>.

\Image[orthogonal-projection-closest-point.png]
{title=The perpendicular residual identifies the closest point on a line and the unique minimum of the squared error}

The general mechanism is the <Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space>. For any $w\in W$, the <vectors> $r=x-P_Wx$ and $P_Wx-w$ are <orthogonal>, so
$$
\|x-w\|^2=\|x-P_Wx\|^2+\|P_Wx-w\|^2.
$$
Moving away from $P_Wx$ inside $W$ adds a nonnegative squared <norm>, which is zero only when $w=P_Wx$. This proves both optimality and uniqueness.

The useful feature is separation of preparation from evaluation. Construct an <orthonormal basis> once with the <Gram-Schmidt process>, then obtain each new best approximation using <inner products> instead of a fresh <optimization> problem. When $W$ is the <column space> of a <matrix>, this gives <linear least squares>; when $W$ is spanned by <orthogonal polynomials>, it gives a <least-squares polynomial in an orthogonal-polynomial basis>. The ambient <inner product space> need not be a <Hilbert space> for this finite-dimensional construction. In an infinite-dimensional target <linear subspace>, existence requires additional hypotheses, supplied by <orthogonal decomposition by a closed subspace>.

= Orthogonal projection residual
{parent=Orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace}
{title2=$r=x-P_Wx$}

An <orthogonal projection residual> is the difference $r=x-P_Wx$ between a <vector> and its <orthogonal projection> onto a <linear subspace> $W$. It is <orthogonal> to every <vector> in $W$, so $\langle r,w\rangle=0$ for all $w\in W$. The <Gram-Schmidt process> uses such residuals to remove directions already represented by earlier basis <vectors>.

This is an optimality condition as well as a geometric description. No permitted change within $W$ can remove a further <orthogonal> component of $r$. In the preceding <dot product> example, $r=(2,-1)^T$ is perpendicular to the line spanned by $(1,2)^T$.

The selected <inner product> matters. Keep $x=(3,1)^T$ and the same line, but use the <weighted Euclidean inner product> with $M=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$. The projection coefficient and residual become
$$
t_M=\frac{\langle x,e\rangle_M}{\langle e,e\rangle_M}
=\frac{14}{8}=\frac74,\qquad
P_W^{(M)}x=\left(\frac74,\frac72\right)^T,\qquad
r_M=\left(\frac54,-\frac52\right)^T.
$$
Now $4(r_M)_1e_1+(r_M)_2e_2=5-5=0$, although $r_M\cdot e=-15/4\ne0$. The <orthogonality> is with respect to $M$, so it need not look perpendicular in the ordinary drawing. The squared weighted error is
$$
\|x-te\|_M^2=4(3-t)^2+(1-2t)^2
=8\left(t-\frac74\right)^2+\frac{25}{2}.
$$
The weighted optimum reduces the first-coordinate error from $2$ to $5/4$, at the cost of increasing the magnitude of the second-coordinate error from $1$ to $5/2$. The weights express which errors are more costly.

\Image[orthogonal-projection-metric-comparison.png]
{title=Changing the inner product changes the closest point: a circle and a weighted error ellipse touch the same permitted line at different points}

In <linear least squares>, residual <orthogonality> to the <column space> is expressed by the <normal equations for linear least squares>. With a <weighted Euclidean inner product>, the corresponding real-matrix equation is $A^TM(x-Ac)=0$. This makes the choice of error measurement part of the mathematical model, rather than merely a plotting convention.

= Orthogonal projection matrix
{parent=Orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace}

A <matrix> represents a <linear projection> when applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once. For an <orthogonal projection> with the standard <Hermitian inner product> on $\mathbb C^m$, the additional condition is that the <conjugate transpose> equals the original <matrix>:
$$
P^2=P=P^\dagger.
$$
Thus $P$ is both <idempotent> and a <Hermitian matrix>. Over the <real numbers> this says $P^2=P=P^T$. Its <image of a linear map> is the retained <linear subspace>, while its <kernel of a linear map> is the <orthogonal complement>: <vectors> in the image are unchanged, and <vectors> in the kernel become zero.

For a <matrix> $A$ with <full column rank>, projecting onto its <column space> gives
$$
P=A(A^\dagger A)^{-1}A^\dagger.
$$
The invertibility of $A^\dagger A$ follows from <linear independence> of the columns. If those columns are an <orthonormal set>, $A^\dagger A=I$ and $P=AA^\dagger$. These formulas and the conditions $P^2=P=P^T$ in the real case are developed in https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/00e9c8f0eafedeab21a3d079a17ed3d8_MIT18_06SCF11_Ses2.2sum.pdf[MIT 18.06SC, Projections onto Subspaces, pp. 1–3].

For the line spanned by $e=(1,2)^T$,
$$
P=\frac{ee^T}{e^Te}
=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P(3,1)^T=(1,2)^T.
$$
In particular, $Pe=e$ and $P(2,-1)^T=0$. The <eigenvalues> are $1$ on the retained direction and $0$ on the removed direction. This exposes the meaning of an <orthogonal projection> as retaining selected components, rather than stretching them.

Symmetry refers to the specified <inner product>. In the real weighted setting, the formula becomes $P_M=A(A^TMA)^{-1}A^TM$ and satisfies $P_M^2=P_M$ and $P_M^TM=MP_M$. For the preceding weighted line example,
$$
P_M=\frac18\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\8&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It is not symmetric under the ordinary <transpose>, but it is an <orthogonal projection> for the <weighted Euclidean inner product>. The matrix criterion $P=P^\dagger$ should therefore be used with the standard <Hermitian inner product>, not silently transferred to a different geometry.

= Orthogonal projection of a circle between planes
{parent=Orthogonal projection matrix}
{title2=$b/a=|\boldsymbol n_1\cdot\boldsymbol n_2|$}

Under <orthogonal projection> from one plane onto another, a <circle> of <radius> $R$ becomes an <ellipse> with semiaxes $R$ and $R|\cos\alpha|$, where $\alpha$ is the <angle> between the planes' unit <normal vectors>. To prove this, choose one in-plane <unit vector> parallel to the planes' intersection and the other perpendicular to it. The first vector is unchanged by the projection; the second has projected length $|\cos\alpha|$ and remains perpendicular to the first. Parametrizing the circle in that <orthonormal basis> therefore gives projected coordinates $(R\cos t,R\cos\alpha\sin t)$. Parallel planes have a circular image, and perpendicular planes give the degenerate case of a line segment. An arbitrary center simply projects to the center of the image.

= Transverse projection operator
{parent=Orthogonal projection matrix}

For a nonnull vector $k$ in a space with a nondegenerate bilinear form, $P_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}-k_\mu k_\nu/k^2$ projects onto the subspace transverse to $k$. It obeys $P_{\mu\nu}k^\nu=0$ and $P^2=P$.

= Least-squares polynomial in an orthogonal-polynomial basis
{parent=Orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace}

For orthogonal polynomials $Q_0,\ldots,Q_n$ under a positive weighted integral inner product, the least-squares approximation to $f$ of degree at most $n$ is
$$
p_n^*=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{\langle f,Q_k\rangle}{\langle Q_k,Q_k\rangle}Q_k.
$$
Its residual is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree at most $n$.

= Orthogonal vectors
{parent=Inner product}
{title2=$\langle u,v\rangle=0$}

= Orthogonal
{synonym}

= Orthogonality
{synonym}

Two <vectors> are orthogonal when their <inner product> is zero.

For real <Euclidean vectors>, the <inner product> is the <dot product>, so $(3,4)$ and $(4,-3)$ are <orthogonal>: $3(4)+4(-3)=0$. For nonzero <Euclidean vectors>, this means their <angle> is $90^\circ$. The <zero vector> is <orthogonal> to every <vector>, although it has no direction or defined <angle>.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/resources/lecture-14-orthogonal-vectors-and-subspaces/[Strang, MIT 18.06, Lecture 14: orthogonal vectors and subspaces]{ref}

In an abstract <inner product space>, <orthogonality> always refers to its chosen <inner product>. For example, the <weighted inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle_w=4u_1v_1+u_2v_2$ makes $(1,1)$ and $(1,-4)$ <orthogonal>, since $4-4=0$. Their ordinary <dot product> is $-3$, so their arrows are not perpendicular in the usual <Cartesian coordinate system>. The algebraic definition also applies to <functions>, where arrows need not be available.

= Orthogonality does not diagonalize a varying potential
{parent=Orthogonal vectors}
{title2=$\langle\phi_1,\phi_2\rangle=0\ \nRightarrow\ \langle\phi_1,V\phi_2\rangle=0$}

In the <Hilbert space> $L^2([0,2\pi])$, choose <orthonormal> functions $\phi_1(x)=\cos x/\sqrt\pi$ and $\phi_2(x)=\sin x/\sqrt\pi$. Their <inner product> is zero, but for the real <potential energy> $V(x)=V_0\sin2x$,
$$
\langle\phi_1,V\phi_2\rangle
=\frac{V_0}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos x\sin x\sin2x\,dx
=\frac{V_0}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2(2x)\,dx
=\boxed{\frac{V_0}{2}}.
$$
Thus ordinary <orthogonality> alone does not remove an off-diagonal <matrix element> of a spatially varying <potential energy>. A constant scalar potential does give zero between distinct <orthonormal> states. The example separates these two claims; it is not a model of localized atomic orbitals in a particular crystal.

= Orthogonal basis
{parent=Orthogonal vectors}
{title2=$\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=0\quad(i\ne j)$}

An <orthogonal basis> of an inner-product <vector space> consists of nonzero mutually <orthogonal vectors> that span the space. The <orthogonal projection> of $v$ onto a finite-dimensional span is $\sum_i\langle v,u_i\rangle u_i/\langle u_i,u_i\rangle$. Dividing each vector by its norm produces an <orthonormal basis>. For centered random variables, the inner product is <covariance>; an orthogonal basis therefore consists of uncorrelated variables, without requiring unit variance.

= Nonorthogonal vectors
{parent=Inner product}

= Nonorthogonal
{synonym}

Two <vectors> are nonorthogonal when their <inner product> is nonzero. In the <power method>, the starting vector must be nonorthogonal to a dominant <eigenvector>.

= Gram-Schmidt process
{parent=Inner product}
{c}
{wiki=Gram–Schmidt_process}
{title2=$u_j=v_j-\sum_{i<j}\langle v_j,q_i\rangle q_i,\quad q_j=u_j/\|u_j\|$}

= Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization
{c}
{synonym}

The <Gram-Schmidt process> turns a finite ordered family of <linearly independent> <vectors> $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ in an <inner product space> over the <real numbers> into an <orthonormal basis> $q_1,\ldots,q_k$ of their <linear span>. It keeps every successive <linear span>, so the first $j$ new <vectors> describe exactly the same directions as the first $j$ inputs. Its idea is geometric: remove the <orthogonal projection> onto directions already found, then divide the remaining <vector> by its <norm>. This makes a complicated <basis> easier to use without changing the <vector subspace> it describes.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/7ac32be444c25e48590f47573833ccc6_MIT18_06SCF11_Ses2.4sum.pdf[MIT 18.06SC, Orthogonal matrices and Gram-Schmidt, pp. 1–3]{ref}

Start with $u_1=v_1$ and $q_1=v_1/\|v_1\|$. At step $j$, use the previously obtained <orthonormal set> to define
$$
u_j=v_j-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle v_j,q_i\rangle q_i,
\qquad q_j=\frac{u_j}{\|u_j\|},
\qquad \|u_j\|=\sqrt{\langle u_j,u_j\rangle}.
$$
The sum is the <orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace> $W_{j-1}=\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_{j-1})$, and $u_j$ is the <orthogonal projection residual>. <Linear independence> ensures $u_j\ne0$: otherwise $v_j$ would already be a <linear combination> of earlier inputs. If the input family is <linearly dependent>, use only the retained <orthonormal> <vectors> in the sum and omit an input whenever its remainder is the <zero vector>; do not divide by zero. The retained outputs still form an <orthonormal basis> of the original <linear span>, but their number can be smaller than $k$.

For a short verification, <orthonormality> makes every earlier <inner product> vanish:
$$
\langle u_j,q_\ell\rangle
=\langle v_j,q_\ell\rangle-\sum_{i<j}\langle v_j,q_i\rangle\langle q_i,q_\ell\rangle=0
\quad(\ell<j).
$$
Also $u_j$ is $v_j$ minus a <linear combination> of earlier <vectors>, while $v_j$ is $u_j$ plus that same <linear combination>. These two inclusions, followed by division by a nonzero <norm>, give
$$
\operatorname{span}(q_1,\ldots,q_j)=\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_j).
$$
The argument also works in an <inner product space> over the <complex numbers> with the existing <inner product> convention, linear in the first argument: the <coefficients> remain $\langle v_j,q_i\rangle$. With the convention linear in the second argument, write $\langle q_i,v_j\rangle$ instead.

For a first picture, take $v_1=(1,1)^T$ and $v_2=(1,0)^T$ in $\mathbb R^2$ with the ordinary <dot product>. Then
$$
q_1=\frac{(1,1)^T}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\langle v_2,q_1\rangle q_1=\frac12(1,1)^T,\qquad
u_2=\frac12(1,-1)^T,\qquad
q_2=\frac{(1,-1)^T}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Subtracting the diagonal component leaves an <orthogonal> direction; dividing by its <norm> $1/\sqrt2$ makes its length one. If the second input were $(2,2)^T$ instead, its <orthogonal projection> would be the entire input and $u_2=0$, revealing <linear dependence>.

\Image[gram-schmidt-vector-steps.png]
{title=<Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization> in $\mathbb R^2$: input <vectors>, subtraction of an <orthogonal projection>, and an <orthonormal basis>}

For a full three-dimensional calculation, use the ordinary <dot product> and the input <vectors>
$$
v_1=(1,1,0)^T,\qquad v_2=(1,0,1)^T,\qquad v_3=(0,1,1)^T.
$$
The first two steps give
$$
q_1=\frac{(1,1,0)^T}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\langle v_2,q_1\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad
u_2=(1/2,-1/2,1)^T,
$$
$$
\|u_2\|^2=\frac32,\qquad
q_2=\frac{(1,-1,2)^T}{\sqrt6}.
$$
At the third step, the <inner products> are $\langle v_3,q_1\rangle=1/\sqrt2$ and $\langle v_3,q_2\rangle=1/\sqrt6$. Consequently
$$
u_3=(0,1,1)^T-\frac12(1,1,0)^T-\frac16(1,-1,2)^T
=\left(-\frac23,\frac23,\frac23\right)^T,
$$
$$
\|u_3\|^2=\frac43,\qquad
q_3=\frac{(-1,1,1)^T}{\sqrt3}.
$$
The three numerator <vectors> have pairwise <dot products> $0$ and squared <Euclidean norms> $2,6,3$. Thus the displayed outputs are indeed an <orthonormal basis>, and the three nonzero remainders also confirm <linear independence> of the inputs. Keeping the removed <coefficients> produces the <QR decomposition> below.

An immediate reward is a reusable <orthogonal projection>: once an <orthonormal basis> of a <vector subspace> $W$ is known, any <vector> $b$ has
$$
P_Wb=\sum_i\langle b,q_i\rangle q_i.
$$
For the <linear span> of just the first two inputs in the three-dimensional example, $b=(0,0,1)^T$ gives $P_Wb=(1/3,-1/3,2/3)^T$. Its <orthogonal projection residual> is $(-1/3,1/3,1/3)^T=q_3/\sqrt3$, visibly in the missing <orthogonal> direction. This is the geometry behind the <linear least-squares problem>.

The formulas above describe exact arithmetic. With <floating-point arithmetic>, subtracting an <orthogonal projection> nearly equal to the input can leave a small remainder; dividing by its small <norm> can magnify existing <roundoff error>. The <classical Gram-Schmidt> and <modified Gram-Schmidt> arrangements agree in exact arithmetic but can give different rounded results. Exact <linear dependence> and a small computed remainder therefore require different judgments in a numerical implementation.https://nhigham.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/high95m.pdf[Higham, The Test Matrix Toolbox for Matlab, §8, p. 22]{ref}

= Classical Gram-Schmidt
{parent=Gram-Schmidt process}
{c}

<Classical Gram-Schmidt> computes all <orthogonal projection> <coefficients> $\langle v_j,q_i\rangle$ from the original input $v_j$, then subtracts their sum as in the displayed <Gram-Schmidt process> formula.

= Modified Gram-Schmidt
{parent=Gram-Schmidt process}
{c}
{title2=$w\leftarrow w-\langle w,q_i\rangle q_i$}

<Modified Gram-Schmidt> starts with $w=v_j$ and, for each retained <orthonormal> <vector> $q_i$ in order, replaces $w$ by $w-\langle w,q_i\rangle q_i$. Each <orthogonal projection> <coefficient> uses the current remainder. In exact arithmetic, subtracting earlier <orthogonal> directions does not change the <inner product> with $q_i$, so the final $w$ equals the <classical Gram-Schmidt> remainder $u_j$; then divide by $\|w\|$ if it is nonzero.

= QR decomposition
{title2=$A=QR$}
{c}
{parent=Gram-Schmidt process}
{wiki}

= QR factorization
{c}
{synonym}

A reduced <QR decomposition> of an $m\times k$ <matrix> over the <real numbers> with <full column rank>, $m\geq k$, writes $A=QR$, where $Q$ is $m\times k$ with <orthonormal> columns and $R$ is a $k\times k$ <upper triangular matrix> with positive diagonal. Applying the <Gram-Schmidt process> to the columns $v_j$ of $A$ constructs $Q=(q_1\ \cdots\ q_k)$ and records the <coefficients>
$$
r_{ij}=\begin{cases}
\langle v_j,q_i\rangle,&i<j,\\
\|u_j\|,&i=j,\\
0,&i>j.
\end{cases}
\qquad v_j=\sum_{i\leq j}r_{ij}q_i.
$$
This last column identity is exactly the <matrix multiplication> $A=QR$. The <upper triangular matrix> records that the $j$th input uses only the first $j$ new directions, while its positive diagonal records the <norms> of the nonzero <orthogonal projection residuals>. Since $Q^TQ=I$, one also has $R=Q^TA$; $Q^T$ denotes the <transpose> and $I$ the <identity matrix>. Over the <complex numbers>, use the <conjugate transpose> $Q^*$ in these identities.

For the three-dimensional <Gram-Schmidt process> example, the exact <matrices> are
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}
1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt6&-1/\sqrt3\\
1/\sqrt2&-1/\sqrt6&1/\sqrt3\\
0&2/\sqrt6&1/\sqrt3
\end{pmatrix},
$$
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}
\sqrt2&1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt2\\
0&\sqrt{3/2}&1/\sqrt6\\
0&0&2/\sqrt3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For example, the last column reconstructs as
$$
\frac{q_1}{\sqrt2}+\frac{q_2}{\sqrt6}+\frac{2q_3}{\sqrt3}
=\frac12(1,1,0)^T+\frac16(1,-1,2)^T+\frac23(-1,1,1)^T
=(0,1,1)^T.
$$
The diagonal entries are approximately $1.414214,1.224745,1.154701$. Each is a <norm> of the new direction before it was scaled to length one.

\Image[gram-schmidt-qr-factorization.png]
{title=The computed <QR factorization>: input <matrix> $A$, <orthonormal> columns of $Q$, and the <upper triangular matrix> $R$ of <orthogonal projection> <coefficients>}

For a <linear least-squares problem>, the <orthogonal projection> of $b$ onto the <column space> of $A$ is $QQ^Tb$. Hence the minimizing <coefficients> solve the <linear system> $Rx=Q^Tb$ by <back substitution>. Let $A_2=(v_1\ v_2)$ consist of the first two columns of the example; its reduced factors are $Q_2=(q_1\ q_2)$ and the leading $2\times2$ block $R_2$ of $R$. For $b=(0,0,1)^T$, the <linear system> $R_2x=Q_2^Tb$ is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2&1/\sqrt2\\0&\sqrt{3/2}\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\2/\sqrt6\end{pmatrix},
\qquad x_2=\frac23,\quad x_1=-\frac13.
$$
Thus $A_2x=(1/3,-1/3,2/3)^T$, agreeing with the <orthogonal projection> computed above, and the minimum <Euclidean norm> of $A_2x-b$ is $1/\sqrt3$. The <QR decomposition> separates building an <orthonormal basis> from recovering <coefficients> in the original <basis>.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-065-matrix-methods-in-data-analysis-signal-processing-and-machine-learning-spring-2018/resources/lecture-9-four-ways-to-solve-least-squares-problems/[Strang, MIT 18.065, Lecture 9: Four Ways to Solve Least Squares Problems]{ref}

= Positive-diagonal QR decomposition preserves a complex structure
{parent=QR decomposition}

Let $K$ be block diagonal with $2\times2$ blocks $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$. If a real invertible <matrix> $A$ commutes with $K$, its <QR decomposition> $A=BC$, with $B$ orthogonal and $C$ upper triangular with positive diagonal, has both $BK=KB$ and $CK=KC$. Indeed $CKC^{-1}=B^{-1}KB$, so $E=K^{-1}CKC^{-1}$ is orthogonal and upper triangular in $2\times2$ blocks. Orthogonality forces its off-diagonal blocks to vanish. Its diagonal blocks are $J^{-1}C_iJC_i^{-1}$. For $C_i=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $a,d>0$, this block is $\begin{pmatrix}d/a&-b/a\\-b/a&(a^2+b^2)/(ad)\end{pmatrix}$. Orthogonality of its columns forces $b=0,d=a$, and hence each block is the identity. Thus $E=I$, proving the claim.

= Thin QR factorization from Cholesky decomposition
{parent=QR decomposition}
{title2=$A=QR,\quad Q^TQ=I,\quad R^TR=A^TA$}

If a real $m$-by-$n$ matrix $A$ has full column rank, with $m\ge n$, then $A^TA$ is <positive definite>. Its upper-triangular <Cholesky decomposition> $A^TA=R^TR$, with positive diagonal, is unique. Defining $Q=AR^{-1}$ gives $Q^TQ=I$ and $A=QR$. Any other such factorization yields another Cholesky factor of $A^TA$, so its $R$ and then its $Q$ must agree. This proves existence and uniqueness; it does not assert that forming $A^TA$ is the most numerically stable algorithm.

= Orthogonal coordinate reduction of a subspace
{parent=QR decomposition}

For a full-column-rank $n\times k$ matrix $V$, extend the thin <QR decomposition> $V=Q_1R_1$ to an <orthogonal matrix> $Q=(Q_1\ Q_2)$. Then
$$
Q^TV=\begin{pmatrix}R_1\\0\end{pmatrix},
$$
so $Q^T$ maps the column space of $V$ onto the first $k$ coordinate directions. Successive <Householder transformations> construct the same reduction without first forming a full orthogonal basis.

= Linear least-squares problem
{title2=$\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2$}
{parent=QR decomposition}

A linear least-squares problem minimizes $\|Ax-b\|_2$ over $x$. If $A=QR$ has full column rank, orthogonality reduces it to the upper-triangular system formed from the leading block of $R$ and the leading entries of $Q^Tb$.

= Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization for a symmetric bilinear form
{parent=Gram-Schmidt process}

For a symmetric bilinear form $B$, the same projection formula
$$
u_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\frac{B(v_k,u_j)}{B(u_j,u_j)}u_j
$$
produces a $B$-orthogonal basis whenever the chosen pivots $B(u_j,u_j)$ are nonzero. Reordering the input vectors can avoid a zero pivot when a suitable anisotropic vector remains.
