= Solution
{parent=a}

A <regularization of an inverse problem> is a family of continuous maps $R_\alpha:\mathcal V\to\mathcal U$, $\alpha>0$, such that
$$
R_\alpha f\longrightarrow A^\dagger f\qquad(\alpha\downarrow0)
$$
for every $f$ in the domain of the <Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator>. A linear regularization has each $R_\alpha$ bounded and linear. For example, <Tikhonov regularization> gives
$$
R_\alpha=(A^*A+\alpha I)^{-1}A^*.
$$
A nonlinear example is <quartic-norm variational regularization>:
$$
R_\alpha f=\arg\min_u\left\{\|Au-f\|^2+\alpha\|u\|^4\right\}.
$$
Its strictly convex norm penalty selects the same <minimum-norm least-squares solution> as $\alpha\downarrow0$, but the map from data to minimizer is generally nonlinear.

A <convergent regularization of an inverse problem> additionally has a parameter rule $\alpha(\delta,f_\delta)>0$ such that, for every admissible exact $f$,
$$
\boxed{\sup_{\|f_\delta-f\|\leq\delta}
\|R_{\alpha(\delta,f_\delta)}f_\delta-A^\dagger f\|\longrightarrow0
\quad(\delta\downarrow0).}
$$
An <a priori regularization parameter choice> depends only on $\delta$ and fixed information about the problem.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $u^\dagger=A^\dagger f$. Linearity and the <triangle inequality> give the <noise-bias decomposition for linear regularization>
$$
\|R_{\alpha(\delta)}f_\delta-u^\dagger\|
\leq\|R_{\alpha(\delta)}(f_\delta-f)\|
+\|R_{\alpha(\delta)}f-u^\dagger\|
\leq\delta\|R_{\alpha(\delta)}\|
+\|R_{\alpha(\delta)}f-A^\dagger f\|.
$$
The first term tends to zero by the assumed noise-amplification bound. The second tends to zero because $\alpha(\delta)\to0$ and $R_\alpha$ is a <regularization of an inverse problem>. The right side is independent of the particular noisy datum within its allowed ball. Its convergence therefore proves the uniform noisy-data convergence required of a <convergent regularization of an inverse problem>.

= Solution

Use the <singular system of a compact operator> convention $Ax_j=\sigma_jy_j$ and $A^*y_j=\sigma_jx_j$. Starting from zero gives no component in $\ker A$. Write $x(t)=\sum_jc_j(t)x_j$. The coefficient equations are
$$
c_j'=-\sigma_j^2c_j+\sigma_j\langle f,y_j\rangle,\qquad c_j(0)=0.
$$
Solving these scalar <linear ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
c_j(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_j^2t}}{\sigma_j}\langle f,y_j\rangle.
$$
Consequently <asymptotic regularization> has the <spectral regularization method> representation
$$
\boxed{u_\alpha=\sum_j\frac{1-e^{-\sigma_j^2/\alpha}}{\sigma_j}\langle f,y_j\rangle x_j.}
$$
The component of $f$ orthogonal to the closure of the range of $A$ is annihilated by $A^*$ and contributes nothing. The scalar filter satisfies $(1-e^{-\sigma^2/\alpha})/\sigma\leq\min(\sigma/\alpha,1/\sigma)\leq\alpha^{-1/2}$, so every fixed-$\alpha$ reconstruction is a bounded <linear operator>. Its long-time limit is the <Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator> on admissible data.

For a <Banach space> $X$, <weak convergence> $u_k\rightharpoonup u$ means
$$
\ell(u_k)\to\ell(u)\quad\text{for every }\ell\in X^*.
$$
For its <continuous dual space> $X^*$, convergence in the <weak-star topology> means
$$
\ell_k(x)\to\ell(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in X.
$$
Weak convergence on $X^*$ tests against all of $X^{**}$, whereas weak-star convergence tests only against the canonical image of $X$. Thus weak convergence always implies weak-star convergence. If $X$ is a <reflexive Banach space>, that image is all of $X^{**}$, so **the weak and weak-star topologies on $X^*$ coincide**. Weak-star topology is defined relative to a chosen predual; it is not an extra intrinsic topology on an arbitrary Banach space without such an identification.

= Solution

The <Radon-Riesz property> of a norm is the implication
$$
u_k\rightharpoonup u,\quad\|u_k\|\to\|u\|
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|u_k-u\|\to0.
$$
In a <Hilbert space>, expand the square using its <inner product>:
$$
\|u_k-u\|^2=\|u_k\|^2+\|u\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle u_k,u\rangle.
$$
<Weak convergence> gives $\langle u_k,u\rangle\to\|u\|^2$, and convergence of norms gives $\|u_k\|^2\to\|u\|^2$. Hence the right side tends to zero. This proves the Hilbert-space <Radon-Riesz theorem>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The assumptions prove convergence to the set of <J-minimizing solutions>, and subsequential convergence to a member of that set. Convergence to a single fixed member requires uniqueness or a suitable selection rule.

Let $u^\dagger$ be any <J-minimizing solution>, whose existence is granted. It is feasible for every noisy-data constraint because $Au^\dagger=f$ and $\|f-f_\delta\|\leq\delta$. Minimality therefore gives $J(u_\delta)\leq J(u^\dagger)$. All reconstructions lie in this strongly <sequentially compact space> sublevel set. For any sequence $\delta_k\downarrow0$, pass to a subsequence with $u_{\delta_k}\to u_*$. Boundedness of $A$ and the <triangle inequality> imply
$$
\|Au_{\delta_k}-f\|\leq\|Au_{\delta_k}-f_{\delta_k}\|+\|f_{\delta_k}-f\|\leq2\delta_k,
$$
so $Au_*=f$. By <sequential lower semicontinuity>,
$$
J(u_*)\leq\liminf_k J(u_{\delta_k})\leq J(u^\dagger).
$$
Thus $u_*$ is a <J-minimizing solution>. The same compactness argument shows the <distance to a set> of exact minimizers tends to zero: otherwise a subsequence at a fixed positive distance would converge to an exact minimizer, a contradiction. If that minimizer is unique, every convergent subsequence has the same limit, giving the asserted full <strong convergence>.

For a counterexample to full convergence under the printed hypotheses alone, take $\mathcal U=\mathcal V=\mathbb R$, $A=0$, $f=f_\delta=0$, and $J(u)=\max\{|u|-1,0\}$. This is nonnegative and continuous, with compact sublevel sets $[-1-C,1+C]$ for $C\geq0$. Every point of $[-1,1]$ is both an exact and a residual-method minimizer. Choosing $u_{1/k}=(-1)^k$ gives a valid sequence of minimizers that does not converge. The reusable corrected statement is <compact-sublevel convergence of the residual method>.

For $u\in L^1(\Omega)$, the <total variation seminorm on a domain> is
$$
\operatorname{TV}(u)=\sup_{\substack{\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)\\\|\varphi\|_\infty\leq1}}
\int_\Omega u\,\operatorname{div}\varphi\,dx.
$$
Using $C_c^1$ instead gives the same definition. The zero test field shows nonnegativity, and $\operatorname{TV}(0)=0$, so this is a <proper extended-real function>. Each test field defines a linear functional of $u$; taking their supremum proves <convexity>, equivalently
$$
\operatorname{TV}(tu+(1-t)v)\leq t\operatorname{TV}(u)+(1-t)\operatorname{TV}(v),\qquad0\leq t\leq1.
$$
It is **not strictly convex**: two distinct constant functions both have zero total variation, as does every convex combination of them.

The <function of bounded variation on a domain> space is
$$
BV(\Omega)=\{u\in L^1(\Omega):\operatorname{TV}(u)<\infty\},\qquad
\|u\|_{BV}=\|u\|_{L^1}+\operatorname{TV}(u).
$$
Total variation is **not coercive on $BV(\Omega)$**: for $u_k\equiv k$ on a domain of positive measure, $\|u_k\|_{BV}=k|\Omega|\to\infty$, whereas $\operatorname{TV}(u_k)=0$. This is <noncoercivity of total variation on constants>; a mean-zero constraint can remove the constant obstruction.

= Solution

An <absolutely one-homogeneous functional> satisfies
$$
J(cu)=|c|J(u)\quad\text{for every scalar }c,
$$
with $J(0)=0$. The printed strictly positive codomain cannot hold at zero; the standard interpretation is a nonnegative, possibly extended-real functional.

For $p\in\partial J(u)$, apply the <subgradient inequality> at $v=0$:
$$
0=J(0)\geq J(u)-\langle p,u\rangle,
$$
so $\langle p,u\rangle\geq J(u)$. Apply it at $v=2u$ and use one-homogeneity:
$$
2J(u)=J(2u)\geq J(u)+\langle p,u\rangle,
$$
which gives the reverse inequality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{J(u)=\langle p,u\rangle.}
$$
This is the <Euler identity for a convex one-homogeneous functional>; differentiability is unnecessary.

Use the interior-domain definition of <total variation seminorm on a domain>, so there is no boundary-extension term at $\pm1$. For any admissible scalar <test function> $\varphi$,
$$
\int_{-1}^1u_R(x)\varphi'(x)\,dx
=\int_{-R}^R\varphi'(x)\,dx
=\varphi(R)-\varphi(-R)\leq2.
$$
Thus $\operatorname{TV}(u_R)\leq2$.

Choose $0<\varepsilon<\min\{R,1-R\}$. From the supplied <smooth bump function>, define
$$
\varphi(x)=e\,\omega_\varepsilon(x-R)-e\,\omega_\varepsilon(x+R).
$$
The two supports are disjoint and inside $(-1,1)$. Each bump has maximum $e^{-1}$, so $\|\varphi\|_\infty\leq1$, while $\varphi(R)=1$ and $\varphi(-R)=-1$. This attains the upper bound, proving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{TV}(u_R)=2.}
$$
Equivalently, its <distributional derivative> is $Du_R=\delta_{-R}-\delta_R$, with total variation two. This is <total variation of an interval indicator>.

= Solution

For a convex functional $J$ and $p\in\partial J(v)$, the <generalized Bregman distance> is
$$
\boxed{D_J^p(u,v)=J(u)-J(v)-\langle p,u-v\rangle\geq0.}
$$
The inequality is exactly the <subgradient inequality>.

Suppose $J$ is <strictly convex> and $u\ne v$, with both values finite. If the distance vanished, the supporting affine function at $v$ would also meet $J$ at $u$. At $w=(1-t)v+tu$, $0<t<1$, the subgradient inequality would give
$$
J(w)\geq J(v)+t\langle p,u-v\rangle
=(1-t)J(v)+tJ(u),
$$
contradicting strict convexity. Hence zero distance implies $u=v$.

Without strict convexity, take $J(s)=|s|$, $v=1$, and $p=1\in\partial J(1)$. For every $u\geq0$,
$$
D_J^1(u,1)=u-1-(u-1)=0,
$$
including $u=2\ne1$. Thus **zero Bregman distance need not identify a point**. It identifies points on the same supporting face, as explained by <zero Bregman distance and supporting faces>.

= Solution

The <source condition in variational regularization> at an exact solution $u^\dagger$ is the existence of a source element $\mu^\dagger\in\mathcal V^*$ such that
$$
\boxed{A^*\mu^\dagger\in\partial J(u^\dagger).}
$$
For a Hilbert data space one identifies $\mathcal V^*$ with $\mathcal V$. If $u$ satisfies $Au=f$ and $A^*\mu\in\partial J(u)$, then every other exact solution $v$ obeys the <subgradient inequality>
$$
J(v)\geq J(u)+\langle A^*\mu,v-u\rangle
=J(u)+\langle\mu,Av-Au\rangle=J(u).
$$
Hence $u$ is a <J-minimizing solution>. This is <source-condition certification of a penalty-minimizing solution>.

= Solution

Let $u^\dagger=u_J^\dagger$, $p^\dagger=A^*\mu^\dagger\in\partial J(u^\dagger)$, and $r_\alpha=Au_\alpha-f$. The <subgradient inequality>, the <dual pairing> bound, and $Au^\dagger=f$ give
$$
J(u_\alpha)-J(u^\dagger)\geq\langle p^\dagger,u_\alpha-u^\dagger\rangle
=\langle\mu^\dagger,r_\alpha\rangle
\geq-\|\mu^\dagger\|\|r_\alpha\|.
$$
Minimality of $u_\alpha$ in the <exact penalty method> gives
$$
\|r_\alpha\|+\alpha[J(u_\alpha)-J(u^\dagger)]\leq0.
$$
Combining them yields
$$
(1-\alpha\|\mu^\dagger\|)\|r_\alpha\|\leq0.
$$
The coefficient is strictly positive, so
$$
\boxed{Au_\alpha=f.}
$$
Now minimality gives $J(u_\alpha)\leq J(u^\dagger)$, and exact feasibility plus the defining minimality of $u^\dagger$ gives the reverse inequality. Thus $u_\alpha$ is a <J-minimizing solution>. Finally,
$$
\boxed{D_J^{p^\dagger}(u_\alpha,u^\dagger)
=J(u_\alpha)-J(u^\dagger)-\langle\mu^\dagger,A(u_\alpha-u^\dagger)\rangle=0.}
$$
This <exact-penalty threshold from a source condition> does not require one-homogeneity; convexity and the source subgradient suffice. If $\mu^\dagger=0$, the same argument works for every $\alpha>0$, interpreting the upper threshold as infinite.
