= Solution
{parent=i}

Expand the secular <Poynting–Robertson drag> rates using $(1-e^2)^{-3/2}=1+\tfrac32e^2+O(e^4)$ and $(1-e^2)^{-1/2}=1+O(e^2)$. Through second order in <orbital eccentricity>,
$$
\boxed{\dot a_{\rm pr}=-\frac{2A}{a}(1+3e^2)+O(e^4),\qquad
\frac{d(e^2)}{dt}\bigg|_{\rm pr}=-\frac{5A}{a^2}e^2+O(e^4).}
$$
The second formula follows from $d(e^2)/dt=2e\dot e$. The cubic correction in $\dot e$ contributes only at fourth order in $d(e^2)/dt$.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In <resonant trapping of dust>, the resonant torque must compensate the inward <Poynting–Robertson drag>. At the nearly fixed resonant <semi-major axis>, $\dot a_{\rm res}+\dot a_{\rm pr}=0$ requires
$$
C e^q\sin\phi=-\frac{A}{a^2(p+q)}(1+3e^2).
$$
Since $|\sin\phi|\le1$, a necessary torque-balance condition is $|C|e^q\ge A(1+3e^2)/[a^2(p+q)]$. When the limiting <orbital eccentricity> is small, this gives
$$
\boxed{e_{\min}\simeq\left(\frac{A}{|C|a^2(p+q)}\right)^{1/q}.}
$$
Equality allows only the extreme phase $|\sin\phi|=1$, with no margin for restoring torque; a robust trapped state needs $e>e_{\min}$. This is a necessary condition for <resonance capture>, not a sufficient probability-of-capture criterion for a migrating particle.

= Solution

Write $u=e^2$ and use the torque balance from part (ii) to eliminate the <resonant argument>. The resonant contribution is
$$
\dot u_{\rm res}=2e\dot e_{\rm res}=-2qCe^q\sin\phi
=\frac{2qA}{a^2(p+q)}(1+3u).
$$
Adding the <Poynting–Robertson drag> contribution gives the <dissipative resonant eccentricity equilibrium> equation
$$
\dot u=\frac A{a^2}\left[\frac{2q}{p+q}-\frac{5p-q}{p+q}u\right]
=-\frac{u-B}{\tau},
$$
where
$$
\boxed{B=\frac{2q}{5p-q},\qquad \tau=\frac{a^2(p+q)}{A(5p-q)}.}
$$
For $5p>q$, integrating with $u(0)=e_0^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{e(t)=\sqrt{B+(e_0^2-B)e^{-t/\tau}}.}
$$
Here $a$ is the fixed resonant <semi-major axis> and the expression describes averaged evolution, neglecting the small <resonant-argument libration>. The physical bound-orbit interpretation also requires $B<1$; a quantitatively controlled second-order calculation needs $B\ll1$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

At $e_{\max}=\sqrt B$, torque balance gives
$$
\boxed{\sin\phi_0=-\frac{A(1+3B)}{Ca^2(p+q)B^{q/2}}
=-\frac{5A}{Ca^2(5p-q)B^{q/2}}.}
$$
The magnitude of the right-hand side must not exceed one. This fixes the dissipative offset of the <resonant-argument libration> centre.

For $C>0$, put $S=5A/[Ca^2(5p-q)B^{q/2}]$. The two torque-balanced phases are $\phi_0=-\arcsin S$ and $\phi_0=\pi+\arcsin S$, modulo $2\pi$. In the usual weak-perturbation pendulum approximation, $\delta\dot\phi\simeq-3(p+q)n\delta a/(2a)$ and $\delta\dot a\simeq-2(p+q)CaB^{q/2}\cos\phi_0\,\delta\phi$. Restoring libration thus selects $\cos\phi_0<0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\phi_0\simeq\pi+\arcsin S\quad(C>0).}
$$
It approaches $\pi$ when drag is weak compared with resonant torque. For $C<0$, the corresponding restoring branch has $\cos\phi_0>0$ and approaches zero. The sine equation is the direct result independent of this leading-order stability approximation.

At <periapsis>, the particle's <mean longitude> equals its <longitude of periapsis>: $\lambda=\varpi$. Hence the <pericentre geometry of an exterior resonance> gives
$$
\phi=p(\varpi-\lambda_p),\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\theta_{\rm peri}=\frac{\phi+2\pi j}{p},\quad j\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The <resonant argument> therefore fixes the angular separation from the planet whenever the particle reaches <periapsis>. With $r_{\rm peri}=a(1-e)$ and planetary radius $a_p$, the actual separation is
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm peri}^2=a_p^2+r_{\rm peri}^2-2a_pr_{\rm peri}\cos\Delta\theta_{\rm peri}.}
$$
Thus a restricted libration phase can prevent close encounters even when the radial ranges of the two orbits overlap: this is <resonance protection>.

= Solution
{parent=vi}

The <specific angular momentum> is $h=\sqrt{GM_\star a(1-e^2)}$, so the <periapsis angular speed> is
$$
\dot\theta_{\rm peri}=\frac h{a^2(1-e)^2}
=n\frac{\sqrt{1+e}}{(1-e)^{3/2}}.
$$
At the <exterior mean-motion resonance>, neglecting <radiation pressure>, $n/n_p=p/(p+q)$. Thus at the limiting <orbital eccentricity> $e=\sqrt{2q/(5p-q)}$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\dot\theta_{\rm peri}}{n_p}
=\frac p{p+q}\frac{\sqrt{1+\sqrt{2q/(5p-q)}}}{[1-\sqrt{2q/(5p-q)}]^{3/2}}.}
$$
The condition for overtaking the planet in angle is
$$
\boxed{\frac{p^2}{(p+q)^2}(1+e)>(1-e)^3,\qquad e=\sqrt{\frac{2q}{5p-q}}<1.}
$$
For the formal second-order equilibrium, it holds for every physical bound value $0<q<5p/3$. To see this, rewrite $q/p=5e^2/(2+e^2)$, so $p/(p+q)=(2+e^2)/(2+6e^2)$. For $0<e<1$, $\sqrt{1+e}/(1-e)^{3/2}>1+2e$, while
$$
(2+e^2)(1+2e)-(2+6e^2)=e(4-5e+2e^2)>0.
$$
The instantaneous angular motion is therefore prograde relative to the planet near <periapsis>, although its mean relative motion is retrograde. The range $q\ll p$ is the regime where the eccentricity expansion is most reliable.

= Solution

Assume spherical bodies of common <mass density> $\rho$, a well-mixed belt, and that $n(D)dD$ counts bodies in the entire belt rather than per unit volume. A body of diameter $D$ has mass $\pi\rho D^3/6$. The <mass normalization of a power-law size distribution> is
$$
M_{\rm tot}=\frac{\pi\rho K}{6}\int_{D_{\min}}^{D_{\max}}D^{3-\alpha}\,dD.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{K=\frac{6M_{\rm tot}}{\pi\rho}
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{4-\alpha}{D_{\max}^{4-\alpha}-D_{\min}^{4-\alpha}},&\alpha\ne4,\\
\displaystyle\frac1{\log(D_{\max}/D_{\min})},&\alpha=4.
\end{cases}}
$$
The thin-torus volume is circumference times radial width times full vertical height,
$$
V\simeq(2\pi r)\,dr\,(2I_{\max}r)=4\pi I_{\max}r^2dr.
$$
The local number-density distribution needed for a <planetesimal collision rate> is $n(D)/V$. Thus belt geometry does not enter this total-number $K$ but does enter collision rates. This approximation treats the stated height as uniform, independent of size.

= Solution

Let $d$ be an impactor diameter and assume it is much smaller than target diameter $D$, with equal material <mass density>. Neglect <gravitational focusing> and take the encounter speed to be the stated target-size law $v_{\rm rel}=v_pD^p$. The <specific impact energy> is
$$
Q=\frac12\left(\frac dD\right)^3v_p^2D^{2p}.
$$
A <catastrophic planetesimal collision> requires $Q\ge Q_D^*=Q_bD^b$. Therefore the <catastrophic projectile diameter> is
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm cc}=\left(\frac{2Q_b}{v_p^2}\right)^{1/3}D^{1+(b-2p)/3}.}
$$
Use $\max(D_{\rm cc},D_{\min})$ as the actual lower size cutoff when integrating a rate. If this exceeds $D_{\max}$, the specified population contains no destructive impactors. The small-impactor approximation itself requires $D_{\rm cc}\ll D$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

In the <gravitational focusing> regime, take $d\ll D$ and $v_{\rm rel}\ll v_{\rm esc}$ at large separation. Mutual gravitational acceleration then makes the impact speed approximately the target's <escape velocity>:
$$
v_{\rm imp}^2\simeq v_{\rm esc}^2
=\frac{2G(\pi\rho D^3/6)}{D/2}
=\frac{2\pi}{3}G\rho D^2.
$$
Setting the <specific impact energy> equal to the <catastrophic disruption threshold> gives
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm cc}\simeq\left(\frac{3Q_b}{\pi G\rho}\right)^{1/3}D^{(1+b)/3}.}
$$
The size-dependent far-field encounter speed drops out of this leading threshold because gravitational acceleration dominates at impact. <Gravitational focusing> also increases the collision cross-section by approximately $1+v_{\rm esc}^2/v_{\rm rel}^2$, but this concerns the rate rather than the energy threshold. These formulas assume the disruption-energy convention remains per unit target mass and that gravitationally accelerated impact energy couples to disruption as specified by $Q_D^*$.

= Solution

Neglect <gravitational focusing> and assume a fixed well-mixed volume $V$, $\alpha>1$, and $D_{\min}\ll D_{\rm cc}\ll D\ll D_{\max}$. For the small impactors that dominate the rate, the <geometric collision cross-section> is approximately $\pi D^2/4$. Thus
$$
R_{\rm cc}(D)\simeq\frac{\pi D^2}{4}\,v_pD^p\frac K V
\int_{D_{\rm cc}}^{D_{\max}}d^{-\alpha}\,dd.
$$
Evaluating and neglecting the upper-end term gives the <catastrophic planetesimal collision rate>
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm cc}(D)\simeq\frac{\pi Kv_p}{4V(\alpha-1)}D^{2+p}D_{\rm cc}^{1-\alpha}.}
$$
Using part (ii), its size dependence is
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm cc}(D)\propto D^{2+p+(1-\alpha)[1+(b-2p)/3]}.}
$$
If the destructive cutoff is near an endpoint or comparable to $D$, retain the full integral and the cross-section $\pi(D+d)^2/4$. The simple power law then need not apply.

= Solution

For equal logarithmic width $d\log D$, the number of bodies is $n(D)D\,d\log D$. Their mass is therefore
$$
dM=\frac{\pi\rho K}{6}D^{4-\alpha}\,d\log D.
$$
The mass removed by <catastrophic planetesimal collisions> per logarithmic bin per unit time scales as
$$
\dot M_{\rm loss}(D)\propto D^{4-\alpha}R_{\rm cc}(D).
$$
Assume a steady <collisional cascade> with supply only at the large end, loss only at the small end, negligible reassembly or cratering contributions, and self-similar fragmentation that transfers mass through comparable logarithmic intervals. <Mass conservation> requires the downward mass flux across every intermediate size boundary to be the same; otherwise mass would accumulate or deplete there. Under the scale-invariant bin-transfer assumption, this is proportional by a size-independent factor to $\dot M_{\rm loss}$, so
$$
\boxed{D^{4-\alpha}R_{\rm cc}(D)=\mathrm{constant}.}
$$
This is <constant mass flux in a collisional cascade>. Steady state alone fixes the net flux, while the identification with the destruction rate of each bin additionally uses the stated fragmentation assumptions.

Combining <constant mass flux in a collisional cascade> with the rate in part (iv) gives
$$
\dot M_{\rm loss}(D)\propto
D^{6+p-\alpha+(1-\alpha)(3+b-2p)/3}.
$$
Set the exponent to zero and multiply by three:
$$
3(6+p-\alpha)+(1-\alpha)(3+b-2p)=0,
$$
or $21+b+p-\alpha(6+b-2p)=0$. Therefore the <velocity-dependent collisional cascade> has
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{21+b+p}{6+b-2p}.}
$$
For $b=p=0$, this recovers the <Dohnanyi collisional cascade> slope $\alpha=7/2$. The denominator must be nonzero, and the resulting slope and destructive cutoff must satisfy the assumptions used for the collision-rate integral. A size-dependent vertical belt thickness would supply an additional volume factor and change this result.

Let $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1$ and $r=|\mathbf r|$. <Newton's second law> for the two <planetesimals> gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=Gm_2\frac{\mathbf r}{r^3}+\frac{\mathbf F_1}{m_1},\qquad
\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=-Gm_1\frac{\mathbf r}{r^3}+\frac{\mathbf F_2}{m_2}.
$$
Subtracting yields the forced relative <two-body problem>
$$
\boxed{\ddot{\mathbf r}+G(m_1+m_2)\frac{\mathbf r}{r^3}
=\frac{\mathbf F_2}{m_2}-\frac{\mathbf F_1}{m_1}.}
$$
The internal gravitational forces cancel from the <centre of mass> equation but add in the relative equation; external forces affect the internal orbit through their difference in acceleration.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $m=m_1+m_2$, $\mu=Gm$, $n=\sqrt{\mu/a^3}$, $v=na$, and $\theta=nt$. Work instantaneously in the inertial frame in which the <centre of mass> is at rest. The unperturbed circular relative orbit has
$$
\mathbf r=a(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad
\mathbf v=v(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta).
$$
The individual velocities are $\mathbf v_1=-(m_2/m)\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf v_2=(m_1/m)\mathbf v$. Linear <gas drag> gives $\mathbf F_i/m_i=-(\mathbf v_i-v_g\hat{\mathbf y})/t_i$, where $t_i=t_{si}$ are the <aerodynamic stopping times>. Define
$$
\kappa=\frac{m_1/t_2+m_2/t_1}{m},\qquad
\Delta=\frac1{t_2}-\frac1{t_1}.
$$
Then the <gas drag on a planetesimal binary> is
$$
\boxed{f_x=\kappa v\sin\theta,\qquad
f_y=-\kappa v\cos\theta+\Delta v_g.}
$$
Equivalently, $\mathbf f=-\kappa\mathbf v+\Delta v_g\hat{\mathbf y}$. If the <centre of mass> moves, $v_g\hat{\mathbf y}$ here is its relative gas velocity, held approximately fixed during one short binary orbit.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

With $\hat{\mathbf r}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$, project the relative <gas drag> to obtain
$$
\boxed{f_r=\Delta v_g\sin\theta,\qquad
f_\theta=-\kappa v+\Delta v_g\cos\theta.}
$$
The <specific orbital energy> is $\varepsilon=v^2/2-\mu/r=-\mu/(2a)$. Differentiation cancels the conservative gravitational work and gives $\dot\varepsilon=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f$. For the circular orbit,
$$
\dot\varepsilon=vf_\theta=-\kappa v^2+\Delta v_gv\cos\theta.
$$
Using $v^2=\mu/a$, the <orbit-averaged drag work> relation therefore gives the instantaneous leading-order change
$$
\boxed{\dot a=-2\kappa a+\frac{2\Delta v_ga^{3/2}}{\sqrt\mu}\cos\theta.}
$$
The differential headwind term can change sign during the orbit; the damping term always removes orbital energy.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For weak <gas drag>, evaluate the work on the unperturbed circular orbit and average over one <orbital period>. The gas velocity changes negligibly during that period and $\langle\cos\theta\rangle=0$. Hence <gas-driven contraction of a planetesimal binary> obeys
$$
\langle\dot a\rangle=-2\kappa a=-\frac a\tau,
\qquad
\boxed{\tau=\frac{m_1+m_2}{2(m_1/t_{s2}+m_2/t_{s1})}.}
$$
With constant <aerodynamic stopping times>,
$$
\boxed{a(t)=a_0e^{-t/\tau}.}
$$
This is the secular, orbit-averaged separation. It assumes the perturbations are small enough for the orbit to remain nearly circular; a large differential headwind can excite eccentricity or disrupt a weakly bound pair instead.

= Solution
{parent=v}

Let $\mathbf V$ be the <centre of mass> velocity and $\mathbf v$ the binary relative velocity. Summing the two linear <gas drag> forces gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf V}\big|_{\rm drag}
=-\kappa_b(\mathbf V-\mathbf v_g)
-\frac{m_1m_2}{(m_1+m_2)^2}\Delta\mathbf v,
\qquad
\kappa_b=\frac{m_1/t_{s1}+m_2/t_{s2}}{m_1+m_2}.
$$
The last term averages to zero over the internal circular orbit. On the nearly circular stellar orbit, $\mathbf V\simeq v_K\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and $\mathbf v_g=(1-\eta)v_K\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$, so the averaged tangential acceleration is $-\eta\kappa_bv_K$.

With <specific orbital energy> $-GM_\star/(2a_b)$, the <orbit-averaged drag work> is $-\eta\kappa_bv_K^2$, where $v_K^2=GM_\star/a_b$. Thus <gas-driven migration of a binary centre of mass> gives
$$
\boxed{\dot a_b=-2\eta\kappa_ba_b=-\frac{a_b}{\tau_b},\qquad
\tau_b=\frac{m_1+m_2}{2\eta(m_1/t_{s1}+m_2/t_{s2})}.}
$$
This uses the weak-drag, nearly Keplerian approximation implicit in applying the circular-orbit work relation. Strongly gas-coupled orbits require a coupled radial-azimuthal drift solution.

Put $\epsilon=m_2/m_1\ll1$ and $t_{s2}/t_{s1}=\epsilon^k$, with $0<k<1$. The <gas-driven contraction of a planetesimal binary> and <gas-driven migration of a binary centre of mass> times obey
$$
\frac\tau{\tau_b}=\eta\frac{m_1/t_{s1}+m_2/t_{s2}}{m_1/t_{s2}+m_2/t_{s1}}
=\eta\frac{1+\epsilon^{1-k}}{\epsilon^{-k}+\epsilon}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac\tau{\tau_b}\simeq\eta\epsilon^k\ll1.}
$$
The low-mass secondary has the shorter <aerodynamic stopping time> and chiefly controls internal damping, whereas the primary chiefly controls <centre of mass> drag. The pair can contract substantially, potentially merging, before its stellar orbital radius changes appreciably. This conclusion is restricted to a pair that remains bound and within the adopted linear-drag regime.

The variables $y_j=I_je^{i\Omega_j}$ are <complex inclinations>, despite the question's description as eccentricities. Diagonalize the <Laplace-Lagrange inclination matrix>: let real <eigenvectors> $\mathbf v_k$ satisfy $B\mathbf v_k=\lambda_k\mathbf v_k$, and form $V=(\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3)$. The physical matrix is diagonalizable by <positive diagonal symmetrization of a matrix>, as shown below.

The <matrix exponential> solution is
$$
\mathbf y(t)=e^{iBt}\mathbf y(0)
=\sum_{k=1}^3 c_k\mathbf v_ke^{i\lambda_kt},\qquad
(c_1,c_2,c_3)^T=V^{-1}\mathbf y(0).
$$
Write $c_k=I_ke^{i\gamma_k}$ with $I_k\ge0$, and define $I_{jk}=I_kv_{jk}$. This gives
$$
\boxed{y_j(t)=\sum_{k=1}^3I_{jk}e^{i(\lambda_kt+\gamma_k)}.}
$$
The <eigenvalues> determine nodal precession frequencies, the <eigenvectors> determine relative inclinations within each <secular eigenmode>, and the initial <complex inclinations> determine their amplitudes and phases. The real $I_{jk}$ can have either sign, encoding opposite nodal phases. The zero-frequency mode is a common fixed tilt.

Let $a=B_{12}$, $b=B_{13}$, $c=B_{21}$, $d=B_{23}$, $e=B_{31}$, and $f=B_{32}$. Zero row sums put $B$ in the form used in the <spectrum of a three-dimensional matrix with zero row sums>. In particular, $B(1,1,1)^T=0$. Define
$$
S=a+b+c+d+e+f,
$$
$$
T=ad+bc+bd+ae+af+bf+ce+cf+de.
$$
Expanding the <characteristic polynomial> gives $\det(\lambda I-B)=\lambda(\lambda^2+S\lambda+T)$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0=0,\qquad\lambda_\pm=\frac{-S\pm\sqrt{S^2-4T}}2.}
$$
For the physical coefficients, $L_jB_{jk}=L_kB_{kj}$ with $L_j=M_j\sqrt{GM_\star a_j}$. Thus $W=\operatorname{diag}(L_j)$ gives <positive diagonal symmetrization of a matrix>, and
$$
\mathbf z^\dagger WB\mathbf z=-\sum_{j<k}L_jB_{jk}|z_j-z_k|^2\le0.
$$
Consequently the <eigenvalues> are real and nonpositive, and $B$ has a complete <eigenvector> basis. This justifies the mode expansion in part (i).

For small $\alpha$, expand the defining integrand of the <Laplace coefficient>:
$$
(1-2\alpha\cos x+\alpha^2)^{-3/2}=1+3\alpha\cos x+O(\alpha^2).

Hence
$$
b_{3/2}^{(1)}(\alpha)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}\cos x[1+3\alpha\cos x+O(\alpha^2)]\,dx.

The constant term integrates to zero and $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2x\,dx=\pi$, giving
$$
\boxed{b_{3/2}^{(1)}(\alpha)=3\alpha+O(\alpha^3).}

There is no quadratic term: its factors are combinations of $\cos x$ and $\cos^3x$, which both integrate to zero over a period. The linear approximation is used for the distant planet, not for the closely spaced inner pair.

Keep the inner-pair <Laplace coefficient> unexpanded because $\alpha=a_1/a_2$ is close to one. Put $\ell=L_1/L_2$ and

The distant couplings $B_{13},B_{23}$ scale as $\beta^3$ relative to a fixed inner architecture, and the reverse couplings have an additional $\sqrt\beta$ suppression.

Ignoring the distant planet at first, the inner two-by-two block has <eigenvalues> zero and $-(B_{12}+B_{21})$. Therefore its fast differential <secular eigenmode> has

\boxed{|\lambda_{\rm fast}|\simeq B_{12}(1+L_1/L_2).}
$$
For the slow mode, strong inner coupling makes $y_1\simeq y_2=y_{\rm in}$. Add the first two evolution equations weighted by <angular momenta> and divide by $L_{12}=L_1+L_2$. The <rigid inner pair in hierarchical secular inclination dynamics> has equations
$$
\dot y_{\rm in}=ic(y_3-y_{\rm in}),\qquad
\dot y_3=ic\frac{L_{12}}{L_3}(y_{\rm in}-y_3),
$$
with
$$
c=\frac{L_1B_{13}+L_2B_{23}}{L_{12}}
\simeq B_{13}\frac{L_1/L_2+\alpha^{-3/2}}{1+L_1/L_2}.
$$
The two-plane matrix has <eigenvalues> zero and $-c(1+L_{12}/L_3)$. The architecture implies $L_3\gg L_{12}$ for comparable masses, so its leading slow frequency is
$$
\boxed{|\lambda_{\rm slow}|\simeq B_{13}\frac{L_1/L_2+\alpha^{-3/2}}{1+L_1/L_2}.}
$$
The neglected factor $1+L_{12}/L_3$ gives a relative correction of order $\sqrt\beta$; imperfect locking of the inner pair supplies higher-order corrections in its weak coupling to the outer planet.

= Solution
{parent=vi}

Initially the two inner orbital normals coincide, so their combined <angular momentum> is $L_{12}=L_1+L_2$ along one direction. Use the outer orbital normal as the vertical axis. The inner pair's vector then has perpendicular component $L_{12}\sin\Delta I$ and parallel component $L_{12}\cos\Delta I$. Adding the outer <angular momentum> gives
$$
L_{\perp}=L_{12}\sin\Delta I,\qquad
L_{\parallel}=L_3+L_{12}\cos\Delta I.
$$
The <invariable plane> is perpendicular to this total vector. Thus the outer orbit's <orbital inclination> to that plane equals the angle between its own normal and the total normal. The <inclination to the invariable plane of two orbital planes> is
$$
\boxed{I_3=\arctan\left(\frac{\sin\Delta I}{A+\cos\Delta I}\right),\qquad A=\frac{L_3}{L_1+L_2}.}
$$
This uses the small, prograde inclinations for which the denominator is positive; in general use the corresponding two-argument arctangent to choose the quadrant. If $A\gg1$, $I_3\simeq\Delta I/(A+1)$ for small $\Delta I$: the distant planet carries most of the <angular momentum> and lies close to the <invariable plane>.
