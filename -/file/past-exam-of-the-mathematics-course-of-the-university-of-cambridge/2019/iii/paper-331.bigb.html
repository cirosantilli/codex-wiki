= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $V=U-c$ and take $k>0$ without loss of generality. The <Boussinesq approximation> replaces density by $\rho_0$ in inertia and pressure acceleration, while retaining the density perturbation in <buoyancy>. Linearizing about the <hydrostatic equilibrium> and substituting the <normal mode> gives
$$
ikV\widehat u+U'\widehat w=-ik\widehat p/\rho_0,\qquad
ikV\widehat w=-\widehat p'/\rho_0-g\widehat\rho/\rho_0,
$$
$$
ik\widehat u+\widehat w'=0,\qquad
ikV\widehat\rho+\rho'\widehat w=0.
$$
The last two equations give $\widehat u=i\widehat w'/k$ and $\widehat\rho=i\rho'\widehat w/(kV)$. The horizontal momentum equation gives
$$
\frac{\widehat p}{\rho_0}=\frac{V\widehat w'-U'\widehat w}{ik}.
$$
Substituting in the vertical momentum equation yields the <Taylor–Goldstein equation>:
$$
\boxed{\widehat w''-k^2\widehat w-\frac{U''}{U-c}\widehat w+\frac{N^2}{(U-c)^2}\widehat w=0,\qquad
N^2=-\frac g{\rho_0}\rho'.}
$$
The <buoyancy frequency> is real for <stable density stratification>. The impermeable walls impose $\widehat w(\pm L)=0$.

For a possibly unstable <normal mode>, $c_i\ne0$ ensures $V=U-c$ never vanishes, so choose one continuous branch of $V^a$. Substitute $\widehat w=V^aq$ into the <Taylor–Goldstein equation> and collect terms:
$$
q''+2a\frac{U'}Vq'+\left[-k^2+(a-1)\frac{U''}V+\frac{N^2+a(a-1)(U')^2}{V^2}\right]q=0.
$$
Multiplication by $V^{2a}$ puts its first two terms in <divergence form>:
$$
(V^{2a}q')'-k^2V^{2a}q+\left[(a-1)U''V^{2a-1}+\{N^2+a(a-1)(U')^2\}V^{2a-2}\right]q=0.
$$
Multiply by the <complex conjugate> $\overline q$ and apply <integration by parts>. Since $q(\pm L)=0$, the boundary term vanishes and the <power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity> is
$$
\boxed{\int_{-L}^LV^{2a}(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz=\int_{-L}^L\left[\{N^2+a(a-1)(U')^2\}V^{2a-2}+(a-1)U''V^{2a-1}\right]|q|^2dz.}
$$
The weight is $V^{2a}$, not $|V|^{2a}$: preserving its complex phase is essential for the subsequent stability proofs.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Choose $a=1/2$ in the <power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity> and let $Q=N^2-(U')^2/4$. The result is
$$
\int V(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz=\int\left(\frac QV-\frac{U''}2\right)|q|^2dz.
$$
Because $\operatorname{Im}V=-c_i$ and $\operatorname{Im}(1/V)=c_i/|V|^2$, its <imaginary part> gives
$$
c_i\int_{-L}^L\left[|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2+\frac{Q}{|U-c|^2}|q|^2\right]dz=0.
$$
If $4N^2>(U')^2$ everywhere, the integral is strictly positive for every nonzero <eigenfunction>, so a mode with $c_i\ne0$ is impossible. Thus the flow is neutrally stable to these inviscid <normal modes>. This is the <Miles–Howard theorem>, expressed as a lower bound $\mathrm{Ri}>1/4$ on the <gradient Richardson number>. It excludes exponential modal growth; it does not by itself exclude <transient growth>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Choose $a=1$. The <power-transformed Taylor–Goldstein energy identity> reduces to
$$
\int_{-L}^L(U-c)^2(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz=\int_{-L}^LN^2|q|^2dz.
$$
The right side is real. For an unstable mode, $c_i>0$, so taking the <imaginary part> gives
$$
\int_{-L}^L(U-c_r)(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz=0.
$$
Consequently the <phase velocity> is a weighted average:
$$
\boxed{c_r=\frac{\int_{-L}^LU(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz}{\int_{-L}^L(|q'|^2+k^2|q|^2)dz},\qquad U_{\min}<c_r<U_{\max}.}
$$
The weights are nonnegative and not identically zero. For a nonconstant shear profile, equality at an extremum would force $q$ to vanish on an interval where $U$ differs from that extremum; uniqueness for the regular <ordinary differential equation> would then give the zero mode. This is the <phase-speed bound for unstable stratified shear modes>.

= Solution

Let $\widehat\eta$ be the displacement amplitude of an interface. Its <kinematic boundary condition> is $ik(U-c)\widehat\eta=\widehat w$, on either side, so the first of the <jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow> is
$$
\boxed{\left[\frac{\widehat w}{U-c}\right]_-^+=0.}
$$
For zero <surface tension>, pressure is continuous at the displaced interface. Since the background <hydrostatic pressure> has derivative $-g\rho$, linearization gives $[\widehat p-g\rho\widehat\eta]_-^+=0$. Using $\widehat p=\rho_0[(U-c)\widehat w'-U'\widehat w]/(ik)$ and the kinematic condition gives
$$
\boxed{\left[(U-c)\widehat w'-U'\widehat w-\frac{g\rho}{\rho_0}\frac{\widehat w}{U-c}\right]_-^+=0.}
$$
This derivation also works when $U$ jumps: the displacement is shared, while the vertical velocity need not be. It avoids ambiguous products of <distributions> at a discontinuous velocity profile.

= Solution

Write $V_0=\Delta U/2$, $z_\pm=\pm h/2$, $B=g\Delta\rho/(2\rho_0)$ and $d=B/(2k)$. The velocity is linear through all three layers, so $U(z_\pm)=\pm V_0$ and $U'$ has no jump. Each interface has density drop $\Delta\rho/2$. The <jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow> become
$$
[\widehat w]=0,\qquad [\widehat w']=-\frac{B}{(U(z_\pm)-c)^2}\widehat w(z_\pm).
$$
Between interfaces, the <Taylor–Goldstein equation> is $\widehat w''-k^2\widehat w=0$. A decaying solution can therefore be written
$$
\widehat w(z)=b_+e^{-k|z-z_+|}+b_-e^{-k|z-z_-|}.
$$
Its derivative jumps are $-2kb_\pm$, while its values at the interfaces are $b_\pm+e^{-kh}b_\mp$. Hence the coefficients satisfy
$$
\begin{pmatrix}(V_0-c)^2-d&-de^{-kh}\\-de^{-kh}&(-V_0-c)^2-d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_+\\b_-\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
The determinant must vanish. With $\widetilde c=c/V_0$, $\alpha=kh/2$ and $J=g\Delta\rho h/(\rho_0\Delta U^2)$, one has $d/V_0^2=J/(2\alpha)$. Expanding gives the <dispersion relation for two density interfaces in uniform shear>:
$$
\boxed{\widetilde c^4-\left(2+\frac J\alpha\right)\widetilde c^2+\frac{(2\alpha-J)^2-J^2e^{-4\alpha}}{4\alpha^2}=0.}
$$

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $y=\widetilde c^2$ and $s=J/(2\alpha)>0$. The <dispersion relation for two density interfaces in uniform shear> is
$$
y^2-2(1+s)y+(1-s)^2-s^2e^{-4\alpha}=0.
$$
Its <discriminant> is $16s+4s^2e^{-4\alpha}>0$, so both roots $y_\pm$ are real. Their sum is positive; therefore one is negative precisely when their product is negative. That condition is
$$
(1-s)^2<s^2e^{-4\alpha},\qquad |1-s|<se^{-2\alpha}.
$$
Solving for $J$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{2\alpha}{1+e^{-2\alpha}}<J<\frac{2\alpha}{1-e^{-2\alpha}}.}
$$
In this interval $y_-<0$, so $\widetilde c=i\sqrt{-y_-}$ is the growing <normal mode>. At either endpoint the corresponding root is zero and the mode is neutral.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For large $\alpha$, the coupling $e^{-kh}=e^{-2\alpha}$ is exponentially weak. If each interface were isolated, its <interfacial gravity waves> would have dimensional <phase velocities> $U(z_\pm)\pm\sqrt d$, or
$$
\widetilde c=1\pm\sqrt{\frac J{2\alpha}},\qquad
\widetilde c=-1\pm\sqrt{\frac J{2\alpha}}.
$$
The upper-interface wave travelling against its positive local current has speed $1-\sqrt{J/(2\alpha)}$. The lower-interface wave travelling against its negative current has speed $-1+\sqrt{J/(2\alpha)}$. Their laboratory speeds match at zero when $J=2\alpha$. Weak interaction then produces <counterpropagating wave instability> rather than two independent travelling waves.

\Image[paper-331-interface-resonance.png]
{title=Instability band and resonance of the two interface waves}
{description=The shaded band lies between the exact neutral curves. The second panel fixes $\alpha=1$ and compares the isolated counterpropagating <phase velocities> with the imaginary <phase velocity> of the coupled unstable mode.}

The band has the large-wavenumber form $J=2\alpha\pm2\alpha e^{-2\alpha}+O(\alpha e^{-4\alpha})$. Exactly at resonance, $\operatorname{Im}\widetilde c\sim e^{-2\alpha}/2$, so
$$
\boxed{k c_i\sim\frac{k\Delta U}{4}e^{-kh}.}
$$
Thus the instability window and <growth rate> both become exponentially small as the interfaces become weakly coupled.

For distinct <eigenvalues>, the formula holds for $n=0,1$. If the lower-left entry of $L^n$ is $b_n=(\lambda_1^n-\lambda_2^n)/(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)$, multiplication by $L$ gives $b_{n+1}=\lambda_1b_n+\lambda_2^n=(\lambda_1^{n+1}-\lambda_2^{n+1})/(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)$. This proves by <mathematical induction> that
$$
L^n=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1^n&0\\(\lambda_1^n-\lambda_2^n)/(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)&\lambda_2^n\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Summing the absolutely convergent <matrix exponential> series gives
$$
\boxed{A(t)=e^{Lt}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda_1t}&0\\\dfrac{e^{\lambda_1t}-e^{\lambda_2t}}{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}&e^{\lambda_2t}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For the repeated <eigenvalue> $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda$, the continuous limit is $A(t)=e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\t&1\end{pmatrix}$. This is also the exponential of a two-dimensional <Jordan block>.

Termwise <differentiation> of the <matrix exponential> gives $A'=LA$ and $A(0)=I$. Thus $x(t)=A(t)x(0)$ satisfies $x'=Lx$ and the prescribed <initial condition>. Uniqueness for a <linear differential equation> makes it the solution.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For real $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$, compute
$$
LL^T-L^TL=\begin{pmatrix}-1&\lambda_1-\lambda_2\\\lambda_1-\lambda_2&1\end{pmatrix}\ne0.
$$
Thus $L$ is a <non-normal matrix>. For distinct <eigenvalues>, take
$$
v_1=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1-\lambda_2\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These satisfy $Lv_j=\lambda_jv_j$ but have <inner product> $v_1^Tv_2=1$. The <eigenvectors> are therefore <nonorthogonal>. When the eigenvalues coincide, only the direction $v_2$ remains as an <eigenvector>; $L$ is a defective <Jordan block>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Differentiate the quadratic <energy>:
$$
\dot E=2\lambda_1x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2\lambda_2x_2^2=2x^THx,\qquad H=\frac{L+L^T}{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&1/2\\1/2&\lambda_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For $x_1\ne0$, put $r=x_2/x_1$. Immediate energy growth occurs precisely when $\lambda_2r^2+r+\lambda_1>0$. With both <eigenvalues> negative, such directions exist iff $1-4\lambda_1\lambda_2>0$. Writing $\delta=\sqrt{1-4\lambda_1\lambda_2}$, the growing initial conditions are the two opposite open wedges
$$
\boxed{x_1\ne0,\qquad \frac{-1+\delta}{2\lambda_2}<\frac{x_2}{x_1}<\frac{-1-\delta}{2\lambda_2}.}
$$
If $x_1=0$, energy decreases. No energy growth at any time, for any initial condition, is possible exactly when
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1\lambda_2\geq\frac14\quad(\lambda_1,\lambda_2<0).}
$$
Indeed, this makes the <symmetric part of a matrix> $H$ a <negative semidefinite matrix>, so $\dot E\leq0$ along every trajectory. If the product is smaller, the wedges already supply counterexamples at $t=0$. This is the <instantaneous energy-growth criterion for a linear system>.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=e}

At each fixed time, the maximum over nonzero initial conditions is the <optimal energy amplification of a linear system>:
$$
G(t)=\max_{x_0\ne0}\frac{\|A(t)x_0\|_2^2}{\|x_0\|_2^2}=\sigma_{\max}(A(t))^2.
$$
Write $a=e^{\lambda_1t}$, $d=e^{\lambda_2t}$ and $b=(a-d)/(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)$. The <symmetric matrix> $A^TA$ has <trace> $\tau=a^2+b^2+d^2$ and <determinant> $a^2d^2$. Its largest <eigenvalue> is
$$
\boxed{G(t)=\frac{\tau+\sqrt{\tau^2-4a^2d^2}}2.}
$$
An optimal initial condition is a <right singular vector> of $A(t)$ associated with its largest <singular value>.

\Image[paper-331-transient-growth.png]
{title=Stable eigenmodes can combine to produce transient energy growth}
{description=For $\lambda_1=-1$ and $\lambda_2=-0.05$, the red directions on the unit circle have positive instantaneous energy derivative. The <eigenvectors> are <nonorthogonal>. The right panel compares the <optimal energy amplification of a linear system> with the monotonically decaying <energy> of each eigenmode.}

If $\lambda_2>\lambda_1$, let $\alpha=1/(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)$. Then $A(t)/e^{\lambda_2t}\to\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}$, so the precise long-time asymptotic statement is
$$
\boxed{G(t)\sim(1+\alpha^2)e^{2\lambda_2t},\qquad \alpha^2=\frac1{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)^2}.}
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> shows that the unit initial conditions achieving this leading factor are
$$
\boxed{x(0)=\pm\frac1{\sqrt{1+\alpha^2}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is an <adjoint eigenvector>, since $L^T(\alpha,1)^T=\lambda_2(\alpha,1)^T$. It differs from the right <eigenvector> $v_2=(0,1)^T$: the initial condition maximizes the projection onto the slow mode, whereas the eventual state aligns with that right eigenvector. For $\lambda_2<0$, even the optimal <energy> ultimately decays to zero; the prefactor describes enhanced excitation of the slow mode.

The zero <streamfunction> gives zero <velocity>. The temperature $\theta_0=-z$ obeys both prescribed temperature values, has no horizontal <gradient>, and has zero <Laplacian>. Thus it satisfies both equations with $\psi_0=0$:
$$
\boxed{\psi_0=0,\qquad\theta_0=-z.}
$$
This is the conductive state of <Darcy-Bénard convection>.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Linearizing about the conductive state gives
$$
\nabla^2\psi'=-R\theta'_x,\qquad \theta'_t+\psi'_x=\nabla^2\theta'.
$$
The perturbations vanish at $z=0,1$. For a <normal mode> with vertical dependence $\sin(n\pi z)$, put $q=k^2+n^2\pi^2$. The two modal equations are $-q\widehat\psi=-ikR\widehat\theta$ and $s\widehat\theta+ik\widehat\psi=-q\widehat\theta$. Eliminating $\widehat\psi$ gives
$$
\boxed{s=\frac{k^2R}{k^2+n^2\pi^2}-(k^2+n^2\pi^2).}
$$
Neutrality requires $R=(k^2+n^2\pi^2)^2/k^2$. The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> bounds this below by $4n^2\pi^2$, with equality at $k=n\pi$. The first unstable vertical mode is therefore $n=1$, giving
$$
\boxed{R_c=4\pi^2,\qquad k_c=\pi,\qquad \text{horizontal wavelength}=2.}
$$
The wavelength statement applies at the first onset. At a general larger $R$, maximizing the <growth rate> within a fixed vertical mode gives $k^2=n\pi\sqrt R-n^2\pi^2$, so its preferred wavelength need not remain exactly two. This is the <onset of convection in a horizontal Darcy layer>.

= Solution

The full perturbation equations are
$$
\nabla^2\psi'+R\theta'_x=0,\qquad
\nabla^2\theta'-\psi'_x=\theta'_t+J(\psi',\theta'),
$$
where the <streamfunction advection bracket> is $J(f,g)=f_zg_x-f_xg_z$. Put $R=R_c+\epsilon^2$ and introduce the <slow time> $T=\epsilon^2t$, so $\partial_t=\epsilon^2\partial_T$ on the amplitude-dependent fields. Then
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\psi'+R_c\theta'_x=-\epsilon^2\theta'_x,\qquad
\nabla^2\theta'-\psi'_x=\epsilon^2\theta'_T+J(\psi',\theta').}
$$
The <weakly nonlinear expansion> balances the small linear growth against the cubic saturation on this slow time scale.

= Solution

At first order, $\nabla^2\psi_1+R_c\theta_{1x}=0$ and $\nabla^2\theta_1-\psi_{1x}=0$. Since $\nabla^2\psi_1=-2\pi^2\psi_1$ and $R_c=4\pi^2$, the first equation fixes $\theta_{1x}=\psi_1/2$. The homogeneous temperature boundary conditions and the second equation remove any horizontally uniform term, giving
$$
\boxed{\theta_1=\frac1{2\pi}\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi z).}
$$
At second order,
$$
\nabla^2\psi_2+R_c\theta_{2x}=0,\qquad
\nabla^2\theta_2-\psi_{2x}=J(\psi_1,\theta_1)=\frac\pi4\sin(2\pi z).
$$
The forcing is horizontally uniform. Taking no additional critical-mode component, a solution satisfying the homogeneous boundaries is
$$
\boxed{\psi_2=0,\qquad\theta_2=-\frac1{16\pi}\sin(2\pi z).}
$$
This <mean-temperature correction in weakly nonlinear Darcy convection> modifies the vertical temperature gradient and provides the feedback that saturates the convection.

= Solution

Let $\Psi_3=A^3\psi_3$ and $\Theta_3=A^3\theta_3$ denote the full third-order coefficients. This notation keeps the detuning term, proportional to $A$, and the slow-time term, proportional to $A_T$, visible. Define
$$
B=J(\psi_1,\theta_2)+J(\psi_2,\theta_1).
$$
The equations at order $\epsilon^3$ are
$$
\nabla^2\Psi_3+R_c\Theta_{3x}=-A\theta_{1x},\qquad
\nabla^2\Theta_3-\Psi_{3x}=A_T\theta_1+A^3B.
$$
Equivalently, where $A\ne0$, the question's coefficients satisfy $\nabla^2\psi_3+R_c\theta_{3x}=-A^{-2}\theta_{1x}$ and $\nabla^2\theta_3-\psi_{3x}=(A_T/A^3)\theta_1+B$. Eliminate $\Theta_3$ from the full coefficients:
$$
(\nabla^4+R_c\partial_x^2)\Psi_3=-A\partial_x\nabla^2\theta_1-R_cA_T\theta_{1x}-R_cA^3\partial_xB.
$$
Use the <Fredholm solvability condition for a self-adjoint operator>. With $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1\int_0^2fg\,dx\,dz$, projection onto the critical <eigenfunction> $\psi_1$ makes the left side vanish. Therefore the <Landau amplitude equation> is
$$
\boxed{A_T=\alpha A-\beta A^3,\qquad
\alpha=-\frac{\langle\psi_1,\partial_x\nabla^2\theta_1\rangle}{R_c\langle\psi_1,\theta_{1x}\rangle},\qquad
\beta=\frac{\langle\psi_1,\partial_x\{J(\psi_1,\theta_2)+J(\psi_2,\theta_1)\}\rangle}{\langle\psi_1,\theta_{1x}\rangle}.}
$$
These integrals already answer the requested coefficient calculation. Evaluating them gives $\langle\psi_1,\theta_{1x}\rangle=1/4$, $\langle\psi_1,\partial_x\nabla^2\theta_1\rangle=-\pi^2/2$, and $\langle\psi_1,\partial_xB\rangle=\pi^2/32$. Hence, for the prescribed normalization,
$$
\boxed{A_T=\frac12A-\frac{\pi^2}{8}A^3.}
$$
The cubic term opposes growth, so the nonzero steady amplitudes $A=\pm2/\pi$ are <stable equilibria> within the chosen roll phase. The conductive state $A=0$ is unstable for this positive detuning. This is <supercritical saturation of Darcy convection rolls>.
