= Solution
{parent=a}

Take $\gamma>0$ so that the short-wavelength quadratic energy is stable. For $B=0$, a <Fourier mode> with wavevector magnitude $q$ has inverse <Gaussian field theory> covariance
$$
G(q)=a+\kappa q^2+\gamma q^4
=a-\frac{\kappa^2}{4\gamma}+\gamma\left(q^2+\frac\kappa{2\gamma}\right)^2.
$$
For $\kappa<0$, its minimum is at
$$
\boxed{q_0=\sqrt{-\frac\kappa{2\gamma}},\qquad a_c=\frac{\kappa^2}{4\gamma}.}
$$
The zero-average constraint excludes the uniform mode but allows $\phi=A\cos(q_0z)$. At $a=a_c$ the modes on the <nonzero-wavevector soft-mode sphere> become soft; below it they have negative quadratic energy, so the homogeneous state loses stability. A modulation along any chosen direction is the simplest <smectic phase> pattern. The quadratic instability selects its wavelength; with $B=0$, a nonlinear stabilizing interaction would still be needed to determine its amplitude.

Average the trial field over an integer number of periods. The squared-field, squared-gradient and squared-Laplacian averages are $A^2/2$, $q_0^2A^2/2$ and $q_0^4A^2/2$, while $\langle\phi^2|\nabla\phi|^2\rangle=A^4q_0^2/8$. Hence the <single-mode smectic mean-field free energy> is
$$
\boxed{\frac HV=\frac{a-a_c}{4}A^2+\frac{Bq_0^2}{8}A^4.}
$$
For the physically stabilizing case $B>0$, minimization gives
$$
\boxed{A^2=\begin{cases}0,&a\geq a_c,\\(a_c-a)/(Bq_0^2),&a<a_c,\end{cases}\qquad a_c=\frac{\kappa^2}{4\gamma}.}
$$
The amplitude tends continuously to zero at $a_c$, so this <mean-field approximation> predicts a <continuous phase transition>. The ordered minimum has <free-energy density> $-(a_c-a)^2/(8Bq_0^2)$.

A nonzero $B$ alone is not sufficient for this conclusion: $B<0$ makes the displayed quartic amplitude energy unbounded below and requires higher-order stabilization. The continuous-transition statement uses $B>0$ and the stipulated fixed wavevector $q_0$.

Apply the <Jensen inequality> to the convex <exponential function> under the trial equilibrium measure:
$$
\left\langle e^{-(H-H_0)}\right\rangle_0\geq e^{-\langle H-H_0\rangle_0}.
$$
The given identity therefore implies $e^{-F}\geq e^{-F_0-\langle H-H_0\rangle_0}$. Taking minus the <logarithm> reverses the inequality and proves the <Feynman-Bogoliubov inequality>
$$
\boxed{F\leq F_0+\langle H-H_0\rangle_0=F_0-\langle H_0\rangle_0+\langle H\rangle_0.}
$$
The units $\beta=1$ make $H$ and $F$ dimensionless here. Equality holds when $H-H_0$ is constant on the support of the trial measure. Optimizing the trial parameters tightens an upper bound, which is the basis of the <Gaussian variational approximation>.

Choose an even positive $J(q)$. Each independent complex <Fourier mode> of the real field has density $(J(q)/\pi)e^{-J(q)|\phi_q|^2}$, so
$$
\langle\phi_q\phi_p\rangle_0=\frac{\delta_{p,-q}}{J(q)}.
$$
The quadratic part therefore has expectation $\sum_q^+G(q)/J(q)$. For the quartic part, apply <Isserlis theorem> to its four jointly centered <Gaussian random variables>, or to their real and imaginary components. Pairing the two differentiated fields together imposes $q_2=-q_1$, and its contribution is
$$
\frac BV\left(\sum_q\frac{q^2}{J(q)}\right)\left(\sum_p\frac1{J(p)}\right).
$$
Each of the two other pairings instead gives $-(B/V)|\sum_q q/J(q)|^2=0$, because the summand is odd under $q\mapsto-q$. Thus only the displayed contribution remains. Each full sum is twice its <positive-wavevector sum for a real field>; putting $S_0=\sum_q^+1/J(q)$ and $S_2=\sum_q^+q^2/J(q)$ gives $\langle H_4\rangle_0=4BS_2S_0/V$.

Substitute these expectations, $F_0$ and $\langle H_0\rangle_0$ into the <Feynman-Bogoliubov inequality>:
$$
\boxed{F\leq\mathcal F[J]=\sum_q^+\left[\log\frac{J(q)}\pi-1+\frac{G(q)}{J(q)}\right]+\frac{4B}{V}S_2S_0.}
$$
The zero mode is absent by the composition constraint. The counting uses one representative of every nonzero $\{q,-q\}$ pair, so no extra factor of two is assigned to an independent complex amplitude.

Differentiate the bound with respect to one $J(q)$, using $\partial S_0/\partial J(q)=-J(q)^{-2}$ and $\partial S_2/\partial J(q)=-q^2J(q)^{-2}$:
$$
0=\frac1{J(q)}-\frac{G(q)}{J(q)^2}-\frac{4B}{VJ(q)^2}(S_2+q^2S_0).
$$
Thus an interior minimizing <Gaussian variational kernel for a gradient quartic interaction> has
$$
\boxed{J(q)=\bar a+\bar\kappa q^2+\gamma q^4,\qquad
\bar a=a+\frac{4B}{V}S_2,\quad\bar\kappa=\kappa+\frac{4B}{V}S_0.}
$$
Use the half-space mode density given in the question. In the <thermodynamic limit>, the coupled <self-consistency equations> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\bar a&=a+2B\int_{|k|<\Lambda}\frac{k^2}{\bar a+\bar\kappa k^2+\gamma k^4}\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d},\\
\bar\kappa&=\kappa+2B\int_{|k|<\Lambda}\frac1{\bar a+\bar\kappa k^2+\gamma k^4}\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The admissible solution must have $J(q)>0$. The coefficient $\gamma$ is unchanged because the interaction's derivative contributes only a constant and a $q^2$ term.

The formal continuum integrals require the coarse-graining <ultraviolet cutoff> $\Lambda$ in three dimensions: the first radial integrand tends to a constant at large $k$, so taking $\Lambda=\infty$ would leave $\bar a$ divergent. The low-wavevector fluctuation argument is independent of this short-distance regularization.

= Solution

In the usual three-dimensional isotropic setting, the transition is expected to be **discontinuous**, by the <Brazovskii fluctuation-induced first-order transition> mechanism.

If $\bar\kappa<0$, write $q_*^2=-\bar\kappa/(2\gamma)$ and $r=\bar a-\bar\kappa^2/(4\gamma)$. Then $J(q)=r+\gamma(q^2-q_*^2)^2$. A continuous finite-wavevector transition would require $r\to0$ with $q_*>0$. But the <soft-mode shell integral> behaves as
$$
\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3J(q)}\sim\frac{q_*}{4\pi\sqrt{\gamma r}},
\qquad
\int\frac{q^2\,d^3q}{(2\pi)^3J(q)}\sim\frac{q_*^3}{4\pi\sqrt{\gamma r}}.
$$
The positive fluctuation corrections in the <self-consistency equations> diverge in that limit. In particular $\bar\kappa=\kappa+2BI_0$ cannot stay at a finite negative value while $I_0$ diverges. Thus the isotropic disordered state cannot lose stability through a zero-mass mode on a fixed nonzero shell in this approximation.

Ordering is instead expected through a free-energy crossing with a finite-amplitude <smectic phase>, giving a <first-order phase transition>. This is a reasoned fluctuation prediction for the finite-wavevector transition; the disordered <Gaussian variational approximation> alone does not locate the coexistence point or calculate the amplitude jump. A zero-wavevector instability or a lower-dimensional setting would require a separate analysis.

Write $\mu_j=\delta F/\delta p_j$ and $\int=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\int d\mathbf r$. Eliminating the noise gives $f=\dot p+\Gamma\mu$. The <Onsager--Machlup path probability> is therefore
$$
\boxed{P_F[p]=\mathcal K[p]\exp\left[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\int|\dot p+\Gamma\mu|^2\right],\qquad
P_B[p]=\mathcal K[p]\exp\left[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\int|-\dot p+\Gamma\mu|^2\right].}
$$
These are conditional path densities given the corresponding starting configuration. For a time-even <vector order parameter>, reversal reads the configurations backward, changes $\dot p$ to $-\dot p$, and keeps $\mu$ unchanged on each corresponding configuration.

The two <Gaussian white noise> measures have the same normalization. With a common midpoint discretization, the noise-to-path Jacobian also agrees under reversal by <time-reversal invariance of a path Jacobian>. The common factor $\mathcal K[p]$ may include that path-dependent Jacobian; it need not be a universal constant. This makes the shared-factor assertion precise while leaving the requested ratio unaffected.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The difference between the two squared actions is $|\dot p+\Gamma\mu|^2-|-\dot p+\Gamma\mu|^2=4\Gamma\dot p\cdot\mu$. The common factor cancels, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{P_F}{P_B}=\exp\left[-\frac{2\Gamma}{\sigma^2}\int\dot p\cdot\frac{\delta F}{\delta p}\,d\mathbf r\,dt\right]
=\exp\left[-\frac{2\Gamma}{\sigma^2}(F_2-F_1)\right].}
$$
The last equality uses the <functional chain rule> with the same midpoint convention as the <Onsager–Machlup functional>.

At equilibrium, <microscopic reversibility> requires $e^{-F_1/(k_BT)}P_F=e^{-F_2/(k_BT)}P_B$, equivalently $P_F/P_B=e^{-(F_2-F_1)/(k_BT)}$. Comparing the coefficients of an arbitrary free-energy change gives the <model A fluctuation-dissipation relation>
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2=2\Gamma k_BT.}
$$

= Solution

The <Fourier transform> turns $\nabla^2$ into multiplication by $-q^2$. Each Cartesian component is an <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process>:
$$
\dot p_{q,i}=-r(q)p_{q,i}+f_{q,i},\qquad\boxed{r(q)=\Gamma(a+\kappa q^2).}
$$
Since $r(q)>0$, the transient from the remote past vanishes, and the integrating-factor solution is
$$
\boxed{p_q(t)=\int_{-\infty}^t f_q(s)e^{-r(q)(t-s)}\,ds.}
$$
Using the stated <Gaussian white noise> covariance and assuming $t_1\geq t_2$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle p_{q,i}(t_1)p_{-q,j}(t_2)\rangle
&=2\Gamma k_BT\,\delta_{ij}\int_{-\infty}^{t_2}e^{-r(q)(t_1+t_2-2s)}\,ds\\
&=\delta_{ij}\frac{\Gamma k_BT}{r(q)}e^{-r(q)(t_1-t_2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Interchanging the times gives the stationary two-time <correlation function>
$$
\boxed{\langle p_{q,i}(t_1)p_{-q,j}(t_2)\rangle=\delta_{ij}\frac{k_BT}{a+\kappa q^2}e^{-\Gamma(a+\kappa q^2)|t_1-t_2|}.}
$$
At equal times this is the equilibrium <Gaussian field theory> covariance, and the decay time is $r(q)^{-1}$.

= Solution

Put $u_j=\dot p_j+\partial_iW_{ij}$. The noises required by a joint trajectory are $f_j=u_j+\Gamma\mu_j$ and $N_{ij}=W_{ij}+M\partial_i\mu_j$. Their independent <Gaussian white noise> weights give the <joint path probability of an order parameter and its flux>:
$$
\boxed{P_F[p,W]=\mathcal K[p,W]\exp\left[-\int\left\{\frac{|u+\Gamma\mu|^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{|W+M\nabla\mu|^2}{2\sigma_N^2}\right\}\right].}
$$
For the physically reversed path, $p$ is time-even and the transport flux $W$ is time-odd. Therefore $u\mapsto-u$, $W\mapsto-W$, and
$$
\boxed{P_B[p,W]=\mathcal K[p,W]\exp\left[-\int\left\{\frac{|-u+\Gamma\mu|^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{|-W+M\nabla\mu|^2}{2\sigma_N^2}\right\}\right].}
$$
The common factor includes the two noise normalizations and the midpoint <Jacobian determinant>. Its calculation must include both relaxation channels. On a finite spatial grid, let $\mathsf D$ and $\mathsf G$ represent the <divergence> and <gradient>, with $\Delta=\mathsf D\mathsf G$ the discrete <Laplacian>, and let $\mathcal H_n=\partial\mu/\partial p$ be the <Hessian matrix> of $F$ at the midpoint of time step $n$. Differentiating the two required noises with respect to the next configuration and the interval flux gives the <Jacobian matrix>
$$
\frac{\partial(f_n,N_n)}{\partial(p_{n+1},W_n)}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{I}{\Delta t}+\Gamma\mathcal H_n/2&\mathsf D\\
M\mathsf G\mathcal H_n/2&I
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Using the <Schur complement>, its <Jacobian determinant>, apart from the path-independent power of $\Delta t$, is
$$
\det\left[I+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\Gamma I-M\Delta)\mathcal H_n\right].
$$
Reversal visits the same midpoint configurations in reverse order, so the product of these factors is unchanged. The joint <time-reversal invariance of a path Jacobian> therefore involves the complete relaxation operator $\Gamma I-M\Delta$.

Taking the action difference gives
$$
\boxed{\log\frac{P_F}{P_B}=-\frac{2\Gamma}{\sigma^2}\int\mu_j(\dot p_j+\partial_iW_{ij})-\frac{2M}{\sigma_N^2}\int W_{ij}\partial_i\mu_j.}
$$
Repeated component indices are summed. The second squared norm sums over both flux indices. This is <mixed conserved and nonconserved order-parameter dynamics>; reversing the order parameter history while leaving the flux unreversed would give the wrong probability ratio.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Set the noise strengths to the <model A fluctuation-dissipation relation> and the <fluctuation-dissipation relation for a conserved flux>:
$$
\sigma^2=2\Gamma k_BT,\qquad\sigma_N^2=2Mk_BT.
$$
The preceding logarithmic ratio becomes
$$
\log\frac{P_F}{P_B}=-\frac1{k_BT}\int[\mu_j\dot p_j+\mu_j\partial_iW_{ij}+W_{ij}\partial_i\mu_j].
$$
The last two terms combine as $\partial_i(\mu_jW_{ij})$. They integrate to zero under the periodic <boundary conditions>, by <integration by parts>. The remaining term is $F_2-F_1$ by the <functional chain rule>, hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{P_F}{P_B}=e^{-(F_2-F_1)/(k_BT)}.}
$$
After including the equilibrium initial weights, the forward and reversed histories have equal probability. Thus <microscopic reversibility> is satisfied by the two channels together, with each channel's own thermal noise strength.

Use the stable parameters $b,\kappa>0$. The bulk stationary points satisfy $f'(\phi)=a\phi+b\phi^3=0$. For $a<0$, $\phi=0$ has negative curvature, while the two minima are
$$
\boxed{\phi=\pm\phi_B,\qquad\phi_B=\sqrt{-a/b}.}
$$
Both have $f(\pm\phi_B)=-a^2/(4b)$ and $f''(\pm\phi_B)=-2a>0$. To establish minimization at fixed total composition, write
$$
f(\phi)=-\frac{a^2}{4b}+\frac b4(\phi^2-\phi_B^2)^2,
\qquad F\geq-\frac{Va^2}{4b}.
$$
For mean composition $\bar\phi$ with $|\bar\phi|<\phi_B$, a mixture of the two minima with volume fraction $\lambda=(\bar\phi+\phi_B)/(2\phi_B)$ in the positive phase attains this lower bound in the bulk. Macroscopic slabs have only a subextensive interfacial excess <free energy>, so the bound is attained per unit volume in the <thermodynamic limit>. Thus constrained minimization gives <phase coexistence> in the <mean-field approximation>; at the endpoints only one phase is needed.

The two equilibrium conditions also follow directly by <constrained optimization>. Introduce a <Lagrange multiplier> $\mu$ for $\lambda\phi_++(1-\lambda)\phi_-=\bar\phi$ in the mixture's bulk <free-energy density>. Varying $\phi_+$ and $\phi_-$ gives $f'(\phi_+)=f'(\phi_-)=\mu$; varying $\lambda$ gives $f(\phi_+)-\mu\phi_+=f(\phi_-)-\mu\phi_-$. These equate the <chemical potentials> and <pressures>, respectively.

Equivalently, their bulk <chemical potentials> are both zero and their <pressures> are equal:
$$
\boxed{\mu=f'(\pm\phi_B)=0,\qquad\Pi=\mu\phi-f(\phi)=\frac{a^2}{4b}.}
$$
These are precisely the conditions of the <common-tangent construction for phase coexistence>: the horizontal supporting tangent touches both minima. Equal <chemical potentials> prevent net composition exchange, and equal <pressure> gives mechanical equilibrium at a flat interface.

Apply force balance to one hemisphere. The pressure force is $\pi R^2(\Pi_{\rm in}-\Pi_{\rm out})$, while the <interfacial tension> around its equator gives $2\pi R\gamma$. The <Young–Laplace equation> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\Pi_{\rm in}-\Pi_{\rm out}=\frac{2\gamma}{R}.}
$$
To leading order in curvature, let the two bulk compositions be $\phi_+=\phi_B+\delta_+$ and $\phi_-=-\phi_B+\delta_-$. Their curvatures agree, $\alpha=f''(\pm\phi_B)=-2a$, so equal <chemical potentials> require $\alpha\delta_+=\alpha\delta_-$ and hence a common shift $\delta$.

Since $d\Pi/d\phi=\phi f''(\phi)$, expansion about the flat coexistence compositions gives $\Pi_+-\Pi_-=2\phi_B\alpha\delta+O(\delta^2)$. Matching the capillary pressure proves the <Gibbs--Thomson relation> in these variables:
$$
\boxed{\delta=\frac{\gamma}{\alpha\phi_BR},\qquad\mu_s=\alpha\delta=\frac{\gamma}{\phi_BR}.}
$$
The pressure jump balances interfacial forces; it does not permit unequal <chemical potentials>. A common shift in both compositions supplies a common interfacial <chemical potential> while their pressures differ. The expansion assumes $R$ large compared with the <phi-four diffuse interface> width.

= Solution

The far-field composition $-\phi_B$ has <chemical potential> zero. Local curved-interface coexistence imposes $\mu(R)=\mu_s=\gamma/(\phi_BR)$. The decaying spherically symmetric solution of the exterior <Laplace equation> is
$$
\boxed{\mu(r)=\mu_s\frac Rr=\frac\gamma{\phi_Br},\qquad r\geq R.}
$$
With constant <mobility>, its outward current is
$$
\boxed{J_r(r)=-M\frac{d\mu}{dr}=\frac{M\gamma}{\phi_Br^2},\qquad J_r(R)=\frac{M\gamma}{\phi_BR^2}.}
$$
The linearized exterior composition is $\phi(r)=-\phi_B+\mu(r)/\alpha$, consistent with the surface shift in the previous part.

For <diffusion-controlled droplet growth>, the interior is uniform to leading order and has no diffusive current. Replacing exterior material by interior material requires the composition jump $2\phi_B$, so conservation gives $2\phi_B\dot R=-J_r(R)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dot R=-\frac{M\gamma}{2\phi_B^2R^2}.}
$$
The negative sign represents evaporation. The total outward current is $4\pi R^2J_r(R)=4\pi M\gamma/\phi_B$, independent of $R$ in this leading quasi-static capillary approximation.

Integrate the <diffusion-controlled droplet growth> equation: $d(R^3)/dt=-3M\gamma/(2\phi_B^2)$. Thus
$$
R(t)^3=R_0^3-\frac{3M\gamma}{2\phi_B^2}t
=R_0^3\left(1-\frac t{\tau_\infty}\right).
$$
Setting the radius to zero gives
$$
\boxed{\tau_\infty(R_0)=\frac{2\phi_B^2R_0^3}{3M\gamma}.}
$$
This is the lifetime predicted by the sharp-interface quasi-static model. The final stage at a radius comparable to the interface width falls outside that approximation, while the leading lifetime for a large initial droplet is dominated by the larger radii.

= Solution

The flat interface is an equilibrium reservoir with $\mu=0$, while the droplet has the constant surface value $\mu_s=\gamma/(\phi_BR)$. The exterior <chemical potential> satisfies the <Laplace equation>, tends to zero far away, and obeys these two surface values. These are exactly the <electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor> for a sphere held at potential $\mu_s$ above a grounded infinite plane.

Introduce an arbitrary <permittivity> $\epsilon$. In the electrostatic problem, $Q=-\epsilon\int_{S_R}\partial_n\mu\,dS=C(R,h)\mu_s$. Therefore the <diffusion-capacitance analogy> gives the outward current
$$
I(R,h)=-M\int_{S_R}\partial_n\mu\,dS=\frac M\epsilon C(R,h)\mu_s.
$$
For an isolated sphere, its <capacitance> is $C(R,\infty)=4\pi\epsilon R$, so $I(R,\infty)=4\pi MR\mu_s=4\pi M\gamma/\phi_B$. Dividing gives
$$
\boxed{I(R,h)=I(R,\infty)c(R/h),\qquad c(R/h)=\frac{C(R,h)}{C(R,\infty)}.}
$$
Thus <sphere-plane capacitance> determines the evaporation enhancement. The assumed spherical shape gives the corresponding law $\dot R=-M\gamma c(R/h)/(2\phi_B^2R^2)$ for <droplet evaporation near a planar reservoir>. The local surface current is generally nonuniform, even though its total is described by a single capacitance.

= Solution

For a fixed center height $h$, the radius decreases according to the preceding current enhancement. Thus
$$
\tau_h(R_0)=\frac{2\phi_B^2}{M\gamma}\int_0^{R_0}\frac{R^2}{c(R/h)}\,dR.
$$
Within the stipulated logarithmic approximation, $c(y)=1+y/2+O(y^2)$, so $1/c(y)=1-y/2+O(y^2)$. Expanding uniformly over $0\leq R\leq R_0\ll h$ and integrating gives
$$
\tau_h=\frac{2\phi_B^2}{M\gamma}\left[\frac{R_0^3}{3}-\frac{R_0^4}{8h}+O\left(\frac{R_0^5}{h^2}\right)\right].
$$
Therefore the leading correction to the isolated-droplet lifetime is
$$
\boxed{\tau_h(R_0)=\tau_\infty(R_0)\left[1-\frac{3R_0}{8h}+O((R_0/h)^2)\right],\qquad
\Delta\tau=-\frac{\phi_B^2R_0^4}{4M\gamma h}.}
$$
The negative correction reflects faster evaporation into the nearby reservoir. This leading coefficient also agrees with the small-$R/h$ expansion of the exact <sphere-plane capacitance>; the stipulated logarithmic approximation need not match higher orders.
