= Solution
{parent=1C}

The <characteristic polynomial> of the homogeneous <linear ordinary differential equation> is
$$
r^2-2r-3=(r-3)(r+1),
$$
so its <complementary solution> is $Ae^{3x}+Be^{-x}$. Because the forcing contains the resonant factor $e^{-x}$, set $y=e^{-x}v$. Substitution gives
$$
y''-2y'-3y=e^{-x}(v''-4v')=-16xe^{-x}.
$$
Writing $w=v'$, a polynomial particular solution of $w'-4w=-16x$ is $w=4x+1$, and hence $v=2x^2+x$. Thus
$$
y=Ae^{3x}+(B+2x^2+x)e^{-x}.
$$
Boundedness as $x\to\infty$ forces $A=0$, while the <initial condition> $y(0)=1$ gives $B=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=(2x^2+x+1)e^{-x}}.
$$

Substituting the proposed form $\mathbf v(t)=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf u$ into the <linear system of ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
e^{\lambda t}(\lambda I-B)\mathbf u=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf x.
$$
If $\lambda$ is not an <eigenvalue> of $B$, the matrix $\lambda I-B$ is <invertible matrix>[invertible], so
$$
\mathbf u=(\lambda I-B)^{-1}\mathbf x
$$
provides the required particular solution.

In the stated two-dimensional case,
$$
2I-B=\begin{pmatrix}2&-3\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
(2I-B)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&2\end{pmatrix},
$$
and therefore $\mathbf u=(3,2)^T$. The <eigenvalues> of $B$ are $\pm\sqrt3$, with corresponding <eigenvectors> $(\sqrt3,1)^T$ and $(-\sqrt3,1)^T$. Adding the <general solution> of the homogeneous system yields
$$
\boxed{
\mathbf v(t)=e^{2t}\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\end{pmatrix}
+C_+e^{\sqrt3t}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3\\1\end{pmatrix}
+C_-e^{-\sqrt3t}\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt3\\1\end{pmatrix}}.
$$

Every allocation is determined by the set of $n$ children on the red bus, so there are $\binom{2n}{n}$ equally likely allocations. Exactly one places every boy on red and every girl on green. Hence
$$
\mathbb P(A_n)=\binom{2n}{n}^{-1}=\frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}.
$$
Applying <Stirling formula> to the numerator and denominator gives the <central binomial coefficient> asymptotic
$$
\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}},
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A_n)\sim\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{4^n}}.
$$

= Solution

<Independence of random variables> and the two unit-rate <exponential distributions> give
$$
f_{X,Y}(x,y)=e^{-x-y}\mathbf1_{\{x\geq0,\ y\geq0\}}.
$$
The <linear transformation>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}U\\V\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}6&8\\2&3\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\end{pmatrix}
$$
has determinant $2$ and inverse
$$
x=\frac{3u-8v}{2},\qquad y=-u+3v.
$$
The inequalities $x,y\geq0$ become $8v/3\leq u\leq3v$. By the <change-of-variables formula for probability densities>,
$$
\boxed{f_{U,V}(u,v)=\frac12e^{-u/2+v}\mathbf1_{\{v\geq0,\ 8v/3\leq u\leq3v\}}}.
$$
The variables $U$ and $V$ are not independent: the support of their joint density is a wedge rather than a <Cartesian product> of one-dimensional supports. For example, conditioning on a positive value of $V$ constrains $U$ to a finite interval depending on that value.

Thus the <centred finite difference> has <truncation error> $O(h^2)$, so $\boxed{\alpha=2}$.

= Solution
{parent=6C}

Substitute the <power series> $y=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$ and compare the coefficient of $x^n$. This gives
$$
(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}+(\lambda^2-n^2)a_n=0,
$$
or
$$
\boxed{a_{n+2}=\frac{n^2-\lambda^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}a_n}.
$$
The two arbitrary coefficients $a_0,a_1$ determine all solutions. The condition $y'(0)=0$ sets $a_1=0$, leaving the even series. It terminates precisely when $\lambda^2=(2k)^2$ for some nonnegative integer $k$. Therefore the permitted real values are $\lambda=0$ or $\lambda=\pm2k$ for $k\geq1$; these are the even <Chebyshev polynomials> up to normalization.

The degrees below six occur for $\lambda=0,\pm2,\pm4$. Taking $a_0=1$, the corresponding polynomials are
$$
1,\qquad 1-2x^2,\qquad 1-8x^2+8x^4.
$$

For the forced equation, try $y=a_0+a_2x^2+a_4x^4$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^4,x^2,1$ gives
$$
-15a_4=8,
\qquad 12a_4-3a_2=0,
\qquad 2a_2+a_0=-3.
$$
Thus one polynomial satisfying $y'(0)=0$ is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{19-32x^2-8x^4}{15}}.
$$

(0,0),\qquad \left(0,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad \left(0,-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).

The <Kolmogorov axioms> for a <probability measure> $\mathbb P$ are nonnegativity, normalization $\mathbb P(\Omega)=1$, and <countable additivity> on pairwise disjoint events. For $\mathbb P(B)>0$, <conditional probability> is defined by
$$
\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)}.
$$
Because the events $A\cap B_i$ are pairwise disjoint and their union is $A$, countable additivity gives the <law of total probability>
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(A\cap B_i)
=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i).
$$
For any $j$ with $\mathbb P(A)>0$, the definition of conditional probability and this identity give <Bayes theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B_j\mid A)=
\frac{\mathbb P(A\mid B_j)\mathbb P(B_j)}
{\sum_i\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i)}}.
$$

Urn $i$ has $2^i$ balls, so a draw from it is white with probability $2^{-i}$. Given that the first ball is white, <Bayes theorem> gives the <posterior probability>
$$
\mathbb P(I=i\mid W)=\frac{2^{-i}}{\sum_{j=1}^n2^{-j}}.

$$

=\frac13(1+2^{-n}).
$$
Consequently $\boxed{p(n)\to1/3}$.

= Solution

Let $h_k$ be the probability that the <simple random walk> started at $k$ hits $n$ before zero. The <first-step analysis> recurrence and <boundary conditions> are
$$
h_k=ph_{k+1}+qh_{k-1},
\qquad h_0=0,\quad h_n=1.
$$
If $p\ne q$, the <characteristic equation of a linear recurrence> has roots $1$ and $q/p$, so the boundary conditions give
$$
\boxed{h_m=\frac{1-(q/p)^m}{1-(q/p)^n}}.
$$
If $p=q=1/2$, the repeated root gives the limiting formula $\boxed{h_m=m/n}$. This is the <gambler's ruin> probability.

For a fixed integer stake $a\leq N$, the fact that $a$ divides $N!$ lets us measure Patricia's wealth in units of £$a$. She starts at $M=N!/a$ and succeeds upon reaching $2M$. The formula above factors as
$$
h_M=\frac{1-r^M}{1-r^{2M}}=\frac1{1+r^M},
\qquad r=\frac qp.
$$
For $p=18/37$, $r=19/18>1$, so this probability increases as $M$ decreases. She should therefore choose the largest stake, $\boxed{a=N}$. For $p=19/37$, $r=18/19<1$, so the probability increases as $M$ increases; she should choose the smallest stake, $\boxed{a=1}$.

For every $u\in\mathbb R^2$,
$$
u^T(AX)=(A^Tu)^TX.
$$
The right-hand side is a <normal random variable> by the definition of a <bivariate normal distribution>. Therefore every linear combination of the coordinates of $AX$ is normal, so $\boxed{AX\text{ is bivariate normal}}$.

{parent=a}

= Solution

For every $\lambda\in\mathbb R^2$, the scalar $\lambda^TX$ is normal with mean $\lambda^T\mu$ and variance $\lambda^TV\lambda$. The <moment-generating function of a normal distribution> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{M_X(\lambda)=\exp\left(\lambda^T\mu+\frac12\lambda^TV\lambda\right)}.
$$
**Thus $\mu$ and $V$ determine the moment-generating function on all of $\mathbb R^2$. By the <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions>, they uniquely determine the <probability distribution> of $X$.**

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <covariance matrix> is
$$
\boxed{V=\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_1^2&\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\
\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_2^2
\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
If $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=0$, the moment-generating function in part (ii) factors into the product of the two marginal moment-generating functions. Thus $X_1$ and $X_2$ are <independent random variables>; this is the <independence of uncorrelated jointly normal variables>.

By bilinearity of <covariance>,
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2-aX_1)
=\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2-a\sigma_1^2.
$$
Choose
$$
\boxed{a=\rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}},
\qquad Y=X_2-aX_1.
$$
Then $(X_1,Y)$ is bivariate normal by part (i) and has zero covariance, so $Y$ is a normal random variable independent of $X_1$. Hence $X_2=aX_1+Y$.

= Solution
{parent=12F}

Let $X_i=\mathbf1_{A_i}$ be the <indicator random variable> of $A_i$. Then $X=\sum_iX_i$, so <linearity of expectation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E X_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb P(A_i)}.
$$
Using <variance of a sum> and
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)
=\mathbb E(X_iX_j)-\mathbb E X_i\mathbb E X_j
=\mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j)-\mathbb P(A_i)\mathbb P(A_j),
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n
\left[\mathbb P(A_i\cap A_j)-\mathbb P(A_i)\mathbb P(A_j)\right]}.
$$

For the circular array, let $X_i$ indicate that bulbs $i$ and $i+1$ are both on, with indices read modulo $n$. Then $\mathbb E X_i=p^2$. Non-neighbouring indicators are independent, while
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=p^2-p^4,
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_{i+1})=p^3-p^4.
$$
There are $n$ individual terms and $2n$ ordered neighbouring pairs in the double covariance sum, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=np^2},
\qquad
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=n(p^2+2p^3-3p^4)}.
$$

The event $B$ is exactly $\{X\geq1\}$. If $p=n^{-0.6}$, <Markov inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb P(B)\leq\mathbb E X=np^2=n^{-0.2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
If $p=n^{-0.4}$, then $B^c=\{X=0\}$ implies $|X-\mathbb EX|\geq\mathbb EX$. By <Chebyshev inequality>,
$$
\mathbb P(B^c)\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{(\mathbb EX)^2}
=\frac1{np^2}+\frac2{np}-\frac3n
=n^{-0.2}+2n^{-0.6}-3n^{-1}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\mathbb P(B)\to1}$ in the second regime.
