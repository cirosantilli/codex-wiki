= Solution
{parent=1E}

A <Jordan normal form> of a complex $n\times n$ <matrix> $A$ is a <similarity transformation> $P^{-1}AP=J$ in which $J$ is a direct sum of <Jordan blocks>
$$
J_r(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1&&0\\&\lambda&\ddots&\\&&\ddots&1\\0&&&\lambda\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For each <eigenvalue>, the multiset of block sizes is uniquely determined by $A$; only the order of the blocks is arbitrary. The change-of-basis <matrix> $P$ is generally not unique.

For the given <matrix>, expansion of the <characteristic polynomial> gives
$$
\det(tI-A)=(t-1)^2(t-2),\qquad A-I=\begin{pmatrix}-8&3&-5\\7&-2&5\\17&-6&11\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The upper-left $2\times2$ minor of $A-I$ is $-5$, so its <rank> is $2$ and the <eigenspace> for $1$ has dimension $1$. Thus the two occurrences of the <eigenvalue> $1$ belong to a single size-$2$ <Jordan block>. More explicitly, the <Jordan chain> and the remaining <eigenvector> can be chosen as
$$
v_1=(1,1,-1)^T,\quad v_2=(1,3,0)^T,\quad v_3=(0,5,3)^T,\qquad (A-I)v_1=0,\quad(A-I)v_2=v_1,\quad Av_3=2v_3.
$$
Consequently, with $P=(v_1\ v_2\ v_3)$, for which $\det P=1$, the answer is
$$
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2G}

Write $q_n(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{n-1}$, a monic <primitive polynomial>. If $n=ab$ with $a,b>1$, grouping terms gives a factorization in the <polynomial ring> $\mathbb Z[X]$:
$$
q_n(X)=(1+X+\cdots+X^{a-1})(1+X^a+\cdots+X^{(b-1)a}).
$$
Both factors have positive degree, so $q_n$ is not an <irreducible polynomial>.

Conversely, if $n=p$ is a <prime number>, change variables by $X=Y+1$. The <binomial theorem> gives
$$
q_p(Y+1)=\frac{(Y+1)^p-1}{Y}=p+\binom p2Y+\cdots+\binom p{p-1}Y^{p-2}+Y^{p-1}.
$$
Every coefficient except the leading one is divisible by $p$, because $p\mid\binom pk$ for $1\leq k<p$, whereas the constant coefficient $p$ is not divisible by $p^2$. The <Eisenstein criterion> makes this <polynomial> irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. The substitution $X\mapsto Y+1$ is an invertible <ring homomorphism>, so $q_p$ is also irreducible over $\mathbb Q$; the <Gauss lemma for polynomials> transfers this to $\mathbb Z[X]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{q_n\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbb Z\iff n\text{ is prime}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=3F}

Both functionals are finite on $X$: a <continuous function> on the <compact metric space> $[0,1]$ is bounded and integrable. Homogeneity follows from $|af|=|a||f|$, and the <triangle inequality> follows by integrating, or taking the supremum of, $|f+g|\leq|f|+|g|$. Each is nonnegative. If $\|f\|_\infty=0$, then $f$ vanishes everywhere. If a <continuous function> $f$ is nonzero at some point, continuity supplies an interval of positive length on which $|f|$ is bounded below by a positive number; hence $\|f\|_1>0$. Thus both are <norms>.

For the nonnegative $f_n$, direct integration gives the <L1 norm>
$$
\|f_n\|_1=n\int_0^1(t^n-t^{n+1})\,dt=\frac{n}{(n+1)(n+2)}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{f_n\longrightarrow0\text{ in }\|\cdot\|_1}$. On the other hand, differentiation places the maximum of $t^n(1-t)$ at $t=n/(n+1)$, giving the <supremum norm>
$$
\|f_n\|_\infty=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n+1}\longrightarrow e^{-1}.
$$
For $t<1$, exponential decay implies $nt^n(1-t)\to0$, while $f_n(1)=0$. Thus the <pointwise convergence> is to zero throughout $[0,1]$. Any convergence in the <supremum norm> would imply <uniform convergence>, hence the same pointwise limit zero, contradicting the displayed positive limiting <norm>. Therefore \b[there is no convergence in the supremum <norm>].

{parent=Paper 4}

{title2=Complex Analysis}

= Solution
{parent=4E}

Set $h=f-g$, an <entire function>. By the <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem>, a subsequence of the distinct zeros $z_i$ converges to a point $a$ in the closed unit disc. An <analytic function> has a convergent <Taylor series> in a neighbourhood of each point. If the first nonzero coefficient of $h$ at $a$ had index $m$, then
$$
h(z)=(z-a)^m k(z),\qquad k(a)\neq0,
$$
with $k$ analytic and nonzero in a sufficiently small disc. The zero $a$ would then be isolated, contradicting the accumulating distinct zeros. All Taylor coefficients at $a$ vanish, so $h$ vanishes on a disc.

To justify continuation across the whole plane, let $Z$ be the set of points at which $h$ vanishes on a neighbourhood. This set is nonempty and open. It is also closed: if points of $Z$ tend to $b$, continuity of each derivative gives $h^{(m)}(b)=0$ for every $m$, and the <Taylor series> at $b$ gives a zero neighbourhood. Since $\mathbb C$ is a <connected space>, $Z=\mathbb C$. This proves the <identity theorem> here and gives $\boxed{f\equiv g}$.

Without boundedness, the distinct zeros need not have an accumulation point in the plane. For example,
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sin(\pi z),\qquad g(z)=0,\qquad z_i=i\ (i=1,2,\ldots)}
$$
are distinct <entire functions> agreeing at all the indicated positive integers.

= Solution
{parent=5A}

For the <half-range Fourier cosine series> on $(0,1)$, the coefficients are
$$
a_0=2\int_0^1x^2\,dx=\frac23,\qquad a_n=2\int_0^1x^2\cos(n\pi x)\,dx.
$$
Two integrations by parts, with $k=n\pi$, give
$$
\int_0^1x^2\cos(kx)\,dx=-\frac2k\int_0^1x\sin(kx)\,dx=\frac{2(-1)^n}{k^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{x^2=\frac13+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\cos(n\pi x)}{n^2}.}
$$
The even, $2$-periodic extension is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable, so the <Fourier series> converges to it, including at $x=1$. Equivalently, the summable coefficient bound gives <uniform convergence> and permits taking the limit from the interior. Evaluating at $x=1$, where $(-1)^n\cos(n\pi)=1$, yields
$$
1=\frac13+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2},\qquad \boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.}
$$
This is the <Basel problem>.

= 6A
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Quantum Mechanics}

= Solution
{parent=6A}

For a time-independent <quantum Hamiltonian>, a <stationary state> is an energy <eigenstate>. The <Schrodinger equation> separates its time dependence as
$$
\psi(x,t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}\chi(x),\qquad \widehat H\chi=E\chi.
$$
Its probability density $|\psi(x,t)|^2=|\chi(x)|^2$ is time independent. Linearity of the <Schrodinger equation> gives
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\chi_1(x)+e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\chi_2(x)\right).}
$$
Put $A_{jk}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi_j^*\widehat A\chi_k\,dx$. The operator for a time-independent <observable> is a <Hermitian operator>, so $A_{21}=\overline{A_{12}}$. Substituting the evolved <wavefunction> into the <expectation value> gives
$$
\langle\psi,\widehat A\psi\rangle=\frac12\left(A_{11}+A_{22}+e^{i(E_1-E_2)t/\hbar}A_{12}+e^{-i(E_1-E_2)t/\hbar}\overline{A_{12}}\right),
$$
and therefore, for a normalized state,
$$
\boxed{\langle A\rangle_\psi=\frac{A_{11}+A_{22}}2+\operatorname{Re}\!\left(e^{i(E_1-E_2)t/\hbar}A_{12}\right).}
$$
The normalization is automatic for orthogonal $\chi_1,\chi_2$, in particular when $E_1\neq E_2$: self-adjointness of $\widehat H$ gives $(E_1-E_2)\langle\chi_1,\chi_2\rangle=0$. If the energies coincide, normalization of the individual <eigenstates> alone does not ensure normalization of their displayed sum; its squared <norm> is $1+\operatorname{Re}\langle\chi_1,\chi_2\rangle$. For a nonzero sum not having unit <norm>, the physical <expectation value> is the displayed numerator divided by this squared <norm>. With orthonormal energy states, the stated formula holds in both the distinct and degenerate cases.

= i

= Solution
{parent=i}

The differential <Gauss law> is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$. On a large ball $B_R$, the product rule and $\nabla\phi=-\mathbf E$ give
$$
\rho\phi=\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf E)+\epsilon_0|\mathbf E|^2.
$$
The <divergence theorem> consequently expresses the <electrostatic energy> as
$$
\frac12\int_{B_R}\rho\phi\,dV=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{B_R}|\mathbf E|^2\,dV+\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\partial B_R}\phi\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
For a localized charge distribution with the <electric potential> chosen to vanish at infinity, $\phi=O(R^{-1})$ and $\mathbf E=O(R^{-2})$, so the surface term is $O(R^{-1})$ and tends to zero. More generally, it suffices to assume that this boundary integral vanishes. Passing to all space gives
$$
\boxed{U=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\mathbb R^3}|\mathbf E|^2\,dV.}
$$
The physical decay and potential reference are relevant: $\mathbf E\to0$ by itself does not specify the additive constant in $\phi$. Adding a constant $C$ changes the charge-based expression by $CQ/2$ when the total charge is $Q$, while leaving the field unchanged. The zero-at-infinity potential is the usual reference implicit in this all-space <electrostatic energy> formula.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Spherical symmetry and the integral <Gauss law> give $4\pi r^2E_r(r)=Q/\epsilon_0$ for $r>R$. Together with the stipulated zero interior field,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\begin{cases}0,&r<R,\\\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r},&r>R.\end{cases}}
$$
The <electric field> jumps across the ideal charged surface; its value exactly on that surface is not needed for the volume integral. The <electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged spherical shell> is
$$
U=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\,4\pi\int_R^\infty\left(\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\right)^2r^2\,dr=\boxed{\frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0R}}.
$$
As a check, the shell has constant <electric potential> $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)$, with zero reference at infinity, so $U=Q\phi(R)/2$ gives the same result.

{title2=Numerical Analysis}

= Solution
{parent=8D}

With $x=\cos\theta$, the weighted <inner product> for the <Chebyshev polynomials> becomes
$$
\int_{-1}^1\frac{T_n(x)T_m(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\int_0^\pi\cos(n\theta)\cos(m\theta)\,d\theta.
$$
The identity $2\cos(n\theta)\cos(m\theta)=\cos((n-m)\theta)+\cos((n+m)\theta)$ and the vanishing of $\int_0^\pi\cos(k\theta)\,d\theta$ for positive integers $k$ give zero when $n\neq m$, $\pi/2$ when $n=m>0$, and $\pi$ when $n=m=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\frac{T_nT_m}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\frac\pi2\delta_{nm}(1+\delta_{n0}).}
$$
For <Gaussian quadrature>, choose the $n$ distinct nodes as the zeros of the degree-$n$ <orthogonal polynomial> for the weight, here $T_n$, and choose the weights as the weighted integrals of their <Lagrange interpolation> basis functions. If a <polynomial> $p$ has degree at most $2n-1$, divide it as $p=qT_n+r$, where both $q$ and $r$ have degree at most $n-1$. Orthogonality makes the integral of $qT_n$ zero, while its quadrature sum also vanishes; interpolation integrates $r$ exactly. This proves degree $2n-1$ exactness. It is optimal because the squared nodal <polynomial>, of degree $2n$, has zero quadrature sum and strictly positive integral.

For $n=3$, $T_3(x)=4x^3-3x$, so the nodes are $-\sqrt3/2,0,\sqrt3/2$. Their interpolation basis functions are
$$
\ell_-(x)=\frac23x\left(x-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right),\quad \ell_0(x)=1-\frac43x^2,\quad \ell_+(x)=\frac23x\left(x+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right).
$$
The weighted zeroth and second moments are $\pi$ and $\pi/2$, and the first moment is zero. Integrating these three basis functions gives equal weights $\pi/3$, hence the <Chebyshev–Gauss quadrature> rule
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx\approx\frac\pi3\left[f\!\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)+f(0)+f\!\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\right],}
$$
which is exact for every <polynomial> of degree at most $5$.

= Solution
{parent=9H}

The <communicating classes> are $T=\{1,2\}$, $B=\{3,4\}$ and $C=\{5,6,7\}$. The class $T$ is open: it can reach $B$ or $C$ and cannot be reached from either. To remain in $T$ for two steps has probability $1/6$, so $\mathbb P_i(X_{2k}\in T)=(1/6)^k$ for $i\in T$. Its states are <transient states>. All returns within $T$ have even length and a two-step return is possible, so their <period of a state in a Markov chain> is $2$.

The <closed communicating class> $B$ alternates deterministically between $3$ and $4$, and therefore consists of <positive recurrent states> of period $2$. The class $C$ is finite, closed and irreducible, so its states are also <positive recurrent states>. The self-loop at $7$ gives period $1$ there, and the <period is constant on a communicating class>, so every state of $C$ has period $1$.

For $C$, solve the <stationary distribution> equations:
$$
\pi_5=\tfrac12\pi_7,\qquad \pi_6=\pi_5,\qquad \pi_7=\pi_6+\tfrac12\pi_7,\qquad \pi_5+\pi_6+\pi_7=1.
$$
Hence $(\pi_5,\pi_6,\pi_7)=(1/4,1/4,1/2)$. Finite irreducibility and aperiodicity imply convergence to this <stationary distribution> from any state of $C$. If $h_i$ is the probability of eventually entering $C$, the <Markov property> gives
$$
h_1=\tfrac12h_2+\tfrac14,\qquad h_2=\tfrac13h_1+\tfrac16,
$$
so $h_1=2/5$ and $h_2=3/10$. Conditioning on the finite entrance time and then letting the remaining time tend to infinity proves that the limiting probabilities in $C$ are $h_i\pi_j$. The probability of entrance after a large cutoff tends to zero, which justifies this conditioning limit.

For $B$, convergence depends on the <parity balance on entry to a period-two Markov class>. Entry always occurs at $3$. Starting from $1$, the probabilities of first entry at time $2k+1$ and at time $2k+2$ are both $\frac14(1/6)^k$. Each parity therefore has total probability $3/10$, so both $p_{13}^{(n)}$ and $p_{14}^{(n)}$ tend to $3/10$, despite the recurrent class being periodic. Starting from $2$, the corresponding odd- and even-entry probabilities are $\frac12(1/6)^k$ and $\frac1{12}(1/6)^k$, with totals $3/5$ and $1/10$. Consequently
$$
(p_{23}^{(2k)},p_{24}^{(2k)})\longrightarrow(1/10,3/5),\qquad (p_{23}^{(2k+1)},p_{24}^{(2k+1)})\longrightarrow(3/5,1/10),
$$
so neither limit exists. From $3$ or $4$, the probabilities at $3$ and $4$ alternate between zero and one; from $C$ they are identically zero. Finally every probability of being in $T$ tends to zero, from all starting states.

All $49$ requested conclusions are encoded below, with $\ast$ denoting a nonexistent limit:
$$
\boxed{\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}p_{ij}^{(n)}\right)_{i,j=1}^7=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&3/10&3/10&1/10&1/10&1/5\\0&0&\ast&\ast&3/40&3/40&3/20\\0&0&\ast&\ast&0&0&0\\0&0&\ast&\ast&0&0&0\\0&0&0&0&1/4&1/4&1/2\\0&0&0&0&1/4&1/4&1/2\\0&0&0&0&1/4&1/4&1/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The class classification is \b[$T$: transient, period $2$; $B$: recurrent, period $2$; $C$: recurrent, period $1$].

{title2=Linear Algebra}

= Solution
{parent=10E}

A <Hermitian matrix> satisfies $A=A^\dagger$, where $\dagger$ means conjugate transpose. If $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\neq0$, then $v^\dagger Av=\lambda\|v\|^2$ is real, since it equals its complex conjugate $v^\dagger A^\dagger v$. Thus every <eigenvalue> is real. To prove the finite-dimensional <spectral theorem>, choose a unit <eigenvector> $v$; one exists because the complex <characteristic polynomial> has a root. Its <orthogonal complement> is invariant, since for $w\perp v$,
$$
v^\dagger Aw=(Av)^\dagger w=\lambda v^\dagger w=0.
$$
The restriction to that complement is again Hermitian. Induction on dimension supplies an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>, so $A=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)U^\dagger$ for a <unitary matrix> $U$.

In this basis, $x^\dagger Ax=\sum_i\lambda_i|(U^\dagger x)_i|^2$. Therefore $\boxed{A>0\iff\lambda_i>0\text{ for every }i}$. In that case the <square root of a matrix>
$$
C=U\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_n})U^\dagger
$$
is Hermitian, positive definite and satisfies $C^2=A$. For uniqueness, any positive-definite Hermitian $D$ satisfying $D^2=A$ commutes with $A$ and hence preserves each <eigenspace> of $A$. Its restriction to the $\lambda$-eigenspace is Hermitian, and its positive <eigenvalues> all solve $\mu^2=\lambda$. Thus that restriction is $\sqrt\lambda I$, forcing $D=C$. This is the <principal square root of a positive semidefinite matrix>, here strictly positive definite.

Now let $D=\sqrt B$ and $X=C-D$. Expanding without assuming that $C$ and $D$ commute gives
$$
CX+XC=2C^2-CD-DC=(A-B)+X^2.
$$
The <Hermitian matrix> $X$ has $X^2\geq0$ because $v^\dagger X^2v=\|Xv\|^2$, whereas $A-B>0$. Hence $CX+XC>0$. If $Xv=\mu v$, $v\neq0$, then
$$
0<v^\dagger(CX+XC)v=2\mu\,v^\dagger Cv.
$$
Since $C>0$, every real <eigenvalue> $\mu$ of $X$ is positive. The positive-eigenvalue criterion now gives the strict finite-dimensional <operator monotonicity of the square root>:
$$
\boxed{CX+XC>0,\qquad \sqrt A-\sqrt B=X>0.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=11G}

An <R-module> $M$ is a <free module> if it has a basis $(e_i)_{i\in I}$: every $m\in M$ has a unique expression as a finite sum $\sum_i r_ie_i$, with $r_i\in R$. Equivalently, it is a <direct sum> of copies of $R$. Let $q:M\to M/N$ be the quotient map. When $M/N$ is free, choose a basis $(\overline f_j)$ and lifts $f_j\in M$. The <universal property of a free module> defines an $R$-linear map $s:M/N\to M$ by $s(\sum_jr_j\overline f_j)=\sum_jr_jf_j$. It satisfies $q\circ s=\operatorname{id}$, so every $m\in M$ decomposes uniquely as
$$
m=\bigl(m-s(q(m))\bigr)+s(q(m)),\qquad m-s(q(m))\in N.
$$
This gives a <split short exact sequence> and $M\cong N\oplus(M/N)$. If $N$ is also free, the union of its basis and the lifted quotient basis is a basis of $M$: spanning follows from the decomposition, and applying $q$ to a putative relation kills all quotient coefficients before independence in $N$ kills the remaining ones. Thus \b[free submodule and free quotient imply a free module].

For the first claim, take $R=\mathbb Z$ and
$$
M=\mathbb Z,\quad N=2\mathbb Z,\qquad M'=\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,\quad N'=\mathbb Z\oplus\{0\}.
$$
Here $N\cong N'\cong\mathbb Z$ are <free modules> and $M/N\cong M'/N'\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. But $M$ is a <torsion-free module>, whereas $M'$ contains the nonzero <torsion element> $(0,\overline1)$. Therefore \b[claim (1) is false].

For the second claim, the same lifting construction works without assuming that $N$ is free. Since both isomorphic <quotient modules> are free, it yields
$$
M\cong N\oplus(M/N)\cong N'\oplus(M'/N')\cong M'.
$$
Thus \b[claim (2) is true]. Equivalently, this uses the fact that <free modules are projective>.

= Solution
{parent=12G}

For a parametrized <curve> on the surface, the <energy of a curve> is
$$
\mathcal E(\gamma)=\frac12\int_a^b|\dot\gamma(t)|^2\,dt=\frac12\int_a^b\left(E\dot u^2+2F\dot u\dot v+G\dot v^2\right)\,dt,
$$
where the <first fundamental form> coefficients are evaluated at $(u(t),v(t))$. A <fixed-endpoint variation of a curve> has coordinate variation fields $\eta,\xi$ vanishing at $a,b$. Differentiating under the integral and integrating their derivative terms by parts gives
$$
\delta\mathcal E=\int_a^b\!\left[\left(\frac{E_u\dot u^2+2F_u\dot u\dot v+G_u\dot v^2}{2}-\frac d{dt}(E\dot u+F\dot v)\right)\eta+\left(\frac{E_v\dot u^2+2F_v\dot u\dot v+G_v\dot v^2}{2}-\frac d{dt}(F\dot u+G\dot v)\right)\xi\right]dt.
$$
The fields can be chosen independently with compact support in $(a,b)$. Stationarity and the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> therefore force both coefficients to vanish. These <Euler-Lagrange equations> are the <geodesic equations>:
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}(E\dot u+F\dot v)=\frac12(E_u\dot u^2+2F_u\dot u\dot v+G_u\dot v^2),\qquad\frac d{dt}(F\dot u+G\dot v)=\frac12(E_v\dot u^2+2F_v\dot u\dot v+G_v\dot v^2).}
$$

= i
{parent=12G}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

The coordinate curves are assumed to be <geodesics> as geometric curves; use their <arc-length parametrization> in the <geodesic equations>. On $u=c$, choose increasing arc length $s$, so $\dot u=0$ and $\dot v=1/\sqrt G$. The transverse equation becomes
$$
\frac d{ds}\left(\frac F{\sqrt G}\right)=\frac{G_u}{2G},\qquad \frac1{\sqrt G}\left(\frac F{\sqrt G}\right)_v=\frac{G_u}{2G}.
$$
Thus $(F/\sqrt G)_v=G_u/(2\sqrt G)=(\sqrt G)_u$. On $v=d$, similarly $\dot v=0$, $\dot u=1/\sqrt E$, and the transverse equation gives $(F/\sqrt E)_u=E_v/(2\sqrt E)=(\sqrt E)_v$. Hence the <geodesic coordinate grid> identities are
$$
\boxed{(F/\sqrt G)_v=(\sqrt G)_u,\qquad(F/\sqrt E)_u=(\sqrt E)_v.}
$$

= ii
{parent=12G}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The angle $\theta$ between the oriented coordinate tangent vectors satisfies $\cos\theta=F/\sqrt{EG}$. The constant-angle assumption therefore gives $F=k\sqrt E\sqrt G$ for a constant $k$. The <first fundamental form> is positive definite, so $EG-F^2>0$ and hence $|k|<1$. Substitution into the <geodesic coordinate grid> identities from part (i) gives
$$
k(\sqrt E)_v=(\sqrt G)_u,\qquad k(\sqrt G)_u=(\sqrt E)_v.
$$
Eliminating either derivative yields $(1-k^2)(\sqrt E)_v=0$ and $(1-k^2)(\sqrt G)_u=0$. Since $E,G>0$ and $1-k^2>0$, this proves
$$
\boxed{E_v=0=G_u.}
$$
If the angle is understood between unoriented curves, its constancy initially fixes $|F|/\sqrt{EG}$; continuity fixes the sign on each connected chart when this quantity is nonzero, and the zero case already has $F=0$. Thus the same conclusion holds. In fact <constant-angle geodesic coordinate grids are locally flat>, although only the displayed derivatives are needed here.

= Solution
{parent=13F}

Two <norms> $\|\cdot\|_a,\|\cdot\|_b$ are <Lipschitz equivalent norms> when constants $c,C>0$ satisfy $c\|x\|_a\leq\|x\|_b\leq C\|x\|_a$ for every vector $x$. Applying these inequalities to $x_j-x_k$ shows that a sequence is a <Cauchy sequence> for one <norm> exactly when it is so for the other; applying them to $x_j-x$ likewise equates convergence. If one <norm> is complete, a <Cauchy sequence> for the other converges using the first <norm> and hence also the second. Interchanging the <norms> proves \b[completeness is preserved in both directions].

Write $x=\sum_i x_ie_i$ and set $C=\sum_i\|e_i\|$. If $\|x\|_2=1$, then $|x_i|\leq1$, and the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\|x\|\leq\sum_i|x_i|\|e_i\|\leq C.
$$
Scaling a nonzero $x$ gives $\|x\|\leq C\|x\|_2$ for every $x$. The reverse <triangle inequality> now implies
$$
\bigl|\|x\|-\|y\|\bigr|\leq\|x-y\|\leq C\|x-y\|_2,
$$
so $x\mapsto\|x\|$ is continuous, indeed Lipschitz, in the <Euclidean norm>.

Suppose there were no positive lower bound on the Euclidean <unit sphere>. We could choose $x_j$ with $\|x_j\|_2=1$ but $\|x_j\|<1/j$. The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> supplies a subsequence converging in the <Euclidean norm> to $x$. Continuity of both <norms> gives $\|x\|_2=1$ and $\|x\|=0$, contradicting definiteness. Therefore some $m>0$ satisfies $\|x\|\geq m$ on the unit sphere. Scaling again proves the <finite-dimensional equivalence of norms>:
$$
\boxed{m\|x\|_2\leq\|x\|\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\|e_i\|\right)\|x\|_2\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R^n.}
$$

{title2=Metric and Topological Spaces}

= Solution
{parent=14F}

A <basis of a topology> is a family $\mathcal B$ of open sets such that every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal B$. Equivalently, whenever $x\in U$ and $U$ is open, there is $B\in\mathcal B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$. For a <metric space>, all open balls form a basis. For the <product topology> on $X\times Y$, the rectangles $U\times V$ with $U,V$ open in their respective factors form a basis; rectangles made from factor bases also suffice.

If $C\subseteq X$ and $D\subseteq Y$ are countable <dense subsets>, then $C\times D$ is countable. Every nonempty basic rectangle meets it, since $U\cap C$ and $V\cap D$ are nonempty. Every nonempty open set contains such a rectangle, proving that \b[a product of two separable spaces is separable].

Suppose a <metric space> has a countable dense set $C$. The balls $B(c,r)$ with $c\in C$ and positive rational $r$ form a countable basis. Indeed, given $x\in U$ open, choose $\epsilon>0$ with $B(x,\epsilon)\subseteq U$, then choose $c\in C$ with $d(c,x)<\epsilon/3$ and rational $r$ satisfying
$$
d(c,x)<r<\epsilon-d(c,x).
$$
The triangle inequality gives $x\in B(c,r)\subseteq B(x,\epsilon)\subseteq U$. Conversely, from any countable basis select one point of each nonempty member. These points form a countable <dense subset>, because every nonempty open set contains a basis member. This direction holds for arbitrary topological spaces. Thus a <metric space> is separable exactly when it is a <second-countable space>. Intersecting a countable basis with a subspace gives a countable basis for its <subspace topology>; selecting points as above shows that \b[every subspace of a separable metric space is separable].

For the <Sorgenfrey line>, $\mathbb Q$ meets every nonempty interval $[a,b)$, so it is a countable <dense subset>. In the <Sorgenfrey plane>, however, for any $x\in\mathbb R$ and $\epsilon>0$,
$$
\bigl([x,x+\epsilon)\times[-x,-x+\epsilon)\bigr)\cap Y=\{(x,-x)\}.
$$
Indeed, $(t,-t)$ in that rectangle requires both $t\geq x$ and $t\leq x$. Every singleton is therefore open in the <subspace topology> on $Y$, which is the <discrete topology>. A dense subset of a discrete space must contain every point. Since $Y$ is in bijection with the uncountable set $\mathbb R$, it has no countable dense subset. Hence \b[$X$ is separable and $Y$ is not separable], illustrating why the metric assumption in the inheritance statement matters.

Choose the principal <branch of the complex logarithm> on $H$, with $-\pi<\arg z<\pi$. Then
$$
\log z=\log|z|+i\arg z,\qquad w=-i\log z=\arg z-i\log|z|.

Thus $-\pi<\operatorname{Re}w<\pi$ and the imaginary part ranges over all real numbers. Conversely, for $w=x+iy$ in the strip, $z=e^{iw}=e^{-y}e^{ix}$ is in $H$ and its principal logarithm is $iw$. Every other continuous branch on the connected domain is $\log z+2\pi ik$ for some fixed integer $k$, so its image is the translated strip $\boxed{S+2\pi k}$.

For $G$, introduce the <Mobius transformation> $\zeta=(z-1)/(z+1)$, whose inverse is $z=(1+\zeta)/(1-\zeta)$. Writing $\zeta=a+ib$, its defining circle inequalities transform as follows:
$$
|z|<1\iff |1+\zeta|<|1-\zeta|\iff a<0,
$$
and
$$
|z+i|>\sqrt2\iff |(1+i)+(1-i)\zeta|^2>2|1-\zeta|^2\iff a+b>0.
$$
Hence $G$ maps bijectively onto the wedge $\pi/2<\arg\zeta<3\pi/4$. The principal <branch of the complex logarithm> is analytic throughout this wedge. $$
\boxed{W(z)=-8i\log\left(\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right)-5\pi,\qquad z\in G.}
$$
Its real part is $8\arg\zeta-5\pi\in(-\pi,\pi)$, and its imaginary part $-8\log|\zeta|$ takes every real value. More explicitly, its inverse on $S$ is obtained from $\zeta=\exp(i(W+5\pi)/8)$ and $z=(1+\zeta)/(1-\zeta)$, which verifies both surjectivity and injectivity.

Consequently

\boxed{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{1+n^2}=\pi\coth\pi.}

If the primed frame moves at speed $v$ in the positive $x$ direction relative to the unprimed frame, the one-dimensional <Lorentz transformation> is
$$
\boxed{x'=\gamma_v(x-vt),\qquad t'=\gamma_v(t-vx/c^2),\qquad \gamma_v=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.}
$$
For a trajectory with velocity $q=dx/dt$, differentiating these expressions gives $q'=(q-v)/(1-vq/c^2)$. Solving for $q$, when the particle has primed-frame speed $u$, proves the <relativistic velocity-addition formula>
$$
\boxed{q=\frac{u+v}{1+uv/c^2}.}
$$
For the train problem the sign of the frame velocity reverses: the train frame $S$ moves at $+v$ relative to the station frame $S'$. Put $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. The transformation appropriate to the subsequent parts is therefore
$$
x'=\gamma(x+vt),\qquad t'=\gamma(t+vx/c^2),\qquad x=\gamma(x'-vt'),\qquad t=\gamma(t'-vx'/c^2).
$$

For constant density $\rho$, the inviscid <Euler equations> with conservative <body force> are
$$
\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\nabla(p/\rho+V).
$$
The vector identity $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u)$ simplifies for <potential flow> $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$, since its <vorticity> vanishes. Also $\partial_t\mathbf u=\nabla\phi_t$. Therefore
$$
\nabla\left(\phi_t+\frac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+V\right)=0.
$$
On each connected fluid region the expression is spatially constant but may depend on time, giving the <unsteady Bernoulli equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\frac12u^2+\chi=f(t),\qquad \chi=p/\rho+V.}
$$
The arbitrariness of $f$ corresponds to the freedom to add a function of time to the <velocity potential> without changing $\mathbf u$. Replacing $\phi$ by $\phi-\int^t f(s)\,ds$ sets the right-hand side to zero.

Use the ideal one-dimensional pipe model: constant density, steady inviscid flow, approximately uniform endpoint speeds $u_1,u_2$, and equal body-force potential at the two ends. <Conservation of mass> gives the constant <volumetric flow rate> $q=u_1S_1=u_2S_2$, and the <mass flow rate> is $m=\rho q$. The steady <Bernoulli equation> gives
$$
\Delta p=\frac\rho2(u_2^2-u_1^2)=\frac\rho2q^2\left(\frac1{S_2^2}-\frac1{S_1^2}\right).
$$
For flow from a wider to a narrower section, $S_1>S_2$ and $\Delta p>0$. Solving for the positive mass throughput gives the <Venturi effect> formula
$$
\boxed{m=S_1S_2\sqrt{\frac{2\rho\Delta p}{S_1^2-S_2^2}}.}

The formula requires these hydraulic assumptions; steady incompressibility alone does not impose uniform endpoint profiles or equal potential. Assume the explanatory variables are not all equal, so $S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2>0$. In the <Gaussian linear model>, the <log-likelihood> is
$$
\ell(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2.
$$
For fixed $\sigma^2$, <maximum likelihood estimation> therefore minimizes the <residual sum of squares>. Differentiating in $\alpha,\beta$ gives the <least-squares normal equations>
$$
\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)=0,\qquad\sum_ix_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)=0.
$$
The first gives $\widehat\alpha=\bar Y-\widehat\beta\bar x$; substituting it into the second gives $\widehat\beta=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, with $S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(Y_i-\bar Y)$. Let $\operatorname{SSE}=\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)^2$. Differentiating the profiled <log-likelihood> in $\sigma^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat\beta=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\qquad\widehat\alpha=\bar Y-\widehat\beta\bar x,\qquad\widehat{\sigma^2}=\frac{\operatorname{SSE}}n.}
$$
For the variance estimator this is the interior maximum when $\operatorname{SSE}>0$, which occurs almost surely under the model when $n>2$.

The coefficient estimators are linear functions of the independent normal errors. In particular,
$$
\widehat\beta-\beta=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)\varepsilon_i}{S_{xx}},\qquad\widehat\alpha-\alpha=\bar\varepsilon-\bar x(\widehat\beta-\beta).
$$
Their <multivariate normal distribution> has mean $(\alpha,\beta)^T$ and <covariance matrix>
$$
\sigma^2\begin{pmatrix}1/n+\bar x^2/S_{xx}&-\bar x/S_{xx}\\-\bar x/S_{xx}&1/S_{xx}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The fitted vector is the <orthogonal projection> onto the two-dimensional span of $(1,\ldots,1)^T$ and $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T$. The residual vector is the complementary projection of the spherical normal error vector. Orthogonal normal projections are independent, and in an <orthonormal basis> of that complementary space its $n-2$ coordinates are independent $N(0,\sigma^2)$. Consequently
$$
\frac{\operatorname{SSE}}{\sigma^2}=\frac{n\widehat{\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-2},
$$
independently of $(\widehat\alpha,\widehat\beta)$. This also gives the <unbiased estimator> $s^2=\operatorname{SSE}/(n-2)$ rather than the variance MLE.

Under the <null hypothesis> $\alpha=0$, for $n>2$ the <Student t test for a simple-regression intercept> uses
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{\widehat\alpha}{s\sqrt{1/n+\bar x^2/S_{xx}}}\sim t_{n-2},\qquad \text{reject if }|T|>t_{n-2,1-\eta/2}.}
$$
Here $\eta$ is the prescribed <significance level>. The normal numerator divided by its true standard deviation is independent of $(n-2)s^2/\sigma^2$, which proves the stated <Student t-distribution>. If $S_{xx}=0$, the two coefficients are not separately identifiable; if $n\leq2$, this residual-variance-based test is unavailable.

$$
\boxed{K_* =\frac{\alpha b}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad L_* =\frac{\beta b}{w(\alpha+\beta)}.}

To verify the unique global maximum, maximize the logarithm of the <Cobb–Douglas production function> along the budget line:
$$
\log f=\alpha\log K+\beta\log(b-K)-\beta\log w,\qquad\frac{d^2}{dK^2}\log f=-\frac\alpha{K^2}-\frac\beta{(b-K)^2}<0.

Its unique stationary point is therefore the unique maximum, including comparison with the zero-output boundary points.
