The <equivalence class> of $x\in X$ is $[x]=\{y\in X:y\sim x\}$. By the reflexivity of the <equivalence relation>, $x\in[x]$, so every <equivalence class> is nonempty and their union is $X$.

Suppose $[x]\cap[z]\ne\varnothing$, and choose $w$ in this <intersection>. We have $w\sim x$ and $w\sim z$. Symmetry and transitivity give $x\sim z$. If $y\in[x]$, then $y\sim x\sim z$, so $y\in[z]$; interchanging $x,z$ proves the reverse inclusion. Thus two <equivalence classes> are either identical or disjoint. **The distinct <equivalence classes> therefore form a <set partition> of $X$.** This also covers $X=\varnothing$, whose family of <equivalence classes> is empty.

For positive <integers>, $m/m=2^0$, establishing reflexivity. If $m/n=2^k$, then $n/m=2^{-k}$, establishing symmetry. If also $n/\ell=2^j$, then $m/\ell=2^{k+j}$, establishing transitivity. Hence this is an <equivalence relation>.

Every positive <integer> can be written uniquely as $m=2^r d$, where $r\geq0$ is an <integer> and $d$ is an odd positive <integer>, its <odd part of a positive integer>. Existence follows by repeatedly dividing by two while the result is even; the process terminates because the positive <integers> strictly decrease. For uniqueness, $2^r d=2^s e$ with $d,e$ odd would make one of $d,e$ even if $r\ne s$; therefore $r=s$ and $d=e$.

Multiplying by a power of two preserves the <odd part of a positive integer>, and two positive <integers> with the same odd part have ratio a power of two. The <equivalence class> indexed by odd $d$ is consequently $\{2^r d:r\geq0\}$. **The required representative set is $\boxed{A=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}}$.**

Put $M=m_1+m_2$ and let $\mathbf R=(m_1\mathbf r_1+m_2\mathbf r_2)/M$ be the <center of mass>. Applying <Newton's second law> separately to the two <point masses> gives
$$
m_1\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=\mathbf f,\qquad m_2\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=-\mathbf f,\qquad M\ddot{\mathbf R}=0.
$$
Thus the <center of mass> has constant <velocity>, $\mathbf R(t)=\mathbf R(0)+t\dot{\mathbf R}(0)$. For the relative <position> $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2$, subtraction gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}=\left(\frac1{m_1}+\frac1{m_2}\right)\mathbf f,\qquad \boxed{\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf f},\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.
$$
Here $\mu$ is the <reduced mass>; it converts the relative motion into a one-body <equation of motion>.

For the given linear <force>, the relative <position> obeys <simple harmonic motion>, $\ddot{\mathbf r}+\Omega^2\mathbf r=0$, where $\Omega^2=k/\mu$. The initial rest condition gives
$$
\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r(0)\cos(\Omega t),\qquad |\mathbf r(0)|=d.
$$
For $d>0$, collision first occurs when the relative <position> vanishes, namely when $\Omega t=\pi/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm collision}=\frac\pi2\sqrt{\frac{m_1m_2}{k(m_1+m_2)}}}.
$$
The separation $d$ cancels because the <frequency> of this <simple harmonic motion> is independent of its <amplitude>.

Introduce $v=\dot x$. The <phase plane> system for the <damped pendulum> is
$$
\dot x=v,\qquad \dot v=-2kv-\omega^2\sin x.
$$
At an <equilibrium point>, $v=0$ and $\sin x=0$, so all <equilibrium points> are $\boxed{(x,v)=(n\pi,0),\ n\in\mathbb Z}$. Angles identified modulo $2\pi$ give one downward and one upward <equilibrium point>.

The <linearization of a dynamical system> at $(n\pi,0)$ has <Jacobian matrix> and <eigenvalues>
$$
J_n=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-\omega^2(-1)^n&-2k\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_\pm=-k\pm\sqrt{k^2-\omega^2(-1)^n}.
$$
When $n$ is even and $k>\omega$, both <eigenvalues> are real and strictly negative, since $0<\sqrt{k^2-\omega^2}<k$. These downward <equilibrium points> are **<stable nodes>**. When $n$ is even and $k<\omega$, the <eigenvalues> are $-k\pm i\sqrt{\omega^2-k^2}$, so the downward <equilibrium points> are **<stable foci>**, approached with decaying oscillations.

When $n$ is odd, $\sqrt{k^2+\omega^2}>k$, so one <eigenvalue> is positive and one is negative in either parameter range. Every upward <equilibrium point> is consequently an **unstable <saddle equilibrium>**. All these <equilibrium points> are <hyperbolic equilibria> in the two requested cases, so their <linearization of a dynamical system> gives their local nonlinear classification.

A <set> is countable if it admits an <injection> into the <natural numbers>; this includes <finite sets> and the empty <set>. Use $\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ for the enumeration. The <Cantor pairing function>
$$
\Pi(i,j)=\frac{(i+j)(i+j+1)}2+j
$$
is a <bijection> $\mathbb N_0^2\to\mathbb N_0$. Indeed, on the diagonal $i+j=s$, its values are the consecutive <integers> from $s(s+1)/2$ through $s(s+1)/2+s$. These intervals are disjoint, and the next starts one beyond the preceding endpoint. Every nonnegative <integer> therefore determines exactly one diagonal $s$, then exactly one $j$, and finally $i=s-j$. If one's convention starts the <natural numbers> at one, translate both coordinates by one. Thus **$\mathbb N\times\mathbb N$ is a <countable set>**.

For <countable sets> $X,Y$, choose <injections> $e_X:X\to\mathbb N_0$ and $e_Y:Y\to\mathbb N_0$. The map $(x,y)\mapsto\Pi(e_X(x),e_Y(y))$ is an <injection>, proving their <Cartesian product> is countable. Repeating this proves that every <finite Cartesian power of a countable set> is countable.

For a sequence of <countable sets> $X_n$, choose an <injection> $e_n:X_n\to\mathbb N_0$ for each $n$. For $x\in\bigcup_nX_n$, let $n(x)$ be the least index containing $x$. The map
$$
x\longmapsto\Pi\bigl(n(x),e_{n(x)}(x)\bigr)
$$
is an <injection>: its value recovers $n(x)$ and then $x$. This proves the **<countable union of countable sets> theorem**. Selecting all $e_n$ uses the usual <axiom of countable choice>; if enumerations are already supplied, the construction itself needs no additional choices.

For a <countable set> $A$, the <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $d$ with coefficients in $A$ are described by coefficient tuples in $A^{d+1}$. The <finite Cartesian power of a countable set> result makes this a <countable set>. Taking the union over $d\geq0$ shows there are only countably many such <polynomials> altogether; restricting to the nonzero <polynomials> preserves countability.

A nonzero <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> $d$ has at most $d$ distinct <roots of a polynomial>. To see the bound, a root $r$ gives the factorization $P(x)=(x-r)Q(x)$ by the <factor theorem>, and each other root is a root of $Q$, whose degree is $d-1$; induction starts with a nonzero constant, which has no roots. Thus $\phi(A)$ is a <countable union of countable sets>, each of them finite, and is a <countable set>.

Starting with the given <countable set> $A_0$, <mathematical induction> now shows that every $A_n=\phi(A_{n-1})$ is countable. A final application of the <countable union of countable sets> theorem gives
$$
\boxed{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\text{ is countable}.}
$$
This countability argument does not require that the sequence of <sets> be increasing.

To obtain a <countable closure under real polynomial roots>, start with $B_0=\{1,\pi\}$ and explicitly retain previous elements at each stage:
$$
B_{n+1}=B_n\cup\phi(B_n),\qquad X=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}B_n.
$$

For a nonzero <polynomial> $P(t)=c_0+\cdots+c_dt^d$ with all $c_j\in X$, choose stages $n_j$ containing the finitely many coefficients and put $N=\max_jn_j$. The inclusions $B_n\subseteq B_{n+1}$ ensure that every coefficient belongs to $B_N$. Every real <root of a polynomial> $P$ therefore belongs to $\phi(B_N)\subseteq B_{N+1}\subseteq X$. **This <countable set> $X$ has all the required properties.** Retaining $B_n$ explicitly avoids assuming the generally false inclusion $A\subseteq\phi(A)$: for example, $\phi(\{1\})$ does not contain $1$, since a nonzero <polynomial> all of whose coefficients are one is positive at $1$.

Let $d=\gcd(a,m)$. If the <linear congruence> $ar\equiv b\pmod m$ holds, then $b=ar-ms$ for some <integer> $s$, so the <greatest common divisor> $d$ divides $b$. Conversely, <Bézout's identity> gives <integers> $u,v$ with $au+mv=d$. If $b=dj$, multiply this identity by $j$ to obtain $a(uj)\equiv b\pmod m$. Hence
$$
\boxed{ar\equiv b\pmod m\text{ has a solution}\iff d\mid b.}
$$
For the number of solutions, write $a=da'$, $m=dm'$ and $b=db'$. The <linear congruence> becomes $a'r\equiv b'\pmod {m'}$, where $a',m'$ are <coprime integers>. Multiplication by $a'$ is invertible modulo $m'$ by <Bézout's identity>, so the solutions form one <residue class> $r\equiv r_0\pmod {m'}$. Modulo $m=dm'$, these are exactly
$$
\boxed{r_0,\ r_0+m',\ldots,r_0+(d-1)m'.}
$$
They are distinct: if two have the same <residue class> modulo $m$, then $dm'$ divides $(j-\ell)m'$, forcing $d\mid j-\ell$; with $0\leq j,\ell<d$, this forces $j=\ell$. Every other solution differs from $r_0$ by a multiple of $m'$, so these exhaust the $d$ solutions.

Finally,
$$
b(m/d)\equiv0\pmod m\iff dm'\mid bm'\iff d\mid b.
$$
Combining this with the existence criterion gives the requested equivalent <modular congruence>.

Take a generator $u$ of the <multiplicative group of a finite field> $\mathbb F_p^\times$, which has order $p-1$. Write $x=u^a$. Since any possible nonzero base is $y=u^r$, the condition of being a $k$th power is
$$
x=y^k\iff u^a=u^{kr}\iff kr\equiv a\pmod {p-1}.

By part (a), this <linear congruence> is soluble exactly when $d=\gcd(k,p-1)$ divides $a$. Meanwhile
$$
x^{(p-1)/d}=1\iff u^{a(p-1)/d}=1\iff (p-1)\mid a(p-1)/d\iff d\mid a.
$$
Thus the required test is
$$
\boxed{x\text{ is a }k\text{th power in }\mathbb F_p^\times\iff x^{(p-1)/d}\equiv1\pmod p.}
$$
The exponent <residue classes> divisible by $d$ are $0,d,\ldots,p-1-d$, so there are exactly $(p-1)/d$ distinct nonzero $k$th powers. Equivalently, part (a) shows each value has exactly $d$ preimages under the power map.

Suppose $x^2+y^2=0$ in the <field> $F$. If $y\ne0$, division by $y^2$ gives $(x/y)^2=-1$, contrary to the hypothesis. Hence $y=0$ and then $x^2=0$; the absence of nonzero <zero divisors> in a <field> gives $x=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x^2+y^2=0\Longrightarrow x=y=0.}
$$
For the stated operations on $F^2$, addition is an <abelian group> operation: <associativity> and <commutativity> hold componentwise, its identity is $(0,0)$, and the negative of $(x,y)$ is $(-x,-y)$.

To check multiplication without omitting associativity, associate to each pair the <matrix>
$$
M(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x&-y\\y&x\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This is an <injection> into the two-by-two <matrices> over $F$, and direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
M(x,y)M(z,w)=M(xz-yw,xw+yz).
$$
It also preserves addition. The <associativity> and distributivity of <matrix multiplication> therefore imply the same laws for the pair multiplication. Its formula is symmetric in the pairs, so it is commutative. Its identity is $(1,0)$, which is different from $(0,0)$.

For a nonzero pair, the first result shows that $x^2+y^2\ne0$. Its multiplicative inverse is
$$
\boxed{(x,y)^{-1}=\left(\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\right),}
$$
since multiplying $(x,y)$ by $(x,-y)$ gives $(x^2+y^2,0)$. This verifies every <field> axiom, so **the given operations make $F^2$ a <field>**. The embedded copy of $F$ is $\{(x,0):x\in F\}$; the element $(0,1)$ has square $(-1,0)$, describing a <quadratic extension> of $F$.

The key <telescoping series> identity is
$$
c_k=\frac{(q+k-1)q!}{(q+k)!}=\frac{q!}{(q+k-1)!}-\frac{q!}{(q+k)!}.
$$
For the requested <mathematical induction>, when $n=1$ the proposed sum is $q/(q+1)=1-q!/(q+1)!$. If the identity holds for $n$, adding $c_{n+1}=q!/(q+n)!-q!/(q+n+1)!$ cancels its last term and yields $1-q!/(q+n+1)!$. Thus it holds for every $n\geq1$. Since $q!/(q+n)!\to0$ for fixed positive <integer> $q$, the infinite <series> has sum $\boxed{1}$.

For the second <series>, the integral digit condition is $0\leq a_n\leq n-1$, and in particular $a_1=0$. Its partial sums are increasing and satisfy
$$
0\leq\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{a_n}{n!}\leq\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{n-1}{n!}=1-\frac1{N!}\leq1.
$$
Thus the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> proves convergence to a <real number> $S\in[0,1]$. The same telescoping calculation gives, for every $N\geq1$,
$$
\sum_{n>N}\frac{n-1}{n!}=\frac1{N!},\qquad R_N:=N!\left(S-\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{a_n}{n!}\right)\in[0,1].
$$
If positive digits occur infinitely often, then every tail contains a positive term, so $R_N>0$. If $a_n\leq n-2$ occurs infinitely often, then every tail contains a digit smaller than its maximum. Indeed,
$$
1-R_N=N!\sum_{n>N}\frac{n-1-a_n}{n!}>0.
$$
Consequently the two conditions together give $0<R_N<1$ for every $N$. Were $S=A/B$ a <rational number>, with positive <integer> $B$, choose $N\geq B$. Because $B\mid N!$, the number $N!S$ is an <integer>, as is $\sum_{n=1}^Na_nN!/n!$. Their difference $R_N$ would be an <integer> strictly between zero and one, a contradiction.

Conversely, if positive digits occur only finitely often, then $S$ is a finite sum of <rational numbers>. If digits below their maximum occur only finitely often, then $a_n=n-1$ for every $n>N$ for some $N$, giving
$$
S=\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{a_n}{n!}+\frac1{N!}\in\mathbb Q.
$$
These are exactly the two ways the conjunction of infinitude conditions can fail. Hence the <irrationality criterion for factorial series> is proved in both directions: **$S$ is irrational exactly when both kinds of digit occur infinitely often.**

During ejection, the remaining <mass> is $m(t)=m_o+m_w-Qt$, and the water is exhausted at $t_c=m_w/Q$. Consider the <momentum> of the octopus and the small portion of water expelled during $dt$. Initially it is $mu$. At the end of this interval, the remaining body has <mass> $m-Q\,dt$ and <velocity> $u+du$, while the expelled <mass> $Q\,dt$ has laboratory <velocity> $u-V$, to first order. Hence the total final <momentum> is
$$
(m-Q\,dt)(u+du)+Q\,dt(u-V)=mu+m\,du-QV\,dt+o(dt).
$$
The external <impulse> from <quadratic drag> is $-\alpha u^2dt$. Equating this to the change in total <momentum>, and dividing by $dt$, proves the <equation of motion>
$$
\boxed{m\frac{du}{dt}=QV-\alpha u^2.}
$$
The outgoing water's <momentum> flux is essential: applying $d(mu)/dt$ to the remaining body alone would omit it.

For <dimensional analysis>, the independent physical inputs are $m_o,m_w,Q,V,\alpha$, with dimensions
$$
[m_o]=[m_w]=\mathsf M,\quad[Q]=\mathsf M\mathsf T^{-1},\quad[V]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1},\quad[\alpha]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-1}.
$$
The scales $m_o$, $m_o/Q$ and $Vm_o/Q$ fix units of <mass>, time and length. In these units, the only remaining independent <dimensionless variables> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{m_w}{m_o},\qquad\mu=\frac{\alpha V}{Q}.}
$$
For example, with $s=Qt/m_o$ and $w=u/V$, the <equation of motion> becomes $(1+\lambda-s)\,dw/ds=1-\mu w^2$, with $w(0)=0$ and ejection ending at $s=\lambda$. Thus the dimensionless terminal value depends only on $\lambda,\mu$, proving $u_c=Vf(\lambda,\mu)$.

For its explicit value, put $m_0=m_o+m_w$ and $U=\sqrt{QV/\alpha}$. The initially stationary solution stays below $U$, since the <acceleration> vanishes at $U$ and is positive below it. <Separation of variables> gives
$$
\int_0^{u(t)}\frac{dv}{QV-\alpha v^2}=\int_0^t\frac{d\tau}{m_0-Q\tau}.
$$
The two integrals yield
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha QV}}\operatorname{artanh}\frac{u(t)}U=\frac1Q\log\frac{m_0}{m_0-Qt}.
$$
This is <constant mass-loss propulsion with quadratic drag>. At $t_c$, the remaining <mass> is $m_o$, giving
$$
\boxed{u_c=\sqrt{\frac{QV}{\alpha}}\tanh\left(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha V}{Q}}\log\frac{m_o+m_w}{m_o}\right)
=\frac{V}{\sqrt\mu}\tanh\bigl(\sqrt\mu\log(1+\lambda)\bigr).}
$$
Thus $f(\lambda,\mu)=\mu^{-1/2}\tanh(\sqrt\mu\log(1+\lambda))$, verifying the required dimensional form. The limit as drag tends to zero is $V\log(1+\lambda)$, the ordinary <rocket equation>, while the positive-drag answer is below the <terminal velocity> $U$.

Multiplying the tangential <equation of motion> by $r$ gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta=0.
$$
Therefore $h=r^2\dot\theta$ is constant. It is the signed <specific angular momentum>; reverse the angular orientation if necessary to take $h>0$ for a nonradial <orbit>. Set $u=1/r$ and denote differentiation with respect to $\theta$ by a prime. Then
$$
\dot\theta=hu^2,\qquad \dot r=-hu',\qquad \ddot r=-h^2u^2u'',\qquad r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3.
$$
Substitution into the radial <equation of motion> and division by $-h^2u^2$ give the <Binet equation>
$$
u''+u=\frac{GM}{h^2}.
$$
Its general solution is $u=GM/h^2+A\cos\theta+B\sin\theta$. Write $A=(GM/h^2)e\cos\theta_0$ and $B=(GM/h^2)e\sin\theta_0$, where $e\geq0$. This produces the <Kepler orbit>
$$
\boxed{\frac{h^2u}{GM}=1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_0).}
$$
Here $e$ is the <orbital eccentricity>, and $\theta_0$ points toward <periapsis> when $e>0$. Only portions with $u>0$ describe positive radial distances. The expression assumes $h\ne0$; a zero-<angular momentum> trajectory is radial and must be treated separately.

= Solution
{parent=10B}

At infinity, the meteorite's incoming asymptote has <speed> $V$ and perpendicular distance $b$ from the Earth’s centre. The magnitude of its <specific angular momentum> is the magnitude of $\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$. On the asymptote, this is $bV$, so <conservation of angular momentum> gives $\boxed{h=bV}$. Its <mechanical energy> at infinity is $E=mV^2/2$. Comparing with part (ii) gives
$$
\boxed{e=\sqrt{1+\frac{b^2V^4}{G^2M^2}}}.
$$
For $b>0$ this <Kepler orbit> is a <hyperbola>. The smallest $r=1/u$ occurs at <periapsis>, where $\cos(\theta-\theta_0)=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{r_{\min}=\frac{b^2V^2}{GM(1+e)}=\frac{GM}{V^2}(e-1)
=\frac{\sqrt{G^2M^2+b^2V^4}-GM}{V^2}.}
$$
The middle equality follows by substituting $e^2-1=b^2V^4/(G^2M^2)$. A trajectory entering the Earth satisfies $r_{\min}<R$, equivalently
$$
e<1+\frac{RV^2}{GM}\iff b^2<R^2+\frac{2GMR}{V^2}.
$$
Therefore the sufficient collision condition is
$$
\boxed{b<\sqrt{R^2+\frac{2GMR}{V^2}}.}
$$
This is <gravitational focusing>: <Newtonian gravity> increases the collision radius measured at infinity. Equality is the ideal grazing trajectory, which touches the surface; a strict inequality penetrates it. For $b=0$ the incoming motion is radial and collides, with $r_{\min}=0$ as the limiting value of the above expression.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $\mathbf e'_1(t),\mathbf e'_2(t),\mathbf e'_3(t)$ be an <orthonormal basis> fixed in the <rotating reference frame> $S'$. A small rotation through $\boldsymbol\omega\,dt$ changes each basis vector by $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e'_j\,dt$, so its derivative in the <inertial frame> is
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf e'_j}{dt}\right)_S=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e'_j.
$$
Write the arbitrary <vector> as $\mathbf a=\sum_j a_j(t)\mathbf e'_j(t)$. Differentiating the components and the moving basis separately gives
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_S=\sum_j\dot a_j\mathbf e'_j+\sum_j a_j\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e'_j
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{S'}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf a.
$$
This proves the **<rotating-frame derivative formula>**, including when the <angular velocity> varies with time.

{parent=11b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use $D=(d/dt)_S$, $D'=(d/dt)_{S'}$, and $\mathbf v'=D'\mathbf r$. By the <rotating-frame derivative formula>, $D\mathbf r=\mathbf v'+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$. Apply the <product rule> for the <cross product> and the same <rotating-frame derivative formula> again:
$$
D^2\mathbf r=D\mathbf v'+(D\boldsymbol\omega)\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\omega\times D\mathbf r.
$$
We have $D\mathbf v'=D'\mathbf v'+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v'$ and $D\boldsymbol\omega=D'\boldsymbol\omega$, since $\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\omega=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D^2\mathbf r=D'^2\mathbf r+2\boldsymbol\omega\times D'\mathbf r+(D'\boldsymbol\omega)\times\mathbf r+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).}
$$
Rearranging this identity into an <equation of motion in a rotating frame> produces the apparent <Coriolis acceleration>, <Euler acceleration> and <centrifugal acceleration>, with signs opposite to their corresponding terms on the right of this inertial <acceleration> identity. No translational term is needed because the two origins coincide.

= Solution
{parent=11B}

Let $\Omega$ be the Earth's <angular velocity> magnitude. Use the local east, north and upward unit <vectors> $\mathbf e_E,\mathbf e_N,\mathbf e_U$, with $\mathbf e_E\times\mathbf e_N=\mathbf e_U$. At latitude $\lambda$, the Earth's <angular velocity> and the train's ground-relative <velocity> are
$$
\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\cos\lambda\,\mathbf e_N+\Omega\sin\lambda\,\mathbf e_U,\qquad \mathbf v'=V\mathbf e_N.
$$
Thus the east component of the <Coriolis force> is
$$
-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'=2m\Omega V\sin\lambda\,\mathbf e_E.
$$
For a north–south track, the train has no eastward <acceleration> relative to Earth. Its path curvature lies in the local north–vertical plane. The <centrifugal acceleration> and <Newtonian gravity> also lie in that plane, and the Earth's constant <angular velocity> gives no <Euler acceleration>. Hence the sideways <force> from the track must cancel the eastward <Coriolis force>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm track,side}=-2m\Omega V\sin\lambda\,\mathbf e_E.}
$$
**Its magnitude is $2m\Omega V\sin\lambda$, and its direction is west** in the Northern hemisphere. The eastward tendency is the apparent <Coriolis force>; the requested track <force> has the opposite direction.

= Solution
{parent=12B}

Let $\rho=3m/(4\pi R_0^3)$ be the uniform <mass density>. In <spherical coordinates> $(r,\vartheta,\phi)$ with the chosen diameter as polar axis, the squared perpendicular distance to that axis is $r^2\sin^2\vartheta$. The <moment of inertia> is therefore
$$
I=\rho\int_0^{R_0}r^4\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin^3\vartheta\,d\vartheta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi
=\frac{8\pi\rho R_0^5}{15}=\boxed{\frac25mR_0^2}.
$$
This derives the <moment of inertia of a uniform solid sphere> about any central axis by <spherical symmetry>.

Put $a=R_1-R_0$. The <center of mass> follows the circle of radius $a$, with <position> $\mathbf r_C=a(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)$. Let $\mathbf e_r=(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)$ and $\mathbf e_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Its <velocity> is $a\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta$, and the contact point is displaced from the <center of mass> by $R_0\mathbf e_r$. With positive spin <angular velocity> $\Omega_s$ counterclockwise, its contact-point <velocity> is
$$
\mathbf v_{\rm contact}=a\dot\theta\mathbf e_\theta+\Omega_s\mathbf e_z\times R_0\mathbf e_r
=(a\dot\theta+R_0\Omega_s)\mathbf e_\theta.
$$
The cylinder is fixed, so <rolling without slipping> requires this <velocity> to vanish. Consequently $\Omega_s=-a\dot\theta/R_0$. If instead the spin angle $\psi$ is positive clockwise, opposite to increasing $\theta$, then the printed positive-coefficient convention is
$$
\boxed{\dot\psi=\frac{R_1-R_0}{R_0}\dot\theta.}
$$
The spin and orbital rotation have opposite directions when both are measured about the same oriented axis; the <angular speed> is $a|\dot\theta|/R_0$. This specifies the sign convention in the printed expression.

The instantaneous contact point is at rest, so the <normal reaction> and <static friction> do no work. <Conservation of energy> equates the loss of <gravitational potential energy> from release to the total <kinetic energy>:
$$
mg a(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha)=\frac12ma^2\dot\theta^2+\frac12I\left(\frac a{R_0}\dot\theta\right)^2
=\frac7{10}ma^2\dot\theta^2.
$$
On the descent from $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, $\theta$ decreases, so
$$
\dot\theta=-\sqrt{\frac{10g}{7a}(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha)}.
$$
Integrating $dt=-d\theta/|\dot\theta|$ from release to the bottom gives
$$
\boxed{T_R=\sqrt{\frac{7(R_1-R_0)}{10g}}\int_0^\alpha\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\alpha}}.}
$$
The integral is finite: at the release point its denominator is asymptotic to $\sqrt{\sin\alpha\,(\alpha-\theta)}$. Contact is maintained, since the inward radial <equation of motion> gives the positive <normal reaction> $N=m(g\cos\theta+a\dot\theta^2)$ throughout this range.

For frictionless sliding, the <normal reaction> passes through the sphere's centre and produces no <torque>; <Newtonian gravity> also exerts no central <torque>. With initial zero spin, the sphere remains nonrotating. All the lost <gravitational potential energy> becomes translational <kinetic energy>, so
$$
\frac12ma^2\dot\theta^2=mga(\cos\theta-\cos\alpha),\qquad
\boxed{T_S=\sqrt{\frac{R_1-R_0}{2g}}\int_0^\alpha\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\alpha}}=\sqrt{\frac57}\,T_R.}
$$
A hollow spherical shell of the same <mass> and radius places its material farther from the centre, giving a larger <moment of inertia>. Under <rolling without slipping>, a given centre <speed> therefore requires more rotational <kinetic energy>; the same gravitational drop yields a smaller <speed>. **Replacing the solid sphere by a hollow shell increases $T_R$ and leaves $T_S$ unchanged.** The sliding case remains nonrotating and has the same <center of mass> path and gravitational drop. This is the general comparison for <rolling inside a fixed circular cylinder>.
