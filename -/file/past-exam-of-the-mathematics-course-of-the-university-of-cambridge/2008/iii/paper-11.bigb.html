A proper <filter on a set> $X$ is a nonempty family $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal P(X)$ that excludes $\varnothing$, is closed under finite <intersections>, and is upward closed under inclusion. In particular $X\in\mathcal F$. In a <topological space>, <convergence of a filter> $\mathcal F\to x$ means that every <neighbourhood> of $x$ belongs to $\mathcal F$. An <ultrafilter> is a proper <filter> maximal under inclusion; equivalently, it contains exactly one of $A$ and $X\setminus A$ for every subset $A\subseteq X$.

To justify the equivalence, if $A\notin\mathcal U$ and every member of $\mathcal U$ met $A$, adjoining $A$ would generate a larger proper <filter>. Maximality therefore supplies $F\in\mathcal U$ with $F\cap A=\varnothing$, and upward closure gives $X\setminus A\in\mathcal U$. Both complementary sets cannot belong to a proper <filter>. Conversely, a <filter> with this dichotomy cannot acquire any new set without also containing its complement, which would give an empty <intersection>.

The <ultrafilter characterization of compact Hausdorff spaces>, stated without proof, is: **a <topological space> is <compact> and <Hausdorff> exactly when every <ultrafilter> on it converges to a unique point.**

For a finite <set partition> $X=A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_n$, if no cell belonged to $\mathcal U$, the <ultrafilter> dichotomy would put all $X\setminus A_j$ in $\mathcal U$. Their finite <intersection> is empty, a contradiction. Thus some $A_j\in\mathcal U$, and at most one cell can belong because distinct cells are disjoint. The converse is: **a proper <filter on a set> meeting every finite partition by containing one cell is an <ultrafilter>.** Apply the partition hypothesis to $X=A\cup(X\setminus A)$ to obtain the dichotomy, then the maximality argument above. Empty cells can be omitted, and the cases $A=\varnothing$ or $A=X$ are automatic.

For a <function> $f:X\to Y$, the <image of a filter> is
$$
f_*\mathcal F=\{B\subseteq Y:f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal F\}.
$$
Since <preimages> preserve inclusions and finite <intersections>, this is a proper <filter on a set> $Y$. Equivalently, it is the <filter> generated by $f(F)$ for $F\in\mathcal F$: if $f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal F$, then its image is contained in $B$, while $f(F)\subseteq B$ implies $F\subseteq f^{-1}(B)$. For an <ultrafilter> $\mathcal U$, the identity $f^{-1}(Y\setminus B)=X\setminus f^{-1}(B)$ shows that $f_*\mathcal U$ contains exactly one of $B,Y\setminus B$. Therefore **the <pushforward filter> of an <ultrafilter> is an <ultrafilter>**.

Now let $x\in\ell^\infty$, the <l-infinity sequence space>, and put $a_x(n)=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^nx_j$. All these <Cesaro means> lie in the closed disk $K_x=\{z:|z|\leq\|x\|_\infty\}$, a <compact Hausdorff space>. The <pushforward filter> $a_{x*}\mathcal U$ contains $K_x$; restricting it to that disk and applying the <ultrafilter> theorem gives a unique limit
$$
\boxed{l(x)=\lim_{n\to\mathcal U}a_x(n).}
$$
For real sequences the disk can be replaced by a closed interval. This is the <ultrafilter construction of a Banach limit>.

The identities $a_{\alpha x+\beta y}(n)=\alpha a_x(n)+\beta a_y(n)$ give linearity of $l$. Explicitly, the two sets on which $|a_x(n)-l(x)|<\delta$ and $|a_y(n)-l(y)|<\delta$ both belong to $\mathcal U$, hence so does their <intersection>. On that <intersection> the difference from $\alpha l(x)+\beta l(y)$ is at most $(|\alpha|+|\beta|)\delta$, proving $l(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha l(x)+\beta l(y)$. If $x_n\geq0$, its means lie in $[0,\|x\|_\infty]$, so $l(x)\geq0$. Thus $l$ is a <positive linear functional>. The bound $|l(x)|\leq\|x\|_\infty$ gives $\|l\|\leq1$, while the constant-one sequence satisfies $l(\mathbf1)=1$. Hence $\boxed{\|l\|=1}$.

Because $\mathcal U$ contains no <finite sets>, its dichotomy puts every cofinite set in $\mathcal U$. If $x_n\to L$ ordinarily, the <Cesaro theorem for convergent sequences> follows directly by splitting the sum: for a fixed $N$ with $|x_j-L|<\varepsilon$ for $j>N$,
$$
|a_x(n)-L|\leq\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^N|x_j-L|+\varepsilon\quad(n>N).
$$
The finite head tends to zero, so $a_x(n)\to L$ ordinarily. Every sufficiently long tail belongs to $\mathcal U$, making its <ultralimit> also $L$. Thus $\boxed{x_n\to L\Longrightarrow l(x)=L}$.

Finally, for the <left shift on bounded sequences> $S$,
$$
a_{Sx}(n)-a_x(n)=\frac{x_{n+1}-x_1}{n},\qquad |a_{Sx}(n)-a_x(n)|\leq\frac{2\|x\|_\infty}{n}\to0.
$$
Taking <ultralimits> along the same cofinite-containing <ultrafilter> gives $\boxed{l(Sx)=l(x)}$. This establishes every defining property of a <Banach limit>.

We construct the density and singular part, rather than assuming the desired <Lebesgue decomposition theorem>. Put $\rho=\mu+\nu$, a finite <dominating measure>, and use the real <Hilbert space> $L^2(\rho)$. The <linear functional>
$$
T(g)=\int_\Omega g\,d\nu
$$
is well defined on its equivalence classes, since a $\rho$-null set is $\nu$-null. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and $\nu\leq\rho$ give
$$
|T(g)|\leq\nu(\Omega)^{1/2}\left(\int g^2\,d\nu\right)^{1/2}\leq\nu(\Omega)^{1/2}\|g\|_{L^2(\rho)}.
$$
By the <Riesz representation theorem>, there is $h\in L^2(\rho)$ with $T(g)=\int gh\,d\rho$. Indicators of all measurable sets belong to this Hilbert space, so
$$
\nu(A)=\int_Ah\,d\rho,\qquad \mu(A)=\int_A(1-h)\,d\rho\quad(A\in\Sigma).
$$
The nonnegativity of these two <measures> forces $0\leq h\leq1$ almost everywhere. For instance, if $\rho(\{h\leq-1/n\})>0$, its $\nu$-<measure> would be negative; the analogous sets $\{h\geq1+1/n\}$ contradict positivity of $\mu$. Their countable union excludes every violation. Modify $h$ on its measurable $\rho$-null exceptional set so that these inequalities hold everywhere. This is the <Hilbert-space construction of dominated measure densities>.

Define
$$
B=\{h=1\},\qquad f=\begin{cases}h/(1-h),&h<1,\\0,&h=1.\end{cases}
$$
Then $B\in\Sigma$, $f$ is a nonnegative <measurable function>, and $\mu(B)=\int_B(1-h)\,d\rho=0$. The identity for integration against $\mu$ follows first for indicators, then for nonnegative simple functions and finally for all nonnegative functions by the <monotone convergence theorem>. Applying it to $f\mathbf1_A$ gives
$$
\int_Af\,d\mu=\int_{A\setminus B}h\,d\rho=\nu(A\setminus B).
$$
In particular $\int_\Omega f\,d\mu=\nu(\Omega\setminus B)<\infty$, so $f\in L^1(\mu)$. Additivity on the disjoint measurable sets $A\setminus B$ and $A\cap B$ now gives
$$
\boxed{\nu(A)=\int_A f\,d\mu+\nu(A\cap B),\qquad \mu(B)=0.}
$$
This is the <Lebesgue decomposition from a sum-measure density>: the first <measure> has a density, and the second is concentrated on a $\mu$-<null set>, so is <mutually singular> with $\mu$.

The <absolute continuity of measures> condition $\nu\ll\mu$ means that $\mu(A)=0$ implies $\nu(A)=0$ for every $A\in\Sigma$. If this holds, then $\nu(B)=0$, eliminating the singular part above. Conversely, a <measure> of the form $\nu(A)=\int_Af\,d\mu$ vanishes on every $\mu$-null set. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\nu\ll\mu\iff\nu(A)=\int_Af\,d\mu\text{ for some nonnegative }f\in L^1(\mu).}
$$
Thus absolute continuity is exactly the absence of the singular part in this construction.

Give $C(G)$, the <space of continuous functions on a compact space>, its <uniform norm>. The <left regular action> is $l_gf(x)=f(g^{-1}x)$. The group law gives $l_gl_h=l_{gh}$ and $l_e=I$, and each translation is a linear <isometry> because $x\mapsto g^{-1}x$ is a bijection of $G$.

For fixed $f\in C(G)$, the map $(g,x)\mapsto f(g^{-1}x)$ is continuous. Compactness of the $x$-space makes this dependence uniform: for a given $g_0$ and $\varepsilon>0$, continuity gives a <neighbourhood> of $g_0$ and a <neighbourhood> of each $x$ on which the difference from $f(g_0^{-1}x)$ is less than $\varepsilon$; a finite cover of $G$ permits one common <neighbourhood> of $g_0$. Hence $\|l_gf-l_{g_0}f\|_\infty\to0$. The estimate
$$
\|l_gf-l_{g_0}f_0\|_\infty\leq\|f-f_0\|_\infty+\|l_gf_0-l_{g_0}f_0\|_\infty
$$
then proves the joint continuity of the action on $G\times C(G)$.

For a left action on the <continuous dual space>, use the contragredient convention
$$
T_g\phi=\phi\circ l_{g^{-1}}.
$$
It satisfies $T_gT_h=T_{gh}$ and preserves the dual <unit ball> $B'$. If the printed prime denotes the literal transpose $l_g^*\phi=\phi\circ l_g$, these maps instead satisfy $l_g^*l_h^*=l_{hg}^*$ and form a right action; replacing $g$ by $g^{-1}$ gives the left action above. Their invariant functionals are the same.

For $g_i\to g$ and $\phi_i\to\phi$ in the <weak-star topology> on $B'$, every $f\in C(G)$ satisfies
$$
|(T_{g_i}\phi_i)(f)-(T_g\phi)(f)|\leq\|l_{g_i^{-1}}f-l_{g^{-1}}f\|_\infty+|(\phi_i-\phi)(l_{g^{-1}}f)|\longrightarrow0.
$$
The first term uses strong continuity of translation and $\|\phi_i\|\leq1$; the second uses weak-star convergence at one fixed function. This proves the requested joint <weak-star topology> continuity.

To obtain <Haar measure>, consider the set $K$ of <positive linear functionals> $\phi$ on $C(G)$ with $\phi(1)=1$. Such functionals have <norm> one: for complex $f$, choose a phase making $\phi(f)$ real and nonnegative and use $\operatorname{Re}(e^{it}f)\leq\|f\|_\infty$; this gives $|\phi(f)|\leq\|f\|_\infty$, with equality on the constant-one function. Thus $K\subseteq B'$. Evaluation at the identity belongs to $K$, and positivity and normalization are weak-star closed conditions. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> makes $K$ a nonempty compact convex set. Each $T_g$ is a continuous affine self-map of it.

Here is the fixed-point step, including why no <commutativity> assumption on $G$ is needed. For any finite list $g_1,\ldots,g_n$, the <Schauder-Tychonoff fixed-point theorem> gives a fixed point $\phi\in K$ of $n^{-1}\sum_jT_{g_j}$. Let $H$ be the closed subgroup they generate and, for real $f\in C(G)$, set $h(x)=\phi(l_xf)$ on $H$. It is continuous and satisfies
$$
h(x)=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nh(g_j^{-1}x).
$$
At a maximum point $x_0$, every summand must have the same maximum value. Iteration gives that value at all inverse-generator words times $x_0$. The closure of their positive-word monoid is a <closed subsemigroup of a compact group>, hence is the entire generated subgroup $H$. Density and continuity make $h$ constant on $H$. Therefore $\phi$ is fixed by each of $T_{g_1},\ldots,T_{g_n}$, first on real functions and then on complex functions by linearity. The closed fixed-point sets consequently have the <finite intersection property> in $K$, so compactness gives a common fixed functional for every $g\in G$.

The <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> converts this positive functional into a regular Borel <probability measure> $\mu_l$ with $\phi(f)=\int f\,d\mu_l$. Its invariance gives left translation invariance of the <measure>. It has full support: if a nonempty open set had <measure> zero, a finite collection of its left translates would cover the compact group and force $\mu_l(G)=0$, contradicting normalization. Thus it is a normalized left <Haar measure>. The right translation action gives a normalized right <Haar measure> $\mu_r$ by the same argument.

For uniqueness and equality, take any $f\in C(G)$ and apply the <Fubini theorem> to
$$
I=\int_G\int_G f(yx)\,d\mu_l(x)\,d\mu_r(y).
$$
For fixed $y$, left invariance of $\mu_l$ makes the inner integral $\int f\,d\mu_l$. Reversing the order, for fixed $x$, right invariance of $\mu_r$ makes the inner integral $\int f\,d\mu_r$. Since both total masses are one,
$$
\int f\,d\mu_l=I=\int f\,d\mu_r.
$$
Uniqueness in the <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> gives $\boxed{\mu_l=\mu_r}$. Comparing any normalized left <Haar measure> to this fixed right <Haar measure> proves uniqueness as well. **The conclusion is uniqueness of normalized <Haar measure>.** Without normalization, the printed uniqueness claim is false: even on the one-point group, $\delta_e$ and $2\delta_e$ are distinct Haar <measures>. In general the result is uniqueness up to a positive scalar, and left and right probability normalizations coincide.

Take a nonzero complex unital <Banach algebra>, with identity $1$. The <spectrum of an element> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(a)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb C:\lambda1-a\text{ is not invertible in }A\}.}
$$
The standard <Banach algebra spectrum> theorem states that it is a nonempty compact subset of $\mathbb C$, contained in the disk $|\lambda|\leq\|a\|$. Conversely, every nonempty compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb C$ occurs: in the <Banach algebra> $C(K)$, the coordinate function $a(z)=z$ has <Banach algebra spectrum> exactly $K$, since $\lambda-a$ has a continuous reciprocal precisely when $\lambda\notin K$. Thus **the possible <Banach algebra spectra> are exactly the nonempty compact subsets of the complex plane**.

For a nonconstant <polynomial> $p$, factor $p(z)-\lambda=c\prod_{j=1}^n(z-z_j)$ over $\mathbb C$. Substitution gives
$$
p(a)-\lambda1=c\prod_{j=1}^n(a-z_j1).
$$
These factors commute. A finite commuting product is invertible exactly when every factor is invertible: if $P=\prod_jx_j$ is invertible, its inverse commutes with each $x_j$, and $x_j^{-1}=(\prod_{k\ne j}x_k)P^{-1}$ is a two-sided inverse. Hence $\lambda$ belongs to the <Banach algebra spectrum> of $p(a)$ exactly when some root $z_j$ belongs to $\sigma_A(a)$, which is equivalent to $\lambda\in p(\sigma_A(a))$. Constant <polynomials> give the same result directly, since $\sigma_A(c1)=\{c\}$. This proves the <polynomial> <spectral mapping theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(p(a))=p(\sigma_A(a)).}
$$
For a closed unital <Banach subalgebra> $B\subseteq A$, use the common identity $1_A\in B$. An inverse in $B$ is an inverse in $A$, so $\boxed{\sigma_A(b)\subseteq\sigma_B(b)}$. Let $\lambda\in\partial\sigma_B(b)$. Choose $\lambda_n\notin\sigma_B(b)$ tending to $\lambda$. Then $\lambda_n\notin\sigma_A(b)$, and $(\lambda_n1-b)^{-1}\in B$. If $\lambda$ were outside $\sigma_A(b)$, continuity of inversion in $A$ would give
$$
(\lambda_n1-b)^{-1}\longrightarrow(\lambda1-b)^{-1}\in B,
$$
where membership follows from closedness. This would make $\lambda\notin\sigma_B(b)$, contrary to the closed <Banach algebra spectrum> having its boundary inside it. Therefore $\lambda\in\sigma_A(b)$ and is approached by points outside that <Banach algebra spectrum>, proving
$$
\boxed{\partial\sigma_B(b)\subseteq\partial\sigma_A(b).}
$$
Continuity of inversion here follows, for example, by writing a nearby element as an invertible element times $1+$ a <norm>-small perturbation and using the <Neumann series>. This is <spectrum in a closed unital subalgebra>.

To retain the original <Banach algebra spectrum> in a commutative subalgebra, take the <resolvent-generated commutative algebra>
$$
C=\overline{\operatorname{alg}\bigl(1,a,(\lambda1-a)^{-1}:\lambda\notin\sigma_A(a)\bigr)}^{\|\cdot\|}.
$$
Every generator commutes with $a$ and with every other generator: commuting invertible elements have commuting inverses, and their inverses commute with all elements commuting with them. <commutativity> persists on the algebra they generate and on its <norm> closure. For every $\lambda\notin\sigma_A(a)$, the required inverse is in $C$ by construction, so $\sigma_C(a)\subseteq\sigma_A(a)$. The inclusion for unital subalgebras gives the reverse containment. Hence $\boxed{\sigma_C(a)=\sigma_A(a)}$.

Finally, the <Banach subalgebra generated by one element> is $D=\overline{\{p(a):p\text{ a polynomial}\}}$, and it is commutative. Put $K=\sigma_D(a)$ and suppose its complement has a bounded connected component $U$. The boundary of $U$ lies in $K$. For $\lambda\in U$, the <maximum modulus principle on a bounded domain> applied to any <polynomial> gives
$$
|p(\lambda)|\leq\max_{z\in\partial U}|p(z)|\leq\max_{z\in K}|p(z)|=r_D(p(a))\leq\|p(a)\|.
$$
Here $r_D$ is the <spectral radius>, and the equality uses the already proved <polynomial> <spectral mapping theorem>. Define $\chi(p(a))=p(\lambda)$. The displayed bound makes this well defined, even when two <polynomials> represent the same algebra element, and bounded on the dense <polynomial> algebra. Its continuous extension to $D$ remains linear and multiplicative, with $\chi(1)=1$ and $\chi(a)=\lambda$, so it is a <character of an algebra>. If $\lambda1-a$ were invertible in $D$, applying $\chi$ to its inverse equation would give $0=1$. Thus $\lambda\in K$, contradicting $\lambda\in U$.

There are therefore no bounded complementary components. A compact subset of the plane has exactly one unbounded complementary component: everything outside a sufficiently large disk is connected, and every unbounded component meets it. **Consequently $\boxed{\mathbb C\setminus\sigma_D(a)\text{ is connected}.}$**

For $\lambda\ne0$, suppose $R=(\lambda1-ba)^{-1}$ exists. The identities $(\lambda1-ab)a=a(\lambda1-ba)$ and $b(\lambda1-ab)=(\lambda1-ba)b$ show, by multiplication on either side, that
$$
\boxed{(\lambda1-ab)^{-1}=\lambda^{-1}(1+aRb).}
$$
For example, multiplying on the left gives $1-\lambda^{-1}ab+\lambda^{-1}a(\lambda1-ba)Rb=1$, and the right product gives the same cancellation. Thus invertibility of $\lambda1-ba$ implies invertibility of $\lambda1-ab$. Interchanging $a,b$ proves the converse. The <nonzero spectra of products in opposite orders> are therefore equal:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(ab)\setminus\{0\}=\sigma_A(ba)\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
In particular the requested inclusion follows. The exclusion of zero matters: for the <unilateral shift operator> $S$, $S^*S=1$ has <Banach algebra spectrum> $\{1\}$, whereas $SS^*=1-P_0$ has <Banach algebra spectrum> $\{0,1\}$.

Use the spectral definition of a <positive element of a C-star algebra>: $h$ is positive if $h=h^*$ and $\sigma_A(h)\subseteq[0,\infty)$. We use the standard <continuous functional calculus> for a <Hermitian element of a C-star algebra>, including its real <Banach algebra spectrum> and the equality of its <norm> with its <spectral radius>. To prove $a^*a$ positive without assuming this as part of the definition, first establish two elementary cone facts.

If $p,q$ are positive and $s=\|p\|+\|q\|$, the <continuous functional calculus> gives $\|\|p\|1-p\|\leq\|p\|$ and the corresponding bound for $q$, hence
$$
\|s1-p-q\|\leq s.
$$
Since $p+q$ is self-adjoint, every spectral value $t$ is real and satisfies $|s-t|\leq s$, so $t\geq0$. Thus the <positive cone of a C-star algebra> is closed under addition. It is proper: if both $h$ and $-h$ are positive, their <Banach algebra spectra> force $\sigma(h)\subseteq\{0\}$, and the self-adjoint <norm> equality gives $h=0$. It is also <norm> closed: if $p_n\geq0$ converges to $p$, choose $r\geq\sup_n\|p_n\|$; then $\|r1-p_n\|\leq r$ passes to the limit and forces every spectral value of $p$ to be nonnegative.

Now put $h=a^*a$, and form its negative part $d=\max(-h,0)$ by <continuous functional calculus>. Let $c=ad^{1/2}$. Because $d$ is a function of $h$, the scalar identity $t\max(-t,0)=-\max(-t,0)^2$ gives
$$
c^*c=d^{1/2}hd^{1/2}=-d^2.
$$
This has nonpositive <Banach algebra spectrum>. The previously proved equality of nonzero product <Banach algebra spectra> shows that $cc^*$ also has nonpositive <Banach algebra spectrum>; it is self-adjoint, so both $c^*c$ and $cc^*$ are negative elements. Write $c=u+iv$, where $u=(c+c^*)/2$ and $v=(c-c^*)/(2i)$ are self-adjoint. Direct multiplication gives
$$
c^*c+cc^*=2(u^2+v^2).
$$
By the <spectral mapping theorem>, the squares $u^2,v^2$ are positive, so their sum is positive by the cone fact above. But the same sum is negative, since both its original summands are negative. Properness forces $c^*c+cc^*=0$. Then $c^*c=-cc^*$ is both negative and positive, so $c^*c=0$, whence $d^2=0$. The <C-star identity> applied to the self-adjoint $d$ gives $\|d\|^2=\|d^2\|=0$. Thus $d=0$, proving
$$
\boxed{a^*a\geq0.}
$$
This establishes <positivity of adjoint products from spectral positivity>. It also proves positivity under congruence: for $p\geq0$ and any $z$, write $p=r^2$ using its functional-calculus square root; then $z^*pz=(rz)^*(rz)\geq0$.

For the comparison of squares, we first derive <inversion reverses the order of strictly positive elements>. If $0<x\leq y$ with both positive and invertible, then
$$
k=x^{-1/2}yx^{-1/2}\geq1.
$$
The <continuous functional calculus> gives $k^{-1}\leq1$, because the scalar function $1-t^{-1}$ is nonnegative on $\sigma(k)\subseteq[1,\infty)$. Congruence then gives
$$
y^{-1}=x^{-1/2}k^{-1}x^{-1/2}\leq x^{-1}.
$$
No commutation of $x,y$ was assumed.

Next derive the <square-root resolvent integral in a C-star algebra>. For $s\geq0$,
$$
\sqrt{s}=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\frac{s}{t+s}\,dt.
$$
For $s>0$, substituting $t=su^2$ reduces the integral to $\sqrt{s}\,\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty2/(1+u^2)\,du=\sqrt{s}$; for $s=0$ it is zero. For a positive element $x$, the <norm> of $x(t1+x)^{-1}$ is at most $\min(1,\|x\|/t)$. Thus the integral converges in <norm>, with integrable bounds $t^{-1/2}$ near zero and $\|x\|t^{-3/2}$ near infinity, uniform on $\sigma(x)$. The <continuous functional calculus> consequently gives
$$
x^{1/2}=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}x(t1+x)^{-1}\,dt.
$$
Put $\alpha=a^2$ and $\beta=b^2$. The assumption $b^2-a^2\geq0$ says $\alpha\leq\beta$. For every $t>0$, inversion reverses the order of the strictly positive elements $t1+\alpha$ and $t1+\beta$. Subtracting the two square-root formulas therefore yields
$$
b-a=\beta^{1/2}-\alpha^{1/2}
=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty t^{1/2}\bigl((t1+\alpha)^{-1}-(t1+\beta)^{-1}\bigr)\,dt\geq0.
$$
Here $\alpha^{1/2}=a$ and $\beta^{1/2}=b$ because $a,b$ themselves are positive. Every truncated integral has a positive integrand, so is positive; <norm> convergence and closedness of the <positive cone of a C-star algebra> make its limit positive. This proves <order preservation by the positive square root> in the required case:
$$
\boxed{b^2\geq a^2,\ a,b\geq0\Longrightarrow b-a\geq0.}
$$
It is important that this argument does not factor $b^2-a^2$ as $(b-a)(b+a)$, which would be invalid for noncommuting elements.

For $p=b-a\geq0$, apply the continuous function $t\mapsto\sqrt t$ on its nonnegative compact <Banach algebra spectrum>. The resulting element $p^{1/2}$ is self-adjoint, has nonnegative <Banach algebra spectrum> by <continuous functional calculus>, and satisfies $(p^{1/2})^2=p$. Thus **$b-a$ has a <positive square root in a C-star algebra>**.

Finally, if the positive element $a$ is invertible, its compact <Banach algebra spectrum> is bounded away from zero. Let $\varepsilon=\min\sigma(a)>0$; <continuous functional calculus> gives $a\geq\varepsilon1$. Since $b\geq a$, we also have $b\geq\varepsilon1$, so $\sigma(b)\subseteq[\varepsilon,\infty)$. The reciprocal function is continuous on this <Banach algebra spectrum> and supplies $b^{-1}$. Therefore $\boxed{a\text{ invertible}\Longrightarrow b\text{ invertible}}$ under the given positivity assumptions.
