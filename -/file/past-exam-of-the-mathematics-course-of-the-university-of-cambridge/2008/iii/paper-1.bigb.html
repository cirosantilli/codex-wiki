Use complex <inner products> linear in the first argument throughout. The <spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators> states that, for a <compact operator> $T=T^*$ on a <Hilbert space>, its nonzero spectral values are real <eigenvalues>, each of finite multiplicity, with no possible accumulation point except zero. Moreover,
$$
\boxed{H=\ker T\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\ne0}\ker(T-\lambda I),
\qquad T=\sum_{\lambda\ne0}\lambda P_\lambda,}
$$
where $P_\lambda$ are the mutually orthogonal <eigenspace> <projections>. The operator series converges in <operator norm> when the nonzero <eigenvalues> are listed in decreasing absolute value. The sum is at most countable, even if the <kernel> is nonseparable. Zero need not be an <eigenvalue>.

First prove that a nonzero compact <self-adjoint operator> has an <eigenvalue> of magnitude its <norm>, without assuming a spectral decomposition. Put $r=\|T\|>0$ and choose unit <vectors> $\xi_n$ with $\|T\xi_n\|\to r$. Since $\|T^2\xi\|\leq r\|T\xi\|$, expansion gives
$$
\|(T^2-r^2I)\xi_n\|^2
\leq r^2\bigl(r^2-\|T\xi_n\|^2\bigr)\longrightarrow0.
$$
<Compactness> of $T^2$ supplies a subsequence for which $T^2\xi_n$ converges; the displayed estimate then forces $\xi_n$ itself to converge to a unit <vector> $\xi$ satisfying $T^2\xi=r^2\xi$. The <vectors> $\xi+T\xi/r$ and $\xi-T\xi/r$ are <eigenvectors> for $r$ and $-r$, respectively, whenever nonzero, and they cannot both vanish. This proves the claim.

Self-adjointness gives reality of every <eigenvalue> and <orthogonality> of <eigenspaces> for distinct <eigenvalues>. An <eigenspace> for $\lambda\ne0$ cannot contain an infinite orthonormal sequence: its images under $T$ would have pairwise distance $\sqrt2|\lambda|$, contradicting <compactness>. The same argument proves that there are only finitely many mutually orthogonal <eigenvectors> with <eigenvalues> of magnitude at least any given $\varepsilon>0$. Hence all nonzero <eigenspaces> together have a countable <orthonormal basis>, and their <eigenvalues> tend to zero if there are infinitely many.

Let $K$ be the <orthogonal complement> of the closed span of all these <eigenspaces>. It is invariant under $T$. If $T|_K$ were nonzero, the norm-attaining <eigenvalue> argument applied to that compact <self-adjoint> restriction would produce another nonzero <eigenvector>, a contradiction. Thus $K\subseteq\ker T$, and the converse inclusion follows from <orthogonality>. This establishes the asserted decomposition. On its orthogonal summands the tail of the operator series has <norm> equal to the supremum of the omitted <eigenvalue> magnitudes, proving <norm> convergence.

Finally, a nonzero complex number not among these <eigenvalues> has positive distance from their set and from zero. Inverting $T-zI$ separately on the <eigenspaces> and <kernel> therefore gives a bounded inverse. This proves that no other nonzero spectral values occur and completes the theorem.

For a <finite-rank operator> $T$, define $|T|=(T^*T)^{1/2}$ and its <trace norm> by
$$
\boxed{\|T\|_1=\operatorname{Tr}|T|=\sum_{j=1}^r s_j,}
$$
where $s_j>0$ are its nonzero <singular values>, counted with multiplicity. The finite-rank spectral theorem gives orthonormal systems $(u_j),(v_j)$ with $T=\sum_js_jR_{u_j,v_j}$, where $R_{x,y}z=\langle z,y\rangle x$.

For every <bounded operator> $B$,
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(BT)=\sum_js_j\langle Bu_j,v_j\rangle,
\qquad |\operatorname{Tr}(BT)|\leq\|B\|\sum_js_j.
$$
Choose the contraction $B$ taking $u_j$ to $v_j$ and vanishing on their <orthogonal complement>. It attains equality, so
$$
\boxed{\|T\|_1=\sup_{\|B\|\leq1}|\operatorname{Tr}(BT)|.}
$$
This formula proves the <triangle inequality> and absolute homogeneity. Also $\|T\|\leq\|T\|_1$, so vanishing of the <trace norm> forces $T=0$. Thus it is a <norm> on the <vector> space of <finite-rank operators>.

The map $B\mapsto F_B$, where $F_B(T)=\operatorname{Tr}(BT)$, is a bounded linear map into the dual of this normed space. Its <norm> is at most $\|B\|$. Rank-one tests give the reverse inequality: $\|R_{x,y}\|_1=\|x\|\|y\|$ and $F_B(R_{x,y})=\langle Bx,y\rangle$, so taking unit <vectors> $x,y$ gives $\|F_B\|=\|B\|$.

Conversely, let $F$ be a <bounded linear functional> for the <trace norm>. The <sesquilinear form> $\beta(x,y)=F(R_{x,y})$ satisfies
$$
|\beta(x,y)|\leq\|F\|\|x\|\|y\|.
$$
The <Riesz representation theorem> gives a unique <bounded operator> $B$ with $\beta(x,y)=\langle Bx,y\rangle$, and $\|B\|\leq\|F\|$. Since every <finite-rank operator> is a finite sum of <rank-one operators>, <linearity> implies $F(T)=\operatorname{Tr}(BT)$ for every $T$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(\mathcal F(H),\|\cdot\|_1)^*\cong B(H)\text{ isometrically}.}
$$
The same identification extends to the completion, the <trace-class operators>. This is <trace-class duality>; completeness of the original finite-rank space is not required to identify its bounded dual.

For an <orthonormal basis> $(e_i)$, define the <Hilbert-Schmidt norm> by $\|T\|_2^2=\sum_i\|Te_i\|^2$. For a possibly uncountable <basis>, the nonnegative sum means the supremum over finite subsets. <Parseval's identity> gives, for any other <orthonormal basis> $(f_j)$,
$$
\sum_i\|Te_i\|^2=\sum_{i,j}|\langle Te_i,f_j\rangle|^2
=\sum_j\|T^*f_j\|^2.
$$
Changing the domain <basis> in the last expression proves <basis> independence. Polarization gives a basis-independent <inner product>
$$
\boxed{\langle T,S\rangle_2=\sum_i\langle Te_i,Se_i\rangle
=\operatorname{Tr}(S^*T),}
$$
with the same first-argument linear convention. The sums converge by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. The operator-norm bound $\|T\|\leq\|T\|_2$ follows by applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the coordinates of a unit <vector>.

To prove completeness, let $(T_n)$ be Cauchy in this <norm>. The operator-norm bound gives a bounded-operator limit $T$. For a finite subset $F$ of the <basis>,
$$
\sum_{i\in F}\|(T_n-T)e_i\|^2
=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{i\in F}\|(T_n-T_m)e_i\|^2.
$$
For large $n$, the right side is uniformly small by the Cauchy property. Taking the supremum over $F$ shows $T_n-T$ is <Hilbert-Schmidt> and $\|T_n-T\|_2\to0$; it also shows $T$ is <Hilbert-Schmidt>. Thus **the <Hilbert-Schmidt operators> form a <Hilbert space>**. <Finite-rank operators> are dense there: keeping finitely many <basis> columns makes the sum of omitted squared column <norms> tend to zero. The operator-norm bound then shows that every <Hilbert-Schmidt operator> is compact.

Now let $U$ be a strongly continuous <unitary representation> of the compact metric <group> $G$, as is standard for a representation of a topological <group>. Fix $\xi\ne0$ and average the positive <rank-one operator> $R_{\xi,\xi}$:
$$
K=\int_G U_gR_{\xi,\xi}U_g^*\,dg.
$$
The integrand is continuous in the <Hilbert-Schmidt norm>, so this <Bochner integral> exists in the complete space just proved. Normalized <Haar measure> and invariance show that $K$ is positive, compact and commutes with every $U_g$. Moreover,
$$
\langle K\xi,\xi\rangle=\int_G|\langle U_g\xi,\xi\rangle|^2\,dg>0:
$$
the integrand is positive near the identity and every nonempty open set has positive <Haar measure>. Thus $K\ne0$. A nonzero <eigenspace> is finite-dimensional and invariant under $U$, by (a). Within it choose a nonzero <invariant subspace> of smallest dimension; it is irreducible. Its <orthogonal complement> is invariant because the representation is unitary, so the finite-dimensional space splits into irreducibles.

Take a maximal orthogonal family of such finite-dimensional irreducible <invariant subspaces>. If their closed span had a nonzero <orthogonal complement>, the same averaging argument on that complement would produce another member, contradicting maximality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H=\bigoplus_\alpha H_\alpha,
\quad\dim H_\alpha<\infty,\quad U|_{H_\alpha}\text{ irreducible}.}
$$
This <compact averaging of a rank-one operator> argument does not require $H$ itself to be separable. Strong <continuity> is the topological representation hypothesis used in the averaging step.

A <Hadamard space> is a complete <CAT(0) space>. A useful equivalent definition, which makes the remaining assertions consequences rather than assumptions, is a <complete metric space> in which every pair $x,y$ has a midpoint $m$, with $d(x,m)=d(y,m)=d(x,y)/2$, satisfying
$$
\boxed{d(z,m)^2\leq\frac12d(z,x)^2+\frac12d(z,y)^2-\frac14d(x,y)^2
\quad\text{for every }z.}
$$
This is the nonpositive-curvature midpoint inequality. Euclidean <Hilbert spaces> satisfy it with equality in the parallelogram formula. The comparison argument in (c) shows that this definition gives the usual CAT(0) triangle condition.

Write $L=d(x,y)$. If $m'$ is any other metric midpoint, apply the inequality from (a) to the chosen midpoint $m$ with $z=m'$. The right side is $\tfrac12(L/2)^2+\tfrac12(L/2)^2-L^2/4=0$, so $m=m'$. **Every pair has a unique midpoint.**

Choose successive midpoints to define $\gamma(k/2^n)$ on all dyadic parameters. Consecutive points at level $n$ have distance $L/2^n$. The <triangle inequality> gives $d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))\leq(t-s)L$ for dyadic $s<t$. Conversely,
$$
L\leq d(x,\gamma(s))+d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))+d(\gamma(t),y)
\leq sL+d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))+(1-t)L,
$$
which gives the reverse bound. Thus equality holds. Completeness extends this map uniquely and continuously to $[0,1]$, retaining $d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|L$. It is a constant-speed <geodesic>.

Every other <geodesic> has the same midpoint, and then the same successive midpoints, so agrees on all dyadic parameters. <Continuity> gives agreement everywhere. Hence $\boxed{\text{there is exactly one geodesic segment between any two points}.}$

Iteration proves its convexity bound for dyadic $t$, and <continuity> then gives the <Hadamard squared-distance inequality>
$$
d(z,\gamma(t))^2\leq(1-t)d(z,p)^2+td(z,q)^2-t(1-t)L^2.
$$
Apply this first to $[a,x]$ with $z=y_t$, and then to $[a,y]$ with $z=x$:
$$
d(x_s,y_t)^2\leq(1-s)t^2B^2+s\bigl[(1-t)A^2+tC^2-t(1-t)B^2\bigr]
-s(1-s)A^2.
$$
Simplification gives
$$
\boxed{d(x_s,y_t)^2\leq s^2A^2+t^2B^2-st(A^2+B^2-C^2).}
$$
In a Euclidean comparison triangle, the cosine rule gives the scalar product of the two <vectors> from the common vertex as $(A^2+B^2-C^2)/2$. The squared distance between their multiples $s$ and $t$ is exactly the right side displayed above. Taking nonnegative square roots proves the required triangle comparison, including degenerate Euclidean triangles.

The <Stone theorem for the circle group> says that a strongly continuous <unitary representation> $U$ of $\mathbb T=\{z:|z|=1\}$ has an orthogonal decomposition
$$
\boxed{H=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb Z}H_n,\qquad U_z|_{H_n}=z^nI.}
$$
Equivalently, $U_z=\sum_nz^nP_n$ strongly, with mutually <orthogonal projections> $P_n$ summing strongly to the identity. Define these <projections> using normalized <Haar measure>:
$$
P_n\xi=\int_{\mathbb T}z^{-n}U_z\xi\,dz.
$$
Haar invariance gives $U_wP_n=w^nP_n$. Changing $z$ to $z^{-1}$ in the adjoint integral proves $P_n^*=P_n$. Applying the defining integral to $P_m\xi$ gives $P_nP_m=\delta_{nm}P_m$ by <orthogonality> of the characters $z^k$. Thus $P_n$ are <orthogonal projections>, and their ranges are exactly the indicated character subspaces.

It remains to prove completeness of these subspaces. The <Fejer kernel> has the finite expansion
$$
K_N(e^{it})=\sum_{|n|<N}\left(1-\frac{|n|}{N}\right)e^{-int}
=\frac1N\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ijt}\right|^2.
$$
It is nonnegative, has normalized integral one, and its integral outside any fixed neighborhood of the identity tends to zero, since the denominator in the usual geometric-sum formula is bounded away from zero there. Strong <continuity> therefore gives
$$
\int K_N(z)U_z\xi\,dz\longrightarrow\xi:
$$
split the integral into that neighborhood, where $\|U_z\xi-\xi\|$ is small, and its complement, where it is bounded by $2\|\xi\|$. The integral on the left is $\sum_{|n|<N}(1-|n|/N)P_n\xi$. Hence the closed span of all $H_n$ is $H$. <Orthogonality> then gives the unweighted strong sum and the stated representation formula.

Conversely, any orthogonal decomposition indexed by the integers gives this representation; <continuity> follows first on finite sums and then on all <vectors> by the uniform unitary <norm> bound. With $z=e^{it}$ its generator is
$$
A\xi=\sum_nnP_n\xi,\qquad
D(A)=\left\{\xi:\sum_nn^2\|P_n\xi\|^2<\infty\right\}.
$$
This diagonal operator is <self-adjoint> and $U_{e^{it}}=e^{itA}$. Thus the circle theorem is the integer-spectrum version of the one-parameter theorem.

The <Stone theorem for one-parameter unitary groups> states that every strongly continuous <unitary representation> $U_t$ of $\mathbb R$ is uniquely $U_t=e^{itA}$ for a possibly unbounded <self-adjoint operator> $A$. Its domain is
$$
D(A)=\left\{\xi:\lim_{t\to0}\frac{U_t\xi-\xi}{it}\text{ exists in norm}\right\}.
$$
Conversely every <self-adjoint> $A$ gives such a <group> by the <Borel functional calculus for a normal operator>.

Prove the forward direction by putting $G=iA$, initially defined as the <norm> derivative at zero. Its domain is dense: for $f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$, the <vector> $\xi_f=\int f(t)U_t\xi\,dt$ has derivative $G\xi_f=-\int f'(t)U_t\xi\,dt$. Smooth approximate identities make $\xi_f\to\xi$. The <group> law gives $U_tD(G)=D(G)$ and $GU_t=U_tG$ on that domain. Differentiation of preserved <inner products> shows that $G$ is skew-symmetric, so $A=-iG$ is symmetric.

The derivative operator is closed. Indeed, if $\xi_n\to\xi$ and $G\xi_n\to\eta$, integrate the orbit derivative to obtain
$$
U_t\xi_n-\xi_n=\int_0^tU_sG\xi_n\,ds.
$$
Taking limits gives the same identity with $\xi,\eta$; dividing by $t$ at zero proves $\xi\in D(G)$ and $G\xi=\eta$.

Now define bounded Laplace integrals
$$
R_+\xi=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}U_t\xi\,dt,\qquad
R_-\xi=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}U_{-t}\xi\,dt.
$$
Shifting the integration variable in $U_hR_\pm\xi$ and taking the derivative at zero shows that $R_\pm\xi\in D(G)$ and
$$
GR_+=R_+-I,\qquad GR_-=I-R_-.
$$
Hence $I-G$ and $I+G$ are both surjective. Since $G=iA$, this means $A+iI$ and $A-iI$ are surjective.

Here is why these range conditions establish self-adjointness rather than just symmetry. If $\eta\in D(A^*)$, solve $(A-iI)\xi=(A^*-iI)\eta$. Then $\eta-\xi\in\ker(A^*-iI)=\operatorname{Ran}(A+iI)^\perp=\{0\}$. Thus $D(A^*)\subseteq D(A)$, and symmetry gives equality with $A^*=A$.

The permitted functional calculus now gives $V_t=e^{itA}$. It is unitary, strongly continuous by dominated convergence against spectral measures, and differentiable with derivative $iAV_t\xi$ on $D(A)$. Differentiate $V_{-t}U_t\xi$ for $\xi\in D(A)$; the two derivative terms cancel because both <groups> preserve the domain and commute with their generator. Its value is consequently the constant $\xi$. Density of the domain implies $U_t=V_t$ on all of $H$.

For completeness, the converse and the exact domain follow from the same calculus. If $\xi\in D(A)$, dominated convergence applied to $|(e^{it\lambda}-1)/t|\leq|\lambda|$ gives the derivative $iA\xi$. If that <norm> derivative exists, Fatou's lemma applied to its squared <norm> gives $\int\lambda^2\,d\langle E_A(\lambda)\xi,\xi\rangle<\infty$, so $\xi\in D(A)$. The derivative characterization also proves uniqueness. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{strongly continuous unitary groups}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \text{self-adjoint generators}.}
$$

For a discrete <group>, one standard definition of amenability is the fixed-point property: every continuous affine action on a nonempty compact <convex set> in a locally convex space has a fixed point. The equivalent state formulation, which may be stated without proof here, is that there is a left-invariant <invariant mean> on $\ell^\infty(\Gamma)$. Equivalently, every <unital> left-translation-invariant <star-subalgebra> of $\ell^\infty(\Gamma)$ admits a left-invariant state, with <continuity> for the supremum <norm>; one may equally pass to its <norm> <closure>. A state means a positive <linear functional> of <norm> one with value one on the constant function one. Restriction proves the <subalgebra> formulation from the full-algebra version, and taking the full algebra proves the converse. Such a mean is finitely additive on <indicator functions>, not necessarily countably additive.

Write $(\lambda(g)\mu)(h)=\mu(g^{-1}h)$ on $\ell^1(\Gamma)$ and $(L_gf)(h)=f(g^{-1}h)$ on bounded functions. We prove equivalence of this mean condition, the <Reiter condition>, and existence of a <Følner sequence>.

Assume first that an <invariant mean> $m$ exists. Fix a finite set $K\subseteq\Gamma$. In the real <Banach space> $\bigoplus_{g\in K}\ell^1(\Gamma)$ with sum <norm>, consider the <convex set>
$$
\mathcal C=\{(\lambda(g)\mu-\mu)_{g\in K}:\mu\text{ a finitely supported probability measure}\}.
$$
If zero were outside its <norm> <closure>, the <Hahn-Banach separation theorem> would give bounded real functions $f_g$ and $\delta>0$ such that
$$
\sum_{g\in K}\sum_h f_g(h)[(\lambda(g)\mu)(h)-\mu(h)]\geq\delta
$$
for every such $\mu$. Testing point masses at $h$ shows
$$
\sum_{g\in K}[f_g(gh)-f_g(h)]\geq\delta\quad\text{for every }h.
$$
Applying the positive <invariant mean> makes the left side zero and the right side at least $\delta$, a contradiction. Therefore zero is in the <closure>: for any $\varepsilon>0$ a finitely supported <probability measure> makes the sum of the translation defects over $K$ less than $\varepsilon$. Enumerate the countable <group>, take the first $n$ elements for $K$ and $\varepsilon=1/n$, and obtain a sequence satisfying the <Reiter condition>. This proves (1) implies (2).

Next assume (2). An $\ell^1$ <probability measure> can be approximated in <norm> by finitely supported <probability measures>, by restricting to a large finite set and renormalizing. Replacing $\mu$ by $\nu$ changes each defect by at most $2\|\mu-\nu\|_1$. Thus simultaneous small defects for finitely many translations can be achieved with finite support.

For such $\nu$, set $F_t=\{h:\nu(h)>t\}$. The elementary identity $|r-s|=\int_0^\infty|1_{r>t}-1_{s>t}|\,dt$ for $r,s\geq0$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty|F_t|\,dt=1,\qquad
\int_0^\infty|F_t\mathbin\triangle gF_t|\,dt
=\|\lambda(g)\nu-\nu\|_1.
$$
If the sum of defects over $K$ is less than $\varepsilon$, some $t$ with $F_t\ne\varnothing$ must satisfy $\sum_{g\in K}|F_t\triangle gF_t|<\varepsilon|F_t|$; otherwise integration would contradict that strict inequality. Applying this <layer-cake extraction of Følner sets> to successive finite sets of translations gives nonempty finite $F_n$ with each relative boundary tending to zero. Hence (2) implies (3).

Conversely, normalized counting measures $\mu_n=1_{F_n}/|F_n|$ satisfy
$$
\|\lambda(g)\mu_n-\mu_n\|_1=\frac{|gF_n\triangle F_n|}{|F_n|},
$$
so (3) implies (2). Finally, from (2) define states $m_n(f)=\sum_h\mu_n(h)f(h)$. The dual <unit ball> of $\ell^\infty(\Gamma)$ is weak-star compact by the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem>; take a convergent subnet, since this <compactness> need not be metrizable. Its limit $m$ is positive with $m(1)=1$, and
$$
|m_n(L_gf)-m_n(f)|\leq\|f\|_\infty\|\lambda(g^{-1})\mu_n-\mu_n\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $m$ is invariant, proving (2) implies (1). Together these implications establish all three equivalences. Countability is used to obtain sequences from finite tests; it is not a justification for replacing the <compactness> subnet by a subsequence.

Assume the invariant set $C$ is nonempty, as required for any fixed-point assertion. For $x\in C$, put $r(x)=\sup_{c\in C}\|x-c\|$ and $r_*=\inf_{x\in C}r(x)$. Boundedness makes these finite. The parallelogram identity gives, for $x,y\in C$,
$$
r\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^2
\leq\frac{r(x)^2+r(y)^2}{2}-\frac{\|x-y\|^2}{4}.
$$
The midpoint lies in $C$, so a minimizing sequence $x_n$ satisfies
$$
\|x_n-x_m\|^2\leq2r(x_n)^2+2r(x_m)^2-4r_*^2\longrightarrow0.
$$
Since $C$ is closed in a <Hilbert space>, the sequence converges to $x_*\in C$. The function $r$ is 1-Lipschitz, hence $r(x_*)=r_*$. The same midpoint inequality proves uniqueness of this minimizing center.

An affine isometry from the <group> maps $C$ onto itself, since its inverse also preserves $C$. Therefore it preserves $r$ and sends its unique minimizer to another minimizer. Uniqueness gives
$$
\boxed{gx_*=x_*\quad\text{for every group element }g.}
$$
This proof applies to a closed bounded convex subset, the substantive meaning of the printed “convex subspace”; if it is literally a bounded <vector> subspace, it is just the zero subspace.

The topology needed here is the <weak-star topology> $\sigma(E^*,E)$, meaning pointwise convergence on $E$. With the literal <weak topology> $\sigma(E^*,E^{**})$, the claimed <compactness> is false. For example, $E=c_0$ is separable and $E^*=\ell^1$. Its unit <vectors> $e_n$ have no weak cluster point: every coordinate functional forces a cluster point to have all coordinates zero, while the bounded functional $x\mapsto\sum_jx_j$ has value one throughout. Thus the dual <unit ball> is not <weakly compact>. We prove the intended weak-star assertion in full.

Choose a countable dense set $(x_k)$ in the <unit ball> of $E$. On $E_1^*$ define
$$
\boxed{d(\phi,\psi)=\sum_{k\geq1}2^{-k}|\phi(x_k)-\psi(x_k)|.}
$$
Each term is at most $2\cdot2^{-k}$, so the series converges. The <triangle inequality> is immediate, and $d=0$ forces equality on the dense set and hence on all of $E$. It is therefore a metric. Convergence in this metric is equivalent to convergence on every $x_k$: one direction follows from any individual term, and the other by bounding the series tail uniformly. Because $\|\phi\|,\|\psi\|\leq1$, approximation of any unit <vector> by $x_k$ then gives convergence on all of $E$. The same finite-tail and approximation argument for neighborhoods proves equality with the <weak-star topology>, not just agreement of its convergent sequences.

Given a sequence $(\phi_n)$ in this ball, diagonal selection gives a subsequence converging at every $x_k$, since each coordinate lies in a compact disk. Uniform <norm> bounds then make it pointwise Cauchy at every $x\in E$. Define $\phi(x)$ as the limit. <Linearity> passes to the limit, and $|\phi(x)|\leq\|x\|$, so $\phi\in E_1^*$. The subsequence converges to it in the metric. Thus this <metric space> is sequentially compact, hence compact, proving
$$
\boxed{E_1^*\text{ is compact and metrizable in }\sigma(E^*,E).}
$$

Invariance and convexity put every $\phi_n$ in $C$. Weak-star closedness and (b) make $C$ compact, so some subsequence converges to $\psi\in C$. Telescoping gives
$$
T\phi_n-\phi_n=\frac{T^n\phi-\phi}{n},\qquad
\|T\phi_n-\phi_n\|\leq\frac2n,
$$
because both orbit points lie in the dual <unit ball>. Weak-star <continuity> now gives $T\psi-\psi=0$. Hence $\boxed{T\text{ has a fixed point in }C.}$ This is the <weak-star fixed point theorem for an adjoint operator>; it does not require $T$ to be a contraction on the entire <dual space>.

If “weakly closed” is interpreted literally as closed for $\sigma(E^*,E^{**})$, the result can fail. Take $E=c_0$, let $S$ be the left shift and $T=S^*$ the right shift on $\ell^1$. The set
$$
C=\{x\in\ell^1:x_j\geq0,\ \sum_jx_j=1\}
$$
is a nonempty weakly closed convex subset of the <unit ball>: its defining coordinate inequalities and sum equality are weakly closed. It is invariant under the right shift. But a fixed <vector> of that shift has first coordinate zero and then every coordinate zero, so none belongs to $C$. This supplies a counterexample to the literal topology and explains why the weak-star qualification is essential.

For a <unital> <star-subalgebra> $A\subseteq B(H)$, the <Von Neumann double commutant theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\overline A^{\mathrm{SOT}}=\overline A^{\mathrm{WOT}}=A''.}
$$
Here $A'$ is the <commutant of an operator algebra>, consisting of operators commuting with every element of $A$. The double commutant is closed in either topology: multiplication by a fixed <bounded operator> is continuous in both. Therefore both closures of $A$ lie in $A''$.

For the converse, let $T\in A''$ and choose finitely many <vectors> $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$. In $H^n$ put
$$
K=\overline{\{(a\xi_1,\ldots,a\xi_n):a\in A\}}.
$$
It is invariant under the diagonal action of $A$ and its adjoints, so its <orthogonal projection> $Q$ commutes with that diagonal action. Consequently each <matrix> entry $Q_{ij}$ belongs to $A'$. The diagonal operator with entry $T$ therefore commutes with $Q$. Since $I\in A$, the tuple $\xi=(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ belongs to $K$, and hence so does $(T\xi_1,\ldots,T\xi_n)$. By the definition of $K$, one element $a\in A$ approximates this entire tuple arbitrarily closely. This is exactly approximation in every strong-operator neighborhood of $T$. It proves $A''\subseteq\overline A^{\mathrm{SOT}}$ and thus both asserted equalities. In particular, $M=A''$ is a <Von Neumann algebra>.

The <Kaplansky density theorem> strengthens the result by preserving <norm> bounds:
$$
\boxed{\overline{\{a\in A:\|a\|\leq1\}}^{\mathrm{SOT}}
=\{x\in M:\|x\|\leq1\}.}
$$
It also holds for the <self-adjoint> and positive unit balls; the full ball can in fact be approximated in the <strong-star topology>, meaning strong convergence of both operators and adjoints. We prove the bound rather than merely scaling the unbounded approximants furnished by the double commutant theorem.

First put $C=\overline A^{\|\cdot\|}$, a <unital> <C-star algebra>. Its <self-adjoint> part is weak-operator dense in $M_{\mathrm{sa}}$: symmetrize any weak approximating net. For a convex subset, strong and weak operator closures agree. To see this explicitly, separation from a strong <closure> supplies a continuous real functional on finitely many image <vectors>, hence one of the form $\operatorname{Re}\sum_j\langle a\xi_j,\eta_j\rangle$, which is also weak-operator continuous. The <Hahn-Banach separation theorem> would then separate the point from the weak <closure> as well. Thus every <self-adjoint> $x\in M$ is a strong limit of <self-adjoint> $a_i\in C$, initially without a common <norm> bound.

The needed functional-calculus <continuity> follows directly from <resolvents>. The <resolvent identity> gives
$$
(a_i-iI)^{-1}-(x-iI)^{-1}
=(a_i-iI)^{-1}(x-a_i)(x-iI)^{-1}.
$$
The first factor has <norm> at most one, so the right side tends strongly to zero. The same holds at $-i$. Both <resolvent> nets are uniformly bounded, so their products converge strongly. Their scalar functions generate a <self-adjoint> algebra separating points of $\mathbb R$ and vanishing at infinity. The <Stone-Weierstrass theorem>, applied to the one-point compactification, makes this algebra uniformly dense in $C_0(\mathbb R)$. Uniform approximation and the functional-calculus <norm> bound therefore show
$$
f(a_i)\longrightarrow f(x)\text{ strongly for every }f\in C_0(\mathbb R).
$$
This proves <strong continuity of functional calculus through resolvents> without an unjustified uniform bound on the original $a_i$.

If $x=x^*$ and $\|x\|\leq1$, choose a real compactly supported continuous $f$ equal to the identity on $[-1,1]$ and bounded in absolute value by one. Then $f(a_i)\in C$ are <self-adjoint> contractions and converge strongly to $f(x)=x$. For a positive contraction $x$, choose instead $0\leq f\leq1$ equal to the identity on $[0,1]$; this gives positive contractions. These prove the two restricted versions for $C$.

For an arbitrary contraction $x\in M$, apply the <self-adjoint> result in the <matrix algebra> $M_2(C)$ to
$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\x^*&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|X\|=\|x\|\leq1.
$$
The strong <closure> of $M_2(C)$ is $M_2(M)$, since finitely many entries may be approximated simultaneously. The upper-right entries $c_i$ of the approximating <self-adjoint> contraction <matrices> belong to $C$, satisfy $\|c_i\|\leq1$, and converge strongly to $x$. The lower-left entries are $c_i^*$ and converge strongly to $x^*$. This proves the full strong-star assertion for $C$.

Finally transfer the bounds from $C$ to the possibly nonclosed algebra $A$. A contraction $c\in C$ has an approximant $b\in A$ with $\|b-c\|<\varepsilon$, so $b/(1+\varepsilon)$ is an $A$-contraction within $2\varepsilon$ of $c$. <Self-adjoint> approximants are obtained by symmetrizing first. For a positive contraction, approximate $c^{1/2}$ by $b\in A$ and use the positive contraction $b^*b/(1+\varepsilon)^2$, which tends in <norm> to $c$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Combining these <norm> approximations with each finite-vector strong approximation proves all versions for $A$. The reverse inclusions follow because the relevant <norm> bounds and the inequalities defining a <positive operator> are preserved by strong limits.

If $D_j$ has rank $r_j$, the left orbit has dimension $n_jr_j$ and the right orbit dimension $m_jr_j$. Cyclicity on both sides therefore forces $r_j=m_j=n_j$. Every $D_j$ is square and invertible.

Right multiplication by a suitable unitary is a unitary change of this identification commuting with the left algebra. The polar decomposition of each $D_j$ therefore lets us assume $D_j>0$. In one such block, for an arbitrary <matrix> $X=aD$, the <Tomita operator> is
$$
S(X)=D^{-1}X^*D.
$$
Its polar factors are
$$
\boxed{J(X)=X^*,\qquad \Delta^{1/2}(X)=DXD^{-1},\qquad
\Delta(X)=D^2XD^{-2}.}
$$
Indeed, $J$ is an antiunitary involution and $J\Delta^{1/2}(X)=D^{-1}X^*D$. The fact that $\Delta^{1/2}$ is a <positive operator> is transparent in a <basis> diagonalizing $D$: it multiplies the <matrix> unit $E_{kl}$ by the positive ratio $d_k/d_l$. These are therefore the unique polar factors of $S$.

Finally, if $L_a$ denotes left multiplication,
$$
(JL_aJ)(X)=(aX^*)^*=Xa^*.
$$
As $a$ ranges over the full <matrix> block, these are exactly its right multiplications. Applying this on every block and transporting back by the unitary identifications proves
$$
\boxed{JAJ=A'.}
$$
This also establishes the required modular definitions intrinsically, independent of the chosen <matrix> coordinates.

A bounded <self-adjoint operator> $T$ is a <positive operator> when $\langle T\xi,\xi\rangle\geq0$ for every $\xi$. Equivalently its <quadratic form> is nonnegative. We construct its <positive square root of an operator> directly and prove uniqueness.

If $T=0$ its positive square root is zero. Otherwise set $r=\|T\|$, $B=T/r$, and $C=I-B$. Then $0\leq B\leq I$ and $C$ is positive. It is also a contraction. Indeed, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for the positive form $\langle B\xi,\eta\rangle$ gives $\|B\xi\|^2\leq\langle B\xi,\xi\rangle$, and hence
$$
\|C\xi\|^2=\|\xi\|^2-2\langle B\xi,\xi\rangle+\|B\xi\|^2\leq\|\xi\|^2.
$$
The scalar binomial series has the form
$$
\sqrt{1-z}=1-\sum_{n\geq1}c_nz^n,\qquad
c_n=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n(2n-1)}>0,\qquad\sum_{n\geq1}c_n=1.
$$
The last equality follows by taking $z\uparrow1$ in the nonnegative series. Therefore
$$
S=\sqrt r\left(I-\sum_{n\geq1}c_nC^n\right)
$$
converges in <operator norm>. Every $C^n$ is a positive contraction: its even powers are squares, its odd powers have form $C^kCC^k$, and their <norms> are at most one. Each partial sum for $S/\sqrt r$ is consequently positive, since it is at least $(1-\sum_{n\leq N}c_n)I$. The limit is positive. Multiplying the absolutely convergent series and using the scalar coefficient identity gives $S^2=r(I-C)=T$. This construction also shows that $S$ is a <norm> limit of polynomials in $T$ and thus commutes with every operator commuting with $T$.

Now suppose $R$ is another positive square root. It commutes with $T=R^2$, hence with $S$. Thus
$$
(R-S)(R+S)=R^2-S^2=0.
$$
The difference vanishes on the range of $R+S$ and on its <closure>. It also vanishes on its <kernel>: if $(R+S)\xi=0$, the fact that $R$ and $S$ are <positive operators> gives $\langle R\xi,\xi\rangle=\langle S\xi,\xi\rangle=0$, and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for their positive forms implies $R\xi=S\xi=0$. Since $R+S$ is <self-adjoint>, the orthogonal sum of its <kernel> and closed range is all of $H$. Therefore $R=S$, proving
$$
\boxed{T\geq0\Longrightarrow\text{a unique }T^{1/2}\geq0\text{ with }(T^{1/2})^2=T.}
$$

One implication is immediate: a unitary module equivalence makes each space an isometric copy of the entire other space, which is closed and invariant. For the converse, choose isometric intertwiners $u:H_1\to H_2$ and $v:H_2\to H_1$ supplied by the two embeddings. Their ranges are closed reducing subspaces. We construct the unitary, proving the <Schröder-Bernstein theorem for Hilbert space modules> in this setting.

$$
K=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}w^nD
$$
is a closed reducing submodule of $H_1$, and $K=D\oplus wK$. Its <orthogonal complement> lies in $vH_2$, because it is orthogonal to $D$. Combining these decompositions gives
$$
vH_2=wK\oplus K^\perp.
$$
Apply the inverse isometry $v^*$ on $vH_2$ to obtain
$$
H_2=uK\oplus v^*K^\perp.
$$
The operator
$$
\boxed{W\xi=u\xi\quad(\xi\in K),\qquad
W\xi=v^*\xi\quad(\xi\in K^\perp)}
$$
is therefore an isometry onto $H_2$: it is isometric on each orthogonal summand and their images are the two orthogonal summands just exhibited. Both pieces intertwine the action of $M$, as do the <projections> onto $K$ and $K^\perp$, so $W$ is a unitary module equivalence. This proves the reverse implication with no finiteness assumption on either module.
