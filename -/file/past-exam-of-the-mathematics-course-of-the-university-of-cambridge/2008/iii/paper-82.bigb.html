= Solution
{parent=a}

The limiting <polynomial> has a <double root> at $x=-1$, so two roots require a <Puiseux series>. For positive $\epsilon$, put $\delta=\sqrt\epsilon$ and $x=-1+a\delta+b\delta^2+\cdots$. Substitution and <dominant balance> give
$$
0=(a^2-1)\delta^2+(2ab+3a)\delta^3+O(\delta^4).
$$
Thus $a=\pm1$ and $b=-3/2$. The two finite roots have the three-term <asymptotic expansions>
$$
\boxed{x_\pm=-1\pm\epsilon^{1/2}-\frac32\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{3/2}).}
$$
This is <square-root splitting of a double polynomial root>. For negative $\epsilon$, the same formulas apply with a chosen complex square root and describe a <complex conjugate> pair.

The third root is a <divergent algebraic root in a singular perturbation>. Set $x=X/\epsilon$: the equation for $X$ is $X^3+X^2+2\epsilon X+\epsilon^2=0$. Its nonzero limiting root is $X=-1$. Substituting $X=-1+A\epsilon+B\epsilon^2+\cdots$ yields $A=2$ and $B=3$, hence
$$
\boxed{x_L=-\epsilon^{-1}+2+3\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
As an independent check, <Vieta formulas> give the sum of roots as $-1/\epsilon$: the two finite-root expansions sum to $-2-3\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$, giving the same large-root expansion.

= Solution

For fixed $x>1$, write $a=\sqrt{x^2-1}$ and $A=x+a$. The maximum of the integrand occurs at $\theta=0$, and
$$
\log(x+a\cos\theta)=\log A-\frac{a}{2A}\theta^2+O(\theta^4).
$$
Here is the required endpoint <Laplace method> derivation. Outside a fixed neighborhood of zero, the integrand is exponentially smaller than $A^n$. Inside that neighborhood, use $\theta=(A/(na))^{1/2}u$. The quadratic term gives $e^{-u^2/2}$ and the higher terms vanish in the scaled limit. The <Gaussian integral> on the half-line is $\sqrt{\pi/2}$, so
$$
\boxed{P_n(x)\sim\frac{(x+\sqrt{x^2-1})^{n+1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sqrt{x^2-1}}},\qquad x>1\ \text{fixed}.}
$$
This is the <hyperbolic Debye asymptotic for Legendre polynomials>. At the endpoint the integrand is identically one, giving **$P_n(1)=1$ exactly**.

The localization width in the <Laplace method> is $O((na)^{-1/2})$. It becomes order one when $n\sqrt{x-1}=O(1)$, so the <Legendre boundary layer at x equals one> has **$q=2$**. With $x=1+\nu/n^2$ and fixed $\nu\ge0$,
$$
n\log(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}\cos\theta)=\sqrt{2\nu}\cos\theta+O(n^{-1})
$$
uniformly in $\theta$. The boundary-layer answer is therefore
$$
\boxed{P_n(1+\nu/n^2)\sim\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{\sqrt{2\nu}\cos\theta}\,d\theta=I_0(\sqrt{2\nu}).}
$$
This <real integral representation of the modified Bessel function I0> gives one at $\nu=0$, agreeing with the endpoint value. Applying the same endpoint <Laplace method> to the integral for $I_0(z)$ gives $I_0(z)\sim e^z/\sqrt{2\pi z}$. Thus, as $\nu\to\infty$, the inner result is $e^{\sqrt{2\nu}}/\sqrt{2\pi\sqrt{2\nu}}$. The outer result has precisely this limit in the overlap $1\ll\nu\ll n$. This proves both matches rather than extending the fixed-$x$ formula directly to $x=1$.

For the derivatives, the <power series> $I_0(\sqrt{2\nu})=1+\nu/2+\nu^2/16+\cdots$ suggests their leading orders. To justify differentiation, expand the original integral itself near $x=1$. Its first three even cosine moments give
$$
P_n(x)=x^n+\frac{n(n-1)}4x^{n-2}(x^2-1)
+\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}{64}x^{n-4}(x^2-1)^2+O((x-1)^3).
$$
Odd cosine moments vanish, and higher even moments start at cubic order. Expanding at $x=1$ yields
$$
\boxed{P_n'(1)=\frac{n(n+1)}2\sim\frac{n^2}2,\qquad
P_n''(1)=\frac{(n-1)n(n+1)(n+2)}8\sim\frac{n^4}8.}
$$
For large $n$, the graph starts at $(1,1)$ with a very large positive slope and positive <curvature>. It rises through the $n^{-2}$ <boundary layer> and then follows the rapidly increasing outer <asymptotic expansion>. The sketch below shows both scales.

The <method of multiple scales> applies when a small perturbation acts cumulatively over a long time, as in weakly perturbed <harmonic oscillators> or slowly modulated waves. A regular <asymptotic expansion> can contain <secular terms>, such as $\epsilon t\sin(\omega t)$, which cease to be small on the time scale of interest. Introduce independent fast and slow times, for example $t$ and $T=\epsilon t$, and allow the leading <amplitude> and phase to depend on $T$. The <solvability condition in the method of multiple scales> removes resonant forcing at each order and determines that slow evolution. For the <Mathieu equation>, these <amplitude> equations reveal <parametric resonance> and its instability intervals.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Set $T=\epsilon t$ and use the <method of multiple scales>:
$$
y=y_0(t,T)+\epsilon y_1(t,T)+\cdots,\qquad
y_0=a(T)\cos(t/2)+b(T)\sin(t/2).
$$
Since $\omega^2=1/4+k\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$, the first correction satisfies
$$
(\partial_t^2+1/4)y_1=-2\partial_t\partial_Ty_0-(k+\cos t)y_0.
$$
The resonant cosine and sine coefficients must vanish to avoid <secular terms>. Using the product-to-sum formulas gives
$$
\boxed{a_T=(k-1/2)b,\qquad b_T=-(k+1/2)a.}
$$
The remaining forcing has <frequency> $3/2$ and permits the bounded particular correction $y_1=(a/4)\cos(3t/2)+(b/4)\sin(3t/2)$. The two slow <amplitudes> carry the two arbitrary constants of the original equation.

For an explicit general leading solution, put
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&k-1/2\\-(k+1/2)&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\lambda^2=1/4-k^2,\qquad M^2=\lambda^2I.
$$
The <matrix exponential> gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a(T)\\b(T)\end{pmatrix}
=\left[\cosh(\lambda T)I+\frac{\sinh(\lambda T)}{\lambda}M\right]
\begin{pmatrix}a(0)\\b(0)\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\boxed{y(t)=a(\epsilon t)\cos(t/2)+b(\epsilon t)\sin(t/2)+O(\epsilon).}
$$
The error is uniform on bounded intervals of $T$, for bounded initial data. When $\lambda$ is imaginary this expression uses ordinary sine and cosine; at $\lambda=0$ its continuous limit is $I+TM$.

The <primary instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator> has a positive slow <eigenvalue> precisely when
$$
\boxed{-\frac12<k<\frac12,\qquad
\text{growth rate}=\epsilon\sqrt{\frac14-k^2}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
These are the leading edges, rather than an exact finite-$\epsilon$ interval. At the leading endpoints the <amplitude> matrix is a <nilpotent matrix>, so the leading solution can grow linearly in $T$ and does not decide the exact exponential instability. For completeness, set $\omega=1/2+\beta\epsilon+\gamma\epsilon^2$. The periodic or antiperiodic edge calculation uses a pure cosine when $\beta=-1/2$ and a pure sine when $\beta=1/2$, with the third-harmonic correction just found. The next resonant coefficient is $-(\gamma+3/8)y_0$, so
$$
\omega_-=\frac12-\frac\epsilon2-\frac38\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^3),\qquad
\omega_+=\frac12+\frac\epsilon2-\frac38\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^3).
$$
Thus the exact growing interval is between these shifted edges. In particular, for positive small $\epsilon$ the fixed leading value $k=-1/2$ lies just inside it, whereas $k=1/2$ lies just outside. Interior detunings bounded away from the edges are already resolved by the leading <method of multiple scales>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Away from the first <parametric resonance>, one forcing interaction changes a harmonic <frequency> by one and produces no resonant coupling of the two conjugate oscillations. Two interactions can couple <frequency> $+1$ to <frequency> $-1$, so the next <parametric resonance> is near $\omega=1$ and its effect first appears at order $\epsilon^2$. This gives **$p=2$** and the appropriate slow time $T=\epsilon^2t$.

Write $\omega=1+\kappa\epsilon^2$ and $y_0=a(T)\cos t+b(T)\sin t$. The first correction satisfies $(\partial_t^2+1)y_1=-\cos t\,y_0$, whose bounded particular solution is
$$
y_1=-\frac a2+\frac a6\cos2t+\frac b6\sin2t.
$$
At second order,
$$
(\partial_t^2+1)y_2=-2\partial_t\partial_Ty_0-2\kappa y_0-\cos t\,y_1.
$$
The resonant part of $-\cos t\,y_1$ is $(5a/12)\cos t-(b/12)\sin t$. Applying the <solvability condition in the method of multiple scales> gives
$$
\boxed{a_T=(\kappa+1/24)b,\qquad b_T=(5/24-\kappa)a.}
$$
The squared slow <eigenvalue> is
$$
\lambda^2=(\kappa+1/24)(5/24-\kappa)
=\frac1{64}-(\kappa-1/12)^2.
$$
The <second instability tongue of a weak Mathieu oscillator> therefore has the leading interval
$$
\boxed{-\frac1{24}<\kappa<\frac5{24},\qquad
1-\frac{\epsilon^2}{24}+o(\epsilon^2)<\omega<1+\frac{5\epsilon^2}{24}+o(\epsilon^2).}
$$
Here the two little-$o$ terms describe the respective exact edge locations; the strict inequality in $\kappa$ is the leading interior criterion. In particular $\omega=1$ is unstable, with physical growth rate $\epsilon^2\sqrt5/24+o(\epsilon^2)$. The general leading solution for bounded $T$ again has $y_0=a(T)\cos t+b(T)\sin t$, where the <matrix exponential> of the displayed two-by-two <amplitude> system evolves arbitrary $a(0),b(0)$. Adding $\epsilon y_1$ identifies the first nonresonant harmonic correction. The leading expression alone has error $O(\epsilon)$ on $t=O(\epsilon^{-2})$.

For comparison, at fixed $\omega\ne1/2,1$, write $y_0=A(T)e^{i\omega t}+\overline A(T)e^{-i\omega t}$. The first forced harmonics have coefficients $A/[2(2\omega+1)]$ and $A/[2(1-2\omega)]$. Their return to the original harmonic at the next order gives
$$
A_T=\frac{i}{4\omega(1-4\omega^2)}A.
$$
It is a phase correction, not <amplitude> growth. Consequently, away from the first two resonance neighborhoods, a general leading solution valid for bounded $\epsilon^2t$ is
$$
y=C\cos(\Omega t)+D\sin(\Omega t)+O(\epsilon),\qquad
\Omega=\omega+\frac{\epsilon^2}{4\omega(1-4\omega^2)}.
$$
Narrower higher-order instability intervals are invisible on this time scale. The detuned resonant calculation above is needed near $\omega=1$ because both the phase shift and the conjugate-mode coupling then matter.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

<Floquet theory> suggests a sequence of instability tongues near
$$
\boxed{\omega=\frac m2,\qquad m=1,2,3,\ldots,\quad
\text{tongue width }O(\epsilon^m).}
$$
At these limiting <frequencies> the unperturbed <Floquet multipliers> $e^{\pm2\pi i\omega}$ coincide. Each multiplication by $\cos t$ changes a Fourier <frequency> by one, so $m$ forcing interactions are needed to couple $+m/2$ and $-m/2$. This explains both the sequence of <parametric resonances> and the successive powers of $\epsilon$ in their widths.

There is no implication that the exact half-integers themselves lie in every finite-$\epsilon$ growing interval. The tongue centers generally shift by $O(\epsilon^2)$, which exceeds the width when $m>2$. It is the narrow intervals near the shifted centers that contain exponentially growing solutions. <Frequencies> staying a fixed distance from every half-integer are stable for sufficiently small $\epsilon$.

= Solution

Retaining the nonlinear factor $f$ determines the order-$\epsilon$ forcing and the matching constants. Write $\delta=\sqrt\epsilon$. The inner leading equation and its <boundary condition> at $r=1$ give $f_0=C(1-r^{-1/2})$. Matching to the far-field value fixes $C=1$.

The tail $-r^{-1/2}$ becomes $-\delta x^{-1/2}$ under $x=\epsilon r$, so a regular expansion in integer powers of $\epsilon$ cannot suffice. This is a <matched expansion with square-root and logarithmic corrections>. Define
$$
E(x)=\int_x^\infty t^{-3/2}e^{-t}\,dt,
\qquad E'=-x^{-3/2}e^{-x},
\qquad E=2x^{-1/2}-2\sqrt\pi+2\sqrt x+O(x^{3/2}).
$$
The <exponential integral> here is normalized by the displayed definite integral. <Integration by parts> also gives $E(x)=2e^{-x}/\sqrt x-2\sqrt\pi\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt x$, using the <complementary error function>.

For the outer <asymptotic expansion>, put $F(x)=f(x/\epsilon)$ and
$$
F=1+\delta F_1+\delta^2F_2+\cdots,\qquad
L g=g''+\left(\frac{3}{2x}+1\right)g'.
$$
The transformed equation gives $LF_1=0$ and $LF_2=-F_1F_1'$, with both corrections vanishing at infinity. Matching the leading inner tail determines $F_1=-E/2$. Multiplying the equation for $F_2$ by its <integrating factor> yields
$$
\left(x^{3/2}e^xF_2'\right)'=\frac14 E(x).
$$
Fix the particular solution $G$ unambiguously by
$$
I(x)=\int_0^x E(s)\,ds
=\sqrt\pi\operatorname{erf}\sqrt x+xE(x),\qquad
G(x)=-\int_x^\infty t^{-3/2}e^{-t}I(t)\,dt.
$$
Then $(x^{3/2}e^xG')'=E$, $G(\infty)=0$, and $G$ has no $x^{-1/2}$ term at zero. The general outer correction is $F_2=\beta E+G/4$, where
$$
G(x)=4\log x+\mu+O(\sqrt x).
$$
The logarithm in this <asymptotic expansion> explains why the inner expansion needs an $\epsilon\log\epsilon$ term.

For the inner <asymptotic expansion>, all corrections larger than order $\epsilon$ solve the homogeneous leading equation and vanish at $r=1$. At order $\epsilon$, the forcing is
$$
f_2''+\frac{3}{2r}f_2'=-f_0f_0'
=-\frac12r^{-3/2}+\frac12r^{-2}.
$$
Integration gives the particular solution $1-\sqrt r+\log r$, which vanishes at $r=1$. Thus write
$$
f=(1+\delta A+\epsilon C\log\epsilon+\epsilon B)(1-r^{-1/2})
+\epsilon(1-\sqrt r+\log r)+o(\epsilon).
$$
Express this inner expansion in $x=\epsilon r$ and compare the terms that survive in the overlap $\epsilon\ll x\ll1$. The order-$\delta$ constant gives $A=\sqrt\pi$. At order $\epsilon$, the term $C\log\epsilon+\log r$ becomes $\log x+(C-1)\log\epsilon$, so $C=1$. The singular term $-\epsilon A/\sqrt x$ matches $2\epsilon\beta/\sqrt x$, giving $\beta=-\sqrt\pi/2$. Finally, the regular constants give $B+1=\pi+\mu/4$.

The requested expansions, including every correction through order $\epsilon$, are therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
f(r,\epsilon)={}&\left[1+\sqrt{\pi\epsilon}+\epsilon\log\epsilon
+\epsilon\left(\pi+\frac\mu4-1\right)\right](1-r^{-1/2})\\
&+\epsilon(1-\sqrt r+\log r)+o(\epsilon),\qquad r\ \text{fixed},\\
f(x/\epsilon,\epsilon)={}&1-\frac{\sqrt\epsilon}{2}E(x)
+\epsilon\left[-\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}E(x)+\frac14G(x)\right]
+o(\epsilon),\qquad x>0\ \text{fixed}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The inner formula satisfies the <boundary condition> at $r=1$ term by term; the outer formula satisfies the condition at infinity. Each formula has its own stated region of validity, and the <matched asymptotic expansion> relates them in their overlap.

The constant can also be made explicit. <Integration by parts> in the definition of $G$ gives $G=-EI-\int_x^\infty E(t)^2\,dt$, and $E(x)I(x)\to8$. Squaring the <complementary error function> expression for $E$ separates the integral into three elementary pieces:
$$
\int_x^\infty E^2
=4\int_x^\infty\frac{e^{-2t}}t\,dt
-8\sqrt\pi\int_x^\infty\frac{e^{-t}}{\sqrt t}\operatorname{erfc}\sqrt t\,dt
+4\pi\int_x^\infty\operatorname{erfc}^2\sqrt t\,dt.
$$
The first piece is $-4\log x-4\gamma-4\log2+o(1)$, where $\gamma$ is the <Euler--Mascheroni constant>. Substitution $u=\sqrt t$ makes the second integral tend to $\sqrt\pi/2$, because $(\operatorname{erfc}u)'=-2e^{-u^2}/\sqrt\pi$. <Integration by parts> in the third integral gives $1/2-1/\pi$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mu=4\gamma+4\log2+2\pi-4,\qquad
B=\frac{3\pi}{2}+\gamma+\log2-2.}
$$
Equivalently, defining $G$ by its convergent integral already determines the same matching constant without needing this closed form.

Consider a nonreal <phase velocity> $c$, so $U-c$ never vanishes. Substitute $\phi=(U-c)\psi$ into the <Rayleigh equation for inviscid shear flow>. Differentiating cancels the two terms involving $U''$, leaving
$$
(U-c)\psi''+2U'\psi'-k^2(U-c)\psi=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\bigl[(U-c)^2\psi'\bigr]'-k^2(U-c)^2\psi=0.}
$$
For $k>0$, the wall condition on normal velocity gives $\phi=0$, hence $\psi=0$, at both walls. Multiply by $\overline\psi$ and use <integration by parts> to obtain the <weighted identity for Howard's semicircle theorem>:
$$
\int_{-y_0}^{y_0}(U-c)^2W\,dy=0,\qquad
W=|\psi'|^2+k^2|\psi|^2\ge0.
$$
For a nonzero <normal mode>, $N=\int W\,dy>0$. The imaginary and real parts of this identity give, respectively,
$$
-2c_i\int(U-c_r)W\,dy=0,\qquad
\int[(U-c_r)^2-c_i^2]W\,dy=0.
$$
Since $c_i\ne0$, these imply
$$
\boxed{\int UW\,dy=c_rN,\qquad
\int U^2W\,dy=(c_r^2+c_i^2)N.}
$$
The first equation says that $c_r$ is a positive <weighted mean> of $U$, and consequently $U_{\min}\le c_r\le U_{\max}$. The division by $c_i$ is essential: the weighted-mean assertion concerns nonreal modes. A neutral mode may have a <critical level> where $U=c$ and the substitution for $\psi$ is singular.

Pointwise, $(U-U_{\max})(U-U_{\min})\le0$. Integrating against the nonnegative weight $W$ and substituting both identities yields
$$
[c_r^2+c_i^2-(U_{\max}+U_{\min})c_r+U_{\max}U_{\min}]N\le0.
$$
Completing the square proves <Howard's semicircle theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\left(c_r-\frac{U_{\max}+U_{\min}}2\right)^2+c_i^2
\le\left(\frac{U_{\max}-U_{\min}}2\right)^2.}
$$
Thus all nonreal <phase velocities> lie in the closed disk with the real interval $[U_{\min},U_{\max}]$ as diameter. For the convention $e^{ik(x-ct)}$ and $k>0$, exponentially growing <normal modes> occupy its upper semicircle, while decaying modes occupy its lower semicircle.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Away from the velocity-profile corners, $U''=0$ and an unstable <normal mode> satisfies $\phi''-k^2\phi=0$. On the upper half of the jet write
$$
\phi=A\cosh ky+B\sinh ky\quad(0<y<1),\qquad
\phi=D e^{-k(y-1)}\quad(y>1).
$$
The exterior solution is selected by decay at infinity. From the given velocity components, the perturbation <pressure> is
$$
p=-(U-c)\phi'+U'\phi.
$$
Continuity of normal velocity and <pressure> therefore gives continuity of $\phi$ and <pressure matching at a piecewise-linear shear interface>:
$$
[(U-c)\phi'-U'\phi]=0,
\qquad (U-c)[\phi']=[U']\phi.
$$
At $y=1$ the slope of $U$ jumps from $-1$ to zero. Using $\phi'(1^+)=-k\phi(1)$ yields
$$
c\,[\phi'(1^-)+k\phi(1)]=\phi(1),\qquad
ck e^k(A+B)=A\cosh k+B\sinh k.
$$
The lower interface then follows by parity.

For the even <sinuous mode of a planar jet>, the derivative can jump at the center: being an <even function> does not require the one-sided derivative to vanish. The slope jump at $y=0$ is $-2$, whereas $[\phi']=2\phi'(0^+)$. Thus
$$
(1-c)\phi'(0^+)+\phi(0)=0,
\qquad A+k(1-c)B=0.
$$
Substitute $A=-k(1-c)B$ into the upper-interface relation. With $q=e^{-2k}$, division by $e^k$ and multiplication by $2k$ give
$$
2kc(1-k+kc)+k(1-c)(1+q)-(1-q)=0.
$$
Collecting powers of the <phase velocity> gives the required <dispersion relation>:
$$
\boxed{2k^2c^2+k(1-2k-e^{-2k})c-[1-k-(1+k)e^{-2k}]=0.}
$$
These equations can also be treated as a homogeneous two-by-two system in $A,B$, avoiding division by any potentially zero <amplitude> or phase-velocity factor.

For the odd <varicose mode of a planar jet>, $\phi(0)=0$, so $A=0$; the central <pressure matching at a piecewise-linear shear interface> is automatic. The upper-interface equation becomes $ck e^k=\sinh k$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{2kc-(1-e^{-2k})=0,\qquad
c=\frac{1-e^{-2k}}{2k}.}
$$
This <phase velocity> is real for every $k>0$, so the discrete <varicose mode of a planar jet> is neutral and there is no exponentially unstable odd mode.

To determine the unstable even modes, factor the reduced <discriminant> of the quadratic:
$$
\begin{aligned}
D(k)&=(2k-3)^2-2(2k+5)e^{-2k}+e^{-4k}\\
&=(2k-3-e^{-2k}-4e^{-k})(2k-3-e^{-2k}+4e^{-k}).
\end{aligned}
$$
The second factor vanishes at zero and has derivative $2(1-e^{-k})^2>0$ for $k>0$, so it is positive there. The first factor starts at $-8$, is strictly increasing, and tends to infinity. It therefore has one positive zero $k_c$. The even <phase velocities> are
$$
c_\pm=\frac{2k+e^{-2k}-1\pm\sqrt{D(k)}}{4k}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{\text{The sinuous branch is exponentially unstable exactly for }0<k<k_c,
\quad 2k_c-3-e^{-2k_c}-4e^{-k_c}=0,
\quad k_c\simeq1.832744.}
$$
For $0<k<k_c$, one member of the <complex conjugate> pair grows at rate $k c_i=\sqrt{-D(k)}/4$ and the other decays. At $k=k_c$ the speeds merge and there is no exponential growth; for $k>k_c$ both speeds are real. This is <inviscid instability of a triangular jet>. The unstable <sinuous mode of a planar jet> also satisfies <Howard's semicircle theorem> with $U_{\min}=0$ and $U_{\max}=1$: its speed lies in the upper half-disk centered at $1/2$ with radius $1/2$.
