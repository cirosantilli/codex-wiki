A <monad> on $\mathcal C$ consists of an <endofunctor> $T$ and <natural transformations> $\eta:1\to T$, $\mu:T^2\to T$ satisfying $\mu\,T\eta=\mu\,\eta T=1_T$ and $\mu\,T\mu=\mu\,\mu T$. Its <Eilenberg-Moore category> has objects $(X,a)$ with $a:TX\to X$, $a\eta_X=1_X$ and $a\mu_X=aTa$. A morphism $f:(X,a)\to(Y,b)$ satisfies $fa=bTf$. An <adjunction> $F\dashv G$, with unit $\eta$ and counit $\varepsilon$, induces $T=GF$, $\mu=G\varepsilon F$. It is a <monadic adjunction> if the <eilenberg-Moore comparison functor> $K(D)=(GD,G\varepsilon_D)$ is an equivalence with the <Eilenberg-Moore category>.

A fork $A\overset{p,q}{\rightrightarrows}B\overset{r}{\to}C$ is a <split coequalizer> if $rp=rq$ and there are $s:C\to B$, $t:B\to A$ with $rs=1$, $pt=1$ and $qt=sr$. It is indeed a <coequalizer>: if $hp=hq$, then $h=hpt=hqt=hsr$, so $h$ factors uniquely through $r$, which is split epic. Every <functor> preserves these splitting identities, making such <coequalizers> absolute.

The <precise monadicity theorem> states: for an <adjunction> $F:\mathcal C\rightleftarrows\mathcal D:G$, the comparison is an equivalence if and only if $G$ reflects <isomorphisms> and every pair in $\mathcal D$ whose $G$-image admits a <split coequalizer> has a <coequalizer> which $G$ preserves. Equivalently, the right adjoint creates these split-underlying <coequalizers>, with creation understood up to the canonical <isomorphism> of the prescribed underlying <coequalizer>. Here is a proof.

First consider the forgetful <functor> from <monad algebras>. It reflects <isomorphisms>: the inverse of an invertible algebra morphism also obeys the algebra-morphism identity, by multiplying that identity by the inverses. For an algebra pair with a split underlying <coequalizer> $r:B\to C$, all powers of $T$ preserve its <coequalizer>. If $b:TB\to B$ is the algebra structure on $B$, the map $rb$ coequalizes $Tp,Tq$, so there is a unique $c:TC\to C$ with
$$
cTr=rb.
$$
The unit law follows by composing with the <epimorphism> $r$: $c\eta_Cr=cTr\eta_B=rb\eta_B=r$. For associativity, naturality and the algebra law give
$$
c\mu_C T^2r=cTr\mu_B=rb\mu_B=rbTb=cT(rb)=cTcT^2r.
$$
Cancel the <epimorphism> $T^2r$ to obtain $c\mu_C=cTc$. Thus $(C,c)$ is an algebra and $r$ is an algebra morphism. Any algebra morphism coequalizing the pair factors through $r$ on underlying objects, and its factor is an algebra morphism by cancellation of the <epimorphism> $Tr$. This creates the <coequalizer>. Transporting these facts through an equivalence proves necessity.

For sufficiency, let $(X,a)$ be a $T$-algebra. In $\mathcal D$ consider the free pair
$$
FTX\ \underset{Fa}{\overset{\varepsilon_{FX}}{\rightrightarrows}}\ FX.
$$
Its underlying pair is $\mu_X,Ta:T^2X\rightrightarrows TX$, with <split coequalizer> $a:TX\to X$: the splittings are $s=\eta_X$ and $t=\eta_{TX}$, using the algebra unit law, the <monad> unit law and naturality of $\eta$. By hypothesis its <coequalizer> $q:FX\to D$ exists and is preserved. Identify $GD$ with $X$ by the unique <coequalizer> <isomorphism>, so $Gq=a$. Naturality of the counit makes $Kq$ an algebra morphism, hence the comparison structure $c$ on $GD$ satisfies $cTa=a\mu_X=aTa$. The map $Ta$ is split epic because $a\eta_X=1$, so $c=a$. Every algebra is therefore isomorphic to a comparison algebra.

For an actual object $D$, its counit $\varepsilon_D:FGD\to D$ is a <coequalizer> of the analogous free pair. It coequalizes that pair by counit naturality. Compare it with the <coequalizer> furnished by the hypothesis; applying $G$ gives an <isomorphism>, since both underlying arrows coequalize the same split pair. Reflection of <isomorphisms> makes the comparison map an <isomorphism>. This proves the counit's asserted <coequalizer> property.

Let $f:KD\to KE$ be an algebra morphism. Its transpose is $h_0=\varepsilon_EFf:FGD\to E$. The two composites of $h_0$ with the free pair have adjunct transposes $G\varepsilon_E\,Tf$ and $fG\varepsilon_D$, equal because $f$ is an algebra morphism. Consequently $h_0$ factors uniquely through $\varepsilon_D$ as $h:D\to E$. Applying $G$ gives $(Gh)G\varepsilon_D=fG\varepsilon_D$; the last factor is split epic, so $Gh=f$. Conversely any lift of $f$ must satisfy $h\varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_EFf$ by naturality and hence is unique. Thus $K$ is full and faithful, and the earlier construction makes it essentially surjective. The comparison is an equivalence, completing the theorem's proof.

Now take $G:\mathbf{CompHaus}\to\mathbf{Set}$. Its left adjoint exists by the permitted topological result; it can be realized as the <Stone-Čech compactification> of the discrete input set. A bijective <continuous map> from a <compact space> to a <Hausdorff space> is a <homeomorphism>, so $G$ reflects <isomorphisms>.

For continuous $f,g:X\rightrightarrows Y$ with a split underlying fork $q,s,t$, let $R$ be the relation of having the same $q$-image. We claim
$$
R=\{(f(x),f(x')):g(x)=g(x')\}.
$$
For the forward inclusion choose $x=t(y)$, $x'=t(y')$: $ft=1$ and $gt=sq$ supply the equality. For the reverse inclusion use $qf=qg$. The subset $\{(x,x'):g(x)=g(x')\}\subset X^2$ is closed, because $Y$ is Hausdorff, and compact. Its continuous image $R\subset Y^2$ is compact and hence closed. A closed <equivalence relation> on a <compact Hausdorff space> has a compact Hausdorff quotient; its quotient map is a <coequalizer> here, since it identifies exactly the generated relation. Its underlying set <coequalizer> is the specified one up to the unique <isomorphism>. Thus $G$ preserves the needed <coequalizers>, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{CompHaus}\rightleftarrows\mathbf{Set}\text{ is a monadic adjunction}.}
$$
The closedness argument is the <closed relation generated by a set-split pair of compact Hausdorff maps> lemma; the splitting maps themselves need not be continuous.

**Theorems matter at least as much as definitions: they show when a categorical description captures actual mathematical structure.** Definitions in <category theory> are exceptionally economical, but economy alone does not explain their applications.

An <adjunction> compresses a universal mapping property into natural hom-set bijections. The free-group <adjunction>, for example, records that assigning images to generators extends uniquely to a homomorphism. That is a useful definition, but proving that a free group exists and satisfies this property still requires mathematics. The definition organizes that proof rather than replaces it.

The <Yoneda lemma> offers a sharper example. A <representable functor> is easy to define; the theorem identifying <natural transformations> out of it with elements of another <functor> gives the definition its computational force. The representation of a set-valued <functor> as a <colimit> of representables is then a substantive consequence, and the theorem that <filtered colimits> commute with <finite limits> turns it into a criterion for finite-limit preservation. None of these conclusions follows merely from choosing attractive terminology.

Likewise a <monad> packages operations and their compatibility, while the <Eilenberg-Moore category> defines the corresponding algebras. The <Beck monadicity theorem> answers a different question: whether a given <category> is really recovered by that algebraic description. Applying it to <compact Hausdorff spaces> needs a topological argument about closed quotient relations. The definitions would not by themselves predict or justify the result.

Finally, <abelian categories> put <kernels in a category>, <cokernels in a category> and exactness into a setting where homological arguments can be reused. Some consequences, such as image factorization, become short because strong structural content has deliberately been included in the definition. Yet <left derived functors>, long exact sequences and the detection of projective objects supply information about obstructions to exactness which the terminology alone cannot provide.

Definitions and theorems therefore play complementary roles. A good definition states what should be invariant across examples; a theorem proves which constructions preserve it, which examples satisfy it, and what can be deduced from it. <Category theory> makes that partnership unusually visible, rather than making proofs less important.

The <comma category> $(A\downarrow F)$ has objects $(C,u)$ with $C\in\mathcal C$ and $u:A\to FC$ a set map. A morphism $(C,u)\to(D,v)$ is an arrow $h:C\to D$ such that $Fh\circ u=v$. Composition and identities are those of $\mathcal C$. For $A=1$, this is the <category of elements> $E$ of $F$: objects are $(C,x)$ with $x\in FC$ and arrows satisfy $Fh(x)=y$.

Associate to $(C,x)$ the covariant <representable functor> $H_C=\mathcal C(C,-)$. An arrow $h:(C,x)\to(D,y)$ of $E$ gives, by precomposition, $H_D\to H_C$. Thus this is a diagram $H:E^{\mathrm{op}}\to[\mathcal C,\mathbf{Set}]$. There is a compatible natural map $H_C\to F$ sending $f:C\to B$ to $Ff(x)$.

<Colimits> in this <functor category> are pointwise. At an object $B$, the induced map from the <colimit> sends a class represented by $(C,x,f:C\to B)$ to $Ff(x)$. It is surjective because $z\in FB$ is represented by $(B,z,1_B)$. It is injective because every representative with value $z$ is identified with that same canonical representative: the arrow $f:(C,x)\to(B,z)$ in $E$ becomes an arrow of the indexing diagram sending $1_B$ to $f$. These bijections are natural in $B$, proving
$$
\boxed{F\cong\operatorname*{colim}_{(C,x)\in E^{\mathrm{op}}}\mathcal C(C,-).}
$$
Smallness of $\mathcal C$ and set-valuedness of $F$ ensure that $E$ is small, so the displayed <colimit> is defined. This proves the introductory density request before the three equivalent conditions.

Assume $F$ preserves <finite limits>. Fix any set $A$ and a finite diagram of objects $(C_j,u_j)$ in $(A\downarrow F)$. Let $L$ be the <categorical limit> of its underlying diagram in $\mathcal C$, with projections $p_j$. Preservation gives $FL\cong\lim_jFC_j$. The maps $u_j:A\to FC_j$ form a compatible cone, so determine a unique $u:A\to FL$ with $Fp_j\circ u=u_j$.

The object $(L,u)$ is the <categorical limit> in the <comma category>. Indeed a cone from $(B,v)$ gives a unique underlying $h:B\to L$, and the identities $Fp_j Fh v=Fp_j u$ for all $j$ force $Fh v=u$ by the <categorical limit>'s uniqueness. For the empty diagram, $L$ is terminal and $FL$ is a one-element set, giving the unique map from $A$. Thus $(A\downarrow F)$ has all <finite limits>. This proves $\boxed{\text{(i)}\Rightarrow\text{(ii)}}$.

A <balanced category> is one in which every morphism that is both monic and epic is an <isomorphism>. If a <faithful functor> sends $f$ to an <isomorphism>, then $f$ is monic: equality $fg=fh$ becomes $FfFg=FfFh$, cancellation gives $Fg=Fh$, and faithfulness gives $g=h$. The same argument on the other side makes $f$ epic. Balance therefore makes $f$ an <isomorphism>. Thus a <faithful functor> out of a <balanced category> reflects <isomorphisms>.

Under the <adjunction>, the transpose of $Ff:FX\to FA$ is $\eta_A f:X\to GFA$. Hence if $F$ is faithful, $\eta_Af=\eta_Ag$ forces $Ff=Fg$ and then $f=g$, making every $\eta_A$ monic. Conversely, if the unit is pointwise monic and $Ff=Fg$, naturality gives $\eta_Bf=GFf\,\eta_A=GFg\,\eta_A=\eta_Bg$, whence $f=g$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F\text{ faithful}\iff\eta\text{ pointwise monic}.}
$$
We also use the standard <adjunction> fact with its justification: $F$ is full and faithful exactly when $\eta$ is invertible. Indeed the composite hom-set map $\mathcal C(X,A)\to\mathcal D(FX,FA)\cong\mathcal C(X,GFA)$ is postcomposition by $\eta_A$. If $F$ is full and faithful these maps are bijective; taking $X=GFA$ produces a right inverse, and taking $X=A$ gives injectivity and therefore a left inverse. Conversely an invertible unit makes these hom-set maps bijective.

For the final example, view the two-element ordered set $0<1$ as a <category> $\mathcal C$, and let $\mathcal D$ have one object and only its identity. The unique $F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D$ is left adjoint to the <functor> $G$ selecting $1$, since every $\mathcal C(x,1)$ is a singleton. Every arrow in an ordered-set <category> is monic, so the unit arrows $x\to1$ and the identity counit are monic. But $F$ is not full: $\mathcal C(1,0)$ is empty whereas $\mathcal D(F1,F0)$ is a singleton. The example does not contradict the upcoming equivalence because $\mathcal C$ is not balanced; its arrow $0\to1$ is both monic and epic but not invertible.

Since $\varepsilon_{FA}$ is monic, this <split epimorphism> is an <isomorphism>: cancelling it in $\varepsilon_{FA}(F\eta_A\varepsilon_{FA})=\varepsilon_{FA}$ gives $F\eta_A\varepsilon_{FA}=1$. Let $q:FA\to B$ be a <regular epimorphism>. Its adjunct factorization is $q=\varepsilon_BFh$ for $h:A\to GB$. A <regular epimorphism> is extremal: if a <coequalizer> $q$ factors through a <monomorphism> $m$, its other factor also coequalizes the defining pair, descends through $q$, and gives a right inverse for $m$; monicity then makes $m$ invertible. Since the full <endofunctor> subcategory contains $1$ and is closed under composition, $1,T^2,T^3$ all belong to it. Terminality supplies unique <natural transformations> $\eta:1\to T$ and $\mu:T^2\to T$. The <monad> unit laws compare arrows $T\to T$, both necessarily the unique endomorphism, namely the identity. The associativity law compares arrows $T^3\to T$, again necessarily equal. Every <monad> structure uses these two unique arrows, so $\boxed{T\text{ has a unique monad structure}}$.

For a <monad> $S$ with underlying <functor> in the subcategory, the unique $\alpha:S\to T$ is a <monad morphism>. Its unit and multiplication compatibility compare, respectively, arrows $1\to T$ and $S^2\to T$ and therefore follow from terminality. A $T$-algebra $a:TX\to X$ restricts to the $S$-algebra $b=a\alpha_X$. The unit law is immediate. For multiplication, naturality and the monad-morphism law give
$$
bSb=aTa\,T\alpha_X\,\alpha_{SX}=a\mu^T_X T\alpha_X\alpha_{SX}=a\alpha_X\mu^S_X=b\mu^S_X.
$$
An algebra morphism for $T$ also commutes with the restricted action by naturality of $\alpha$. Keeping underlying objects and arrows gives the required <functor> $\mathcal C^T\to\mathcal C^S$.

Now define $TA$ to be the set of <ultrafilters> on $A$, and for $f:A\to B$ define
$$
Tf(\mathcal U)=\{V\subseteq B:f^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal U\}.
$$
Inverse images preserve inclusions, intersections and complements, so this is an <ultrafilter>; composition of inverse images makes $T$ a <functor>. There are no <ultrafilters> on the empty set. Every <ultrafilter> on a disjoint binary union chooses exactly one summand, and restriction to that summand gives an <ultrafilter> there; extension back is inverse to restriction. Hence $T(A\amalg B)\cong TA\amalg TB$ canonically, and $T$ preserves finite <coproducts>, including the empty one.

Let $H$ be any other finite-coproduct-preserving <endofunctor>. For $x\in HA$, put
$$
\alpha_A(x)=\{U\subseteq A:x\in\operatorname{im}(H(U\hookrightarrow A))\}.
$$
The decomposition $A=U\amalg U^c$ and <coproduct> preservation show that exactly one of $U,U^c$ belongs. Refining two subsets into the four disjoint parts $U\cap V,U\setminus V,V\setminus U,(U\cup V)^c$ shows that an element lying in both indicated summands lies in their intersection summand. The same refinement for $U\subseteq V$ proves upward closure. Thus $\alpha_A(x)$ is an <ultrafilter>. For $f:A\to B$, decomposition by $f^{-1}(V)$ and its complement shows
$$
V\in\alpha_B(Hf(x))\iff f^{-1}(V)\in\alpha_A(x),
$$
proving naturality.

For uniqueness, suppose $\beta:H\to T$ is natural. Since $T1$ is a singleton, naturality on the two inclusions $1\to2$ forces $\beta_2$ to send every element in each of the two $H1$ summands of $H2$ to the corresponding <principal ultrafilter>. Apply naturality to the characteristic map $\chi_U:A\to2$. It gives $U\in\beta_A(x)$ precisely when $H\chi_U(x)$ lies in the selected summand, which by the decomposition is precisely when $x$ lies in $HU\subseteq HA$. Hence $\beta_A=\alpha_A$ for every $A$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T\text{ is terminal and }TA=\operatorname{Ult}(A).}
$$
This proves <ultrafilter functor>. For clarity, the resulting unit sends $a$ to its <principal ultrafilter>; multiplication sends an <ultrafilter> $\mathfrak U$ on $\operatorname{Ult}(A)$ to the <ultrafilter> whose members are $U$ with $\{\mathcal V:U\in\mathcal V\}\in\mathfrak U$. Naturality and the <monad> laws for these formulas also follow from the terminal construction.

An <abelian category> is an <additive category> with <kernels in a category> and <cokernels in a category> in which the canonical comparison from <coimage> to image of every morphism is an <isomorphism>. This is the standard image-coimage form of the definition, equivalently expressed by normality of every <monomorphism> and <epimorphism>. Additivity means abelian-group hom-sets with bilinear composition, a <zero object> and finite <biproducts>. A sequence is exact at an object when the image of the incoming morphism is the <kernel in a category> of the outgoing one.

For $f:X\to Y$, construct $q:X\to\operatorname{coim}f=\operatorname{coker}(\ker f)$ and $j:\operatorname{im}f=\operatorname{ker}(\operatorname{coker}f)\to Y$. The <universal properties> give $f=j\bar f q$: first $f$ kills its <kernel in a category> and factors through $q$; then the resulting map is killed by $\operatorname{coker}f$ because $q$ is epic, so it factors through $j$. The comparison $\bar f$ is invertible by the abelian axiom. <Cokernels in a category> are epic and <kernels in a category> monic, directly by uniqueness in their <universal properties>, so $e=\bar f q$ is epic and $j$ monic. Thus $\boxed{f=je\text{ is an epimorphism-monomorphism factorization}}$.

In a short exact sequence $0\to X\overset{i}{\to}Y\overset{p}{\to}Z\to0$, we have $i=\ker p$ and $p=\operatorname{coker}i$, up to the canonical <isomorphisms>. If $r:Y\to X$ retracts $i$, the map $1_Y-ir$ kills $i$ and factors as $sp$ for some $s:Z\to Y$. Composing with $p$ gives $psp=p$, so epicity gives $ps=1_Z$. Conversely if $ps=1_Z$, then $1_Y-sp$ is killed by $p$ and factors as $ir$. Composing with $i$ gives $iri=i$, so monicity gives $ri=1_X$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{i\text{ is split monic}\iff p\text{ is split epic}.}
$$
In that case $Y\cong X\oplus Z$.

For the derived-functor requests, use additive <right exact functors>, as customary in abelian homological algebra. An <abelian category with enough projectives> permits an augmented <projective resolution> $\cdots\to P_2\to P_1\to P_0\to X\to0$, obtained by repeatedly taking an <epimorphism> from a projective onto the previous <kernel in a category>. Apply $F$ and define
$$
L_nF(X)=H_n(FP_\bullet),\qquad L_0F(X)\cong F(X).
$$
Projective lifts construct maps of resolutions; the comparison and chain-homotopy results make the construction independent of choices and functorial. For a short exact sequence, the horseshoe construction produces a degreewise split short exact sequence of resolutions, hence of their images under additive $F$. Its <homology objects> give the long exact sequence
$$
\cdots\to L_2F(X)\to L_2F(Y)\to L_2F(Z)\to
L_1F(X)\to L_1F(Y)\to L_1F(Z)\to
F(X)\to F(Y)\to F(Z)\to0.
$$
This supplies the requested sketch; detailed comparison and functoriality proofs are not needed here.

A projective object $X$ has a resolution concentrated in degree zero, so $L_1F(X)=0$ for every such $F$. Conversely choose $0\to K\overset{i}{\to}P\to X\to0$ with $P$ projective, and take $F=\mathcal A(-,K)$ with target $\mathbf{Ab}^{\mathrm{op}}$. Precomposition makes this covariant to the <opposite category>. The usual left exactness of contravariant Hom, which follows directly from the <cokernel in a category> <universal property>, says exactly that this $F$ is <right exact> in that opposite target. If $L_1F(X)=0$, the long exact sequence and $L_1F(P)=0$ make $F(K)\to F(P)$ monic in $\mathbf{Ab}^{\mathrm{op}}$. Equivalently,
$$
\operatorname{Hom}(P,K)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(K,K),\qquad r\longmapsto ri,
$$
is epic, hence surjective, in <abelian groups>. The identity of $K$ has a preimage, giving a retraction of $i$. The sequence splits, and $X$ is a <direct summand> of $P$, hence projective: a lifting problem for $X$ extends along the projection $P\to X$, is solved for $P$, and restricts along the summand inclusion. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X\text{ projective}\iff L_1F(X)=0\text{ for every additive right exact }F.}
$$
This is <projectivity detected by all first left derived functors>. The following parts prove the three requested equivalent formulations of a <hereditary abelian category with enough projectives>.

0\to L_1F(X)\to L_1F(Y)\to L_1F(Z).
$$
It is exact because the preceding term $L_2F(Z)$ vanishes. This is precisely left exactness of $L_1F$, proving $\boxed{\text{(ii)}\Rightarrow\text{(iii)}}$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Assume $L_1F$ is left exact for every additive <right exact> $F$. For $0\to K\to P\to X\to0$ with $P$ projective, left exactness makes $L_1F(K)\to L_1F(P)=0$ a <monomorphism>. Hence $L_1F(K)=0$. Dimension shifting then gives $L_2F(X)=0$. As $X,F$ are arbitrary, (iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii), completing
$$
\boxed{\text{(i)}\iff\text{(ii)}\iff\text{(iii)}.}
$$
The argument also recovers projectivity of every such <kernel in a category> directly from the universal first-derived-functor criterion.
