Set $S=A\setminus\mathfrak m$, a <multiplicative subset> because $\mathfrak m$ is a <prime ideal>. The <quotient module> $B=A/\mathfrak a$ is a <cyclic module> generated by $\bar1$, so its <localization of a module> $S^{-1}B$ is zero precisely when $\bar1/1=0$. By the <vanishing criterion in a module localization>, this is equivalent to the existence of $s\in S$ with $s\bar1=0$ in $B$. The latter condition says $s\in\mathfrak a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{B_{\mathfrak m}=0\iff\mathfrak a\cap(A\setminus\mathfrak m)\ne\varnothing\iff\mathfrak a\not\subseteq\mathfrak m.}
$$
Explicitly, if $s\in\mathfrak a\setminus\mathfrak m$, multiplication by $s$ annihilates $B$ but becomes invertible after <localization>, forcing every localized element to be zero.

The forward implication is immediate: the <localization of a module> that is zero remains zero. Conversely, suppose $M\ne0$ and choose $0\ne x\in M$. Its <annihilator>
$$
J=\operatorname{Ann}_A(x)=\{a\in A:ax=0\}
$$
is a proper <ideal>, since $1\notin J$. Choose a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$ containing $J$. Then $x/1$ cannot be zero in $M_{\mathfrak m}$: otherwise the <vanishing criterion in a module localization> would give $sx=0$ for some $s\notin\mathfrak m$, contradicting $s\in J\subseteq\mathfrak m$. Thus some maximal <localization> is nonzero, proving
$$
\boxed{M=0\iff M_{\mathfrak m}=0\text{ for every maximal ideal }\mathfrak m.}
$$
In the suggested cyclic description, $Ax\cong A/J$ and part (a) gives $(Ax)_{\mathfrak m}\ne0$; the same fraction argument embeds it in $M_{\mathfrak m}$. This is <localization detects zero elements>, applied to an arbitrary <module>; finite generation is unnecessary.

Suppose $f$ is <injective>. If $f_{\mathfrak m}(x/s)=0$, some $t\notin\mathfrak m$ satisfies $tf(x)=f(tx)=0$. Injectivity gives $tx=0$, and the <vanishing criterion in a module localization> gives $x/s=0$. Hence every localized <module homomorphism> is <injective>.

Conversely, suppose all $f_{\mathfrak m}$ are <injective>. For $x\in\ker f$, its image $x/1$ in $M_{\mathfrak m}$ maps to zero and therefore is zero for every <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. The <annihilator> argument in part (b) implies $x=0$. Thus $\ker f=0$ and $f$ is <injective>. We have proved that <injectivity of a module map is detected at maximal localizations>:
$$
\boxed{f\text{ injective}\iff f_{\mathfrak m}\text{ injective for every maximal }\mathfrak m.}
$$
Equivalently, <exactness of localization> gives $(\ker f)_{\mathfrak m}\cong\ker(f_{\mathfrak m})$, and part (b) detects whether this <kernel> is zero.

Use the defining criterion that a <flat module> $E$ makes $u\otimes1_E$ <injective> for every <injective> <module homomorphism> $u$. By <right exactness of the tensor product>, preserving injections is equivalent to preserving every <short exact sequence>.

First assume $E$ is <flat> over $A$, and fix a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. Let $u:U\hookrightarrow V$ be an <injection> of $A_{\mathfrak m}$-modules. Regard it as an <injection> of $A$-modules. There are natural <module isomorphisms>
$$
U\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak m}}E_{\mathfrak m}\cong U\otimes_AE,\qquad V\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak m}}E_{\mathfrak m}\cong V\otimes_AE.
$$
For example, $u_0\otimes(e/s)$ maps to $s^{-1}u_0\otimes e$, with inverse $u_0\otimes e\mapsto u_0\otimes(e/1)$. These are well-defined because every $s\notin\mathfrak m$ already acts invertibly on $U$. Under these <isomorphisms> the tensor map is $u\otimes_A1_E$, which is <injective> by flatness. Thus $E_{\mathfrak m}$ is <flat> over $A_{\mathfrak m}$.

For the converse, assume every $E_{\mathfrak m}$ is <flat> over $A_{\mathfrak m}$ and take any <injection> $u:U\hookrightarrow V$ of $A$-modules. By part (c), each $u_{\mathfrak m}$ is <injective>. Since <localization commutes with tensor products>, the <localization> of $u\otimes_A1_E$ identifies with
$$
u_{\mathfrak m}\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak m}}1_{E_{\mathfrak m}}:U_{\mathfrak m}\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak m}}E_{\mathfrak m}\longrightarrow V_{\mathfrak m}\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak m}}E_{\mathfrak m}.
$$
It is <injective> by the assumed local flatness. Apply part (c) again, now to $u\otimes_A1_E$, to conclude that it is <injective> globally. Since $u$ was arbitrary, $E$ is <flat>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E\text{ is <flat> over }A\iff E_{\mathfrak m}\text{ is <flat> over }A_{\mathfrak m}\text{ for every maximal }\mathfrak m.}
$$
This proves <flatness is local> without any finite-generation hypothesis.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Take $B=\mathbb Z$, $N=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, and the <short exact sequence>
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
$$
Multiplication by two is <injective>, and the reduction map has image all of $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ and <kernel> $2\mathbb Z$, proving exactness. Any <module homomorphism> from $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ to $\mathbb Z$ is zero: its generator must map to an integer killed by two. On the other hand, a homomorphism from $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ to itself is determined arbitrarily by the image of its generator, so its Hom group is $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. Applying the <Hom functor> therefore gives
$$
0\longrightarrow0\longrightarrow0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
$$
**This sequence is not exact at $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$:** the incoming image is zero while the outgoing <kernel> is the whole nonzero group. In particular, the identity of $N$ cannot lift through the reduction map to a map $N\to\mathbb Z$.

For any <injection> $u:U\hookrightarrow V$, the <direct sum> decomposition $F=P\oplus Q$ gives natural <module isomorphisms>
$$
U\otimes_AF\cong(U\otimes_AP)\oplus(U\otimes_AQ),\qquad V\otimes_AF\cong(V\otimes_AP)\oplus(V\otimes_AQ).
$$
Under these, $u\otimes1_F$ is the block-diagonal map $(u\otimes1_P)\oplus(u\otimes1_Q)$. It is <injective> because $F$ is a <flat module>. If an element lies in the <kernel> of $u\otimes1_P$, insert zero in the other component; injectivity of the whole map forces that element to be zero. Hence $u\otimes1_P$ is <injective> for every $u$, which proves $\boxed{P\text{ is flat}}$. This establishes that <direct summands of flat modules are flat>, without a finite-generation assumption.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Here the Hom groups must be taken over $A$; the printed subscript $B$ in this part has no defined coefficient ring. For an arbitrary <module> $P$, form the <free module>
$$
F=\bigoplus_{p\in P}Ae_p,
$$
and define a <module homomorphism> $\pi:F\to P$ by $e_p\mapsto p$. Each element of $F$ has finite support, so this prescription extends uniquely and is well-defined. It is <surjective> because every $p\in P$ is the image of $e_p$. Thus every module is a quotient of a <free module>.

Let $K=\ker\pi$. The <short exact sequence> $0\to K\to F\xrightarrow{\pi}P\to0$ and the defining exactness condition for a <projective module> make
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_A(P,F)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_A(P,P),\qquad s\longmapsto\pi s
$$
<surjective>. Lift $1_P$ to a map $s:P\to F$. Then $\pi s=1_P$, so $s$ is <injective>. Every $f\in F$ has the decomposition
$$
f=(f-s\pi(f))+s\pi(f),
$$
whose first term lies in $K$ and second lies in $s(P)$. If $s(p)\in K$, applying $\pi$ gives $p=0$, so the intersection is zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{F=K\oplus s(P)\cong K\oplus P.}
$$
This is a <split short exact sequence> and proves that <projective modules are direct summands of free modules>, including modules that are not finitely generated.

First show that <free modules are flat>. If $F=\bigoplus_{j\in J}Ae_j$, distributivity of the <tensor product of modules> gives
$$
U\otimes_AF\cong\bigoplus_{j\in J}U.
$$
Explicitly, $u\otimes\sum_j a_je_j$ maps to $(a_ju)_j$, and the inverse sends a finitely supported tuple $(u_j)_j$ to $\sum_j u_j\otimes e_j$. For an <injection> $u:U\hookrightarrow V$, the tensor map is therefore the coordinatewise <injection> $\bigoplus_JU\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_JV$. This proves flatness for any free rank.

By part (c), a <projective module> $P$ is a <direct summand> of a <free module> $F$. The latter is flat by the preceding argument, and part (b) proves that its summand $P$ is <flat>. Therefore $\boxed{\text{every projective }A\text{-module is flat}}$. This proves <projective modules are flat> directly from the exactness and splitting definitions.

Choose $a_1,\ldots,a_d\in\mathfrak m$ whose classes form a <basis> of the <cotangent space of a local ring> $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, and put $J=(a_1,\ldots,a_d)$. Spanning gives $\mathfrak m=J+\mathfrak m^2$. Thus the <quotient module> $N=\mathfrak m/J$ satisfies $N=\mathfrak mN$. It is a <finitely generated module>, since $A$ is a <Noetherian ring>.

Here is the needed form of the <Nakayama lemma>, with proof. Choose generators $n_1,\ldots,n_r$ of $N$ and write $n_i=\sum_jc_{ij}n_j$ with $c_{ij}\in\mathfrak m$. For the column vector $n$, $(I-C)n=0$. Multiply by the adjugate to obtain $\det(I-C)n=0$. Since $\det(I-C)\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak m}$, this determinant is a <unit> in the <local ring> $A$, so every $n_i=0$. Therefore $N=0$, proving $\mathfrak m=J$. Conversely, any generators of $\mathfrak m$ must span its cotangent space, so at least $d$ are needed. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mu(\mathfrak m)=d,}
$$
Here $\mu(M)$ denotes the <minimal number of generators of a module> $M$.

For the additional minimum, the <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$ is itself $\mathfrak m$-primary, so it supplies an eligible <ideal> generated by $d$ elements. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\min\{\mu(Q):Q\text{ is }\mathfrak m\text{-primary}\}\leq d.}
$$
The minimum here is over <primary ideals>. **A bound $\mu(Q)\leq d$ for every $\mathfrak m$-primary <ideal> would be false.** Indeed, for $A=k[[u,v]]$ and $\mathfrak m=(u,v)$, $d=2$ but $Q=\mathfrak m^2=(u^2,uv,v^2)$ is primary and needs three generators. To verify primaryness, if $ab\in Q$ and $a\notin Q$, then $b$ cannot be a <unit>, so $b\in\mathfrak m$ and $b^2\in Q$. The three displayed monomials form a <basis> of $Q/\mathfrak mQ=\mathfrak m^2/\mathfrak m^3$, proving the lower bound of three, while they already generate $Q$. This illustrates that <primary ideals can need more generators than the maximal ideal>.

A <formal power series> $f=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$ is a <unit> exactly when its constant coefficient is a <unit> in $A$. If $fg=1$, constant coefficients give $a_0b_0=1$. Conversely, for invertible $a_0$, set
$$
b_0=a_0^{-1},\qquad b_n=-a_0^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^na_ib_{n-i}\quad(n\geq1).
$$
Then every positive-degree coefficient of $fg$ is zero and its constant coefficient is one, so $g=\sum b_nx^n$ is an inverse. This is a formal coefficient calculation and requires no convergence.

Since $A$ is a <local ring>, its nonunits are exactly $\mathfrak m$. The nonunits of $B$ are consequently the series with $a_0\in\mathfrak m$, which are exactly $I=\mathfrak mB+xB$: such a series is $a_0+xg$, and conversely an element of $I$ has constant coefficient in $\mathfrak m$. The map $B\to k$ taking the constant coefficient modulo $\mathfrak m$ is <surjective> with <kernel> $I$, so $I$ is a <maximal ideal>. Every proper <ideal> consists of nonunits and hence lies in $I$, proving that $I$ is the unique <maximal ideal>. Thus
$$
\boxed{B\text{ is local},\qquad\operatorname{Max}(B)=\{I\},\qquad B/I\cong k.}
$$
This proves the description of <formal power series over a local ring>.

To compute the <cotangent space of a formal power series ring>, expand
$$
I^2=\mathfrak m^2B+x\mathfrak mB+x^2B.
$$
Define the $k$-linear map
$$
\Theta:(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)\oplus k\longrightarrow I/I^2,\qquad(\bar a,\bar b)\longmapsto a+bx\pmod{I^2}.
$$
Changing a lift $a$ by $\mathfrak m^2$ or a lift $b$ by $\mathfrak m$ changes the image by $I^2$, so the map is well-defined. Every series in $I$ is congruent modulo $x^2B\subseteq I^2$ to its first two coefficients, proving surjectivity. If $a+bx\in I^2$, its constant coefficient lies in $\mathfrak m^2$ and its linear coefficient lies in $\mathfrak m$, so both input classes are zero. This proves injectivity. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I/I^2\cong(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)\oplus k,\qquad\dim_k(I/I^2)=d+1.}
$$
Finally, part (a) gives generators $a_1,\ldots,a_d$ of $\mathfrak m$, so $I=(a_1,\ldots,a_d,x)$. Use the <Krull height theorem> in its lecture form: in a <Noetherian ring>, a prime minimal over an <ideal> generated by $r$ elements has height at most $r$. The problem allows Noetherianity of $B$ to be assumed. Apply the theorem to $I$ itself, whose height is the <Krull dimension> of the <local ring> $B$. Hence $\boxed{\dim B=\operatorname{ht}(I)\leq d+1}$.

Since $A$ is a <regular local ring>, $\dim A=d$ is finite. Choose a chain of <prime ideals> of length $d$,
$$
\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\mathfrak p_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_d=\mathfrak m.
$$
Such a chain exists because the supremum defining this finite integer <Krull dimension> is attained; a maximal-length chain in a <local ring> ends at its <maximal ideal>.

For each $i$, the <ideal> $\mathfrak p_i$ is finitely generated because $A$ is <Noetherian>. This ensures that its extension $\mathfrak p_iB$ consists precisely of series all of whose coefficients lie in $\mathfrak p_i$: if $\mathfrak p_i=(b_1,\ldots,b_r)$, write each coefficient as $\sum_j b_jc_{jn}$ and gather them into the finitely many series $\sum_n c_{jn}x^n$. Thus coefficient reduction gives
$$
B/\mathfrak p_iB\cong(A/\mathfrak p_i)[[x]].
$$
The coefficient ring $A/\mathfrak p_i$ is an <integral domain>, and so is its <formal power series ring>: the first nonzero coefficient of a product is the product of the first nonzero coefficients of its factors. Hence each $\mathfrak p_iB$ is prime. Also $\mathfrak p_iB\cap A=\mathfrak p_i$, so the strict inclusions persist upon extension. Finally, $x\notin\mathfrak mB$, since its image in $B/\mathfrak mB\cong k[[x]]$ is nonzero. We obtain the prime chain
$$
\mathfrak p_0B\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_dB=\mathfrak mB\subsetneq\mathfrak mB+(x)=I
$$
of length $d+1$. Therefore $\dim B\geq d+1$. Together with part (b),
$$
\boxed{\dim A[[x]]=d+1.}
$$
Its <embedding dimension> is also $d+1$, by part (b), so $B$ is itself a <regular local ring>. This proves that <formal power series preserve regular local rings>.

Let $S=A\setminus\mathfrak p$, let $S$ act on $B$ through $\varphi$, and write $\mathfrak pB$ for the <extension of an ideal> generated by $\varphi(\mathfrak p)$. Since $\kappa(\mathfrak p)=S^{-1}(A/\mathfrak p)$, the <fiber ring> has the canonical presentations
$$
C=B\otimes_A\kappa(\mathfrak p)\cong S^{-1}(B/\mathfrak pB)\cong S^{-1}B/\mathfrak pS^{-1}B.
$$
The first <isomorphism> sends $b\otimes(\bar a/s)$ to $\varphi(a)b/s$ modulo $\mathfrak pB$. Its inverse sends the class of $b/s$ to $b\otimes(\bar1/s)$. The tensor balancing relation, reduction modulo $\mathfrak p$ and the fraction relations show that these maps are well-defined and inverse. Thus the description is canonical and does not depend on chosen representatives.

Suppose $\mathfrak q$ is a <prime ideal> of $B$ with $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)=\mathfrak p$. Then $\mathfrak pB\subseteq\mathfrak q$ and $\mathfrak q$ is disjoint from $\varphi(S)$. Its <localization> $S^{-1}\mathfrak q$ is a proper prime, since the quotient is the <localization> of the <integral domain> $B/\mathfrak q$. It contains $\mathfrak pS^{-1}B$, and therefore gives a prime
$$
\mathfrak q\longmapsto\mathfrak Q=S^{-1}\mathfrak q/\mathfrak pS^{-1}B\quad\text{in }C.
$$
Conversely, for a prime $\mathfrak Q\subset C$, take its contraction $\mathfrak q$ under the <ring homomorphism> $\psi:B\to C$, $b\mapsto b\otimes1$. Contraction preserves primality. Every $\varphi(a)$ with $a\in\mathfrak p$ maps to zero, while every $\varphi(s)$ with $s\in S$ maps to a <unit> and hence cannot belong to a proper prime. Thus $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)=\mathfrak p$.

These constructions are inverse. For a prime disjoint from $\varphi(S)$, the fraction $b/1$ belongs to its localized extension exactly when some $s\in S$ has $\varphi(s)b\in\mathfrak q$; primality then implies $b\in\mathfrak q$. In the other direction, every element of $C$ has a representative $b/s$, and membership in $\mathfrak Q$ is equivalent to membership of $b/1$ because the denominator is a <unit>. Hence $\mathfrak Q$ is recovered by extending its contraction and quotienting. We have proved the canonical <fiber of the map on spectra> correspondence:
$$
\boxed{\{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}B:\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)=\mathfrak p\}\ \cong\ \operatorname{Spec}(B\otimes_A\kappa(\mathfrak p)).}
$$
If a denominator maps to zero, the fiber ring is the zero ring and both sets are empty; the same argument includes this case.

Let $\mathfrak q$ be a <prime ideal> of $C$. Its <quotient ring> $D=C/\mathfrak q$ is a nonzero <integral domain> and a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space. For $0\ne a\in D$, multiplication by $a$ is an <injective> $K$-linear map $D\to D$, because $D$ has no zero divisors. An <injective> <endomorphism> of a <finite-dimensional vector space> is <surjective>. In particular $ab=1$ for some $b\in D$. Every nonzero element is therefore invertible, so $D$ is a <field> and $\mathfrak q$ is a <maximal ideal>.

To prove finiteness, select any $r$ distinct <maximal ideals> $\mathfrak m_1,\ldots,\mathfrak m_r$. Distinct <maximal ideals> are comaximal: $\mathfrak m_i+\mathfrak m_j$ strictly contains $\mathfrak m_i$, so must equal $C$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> gives a <surjection>
$$
C\longrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^rC/\mathfrak m_i.
$$
Here is the needed surjectivity explicitly. For each $j\ne i$, choose $a_{ij}\in\mathfrak m_j$ with $a_{ij}\equiv1\pmod{\mathfrak m_i}$, using comaximality. Then $e_i=\prod_{j\ne i}a_{ij}$ is one modulo $\mathfrak m_i$ and zero modulo the other selected <ideals>. Given any residue tuple represented by $b_i$, the element $\sum_i b_ie_i$ maps to that tuple. Thus the displayed map really is <surjective>.

Each nonzero <field> quotient has $K$-dimension at least one, so
$$
r\leq\sum_{i=1}^r\dim_K(C/\mathfrak m_i)\leq\dim_KC.
$$
There cannot be more than $\dim_KC$ <maximal ideals>, because otherwise one could select a finite list exceeding this bound. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Spec}C=\operatorname{MaxSpec}C,\qquad |\operatorname{MaxSpec}C|\leq\dim_KC<\infty.}
$$
This proves the description of the <spectrum of a finite-dimensional commutative algebra>. If $C=0$, its spectrum is empty and the same conclusion holds.

Choose algebra generators $b_1,\ldots,b_n$ for $B$ over $A$. Because the extension is integral, each $b_i$ satisfies a monic equation
$$
b_i^{e_i}+a_{i,e_i-1}b_i^{e_i-1}+\cdots+a_{i,0}=0\qquad(a_{i,j}\in A).
$$
Repeatedly reduce any exponent at least $e_i$ using this equation. Since the generators commute, every polynomial in them becomes an $A$-linear combination of the finitely many monomials
$$
b_1^{\nu_1}\cdots b_n^{\nu_n},\qquad0\leq\nu_i<e_i.
$$
Thus $B$ is a <finitely generated module> over $A$, with at most $D=\prod_i e_i$ generators. This proves the relevant <subalgebra generated by finitely many integral elements> assertion rather than merely invoking it.

For a fixed <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p\subset A$, form the <fiber ring> $C=B\otimes_A\kappa(\mathfrak p)$. Tensoring the finite generating list shows $\dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak p)}C\leq D$: its <pure tensors> with one span the fiber over the <residue field> $\kappa(\mathfrak p)$. Part (b) therefore gives only finitely many primes in $C$. By the canonical bijection in part (a), these are precisely the primes of $B$ contracting to $\mathfrak p$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\#\{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}B:A\cap\mathfrak q=\mathfrak p\}\leq\dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak p)}(B\otimes_A\kappa(\mathfrak p))\leq D<\infty.}
$$
No <Noetherian> hypothesis on $A$ is needed. For an empty algebra-generating list, $B=A$ and the empty product $D=1$ gives the same conclusion.
