= Solution
{parent=1}

A <phase diagram> locates stable <pure thermodynamic phases> and their <phase coexistence> boundaries in a space of control parameters. For a concrete three-dimensional example, take the temperature, crystal-field parameter, and magnetic field of the <Blume–Capel model>. Its spins take values $0,\pm1$; changing the cost of the zero-spin state can turn a continuous ordering transition into a discontinuous one. A local scalar description near the resulting <tricritical point> is the <Landau-Ginzburg theory>
$$
\mathcal F[M]=\int d^Dx\left[\frac K2|\nabla M|^2+V(M)\right],\qquad
V(M)=\frac r2M^2+\frac u4M^4+\frac v6M^6-hM,\quad K,v>0.
$$
The three local control coordinates are $(r,u,h)$, with $r$ varying linearly with the thermal scaling field. For $u>0$, $h=0$, the transition at $r=0$ is continuous. For $u<0$, zero-field coexistence is at $r=3u^2/(16v)$, and the <magnetization> jumps between zero and $\pm\sqrt{-3u/(4v)}$. The two loci meet at $r=u=h=0$, the <tricritical point>.

The full three-dimensional <phase diagram> has an ordered coexistence sheet at $h=0$, where the two opposite magnetizations exchange stability when $h$ changes sign. On its three-phase line it meets two nonzero-field <tricritical wings>. These wings are <first-order phase transition> surfaces between minima of the same sign but different magnitude, terminating at ordinary <thermodynamic critical points>. Their <tricritical wing critical edges> are found by $V'=V''=V'''=0$:
$$
M_c^2=-\frac{3u}{10v},\qquad r_c=\frac{9u^2}{20v},\qquad h_c=\frac{6u^2M_c}{25v}\quad(u<0).
$$
Indeed $V'''=6uM+20vM^3$ fixes $M_c^2$, and substituting into $V''=0$ and $V'=0$ gives $r_c,h_c$; $V''''=-12u>0$ ensures a stable critical quartic minimum. The two signs give the two wings. All transition surfaces and critical edges end together at the <tricritical point>. The following plot uses $v=1$; its wing surfaces come from the exact <tricritical wing coexistence factorization>, rather than schematic unconstrained surfaces.

\Image[paper-50-landau.png]
{title=Tricritical coexistence surfaces, the zero-field phase diagram, and ordinary, first-order and tricritical Landau potentials}

The unheaded requests for <critical exponents> follow from the uniform equation of state
$$
h=rM+uM^3+vM^5.
$$
For an ordinary <thermodynamic critical point>, $u>0$ is fixed and $r=At$, $A>0$. At $h=0$, $t<0$, the small ordered minimum obeys $M^2\sim-r/u$, so the <order-parameter critical exponent> is $\beta=1/2$. Above the transition $\chi=(\partial M/\partial h)_{h=0}=1/r$, giving the <magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent> $\gamma=1$. At $r=0$, $h\sim uM^3$, so the critical-isotherm exponent is $\delta=3$. At the tuned <tricritical point>, $u=0$, instead $M^4=-r/v$ below the transition and $h=vM^5$ on its critical isotherm, while the disordered susceptibility remains $1/r$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(\beta,\gamma,\delta)_{\rm ordinary}=(1/2,1,3),\qquad
(\beta,\gamma,\delta)_{\rm tricritical}=(1/4,1,5).}
$$
Below the transition the inverse susceptibility is $r+3uM^2+5vM^4$, giving $2|r|$ in the ordinary limit and $4|r|$ in the tricritical limit. Thus both sides give the same susceptibility power, with different amplitudes.

To derive the general <Ginzburg criterion>, tune away all lower even nonlinearities and let the leading stabilizer be $v_nM^{2n}/(2n)$, $n\geq2$. The equation of state gives $M_0^2\asymp|r|^{1/(n-1)}$, and its curvature at the ordered saddle is $2(n-1)|r|$, so $\xi\asymp|r|^{-1/2}$. The fluctuations of the <order parameter> averaged over a <correlation volume> are estimated by its long-wavelength quadratic covariance:
$$
\langle(\delta M)^2\rangle_\xi
\asymp k_BT\int_{|q|\lesssim\xi^{-1}}\frac{d^Dq}{(2\pi)^D}\frac1{Kq^2+c|r|}
\asymp\xi^{2-D}.
$$
The integral has a fixed dimensionless upper limit after $q=p/\xi$; this is a coarse-volume fluctuation estimate, not the ultraviolet-dominated point variance. Consequently the <multicritical Ginzburg ratio> is
$$
\frac{\langle(\delta M)^2\rangle_\xi}{M_0^2}
\asymp |r|^{(D-2)/2-1/(n-1)}.
$$
The <Landau approximation> is asymptotically controlled by this test only when the ratio tends to zero. This gives the <upper critical dimension>
$$
\boxed{D_c=\frac{2n}{n-1};\qquad D_c=4\ (n=2),\quad D_c=3\ (n=3).}
$$
The same threshold follows by giving $M$ its Gaussian <engineering dimension> $(D-2)/2$: the coupling to $M^{2n}$ has scaling exponent $D-n(D-2)=2n-(n-1)D$, which becomes relevant below $D_c$.

For $D<D_c$, the fluctuation ratio diverges and the saddle calculation cannot predict the generic interacting <critical exponents>. At $D=D_c$ the test is marginal, not a proof of changed power exponents: the ratio has zero thermal power and marginal interactions can produce logarithmic corrections. Thus the PDF's inclusion of equality in a blanket claim about wrong power exponents needs this qualification. The <Landau-Ginzburg theory> as a fluctuating field theory remains useful; what loses validity is its fluctuation-free <Landau approximation>.

An <order parameter> distinguishes phases by an equilibrium expectation value. For an <Ising model>, the local field $M(x)$ is the coarse-grained <magnetization>, and the uniform value is $M=N^{-1}\langle\sum_r\sigma_r\rangle$. The <spin inversion symmetry> maps $M$ to $-M$, so at zero field the <Landau free energy> contains even powers. Its disordered minimum is $M=0$; ordered <pure thermodynamic phases> have $M=\pm M_0$. Their sign is selected by taking the <thermodynamic limit> before sending $h$ to zero. A symmetric mixture can have zero average without being a single disordered phase. The term $-hM$ identifies $h$ as the <field conjugate to an order parameter>.

Use the <connected correlation function> $G(x)=\langle M(x)M(0)\rangle-\langle M\rangle^2$ in a selected <pure thermodynamic phase>. The exponential <correlation length> is defined by $\xi^{-1}=-\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x|^{-1}\log|G(x)|$, when this limit exists in the chosen direction. Linearizing the <Landau-Ginzburg theory> around a stable uniform minimum $M_0$ gives
$$
\mathcal F^{(2)}=\frac12\int d^Dx\,[K|\nabla\delta M|^2+V''(M_0)(\delta M)^2],\qquad
\widetilde G(q)=\frac{k_BT}{Kq^2+V''(M_0)}.
$$
Hence the <Landau scalar correlation length> is $\boxed{\xi=\sqrt{K/V''(M_0)}}$. Away from criticality the <massive Gaussian field correlation tail> behaves as $|x|^{-(D-1)/2}e^{-|x|/\xi}$, up to its amplitude. At a continuous transition $V''$ tends to zero, so $\xi$ diverges and fields vary on scales much longer than the lattice spacing. This motivates a <gradient> expansion, but the simultaneous growth of correlated fluctuations is precisely why the <Ginzburg criterion> must be checked.

A <first-order phase transition> exchanges distinct <global minima> of the <Landau free energy>; the <order parameter> jumps and a thermal crossing generically has <latent heat>. A <continuous phase transition> has minima merging continuously and a diverging <correlation length>. A local loss of stability at a <spinodal point> is not the equilibrium coexistence condition.

For the <sextic even Landau potential>, write $y=M^2$. At a nonzero zero-field <stationary point>, $r+uy+vy^2=0$. To coexist with $M=0$, also require $ry/2+uy^2/4+vy^3/6=0$. Eliminate $r$ to obtain $y=-3u/(4v)$ and $r=3u^2/(16v)$, which requires $u<0$. On this locus
$$
V(M)=\frac v6M^2\left(M^2+\frac{3u}{4v}\right)^2\geq0,
$$
proving that all three equal minima are globally stable. Their curvatures are $3u^2/(16v)$ at zero and $3u^2/(4v)$ at the nonzero minima, so the <correlation length> is finite when $u<0$ is fixed. For $u>0$, by contrast, the nonzero minimum approaches zero as $r\uparrow0$, giving the ordinary continuous transition. At fixed $u>0$ and $r<0$, crossing $h=0$ exchanges the two ordered minima discontinuously even though the zero-field thermal transition at $r=0$ is continuous.

<Universality classes> identify systems with the same long-distance <renormalization-group fixed point>. Spatial dimension, <order parameter> symmetry and number of components, and the range of interactions help determine the equilibrium class. Microscopic lattice details and many local interactions become <irrelevant operators> under coarse-graining. Universal quantities include <critical exponents>, scaling functions after fixing metric normalizations, and suitable amplitude ratios. The critical temperature, bare couplings, absolute correlation-length and susceptibility amplitudes, analytic <free-energy density> backgrounds, and microscopic length scales are generally not universal. Dynamic universality can additionally depend on conservation laws, so it should not be inferred solely from a static scalar potential.

A <critical exponent> describes the leading singular power of an observable in a specified scaling limit. The <order-parameter critical exponent> describes the zero-field ordered branch; the <magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent> describes response to its <conjugate field>; and the critical-isotherm exponent describes response at the critical temperature. They are found in the <Landau approximation> by minimizing the potential and differentiating the resulting equation of state, as calculated in the question's root solution. For a leading $M^{2n}$ stabilizer the same calculation gives $M\asymp(-r)^{1/(2n-2)}$, $\chi\asymp r^{-1}$ and $M\asymp h^{1/(2n-1)}$. Thus $\beta=1/(2n-2)$, $\gamma=1$ and $\delta=2n-1$. Beyond the <Landau approximation>, the <renormalization-group relevance> exponents and <anomalous dimension> determine the singular powers; Question 2 derives their relations. The <critical exponents> refer to specified paths, and tuning to a higher multicritical point requires eliminating its lower relevant perturbations.

= Solution

At a <tricritical point>, both $r$ and $u$ vanish, while $v>0$ stabilizes the <Landau free energy>. It separates ordinary continuous transitions for $u>0$ from first-order coexistence for $u<0$. The zero-field order-parameter jump shrinks to zero as $u\uparrow0$, and the <tricritical wings> terminate there in the full $(r,u,h)$ diagram. For $r<0$, $u=0$, minimizing gives $M^4=-r/v$ and
$$
V(M_0)=-\frac{|r|^{3/2}}{3\sqrt v}.
$$
Two thermal derivatives give the <heat-capacity critical exponent> $\alpha=1/2$, compared with the finite mean-field heat-capacity jump, $\alpha=0$, at an ordinary quartic transition. The remaining tricritical <mean-field critical exponents> are $(\beta,\gamma,\delta)=(1/4,1,5)$, and the mean-field <correlation-length critical exponent> is $\nu=1/2$. To observe tricritical scaling one must tune the quartic scaling field as well as the thermal field: a generic path with fixed $u>0$ instead has ordinary critical behavior. Its <upper critical dimension> is three, as the <multicritical Ginzburg ratio> shows.

= Solution

Use $\beta_{\rm th}=1/(k_BT)$ for inverse temperature, to distinguish it from the order-parameter exponent. In a translation-invariant <pure thermodynamic phase>, the <connected correlation function> is
$$
G(r)=\langle\sigma_r\sigma_0\rangle-\langle\sigma_r\rangle\langle\sigma_0\rangle.
$$
The subtraction is essential below the transition. Its exponential <correlation length> is defined by $\xi^{-1}=-\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{-1}\log|G(r)|$, with direction specified if the lattice is anisotropic. Away from criticality, $G(r)$ has a tail proportional to $e^{-r/\xi}$ times an algebraic prefactor. In the massive continuum scalar approximation that prefactor is $r^{-(D-1)/2}$; at criticality the exponential cutoff disappears and $G(r)\asymp r^{-(D-2+\eta)}$ instead. The large-distance limit here is $r\gg\xi$, as the PDF shows.

Let $F_{\rm tot}=-\beta_{\rm th}^{-1}\log Z$. With the energy convention $-h\sum_r\sigma_r$, the <magnetization> per site and <magnetic susceptibility> are
$$
M=-\frac1N\frac{\partial F_{\rm tot}}{\partial h},\qquad
\chi=\frac{\partial M}{\partial h}=-\frac1N\frac{\partial^2F_{\rm tot}}{\partial h^2}.
$$
Writing $S=\sum_r\sigma_r$, direct differentiation of the <partition function> gives $\partial_h\langle S\rangle=\beta_{\rm th}(\langle S^2\rangle-\langle S\rangle^2)$. Thus the <correlation-function susceptibility sum rule> is
$$
\boxed{\chi=\frac{\beta_{\rm th}}N\sum_{r,r'}\langle\sigma_r\sigma_{r'}\rangle_c
=\beta_{\rm th}\sum_rG(r).}
$$
In physical continuum coordinates the lattice sum becomes $a^{-D}\int d^Dr\,G(r)$, so omitting the site-density factor would change the normalization. A derivative with respect to the dimensionless source $\beta_{\rm th}h$ instead absorbs the explicit inverse-temperature factor.

An exact <renormalization-group transformation> with length factor $b>1$ can be defined by a nonnegative <normalized blocking kernel> $K_b(\sigma',\sigma)$ satisfying $\sum_{\sigma'}K_b(\sigma',\sigma)=1$. A deterministic majority rule is one example; randomized tie-breaking preserves its normalization. Define the blocked energy, including its constant, by
$$
e^{-\beta_{\rm th}H(u',\sigma')-\beta_{\rm th}N'C'}
=\sum_\sigma K_b(\sigma',\sigma)e^{-\beta_{\rm th}H(u,\sigma)-\beta_{\rm th}NC},
\qquad N'=N/b^D.
$$
Summing over $\sigma'$ proves $Z(u',C',N')=Z(u,C,N)$. All generated operators must be retained for this identity to remain exact; a finite-coupling truncation is an approximation. After $p$ iterations, $N_p=N/b^{pD}$ and the spacing is $a_p=b^pa$. Long-distance physics is preserved because this is a marginalization of microscopic probabilities, with observables and sources transformed along with the energy. It integrates short-distance variables rather than changing their effects on retained observables. Physical lengths remain fixed even though the <correlation length> in units of the new spacing is reduced by $b^p$.

For clarity, now use dimensionless <free energy> per site $F(u,C)=-N^{-1}\log Z=f(u)+\beta_{\rm th}C$. Exact equality of the <partition functions> gives
$$
F(u_0,C_0)=b^{-pD}F(u_p,C_p).
$$
A constant microscopic energy shifts every blocked weight by the same factor, so $C'=b^DC+c(u)$. Substitution at one step yields the <additive free-energy recursion under blocking>
$$
f(u)=b^{-D}f(R_bu)+g(u),\qquad g(u)=\beta_{\rm th}b^{-D}c(u).
$$
Induction now gives the requested relation
$$
\boxed{f(u_0)=b^{-pD}f(u_p)+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b^{-jD}g(u_j).}
$$
The function $g$ is the <identity-operator contribution to renormalization-group free energy>: it records eliminated-mode entropy, integration constants and normalization factors. A free-energy function satisfying this inhomogeneous equation need not be purely singular until its analytic background has been subtracted.

At a <renormalization-group fixed point>, $R_bu_*=u_*$. Linearize in scaling coordinates $v_i$ so $v_i'=b^{\lambda_i}v_i$. Here $\lambda_i$ is a scaling exponent, not the discrete multiplier itself. A positive $\lambda_i$ gives a <relevant operator>, a negative one an <irrelevant operator>, and zero a <marginal operator>. The <critical surface> is the stable manifold: tune every relevant scaling field to zero and the irrelevant coordinates flow toward $u_*$. A <repulsive renormalization-group trajectory> leaves along a relevant direction; it can still have irrelevant coordinates contracting toward that manifold. The plot shows the $h=0$ section when temperature and field are relevant: $t=0$ is the critical line in this section, while an independent nonzero field is another direction away from criticality.

\Image[paper-50-rg-flow.png]
{title=Renormalization-group trajectories near a critical fixed point, showing thermal repulsion and irrelevant contraction in the zero-field section}

The inhomogeneous term cannot simply be discarded from the full <free energy>. If an analytic background $A(u)$ solves $A(u)=b^{-D}A(R_bu)+g(u)$, its subtraction leaves $F_s=f-A$ obeying homogeneous scaling. For example, in linear thermal and field coordinates, a source monomial $g_{mn}t^mh^n$ is removed by
$$
A_{mn}=\frac{g_{mn}}{1-b^{m\lambda_t+n\lambda_h-D}}.
$$
When the denominator vanishes, the source is resonant and can instead produce a logarithm. Thus the homogeneous singular equation is justified after analytic subtraction when there is no relevant resonance affecting the leading singularity, no marginal logarithm that has been omitted, and no singular dependence on a <dangerously irrelevant coupling>. Merely saying that $g$ is regular is insufficient to discard the whole weighted sum without this analysis.

Under these assumptions, with two relevant scaling fields $t,h$, choose $p$ so that $b^{p\lambda_t}|t|$ is of order one. The <scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena> then gives
$$
F_s(t,h)=|t|^{D/\lambda_t}\Phi_\pm\left(\frac{h}{|t|^{\lambda_h/\lambda_t}}\right).
$$
Writing $\Phi_\pm=f_\pm+I_\pm$ recovers the PDF's notation:
$$
\boxed{F_\pm=|t|^{D/\lambda_t}\left[f_\pm\left(h/|t|^{\lambda_h/\lambda_t}\right)+I_\pm\right].}
$$
The sign labels the two matching branches $t>0$ and $t<0$, which enter different phases when the flow leaves the critical neighborhood. The constants $I_\pm$ retain a branch-dependent identity contribution or a chosen split of the scaling function. They are determined by the same matching problem, not independently adjustable nonuniversal free-energy offsets. An arbitrary analytic offset from $C_0$ belongs to the subtracted background. The formula describes the singular part; adding that background recovers the full physical <free energy>.

At $h=0$, restore the common metric factors as $F_{s,\pm}=A_f|a_tt|^{D/\lambda_t}\Phi_\pm(0)$. Their ratio at equal $|t|$ is $\Phi_+(0)/\Phi_-(0)$, proving the expected <universal singular free-energy amplitude ratio> because $A_f$ and $a_t$ cancel. The ratio of full <free energies> including an arbitrary regular $C_0$ is not universal; zero amplitudes and logarithmic resonances also require separate treatment.

The <critical exponents> are obtained by rescaling and differentiating, not by identifying them with the discrete multipliers. The <correlation length> obeys $\xi(t,h)=b^p\xi(b^{p\lambda_t}t,b^{p\lambda_h}h)$, hence $\nu=1/\lambda_t$. Two temperature derivatives of $F_s(t,0)$ give $C_{V,s}\asymp|t|^{D/\lambda_t-2}$, while one and two field derivatives give the ordered <magnetization> and susceptibility powers. Thus
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac1{\lambda_t},\quad
\alpha=2-\frac D{\lambda_t},\quad
\beta=\frac{D-\lambda_h}{\lambda_t},\quad
\gamma=\frac{2\lambda_h-D}{\lambda_t},\quad
\delta=\frac{\lambda_h}{D-\lambda_h}.}
$$
For $\delta$, instead set $t=0$ and choose the blocking scale by $h$, obtaining $F_s(0,h)\asymp|h|^{D/\lambda_h}$ and then differentiate. A nonzero spontaneous ordered-branch derivative is assumed in the formula for $\beta$. In particular the <hyperscaling relation> is $\boxed{\alpha=2-D\nu}$. Its physical assumption is an order-one singular <free energy> per <correlation volume>, with a single diverging length and no dangerous variable changing its volume scaling. Above the <upper critical dimension>, a stabilizing quartic coupling can invalidate that assumption even though the <correlation length> still scales.

For the last model, take the quadratic Hamiltonian exactly as visually printed: its source term is $+h\phi$, outside the half multiplying the <gradient> and mass terms. With $\kappa>0$ and $m^2>0$, its connected quadratic covariance is proportional to
$$
\widetilde G(q)=\frac1{\kappa^{-1}q^2+m^2}=\frac\kappa{q^2+\kappa m^2}.
$$
The pole scale, or the <massive Gaussian field correlation tail> obtained by its <Fourier transform>, gives
$$
\boxed{\xi=(\kappa m^2)^{-1/2}=\frac1{\sqrt\kappa\,m}\quad(m>0).}
$$
The uniform source shifts the mean to $\langle\phi\rangle=-h/m^2$ and does not change the connected covariance. Replacing $h$ by $-h$ matches the earlier magnetic source convention without changing any scaling index.

Define a <Gaussian thinning transformation> by retaining momenta $|q|<\Lambda/b$, integrating the Gaussian shell, and rescaling
$$
x'=x/b,\qquad \phi'(x')=b^{(D-2)/2}\phi_<(bx').
$$
The <gradient> coefficient is unchanged. Counting volume and field powers in the remaining action gives
$$
\kappa'=\kappa,\qquad (m^2)'=b^2m^2,\qquad h'=b^{(D+2)/2}h.
$$
Shell integration contributes only the additive determinant term. Thus $\lambda_t=2$ when the thermal variable is proportional to $m^2$, and $\lambda_h=(D+2)/2$. Inserting these into the homogeneous index formulas gives the requested <Gaussian critical exponents>
$$
\boxed{\alpha_G=(4-D)/2,\qquad \beta_G=(D-2)/4.}
$$
The specific-heat power can also be verified without hyperscaling: two mass-squared derivatives of $\tfrac12\int d^Dq\,(2\pi)^{-D}\log(\kappa^{-1}q^2+m^2)$ produce an integral proportional to $\int d^Dq\,(q^2+\kappa m^2)^{-2}$. For $0<D<4$, rescaling $q=\sqrt\kappa m\,p$ gives $(m^2)^{D/2-2}$; at $D=4$ the divergence is logarithmic, with power index zero. At $D=2$ the determinant itself has an additive $m^2\log m^2$ resonance, although its heat-capacity power remains one.

There is a genuine limitation to the last printed request. The <Gaussian order-parameter scaling index> $\beta_G$ is formal: a pure quadratic theory has $\langle\phi\rangle=0$ at $h=0,m^2>0$, and at $m^2<0$ the uniform energy $m^2\phi^2/2$ is unbounded below, so its <partition function> diverges. It therefore has no stable ordered branch from which a spontaneous-magnetization exponent can be measured. The thinning derivation establishes the index requested by the PDF, but not a nonexistent ordered phase. A stabilizing quartic interaction is relevant for $D<4$ and generally changes these Gaussian indices to the interacting critical ones.

Replace each <XY model> bond weight by its periodic Gaussian approximation. With angles integrated using $d\theta/(2\pi)$, the <Villain model> is
$$
\boxed{Z_V=(2\pi\beta)^{-N_b/2}\int\prod_x\frac{d\theta_x}{2\pi}
\sum_{\{n_{x,\mu}\in\mathbb Z\}}
\exp\left[-\frac1{2\beta}\sum_{x,\mu}n_{x,\mu}^2
+i\sum_{x,\mu}n_{x,\mu}(\theta_{x+\hat\mu}-\theta_x)\right],}
$$
where $N_b$ is the number of bonds. Changing the angle-measure normalization multiplies $Z_V$ by an explicit constant only. The <Poisson summation formula> applied to one bond gives the equivalent winding representation
$$
\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-n^2/(2\beta)+in\psi}
=\sum_{p\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\beta(\psi-2\pi p)^2/2}.
$$
Thus the same <Villain model> can be written $Z_V=\int\prod_xd\theta_x/(2\pi)\sum_{\{p_e\}}\exp[-\beta\sum_e(\Delta_e\theta-2\pi p_e)^2/2]$. The coefficient $1/\sqrt{2\pi\beta}$ is the PDF's value; the converted TeX incorrectly places $\beta$ outside the square root. The cosine-to-Gaussian replacement is a large-$\beta$ approximation, but the rest of the calculation treats $Z_V$ as the exact model for every positive $\beta$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Angle integration gives a dual representation in any lattice dimension. Discrete summation by parts rewrites the phase as
$$
\sum_{x,\mu}n_{x,\mu}(\theta_{x+\hat\mu}-\theta_x)
=-\sum_x\theta_x\sum_\mu(n_{x,\mu}-n_{x-\hat\mu,\mu}).
$$
Each normalized angle integral is a Kronecker delta. This proves the <integer-current representation of the Villain model>
$$
Z_V=(2\pi\beta)^{-N_b/2}\sum_{\operatorname{div}n=0}\exp\left[-\frac1{2\beta}\sum_en_e^2\right].
$$
The PDF does not specify dimension or boundaries. The point-vortex <vortex Coulomb gas> interpretation below is two-dimensional. In three dimensions the defects are <vortex> lines, and the dual constrained-current representation still holds but is not a gas of point charges with logarithmic interactions.

First take a connected simply connected planar lattice, with free outer angles, so no torus winding sectors are silently discarded. Assign an integer height $\ell$ to each dual face, fixing the exterior face height to zero. A divergence-free integer current is exactly the oriented height difference on the two faces adjoining its bond. To see existence, integrate the rotated current along a dual path: the change around a closed dual path is the total divergence inside, hence zero. Integer currents give integer heights, and fixing the exterior removes their constant-shift ambiguity. Therefore
$$
\sum_en_e^2=\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle_{\rm dual}}(\ell_x-\ell_y)^2=\ell^TL\ell,
$$
where $L$ is the positive dual <graph Laplacian> with the exterior height fixed. This gives the <dual integer-height representation of the planar Villain model>.

Apply the supplied <Poisson summation formula> separately to every interior height. For $N_f$ interior faces,
$$
Z_V=(2\pi\beta)^{-N_b/2}\sum_{q\in\mathbb Z^{N_f}}
\int_{\mathbb R^{N_f}}d\phi\,
\exp\left[-\frac1{2\beta}\phi^TL\phi+2\pi i q^T\phi\right].
$$
Complete the Gaussian square, or use its <characteristic function>, to evaluate the integral as
$$
\frac{(2\pi\beta)^{N_f/2}}{\sqrt{\det L}}\exp[-2\pi^2\beta q^TL^{-1}q].
$$
Thus the smooth fluctuations and integer defects separate:
$$
\boxed{Z_V=Z_{\rm sw}Z_{\rm v},\qquad
Z_{\rm v}=\sum_{q\in\mathbb Z^{N_f}}e^{-2\pi^2\beta q^TGq},\quad G=L^{-1}.}
$$
The Gaussian factor is $Z_{\rm sw}=(2\pi\beta)^{-(N_b-N_f)/2}/\sqrt{\det L}$. It really is the <spin wave> factor of the original lattice: the planar Euler identity gives $N_b-N_f=N_x-1$, and the <matrix-tree theorem> identifies $\det L$ with the spanning-tree count of the original graph. Its reduced primal <Laplacian> has the same determinant, so the noncompact Gaussian angle integral with one angle fixed has exactly this prefactor. The compact common-angle mode has been normalized to one. The remaining $q$ variables are integer <vortex> windings on the faces, giving the <vortex Coulomb gas>.

For periodic boundaries, $L$ has a zero mode, and there are also two noncontractible current sectors. Neutral charges use the inverse $G$ on the mean-zero subspace. An integer dual height may have twists $w_x,w_y\in\mathbb Z$ across periods $L_x,L_y$. Subtracting $w_xx/L_x+w_yy/L_y$ makes the remaining height periodic; the <gradient> energy gains $(L_yw_x^2/L_x+L_xw_y^2/L_y)/(2\beta)$. Its height <Poisson summation> supplies the phase $\exp[2\pi i(w_xP_x/L_x+w_yP_y/L_y)]$, where $P_x=\sum q_xx$ and $P_y=\sum q_xy$. A second <Poisson summation> in $w_x,w_y$ yields the positive <harmonic-sector factor in periodic Villain duality>
$$
\mathcal W(q)=\sum_{W_x,W_y\in\mathbb Z}\exp\left[-2\pi^2\beta\left\{
\frac{L_x}{L_y}\left(W_x-\frac{P_x}{L_x}\right)^2+
\frac{L_y}{L_x}\left(W_y-\frac{P_y}{L_y}\right)^2\right\}\right].
$$
Up to charge-independent normalization, the exact periodic result is $Z_V=Z_{\rm sw}\sum_{\sum q=0}e^{-2\pi^2\beta q^TGq}\mathcal W(q)$. Smooth <spin waves> still factor out, but the finite-volume global winding sectors couple to <vortex> dipole moments. The familiar neutral bulk formula suppresses this boundary information; it must not be asserted as an exact finite-torus formula without specifying those sectors.

At long distances in two dimensions the lattice <Green function> solves $-\nabla^2G=\delta$ and has $G(r)=-(2\pi)^{-1}\log(r/a)+\text{constant}$. Indeed the flux of $-\nabla G$ through a circle is one, fixing that coefficient. For well-separated <vortices> at $r_i$ with integer charges $q_i$, the <vortex Coulomb gas> action becomes
$$
\boxed{S_{\rm v}=\mu_{\rm core}\sum_iq_i^2-2\pi\beta\sum_{i<j}q_iq_j\log(r_{ij}/a),}
$$
with the self-energy convention, boundary terms and any global sectors retained separately. The factor two in the pair sum comes from the two ordered terms in $q^TGq$. Opposite charges have pair action $+2\pi\beta|q_iq_j|\log(r/a)$, so separating them costs energy and they attract. Like charges have a negative logarithmic pair coefficient and repel. The ultraviolet lattice regularizes the core, while multiply charged defects have a quadratic self-energy cost. A separated unit vortex-antivortex pair costs $2\mu_{\rm core}+2\pi\beta\log(r/a)$, the energetic basis for <vortex-antivortex binding> at low temperature. In three dimensions one instead has interacting closed <vortex> lines with a three-dimensional Green kernel; the logarithmic point-pair formula is specifically two-dimensional.

For a periodic two-dimensional lattice the <vortex charge neutrality> constraint is $\boxed{\sum_xq_x=0}$. In the winding form of the <Villain model>, $q$ is the oriented plaquette curl of the integer bond variable $p$, up to an orientation sign. Summing all plaquette curls cancels each bond against its neighboring plaquette. This proves neutrality directly. Equivalently, restoring the common dual-height mode modulo its integer shift gives $\int_0^1d\phi_0\,e^{2\pi i\phi_0\sum q}$, which vanishes unless the total charge is zero; only then is the inverse lattice <Laplacian> well-defined on the charge source.

On an infinite plane, the same condition is required for a localized finite-energy configuration with constant phase at infinity. Net charge $Q$ produces $\theta\sim Q\arg x$ far away, so its exterior action is
$$
\frac\beta2\int_a^L2\pi r\,dr\,\frac{Q^2}{r^2}=\pi\beta Q^2\log(L/a),
$$
which diverges unless $Q=0$. With an open boundary the total charge instead equals the boundary winding, and the finite planar duality in part (b) can sum nonneutral configurations. Thus neutrality follows from periodicity or the stated finite-energy boundary condition, not from arbitrary boundaries. In three dimensions the analogous local constraint is that <vortex> lines cannot end in the bulk.

Write $\rho=m^2\Lambda^{-2}$ for the PDF's signed mass coordinate $u^2$, and $K_D=\Omega_D/(2\pi)^D$. The superscript on $u^2$ is notation here, not a restriction to positive mass. In a <momentum-shell renormalization group> step, remove momenta $\Lambda e^{-d\ell}<|p|<\Lambda$ and set $\ell=\log(\Lambda_0/\Lambda)$, the PDF's $b$. Split the <scalar field> into a slow field $\varphi$ and a fast field $\eta$, whose <Gaussian shell covariance> is $(p^2+m^2)^{-1}$. The shell must have a positive quadratic kernel; $1+\rho>0$ near the fixed point meets this requirement.

Expand the coarse action by the <cumulant expansion of a coarse-grained free energy>. In the first cumulant of $V=g\int(\varphi+\eta)^4/4!$, the term with two fast fields is $(g/4)\int\varphi^2\langle\eta^2\rangle$. Thus the <one-loop shell mass renormalization in scalar quartic theory> is
$$
\delta m^2=\frac g2 I_1,\qquad I_1=\int_{\rm shell}\frac{d^Dp}{(2\pi)^D}\frac1{p^2+m^2}.
$$
For the quartic correction, the two-fast-field vertex is $(g/4)\int\varphi^2\eta^2$. Its connected second cumulant has a minus sign and uses $\langle\eta^2(x)\eta^2(y)\rangle_c=2G_>(x-y)^2$. It gives
$$
-\frac{g^2}{16}\int d^Dx\,d^Dy\,\varphi^2(x)\varphi^2(y)G_>(x-y)^2.
$$
Taking the local zero-external-momentum quartic part produces $\delta g=-3g^2I_2/2$, where $I_2=\int_{\rm shell}d^Dp\,(2\pi)^{-D}(p^2+m^2)^{-2}$. This derives the combinatorial factors in the <one-loop shell quartic renormalization>. The mass tadpole is independent of external momentum; kinetic normalization changes only at higher order for the leading epsilon calculation.

For an infinitesimal spherical shell, radial integration gives
$$
I_1=K_D\frac{\Lambda^{D-2}}{1+\rho}\,d\ell,
\qquad I_2=K_D\frac{\Lambda^{D-4}}{(1+\rho)^2}\,d\ell.
$$
Lowering the cutoff adds the engineering terms $2\rho$ and $\epsilon\lambda$ to the running dimensionless couplings. Since $g=\lambda\Lambda^\epsilon$ and $D=4-\epsilon$, the remaining cutoff powers cancel exactly. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\rho}{d\ell}=2\rho+\frac{K_D}{2}\frac{\lambda}{1+\rho},\qquad
\frac{d\lambda}{d\ell}=\epsilon\lambda-\frac{3K_D}{2}\frac{\lambda^2}{(1+\rho)^2}.}
$$
These are the requested flows, to the stated one-loop order. They implement cutoff independence of long-wavelength observables after changing the couplings and retaining the generated constant and other effective operators to the relevant approximation order. They are not an exact closure of the complete effective action.

Besides the <Gaussian fixed point>, solve the nonzero-coupling fixed-point equations. The quartic equation gives $\lambda_*=2\epsilon(1+\rho_*)^2/(3K_D)$. Substitute into the mass equation to get $2\rho_*+\epsilon(1+\rho_*)/3=0$, hence $\rho_*=-\epsilon/(6+\epsilon)$ within the displayed one-loop truncation. Since $K_4=2\pi^2/(2\pi)^4=1/(8\pi^2)$,
$$
\boxed{\rho_*=-\epsilon/6+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\lambda_*=16\pi^2\epsilon/3+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
These are the <Wilson-Fisher fixed point> coordinates in this cutoff scheme. The higher terms obtained by solving the truncated equations exactly should not be regarded as a full higher-order epsilon calculation.

The attraction claim in the PDF needs a thermal qualification. The <stability matrix of a renormalization-group fixed point> at this solution has entries
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}
2-\epsilon/3&K_D/[2(1+\rho_*)]\\
3K_D\lambda_*^2/(1+\rho_*)^3&-\epsilon
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The lower-left entry is $O(\epsilon^2)$, so its <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{y_t=2-\epsilon/3+O(\epsilon^2)>0,\qquad
y_g=-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)<0.}
$$
Increasing $\ell$ means approaching the infrared. The quartic scaling direction is attractive, but a perturbation in the thermal scaling direction grows as $e^{y_t\ell}$. Therefore the <Wilson-Fisher fixed point> is an infrared attractor **on its tuned <critical surface>**, not an attractive fixed point of the entire two-dimensional coupling plane. In the plane it is a saddle; an arbitrary small thermal perturbation is a concrete counterexample to unconditional attraction. This is the <thermal relevant direction at the Wilson-Fisher fixed point>.

For $\epsilon>0$, the quartic interaction has a nonzero limiting value $\lambda_*=O(\epsilon)$ instead of disappearing under coarse-graining. Long-wavelength <thermal fluctuations> are therefore interacting, so the fluctuation-free <Landau approximation> cannot give the generic critical powers. For example, its ordinary <correlation-length critical exponent> is $1/2$, whereas the relevant thermal <eigenvalue> just derived gives
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac1{y_t}=\frac12+\frac\epsilon{12}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
The tadpole is momentum independent, so the <anomalous dimension> begins at $O(\epsilon^2)$. Combining this with the scaling relations yields $\gamma=1+\epsilon/6+O(\epsilon^2)$, $\beta=1/2-\epsilon/6+O(\epsilon^2)$, and $\alpha=\epsilon/6+O(\epsilon^2)$, exhibiting the deviation from ordinary mean-field indices. These are controlled statements for small positive $\epsilon$, not exact numerical exponents at arbitrary dimension. The <Landau-Ginzburg theory> itself is not invalid: integrating its fluctuations is precisely how the <Wilson-Fisher fixed point> was obtained. The invalid step is replacing that interacting long-distance theory by a uniform saddle and ignoring its fluctuation corrections.
