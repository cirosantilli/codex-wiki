A <sigma-algebra> on $\Omega$ is a collection $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal P(\Omega)$ containing $\Omega$, closed under <complements> relative to $\Omega$, and closed under <countable unions>. These properties also give closure under <countable intersections> by <De Morgan laws>.

Let $\mathcal G=\bigcap_{r\in R}\mathcal F_r$. Every constituent <sigma-algebra> contains $\Omega$, so $\Omega\in\mathcal G$. If $A\in\mathcal G$, its <complement> belongs to every $\mathcal F_r$, hence to $\mathcal G$. Likewise, if $A_j\in\mathcal G$ for all $j$, then $\bigcup_j A_j$ belongs to every $\mathcal F_r$. Thus $\mathcal G$ is a <sigma-algebra>. For an empty indexing set, the usual <intersection> convention gives $\mathcal G=\mathcal P(\Omega)$, which is also a <sigma-algebra>.

A <union> need not work. On $\Omega=\{1,2,3\}$ take
$$
\mathcal F_1=\{\varnothing,\Omega,\{1\},\{2,3\}\},\qquad
\mathcal F_2=\{\varnothing,\Omega,\{2\},\{1,3\}\}.
$$
Their <union> contains $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ but not $\{1,2\}$, so it is not a <sigma-algebra>. Put $\mathcal A=\bigcup_r\mathcal F_r$ and let $\mathfrak C$ be the collection of all <sigma-algebras> on $\Omega$ containing $\mathcal A$. This collection is nonempty because it contains the <power set> $\mathcal P(\Omega)$. By the preceding <intersection> argument,
$$
\boxed{\sigma(\mathcal A)=\bigcap_{\mathcal H\in\mathfrak C}\mathcal H}
$$
is a <sigma-algebra> containing every $\mathcal F_r$, and it is contained in every other such <sigma-algebra>. This proves existence and minimality.

For the <independent random variables>, define the <tail sigma-algebra> by
$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{m\geq1}\sigma(X_m,X_{m+1},\ldots).
$$
Fix a <tail event> $A\in\mathcal T$. For each $m$, it belongs to $\sigma(X_{m+1},X_{m+2},\ldots)$, which is <independent> of $\mathcal H_m=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_m)$. To justify independence of the generated <sigma-algebras>, first use independence of finite collections on cylinder <events>, then extend twice by the <pi-lambda theorem>. In particular, $\mathbb P(A\cap C)=\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(C)$ for every $C\in\mathcal H_m$ and every $m$.

The increasing <union> $\bigcup_m\mathcal H_m$ is an <algebra of sets> generating $\mathcal H=\sigma(X_1,X_2,\ldots)$. The collection of $C\in\mathcal H$ satisfying this factorization is a <Dynkin system>: it contains $\Omega$, is closed under <complements>, and under countable disjoint <unions>. The <pi-lambda theorem> therefore extends the factorization to all $C\in\mathcal H$. Since $A\in\mathcal H$, taking $C=A$ gives $\mathbb P(A)=\mathbb P(A)^2$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)\in\{0,1\}\qquad(A\in\mathcal T).}
$$
This is the <Kolmogorov zero-one law>, proved here directly.

For each fixed $m$, write $S_n=S_{m-1}+\sum_{j=m}^nX_j$ for $n\geq m$, with $S_0=0$. The <random variable> $S_{m-1}$ is finite, so $S_{m-1}/\sqrt n\to0$. Adding a real sequence tending to zero changes neither the extended-real <limit inferior> nor the extended-real <limit superior>. Hence
$$
\liminf_n\frac{S_n}{\sqrt n}=\liminf_n\frac{\sum_{j=m}^nX_j}{\sqrt n},\qquad
\limsup_n\frac{S_n}{\sqrt n}=\limsup_n\frac{\sum_{j=m}^nX_j}{\sqrt n}.
$$
The quantities on the right are measurable with respect to $\sigma(X_m,X_{m+1},\ldots)$: write the <limit inferior> as $\sup_N\inf_{n\geq N}$ and the <limit superior> as $\inf_N\sup_{n\geq N}$, using countable operations on <measurable functions>. Therefore both threshold <events> belong to every such <sigma-algebra>, and hence are <tail events>, for every real threshold, including when either limit is infinite.

**The final recurrence claim is false under the printed hypotheses.** For $c>0$, let $\varepsilon_j$ be independent signs with equal probabilities for $+1$ and $-1$, and set $X_j=c4^{-(j-1)}\varepsilon_j$. These <random variables> are independent, have <symmetric distributions>, and obey $|X_j|\leq c$. Nevertheless, the <geometric series> gives, on every sample path,
$$
|S_n|\geq c-\sum_{j=2}^n c4^{-(j-1)}\geq c-\frac c3=\frac{2c}{3}>\frac c2.
$$
Thus the proposed repeated-return <event> has <probability> zero, even though none of the increments is degenerate. The <Kolmogorov zero-one law> does not determine which of zero and one occurs.

An additional identical-distribution hypothesis makes the intended conclusion true. Here is a full proof of that qualified version, using the <oscillation of a bounded centered iid random walk>. Symmetry and integrability give $\mathbb E X_j=0$, so $S_n$ is a <martingale> for its <natural filtration>. If the common <probability distribution> is concentrated at zero, every $S_n$ is zero and the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise choose $\delta>0$ with $\mathbb P(|X_1|>\delta)>0$. The <Borel-Cantelli lemmas> then give $|X_n|>\delta$ infinitely often <almost surely>; thus $S_n$ cannot converge to a finite limit.

For $a>0$, let $T_a=\inf\{n:S_n\geq a\}$. Before $T_a$ the sum is below $a$, and at $T_a$ it is at most $a+c$. The <stopped martingale> $a+c-S_{n\wedge T_a}$ is therefore nonnegative. A stopped integrable <martingale> remains a <martingale> at these bounded times, and a nonnegative <martingale> converges finitely <almost surely> by the <martingale convergence theorem>. On $\{T_a=\infty\}$ this would force $S_n$ to converge finitely, which has just been ruled out. Hence $T_a<\infty$ <almost surely>. Apply the same argument to $-S_n$ and take a countable intersection over positive integer $a$. It follows that
$$
\limsup_n S_n=+\infty,\qquad \liminf_n S_n=-\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
There are therefore arbitrarily late passages from above $c/2$ to below $-c/2$. A path with <bounded increments> of magnitude at most $c$ cannot make such a passage without visiting $[-c/2,c/2]$: jumping directly between its two open complementary half-lines would require an increment larger than $c$. There are infinitely many such visits. Thus **with the added identical-distribution hypothesis**, the intended answer is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\bigl(|S_n|\leq c/2\text{ infinitely often}\bigr)=1.}
$$

For <events> $A_n$, define their <limit superior> by
$$
A_\infty=\limsup_n A_n=\bigcap_{N\geq1}\bigcup_{n\geq N}A_n.
$$
It is the <event> that infinitely many $A_n$ occur. The two <Borel-Cantelli lemmas> are as follows: if $\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)<\infty$, then $\mathbb P(A_\infty)=0$, without any independence assumption; if the $A_n$ are independent and $\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)=\infty$, then $\mathbb P(A_\infty)=1$.

For the first assertion, the <union bound> gives, for every $N$,
$$
\mathbb P(A_\infty)\leq\mathbb P\!\left(\bigcup_{n\geq N}A_n\right)
\leq\sum_{n\geq N}\mathbb P(A_n).
$$
The tail of a convergent nonnegative <series> tends to zero, proving the first <Borel-Cantelli lemma>.

For the second assertion, independence and $1-u\leq e^{-u}$ for $0\leq u\leq1$ give
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^K A_n^c\right)
=\prod_{n=N}^K(1-\mathbb P(A_n))
\leq\exp\!\left(-\sum_{n=N}^K\mathbb P(A_n)\right)\longrightarrow0.
$$
By <continuity of probability>, $\mathbb P(\bigcap_{n\geq N}A_n^c)=0$. The <event> that only finitely many $A_n$ occur is the countable <union> of these zero-probability <events> over $N$. Its <probability> is zero. This proves the second <Borel-Cantelli lemma>, and hence both requested assertions:
$$
\boxed{\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)<\infty\Rightarrow\mathbb P(A_\infty)=0,\qquad
\text{independence and }\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)=\infty\Rightarrow\mathbb P(A_\infty)=1.}
$$

Write $Q(x)=1-\Phi(x)=\int_x^\infty\phi(t)\,dt$, where $\phi(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-t^2/2}$ is the <standard normal density>. For $x>0$, $\phi'(t)=-t\phi(t)$ and <integration by parts> yield
$$
Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac{-\phi'(t)}t\,dt
=\frac{\phi(x)}x-\int_x^\infty\frac{\phi(t)}{t^2}\,dt.
$$
The boundary term at infinity vanishes. The remainder is nonnegative and is at most $Q(x)/x^2$, so
$$
\frac{x\phi(x)}{x^2+1}\leq Q(x)\leq\frac{\phi(x)}x.
$$
Dividing by the positive quantity $\phi(x)/x$ gives lower and upper bounds $x^2/(x^2+1)$ and $1$. The <squeeze theorem> proves the <Mills ratio> asymptotic
$$
\boxed{1-\Phi(x)\sim\frac{\phi(x)}x\qquad(x\to\infty).}
$$

For $a>0$ and $n\geq2$, let $A_n(a)=\{X_n^2>a\log n\}$. Symmetry of the <standard normal distribution> and the <Mills ratio> asymptotic just proved give
$$
\mathbb P(A_n(a))=2\bigl[1-\Phi(\sqrt{a\log n})\bigr]
\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi a}}\frac{n^{-a/2}}{\sqrt{\log n}}.
$$
For $a>2$ the <series> of these <probabilities> converges, since its terms are eventually bounded by a constant times the convergent <p-series> $n^{-a/2}$. For $0<a<2$ it diverges: choose $r$ strictly between $a/2$ and one, and use $(\log n)^{-1/2}\geq n^{-(r-a/2)}$ for sufficiently large $n$, reducing to the divergent <p-series> $n^{-r}$. At $a=2$ it still diverges, as the <integral test> gives
$$
\int_2^R\frac{dt}{t\sqrt{\log t}}
=2\bigl(\sqrt{\log R}-\sqrt{\log2}\bigr)\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
For $a>2$, the first <Borel-Cantelli lemma> makes $A_n(a)$ occur only finitely often. For $0<a\leq2$, independence of the <random variables> makes the <events> $A_n(a)$ independent, so the second <Borel-Cantelli lemma> makes them occur infinitely often <almost surely>. Apply these conclusions simultaneously at $a=2+1/k$ for positive integers $k$, and at $a=2$. On the resulting <probability>-one <event>, the <limit superior> is at most every $2+1/k$ and at least two. Therefore the <Gaussian sample limsup at logarithmic scale> is
$$
\boxed{\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_n^2}{\log n}=2\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

The real <L2 space> on a <probability space> consists of equivalence classes of <measurable functions> $X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ satisfying $\mathbb E[X^2]<\infty$, where two <random variables> represent the same element if they are equal <almost surely>. Its <norm> is
$$
\|X\|_2=(\mathbb E[X^2])^{1/2}.
$$
Passing to equivalence classes makes this a genuine <norm>: $\|X\|_2=0$ exactly when $X=0$ <almost surely>. The <Minkowski inequality> gives the <triangle inequality>.

Let $(X_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> in this <norm>. Choose an increasing sequence of indices $n_k$ such that
$$
\|X_{n_{k+1}}-X_{n_k}\|_2\leq2^{-k}\qquad(k\geq1).
$$
This is possible by choosing $n_k$ beyond a Cauchy threshold for tolerance $2^{-k}$. Set $D_k=X_{n_{k+1}}-X_{n_k}$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> on a <probability space> gives $\mathbb E|D_k|\leq\|D_k\|_2$. By <Tonelli theorem>,
$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{k\geq1}|D_k|\right]
=\sum_{k\geq1}\mathbb E|D_k|\leq\sum_{k\geq1}2^{-k}<\infty.
$$
Thus the <series> of absolute differences is finite <almost surely>. Define $X=X_{n_1}+\sum_{k\geq1}D_k$ on this <probability>-one <event>, and give it the value zero on its complement. It is a <measurable function>, and $X_{n_k}\to X$ <almost surely>.

For $l>k$, the <Minkowski inequality> gives
$$
\|X_{n_l}-X_{n_k}\|_2\leq\sum_{j=k}^{l-1}2^{-j}.
$$
Apply the <Fatou lemma> to the squared difference as $l\to\infty$. It follows that
$$
\|X-X_{n_k}\|_2\leq\sum_{j=k}^\infty2^{-j}=2^{1-k}.
$$
In particular $X-X_{n_1}\in L^2$, and therefore $X\in L^2$ by the <Minkowski inequality>. This proves <convergence in L2> of the subsequence. To recover the entire sequence, for a prescribed $\varepsilon>0$ choose a Cauchy threshold $N$ for $\varepsilon/2$, then choose $k$ with $n_k\geq N$ and $\|X-X_{n_k}\|_2<\varepsilon/2$. For every $n\geq N$,
$$
\|X_n-X\|_2\leq\|X_n-X_{n_k}\|_2+\|X_{n_k}-X\|_2<\varepsilon.
$$
Hence $\boxed{X_n\to X\text{ in }L^2}$, proving <completeness of Lp spaces> for $p=2$ without assuming the desired completeness in the argument.

A <filtration> $(\mathcal F_n)_{n\geq0}$ on a <probability space> is an increasing sequence of <sigma-algebras> contained in $\mathcal F$: $\mathcal F_n\subseteq\mathcal F_{n+1}$. A real <stochastic process> $(X_n)$ is a <martingale> for this <filtration> if $X_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, $\mathbb E|X_n|<\infty$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n\quad\text{almost surely for every }n.}
$$
The first condition is <adaptedness>; the <tower property of conditional expectation> then gives $\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_m]=X_m$ whenever $m\leq n$.

The almost-sure <martingale convergence theorem> states that if a real <martingale> satisfies $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|<\infty$, then some integrable <random variable> $X_\infty$ satisfies $X_n\to X_\infty$ <almost surely>. The more general <almost sure submartingale convergence theorem> needs only $\sup_n\mathbb E[X_n^+]<\infty$ for a <submartingale>. In particular, a nonnegative <martingale> converges to a finite integrable limit <almost surely>, because its <expected value> is constant. Mere boundedness in the <L1 norm> does not imply <convergence in L1>. The stronger <uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem> gives both almost-sure convergence and <convergence in L1> when the <martingale> is <uniformly integrable>.

The <martingale> property gives $\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_m]=X_m$. Both $X_m$ and $X_n$ are <square-integrable>, so their product is integrable by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Using the $\mathcal F_m$-measurability of $X_m$ in <conditional expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X_nX_m]=\mathbb E\!\left[X_m\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_m]\right]=\mathbb E[X_m^2].
$$
The pull-out step for the possibly unbounded $X_m$ can be justified by first truncating it, then passing to the limit in the <L2 norm> and using the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Expanding the square now yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[(X_n-X_m)^2]=\mathbb E[X_n^2]-\mathbb E[X_m^2]\qquad(m\leq n).}
$$
The left side is nonnegative. Thus $a_n=\mathbb E[X_n^2]$ is nondecreasing and bounded above by $M$, and so $a_n\to a\leq M$. For $m\leq n$,
$$
\|X_n-X_m\|_2^2=a_n-a_m\leq a-a_m\longrightarrow0.
$$
By symmetry of the <norm> difference this proves that $(X_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> in the <L2 space>. The completeness established in part (a) gives an $X_\infty\in L^2$ with
$$
\boxed{X_n\longrightarrow X_\infty\text{ in }L^2.}
$$
This is the <L2-bounded martingale convergence theorem>, derived from <martingale-difference orthogonality> and completeness. The same sequence also converges <almost surely> by part (b), since $\mathbb E|X_n|\leq\sqrt M$; uniqueness of a limit in <probability> identifies that limit with this $X_\infty$.

The <hitting time> $T$ is a <stopping time> for the <natural filtration>, because $\{T\leq n\}=\bigcup_{k\leq n}\{S_k=b\}$. The integer-valued increments are at most one. A path started at zero therefore cannot reach or exceed $b$ without first visiting $b$, so
$$
S_{n\wedge T}\leq b\quad\text{for every }n.
$$
Define $Y_n=b-S_{n\wedge T}$. It is nonnegative and integrable: $|S_{n\wedge T}|\leq\sum_{j\leq n}|X_j|$. Moreover,
$$
S_{(n+1)\wedge T}-S_{n\wedge T}=\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}X_{n+1}.
$$
The <indicator function> is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, and $X_{n+1}$ is independent of $\mathcal F_n$ with zero <expected value>. Taking <conditional expectation> therefore proves directly that $(Y_n)$ is a nonnegative <martingale>.

The general result used here is that a nonnegative discrete-time <martingale> has a finite almost-sure limit; this is the <martingale convergence theorem>, since $\mathbb E Y_n=\mathbb E Y_0=b$ bounds its <L1 norm>. On $\{T=\infty\}$ it follows that $S_n=b-Y_n$ converges finitely. A convergent sequence of integers is eventually constant, so $X_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$ would eventually be zero on this <event>.

However, the independent <events> $\{X_n=1\}$ all have the same strictly positive <probability>. The second <Borel-Cantelli lemma> says that they occur infinitely often <almost surely>. This contradicts eventual constancy on any positive-probability subset of $\{T=\infty\}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(T<\infty)=1.}
$$
Notice that no lower bound on the negative increments was needed.

The <stopped martingale> $Z_{n\wedge T}$ is a <martingale>. For completeness, the bounded-time stopping rule follows directly from
$$
Z_{(n+1)\wedge T}-Z_{n\wedge T}
=\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}(Z_{n+1}-Z_n),
$$
whose <conditional expectation> given $\mathcal F_n$ is zero. Integrability at each fixed $n$ follows by expressing the stopped value as a finite sum of the integrable $Z_j$ restricted to disjoint <events>. Therefore $\mathbb E Z_{n\wedge T}=\mathbb E Z_0=1$.

The crucial bound is deterministic. Since $S_{n\wedge T}\leq b$ and $M(\tau)\geq1$,
$$
0<Z_{n\wedge T}=\frac{e^{\tau S_{n\wedge T}}}{M(\tau)^{n\wedge T}}\leq e^{\tau b}.
$$
A bounded family is <uniformly integrable>: for $K>e^{\tau b}$ all its quantities $\mathbb E[|Z_{n\wedge T}|\mathbf1_{\{|Z_{n\wedge T}|>K\}}]$ vanish. Part (c) gives $T<\infty$ <almost surely>, and then $S_T=b$. Hence
$$
Z_{n\wedge T}\longrightarrow e^{\tau b}M(\tau)^{-T}\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
The general result for passing to expectations is the <dominated convergence theorem>: almost-sure convergence under one integrable bound implies <convergence in L1> and convergence of expectations. Applying it with the bound $e^{\tau b}$ gives
$$
1=\lim_n\mathbb E Z_{n\wedge T}=e^{\tau b}\mathbb E[M(\tau)^{-T}],
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb E[M(\tau)^{-T}]=e^{-\tau b}.}
$$
This is the <exponential first-passage transform for an upward skip-free random walk>; it avoids an unjustified use of <optional stopping> at an unbounded <stopping time>.

For a given $\alpha>0$, choose the unique $\tau>0$ satisfying $\cosh\tau=e^\alpha$. The transform in part (d) becomes $\mathbb E e^{-\alpha T}=e^{-b\tau}$. \boxed{\mathbb E[e^{-\alpha T}]

The second expression follows by rationalizing the first root. As $\alpha\downarrow0$ the transform tends to one, consistent with the almost-sure finiteness proved in part (c).

A family $(Y_i)_{i\in I}$ of integrable <random variables> is <uniformly integrable> if
$$
\boxed{\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{i\in I}\mathbb E\bigl[|Y_i|\mathbf1_{\{|Y_i|>K\}}\bigr]=0.}
$$
This controls the contributions of large values uniformly over the family, rather than merely bounding its <L1 norms>.

Put $X_n=\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal F_n]$ and $A_n(K)=\{|X_n|>K\}\in\mathcal F_n$. The <conditional Jensen inequality> gives $|X_n|\leq\mathbb E[|Z|\mid\mathcal F_n]$, hence $\mathbb E|X_n|\leq\mathbb E|Z|$. The <Markov inequality> therefore gives
$$
\mathbb P(A_n(K))\leq\frac{\mathbb E|Z|}{K}.
$$
Using the measurability of $A_n(K)$ in the defining integral identity for <conditional expectation>, for every $R>0$ we get
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[|X_n|\mathbf1_{A_n(K)}]
&\leq\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{A_n(K)}]\\
&\leq\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]+R\mathbb P(A_n(K))\\
&\leq\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]+\frac{R\mathbb E|Z|}{K}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first term tends to zero as $R\to\infty$ because $Z$ is integrable, by the <dominated convergence theorem>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, first choose $R$ to make it less than $\varepsilon/2$, then choose $K$ to make the second term less than $\varepsilon/2$. These choices work for every $n$. This proves <uniform integrability of conditional expectations>:
$$
\boxed{\{\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal F_n]:n\geq1\}\text{ is uniformly integrable}.}
$$
In fact the argument works for an arbitrary family of <sigma-algebras>; nesting is not needed for this conclusion.

Every <dyadic interval> at level $n+1$ lies in a <dyadic interval> at level $n$. \mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[f(U)\mid\mathcal F_{n+1}]\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\mathbb E[f(U)\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n.
$$
Thus $(X_n)$ is a <martingale>, and part (a) proves that it is <uniformly integrable>.

For $I_{n,k}=[k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n})$, the <event> $\{U\in I_{n,k}\}$ is an <atom of a sigma-algebra> $\mathcal F_n$, with <probability> $2^{-n}$. The value of the <conditional expectation> there is therefore
$$
\frac{\mathbb E[f(U)\mathbf1_{\{U\in I_{n,k}\}}]}{\mathbb P(U\in I_{n,k})}
=2^n\int_{I_{n,k}}f(u)\,du.
$$
Consequently $\boxed{X_n=f_n(U)\text{ almost surely}.}$ This also shows directly that $\int_0^1|f_n|\leq\int_0^1|f|$.

By the <uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem>, there is an integrable $Y$ such that $X_n\to Y$ <almost surely> and in the <L1 norm>. To identify this limit, put $\mathcal F_\infty=\sigma(\bigcup_n\mathcal F_n)$. Since $0\leq U-b_n(U)<2^{-n}$ away from the harmless endpoint, $U=\lim_n b_n(U)$, and $U$ is $\mathcal F_\infty$-measurable. Conversely every $b_n(U)$ is a measurable function of $U$. Thus $\mathcal F_\infty=\sigma(U)$, and both $Y$ and $f(U)$ have $\mathcal F_\infty$-measurable versions.

For $A\in\mathcal F_m$, the defining property of <conditional expectation> gives $\mathbb E[X_n\mathbf1_A]=\mathbb E[f(U)\mathbf1_A]$ whenever $n\geq m$. Passing to the limit in the <L1 norm> yields
$$
\mathbb E[Y\mathbf1_A]=\mathbb E[f(U)\mathbf1_A].
$$
The increasing <union> $\bigcup_m\mathcal F_m$ is an <algebra of sets> generating $\mathcal F_\infty$. The class of <events> for which this integral identity holds is a <Dynkin system>, so the <pi-lambda theorem> extends the identity to every $A\in\mathcal F_\infty$. Since both <random variables> are measurable there, uniqueness of <conditional expectation> gives $Y=f(U)$ <almost surely>. We have proved that the <dyadic conditional averages recover integrable functions>:
$$
\boxed{f_n(x)\longrightarrow f(x)\quad\text{for Lebesgue-almost every }x\in[0,1],}
$$
because a <probability>-one assertion for $U$ is a full-<Lebesgue measure> assertion for $x$. Likewise, the <uniform distribution> of $U$ turns <convergence in L1> into
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1|f_n(u)-f(u)|\,du
=\mathbb E|X_n-f(U)|\longrightarrow0.}
$$
No pointwise continuity of $f$ is required.

= Solution
{parent=6}

A standard <Brownian motion> is a real <stochastic process> $(B_t)_{t\geq0}$ with $B_0=0$ <almost surely>, continuous <sample paths> <almost surely>, and independent increments such that $B_t-B_s$ has <normal distribution> $N(0,t-s)$ whenever $0\leq s<t$. With its completed, right-continuous <natural filtration> $(\mathcal F_t)$, its <strong Markov property> is the following: for every almost-surely finite <stopping time> $T$, the process
$$
W_s=B_{T+s}-B_T,\qquad s\geq0,
$$
is a standard <Brownian motion> independent of $\mathcal F_T$. Here the <stopping-time sigma-algebra> is
$$
\mathcal F_T=\{A\in\mathcal F:A\cap\{T\leq t\}\in\mathcal F_t\text{ for all }t\geq0\}.
$$
In particular, for every bounded $\mathcal F_T$-measurable <random variable> $H$ and bounded Borel functional $G$ on the continuous path space,
$$
\mathbb E\bigl[H\,G((B_{T+s}-B_T)_{s\geq0})\bigr]
=\mathbb E[H]\,\mathbb E\bigl[G((B_s)_{s\geq0})\bigr].
$$
Thus the independence is from the entire stopped past, not only from $B_T$, and is a statement about the whole future path, not just a single increment.

To prove the <Brownian reflection principle> without assuming that a level is hit in finite time, fix $t>0$, $m>0$, and set $T_m=\inf\{s\geq0:B_s\geq m\}$ and $R=T_m\wedge t$. Continuity of the <sample paths> makes $T_m$ a <stopping time> and gives $B_{T_m}=m$ whenever $T_m<\infty$. The <stopping time> $R$ is bounded. Reflect the path after $R$:
$$
\widehat B_s=
\begin{cases}
B_s,&s\leq R,\\
2B_R-B_s,&s>R.
\end{cases}
$$
By the <strong Markov property>, the future increments after $R$ are an independent <Brownian motion>. Negating them preserves their <probability distribution>, by symmetry of their joint <normal distributions>. Hence the reflected process $\widehat B$ has the same path law as $B$.

If $T_m>t$, reflection changes nothing on $[0,t]$ and $\widehat B_t=B_t<m$. If $T_m\leq t$, both paths first hit $m$ at the same time and $\widehat B_t=2m-B_t$. Therefore, for $x\leq m$, the following <events> agree path by path:
$$
\{M_t\geq m,\ B_t\leq x\}
=\{\widehat B_t\geq2m-x\}.
$$
Indeed, the right-hand endpoint is at least $m$, so the reflected path must have hit $m$ before or at $t$; this also forces the original path to have done so. The reflection relation then makes the endpoint inequality equivalent to $B_t\leq x$. Equality cases at $T_m=t$ satisfy the same relation. Since $\widehat B_t$ and $B_t$ have the same <probability distribution>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(M_t\geq m,\ B_t\leq x)
=\mathbb P(B_t\geq2m-x)\qquad(m>0,\ x\leq m).}
$$
For $t=0$, both sides are zero, because $B_0=M_0=0$ and $2m-x\geq m>0$.

For $t>0$, set $x=m$ in this identity. Continuity implies $\{B_t>m\}\subseteq\{M_t\geq m\}$, and the <normal distribution> of $B_t$ has no atom at $m$. Splitting according to whether $B_t\leq m$ gives
$$
\mathbb P(M_t\geq m)=\mathbb P(B_t\geq m)+\mathbb P(B_t>m)
=2\mathbb P(B_t\geq m)=\mathbb P(|B_t|\geq m).
$$
Both <random variables> are nonnegative. Their tails agree for all $m>0$, which determines their <probability distributions>, including the mass at zero. Thus the <Brownian running maximum> satisfies
$$
\boxed{M_t\overset d=|B_t|\quad(t\geq0).}
$$

For $x>0$ and $t>0$, continuity again gives $\{T_x\leq t\}=\{M_t\geq x\}$. Since $B_t/\sqrt t$ is a <standard normal random variable>, the <distribution function> of the <Brownian first-passage time> is
$$
F_{T_x}(t)=2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt t}\right)\right].
$$
It tends to one as $t\to\infty$ and to zero as $t\downarrow0$, proving almost-sure finiteness and absence of an atom at zero. For the <standard normal random variable> $B_1$,
$$
\mathbb P\!\left(\left(\frac{x}{B_1}\right)^2\leq t\right)
=\mathbb P\!\left(|B_1|\geq\frac{x}{\sqrt t}\right)
=2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt t}\right)\right].
$$
The value of this ratio on the zero-probability <event> $\{B_1=0\}$ may be defined arbitrarily. Matching the <distribution functions> proves
$$
\boxed{T_x\overset d=\left(\frac{x}{B_1}\right)^2.}
$$
Finally, differentiate the <distribution function> on $t>0$, using $\Phi'=\phi$ and $\frac{d}{dt}(x/\sqrt t)=-x/(2t^{3/2})$. The <first-passage-time density> is
$$
\boxed{f_{T_x}(t)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,t^{3/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2t}\right)\quad(t>0),}
$$
and is zero for $t\leq0$. Its integral is one by the endpoint limits of $F_{T_x}$, so no missing mass at an infinite <hitting time> is present.
