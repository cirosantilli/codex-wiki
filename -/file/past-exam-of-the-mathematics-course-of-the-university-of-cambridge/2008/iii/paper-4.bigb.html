= Solution
{parent=1}

Write $A=RG$. An <idempotent> is an element $e$ with $e^2=e$; it is <central idempotent> when it commutes with every element of the <ring>. In a commutative <ring> centrality is automatic. Two <idempotents> are <orthogonal idempotents> if $ef=fe=0$. A nonzero <primitive central idempotent> cannot be expressed as the sum of two nonzero orthogonal <central idempotents>; this is primitivity in $Z(A)$, not necessarily primitivity in $A$.

A <block of a group algebra> is an indecomposable two-sided direct-summand <ideal> of $A$, equivalently an <ideal> $Ae$ with $e$ a <primitive central idempotent>. Here is the equivalence. If $A=B\oplus C$ as two-sided <ideals>, then $BC=CB=0$. Write $1=e+f$ with $e\in B,f\in C$. Multiplication by this equality shows that $e$ is the identity of $B$, that $f$ is the identity of $C$, and that $e,f$ are orthogonal <central idempotents>. Conversely a <central idempotent> gives $A=Ae\oplus A(1-e)$. Splitting the <ideal> further is therefore equivalent to splitting its <central idempotent> further.

For uniqueness, suppose $1=\sum_i e_i=\sum_j f_j$ are decompositions into <primitive central idempotents>. For each $i$,
$$
e_i=\sum_j e_if_j
$$
is an orthogonal central-idempotent decomposition. Exactly one summand is nonzero, and that summand equals $e_i$. Applying the same reasoning to the corresponding $f_j$ gives $e_i=f_j$. Thus **the blocks, and their identities, are unique up to ordering**. Existence in the paper's coefficient <rings> follows from finite-dimensionality over $k$, and over $\mathcal O$ from completeness and <idempotent lifting> of the decomposition modulo its maximal <ideal>.

Over $k$, a <principal indecomposable module> is an indecomposable summand of the regular left <module> $A=kG$, equivalently $P=Ae$ for a <primitive idempotent> $e$ of $A$. Put $J=J(A)$, the <Jacobson radical>. The <head of a module> $P/JP$ is nonzero and semisimple. It is simple: projectivity gives a surjection
$$
\operatorname{End}_A(P)\longrightarrow\operatorname{End}_{A/J}(P/JP),
$$
whose kernel consists of maps into $JP$ and is nilpotent, since a product of $r$ such maps sends $P$ into $J^rP$. If the <head of a module> split, a nontrivial projection on it would lift by <idempotent lifting> to a nontrivial projection on $P$, contradicting indecomposability. Every maximal submodule contains $JP$, since $J$ annihilates simple <modules>. The simplicity of the <head of a module> now proves **$J(P)=JP$ is the unique maximal submodule**, and $P$ is its <head of a module>'s <projective cover>.

Choose the simple <modules> $S_1,\ldots,S_r$, with corresponding <projective covers> $P_1,\ldots,P_r$. Our convention for the <Cartan matrix of a group algebra> is
$$
\boxed{(C_G)_{ij}=[P_j:S_i],}
$$
where brackets denote the multiplicity in a <composition series>.

For the final two unheaded requests, retain the additional hypothesis $G=NC_G(N)$. Choose a central <subgroup> $Z=\langle z\rangle$ of $N$ of order $p$. Every element of $N$ centralizes $Z$, and so does every element of $C_G(N)$; hence $Z\le Z(G)$. Set $t=z-1$, so $t$ is central and $t^p=0$, and $A/tA\cong k(G/Z)$.

To lift a <central idempotent> $\bar e$ of this quotient, first lift it as an <idempotent> $e$ by <idempotent refinement theorem>. Since $\bar e$ is central, $eA(1-e)\subseteq tA$. Multiplying a representation $e a(1-e)=t b$ on the left and right by $e,1-e$ gives
$$
eA(1-e)\subseteq t eA(1-e)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq t^p eA(1-e)=0.
$$
The other off-diagonal corner vanishes likewise, so $e$ is central. Two central lifts of the same <idempotent> coincide: their mismatched products $e(1-f)$ and $f(1-e)$ are <idempotents> in the nilpotent kernel and therefore zero. Primitivity is preserved because orthogonal <idempotent> decompositions lift. Thus the central $p$-quotient gives a bijection of block <idempotents>.

The quotient $G/Z$ still satisfies the required hypothesis for $N/Z$, because the image of $C_G(N)$ centralizes $N/Z$. Induction on $|N|$ therefore proves **$\tau$ bijects the block <idempotents> of $kG$ and $k(G/N)$**. The centrality argument is essential: a general nilpotent <algebra> quotient need not give a bijection of central block <idempotents>.

For the <Cartan matrix of a group algebra>, every projective $A$-module is free over $kZ$: $A$ is free over $kZ$, a projective $A$-module is a summand of a finite free $A$-module, and finite projectives over the <local ring> $kZ\cong k[t]/(t^p)$ are free. If $P$ is a <principal indecomposable module>, $\bar P=P/tP$ is a <principal indecomposable module> over $A/tA$: an <idempotent> splitting of its <endomorphism ring> would lift and split $P$. The $p$ quotients of its filtration
$$
P\supset tP\supset\cdots\supset t^{p-1}P\supset0
$$
are all isomorphic to $\bar P$ as $k(G/Z)$-modules, by multiplication by the corresponding power of $t$. All simple <modules> are annihilated by the <nilpotent ideal> $tA$. Hence $[P:S]=p[\bar P:S]$, with the natural indexing of simple <modules>, and $C_G=pC_{G/Z}$. Iterating over $N$ gives
$$
\boxed{C_G=|N|C_{G/N}.}
$$
This is <blocks and Cartan matrices under central p-quotients>. The extra hypothesis is still in force: for example $N=C_3\trianglelefteq S_3$ in characteristic three does not satisfy it, and its <Group algebra Cartan matrix> is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ rather than $3I$.

Let $I$ be the <augmentation ideal> of $kN$, generated by the elements $n-1$. First prove its nilpotence. A nontrivial finite $p$-group has a central element $z$ of order $p$. Put $t=z-1$. In characteristic $p$, $t^p=z^p-1=0$, and
$$
kN/tkN\cong k(N/\langle z\rangle).
$$
Induction on $|N|$ says that the image of $I$ in this smaller <group algebra> is nilpotent. Thus $I^m\subseteq tkN$ for some $m$, and $I^{mp}\subseteq t^pkN=0$. The trivial <group> starts the induction. This proves <nilpotence of a p-group augmentation ideal>.

The quotient homomorphism kills exactly the differences of elements in each $N$-coset, so
$$
\ker\tau=I kG=kG I.
$$
Normality of $N$ makes $I$ stable under conjugation by $G$. Moving <group> elements past factors from $I$ therefore gives $(I kG)^r=I^r kG$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\ker\tau\text{ is a nilpotent ideal of }kG.}
$$

Partition the <conjugacy class> into its orbits under conjugation by $N$. Since none of its elements centralizes $N$, each orbit has size $[N:C_N(x)]>1$, a power of $p$. All elements of such an orbit have the same image in $G/N$, because conjugation by an element of $N$ does not alter an $N$-coset. Its orbit sum therefore has image

Use the usual <Krull–Schmidt theorem> setting, in which indecomposable summands have local <endomorphism rings>; the paper's <rings> $\mathcal O,k$ satisfy it for the <subgroups> involved. The <relative projective module> condition for $H$ means that $M$ is a <direct summand> of a <module> induced from $H$, equivalently, $M$ is a summand of $\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\operatorname{Res}_H^G M$. A <vertex of an indecomposable module> is an inclusion-minimal such <subgroup>. A <source of an indecomposable module> at its <module vertex> $D$ is an indecomposable $RD$-summand $S$ of $M\downarrow_D$ such that $M\mid S\uparrow^G$.

Existence of a <module source> follows by decomposing $M\downarrow_D$ and using <Krull–Schmidt theorem> on the induced decomposition: the indecomposable summand $M$ must occur in the induction of one indecomposable summand $S$. Such $S$ has <module vertex> $D$ as a $D$-module, since relative projectivity of $S$ for a proper <subgroup> would make $M$ relatively projective for that <subgroup>.

In the paper's modular coefficient <rings>, an <indecomposable module> is a <trivial source module> when its <module source> is the rank-one trivial <module> $R$ of its <module vertex>. It is then a summand of $R[G/D]$, a <permutation module>. Conversely suppose $M$ is an indecomposable summand of a <permutation module>. Restriction to a <module vertex> $D$ is a sum of <modules> $R[D/E]$, one for each $D$-orbit of the permutation basis. For a $p$-group $D$, each transitive <permutation module> is indecomposable: over $k$ it has simple <head of a module> over the <local ring> $kD$, and over $\mathcal O$ a nontrivial splitting would give a nontrivial splitting after reduction. A <module source> $S$ must be one of these summands by <Krull–Schmidt theorem>. Its <module vertex> is $D$, whereas $R[D/E]$ is relatively $E$-projective. Thus $E=D$, and $S\cong R$. Therefore **trivial <module source> is equivalent to being an indecomposable permutation-module summand**.

The rank-one formulation uses the paper's coefficient <rings>. If the earlier general coefficient-ring wording were applied to a disconnected <ring>, it would need qualification: for $R=k\times k$ and $G=1$, $(k,0)$ is an indecomposable summand of the <permutation module> $R$, but is not the rank-one trivial <module> $R$. One can instead allow indecomposable summands of the trivial coefficient <module> as <module sources> in that generality.

For the homomorphism request, take $M_i=\mathcal O[G/H_i]$. <Frobenius reciprocity> and <Mackey decomposition> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal OG}(M_1,M_2)
&\cong (\mathcal O[G/H_2])^{H_1}\\
&\cong\bigoplus_{g\in H_1\backslash G/H_2}\mathcal O.
\end{aligned}
$$
The summand for $g$ is generated by the sum of the cosets in the $H_1$-orbit of $gH_2$. The identical orbit-sum construction over $k$ gives one copy of $k$ per <double coset>. These are free bases in both characteristics, since invariance means coefficients are constant on orbits, with no division by orbit size. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal OG}(M_1,M_2)\twoheadrightarrow
\operatorname{Hom}_{kG}(\bar M_1,\bar M_2).}
$$
The same argument applies to <direct sums> of coset <permutation modules>.

If $M$ is a trivial <module source> $kG$-module, choose a permutation lattice $X$ with $M\mid\bar X$. Its projection is an <idempotent> $f\in\operatorname{End}_{kG}(\bar X)$. The endomorphism reduction is surjective by the preceding calculation. Its kernel is $\mathfrak p\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal OG}(X)$, and the endomorphism <algebra> is complete over $\mathcal O$. By <idempotent refinement theorem>, $f$ lifts to an <idempotent> $\hat f$. The lattice $\hat fX$ is a permutation-module summand, hence a trivial <module source> lattice, and reduces to $M$.

To prove uniqueness within the class of trivial <module source> lattices, let $L,L'$ be two such lifts. They are summands of permutation lattices. Lift a modular isomorphism and its inverse between them by the same homomorphism-surjectivity argument, inserting the summand projections on either side. The lifted composites are identity modulo $\mathfrak p$ and therefore invertible over $\mathcal O$, by <Nakayama lemma> or the determinant criterion. The lifted maps are consequently inverse up to an invertible composite and give $L\cong L'$. Thus **each trivial <module source> <module> has a unique trivial <module source> lift up to isomorphism**. This is <permutation homomorphisms lift through modular reduction>; uniqueness is not claimed among arbitrary lattice lifts.

Let $D,E$ be <module vertices> of $M$, and choose a <module> $T$ such that $M\mid\operatorname{Ind}_E^G T$. Relative $D$-projectivity then gives
$$
M\mid\operatorname{Ind}_D^G\operatorname{Res}_D^G\operatorname{Ind}_E^G T.
$$
Apply <Mackey decomposition> to the inner restriction and use induction transitivity. The resulting <module> is a <direct sum> of <modules> induced to $G$ from <subgroups> $D\cap{}^gE$, for representatives $g\in D\backslash G/E$. By <Krull–Schmidt theorem>, the <indecomposable module> $M$ is a summand of one of these <induced representations>. It is therefore relatively projective for one intersection contained in $D$. Minimality of $D$ forces $D=D\cap{}^gE$, so $D\le{}^gE$.

Interchanging $D,E$ gives $E\le{}^hD$ for some $h$. The two inclusions force equal orders, and hence
$$
\boxed{D={}^gE.}
$$
Thus all <vertices of an indecomposable module> are conjugate in $G$.

Let $S,T$ be <module sources> at $D$. Since $S\mid M\downarrow_D$ and $M\mid T\uparrow^G$, <Mackey decomposition> gives
$$
S\mid\operatorname{Res}_D^G\operatorname{Ind}_D^G T
\cong\bigoplus_{g\in D\backslash G/D}
\operatorname{Ind}_{D\cap{}^gD}^D
\operatorname{Res}_{D\cap{}^gD}^{{}^gD}({}^gT).
$$
The <module source> $S$ has <module vertex> $D$. By <Krull–Schmidt theorem> it is a summand of one term, and minimality of its <module vertex> forces $D\cap{}^gD=D$. As both <subgroups> have order $|D|$, $g\in N_G(D)$. The corresponding term is simply ${}^gT$, which is indecomposable. Thus
$$
\boxed{S\cong{}^gT\quad\text{for some }g\in N_G(D).}
$$
This proves conjugacy of <sources of an indecomposable module> at the specified <module vertex>.

\operatorname{Tr}_P^D\left(\frac{\operatorname{id}_U}{[D:P]}\right)=\operatorname{id}_U.
$$
The <D. Higman criterion> therefore makes every $RD$-module relatively $P$-projective. Since $M$ is relatively $D$-projective, transitivity of induction makes it relatively $P$-projective. Minimality of the <module vertex> now forces $D=P$. Thus **every <module vertex> is a $p$-subgroup**.

Put $q=p^n$ and choose a generator $h$ of $H$. In characteristic $p$, $(h-1)^q=h^q-1=0$, so
$$
kH\cong k[t]/(t^q),\qquad t=h-1.
$$
A <module> is a vector space with a <nilpotent operator> $t$ whose nilpotence index is at most $q$. The nilpotent <Jordan normal form>, valid over every <field>, splits it into Jordan blocks. One block of size $i$ is
$$
\boxed{V_i=k[t]/(t^i),\qquad1\le i\le q.}
$$
It is indecomposable: its <endomorphism ring> is $k[t]/(t^i)$, a <local ring> with no nontrivial <idempotents>. Distinct $i$ have distinct dimensions. Conversely a <direct sum> of at least two Jordan blocks is decomposable. Thus there are exactly $q$ indecomposable isomorphism classes.

A homomorphism $V_i\to V_j$ is determined by the image of $1$, which can be any element of $V_j$ annihilated by $t^i$. If $i\ge j$ this is all of $V_j$, of dimension $j$; if $i<j$ it is the span of $t^{j-i},\ldots,t^{j-1}$, of dimension $i$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim_k\operatorname{Hom}_{kH}(V_i,V_j)=\min(i,j).}
$$
This realizes the <indecomposable modules for a cyclic p-group>.

For generators $g,h$ of $P$, write $x=g-1$, $y=h-1$. Then
$$
kP\cong k[x,y]/(x^p,y^p).
$$
For each $\lambda\in k$, define a two-dimensional <module> $M_\lambda$ on basis $v,w$ by $xv=w$, $xw=0$, $yv=\lambda w$, $yw=0$. Every product of two of these operators is zero, so the defining relations hold even when $p=2$.

The action of $x$ is one nonzero nilpotent Jordan block. A decomposition of $M_\lambda$ would decompose its restriction to $k[x]/(x^p)$, which is already indecomposable. Thus $M_\lambda$ is indecomposable. An isomorphism $F:M_\lambda\to M_\mu$ commutes with $x$ and $y$, and consequently
$$
\lambda Fx=Fy=yF=\mu xF=\mu Fx.
$$
Since $F$ is invertible and $x\ne0$, $\lambda=\mu$. The infinite <field> supplies infinitely many parameters, proving **$kP$ has infinite representation type**. This is the <parameter family for an elementary abelian p-group>.

Let $B=kGe$ have defect <group> $D$. Use two vertex-and-source facts, with their relevant content made explicit. Every <indecomposable module> in $B$ has a <module vertex> conjugate into $D$. Also $B$, as a $k[G\times G]$-module with $(g,h)a=gah^{-1}$, is an indecomposable permutation-module summand with <module vertex> $\Delta D=\{(d,d):d\in D\}$ and trivial <module source>. The second fact is the bimodule characterization of a <defect group of a block>; the first follows by applying its relative-$\Delta D$ induction splitting to $B\otimes_{kG}M=M$.

If $D$ is cyclic, every <module source> for every <module vertex> $Q\le D$ occurs among the finitely many <indecomposable modules for a cyclic p-group>. Each <module> in the block is a summand of an induction of one of these finitely many <module sources>. There are finitely many <subgroups> $Q$ and each <induced representation> has finitely many indecomposable summands by <Krull–Schmidt theorem>. Thus **cyclic defect implies finite representation type of the block**.

For the converse, the restriction $B\downarrow_{D\times D}$ has the regular $kD$-bimodule as a <direct summand>. To see this from <module vertices> and <module sources>, its <Brauer quotient of a module> at $\Delta D$ is nonzero. Its summands are <permutation modules> for the $p$-group $D\times D$ and have <module vertices> of order at most $|D|$. A summand detected at $\Delta D$ therefore has <module vertex> $\Delta D$ and trivial <module source>, hence is
$$
\operatorname{Ind}_{\Delta D}^{D\times D}k\cong kD.
$$
Here a transitive <permutation module> for a $p$-group is indecomposable: it has one-dimensional <head of a module> over the local <group algebra> and cannot split into two nonzero <modules>. This also explains the Brauer-quotient detection used in the argument.

For any $kD$-module $U$, tensor that split bimodule inclusion with $U$. It gives
$$
U\mid\operatorname{Res}_D^G(B\otimes_{kD}U),
$$
where $\mid$ denotes being a <direct summand>. If $B$ has only finitely many <indecomposable modules> $M_1,\ldots,M_s$, each $B\otimes_{kD}U$ is a sum of these. Therefore every indecomposable $U$ must occur among the summands of the finite list $M_i\downarrow_D$. Thus $kD$ has finite representation type.

A noncyclic $p$-group has a quotient $C_p\times C_p$: its <Frattini quotient> has rank at least two, since a one-generator <Frattini quotient> forces the <group> itself to be cyclic. Inflation along this quotient embeds all the <indecomposable modules> of that elementary abelian <group>, preserving isomorphisms and indecomposability. Part (b) rules out finite type when $k$ is infinite.

The conclusion remains valid if the splitting <field> $k$ is finite. For completeness, replace the scalar parameter in part (b) by an $m\times m$ companion matrix $T_f$ of an irreducible polynomial $f$. On $k^m\oplus k^m$ use
$$
x=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\I&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\T_f&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
An endomorphism commuting with these has form $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&0\\C&A\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $AT_f=T_fA$. Its <endomorphism ring> has a square-zero <ideal> of the $C$-matrices and quotient $k[T_f]\cong k[t]/(f)$, a <field>, so it is local and the <module> is indecomposable. Finite <fields> have irreducible polynomials of arbitrarily large degrees, giving infinitely many dimensions. This also excludes noncyclic $D$ in that case. Altogether,
$$
\boxed{B\text{ has finite representation type}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\text{ is cyclic}.}
$$

For the conjugation action, fixed elements have coefficients constant on $G$-conjugacy classes. Therefore **$(\mathcal OG)^G=Z(\mathcal OG)$**. More generally an $\mathcal O$-basis of $(\mathcal OG)^H$ consists of sums of the $H$-conjugation orbits on $G$.

For $a\in(\mathcal OG)^H$ define the <relative trace>, or transfer, by
$$
\operatorname{Tr}_H^G(a)=\sum_{g\in G/H}gag^{-1}.
$$
The expression is independent of the coset representatives and is $G$-fixed. If $z\in Z(\mathcal OG)$, then $z\operatorname{Tr}_H^G(a)=\operatorname{Tr}_H^G(za)$; hence its image is a <transfer ideal of conjugation-fixed elements> in the <center of an associative algebra>.

Let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $H$. Trace transitivity gives $\operatorname{Tr}_P^G=\operatorname{Tr}_H^G\operatorname{Tr}_P^H$, proving inclusion of the $P$-image in the $H$-image. Conversely $[H:P]$ is a unit of $\mathcal O$, and for an $H$-fixed element $a$,
$$
\operatorname{Tr}_P^H(a)=[H:P]a.
$$
Thus $\operatorname{Tr}_H^G(a)=\operatorname{Tr}_P^G(a/[H:P])$, proving the reverse inclusion and
$$
\boxed{(\mathcal OG)_H^G=(\mathcal OG)_P^G.}
$$

Use the basis of $D$-conjugation orbit sums in $kG$. A singleton orbit is exactly an element of $C_G(D)$, so these basis vectors span the subring $kC_G(D)$. For a nonsingleton orbit with representative $x$, its stabilizer is the proper <subgroup> $E=C_D(x)<D$, and its orbit sum is $\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(x)$. Thus every nonsingleton basis vector belongs to the sum of proper-subgroup transfer images.

Conversely the coefficient at a $D$-fixed basis element $x\in C_G(D)$ in $\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(a)$ is $[D:E]a_x=0$ in characteristic $p$ whenever $E<D$. Hence the two spans intersect trivially, giving
$$
\boxed{(kG)^D=kC_G(D)\oplus I_D^{\mathrm{prop}},\qquad
I_D^{\mathrm{prop}}=\sum_{E<D}\operatorname{Tr}_E^D((kG)^E).}
$$
For $u\in(kG)^D$, multiplication by a transfer element satisfies $u\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(a)=\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(ua)$ and $\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(a)u=\operatorname{Tr}_E^D(au)$. Therefore the second summand is a two-sided <ideal>, as required; it need not be nilpotent in this general setting.

Projection onto the subring in part (b), along the two-sided <ideal>, is the <Brauer homomorphism>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}_D:(kG)^D\to kC_G(D),\qquad
\sum_x a_xx\longmapsto\sum_{x\in C_G(D)}a_xx.}
$$
It is a unital <algebra> homomorphism because it is the quotient by that <ideal> followed by the displayed subring identification.

Put $N=N_G(D)$. The projection is $N$-equivariant, since $N$ preserves the <centralizer>. Its restricted kernel is
$$
\boxed{\ker(\operatorname{Br}_D|_{(kG)^N})
=(kG)^N\cap\sum_{E<D}\operatorname{Tr}_E^D((kG)^E).}
$$
Equivalently it is spanned by the sums of the $N$-conjugation orbits on $G$ disjoint from $C_G(D)$. Normality of $D$ in $N$ ensures that an $N$-orbit either lies wholly inside that <centralizer> or wholly outside it.

Write $N=N_G(D)$, $C=C_G(D)$, and use the commutative transfer <ideals>
$$
I_D=\operatorname{Tr}_D^G((kG)^D)\subseteq Z(kG),\qquad
J_D=\operatorname{Tr}_D^N(kC)\subseteq(kC)^N.
$$
The target fixed <algebra> is commutative, because $C\le N$ makes every $N$-fixed element of $kC$ central in $kC$.

The <Brauer morphism and relative trace> identity is
$$
\operatorname{Br}_D(\operatorname{Tr}_D^G a)=\operatorname{Tr}_D^N(\operatorname{Br}_D a).
$$
Indeed let $D$ act on $G/D$ by left multiplication in the trace sum. Its fixed cosets are exactly $gD$ with $g\in N$; every other orbit has size divisible by $p$. After Brauer projection the contributions on each such nonfixed orbit are equal, so they cancel. Fixed cosets give precisely the right-hand side. Since the Brauer projection of $(kG)^D$ is all of $kC$, this proves a surjection **$I_D\twoheadrightarrow J_D$**.

We use the <trace criterion for defect groups>: a block <idempotent> $e$ belongs to $I_D$ exactly when its defect <group> is conjugate into $D$; among these, $\operatorname{Br}_D(e)\ne0$ exactly when its defect <group> is $D$ up to conjugacy. The criterion can be read from the class-sum basis: if $E=C_D(x)$ then
$$
\operatorname{Tr}_D^G(\operatorname{Tr}_E^D x)
=\operatorname{Tr}_E^G x=[C_G(x):E]\,[x^G].
$$
This coefficient is nonzero exactly when $E$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $C_G(x)$. Thus $I_D$ is spanned by classes whose class defect is conjugate into $D$, and the kernel of its Brauer projection is the sum of the corresponding proper-subgroup transfer <ideals>. The block version follows by refinement of the <central idempotents> in these <ideals>.

For clarity, the refinement fact needed here is <primitive idempotents under a surjection of commutative Artinian ideals>: a surjection between <ideals> of commutative Artinian <algebras> bijects the <primitive idempotents> in the <module source> whose images are nonzero with the <primitive idempotents> in the target. Decompose the <algebras> into local factors. An <ideal> in a local Artinian factor either contains the identity and is the whole factor, or lies in its nilpotent maximal <ideal> and has no nonzero <idempotents>. A surviving local factor has a local quotient, so its identity remains primitive. This proves the stated refinement fact without imposing nilpotence on the entire kernel.

The <primitive idempotents> in $I_D$ are the block <idempotents> with defect conjugate into $D$, and the surviving ones have defect $D$. Applying refinement to the surjection above gives
$$
\boxed{e\longmapsto\operatorname{Br}_D(e):
\{\text{blocks of }G\text{ with defect }D\}\ \longleftrightarrow\
\{\text{primitive idempotents of }J_D\}.}
$$
This is the <Brauer morphism on a defect transfer ideal>; the target is the transfer <ideal> $J_D$, not the entire fixed <center of an associative algebra>.

Let $D$ be a $p$-subgroup and let $N_G(D)\le H\le G$. Put

\mathcal Y=\{H\cap{}^gD:g\in G\setminus H\}.

A <module> relatively projective for a family means a summand of a sum of <modules> induced from its members, allowing <subgroups> and conjugates. The <Green correspondence> gives inverse bijections $f,g$ between the indecomposable $RG$-modules and $RH$-modules with <module vertex> $D$, up to isomorphism, such that
$$
\operatorname{Res}_H^G M\cong f(M)\oplus M_{\mathcal Y},\qquad
\operatorname{Ind}_H^G U\cong g(U)\oplus U_{\mathcal X}.
$$
The error terms are respectively relatively $\mathcal Y$-projective and $\mathcal X$-projective. In particular the displayed correspondents are the unique summands of <module vertex> $D$, occur with multiplicity one, and have the same <module source> as the original <module>, up to <normalizer> conjugacy.

We will also use the general form of <Green correspondence>: replace <module vertex> $D$ by any <module vertex> $Q\le D$ not conjugate into a member of $\mathcal X$. Put $b=\operatorname{Br}_D(e)$. By equivariance of the <Brauer homomorphism>, $b$ is a <central idempotent> of $kK$, since $C_G(D)\le K\le N_G(D)$. The <Nagao module theorem> asserts that, if $eM=M$, then
$$
\boxed{M\downarrow_K=bM\oplus(1-b)M,}
$$
and every indecomposable summand of $(1-b)M$ is relatively projective for a $p$-subgroup of $K$ not containing $D$. Because $D$ is normal in $K$, its <module vertices> cannot contain $D$ even after $K$-conjugation.

Here is a direct proof. As an element of $(kG)^K$, $e-b$ is a linear combination of $K$-conjugation orbit sums supported outside $C_G(D)$. An orbit represented by $x$ has sum
$$
\operatorname{Tr}_{C_K(x)}^K(x).
$$
Its stabilizer $C_K(x)$ does not contain $D$. On $T=(1-b)M$, the element $e-b$ acts as the identity. Map the orbit-sum expression into $\operatorname{End}_k(M)$ by left multiplication, and compose on both sides with the $K$-linear projection onto $T$. It follows that
$$
\operatorname{id}_T\in\sum_{x\notin C_G(D)}
\operatorname{Tr}_{C_K(x)}^K\operatorname{End}_{kC_K(x)}(T).
$$
Replace each <centralizer> by a Sylow $p$-subgroup of it using trace transitivity and its invertible prime-to-$p$ index. Project this identity further onto an indecomposable summand $U$ of $T$. Its <endomorphism ring> is local by <Krull–Schmidt theorem>. A sum of nonunits cannot be the identity in a <local ring>, so one of the trace terms is a unit $u$ in $\operatorname{End}_{kK}(U)$. Multiplying the endomorphism inside that trace by $u^{-1}$ gives $\operatorname{id}_U$ as a single relative trace. The <D. Higman criterion> makes $U$ relatively projective for that <Sylow subgroup> $Q\le C_K(x)$, and $D\nleq Q$. This proves the assertion rather than just invoking the theorem's name.

Take $N=N_G(D)$ and $b=\operatorname{Br}_D(e)$. If $eM=M$, the <Nagao module theorem> with $K=N$ puts every vertex-$D$ summand of $M\downarrow_N$ inside $bM$. In particular its <Green correspondence> summand $M'$ satisfies $bM'=M'$.

Conversely a <central idempotent> acts as zero or one on the <indecomposable module> $M$. If $eM=0$, apply the same theorem to $1-e$. Its Brauer image is $1-b$, so $(1-b)M'=M'$, incompatible with $bM'=M'$. Thus
$$
\boxed{eM=M\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Br}_D(e)M'=M'.}

For the unheaded induction request, write $\mathcal X=\{D\cap{}^gD:g\notin N\}$. We prove the sharper intersection bound, not just a drop in <module vertex> size. We use <Green correspondence> on permutation bimodules and the usual <module vertex> test for a permutation-module summand: its <Brauer quotient of a module> at $P$ is nonzero precisely when a <module vertex> contains a conjugate of $P$. This test follows by restricting to the $p$-group $P$, decomposing the permutation basis into its orbits, and noting that proper-orbit sums are precisely the proper-subgroup transfer terms.

Consider $kG$ as a $k[G\times D]$-module, with $(g,d)a=gad^{-1}$. It is the transitive <permutation module> induced from $\Delta D$. Set $L=G\times D$ and $L_0=N\times D$; the latter contains $N_L(\Delta D)$. Its restriction decomposes as
$$
kG\downarrow_{L_0}=kN\oplus\bigoplus_{g\in N\backslash G/D,\ g\notin N}k[NgD].
$$
Each outside term has stabilizer a conjugate of a <subgroup> of $\Delta D$. If a diagonal <subgroup> $\Delta Q\le\Delta D$ embeds into one of these stabilizers, then $Q\le D\cap{}^gD$ for an outside representative. Thus its diagonal <module vertices> are in the intersection error family.

Every indecomposable $L_0$-summand of $kN$ has <module vertex> $\Delta D$. To justify this, its endomorphisms are right multiplications by elements of $(kN)^D$. The coefficient projection $\operatorname{Br}_D^N$ on this <algebra> has nilpotent kernel: every nonsingleton $D$-orbit sum lies in the kernel of $kN\to k(N/D)$, by part 1(b), and that kernel is nilpotent by part 1(a). Hence no nonzero <idempotent> endomorphism can have zero Brauer image. Its image summand has nonzero <Brauer quotient of a module> at $\Delta D$, and its <module vertex>, already contained in a conjugate of $\Delta D$, must be $\Delta D$.

Apply the general <Green correspondence> from part (a) to $L,L_0$. If an indecomposable summand of $kG$ has <module vertex> outside the intersection error family, its correspondent must be a summand of $kN$, since the outside double-coset <modules> have only error-family <module vertices>. It therefore has <module vertex> $\Delta D$. Consequently **every summand of $kG$ not having <module vertex> $\Delta D$ has <module vertex> conjugate into an outside diagonal intersection**. An intersection
$$
\Delta D\cap{}^{(g,d)}\Delta D
$$
with $(g,d)\notin L_0$ projects in its first coordinate into $D\cap{}^gD$ with $g\notin N$; this is the error family required in the question.

Now form the split permutation bimodule
$$
W=(1-e)kG b.
$$
Its <Brauer quotient of a module> at $\Delta D$ is zero. Indeed $kG(\Delta D)=kC_G(D)$, left multiplication by $e$ becomes multiplication by $\operatorname{Br}_D(e)=b$, and right multiplication by $b$ remains multiplication by $b$. Therefore
$$
W(\Delta D)=(1-b)kC_G(D)b=0.
$$
Here $b$ is central in $kC_G(D)$, so the last product is zero. Every indecomposable bimodule summand of $W$ consequently has <module vertex> in the outside intersection family, by the preceding paragraph, and has trivial <module source> since it is a permutation-module summand.

Since $V$ has vertex $D$, necessarily $D\le K$. Let $S$ be a <module source> of $V$ at $D$. The splitting $V\mid kK\otimes_{kD}S$ remains a splitting after applying $b$, since $bV=V$. Inducing to $G$ and applying $1-e$ gives
$$
(1-e)\operatorname{Ind}_K^G V\mid W\otimes_{kD}S.
$$
A trivial-source bimodule summand with <module vertex> conjugate into $\Delta Q$ is a summand of $\operatorname{Ind}_{\Delta Q}^{G\times D}k$. Tensoring with $S$ turns this into a <module> induced to $G$ from $Q$ (with the appropriate conjugate restriction of $S$). Thus every summand on the right is relatively projective for some $Q\le D\cap{}^gD$, $g\notin N$. Each $V_j$ lies in the left-hand side, and <Krull–Schmidt theorem> now gives
$$
\boxed{D_j\le_G D\cap{}^gD\quad\text{for some }g\in G\setminus N_G(D).}
$$
This proves <Juhász induction refinement>. If there are no outside elements, the error family is empty and the complementary <module> is zero.

A finite-dimensional <group algebra> has <finite representation type of a group algebra> when there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional <indecomposable modules>. The definition concerns all dimensions, not merely finitely many <modules> in each fixed dimension.

Let $e_B$ be the block <idempotent> and choose a maximal <Brauer pair> $(D,b_D)$: $b_D$ is a block <idempotent> of $kC_G(D)$ with $b_D\operatorname{Br}_D(e_B)\ne0$. The <inertial index of a block> is
$$
\boxed{e(B)=|N_G(D,b_D):DC_G(D)|,}
$$
where $N_G(D,b_D)$ is the stabilizer of the chosen pair. Changing the pair by conjugation does not alter this number. For cyclic $D$, it is a prime-to-$p$ divisor of $p-1$. Here $n\ge1$, as required for the order-$p$ <subgroup> $Q$ to exist.

Put $H=N_G(Q)$. Since $Q$ is characteristic in $D$, $N_G(D)\le H$, so <Green correspondence> applies. In fact the nonprojective <modules> in $B$ have a particularly simple correspondence. If $g\notin H$, a nontrivial intersection $D\cap{}^gD$ would contain the unique order-$p$ <subgroup> of both cyclic <groups>, giving $Q={}^gQ$ and hence $g\in H$, a contradiction. Thus every such intersection is trivial. The error <modules> in induction are projective. In restriction, an intersection $H\cap{}^gD$ cannot contain a conjugate of a nontrivial <subgroup> of $D$ by an element of $H$: such a conjugate contains $Q$, and that would again force ${}^gQ=Q$. The general <Green correspondence> therefore pairs all <indecomposable modules> with nontrivial <module vertex> contained in a conjugate of $D$. In particular it pairs the nonprojective <modules> of $B$ with the nonprojective <modules> of its Brauer correspondent $b$ in $H$; the block-compatible form of the correspondence selects $b$. Explicitly,
$$
M\downarrow_H=f(M)\oplus M_{\mathcal Y},\qquad
f(M)\uparrow^G=M\oplus P,
$$
where $P$ is projective and the restriction error family is $\mathcal Y=\{H\cap{}^gD:g\notin H\}$. One should not replace this restriction error term by projectives without an additional argument about $H$.

For the final structure calculation we use the general cyclic-defect structure theorem, which is permitted here as a general fact: the basic <algebra> of a split <block with cyclic defect group> is a <Brauer tree algebra>. Its tree has $e(B)$ edges, one simple <module> per edge, and exceptional multiplicity
$$
m=\frac{|D|-1}{e(B)}.
$$
For a one-edge tree the basic <algebra> presentation is $k[t]/(t^{m+1})$. This is the local presentation of the tree <algebra>, not an assertion that an arbitrary block with one simple <module> is automatically uniserial.

With $e(B)=1$ the tree has one edge and $m=p^n-1$. Thus the basic <algebra> of $B$ is
$$
A_0=k[t]/(t^{p^n}).
$$
It has exactly one simple <module>, $k=A_0/(t)$. Its <ideals> are precisely $(t^i)$: for any nonzero element, factor out its lowest power of $t$, after which the remaining factor is a unit. The regular projective therefore has the unique submodule chain
$$
A_0\supset tA_0\supset\cdots\supset t^{p^n-1}A_0\supset0,
$$
with each consecutive factor one-dimensional and simple. A <Morita equivalence> from $A_0$ to $B$ preserves simple <modules>, <projective covers>, submodule lattices and composition length. Hence
$$
\boxed{B\text{ has one simple module }S,\qquad P(S)\text{ is uniserial of length }p^n.}
$$
In the split setting one may also write $B\cong M_{\dim_k S}(A_0)$. The <projective cover> has $p^n$ <composition factors> all isomorphic to $S$, so its vector-space dimension is $p^n\dim_kS$, not generally $p^n$.

There is also an elementary reason why the uniserial conclusion follows once the one-simple-module conclusion and finite type are known. If the basic local <algebra> had $\dim J/J^2\ge2$, quotienting its radical square and all but two generators would give $k[x,y]/(x,y)^2$. The two-dimensional parameter family from Question 3(b) would then give infinitely many indecomposables. Thus $J/J^2$ is one-dimensional. <Nakayama lemma> makes $J$ generated by one element $t$, so its powers yield a chain and the basic <algebra> is $k[t]/(t^r)$. The cyclic-defect calculation above identifies $r=p^n$. This explains why finite representation type rules out branching in this <projective cover>.
