= 1
{parent=Paper 25}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=1}

Write $A=E\times\{p\}$, $B=\{p\}\times E$ and $D=\Delta_E$. Their <self-intersection numbers> vanish: each factor curve has trivial <normal bundle>, and the <self-intersection of the diagonal of a curve> is $2-2g(E)=0$. Each pair meets transversely once. Their <intersection pairing> is therefore
$$
\begin{pmatrix}A^2&A\cdot B&A\cdot D\\B\cdot A&B^2&B\cdot D\\D\cdot A&D\cdot B&D^2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det=2.
$$
If a rational <linear combination> of their <cycle classes> vanished in $H^2(E\times E,\mathbb Q)$, intersecting it with $A,B,D$ would multiply its coefficients by this invertible matrix and give zero. All coefficients must vanish, proving **the three classes are <linearly independent>**.

The <cycle class map> detects the <Chow Künneth failure for an elliptic self-product>. The codimension-one image of the <external product of Chow classes> consists of sums from $\operatorname{CH}^1(E)\otimes\operatorname{CH}^0(E)$ and $\operatorname{CH}^0(E)\otimes\operatorname{CH}^1(E)$. Here $\operatorname{CH}^0(E)=\mathbb Z[E]$, and every divisor's <cohomology class> is its <degree of a divisor> times the class of a point. Thus the image's cohomology is contained in the span of $[A],[B]$. The diagonal's independent class lies outside that span, so its <Chow class> lies outside the external-product image. Therefore **the natural map of Chow groups is not surjective**.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

Embed $Y$ in <projective space> and let $H$ be its hyperplane <Cartier divisor>. Since $f$ is surjective between integral surfaces, it is <generically finite>, with degree $r>0$. Its <pullback of a Cartier divisor> $L=f^*H$ is a <nef divisor>, and the <projection formula for algebraic cycles> gives $L^2=rH^2>0$. The same formula gives $L\cdot C=H\cdot f_*C=0$, because the curve is contracted to a point.

The <Hodge index theorem for algebraic surfaces> makes the <intersection pairing on the Picard group of a surface> have signature $(1,\rho-1)$ on $N^1(X)_{\mathbb R}$. The orthogonal complement of any positive-square vector, including $L$, is <negative definite>. The numerical class of $C$ is nonzero, since an <ample divisor> on $X$ intersects every nonzero <effective divisor> positively. Thus the <contracted curve has negative self-intersection> conclusion applies:
$$
\boxed{C^2<0.}
$$

= Solution

Let $D$ be an <effective divisor> <linearly equivalent> to $C$. If $C$ were not a component of $D$, all <intersection multiplicities> between its components and $C$ would be nonnegative, contradicting $D\cdot C=C^2<0$. Hence $D=C+D'$ with $D'$ effective and <linearly equivalent> to zero. For an <ample divisor> $H$, this gives $H\cdot D'=0$. Positivity of $H$ on every irreducible component of a nonzero effective divisor forces $D'=0$. Therefore $\boxed{D=C}$, illustrating the <rigidity of a negative curve>.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use <countability of negative curves>. A <smooth projective surface> over $\mathbb C$ is a <compact> <smooth manifold> homotopy equivalent to a <finite CW complex>, so its integral <cohomology group> $H^2(X,\mathbb Z)$ is finitely generated and therefore countable. Distinct irreducible curves $C,D$ with $C^2<0$ cannot have the same <cohomology class>: that would give $C\cdot D=C^2<0$, while distinct effective curves have nonnegative <intersection multiplicities>. Assigning a <negative curve> its <cohomology class> is thus <injective>, proving **there are at most countably many negative curves**.

For zero <self-intersection>, uncountable families do occur. On the product of two <projective lines> $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$, every fiber $\mathbb P^1\times\{p\}$ has <self-intersection> zero, since a different fiber is disjoint and has the same <numerical equivalence of divisors> class. The parameter $p\in\mathbb P^1(\mathbb C)$ varies over an uncountable set. Thus **yes, there can be uncountably many such curves**.

= c
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $X\subset\mathbb P^3$ have degree $d\geq3$ and hyperplane class $H$. The <adjunction formula> gives $K_X=(d-4)H$. For a line $L\subset X$, the same formula on the <smooth rational curve> $L$ yields $(K_X+L)\cdot L=-2$, hence
$$
\boxed{L^2=2-d<0.}
$$
Thus all lines are <negative curves>, and part (b) makes their set countable. The <Fano scheme of lines> is a closed <projective scheme> of $\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)$. A positive-dimensional complex algebraic component would have uncountably many points, so this scheme is zero-dimensional. A <projective scheme> of <finite type> has only finitely many zero-dimensional points. This proves the <finiteness of lines on a smooth surface of degree at least three>.

{parent=Paper 25}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=4}

Choose a <projective embedding> of the <abelian surface> $X$. By <Bertini's theorem>, a general <hyperplane section> $C$ is a <smooth projective curve> that is <connected>. It is <ample>, so $C^2>0$. Choose $p\in C$ and translate the inclusion so it sends $p$ to the identity of $X$. The <universal property of the Jacobian variety> then gives a homomorphism of <abelian varieties>
$$
\Phi:J(C)\longrightarrow X
$$
whose composition with the based <Abel map of an algebraic curve> is this translated inclusion.

The image is a closed connected algebraic subgroup, since $J(C)$ is projective, and it contains a translate of $C$. If the image had dimension one, that irreducible curve would be the image subgroup itself. A distinct coset is disjoint from it and has the same numerical divisor class: translation acts trivially on cohomology, being homotopic to the identity on the <complex torus>. Its <self-intersection> would therefore be zero, contradicting $C^2>0$. The image has dimension two and is all of $X$. Thus **$\Phi$ is surjective**, as expressed by the principle that an <ample curve on an abelian surface generates the surface>.

The divisors have no common irreducible component, since a common curve would give infinitely many intersection points. The <Bézout theorem> therefore gives total <intersection multiplicity> $\deg C\deg D=3\cdot3=9$. As all intersection is supported at $p$,
$$
\boxed{I_p(C,D)=9.}
$$

= b
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $D=\{y^2z=x^3\}$, $L=\{z=0\}$ and $C=3L$. This projective <cuspidal cubic> is irreducible and meets $L$ only at $p=[0:1:0]$. At $p$, the derivative with respect to $z$ is $y^2=1$, so $D$ is smooth. In the affine chart $y=1$, it is $z=x^3$, and $L$ is $z=0$, so $I_p(L,D)=3$. Multiplicity is linear in the divisor $C=3L$, giving
$$
\boxed{I_p(C,D)=3\,I_p(L,D)=9.}
$$

= c
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Apply a <projective transformation> to put the <cuspidal cubic> in the form $D=\{F=y^2z-x^3=0\}$. Since $L$ meets $D$ at only one smooth point $p$, the <Bézout theorem> forces intersection multiplicity three there. Hence $L$ is the <tangent line> at a <flex>.

The homogeneous <Hessian matrix> has determinant
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}-6x&0&0\\0&2z&2y\\0&2y&0\end{pmatrix}=24xy^2.
$$
By the <Hessian criterion for a flex>, every smooth flex lies in $D\cap\{xy^2=0\}$. These intersections are $[0:0:1]$, the <cusp>, and $[0:1:0]$, which is smooth. Thus the <unique smooth flex of a cuspidal cubic> is $p=[0:1:0]$, with <tangent line> $L=\{z=0\}$. Conversely the pair in part (b) has exactly the required properties. Therefore **there is one projective-equivalence class**, represented by
$$
\boxed{(C,D)=\bigl(3\{z=0\},\ \{y^2z=x^3\}\bigr).}
$$

= Solution

The <adjunction formula> on the degree-$d$ <projective hypersurface> gives $K_X=(d-4)H$. Since $C$ is a <smooth rational curve>, adjunction on $C$ gives $(K_X+C)\cdot C=2g(C)-2=-2$. Its degree is $H\cdot C=a$, so the <smooth rational-curve self-intersection on a projective hypersurface> formula is
$$
\boxed{C^2=-2-(d-4)a.}
$$

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Noether–Lefschetz theorem> says that a <very general> smooth degree-$d$ surface in $\mathbb P^3_{\mathbb C}$, for $d\geq4$, has <Picard group> freely generated by its hyperplane class $H$. Here very general means outside a countable union of proper closed loci in the parameter space of surfaces.

If such a surface contained a <smooth rational curve> $C$, its divisor class would be $mH$ for an integer $m$. Since $H\cdot C=md>0$, one has $m>0$ and therefore $C^2=m^2d>0$. Part (a), however, gives $C^2=-2-(d-4)\deg C\leq-2$, a contradiction. Thus **a <very general surface of degree at least four has no smooth rational curve>**. The statement concerns smooth embedded curves; a singular curve can still have rational normalization.
