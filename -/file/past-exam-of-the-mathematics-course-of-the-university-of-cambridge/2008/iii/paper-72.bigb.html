The five printed subparts are separated below. All use one well-mixed <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution> with a <stellar yield> defined per permanently locked mass; the gross <star formation rate> must be distinguished from that locked-mass rate.

= i
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write the gross <star formation rate> as $\psi=kM_g$, with $k>0$ constant. Use the <instantaneous recycling approximation>: a fraction $F$ returns promptly, while a fraction $1-F$ stays in long-lived <stars> and remnants. Assume $0\le F<1$, instantaneous mixing, and no <galactic gas inflow> or <galactic outflow>. If $M_s$ denotes the permanently locked stellar mass, <mass conservation> gives
$$
\dot M_s=(1-F)\psi,\qquad \dot M_g=-(1-F)\psi=-(1-F)kM_g.
$$
Set $\lambda=(1-F)k$. Integration, with $M_g(0)=M_0$, gives the <exponential closed-box gas consumption> law
$$
\boxed{M_g(t)=M_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad \psi(t)=kM_0e^{-\lambda t}.}
$$
The locked mass is $M_s=M_0(1-e^{-\lambda t})$, so $M_g+M_s=M_0$ at every time. The decay time is $[(1-F)k]^{-1}$, longer than the gross gas-processing time $k^{-1}$ because returned gas can form subsequent generations. The formal $F=1$ limit retains all gas and locks no mass; a finite <stellar yield> defined per locked mass then needs separate treatment.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $Z$ be the well-mixed <gas-phase metallicity>, measured as a metal mass fraction. Removing gas into <stars> takes metals at rate $Z\psi$; prompt return restores existing metals at rate $ZF\psi$, together with newly synthesized metals at rate $Y(1-F)\psi$. Thus the <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution> has metal budget
$$
\frac{d(M_gZ)}{dt}=-Z(1-F)\psi+Y(1-F)\psi.
$$
Expand the left side and use $\dot M_g=-(1-F)\psi$. The terms proportional to existing <gas-phase metallicity> cancel, leaving
$$
M_g\dot Z=Y(1-F)\psi.
$$
Since $\psi=kM_g$, initially pristine gas obeys
$$
\boxed{Z(t)=Y(1-F)kt=Y\lambda t.}
$$
The <stellar yield> here is per permanently locked mass, so inserting another factor $1-F$ into $Y$ would double-count recycling. The approximation treats metals as a trace constituent and ignores finite stellar-return delays. Apply it while $Y\lambda t\ll1$; the formal unlimited linear growth is not a physical prediction for almost entirely metallic gas.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A newly formed <stellar population> inherits the current <gas-phase metallicity>. Eliminate time between locked-mass growth and enrichment:
$$
dM_s=-dM_g,\qquad M_g\,dZ=Y\,dM_s.
$$
For $Y>0$, integration from pristine gas gives $M_g=M_0e^{-Z/Y}$ and hence the <closed-box metallicity distribution>
$$
\frac{dM_s}{dZ}=\frac{M_0}{Y}e^{-Z/Y}.
$$
To obtain numbers rather than mass, specify a fixed <initial mass function> and count a long-lived tracer population. Let $\eta$ be its number of surviving <stars> per unit locked stellar mass. At the final <gas-phase metallicity> $Z_f$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{dN}{dZ}=\frac{\eta M_0}{Y}e^{-Z/Y},\qquad 0\le Z\le Z_f,}
$$
with zero density outside that interval. The cumulative count is $N(<Z)=\eta M_0(1-e^{-Z/Y})$ within the interval, and the normalized <metallicity distribution function> is
$$
p(Z)=\frac{e^{-Z/Y}}{Y(1-e^{-Z_f/Y})}.
$$
These equations do not use $\psi(t)$: any positive <star formation rate> gives the same distribution under the same chemical assumptions. If the tracer number is instead normalized per gross formed mass, replace $\eta$ by that normalization divided by $1-F$. Short-lived <stars> require an age-dependent survival factor and generally do not follow this number distribution. In a <logarithmic metallicity distribution>, $dN/d\log_{10}Z=(\ln10)Z\,dN/dZ$; its interior peak is at $Z=Y$, whereas the distribution per linear interval decreases from $Z=0$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Divide metal enrichment by gas depletion in the <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution>:
$$
\frac{dZ}{dM_g}=-\frac{Y}{M_g}.
$$
The <logarithm> appears on integrating from $M_0$ and $Z=0$:
$$
Z=-Y\int_{M_0}^{M_g}\frac{dm}{m}=Y\ln\frac{M_0}{M_g}.
$$
With the <gas fraction of a galaxy> $\mu=M_g/M_0$, this is
$$
\boxed{Z=Y\ln(1/\mu).}
$$
It gives $Z=0$ at $\mu=1$ and increasing <gas-phase metallicity> as gas is consumed, independently of the time dependence of the <star formation rate>. For this closed initially star-free system, $M_0=M_g+M_s$, so this initial-reservoir fraction equals the present gas-to-total baryonic fraction. That equality fails in an open system.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

The qualitative prediction of <galactic chemical evolution> is useful: later <stellar populations> tend to inherit more enriched gas, and old <stellar populations> can preserve low <stellar metallicity>. The <closed-box metallicity distribution>, however, predicts many more metal-poor long-lived dwarfs in the solar neighbourhood than are observed. This is the <G-dwarf problem>, also apparent with other long-lived tracers. A single well-mixed box cannot describe the different age and abundance distributions of the <galactic disk>, <galactic bulge>, and <stellar halo> of the <Milky Way>. Nor need its exponentially declining <star formation rate> match the history of ongoing disk <star formation>.

Prolonged relatively metal-poor <galactic gas inflow> supplies fuel while diluting the enrichment, allowing many <stars> to form after the earliest enrichment stage; pre-enrichment of the initial reservoir also removes a pristine tail. <Galactic outflows> lower the <effective yield>, but a constant well-mixed outflow alone still gives an exponential <metallicity distribution function>, and is not automatically a cure for the <G-dwarf problem>. Spatially varying <star formation>, radial transport and migration, and selection of local survivors complicate a comparison with one global box. A varying <initial mass function> or <stellar yield> changes the enrichment law. Finally, finite stellar lifetimes and <Type Ia supernovae> make iron enrichment delayed, so an observed iron-abundance distribution is not interchangeable with the instantaneous total-metal distribution. **Gas exchange, spatially distinct populations, and delayed enrichment are essential qualifications to the closed-box prediction.**

= Solution

The stated kinematics do not, by themselves, fix a <galaxy>'s intrinsic axis ratio. The requested curve is the usual simplified <oblate-rotator flattening proxy>; its additional geometric and streaming conventions should be explicit. Put $q=b/a=1-e$ and adopt the approximate moment prescription
$$
q\simeq\frac{\sigma_z^2}{\sigma_x^2+v_p^2}.
$$
Here $v_p^2$ is the ordered support assigned to one in-plane component. Using $\sigma_z^2=(1-\beta)\sigma_x^2$ and solving gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_p}{\sigma_x}\simeq\sqrt{\frac{e-\beta}{1-e}}.}
$$
In this prescription, isotropic random motions give the <oblate isotropic rotator> curve; positive $\beta$ allows flattening with less rotation. A real solution requires $0\le e<1$, $\beta<1$, and $e\ge\beta$. Equality $e=\beta$ gives no rotation. This is a useful diagnostic approximation, not an exact identity for arbitrary density distributions.

To see what the <tensor virial theorem> actually requires, take a finite stationary axisymmetric system with mass-weighted constant <velocity dispersions> and azimuthal streaming speed $v_{\rm az}$. Since $u_x=-v_{\rm az}\sin\phi$, the azimuthal average has $\langle u_x^2\rangle=v_{\rm az}^2/2$. The $xx$ and $zz$ components give
$$
-W_{xx}=M(\sigma_x^2+v_{\rm az}^2/2),\qquad -W_{zz}=M\sigma_z^2,
$$
and therefore, with the <oblate tensor-virial shape factor> $Q=W_{xx}/W_{zz}$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_{\rm az}^2}{\sigma_x^2}=2\big[(1-\beta)Q-1\big].}
$$
For similar oblate self-gravitating density surfaces, write $s=\sqrt{1-q^2}$. The ellipsoidal force coefficients, obtained by integrating the Newtonian force over those surfaces, are
$$
A_1=\frac{q\arccos q}{s^3}-\frac{q^2}{s^2},\quad A_3=\frac2{s^2}-\frac{2q\arccos q}{s^3},\quad Q=\frac{A_1}{q^2A_3}.
$$
The common radial integral in the <stellar potential-energy tensor> cancels, but these shape coefficients remain. At $q=1/2$, $Q\simeq1.79$, rather than $1/q=2$. With $\beta=0$, the intrinsic azimuthal ratio is about $1.26$, whereas the printed simplified ratio is $1$. Recovering the latter uses both $v_p=v_{\rm az}/\sqrt2$ and $Q\simeq1/q$. Thus, if the printed $v$ literally means intrinsic azimuthal speed, its claimed exact relation omits necessary model assumptions and a streaming normalization. The next sketch displays the requested approximate curve with that qualification.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Use cylindrical coordinates and tracer density $\nu(R,z)$. A steady axisymmetric <Jeans equation>, with no mean radial or vertical flow, is
$$
\frac{\partial(\nu\overline{v_R^2})}{\partial R}+\frac{\partial(\nu\overline{v_Rv_z})}{\partial z}+\frac{\nu}{R}(\overline{v_R^2}-\overline{v_\phi^2})=-\nu\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial R}.
$$
Write $\overline{v_R^2}=\sigma_R^2$, $\overline{v_\phi^2}=\bar v_\phi^2+\sigma_\phi^2$, and $V_c^2=R\partial_R\Phi$. Rearrangement gives the <stellar asymmetric drift> equation
$$
V_c^2-\bar v_\phi^2=\sigma_\phi^2-\sigma_R^2-\frac{R}{\nu}\frac{\partial(\nu\sigma_R^2)}{\partial R}-\frac{R}{\nu}\frac{\partial(\nu\overline{v_Rv_z})}{\partial z}.
$$
For an isotropic <velocity ellipsoid>, all three dispersions equal $\sigma$ and the cross stress vanishes. Therefore the required general result is
$$
\boxed{\bar v_\phi=\sqrt{V_c^2-A\sigma^2},\qquad A=-\frac{d\ln(\nu\sigma^2)}{d\ln R}.}
$$
The positive root is for a prograde <galactic disk>. A declining radial stress gives $A>0$: random motions supply some support, so mean stellar rotation is slower than cold-gas circular rotation. For $A\sigma^2\ll V_c^2$, $V_c-\bar v_\phi\simeq A\sigma^2/(2V_c)$. The condition $\sigma<V_c$ alone is insufficient; one also needs $A\sigma^2\le V_c^2$.

There is no universal numerical coefficient expressible from $V_c$ and $\sigma$ alone. For constant <velocity dispersion> and $\nu\propto R^{-p}$, $A=p$. Thus $p=2$ gives the often-used estimate $\bar v_\phi=\sqrt{V_c^2-2\sigma^2}$, while $p=1$ gives a different answer with the same $V_c$ and $\sigma$. For an <exponential galactic disk> tracer with $\nu\propto e^{-R/R_d}$ and $\sigma^2\propto e^{-R/R_\sigma}$, the <exponential-disk asymmetric drift> has $A=R(R_d^{-1}+R_\sigma^{-1})$.

Real <galactic disks> generally have anisotropic <velocity dispersions>, often $\sigma_R>\sigma_\phi>\sigma_z$. In the small-random-motion <epicyclic motion> limit for a flat <galaxy rotation curve>, $\sigma_\phi^2/\sigma_R^2\simeq1/2$, so even the non-gradient term matters. The <velocity ellipsoid> can tilt, and bars or spiral structure can violate steady axisymmetry. Age-dependent <galactic disk heating> makes the lag population dependent. Gas has finite turbulent and thermal stresses too, though a cold gas disk often approximates $V_c$ much better than a hot <stellar population>.

= Solution

For a <singular isothermal sphere>, spherical integration of the <mass density> gives
$$
M(<R)=\int_0^R4\pi r^2\frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi Gr^2}\,dr=\frac{2\sigma^2R}{G}.
$$
The <spherical shell theorem> makes the radial attraction $GM(<R)/R^2$. Balancing it with circular centripetal acceleration yields
$$
\boxed{M(<R)=\frac{2\sigma^2R}{G},\qquad V_c(R)=\sqrt{\frac{GM(<R)}R}=\sqrt2\sigma.}
$$
Thus the model has a <flat galaxy rotation curve>, resembling the outer <galaxy rotation curves> of many <spiral galaxies>. Central rotation curves usually rise, and not all real curves are flat throughout. A pressure-supported <elliptical galaxy> does not itself rotate at this test-particle <circular speed>.

The model has divergent central <mass density>, although finite enclosed mass at each finite radius, and an infinite total mass because $M(<R)\propto R$. Its potential is logarithmic, requiring an outer cutoff for a finite escape energy. A globally constant isotropic <velocity dispersion>, spherical symmetry, and one common profile for all gravitating components are further idealizations. Stellar light commonly has a quite different radial profile from the <dark-matter halo>; real disks are flattened, and halos are neither exact isothermal spheres nor infinite. Tidal truncation also changes the distribution near the satellite boundary.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

An orbital prescription is needed to fix the coefficient in a <tidal radius>. Adopt a low-mass satellite on a circular orbit of separation $D$, with $r_t\ll D$. The host <singular isothermal sphere> has
$$
\Phi_h'(D)=\frac{2\sigma_h^2}{D},\qquad \Phi_h''(D)=-\frac{2\sigma_h^2}{D^2},\qquad \Omega^2=\frac{\Phi_h'(D)}D=\frac{2\sigma_h^2}{D^2}.
$$
In the corotating frame, subtract the host force on the satellite centre and add the differential centrifugal force. Along the line joining the centres, the outward effective acceleration to first order in displacement $r$ is $(\Omega^2-\Phi_h'')r=4\sigma_h^2r/D^2$. Equate this to the dwarf's inward acceleration at its edge:
$$
\frac{Gm_d(<r_t)}{r_t^2}=\frac{2\sigma_d^2}{r_t}=\frac{4\sigma_h^2r_t}{D^2}.
$$
This gives the <Jacobi truncation of two singular isothermal spheres>
$$
\boxed{r_t=\frac{D\sigma_d}{\sqrt2\sigma_h}.}
$$
It is the distance to the radial escape saddle, not an exact spherical surface: the actual tidal lobe is distorted. For an eccentric orbit, separation alone does not specify an instantaneous effective angular speed or the stripping history; a pericentric estimate is commonly used with further assumptions.

A simpler order-of-magnitude convention equates mean internal and host densities, $m_d/r_t^3=M_h(D)/D^3$, giving $r_t\sim D\sigma_d/\sigma_h$. Equivalently it retains the host gradient without the circular-orbit centrifugal contribution. Both reproduce the scaling $r_t\propto D\sigma_d/\sigma_h$, but their coefficients differ by $\sqrt2$. The familiar point-mass factor three is not appropriate for this extended host, whose enclosed mass grows linearly with radius. The circular-orbit <Jacobi tidal radius> will be used consistently below, with the rough convention's numerical values also given.

Integrate the dwarf <singular isothermal sphere> only to its truncation radius:
$$
m_d=\frac{2\sigma_d^2r_t}{G}=\frac{2\sigma_d^2}{G}\frac{D\sigma_d}{\sqrt2\sigma_h}.

Consequently
$$
\boxed{m_d=\frac{\sqrt2D\sigma_d^3}{G\sigma_h}.}

The dimensions are $(\text{length})(\text{speed})^2/G$, as required for mass. At fixed host dispersion and separation the mass scales as $\sigma_d^3$, rather than the luminosity's fourth-power scaling used later. Under the rough equal-density <tidal radius> convention, the corresponding mass is $2D\sigma_d^3/(G\sigma_h)$, larger by $\sqrt2$.

Use the classical <Faber-Jackson relation> $L\propto\sigma^4$ as the explicitly requested extrapolation. The supplied host fixes its normalization:
$$
L_d=10^{11}\left(\frac{10}{300}\right)^4L_\odot=1.23\times10^5L_\odot.

$$
\Upsilon_d=\frac{5.05\times10^7}{1.23\times10^5}\frac{M_\odot}{L_\odot}=4.09\times10^2\frac{M_\odot}{L_\odot}.
$$
Hence to one significant figure
$$
\boxed{L_d\simeq10^5L_\odot,\qquad \Upsilon_d\simeq4\times10^2M_\odot/L_\odot.}
$$
The rough equal-density truncation convention gives $\Upsilon_d\simeq6\times10^2M_\odot/L_\odot$ instead. Both conclusions indicate a very large dynamical <mass-to-light ratio> compared with a normal old stellar population.

Within their bright stellar bodies, typical giant <elliptical galaxies> have optical dynamical <mass-to-light ratios> of order a few to ten in solar units, with variations in band, <initial mass function>, and <dark matter>. Large outer apertures can give substantially larger ratios. Many <dwarf spheroidal galaxies> have much larger inferred ratios, from tens to hundreds and, for some very faint systems, still larger values. Thus the computed ratio is plausible as the scale of a very dark-matter-dominated faint dwarf, not as a purely stellar ratio. Dynamical inference depends on member identification, binary motions, equilibrium, and the assumed mass profile.

The stipulated <Faber-Jackson relation> is not a reliable universal extrapolation down to every <dwarf spheroidal galaxy>. Also, the <fundamental plane of elliptical galaxies> contains radius and <surface brightness> as well as <velocity dispersion>; the two-variable <Faber-Jackson relation> is a projection of that information, not an interchangeable definition of the full plane.

The model calculates total gravitating mass: reading its density literally as stars alone would imply an implausibly large purely stellar ratio, exposing the limits of that interpretation.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The relevant comparison is continuing <hierarchical galaxy formation> in the <Lambda-CDM model> against an idealized <monolithic galaxy formation> model in which both formation and assembly were essentially complete early. Each observation below concerns a population or a fossil record, rather than a currently merging pair. None individually proves that every <galaxy> formed through the same route.

**Fossil substructure in stellar halos.** Old <stars> in the <Milky Way>'s <stellar halo> include coherent groups in orbital-integral space, and external halos contain extended debris and shells. <Phase mixing> can erase spatial concentration while retaining correlations of orbital energy or <angular momentum>. Such <stellar streams>, when linked to a disrupted dwarf progenitor, record accretion of an already formed <stellar population>. A smooth single collapse followed by quiet passive evolution has no equally direct mechanism for creating several dynamically and chemically distinct accreted populations. This supports a composite assembly history, without requiring that every halo star was accreted or counting a current merging pair as evidence.

**Growth of the quiescent population.** The <galaxy red sequence> contains old <stellar populations>, yet its inferred stellar mass density grows between approximately redshift one and the present. Pure fading of an already fixed early population changes luminosities and colours, but cannot increase the mass in that population. Quenching adds newly passive systems, while subsequent assembly combines older <stellar populations> into larger galaxies. The observed evolution therefore requires demographic evolution after the putative completed collapse epoch. It is consistent with <hierarchical galaxy formation> plus quenching, although red-sequence growth alone does not distinguish assembly from all possible histories of in-situ <star formation>; stellar-mass estimates and survey selection must be treated consistently.

**Evolution of galaxy morphology.** Distant <galaxy> samples contain a larger prevalence of irregular and peculiar structures than a simple non-evolving extrapolation of the local population. Settled discs and smooth spheroids become common outcomes as time proceeds. Continued gas supply, dynamical interactions, and internal rearrangement in <hierarchical galaxy formation> naturally allow this <galaxy morphological evolution>. A model in which structure became fixed during one early collapse and then only stellar light faded has difficulty producing the population change. Rest-frame wavelength, resolution, and surface-brightness selection can affect the observed classifications, and clumpiness is not unique evidence for a <galaxy merger>: unstable gas-rich discs can also be irregular.

**Evolution of sizes at fixed stellar mass.** Massive high-redshift <galaxies>, including concentrated systems, are often more compact than comparable populations at low redshift. <Galaxy size evolution> implies structural growth beyond passive luminosity fading. In particular, deposition of accreted stars at large radii in <minor galaxy mergers> can enlarge the outer stellar body while preserving an old central <stellar population>; gas accretion can grow discs from the inside outward. A completed early collapse with a fixed stellar structure does not naturally supply those new outskirts. Changes in sample membership and expansion associated with stellar mass loss also contribute, so this is evidence requiring continued structural evolution rather than a unique quantitative measurement of a merger rate.

**Diffuse intracluster stars.** <Intracluster light> and extended outer envelopes show that some <stars> no longer belong to individual cluster <galaxies>. Their irregular distribution and substructure retain evidence of redistribution within an assembling cluster. <Tidal stripping> and disruption of member systems supply stars to the common environment, as expected when smaller bound systems are incorporated into a growing hierarchy. Passive evolution of independent galaxies formed in isolated early collapses cannot itself move their stars into the space between galaxies. Early stellar formation followed by later stripping remains possible, but admitting that later dynamical assembly already departs from the strict completed-collapse picture. This evidence concerns the cumulative stellar distribution, not merely the existence of an occasional present-day merging pair.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Interpret the model comparison at the exam's 2008 epoch. A simulation's failure depends on its treatment of baryons, resolution, and adopted <stellar feedback>; it is not a theorem that the <Lambda-CDM model> cannot produce the observation under any physical implementation. The following were important tensions for then-standard realizations, with different implications for <monolithic galaxy formation>.

**Old, alpha-enhanced massive spheroids and downsizing.** The most massive quiescent <elliptical galaxies> tend to contain older <stellar populations> and elevated <alpha element> abundances relative to iron, indicating that much of their <star formation> occurred early and rapidly. Prompt core-collapse enrichment followed by shutdown before substantial <delayed iron enrichment> accounts for the abundance pattern. Models that spread a massive galaxy's <star formation> over many late progenitor-merger episodes can make its stars too young or insufficiently alpha enhanced. A rapid early dissipative collapse naturally gives a short formation interval and is therefore more immediately consistent with this particular observation. However, <downsizing in galaxy formation> is about stellar formation, not necessarily final assembly: late <dry galaxy mergers> can assemble already old alpha-enhanced stars. Early quenching and an appropriate chemical-enrichment model can consequently reconcile hierarchical assembly with the fossil record.

**Thin, high-angular-momentum stellar discs.** The existence of extended <thin galactic disks>, including systems with small <galactic bulges>, challenged simulations that produced compact central stellar concentrations or overly thick discs. Premature gas cooling into small progenitors, transfer of <specific angular momentum> to the halo, and repeated dynamical perturbations could cause the <galactic angular-momentum catastrophe>; satellite encounters also increase <galactic disk heating>. A comparatively quiet dissipative collapse with approximate conservation of baryonic <specific angular momentum> more readily produces an extended rotating disc, and avoiding subsequent strong perturbations helps retain thinness. Nevertheless, rapid collapse alone does not explain a disc's prolonged <star formation> and gas replenishment. Hierarchical histories with quieter late accretion, better feedback, delayed cooling, and dissipative disc regrowth can reduce these tensions; a problem in early implementations is not an unavoidable property of the cosmological framework.

**Cores inferred in low-mass galaxies.** Resolved inner <galaxy rotation curves> in a number of low-surface-brightness and dwarf systems favoured shallower central <mass densities> than the cusps of collisionless cold-dark-matter simulations. For a roughly constant-density <dark-matter core>, $M(<r)\propto r^3$ and $V_c\propto r$; an inner <Navarro--Frenk--White profile> has $\rho\propto r^{-1}$, hence $M(<r)\propto r^2$ and $V_c\propto r^{1/2}$. The differing slopes explain the <cusp--core problem>. Beam smearing, inclination, and noncircular motions complicate inference, but do not make the issue merely a normalization mismatch. A simple early dissipative collapse does not automatically solve it: baryonic contraction can deepen the central concentration. Potential fluctuations from gas loss and <stellar feedback> can instead redistribute dark matter and modify a cusp. Thus this observation tests the joint halo-and-baryon model rather than preferentially vindicating the old collapse picture.

**Rapid formation of the stars and hierarchical assembly of the final galaxy are compatible histories.** The comparison must keep those clocks separate, and the historical modelling tensions should not be presented as universal failures of all later simulations.
