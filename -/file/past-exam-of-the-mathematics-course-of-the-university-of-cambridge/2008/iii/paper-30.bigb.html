= Solution
{parent=1}

A nonempty compact <dynamical system> $(X,T)$ is a <minimal dynamical system> if it has no proper nonempty closed forward-invariant subset. Equivalently, every forward <orbit> is dense: the <orbit closure> of any point is a nonempty closed forward-invariant set, and conversely a closed invariant set contains the <orbit closure> of each of its points.

Consider the collection of nonempty closed forward-invariant subsets of $X$, ordered by reverse inclusion. Every chain has an upper bound, its intersection: the chain has the finite-intersection property, <compactness> makes the intersection nonempty, and continuity is not even needed for its forward invariance. The <Zorn lemma> supplies a minimal member $Y$, so $(Y,T|_Y)$ is a <minimal subsystem>. Also $T(Y)$ is compact, nonempty and forward invariant, so minimality gives $T(Y)=Y$. Here the printed word “nontrivial” must mean nonempty; a singleton with its identity map shows that a subsystem with more than one point cannot always be required.

If $y\in Y$, every tail of its forward <orbit> is dense in $Y$, since it is the orbit of $T^Ny\in Y$. Therefore every neighborhood of $y$ contains $T^ny$ for arbitrarily large $n$. Choosing these times successively for balls of radii $1/j$ gives $n_j\to\infty$ with $T^{n_j}y\to y$. This proves the <Birkhoff recurrence theorem>: **every <continuous map> of a nonempty compact metric space has a recurrent point**. In fact every point of the chosen <minimal subsystem> is recurrent.

Write the continuous circle extension as the <skew product>
$$
\widetilde X=X\times\mathbb R/\mathbb Z,\qquad\widetilde T(x,y)=(Tx,y+\rho(x)).
$$
A sufficient condition, and in this setting also a necessary one, is the <circle skew-product minimality criterion>: for every integer $m\ne0$ there is no continuous function $f:X\to\mathbb S^1$ such that
$$
\boxed{f(Tx)=e^{2\pi i m\rho(x)}f(x)\quad\hbox{for every }x.}
$$
The <cocycle> $\rho$ is assumed continuous; a merely measurable <cocycle> need not define this topological extension.

To prove sufficiency, choose a <minimal subsystem> $Z$ of the extension. Its projection is a nonempty closed forward-invariant subset of $X$, hence all of $X$. For $a\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$, vertical translation sends $Z$ to another <minimal subsystem>, since it commutes with $\widetilde T$. Two such translates either are disjoint or coincide: a nonempty intersection is closed and forward invariant, so minimality forces equality. Define their common stabilizer
$$
H=\{a:Z+a=Z\}.
$$
This is a closed subgroup of the <circle group>. Its closedness follows by taking limits of translations of points in the compact set $Z$, using both a translation and its inverse. Each fiber $Z_x$ is nonempty. If $y,z\in Z_x$, the sets $Z$ and $Z+(z-y)$ intersect at $(x,z)$, so $z-y\in H$. Conversely translating any fiber point by any member of $H$ stays in the fiber. Thus every fiber is exactly one coset of $H$.

If $Z$ is proper, $H$ is proper and therefore finite, say $H=\{0,1/m,\ldots,(m-1)/m\}$ for some positive integer $m$. The character $y\mapsto e^{2\pi imy}$ is constant on each fiber coset, giving a well-defined function $f(x)$ from $Z_x$. This function is continuous: its graph is the continuous image of $Z$ in $X\times\mathbb S^1$, and projection of that compact graph onto the Hausdorff space $X$ is a continuous bijection and hence a homeomorphism. The relation $\widetilde T(Z)=Z$ gives $f(Tx)=e^{2\pi im\rho(x)}f(x)$, contrary to the criterion. Hence $Z=\widetilde X$ and the extension is minimal.

Conversely, a solution of the displayed equation makes $F(x,y)=e^{2\pi imy}/f(x)$ a nonconstant continuous invariant function on the extension. Its nonempty level sets are proper closed invariant subsets, contradicting minimality. This also proves necessity of the <circle skew-product minimality criterion>.

Apply it with $\alpha=\sqrt2$, $X=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$, $Tx=x+\alpha$, and $\rho(x)=2x+\alpha$. The base is a <minimal dynamical system>: the closure of the cyclic subgroup generated by an irrational angle is the whole <circle group>, and its positive forward orbit has the same closure. To see the subgroup assertion, a proper closed subgroup of the circle is finite; if it contained an irrational angle it would be infinite. The elementary classification follows because a closed subgroup with arbitrarily small nonzero elements is dense, while otherwise its least positive representative generates a finite subgroup.

There can be no forbidden $f$ for any $m\ne0$. Indeed, the <winding number of a continuous closed path> of $x\mapsto f(x+\alpha)$ equals that of $x\mapsto f(x)$, by translation and <homotopy invariance of winding number>. Consequently their quotient has <winding number> zero. But the proposed quotient $e^{2\pi im(2x+\alpha)}$ has <winding number> $2m\ne0$. Thus the <skew product> is minimal. Starting at $(0,0)$, induction gives
$$
\widetilde T^n(0,0)=(n\alpha,n^2\alpha)\pmod1,
$$
because $n^2\alpha+2n\alpha+\alpha=(n+1)^2\alpha$. Its <orbit> is dense in the two-dimensional <torus>, so its second coordinate is dense in the circle. Removing the initial point does not change density, since every orbit tail in a <minimal dynamical system> is dense. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{n^2\sqrt2\pmod1:n\geq1\}\text{ is dense in }\mathbb R/\mathbb Z.}
$$

For a probability <measure-preserving system>, <ergodicity> means that every measurable set $A$ satisfying $T^{-1}A=A$ modulo null sets has measure zero or one. Equivalently, the only invariant functions are almost everywhere constant. The <pointwise ergodic theorem> states that, for $f\in L^1(\mu)$,
$$
\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f(T^jx)\longrightarrow\mathbb E(f\mid\mathcal I)(x)\quad\hbox{almost everywhere},
$$
where $\mathcal I$ is the invariant sigma-algebra; on a probability space the convergence also holds in $L^1$. For an <ergodic transformation>, the limit is the constant $\int f\,d\mu$.

For the <Gauss continued-fraction map>, the <Gauss measure> is the probability measure
$$
\boxed{d\nu(x)=\frac{dx}{(\log2)(1+x)},\qquad 0\leq x\leq1.}
$$
Its density integrates to one and is bounded above and below by positive constants, so it has the same null sets as <Lebesgue measure>. Apart from countably many endpoints, the inverse branches are $h_a(y)=1/(a+y)$, $a=1,2,\ldots$. For a bounded measurable test function $F$, substitution on each branch gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int F(Tx)\,d\nu(x)
&=\frac1{\log2}\sum_{a=1}^\infty\int_0^1\frac{F(y)}{(a+y)(a+y+1)}\,dy\\
&=\frac1{\log2}\int_0^1F(y)\sum_{a=1}^\infty\left(\frac1{a+y}-\frac1{a+y+1}\right)\,dy\\
&=\frac1{\log2}\int_0^1\frac{F(y)}{1+y}\,dy=\int F\,d\nu.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $T$ is a <measure-preserving transformation>. Its definition at zero and the rational branch endpoints does not affect these identities.

For <ergodicity>, we give the full shrinking-cylinder argument. A digit cylinder with first $n$ partial quotients $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ is an interval $I=h((0,1))$, where $h=h_{a_1}\circ\cdots\circ h_{a_n}$ and $T^n\circ h$ is the identity. The <continued fraction convergents> give
$$
h(y)=\frac{p_n+p_{n-1}y}{q_n+q_{n-1}y},\qquad
|h'(y)|=\frac1{(q_n+q_{n-1}y)^2},\qquad
|I|=\frac1{q_n(q_n+q_{n-1})}.
$$
Here $q_n\geq q_{n-1}>0$ after the initial step, and the recurrence $q_n=a_nq_{n-1}+q_{n-2}$ makes $q_n$ tend to infinity at least at the Fibonacci rate. Hence the nested cylinders of every irrational point shrink to that point. The <bounded distortion of continued-fraction cylinders> is uniform: the ratio of the largest to the smallest value of $|h'|$ is at most four. Since $1/(1+h(y))$ ranges between $1/2$ and $1$, the density
$$
w(y)=\frac{|h'(y)|}{(\log2)(1+h(y))}
$$
has maximum-to-minimum ratio at most eight. Consequently every measurable $E\subseteq(0,1)$ satisfies
$$
\frac{\nu(h(E))}{\nu(I)}\geq\frac18\operatorname{Leb}(E)\geq\frac{\log2}{8}\nu(E).
$$
The last inequality uses $d\nu/dx\leq1/\log2$.

Now suppose $T^{-1}A=A$ modulo null sets and $\nu(A)>0$. Choose an irrational <density point> $x\in A$, and let $I_n$ be its successive digit cylinders. The <Lebesgue density theorem> applies to these intervals even though they need not be centered: an interval of length $\ell$ containing $x$ lies in the centered interval of radius $\ell$, so its relative complement tends to zero. Comparability of the two densities then gives
$$
\frac{\nu(I_n\setminus A)}{\nu(I_n)}\longrightarrow0.
$$
But invariance gives $\mathbf1_A(h(y))=\mathbf1_A(y)$ almost everywhere for each inverse branch of $T^n$. Pulling the exceptional null sets through $h$ is legitimate since $h$ has a positive smooth Jacobian on this fixed branch. Thus the distortion estimate with $E=A^c$ gives
$$
\frac{\nu(I_n\setminus A)}{\nu(I_n)}=\frac{\nu(h(A^c))}{\nu(I_n)}\geq\frac{\log2}{8}\nu(A^c).
$$
Letting $n\to\infty$ forces $\nu(A^c)=0$. Therefore **the Gauss continued-fraction map is ergodic for the <Gauss measure>**.

For an irrational $x$, the successive <continued fraction> digits satisfy $a_j(x)=\lfloor1/T^{j-1}x\rfloor$. The event that a digit equals two is the interval $(1/3,1/2]$, up to null endpoints. Apply the <pointwise ergodic theorem> to its indicator:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1N|\{1\leq j\leq N:a_j(x)=2\}|
&\longrightarrow\nu((1/3,1/2])\\
&=\frac{\log(3/2)-\log(4/3)}{\log2}
=\frac{\log(9/8)}{\log2}
=\boxed{\log_2 9-3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This holds for almost every $x$ with respect to both the <Gauss measure> and <Lebesgue measure>, since they have the same null sets.

The discrete <Bochner–Herglotz theorem> says that a sequence $r:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb C$ is a <positive-definite function>, meaning
$$
\sum_{j,k=0}^{N-1}c_j\overline{c_k}\,r(j-k)\geq0\quad\hbox{for every }N\text{ and every }c_0,\ldots,c_{N-1},
$$
if and only if there is a unique finite positive <Borel measure> $\sigma$ on $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ such that
$$
\boxed{r(n)=\int_{\mathbb T}e^{2\pi int}\,d\sigma(t)\quad(n\in\mathbb Z).}
$$
The mass is $\sigma(\mathbb T)=r(0)$. The necessity of positive definiteness follows by integrating $|\sum_j c_je^{2\pi ijt}|^2$.

For sufficiency, positive definiteness makes the relevant <Gram matrices> Hermitian positive semidefinite, so $r(-n)=\overline{r(n)}$ and $|r(n)|\leq r(0)$. Define
$$
p_N(t)=\frac1N\sum_{j,k=0}^{N-1}r(j-k)e^{-2\pi i(j-k)t}
=\sum_{|n|<N}\left(1-\frac{|n|}{N}\right)r(n)e^{-2\pi int}.
$$
Taking $c_j=e^{-2\pi ijt}$ in the positive-definiteness condition gives $p_N(t)\geq0$, while $\int_{\mathbb T}p_N(t)\,dt=r(0)$. Moreover,
$$
\int_{\mathbb T}e^{2\pi imt}p_N(t)\,dt
=\begin{cases}(1-|m|/N)r(m),&|m|<N,\\0,&|m|\geq N.
\end{cases}
$$
Thus the integrals against every <trigonometric polynomial> converge. These polynomials are uniformly dense in continuous functions on the circle: smooth periodic approximation followed by <Fourier inversion> for smooth functions gives this density. The uniform mass bound $r(0)$ then makes the integrals against every continuous $\varphi$ converge as well. Their limit is a positive linear functional $L$ with $|L(\varphi)|\leq r(0)\|\varphi\|_\infty$. The standard <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> gives a finite positive <Borel measure> $\sigma$ with $L(\varphi)=\int\varphi\,d\sigma$. The preceding coefficient computation proves its required <Fourier coefficients>. Finally, two such measures agree on all <trigonometric polynomials>, hence on all continuous functions by uniform density, and therefore are identical. This proves the <Bochner–Herglotz theorem>, including existence and uniqueness; if $r(0)=0$, the estimate $|r(n)|\leq r(0)$ gives the zero measure directly.

Now let $U$ be the <Koopman operator> $Uf=f\circ T$ on the complex <L2 space> of the probability <measure-preserving system>. We use $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f\overline g\,d\mu$, linear in the first entry. Since $U$ is an <isometry>, the sequence $r_f(n)=\langle U^nf,f\rangle$ for $n\geq0$, extended by $r_f(-n)=\overline{r_f(n)}$, is positive-definite: its matrix is the <Gram matrix> of $f,Uf,\ldots$. The <Bochner–Herglotz theorem> gives its <spectral measure of a Koopman observable> $\sigma_f$.

We first prove exactly how an <atom of a measure> detects an <eigenfunction>. Fix $\zeta=e^{2\pi it_0}$ and set
$$
A_N^\zeta f=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\zeta^{-j}U^jf.
$$
The <mean ergodic theorem>, applied to the <isometry> $V=\zeta^{-1}U$, says these averages converge in <L2 space> to the <orthogonal projection> $P_\zeta f$ onto $\ker(U-\zeta I)$. Its usual Hilbert-space proof works without invertibility: the orthogonal complement of $\overline{\operatorname{ran}(I-V)}$ is $\ker(I-V^*)=\ker(I-V)$, and the averages of $(I-V)h$ telescope to $(h-V^Nh)/N\to0$. The equality of the two kernels follows from $V$ being an <isometry>, by expanding $\|Vh-h\|^2$ when $V^*h=h$.

Expanding the averaged norm through the <spectral measure of a Koopman observable> gives
$$
\|A_N^\zeta f\|_2^2=\int_{\mathbb T}\left|\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi ij(t-t_0)}\right|^2\,d\sigma_f(t).
$$
The integrand is at most one and converges to $\mathbf1_{\{t_0\}}$. By the <dominated convergence theorem>,
$$
\boxed{\|P_\zeta f\|_2^2=\sigma_f(\{t_0\}).}
$$
Thus a nonzero atom corresponds to a nonzero <Koopman operator> <eigenfunction>, with eigenvalue $\zeta$, in the cyclic spectral data of $f$.

We also need the <Wiener Fourier coefficient lemma>, which here has a short proof. For any finite positive circle measure $\sigma$ with coefficients $r(n)$,
$$
\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|r(n)|^2
=\iint\left(\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi in(t-s)}\right)\,d\sigma(t)\,d\sigma(s).
$$
The bracket is bounded in absolute value by one and tends to the indicator of the diagonal $t=s$. Another application of the <dominated convergence theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|r(n)|^2=\sum_{t\in\mathbb T}\sigma(\{t\})^2.}
$$
In particular the limit is zero for an <atomless measure>.

Suppose there are no nonconstant <Koopman operator> <eigenfunctions>. For every $f$ in the <mean-zero L2 space>, all its projections $P_\zeta f$ vanish: a nonzero projection would be a nonconstant <eigenfunction>, since it has mean zero. Hence $\sigma_f$ is atomless, and the <Wiener Fourier coefficient lemma> gives
$$
\frac1N\sum_{n<N}|\langle U^nf,f\rangle|^2\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> also gives vanishing averaged absolute autocorrelations. For two mean-zero observables $f,g$, the sesquilinear polarization identity
$$
\langle U^nf,g\rangle=\frac14\sum_{j=0}^3i^j\langle U^n(f+i^jg),f+i^jg\rangle
$$
then gives vanishing averaged absolute cross-correlations. Taking $f=\mathbf1_A-\mu(A)$ and $g=\mathbf1_B-\mu(B)$ proves
$$
\frac1N\sum_{n<N}|\mu(T^{-n}A\cap B)-\mu(A)\mu(B)|\longrightarrow0,
$$
which is <weak mixing>.

Conversely, this set formulation of <weak mixing> extends to all pairs of mean-zero <L2 space> observables by finite-simple-function approximation and the uniform estimate $|\langle U^nf,g\rangle|\leq\|f\|_2\|g\|_2$. If $Uh=\zeta h$ and $h\ne0$, the <isometry> property gives $|\zeta|=1$. If $\zeta\ne1$, invariance of the integral gives $\int h=0$. If $\zeta=1$ and $h$ is nonconstant, replace it by $h-\int h$, a nonzero mean-zero <eigenfunction>. But in either case
$$
|\langle U^nh,h\rangle|=\|h\|_2^2>0
$$
for every $n$, contradicting <weak mixing>. We have proved **weak mixing is equivalent to absence of nonconstant <eigenfunctions>**, including for noninvertible transformations.

Finally, the <doubling map> $Tx=2x\pmod1$ preserves normalized <Lebesgue measure>, as substitution on its two inverse branches shows. Let $f\in L^2(\mathbb T)$ be an <eigenfunction>, $f(2x)=\zeta f(x)$, with <Fourier coefficients> $c_k$. The <Fourier basis> calculation gives
$$
\widehat{Uf}(k)=\begin{cases}c_{k/2},&k\text{ even},\\0,&k\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
$$
Since $\zeta\ne0$, all odd coefficients vanish. Every nonzero integer is $2^rm$ with $m$ odd, and repeated use of $\zeta c_{2^rm}=c_{2^{r-1}m}$ makes all nonzero coefficients vanish. Completeness of the <Fourier basis> then says $f$ is constant. Therefore the <doubling map> has no nonconstant <eigenfunctions>, and
$$
\boxed{x\mapsto2x\pmod1\text{ is weakly mixing for Lebesgue measure}.}
$$

Use the convention that level $k$ counts the $k$ nonzero shifts, hence an <arithmetic progression> of length $k+1$. The <SZ property> at level $k$ means that every measurable set $A$ of positive measure satisfies
$$
\boxed{\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\mu\bigl(A\cap T^{-n}A\cap T^{-2n}A\cap\cdots\cap T^{-kn}A\bigr)>0.}
$$
This is the averaged multiple-recurrence property, stronger than merely finding one positive intersection. Shifting the indexing convention from $k+1$ terms to $k$ terms does not change the all-levels assertion. We work with probability <measure-preserving systems>.

Here is the <Furstenberg correspondence principle> construction proving the <Szemerédi theorem>. Let $D\subseteq\mathbb N$ have positive upper density $\delta=\limsup_N|D\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|/N$. Put $z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z}$ equal to the indicator of $D$ on the positive coordinates and zero elsewhere, and let $S$ be the <left shift>. The binary <full shift> $\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z}$ is a compact <metric space> in the <product topology>, and $S$ is a <homeomorphism>. Choose $N_i\to\infty$ attaining the upper density and define the <empirical measures>
$$
\mu_i=\frac1{N_i}\sum_{m=1}^{N_i}\delta_{S^mz}.
$$
We use the standard weak <compactness> fact that every sequence of <Borel probability measures> on a compact <metric space> has a weakly convergent subsequence, where weak convergence means convergence of integrals of continuous functions. Pass to a subsequence with limit $\mu$. For every continuous $F$, telescoping gives
$$
\int F\circ S\,d\mu_i-\int F\,d\mu_i
=\frac{F(S^{N_i+1}z)-F(Sz)}{N_i}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Passing to the limit proves $S_*\mu=\mu$. The <cylinder set> $A=\{w:w(0)=1\}$ is clopen, so its indicator is continuous and
$$
\mu(A)=\lim_i\mu_i(A)=\delta>0.
$$
If every <measure-preserving system> has the <SZ property> at level $k$, there is some $n\geq1$ for which $\mu(C_n)>0$, where $C_n=\bigcap_{j=0}^kS^{-jn}A$. This is also a clopen <cylinder set>, so $\mu_i(C_n)\to\mu(C_n)>0$. For some sufficiently large $i$, at least one term of the empirical average is positive: $S^mz\in C_n$ for some $1\leq m\leq N_i$. Unwinding the coordinates gives
$$
z(m)=z(m+n)=\cdots=z(m+kn)=1.
$$
Therefore **$D$ contains an <arithmetic progression> of length $k+1$ with positive common difference**. Having the <SZ property> at every level gives the infinite-density form of the <Szemerédi theorem>.

The finite uniform form follows from the same argument. If, for fixed $\delta>0$ and $k$, arbitrarily large $N_i$ admitted sets $D_i\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N_i\}$ with $|D_i|\geq\delta N_i$ and no such <arithmetic progression>, replace $z$ above by the indicator $z_i$ of $D_i$, extended by zero outside that interval. Their empirical measures still have an invariant weak limit with $\mu(A)\geq\delta$. For every fixed positive $n$, however, $\mu_i(C_n)=0$, since a point contributing to that cylinder would be an actual progression in $D_i$. Thus $\mu(C_n)=0$ for all positive $n$, contradicting the <SZ property>. Consequently a sufficiently large $N$ depending only on $\delta,k$ forces the required progression.

A <compact measure-preserving system> is one for which every observable $f\in L^2(\mu)$ has relatively compact forward orbit $\{U^nf:n\geq0\}$ under the <Koopman operator> $Uf=f\circ T$. Equivalently, each of these orbits is totally bounded in the global $L^2$ norm; <compactness> refers to observables, not just to the underlying topological space. For an invertible system it is usual to use all integer times. One may also define <compactness> by density of <almost periodic observables>: the two definitions coincide because $\|U^n(f-g)\|_2=\|f-g\|_2$, so a finite net for an approximating observable is a finite net, with slightly larger radius, for $f$.

To prove the <SZ property>, fix $A$ with $a=\mu(A)>0$, put $f=\mathbf1_A$, and choose
$$
\varepsilon^2<\frac{a}{2\sum_{j=1}^kj^2}.
$$
<Compactness> gives a finite $\varepsilon$-net for its orbit with centers $U^{r_1}f,\ldots,U^{r_s}f$, where $r_i\geq0$. Let $M=\max_i r_i$. For every integer $n\geq M+1$, some center satisfies $\|U^nf-U^{r_i}f\|_2<\varepsilon$. Since $U$ is an <isometry>, cancellation gives
$$
\|U^{n-r_i}f-f\|_2<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus the return set $R=\{r\geq1:\|U^rf-f\|_2<\varepsilon\}$ meets every interval $\{n-M,\ldots,n\}$ for $n\geq M+1$. In particular, $R$ is a <syndetic set> with lower density at least $1/(M+1)$. This proves the needed <syndetic near returns of an almost periodic observable> without any assumption of <ergodicity>.

For $r\in R$, telescoping and the <isometry> property give
$$
\|U^{jr}f-f\|_2\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}\|U^{\ell r}(U^rf-f)\|_2<j\varepsilon\qquad(1\leq j\leq k).
$$
As $f$ is an indicator, $\|U^{jr}f-f\|_2^2=\mu(A\mathbin\triangle T^{-jr}A)$. The <union bound> therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu\left(\bigcap_{j=0}^kT^{-jr}A\right)
&\geq a-\sum_{j=1}^k\mu(A\setminus T^{-jr}A)\\
&\geq a-\sum_{j=1}^k\|U^{jr}f-f\|_2^2
>a-\varepsilon^2\sum_{j=1}^kj^2>\frac a2.
\end{aligned}
$$
All other multiple-intersection measures are nonnegative. Averaging over the bounded-gap set $R$ yields
$$
\boxed{\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{r=1}^N\mu\left(\bigcap_{j=0}^kT^{-jr}A\right)\geq\frac{a}{2(M+1)}>0.}
$$
Thus **every compact <measure-preserving system> has the <SZ property> at every level**, including nonergodic systems and, under the forward-orbit definition, noninvertible transformations.
