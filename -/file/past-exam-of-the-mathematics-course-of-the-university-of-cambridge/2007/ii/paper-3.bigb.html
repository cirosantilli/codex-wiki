The successive complete quotients after the integer part two are $(\sqrt7+2)/3$, $(\sqrt7+1)/2$, $(\sqrt7+1)/3$, and $\sqrt7+2$. Their integer parts are $1,1,1,4$, and taking the next reciprocal repeats the first complete quotient. Therefore

The first four <continued fraction convergents> are $2,3,5/2,8/3$. The last gives $8^2-7\cdot3^2=1$. Squaring its unit $8+3\sqrt7$ gives $127+48\sqrt7$, whose <norm> is again one. Thus two positive solutions of the <Pell equation> are

\boxed{(x,y)=(8,3),\quad(127,48).}
$$
Direct verification gives $127^2-7\cdot48^2=16129-16128=1$.

= Solution

The <polynomial> form of <Runge theorem> says that a function holomorphic on a neighbourhood of a compact set $K\subset\mathbb C$ with connected complement can be approximated uniformly on $K$ by <polynomials>.

For $n\geq3$, take
$$
K_n=\{0\}\cup\{z:1/n\leq|z|\leq1-1/n,\ 0\leq\arg z\leq2\pi-1/n\}.
$$
The complement is connected: the missing angular sector connects the inner disk to the exterior, and removing the single point zero from the inner disk does not disconnect it. On a neighbourhood of the annular sector choose an analytic logarithm with arguments between $-1/(2n)$ and $2\pi-1/(2n)$. Its exponential $\exp(3\log z/2)$ agrees with the prescribed function on the sector. On a disjoint small neighbourhood of zero prescribe the analytic function zero. These two neighbourhoods define a <holomorphic function> $g_n$ near $K_n$ with $g_n=f$ on $K_n$.

By <Runge theorem>, choose a <polynomial> $p_n$ with $\sup_{K_n}|p_n-g_n|<1/n$. Every fixed nonzero point of the disk eventually belongs to all $K_n$, including points on the positive real axis; zero belongs to every $K_n$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{p_n(z)\longrightarrow f(z)\quad\text{for every }z\in D.}
$$
Each <polynomial> restricts to an analytic function on $D$. The excluded sector lies immediately below the positive axis and shrinks with $n$, so the construction correctly preserves the chosen value on the branch cut. It gives <pointwise convergence>, not local <uniform convergence> across that cut. This is <pointwise approximation by a Runge exhaustion>.

For a <plane crystallographic group>, the translation <subgroup> consists of translations by <vectors> in a rank-two <lattice> $\Lambda$; the <lattice> is this additive group of translation <vectors>. The <point group> is the image under the linear-part <homomorphism> $G\to O(2)$, equivalently $G/\Lambda$. Conjugating a translation by an isometry with linear part $A$ gives translation by $Av$, so every point-group element preserves $\Lambda$.

Choose a <lattice> basis. A point-group rotation is represented in that basis by an integral <matrix>, because it maps <lattice> <vectors> to <lattice> <vectors>, and its inverse does likewise. The <trace> is therefore an integer. In an <orthonormal basis> the same rotation has <trace> $2\cos\theta$, which lies between minus two and two. Hence $2\cos\theta\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$. The value two gives the identity, while the other values give angles $\pi$, $\pm2\pi/3$, $\pm\pi/2$, or $\pm\pi/3$. Thus every nonidentity rotation has order
$$
\boxed{2,3,4,\text{ or }6.}
$$
This proves the <crystallographic restriction theorem> from the <lattice> action rather than assuming the classification of wallpaper groups.

= Solution

The <polynomials> are over $\mathbb F_2$, and multiplication gives $gh=X^7+1=X^7-1$. A <cyclic code> of length seven generated by a degree-three <polynomial> has dimension $7-3=4$: the multiples $ag$ with $\deg a<4$ are distinct representatives, so its rank is **four**.

The root $\alpha$ has order seven in $\mathbb F_8^\times$: it is neither zero nor one, and the multiplicative group has prime order seven. A weight-one word cannot vanish at $\alpha$. A weight-two word $X^i+X^j$, with distinct indices between zero and six, cannot vanish either, because that would give $\alpha^{i-j}=1$. Every codeword vanishes at $\alpha$, so its nonzero weight is at least three. Since $g$ itself has weight three,
$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=3.}
$$
From $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$ obtain $\alpha^4=\alpha^2+\alpha$ and $\alpha^5=\alpha^2+\alpha+1$. Therefore $r(\alpha)=\alpha^5+\alpha^3+\alpha=\alpha^2+\alpha=\alpha^4$. The one-bit error $X^4$ has precisely this <syndrome>, so correcting it gives
$$
\boxed{c(X)=X^5+X^4+X^3+X=g(X)(X^2+X).}
$$
Its distance from the received word is one. The <minimum distance> three makes this nearest codeword unique: two codewords each at distance one from the received word would be at distance at most two from each other.

= Solution
{parent=5I}

Write $S(\beta)=\sum_{ij}\mu_{ij}(\beta)$. Ignoring only terms independent of $\beta$, the Poisson <log-likelihood> is $\ell_P=\sum y_{ij}\log\mu_{ij}-S(\beta)$. The multinomial model is defined on the constraint $S(\beta)=n$, since its probabilities must sum to one. On this constraint its full <log-likelihood> satisfies
$$
\ell_M(\beta)=\ell_P(\beta)+n-n\log n+\log(n!),
$$
where both likelihoods include the same $-\sum\log(y_{ij}!)$ terms. Thus they differ by a constant on the admissible multinomial parameter set.

If the Poisson <maximum likelihood estimator> has $S(\widehat\beta)=n$, it is feasible for the multinomial problem, and no feasible parameter can have a larger likelihood than it. Consequently it is also a multinomial maximum. With the usual uniqueness and identifiability assumption,
$$
\boxed{\widehat\beta=\beta^*,\qquad\widehat y_{ij}=y^*_{ij}.}
$$
Without uniqueness, the precise conclusion is that $\widehat\beta$ is a multinomial maximizer; arbitrary independently chosen maximizers need not be identical. This qualification is necessary for a general unspecified parametrization.

In a log-linear <generalized linear model> with an unrestricted intercept, differentiation with respect to that intercept gives $\sum(y_{ij}-\widehat\mu_{ij})=0$. Thus the fitted total automatically equals $n$. One can fit independent Poisson cell counts using the usual GLM machinery and recover the multinomial fitted probabilities as $\widehat\mu_{ij}/n$. The result is <constrained Poisson and multinomial likelihood equivalence>, not an assertion that the sampling experiments themselves are identical.

The pair-birth rate here is the constant immigration rate $\lambda$, while deaths occur at total rate $\beta n$. This interpretation is fixed by the requested mean formula. With $P_n(t)=\Pr(N_t=n)$ and $P_j=0$ for $j<0$, the <master equation> is
$$
\boxed{P_n'=\lambda P_{n-2}+\beta(n+1)P_{n+1}-(\lambda+\beta n)P_n.}
$$
The <Markov jump-process generator> acts as $Lf(n)=\lambda[f(n+2)-f(n)]+\beta n[f(n-1)-f(n)]$. Taking $f(n)=n$ gives $m'=2\lambda-\beta m$, hence
$$
\boxed{m(t)=\frac{2\lambda}{\beta}(1-e^{-\beta t})+n_0e^{-\beta t}.}
$$
For the <second moment> $s=\mathbb E N_t^2$, the two jumps give $s'=4\lambda m+4\lambda-2\beta s+\beta m$. Differentiating $V=s-m^2$ therefore yields $V'=4\lambda+\beta m-2\beta V$. Substitution of the mean and solution of this scalar linear equation give
$$
V(t)=\frac{3\lambda}{\beta}+(V(0)-3\lambda/\beta)e^{-2\beta t}
+(n_0-2\lambda/\beta)(e^{-\beta t}-e^{-2\beta t}).
$$
Thus **$V(t)\to3\lambda/\beta$**, assuming $\beta>0$ and finite initial <second moment>. This is <pair immigration with linear deaths>; births at rate $\lambda n$ would be a different model and would not give the printed mean.

A form of the <Poincare-Bendixson theorem> states that a nonempty <omega-limit set> of a forward orbit of a $C^1$ planar autonomous <vector> field, contained in a compact region and containing no <equilibrium>, is a <periodic orbit>.

Set $\rho=x^2+y^2$. Direct calculation gives
$$
\dot\rho=2\rho(1-\rho)-4k^2x^2y^2,
\qquad2\rho-(2+k^2)\rho^2\leq\dot\rho\leq2\rho(1-\rho),
$$
because $0\leq x^2y^2\leq\rho^2/4$. At the inner boundary $\rho=2/(2+k^2)$ the flow points into or along the annulus, and at the outer boundary $\rho=1$ it does likewise. Smoothness and these inequalities make the compact annulus forward invariant.

For nonzero $(x,y)$, write $x=\sqrt\rho\cos\theta$, $y=\sqrt\rho\sin\theta$. Then
$$
\dot\theta=\frac{x\dot y-y\dot x}{\rho}
=1-\frac{k^2\rho}{4}\sin4\theta\geq1-\frac{k^2}{4}>0
$$
throughout the annulus. Hence it contains no <equilibrium>. Any orbit starting there remains compactly trapped, has a nonempty <omega-limit set>, and the <Poincare-Bendixson theorem> supplies the required **<periodic orbit> in the stated annulus**. For $k=0$ the annulus reduces to $\rho=1$ itself, and there $\dot\theta=1$, giving the <periodic orbit> directly.

For the second-order Fuchsian equation represented by the <Papperitz symbol>, the coefficient of $w'$ is
$$
p(z)=\frac{1-\alpha-\alpha'}z+\frac{1-\gamma-\gamma'}{z-1}.

This follows from the sums of local exponents at its two finite <regular singular points>. Abel's <Wronskian> identity, obtained by differentiating $w_1w_2'-w_1'w_2$ and using the differential equation, gives $W'=-pW$. Thus, on every branch domain avoiding zero and one,
$$
\frac d{dz}\left[z^{1-\alpha-\alpha'}(1-z)^{1-\gamma-\gamma'}W(z)\right]=0.
$$
The normalized expression is locally constant. Its analytic continuation around either singularity is unchanged: the power factors cancel the <determinant> of the local <monodromy>, equivalently the integral of $-p$ around that singularity. It therefore defines a single-valued <holomorphic function> on the punctured plane, locally constant near both punctures. Those punctures are removable, so **the normalized expression extends to an entire function**. This argument also covers resonant exponents and avoids assuming two pure power solutions in that case.

Here the binding energy uses $2m_p+2m_n-m_{\rm He}$; the PDF prints $2n_n$ in that mass relation, a dimensional typo.

In adiabatic expansion without photon-producing entropy release, $T\propto a^{-1}$ and $n_\gamma\propto T^3\propto a^{-3}$, so **$\eta=n_B/n_\gamma$ stays constant**. This statement is for that epoch; entropy transfer such as electron-positron annihilation modifies the photon normalization.

$$
\boxed{\frac{X_p^2X_n^2}{X_{\rm He}}
=\frac{\eta^{-3}}{32}\left(\frac\pi2\right)^{3/2}
\left(\frac{m_pc^2}{kT}\right)^{9/2}e^{-B_{\rm He}/kT}.}

The <Chinese remainder theorem> says that for pairwise coprime positive moduli $m_i$, the map $\mathbb Z/(\prod m_i)\to\prod_i\mathbb Z/m_i$ is an isomorphism; in particular prescribed residues have a unique solution modulo the product.

Write $n=p^e m$, with $e\geq2$ and $(p,m)=1$. Choose $z\equiv1+p^{e-1}\pmod{p^e}$ and $z\equiv1\pmod m$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> supplies $z$. The binomial theorem gives $z^p\equiv1\pmod{p^e}$, since every nonconstant term is divisible by $p^e$; also $z^p\equiv1\pmod m$. Thus $z^p\equiv1\pmod n$, whereas $z\not\equiv1\pmod n$. Its multiplicative order is exactly $p$.

For odd $n=\prod p_i^{e_i}$ define the <Jacobi symbol> $(a/n)=\prod_i(a/p_i)^{e_i}$, using the <Legendre symbol>, with $(a/1)=1$. It is zero if $a$ shares a prime factor with $n$. For example $(2/9)=(2/3)^2=1$, but the square residues modulo nine are $0,1,4,7$, so two is not a square.

For positive odd coprime $u,v$, <Jacobi reciprocity> is
$$
\boxed{(u/v)=(-1)^{(u-1)(v-1)/4}(v/u).}
$$
Factor $u=\prod p_i^{a_i}$ and $v=\prod q_j^{b_j}$ and multiply <quadratic reciprocity> for each prime pair. Its sign exponent is $\sum_{i,j}a_i b_j(p_i-1)(q_j-1)/4$. For odd numbers, $(rs-1)/2\equiv(r-1)/2+(s-1)/2\pmod2$, so this exponent has the same parity as $(u-1)(v-1)/4$. Multiplicativity of the <Legendre symbol> gives the two Jacobi products, proving the formula. If $u,v$ are not coprime, both sides of the displayed reciprocity equality are zero.

For the previously constructed $z$, every prime divisor of $n$ sees residue one, so $(z/n)=1$. But $p\nmid(n-1)/2$, because $p\mid n$ and $p$ is odd. The order-$p$ unit therefore has $z^{(n-1)/2}\not\equiv1\pmod n$. This supplies a unit failing the proposed test.

Finally the passing units form the <kernel> of the <homomorphism>
$$
F:(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times\longrightarrow(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times,
\qquad F(a)=a^{(n-1)/2}(a/n)^{-1}.
$$
Multiplicativity makes this a <homomorphism>, and the failing unit shows its <kernel> is proper. A proper <subgroup> has index at least two. Hence **the test fails on at least half the units modulo $n$**.

The <Liouville approximation theorem> states that if a real irrational algebraic number $\alpha$ has degree $d$, there is $c>0$ such that $|\alpha-p/q|\geq c/q^d$ for every integer $p$ and positive integer $q$.

Choose its irreducible <polynomial> $F\in\mathbb Z[X]$ of degree $d$. Since $\alpha$ is irrational, $F(p/q)\ne0$; after multiplication by $q^d$ it is a nonzero integer. Hence $|F(p/q)|\geq q^{-d}$. On $[\alpha-1,\alpha+1]$ the <polynomial> derivative has a finite bound $M>0$. For $|p/q-\alpha|<1$, the <mean value theorem> gives $|F(p/q)|\leq M|p/q-\alpha|$, proving the required bound with $c=1/M$. For more distant rationals, the bound holds with $c\leq1$. Taking $c=\min\{1,1/M\}$ covers every rational and proves the theorem.

Let $\xi_{n+1}=a_{n+1}+1/(a_{n+2}+\cdots)$ be the complete quotient. Iterating the fractional-linear formula for a <continued fraction> gives
$$
x=\frac{p_n\xi_{n+1}+p_{n-1}}{q_n\xi_{n+1}+q_{n-1}}.
$$
The supplied <determinant> identity and $\xi_{n+1}>a_{n+1}$ therefore imply
$$
\boxed{\left|x-\frac{p_n}{q_n}\right|
=\frac1{q_n(q_n\xi_{n+1}+q_{n-1})}
<\frac1{q_nq_{n+1}}.}
$$
The positive partial quotients ensure increasing denominators tending to infinity; the same <determinant> identity makes the even and odd convergents nested, giving their common limit $x$. The displayed formula follows by passing to the limit in the finite-tail identity.

An explicit choice is **$a_n=10^{n!}$ for every $n\geq1$**. The recurrence gives $q_n\leq\prod_{j=1}^n(a_j+1)\leq2^n10^{\sum_{j=1}^nj!}$. For $n\geq2$, $\sum_{j=1}^nj!\leq2n!$, so $\log_{10}q_n\leq3n!$. Consequently $a_{n+1}=10^{(n+1)!}\geq q_n^{(n+1)/3}$ and
$$
0<\left|x-\frac{p_n}{q_n}\right|<q_n^{-2-(n+1)/3}.
$$
First $x$ is irrational: if $x=P/Q$, the nonzero difference from a convergent is at least $1/(Qq_n)$, contradicting the error bound once $q_{n+1}>Q$. If $x$ were algebraic of any fixed degree $d$, the last displayed upper bound would eventually be smaller than $c/q_n^d$ for the positive constant in part (i), since $q_n\to\infty$. This contradicts the <Liouville approximation theorem>. Thus **this explicit <continued fraction> is transcendental**.

<Hall marriage theorem> says that a <bipartite graph> with equal finite vertex-class sizes has a <perfect matching> exactly when $|\Gamma(A)|\geq|A|$ for every $A\subseteq X$. Necessity follows because distinct matched partners of vertices of $A$ belong to $\Gamma(A)$.

The remaining graph also satisfies it: for $B\subseteq X\setminus A$, the condition on $A\cup B$ gives $|\Gamma(B)\setminus\Gamma(A)|\geq|B|$. Induction gives the second matching, and their union is perfect. Otherwise every nonempty proper subset has strictly more neighbours. Choose an edge $xy$ and remove its ends. Every remaining nonempty subset loses at most one neighbour and originally had at least one spare neighbour, so Hall's condition survives. Induction matches the remaining graph, and restoring $xy$ completes the proof.

The last argument also proves flexibility under the strict condition: it works for every selected edge, so **every vertex is flexible**. The case $n=1$ is again immediate.

Now suppose only that a <perfect matching> exists. Choose a minimal nonempty tight set $A\subseteq X$; such a set exists since $X$ is tight. Every nonempty proper subset of $A$ satisfies the strict condition in the induced graph on $A,\Gamma(A)$. Hence every edge from a vertex of $A$ extends to a matching of that induced graph. There are no edges from $A$ to the complement of $\Gamma(A)$. Restrict the original <perfect matching> to the complementary vertex classes and append it to this new induced matching. This makes every vertex of $A$ flexible in the whole graph, proving that **at least one flexible vertex exists**.

\boxed{u(y)=K(5a^2y^4-y^6)+\tfrac12Cy^2+D,\qquad
K=\frac{G^2}{120\rho^2\nu^3R}.}
$$
The odd integration term vanishes because both no-slip conditions are equal. The remaining no-slip and zero-flux conditions are
$$
\boxed{4Ka^6+\tfrac12Ca^2+D=0,\qquad
\tfrac67Ka^7+\tfrac16Ca^3+Da=0.}
$$
The second equation is half of $\int_{-a}^au(y)dy=0$. Optionally solving gives $C=-66Ka^4/7$ and $D=5Ka^6/7$. With $s=y/a$ the velocity is $Ka^6(1-s^2)(s^4-4s^2+5/7)$, so the ratio to the primary velocity is bounded, including its limiting value at the walls, by a constant times $Ga^4/(\rho\nu^2R)$. A sufficient parameter ordering is
$$
\boxed{a\ll b\ll R,\qquad\frac{Ga^4}{\rho\nu^2R}\ll1.}
$$
Equivalently the last condition is $\mathrm{Re}\,a/R\ll1$ for $\mathrm{Re}=Ga^3/(\rho\nu^2)$. It keeps the secondary circulation weak; the neglected radial return flow and side-wall structure supply closure outside this central wide-channel approximation.

= Solution
{parent=37C}

Let the rapidly varying phase be $\Theta$, so $\mathbf k=\nabla\Theta$ and $\omega=-\Theta_t$. The dispersion law is the Hamilton-Jacobi equation $\Theta_t+\Omega(\nabla\Theta,\mathbf x,t)=0$. Differentiating it and following a path with <group velocity> gives the <ray tracing> equations
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf x}=\Omega_{\mathbf k},\qquad
\dot{\mathbf k}=-\Omega_{\mathbf x},\qquad\dot\omega=\Omega_t.}
$$
The final relation is obtained by the chain rule along that path; its two spatial terms cancel.

The medium is stationary and independent of $y$, so $\omega=\omega_\infty$ and $k_2=K$ are conserved. For deep water far from shore, $\kappa_\infty=\omega_\infty^2/g$. Choose the incident branch with $k_1<0$ and $K=(\omega_\infty^2/g)\sin\theta_\infty>0$; choosing the opposite alongshore direction changes its sign. Since the <dispersion relation> depends on direction only through $\kappa$, the group-velocity component ratio is $k_2/k_1$. Hence the required implicit ray equation is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac K{\sqrt{\kappa(x)^2-K^2}},\qquad
\omega_\infty^2=g\kappa(x)\tanh(\kappa(x)\alpha x^p).}
$$
Near shore, $\kappa h\to0$ and $\kappa\sim\omega_\infty/\sqrt{g\alpha}\,x^{-p/2}$. Thus $y'\sim-K\sqrt{g\alpha}\,x^{p/2}/\omega_\infty$, whose integral gives
$$
\boxed{y-y_0\sim A x^q,\qquad q=1+p/2,\qquad
A=-\frac{K\sqrt{g\alpha}}{\omega_\infty(1+p/2)}.}
$$
The rays approach the shoreline normally. A crest is a level set of $\int k_1dx+Ky-\omega t$. Its slope is $dy/dx=-k_1/K\sim\omega_\infty x^{-p/2}/(K\sqrt{g\alpha})$, so crests become parallel to the shoreline. Their separation in the shore-normal direction is $2\pi/|k_1|\sim2\pi\sqrt{g\alpha}x^{p/2}/\omega_\infty$ and decreases to zero. These are formal ray asymptotics; for $p<2$, the slow-variation assumption eventually fails arbitrarily close to the shoreline. The sketch shows a representative shallow-water case $p=1$; it illustrates these limiting directions rather than assuming that value in the derivation.

These <vectors> do not depend on $a,b$. To prove they form a basis even when <eigenvalues> of $A$ coincide, apply the calculation first to the <symmetric matrix> with $a=0,b=1$, whose <eigenvalues> $2\cos\theta_k$ are distinct. Its $M$ nonzero <eigenvectors> are orthogonal and form a basis. Their squared lengths are $(M+1)/2$, so multiplying each by $\sqrt{2/(M+1)}$ gives a common <orthonormal eigenbasis> for every <matrix> in the family.

With the endpoint values zero, move the new-level differences to the left and keep the old-level differences on the right. Then $B$ is tridiagonal with diagonal $1+\mu$ and off-diagonal entries $-\mu/2$, while $C$ has diagonal $1-\mu$ and off-diagonal entries $\mu/2$. Thus **$Bu^{n+1}=Cu^n$**.

Part (a) supplies their common <orthonormal eigenbasis>. Their <eigenvalues> are $1+\mu(1-\cos\theta_k)$ and $1-\mu(1-\cos\theta_k)$. For $\mu\geq0$, $B$ is invertible, and the step <matrix> $B^{-1}C$ has <eigenvalues>
$$
\eta_k=\frac{1-\mu(1-\cos\theta_k)}{1+\mu(1-\cos\theta_k)},\qquad |\eta_k|\leq1.
$$
Since the eigenbasis is orthonormal, $\|(B^{-1}C)^n\|_2=\max_k|\eta_k|^n\leq1$, uniformly in the number of steps and mesh. Hence the method is **unconditionally stable for every $\mu\geq0$**, which includes all physical positive time steps. For negative $\mu$, every nonzero modal parameter has modulus larger than one whenever its denominator is nonzero; if a denominator vanishes the step is undefined. Thus the algebraic stable range is exactly $[0,\infty)$. This proof uses simultaneous <matrix> diagonalization, not Fourier stability analysis.
