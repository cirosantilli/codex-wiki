= Solution

A <central series> is a finite chain of <normal subgroups>
$$
1=G_0\le G_1\le\cdots\le G_r=G
$$
with $G_i/G_{i-1}\le Z(G/G_{i-1})$ for every $i\ge1$. Equivalently, writing $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$ and $[A,B]$ for the subgroup generated by these <group commutators>, the condition is $[G_i,G]\le G_{i-1}$. Centrality is relative to the whole <quotient group>, not just to the next subgroup in the chain.

**A group is nilpotent if it admits a finite <central series>.** The trivial group admits a series of length zero. For a nontrivial <nilpotent group>, the minimum possible length is its <nilpotency class>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use induction on the order of the finite <p-group>. The trivial group is nilpotent. For $G\ne1$, the permitted center theorem gives $Z=Z(G)\ne1$, so the <quotient group> $G/Z$ is a smaller <p-group>. By induction it has a <central series>
$$
1=\overline H_0\le\overline H_1\le\cdots\le\overline H_s=G/Z.
$$
Let $H_i$ be the inverse image of $\overline H_i$ under the quotient map, so $H_0=Z$ and $H_s=G$. Centrality in the quotient gives $[H_i,G]\le H_{i-1}$ for $i\ge1$: the image of every such <group commutator> lies in $\overline H_{i-1}$. Also $[Z,G]=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{1\le Z=H_0\le H_1\le\cdots\le H_s=G}
$$
is a <central series> of $G$. This proves **every finite p-group is nilpotent**, including the case $Z=G$, when the quotient is trivial and the series is simply $1\le G$.

= Solution

Assume $[H,G]\le K$. For $k\in K\le H$ and $g\in G$,
$$
g^{-1}kg=k[k,g]\in K.
$$
Thus $g^{-1}Kg\le K$ for every $g$; applying this also to $g^{-1}$ gives equality. Hence $K$ is a <normal subgroup> of $G$, even though initially it was assumed normal only in $H$. Now the quotient $G/K$ is defined, and for $h\in H$, $g\in G$,
$$
[hK,gK]=[h,g]K=K.
$$
Every element of $H/K$ consequently lies in the <center of a group> $Z(G/K)$.

Conversely, if $K\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}G$ and $H/K\le Z(G/K)$, each displayed quotient <group commutator> is trivial, so $[h,g]\in K$. Taking the subgroup generated by these elements gives $[H,G]\le K$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[H,G]\le K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}G\ \text{and}\ H/K\le Z(G/K).}
$$
The forward argument also implies $H\mathrel{\trianglelefteq}G$, since $g^{-1}hg=h[h,g]\in H$.

Let $1=A_0\le\cdots\le A_r=A$ and $1=B_0\le\cdots\le B_s=B$ be <central series>. Pad the shorter chain by repeating its terminal group, so both have length $t=\max(r,s)$. In the <direct product of groups>, <group commutators> are coordinatewise, hence
$$
[A_i\times B_i,A\times B]=[A_i,A]\times[B_i,B]\le A_{i-1}\times B_{i-1}.
$$
The equality holds because commutators supported in either coordinate generate the corresponding factor independently. Therefore
$$
\boxed{1=A_0\times B_0\le\cdots\le A_t\times B_t=A\times B}
$$
is a <central series>, proving that **a direct product of two nilpotent groups is nilpotent**. More precisely, the coordinatewise recurrence for the <lower central series> gives $\Gamma_j(A\times B)=\Gamma_j(A)\times\Gamma_j(B)$, so the <nilpotency class of a direct product> is the maximum of the two factor classes.

Define the <lower central series> by $\Gamma_1(G)=G$ and $\Gamma_{j+1}(G)=[\Gamma_j(G),G]$. Its terms are characteristic <normal subgroups> and form a descending chain. Define the <upper central series> by $Z_0(G)=1$ and
$$
Z_{i+1}(G)/Z_i(G)=Z\big(G/Z_i(G)\big).
$$
Equivalently, $Z_{i+1}(G)=\{x\in G:[x,G]\subseteq Z_i(G)\}$. These are characteristic <normal subgroups> in an ascending chain, and successive factors are central in the appropriate quotient.

For any length-$r$ <central series> $1=G_0\le\cdots\le G_r=G$, prove the two comparisons independently. Starting at $\Gamma_1(G)=G_r$, if $\Gamma_{r-i+1}(G)\le G_i$, then
$$
\Gamma_{r-i+2}(G)=[\Gamma_{r-i+1}(G),G]\le[G_i,G]\le G_{i-1}.
$$
Descending induction gives the lower containment. Starting at $G_0=Z_0(G)=1$, if $G_{i-1}\le Z_{i-1}(G)$, then $[G_i,G]\le G_{i-1}\le Z_{i-1}(G)$, which by definition gives $G_i\le Z_i(G)$. Thus the <central-series comparison theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{r-i+1}(G)\le G_i\le Z_i(G)\quad(0\le i\le r).}
$$
In particular, a <nilpotent group> satisfies $\Gamma_{r+1}(G)=1$ and $Z_r(G)=G$.

Conversely, if $\Gamma_n(G)=1$, reverse the finite lower chain to obtain $1=\Gamma_n\le\Gamma_{n-1}\le\cdots\le\Gamma_1=G$. The defining commutator recurrence makes it a <central series>. If $Z_n(G)=G$, the finite upper chain is already a <central series>. This proves
$$
\boxed{G\text{ nilpotent}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \Gamma_n(G)=1\text{ for some }n\ \Longleftrightarrow\ Z_n(G)=G\text{ for some }n.}
$$
Finally, for an integer $c>0$, termination at $\Gamma_{c+1}=1$ gives a <central series> of length $c$ and hence $Z_c=G$ by the comparison. Conversely, $Z_c=G$ gives a <central series> of length $c$ and hence $\Gamma_{c+1}=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{c+1}(G)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z_c(G)=G.}
$$
Repeated terms cause no problem; the least such $c$ for a nontrivial group is its <nilpotency class>.

Choose a representative <Sylow subgroup> with generators
$$
r=(1\ 2\ 3\ 4),\qquad s=(1\ 3),\qquad t=(5\ 6).
$$
On the first four points, $r^4=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$, so $\langle r,s\rangle$ is the <dihedral group> of order eight. The disjoint <transposition> $t$ commutes with it and generates an independent factor of order two. Thus $G=\langle r,s,t\rangle\cong D_8\times C_2$ has order 16. Since the power of two dividing $7!$ is $2^{\lfloor7/2\rfloor+\lfloor7/4\rfloor}=2^4$, this is a Sylow 2-subgroup of $S_7$. Any other choice is conjugate to it and has conjugate series.

The dihedral relation gives $[r,s]=r^2$, while $t$ commutes with both generators. Modulo $\langle r^2\rangle$ all three generators commute, so the <commutator subgroup> is exactly $\langle r^2\rangle$, and $r^2$ is central. Hence the <lower central series> is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_1(G)=G,\qquad \Gamma_2(G)=\langle(1\ 3)(2\ 4)\rangle,\qquad \Gamma_3(G)=1.}
$$
Within the dihedral factor only $1,r^2$ commute with both $r$ and $s$. The center of $G$ is therefore $\langle r^2,t\rangle\cong C_2\times C_2$. Its quotient has order four and is abelian, so the <upper central series> is
$$
\boxed{Z_0(G)=1,\qquad Z_1(G)=\langle(1\ 3)(2\ 4),(5\ 6)\rangle,\qquad Z_2(G)=G.}
$$
Both series show that the <nilpotency class> is two.

Equivalently, one may use an isomorphic permutation representation with a distinguished point whose stabilizer acts on the remaining points as the original group.

\boxed{|G^+|=(|\Omega|+1)|G|.}

Suppose an extension $H$ existed. Its degree would be $p$, and its order would be $2p(p-1)$ by the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>. A Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ consequently has order $p$, and the <Sylow theorems> imply that its number $n_p$ divides $2(p-1)$ and is congruent to one modulo $p$. Since $2(p-1)<2p$, the only possibilities are $1$ and $p+1$. The latter would require $p+1\mid2(p-1)$ and hence $p+1\mid4$, impossible for $p\ge5$. Thus $P$ is a <normal subgroup> of $H$.

A nonidentity element of $P$ is a $p$-cycle, so $P$ acts regularly on the $p$ points. Label them by $\mathbb F_p$ so that $P$ consists of translations. A permutation normalizing $P$ must have the form $x\mapsto ax+b$: its conjugation sends translation by one to translation by some $a\ne0$, and its value at zero determines $b$. Therefore the <normalizer> of $P$ in $S_p$ has order $p(p-1)$. Normality would place $H$ inside this normalizer, contradicting $|H|=2p(p-1)$. Hence **the specified dihedral permutation group has no one-point extension**.

Here is an <involution> form of the <double-coset criterion for a one-point extension>. Embed $G$ in $\operatorname{Sym}(\Omega\cup\{\infty\})$ by fixing $\infty$, choose $a\in\Omega$, and put $H=G_a$. The necessary and sufficient condition is the existence of an <involution> $t$ such that
$$
\boxed{t(\infty)=a,\quad t(a)=\infty,\quad tHt=H,\quad tgt\in GtG\ \text{for every }g\in G\setminus H.}
$$
Products in this part are compositions of maps, with the rightmost map applied first. The criterion is a finite test on permutations of the enlarged set; it does not presuppose that a larger group has already been found.

For sufficiency, put $K=G\cup GtG$. Multiplying a <double coset> by an element of $G$ on either side leaves it unchanged. A product of two elements of $GtG$ reduces to $g_1tgtg_2$. If $g\in H$, then $tgt\in H\le G$; otherwise the last condition puts it in $GtG$. Hence $K$ is closed under multiplication. It contains the identity and is closed under inverses, since $t^{-1}=t$, so it is a group.

Every element $g_1tg_2$ sends $\infty$ to $g_1(a)\in\Omega$, whereas elements of $G$ fix $\infty$. Thus $K_\infty=G$. Moreover the orbit of $\infty$ contains $a$ and then all of $\Omega$, so $K$ is transitive and is the desired <one-point extension of a permutation group>. For the order check, $G\cap tGt=H$: an element of the intersection fixes both $\infty$ and $a$, and the reverse containment follows from $tHt=H$. Consequently $|GtG|=|G|^2/|H|=|\Omega||G|$, as required.

For necessity, let $K$ be an extension. It is <doubly transitive group action>, of order $|\Omega|(|\Omega|+1)|G_a|$, which is even. By <Cauchy theorem for groups> it contains a nontrivial <involution>, and a pair interchanged by this <involution> can be carried to $(\infty,a)$ by double transitivity. Conjugating gives an <involution> $t$ interchanging these two points. Their common <point stabilizer> is $H$, so $tHt=H$. Finally $G$ has just two orbits on the enlarged set, namely $\{\infty\}$ and $\Omega$, giving $K=G\cup GtG$. More explicitly, for $k\in K\setminus G$, choose $g\in G$ with $g(a)=k(\infty)$; then $tg^{-1}k$ fixes $\infty$ and belongs to $G$. If $g\notin H$, the element $tgt$ moves $\infty$, so it belongs to $GtG$, completing necessity.

Adjoin $\infty$ to $\Omega=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and take $G=A_n$ fixing it. Choose three distinct points $a,b,c\in\Omega$ and put $t=(\infty\ a)(b\ c)$. This is an even <involution> interchanging $\infty$ and $a$. Conjugation by it preserves $H=G_a$, because it permutes the remaining $n-1$ points and preserves parity. Thus $tHt=H$.

If $g\in G\setminus H$, the even permutation $k=tgt$ moves $\infty$ into $\Omega$. The <alternating group> $A_n$ is transitive for $n\ge3$: a suitable 3-cycle carries any prescribed point to another. Choose $u\in G$ with $u(a)=k(\infty)$. Then $v=tu^{-1}k$ is even and fixes $\infty$, so $v\in G$ and $k=utv\in GtG$. All conditions of the <double-coset criterion for a one-point extension> hold, including when $n=3$ and $H$ is trivial.

The constructed extension lies in $A_{n+1}$ and has order $(n+1)|A_n|=(n+1)!/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{G^+=A_{n+1}\text{ in its natural action is a one-point extension of }A_n.}
$$

A nonidentity <transvection> is a linear automorphism $\tau=I+N$ with $\operatorname{rank}N=1$ and $N^2=0$. Equivalently, it fixes a <hyperplane> pointwise and its displacement has image in a line lying in that hyperplane. The notation $T\setminus\{1\}$ allows the identity as a degenerate <transvection>; use that convention here, with $N=0$ also allowed.

For a nonidentity <transvection>, choose $d\ne0$ spanning the image of $N$. There is a unique nonzero <linear functional> $f$ such that $N(v)=f(v)d$. Since $N^2(v)=f(v)f(d)d$, we have $f(d)=0$, so $d\in\ker f$. Conversely, this condition makes $(df)^2=0$ and gives inverse $I-df$. The identity is represented by $f=0$ and any $d\ne0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\tau_{f,d}(v)=v+f(v)d,\qquad d\ne0,\quad f(d)=0.}
$$
When $f(d)=f'(d)=0$, the two displacement maps have zero product, giving
$$
\boxed{\tau_{f,d}\tau_{f',d}=\tau_{f+f',d},\qquad \tau_{f,d}^{-1}=\tau_{-f,d}.}
$$
For clarity use left actions of linear maps. Applying $g^{-1}$, then $\tau_{f,d}$, then $g$ gives
$$
\boxed{g\tau_{f,d}g^{-1}=\tau_{f\circ g^{-1},\,g(d)}.}
$$
With the alternative conjugation notation $\tau^g=g^{-1}\tau g$, the answer is $\tau_{f,d}^g=\tau_{f\circ g,\,g^{-1}(d)}$. In either convention the transformed direction lies in the kernel of the transformed functional.

For a nonidentity <transvection> $\tau_{f,d}$, take $e_1=d$ and choose $e_2$ with $f(e_2)=1$. Complete $e_1$ to a basis of $\ker f$ using $e_3,\ldots,e_n$. Then $e_1,e_2,e_3,\ldots,e_n$ is a basis of $V$, and the matrix of the <transvection> is $I+E_{12}$: it sends $e_2$ to $e_2+e_1$ and fixes the other basis vectors. Consequently all nonidentity <transvections> form one <conjugacy class> in $\operatorname{GL}(V)$. This matrix has <determinant> one, and conjugation preserves <determinant>, so
$$
\boxed{T^\#\text{ is a single GL-conjugacy class contained in }\operatorname{SL}(V).}
$$
For $n\ge3$, let $k$ be any GL-conjugator carrying one <transvection> to another. In an adapted basis for the first, the matrix $c=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,s,1,\ldots,1)$ centralizes it and has <determinant> $s$. Set $s=(\det k)^{-1}$. Then $kc\in\operatorname{SL}(V)$ gives the same conjugation as $k$. This proves the stronger <transvection conjugacy over a finite field> result
$$
\boxed{n\ge3\ \Longrightarrow\ T^\#\text{ is a single SL-conjugacy class}.}
$$
The extra coordinate is essential to this <determinant> adjustment; no such claim is required in dimension two.

Write $x_{ij}(a)=I+aE_{ij}$ for $i\ne j$. For $a\ne0$ these are <transvections>, and for $a=0$ they are the identity. Left multiplication by $x_{ij}(a)$ adds $a$ times row $j$ to row $i$. We show that these <elementary transvection matrices> generate the <special linear group>.

Apply <Gaussian elimination> using only row additions. If a pivot is zero, invertibility ensures some lower entry in its column is nonzero; adding that row produces a nonzero pivot without interchanging or scaling rows. Clear entries below each pivot, and then above pivots in reverse order. This reduces any invertible matrix to a diagonal matrix $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$, with all $d_i\ne0$, preserving <determinant> throughout. For a determinant-one matrix, $\prod_i d_i=1$.

A two-coordinate diagonal block is itself a product of <elementary transvection matrices>. On those two coordinates put
$$
w(a)=x_{12}(a)x_{21}(-a^{-1})x_{12}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad w(a)w(-1)=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This formula is valid in characteristic two as well. For $n\ge2$, multiply the diagonal blocks with entries $d_i,d_i^{-1}$ on coordinates $(i,n)$ for $1\le i<n$. Their product is $D$, because its last entry is $\prod_{i<n}d_i^{-1}=d_n$. Thus $D$ and the original matrix lie in the subgroup generated by <transvections>. Conversely every <transvection> has <determinant> one by part ii. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle T\rangle=\operatorname{SL}(V).}
$$
For $n=1$, both the <special linear group> and the subgroup generated by the degenerate identity <transvection> are trivial. The argument also establishes <transvections generate the special linear group> over any field.

Use the <group commutator> convention $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$. If $n\ge3$, choose distinct $i,j,k$. Multiplying elementary matrices, using $E_{ik}E_{kj}=E_{ij}$ and all reverse or repeated products here equal to zero, gives
$$
[x_{ik}(a),x_{kj}(1)]=x_{ij}(a).
$$
Every elementary generator is therefore in the <commutator subgroup>. Part iii shows these generators generate $\operatorname{SL}(V)$, proving it is a <perfect group>.

It remains to handle $n=2$ and $q>3$. The polynomial $s^2-1$ has at most two roots, whereas $\mathbb F_q^\times$ has more than two elements, so choose $s\ne0$ with $s^2\ne1$. For $h=\operatorname{diag}(s,s^{-1})$, direct conjugation gives
$$
[h,x_{12}(t)]=x_{12}((1-s^{-2})t),\qquad [h,x_{21}(t)]=x_{21}((1-s^2)t).
$$
Both coefficients are nonzero. As $t$ varies, these <group commutators> give every upper and lower elementary <transvection>, which generate $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_q)$. Thus the <perfectness of the special linear group> is established in all requested cases:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbb F_q)'=\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbb F_q)\quad\text{if }n\ge2,\ (n,q)\notin\{(2,2),(2,3)\}.}
$$
The two pairs in the PDF are exclusions, not a literal logical disjunction. They really are exceptions: $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_2)\cong S_3$ has a nontrivial sign quotient. Also $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb F_3)$ maps onto $\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_3)\cong A_4$, which has the nontrivial abelian quotient $A_4/V_4\cong C_3$. For the latter identification, the faithful projective action has degree four and image of order 12, hence the index-two subgroup $A_4$ of $S_4$. Neither exceptional group is perfect.

First determine when a <group automorphism> can carry a <transposition> to another type of <involution>. An <involution> in $S_n$ is a product of $k$ disjoint <transpositions>, and its <conjugacy class> has size
$$
\frac{n!}{2^k k!(n-2k)!}.
$$
An automorphism preserves element orders and conjugacy-class sizes. Equality with the transposition-class size $n(n-1)/2$ requires
$$
\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-2k)!}=2^{k-1}k!.
$$
For $k=2$ this says $(n-2)(n-3)=4$, which has no integral solution $n\ge4$. For $k=3$, the minimum value of the left side, at $n=6$, is $4!=24=2^2\cdot3!$; it increases strictly for larger $n$. For $k\ge4$, even its minimum $(2k-2)!$ exceeds $2^{k-1}k!$: at $k=4$ this is $720>192$, and the ratio of these two quantities increases at the next step by $(2k)(2k-1)/(2(k+1))>1$. Thus only in degree six can the <transposition> class be exchanged with a different <involution> class, namely the triple <transpositions>.

We next prove that any automorphism preserving <transpositions> is an <inner automorphism>. Regard a <transposition> as an edge of the <complete graph> on the $n$ letters. Two distinct <transpositions> fail to commute exactly when their edges meet. Any family of pairwise intersecting edges is contained in a vertex star or a triangle: after choosing $\{a,b\}$ and $\{a,c\}$, an edge not containing $a$ must be $\{b,c\}$, which precludes any fourth edge. For $n\ge5$, the stars are therefore distinguished as the maximal such families of size $n-1\ge4$. An automorphism permutes stars, inducing a permutation $\pi$ of the letters; their pairwise intersections show that $(i\ j)$ is sent to $(\pi(i)\ \pi(j))$. Since <transpositions> generate $S_n$, this is conjugation by a permutation.

For $n=4$, stars and triangles both have three edges, but a star generates $S_4$ while a triangle generates a subgroup $S_3$. An automorphism preserves the order of the generated subgroup and hence still distinguishes stars. For $n=3$, every permutation of the three <transpositions> is induced by conjugation in $S_3$, since its conjugation action on them is faithful and has order six. The groups $S_1,S_2$ have trivial automorphism groups. This completes the small cases.

It follows that all automorphisms for $n\ne6$ are inner. In degree six, an automorphism permutes the two <involution> classes of size 15; the kernel of this action consists precisely of the inner automorphisms. Therefore the <automorphisms of the symmetric group> satisfy
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Out}(S_n)=1\ (n\ne6),\qquad |\operatorname{Out}(S_6)|\le2.}
$$

A <duad> is an unordered pair of letters, a <syntheme> is a partition of the six letters into three <duads>, and a <total of synthemes> is a set of five <synthemes> partitioning the 15 <duads>. There are $6!/(2^3\cdot3!)=15$ <synthemes>. Fix one, $M$. Each of its three <duads> lies in three <synthemes>, and two distinct <duads> of $M$ can occur together only in $M$ itself. Thus precisely $1+3\cdot2=7$ <synthemes> meet $M$ in a <duad>, leaving eight disjoint from it.

For any <syntheme> $M'$ disjoint from $M$, their union as edges in $K_6$ is a six-cycle: the two <perfect matchings> alternate, and no two-cycle is possible in a simple graph. Its complement is a triangular prism, namely two triangles joined by three corresponding edges. The prism has four <perfect matchings>. One consists of all three joining edges; each of the other three consists of one joining edge and the remaining edge in each triangle. To partition the prism into three <perfect matchings>, one cannot use the all-joining matching, since what remains is two odd triangles. The other three do partition it. Hence **two disjoint <synthemes> extend to a unique <synthematic total>**.

There are eight choices for $M'$ and four choices of $M'$ within each <synthematic total> containing $M$. Thus $M$ lies in exactly two <synthematic totals>. Counting incidences of <synthemes> and <synthematic totals> now gives the <counting synthematic totals> formula
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{totals}\}=\frac{15\cdot2}{5}=6.}
$$
Two distinct <synthematic totals> share at most one <syntheme>, since sharing two would force them to be the unique <synthematic total> extending that pair. Each <syntheme> gives a pair of <synthematic totals> containing it, and the 15 <synthemes> account for all $\binom62=15$ pairs. Therefore every pair of <synthematic totals> shares exactly one <syntheme>.

This also explains why the action on the six <synthematic totals> defines an automorphism of $S_6$. A permutation fixing all <synthematic totals> fixes their pairwise intersections, hence every <syntheme>. Each <duad> is the intersection of two distinct <synthemes> containing it, so every <duad> is then fixed. Intersecting two <duads> through a point shows that every point is fixed. The action is faithful and, by equality of orders, maps $S_6$ onto the full symmetric group of the <synthematic totals>. Part iii shows that a <transposition> acts as a triple <transposition>, establishing that this is an <outer automorphism>.

(3\ 4)^\alpha&=(1\ 2)(3\ 6)(4\ 5),\\
(4\ 5)^\alpha&=(1\ 6)(2\ 5)(3\ 4),\\
(5\ 6)^\alpha&=(1\ 2)(3\ 5)(4\ 6).
\end{aligned}}
$$
These formulae use the PDF's numbering of the <synthematic totals>, which is essential for the particular fixed elements below. Compose permutations from left to right, consistently with the right action on <synthematic totals>. Multiplying the second and fourth images gives $(2\ 3)(4\ 5)$, while multiplying the third and fifth gives $(3\ 4)(5\ 6)$. Hence the two <involutions>
$$
\boxed{u=(2\ 3)(4\ 5),\qquad v=(3\ 4)(5\ 6)}
$$
are fixed by the <outer automorphism> $\alpha$. Since $\alpha$ is a <group automorphism>, their product is fixed as well. Applying $u$ and then $v$ yields
$$
\boxed{uv=(2\ 4\ 6\ 5\ 3),\qquad (uv)^\alpha=uv.}
$$
Under right-to-left composition the product is the inverse cycle, which is also fixed; the displayed convention removes that ambiguity.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

A <Steiner system> $S(5,6,12)$ consists of 12 points and a collection of six-element blocks such that every five-element subset lies in exactly one block. The blocks are often called hexads. Necessarily their number is $\binom{12}{5}/\binom65=132$. We give the <synthematic construction of the small Witt design> explicitly and prove this defining property.

Write $X=\{1,\ldots,6\}$ and $Y=\{T_1,\ldots,T_6\}$. For an unordered pair $P=\{T_i,T_j\}\subset Y$, let $S(P)$ denote the unique <syntheme> shared by its two <synthematic totals>. A <duad> $D\subset X$ belongs to three <synthemes>. The corresponding three pairs $P$ partition $Y$: every <synthematic total> contains $D$ in exactly one of its <synthemes>, and each <syntheme> lies in exactly two <synthematic totals>. Thus the incidence $D\in S(P)$ gives a perfect matching of the six <synthematic totals> for each <duad>.

To describe the blocks with three points in each half, take an unordered partition $X=A\sqcup A^c$ into triples. A <syntheme> is cross if each of its three <duads> joins $A$ to $A^c$. Form a graph on $Y$ by declaring $P$ an edge exactly when $S(P)$ is cross. This graph is the union of two disjoint triangles. Indeed a <synthematic total> partitions the nine cross <duads>; every <syntheme> has either one or three cross <duads>, so if $k$ of its five <synthemes> are cross, then $3k+(5-k)=9$, giving $k=2$. Each vertex therefore has degree two. The stronger assertion about its components follows directly for $A=\{1,2,3\}$, when the two triangles are
$$
\{T_1,T_4,T_5\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{T_2,T_3,T_6\};
$$
any other triple partition is obtained by relabelling $X$, which also permutes the <synthematic totals>. Denote the two components by $B,B^c$.

The complete block collection is given by the following three rules:

* Include $X$ and $Y$.
* For each $P\subset Y$ of size two and each <duad> $D\in S(P)$, include $D\cup(Y\setminus P)$ and its complement $(X\setminus D)\cup P$.
* For each unordered triple partition $A\sqcup A^c$ and its two components $B,B^c$, include $A\cup B$, $A\cup B^c$, $A^c\cup B$, and $A^c\cup B^c$.

There are $2+15\cdot3\cdot2+10\cdot4=132$ distinct blocks. The first two rules have different intersection sizes with $X$ from the third; within the third, a block determines its triple $A$ and its component. We next verify the <Steiner system> property, rather than relying solely on this count.

We need one further consequence of the triangle construction. The three <synthemes> attached to the three pairs within $B$ are pairwise disjoint, since any two belong to their common <synthematic total>, and together they are all nine cross <duads> of the <complete bipartite graph> between $A$ and $A^c$. Thus $B$ determines the unordered partition $A\sqcup A^c$ uniquely: it is the bipartition of this connected graph. There are 10 such partitions, each giving two different components, hence 20 different triples in $Y$. Since $\binom63=20$, **each triple in $Y$ corresponds to exactly one triple partition of $X$**. The same reasoning applies to $B^c$.

Let $F$ be any five-point set and put $r=|F\cap X|$. If $r=5$, only $X$ can contain it; if $r=0$, only $Y$ can contain it, because no other block has five points in either half.

If $r=4$, set $D=X\setminus F$ and write $F\cap Y=\{T\}$. The three pairs attached to $D$ partition $Y$, so exactly one contains $T$; its block $(X\setminus D)\cup P$ is the unique block through $F$. If $r=1$, put $P=Y\setminus F$ and write $F\cap X=\{x\}$. The three <duads> of $S(P)$ partition $X$, so exactly one contains $x$; this gives the unique block $D\cup(Y\setminus P)$.

If $r=3$, write $A=F\cap X$ and $P=F\cap Y$. A <syntheme> has either one or three <duads> crossing $A\sqcup A^c$. In the one-cross case, $S(P)$ has exactly one <duad> $D$ internal to $A^c$, and the unique block is $(X\setminus D)\cup P$. No block with three points in each half is possible, since $P$ is then not an edge of either triangle. In the three-cross case, $P$ is an edge of exactly one triangle $B$, giving the unique block $A\cup B$; no <duad> of $S(P)$ is internal to $A^c$, so a block with four points in $X$ is impossible.

Finally let $r=2$, write $D=F\cap X$ and $B=F\cap Y$, and use the uniquely associated partition $X=A\sqcup A^c$ just proved. If $D$ crosses this partition, the three <synthemes> belonging to the pairs inside $B$ contain each cross <duad> exactly once. Thus the matching of $Y$ attached to $D$ has exactly one pair internal to $B$. It has exactly one pair $P$ internal to $B^c$ as well, since a matching must leave equal numbers of vertices in the two halves for cross pairs. The unique block containing $F$ is then $D\cup(Y\setminus P)$; $D$ is not contained in either $A$ or $A^c$, so no block of the third rule applies. If $D$ is internal to $A$ or $A^c$, none of its attached pairs lies inside $B$ or $B^c$, since the <synthemes> attached to these internal pairs are cross. Thus the second rule gives no block. The third rule gives exactly one, namely $A\cup B$ or $A^c\cup B$, according to which part contains $D$.

All possibilities have now been exhausted, proving
$$
\boxed{\text{These 132 hexads form an }S(5,6,12)\text{ on }X\sqcup Y.}
$$
This is the <small Witt design>; the construction uses only <duads>, <synthemes>, their <synthematic totals>, and the induced action on those <synthematic totals>.
