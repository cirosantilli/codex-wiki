Let $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\omega_1+\mathbb Z\omega_2$ be a <complex lattice>. Orient its basis so that $\operatorname{Im}(\omega_1/\omega_2)>0$. Multiplication by $\omega_2^{-1}$ gives the normalized <complex lattice> $\mathbb Z\tau+\mathbb Z$ with $\tau=\omega_1/\omega_2\in\mathbb H$. Thus every <homothety of complex lattices> class has a normalized representative.

If $\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)$, then
$$
\mathbb Z(\gamma\tau)+\mathbb Z
=(c\tau+d)^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb Z(a\tau+b)+\mathbb Z(c\tau+d)\bigr)
=(c\tau+d)^{-1}(\mathbb Z\tau+\mathbb Z).
$$
The last equality follows because the two integer <vectors> are an integral basis: their <determinant> is one. Hence points in the same modular <orbit> give <homothetic complex lattices>.

Conversely, suppose $\Lambda_{\tau'}=\alpha\Lambda_\tau$ for $\alpha\ne0$. There is an integer basis-change <matrix> of <determinant> $\pm1$ such that
$$
\tau'=\alpha(a\tau+b),\qquad 1=\alpha(c\tau+d).
$$
Both normalized bases have positive <orientation>, and multiplication by $\alpha$ preserves real <orientation> because its real <determinant> is $|\alpha|^2>0$. The basis-change <determinant> is therefore $+1$. Dividing the two equalities gives $\tau'=(a\tau+b)/(c\tau+d)$. Thus **the modular orbits and <homothety of complex lattices> classes correspond bijectively**.

G_6(\alpha\Lambda)=\alpha^{-6}G_6(\Lambda).
$$

$$
\boxed{\Lambda'=\Lambda.}

Adjoin $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Q)$ to the <complex upper half-plane>. Ordinary points retain their usual neighborhoods. At a rational boundary point $a=\sigma\infty$, a neighborhood basis consists of $a$ together with $\sigma\{z\in\mathbb H:\operatorname{Im}z>Y\}$, with $Y$ arbitrarily large. Different choices of $\sigma\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ change it by a translation and sign, so give the same neighborhood system. Give $X(1)$ the <quotient topology>. The <modular group> acts properly discontinuously on $\mathbb H$; its <modular cusp> neighborhoods descend to punctured neighborhoods with their <modular cusp> point filled in. This quotient is Hausdorff: interior orbits can be separated by proper discontinuity, and a sufficiently high <modular cusp> neighborhood avoids any prescribed compact interior neighborhood.

Away from fixed points of the effective group $PSL_2(\mathbb Z)$, a small disk is disjoint from its nonidentity translates, so its coordinate gives a <holomorphic> quotient coordinate. At an elliptic fixed point $\tau_0$, use
$$
u=\frac{\tau-\tau_0}{\tau-\overline{\tau_0}}.
$$
Its effective <stabilizer> rotates $u$ by an $e$th root of unity, where $e=2$ at $i$ and $e=3$ at $\rho$. The quotient coordinate is $w=u^e$, not $u$. All rational boundary points lie in one <modular cusp> class. At infinity the <stabilizer> acts by $\tau\mapsto\tau+1$, and
$$
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
$$
is a punctured-disk coordinate; adjoining $q=0$ fills in the <modular cusp>. These charts and their <holomorphic> transition maps define the complex structure of the <compactified modular curve>.

For <compactness> use the standard region
$$
\mathcal D=\{\tau\in\mathbb H:|\operatorname{Re}\tau|\leq1/2,\ |\tau|\geq1\}.
$$
Its part with $\operatorname{Im}\tau\leq Y$, $Y>1$, is compact: the imaginary part is at least $\sqrt3/2$. The quotient of its upper tail, with its two vertical sides identified and infinity added, is the closed disk $|q|\leq e^{-2\pi Y}$. These two compact images cover $X(1)$, including the boundary-edge identifications. Thus **$X(1)$ is a <compact Riemann surface>**.

The $j$ function also extends over the <modular cusp> with one <simple pole>, since $j=q^{-1}+O(1)$. Its local expressions at <elliptic points> descend to <holomorphic functions> of $u^e$. By Question 1 it maps the remaining quotient bijectively onto $\mathbb C$, so its extension is a degree-one <holomorphic map> $X(1)\to\mathbb P^1$. A degree-one map of compact Riemann surfaces is <biholomorphic>: any local ramification would already give degree at least two. Therefore $X(1)$ is the <Riemann sphere>; this will also identify the base <genus> in Question 5.

A weight-$2k$ <modular form> gives an invariant tensor differential
$$
\omega_f=f(\tau)(d\tau)^k,
$$
because $d(\gamma\tau)=(c\tau+d)^{-2}d\tau$ cancels its <automorphy factor>. Assume first $k\geq0$. At an ordinary quotient point this differential is <holomorphic>. At an <elliptic point> of order $e$, use $u$ and $w=u^e$ from part (a), and write the pullback as $A(u)(du)^k$, with $A$ <holomorphic>. Its invariance says $A(\zeta u)\zeta^k=A(u)$, so a nonzero term $a_ru^r$ has $r+k\equiv0\pmod e$. Since
$$
u^r(du)^k=\frac1{e^k}u^{r-k(e-1)}(dw)^k,
$$
the exponent in the descended coefficient is the integer $(r-k(e-1))/e$. With $r\geq0$, it is at least $-\lfloor k(e-1)/e\rfloor$. At the <modular cusp>, $d\tau=dq/(2\pi iq)$ and $f$ has a power <series> with nonnegative exponents, so $\omega_f$ has a <pole> of order at most $k$.

Consequently the <linear map> $f\mapsto\omega_f$ injects $M_{2k}$ into the <meromorphic> sections of $K^{\otimes k}$ with poles bounded by
$$
D=k[\infty]+\lfloor k/2\rfloor[i]+\lfloor2k/3\rfloor[\rho].
$$
This section space is $H^0(X(1),K^{\otimes k}(D))$. It is finite-dimensional on a <compact Riemann surface> by the <Riemann-Roch theorem>. Concretely choose one nonzero <meromorphic> section of the line bundle; dividing by it identifies these sections with a <Riemann-Roch space> with a fixed finite <divisor> bound. This proves the requested finite-dimensionality by compact-surface theory.

In fact the injection is an isomorphism. If a descended coefficient has order at least $-\lfloor k(e-1)/e\rfloor$, its pulled-back exponent $ed+k(e-1)$ is nonnegative; the <modular cusp> <pole> bound likewise gives a <holomorphic> <Fourier expansion of a modular form>. These pullbacks therefore recover <modular forms>. Since $X(1)$ is the <Riemann sphere>, $\deg K=-2$, and a line bundle of degree $d$ on the sphere has $\max(0,d+1)$ sections. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim M_{2k}=\max\left(0,1-k+\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2k}{3}\right\rfloor\right)\quad(k\geq0).}
$$
For negative $k$, the valence formula excludes a nonzero <holomorphic> <modular form> because all its orders would be nonnegative but their sum would be $k/6<0$; the space is zero. The mechanism behind finite-dimensionality is <modular forms as meromorphic differentials on X(1)>, with explicit elliptic and <modular cusp> <pole> bounds.

The <infinite product> converges locally uniformly in $\mathbb H$, because $\sum_{n\geq1}|q|^n<\infty$ uniformly on compact subsets. None of its factors vanishes there, and the convergent logarithm of its tail shows that the product is nonzero. Logarithmic differentiation, justified by locally <uniform convergence>, gives
$$
\Theta\log F=1-24\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{nq^n}{1-q^n}
=1-24\sum_{m\geq1}\sigma_1(m)q^m=E_2.
$$
The middle equality groups the absolutely convergent terms $nq^{nr}$ by $m=nr$. Thus $F'/F=2\pi iE_2$.

Let $H(\tau)=\tau^{-12}F(-1/\tau)/F(\tau)$. It is nonzero and <holomorphic> on the simply connected upper half-plane. Using the printed transformation $E_2(-1/\tau)=\tau^2E_2(\tau)+6\tau/(\pi i)$, its logarithmic <derivative> is
$$
\frac{H'}H=-\frac{12}{\tau}+
\frac{2\pi i}{\tau^2}E_2(-1/\tau)-2\pi iE_2(\tau)=0.
$$
So $H$ is constant. At $\tau=i$ we have $-1/i=i$ and $i^{-12}=1$, giving $H(i)=1$. Therefore $F(-1/\tau)=\tau^{12}F(\tau)$. Also $F(\tau+1)=F(\tau)$ directly from $q=e^{2\pi i\tau}$. Since these two transformations generate $SL_2(\mathbb Z)$, $F$ has the weight-twelve <modular form> transformation law.

At the unique <modular cusp> its expansion is $F=q+O(q^2)$, so it extends holomorphically with zero value. Hence
$$
\boxed{F\in S_{12}(SL_2(\mathbb Z)),\qquad \Theta F=E_2F.}
$$
This is the <modular discriminant>. The transformed argument contributes $6\tau/(\pi i)$; the converted TeX's numerator $67$ is a transcription error, not the printed relation.

Write $w=2k$. For a positive-determinant real <matrix> $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, extend the <slash operator for modular forms> by
$$
(f|_wA)(\tau)=(\det A)^{w/2}(c\tau+d)^{-w}f(A\tau).
$$
Let $\mathcal M_n$ be the integral <matrices> of <determinant> $n>0$. Each left $SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ <orbit> has a unique representative $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $ad=n$, $a,d>0$, $0\leq b<d$. To obtain it, the <Euclidean algorithm> on the first column produces $(a,0)$ with $a>0$; the <determinant> fixes $d$, and a row shear reduces $b$ modulo $d$. This also proves uniqueness.

Define the normalized <Hecke operator>
$$
\boxed{T_w(n)f=n^{w/2-1}\sum_{A\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)\backslash\mathcal M_n}f|_wA
=n^{w-1}\sum_{ad=n}d^{-w}\sum_{b=0}^{d-1}f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{d}\right).}
$$
Right multiplication by $SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ permutes these left orbits, and the slash operation is associative. Hence the sum satisfies the same <modular form> transformation law. It is <holomorphic> on $\mathbb H$ since each argument stays there.

For $f=\sum_{r\geq0}c(r)q^r$, summing the phase $e^{2\pi irb/d}$ over $b$ gives zero unless $d\mid r$, and gives $d$ when $d\mid r$. For the resulting term to contribute to $q^m$, one needs $a\mid m$ and $r=dm/a=nm/a^2$. Its scalar factor is $n^{w-1}d^{1-w}=a^{w-1}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{T_w(n)f=\sum_{m\geq0}\left(\sum_{a\mid\gcd(n,m)}a^{w-1}c\left(\frac{nm}{a^2}\right)\right)q^m,}
$$
where $\gcd(n,0)=n$. All powers are nonnegative, so the sum is <holomorphic> at infinity and belongs to $M_w$; level one has only this <modular cusp> class. Its constant coefficient is $\sigma_{w-1}(n)c(0)$, so <modular cusp> forms are preserved. This is the full <Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator> formula, including the constant term.

An eigenform is understood to be nonzero; the zero form would put no constraint on the alleged <eigenvalues>. The coefficient of $q$ in $T_w(n)f$ is $c(n)$, since $\gcd(n,1)=1$. Comparing with the coefficient in $\lambda(n)f$ gives
$$
\boxed{c(n)=c(1)\lambda(n).}
$$
The constant coefficient comparison gives $\lambda(n)c(0)=\sigma_{w-1}(n)c(0)$, so
$$
\boxed{c(0)\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda(n)=\sigma_{w-1}(n).}
$$

For completeness derive the operator multiplication law underlying the last relation. On formal <Fourier series> define
$$
U_p\left(\sum c(r)q^r\right)=\sum c(pr)q^r,\qquad
V_p f(q)=f(q^p),\qquad A=p^{w-1}.
$$
These auxiliary maps need not separately preserve modularity, but coefficient identities are valid and $U_pV_p=I$. Part (a) gives
$$
T_w(p^e)=\sum_{j=0}^e A^jV_p^jU_p^{e-j}.
$$
Multiplication on the left by $U_p+AV_p$ yields the $T_w(p^{e+1})$ sum and, from $U_pV_p^j=V_p^{j-1}$ for $j\geq1$, the remaining sum $A T_w(p^{e-1})$. Hence
$$
T_w(p)T_w(p^e)=T_w(p^{e+1})+A T_w(p^{e-1})\quad(e\geq1).
$$
Induction on the smaller exponent, applying this recurrence and cancelling its two overlapping sums, now gives
$$
T_w(p^r)T_w(p^s)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(r,s)}A^jT_w(p^{r+s-2j}).
$$
To make the induction explicit, write $T_r=T_w(p^r)$ and assume $2\leq r\leq s$. Substituting the formulas already established for $r-1$ and $r-2$ into $T_rT_s=(T_1T_{r-1}-AT_{r-2})T_s$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
T_rT_s&=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}A^jT_{r+s-2j}
+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}A^{j+1}T_{r+s-2-2j}
-\sum_{j=0}^{r-2}A^{j+1}T_{r+s-2-2j}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}A^jT_{r+s-2j}+A^rT_{s-r}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last term is exactly the missing $j=r$ term. The cases $r=0,1$ are the identity operator and the prime recurrence above.

For distinct primes, their $U$ and $V$ maps commute, and the <divisor> formula in (a) factors independently at each prime. It follows that $T_w(m)T_w(n)=T_w(mn)$ for coprime $m,n$. Multiplying the prime-power identities therefore gives the general <Hecke multiplication relations>
$$
T_w(m)T_w(n)=\sum_{a\mid\gcd(m,n)}a^{w-1}T_w(mn/a^2).
$$
Apply this identity to the same nonzero eigenform and cancel the <vector> $f$, obtaining
$$
\boxed{\lambda(m)\lambda(n)=\sum_{a\mid\gcd(m,n)}a^{w-1}\lambda(mn/a^2).}
$$
This reasoning does not silently divide by $c(1)$, which is important when considering noncuspidal weight-zero forms.

For a nonzero <Hecke eigenform> that is a <cusp form>, $c(1)\ne0$: otherwise part (b) would make all its positive <Fourier coefficients> zero, and cuspidality already gives $c(0)=0$, forcing $f=0$. $$
\sum_{r\geq0}\lambda(p^r)X^r
=\frac1{1-\lambda(p)X+p^{w-1}X^2}.

$$
\boxed{L(f,s)=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{c(n)}{n^s}
=c(1)\prod_p\left(1-\lambda(p)p^{-s}+p^{w-1-2s}\right)^{-1}.}

Pass to the effective subgroup $\overline\Gamma\subset PSL_2(\mathbb Z)$. Its index is $\mu$: adjoining $-I$ halves the original index precisely when $-I\notin\Gamma$. The natural map of compactified modular curves
$$
\pi:X(\Gamma)\longrightarrow X(1)\cong\mathbb P^1
$$
has degree $\mu$, since a generic <orbit> has that many lifts. It branches only over the order-two elliptic <orbit>, the order-three elliptic <orbit>, and the <modular cusp>. Away from these, the modular quotient charts are genuine unramified covering charts.

Over $i$, a lift with its own effective <stabilizer> of order two is unramified; there are $\nu_2$ such lifts. Every other lift has <ramification index> two. As the sum of all ramification indices in that fibre is $\mu$, their number is $(\mu-\nu_2)/2$ and their contribution to the ramification <divisor> is $(\mu-\nu_2)/2$. Similarly, over $\rho$ there are $\nu_3$ unramified elliptic lifts and $(\mu-\nu_3)/3$ lifts of <ramification index> three, contributing $2(\mu-\nu_3)/3$.

At a <modular cusp> of width $h$, the local parameters are $q_h=e^{2\pi i\tau/h}$ upstairs and $q=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ downstairs. Thus $q=q_h^h$ and the <ramification index> is $h$. Summing the widths gives $\mu$, so all $\nu_\infty$ <modular cusps> contribute $\mu-\nu_\infty$ to ramification. The <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> now gives
$$
2g-2=-2\mu+\frac{\mu-\nu_2}{2}
+\frac{2(\mu-\nu_3)}{3}+\mu-\nu_\infty
=\frac{\mu}{6}-\frac{\nu_2}{2}-\frac{2\nu_3}{3}-\nu_\infty.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{g=1+\frac{\mu}{12}-\frac{\nu_2}{4}-\frac{\nu_3}{3}-\frac{\nu_\infty}{2}.}
$$
The effective index and the projective <modular cusp> widths ensure that the formula also applies when the original subgroup does not contain $-I$; no spurious factor of two is introduced.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Reduction modulo $N$ has <kernel> $\Gamma(N)$ and is onto $SL_2(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)$. To see <surjectivity>, use the <Chinese remainder theorem> to reduce to $\mathbb Z/p^a\mathbb Z$. In that local ring a unimodular column has a unit entry. Elementary row operations move a unit to the upper position and clear the lower one; diagonal <matrices> $\operatorname{diag}(u,u^{-1})$ are products of elementary <matrices> as well. Explicitly, with $E_{12},E_{21}$ denoting elementary shears,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&u\\-u^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}
=E_{12}(u)E_{21}(-u^{-1})E_{12}(u),
$$
and multiplying this by the same <matrix> with $u=-1$ gives $\operatorname{diag}(u,u^{-1})$. Thus elementary <matrices> generate the special linear group over each local ring. Chinese remaindering their parameters gives generation over the product ring, and every such parameter lifts to an integer. This proves the reduction is onto.

Over $\mathbb F_p$, there are $p^2-1$ choices for a nonzero first column and $p$ choices for a second column with <determinant> one, so $|SL_2(\mathbb F_p)|=p(p^2-1)$. Each reduction from modulus $p^{a+1}$ to $p^a$ has <kernel> represented by $I+p^aB$, with $B$ modulo $p$ and $\operatorname{tr}B=0$, of size $p^3$. Hence, for $N>1$,
$$
[SL_2(\mathbb Z):\Gamma(N)]
=|SL_2(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)|
=N^3\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2}).
$$
For $N=1$ the index is one. Since $-I\in\Gamma(N)$ exactly when $N=1,2$,
$$
\mu=\begin{cases}1&N=1,\\6&N=2,\\
\frac{N^3}{2}\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2})&N\geq3.
\end{cases}
$$

For $N\geq2$ there are no nontrivial effective elliptic stabilizers. Indeed $A=I+NB\in\Gamma(N)$ with <determinant> one has $\operatorname{tr}B=-N\det B$, and therefore
$$
\operatorname{tr}A=2-N^2\det B\equiv2\pmod{N^2}.
$$
A noncentral elliptic element of $SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ has <trace> $0$ or $\pm1$, none congruent to $2$ modulo $N^2$ for $N\geq2$. The central element $-I$ at level two acts trivially and is not an <elliptic point> of the effective quotient. Thus $\nu_2=\nu_3=0$ for $N\geq2$, while at level one both numbers are one.

The subgroup $\Gamma(N)$ is normal, so all its <modular cusps> have the same effective width as infinity. That width is $N$: the shear $T^h$ belongs to $\pm\Gamma(N)$ exactly when $N\mid h$; for $N\geq3$ its diagonal entries exclude the minus sign, and for $N=1,2$ that sign changes nothing. Since <modular cusp> widths sum to $\mu$,
$$
\nu_\infty=\frac{\mu}{N}
=\begin{cases}1&N=1,\\3&N=2,\\
\frac{N^2}{2}\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2})&N\geq3.
\end{cases}
$$
For $N\geq3$ the resulting <principal congruence modular curve genus> is
$$
\boxed{g(X(N))=1+\frac{N^2(N-6)}{24}\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2}).}
$$
For clarity, the small levels, including all exceptional terms, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
N &[SL_2:\Gamma(N)]&\mu&\nu_2&\nu_3&\nu_\infty&g\\\hline
1&1&1&1&1&1&0\\
2&6&6&0&0&3&0\\
3&24&12&0&0&4&0\\
4&48&24&0&0&6&0\\
5&120&60&0&0&12&0\\
6&144&72&0&0&12&1
\end{array}
$$
For $N>6$, every factor in the <genus> correction is positive, so $g>1$. Therefore **the genus-zero levels are exactly $N=1,2,3,4,5$**.

For an integer weight $k$, a <cusp form> for a <congruence subgroup> $\Gamma$ is a <holomorphic function> on $\mathbb H$ with $f|_k\gamma=f$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$, whose <Fourier expansion of a modular form> vanishes at every <modular cusp>. More explicitly, choose $\sigma\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ representing a <modular cusp> $\sigma\infty$, and a genuine translation period $h>0$ with $\sigma T^h\sigma^{-1}\in\Gamma$. Then the requirement is
$$
(f|_k\sigma)(\tau)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_{\sigma,n}e^{2\pi in\tau/h},
$$
convergent for sufficiently large imaginary part. Thus the slash translate extends holomorphically at $q_h=0$ and has value zero. The <vector> space of these functions is $S_k(\Gamma)$.

A genuine period is convenient when $-I$ is absent and the weight is odd: the smaller effective <cusp width> may supply a negative rather than positive translation in $\Gamma$, but an actual period always exists, possibly twice that width. Using an actual period avoids imposing an incorrect periodicity. The zero-value condition is independent of the chosen representative or period. If $-I\in\Gamma$ and $k$ is odd, its slash relation forces $f=(-1)^kf=-f$, so the space is zero.

Write $\tau=x+iy$ and $\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Since $ad-bc=1$,
$$
\operatorname{Im}(\sigma\tau)=\frac{y}{|c\tau+d|^2}.
$$
Consequently, for the <invariant norm of a modular form>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(\sigma\tau)
&=|f(\sigma\tau)|\left(\frac{y}{|c\tau+d|^2}\right)^{k/2}\\
&=|(c\tau+d)^{-k}f(\sigma\tau)|y^{k/2}
=|f|_k\sigma(\tau)|y^{k/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This computation applies to an arbitrary function; modularity is needed only to deduce $\Gamma$-invariance of $\phi$. The exponent is **$k/2$**, as printed in the PDF, rather than the converted TeX's $1/2$.

= Solution

For positive integer $k$, the equivalence is the <bounded invariant norm characterization of cusp forms>. The three conditions below work together: meromorphicity and the bound remove interior poles; the transformation law produces a periodic local function at every <modular cusp>; the same bound makes that function tend to zero at the <modular cusp>. Conversely a <cusp form> has exponentially decaying <Fourier expansions of a modular form>, and finitely many compact core regions control its invariant <norm> everywhere. These implications are proved under the corresponding labelled items.

**Without a positive-weight convention, weight zero is a genuine exception.** The function $f=1$ satisfies all three printed conditions at $k=0$ for every <congruence subgroup>, but is not a <cusp form>. Indeed a weight-zero <cusp form> would descend to a <holomorphic function> on the compact curve $X(\Gamma)$ and hence be constant, and vanishing at a <modular cusp> would make it zero. Thus the literal unrestricted claim needs the qualification $k>0$.

If negative integer weights are included, both sides contain only the zero function. To check the bounded side, each <modular cusp> translate has at most power growth in $y$, hence at most a power of $\log(1/|q_h|)$. For a negative <Laurent coefficient> of order $m$, Cauchy's coefficient estimate bounds its absolute value by $C r^m(\log(1/r))^{|k|/2}\to0$, so there is no negative coefficient and every <modular cusp> is <holomorphic>. If such a nonzero negative-weight form existed, the product of its slash translates over $\Gamma\backslash SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ would be a nonzero <holomorphic> level-one <modular form> of negative weight. Right multiplication permutes the translates, and all <modular cusp> factors are bounded, so that product really is a level-one form. Its existence contradicts the valence formula. This also excludes negative-weight <modular cusp> forms. The nonzero counterexample is specifically weight zero.

A <cusp form> is <holomorphic> in the upper half-plane by definition, so it is <meromorphic> there. In the reverse direction suppose $f$ is <meromorphic> and
$$
|f(\tau)|y^{k/2}\leq B\qquad(\tau\in\mathbb H).
$$
On a small closed disk around an interior point, $y$ is bounded above and away from zero. Hence $|f|$ is bounded there by a fixed constant. A <pole> of positive order would make $|f|$ unbounded along points approaching its center, a contradiction. Every possible <pole> is therefore removable, and **$f$ is <holomorphic> on $\mathbb H$**. Meromorphicity alone would not establish this conclusion; the bounded invariant <norm> is being used here.

The transformation condition is necessary by the definition of a <cusp form>. For sufficiency, fix $\sigma\in SL_2(\mathbb Z)$ representing any <modular cusp>, and set $g=f|_k\sigma$. By item (i), $g$ is <holomorphic> in the upper half-plane. Choose an actual period $h$ with $\sigma T^h\sigma^{-1}=\gamma\in\Gamma$. Associativity of slash operations and the given transformation law give
$$
g|_kT^h=f|_k\sigma T^h=f|_k\gamma\sigma=(f|_k\gamma)|_k\sigma=g.
$$
Thus $g(\tau+h)=g(\tau)$ and $g$ is a single-valued <holomorphic function> on a punctured disk with coordinate $q_h=e^{2\pi i\tau/h}$. The calculation in part (b) transports the original uniform bound to this translate:
$$
|g(\tau)|y^{k/2}=\phi(\sigma\tau)\leq B.
$$
This puts every <modular cusp> in exactly the situation needed in item (iii). No assumption that a single <modular cusp> represents all of $\Gamma$'s <modular cusps> is made.

= Solution

First suppose $f$ is a <cusp form>. Choose finitely many representatives $\sigma_j$ for $\Gamma\backslash SL_2(\mathbb Z)$; their translates of the standard modular region cover a fundamental region for $\Gamma$. On the compact truncated part of each translate, the <continuous function> $|f(\tau)|y^{k/2}$ is bounded. On its tail, the corresponding <modular cusp> expansion of $g_j=f|_k\sigma_j$ has zero constant term, so $g_j=O(e^{-2\pi y/h_j})$ uniformly in the real part. Therefore $y^{k/2}|g_j|$ is bounded there and tends to zero. Part (b) identifies this expression with $\phi(\sigma_j\tau)$. There are only finitely many representatives; taking the maximum of these bounds and using $\Gamma$-invariance proves that **$\phi$ is bounded on all of $\mathbb H$**.

Conversely assume all three conditions and $k>0$. Items (i) and (ii) show that at each <modular cusp> a <holomorphic> periodic translate $g$ satisfies $|g(\tau)|\leq B y^{-k/2}$. In its punctured-disk coordinate,
$$
y=\frac{h}{2\pi}\log\frac1{|q_h|},\qquad
|g(q_h)|\leq B\left(\frac{h}{2\pi}\log\frac1{|q_h|}\right)^{-k/2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <Riemann removable singularity theorem> extends $g$ holomorphically to zero with $g(0)=0$. Equivalently its Laurent expansion has no negative powers and no constant term. Since this holds at every <modular cusp>, $f$ is a <cusp form>. Hence the three conditions are sufficient as well as necessary in positive weight, with the weight-zero exception explained in the root solution.
