Use unital <rings>, and distinguish left and right <Noetherianity>. A useful form of the <noncommutative Hilbert basis theorem> is the following: if a <ring> $B$ is generated by a subring $A$ and an element $x$, if $A+xA=A+Ax$, and if $A$ is a <right Noetherian ring>, then $B$ is a <right Noetherian ring>. The corresponding left-handed statement holds as well. In particular, a central <polynomial ring> over a <Noetherian ring> is <Noetherian>, and a <skew polynomial ring of an automorphism> preserves either side's <Noetherianity>.

Here is a proof of the general right-handed statement. Put $F_{-1}=0$ and
$$
F_n=A+Ax+\cdots+Ax^n=A+xA+\cdots+x^nA.
$$
The equality follows inductively from $A+xA=A+Ax$. These groups form an exhaustive <ascending filtration>, stable under multiplication by $A$ on either side, with $F_jF_k\subseteq F_{j+k}$. For any $b\in A$ and $n\geq0$, moving coefficients one step at a time also gives
$$
bx^n=x^nc+v\qquad(c\in A,\ v\in F_{n-1}).
$$
Fix a <right ideal> $I$ of $B$ and define coefficient sets
$$
L_n=\{a\in A:ax^n\in I+F_{n-1}\}.
$$
Each $L_n$ is a <right ideal> of $A$. Additive closure is immediate. If $a\in L_n$ and $b\in A$, use the preceding transport identity to obtain
$$
abx^n=(ax^n)c+av\in I+F_{n-1}.
$$
Here $(ax^n)c$ has this membership because $I$ is a <right ideal> and $F_{n-1}$ is stable under right multiplication by $A$. Also $L_n\subseteq L_{n+1}$, by multiplying $ax^n\in I+F_{n-1}$ on the right by $x$. The <ascending chain condition> in $A$ yields $N$ with $L_n=L_N$ for $n\geq N$.

For each $0\leq k\leq N$, choose finite right generators $a_{kj}$ of $L_k$ and lifts $f_{kj}\in I$ with $f_{kj}-a_{kj}x^k\in F_{k-1}$. These finitely many lifts generate $I$ as a <right ideal> of $B$. To prove this, let $f\in I\cap F_d$ and write $f=ax^d+v$, $v\in F_{d-1}$. Then $a\in L_d$. Put $k=\min(d,N)$ and write $a=\sum_ja_{kj}b_j$. Transport each coefficient as $b_jx^d=x^dc_j+v_j$ with $v_j\in F_{d-1}$. Consequently
$$
f-\sum_j f_{kj}x^{d-k}c_j\in I\cap F_{d-1}.
$$
Induction on $d$, with $F_{-1}=0$, expresses $f$ in the right ideal generated by the lifts. Every <right ideal> is therefore finitely generated, proving the theorem. Passing to the <opposite ring> proves the left-handed version. This proof does not require unique coefficient normal forms.

A <poly-(cyclic or finite) group> has a finite <subnormal series>
$$
1=G_0\triangleleft G_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_r=G
$$
whose factors $G_i/G_{i-1}$ are cyclic or finite. Normality is required between consecutive terms. We show inductively that each <group ring> $\mathbb Z[G_i]$ is both a <left Noetherian ring> and a <right Noetherian ring>.

The starting <ring> $\mathbb Z$ is <Noetherian>, since every <ideal> is principal. The other module fact we use is that a <finitely generated module> over a <Noetherian ring> on the relevant side is a <Noetherian module>: it is a quotient of a finite <direct sum> of regular modules, and the <ascending chain condition> passes to finite sums and quotients. For a finite sum this follows by induction, comparing the intersections of a chain with one summand and its images in the quotient by that summand.

If $H\triangleleft K$ has finite index, choosing coset representatives shows that $\mathbb Z[K]$ is finite free as a left and as a right $\mathbb Z[H]$-<module>. By <finite-module extension preserves Noetherianity>, every one-sided <ideal> of $\mathbb Z[K]$ is finitely generated already as a $\mathbb Z[H]$-<module>, and those same generators generate it as a $\mathbb Z[K]$-ideal. Thus either chain condition passes through a finite factor.

If $K/H$ is infinite cyclic, choose $t\in K$ lifting its generator. Every element of $K$ has a unique form $ht^n$, and conjugation by $t$ gives an <automorphism> $\sigma$ of $\mathbb Z[H]$. Hence
$$
\mathbb Z[K]\cong\mathbb Z[H][t,t^{-1};\sigma],\qquad ta=\sigma(a)t.
$$
For the polynomial subring $C=\mathbb Z[H][t;\sigma]$, the condition $A+tA=A+At$ follows from invertibility of $\sigma$, so the proved <noncommutative Hilbert basis theorem> makes $C$ <Noetherian> on both sides. A <right ideal> $J$ of the <skew Laurent polynomial ring> is generated by $J\cap C$: for any $f\in J$, choose $m$ large enough that $ft^m\in C$, and recover $f=(ft^m)t^{-m}$. Finite generators of $J\cap C$ over $C$ therefore generate $J$ over the Laurent ring. Left multiplication by a large power of $t$ proves the left-handed statement. These are the Laurent-extension facts used in the induction.

Every factor of the <subnormal series> is covered by one of these two cases. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[G]\text{ is left and right Noetherian}.}
$$
The argument proves the more general result that <group rings of poly-(cyclic or finite) groups are Noetherian> over any coefficient <ring> satisfying both chain conditions.

For a <commutative ring> $R$ and a <multiplicative subset> $S$ containing $1$, the <universal property of localization> specifies a pair $(R_S,\iota)$ with $\iota:R\to R_S$ a unital <ring homomorphism>, such that every $\iota(s)$, $s\in S$, is a <unit>, and any unital <ring homomorphism> $f:R\to T$ into a commutative <ring> that sends $S$ to <units> factors uniquely through $\iota$. Thus there is a unique $\widetilde f:R_S\to T$ satisfying $\widetilde f\iota=f$. The property determines the pair up to a unique compatible <isomorphism>.

For existence, take pairs $(r,s)\in R\times S$ and declare
$$
(r,s)\sim(r',s')\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u(s'r-sr')=0\text{ for some }u\in S.
$$
The extra multiplier is essential when $R$ has <zero divisors>. This is an <equivalence relation>: for transitivity, multiply the two cross-multiplication equations by appropriate denominators and add them, using the product of their annihilating multipliers. Write the class as $r/s$ and define
$$
\frac rs+\frac{r'}{s'}=\frac{rs'+r's}{ss'},\qquad\frac rs\frac{r'}{s'}=\frac{rr'}{ss'},\qquad\iota(r)=\frac r1.
$$
Multiplying the defining equivalence equations verifies that these operations are well defined. They give a <commutative ring>, and $s/1$ has inverse $1/s$. The required map is necessarily
$$
\widetilde f(r/s)=f(r)f(s)^{-1}.
$$
It is well defined because applying $f$ to $u(s'r-sr')=0$ and cancelling the units $f(u),f(s),f(s')$ equates the two images. Its addition and multiplication laws follow directly from the fraction formulas. This establishes existence and the <universal property of localization>. If $0\in S$, this construction gives the zero <ring>, which is allowed as a localization.

For a <prime ideal> $P$, its complement $S=R\setminus P$ is multiplicatively closed, and <localization at a prime ideal> means $R_P=S^{-1}R$. The map
$$
R_P\longrightarrow\operatorname{Frac}(R/P),\qquad r/s\longmapsto\overline r/\overline s
$$
is well defined and surjective, with <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $PR_P$: a numerator has zero image exactly when it belongs to $P$. Since $R/P$ is an <integral domain>, its <field of fractions> is a nonzero <field>, so $PR_P$ is a proper <maximal ideal>. Every fraction $r/s$ with $r\notin P$ is a <unit>, with inverse $s/r$. Every fraction with $r\in P$ belongs to this proper ideal and cannot be a <unit>. Thus the nonunits are exactly $PR_P$, and every proper ideal lies within it. Consequently
$$
\boxed{R_P\text{ is a local ring with unique maximal ideal }PR_P.}
$$
Now suppose each $R_P$ is <reduced>. If $x\in R$ is <nilpotent>, its image $x/1$ is <nilpotent> in every $R_P$, hence is zero. If $x\ne0$, its <annihilator> $\operatorname{Ann}_R(x)$ is a proper ideal, so choose a <maximal ideal> $P$ containing it. Maximal ideals in a <commutative ring> are <prime ideals>. The <vanishing criterion in a module localization> would give $sx=0$ for some $s\notin P$. But then $s\in\operatorname{Ann}_R(x)\subseteq P$, a contradiction. Hence **$R$ is reduced**. This is <reducedness is detected by prime localizations>; no finiteness hypothesis is needed.

The stronger local-domain condition does not force $R$ to be an <integral domain>. Take $R=k\times k$ for a <field> $k$. Its only <prime ideals> are $P_1=0\times k$ and $P_2=k\times0$: a <prime ideal> must contain one of the orthogonal <idempotents> $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, and the remaining quotient is the <field> $k$. At $P_1$, inverting $(1,0)$ kills the second component, so $R_{P_1}\cong k$; similarly $R_{P_2}\cong k$. Both localizations are <integral domains>, but
$$
(1,0)(0,1)=0
$$
is a product of nonzero elements in $R$. Therefore **the answer is no**, as illustrated by <domain prime localizations do not imply a domain>.

Assume $R$ is a nonzero unital <ring> and all its <modules> are unital. Order the proper <left ideals> by inclusion. A chain has an upper bound given by its union, which is still a proper <left ideal>: if it contained $1$, one member of the chain would contain $1$ and be all of $R$. The family is nonempty because it contains $0$. The <Zorn lemma> gives a maximal proper <left ideal> $L$. The nonzero quotient $R/L$ has no proper nonzero <submodules>, because its <submodules> correspond to <left ideals> between $L$ and $R$. Thus **$R/L$ is a simple left module**. The nonzero-ring qualification is necessary: the zero <ring> has no nonzero unital <simple module>.

Define the <Jacobson radical> $J(R)$ as the intersection of all maximal <left ideals>. It is equivalently the intersection of the ideals $\operatorname{Ann}_R(S)$ over all <simple modules>, where $\operatorname{Ann}$ is the <annihilator of a module>. To verify this equivalence, for a nonzero vector $v$ in a <simple module> $S$, the surjection $R\to S$, $r\mapsto rv$, has a maximal left-ideal kernel. Thus $x\in J(R)$ implies $xv=0$ for every $v$ in every <simple module>. Conversely, if $x$ annihilates all <simple modules>, it annihilates $1+L$ in every $R/L$, so $x\in L$. An <annihilator of a module> is a two-sided <ideal>; this description therefore proves that $J(R)$ is a two-sided <ideal>.

For the forward implication of the <unit criterion for the Jacobson radical>, first let $y\in J(R)$. If $R(1-y)$ were a proper <left ideal>, it would lie in a maximal one $L$. Both $y$ and $1-y$ would belong to $L$, an impossibility. Hence $c(1-y)=1$ for some $c\in R$. A left inverse alone does not yet establish that $1-y$ is a <unit> in a general noncommutative <ring>. However, $c=1+cy$, with $cy\in J(R)$, so the same argument gives $d c=1$ for some $d$. Multiplying $c(1-y)=1$ by $d$ on the left gives $d=1-y$. Therefore $(1-y)c=dc=1$ as well. This proves that $1-y$ is a <unit>.

For $x\in J(R)$, its two-sided-ideal property gives $axb\in J(R)$ for all $a,b$, so $1-axb$ is a <unit>. Conversely, if $x\notin J(R)$, choose a maximal <left ideal> $L$ with $x\notin L$. Then $L+Rx=R$, so $l+ax=1$ for some $l\in L$, $a\in R$. Hence $1-ax=l\in L$ is not a <unit>: if it were invertible, multiplying it on the left by its inverse would put $1$ in $L$. Taking $b=1$ contradicts the given unit property. We have proved
$$
\boxed{J(R)=\{x\in R:1-axb\text{ is a unit for every }a,b\in R\}.}
$$
Finally let $R$ be <commutative> and $I$ an <ideal>. The set $S=1+I$ contains $1$, and
$$
(1+i)(1+j)=1+(i+j+ij)\in1+I,
$$
so it is a <multiplicative subset>. In the <localization of a module> $I_S\subseteq R_S$, any element is $i/s$ with $i\in I$ and $s\in S$. For any $r/t\in R_S$,
$$
1-\frac rt\frac is=\frac{ts-ri}{ts}.
$$
Both $ts$ and $ts-ri$ lie in $S$, since $ts\in1+I$ and $ri\in I$. The fraction is consequently a <unit> in $R_S$. In a <commutative ring>, the product of any two coefficients is again a single coefficient, so the proved <unit criterion for the Jacobson radical> applies to all expressions $1-a(i/s)b$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I_S\subseteq J(R_S).}
$$
This proves <localization away from one plus an ideal lies in the Jacobson radical>. If $I=R$, then $0\in S$, $R_S=0$, and the inclusion holds trivially.

The ring form of the <Artin–Wedderburn theorem> states that the following conditions on a unital <ring> $R$ are equivalent: $R$ is a <semisimple ring>; $R$ satisfies the descending chain condition on <left ideals> and $J(R)=0$; and
$$
\boxed{R\cong\prod_{i=1}^s M_{n_i}(D_i),}
$$
where $s$ is finite, each $n_i\geq1$, and each $D_i$ is a <division ring>. The factors are unique up to permutation and isomorphism. The zero <ring> may be included as the empty product. This theorem also implies the corresponding right-handed statements.

First prove the implication from the chain condition and zero <Jacobson radical>. Among finite intersections of maximal <left ideals>, choose a minimal one, say $L=L_1\cap\cdots\cap L_t$. Such a minimal intersection exists by the descending chain condition. Intersecting with any other maximal <left ideal> cannot make $L$ smaller, so $L$ is contained in every maximal <left ideal>. Thus $L\subseteq J(R)=0$. It follows that the map of left <modules>
$$
R\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^t R/L_j
$$
is injective. Each quotient is a <simple module>.

For completeness, a <submodule> $N$ of a finite <direct sum> $V=\bigoplus_j T_j$ of <simple modules> is itself a finite <direct sum> of <simple modules> and is a <direct summand> of $V$. Choose a sum $W$ of coordinate summands, maximal among those with $N\cap W=0$. For any omitted coordinate summand $T_j$, simplicity gives $T_j\cap(N+W)=0$ or $T_j\subseteq N+W$. In the first case $N\cap(W\oplus T_j)=0$, contradicting maximality; hence every coordinate summand lies in $N+W$. Therefore $V=N\oplus W$. Projection onto $N$ shows $N$ is a finite sum of images of the $T_j$, each image zero or simple. Add these images successively, discarding any image already in the sum: simplicity makes each new intersection zero. This yields a direct-sum decomposition. Applying this argument to the displayed injection proves that the left regular <module> is a finite sum of <simple modules>.

Conversely, if the regular <module> is a <direct sum> of <simple modules>, that sum is finite because $1$ lies in a finite subsum and generates the whole regular <module>. Every <left ideal>, being a <submodule>, has the preceding decomposition and splitting property. Its chain lengths are bounded by the number of simple summands, so the descending chain condition holds. The <Jacobson radical> annihilates every simple summand of the regular <module>, hence annihilates $1$ and is zero. This identifies the first two conditions.

Write the finite decomposition using pairwise nonisomorphic simple left <modules>:
$$
{}_RR\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^s S_i^{n_i}.
$$
Every <simple module> is a quotient of $R$, by sending $1$ to any nonzero vector, so this list includes every simple isomorphism class. The <Schur lemma> gives $\operatorname{Hom}_R(S_i,S_j)=0$ for $i\ne j$, and $E_i=\operatorname{End}_R(S_i)$ is a <division ring>: any nonzero endomorphism has zero kernel and full image by simplicity. Consequently
$$
\operatorname{End}_R({}_RR)\cong\prod_{i=1}^sM_{n_i}(E_i).
$$
Every endomorphism of the left regular <module> is right multiplication by its value on $1$. Composition reverses multiplication of those values, so the left side is $R^{\mathrm{op}}$. Taking the <opposite ring>, and using transpose to identify $M_n(E)^{\mathrm{op}}$ with $M_n(E^{\mathrm{op}})$, gives the theorem's decomposition with $D_i=E_i^{\mathrm{op}}$. This keeps the left/right conventions explicit.

Conversely, the standard column <module> $D^n$ over $M_n(D)$ is simple: a nonzero coordinate of a vector can be inverted to construct a <matrix> sending it to any prescribed vector. The left regular <module> decomposes as the sum of its $n$ column ideals, each isomorphic to $D^n$. A finite product of these matrix <rings> is therefore a <semisimple ring>. This proves all implications. For uniqueness, the isomorphism classes of simple <modules> and their multiplicities in the regular <module> determine the numbers $n_i$ and the division <rings> $E_i^{\mathrm{op}}$. The multiplicity can also be recovered as the <dimension> of $\operatorname{Hom}_R(S_i,R)$ as a right $E_i$-<module>, so it does not depend on the chosen decomposition.

Now let $A=\mathbb C[G]$ for the finite <group> $G$. It has <dimension> $|G|$, with the elements of $G$ as a <basis>, so its <left ideals> satisfy the descending chain condition by their dimensions. The permitted assumption $J(A)=0$ makes the <Artin–Wedderburn theorem> applicable. Its decomposition is a complex-algebra decomposition
$$
A\cong\prod_iM_{n_i}(D_i),
$$
with each $D_i$ finite-dimensional over the central copy of $\mathbb C$. Each such <division algebra> is just $\mathbb C$. Indeed, for $d\in D_i$, finite dimensionality gives a nonzero polynomial relation $p(d)=0$ with complex coefficients. Factor $p$ into linear factors over $\mathbb C$. Their product at $d$ is zero, so the absence of <zero divisors> forces $d-\lambda=0$ for some scalar root $\lambda$. Thus every element is scalar.

The pairwise nonisomorphic simple <modules> of $\prod_iM_{n_i}(\mathbb C)$ are the column <modules> $\mathbb C^{n_i}$, with all other factors acting as zero. Hence $\dim_{\mathbb C}S_i=n_i$. Taking complex <dimensions> gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^k(\dim_{\mathbb C}S_i)^2=\sum_{i=1}^kn_i^2=\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathbb C[G]=|G|.}
$$

An <almost commutative algebra> over $\mathbb C$ has an exhaustive nonnegative increasing multiplicative <ascending filtration>
$$
0=F_{-1}R\subseteq F_0R\subseteq F_1R\subseteq\cdots,\qquad 1\in F_0R,\qquad F_iR\,F_jR\subseteq F_{i+j}R,
$$
for which the <associated graded ring>
$$
\operatorname{gr}R=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}F_nR/F_{n-1}R
$$
is a commutative finitely generated complex <algebra>. This includes the customary degree filtration with $F_0R=\mathbb C$, finitely many degree-one generators, and commutators lowering degree. The finite-generation clause is part of the convention used here and is necessary for the conclusion.

A finitely generated commutative complex <algebra> is a quotient of a <polynomial ring> $\mathbb C[X_1,\ldots,X_d]$. Repeated application of the central-variable case of the <Hilbert basis theorem> makes that polynomial <ring> <Noetherian>; quotients preserve the <ascending chain condition> by lifting ideal chains. Thus $\operatorname{gr}R$ is <Noetherian>.

We prove <ascending filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity> directly. Give a <left ideal> $I\subseteq R$ the induced <ascending filtration> $I\cap F_nR$. Its <associated graded module> embeds into $\operatorname{gr}R$ as a homogeneous <ideal>, denoted $\operatorname{gr}I$. Choose finitely many homogeneous generators $\overline u_1,\ldots,\overline u_m$ of this ideal, of degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_m$, and lifts $u_j\in I\cap F_{d_j}R$. Homogeneous generators may be chosen by taking homogeneous components of any finite generating set.

For $u\in I\cap F_nR$, its degree-$n$ symbol can be expressed as
$$
\overline u=\sum_j\overline a_j\overline u_j,\qquad\deg\overline a_j=n-d_j,
$$
with terms of negative coefficient degree omitted. Lift each coefficient to $a_j\in F_{n-d_j}R$. Then
$$
u-\sum_ja_ju_j\in I\cap F_{n-1}R.
$$
Induction on $n$, ending at $F_{-1}R=0$, expresses $u$ in the <left ideal> generated by the $u_j$. Hence every <left ideal> is finitely generated. Applying the same argument to <right ideals>, or to the <opposite ring>, proves
$$
\boxed{R\text{ is left and right Noetherian}.}
$$
This degree-lowering proof terminates in finitely many steps and requires no completeness assumption on the <filtered ring>. Without finite generation of the graded algebra, a counterexample would be the commutative <polynomial ring> in infinitely many variables, whose ideals $(X_1)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2)\subsetneq\cdots$ do not stabilize.

For the <universal enveloping algebra> $U(\mathfrak g)$, use the degree <ascending filtration> inherited from the <tensor algebra> of the finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> $\mathfrak g$. For $x,y\in\mathfrak g$, the defining relation is $xy-yx=[x,y]\in F_1U(\mathfrak g)$, whereas the product $xy$ has filtration degree at most $2$. Thus the degree-one symbols commute in $\operatorname{gr}U(\mathfrak g)$. Those symbols generate the graded algebra, so it is a quotient of
$$
\operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak g)\cong\mathbb C[X_1,\ldots,X_d],\qquad d=\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathfrak g.
$$
This already proves the required almost-commutative property. The <Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem> gives the stronger identification $\operatorname{gr}U(\mathfrak g)\cong\operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak g)$, but injectivity of this map is not needed for the chain-condition argument. Therefore
$$
\boxed{U(\mathfrak g)\text{ is left and right Noetherian}.}
$$

Since $M$ is a <finitely generated module>, choose a surjection from a finite <free module>, $F_0\twoheadrightarrow M$. A finite <free module> over a <left Noetherian ring> is a <Noetherian module>, so the kernel $K_1$ is finitely generated. Choose a finite <free module> $F_1$ surjecting onto $K_1$, then compose its map with $K_1\hookrightarrow F_0$. Its kernel is again finitely generated. Repeating constructs an exact sequence
$$
\boxed{\cdots\longrightarrow F_2\longrightarrow F_1\longrightarrow F_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0,}
$$
with all $F_i$ finite <free modules>, hence <finitely generated projective modules>. Thus the requested <projective resolution> exists, without asserting that it has finite length.

Assume now that $M\ne0$ and $\operatorname{pd}_RM=n<\infty$. We may arrange a length-$n$ <projective resolution> with all terms finite projective. Here is why the preceding construction gives one. Set $K_0=M$, with short exact sequences $0\to K_{j+1}\to F_j\to K_j\to0$. Applying the <Ext functor>, whose positive degrees vanish on a projective first argument, gives by repeated dimension shifting
$$
\operatorname{Ext}^1_R(K_n,N)\cong\operatorname{Ext}^{n+1}_R(M,N)=0
$$
for every left <module> $N$. The final equality follows from the existence of any length-$n$ <projective resolution>. Vanishing of these degree-one groups means that every short exact sequence ending in $K_n$ splits: its extension class is zero. In particular a free surjection onto $K_n$ splits, so $K_n$ is a <projective module>. It is finitely generated by the <Noetherian> kernel construction. Use $P_n=K_n$ and $P_j=F_j$ for $j<n$ to obtain
$$
0\longrightarrow P_n\xrightarrow{d_n}P_{n-1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow P_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0.
$$
For a left <finite projective module> $P$, put $P^*=\operatorname{Hom}_R(P,R)$, a right <module> under $(fr)(p)=f(p)r$. It is a right <finite projective module>, and its natural evaluation map
$$
P\longrightarrow P^{**}=\operatorname{Hom}_{R^{\mathrm{op}}}(P^*,R),\qquad p\longmapsto(f\longmapsto f(p)),
$$
is an <isomorphism>. These claims hold for a finite <free module> by coordinates, and pass to <direct summands>, proving them for every <finite projective module> without any right-Noetherian hypothesis.

Suppose $n\geq1$ and $\operatorname{Ext}^n_R(M,R)=0$. Computing the <Ext functor> from this resolution shows that its last dual map
$$
d_n^*:P_{n-1}^*\longrightarrow P_n^*
$$
is surjective. Since $P_n^*$ is projective as a right <module>, this map has a right-module section $h:P_n^*\to P_{n-1}^*$, with $d_n^*h=1$. Dualizing gives $h^*d_n^{**}=1$. Under the natural double-dual identifications, $d_n^{**}$ is $d_n$, so this gives a left-module retraction of $d_n$.

Therefore $P_{n-1}=d_n(P_n)\oplus Q$ for a <finite projective module> $Q$. The next differential has kernel $d_n(P_n)$, so restricting it to $Q$ gives an exact resolution with $Q$ replacing $P_{n-1}$ and the initial $P_n$ removed. This has length $n-1$, contradicting the definition of <projective dimension>. For $n=0$, the nonzero $M$ is a <finite projective module> and a summand of some $R^d$. A nonzero vector in that summand has a nonzero coordinate, so one coordinate projection restricts to a nonzero map $M\to R$. Thus the degree-zero <Hom functor> is nonzero as well. We have proved
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ext}^n_R(M,R)\ne0.}
$$
This is <top Ext detects finite projective dimension over a left Noetherian ring>. The nonzero-module convention matters when $n=0$: if one assigns projective dimension zero to the zero module, then $\operatorname{Hom}_R(0,R)=0$ is an exception. With the convention $\operatorname{pd}_R0=-\infty$, it is outside the asserted finite nonnegative case.

For an example of infinite <projective dimension>, take the <dual numbers> $R=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ over a <field> $k$, and $M=k=R/(\varepsilon)$. The <periodic resolution over dual numbers> is
$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}R\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}R\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}R\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow0.
$$
It is exact because both the kernel and image of multiplication by $\varepsilon$ are the ideal $(\varepsilon)$, and that is also the kernel of $R\to k$. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-,k)$ gives a copy of $k$ in every degree and zero differentials, since $\varepsilon$ acts as zero on $k$. Consequently
$$
\operatorname{Ext}^j_R(k,k)\cong k\ne0\qquad\text{for every }j\geq0.
$$
