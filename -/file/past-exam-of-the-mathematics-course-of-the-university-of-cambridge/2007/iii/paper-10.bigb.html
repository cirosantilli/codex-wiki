Use the complex <inner product> convention linear in its first argument. Give the <exterior algebra> its induced <inner product>, with an <orthonormal> wedge basis. Exterior multiplication is **$e(v)\eta=v\wedge\eta$**. Its adjoint is contraction:
$$
e(b)^*(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_r)
=\sum_{j=1}^r(-1)^{j-1}(v_j,b)\,v_1\wedge\cdots\widehat v_j\cdots\wedge v_r.
$$
Applying this formula to $a\wedge\eta$ separates its first term from the remaining terms, giving $e(b)^*e(a)\eta=(a,b)\eta-e(a)e(b)^*\eta$. Hence the <canonical anticommutation relations> are
$$
\boxed{e(a)e(b)^*+e(b)^*e(a)=(a,b)I.}
$$
For irreducibility, choose an <orthonormal basis> $u_1,\ldots,u_d$. The occupancy projections are $N_j=e(u_j)e(u_j)^*$, and $P_0=\prod_j(I-N_j)$ projects onto the vacuum $1\in\Lambda^0V$. For an increasing index set $J$, put $E_J=e(u_{j_1})\cdots e(u_{j_r})$. Then $E_IP_0E_J^*$ maps the wedge basis <vector> $u_J$ to $u_I$ and kills every other wedge basis <vector>. Thus these products are all <matrix units> and the generated star-algebra is **$\operatorname{End}(\Lambda V)$**. Any common <invariant subspace> for the <creation and annihilation operators> is therefore either zero or all of $W$.

The <commutant> is $A'=\{T\in\operatorname{End}V:Ta=aT\text{ for every }a\in A\}$. Clearly $A\subseteq A''$. For the reverse inclusion, first note that $A\xi$ is an <invariant subspace> for the unital star-algebra $A$, and its <orthogonal complement> is invariant too. Its projection belongs to $A'$. Thus if $B\in A''$, $B\xi\in A\xi$.

To make the same algebra element work for all <vectors>, amplify to $V^d$, where $d=\dim V$. The diagonal action $a^{(d)}$ has <commutant> consisting of block <matrices> whose entries belong to $A'$. Therefore $B^{(d)}$ commutes with this amplified <commutant>. For a basis $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_d$, the subspace $\{(a\xi_1,\ldots,a\xi_d):a\in A\}$ is invariant and reducing for the amplified algebra. Its projection lies in that <commutant>. The preceding argument gives some $a\in A$ with $(B\xi_1,\ldots,B\xi_d)=(a\xi_1,\ldots,a\xi_d)$. Since these <vectors> form a basis, $B=a$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A''=A.}
$$
This finite-dimensional <double commutant theorem for star-algebras> uses the star hypothesis to make the orbit subspaces reducing.

A strongly continuous <unitary representation> of the <circle group> decomposes as $H=\widehat\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb Z}H_m$, with $U_z\xi=z^m\xi$ on $H_m$. Its <positive energy unitary representation of the circle> condition is that the occurring integers are bounded below. Equivalently $U_{e^{it}}=e^{itL}$ with a <self-adjoint> generator bounded below. An integer character can shift the lowest weight to zero.

Let $M=S''$. Because $S=S^*$, this is a <von Neumann algebra>, and rotation invariance makes $U_zM'U_z^*=M'$. Joint irreducibility implies $M'\cap\{U_z\}'=\mathbb CI$: a nonscalar <self-adjoint> element of that joint <commutant> would have a nontrivial invariant <spectral projection gives a reducing subspace>.

For $T\in M'$, take its strong <Fourier coefficients> $T_k=\int_{\mathbb T}z^{-k}U_zTU_z^*\,dz$. They lie in $M'$ and satisfy $U_zT_kU_z^*=z^kT_k$. Both $T_k^*T_k$ and $T_kT_k^*$ are rotation-invariant elements of $M'$, hence scalar. If $T_k\ne0$, both scalars equal $\|T_k\|^2$, so $T_k/\|T_k\|$ is a unitary. It shifts energy by $k$. For $k\ne0$, applying it or its adjoint to a nonzero lowest-energy <vector> produces a <vector> below the lowest energy, a contradiction. Thus $T_k=0$ for every $k\ne0$, while $T_0$ is scalar.

<Fejér sum> averaging of the strongly continuous orbit $U_zTU_z^*$ converges strongly to $T$. Its <Fourier coefficients> have just shown every such average is $T_0$, so $T=T_0$ is scalar. In particular **every <bounded operator> commuting with $S$ commutes with every $U_z$**. In fact the hypotheses force the stronger conclusion $S'=\mathbb CI$.

Write $X=I+E$. Use a submultiplicative <Sobolev algebra> <norm> for which the Fourier projections are contractive, and its compatible <matrix>/column <norms>. The numeric hypothesis $\|E\|<1$ is understood in this algebra <norm>; the unscaled Fourier <Sobolev norm> need not have multiplication constant one.

Let $A_{>0}$ and $A_{<0}$ denote the strictly positive and strictly negative Fourier subspaces. The equation $y_+=-P_{>0}(E+Ey_+)$ is a contraction on $M_n(A_{>0})$, so it has a unique solution. Put $Y_+=I+y_+$ and $Z_-=XY_+$. The equation says $Z_-\in M_n(A_-)$, and $Y_+(0)=I$. Similarly the contraction $v_-=-P_{<0}(E+v_-E)$ gives $V_-=I+v_-$ with $Z_+=V_-X\in M_n(A_+)$. No invertibility of these solutions has yet been assumed.

Now $V_-Z_-=Z_+Y_+$ belongs to both Fourier subalgebras, hence is a constant <matrix> $C$. Taking constant coefficients on either side shows $C=Z_-(0)=Z_+(0)$. We claim $C$ is invertible. If $Cv=0$, then the Hardy compression $T_X=P_{\geq0}X$ annihilates $Y_+v$, because $P_{\geq0}Z_-v=Cv=0$. But $T_X=I+P_{\geq0}E$ on the Hardy <Sobolev space> has inverse given by a <Neumann series>, since the perturbation has <norm> less than one. Thus $Y_+v=0$, and its constant coefficient is $v$, forcing $v=0$. Finite-dimensionality proves the claim.

Consequently $C^{-1}Z_+$ is a left inverse of $Y_+$, and $C^{-1}V_-$ is a left inverse of $Z_-$. <Matrix> entries are functions in a commutative algebra, so pointwise finite-dimensional inversion makes these left inverses two-sided inverses in their respective Fourier subalgebras. Set
$$
\boxed{X_-=Z_-,\qquad X_+=Y_+^{-1}.}
$$
Then $X=X_-X_+$, both factors are invertible in the stated algebras, and $X_+(0)=I$. If two normalized factorizations exist, $\widetilde X_-^{-1}X_-=\widetilde X_+X_+^{-1}$ lies in $A_-\cap A_+$ and is constant; evaluating the analytic side at zero makes it $I$. This proves uniqueness. The argument is <near-identity Sobolev loop factorization> and, crucially, proves invertibility rather than treating the contraction solution itself as automatically invertible.

Give $G(H)$ its natural <trace-class> topology: differentiability means $F(t+h)=F(t)+h\dot F(t)+o_1(h)$, with remainder small in <trace norm>. The <Fredholm determinant> is the continuous extension of finite-rank <determinants>; it is multiplicative by finite-rank approximation and satisfies
$$
\det(I+K)=1+\operatorname{Tr}K+O(\|K\|_1^2)
$$
near zero. For example its exterior-power <series> bounds the remainder by $e^{\|K\|_1}-1-\|K\|_1$, proving that estimate directly.

Factor $F(t+h)=F(t)[I+hF(t)^{-1}\dot F(t)+o_1(h)]$. Multiplicativity and the preceding expansion give
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\det F(t)=\det F(t)\operatorname{Tr}(F(t)^{-1}\dot F(t)).}
$$
The <determinant> is nonzero for invertible $F$, so division yields the required logarithmic <derivative>. Differentiability only in the bounded-operator <norm> would not justify this <trace> formula; the specified topology is essential.

Let $C=[A,B]$ be <trace> class and set $F(t)=e^{tA}e^Be^{-tA}e^{-B}$. The integral identity $e^BAe^{-B}-A=\int_0^1e^{sB}[B,A]e^{-sB}ds$ is trace-norm convergent. It shows that this difference is <trace> class. The analogous Duhamel identity then makes $F(t)-I$ <trace> class and differentiable in that <norm>. Hence $F(t)\in G(H)$.

Compute the right logarithmic <derivative> without taking separate traces of $A$:
$$
\dot F F^{-1}=e^{tA}(A-e^BAe^{-B})e^{-tA}.
$$
<Trace> invariance under bounded invertible similarity and the integral identity give $\operatorname{Tr}(\dot F F^{-1})=-\int_0^1\operatorname{Tr}[B,A]ds=\operatorname{Tr}[A,B]$. Cyclicity with a <trace-class> factor makes this equal to the <trace> in part (b). As $F(0)=I$, integrating the scalar differential equation gives
$$
\boxed{\det(e^Ae^Be^{-A}e^{-B})=\exp\operatorname{Tr}[A,B].}
$$
This <Fredholm determinant of an exponential commutator> can be nontrivial in infinite <dimension>: it is not legitimate to set the <trace> of a <trace-class> <commutator> to zero when $A,B$ themselves need not be <trace> class.

Split $f=f_-+a_0+f_+$ into strictly negative and strictly positive Fourier parts, and let $A=T(f_-)$, $B=T(f_+)$. The Toeplitz multiplication rule is $T(g)T(h)=T(gh)$ when $g$ is coanalytic or $h$ is analytic. It follows directly because the discarded negative Fourier part cannot return to the Hardy space in those cases. Thus exponentiation within either subalgebra is exact, and
$$
T(e^f)T(e^{-f})=e^Ae^Be^{-A}e^{-B}.
$$
The constant terms cancel. If $S=T(z)$ is the unilateral shift, $A=\sum_{m>0}a_{-m}(S^*)^m$ and $B=\sum_{n>0}a_nS^n$. The <commutator> $[(S^*)^m,S^n]$ is finite <rank>, with <trace> $n$ when $m=n$ and zero otherwise. For equal powers it is the projection onto the first $n$ basis <vectors>; for unequal powers its <matrix> has no diagonal entries. Its <trace norm> is bounded by $2\min(m,n)$. Smoothness of $f$ makes the weighted double coefficient sum finite, so the <commutator> <series> converges in <trace norm> and
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[A,B]=\sum_{n>0}n a_na_{-n}.
$$
Applying part (c) proves the <Toeplitz exponential determinant identity>
$$
\boxed{\det(T(e^f)T(e^{-f}))=\exp\left(\sum_{n>0}na_na_{-n}\right).}
$$
In particular the product is an invertible identity-plus-trace-class operator, so the <determinant> in the formula is well-defined.

A unitary family with the required <Weyl relations> is
$$
\boxed{(W(x,y)f)(t)=e^{ixy+2iyt}f(t+x).}
$$
Translations preserve the $L^2$ <norm> and the multiplier has modulus one. Multiplication of two such operators leaves the phase $x_1y_2-y_1x_2$ after extracting $W(x_1+x_2,y_1+y_2)$, proving the relation. Translation and modulation are strongly continuous, first on compactly supported <smooth function>s by direct estimates, then on all of $L^2$ by density and unitarity.

If a <bounded operator> $A$ commutes with all $W$, it commutes with translations and every modulation $e^{2iyt}$. The <spectral theorem> for the multiplication coordinate therefore makes it commute with all bounded multiplication operators and interval projections. One can see its form directly: on a finite interval $I$, set $h_I=A1_I$. For bounded $\phi$ supported in $I$, commutation gives $A\phi=\phi h_I$. Testing characteristic functions shows $|h_I|\leq\|A\|$ almost everywhere. On increasing intervals these functions agree on overlaps and yield a single $h\in L^\infty$ with $A=M_h$.

Commutation with translations says $h(t+x)=h(t)$ almost everywhere for every $x$. Convolving with a smooth <approximate identity> produces <smooth function>s invariant under every translation, hence constants. Passing to the local $L^1$ limit makes $h$ constant almost everywhere. Therefore **$A$ is a scalar operator**, and the unitary family acts irreducibly.

Let $\widetilde W$ be the given regular irreducible family. Write $a=(x,y)$ and $a\wedge b=x_1y_2-y_1x_2$. Define the strongly integrated operator
$$
P=\frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-|a|^2/2}\widetilde W(a)\,da.
$$
The Gaussian is integrable and the operators are unitary, so the integral defines a <bounded operator> on each <vector>. The Weyl relation and $\widetilde W(a)^*=\widetilde W(-a)$ give $P^*=P$. For its square, the coefficient at $\widetilde W(c)$ is
$$
\frac1{\pi^2}\int e^{-(|a|^2+|c-a|^2)/2}e^{ia\wedge c}da
=\frac1\pi e^{-|c|^2/2},
$$
by the two-dimensional <Gaussian integral>. Thus $P^2=P$. The same Gaussian integration, with an inserted Weyl operator, gives
$$
\boxed{P\widetilde W(b)P=e^{-|b|^2/2}P.}
$$
This is the <Gaussian projection in a Weyl representation>.

It is nonzero. Otherwise all its conjugates would vanish; the Weyl relation makes their <matrix> coefficients the Fourier transforms of $e^{-|a|^2/2}(\widetilde W(a)\xi,\eta)$, with the dual variable $2b\wedge a$. Uniqueness of the <Fourier transform> of an integrable function would make each such function zero. <Strong continuity> then makes its value at zero vanish, contradicting $(\xi,\xi)>0$ for a nonzero <vector>.

Choose a unit <vector> $v\in PH$. Irreducibility makes the span of $\widetilde W(a)v$ dense. If $w\in PH$ is perpendicular to $v$, the boxed identity makes it perpendicular to this entire span, so $w=0$. Hence $P$ has <rank> one, and its identity gives $(\widetilde W(a)v,v)=e^{-|a|^2/2}$.

In the concrete representation of part (a), the Gaussian $v_0(t)=(2/\pi)^{1/4}e^{-t^2}$ has precisely this expectation. With the linear-in-first inner-product convention, both orbit families have <Gram matrix>
$$
(\widetilde W(a)v,\widetilde W(b)v)=e^{ia\wedge b}e^{-|a-b|^2/2}
=(W(a)v_0,W(b)v_0).
$$
Therefore the map $\sum c_jW(a_j)v_0\mapsto\sum c_j\widetilde W(a_j)v$ is well-defined and isometric. Its source and target spans are dense, so it extends to a <unitary operator> intertwiner. This proves the **<unitary equivalence>**, the <Stone-von Neumann theorem> in the normalization of this question, rather than invoking that theorem as the proof.

One form of the <Jacobi triple product> is, for $0<|q|<1$ and $z\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}q^{n^2}z^n
=\prod_{j=1}^\infty(1-q^{2j})(1+zq^{2j-1})(1+z^{-1}q^{2j-1}).}
$$
Both sides converge locally uniformly on the punctured $z$ plane. Here is a finite-product proof which also determines the multiplicative constant.

Put $Q=q^2$ and $(Q;Q)_m=\prod_{j=1}^m(1-Q^j)$, with value one at $m=0$. Define the <Gaussian binomial coefficient> ${m\brack k}_Q=(Q;Q)_m/[(Q;Q)_k(Q;Q)_{m-k}]$. The recurrence ${m\brack k}_Q={m-1\brack k}_Q+Q^{m-k}{m-1\brack k-1}_Q$ proves by induction that
$$
\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(1+tQ^j)
=\sum_{k=0}^mQ^{k(k-1)/2}{m\brack k}_Qt^k.
$$
Rewrite the two finite factors as
$$
\prod_{j=1}^N(1+zq^{2j-1})(1+z^{-1}q^{2j-1})
=z^{-N}q^{N^2}\prod_{j=0}^{2N-1}(1+zq^{1-2N}Q^j).
$$
The finite identity, with $n=k-N$, makes this $\sum_{n=-N}^Nz^nq^{n^2}{2N\brack N+n}_Q$. Multiply by $(Q;Q)_N$. For each fixed integer $n$, the resulting coefficient tends to one, because $(Q;Q)_m$ tends to a nonzero limit and ${2N\brack N+n}_Q\to1/(Q;Q)_\infty$.

All finite $(Q;Q)_m$ are uniformly bounded above and away from zero. Thus these coefficients have one uniform bound, and $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|q|^{n^2}|z|^n$ is uniformly summable on compact annuli. Dominated convergence passes the limit through the finite sums and proves the boxed formula. At $q=0$ the formula follows by its limiting value one. The frequently used alternative form is
$$
\prod_{j\geq1}(1-t^j)(1+u t^{j-1})(1+u^{-1}t^j)
=\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z}u^m t^{m(m-1)/2},\qquad |t|<1.
$$
It follows by the substitution $q^2=t$, $z=u/q$, and is the version used for the charge-counting identity in Question 8.

Write $p:U(H)\to PU(H)$ for the scalar quotient. Local strongly continuous sections exist: near a fixed projective unitary, fix its phase by requiring one nonzero <matrix> coefficient, taken relative to a fixed representative, to be positive real. Thus the pullback $E=\{(z,V):p(V)=\rho(z)\}$ is a locally trivial central extension of the <circle> by the <circle>. It is compact, since finitely many closed arcs inside such trivializing neighbourhoods cover the compact base and each pullback is a product with the compact fibre.

Any two lifts commute up to a scalar. Their <commutator> defines a continuous alternating bicharacter $c(z,w)$ on the base: the scalar <commutator> is independent of phase choices and multiplicative in each argument. If $z$ is an irrational rotation, its powers are dense and $c(z,z^m)=1$ for every integer $m$. Continuity makes $c(z,w)=1$ for every $w$. Such $z$ are themselves dense, so **all the lifts commute**, and $E$ is abelian.

The tautological representation $R(z,V)=V$ is strongly continuous. Choose a unit <vector> $\xi$ and average its <rank-one orthogonal projection> over <Haar measure> on $E$:
$$
K=\int_E R(e)|\xi\rangle\langle\xi|R(e)^*\,de.
$$
The integrand is trace-norm continuous, so $K$ is positive compact with <trace> one, and commutes with $R(E)$. A positive <eigenvalue> has a nonzero finite-dimensional <eigenspace> invariant under $E$. Commuting unitary <matrices> on that space have a common unit <eigenvector> $\eta$. Therefore $R(e)\eta=\chi(e)\eta$ for a continuous character $\chi$ of $E$.

On the central fibre, $R(1,\lambda I)=\lambda I$, so $\chi(1,\lambda I)=\lambda$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{U_z=\chi(z,V)^{-1}V\quad\text{for any lift }V\text{ of }\rho(z)}
$$
is independent of that lift. It is a <homomorphism> and strongly continuous, because the continuous map on $E$ descends through its quotient to the base. It satisfies $p(U_z)=\rho(z)$. This proves <circle projective representations lift to unitary representations> using an actual central character, rather than assuming a global choice of phases.

First, all lifts of the real one-parameter projective group commute. Its scalar <commutator> $c(s,t)$ is again a continuous alternating bicharacter. For fixed nonzero $t$ and an integer $n>0$, a lift of $t$ is a phase multiple of the $n$th power of a lift of $t/n$; hence $c(t,t/n)=1$ and $c(t,mt/n)=1$ for every integer $m$. = Solution

The <Fredholm index> measures the finite-dimensional obstruction to inverting an operator. For a <bounded operator> $T:H_1\to H_2$ between <Hilbert spaces>, the <Fredholm> conditions are that its <range of a bounded linear operator> is closed and its <kernel> and <cokernel> are finite-dimensional. The <orthogonal complement> of the range is $\ker T^*$, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ind}T=\dim\ker T-\dim\ker T^*.}
$$
An <invertible linear map> has <Fredholm index> zero; the converse needs an additional finite-dimensional correction, since an operator can have equally large <kernel> and <cokernel>.

First construct an inverse up to <finite-rank operators>. On $(\ker T)^\perp$, the restriction of $T$ is a bounded bijection onto its closed range, with bounded inverse by the <bounded inverse theorem>. Extend that inverse by zero on $(\operatorname{ran}T)^\perp$, obtaining $S:H_2\to H_1$. Then
$$
ST=I-P_{\ker T},\qquad TS=I-P_{\ker T^*}.
$$
Conversely, suppose $ST-I$ and $TS-I$ are <compact operators>. On $\ker T$, the first identity expresses the identity as a <compact operator>, so this <kernel> is finite-dimensional. If $T$ were not bounded below on $(\ker T)^\perp$, there would be unit <vectors> $x_j$ in that complement with $Tx_j\to0$. Write $ST=I+K$. <Compactness> of $K$ gives a subsequence for which $Kx_j$ converges, hence $x_j=STx_j-Kx_j$ converges to a unit <vector> $x$ in $(\ker T)^\perp$ with $Tx=0$, a contradiction. This lower bound makes the range closed. Applying the analogous argument to $T^*$, using $S^*T^*=I+K'^*$, proves that its <kernel> is finite-dimensional. This proves the <Atkinson theorem>: a <bounded operator> is <Fredholm> exactly when it has an inverse modulo <compact operators>. For operators on one <Hilbert space> this says that its image in the <Calkin algebra> is invertible.

The <Fredholm index> is stable. Decompose the domain as $N\oplus N^\perp$, where $N=\ker T$, and the target as $C\oplus R$, where $C=\ker T^*$ and $R=\operatorname{ran}T$. A sufficiently small <norm> perturbation has block <matrix>
$$
T+E=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad D:N^\perp\longrightarrow R\text{ invertible}.
$$
Multiplying by invertible triangular block <matrices> reduces this <matrix> to the <direct sum> of $D$ and the <Schur complement> $a-bD^{-1}c:N\to C$. The latter's <kernel> <dimension> minus <cokernel> <dimension> is $\dim N-\dim C$, whatever its <rank>. Therefore the Fredholm set is open and the index is locally constant. If $K$ is compact, the same parametrix works modulo <compact operators> for every $T+tK$, $0\leq t\leq1$. Local constancy along this path gives
$$
\operatorname{ind}(T+K)=\operatorname{ind}T.
$$

For <Fredholm operators> $A:H_1\to H_2$ and $B:H_2\to H_3$, multiplication of parametrices shows that $BA$ is Fredholm. Its index is additive. Here is a direct proof, rather than an assertion of additivity. There is an <exact sequence> of finite-dimensional spaces
$$
0\longrightarrow\ker A\longrightarrow\ker BA
\xrightarrow{A}\ker B\longrightarrow\operatorname{coker}A
\xrightarrow{B}\operatorname{coker}BA
\longrightarrow\operatorname{coker}B\longrightarrow0.
$$
The map from $\ker B$ takes a <vector> to its class modulo $\operatorname{ran}A$; the next takes the class of $y$ to that of $By$; the last is the quotient map. For example, a class $y+\operatorname{ran}A$ lies in the <kernel> of the middle map exactly when $By=BAx$ for some $x$, so $y-Ax\in\ker B$. This verifies the only less immediate exactness assertion; the others follow directly from their definitions. Alternating the <dimensions> gives
$$
\operatorname{ind}(BA)=\operatorname{ind}B+\operatorname{ind}A.
$$
Also $\operatorname{ind}T^*=-\operatorname{ind}T$, and indices add under <direct sums>. For $I+K$ with $K$ compact, the index is zero, so <injectivity> is equivalent to <surjectivity>. More generally $Tx=f$ is solvable exactly when $f$ is orthogonal to $\ker T^*$: these are the finitely many compatibility conditions in the <Fredholm alternative>.

<Toeplitz operators> exhibit how this analytic index detects a topological winding. Let $H^2\subset L^2(S^1)$ be the <Hardy space of the circle>, spanned by $1,z,z^2,\ldots$, and let $P$ be its <orthogonal projection>. For a continuous symbol $g$, define $T_g=P M_g|_{H^2}$, where $M_g$ is multiplication by $g$. Then $\|T_g\|\leq\|g\|_\infty$. The product defect is
$$
T_fT_g-T_{fg}=-P M_f(I-P)M_g|_{H^2}.
$$
For <trigonometric polynomials> the crossing of negative and nonnegative <Fourier modes> has finite <rank>. Uniform approximation of continuous symbols by <trigonometric polynomials>, together with the displayed <norm> estimate, therefore makes this defect a <compact operator> for all continuous $f,g$. If $g$ is nowhere zero, $1/g$ is continuous and $T_{1/g}$ is a two-sided parametrix modulo <compact operators>. Thus $T_g$ is Fredholm.

The converse is instructive. If $g(\zeta)=0$ at $\zeta\in S^1$, consider the normalized Hardy <kernels>
$$
k_{r,\zeta}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{1-r^2}}{1-r\overline\zeta z},\qquad 0<r<1.
$$
Their <norms> are one, their individual <Fourier coefficients> tend to zero as $r\uparrow1$, and hence they converge weakly to zero. Their squared moduli are the <Poisson kernel>, so continuity of $g$ gives
$$
\|T_g k_{r,\zeta}\|_2^2\leq\|gk_{r,\zeta}\|_2^2
=\int_{S^1}|g(z)|^2|k_{r,\zeta}(z)|^2\,\frac{|dz|}{2\pi}\longrightarrow0.
$$
A <Fredholm operator> is bounded below off its finite-dimensional <kernel>. The projection of these weakly vanishing <vectors> onto that <kernel> tends to zero, contradicting this lower bound. Thus **a continuous-symbol <Toeplitz operator> is Fredholm exactly when its symbol never vanishes**. Applying the same argument to $g-\lambda$ gives its <essential spectrum> as $g(S^1)$, with essential <spectrum> defined by failure of the Fredholm property.

For nonvanishing $g$, let $m=\operatorname{wind}(g,0)$. Lifting a continuous argument of $g(e^{it})$ on $0\leq t\leq2\pi$ gives an argument change $2\pi m$. After division by $z^m$, its argument has matching endpoints, so there is a <continuous function> $h:S^1\to\mathbb C$ with $g(z)=z^m e^{h(z)}$. The nonvanishing-symbol homotopy $g_t=z^m e^{th}$ reduces the index to that of $T_{z^m}$. For $m\geq0$ this is the $m$th power of the <unilateral shift operator>, whose <kernel> is zero and whose range has codimension $m$. For $m<0$ it is the $(-m)$th power of the adjoint shift, whose <kernel> has <dimension> $-m$ and whose range is all of $H^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ind}T_g=-\operatorname{wind}(g,0).}
$$
The minus sign comes from counting missing nonnegative <Fourier modes>. This is a concrete bridge between the <Fredholm index>, stability under compact perturbations, and the <winding number> of a loop.

On the <torus> $\mathbb T^n=(\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z)^n$, choose normalized <Haar measure>, so that $e_k(x)=e^{ik\cdot x}$, $k\in\mathbb Z^n$, is an <orthonormal basis> of $L^2$. A <periodic Sobolev space> is defined, for any real $s$, by
$$
H^s(\mathbb T^n)=\left\{u:\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z^n}(1+|k|^2)^s|\widehat u(k)|^2<\infty\right\},\qquad
\|u\|_{H^s}^2=\sum_k(1+|k|^2)^s|\widehat u(k)|^2.
$$
For negative $s$, the coefficients describe <distributions> rather than necessarily functions. For a nonnegative integer $s$, the weight is comparable to $\sum_{|\alpha|\leq s}|k^\alpha|^2$, so this <norm> is equivalent to the sum of the squared $L^2$ <norms> of all <weak derivatives> through order $s$. Fourier truncation proves that smooth <trigonometric polynomials> are dense. The $L^2$ pairing identifies the continuous dual of $H^s$ with $H^{-s}$, giving <Sobolev duality>.

Two Fourier estimates explain the usefulness of these spaces. If $s>m+n/2$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
\sum_k |k|^m|\widehat u(k)|
\leq\|u\|_{H^s}\left(\sum_k\frac{|k|^{2m}}{(1+|k|^2)^s}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.
$$
<Uniform convergence> of the differentiated <Fourier series> proves the <Sobolev embedding> $H^s\hookrightarrow C^m$. If $\delta>0$, a bounded subset of $H^{s+\delta}$ has uniformly small high-frequency tails in $H^s$:
$$
\sum_{|k|>R}(1+|k|^2)^s|\widehat u(k)|^2
\leq(1+R^2)^{-\delta}\|u\|_{H^{s+\delta}}^2.
$$
Finite-dimensional Fourier truncation therefore proves the compact embedding $H^{s+\delta}\hookrightarrow H^s$, the periodic version of <Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem>. In addition, for $s>n/2$ multiplication is continuous $H^s\times H^s\to H^s$, making it a <Sobolev algebra>. Indeed the weighted coefficient of a product is bounded by the sum of the convolutions of $\langle k\rangle^s|\widehat u(k)|$ with $|\widehat v(k)|$ and of $|\widehat u(k)|$ with $\langle k\rangle^s|\widehat v(k)|$; Cauchy-Schwarz puts unweighted coefficients in $\ell^1$, and the $\ell^1*\ell^2\to\ell^2$ case of <Young's convolution inequality> finishes the estimate.

For <eigenfunction expansions>, distinguish ellipticity from <self-adjointness>. An arbitrary non-self-adjoint elliptic operator need not have an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenfunctions>. A useful definite setting is
$$
L u=-\sum_{i,j}\partial_i(a_{ij}(x)\partial_j u)+V(x)u,
$$
with smooth real periodic coefficients, $a_{ij}=a_{ji}$, real $V$, and <uniform ellipticity>
$$
\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(x)\xi_i\xi_j\geq c|\xi|^2\qquad(c>0).
$$
Take the <self-adjoint> realization in $L^2$ with domain $H^2$. Its construction and spectral properties can be seen from the associated form on $H^1$,
$$
b(u,v)=\int\left(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\partial_j u\,\overline{\partial_i v}+V u\overline v\right).
$$
For a sufficiently large constant $C$, $b_C=b+C(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a positive <coercive> form: $b_C(u,u)\geq c_1\|u\|_{H^1}^2$, and $L+C\geq I$. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> supplies the unique weak solution of $(L+C)u=f$ for $f\in H^{-1}$; the form is Hermitian, which gives symmetry of its inverse on $L^2$.

The regularity input is the <periodic elliptic estimate>
$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+2}}\leq C_s\bigl(\|Lu\|_{H^s}+\|u\|_{H^s}\bigr).
$$
Here is why an elliptic operator gains two <derivatives>. With coefficients frozen at a point, its principal symbol $\sum a_{ij}\xi_i\xi_j$ dominates $c|\xi|^2$, and the estimate follows by multiplying <Fourier coefficients> by that symbol. On a sufficiently small coordinate patch, the difference of the leading coefficients from these constants has arbitrarily small supremum <norm>. In the integer-order differentiated estimate, this absorbs the highest <derivatives> into the left side; <derivatives> falling on coefficients contribute only lower-order terms. A <partition of unity> reduces the variable-coefficient problem to such patches. Its <commutators> with $L$ have order one, controlled by
$$
\|u\|_{H^{s+1}}\leq\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^{s+2}}+C_\varepsilon\|u\|_{H^s},
$$
which follows by splitting low and high <Fourier modes>. Absorbing these terms gives the displayed estimate. For a weak $H^1$ solution, the same localization with difference quotients first bounds its second <weak derivatives> for $f\in L^2$; differentiating and repeating then gives $f\in H^s\Rightarrow u\in H^{s+2}$ for nonnegative integer $s$. Interpolation and duality extend the scale to real orders. In particular $(L+C)^{-1}:L^2\to H^2$ is bounded.

Let $R=(L+C)^{-1}$. <Compactness> of $H^2\hookrightarrow L^2$ makes $R$ a <compact operator>. Symmetry of the form makes $R$ <self-adjoint>, and positivity of $L+C$ makes $R$ positive and <injective>. Its range is dense because $\ker R^*=\ker R=0$. The <spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators> consequently gives an <orthonormal basis> $\phi_j$ with
$$
R\phi_j=\mu_j\phi_j,\quad\mu_j>0,\quad\mu_j\longrightarrow0,\qquad
L\phi_j=\lambda_j\phi_j,\quad\lambda_j=\mu_j^{-1}-C\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$
<Elliptic regularity> bootstraps every $\phi_j$ to a <smooth function>. Each <eigenvalue> has finite multiplicity. Thus **a <self-adjoint> uniformly elliptic second-order operator on the <torus> has a complete smooth <orthonormal> <eigenfunction expansion> with discrete <eigenvalues> tending to infinity**.

Writing $u_j=(u,\phi_j)$, every $u\in L^2$ has $u=\sum_j u_j\phi_j$ in $L^2$, with $\|u\|_2^2=\sum_j|u_j|^2$. Iterating the elliptic estimate shows that the graph <norm> of $(L+C)^m$ is equivalent to the $H^{2m}$ <norm>. Interpolation and duality give the more general spectral description
$$
\boxed{\|u\|_{H^s}^2\asymp\sum_j(\lambda_j+C)^s|u_j|^2.}
$$
The comparison constants depend on the operator and $s$. In particular <smooth function>s have coefficients decaying faster than every power of $\lambda_j+C$, and their expansions converge in every <Sobolev norm> and hence in every $C^m$ <norm>.

This gives a practical way to solve elliptic equations. The equation $Lu=f$ is solvable if and only if $f_j=0$ whenever $\lambda_j=0$; then one may take $u_j=f_j/\lambda_j$ on the other <eigenspaces> and add an arbitrary <vector> in $\ker L$. The Sobolev description proves the two-derivative gain and the <Fredholm alternative>, rather than leaving them as formal <series> manipulations. Similarly the solution of the <heat equation> $\partial_tu+Lu=0$ is
$$
u(t)=\sum_j e^{-t\lambda_j}u_j(0)\phi_j.
$$
For every $t>0$ the exponential decay at high <eigenvalues> places this solution in every <Sobolev space>, hence it is smooth.

For the <Laplacian> $-\Delta$ the <eigenfunctions> are precisely $e^{ik\cdot x}$ with <eigenvalues> $|k|^2$. The number with $|k|^2\leq R$ is the number of lattice points in a radius-$\sqrt R$ ball, asymptotic to its volume by comparison with unit cubes. For the general <self-adjoint> operator above, bounds on its form above and below by positive multiples of $\|\nabla u\|_2^2$ plus constants, together with the <Courant–Fischer min-max principle>, imply $\lambda_j+C\asymp j^{2/n}$. Thus the growth reflects <dimension> and the two-derivative order. <Compactness> of the <torus> is essential: on $\mathbb R^n$, translated bumps prevent the compact embedding, and the <Laplacian> instead has continuous <spectrum> described by the <Fourier transform>.

Use the unitary normalization of the <Fourier transform>,
$$
\mathcal Ff(\xi)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}f(x)\,dx,\qquad
\mathcal Gg(x)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{ix\cdot\xi}g(\xi)\,d\xi.
$$
The <Schwartz space> $\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)$ consists of <smooth function>s for which every <Schwartz seminorm> $\sup_x|x^\alpha\partial^\beta f(x)|$ is finite. Such functions and all their <derivatives> are integrable. Differentiating under the integral and integrating by parts give
$$
\partial_\xi^\alpha\mathcal Ff=(-i)^{|\alpha|}\mathcal F(x^\alpha f),\qquad
\xi^\beta\mathcal Ff=(-i)^{|\beta|}\mathcal F(\partial^\beta f).
$$
Consequently
$$
\sup_\xi|\xi^\beta\partial_\xi^\alpha\mathcal Ff(\xi)|
\leq(2\pi)^{-n/2}\|\partial^\beta(x^\alpha f)\|_1.
$$
Expand the <derivative> by the product rule and insert the integrable weight $(1+|x|)^{-n-1}$; the right side is bounded by finitely many Schwartz seminorms of $f$. This proves continuity $\mathcal F:\mathcal S\to\mathcal S$. The identical argument with the opposite sign proves continuity of $\mathcal G$.

For <surjectivity> and <injectivity> an inversion proof is needed. If $f\in\mathcal S$, multiply $\mathcal Ff(\xi)$ by $e^{-\varepsilon|\xi|^2/2}$. <Fubini's theorem> and the elementary <Gaussian integral> give
$$
\mathcal G\bigl(e^{-\varepsilon|\xi|^2/2}\mathcal Ff\bigr)(x)
=\int_{\mathbb R^n}f(y)\rho_\varepsilon(x-y)\,dy,
\qquad
\rho_\varepsilon(x)=(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-n/2}e^{-|x|^2/(2\varepsilon)}.
$$
The Gaussian has total integral one and its mass outside any fixed ball tends to zero. The right side therefore tends to $f(x)$, by uniform continuity of $f$. On the left, $\mathcal Ff\in\mathcal S\subset L^1$, so dominated convergence gives $\mathcal G\mathcal Ff(x)$. This proves $\mathcal G\mathcal F=I$. Reversing the signs in the same calculation proves $\mathcal F\mathcal G=I$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F:\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\longrightarrow\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\text{ is a continuous bijection, with inverse }\mathcal G.}
$$
Its inverse is continuous by the seminorm estimate already proved. This establishes the requested bijection without assuming <Fourier inversion> as an unexplained theorem.

Inversion gives $\mathcal F^2f(x)=f(-x)$ and the <Parseval identity>. Indeed, inserting the integral for $\mathcal Ff$ into $\int\mathcal Ff\,\overline{\mathcal Fg}$ and using $f,\mathcal Fg\in L^1$ gives $\int f\overline g$. Density of <Schwartz functions> in $L^2$ extends $\mathcal F$ to a <unitary operator> on $L^2$, the <Plancherel theorem>. Translation of $f$ becomes multiplication by a phase, modulation becomes frequency translation, and
$$
\mathcal F(f*g)=(2\pi)^{n/2}(\mathcal Ff)(\mathcal Fg),\qquad
\mathcal F(\partial_jf)=i\xi_j\mathcal Ff.
$$
These formulas explain why the <Fourier transform> diagonalizes constant-coefficient differential operators. By transpose it also defines a bijection of the <tempered distributions>: with the complex-linear distributional pairing, $\langle\mathcal FT,\phi\rangle=\langle T,\mathcal F\phi\rangle$, since the Fourier <kernel> is symmetric in its variables.

The connection with <creation and annihilation operators> is especially transparent for the <harmonic oscillator>. On the common invariant dense domain $\mathcal S$, set
$$
a_j=\frac{x_j+\partial_j}{\sqrt2},\qquad
 a_j^\dagger=\frac{x_j-\partial_j}{\sqrt2}.
$$
<Integration by parts> shows that these are formal Hilbert-space adjoints. They are closable unbounded operators; <commutators> below are computed on $\mathcal S$, avoiding undefined products on arbitrary $L^2$ <vectors>. The identity $[\partial_j,x_k]=\delta_{jk}I$ gives the <canonical commutation relations>
$$
[a_j,a_k^\dagger]=\delta_{jk}I,\qquad[a_j,a_k]=[a_j^\dagger,a_k^\dagger]=0.
$$
It also gives
$$
H=-\Delta+|x|^2=2\sum_j a_j^\dagger a_j+nI.
$$
The normalized vacuum $h_0(x)=\pi^{-n/4}e^{-|x|^2/2}$ satisfies $a_jh_0=0$. Define the <Hermite functions>
$$
h_\alpha=\frac{(a_1^\dagger)^{\alpha_1}\cdots(a_n^\dagger)^{\alpha_n}}{\sqrt{\alpha_1!\cdots\alpha_n!}}h_0.
$$
Moving <annihilation operators> past <creation operators> with the commutation relations proves
$$
a_jh_\alpha=\sqrt{\alpha_j}\,h_{\alpha-e_j},\qquad
 a_j^\dagger h_\alpha=\sqrt{\alpha_j+1}\,h_{\alpha+e_j},\qquad
Hh_\alpha=(2|\alpha|+n)h_\alpha.
$$
These same relations show that the <Hermite functions> are <orthonormal>: unequal occupations are orthogonal <eigenvectors> of one of the commuting symmetric <number operators> $a_j^\dagger a_j$, and the ladder relations recursively give <norm> one.

For completeness, let $g\in L^2$ be orthogonal to every $h_\alpha$. The Hermite <polynomials> have nonzero leading monomials and triangular lower-degree terms, so these orthogonality relations say that all <polynomial> moments of $h(x)=g(x)e^{-|x|^2/2}\in L^1$ vanish. Its Fourier integral extends to an <entire function> of $\zeta\in\mathbb C^n$:
$$
F(\zeta)=\int g(x)e^{-|x|^2/2}e^{-ix\cdot\zeta}\,dx.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, with the Gaussian absorbing every exponential linear weight and every <polynomial>, justifies differentiation uniformly on compact sets of $\zeta$. Every Taylor coefficient at zero is a vanishing moment, so the <entire function> is zero. Fourier uniqueness on $L^1$ follows here without circularity: if an $L^1$ function has zero <Fourier transform>, convolving it with $\rho_\varepsilon$ gives zero by the <Gaussian integral> and Fubini, and the approximate-identity limit in $L^1$ gives the original function zero. Thus $h=0$ and $g=0$. The <Hermite functions> form an <orthonormal basis> of $L^2$.

Finally, the elementary <Gaussian integral> gives $\mathcal Fh_0=h_0$. The <derivative> and multiplication identities above imply
$$
\mathcal F a_j=i a_j\mathcal F,\qquad
\mathcal F a_j^\dagger=-i a_j^\dagger\mathcal F,
$$
where the operators on the right act on the frequency variable. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal Fh_\alpha=(-i)^{|\alpha|}h_\alpha.}
$$
Thus creation and annihilation organize both the oscillator's spectral decomposition and the four possible Fourier <eigenvalues>. On <Schwartz functions> the oscillator powers control weighted <derivatives>, and Hermite coefficients decay faster than every power of $1+|\alpha|$; conversely this rapid coefficient decay implies all Schwartz seminorms are finite. The Fourier phases preserve this decay, providing another explanation of the Fourier automorphism of <Schwartz space>.

The <loop group> here is $LU(1)=C^\infty(S^1,U(1))$, with pointwise multiplication. Although it is abelian, its nontrivial positive-energy quantum representations are projective: implementers can commute only up to a phase. A <positive energy representation of the loop group of U(1)> has rotations $R_t$ whose <self-adjoint> generator is bounded below and which implement $g(z)\mapsto g(e^{it}z)$ in the <projective unitary group>. The fermionic construction gives both that phase and an explicit bosonic description.

Choose the one-particle <Hilbert space> $H=L^2(S^1)$ with modes $e_r(z)=z^r$, $r\in\mathbb Z$, and projection $P$ onto $r\geq0$. Use the <polarized fermionic Fock space>
$$
\mathcal F_P=\Lambda(PH)\,\widehat\otimes\,\Lambda(\overline{(I-P)H}),
$$
where the <exterior powers> are Hilbert-completed and the <tensor product> is graded. Its vacuum fills all modes $r<0$ and leaves $r\geq0$ empty; excitations are particles in the empty modes and holes in the filled modes. The <fermionic annihilation operators> $c_r$ and <fermionic creation operators> $c_r^*$ satisfy the <canonical anticommutation relations>
$$
\{c_r,c_s^*\}=\delta_{rs}I,\qquad\{c_r,c_s\}=\{c_r^*,c_s^*\}=0,
\qquad c_r\Omega=0\ (r\geq0),\quad c_r^*\Omega=0\ (r<0).
$$
Finite particle-hole configurations form an <orthonormal basis>. As in the finite-dimensional <exterior algebra> construction, their <creation and annihilation operators> act irreducibly: an operator in the <commutant> sends the vacuum to a scalar vacuum, since the joint <kernel> of its vacuum annihilators is one-dimensional, and then acts by that scalar on the dense configuration basis.

<Segal's quantization criterion for fermions> says that a unitary one-particle operator $u$ has a unitary implementer $\Gamma(u)$ on this Fock space, satisfying $\Gamma(u)c^*(f)\Gamma(u)^*=c^*(uf)$, exactly when
$$
\boxed{[P,u]\text{ is Hilbert-Schmidt}.}
$$
Equivalently $P-uPu^*$ is <Hilbert-Schmidt>. Implementers are unique up to phase, hence form a <projective unitary representation> of the <restricted unitary group>. This is the fermionic, polarized criterion; it is not a condition that the entire operator $u-I$ be <Hilbert-Schmidt>. The criterion and the corresponding continuity topology are stated in \a[https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0409044][Theorem III.2.1.1 of the fermionic construction of loop-group representations]. For the compact principal-angle part of a pair of polarizations, the threshold has a concrete interpretation. Its two-dimensional blocks are rotations through angles $\theta_j$; finitely many unmatched modes can be handled separately. Each angle $\theta_j$ rotates the vacuum into $\cos\theta_j$ times its original component plus $\sin\theta_j$ times a particle-hole pair. After discarding finitely many exceptional blocks, the successive <tensor products> are Cauchy precisely when $\prod_j\cos\theta_j$ converges to a nonzero value, equivalently $\sum_j\sin^2\theta_j<\infty$. In this block model the sum is exactly half the squared <Hilbert-Schmidt> <norm> of the projection difference; finitely many unmatched modes account for a change of charge. Infinitely many unmatched modes already violate the criterion. This explains why infinitely many arbitrarily small mixings can fail to be implementable.

For $g(z)=\sum_n g_nz^n\in LU(1)$, multiplication $M_g$ is a unitary one-particle operator. Its <matrix> entry from $e_s$ to $e_r$ is $g_{r-s}$. Therefore
$$
\|[P,M_g]\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
=\sum_{r,s}|\mathbf1_{r\geq0}-\mathbf1_{s\geq0}|^2|g_{r-s}|^2
=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|n||g_n|^2.
$$
For a fixed difference $n$, exactly $|n|$ pairs cross the polarization boundary. Smoothness makes the sum finite, so <Segal's quantization criterion for fermions> constructs a continuous <projective unitary representation> of every smooth loop. Smooth convergence gives operator-norm convergence of $M_g$ and <Hilbert-Schmidt> convergence of its <commutator>, which is sufficient for the strong projective continuity of the implementers.

<Normal ordering> fixes the charge and rotation energy. On the dense finite-configuration domain put
$$
E_{rs}=:c_r^*c_s:=c_r^*c_s-\delta_{rs}\mathbf1_{r<0}I,
\qquad Q=\sum_rE_{rr},\qquad L_0=\sum_r rE_{rr}.
$$
The sums are finite on each configuration. A particle at $r\geq0$ adds charge one and energy $r$; a hole at $r<0$ adds charge minus one and energy $-r$. Consequently $L_0$ is a nonnegative <self-adjoint> diagonal operator with integer <spectrum> and finite-dimensional <eigenspaces>. The unitaries $R_t=e^{itL_0}$ are strongly continuous, have period $2\pi$, and implement $e_r\mapsto e^{itr}e_r$. Conjugating multiplication by this one-particle rotation gives $M_{g(e^{it}\,\cdot)}$, so their Fock implementers rotate the loop representation projectively. This is an actual positive-energy <circle> action.

The scalar anomaly is visible in the <Heisenberg current algebra of a fermion on the circle>. Define on finite-energy <vectors>
$$
J_n=\sum_{r\in\mathbb Z}E_{r,r+n},\qquad J_n^*=J_{-n},\qquad J_0=Q.
$$
For fixed $n$ the sum has only finitely many nonzero actions on any configuration. Direct use of the <canonical anticommutation relations> gives
$$
[E_{rs},E_{tu}]=\delta_{st}E_{ru}-\delta_{ur}E_{ts}
+\delta_{st}\delta_{ur}(\mathbf1_{r<0}-\mathbf1_{s<0})I.
$$
On summing, the two noncentral sums cancel. If $m=-n$ the central sum is $\sum_r(\mathbf1_{r<0}-\mathbf1_{r+n<0})=n$: for $n>0$ the terms are precisely $r=-n,\ldots,-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{[J_n,J_m]=n\delta_{n+m,0}I,\qquad[L_0,J_n]=-nJ_n.}
$$
In particular $J_n\Omega=0$ for $n>0$. The coefficient one in the central term is the level of this basic representation.

Let $h(\theta)=\sum_n h_ne^{in\theta}$ be smooth and real. The normal-ordered infinitesimal implementer of multiplication by $h$ is
$$
A(h)=\sum_n h_nJ_{-n}.
$$
The sign of the current index matters: multiplication by $z^n$ raises the one-particle mode by $n$, so it corresponds to $J_{-n}$. It follows either directly from the bilinear <commutator> or from the <fermionic currents on the circle> that $[A(h),c_r^*]=\sum_n h_nc_{r+n}^*$. For trigonometric $h$ these are oscillator fields on the finite-energy domain; their <self-adjoint> closures exponentiate to the implementers of $e^{ih}$. For general smooth $h$, their extensions are obtained by the oscillator-field construction below: $\sum_{n>0}n|h_n|^2<\infty$ guarantees the square-summable field coefficients, and Fourier truncation converges in the corresponding <Weyl relations> implementers. Thus no unjustified infinite operator product is required.

Set
$$
\omega(h,k)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}h'(\theta)k(\theta)\,d\theta
=i\sum_n nh_nk_{-n}.
$$
It is real and antisymmetric for real $h,k$. The <Heisenberg current algebra of a fermion on the circle> relations give $[A(h),A(k)]=i\omega(h,k)I$. The <Weyl relations>, or the finite-mode Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff computation followed by the square-summable limit, yield
$$
e^{iA(h)}e^{iA(k)}=e^{-i\omega(h,k)/2}e^{iA(h+k)}.
$$
This is an explicit <two-cocycle> on the identity component of the <loop group>. A different phase choice changes it by a <group coboundary>, but its antisymmetric <commutator> remains $e^{-i\omega(h,k)}$, and hence cannot be removed.

The winding components also matter. Every smooth loop is $z^we^{ih}$ with $w\in\mathbb Z$, real periodic $h$, and mean $h_0$ determined modulo $2\pi$. Let $S$ implement multiplication by $z$. It moves the filled boundary one place upwards, so it raises charge by one; phases of charge <ground states> can be chosen so that $S\Omega_q=\Omega_{q+1}$. Conjugating the <fermionic currents on the circle> gives
$$
SQS^*=Q-I,\qquad SJ_nS^*=J_n\quad(n\ne0).
$$
Choose $U(w,h)=S^we^{iA(h)}$. Since $e^{iA(h)}S^v=S^ve^{i(A(h)+vh_0I)}$, one obtains the full <loop group two-cocycle for U(1)>
$$
\boxed{U(w,h)U(v,k)=c((w,h),(v,k))U(w+v,h+k),\quad
c((w,h),(v,k))=\exp\bigl(ivh_0-i\omega(h,k)/2\bigr).}
$$
The exponent is bilinear in the additive winding-logarithm coordinates, so expansion immediately gives $c(a,b)c(a+b,d)=c(b,d)c(a,b+d)$. Replacing $h$ by $h+2\pi\ell$ changes $A(h)$ by $2\pi\ell Q$, whose exponential is identity since charges are integers; the scalar cocycle also stays unchanged since $v\in\mathbb Z$. Thus the expression is well-defined on loops, not just on chosen logarithms. It records both the <derivative> anomaly and the <commutator> of winding with constant loops.

For the <fermion-boson correspondence on the circle>, decompose $\mathcal F_P=\bigoplus_{q\in\mathbb Z}\mathcal F_q$ into charge <eigenspaces>. The charge-$q$ <ground state> $\Omega_q$ fills all integer modes $r<q$. Its energy is
$$
L_0\Omega_q=\frac{q(q-1)}2\Omega_q.
$$
For $q>0$, sum the energies of the added modes $0,\ldots,q-1$; for $q<0$, sum the hole energies of the removed modes $q,\ldots,-1$. Every other charge-$q$ configuration is obtained by moving finitely many occupied levels upwards, so this is the lowest energy. Since $J_n$ preserves charge and lowers energy by $n>0$, $J_n\Omega_q=0$ for $n>0$.

Define <bosonic annihilation operators> $b_n=J_n/\sqrt n$ and $b_n^*=J_{-n}/\sqrt n$, $n>0$. They obey $[b_n,b_m^*]=\delta_{nm}I$. Their occupation <vectors>
$$
\prod_{n>0}\frac{(J_{-n})^{m_n}}{\sqrt{n^{m_n}m_n!}}\Omega_q,
\qquad m_n\in\mathbb N_0\text{ with finite support},
$$
are <orthonormal> by commuting positive <fermionic currents on the circle> through negative <fermionic currents on the circle>. Their energies are $q(q-1)/2+\sum_{n>0}nm_n$. It remains to prove that they span, rather than assume the boson correspondence. Count configurations by formal charge $u$ and energy $t$. Empty modes $r\geq0$ can contain one extra particle, while filled modes $-r$, $r\geq1$, can contain one hole. Therefore the fermionic character is
$$
\prod_{r\geq0}(1+ut^r)\prod_{r\geq1}(1+u^{-1}t^r).
$$
The charge form of the <Jacobi triple product> proved in Question 4 identifies this with
$$
\sum_{q\in\mathbb Z}\frac{u^qt^{q(q-1)/2}}{\prod_{n\geq1}(1-t^n)}.
$$
The reciprocal product counts bosonic occupations, or <partitions of an integer>. For fixed charge and energy, the finite-dimensional fermion space has exactly as many basis configurations as the independent <fermionic current on the circle> monomials already constructed. Hence those monomials exhaust every such space, proving their dense completeness. This establishes a unitary identification of every charge sector with a <bosonic Fock space>, and on that identification
$$
\boxed{L_0=\frac{Q(Q-I)}2+\sum_{n>0}n b_n^*b_n.}
$$
The usual half-integer fermion convention instead gives ground energy $q^2/2$ and requires the corresponding adjustment of rotations. Here integer modes were deliberately chosen to obtain a genuine <circle> action with integral nonnegative energies. The two <ground states> of energy zero, at charges zero and one, are therefore consistent, not a negative-energy defect.

This is also a constructive bosonic realization of the loop representation: the mean of $h$ acts by $h_0Q$, nonzero modes of $h$ act as the square-summable oscillator field $\sum_{n>0}\sqrt n(h_n b_n^*+\overline{h_n}b_n)$, and winding shifts the charge sectors. Taking $k$ tensor copies adds the <fermionic current on the circle> central terms and gives level $k\in\mathbb N$. Positivity constrains their sign, since in a vacuum sector $\|J_{-n}\Omega\|^2=kn$ for $n>0$. The level-one construction, its explicit loop cocycle, and its complete bosonic occupation basis show how an abelian classical <loop group> acquires noncommuting quantum currents while retaining positive rotation energy.
