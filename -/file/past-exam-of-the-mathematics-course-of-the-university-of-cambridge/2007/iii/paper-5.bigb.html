A <topological group> is a <group> with a <topology> for which multiplication $G\times G\to G$ and inversion $G\to G$ are continuous. A <profinite group> has either of the following equivalent descriptions: **a <compact> <Hausdorff> <totally disconnected> <topological group>**, or **an <inverse limit> of finite discrete <groups>**. <Totally disconnected> means that every <connected component> is a singleton.

For clarity, an <inverse system> over a <directed set> $I$ consists of <groups> $G_i$ and maps $\phi_{ij}:G_j\to G_i$ for $i\le j$, with $\phi_{ii}=1$ and $\phi_{ik}=\phi_{ij}\phi_{jk}$. Its <inverse limit> is
$$
\varprojlim G_i=\{(g_i)\in\prod_iG_i:\phi_{ij}(g_j)=g_i\text{ whenever }i\le j\},
$$
with coordinatewise multiplication and the <subspace topology>. Equivalently, it is a <group> with compatible continuous projections such that every other compatible family of maps into the $G_i$ factors uniquely through it.

The displayed limit is a <closed subgroup> of the <compact> <Hausdorff> product of the <finite groups>, and is <totally disconnected>, since two different tuples are separated by a <clopen> coordinate condition. Conversely a <compact> <Hausdorff> <totally disconnected> <group> has a <neighbourhood basis> of <open normal subgroups>. One way to see the <group> part of this standard clopen-basis fact is to choose a <clopen> identity <neighbourhood> $U$ inside a prescribed <neighbourhood>. <Compactness> of $U$ and <continuity> give a symmetric identity <neighbourhood> $V$ with $VU\subseteq U$. The stabilizer $H=\{g:gU=U\}$ contains $V$, is open, and is contained in $U$ because $1\in U$. Its index is finite by part (ii); its <normal core> is the intersection of finitely many conjugates and is open and normal. The canonical map
$$
G\longrightarrow\varprojlim_{N\trianglelefteq_oG}G/N
$$
is <injective> because these $N$ separate points, and <surjective> because compatible <cosets> have the <finite intersection property> and $G$ is <compact>. It is a <homeomorphism> by <compactness> and the <Hausdorff> property. This explains the equivalence of the two definitions.

The <profinite Frattini subgroup> is $\Phi(G)=\bigcap M$, where $M$ ranges over all maximal proper <open subgroups>; for the trivial <group> the empty intersection is $G$. For a <pro-p group>,
$$
\boxed{\Phi(G)=\overline{G^p[G,G]}.}
$$
Here powers and <group commutators> mean generated <subgroups> and the bar supplies <closure>. Every maximal <open subgroup> in a <pro-p group> is normal of index $p$, as follows by passing to the <finite p-group> quotient by its <normal core>. Such a <subgroup> therefore contains all powers and <group commutators>. Conversely the quotient by $D=\overline{G^p[G,G]}$ is an abelian <pro-p group> of exponent $p$. If an element of this quotient is nontrivial, some finite <elementary abelian> quotient detects it; a <linear functional> to $\mathbb F_p$ then detects it as well. Its <group homomorphism kernel> pulls back to a maximal <open subgroup> of $G$. Thus the intersection of these kernels is exactly $D$.

We also use the topological generation criterion: a subset generates $G$ topologically if and only if its image generates $G/\Phi(G)$ topologically. To prove the nontrivial direction, a proper closed generated <subgroup> $K$ lies in a proper <open subgroup> $KN$ for a suitably small open normal $N$, and hence in a maximal <open subgroup> obtained from the finite quotient $G/N$. Since that maximal <subgroup> contains $\Phi(G)$, the image of $K$ cannot generate the <Frattini quotient>.

Now suppose $G$ is a nontrivial procyclic <pro-p group>. If it had distinct maximal <open subgroups> $M_1,M_2$, the <homomorphism> to $C_p\times C_p$ induced by the two quotient maps would be onto: its projections are onto, and a proper such <subgroup> would be a one-dimensional graph, forcing the two kernels to coincide. Hence $G/(M_1\cap M_2)\cong C_p^2$, contradicting procyclicity. Thus there is exactly one maximal <open subgroup> $M$, and $\Phi(G)=M$. Any $g\notin M$ generates $G/\Phi(G)\cong C_p$, so the criterion gives
$$
\boxed{G=\overline{\langle g\rangle}.}
$$
For the trivial <group> use its identity as generator. **The additive <group> $\mathbb Z_p$ is an infinite example**, topologically generated by $1$: its finite continuous quotients are cyclic $\mathbb Z/p^n\mathbb Z$.

Suppose $(L,(\pi_i))$ and $(L',(\pi'_i))$ are <inverse limits> of the same system, in the universal-property sense. The compatible family $(\pi'_i)$ induces a unique continuous <homomorphism> $u:L'\to L$ with $\pi_i u=\pi'_i$; similarly there is a unique $v:L\to L'$ with $\pi'_i v=\pi_i$. Then $uv$ and $1_L$ both induce the projections $(\pi_i)$, so uniqueness gives $uv=1_L$. Likewise $vu=1_{L'}$. Thus **the limits are uniquely isomorphic by an <isomorphism> preserving every projection**. In particular the inverse maps are continuous, so this is an <isomorphism> of <topological groups>, not just abstract <groups>.

For odd $p$, a <subgroup> $N$ is <powerfully embedded> in $G$ if $[N,G]\le N^p$, where $N^p=\langle n^p:n\in N\rangle$. The condition implies normality since $n^g=n[n,g]\in N$. The <group> $G$ is a <powerful p-group> if $[G,G]\le G^p$, equivalently if $G$ itself is <powerfully embedded>. For a <finite p-group>, the <Burnside basis theorem> gives $d(Q)=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}Q/\Phi(Q)$, and $\Phi(Q)=Q^p[Q,Q]$. Thus for powerful $Q$, $d(Q)=\dim Q/Q^p$.

We prove $d(H)\le d(G)$ by induction on $|G|$, with the trivial <group> immediate. The supplied embedding result applied to $N=G$ makes $G^p$ <powerfully embedded>, and hence powerful. It is a proper <subgroup> when $G\ne1$, because $G^p=\Phi(G)$ is proper in a nontrivial <finite p-group>. Set
$$
K=H\cap G^p,\quad V=G/G^p,\quad W=G^p/G^{p^2},\quad A=HG^p/G^p\le V.
$$
All the displayed quotients are <elementary abelian>. The supplied onto <homomorphism> $\theta:V\to W$, $xG^p\mapsto x^pG^{p^2}$, is therefore linear. Write $v=\dim V=d(G)$, $w=\dim W=d(G^p)$, $r=\dim A$, and $t=\dim\theta(A)$.

Since $H/K$ embeds in $V$, $\Phi(H)\le K$. Also
$$
\Phi(K)\le\Phi(H),\qquad\Phi(K)=K^p[K,K]\le(G^p)^p[G^p,G^p]=G^{p^2}.
$$
Here $(G^p)^p=G^{p^2}$ is the iterated-power convention, valid for powerful <groups>. Hence the natural map $K/\Phi(K)\to W$ is defined. The image under this map of the subspace $\Phi(H)/\Phi(K)$ contains $\theta(A)$, because for every $h\in H$ its power $h^p$ lies in $\Phi(H)\cap K$. It follows that
$$
\dim K/\Phi(H)\le d(K)-t.
$$
The <exact sequence> $1\to K/\Phi(H)\to H/\Phi(H)\to H/K\to1$ consists of <elementary abelian groups>, and $\dim H/K=r$. By induction inside $G^p$, $d(K)\le d(G^p)=w$. Therefore
$$
d(H)=r+\dim K/\Phi(H)\le r+w-t=w+\dim\ker(\theta|_A)\le w+\dim\ker\theta=v.
$$
This proves **$d(H)\le d(G)$**, including <subgroups> $H$ that are not themselves powerful.

The <rank of a profinite group> is $\operatorname{rk}(P)=\sup\{d(K):K\le P\text{ closed}\}$, where $d(K)$ is its least topological generator number. If $P$ has finite rank then every <closed subgroup>, including the <open subgroup> $H$, has no larger rank. Conversely suppose $H$ has rank $r<\infty$. Its <normal core> $C=\bigcap_{g\in P}H^g$ is an <open normal subgroup>: there are finitely many conjugates because $H$ has finite index. It has rank at most $r$.

For every closed $K\le P$, the <subgroup> $K\cap C$ is normal in $K$ and has at most $r$ topological generators. The quotient $K/(K\cap C)$ embeds in the <finite group> $P/C$. A <finite group> of order at most $M=[P:C]$ has at most $\lfloor\log_2M\rfloor$ generators: in a minimal sequential generating list every strict enlargement at least doubles the <subgroup> order. Lift such quotient generators and adjoin generators of $K\cap C$. Their closed generated <subgroup> contains the <group homomorphism kernel> and maps onto the quotient, so equals $K$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rk}(P)\le r+\lfloor\log_2[P:C]\rfloor<\infty.}
$$
Thus **an <open subgroup> has finite rank exactly when the whole <profinite group> does**.

For a <pro-p group>, the <lower p-series> is the descending sequence of closed <characteristic subgroups>
$$
\boxed{P_1(G)=G,\qquad P_{i+1}(G)=\overline{P_i(G)^p[P_i(G),G]}.}
$$
The bar means <closure> of the <subgroup> generated by the displayed powers and <group commutators>. Throughout this question $p$ is odd. For a finitely generated <powerful pro-p group>, <group commutator> collection gives $P_i(G)=G^{p^{i-1}}$, each $P_i$ is powerful, and $\Phi(P_i)=P_{i+1}$. All these <subgroups> are open. A <uniform pro-p group> is such a <group> with all <subgroup> indices $[P_i:P_{i+1}]$ equal to $[G:P_2]$. We use the standard powerful-group torsion criterion: a finitely generated <powerful pro-p group> is uniform exactly when it is a <torsion-free group>. For a uniform <group> of generator number $d$, the indices are $p^d$, and $x\mapsto x^{p^a}$ is a <homeomorphism> from $G$ onto $G^{p^a}$.

We record explicitly the collection facts used for the operations below. With $U_b=G^{p^b}=P_{b+1}$, one has
$$
[U_a,U_b]\le U_{a+b+1},\qquad x\equiv y\pmod{U_b}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x^{p^a}\equiv y^{p^a}\pmod{U_{a+b}}.
$$
The second statement is the power-congruence property: the $p$th-power map is an <isomorphism> on adjacent uniform layers, and collection gives $(yu)^p\equiv y^pu^p\pmod{U_{b+2}}$ for $u\in U_b$. Thus the first nonzero layer of a difference moves down exactly one step under a $p$th power. Iterating gives the asserted equivalence and also justifies taking roots of congruences. These facts follow from powerful-group <group commutator> collection, using divisibility by $p$ of $\binom p2$ and the successive-layer power isomorphisms; they are the uniform-group structure results being used here.

The <transported addition on a uniform pro-p group> is
$$
\boxed{x+_ny=(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})^{p^{-n}}\qquad(n\ge1).}
$$
The product is in $U_n$ and has a unique $p^n$th root in $G$. For the comparison at $n=1$ define $x+_0y=xy$. The numbered proofs below establish <associativity>, approximate <commutativity> and compatibility of these operations.

The intrinsic <Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group> has underlying set $G$, zero equal to the <group> identity, additive inverse $x^{-1}$, and operations
$$
\boxed{x+_Ly=\lim_{n\to\infty}(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})^{p^{-n}},\qquad [x,y]_L=\lim_{n\to\infty}[x^{p^n},y^{p^n}]^{p^{-2n}},\qquad \lambda x=x^\lambda\ (\lambda\in\mathbb Z_p).}
$$
Here the <group commutator> is $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$. For $\lambda\in\mathbb Z_p$, $x^\lambda$ is the limit of $x^{m_j}$ for integers $m_j\to\lambda$; the pro-$p$ <topology> makes it well defined. The addition sequence is <Cauchy> by the compatibility proved below. The <group commutator> estimate puts $[x^{p^n},y^{p^n}]$ in $U_{2n+1}$, so its displayed root exists and lies in $U_1$. Collection of <group commutators> shows that successive bracket approximants differ in layers tending to the identity, giving the other limit. The <group commutator> identities give additivity of the limiting bracket, and the <Hall-Witt identity> gives its <Jacobi identity>. Approximate <commutativity> makes limiting addition <commutative>. The resulting lattice $L_G$ is a <free module> over $\mathbb Z_p$ of rank $d(G)$, and $[L_G,L_G]\le pL_G$.

A <powerful Lie algebra over p-adic integers>, in the convention relevant here, is a <Lie algebra> $L$ whose underlying <module> is finite free over $\mathbb Z_p$ and whose bracket satisfies $[L,L]\le pL$. For $p=2$ the condition is instead $[L,L]\le4L$. For odd $p$, define multiplication on its underlying set by the convergent <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula>
$$
x*y=\operatorname{BCH}(x,y)=x+y+\frac12[x,y]+\frac1{12}\bigl([x,[x,y]]+[y,[y,x]]\bigr)+\cdots.
$$
An iterated bracket of degree $m$ lies in $p^{m-1}L$. The BCH coefficient bound loses at most $\lfloor(m-1)/(p-1)\rfloor$ powers of $p$, so all higher-degree terms are integral, and their valuations tend to infinity. This explains both convergence and why multiplying a lattice with small bracket stays inside the lattice. Formal <associativity> of BCH passes to the convergent series, the identity is $0$, the inverse is $-x$, and every <group> power satisfies $x^{*m}=mx$. The quotients by $p^aL$ are <finite p-groups>. Hence the resulting <compact group> is a finitely generated <torsion-free group> and a <pro-p group>, and its <group commutator> lies in $pL$. Thus **$(L,*)$ is a <uniform pro-p group>**, with $P_i(L,*)=p^{i-1}L$ and successive index $p^{\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Z_p}L}$.

The standard uniform-group/Lie-lattice correspondence identifies these constructions as mutually inverse: **$L_{(L,*)}\cong L$ and $(L_G,*)\cong G$**. To see why the <inverse limits> recover the lattice operations, the addition approximant for a BCH <group> is $p^{-n}\operatorname{BCH}(p^nx,p^ny)$: its linear term is $x+y$ and every higher term tends to zero. Similarly its <group commutator> approximant has limiting term $[x,y]$. In the other direction, a local <logarithm> in the complete normed <group algebra> identifies the intrinsic operations with algebra addition and <commutator> and identifies <group> multiplication with BCH; the uniform power maps extend the identification from a sufficiently small power <subgroup> to all of $G$. Continuous <group homomorphisms> respect powers, unique roots and limits, while <Lie algebra homomorphisms> respect every BCH term, so the equivalence also preserves morphisms.

Suppose first that $G$ is uniform and $H$ is a powerful <open subgroup>. The torsion criterion stated above makes $G$ a <torsion-free group>; so $H$ is a <torsion-free group> too. It is finitely generated, for example by the <Schreier lemma>, and hence is uniform by the same criterion. Put $d=d(G)$ and $e=d(H)$, and choose $k$ with $P_k(G)\le H$. For every $n\ge0$,
$$
P_{n+k}(G)\le H^{p^n}\le G^{p^n}=P_{n+1}(G).
$$
Uniformity of the two <groups> gives
$$
p^{nd}\le [G:H^{p^n}]=[G:H]p^{ne}\le p^{(n+k-1)d}.
$$
Taking logarithms and dividing by $n$, then letting $n\to\infty$, proves $e=d$. This proves (i)$\Rightarrow$(iii) without assuming that $H$ is a power <subgroup>. Conversely every $P_i(G)$ is powerful and open, so (iii) applied to these <subgroups> gives (ii). Therefore **all three conditions are equivalent**.

The objective is a continuous <faithful representation> of a <uniform pro-p group> $G$ in some $\operatorname{GL}_N(\mathbb Z_p)$. The proof proceeds from the intrinsic Lie lattice to a small <matrix group>, then extends the resulting representation to all of $G$. This last step matters: a representation defined only on an <open subgroup> does not yet establish linearity of the original <group>.

Let $L=L_G$ be the <Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group> and put $\mathfrak g=\mathbb Q_p\otimes_{\mathbb Z_p}L$. The uniform-group/Lie-lattice correspondence identifies the <group> law on $L$ with BCH. The substantial finite-dimensional Lie-theoretic input is the <Ado theorem>: every finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> over a characteristic-zero <field> admits a faithful finite-dimensional representation. Apply it to obtain
$$
\rho:\mathfrak g\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb Q_p}(V),\qquad\rho([x,y])=\rho(x)\rho(y)-\rho(y)\rho(x).
$$
An <adjoint representation> would not suffice here, since it kills the centre; Ado supplies <injectivity> even in central directions.

Choose a <p-adic lattice> $\Lambda$ spanning $V$. Since $L$ has finitely many lattice generators, their representing <matrices> have bounded denominators. There is therefore an integer $a\ge0$ such that
$$
\rho(p^aL)\subseteq p^\epsilon\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb Z_p}(\Lambda),\qquad\epsilon=\begin{cases}1,&p\ne2,\\2,&p=2.\end{cases}
$$
On this <matrix> lattice the series $\exp X=\sum_{n\ge0}X^n/n!$ and $\log(1+Y)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}Y^n/n$ converge and are inverse. For example $v_p(n!)\le n/(p-1)$, so the valuations of the exponential terms tend to infinity with this choice of $\epsilon$; the <logarithm> terms do as well. They take values in $1+p^\epsilon\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$ and in $p^\epsilon\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$ respectively. The formal identity $\exp(\operatorname{BCH}(X,Y))=\exp X\exp Y$ holds for the convergent series.

Under the correspondence, $U=G^{p^a}$ has Lie lattice $p^aL$ and is an open <characteristic subgroup>. Define
$$
\eta:U\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda),\qquad\eta(u)=\exp(\rho(\log_Gu)),
$$
where $\log_G$ denotes the identification of $U$ with $p^aL$. A Lie <homomorphism> preserves BCH, so the exponential identity proves $\eta(uv)=\eta(u)\eta(v)$. If $\eta(u)=I$, the <matrix> <logarithm> gives $\rho(\log_Gu)=0$; faithfulness of $\rho$ gives $u=1$. Thus $\eta$ is a continuous faithful integral representation of $U$.

To extend it, take the <module>
$$
W=\{f:G\to\Lambda:f(ux)=\eta(u)f(x)\text{ for all }u\in U,\ x\in G\}.
$$
Values on a transversal of $U\backslash G$ determine $f$, so $W$ is a <free module> over $\mathbb Z_p$ of rank $[G:U]\dim V$. These functions are continuous, since on each open <coset> they are given by the continuous representation $\eta$. Define $(T_gf)(x)=f(xg)$. The equivariance condition is preserved, and $T_gT_h=T_{gh}$. In a transversal <basis> each $T_g$ is a permutation of blocks followed by <matrices> from $\eta(U)$, so it belongs to $\operatorname{GL}(W)$ and depends continuously on $g$.

Faithfulness can be checked directly. If $g\notin U$, right multiplication moves the <coset> $U$ to a different <coset>, so $T_g$ moves a function supported on one block and cannot be the identity. If $g\in U$ and $T_g=1$, evaluation at $1$ gives $f(g)=f(1)$ for every $f$, whereas equivariance gives $f(g)=\eta(g)f(1)$. Since $f(1)$ is arbitrary, $\eta(g)=I$, and hence $g=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{G\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{[G:U]\dim V}(\mathbb Z_p).}
$$
<Compactness> of $G$ makes this continuous injection a <homeomorphism> onto its closed image. This proves the <linearity of a uniform pro-p group> for the full <group>, including its centre, and explains why taking a sufficiently small power lattice permits <matrix> integration before finite-index induction restores the whole <group>.

Write $k=\mathbb F_p$. The <Nottingham group> $\mathcal N$ is the set of formal series $f(t)=t+\sum_{i\ge2}a_it^i$ over $k$, with ordinary composition $f\circ g$ as multiplication and the $t$-adic <topology>. The identity is $t$. A compositional inverse exists uniquely by solving its coefficients successively, since the linear coefficient is $1$.

Equivalently $\mathcal N$ is the <group> of continuous $k$-automorphisms of $k((t))$ whose action on $(t)/(t^2)$ is the identity. Such an automorphism is determined by the image of $t$, which is $t+O(t^2)$. To identify this with ordinary series composition without reversing multiplication, send $f$ to the automorphism $h(t)\mapsto h(f^{-1}(t))$. Its composite with the automorphism for $g$ is the one for $f\circ g$.

The depth filtration is
$$
\mathcal N_m=\{f:f(t)\equiv t\pmod{t^{m+1}}\},\qquad m\ge1.
$$
Each $\mathcal N_m$ is open and normal, and the coefficient of $t^{m+1}$ identifies $\mathcal N_m/\mathcal N_{m+1}$ with $(k,+)$. Thus $[\mathcal N:\mathcal N_m]=p^{m-1}$, and $\mathcal N$ is the <inverse limit> of its <finite p-group> truncations.

Here is a local-field proof that **every <finite p-group> embeds in $\mathcal N$**. We first establish the realization step rather than assume the Nottingham embedding itself. Put $F=k((t))$ and $\Gamma=\operatorname{Gal}(F^{\mathrm{sep}}/F)$, and let $I\trianglelefteq\Gamma$ be the inertia <subgroup>, the <group homomorphism kernel> of the action on the <algebraic closure> of the <residue field>. We use the standard additive <normal basis theorem> consequence $H^j(\Gamma,F^{\mathrm{sep}})=0$ for $j>0$. It follows by taking direct limits of the finite-Galois normal-basis <modules>, whose additive <cohomology> vanishes. The Artin-Schreier sequence
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathbb F_p\longrightarrow F^{\mathrm{sep}}\xrightarrow{z\mapsto z^p-z}F^{\mathrm{sep}}\longrightarrow0
$$
then gives
$$
H^1(\Gamma,\mathbb F_p)=F/\{z^p-z:z\in F\},\qquad H^2(\Gamma,\mathbb F_p)=0.
$$
The classes $t^{-m}$ for positive $m$ prime to $p$ are <linearly independent>: a nonzero finite linear combination has a most negative <valuation> prime to $p$, whereas a negative <valuation> of $z^p-z$ is divisible by $p$. Thus there are infinitely many independent continuous characters $\Gamma\to C_p$.

Induct on the order of a <finite p-group> $E$. Choose a central <subgroup> $C\cong C_p$ and put $Q=E/C$. By induction there is an onto map $\phi:\Gamma\to Q$ with $\phi(I)=Q$, corresponding to a <totally ramified> $Q$-extension. The central extension $1\to C\to E\to Q\to1$ is specified by a <two-cocycle>. Its pullback along $\phi$ is a <coboundary> because $H^2(\Gamma,\mathbb F_p)=0$. Correcting a set-theoretic section by that <coboundary> gives a continuous <homomorphism> $\rho_0:\Gamma\to E$ lifting $\phi$.

We must make the lift onto on inertia, not merely onto on the whole <Galois group>. Let $A=I\cap\ker\phi$. On $A$, the lift $\rho_0$ takes values in $C$. Characters vanishing on $A$ form a finite-dimensional space: $\Gamma/A$ has a finite <subgroup> $I/A\cong Q$ and procyclic quotient $\Gamma/I\cong\widehat{\mathbb Z}$, so a character is determined by finitely many values on generators of these two pieces. The infinite-dimensional character space therefore has infinitely many distinct restrictions to $A$. Choose a character $\chi:\Gamma\to C$ whose restriction to $A$ does not cancel $\rho_0|_A$, and set $\rho(\gamma)=\chi(\gamma)\rho_0(\gamma)$. Centrality of $C$ makes this another <homomorphism> lifting $\phi$. Its image on $A$ is nontrivial and hence all of $C$, while its image on $I$ maps onto $Q$. Therefore $\rho(I)=E$. The fixed <field> of $\ker\rho$ is the required <totally ramified> finite <Galois extension> $K/F$ with <group> $E$. This proves the realization step for every <finite p-group>.

Since the <residue field> of $K$ is still $k$, choosing a <uniformizer> $u$ identifies $K$ with $k((u))$. Indeed successive subtraction of its residue coefficient and division by $u$ gives each element of its <valuation ring> a unique convergent expansion $\sum_{i\ge0}b_iu^i$ with $b_i\in k$, and fractions give the Laurent expansions. Each element of $E$ preserves the <valuation> and fixes $k$, so sends $u$ to $a_1u+a_2u^2+\cdots$. The linear coefficient is a <homomorphism> $E\to k^\times$. Since $E$ is a $p$-group and $|k^\times|=p-1$, that <homomorphism> is trivial. Thus the faithful Galois action lies in $\mathcal N(k)$, completing the embedding proof.

An infinite <profinite group> is <hereditarily just infinite> when every <open subgroup> is infinite and every nontrivial closed <normal subgroup> of each such <open subgroup> is open. We sketch the normal-subgroup argument, giving the depth calculation in odd characteristic explicitly. For series of depths $r,s$,
$$
f=t+at^{r+1}+\cdots,\quad g=t+bt^{s+1}+\cdots\quad\Longrightarrow\quad[f,g]=t+ab(r-s)t^{r+s+1}+O(t^{r+s+2}),
$$
where $[f,g]=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}\circ f\circ g$. The coefficient follows by comparing $f\circ g$ and $g\circ f$ in degree $r+s+1$: their cross terms are $ab(r+1)$ and $ab(s+1)$.

Let $H$ be open and $1\ne K\trianglelefteq H$ closed. Choose $m$ with $\mathcal N_m\le H$ and choose $f\in K$ of depth $r$. For every $j\ge m$ with $j\not\equiv r\pmod p$, commutation with $t+bt^{j+1}$ supplies an element of $K$ of depth $r+j$, with any prescribed nonzero <leading coefficient> by varying $b$. Thus every sufficiently high layer except possibly depths congruent to $2r$ modulo $p$ occurs in $K$.

When $p$ is odd, choose one available depth $u\ge r+m$ with $u\not\equiv2r,r\pmod p$. Such a residue exists even for $p=3$. An element $h\in K$ of that depth can be commuted with $t+bt^{k-u+1}$ for every sufficiently large missing depth $k\equiv2r\pmod p$. The new <leading coefficient> contains $2u-k\equiv2(u-r)\ne0$, so those missing layers occur too. Hence $K$ contains an element with any chosen coefficient in every depth $k\ge M$, for some $M$.

Successive coefficient correction now proves $\mathcal N_M\le K$: match the coefficient of an arbitrary target in depth $M$ by an element of $K$, correct the error in depth $M+1$, and continue. The partial products converge $t$-adically to the target and remain in $K$, which is closed. Thus $K$ is open. This proves hereditary just infiniteness for odd $p$.

For characteristic two, the <leading coefficient> alone misses even depths. The standard characteristic-two normal-closure collection lemma supplies the needed extra input: for every $m\ge1$ and every nonidentity $f\in\mathcal N(\mathbb F_2)$, the <closed subgroup> generated by the conjugates $f^g$ with $g\in\mathcal N_m$ contains $\mathcal N_M$ for some $M$. Its proof retains the next coefficients in pairs of <group commutators> to fill the parity gaps, then uses the same successive coefficient correction. Apply this lemma to $f\in K$ and $\mathcal N_m\le H$: every such conjugate lies in $K$, so $K$ contains the tail and is open. This states the additional collection result used in the characteristic-two sketch; the odd-prime leading-term argument is not being applied there. Since every <open subgroup> contains a tail and is infinite, **the <Nottingham group> is <hereditarily just infinite> for every prime $p$**.

= Solution

The first alternative in the supplied analytic-group theorem would give a <faithful representation> of $\mathcal N$ in some $\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb Z_p)$ or $\operatorname{GL}_d(\mathbb F_p[[t]])$. The embedding proved above supplies cyclic <subgroups> of order $p^a$ for arbitrarily large $a$. Such orders are impossible in either fixed <matrix> <dimension>.

In <characteristic zero>, a <matrix> $A$ of order $p^a$ is diagonalizable over an algebraic <closure>, because its <minimal polynomial> divides the <separable polynomial> $X^{p^a}-1$. At least one <eigenvalue> must be a primitive $p^a$th <root of unity>; otherwise $A^{p^{a-1}}=I$. The <cyclotomic polynomial> $\Phi_{p^a}$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q_p$, since $\Phi_{p^a}(1+X)$ satisfies the <Eisenstein criterion> at $p$. Consequently that <eigenvalue> has degree $\varphi(p^a)=p^{a-1}(p-1)$ over $\mathbb Q_p$, which must be at most the <matrix> <dimension> $d$. This bounds $a$.

In characteristic $p$, pass from the power-series ring to its fraction <field>. A <matrix> of $p$-power order satisfies $X^{p^a}-1=(X-1)^{p^a}$, so it is unipotent. Writing $A=I+B$, nilpotence gives $B^d=0$. For $p^e\ge d$,
$$
A^{p^e}=I+B^{p^e}=I.
$$
Thus its order is at most $p^{\lceil\log_pd\rceil}$, again a fixed bound. Therefore **the linear alternative is impossible** in both coefficient rings.

We next bound the closed <derived series>. For odd $p$, the depth calculation yields
$$
[\mathcal N_m,\mathcal N_m]=\mathcal N_{2m+1}.
$$
For the inclusion from left to right, two series of depth at least $m$ commute modulo $\mathcal N_{2m+1}$: at depth $2m$ the only possible leading pair has equal depths and its coefficient vanishes. For the reverse inclusion, every depth $k\ge2m+1$ can be written $r+s=k$ with $r,s\ge m$ and $r\not\equiv s\pmod p$. Start with $r=m,s=k-m$; if their difference vanishes modulo $p$, replace them by $r=m+1,s=k-m-1$, which is allowed except at $k=2m+1$, where the original difference is already nonzero. The difference changes by $2$, nonzero for odd $p$. <Commutators> thus supply every high <leading coefficient>, and closed successive coefficient correction fills the tail.

With $\mathcal N^{(0)}=\mathcal N_1$, induction on $n$ gives
$$
\mathcal N^{(n)}=\mathcal N_{2^{n+1}-1},\qquad\log_p[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]=2^{n+1}-2.
$$
For $p=2$ use the standard characteristic-two derived-series collection bound, explicitly stated as an additional result:
$$
\log_2[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]\le C\kappa^n,\qquad\kappa=\frac{1+\sqrt{17}}2<3,
$$
for a fixed constant $C$. One can take a fixed enlarged constant from the tail estimate $\mathcal N^{(n)}\supseteq\mathcal N_{\lceil4\kappa^n\rceil+1}$. The more involved two-coefficient collection is necessary in characteristic two; the exact odd-prime tail formula is not asserted there.

In either case there are constants $C'>0$ and $\lambda<3$ such that $\log_p[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]\le C'\lambda^n$. If an analytic structure existed, its <dimension> $d$ would be positive, since a zero-dimensional analytic <pro-p group> is discrete and <compact>, hence finite. The second alternative of the supplied theorem would require
$$
\log_p[\mathcal N:\mathcal N^{(n)}]\ge d\,3^{n-s}
$$
for all sufficiently large $n$, for some fixed $s$. Combining the bounds gives $d3^{-s}\le C'(\lambda/3)^n$, impossible as $n\to\infty$.

The root solution proved hereditary just infiniteness, so the supplied theorem applies. Part (i) excluded its linear alternative, and this calculation excludes its growth alternative. Hence **the <Nottingham group> is not analytic over any pro-$p$ ring**.
