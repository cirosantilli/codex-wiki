= Solution
{parent=1}

Let $I=(U')^{-1}$ be the <inverse marginal utility>. The <derivative> $U'$ is continuous and strictly decreasing: concavity makes it nonincreasing, and equality at two distinct points would make $U$ affine between them, contradicting <strict concavity>. The <Inada conditions> therefore make $U'$ a bijection from $(0,\infty)$ onto $(0,\infty)$. For each $y>0$, the <derivative> of $U(x)-xy$ is positive below $I(y)$ and negative above it. Consequently its unique global maximum is finite and
$$
V(y)=U(I(y))-yI(y).
$$
There is no need to differentiate $I$ to find the first <derivative> of this <utility conjugate>. Comparing the maxima at $y$ and $y+h$ gives, for $h>0$,
$$
-hI(y)\leq V(y+h)-V(y)\leq-hI(y+h).
$$
Continuity of $I$, together with the corresponding inequalities for $h<0$, gives
$$
\boxed{V'(y)=-I(y),\qquad V'(0+)=-\infty,\qquad V'(\infty)=0.}
$$
Thus $V$ is strictly decreasing and <strictly convex>, since its <derivative> is strictly increasing. This proves all the asserted properties except the second <differentiability>, which does not follow from the printed hypotheses.

Here is an explicit <Inada utility with vanishing curvature>. Put
$$
w(s)=\frac{(s-1)^2}{s^2(s+1)^2},\qquad p(x)=\int_x^\infty w(s)\,ds=\frac1x+\frac4{x+1}+4\log\frac{x}{x+1},\qquad U(x)=\int_1^x p(r)\,dr.
$$
The positive integral defining $p$ makes $U$ strictly increasing. Also $p'=-w$ is negative except at one point, so $p$ is strictly decreasing and $U$ is <strictly concave>. Near zero $w(s)$ is asymptotic to $s^{-2}$, and at infinity it is asymptotic to $s^{-2}$; thus $p(0+)=\infty$ and $p(\infty)=0$. This smooth <utility function> satisfies every printed positive-domain assumption, but $U''(1)=0$. At $y_0=p(1)=3-4\log2$, differentiability of $I$ would imply, by differentiating $p(I(y))=y$,
$$
0\cdot I'(y_0)=1,
$$
which is impossible. Hence $V'=-I$ has no finite <derivative> there. More explicitly, $p(x)-p(1)=-(x-1)^3/12+o((x-1)^3)$, so its <inverse function> has a cube-root singularity. **The twice-differentiable conclusion is false as printed.** With the additional assumption $U''(x)<0$ everywhere, the <inverse function theorem> proves the intended <dual differentiability with nonvanishing utility curvature>:
$$
\boxed{V''(y)=-\frac1{U''(I(y))}>0.}
$$

For the financial assertions, use the positive-wealth <admissible trading strategy> class appropriate to the positive-domain <utility function>, and expectations for which the expressions are defined. A normalized terminal <state-price density> satisfies $Z_N>0$, $\mathbb EZ_N=1$, and defines an <equivalent martingale measure> $Q$ by $dQ=Z_N\,dP$. A <self-financing portfolio> obeys
$$
X_n=x+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\pi_k\cdot(S_{k+1}-S_k),
$$
where the holdings are measurable before the next price increment. Under $Q$ the gains are a <martingale> when integrable; for the usual lower-bounded admissible class, localization gives a <supermartingale>. In particular the <state-price budget constraint> is $\mathbb E[Z_NX_N]\leq x$. The positive-domain qualification matters: assumptions only on $U$ at positive arguments do not control its values at nonpositive wealth.

The definition of the <utility conjugate> gives the pointwise inequality
$$
U(X_N)\leq V(yZ_N)+yZ_NX_N.
$$
Taking expectations, using the <state-price budget constraint>, and then taking the supremum over strategies gives $u(x)\leq\mathbb EV(yZ_N)+yx$ for every $y>0$ and every <state-price density>. Taking the two infima proves the <utility duality with martingale deflators> upper bound:
$$
\boxed{u(x)\leq\inf_{y>0}\{v(y)+xy\}.}
$$
If an expectation is infinite, use the same inequality in the extended-real sense whenever the negative parts make it well defined; no subtraction of two infinities is needed.

A <complete market> is one in which every terminal <contingent claim> in the specified claim class is attainable by a <self-financing portfolio>. Bounded terminal claims suffice for the uniqueness argument. In the standard finite-horizon discrete-time market, absence of <arbitrage> gives existence of an <equivalent martingale measure> by the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing>. To prove uniqueness, let $Z_N$ and $\widetilde Z_N$ be two normalized <state-price densities>. Every bounded claim $H$ has a replicating price $p(H)$, and its replication has the same price under either <equivalent martingale measure>:
$$
\mathbb E[Z_NH]=p(H)=\mathbb E[\widetilde Z_NH].
$$
Take $H=\mathbf1_A$ for every terminal event $A$. The integrable difference $Z_N-\widetilde Z_N$ has zero integral over every event, hence vanishes almost surely: its positive and negative sets would otherwise give a nonzero integral. **The normalized terminal state-price density is unique up to null sets.** Its density process is then uniquely $Z_n=\mathbb E[Z_N\mid\mathcal F_n]$. This uses the usual pricing replication class, where bounded claims have bounded replicating value processes; arbitrary doubling strategies are not an admissible definition of completeness.

For <logarithmic utility>, direct maximization gives $V(y)=-\log y-1$. In the <complete market>, write $Z=Z_N$. The unique-density <utility conjugate> bound is minimized at $y=1/x$, giving
$$
u(x)\leq\log x-\mathbb E\log Z.
$$
The candidate terminal <portfolio wealth> is $H^*=x/Z$, whose cost is exactly $\mathbb E[ZH^*]=x$. If completeness includes nonnegative $Q$-integrable claims, it is replicable and attains the bound. There is also an approximation proof using only bounded-claim completeness. Define
$$
b_n=\mathbb E\frac{Z}{Z\vee n^{-1}},\qquad H_n=\frac{x}{b_n(Z\vee n^{-1})}.
$$
Then $0<b_n\leq1$, $b_n\uparrow1$, $H_n$ is positive and bounded, and $\mathbb E[ZH_n]=x$. Replicating $H_n$ therefore gives an admissible terminal claim of initial cost $x$. Since $\mathbb E(\log Z)^+\leq\mathbb EZ=1$, the negative parts of $-\log(Z\vee n^{-1})$ have an integrable common bound. <Monotone convergence> after adding this lower bound gives
$$
\mathbb E\log H_n=\log x-\log b_n-\mathbb E\log(Z\vee n^{-1})\longrightarrow\log x-\mathbb E\log Z.
$$
Consequently the <logarithmic terminal wealth in a complete market> is
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\log x-\mathbb E\log Z_N,\qquad H^*=\frac{x}{Z_N}.}
$$
The value may be $+\infty$ if $\mathbb E(\log Z_N)^-=\infty$; the displayed formula remains meaningful, while attainment by $H^*$ depends on the precise admissible claim class.

Write $A=\sigma\sigma^T$ and let $V(x,t)$ denote the optimal remaining expected reward from positive <portfolio wealth> $x$ at time $t$. The <Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation> and terminal condition are
$$
\boxed{V_t+\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb R^d,\ c\geq0}\left\{(\theta^T\mu-c)V_x+\frac12\theta^TA\theta\,V_{xx}+U_{\mathrm{consumption}}(c)\right\}=0,\qquad V(x,T)=U_{\mathrm{wealth}}(x).}
$$
The controls are progressively measurable, the investment integrals exist, consumption is nonnegative and locally integrable, and the resulting <portfolio wealth> stays in the utility domain. At zero consumption use the right limit of the <utility function> if needed. This is the usual <admissible trading strategy> formulation; without restrictions preserving the domain the reward need not be defined.

For the verification bound, assume $V$ is a nonnegative classical solution, continuous through the terminal time, with one time <derivative> and two wealth <derivatives> in the interior. Fix any admissible controls and put
$$
R_t=V(X_t,t)+\int_0^tU_{\mathrm{consumption}}(C_s)\,ds.
$$
The <Itô formula> gives
$$
dR_t=\left[V_t+(\theta_t^T\mu-C_t)V_x+\frac12\theta_t^TA\theta_tV_{xx}+U_{\mathrm{consumption}}(C_t)\right]dt+V_x\theta_t^T\sigma\,dW_t.
$$
The drift is nonpositive because the actual controls cannot exceed the supremum in the <Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation>. Localize by <stopping times> on which the <stochastic integral> is a true <martingale>, the derivatives and wealth stay in their interior domain, and the accumulated reward is finite. For each such stop $\tau_n$,
$$
\mathbb E R_{T\wedge\tau_n}\leq V(X_0,0).
$$
Both utilities and $V$ are nonnegative. Thus the <Fatou lemma>, continuity, and the terminal condition give
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left[U_{\mathrm{wealth}}(X_T)+\int_0^T U_{\mathrm{consumption}}(C_s)\,ds\right]\leq V(X_0,0).}
$$
For admissible paths strictly positive on the closed horizon, the domain-localizing stops eventually reach $T$. If zero wealth is allowed, the same proof uses the continuous boundary extension of $V$ and the utilities there. Nonnegativity is what permits removing the stops without assuming the uncontrolled stochastic term already has zero expectation. This is <verification by a nonnegative control supermartingale>.

The square-root case needs an additional no-<arbitrage> qualification because the printed volatility matrix need not be invertible. If there is $v\in\ker\sigma^T$ with $v^T\mu>0$, investment $\theta=Kv$ has zero volatility and arbitrarily large positive drift. With any fixed positive consumption, sufficiently large $K$ preserves positive wealth and makes terminal square-root utility tend to infinity. For example, in one dimension $\sigma=0$, $\mu=1$, $\theta=K$, $C=1$ gives $X_s=x+(K-1)(s-t)$, so the value is infinite when $T>t$. **A finite separated solution does not exist for all matrices allowed by the printed hypotheses.**

The needed condition is $\mu\in\operatorname{Range}A$, equivalent to $\mu$ being orthogonal to $\ker\sigma^T$, because $A$ is a <symmetric matrix> and $\ker A=\ker\sigma^T$. Assume this condition and let $B=A^+$ be its <Moore-Penrose inverse>; if $A$ is invertible, simply use $B=A^{-1}$. Put $q=\mu^TB\mu\geq0$. Seek the <square-root investment-consumption value> in the form
$$
V(x,t)=2\sqrt{x}\,g(t),\qquad g(T)=1,\qquad g(t)>0.
$$
Then $V_x=g/\sqrt{x}$ and $V_{xx}=-g/(2x^{3/2})$. Completing the quadratic investment expression, using $AB\mu=\mu$, shows that its maximum is attained at $\theta^*=2xB\mu$ and has value $qg\sqrt{x}$:
$$
\frac{g}{\sqrt{x}}\theta^T\mu-\frac{g}{4x^{3/2}}\theta^TA\theta=qg\sqrt{x}-\frac{g}{4x^{3/2}}(\theta-2xB\mu)^TA(\theta-2xB\mu).
$$
Any investment component in $\ker A$ has no effect. For consumption, set $z=\sqrt c$. Its contribution is the concave quadratic $2z-(g/\sqrt{x})z^2$, whose maximum is $\sqrt{x}/g$ at $c^*=x/g^2$. Substituting both maxima into the <Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation> gives
$$
2g'+qg+\frac1g=0.
$$
With $h=g^2$, this becomes $h'+qh+1=0$, $h(T)=1$. Solving this linear <ordinary differential equation> gives
$$
\boxed{h(t)=\begin{cases}e^{q(T-t)}+\dfrac{e^{q(T-t)}-1}{q},&q>0,\\1+T-t,&q=0,\end{cases}\qquad V(x,t)=2\sqrt{x}\sqrt{h(t)}.}
$$
In particular $h\geq1$, so $g=\sqrt h$ stays positive, and the two controls are
$$
\boxed{\theta_t^*=2X_tB\mu,\qquad C_t^*=\frac{X_t}{h(t)}.}
$$
These controls are admissible and actually attain the value. Indeed their <portfolio wealth> satisfies
$$
\frac{dX_s}{X_s}=\left(2q-\frac1{h(s)}\right)ds+2\mu^TB\sigma\,dW_s.
$$
Since $\lVert2\mu^TB\sigma\rVert^2=4q$, the explicit positive solution starting at $(x,t)$ is
$$
X_s=x\exp\left[-\int_t^s\frac{dr}{h(r)}+2\mu^TB\sigma(W_s-W_t)\right].
$$
Its finite-horizon moments of every positive order are finite. More directly,
$$
\mathbb E\sqrt{X_s}=\sqrt{x}\exp\left[\frac q2(s-t)-\frac12\int_t^s\frac{dr}{h(r)}\right].
$$
Differentiating $g(s)\mathbb E\sqrt{X_s}$ and using the equation for $g$ gives $\frac{d}{ds}(g(s)\mathbb E\sqrt{X_s})=-\mathbb E\sqrt{X_s}/g(s)$. Integrating from $t$ to $T$ therefore yields
$$
\mathbb E\left[2\sqrt{X_T}+\int_t^T2\sqrt{C_s^*}\,ds\right]=2g(t)\sqrt{x}.
$$
This proves attainment without relying on an unjustified expectation of a local <martingale>. When $\mu$ is outside $\operatorname{Range}A$, the <singular-volatility drift arbitrage> counterexample applies instead.

Fix $t>0$. For $0\leq s\leq t$, the residual observed path is
$$
Y_s-\frac{s}{t}Y_t=W_s-\frac{s}{t}W_t.
$$
This <Brownian bridge> is independent of $(\lambda,Y_t)$. Indeed it is independent of $\lambda$, while its <covariance> with $W_t$ is $sI-(s/t)tI=0$; zero <covariance> implies independence in the joint <Gaussian> law. The same argument for every finite collection of bridge times, followed by continuity and generation from rational times, gives independence of the entire bridge. Since the full observed path is determined by its endpoint and this independent bridge, <Brownian endpoint sufficiency for a constant drift> gives
$$
\mathcal L(\lambda\mid\mathcal G_t)=\mathcal L(\lambda\mid Y_t).
$$

First suppose the prior <covariance matrix> $V_0$ is positive definite. The <Bayes theorem> multiplies the prior <Gaussian density> by the conditional observation density, whose dependence on $\lambda$ is $\exp(\lambda^TY_t-t|\lambda|^2/2)$. Thus the posterior exponent, up to terms not depending on $\lambda$, is
$$
-\frac12\lambda^T(V_0^{-1}+tI)\lambda+\lambda^T(V_0^{-1}\lambda_0+Y_t).
$$
Completing the square gives the <Gaussian Brownian drift filter>:
$$
\boxed{\lambda_t=(I+tV_0)^{-1}(\lambda_0+V_0Y_t),\qquad V_t=V_0(I+tV_0)^{-1},\qquad\mathcal L(\lambda\mid\mathcal G_t)=N(\lambda_t,V_t).}
$$
For invertible $V_0$ these are equivalently $V_t=(V_0^{-1}+tI)^{-1}$ and $\lambda_t=V_t(V_0^{-1}\lambda_0+Y_t)$. At $t=0$ they reduce to the prior parameters.

The boxed formulas remain valid for a singular <positive semidefinite matrix> $V_0$. To see this directly without inverse prior matrices, the joint <Gaussian random vector> $(\lambda,Y_t)$ has
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\lambda,Y_t)=tV_0,\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t)=t(I+tV_0).
$$
The latter matrix is invertible for $t>0$. The <conditional multivariate normal distribution> therefore has mean
$$
\lambda_0+V_0(I+tV_0)^{-1}(Y_t-t\lambda_0)=\lambda_t
$$
and <covariance matrix>
$$
V_0-tV_0(I+tV_0)^{-1}V_0=V_0(I+tV_0)^{-1}=V_t.
$$
Alternatively, subtract this mean from $\lambda$; the resulting <Gaussian random vector> has zero <covariance> with $Y_t$ and is therefore independent of it, which proves these conditional parameters.

Write the proposed <innovation process> in the observable form
$$
\widehat W_t=Y_t-\int_0^t\lambda_s\,ds.
$$
It is adapted to the observation <filtration> and continuous. To prove the <martingale> property, take $0\leq u<v$. Future increments of $W$ are independent of the joint information generated by $\lambda$ and the past of $W$, so
$$
\mathbb E[Y_v-Y_u\mid\mathcal G_u]=(v-u)\mathbb E[\lambda\mid\mathcal G_u]=(v-u)\lambda_u.
$$
Also the <tower property of conditional expectation> gives $\mathbb E[\lambda_s\mid\mathcal G_u]=\lambda_u$ for $s\geq u$. Conditional integration is legitimate because $\mathbb E|\lambda_s|^2\leq\mathbb E|\lambda|^2<\infty$. Subtracting the integral proves $\mathbb E[\widehat W_v-\widehat W_u\mid\mathcal G_u]=0$. Removing a continuous <finite variation> process does not change <quadratic covariation>, hence
$$
[\widehat W^i,\widehat W^j]_t=[W^i,W^j]_t=\delta_{ij}t.
$$
The <Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion> now applies: a continuous zero-start <martingale> with this bracket matrix is a standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion> relative to its given <filtration>. **The observable innovation is a Wiener process in the observation filtration.**

Differentiating the posterior formulas yields $V_t'=-V_t^2$ and
$$
d\lambda_t=V_t(dY_t-\lambda_tdt)=V_t\,d\widehat W_t.
$$
For the final expression, the printed inverse matrices require the extra assumption that $V_0$ is positive definite. Under that assumption put $A_t=V_t^{-1}=V_0^{-1}+tI$. The <Itô formula> gives
$$
d(\lambda_t^TA_t\lambda_t)=2\lambda_t^T\,d\widehat W_t+\left(|\lambda_t|^2+\operatorname{tr}V_t\right)dt,
$$
since $A_tV_t=I$ and $\operatorname{tr}(A_tV_t^2)=\operatorname{tr}V_t$. On the other hand,
$$
d\log\det(I+tV_0)=\operatorname{tr}V_t\,dt.
$$
The trace terms cancel in the <innovation exponential for Gaussian drift filtering>, leaving
$$
d\log Z_t=-\lambda_t^T\,d\widehat W_t-\frac12|\lambda_t|^2dt,\qquad\boxed{dZ_t=-Z_t\lambda_t^T\,d\widehat W_t.}
$$
Thus $Z_0=1$ and $Z$ is a positive <stochastic exponential>, hence a <nonnegative local martingale>. To obtain the requested <supermartingale> conclusion explicitly, choose localizing <stopping times> $\tau_n\uparrow\infty$ for which $Z^{\tau_n}$ is a true <martingale>. The <conditional Fatou lemma> gives
$$
\mathbb E[Z_v\mid\mathcal G_u]\leq\liminf_n\mathbb E[Z_{v\wedge\tau_n}\mid\mathcal G_u]=\liminf_nZ_{u\wedge\tau_n}=Z_u.
$$
The same argument at $u=0$ gives $\mathbb EZ_v\leq1$, supplying integrability. **The process is a supermartingale in the observation filtration.** No unproved exponential-integrability condition on the random drift is required.

For a singular prior the literal inverse-matrix expression in the PDF is undefined, but there is a natural extension with the same conclusion. Define, for fixed $y$,
$$
L(t,y)=\mathbb E_{a\sim N(\lambda_0,V_0)}\exp\left(a^Ty-\frac t2|a|^2\right),\qquad Z_t=L(t,Y_t)^{-1}.
$$
For positive definite $V_0$, a <Gaussian integral> shows this reciprocal is exactly the printed expression. For any positive semidefinite $V_0$, the integral is positive and finite, $L_t=-\Delta_yL/2$, and $\nabla_y\log L(t,Y_t)=\lambda_t$, by the same posterior weighting. The <Itô formula> consequently gives $d\log L(t,Y_t)=\lambda_t^T\,dY_t-|\lambda_t|^2dt/2=\lambda_t^T\,d\widehat W_t+|\lambda_t|^2dt/2$. Its reciprocal satisfies the boxed <stochastic differential equation> and the same <supermartingale> proof. This completes the singular-covariance extension while explicitly separating it from the undefined printed formula.

Use the usual stochastic-integrability assumptions implicit in the price <stochastic differential equation>: the coefficients have progressively measurable versions, the volatility is locally square integrable, and initial prices are finite deterministic positive numbers. A no-consumption <admissible trading strategy> consists of <predictable> risky share holdings $\pi$ for which the price <stochastic integral> exists and the <portfolio wealth> of the <self-financing portfolio> is
$$
X_t=x+\int_0^t\pi_s^T\,dS_s.
$$
The remainder $X_t-\pi_t^TS_t$ is held in the unit cash asset. Admissibility requires $X_t\geq-a$ throughout the horizon for some deterministic finite $a$; the more restrictive nonnegative-wealth convention also works. A finite-horizon <arbitrage> is such a strategy with $x=0$, $X_T\geq0$ almost surely and $P(X_T>0)>0$. The lower bound is essential to exclude doubling strategies.

Interpret the stated uniform ellipticity as $\sigma_t\sigma_t^T\geq\varepsilon I$ for a deterministic $\varepsilon>0$. If $|\mu_t|\leq K$, the <market price of risk> is bounded:
$$
\lambda_t=\sigma_t^{-1}\mu_t,\qquad |\lambda_t|^2=\mu_t^T(\sigma_t\sigma_t^T)^{-1}\mu_t\leq\frac{K^2}{\varepsilon}.
$$
The authoritative PDF puts both integrals inside the exponent. With that interpretation,
$$
Z_t=\exp\left(-\int_0^t\lambda_s^T\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\lambda_s|^2ds\right),\qquad dZ_t=-Z_t\lambda_t^T\,dW_t.
$$
On every finite horizon $T$, boundedness of $\lambda$ gives the <Novikov condition>:
$$
\mathbb E\exp\left(\frac12\int_0^T|\lambda_s|^2ds\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{K^2T}{2\varepsilon}\right)<\infty.
$$
Hence the positive <stochastic exponential> $Z$ is a true <martingale> with $\mathbb EZ_T=1$. Define the equivalent probability measure $Q_T$ by $dQ_T=Z_T\,dP$. The <Girsanov theorem> says that $W_t^Q=W_t+\int_0^t\lambda_sds$ is a <Brownian motion> under $Q_T$, up to time $T$. Consequently
$$
dS_t=\operatorname{diag}(S_t)\sigma_t\,dW_t^Q,
$$
so the prices are <local martingales> under $Q_T$.

Every admissible zero-initial-capital <self-financing portfolio> has <portfolio wealth> which is therefore a $Q_T$-<local martingale> bounded below by $-a$. The shifted process $X+a$ is a <nonnegative local martingale>, hence a <supermartingale> by localization and the <conditional Fatou lemma>. In particular $\mathbb E_{Q_T}X_T\leq0$. If the strategy were an <arbitrage>, nonnegativity of $X_T$ would force $X_T=0$ $Q_T$-almost surely. Equivalence of $P$ and $Q_T$ would then force $P(X_T>0)=0$, a contradiction. **The market has no admissible arbitrage on any finite horizon.** This proves the exclusion argument itself, rather than just asserting the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing>.

For the deflated price calculation, let $\sigma_{t,i}$ denote the column vector whose transpose is the $i$th row of $\sigma_t$. The <Itô product rule> includes the <quadratic covariation> term
$$
d[Z,S^i]_t=-Z_tS_t^i\sigma_{t,i}^T\lambda_t\,dt.
$$
Since $\sigma_{t,i}^T\lambda_t=\mu_t^i$, the full product equation is
$$
\begin{aligned}
d(Z_tS_t^i)&=Z_tS_t^i\left[(\mu_t^i-\sigma_{t,i}^T\lambda_t)dt+(\sigma_{t,i}-\lambda_t)^T\,dW_t\right]\\
&=Z_tS_t^i(\sigma_{t,i}-\lambda_t)^T\,dW_t.
\end{aligned}
$$
Localization makes the last <stochastic integral> a true <martingale> on each stopped interval, proving **each component of the deflated price vector is a local martingale under the original measure**. This is the <bounded-coefficient asset deflator> identity; bounded volatility has not yet been used.

If $\sigma$ is bounded, then $\sigma_{t,i}-\lambda_t$ is bounded as well. Solving the last linear <stochastic differential equation> gives
$$
Z_tS_t^i=S_0^i\exp\left(\int_0^t(\sigma_{s,i}-\lambda_s)^T\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^t|\sigma_{s,i}-\lambda_s|^2ds\right).
$$
The <Novikov condition> now holds for this <stochastic exponential> on every finite horizon, because its bracket is bounded by a deterministic constant times the horizon. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Z_tS_t^i\mid\mathcal F_s]=Z_sS_s^i\quad(0\leq s\leq t),\qquad\mathbb E[Z_tS_t^i]=S_0^i.}
$$
**The entire deflated price vector is a true martingale when volatility is bounded.** This finite-horizon argument does not assert uniform integrability as time tends to infinity, which is not required. Integrable random initial prices can also be handled by the conditional martingale argument; without any integrability assumption on initial prices, a true-martingale assertion would not be meaningful.
