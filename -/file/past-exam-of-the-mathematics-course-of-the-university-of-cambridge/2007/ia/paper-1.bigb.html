= Solution
{parent=i}

The three spherical <basis vectors> form an <orthonormal basis>: their displayed Cartesian components have unit lengths and pairwise zero <dot products>. For an <orthonormal basis>, a <vector> is the sum of its projections onto the <basis vectors>. Taking the <dot product> of each Cartesian <unit vector> with each spherical <unit vector> therefore gives the inverse <change of basis>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf i&=\cos\phi\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_r+\cos\phi\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta-\sin\phi\,\mathbf e_\phi,\\
\mathbf j&=\sin\phi\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_r+\sin\phi\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta+\cos\phi\,\mathbf e_\phi,\\
\mathbf k&=\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r-\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Equivalently, the <change-of-basis matrix> is <orthogonal>, so its <matrix inverse> is its transpose. These formulas use the stated angular convention for <spherical polar coordinates>; at a pole the tangential <basis vectors> still depend on the chosen azimuth.

{parent=1a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use <suffix notation>, summing repeated indices, and the <Levi-Civita symbol> convention $(A\times B)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}A_jB_k$. Contracting the outer symbol with the symbol from the second <cross product> gives the <epsilon-delta identity>
$$
\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{kpq}=\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}-\delta_{iq}\delta_{jp},
$$
where $\delta$ is the <Kronecker delta>. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigl((A\times B)\times(A\times C)\bigr)_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{j\ell m}\epsilon_{kpq}A_\ell B_mA_pC_q\\
&=(\delta_{ip}\delta_{jq}-\delta_{iq}\delta_{jp})\epsilon_{j\ell m}A_\ell B_mA_pC_q\\
&=A_i\epsilon_{q\ell m}C_qA_\ell B_m
-C_i\epsilon_{p\ell m}A_pA_\ell B_m\\
&=A_i\epsilon_{\ell mq}A_\ell B_mC_q.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second term is zero because $A_pA_\ell$ is symmetric in $p,\ell$ and the <Levi-Civita symbol> is antisymmetric. The cyclic rearrangement of the remaining symbol has positive sign. Its contraction is the <scalar triple product> $A\cdot(B\times C)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(A\times B)\times(A\times C)=(A\cdot(B\times C))A.}
$$
This <cross products with a common vector> identity requires no independence assumption, so it also covers zero or parallel vectors.

The coefficient <matrix> has <determinant>
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}p&1&1\\1&2&4\\1&4&10\end{pmatrix}
=4p-6+2=4(p-1).
$$
A square <linear system> has a unique solution for every right-hand side precisely when its coefficient <matrix> is an <invertible matrix>. Here **a unique solution exists exactly when $p\ne1$, for every real $t$**.

For an explicit <Gaussian elimination>, subtract twice the second equation from the third, then use the second equation, obtaining $x=2z+2t-t^2$ and $y=(t^2-t)/2-3z$. The first equation determines
$$
z=\frac{2+(2p-1)t^2+(1-4p)t}{4(p-1)},
$$
and the preceding formulas determine $x,y$. When $p=1$ this last coefficient vanishes; the next parts determine compatibility rather than dividing by zero.

= Solution

For $p=1$, the third coefficient row equals three times the second minus twice the first. The first two rows are independent, so the coefficient <matrix rank> is two. The same dependence must hold for the right-hand side, giving
$$
t^2=3t-2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(t-1)(t-2)=0.
$$
Thus **infinitely many solutions occur exactly for $p=1$ and $t\in\{1,2\}$**. The <null space> has dimension one by the <rank-nullity theorem>, and an explicit parameterization, with $s\in\mathbb R$, is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
t=1:&(x,y,z)=(1+2s,-3s,s),\\
t=2:&(x,y,z)=(2s,1-3s,s).
\end{array}}
$$
Substitution verifies all three equations in each family.

= Solution
{parent=3F}

The two-variable <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> follows from a square:
$$
0\leq(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)^2=a+b-2\sqrt{ab}.
$$
Hence **$2\sqrt{ab}\leq a+b$**, with equality exactly when $a=b$.

For the <series>, apply that same inequality with $a=a_n$ and $b=n^{-2}$ to obtain
$$
0\leq\frac{\sqrt{a_n}}n\leq\frac12\left(a_n+\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$
The comparison <series> converges: its first summand has finite sum by hypothesis, and the inverse-square <series> converges, for example by the telescoping estimate $n^{-2}\leq1/[n(n-1)]=1/(n-1)-1/n$ for $n\geq2$. Thus the <comparison test for series> proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\sqrt{a_n}}n<\infty.}
$$
Equivalently, its positive partial sums are increasing and bounded above by $\tfrac12\sum a_n+\tfrac12\sum n^{-2}$, so the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> proves convergence directly. This is the case $p=1$ of <weighted square roots of a summable sequence>.

{parent=Paper 1}

= Solution
{parent=4D}

First establish a comparison fact for a <power series>. If it converges at $w\ne0$, its terms $a_nw^n$ are bounded, say $|a_nw^n|\leq C$. For $|z|<|w|$,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty|a_nz^n|\leq C\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac{|z|}{|w|}\right)^n<\infty
$$
by the <geometric series>. Thus convergence at one point implies <absolute convergence> at every point of smaller modulus.

Let $S$ be the set of moduli of points at which the <power series> converges. It contains zero. Define the <radius of convergence> $R=\sup S\in[0,\infty]$. If $|z|<R$, the definition of <supremum> gives a convergent point $w$ with $|w|>|z|$; the comparison just proved gives <absolute convergence> at $z$. This reasoning also applies when $R=\infty$. If $|z|>R$ and the <power series> converged there, its modulus would belong to $S$, contradicting the definition of $R$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{convergence for }|z|<R,\qquad\text{divergence for }|z|>R.}
$$
The boundary $|z|=R$ is intentionally undecided by this argument. If $R=0$, only the exterior assertion is nonvacuous.

For the particular coefficients $a_n=1/n$ for $n\geq1$, comparison with $\sum |z|^n$ gives <absolute convergence> whenever $|z|<1$. If $|z|=r>1$, then $r^n/n$ fails to tend to zero: its successive ratio is $rn/(n+1)$, eventually greater than a fixed number bigger than one. The necessary term condition for convergence of a <series> therefore fails. Hence
$$
\boxed{R=1.}
$$
For instance the positive boundary point gives the divergent <harmonic series>; no assertion of universal boundary convergence is needed.

= Solution

Decompose the position <vector> into its components parallel and perpendicular to the <unit vector> $\mathbf u$:
$$
\mathbf r=s\mathbf u+\mathbf v,\qquad s=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf u,\qquad\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
This is an <orthogonal projection>, and the <Pythagorean theorem> gives $|\mathbf r|^2=s^2+|\mathbf v|^2$. The two surfaces are distinguished by fixing an angle in part (a), and fixing distance from an axis in part (b).

= a
{parent=i}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

For $\mathbf r\ne0$, the <dot product> equation says that the angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf r$ and the oriented <unit vector> $\mathbf u$ satisfies $\cos\theta=\alpha$. Thus $\theta=\arccos\alpha$ is fixed. More explicitly, using the <orthogonal projection> from the introductory solution,
$$
s=\alpha\sqrt{s^2+|\mathbf v|^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|\mathbf v|=|s|\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}}{|\alpha|},\qquad \operatorname{sgn}s=\operatorname{sgn}\alpha.
$$
Consequently **the surface is one infinite nappe of a <right circular cone>, with apex at the origin**, including the apex itself. The cone's axis points along $\operatorname{sgn}(\alpha)\mathbf u$, and its acute semi-angle is $\arccos|\alpha|$. Equivalently, $\alpha$ is the cosine of the angle from the oriented $\mathbf u$ axis. Squaring the original equation without retaining the sign of $s$ would incorrectly add the opposite nappe of a <double circular cone>. No base disk is included in this infinite lateral surface.

= Solution

The <vector> $\mathbf r-(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u$ is the perpendicular component of $\mathbf r$ relative to the axis $\mathbb R\mathbf u$, so its length is the distance from that axis. Hence **the surface is an infinite <circular cylinder> of radius $\beta$ about the line through the origin parallel to $\mathbf u$**. Here $\beta$ is the radius and $\mathbf u$ fixes the axis direction; reversing $\mathbf u$ leaves the same <circular cylinder>.

If $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2$ are an <orthonormal basis> of the <plane> perpendicular to $\mathbf u$, a parameterization is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r=s\mathbf u+\beta\bigl(\cos\varphi\,\mathbf v_1+\sin\varphi\,\mathbf v_2\bigr),\qquad s\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This verifies that all heights and all angular positions, but no interior points, belong to the surface.

The <sphere-plane intersection> is governed by the <orthogonal projection> of the <sphere>'s centre onto the <plane>. Since $\mathbf n$ has unit length, let $h=p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c$, the signed distance from $\mathbf c$ to the <plane> along $\mathbf n$. The following parts determine the centre and radius and retain this sign; the radii alone cannot distinguish the two possible sides of the oriented <plane>.

= a
{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Set $\mathbf b_0=\mathbf c+h\mathbf n$, where $h=p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c$. Then $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf b_0=p$, so $\mathbf b_0$ lies in the <plane>. For any position $\mathbf r$ in that <plane>, $\mathbf r-\mathbf b_0$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf b_0-\mathbf c=h\mathbf n$. The <Pythagorean theorem> therefore gives
$$
|\mathbf r-\mathbf c|^2=|\mathbf r-\mathbf b_0|^2+h^2.
$$
On the <sphere> the left side is $a^2$. Thus the intersection is the <circle> in the <plane> centred at $\mathbf b_0$, with squared radius $a^2-h^2$. Its centre must therefore be $\mathbf b=\mathbf b_0$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf b-\mathbf c=\lambda\mathbf n,\qquad
\lambda^2=a^2-\rho^2.}
$$
In terms of the radii, **$\lambda=\pm\sqrt{a^2-\rho^2}$**, with the sign fixed by $\lambda=p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c$. In the central-plane case $\rho=a$, both signs give zero. This is the <center and radius of a sphere-plane intersection> formula with its orientation made explicit.

{parent=ii}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Substitute the signed displacement $\lambda=p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c$ into the <Pythagorean theorem> relation from part (a):
$$
\boxed{\rho=\sqrt{a^2-(p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c)^2}.}
$$
A genuine <circle> requires $|p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c|<a$. Equality gives a tangent <plane> and a single point, interpreted as radius zero; a larger distance gives no intersection. Under the stipulated <circle> intersection, the radicand is positive. Its centre is $\mathbf c+(p-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf c)\mathbf n$.

= Solution

Write $\mathbf x=s\mathbf n+\mathbf v$, with $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n=0$. The <linear map> sends $s\mathbf n$ to $as\mathbf n$ and sends $\mathbf v$ to $a\mathbf v+b(\mathbf n\times\mathbf v)$. On the perpendicular <plane>, $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf n\times\mathbf v$ are perpendicular vectors of equal length. Thus
$$
a\mathbf v+b(\mathbf n\times\mathbf v)
=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\bigl(\cos\vartheta\,\mathbf v+\sin\vartheta\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf v\bigr),
\qquad\vartheta=\arctan(b/a).
$$
The expression in parentheses is a <rotation in three dimensions> by $\vartheta$ about the oriented $\mathbf n$ axis, with the positive direction fixed by the <cross product>. Therefore **the map scales the axial component by $a$, scales the perpendicular component by $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, and rotates the perpendicular <plane> by $\arctan(b/a)$**. Since $a,b>0$, the angle lies between zero and $\pi/2$. This is an <axis-angle decomposition>, with different axial and transverse scale factors.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In <suffix notation>, $x'_i=ax_i+b\epsilon_{ikj}n_kx_j$, so the <matrix> entries are
$$
\boxed{M_{ij}=a\delta_{ij}+b\epsilon_{ikj}n_k
=a\delta_{ij}-b\epsilon_{ijk}n_k.}
$$
Here $\delta$ is the <Kronecker delta> and $\epsilon$ the <Levi-Civita symbol>. The diagonal determines $a=2$. Comparing the off-diagonal entries with
$$
b[\mathbf n\times]=\begin{pmatrix}
0&-bn_3&bn_2\\bn_3&0&-bn_1\\-bn_2&bn_1&0
\end{pmatrix}
$$
gives $b\mathbf n=(1,2,2)$. As $\mathbf n$ is a <unit vector> and $b>0$, **$b=3$ and $\mathbf n=(1,2,2)/3$**. Thus
$$
\boxed{a=2,\qquad b=3,\qquad\mathbf n=\tfrac13(1,2,2).}
$$
In this example the transverse scaling is $\sqrt{13}$ and the axial scaling is two; the <rotation> angle is $\arctan(3/2)$.

= Solution

Let $P\mathbf x=(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf n$ be the axial <orthogonal projection> and $N\mathbf x=\mathbf n\times\mathbf x$. Then $NP=PN=0$, while the <vector> triple-product identity gives $N^2=P-I$. The map is $aP+a(I-P)+bN$. Its inverse is $P/a$ on the axis. On the perpendicular <plane>,
$$
(aI-bN)(aI+bN)=a^2I-b^2N^2=(a^2+b^2)I.
$$
Thus the <inverse of an axis-angle linear map> is
$$
\mathbf x=\frac{P\mathbf x'}a+\frac{a(I-P)\mathbf x'-bN\mathbf x'}{a^2+b^2},
$$
or, in the requested <vector> form,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf x=\frac{a\mathbf x'-b(\mathbf n\times\mathbf x')+(b^2/a)(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x')\mathbf n}{a^2+b^2}.}
$$
The denominators are nonzero because $a,b>0$. Multiplication on each invariant component verifies the <matrix inverse> without choosing a coordinate frame.

= Solution
{parent=7C}

For the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, take $\lambda=(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y)/(\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf y)$ and use positivity of the <inner product>:
$$
0\leq|\mathbf x-\lambda\mathbf y|^2
=\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x-\frac{(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y)^2}{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf y}.
$$
Multiplication by $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf y>0$ proves
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y)^2\leq(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x)(\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf y).}
$$
Equality holds exactly when $|\mathbf x-\lambda\mathbf y|^2=0$. Positive definiteness of the <inner product> makes this equivalent to $\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf y$. Conversely, substitution of a scalar multiple gives equality. Thus **equality holds precisely for linearly dependent nonzero vectors**.

= Solution

Write a <straight line> in Cartesian form $Ax+By+C=0$, where $A,B,C$ are real and $(A,B)\ne(0,0)$. With $z=x+iy$, set $c=(A+iB)/2$ and $r=C$. Using the <complex conjugate>,
$$
\overline c z+c\overline z=2\operatorname{Re}(\overline c z)=Ax+By.
$$
Thus the <real linear equation of a line in the complex plane> is
$$
\boxed{\overline c z+c\overline z+r=0,\qquad c\ne0,\quad r\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Conversely, writing $c=u+iv$ gives $2ux+2vy+r=0$, an ordinary <straight line> with normal <vector> $(u,v)$. The nonzero condition on $c$ matters: $c=0$ would give either the whole <complex plane> or the empty set.

= ii
{parent=8a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The quotient is defined only when $z+\overline u\ne0$. Its unit-modulus condition then gives $|z+u|^2=|z+\overline u|^2$. On expanding with <complex conjugates>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=(z+u)(\overline z+\overline u)-(z+\overline u)(\overline z+u)\\
&=(z-\overline z)(\overline u-u)
=4\operatorname{Im}z\,\operatorname{Im}u.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence at least one imaginary part is zero, and **either $z$ or $u$ is real**. Conversely, either reality condition makes the two squared moduli equal; provided the denominator is nonzero, the quotient has modulus one. No conclusion is drawn from an undefined quotient.

{parent=8a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For real $x$ at which the denominator is nonzero, mapping to the <unit circle> gives $|ax+b|^2=|cx+d|^2$. There are infinitely many such real $x$, so the resulting quadratic <polynomial> identity has all coefficients zero:
$$
|a|^2=|c|^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Re}(a\overline b)=\operatorname{Re}(c\overline d),\qquad
|b|^2=|d|^2.
$$
If $c=0$, the first equality gives $a=0$, contradicting the nonzero <determinant> $ad-bc$. Thus $a,c\ne0$. Put $\lambda=a/c$, $k=b/a$ and $\ell=d/c$. The equalities become
$$
|\lambda|=1,\qquad\operatorname{Re}k=\operatorname{Re}\ell,\qquad |k|=|\ell|.
$$
Two <complex numbers> with equal real parts and equal moduli have imaginary parts equal in magnitude. Consequently $\ell=k$ or $\ell=\overline k$. The first option would give $ad-bc=ac(\ell-k)=0$, so it is excluded. The second gives
$$
\boxed{w=\lambda\frac{z+k}{z+\overline k},\qquad |\lambda|=1.}
$$
Moreover $k$ must be nonreal, since otherwise the two options coincide and the map would be constant. This proves the <Möbius transformations from the real axis to the unit circle> representation directly from coefficients.

Conversely, for real $x$, the numbers $x+k$ and $x+\overline k$ are <complex conjugates>, and their moduli agree. Nonreal $k$ ensures the denominator is nonzero on the <real line>; multiplication by $\lambda$ preserves modulus. At the point at infinity on the <Riemann sphere>, the map takes the value $\lambda$. Thus the representation also respects the extended real axis.

= Solution
{parent=9F}

Let $F(x)=\sqrt{2+\sqrt x}$ for $x\geq0$. This is an increasing <continuous function>. Starting at $a_1=\sqrt2$, induction gives $\sqrt2\leq a_n\leq2$: if $a_n\leq2$, then $a_{n+1}\leq\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}<2$, and every iterate is at least $\sqrt2$.

The first step has $a_2>\sqrt2=a_1$. Since $F$ is increasing, $a_n\geq a_{n-1}$ implies $a_{n+1}=F(a_n)\geq F(a_{n-1})=a_n$. Thus this <sequence> is increasing and bounded above. Let $L_-=\sup_n a_n$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, a term exceeds $L_--\varepsilon$; all later terms lie between it and $L_-$. This proves convergence, the elementary <bounded monotone sequence theorem>.

For the second starting value, write the iterates as $b_1=4$ and $b_{n+1}=F(b_n)$. Here $b_2=2<4$. Monotonicity of $F$ propagates this inequality, so $b_n$ is decreasing. It is bounded below by $\sqrt2$ because every positive iterate is at least that value. Hence it converges to its <infimum> $L_+$. From $b_2=2$ onward all terms lie in $[\sqrt2,2]$.

<Continuity> of $F$ permits passing to each <limit> in the recurrence, giving $L_\pm^2=2+\sqrt{L_\pm}$. To prove that these <fixed points> agree, suppose $r,s\geq\sqrt2$ both satisfy that equality. Subtracting the two equations and rationalizing gives
$$
0=(r-s)\left(r+s-\frac1{\sqrt r+\sqrt s}\right).
$$
The second factor is positive: $r+s\geq2\sqrt2$, whereas $(\sqrt r+\sqrt s)^{-1}\leq1/(2\sqrt{\sqrt2})<2\sqrt2$. Thus $r=s$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L_-=L_+=L,\qquad \sqrt2<L<2,\qquad L^2=2+\sqrt L.}
$$
Existence has been supplied by the two convergent <sequences>, and the subtraction argument gives the required uniqueness in their common interval.

= Solution
{parent=10E}

The <mean value theorem> states that a real-valued function continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, with $a<b$, has a point $\xi\in(a,b)$ such that
$$
\boxed{f'(\xi)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.}
$$
For the proof, subtract the secant line and put $h(t)=f(t)-f(a)-(t-a)[f(b)-f(a)]/(b-a)$. Then $h(a)=h(b)=0$. If $h$ is identically zero, every interior point works. Otherwise it has a positive maximum or a negative minimum. The <extreme value theorem> supplies that extremum, and its nonzero value makes its location interior. At an interior differentiable extremum, the difference quotients on the two sides have opposite weak signs, so their common <limit> is zero. Hence $h'(\xi)=0$, proving the formula. This also gives the <Rolle theorem> argument rather than merely citing it.

Because $f''$ exists everywhere, $f'$ is differentiable everywhere and therefore continuous everywhere; <continuity> of $f''$ is not required. For $x<y$, apply the <mean value theorem> to $f'$ on $[x,y]$. It gives $\xi\in(x,y)$ with

f'(y)-f'(x)=(y-x)f''(\xi)\geq0.

For $x=y$ equality is immediate. Hence **$f'$ is nondecreasing: $x\leq y$ implies $f'(x)\leq f'(y)$**.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $q=\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b$. Since $0<\lambda<1$ and $a<b$, $a<q<b$. Apply the <mean value theorem> separately to $[a,q]$ and $[q,b]$. There exist $x\in(a,q)$ and $y\in(q,b)$ such that
$$
\boxed{f(q)=f(a)+(1-\lambda)(b-a)f'(x)
=f(b)-\lambda(b-a)f'(y).}
$$
These are the requested points, and $x<y$. Part (i) makes $f'(x)\leq f'(y)$, so the corresponding secant slopes satisfy
$$
\frac{f(q)-f(a)}{(1-\lambda)(b-a)}
\leq\frac{f(b)-f(q)}{\lambda(b-a)}.
$$
Multiply by the positive denominators and collect $f(q)$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{f(\lambda a+(1-\lambda)b)\leq\lambda f(a)+(1-\lambda)f(b).}
$$
This is precisely the defining chord inequality for a <convex function>. The proof uses only existence and nonnegativity of $f''$, with no additional regularity hypothesis on that derivative.

= Solution

To prove boundedness on $[a,b]$, suppose instead that $f$ is unbounded and choose $x_n\in[a,b]$ with $|f(x_n)|>n$. The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> gives a subsequence converging to $x_*\in[a,b]$. <Continuity> then gives $f(x_{n_j})\to f(x_*)$, contradicting $|f(x_{n_j})|>n_j$. Hence $f$ is bounded.

Let $M=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)$ and $m=\inf_{x\in[a,b]}f(x)$. Choose $d_n$ with $M-1/n<f(d_n)\leq M$. Another application of <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> yields a subsequence tending to $d\in[a,b]$, and <continuity> gives $f(d)=M$. Applying the same argument to $-f$ gives $c\in[a,b]$ with $f(c)=m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{f(c)\leq f(x)\leq f(d)\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b].}
$$
This proves the <extreme value theorem> here, including both attainment assertions.

For $g$, its two limits supply $R>0$ such that $|g(x)|<1$ whenever $|x|>R$. On $[-R,R]$, <continuity> and the result just proved give a finite bound $K$. Consequently **$|g(x)|\leq\max\{K,1\}$ on the whole real line**.

For the final value assertion, if $c=g(a)$, take $x=a$. Otherwise $0<c<g(a)$. The positive-infinity <limit> gives a point $b>a$ with $|g(b)|<c$, so $g(b)<c<g(a)$. The <intermediate value theorem> on $[a,b]$ now gives $x\in(a,b)$ with
$$
\boxed{g(x)=c.}
$$
Positivity of $c$ is essential to this tail argument; a function can tend to zero without ever attaining zero.

= Solution
{parent=12D}

For a bounded function, a <partition of an interval> is $P:0=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_N=1$. Put $m_j=\inf_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f$ and $M_j=\sup_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f$. Its <lower Darboux sum> and <upper Darboux sum> are
$$
L(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^Nm_j(x_j-x_{j-1}),\qquad
U(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^NM_j(x_j-x_{j-1}).
$$
The <lower Darboux integral> is $\sup_P L(f,P)$ and the <upper Darboux integral> is $\inf_P U(f,P)$. **<Riemann integrability> means that these two real numbers coincide**, and their common value is the <Riemann integral>.

Equivalently, the <Riemann integrability criterion> says that for every $\varepsilon>0$ some partition satisfies $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. To see equivalence, all lower sums are at most all upper sums: refine any two partitions to their common refinement and use <Darboux sum refinement monotonicity>. Thus a small gap forces the difference of the two integrals below $\varepsilon$. Conversely, if both integrals equal $I$, choose a lower sum above $I-\varepsilon/2$ and an upper sum below $I+\varepsilon/2$; their common refinement has gap below $\varepsilon$.

This also agrees with the tagged <Riemann sum> definition: there is $I$ such that every tagged sum on a partition of sufficiently small mesh is within any prescribed tolerance of $I$. Each tagged sum lies between the two <Darboux sums>. From a partition with small gap, an arbitrary small-mesh partition differs from its refinement at only the cells crossing the finitely many original division points; boundedness makes their total contribution tend to zero. Conversely, on a fine partition tags approximating each cell <supremum> or <infimum> force both <Darboux sums> close to the same tagged-sum <limit>.

Now suppose $f$ is continuous on $[0,1]$. It is bounded by the result of Question 11. It is also uniformly continuous: otherwise there are $x_n,y_n\in[0,1]$ with $|x_n-y_n|\to0$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\geq\varepsilon_0>0$. The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> gives a convergent subsequence of $x_n$; the corresponding $y_n$ have the same <limit>, contradicting <continuity>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, this <uniform continuity> gives $\delta>0$ such that points less than $\delta$ apart have function values less than $\varepsilon/2$ apart. A partition of mesh below $\delta$ then satisfies
$$
U(f,P)-L(f,P)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}2\sum_j(x_j-x_{j-1})=\frac{\varepsilon}2<\varepsilon.
$$
Hence **every <continuous function> on the interval is Riemann integrable**, with the required proof supplied by its oscillation estimates.

Indeed the sequences $x_n=(\pi/2+2\pi n)^{-1}$ and $y_n=(3\pi/2+2\pi n)^{-1}$ tend to zero with function values $1$ and $-1$.

That single discontinuity does not prevent <Riemann integrability>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $0<\delta<\min\{1,\varepsilon/4\}$. The cell $[0,\delta]$ contributes at most $2\delta<\varepsilon/2$ to the difference between the <upper Darboux sum> and the <lower Darboux sum>. On $[\delta,1]$ the function is uniformly continuous, so choose a partition there whose oscillation sum is less than $\varepsilon/2$, by the introductory proof. Joining that partition to the cell $[0,\delta]$ gives
$$
\boxed{U(g,P)-L(g,P)<2\delta+\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon.}
$$
Thus **the PDF's function is Riemann integrable despite its endpoint discontinuity**. This is a direct application of <bounded functions continuous away from one endpoint are Riemann integrable>, proved here with the actual oscillation bound rather than an improper-integral argument.
