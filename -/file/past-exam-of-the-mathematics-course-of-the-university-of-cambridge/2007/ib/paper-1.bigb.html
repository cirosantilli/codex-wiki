= Solution
{parent=1G}

In the given <basis>, the <matrix> is $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Let $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, a primitive cube <root of unity>. For each $\xi\in\{1,\omega,\omega^2\}$,
$$
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\\xi\\\xi^2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\xi^2\\1\\\xi\end{pmatrix}
=\xi^2\begin{pmatrix}1\\\xi\\\xi^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus these are <eigenvectors> with distinct <eigenvalues> $1,\omega^2,\omega$, respectively, and form an eigenbasis. Set
$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&\omega&\omega^2\\1&\omega^2&\omega\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The columns are mutually orthogonal with squared norm three, so $P^{-1}=P^*/3$. **The <diagonalization> and <characteristic polynomial> are**
$$
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(1,\omega^2,\omega),\qquad
\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)=(t-1)(t-\omega)(t-\omega^2)=t^3-1.}
$$
Scaling $P$ by $1/\sqrt3$ gives a unitary diagonalizing <matrix>.

= Solution
{parent=2A}

For a geodesic <spherical triangle> on a sphere of radius $R$, the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}=R^2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi),}
$$
where $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are its interior angles. Indeed, its geodesic sides have zero geodesic curvature, its Gaussian curvature is $R^{-2}$, and its three exterior turning angles sum to $3\pi-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)$.

Choose a point strictly inside a full-dimensional convex polyhedron and radially project its boundary onto the unit sphere. Triangulate each projected face by diagonals, using only its existing vertices. Convexity ensures that the resulting spherical triangles cover the sphere once, with disjoint interiors. A face with $k$ edges gives $k-2$ triangles, so their number is $T=\sum_{\mathrm{faces}}(k-2)=2E-2F$, since every original edge belongs to two faces.

Sum the spherical excess formula over all triangles. The total area is $4\pi$, and the angles incident to each projected vertex sum to $2\pi$. No additional vertices were introduced by the face triangulations. Hence
$$
4\pi=2\pi V-\pi T=2\pi V-\pi(2E-2F),
\qquad \boxed{F-E+V=2.}
$$
This is the <spherical-triangle proof of the polyhedron Euler formula>.

= Solution
{parent=3F}

The <partial fraction decomposition> is $f(z)=1/(z-i)+1/(z+i)$. Put $w=z-1$ and expand each reciprocal by a <geometric series>:
$$
\frac1{1\mp i+w}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nw^n}{(1\mp i)^{n+1}},\qquad |w|<\sqrt2.
$$
Adding gives **the <Taylor series>**
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n\left[(1-i)^{-n-1}+(1+i)^{-n-1}\right](z-1)^n.}
$$
Equivalently, since $1\pm i=\sqrt2e^{\pm i\pi/4}$, its coefficient is $(-1)^n2^{(1-n)/2}\cos((n+1)\pi/4)$. The first terms are $1-\frac12w^2+\frac12w^3-\frac14w^4+\cdots$.

The nearest nonremovable <poles> are $i$ and $-i$, both at distance $\sqrt2$ from the center. The function is <holomorphic> throughout the open disk of that radius and cannot be <holomorphic> across either pole. Therefore **the largest <radius of convergence> is $r=\sqrt2$**.

= Solution
{parent=4B}

Use the <position four-vector> $X^a=(ct,\mathbf x)$ and Minkowski signature $(+,-,-,-)$. Along a massive particle's timelike path,
$$
c^2d\tau^2=c^2dt^2-|d\mathbf x|^2,
\qquad d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-|\mathbf v|^2/c^2}=dt/\gamma.
$$
Thus its <four-velocity> is $U^a=dX^a/d\tau=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$, and multiplying by the invariant <rest mass> gives
$$
\boxed{p^a=MU^a=(M\gamma c,M\gamma\mathbf v).}
$$
Its temporal component is $E/c$, and the invariant relation is $(E/c)^2-|\mathbf p|^2=M^2c^2$.

For a massless particle, use the null energy–momentum relation rather than substituting $M=0$ into $M\gamma$: a massless particle has no proper-time parametrization of a timelike rest frame. With positive energy, $p^ap_a=0$ implies $E/c=|\mathbf p|$. Hence
$$
\boxed{p^a=(|\mathbf p|,\mathbf p),\qquad E=c|\mathbf p|.}
$$
The original PDF retains the spatial vector in this expression; the TeX conversion incorrectly replaced it by its magnitude.

\boxed{\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,\partial_xu_y-\partial_yu_x)=(0,0,2\beta).}

= Solution

The velocity divergence is $\partial_xu_x+\partial_yu_y=\alpha-\alpha=0$, satisfying the constant-density <incompressible flow> condition. Choose the <stream function> convention $(u_x,u_y)=(\psi_y,-\psi_x)$. Integration then gives
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,y)=\alpha xy-\frac\beta2(x^2+y^2)+C.}
$$
Indeed, $\psi_y=\alpha x-\beta y$ and $-\psi_x=\beta x-\alpha y$. <Streamlines> are contours of this function. The <vector field> is also compatible with steady inviscid momentum balance without body forces: $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=(\alpha^2-\beta^2)(x,y)$ is balanced by <pressure> $p=p_0-\rho(\alpha^2-\beta^2)(x^2+y^2)/2$.

= iii

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Rotate coordinates to $s=(x+y)/\sqrt2$ and $t=(x-y)/\sqrt2$. The <stream function> becomes
$$
\psi=\frac12\left((\alpha-\beta)s^2-(\alpha+\beta)t^2\right).
$$
For $\alpha<\beta$, both coefficients are negative, so every nonzero contour is a bounded ellipse surrounding the stationary origin. For $\alpha>\beta$, the form is indefinite: nonzero contours are unbounded hyperbolas, and the zero contour consists of straight separatrices. Consequently **all nonstationary <streamlines> are bounded exactly when $\alpha<\beta$**. The origin is itself a stationary bounded trajectory in every case; the assertion about boundedness concerns the nonstationary <streamlines>. The equality case is handled separately below. These are the <linear planar strain and rotation streamlines>.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

If $\alpha=\beta$, $\psi=-\alpha(x-y)^2/2$. Its contours are the parallel lines $x-y=d$. On each such line,
$$
\mathbf u=\alpha d(1,1),\qquad
\boxed{(x(t),y(t))=(x_0,y_0)+\alpha d\,t(1,1).}
$$
For $d\ne0$ this is an unbounded straight streamline. Every point on $x=y$ has zero velocity, so that diagonal is a line of equilibria, rather than one moving streamline.

= Solution

The first <matrix> column is already the unit vector $e_1$, so the first QR elimination needs no reflection. In the remaining three coordinates, the second column is $v=(2,2,1)^T$, with norm three. To map it to $(-3,0,0)^T$, use the <Householder transformation>
$$
w=v+3e_1=(5,2,1)^T,\qquad H=I-\frac{2ww^T}{w^Tw}=I-\frac{ww^T}{15}.
$$
It is symmetric and orthogonal and satisfies $Hv=(-3,0,0)^T$. Extend it to $Q^T=\operatorname{diag}(1,H)$. The <QR decomposition> and transformed right side are
$$
Q^TA=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\0&-3\\0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q^Tb=\begin{pmatrix}4\\-3\\12/5\\-9/5\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For the lower right-side block, $w^T(1,4,-1)^T=12$, so its transformed value is $(1,4,-1)^T-(12/15)w=(-3,12/5,-9/5)^T$.

Orthogonality preserves the least-squares residual norm. Thus the first two squared residual terms can and must be set to zero, while the last two are independent of the unknowns:
$$
x_1+3x_2=4,\quad -3x_2=-3,
\qquad\boxed{x_1=x_2=1.}
$$
The minimum squared residual is $(12/5)^2+(-9/5)^2=9$. The solution is unique since the triangular factor has nonzero diagonal entries. No normal equations are used.

= Solution
{parent=7C}

Write $\bar X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i$, $Q=\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2$, and $Q_0=\sum_i(X_i-\mu_0)^2=Q+n(\bar X-\mu_0)^2$. The normal <likelihood> is proportional to $(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp[-\sum_i(X_i-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)]$. Its unrestricted maximizers are $\widehat\mu=\bar X$, $\widehat\sigma^2=Q/n$. Under the null the <variance> maximizer is $Q_0/n$. Substitution gives the <generalized likelihood-ratio test> statistic
$$
\Lambda=\left(\frac Q{Q_0}\right)^{n/2}
=\left(1+\frac{T^2}{n-1}\right)^{-n/2},\qquad
T=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu_0)}S,\quad S^2=\frac Q{n-1}.
$$
The ratio decreases as $|T|$ increases, so the likelihood-ratio rejection region is two-sided in $T$. Under $H_0$, $\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu_0)/\sigma$ is standard normal and $Q/\sigma^2$ is independent chi-squared with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. Independence follows by resolving the isotropic Gaussian sample vector into its projection on the constant vector and its orthogonal complement. Hence $T$ has the exact <Student t-distribution> with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.

For a chosen <significance level> $\alpha$, compute the <sample mean> and the sample <standard deviation> with denominator $n-1$, and **reject precisely when**
$$
\boxed{|T|>t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}.}
$$
Equivalently, report the two-sided $p$-value $2[1-F_{t_{n-1}}(|T|)]$. This requires $n\geq2$ and positive population <variance>; $S>0$ holds almost surely under that model. The nuisance <variance> is removed by the exact null distribution, so one should not substitute a normal critical value or a large-sample chi-squared approximation. This is the <normal-mean likelihood ratio with unknown variance>.

= 8C
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}
{title2=Optimization}

= Solution
{parent=8C}

For a finite directed <flow network> with nonnegative finite edge capacities, a source $s$ and a distinct sink $t$, the <max-flow min-cut theorem> states that the largest feasible flow value equals the smallest capacity of a cut separating $s$ from $t$. A feasible flow has $0\leq f_e\leq c_e$ on each directed edge and <flow conservation> at all other vertices. Its value is the net outflow at $s$.

For any cut $S$ containing $s$ but not $t$, sum conservation over vertices of $S$. Internal edges cancel, giving
$$
|f|=\sum_{e:S\to S^c}f_e-\sum_{e:S^c\to S}f_e
\leq\sum_{e:S\to S^c}c_e=\operatorname{cap}(S).
$$
Thus every flow value is bounded by every cut capacity.

A maximum flow exists: the feasible set is nonempty, closed and bounded in a finite-dimensional edge-coordinate space, hence compact, and the flow value is <continuous>. For such a flow, form its <residual network>: each original edge has forward residual capacity $c_e-f_e$ and backward residual capacity $f_e$. Keep these as edge-labelled arcs if the original network already has edges in both directions. If a directed positive-residual path joined $s$ to $t$, increasing the flow along its forward arcs and decreasing it along its backward arcs by the path's positive bottleneck capacity would preserve feasibility and increase the value. That contradicts maximality.

Let $S$ be the vertices reachable from $s$ by positive residual arcs. Then $t\notin S$. Every original edge from $S$ to $S^c$ is saturated, otherwise its forward residual arc would extend reachability. Every original edge from $S^c$ to $S$ has zero flow, otherwise its backward residual arc would extend reachability. Therefore
$$
|f|=\sum_{e:S\to S^c}c_e=\operatorname{cap}(S).
$$
Together with the bound for every flow and cut, this proves
$$
\boxed{\max_f|f|=\min_{S:s\in S,\,t\notin S}\operatorname{cap}(S).}
$$
The compactness step makes the proof valid for arbitrary real capacities; it does not assume that a particular augmenting-path algorithm terminates after finitely many augmentations.

= Solution
{parent=9G}

<Sylvester's law of inertia> states that a real <quadratic form> can be brought by an invertible real change of coordinates to
$$
Q=\sum_{j=1}^p u_j^2-\sum_{j=p+1}^{p+q}u_j^2,
$$
with $r$ remaining zero coordinates, and that the integers $(p,q,r)$ are uniquely determined. To prove existence, represent it by a <symmetric matrix>. The allowed orthogonal <diagonalization> yields real <eigenvalues>, and scaling each nonzero eigen-coordinate by the square root of its absolute <eigenvalue> changes its coefficient to $+1$ or $-1$.

For uniqueness, $p$ is the largest <dimension> of a subspace on which the form is positive definite. The positive coordinate subspace attains $p$. Any subspace of <dimension> greater than $p$ intersects the $(q+r)$-dimensional span of the negative and zero coordinates nontrivially, by the <dimension> formula. A nonzero vector in that intersection has nonpositive quadratic value, contradicting positive definiteness. Similarly, $q$ is the maximal <dimension> of a negative-definite subspace. These descriptions are independent of coordinates, and $r=\dim V-p-q$ is then fixed. This proves existence and uniqueness, including degenerate forms.

For the final application, nonsingularity gives $p+q=2m$. In diagonal coordinates write $v=(v_+,v_-)$, with $Q(v)=|v_+|^2-|v_-|^2$. If $v\in U$ projects to zero in the positive coordinates, $Q(v)=0$ forces $v_-=0$, so projection $U\to\mathbb R^p$ is injective. The same argument applies to the negative coordinates. Hence $m\leq p$ and $m\leq q$, and their sum is $2m$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p=q=m.}
$$
The inertia signature is $(m,m)$, or signature difference $p-q=0$ if that convention is used. This is the <isotropic dimension bound from real inertia>.

The <fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups> says that every finitely generated <abelian group> is isomorphic to
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z^r\oplus\mathbb Z/d_1\mathbb Z\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z/d_s\mathbb Z,\qquad
1<d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s.}
$$
The <rank of an abelian group> $r$ and the invariant factors $d_j$ are uniquely determined, with the finite summand allowed to be absent. Equivalently, one can decompose the torsion subgroup into cyclic groups of prime-power orders. The infinite cyclic part and torsion part must both be included in the statement.

= ii
{parent=10g}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A nonzero <polynomial> of degree $n$ over a <field> has **at most $n$ distinct roots in that <field>**. For a proof, the <factor theorem> gives $(t-a)\mid f(t)$ whenever $f(a)=0$. Distinct linear factors are coprime in $K[t]$, a <unique factorization domain>, so for distinct roots $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ their product divides $f$. Its degree is $k$, giving $k\leq n$. One may equivalently remove one linear factor at a time and argue by induction.

If the <field> has at least $n$ elements, this bound is attained by a product of $n$ distinct linear factors. For a fixed finite <field> with fewer than $n$ elements, the largest possible number of distinct roots is $\min(n,|K|)$ instead; multiplicities do not create more distinct roots. The zero <polynomial> is excluded since it has no finite degree.

= iii
{parent=10g}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $G$ be a finite subgroup of $K^\times$. It is abelian since multiplication in the <field> commutes. The structure theorem therefore writes $G$ as a product of cyclic groups with invariant factors $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s$, omitting trivial factors. Every element satisfies $g^{d_s}=1$, so $G$ consists of roots of $t^{d_s}-1$. The root bound from part (ii) gives
$$
|G|\leq d_s,
$$
while the decomposition gives $|G|=\prod_jd_j\geq d_s$. Equality forces every factor except the last to be trivial. Hence **$G$ is cyclic**. The trivial subgroup is cyclic as well. This is the <finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic> theorem, proved here using the preceding two parts.

The infinite case is false. In $\mathbb C^\times$, the group of all <roots of unity> is infinite, but every element has finite order. If it were cyclic, its generator would have finite order and the whole group would be finite, a contradiction.

= Solution
{parent=11H}

<Differentiability> at $p$ means there is a <linear map> $A=Df|_p$ such that
$$
f(p+h)=f(p)+Ah+r(h),\qquad
\frac{\|r(h)\|}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0\quad(h\to0).
$$
This is the <Frechet derivative> definition; the same linear approximation must work in every direction, not merely along coordinate axes.

The <chain rule> says that if $f$ is <differentiable> at $p$ and $g$ is <differentiable> at $f(p)$, then $g\circ f$ is <differentiable> at $p$ and
$$
\boxed{D(g\circ f)|_p=Dg|_{f(p)}\circ Df|_p.}
$$
For its proof, put $A=Df|_p$, $B=Dg|_{f(p)}$, and $k=f(p+h)-f(p)=Ah+r(h)$. Since a <linear map> between these finite-dimensional normed spaces is bounded, $\|k\|=O(\|h\|)$. <Differentiability> of $g$ gives $g(f(p)+k)=g(f(p))+Bk+s(k)$ with $s(k)=o(\|k\|)$. Therefore
$$
g(f(p+h))-g(f(p))-BAh=Br(h)+s(k)=o(\|h\|).
$$
If $k=0$, take $s(0)=0$; otherwise $\|s(k)\|/\|h\|=(\|s(k)\|/\|k\|)(\|k\|/\|h\|)\to0$. This proves the required full linear approximation.

For the <Euclidean norm>, at every $x\ne0$ the ordinary one-coordinate differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial\|x\|}{\partial x_i}=\frac{x_i}{\|x\|}.}
$$
At the origin its difference quotient in direction $e_i$ is $|t|/t$, whose right and left limits are $1$ and $-1$. Thus **none of its coordinate <partial derivatives> exists at the origin**.

= Solution
{parent=12A}

The <product topology> on $X\times Y$ has as a <basis> the rectangles $U\times V$, where $U$ is open in $X$ and $V$ is open in $Y$. Equivalently, it is the coarsest topology making both coordinate projections <continuous>.

To show that the product of two <Hausdorff spaces> is <Hausdorff>, take distinct points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$. If $x_1\ne x_2$, choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U_1,U_2$ in $X$; the rectangles $U_1\times Y$ and $U_2\times Y$ separate the product points. If the first coordinates agree, the second coordinates differ, and the same argument uses disjoint open neighborhoods in $Y$. Thus every pair of distinct product points has disjoint open neighborhoods.

The three implications below prove the requested equivalence without assuming metrizability.

= i
{parent=12a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Assume $X$ is <Hausdorff>. For any $(x_1,x_2)$ outside the diagonal, the points differ, so choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U_1,U_2$. Their rectangle contains $(x_1,x_2)$ and misses the diagonal. Thus the complement of the diagonal is open in the <product topology>, proving **(i) implies (ii)**. This is one direction of the <closed diagonal theorem>.

= ii
{parent=12a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Assume the diagonal $\Delta\subset X\times X$ is closed. For <continuous> $f,g:Y\to X$, the map $h:Y\to X\times X$, $h(y)=(f(y),g(y))$, is <continuous>: the inverse image of a basic rectangle $U\times V$ is $f^{-1}(U)\cap g^{-1}(V)$. Therefore
$$
\{y:f(y)=g(y)\}=h^{-1}(\Delta)
$$
is closed as the inverse image of a <closed set> under a <continuous map>. This proves **(ii) implies (iii)**.

= iii
{parent=12a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Assume (iii). Take $Y=X\times X$ and let $f,g$ be the two coordinate projections, <continuous> by the definition of the <product topology>. Their equality set is the diagonal, so it is closed. For distinct $x_1,x_2$, the point $(x_1,x_2)$ lies in its open complement. Choose a basic rectangle $U_1\times U_2$ containing it and contained in that complement. If $U_1\cap U_2$ were nonempty, a point $u$ in their intersection would give $(u,u)$ in the rectangle, impossible. Thus $U_1,U_2$ are disjoint open neighborhoods of $x_1,x_2$, proving that $X$ is <Hausdorff>. Hence **(iii) implies (i)**, completing all three equivalences.

= Solution
{parent=13F}

Integrate the entire function $e^{iz^2}$ around the positively oriented sector boundary: the real segment $0\to R$, the arc $Re^{i\theta}$ for $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/4$, and the radial segment back to zero. <Cauchy integral theorem> gives
$$
\int_0^Re^{ix^2}\,dx+\int_{\mathrm{arc}}e^{iz^2}\,dz
-e^{i\pi/4}\int_0^Re^{-r^2}\,dr=0.
$$
The last term follows from $z=re^{i\pi/4}$ and $iz^2=-r^2$; its minus sign is the return orientation.

On the arc, $|e^{iz^2}|=e^{-R^2\sin(2\theta)}$. The supplied sine bound gives $\sin(2\theta)\geq4\theta/\pi$ throughout this sector, so
$$
\left|\int_{\mathrm{arc}}e^{iz^2}\,dz\right|
\leq R\int_0^{\pi/4}e^{-4R^2\theta/\pi}\,d\theta
=\frac\pi{4R}(1-e^{-R^2})\longrightarrow0.
$$
The Gaussian integral then yields $\int_0^\infty e^{ix^2}\,dx=e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt\pi/2$. Taking real and imaginary parts proves **both <Fresnel integrals>**:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\cos x^2\,dx=\int_0^\infty\sin x^2\,dx=\sqrt{\frac\pi8}.}
$$
The contour estimate proves existence of the improper oscillatory integrals as well as their values; absolute convergence is neither asserted nor required.

= Solution
{parent=14D}

Use the <Fourier transform> convention
$$
\widetilde f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,\qquad
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widetilde f(k)e^{ikx}\,dk.
$$
Decay alone does not guarantee existence of the ordinary integrals, so impose the usual integrability and regularity assumptions. One sufficient version of the <Fourier inversion theorem> is that $f$ and $\widetilde f$ are integrable and $f$ is <continuous>, in which case the inversion integral recovers $f$. Under the standard piecewise-smooth Dirichlet assumptions, inversion instead gives the midpoint of the two one-sided limits at a jump, with a suitable improper integral interpretation.

For integrable $f,g$, define $(f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x-y)g(y)\,dy$. The <convolution theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{f*g}(k)=\widetilde f(k)\widetilde g(k).}
$$
To prove it, absolute integrability permits <Fubini's theorem>. Set $t=x-y$ in the inner integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{f*g}(k)
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}f(x-y)g(y)e^{-ikx}\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}g(y)e^{-iky}\,dy\int_{\mathbb R}f(t)e^{-ikt}\,dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
The absolute double integral is $\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$, justifying the interchange. The computations below assume $a,b>0$ and use the source PDF's factor $e^{ikx}$ in the final integral, omitted in the converted TeX.

\widetilde f(k)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(a+ik)x}\,dx+\int_{-\infty}^0e^{(a-ik)x}\,dx
=\frac1{a+ik}+\frac1{a-ik}.

Thus **its <Fourier transform> is**
$$
\boxed{\widetilde f(k)=\frac{2a}{a^2+k^2}.}
$$
Both half-line integrals converge absolutely because $a>0$.

The interval indicator has transform
$$
\boxed{\widetilde g(k)=\int_{-b}^be^{-ikx}\,dx=\frac{2\sin(bk)}k,}
$$
with <continuous> value $2b$ at $k=0$. By the <convolution theorem>, $h=f*g$ has transform $4a\sin(bk)/[k(a^2+k^2)]$. Fourier inversion therefore expresses the requested integral as
$$
I(x):=\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\sin(bk)e^{ikx}}{k(a^2+k^2)}\,dk
=\frac\pi{2a}h(x),\qquad h(x)=\int_{-b}^be^{-a|x-y|}\,dy.

For $x>b$, $|x-y|=x-y$, so $h(x)=2e^{-ax}\sinh(ab)/a$. Symmetry gives the same expression with $|x|$ for $x<-b$. For $|x|\leq b$, splitting the integral at $y=x$ gives
$$
h(x)=\frac{1-e^{-a(x+b)}}a+\frac{1-e^{-a(b-x)}}a
=\frac2a\bigl(1-e^{-ab}\cosh(ax)\bigr).
$$
Consequently **the value for every real $x$ is**
$$
\boxed{I(x)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac\pi{a^2}\sinh(ab)e^{-a|x|},&|x|\geq b,\\[2mm]
\displaystyle\frac\pi{a^2}\left(1-e^{-ab}\cosh(ax)\right),&|x|\leq b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The two expressions agree at $x=\pm b$. This is the <exponential kernel convolved with an interval indicator>. The spectral integral is absolutely convergent: its apparent singularity at zero is removable, and the tail decays as $O(|k|^{-3})$.

= 15B
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}
{title2=Quantum Mechanics}

= Solution
{parent=15B}

For a stationary spherically symmetric state, set $u(r)=r\psi(r)$. The <Schrödinger equation> becomes
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u''+V(r)u=Eu.
$$
A <bound state> must have $E<0$, while the well's lower bound gives $E>-U$ for a nonzero square-integrable eigenstate. Define $k=\sqrt{2m(U+E)}/\hbar$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$. Regularity at the origin requires $u(0)=0$, so the interior cosine solution is excluded; square integrability at infinity excludes the growing exterior exponential. Matching $u$ and $u'$ at $r=a$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\psi(r)=C\begin{cases}
\sin(kr)/r,&0<r\leq a,\\
\sin(ka)e^{-\kappa(r-a)}/r,&r\geq a,
\end{cases}\qquad k\cot(ka)=-\kappa.}
$$
The limit at the origin is the finite value $Ck$. Continuity of $u,u'$ is equivalent here to continuity of $\psi,\psi'$ at the finite potential jump. If desired, the normalized state has
$$
|C|^{-2}=4\pi\left[\frac a2-\frac{\sin(2ka)}{4k}+\frac{\sin^2(ka)}{2\kappa}\right].
$$
The equation for $k$ implicitly determines the energy, with $k^2+\kappa^2=2mU/\hbar^2$.

Write $K=a\sqrt{2mU}/\hbar$ and $z=ka$. The matching condition is $z\cot z=-\sqrt{K^2-z^2}$. The first possible solution has $\pi/2<z<\pi$. If $K\leq\pi/2$, every permitted interior $z<K$ has nonnegative cotangent and cannot match a negative exterior logarithmic <derivative>. If $K>\pi/2$, at $z\downarrow\pi/2$ the sum $z\cot z+\sqrt{K^2-z^2}$ is positive; it is negative either at $z\uparrow K$ when $K<\pi$, or at $z\uparrow\pi$ when $K\geq\pi$. Continuity supplies a first bound-state solution. Thus **at least one spherically symmetric <bound state> exists exactly when**
$$
\boxed{U>\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}.}
$$
At equality the limiting energy is zero and the exterior wave is proportional to $1/r$, whose norm diverges. Hence the inequality is strict. Deeper wells can support additional radial states; their negative-energy matching solutions have the same displayed form. This is the <spherically symmetric bound state of a finite well>.

= 16E
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}
{title2=Electromagnetism}

= Solution
{parent=16E}

A steady localized <electric current density> satisfies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0$, from charge conservation or the divergence of Ampère's equation. Choose an enclosing region whose boundary lies outside its support; extending the integration region does not change the source integrals. The <divergence theorem> applied componentwise gives
$$
\nabla\cdot(x_i\mathbf j)=j_i,\qquad
\nabla\cdot(x_ix_k\mathbf j)=x_kj_i+x_ij_k.
$$
Their boundary fluxes vanish, proving the <localized steady-current moment identities>
$$
\boxed{\int_V\mathbf j\,dV=0,\qquad
\int_Vx_ij_k\,dV=-\int_Vx_kj_i\,dV.}
$$
For discontinuous but localized currents the same argument uses the distributional steady conservation law and an outer enclosing surface.

The <magnetic vector potential> is a <vector field> with $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, consistent with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. It is defined up to $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$. The steady Maxwell equation $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf j$ gives
$$
\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf j.
$$
In the <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, the equation is $\nabla^2\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf j$, whose decaying Green-function solution is the one supplied in the source.

Let $r=|\mathbf x|$ and expand its kernel uniformly over the bounded source region:
$$
\frac1{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x'}{r^3}+O(r^{-3}).
$$
The first term integrates to zero. Put $T_{ki}=\int x'_kj_i(\mathbf x')\,dV'$, an antisymmetric tensor by the earlier identity. Define the <magnetic dipole moment> $m_\ell=\frac12\epsilon_{\ell ki}T_{ki}$, so $T_{ki}=\epsilon_{ki\ell}m_\ell$. Consequently
$$
A_i=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}x_kT_{ki}+O(r^{-3})
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}(\mathbf m\times\mathbf x)_i+O(r^{-3}),
$$
or
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A(\mathbf x)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\mathbf m\times\mathbf x}{r^3}+O(r^{-3}),\qquad
\mathbf m=\frac12\int_V\mathbf x'\times\mathbf j(\mathbf x')\,dV'.}
$$
The displayed dipole term has size $r^{-2}$. If $\mathbf m=0$, it vanishes and a higher multipole determines the leading <magnetic field> instead.

For $r>0$, $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf x/r^3)=0$ and
$$
\nabla\times(\mathbf m\times\mathbf x/r^3)
=-(\mathbf m\cdot\nabla)(\mathbf x/r^3)
=\frac{3\mathbf x(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf x)}{r^5}-\frac{\mathbf m}{r^3}.
$$
Therefore **the far <magnetic field> is**
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf x)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}
\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf x})\widehat{\mathbf x}-\mathbf m\right]+O(r^{-4}).}
$$

= 17D
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}
{title2=Fluid Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=17D}

For a steady inviscid incompressible fluid of constant density in uniform gravity $\mathbf g=-g\mathbf e_z$, the <Euler equation for fluid motion> and continuity equation are
$$
\rho(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\nabla p+\rho\mathbf g,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$
Taking the scalar product with $\mathbf u$ gives
$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\left(\frac12|\mathbf u|^2+\frac p\rho+gz\right)=0.
$$
Thus **Bernoulli's quantity is constant along each streamline**. For an irrotational connected flow it is constant throughout the flow; constancy across different <streamlines> is not assumed in general.

Since incompressibility makes $\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u)=\rho(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u$, integrating over a fixed control volume and applying the <divergence theorem> yields the integral momentum balance
$$
\boxed{\int_S\rho\mathbf u(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,dS
=-\int_Sp\mathbf n\,dS+\int_V\rho\mathbf g\,dV.}
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is outward and the left side is the net outgoing <momentum flux>. Uniform atmospheric <pressure> has zero resultant on a closed control surface, so it may be subtracted from all pressures in the jet calculations.

= i
{parent=17d}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

The free jet is at atmospheric <pressure>. Bernoulli's equation from the nozzle to height $z$ gives $U(z)^2/2+gz=U_0^2/2$. Conservation of volume flux gives $A(z)U(z)=A_0U_0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{U(z)=U_0\sqrt{1-\frac{2gz}{U_0^2}},\qquad
A(z)=A_0\left(1-\frac{2gz}{U_0^2}\right)^{-1/2}.}
$$
For the prescribed well-below-apex regime, $U(z)=U_0[1-gz/U_0^2+O((gz/U_0^2)^2)]$ and $A(z)=A_0[1+gz/U_0^2+O((gz/U_0^2)^2)]$. The slowing jet therefore thickens. These expressions use the slender-jet, nearly uniform cross-sectional speed approximation underlying the question; the formal vanishing-speed apex is outside that approximation.

= ii
{parent=17d}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Choose a control volume containing the small impingement region, with an inlet section across the incident jet and an outlet annulus across the conical sheet. The remaining boundary follows free surfaces and the wetted cone. Subtract atmospheric <pressure> and neglect gravity. Bernoulli's equation gives the same speed $U$ in the outgoing sheet as in the incoming jet, and its total volume flux is $AU$.

The incoming axial momentum rate is $\rho AU^2$. The outgoing velocity makes angle $\alpha$ with the cone axis, so its axial momentum rate is $\rho AU^2\cos\alpha$; transverse components cancel by axisymmetry. The integral momentum equation says that the cone's axial force on the water is their difference, $-\rho AU^2(1-\cos\alpha)$. By equal and opposite reaction, **the jet's force on the cone is along the incident jet, with magnitude**
$$
\boxed{F=\rho AU^2(1-\cos\alpha).}
$$
The outlet control surface is across the sheet, not along it, so its entire outgoing volume flux is counted.

At the cone's vertex height $h$, the first part gives $U(h)=U_0\sqrt{1-2gh/U_0^2}$ and $A(h)U(h)=A_0U_0$. Insert these into the assumed local impingement force from part (ii). Static support requires upward force equal to $Mg$, giving
$$
\boxed{\rho A_0U_0^2\sqrt{1-\frac{2gh}{U_0^2}}(1-\cos\alpha)=Mg.}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
h=\frac{U_0^2}{2g}\left[1-\left(\frac{Mg}{\rho A_0U_0^2(1-\cos\alpha)}\right)^2\right].

For a nonnegative support height the ratio in parentheses must not exceed one, and the prescribed $h\ll U_0^2/(2g)$ requires that ratio to be close to one. If the weight exceeds the lift available at the nozzle, this model has no supported height above the nozzle.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $K=\sum_iX_i$ and $\bar X=K/n$. The <likelihood> is $\theta^K(1-\theta)^{n-K}$, so for $0<K<n$ its unique maximizer is $\widehat\theta=K/n$. By invariance of <maximum likelihood estimation>,
$$
\boxed{\widehat\phi=\widehat\theta(1-\widehat\theta)=\frac{K(n-K)}{n^2}.}
$$
For $K=0$ or $n$, the strict parameter interval $(0,1)$ has no attained <likelihood> maximum; the usual extended estimate takes the limiting endpoint and gives $\widehat\phi=0$. Thus the displayed estimator is the conventional extended MLE on those samples, an important boundary qualification. This is the first estimator in <estimating Bernoulli variance>.

= ii
{parent=18c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Independence gives $\mathbb E[X_1(1-X_2)]=\theta(1-\theta)=\phi$, proving unbiasedness. Conditional on $K=k$, all binary sample strings with $k$ ones are equally likely. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[X_1(1-X_2)\mid K=k]
=\frac kn\frac{n-k}{n-1}=\frac{k(n-k)}{n(n-1)}.
$$
The <Rao-Blackwell theorem>, or the tower property and total <variance> decomposition directly, yields the improved estimator
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\phi=\frac{K(n-K)}{n(n-1)}=\frac n{n-1}\widehat\phi.}
$$
It is unbiased by <conditional expectation> and is a function of the requested MLE. Its <variance> is strictly smaller, since
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_1(1-X_2))
=\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde\phi)+\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X_1(1-X_2)\mid K)].
$$
For $0<\theta<1$, the event $K=1$ has positive probability. On it, $X_1(1-X_2)$ has probability $1/n$ of being one, strictly between zero and one for $n\geq2$. Thus the final expectation is strictly positive. This proves **strict <variance> reduction**, not merely a weak inequality.

= iii
{parent=18c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The uniform prior and <likelihood> give the posterior density
$$
\pi(\theta\mid K)=\frac{(n+1)!}{K!(n-K)!}\theta^K(1-\theta)^{n-K},
$$
a <Beta distribution> with parameters $K+1,n-K+1$. Under <squared-error loss> for $\phi$, the posterior expected loss is $\mathbb E[\phi^2\mid K]-2a\mathbb E[\phi\mid K]+a^2$, minimized uniquely at the posterior mean of $\phi$, not at the product of the two posterior mean factors.

Using the supplied beta integral,
$$
\mathbb E[\theta(1-\theta)\mid K]
=\frac{(K+1)!(n-K+1)!/(n+3)!}{K!(n-K)!/(n+1)!}.
$$
Thus **the Bayes estimate is**
$$
\boxed{\widehat\phi_B=\frac{(K+1)(n-K+1)}{(n+2)(n+3)}.}
$$
This remains positive even on the endpoint samples, unlike the extended MLE; it integrates over the posterior uncertainty in $\theta$.

= 19C
{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}
{title2=Markov Chains}

= Solution
{parent=19C}

Put $h_0=1$ and $h_r=0$. The <Markov property> and first-step conditioning give $h_i=\sum_jP_{ij}h_j$. Consider a finite history ending at state $i$ and having positive conditional probability given $A$. The chance of eventual absorption at zero after that history is $h_i$: the future depends only on the present state, and if zero has already been reached, absorption keeps the chain there. If the next state is $j$, this chance is $h_j$. Bayes' rule therefore gives
$$
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_0,\ldots,X_n=i,A)
=\frac{P_{ij}h_j}{h_i}.
$$
This depends on the history only through $i$, proving the conditional <Markov property>. **The conditional <transition matrix> is**
$$
\boxed{Q_{ij}=P_{ij}\frac{h_j}{h_i}\quad(0\leq i<r).}
$$
Its row sums are one by harmonicity of $h$, and $Q_{ir}=0$. State $r$ is never visited under the conditioning and is naturally removed; any row assigned there on an enlarged formal state space is immaterial, since conditioning starting at $r$ is undefined. State zero stays absorbing. For a random initial law $\nu$, its conditional law is $\nu_i h_i/\sum_j\nu_jh_j$. This is the <Doob h-transform> with the absorption probability as harmonic function.

For the simple symmetric walk, $h_i=(r-i)/r$ solves the linear harmonic recurrence and its boundary values. Thus
$$
Q_{i,i-1}=\frac{r-i+1}{2(r-i)},\qquad
Q_{i,i+1}=\frac{r-i-1}{2(r-i)},\qquad 1\leq i<r.
$$
In particular, the conditioned chain moves downward with probability one from $r-1$.

Let $\tau$ be the first hit of $\{0,r\}$ in the original walk and let $g_i=\mathbb E_i[\tau\mathbf1_A]$. Its absorption time has finite expectation: from any transient state a run of at most $r$ downward steps hits zero with probability at least $2^{-r}$, giving a geometric tail bound by blocks. First-step conditioning then gives
$$
g_0=g_r=0,\qquad g_i=h_i+\frac12(g_{i-1}+g_{i+1}).
$$
Equivalently, $g_{i+1}-2g_i+g_{i-1}=-2(r-i)/r$. The cubic <polynomial>
$$
g_i=\frac{i(r-i)(2r-i)}{3r}
$$
has exactly these second differences and boundary values. It is the unique solution, since the difference of any two solutions is a linear sequence vanishing at both endpoints. Conditional on $A$, $\tau$ is the first time in zero, so division by $h_i$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_i[\tau_0\mid A]=\frac{g_i}{h_i}=\frac{i(2r-i)}3.}
$$
This is the <duration of gambler's ruin conditioned on a chosen boundary>.
