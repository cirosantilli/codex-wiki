= Solution
{parent=1G}

The <dual map> reverses the direction of the <linear map>:
$$
\boxed{\alpha^*:W^*\longrightarrow V^*,\qquad \alpha^*(\lambda)=\lambda\circ\alpha.}
$$
Here the <dual spaces> consist of complex-linear <functionals>. Composition with $\alpha$ is linear both as a <functional> on $V$ and as an operation on $W^*$, so this does define a <linear map> between the <dual spaces>.

Let $(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ and $(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$ be the chosen <bases>, and let $(v^1,\ldots,v^n)$ and $(w^1,\ldots,w^m)$ be their <dual bases>. If $A$ represents $\alpha$, then $\alpha(v_i)=\sum_jA_{ji}w_j$. Therefore
$$
(\alpha^*w^j)(v_i)=w^j(\alpha(v_i))=A_{ji},\qquad\alpha^*w^j=\sum_iA_{ji}v^i.
$$
The coefficient in row $i$, column $j$ of the <matrix> of $\alpha^*$ is thus $A_{ji}$, giving **the <matrix transpose> $A^{\mathsf T}$**. This calculation introduces no <complex conjugation>: the algebraic <dual map> uses complex-linear <functionals>, whereas a <Hermitian transpose> belongs to the inner-product adjoint construction.

The <fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups> expresses every <finite abelian group> of this order as a product of <cyclic groups> of prime-power order. Their exponents correspond to the <partitions of an integer> $4$:
$$
\boxed{C_{p^4},\quad C_{p^3}\times C_p,\quad C_{p^2}\times C_{p^2},\quad C_{p^2}\times C_p\times C_p,\quad C_p^4.}
$$
These are pairwise nonisomorphic: the largest <order of a group element> distinguishes all except the two groups with largest element order $p^2$, and those have respectively $p^2$ and $p^3$ elements annihilated by multiplication by $p$. The classification exhausts the possibilities, so **there are five <isomorphism> classes**.

The sequence has <uniform convergence> to $f$ on $(0,1)$ precisely when
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N\ \forall x\in(0,1):\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon.}
$$
The same $N$ must work throughout the interval; equivalently $\sup_{0<x<1}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$.

For each fixed $x>0$, <continuity> of the <logarithm> gives the <pointwise convergence> $f_n(x)\to\log x$. But at $x_n=1/n$, which belongs to $(0,1)$ for $n>1$,
$$
f_n(x_n)-\log x_n=\log\frac{2/n}{1/n}=\log2.
$$
Consequently the uniform error cannot tend to zero. In fact $f_n(x)-\log x=\log(1+1/(nx))$ is unbounded as $x\downarrow0$ for every fixed $n$. **The <pointwise limit> is $\log x$, and convergence is not uniform.**

= Solution
{parent=4H}

The <argument principle> states that if $h$ is <meromorphic> on a <neighbourhood> of a positively oriented simple closed <contour> and its interior, with neither zeros nor poles on the <contour>, then
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{h'(z)}{h(z)}\,dz=N-P,
$$
where $N$ and $P$ count its interior zeros and poles with <multiplicity>. This integer is also the <winding number> of $h(\gamma)$ about zero.

Fix $z_0\in U$. Injectivity prevents $f$ from being constant on a <neighbourhood> of $z_0$, so its <Taylor expansion> has the form
$$
f(z)-f(z_0)=(z-z_0)^m h(z),\qquad m\ge1,\quad h(z_0)\ne0.
$$
Choose a small closed disk $D\subset U$, centred at $z_0$, such that $h$ and $mh+(z-z_0)h'$ are nonzero throughout $D$. Then $f-f(z_0)$ has its sole zero at $z_0$ in $D$, of <multiplicity> $m$, while $f'$ has no zero in $D\setminus\{z_0\}$. On its boundary let $\delta=\min|f-f(z_0)|>0$.

Choose $0<|w|<\delta$. For every $0\le t\le1$, the <holomorphic function> $f-f(z_0)-tw$ is nonzero on $\partial D$. By the <argument principle>, its number of zeros is an integer given by a <contour integral> varying continuously with $t$, hence remains $m$. At $t=1$ none of these zeros is $z_0$, so their <derivatives> are nonzero and they are <simple zeros>. Thus there are $m$ distinct points taking the value $f(z_0)+w$. Injectivity forces $m=1$, proving **$f'(z_0)\ne0$ everywhere in $U$**.

= Solution
{parent=5B}

Take the <wave speed> $c>0$ and introduce <characteristic coordinates> $\xi=x+ct$, $\eta=x-ct$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x=\partial_\xi+\partial_\eta,\qquad\partial_t=c\partial_\xi-c\partial_\eta,\qquad y_{tt}-c^2y_{xx}=-4c^2y_{\xi\eta}.
$$
The <wave equation> therefore says $y_{\xi\eta}=0$. Integrating first with respect to $\eta$ gives $y_\xi=F(\xi)$, and integrating with respect to $\xi$ gives **$y=f(x+ct)+g(x-ct)$** with twice differentiable $f,g$. Conversely substitution shows that every such sum solves the <wave equation>. The two terms are <travelling waves> of unchanged shape, moving respectively to the left and right at speed $c$.

The zero initial displacement gives $g(x)=-f(x)$, and the initial velocity gives $2cf'(x)=\psi(x)$. Hence the <D'Alembert formula with initial velocity> is
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac1{2c}\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}\psi(s)\,ds.}
$$
For a classical twice differentiable solution one may take $\psi\in C^1$. <Differentiation> gives $y_t(x,0)=\psi(x)$ and $y(x,0)=0$ directly. The coordinate calculation also works for any nonzero signed $c$, with physical speed $|c|$. If the printed constant is allowed to be $c=0$, the equation instead gives $y=A(x)+tB(x)$ and these initial data give $y=t\psi(x)$; the displayed travelling-wave representation requires $c\ne0$.

= Solution
{parent=6B}

For the <wavefunction> of a particle of <mass> $m$, the <probability density> and <probability current> are
$$
\boxed{\rho=\Psi^*\Psi,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\Psi^*\Psi_x-\Psi\Psi_x^*)=\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\Psi_x).}
$$
For a real potential $V$, the <time-dependent Schrodinger equation> and its <complex conjugate> read
$$
i\hbar\Psi_t=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Psi_{xx}+V\Psi,\qquad -i\hbar\Psi_t^*=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Psi_{xx}^*+V\Psi^*.
$$
Multiplying by $\Psi^*$ and $\Psi$ respectively yields
$$
\rho_t=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\Psi^*\Psi_{xx}-\Psi\Psi_{xx}^*)=-j_x.
$$
The potential terms cancel because $V$ is real. This is the <continuity equation>, expressing local conservation of probability.

For the given <superposition> the common time phase cancels in $\Psi^*\Psi_x$, and
$$
\Psi^*\Psi_x=ik(1-|R|^2)+ik(R^*e^{2ikx}-Re^{-2ikx}).
$$
The expression in the second parentheses is purely imaginary, so the second term is real and contributes nothing to the <probability current>. Thus
$$
\boxed{j=\frac{\hbar k}{m}(1-|R|^2).}
$$
It is the incoming <probability current> minus the reflected <probability current>.

= Solution
{parent=7E}

For a moving circuit, <Faraday's law> takes the form
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E(t)=\oint_{C(t)}(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac d{dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
Here $C(t)$ is the instantaneous wire <contour>, $\mathbf v$ its local material <velocity>, and $S(t)$ any spanning surface. The <contour> <orientation> and surface <normal vector> obey the right-hand convention. The <integral> on the right is the <magnetic flux>, differentiated with both the field and circuit position allowed to vary. The <electric field> term is the electric force per unit <electric charge> around the wire, and $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ supplies the <motional electromotive force>. Their sum is the <electromotive force> driving charge around the moving circuit. The condition $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ makes the <magnetic flux> independent of the spanning surface.

Choose the initial loop <normal vector> to be $+\mathbf e_y$, so <rotation> about $+\mathbf e_z$ gives $\mathbf n(t)=(-\sin\Omega t,\cos\Omega t,0)$. The spatially uniform <magnetic field> gives
$$
\Phi(t)=\pi a^2B_0\bigl[-\sin\Omega t\cos\omega t+\cos\Omega t\sin\omega t\bigr]=\pi a^2B_0\sin((\omega-\Omega)t).
$$
Using <Ohm's law> for the resistive loop,
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{\pi a^2B_0(\Omega-\omega)}R\cos((\omega-\Omega)t).}
$$
Positive <electric current> is measured in the <contour> <orientation> associated with $\mathbf n(t)$. Reversing that convention reverses the signed answer. When $\Omega=\omega$, the <magnetic flux> is constant and the induced <electric current> vanishes. This uses the usual thin resistive-circuit approximation with <self-inductance> neglected.

= Solution
{parent=8F}

Let $L(f)=f'(0)-\mu(f)$. Direct evaluation on $1,x,x^2$ gives $L=0$, so the <Peano kernel theorem> gives
$$
L(f)=\int_0^2K(t)f^{(3)}(t)\,dt,\qquad K(t)=L\left(\frac{(x-t)_+^2}{2}\right).
$$
This representation also follows by applying $L$ to the <Taylor expansion> of $f$ with <integral> remainder. The <derivative> at zero of the truncated quadratic is zero for $t\ge0$, and its sampled values give
$$
K(t)=-(1-t)_+^2+\frac14(2-t)^2=\begin{cases}t-\frac34t^2,&0\le t\le1,\\\frac14(2-t)^2,&1\le t\le2.\end{cases}
$$
The <Peano kernel> is nonnegative, and
$$
\int_0^2|K(t)|\,dt=\left[\frac{t^2}{2}-\frac{t^3}{4}\right]_0^1+\int_1^2\frac{(2-t)^2}{4}\,dt=\frac14+\frac1{12}=\frac13.
$$
Hence $|L(f)|\le\frac13\|f^{(3)}\|_\infty$. For $f(x)=x^3/6$, the third <derivative> is $1$, while $f'(0)=0$ and $\mu(f)=-1/3$. Thus equality holds, proving **the least constant is $c=1/3$, attained by $f(x)=x^3/6$**.

For a time-homogeneous <Markov chain> with <transition matrix> $P$, two states are <communicating states> when each can be reached from the other with positive probability in finitely many steps:
$$
\boxed{i\leftrightarrow j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists m,n\ge0:\ (P^m)_{ij}>0,\ (P^n)_{ji}>0.}
$$
The convention $P^0=I$ includes communication of a state with itself.

= ii
{parent=9c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $T_i^+=\inf\{n\ge1:X_n=i\}$ be the first positive return time. A <recurrent state> is one for which
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_i(T_i^+<\infty)=1.}
$$
The subscript means that the <Markov chain> starts at $i$. The positive-time condition distinguishes a genuine return from the automatic visit at time zero.

= Solution
{parent=9C}

Communication is reflexive because $(P^0)_{ii}=1$, and symmetric by its definition. If $i\leftrightarrow j$ and $j\leftrightarrow k$, choose positive-probability paths $i\to j$ in $m$ steps and $j\to k$ in $n$ steps. The <Chapman-Kolmogorov equation> gives
$$
(P^{m+n})_{ik}\ge(P^m)_{ij}(P^n)_{jk}>0.
$$
Combining the reverse paths gives a positive-probability path $k\to i$ as well. Thus communication is transitive and is an <equivalence relation>.

To transfer recurrence, first establish the <recurrence criterion by return probabilities>. Put $r_i=\mathbb P_i(T_i^+<\infty)$ and let $N_i=\sum_{n\ge0}\mathbf1_{\{X_n=i\}}$ under $\mathbb P_i$. After each return the <strong Markov property> restarts the chain at $i$, so $\mathbb P_i(N_i\ge k+1)=r_i^k$. Therefore
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}(P^n)_{ii}=\mathbb E_iN_i=\sum_{k\ge0}r_i^k.
$$
For $r_i<1$ this equals $(1-r_i)^{-1}$; for $r_i=1$ it is infinite. The <expectation> identity follows by summing the nonnegative indicators, so remains valid when the <expectation> is infinite. Consequently $i$ is a <recurrent state> exactly when its return-probability series diverges.

If $i\leftrightarrow j$, choose $a,b$ with $(P^a)_{ji}>0$ and $(P^b)_{ij}>0$. For every $n\ge0$,
$$
(P^{a+n+b})_{jj}\ge(P^a)_{ji}(P^n)_{ii}(P^b)_{ij}.
$$
Summing over $n$, recurrence of $i$ forces the return-probability series for $j$ to diverge. The proved criterion shows **$j$ is recurrent, so recurrence is a property of the <communicating class>**.

The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> states that every square complex <matrix> satisfies its own <characteristic polynomial>: if $\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)=t^n+c_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0$, then
$$
\boxed{A^n+c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0I=0.}
$$
For a proof, expand the <adjugate matrix> as $\operatorname{adj}(tI-A)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B_kt^k$. The <adjugate identity> gives
$$
(tI-A)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B_kt^k=\chi_A(t)I.
$$
Comparing coefficients yields $-AB_0=c_0I$, $B_{k-1}-AB_k=c_kI$ for $1\le k\le n-1$, and $B_{n-1}=I$. Multiply the middle identities on the left by $A^k$, include the first identity, and add $A^nB_{n-1}=A^n$. The terms involving the $B_k$ cancel consecutively:
$$
-AB_0+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(A^kB_{k-1}-A^{k+1}B_k)+A^nB_{n-1}=0.
$$
Their right sides sum to $c_0I+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_kA^k+A^n$. This proves the theorem without any substitution of noncommuting <matrices> into a scalar <polynomial> identity.

First, the <orbit-stabilizer theorem> applied to a face gives $|G|=20\cdot3=60$: its <stabilizer subgroup> consists of the identity and the two <rotations> through $120^\circ$ about the axis through the centres of that face and its opposite. A <rotation> preserving a face must preserve its outward normal and cyclically permute its three vertices, so there are no further stabilizing <rotations>.

The <conjugacy classes> are as follows:

* The identity: **size $1$**.
* Half-turns about axes through midpoints of opposite edges: **size $15$**.
* <Rotations> through $\pm2\pi/3$ about opposite-face axes: **size $20$**.
* <Rotations> through $\pm2\pi/5$ about opposite-vertex axes: **size $12$**.
* <Rotations> through $\pm4\pi/5$ about opposite-vertex axes: **size $12$**.

There are $30/2=15$ edge axes, $20/2=10$ face axes, and $12/2=6$ vertex axes. They give respectively $15$, $2\cdot10=20$, and $4\cdot6=24$ distinct nonidentity <rotations>, already accounting for all $59$ nonidentity elements.

To justify the class assertions, conjugation by a <rotation> carries the <rotation> axis to its image and preserves the <rotation> angle about the transported oriented axis. The <transitive group action> on edges puts all half-turns in one <conjugacy class>. The <transitive group action> on faces puts the $120^\circ$ <rotations> defined using outward face normals in one <conjugacy class>; opposite faces encode opposite signs about the same unoriented axis. The same reasoning with outward vertex directions gives a class of twelve $72^\circ$ <rotations> and a class of twelve $144^\circ$ <rotations>, including their inverses. These last classes cannot merge: the <matrix trace> of a three-dimensional <rotation> is $1+2\cos\theta$, which differs for $\theta=2\pi/5$ and $4\pi/5$. Thus the list is complete.

= Solution
{parent=12A}

In the <Poincare half-plane model>, $\mathbb H=\{x+iy:y>0\}$ has <Riemannian metric>
$$
\boxed{ds^2=\frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}.}
$$
Its orientation-preserving <isometries> are the <Möbius transformations> $z\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)$ with real coefficients and $ad-bc=1$, with a simultaneous sign change representing the same map. The full <isometry group> also contains their compositions with $z\mapsto-\overline z$, which reverse <orientation>. The <hyperbolic lines> are vertical Euclidean lines and upper semicircles centred on the real axis; each meets the boundary orthogonally.

A <hyperbolic line> with finite endpoints $p<q$ is mapped to the <imaginary axis> by
$$
T(z)=\frac{z-p}{q-z}.
$$
Its coefficient <determinant> is $q-p>0$, so rescaling the coefficients makes this an orientation-preserving <isometry>. The endpoints map to $0$ and $\infty$, which characterize the imaginary-axis <hyperbolic line>. For a vertical line with endpoints $p,\infty$, use $T(z)=z-p$. If $T_1,T_2$ send the two prescribed lines to the <imaginary axis>, $T_2^{-1}T_1$ sends the first to the second. This proves <transitivity> on <hyperbolic lines>.

For two <ultraparallel hyperbolic lines>, choose their common perpendicular and map it to the <imaginary axis>. A <hyperbolic line> perpendicular to that axis is a semicircle centred at zero: <orthogonality> at $iy$ forces the <circle> centre to have real coordinate zero. Thus the reflecting lines become semicircles of distinct radii $r_1,r_2>0$. The <hyperbolic reflection> in the radius-$r$ semicircle is $S_r(z)=r^2/\overline z$; it fixes that semicircle pointwise and reverses <orientation>. Their composition is
$$
S_{r_2}S_{r_1}(z)=\lambda z,\qquad\lambda=(r_2/r_1)^2\ne1.
$$
Its $k$th power is $z\mapsto\lambda^kz$, and no positive power is the identity since $\lambda>0$ and $\lambda\ne1$. Conjugation by the chosen <isometry> preserves <order of a group element>, so **the original composition has infinite order**. Reversing the order of the reflections replaces $\lambda$ by $\lambda^{-1}$ and gives the same conclusion.

= Solution
{parent=13H}

The <contraction mapping theorem> says that if $(X,d)$ is a nonempty <complete metric space> and $T:X\to X$ satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y)$ for a constant $0\le q<1$, then $T$ has exactly one <fixed point>.

For the proof, start at $x_0\in X$ and set $x_n=T^nx_0$. Iterating the <contraction> bound gives $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le q^nd(x_1,x_0)$. The <triangle inequality> then gives, for $m>n$,
$$
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}q^kd(x_1,x_0)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).
$$
Thus $(x_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence> and has a limit $x_*$ by completeness. Since the <contraction> is continuous, $Tx_*=\lim Tx_n=\lim x_{n+1}=x_*$. If $y_*$ is another <fixed point>, then $d(x_*,y_*)\le qd(x_*,y_*)$, forcing distance zero. This proves existence and uniqueness.

For the <integral> map, the <fundamental theorem of calculus> shows that $Tf$ is continuous whenever $f$ is continuous. In the <supremum norm>,
$$
|Tf(x)-Tg(x)|\le3\|f-g\|_\infty\left|\int_0^x|t|\,dt\right|=\frac32x^2\|f-g\|_\infty.
$$
Hence $\|Tf-Tg\|_\infty\le\frac32\max(a^2,b^2)\|f-g\|_\infty$. We can choose
$$
\boxed{a=-\frac12,\quad b=\frac12,\quad q=\frac38.}
$$
The space $C[a,b]$ with this <supremum norm> is complete: a uniformly <Cauchy sequence> has pointwise real limits, the Cauchy bound passes to those limits to give <uniform convergence>, and a <uniform limit> of <continuous functions> is continuous. The <contraction mapping theorem> therefore gives a unique continuous $y$ with $y(x)=1+\int_0^x3ty(t)\,dt$. The <fundamental theorem of calculus> makes this $y$ differentiable, with $y'=3xy$ and $y(0)=1$. Conversely every solution of that initial-value problem obeys the <integral> equation, so uniqueness transfers to the <ordinary differential equation>. Explicitly **$y(x)=e^{3x^2/2}$ on $[-1/2,1/2]$**, as direct <differentiation> confirms.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <closure> $\operatorname{cl}(A)$ is the intersection of all <closed subsets> containing $A$; equivalently it consists of the points whose every open <neighbourhood> meets $A$.

Suppose $f$ is continuous and $x\in\operatorname{cl}(A)$. For any open <neighbourhood> $V$ of $f(x)$, <continuity> makes $f^{-1}(V)$ an open <neighbourhood> of $x$, so it meets $A$. Hence $V$ meets $f(A)$, showing $f(x)\in\operatorname{cl}(f(A))$. This proves $f(\operatorname{cl}(A))\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(f(A))$.

Conversely suppose this inclusion holds for every subset $A$. For a <closed subset> $C\subseteq Y$, let $A=f^{-1}(C)$. Then $f(A)\subseteq C$ and therefore $\operatorname{cl}(f(A))\subseteq C$. By the assumed inclusion, $f(\operatorname{cl}(A))\subseteq C$, so $\operatorname{cl}(A)\subseteq f^{-1}(C)=A$. Since $A\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A)$ always, $A$ is closed. Thus every <closed set> has closed <preimage>, equivalently every <open set> has open <preimage>. This is <continuity>, proving **the claimed equivalence**.

For each integer $n\ge1$, the <open balls> $\{B(x,1/n):x\in M\}$ cover the <compact metric space> $M$. <Compactness> supplies a finite subcover with centres forming a <finite set> $S_n$. Define $S=\bigcup_{n\ge1}S_n$, a <countable union> of <finite sets> and therefore countable.

For any $x\in M$ and $\varepsilon>0$, choose $n$ with $1/n<\varepsilon$. One of the covering balls contains $x$, so there is $s\in S_n\subseteq S$ with $d(x,s)<1/n<\varepsilon$. Every open <neighbourhood> of $x$ therefore meets $S$, giving $x\in\operatorname{cl}(S)$. Thus **$\operatorname{cl}(S)=M$, and $S$ is a countable <dense subset>**. If $M$ is empty, simply take $S=\varnothing$.

For an integer $n\ge0$ and $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, the definition of the <Laplace transform> gives $F_0(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,dt=1/s$. For $n\ge1$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
F_n(s)=\left[-\frac{t^ne^{-st}}s\right]_0^\infty+\frac ns\int_0^\infty t^{n-1}e^{-st}\,dt=\frac nsF_{n-1}(s).
$$
The endpoint term vanishes because <exponential decay> dominates every fixed power of $t$. Induction therefore gives $F_n(s)=n!/s^{n+1}$. Multiplication by $e^{at}$ replaces $e^{-st}$ by $e^{-(s-a)t}$ in the defining <integral>, so
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(t^n)(s)=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad\mathcal L(e^{at}t^n)(s)=\frac{n!}{(s-a)^{n+1}}.}
$$
The second formula holds for $\operatorname{Re}(s-a)>0$ and is the <Laplace transform shift theorem>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

By the <Bromwich inversion formula>, for $t>0$ and $\gamma>0$,
$$
f(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}\frac{e^{st}}{s^2(s+1)^2}\,ds.
$$
Close the upward vertical segment by a left semicircle of radius $R$ centred at $\gamma$. On that arc $\operatorname{Re}s\le\gamma$, so $|e^{st}|\le e^{\gamma t}$, while $|s^2(s+1)^2|$ is bounded below by a constant times $R^4$ for large $R$. Its length is $\pi R$, hence the arc <integral> tends to zero as $O(R^{-3})$. The <contour> is positively oriented and encloses the <double poles> at $0$ and $-1$.

Their <residues> are
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{s=0}\frac{e^{st}}{s^2(s+1)^2}=\left.\frac d{ds}\frac{e^{st}}{(s+1)^2}\right|_{s=0}=t-2,
$$
and
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{s=-1}\frac{e^{st}}{s^2(s+1)^2}=\left.\frac d{ds}\frac{e^{st}}{s^2}\right|_{s=-1}=(t+2)e^{-t}.
$$
The <residue theorem> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{f(t)=t-2+(t+2)e^{-t}\qquad(t\ge0).}
$$
The value at zero follows by <continuity> and is $f(0)=0$.

Part (i) gives $\mathcal L(t)=1/s^2$ and $\mathcal L(te^{-t})=1/(s+1)^2$. The <convolution theorem for Laplace transforms> therefore gives
$$
f(t)=\int_0^t(t-v)ve^{-v}\,dv=t\int_0^tve^{-v}\,dv-\int_0^tv^2e^{-v}\,dv.

Repeated <integration by parts> yields

$$
Substitution gives **$f(t)=t-2+(t+2)e^{-t}$**, agreeing with the <contour integration> result.

= Solution
{parent=16E}

For fixed endpoints, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $F_y-\frac d{dx}F_{y'}=0$. Since $F$ has no explicit dependence on $x$, differentiating gives
$$
\frac d{dx}(F-y'F_{y'})=F_yy'+F_{y'}y''-y''F_{y'}-y'\frac d{dx}F_{y'}=y'\left(F_y-\frac d{dx}F_{y'}\right)=0.
$$
Thus **$F-y'F_{y'}=C$**, the <Beltrami identity>.

With $r'=dr/d\theta$, the <arc length> element and the prescribed speed give the travel-time <functional>
$$
\boxed{T[r]=\frac1k\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\sqrt{r'^2+r^2}}{r^2}\,d\theta.}
$$
For the circular road $r=a$, this is **$T_{\mathrm{circle}}=\pi/(2ka)$**.

For the integrand $F=\sqrt{r'^2+r^2}/r^2$, the <Beltrami identity> simplifies to
$$
F-r'F_{r'}=\frac1{\sqrt{r'^2+r^2}}=C.
$$
The candidate $r=a(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)$ has $r'^2+r^2=2a^2$ and the required endpoints. To prove it really minimizes the travel time, rather than merely satisfies a <first integral>, put $u=1/r$. Then
$$
\frac{\sqrt{r'^2+r^2}}{r^2}=\sqrt{u'^2+u^2}.
$$
This is the Euclidean <arc length> integrand for the inverted curve $(X,Y)=(u\cos\theta,u\sin\theta)$, whose endpoints are $(1/a,0)$ and $(0,1/a)$. Every connecting curve has length at least their <Euclidean distance> $\sqrt2/a$, by the <triangle inequality>. Equality is attained by the straight <line segment> $X+Y=1/a$. In polar form it gives $u(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)=1/a$, hence the proposed $r$. It stays strictly positive throughout the interval. Consequently
$$
\boxed{r(\theta)=a(\cos\theta+\sin\theta),\qquad T_{\min}=\frac{\sqrt2}{ka}.}
$$
This <travel-time minimization with speed proportional to squared radius> is globally solved by the inversion: its minimum is strictly less than $\pi/(2ka)$.

= Solution

In the <pion> <rest frame>, conservation of three-momentum makes the <electron> and massless <neutrino> momenta equal and opposite, of common magnitude $p$. Their energies obey
$$
E_e+pc=m_\pi c^2,\qquad E_e^2=p^2c^2+m_e^2c^4.
$$
Squaring $E_e=m_\pi c^2-pc$ and cancelling $p^2c^2$ gives
$$
pc=\frac{m_\pi^2-m_e^2}{2m_\pi}c^2,\qquad E_e=\frac{m_\pi^2+m_e^2}{2m_\pi}c^2.
$$
The <electron> <four-momentum> satisfies $p=E_ev/c^2$. Thus, for the physically allowed decay $m_\pi>m_e$,
$$
\boxed{v=c\frac{m_\pi^2-m_e^2}{m_\pi^2+m_e^2}=c\frac{1-(m_e/m_\pi)^2}{1+(m_e/m_\pi)^2}.}
$$

= 18D
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Fluid Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=18D}

For constant <mass density> and <incompressible flow>, use $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$ in the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>. Taking the <curl> removes the <pressure> and <kinetic energy> <gradients>, giving $\boldsymbol\omega_t=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega)$. The supplied vector identity, with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=0$, yields the <vorticity equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=\boldsymbol\omega_t+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
In a two-dimensional flow $\mathbf u=(u,v,0)$ is independent of $z$, and $\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\mathbf e_z$. The right side vanishes, so $D\omega/Dt=0$: each material element retains its <vorticity>. The <vortex lines> are parallel to $z$ and are carried by the planar velocity. Also planar incompressibility preserves the area of a material cross-section; integrating its conserved <vorticity> over that area shows that its <circulation>, or vortex strength, stays constant. Thus the <vortex lines> move with the fluid without changing strength.

Place a <line vortex> of <circulation> $\Gamma$ at $(0,b)$ above the wall $y=0$. Its <velocity field> is $\Gamma\mathbf e_z\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)/(2\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^2)$. The <method of images> puts a vortex of <circulation> $-\Gamma$ at $(0,-b)$. With $\mathbf u=(\psi_y,-\psi_x)$, the resulting <stream function> is
$$
\psi(x,y)=-\frac\Gamma{2\pi}\log\sqrt{x^2+(y-b)^2}+\frac\Gamma{2\pi}\log\sqrt{x^2+(y+b)^2}.
$$
It is identically zero on the wall, so $u_y=-\psi_x=0$ there. In the fluid half-plane it has just the required vortex singularity, with no other singularities, and its velocity decays at infinity. This is the no-background-flow solution with the wall: the difference from another such solution is a regular irrotational, divergence-free decaying field with zero normal <velocity>. Reflect its tangential component evenly and its normal component oddly across the wall. The reflected components are bounded harmonic functions on the whole plane, so they are constant by <harmonic Liouville theorem>, and decay forces both constants to be zero. Thus replacing the wall by the image gives the same flow. The real vortex moves under the image velocity, excluding its own singular velocity, so
$$
\dot{\mathbf r}_0=\frac{-\Gamma}{2\pi}\frac{\mathbf e_z\times(0,2b)}{4b^2}=\left(\frac\Gamma{4\pi b},0\right).
$$
Its **signed velocity parallel to the wall is $\Gamma/(4\pi b)$**, and its speed is $|\Gamma|/(4\pi b)$.

For the quarter-plane, place images of <circulation> $-\Gamma$ at $(-x,y)$ and $(x,-y)$, and $+\Gamma$ at $(-x,-y)$. The paired <stream functions> make both walls <streamlines>. Summing their velocities at the real vortex gives
$$
\dot x=\frac\Gamma{4\pi}\left(\frac1y-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right)=\frac{\Gamma x^2}{4\pi y(x^2+y^2)},\qquad\dot y=\frac\Gamma{4\pi}\left(-\frac1x+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right)=-\frac{\Gamma y^2}{4\pi x(x^2+y^2)}.
$$
In <polar coordinates>,
$$
\dot r=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{r}=\frac\Gamma{2\pi r}\cot2\theta,\qquad\dot\theta=\frac{x\dot y-y\dot x}{r^2}=-\frac\Gamma{4\pi r^2}.
$$
It follows directly that
$$
\frac d{dt}(r\sin2\theta)=\dot r\sin2\theta+2r\dot\theta\cos2\theta=0.
$$
Thus the <line-vortex trajectory in a quarter-plane> is **$r\sin2\theta=\text{constant}$**.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write $S_{xx}=\sum_i x_i^2$ and $\bar Y=n^{-1}\sum_iY_i$. For an identifiable two-parameter <linear regression> assume $S_{xx}>0$; for the <variance> estimate and test below also assume $n>2$. The <least-squares normal equations> are
$$
\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)=0,\qquad\sum_i x_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)=0.
$$
Using $\sum_i x_i=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat\alpha=\bar Y,\qquad\widehat\beta=\frac{\sum_i x_iY_i}{S_{xx}}.}
$$
The <residual sum of squares> has positive-definite quadratic part $n\alpha^2+S_{xx}\beta^2$, so these equations give its unique minimum. The <normal distribution> of the independent errors gives the <log-likelihood>
$$
\ell(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(Y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2.
$$
For every fixed positive $\sigma^2$, maximizing this over $\alpha,\beta$ is exactly minimizing the same <residual sum of squares>. Thus the <ordinary least squares estimators> are also the <maximum likelihood estimators>.

The printed hypotheses do not explicitly require $S_{xx}>0$ or $n>2$. If $S_{xx}=0$, all $x_i$ vanish and $\beta$ is unidentifiable; this is an intercept-only model with $n-1$ residual degrees of freedom. For $n=1$ its residual is identically zero, so the likelihood has no finite maximizer with positive variance. If $S_{xx}>0$ but $n=2$, the fit is saturated, leaving no residual <variance> estimate or Student test with positive degrees of freedom. The following nondegenerate formulas use $S_{xx}>0$ and $n>2$.

= ii
{parent=19c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $\operatorname{RSS}=\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\alpha-\widehat\beta x_i)^2$. Differentiating the <log-likelihood> in $v=\sigma^2$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v}=-\frac n{2v}+\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{2v^2}.
$$
For $\operatorname{RSS}>0$ this changes from positive to negative at $v=\operatorname{RSS}/n$, so
$$
\boxed{\widehat\sigma^2=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}n.}
$$
To find its bias, substitute the model and use the orthogonal fit directions $\mathbf1$ and $\mathbf x$, obtaining
$$
\operatorname{RSS}=\sum_i\varepsilon_i^2-n\bar\varepsilon^2-\frac{(\sum_i x_i\varepsilon_i)^2}{S_{xx}}.
$$
The three expectations are $n\sigma^2$, $\sigma^2$, and $\sigma^2$, respectively: $\operatorname{Var}(\bar\varepsilon)=\sigma^2/n$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\sum_i x_i\varepsilon_i)=\sigma^2S_{xx}$. Hence $\mathbb E\operatorname{RSS}=(n-2)\sigma^2$, giving the <unbiased estimator>
$$
\boxed{s^2=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{n-2}=\frac n{n-2}\widehat\sigma^2.}
$$
With $n>2$, a zero <residual sum of squares> is a probability-zero event in the model. For that exceptional dataset the likelihood is unbounded as $\sigma^2\downarrow0$, rather than having a maximum at a positive <variance>. In the intercept-only case $S_{xx}=0$, the corresponding unbiased multiplier is $n/(n-1)$ for $n>1$.

= iii
{parent=19c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Set $\mathbf e=\mathbf1/\sqrt n$ and $\mathbf f=\mathbf x/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$. These are orthonormal because $\sum_i x_i=0$. Complete them to an <orthonormal basis> $\mathbf e,\mathbf f,\mathbf q_3,\ldots,\mathbf q_n$ of $\mathbb R^n$. An <orthogonal transformation> of the standard normal error vector $\boldsymbol\varepsilon/\sigma$ has independent <normal random variables> as coordinates $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$: its <joint probability density> remains $(2\pi)^{-n/2}\exp(-\|z\|^2/2)$ because the transformation preserves squared length and has <Jacobian determinant> of absolute value one.

Projection onto these <basis> directions gives
$$
\widehat\alpha=\alpha+\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}Z_1,\qquad\widehat\beta=\beta+\frac\sigma{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}Z_2,\qquad\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{\sigma^2}=\sum_{j=3}^nZ_j^2.
$$
Therefore the <joint distribution of least-squares and variance estimators> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\alpha\sim N(\alpha,\sigma^2/n),\quad\widehat\beta\sim N(\beta,\sigma^2/S_{xx}),\quad\frac{n\widehat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-2},\quad\text{all three independent}.}
$$
The last law is the <chi-squared distribution> of a sum of $n-2$ squared independent <normal random variables> with mean zero and variance one, and <independence> follows because the three statistics use disjoint independent coordinate sets. When all $x_i=0$, omit the slope direction: $\widehat\alpha$ and $n\widehat\sigma^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ are independent, and there is no identifiable slope estimator.

= iv
{parent=19c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Under the <null hypothesis>, the standardized intercept $Z=\sqrt n(\widehat\alpha-\alpha_0)/\sigma$ is standard normal, and $V=\operatorname{RSS}/\sigma^2$ is independent with <chi-squared distribution> of $\nu=n-2$ degrees of freedom. Consequently the <Student t test for a simple-regression intercept> uses
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{\widehat\alpha-\alpha_0}{s/\sqrt n}=\frac Z{\sqrt{V/\nu}}\sim t_{n-2}\quad\text{under }H_0.}
$$
Choose a <significance level> $\gamma\in(0,1)$ before observing the test result. For the two-sided alternative, **reject $H_0$ when $|T|>t_{n-2,\,1-\gamma/2}$**, where the subscript denotes the indicated <quantile> of the <Student t-distribution>. The null rejection probability is exactly $\gamma$, independent of the nuisance slope and <variance>. Equivalently report the <p-value> $2[1-F_{t_{n-2}}(|T_{\mathrm{obs}}|)]$. The test assumes the normal independent-error model and a positive residual <variance> estimate. In the intercept-only case with $n>1$, use $s^2=\operatorname{RSS}/(n-1)$ and $t_{n-1}$ instead.

Divide the pivot row by $6$, then eliminate $x_1$ from the other rows. The resulting <simplex dictionary> is

\begin{aligned}

All <slack variables> and original variables are nonnegative. The last row therefore proves $P\le8$ for every <feasible point>; setting the nonbasic variables $x_3,z_1,z_2$ to zero attains it and leaves all basic variables nonnegative. Hence
$$
\boxed{(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(\tfrac12,3,0),\qquad\min(2x_1-3x_2-2x_3)=-8.}
