= Solution
{parent=a}

Take the cube with vertices $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$, and choose the edge $E=\{(1,1,z):-1\leq z\leq1\}$. Here the <symmetry group of a cube> consists of all rigid symmetries, including reflections. A symmetry fixes the centre and permutes the three pairs of opposite faces, so its matrix is a <signed permutation matrix>.

A symmetry in the <stabilizer subgroup> of $E$ must preserve its midpoint $(1,1,0)$ and send its direction $(0,0,1)$ to itself or its negative. In this <cube symmetry action on edges>, its only choices are to interchange the first two coordinates and to reverse the third coordinate. Thus the <stabilizer subgroup> consists of
$$
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,z),\quad(y,x,z),\quad(x,y,-z),\quad(y,x,-z).
$$
The two generating reflections commute and have order two, so this <subgroup> is a <Klein four-group>, isomorphic to $C_2\times C_2$ and has order four. Permuting coordinates and changing signs carries $E$ to any edge. Its <orbit of a group action> is therefore the set of all twelve edges. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{|G|=12\cdot4=48.}
$$
If one restricts to orientation-preserving symmetries, only two of the four stabilizer elements remain, giving the <rotational symmetry group of a cube> of order $24$; the full symmetry <group> asked for here has order $48$.

A <permutation> preserves whether the two coordinates are equal. Consequently the diagonal and its complement are invariant under this <group action>. Any $(i,i)$ can be carried to any $(j,j)$ by a <permutation> sending $i$ to $j$. Likewise, for $i\ne j$ and $k\ne l$, the assignment $i\mapsto k$, $j\mapsto l$ extends to a <permutation> of the remaining letters.

For $n\geq2$ the <equality-pattern orbits of ordered pairs> are the two <orbits of a group action> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\{(i,i):1\leq i\leq n\},\qquad\{(i,j):i\ne j\},}
$$
with sizes $n$ and $n(n-1)$. For $n=1$ only the diagonal <orbit of a group action> exists.

= Solution

<Lagrange's theorem> says that for a <finite group> $G$ and a <subgroup> $H$,
$$
|G|=[G:H]|H|.
$$
In particular, $|H|$ divides $|G|$. To prove it, consider the left <cosets> $gH$. Every element lies in one, and if $gH$ and $g'H$ intersect, then $gh=g'h'$ for some $h,h'\in H$. Hence $g=g'h'h^{-1}$ and $gH=g'H$. Distinct <cosets> are therefore disjoint and partition $G$. Multiplication by $g$ is a <bijection> $H\to gH$, so every <coset> has $|H|$ elements. Counting this partition proves the formula.

Write the <dihedral group> as
$$
D_{2n}=\langle r,s:r^n=s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle,
$$
where $r$ is a rotation and $s$ a reflection. If $k\mid n$, the <cyclic subgroup> $\langle r^{n/k}\rangle$ has order $k$. If $k\mid2n$ but $k\nmid n$, then $k$ is even: an odd divisor of $2n$ would divide $n$. Put $m=k/2$, so $m\mid n$. The <subgroup>
$$
\boxed{\langle r^{n/m},s\rangle}
$$
contains exactly the $m$ rotations $r^{jn/m}$ and the $m$ reflections $r^{jn/m}s$, for $0\leq j<m$. The inversion relation makes this set closed under multiplication and inverses. These $2m=k$ elements are distinct, so it has the required order. This proves the <dihedral subgroups of every divisor order> property. The construction works for every even divisor, including those already covered by the cyclic construction.

Differentiate the position vector with respect to each of the <spherical polar coordinates>. The derivatives are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_r\mathbf x&=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),\\
\partial_\theta\mathbf x&=r(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta),\\
\partial_\phi\mathbf x&=r\sin\theta(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0).
\end{aligned}
$$
Their lengths give the <scale factors of orthogonal coordinates>:
$$
\boxed{h_r=1,\qquad h_\theta=r,\qquad h_\phi=r\sin\theta.}
$$
Dividing the derivatives by these lengths yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf e_r&=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),\\
\mathbf e_\theta&=(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta),\\
\mathbf e_\phi&=(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0).
\end{aligned}
$$
Each has unit length. For example, $\mathbf e_r\cdot\mathbf e_\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta(\cos^2\phi+\sin^2\phi)-\cos\theta\sin\theta=0$; the other two <dot products> vanish because their first two terms cancel. Thus these are mutually <orthogonal vectors>, and the total differential has the required form. The coordinates degenerate at $r=0$ and on the polar axis, but this does not affect an area integral.

On the <parametrized surface> $\theta=\alpha$, use $r,\phi$ as parameters. The <surface integral> area element is
$$
dS=|\partial_r\mathbf x\times\partial_\phi\mathbf x|\,dr\,d\phi=r\sin\alpha\,dr\,d\phi.
$$
For $0\leq\alpha\leq\pi$, its lateral area, with no closing disk, is therefore
$$
\boxed{A=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^R r\sin\alpha\,dr\,d\phi=\pi R^2\sin\alpha.}
$$
At $\alpha=0,\pi$ the surface collapses to a line and its area is zero.

= Solution
{parent=4C}

In <Cartesian coordinates>, independent coordinate variables satisfy
$$
\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial x_j}=\delta_{ij},\qquad
\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_j}=\frac{x_j}{r}\quad(r>0),
$$
where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. Applying the <product rule> and <chain rule> to $u_i=r^\alpha x_i$ gives the <Jacobian matrix>
$$
\boxed{d_{ij}=r^\alpha\delta_{ij}+\alpha r^{\alpha-2}x_ix_j.}
$$
This <tensor> is symmetric in $i,j$. Hence its contraction with the antisymmetric <Levi-Civita symbol> vanishes:
$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf u)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}d_{kj}=0.
$$
Similarly, $v_i=\epsilon_{ijk}k_ju_k$ and the constancy of $\mathbf k$ give
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=\epsilon_{ijk}k_jd_{ki}=0.
$$
The trace of the <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=(3+\alpha)r^\alpha,
$$
which vanishes for $\alpha=-3$. The <curl of a cross product> identity, with $\mathbf k$ constant, now gives
$$
\nabla\times(\mathbf k\times\mathbf u)
=\mathbf k(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)-(\mathbf k\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.
$$
For $\alpha=-3$,
$$
(\mathbf k\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=r^{-3}\mathbf k-3r^{-5}(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf v=\frac{3(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x-r^2\mathbf k}{r^5}.}
$$
All identities for this singular case hold on $r>0$. In particular, zero <divergence> here is a pointwise assertion away from the origin, not an assertion that the flux through a sphere enclosing the singularity is zero.

The <order of a group element> $g$ is the least positive integer $m$ for which $g^m=e$, if one exists. In a <finite group>, two terms of $e,g,g^2,\ldots,g^{|G|}$ must agree. If $g^i=g^j$ with $i<j$, cancellation gives $g^{j-i}=e$. Thus such positive integers exist and have a least member: **every element of a <finite group> has finite order**.

If $h=aga^{-1}$, repeated cancellation shows $h^m=ag^ma^{-1}$ for every positive integer $m$. Consequently $h^m=e$ exactly when $g^m=e$. The least positive exponents are therefore equal:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(aga^{-1})=\operatorname{ord}(g).}
$$
This proof also shows that <conjugation> preserves infinite order when the ambient <group> is not finite.

For a <permutation> $g$, start with a letter $i$ and follow $i,g(i),g^2(i),\ldots$. Finiteness gives a repetition; since $g$ is invertible, the first return is to $i$. The visited letters form a <permutation cycle> on which $g$ acts by moving to the next letter. If there are letters left, repeat the construction starting with one of them. Its trajectory cannot meet an earlier one, because applying a suitable inverse power of $g$ would put its starting letter in that earlier trajectory. Continuing partitions the letters into disjoint cycles, including fixed points as cycles of length one.

The resulting <disjoint permutation cycles> have disjoint supports, and their product agrees with $g$ on every letter. This proves the <cycle decomposition of a permutation>. A power $g^m$ fixes a cycle of length $l$ exactly when $l\mid m$, so if the cycle lengths are $l_1,\ldots,l_t$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}(g)=\operatorname{lcm}(l_1,\ldots,l_t).}
$$
The <least common multiple> is essential: multiplying the lengths would overcount whenever cycles share a factor.

The <alternating group> is the <subgroup> of the <symmetric group> consisting of <even permutations>:
$$
A_n=\ker(\operatorname{sgn}:S_n\to\{1,-1\}).
$$
For a <permutation cycle> of length $l$,
$$
(a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_l)=(a_1\ a_l)(a_1\ a_{l-1})\cdots(a_1\ a_2),
$$
using rightmost-first composition. Its <sign of a permutation> is therefore $(-1)^{l-1}$. Multiplicativity of the <sign homomorphism> gives
$$
\operatorname{sgn}(g)=(-1)^{\sum_j(l_j-1)}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{g\in A_n\iff\text{the number of even-length cycles is even}.}
$$
Equivalently, $n-t$ is even when $t$ counts all cycles, including fixed points. Odd-length cycles contribute positive sign and impose no further restriction.

Extend $\sigma\in S_n$ to a <permutation> $\widehat\sigma$ of $n+2$ letters that fixes the last two letters. Let $t=(n+1\ n+2)$, and let $\epsilon(\sigma)$ be $0$ or $1$ according as $\sigma$ is even or odd. Define
$$
\iota(\sigma)=\widehat\sigma\,t^{\epsilon(\sigma)}.
$$
The two factors have disjoint supports and commute. Their signs cancel, so $\iota(\sigma)\in A_{n+2}$. Additivity of <parity of a permutation> modulo two gives
$$
\iota(\sigma\tau)=\widehat\sigma\widehat\tau\,t^{\epsilon(\sigma)+\epsilon(\tau)}
=\iota(\sigma)\iota(\tau).
$$
Thus $\iota$ is a <group homomorphism>. Its restriction to the first $n$ letters recovers $\sigma$, making it injective. Its image is therefore a <subgroup> isomorphic to $S_n$:
$$
\boxed{S_n\cong\iota(S_n)\leq A_{n+2}.}
$$
This <group embedding> works for every positive $n$, including the trivial case $n=1$.

For either coefficient system $\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb F_p$, multiplying two matrices keeps all entries in that system, and multiplicativity of the <determinant> preserves <determinant> one. The identity matrix has <determinant> one, and for a determinant-one matrix the <matrix inverse> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}
=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its entries again belong to the same coefficient system and its <determinant> is one. Together with associativity of <matrix multiplication>, these facts establish all the <group axioms>. Thus both sets are <special linear groups>.

Reduce each entry modulo five to define
$$
\pi:SL_2(\mathbb Z)\longrightarrow SL_2(\mathbb F_5),\qquad M\longmapsto\overline M.
$$
Reduction respects sums and products, so $\overline{MN}=\overline M\,\overline N$. It also respects the <determinant>, ensuring that the image has <determinant> one. Hence $\pi$ is a <group homomorphism>. Its <kernel of a group homomorphism> consists exactly of determinant-one integer matrices congruent to the identity modulo five:
$$
\boxed{\ker\pi=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+5a&5b\\5c&1+5d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_2(\mathbb Z):a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z\right\}.}
$$
The determinant-one condition remains part of this set; the four integers cannot be chosen independently without it. A <kernel of a group homomorphism> is a <normal subgroup>, since $\pi(gkg^{-1})=\pi(g)e\pi(g)^{-1}=e$ for $k\in\ker\pi$. This is the <principal congruence subgroup> of level five.

The <general linear group> $GL_2(\mathbb F_5)$ consists of invertible two-by-two matrices over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_5$, under <matrix multiplication>. Such a matrix has two <linearly independent> columns. There are $5^2-1=24$ choices for a nonzero first column, and $5^2-5=20$ choices for a second column outside its one-dimensional span. Thus
$$
\boxed{|GL_2(\mathbb F_5)|=24\cdot20=480.}
$$
The <determinant> is a <group homomorphism>
$$
\det:GL_2(\mathbb F_5)\longrightarrow\mathbb F_5^\times,
$$
where $\mathbb F_5^\times=\{1,2,3,4\}$ is the multiplicative <group> of nonzero field elements. It is surjective because $\operatorname{diag}(t,1)$ has <determinant> $t$. Its <kernel of a group homomorphism> is $SL_2(\mathbb F_5)$. The <first isomorphism theorem> gives a quotient of order four, whence
$$
\boxed{|SL_2(\mathbb F_5)|=480/4=120.}
$$
The nonzero squares form the <subgroup> $\{1,4\}$ of $\mathbb F_5^\times$. Its inverse image
$$
\boxed{H=\{M\in GL_2(\mathbb F_5):\det M\in\{1,4\}\}}
$$
is a <subgroup> with index two. Indeed, composing the <determinant> with the quotient map to $\mathbb F_5^\times/\{1,4\}$ makes $H$ the kernel of a surjective <group homomorphism> to a group of order two. Thus $|H|=240$.

For a <group action>, the <orbit-stabilizer theorem> identifies the <orbit of a group action> of $x$ with the left <cosets> of its <stabilizer subgroup>:
$$
G/G_x\longrightarrow Gx,\qquad gG_x\longmapsto gx.
$$
This is well defined and bijective because $gx=hx$ exactly when $h^{-1}g\in G_x$. In particular, for finite $G$,
$$
\boxed{|Gx|=[G:G_x],\qquad |G|=|Gx||G_x|.}
$$
Under the <conjugation action> $g\cdot x=gxg^{-1}$, a singleton <orbit of a group action> means that $gxg^{-1}=x$ for every $g$. This is equivalent to $gx=xg$ for every $g$, so precisely these elements constitute the <centre of a group>
$$
Z(G)=\{x\in G:xg=gx\text{ for all }g\in G\}.
$$
The identity is central, and if $x,y$ commute with every element then so do $xy$ and $x^{-1}$. Thus $Z(G)$ is a <subgroup>. Each central element is fixed under every conjugation, giving $gZ(G)g^{-1}=Z(G)$: **the centre is a <normal subgroup>**.

By <Lagrange's theorem>, a <stabilizer subgroup> of $G$ has order $p^j$ for some $0\leq j\leq n$. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> therefore gives
$$
|Y|=p^{n-j}.
$$
Thus an <orbit of a group action> either has one element or has size divisible by $p$, even if the acted-on set is infinite.

Apply this to the <conjugation action>. Its singleton <orbits of a group action> are the elements of the <centre of a group>, while every other orbit has size divisible by $p$. Partitioning the finite <group> into these orbits gives the <class equation>, and hence
$$
|Z(G)|\equiv|G|\equiv0\pmod p.
$$
Since the identity belongs to $Z(G)$, its order is positive and divisible by $p$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{|Z(G)|\geq p>1.}
$$
Now suppose that $|Z(G)|=p^{n-1}$. It is then proper, so choose $x\notin Z(G)$. The <centralizer>
$$
C_G(x)=\{g\in G:gx=xg\}
$$
is a <subgroup>: commuting with $x$ is preserved by multiplication and inverses. It contains $Z(G)$ and also $x$, so $|C_G(x)|>|Z(G)|=p^{n-1}$. By <Lagrange's theorem>, its order is a power of $p$ dividing $p^n$, forcing $C_G(x)=G$. But that would make $x$ central, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|Z(G)|\ne p^{n-1}.}
$$

When $[G:H]=2$, there are exactly two left <cosets> and two right <cosets>. For $g\notin H$, both $gH$ and $Hg$ are the complement $G\setminus H$; for $g\in H$, both equal $H$. This is the <index-two subgroup is normal> argument: $gH=Hg$ for every $g$, which is equivalent to $gHg^{-1}=H$. Therefore **every <subgroup> of index two is normal**.

In the <dihedral group> $D_{2n}=\langle r,s\rangle$, the rotation <cyclic subgroup> $\langle r\rangle$ has order $n$ and index two, so it is a <normal subgroup>. It is nontrivial and proper for the usual dihedral convention $n\geq2$ (polygon symmetries have $n\geq3$). If the degenerate convention $D_2=C_2$ were allowed, the claimed nontrivial proper <subgroup> would not exist; the rotation argument uses $n>1$.

For any $k\geq3$, let $G=S_k$ and take the <stabilizer subgroup> $H$ of the letter $1$. Its elements freely permute the other $k-1$ letters, so
$$
|G|=k!,\qquad |H|=(k-1)!,\qquad [G:H]=k.
$$
Conjugation by $(1\ 2)$ sends $H$ to the stabilizer of $2$, which is different: for instance $(2\ 3)$ belongs to $H$ but does not fix $2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{G=S_k,\quad H=\{\sigma:\sigma(1)=1\}}
$$
gives a nonnormal <subgroup> of every prescribed index $k\geq3$.

A <simple group> has no <normal subgroup> except the identity <subgroup> and the whole group. To prove the <simplicity of the alternating group A5>, we compute its <conjugacy classes>. Since the <sign homomorphism> is onto, $|A_5|=5!/2=60$. Its possible nonidentity <cycle types> are a three-cycle, a product of two disjoint transpositions, and a five-cycle.

There are $\binom53\cdot2=20$ three-cycles. The <centralizer> in $S_5$ of $(1\ 2\ 3)$ consists of its three powers and an independent interchange of the two unused letters. Only the three powers are even. Its <centralizer> in $A_5$ therefore has order three, giving one <conjugacy class> of size $60/3=20$ by the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>.

There are $5\cdot3=15$ products of two disjoint transpositions: choose the fixed letter and then a pairing of the other four letters. For $(1\ 2)(3\ 4)$, a commuting <permutation> fixes $5$ and may interchange the two pairs and swap within them, giving a <centralizer> of order eight in $S_5$. It contains an odd element, $(1\ 2)$, so exactly half its elements are even. The <centralizer> in $A_5$ has order four, and these fifteen elements form one <conjugacy class> of size $60/4=15$.

Finally, there are $4!=24$ five-cycles. A <permutation> commuting with a five-cycle is determined by the image of one letter and must be a power of that cycle. All five powers are even, so the <centralizer> in $A_5$ has order five. Each such <conjugacy class> has size $60/5=12$, giving two classes of size twelve.

A <normal subgroup> must be a union of <conjugacy classes> and must include the identity. Its order therefore has the form
$$
1+20a+15b+12c,\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\},\quad c\in\{0,1,2\}.
$$
The resulting distinct possibilities are
$$
1,13,16,21,25,28,33,36,40,45,48,60.
$$
Of these, only $1$ and $60$ divide $60$. <Lagrange's theorem> rules out every other possible order. Hence
$$
\boxed{A_5\text{ is simple}.}
$$

Here <isotropic tensors> are invariant under proper <rotation matrices>, $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$. The <isotropic second-rank tensor> and <isotropic third-rank tensor> forms in three dimensions are
$$
\boxed{T_{ij}=A\delta_{ij},\qquad T_{ijk}=B\epsilon_{ijk},}
$$
with scalar coefficients $A,B$. One can see completeness directly. A half-turn about each coordinate axis kills every off-diagonal component of a rank-two <tensor>, and quarter-turns make its three diagonal components equal. For rank three, the half-turns require that each coordinate label occur an odd number of times in a nonzero component. Thus only permutations of $(1,2,3)$ survive. Quarter-turns interchange two labels while reversing one sign, forcing the surviving components to alternate with the <parity of a permutation>. This gives a multiple of the <Levi-Civita symbol>.

The <tensor> transformation law verifies invariance of these forms:
$$
T'_{ij}=AR_{ip}R_{jq}\delta_{pq}=A\delta_{ij},\qquad
T'_{ijk}=BR_{ip}R_{jq}R_{kr}\epsilon_{pqr}=B(\det R)\epsilon_{ijk}=B\epsilon_{ijk}.
$$
The first equality uses <orthogonality> and the second uses the <determinant> formula. If reflections were also required while using the ordinary <tensor> transformation law, the rank-three coefficient would have to vanish. The proper-rotation convention is the one under which $B\epsilon_{ijk}$ is an <isotropic tensor>.

The ball is unchanged by any <rotation matrix>, and rotating coordinates has <Jacobian determinant> one. Thus changing variables $\mathbf y=R\mathbf x$ gives
$$
R_{i_1j_1}\cdots R_{i_nj_n}\int_Vx_{j_1}\cdots x_{j_n}\,dV
=\int_Vy_{i_1}\cdots y_{i_n}\,dV=T_{i_1\cdots i_n}.
$$
These moments are therefore <isotropic tensor integrals>. For the <Cartesian moments of a three-dimensional ball>, the second moment is $\alpha\delta_{ij}$. Taking its <tensor contraction> gives
$$
3\alpha=\int_Vr^2\,dV=4\pi\int_0^a r^4\,dr=\frac{4\pi a^5}{5},\qquad
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{4\pi a^5}{15}.}
$$
The third moment vanishes: $\mathbf x\mapsto-\mathbf x$ preserves the ball and changes the sign of its integrand. Equivalently, its symmetry under index interchange excludes a nonzero multiple of the antisymmetric <Levi-Civita symbol>.

The fourth moment is symmetric under every <permutation> of its four indices. The general rank-four <isotropic tensor> form consequently has equal coefficients, yielding
$$
T_{ijkl}=\beta(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).
$$
For example, comparing $T_{1122},T_{1212},T_{1221}$ shows that the three coefficients agree. Contracting $i=j$ and $k=l$ gives
$$
15\beta=\int_V r^4\,dV=4\pi\int_0^a r^6\,dr=\frac{4\pi a^7}{7},\qquad
\boxed{\beta=\frac{4\pi a^7}{105}.}
$$
Finally the <vector triple product identity> gives $\mathbf x\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)=r^2\boldsymbol\Omega-(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x$. The first term integrates to $3\alpha\boldsymbol\Omega$ and the second to $\alpha\boldsymbol\Omega$, using the second moment. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_V\mathbf x\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)\,dV=\frac{8\pi a^5}{15}\boldsymbol\Omega.}
$$

The <divergence theorem> states that a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a bounded region with piecewise smooth boundary satisfies
$$
\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\,dV=\int_{\partial V}\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dS,
$$
with the unit normal pointing out of $V$. Apply it separately to $\mathbf u=f\mathbf e_i$, where $\mathbf e_i$ is each constant Cartesian basis vector. Since $\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf e_i)=\partial_i f$ and $(f\mathbf e_i)\cdot\mathbf n=fn_i$, the three component identities combine to give
$$
\boxed{\int_V\nabla f\,dV=\int_{\partial V}f\,d\mathbf S.}
$$

For the specified <scalar field>, $\nabla f=(z,0,x)$. The $x$ term integrates to zero by reflection symmetry. At height $z$, the region has a disk cross-section of area $\pi(a^2-cz)$, and $0\leq z\leq a^2/c$. Therefore the <volume integral> is
$$
\int_V\nabla f\,dV
=\mathbf e_x\pi\int_0^{a^2/c}z(a^2-cz)\,dz
=\boxed{\frac{\pi a^6}{6c^2}\mathbf e_x.}
$$
To check the <surface integral> independently, split the boundary into the base disk and the curved graph $z=g(x,y)=(a^2-x^2-y^2)/c$. On the base $f=0$, so its contribution vanishes. The region lies below the curved graph, whose outward <vector surface element of a graph> is
$$
d\mathbf S=(-g_x,-g_y,1)\,dx\,dy=\left(\frac{2x}{c},\frac{2y}{c},1\right)dx\,dy.
$$
Over the disk $x^2+y^2\leq a^2$, the $y$ and $z$ components of $f\,d\mathbf S$ are odd in $x$ and integrate to zero. Its $x$ component gives, in <polar coordinates>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\partial V}f\,d\mathbf S
&=\frac{2\mathbf e_x}{c^2}\int_{x^2+y^2\leq a^2}x^2(a^2-x^2-y^2)\,dx\,dy\\
&=\frac{2\mathbf e_x}{c^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\phi\,d\phi\int_0^a s^3(a^2-s^2)\,ds\\
&=\frac{\pi a^6}{6c^2}\mathbf e_x.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the two explicitly evaluated integrals agree, with the outward orientation included.

= Solution

Integrating the local electric-field <divergence> equation and applying the <divergence theorem> gives <Gauss's law>:
$$
\boxed{Q=\int_V\rho\,dV=\varepsilon_0\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,dV
=\varepsilon_0\int_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S.}
$$
For a <point charge> at the origin, <spherical symmetry> requires $\mathbf E=E_r(r)\mathbf e_r$. A sphere of radius $r>0$ encloses charge $q$, so its electric flux is $4\pi r^2E_r(r)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf x)=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\mathbf e_r
=\frac{q\mathbf x}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}.}
$$
The <electrostatic potential> satisfies $E_r=-d\phi/dr$. Integrating and choosing the reference value $\phi(\infty)=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{\phi(\mathbf x)=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\quad(r>0).}
$$
An arbitrary additive constant gives any other choice of potential reference. The singularity at the origin is represented by the <Dirac delta distribution>; it is not a point where the ordinary derivatives of this field are finite.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Combining the electric-field equations gives the <Poisson equation> $\nabla^2\phi_i=-\rho_i/\varepsilon_0$. Apply the <product rule for divergence> to
$$
\mathbf F=\phi_1\nabla\phi_2-\phi_2\nabla\phi_1.
$$
The two cross terms in its <divergence> cancel, leaving
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\phi_1\nabla^2\phi_2-\phi_2\nabla^2\phi_1
=\frac{\phi_2\rho_1-\phi_1\rho_2}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$
This <electrostatic reciprocity identity> follows from the <divergence theorem>, and is exactly <Green's second identity> for these potentials. Integrating and rearranging gives
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\varepsilon_0}\int_V\phi_1\rho_2\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\phi_1\nabla\phi_2\cdot d\mathbf S
=\frac1{\varepsilon_0}\int_V\phi_2\rho_1\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\phi_2\nabla\phi_1\cdot d\mathbf S.}
$$
The boundary orientation is outward from the chosen region. For smooth charge densities this follows directly from classical differentiation. For isolated <point charges>, one may excise small spheres and take their radii to zero, provided the other potential is regular at each charge.

= Solution

Use the auxiliary <point charge> of strength $q\ne0$ at the origin, with potential $\phi_2=q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$ from part (a). Apply the <electrostatic reciprocity identity> of part (b) to the exterior region $a<r<R$, then let $R\to\infty$. Its inner boundary has outward normal $-\mathbf e_r$, and $\rho_2=0$ everywhere in this exterior region. The outer boundary terms vanish under the stipulated decay assumption.

On $r=a$, $\phi_1=\Phi$ and
$$
\nabla\phi_2\cdot(-\mathbf e_r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a^2}.
$$
Thus the surviving boundary term on the left is $\Phi q/\varepsilon_0$. On the right, $\phi_2$ is constant on the inner sphere, so its boundary term is
$$
\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}\int_{r=a}\nabla\phi_1\cdot(-\mathbf e_r)\,dS=0.
$$
Indeed, $\nabla\phi_1=-\mathbf E_1$, and this integral is the outward electric flux from the interior ball, whose enclosed charge is zero by the hypothesis and <Gauss's law>. No symmetry of the exterior charge distribution is needed.

The <electrostatic reciprocity identity> now reduces to
$$
\frac{q\Phi}{\varepsilon_0}
=\frac1{\varepsilon_0}\int_{r>a}\frac{q\rho_1(\mathbf x)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\,dV.
$$
Canceling $q/\varepsilon_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{r>a}\frac{\rho_1(\mathbf x)}{r}\,dV.}
$$
The inward normal on the inner boundary is essential to the positive sign in this result.

= Solution
{parent=12C}

Use <suffix notation>, with repeated indices summed. Contracting the two <Levi-Civita symbols> gives
$$
[\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)]_i
=\epsilon_{ijk}u_j\epsilon_{klm}\partial_lA_m
=u_j(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i).
$$
Applying the <product rule> to $h=A_iB_i$ and substituting the two evolution equations yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t h
&=B_iu_j\partial_iA_j-B_iu_j\partial_jA_i+B_i\partial_i\psi
+A_iB_j\partial_ju_i-A_iu_j\partial_jB_i\\
&=B_i\partial_i(u_jA_j+\psi)-u_j\partial_j(A_iB_i).
\end{aligned}
$$
In the second line we renamed dummy indices in $A_iB_j\partial_ju_i$ and then combined product derivatives. Hence
$$
\boxed{f=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A+\psi,\qquad
\partial_t h=\mathbf B\cdot\nabla f-\mathbf u\cdot\nabla h.}
$$
No zero-divergence assumption was used in this local identity.

When both fields are <solenoidal vector fields>, the <product rule for divergence> rewrites this as a <conservation law>:
$$
\partial_t h=\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf B-h\mathbf u).
$$
Because the volume is fixed, differentiation under its integral and the <divergence theorem> give
$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=\int_{\partial V}(f\mathbf B-h\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=0
$$
under the two tangency conditions. Thus **the volume integral is conserved**. This is <helicity conservation by tangent boundary conditions>; when $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, the density is the <magnetic helicity> density.

For the given axisymmetric field, the <curl in spherical coordinates> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
B_r&=\frac1{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta A_\phi)
=2(a^2-r^2)\cos\theta,\\
B_\theta&=-\frac1r\partial_r(rA_\phi)
=(4r^2-2a^2)\sin\theta,\\
B_\phi&=\frac1r\partial_r(rA_\theta)=3ar\sin\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$
There are no $\phi$ derivatives and $A_r=0$. Consequently
$$
\begin{aligned}
h&=A_\theta B_\theta+A_\phi B_\phi\\
&=ar^2(4r^2-2a^2)\sin^2\theta+3ar^2(a^2-r^2)\sin^2\theta\\
&=\boxed{ar^2(a^2+r^2)\sin^2\theta.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The <volume form> in <spherical polar coordinates> is $r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
H&=2\pi a\int_0^a r^4(a^2+r^2)\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{8\pi a}{3}\left(\frac{a^7}{5}+\frac{a^7}{7}\right)
=\boxed{\frac{32\pi a^8}{35}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $B_r=0$ at $r=a$, as required for the magnetic-field tangency condition in the conservation argument; tangency of $\mathbf u$ is a separate hypothesis.
