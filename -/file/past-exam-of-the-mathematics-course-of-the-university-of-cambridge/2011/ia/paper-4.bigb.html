= Solution
{parent=1E}

An <inverse function> of $f:X\to Y$ is a function $h:Y\to X$ satisfying both
$$
h\circ f=\operatorname{id}_X,\qquad f\circ h=\operatorname{id}_Y.
$$
These are two-sided inverse identities. If $f(x)=f(x')$, apply $h$ to get $x=x'$, so $f$ is <injective>. For every $y\in Y$, the identity $f(h(y))=y$ supplies a preimage, so $f$ is <surjective>. Thus $f$ is a <bijection>.

Conversely, if $f$ is a <bijection>, each $y\in Y$ has exactly one preimage. Define $h(y)$ to be that preimage. Then $f(h(y))=y$ and $h(f(x))=x$, giving both identities. Hence **a function has a two-sided inverse exactly when it is a bijection**. This also makes its <inverse function> unique.

= Solution

**Always true.** If $(f\circ g)(x)=(f\circ g)(x')$, <injectivity> of $f$ gives $g(x)=g(x')$, and <injectivity> of $g$ gives $x=x'$. Thus the <function composition> is <injective>. For any $y\in X$, since $f$ is <surjective>, choose $z$ with $f(z)=y$. Since $g$ is <surjective>, choose $x$ with $g(x)=z$. Then $(f\circ g)(x)=y$, proving that the composition is <surjective>. Consequently $f\circ g$ is a <bijection>; its <inverse function> is $g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**Can be false.** Take $X=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ and define
$$
g(n)=n+1,\qquad f(0)=0,\qquad f(n)=n-1\quad(n\geq1).
$$
Then $(f\circ g)(n)=n$, so their <function composition> is the <identity function> and hence a <bijection>. However, $g$ is not <surjective>, since zero has no preimage, while $f$ is not <injective>, since $f(0)=f(1)=0$.

The valid implications are only that $g$ must be <injective> and $f$ must be <surjective>: equality of two $g$ values implies equality after composition, and every output of the composition is an output of $f$. On a finite $X$, either of these properties implies a <bijection>, so an infinite set is essential to this counterexample.

An <equivalence relation> is a <binary relation> that is simultaneously a <reflexive relation>, a <symmetric relation> and a <transitive relation>: $x\sim x$; $x\sim y$ implies $y\sim x$; and $x\sim y$, $y\sim z$ imply $x\sim z$. The <equivalence class> of $x$ is
$$
[x]=\{y\in X:y\sim x\}.
$$
Each <equivalence class> is nonempty by <reflexivity>, and every $x$ belongs to $[x]$, so the classes cover $X$. If $z\in[x]\cap[y]$, then the <symmetric relation> and <transitive relation> properties give $x\sim z\sim y$. Every $w\in[x]$ therefore satisfies $w\sim x\sim y$, so $[x]\subseteq[y]$; the reverse inclusion follows similarly. Thus two classes are either identical or disjoint. The distinct <equivalence classes> consequently form a <set partition> of $X$.

The divisibility-comparability relation is a <reflexive relation> and a <symmetric relation>, but **is not an equivalence relation**. Indeed, $2\sim6$ and $6\sim3$, whereas neither $2\mid3$ nor $3\mid2$, so <transitivity> fails.

For the requested four-class construction, partition the positive integers into
$$
C_1=\{1\},\quad C_2=\{2\},\quad
C_3=\{3,5,7,\ldots\},\quad C_4=\{4,6,8,\ldots\}.
$$
Define $x\approx y$ exactly when they lie in the same $C_j$. Equality of the class label is an <equivalence relation>. **Its four classes are the two finite singletons and the two infinite parity classes displayed above.**

= Solution
{parent=3B}

In a short time $dt$, the radius vector sweeps an oriented area $d\mathcal A=\tfrac12r^2d\theta$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathcal A}{dt}=\frac h2.}
$$
The constant $h$ is twice the oriented <areal velocity>, or the <angular momentum> per unit mass. Its constancy is <Kepler's second law>: equal areas are swept in equal times.

For a <circular orbit> of radius $a$, set $\ddot r=0$ in the radial equation. The balance is
$$
h^2=GMa,\qquad |\dot\theta|=\frac{|h|}{a^2}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a^3}}.
$$
Hence a <circular orbit> exists for any $a>0$ with this nonzero $|h|$. Its period gives <Kepler's third law>:
$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}},\qquad T^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GM}.}
$$
To check <circular-orbit stability for a power-law central force> directly, keep $h$ fixed and set $r=a+\rho$. Expanding
$$
\ddot r=\frac{h^2}{r^3}-\frac{GM}{r^2}
$$
about the balanced radius gives
$$
\ddot\rho=\left(-\frac{3h^2}{a^4}+\frac{2GM}{a^3}\right)\rho+O(\rho^2)
=-\frac{GM}{a^3}\rho+O(\rho^2).
$$
The linear radial perturbation therefore obeys
$$
\boxed{\ddot\rho+\Omega^2\rho=0,\qquad \Omega^2=GM/a^3>0.}
$$
It remains bounded and oscillates, proving stability under the stipulated small perturbations. Equivalently, the specific <effective potential> $h^2/(2r^2)-GM/r$ has a strict minimum at $a$.

= Solution
{parent=4B}

Write the standard <Lorentz transformation> as $x'=\gamma(x-vt)$, $ct'=\gamma(ct-vx/c)$, with <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ and $|v|<c$. In the <null coordinates in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime>, addition and subtraction give
$$
x'_+=\gamma(1-v/c)x_+,\qquad x'_-=\gamma(1+v/c)x_-.
$$
Since both factors are positive,
$$
\boxed{x'_+=\lambda(v)x_+,\quad x'_-=\lambda(-v)x_-,\qquad
\lambda(v)=\sqrt{\frac{c-v}{c+v}}.}
$$
Their product is one, so <Lorentz invariance> of the interval follows immediately:
$$
\boxed{x'^2-c^2t'^2=x'_+x'_-=x_+x_-=x^2-c^2t^2.}
$$
Two successive collinear <Lorentz boosts> multiply the positive-coordinate factor by $\lambda(v_2)\lambda(v_1)$ and the negative-coordinate factor by its reciprocal. Thus they have the same form with a velocity $v_3$ determined by
$$
\frac{c-v_3}{c+v_3}
=\frac{(c-v_1)(c-v_2)}{(c+v_1)(c+v_2)}.
$$
Cross multiplication yields the <relativistic velocity-addition formula> formula
$$
\boxed{v_3=\frac{v_1+v_2}{1+v_1v_2/c^2}.}
$$
For $|v_1|,|v_2|<c$, the denominator is positive and
$$
1-\frac{v_3^2}{c^2}
=\frac{(1-v_1^2/c^2)(1-v_2^2/c^2)}{(1+v_1v_2/c^2)^2}>0,
$$
so $v_3$ is admissible. The positive square roots then verify the factor identity itself, not merely its square. In terms of <rapidity> $v/c=\tanh\eta$, this same calculation is addition of rapidities because $\lambda(v)=e^{-\eta}$.

The <greatest common divisor> $d=\gcd(a,b)$ is the largest positive integer dividing both $a$ and $b$. To prove <Bezout identity> without assuming unique factorization, let $d_0$ be the least positive member of
$$
\{\lambda a+\mu b:\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$
This set has positive members, for example $a$, so <well-ordering principle> supplies $d_0$. Divide $a$ by $d_0$ using <Euclidean division>: $a=qd_0+r$, $0\leq r<d_0$. The remainder $r=a-qd_0$ is another integer linear combination of $a,b$. Minimality forces $r=0$, so $d_0\mid a$. The same argument gives $d_0\mid b$. Every common divisor divides every integer linear combination, hence divides $d_0$. Therefore $d_0$ is the <greatest common divisor>, and
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a,b)=\lambda a+\mu b\quad\text{for some }\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
This argument also proves that every common divisor divides the <greatest common divisor>.

The positive integers with the stated product-divisibility property are
$$
\boxed{n=1\quad\text{or}\quad n\text{ is a prime number}.}
$$
The case $n=1$ is immediate. If $p$ is <prime> and $p\nmid a$, its only possible common positive divisor with $a$ is one. By <Bezout identity>, $\lambda p+\mu a=1$. Multiplying by $b$ shows that $p\mid ab$ implies $p\mid b$. This proves <Euclid lemma> directly. Conversely, if $n>1$ is not <prime>, it has a divisor $r$ with $1<r<n$. Put $s=n/r$; then $1<s<n$. Although $n\mid rs$, neither $r$ nor $s$ is divisible by $n$, disproving the property for a composite $n$.

Finally, suppose $ab=cd$ for four distinct <primes>. Then $a\mid cd$, so <Euclid lemma> gives $a\mid c$ or $a\mid d$. A <prime> has no positive divisor other than one and itself, so this would mean $a=c$ or $a=d$, a contradiction. **Products of these two disjoint pairs cannot be equal.** No form of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic was used: the required prime-divisibility fact was proved from <Bezout identity>.

**Yes.** A concrete example of <nonunique factorization in a congruence submonoid> is
$$
\boxed{21\cdot221=51\cdot91=4641.}
$$
The four factors are distinct and each is congruent to one modulo ten. The primes $3,7,13,17$ can be checked by trial division by two and three. Their elementary prime decompositions are
$$
21=3\cdot7,\quad221=13\cdot17,\quad51=3\cdot17,\quad91=7\cdot13.
$$
For any one of these products $pq$, where $p,q$ are the indicated distinct <primes>, its positive divisors are $1,p,q,pq$. This divisor assertion does not require unique factorization: if $d\mid pq$ and $p\nmid d$, then $p\mid pq/d$ by the <Euclid lemma> proved in (a), so $d\mid q$ and $d$ is either one or $q$. If $p\mid d$, write $d=pe$ and obtain $e\mid q$, giving $d=p$ or $pq$.

None of $3,7,13,17$ is congruent to one modulo ten. Consequently no listed product has a proper nonunit divisor in $S$. Thus each is an <irreducible element of a commutative monoid>, and the displayed equality proves the claim. Ordinary prime decompositions and irreducibility relative to the smaller <commutative monoid> $S$ are different notions.

<Fermat's little theorem> states that for a <prime> $p$ and an integer $a$,
$$
a^p\equiv a\pmod p;
\qquad p\nmid a\ \Longrightarrow\ a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.
$$
<Wilson theorem> states that for a <prime> $p$,
$$
\boxed{(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p.}
$$
All the following <modular congruences> are taken modulo the indicated <prime>.

Suppose $x^2\equiv-1\pmod p$. Then $p\nmid x$, so <Fermat's little theorem> gives $x^{p-1}\equiv1$. But $p\equiv3\pmod4$ makes $(p-1)/2$ odd, and hence
$$
x^{p-1}=(x^2)^{(p-1)/2}\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}=-1\pmod p.
$$
Since this $p$ is odd, $1\not\equiv-1\pmod p$, a contradiction. **There is no square root of minus one in this case.**

= Solution
{parent=7E}

Define the <binomial coefficient> $\binom ni$ to be the number of $i$-element subsets of an $n$-element set. Counting ordered selections and dividing by the $i!$ orders of each subset gives
$$
\binom ni=\frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!},\qquad0\leq i\leq n.
$$
Every subset has exactly one size, while each of the $n$ elements can independently be included or omitted. Hence the <power set> has $2^n$ members and
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom ni=2^n.}
$$
To prove the <binomial theorem>, expand the product of $n$ factors $(1+x)$. A term $x^i$ results from choosing $x$ from precisely an $i$-element subset of the factors and choosing one elsewhere. Its coefficient is therefore $\binom ni$. This is a finite distributive expansion, valid for every real $x$, and gives
$$
(1+x)^n=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom ni x^i.
$$
Differentiate this <polynomial identity> and set $x=1$:
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{n}i\binom ni=n2^{n-1}.}
$$
A second derivative gives $\sum i(i-1)\binom ni=n(n-1)2^{n-2}$ for $n\geq2$. Since $i^2=i(i-1)+i$, it follows that
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{n}i^2\binom ni=n(n+1)2^{n-2}.}
$$
The formula also holds for $n=1$, when its value is one; that case can be checked directly without differentiating twice.

For the squared <binomial coefficients>, compare the coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1+x)^n(1+x)^n=(1+x)^{2n}$. On the left it is $\sum_i\binom ni\binom n{n-i}$, and the <binomial coefficient> symmetry gives $\binom n{n-i}=\binom ni$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom ni^2=\binom{2n}{n}.}
$$
Finally put $S=\sum_i i\binom ni^2$ and replace the index $i$ by $n-i$. The same symmetry gives $S=\sum_i(n-i)\binom ni^2$. Adding the two expressions and using the preceding identity yields
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{n}i\binom ni^2=\frac n2\binom{2n}{n}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=8E}

For any function $f:X\to\mathcal P(X)$, form the diagonal subset
$$
D=\{x\in X:x\notin f(x)\}.
$$
If $f$ were <surjective>, some $d\in X$ would satisfy $f(d)=D$. Then $d\in D$ would hold exactly when $d\notin D$, which is impossible. Thus **no set surjects onto its power set**, including the empty-set case. This is <Cantor's theorem> proved by the <Cantor diagonal argument>.

Use <real-number pairing by separated digits> for an explicit <injection> $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$. First map a real number to $(0,1)$ by $q(x)=\tfrac12+\pi^{-1}\arctan x$, and use its unique <binary expansion> that is not eventually all ones. If $q(x)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_n2^{-n}$ and $q(y)=\sum_{n\geq1}b_n2^{-n}$, define
$$
\boxed{J(x,y)=\sum_{n\geq1}\left(\frac{a_n}{3^{2n-1}}+\frac{b_n}{3^{2n}}\right).}
$$
This places the two binary digit strings in alternating ternary positions. Such ternary strings, using only digits zero and one, are unique: at a first differing position $k$, the difference $3^{-k}$ exceeds the maximum tail $\sum_{j>k}3^{-j}=\tfrac12 3^{-k}$. Thus the output recovers both digit strings and both inputs. The use of base three avoids any endpoint ambiguity caused by interleaving digits in base two.

For a fixed $A\subseteq\mathbb R^2$, the <affine sections of a planar set> are indexed by
$$
\mathcal I=\{(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\in\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb R^2:\mathbf b\ne0\}.
$$
There is an <injection> $\iota:\mathcal I\to\mathbb R$, for example by applying $J$ to each coordinate pair and then once more to those two results. Suppose every subset of $\mathbb R$ were a section of $A$. Define a function from $\mathbb R$ to $\mathcal P(\mathbb R)$ by assigning to $\iota(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)$ the corresponding section, and assigning the empty set to every real outside $\iota(\mathcal I)$. <Injectivity> of $\iota$ makes this definition unambiguous. It would be a <surjection> onto $\mathcal P(\mathbb R)$, contradicting the first argument. **No planar set has every subset of the real line as a section.** Allowing different parametrizations of the same line does not evade this obstruction.

For all <countable sets>, however, **such a planar set does exist**. To build one, first encode every real sequence $s=(s_1,s_2,\ldots)$ by one real number. Let $a_{nk}$ be the canonical binary digits of $q(s_n)$, and choose the explicit indexing
$$
j(n,k)=\frac{(n+k-2)(n+k-1)}2+n\quad(n,k\geq1).
$$
This is a <bijection> from positive integer pairs to positive integers. Define the <coding of real sequences by ternary digits>
$$
c(s)=\sum_{n,k\geq1}\frac{a_{nk}}{3^{j(n,k)}}.
$$
The same first-differing-digit argument proves that $c$ is <injective>; all its values lie in $[0,1/2]$. Now take
$$
\boxed{A=\{(c(s),s_n):s\in\mathbb R^{\mathbb N},\ n\geq1\}.}
$$
For every nonempty <countable set> $S\subseteq\mathbb R$, choose an enumeration $s$ of $S$, repeating terms if $S$ is finite. On the vertical line with $\mathbf a=(c(s),0)$ and $\mathbf b=(0,1)$, the section of $A$ is precisely $S$: injective sequence coding means no other sequence can contribute extra values in this column. The empty set is the section on the line $x=2$, since all code columns have first coordinate at most $1/2$. This constructs the required <universal planar set for countable real sections>. No assertion about its other, nonvertical sections is needed.

Take the scalar product of <Newton's second law> with the <velocity> $\dot{\mathbf r}$. The <cross product> is perpendicular to its first factor, so
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(\frac12m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2\right)
=\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf F
=-\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\nabla\phi
+\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf B)
=-\frac d{dt}\phi(\mathbf r).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2+\phi(\mathbf r)\quad\text{is constant}.}
$$
This is <conservation of energy>: the sum of <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> stays fixed. The magnetic-type <Lorentz force> $\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf B$ does no <work>, because it is perpendicular to the instantaneous <velocity>. It may turn the trajectory but cannot directly change its speed, regardless of the spatial variation of $\mathbf B$.

For $\psi=\mu/r$, the radial <gradient> gives
$$
\mathbf B=-\nabla\psi=\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf r.
$$
The force $-\nabla\phi$ is radial and has no <torque> about the origin. For the remaining force, use the <vector triple product>:
$$
\frac d{dt}(m\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r})
=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F
=\frac\mu{r^3}\mathbf r\times(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf r)
=\frac\mu r(\dot{\mathbf r}-\dot r\,\widehat{\mathbf r}).
$$
Meanwhile the radial unit vector obeys
$$
\frac d{dt}\widehat{\mathbf r}
=\frac{\dot{\mathbf r}-\dot r\,\widehat{\mathbf r}}r.
$$
Since $\psi\mathbf r=\mu\widehat{\mathbf r}$, these derivatives cancel. Thus the <generalized angular momentum in a radial monopole field> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf L=m\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}-\mu\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad\dot{\mathbf L}=0.}
$$
The argument applies away from $r=0$, where the field is defined, for any radial $\phi$. In contrast to ordinary <angular momentum>, this conserved vector has $\mathbf L\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}=-\mu$; it fixes a cone rather than generally fixing a plane.

With $\psi=0$, the <angular momentum> becomes $\mathbf L=m\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}$ and is constant. The identity $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf r=0$ confines the trajectory to the plane through the origin perpendicular to $\mathbf L$ when $\mathbf L\ne0$. If $\mathbf L=0$, the <velocity> is radial and a <central force> preserves the radial line; this degenerate motion is also planar.

For the proposed <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> convention, differentiate $\mathbf K=\mathbf L\times\dot{\mathbf r}-\phi(r)\mathbf r$. Since $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\phi'(r)\widehat{\mathbf r}/m$ and $\mathbf L$ is constant, the <vector triple product> gives
$$
\mathbf L\times\ddot{\mathbf r}
=-\phi'(r)(r\dot{\mathbf r}-\dot r\,\mathbf r).
$$
Therefore
$$
\dot{\mathbf K}
=-\phi'(r)(r\dot{\mathbf r}-\dot r\,\mathbf r)
-\phi'(r)\dot r\,\mathbf r-\phi(r)\dot{\mathbf r}
=-[r\phi'(r)+\phi(r)]\dot{\mathbf r}.
$$
It vanishes for general motion precisely when $(r\phi)'=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\phi(r)=\frac Cr\quad\text{for a constant }C,\qquad\dot{\mathbf K}=0.}
$$
This is an <inverse-square potential>, attractive when $C<0$ and repulsive when $C>0$; $C=0$ is the free-particle case. An additive constant in $\phi$ leaves the force unchanged but would change this particular $\mathbf K$ by a constant multiple of $\mathbf r$, so its definition requires the displayed choice of energy zero.

Apply the <rotating-frame derivative formula> first to position and then to its inertial <velocity>:
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\right)_I=\dot{\mathbf r}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r,
$$
$$
\left(\frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2}\right)_I
=\ddot{\mathbf r}+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}
+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
The angular velocity is constant, so there is no term involving $\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$. By <Newton's second law> in the <inertial frame>, the inertial acceleration is $\mathbf F/m$. Rearrangement gives the <equation of motion in a rotating frame>:
$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf F-2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}
-m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r).}
$$
The two additional accelerations are the <Coriolis acceleration> and <centrifugal acceleration>.

= Solution

At $P$, use the local right-handed basis $\mathbf e_E,\mathbf e_N,\mathbf e_U$ pointing east, north and vertically outward. The Earth's rotation vector has components
$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\omega(\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_N+\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_U),\qquad
\mathbf r=R\mathbf e_U.
$$
Initially $\dot{\mathbf r}=0$ in the <rotating reference frame>, so the <Coriolis acceleration> is zero. Evaluating the <centrifugal acceleration> gives
$$
-\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)
=\omega^2R(\sin^2\theta\,\mathbf e_U-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_N).
$$
The <gravitational acceleration> is $-g\mathbf e_U$. Hence
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}(0)
=-(g-\omega^2R\sin^2\theta)\mathbf e_U
-\omega^2R\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_N.
$$
The small angle $\delta$ from the downward vertical therefore satisfies
$$
\tan\delta=\frac{\omega^2R|\sin\theta\cos\theta|}{g-\omega^2R\sin^2\theta},\qquad
\boxed{\delta\simeq\frac{\omega^2R}{g}|\sin\theta\cos\theta|.}
$$
For the northern hemisphere, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$, this is the printed expression without absolute values. More generally, the printed signed meridional expression specifies the direction of the tilt; an unsigned angle uses its absolute value. The horizontal component is toward the equator and vanishes at both poles and at the equator.

= b
{parent=10b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Neglect the <centrifugal acceleration>, which is of order $\omega^2$, and use the local flat-surface approximation. To zeroth order in $\omega$, the height above $P$ and flight time are
$$
z_0(t)=vt-\frac12gt^2,\qquad \dot z_0=v-gt,\qquad T_0=\frac{2v}{g}.
$$
Inserting this vertical <velocity> into the <Coriolis acceleration> gives the eastward component
$$
\ddot x=-2\omega\sin\theta\,(v-gt)+O(\omega^2).
$$
With $x(0)=\dot x(0)=0$, two integrations yield
$$
\dot x=-2\omega\sin\theta\left(vt-\frac12gt^2\right),\qquad
x=-\omega\sin\theta\left(vt^2-\frac13gt^3\right)
$$
to first order. Horizontal <velocity> is already of order $\omega$, so its effect on vertical acceleration is of order $\omega^2$. Thus the flight time remains $T_0$ at this order. The <Coriolis deflection of a vertical projectile> at landing is
$$
\boxed{x(T_0)=-\frac{4\omega v^3}{3g^2}\sin\theta.}
$$
The displacement is **westward**, and its magnitude is $4\omega v^3\sin\theta/(3g^2)$. The horizontal speed remains westward during the flight: $\dot x=-2\omega\sin\theta\,z_0(t)$. This explains why the eastward acceleration on descent does not cancel the earlier displacement.

{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics and Relativity}

= Solution
{parent=11B}

Let $dm$ be the gas mass ejected in a short interval, and let $dD$ be the stationary dust mass acquired. The rocket mass change is $dM=dD-dm$. Before the interval the <momentum> of the rocket and this dust is $Mv$, because the incoming dust is at rest. Afterwards it is
$$
(M+dM)(v+dv)+(v-u)dm
$$
to first order; changing the gas ejection speed by $dv$ affects only second-order terms. <Momentum conservation>, with no external force, therefore gives
$$
M\,dv+v\,dM+(v-u)\,dm=0.
$$
Dividing by the time interval produces the <rocket equation with stationary mass accretion>:
$$
\boxed{M\dot v+v\dot M+(v-u)\dot m=0.}
$$
The incoming material's zero initial <momentum> is essential: accelerating it to the rocket's speed consumes part of the thrust.

= a
{parent=11b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Without dust collection, $dM=-dm$. The <rocket equation> reduces to
$$
M\,dv=u\,dm=-u\,dM.
$$
Integrating from $v=0$, $M=M_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{v=u\log\frac{M_0}{M}.}
$$
This is the usual <rocket equation> with constant relative exhaust speed, valid while the mass remains positive and the prescribed ejection continues.

= b
{parent=11b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Now $\dot m=\beta$ and $\dot M=\alpha-\beta$, so
$$
M(t)=M_0+(\alpha-\beta)t,\qquad
M\dot v+\alpha v=u\beta.
$$
For $\alpha>0$, set $w=u\beta-\alpha v$. Then $\dot w=-\alpha w/M$ and $w(0)=u\beta$. For $\alpha\ne\beta$, integration gives
$$
w=u\beta\exp\left(-\alpha\int_0^t\frac{ds}{M(s)}\right)
=u\beta\left(\frac{M}{M_0}\right)^{\alpha/(\beta-\alpha)}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{v=\frac{u\beta}{\alpha}
\left[1-\left(\frac{M}{M_0}\right)^{\alpha/(\beta-\alpha)}\right].}
$$
This solution of the <rocket equation with stationary mass accretion> covers increasing or decreasing rocket mass, as long as $M>0$. For <equal-rate rocket burning and accretion>, in the case $\alpha=\beta>0$, the mass is constant and the linear equation instead gives
$$
\boxed{v(t)=u\left(1-e^{-\alpha t/M_0}\right).}
$$
It is also the continuous equal-rate limit of the preceding expression when written in terms of $t$.

For the no-accretion limit, expand the power as an exponential:
$$
\left(\frac{M}{M_0}\right)^{\alpha/(\beta-\alpha)}
=1+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\log\frac{M}{M_0}+O(\alpha^2).
$$
Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\alpha\to0}v=u\log\frac{M_0}{M},}
$$
recovering (a). At a fixed time with $M_0-\beta t>0$, the limiting mass is $M=M_0-\beta t$, so the same calculation recovers the time-dependent no-dust solution.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}

The relativistic energy of a particle of <rest mass> $m$ is $E=\gamma mc^2$, where the <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. Using the <binomial series> at small $v/c$ gives
$$
\boxed{E=mc^2+\frac12mv^2+O\left(\frac{mv^4}{c^2}\right).}
$$
The second term is the familiar Newtonian <kinetic energy>. The first is the <rest energy>, which remains when $v=0$. Thus the new physical idea is **mass-energy equivalence: a body's mass represents energy $mc^2$ even at rest**. The total energy is not merely energy due to motion.

= b
{parent=12b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The incident <four-momentum> has energy $E$ and spatial momentum $p=\gamma mv$, while the stationary target has energy $mc^2$ and zero spatial momentum. By the <relativistic energy-momentum relation>, $c^2p^2=E^2-m^2c^4$. For the <fixed-target threshold for production of equal-mass particles>, the squared total <centre-of-momentum energy> is
$$
W^2=(E+mc^2)^2-c^2p^2=2mc^2(E+mc^2).
$$
In the <centre-of-momentum frame>, each of the final $N$ particles has energy at least its <rest energy> $mc^2$. <Four-momentum conservation> therefore requires $W\geq Nmc^2$, and thus
$$
\boxed{E\geq\frac{N^2-2}{2}mc^2.}
$$
For the physically relevant $N\geq2$, this is equivalent to $\gamma\geq(N^2-2)/2$. The speed is an increasing function of $\gamma$, so
$$
\frac{v}{c}=\sqrt{1-\gamma^{-2}}
\geq\sqrt{1-\frac4{(N^2-2)^2}}
=\frac{N\sqrt{N^2-4}}{N^2-2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{v\geq\frac{N\sqrt{N^2-4}}{N^2-2}\,c.}
$$
Equality is the kinematic threshold at which all final particles are at rest in their common <centre-of-momentum frame>. It shows why a collision with a stationary target needs more incident energy than merely the additional rest energy: some laboratory energy must remain as collective motion. A one-particle final state of the specified mass, with no other outgoing energy, is impossible because its invariant energy would be $mc^2$, whereas the two-particle initial state has invariant energy at least $2mc^2$.
