= Solution

A <Noetherian ring> is a ring whose <ideals> satisfy the <ascending chain condition>; equivalently every <ideal> is finitely generated. For the surjective <ring endomorphism>, put $K_j=\ker f^j$. The ascending chain of <ideals> $K_1\subseteq K_2\subseteq\cdots$ stabilizes, say $K_m=K_{m+1}$. If $a\in\ker f$, surjectivity of $f^m$ gives $b$ with $f^m(b)=a$. Then $f^{m+1}(b)=0$, so $b\in K_{m+1}=K_m$ and $a=0$. **The <endomorphism> is therefore injective**, hence a <ring automorphism>.

The <nilradical> is the <ideal>
$$
N(R)=\sqrt{(0)}=\{a\in R:a^j=0\text{ for some }j\geq1\}.
$$
For example, if $a^u=b^v=0$, the binomial expansion gives $(a+b)^{u+v-1}=0$; multiplication by any ring element also preserves nilpotence. Every <prime ideal> contains each <nilpotent element>, since $a^j\in P$ implies $a\in P$. Conversely, if $a$ is not nilpotent, the <multiplicative subset> $\{1,a,a^2,\ldots\}$ avoids $(0)$. By <Zorn's lemma>, choose an <ideal> maximal among those disjoint from this subset. It is a <prime ideal>: if $xy\in J$ but neither factor is in $J$, then $J+(x)$ and $J+(y)$ both meet the subset, and multiplying their two witnesses puts a power of $a$ in $J$, a contradiction. This <prime ideal> excludes $a$. **Hence**
$$
\boxed{N(R)=\bigcap_{P\in\operatorname{Spec}R}P.}
$$
For the zero ring the empty intersection is the whole ring, as required.

Here is a <finite prime-intersection representation of a radical> argument that does not assume the result. In a <Noetherian ring>, suppose some <ideal> $J$ has <ideal radical> not expressible as a finite intersection of <prime ideals>, and choose such a $J$ maximal by the <ascending chain condition>. It is proper and cannot be prime. Choose $a,b\notin J$ with $ab\in J$. The two larger <ideals> $J+(a)$ and $J+(b)$ have finite prime-intersection radicals, and
$$
\sqrt J=\sqrt{J+(a)}\cap\sqrt{J+(b)}.
$$
Indeed, if $x^u\in J+(a)$ and $x^v\in J+(b)$, then $x^{u+v}\in J+(ab)=J$. This contradicts the choice of $J$. Applying the result to $(0)$ proves **that $N(R)$ is the intersection of finitely many <prime ideals>**. Empty intersections handle the unit <ideal>.

For <reducedness of a formal power series ring>, the inclusion of constants gives one implication: a nonzero <nilpotent element> of $R$ remains nonzero and nilpotent in $R[[X]]$. For the other, let $R$ be a <reduced ring> and take a nonzero <formal power series> $F=\sum_{j\geq r}a_jX^j$ with $a_r\ne0$. The coefficient of $X^{mr}$ in $F^m$ is $a_r^m\ne0$, because $R$ is reduced. Thus $F$ is not nilpotent. **Therefore**
$$
\boxed{N(R)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N(R[[X]])=0.}
$$
No <Noetherian ring> hypothesis is needed for this argument.

For a <nilradical that is not nilpotent>, take
$$
\boxed{R=k[t_1,t_2,\ldots]/(t_1^2,t_2^2,\ldots),\qquad N(R)=(\bar t_1,\bar t_2,\ldots).}
$$
Every element of this <ideal> uses finitely many variables and is nilpotent: an element using $s$ variables lies in an <ideal> whose $(s+1)$st power is zero. The quotient by this <ideal> is the <field> $k$, so it is exactly the <nilradical>. Nevertheless $\bar t_1\cdots\bar t_m\ne0$ for every $m$, because the square-free <monomials> form a <vector space basis>. Thus $N(R)^m\ne0$ for all $m$.

A <prime ideal> $P$ is a proper <ideal> such that $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. Equivalently $R/P$ is an <integral domain>.

For each $i$, and each $j\ne i$, choose $a_{ji}\in P_j\setminus P_i$, using incomparability. Put
$$
b_i=\prod_{j\ne i}a_{ji}.
$$
Then $b_i$ belongs to the other two <prime ideals> and does not belong to $P_i$, by primality. In particular each $b_i$ lies in the union. But $b_1+b_2+b_3\notin P_i$ for every $i$: modulo $P_i$, the sum equals $b_i\ne0$. **The union is not closed under addition and is not an <ideal>.** This is a concrete instance of <prime avoidance>.

The <union of three incomparable nonprime ideals> example can retain pairwise incomparability. In the <ring>
$$
R=\mathbb F_2[u,v]/(u^2,uv,v^2),
$$
take
$$
\boxed{I_1=(u),\qquad I_2=(v),\qquad I_3=(u+v).}
$$
These distinct two-element <ideals> are pairwise incomparable, yet
$$
I_1\cup I_2\cup I_3=\{0,u,v,u+v\}=(u,v),
$$
which is an <ideal>. None is prime: its <quotient ring> still contains a nonzero element of square zero. The three one-dimensional <vector subspaces> cover the two-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb F_2$.

= Solution

Use the convention that a <multiplicative subset> contains $1$ and is closed under multiplication. Usually $0$ is excluded; allowing it gives the harmless zero-ring localization. A <prime ideal> disjoint from $S$ is mapped to
$$
S^{-1}P=\{a/s:a\in P,\ s\in S\}
$$
in the <localization of a ring>. This <ideal> is proper, and
$$
(S^{-1}R)/(S^{-1}P)\cong S^{-1}(R/P)
$$
is an <integral domain>, since the denominators are nonzero in the <integral domain> $R/P$. Thus $S^{-1}P$ is a <prime ideal>.

Conversely, for a <prime ideal> $Q$ in $S^{-1}R$, its contraction
$$
P=\{a\in R:a/1\in Q\}
$$
is a <prime ideal> disjoint from $S$: each $s/1$ is a unit and cannot belong to a proper <ideal>. Membership in $Q$ is equivalent to numerator membership in $P$, since $a/s=(a/1)(s/1)^{-1}$. Hence $Q=S^{-1}P$.

To check the other composite, $a/1\in S^{-1}P$ implies $sa\in P$ for some $s\in S$, by the equality criterion for fractions in a <localization of a ring>. Since $s\notin P$, primality gives $a\in P$. Therefore **extension and contraction are mutually inverse:**
$$
\boxed{\{P\in\operatorname{Spec}R:P\cap S=\varnothing\}
\ \longleftrightarrow\ \operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R),\qquad P\mapsto S^{-1}P.}
$$
If $0\in S$, both sets are empty. This is the <prime ideal correspondence for localization>.

Suppose $S$ is a <saturated multiplicative subset>, and take $a\notin S$. The principal <ideal> $(a)$ is disjoint from $S$: otherwise $ra\in S$ would force $a\in S$. The <prime ideal avoiding a multiplicative subset> argument from Question 1 extends $(a)$ to a <prime ideal> $P_a$ disjoint from $S$. Thus every point of the complement belongs to such a <prime ideal>, and
$$
\boxed{R\setminus S=\bigcup_{P\cap S=\varnothing}P.}
$$
If $S=R$, this is the empty union.

Conversely suppose $R\setminus S$ is a union of <prime ideals>. If $xy\in S$ and $x\notin S$, some <prime ideal> in that union contains $x$, and hence $xy$, a contradiction. The same holds for $y$. Thus **The <saturated complement as a union of prime ideals> characterization holds**.

For <units with no finite prime-union complement>, take
$$
\boxed{R=\mathbb Z,\qquad S=\mathbb Z^\times=\{-1,1\}.}
$$
Products of units are units, and a product is a unit only if both factors are units, so $S$ is a <saturated multiplicative subset>. Every nonunit integer lies in some $(p)$ with $p$ a positive prime, giving $\mathbb Z\setminus S=\bigcup_p(p)$. If finitely many <prime ideals> sufficed, discard any $(0)$ and write the others as $(p_1),\ldots,(p_r)$. A prime $\ell$ outside this finite list belongs to none of them but is a nonunit, a contradiction. There is always such an $\ell$: a prime divisor of $p_1\cdots p_r+1$ is outside the list.

An <integral extension> $R\subseteq T$ means every $t\in T$ satisfies a <monic polynomial>
$$
t^d+a_{d-1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0=0,\qquad a_j\in R.
$$
To prove the <lying-over theorem>, localize at $S=R\setminus P$. The injection $R_P\hookrightarrow S^{-1}T$ remains injective, and $S^{-1}T$ is integral over $R_P$: a monic relation for $t$ divided by suitable powers of $s$ gives one for $t/s$. This is a nonzero ring, so choose a <maximal ideal> $M$ of it.

The contraction of $M$ to $R_P$ is maximal. To see this directly, a <field> integral over a subring forces that subring to be a <field>. For nonzero $a$ in the subring, $a^{-1}$ is integral; multiply its monic equation of degree $d$ by $a^{d-1}$ to express $a^{-1}$ as a polynomial in $a$ with coefficients in the subring. Apply this to the <integral extension>
$$
R_P/(M\cap R_P)\ \subseteq\ (S^{-1}T)/M.
$$
Since $R_P$ is a <local ring> with unique <maximal ideal> $PR_P$, we obtain $M\cap R_P=PR_P$.

Contract $M$ to $T$, obtaining a <prime ideal> $Q$. Its contraction to $R$ is the contraction of $PR_P$, namely $P$. **Thus**
$$
\boxed{\text{there exists }Q\in\operatorname{Spec}T\text{ with }Q\cap R=P.}
$$

= Solution

The key is a <Laurent normalization by a monomial change of variables>. We prove the slightly stronger assertion that every nonzero quotient of a <Laurent polynomial ring> in $r$ variables is finite as a <module> over an embedded <Laurent polynomial ring> in some number $m\leq r$ of variables.

Induct on $r$. If the defining <ideal> is zero, the ring itself works. Otherwise choose a nonzero Laurent polynomial
$$
f=\sum_{\mathbf a\in F}c_{\mathbf a}X^{\mathbf a}
$$
in that <ideal>, with nonzero coefficients. For a sufficiently large positive integer $N$, the weights
$$
N a_1+N^2a_2+\cdots+N^{r-1}a_{r-1}+a_r
$$
are distinct for all $\mathbf a\in F$. Indeed, each difference gives a nonzero polynomial in $N$, which has only finitely many integer roots. Make the invertible <monomial> substitution
$$
X_j=Y_jY_r^{N^j}\ (j<r),\qquad X_r=Y_r.
$$
Its inverse is $Y_j=X_jX_r^{-N^j}$, so it is an <automorphism> of the <Laurent polynomial ring>. When $r=1$ there is no substitution and distinct exponents already give distinct weights.

As a Laurent polynomial in $Y_r$, the transformed relation has unique lowest and highest exponents. Their coefficients are nonzero <scalar> multiples of <monomials> in $Y_1,\ldots,Y_{r-1}$, hence units in
$$
B_0=k[Y_1^{\pm1},\ldots,Y_{r-1}^{\pm1}].
$$
Multiplying by a power of $Y_r$ and the inverse leading coefficient gives
$$
Y_r^d+b_{d-1}Y_r^{d-1}+\cdots+b_0=0,\qquad b_j\in B_0,\quad b_0\in B_0^\times.
$$
Here $d>0$: a single-term Laurent polynomial would be a unit and the quotient would be zero. Reversing the equation and dividing by $b_0Y_r^d$ gives a monic equation for $Y_r^{-1}$. Thus both $Y_r$ and $Y_r^{-1}$ are integral over the image $B$ of $B_0$ in $T$. In fact $b_0$ being a unit also expresses $Y_r^{-1}$ as a polynomial in $Y_r$, so $T$ is generated as a $B$-<module> by $1,Y_r,\ldots,Y_r^{d-1}$. 

The ring $B$ is itself a nonzero quotient of an $(r-1)$-variable <Laurent polynomial ring>. By induction, it is finite over an embedded <Laurent polynomial ring> in $m\leq r-1$ variables. Finiteness of <modules> is transitive, so the same is true of $T$; a finite ring extension is an <integral extension>, by the <determinant trick> applied to multiplication by an element.

Applying this to $r=3$ yields **an embedded subring**
$$
\boxed{k[Y_1^{\pm1},\ldots,Y_m^{\pm1}]\subseteq T,\qquad T\text{ integral over it}.}
$$
The base case $m=0$ means the subring $k$; finiteness over it would make $T$ a finite-dimensional <vector space>. The hypothesis excludes this, so **$1\leq m\leq3$**, as required. No infinitude assumption on $k$ is used.

The <height of a prime ideal> is the supremum of the lengths of strict chains of <prime ideals> ending at $P$:
$$
\operatorname{ht}P=\sup\{d:P_0\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq P_d=P\}
=\dim R_P.
$$
The last equality follows from the <prime ideal correspondence for localization>. Here is a length-growth proof of the <Krull height theorem> that makes the bound explicit.

We first establish a <prime-chain lower bound for local length>. For a <Noetherian local ring> $(B,\mathfrak m)$ admitting a <prime chain> of length $d$, put $H_B(t)=\operatorname{length}(B/\mathfrak m^t)$. Then
$$
H_B(t)\geq c\,t^d\qquad(t\gg0)
$$
for some $c>0$. For $d=0$, use $H_B(t)\geq1$. For $d>0$, quotient by the first <prime ideal> of the chain; this only decreases $H_B$ and reduces us to a <local integral domain> $B$ with
$$
0\subsetneq P_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq P_d=\mathfrak m.
$$
Take $0\ne a\in P_1$ and put $C=B/(a)$. It has a <prime chain> of length $d-1$, so induction bounds $H_C(t)$ below by a positive multiple of $t^{d-1}$.

The <Artin-Rees lemma> applied to $(a)\subseteq B$ gives, for a fixed integer $c_0\geq1$,
$$
(a)\cap\mathfrak m^t\subseteq a\mathfrak m^{t-c_0}\qquad(t\geq c_0).
$$
Because $a$ is a <non-zero-divisor>, the first term in the <exact sequence>
$$
0\longrightarrow (a)/((a)\cap\mathfrak m^t)
\longrightarrow B/\mathfrak m^t
\longrightarrow B/((a)+\mathfrak m^t)\longrightarrow0
$$
has length at least $H_B(t-c_0)$. Hence
$$
H_B(t)\geq H_B(t-c_0)+H_C(t).
$$
Iterate this inequality about $t/(2c_0)$ times, while its arguments remain at least $t/2$. This gives the desired positive multiple of $t^d$. The particular <Artin-Rees lemma> inclusion used here follows directly from finite generation: the <Rees algebra> $\bigoplus_{j\geq0}\mathfrak m^jt^j$ is <Noetherian>, by the <Hilbert basis theorem>, and its graded submodule $\bigoplus_j((a)\cap\mathfrak m^j)t^j$ has finitely many homogeneous generators. A bound on their degrees gives $c_0$.

Now localize at the given minimal <prime ideal> $P$. Write $B=R_P$, $\mathfrak m=PR_P$, and $J=IR_P$. The only <prime ideal> of $B$ containing $J$ is $\mathfrak m$, so $\sqrt J=\mathfrak m$. Since $\mathfrak m$ is finitely generated, some $\mathfrak m^q\subseteq J$. Thus $B/J$ has finite <module length>, say $L$. The <ideal> $J$ is generated by at most $n$ elements.

For $n>0$, products of these generators give a surjection from $\binom{j+n-1}{n-1}$ copies of $B/J$ onto $J^j/J^{j+1}$. Summing the lengths gives the <generator bound for primary-ideal length>
$$
\operatorname{length}(B/J^t)
\leq L\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\binom{j+n-1}{n-1}
=L\binom{t+n-1}{n}=O(t^n).
$$
Since $J\subseteq\mathfrak m$, we have $J^t\subseteq\mathfrak m^t$, and therefore
$$
H_B(t)\leq\operatorname{length}(B/J^t)=O(t^n).
$$
The <prime-chain lower bound for local length> now excludes any chain of length greater than $n$. **Consequently**
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ht}P=\dim R_P\leq n.}
$$
For $n=0$, minimality over the zero <ideal> says that $P$ is a minimal <prime ideal>, so its height is zero. This also handles that boundary case.

A <local ring> is a nonzero ring with one <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. Write $\kappa=R/\mathfrak m$ for its <residue field>. Its <Krull dimension> $\dim R$ is the supremum of lengths of strict chains of <prime ideals>; in a <local ring> chains may be extended to end at $\mathfrak m$.

For the local Hilbert-function convention, define the <Hilbert–Samuel growth dimension>
$$
\boxed{d(R)=\deg\bigl(\text{the eventual polynomial }
H_R(t)=\operatorname{length}(R/\mathfrak m^t)\bigr).}
$$
Equivalently it is the order of the pole at $z=1$ of the <Hilbert series> of the <associated graded ring>
$$
\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}R=\bigoplus_{j\geq0}\mathfrak m^j/\mathfrak m^{j+1}.
$$
This finite standard graded $\kappa$-algebra is generated by $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$. The <Hilbert-Serre theorem> makes the cumulative Hilbert function eventually polynomial, which establishes the definition. For an <Artinian local ring>, the polynomial is constant and nonzero, so $d(R)=0$.

For every <prime chain> of length $s$, the <prime-chain lower bound for local length> proved in Question 4 gives $H_R(t)\geq c t^s$. A polynomial of degree $d(R)$ cannot satisfy this when $s>d(R)$. Thus **the requested inequality is**
$$
\boxed{\dim R\leq d(R).}
$$
For clarity, the <embedding dimension> is the different invariant
$$
e(R)=\dim_\kappa(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)
=\text{the minimal number of generators of }\mathfrak m.
$$
The equality follows from the <Nakayama lemma>. Applying Question 4 to $I=\mathfrak m$ gives $\dim R\leq e(R)$, while the polynomial-algebra surjection below also gives $d(R)\leq e(R)$. In particular, growth dimension and embedding dimension should be kept distinct.

A <regular local ring> is a <Noetherian local ring> with $e(R)=\dim R$. Let this common value be $e$ and choose a minimal generating set $x_1,\ldots,x_e$ of $\mathfrak m$. The initial forms give a surjective graded <ring homomorphism>
$$
\kappa[T_1,\ldots,T_e]\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}R.
$$
For $e=0$, the <Nakayama lemma> gives $\mathfrak m=0$, so $R$ is a <field>. Assume $e>0$. If the kernel contained a nonzero <homogeneous polynomial> $F$ of degree $q>0$, the cumulative Hilbert function of the target would be bounded by that of $\kappa[T_1,\ldots,T_e]/(F)$. Multiplication by $F$ is injective in the polynomial <integral domain>, so this bound is
$$
\binom{t+e-1}{e}-\binom{t-q+e-1}{e}=O(t^{e-1})\qquad(t>q).
$$
But $\dim R=e$ provides a <prime chain> of length $e$, and Question 4 gives $H_R(t)\geq ct^e$. This is a contradiction. Hence the <associated graded ring of a regular local ring> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}R\cong\kappa[T_1,\ldots,T_e],}
$$
an <integral domain>.

Finally the <Krull intersection theorem> gives $\bigcap_j\mathfrak m^j=0$. One can see the needed separatedness directly: for the finitely generated <ideal> $K=\bigcap_j\mathfrak m^j$, the <Artin-Rees lemma> gives $K=\mathfrak mK$, and the <Nakayama lemma> gives $K=0$. Therefore each nonzero $a\in R$ has finite $\mathfrak m$-adic order $r$, with nonzero initial form in $\mathfrak m^r/\mathfrak m^{r+1}$. For nonzero $a,b$, their initial forms have nonzero product in the graded <integral domain>. It follows that $ab\ne0$, and indeed its order is the sum of their orders. **Thus $R$ is an <integral domain>.**

For $a\in R$, write $m_a$ for multiplication by $a$ in $\operatorname{End}_k(R)$. The <algebraic differential operator ring> is defined by the filtration
$$
D_k^{-1}(R)=0,\qquad
D_k^j(R)=\{\theta\in\operatorname{End}_k(R):
[\theta,m_a]\in D_k^{j-1}(R)\text{ for every }a\in R\},\qquad
D_k(R)=\bigcup_{j\geq0}D_k^j(R).
$$
The <order of an algebraic differential operator> is the least $j$ for which $\theta\in D_k^j(R)$; the zero operator may be assigned order $-\infty$. In degree zero, commuting with all multiplication maps means $\theta(a)=a\theta(1)$, so $D_k^0(R)=\{m_a:a\in R\}\cong R$. The <commutator> identity $[UV,m_a]=U[V,m_a]+[U,m_a]V$ proves by induction that $D_k^rD_k^s\subseteq D_k^{r+s}$, so the union is a ring under addition and composition.

The <derivations of an algebra> form
$$
\operatorname{Der}_k(R)=\{\delta:R\to R\text{ linear over }k:
\delta(ab)=a\delta(b)+b\delta(a)\}.
$$
Every such <derivation> has $\delta(1)=0$ and $[\delta,m_a]=m_{\delta(a)}$, so has differential order at most one. Conversely, if $\theta\in D_k^1(R)$, set $c=\theta(1)$ and $\delta=\theta-m_c$. Then $\delta(1)=0$ and $[\delta,m_a]$ is a multiplication map. Evaluating at $1$ shows it is $m_{\delta(a)}$; evaluating at $b$ gives the <Leibniz rule>. **Hence the <first-order algebraic differential operator decomposition> is**
$$
\boxed{D_k^1(R)=R+\operatorname{Der}_k(R).}
$$
It is a direct sum as $k$-<vector spaces>: a multiplication map that is a <derivation> vanishes at $1$, and is zero.

The complex <Weyl algebra> has generators $x_1,\ldots,x_n,\partial_1,\ldots,\partial_n$ and relations
$$
\boxed{A_n=\mathbb C\langle x_i,\partial_i\rangle/
([x_i,x_j],\,[\partial_i,\partial_j],\,[\partial_i,x_j]-\delta_{ij}).}
$$
Take $n\geq1$. If a nonzero unital <module> $M$ had finite <dimension of a vector space> over $\mathbb C$ $m$, its representing matrices would satisfy $[\partial_1,x_1]=I_m$. Taking the <matrix trace> gives $0=m$, impossible in characteristic zero. **The <Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules>.** For $n=0$ the algebra is $\mathbb C$ and this assertion has the obvious exception.

For a nonzero finitely generated <Weyl algebra> <module>, use the <Bernstein filtration>
$$
F_jA_n=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{x^{\mathbf a}\partial^{\mathbf b}:|\mathbf a|+|\mathbf b|\leq j\}.
$$
The ordered <monomials> form a <vector space basis>, so
$$
\dim_{\mathbb C}F_jA_n=\binom{j+2n}{2n},\qquad
\operatorname{gr}_F A_n\cong\mathbb C[X_1,\ldots,X_n,\Xi_1,\ldots,\Xi_n].
$$
To justify the ordered basis, the relations move all $\partial_i$ to the right and give spanning. Independence follows in the action on $\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$: apply a putative zero operator $\sum_{\mathbf b}p_{\mathbf b}(x)\partial^{\mathbf b}$ coefficientwise to the formal exponential $e^{t\cdot x}$, obtaining $\sum_{\mathbf b}p_{\mathbf b}(x)t^{\mathbf b}=0$. Thus every coefficient vanishes.

Choose a finite-dimensional nonzero generating subspace $M_0\subseteq M$ and set $M_j=F_jA_n M_0$. The <associated graded module> is finitely generated over the polynomial <associated graded ring>, so the <Hilbert-Serre theorem> implies $\dim_{\mathbb C}M_j$ is eventually a polynomial. Define
$$
\boxed{d(M)=\deg\bigl(\text{the eventual polynomial }\dim_{\mathbb C}M_j\bigr)
=\operatorname{GKdim}_{A_n}M.}
$$
This <Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module> equals the <Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module>, not its ordinary vector-space dimension. If a different finite generating subspace is used, each lies in some fixed filtration step of the other. The corresponding $M_j$ are sandwiched between shifted filtrations, so the degree is unchanged.

We prove <Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules> using an injectivity estimate. The <faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra> claim is that the map
$$
F_jA_n\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C}(M_j,M_{2j}),\qquad
a\longmapsto(v\mapsto av)
$$
is injective for every $j$. Induct on $j$. For $j=0$, a <scalar> annihilating $M_0\ne0$ is zero. Suppose $a\in F_jA_n$ annihilates $M_j$. For any generator $z=x_i$ or $\partial_i$, the <commutator> $[a,z]$ belongs to $F_{j-1}A_n$ and annihilates $M_{j-1}$: both $a(zv)$ and $z(av)$ vanish. The induction hypothesis gives $[a,z]=0$ for every generator. In the ordered basis,
$$
[a,x_i]=\sum_{\mathbf a,\mathbf b}b_i c_{\mathbf a,\mathbf b}
x^{\mathbf a}\partial^{\mathbf b-\mathbf e_i},\qquad
[a,\partial_i]=-\sum_{\mathbf a,\mathbf b}a_i c_{\mathbf a,\mathbf b}
x^{\mathbf a-\mathbf e_i}\partial^{\mathbf b}.
$$
In characteristic zero these identities force $a$ to be a <scalar>. Since it annihilates $M_0$, it is zero. This proves injectivity.

Consequently
$$
\binom{j+2n}{2n}\leq(\dim_{\mathbb C}M_j)(\dim_{\mathbb C}M_{2j}).
$$
The left side grows as a positive constant times $j^{2n}$; the right side has growth degree $2d(M)$. **Comparing powers proves**
$$
\boxed{d(M)\geq n.}
$$
The polynomial <module> $\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, with the usual multiplication and differentiation actions, has $d(M)=n$, showing the bound is sharp.
