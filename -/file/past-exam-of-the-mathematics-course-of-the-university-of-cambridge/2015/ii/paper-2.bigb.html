= Solution
{parent=1H}

The <Euler totient function> counts integers $1\leq a\leq n$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$. The <Möbius function> has $\mu(1)=1$, is zero when a prime square divides $n$, and equals $(-1)^r$ when $n$ is a product of $r$ distinct primes. Consequently
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)=\begin{cases}1&n=1,\\0&n>1,\end{cases}
$$
because for $n>1$ the sum over squarefree divisors is $(1-1)^r$.

The <Möbius inversion formula> is **$g(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)f(n/d)$**. Substituting the divisor sum defining $f$ and collecting the coefficient of $g(e)$ gives
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)f(n/d)=\sum_{e\mid n}g(e)\sum_{d\mid n/e}\mu(d)=g(n).
$$
This proof uses only finite sums, so no convergence hypothesis is needed.

Classifying $1,\ldots,n$ by their greatest common divisor with $n$ gives $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$: the class with greatest common divisor $n/d$ contains exactly $\varphi(d)$ integers. Applying the <Möbius inversion formula> yields
$$
\boxed{\varphi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\frac nd=n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-1}).}
$$
The <Riemann zeta function> is $\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. <Absolute convergence> justifies multiplication of <Dirichlet series>. The divisor identity for the <Möbius function> gives $\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}=1/\zeta(s)$ there. For $\operatorname{Re}s>2$ both factors below converge absolutely, and their <Dirichlet convolution> is the <Euler totient function>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(n)}{n^s}.}
$$

A degree-$n$ <Legendre polynomial> $P_n$ is orthogonal, for integration on $[-1,1]$, to every <polynomial> of degree at most $n-1$, and has $n$ distinct roots in $(-1,1)$. Divide $p$ by $P_n$:
$$
p=P_nq+r,\qquad \deg q\leq n-1,\quad\deg r\leq n-1.
$$
The <orthogonality of Legendre polynomials> gives $\int p=\int r$. At every quadrature node $x_j$, $p(x_j)=r(x_j)$. The assumed exactness for $r$ therefore proves **exactness up to degree $2n-1$**, the characteristic property of <Gaussian quadrature>.

Use the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial>

\ell_j(t)=\prod_{i\ne j}\frac{t-x_i}{x_j-x_i},\qquad \ell_j(x_i)=\delta_{ij}.

Its square has degree $2n-2$, so the exactness already proved gives

\boxed{A_j=\int_{-1}^1\ell_j(t)^2\,dt>0.}

The <positive weights of Gaussian quadrature> follow because $\ell_j$ is a nonzero continuous <polynomial>. Thus these <Gaussian quadrature> weights are in fact positive, rather than merely nonnegative.

To prove <convergence of positive quadrature on continuous functions>, for a continuous $f$, the <Weierstrass approximation theorem> supplies a <polynomial> $p$ with $\|f-p\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. For every sufficiently large $n$, the <Gaussian quadrature> rule is exact for this fixed <polynomial>. Positivity of its weights and their sum $2$ then give
$$
\left|Q_n(f)-\int_{-1}^1f\right|\leq\sum_jA_j|f(x_j)-p(x_j)|+\int_{-1}^1|f-p|\leq4\varepsilon.
$$
Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, **$Q_n(f)\to\int_{-1}^1f$**. Notice that no differentiability of $f$ is needed; uniform <polynomial> approximation and positivity provide the whole argument.

The <Shannon entropy>, in bits, is
$$
H(A)=-\sum_ap_a\log_2p_a=\log_2 5-\frac15\approx\boxed{2.121928}.
$$
For a prescribed alphabet distribution and code alphabet, an <optimal code> is a <uniquely decodable code> minimizing expected codeword length. <Huffman coding> produces an optimal binary <prefix code>, and its minimum is also the minimum among binary <uniquely decodable codes>.

Two optimal binary <prefix codes>, listing words in the order $a,b,c,d,e$, are
$$
(00,01,10,110,111),\qquad(0,10,110,1110,1111).
$$
Their length vectors are $(2,2,2,3,3)$ and $(1,2,3,4,4)$, and both have **expected length $2.2$ bits**. To verify optimality rather than just equal length, run <Huffman coding>. First combine $d,e$ into a node of weight $0.2$. Among the resulting three weight-$0.2$ nodes, combining $b,c$ and then combining the remaining weight-$0.2$ node with one weight-$0.4$ node gives the first length vector. Instead combine $c$ with the $d,e$ node, then combine that weight-$0.4$ node with $b$, and finally with $a$; this gives the second. Each step combines two smallest current weights, so both constructions are <Huffman coding> trees. Ties therefore allow **different optimal length profiles**.

= Solution
{parent=4J}

Writing $y_i$ for the observed counts, independence of the <Poisson distributions> gives the <log-likelihood>
$$
\ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\left\{y_i\beta x_i-e^{\beta x_i}-\log(y_i!)\right\}.
$$
Its <score function> and negative second derivative are
$$
U(\beta)=\sum_ix_i(y_i-e^{\beta x_i}),\qquad J(\beta)=\sum_ix_i^2e^{\beta x_i}.
$$
Unless all $x_i$ vanish, $J(\beta)>0$, so the <log-likelihood> is strictly concave. Use <Newton method>, starting from a finite $\beta_0$, with
$$
\boxed{\beta_{r+1}=\beta_r+\frac{\sum_ix_i(y_i-e^{\beta_rx_i})}{\sum_ix_i^2e^{\beta_rx_i}}.}
$$
Stop when the <score function> or the change in $\beta$ is small; a reduced step can ensure that the <log-likelihood> increases. Any finite zero of the <score function> is the unique <maximum-likelihood estimator>.

Existence needs a qualification. If the nonzero $x_i$ include both signs, $U$ decreases from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$, so a finite root exists. If all nonzero $x_i$ are positive, a finite root exists exactly when $\sum_ix_iy_i>0$; otherwise the supremum is approached as $\beta\to-\infty$. The corresponding condition for all negative $x_i$ is $\sum_ix_iy_i<0$, with a supremum at $+\infty$ otherwise. When all $x_i=0$, the parameter is unidentifiable. These cases explain when the iterative algorithm actually has a finite estimate to find.

At a positive spatially homogeneous <equilibrium point>, $v=u^2$ and $2+u-v=0$. Hence **$(u,v)=(2,4)$**. The reaction <Jacobian matrix> there is
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\4a&-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{tr}J=2-a,\quad\det J=6a.
$$
The <two-dimensional Routh-Hurwitz stability criterion> gives **strict linear asymptotic stability for $a>2$**, and instability for $0<a<2$.

For a spatial mode with $s=k^2$, the reaction-diffusion <linearization> is $J_s=J-\operatorname{diag}(s,ds)$. When $a>2$, its trace is negative for every $s\geq0$, while
$$
D(s)=\det J_s=ds^2+(a-2d)s+6a.
$$
A growing mode exists precisely when this quadratic is negative at some $s>0$. Its minimum must occur at positive $s$, and must be strictly negative, giving
$$
2d>a,\qquad(2d-a)^2>24ad.
$$
With $z=d/a$, the latter inequality becomes $4z^2-28z+1>0$. Selecting the root compatible with $2d>a$ yields the <Turing instability> threshold
$$
\boxed{d>\left(\frac72+2\sqrt3\right)a.}
$$
The unstable band is
$$
\frac{2d-a-\sqrt{(2d-a)^2-24ad}}{2d}<k^2<\frac{2d-a+\sqrt{(2d-a)^2-24ad}}{2d}.
$$
On a bounded domain a boundary condition must permit a mode in this band. Equality at the threshold gives a neutral mode, not positive growth.

At $a=2$, the homogeneous <linearization> has purely imaginary <eigenvalues>, so it is not strictly linearly asymptotically stable. If “stable” is read as nonlinear stability, the boundary deserves attention. Put $x=u-2$, $y=(v-4-x)/\sqrt3$, and $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. The exact equations give $\dot r=r^2F(\theta)$, $\dot\theta=\omega+rG(\theta)$, where $\omega=2\sqrt3$,
$$
F=-\frac{\cos^2\theta\sin\theta}{\sqrt3}+\cos\theta\sin^2\theta,\qquad G=\frac{2\cos^3\theta}{\sqrt3}+\cos^2\theta\sin\theta+\sqrt3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta.
$$
Expanding $dr/d\theta$ through cubic order, $\int_0^{2\pi}F=0$ cancels the quadratic displacement. The correction from the quadratic change in $r$ also integrates to zero, because it is proportional to $(\int F)^2$. Since $\int_0^{2\pi}FG=\pi/(2\sqrt3)$, the <Poincaré return map> is
$$
r\longmapsto r-\frac{\pi}{24\sqrt3}r^3+O(r^4).
$$
This <cubic return-map stability of a weak focus> shows that $a=2$ is a weak attracting focus. The strict linear range used in the <Turing instability> calculation is $a>2$; the positive homogeneous equilibrium is also nonlinearly asymptotically stable at the boundary $a=2$. For that boundary, $\operatorname{tr}J_s=-(1+d)s<0$ for every nonzero spatial mode. The same strict <Turing instability> threshold and unstable band therefore also apply there, with the homogeneous mode understood through nonlinear stability.

<Analytic continuation> extends a <holomorphic function> by holomorphic elements agreeing on overlaps; along a path, a chain of overlapping neighbourhoods transports the initial element. The <identity theorem> makes compatible continuation unique on connected overlaps, although different paths can lead to different branches.

Choose the principal <complex logarithm> and the slit domain $D=\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$. On $D$, the function
$$
h(z)=-\frac{\operatorname{Log}(1-z)}z
$$
is holomorphic, with the removable value $h(0)=1$. The domain $D$ is simply connected, so $h$ has a primitive and its integral from $0$ to $z$ is independent of the path in $D$. For $|z|<1$, the uniformly convergent <power series> on compact subdiscs gives
$$
f(z)=\int_0^zh(u)\,du=\int_0^z\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{u^{n-1}}n\,du=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{z^n}{n^2}.
$$
Therefore **$f$ is a single-valued <analytic continuation> of the <dilogarithm> to $\mathbb C\setminus[1,\infty)$**. The apparent problem at $0$ is removable; $1$ is a genuine branch point, even though the function has a finite one-sided limit there, because its derivative has a logarithmic singularity. Other cuts from $1$ to infinity give corresponding branches.

The two cyclic <Euler angles> give conserved <canonical momenta>
$$
L_3=p_\psi=I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta),\qquad L_z=p_\phi=I_1\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta.
$$
Here $L_3$ is the body-axis <angular momentum> and $L_z$ the vertical <angular momentum>. Thus **$\omega_3=L_3/I_3$ is constant**. Since the <Lagrangian> has no explicit time dependence, the <energy> is also conserved:
$$
E=\frac{I_1}2\dot\theta^2+\frac{I_1}2\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta+\frac{L_3^2}{2I_3}+Mgl\cos\theta.
$$
These are the three independent conserved quantities furnished by the continuous symmetries of the <heavy symmetric top>.

Eliminate the cyclic velocities using $\dot\phi=(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)/(I_1\sin^2\theta)$. The conserved <energy> becomes
$$
E-\frac{L_3^2}{2I_3}=\frac{I_1}2\dot\theta^2+V_{\rm eff}(\theta),\qquad\boxed{V_{\rm eff}=\frac{(L_z-L_3\cos\theta)^2}{2I_1\sin^2\theta}+Mgl\cos\theta.}
$$
Differentiating the <effective potential> gives
$$
-\frac{dV_{\rm eff}}{d\theta}=I_1\dot\phi^2\sin\theta\cos\theta-L_3\dot\phi\sin\theta+Mgl\sin\theta,
$$
which is also the $\theta$ <Euler-Lagrange equation>. Hence **$I_1\ddot\theta=-V_{\rm eff}'(\theta)$**. Deriving it directly from the <Euler-Lagrange equation> ensures it also holds at turning points, where division by $\dot\theta$ in an <energy> argument would be invalid.

For nonvertical <uniform precession of a heavy symmetric top>, write $\dot\phi=\Omega$ and fix $\theta$. The $\theta$ <Euler-Lagrange equation> reduces to
$$
\boxed{I_1\cos\theta\,\Omega^2-I_3\omega_3\Omega+Mgl=0.}
$$
For $\cos\theta\ne0$ its solutions are
$$
\Omega=\frac{I_3\omega_3\pm\sqrt{I_3^2\omega_3^2-4I_1Mgl\cos\theta}}{2I_1\cos\theta}.
$$
For $0<\theta<\pi/2$, uniform <precession> therefore requires **$|\omega_3|\geq2\sqrt{I_1Mgl\cos\theta}/I_3$**. For $\pi/2<\theta<\pi$, the discriminant is positive for every <spin>, including zero. At $\theta=\pi/2$, the condition is $\Omega=Mgl/(I_3\omega_3)$, requiring nonzero <spin>. Once this condition holds, choose $L_z=I_1\Omega\sin^2\theta+L_3\cos\theta$ and $\dot\psi=\omega_3-\Omega\cos\theta$; both cyclic equations are then satisfied. The exactly vertical configurations are also solutions for arbitrary <spin>, but the azimuthal member of the <Euler angles> is degenerate there, so their existence is separate from this nonvertical discriminant condition.

The flat matter-dominated <Friedmann equation> gives $\rho=1/(6\pi Gt^2)$ when $a=t^{2/3}$. With $P=C\rho^{4/3}$,
$$
c_s^2=\frac{dP}{d\rho}=\frac{4C}3\rho^{1/3}=\bar c_s^2t^{-2/3},\qquad\bar c_s^2=\frac{4C}3(6\pi G)^{-1/3}.
$$
For <linear density modes with polytropic pressure>, substitution gives the <Euler-Cauchy equation>
$$
\ddot\delta+\frac4{3t}\dot\delta+\frac{\bar c_s^2k^2-2/3}{t^2}\delta=0.
$$
A <power law> has exponent satisfying $n(n-1)+4n/3+\bar c_s^2k^2-2/3=0$. Thus, for distinct roots,
$$
\boxed{\delta=A_kt^{n_+}+B_kt^{n_-},\qquad n_\pm=-\frac16\pm\sqrt{\frac{25}{36}-\bar c_s^2k^2}.}
$$
For $k\ll k_J$, the two modes approach $t^{2/3}$ and $t^{-1}$: gravity produces a growing <linear cosmological density perturbation> and a decaying mode. For $k\gg k_J$, put $\nu=\sqrt{\bar c_s^2k^2-25/36}$; the real solution is
$$
\delta=t^{-1/6}\{C_k\cos(\nu\log t)+D_k\sin(\nu\log t)\}.
$$
These are pressure-supported oscillations with a decaying envelope. For the <repeated-root cosmological density mode> $k=k_J$, the complete solution is **$t^{-1/6}(A_k+B_k\log t)$**. The specified $k_J=5/(6\bar c_s)$ separates real exponents from logarithmic oscillations; the growing exponent actually crosses zero at $k=\sqrt{2/3}/\bar c_s$, a slightly smaller value.

= Solution
{parent=9I}

<Sperner's lemma> says that a triangulated triangle whose vertices are labelled $1,2,3$, with each outer vertex having its own label and each boundary edge using only its endpoint labels, contains a small triangle with all three labels. In fact the number of such triangles is odd. Count incidences of small triangles with edges labelled $1,2$. Interior edges contribute twice. On the outer $1,2$ edge, the number of changes between these labels is odd; the other two outer edges contribute zero. A small triangle contributes one incidence if it has all three labels, two if it uses both $1,2$ but not $3$, and zero otherwise. The total parity proves the assertion.

The resulting <no-retraction theorem> is: **there is no continuous <retraction> of a closed disc onto its boundary**. Work first with a closed triangle $P$. If $r:P\to\partial P$ fixes the boundary, colour each vertex of increasingly fine triangulations by an index of a largest barycentric coordinate of $r(v)$. The largest coordinate is at least $1/3$; on the boundary the colouring satisfies <Sperner's lemma>. Choose a tricoloured small triangle in each triangulation. By <compactness> and shrinking mesh, a subsequence of their three vertices converges to one point $x$. Continuity forces all three barycentric coordinates of $r(x)$ to be at least $1/3$, so $r(x)$ is the triangle's centre, contradicting $r(x)\in\partial P$. A <homeomorphism> of a disc with $P$ proves the disc assertion.

<Brouwer fixed-point theorem> states that every continuous self-map $F$ of a closed disc has a fixed point. If it had none, the ray from $F(x)$ through $x$ would meet the boundary beyond $x$, continuously in $x$. That intersection defines a <retraction>, since a boundary point is already the outgoing intersection. This contradicts the <no-retraction theorem>.

Finally fix $y\in\mathbb R^2$ and define $F_y(x)=y+x-g(x)$. The displacement bound implies $\|F_y(x)-y\|\leq K$, so $F_y$ maps the closed radius-$K$ disc centred at $y$ into itself. <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> gives $F_y(x)=x$, which is exactly $g(x)=y$. Since $y$ was arbitrary, **$g$ is surjective**.

In <RSA>, choose $N=pq$ with distinct large primes, an exponent $e$ coprime to $\varphi(N)$, and $d$ satisfying $ed\equiv1\pmod{\varphi(N)}$. Publish $(N,e)$ and keep $d$ secret. Encrypt a <residue> $m$ as $c=m^e\bmod N$ and decrypt by $c^d\bmod N$. <Fermat's little theorem> on each prime and the <Chinese remainder theorem> prove decryption, including messages not coprime to $N$.

A customer knows a nonzero multiple $M=ed-1$ of $\varphi(N)$. Write $M=2^st$ with $t$ odd. Choose a base $a$ at random. A nontrivial $\gcd(a,N)$ already factors $N$. Otherwise compute $a^t,a^{2t},\ldots,a^{2^st}$ modulo $N$. The last entry is $1$. If the first occurrence of $1$ is preceded by $z\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N$, then
$$
\boxed{\gcd(z-1,N)\text{ is a nontrivial factor of }N.}
$$
Indeed, the two prime components of this square root of $1$ must have opposite signs. This procedure succeeds for some bases: by the <Chinese remainder theorem>, choose a base whose component modulo $p$ has order $2$ and whose component modulo $q$ has order $1$. Its odd $t$-th power is already a nontrivial square root of $1$. Trying bases therefore gives an explicit factoring algorithm, and repeated random choices give the usual efficient attack. More precisely, if the two components' orders have different powers of $2$, the procedure succeeds. For a uniform element of a cyclic group with $2$-part $2^r$, those order valuations have probabilities $2^{-r}$ for $0$ and $2^{j-1-r}$ for $1\leq j\leq r$. Independence of the two prime components shows that equal valuations have probability at most $1/2$. Thus a random unit succeeds with probability at least $1/2$. Once $N$ is factored, every customer's private exponent is computable. The exceptional $ed=1$ is an identity cipher and is already insecure.

For the outsider's <RSA common-modulus attack>, find integers $a,b$ with $ae_1+be_2=1$ by <Bézout's identity>. For a unit message,
$$
\boxed{m\equiv c_1^a c_2^b\pmod N,}
$$
using modular inverses for negative powers. If a ciphertext is a nonzero nonunit, its greatest common divisor with $N$ factors $N$ instead; zero ciphertext corresponds to the zero message for a squarefree modulus. **The common message is recoverable without a private key.** For a generic unit message this alone does not supply the factorization or all private keys, so it does not demonstrate a complete break for unrelated messages. The nonunit case does give that stronger break.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, expand $(e^t-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-nt}$. <Absolute convergence> of the integrated series follows from the same expression with $\operatorname{Re}s$ in place of $s$. Integrating termwise and substituting $u=nt$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^t-1}\,dt=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-s}\Gamma(s)=\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).
$$
This proves the <Mellin transform> representation of the <Riemann zeta function>.

Take the <Hankel contour> along the lower side of the negative axis towards zero, around zero counterclockwise, and back along the upper side. For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ the small-circle contribution tends to zero. On the lower and upper rays, respectively, $\arg t=-\pi,+\pi$, so the two integrals combine to
$$
\int_H\frac{t^{s-1}}{e^{-t}-1}\,dt=\left(e^{-i\pi(s-1)}-e^{i\pi(s-1)}\right)\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{s-1}}{e^x-1}\,dx=2i\sin(\pi s)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).
$$
The <gamma reflection formula> now proves agreement of the two representations. At an integer $s=m\geq2$, the contour integral has a zero while $\Gamma(1-s)$ has a pole: **their product is interpreted by its finite limit, equal to $\zeta(m)$**, not by multiplying literal zero and infinity.

For nonpositive integers the integrand is single-valued, and the contour closes around zero. Since
$$
\frac1{e^{-t}-1}=-\frac1t-\frac12+O(t),
$$
the <residue> of $t^{-1}/(e^{-t}-1)$ is $-1/2$, giving **$\zeta(0)=-1/2$**. Also
$$
\frac1{e^{-t}-1}+\frac12=-\frac12\coth(t/2)
$$
is odd. Its Laurent series therefore has only odd powers. Multiplication by $t^{-2n-1}$ cannot produce a $t^{-1}$ term, and the subtracted constant cannot do so when $n\geq1$. Hence the contour integral vanishes and **$\zeta(-2n)=0$ for every positive integer $n$**.

The <canonical momenta> are $p_x=m\dot x$, $p_y=m\dot y+qBx$, $p_z=m\dot z$. The <Legendre transform in mechanics> gives
$$
\boxed{H=\frac{p_x^2+(p_y-qBx)^2+p_z^2}{2m}+qEy.}

<Hamilton's equations> are
$$
\dot x=p_x/m,\quad\dot y=(p_y-qBx)/m,\quad\dot z=p_z/m,\qquad\dot p_x=qB\dot y,\quad\dot p_y=-qE,\quad\dot p_z=0.

Thus $\ddot x=\Omega\dot y$, $\ddot y=-\Omega\dot x-qE/m$, and $\ddot z=0$, where $\Omega=qB/m$. The signs also follow from $\mathbf E=-E\mathbf e_y$ and $\mathbf B=B\mathbf e_z$.

\boxed{\begin{aligned}x(t)&=x_0+v_dt+\frac{a\sin\Omega t+b(1-\cos\Omega t)}\Omega,\\y(t)&=y_0+\frac{a(\cos\Omega t-1)+b\sin\Omega t}\Omega,\\z(t)&=z_0+v_{z0}t.\end{aligned}}

The recursive definition of <ordinal addition> is $\alpha+0=\alpha$, $\alpha+(\beta+1)=(\alpha+\beta)+1$, and $\alpha+\lambda=\sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$ for a nonzero <limit ordinal> $\lambda$. \boxed{N=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(E)}{e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}\,dE,\qquad U=\int_0^\infty\frac{Eg(E)}{e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1}\,dE.}
$$
If the zero-energy <ground state> is macroscopically occupied, its discrete occupation $N_0$ must be added to the first integral; it contributes no <energy> to the second. This distinction is essential when applying the formulas to <Bose-Einstein condensation>.

The <photon> number in thermal <blackbody radiation> is not fixed, so **$\mu=0$**. With $x=E/(k_BT)$,

U=AV(k_BT)^4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx=AV(k_BT)^4\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$
The integral is a temperature-independent finite constant (its explicit value follows from the gamma-zeta integral). Differentiating at fixed volume gives
$$
\boxed{C_V=\frac{4\pi^4}{15}AVk_B^4T^3,\qquad\alpha=3.}
$$

= Solution

For fixed particle number, <Bose-Einstein condensation> means that a macroscopic fraction occupies the single-particle <ground state>. As $\mu\uparrow0$, the excited-state population reaches its maximum
$$
N_{\rm ex}=B(k_BT)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2}{e^x-1}\,dx=2B\zeta(3)(k_BT)^3.
$$
At the transition this equals $N$, so
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac1{k_B}\left(\frac{N}{2B\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3}\propto N^{1/3}.}
$$
Below $T_c$, the <chemical potential> is approximately zero in the large-$N$ continuum description. Additional particles occupy the <ground state>, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{N_0}{N}\simeq1-\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^3,\qquad\gamma=3.}
$$
For finite $N$, the actual <chemical potential> remains slightly negative; the saturation statement is the macroscopic approximation used here.

Let $f(r)=1-r_s/r$ and parametrize the <timelike geodesic> by <proper time> $\tau$. Time-translation and rotational symmetry conserve $E=f\dot t$ and $h=r^2\dot\phi$ in the equatorial plane. The normalized <four-velocity> obeys
$$
-1=-f\dot t^2+f^{-1}\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2.
$$
Substitution and multiplication by $f$ give $\dot r^2+f(1+h^2/r^2)=E^2$, or
$$
\boxed{\tfrac12\dot r^2+V(r)=\tfrac12E^2,\qquad2V=(1-r_s/r)(1+h^2/r^2).}
$$
The dot denotes a proper-time derivative, and $E,h$ are <energy> and <angular momentum> per unit rest mass.

At a circular orbit, $V'=0$. Differentiating the Schwarzschild <effective potential> at fixed $h$ gives
$$
V'=\frac{r_s}{2r^2}-\frac{h^2}{r^3}+\frac{3r_sh^2}{2r^4},\qquad\boxed{h^2=\frac{r^2r_s}{2r-3r_s}.}
$$
A timelike circular orbit requires $r>3r_s/2$. Evaluating the second derivative after this substitution gives
$$
V''=\frac{r_s(r-3r_s)}{r^3(2r-3r_s)}.
$$
A stable nondegenerate minimum therefore requires **$r>3r_s$**. The boundary $r=3r_s$ is the marginal <innermost stable circular orbit>, not a strict stable minimum.

Near the closest point, in $X=x-Ut$, the gap is $h(X)\simeq h_0+X^2/(2a)$. The horizontal length scale is **$\ell=\sqrt{2ah_0}$**, up to an inessential constant. Thus the aspect ratio $h_0/\ell\ll1$ follows from $h_0\ll a$. The <lubrication approximation> assumes an incompressible <Newtonian fluid>, no slip on the surfaces, slow variation of the gap, and negligible inertia relative to transverse viscous stress:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\rho U h_0^2}{\mu\ell}\ll1.}
$$
This is the reduced Reynolds-number condition; it is weaker than requiring a <Reynolds number> based on $\ell$ to be small. In the cylinder frame the geometry and flow are steady. <Pressure> is uniform across the narrow gap to leading order, and equal ambient <pressures> far to either side close the model. The calculation assumes a full liquid film; <cavitation> would change that <pressure> condition.

{parent=35e}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Reflection about the closest point leaves the gap geometry unchanged and reverses the direction of translation. The linear Stokes equations make the excess <pressure> change sign when the imposed <velocity> changes sign. Combining reflection with this linearity shows that <pressure> is odd in $X$. Its leading normal force on the stationary plane therefore cancels between the two sides, giving **$\mathbf e_y\cdot\mathbf F=0$ in the lubrication limit**. Normal viscous stresses are of smaller order in the aspect ratio. This uses symmetry only, rather than a <pressure> calculation.

{parent=35e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

In the laboratory frame the lower wall has <velocity> zero and the cylinder surface has <velocity> $U$. Solving $\mu u_{yy}=p_X$ with these no-slip conditions gives
$$
\boxed{u(X,y)=\frac{Uy}{h}+\frac{p_X}{2\mu}y(y-h).}
$$
The relative <velocity> in the cylinder frame is $u-U$. Its flux is constant by incompressibility:
$$
q=-\frac{Uh}{2}-\frac{h^3p_X}{12\mu}.
$$
Equal <pressures> at the two infinities require $\int p_X\,dX=0$. Put $I_j=\int h^{-j}\,dX$; then $q=-(U/2)I_2/I_3$. With $X=\ell s$ and $h=h_0(1+s^2)$, the given integrals yield $I_2/I_3=4h_0/3$. Hence $q=-2Uh_0/3$ and
$$
\boxed{p_X=\frac{2\mu U(4h_0-3h)}{h^3}.}
$$
Indeed $p=2\mu UX/h^2$ has this derivative and tends to ambient <pressure> at both ends. The distinction between the constant comoving flux and the varying laboratory flux is necessary because the gap translates.

{title2=Waves}

= Solution
{parent=36B}

The linear elastic wave equation is $\rho\mathbf u_{tt}=(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u)+\mu\Delta\mathbf u$. For a <shear-horizontal wave>, take $\mathbf u=(0,v(x,z,t),0)$ with no $y$ dependence. Its divergence vanishes, and
$$
v_{tt}=c_s^2(v_{xx}+v_{zz}),\qquad c_s=\sqrt{\mu/\rho}.
$$
Rigid fixed boundaries require $v=0$ at $z=0,h$. The modes are $v=A\sin(q_nz)\cos(kx-\omega_nt)$ with $q_n=n\pi/h$, $n=1,2,\ldots$, and
$$
\boxed{\omega_n(k)=c_s\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2},\qquad\omega_{n,\mathrm{cut}}=c_sq_n.}
$$
There is no $n=0$ displacement mode with these clamped boundary conditions. For $k>0$, the <phase velocity> and <group velocity> are
$$
\boxed{c(k)=c_s\frac{\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2}}k,\qquad c_g(k)=c_s\frac{k}{\sqrt{k^2+q_n^2}}.}
$$
The <phase velocity> decreases from infinity to $c_s$, while the <group velocity> increases from zero to $c_s$; their product is $c_s^2$.

\Image[paper-2-sh-waveguide.png]
{title=Dispersion, phase velocity and group velocity of a clamped shear-horizontal waveguide mode}

For a mode of frequency $\omega$, the appropriate time and cross-sectional average is $\langle A\rangle=\omega/(2\pi h)\int_0^{2\pi/\omega}\int_0^h A\,dz\,dt$, per unit transverse width. Its average <kinetic energy density> is $\rho A^2\omega^2/8$. The only nonzero strains are $e_{xy}=e_{yx}=v_x/2$ and $e_{yz}=e_{zy}=v_z/2$, while $e_{kk}=0$. Thus the elastic <energy> density is $\mu(v_x^2+v_z^2)/2$, whose average is $\mu A^2(k^2+q_n^2)/8$. The <dispersion relation> proves **equality of average kinetic and elastic energies**.

For the <ray asymptotics of a clamped elastic waveguide mode>, expand the localized initial displacement in transverse sine modes and <Fourier transform> in $x$. For a displacement released from rest, each mode evolves with $\cos(\omega_n(k)t)$. At $x=Vt$ the oscillatory phases are $t(kV\pm\omega_n(k))$. If $0<|V|<c_s$, one phase has a <stationary point> with
$$
|k_*|=\frac{q_n|V|}{\sqrt{c_s^2-V^2}},\qquad\omega_n''(k_*)>0.
$$
The <stationary phase method> gives a **generic oscillatory amplitude proportional to $t^{-1/2}$** for each contributing mode. If the initial <Fourier coefficient> vanishes at the <stationary point>, that mode can decay faster; smooth localized data make the modal sum well behaved. If $|V|>c_s$, there is no <stationary point>. Repeated integration by parts gives rapid decay for smooth rapidly decaying data; for initially compactly supported displacement, <finite propagation speed> makes the displacement on that ray **exactly zero after a sufficiently long time**. These are the requested subsonic and super-shear-speed regimes.

= Solution

Order the interior grid values into a vector. The coefficient matrix has diagonal $4+ch^2$ and off-diagonal entries $-1$ at horizontal and vertical neighbours, with boundary values already zero. Every row is strictly diagonally dominant because $c>0$. The standard convergence theorem for <strict diagonal dominance> gives **convergence of both the <Jacobi method> and the <Gauss-Seidel method>**. The matrix is also symmetric positive definite.

For $D=(4+ch^2)I$, the <weighted Jacobi method> is $u^{(r+1)}=u^{(r)}+\omega D^{-1}(b-Au^{(r)})$, with iteration matrix $H_\omega=I-\omega D^{-1}A$. The sine-product grid <eigenvectors> give
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{k,l}=1-\omega\frac{4\{\sin^2(\pi kh/2)+\sin^2(\pi lh/2)\}+ch^2}{4+ch^2},\qquad1\leq k,l\leq m.}
$$
These <weighted Jacobi eigenvalues for a square Dirichlet grid> determine convergence. All <eigenvalues> of $A$ are positive. Thus for fixed $h$ convergence requires $0<\omega<2(4+ch^2)/a_{\max}$, where $a_{\max}=8\cos^2(\pi h/2)+ch^2$. As $h\downarrow0$, this upper bound tends to $1$ from above. Any fixed $\omega>1$ therefore fails on sufficiently fine grids; $\omega\leq0$ also fails. Consequently **$0<\omega\leq1$ is necessary for all mesh sizes**, and it is sufficient, since $a_{\max}<2(4+ch^2)$ for every finite grid.

= b
{parent=37e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <multigrid method> combines a fine-grid smoother with a <coarse-grid correction>. Starting with $u_h$, apply a few smoothing iterations, compute $r_h=b_h-A_hu_h$, restrict this residual to a coarser grid, and approximately solve $A_He_H=Rr_h$. Prolong the coarse error and update $u_h\leftarrow u_h+Pe_H$, then post-smooth. The coarse solve is itself performed recursively, with a direct solve at the coarsest level; visiting each coarser level once gives a V-cycle. The smoother damps rapidly varying error, while error smooth on the fine grid is efficiently represented and corrected on the coarse grid.

For ordinary Jacobi, $\omega=1$, the highest-frequency sine-product error has <eigenvalue> approaching $-1$, not zero. In particular for the discrete Poisson equation it is $-\cos(\pi h)$, whose magnitude approaches one. This nearly alternating error changes sign between sweeps but is barely damped, and ordinary coarse-grid restriction does not represent it well. Thus **with the standard point-Jacobi smoother and coarse-grid transfers one chooses $\omega\ne1$, usually $0<\omega<1$, for fast multigrid convergence**. For example $\omega=2/3$ makes the extreme high-frequency <eigenvalue> approach $-1/3$. Convergence of Jacobi as a standalone iteration is therefore insufficient to guarantee that it is an effective smoother. This conclusion concerns that standard multigrid choice; other smoothers or specially designed transfers can change it.
