Write $\Delta_t^2f(x)=f(x+t)-2f(x)+f(x-t)$, and use the <second modulus of smoothness> $\omega_2(f,h)=\sup_{|t|\le h}\|\Delta_t^2f\|_\infty$. The <Jackson kernel> is even, nonnegative and has unit integral. Pairing the two halves of its <convolution> therefore gives
$$
j_nf(x)-f(x)=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\Delta_t^2f(x)J_n(t)\,dt.
$$
We need a weighted moment estimate rather than just concentration of the <Jackson kernel>. For $|t|\le\pi$, the bounds $|\sin(t/2)|\ge |t|/\pi$ and $|\sin(nt/2)|\le\min(n|\sin(t/2)|,1)$ imply
$$
0\le J_n(t)\le C\min\{n,n^{-3}|t|^{-4}\}.
$$
Consequently, splitting the integral at $1/n$ gives
$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}t^2J_n(t)\,dt
\le 2C\left(n\int_0^{1/n}t^2\,dt+n^{-3}\int_{1/n}^{\pi}t^{-2}\,dt\right)\le C'n^{-2}.
$$
The <second modulus of smoothness> satisfies $\omega_2(f,t)\le(1+t/h)^2\omega_2(f,h)$ for $t\ge0$. To see this, choose $q=\lceil t/h\rceil$ when $t>0$, put $u=t/q$, and factor the <function translation> difference $T_t-I=(T_u-I)\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}T_u^r$. Squaring gives at most $q^2$ translated second differences of step $u$. Each <function translation> is an <isometry> in the <supremum norm>, and centered and forward second differences have the same <supremum norm>. This proves the stated scaling bound.

Taking $h=1/n$, using $(1+n|t|)^2\le2+2n^2t^2$, and integrating against the <Jackson kernel> now proves
$$
\|j_nf-f\|_\infty\le\frac12\omega_2(f,1/n)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(1+n|t|)^2J_n(t)\,dt\le C''\omega_2(f,1/n).
$$
Thus **the Jackson operator estimate is uniform in both $f$ and $n$**.

For the requested degree-$n$ <best uniform approximation>, note that the <Jackson kernel>, and hence $j_mf$, has <trigonometric polynomial> degree at most $2(m-1)$. Indeed the fourth power in the <Jackson kernel> is the squared modulus of a squared sum of $m$ consecutive complex exponentials. Choose $m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$, so $2(m-1)\le n$ and $m\ge n/2$. The <second-difference integral formula> gives
$$
\Delta_t^2f(x)=\int_{-|t|}^{|t|}(|t|-|u|)f''(x+u)\,du,
\qquad \omega_2(f,h)\le h^2\|f''\|_\infty.
$$
Applying the <Jackson operator estimate> with this $m$ yields the concise answer
$$
\boxed{E_n(f)\le \|j_mf-f\|_\infty\le \frac{4C''}{n^2}\|f''\|_\infty\quad(n\ge1).}
$$
The change of index is essential: $j_nf$ itself generally has degree $2n-2$, exceeding the permitted degree.

The <Bernstein polynomial> is the positive weighted average
$$
B_n(f,x)=\sum_{k=0}^n f(k/n)b_{n,k}(x),\qquad b_{n,k}(x)=\binom nkx^k(1-x)^{n-k}.
$$
For $0\le x\le1$, the <Bernstein basis> weights are nonnegative and the <binomial theorem> gives $\sum_{k=0}^nb_{n,k}(x)=1$. Hence $|B_n(f,x)|\le\sum_k\|f\|_\infty b_{n,k}(x)=\|f\|_\infty$. In particular,
$$
\boxed{\|B_n(f)\|_\infty\le\|f\|_\infty.}
$$
This is the contraction property of the <positive linear operator on continuous functions> defined by the <Bernstein polynomial>.

The <integer> endpoint hypothesis is important. Let $r_{n,k}=\binom nk f(k/n)-\lfloor\binom nk f(k/n)\rfloor$. The <floor function> gives $0\le r_{n,k}<1$, while $r_{n,0}=r_{n,n}=0$ because $f(0)$ and $f(1)$ are <integers>. Therefore the <rounded Bernstein polynomial> satisfies
$$
0\le B_n(f,x)-B_n^*(f,x)\le S_n(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}x^k(1-x)^{n-k}.
$$
For $0\le x\le1/4$, the successive terms have ratio $x/(1-x)\le1/3$, so the <geometric series> estimate gives
$$
S_n(x)\le\frac32x(1-x)^{n-1}\le\frac{3}{2n}.
$$
The last inequality follows by maximizing $x(1-x)^{n-1}$ at $x=1/n$. The same bound holds on $[3/4,1]$ by reflection. On $[1/4,3/4]$, each factor $x$ and $1-x$ is at most $3/4$, giving $S_n(x)\le(n-1)(3/4)^n$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\|B_n(f)-B_n^*(f)\|_\infty\le\max\left\{\frac{3}{2n},(n-1)(3/4)^n\right\}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Here $n\ge2$; for $n=1$ the difference vanishes. Without the <integer> endpoint hypothesis the claim would fail: at $x=0$ the error is $f(0)-\lfloor f(0)\rfloor$, independently of $n$.

First prove necessity. If <polynomials> with <integer> <coefficients> converge to $f$ in the <supremum norm>, their values at $0$ and $1$ are convergent sequences of <integers>. Each such sequence is eventually constant, since a convergent sequence is <Cauchy> and two distinct <integers> are at distance at least one. Its limit is an <integer>. Thus $f(0),f(1)\in\mathbb Z$.

For sufficiency we also need <uniform convergence> of the <Bernstein polynomial> to $f$. Here is a direct argument. For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,x)$, write $B_n(f,x)=\mathbb E f(X/n)$. The <binomial distribution> has $\mathbb E(X/n)=x$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X/n)=x(1-x)/n\le1/(4n)$. For every $\delta>0$, <uniform continuity> of $f$ and the <Chebyshev inequality> give
$$
|B_n(f,x)-f(x)|\le\omega(f,\delta)+2\|f\|_\infty\mathbb P(|X/n-x|>\delta)
\le\omega(f,\delta)+\frac{\|f\|_\infty}{2n\delta^2}.
$$
First make $\delta$ small and then $n$ large; the bound is uniform in $x$. Hence $\|B_n(f)-f\|_\infty\to0$.

If the endpoints are <integers>, part (b) gives $\|B_n^*(f)-B_n(f)\|_\infty\to0$. Each coefficient multiplying $x^k(1-x)^{n-k}$ in $B_n^*$ is an <integer>. Expanding $(1-x)^{n-k}$ by the <binomial theorem> therefore gives <integer> <coefficients> in the ordinary monomial <basis>. The <triangle inequality> proves $\|B_n^*(f)-f\|_\infty\to0$. Thus **the <integer> endpoint condition is necessary and sufficient** for <integer-coefficient polynomial approximation>.

Use real-valued <continuous functions> and real <trigonometric polynomials>. The <trigonometric Chebyshev alternation theorem> says that $p\in\mathcal T_n$ is the unique <best uniform approximation> to $f$ if and only if there are distinct points, ordered within one period,
$$
x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_{2n+1}<x_0+2\pi,
\qquad f(x_j)-p(x_j)=\varepsilon(-1)^j\|f-p\|_\infty,
\quad\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}.
$$
Thus **the required number of alternating extrema is $2n+2$**. When the error is zero, the assertion is interpreted trivially and $p=f$ is unique. The count reflects $\dim\mathcal T_n=2n+1$ and the fact that a nonzero degree-$n$ <trigonometric polynomial> has at most $2n$ zeros in one period. In particular a strictly better candidate would force its difference from $p$ to have alternating signs at these points and hence at least $2n+2$ zeros, counting the closing interval around the circle. The <trigonometric Chebyshev alternation theorem> also gives necessity and uniqueness.

Use the usual strictly increasing <spline knot sequence>, and initially assume $k\ge2$. The explicit formula for the order-$k$ <divided difference> gives
$$
M_i(t)=k\sum_{j=i}^{i+k}\frac{(t_j-t)_+^{k-1}}{\prod_{\substack{\ell=i\\\ell\ne j}}^{i+k}(t_j-t_\ell)}.
$$
Each summand is a <truncated power function> in $t$, with its junction at $t_j$. It is a <piecewise polynomial function> of degree at most $k-1$ and has $k-2$ <continuous> <derivatives>. Finite summation preserves these properties.

For $t\ge t_{i+k}$ all summands vanish. For $t\le t_i$, all <spline knot> data come from the <polynomial> $u\mapsto(u-t)^{k-1}$. An order-$k$ <divided difference> annihilates a <polynomial> of smaller degree, so $M_i(t)=0$ there too. Thus the <support> is contained in $[t_i,t_{i+k}]$.

On the last open <spline knot> interval only the $j=i+k$ term survives; it is a nonzero multiple of $(t_{i+k}-t)^{k-1}$. On the first open <spline knot> interval, compare the truncated <spline knot> data with the untruncated <polynomial> data: only the $j=i$ value differs. Since the untruncated <divided difference> is zero, the result is a nonzero multiple of $(t_i-t)^{k-1}$. Consequently both outer pieces are nonzero, giving exact closed <support>. The <Cox-de Boor recurrence> used below also proves positivity throughout the open <support>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{M_i\in C^{k-2}(\mathbb R),\qquad\operatorname{supp}M_i=[t_i,t_{i+k}],\qquad\deg\text{ each piece}\le k-1.}
$$
This is <simple-knot B-spline regularity>. For $k=1$ the <B-splines> are interval indicators; they are piecewise constant and need not be <continuous>. The notation $C^{k-2}$ should then be read as no <continuity> requirement, not as a classical negative-order differentiability space.

The <divided difference> is a finite linear combination of <spline knot> values, so it commutes with the finite integral. For every <spline knot> $t_j$ in the defining interval,
$$
\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+k}}(t_j-t)_+^{k-1}\,dt=\frac{(t_j-t_i)^k}{k}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\int_{t_i}^{t_{i+k}}M_i(t)\,dt=[t_i,\ldots,t_{i+k}](u-t_i)^k=1,
$$
because the order-$k$ <divided difference> of a monic degree-$k$ <polynomial> is its leading coefficient. This proves the <unit-integral normalization of a B-spline>.

For the <partition of unity>, let $N_{i,r}$ denote the partition-normalized order-$r$ <B-spline>. The <Cox-de Boor recurrence> is
$$
N_{i,r}(t)=\frac{t-t_i}{t_{i+r-1}-t_i}N_{i,r-1}(t)+\frac{t_{i+r}-t}{t_{i+r}-t_{i+1}}N_{i+1,r-1}(t).
$$
It follows by substituting the explicit <divided difference> formula into both sides and collecting each <truncated power function>. The starting case is $N_{i,1}=\mathbf1_{[t_i,t_{i+1})}$, with endpoint conventions irrelevant when $r\ge2$. The two weights are nonnegative wherever their respective lower-order <B-splines> are nonzero. Induction therefore gives $N_{i,r}\ge0$ and strict positivity in its open <support>.

Extend the finite <spline knot sequence> strictly in both directions, with no finite accumulation. At each point only finitely many <B-splines> are nonzero. For the full extended family the sum is one for order one. Summing the <Cox-de Boor recurrence>, the combined coefficient of $N_{j,r-1}$ is
$$
\frac{t-t_j}{t_{j+r-1}-t_j}+\frac{t_{j+r-1}-t}{t_{j+r-1}-t_j}=1.
$$
Induction gives a full <partition of unity> at every order. On $t_k<t<t_{n+1}$, all extended order-$k$ <functions> with index $i\le0$ or $i\ge n+1$ vanish by their supports. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^nN_i(t)=1\quad(t_k<t<t_{n+1}),\qquad\int M_i=1.}
$$
The same argument gives the useful global <subpartition of unity for B-splines>, $0\le\sum_{i=1}^nN_i(t)\le1$, including outside the basic <spline knot> interval.

The <orthogonal projection> has range $\mathcal S$, not all of $C[0,1]$: the latter is an <infinite-dimensional vector space> whereas $\mathcal S$ is a <finite-dimensional vector space>. For a concrete counterexample, $e^x$ cannot belong to any finite-degree piecewise polynomial space, since all its <derivatives> are nonzero on every open interval. For $k\ge2$, the <B-splines> are <continuous>, so the <orthogonal projection> is a map $C[0,1]\to\mathcal S\subset C[0,1]$. For order one it instead has a generally discontinuous piecewise constant range. For example, a single interval indicator on $[1/4,3/4)$ is the projection of the constant <function> one onto its span, and is not <continuous>.

Write $s^*=\sum_ja_jN_j$. Since $M_i$ is a positive <scalar multiple> of $N_i$, the <operator normal equations> $\langle f-s^*,N_i\rangle=0$ are equivalent to
$$
Ga=b,\qquad b_i=\langle M_i,f\rangle.
$$
The <mixed-normalization spline Gram matrix> is invertible: $G=DH$ where $D_{ii}=k/(t_{i+k}-t_i)>0$ and $H_{ij}=\langle N_i,N_j\rangle$ is the ordinary <positive-definite matrix> of <inner products> of a linearly independent <basis>. The positivity and <unit-integral normalization of a B-spline> give $|b_i|\le\|f\|_\infty$. The <subpartition of unity for B-splines> gives
$$
|s^*(t)|\le\|a\|_{\ell^\infty}\sum_jN_j(t)\le\|a\|_{\ell^\infty}.
$$
Here the <operator norm> of a <matrix> on $\ell^\infty$ is its maximum absolute row sum. Consequently,
$$
\|P_{\mathcal S}f\|_\infty\le\|a\|_{\ell^\infty}\le\|G^{-1}\|_{\ell^\infty}\|b\|_{\ell^\infty}\le\|G^{-1}\|_{\ell^\infty}\|f\|_\infty,
\qquad\boxed{\|P_{\mathcal S}\|_\infty\le\|G^{-1}\|_{\ell^\infty}.}
$$
This proves the <maximum-norm bound for spline projection>.

An <orthonormal> <multiresolution analysis> consists of <closed subspaces of a Hilbert space> $(V_j)_{j\in\mathbb Z}$ of the <Hilbert space> $L^2(\mathbb R)$ such that
$$
V_j\subset V_{j+1},\qquad\overline{\bigcup_jV_j}=L^2(\mathbb R),\qquad\bigcap_jV_j=\{0\},\qquad
g\in V_j\ \Longleftrightarrow\ g(2\cdot)\in V_{j+1}.
$$
There is a <scaling function> $\phi$ whose <integer> <function translations> form an <orthonormal basis> of $V_0$. Consequently $\phi_{j,k}(x)=2^{j/2}\phi(2^jx-k)$ form an <orthonormal basis> of $V_j$; in particular $V_0$ is invariant under <integer> <function translations>.

Let $W_j=V_{j+1}\ominus V_j$ be the <orthogonal complement> of $V_j$ in $V_{j+1}$. Nesting, density and the trivial intersection imply the <orthogonal direct sum>
$$
L^2(\mathbb R)=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb Z}W_j.
$$
For example, telescoping $V_{J+1}=V_L\oplus\bigoplus_{j=L}^JW_j$ and letting $L\to-\infty$, $J\to\infty$ gives this decomposition; the corresponding <orthogonal projections> converge strongly to zero and the identity by the intersection and density properties.

To construct the <orthonormal wavelet>, use $\phi\in V_1$ to write the <scaling refinement equation> $\phi=\sum_na_n\phi(2\cdot-n)$, with $a\in\ell^2$ and $\sum_n|a_n|^2=2$. Put $m(t)=\frac12\sum_na_ne^{-int}$. The <orthonormal translates and Fourier periodization> criterion proved in part (b), applied to the <scaling refinement equation>, yields
$$
1=\sum_k|\widehat\phi(2t+2\pi k)|^2
=|m(t)|^2\sum_\ell|\widehat\phi(t+2\pi\ell)|^2+|m(t+\pi)|^2\sum_\ell|\widehat\phi(t+\pi+2\pi\ell)|^2
=|m(t)|^2+|m(t+\pi)|^2.
$$
This is the <quadrature mirror filter> identity. Define
$$
q(t)=-e^{-it}\overline{m(t+\pi)},\qquad b_n=(-1)^n\overline{a_{1-n}},\qquad
\psi(x)=\sum_nb_n\phi(2x-n).
$$
The <Fourier series> of $q$ is $\frac12\sum_nb_ne^{-int}$, and $\widehat\psi(2t)=q(t)\widehat\phi(t)$. The two rows $(m(t),m(t+\pi))$ and $(q(t),q(t+\pi))$ form a unitary $2\times2$ <matrix> almost everywhere: both have squared <norm> one and their <inner product> is zero. This <wavelet completion of a multiresolution filter> proves not just orthogonality but completeness. Splitting a finer-scale coefficient <Fourier series> into its values at $t$ and $t+\pi$, this invertible two-channel matrix resolves it into a coarse-scale channel and its complementary channel. Thus the <integer> <function translations> of $\psi$ form an <orthonormal basis> of $W_0$.

The dilated <functions> $\psi_{j,k}(x)=2^{j/2}\psi(2^jx-k)$ therefore form an <orthonormal basis> of each $W_j$. The <orthogonal direct sum> proves the conclusion: **every <orthonormal> multiresolution analysis produces an <orthonormal> wavelet <basis>** $\{\psi_{j,k}:j,k\in\mathbb Z\}$ of $L^2(\mathbb R)$.

The half-open translates $[-\pi,\pi)+2\pi k$ partition the real line. Exactly one summand is therefore nonzero in the periodization of $|f|^2$, giving $\sum_k|f(t+2\pi k)|^2=1$.

Choose the <Shannon scaling mask> to be the $2\pi$-periodic extension of $\mathbf1_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2)}$. On the <support> of $f$, its product with $f$ is $\mathbf1_{[-\pi/2,\pi/2)}(t)=f(2t)$; outside that <support> both sides vanish. Thus the <scaling refinement equation> holds in frequency. The <Fourier coefficients> give its precise filter normalization:
$$
a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}e^{int}\,dt
=\begin{cases}1,&n=0,\\\dfrac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{\pi n},&n\ne0.\end{cases}
$$
These <coefficients> are square summable, so the interpretation in part (b) applies.

The <Fourier inversion theorem> gives the <Shannon scaling function>
$$
\phi(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{ixt}\,dt,
\qquad\boxed{\phi(x)=\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}\ (x\ne0),\qquad\phi(0)=1.}
$$
This is the normalized <sinc function>. Its <Fourier transform> is taken in the <Plancherel theorem> sense: the <Shannon scaling function> belongs to $L^2$ but not $L^1$, so its Fourier integral is not absolutely convergent. The inverse integral above is an ordinary integral because the rectangular transform belongs to $L^1$. The resulting refinement spaces are exactly the $L^2$ <functions> with <Fourier transform> supported in $[-2^j\pi,2^j\pi]$; their union is <dense> and their intersection is zero. Thus this example also gives a full <multiresolution analysis>.
