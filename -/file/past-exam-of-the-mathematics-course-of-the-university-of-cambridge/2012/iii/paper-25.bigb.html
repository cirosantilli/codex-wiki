For the covariant version of the <Yoneda embedding>, put $Y(A)=\mathcal C(A,-)$. A <morphism> $u:A\to B$ induces the <natural transformation> $Y(u):Y(B)\to Y(A)$ whose component sends $v:B\to X$ to $vu$. Thus $Y$ is a <functor> from the <opposite category> $\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}$ to the <functor category> $[\mathcal C,\mathbf{Set}]$. The <locally small category> hypothesis makes each value a <set>.

The <Yoneda lemma> says that, for a <functor> $F:\mathcal C\to\mathbf{Set}$, evaluation at the <identity morphism> gives a <natural bijection>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Nat}(\mathcal C(A,-),F)\cong F(A),\qquad \theta\longmapsto\theta_A(1_A).}
$$
Given $a\in F(A)$, define $\theta^a_X(v)=F(v)(a)$. For $w:X\to X'$, the <functor> law gives $F(w)\theta^a_X(v)=F(wv)(a)=\theta^a_{X'}(wv)$, proving <naturality>. Conversely, <naturality> of $\theta$ at $v:A\to X$ gives $\theta_X(v)=F(v)\theta_A(1_A)$. These two constructions are inverse, because $F(1_A)(a)=a$.

The <bijection> is natural in both variables. A <natural transformation> $\beta:F\to H$ sends the element $a$ to $\beta_A(a)$, matching postcomposition by $\beta$. For $u:A\to B$, precomposing with $Y(u)$ sends $\theta$ to a transformation from $Y(B)$ and its distinguished element is $\theta_B(u)=F(u)(a)$. This is exactly the required dependence on $A$.

Taking $F=Y(B)$ gives $\operatorname{Nat}(Y(A),Y(B))\cong\mathcal C(B,A)$. Consequently **the covariant <Yoneda embedding> is <full and faithful>**, with the reversal of arrows accounted for by its <opposite category> domain.

Suppose every component $\alpha_A$ is a <monomorphism> in the <category of sets>, hence an <injective function>. If $\beta,\gamma:H\to F$ are <natural transformations> with $\alpha\beta=\alpha\gamma$, then $\alpha_A\beta_A=\alpha_A\gamma_A$ at every object. Componentwise cancellation gives $\beta_A=\gamma_A$, so $\beta=\gamma$. Thus $\alpha$ is a <monomorphism> in the <functor category>.

Conversely, suppose $\alpha$ is a <monomorphism> and $x,y\in F(A)$ satisfy $\alpha_A(x)=\alpha_A(y)$. The <Yoneda lemma> supplies <natural transformations> $\theta^x,\theta^y:Y(A)\to F$. Its <naturality> in $F$ identifies $\alpha\theta^x$ and $\alpha\theta^y$ with the equal elements $\alpha_A(x)$ and $\alpha_A(y)$. Cancellation by $\alpha$ gives $\theta^x=\theta^y$; evaluating at $1_A$ gives $x=y$. Therefore **a <natural transformation> is a <monomorphism> exactly when all its components are <injective functions>**. This is the <pointwise monomorphism in a set-valued functor category> criterion.

Let $D:J\to\mathcal C$ be a finite <diagram in a category>. Form the finite <products in a category>
$$
P=\prod_{j\in\operatorname{Ob}J}D(j),\qquad Q=\prod_{a:j\to k\ \text{in }J}D(k).
$$
Define $s,t:P\rightrightarrows Q$ by $\pi_a s=D(a)\pi_j$ and $\pi_a t=\pi_k$. Take their <equalizer> $e:L\to P$. The maps $\lambda_j=\pi_j e$ satisfy $D(a)\lambda_j=\lambda_k$, so they constitute a <categorical cone>.

Any other <categorical cone> $x_j:X\to D(j)$ gives a unique $x:X\to P$ by the <product in a category> property. Its cone equations say $sx=tx$, so the <equalizer> gives a unique $\bar x:X\to L$ with $e\bar x=x$. The projections then give $\lambda_j\bar x=x_j$, and uniqueness follows from both universal properties. Thus **this <equalizer> of two product maps is the <finite limit> of the <diagram in a category>**.

The empty indexing <category> is included: its empty <product in a category> is a <terminal object>, which is the empty <categorical limit>. Hence no nonemptiness restriction has entered the construction.

With a <terminal object> $1$, the <pullback in a category> of $A\to1\leftarrow B$ is the binary <product in a category> $A\times B$. Repetition gives all nonempty finite <products in a category>, and $1$ supplies the empty one.

For $f,g:A\rightrightarrows B$, form the diagonal $\Delta_B:B\to B\times B$ and pull it back along $\langle f,g\rangle:A\to B\times B$. Write the resulting maps as $e:E\to A$ and $h:E\to B$. The equation $\langle f,g\rangle e=\Delta_B h$ says $fe=ge=h$. A map $x:X\to A$ with $fx=gx$ pairs with $fx:X\to B$ to give a unique map into this <pullback in a category>. Thus $e$ is the <equalizer> of $f,g$.

We now have the finite <products in a category> and <equalizers> needed in part (a). **A <terminal object> and <pullbacks in a category> therefore suffice for all <finite limits>.**

An object of the <slice category> $\mathcal C/A$ is a <morphism> $x:X\to A$; a map from $x$ to $y:Y\to A$ is $u:X\to Y$ with $yu=x$. The <identity morphism> $1_A:A\to A$ is a <terminal object> of this <slice category>, even when $\mathcal C$ itself has no <terminal object>.

For a cospan $X\xrightarrow{u}Z\xleftarrow{v}Y$ in the <slice category>, form its underlying <pullback in a category> $P$ in $\mathcal C$, with maps $p:P\to X$ and $q:P\to Y$. If $x,y,z$ are the structure maps to $A$, then $xp=zup=zvq=yq$. This common map equips $P$ with its slice structure. An underlying mediating map into $P$ is automatically over $A$, because its composite with $xp$ is the prescribed structure map. Hence the same square is a <pullback in a category> in $\mathcal C/A$.

Part (b) applies to the slice's <terminal object> and <pullbacks in a category>. **Every <slice category> has all <finite limits> when the ambient <category> has <pullbacks in a category>.** The <finite completeness of slice categories> result needs no ambient terminal-object hypothesis.

A <monomorphism> $m:A\to B$ satisfies $mu=mv\Rightarrow u=v$ for every parallel pair into $A$. An <epimorphism> $e:X\to Y$ satisfies $ue=ve\Rightarrow u=v$ for every parallel pair out of $Y$. A <strong monomorphism> is a <monomorphism> with the following lifting property: whenever $e:X\to Y$ is an <epimorphism> and $mu=ve$, with $u:X\to A$ and $v:Y\to B$, there is a unique $d:Y\to A$ with $de=u$ and $md=v$. A <regular monomorphism> is a <morphism> occurring as the <equalizer> of two parallel maps. A <balanced category> is one in which every <morphism> that is both a <monomorphism> and an <epimorphism> is an <isomorphism>.

Suppose $m:A\to B$ equalizes $p,q:B\rightrightarrows D$. If $mu=mv$, the common composite has two factorizations through the <equalizer>, so its uniqueness property gives $u=v$. Hence every <regular monomorphism> is indeed a <monomorphism>.

Now take the lifting square just described. The equations $pmu=qmu$ imply $pve=qve$. Cancel the <epimorphism> $e$ to obtain $pv=qv$. The <equalizer> gives a unique $d$ with $md=v$. Since $mde=ve=mu$ and $m$ is a <monomorphism>, $de=u$. Thus **every <regular monomorphism> is a <strong monomorphism>**; the existence of the lift uses epic cancellation, and its uniqueness uses the equalizer property.

If every <monomorphism> is strong, let $m:A\to B$ be both a <monomorphism> and an <epimorphism>. Use $m$ as both vertical maps in the lifting square, with horizontal maps $1_A$ and $1_B$. The <strong monomorphism> property gives $d:B\to A$ satisfying $dm=1_A$ and $md=1_B$. Thus $m$ is an <isomorphism>, proving that the <category> is <balanced>.

For the converse, take $e:X\to Y$ epic, $m:A\to B$ monic, and $mu=ve$. Form the <pullback in a category> of $m$ along $v$:
$$
P=Y\times_B A,\qquad p:P\to Y,\quad q:P\to A,\quad mq=vp.
$$
The equality $mu=ve$ gives $t:X\to P$ with $pt=e$ and $qt=u$. By preservation of <monomorphisms> under <pullback in a category>, $p$ is monic. It is also epic: if $hp=kp$, then $he=hpt=kpt=ke$, and cancellation of $e$ gives $h=k$. The <balanced category> hypothesis therefore makes $p$ an <isomorphism>.

Set $d=qp^{-1}$. Then $md=v$ and $de=qt=u$. Any other such lift agrees with $d$ after composing with the <monomorphism> $m$, so it is equal to $d$. Consequently **in a <category> with <pullbacks in a category>, being <balanced> is equivalent to every <monomorphism> being a <strong monomorphism>**. This is the <balanced categories with pullbacks have strong monomorphisms> principle; the proof does not assume pullback stability of epimorphisms.

The limit form of the <special adjoint functor theorem> is as follows. Let $\mathcal C$ be a <locally small category>, a <complete category>, and a <well-powered category>, and suppose it has a <small cogenerating family> $(Q_i)_{i\in I}$. Let $\mathcal D$ be a <locally small category>. Then a <functor> $G:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D$ has a <left adjoint> **if and only if it preserves all small <categorical limits>**. The dual exchanges completeness, well-poweredness and cogenerators for cocompleteness, well-copoweredness and generators, and characterizes <functors> with a <right adjoint>.

Here a <cogenerating set> distinguishes unequal parallel maps by postcomposition into one of the $Q_i$. A <well-powered category> has a set of <subobjects> of each fixed object. Completeness means existence of all small <categorical limits>. We use the <initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set>: a <complete category> that is <locally small> has an <initial object> exactly when it has a <weakly initial set>. A proof is given in Question 5(c). We also use the fact that when $G$ preserves small <categorical limits>, the <comma category> $(B\downarrow G)$ is complete, with its limits constructed in $\mathcal C$; local smallness is inherited from $\mathcal C$. Finally, having an <initial object> in $(B\downarrow G)$ for each $B$ is the <universal arrow from an object to a functor> characterization of a <left adjoint>.

Assume $G$ preserves small <categorical limits>, and fix $B\in\mathcal D$. We will construct a <weakly initial set> in $(B\downarrow G)$, rather than assume a solution set. Start with an arbitrary object $(C,x)$, where $x:B\to GC$. Call a <subobject> $m:S\hookrightarrow C$ supporting if $x=G(m)x_S$ for some $x_S:B\to GS$. There is at least one, namely $1_C$. Because $G$ preserves <pullbacks in a category>, it preserves <monomorphisms>: the self-pullback characterization of a monic map is preserved. Consequently each $x_S$ is unique.

There is a set of supporting <subobjects> by well-poweredness. Their <intersection of subobjects> exists by completeness; write it $m_0:C_0\hookrightarrow C$. Applying $G$ to this intersection <categorical limit> gives a unique $x_0:B\to GC_0$ lifting all the $x_S$, and hence $x=G(m_0)x_0$. Minimality says that any <subobject> $n:R\hookrightarrow C_0$ supporting $x_0$ is invertible: $m_0n$ supports $x$, so $m_0$ factors through $m_0n$, giving a right inverse to the monic $n$.

If $u,v:C_0\to Q_i$ satisfy $G(u)x_0=G(v)x_0$, their <equalizer> supports $x_0$, since $G$ preserves that <equalizer>. It is therefore an <isomorphism>, and $u=v$. We have obtained an <injective function>
$$
\mathcal C(C_0,Q_i)\longrightarrow\mathcal D(B,GQ_i),\qquad u\longmapsto G(u)x_0.
$$
Let $J_i$ be its image, a subset of the fixed <set> $S_i=\mathcal D(B,GQ_i)$. The <evaluation embedding into cogenerator products> is a <monomorphism>
$$
C_0\hookrightarrow P_J:=\prod_{i\in I}\prod_{s\in J_i}Q_i.
$$
Indeed each $s\in J_i$ corresponds to a unique map $C_0\to Q_i$, and the <cogenerating set> property makes the resulting family jointly monic. Notice that we use the realized subsets $J_i$, not all of $S_i$: there need not be a map $C_0\to Q_i$ available for an unused index.

There is a set of tuples $J=(J_i)$, since $I$ and every $S_i$ are sets. For each resulting $P_J$, choose representatives of its <subobjects>; these form a set by well-poweredness. For every representative $R$, include every map $y:B\to GR$, a set by local smallness of $\mathcal D$. All resulting pairs $(R,y)$ form a set. Our given $(C,x)$ receives a map from such a pair: transport $x_0$ along the <isomorphism> between $C_0$ and the chosen representative, then compose its inverse with $m_0$. Thus these pairs form a <weakly initial set> in $(B\downarrow G)$. This is the <cogenerator bound for comma-category solution sets>.

The <initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set> gives an <initial object> $(LB,\eta_B)$. For $h:B\to B'$, define $Lh$ by the unique equation
$$
G(Lh)\eta_B=\eta_{B'}h.
$$
Uniqueness proves the <functor> laws and gives the <natural bijections>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal C(LB,C)\cong\mathcal D(B,GC),\qquad f\longmapsto G(f)\eta_B.}
$$
Thus $L\dashv G$. Conversely, a <right adjoint> preserves small <categorical limits>: its adjunction identifies maps into the proposed limiting object with compatible families of maps into the diagram. This proves both directions of the <limit form of the special adjoint functor theorem>. Applying the argument to the <opposite categories> proves the dual statement. Choices of representatives and adjoint objects are understood in the usual ambient-universe convention for large <categories>.

The <comma category> $(X\downarrow G)$ has objects $(A,x)$ with $A\in\mathcal C$ and a <function> $x:X\to G(A)$. Its <morphisms> $(A,x)\to(B,y)$ are maps $f:A\to B$ in $\mathcal C$ satisfying $G(f)x=y$. For a singleton $1$, its map $1\to G(A)$ is just an element $a\in G(A)$. Thus $(1\downarrow G)$ is the covariant <category of elements> of $G$, with arrows $f:(A,a)\to(B,b)$ satisfying $G(f)(a)=b$.

Suppose $G$ is a <representable functor>, with a <natural isomorphism> $\theta:\mathcal C(R,-)\to G$. Put $r=\theta_R(1_R)$. For any $(A,a)$ in the <comma category> $(1\downarrow G)$, the representing <bijection> gives a unique $f:R\to A$ with $\theta_A(f)=a$. By <naturality>, $\theta_A(f)=G(f)(r)$, exactly the condition for $f:(R,r)\to(A,a)$. Hence $(R,r)$ is an <initial object>.

Conversely, if $(R,r)$ is an <initial object>, then
$$
\boxed{\theta_A:\mathcal C(R,A)\longrightarrow G(A),\qquad f\longmapsto G(f)(r)}
$$
is a <bijection> for each $A$: existence and uniqueness are precisely initiality applied to every $(A,a)$. For $h:A\to B$, $G(h)\theta_A(f)=G(hf)(r)=\theta_B(hf)$, proving <naturality>. Thus $r$ is a <universal element> and $\theta$ is a <representation of a functor>. **Being <representable> is equivalent to the singleton <comma category> having an <initial object>.**

A <weakly initial set> is a set of objects $(W_i)_{i\in I}$ such that every object receives at least one map from some $W_i$. An <initial object> is itself a singleton <weakly initial set>. We prove the converse without replacing uniqueness by weak initiality.

Let $\mathcal E$ be a <complete category> that is <locally small>. Form $W=\prod_i W_i$. For every object $A$, choose $W_i\to A$ and compose with the projection $W\to W_i$, so $W$ is weakly initial. Completeness supplies a <terminal object>, so the given <weakly initial set> cannot be empty.

The <endomorphisms> of $W$ form a <set>. Take a simultaneous <equalizer> $e:I\to W$ of all of them with $1_W$; equivalently, equalize the two maps from $W$ into $\prod_{s\in\mathcal E(W,W)}W$ with components $s$ and $1_W$. Thus $se=e$ for every endomorphism $s$. The object $I$ is weakly initial, since $I\xrightarrow eW\to A$ exists for every $A$. Weak initiality of $W$ gives a map $u:W\to I$. Applying the equalizing identity to $eu$ yields $eue=e$, so monicity of $e$ gives $ue=1_I$.

For any <endomorphism> $v:I\to I$, the map $evu$ is an <endomorphism> of $W$. Therefore $evue=e$. Since $ue=1_I$, this becomes $ev=e$, giving $v=1_I$. Now let $f,g:I\rightrightarrows A$ and form their <equalizer> $j:E\to I$. Weak initiality of $I$ gives $h:I\to E$. The composite $jh$ is an <endomorphism> of $I$, hence the identity. Consequently $f=fjh=gjh=g$.

There is at least one map from $I$ to every object, and the argument proves that there is at most one. **The object $I$ is an <initial object>.** This establishes the <initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set> using only small <products in a category> and <equalizers>.

If $G$ is a <representable functor>, it preserves small <categorical limits>. Part (b) makes $(1\downarrow G)$ have an <initial object>, which gives a <weakly initial set>.

A <monad> $\mathbb T=(T,\eta,\mu)$ consists of an <endofunctor> $T:\mathcal C\to\mathcal C$ and <natural transformations> $\eta:1_{\mathcal C}\Rightarrow T$ and $\mu:T^2\Rightarrow T$ satisfying
$$
\mu_A\eta_{TA}=1_{TA}=\mu_A T\eta_A,\qquad \mu_A\mu_{TA}=\mu_A T\mu_A.
$$
An <algebra for a monad> is $(A,a)$ with $a:TA\to A$ satisfying $a\eta_A=1_A$ and $a\mu_A=aT(a)$. A <morphism of algebras for a monad> $h:(A,a)\to(B,b)$ obeys $ha=bT(h)$. The <functor> laws show that identities and composites obey this equation, giving the <Eilenberg-Moore category> $\mathcal C^{\mathbb T}$.

For an <adjunction> $F\dashv G$, let $\eta$ be its <adjunction unit> and $\varepsilon$ its <adjunction counit>. The <monad induced by an adjunction> is
$$
\boxed{T=GF,\qquad \eta:1\Rightarrow GF,\qquad \mu_A=G(\varepsilon_{FA}).}
$$
The two unit laws are $G\varepsilon_{FA}\eta_{GFA}=1_{GFA}$ and $G(\varepsilon_{FA}F\eta_A)=1_{GFA}$, respectively the two <triangle identities for an adjunction>.

For associativity, <naturality> of the <adjunction counit> at $\varepsilon_{FA}:FGFA\to FA$ says
$$
\varepsilon_{FA}\,\varepsilon_{FGFA}=\varepsilon_{FA}\,FG(\varepsilon_{FA}).
$$
Apply $G$ to obtain $\mu_A\mu_{TA}=\mu_AT\mu_A$. The composites defining $\eta$ and $\mu$ are <natural transformations>, so all <monad> axioms have been checked, not only their underlying objectwise types.

Define the <forgetful functor> $G^{\mathbb T}(A,a)=A$, acting as the identity on underlying <morphisms>. Define the <free algebra functor> by
$$
F^{\mathbb T}(X)=(TX,\mu_X),\qquad F^{\mathbb T}(h)=T(h).
$$
The <monad> laws make $\mu_X$ an <algebra for a monad> structure, and <naturality> of $\mu$ makes $T(h)$ an <monad algebra morphism>.

For $(A,a)$, define the transpose maps
$$
\Phi(h)=h\eta_X,\qquad \Psi(k)=aT(k),\qquad
\Phi:\mathcal C^{\mathbb T}(F^{\mathbb T}X,(A,a))\longrightarrow\mathcal C(X,A).
$$
The map $\Psi(k)$ is an <monad algebra morphism>, because <naturality> of $\mu$ and the algebra associativity law give
$$
aT(k)\mu_X=a\mu_A T^2(k)=aT(a)T^2(k)=aT(aT(k)).
$$
For $k:X\to A$, <naturality> of $\eta$ gives $\Phi\Psi(k)=aT(k)\eta_X=a\eta_A k=k$. Conversely, if $h$ is an <monad algebra morphism>, then
$$
\Psi\Phi(h)=aT(h)T\eta_X=h\mu_X T\eta_X=h.
$$
Precomposing by a map into $X$ or postcomposing by an <monad algebra morphism> respects both formulas. Thus the <bijection> is natural and **$F^{\mathbb T}\dashv G^{\mathbb T}$**. This is the <free-forgetful Eilenberg-Moore adjunction>.

Its <adjunction unit> is the given $\eta_X$; its <adjunction counit> at $(A,a)$ is the <monad algebra morphism> $a:(TA,\mu_A)\to(A,a)$. Hence $G^{\mathbb T}F^{\mathbb T}=T$, and the induced multiplication is the underlying counit at $(TX,\mu_X)$, namely $\mu_X$. Therefore **this <adjunction> induces exactly the original <monad>, including its <unit and multiplication of a monad>**.

A <semi-additive category> has <commutative-monoid enrichment>: each <hom-set> is a <commutative monoid>, and <composition in a category> preserves addition and zero in each variable. In the finite-biproduct convention, which we use here, it also has finite <products in a category> and <coproducts in a category>. A <preadditive category> has <abelian groups> as its <hom-sets>, with composition additive in each variable; this definition alone does not require finite <products in a category> or a <zero object>.

Let $P=\prod_{i=1}^n A_i$ with projections $p_i$. Define $i_j:A_j\to P$ by $p_i i_j=1_{A_j}$ for $i=j$ and $p_i i_j=0$ otherwise. Bilinearity gives
$$
p_k\left(\sum_j i_jp_j\right)=p_k,
$$
so product uniqueness gives $\sum_j i_jp_j=1_P$. For a family $f_j:A_j\to X$, set $f=\sum_j f_jp_j$. Then $fi_j=f_j$. If $h:P\to X$ has the same restrictions, $h=h\sum_j i_jp_j=\sum_j f_jp_j=f$. Thus the <product in a category> is also the <coproduct in a category>, with its canonical injections. Conversely, the analogous construction from a finite <coproduct in a category> gives projections and the same identities, proving that it is a <product in a category>.

For the empty family, a <terminal object> $T$ has a singleton endomorphism monoid, so $1_T=0$. Every map $T\to X$ equals its composite with $1_T=0$, hence equals the zero map; this map exists by enrichment. Therefore $T$ is also an <initial object>, a <zero object>. The dual argument applies to an <initial object>. Consequently **finite <products in a category> and <coproducts in a category> coincide canonically as <biproducts>**, including the nullary case.

Fix an object $C$. For $x:C\to B$, use $rx:C\to A$ as its identity arrow. For $a:C\to A$, its source is $fa$ and target is $ga$. If $ga=fb$, define the composite of $a$ followed by $b$ by
$$
\boxed{b\circ a=a+b-rga,\qquad a^{-1}=rfa+rga-a.}
$$
These expressions use addition and subtraction in the <hom-set> of the <preadditive category>. The <reflexive pair> identities $fr=gr=1_B$ give
$$
f(b\circ a)=fa+fb-ga=fa,\qquad g(b\circ a)=ga+gb-ga=gb,
$$
so the composite has the required endpoints. Also $f(a^{-1})=ga$ and $g(a^{-1})=fa$.

The unit identities follow directly: $a\circ rfa=rfa+a-rfa=a$ and $rga\circ a=a+rga-rga=a$. If $ga=fb$ and $gb=fc$, either parenthesization of three arrows equals $a+b+c-rga-rgb$, so composition is associative. The inverse identities are
$$
a^{-1}\circ a=a+a^{-1}-rga=rfa,\qquad a\circ a^{-1}=a^{-1}+a-rfa=rga.
$$
Therefore these operations define a <groupoid> on the specified object and arrow <sets>. Precomposing by $k:C'\to C$ respects each expression, by additivity of composition, so the <groupoids> are natural in $C$.

**Every <reflexive pair> therefore has the required <groupoid> structure on generalized points.** This is the <reflexive-pair groupoid formula in a preadditive category>. If the composable-arrow <pullback in a category> $A\times_{g,B,f}A$ exists, its projections $p,q$ give the actual internal composition map $p+q-rgp$. The printed generalized-point definition does not assume that this pullback exists, and preadditivity alone would not ensure it.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Take any <morphism> $h:A\to B$ and let $c:B\to C$ be its <categorical cokernel>. Let $i:I\to B$ be the <categorical kernel> of $c$. Since $ch=0$, kernel universality gives $e:A\to I$ with $h=ie$. The map $i$ is a <monomorphism>. We must establish the <pseudo-epimorphism> property of $e$, rather than merely name $I$ an image.

Suppose $t:I\to Z$ satisfies $te=0$. Let $k:K\to I$ be its <categorical kernel>. Then $e=ka$ for some $a:A\to K$. The composite $ik$ is a <monomorphism>, so the hypothesis that every <monomorphism> is normal provides a map $q:B\to D$ of which $ik$ is a <categorical kernel>, up to its unique compatible isomorphism. Now $qh=qika=0$. The <categorical cokernel> property of $c$ gives $q=\bar q c$, and therefore $qi=\bar q ci=0$.

Since $ik$ is the <categorical kernel> of $q$, the map $i$ factors through it: $i=ik\ell$ for some $\ell:I\to K$. Cancel the <monomorphism> $i$ to get $k\ell=1_I$. As $k$ is also a <monomorphism>, $\ell k=1_K$, so $k$ is an <isomorphism>. Finally $t=tk\ell=0$, proving that $e$ is a <pseudo-epimorphism>.

Thus
$$
\boxed{h=ie,\qquad i=\ker(\operatorname{coker}h),\qquad e\text{ pseudo-epic},\quad i\text{ monic}.}
$$
The <pseudo-epimorphism factorization through the kernel of a cokernel> proof uses a <pointed category>, existence of <categorical kernels> and <categorical cokernels>, and normality of every monic map. It does not silently replace pseudo-epic cancellation against zero by the stronger <epimorphism> condition.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In an <abelian category>, for $f:A\to B$ set $\operatorname{coim}f=\operatorname{coker}(\ker f)$ and $\operatorname{im}f=\ker(\operatorname{coker}f)$. The canonical map from coimage to image is an <isomorphism>. Thus the <image factorization in an abelian category> is
$$
\boxed{A\xrightarrow{e}\operatorname{im}f\xrightarrow{m}B,\qquad f=me,\quad e\text{ epic},\quad m\text{ monic},}
$$
unique up to a unique compatible <isomorphism>.

The <short five lemma> states that in a commuting diagram of <short exact sequences> $0\to L\to M\to R\to0$ and $0\to L'\to M'\to R'\to0$ in an <abelian category>, if the vertical maps $L\to L'$ and $R\to R'$ are <isomorphisms>, then so is $M\to M'$.

For the <five lemma>, consider commuting exact rows
$$
A_1\xrightarrow{d_1}A_2\xrightarrow{d_2}A_3\xrightarrow{d_3}A_4\xrightarrow{d_4}A_5,
\qquad
B_1\xrightarrow{d'_1}B_2\xrightarrow{d'_2}B_3\xrightarrow{d'_3}B_4\xrightarrow{d'_4}B_5,
$$
with vertical maps $v_i:A_i\to B_i$. We prove the usual stronger version: $v_1$ epic, $v_2$ and $v_4$ invertible, and $v_5$ monic imply that $v_3$ is invertible. In particular the conclusion holds if all four outer vertical maps are <isomorphisms>.

Put $L=\operatorname{im}d_2$ and $R=\operatorname{im}d_3$, and define $L',R'$ similarly. Exactness gives <short exact sequences>
$$
0\longrightarrow L\longrightarrow A_3\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
0\longrightarrow L'\longrightarrow B_3\longrightarrow R'\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <functoriality of abelian image factorization> supplies their vertical outer maps. We show that both are <isomorphisms>.

First, $L\cong\operatorname{coker}d_1$ and $L'\cong\operatorname{coker}d'_1$: exactness at $A_2$ identifies $\operatorname{im}d_1$ with $\ker d_2$, and the image-coimage <isomorphism> for $d_2$ gives the claimed cokernel. Write $q:A_2\to L$ and $q':B_2\to L'$ for these <categorical cokernels>. To construct an inverse to the induced map $\ell:L\to L'$, observe
$$
(qv_2^{-1}d'_1)v_1=qv_2^{-1}v_2d_1=0.
$$
Cancellation of the <epimorphism> $v_1$ gives $qv_2^{-1}d'_1=0$, so $qv_2^{-1}$ factors uniquely as $\bar\ell q'$ with $\bar\ell:L'\to L$. From $\ell q=q'v_2$, composing with the epic $q,q'$ gives $\bar\ell\ell=1_L$ and $\ell\bar\ell=1_{L'}$.

Second, $R\cong\ker d_4$ and $R'\cong\ker d'_4$ by exactness at the fourth objects. Write their inclusions as $j:R\to A_4$ and $j':R'\to B_4$. The equality $d'_4v_4=v_5d_4$ gives the induced map $\rho:R\to R'$. Since
$$
v_5d_4v_4^{-1}j'=d'_4j'=0
$$
and $v_5$ is a <monomorphism>, $d_4v_4^{-1}j'=0$. Kernel universality gives $\bar\rho:R'\to R$ with $j\bar\rho=v_4^{-1}j'$. Cancelling the monic $j,j'$ proves $\bar\rho\rho=1_R$ and $\rho\bar\rho=1_{R'}$.

Apply the <short five lemma> to the two <short exact sequences>: their outer maps $\ell,\rho$ are invertible, so **$v_3$ is an <isomorphism>**. This <five lemma via image factorization> argument uses only universal properties and therefore works in any <abelian category>, without treating its objects as literal sets of elements.
