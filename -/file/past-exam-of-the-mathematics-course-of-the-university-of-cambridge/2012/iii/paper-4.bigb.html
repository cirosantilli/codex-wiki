= Solution
{parent=a}

Choose a separation $\operatorname{Spec}R=U\sqcup V$ into two nonempty <clopen sets>. Both are closed in the <Zariski topology>, so write $U=V(I)$ and $V=V(J)$ for <ideals> $I,J$. Their disjointness gives $V(I+J)=\varnothing$. A proper <ideal> lies in a <maximal ideal>, so this forces $I+J=R$.

Their union is all of the <spectrum of a commutative ring>, and $V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ)$. Thus every <prime ideal> contains $IJ$, so
$$
IJ\subseteq\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R}\mathfrak p
=\sqrt{(0)}=0.
$$
The last equality uses that $R$ is a <reduced ring>. For <comaximal ideals>, $I\cap J=IJ$: if $i+j=1$ and $r\in I\cap J$, then $r=ri+rj\in IJ$. Therefore the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{R\cong R/I\times R/J}.
$$
The isomorphism sends $r$ to its two residue classes. Its kernel is $I\cap J=0$; for surjectivity, $a\bmod I$ and $b\bmod J$ are obtained from $aj+bi$, where $i\in I$, $j\in J$ and $i+j=1$. The two factors are nonzero because $U,V$ are nonempty, so $I,J$ are proper.

This is a <disconnected reduced spectrum product decomposition>. The elements $i,j$ also satisfy $ij=0$, $i^2=i$, $j^2=j$, exhibiting the <ring product decomposition by an idempotent>. No Noetherian hypothesis is required.

Put $S=R^G$, the <ring of invariants>. It is an <integral domain> because it is a <subring> of the domain $R$. Embed $\operatorname{Frac}S$ in $\operatorname{Frac}R$ in the evident way.

Suppose $q\in\operatorname{Frac}S$ is an <integral element> over $S$. Its monic equation has coefficients in $S\subseteq R$, so $q$ is integral over $R$. Since $R$ is a <normal domain>, $q\in R$.

Write $q=a/b$ with $a,b\in S$ and $b\ne0$. Each <ring automorphism> in $G$ extends to the <fraction field> by acting on numerator and denominator, and fixes this fraction. Hence $q\in R^G=S$. We have proved that $S$ is integrally closed in its own <fraction field>:
$$
\boxed{R^G\text{ is a normal domain}}.
$$
This <normality of a ring of invariants> argument actually works for any group; finiteness is not needed for this conclusion. For finite $G$, there is additionally an <integral extension> $R^G\subseteq R$, since for each $r\in R$ the <orbit polynomial under a finite automorphism group>
$$
\prod_{g\in G}(T-g(r))
$$
is monic, has coefficients fixed by $G$ and vanishes at $r$. Neither argument divides by $|G|$, so it is valid when the characteristic divides the group order. Here normality means integral closedness of a domain.

Use $y$ on the target and $x$ on the source, so the map of coordinate <rings> sends $y$ to $x^4$. A <closed point> of the real affine line corresponds to a <maximal ideal> generated either by $y-a$, $a\in\mathbb R$, or by a monic irreducible quadratic $q(y)$. The <scheme-theoretic fibre> over that point has coordinate algebra
$$
B_p=\mathbb R[x]\otimes_{\mathbb R[y]}\kappa(p)
=\mathbb R[x]/(q_p(x^4)).
$$
Here $\kappa(p)$ is the <residue field>, and $q_p=y-a$ in the real-point case. The fibre is a scheme, so its nilpotents must be retained.

For $a>0$, put $r=a^{1/4}$. The factors $x-r$, $x+r$, $x^2+r^2$ are pairwise coprime, with the last one irreducible over $\mathbb R$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> identifies $B_p$ with $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb C$, giving three <irreducible components>.

For $a<0$, put $r=(-a)^{1/4}$. The exact factorization is
$$
x^4+r^4=(x^2+\sqrt2rx+r^2)(x^2-\sqrt2rx+r^2).
$$
Each quadratic has discriminant $-2r^2<0$, and they are distinct and coprime. Thus $B_p\cong\mathbb C\times\mathbb C$, giving two <irreducible components>.

For $a=0$, $B_p=\mathbb R[x]/(x^4)$. It has one <prime ideal>, generated by the class of $x$, so one <irreducible component>. It is not the reduced point $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R$: its class of $x$ is a nonzero <nilpotent element> with nilpotency index four.

Finally write the irreducible quadratic as $q_p(y)=(y-z)(y-\overline z)$, with $z\notin\mathbb R$. Over $\mathbb C$, $q_p(x^4)$ has four roots above $z$ and four above $\overline z$. They are all simple, because $z\ne0$, and the two sets are disjoint. None is real. Conjugation pairs them into four distinct irreducible real quadratics. Hence $B_p\cong\mathbb C^4$ and this fibre has four <irreducible components>.

The <real fourth-power fibre classification> is:

|| Target closed-point type
|| Real coordinate algebra of the fibre
|| <Irreducible components>

| $y-a$, $a>0$
| $\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb C$
| $3$

| $y-a$, $a<0$
| $\mathbb C^2$
| $2$

| $y$
| $\mathbb R[x]/(x^4)$
| $1$

| Irreducible quadratic
| $\mathbb C^4$
| $4$

All fibres in each row are isomorphic as affine schemes over $\mathbb R$. The rows are distinct: their numbers of <irreducible components> already distinguish them, and the zero fibre is additionally nonreduced. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{exactly four real-scheme isomorphism classes}}.
$$
These are ordinary scheme components, not geometric components counted after complexifying the base. Counting only real roots would miss both the complex-residue factors and the nonreal <closed points>.

= Solution

Let $A=\mathbb C[x,y,z]$. This <polynomial ring> is a <unique factorization domain> of <Krull dimension> three. For a <height-one prime in a unique factorization domain> $\mathfrak p$, choose a nonzero element of it and an irreducible factor $q$ belonging to $\mathfrak p$. In a <unique factorization domain>, $q$ is prime, so $(q)$ is a nonzero <prime ideal> contained in $\mathfrak p$. Strict containment would give a chain $(0)\subsetneq(q)\subsetneq\mathfrak p$ of length two. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=1\Longrightarrow\mathfrak p=(q)}.
$$
If $\mathfrak p$ has height three, it is maximal: any strictly larger proper prime would extend a length-three chain and contradict $\dim A=3$. The <weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz> over the <algebraically closed field> $\mathbb C$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=3\Longrightarrow\mathfrak p=(x-a,y-b,z-c)}
$$
for some $a,b,c\in\mathbb C$. This proves both requested generator assertions.

For the <monomial curve with exponents three, four and five>, put
$$
g_1=y^2-xz,\qquad g_2=yz-x^3,\qquad g_3=z^2-x^2y,\qquad J=(g_1,g_2,g_3).
$$
All three map to zero under the given parametrization, so $J\subseteq I=\ker\varphi$. Reduce any <monomial> using $y^2\mapsto xz$, $yz\mapsto x^3$ and $z^2\mapsto x^2y$. Each reduction decreases the sum of the exponents of $y,z$, so it terminates. Every polynomial has, modulo $J$, the form
$$
A_0(x)+A_1(x)y+A_2(x)z.
$$
Its image is $A_0(t^3)+t^4A_1(t^3)+t^5A_2(t^3)$. These three terms occupy different exponent classes modulo three, so they cannot cancel. A zero image forces each $A_i=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I=(y^2-xz,\ yz-x^3,\ z^2-x^2y)}.
$$
The quotient is the domain $\mathbb C[t^3,t^4,t^5]$, hence $I$ is a <prime ideal>. It is finite free over $\mathbb C[t^3]$, with <basis of a module> $1,t^4,t^5$, by the same normal-form argument. Its dimension is therefore one, using that integral extensions preserve <Krull dimension>. The <polynomial-ring height and dimension formula> gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ht}I=3-\dim(A/I)=2}.
$$
For the generator obstruction, let $\mathfrak m=(x,y,z)$. Since $I\subseteq\mathfrak m^2$, we have $\mathfrak mI\subseteq\mathfrak m^3$. The degree-two initial forms of $g_1,g_2,g_3$ are
$$
y^2-xz,\qquad yz,\qquad z^2.
$$
They are <linearly independent> over $\mathbb C$ in $\mathfrak m^2/\mathfrak m^3$. Thus no nonzero constant linear combination of the $g_i$ belongs to $\mathfrak mI$, and their classes are independent in $I/\mathfrak mI$. Since the three generators span that quotient,
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb C}I/\mathfrak mI=3}.
$$
Two global generators would span it by two vectors, which is impossible. This is the <initial-form lower bound for ideal generators>; it shows why height two does not force two generators, even though height one and height three behave as above.

= Solution

We use <Zorn's lemma>, and no <basis> or other existence theorem from linear algebra. A subset $B\subseteq V$ is <linearly independent> if every finite relation $\sum_{b\in F}\lambda_b b=0$ forces every coefficient to be zero.

Order the <linearly independent> subsets by inclusion. This partially ordered set is nonempty, because the empty subset is independent. An empty chain has the empty subset as an upper bound. For any nonempty chain, its union is independent: every finite subset of that union lies in one member of the chain. Indeed choose one chain member containing each of its finitely many vectors, then take the largest among that finite collection of comparable members. Every finite relation in the union is therefore a relation in an independent chain member. The union is an upper bound.

<Zorn's lemma> gives a maximal independent subset $B$. If $v$ were outside its span, then $B\cup\{v\}$ would still be independent. In a finite relation
$$
\lambda v+\sum_{b\in F}\lambda_b b=0,
$$
a nonzero $\lambda$ could be inverted in the <field> $k$, placing $v$ in the span of $B$. If $\lambda=0$, independence of $B$ forces the remaining coefficients to be zero. This contradicts maximality, so $B$ spans $V$.

Define the <free module> on $B$ as the <direct sum> $k^{(B)}=\bigoplus_{b\in B}k$: its elements are coefficient families with finite support. The map
$$
\Phi:k^{(B)}\longrightarrow V,\qquad(\lambda_b)\longmapsto\sum_b\lambda_b b
$$
is a module homomorphism. Spanning proves surjectivity; independence proves injectivity. Hence the <vector-space freeness from maximal independence> gives
$$
\boxed{V\cong\bigoplus_{b\in B}k}.
$$
Finite support is essential; this is a <direct sum>, not an unrestricted product. The zero <vector space> uses $B=\varnothing$ and is free of rank zero. The proof for arbitrary <vector spaces> explicitly uses the <axiom of choice> through <Zorn's lemma>.

A generic linear projection over an infinite <field> is not enough for the stated arbitrary <field> $k$. Instead use <Noether normalization by weighted substitutions> to make the defining polynomial monic in one coordinate.

For $n>1$, choose an integer $M$ at least every exponent occurring in a nonzero <monomial> of $f$, put $B=M+1$, and use the triangular polynomial change of variables
$$
x_i=y_i+y_n^{B^i}\quad(1\leq i<n),\qquad x_n=y_n.
$$
Here $M\geq1$ because the irreducible polynomial is nonconstant. This is an automorphism, with inverse $y_i=x_i-x_n^{B^i}$, $y_n=x_n$. A <monomial> $x_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$ contributes a highest $y_n$ power
$$
w(\alpha)=\alpha_n+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_iB^i.
$$
These weights are all distinct: the exponents are base-$B$ digits bounded by $M$. All other terms in the expansion have smaller $y_n$ degree than that <monomial>'s weight. Therefore the highest-degree $y_n$ term in the transformed $f$ comes from exactly one original <monomial> and has a nonzero coefficient in $k$, independent of the other $y_i$. No assumption about the cardinality or characteristic of $k$ enters this argument.

Multiply by the inverse of that coefficient to obtain a <monic polynomial> $F\in A[y_n]$ of some degree $D>0$, where $A=k[y_1,\ldots,y_{n-1}]$. The same construction for $n=1$ simply rescales $f$ to be monic over $A=k$.

The <monic polynomial quotient is finite free> because division by $F$ gives a unique representative of degree less than $D$. Thus
$$
\boxed{k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/(f)\cong A[y_n]/(F)
\cong A\oplus Ay_n\oplus\cdots\oplus Ay_n^{D-1}}
$$
as $A$-modules. Uniqueness follows because any nonzero multiple of a monic $F$ has $y_n$ degree at least $D$. In particular the map $A\to A[y_n]/(F)$ is injective.

The induced morphism
$$
\boxed{X\longrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A=\mathbb A_k^{n-1}}
$$
is finite, since its coordinate algebra is a finite module, and flat, since tensoring with a <finite free module> is a finite <direct sum> of copies of the original module and preserves exactness. This is a <finite flat Noether normalization of an affine hypersurface>, with the explicit projection coordinates $y_i=x_i-x_n^{B^i}$. The construction actually applies to any nonconstant polynomial, not only an irreducible one.

The characteristic of $K$ is either a prime $p$ or zero. In characteristic $p$, it is a <finitely generated algebra> over $\mathbb F_p$. The course's <Zariski lemma> says that a <field> finitely generated as an algebra over a <field> is a finite algebraic extension of that <field>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{char}K=p\Longrightarrow |K|=p^d\text{ for some finite }d}.
$$
It remains to exclude characteristic zero. Write $K=\mathbb Z[a_1,\ldots,a_m]$. Because $K$ is then a <field> containing $\mathbb Q$, also $K=\mathbb Q[a_1,\ldots,a_m]$. <Zariski lemma> makes $K/\mathbb Q$ finite algebraic. Each $a_i$ has a monic equation with rational coefficients. Choose a positive integer $N$ clearing all their coefficient denominators. Each $a_i$ is then integral over $A=\mathbb Z[1/N]$, so $A[a_1,\ldots,a_m]$ is integral over $A$. This algebra equals $K$: it contains the given integer algebra $K$, and $1/N$ already lies in the <field> $K$.

An <integral field extension forces the base domain to be a field>. To see it here, for a nonzero $a\in A$ its inverse in $K$ satisfies
$$
(a^{-1})^r+c_{r-1}(a^{-1})^{r-1}+\cdots+c_0=0,\qquad c_i\in A.
$$
Multiplying by $a^{r-1}$ gives
$$
a^{-1}=-(c_{r-1}+c_{r-2}a+\cdots+c_0a^{r-1})\in A.
$$
Thus $A$ would be a <field>. But choose a prime $\ell\nmid N$. Reduction modulo $\ell$ is a well-defined map $\mathbb Z[1/N]\to\mathbb F_\ell$, so the nonzero element $\ell$ is not invertible in $A$. This contradiction rules out characteristic zero. Therefore the <finite-field theorem for finitely generated integer algebras> yields
$$
\boxed{K\text{ is a finite field}}.
$$
This uses <Zariski lemma> and elementary integrality, not a claim that a <field> merely finitely generated as a <field extension> must be algebraic.

A <nilpotent element> belongs to every <prime ideal> and therefore to every <maximal ideal>, so
$$
\sqrt{(0)}\subseteq\operatorname{Jac}(R).
$$
For the reverse containment, let $r$ be nonnilpotent. The <localization of a ring> $S=R[1/r]$ is nonzero: if $1=0$ there, some power of $r$ would annihilate $1$ in $R$. This is still a <finite-type integer algebra>, because it can be presented as $R[T]/(rT-1)$.

Choose a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak n$ of $S$. Its <residue field> $L=S/\mathfrak n$ is a <finite-type integer algebra>, hence finite by part (a). Consider the image $F$ of $R$ in $L$. It is a finite <integral domain> containing $1$, so it is a <field>: multiplication by a nonzero element is injective on the finite set $F$, hence surjective, and therefore has an inverse in $F$.

The kernel $\mathfrak m$ of $R\to F$ is consequently maximal. The image of $r$ is nonzero, since $r$ became a unit in $S$ and remains a unit in its nonzero <residue field>. Thus $r\notin\mathfrak m$. Every nonnilpotent element can therefore be avoided by a <maximal ideal>, giving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Jac}(R)=\sqrt{(0)}}.
$$
This <radical equality for finitely generated integer algebras> uses part (a) to ensure that the contraction of the localized <maximal ideal> is maximal. Such contraction is not generally maximal for arbitrary <localization of a ring>; the finite-field image is the decisive additional step. The zero <ring> is immediate, with the intersection of its empty set of <maximal ideals> understood as the whole <ring>. No general theorem that integer algebras are <Jacobson rings> is being quoted.
