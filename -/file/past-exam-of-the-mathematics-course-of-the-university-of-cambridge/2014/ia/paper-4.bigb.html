= Solution
{parent=1E}

The <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
203=147+56,\quad147=2\cdot56+35,\quad56=35+21,\quad35=21+14,\quad21=14+7,\quad14=2\cdot7.
$$
Thus the <greatest common divisor> is **$d=7$**. The <extended Euclidean algorithm> reverses these divisions:
$$
7=21-14=2\cdot21-35=2\cdot56-3\cdot35=8\cdot56-3\cdot147=8\cdot203-11\cdot147.
$$
This supplies the <Bezout identity> with $x=8$, $y=-11$.

To obtain <all solutions of a linear Diophantine equation>, subtract this particular solution from any other and divide by $7$. The result is $29(x-8)+21(y+11)=0$. Since $29$ and $21$ are <coprime integers>, $21$ divides $x-8$, giving
$$
\boxed{x=8+21t,\qquad y=-11-29t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Every displayed pair satisfies the original <linear Diophantine equation>, so the parametrization is exhaustive.

For the <modular arithmetic> calculation, $21\cdot18=378\equiv1\pmod{29}$, so $18$ is the <modular inverse> of $21$. Multiplication gives $n\equiv25\cdot18\equiv15\pmod{29}$. The admissible <integers> are $15+29t$ for $0\leq t\leq68$, ending at $1987$; the next is $2016$. **There are $\boxed{69}$ such integers.**

= Solution
{parent=2E}

For <integers> $0\leq k\leq n$, define the <binomial coefficient> by
$$
\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!},\qquad0!=1.
$$
Here the <factorial> $n!$ is the product of the positive <integers> up to $n$. This also counts the $k$-element <subsets> of an $n$-element <set>: an ordered choice has $n!/(n-k)!$ possibilities, and each <subset> is ordered in $k!$ ways.

Directly from the <factorial> definition, when $0\leq k<n$,
$$
\binom nk+\binom n{k+1}
=\frac{n!(k+1)+n!(n-k)}{(k+1)!(n-k)!}
=\frac{(n+1)!}{(k+1)!(n-k)!}
=\boxed{\binom{n+1}{k+1}}.
$$
This is <Pascal's identity>.

The required <hockey-stick identity> follows by <mathematical induction> on $m$, with $n\geq0$ fixed. For $m=0$, both sides are $1$. If it holds at $m$, adding the next <binomial coefficient> and using <Pascal's identity> gives
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{m+1}\binom{n+k}k
=\binom{n+m+1}m+\binom{n+m+1}{m+1}
=\binom{n+m+2}{m+1}.
$$
Therefore **for every $n,m\geq0$**,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^m\binom{n+k}k=\binom{n+m+1}m.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=3C}

In classical <charged particle in a uniform magnetic field> motion, the <Lorentz force> and <Newton's second law> give
$$
m\dot{\mathbf v}=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B.
$$
Assume $\mathbf B\ne0$ and choose an orthonormal coordinate system with $\mathbf B=B\mathbf e_z$, $B=|\mathbf B|$. Write $\Omega=qB/m$, retaining the sign of the <electric charge>. The <velocity> equations are
$$
\dot v_x=\Omega v_y,\qquad\dot v_y=-\Omega v_x,\qquad\dot v_z=0.
$$
Thus the parallel <velocity> is constant, and the perpendicular <velocity> rotates at constant <angular frequency>. Choosing the time origin and perpendicular axes conveniently gives
$$
v_x=v_\perp\cos\Omega t,\qquad v_y=-v_\perp\sin\Omega t,\qquad v_z=v_\parallel.
$$
Integrating the <velocity>, one obtains
$$
x=x_c+\frac{v_\perp}{\Omega}\sin\Omega t,\qquad y=y_c+\frac{v_\perp}{\Omega}\cos\Omega t,\qquad z=z_0+v_\parallel t.
$$
The first two coordinates describe a <circle> of radius $mv_\perp/(|q|B)$, while the third advances uniformly. This is <helical motion in a uniform magnetic field>: the <helix> has **axis parallel to $\mathbf B$** through the <guiding centre>. Its <cyclotron frequency> is
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m}},
$$
and its signed <angular velocity> vector about that axis is $-q\mathbf B/m$. The minus sign follows from the ordering $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ in the <Lorentz force>. The <helix> degenerates to a <circle> when $v_\parallel=0$ and to a straight line when $v_\perp=0$. If $q=0$ or $B=0$, there is only straight-line motion. In a relativistic extension with fixed <Lorentz factor> $\gamma$, the frequency becomes $|q|B/(\gamma m)$; the classical result above uses $m\mathbf v$ as <momentum>.

= Solution

A <four-vector> is a collection of four components $A^\mu=(A^0,\mathbf A)$ that transforms by the same <Lorentz transformation> as $(ct,\mathbf x)$. Use the <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$. The <Lorentzian inner product> is
$$
A\cdot B=-A^0B^0+A^1B^1+A^2B^2+A^3B^3.
$$
A nonzero <four-vector> is a <timelike vector>, <null vector> or <spacelike vector> according as $A\cdot A$ is negative, zero or positive. Reversing the <metric signature> reverses the signs used to name these three classes, without changing their geometric meaning.

For a frame moving at <speed> $v$ along the positive $x$ axis, write $\beta=v/c$ and use the <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$. The component <Lorentz transformation> is
$$
\boxed{A'^0=\gamma(A^0-\beta A^1),\quad A'^1=\gamma(A^1-\beta A^0),\quad A'^2=A^2,\quad A'^3=A^3.}
$$
Expanding the first two squares gives
$$
-(A'^0)^2+(A'^1)^2=\gamma^2(1-\beta^2)\bigl(-(A^0)^2+(A^1)^2\bigr)=-(A^0)^2+(A^1)^2.
$$
The other two components are unchanged, so $A'\cdot A'=A\cdot A$. Consequently **a <timelike vector> remains timelike under a <Lorentz transformation>**.

For a nonzero <null vector>, $|\mathbf A|=|A^0|$ and $A^0\ne0$. Set $a=A^0$ and $\hat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf A/a$. Then $|\hat{\mathbf n}|=1$ and $A=a(1,\hat{\mathbf n})$. If the zero <four-vector> is included among <null vectors>, take $a=0$ and any <unit vector>.

For two future-pointing <null vectors>, write $A=a(1,\hat{\mathbf n})$ and $B=b(1,\hat{\mathbf m})$ with $a,b>0$. The <sum of future-pointing null vectors> satisfies
$$
(A+B)\cdot(A+B)=-2ab\bigl(1-\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m}\bigr)\leq0,
$$
because the ordinary <inner product> of two <unit vectors> is at most one. **The sum is null if their spatial directions coincide, and timelike otherwise.** Its positive time component makes the sum nonzero and future-pointing.

= Solution
{parent=5E}

A real <sequence> $(x_n)$ has <limit of a sequence> $x$ when
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N\ \forall n\geq N:\quad |x_n-x|<\varepsilon.
$$
A <series> $\sum_{n\geq1}x_n$ is a <convergent series> with sum $s$ when its <partial sums> $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nx_n$ have <limit of a sequence> $s$.

For the first <comparison test for series>, either of the two allowed bounds implies $0<x_n\leq a_n+b_n$. Hence
$$
0<\sum_{n=1}^Nx_n\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n<\infty.
$$
The <partial sums> form a <monotone bounded sequence>, so they converge. **The <series> $\sum x_n$ is convergent.**

For the exponent $2$, use $1/n^2\leq1/[n(n-1)]=1/(n-1)-1/n$ for $n\geq2$. The resulting <telescoping series> bounds every <partial sum> by $2$, proving that the <series> converges by the <monotone bounded sequence> property. To prove divergence of the <harmonic series>, group the terms with $2^j<n\leq2^{j+1}$: their sum is at least $2^j/2^{j+1}=1/2$. Thus its <partial sums> are unbounded. If $\alpha\leq1$, then $n^{-\alpha}\geq n^{-1}$, so the <comparison test for series> proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-2}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-\alpha}=\infty\quad(\alpha\leq1).}
$$

For the weighted square assumption, the finite-dimensional <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives, for every $N$,
$$
\sum_{n=1}^Nx_n=\sum_{n=1}^N(nx_n)\frac1n
\leq\left(\sum_{n=1}^Nn^2x_n^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{n^2}\right)^{1/2}
\leq\left(\sum_{n\geq1}n^2x_n^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.
$$
Again the positive <partial sums> form a <monotone bounded sequence>. This is the <weighted square roots of a summable sequence> argument, with $a_n=n^2x_n^2$. **The weighted square condition implies $\sum x_n<\infty$.**

**The converse is false.** Take $x_n=n^{-3/2}$. Each block $2^j\leq n<2^{j+1}$ contributes at most $2^j2^{-3j/2}=2^{-j/2}$, and the resulting <geometric series> converges. But $n^2x_n^2=1/n$, whose <harmonic series> diverges. This proves the counterexample without assuming the full <P-series> criterion.

The <Fermat-Euler theorem> states that, for an <integer> $N\geq2$ and any <integer> $a$ <coprime> to $N$,
$$
\boxed{a^{\varphi(N)}\equiv1\pmod N,}
$$
where the <Euler totient function> $\varphi(N)$ counts the <residue classes> <coprime> to $N$.

Let $r_1,\ldots,r_{\varphi(N)}$ be representatives of these <residue classes>. Multiplication by $a$ gives a <permutation> of them: each $ar_i$ remains <coprime> to $N$, and $ar_i\equiv ar_j$ implies $r_i\equiv r_j$ by cancellation using the <modular inverse> of $a$. Multiplying all the resulting <modular congruences> yields
$$
a^{\varphi(N)}r_1\cdots r_{\varphi(N)}\equiv r_1\cdots r_{\varphi(N)}\pmod N.
$$
The product is itself <coprime> to $N$, so it has a <modular inverse> and can be canceled. This proves the <Fermat-Euler theorem>. For $N=1$, the congruence is trivial; no separate argument is needed.

For the <odd prime> $p$, the <Fermat-Euler theorem> gives $x^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. Therefore the <residue class> $z=x^{(p-1)/2}$ obeys $(z-1)(z+1)\equiv0\pmod p$. Since a <prime number> dividing a product divides one of its factors,
$$
\boxed{x^{(p-1)/2}\equiv1\text{ or }-1\pmod p.}
$$
If $x$ is a <quadratic residue>, choose $y$ with $y^2\equiv x\pmod p$. Then $y$ is nonzero modulo $p$, and <Fermat's little theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{x^{(p-1)/2}\equiv y^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p.}
$$
This proves the <quadratic residue> direction of <Euler's criterion>.

{parent=6e}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Suppose $x$ is a <quadratic nonresidue>. On the nonzero <residue classes> modulo $p$, define the <involution>
$$
T(a)=xa^{-1}.
$$
It is an <involution> because $T(T(a))=a$. A fixed point would satisfy $a^2=x$, which is excluded by the <quadratic nonresidue> hypothesis. Thus it partitions the $p-1$ classes into $(p-1)/2$ disjoint pairs $\{a,xa^{-1}\}$, each with product $x$. Consequently
$$
\prod_{a=1}^{p-1}a\equiv x^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p.
$$
Now evaluate the same product by pairing every class with its own <modular inverse>. The only classes equal to their <modular inverses> solve $(a-1)(a+1)=0$ and hence are $1$ and $-1$. All other pairs have product one. The complete product is therefore $-1$, so
$$
\boxed{x^{(p-1)/2}\equiv-1\pmod p.}
$$
Together with the preceding part, this is <Euler's criterion>; the pairing argument establishes the converse without assuming it.

<Fermat's little theorem> reduces the exponent modulo $22$ because $23$ is a <prime number> and $5$ is <coprime> to it. Compute $5^2\equiv3\pmod{22}$, then $5^4\equiv9\pmod{22}$ and $5^5\equiv45\equiv1\pmod{22}$. Thus $5^5=22k+1$ for an <integer> $k$, and
$$
\boxed{5^{5^5}=5^{22k+1}\equiv(5^{22})^k5\equiv5\pmod{23}.}
$$
The exponent is $5^5=3125$, rather than an iterated left-associated power.

A <set> is a <countable set> if it admits an <injective function> into the <natural numbers>; this includes <finite sets> and <countably infinite sets>. A <countably infinite set> can be listed by a <bijection> from $\mathbb N$.

Let $Z$ be the <set> of infinite <binary sequences>. Suppose it were countable and list all its members as $s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\ldots$, writing $s^{(j)}_n$ for the $n$th entry of row $j$. A finite listing could be repeated to make such a list as well. Define the <binary sequence> $y$ by $y_n=1-s^{(n)}_n$. It differs from the $n$th row in coordinate $n$, for every $n$. Thus it belongs to $Z$ but is missing from the list, a contradiction. **The <set> $Z$ is uncountable**, by <Cantor's diagonal argument>.

= ii
{parent=7e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use $\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$. If a <bijection> $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ maps $S$ onto the even <natural numbers>, its restriction is a <bijection> from $S$ onto an infinite <set>. Moreover, its restriction to the <complement> of $S$ maps onto the odd <natural numbers>. Both <sets> are therefore infinite.

Conversely, suppose that $S$ and its <complement> are infinite. Applying the <increasing enumeration of an infinite subset of natural numbers> to each gives
$$
S=\{s_1<s_2<\cdots\},\qquad\mathbb N\setminus S=\{t_1<t_2<\cdots\}.
$$
For completeness, successively choose the least unused member. The process never stops because the <set> is infinite, and it lists every member: an omitted member would have infinitely many selected <natural numbers> below it, which is impossible. Define
$$
\boxed{f(s_j)=2j,\qquad f(t_j)=2j-1.}
$$
This is <injective> on each list, their images are disjoint, and together their images cover $\mathbb N$. Thus it is a <bijection> and maps $S$ onto $2\mathbb N$. **Both infinitude conditions are necessary and sufficient.** If a convention includes zero in $\mathbb N$, index each list from zero and use $2j$, $2j+1$ instead.

= Solution

Use <uncountability by interleaving prescribed binary coordinates>. For each arbitrary <binary sequence> $b=(b_k)_{k\geq1}$, define another <binary sequence> by
$$
x_{3k-2}=0,\qquad x_{3k-1}=a_{3k-1},\qquad x_{3k}=b_k\quad(k\geq1).
$$
There are infinitely many zero coordinates, namely $3k-2$, so $x\in X$. There are also infinitely many coordinates agreeing with the prescribed <binary expansion>, namely $3k-1$, so $x\in Y$. The coordinates $3k$ recover $b$, making this map an <injection> of the <uncountable set> of all <binary sequences> into $Y$. If $Y$ were a <countable set>, composing with an <injection> into $\mathbb N$ would make all <binary sequences> countable, contrary to part (i). **Therefore $\boxed{Y\text{ is uncountable}.}$** The construction works for every prescribed <binary sequence> $(a_n)$; no special distribution property of the digits of $\sqrt2$ is needed.

For <finite sets> $A_1,\ldots,A_r$, the <inclusion-exclusion principle> states
$$
\boxed{\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^rA_i\right|=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|.}
$$
To prove it, count the contribution of one element. If it belongs to exactly $q\geq1$ of the <sets>, it is counted by $\binom qk$ intersections of size $k$. Its total weight is
$$
\sum_{k=1}^q(-1)^{k+1}\binom qk=1-(1-1)^q=1,
$$
using the <binomial theorem>. An element belonging to none contributes zero. Summing these weights gives the <cardinality> of the union and proves the <inclusion-exclusion principle>. Equivalently, within a finite ambient <set> $U$, the number avoiding every $A_i$ is the sum over all $J$, with sign $(-1)^{|J|}$ and the empty intersection interpreted as $U$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the ambient <set> of all $n^n$ <functions> from $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ to itself, let $A_j$ be the forbidden event $f(j)-f(j-1)\equiv j\pmod n$. Apply the avoidance form of the <inclusion-exclusion principle> from part (i).

For a proper <subset> $J$ of the $n$ <cyclic difference constraints>, with $|J|=k<n$, the selected edges form disjoint paths around the cycle, including isolated vertices. There are $n-k$ components. On each component, choosing one value of $f$ arbitrarily determines all the other values by the prescribed differences. No consistency condition occurs because the cycle has been broken. Therefore
$$
\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|=n^{n-k}\qquad(k<n).
$$
This also works for $n=2$, whose two directed constraints use the same pair of vertices: any single constraint leaves one freely chosen value.

If all $n$ constraints hold, summing them around the cycle gives the necessary condition
$$
0\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}j=\frac{n(n-1)}2\pmod n.
$$
It holds when $n$ is odd and fails when $n$ is even, the latter sum being congruent to $n/2$. When it holds, choose $f(0)$ in $n$ ways and determine every other value; the final constraint is then consistent. Thus the full intersection has size $F=n$ for odd $n$ and $F=0$ for even $n$.

There are $\binom nk$ <subsets> of size $k$. By the <binomial theorem>,
$$
|X|=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^k\binom nk n^{n-k}+(-1)^nF
=(n-1)^n+(-1)^n(F-1).
$$
Hence **the number of admissible <functions> is**
$$
\boxed{|X|=\begin{cases}(n-1)^n+1-n,&n\text{ odd},\\(n-1)^n-1,&n\text{ even}.\end{cases}}
$$

= Solution

First apply <momentum conservation> to the rocket and the infinitesimal amount of expelled gas. During $dt$, the exhaust <mass> is $\alpha\,dt$ and its inertial <velocity>, to first order, is $v-u$. With no dust, the momentum balance is
$$
mv=(m-\alpha\,dt)(v+dv)+\alpha\,dt(v-u)+o(dt).
$$
It gives the <rocket equation> **$\boxed{m\dot v=\alpha u}$**, while $\dot m=-\alpha$.

For a <dust-collecting rocket>, the incoming dust <mass> is $\beta v\,dt$ with zero initial <momentum>. The same balance has rocket <mass> $m+(\beta v-\alpha)dt$ after the interval, so
$$
mv=\bigl(m+(\beta v-\alpha)dt\bigr)(v+dv)+\alpha\,dt(v-u)+o(dt).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dot m=\beta v-\alpha,\qquad m\dot v=\alpha u-\beta v^2.}
$$
The second term in the <acceleration> equation accounts for the <momentum> required to bring collected dust up to rocket <speed>.

Set $\lambda=\sqrt{\alpha/(\beta u)}$ and $V=\lambda u$, so $\alpha u=\beta V^2$. As long as the engines operate and $0\leq v<V$, the <velocity> increases and time can be eliminated:
$$
\frac{d\log m}{dv}=\frac{v-\lambda^2u}{V^2-v^2}
=-\frac{\lambda-1}{2(V-v)}-\frac{\lambda+1}{2(V+v)}.
$$
Integrating from $v=0$, $m=m_0$, gives
$$
\log\frac m{m_0}=\frac{\lambda-1}{2}\log\frac{V-v}{V}-\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\log\frac{V+v}{V}.
$$
Thus the required <mass>–<velocity> relation is
$$
\boxed{m=\lambda m_0u\sqrt{\frac{(\lambda u-v)^{\lambda-1}}{(\lambda u+v)^{\lambda+1}}}.}
$$

If $\lambda>1$, this formula would give $m\to0$ as $v\to V^-$. In this interval, $\dot m=\beta(v-\lambda^2u)<0$. A real rocket retains positive body <mass> even after its fuel is gone, and collected dust only increases that residual <mass>. It therefore cannot follow this branch all the way to zero <mass>: **its fuel must run out while $v<\lambda u$**. Indeed the hypothetical time integral $dt/dv=m/[\beta(V^2-v^2)]$ is integrable at $V$ when $\lambda>1$, so even the formal zero-mass endpoint would occur at finite time.

If $0<\lambda<1$, the same relation instead gives $m\to\infty$ as $v\to V^-$. The <mass> first decreases, reaches its minimum at $v=\lambda^2u$, and then increases because dust arrives faster than fuel is expelled. The time integral now diverges at $V$: under indefinitely maintained thrust, **$v$ approaches $\lambda u$ asymptotically while stored dust grows without bound**. At that limiting <speed>, thrust $\alpha u$ balances the dust-loading term $\beta v^2$, and $\dot m\to\beta u\lambda(1-\lambda)>0$. This is the physical reason the total rocket <mass> no longer signals fuel exhaustion. A rocket with a finite initial fuel supply still runs out at finite time $t=M_{\rm fuel}/\alpha$ and before reaching $V$; increasing total <mass> does not create new fuel.

= Solution
{parent=10C}

For any <vector> $\mathbf a$, differentiating its components and the rotating basis gives the <rotating-frame derivative formula>
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_S=\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{S'}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf a.
$$
Apply it twice to the <position> $\mathbf x$, with constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\omega$. The inertial <acceleration> is
$$
\mathbf a_S=\ddot{\mathbf x}+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x),
$$
where dots denote rotating-frame derivatives. <Newton's second law> therefore becomes
$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf F-2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}-m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x).}
$$
The last terms are the <Coriolis force> and the force corresponding to <centrifugal acceleration>.

For the <particle on a uniformly rotating inclined plane>, choose orthonormal vectors $\mathbf e_1$ along the horizontal $\xi$ axis and $\mathbf e_2$ up the slope, and set $\mathbf e_3=\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2$, the upward <normal vector>. Then $\mathbf e_z=\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_2+\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_3$ and
$$
\mathbf x=\xi\mathbf e_1+\eta\mathbf e_2,\qquad\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\mathbf e_z,\qquad\mathbf F=-mg\mathbf e_z+N\mathbf e_3.
$$
Here $N$ is the <normal reaction>. The marble is modeled as the smooth sliding particle specified by the printed equations, without an additional rolling constraint. The rotating-frame <Coriolis acceleration> and <centrifugal acceleration> are respectively
$$
-2\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}=2\omega\dot\eta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_1-2\omega\dot\xi\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_2+2\omega\dot\xi\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_3,
$$
$$
-\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x)=\omega^2\xi\mathbf e_1+\omega^2\eta\cos^2\theta\,\mathbf e_2-\omega^2\eta\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_3.
$$
The normal rotating-frame <acceleration> is zero, so the <normal reaction> is not generally $mg\cos\theta$; it satisfies
$$
N=m\bigl(g\cos\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\sin\theta+\omega^2\eta\sin\theta\cos\theta\bigr).
$$
Projecting the same equation onto the two tangential axes gives exactly
$$
\boxed{\ddot\xi=\omega^2\xi+2\omega\dot\eta\cos\theta,\qquad\ddot\eta=\omega^2\eta\cos^2\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\cos\theta-g\sin\theta.}
$$

Multiply the equations by $\dot\xi$ and $\dot\eta$ and add. The <Coriolis force> terms cancel, as expected because this force does no work relative to the rotating frame. The <normal reaction> also does no work along the plane. The conserved quantity is **rotating-frame <kinetic energy> plus gravitational and <centrifugal potential> energy**:
$$
\boxed{E'=\frac m2(\dot\xi^2+\dot\eta^2)+mg\eta\sin\theta-\frac{m\omega^2}{2}(\xi^2+\eta^2\cos^2\theta)=\text{constant}.}
$$
This need not be the conserved inertial <energy>, since the externally driven rotating plane can exchange energy with the particle.

The area element in <plane polar coordinates> is $r\,dr\,d\theta$. Thus the axial <moment of inertia> is
$$
I_0=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r^2\rho_0(a-r)r\,dr\,d\theta
=2\pi\rho_0\left[\frac{ar^4}{4}-\frac{r^5}{5}\right]_0^a
=\boxed{\frac{\pi\rho_0a^5}{10}}.
$$
For the removed material, the angular width is $2\pi/13$, and
$$
\int_{a/2}^a r^3(a-r)\,dr=\frac{13a^5}{320}.
$$
Its <moment of inertia> is therefore $I_{\rm cut}=\pi\rho_0a^5/160$, leaving
$$
I=I_0-I_{\rm cut}=\frac{3\pi\rho_0a^5}{32}.
$$
The <torque> equation $I\dot\Omega=\tau$ gives constant <angular acceleration>, so the time to reach the prescribed <angular speed> from rest is
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{I\Omega}{\tau}=\frac{3\pi\rho_0a^5\Omega}{32\tau}.}
$$

To find the <centre of mass after removing material>, let $\mathbf e_1$ point along the bisector of the missing sector in the disc. The original disc has zero first <mass> moment about its centre. The missing sector has zero transverse first moment by reflection symmetry, while its component along $\mathbf e_1$ is
$$
Q_{\rm cut}=\rho_0\int_{a/2}^a r^2(a-r)\,dr\int_{-\pi/13}^{\pi/13}\cos\theta\,d\theta
=\frac{11\rho_0a^4}{96}\sin\frac\pi{13},
$$
because the radial integral is $11a^4/192$. The remaining first moment is $-Q_{\rm cut}\mathbf e_1$. Dividing by the given <mass> $k\rho_0a^3$ gives the body-fixed <centre of mass>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf R=-\frac{11a}{96k}\sin\frac\pi{13}\,\mathbf e_1.}
$$
It lies away from the missing sector. In the inertial frame, let $t_*$ be the moment the applied <torque> stops and let $\psi=\psi_*+\Omega(t-t_*)$ give the orientation of that sector's bisector. Then, with $d=11a\sin(\pi/13)/(96k)$,
$$
\mathbf R(t)=-d(\cos\psi,\sin\psi,0),\qquad\boxed{\ddot{\mathbf R}=-\Omega^2\mathbf R}.
$$
The <centre of mass> has inward <centripetal acceleration> of magnitude $\Omega^2d$. **The required real <force> is the constraint reaction exerted by the fixed rod and its support**. Its horizontal resultant is $k\rho_0a^3\ddot{\mathbf R}$; a zero applied axial <torque> does not imply a zero resultant <force>.

= Solution
{parent=12C}

For a massive particle of <rest mass> $m$, its <four-momentum> is $P^\mu=mU^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)$, where the <four-velocity> is $U^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$, $E=\gamma mc^2$ and $\mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v$. With <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$, $P\cdot P=-m^2c^2$. A <photon> has <four-momentum> $(E/c,(E/c)\hat{\mathbf n})$, a future-pointing <null vector>. <Four-momentum conservation> states that the total incoming and outgoing <four-momenta> agree for an isolated interaction; this includes both <energy> and <momentum conservation> in every inertial frame.

Take the incoming <photon> direction as $x$. Its <energy> is $\hbar\omega=\mu mc^2$, so the total initial <four-momentum> is
$$
P=mc(1+\mu,\mu,0,0),\qquad -P\cdot P=m^2c^2(1+2\mu).
$$
The <invariant mass> available after <photon absorption> is therefore $M=m\sqrt{1+2\mu}$. In the <centre-of-momentum frame>, the two equal daughters have opposite <momenta>. Their total <energy> is at least their combined <rest energy> $2\alpha mc^2$, with equality precisely when both daughters have zero <momentum> in that frame. Thus the <mass threshold after photon absorption> is
$$
\boxed{\alpha_{\max}=\frac12\sqrt{1+2\mu}.}
$$
The bound is attainable kinematically by taking each daughter <four-momentum> equal to $P/2$; each then has the required <rest mass>. It immediately gives **$\alpha_{\max}\to1/2$ as $\mu\to0$**.

At this threshold both daughters move with the <centre-of-momentum frame>. The common laboratory <velocity> follows from $\mathbf v=c\mathbf P/P^0$:
$$
\boxed{v=\frac{c\mu}{1+\mu}=\frac{c}{1+\mu^{-1}}.}
$$
For $\mu=0$, its continuous limiting value is zero. At smaller allowed daughter <rest masses>, some of the <centre-of-momentum energy> appears as their relative <kinetic energy>, so their <four-momenta> need not be $P/2$.
