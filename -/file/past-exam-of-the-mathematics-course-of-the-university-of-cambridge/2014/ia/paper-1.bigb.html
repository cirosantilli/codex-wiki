Write a nonzero <complex number> as $w=re^{i\theta}$. Matching <modulus> and <complex argument> gives $r^4=2\sqrt2$ and $4\theta=\pi/4+2\pi k$. Thus **the four distinct fourth roots are**

These angles differ by $\pi/2$, so the values are distinct. Every further integer $k$ repeats one of them; this is multiplication by the fourth <roots of unity>.

The exponent in the PDF is $2z^2$. A <complex exponential> equals one precisely when its exponent is an integer multiple of $2\pi i$: its real part must be zero, and its imaginary part must be a full rotation. Therefore $z^2=\pi i k$ for $k\in\mathbb Z$. Taking both square roots yields **all solutions**
$$
\boxed{\{0\}\ \cup\ \left\{\pm\sqrt{\frac{\pi k}2}(1+i):k=1,2,\ldots\right\}
\ \cup\ \left\{\pm\sqrt{\frac{\pi k}2}(1-i):k=1,2,\ldots\right\}.}
$$
The first nonzero family corresponds to positive exponent index, and the second to negative index. Both signs are required.

For a real line $ux+vy+w=0$, with $(u,v)\ne(0,0)$, choose $B=(u-iv)/2$ and $C=w$. Direct multiplication gives $Bz+\overline B\,\overline z=ux+vy$, so **every line has the required form** with $B\ne0$.

For a <circle> of centre $c$ and radius $\rho>0$, expand its <modulus> equation:
$$
|z-c|^2=\rho^2
\iff z\overline z-\overline c z-c\overline z+|c|^2-\rho^2=0.
$$
Thus **the required <circle> parameters are** $\boxed{B=-c,\ C=|c|^2-\rho^2}$. Conversely, completing the square in the <circle> form gives $|z+B|^2=|B|^2-C$, which describes a genuine <circle> when $|B|^2-C>0$. The <complex conjugate> terms make both equations real-valued.

An <eigenvector> of a <matrix> $A$ is a nonzero vector $v$ for which $Av=\lambda v$. The scalar $\lambda$ is its <eigenvalue>. For a fixed <eigenvalue>, the zero vector together with all corresponding <eigenvectors> forms the <eigenspace> $\ker(A-\lambda I)$. The requirement $v\ne0$ prevents every scalar from being an <eigenvalue> merely because $A0=\lambda0$.

= b
{parent=2a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expanding the <characteristic polynomial> gives
$$
\det(\lambda I-A)=\lambda^3-2\lambda^2-\lambda+2
=(\lambda+1)(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2).
$$
Solving the three nullspace systems gives **the <eigenvalues> and <eigenspaces>**
$$
\boxed{E_{-1}=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,1)^T\},\quad
E_1=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,0)^T\},\quad
E_2=\operatorname{span}\{(0,1,-1)^T\}.}
$$
For example, at $\lambda=1$ the first two equations force $z=0$ and $x=y$; at $\lambda=2$ they force $x=0$ and $y=-z$; at $\lambda=-1$ they force $x=y=z$.

Putting these <eigenvectors> in columns proves their <linear independence>:
$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&1\\1&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det P=1,\qquad
P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,2).
$$
Hence **$A$ is a <diagonalizable matrix> over the reals**.

= Solution
{parent=3D}

Call an index $n$ a peak if $a_n\geq a_m$ for every $m>n$. If there are infinitely many peaks, list their indices increasingly. Each selected value is at least every later selected value, so this gives a nonincreasing <subsequence>.

If there are only finitely many peaks, choose an index beyond the last one. Every index from then onward has a later index with strictly larger value. Select these recursively: $n_{j+1}>n_j$ with $a_{n_{j+1}}>a_{n_j}$. This gives an increasing <subsequence>. Neither <complex argument> requires the original <sequence> to be bounded. **Every real <sequence> therefore has a monotone <subsequence>**, as asserted by the <monotone subsequence theorem>.

Let $a_n=n!/n^n$. Its consecutive coefficient ratio is
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-1}.
$$
The <ratio test> for the full terms therefore has limit $|z|/e$: the series has <absolute convergence> when $|z|<e$ and its terms eventually grow in magnitude when $|z|>e$. Thus **the <radius of convergence> is** $\boxed{R=e}$. No boundary decision is needed to determine the <radius of convergence>.

{parent=4f}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

This is a <lacunary power series>: the coefficient of $z^{n!}$ is $n^n$, while the other coefficients are zero. Along its nonzero coefficients,
$$
(n^n)^{1/n!}=\exp\!\left(\frac{n\log n}{n!}\right)\longrightarrow1.
$$
The factorial dominates $n\log n$; for example $n!\geq n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)$ for large $n$ already suffices for this limit. Hence the coefficient limsup in the <Cauchy-Hadamard theorem> is one. **The <radius of convergence> is** $\boxed{R=1}$.

Directly, for $|z|<1$ the consecutive term ratio is $(n+1)^{n+1}n^{-n}|z|^{n n!}$, which tends to zero; for $|z|\geq1$ the term magnitudes are at least $n^n$ and fail to tend to zero. The factorial is the exponent of $z$, not part of its coefficient.

The first coordinate of the <vector triple product> is
$$
[a\times(b\times c)]_1=a_2(b_1c_2-b_2c_1)-a_3(b_3c_1-b_1c_3)
=b_1(a\cdot c)-c_1(a\cdot b).
$$
The other two coordinates follow by cyclically permuting the indices, proving the identity.

A point on the line has the form $r=b+s m$. Substituting in the <plane> equation gives $s(m\cdot n)=(a-b)\cdot n$. When $m\cdot n\ne0$ the parameter, and therefore the intersection point, is unique. The <vector triple product> gives $n\times(b\times m)=(n\cdot m)b-(n\cdot b)m$, so **the unique intersection is**
$$
\boxed{r=b+\frac{(a-b)\cdot n}{m\cdot n}m
=\frac{(a\cdot n)m+n\times(b\times m)}{m\cdot n}.}
$$
For the parallel case $m\cdot n=0$, the line is entirely in the <plane> if $(b-a)\cdot n=0$, and otherwise has no intersection. As usual, a genuine line and <plane> require nonzero direction and normal vectors. This is the <line-plane intersection criterion>.

The <plane> through $a_i$ has the constant normal coordinate $r\cdot\widehat n=a_i\cdot\widehat n$. Since $\widehat n$ is a <unit vector>, the separation of these normal coordinates is a length. Every displacement joining the <planes> has this fixed normal component, so its length is at least its absolute value; a displacement parallel to $\widehat n$ attains that bound. Hence **the distance between parallel <planes> is**
$$
\boxed{d=|(a_1-a_2)\cdot\widehat n|.}
$$
The absolute value, present in the PDF, is necessary because distance is nonnegative.

Take points $p=(3,0,4)$ and $q=(-2,3,3)$ with directions $u=(1,3,-1)$ and $v=(0,1,-1)$. A common normal is $u\times v=(-2,1,1)$, of length $\sqrt6$. The <planes> through the two lines with this common normal have separation

= Solution

View a possible complex <eigenvector> $v$ using the <Hermitian inner product>. For a real <symmetric matrix>, $A^\dagger=A$, so $v^\dagger Av$ is real. Since $v^\dagger Av=\lambda v^\dagger v$ and $v^\dagger v>0$, **every <eigenvalue> is real**.

For <eigenvectors> $v,w$ with distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda,\mu$, symmetry gives
$$
\lambda v^\dagger w=(Av)^\dagger w=v^\dagger Aw=\mu v^\dagger w,
$$
so **$v^\dagger w=0$**. In particular, real <eigenvectors> are <orthogonal> in the real <dot product>.

Use the permitted diagonalizability assumption to take bases of the real <eigenspaces> whose union spans $\mathbb R^n$. Apply the <Gram-Schmidt process> within each <eigenspace>. Its linear combinations remain <eigenvectors> of that same <eigenvalue>; vectors in different <eigenspaces> are already <orthogonal>. The resulting union is **an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>**, giving an <orthogonal diagonalization of a real symmetric matrix>.

= ii
{parent=6a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $Ax=b$ and $h\in\ker A$, symmetry gives $b\cdot h=(Ax)\cdot h=x\cdot Ah=0$, proving necessity. Conversely, expand $b$ in the <orthonormal basis> from part (i), with $Ay_i=\lambda_i y_i$. The zero-<eigenvalue> vectors span the <kernel of a linear map>. If $b$ is <orthogonal> to that kernel, then $b\cdot y_i=0$ whenever $\lambda_i=0$, so the remaining coordinates can be solved independently.

For a nonzero <eigenvalue> and any associated nonzero <eigenvector> $v$, symmetry gives $b\cdot v=\lambda x\cdot v$. Thus **the component vector along $v$ is**
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{proj}_v x=\frac{b\cdot v}{\lambda\|v\|^2}v.}
$$
For the unit vectors $y_i$, this coefficient is $(b\cdot y_i)/\lambda_i$. Consequently **the general solution is**
$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{\lambda_i\ne0}\frac{b\cdot y_i}{\lambda_i}y_i
+\sum_{\lambda_i=0}c_i y_i,\qquad c_i\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This proves sufficiency as well as the claimed kernel criterion. If the compatibility condition fails, there is no solution; if it holds, the freedom is exactly an arbitrary kernel vector.

= Solution

Reading the coefficients of the <linear map> gives
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&1\\1&e^{-i\phi}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det A=e^{i(\theta-\phi)}-1
=2i\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta-\phi}2\right)e^{i(\theta-\phi)/2}.}
$$
Under the inverse identification, the four standard real basis vectors map respectively to $(1,0)$, $(i,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(0,i)$ in $\mathbb C^2$. Applying $A$ and taking real and imaginary parts therefore gives the columns
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal B e_1&=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,1,0)^T,\\
\mathcal B e_2&=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0,1)^T,\\
\mathcal B e_3&=(1,0,\cos\phi,-\sin\phi)^T,\\
\mathcal B e_4&=(0,1,\sin\phi,\cos\phi)^T.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **the real <matrix> is**
$$
\boxed{B=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&1&0\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0&1\\
1&0&\cos\phi&\sin\phi\\
0&1&-\sin\phi&\cos\phi
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For verification, write its blocks as $\begin{pmatrix}R_\theta&I\\I&R_{-\phi}\end{pmatrix}$, where $R_\psi$ is the planar rotation <matrix>. Block elimination gives $\det B=\det(R_{\theta-\phi}-I)=2-2\cos(\theta-\phi)$. Therefore **the determinant identity is**
$$
\boxed{\det B=4\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\theta-\phi}2\right)=|\det A|^2.}
$$
This is an instance of the <realification determinant identity>. The identification is real-linear; it is not an identification of complex vector-space dimensions.

Expanding the <commutator> immediately gives $[A,A]=0$, $[A,B]=-[B,A]$, and $[A,\lambda B]=\lambda[A,B]$. For its <matrix trace>, write
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}
=\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(BA).
$$
Consequently **every <matrix> <commutator> is traceless**: $\boxed{\operatorname{tr}[A,B]=0}$. This is the <trace of a matrix commutator> identity, valid for arbitrary square <matrices> over the <complex numbers>.

The <conjugate transpose> reverses multiplication. If $A^\dagger=-A$ and $B^\dagger=-B$, then
$$
[A,B]^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger-A^\dagger B^\dagger
=BA-AB=-[A,B].
$$
Thus **the <commutator> is again a <skew-Hermitian matrix>**. The order reversal is what produces the required minus sign.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ the map has the explicit form
$$
\mathcal M(a)=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}ia_1&a_2+ia_3\\-a_2+ia_3&-ia_1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its diagonal entries sum to zero and its <conjugate transpose> is its negative, because the three coordinates are real. Hence it is traceless and a <skew-Hermitian matrix>.

Direct multiplication of the given basis <matrices> yields
$$
[M_1,M_2]=M_3,\qquad[M_2,M_3]=M_1,\qquad[M_3,M_1]=M_2.
$$
The reverse products give the negative <commutators>, and equal-index <commutators> vanish. Bilinearity then gives
$$
[\mathcal M(a),\mathcal M(b)]
=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2)M_1+(a_3b_1-a_1b_3)M_2+(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)M_3.
$$
Thus **the <cross product> is represented by the <commutator>**:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal M(a\times b)=[\mathcal M(a),\mathcal M(b)].}
$$
This realizes the <cross-product model of su(2)> with exactly the normalization and cyclic orientation used here.

Use $\sin0=0$, the <derivative> identity $\sin'=\cos$, <continuity> of cosine at zero, and $\cos0=1$. For $u\ne0$, the <mean value theorem> gives $\sin u/u=\cos c_u$ for a point between zero and $u$. Hence $\sin u/u\to1$ as $u\to0$. For $x\ne0$, apply this with $u=x/3^k$ to obtain **the limit** $\boxed{3^k\sin(x/3^k)\to x}$. For $x=0$ every term is zero.

The additional bound $|\cos t|\leq1$ and the same <mean value theorem> give $|\sin u|\leq|u|$. Therefore
$$
2^n\left|\sin\left(\frac x{3^n}\right)\right|\leq|x|\left(\frac23\right)^n.
$$
The right side is summable. By the <comparison test for series> **the stated series has <absolute convergence> for every real $x$**.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $r=e^{i\theta}\ne1$. The <geometric series> from any starting index is bounded:
$$
B_j=\sum_{n=m}^j r^n=\frac{r^m(1-r^{j-m+1})}{1-r},\qquad
|B_j|\leq K=\frac2{|1-r|}.
$$
Using <summation by parts>,
$$
\sum_{n=m}^N a_nr^n=a_NB_N+\sum_{n=m}^{N-1}(a_n-a_{n+1})B_n.
$$
Since the coefficients are nonincreasing and positive, the <modulus> is at most $K(a_N+a_m-a_N)=Ka_m\to0$. The Cauchy criterion proves **convergence for every $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$**. This is a direct proof of the <Dirichlet test> in this setting.

For $a_n=1/n$, the imaginary parts of the convergent partial sums give convergence of the sine series at those angles. If $\theta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, every sine term is zero. Hence **the sine series converges for all real angles**.

The <mean value theorem> states that a function <continuous> on $[p,q]$ and differentiable on $(p,q)$ has some $c\in(p,q)$ with $f(q)-f(p)=f'(c)(q-p)$.

Take any $p<q$ inside the given open interval. Differentiability implies the <continuity> needed on $[p,q]$, and the <derivative> is zero throughout it. The <mean value theorem> therefore gives $f(q)=f(p)$. Since the two points were arbitrary, **the function is constant on the interval**.

For <continuity> at any $x$, choose $\delta=\eta^{1/\alpha}$ for a desired error $\eta>0$. If $|y-x|<\delta$, the stated bound gives $|f(y)-f(x)|<\eta$. Thus **$f$ is <continuous>**, in fact <uniformly continuous>.

When $\alpha>1$, the difference quotient satisfies

\left|\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h\right|\leq|h|^{\alpha-1}\longrightarrow0.

Therefore the <derivative> exists at every point and is zero. Part (i) proves **$f$ is constant**. This is the lemma that <Hölder exponent greater than one forces constancy>; no differentiability was assumed in advance.

Let $D=f'_+(a)$ and write $g(t)=(f(t)-f(a))/(t-a)$ for $t>a$. By the assumed right <derivative>, choose $\delta>0$ with $|g(t)-D|<\epsilon/2$ whenever $a<t<a+\delta$. Choose $b_0\in(a,\min(b,a+\delta))$.

This proves the <endpoint secant-tangent approximation> without any <continuity> assumption on $f'$. The <continuity> required by the theorem is only that of $f$ itself.

= Solution

For $x\ne0$, put $P(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f^{(k)}(0)t^k/k!$ and $K=(f(x)-P(x))/x^n$. The auxiliary function $F(t)=f(t)-P(t)-Kt^n$ satisfies
$$
F(x)=F(0)=0,\qquad F^{(j)}(0)=0\quad(1\leq j\leq n-1).
$$
<Rolle's theorem> first gives a zero of $F'$ strictly between zero and $x$. Apply <Rolle's theorem> to $F'$ between that zero and zero, where $F'(0)=0$, to obtain a zero of $F''$. Continue in this way. The <derivatives> through order $n-1$ are <continuous>, since they are differentiable; hence every application is valid even if $f^{(n)}$ is not <continuous>. After $n$ applications there is $c$ strictly between zero and $x$ with $F^{(n)}(c)=0$.

But $F^{(n)}(c)=f^{(n)}(c)-n!K$, so $K=f^{(n)}(c)/n!$. Writing $c=\theta x$ yields **the Taylor formula with Lagrange remainder**
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k
+\frac{f^{(n)}(\theta x)}{n!}x^n,\qquad0<\theta<1.}
$$
The same construction using repeated applications of <Rolle's theorem> applies when $x<0$, with intervals ordered from $x$ to zero. For $x=0$ the identity is immediate and any $\theta\in(0,1)$ works. This proves the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> using only the allowed theorem and elementary differentiation.

= ii
{parent=11e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

From $f''=f$, differentiating successively gives $f^{(2r)}=f$ and $f^{(2r+1)}=f'$ whenever those <derivatives> have been constructed. Starting with twice differentiability, this relation supplies the next two <derivatives> at each stage. Thus **$f$ is infinitely differentiable**, and its origin series is
$$
\boxed{A\sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{x^{2r}}{(2r)!}
+B\sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{x^{2r+1}}{(2r+1)!}.}
$$
For a fixed $x$, both $f$ and $f'$ are bounded on the compact interval between zero and $x$, say by $M_x$. Every higher <derivative> is one of these two functions. <Taylor's theorem>, with the remainder just proved, therefore bounds the order-$n$ remainder by $M_x|x|^n/n!$, which tends to zero: its consecutive positive-term ratio is $|x|/(n+1)$. Hence **the series converges to $f(x)$ for every real $x$**. It also gives $f(x)=A\cosh x+B\sinh x$ by the usual hyperbolic-function series.

At any other centre $a$, apply the proved origin result to $g(t)=f(a+t)$, which also satisfies $g''=g$. Its even <derivatives> at zero are $f(a)$, and its odd ones are $f'(a)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{f(a+h)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}h^k
=f(a)\sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{h^{2r}}{(2r)!}
+f'(a)\sum_{r=0}^\infty\frac{h^{2r+1}}{(2r+1)!}.}
$$
This proves convergence at every centre without assuming that smoothness alone implies equality to a <Taylor series>.

= Solution

For a bounded real function on $[0,1]$, take a partition $P$ with nodes $0=x_0<\cdots<x_N=1$. Its <lower Darboux sum> and <upper Darboux sum> are
$$
L(f,P)=\sum_j\inf_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f\,(x_j-x_{j-1}),\qquad
U(f,P)=\sum_j\sup_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f\,(x_j-x_{j-1}).
$$
The function is <Riemann integrable> when $\sup_P L(f,P)=\inf_P U(f,P)$; their common value is its <Riemann integral>. Equivalently, for every $\eta>0$ some partition has $U-L<\eta$. Indeed a common refinement of nearly extremizing upper and lower partitions proves necessity, while arbitrarily small gaps force equality of the two integrals. This is the <Riemann integrability criterion>.

A <continuous> function on a compact interval is bounded and <uniformly continuous>. Choose a mesh small enough that its oscillation on each subinterval is less than $\eta$. Then $U-L\leq\eta\sum_j(x_j-x_{j-1})=\eta$; using $\eta/2$ first makes the desired inequality strict. Thus **every <continuous> function on $[0,1]$ is <Riemann integrable>**.

= Solution

For a nondecreasing function, the endpoint bounds show it is bounded. On the uniform partition into $N$ intervals, its <Darboux sums> satisfy
$$
U-L=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\left[f\left(\frac jN\right)-f\left(\frac{j-1}N\right)\right]
=\frac{f(1)-f(0)}N\longrightarrow0.
$$
For a nonincreasing function apply the same calculation to $-f$, or use $|f(1)-f(0)|/N$. Thus **every <monotone function> on $[0,1]$ is <Riemann integrable>**.

For the unheaded rational-weight example, each summand is nonnegative, and increasing $x$ only adds indices. Hence its sum is nondecreasing and lies between zero and one, including the separately specified value at zero. It is a <left-continuous cumulative function of an atomic measure>.

If $q_j\in[0,1)$ and $q_j<x\leq1$, the index $j$ appears in the sum at $x$ but not at $q_j$. All other changes are nonnegative, so $f(x)-f(q_j)\geq2^{-j}$. This remains true for arbitrarily small positive $x-q_j$, proving discontinuity at $q_j$, including a right discontinuity at zero when that rational is encountered. Every interval of positive length contains such a rational in its interior. **The discontinuities are therefore dense**, although **the function is <Riemann integrable>** by the monotone-function result just proved.

More concretely, truncate after the first $N$ weights. The resulting finite sum of step functions is integrable, and the uniform tail is at most $2^{-N}$. This also proves integrability and gives, if its value is desired,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^\infty2^{-k}(1-q_k).}
$$
The integral formula follows by integrating the finite sums and using the uniform tail bound. Here “every interval” has the usual nondegenerate-interval meaning; a singleton is not being asserted to contain a discontinuity.
