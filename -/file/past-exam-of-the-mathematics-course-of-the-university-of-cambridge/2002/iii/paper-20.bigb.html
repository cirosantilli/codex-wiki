For a <categorical presheaf> $X:\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Set}$ and $A\in\mathcal C$, the <Yoneda lemma> gives a <natural bijection>
$$
\operatorname{Nat}(\mathcal C(-,A),X)\cong X(A),\qquad \theta\longmapsto\theta_A(1_A).
$$
To construct its inverse, take $x\in X(A)$ and <set> $\theta^x_U(h)=X(h)x$ for $h:U\to A$. For $k:V\to U$, the <functor> law gives $X(k)\theta^x_U(h)=X(hk)x=\theta^x_V(hk)$, so $\theta^x$ is a <natural transformation>. Conversely, <naturality> of any $\theta$ at $h:U\to A$ gives $\theta_U(h)=X(h)\theta_A(1_A)$. Thus the two constructions are inverse, proving the <bijection>.

The <bijection> is natural in $X$: a <natural transformation> $t:X\to Y$ sends the distinguished element $x$ to $t_A(x)$ on either side. It is natural in $A$: for $f:A\to B$, precomposing a transformation $\mathcal C(-,B)\to X$ with postcomposition by $f$ sends its distinguished element $x\in X(B)$ to $X(f)x$. These equations prove the claimed <naturality>, rather than just an objectwise correspondence. Reversing <morphisms> gives the covariant form $\operatorname{Nat}(\mathcal C(A,-),Z)\cong Z(A)$ for $Z:\mathcal C\to\mathbf{Set}$.

Define the <Yoneda embedding> $H_\bullet:\mathcal C\to[\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{Set}]$ by $H_A(U)=\mathcal C(U,A)$ and $H_A(k)(h)=hk$. For $f:A\to B$, define $H_\bullet(f)_U(h)=fh$. Associativity proves <naturality> in $U$, and preservation of <identity morphisms> and <composition in a category> proves that $H_\bullet$ is a <functor>.

Apply the <Yoneda lemma> to $X=H_B$. It gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Nat}(H_A,H_B)\cong\mathcal C(A,B).}
$$
The <natural transformation> corresponding to $f$ has component $h\mapsto fh$, exactly $H_\bullet(f)$. Therefore the induced map on every <hom-set> is a <bijection>: **the Yoneda embedding is <full and faithful>**.

If $f:A\to B$ is a <monomorphism>, each <function> $\mathcal C(U,A)\to\mathcal C(U,B)$ given by postcomposition with $f$ is injective. Consequently, if two <natural transformations> into $H_A$ become equal after $H_\bullet(f)$, their components are equal, and the transformations themselves are equal. This proves that $H_\bullet(f)$ is a <monomorphism> in the <presheaf category>.

Conversely, suppose $H_\bullet(f)$ is a <monomorphism> and $u,v:U\to A$ satisfy $fu=fv$. Then $H_\bullet(f)H_\bullet(u)=H_\bullet(f)H_\bullet(v)$. Cancellation gives $H_\bullet(u)=H_\bullet(v)$, and faithfulness of the <Yoneda embedding> gives $u=v$. Hence **$H_\bullet(f)$ is monic exactly when $f$ is monic**.

We first justify the relevant <pointwise epimorphism in a functor category> criterion. A componentwise <surjective function> gives an <epimorphism>, because equality of two composites can be cancelled at each component. Conversely, for a <natural transformation> $t:X\to Y$, form the pointwise <pushout> $P=Y\amalg_XY$. Restrictions of $Y$ preserve the identifications imposed by $t$, so these pointwise <sets> and restrictions form a <categorical presheaf>. The two inclusions $j_1,j_2:Y\to P$ satisfy $j_1t=j_2t$. If $t$ is epic, $j_1=j_2$. At an object $U$, an element outside the image of $t_U$ would retain two distinct copies in $P(U)$, contradicting this equality. Thus every $t_U$ is surjective.

Apply this criterion to $H_\bullet(f)$ at $B$. Surjectivity of $\mathcal C(B,A)\to\mathcal C(B,B)$ supplies $g:B\to A$ with $fg=1_B$. Conversely, if $fg=1_B$, then $H_\bullet(f)H_\bullet(g)=1_{H_B}$, making $H_\bullet(f)$ a <split epimorphism> and hence an <epimorphism>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H_\bullet(f)\text{ epic}\iff f\text{ split epic}.}
$$

Here is a precise <base change of a pullback square> statement. Suppose $A=B\times_D C$ is a <pullback in a category> and $t:D'\to D$ is a <morphism>. Assume the <pullbacks in a category>
$$
A'=A\times_D D',\quad B'=B\times_D D',\quad C'=C\times_D D'
$$
exist, where $A\to D$ is the common composite. The induced square with vertices $A',B',C',D'$ is a <pullback in a category>; equivalently,
$$
\boxed{(B\times_D C)\times_D D'\cong(B\times_D D')\times_{D'}(C\times_D D').}
$$
Indeed, compatible <morphisms> $T\to B'$ and $T\to C'$ give <morphisms> $b:T\to B$, $c:T\to C$, and a common $v:T\to D'$, with the composites of $b,c$ to $D$ both equal to $tv$. The original <pullback in a category> gives a unique <morphism> $h:T\to A$ with projections $b,c$. Its composite to $D$ is $tv$, so the defining <pullback in a category> for $A'$ gives a unique <morphism> $T\to A'$ above $h,v$. Its maps to $B'$ and $C'$ are the prescribed ones by uniqueness in those <pullbacks in a category>. Conversely, these projections recover $b,c,v$, so every possible factorization is the same. This proves the <universal property> without requiring arbitrary <categorical limits> in the ambient <category>.

For a <functor> $D:\mathcal J\to\mathcal C$ with finite indexing <category>, form the finite <products in a category>
$$
P=\prod_{j\in\operatorname{Ob}\mathcal J}D(j),\qquad Q=\prod_{u:i\to j\text{ in }\mathcal J}D(j).
$$
Define $s,t:P\rightrightarrows Q$ by requiring the coordinate indexed by $u:i\to j$ to be $D(u)\pi_i$ for $s$ and $\pi_j$ for $t$. Let $e:L\to P$ be their <equalizer>, and put $\lambda_j=\pi_j e$. The equality $se=te$ says exactly that $D(u)\lambda_i=\lambda_j$ for every <morphism> $u$, so these <morphisms> form a <categorical cone>.

For any other <categorical cone> $(x_j:X\to D(j))$, the <product in a category> gives a unique $x:X\to P$ with $\pi_jx=x_j$. The <categorical cone> equations give $sx=tx$, so the <equalizer> gives a unique $\bar x:X\to L$ with $e\bar x=x$. This is exactly the <universal property> of a <categorical limit>. Empty indexing products are <terminal objects>, so the construction includes the empty <diagram in a category>. Thus **finite products and <equalizers> give all <finite limits>**.

Let $1$ be a <terminal object>. The <pullback in a category> of $X\to1\leftarrow Y$ has precisely the <universal property> of the binary <product in a category> $X\times Y$: every pair of <morphisms> to $X,Y$ is compatible over $1$. Repeated binary <products in a category>, together with $1$ as the empty product, give all finite <products in a category>.

Conversely any such <morphism> must project to that pair. Hence $e$ is the <equalizer> of $f,g$. The preceding construction now gives **all <finite limits>**.

\boxed{\mathcal C(-,L)\cong\operatorname{Cone}(-,D).}

Thus this definition is exactly existence and uniqueness of factorization of every <categorical cone> through $\lambda$. Conversely that <universal property> gives the representing <natural bijection>.

For $v:j\to k$, the <morphisms> $F(1_i,v)\lambda_{i,j}$ form a <categorical cone> over $F(-,k)$, since the two actions of the product <functor> $F$ commute. Define $L(v):L_j\to L_k$ by the equations
$$
\lambda_{i,k}L(v)=F(1_i,v)\lambda_{i,j}\qquad(i\in\mathcal I).

Existence and uniqueness follow from the <universal property>. The <identity morphism> of $L_j$ satisfies the equations for $v=1_j$, and $L(w)L(v)$ satisfies those for $wv$, so uniqueness gives $L(1_j)=1_{L_j}$ and $L(wv)=L(w)L(v)$. Hence $L:\mathcal J\to\mathcal D$ is a <functor>.

The same equations show $u_kL(v)=L'(v)u_j$, because their composites with all $\lambda'_{i,k}$ agree. Thus the choices give **a unique <natural isomorphism> compatible with the chosen <categorical limit> <categorical cones>**. Without the compatibility condition, uniqueness of an arbitrary <natural isomorphism> is not asserted.

A precise <commutation of iterated categorical limits> statement is the following. For small <categories> $\mathcal I,\mathcal J$ and $F:\mathcal I\times\mathcal J\to\mathcal D$, if every column <categorical limit> and the resulting $\mathcal J$-limit exist, then
$$
\lim_j\lim_iF(i,j)\cong\lim_{(i,j)}F(i,j).
$$
If every row <categorical limit> and the resulting $\mathcal I$-limit also exist, then
$$
\boxed{\lim_j\lim_iF(i,j)\cong\lim_{(i,j)}F(i,j)\cong\lim_i\lim_jF(i,j).}
$$
Each <isomorphism> is the unique one identifying all projections to $F(i,j)$. Indeed, the preceding part proves that each iterated <categorical limit> has the <universal property> of the whole product <diagram in a category>. Its uniqueness gives the canonical <isomorphism>. Equivalently, <categorical cones> with vertex $X$ over the product <diagram in a category> are exactly compatible families, indexed by $j$, of <categorical cones> over $F(-,j)$; regrouping the same compatibility equations cannot change their representing object. In a <complete category> all the stated existence hypotheses hold for small $\mathcal I,\mathcal J$. The theorem is about <categorical limits> with <categorical limits>, and makes no unconditional claim about commuting a <categorical limit> with a <colimit>.

Let $T:\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathcal C\to\mathcal D$. A <categorical end> is an object $E$ with <morphisms> $e_A:E\to T(A,A)$ satisfying, for every $f:A\to B$,
$$
T(1_A,f)e_A=T(f,1_B)e_B.
$$
It is universal among such families: any $x_A:X\to T(A,A)$ satisfying the same equations is uniquely $e_Ah$ for one $h:X\to E$. This is a universal <dinatural transformation> from the constant value $E$, and is written $E=\int_A T(A,A)$.

Dually a <categorical coend> is $Q$ with <morphisms> $j_A:T(A,A)\to Q$ satisfying
$$
j_BT(1_B,f)=j_AT(f,1_A):T(B,A)\to Q.
$$
Any family $x_A:T(A,A)\to X$ satisfying these equations is uniquely $h j_A$ for one $h:Q\to X$. Write $Q=\int^A T(A,A)$. For small $\mathcal C$, the <categorical end> can be constructed as the <equalizer> of $\prod_AT(A,A)\rightrightarrows\prod_{f:A\to B}T(A,B)$ when these objects exist. The <categorical coend> is the <coequalizer> of $\coprod_{f:A\to B}T(B,A)\rightrightarrows\coprod_AT(A,A)$ when those objects exist. The two <morphisms> impose exactly the displayed equations for a <dinatural transformation>.

Working with a <small category> $\mathcal C$ (or in a fixed ambient universe), for a <categorical presheaf> $X$ and fixed $U$, apply the <categorical coend> construction to $T(A,B)=\mathcal C(U,B)\times X(A)$. It is the quotient of the <disjoint union> of pairs $(h:U\to W,x\in X(W))$ by the relations
$$
(fh,x)\sim(h,X(f)x)\qquad(f:W\to V,\ x\in X(V)).
$$
Define $\Phi_U([h,x])=X(h)x$. It respects every relation because $X(fh)x=X(h)X(f)x$. Define $\Psi_U(y)=[1_U,y]$. Then $\Phi_U\Psi_U(y)=y$. Conversely the relation with $f=h:U\to W$ gives $[h,x]=[1_U,X(h)x]$, so $\Psi_U\Phi_U$ is the <identity morphism>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X(U)\cong\int^W\mathcal C(U,W)\times X(W).}
$$
For $k:V\to U$, restriction on the <categorical coend> sends $[h,x]$ to $[hk,x]$, and $\Phi_V([hk,x])=X(k)\Phi_U([h,x])$. A <natural transformation> $X\to Y$ likewise acts on the second coordinate and commutes with $\Phi$. Thus the <density formula for presheaves> is a <natural isomorphism>, not just a family of unrelated <bijections>.

Write the <adjunction> as a <natural bijection> $\alpha_{C,D}:\mathcal D(FC,D)\to\mathcal C(C,GD)$. The <adjunction unit> and <adjunction counit> are defined by
$$
\eta_C=\alpha_{C,FC}(1_{FC}),\qquad\varepsilon_D=\alpha^{-1}_{GD,D}(1_{GD}).
$$
<Naturality> of $\alpha$ implies $GF(u)\eta_C=\eta_{C'}u$ for $u:C\to C'$ and $v\varepsilon_D=\varepsilon_{D'}FG(v)$ for $v:D\to D'$, so these are <natural transformations> $1_{\mathcal C}\to GF$ and $FG\to1_{\mathcal D}$. More explicitly, <naturality> at $f:FC\to D$ and $g:C\to GD$ gives the two transpose formulas
$$
\alpha(f)=G(f)\eta_C,\qquad\alpha^{-1}(g)=\varepsilon_DF(g).
$$
These formulas will also prove the <triangle identities for an adjunction>.

A <poset> $P$ becomes a <category> with the elements of $P$ as objects and exactly one <morphism> $x\to y$ when $x\leq y$, with no <morphism> otherwise. Reflexivity supplies the <identity morphisms> and transitivity supplies <composition in a category>; uniqueness of <morphisms> proves associativity. A <functor> $f:P\to Q$ is exactly a <monotone function>: it must send $x\leq y$ to $f(x)\leq f(y)$, and this condition suffices because all the required <morphisms> are unique. Antisymmetry says that isomorphic objects of this <category> are equal.

Each <hom-set> in a <poset> is either empty or a singleton. Thus a <natural bijection> $Y(fx,y)\cong X(x,gy)$ is precisely the condition
$$
\boxed{f(x)\leq y\iff x\leq g(y).}

The <naturality> squares commute automatically, because maps between the relevant singleton <hom-sets> are unique. This is a <Galois connection>. Equivalently, the <adjunction unit> and <adjunction counit> are the inequalities $x\leq gf(x)$ and $fg(y)\leq y$. These inequalities imply the displayed equivalence: $f(x)\leq y$ gives $x\leq gf(x)\leq g(y)$, and $x\leq g(y)$ gives $f(x)\leq fg(y)\leq y$.

The two <adjoints of inverse image on power sets> are
$$
\boxed{\exists_p(U)=p(U),\qquad\forall_p(U)=\{b\in B:p^{-1}(\{b\})\subseteq U\}.}
$$
These maps and $p^*(V)=p^{-1}(V)$ are <monotone functions> on the inclusion-ordered <power sets>. For $U\subseteq A$ and $V\subseteq B$,
$$
p(U)\subseteq V\iff U\subseteq p^{-1}(V),\qquad p^{-1}(V)\subseteq U\iff V\subseteq\forall_p(U).
$$
The first equivalence follows by following each element of $U$ through $p$. For the second, every <fiber> over $b\in V$ must lie in $U$, which is precisely the defining condition for $b\in\forall_p(U)$. These are the two <Galois connections>, proving $\exists_p\dashv p^*\dashv\forall_p$. In particular, a point outside $p(A)$ has an empty <fiber> and belongs to $\forall_p(U)$ even when $U$ is empty; omitting this case would give an incorrect <right adjoint>.

For a <presheaf of sets on a topological space> $P$, define $LP=P(\varnothing)$ and $RP=P(S)$. Then the <adjoints to constant presheaves on open sets> are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ev}_{\varnothing}\dashv\Delta\dashv\operatorname{ev}_S.}
$$
To prove the first <adjunction>, a <natural transformation> $a:P\to\Delta A$ must satisfy $a_U=a_{\varnothing}\circ\operatorname{res}_{U,\varnothing}$ by <naturality> at the inclusion $\varnothing\subseteq U$. Hence it is uniquely determined by a <function> $P(\varnothing)\to A$. Every such <function> defines a <natural transformation> by this formula, because restriction maps compose. This gives $\operatorname{Nat}(P,\Delta A)\cong\mathbf{Set}(P(\varnothing),A)$.

For the second <adjunction>, a <natural transformation> $b:\Delta A\to P$ satisfies $b_U=\operatorname{res}_{S,U}\circ b_S$, so it is uniquely determined by a <function> $A\to P(S)$. Every such <function> defines the remaining components by restriction, giving $\operatorname{Nat}(\Delta A,P)\cong\mathbf{Set}(A,P(S))$. Evaluation acts on <presheaf morphisms> componentwise, so these object assignments are indeed <functors>. The use of $P(\varnothing)$ is essential: a <constant presheaf of sets> has value $A$ there, whereas a <sheaf> has a singleton value there. No sheaf condition is imposed in this question.

\alpha\beta(g)=G\varepsilon_d\,GFg\,\eta_c=G\varepsilon_d\,\eta_{Gd}g=g.
$$

\qquad e_c=\varepsilon_{Fc}F\eta_c.

As $e$ is a composite of <natural transformations>, this proves that **$e$ is an idempotent in the <functor category>**, including the required <naturality>.

The maps $h\mapsto hr$ and $f\mapsto fi$ give inverse <bijections> between $\mathcal D(Hc,d)$ and the <subset> of $\mathcal D(Fc,d)$ consisting of maps with $fe_c=f$. Indeed $(hr)e_c=hrir=hr$, $(hr)i=h$, and $(fi)r=fe_c=f$. The maps $\alpha,\beta$ give inverse <bijections> between that <subset> and $\mathcal C(c,Gd)$, since $\beta\alpha(f)=fe_c$ and $\alpha\beta=1$. Combining them gives the <natural bijection>
$$
\mathcal D(Hc,d)\cong\mathcal C(c,Gd),\qquad h\longmapsto G(hr)\eta_c,
$$
with inverse $g\mapsto\varepsilon_dF(g)i_c$. Hence $H\dashv G$. Its <adjunction unit> is $Gr\,\eta$ and its <adjunction counit> is $\varepsilon\,i_G$.

Conversely, suppose $H\dashv G$, with <natural bijection> $\theta:\mathcal D(Hc,d)\cong\mathcal C(c,Gd)$. Set $\tau=\theta^{-1}\alpha$ and $\sigma=\beta\theta$. Then $\tau\sigma=1$ and $\sigma\tau(f)=fe_c$. By the covariant <Yoneda lemma>, the transformation $\tau:\mathcal D(Fc,-)\to\mathcal D(Hc,-)$ is precomposition with a unique <morphism> $i_c:Hc\to Fc$, and $\sigma$ is precomposition with a unique <morphism> $r_c:Fc\to Hc$. Thus they split $e$ in the whole <functor category>, rather than merely at individual objects. We have proved
$$
\boxed{G\text{ has a left adjoint}\iff e=\varepsilon_FF\eta\text{ splits in }[\mathcal C,\mathcal D].}
$$

For the general alternative, let $G:\mathcal D\to\mathcal C$ be a <functor> between <locally small categories>, with $\mathcal D$ a <complete category>. The <Freyd general adjoint functor theorem> says that $G$ has a <left adjoint> if and only if it preserves small <categorical limits> and satisfies the <solution-set condition>. Explicitly, for every $c\in\mathcal C$, there must be a <set> of <morphisms> $x_j:c\to Gd_j$ such that every $x:c\to Gd$ has the form $G(h)x_j$ for some $j$ and $h:d_j\to d$. Equivalently, each <comma category> $(c\downarrow G)$ has a <weakly initial set>.

We prove the <initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set>. Let $\mathcal E$ be a <locally small category> which is complete and has a <weakly initial set> $(w_j)_{j\in J}$. Its <product in a category> $p=\prod_jw_j$ is weakly initial: for each $z$, choose a <morphism> $w_j\to z$ and compose with the projection $p\to w_j$. The <hom-set> $\mathcal E(p,p)$ is a <set>. Form $e:i\to p$ equalizing every <endomorphism> of $p$ with $1_p$, by taking the <equalizer> of the two <morphisms>
$$
p\rightrightarrows\prod_{v\in\mathcal E(p,p)}p,
$$
whose $v$-coordinates are $v$ and $1_p$. Then $ve=e$ for every <endomorphism> $v$, and $e$ is monic. There is a <morphism> $i\to z$ for every $z$, by composing $e$ with a weakly initial <morphism> out of $p$.

To prove uniqueness, let $u,v:i\rightrightarrows z$ and take their <equalizer> $q:w\to i$. Weak initiality of $p$ gives $a:p\to w$. The composite $eqa:p\to p$ is an <endomorphism>, so $eqa e=e$. Cancel the <monomorphism> $e$ to obtain $qa e=1_i$. Thus $q$ is both monic and split epic, hence an <isomorphism>: if $qk=1$, cancellation from $qkq=q$ gives $kq=1$. Since $uq=vq$, it follows that $u=v$. Therefore $i$ is an <initial object>, proving the lemma fully.

Now assume <categorical limit> preservation and the <solution-set condition>. The <comma category> $(c\downarrow G)$ is locally small. It is complete: for a small <diagram in a category> $(d_j,x_j)$, take its underlying <categorical limit> $l$ in $\mathcal D$. Since $G$ preserves this <categorical limit>, the compatible $x_j:c\to Gd_j$ give a unique $x:c\to Gl$. The same <universal property> shows that $(l,x)$ is the <categorical limit> in the <comma category>. Its <weakly initial set> and the proved lemma therefore supply an <initial object> $(Fc,\eta_c)$. For $t:c\to c'$, its <universal property> defines a unique $Ft:Fc\to Fc'$ with $G(Ft)\eta_c=\eta_{c'}t$. Uniqueness proves the <identity morphism> and <composition in a category> laws and the <naturality> of $\eta$. The same <universal property> gives the <natural bijection> $\mathcal D(Fc,d)\cong\mathcal C(c,Gd)$, so $F\dashv G$.

For necessity, an <adjunction> $F\dashv G$ supplies the singleton <weakly initial set> $(Fc,\eta_c)$ at $c$. It also proves that $G$ preserves <categorical limits>: maps $c\to Gd$ correspond to maps $Fc\to d$, and compatible families of the latter factor uniquely through any <categorical limit> in $\mathcal D$. Hence the image <categorical cone> under $G$ has the required <universal property>. This proves both directions of the general alternative.

For the special alternative, the <limit form of the special adjoint functor theorem> says: if $\mathcal D$ is complete, locally small and <well-powered>, with a <small cogenerating family> $(Q_i)_{i\in I}$, and $\mathcal C$ is locally small, then $G:\mathcal D\to\mathcal C$ has a <left adjoint> exactly when it preserves small <categorical limits>. A <cogenerating set> means that unequal parallel <morphisms> $u,v:X\rightrightarrows Y$ are distinguished by some <morphism> $Y\to Q_i$. We prove the needed <solution-set condition>, so that the general theorem applies.

Fix $x:c\to Gd$. A supporting <subobject> of $d$ is a <monomorphism> $m:d'\to d$ through whose image under $G$ the <morphism> $x$ factors. There is at least one, namely $1_d$. Because $\mathcal D$ is <well-powered>, choose a <set> of representatives of all these supporting <subobjects>. A limit-preserving <functor> preserves <monomorphisms>: a map is monic exactly when its diagonal into its self-<pullback in a category> is an <isomorphism>, and both the <pullback in a category> and this <isomorphism> are preserved. Consequently each factorization of $x$ through $Gm$ is unique. Form the <intersection of subobjects> of the supporting representatives, as a wide <pullback in a category> over $d$. The unique lifts of $x$ are compatible and, since $G$ preserves that <categorical limit>, they give a factorization
$$
x=G(m_0)x_0,\qquad m_0:d_0\hookrightarrow d,\quad x_0:c\to Gd_0.
$$
This is a <minimal supported subobject>. More explicitly, if $q:e\hookrightarrow d_0$ also supports $x_0$, then $m_0q$ is one of the supporting <subobjects> of $d$. The intersection therefore factors through $m_0q$, giving $r:d_0\to e$ with $m_0qr=m_0$. Monicity of $m_0$ gives $qr=1_{d_0}$; monicity of $q$ then makes $q$ invertible.

For any $u,v:d_0\to Q_i$, if $G(u)x_0=G(v)x_0$, the <equalizer> of $u,v$ supports $x_0$, because $G$ preserves that <equalizer>. Minimality makes it invertible, so $u=v$. We have therefore obtained <injective functions>
$$
\mathcal D(d_0,Q_i)\longrightarrow\mathcal C(c,GQ_i),\qquad u\longmapsto G(u)x_0.
$$
Let $S_i$ be the realized image <subset> of the fixed <set> $\mathcal C(c,GQ_i)$. Use the unique <morphism> corresponding to each $s\in S_i$ to form the <evaluation embedding into cogenerator products>
$$
d_0\longrightarrow P_S=\prod_{i\in I}\prod_{s\in S_i}Q_i.
$$
This <morphism> is monic: if two <morphisms> into $d_0$ have identical product composites, every <morphism> from $d_0$ to every $Q_i$ gives identical composites, and the cogenerating property makes the two <morphisms> equal. There is only a <set> of possible families of <subsets> $(S_i)$, and hence a <set> of possible products $P_S$. Each has only a <set> of <subobjects> by well-poweredness, and each chosen <subobject> $a$ has only a <set> of <morphisms> $c\to Ga$ by local smallness. Take all these pairs $(a,z:c\to Ga)$ as a <weakly initial set>. Every original $(d,x)$ receives a <morphism> from a pair representing its $(d_0,x_0)$, through $m_0$. Thus the <set> is weakly initial in $(c\downarrow G)$.

The general theorem now supplies a <left adjoint>. Necessity again follows from preservation of <categorical limits> by a <right adjoint>. This proves the special alternative, including its size argument. Using only the realized <subsets> $S_i$ avoids assuming that an arbitrary object admits a <morphism> into every cogenerator. **Both adjoint <functor> theorem alternatives are proved.**

A <monoidal category> consists of a <category> $\mathcal C$, a <bifunctor> $\otimes:\mathcal C\times\mathcal C\to\mathcal C$, a <monoidal unit object> $I$, and <natural isomorphisms>
$$
\alpha_{A,B,C}:(A\otimes B)\otimes C\to A\otimes(B\otimes C),\quad
\lambda_A:I\otimes A\to A,\quad\rho_A:A\otimes I\to A.
$$
The <associator> obeys the <pentagon identity for a monoidal category>,
$$
\alpha_{A,B,C\otimes D}\,\alpha_{A\otimes B,C,D}
=(1_A\otimes\alpha_{B,C,D})\,\alpha_{A,B\otimes C,D}\,(\alpha_{A,B,C}\otimes1_D),
$$
and the <unitors> obey the <triangle identity for a monoidal category>,
$$
(1_A\otimes\lambda_B)\,\alpha_{A,I,B}=\rho_A\otimes1_B.
$$
<Composition in a category> here acts from right to left. The first equation compares two paths from $((A\otimes B)\otimes C)\otimes D$ to $A\otimes(B\otimes(C\otimes D))$; the second compares two ways of removing the intervening unit.

It is precisely a <bicategory> with one object $*$. The <category> $\mathcal B(*,*)$ is $\mathcal C$: its objects are the 1-morphisms and its <morphisms> are the 2-morphisms. Horizontal <composition in a category> is $\otimes$, the identity 1-morphism is $I$, and the <associator> and <unitors> give the weak associativity and unit constraints. Vertical <composition in a category> is ordinary <composition in a category> in $\mathcal C$; the interchange law follows because $\otimes$ is a <bifunctor>. The bicategory pentagon and triangle axioms are exactly the two equations above. Conversely the sole hom-category of any one-object <bicategory> gives these data, with the convention that horizontal <composition in a category> uses the indicated ordering of tensor factors.

For the <category of sets>, take the <monoidal tensor product> to be the <Cartesian product>, $A\otimes B=A\times B$, with unit a singleton $1$. On <morphisms> use $(f\times g)(a,b)=(f(a),g(b))$. Define the <associator> by $((a,b),c)\mapsto(a,(b,c))$ and the <unitors> by $(*,a)\mapsto a$ and $(a,*)\mapsto a$. These are <natural isomorphisms>, with inverses obtained by rebracketing or adjoining $*$. Both paths in the pentagon send $(((a,b),c),d)$ to $(a,(b,(c,d)))$, and both paths in the triangle send $((a,*),b)$ to $(a,b)$. This proves the axioms elementwise.

There is a second structure: use <disjoint union> $A\amalg B$ and unit $\varnothing$. Define the <associator> by preserving each element and its summand while changing the nested tags, and define the <unitors> by removing the empty summand. These are natural and invertible. In the pentagon, either path takes an element in any of the four summands to that same element in the same summand of $A\amalg(B\amalg(C\amalg D))$; the triangle removes an empty intermediate summand on either path. Thus both axioms hold.

These <product and coproduct monoidal structures on sets> have different units, $1$ and $\varnothing$, which are not isomorphic. They are even inequivalent as <monoidal categories>: an equivalence of the underlying <category of sets> preserves <terminal objects>, so it cannot take the singleton unit of the product structure to the empty unit of the coproduct structure. Hence **the monoidal structure on <sets> is not unique**.
