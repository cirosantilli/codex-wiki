= Solution
{parent=i}

Write the <gravitational potential> with <spherical symmetry> as $\Phi(r)$ and let $\mathbf n$ be the unit <vector> normal to a <circular orbit>, oriented in the direction of its <angular velocity>. Put $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\mathbf n$ and denote the <specific angular momentum> by $\mathbf h=h\mathbf n$. Circular force balance and the <specific orbital energy> give
$$
\Phi'(r)=r\Omega^2,\qquad h=r^2\Omega,\qquad \varepsilon=\Phi(r)+\frac12r^2\Omega^2.
$$
Differentiating along the family of <circular orbits>,
$$
\frac{d\varepsilon}{dr}=2r\Omega^2+r^2\Omega\Omega'=\Omega\frac{dh}{dr}.
$$
A change in orbital orientation contributes $h\,d\mathbf n$ to $d\mathbf h$, but $\mathbf n\cdot d\mathbf n=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{d\varepsilon=\Omega\,dh=\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf h.}
$$
This <circular-orbit energy gradient> accounts for radial and inclination changes together; <spherical symmetry> makes the orbital <energy> independent of the plane.

For either variable-mass particle, $\mathbf H=M\mathbf h$ and $E=M\varepsilon$. Thus $d\mathbf H=M\,d\mathbf h+\mathbf h\,dM$, so
$$
dE=\varepsilon\,dM+M\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf h=(\varepsilon-\Omega h)dM+\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot d\mathbf H.
$$
<Conservation of mass> and <conservation of angular momentum> impose $dM_2=-dM_1$, $d\mathbf H_2=-d\mathbf H_1$. Adding the two particle contributions proves
$$
\boxed{dE=\{(\varepsilon_1-\Omega_1h_1)-(\varepsilon_2-\Omega_2h_2)\}\,dM_1+(\boldsymbol\Omega_1-\boldsymbol\Omega_2)\cdot d\mathbf H_1.}
$$
To interpret the signs, label the inner orbit 1 and the outer orbit 2. For $f(r)=\varepsilon-\Omega h$ the first identity gives $f'=-h\Omega'$. The assumed outward decrease of <angular velocity> therefore makes $f_1<f_2$, so the mass-exchange part lowers the <energy> when $dM_1>0$: <mass> is transferred inward. In a coplanar, co-rotating pair, $\Omega_1>\Omega_2$ and an outward <angular momentum> transfer has $d\mathbf H_1=-dH\mathbf n$ with $dH>0$. Its contribution is $-(\Omega_1-\Omega_2)dH<0$.

The <vector> difference also identifies the inclination-reducing exchange. For a fixed small magnitude of admissible <angular momentum> exchange, its <energy> contribution is most negative when
$$
d\mathbf H_1=-s(\boldsymbol\Omega_1-\boldsymbol\Omega_2),\qquad s>0,
$$
by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. This gives $dE_{\rm angular}=-s|\boldsymbol\Omega_1-\boldsymbol\Omega_2|^2$. Let $q=\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2=\cos\iota$ and $H_i=|\mathbf H_i|$, taking fixed particle masses for this exchange. Projecting $d\mathbf H_i$ perpendicular to its own direction gives
$$
d\mathbf n_1=\frac{s\Omega_2}{H_1}(\mathbf n_2-q\mathbf n_1),\qquad d\mathbf n_2=\frac{s\Omega_1}{H_2}(\mathbf n_1-q\mathbf n_2).
$$
Therefore
$$
dq=s(1-q^2)\left(\frac{\Omega_2}{H_1}+\frac{\Omega_1}{H_2}\right)>0\qquad(0<\iota<\pi).
$$
The downhill exchange reduces the relative <orbital inclination>. Its inner <angular momentum> component is $\mathbf n_1\cdot d\mathbf H_1=-s(\Omega_1-\Omega_2q)<0$; in the aligned limit the outer orbit receives that <angular momentum> directly. For strongly inclined orbits, part of the exchange instead cancels differently directed angular momenta as the planes align. Together these gradients explain the tendencies toward inward <mass> transfer, outward <angular momentum> transport and coplanarity.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $M_*$ be the central <mass>, so $\Omega^2=GM_*/r^3$ and the <specific orbital energy> is $\varepsilon=-GM_*/(2r)$. Define the inward <accretion rate> $\dot M>0$. The rate at which the gas acquires binding <energy> in moving from $b$ to $a$ is
$$
\boxed{B_{a,b}=\dot M\{\varepsilon(b)-\varepsilon(a)\}=\frac{GM_*\dot M}{2}\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right).}
$$
The vertically integrated viscous heating per unit horizontal area, including the entire column, is $\bar\nu\Sigma(r\Omega')^2$. Since $r\Omega'=-3\Omega/2$, the dissipated power in the annulus is
$$
D_{a,b}=\int_a^b2\pi r\,\bar\nu\Sigma\frac94\Omega^2\,dr=\frac{3GM_*\dot M}{2}\int_a^b\frac1{r^2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{r_{\rm in}}r}\right)dr.
$$
Here the column <integral> already includes dissipation associated with both disk faces; adding another factor of two would double-count it. Performing the elementary <integrals> gives
$$
\boxed{D_{a,b}=GM_*\dot M\left[\frac32\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right)-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}}\left(a^{-3/2}-b^{-3/2}\right)\right].}
$$
For the whole disk, take $a=r_{\rm in}$ and $b\to\infty$. Both expressions give
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm disk}=B_{\rm disk}=\frac{GM_*\dot M}{2r_{\rm in}}.}
$$
If $a\gg r_{\rm in}$, the bracket $1-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}/r}$ is uniformly close to one throughout the annulus. Comparing the positive <integrals> shows
$$
\frac{D_{a,b}}{B_{a,b}}=3\left[1+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{r_{\rm in}}a}\right)\right],
$$
with the correction negative. Thus **far from the inner edge the <viscous dissipation> is approximately three times the local binding-energy acquisition**.

There is no violation of <conservation of energy> because the <viscous torque in an accretion disk> also transports mechanical <energy>. The outward <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is
$$
\mathcal G=-2\pi\bar\nu\Sigma r^3\Omega'=\dot M\big(\sqrt{GM_*r}-\sqrt{GM_*r_{\rm in}}\big),
$$
and its outward <energy> flux is $\Omega\mathcal G=GM_*\dot M[r^{-1}-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}}r^{-3/2}]$. The explicit expressions satisfy
$$
\boxed{D_{a,b}=B_{a,b}+\Omega(a)\mathcal G(a)-\Omega(b)\mathcal G(b).}
$$
<Energy> transported from the inner disk supplies the extra dissipation at larger radii. The torque power vanishes at the <zero-torque inner boundary condition> and tends to zero at infinity, leaving global balance. This is <torque transport of energy in a steady accretion disk>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> equation balances the gradient of the total <pressure> against gravity. In a <thin disk> with <Keplerian rotation>, expanding the central gravitational field about the midplane gives vertical acceleration $-\Omega^2z$, so $p_z=-\rho\Omega^2z$. Disk self-gravity is neglected in this approximation.

The flux-gradient equation is local thermal balance: the upward <radiative flux> increases with height because <differential rotation> dissipates <energy>. For a Newtonian effective <dynamic viscosity> $\mu$, the volumetric heating rate is $\mu(r\Omega')^2$, which becomes $9\mu\Omega^2/4$ for <Keplerian rotation>. The symbol $\mu$ here is <dynamic viscosity>, not <kinematic viscosity>; the latter is $\nu=\mu/\rho$.

The <radiative diffusion> equation applies to an <optically thick medium> in <local thermodynamic equilibrium>. Its <diffusion> coefficient is $c/(3\kappa\rho)$, and the equilibrium radiation <energy density> is $u_{\rm rad}=4\sigma T^4/c$. Hence
$$
F=-\frac{c}{3\kappa\rho}\frac{du_{\rm rad}}{dz}=-\frac{16\sigma T^3}{3\kappa\rho}\frac{dT}{dz}.
$$
The minus sign means <energy> flows down the <temperature> gradient. Here $\sigma$ is the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>, $\kappa$ the <opacity> per unit <mass>, and $c$ the <speed of light>. Constant <Thomson scattering> <opacity> is the adopted transport approximation; <local thermodynamic equilibrium> is an additional assumption behind the radiation <equation of state>.

Finally the <equation of state> adds <ideal gas> <gas pressure> $p_g=\rho kT/(\mu_m m_H)$ and isotropic <radiation pressure> $p_r=u_{\rm rad}/3=4\sigma T^4/(3c)$. The <Boltzmann constant> is $k$, and $\mu_m m_H$ is the mean <mass> per gas particle. Both contributions supply the total <pressure> entering vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the limiting model with negligible <gas pressure>, $p=p_r=4\sigma T^4/(3c)$. Its gradient is $p_z=16\sigma T^3T_z/(3c)$. Combining <radiative diffusion> and <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives
$$
F=-\frac{c}{\kappa\rho}p_z=\frac{c\Omega^2}{\kappa}z.
$$
Because $\kappa$ and $\Omega$ are constant with height at the fixed disk radius,
$$
\frac{dF}{dz}=\frac{c\Omega^2}{\kappa}.
$$
Equating this to the viscous heating rate $9\mu\Omega^2/4$ forces
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{4c}{9\kappa}.}
$$
This proves <constant viscosity in radiation-supported vertical balance>. It fixes the local effective <dynamic viscosity> uniquely, independent of height, <surface density> and radius within this ideal limiting model. It does not fix a height-independent <kinematic viscosity>: $\nu(z)=4c/(9\kappa\rho(z))$ still depends on the local <mass density>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Set $\mathcal R=k/(\mu_m m_H)$, so $p_g=\mathcal R\rho T$ and the prescribed <dynamic viscosity> is $\mu=\alpha\mathcal R\rho T/\Omega$. Keplerian shear and <radiative diffusion> then give
$$
F_z=\frac94\alpha\mathcal R\Omega\rho T,\qquad F=-\frac{4\sigma}{3\kappa\rho}(T^4)_z.
$$
Differentiating the second expression, equating it to the first and dividing by $\rho$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac1\rho\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac1\rho\frac{dT^4}{dz}\right)+A\Omega T=0,\qquad A=\frac{27\alpha\kappa\mathcal R}{16\sigma}=\frac{27\alpha\kappa k}{16\sigma\mu_m m_H}.}
$$
This reduction uses the gas-pressure <viscosity> closure, not an assumption that <gas pressure> dominates the hydrostatic support.

For $z\ge0$, introduce the <vertical mass coordinate of a disk>
$$
m(z)=\int_0^z\rho(z')\,dz',\qquad \frac{d}{dm}=\frac1\rho\frac{d}{dz}.
$$
Let $\Sigma=\int_{-z_s}^{z_s}\rho\,dz$ be the full <surface density> of a reflection-symmetric disk. The upper surface is at $m_s=\Sigma/2$. With $\zeta=2m/\Sigma$, the <temperature> equation becomes
$$
\frac4{\Sigma^2}\frac{d^2T^4}{d\zeta^2}+A\Omega T=0.
$$
Write $T=T_*t(\zeta)$ and choose
$$
\boxed{T_*^3=\frac{A\Omega\Sigma^2}4=\frac{27\alpha\kappa\mathcal R\Omega\Sigma^2}{64\sigma}.}
$$
The equation reduces exactly to $(t^4)''+t=0$. Midplane symmetry gives $t'(0)=0$, and the zero surface <temperature> gives $t(1)=0$. The stipulated unique positive nontrivial dimensionless solution therefore applies at every radius and <surface density>, with all physical dependence contained in $T_*$ and the mass-coordinate scaling. A zero <temperature> at the ideal surface does not mean zero escaping flux: $F=-(4\sigma/(3\kappa))\partial_mT^4$ can have a finite nonzero surface value.

The required <viscosity> is the <density-weighted mean kinematic viscosity>, not an unweighted vertical mean. Since $\rho\nu=\mu$,
$$
\bar\nu=\frac1\Sigma\int_{-z_s}^{z_s}\mu\,dz=\frac{2\alpha\mathcal R}{\Omega\Sigma}\int_0^{\Sigma/2}T(m)\,dm.
$$
Substituting the dimensionless solution gives, with $I_t=\int_0^1t(\zeta)\,d\zeta$,
$$
\boxed{\bar\nu=\frac{\alpha\mathcal R}{\Omega}\left(\frac{A\Omega\Sigma^2}4\right)^{1/3}I_t=\frac{3I_t}{4}\left(\frac{\alpha^4\kappa\mathcal R^4}{\sigma}\right)^{1/3}\Sigma^{2/3}\Omega^{-2/3}.}
$$
For central <mass> $M_*$, $\Omega=(GM_*/r^3)^{1/2}$, so the requested explicit radius and surface-density dependence is
$$
\boxed{\bar\nu(r,\Sigma)=\frac{3I_t}{4}\left(\frac{\alpha^4\kappa k^4}{\sigma(\mu_m m_H)^4GM_*}\right)^{1/3}r\Sigma^{2/3}.}
$$
The universal <integral> need not be evaluated. No separate solution for $\rho(z)$ is needed to find this mean: the mass-coordinate transformation removes it from both the <temperature> equation and the <viscosity> <integral>. The result is the <gas-pressure viscosity closure with mixed pressure support>.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Choose a reference <circular orbit> at radius $r_0$, rotate the frame with $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega(r_0)\mathbf e_z$, and introduce local Cartesian coordinates $x=r-r_0$, $y=r_0(\phi-\Omega t)$ and vertical coordinate $z$. On scales small compared with $r_0$, neglect curvature of the coordinate directions but retain the leading <differential rotation>. The background <velocity> relative to the rotating frame is
$$
\mathbf u_0=r_0\Omega'(r_0)x\mathbf e_y=-2Ax\mathbf e_y,\qquad A=-\frac12r_0\Omega'(r_0).
$$
Thus $A=3\Omega/4$ for a <Keplerian disk>. The <shearing sheet> retains <Coriolis acceleration> and the linear tidal acceleration. The radial <shearing-sheet tidal potential> is $-2\Omega Ax^2$, whose force $4\Omega Ax\mathbf e_x$ balances the background <Coriolis acceleration>. If vertical tidal gravity is included, its equilibrium contribution is balanced by a background <pressure> $p_0(z)$ and disappears when that equilibrium is subtracted.

Write the total <velocity> as $\mathbf u=\mathbf u_0+\mathbf v$. In the rotating-frame <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation>, background advection is $(\mathbf u_0\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v=-2Ax\partial_y\mathbf v$ and advection of the background by the perturbation is $(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u_0=-2Av_x\mathbf e_y$. The uniform-viscosity <diffusion> of the linear background shear is zero. For a solenoidal <magnetic field> the <Lorentz force> per unit <mass> is
$$
\frac{(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B}{\mu_0\rho}=\frac{\mathbf B\cdot\nabla\mathbf B}{\mu_0\rho}-\nabla\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0\rho}.
$$
At constant <density>, absorb the magnetic-pressure gradient into $\psi=(p-p_0)/\rho+B^2/(2\mu_0\rho)$. Subtracting the background force balance then gives the stated momentum equation, with the remaining nonlinear advection $\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\mathbf v$, <Coriolis acceleration> $2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v$, <magnetic tension> and viscous <diffusion>.

For constant finite <electrical conductivity> $\sigma_e$, put $\eta=(\mu_0\sigma_e)^{-1}$. The <resistive induction equation> with uniform <magnetic diffusivity> is $\partial_t\mathbf B+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf B=\mathbf B\cdot\nabla\mathbf u+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf B$. Substituting the background shear supplies the term $-2AB_x\mathbf e_y$ and the same background-advection operator as in the momentum equation. The two further conditions are
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0,\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0.}
$$
They express <incompressibility> and the absence of <magnetic monopoles>, respectively.

Now take real <plane waves> with common phase $\vartheta=\mathbf k(t)\cdot\mathbf x$ and real <wavevector>. The solenoidal conditions become
$$
\mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf v}=0,\qquad \mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf B}=0.
$$
Every field depends on position only through $\vartheta$. Hence for either transverse <vector> field $\mathbf P$ and either wave field $\mathbf Q$, $(\mathbf P\cdot\nabla)\mathbf Q=(\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf k)\partial_\vartheta\mathbf Q=0$. This proves that all the displayed quadratic advection and tension terms vanish pointwise, including products involving conjugate <amplitudes>. These waves are therefore exact solutions at finite <amplitude>, not merely a <linearization>.

Acting with $D_0=\partial_t-2Ax\partial_y$ on the phase yields
$$
D_0\vartheta=(\dot k_x-2Ak_y)x+\dot k_y y+\dot k_z z.
$$
Canceling its position-dependent part requires
$$
\boxed{\dot k_x=2Ak_y,\qquad\dot k_y=\dot k_z=0,\qquad k_x(t)=k_{x0}+2Ak_yt.}
$$
The remaining <amplitude> equations are
$$
\boxed{\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf v}}-2A\widetilde v_x\mathbf e_y+2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\widetilde{\mathbf v}=-i\mathbf k\widetilde\psi-\nu k^2\widetilde{\mathbf v},\qquad\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf B}}=-2A\widetilde B_x\mathbf e_y-\eta k^2\widetilde{\mathbf B}.}
$$
Differentiating the <velocity> constraint determines the <pressure> <amplitude>:
$$
i k^2\widetilde\psi=4Ak_y\widetilde v_x-2\mathbf k\cdot(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\widetilde{\mathbf v}).
$$
The <resistive induction equation> preserves its constraint because $\dot{\mathbf k}\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf B}=2Ak_y\widetilde B_x$ cancels the corresponding shear term in $\mathbf k\cdot\dot{\widetilde{\mathbf B}}$. The specified <magnetic field> has no spatially uniform part. Thus there is no background <magnetic field> producing an additional linear magnetic-tension coupling. The scalar $\psi$ is the modified <pressure>; the physical <pressure> can include a constant and a second spatial harmonic from $-B^2/(2\mu_0)$ while $\psi$ retains the stipulated plane-wave form. This is an exact <zero-mean magnetic shearing wave>.

For decay, take the real part of the <scalar product> of the velocity-amplitude equation with its <complex conjugate>. <Pressure> does no work because $\mathbf k\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf v}=0$, and the real part of Coriolis work is zero. Therefore
$$
\frac{d}{dt}|\widetilde{\mathbf v}|^2=4A\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde v_x\widetilde v_y^*)-2\nu k^2|\widetilde{\mathbf v}|^2\le(2|A|-2\nu k^2)|\widetilde{\mathbf v}|^2.
$$
The <resistive induction equation> similarly gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}|\widetilde{\mathbf B}|^2=-4A\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde B_x\widetilde B_y^*)-2\eta k^2|\widetilde{\mathbf B}|^2\le(2|A|-2\eta k^2)|\widetilde{\mathbf B}|^2,
$$
using $2|u_xu_y|\le|\mathbf u|^2$. Integrating these differential inequalities, define
$$
J(t)=\int_0^t k(s)^2\,ds=(k_{x0}^2+k_y^2+k_z^2)t+2Ak_{x0}k_yt^2+\frac43A^2k_y^2t^3.
$$
Then
$$
|\widetilde{\mathbf v}(t)|^2\le|\widetilde{\mathbf v}(0)|^2e^{2|A|t-2\nu J(t)},\qquad |\widetilde{\mathbf B}(t)|^2\le|\widetilde{\mathbf B}(0)|^2e^{2|A|t-2\eta J(t)}.
$$
A non-axisymmetric wave has $k_y\ne0$. For nonzero differential shear and positive $\nu,\eta$, the negative cubic terms dominate the linear upper bound on shear work. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\mathbf v}(t)\longrightarrow0,\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf B}(t)\longrightarrow0\quad(t\to\infty).}
$$
This <cubic-exponent decay of a nonaxisymmetric shearing wave> allows transient <energy> growth before the winding to short radial wavelengths makes <diffusion> dominant. The magnetic <amplitudes> can also be written explicitly as
$$
\widetilde{\mathbf B}(t)=e^{-\eta J(t)}\big(\widetilde B_x(0),\ \widetilde B_y(0)-2At\widetilde B_x(0),\ \widetilde B_z(0)\big),
$$
which confirms the damping independently. Positivity of the dissipative coefficients is necessary: with $k_z=0$, an inviscid vertical <velocity> <amplitude> can remain constant, and an ideal vertical magnetic <amplitude> can remain constant when $\eta=0$. The asserted decay applies to the viscous, finitely conducting model; taking an ideal limit before the long-time limit changes the conclusion.
