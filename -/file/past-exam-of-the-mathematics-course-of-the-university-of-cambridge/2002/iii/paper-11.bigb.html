= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the pair-design convention: each pair of distinct points must lie in exactly one <Steiner block> of size $k$. With this convention the printed endpoint $k=1$ is false for every $v\geq2$, since a singleton contains no pair. Thus the intended assertion requires $k\geq2$. For $k=2$, take all pairs as <Steiner blocks> for any $v\geq2$. We now give a finite-field construction for every fixed $k\geq3$, without assuming the desired design-existence theorem.

Put $m=\binom{k}{2}$ and take an odd <prime> power $q\equiv1\pmod{2m}$. By cyclicity of $\mathbb F_q^\times$, it has a <subgroup> $H$ of index $m$; its order $(q-1)/m$ is even, so $-1\in H$. We will find a $k$-set $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_k\}$ such that its $m$ unordered pair differences occupy the $m$ distinct <cyclotomic classes of a finite field> modulo $H$. The following argument supplies that set for every sufficiently large compatible $q$.

First establish the required two-class intersection estimate by elementary <character> sums. Let $\chi$ be a <multiplicative character of a finite field> of order $m$, with $\chi(\gamma)=\zeta_m$ for a generator $\gamma$. Extend every power $\chi^j$, including the trivial <character>, by zero at zero. For $C_i=\gamma^iH$,
$$
\mathbf1_{C_i}(x)=\frac1m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\zeta_m^{-ij}\chi^j(x).
$$
For a nontrivial additive <character> $\psi$ of $\mathbb F_q$ and a nontrivial multiplicative <character> $\eta$, its <Gauss sum of a finite-field character> satisfies
$$
|G(\eta)|^2
=\sum_{t\ne0}\eta(t)\sum_{y\ne0}\psi((t-1)y)
=(q-1)-\sum_{t\ne0,1}\eta(t)=q.
$$
Here the inner sum equals $q-1$ for $t=1$ and $-1$ otherwise, by additive-character <orthogonality>; multiplicative-character <orthogonality> gives the last equality. Such a nontrivial additive <character> exists by identifying the additive <group> with a <vector space> over its <prime field> and choosing a nonzero <linear functional> into that <prime field>.

If $\eta,\xi,\eta\xi$ are nontrivial, substituting $x=uz$, $y=(1-u)z$ in the product of their Gauss sums proves
$$
G(\eta)G(\xi)=J(\eta,\xi)G(\eta\xi),\qquad
J(\eta,\xi)=\sum_u\eta(u)\xi(1-u).
$$
The terms with $x+y=0$ sum to zero because $\eta\xi$ is nontrivial. Hence $|J|=\sqrt q$. If exactly one <character> is trivial, subtracting the excluded zero and one terms gives absolute value one. If both are nontrivial but their product is trivial, the substitution $u/(1-u)$ gives $J(\eta,\eta^{-1})=-\eta(-1)$, again of absolute value one. If both are trivial, the sum is $q-2$. Expanding the two cyclotomic indicators and scaling a nonzero translate to one therefore gives, uniformly in $i,j$ and $t\ne0$,
$$
|C_i\cap(t+C_j)|=\frac{q}{m^2}+O_m(\sqrt q).
$$
All constants depend only on the fixed $m$.

To extend a prescribed difference pattern on $r$ coordinates, fix desired classes $D_1,\ldots,D_r$ for the new differences $x-a_i$ and set
$$
M(a)=\#\{x:x-a_i\in D_i\text{ for all }i\},\qquad a\in\mathbb F_q^r.
$$
Average over all $q^r$ tuples, allowing repeated coordinates just for this average. With $f=(q-1)/m$, counting choices for each $a_i$ independently gives
$$
\mathbb EM=q(f/q)^r=q/m^r+O_{m,r}(1).
$$
For the second moment, sum over the two potential extensions $x,y$. The $x=y$ terms contribute $qf^r/q^r$. For $x\ne y$, the choices of $a_i$ number $q/m^2+O_m(\sqrt q)$ each, by the preceding intersection estimate. Thus
$$
\mathbb EM^2=q^2/m^{2r}+O_{m,r}(q^{3/2}),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}M=O_{m,r}(q^{3/2}).
$$
The <Chebyshev inequality> shows that the proportion of tuples with $M(a)<q/(2m^r)$ is $O_{m,r}(q^{-1/2})$. Inductively, any fixed compatible difference pattern on $r$ distinct coordinates is realized by at least $c_rq^r$ tuples, with $c_r>0$ independent of sufficiently large $q$. The assertion starts with $r=1$. Almost all tuples are outside the exceptional set, so at least half the realizing tuples have at least $q/(2m^r)$ extensions. Each extension is distinct from the old coordinates because its differences belong to nonzero classes. This proves the assertion for $r+1$. This <cyclotomic pattern extension by a second-moment bound> avoids the false assumption that every partial choice can be extended.

Prescribe a different one of the $m$ <cosets> to each pair $a_j-a_i$, $i<j$. Since $-1\in H$, changing an orientation does not change its <coset>, so the prescriptions are compatible. The preceding induction supplies $A$.

Choose $S\subset H$ containing one element from each pair $\{h,-h\}$. Form the <Steiner blocks>
$$
\mathcal B=\{sA+t:s\in S,\ t\in\mathbb F_q\}.
$$
For two distinct points $x,y$, their difference identifies exactly one unordered pair $\{a_i,a_j\}$ of $A$ having the same $H$-coset. Exactly one of $(x-y)/(a_i-a_j)$ and its negative lies in $S$. Choose the orientation $a,b$ of that pair making $s=(x-y)/(a-b)\in S$, and then set $t=x-sa$. This yields $x=sa+t$, $y=sb+t$. Both the oriented base pair and $(s,t)$ are unique, so precisely one <Steiner block> contains the pair. In particular different <Steiner block> parameters cannot produce repeated <Steiner blocks>. Equivalently, the <Steiner blocks> $sA$ form a <difference family for a Steiner 2-design>, and translation gives the <cyclotomic construction of a Steiner 2-design>.

There are infinitely many suitable <field> orders: choose one odd <prime> $\ell$ not dividing $2m$, and take $q=\ell^{\varphi(2m)u}$ for <integers> $u\geq1$. Euler's congruence gives $q\equiv1\pmod{2m}$, and these orders become arbitrarily large. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{For every }k\geq2,\text{ there are }2\text{-}(v,k,1)\text{ designs for infinitely many }v.}
$$
The qualification at $k=1$ is necessary for the standard pair-design definition.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At any <vertex>, each <complete bipartite graph> $K_{k,k}$ containing it contributes exactly $k$ incident <edges>. Since the copies partition all incident <edges> of the <complete graph>, $n-1$ is a multiple of $k$. In particular $\gcd(n,k)=1$.

If there are $b$ copies, counting their <edges> gives
$$
\frac{n(n-1)}2=bk^2,\qquad n(n-1)=2bk^2.
$$
Thus $k^2\mid n(n-1)$. Since $n$ is coprime to $k^2$, it can be cancelled in this divisibility, yielding
$$
\boxed{k^2\mid n-1.}
$$
If $n=1$, the empty partition is consistent with the same conclusion, since every positive <integer> divides zero.

Let $G$ be a finite undirected $d$-regular <graph> on $N$ <vertices>, with <adjacency matrix> $A$ and normalized <random walk> matrix $P=A/d$. Define <edge> expansion and normalized conductance by
$$
h(G)=\min_{0<|S|\leq N/2}\frac{e(S,S^c)}{|S|},\qquad \Phi(G)=h(G)/d.
$$
An <expander family> has orders tending to infinity, uniformly bounded maximum degree, and a uniform positive lower bound on $h$. For a <regular graph> the constant vector is an <eigenvector> of $P$ with <eigenvalue> one. Write its <eigenvalues> as $1=\mu_1\geq\mu_2\geq\cdots\geq\mu_N\geq-1$, and let $\delta=1-\mu_2$. This <spectral gap> measures the energetic cost of departing from a constant function.

For every mean-zero vector $f$, the <spectral theorem> and the <graph>'s energy identity give
$$
\frac1d\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}(f(u)-f(v))^2
=\langle f,(I-P)f\rangle\geq\delta\|f\|_2^2.
$$
Apply this to $f=\mathbf1_S-|S|\mathbf1/N$. Its norm squared is $|S|(1-|S|/N)$ and its energy is $e(S,S^c)/d$. Therefore
$$
e(S,S^c)\geq d\delta |S|(1-|S|/N),\qquad
\boxed{\Phi(G)\geq\delta/2.}
$$
Thus a uniform <spectral gap> gives uniform <edge> expansion when $d$ is fixed. This also bounds exterior <vertex> expansion: each exterior <vertex> accounts for at most $d$ boundary <edges>, so $|N(S)\setminus S|\geq e(S,S^c)/d$.

For the converse, take a mean-zero <eigenvector> $f$ for $\mu_2$ and choose its sign so that its positive support has at most $N/2$ <vertices>. Put $g=f^+$, which is nonzero. Truncation decreases energy in the following precise sense: for any two real numbers $a,b$,
$$
(a^+-b^+)^2\leq(a^+-b^+)(a-b).
$$
Summing over <edges> yields $\langle g,(I-P)g\rangle\leq\langle g,(I-P)f\rangle=\delta\|g\|_2^2$. For the threshold sets $S_t=\{v:g(v)^2>t\}$, every nonempty set lies in that small positive support. Integrating its boundary count gives the layer-cake identity
$$
\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E}|g(u)^2-g(v)^2|
=\int_0^\infty e(S_t,S_t^c)\,dt\geq h(G)\|g\|_2^2.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> bounds the left side by
$$
\left(\sum_E(g(u)-g(v))^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_E(g(u)+g(v))^2\right)^{1/2}
\leq d\sqrt{2\delta}\,\|g\|_2^2,
$$
because the first sum is at most $d\delta\|g\|_2^2$ and the second at most $2d\|g\|_2^2$. Cancelling proves the <Cheeger inequality> in both directions:
$$
\boxed{\frac{1-\mu_2}{2}\leq\Phi(G)\leq\sqrt{2(1-\mu_2)}.}
$$
Hence bounded-degree regular families have uniform <edge> expansion exactly when their second-largest normalized <eigenvalue> stays uniformly below one.

Absolute <eigenvalues> give additional information. Put $\rho=\max_{i\geq2}|\mu_i|$. Centering the indicators of $S,T$ and applying the operator-norm bound on the mean-zero subspace proves the <expander mixing lemma>:
$$
\left|e(S,T)-\frac{d|S||T|}{N}\right|
\leq d\rho\sqrt{|S|(1-|S|/N)|T|(1-|T|/N)}.
$$
Here $e(S,T)=\mathbf1_S^TA\mathbf1_T$, so internal <edges> are counted as oriented incidences. A small $\rho$ makes <edge> counts resemble uniformly distributed <edges>. It also gives rapid <random walk> mixing: starting at a <vertex>, $P^t$ contracts the mean-zero component of its point mass by at most $\rho^t$ in <Euclidean norm>; <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> then gives total variation distance at most $\tfrac12\sqrt N\rho^t$ from the uniform distribution. A <bipartite graph> has <eigenvalue> $-1$ and its non-lazy walk alternates sides, even if it has good <edge> expansion. The lazy walk $(I+P)/2$ has nonconstant <eigenvalues> between zero and $1-\delta/2$, resolving this periodicity. It is important to distinguish the gap $1-\mu_2$ controlling cuts from the absolute bound controlling non-lazy mixing.

Here is a fully specified deterministic construction of an infinite family. Its degree is large but fixed. Set
$$
d=2^{16},\qquad D=d^2=2^{32},\qquad W=\mathbb F_2^{64},\quad |W|=D^2.
$$
Choose the lexicographically first ordered list $a_1,\ldots,a_d\in W$ satisfying
$$
\left|\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d(-1)^{u\cdot a_j}\right|\leq\frac1{10}\qquad\text{for every }u\in W\setminus\{0\}.
$$
This finite deterministic search terminates. To prove existence, choose the list uniformly and independently. For each nonzero $u$, its signs are independent unbiased signs; the exponential-moment estimate $\cosh t\leq e^{t^2/2}$ and <Markov inequality> give an exceptional <probability> at most $2e^{-d/200}$. A <union bound> gives total failure <probability> at most
$$
2(2^{64}-1)e^{-2^{16}/200}<1.
$$
Thus at least one qualifying list exists. Fix it once and for all.

Let $H$ be the <Cayley graph> on $W$ whose $j$th neighbor of $x$ is $x+a_j$. It is an undirected $d$-regular port-labeled <graph>, allowing repeated <edges> and loops in this intermediate construction. Its <characters> $x\mapsto(-1)^{u\cdot x}$ form an <orthogonal> <eigenbasis>, with the displayed averages as normalized <eigenvalues>. Therefore $\rho(H)\leq1/10$. Each loop counts as one transition port; degrees here mean row sums of the <adjacency matrix>.

We next define the <zig-zag product> and prove the bound used for iteration. A port labeling of a $D_0$-regular undirected <graph> $F$ gives an involution $\operatorname{Rot}_F(v,a)=(w,b)$, recording the endpoint and reverse port. If a $d$-regular <graph> $H_0$ has <vertex> set $[D_0]$, the product has <vertices> $(v,a)\in V(F)\times[D_0]$. For each ordered pair of small-graph ports, move from $a$ to $a'$ in $H_0$, follow the big <edge> $\operatorname{Rot}_F(v,a')=(w,b')$, and move from $b'$ to $b$ in $H_0$. The endpoint is $(w,b)$; reversing the steps reverses the two small-graph port labels. The product is undirected, has $|V(F)|D_0$ <vertices>, and has degree $d^2$.

Write $B=I\otimes P_{H_0}$ and let $R$ be the permutation matrix of $\operatorname{Rot}_F$. The normalized product operator is $BRB$. For a mean-zero unit vector, decompose $f=u+v$ into its cloud-constant part and its cloud-orthogonal part. Then $Bu=u$, $\|Bv\|\leq\rho(H_0)\|v\|$, and $\|R\|=1$. The compression of $R$ to cloud-constant vectors is exactly $P_F$, so $|\langle u,Ru\rangle|\leq\rho(F)\|u\|^2$. Expanding the quadratic form and applying <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|\langle f,BRBf\rangle|
\leq\rho(F)\|u\|^2+2\rho(H_0)\|u\|\|v\|+\rho(H_0)^2\|v\|^2
\leq\rho(F)+2\rho(H_0)+\rho(H_0)^2.
$$
The operator is symmetric, so its mean-zero <operator norm> is bounded by this same quantity. No unproved product spectral estimate is needed.

Start with the loop-inclusive <complete graph> $G_0$ on $D$ <vertices>, whose normalized matrix is $J/D$ and whose nonconstant <eigenvalues> are all zero. Given a $D$-regular $G_t$, its two-step <graph> $G_t^2$ has degree $D^2$ and normalized operator $P_{G_t}^2$. Label a two-step <edge> by its ordered pair of ports; reversal reverses the path and its reverse ports. These $D^2$ labels are the <vertex> set of the fixed <graph> $H$. Define
$$
G_{t+1}=G_t^2\mathbin{\mathrm{zigzag}}H.
$$
It has degree $d^2=D$, and its number of <vertices> is $N_{t+1}=N_tD^2$, hence $N_t=D^{2t+1}$. By the proved bound,
$$
\rho(G_{t+1})\leq\rho(G_t)^2+\frac{21}{100}.
$$
Starting from zero, induction gives $\rho(G_t)\leq1/2$, since $(1/2)^2+21/100=46/100<1/2$. The cut-energy argument therefore gives multiplicity-counted <edge> expansion at least $D/4$ for every $G_t$.

To obtain ordinary simple <graphs>, delete loops and merge parallel <edges>. The maximum degree is at most $D$, and any one unordered <vertex> pair had multiplicity at most $D$. Thus every cut retains at least $1/D$ of its multiplicity-counted boundary, giving
$$
\boxed{|V(G_t)|=D^{2t+1}\longrightarrow\infty,\qquad \Delta\leq D,\qquad h\geq1/4.}
$$
These simple <graphs> form an <expander family>. Every choice is deterministic: the finite base search is fixed independently of $t$, and the recursive <edge> rules determine all subsequent adjacency lists. Once that base is fixed, adjacency lists can be generated successively using the previous lists, with a constant amount of work per output <edge>. This is <squaring and zig-zag iteration yields bounded-degree expanders>. The spectral argument supplies the uniform expansion guarantee and explains why squaring improves the <eigenvalues> while the product restores a constant degree.

The <coefficient> form of the <Combinatorial Nullstellensatz> is as follows. Let $F$ be a <field>, let $P\in F[x_1,\ldots,x_r]$ have total degree at most $t_1+\cdots+t_r$, and suppose the <coefficient> of $x_1^{t_1}\cdots x_r^{t_r}$ is nonzero. For finite <subsets> $S_i\subset F$ with $|S_i|>t_i$, there is a point of $S_1\times\cdots\times S_r$ where $P$ is nonzero.

For completeness, restrict to $|S_i|=t_i+1$. The <coefficient> identity is
$$
[x_1^{t_1}\cdots x_r^{t_r}]P
=\sum_{a_i\in S_i}\frac{P(a_1,\ldots,a_r)}{\prod_i\prod_{b\in S_i\setminus\{a_i\}}(a_i-b)}.
$$
By <Lagrange interpolation>, the one-variable weighted sum on the right kills powers below $t_i$ and equals one on the power $t_i$. Any <monomial> of total degree at most $\sum t_i$, other than the specified one, has some exponent below $t_i$ and therefore contributes zero; the specified <monomial> contributes its <coefficient>. Every denominator is nonzero in the <field>. Hence a nonzero <coefficient> forces a nonzero grid value. The two applications below use this <coefficient> form.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Choose a fixed <prime> $p\geq k$, and put $s=2p-2$. We first derive the <polynomial> step: a <graph> $H$ on $N$ <vertices> and $M>(p-1)N$ <edges>, with maximum degree at most $2p-1$, contains a nonempty $p$-regular subgraph. Use a Boolean variable $x_e$ for each <edge> and work over $\mathbb F_p$. Define
$$
R(x)=\prod_{v\in V(H)}\left[1-\left(\sum_{e\ni v}x_e\right)^{p-1}\right]-\prod_{e\in E(H)}(1-x_e).
$$
The first product has degree at most $(p-1)N<M$, while the full squarefree <monomial> $\prod_e x_e$ has <coefficient> $(-1)^{M+1}\ne0$ in the second term with its minus sign. The <Combinatorial Nullstellensatz>, with every grid $\{0,1\}$, gives a nonzero value of $R$. Since $R(0)=0$, this is a nonempty <edge> selection. The second product is then zero, so the first is nonzero. <Fermat's little theorem> makes each selected <vertex> degree zero modulo $p$. The maximum-degree bound implies that every positive selected degree is precisely $p$. This proves the <prime-regular subgraph from Boolean polynomial constraints>.

We next turn density into an almost-regular bipartite subgraph, keeping the logarithmic loss explicit. Let the original <graph> have $n\geq2$ <vertices> and $m>0$ <edges>. Some bipartition retains at least $m/2$ <edges>: choose the sides independently with <probability> $1/2$ and average the number crossing. Its average degree is at least $m/n$. Repeatedly delete a <vertex> of degree at most half the current average degree. Such a deletion cannot lower that average, since
$$
\frac{2(e-d(v))}{N-1}\geq\frac{2e}{N}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d(v)\leq e/N.
$$
The process cannot delete the last positive-density <graph>, because the maintained average is positive. At termination the minimum degree is an <integer> $\sigma>m/(2n)$.

Choose the larger side $A$ of this remaining <bipartite graph>, with opposite side $B$. Keep exactly $\sigma$ <edges> at each <vertex> of $A$; choices at different <vertices> of $A$ do not conflict. Remove any now-isolated <vertices> of $B$. Thus $|A|\geq|B|$, and every <vertex> of $A$ has degree $\sigma$.

Such a half-regular <graph> contains a half-regular restriction with a <perfect matching>. Choose a minimal nonempty $X\subseteq A$ satisfying $|N(X)|\leq|X|$, which exists because the whole side $A$ satisfies this inequality. In fact equality holds: if it were strict, deleting a <vertex> of $X$ would retain the inequality, contradicting minimality; the singleton case has at least one neighbor. Every proper <subset> $Y\subset X$ satisfies $|N(Y)|\geq|Y|$. <Hall's marriage theorem> therefore supplies a <perfect matching> on $X\cup N(X)$. All $\sigma$ <edges> incident with each <vertex> of $X$ remain inside that restriction.

Delete this <perfect matching> and repeat the restriction argument. The left degrees decrease by one at each step. We obtain $\sigma$ edge-disjoint <perfect matchings> $M_0,\ldots,M_{\sigma-1}$ on nested nonempty <vertex> sets, with sizes
$$
n=N_{-1}\geq N_0\geq N_1\geq\cdots\geq N_{\sigma-1}\geq2.
$$
For any $i$ with $i+s\leq\sigma-1$, the union of $s+1=2p-1$ consecutive <matchings in a graph> has maximum degree at most $2p-1$ and at least $(s+1)N_{i+s}/2$ <edges> on $N_i$ <vertices>. If
$$
N_{i+s}>\frac{s}{s+1}N_i,
$$
it has more than $(p-1)N_i$ <edges>, so the <polynomial> step yields a $p$-regular bipartite subgraph. Every regular <bipartite graph> decomposes into <perfect matchings>: for a set in one part, counting incident <edges> gives Hall's inequality, so a <perfect matching> exists; remove it and repeat. Choosing $k$ of these $p$ <matchings in a graph> gives a nonempty $k$-regular subgraph. This is the <nested matching criterion for a regular subgraph>.

If no $k$-regular subgraph exists, every displayed size ratio must therefore satisfy the reverse inequality. Iterating at indices $0,s,2s,\ldots$ gives, with $r=\lfloor(\sigma-1)/s\rfloor$,
$$
2\leq N_{rs}\leq n\left(\frac{s}{s+1}\right)^r.
$$
Consequently $r\leq\log n/\log((s+1)/s)$ and
$$
\sigma\leq1+s\left(1+\frac{\log n}{\log((s+1)/s)}\right).
$$
Combining with $\sigma>m/(2n)$ proves the required <edge> bound. For example the finite constant
$$
c_k=2\left(\frac{1+s}{\log2}+\frac{s}{\log((s+1)/s)}\right),\qquad s=2p-2,\ p\geq k\text{ fixed prime},
$$
works for every $n\geq2$, and the case $n=1$ has no <edges>. Thus
$$
\boxed{m\leq c_k n\log n.}
$$
The logarithm comes from how often a nested <matching in a graph> <vertex> set can shrink by a fixed factor before becoming empty; it is not supplied by the <polynomial> step alone.

Write $a=|A|$ and $b=|B|$, so $p\geq a>b\geq1$. To cover both the unsaturated and saturated cases with the same <coefficient> calculation, set
$$
b'=\min\{b,p+2-a\},\qquad r=a+b'-2=\min\{p,a+b-2\}.
$$
Choose $B'\subseteq B$ of size $b'$. These parameters satisfy $1\leq b'<a\leq p$ and $1\leq r\leq p$.

Assume, for a contradiction, that the restricted sumset $C$ has fewer than $r$ elements. Extend it to a <subset> $D\subseteq\mathbb F_p$ of size $r-1$, and form
$$
F(x,y)=(x-y)\prod_{z\in D}(x+y-z).
$$
On $A\times B'$, this <polynomial> vanishes: when $x=y$ the first factor vanishes, and when $x\ne y$ the sum belongs to $C\subseteq D$. Its total degree is $r=(a-1)+(b'-1)$. Only the leading homogeneous part $(x-y)(x+y)^{r-1}$ contributes to the <coefficient> of $x^{a-1}y^{b'-1}$. That <coefficient> is
$$
\binom{r-1}{a-2}-\binom{r-1}{a-1}
=(a-b')\frac{(r-1)!}{(a-1)!(b'-1)!}.
$$
The equality includes $b'=1$, with an out-of-range binomial <coefficient> interpreted as zero. All factorial arguments are between zero and $p-1$, and $0<a-b'<p$, so this <coefficient> is nonzero in $\mathbb F_p$. The <Combinatorial Nullstellensatz> applied to $A\times B'$ forces a nonzero value, contradicting the vanishing just proved. Hence the <restricted sumset bound for unequal subsets of a prime field> is
$$
\boxed{|C|\geq\min\{p,|A|+|B|-2\}.}
$$
The strict size inequality is used precisely in the nonzero factor $a-b'$. The argument includes $r=p$ without dividing by $p!$, so no separate saturation assumption is hidden.
