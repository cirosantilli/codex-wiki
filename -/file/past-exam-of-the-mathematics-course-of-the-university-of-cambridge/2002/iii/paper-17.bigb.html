A <finite CW complex> can be defined without assuming separation properties in advance. Start with a finite discrete set $X^0$. Inductively form $X^k$ from $X^{k-1}\sqcup\coprod_\alpha D^k_\alpha$ by identifying each boundary point with its image under a continuous attaching map $a_\alpha:S^{k-1}\to X^{k-1}$. Only finitely many disks are attached in total, and $X=X^N$ for some finite $N$. The interiors of the disks are its cells. Its <weak topology of a CW complex> is the final topology of the characteristic maps: a subset is closed exactly when its inverse image in every characteristic disk is closed. In this finite construction it is the topology obtained by the successive quotients. Closure-finiteness is automatic because there are only finitely many cells.

Here is a direct proof of both requested topological properties, without presupposing that arbitrary attaching maps give a triangulable space. Suppose the <compact> previous stage $Y$ embeds by $e:Y\hookrightarrow\mathbb R^m$, and attach one $k$-cell, $k\ge1$. Extend the boundary coordinate map radially by
$$
G(ru)=r\,e(a(u)),\quad u\in S^{k-1},\qquad G(0)=0.
$$
It is continuous at zero because the image of the <sphere> is bounded. Map the two pieces into $\mathbb R^{m+k+1}$ by
$$
y\longmapsto(e(y),0,0),\qquad x\longmapsto\bigl(G(x),(1-|x|)x,1-|x|\bigr).
$$
At $|x|=1$ these agree with the attaching identification. They therefore induce a <continuous map> on the quotient. Its restriction to the old stage is injective. An interior point has positive last coordinate $t=1-|x|$, and the preceding coordinate block recovers $x$ by division by $t$. Hence it cannot coincide with an old point or another interior point. The induced map is injective.

The quotient is <compact>, being the image of a <compact> <disjoint union>. A continuous injection into <Euclidean space> is <Hausdorff>, by inverse images of disjoint separating neighborhoods; it is also a <homeomorphism> onto its image because closed subsets of the <compact> source have <compact>, hence closed, images in the <Hausdorff> target. Finite discrete $X^0$ embeds in $\mathbb R$, and induction over the finite list of cells proves **$X$ is <Hausdorff> and its weak topology is exactly an induced Euclidean <subspace topology>**. This is <Euclidean embedding by finite cell attachment>; no optimal ambient dimension is needed.

For <real projective space>, filter by $\mathbb{RP}^0\subset\cdots\subset\mathbb{RP}^n$. The complement of $\mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$ in $\mathbb{RP}^k$ is an affine $k$-cell. A characteristic disk is a hemisphere of $S^k$, whose boundary is attached by the antipodal quotient $S^{k-1}\to\mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$. Thus there is one cell in every dimension $0,\ldots,n$.

The incidence number is the sum of the two sheet contributions. Fixing the orientation of one sheet gives degree one; the other differs by the antipodal map on $S^{k-1}$, whose degree is $(-1)^k$. Consequently the <cellular homology of real projective space> has
$$
C_k=\mathbb Z\quad(0\le k\le n),\qquad d_k=1+(-1)^k=\begin{cases}2&k\text{ even},\\0&k\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
$$
Taking kernels modulo images gives, for $n\ge1$,
$$
\boxed{H_q(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb Z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z&q=0,\\\mathbb Z/2&0<q<n\text{ and }q\text{ odd},\\\mathbb Z&q=n\text{ and }n\text{ odd},\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Over $\mathbb R$, multiplication by two is invertible, so
$$
\boxed{H_q(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb R)=\begin{cases}\mathbb R&q=0,\text{ or }q=n\text{ with }n\text{ odd},\\0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Over $\mathbb F_2=\mathbb Z/2$, every differential vanishes, giving **$H_q(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb F_2)=\mathbb F_2$ for $0\le q\le n$, and zero otherwise**. For $n=0$ the space is a point and only degree-zero <homology> is present, with the chosen coefficient <group>.

A map $p:E\to B$ is a <Serre fibration> if every <homotopy> $H:D^k\times I\to B$, $k\ge0$, with a lift of its initial slice has a lift on the whole cylinder extending that initial lift. By attaching cells, this gives the relative <homotopy lifting property> for <CW pairs>: a specified lift on the initial slice and on the subcomplex cylinder can be extended.

Give $PY$ the <compact-open topology>. Suppose the initial lifting is a family of paths $\gamma_x$ starting at $y_0$, with $\gamma_x(1)=H(x,0)$. Define
$$
\widetilde H(x,s)(t)=\begin{cases}\gamma_x((1+s)t)&0\le t\le(1+s)^{-1},\\H(x,(1+s)t-1)&(1+s)^{-1}\le t\le1.\end{cases}
$$
The two expressions coincide on their common boundary, the initial path at $s=0$ is exactly $\gamma_x$, and the endpoint is $H(x,s)$. The adjoint map on $D^k\times I\times I$ is continuous by pasting; <compactness> of the path parameter gives continuity into the <path space>. Thus **endpoint evaluation $PY\to Y$ is a <Serre fibration>**. The variable subdivision avoids changing the initial lift by an unwanted reparametrization.

For a based pair $(E,F,e_0)$, the <long exact sequence of relative homotopy groups> is
$$
\cdots\to\pi_n(F)\xrightarrow{i_*}\pi_n(E)\xrightarrow{j_*}\pi_n(E,F)\xrightarrow{\partial}\pi_{n-1}(F)\xrightarrow{i_*}\pi_{n-1}(E)\to\cdots,
$$
ending in $\pi_1(E)\to\pi_1(E,F)\to\pi_0(F)\to\pi_0(E)$. The low-dimensional end is an exact sequence of <pointed sets> with its usual actions, rather than an entirely abelian sequence.

The boundary sends a relative disk map to its restriction on the boundary <sphere>. An absolute class has constant boundary, so $\partial j_*=0$. Conversely, if a relative map has <null-homotopic> boundary in $F$, choose a based null-homotopy there. Attach this null-homotopy as a collar to the disk, leaving the original map on a smaller inner disk. The resulting boundary is constant at $e_0$, so it defines an absolute class in $\pi_n(E)$; inserting the collar is a relative <homotopy>, so its image is the original relative class. This proves **$\operatorname{im}j_*=\ker\partial$**. At $n=1$, append a path in $F$ from the terminal point to $e_0$, giving the corresponding based loop and pointed-set exactness.

Now suppose $F=p^{-1}(b_0)$. Use a cube model in which the bottom and side faces are based and the top face maps into $F$. A based $n$-cube in $B$ lifts from its constant bottom face, with sides held at $e_0$ by relative lifting. Its top face lies in $F$, proving surjectivity of the <relative homotopy of a Serre fibration> comparison $p_*:\pi_n(E,F)\to\pi_n(B,b_0)$.

For injectivity, lift a based <homotopy> of two projected cubes from the first relative lift; this ends at some lift of the second base cube. Any two lifts of that same base cube are relatively homotopic: insert an extra parameter between them, prescribe the two liftings on its two ends and the constant lift on the bottom, and apply relative lifting in the remaining cube direction. The top face stays in $F$ because its projection is $b_0$. Combining these <homotopies> proves injectivity. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p_*:\pi_n(E,F)\xrightarrow{\cong}\pi_n(B,b_0)\quad(n\ge2).}
$$
At $n=1$ the same construction gives a bijection of <pointed sets>, and one can transport the base fundamental-group law across it. An arbitrary pair's relative $\pi_1$ has no general <group> law. The printed target index $a$ is a typographical error: the map preserves degree $n$.

The last-column map $U(n)\to S^{2n-1}$ is surjective because a unit vector can be completed to a unitary basis. Its stabilizer is the block subgroup $\operatorname{diag}(U(n-1),1)$, so it induces a continuous bijection $U(n)/U(n-1)\to S^{2n-1}$. <Unitary matrices> form a <compact> set in their matrix-space topology; the quotient is <compact> and the <sphere> <Hausdorff>. Hence
$$
\boxed{U(n)/U(n-1)\cong S^{2n-1}.}
$$
This quotient is a fiber bundle. Near the last standard unit vector, project the other standard vectors by $I-vv^*$ onto $v^\perp$ and apply <Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization>; their independence persists in a small neighborhood. The resulting orthonormal columns together with $v$ give a continuous local section. Translating these sections covers the <sphere> and trivializes the projection, so it is a <Serre fibration>.

Apply this to $U(n)\to U(n+1)\to S^{2n+1}$. Its <homotopy> sequence contains
$$
\pi_{i+1}(S^{2n+1})\to\pi_i(U(n))\to\pi_i(U(n+1))\to\pi_i(S^{2n+1}).
$$
For $i\le2n-1$, both outer <groups> vanish by <cellular approximation theorem>. Thus **$\pi_i(U(n))\cong\pi_i(U(n+1))$ in the stated range**. The component assertion also holds: diagonalizing a <unitary matrix> and varying its eigenvalue phases continuously connects it to the identity. This establishes the <unitary sphere quotient and stability range>.

Interpret the connectivity hypothesis in its usual based sense: $X$ is $(n-2)$-connected, with $n\ge2$ for the nontrivial suspension range. Form the suspension as two cones $C_-X$ and $C_+X$ meeting in $X$. Both cones are <contractible>. Their relative <homotopy> sequences give $\pi_j(C_\pm X,X)\cong\pi_{j-1}(X)$ in the relevant degrees, so both cone pairs are $(n-1)$-connected.

The <homotopy excision theorem> says that if two <CW pairs> meeting in a connected subcomplex are $p$- and $q$-connected, $p,q\ge1$, the map from the first relative pair to the union relative to the second part is an <isomorphism> in degrees $i<p+q$ and a surjection in degree $p+q$. Here $p=q=n-1$, giving
$$
\pi_i(C_+X,X)\longrightarrow\pi_i(\Sigma X,C_-X)
$$
as an <isomorphism> for $i<2n-2$ and a surjection for $i=2n-2$. Contractibility identifies the domain with $\pi_{i-1}(X)$ and the target with $\pi_i(\Sigma X)$. The comparison under these identifications is the suspension map: a <sphere> representative is coned in each half and the two cones give its suspended <sphere> representative. Hence the <Freudenthal suspension from two cones> proves
$$
\boxed{\pi_{i-1}(X)\to\pi_i(\Sigma X)\text{ is onto for }i\le2n-2,\text{ and is an isomorphism for }i<2n-2.}
$$
For connected <CW complexes>, reduced and unreduced suspension have the same based <homotopy> type, so either standard suspension convention gives this result. In the low-dimensional range, <pointed sets> are used where necessary; the displayed positive-degree <homomorphism> assertion concerns the usual <groups>.

For $S^r$ with $r\ge2$, cellular approximation makes every <sphere> map of degree below $r$ <null-homotopic>: it can be homotoped into the point skeleton of the CW structure having cells only in degrees zero and $r$. The <Hurewicz theorem> therefore identifies its first possible nonzero <homotopy group> with <homology>. Its <cellular chain complex> gives $H_r(S^r;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z$. For $r=1$, lifting a based loop to the <universal cover> $\mathbb R\to S^1$ classifies it by an integer endpoint displacement, so $\pi_1(S^1)=\mathbb Z$; the degree-one Hurewicz map is <abelianization> and is an <isomorphism> here. Thus
$$
\boxed{\pi_r(S^r)\cong H_r(S^r;\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z\qquad(r\ge1).}
$$
Alternatively, after the $r=2$ Hurewicz base case, the suspension <isomorphisms> above propagate the generator to every higher <sphere>.

= Solution

Use finitely generated right <projective modules> throughout. The <group> <K0 of a ring> is the <Grothendieck group> of their direct-sum <monoid>, with $[P\oplus Q]=[P]+[Q]$. The <group> <K1 of a ring> is $\mathrm{GL}_\infty(A)/E_\infty(A)$, equivalently the <abelianization> of the <stable general linear group>. Stabilization is $g\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(g,1)$; $E_\infty$ is generated by elementary <transvections>. The <stable elementary subgroup> is the <commutator subgroup>: elementary <transvections> are commutators using a third index, and stable block interchange and elementary row identities make the quotient abelian.

Write $A_0$ for the lower-right <ring>, printed as $A'$ in the source. A <Milnor square> has unital commuting maps, is a <pullback>
$$
A=\{(a_1,a_2)\in A_1\times A_2:j_1(a_1)=j_2(a_2)\},
$$
and has at least one of $j_1,j_2$ surjective. Interchange the two sides if necessary so that $j_1$ is surjective.

We first establish the patching fact used in the proof. For projective $P_i$ and an <isomorphism> $\phi:P_1\otimes A_0\to P_2\otimes A_0$, set
$$
M(P_1,P_2,\phi)=\{(x_1,x_2):\phi(\bar x_1)=\bar x_2\}.
$$
For <free modules> denote the result by $M_g$. The block <matrix> identity
$$
\begin{pmatrix}g&0\\0&g^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=W(g)W(-I),\qquad W(g)=\begin{pmatrix}I&g\\0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\-g^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&g\\0&I\end{pmatrix}
$$
expresses $\operatorname{diag}(g,g^{-1})$ as <elementary matrices>. Their entries lift through $j_1$, and their elementary lifts remain invertible. A change of basis on the first side then trivializes this gluing. Thus $M_g\oplus M_{g^{-1}}\cong A^{2r}$, proving that $M_g$ is finitely generated projective. Notice that we lifted <elementary matrices>, not arbitrary invertible <matrices>.

For general $P_1,P_2$, choose $P_1\oplus Q_1\cong A_1^r$ and $P_2\oplus Q_2\cong A_2^s$. The reductions of $Q_1\oplus A_1^s$ and $Q_2\oplus A_2^r$ are isomorphic: insert the common reduced $P_i$ and reorder the two complements. Patch them by such an <isomorphism>. The sum with $M(P_1,P_2,\phi)$ now patches two <free modules> of equal size, so is projective by the free case, making each summand projective. The same free change of basis followed by these direct-summand projections shows that extension to $A_i$ recovers $P_i$. Conversely, write an $A$-projective as the image of an <idempotent> over a finite <free module>. Since <matrices> over the <pullback> are compatible pairs of <matrices>, applying the compatible <idempotents> to the free <pullback> reconstructs this projective from its two reductions. Compatible module <isomorphisms> patch as well. This proves <Milnor patching of projective modules> in the form needed below.

The diagonal maps in the sequence are <extension of scalars>; the two maps to $A_0$ are differences $j_{1*}-j_{2*}$. Define the connecting map by
$$
\partial[g]=[M_g]-[A^r],\qquad g\in\mathrm{GL}_r(A_0).
$$
It is unchanged by stabilization or elementary multiplication: an elementary factor lifts through $j_1$ and changes a free basis in the gluing. The identity $\operatorname{diag}(g,h)=\operatorname{diag}(gh,I)\operatorname{diag}(h^{-1},h)$ and the preceding elementary factorization show $M_g\oplus M_h\cong M_{gh}\oplus A^r$ after matching sizes. Thus $\partial$ is an additive <homomorphism> on $K_1(A_0)$.

**Exactness at $K_1(A_1)\oplus K_1(A_2)$.** If the two reduced classes agree, choose stabilized representatives $g_1,g_2$ of the same size. Their reductions differ by an <elementary matrix> $e$ over $A_0$. Lift $e$ to an <elementary matrix> over $A_1$ and adjust $g_1$ without changing its $K_1$ class. Its reduction now equals that of $g_2$. The compatible pair and its compatible inverse give an invertible <matrix> over $A$, so the original pair of classes is in the diagonal image. The reverse containment follows from commutativity of the square.

**Exactness at $K_1(A_0)$.** A gluing obtained from a change of basis over either upper <ring> patches a <free module>, so the difference-map image is killed by $\partial$. Conversely, $\partial[g]=0$ means that $M_g$ becomes free after adding a common projective summand. Add a complement of that summand to obtain an actual <isomorphism> $M_g\oplus A^s\cong A^{r+s}$. Its two local bases express $g\oplus I_s=j_2(v_2)j_1(v_1)^{-1}$. Therefore $[g]=j_{2*}[v_2]-j_{1*}[v_1]$, which lies in the image of the difference map. This uses stable equality of projective classes, not cancellation of arbitrary projectives.

**Exactness at $K_0(A)$.** Connecting classes reduce to zero on both sides. Conversely, write a class in the kernel as $[P]-[A^r]$ by adding a complement to its negative projective term. Its reductions have the same classes as $A_i^r$. Equality in $K_0(A_i)$ becomes an <isomorphism> after a finite common free summand, by taking a complement to the auxiliary projective. Use one sufficiently large free summand on both sides. The stabilized $P$ is then locally free with gluing $g$, so the original class is $[M_g]-[A^{r+s}]=\partial[g]$.

**Exactness at $K_0(A_1)\oplus K_0(A_2)$.** Write a kernel pair as $([P_1]-[A_1^r],[P_2]-[A_2^r])$, using complements and padding to make the free sizes equal. Their reduced positive classes agree. Adding a common finite free summand makes those reductions isomorphic. Patch the resulting projectives, and subtract the matching free $A$-module. Its restrictions are the desired pair. The reverse containment is immediate because both reductions to $A_0$ agree.

These arguments prove the <Mayer-Vietoris sequence in algebraic K-theory>
$$
\boxed{K_1A\to K_1A_1\oplus K_1A_2\to K_1A_0\xrightarrow{\partial}K_0A\to K_0A_1\oplus K_0A_2\to K_0A_0.}
$$
No zero is inserted at either end; this fragment does not assert injectivity at the first term or surjectivity at the last.

For the <group ring> of the <cyclic group>, take the <cyclotomic Milnor square>
$$
\begin{array}{ccc}\mathbb Z[C_p]&\longrightarrow&\mathbb Z\\\downarrow&&\downarrow\\\mathbb Z[\zeta]&\longrightarrow&\mathbb F_p.\end{array}
$$
The maps send the cyclic generator to $1$ and to $\zeta$; the lower map sets $\zeta=1$, and the right map reduces modulo $p$. Since $\Phi_p(1)=p$, $\mathbb Z[\zeta]/(\zeta-1)=\mathbb F_p$. To verify the <pullback>, represent an element by a <polynomial> of degree at most $p-1$. If both evaluations vanish, it is a constant multiple of the <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $\Phi_p$, and evaluation at one forces that constant to be zero. Conversely, represent $b\in\mathbb Z[\zeta]$ by $f$ of degree below $p-1$. For a compatible integer $a$, $a-f(1)$ is divisible by $p$; replacing $f$ by $f+[(a-f(1))/p]\Phi_p$ yields the pair $(a,b)$. The right map is surjective, so this is a <Milnor square>.

The hinted <cyclotomic units> are $u_k=1+\zeta+\cdots+\zeta^{k-1}$. If $kl\equiv1\pmod p$ with $1\le l<p$, the geometric sum $\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\zeta^{kj}$ is their inverse, because $\zeta^{kl}=\zeta$. Their residues are $k\in\mathbb F_p^\times$, covering every unit of the common <field>. <Gaussian elimination> identifies $K_1(\mathbb F_p)$ with $\mathbb F_p^\times$ by <determinant>. Thus $K_1(\mathbb Z[\zeta])\to K_1(\mathbb F_p)$ is surjective, and the connecting map vanishes.

The remaining exact sequence injects $K_0(\mathbb Z[C_p])$ into $\mathbb Z\oplus K_0(\mathbb Z[\zeta])$, with image $\{(r,x):r=\operatorname{rank}x\}$. Here $K_0(\mathbb Z)=K_0(\mathbb F_p)=\mathbb Z$, and reduction of a projective over the <integral domain> $\mathbb Z[\zeta]$ has dimension its rank: localize at $(\zeta-1)$, where the projective is free, and compare its residue-field dimension with its fraction-field dimension. Projection onto the second factor is therefore a bijection on this kernel. Hence extension along the cyclotomic evaluation gives
$$
\boxed{K_0(\mathbb Z[C_p])\cong K_0(\mathbb Z[\zeta]).}
$$
The essential step is <reduction of cyclotomic units at the ramified prime>; surjectivity of a <ring> map alone would not imply surjectivity on $K_1$.

Work in the based CW <homotopy category>. A homotopy-invariant <functor> depends on a map only through its based <homotopy class>. In a sequence of <pointed sets>, exactness at the middle means that the preceding image equals the inverse image of the distinguished point; it does not imply injectivity or surjectivity at the ends. For <groups> this is the familiar image-equals-kernel condition. A <half-exact functor> preserves such middle exactness on the appropriate elementary sequence.

For a contravariant <functor> $F$, the relevant elementary sequence is a <cofibration> $A\hookrightarrow X\to X/A$. Half-exactness is
$$
F(X/A)\longrightarrow F(X)\longrightarrow F(A)
$$
exact at $F(X)$. One should not silently turn this into a short exact sequence. In particular, arbitrary <group> actions and <pointed sets> at low degrees are not interchangeable with <abelian groups>.

The basic contravariant example is $F_Y(X)=[X,Y]_*$, often called a cohomotopy-type <functor>. Suppose a map $f:X\to Y$ becomes <null-homotopic> on $A$. A chosen null-homotopy extends it over the cone attached to $A$, giving a map from the <topological mapping cone> to $Y$. Conversely every such extension restricts to a <null-homotopic> map on $A$. Since the <topological mapping cone> of a <subcomplex> inclusion models $X/A$, this proves half-exactness, rather than merely asserting it.

Repeating the <topological mapping cone> construction gives the <Puppe sequence>
$$
A\to X\to C_i\to\Sigma A\to\Sigma X\to\cdots.
$$
Contravariant mapping into $Y$ gives
$$
\cdots\to[\Sigma X,Y]_*\to[\Sigma A,Y]_*\to[C_i,Y]_*\to[X,Y]_*\to[A,Y]_*.
$$
This is exact as <pointed sets>. An <H-group> structure on $Y$, for example a <loop space> with path concatenation, provides <group> structures; repeated looping gives <abelian group> structures in the usual ranges. The <suspension-loop adjunction> $[\Sigma X,Y]_*\cong[X,\Omega Y]_*$ explains the shifted terms and their compatibility.

The covariant counterpart is $H_T(Y)=[T,Y]_*$, with ordinary <homotopy groups> obtained from $T=S^n$. Its natural elementary sequence is a <homotopy fiber sequence> $F\to E\to B$, not an arbitrary <cofibration>. A map $a:T\to E$ whose composite to $B$ is <null-homotopic>, together with that null-homotopy, determines a map into the <homotopy fibre>. Conversely a fibre map projects to such a null composite. This proves exactness of $[T,F]_*\to[T,E]_*\to[T,B]_*$ at the middle. Adding the loop terms produces the <homotopy> long exact sequence. For a <Serre fibration>, its ordinary fibre supplies the same <homotopy> information.

This variance distinction matters. Include one <circle>, generated by $a$, in $S^1\vee S^1$, with other generator $b$. The quotient is the second <circle>. The kernel of $\pi_1(S^1\vee S^1)\to\pi_1(S^1)$ is the <normal closure> of $a$, which contains $bab^{-1}$. The image of the included <circle> is only the <cyclic subgroup> generated by $a$, and does not contain that reduced word. Thus covariant $\pi_1$ is not half-exact on this <cofibration>. Fibre and cofibre statements cannot be swapped just by reversing notation.

Reduced <cohomology> gives group-valued contravariant examples. For an abelian coefficient <group> $G$, the degree-$q$ <functor> is represented by an <Eilenberg–MacLane space>:
$$
\widetilde H^q(X;G)\cong[X,K(G,q)]_*.
$$
Its <cofibration> exact sequence continues as $\cdots\to\widetilde H^{q-1}(A)\to\widetilde H^q(X/A)\to\widetilde H^q(X)\to\widetilde H^q(A)\to\cdots$. Ordinary <homotopy groups> are instead covariant, with decreasing <group> degree across a fibre connecting map. These are the two complementary representable patterns, maps out of a test object and maps into a representing object.

<Brown's representability theorem> makes the contravariant pattern structural. A reduced contravariant <homotopy> <functor> on based <CW complexes> is represented by a <CW complex> if it converts arbitrary wedges to products and satisfies the Mayer-Vietoris weak-pullback gluing axiom. The latter says that compatible classes on two subcomplexes extend to their union. It contains more information than the single kernel statement of half-exactness. Consequently half-exactness alone is not a representability criterion. In a representing <functor> these axioms are natural: a map on a wedge is a family of based maps, while compatible maps on an intersection can be adjusted by a <homotopy> and glued across a <homotopy pushout>.

A reduced <generalized cohomology theory> with the usual wedge axiom supplies these axioms degree by degree. Its representing spaces $E_q$ have $E_q\simeq\Omega E_{q+1}$ by suspension compatibility, assembling an <Omega-spectrum>. Thus generalized <cohomology> can be expressed as $E^q(X)=[\Sigma^\infty X,\Sigma^qE]_{\mathrm{st}}$, while represented <homology> is $E_q(X)=\pi_q(E\wedge\Sigma^\infty X)$. The appearance of mapping into a spectrum in one case and smashing with it in the other is another form of the same variance distinction.

There is a literal duality after stabilization and a finiteness restriction. Here a finite based <CW complex> is identified with its <suspension spectrum>. For a <finite spectrum> $X$, let $DX=F(X,\mathbb S)$ be its <Spanier-Whitehead dual>. Then $D^2X\simeq X$ and $[X,Y]_{\mathrm{st}}\cong[DY,DX]_{\mathrm{st}}$ for <finite spectra>. Duality reverses a cofibre triangle into a fibre triangle. In particular,
$$
\widetilde E^q(X)\cong\pi_{-q}(E\wedge DX),\qquad \widetilde E_q(X)\cong[\Sigma^qDX,E]_{\mathrm{st}}.
$$
This turns a <cohomology> calculation on a finite object into a <homology> calculation on its dual, and vice versa. It is <Spanier-Whitehead duality>, not a claim that the full category of ordinary spaces is self-dual.

**Half-exactness is the local image–kernel property; fibres and cofibres, representability, and finite stable duality explain how that property organizes <homotopy> and <cohomology>.** The distinctions between variance, <pointed sets> and <groups>, and finite stable objects and arbitrary spaces are essential to using the duality correctly.
