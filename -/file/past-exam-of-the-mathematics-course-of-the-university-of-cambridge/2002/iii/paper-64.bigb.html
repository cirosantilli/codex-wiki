= Solution
{parent=a}

An <order parameter> distinguishes thermodynamic phases. For a ferromagnet it is the <magnetization>; for a fluid near its <liquid-gas critical point> it can be the density relative to its critical value. In a system with a scalar <order parameter>, introduce a slowly varying real <scalar field> $\phi(x)$ whose spatial average represents that <order parameter>. The <Landau-Ginzburg theory> organizes the effective <free energy> as a local expansion consistent with the symmetry:
$$
\mathcal F[\phi]=\int d^dx\left\{\frac K2(\nabla\phi)^2+\frac r2\phi^2+\frac u4\phi^4+\frac v6\phi^6-H\phi\right\}.
$$
Here $K>0$ is the stiffness in the <gradient energy>, and $H$ is the <field conjugate to an order parameter>. The even powers assume a symmetry $\phi\mapsto-\phi$ at $H=0$. Without that symmetry further terms, including a cubic term, may be allowed. The coefficients are smooth functions of temperature and other controls; near a mean-field transition one usually writes $r=a(T-T_0)$ with $a>0$. A positive highest retained even coefficient makes the local potential bounded below.

The <partition function> is obtained by weighting configurations with $\exp[-\mathcal F/(k_BT)]$. The <Landau approximation> replaces this functional average by minimization, taking a uniform $\phi=M$ in a homogeneous phase. In a symmetric finite system the zero-field average can vanish even below the transition because it averages both signs of $M$. The spontaneous <order parameter> selects a <pure thermodynamic phase> by taking the <thermodynamic limit> before removing the conjugate field:
$$
M=\lim_{H\downarrow0}\lim_{V\to\infty}
\left\langle V^{-1}\int_V\phi(x)\,d^dx\right\rangle_H.
$$
Thus \b[the order parameter measures the ordering of a selected thermodynamic phase], rather than the average over all symmetry-related phases.

A <first-order phase transition> has a discontinuity in a first derivative of the equilibrium <free energy>, such as <entropy> or the <order parameter>; a thermal first-order transition normally has <latent heat>. At a <continuous phase transition> the <order parameter> goes continuously to zero and there is no <latent heat>, although response functions and higher derivatives of the <free energy> can be singular.

For the symmetric scalar <Landau free energy> at zero conjugate field, minimize
$$
V(M)=\frac r2M^2+\frac u4M^4+\frac v6M^6,\qquad
V'(M)=M(r+uM^2+vM^4).
$$
If $u>0$, the sixth-order term is unnecessary near $r=0$. For $r>0$ the minimum is $M=0$, whereas for $r<0$ there are two symmetry-related minima with $M^2=-r/u$ to leading order. The <order parameter> vanishes continuously as $r\uparrow0$. The equilibrium <free-energy density> changes from zero to $-r^2/(4u)$, so its first temperature derivative is continuous and its second derivative has a jump. This is the ordinary mean-field <continuous phase transition>.

If $u<0$, keep $v>0$ for stability. A nonzero stationary <order parameter>, with $s=M^2$, obeys $r+us+vs^2=0$. For <phase coexistence> with $M=0$ its <free energy> must also vanish. Substituting $r=-us-vs^2$ into $V$ gives
$$
V=-\frac u4s^2-\frac v3s^3.
$$
The nonzero solution of $V=0$ is therefore
$$
\boxed{M_t^2=-\frac{3u}{4v},\qquad r_t=\frac{3u^2}{16v}.}
$$
The competing minima exchange stability at positive $r_t$, with a finite jump of the <order parameter>. This is a <first-order phase transition>. The <metastability> region is also visible: nonzero stationary points first appear at the <spinodal> $r=u^2/(4v)$, and the disordered local minimum loses stability at $r=0$.

For example, holding $u,v$ fixed and taking $r=a(T-T_0)$, the ordered phase has <entropy> lower than the disordered phase by $aM_t^2/2$ at coexistence. Heating through the transition absorbs <latent heat> per volume $T_taM_t^2/2$. More generally the temperature dependence of all coefficients contributes to this <entropy> difference. With no inversion symmetry, a cubic term can also produce a <first-order phase transition>, as comparison of the competing minima of the <Landau free energy> shows.

<Critical exponents> describe leading singular powers near a <continuous phase transition>. Using the <reduced temperature> $t=(T-T_c)/T_c$, the standard definitions include
$$
M(t,0)\sim(-t)^\beta\quad(t<0),\qquad
\chi(t,0)\sim |t|^{-\gamma},\qquad
C_{\mathrm{sing}}(t)\sim |t|^{-\alpha},\qquad
\xi(t)\sim |t|^{-\nu}.
$$
These are the <order-parameter critical exponent>, <magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent>, <heat-capacity critical exponent> and <correlation-length critical exponent>. The <critical-isotherm exponent> is defined by $M(0,H)\sim\operatorname{sgn}(H)|H|^{1/\delta}$. The <anomalous dimension> $\eta$ gives the critical long-distance <connected correlation function>, $G_c(x)\sim |x|^{-(d-2+\eta)}$, when an ordinary scale-invariant critical point exists.

In the <Landau approximation>, minimize the <Landau free energy> to obtain the <equation of state>, differentiate it with respect to the <field conjugate to an order parameter> to obtain <magnetic susceptibility>, and differentiate the minimized <free energy> with respect to temperature to obtain <heat capacity>. These operations determine $\beta,\delta,\gamma,\alpha$, respectively. Expanding the <Landau-Ginzburg theory> about its uniform minimum gives the quadratic fluctuation kernel $Kq^2+V''(M)$, whose <Fourier transform> determines the <correlation length> and the <anomalous dimension>. At the ordinary quartic critical point this gives $\xi^2=K/V''(M)\propto |t|^{-1}$ and $\eta=0$.

For $u>0$, $r=0$ gives $H=uM^3$ and hence $\delta=3$; the equilibrium <free energy> $-r^2/(4u)$ gives a finite <heat capacity> discontinuity, conventionally $\alpha=0$. Together with the explicit calculation below, the ordinary <mean-field critical exponents> are
$$
\boxed{\alpha=0,\quad\beta=\frac12,\quad\gamma=1,\quad
\delta=3,\quad\nu=\frac12,\quad\eta=0.}
$$
These describe neglect of fluctuation corrections, rather than the exact exponents of every low-dimensional <Landau-Ginzburg theory>.

A <tricritical point> is the meeting of a line of <continuous phase transitions> and a line of <first-order phase transitions>. In the symmetric scalar <Landau free energy>, it occurs at $r=u=0$ with $v>0$. Two independent controls are needed to tune both coefficients. The continuous transition lies at $r=0,u>0$, and the <tricritical three-phase line> found above has $r=3u^2/(16v),u<0$; both approach the <tricritical point>. At zero conjugate field the three coexisting states on this first-order line are the disordered state and the two opposite ordered states.

Setting $u=H=0$ makes the leading stabilizing interaction sextic. The nonzero minimum obeys $M^4=-r/v$, so the <order-parameter critical exponent> along this tuned direction is $\beta_{\mathrm{tri}}=1/4$. Substitution gives
$$
f_{\min}=-\frac{|r|^{3/2}}{3\sqrt v}\quad(r<0),
$$
and two temperature derivatives give the <heat-capacity critical exponent> $\alpha_{\mathrm{tri}}=1/2$. At $r=u=0$, the <equation of state> is $H=vM^5$, giving the <critical-isotherm exponent> $\delta_{\mathrm{tri}}=5$.

For <magnetic susceptibility>, differentiating $H=rM+vM^5$ gives $\chi^{-1}=r+5vM^4$. This is $r$ above the transition and $-4r$ below, hence $\gamma_{\mathrm{tri}}=1$. The quadratic fluctuation kernel still has a mass proportional to $|r|$, giving $\nu_{\mathrm{tri}}=1/2$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{tri}}=0$. Thus the <mean-field tricritical exponent calculation> yields
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\mathrm{tri}}=\frac14,\quad
\alpha_{\mathrm{tri}}=\frac12,\quad
\delta_{\mathrm{tri}}=5,\quad
\gamma_{\mathrm{tri}}=1,\quad\nu_{\mathrm{tri}}=\frac12.}
$$
Detuning $u$ gives <tricritical crossover scaling>; one must specify the approach to the <tricritical point> when quoting these <critical exponents>. The sextic interaction has <upper critical dimension> three, so sufficiently strong long-distance fluctuations change these mean-field predictions below three dimensions.

The relevant boundary here is the <upper critical dimension>: above it the critical interactions become negligible at long distances and the ordinary <mean-field critical exponents> apply; at it there can be logarithmic corrections. This differs from the <lower critical dimension>, below which a particular kind of ordering may fail to exist.

Normalize the coefficient of $(\nabla\phi)^2$ in the <Landau-Ginzburg theory>. Under a change of length scale $x=bx'$, its invariance gives the <engineering dimension> $x_\phi=(d-2)/2$. The quartic interaction then scales as
$$
u'=b^{d-4x_\phi}u=b^{4-d}u.
$$
It is a <relevant operator> below four dimensions and an <irrelevant operator> above four. Thus \b[the ordinary scalar transition has upper critical dimension $d_c=4$]. At a <tricritical point>, where the quartic interaction is tuned away, the sextic coupling scales as $v'=b^{6-2d}v$, giving \b[the tricritical upper critical dimension $d_c=3$]. This is the <upper critical dimension of an even scalar interaction> applied to powers four and six.

The <Ginzburg criterion> makes the reason for failure of the <Landau approximation> quantitative. In the ordered quartic theory, $M^2=|r|/u$, $V''(M)=2|r|$ and $\xi^2=K/(2|r|)$. The fluctuation of the <order parameter> averaged over a <correlation volume> has the scale
$$
\left\langle(\delta\phi)^2\right\rangle_{\xi}
\asymp k_BT\int_{|q|\lesssim\xi^{-1}}\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}
\frac1{Kq^2+2|r|}
\asymp\frac{k_BT}{K}\xi^{2-d}.
$$
Compared with the squared mean <order parameter>, the <Ginzburg criterion> therefore gives
$$
\frac{\langle(\delta\phi)^2\rangle_{\xi}}{M^2}
\asymp\frac{k_BTu}{K^2}\xi^{4-d}.
$$
For $d<4$ this ratio grows without bound as the <correlation length> diverges. The assumption that fluctuations are small relative to the ordered value fails arbitrarily near the transition, and the interacting <renormalization-group fixed point> determines the <critical exponents>. For $d>4$ the ratio tends to zero. At $d=4$ scale counting is marginal, and the running quartic coupling produces logarithms.

Similarly, at the <tricritical point> $M^2\asymp\sqrt{K/v}\,\xi^{-1}$, so the <multicritical Ginzburg ratio> scales as $(k_BT\sqrt v/K^{3/2})\xi^{3-d}$. The <Landau-Ginzburg theory> remains a useful effective description below its <upper critical dimension>; it is its fluctuation-free exponent calculation that fails, not the possibility of including fluctuations in the theory.

Take the symmetric scalar <Landau free energy> with $u>0$, $r=at$ for a positive constant $a$, and leading <equation of state>
$$
H=rM+uM^3.
$$
At zero conjugate field, minimization gives $M=0$ for $t>0$ and $M=\pm\sqrt{-r/u}$ for $t<0$. Selecting the positive <pure thermodynamic phase>,
$$
M=\sqrt{\frac au}\,(-t)^{1/2}.
$$
Consequently the <order-parameter critical exponent> is $\boxed{\beta=1/2}$.

Differentiate the <equation of state> at fixed temperature, within the selected phase:
$$
\chi=\left.\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\right|_t
=\frac1{r+3uM^2}
=\begin{cases}
(at)^{-1},&t>0,\\
(2a|t|)^{-1},&t<0.
\end{cases}
$$
Both sides diverge as $|t|^{-1}$, so the <magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent> is $\boxed{\gamma=1}$. The leading above-to-below <magnetic susceptibility> amplitude ratio is two. These are the ordinary <mean-field critical exponents>, valid under the fluctuation qualification explained above.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Write $\beta_T=(k_BT)^{-1}$ for inverse temperature to distinguish it from the <order-parameter critical exponent>. A <normalized blocking kernel> $B_b(\sigma',\sigma)$ is a nonnegative conditional distribution of coarse spins given the fine spins, normalized by
$$
\sum_{\sigma'}B_b(\sigma',\sigma)=1.
$$
For deterministic blocking it can impose a majority rule or block average, with a <Dirac delta> replacing the sum constraint for continuous coarse variables. Each block occupies $b^D$ original cells, so the new lattice spacing and number of sites are
$$
\boxed{a'=ba,\qquad N'=b^{-D}N.}
$$
The physical volume $Na^D$ is unchanged. After $p$ blockings, $a_p=b^pa$ and $N_p=b^{-pD}N$.

Define the coarse <statistical Hamiltonian> by the exact Boltzmann-weight identity
$$
\sum_\sigma B_b(\sigma',\sigma)e^{-\beta_TH(u,\sigma)}
=e^{-\beta_T[H(Ru,\sigma')+N g(u)]}.
$$
The operator family must include all generated interactions for this <renormalization-group transformation> to be exact; restricting it to a few couplings is an approximation. The scalar $g(u)$ has energy units and is the contribution per original site that is independent of $\sigma'$. Restoring the original constant term $NC$ gives
$$
H(u',\sigma')+N'C'=H(Ru,\sigma')+N[C+g(u)],
$$
hence
$$
\boxed{u'=Ru,\qquad C'=b^D[C+g(u)].}
$$
This convention measures $g$ per original site; defining a constant per new site would move a factor $b^D$ into its definition.

The <identity-operator contribution to renormalization-group free energy> $C_p$ records the configuration-independent normalization generated by the eliminated spins. It includes their contribution to <free energy>, including <entropy> and measure factors, and need not be independent of temperature. It has no effect on normalized probabilities of the retained spins, but is indispensable for the total <partition function> and its temperature derivatives. Iterating its recursion gives
$$
C_p=b^{pD}\left[C_0+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b^{-jD}g(u_j)\right].
$$
Thus $N_pC_p$ is the original constant $NC_0$ plus the accumulated free-energy contributions of the eliminated degrees of freedom.

To obtain the <additive free-energy recursion under blocking>, use energy-valued <free energy> per current lattice site,
$$
F(u,C)=-\frac1{\beta_TN}\log Z(u,C,N)=C+f_0(u).
$$
The <blocking kernel> normalization implies
$$
Z(u,C,N)=Z(Ru,C',N/b^D),
\qquad F(u,C)=b^{-D}F(Ru,C').
$$
Substituting the recursion for $C'$ cancels $C$, giving
$$
f_0(u)=b^{-D}f_0(Ru)+g(u).
$$
A second step gives $f_0(u_0)=b^{-2D}f_0(u_2)+g(u_0)+b^{-D}g(u_1)$, and induction proves
$$
\boxed{f_0(u_0)=b^{-pD}f_0(u_p)+
\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}b^{-jD}g(u_j).}
$$
For dimensionless <free energy> $-\log Z/N$, multiply $F,f_0,g,C$ throughout by $\beta_T$.

The singular <free-energy density> must be distinguished from the exact per-site representative $f_0$. If $a(u)$ is a regular background and $f_s=f_0-a$, then
$$
f_s(u)=b^{-D}f_s(Ru)+g_s(u),\qquad
g_s(u)=g(u)-a(u)+b^{-D}a(Ru).
$$
Thus the same displayed inhomogeneous recursion holds with $f_s,g_s$ after subtraction. When a suitable analytic background solves $a(u)=g(u)+b^{-D}a(Ru)$, it leaves a homogeneous singular recursion. <Analytic subtraction of an inhomogeneous renormalization recursion> explains this separation. A <renormalization-group free-energy resonance> can obstruct analytic subtraction and produce a logarithm. The original function $g$ originates in the short-distance spins that were summed out, rather than in an extra external interaction.

Near a <renormalization-group fixed point> $u_*$, linearize the transformation:
$$
R(u_*+\delta u)=u_*+\mathsf A\delta u+O(|\delta u|^2).
$$
An <eigenvector> of $\mathsf A$ with <eigenvalue> $b^{y_i}$ defines a scaling coordinate $t_i$ with $t_i'=b^{y_i}t_i$ to leading order. A <relevant operator> has $y_i>0$: its coupling grows towards large distances, so it must be tuned to reach the fixed point. An <irrelevant operator> has $y_i<0$ and dies away; a <marginal operator> has $y_i=0$ and requires higher-order flow analysis. An operator of scaling dimension $x_i$ has $y_i=D-x_i$.

The coarse spins also require a multiplicative normalization. In continuum notation, write
$$
x'=x/b,\qquad \phi'(x')=b^{x_\phi}\phi_<(bx'),
\qquad x_\phi=\frac{D-2+\eta}{2},
$$
where $\phi_<$ is the retained field and $\eta$ is its <anomalous dimension>. This restores the normalization of the critical long-distance <connected correlation function>. Since a uniform <field conjugate to an order parameter> enters through $\int d^Dx\,H\phi$, it has eigenvalue exponent $y_h=D-x_\phi=(D+2-\eta)/2$.

Let $t$ be the thermal relevant coordinate with exponent $y_t>0$. After regular backgrounds have been removed, the leading singular <free-energy density> obeys
$$
f_s(t,H)=B^{-D}f_s(B^{y_t}t,B^{y_h}H)
$$
for a large total scale factor $B=b^p$. Choosing $B=|t|^{-1/y_t}$ gives the <scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena>
$$
f_s(t,H)=|t|^{D/y_t}
\Phi_\pm\!\left(H|t|^{-y_h/y_t}\right).
$$
Two temperature derivatives give the <heat-capacity critical exponent>; one conjugate-field derivative gives the <order-parameter critical exponent>; two such derivatives give the <magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent>. Scaling the <correlation length> gives $\xi(t)=B\xi(B^{y_t}t)$. Consequently,
$$
\alpha=2-\frac D{y_t},\qquad
\beta=\frac{D-y_h}{y_t},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{2y_h-D}{y_t},\qquad
\nu=\frac1{y_t}.
$$
At $t=0$, instead choose $B=|H|^{-1/y_h}$; differentiating gives $\delta=y_h/(D-y_h)$. These <scaling relations for critical exponents> assume that irrelevant variables can be set to their fixed-point values without singular consequences. A <dangerously irrelevant coupling> or logarithmic correction requires keeping the corresponding dependence.

For the <Gaussian field theory>, integrate out the <Fourier modes> in $\Lambda/b<|q|<\Lambda$ and retain $\phi_<$. The modes are independent at zero external field, so this <Gaussian thinning transformation> changes the normalization by a Gaussian determinant and leaves a quadratic <statistical Hamiltonian> for the retained modes. Restore the cutoff with
$$
x'=x/b,\qquad
\phi'(x')=b^{(D-2)/2}\phi_<(bx').
$$
In the integrated <statistical Hamiltonian>, the volume element contributes $b^D$, each <gradient> contributes $b^{-1}$, and each field contributes $b^{-(D-2)/2}$. Thus the coefficient $\kappa^{-1}$ of the <gradient energy> is unchanged, while
$$
(m^2)'=b^2m^2,\qquad h'=b^{(D+2)/2}h.
$$
In particular $y_t=2$, $y_h=(D+2)/2$ and $\eta=0$. The <Gaussian critical exponents> and the <Gaussian order-parameter scaling index> therefore give the requested indices
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{4-D}{2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{D-2}{4}.}
$$
The sign convention $+h\phi$ corresponds to the physical conjugate field $H=-h$ and does not affect these powers.

There are physical qualifications to these Gaussian indices. The strictly quadratic <Gaussian field theory> is stable for positive $m^2$ and has no stable ordered phase for negative $m^2$; its purported spontaneous $\beta$ is therefore the formal <Gaussian order-parameter scaling index>. A stabilizing quartic term gives an actual ordered phase. Below four dimensions that term is relevant and changes the exponents; above four dimensions it is a <dangerously irrelevant coupling> and gives the actual mean-field $\beta=1/2$, rather than the Gaussian hyperscaling value.

The <heat capacity> power can also be checked directly. Apart from constants, the zero-field Gaussian <free-energy density> is
$$
f_0=\frac{k_BT}{2}\int_{|q|<\Lambda}
\frac{d^Dq}{(2\pi)^D}\log(\kappa^{-1}q^2+m^2).
$$
If $m^2\propto t$, its singular second temperature derivative is controlled by $\int d^Dq\,(q^2+\kappa m^2)^{-2}$. For $0<D<4$, rescaling $q=\sqrt{\kappa m^2}\,k$ gives a power $t^{D/2-2}$, as above. At $D=4$ there is a logarithm, and for $D>4$ an analytic background dominates the <heat capacity>. The negative formal Gaussian $\alpha$ then describes the subleading singular piece, with logarithmic resonances at appropriate even dimensions. This is the <Gaussian specific-heat infrared threshold>, and it prevents confusing singular indices with the entire observable.

The <correlation length> is the distance over which fluctuations remain appreciably correlated. More precisely, in a massive phase the large-distance <connected correlation function>
$$
G_c(x)=\langle\phi(x)\phi(0)\rangle-\langle\phi\rangle^2
$$
has an exponential factor $e^{-|x|/\xi}$, possibly multiplied by a power. Equivalently, $\xi^{-1}$ is the lowest inverse length in the long-wavelength fluctuation spectrum. In the <Gaussian field theory> with unit gradient coefficient, $\widetilde G_c(q)\propto(q^2+m_R^2)^{-1}$ gives $\xi=1/m_R$ for positive renormalized mass.

At an ordinary <continuous phase transition>, the restoring force for long-wavelength order-parameter fluctuations vanishes and there is no finite correlation scale. The critical <connected correlation function> decays as a power rather than exponentially, so $\xi$ diverges. The <correlation-function susceptibility sum rule> relates <magnetic susceptibility> to the integral of this <connected correlation function>; its divergence is consistent with correlations extending over increasingly large distances. Interactions change the powers, so the renormalized mass and the bare mass need not vanish at the same temperature.

Absorb the thermal normalization into the action and set
$$
r=u^2=\frac{m^2(\Lambda,T)}{\Lambda^2},\qquad
\lambda=g(\Lambda,T)\Lambda^{-\epsilon},\qquad
d=4-\epsilon,\qquad
K_d=\frac{\Omega_d}{(2\pi)^d}.
$$
Here $r$, although denoted $u^2$ in the question, is a signed mass-squared coordinate, not a nonnegative square. This matters because its interacting fixed-point value is negative. Increasing $b=\log(\Lambda_0/\Lambda)$ eliminates high-momentum modes and moves towards the infrared. The initial values at $b=0$ are $r_0=m^2(\Lambda_0,T)/\Lambda_0^2$ and $\lambda_0=g(\Lambda_0,T)\Lambda_0^{-\epsilon}$.

To derive the flow, use the <momentum-shell decomposition of a scalar field> $\phi=\varphi+\chi$, where $\chi$ has momenta $\Lambda e^{-db}<|q|<\Lambda$. Its <Gaussian shell covariance> is $(q^2+m^2)^{-1}$. Integrating out $\chi$ while preserving low-momentum observables gives
$$
S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\varphi]=S_0[\varphi]
-\log\left\langle e^{-V[\varphi+\chi]}\right\rangle_>,
\qquad V=\frac g{4!}\int d^dx\,(\varphi+\chi)^4.
$$
The <cumulant expansion of a coarse-grained free energy> yields $\langle V\rangle_>-\frac12\langle V^2\rangle_{>,c}+\cdots$. By <Wick theorem>,
$$
\langle V\rangle_>=\frac g{24}\int d^dx\,
\left[\varphi^4+6\varphi^2G_>(0)+3G_>(0)^2\right].
$$
The last term is field independent, while the quadratic term gives the <one-loop shell mass renormalization in scalar quartic theory>
$$
\delta m^2=\frac g2 I_1,\qquad
I_1=\int_{\mathrm{shell}}\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}
\frac1{q^2+m^2}.
$$
This tadpole is independent of the external momentum, so no gradient renormalization occurs at this order.

For the quartic correction, the mixed vertex is $(g/4)\int\varphi^2\chi^2$. Its connected second cumulant uses $\langle\chi^2(x)\chi^2(y)\rangle_c=2G_>(x-y)^2$ and gives
$$
-\frac{g^2}{16}\int d^dx\,d^dy\,
\varphi^2(x)\varphi^2(y)G_>(x-y)^2.
$$
Extracting the local zero-external-momentum quartic interaction, the integral over $x-y$ is $I_2=\int_{\mathrm{shell}}d^dq\,(2\pi)^{-d}(q^2+m^2)^{-2}$. Comparing its coefficient with $g\varphi^4/4!$ gives the <one-loop shell quartic renormalization>
$$
\delta g=-\frac32g^2I_2.
$$
The negative sign comes from the second cumulant; the numerical factor incorporates the three quartic channels.

For the thin spherical shell, radial integration gives
$$
I_1=K_d\frac{\Lambda^{d-2}}{1+r}\,db+O(db^2),
\qquad
I_2=K_d\frac{\Lambda^{d-4}}{(1+r)^2}\,db+O(db^2).
$$
The shell is stable when $1+r>0$, including a small negative critical mass. Because $d\Lambda/db=-\Lambda$, differentiating the dimensionless couplings contributes their <engineering dimensions> $2$ and $\epsilon$. Together with the shell corrections this yields the <renormalization-group flow>
$$
\boxed{\frac{dr}{db}=2r+\frac{K_d}{2}\frac{\lambda}{1+r},
\qquad
\frac{d\lambda}{db}=\epsilon\lambda-
\frac{3K_d}{2}\frac{\lambda^2}{(1+r)^2}.}
$$
These are the requested equations with $r=u^2$. They express independence of the long-distance theory from the arbitrarily chosen <ultraviolet cutoff>, to the retained perturbative order; higher interactions and gradient corrections enter at higher orders.

Besides the <Gaussian fixed point> $(r,\lambda)=(0,0)$, a nonzero fixed point satisfies
$$
\lambda_*=\frac{2\epsilon}{3K_d}(1+r_*)^2,\qquad
0=2r_*+\frac{\epsilon}{3}(1+r_*).
$$
Thus, within these truncated equations,
$$
r_*=-\frac{\epsilon}{6+\epsilon},\qquad
\lambda_*=\frac{2\epsilon}{3K_d}
\left(\frac6{6+\epsilon}\right)^2.
$$
Since $\Omega_4=2\pi^2$ and $K_4=1/(8\pi^2)$, their leading <epsilon expansion> is
$$
\boxed{r_*=-\frac{\epsilon}{6}+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\lambda_*=\frac{16\pi^2\epsilon}{3}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
The rational expressions from the truncated flow are not a calculation of the genuine higher-order coefficients.

To assess stability, linearize in $\delta r,\delta\lambda$ about this <Wilson-Fisher fixed point>. The <stability matrix of a renormalization-group fixed point> is
$$
\mathsf M=
\begin{pmatrix}
2-\dfrac{K_d\lambda_*}{2(1+r_*)^2}
&\dfrac{K_d}{2(1+r_*)}\\
\dfrac{3K_d\lambda_*^2}{(1+r_*)^3}
&\epsilon-\dfrac{3K_d\lambda_*}{(1+r_*)^2}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Using the fixed-point equation, its diagonal entries are $2-\epsilon/3$ and $-\epsilon$, respectively. Its upper off-diagonal entry is $1/(16\pi^2)+O(\epsilon)$ and its lower off-diagonal entry is $O(\epsilon^2)$. Hence its <eigenvalues> are
$$
y_t=2-\frac{\epsilon}{3}+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
y_{\mathrm{irr}}=-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
The interaction perturbation along the <critical surface> decays towards the infrared for small positive $\epsilon$, whereas the thermal perturbation grows. Therefore \b[infrared attraction requires tuning to the critical surface]. The source's unqualified wording cannot mean attraction in both coupling directions: the positive thermal eigenvalue is precisely what produces the divergence of the <correlation length>. This is the <thermal relevant direction at the Wilson-Fisher fixed point>.

Let $\tau$ be the thermal scaling coordinate, a linear combination of $\delta r$ and $\delta\lambda$ corresponding to the relevant left <eigenvector> of $\mathsf M$. To leading order, $\tau=\delta r+[1/(32\pi^2)+O(\epsilon)]\delta\lambda$ is possible. Its initial value is proportional to $T-T_c$ for a generic thermal path crossing the <critical surface>; the interaction also shifts the nonuniversal value of $T_c$. In the linear regime,
$$
\tau(b)\simeq e^{y_tb}\tau(0).
$$
Choose $b_*$ so that $|\tau(b_*)|$ is of order one. At this scale the dimensionless <correlation length> is finite. Restoring the eliminated length scale gives
$$
\xi\asymp\Lambda_0^{-1}e^{b_*}
\asymp|\tau(0)|^{-1/y_t}
\asymp|T-T_c|^{-\nu}.
$$
Consequently the <correlation-length critical exponent> is
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac1{2-\epsilon/3+O(\epsilon^2)}
=\frac12+\frac{\epsilon}{12}+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
There is no field anomalous dimension at one loop in this scalar quartic theory, since the one-loop self-energy is momentum independent; this is consistent with $\eta=O(\epsilon^2)$.
