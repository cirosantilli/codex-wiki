= Solution

Write $K_s(t)$ for a <balanced complete multipartite blow-up> with $s$ classes of size $t$. In the nonvacuous range $0<\varepsilon<1/r$, the answer is
$$
\boxed{t(r,\varepsilon,n)=\Theta_{r,\varepsilon}(\log n).}
$$
We prove both bounds, including the quantitative lower bound.

First, suppose a <bipartite graph> with parts $A,B$, of sizes $m,N$, has at least $pmN$ <edges>. For any <integer> $s\le pm/2$, counting pairs consisting of an $s$-subset of $A$ and a <common neighbour> in $B$ gives
$$
\sum_{S\in\binom As}|N(S)|=\sum_{v\in B}\binom{d_A(v)}s\ge N\binom{\lfloor pm\rfloor}s.
$$
The inequality follows from discrete <convexity>: $\binom{x+1}s-\binom xs=\binom{x}{s-1}$ is nondecreasing, so balancing <integer> <degrees of vertices> minimizes their sum. Consequently some $S$ has
$$
|N(S)|\ge N\frac{\binom{\lfloor pm\rfloor}s}{\binom ms}
\ge N\left(\frac{pm-s}{m}\right)^s\ge N(p/2)^s.
$$
The product estimate uses $\lfloor pm\rfloor-j\ge pm-s$ for $0\le j<s$. This is a <common neighbourhood from bipartite density> bound.

We next prove by <induction> on $q\ge1$ that, for every fixed $\alpha>0$, a <graph> $J$ of order $N$ with <minimum degree> at least $(1-1/q+\alpha)N$ contains $K_{q+1}(\lfloor c\log N\rfloor)$, for some $c=c(q,\alpha)>0$ and all sufficiently large $N$. Impossible minimum-<vertex degree> parameters need no argument. For $q=1$, take two copies of $V(J)$ with adjacency given by $J$. The preceding count with $p=\alpha$ and $s=\lfloor c\log N\rfloor$, where $c\log(2/\alpha)<1/2$, gives at least $\sqrt N$ <common neighbours>. The selected <vertices> and their <common neighbours> are disjoint in $J$, since a <vertex> is not its own <neighbour>. Selecting $s$ <common neighbours> gives $K_2(s)$.

For $q\ge2$, the <minimum degree> hypothesis implies the <induction> hypothesis for $q-1$, for example with excess $1/[q(q-1)]$. Thus $J$ contains $K_q(m)$ with $m=\lfloor a\log N\rfloor$ for a positive constant $a$. Pair the <vertices> in its classes into $m$ disjoint transversal <cliques> $C_1,\ldots,C_m$, each of order $q$. By the <union bound>, every $C_i$ has at least
$$
N-q\bigl(N-\delta(J)\bigr)\ge q\alpha N
$$
<common neighbours>. Form an auxiliary <bipartite graph> between these $m$ <cliques> and the $N$ <vertices>, using common-neighbour incidence, and put $p=q\alpha$. Choose $c>0$ small enough that $c<pa/3$ and $c\log(2/p)<1/2$. The count above, with $s=\lfloor c\log N\rfloor$, gives $s$ <cliques> with at least $\sqrt N$ simultaneous <common neighbours>. Their <vertices> form $q$ classes of size $s$, and $s$ of those <common neighbours> form the last class. None of the <common neighbours> belongs to a selected <clique>, because that would require a loop. This proves the <logarithmic clique blow-up from minimum degree> assertion.

To pass from an <edge> count to <minimum degree>, put $b=1-1/r+\varepsilon$ and $a=b-\varepsilon/2$. Repeatedly remove a <vertex> whose current <degree of a vertex> is below $a$ times the current order. If the process reaches order $m$, its deleted <edges> and remaining <edges> give
$$
b\binom n2\le e(G)\le a\sum_{j=m+1}^n j+\binom m2
=\frac a2(n^2+n-m^2-m)+\frac12(m^2-m).
$$
Thus $(1-a)m^2\ge (\varepsilon/2)n^2-O(n)$. In particular, for a fixed sufficiently small $\gamma>0$, the process cannot reach $m=\lfloor\gamma n\rfloor$: the displayed inequality would fail. It stops earlier with a subgraph of order $N\ge\gamma n$ and <minimum degree> at least $(1-1/r+\varepsilon/2)N$. The <induction> just proved supplies $K_{r+1}(\lfloor c\log N\rfloor)$, hence a lower bound $c'\log n$ on $t(r,\varepsilon,n)$. In particular, **the guaranteed part size tends to infinity**.

For the upper bound, choose a fixed $p$ with $b<p<1$ and take a <binomial random graph> $G(n,p)$. Its <edge> count has <expectation> $p\binom n2$ and <variance> $p(1-p)\binom n2$. The <Chebyshev inequality> shows that $e(G)\ge b\binom n2$ with <probability> tending to one. The expected number of labeled copies of $K_{r+1}(t)$ is at most
$$
n^{(r+1)t}p^{\binom{r+1}2t^2}.
$$
For $t=\lceil C\log n\rceil$ and $C>2/[r\log(1/p)]$, this tends to zero. The <Markov inequality> therefore shows that with positive <probability> both the required <edge> density holds and there is no such copy. This proves $t(r,\varepsilon,n)=O(\log n)$ and completes the <random obstruction to larger clique blow-ups> argument.

There is a necessary qualification to the density parameter. If $\varepsilon=1/r$, the only eligible <graph> is the <complete graph>, and **$t(r,1/r,n)=\lfloor n/(r+1)\rfloor$**. If $\varepsilon>1/r$ and $n\ge2$, there are no eligible simple <graphs>; the universal assertion is vacuous for every $t$, so the printed maximum has no finite value in that range.

Each <vertex> of a <bipartite graph> has a strict <preference> ordering of its <neighbours>; every <neighbour> is preferred to being unmatched. A <stable matching in a bipartite graph> is a <matching in a graph> with no blocking <edge>: an unused <edge> blocks if both endpoints prefer each other to their assigned partners, interpreting an unmatched endpoint as willing to accept any <neighbour>. Stability does not require a perfect <matching in a graph>.

For existence, run the <Gale-Shapley algorithm>. Whenever a <vertex> in the first part is unmatched and has an untried <neighbour>, it proposes to its most preferred untried <neighbour>. A receiver keeps its preferred proposal and rejects the other, including any previously held proposal. At most $e(G)$ proposals occur, so the procedure stops with a <matching in a graph>. Suppose an <edge> $bg$ blocked this final <matching in a graph>. Since $b$ prefers $g$ to its final partner, or is unmatched, $b$ must have proposed to $g$. But a receiver's held proposal only improves, so $g$'s final partner is preferred to $b$ if $b$ was rejected. If $b$ was not rejected, the <edge> is in the <matching in a graph>. Both alternatives contradict blocking. **Every finite bipartite preference instance therefore has a <stable matching>.**

For the <mutual-worst edge in stable matchings>, let $M,N$ be <stable matchings> with $bg\in M$ and $bg\notin N$. We give the argument using alternating <graph components> including possible unmatched endpoints. If $b$ were unmatched in $N$, then $g$ would have to prefer its $N$-partner to $b$, since otherwise $bg$ would block $N$. Following the $N$-edge from $g$, stability of $M$ forces its other endpoint to have and prefer an $M$-partner. Stability of $N$ then forces the next <vertex> to have and prefer an $N$-partner. These implications continue along the alternating <graph component> of $M\mathbin\triangle N$. They cannot stop, because at a missing next <edge> the preceding <edge> would block the appropriate <matching in a graph>. They also cannot close into a <cycle in a graph>, since the <graph component> already has endpoint $b$. This contradiction proves that $b$ is matched in $N$. The same reasoning applies to $g$.

Now $b$ prefers its $N$-partner to $g$, because $g$ is its worst choice. Along the alternating <graph component>, stability of $M$ forces that partner to prefer its $M$-partner; stability of $N$ forces the next <vertex> in $b$'s part to prefer its $N$-partner, and so on. A <graph path> <graph component> is impossible by the same endpoint argument, so this is a <cycle in a graph>. Thus throughout this <cycle in a graph> the part containing $b$ prefers $N$ and the part containing $g$ prefers $M$. In particular $g$ must prefer $b$ to its $N$-partner. That contradicts $b$ being $g$'s worst choice. Hence **an <edge> ranked last by both endpoints, if present in one <stable matching>, is present in every <stable matching>**.

For the <list edge coloring> assertion, let $\Delta$ be the <maximum degree>. We first prove that the <chromatic index> is $\Delta$. Use <induction> on the number of <edges>, with a proper $\Delta$-<edge coloring> of $G-uv$. Some color $a$ is missing at $u$ and some color $b$ is missing at $v$. If $a=b$, color $uv$ with it. Otherwise the <edges> of colors $a,b$ form alternating <graph paths> and even <cycles in a graph>. The <graph component> containing $u$ cannot contain $v$: an alternating <graph path> from $u$, starting with color $b$, would end at $v$ with color $a$, have even length, and place $u,v$ in the same bipartition class. Swap $a,b$ in the <graph component> containing $u$. Now $b$ is missing at both ends of $uv$, so it can be colored $b$. The lower bound $\chi'(G)\ge\Delta$ is immediate at a <vertex> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$.

Fix such a proper <coloring> $c:E(G)\to\{1,\ldots,\Delta\}$. Orient the <line graph> toward the smaller color at <vertices> in the first part of $G$, and toward the larger color at <vertices> in the second part. A <vertex> of the <line graph> corresponding to an <edge> of color $j$ has <outdegree> at most
$$
(j-1)+(\Delta-j)=\Delta-1.
$$
Every <induced subgraph> of this orientation has a <digraph kernel>. Indeed, restrict to its <edge> <set> in the original <bipartite graph>, give first-part <vertices> a preference for smaller colors and second-part <vertices> a preference for larger colors, and choose a <stable matching>. Its <edges> form an <independent set> in the <line graph>. Every <edge> outside the <matching in a graph> has an endpoint whose partner is preferred to it, by stability; thus it has an outgoing <edge> to the <independent set>. This proves that the orientation is a <kernel-perfect directed graph>.

Here is the <kernel list-coloring lemma> with its proof. If a <kernel-perfect directed graph> $D$ has lists satisfying $|L(v)|\ge d_D^+(v)+1$, choose any color $a$ appearing in a list and a <digraph kernel> $K$ of the <induced subgraph> on <vertices> whose lists contain $a$. Color $K$ with $a$, delete $K$, and delete $a$ from the remaining lists. Each remaining <vertex> that loses a color had an outgoing <neighbour> in $K$, so its <outdegree> decreases by at least one; the list inequality persists. <Vertices> not losing a color also retain the inequality. <Induction> on the number of <vertices> completes a proper list <coloring>.

Lists of size $\Delta$ satisfy that lemma for our oriented <line graph>, so $\chi'_\ell(G)\le\Delta$. Identical lists show $\chi'_\ell(G)\ge\chi'(G)$. Consequently the <Galvin theorem for bipartite list edge coloring> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi'_\ell(G)=\chi'(G)=\Delta(G).}
$$
The empty <graph> is immediate; if parallel <edges> are allowed, orient their line-graph adjacency in both directions when the two endpoints prescribe opposite directions. The <outdegree> bound still holds, and the same <stable matching> and <digraph kernel> proof applies with preferences on incident <edges>.

A <linked graph> is $k$-linked if it has at least $2k$ <vertices> and, for every choice of distinct terminals $s_1,\ldots,s_k,t_1,\ldots,t_k$, it has pairwise vertex-disjoint <graph paths> joining $s_i$ to $t_i$ for all $i$. The pairing is prescribed, which is stronger than the unpaired conclusion of the <Menger theorem>.

We prove the <rooted dense minor linkage> step carefully. We first prove the <rooted branch-set reduction>. Let $q\ge2$ be even, let $S=\{x_1,\ldots,x_q\}$, and suppose a <graph> has $h$ disjoint nonempty <vertex> <sets> $C_1,\ldots,C_h$, where $h\ge d+3q/2$. Suppose each $G[C_i]$ is connected or every one of its <graph components> meets $S$, and each $C_i$ is adjacent to all but at most $d$ of the other $C_j$ not meeting $S$. Assume also that no <rooted separation of a graph> of order below $q$ avoids $d+1$ of these <sets>. Then there are $m=h-q/2$ disjoint connected <vertex> <sets> $D_1,\ldots,D_m$ such that $x_i\in D_i$ for $1\le i\le q$ and each of these first $q$ <sets> is adjacent to all but at most $d$ of $D_{q+1},\ldots,D_m$.

Here adjacency of <sets> means existence of an <edge> between them. A <rooted separation of a graph> is $(A,B)$ with $A\cup B=V(G)$, $S\subseteq A$, no <edges> between $A\setminus B$ and $B\setminus A$, and order $|A\cap B|$. It avoids $C_i$ when $A\cap C_i=\varnothing$. We prove the reduction by <induction>, first on the <vertex> count and then on the <edge> count; any counterexample chosen minimal in this ordering will be impossible.

We may delete <edges> joining two <vertices> of $S$. They cannot affect <rooted separations of a graph>, since $S$ lies wholly on the first side. Within a branch <set>, their deletion can only split a <graph component> into <graph components> each containing an endpoint in $S$, so the branch-set hypothesis also persists. An isolated <vertex> outside $S$ and all branch <sets> can be deleted. An isolated <vertex> $v\in S$ is also impossible, since $(S,V(G)\setminus\{v\})$ has order $q-1$ and avoids at least $h-q\ge d+q/2\ge d+1$ <sets>. Thus in a minimal counterexample $S$ is independent, and every isolated <vertex> outside $S$ belongs to a singleton branch <set>.

Consider a <rooted separation of a graph> $(A,B)$ of order exactly $q$ avoiding at least $d+1$ branch <sets>, and put $S'=A\cap B$. Restrict to $G'=G[B]-E(G[S'])$ and $C'_i=C_i\cap B$. Each $C'_i$ is nonempty: any $C_i$ not itself avoided is adjacent to at least one of the $d+1$ avoided <sets>, and the endpoint of such an <edge> in $C_i$ must lie in $B$. If a <graph component> of $G'[C'_i]$ misses $S'$, it lies in $B\setminus A$ and is a whole <graph component> of $G[C_i]$, with no <vertex> of $S$. The original hypothesis then forces $G[C_i]$ to be connected and $C'_i=C_i$, entirely outside $A$. Thus each restricted <set> is connected or all its <graph components> meet $S'$. In particular every $C'_j$ missing $S'$ equals an original <set> outside $A$, so all its required adjacencies from $C'_i$ persist.

A <rooted separation of a graph> $(A',B')$ of $G'$ avoiding $d+1$ restricted <sets> lifts to $(A\cup A',B')$ in $G$, of the same order: an avoided restricted <set> misses $S'$, hence is an original <set> outside $A$. The <edges> removed inside $S'$ do not obstruct lifting, since $S'\subseteq A'$. This proves the required separation hypothesis for $G'$. If $G'$ is smaller, <induction> gives the desired connected <sets> rooted at $S'$. Moreover $G[A]$ has $q$ disjoint <graph paths> from $S$ to $S'$, by the <Menger theorem>. Indeed a separator of smaller order between these <sets> would give a <rooted separation of a graph> of $G$ of smaller order still avoiding those $d+1$ original <sets>. Truncate the <graph paths> at their first visits to $S'$, label their endpoints accordingly, and adjoin each <graph path> to the corresponding rooted connected <set>. They meet $B$ only in their distinct endpoints, so this gives the desired <sets> for $G$, a contradiction. Consequently in a minimal counterexample every such separation of order $q$ has $B=V(G)$ and $A=S$, an <independent set>.

Now contract any <edge> whose endpoints do not belong to two different branch <sets>. Its endpoints cannot both lie in $S$, so the image of $S$ still has size $q$. The branch-set conditions persist. If the contraction created a forbidden <rooted separation of a graph> of order below $q$, its lift would have order below $q$, except possibly when the contracted <vertex> lies in its separator. In that case the lifted order increases by one and is at most $q$. Equality would put the contracted <edge> inside $A$, contradicting the just-proved independence of $A$ for an order-$q$ separation avoiding $d+1$ <sets>. Thus the separation hypothesis also persists. Applying <induction> in the contracted <graph> and expanding the contracted <vertex> would produce the required disjoint connected <sets>. Hence **every <edge> in a minimal counterexample joins two different branch <sets>**.

Every <vertex> outside the branch <sets> would be isolated, since every <edge> joins two branch <sets>; such an isolated <vertex> could be deleted. Thus the branch <sets> cover the <graph>. Each nonsingleton branch <set> is therefore an <independent set>, and all its <vertices> belong to $S$, because each of its singleton <graph components> must meet $S$. Let $C$ be the union of the nonsingleton branch <sets>, and write $c=|C|\le q$. There is a <matching in a graph> from $C$ into $V(G)\setminus S$. Otherwise the <Hall marriage theorem> gives $X\subseteq C$ with neighbourhood $Y\subseteq V(G)\setminus S$ satisfying $|Y|<|X|$. Since $S$ is independent, $(S\cup Y,V(G)\setminus X)$ is a <rooted separation of a graph> of order $q-|X|+|Y|<q$. It avoids every singleton branch <set> outside $S\cup Y$, of which there are at least
$$
|V(G)|-q-|Y|\ge |V(G)|-q-c+1\ge h-\frac c2-q+1\ge h-\frac{3q}2+1\ge d+1.
$$
The penultimate counting bound uses that the nonsingleton <sets> number at most $c/2$, so $|V(G)|-c\ge h-c/2$. This contradicts the separation hypothesis.

Use that <matching in a graph> to make a two-vertex connected <set> for each terminal in $C$; terminals outside $C$ remain singleton <sets>. There are at least
$$
|V(G)|-q-c\ge h-\frac{3q}2=m-q
$$
unused <vertices>, all singleton original branch <sets>. Choose $m-q$ of them for the remaining $D_i$. Every rooted $D_i$ contains a singleton original branch <set>: either its terminal, or its terminal's matched <neighbour>. That singleton misses at most $d$ of the selected nonterminal singleton <sets>. This constructs exactly the desired family, contradicting minimality and proving the branch-set reduction.

Return to the given $22k$-connected <graph>. By the allowed assumption it has a <graph minor> $H$ with $2\delta(H)\ge h+4k-1$, where $h=|H|$. Put $d=h-1-\delta(H)$. Then
$$
h\ge2d+4k+1.
$$
Take a branch-set model of $H$ and the $2k$ prescribed terminals as $S$. The branch <sets> are connected and each misses at most $d$ of the others. The separation hypothesis holds with $q=2k$: a separation of order below $2k$ avoiding a branch <set> would separate a surviving terminal from that <set>, contrary even to $2k$-connectivity. Since $h\ge d+3k$, the reduction applies. It produces $m=h-k$ <sets> with $s_i\in D_i$, $t_i\in D_{k+i}$, each terminal <set> missing at most $d$ of the $m-2k$ nonterminal <sets>. For each pair $D_i,D_{k+i}$ at least
$$
m-2k-2d=h-3k-2d\ge k+1
$$
nonterminal <sets> are adjacent to both. Choose different such <sets> for the $k$ pairs successively. The <connected graph> property inside the three <sets> and the two joining <edges> give a <graph path> from $s_i$ to $t_i$; take a simple <graph path> within their union. Different <graph paths> use disjoint <sets>. We have proved
$$
\boxed{\kappa(G)\ge22k\ \Longrightarrow\ G\text{ is }k\text{-linked}.}
$$
In fact the argument proves the stronger transfer statement with $2k$-connectivity and the stated dense <graph minor>.

For the negative assertion, it suffices to give a value of $k$ for which the proposed general implication fails. Take $k=2$ and the <graph> on cyclic indices $0,\ldots,7$ in which two indices are adjacent when their cyclic distance is one or two. This is the <four-connected planar obstruction to two-linkage>. Draw the even <cycle in a graph> $0,2,4,6$ as the outer square, the odd <cycle in a graph> $1,3,5,7$ as an inner square rotated by $45$ <degrees of vertices>, and join each odd <vertex> to the two neighbouring even <vertices>; the annulus is triangulated without crossings. The outer face has <vertices> $0,2,4,6$ in this order.

\Image[paper-9-antiprism.png]
{title=A four-connected planar graph with alternating terminal pairs on a square face}

Deleting at most three <vertices> leaves the <graph> connected. In cyclic order, consecutive surviving <vertices> are joined unless separated by a run of at least two deleted <vertices>. Disconnection requires two distinct such runs, hence at least four deletions. Conversely deleting the four <neighbours> of a <vertex> isolates it, so the <vertex connectivity> is exactly four. Disjoint <graph paths> joining $0$ to $4$ and $2$ to $6$ cannot exist: both would lie in the closed disk complementary to the outer face, and their endpoints alternate on its boundary. A first such <graph path> is a crosscut of the disk; the <Jordan curve theorem> puts the other two endpoints on opposite sides, forcing the second <graph path> to meet it. Thus
$$
\boxed{\kappa(G)=4=3\cdot2-2,\qquad G\text{ is not }2\text{-linked}.}
$$
This is a counterexample to the unrestricted implication; no claim that the same construction works for every $k$ is needed.

We use the following precise form of the <Szemerédi regularity lemma>. For every $0<\eta<1/2$ and positive <integer> $m_0$, there are <integers> $M,n_0$ such that every <graph> on $n\ge n_0$ <vertices> has a partition
$$
V(G)=V_0\sqcup V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_k,
\qquad m_0\le k\le M,\quad |V_0|\le\eta n,\quad |V_1|=\cdots=|V_k|,
$$
with at most $\eta k^2$ irregular unordered pairs among the nonexceptional classes. Here $d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(|A||B|)$ for disjoint nonempty <sets>, and $(A,B)$ is an $\eta$-<regular pair> if
$$
|d(X,Y)-d(A,B)|\le\eta
$$
whenever $X\subseteq A$, $Y\subseteq B$, $|X|\ge\eta|A|$, $|Y|\ge\eta|B|$.

For the proof, let $f(x,y)$ be the adjacency indicator, including $f(x,x)=0$, and define $d(A,B)$ by averaging $f$ on $A\times B$ even for diagonal cells. The <regularity energy> of a partition $\mathcal P$ is
$$
q(\mathcal P)=\sum_{A,B\in\mathcal P}\frac{|A||B|}{n^2}d(A,B)^2,\qquad 0\le q\le1.
$$
Every exceptional <vertex> is represented by its own singleton cell in this energy. If a rectangle $A\times B$ is refined into smaller rectangles with densities $d_{ij}$ and weights $w_{ij}=|A_i||B_j|$, expanding the square gives the <refinement variance identity for regularity energy>
$$
\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}d_{ij}^2-|A||B|d(A,B)^2
=\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}(d_{ij}-d(A,B))^2\ge0.
$$
Thus refinement never decreases energy.

Suppose an equitable partition with $k$ classes of size $L$ fails the desired conclusion. For every <irregular pair> choose witness subsets $X\subseteq V_i$, $Y\subseteq V_j$, each of size at least $\eta L$, whose density differs from $d(V_i,V_j)$ by more than $\eta$. Split each class by all witness subsets involving it. Each class has at most $2^{k-1}$ atoms. On a witness rectangle, the weighted mean of $d_{ab}-d(V_i,V_j)$ is $d(X,Y)-d(V_i,V_j)$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> therefore bounds the energy increment on this rectangle below by
$$
\frac{|X||Y|}{n^2}\bigl(d(X,Y)-d(V_i,V_j)\bigr)^2
>\eta^4\frac{L^2}{n^2}.
$$
The different original cell pairs have disjoint rectangles. More than $\eta k^2$ irregular unordered pairs, with both orientations counted in the energy, thus increase it by more than $2\eta^5k^2L^2/n^2$. As long as $|V_0|\le\eta n<n/2$, this is greater than $\eta^5/2$, and in particular greater than $\eta^5/4$.

Restore equitability by dividing each atom into pieces of common size $\ell=\lfloor L/4^k\rfloor$, moving each leftover <vertex> into the exceptional <set> as a singleton energy cell. This remains a refinement, so the energy gain persists. There are at most $k2^k$ atoms, so the additional exceptional <vertices> number at most
$$
k2^k\ell\le kL/2^k\le n/2^k.
$$
If $L\ge2\cdot4^k$, then $\ell\ge L/(2\cdot4^k)$, and the new number $k'$ of equal cells satisfies $k\le k'\le4k4^k$. For the lower bound, within each old class the leftover count is at most $2^k\ell<L$, so at least one full new cell survives. For the upper bound, $n/\ell\le2(n/L)4^k\le4k4^k$, using $kL\ge n/2$. This proves <equalization with a controlled exceptional set> without any energy loss.

Put $T=\lceil4\eta^{-5}\rceil+1$. Choose $k_0\ge m_0$ with $T2^{-k_0}\le\eta/2$. Start with $k_0$ equal classes and fewer than $k_0$ exceptional <vertices>. Iterate the bounded recurrence $k\mapsto4k4^k$ $T$ times to obtain a bound $M$. Choose $n_0$ so large that the initial remainder is at most $\eta n/2$ and, for every $k\le M$, $(1-\eta)n/k\ge2\cdot4^k$. The total exceptional <set> remains at most $\eta n$, since at most $T$ rounds each add at most $n2^{-k_0}$. Each failing round raises an energy in $[0,1]$ by more than $\eta^5/4$, so fewer than $T$ rounds can fail. This proves the lemma with all constants independent of the input <graph>.

For the application, write $h=|V(F)|$ and $\chi(F)=r+1$. We may replace the given tolerance by a smaller one, so assume $0<\varepsilon<1$. <Set> $d=\varepsilon/4$, take $m_0\ge8/\varepsilon$, and choose
$$
0<\eta<\min\left\{\frac\varepsilon8,\frac d2,\frac{(d/2)^h}{4h}\right\}.
$$
Apply the lemma and delete all <edges> incident with $V_0$, all <edges> inside a class, all <edges> across <irregular pairs>, and all <edges> across <regular pairs> of density below $d$. The resulting spanning subgraph $H$ loses at most
$$
\eta n^2+\frac{n^2}{2k}+\eta k^2L^2+\frac d2 k^2L^2
\le\left(2\eta+\frac1{2m_0}+\frac d2\right)n^2
<\varepsilon n^2.
$$
Let $R$ be the <reduced graph of a regularity partition>, whose <edges> represent the surviving <regular pairs> of density at least $d$. We show that a copy of $K_{r+1}$ in $R$ forces a copy of $F$ in $G$.

Choose a proper $(r+1)$-coloring of $F$ and assign its color classes to the corresponding clusters. Embed its <vertices> one at a time. For each unembedded <vertex> maintain the candidate <set> in its cluster consisting of unused <vertices> adjacent to all already embedded <neighbours>. After at most $h$ restrictions its size is at least $(d-\eta)^hL-h\ge(d/2)^hL-h$. This last quantity is at least $2h\eta L$ when $L$ is large, so every such candidate <set> is large enough for regularity. In an $\eta$-regular pair of density $p\ge d$, fewer than $\eta L$ <vertices> on one side have fewer than $(p-\eta)|Y|$ <neighbours> in any fixed <set> $Y$ on the other side of size at least $\eta L$: otherwise these <vertices> and $Y$ violate regularity. For the <vertex> currently being embedded, there are at most $h$ relevant future candidate <sets>. At most $h\eta L$ <vertices> are bad for one of them. Its own candidate <set> has at least $2h\eta L$ <vertices>, so an unused good <vertex> can be chosen. All future candidate <sets> shrink by at most the factor $d-\eta$, apart from the removal of a used <vertex>; the stated size bound follows inductively, since the accumulated removals are at most $h$. This completes the <graph embedding lemma for regular pairs> in the form needed here.

Since $G$ is $F$-free, $R$ is $K_{r+1}$-free. Any $K_{r+1}$ in $H$ would use distinct clusters, because intraclass <edges> were deleted, and project to a $K_{r+1}$ in $R$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{K_{r+1}\not\subseteq H,\qquad e(G)-e(H)<\varepsilon n^2.}
$$
The required threshold on $n$ depends only on $F$ and $\varepsilon$, since $k\le M$ makes every nonexceptional cluster sufficiently large.
