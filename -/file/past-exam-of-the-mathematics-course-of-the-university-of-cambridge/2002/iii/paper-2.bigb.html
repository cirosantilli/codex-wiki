Let $\operatorname{Irr}(G)$ denote the <irreducible characters> of finite-dimensional complex <group representations> of $G$. A <generalized character>, or <virtual character>, is an integral linear combination of these <irreducible characters>. A <class function> is a function constant on <conjugacy classes>. For <class functions> use the <character inner product>
$$
\langle f,h\rangle_G=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\overline{h(g)}.
$$
A <p-elementary group> is a <direct product of groups> $P\times C$, where $P$ is a <p-group> and $C$ is a <cyclic group> of order coprime to $p$. An <elementary subgroup> is a <subgroup> of this form for some prime $p$. Either factor may be trivial. This notion is different from an <elementary abelian group>.

<Brauer's characterization of characters> says that a complex <class function> $f$ on $G$ is a <generalized character> exactly when its restriction to every <elementary subgroup> is a <generalized character> there. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{f\in\mathbb Z\operatorname{Irr}(G)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\langle f_E,\alpha\rangle_E\in\mathbb Z\ \text{for every elementary }E\leq G,\ \alpha\in\operatorname{Irr}(E)}.
$$
The <character orthogonality> relations make these inner products the coefficients of the restrictions in their irreducible bases. The theorem detects the integral <character> lattice. An ordinary <character of a representation> additionally requires every global irreducible coefficient $\langle f,\chi\rangle_G$ to be nonnegative; integrality alone characterizes virtual <characters>, not necessarily actual <representations>.

If $A$ is empty, take the zero <generalized character>; if $B$ is empty, take the <trivial character>. Otherwise let $\pi$ be the set of primes dividing the orders of elements of $A$. The coprimality hypothesis makes every element of $B$ a $\pi'$-element. Thus every nonidentity element of $G$ is either a <pi-element> in $A$ or a complementary-prime element in $B$. In particular these sets are unions of <conjugacy classes>, since conjugation preserves element order.

Put $m=|G|_\pi$ and $n=|G|_{\pi'}$. Their <greatest common divisor> is one, so the <Chinese remainder theorem> supplies an integer $d$ such that
$$
d\equiv1\pmod m,\qquad d\equiv0\pmod n.
$$
Define the <class function> $f$ by $f(1)=d$, $f=1$ on $A$ and $f=0$ on $B$. We verify <Brauer's characterization of characters> directly.

For an <elementary subgroup> $E=P\times C$, the cyclic factor can be split into its $\pi$-part and $\pi'$-part. Attach $P$ to the part containing its prime. This expresses $E=E_\pi\times E_{\pi'}$. Both factors cannot be nontrivial: choosing a nonidentity element in each would give a commuting product of mixed prime order, whereas every element of $G$ has order involving only one of the two prime sets. Hence $E$ is wholly a $\pi$-group or wholly a $\pi'$-group.

Let $r_E$ be the <character> of the <regular representation> of $E$, with value $|E|$ at the identity and zero elsewhere. In the two cases respectively,
$$
f_E=1_E+\frac{d-1}{|E|}r_E,\qquad
f_E=\frac d{|E|}r_E.
$$
The coefficients are integers because $|E|$ divides $m$ or $n$. Thus both are <generalized characters>. The trivial <subgroup> causes no difficulty since $d$ is an integer. By <Brauer's characterization of characters>, $f$ is a <generalized character> of $G$.

**Taking $\vartheta=f$ gives the required values.**

This is a <generalized character separating complementary prime elements>; the freely chosen value at the identity is what makes all elementary restrictions integral.

A <Frobenius group> is a finite <transitive group action> on more than one point in which a nonidentity element fixes at most one point and a point stabilizer is nontrivial. Equivalently it has a nontrivial proper <subgroup> $H$ with $H\cap H^g=1$ for every $g\notin H$. The <Frobenius complement> is such a point stabilizer $H$.

The <Frobenius kernel> is the identity together with all fixed-point-free elements in this action, equivalently
$$
N=\{1\}\cup\left(G\setminus\bigcup_{g\in G}H^g\right).
$$
The <Frobenius kernel theorem> asserts that this set is a <normal subgroup>, acts regularly on the points, and gives $G=N\rtimes H$. In particular $C_N(h)=1$ for $1\ne h\in H$.

<Brauer's permutation lemma> states that an <automorphism> of a <finite group> fixes equally many <conjugacy classes> and <irreducible characters>. More generally a group of automorphisms has equal <permutation characters> on those two sets. Indeed, the <character table> is invertible and intertwines the two permutation matrices: simultaneous permutation of rows and columns preserves <character> evaluation. Similar matrices have equal traces, and a permutation-matrix trace counts its fixed points. The fixed objects on the group side are classes, not individual elements.

Suppose first that $N$ is the <Frobenius kernel>. An element $g\notin N$ fixes a point in the Frobenius action. If $n\in C_N(g)$, then $n$ maps that point to another point fixed by $g$. Since $g$ has only one fixed point and $N$ acts regularly, $n=1$. Thus
$$
C_N(g)=1\qquad(g\notin N).
$$
No nonidentity <conjugacy class> of $N$ can be fixed by such a $g$. If the class of $n\ne1$ were fixed, $gng^{-1}=vnv^{-1}$ for some $v\in N$, so $v^{-1}g\notin N$ would centralize $n$, a contradiction. Conjugation by $g$ consequently fixes just the identity class. By <Brauer's permutation lemma> it fixes just one <irreducible character>, necessarily $1_N$.

For every nonprincipal $\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)$, its <inertia group of a character> is therefore $I_G(\varphi)=N$. Since $N$ is normal, the restriction of the <induced character> is the sum of its conjugates, counted over $G/N$:
$$
(\varphi^G)_N=\sum_{gN\in G/N}\varphi^g.
$$
<Frobenius reciprocity> and <character orthogonality> give
$$
\langle\varphi^G,\varphi^G\rangle_G
=\langle\varphi,(\varphi^G)_N\rangle_N
=|I_G(\varphi):N|=1.
$$
An actual <character> of norm one is irreducible. **Every nonprincipal <irreducible character> of $N$ therefore induces irreducibly to $G$.**

Conversely, the same induction-norm formula shows that $I_G(\varphi)=N$ for every nonprincipal $\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)$. Conjugation by each $g\notin N$ fixes only $1_N$, so <Brauer's permutation lemma> says that it fixes only the identity <conjugacy class> of $N$. A nonidentity element centralizing $g$ would provide another fixed class. Therefore $C_N(g)=1$ for every $g\notin N$.

We first prove that $|N|$ and $[G:N]$ are coprime. If a prime $p$ divided both, a <Sylow p-subgroup> $S$ of $G$ would have $1<S\cap N<S$. The nontrivial <normal subgroup> $S\cap N$ meets $Z(S)$ nontrivially: the class equation for the conjugation action of the <p-group> $S$ on $S\cap N$ makes the number of fixed elements divisible by $p$, and one of them is the identity. Choose $1\ne z\in N\cap Z(S)$ and $s\in S\setminus N$. Then $z\in C_N(s)$, contradicting the previous conclusion.

Thus $N$ is a <normal subgroup> that is also a <Hall subgroup>. The <Schur-Zassenhaus theorem> supplies a <group complement> $H$, meaning $G=NH$ and $N\cap H=1$. The theorem's existence assertion requires only normality and coprimality of order and index. Here $H$ is nontrivial and proper by the assumptions on $N$.

For $n\in N\setminus\{1\}$, suppose $1\ne h\in H\cap nHn^{-1}$. Write $h=nh'n^{-1}$ with $h'\in H$. Projection to $G/N$ gives $hN=h'N$, hence $h=h'$ because $H\to G/N$ is injective. This makes $n$ centralize $h\notin N$, which is impossible. Hence $H\cap nHn^{-1}=1$. Writing an arbitrary element outside $H$ as $nh_0$ reduces its conjugate intersection to this case. It follows that $H$ is a <Frobenius complement> and $G$ is a <Frobenius group>.

Moreover, for each $1\ne h\in H$, the map $v\mapsto vhv^{-1}$ from $N$ into the coset $Nh$ is injective, since $C_N(h)=1$, and hence bijective. Thus every element outside $N$ belongs to a conjugate of $H$, while no nonidentity element of $N$ does. **The <Frobenius kernel> is exactly $N$.** This completes both directions of the <Frobenius kernel criterion by irreducible induction>.

A finite <monomial group>, also called an <M-group>, is a <finite group> for which every <irreducible character> is a <monomial character>: for every $\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)$ there are a <subgroup> $H\leq G$ and a <linear character> $\lambda$ of $H$ such that $\chi=\operatorname{Ind}_H^G\lambda$. The inducing <subgroup> and <linear character> may depend on $\chi$.

<Taketa's theorem> states that **every finite monomial group is solvable**. Here being a <solvable group> means that the iterated <derived subgroups> eventually reach the identity.

<Dade's embedding theorem for monomial groups> states that **every finite solvable group embeds as a <subgroup> of a finite monomial group**. It does not say that every solvable group is itself monomial, nor that the embedding can always be taken normal. Together the results identify solvability as a necessary property of monomial groups without making monomiality closed under taking arbitrary <subgroups>.

Let $K$ be the intersection under consideration. The family is nonempty since it contains $G$, and is invariant under conjugation: conjugating an irreducible inducing pair $(H,\varphi)$ gives another such pair. Hence $K$ is a <normal subgroup> of $G$.

For any $\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)$, monomiality gives $\chi=\lambda^G$ with $\lambda$ a <linear character> of some $H$. This $H$ belongs to the family, so $K\leq H$. Because $K$ is normal, $K\leq tHt^{-1}$ for every $t\in G$. In the <induced representation> on
$$
\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}\mathbb C_\lambda,
$$
each coset line $t\otimes\mathbb C_\lambda$ is therefore invariant under $K$: for $k\in K$,
$$
k(t\otimes v)=t\otimes\lambda(t^{-1}kt)v.
$$
Thus the restriction to $K$ is a direct sum of one-dimensional <group representations>. All representing matrices of $K$ commute, and the <derived subgroup> $K'$ acts as the identity. Equivalently,
$$
K'\leq\ker\chi\qquad\text{for every }\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G).
$$
The intersection of the <character kernels> is trivial: the <regular representation> is faithful and is a direct sum of <irreducible representations>. It follows that $K'=1$.

$$
\boxed{K\text{ is abelian}.}
$$

This proves the <abelian intersection of irreducible inducing subgroups> without assuming that every <subgroup> of an M-group is itself monomial.

Write $o(\theta)$ for the order of the <determinant character> of an <irreducible character> $\theta$. An extension has the same degree and restricts exactly to $\theta$, rather than just containing it as a constituent.

**Extension theorem.** <Thompson's coprime character extension theorem> says that if $N\triangleleft G$, $\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)$ is $G$-invariant, and $\gcd(\theta(1),[G:N])=1$, then $\theta$ extends to $G$ exactly when its <determinant character> extends to a <linear character> of $G$. In particular, if also $\gcd(o(\theta),[G:N])=1$, there is a unique extension with determinant order coprime to the index. For a <p-group> quotient, the version used below gives a canonical extension whenever both the degree and the determinant order are prime to $p$. This theorem is an assertion of extendibility, not the false statement that every invariant <irreducible character> extends.

**The p-nilpotency lemma.** Define
$$
s_p(G)=\sum_{\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G),\ p\nmid\chi(1)}\chi(1)^2,\qquad
h_p(G)=|G:G'|_p,
$$
where $m_p$ is the largest power of $p$ dividing an integer $m$. <Isaacs' character-degree criterion for p-nilpotency> asserts
$$
\boxed{h_p(G)\mid s_p(G),\qquad
G\text{ has a normal }p\text{-complement}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p\nmid\frac{s_p(G)}{h_p(G)}}.
$$
Equivalently, $s_p(G)_p=h_p(G)$. We prove both the divisibility and the criterion.

Let $N=O^p(G)$ be the <p-residual of a finite group>, the least <normal subgroup> with <p-group> quotient. It can also be described as the <subgroup> generated by all p-prime-order elements: every homomorphism to a <p-group> kills those elements, and after quotienting by their generated <subgroup> the image of every element has p-power order, so the quotient is a <p-group> by Cauchy's theorem.

The residual is p-perfect, meaning $O^p(N)=N$. Indeed $O^p(N)$ is characteristic in $N$, hence normal in $G$, and $G/O^p(N)$ is an extension of the two <p-groups> $N/O^p(N)$ and $G/N$. Minimality of $N$ forces equality. Consequently $N/N'$ has order prime to $p$, since otherwise it would have a quotient of order $p$. Every <linear character> of $N$, in particular every <determinant character> of $N$, therefore has order prime to $p$.

Let $\mathcal T$ consist of the $G$-invariant <irreducible characters> $\theta$ of $N$ of degree prime to $p$. Each extends to an <irreducible character> $\widehat\theta$ of $G$ by the extension theorem. The <irreducible characters> of $G$ lying over $\theta$ are exactly
$$
\widehat\theta\,\nu,\qquad \nu\in\operatorname{Irr}(G/N).
$$
Here is the <representation> argument for this description. If a <representation> lies over the invariant $\theta$, its $N$-module is copies of the module for $\theta$. Identify it with that module tensored with its multiplicity space. Dividing the $G$-action by the fixed extending action on the first factor leaves an action on the multiplicity space, trivial on $N$. Irreducibility is equivalent to irreducibility of that quotient action, proving the displayed parametrization.

Every irreducible degree of the <p-group> $G/N$ is a power of $p$, since it divides the group order. Thus the p-prime-degree <characters> lying over $\theta$ are exactly those with $\nu$ linear, and there are
$$
|(G/N):(G/N)'|=|G:NG'|=h_p(G)
$$
of them. For the last equality, $G/NG'$ is the largest abelian <p-group> quotient of $G$, hence the p-primary part of its <abelianization>. In particular all these <characters> have degree $\theta(1)$.

Conversely, for any $\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)$ of p-prime degree, Clifford theory writes its restriction to $N$ as $e$ times the sum of a single orbit of <irreducible characters>. The orbit size divides $[G:N]$ and also divides $\chi(1)$, so it is one; the constituent $\theta$ is invariant and has p-prime degree. Hence $\theta\in\mathcal T$. The preceding extension parametrization now forces the quotient <character> to be linear. We have therefore obtained the exact identity
$$
\boxed{s_p(G)=h_p(G)\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal T}\theta(1)^2}.
$$
This proves the required divisibility without assuming p-nilpotency.

The <p-group> $G/N$ acts on $\operatorname{Irr}(N)$ by conjugation. Every nonfixed orbit has size divisible by $p$, and degrees are constant on each orbit. Fixed <characters> whose degrees are divisible by $p$ also contribute zero modulo $p$. The <sum of squares of irreducible degrees> consequently gives
$$
\frac{s_p(G)}{h_p(G)}=\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal T}\theta(1)^2
\equiv\sum_{\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)}\theta(1)^2
=|N|\pmod p.
$$
The quotient is prime to $p$ exactly when $N$ has order prime to $p$. Since $G/N$ is a <p-group>, that is exactly the assertion that $N$ is a <normal p-complement>. Conversely, any normal p-complement equals $O^p(G)$ because it contains all p-prime-order elements and has <p-group> quotient. This finishes the lemma.

**Detection from the <group algebra>.** For <finite groups> $G$ and $H$ with isomorphic complex <group algebras>, the <Artin-Wedderburn theorem> gives
$$
\mathbb C[G]\cong\bigoplus_{\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)}M_{\chi(1)}(\mathbb C).
$$
The simple matrix-block sizes and their multiplicities are determined by the algebra isomorphism. Hence $G$ and $H$ have identical irreducible degree multisets. They have the same order, from the degree-square sum, and the same number of <linear characters>, which equals $|G:G'|$. Thus $s_p$ and $h_p$ coincide for every prime $p$. The lemma shows that one group is p-nilpotent exactly when the other is.

A <finite group> is <nilpotent> exactly when it has a normal p-complement for every prime dividing its order. For completeness, if all those complements exist, set $L_q=\bigcap_{p\ne q}K_p$, where $K_p$ is the normal p-complement. The diagonal map $G/L_q\to\prod_{p\ne q}G/K_p$ is injective, so $[G:L_q]$ is prime to $q$. At the same time, containment in each $K_p$ removes every prime other than $q$ from $|L_q|$. Thus $L_q$ is a normal Sylow q-subgroup. Distinct normal <Sylow subgroups> commute, giving the direct-product decomposition. Conversely, the direct-product decomposition of a finite nilpotent group provides all the complements. We conclude
$$
\boxed{\mathbb C[G]\cong\mathbb C[H]\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(G\text{ nilpotent}\Longleftrightarrow H\text{ nilpotent})}.
$$
This is the assertion that the <complex group algebra detects p-nilpotency> and hence nilpotency. It needs the multiplicities of the degrees, not just their distinct values.

**Normal-complement consequence.** If $p$ divides every nonlinear irreducible degree, the p-prime-degree <characters> are exactly the <linear characters>. Therefore
$$
s_p(G)=|G:G'|,\qquad s_p(G)_p=h_p(G).
$$
The lemma yields a normal p-complement immediately:
$$
\boxed{p\mid\chi(1)\text{ for every nonlinear }\chi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
G\text{ has a normal }p\text{-complement}}.
$$
This proves <Thompson's character-degree normal complement theorem>, including the abelian case where the hypothesis on nonlinear <characters> is vacuous.

Let $V$ afford the given <group representation> of $H$. Its <induced representation> is the left $G$-module
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ind}_H^G V=\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}V}.
$$
Choose representatives $t$ of the left cosets $G/H$; then it is the direct sum of the spaces $t\otimes V$. If $gt=t'h$ with $h\in H$, the action sends $t\otimes v$ to $t'\otimes\Psi(h)v$. This is independent of the representatives up to equivalence, and its dimension is $[G:H]\dim V$.

Only cosets fixed by $g$ contribute to its trace. Their contribution is $\psi(t^{-1}gt)$, giving the <induced character> formula
$$
\boxed{\psi^G(g)=\sum_{\substack{tH\in G/H\\t^{-1}gt\in H}}\psi(t^{-1}gt)
=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{\substack{x\in G\\x^{-1}gx\in H}}\psi(x^{-1}gx)}.
$$
For an arbitrary <class function> $f$ on $H$, the same averaging formula defines its induced <class function> on $G$. Equivalently, extend $f$ by zero outside $H$ and average $|H|^{-1}\sum_x f(x^{-1}gx)$. This definition is linear and does not require $f$ to be an actual <character>.

For a complex <character> $\psi$ of a <finite group> $G$ afforded by a <representation> $\rho$, its <determinant character> is
$$
\boxed{(\det\psi)(g)=\det\rho(g)}.
$$
Multiplicativity of the determinant makes this a <linear character>; it does not mean the determinant of the scalar value $\psi(g)$. The definition depends only on the <character> because <characters> determine the equivalence class of a finite-dimensional complex <representation>. In particular the <determinant character> is trivial on the <derived subgroup> $G'$.

<Frobenius reciprocity> is the adjointness of <character> induction and restriction: for <class functions> $f$ on $H$ and $u$ on $G$,
$$
\boxed{\langle f^G,u\rangle_G=\langle f,u_H\rangle_H}.
$$
For <characters> this says that the multiplicity of an irreducible $G$-character in an <induced character> equals the corresponding multiplicity after restriction. To prove the identity directly, insert the induced <class function> formula and interchange the two finite sums. For each $x$, substitute $g=xhx^{-1}$ with $h\in H$. Since $u$ is constant on <conjugacy classes>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle f^G,u\rangle_G
&=\frac1{|G||H|}\sum_{x\in G}\sum_{h\in H}f(h)\overline{u(xhx^{-1})}\\
&=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}f(h)\overline{u(h)}
=\langle f,u_H\rangle_H.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves the theorem for all <class functions>, not just <irreducible characters>.

For the group-theoretic conclusion, suppose that $p$ divides $|G'\cap Z(G)|$. A central element of order $p$ lies in every <Sylow p-subgroup>: adjoining it to a <Sylow subgroup> gives a p-subgroup, so maximality forces it into that <subgroup>. Hence there is a <subgroup> $Q\leq P\cap G'\cap Z(G)$ of order $p$. Choose a nontrivial <linear character> $\lambda$ of $Q$.

Since $P$ is abelian, $\lambda$ extends to a <linear character> $\mu$ of $P$. One direct justification is to extend from an abelian <subgroup> $A$ to $\langle A,x\rangle$ one generator at a time: if $d$ is the least positive exponent with $x^d\in A$, choose a complex $d$th root of $\lambda(x^d)$ as the value at $x$. The formula on $ax^j$ is then well defined and multiplicative. Iteration yields the claimed extension to $P$.

The <induced character> $\mu^G$ has degree $[G:P]$, which is prime to $p$. In its decomposition into <irreducible characters>, at least one constituent $\chi$ must have degree prime to $p$; otherwise the total degree would be divisible by $p$. <Frobenius reciprocity> also gives $\langle\chi_P,\mu\rangle_P>0$ for this constituent. Restriction to the central <subgroup> $Q$ forces
$$
\chi_Q=\chi(1)\lambda.
$$
Indeed, the <induced representation> itself restricts to $Q$ as copies of $\lambda$, because conjugation by every coset representative is trivial on $Q$; alternatively this follows from <Schur's lemma> and the positive multiplicity after restriction.

Each $q\in Q$ consequently acts in the <representation> affording $\chi$ as the scalar $\lambda(q)$. Taking its <determinant character> gives
$$
(\det\chi)_Q=\lambda^{\chi(1)}.
$$
The left side is trivial since $Q\leq G'$ and every <linear character> kills $G'$. The right side is nontrivial since $\lambda$ has order $p$ and $p\nmid\chi(1)$. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{p\nmid|G'\cap Z(G)|}.
$$
The argument is the <central commutator has no torsion from an abelian Sylow subgroup> determinant obstruction.
