= Solution
{parent=a}

Treat each column as the payoff from one unit of initial investment, so the cost of holdings $h=(h_1,h_2,h_3)^t$ is $h_1+h_2+h_3$. The first asset weakly dominates the third at the same price, and so does the second. Long the first and short the third gives zero initial cost and terminal payoff $(0,0,2)^t$. Long the second and short the third gives payoff $(2,0,0)^t$, also at zero cost. These are <arbitrages> provided the displayed states have positive <probability>. Combining them gives the zero-cost position $(1,1,-2)^t$ with payoff $(2,0,2)^t$.

More generally, imposing zero cost means $h_3=-h_1-h_2$, and its payoff is $(2h_2,0,2h_1)^t$. Thus every $h_1,h_2\ge0$ with at least one positive gives a <pure-investment arbitrage>. **The market is not arbitrage-free.**

There is nevertheless no <arbitrage> of the other requested kind, with every future net cash flow identically zero. Indeed the middle-state payoff is $2(h_1+h_2+h_3)$, so a zero terminal payoff already forces zero initial cost. The other two states then force $h_1=h_2=0$, hence $h_3=0$. Equivalently, the return matrix has <determinant> $8$ and is invertible. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Zero-cost positive future payoffs exist; a positive receipt now with zero future payoff does not.}}
$$
The unique vector pricing the three unit-investment payoffs is $(0,\tfrac12,0)^t$. It is nonnegative but not strictly positive. This illustrates why <nonnegative state prices need not exclude arbitrage>: strictly positive state prices would assign positive cost to either of the nonzero nonnegative payoffs above.

The current price of one unit of certain future <consumption> is the sum of the <Arrow state prices>, namely $0.9$. A unit-price <risk-free asset> yielding gross return $R$ must satisfy $0.9R=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{10}{9}.}
$$
Normalize the <Arrow state prices> by their sum, or multiply them by $R$, to obtain the <risk-neutral probabilities>:
$$
\boxed{Q=(\tfrac19,\tfrac29,\tfrac13,\tfrac13).}
$$
They are positive and sum to one. Any terminal payoff $Y$ has current price $\sum_sp_sY_s=R^{-1}\mathbb E_QY$. In particular any asset with current price one has $\mathbb E_Q r_i=R$. These pricing probabilities need not equal the physical probabilities governing the investor's <utility>.

The <complete market> makes every state-contingent <consumption> vector attainable. Its budget is $\sum_sp_sc_s=100$, and expected <logarithmic utility> under the equally likely states is $\tfrac14\sum_s\log c_s$, with $c_s>0$. Increasing <utility> makes the budget bind. For a <Lagrange multiplier> $\eta$, the <first-order conditions> are
$$
\frac1{4c_s}=\eta p_s,\qquad c_s=\frac1{4\eta p_s}.
$$
Substitution in the budget gives $1/\eta=100$, so $\eta=0.01$ and
$$
\boxed{c=(250,\ 125,\ \tfrac{250}{3},\ \tfrac{250}{3})^t.}
$$
Every state receives an expenditure $p_sc_s=25$, rather than equal <consumption>. <Strict concavity> of <logarithmic utility> makes this feasible stationary point the unique global maximizer. The physical probabilities enter the <utility> objective; the supporting prices enter the budget, and they must not be interchanged.

Form a <portfolio> with weights $\beta$ in the first factor-exposure asset, $\gamma$ in the second, and $1-\beta-\gamma$ in the <risk-free asset>. Its total initial investment is one. Its two factor exposures match those of asset $k$, so subtracting its return from $r_k$ leaves a constant. Any nonzero constant difference would allow a zero-cost long-short position with a strictly positive certain payoff after choosing its direction, an <arbitrage>. Under absence of <arbitrage>, the returns must therefore have the same <expectation>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E r_k=1.05+0.05\beta+0.10\gamma.}
$$
The coefficients are the two factor <risk premiums> relative to the risk-free return, not the expected factor levels themselves. This reasoning permits negative weights, as assumed in the unconstrained <arbitrage pricing theory> model.

Let $M$ denote the displayed matrix of excess-return coefficients. If it were invertible, choose $v$ with $Mv=(1,0,0)^t$. Hold $v_i$ units of the three risky investments and short $\sum_iv_i$ units of the <risk-free asset>. Initial net cost is zero. The terminal payoff is
$$
\sum_iv_i(r_i-R)=\sum_iv_i(a_i-R)+\left(\sum_iv_ib_i\right)f_1+
\left(\sum_iv_ic_i\right)f_2=1.
$$
This is a guaranteed <arbitrage>, contradicting the assumption. Hence
$$
\boxed{\det M=0.}
$$
The contradiction does not require guessing probabilities for the factors: invertibility would allow simultaneous cancellation of both random exposures while keeping a nonzero constant gain.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

When the two factor-exposure rows are <independent>, every three-column excess-coefficient matrix being singular means that the entire coefficient matrix has <rank> at most two. The constant excess-return row must therefore be in the span of the two exposure rows. There are common constants $\theta_1,\theta_2$ such that
$$
a_i-R=\theta_1b_i+\theta_2c_i\quad\text{for every }i.
$$
To see that the constants are common, fix two assets with <independent> exposure columns, solve their two equations for $\theta_1,\theta_2$, and apply the zero <determinant> with any third asset; its constant entry is forced to satisfy the same equation. Taking <expectations> gives the exact two-factor <arbitrage pricing theory> formula
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E r_i=R+b_i\lambda_1+c_i\lambda_2,\qquad
\lambda_1=\theta_1+\mathbb E f_1,\quad\lambda_2=\theta_2+\mathbb E f_2.}
$$
An asset with unit loading on one factor and zero loading on the other has excess <expected return> equal to that factor's premium. This recovers part (i).

The <rank> qualification matters: singularity by itself would not identify two premiums if both exposure rows were dependent. More generally, write the exposure matrix as $B$. A position $v\in\ker B$ has a constant excess payoff $(a-R\mathbf1)^tv$. Absence of <arbitrage> forces this constant to vanish, so $a-R\mathbf1$ annihilates $\ker B$ and belongs to the <row space> of $B$. This proves <exact factor pricing without idiosyncratic risk> also in a reduced-rank model, though factor premiums need not then be unique. Independence of the random factors is not a substitute for their being spanned by traded exposures.

Assume the <covariance matrix> $\Sigma$ is positive definite and the excess-mean vector $d=z-R\mathbf1$ is nonzero. Put $A=d^t\Sigma^{-1}d>0$. Substituting $w=\lambda\Sigma^{-1}d$ in the mean constraint gives $\lambda A=\mu-R$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\mu-R}{d^t\Sigma^{-1}d}.}
$$
These weights are indeed the minimum-variance solution, not just a stationary point. Any other feasible vector is $w+v$ with $d^tv=0$. Its <variance> is $w^t\Sigma w+2\lambda d^tv+v^t\Sigma v=w^t\Sigma w+v^t\Sigma v$, which is strictly larger unless $v=0$. If $d=0$, only mean $R$ is feasible, and the minimum-risk <portfolio> invests entirely in the <risk-free asset>; the multiplier formula with zero denominator is not applicable.

Using $A$ from part (a), the <portfolio> <variance> is
$$
v=w^t\Sigma w=\lambda^2A=\frac{(\mu-R)^2}{A}.
$$
Thus the minimum-variance correspondence for fixed means has the two branches $\mu=R\pm\sqrt{Av}$. The lower branch is dominated by the upper one with the same <variance>, and <portfolios> with mean below $R$ are also dominated by the risk-free position. Consequently the actual <mean-variance efficient frontier> is the upper <capital market line>:
$$
\boxed{\mu=R+\sqrt{A}\,\sqrt v,\qquad v\ge0.}
$$
Its slope in standard-deviation/mean coordinates is the maximal <Sharpe ratio> $\sqrt A$. In <variance>/mean coordinates it is the upper branch of $v=(\mu-R)^2/A$.

The risk-free weight is $1-\mathbf1^tw$, so a zero risk-free weight requires $\mathbf1^tw=1$. Define $H=\mathbf1^t\Sigma^{-1}d$. If $H\ne0$, this condition gives $\lambda=1/H$ and
$$
\boxed{w_T=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}d}{H},\qquad
\mathbb E r_T=R+\frac A H,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(r_T)=\frac A{H^2}.}
$$
These are the requested tangent-portfolio mean and <variance> under the usual condition $H>0$. Then $\lambda>0$, the normalized <portfolio> lies on the upper efficient ray, and its <Sharpe ratio> is $\sqrt A$.

There is a real qualification if the data are allowed to be arbitrary. If $H=0$, the efficient risky direction has zero total risky weight, and no finite multiple makes its risky weights sum to one. If $H<0$, the formal normalized <portfolio> above has negative excess mean and lies on the lower, dominated branch; it is not an efficient tangent <portfolio> as described in the question. The <sign of the normalized tangency portfolio> must therefore be checked, rather than inferred from <positive definiteness> of $\Sigma$ alone.

Use the usual frictionless option model with nonnegative deterministic <interest rates> and no <stock> dividends. Let $B(t,T)\le1$ be the price of a unit <zero-coupon bond>, $C_E$ the European call price, and $C_A$ the American call price. A call payoff dominates $S_T-X$, so no-arbitrage pricing gives $C_E\ge S_t-XB(t,T)$, and $C_A\ge C_E$. If $S_t>X$, then
$$
C_A\ge C_E\ge S_t-XB(t,T)\ge S_t-X.
$$
The last term is the immediate exercise payoff. Waiting retains downside optionality and defers payment of the strike. For positive interest and $X>0$ the inequality is strict while exercise has positive payoff. If $S_t\le X$, exercising for a zero payoff provides no advantage over keeping the right. Thus **early exercise of a non-dividend-paying call is never strictly beneficial** under these assumptions, and $C_A=C_E$. For completeness, discounted European call value is a true <martingale> in the standard pricing model. For any exercise <stopping time> $\tau\le T$, its pointwise dominance of immediate payoff gives $\mathbb E_Q[B(t,\tau)(S_\tau-X)^+\mid\mathcal F_t]\le\mathbb E_Q[B(t,\tau)C_E(\tau)\mid\mathcal F_t]=C_E(t)$. Taking the <supremum> over exercise rules proves $C_A\le C_E$, and waiting to maturity gives the reverse inequality. This proves <no early exercise of a call without dividends>.

The contrast for a put is the interest earned on a strike received sooner. The European put has payoff at most $X$, so $P_E\le XB(t,T)$. When interest is positive and $S_t<X(1-B(t,T))$, immediate exercise gives $X-S_t>XB(t,T)\ge P_E$. Hence committing never to exercise before maturity is worse than an available early-exercise strategy in such a deep-in-the-money state. This does not say that every put should be exercised early, only that a general prohibition is not rational.

The interest-rate hypothesis is essential to the printed claim. If $r<0$ and the <stock> is deterministic, $S_u=S_te^{r(u-t)}$ with no dividends, choose spot sufficiently large that it stays in the money through $T$. The European call price is $S_t-Xe^{-r(T-t)}<S_t-X$, so immediate exercise is better. At zero interest, early exercise can be indifferent in degenerate cases; it is the usual positive-interest setting that makes the non-exercise comparison strict.

Assume the stated cash dividend is the only dividend before maturity and <interest rates> are nonnegative. Before its ex-dividend date, compare exercise now with retaining the option until any later deterministic date $u<t_1$. There is no dividend between these dates, and retaining the option has value at least that of a European call expiring at $u$, namely at least $S_t-XB(t,u)$. This is at least the immediate in-the-money payoff, and is strictly larger with positive interest. Thus exercise earlier in the open pre-dividend interval is not optimal; the only possible dividend-induced early-exercise opportunity is the last cum-dividend instant, conventionally denoted $t_1-$.

At that instant write $S_-=S_{t_1-}$ and $S_+=S_--D$, the ex-dividend price. Immediate in-the-money exercise pays $S_--X$. Continuing across the dividend and retaining the call to maturity gives at least $S_+-XB(t_1,T)$, since no further dividend remains. Therefore continuation minus immediate exercise is bounded below by
$$
S_--D-XB(t_1,T)-(S_--X)=X(1-B(t_1,T))-D.
$$
Consequently the <dividend-date call-exercise criterion> is
$$
\boxed{D<X(1-B(t_1,T))\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{no beneficial early exercise before }T.}
$$
If the pre-dividend call is out of the money, exercising gives no benefit in the first place. After the dividend, part (a) excludes later early exercise. The comparison is a sufficient condition: failing it does not prove exercise is optimal, because continuation also includes put/time value not present in this lower bound.

The date convention matters. A dividend can make exercise just before the ex-dividend date optimal. Thus a literal assertion excluding $t_1-$ under all dividends would be false; the pre-dividend non-exercise argument excludes earlier dates, leaving this last cum-dividend opportunity to be tested. Further dividends would require separate tests at their own dates.

= Solution

For European options with the same strike and maturity, the terminal payoff identity is
$$
(S_T-X)^+-(X-S_T)^+=S_T-X.
$$
Thus long the call and short the put has the same terminal payoff as one <stock> less $X$ units of the maturity-$T$ <zero-coupon bond>. With no dividends, the <stock> strategy has no intervening payments. The <law of one price> gives <put-call parity>:
$$
\boxed{C_E(t)-P_E(t)=S_t-XB(t,T).}
$$
Equivalently the two nonnegative terminal-payoff <portfolios>, call plus strike bond and put plus <stock>, agree in every state. The parity proof does not require lognormal <stock> prices or positive interest.

For <American options> the exercise times can differ, so equality of their maturity payoffs is not an identity between their full rights. Under the nonnegative-interest, non-dividend assumptions of part (a), $C_A=C_E$ while $P_A=P_E+\Pi_P$ with early-exercise premium $\Pi_P\ge0$. Hence
$$
C_A-P_A=S_t-XB(t,T)-\Pi_P,
$$
which generally differs from European parity. For a concrete limiting state, if the <stock> is zero and remains zero and interest is positive, then $C_A=C_E=0$, $P_A=X$ and $P_E=XB(t,T)<X$. **European parity is not a general American-option equality.** It holds in a particular case only when the relevant early-exercise premiums cancel, or both vanish.

The two binary terminal payoffs are complementary:
$$
\mathbf1_{\{S_T\ge X\}}+\mathbf1_{\{S_T<X\}}=1
$$
in every state, including $S_T=X$. Long one of each is therefore exactly a unit <zero-coupon bond>. The <law of one price> gives <digital put-call parity>:
$$
\boxed{C_t^{\rm bin}+P_t^{\rm bin}=B(t,T).}
$$
With deterministic interest, the two separate values are $B(t,T)Q(S_T\ge X\mid\mathcal F_t)$ and $B(t,T)Q(S_T<X\mid\mathcal F_t)$. There is no need to assume a continuous terminal price distribution: the strict inequality for the put and weak inequality for the call allocate any atom at the strike exactly once.

Take $r>0$, $\sigma>0$ and proportional <continuous dividend yield> $\delta\ge0$, the usual perpetual-option setting. Write $L$ for the prescribed downward exercise trigger, with $0<L<X$. Above the trigger there is no dependence on calendar time, so the <Black-Scholes equation with continuous stock dividends> reduces to
$$
\frac12\sigma^2S^2V''+(r-\delta)SV'-rV=0.
$$
Substitute $V=S^\beta$. The characteristic equation and its roots are
$$
F(\beta)=\frac12\sigma^2\beta(\beta-1)+(r-\delta)\beta-r=0,
\qquad \beta_\pm=\frac{-(r-\delta-\sigma^2/2)\pm\Delta}{\sigma^2},
\quad \Delta=\sqrt{(r-\delta-\sigma^2/2)^2+2r\sigma^2}.
$$
Since the root product is $-2r/\sigma^2<0$, $\beta_-<0<\beta_+$. The <boundary condition> $V(S)\to0$ as $S\to\infty$ excludes the positive-power solution. Value matching at the trigger requires $V(L)=X-L$, giving the fixed-rule price
$$
\boxed{V_L(S)=\begin{cases}
X-S,&0<S\le L,\\
(X-L)(S/L)^{\beta_-},&S>L.
\end{cases}}
$$
The lower branch means exercise immediately if the starting price is already below the trigger. Above it, the formula also equals $\mathbb E_Q[e^{-r\tau_L}(X-L)\mathbf1_{\{\tau_L<\infty\}}]$, where $\tau_L$ is the first passage to $L$. To verify this <expectation>, apply <Itô formula> to the bounded stopped discounted solution on $S\ge L$; the generator equation makes it a <martingale> before the hit, and the residual term on surviving paths vanishes as the time horizon tends to infinity because $r>0$. A fixed trigger is a specified exercise rule, not yet the optimized American price.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a starting price above the trigger, maximizing $V_L(S)$ amounts to maximizing $g(L)=(X-L)L^{-\beta_-}$ on $0<L<X$. Its derivative is
$$
g'(L)=L^{-\beta_--1}\bigl[(\beta_--1)L-\beta_-X\bigr].
$$
Because $\beta_-<0$, this is positive below $\beta_-X/(\beta_--1)$ and negative above it. Both endpoint limits of $g$ are zero. Thus the unique maximizing trigger is
$$
\boxed{S^*=\frac{\beta_-}{\beta_--1}X,\qquad 0<S^*<X.}
$$
Equivalently <smooth fit> requires $\beta_-(X-S^*)/S^*=-1$, matching the derivative of $X-S$ below the boundary. The optimized <perpetual put option> value is $X-S$ for $S\le S^*$ and $(X-S^*)(S/S^*)^{\beta_-}$ above it.

We can verify optimality among all stopping rules, not only constant triggers. The continuation branch is convex, with value and slope matching $X-S$ at $S^*$, so it dominates $X-S$ above the boundary; it is also positive, and hence dominates $(X-S)^+$ everywhere. Its discounted generator vanishes in continuation. In the exercise region, the generator of $X-S$ is $\delta S-rX$. The root equation gives
$$
\delta S^*/X=r+\tfrac12\sigma^2\beta_-<r,
$$
so for $\delta\ge0$ this generator is nonpositive throughout $S\le S^*$. <Smooth fit> eliminates a local-time contribution at the boundary. <Itô formula> and localization therefore make $e^{-rt}V(S_t)$ a nonnegative <supermartingale>, providing an upper bound for every discounted exercise payoff. The threshold strategy attains that bound by the <martingale> calculation in part (a). This proves the boundary is globally optimal.

The positive-interest assumption is substantive. If $r=0$ and $\delta\ge0$, the <stock> tends to zero almost surely, so the <supremum> of perpetual put payoffs has value $X$, approached by triggers $L\downarrow0$. No positive finite trigger attains that <supremum> for positive spot. The displayed finite-boundary formula belongs to the $r>0$ case. If $r<0$ and $\delta\ge0$, the <stock> still tends to zero, while the <discount factor> grows without bound. Even exercise at deterministic times tending to infinity makes the expected discounted put payoff unbounded, so there is no finite perpetual price in that extension.

Differentiate the negative-root equation $F(\beta_-,\delta)=0$, keeping $r$ and $\sigma$ fixed. Its derivatives are $F_\delta=-\beta_-$ and
$$
F_\beta=\sigma^2\beta_-+r-\delta-\sigma^2/2=-\Delta<0.
$$
Thus $d\beta_-/d\delta=\beta_-/F_\beta=-\beta_-/\Delta>0$. Differentiating the trigger ratio then gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dS^*}{d\delta}=-\frac{X}{(\beta_--1)^2}\frac{d\beta_-}{d\delta}
=\frac{X\beta_-}{\Delta(\beta_--1)^2}<0.}
$$
This is the <dividend-yield sensitivity of the perpetual put trigger>. A larger dividend yield lowers the risk-neutral drift of the ex-dividend <stock>; waiting for further price reductions becomes more attractive to the put holder, so exercise occurs at a lower price. As checks, at $\delta=0$ the negative root is $-2r/\sigma^2$, giving $S^*=2rX/(2r+\sigma^2)$. As $\delta\to\infty$, $\beta_-\to0-$ and $S^*\to0$.

A <credit derivative> separates an exposure to a borrower's credit quality from outright ownership of its loan or bond. In the early-2002 setting, these were a growing, largely dealer-intermediated over-the-counter market. For scale, the contemporaneous BIS comparison reports ISDA member positions of about US$919 billion at end-2001, versus US$632 billion at mid-2001. These are notional amounts, not market values or expected losses, and the survey did not eliminate double-counting; estimates from other surveys cannot simply be combined because their coverage differed. This historical description uses the \a[https://www.bis.org/publ/r_qt0206d.pdf][BIS Quarterly Review, June 2002, derivatives-market comparison], rather than projecting later market totals back into the paper's date.

The basic unfunded contract is a <credit default swap>. The protection buyer pays a periodic spread until maturity or a defined credit event; the seller pays the contracted <default> loss, often notional times one minus recovery. Physical settlement instead delivers eligible defaulted debt in exchange for par. A <total return swap> exchanges the reference asset's coupons and price changes against a funding leg: its receiver earns gains, bears price losses and pays funding, obtaining broader market and credit exposure than <default> protection alone. A <credit spread option> pays on widening or narrowing of a quoted <credit spread>, transferring mark-to-market <credit risk> even without a <default>. A <credit-linked note> is funded: the investor supplies principal and accepts a reduction of coupons or principal after a reference credit event, in return for enhanced yield. The investor is economically selling protection and can also face the note issuer's own <credit risk>.

Basket products extend these ideas. A <first-to-default swap> pays when the first member of a reference basket defaults. A <synthetic CDO> uses <credit default swaps> to create <portfolio> credit exposure and allocates aggregate losses among tranches. For monetary <portfolio> loss $L$ and attachment/exhaustion amounts $A<D$, the <credit tranche loss function> is $\min((L-A)^+,D-A)$. These contracts require a model of joint defaults and recoveries, not just a list of individual <default> probabilities. The separation of credit exposure from financing and the role of synthetic structures were already central to the \a[https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/understanding-credit-derivatives-and-their-potential-to-synthesize-riskless-assets.htm][Federal Reserve's December 2001 discussion of credit derivatives].

For risk management, a bank can buy protection on a concentrated borrower exposure while retaining the loan and customer relationship. It can transfer sectors or names it already holds heavily and diversify the remaining <portfolio>. Recognition of a hedge for capital purposes depends on the actual transfer and applicable rules, not merely on the instrument's name. Investors can instead sell protection or buy <credit-linked notes> to obtain chosen credit exposure without buying the cash bond, seek a <risk premium>, or take relative-value positions between cash bonds and swaps. Such positions still depend on funding and on the credit-spread basis: contractual events, maturity, currency and recovery terms may fail to match the original exposure. Protection also replaces part of the reference risk with <counterparty credit risk>, particularly if the seller may fail when the protected borrower is distressed.

Pricing equates expected discounted contractual cash flows under a <risk-neutral measure>, calibrated to traded bonds and derivatives rather than substituting historical <default> frequencies. To make this concrete, suppose the risk-neutral <default intensity> $\lambda(u)$ and <discount factor> $B(0,u)$ are deterministic, fractional recovery $\mathcal R$ is fixed, and premiums are continuous. Survival is $G(u)=\exp(-\int_0^u\lambda(v)dv)$. A notional-$N$ protection leg pays $N(1-\mathcal R)$ at <default>, so its value is $N(1-\mathcal R)\int_0^T B(0,u)\lambda(u)G(u)du$. Premium at annual rate $s$ has value $Ns\int_0^T B(0,u)G(u)du$. A zero-upfront par swap therefore has the <continuous-premium credit-default swap spread>
$$
\boxed{s=(1-\mathcal R)\frac{\int_0^T B(0,u)\lambda(u)G(u)du}{\int_0^T B(0,u)G(u)du}.}
$$
For constant intensity this reduces exactly to $s=(1-\mathcal R)\lambda$ in this simplified model. Actual discrete premium dates and premium accrued at <default> change the premium leg; stochastic recovery, discounting and counterparty losses change the <expectations>. A quoted spread thus cannot universally be read as a physical <default> <probability>. Default-risk premiums and other market effects enter the calibrated pricing measure.

A structural alternative is the <Merton model>. If firm assets at debt maturity are $V_T$ and promised debt is $F$, equity pays $(V_T-F)^+$ and debt pays $\min(V_T,F)=F-(F-V_T)^+$. Under the model's diffusion assumptions, debt value is consequently the discounted face value less a put on firm assets. This derives credit prices from asset value, volatility and capital structure, while the simple model allows <default> only at the debt maturity. Intensity models instead calibrate a default-arrival law directly to market credit prices. Neither approach removes model risk in recovery, dependence or calibration, and incomplete credit markets need not select a unique pricing measure. **A useful credit hedge and a fair derivative price both depend on the actual cash-flow and <default> specifications, not the product label alone.**
