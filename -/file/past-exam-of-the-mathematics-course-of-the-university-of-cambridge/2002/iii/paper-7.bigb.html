Use $\sigma_A(x)$ and $\rho_A(x)$ for <Banach algebra spectrum> and <algebra resolvent set>. An inverse in the smaller <unital algebra> is also an inverse in the larger one, so $\rho_B(x)\subseteq\rho_A(x)$.

If $\lambda\in\rho_B(x)$ and $r=(\lambda1-x)^{-1}\in B$, then for $|\mu-\lambda|\|r\|<1$ the <Neumann series> gives
$$
(\mu1-x)^{-1}=(1+(\mu-\lambda)r)^{-1}r\in B.
$$
Thus $\rho_B(x)$ is <open>. To prove relative closedness, suppose $\lambda_n\in\rho_B(x)$ and $\lambda_n\to\lambda\in\rho_A(x)$. <Continuity> of inversion in $A$ gives $(\lambda_n1-x)^{-1}\to(\lambda1-x)^{-1}$. Since $B$ is <norm> <closed>, the limiting inverse belongs to $B$, so $\lambda\in\rho_B(x)$. Consequently **$\rho_B(x)$ is relatively clopen in $\rho_A(x)$**.

= Solution

A <connected component> $U$ of $\rho_A(x)$ meets the relatively clopen subset $\rho_B(x)$ either everywhere or nowhere: its intersection and its complement would otherwise disconnect $U$. If it meets nowhere, $U\subseteq\sigma_B(x)$. If it meets everywhere, $U$ is a <connected> subset of $\rho_B(x)$, and any larger <connected> subset of $\rho_B(x)$ would be a <connected> subset of $\rho_A(x)$ larger than its component $U$. Thus **$U$ is either a whole component of $\rho_B(x)$ or lies wholly in $\sigma_B(x)$**.

For $|\lambda|>\|x\|$, the <Neumann series>
$$
(\lambda1-x)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{-n-1}x^n
$$
converges in $B$. The exterior of this disk is <connected> and belongs to both <algebra resolvent sets>. Since each <Banach algebra spectrum> is <compact>, each <algebra resolvent set> has exactly one unbounded component: any unbounded component meets that exterior, and two components meeting a common <connected> exterior must coincide. Part (ii) says that the component of $\rho_A(x)$ meeting the exterior is also a whole component of $\rho_B(x)$. Hence **the unbounded components are identical**.

Now $B=\overline{\mathbb C[1,x]}$. Let $U$ be a bounded component of $\rho_A(x)$ and $\lambda\in U$. Its <boundary> lies in $\sigma_A(x)$: an <open> disk about a <boundary> point in the <algebra resolvent set> would meet $U$ and would belong to the same component, contradicting <boundary> membership. The <maximum modulus principle> on the bounded domain $U$ therefore gives, for every complex <polynomial> $p$,
$$
|p(\lambda)|\le\max_{\partial U}|p|
\le\max_{\sigma_A(x)}|p|=r_A(p(x))\le\|p(x)\|.
$$
Here <spectral mapping theorem> follows by factoring $p(z)-\mu$: its commuting factors are invertible exactly when every root avoids the <Banach algebra spectrum>.

The rule $p(x)\mapsto p(\lambda)$ is well-defined by the displayed bound, multiplicative and bounded on the dense <polynomial> <subalgebra>. It extends to a <unital> <character> $\varphi_\lambda$ on $B$. Since $\varphi_\lambda(\lambda1-x)=0$, that element cannot have an inverse in $B$. Thus **$U\subseteq\sigma_B(x)$**. Together with part (iii), this proves that the <Banach algebra spectrum> of a polynomial-generated Banach <subalgebra> is exactly the original <Banach algebra spectrum> with all its bounded complementary components filled in.

Give $\mathcal O(U)$ the <compact-open topology>. Choose a finite polygonal cycle $\Gamma$ in $U\setminus\sigma_A(x)$ whose <winding number> is one on the <Banach algebra spectrum> and zero outside $U$. One can take the oriented <boundary> of a finite union of small squares surrounding the <compact> <Banach algebra spectrum> and contained in $U$. Define
$$
\Theta_x(f)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma f(z)(z1-x)^{-1}\,dz.
$$
The integral is an algebra-norm integral of a <continuous> function on finitely many segments. Cauchy's theorem, applied after any <bounded linear functional> on $A$, makes it independent of the cycle. Moreover
$$
\|\Theta_x(f)\|\le C_\Gamma\sup_{z\in\Gamma}|f(z)|,\qquad
C_\Gamma=\frac{\operatorname{length}(\Gamma)}{2\pi}\max_\Gamma\|(z1-x)^{-1}\|,
$$
so it is <continuous> for the <compact-open topology>.

Here is a direct verification of the algebra properties and uniqueness using the allowed <Runge theorem>. The version required is that every <holomorphic function> on an <open> plane <set> is a locally uniform limit of <rational functions> with poles outside that <set>, allowing a <polynomial> part. A <rational function> $r=p/q$ of this kind has the unambiguous value $r(x)=p(x)q(x)^{-1}$, since the roots of $q$ avoid the <Banach algebra spectrum>. These rational values form a <unital algebra> <homomorphism>.

They agree with the <contour integral>. First $\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma(z1-x)^{-1}\,dz=1$, by replacing the cycle with a large circle and integrating its Neumann expansion. The identity $z^j1-x^j=(z1-x)\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}z^{j-1-k}x^k$ then proves the assertion for <polynomials>. For $a\notin U$, the <resolvent identity> gives
$$
\frac{(z1-x)^{-1}}{z-a}
=(x-a1)^{-1}\left((z1-x)^{-1}-\frac1{z-a}1\right).
$$
The cycle has <winding number> zero at $a$, so integration gives $(x-a1)^{-1}$. This identity holds on a neighborhood of $a$ disjoint from the cycle and the <Banach algebra spectrum>. Differentiating with respect to $a$ gives the analogous result for every power $(z-a)^{-k}$, and partial fractions give it for all the <rational functions> under consideration.

If $r_n\to f$ and $s_n\to g$ locally uniformly, the contour estimate makes their evaluated sequences converge. Since $r_ns_n\to fg$ locally uniformly and multiplication in $A$ is <continuous>, their limits satisfy $\Theta_x(fg)=\Theta_x(f)\Theta_x(g)$. Also $\Theta_x(1)=1$ and $\Theta_x(Z)=x$. Conversely any <continuous> <unital> complex-algebra <homomorphism> sending $Z$ to $x$ must send $Z-a$ to $x-a1$ and its reciprocal to the inverse. It therefore agrees on <rational functions> and then, by Runge density and <continuity>, on all of $\mathcal O(U)$. This proves **existence and uniqueness of the <holomorphic functional calculus>**.

For its spectral mapping property, first note compatibility on a smaller <open> spectral neighborhood $W\subseteq U$: composing restriction to $W$ with the calculus there gives the calculus on $U$ by uniqueness. If $\mu\notin g(\sigma_A(x))$, choose such a $W$ where $\mu-g$ never vanishes. Then
$$
(\mu1-\Theta_x(g))^{-1}=\Theta_x\bigl((\mu-g)^{-1}\bigr),
$$
with the right side evaluated on $W$. Conversely, for $\lambda\in\sigma_A(x)$, write $g(z)-g(\lambda)=(z-\lambda)q(z)$ with $q$ <holomorphic> on $U$. The evaluated factors commute. If their product were invertible, both factors would be invertible, contradicting $\lambda\in\sigma_A(x)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sigma_A(\Theta_x(g))=g(\sigma_A(x))\subseteq g(U)\subseteq V.}
$$
Pullback $h\mapsto h\circ g$ is <continuous> from $\mathcal O(V)$ to $\mathcal O(U)$ because the image under $g$ of a <compact> <set> is <compact>. The composite $h\mapsto\Theta_x(h\circ g)$ is a <continuous> <unital> <homomorphism> taking the coordinate function to $y=\Theta_x(g)$. Uniqueness of the calculus at $y$ proves
$$
\boxed{\Theta_y(h)=\Theta_x(h\circ g).}
$$
For the exponential conclusion, let $K=\sigma_A(x)$. There is a simple polygonal arc from zero through its unbounded complementary component to outside a disk containing $K$, continued by a ray to infinity and avoiding $K$. Loops in an initial polygonal path can be removed to make the arc simple. The complement of this slit is a <simply connected> <open> neighborhood of $K$ avoiding zero, and hence admits a <holomorphic logarithm> $\ell$. <Set> $y=\Theta_x(\ell)$. The composition rule gives
$$
\boxed{e^y=\Theta_x(e^\ell)=\Theta_x(Z)=x.}
$$
The calculus of the entire exponential agrees with the usual algebra exponential because its power-series partial sums converge locally uniformly. Finally an invertible <matrix> over the <complex numbers> has a finite <Banach algebra spectrum> avoiding zero. Removing finitely many plane points leaves a <connected> <set>, so the hypothesis always holds: **every invertible <matrix> over the <complex numbers> is an exponential of a <matrix> over the <complex numbers>**.

A <C-star algebra> is a complex <Banach algebra> with a conjugate-linear involution satisfying $(ab)^*=b^*a^*$ and $\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2$. The inequality $\|a\|^2\le\|a^*\|\|a\|$, and then the same inequality for $a^*$, show that the involution is <isometric>. In a nonzero <unital> <C-star algebra>, the identity has <norm> one.

For a <Hermitian algebra element> $h$, the exponential $e^{ith}$ is unitary for real $t$ and has <norm> one. If $\lambda\in\sigma(h)$, then $e^{it\lambda}\in\sigma(e^{ith})$: factoring $e^{itz}-e^{it\lambda}=(z-\lambda)q(z)$ with entire $q$ proves this, since the evaluated factors commute. Hence $|e^{it\lambda}|\le1$ for all positive and negative $t$, forcing $\operatorname{Im}\lambda=0$. Thus **<Hermitian algebra elements> have real <Banach algebra spectrum>**.

For a <normal C-star element> $a$, commutation of $a,a^*$ gives
$$
\|a^2\|^2=\|(a^*)^2a^2\|=\|(a^*a)^2\|=\|a^*a\|^2=\|a\|^4.
$$
The middle equality of <norms> uses the <C-star identity> on the <Hermitian algebra element> $a^*a$. Iterating along powers $2^n$ and applying the <spectral radius formula> proves $r(a)=\|a\|$.

Now suppose $A$ is commutative and <unital>. Every <maximal ideal> is <closed>: if it were dense, it would contain an element within distance less than one of the identity, hence an invertible element by the <Neumann series>, which is impossible for a proper ideal. Its quotient is a complex Banach division algebra and is therefore $\mathbb C$ by the <Gelfand-Mazur theorem>. <Maximal ideals> thus give <characters>. Conversely, every <character> $\varphi$ satisfies $\varphi(a)\in\sigma_A(a)$, because applying it to an inverse of $a-\varphi(a)1$ would give a contradiction. <Characters> are consequently contractive. The converse spectral inclusion follows by placing the proper ideal generated by $a-\lambda1$ in a <maximal ideal>. We have proved
$$
\sigma_A(a)=\{\varphi(a):\varphi\in\Phi_A\}.
$$
The <character space> $\Phi_A$ is a weak-star <closed> subset of the dual unit ball, characterized by $\varphi(1)=1$ and $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> makes it <compact> Hausdorff. Define the <Gelfand transform> by $\widehat a(\varphi)=\varphi(a)$. For Hermitian $h$, its values are real; writing $a=h+ik$ with both terms Hermitian shows $\widehat{a^*}=\overline{\widehat a}$. In the commutative algebra every element is normal, so
$$
\|\widehat a\|_\infty=r(a)=\|a\|.
$$
The transform is therefore an <isometric> <unital> star-homomorphism and has <closed> range in $C(\Phi_A)$. Its range separates <characters> by their definition and is <closed> under complex conjugation. The complex <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> makes that range dense, hence equal to $C(\Phi_A)$. This proves the <commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem>:
$$
\boxed{A\cong C(\Phi_A)\quad\text{isometrically as a star algebra}.}
$$
For a nonunital <C-star algebra>, its <C-star unitization> can be constructed without assuming an operator representation on a <Hilbert space>. Represent $A$ by left multiplication on itself, which is <isometric> because testing $L_a$ on $a^*/\|a\|$ gives $\|L_a\|=\|a\|$. Adjoin the identity operator and <set> $\|z\|=\|L_z\|$. For $z=a+\lambda1$ and $b\in A$, the <C-star identity> in $A$ gives $\|zb\|^2=\|b^*z^*zb\|\le\|b\|\|L_{z^*z}b\|$. Consequently $\|L_z\|^2\le\|L_{z^*z}\|\le\|L_{z^*}\|\|L_z\|$. Applying this also to $z^*$ proves that the new involution is <isometric> and that its <C-star identity> holds. The extension by the identity is complete, since the <isometric> image of $A$ is <closed> and one adjoins just one dimension. Apply the <unital> result to a commutative $A^+$; the <character> $\infty$ given by $a+\lambda1\mapsto\lambda$ has kernel $A$. Under the isomorphism, this kernel consists of the <continuous> functions vanishing at $\infty$. Thus **$A\cong C_0(\Phi_A)$** in the nonunital case, with locally <compact> <character space> $\Phi_A=\Phi_{A^+}\setminus\{\infty\}$.

For a Hermitian $h$ in a possibly noncommutative algebra, apply this result to the commutative <unital> <C-star subalgebra> $C^*(1,h)$ in the <unitization>. Its <Banach algebra spectrum> at $h$ equals the ambient <Banach algebra spectrum>: both <Banach algebra spectra> are real, the complement of the ambient <compact> real <Banach algebra spectrum> is <connected>, and the resolvent-component result of Question 1 retains that whole complement in the smaller algebra. <Characters> of this <subalgebra> are determined by their values at $h$, so its <character space> is homeomorphic to $\sigma(h)$. We obtain the <continuous functional calculus> $C(\sigma(h))\to C^*(1,h)$.

Interpret $\mathbb R^+$ here as $[0,\infty)$. If $\sigma(h)\subseteq[0,\infty)$, apply this calculus to $t\mapsto\sqrt t$, obtaining a Hermitian $k$ with $k^2=h$. In a nonunital algebra this element remains in $A$ because the defining scalar function vanishes at zero. Conversely, if $h=k^2$ with $k=k^*$, <spectral mapping theorem> and the real <Banach algebra spectrum> of $k$ give $\sigma(h)=\{t^2:t\in\sigma(k)\}\subseteq[0,\infty)$. Hence **nonnegative <Banach algebra spectrum> is equivalent to being the square of a <Hermitian algebra element>**. A strict-positive interpretation would exclude $h=0$, and would not be equivalent to the assertion about an arbitrary Hermitian square.

Finally, use the two <continuous> real functions $t\mapsto\sqrt{\max(t,0)}$ and $t\mapsto\sqrt{\max(-t,0)}$ on $\sigma(x)$. Their calculus values $u,v$ are Hermitian, belong to $A$ also in the nonunital case, and satisfy
$$
\boxed{x=u^2-v^2.}
$$

Put $K=\sigma(T)$ and use the <continuous functional calculus> $\pi:C(K)\to\mathcal B(H)$. It is a <unital> star-homomorphism taking the coordinate function to $T$. This follows from the commutative C-star theorem applied to $C^*(I,T,T^*)$, together with spectral permanence for <C-star subalgebras>, which is among the permitted C-star results.

Take the <Hilbert space> inner product linear in its first variable. For a <vector> $\xi$, let $H_\xi=\overline{\{\pi(f)\xi:f\in C(K)\}}$. This cyclic subspace is invariant under every $\pi(f)$ and its adjoint, so it is reducing. The functional $f\mapsto\langle\pi(f)\xi,\xi\rangle$ is positive because $f\ge0$ implies $\pi(f)=\pi(\sqrt f)^*\pi(\sqrt f)$. It has, by the <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem>, a finite regular positive <Borel measure> $\mu_\xi$ on $K$. The identity
$$
\|\pi(f)\xi\|^2=\int_K|f|^2\,d\mu_\xi
$$
makes $f\mapsto\pi(f)\xi$ an <isometry> on its quotient by the functions of zero $L^2$ <norm>. Density of <continuous> functions in $L^2(\mu_\xi)$ extends it to a unitary $U_\xi:L^2(\mu_\xi)\to H_\xi$. Multiplication by a <continuous> function intertwines with $\pi$ on this subspace.

Choose a maximal family of mutually <orthogonal> nonzero cyclic <reducing subspaces>. Their <orthogonal> sum is all of $H$: otherwise its reducing <orthogonal complement> contains a nonzero <vector> generating another such subspace. This argument does not require separability. For a bounded <Borel measurable function> $g$ on $K$, multiplication $M_g$ on every $L^2(\mu_\xi)$ is bounded with <norm> at most $\|g\|_\infty$. Define on that <orthogonal> sum
$$
\boxed{\beta_T(g)=\bigoplus_\xi U_\xi M_gU_\xi^{-1}.}
$$
<Multiplication operators> satisfy $M_{fg}=M_fM_g$, $M_{\overline g}=M_g^*$ and $M_1=I$. The same identities hold for their <direct sums>, and the <norm> bound is uniform in $\xi$. Thus $\beta_T$ is a norm-decreasing <unital> star-homomorphism on the algebra of actual bounded <Borel measurable functions>, and $\beta_T(Z)=T$ because it extends $\pi$. No quotient by a single unspecified measure is being substituted for that Borel algebra.

Choose the bounded Borel measurable square root $s(0)=0$ and $s(z)=|z|^{1/2}e^{i\operatorname{Arg}(z)/2}$ for $z\ne0$, taking $\operatorname{Arg}\in[0,2\pi)$. Let $S=\beta_T(s)$. Then
$$
\boxed{S^2=T,\qquad S^*S=\beta_T(|s|^2)=SS^*.}
$$
Thus every bounded <normal operator> has a normal square root. The Borel branch is essential in general: on a circle winding about zero a <continuous> square-root branch need not exist.

A <representation of a Banach algebra> is a complex-linear multiplicative map $\pi:A\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(X)$. A nonzero irreducible action of a <unital algebra> automatically has $\pi(1)=I$: the image of the idempotent $\pi(1)$ is a nonzero invariant subspace and hence is all of $X$. An <algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra> is a nonzero action if it has no nonzero proper <invariant submodule>, whether <closed> or not. A <normed representation of a Banach algebra> has $\pi(a)$ bounded for each $a$ on a preassigned <normed vector space> $X$; <continuity> of $\pi$ as an operator-norm-valued map is a further conclusion. This must not be confused with topological irreducibility, which excludes only <closed> <invariant subspaces>. Unitizing the algebra extends a nonunital action by $\pi(a+\lambda1)=\pi(a)+\lambda I$, so it is enough to discuss the <unital> case.

For $\xi\ne0$, irreducibility gives $A\xi=X$. The annihilator $L_\xi=\{a:\pi(a)\xi=0\}$ is a <maximal left ideal>: an intermediate <left ideal> gives an intermediate <submodule> of $X$. It is <closed> by the Neumann-series argument for <maximal left ideals>. Consequently $A/L_\xi$ transports a complete quotient <norm> to $X$, and left multiplication is contractive in this <norm>. Every <algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra> can therefore be realized continuously on a <Banach space>. This alone does not prove <continuity> for its preassigned <norm>.

The kernel of $\pi$ is the intersection of the <closed> <left ideals> $L_\xi$ and is a <closed> <primitive ideal>. The <Jacobson radical> is the intersection of all <primitive ideals>, equivalently the intersection of <maximal left ideals>. Thus these quotient representations detect semisimplicity. Equivalent <algebra representations> are related by a bijective <module homomorphism>.

The algebra of <module endomorphisms> is exactly $\mathbb C I$. Indeed, if a <module endomorphism> $D$ sends a fixed cyclic <vector> $\xi$ to $a\xi$, then $D(b\xi)=ba\xi$. It is induced by bounded right multiplication by $a$ on $A/L_\xi$, so is bounded for the <quotient norm on an irreducible Banach module>. Every nonzero <module endomorphism> is injective and surjective by irreducibility, and its inverse is bounded by the same argument. This <commutant of an operator algebra> is a <closed> division <subalgebra> of the <bounded operators> on this Banach module; the <Gelfand-Mazur theorem> makes it $\mathbb C I$.

The <Jacobson density theorem> now says that for finitely many <linearly independent> $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ and arbitrary $\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n$, one element of $A$ sends all $\xi_j$ to $\eta_j$. Here is the induction behind it. The one-vector assertion is $A\xi_1=X$. Assuming the first $n-1$ coordinates can be prescribed, set $L=\{a:a\xi_j=0\ (j<n)\}$. Its image $L\xi_n$ is an <invariant subspace>. If it is $X$, it adjusts the last image without changing the first ones. If it is zero, the rule $(a\xi_1,\ldots,a\xi_{n-1})\mapsto a\xi_n$ is a well-defined module map $X^{n-1}\to X$. Each coordinate map is scalar by the <module endomorphism> result, forcing $\xi_n$ to be a <linear combination> of the earlier <vectors>, a contradiction. This proves the assertion.

We can now give Johnson's <continuity> argument with its crucial construction. If $X$ is finite dimensional, the <closed> kernel has finite codimension and $A/\ker\pi$ is finite dimensional, so $\pi$ is <continuous>. Suppose instead that $X$ is infinite dimensional. Let
$$
Y=\{\xi\in X:a\mapsto\pi(a)\xi\text{ is continuous}\}.
$$
This is an <invariant submodule>, because the orbit map of $\pi(b)\xi$ is the orbit map of $\xi$ composed with the bounded map $a\mapsto ab$. Thus either $Y=X$ or $Y=0$.

To rule out $Y=0$, choose <linearly independent> unit <vectors> $\xi_0,\xi_1,\ldots$. There are elements $a_n\in A$ such that, with $c_n=a_n\cdots a_1$,
$$
\pi(c_m)\xi_n=0\quad(m>n),\qquad
\{\pi(c_n)\xi_j:j\ge n\}\text{ is linearly independent}.
$$
For completeness, construct them in the canonical quotient-norm Banach module. At stage $n$ the current tail <vectors> are independent. In the <closed> left annihilator of its first <vector>, density permits arbitrary images of any finite list of the remaining <vectors>. The condition that finitely many such images are independent is <open> in this Banach subspace and dense: perturb any element by $t$ times an element prescribing independent images; a suitable determinant is a nonzero <polynomial> in $t$, with only finitely many exceptional values. The <Baire category theorem> intersects these <open> dense conditions for all initial finite tail lists. The resulting $a_n$ kills the first current <vector> and retains independence of the entire remaining tail. This is the <countable annihilation sequence in an irreducible Banach module>.

The <gliding-hump continuity principle> supplies the other ingredient: if $E,F$ are <Banach spaces>, $S:E\to F$ is linear, $T_j:E\to E$ and $U_n:F\to F_n$ are bounded, and $U_nST_1\cdots T_m$ is <continuous> whenever $m>n$, then $U_nST_1\cdots T_n$ is <continuous> eventually. The product form of this principle is recorded in \a[https://msp.org/pjm/1993/159-1/pjm-v159-n1-p08-p.pdf][Proposition 1.3 of Thomas's paper]. Here is the contradiction mechanism. Rescale so $\|T_j\|,\|U_n\|\le1$ and put $P_m=T_1\cdots T_m$. If infinitely many diagonal maps are discontinuous, choose increasing $n_j$ and successively tiny $x_j$ so
$$
\|U_{n_j}SP_{n_j}x_j\|>j+1+\sum_{i<j}\|U_{n_j}SP_{n_i}x_i\|.
$$
Also choose $\|x_j\|\le2^{-j}/(1+\max_{i<j}\|U_{n_i}SP_{n_i+1}\|)$. The convergent sum $x=\sum_jP_{n_j}x_j$ has, after its $j$th term, a tail factoring through $P_{n_j+1}$. Applying that <continuous> composite bounds the tail contribution by one. The display forces $\|U_{n_j}Sx\|>j$, contradicting $\|U_{n_j}Sx\|\le\|Sx\|$. Only finite decompositions and the <continuous> tail composites are used; no unjustified interchange of a discontinuous map with an infinite sum occurs.

Apply this principle with $E=A$, $F=\mathcal B(\overline X)$, where $\overline X$ is the completion in the original <norm>, and $S(a)$ the bounded extension of $\pi(a)$. Set $T_j(a)=aa_j$ and $U_n(R)=R\xi_n$. Then
$$
U_nST_1\cdots T_m(a)=\pi(a)\pi(a_m\cdots a_1)\xi_n.
$$
For $m>n$ this is zero, hence <continuous>. For $m=n$ it is the orbit map of a nonzero <vector>, hence discontinuous if $Y=0$. This contradicts the principle. Therefore $Y=X$.

Finally, apply the <uniform boundedness principle> on the <Banach space> $A$ to the <continuous> maps $a\mapsto\pi(a)\xi$ indexed by $\|\xi\|\le1$. They are pointwise bounded because each individual $\pi(a)$ is bounded in the original <norm>. It gives
$$
\boxed{\|\pi(a)\|\le C\|a\|\quad(a\in A).}
$$
This proves <Johnson's continuity theorem for irreducible normed representations>, including when the original <normed vector space> is incomplete. The quotient <norm>, countable density construction, and gliding hump explain the substantive steps rather than assuming the desired <continuity> in advance.

We first extend the scalar <Hadamard three-circle theorem> to a <holomorphic> Banach-space-valued function $F$. Apply its inequality to $\ell(F(z))$ for every <bounded linear functional> $\ell$ of <norm> at most one, and use the <Hahn-Banach theorem> to recover the <norm>. For the radii $1/R,1,R$ this gives
$$
\|F(1)\|^2\le\sup_{|z|=R}\|F(z)\|\;\sup_{|z|=1/R}\|F(z)\|.
$$
Now take $F(z)=p(z)^{2^n}$. Even for noncommuting coefficients this is an algebra-valued <holomorphic> <polynomial>. Taking the $2^{-n}$th power gives
$$
\|p(1)^{2^n}\|^{2/2^n}\le
\max_{|z|=R}\|p(z)^{2^n}\|^{1/2^n}
\max_{|z|=1/R}\|p(z)^{2^n}\|^{1/2^n}.
$$
On each <boundary> circle the <continuous> functions $f_n(z)=\|p(z)^{2^n}\|^{1/2^n}$ decrease, by submultiplicativity, to $r_A(p(z))$ by the <spectral radius formula>. Their maxima decrease to the <supremum> of this <pointwise limit>. To justify that passage without assuming <continuity> of the <spectral radius>, take maximizing points along a convergent subsequence on the <compact> circle. For any fixed $k$, all later $f_n$ are bounded by $f_k$; <continuity> of $f_k$ therefore bounds the limiting maxima by $f_k$ at the limiting point. Taking the infimum over $k$ gives the desired upper bound, while the reverse bound is immediate.

Passing to the limit proves the <spectral-radius three-circle inequality> at the geometric mean:
$$
\boxed{r_A(p(1))^2\le
\sup_{|z|=R}r_A(p(z))\;\sup_{|z|=1/R}r_A(p(z)).}
$$
For a nonunital algebra, use its <unitization of an algebra>; the <spectral radius> of an original algebra element is unchanged. Zero <boundary> suprema cause no difficulty in the decreasing-limit argument.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

It suffices first to treat <unital algebras> and a <unital> <homomorphism>. A surjective <homomorphism> between <unital algebras> automatically preserves the identity. If $B=0$, <continuity> is immediate. In the nonunital case, extend to the forced <unitizations> by $T^+(a+\lambda1)=T(a)+\lambda1$. This is still surjective and the <unitization> of a semisimple algebra is semisimple: its radical projects to zero in its scalar quotient, and its remaining intersection with the original algebra is its original radical. <Continuity> of the extension implies <continuity> of the original map.

An <algebra homomorphism> decreases the <spectral radius>, even without any <continuity> assumption, because it takes an inverse of $\lambda1-a$ to an inverse of $\lambda1-T(a)$. Suppose $a_n\to0$ in $A$ and $T(a_n)\to b$ in $B$. Fix any $c\in B$ and choose $u,v\in A$ with $T(u)=b$, $T(v)=c$. Put
$$
w=vu,\quad w_n=va_n,\quad d=T(w)=cb,\quad d_n=T(w_n)\longrightarrow d.
$$
Consider the <polynomial> $q_n(z)=T((1-z)w_n+zw)=(1-z)d_n+zd$. Its value at one is exactly $d$. On the outer circle $|z|=R$,
$$
r_B(q_n(z))\le\|q_n(z)\|\le\|d\|+(1+R)\|d_n-d\|.
$$
On the inner circle $|z|=1/R$, spectral-radius decrease gives
$$
r_B(q_n(z))\le r_A((1-z)w_n+zw)
\le(1+1/R)\|w_n\|+\|w\|/R.
$$
Applying part (i) in $B$ and then letting $n\to\infty$, with $R$ fixed, gives
$$
r_B(cb)^2\le\frac{\|cb\|\|vu\|}{R}.
$$
This holds for every $R>1$, so **$r_B(cb)=0$ for every $c\in B$**.

This forces $b$ into the <Jacobson radical>. To see the criterion directly, if $b$ were outside a <maximal left ideal> $L$, then $L+Bb=B$, so $1=l+cb$ for some $l\in L$, $c\in B$. The equality $r_B(cb)=0$ makes $1-cb$ invertible, but this element equals $l\in L$, impossible in a proper <left ideal>. Hence $b$ lies in every <maximal left ideal>. Since $B$ is semisimple, $b=0$.

We have shown that the separating space of $T$ is zero. If $a_n\to a$ and $T(a_n)\to y$, apply the result to $a_n-a$ to obtain $y=T(a)$. Its graph is <closed>, and the <closed graph theorem> now proves
$$
\boxed{T\text{ is continuous}.}
$$
The crucial estimate uses part (i) with an exactly fixed central value; it does not assume the false general assertion that the <spectral radius> is <continuous> under <norm> limits.
