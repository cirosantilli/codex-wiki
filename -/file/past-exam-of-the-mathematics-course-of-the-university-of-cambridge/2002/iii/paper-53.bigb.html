= Solution
{parent=1}

An isotropic <simple fluid> has local <stress> determined by the deformation history of the material element. A <viscometric flow> is one whose relative deformation history is that of steady <simple shear flow>, up to rigid changes of frame. At a given element the <shear rate> is constant along its history, although it can differ between elements. This includes the locally simple-shear kinematics used in standard viscometric measurements.

Use the component-first <velocity gradient> $K_{ij}=\partial v_i/\partial x_j$. The first two <Rivlin-Ericksen tensors> are
$$
A_1=K+K^T,\qquad A_2=D_tA_1+A_1K+K^TA_1.
$$
In the axes of a steady shear $v_x=sy$, the relative <deformation gradient> over a lag $q$ is $F=I+qK$, since $K^2=0$. The material history is determined by $s$, and its tensor symmetries exclude $xz$ and $yz$ <stress> components. The remaining symmetric <stress> consists of an isotropic part, one shear component, and two independent <normal-stress differences>. The relevant matrices are
$$
A_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&s&0\\s&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_1^2=\begin{pmatrix}s^2&0&0\\0&s^2&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&2s^2&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus $I,A_1,A_1^2,A_2$ span the allowed <stress> components. <Material isotropy> and reversal of the shear show that their scalar coefficients depend on $|s|=(A_1:A_1/2)^{1/2}$. Define the <viscometric functions> by
$$
\mu=\frac{\sigma_{xy}}{s},\qquad
\psi_1=\frac{\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy}}{s^2},\qquad
\psi_2=\frac{\sigma_{yy}-\sigma_{zz}}{s^2}.
$$
Solving for the matrix coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma=-pI+\mu A_1+(\psi_1+\psi_2)A_1^2-\frac12\psi_1A_2.}
$$
This is an exact steady-viscometric representation, rather than an assumption that the fluid is everywhere a <second-order fluid>. The <shear viscosity> $\mu$ measures the shear resistance, while $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ measure the first and second <normal-stress differences> generated by shear. Their zero-rate values are understood as limits when these exist. An isotropic <pressure> changes neither normal difference.

For a flow in the <lubrication approximation> with gap $H$, longitudinal scale $\ell\gg H$ and speed $U$, the shear-rate scale is $U/H$. The viscous shear force per unit volume scales as $\mu U/H^2$. Quadratic viscoelastic <normal stresses> scale as $\psi_iU^2/H^2$; their longitudinal gradients, and the comparable mixed-component corrections, contribute forces of scale $\psi_iU^2/(H^2\ell)$. Therefore the <normal-stress smallness in lubrication> criterion is
$$
\boxed{\max_{i=1,2}\frac{|\psi_i|U}{\mu\ell}\ll1.}
$$
Equivalently, the thin-gap aspect ratio must multiply the shear-based elastic parameter to give a small quantity: $(H/\ell)|\psi_i|\dot\gamma/\mu\ll1$. Normal stresses need not themselves be smaller than the <shear stress>; it is their effect on the leading force balance that matters. If the flow changes on an additional short time scale $T$, the $D_tA_1$ term also requires $|\psi_1|/(\mu T)\ll1$ for its neglect. Material coefficients should be evaluated at the characteristic <shear rate>.

Now take positive cylinder rotation, measure $\theta$ counterclockwise from the rightward horizontal, and let $y=r-a$ increase outward through the film. The tangent has upward component $\cos\theta$, so the tangential gravitational acceleration is $-g\cos\theta$. To leading order in $h/a$, neglect inertia and higher-order hydrostatic or capillary pressure gradients, and write $U=\Omega a$. Tangential <force balance> and the shear-free <free surface> give
$$
\frac{\partial\sigma_{\theta y}}{\partial y}=\rho g\cos\theta,\qquad \sigma_{\theta y}(h)=0,
$$
hence
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\theta y}(y)=\rho g\cos\theta\,(y-h),\qquad 0\le y\le h.}
$$
The wall <shear stress> is $-\rho gh\cos\theta$. It opposes rotation on the uphill side and assists it on the downhill side.

For a <power-law fluid>, $\sigma_{\theta y}=k|u_y|^{n-1}u_y$. Its inversion must retain the sign:
$$
u_y=-\operatorname{sgn}(\cos\theta)\left(\frac{\rho g|\cos\theta|}{k}\right)^{1/n}(h-y)^{1/n}.
$$
Integrating with the <no-slip boundary condition> $u(0)=U$ yields
$$
u(y)=U-\operatorname{sgn}(\cos\theta)\frac{n}{n+1}\left(\frac{\rho g|\cos\theta|}{k}\right)^{1/n}\left[h^{(n+1)/n}-(h-y)^{(n+1)/n}\right].
$$
The <volume flux> per unit axial length is $Q=\int_0^h u(y)dy$. The integrated bracket is $[(n+1)/(2n+1)]h^{(2n+1)/n}$, so the <power-law coating on a rotating cylinder> has
$$
\boxed{Q=\Omega ah-\operatorname{sgn}(\cos\theta)\frac{n}{2n+1}\left(\frac{\rho g|\cos\theta|}{k}\right)^{1/n}h^{(2n+1)/n}.}
$$
At $\cos\theta=0$ the gravitational term vanishes by continuity. Steady <mass conservation> makes $Q$ independent of $\theta$.

On the uphill half, put $D=(\rho g\cos\theta/k)^{1/n}$ and $m=(2n+1)/n$. The local flux is $Q(h)=Uh-(D/m)h^m$, with
$$
Q'(h)=U-Dh^{(n+1)/n},\qquad Q''(h)<0\quad(h>0).
$$
It has a unique maximum at
$$
h_*(\theta)=\left(\frac{kU^n}{\rho g\cos\theta}\right)^{1/(n+1)},\qquad
Q_*(\theta)=\frac{n+1}{2n+1}Uh_*(\theta).
$$
A positive coating must carry the same steady flux at every angle. The tightest local bound occurs at $\cos\theta=1$, not on the downhill side, where the flux increases monotonically with thickness. Consequently
$$
\boxed{Q_{\max}=\frac{n+1}{2n+1}\left(\frac{k}{\rho g}\right)^{1/(n+1)}(\Omega a)^{(2n+1)/(n+1)}.}
$$
For $0<Q<Q_{\max}$ the thinner positive root on the uphill side connects through $\cos\theta=0$ to the unique downhill root, giving a periodic leading-order coating profile. At the limiting flux the two uphill roots meet at $\theta=0$; it is the maximum flux of this lubrication model, with a possible limiting corner resolved by neglected higher-order effects. For $n=1$ the check is $Q_{\max}=(2/3)U\sqrt{kU/(\rho g)}$. All these conclusions require the resulting film to remain thin enough for the <lubrication approximation> and for the neglected <normal stresses> and inertia to satisfy their scaling restrictions.

= Solution

A <simple fluid> reduces to a <linear viscoelastic fluid> when its deformation and rotation over the relevant memory interval are small enough to linearize the constitutive history about an isotropic relaxed state. Small amplitude is essential; small frequency is not. For example, arbitrarily high-frequency oscillation can still be linear if its strain amplitude is sufficiently small. The <relaxation modulus> $G(t)$ is the shear-stress response to a unit small step of shear <strain>.

Choose the harmonic convention $e^{i\omega t}$. If the imposed <shear rate> is $\operatorname{Re}(\widehat{\dot\gamma}e^{i\omega t})$, the corresponding <shear stress> is $\operatorname{Re}(\eta^*(\omega)\widehat{\dot\gamma}e^{i\omega t})$. Substitution into the constitutive <convolution> gives the <complex viscosity>:
$$
\boxed{\eta^*(\omega)=\int_0^\infty G(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds.}
$$
For a nondecaying elastic part this formula is understood through the limit from a positive Laplace damping parameter. The complex shear modulus is $G^*=i\omega\eta^*=G'+iG''$, so $G'$ is the <storage modulus>, $G''$ the <loss modulus>, and $\eta^*=G''/\omega-iG'/\omega$ in this convention.

Creep is the progressive deformation produced by a held <stress>. For a small step $S_0H(t)$ of shear stress, define the <creep compliance> $J$ by $\gamma(t)=S_0J(t)$ from a relaxed state. Writing hats for <Laplace transforms>, the same constitutive law gives $\widehat S=p\widehat G\widehat\gamma$. Since $\widehat S=S_0/p$, this yields
$$
\boxed{\widehat J(p)=\frac{1}{p^2\widehat G(p)}.}
$$
Here $H$ is the <Heaviside step function>. Inversion determines the full creep response, including any instantaneous elastic strain jump. Equivalently, the causal relaxation/creep <convolution> includes that jump through $\int_{0^-}^tG(t-s)dJ(s)=1$ for $t>0$.

For the <Maxwell fluid>, $\widehat G(p)=G_0/(p+1/\tau)$. Thus the two material responses are
$$
\eta^*(\omega)=\frac{G_0\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},\qquad
J(t)=\frac1{G_0}+\frac{t}{G_0\tau}\quad(t\ge0).
$$
The creep response is an elastic jump followed by constant-rate viscous deformation. The zero-frequency <shear viscosity> is $\eta=G_0\tau$.

For the wall problem, take $v=u(y,t)e_x$ and denote the shear component of <stress> by $S$. The <incompressibility> constraint is satisfied, and the advective part of the <material derivative> vanishes because no field depends on $x$. With no imposed longitudinal <pressure gradient>, <momentum conservation> and the linear Maxwell law give
$$
\rho u_t=S_y,\qquad S_t+\frac S\tau=G_0u_y.
$$
The initial conditions are $u=S=0$ in $y>0$; the <no-slip boundary condition> is $u(0,t)=UH(t)$, with decay as $y\to\infty$. Transforming these equations gives
$$
\widehat S=\frac{G_0}{p+1/\tau}\widehat u_y,\qquad
\widehat u_{yy}=q(p)^2\widehat u,\qquad
q(p)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho p(1+p\tau)}{G_0\tau}}.
$$
Choose the analytic square-root branch in $\operatorname{Re}p>0$ with $\operatorname{Re}q>0$, so that the spatially increasing solution is excluded. The <Maxwell start-up shear layer> consequently has
$$
\widehat u(y,p)=\frac Up e^{-q(p)y}.
$$
The <Bromwich inversion formula> now gives
$$
\boxed{u(y,t)=\frac{U}{2\pi i}\int_{b-i\infty}^{b+i\infty}\frac1p\exp\left[pt-y\sqrt{\frac{\rho p(1+p\tau)}{G_0\tau}}\right]dp,\qquad b>0.}
$$
The vertical <Bromwich contour> is directed upward and lies to the right of all singularities, including the origin and the branch points at $0$ and $-1/\tau$. A cut joining these branch points is a possible continuation into the left half-plane; inversion only requires the stated right-half-plane branch.

The Newtonian limit holds $\eta=G_0\tau$ fixed while $\tau\to0$, so $G_0\to\infty$. Then $q(p)\to\sqrt{\rho p/\eta}$. With $\nu=\eta/\rho$, the inverse <Laplace transform> gives the <complementary error function> profile
$$
\boxed{u(y,t)=U\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{y}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right).}
$$
This is diffusive momentum penetration in a <Newtonian fluid>, with thickness of order $\sqrt{\nu t}$.

The elastic limit instead holds $G_0$ fixed while $\tau\to\infty$. Write $c=\sqrt{G_0/\rho}$. On the chosen branch, $q(p)\to p/c$, so the <Laplace transform time-shift rule> yields
$$
\boxed{u(y,t)=U H(t-y/c).}
$$
An elastic shear front travels at speed $c$: the material ahead is at rest, while that behind moves at the wall speed. Indeed the limiting displacement is $d(y,t)=U(t-y/c)H(t-y/c)$, whose shear <strain> is $d_y=-(U/c)H(t-y/c)$. A nonzero pointwise velocity gradient at the front is interpreted distributionally, or by smoothing the ideal wall start.

**The use of linear viscoelasticity is conditional, rather than automatically justified for arbitrary $U$.** The <material derivative> simplifying to a time derivative does not remove the nonlinear constitutive effects of finite shear <strain>. In this Maxwell problem the rapid elastic response supplies the natural strain scale $U/c=U\sqrt{\rho/G_0}$, which must be small for linearization through start-up. Away from the front, the strain accumulated over an effective memory interval must likewise be small. In the late diffusive regime its characteristic size is $\tau U/\sqrt{\nu t}$, which decreases with time. Large absolute wall displacement $Ut$ alone is not a disqualification for a fluid with finite memory; it is relative deformation during that memory that matters. In the elastic limit, where memory does not decay, the explicit strain behind the front confirms the condition $U/c\ll1$. Without such a small-strain restriction, the computed field is a formal linear response and need not describe an arbitrary <simple fluid>.

**With its material state fixed by a prescribed deformation history, the differential pom-pom model is a simple-fluid constitutive law.** The <stress> depends on the local history of a material element through its molecular stretch and orientation, without gradients of that history between neighbouring elements. A differential representation does not prevent a material from being a <simple fluid>.

To see the history dependence explicitly, use the component-first <velocity gradient> $K$, so the tensor evolution is $D_tA=KA+AK^T-(A-I)/\tau_1$. Let $F(t,s)$ be the relative <deformation gradient>, satisfying $\partial_tF=K(t)F$ and $F(s,s)=I$. Along a material trajectory, an <integrating factor> gives
$$
A(t)=e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_1}F(t,t_0)A(t_0)F(t,t_0)^T
+\frac1{\tau_1}\int_{t_0}^t e^{-(t-s)/\tau_1}F(t,s)F(t,s)^Tds.
$$
Thus $A$ is a functional of the local deformation history and its prepared initial state. If the initial $A$ is a <positive-definite matrix>, it retains that property, so $B=A/\operatorname{tr}A$ is well defined. Given this $B(t)$, the <scalar> stretch solves $\dot\lambda=r(t)\lambda+1/\tau_2$, where $r=B:K-1/\tau_2$. Its <variation of constants> formula again depends only on that local history:
$$
\lambda(t)=\lambda(t_0)e^{\int_{t_0}^t r(q)dq}
+\frac1{\tau_2}\int_{t_0}^t e^{\int_s^t r(q)dq}ds.
$$
The <differential pom-pom model> also satisfies <material frame indifference>. Under an observer rotation, $A$ and $B$ transform as objective tensors, while $\lambda$ is a scalar. The extra skew part of the transformed <velocity gradient> has zero contraction with symmetric $B$, so the stretch equation is unchanged. The tensor evolution uses an <upper-convected derivative>, and the final <stress> transforms objectively. Initial values cannot be independently forgotten when specifying a finite-past experiment: a fully specified prepared state, such as rest, is part of the history specification.

= Solution
{parent=b}

At rest the tensor equation relaxes $A$ toward $I$ and the stretch equation relaxes $\lambda$ toward one. Thus
$$
\boxed{A_0=I,\qquad B_0=\frac I3,\qquad\lambda_0=1.}
$$
The equilibrium nonpressure <stress> $GI/3$ is isotropic and can be absorbed into the arbitrary <pressure> in <incompressible flow>.

For the linear response, put $A=I+a+O(\varepsilon^2)$, $\lambda=1+\ell+O(\varepsilon^2)$ and take the <velocity gradient> to be $O(\varepsilon)$. The first-order tensor equation is
$$
D_ta+\frac a{\tau_1}=K+K^T=A_1.
$$
Its <matrix trace> has no forcing, because <incompressibility> gives $\operatorname{tr}K=0$. Starting relaxed, $\operatorname{tr}a=0$. The first-order stretch equation is $D_t\ell+\ell/\tau_2=(I/3):K=0$, hence $\ell=0$. Consequently $B=I/3+a/3+O(\varepsilon^2)$, and its anisotropic <stress> is $Ga/3$. From the relaxed remote past,
$$
\sigma^{\rm lin}=-p_*I+\frac G3\int_{-\infty}^t e^{-(t-s)/\tau_1}A_1(s)ds
=-p_*I+2\int_{-\infty}^t\frac G3e^{-(t-s)/\tau_1}E(s)ds.
$$
The <linear and second-order response of the differential pom-pom model> therefore has
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm rel}(t)=\frac G3e^{-t/\tau_1}\quad(t\ge0),\qquad\mu_0=\int_0^\infty G_{\rm rel}(t)dt=\frac{G\tau_1}{3}.}
$$
The stretch time $\tau_2$ does not enter the linear shear <relaxation modulus>.

For the <second-order fluid> constants, additionally make the slow-flow expansion: count $K$ as first order and $D_t$ as raising the order by one. Iterating
$$
A-I=\tau_1(KA+AK^T-D_tA)
$$
gives
$$
A=I+\tau_1A_1+\tau_1^2(KA_1+A_1K^T-D_tA_1)+O(3).
$$
Using the <Rivlin-Ericksen tensor> convention $A_2=D_tA_1+A_1K+K^TA_1$, the identity
$$
KA_1+A_1K^T+A_1K+K^TA_1=2A_1^2
$$
reduces this to $A=I+\tau_1A_1+\tau_1^2(2A_1^2-A_2)+O(3)$. Since $\operatorname{tr}A_2=\operatorname{tr}A_1^2$, normalization gives
$$
B=\frac I3+\frac{\tau_1}{3}A_1+\frac{\tau_1^2}{3}(2A_1^2-A_2)
-\frac{\tau_1^2}{9}\operatorname{tr}(A_1^2)I+O(3).
$$
Stretch first changes at second order. Its equation gives $\lambda-1=\tau_1\tau_2\operatorname{tr}(A_1^2)/6+O(3)$. Multiplication by $\lambda^2$ therefore adds only an isotropic <stress> at second order; multiplying its second-order increment by the anisotropic first-order term would be third order. Absorb these isotropic corrections into $p_*$ to obtain
$$
\sigma=-p_*I+\frac{G\tau_1}{3}A_1+\frac{G\tau_1^2}{3}(2A_1^2-A_2)+O(3).
$$
Comparison with the <viscometric functions> parametrization yields
$$
\boxed{\mu_0=\frac{G\tau_1}{3},\qquad\psi_1=\frac{2G\tau_1^2}{3},\qquad\psi_2=0.}
$$
Equivalently, in $\sigma=-pI+\mu_0A_1+\alpha_1A_2+\alpha_2A_1^2$, the constants are $\alpha_1=-G\tau_1^2/3$ and $\alpha_2=2G\tau_1^2/3$. The general small-amplitude linear response above permits fast oscillation; the local <second-order fluid> expansion also requires the slow-flow ordering just used.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Choose the extending direction as $x$ and write $s=\dot\gamma>0$. The <uniaxial extensional flow>, satisfying <incompressibility>, has
$$
K=\operatorname{diag}(s,-s/2,-s/2).
$$
Starting from rest, symmetry preserves $A=\operatorname{diag}(a,b,b)$. The <differential pom-pom model> tensor equation becomes
$$
\dot a=(2s-1/\tau_1)a+1/\tau_1,\qquad
\dot b=-(s+1/\tau_1)b+1/\tau_1,\qquad a(0)=b(0)=1.
$$
Put $x=s\tau_1$. For $x\ne1/2$, the solutions are
$$
a(t)=\frac{1-2x e^{(2x-1)t/\tau_1}}{1-2x},\qquad
b(t)=\frac{1+x e^{-(1+x)t/\tau_1}}{1+x}.
$$
If $x<1/2$, both transients decay, giving
$$
\boxed{A_\infty=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac1{1-2x},\frac1{1+x},\frac1{1+x}\right).}
$$
If $x>1/2$, the axial component is instead $a=(2x e^{(2x-1)t/\tau_1}-1)/(2x-1)$ and grows exponentially without bound, while $b\to1/(1+x)$. At the boundary $x=1/2$, which lies between the two stated regimes, $a=1+t/\tau_1$ grows linearly and $b\to2/3$.

Normalization makes the molecular orientation finite even when the unnormalized <conformation tensor> diverges. For $0\le x<1/2$, direct division by $a+2b$ gives
$$
\boxed{B_\infty=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1+x}{3(1-x)},\frac{1-2x}{3(1-x)},\frac{1-2x}{3(1-x)}\right).}
$$
For $x>1/2$, the divergent axial component dominates the <matrix trace>, so
$$
\boxed{B_\infty=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0).}
$$
The same normalized limit holds at $x=1/2$, agreeing continuously with the limit of the subcritical expression. The limiting orientations have nonnegative <eigenvalues> and unit <matrix trace>, as required for the normalized molecular orientation.

Now set $S(t)=B(t):K=s(a-b)/(a+2b)$. Its limiting value is
$$
S_\infty(s)=\begin{cases}
s^2\tau_1/(1-s\tau_1),&s\tau_1<1/2,\\
s,&s\tau_1\ge1/2.
\end{cases}
$$
The <scalar> stretch equation is $\dot\lambda=[S(t)-1/\tau_2]\lambda+1/\tau_2$. If $S_\infty<1/\tau_2$, its coefficient is eventually bounded above by a negative constant. The <variation of constants> formula gives a bounded solution, converging to
$$
\lambda_\infty=\frac1{1-\tau_2S_\infty}.
$$
If $S_\infty>1/\tau_2$, the coefficient is eventually positive, and the positive stretch grows without bound. At equality a bounded stretch would imply $\dot\lambda\to1/\tau_2>0$, which contradicts boundedness. Thus equality is not an additional bounded steady regime.

The function $S_\infty(s)$ is continuous and strictly increasing from zero to infinity. Consequently there is a unique critical extension rate defined by
$$
\tau_2 S_\infty(\dot\gamma_c)=1,
$$
and **the molecular stretch remains bounded precisely for $\dot\gamma<\dot\gamma_c$**. This is distinct from the tensor threshold $\dot\gamma\tau_1=1/2$. If $\tau_2<2\tau_1$, for example, the normalized orientation can remain well defined and the stretch remain bounded for some rates above the tensor threshold; divergence of $A$ alone does not prove divergence of the physical <stress>.

In the requested doubly subcritical regime, write $D=1-s\tau_1-s^2\tau_1\tau_2>0$. The <steady uniaxial extension of the differential pom-pom model> has
$$
\lambda_\infty=\frac{1-s\tau_1}{D},\qquad
B_{xx,\infty}-B_{yy,\infty}=\frac{s\tau_1}{1-s\tau_1}.
$$
The isotropic <pressure> cancels from the tensile <normal-stress difference>. Hence the <extensional viscosity> is
$$
\eta_E=\frac{\sigma_{xx}-\sigma_{yy}}s
=\frac{G\lambda_\infty^2}{s}(B_{xx,\infty}-B_{yy,\infty}),
$$
or
$$
\boxed{\eta_E(s)=\frac{G\tau_1(1-s\tau_1)}{(1-s\tau_1-s^2\tau_1\tau_2)^2},\qquad
s\tau_1<\frac12,\quad s<\dot\gamma_c.}
$$
Its zero-rate limit is $G\tau_1=3\mu_0$, recovering the <Trouton ratio> of three. The restrictions exclude both loss of a finite tensor steady state and loss of a finite stretch steady state; neither denominator may be continued through its singularity as a physical steady constitutive response.
