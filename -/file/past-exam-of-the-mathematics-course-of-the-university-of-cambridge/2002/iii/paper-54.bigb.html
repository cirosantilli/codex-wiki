= Solution
{parent=1}

Because $s$ is <arc length>, $|d\mathbf x/ds|=1$. Thus $\mathbf y=\mathbf n/\alpha$, where $\mathbf n$ is the unit tangent to the <elastic ray>. In an <isotropic> medium this is also the <wavefront> normal. Consequently **$\mathbf y$ is the vector <slowness>**, of magnitude $1/\alpha$; locally $\mathbf y=\nabla T=\mathbf k/\omega$, with $T$ the <travel time> and $\mathbf k$ the <wavevector>.

For depth-dependent <wave speed> $\alpha(z)$, the ray equation gives $dy_x/ds=dy_y/ds=0$. The horizontal component of $d\mathbf x/ds$ therefore has a fixed direction, and the <elastic ray> lies in the vertical plane determined by that direction and its initial point. A vertical <elastic ray> is the limiting case. If $i$ is the angle to the vertical, its conserved horizontal <slowness> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin i}{\alpha(z)}=p_{\mathrm{flat}}.}
$$
At a horizontal interface, matching the tangential <wavevector> at fixed <frequency> gives the same invariant on both sides: this is <Snell's law>.

For radial <wave speed> $\alpha(r)$, take the <cross product> of position with <slowness>. The ray equations give
$$
\frac{d}{ds}(\mathbf x\times\mathbf y)
=\alpha\mathbf y\times\mathbf y+\mathbf x\times\nabla(1/\alpha)=0,
$$
since the second vector is radial. A nonzero constant $\mathbf L=\mathbf x\times\mathbf y$ is perpendicular to every position on the <elastic ray>, so the ray lies in a fixed plane through the centre. Radial rays obey the same conclusion with a nonunique plane. The <spherical elastic ray invariant> is therefore
$$
\boxed{p=|\mathbf L|=\frac{r\sin i}{\alpha(r)},}
$$
where $i$ is measured from the radial direction. Across a spherical interface the radius is common and tangential <slowness> is continuous, so this form of <Snell's law> survives an interface discontinuity.

In a homogeneous sphere a <P-wave> follows the straight chord between the two surface points. If their principal angular separation is $0\leq\Delta\leq\pi$, its length is $2R\sin(\Delta/2)$. For constant <P-wave> speed $c_P$,
$$
\boxed{T_D(\Delta)=\frac{2R}{c_P}\sin\frac\Delta2.}
$$
Now put $a=R'$ and denote the mantle and core <P-wave> speeds by $c_m>c_c$. Equal incidence and reflection angles put the core-boundary reflection point on the angular bisector. Each mantle leg has length $L$ given by $L^2=R^2+a^2-2Ra\cos(\Delta/2)$. Hence the <core-reflected travel time> is
$$
\boxed{T_R(\Delta)=\frac{2}{c_m}\sqrt{R^2+a^2-2Ra\cos(\Delta/2)},\qquad
0\leq\Delta\leq\Delta_g=2\arccos(a/R).}
$$
The stated domain is essential. At the reflection point the incoming line approaches from the mantle only if $R\cos(\Delta/2)\geq a$. Otherwise it has already crossed the core boundary. The mantle-only direct chord also exists only for $\Delta\leq\Delta_g$, with $T_D=2R\sin(\Delta/2)/c_m$. In this interval,
$$
L^2-R^2\sin^2(\Delta/2)=[R\cos(\Delta/2)-a]^2\geq0,
$$
so the reflected arrival is later. Its intercept is $2(R-a)/c_m$, whereas the direct intercept is zero. At grazing incidence the branches meet at $T=2\sqrt{R^2-a^2}/c_m$, and both have slope $dT/d\Delta=a/c_m$. A continuation of the homogeneous-sphere direct formula beyond this endpoint is not a mantle-only arrival in the modified model.

The core-transmitted <elastic rays> consist of three straight legs. Their impact distances in mantle and core are $b_m=pc_m$ and $b_c=pc_c$, respectively. At core entry,
$$
\frac{\sin i_m}{c_m}=\frac{\sin i_c}{c_c}=\frac pa,
\qquad \sin i_R=\frac{pc_m}{R},\qquad 0\leq p\leq a/c_m.
$$
Since $c_c<c_m$, the <elastic ray> bends towards the normal on entry and away from it on exit. The mantle leg subtends $i_m-i_R$ at the centre, and the core chord subtends $\pi-2i_c$. Thus the total angular sweep along the transmitted path is $2(i_m-i_R)+\pi-2i_c$. This sweep need not equal the principal separation if it exceeds $\pi$; the diagram uses a path with sweep below $\pi$.

= Solution

An <elastic deformation> is reversible: after removal of the deforming loads the material returns to its reference state, and its instantaneous <stress> is determined by its current deformation rather than a plastic or viscous history. In the infinitesimal approximation the <displacement field> $u_i$ gives the <infinitesimal strain tensor> $e_{ij}=(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})/2$. The <Cauchy stress tensor> is defined by <traction> $t_i=\sigma_{ij}n_j$ on a plane of unit normal $\mathbf n$; absence of body couples makes $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$.

Let $\mathcal E(e)$ be the recoverable <elastic energy> per unit mass. The local mechanical power per unit volume is
$$
\sigma_{ij}\partial_j\dot u_i=\sigma_{ij}\dot e_{ij},
$$
because the symmetric <Cauchy stress tensor> annihilates the antisymmetric part of the velocity gradient. Conservation of <energy> during the deformation gives $\rho\dot{\mathcal E}=\sigma_{ij}\dot e_{ij}$. Since $\dot{\mathcal E}=(\partial\mathcal E/\partial e_{ij})\dot e_{ij}$ for arbitrary symmetric <strain> rates,
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{ij}=\rho\frac{\partial\mathcal E}{\partial e_{ij}}.}
$$
Here the derivative is the gradient on the space of symmetric tensors, with the double-contraction inner product. This convention accounts for the two occurrences of each off-diagonal entry; treating six independent coordinates without their corresponding factors would give an erroneous factor of two in shear components.

Take a stress-free reference state of <mass density> $\rho_0$. The <Taylor series> of the <elastic energy> starts quadratically:
$$
\mathcal E(e)=\mathcal E(0)+\frac1{2\rho_0}c_{ijkl}e_{ij}e_{kl}+O(|e|^3).
$$
Using $\rho=\rho_0+O(|e|)$ gives $\sigma_{ij}=c_{ijkl}e_{kl}+O(|e|^2)$, the relation of <linear elasticity>. If the reference state has initial <stress>, the first-order expansion also retains that constant <stress>. Symmetry of <stress> and <strain> gives the minor symmetries $c_{ijkl}=c_{jikl}=c_{ijlk}$, while equality of the mixed second derivatives of <elastic energy> gives the major symmetry $c_{ijkl}=c_{klij}$. Thus the <elastic stiffness tensor> is a symmetric map on a six-dimensional <strain> space: **there are $6\cdot7/2=21$ independent coefficients**.

For <twofold elastic symmetry>, choose the symmetry axis as $x_3$. Rotation through $\pi$ is $Q=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$, and transforms the <infinitesimal strain tensor> into $QeQ^T$. Components $e_{11},e_{22},e_{33},e_{12}$ are even and $e_{23},e_{13}$ are odd. Invariance of <elastic energy> excludes every coupling between these parity classes. In the engineering <strain> ordering $(e_{11},e_{22},e_{33},2e_{23},2e_{13},2e_{12})$, with corresponding <stress> ordering $(\sigma_{11},\sigma_{22},\sigma_{33},\sigma_{23},\sigma_{13},\sigma_{12})$, the symmetric stiffness matrix has the form
$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}
C_{11}&C_{12}&C_{13}&0&0&C_{16}\\
C_{12}&C_{22}&C_{23}&0&0&C_{26}\\
C_{13}&C_{23}&C_{33}&0&0&C_{36}\\
0&0&0&C_{44}&C_{45}&0\\
0&0&0&C_{45}&C_{55}&0\\
C_{16}&C_{26}&C_{36}&0&0&C_{66}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its symmetric even block has ten independent entries and its symmetric odd block has three. Hence **a twofold axis leaves $13$ independent coefficients**. No invariance under other rotation angles has been assumed.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For a homogeneous lossless elastic solid, insert the <plane wave> $u_i=\operatorname{Re}\{U a_i e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}\}$ into the equations of <linear elasticity>. Put $\mathbf k=\kappa\mathbf n$, $|\mathbf n|=1$, and $v=\omega/\kappa$. The resulting <eigenvalue> problem is
$$
\Gamma_{ik}(\mathbf n)a_k=\rho v^2 a_i,\qquad
\Gamma_{ik}=c_{ijkl}n_jn_l.
$$
The minor and major symmetries make this <acoustic tensor> a real <symmetric matrix>. For a stable material it is <positive-definite>, so its three positive <eigenvalues> give three real positive <phase velocities> and therefore **three <slownesses> $s_a=1/v_a$**. Coincident <eigenvalues> are possible, so there need not be three distinct speeds. For distinct <eigenvalues> the displacement <polarization (waves)> are orthogonal; in an <anisotropic> solid they are generally quasi-longitudinal and quasi-transverse rather than exactly longitudinal and transverse. Stability, or at least <strong ellipticity in elasticity>, is required for the assertion about positive real speeds.

The <elastic slowness surface> is the locus of the three vectors $\mathbf p=\mathbf n/v_a(\mathbf n)=\mathbf k/\omega$. Its equation is
$$
\det[c_{ijkl}p_jp_l-\rho\delta_{ik}]=0.
$$
For each <wavefront> normal there are three radial sheets, which may meet at degeneracies. To obtain the <energy flux> directly, multiply the elastic equation by $\dot u_i$. For
$$
W=\tfrac12\rho\dot u_i\dot u_i+\tfrac12 c_{ijkl}e_{ij}e_{kl},
$$
the symmetry of <stress> gives $\partial_t W=\partial_j(\sigma_{ij}\dot u_i)$. Thus the instantaneous <elastic-wave energy flux> is $F_j=-\sigma_{ij}\dot u_i$. With a real branch <polarization (waves)> $\mathbf a$, harmonic averaging gives
$$
\langle F_j\rangle=\frac12\omega|U|^2c_{ijkl}k_l a_i a_k
=\frac12\omega^2|U|^2c_{ijkl}p_l a_i a_k.
$$
Now differentiate $c_{ijkl}p_jp_l a_k=\rho a_i$ along a regular <slowness surface> sheet and contract with $a_i$. The terms containing $d\mathbf a$ cancel by symmetry and the original <eigenvalue> equation, leaving
$$
2c_{ijkl}a_i a_k p_l\,dp_j=0.
$$
Every tangent $d\mathbf p$ is consequently perpendicular to $\langle\mathbf F\rangle$: **the <energy flux> is normal to the <slowness surface>**. At a degenerate point this statement is made on the limiting branches, rather than assuming a unique normal.

For completeness, equality of the mean kinetic and <strain> energies gives $\langle W\rangle=\rho\omega^2|U|^2|\mathbf a|^2/2$. Differentiating the <acoustic tensor> equation in the <wavevector> variables similarly gives
$$
g_j=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k_j}
=\frac{c_{ijkl}k_l a_i a_k}{\rho\omega|\mathbf a|^2},\qquad
\boxed{\langle\mathbf F\rangle=\langle W\rangle\mathbf g.}
$$
Thus the <group velocity> is the <elastic energy velocity>. The homogeneous elastic <dispersion relation> is degree one in $\mathbf k$, so differentiating $\omega(\lambda\mathbf k)=\lambda\omega(\mathbf k)$ at $\lambda=1$ gives $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf g=1$.

The <elastic wave surface> is the unit-time locus of these <group velocities>. From an impulsive point source, the propagating <wavefronts> at time $t$ are its sheets scaled to $\mathbf x=t\mathbf g$. A particular source may fail to excite a mode in some directions, and its amplitudes require the source <polarization (waves)> and geometrical spreading; the <wave surface> determines the arrival geometry. It does not specify all near-field motion between the radiative fronts. Geometrically it is the envelope of the planes $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x=1$: tangent variation gives $d\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x=0$, so $\mathbf x$ is the appropriately normalized normal to the <slowness surface>.

In a symmetry-plane cross-section write $\mathbf p=r(\theta)\mathbf n$, $\mathbf n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $\mathbf t=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$. The two envelope conditions give
$$
\mathbf g=\frac{\mathbf n}{r}-\frac{r'}{r^2}\mathbf t
=v\mathbf n+v'\mathbf t,\qquad
\mathbf g'=(v+v'')\mathbf t
=\frac{r^2+2r'^2-r r''}{r^3}\mathbf t.
$$
The numerator is the signed curvature numerator of the <slowness surface> cross-section. At a simple <inflection point>, $h=v+v''=0$ and $h'\ne0$. Then $\mathbf g''=h'\mathbf t$ and $\mathbf g'''=h''\mathbf t+2h'\mathbf t'$ are linearly independent. These are precisely the local conditions for an ordinary <cusp>. The <slowness curvature and wave-surface cusps> relation explains why a smooth nonconvex <slowness surface> can generate a cusped <wavefront> and multiple ray arrivals.

The example below uses a positive orthotropic stiffness in normalized units: $\rho=1$, $C_{11}=C_{22}=C_{33}=4$, $C_{44}=C_{55}=1.2$, $C_{66}=1$, and all normal off-diagonal stiffnesses zero. In the symmetry plane $n_3=0$, its two in-plane <acoustic tensor> <eigenvalues> are $v_\pm^2=(5\pm\sqrt{5+4\cos4\theta})/2$, and its out-of-plane shear speed is $\sqrt{1.2}$. The in-plane shear sheet is nonconvex and produces the marked <cusps>; the construction uses an actual stable elastic tensor.

= Solution

Let $\Omega$ be the solid exterior to the cavity, and choose $\mathbf n$ outward from the solid, hence into the cavity on its boundary $S$. Subtraction of the incident solution from the total solution gives the scattered <displacement field> equations
$$
\rho\ddot u_i^s=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}^s,\qquad
\sigma_{ij}^s=c_{ijkl}e_{kl}^s,\qquad
e_{kl}^s=\tfrac12(u_{k,l}^s+u_{l,k}^s),\qquad \mathbf x\in\Omega.
$$
The traction-free cavity condition for the total field gives **$\sigma_{ij}^s n_j=-\sigma_{ij}^0 n_j$ on $S$**. The scattered field has zero <initial displacement> and <initial velocity> before the pulse reaches the cavity and satisfies an outgoing <radiation condition>, excluding an additional incoming <elastic wave>. Equivalently one seeks the causal response to this prescribed boundary <traction>. Although the incident plane pulse extends across an infinite plane, the causal scattered field from the bounded cavity has finite propagation distance at each finite time.

To prove <energy uniqueness for traction-driven elasticity>, take the difference $\mathbf w$ of two solutions with the same <initial conditions>, <body force>, boundary <traction> and outgoing condition. It satisfies
$$
\rho\ddot w_i=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}(w),\quad
\sigma_{ij}(w)n_j=0\text{ on }S,\quad
\mathbf w(\cdot,t_0)=\dot{\mathbf w}(\cdot,t_0)=0.
$$
For a large enclosing ball $B_L$, multiply by $\dot w_i$ and integrate over $\Omega\cap B_L$. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_{\Omega\cap B_L}\rho\ddot w_i\dot w_i\,dV
=\int_{\partial(\Omega\cap B_L)}\dot w_i\sigma_{ij}(w)n_j\,dS
-\int_{\Omega\cap B_L}\sigma_{ij}(w)\partial_j\dot w_i\,dV.
$$
Symmetry of <stress> replaces the last derivative by $\dot e_{ij}(w)$, and major symmetry of the <elastic stiffness tensor> makes that integral the derivative of the <strain> <energy>. Therefore
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\int_{\Omega\cap B_L}
[\rho|\dot{\mathbf w}|^2+c_{ijkl}e_{ij}(w)e_{kl}(w)]\,dV
=\int_{\partial(\Omega\cap B_L)}\dot w_i\sigma_{ij}(w)n_j\,dS.
$$
The cavity contribution is zero. On any fixed finite time interval choose $L$ beyond the causal support of the scattered difference; the outer contribution is then zero as well. This gives conservation of its nonnegative <elastic energy>. Positive <mass density> and positive <strain> <energy> imply that zero initial <energy> forces $\dot{\mathbf w}=0$ everywhere; the zero initial <displacement field> then gives $\mathbf w=0$. The same argument works for finite-energy outgoing solutions by taking the limit of enclosing surfaces and excluding incoming <energy>. Hence **the causal scattered field $\mathbf u^s$ is unique**. Specifying the incident field and cavity <traction> alone without the initial and outgoing conditions would not establish uniqueness.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Use fixed spatial coordinates in a stationary heterogeneous Earth and define the operator
$$
(\mathcal L u)_i=\rho(\mathbf x)\partial_t^2u_i
-\partial_{x_j}[c_{ijkl}(\mathbf x)\partial_{x_l}u_k].
$$
Here the <elastic stiffness tensor> has its minor and major symmetries, and the physical <body force> per unit volume is $q_i=\rho f_i$. The retarded <elastodynamic Green tensor> $G_i^{\ j}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\xi,t)$ is the displacement in direction $i$ due to a unit physical force impulse in direction $j$ at $\boldsymbol\xi$:
$$
\mathcal L_{ik}G_k^{\ j}
=\delta_i^{\ j}\delta(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)\delta(t),\qquad
G_i^{\ j}=0\quad(t<0).
$$
It satisfies the actual Earth's homogeneous <boundary conditions>, including zero <traction> on the free surface and continuity of displacement and <traction> at bonded internal interfaces. Integration through $t=0$ gives $\rho(\mathbf x)\partial_tG_i^{\ j}(0^+)=\delta_i^{\ j}\delta(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\xi)$, with $G(0^+)=0$. No spherical symmetry is required.

For zero <initial conditions>, form the <convolution>
$$
\boxed{u_i(\mathbf x,t)=\int_0^t\!d\tau\int_V
G_i^{\ j}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\xi,t-\tau)\rho(\boldsymbol\xi)f_j(\boldsymbol\xi,\tau)\,dV_\xi.}
$$
Applying $\mathcal L$ to this expression uses the defining <Dirac delta function> to give $\rho(\mathbf x)f_i(\mathbf x,t)$. Causality gives zero <initial conditions>, and each Green column supplies the required <boundary conditions>. The positive-energy uniqueness argument then identifies this expression with the physical response. The source factor is $\rho(\boldsymbol\xi)$ because $f$ is force per unit mass, whereas the Green tensor was normalized to unit force.

A force dipole is a limiting pair of opposite point forces separated by a small vector. Put $+F_j$ at $\boldsymbol\xi+\varepsilon\mathbf d/2$ and $-F_j$ at $\boldsymbol\xi-\varepsilon\mathbf d/2$, and hold $D_{jk}=\varepsilon F_jd_k$ fixed as $\varepsilon\to0$. Expansion of the two <Dirac delta functions> gives
$$
q_j(\boldsymbol\eta,t)=-D_{jk}(t)\partial_{\eta_k}\delta(\boldsymbol\eta-\boldsymbol\xi).
$$
Its resultant force is zero, and <integration by parts> gives its radiated displacement as $\int_0^t D_{jk}(\tau)\partial_{\xi_k}G_i^{\ j}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\xi,t-\tau)\,d\tau$. Thus the source derivative of the <elastodynamic Green tensor> represents a dipole with force direction $j$ and separation direction $k$.

For the surface source choose a normal $\mathbf n$ from the minus side to the plus side and define
$$
b_i=[u_i]=u_i^+-u_i^-,\qquad [t_i]=(\sigma_{ij}^+-\sigma_{ij}^-)n_j.
$$
The same normal is used in both tractions. If the elastic coefficients are continuous through the surface, the distributional gradient of a piecewise smooth displacement is $\partial_j u_i=(\partial_j u_i)_{\mathrm{reg}}+b_i n_j\delta_\Sigma$. Its <stress> therefore has a singular part $c_{ijkl}b_k n_l\delta_\Sigma$. Taking the divergence includes the regular <stress> jump, so a field satisfying the source-free equations on both sides has equivalent source
$$
q_i=-[t_i]\delta_\Sigma-\partial_j[c_{ijkl}b_k n_l\delta_\Sigma].
$$
In particular, the signs follow from the chosen jump convention, not from an unspecified orientation. The <elastodynamic surface-jump representation> is consequently
$$
\boxed{u_i=\int_0^t\!d\tau\int_\Sigma
\left[-G_i^{\ j}[t_j]+M_{jk}\partial_{\xi_k}G_i^{\ j}\right]dS_\xi,
\qquad M_{jk}=c_{jk\ell m}b_\ell n_m,}
$$
with the Green tensor evaluated at $(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\xi,t-\tau)$. The first term is a surface distribution of forces, and the second is a surface distribution of dipoles.

One can derive the same formula without multiplying discontinuous material coefficients by a surface delta. Apply <integration by parts> on the two sides of $\Sigma$ to the field $u$ and the reciprocal Green field $v$. Major symmetry cancels the volume <strain> terms; the spatial identity is
$$
\int (v_i\mathcal L u_i-u_i\mathcal L v_i)\,dV
=\int_{\partial V}[u_i t_i(v)-v_i t_i(u)]\,dS
$$
when the temporal terms are handled by the retarded time <convolution>. More explicitly, take $v_i(\boldsymbol\xi,\tau)=G_i^{\ a}(\boldsymbol\xi,\mathbf x,t-\tau)$, extend the zero-initial-data solution causally to $\tau<0$, and integrate over all source times. Integration by parts cancels the two temporal terms, with zero endpoint terms; the causal supports subsequently reduce the representation to $0\leq\tau\leq t$. The Green impulse contributes $-u_a(\mathbf x,t)$ on the left. The two outward face normals are $\mathbf n$ and $-\mathbf n$, so their sum on the right is $-[u_i]t_i(v)+v_i[t_i]$. Moving it to the other side gives $[u_i]t_i(v)-v_i[t_i]$. The same identity applied to two force Green fields with interchanged source and receiver proves the reciprocal relation $G_i^{\ j}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\xi,t)=G_j^{\ i}(\boldsymbol\xi,\mathbf x,t)$; hence the displayed surface formula follows. On a bonded material interface the Green displacement and common Green <traction> are continuous: $[u_i]t_i(v)$ is then the unambiguous version of the dipole term, evaluated with the matching one-sided coefficients. External free-surface terms vanish because both tractions vanish.

For a fault in <isotropic> material, the <Lamé parameters> give
$$
c_{jk\ell m}=\lambda\delta_{jk}\delta_{\ell m}
+\mu(\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{km}+\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell}),\qquad
M_{jk}=\lambda(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf n)\delta_{jk}
+\mu(b_jn_k+n_jb_k).
$$
Tangential slip obeys $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf n=0$, and ordinary fault <traction> is continuous across the two faces. Therefore **the source is a <double-couple fault source>** with
$$
\boxed{M_{jk}=\mu(b_jn_k+n_jb_k),\qquad [t_j]=0.}
$$
For a uniform patch of area $A$ with $\mathbf b=b\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf n=\mathbf e_3$, its integrated <seismic moment tensor> is
$$
\mathsf M=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&M_0\\0&0&0\\M_0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_0=\mu A b.
$$
The $13$ dipole is a force in the slip direction separated in the normal direction; the $31$ dipole is a normal force separated in the slip direction. Their equal and opposite <torques> cancel. The symmetric trace-free <seismic moment tensor> has <eigenvalues> $M_0,-M_0,0$, with zero resultant force and <torque>. A temporal <stress> drop on a fault is not a jump of <traction> between its two simultaneously matching faces; it does not add a single-force layer to this tangential-slip representation.
