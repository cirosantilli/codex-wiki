= Solution
{parent=1}

For $m=1$ the conclusion is immediate, so suppose $m\ge2$ and index the terms of an <arithmetic progression> by $0,\ldots,m-1$. There are only finitely many <equivalence relations> on this index <set>. Consequently, recording which terms have equal colours produces a <finite colouring> of the positive parameter pairs $(a,d)$, even though the original colouring need not be finite.

We use the <Gallai theorem for an integer lattice> in this form: a <finite colouring> of the positive integer lattice contains a <monochromatic> <homothetic copy> of any prescribed finite integer pattern. Patterns with negative coordinates are allowed here by first translating them into the positive quadrant; the translation is absorbed into the base point of the resulting copy.

Consider the finite pattern
$$
F=\bigcup_{0\le r<s<m}\{(su-rv,v-u):0\le u,v<m\}\subseteq\mathbb Z^2.
$$
The <Gallai theorem for an integer lattice> supplies integers $a_0,d_0$ and $q>0$ such that every point of $(a_0,d_0)+qF$ lies in the positive quadrant and has the same equality <equivalence relation> $E$. If $E$ has only singleton classes, any parameter pair in this copy gives an <arithmetic progression> whose colours are pairwise distinct, so the original colouring is <injective> on it.

Otherwise choose $r<s$ with $r\mathrel E s$. For every $u,v\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$, the parameter pair
$$
(a,d)=(a_0+q(su-rv),\ d_0+q(v-u))
$$
has relation $E$. Its terms at indices $r$ and $s$ are therefore equally coloured. But these terms simplify to
$$
a+rd=a_0+rd_0+q(s-r)u=:A_u,\qquad
a+sd=a_0+sd_0+q(s-r)v=:B_v.
$$
Thus $c(A_u)=c(B_v)$ for every $u,v$. Fixing $v=0$ proves that all $A_u$ have the same colour. Every $A_u$ is positive, because it is a term of one of the positive-parameter progressions above, and the <common difference> $q(s-r)$ is positive. Hence $(A_u)_{u=0}^{m-1}$ is a <monochromatic> <arithmetic progression> of length $m$.

The <canonical arithmetic progression dichotomy> is therefore **constant or injective on an <arithmetic progression> of the prescribed length**. The key point is that one equal-colour index pair has been made to vary independently over two complete progressions; a merely <monochromatic> block of parameter pairs would not by itself give this conclusion.

**Van der Waerden theorem.** Given <positive integers> $r,k$, there is a finite $W(r,k)$ such that every <finite colouring> of $\{1,\ldots,W(r,k)\}$ with at most $r$ colours has a <monochromatic> <arithmetic progression> of length $k$ and positive <common difference>. In particular, every <finite colouring> of the <positive integers> contains such progressions of every finite length.

Here is a <colour-focusing proof of Van der Waerden theorem>. We induct on $k$, simultaneously for all numbers of colours. The assertion for $k=1$ is immediate. Suppose $W(r,k)$ exists for every $r$, and fix $r$. We construct bounds $F_t$, for $1\le t\le r$, with this property: every colouring of $[F_t]$ either has a <monochromatic> progression of length $k+1$, or has $t$ progressions of length $k$, of distinct colours, which share a next point $f\in[F_t]$. Write those progressions as
$$
f-hd_i,\qquad 1\le h\le k,\quad 1\le i\le t,
$$
with positive steps $d_i$ and all displayed points in $[F_t]$.

For $t=1$, put $L=W(r,k)$ and $F_1=2L$. A <monochromatic> progression of length $k$ in $[L]$ has its next point in $[2L]$, since its step is at most $L$; for $k=1$ choose step $1$. This establishes the initial focusing property.

Suppose $F_t=N$ has been constructed. Put $M=W(r^N,k)$ and $F_{t+1}=2NM$. Divide the first $NM$ integers into $M$ consecutive blocks of size $N$. Colour a block by its full <colour profile>, an $N$-tuple having at most $r^N$ possible values. The induction hypothesis on $k$ gives block indices
$$
j_0,j_0+D,\ldots,j_0+(k-1)D
$$
with the same <colour profile>. For $k=1$ use any one block and $D=1$. Suppose that the whole interval has no <monochromatic> progression of length $k+1$. Its repeated profile has $t$ focused length-$k$ progressions with positions $f-hd_i$ and distinct colours $q_i$. The colour $q$ of position $f$ differs from every $q_i$, since equality would complete a length-$(k+1)$ progression in the block.

Set $j_*=j_0+kD$ and $F=N(j_*-1)+f$. The index $j_*$ is at most $2M$, so this focus is in the full interval. For $1\le h\le k$, the point
$$
F-h(ND+d_i)=N(j_*-hD-1)+(f-hd_i)
$$
is in one of the chosen blocks and has colour $q_i$. Also $F-hND$ is at position $f$ in that block and has colour $q$. We have produced $t+1$ focused length-$k$ progressions of distinct colours. This proves the recursive focusing property. At $t=r$, the focus itself must have one of the $r$ colours, so one of the progressions extends to length $k+1$. Thus $W(r,k+1)=F_r$ is a valid bound, completing the <mathematical induction> and the requested proof.

To obtain the equation criterion, we first prove the small strengthening of <Van der Waerden theorem> that we need. For any <positive integers> $r,s,L$, there is a finite bound forcing a <monochromatic> configuration
$$
\{sd,\ b,b+d,\ldots,b+(L-1)d\},\qquad b,d>0.
$$
This is the <Brauer progression theorem>, and the following argument proves it from the theorem just established. Induct on $r$. One colour is immediate. Given a bound $M$ for $r-1$ colours with the same $s,L$, let $T=W(r,(L-1)M+1)$ and colour an interval at least as long as $\max(T,sMT)$. Find a <monochromatic> progression $a,a+d,\ldots,a+(L-1)Md$ in $[T]$, of colour $q$; take $d=1$ if this progression has one term. If some $jsd$, $1\le j\le M$, has colour $q$, its first $L$ suitably spaced terms $a,a+jd,\ldots,a+(L-1)jd$ and the point $s(jd)$ give the configuration. Otherwise the colouring of the indices $1,\ldots,M$ obtained from $j\mapsto c(jsd)$ uses at most $r-1$ colours. The induction hypothesis supplies indices $se,b,b+e,\ldots,b+(L-1)e$ of one colour. Multiplication by $sd$ gives the desired configuration with step $esd$. This completes the strengthening without assuming any <Rado theorem>.

Multiply the rational coefficients by a common positive denominator, so that all $a_i$ are nonzero <integers>; this does not alter the equation or <partition regularity>. By definition, the row is <partition regular> when every <finite colouring> admits positive <monochromatic> $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ satisfying $\sum_i a_ix_i=0$. The variables need not be distinct.

For necessity, choose a <prime number> $p>\sum_i|a_i|$. Write each positive $x$ uniquely as $p^{v_p(x)}u$, with $p\nmid u$, and colour $x$ by $u\bmod p$. This is a <finite colouring> with $p-1$ colours. If a <monochromatic> solution exists, let $v=\min_i v_p(x_i)$ and $I=\{i:v_p(x_i)=v\}$. Dividing the equation by $p^v$ and reducing modulo $p$ gives
$$
0\equiv u_0\sum_{i\in I}a_i\pmod p,
$$
where $u_0\ne0\pmod p$ is the common colour. Thus $\sum_{i\in I}a_i\equiv0\pmod p$. Its absolute value is strictly less than $p$, so $\sum_{i\in I}a_i=0$. The index set $I$ is nonempty. This proves the <leading-residue obstruction to partition regularity>.

Conversely, suppose $\sum_{i\in I}a_i=0$ for a nonempty $I$. If $I$ is all indices, taking every variable equal to any one positive integer already gives a <monochromatic> solution. Otherwise fix $i_0\in I$, put $s=|a_{i_0}|$, $C=\sum_{j\notin I}a_j$, and define integer offsets
$$
\lambda_{i_0}=-\operatorname{sgn}(a_{i_0})C,\qquad
\lambda_i=0\quad(i\in I\setminus\{i_0\}).
$$
Let $u=\min_{i\in I}\lambda_i$ and choose $L=1+\max_{i\in I}\lambda_i-u$. The proved <Brauer progression theorem> gives a <monochromatic> configuration consisting of $sd$ and a length-$L$ progression starting at $b$. Set
$$
x_i=b+(\lambda_i-u)d\quad(i\in I),\qquad x_j=sd\quad(j\notin I).
$$
All variables are positive and of one colour. Their weighted sum is
$$
\sum_i a_ix_i=(b-ud)\sum_{i\in I}a_i+d\sum_{i\in I}a_i\lambda_i+sdC
=0-sCd+sCd=0.
$$
This explicitly gives a <Brauer configuration for a one-row zero-sum equation>. We conclude
$$
\boxed{(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\text{ is partition regular}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists\varnothing\ne I\subseteq[n],\ \sum_{i\in I}a_i=0.}
$$
Both directions have been proved independently of the general <Rado theorem>.

We take $\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$. An <ultrafilter> $p$ on $\mathbb N$ is a proper <filter on a set> which decides every subset: it contains $\mathbb N$ but not $\varnothing$, is closed under finite <intersections> and passage to supersets, and contains exactly one of $A$ and $\mathbb N\setminus A$ for every $A\subseteq\mathbb N$. A <principal ultrafilter> has the form $\delta_n=\{A:n\in A\}$; all other <ultrafilters> are <nonprincipal ultrafilters>.

To prove existence, start with the <cofinite filter> and order its proper filter extensions by inclusion. The union of a chain is again a proper <filter on a set>: the finitely many sets involved in an intersection all belong to one member of the chain, and no member contains the empty set. Thus <Zorn's lemma> supplies a maximal proper filter $p$. If $A\notin p$, adjoining $A$ must make the generated filter improper; equivalently, some $B\in p$ has $A\cap B=\varnothing$. Then $B\subseteq\mathbb N\setminus A$, so $\mathbb N\setminus A\in p$. This proves that $p$ is an <ultrafilter>. It contains the complement of every finite set and hence no finite set. In particular, it contains no singleton and is **nonprincipal**.

The <Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers> $\beta\mathbb N$ is the set of all <ultrafilters> with basic <open sets>
$$
\widehat A=\{p:A\in p\},\qquad A\subseteq\mathbb N.
$$
We have $\widehat A\cap\widehat B=\widehat{A\cap B}$ and $\beta\mathbb N\setminus\widehat A=\widehat{\mathbb N\setminus A}$, so these basic sets define a <topology> and are <clopen sets>. If $p\ne q$, choose $A\in p\setminus q$. The disjoint basic <neighbourhoods> $\widehat A$ and $\widehat{\mathbb N\setminus A}$ separate them. Thus the space is <Hausdorff>.

For <compactness>, it suffices to show every basic open cover has a finite subcover, since an arbitrary open cover can be refined by basic <open sets>. Suppose $\{\widehat{A_i}:i\in I\}$ has no finite subcover. The complements $\mathbb N\setminus A_i$ have the <finite intersection property>: if a finite intersection were empty, the corresponding finite family of $\widehat{A_i}$ would cover every <ultrafilter>. Their generated proper <filter on a set> extends to an <ultrafilter>, by the same <Zorn's lemma> argument. This <ultrafilter> contains every $\mathbb N\setminus A_i$, and therefore belongs to none of the alleged cover members, a contradiction. We have proved that $\beta\mathbb N$ is a **compact Hausdorff space**. The map $n\mapsto\delta_n$ embeds the discrete natural numbers densely: any nonempty $\widehat A$ contains $\delta_n$ for some $n\in A$.

For <addition on the Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers>, define
$$
A-n=\{m\in\mathbb N:n+m\in A\},\qquad D_q(A)=\{n:A-n\in q\},
$$
and put
$$
A\in p+q\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_q(A)\in p.
$$
The map $D_q$ preserves intersections and complements, because translation does and $q$ is an <ultrafilter>. It also sends $\mathbb N$ to $\mathbb N$ and the empty set to itself. Consequently the displayed rule defines an <ultrafilter> $p+q$. On principal points it satisfies $\delta_n+\delta_m=\delta_{n+m}$, so it extends ordinary addition.

Write $(\forall^p n)\,\varphi(n)$ to mean that $\{n:\varphi(n)\}\in p$. The <ultrafilter> addition rule is then an iterated quantifier, with its order fixed. Expansion gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\in(p+q)+r
&\Longleftrightarrow (\forall^p n)(\forall^q m)(\forall^r \ell)\,[n+m+\ell\in A],\\
A\in p+(q+r)
&\Longleftrightarrow (\forall^p n)(\forall^q m)(\forall^r \ell)\,[n+(m+\ell)\in A].
\end{aligned}
$$
Ordinary addition is an <associative operation>, and the quantifiers are in the same order on both lines. Thus $\boxed{(p+q)+r=p+(q+r)}$. This argument does not interchange <ultrafilter> quantifiers and does not assert that this extended addition is commutative.

For fixed $q$, the translation $R_q:p\mapsto p+q$ has
$$
R_q^{-1}(\widehat A)=\widehat{D_q(A)},
$$
a basic <clopen set>. Therefore addition is **continuous in its left variable**. This is the left-continuity convention used here: right translations are continuous, giving a <left-topological semigroup>. Continuity in both variables jointly is not part of the assertion.

**Hindman's theorem** says that every <finite colouring> of the <positive integers> has an increasing sequence $x_1<x_2<\cdots$ whose <finite-sums set>
$$
\operatorname{FS}(x_1,x_2,\ldots)=\left\{\sum_{i\in F}x_i:\varnothing\ne F\subseteq\mathbb N\text{ finite}\right\}
$$
is <monochromatic>. Assume, as permitted, that the addition just defined has an <idempotent ultrafilter> $p$, so $p+p=p$. This <ultrafilter> is nonprincipal: a principal $\delta_n$ would satisfy $\delta_n+\delta_n=\delta_{2n}\ne\delta_n$ for positive $n$. Hence every member of $p$ is infinite and every <cofinite set> belongs to $p$.

Exactly one cell $A$ of the finite colour partition belongs to $p$. Define its star set by
$$
A^*=A\cap D_p(A)=\{n\in A:A-n\in p\}.
$$
Since $A\in p+p$, we have $D_p(A)\in p$ and therefore $A^*\in p$. Moreover, for each $n\in A^*$, the set $A-n$ belongs to $p$. Idempotence gives $D_p(A-n)\in p$, and translation of the defining formula shows
$$
D_p(A-n)=D_p(A)-n,\qquad
A^*-n=(A-n)\cap D_p(A-n)\in p.
$$
This proves the <idempotent-ultrafilter star-set lemma> rather than merely invoking it.

Choose $x_1\in A^*$. Suppose $x_1<\cdots<x_k$ have been chosen with every nonempty finite sum in $A^*$. Choose $x_{k+1}$ from
$$
A^*\cap\bigcap_{s\in\operatorname{FS}(x_1,\ldots,x_k)}(A^*-s)\cap\{n:n>x_k\}.
$$
This is a finite intersection of members of the <ultrafilter> $p$, and so it is nonempty. The new sums are $x_{k+1}$ and $s+x_{k+1}$; all lie in $A^*$ by the choice. <Mathematical induction> yields an increasing infinite sequence with
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{FS}(x_1,x_2,\ldots)\subseteq A^*\subseteq A.}
$$
Thus the assumed idempotent gives the full <Hindman theorem>, with distinct increasing generators and all their nonempty finite sums of one colour.

Write $[A]^\omega$ for the infinite subsets of an infinite $A\subseteq\mathbb N$. A <Ramsey set of infinite subsets> is a family $E\subseteq[\mathbb N]^\omega$ such that each infinite ground set $A$ has an infinite subset $B$ with
$$
[B]^\omega\subseteq E\qquad\text{or}\qquad [B]^\omega\cap E=\varnothing.
$$
The convention asking only for one such homogeneous cone is weaker; the proof below establishes the displayed property and, in fact, the stronger <completely Ramsey set> property.

For a finite stem $s$ and an infinite reservoir $A$ lying above every element of $s$, put
$$
[s,A]=\{s\cup X:X\in[A]^\omega\}.
$$
These sets form the basic open <neighbourhoods> of the <Ellentuck topology>, or <star topology>. Here $s$ is an initial segment of every member of $[s,A]$. A star-<Borel set> means a member of the <sigma-algebra> generated by these <open sets>. A family is <completely Ramsey> if every $[s,A]$ has a homogeneous refinement $[s,B]$ with $B\subseteq A$ infinite and the same stem $s$. We prove this for every star-<Borel set>, beginning with <open sets>.

Fix a star-open family $O$ and a basic <neighbourhood> $[s,A]$. An infinite reservoir $T$ above a finite stem $t$ accepts $t$ if $[t,T]\subseteq O$. It rejects $t$ if no infinite subset of $T$ accepts $t$. A reservoir decides $t$ if it accepts or rejects it. Any reservoir has a refinement deciding a prescribed stem: take an accepting refinement if there is one, and otherwise the current reservoir rejects. Both kinds of decision persist under infinite thinning.

First refine $A$ to decide $s$. If the decision is acceptance, the required homogeneous <neighbourhood> has already been found. Suppose it is rejection. We use <deciding all finite stems by fusion> to select
$$
b_1<b_2<\cdots
$$
from nested infinite reservoirs. After selecting $b_n$, successively thin the remaining reservoir to decide every stem $s\cup a$ with $a\subseteq\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}$. There are only finitely many such $a$, so every stage can be completed. Choose $b_{n+1}$ from that thinned reservoir and repeat. Let $B=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots\}$. For every finite $a\subseteq B$, the tail
$$
B/a=\{b\in B:b>\max a\}
$$
decides $s\cup a$; for $a=\varnothing$, use all of $B$. Indeed, the stem was decided at the stage when its largest point was selected, and its final tail is a subset of the reservoir retained at that stage. The empty stem remains rejected by the initial decision.

The crucial finite-obstruction fact is that a rejected stem has only finitely many accepting one-point extensions. More precisely, suppose $B/a$ rejects $s\cup a$. If infinitely many $b\in B/a$ had $B/b$ accepting $s\cup a\cup\{b\}$, collect those $b$ into an infinite $C$. Any $X\in[C]^\omega$ has a least point $b$, and its remaining tail lies in $B/b$. Acceptance at $b$ would then imply $s\cup a\cup X\in O$. Thus $C$ would accept $s\cup a$, contradicting rejection. This proves the <finitely many accepting extensions of a rejected stem> assertion with the reservoirs specified.

Thin $B$ a second time. Having selected a finite prefix of $C$, maintain that every stem $s\cup a$ with $a$ a subset of that prefix is rejected relative to its tail in $B$. Each such stem has finitely many forbidden next points leading to acceptance. Choose the next point of $C$ above the prefix and outside the union of these finitely many finite forbidden sets. The deciding property of $B$ then makes every newly created stem rejected. Starting with the rejected empty extension gives an infinite $C\subseteq B$ all of whose finite extensions of $s$ are rejected.

We claim $[s,C]\cap O=\varnothing$. Otherwise take $Y\in[s,C]\cap O$. Star-openness supplies a basic <neighbourhood> inside $O$ containing $Y$. Extend its finite stem along $Y$ until it contains $s$; the new stem has the form $s\cup a$ for a finite initial segment $a$ of $Y\setminus s$. The remaining infinite tail of $Y$ is a subset of $B/a$ and accepts that stem, contradicting rejection. This proves the <fusion proof for open Ellentuck sets>: **every star-<open set> is completely Ramsey**.

Next call $N$ <completely Ramsey-null> if every $[s,A]$ has a same-stem refinement disjoint from $N$. Every star-<nowhere dense set> is completely Ramsey-null. Indeed, the complement $D$ of its star-closure is star-open and <dense>. Apply the just-proved open-set result inside $[s,A]$. The homogeneous alternative disjoint from $D$ would be a nonempty <open set> contained in the closure of a nowhere-dense set, which is impossible. Hence some $[s,B]$ lies entirely in $D$ and avoids $N$.

Countable unions of completely Ramsey-null sets are again completely Ramsey-null. Here is the full <Ellentuck meagre-set fusion lemma>. Given $N_1,N_2,\ldots$ and $[s,A]$, construct selected prefixes $P_j=\{b_1,\ldots,b_j\}$ and nested unused infinite reservoirs. At stage $j$, refine the reservoir, still lying above $\max P_j$, to arrange
$$
[s\cup a,T_j]\cap N_i=\varnothing\qquad
\text{for every }a\subseteq P_j\text{ and every }1\le i\le j+1.
$$
Each constraint can be imposed by complete Ramsey-nullness, with all earlier constraints preserved by thinning; there are finitely many constraints. Choose $b_{j+1}\in T_j$ and retain the tail above it for the next stage. Start with $P_0=\varnothing$ and the reservoir $A$.

Let $B=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots\}$. If $Y\in[s,B]$ and $i$ is fixed, take any $j\ge i-1$ and put $a=(Y\setminus s)\cap P_j$. Every remaining point of $Y\setminus s$ lies in $T_j$, so $Y\in[s\cup a,T_j]$ and $Y\notin N_i$. Therefore
$$
[s,B]\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty N_i=\varnothing.
$$
This construction handles all subsets of a selected prefix, not merely the full prefix: members of $[s,B]$ may skip arbitrarily many selected points. Since a <meagre set> is a countable union of nowhere-dense sets, **every star-<meagre set> is completely Ramsey-null**.

It remains to pass from star-<open sets> to star-<Borel sets>. Let $\mathcal C$ consist of the families $E$ expressible as
$$
E\triangle O\subseteq M,
$$
where $O$ is star-open and $M$ is star-meagre. We verify directly that $\mathcal C$ is a <sigma-algebra>. It contains every <open set>. Countable unions remain in $\mathcal C$, because
$$
\left(\bigcup_i E_i\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup_i O_i\right)
\subseteq\bigcup_i(E_i\triangle O_i),
$$
and a countable union of <meagre sets> is meagre. For complements, replace $O^c$ by the <open set> $\operatorname{int}(O^c)$. The omitted part is $\overline O\setminus O$, the boundary of the <open set> $O$, which is closed and <nowhere dense>: a nonempty open subset of $\overline O$ must meet $O$, so it cannot lie in this boundary. Thus
$$
E^c\triangle\operatorname{int}(O^c)\subseteq M\cup(\overline O\setminus O),
$$
a <meagre set>. Subsets of <meagre sets> are meagre as well, since taking a subset preserves nowhere denseness. These observations prove the claimed <sigma-algebra> property. Hence every star-<Borel set> belongs to $\mathcal C$.

Finally, for $E\triangle O\subseteq M$ and any $[s,A]$, first use complete Ramsey-nullness to obtain $[s,B]$ avoiding $M$. On that neighbourhood $E$ and $O$ coincide. Apply the open-set result to $O$ within $[s,B]$ to get a homogeneous $[s,C]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Every star-Borel family is completely Ramsey, and hence Ramsey.}}
$$
Taking $s=\varnothing$ gives the requested homogeneous infinite-subset cone inside any given infinite ground set. Every closure, interior, <open set> and meagreness used in this proof refers to the <Ellentuck topology>; substituting the coarser ordinary topology at an intermediate step would change the assertion.
