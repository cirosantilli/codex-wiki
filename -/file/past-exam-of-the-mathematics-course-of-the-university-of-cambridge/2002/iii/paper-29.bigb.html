Normalize the riskless <financial asset> to $B_0=1$, $B_1=b>0$, and write $S_0,S_1$ for the $s$ risky asset prices. Their discounted gain <random vector> is $Y=S_1/b-S_0$. A zero-cost <portfolio> holding $h\in\mathbb R^s$ in the risky assets and $-h\cdot S_0$ in the riskless asset has terminal value $b\,h\cdot Y$. Thus an <arbitrage> means
$$
h\cdot Y\geq0\quad\text{almost surely},\qquad \mathbb P(h\cdot Y>0)>0.
$$
An <equivalent martingale measure> is a <probability measure> $Q$ with the same <null sets> as $P$, under which $Y$ is <integrable> and $\mathbb E_QY=0$. The discounted risky prices are then <martingales> over the single trading period.

If such $Q$ exists, a nonnegative terminal gain has <expectation> zero under $Q$. A nonnegative <random variable> with zero <expectation> vanishes <almost surely>: on $\{X\geq1/n\}$ its <expectation> is at least $\mathbb P(X\geq1/n)/n$, and the union of these events is $\{X>0\}$. Equivalence transfers this conclusion to $P$, excluding <arbitrage>.

For the converse we prove the needed <positive-density separation proof of the one-period asset-pricing theorem>. First make the gain <integrable> without changing its <null sets>. Define an <equivalent probability measure> $P_0$ by
$$
\frac{dP_0}{dP}=\frac{k}{1+\|Y\|},\qquad
k^{-1}=\mathbb E_P\frac1{1+\|Y\|}.
$$
Let $N=\{h:h\cdot Y=0\text{ almost surely}\}$ and let $L=N^\perp$ be its <orthogonal complement>. Taking a finite <basis> of $N$ shows $Y\in L$ <almost surely>. If $L=\{0\}$, the gain is zero and $P_0$ already gives the required measure. Otherwise consider the <positive-weight expectation cone>
$$
C=\{\mathbb E_{P_0}[ZY]: Z\text{ bounded and }Z>0\text{ almost surely}\}\subset L.
$$
Positive combinations of weights show that $C$ is a <convex cone>. It is open relative to $L$. Indeed, for any admissible weight $Z$, perturb it to
$$
Z_\varepsilon=Z\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon\cdot Y}{1+\|Y\|}\right),\qquad \varepsilon\in L,\quad \|\varepsilon\|<1.
$$
These weights remain bounded and strictly positive, while
$$
\mathbb E_{P_0}[Z_\varepsilon Y]=\mathbb E_{P_0}[ZY]+A_Z\varepsilon,
\qquad A_Z=\mathbb E_{P_0}\frac{ZYY^T}{1+\|Y\|}.
$$
For every nonzero $v\in L$, $v\cdot Y$ is nonzero on an event of positive <probability>; otherwise $v\in N\cap L$. Consequently
$$
v^TA_Zv=\mathbb E_{P_0}\frac{Z(v\cdot Y)^2}{1+\|Y\|}>0.
$$
The <matrix> is finite because $\|Y\|^2/(1+\|Y\|)\leq\|Y\|$. The <kernel> of $A_Z$ on $L$ is zero, so it defines an <invertible matrix> on the <finite-dimensional vector space> $L$. For any sufficiently small $\delta>0$, every $y$ with $\|y\|<\delta/\|A_Z^{-1}\|$ is the image of a vector in the radius-$\delta$ ball, since $\|A_Z^{-1}y\|<\delta$. Hence the perturbations give a <neighborhood> of zero, proving relative openness of $C$.

If $0\notin C$, the <hyperplane separation theorem> gives a nonzero $h\in L$ with $h\cdot c\geq0$ for every $c\in C$. The separating constant can be taken to be zero because $C$ is a cone. Put $A=\{h\cdot Y<0\}$ and use the strictly positive weights $Z=\mathbf1_A+\varepsilon$. Taking the <limit> as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ yields $\mathbb E_{P_0}[(h\cdot Y)\mathbf1_A]\geq0$, forcing $P_0(A)=0$. But $h\notin N$, so $P_0(h\cdot Y>0)>0$. This is an <arbitrage>, a contradiction. Hence $0\in C$: some bounded strictly positive $Z$ satisfies $\mathbb E_{P_0}[ZY]=0$. Normalize it by $dQ/dP_0=Z/\mathbb E_{P_0}Z$. This gives an <equivalent martingale measure> with <integrable> gains, establishing the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing> here without any further existence theorem.

Now retain only the $m$ positive-probability states of the finite <probability space>. Let $V\subseteq\mathbb R^m$ be the <linear span> of the discounted terminal asset payoffs; it contains the constant vector $\mathbf1$. A <complete market> means $V=\mathbb R^m$. An <equivalent martingale measure> is a vector $q$ of strictly positive state probabilities whose pairing with each asset payoff gives its initial price. By linearity it prices every attainable payoff. If $V=\mathbb R^m$, the <indicator functions> of individual states are attainable, so these prices determine every $q_j$ uniquely.

If $V$ is proper, choose $0\ne v\in V^\perp$. Since $\mathbf1\in V$, $\sum_jv_j=0$. For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, both $q+\varepsilon v$ and $q-\varepsilon v$ have strictly positive entries, sum to one, and give the same pairings with every asset payoff. They are distinct <equivalent martingale measures>. Therefore <market completeness> is equivalent to uniqueness of the <equivalent martingale measure>.

Take bank-account values $B_k=R^k$ with $R>0$, and let the <stock> multiply independently by $u$ or $d$ at each step, where $0<d<R<u$. Under the physical <probability measure> $P$, the up probability is $p\in(0,1)$; the <filtration> records the successive moves. These assumptions specify the standard <binomial market>. If $R$ lies outside the strict interval $(d,u)$, a long or short stock position financed through the <bank account> gives an <arbitrage>.

The <risk-neutral probability in a binomial market> is fixed by $qu+(1-q)d=R$, giving
$$
\boxed{q=\frac{R-d}{u-d}\in(0,1).}
$$
For successor claim values $V_u,V_d$ at a node with current stock price $S$, solve the two replication equations. The current stock holding and current cash value are
$$
\Delta=\frac{V_u-V_d}{S(u-d)},\qquad
C=\frac{uV_d-dV_u}{R(u-d)}.
$$
The cost of this <replicating portfolio in a binomial market> is
$$
\Delta S+C=\frac{qV_u+(1-q)V_d}{R}.
$$
Apply this <backward option pricing> recursion from any terminal <contingent claim> $H$, including claims depending on the whole sequence of moves. It constructs a <self-financing portfolio> and proves <market completeness> by induction on the remaining number of periods. Taking successive <conditional expectations> under the measure $Q$ with independent up probability $q$ gives the unique <risk-neutral pricing> value
$$
V_k=R^{-(n-k)}\mathbb E_Q[H\mid\mathcal F_k].
$$
Any other price would give an <arbitrage> by buying the cheaper of the claim and its replica and selling the dearer.

If $K$ is the number of up moves, a particular path has probabilities $p^K(1-p)^{n-K}$ under $P$ and $q^K(1-q)^{n-K}$ under $Q$. Therefore the <binomial-market probability density>, namely the <Radon-Nikodym derivative>, is
$$
\boxed{Z=\frac{dQ}{dP}=\left(\frac qp\right)^K\left(\frac{1-q}{1-p}\right)^{n-K},\qquad
K=\frac{\log(S_n/(S_0d^n))}{\log(u/d)}.}
$$
This last expression makes $Z$ a function of the terminal <stock> price. Summing over paths gives $\mathbb E_PZ=1$, and strict positivity proves equivalence of the two <probability measures>.

For unrestricted terminal <portfolio wealth> $H$, its initial replication cost is $R^{-n}\mathbb E_QH$. Hence feasible wealth satisfies the <state-price budget constraint> $\mathbb E_QH=R^nw_0$. For the stated <exponential utility>, $v'(x)=e^{-ax}$ and $v''(x)=-ae^{-ax}<0$. The <binomial exponential-utility terminal wealth> is
$$
\boxed{H^*=R^nw_0+\frac{\mathbb E_Q\log Z-\log Z}{a},}
$$
where
$$
\mathbb E_Q\log Z
=n\left[q\log\frac qp+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-p}\right].
$$
It satisfies the budget, and its <marginal utility> is $v'(H^*)=\lambda Z$ with $\lambda=\exp(-aR^nw_0-\mathbb E_Q\log Z)>0$. The <concave supporting-tangent inequality> gives
$$
v(H)\leq v(H^*)+\lambda Z(H-H^*).
$$
Taking <expectations> under $P$ makes the last term zero by the budget. Strict <concavity> makes equality possible only when $H=H^*$ in every positive-probability state. The already constructed <complete market> supplies its <replicating strategy>, so this proves global optimality and uniqueness. The formula allows negative final wealth, as permitted by the unrestricted trading problem.

Let $\phi_T(y)=(2\pi T)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/(2T)}$ be the <normal distribution> density. The <Brownian reflection principle> gives the killed endpoint density
$$
\mathbb P(W_T\in dy,\ \sup_{s\leq T}W_s<a)
=[\phi_T(y)-\phi_T(y-2a)]\,dy,\qquad y<a.
$$
Indeed, reflection after the first hit of $a$ maps paths ending below $a$ that have hit the barrier to paths ending at $2a-y$; symmetry gives $\phi_T(2a-y)=\phi_T(y-2a)$.

By the <Girsanov theorem>, weighting the path law by $\exp(\nu W_T-\nu^2T/2)$ makes the coordinate process a <Brownian motion with drift> $\nu$. Completing the two squares yields
$$
e^{\nu y-\nu^2T/2}[\phi_T(y)-\phi_T(y-2a)]
=\phi_T(y-\nu T)-e^{2a\nu}\phi_T(y-2a-\nu T).
$$
Integration up to $x\leq a$ proves the <joint endpoint and maximum law for drifted Brownian motion>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(W_T^\nu\leq x,M_T^\nu<a)
=\Phi\left(\frac{x-\nu T}{\sqrt T}\right)
-e^{2a\nu}\Phi\left(\frac{x-2a-\nu T}{\sqrt T}\right).}
$$
Here $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution function>. Setting $x=a$ also gives a continuous barrier distribution, so equality at the barrier has zero <probability>.

For the <up-and-in option>, use the <risk-neutral measure> of the <Black-Scholes model> with <interest rate> $\rho$ and <spot volatility> $\sigma>0$. Then
$$
S_t=S_0e^{\sigma(W_t+\nu t)},\qquad
\nu=\frac{\rho-\sigma^2/2}{\sigma},\qquad
a=\frac{\log(b/S_0)}{\sigma}>0.
$$
Above $a$, the barrier has certainly been reached. Below $a$, subtracting the killed density from the unrestricted endpoint density leaves $e^{2a\nu}\phi_T(y-2a-\nu T)$. Thus the discounted <expected value> of its payoff is
$$
e^{-\rho T}\left[\int_a^\infty f(S_0e^{\sigma y})\phi_T(y-\nu T)\,dy
+e^{2a\nu}\int_{-\infty}^a f(S_0e^{\sigma y})\phi_T(y-2a-\nu T)\,dy\right].
$$
In the second integral put $z=y-2a$, and define
$$
\boxed{\kappa=e^{-2\sigma a}=\left(\frac{S_0}{b}\right)^2.}
$$
Then $S_0e^{\sigma y}=S_0e^{\sigma z}/\kappa$, $z\leq-a$ means $S_0e^{\sigma z}\leq\kappa b$, and $e^{2a\nu}=\kappa^{-\nu/\sigma}$. Both integrals now use the unrestricted terminal <stock> distribution. This proves that the <reflected European payoff for an up-and-in option> is
$$
\boxed{g(x)=f(x)\mathbf1_{\{x>b\}}+\kappa^{-\nu/\sigma}f(x/\kappa)\mathbf1_{\{x\leq\kappa b\}}.}
$$
Their initial <risk-neutral pricing> values agree for payoffs with finite absolute expectations in these integrals. The identity concerns prices; the two terminal payoffs need not agree along individual paths.

Write $T=t_0$, $B_t=e^{-\rho(T-t)}$, and let $V_t=f(S_t,t)$ with $f(x,t)=xg(x,t)+B_th(x,t)$. The <portfolio> holds $g(S_t,t)$ shares of <stock> and $h(S_t,t)$ units of the <zero-coupon bond>. It is <self-financing> when all changes of holdings are funded within it, which is the gain identity
$$
dV_t=g(S_t,t)\,dS_t+h(S_t,t)\,dB_t.
$$
Under the physical <probability measure>, write $dS_t=\mu S_tdt+\sigma S_tdW_t$, where $\sigma>0$ and $dB_t=\rho B_tdt$. The <Itô formula> gives
$$
df(S_t,t)=\left(f_t+\mu S_tf_x+\frac12\sigma^2S_t^2f_{xx}\right)dt
+\sigma S_tf_x\,dW_t.
$$
Matching the <Brownian motion> coefficients forces $f_x=g$. Since $f_x=g+xg_x+B_th_x$, this is precisely
$$
xg_x+B_th_x=0.
$$
Differentiating $f_x=g$ gives $f_{xx}=g_x$. Since $f_t=xg_t+\rho B_th+B_th_t$, matching the <drift> coefficients now gives
$$
xg_t+B_th_t+\frac12\sigma^2x^2g_x=0.
$$
These are the <self-financing conditions for smooth stock and bond holdings>. Conversely, substituting both identities into the <Itô formula> gives the gain identity, so they are sufficient as well. Smoothness and the positive <lognormal distribution> density of $S_t$ extend the identities along the price process to every $x>0$, with boundary times obtained by continuity.

The <portfolio with constant stock value in bond units> must have $xg(x,t)=\theta B_t$, so $g(x,t)=\theta B_t/x$. The first condition then gives $h_x=\theta/x$, hence
$$
h(x,t)=\theta\log(x/S_0)+c(t).
$$
The second condition reduces to $c'(t)=\theta(\sigma^2/2-\rho)$. Initial <portfolio wealth> requires $h(S_0,0)=w_0/B_0-\theta=w_0e^{\rho T}-\theta$. Therefore the holdings are
$$
\boxed{g(x,t)=\frac{\theta e^{-\rho(T-t)}}x,\qquad
h(x,t)=w_0e^{\rho T}-\theta+\theta\log(x/S_0)+\theta(\sigma^2/2-\rho)t.}
$$
The corresponding <portfolio wealth> is
$$
\boxed{V_t=e^{-\rho(T-t)}\left[w_0e^{\rho T}+\theta\log(S_t/S_0)+\theta(\sigma^2/2-\rho)t\right].}
$$
Under the <risk-neutral measure>, $\log(S_t/S_0)=(\rho-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma W_t^Q$, so its value in <zero-coupon bond> units is $V_t/B_t=w_0e^{\rho T}+\theta\sigma W_t^Q$. This also verifies the <self-financing> gain equation after using the bond as <numéraire>.

Let $T=t_0$, $\tau=T-t$, and let $N$ have the <standard normal distribution>. For a twice <differentiable> payoff with enough growth control for the following <expectations> and derivatives, <risk-neutral pricing> in the dividend-free <Black-Scholes model> gives
$$
\boxed{V(x,t)=e^{-\rho\tau}\mathbb E[f(xM)],\qquad
M=e^{(\rho-\sigma^2/2)\tau+\sigma\sqrt\tau N}.}
$$
The conditional discounted payoff is a <martingale> under the <risk-neutral measure>. Applying the <Itô formula> to $e^{-\rho s}V(S_s,s)$ and setting its <drift> to zero therefore gives the <Black-Scholes equation>
$$
\boxed{V_t+\frac12\sigma^2x^2V_{xx}+\rho xV_x-\rho V=0,\qquad V(x,T)=f(x).}
$$
The <delta hedge> holds $\Delta=V_x$ shares; the remaining bond value is $\beta=V-x\Delta$, or $h=\beta/e^{-\rho(T-t)}$ units of the <zero-coupon bond>. Substitution in the <Itô formula> and the displayed equation gives $dV=\Delta\,dS+h\,dB$, proving replication and <self-financing>.

Differentiating the pricing expectation in the current <stock> price gives its <option delta> and <option gamma>:
$$
\Delta=e^{-\rho\tau}\mathbb E[f'(xM)M],\qquad
\Gamma=e^{-\rho\tau}\mathbb E[f''(xM)M^2].
$$
Thus a nondecreasing payoff has a nondecreasing price; a nonincreasing payoff has a nonincreasing price. A <convex> payoff has $\Gamma\geq0$, and a <concave> payoff has $\Gamma\leq0$. This is <convexity preservation in Black-Scholes pricing>.

The <Black-Scholes parameter sensitivities> follow directly from the same expectation. Differentiation in the <interest rate> gives the <option rho>
$$
\boxed{V_\rho=\tau(x\Delta-V)=-\tau\beta.}
$$
For <spot volatility>, differentiation first gives
$$
V_\sigma=e^{-\rho\tau}\mathbb E[f'(xM)xM(\sqrt\tau N-\sigma\tau)].
$$
The <Gaussian integration by parts> identity $\mathbb E[N\psi(N)]=\mathbb E[\psi'(N)]$, applied to $\psi(N)=f'(xM)xM$, cancels the term containing $f'$ and gives the <option vega>
$$
\boxed{V_\sigma=\sigma\tau x^2\Gamma.}
$$
Consequently increasing <spot volatility> increases a <convex> payoff's value and decreases a <concave> payoff's value, with weak inequalities in affine or degenerate cases. A short bond position gives positive <option rho>, while a long bond position gives negative <option rho>. The physical <stock> <drift> $\mu$ does not enter these prices: the <risk-neutral measure> replaces it by $\rho$.

The <option theta> is the calendar-time derivative at fixed current <stock> price. The <Black-Scholes equation> gives
$$
\boxed{V_t=\rho\beta-\frac12\sigma^2x^2\Gamma.}
$$
For $\rho\geq0$, a <convex> payoff with $\beta<0$ therefore has $V_t\leq0$, while a <concave> payoff with $\beta>0$ has $V_t\geq0$. The signs are strict when $\rho>0$. At $\rho=0$, an affine payoff can give equality. Remaining-maturity sensitivity is $V_T=-V_t$ when the payoff is independent of $T$. All these time comparisons hold at fixed current <stock> price.

The stated time-monotonicity conclusion needs the nonnegative-rate condition. For example, the <convex> affine payoff $f(x)=x-K$ has
$$
V=x-Ke^{-\rho(T-t)},\qquad \beta=-Ke^{-\rho(T-t)}<0,\qquad
V_t=-\rho Ke^{-\rho(T-t)}>0\quad(\rho<0).
$$
Thus with negative <interest rates> it provides an explicit counterexample even though the replica is short in bonds.

Use $T$ for the fixed maturity and $s\leq T$ for the observation time. The original PDF's discounting uses the <instantaneous forward rate> over $[s,T]$ and the <short rate> over $[0,s]$. Let $X(s,u)=F_{s,u}-\mu_{s,u}$ be the centered <Gaussian forward-rate field>, and define
$$
A(s,T)=\int_0^s\mu_{u,u}\,du+\int_s^T\mu_{s,u}\,du,\qquad
Y(s,T)=\int_0^T X(s\wedge u,u)\,du.
$$
Assume the <covariance> and mean have the regularity needed for these <mean-square integrals> and the maturity differentiation below. Splitting $Y$ at $u=s$ shows that the discounted <zero-coupon bond> price is
$$
Z_{s,T}=\exp[-A(s,T)-Y(s,T)].
$$
This <integrated Gaussian forward-rate process> has deterministic <variance>
$$
v(s,T)=\int_0^T\int_0^T c(s\wedge u\wedge w,u,w)\,du\,dw.
$$
Its <independent increments> hold relative to the entire specified <filtration>. Indeed, if $r\leq s$ and $z\leq r$, the <covariance> between $Y(s,T)-Y(r,T)$ and any earlier $X(z,w)$ is the integral of
$$
c((s\wedge u)\wedge z,u,w)-c((r\wedge u)\wedge z,u,w)=0.
$$
Since <uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent>, the increment is independent of every finite family of earlier observations and hence of their generated <sigma-algebra>. The increment has <normal distribution> with mean zero and variance $v(s,T)-v(r,T)$. Also $Y(0,T)=0$ because $c(0,u,w)=0$.

The <Gaussian moment-generating function> now gives
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{s,T}\mid\mathcal F_r]
=\exp[-A(s,T)-Y(r,T)+\tfrac12(v(s,T)-v(r,T))].
$$
Therefore the <Gaussian forward-rate covariance drift restriction> is exactly the deterministic condition
$$
\boxed{A(s,T)-A(0,T)=\frac12v(s,T).}
$$
It turns $Z_{s,T}/Z_{0,T}$ into a <Gaussian exponential martingale with deterministic variance>. The subparts establish the requested equivalent descriptions from this common calculation.

If the discounted <zero-coupon bond> price is a <martingale>, its <expectation> is $Z_{0,T}=e^{-A(0,T)}$. The <Gaussian moment-generating function> applied to the representation above gives $\mathbb E Z_{s,T}=e^{-A(s,T)+v(s,T)/2}$, so
$$
A(s,T)-A(0,T)=\frac12v(s,T).
$$
Differentiate in maturity $T\geq s$. The two mean derivatives are $\mu_{s,T}$ and $\mu_{0,T}$. Symmetry of the <covariance> in its last two arguments makes the two boundary terms in the derivative of the double integral equal. Hence
$$
\frac12\partial_Tv(s,T)=\int_0^T c(s\wedge u,u,T)\,du.
$$
We obtain the <Gaussian forward-rate covariance drift restriction>
$$
\boxed{\mu_{s,T}=\mu_{0,T}+\int_0^T c(s\wedge u,u,T)\,du,}
$$
which proves that the <martingale> condition implies the mean restriction. Only maturity differentiation is used; a time derivative of the <covariance> is unnecessary.

Assume the displayed <Gaussian forward-rate covariance drift restriction>. Its diagonal instance gives
$$
\mu_{u,u}-\mu_{0,u}=\int_0^u c(w,w,u)\,dw.
$$
Using it on the two parts of the deterministic discounted exponent yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
A(s,T)-A(0,T)
&=\int_0^s\int_0^u c(w,w,u)\,dw\,du
+\int_s^T\int_0^u c(s\wedge w,w,u)\,dw\,du\\
&=\int_0^T\int_0^u c(s\wedge w,w,u)\,dw\,du\\
&=\frac12\int_0^T\int_0^T c(s\wedge u\wedge w,u,w)\,du\,dw
=\frac12v(s,T).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality uses <covariance> symmetry to reflect the triangular integration region across its diagonal. The <independent increments> of the <integrated Gaussian forward-rate process> and its conditional <Gaussian moment-generating function> then give, for $r\leq s\leq T$,
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{s,T}\mid\mathcal F_r]
=e^{-A(s,T)-Y(r,T)+(v(s,T)-v(r,T))/2}
=e^{-A(r,T)-Y(r,T)}=Z_{r,T}.
$$
All these prices are <integrable> because their exponents are <Gaussian random variables> with finite <variance>. Thus the mean restriction implies the discounted bond <martingale> condition, completing the equivalence of the first two descriptions.

Let $D_s=\exp(-\int_0^sR_u\,du)$, where $R_u=F_{u,u}$ is the <short rate>. At maturity the <zero-coupon bond> pays one, so $Z_{T,T}=D_T$. If the discounted bond price is a <martingale>, its terminal <conditional expectation> is
$$
D_sP_{s,T}=Z_{s,T}=\mathbb E[D_T\mid\mathcal F_s].
$$
The positive factor $D_s$ is measurable with respect to the current <filtration>. Dividing it out gives
$$
\boxed{P_{s,T}=\mathbb E\left[\exp\left(-\int_s^TR_u\,du\right)\middle|\mathcal F_s\right].}
$$
Conversely, this identity says $Z_{s,T}=\mathbb E[D_T\mid\mathcal F_s]$. The <tower property> gives $\mathbb E[Z_{s,T}\mid\mathcal F_r]=Z_{r,T}$ for every $r\leq s$, establishing the <martingale> condition. The integrated <short rate> is a <Gaussian random variable> with finite <variance>, so the <Gaussian moment-generating function> proves that $D_T$ is <integrable>. Together with the preceding subparts, this proves all three descriptions equivalent.
