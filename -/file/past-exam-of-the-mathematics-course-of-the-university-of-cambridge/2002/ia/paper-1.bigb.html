For a finite <group> $G$ acting on a <set> $X$, write $G_x=\{g\in G:gx=x\}$ for the <stabilizer subgroup> and $Gx=\{gx:g\in G\}$ for the <orbit> of $x$. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{|G|=|G_x|\,|Gx|.}
$$
Indeed, $gG_x\mapsto gx$ is a <bijection> from the left <cosets> of $G_x$ onto $Gx$: two images agree precisely when $h^{-1}g\in G_x$. Each <coset> has $|G_x|$ elements, proving the formula.

Place the cube vertices at $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$. The four vertices whose coordinate product is $+1$ form $T$; those with product $-1$ form $T'$. Within either <set>, two distinct vertices differ in exactly two coordinates, so every edge has length $2\sqrt2$ and each <set> is a <regular tetrahedron>.

A member of the <cube rotation group> is a <signed permutation matrix> of <determinant> one. There are $3!$ permutations of the coordinate axes and four choices of signs for each permutation that give <determinant> one, so $|G|=24$. Such a <matrix> maps a coordinate-product class to a coordinate-product class. Moreover, the <rotation> through a quarter-turn $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-y,x,z)$ interchanges the classes. Thus the <orbit> of $T$ consists of exactly $T,T'$. By the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>, the <tetrahedron stabilizer in the cube rotation group> has order
$$
\boxed{|G_T|=24/2=12.}
$$

The <fundamental theorem of algebra> says that every nonconstant <polynomial> with <complex number> coefficients has a root in the <complex numbers>. Repeated <polynomial division> by a factor of degree one therefore factors a degree-three <polynomial> with <complex number> coefficients into three factors of degree one, with <algebraic multiplicities> included.

For a <matrix> with <complex number> entries $A$ of size three, its <characteristic polynomial> is $\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)$, and its characteristic equation is $\chi_A(t)=0$. A <complex number> $\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue> exactly when $\lambda I-A$ is singular, equivalently when its <null space> contains a nonzero <vector>. Thus the three roots of the <characteristic polynomial> are the three <eigenvalues> counted with <algebraic multiplicity>.

For the specified <matrix>, expansion along the first row gives
$$
\chi_A(t)=(t-1)\det\begin{pmatrix}t&-i\\i&t\end{pmatrix}
=(t-1)(t^2-1)=(t-1)^2(t+1).
$$
Hence **the <eigenvalues> are $1,1,-1$**. Corresponding <eigenvectors> are
$$
v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v_2=\begin{pmatrix}0\\i\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v_3=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-i\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad Av_1=v_1,\quad Av_2=v_2,\quad Av_3=-v_3.
$$
The <matrix> with these three columns has <determinant> $2i\ne0$, so these <eigenvectors> are <linearly independent>. A repeated <eigenvalue> does not in general guarantee two independent <eigenvectors>; here they have been exhibited directly.

The <limit of a sequence> of <real numbers> is $a$ if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is an <integer> $N$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $|a_n-a|<\varepsilon$. It tends to positive infinity if, for every real $M$, there is an <integer> $N$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $a_n>M$.

If $a_n\to+\infty$, choose the latter threshold with $M=1/\varepsilon$. Then $a_n>1/\varepsilon>0$ eventually, so $|1/a_n|<\varepsilon$. Thus $1/a_n\to0$. **The converse is false:** $a_n=-n$ has reciprocal tending to zero and tends to negative infinity. The precise sign-free statement is given by <reciprocal limits and escape in absolute value>: $1/a_n\to0$ is equivalent to $|a_n|\to\infty$.

If $a_n\to a\ne0$, eventually $|a_n-a|<|a|/2$, and the <triangle inequality> gives $|a_n|>|a|/2$. Consequently
$$
\left|\frac1{a_n}-\frac1a\right|
=\frac{|a_n-a|}{|a_n||a|}\le\frac{2|a_n-a|}{|a|^2}.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, take $N$ large enough that $|a_n-a|<\min(|a|/2,\varepsilon|a|^2/2)$ for all $n\ge N$. This proves **$1/a_n\to1/a$** directly from the definition of the <limit of a sequence>.

Here is a nested-interval proof of the <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem>. Put every term of the bounded real <sequence> in a <closed interval> $I_0=[-M,M]$, with $M>0$. Bisect this interval and choose a closed half containing infinitely many terms. Repeating gives nested <closed intervals> $I_j=[l_j,r_j]$, each containing infinitely many <sequence> terms, with length $2M/2^j$.

By the <least-upper-bound property>, $x_* =\sup_j l_j$ exists. For each $j$, every lower endpoint lies at most $r_j$, while $l_j\le x_*$; hence $x_*\in I_j$. Choose increasing indices $n_j$ with $a_{n_j}\in I_j$, possible because each interval contains infinitely many terms. Then
$$
|a_{n_j}-x_*|\le 2M/2^j\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus **every bounded real <sequence> has a <convergent subsequence>**.

For a <sequence> with no <convergent subsequence>, take $a_n=n$: any <subsequence> has $n_j\ge j$, so its terms tend to positive infinity rather than to a finite <limit of a sequence>. For an unbounded <sequence> with a <convergent subsequence>, take $a_{2n}=0$ and $a_{2n-1}=n$. Its odd terms are unbounded, while its even <subsequence> converges to zero.

Take the scalene triangle vertex <set> $T=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,2)\}$. Its edge lengths are $1,2,\sqrt5$. Each vertex has a different unordered pair of incident edge lengths. A <Euclidean isometry> preserving the <set> must preserve these pairs, and therefore fixes every vertex.

To justify <rigidity of a scalene triangle> fully, <Euclidean distances> from an arbitrary plane point to these three <fixed points> determine that point uniquely: subtracting its squared <Euclidean distance> to the origin from the other two squared <Euclidean distances> determines its two coordinates. Thus an <isometry> fixing the three vertices fixes every plane point. **Only the identity preserves $T$.**

For the general union of <group> translates, let $h\in G$. Left multiplication by $h$ permutes $G$, so
$$
h(S)=\bigcup_{g\in G}hg(T)=\bigcup_{k\in G}k(T)=S.
$$
Therefore **$G\subseteq H$**. This containment uses only the <group action>; the absence of symmetries of $T$ is not needed for the containment itself.

Write a <Möbius transformation> as $g(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$, with $ad-bc\ne0$, on the <Riemann sphere>. If $c\ne0$, infinity is not fixed, and its finite <fixed points> solve
$$
cz^2+(d-a)z-b=0.
$$
The pole $-d/c$ cannot solve this equation, since substitution gives $(ad-bc)/c\ne0$. The quadratic has one or two distinct complex roots, so these give exactly one or two <fixed points>.

If $c=0$, then $a,d\ne0$ and infinity is fixed. If $a\ne d$, there is one additional finite <fixed point>, namely $b/(d-a)$. If $a=d$ and $b\ne0$, the transformation is a nonzero <translation>, with no finite <fixed point>. If $a=d$ and $b=0$, it is the identity and fixes the entire sphere.

Thus **a nonidentity <Möbius transformation> has one or two <fixed points>; the identity has infinitely many**. The examples $z\mapsto z+1$, $z\mapsto2z$, and $z\mapsto z$ realize the three possibilities respectively.

A <vector space> over the <real numbers> is a <set> $V$ with an <addition> making it an <abelian group> and a <scalar multiplication> by $\mathbb R$ satisfying, for <scalars> $a,b$ and <vectors> $u,v$, the identities $a(u+v)=au+av$, $(a+b)v=av+bv$, $a(bv)=(ab)v$ and $1v=v$. A <vector subspace> is a nonempty subset closed under <addition> and <scalar multiplication>; a <proper vector subspace> is one unequal to $V$. A <spanning set> is a <set> whose finite <linear combinations> give every <vector> of $V$. A <basis> is a <linearly independent> <spanning set>. The <dimension> is the number of <vectors> in a <basis>, or its cardinality when the <basis> is infinite.

The <sum of vector subspaces> and their <intersection of vector subspaces> are
$$
U+W=\{u+w:u\in U,\ w\in W\},\qquad
U\cap W=\{v\in V:v\in U\text{ and }v\in W\}.
$$
Both are <vector subspaces>: <addition> and <scalar multiplication> preserve the displayed forms or simultaneous membership. The <intersection of vector subspaces> is never empty because **$0\in U\cap W$**.

For the two given <hyperplanes>, adding and subtracting their defining equations gives $x_1=x_2$ and $x_3=x_4$. Hence
$$
U\cap W=\{(a,a,b,b):a,b\in\mathbb R\}
=\operatorname{span}\{b_1,b_2\},\quad b_1=(1,1,0,0),\quad b_2=(0,0,1,1).
$$
These two nonzero <vectors> are <orthogonal>, and consequently form an <orthogonal basis> of the <intersection of vector subspaces>. Put $u=(1,-1,-1,1)$ and $w=(1,-1,1,-1)$. Direct substitution gives $u\in U$, $w\in W$. Their <dot products> with $b_1,b_2$ vanish, and $u\cdot w=0$. Each defining equation of $U$ or $W$ is one nonzero linear constraint in four variables, so each <vector subspace> has <dimension> three. Thus each space has the indicated <orthogonal basis>:
$$
\boxed{U:\ (b_1,b_2,u),\qquad W:\ (b_1,b_2,w),\qquad U+W:\ (b_1,b_2,u,w).}
$$
The last four <vectors> are nonzero and mutually <orthogonal>, so they are a <basis> of $\mathbb R^4$. They lie in $U+W$, proving $U+W=V$. The <dimension> identity is therefore verified numerically:
$$
\boxed{\dim U+\dim W=3+3=4+2=\dim(U+W)+\dim(U\cap W).}
$$

Use the <least-upper-bound property> as the least upper bound axiom: every nonempty <set> of <real numbers> bounded above has a least upper bound in $\mathbb R$. It implies convergence of a bounded increasing <sequence>: if $L$ is the <supremum> of its terms, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ some term exceeds $L-\varepsilon$, and all subsequent terms lie between that term and $L$. A bounded decreasing <sequence> converges by applying this argument to its negative.

The <alternating series test> states that if $b_n\ge0$, $b_{n+1}\le b_n$, and $b_n\to0$, then $\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}b_n$ converges. To prove it, let $S_N$ denote its <partial sums>. The even <partial sums> are increasing because $S_{2m+2}-S_{2m}=b_{2m+1}-b_{2m+2}\ge0$. The odd <partial sums> are decreasing because $S_{2m+3}-S_{2m+1}=-b_{2m+2}+b_{2m+3}\le0$. Moreover,
$$
0\le S_{2m}\le S_{2m+1}\le b_1.
$$
<Completeness of the real numbers> therefore gives a <limit of a sequence> for each subsequence, denoted $L_e,L_o$ respectively for the even and odd <subsequences>. Their difference is $S_{2m+1}-S_{2m}=b_{2m+1}\to0$, so $L_e=L_o$. Every sufficiently late <partial sum> belongs to one of these two <subsequences>, proving convergence of the full <sequence> of <partial sums>. Multiplying the terms by $-1$ leaves convergence unchanged.

Let $f$ be <continuous> on a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$. First prove that $f$ is a <bounded function>. If it failed, we could choose $x_n\in[a,b]$ with $|f(x_n)|>n$. The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> gives a <subsequence> $x_{n_j}\to x_*$. Because the interval is closed, $x_*\in[a,b]$. <Continuity> gives $f(x_{n_j})\to f(x_*)$, contradicting $|f(x_{n_j})|>n_j\to\infty$.

Now let $M=\sup\{f(x):x\in[a,b]\}$, which exists by <completeness of the real numbers> and the bound just proved. Choose $y_n\in[a,b]$ with $f(y_n)>M-1/n$. A <convergent subsequence> has <limit of a sequence> $y_*\in[a,b]$, and <continuity> gives $f(y_*)=M$. Applying the same argument to $-f$ gives attainment of the <infimum>. This proves the <extreme value theorem>: **the maximum and minimum both exist and are attained**. The argument also covers a one-point interval, where the conclusions are immediate.

= Solution

The <mean value theorem> states that, if $f$ is <continuous> on $[a,b]$ and <differentiable> on $(a,b)$, with $a<b$, there is $c\in(a,b)$ such that
$$
f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.
$$
To deduce it from <Rolle's theorem>, subtract the secant line: $k(t)=f(t)-f(a)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(t-a)$. This <function> has the same <continuity> and <differentiability> properties and satisfies $k(a)=k(b)=0$. <Rolle's theorem> gives an interior point with $k'(c)=0$, which is precisely the required equality.

If $h'=0$ on $\mathbb R$, apply the <mean value theorem> on any $[x,y]$ with $x<y$. It gives $h(y)-h(x)=(y-x)h'(c)=0$. Hence **$h$ is constant**.

For the <differential equation>, the <product rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(e^{-ax}f(x)\bigr)=e^{-ax}\bigl(f'(x)-af(x)\bigr)=0.
$$
The preceding result makes this product constant. Thus **all solutions are $f(x)=Ce^{ax}$**, and differentiation verifies every such solution.

For the <integral> assertion, <continuity> ensures that $f$ is <Riemann integrable> on every finite <closed interval>. Use the usual orientation for an <integral> with negative upper endpoint. For $h\ne0$, additivity of the <integral> gives
$$
\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}h-f(x)
=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}\bigl(f(t)-f(x)\bigr)\,dt.
$$
The <absolute value> of this expression is at most the <supremum> of $|f(t)-f(x)|$ on the segment joining $x$ and $x+h$. <Continuity> at $x$ makes this bound tend to zero for either sign of $h$. Consequently **$F'(x)=f(x)$**, proving this form of the <fundamental theorem of calculus>.

Finally, a <differentiable> solution of the <integral equation> is <continuous>, so the <fundamental theorem of calculus> yields $g'=g$; setting $x=0$ gives $g(0)=A$. The differential-equation calculation above now forces
$$
\boxed{g(x)=Ae^x.}
$$
Conversely, $A+\int_0^xAe^t\,dt=A+A(e^x-1)=Ae^x$, so this <function> satisfies the original <integral equation>. It is unique because every <differentiable> solution has already been forced to this form and this <initial condition>.

= Solution
{parent=12C}

For a <function> with <continuous> <derivatives> through order $m+1$ on the interval between $0$ and $x$, the <Taylor formula with integral remainder> is
$$
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{f^{(j)}(0)}{j!}x^j+R_m(x),\qquad
R_m(x)=\frac1{m!}\int_0^x(x-t)^m f^{(m+1)}(t)\,dt.
$$
This is <Taylor's theorem> about zero; <translation> gives the formula about any other point. To prove it, the case $m=0$ is the <fundamental theorem of calculus>. For $m\ge1$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
R_m(x)=-\frac{f^{(m)}(0)x^m}{m!}
+\frac1{(m-1)!}\int_0^x(x-t)^{m-1}f^{(m)}(t)\,dt
=R_{m-1}(x)-\frac{f^{(m)}(0)x^m}{m!}.
$$
Substitution into the formula at order $m-1$ proves the formula at order $m$ by induction. The <integral> estimate, valid also for negative $x$, is
$$
|R_m(x)|\le\frac{|x|^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}
\sup_{t\text{ between }0\text{ and }x}|f^{(m+1)}(t)|.
$$
For the <smooth function> satisfying $f^{(3)}=f$, each fixed <derivative> is <continuous> on $[-R,R]$ and hence is bounded there by the <extreme value theorem>. Thus a bound $M_j$ exists for every $j$. Differentiating the <differential equation> repeatedly gives $f^{(j+3)}=f^{(j)}$. Only the three <functions> $f,f',f''$ are needed: take $M$ to be the maximum of their absolute-value bounds on $[-R,R]$. Then $|f^{(j)}(x)|\le M$ simultaneously for every $j\ge0$ and $|x|\le R$.

At zero the <derivatives> consequently repeat the values $1,0,0$. <Taylor's theorem> therefore gives
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor m/3\rfloor}\frac{x^{3n}}{(3n)!}+R_m(x),\qquad
\sup_{|x|\le R}|R_m(x)|\le\frac{MR^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The last <limit of a sequence> follows because the ratio of consecutive majorants is $R/(m+2)$, eventually less than $1/2$. Since $R$ is arbitrary, <Taylor expansion from a periodic derivative equation> proves
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{3n}}{(3n)!}\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This proves convergence to the <function> using a remainder bound; it does not assume beforehand that a <smooth function> equals its <Taylor series>.
