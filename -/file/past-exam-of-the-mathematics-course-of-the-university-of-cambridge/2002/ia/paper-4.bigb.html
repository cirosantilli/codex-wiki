= Solution
{parent=1C}

An <injective function> satisfies $f(a)=f(a')\Rightarrow a=a'$ for all $a,a'\in A$. A <surjective function> $g:A\to B$ satisfies: for every $b\in B$, some $a\in A$ has $g(a)=b$. The following arguments apply to <function composition> with the stated domains and codomains.

= i
{parent=1c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $c\in C$. Since $g$ is <surjective>, choose $b\in B$ with $g(b)=c$. Since $f$ is <surjective>, choose $a\in A$ with $f(a)=b$. Then $(g\circ f)(a)=g(b)=c$. Thus every element of $C$ has a preimage, proving that $g\circ f$ is <surjective>.

= ii
{parent=1c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Suppose $(g\circ f)(a)=(g\circ f)(a')$. The <injectivity> of $g$ implies $f(a)=f(a')$, and the <injectivity> of $f$ then implies $a=a'$. Hence $g\circ f$ is <injective>.

= iii
{parent=1c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If $f(a)=f(a')$, applying $g$ gives $(g\circ f)(a)=(g\circ f)(a')$. The <injectivity> of $g\circ f$ implies $a=a'$, so $f$ is <injective>.

= iv
{parent=1c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For any $c\in C$, the <surjectivity> of $g\circ f$ supplies $a\in A$ with $g(f(a))=c$. Therefore $f(a)\in B$ is a preimage of $c$ under $g$, proving <surjectivity> of $g$.

For the requested counterexample, take $A=C=\{0\}$, $B=\{0,1\}$, $f(0)=0$, and $g(0)=g(1)=0$. The <function composition> is the identity <bijection> of the singleton, but $f$ is not <surjective> and $g$ is not <injective>.

= 2C
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Numbers and Sets}

= Solution
{parent=2C}

The base case $n=1$ is the <product rule>. Suppose the <Leibniz rule> holds at order $n$. Taking one more <derivative> of each term and collecting the coefficient of $f^{(n+1-r)}g^{(r)}$ gives
$$
(fg)^{(n+1)}=\sum_{r=0}^{n+1}\left[\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}\right]f^{(n+1-r)}g^{(r)}.
$$
Here <binomial coefficients> outside $0\le r\le n$ are zero. For $1\le r\le n$, the factorial definition proves <Pascal's identity> directly:
$$
\binom nr+\binom n{r-1}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}+\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!}=\frac{(n+1)!}{r!(n+1-r)!}=\binom{n+1}r.
$$
At $r=0,n+1$, the identity is $1+0=1$. Substitution gives the required order-$n+1$ formula, completing the <induction>.

= 3E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=3E}

Put $A=g\sin\alpha>0$ and $v=\dot x$. Multiplying the equation by $xv$ gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\frac{(xv)^2}2=Ax^2\dot x=\frac d{dt}\frac{Ax^3}3,
$$
so its <first integral> is
$$
\boxed{x^2v^2=\frac{2A}3x^3+c},\qquad v=\sqrt{\frac{2A}3x+\frac c{x^2}}.
$$
These level curves give the positive <phase plane> for the <avalanche front with linear mass entrainment>. For $c<0$, the curve starts at $v=0$, $x=(-3c/(2A))^{1/3}$ and rises. For $c>0$, it diverges as $x\downarrow0$, has a minimum at $x=(3c/A)^{1/3}$, and then rises. The $c=0$ curve is $v=\sqrt{2Ax/3}$. Forward-time arrows have increasing $x$; at a zero-velocity endpoint the equation gives $\ddot x=A>0$.

\Image[paper-4-avalanche-phase-plane.png]
{title=Avalanche phase trajectories approaching the linear-entrainment asymptote}

Every forward-moving trajectory has $x\to\infty$: after it has entered $v>0$, a bounded limiting $x$ would force a zero of the displayed positive-branch speed ahead of it, but its only possible zero is the lower endpoint. For large $x$,
$$
\frac{v}{\sqrt{2Ax/3}}=\sqrt{1+\frac{3c}{2Ax^3}}\longrightarrow1,\qquad v-\sqrt{2Ax/3}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Expanding the equation as $x\ddot x+v^2=Ax$ and substituting the <first integral> gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot x=\frac A3-\frac c{x^3}\longrightarrow\frac13g\sin\alpha}.
$$
Thus both the asymptotic <phase plane> trajectory and the limiting <acceleration> are independent of the initial data.

= 4E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=4E}

Apply the rotating-vector derivative rule first to the position <vector> and then to its <velocity>. Since $\boldsymbol\omega$ is constant,
$$
\mathbf v=\mathbf v'+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf a=\mathbf a'+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v'+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x).
$$
For example, the derivative of $\mathbf v'$ contributes $\mathbf a'+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v'$, while that of $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x$ contributes the other two terms. This is the <equation of motion in a rotating frame>. The mutual <central forces> are unchanged in form because rotations preserve lengths and differences of position vectors. <Newton's second law> for particle $i$ becomes
$$
m\mathbf a_i'=\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}+e(\mathbf v_i'+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x_i)\times\mathbf B-2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_i'-m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x_i).
$$
Set $\boldsymbol\omega=-e\mathbf B/(2m)$. The velocity-dependent <Lorentz force> is $e\mathbf v_i'\times\mathbf B=-e\mathbf B\times\mathbf v_i'$, and it cancels the <Coriolis force> $-2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_i'=e\mathbf B\times\mathbf v_i'$. The remaining terms combine to $m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x_i)$ and are quadratic in the <magnetic field>. Neglecting them gives
$$
\boxed{m\mathbf a_i'=\sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf F_{ij}+O(B^2)},
$$
identical to the zero-field equations to first order. The <Larmor theorem> requires the common charge-to-mass ratio; a mixture with different ratios cannot use one rotating frame to cancel every magnetic term.

= 5F
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Numbers and Sets}

= Solution
{parent=5F}

A <set> is <countable> if it is finite or admits an <injection> into the natural numbers; an infinite <countable set> can equivalently be listed as a sequence. Let $A=\bigcup_{i\ge1}A_i$ with each $A_i$ <countable>. Choose an enumeration of each nonempty $A_i$, allowing repetition if it is finite. List the pairs $(i,j)\in\mathbb N^2$ in successive finite diagonals of constant $i+j$ and output the $j$th member of $A_i$. This lists every member of $A$, so $A$ is <countable>; empty sets contribute nothing, and repetitions can be removed by retaining first occurrences. This is the <countable union of countable sets> theorem, in the usual setting with <axiom of countable choice> for selecting the enumerations.

Each positive-length interval $J_i$ has a nonempty interior containing a <rational number> by the <density of the rational numbers>. Fix one enumeration of the <rational numbers> and let $q_i$ be its first member in the interior of $J_i$. Disjointness gives $i\ne j\Rightarrow q_i\ne q_j$, so $i\mapsto q_i$ is an <injection> of $I$ into a <countable set>. Hence $I$ is <countable>. This proof of the <countability of disjoint positive-length intervals> uses a fixed enumeration and needs no arbitrary simultaneous choice of rationals.

= a
{parent=6f}

= Solution
{parent=a}

First, <finite additivity of a set function> gives $f(\varnothing)=0$, since $f(\varnothing)=2f(\varnothing)$. Put $w_s=f(\{s\})$. Decomposing any subset into its singleton elements and using <induction> gives $f(A)=\sum_{s\in A}w_s$. Fix $s\in S$ and let $r$ be the number of sets $A_i$ containing it. Its total coefficient in the alternating intersection sum is zero if $r=0$, and otherwise is
$$
\sum_{k=1}^r(-1)^{k+1}\binom rk=1-(1-1)^r=1
$$
by the <binomial theorem>. These are exactly the coefficients in $f(\bigcup_iA_i)$. Summing against $w_s$ proves the required <inclusion-exclusion principle> for the additive <set function>, including signed weights.

= b
{parent=6f}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take the finite ambient <set> $S=\bigcup_iA_i$ and the additive <set function> $f(A)=|A|$. Disjoint subsets have additive <cardinality>, so (a) applies and gives
$$
\boxed{\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^nA_i\right|=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|}.
$$
This is the finite <inclusion-exclusion principle>.

= c
{parent=6f}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

In the <set> of all $n!$ <permutations>, let $A_i$ consist of those fixing $i$. For a prescribed collection of $k$ fixed points, its intersection has $(n-k)!$ members. Applying the <inclusion-exclusion principle> to the complement of $\bigcup_iA_i$ gives the number of <derangements>:
$$
\boxed{d_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk(n-k)!=n!\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}}.
$$
The convergent <power series> for the <exponential function> at $-1$ therefore gives $\boxed{d_n/n!\longrightarrow e^{-1}}$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For each $1\le j\le p-1$, <Fermat's little theorem> gives $j^p\equiv j\pmod p$. Since $p$ is odd,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j^p\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}j=\frac{p(p-1)}2\equiv0\pmod p}.
$$

= b
{parent=7b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $m=(p-1)/2$, so the requested modulus is $pm$. The <factorial> $(p-1)!$ is divisible by $m$, and so is $p-1=2m$. Also, <Wilson's theorem> gives $(p-1)!\equiv-1\equiv p-1\pmod p$. Because $m$ and $p$ are <coprime integers>, their product divides the difference. Thus the <Wilson factorial congruence modulo a triangular number> is
$$
\boxed{(p-1)!\equiv p-1\pmod{p(p-1)/2}}.
$$
The displayed residue lies in the standard range even at $p=3$.

= 8B
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Numbers and Sets}

= Solution
{parent=8B}

For $b>1$, <Euler's theorem> allows $d=\varphi(b)>0$, since $a,b$ are <coprime integers>. At $b=1$ every congruence holds and one may take $d=1$. Let $y$ be the <multiplicative order> of $a$ modulo $b$. Write a nonnegative exponent $x=qy+r$ with $0\le r<y$ using the <Euclidean division>. Then $a^x\equiv a^r\pmod b$, so $a^x\equiv1$ implies $r=0$ by minimality of $y$. Thus $y\mid x$. Negative exponents, if included, give the same conclusion using the inverse residue of $a$.

A <prime divisor of a Fermat number> $F_n=2^{2^n}+1$ is odd, and satisfies $2^{2^n}\equiv-1\pmod p$. Therefore $2^{2^{n+1}}\equiv1\pmod p$, so its <multiplicative order> divides $2^{n+1}$. It does not divide $2^n$, since $-1\ne1$ modulo the odd prime. Every divisor of $2^{n+1}$ is a power of two, hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2^{n+1}}.
$$
By <Fermat's little theorem> and the divisibility just proved, $2^{n+1}\mid p-1$, giving $\boxed{p\equiv1\pmod{2^{n+1}}}$.

= 9E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=9E}

For distinct positions and positive masses, <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives
$$
m_i\ddot{\mathbf x}_i=-G\sum_{j\ne i}m_im_j\frac{\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j}{|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j|^3}.
$$
The proposed <homothetic gravitational motion> has $\ddot{\mathbf x}_i=-(2/9)t^{-4/3}\mathbf a_i$ and pair distances $t^{2/3}|\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j|$, so cancellation of $t^{-4/3}$ gives the time-independent equations
$$
\boxed{\frac29\mathbf a_i=G\sum_{j\ne i}m_j\frac{\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j}{|\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j|^3}}.
$$
These define a <Newtonian central configuration> with this choice of scale. For
$$
\Phi=\frac19\sum_i m_i|\mathbf a_i|^2+\sum_{i<j}\frac{Gm_im_j}{|\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j|},
$$
<differentiation> in $\mathbf a_i$ gives $\nabla_{\mathbf a_i}\Phi=(2/9)m_i\mathbf a_i-G\sum_{j\ne i}m_im_j(\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j)/|\mathbf a_i-\mathbf a_j|^3=0$, exactly the displayed equations. The double sum in the paper counts each pair twice, so it agrees with this expression.

Multiply the configuration equations by $m_i$ and sum. Pair contributions cancel, giving $\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i=0$. Thus the total <linear momentum> is $\mathbf P=(2/3)t^{-1/3}\sum_i m_i\mathbf a_i=0$. Each position is parallel to its <velocity>, so every $m_i\mathbf x_i\times\dot{\mathbf x}_i=0$ and the total <angular momentum> is zero. About any other fixed point $\mathbf b$, it is $\mathbf L_{\mathbf b}=\mathbf L_O-\mathbf b\times\mathbf P=\boxed{\mathbf0}$.

= 10E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=10E}

A <central force> has zero <torque>, so $h=r^2\dot\theta$ is constant and the <angular momentum> is $mh$. The <areal velocity> is $h/2$. Put $u=1/r$ and let primes denote angular <derivatives>. Then $\dot r=-hu'$, $\ddot r=-h^2u^2u''$, and $r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3$. The radial equation with inward force magnitude $f(u)$ is $m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=-f(u)$, giving the <Binet equation>
$$
\boxed{u''+u=\frac{f(u)}{mh^2u^2}}.
$$
Choose the angular origin along a diameter of a radius-$R$ circle through $O$. Its polar equation is $r=2R\cos\theta$, so $u=(2R)^{-1}\sec\theta$ on $-\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2$. Since $(\sec\theta)''+\sec\theta=2\sec^3\theta$, substitution gives $u''+u=8R^2u^3$ and hence
$$
\boxed{f(u)=cu^5,\qquad c=8mh^2R^2>0}.
$$
The force is **attractive**. For fixed force coefficient $c$ and mass, $R|mh|=\sqrt{mc/8}$, so $R$ is inversely proportional to the magnitude of the <angular momentum>. The time along the circle is
$$
\boxed{T=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{r^2}{|h|}\,d\theta=\frac{2\pi R^2}{|h|}=4\sqrt2\,\pi\sqrt{\frac mc}R^3}.
$$
This <circular orbit through an inverse-fifth-power singularity> reaches the force center at the two endpoints of this angular interval. The integral is finite; continuation through the singular collision is not specified by the force law alone.

= 11E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=11E}

Neglecting gravity, the <equation of motion> is $m\ddot{\mathbf x}=a\dot{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf x/r^3$, where $r=|\mathbf x|>0$. Dotting with the <velocity> gives $d(m|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2/2)/dt=0$, because a <cross product> is perpendicular to its factors. Thus the <speed> is constant. The <vector triple product> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(m\mathbf x\times\dot{\mathbf x})=\frac a{r^3}\mathbf x\times(\dot{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf x)=a\left(\frac{\dot{\mathbf x}}r-\frac{\mathbf x(\mathbf x\cdot\dot{\mathbf x})}{r^3}\right)=a\frac d{dt}\frac{\mathbf x}r.
$$
Hence the <generalized angular momentum in a radial monopole field> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf L=m\mathbf x\times\dot{\mathbf x}-a\frac{\mathbf x}r=\text{constant}}.
$$
Dotting with $\mathbf x$ gives $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf x=-ar$, or $\widehat{\mathbf L}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf x}=-a/|\mathbf L|$. For $\mathbf L\ne0$, the direction of $\mathbf x$ therefore makes the fixed angle $\boxed{\cos\beta=-a/|\mathbf L|}$ with the axis $\mathbf L$, describing a <right circular conical surface> with vertex at the North Pole. Orthogonality gives $|\mathbf L|^2=m^2|\mathbf x\times\dot{\mathbf x}|^2+a^2$, so this cosine is in $[-1,1]$. Radial motion gives a degenerate cone; when $a=0$ and the ordinary <angular momentum> is nonzero the cone is the usual orbital plane. If $\mathbf L=0$, the norm identity forces $a=0$ and $\mathbf x\times\dot{\mathbf x}=0$, giving a radial straight line.

= 12E
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=12E}

The <moment of inertia> of a uniform length-$2l$ rod about its transverse central axis is
$$
\boxed{I=\frac M{2l}\int_{-l}^ls^2\,ds=\frac13Ml^2}.
$$
The <kinetic energy decomposition about the center of mass> gives $\boxed{T=MV^2/2+Ml^2\dot\theta^2/6}$ for center <speed> $V$ and rod angle $\theta$.

For a falling arch with $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, the symmetric <two hinged rods with both ends sliding on a floor> have the following motion. The hinge remains vertically over the arch center. Their centers are $\mathbf R_\pm=(\pm l\cos\theta,l\sin\theta)$, so each has squared <speed> $l^2\dot\theta^2$. Total <kinetic energy> is $T=4Ml^2\dot\theta^2/3$, and total gravitational <potential energy> is $U=2Mgl\sin\theta$. <Conservation of energy> from rest at angle $\alpha$ gives $\dot\theta^2=3g(\sin\alpha-\sin\theta)/(2l)$. At the hinge impact $\theta=0$, its height derivative is $2l\cos\theta\dot\theta$, giving
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm hinge}=\sqrt{6gl\sin\alpha}}.
$$
The floor-contact assumption is consistent: differentiating the energy relation gives $\ddot\theta=-3g\cos\theta/(4l)$, and the normal reaction on each outer endpoint is $N=Mg[1+9\sin^2\theta-6\sin\alpha\sin\theta]/4$. Completing the square shows $N\ge Mg\cos^2\alpha/4\ge0$ throughout the fall.
