= Solution
{parent=1A}

Since $AB=0$, the <image of a linear map> represented by $B$ lies in the <kernel> of $A$. The <matrix> $B$ is nonzero, so this <image of a linear map> contains a nonzero <vector>; consequently $\dim\ker A\geq1$. On the other hand, $A\neq0$ gives $\operatorname{rank}A\geq1$. The <rank-nullity theorem> in dimension two therefore gives
$$
\dim\ker A=1,\qquad\operatorname{rank}A=1.
$$
Thus **the image of $A$ is a line through the origin**, and $A$ maps onto that line.

Reversing the order of the <matrix multiplication> need not give zero. For example,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
gives $AB=0$ but $BA=B\neq0$. This also shows why information about one composition of <linear maps> does not establish the corresponding property of the reversed composition.

Write $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Its <rank> is one, so its <determinant> is zero. Choose its <adjugate matrix>:
$$
\boxed{C=\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $AC=CA=(ad-bc)I=0$. Since the entries of $C$ are the entries of the nonzero <matrix> $A$, rearranged with possible sign changes, $C\neq0$. This establishes both required annihilation properties.

A <group> $G$ is a <cyclic group> if some element $g\in G$ generates it:
$$
G=\langle g\rangle=\{g^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$
For a finite <cyclic group>, the <order of a group element> of a generator equals the number of elements of the <group>.

An example of a finite <abelian group> that is not a <cyclic group> is the <Klein four-group>, realized as
$$
G=(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2
$$
with componentwise addition modulo two. Addition commutes, so this is an <abelian group> of four elements. Each of its three nonzero elements satisfies $g+g=0$, so the <cyclic subgroup> it generates has just two elements; the identity generates only itself. **No element generates all four elements.**

= Solution

**Yes: every finite group of rotations about the origin in the plane is cyclic.** A <planar rotation> is determined by an angle modulo $2\pi$, and composition adds those angles. If $G$ consists only of the identity, it is a <cyclic group>. Otherwise, finiteness allows us to choose the smallest positive representative angle $\alpha\in(0,2\pi)$ occurring in $G$.

Take any <planar rotation> in $G$ with representative angle $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Write $\theta=q\alpha+r$, where $q$ is an integer and $0\leq r<\alpha$. The composition $R_\theta R_\alpha^{-q}$ belongs to $G$ and has angle $r$. Minimality of $\alpha$ forces $r=0$, so every element of $G$ is a power of $R_\alpha$.

Similarly, write $2\pi=m\alpha+r$ with $0\leq r<\alpha$. If $r>0$, the <planar rotation> $R_\alpha^{-m}$ has representative angle $r$, a contradiction. Thus $\alpha=2\pi/m$ and
$$
\boxed{G=\langle R_{2\pi/m}\rangle.}
$$
This proves <finite groups of planar rotations are cyclic> by an elementary angle argument.

For an <exact differential> $M\,d\theta+N\,d\phi=dF$, equality of mixed <partial derivatives> requires $M_\phi=N_\theta$. Here the two <partial derivatives> are
$$
M_\phi=-\sinh\theta\sin\phi,\qquad N_\theta=\sinh\theta\sin\phi.
$$
They are not identically equal, so the <differential form> is not a <closed differential form> and hence **is not an exact differential**. Equality along special lines such as $\theta=0$ does not supply a potential on a two-dimensional open neighborhood, because every such neighborhood contains points where the two <partial derivatives> differ.

= Solution

To construct a potential for the <exact differential>, first integrate its $dx$ coefficient with respect to $x$:
$$
F=x^3(y^2+1)+g(y,z).
$$
Matching the $dy$ coefficient gives $g_y=-2z^2$, hence $g=-2yz^2+h(z)$. The remaining <partial derivative> is then
$$
F_z=-4yz+h'(z).
$$
Matching the $dz$ coefficient forces $h'=0$. Therefore a potential, valid throughout $\mathbb R^3$, is
$$
\boxed{F(x,y,z)=x^3(y^2+1)-2yz^2+C.}
$$
Differentiating this expression recovers all three coefficients, proving that the <differential form> really is an <exact differential>.

= Solution
{parent=4A}

The <divergence theorem> states that, for a bounded region $V$ with piecewise smooth boundary $S$, outward unit <normal vector> $n$, and a continuously differentiable <vector field> $F$ on a neighborhood of $\overline V$,
$$
\int_S F\cdot n\,dS=\int_V\nabla\cdot F\,dV.
$$
The outward orientation fixes the sign of the <flux>.

On the <sphere> of radius $R$, the outward unit <normal vector> is $\mathbf r/R$. Thus $r^n\mathbf r\cdot n=R^{n+1}$. Multiplying by the <surface area> $4\pi R^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{I=4\pi R^{n+3}.}
$$
For the second calculation, use the <product rule> in <Cartesian coordinates>:
$$
\nabla\cdot(r^n\mathbf r)=3r^n+\mathbf r\cdot\nabla r^n=(n+3)r^n\qquad(r>0).
$$
The field remains continuously differentiable at the origin when $n>0$. Indeed, for $r>0$ its <Jacobian matrix> entries are $r^n\delta_{ij}+nr^{n-2}x_ix_j$, all tending to zero as $r\to0$; its derivative at zero is also zero because $|r^n\mathbf r|/r=r^n\to0$. The <divergence theorem> therefore applies to the entire ball. In <spherical coordinates>, it yields
$$
I=4\pi(n+3)\int_0^R r^{n+2}\,dr=4\pi R^{n+3},
$$
in agreement with the direct <surface integral>.

= Solution
{parent=5E}

Using <Einstein summation convention> and the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
[a\times(b\times c)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{k\ell m}a_jb_\ell c_m\\
&=(\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{j\ell})a_jb_\ell c_m\\
&=b_i(a\cdot c)-c_i(a\cdot b).
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves the <vector triple product identity> directly from the <Levi-Civita symbol> and <Kronecker delta>.

Define the <scalar triple product> by $[a,b,c]=a\cdot(b\times c)$. Its expression $\epsilon_{ijk}a_ib_jc_k$ is unchanged by cyclically relabeling the three indices: a three-cycle has positive sign. Thus
$$
[a,b,c]=[b,c,a]=[c,a,b].
$$
In particular, let $\Delta=[e_1,e_2,e_3]$. Since the three vectors form a <basis>, their <determinant> $\Delta$ is nonzero, so their <reciprocal basis> vectors are well defined. Cyclic invariance and vanishing of a <scalar triple product> with repeated vectors give
$$
\widehat e_i\cdot e_j=\delta_{ij}.
$$
To calculate the new <scalar triple product> explicitly, apply the already proved <vector triple product identity> with $A=e_3\times e_1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat e_2\times\widehat e_3
&=\frac{(e_3\times e_1)\times(e_1\times e_2)}{\Delta^2}\\
&=\frac{[(e_3\times e_1)\cdot e_2]e_1-[(e_3\times e_1)\cdot e_1]e_2}{\Delta^2}
=\frac{e_1}{\Delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{[\widehat e_1,\widehat e_2,\widehat e_3]=\frac1\Delta\neq0,}
$$
so the reciprocal vectors also form a <basis>. Taking the <reciprocal basis> of that <basis> now gives
$$
\widehat{\widehat e}_1
=\frac{\widehat e_2\times\widehat e_3}{[\widehat e_1,\widehat e_2,\widehat e_3]}
=\frac{e_1/\Delta}{1/\Delta}=e_1.
$$
The same cyclic calculation gives $\widehat{\widehat e}_2=e_2$ and $\widehat{\widehat e}_3=e_3$, proving <reciprocal basis involution>.

Finally, write $V=V_1e_1+V_2e_2+V_3e_3$. Taking a <dot product> with $\widehat e_j$ isolates $V_j$. Applying the same argument to the <reciprocal basis>, whose reciprocal is the original <basis>, gives the two sets of coordinates:
$$
\boxed{V_j=V\cdot\widehat e_j,\qquad \widehat V_j=V\cdot e_j.}
$$
No <orthonormality> assumption is needed; the <reciprocal basis> is what makes these coordinate-extraction formulas work.

Set $w=(x-y)/2$. The sum condition then gives $x=a+w$ and $y=a-w$, and their <dot product> is
$$
x\cdot y=(a+w)\cdot(a-w)=|a|^2-|w|^2.
$$
Therefore $|w|^2=|a|^2-c$. Since $c<|a|^2$, this is a strictly positive squared radius. The complete solution is
$$
\boxed{x=a+Rn,\qquad y=a-Rn,\qquad R=\sqrt{|a|^2-c},\qquad |n|=1.}
$$
Every choice of <unit vector> $n$ satisfies both conditions, and every solution has this form. The endpoints lie on the <sphere> with center $a$ and radius $R$, and their midpoint is $a$. They are therefore opposite ends of one diameter of that <sphere>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Translate one vertex of the <tetrahedron> to the origin and denote the other vertex vectors by $a,b,c$. Translation preserves edge vectors and their <dot products>. We may label the two known perpendicular pairs of <opposite edges of a tetrahedron> as the pairs represented by $a,c-b$ and $b,a-c$. Their orthogonality gives
$$
a\cdot c=a\cdot b,\qquad b\cdot a=b\cdot c.
$$
Hence all three pairwise <dot products> have the same value $s$. The third pair satisfies
$$
c\cdot(b-a)=c\cdot b-c\cdot a=s-s=0,
$$
so **the third pair of opposite edges is also perpendicular**.

Expanding squared lengths using the <dot product> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
|a|^2+|c-b|^2
&=|b|^2+|a-c|^2\\
&=|c|^2+|b-a|^2\\
&=|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2-2s.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **the sums of squared opposite-edge lengths are equal**. These two conclusions are the basic identities for <orthogonal opposite edges of a tetrahedron>.

= Solution

For the <linear map> $\alpha$ represented by $A$, a solution to the <system of linear equations> exists precisely when $b\in\operatorname{im}\alpha$. If $x_0$ is one solution, then
$$
Ax=b\iff A(x-x_0)=0\iff x\in x_0+\ker\alpha.
$$
Thus the <affine solution space of a linear equation> completely describes the possibilities: **no solutions** when $b\notin\operatorname{im}\alpha$; **one solution** when $b\in\operatorname{im}\alpha$ and $\ker\alpha=\{0\}$; and **infinitely many solutions** when $b\in\operatorname{im}\alpha$ and $\ker\alpha\neq\{0\}$. In the last case, a nonzero $w\in\ker\alpha$ supplies the distinct solutions $x_0+tw$ for every real $t$. In dimension three, the <rank-nullity theorem> also says that a trivial <kernel> is equivalent to an invertible <matrix> $A$.

For the <matrix> equation, $AX=B$ means that $\beta=\alpha\circ\chi$, where $\chi$ is represented by $X$. Necessarily $\operatorname{im}\beta\subseteq\operatorname{im}\alpha$. Conversely, if that containment holds, choose an $\alpha$-preimage of each $\beta(e_j)$ for the three standard <basis vectors>, and use those preimages as the columns of $X$. Their linear extension gives $AX=B$. This proves the <image criterion for a matrix factorization>:
$$
\boxed{AX=B\text{ is solvable}\iff\operatorname{im}\beta\subseteq\operatorname{im}\alpha.}
$$
Whenever it is solvable, every solution is $X_0+N$ with $AN=0$. It is unique exactly when $\ker\alpha=\{0\}$. Indeed, a nonzero $w\in\ker\alpha$ produces a nonzero <matrix> $N$ with first column $w$ and other columns zero, so uniqueness fails for any compatible $B$ if the <kernel> is nontrivial.

For the printed numerical <matrix> $A$, its <determinant> is $-2$, so the solution is unique. To calculate it, solve $A(x,y,z)^T=(b_1,b_2,b_3)^T$. The last equation gives $z=b_3-3y$; the second then gives $x=b_2-b_3+y$. Substitution into the first yields
$$
y=\frac{b_1-4b_2+3b_3}{2},\quad
x=\frac{b_1-2b_2+b_3}{2},\quad
z=\frac{-3b_1+12b_2-7b_3}{2}.
$$
Applying these formulas to the three columns of the PDF's $B$ gives
$$
\boxed{X=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&3/2\\5&0&7/2\\-12&1&-17/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Multiplication gives $AX=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\0&1&0\\3&1&2\end{pmatrix}$, confirming the result against the printed <matrix>.

The <chain rule> along the specified straight line gives
$$
F'(0)=\alpha f_1(a,b)+\beta f_2(a,b),\qquad
F''(0)=\alpha^2f_{11}(a,b)+2\alpha\beta f_{12}(a,b)+\beta^2f_{22}(a,b).
$$
Here equality of mixed <partial derivatives> follows from smoothness. Apply <Taylor theorem> in the single variable $t$:
$$
F(t)=F(0)+tF'(0)+\tfrac12t^2F''(0)+o(t^2).
$$
Writing $h_1=x_1-a=\alpha t$ and $h_2=x_2-b=\beta t$ produces the linear and quadratic terms
$$
f(a+h_1,b+h_2)=f(a,b)+h_1f_1+h_2f_2
+\tfrac12(h_1^2f_{11}+2h_1h_2f_{12}+h_2^2f_{22})+o(|h|^2),
$$
with all displayed <partial derivatives> evaluated at $(a,b)$. For a remainder valid uniformly over directions, apply the <Taylor formula with integral remainder> to $s\mapsto f((a,b)+sh)$:
$$
f((a,b)+h)=f(a,b)+\nabla f(a,b)\cdot h
+\int_0^1(1-s)h^TH((a,b)+sh)h\,ds.
$$
Continuity of the <Hessian matrix> makes the difference between the integral and $\tfrac12h^TH(a,b)h$ be $o(|h|^2)$. The printed ellipsis is therefore justified as a finite <Taylor expansion> with a remainder. Smoothness alone does not assert convergence of an infinite <Taylor series>.

At a <stationary point>, the linear term vanishes. The symmetric <Hessian matrix> satisfies
$$
h^THh
=H_{11}\left(h_1+\frac{H_{12}}{H_{11}}h_2\right)^2
+\frac{\det H}{H_{11}}h_2^2.
$$
If $H_{11}>0$ and $\det H>0$, this is a <positive-definite quadratic form>. Its minimum over the <unit circle> is a positive number $c$, so $h^THh\geq c|h|^2$. The <Taylor remainder> is smaller than $c|h|^2/4$ for sufficiently small $h$, proving $f((a,b)+h)>f(a,b)$ for nonzero nearby $h$. Thus **the stationary point is a strict local minimum**.

For the given polynomial, the <gradient> equations reduce to
$$
x_2=-x_1,\qquad 4x_1(x_1^2-1)=0.
$$
The <stationary points> are $(0,0),(1,-1),(-1,1)$, and
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}12x_1^2-2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At both points with $x_1=\pm1$, we have $H_{11}=10>0$ and $\det H=16>0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{(1,-1)\text{ and }(-1,1)\text{ are local minima, with }f=-1.}
$$
At the origin the <determinant> of the <Hessian matrix> is $-8$, giving a <saddle point>. An independent check is the identity $f=(x_1^2-1)^2+(x_1+x_2)^2-1$, which proves that the two displayed <local minima> are also the only global minima.

Choose coordinates that measure the curved boundary and the slope of a ray:
$$
\boxed{u=xy^2,\qquad v=y/x.}
$$
On the positive interior, the inverse <change of variables> is
$$
x=u^{1/3}v^{-2/3},\qquad y=u^{1/3}v^{1/3}.
$$
The two rays become $v=a$ and $v=1$, while the curve becomes $u=1$. The interior of $S$ therefore maps to the rectangle $0<u<1$, $a<v<1$. For $a=0$, the lower edge $v=0$ describes a limiting boundary, since the original region is unbounded; the origin also has no uniquely defined ratio $y/x$. For $a=1$ the region has zero area. These endpoint cases do not invalidate the <change of variables> on the positive interior.

For the three-dimensional <integral>, retain $z$ as the third coordinate. The transformed region is
$$
0<z<1,\qquad z<v<1,\qquad0<u<1.
$$
The planar <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}
=\det\begin{pmatrix}y^2&2xy\\-y/x^2&1/x\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{3y^2}{x},\qquad
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|=\frac{x}{3y^2}.
$$
The integrand cancels this <Jacobian determinant> especially simply:
$$
\frac{y^2z^2}{x}\,dx\,dy\,dz=\frac{z^2}{3}\,du\,dv\,dz.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_D\frac{y^2z^2}{x}\,dx\,dy\,dz
=\frac13\int_0^1z^2(1-z)\,dz=\frac1{36}.}
$$
One can first use the <change of variables formula> with $z\geq\varepsilon>0$ and then pass to the limit. The nonnegative integrand allows this by the <monotone convergence theorem>, so the unbounded limiting slice at $z=0$ causes no omitted contribution.

= Solution

In <Einstein summation convention>, the <curl> of a <cross product> has components
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\nabla\times(a\times b)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j(\epsilon_{k\ell m}a_\ell b_m)\\
&=\partial_j(a_i b_j-a_j b_i)\\
&=a_i\partial_jb_j-b_i\partial_ja_j+b_j\partial_ja_i-a_j\partial_jb_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second line uses the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols>; the last uses the <product rule>. These components prove the <curl of a cross product> identity, including both <divergence> and directional-derivative terms.

Now apply that identity to $m\times v$. On the <orientable surface>, $m=n$, $m\cdot v=0$, and $|m|=1$. Differentiating $m\cdot m=1$ gives $m_i\partial_jm_i=0$. Taking a <dot product> with $m$ in the <curl of a cross product> formula therefore gives, on $S$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
n\cdot\nabla\times(m\times v)
&=\nabla\cdot v-(m\cdot v)\nabla\cdot m
+m_i v_j\partial_jm_i-m_i m_j\partial_jv_i\\
&=(\delta_{ij}-n_in_j)\partial_jv_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
Only tangency on the surface is needed; no claim is made that the extension $v$ is tangent away from it. The right side is the <surface divergence> of the tangent <vector field>.

With the boundary oriented according to <Stokes theorem>, cyclic invariance of the <scalar triple product> gives
$$
(m\times v)\cdot d\mathbf r=v\cdot(d\mathbf r\times n)=v\cdot u\,ds,
$$
where $u$ is the <outward conormal of a surface boundary>. Thus <Stokes theorem> proves
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\delta_{ij}-n_in_j)\partial_jv_i\,dS
=\oint_C u\cdot v\,ds.}
$$
This is the <surface divergence theorem for a tangent field>.

For the disc in the $z=0$ plane, take $n=(0,0,1)$ and $v=\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$. The field is tangent on the disc, and $\partial_jv_i=\delta_{ij}$. The contraction is $3-|n|^2=2$, so the <surface integral> is $2\pi R^2$. On the positively oriented circular boundary, $u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)$ and $v=Ru$, so the boundary <flux> is $R(2\pi R)=2\pi R^2$. **Both sides agree.**

Assume $r_0$ is an interior point and choose $\varepsilon$ small enough that its closed ball lies inside $V$. Apply <Green's second identity> to the punctured region $V_\varepsilon=V\setminus\overline{B_\varepsilon(r_0)}$, where the potential $\psi=1/|r-r_0|$ is smooth and satisfies <Laplace equation>. Since $\nabla^2\phi=-\rho$, this gives
$$
\int_{V_\varepsilon}\frac{\rho(r)}{|r-r_0|}\,dV
=\int_S(\phi\partial_n\psi-\psi\partial_n\phi)\,dS
+\int_{S_\varepsilon}(\phi\partial_{n_\varepsilon}\psi-\psi\partial_{n_\varepsilon}\phi)\,dS.
$$
The crucial orientation is the outward unit <normal vector> of the punctured region: on the inner <sphere>, it is $n_\varepsilon=-e_r$, pointing toward $r_0$. Consequently $\psi=1/\varepsilon$ and $\partial_{n_\varepsilon}\psi=1/\varepsilon^2$. The inner boundary term becomes
$$
\int_{S_\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\phi}{\varepsilon^2}+\frac1\varepsilon\partial_r\phi\right)dS.
$$
Writing $r=r_0+\varepsilon\omega$ and $dS=\varepsilon^2d\Omega$, its first part tends to $\int_{|\omega|=1}\phi(r_0)\,d\Omega=4\pi\phi(r_0)$ by continuity. The second part is $O(\varepsilon)$ because the <gradient> of $\phi$ is bounded near $r_0$, and hence tends to zero.

The omitted volume term also tends to zero: for bounded $\rho$ near $r_0$, its absolute value is bounded by a constant times $4\pi\int_0^\varepsilon r\,dr=O(\varepsilon^2)$. Thus the limiting <integral> exists, and rearranging the outer boundary terms gives
$$
\boxed{4\pi\phi(r_0)
=\int_V\frac{\rho(r)}{|r-r_0|}\,dV
+\int_S\left[\frac1{|r-r_0|}\partial_n\phi
-\phi\partial_n\frac1{|r-r_0|}\right]dS.}
$$
This is <Green's third identity> for the <Poisson equation> with the stated sign convention. It retains both boundary terms; no homogeneous boundary condition has been assumed. The punctured-domain calculation accounts for the singular source without incorrectly applying the regular <divergence theorem> through $r_0$.
