= Solution
{parent=1E}

If $m_1,\ldots,m_n$ generate the <module> $M$, define the <R-module homomorphism>
$$
T:R^n\longrightarrow M,\qquad (r_1,\ldots,r_n)\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^nr_jm_j.
$$
This is a <surjection> by the definition of generation. Conversely, an <R-module homomorphism> that is a <surjection> sends the standard <basis> vectors of the <free module> $R^n$ to a generating family, since every vector in $R^n$ is their linear combination. Thus
$$
\boxed{M\text{ is finitely generated}\iff M\text{ is a quotient of some finite free module }R^n.}
$$
This is the <finite generation as a quotient of a finite free module> criterion. The zero <module> causes no exception: it is generated by the empty family, or by one zero element.

For the requested example, take $M=(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$, with the usual integer scalar action. Reducing both coordinates modulo two gives an <R-module homomorphism> that is a <surjection> $\mathbb Z^2\to M$ with nonzero <kernel> $2\mathbb Z^2$, so it is not an <isomorphism>. Every image of $\mathbb Z$ is a <cyclic module>, whereas every element of $M$ has additive order at most two and $M$ has four elements. Hence $\boxed{M=(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2}$ admits no such surjection from $\mathbb Z$.

A <metric space> is a <complete metric space> when every <Cauchy sequence> converges to a point of that space: for each $\varepsilon>0$, the condition $d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon$ for all sufficiently large $m,n$ must imply existence of $x$ with $d(x_n,x)\to0$.

A <Cauchy sequence> in $[0,1]$ is also Cauchy in $\mathbb R$, so it converges there by the assumed completeness. Its limit remains between zero and one, since inequalities are preserved under limits. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[0,1]\text{ with the Euclidean metric is complete}.}
$$
This is an instance of the fact that a <closed subset> of a <complete metric space> is complete with its induced <metric>.

The rational sequence $q_n=10^{-n}\lfloor10^n\sqrt2\rfloor$ satisfies $0\le\sqrt2-q_n<10^{-n}$. It is therefore a <Cauchy sequence> in the induced <metric> on $\mathbb Q$. Any limit in $\mathbb Q$ would also be its real limit, which is $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ with the Euclidean metric is not complete}.}
$$
Completeness concerns limits lying in the space, so being a <dense subset> of $\mathbb R$ does not suffice.

Let $x_n=(\pi/2+2\pi n)^{-1}$. The points $p_n=(x_n,1)$ lie on the specified graph and converge in $\mathbb R^2$ to $(0,1)$. They form a <Cauchy sequence> in the induced <metric>, but their only possible limit is absent from the space. Adding $(0,0)$ does not supply that missing limit. Hence the specified space is \b[not complete].

For the unheaded request on $\mathbb R$, use the <arctangent pullback metric>
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=|\arctan x-\arctan y|.}
$$
Positivity, symmetry and the <triangle inequality> follow from the ordinary distance, and injectivity of $\arctan$ gives $d(x,y)=0$ only if $x=y$. The sequence $n$ is Cauchy for this <metric>, because $\arctan n\to\pi/2$, but cannot converge to any finite real $x$: that would require $\arctan x=\pi/2$. Thus this <metric> makes $\mathbb R$ incomplete, even though it induces its usual topology.

The <product topology> has as a <basis of a topology> all sets $U\times V$, where $U$ is open in $X$ and $V$ is open in $Y$. Open sets are arbitrary unions of these rectangles. The <projection maps> are $p(x,y)=x$ and $q(x,y)=y$. For any open $U\subset X$ and $V\subset Y$,
$$
\boxed{p^{-1}(U)=U\times Y,\qquad q^{-1}(V)=X\times V.}
$$
These are open rectangles, including the empty case. Taking inverse images of open sets therefore proves that both <projection maps> are <continuous functions>.

{parent=3e}

= Solution
{parent=b}

If $X$ is <Hausdorff>, every pair $x\ne y$ has disjoint open neighborhoods $U$ and $V$. The rectangle $U\times V$ is an open neighborhood of $(x,y)$ disjoint from the diagonal. Hence every point of its complement is interior, so the diagonal is a <closed set>.

Conversely, if the diagonal is closed, its complement is open in the <product topology>. For $x\ne y$, the rectangle-basis property gives open $U\ni x$ and $V\ni y$ with $U\times V$ disjoint from the diagonal. If $z\in U\cap V$, then $(z,z)$ lies in that rectangle, a contradiction. Thus $U\cap V=\varnothing$, giving the <Hausdorff> separation condition. Consequently
$$
\boxed{X\text{ is Hausdorff}\iff\Delta_X\text{ is closed in }X\times X.}
$$
The argument includes spaces with zero or one point, for which both conditions hold.

Let $Y(s)$ be the <Laplace transform> of $y$. The initial values give $\mathcal L(y')=sY$ and $\mathcal L(y'')=s^2Y-1$, while $\mathcal L(te^{-3t})=(s+3)^{-2}$. Therefore, for $\operatorname{Re}s$ sufficiently large,
$$
(s^2-4s+3)Y(s)-1=\frac1{(s+3)^2},\qquad
Y(s)=\frac{1+(s+3)^{-2}}{(s-1)(s-3)}.
$$
The <partial fraction decomposition> is
$$
Y(s)=\frac{37}{72(s-3)}-\frac{17}{32(s-1)}+\frac5{288(s+3)}+\frac1{24(s+3)^2}.
$$
Inverting the elementary <Laplace transforms> gives
$$
\boxed{y(t)=\frac{37}{72}e^{3t}-\frac{17}{32}e^t+\left(\frac5{288}+\frac t{24}\right)e^{-3t},\qquad t\ge0.}
$$
These coefficients give $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=1$; the transformed equation, or direct differentiation, verifies the forcing. Uniqueness follows from uniqueness for the corresponding linear initial-value <ordinary differential equation>.

Write $w$ for the complex coordinate. The inverse <stereographic projection> is
$$
\pi^{-1}(w)=\frac{(2\operatorname{Re}w,\,2\operatorname{Im}w,\,|w|^2-1)}{1+|w|^2},
$$
with $\infty$ mapped to the north pole. A rotation $R_z(\theta)$ about the third axis sends $w$ to $e^{i\theta}w$, represented by
$$
U_z(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta/2}&0\\0&e^{-i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}\in SU(2).
$$
For the rotation $R_y(\theta):(x,y,z)\mapsto(x\cos\theta+z\sin\theta,y,-x\sin\theta+z\cos\theta)$, substitution in the <stereographic projection> gives
$$
w\longmapsto\frac{cw-s}{sw+c},\qquad
U_y(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}\in SU(2),\quad
c=\cos(\theta/2),\ s=\sin(\theta/2).
$$
Both displayed matrices are unitary with determinant one, hence belong to the <special unitary group>.

Use the rotation-generation result that every $R\in SO(3)$ can be written $R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_z(\gamma)$, with angles chosen suitably. This is the Euler-angle decomposition, which is allowed here. Composition of <Möbius transformations> corresponds to multiplication of their matrix representatives, so
$$
\boxed{\pi R\pi^{-1}\text{ is represented by }U_z(\alpha)U_y(\beta)U_z(\gamma)\in SU(2).}
$$
The formulas extend at poles in the <Riemann sphere>. This realizes <sphere rotations as special-unitary Möbius transformations>. The two representatives $U$ and $-U$ define the same <Möbius transformation>, as expected for the double covering of the <special orthogonal group>.

For the boundary constraint $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1=0$, the <Lagrange multiplier> equations for $z=xy$ are
$$
y=2\lambda x,\qquad x=2\lambda y,\qquad x^2+y^2=1.
$$
Neither coordinate can vanish at a stationary point. Substitution gives $4\lambda^2=1$. For $\lambda=1/2$, $x=y=\pm1/\sqrt2$ and $z=1/2$; for $\lambda=-1/2$, $x=-y=\pm1/\sqrt2$ and $z=-1/2$. The constraint circle is compact, its <gradient> is nowhere zero, and every extremum must occur among these stationary points. Thus
$$
\boxed{z_{\max}=\frac12\text{ at }(\pm2^{-1/2},\pm2^{-1/2}),\quad z_{\min}=-\frac12\text{ at }(\pm2^{-1/2},\mp2^{-1/2}).}
$$
The paired signs in each expression are linked. The inequality $2|xy|\le x^2+y^2=1$ also confirms the global values.

Let $c>0$ denote the stated unit cost of cylindrical wall, and assume $V>0$. The <circular cylinder> and <hemisphere> give the volume constraint and total cost
$$
V=\pi r^2h+\frac23\pi r^3,\qquad C=2\pi crh+4\pi cr^2.

\boxed{r=\left(\frac{3V}{8\pi}\right)^{1/3},\qquad h=2r.}
$$

The substitution removes the zero-order term: $u_x=e^{-x^2}(v_x-2xv)$ and $u_y=e^{-x^2}v_y$, so the <partial differential equation> becomes $v_x+xv_y=1$. Its <method of characteristics> gives
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=x,\qquad \frac{dv}{dx}=1.
$$
The invariant is $\eta=y-x^2/2$. Therefore $v=x+F(\eta)$. The initial line $x=0$ is noncharacteristic, and the boundary data give $F(\eta)=\eta e^{-\eta^2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=e^{-x^2}\left[x+\left(y-\frac{x^2}{2}\right)e^{-(y-x^2/2)^2}\right].}
$$
Each characteristic meets the data line once, ensuring uniqueness among differentiable solutions, and substituting the result verifies both the equation and the data.

Inside the <infinite square well>, the time-independent <Schrodinger equation> is $-(\hbar^2/(2m))\psi''=E\psi$. The infinite exterior potential imposes $\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0$. For $E>0$, the first condition leaves a sine and the second requires $ka=n\pi$. A zero or negative energy gives only the zero solution under these conditions. The normalized <energy eigenstates> and <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2ma^2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The <wavefunctions> vanish outside the well, and a constant phase is immaterial. Normalization uses $\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx=a/2$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Born rule> gives the probability of $E_n$ as the squared coefficient of the initial state in the <energy eigenstate> basis. Direct integration gives
$$
c_n=\int_0^{a/2}\sqrt{\frac2a}\,\psi_n(x)\,dx
=\frac2{n\pi}\left(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2\right).
$$
The strict energy cutoff corresponds to $n^2<5$, so only $n=1,2$ contribute. Both coefficients equal $2/\pi$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\!\left(E<\frac{5\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}\right)=\frac8{\pi^2}.}
$$
The discontinuous initial <wavefunction> is a legitimate normalized $L^2$ state for this spectral probability calculation, although it is not in the Hamiltonian's differentiability domain; finite mean energy is not required to compute this probability.

A <reversible Markov chain> has a stationary law $\pi$ for which every finite path has the same distribution as its time-reversed path:
$$
\boxed{(X_0,\ldots,X_k)\overset d=(X_k,\ldots,X_0)\quad\text{for every }k\ge0.}
$$
For a transition matrix $P$, the equivalent <detailed balance> condition is $\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}$ for all $i,j$. It implies $\pi P=\pi$ by summing over $i$. Multiplying the balance identities along a path shows equality of its forward and reverse probabilities when $X_0\sim\pi$; conversely, taking paths of length one gives those identities.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the usual undirected graph with at least two vertices, a <simple random walk> chooses a neighbor uniformly at each step, so $P_{ij}=1/d_i$ when $i$ and $j$ are adjacent and zero otherwise, where $d_i$ is the <degree of a vertex>. The <handshaking lemma> gives $\sum_id_i=2|E|$. Thus the <stationary distribution of a graph random walk> is
$$
\boxed{\pi_i=\frac{d_i}{2|E|}.}
$$
For adjacent $i,j$, $\pi_iP_{ij}=1/(2|E|)=\pi_jP_{ji}$, and for nonadjacent pairs both sides vanish. By <detailed balance>, this is an <invariant distribution> and the walk started from it is a <reversible Markov chain>. Connectedness makes the finite walk an <irreducible Markov chain>, giving uniqueness of its <invariant distribution>. No aperiodicity assumption is needed for uniqueness, so a bipartite graph is allowed.

If the connected graph has a single isolated vertex, the usual neighbor-choice formula is undefined. With the natural absorbing convention $P_{11}=1$, its unique <invariant distribution> is $\pi_1=1$ and it is reversible. The degree-normalization formula presupposes at least one edge.

= Solution
{parent=10F}

Associate to $f$ the symmetric <bilinear form> $B(u,v)=\tfrac12(f(u+v)-f(u)-f(v))$. If $f$ is identically zero, every <basis> is diagonal. Otherwise choose $v$ with $f(v)\ne0$. Every $w$ has the decomposition
$$
w=\frac{B(w,v)}{B(v,v)}v+\left(w-\frac{B(w,v)}{B(v,v)}v\right),
$$
whose second term lies in $v^\perp$. Thus $V=\mathbb Rv\oplus v^\perp$, with no cross term in $f$. Induction on dimension gives a diagonal <basis>. Rescaling its nonzero entries reduces the form to
$$
f=\sum_{i=1}^px_i^2-\sum_{j=1}^qy_j^2,
$$
with $k$ additional zero coordinates. Define the <rank of a quadratic form> $r=p+q$ and its <signature> $s=p-q$. Rank is intrinsic, since it is the rank of $v\mapsto B(v,\cdot)$, equivalently $n-\dim\operatorname{rad}B$. Also $p$ is the greatest dimension of a subspace on which the restriction of $f$ is a <positive-definite quadratic form>: any such subspace intersects the negative-plus-zero coordinate space only at zero, so its dimension is at most $p$, while the positive coordinate space attains $p$. The corresponding negative statement identifies $q$. This proves the basis independence of $r$ and $s$, the <Sylvester's law of inertia> conclusion.

For a <totally isotropic subspace> $W$ on which $f$ vanishes, <polarization identity> also makes $B$ vanish on $W\times W$. Quotient by the <radical of a bilinear form>. In the resulting nondegenerate form, projection of an isotropic subspace to either the positive or negative coordinate space is injective, so its dimension is at most $\min(p,q)$. Consequently $\dim W\le k+\min(p,q)$. The <radical of a bilinear form> together with the vectors $e_i^++e_i^-$, $1\le i\le\min(p,q)$, attains this bound. The <maximum dimension of a totally isotropic subspace> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\dim W_{\max}=n-r+\frac{r-|s|}{2}=n-\frac{r+|s|}{2}.}
$$
The <criterion for membership in a diagonal basis> follows similarly: a nonzero vector can belong to a diagonal <basis> if it has nonzero quadratic value, by the same orthogonal splitting, or if it lies in the <radical of a bilinear form>, by choosing a radical basis containing it. An <isotropic vector> in a diagonal <basis> must be orthogonal to every basis vector, and hence must lie in that radical. For the specified form, its radical is the $z$-axis, giving exactly
$$
\boxed{v=(x,y,z)\text{ qualifies iff }(x^2\ne y^2)\text{ or }(x=y=0\text{ and }z\ne0).}
$$
The zero vector never belongs to a <basis>.

A <Euclidean domain> is an <integral domain> $R$ with a function $\nu:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb N$ such that for each $a\in R$ and $b\ne0$ there are $q,r\in R$ with $a=qb+r$ and either $r=0$ or $\nu(r)<\nu(b)$. Some conventions add a multiplicative monotonicity condition; it is unnecessary for this implication.

Let $I$ be a nonzero <ideal>. Choose $d\in I\setminus\{0\}$ minimizing $\nu(d)$, possible because the values lie in the nonnegative integers. For $a\in I$, division by $d$ gives $a=qd+r$, with $r=a-qd\in I$. A nonzero remainder would contradict minimality, so $r=0$ and $a\in(d)$. Since $d\in I$, the reverse inclusion also holds. The zero <ideal> is $(0)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{every ideal is principal, so every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain}.}
$$

= Solution

Reducing coefficients modulo $p$ gives the <ring isomorphism>
$$
\mathbb Z[x]/(p,f)\cong\mathbb F_p[x]/(\bar f).
$$
If $d=\deg f>0$, its monic leading coefficient remains one modulo $p$, so $\deg\bar f=d$. <Polynomial division> gives a unique representative of degree less than $d$: existence follows by division, and the difference of two such representatives cannot be a nonzero multiple of a degree-$d$ monic <polynomial>. There are $p$ choices for each of the $d$ coefficients, proving
$$
\boxed{|\mathbb Z[x]/(p,f)|=p^d.}
$$
Irreducibility of $\bar f$ is not needed for finiteness; without it the <quotient ring> need not be a field.

Write the <Gaussian integer> $\alpha=a+bi$, with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$, and set $R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$. Complex conjugation preserves $R$, because $\bar\alpha=2a-\alpha$. Choose a nonzero $\beta\in P$. Then $N=\beta\bar\beta$ is a positive integer in $P$. It cannot be one because $P$ is a proper <prime ideal>. Factoring $N$ into rational primes and applying primality repeatedly gives a prime $p\in P$.

The element $\alpha$ satisfies the monic <polynomial> $F(t)=t^2-2at+a^2+b^2$. Evaluation induces a <ring homomorphism> that is a <surjection>
$$
\mathbb Z[t]/(p,F)\longrightarrow R/P.
$$
The source has $p^2$ elements by part (b), so
$$
\boxed{|\mathbb Z[\alpha]/P|\le p^2<\infty.}
$$
If $b=0$, use $F(t)=t-a$ instead and get at most $p$ elements. Moreover $R/P$ is an <integral domain>, so its finiteness makes it a field. This proves the <finite residue-field theorem for Gaussian integer orders> without incorrectly assuming that every Gaussian integer order is a <principal ideal domain>.

= Solution
{parent=12G}

A <contraction mapping> satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y)$ for all $x,y$, with one fixed $0\le q<1$. The <contraction mapping theorem> states that such a map on a nonempty <complete metric space> has a unique fixed point and that every sequence of iterates converges to it.

Choose $x_0$ and put $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$. Iteration gives $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le q^nd(x_1,x_0)$, so for $m>n$,
$$
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}q^jd(x_1,x_0)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).
$$
Thus the iterates form a <Cauchy sequence> and converge by completeness to $x_*$. The Lipschitz bound gives continuity of $T$, hence $Tx_*=x_*$. Two fixed points satisfy $d(x_*,y_*)\le qd(x_*,y_*)$, forcing equality of the points. Passing $m\to\infty$ also gives the usual geometric error estimate. For $q=0$, convergence occurs after one application.

If $f^k$ is a <contraction mapping>, let $x_*$ be its unique fixed point. The commutation identity $f^kf=ff^k$ gives $f^k(fx_*)=fx_*$, so uniqueness implies $fx_*=x_*$. Any fixed point of $f$ is a fixed point of $f^k$, giving uniqueness, without even assuming $f$ continuous. On the complete discrete <metric space> $\{0,1,2\}$, the map $f(0)=f(1)=0$, $f(2)=1$ has constant second iterate but is not a contraction: $d(f2,f0)=d(2,0)=1$.

For the specified function, $x+\sin x\ge0$ on $[0,\infty)$ because it starts at zero and has derivative $1+\cos x\ge0$. Therefore $f$ maps the complete half-line into itself. Its derivative satisfies
$$
-\frac13\le f'(x)=\frac13\left(1+\cos x-\frac1{(x+1)^2}\right)\le\frac23.
$$
The <mean value theorem> therefore makes it a contraction of constant at most $2/3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f\text{ has exactly one fixed point on }[0,\infty).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=13F}

Fix $R>|z|$. The <Cauchy integral formula> and the uniformly convergent geometric series on $|\zeta|=R$ give
$$
f(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^n\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta^{n+1}}\,d\zeta.
$$
Defining
$$
c_j=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta^{j+1}}\,d\zeta,
$$
the remainder after degree $N$ has modulus at most $M_R(|z|/R)^{N+1}/(1-|z|/R)$, which tends to zero uniformly on each smaller disk. Differentiating the integral formula at zero identifies $c_j=f^{(j)}(0)/j!$, independently of $R$. Since $f$ is an <entire function>, $R$ can be chosen larger than any given $|z|$, proving the global <Taylor series>.

For the requested radius $r$, the same coefficient integral gives the <Cauchy estimate>
$$
\boxed{|c_n|\le\frac{M_r}{r^n}.}
$$
To characterize <equality in the Cauchy coefficient estimate>, parametrize its circle:
$$
r^nc_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(re^{i\theta})e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta.
$$
If $M_r=0$, the <identity theorem> gives $f=0$. Otherwise equality in the modulus bound requires the continuous integrand to have both constant modulus $M_r$ and constant complex argument. To see this directly, rotate its average to the positive real axis: its real part is bounded above by $M_r$, and equality of its average with $M_r$ forces that real part to equal $M_r$ everywhere. Thus $f(re^{i\theta})=c_nr^ne^{in\theta}$ on the circle. The <identity theorem> extends that equality to the plane. Conversely every such monomial attains the bound. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{equality holds exactly for }f(z)=cz^n,\quad c\in\mathbb C,}
$$
including the zero function and, when $n=0$, arbitrary constant functions.

At a regular point, choose the unit normal $\mathbf n=(\sigma_x\times\sigma_y)/|\sigma_x\times\sigma_y|$. The <first fundamental form> is the induced squared length
$$
I=E\,dx^2+2F\,dx\,dy+G\,dy^2,\quad E=\sigma_x\cdot\sigma_x,\ F=\sigma_x\cdot\sigma_y,\ G=\sigma_y\cdot\sigma_y.
$$
The <second fundamental form> is
$$
II=e\,dx^2+2f\,dx\,dy+g\,dy^2,\quad e=\mathbf n\cdot\sigma_{xx},\ f=\mathbf n\cdot\sigma_{xy},\ g=\mathbf n\cdot\sigma_{yy}.
$$
The <Gaussian curvature> is the determinant of the <shape operator>, $K=(eg-f^2)/(EG-F^2)$, independent of the normal orientation.

For the given graph, $\sigma_x=(1,0,y)$ and $\sigma_y=(0,1,x)$, so with $S=1+x^2+y^2$,
$$
I=(1+y^2)\,dx^2+2xy\,dx\,dy+(1+x^2)\,dy^2,\qquad
\mathbf n=\frac{(-y,-x,1)}{\sqrt S},\qquad II=\frac{2\,dx\,dy}{\sqrt S}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{K(x,y)=-\frac1{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}.}
$$
The surface is regular everywhere. The printed description “hyperboloid” is a naming error: this graph is a <hyperbolic paraboloid>, and the computation uses the actual parametrization.

Assume the parametrization is $C^2$ and regular, so its curvature is defined. Write $\mathbf v=\mathbf a'+y\mathbf b'$ and $W=|\mathbf v\times\mathbf b|>0$. Since $\sigma_x=\mathbf v$ and $\sigma_y=\mathbf b$, its <first fundamental form> is
$$
\boxed{I=|\mathbf a'+y\mathbf b'|^2\,dx^2+2(\mathbf a'+y\mathbf b')\cdot\mathbf b\,dx\,dy+|\mathbf b|^2\,dy^2.}
$$
For the unit normal $\mathbf n=(\mathbf v\times\mathbf b)/W$, the mixed coefficient of the <second fundamental form> is
$$
f=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf b'=\frac{\mathbf a'\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf b')}{W},
$$
where the term containing $\mathbf b'$ twice vanishes. Also $g=\mathbf n\cdot\sigma_{yy}=0$, while $EG-F^2=W^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{K=-\frac{[\mathbf a'\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf b')]^2}{W^4}.}
$$
The <Gaussian curvature> vanishes exactly when the displayed scalar triple product is zero. This is the <Gaussian curvature of a ruled surface> criterion; at a singular point with $W=0$, curvature and the claimed equivalence are not defined.

= Solution
{parent=15A}

Interpret the three conditions at zero as an initial-value problem on $x\ge0$, with interior source $\xi>0$. A <causal Green function> satisfies $\mathcal L_xG=\delta(x-\xi)$ and vanishes for $x<\xi$. Since the leading coefficient of the third derivative is one, the <jump conditions for a third-order Green function> require $G$ and $G'$ continuous and $G''$ to jump by one. For $t=x-\xi>0$, the homogeneous roots are $0,2,-1$. Solving $g(0)=g'(0)=0$, $g''(0)=1$ yields
$$
\boxed{G(x;\xi)=\Theta(x-\xi)\left(\frac{e^{2(x-\xi)}}6+\frac{e^{-(x-\xi)}}3-\frac12\right).}
$$
For $\xi>0$ it vanishes near zero, so all three initial boundary conditions hold. The matching conditions ensure that $\partial_x^3G$ supplies precisely a Dirac delta, without unwanted delta derivatives.

The homogeneous solution carrying the nonzero initial data is $y_h=\tfrac12-\tfrac16e^{2x}+\tfrac23e^{-x}$. The <Green-function representation> therefore gives
$$
y(x)=y_h(x)+\int_0^xG(x;\xi)e^\xi\Theta(\xi-1)\,d\xi.
$$
For $x<1$ the integral vanishes. For $x\ge1$, set $t=x-1$ and integrate to obtain $\tfrac e6(e^{2t}-3e^t-e^{-t}+3)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac12-\frac16e^{2x}+\frac23e^{-x}+\frac e6\Theta(x-1)\left(e^{2(x-1)}-3e^{x-1}-e^{-(x-1)}+3\right).}
$$
The bracket and its first two derivatives vanish at $x=1$, so $y,y',y''$ match continuously there; its third derivative jumps by the stated forcing. The value assigned to $\Theta(0)$ is immaterial. The source at the initial endpoint $\xi=0$ would need a separate endpoint-delta convention, which is not used in this integral.

The orbital <angular momentum operators> are
$$
\boxed{\hat L_i=\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}\hat x_j\hat p_k=-i\hbar\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}x_j\partial_k,\qquad \hat L^2=\sum_{i=1}^3\hat L_i^2.}
$$
In particular $\hat L_3=-i\hbar(x_1\partial_2-x_2\partial_1)$. For smooth compactly supported functions $\phi,\psi$, integration by parts produces no boundary term, and $\partial_2x_1=\partial_1x_2=0$. Hence
$$
\langle\phi,\hat L_3\psi\rangle=\langle\hat L_3\phi,\psi\rangle.
$$
This proves Hermiticity on the common test-function domain, and the same calculation applies on suitable decaying domains. As an unbounded operator, it is not defined on every $L^2$ function: the usual <self-adjoint operator> realization is the generator of unitary rotations about the third axis. Thus the integration statement includes its necessary domain/boundary assumptions.

{parent=16b}

= Solution

Take a normalized state for which the indicated variances and operator pairings exist. Set $A=\hat L_3-\langle\hat L_3\rangle$ and $B=\hat x_1-\langle\hat x_1\rangle$. For real $\lambda$, positivity of the squared <norm> gives
$$
0\le\|(A+i\lambda B)\psi\|^2=(\Delta_\psi L_3)^2+\lambda^2(\Delta_\psi x_1)^2+i\lambda\langle[\hat L_3,\hat x_1]\rangle.
$$
The <canonical commutation relations> yield $[\hat L_3,\hat x_1]=i\hbar\hat x_2$. Thus the quadratic polynomial in $\lambda$ is nonnegative everywhere only if its discriminant is nonpositive. This gives the <Robertson uncertainty principle>
$$
\boxed{\Delta_\psi\hat L_3\,\Delta_\psi\hat x_1\ge\frac\hbar2|\langle\hat x_2\rangle_\psi|,\qquad M=\frac\hbar2|\langle\hat x_2\rangle_\psi|.}
$$
If either variance is zero, the same polynomial argument forces the commutator expectation to vanish, so that edge case is included. Centering the operators is essential to obtain variances rather than uncentered second moments.

Use the radial exponential $e^{-\alpha r}$ shown in the original PDF; the TeX's $e^{-\alpha x}$ is a transcription defect. Every orbital <angular momentum operator> annihilates a radial function, so it acts only on $P=x_1+x_2+2x_3$. The <angular momentum of a radial function times a linear polynomial> is determined by its degree-one angular factor: direct application of the rotation derivatives gives
$$
\hat L^2P=2\hbar^2P.
$$
Equivalently its restriction to the sphere is a degree-one <spherical harmonic>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\hat L^2\psi=2\hbar^2\psi,\qquad l(l+1)=2,\quad l=1.}

= Solution

Let the smaller charge be $q$ at $\mathbf a$ and the other charge be $-Q$ at $\mathbf b$, where $0<q<Q$; an overall sign reversal changes no zero-potential locus. Put $\lambda=q/Q\in(0,1)$. The <electrostatic potential> vanishes exactly when $|\mathbf x-\mathbf a|=\lambda|\mathbf x-\mathbf b|$. Completing the square gives the <Apollonius sphere>
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf x-\mathbf c|^2=R^2,\qquad \mathbf c=\frac{\mathbf a-\lambda^2\mathbf b}{1-\lambda^2},\quad R=\frac{\lambda|\mathbf a-\mathbf b|}{1-\lambda^2}.}
$$
The distances from its center to the charge positions are $|\mathbf c-\mathbf a|=\lambda R<R$ and $|\mathbf c-\mathbf b|=R/\lambda>R$. Thus it encloses exactly the charge of smaller magnitude. Unequal magnitudes are essential: the equal-magnitude case instead gives the perpendicular bisector plane.

= ii
{parent=17c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $R=|\mathbf p|>a$. In SI units, the exterior <electrostatic potential> solves
$$
\Delta\Phi=-\frac q{\varepsilon_0}\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\quad(|\mathbf x|>a),\qquad
\Phi=0\quad(|\mathbf x|=a),\qquad \Phi\to0\quad(|\mathbf x|\to\infty),
$$
with the point-charge singularity $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|\mathbf x-\mathbf p|)$. The <image charge for a grounded conducting sphere> choices
$$
\boxed{\mathbf p'=\frac{a^2}{R^2}\mathbf p,\qquad q'=-\frac aR q}
$$
lie inside the sphere. On $|\mathbf x|=a$, direct squaring gives $|\mathbf x-\mathbf p'|=(a/R)|\mathbf x-\mathbf p|$. Therefore
$$
\Phi(\mathbf x)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac q{|\mathbf x-\mathbf p|}+\frac{q'}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf p'|}\right)
$$
satisfies the source equation, grounded boundary value and decay at infinity. The <uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem>, applied to the harmonic difference with its singularity removed, identifies this with the physical exterior potential. Thus its gradient gives the required exterior <electric field>.

The charge feels the induced field, equivalently the field of the <image charge>, excluding its own singular field. Their separation is $(R^2-a^2)/R$, so <Coulomb's law> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\frac{q^2aR}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(R^2-a^2)^2}\,\frac{\mathbf p}{R}.}
$$
Its magnitude is $q^2aR/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(R^2-a^2)^2]$ and its direction is toward the sphere's center, independent of the sign of $q$. No factor one-half belongs in this force; that factor occurs in induced electrostatic energy instead.

{parent=Paper 3}

{title2=Fluid Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=18D}

For constant-density incompressible inviscid flow with conservative body force, <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> give $\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\nabla(p/\rho+\Phi)$. Using $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)-\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega$, take the <curl> to obtain $\partial_t\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega)$. Since both divergences vanish, the vector identity expands this to
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
Without incompressibility or the constant-density/barotropic hypothesis, extra compression or baroclinic terms are present; the printed equation uses these flow assumptions.

Let $\kappa(t)=\dot h/h$, with $h>0$. Axisymmetric <mass conservation> is $r^{-1}\partial_r(ru_r)+\partial_zu_z=0$. If $u_r$ is independent of $z$, then $u_z$ is affine in $z$. The impermeable moving-plate conditions $u_z(r,\pm h,t)=\pm\dot h$ force $u_z=\kappa z$, and integrating the radial equation gives $u_r=-\kappa r/2+C(t)/r$. Regularity at the axis excludes $C(t)/r$. The meridional part of the <axisymmetric inviscid flow between moving parallel plates> is therefore
$$
\boxed{u_r=-\frac{\dot h}{2h}r,\qquad u_z=\frac{\dot h}{h}z.}
$$
For no swirl, $u_\theta=0$ completes the field. Continuity alone does not determine azimuthal motion; this must be specified separately. More generally, prescribe a smooth regular initial profile $U(r)$ independent of $z$. The azimuthal part of <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> is
$$
\partial_tu_\theta-\frac{\kappa r}{2}\partial_ru_\theta-\frac\kappa2u_\theta=0,
$$
so conservation of $r u_\theta$ along radial particle paths gives $u_\theta(r,t)=\lambda U(\lambda r)$, where $\lambda=\sqrt{h/h_0}$. Together with the meridional components above, this gives a family of complete fields, with a suitable <pressure> determined by the radial and vertical equations. The field is therefore not unique without rotational data.

With the stated solid-body rotation, $u_\theta=\Omega(t)r$ and $\boldsymbol\omega=2\Omega\mathbf e_z$. The <vorticity equation> gives $2\dot\Omega=2\Omega\partial_zu_z=2\Omega\kappa$, hence
$$
\boxed{\Omega(t)=\Omega_0\frac{h(t)}{h_0},\qquad
\mathbf u=-\frac{\dot h}{2h}r\,\mathbf e_r+\Omega_0\frac h{h_0}r\,\mathbf e_\theta+\frac{\dot h}{h}z\,\mathbf e_z.}
$$
For a fluid particle, $\dot r=-\kappa r/2$, so $r^2h$ is constant and $r^2\Omega$ is constant. This is <conservation of angular momentum> about the axis: radial spreading when the plates approach decreases the angular velocity. The meridional and rotating fields satisfy the inviscid equations with an appropriate pressure; no tangential no-slip condition is imposed on inviscid plates.

{title2=Numerical Analysis}

= Solution
{parent=19C}

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_k$ be the distinct interior roots of $p_n$ where its sign changes, and put $q(x)=\prod_{j=1}^k(x-x_j)$. If $k<n$, <orthogonality> gives $\int_a^bw p_nq=0$. But $p_nq$ has constant sign on $(a,b)$, apart from its isolated zeros, because every sign change has been canceled. It is not identically zero, and the positive weight makes its integral nonzero, a contradiction. Thus $k\ge n$. Degree $n$ forces exactly $n$ distinct simple roots, all inside $(a,b)$.

For distinct nodes, let $\ell_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}(x-c_j)/(c_i-c_j)$. <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> gives $f=\sum_i f(c_i)\ell_i$ for every polynomial of degree at most $n-1$. Integrating this identity proves exactness with $b_i=\int_a^bw\ell_i$.

For the <Gaussian quadrature> nodes, divide any $f\in\mathbb P_{2n-1}$ as $f=qp_n+r$, with $q,r\in\mathbb P_{n-1}$. The integral of $qp_n$ vanishes by <orthogonality>, and its nodal values vanish because $p_n(c_i)=0$. The remaining polynomial $r$ is integrated exactly by the preceding interpolation result. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{the n-node Gaussian rule is exact through degree }2n-1.}
$$
Its weights are positive: exactness applied to $\ell_i^2$ gives $b_i=\int_a^bw\ell_i^2>0$.

Define the error functional $L(f)=\int_a^bw(x)f(x)\,dx-\sum_ib_if(c_i)$. It annihilates polynomials of degree at most $2n-1$. For $f\in C^{2n}[a,b]$, the <Peano kernel theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{L(f)=\int_a^bK(t)f^{(2n)}(t)\,dt,}
$$
where a formal <Peano kernel> with the normalization made explicit is
$$
K(t)=\frac1{(2n-1)!}\left[\int_t^bw(x)(x-t)^{2n-1}\,dx-\sum_i b_i(c_i-t)_+^{2n-1}\right].
$$
No evaluation of $K$ is needed. The supplied inner product presupposes an integrable positive weight on the finite interval, so the integral and point-evaluation error functional is bounded on continuous functions.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <Gauss-Markov theorem> states that with full-column-rank design, mean-zero errors and covariance $\sigma^2I$, the <ordinary least squares> estimator is the best linear unbiased estimator: its covariance is no larger than any other such estimator's in the <Loewner order>. Normality is unnecessary for that theorem.

In the given <normal linear model>, maximizing the <likelihood function> is equivalent to minimizing $\|Y-X\beta\|^2$. The <normal equations> and full rank give
$$
\boxed{\widehat\beta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY,\qquad\mathbb E\widehat\beta=\beta,\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}.}
$$
For a direct variance comparison, write $\beta^*=AY$. Unbiasedness for every $\beta$ requires $AX=I_p$. Set $B=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T$ and $D=A-B$, so $DX=0$. The cross covariance terms vanish because $BD^T=(X^TX)^{-1}(DX)^T=0$. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\beta^*)=\sigma^2[(X^TX)^{-1}+DD^T],\qquad
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(t^T\beta^*)-\operatorname{Var}(t^T\widehat\beta)=\sigma^2\|D^Tt\|^2\ge0.}
$$
This proves the requested comparison for every contrast $t$ without relying only on the theorem's name.

{parent=20h}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write the one-column design as $x\in\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}$ and $S=x^Tx$. Maximizing the <normal linear model> likelihood first gives $\widehat\beta=x^TY/S$. Its minimized <residual sum of squares> is $R=\|Y-x\widehat\beta\|^2$. For variance $v>0$, the remaining log likelihood is $-\tfrac n2\log v-R/(2v)$ plus a constant, whose maximum is at
$$
\boxed{\widehat\sigma^2=\frac Rn.}
$$
Here $R>0$ almost surely because $n>1$ and the true variance is positive. In the exceptional data case $R=0$, the likelihood is unbounded as $v\downarrow0$, so there is no positive-variance maximum.

Let $P=xx^T/S$, the <orthogonal projection matrix> onto the design column. Then $\widehat\beta-\beta=x^T\varepsilon/S$ and $Y-x\widehat\beta=(I-P)\varepsilon$. These are orthogonal linear projections of an isotropic <multivariate normal distribution>, hence independent. An orthonormal coordinate system adapted to $x$ makes the residual squared length a sum of $n-1$ independent squared standard normals. Thus the <joint distribution of least-squares and variance estimators> is the product law
$$
\boxed{\widehat\beta\sim N\!\left(\beta,\frac{\sigma^2}{S}\right),\qquad \frac{n\widehat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1},\qquad\widehat\beta\ \text{and}\ \widehat\sigma^2\ \text{are independent}.}
$$
In particular the maximum likelihood variance estimator is biased: $\mathbb E\widehat\sigma^2=(n-1)\sigma^2/n$; the unbiased residual estimator uses denominator $n-1$.

{title2=Optimisation}

= a
{parent=21h}

= Solution
{parent=a}

In a <matrix game>, entry $a_{ij}$ is the row player's payoff, and the column player's payoff is $-a_{ij}$. A <mixed strategy> is a probability vector over the available rows or columns. With strategies $p,q$, the row payoff is $p^TAq$. Its <optimal mixed strategy> maximizes its guaranteed payoff, while the column player minimizes the largest row payoff:
$$
\boxed{\max_{p\in\Delta_m}\min_{q\in\Delta_n}p^TAq
=\min_{q\in\Delta_n}\max_{p\in\Delta_m}p^TAq=v.}
$$
The equality is the finite <minimax theorem>. Equivalently the row player maximizes $\min_j(p^TA)_j$, and the column player minimizes $\max_i(Aq)_i$. Each optimal strategy guarantees its corresponding bound against every opponent strategy; for the column player's own negative payoff this is also a maximin strategy.

= b
{parent=21h}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a real <antisymmetric matrix> $A$, $p^TAp=0$ for every real vector $p$. Thus for each row strategy $p$, the column player could choose $q=p$, giving $\min_qp^TAq\le0$. Hence the row security value is at most zero. Conversely, for each column strategy $q$, the row choice $p=q$ gives $\max_pp^TAq\ge0$, so the column security value is at least zero. The <minimax theorem> equates these values, proving
$$
\boxed{v=0.}
$$
The choices matching the opponent in this proof establish inequalities for security levels; they do not by themselves specify a single universal optimal strategy.

In this <consecutive-number antisymmetric game>, with the row player's number denoted $i$, the <payoff matrix> is
$$
a_{ij}=\begin{cases}
0,&i=j,\\
1,&i=j+1,\\
-1,&j=i+1,\\

\end{cases}

satisfies $Ap=0$ and $p^TA=0$, proving optimality for both players.

$$
\boxed{v=0,\qquad p=q=\left(\frac14,\frac12,\frac14,0,\ldots,0\right).}
