Use the positive-exponent <quantum Fourier transform> convention $F_N|x\rangle=N^{-1/2}\sum_t e^{2\pi ixt/N}|t\rangle$. The <period of a function> here is the least positive translation leaving the function unchanged on the <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z_N$. The <injective> promise means $f(x)=f(x')$ exactly when $x\equiv x'\pmod r$. In particular $r\mid N$: the subgroup of period translations is generated by $r$, and wrapping the argument by $N$ preserves its value. This is exact <quantum period finding> on a <finite cyclic group>, rather than the truncated, nondividing-period version requiring <continued fractions>.

Prepare a <uniform superposition state> in the first <quantum register> using $F_N|0\rangle$, and perform a <reversible computation> of the function into a second <quantum register>, cleaning its workspace by <uncomputation>:
$$
\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{x=0}^{N-1}|x\rangle|0\rangle\longmapsto\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{x=0}^{N-1}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle.
$$
A <quantum measurement in the computational basis> of the second <quantum register> leaves a <periodic coset state> in the first:
$$
|C_a\rangle=\sqrt{\frac rN}\sum_{j=0}^{N/r-1}|a+jr\rangle,\qquad 0\leq a<r.
$$
For the <quantum Fourier transform> of this <periodic coset state>, the <finite geometric series> vanishes unless the measured label is $t=mN/r$. On those labels its normalized amplitude is $r^{-1/2}e^{2\pi iam/r}$. Thus $m$ has the <uniform distribution on a finite set> on $\{0,\ldots,r-1\}$, irrespective of $a$.

Compute the <greatest common divisor> by the <Euclidean algorithm>, and set $q=N/\gcd(t,N)$, with $\gcd(0,N)=N$. Since $t=mN/r$,
$$
q=\frac r{\gcd(m,r)}.
$$
The <exact period recovery from a Fourier sample> is therefore certified by testing $f(q\bmod N)=f(0)$. Every candidate $q$ divides $r$ and lies between $1$ and $r$, so the <injective> promise makes that equality equivalent to $q=r$. This includes $r=N$, where the test uses input $0$, and $r=1$, which always succeeds. The test does not assert that an arbitrary equality of function values certifies an arbitrary period; the Fourier-derived divisor restriction is essential.

The concise result is
$$
\boxed{q=\frac N{\gcd(t,N)},\quad \text{accept iff }f(q\bmod N)=f(0),\quad P_{\mathrm{accept}}=\frac{\varphi(r)}r.}
$$
Here $\varphi$ is the <Euler totient function>, with $\varphi(1)=1$. The <totient lower bound from the Mertens product> gives $\varphi(r)/r=\Omega(1/\max\{1,\log\log N\})$ for $r\leq N$. Each trial uses <polynomial time> <quantum Fourier transforms>, function evaluations and <Euclidean algorithm> arithmetic in $\log N$. Repeating $O(\max\{1,\log\log N\})$ independent trials gives constant success probability; the herald accepts only correct answers.

**The printed asymptotic bound has the wrong direction for this argument.** In <Big O notation>, the stated upper bound on the number of <coprime integers> supplies no success lower bound and is itself false uniformly: for prime $N$, $\varphi(N)=N-1$, not $O(N/\log\log N)$. Likewise the single-trial success need not be $O(1/\log\log N)$, since it tends to one for $r=N$ prime. The meaningful corrected guarantee is the lower bound above. If an upper-bounded success rate is literally required, one may deliberately retain an accepted result with an independent probability proportional to $1/\max\{1,\log\log N\}$; this discards useful successes and is unnecessary for efficient recovery.

First require $x\neq0$. A <discrete logarithm> to a <generator of a group> of $\mathbb Z_p^*$ exists only for nonzero $x$; the printed membership $x\in\mathbb Z_p$ inadvertently also permits zero, for which $x^{-b}$ is undefined. Zero can be rejected before any <quantum circuit>.

Put $M=p-1$ and write $x=g^y$. Since $g$ is a <generator of a group> of order $M$, the map $k\mapsto g^k$ is a <bijection> from $\mathbb Z_M$ to the <multiplicative group of a finite field>. Hence every nonzero $c$ has a unique $k\in\mathbb Z_M$ with $c=g^k$. Equality of powers of $g$ is equality of their exponents modulo $M$, giving
$$
\boxed{f(a,b)=g^{a-by}=c\ \Longleftrightarrow\ a-by\equiv k\pmod M.}
$$
The <fiber> over $c$ is an affine <coset> of the <subgroup> $\{(yb,b):b\in\mathbb Z_M\}$, and contains exactly $M$ pairs. This identifies the <hidden subgroup problem> underlying <discrete-logarithm Fourier sampling>; knowing $k$ numerically is not needed to construct the <coset state>.

Apply the same positive-exponent <quantum Fourier transform> to each of the two <quantum registers>. The amplitude at $(c_1,c_2)$ of the <coset state> is
$$
\frac{e^{2\pi i k_0c_1/M}}{M\sqrt M}\sum_{b=0}^{M-1}e^{2\pi ib(yc_1+c_2)/M}.
$$
The <finite geometric series> is $M$ when $yc_1+c_2\equiv0\pmod M$ and zero otherwise. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c_2\equiv-yc_1\pmod M,\qquad P(c_1,c_2)=\frac1M\text{ on this support}.}
$$
There are $M$ allowed pairs, with $c_1$ following the <uniform distribution on a finite set>. The factor $e^{2\pi i k_0c_1/M}$ changes the <probability amplitudes> by phases depending on the output label $c_1$, but has no effect on their <probabilities>. Using an inverse rather than forward <quantum Fourier transform> on both <quantum registers> gives the same support relation.

A <modular inverse> of $c_1$ exists precisely when $\gcd(c_1,M)=1$. In that case the <discrete logarithm> is
$$
\boxed{y\equiv-c_2c_1^{-1}\pmod M.}
$$
It cannot generally be determined from every allowed pair. Put $d=\gcd(c_1,M)$. The supported <linear congruence> has $d$ solutions modulo $M$, and determines only $y$ modulo $M/d$; $(0,0)$ gives no information when $M>1$. For instance $p=5$ and $(c_1,c_2)=(2,2)$ are compatible with both $y=1$ and $y=3$. One may verify a candidate by <modular exponentiation>, checking $g^y=x$. Repeated <discrete-logarithm Fourier sampling> finds an invertible $c_1$ with per-trial probability $\varphi(M)/M$, which has the same <totient lower bound from the Mertens product> as before. When $p=2$, the group is trivial and $x=1,y=0$ are determined without Fourier samples.

Assume first $0<k<N$. Define the orthonormal <quantum states>
$$
|B\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{N-k}}\sum_{x\notin G}|x\rangle,\qquad |G\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{x\in G}|x\rangle,
$$
and $\theta\in(0,\pi/2)$ by $\sin\theta=\sqrt{k/N}$. The relevant <invariant subspace> is the two-dimensional <span> of these <quantum states>, not the entire <Hilbert space>. The <uniform quantum superposition> is $|\psi_0\rangle=\cos\theta|B\rangle+\sin\theta|G\rangle$.

In the ordered <orthonormal basis> $(|B\rangle,|G\rangle)$, the <marked-state phase oracle> is the <reflection operator> $I_G=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$. Since $I_0=I-2|0^n\rangle\langle0^n|$, conjugation by the <Walsh-Hadamard transform> gives the <Grover diffusion operator>
$$
D=-H_nI_0H_n=2|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|-I.
$$
It is the reflection in the line through $|\psi_0\rangle$. The product of these two <reflection operators> is a <rotation matrix> by $2\theta$ toward the marked <quantum state>:
$$
Q=DI_G=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta&-\sin2\theta\\\sin2\theta&\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q^j|\psi_0\rangle=\cos((2j+1)\theta)|B\rangle+\sin((2j+1)\theta)|G\rangle.
$$
Thus the <Born rule> gives $P_j=\sin^2((2j+1)\theta)$. Choose a nonnegative integer $j_*$ nearest to $\pi/(4\theta)-1/2$. Rounding changes the angle from $\pi/2$ by at most $\theta$, so
$$
\boxed{P_{j_*}\geq\cos^2\theta=1-\frac kN,\qquad j_*=O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac Nk}\right).}
$$
Each <Grover search algorithm> iteration uses one <marked-state phase oracle> query; all other gates are known. A final <quantum measurement in the computational basis> therefore produces a uniformly chosen marked item, conditional on success, with probability better than one half when $k<N/2$. This uses the supplied marked count $k$ to select the stopping time; an unknown $k$ needs an additional search schedule or counting procedure. If $k=N$, no oracle query is needed; if $k=0$, no marked item exists, so a successful search is impossible.

Interpret a <function collision> as two distinct inputs with equal outputs. Without distinctness the request is vacuous, since an input paired with itself always qualifies. Set $m=\lceil N^{1/3}\rceil$ (handling small $N$ by direct queries), choose a known set $A$ of size $m$, and query all its inputs. Store a classical table of their outputs and corresponding inputs. If this table contains a <function collision>, return it immediately.

Otherwise the table contains $m$ distinct outputs. Because $g$ is exactly two-to-one, each $a\in A$ has exactly one partner in $B$, the known complement of $A$. None lies in $A$, and different table outputs have different partners. Thus exactly $m$ inputs in $B$, of size $L=N-m$, satisfy the known-table membership predicate
$$
h(b)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(b)\in\{g(a):a\in A\}.
$$
Build a <marked-state phase oracle> for this predicate by a <compute-phase-uncompute construction>: use $U_g$ on a clean answer <quantum register>, reversibly compare its output against the classical table, apply the conditional minus sign, undo the comparison, and apply $U_g$ again. Since the answer operation is bitwise <exclusive or>, $U_g^{-1}=U_g$. All workspace returns to zero, and each phase query costs two calls to $U_g$.

Perform <known-subset Grover search> in the <span> of the <computational basis> states labelled by $B$, starting in their <uniform superposition state>. Reflection about this known state uses no $U_g$ queries; choosing $A$ as an initial interval makes $B$ a known interval that can also be encoded by its own $L$ labels. Its marked fraction is $m/L$, so the previous <Grover rotation angle> analysis uses $O(\sqrt{L/m})$ phase queries. On measuring $b$, one further $U_g$ query checks the output and retrieves its partner $a$ from the table. The inputs are distinct because the two sets are disjoint.

Consequently the <quantum query complexity> is
$$
\boxed{m+O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{N-m}{m}}\right)+1=O(N^{1/3}).}
$$
For large $N$, rounding the optimal <Grover search algorithm> iteration count gives success at least $1-m/(N-m)>1/2$. An internal table <function collision> already gives certain success. The balance $m\asymp\sqrt{N/m}$ explains the cube-root choice. This is the <Brassard–Høyer–Tapp collision algorithm>: its table uses $O(m\log N)$ classical bits. The bound counts calls to $U_g$, as requested; it does not charge table lookup and its reversible implementation as constant-time gates, or claim an overall polynomial time in $\log N$.

Use <exact quantum phase estimation> with $n$ control <qubits> initially $|0^n\rangle$ and one target <quantum register> prepared in the given <eigenvector> $|\psi\rangle$. The target stays in $|\psi\rangle$, while <quantum phase kickback> produces
$$
\frac1{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^n-1}e^{2\pi ix\phi}|x\rangle|\psi\rangle.
$$
Since $\phi=c/2^n$, this is $F_{2^n}|c\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle$. The inverse <quantum Fourier transform> on the control <quantum register> therefore gives $|c\rangle|\psi\rangle$. The <quantum measurement in the computational basis> returns $c$ with certainty, so
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac c{2^n}\quad\text{with success probability }1.}
$$

Let $V$ denote the coherent <exact quantum phase estimation> <unitary operator> from the preceding part, with the data <quantum register> written first. For every <eigenvector> of $A$,
$$
V|u_j\rangle|0^n\rangle=|u_j\rangle|c_j\rangle,

because $e^{2\pi iA}|u_j\rangle=e^{2\pi i\lambda_j}|u_j\rangle$ and $\lambda_j=c_j/2^n$ lies in $[0,1)$, with no phase-aliasing ambiguity. Apply $V$ coherently to the input <quantum state> and a clean <quantum register>; do not measure the <eigenvalue> label. By <linearity> it produces $\sum_j\beta_j|u_j\rangle|c_j\rangle$.

Append a <quantum ancilla> in $|0\rangle$, and use the provided <quantum variable rotation> with $\theta_{c_j}=\arcsin\lambda_j$, choosing the nonnegative cosine. The resulting normalized <quantum state> is
$$
\boxed{\sum_j\beta_j|u_j\rangle|c_j\rangle\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda_j^2}|0\rangle+\lambda_j|1\rangle\right).}
$$
The sum covers both terms; this fixes the potentially ambiguous sum placement in the abbreviated display. The <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors> and the unit length of each rotated <quantum ancilla> show that its <norm> is one. This is <quantum spectral filtering> by multiplication, not the reciprocal rotation used in the <HHL algorithm>.

Apply $V^{-1}$ to the data and phase <quantum registers>, leaving the flag <quantum ancilla> untouched. This inverse needs no extra oracle assumption: the promised dyadic <eigenvalues> give $U^{2^n}=I$, hence $U^{-1}=U^{2^n-1}$. The inverse of each <controlled unitary gate> used in $V$ can therefore be built from repeated uses of the supplied controlled-$U$, and the known <Hadamard gates> and <quantum Fourier transform> gates can be reversed. <Uncomputation> is necessary to erase the <eigenvalue> label coherently. The resulting <quantum state> is
$$
\sum_j\beta_j|u_j\rangle|0^n\rangle\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda_j^2}|0\rangle+\lambda_j|1\rangle\right).
$$
A <quantum measurement in the computational basis> of the flag followed by <postselection> on one yields
$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle=\frac{A|b\rangle}{\|A|b\rangle\|},\qquad P_{\mathrm{success}}=\sum_j|\beta_j|^2\lambda_j^2=\|A|b\rangle\|^2.}
$$
This requires $A|b\rangle\neq0$. For a normalized input and nonnegative <eigenvalues>, the <success probability of positive quantum spectral filtering> satisfies $P_{\mathrm{success}}\geq\lambda_{\min}^2$. In the general inequality, equality holds precisely when the input is supported on the minimum-<eigenvalue> <eigenspace>. Omitting <uncomputation> and discarding the phase <quantum register> would instead leave a <mixed state> with diagonal weights proportional to $|\beta_j|^2\lambda_j^2$, rather than the desired coherent <pure state>.

**The printed universal nonzero-success request needs a nonkernel-input hypothesis.** An $n$-<qubit> <Hermitian operator> has $2^n$ <eigenvalues>, counted with multiplicity. All are distinct, and the printed dyadic grid contains exactly $2^n$ possible values. They therefore occupy the entire grid, including zero: this is a <multiplicity-free complete dyadic spectrum>, and $\lambda_{\min}=0$. Taking $n=1$, $A=\operatorname{diag}(0,1/2)$, and $|b\rangle=|0\rangle$ satisfies every printed spectral promise but gives $A|b\rangle=0$. No normalized output vector exists, so no algorithm can deliver it with nonzero probability.

For every input outside the <kernel>, the procedure above has $P_{\mathrm{success}}>0=\lambda_{\min}^2$, giving the requested strict bound on the meaningful domain. The corrected result is therefore exact <quantum spectral filtering> conditional on $A|b\rangle\neq0$, with the boxed probability. This does not require knowing the input <probability amplitudes> or making extra copies of an unknown <quantum state>. Arbitrarily small nonzero support outside the <kernel> gives arbitrarily small success probability, and a failed flag measurement disturbs the input; fresh independent trials cannot be assumed when only one unknown physical input is supplied.

The basic <one-bit teleportation> primitive uses the input as <qubit> 1 and a fresh $|+\rangle$ <quantum ancilla> as <qubit> 2. Apply the <controlled-Z gate> $E_{12}$ and measure <qubit> 1 in the <equatorial qubit measurement> basis with angle $\alpha$. If $s\in\{0,1\}$ is the result, the normalized <post-measurement state> on <qubit> 2 is
$$
\boxed{X_2^sJ(\alpha)_2|\psi\rangle.}
$$
Both results have probability one half. For example, writing $|\psi\rangle=a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle$, the unnormalized branch is $(a|+\rangle+(-1)^se^{i\alpha}b|-\rangle)/\sqrt2=X^sJ(\alpha)|\psi\rangle/\sqrt2$. Thus $J(\alpha)$ is applied deterministically as a logical gate with known <Pauli frame> $X^s$; a permitted conditional <Pauli X gate> would remove this byproduct physically.

The literal resource restriction does not need an unstated direct <Pauli X gate>. A <Pauli Z gate> can be enacted by applying $E$ to the data and a fixed $|1\rangle$ <quantum ancilla>. Two consecutive angle-zero <one-bit teleportations>, with results $p,q$, map an arbitrary current state $|\chi\rangle$ to $X^qH X^pH|\chi\rangle=X^qZ^p|\chi\rangle$. Apply the available $Z^p$ to remove the latter factor, up to an irrelevant <global phase>. This implements a heralded $X^q$: if $q=0$, the input is unchanged; if $q=1$, the required <Pauli X gate> has been applied.

If the original result is $s=1$ and a physically corrected output is required, repeat this <heralded Pauli X correction using controlled-Z and measurements> until $q=1$. Each attempt succeeds with probability one half independent of the input, so it terminates with probability one, using two attempts on average. Only $E$, fixed ancillary <quantum states> and single-<qubit> <quantum measurements> are used. This exact physical correction has no finite worst-case measurement bound; the standard finite deterministic realization is the logical <Pauli frame> version, which suffices for the later output-simulation parts.

The <logical depth of a measurement pattern> counts sequential layers of <quantum measurements> forced by dependence of measurement bases on earlier outcomes. Depth one means every basis can be fixed before measurements start, allowing all <quantum measurements> on distinct <qubits> to be performed in parallel. This does not require depth-one resource-state preparation or constant-depth classical parity processing.

Suppose the circuit contains $m$ gates $J(\alpha_1),\ldots,J(\alpha_m)$ in time order, with each $\alpha_j\in\{0,\pi/2\}$. Prepare an $(m+1)$-<vertex> path <graph state> from $|+\rangle^{\otimes(m+1)}$ and <controlled-Z gates> between neighbours. Its first <qubit> supplies the specified input $|+\rangle$. Measure <vertex> $j-1$ in the fixed <equatorial qubit measurement> basis at angle $\alpha_j$ for $j=1,\ldots,m$, obtaining $s_j$. Measure the final <vertex> in the <computational basis>, obtaining $d$.

Track the <Pauli frame> as $X^{a_j}Z^{b_j}$, starting with $a_0=b_0=0$. A hypothetical sequential reading of the same <quantum measurements> gives the step $X^{s_j}J(\alpha_j)X^{a_{j-1}}Z^{b_{j-1}}$. The supplied commutation rules imply, up to <global phase>,
$$
X^{s_j}J(\alpha_j)X^aZ^b=X^{s_j\oplus b}Z^aJ((-1)^a\alpha_j).
$$
For $\alpha_j=0$, the sign is irrelevant. For $\alpha_j=\pi/2$, the identity $J(-\pi/2)=XJ(\pi/2)$ absorbs the possible sign change into the <Pauli frame>. If $t_j=0$ for $\alpha_j=0$ and $t_j=1$ for $\alpha_j=\pi/2$, the update is
$$
\boxed{a_j=s_j\oplus b_{j-1}\oplus t_ja_{j-1},\qquad b_j=a_{j-1},\qquad k=d\oplus a_m.}
$$
