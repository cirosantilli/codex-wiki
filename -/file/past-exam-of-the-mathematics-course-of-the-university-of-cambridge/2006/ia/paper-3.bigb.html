= Solution

Let $c=1/\sqrt2$. A concrete real <matrix> is
$$
\boxed{A_0=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\0&c&-c\\0&c&c\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Its first coordinate has <eigenvalue> $-1$. The lower block has <characteristic polynomial> $(\lambda-c)^2+c^2$, whose roots are $c\pm ic=(1\pm i)/\sqrt2$. Thus this example has all three required <eigenvalues>. It is also an <orthogonal matrix>, but orthogonality is not forced by its spectrum, as the second part shows.

= Solution
{parent=i}

**True.** All three <eigenvalues> are distinct in $\mathbb C$, so their nonzero <eigenvectors> are <linearly independent>. Here is a direct proof of that assertion. If $a_1v_1+a_2v_2+a_3v_3=0$, apply $(A-\lambda_2I)(A-\lambda_3I)$ to obtain
$$
a_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)(\lambda_1-\lambda_3)v_1=0.
$$
Distinctness makes both scalar factors nonzero, hence $a_1=0$. Applying the analogous products gives $a_2=a_3=0$. The three <eigenvectors> therefore form a <basis> of $\mathbb C^3$. Taking them as the columns of $P$ gives
$$
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=\operatorname{diag}\left(-1,\frac{1+i}{\sqrt2},\frac{1-i}{\sqrt2}\right)}.
$$
This proves that every such matrix is a <diagonalizable matrix> over $\mathbb C$, rather than merely showing it for the example.

**False.** Consider the real <matrix>
$$
A_1=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\0&c&-c\\0&c&c\end{pmatrix},\qquad c=\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$
It is block upper triangular, so its <characteristic polynomial> is the same as that of $A_0$ and it has the required <eigenvalues>. Its second column, however, has squared <Euclidean norm> $1+c^2+c^2=2$, whereas every column of an <orthogonal matrix> has norm one. Thus
$$
\boxed{A_1^{\mathsf T}A_1\ne I}.
$$
Unit-modulus <eigenvalues> alone do not imply orthogonality; they do not control the geometry of the <eigenvectors>.

**False; in fact no matrix with this spectrum is diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$.** If a real invertible $P$ gave $P^{-1}AP=D$ with $D$ a real <diagonal matrix>, all <eigenvalues> of $A$ would be the real diagonal entries of $D$. But two of the required <eigenvalues> have nonzero imaginary parts. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is not diagonalizable over }\mathbb R}.
$$
The real block form in the initial example is consistent with this: a two-dimensional rotation block is not a real diagonalization.

Both <subgroups> contain the <identity element>, so $H\cap K$ is nonempty. If $x,y\in H\cap K$, then $xy^{-1}$ belongs to both $H$ and $K$, hence to their intersection. This gives closure under inverses by setting $x=e$, and closure under products by using the inverse of $y$. Therefore **$H\cap K$ is a subgroup of $G$**, and also of both $H$ and $K$.

<Lagrange's theorem for finite groups> states that the order of a <subgroup> divides the order of its finite ambient <group>; more precisely $|L|=[L:M]|M|$ for $M\le L$. Applying it twice gives
$$
|H\cap K|\mid p,\qquad |H\cap K|\mid q.
$$
Since $p$ and $q$ are <coprime>, their only positive common divisor is one. Thus
$$
\boxed{H\cap K=\{e\}}.
$$

Take the <unit normal> to point outwards. The <divergence> of the field is
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=(9x^2-2x)y+(3y^2-4y+1)x+2z.
$$
The <divergence theorem> turns the closed <surface integral> into a volume integral over $[0,1]^3$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1
\left[(9x^2-2x)y+(3y^2-4y+1)x+2z\right]dx\,dy\,dz\\
&=(3-1)\frac12+\frac12(1-2+1)+1
=\boxed{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For a direct check of the <surface integral>, on $x=1$ the outward component is $F_x=2y$, contributing $\int_0^1\int_0^1 2y\,dy\,dz=1$. On $x=0$ it vanishes. On both $y=0$ and $y=1$, $F_y=x y(y-1)^2=0$. On $z=1$, $F_z=0$, whereas on $z=0$ the field component is $F_z=-1$ and the outward normal is $-\widehat{\mathbf z}$, giving a contribution $1$. The six face contributions are therefore $1,0,0,0,0,1$, again totaling **2**. The positive bottom-face contribution is a consequence of its outward orientation.

= Solution
{parent=4A}

Use <suffix notation>, summing repeated indices, and write $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x_i$. For a twice continuously differentiable <vector field>, mixed derivatives commute. Thus the <divergence> of its <curl> satisfies
$$
\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jv_k)
=\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_i\partial_jv_k=0,
$$
because the derivatives are symmetric in $i,j$, while the <Levi-Civita symbol> is antisymmetric in those indices. Hence
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf v)=0}.
$$
For the second identity, use the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\mathbf v\times(\nabla\times\mathbf v)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}v_j\epsilon_{klm}\partial_lv_m\\
&=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})v_j\partial_lv_m\\
&=v_j\partial_iv_j-v_j\partial_jv_i\\
&=\partial_i\left(\tfrac12v_jv_j\right)-[(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v]_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
Rearranging each component proves the <vorticity cross-product identity>
$$
\boxed{(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v=
\nabla\left(\tfrac12|\mathbf v|^2\right)-\mathbf v\times(\nabla\times\mathbf v)}.
$$
The second identity needs first derivatives; the first additionally uses equality of the mixed second derivatives.

= Solution
{parent=5D}

For the <centralizer of a subset>, the identity commutes with every $h\in A$. If $g,k$ commute with each member of $A$, then for every such $h$,
$$
(gk)h=g(kh)=g(hk)=(gh)k=h(gk).
$$
Also $gh=hg$ implies $g^{-1}h=hg^{-1}$ after multiplication by $g^{-1}$ on both sides. Thus identity, products and inverses lie in $C(A)$, proving that it is a <subgroup>.

For the <conjugation action>, identity acts trivially, and
$$
\rho(g_1,\rho(g_2,h))=g_1g_2h g_2^{-1}g_1^{-1}
=\rho(g_1g_2,h).
$$
These are the defining laws of a left <group action>. Its <orbits of a group action> are the <conjugacy classes>. The stabilizer of $h$ is exactly its <centralizer>, since $ghg^{-1}=h$ if and only if $gh=hg$. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> therefore gives
$$
|O_i|=[G:C(\{h_i\})]=\frac{|G|}{|C(\{h_i\})|}.
$$
A class has one element exactly when that element commutes with all of $G$, so the singleton classes are precisely the elements of $C(G)$, the <centre of a group>. Partitioning $G$ into all its <conjugacy classes> now proves the <class equation>
$$
\boxed{|G|=|C(G)|+\sum_{|O_i|>1}\frac{|G|}{|C(\{h_i\})|}}.
$$
For $|G|=p^r$, <Lagrange's theorem for finite groups> makes every class size a power of $p$. A nonsingleton class has size at least $p$ and is therefore divisible by $p$. Reducing the <class equation> modulo $p$ yields $|C(G)|\equiv0\pmod p$, because $r>0$. The identity lies in $C(G)$, so its order is positive and divisible by $p$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{|C(G)|\ge p>1}.
$$
This proves the <nontrivial center of a finite p-group> directly from the conjugation classes.

= Solution
{parent=6D}

A <group homomorphism> sends identity to identity. For $\theta(g),\theta(h)$ in its image,
$$
\theta(g)\theta(h)^{-1}=\theta(gh^{-1}),
$$
which is still in the image; hence the <image of a group homomorphism> is a <subgroup> of $H$. Let $N=\ker\theta$. It is a <subgroup> by the same identity, and for $k\in N$ and $g\in G$,
$$
\theta(gkg^{-1})=\theta(g)\theta(k)\theta(g)^{-1}=e.
$$
Thus $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$, and replacing $g$ by $g^{-1}$ gives equality, proving that $N$ is a <normal subgroup>.

Define a map from the <quotient group> by
$$
\Phi:G/N\longrightarrow\theta(G),\qquad \Phi(gN)=\theta(g).
$$
If $gN=hN$, then $h^{-1}g\in N$ and $\theta(g)=\theta(h)$, so the map is well defined. Conversely equality of the images implies $h^{-1}g\in N$, giving injectivity. It is onto by the definition of image, and $\Phi((gN)(hN))=\theta(gh)=\theta(g)\theta(h)$. This proves the <first isomorphism theorem for groups> in this case:
$$
\boxed{G/\ker\theta\cong\theta(G)}.
$$
An <orthogonal matrix> $Q$ has $Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I$, so $(\det Q)^2=1$. The <determinant> is therefore a <group homomorphism> $O(3)\to\{1,-1\}$, with image both signs because $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1)$ has determinant $-1$. Its kernel is the <special orthogonal group> $SO(3)$. Hence **$SO(3)$ is normal in $O(3)$**, and
$$
\boxed{O(3)/SO(3)\cong\{1,-1\}\cong C_2}.
$$
For the last request, the <determinant-twist projection in odd dimension> gives an explicit example:
$$
\boxed{\pi:O(3)\longrightarrow SO(3),\qquad \pi(Q)=(\det Q)Q}.
$$
Indeed $\det\pi(Q)=(\det Q)^4=1$, and scalar factors commute, so $\pi(QR)=\pi(Q)\pi(R)$. If $\pi(Q)=I$, then $Q=(\det Q)I$, making $Q=I$ or $Q=-I$. Both lie in the kernel, which is therefore exactly $\{I,-I\}$, of order two. The map also fixes every member of $SO(3)$, so it is onto.

= Solution
{parent=7D}

Since $\sigma$ is a <group homomorphism>, $\sigma(0)=I$, $\sigma(x+y)=\sigma(x)\sigma(y)$, and $\sigma(-x)=\sigma(x)^{-1}$. The product is closed on $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2$. Its two associative bracketings give
$$
\begin{aligned}
[(x,v)*(y,w)]*(z,u)
&=(x+y+z,v+\sigma(x)w+\sigma(x+y)u),\\
(x,v)*[(y,w)*(z,u)]
&=(x+y+z,v+\sigma(x)w+\sigma(x)\sigma(y)u),
\end{aligned}
$$
and coincide by the homomorphism property. The <identity element> is $(0,0)$, and the two-sided inverse is
$$
\boxed{(x,v)^{-1}=(-x,-\sigma(-x)v)}.
$$
For example, multiplying on the right gives $v-\sigma(x)\sigma(-x)v=0$, and multiplying on the left gives $-\sigma(-x)v+\sigma(-x)v=0$. Thus this law defines a <group>, specifically a <semidirect product>.

If it is an <abelian group>, comparing $(x,0)*(0,w)$ and $(0,w)*(x,0)$ gives $\sigma(x)w=w$ for every $w$. Hence $\sigma(x)=I$ for every $x$. Conversely this trivial action makes the product ordinary vector addition. Therefore **the unique action giving a commutative group is**
$$
\boxed{\sigma(x)=I\quad\text{for all }x}.
$$
For the final condition, compare $(0,ae_1)*(x,w)$ and $(x,w)*(0,ae_1)$. They are $(x,ae_1+w)$ and $(x,w+\sigma(x)ae_1)$ respectively. They coincide for every $a,x,w$ exactly when $\sigma(x)e_1=e_1$ for every $x$. The first column of $\sigma(x)$ is then $(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$; since it lies in the <special linear group>, its determinant is one and its other diagonal entry is also one. Thus
$$
\sigma(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&r(x)\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Matrix multiplication gives $r(x+y)=r(x)+r(y)$. Conversely any such <additive function> gives a homomorphism into $SL(2,\mathbb R)$ fixing $e_1$ and therefore makes these translations central. This proves the full characterization of the <central translations in a linear semidirect product>:
$$
\boxed{r:(\mathbb R,+)\longrightarrow(\mathbb R,+)\text{ may be any homomorphism}}.
$$
No continuity or measurability is assumed, so it would be unjustified to restrict the answer to $r(x)=cx$.

= Solution
{parent=8D}

Let $T(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)$, with $ad-bc\ne0$. If $c=0$, then $d\ne0$ and
$$
T(z)=\frac ad z+\frac bd,
$$
a composition of a nonzero scaling and a translation. If $c\ne0$, rearrange the fraction as
$$
T(z)=\frac ac+\frac{bc-ad}{c^2}\frac1{z+d/c}.
$$
Thus every <Möbius transformation> is a composition, in order, of translation by $d/c$, reciprocal inversion, nonzero scaling by $(bc-ad)/c^2$, and translation by $a/c$. All formulas act on the <Riemann sphere>, including zero and infinity. The scaling parameter must be nonzero for that factor to be a <Möbius transformation>.

For three distinct finite points, define the <cross-ratio> normalization
$$
F_z(\zeta)=\frac{(\zeta-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(\zeta-z_3)(z_2-z_1)}.
$$
It sends $z_1,z_2,z_3$ to $0,1,\infty$ respectively. If $z_3=\infty$, use $(\zeta-z_1)/(z_2-z_1)$; if $z_1=\infty$, use $(z_2-z_3)/(\zeta-z_3)$; if $z_2=\infty$, use $(\zeta-z_1)/(\zeta-z_3)$. These are the corresponding limits of the same formula and cover all possible triples. Form the analogous normalization $F_w$ of the target triple. Then
$$
\boxed{T=F_w^{-1}\circ F_z}
$$
has all three specified images, proving existence. For uniqueness, the quotient of any two such maps fixes the three source points. Conjugating by $F_z$ gives a <Möbius transformation> fixing $0,1,\infty$. Fixing zero and infinity forces it to be $\lambda\zeta$, and fixing one forces $\lambda=1$. The quotient is identity, so the two maps are equal.

The <Möbius transformations permuting three points> are therefore in one-to-one correspondence with permutations of the triple. For the normalized set the six maps and their ordered images of $(0,1,\infty)$ are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
T(z)&(T(0),T(1),T(\infty))\\\hline
z&(0,1,\infty)\\
1-z&(1,0,\infty)\\
1/z&(\infty,1,0)\\
1/(1-z)&(1,\infty,0)\\
z/(z-1)&(0,\infty,1)\\
(z-1)/z&(\infty,0,1).
\end{array}
$$
They realize all six permutations. The action map to the <symmetric group> is a homomorphism, is injective by the three-point uniqueness proof, and is onto by this list. Hence
$$
\boxed{G\cong S_3}.
$$

When $\alpha=2\beta$, all three values reduce to $-\beta/3$. To prove <path independence> for every piecewise smooth path, construct a global potential rather than infer it from agreement on only three paths. Integrating the coefficient of $dx$ and then matching the coefficient of $dy$ gives

= Solution
{parent=10A}

For a smooth <vector field> near an oriented piecewise smooth surface $S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf x
=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot d\mathbf S,
$$
where the boundary orientation is induced by the surface <unit normal> through the right-hand rule. For a constant vector $\mathbf k$, the <curl> of $\phi\mathbf k$ is $\nabla\phi\times\mathbf k$. Apply <Stokes theorem> and use the scalar triple product:
$$
\mathbf k\cdot\oint_{\partial S}\phi\,d\mathbf x
=\int_S(\nabla\phi\times\mathbf k)\cdot d\mathbf S
=\mathbf k\cdot\int_Sd\mathbf S\times\nabla\phi.
$$
Since this holds for every constant $\mathbf k$, equality of all vector components proves the <vector-valued Stokes identity>
$$
\boxed{\int_Sd\mathbf S\times\nabla\phi=\oint_{\partial S}\phi\,d\mathbf x}.
$$
For the specified field, the two axis segments contribute zero because $x^2y^4$ vanishes on them. On the arc the direct <line integral> is
$$
I=\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin^4\theta(\cos\theta-\sin\theta)\,d\theta.
$$
Its two terms give
$$
\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^3\theta\sin^4\theta\,d\theta
=\int_0^1u^4(1-u^2)\,du=\frac2{35},
$$
and
$$
\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin^5\theta\,d\theta
=\int_0^1u^2(1-u^2)^2\,du=\frac8{105}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{I=-\frac2{105}}.
$$
One can also check the orientation and sign by <Stokes theorem>. For the upward-oriented quarter disk, $(\nabla\times\mathbf A)_z=2xy^4-4x^2y^3$. Polar integration gives
$$
\int_0^1r^6dr\int_0^{\pi/2}(2\cos\theta\sin^4\theta-4\cos^2\theta\sin^3\theta)d\theta
=\frac17\left(\frac25-\frac8{15}\right)=-\frac2{105},
$$
consistent with the positively oriented boundary in the question.

= Solution
{parent=11A}

Let $u=\phi_1-\phi_2$. It is a <harmonic function> in the bounded region and vanishes on its entire boundary. For smooth solutions and boundary, the <divergence theorem> applied to $u\nabla u$ gives the energy identity
$$
\int_R|\nabla u|^2dV
=\int_Su\,\partial_nu\,dS-\int_Ru\nabla^2u\,dV=0.
$$
The integrand is nonnegative, so $\nabla u=0$ in $R$. Thus $u$ is constant on each connected component, and its zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> makes every constant zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi_1=\phi_2}.
$$
This proves the <uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> rather than assuming it.

For the separated expression, write $\phi=F(x)G(y)$. The hyperbolic factor obeys $F''=\lambda^2F$ and the trigonometric factor obeys $G''=-\lambda^2G$, so
$$
\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=F''G+FG''=0
$$
for all the constants, including $\lambda=0$. In the half-strip take $a>0$ so the region has positive width. For every nonzero integer $n$, the decaying <harmonic sine mode in a half-strip> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y)=e^{-|n|\pi x/a}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi y}{a}\right)}.
$$
Its second derivatives cancel, it vanishes at $y=0,a$, and it has the prescribed sine trace at $x=0$. The absolute value in the exponential ensures decay for negative as well as positive $n$. For $n=0$, the zero function is the solution. Thus all integer cases are covered, and the decay is uniform across the strip.

= Solution
{parent=12A}

A Cartesian <isotropic tensor> is unchanged by every proper <rotation matrix>. For a rank-two tensor this means $B'=RBR^{\mathsf T}=B$, or $B'_{ij}=R_{ik}R_{jl}B_{kl}=B_{ij}$. No symmetry of $B$ is assumed. For a quarter-turn about the $z$ axis,
$$
R_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R_zBR_z^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}
B_{22}&-B_{21}&-B_{23}\\
-B_{12}&B_{11}&B_{13}\\
-B_{32}&B_{31}&B_{33}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Comparing entries yields $B_{11}=B_{22}$, $B_{12}=-B_{21}$, $B_{13}=-B_{23}= -B_{13}$, and $B_{31}=-B_{32}=-B_{31}$. In particular,
$$
\boxed{B_{13}=B_{31}=B_{23}=B_{32}=0}.
$$
The remaining matrix has the form $\begin{pmatrix}b&c&0\\-c&b&0\\0&0&d\end{pmatrix}$. A quarter-turn about the $x$ axis gives
$$
R_x=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R_xBR_x^{\mathsf T}=\begin{pmatrix}b&0&c\\0&d&0\\-c&0&b\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Invariance now forces $c=0$ and $d=b$, proving that an <isotropic second-rank tensor> is
$$
\boxed{B_{ij}=b\delta_{ij}},
$$
a scalar multiple of the <Kronecker delta>.

For the final unheaded conductivity identity, let $P=nn^{\mathsf T}$, the orthogonal <projection> onto the unit vector $n$. Then $P^2=P$ and the <uniaxial conductivity tensor> is $\sigma=\alpha I+\gamma P$. The antisymmetric tensor $D$ sends a vector $v$ to $v\times n$. Applying it twice and using the vector triple product gives
$$
D^2v=(v\times n)\times n=n(n\cdot v)-v,
\qquad D^2=P-I.
$$
Consequently
$$
\sigma D^2=(\alpha I+\gamma P)(P-I)=\alpha P-\alpha I.
$$
This equals $-\sigma=-\alpha I-\gamma P$ exactly when $(\alpha+\gamma)P=0$. Since $P$ has rank one and is nonzero, the requested parameter is
$$
\boxed{\gamma=-\alpha}.
$$
At this value conductivity vanishes along $n$; it is precisely the singular case excluded in part (ii), so there is no conflict with that part's conclusion.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Writing the <uniaxial conductivity tensor> in vector form gives the <electric current density>
$$
\mathbf J=\alpha\mathbf E+\gamma n(n\cdot\mathbf E).
$$
Choose the plane through the origin perpendicular to $n$:
$$
\boxed{\Pi=\{\mathbf E:n\cdot\mathbf E=0\}}.
$$
For an <electric field> in this plane, the anisotropic term vanishes and $\mathbf J=\alpha\mathbf E$. Thus every nonzero such field is parallel or antiparallel to its current according to the sign of the nonzero $\alpha$. The zero field gives zero current. This establishes collinearity throughout the plane without assuming that the tensor is isotropic.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Decompose the <electric field> into its orthogonal parts $\mathbf E=\mathbf E_\perp+\mathbf E_\parallel$, where $\mathbf E_\parallel=n(n\cdot\mathbf E)$. The <uniaxial conductivity tensor> acts on them as
$$
\mathbf J=\alpha\mathbf E_\perp+(\alpha+\gamma)\mathbf E_\parallel.
$$
If $\mathbf J=0$, its perpendicular and parallel components each vanish. Since $\alpha\ne0$ and $\alpha+\gamma\ne0$, this forces both field components to be zero. The contrapositive gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E\ne0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf J\ne0}.
$$
