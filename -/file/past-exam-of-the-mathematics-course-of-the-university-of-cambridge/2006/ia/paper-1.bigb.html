= Solution
{parent=1B}

The <gradient> of $x_3^2-x_1^2-x_2^2$ is $2(-x_1,-x_2,x_3)$. On the upper cone, its <Euclidean norm> is $2\sqrt2\,x_3$. Thus the <upward unit normal to an upper circular cone> is
$$
\boxed{n=\frac{(-x_1,-x_2,x_3)}{\sqrt2\,x_3},\qquad n_3=\frac1{\sqrt2}.}
$$
Put $x_h=(x_1,x_2)$ and $p_h=(p_1,p_2)$. The hypotheses imply $|x_h|=x_3$ and $|p_h|\le p_3$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> therefore gives
$$
p\cdot n=\frac{p_3x_3-p_h\cdot x_h}{\sqrt2\,x_3}
\ge\frac{p_3x_3-|p_h|\,|x_h|}{\sqrt2\,x_3}
=\frac{p_3-|p_h|}{\sqrt2}\ge0.
$$
This is also a direct instance of <self-duality of a second-order cone>: both $p$ and the chosen unit normal belong to the closed <Lorentz cone>.

In the standard <spherical coordinate system>, $\theta$ is measured from the positive $z$ axis. The <radial unit vector in spherical coordinates> is
$$
\boxed{e_r=\sin\theta\cos\phi\,i+\sin\theta\sin\phi\,j+\cos\theta\,k.}
$$
The coordinate forms of the <elliptic paraboloid> are given in the labelled parts below.

For the other surface, $r^2\cos2\theta=r^2(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)$ converts to
$$
\boxed{z^2-x^2-y^2=a.}
$$
For $a>0$, this is a <two-sheeted hyperboloid>, with two disconnected sheets starting at $z=\pm\sqrt a$ and opening along the $z$ axis. For $a<0$, it is a <one-sheet hyperboloid>:
$$
x^2+y^2-z^2=|a|.
$$
Its waist is the circle $z=0$, $x^2+y^2=|a|$. Both are surfaces of revolution about the $z$ axis and approach the cone $z^2=x^2+y^2$ far from the origin.

The figure shows $a=1$ and $a=-1$; scaling all coordinates by $\sqrt{|a|}$ gives the other magnitudes.

In <spherical coordinates>, $z=r\cos\theta$ and $x^2+y^2=r^2\sin^2\theta$, so the <elliptic paraboloid> has equation

= Solution
{parent=3F}

The <series> $\sum_{n\ge1}a_n$ converges to $A$ when its <partial sums> $s_n$ have <limit of a sequence> $A$: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $N$ such that $|s_n-A|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$.

Its averages are the <Cesaro means> of the sequence of <partial sums>. To prove their convergence, choose $N$ with $|s_k-A|<\varepsilon/2$ for $k\ge N$. Then
$$
\left|\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n s_k-A\right|
\le\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}|s_k-A|+\frac{n-N+1}{n}\frac\varepsilon2.
$$
The finite first sum divided by $n$ tends to zero. This proves the <Cesaro theorem for convergent sequences> in this setting, and
$$
\boxed{\widetilde A=A.}
$$
The converse fails. Take $a_n=(-1)^{n+1}$, so $s_n=1$ for <odd> $n$ and $s_n=0$ for <even> $n$. The <partial sums> do not converge, but
$$
\boxed{\frac{s_1+\cdots+s_n}{n}=\frac{\lceil n/2\rceil}{n}\longrightarrow\frac12.}
$$
Thus the alternating <series> is <Cesàro summable> without having an ordinary sum.

= Solution
{parent=4D}

Use these standard facts about a <power series>: it is absolutely convergent at every point strictly inside its <radius of convergence> and divergent at every point strictly outside it. Also, the <triangle inequality> implies that a sum or difference of two absolutely convergent <series> is absolutely convergent.

Let $T$ be the <radius of convergence> of the coefficientwise sum. For $|z|<\min(R,S)$,
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}|(a_n+b_n)z^n|
\le\sum_{n\ge0}|a_nz^n|+\sum_{n\ge0}|b_nz^n|<\infty.
$$
Hence $T\ge\min(R,S)$.

Suppose $R<S$. If $T>R$, choose a finite $\rho$ with $R<\rho<\min(T,S)$ and choose $z$ of <complex modulus> $\rho$. The sum series and the $b_n$ series both converge absolutely there, so
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}|a_nz^n|
\le\sum_{n\ge0}|(a_n+b_n)z^n|+\sum_{n\ge0}|b_nz^n|<\infty,
$$
contradicting $\rho>R$. Thus $T=R$. Interchanging the series covers $S<R$, proving the <sum of power series with unequal radii of convergence> rule:
$$
\boxed{T=\min(R,S).}
$$
The argument also covers a zero radius or an infinite larger radius. When the two radii are equal, cancellation can enlarge the radius, so the inequality of the radii matters.

= Solution
{parent=5C}

For $y\ne0$, the squared <Euclidean norm> of the <orthogonal projection> residual is nonnegative:
$$
0\le\left|x-\frac{x\cdot y}{|y|^2}y\right|^2
=|x|^2-\frac{(x\cdot y)^2}{|y|^2}.
$$
This proves the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> $|x\cdot y|\le|x||y|$. Equality holds exactly when $x$ is a scalar multiple of $y$. If $y=0$, equality is automatic. Equivalently, equality means the two vectors are <linearly dependent>, including the cases with a zero vector.

For a point $C$ equidistant from the coordinate vertices,
$$
|C-e_i|^2=|C|^2-2C_i+1
$$
must be independent of $i$. Thus all $C_i$ are equal, and their sum is one. The <centroid> is therefore
$$
\boxed{C=\frac1n(1,\ldots,1),\qquad |OC|=\frac1{\sqrt n}.}
$$
For every $x$ in the base <probability simplex>, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $1=(1,\ldots,1)\cdot x\le\sqrt n\,|x|$. Equality forces the same coordinates as $C$. Thus $C$ is the unique <closest point of a probability simplex to the origin>.

The angle $\alpha$ between $OC$ and any edge from the origin to $e_i$ satisfies
$$
\cos\alpha=\frac{C\cdot e_i}{|C|\,|e_i|}=\frac1{\sqrt n}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\arccos(n^{-1/2})\longrightarrow\frac\pi2,\qquad |OC|\longrightarrow0.}
$$

{parent=Paper 1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=6C}

A positively oriented planar <rotation matrix> acts by
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}x_1'\\x_2'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For a <unit vector> $n$ in three dimensions, the parallel and perpendicular <orthogonal projections> are
$$
x_\parallel=(x\cdot n)n,\qquad x_\perp=x-(x\cdot n)n.
$$
On the plane perpendicular to $n$, the map $v\mapsto n\times v$ preserves lengths and turns vectors through a positive right angle about $n$. It squares to minus the identity there. Thus $\cos\theta\,x_\perp+\sin\theta(n\times x_\perp)$ rotates that component, while the parallel component remains fixed. This proves the <Rodrigues rotation formula>
$$
x'=(x\cdot n)n+\cos\theta\,[x-(x\cdot n)n]+\sin\theta(n\times x).
$$
Its component form uses the <Levi-Civita symbol>:
$$
\boxed{R_{ij}=\cos\theta\,\delta_{ij}+(1-\cos\theta)n_in_j-\sin\theta\,\epsilon_{ijk}n_k.}
$$
Since $n_in_i=1$ and $\epsilon_{iik}=0$, its <matrix trace> is $3\cos\theta+1-\cos\theta=1+2\cos\theta$.

For the given matrix, the <matrix trace> is $1+2r$ with $r=1/\sqrt2$, so $\cos\theta=1/\sqrt2$. In the stated angular range, $\theta=\pm\pi/4$. The antisymmetric entries give
$$
2\sin\theta\,n=(R_{32}-R_{23},R_{13}-R_{31},R_{21}-R_{12})=(1,1,0).
$$
Therefore the two axis-angle pairs are
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac\pi4,\frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad
\left(-\frac\pi4,-\frac{(1,1,0)}{\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
Reversing the axis and the angle leaves the <Rodrigues rotation formula> unchanged, so these describe the same rotation.

If $u\cdot v=0$, use the positively oriented <orthonormal basis> $(u,v\times u,v)$. The <cross product> identities give $Au=u+v\times u$, $A(v\times u)=-u$ and $Av=0$. In this basis,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\lambda(\lambda^2-\lambda+1)$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda=0,\quad\frac{1+i\sqrt3}{2},\quad\frac{1-i\sqrt3}{2}.}
$$

{parent=7b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A trace-zero <Hermitian matrix> has the unique form
$$
\begin{pmatrix}h&w\\\overline w&-h\end{pmatrix},
\qquad h\in\mathbb R,\quad w\in\mathbb C.
$$
Set $x_3=h$, $x_1=\operatorname{Re}w$ and $x_2=-\operatorname{Im}w$. Then it is exactly $R(x)$; these assignments also prove uniqueness. This is the real <Pauli matrix> representation $R(x)=x_1\sigma_1+x_2\sigma_2+x_3\sigma_3$.

For a <unitary matrix>, $U^{-1}=U^\dagger$. Thus
$$
(U^{-1}AU)^\dagger=U^\dagger A^\dagger(U^{-1})^\dagger=U^{-1}AU,
$$
and invariance of the <matrix trace> under <similarity transformations> gives $\operatorname{tr}(U^{-1}AU)=0$. Therefore conjugation maps the space to itself.

Finally,
$$
\det R(x)=-x_3^2-(x_1-ix_2)(x_1+ix_2)=-|x|^2.
$$
The <determinant> is invariant under <similarity transformations>, so $\det R(y)=\det R(x)$ implies
$$
\boxed{|y|=|x|.}
$$

For every integer $m$, <De Moivre's theorem> states $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^m=\cos(m\theta)+i\sin(m\theta)$. Taking the real part for $m=5$ gives
$$
\cos5\theta=\cos^5\theta-10\cos^3\theta\sin^2\theta+5\cos\theta\sin^4\theta.
$$
Replace $\sin^2\theta$ by $1-\cos^2\theta$ and expand:
$$
\boxed{\cos5\theta=16\cos^5\theta-20\cos^3\theta+5\cos\theta.}
$$
The resulting polynomial is the fifth <Chebyshev polynomial>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the given <Möbius transformation>, the <fixed point> equation is $z(z+3)=3z+1$, hence
$$
\boxed{z=1,\qquad z=-1.}
$$

For the general <Möbius transformation>, its finite fixed points satisfy
$$
cz^2+(d-a)z-b=0.
$$
With $c\ne0$ and nonzero <discriminant>, the <quadratic formula> gives the two distinct <fixed points of a Möbius transformation>:
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{a-d+m}{2c},\qquad\beta=\frac{a-d-m}{2c},\qquad m^2=(a-d)^2+4bc.}
$$
The fixed-point equation for $\alpha$ also gives $b-d\alpha=-\alpha(a-c\alpha)$. Therefore
$$
\omega-\alpha=\frac{(a-c\alpha)(z-\alpha)}{cz+d},
\qquad
\omega-\beta=\frac{(a-c\beta)(z-\beta)}{cz+d}.
$$
Dividing yields the <conjugation of a Möbius transformation with two distinct fixed points>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\omega-\alpha}{\omega-\beta}
=k\frac{z-\alpha}{z-\beta},\qquad
k=\frac{a-c\alpha}{a-c\beta}=\frac{a+d-m}{a+d+m}.}
$$
Neither factor in this quotient vanishes, since $(a-c\alpha)(a-c\beta)=ad-bc\ne0$. The formula extends over the <Riemann sphere>, including the transformation's pole. For the special transformation and the ordering $\alpha=1,\beta=-1$, $k=1/2$.

{parent=Paper 1}

{title2=Analysis}

= Solution
{parent=9E}

The <intermediate value theorem> states that a continuous real-valued function on $[a,b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. To prove it, suppose $f(a)<k<f(b)$ and repeatedly bisect the interval, keeping endpoints whose function values bracket $k$. The resulting nested closed intervals have lengths tending to zero and a common point $c$ by <completeness of the real numbers>. Both endpoint sequences tend to $c$, so <continuity> implies $f(c)=k$. Endpoint values are immediate, and reversing the inequalities handles $f(a)>f(b)$.

For the derivative assertion, put $m=(f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)$. The <intermediate secant slope from endpoint derivatives> can be constructed in three cases. If $m=k$, take $[a',b']=[a,b]$. If $m>k$, the function
$$
g(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a},\qquad g(a)=f'(a),
$$
is continuous on $[a,b]$: differentiability at $a$ supplies the endpoint limit, and differentiability elsewhere implies continuity. Since $g(a)<k<g(b)$, the <intermediate value theorem> gives $x\in(a,b)$ with $g(x)=k$. Take $[a',b']=[a,x]$.

If $m<k$, use instead
$$
h(x)=\frac{f(b)-f(x)}{b-x},\qquad h(b)=f'(b).
$$
Its continuous endpoint extension satisfies $h(a)<k<h(b)$, so there is $y\in(a,b)$ with $h(y)=k$. Take $[a',b']=[y,b]$. In every case,
$$
\frac{f(b')-f(a')}{b'-a'}=k.
$$
The <mean value theorem> on this subinterval now gives
$$
\boxed{\text{some }c\in(a,b)\text{ satisfies }f'(c)=k.}
$$
This proves the <Darboux theorem for derivatives> without assuming that $f'$ is continuous.

= Solution
{parent=10E}

The <Taylor formula with integral remainder> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!}(x-a)^j+R_n(f,a,x),\qquad
R_n(f,a,x)=\frac1{n!}\int_a^x(x-t)^n f^{(n+1)}(t)\,dt.}
$$
For $n=0$, this is the <fundamental theorem of calculus>. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
R_n(f,a,x)=-\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n+R_{n-1}(f,a,x).
$$
Rearranging and applying <mathematical induction> proves the displayed expansion. The oriented integral also covers $x<a$.

For $x\ne0$, the <chain rule> and <product rule> give
$$
f'(x)=\left[2x^{-3}p(1/x)-x^{-2}p'(1/x)\right]e^{-1/x^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{q(t)=2t^3p(t)-t^2p'(t).}
$$
Because <Gaussian decay dominates every power>, $p(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}/x\to0$ as $x\to0$. Hence $f'(0)=0$, and the derivative formula extends continuously to zero. Repeating the argument replaces $p$ by another polynomial at each step: $p_{j+1}(t)=2t^3p_j(t)-t^2p_j'(t)$. All derivative difference quotients at zero tend to zero, so the <polynomial multiples of a Gaussian flat function> are <smooth functions> with
$$
f^{(j)}(0)=0\qquad(j\ge0).
$$
The <Taylor polynomial> about zero is therefore identically zero for every degree, and
$$
\boxed{R_n(f,0,x)=f(x)\quad\text{for every }n.}
$$
A nonzero polynomial has only finitely many <roots of a polynomial>, so $p(1/x)\ne0$ for every sufficiently small nonzero $x$. At each such fixed $x$, this remainder never tends to zero. The <flat function> is therefore smooth but not <real analytic> at zero.

= Solution
{parent=11F}

A real <sequence> has limit $a\in\mathbb R$ if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $N$ such that $|a_n-a|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$. It tends to $+\infty$ if, for every real $M$, eventually $a_n>M$; it tends to $-\infty$ if eventually $a_n<M$ for every real $M$.

The <Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem> states that every <bounded sequence> of real numbers has a real convergent <subsequence>. This proves the claim for bounded sequences. If the sequence is unbounded above, every tail remains unbounded above because a finite prefix is bounded. Choose indices $n_j$ increasing with $a_{n_j}>j$. This subsequence tends to $+\infty$. If it is unbounded below, choose $a_{n_j}<-j$ to obtain a subsequence tending to $-\infty$. This proves the <extended real subsequence theorem>.

For the final example, a <bounded sequence with vanishing increments need not converge>. Take
$$
\boxed{a_n=\sin\sqrt n.}
$$
The <mean value theorem> gives
$$
|a_{n+1}-a_n|\le\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n=\frac1{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Nevertheless, along $n_j=\lfloor(\pi/2+2\pi j)^2\rfloor$, the square-root rounding error tends to zero, so $a_{n_j}\to1$. Along $m_j=\lfloor(3\pi/2+2\pi j)^2\rfloor$, it tends to $-1$. These two <subsequences> have different limits, proving nonconvergence.

{parent=Paper 1}

= Solution
{parent=12D}

For a bounded function on an interval $I$, write $\omega_I(f)=\sup_I f-\inf_I f$ for its <oscillation of a function on an interval>. For a partition $P$, the difference between its <upper Darboux sum> and <lower Darboux sum> is $\sum_{I\in P}|I|\omega_I(f)$. The <Riemann integrability criterion> says that this can be made arbitrarily small.

Since $\omega_I(f_1+f_2)\le\omega_I(f_1)+\omega_I(f_2)$, choose good partitions for the two functions and take their common refinement. <Darboux sum refinement monotonicity> makes both oscillation sums small on this refinement, so their sum is <Riemann integrable>. The same argument handles differences and scalar multiples.

Both $t\mapsto\max(t,0)$ and $t\mapsto|t|$ are <Lipschitz continuous> with constant one. Thus $\omega_I(f^+)\le\omega_I(f)$ and $\omega_I(|f|)\le\omega_I(f)$. This calculation shows that <Lipschitz composition preserves Riemann integrability>, so the <positive part> and the <absolute value> are integrable.

The converse for the absolute value is false. Define $f=1$ on <rational numbers> and $f=-1$ on <irrational numbers>. Then $|f|=1$ is integrable, but every interval has supremum one and infimum minus one, so every Darboux difference for $f$ is $2(b-a)$. Hence $f$ is not <Riemann integrable>.

Finally,
$$
\boxed{\max(f_1,f_2)=\frac{f_1+f_2+|f_1-f_2|}{2}}
$$
is integrable by the closure properties just proved. This establishes that <Riemann integrability is closed under addition and maximum>.

A countable supremum can fail, even for a uniformly bounded family. Enumerate the rational points of $[a,b]$ as $q_1,q_2,\ldots$ and set $f_n=1_{\{q_n\}}$. Each singleton <indicator function> is <Riemann integrable> with integral zero: isolate its single nonzero point in intervals of arbitrarily small total length. But
$$
\boxed{\sup_n f_n=1_{\mathbb Q\cap[a,b]}}
$$
is the nonintegrable <Dirichlet function>. This proves that a <pointwise supremum need not preserve Riemann integrability>.
