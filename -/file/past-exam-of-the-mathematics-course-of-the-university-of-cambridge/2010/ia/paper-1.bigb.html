= Solution
{parent=1A}

Define the <change-of-basis matrices> by expressing each new <basis vector> in the corresponding old <basis>:
$$
\mathbf b'_i=\sum_{k=1}^n B_{ki}\mathbf b_k,
\qquad
\mathbf c'_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^m C_{\ell j}\mathbf c_\ell.
$$
Thus $B$ is $n\times n$, $C$ is $m\times m$, and both are <invertible matrices>: a <linear combination> of their columns is zero exactly when the same combination of the new <basis vectors> is zero. For any vector $v$, its coordinate columns therefore satisfy $[v]_{\mathbf b}=B[v]_{\mathbf b'}$; likewise $[w]_{\mathbf c}=C[w]_{\mathbf c'}$.

The <matrix representation of a linear map> gives
$$
C[\Phi(v)]_{\mathbf c'}=[\Phi(v)]_{\mathbf c}
=A[v]_{\mathbf b}=AB[v]_{\mathbf b'}.
$$
Multiplying by the <matrix inverse> of $C$ and comparing with $[\Phi(v)]_{\mathbf c'}=A'[v]_{\mathbf b'}$ for every $v$ proves
$$
\boxed{A'=C^{-1}AB.}
$$
The domain <change of basis> acts on the right, while the codomain <change of basis> acts on the left through its inverse.

The <polar form of a complex number> and the <roots of unity> give
$$
z_1=e^{2\pi i j/3},\quad j=0,1,2,
\qquad z_2=2e^{2\pi i k/9},\quad k=0,\ldots,8,
$$
since $512=2^9$. Using the <complex conjugate> to compute the squared <complex modulus>,
$$
|z_1-z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2-2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})
=5-4\cos\frac{2\pi(3j-k)}9.
$$
The residue $3j-k$ runs through every class modulo $9$, already with $j=0$. The <cosine> is even, so the nine angles give five distinct values, represented by $r=0,1,2,3,4$. They are distinct because <cosine> is strictly decreasing on $[0,\pi]$ and these representative angles lie in that interval. Hence
$$
\boxed{|z_1-z_2|\in
\left\{1,\sqrt{5-4\cos\frac{2\pi}9},
\sqrt{5-4\cos\frac{4\pi}9},\sqrt7,
\sqrt{5-4\cos\frac{8\pi}9}\right\}.}
$$
Every listed value is attained by choosing $z_1=1$ and $z_2=2e^{2\pi i r/9}$.

For any <complex number> $w$, $\operatorname{Re}w\leq |w|$, while the <complex modulus> is multiplicative and unchanged by <complex conjugation>. Consequently
$$
|z_1+z_2|^2=|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2
+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\overline{z_2})
\leq |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2|z_1||z_2|
=(|z_1|+|z_2|)^2.
$$
Both sides are nonnegative, so taking square roots proves the <triangle inequality>:
$$
\boxed{|z_1+z_2|\leq |z_1|+|z_2|.}
$$
The proposed further inequality fails for perpendicular vectors in the <complex plane>. Taking $z_1=1$, $z_2=i$ gives
$$
\boxed{|1+i|+|1-i|=2\sqrt2>2=2\max(|1|,|i|).}
$$

A <power series> has <radius of convergence> $R$ when it converges absolutely for every $|z|<R$ and diverges for every $|z|>R$. Its behaviour on $|z|=R$ is not specified by this definition. When $R=0$, it converges at the centre and diverges at every nonzero point; an infinite <radius of convergence> means <absolute convergence> at every finite $z$.

Suppose, towards a contradiction, that $|a_nz^n|\leq M$ for all $n$ at some $|z|>R$. Choose a <complex number> $w$ with $R<|w|<|z|$. Then
$$
|a_nw^n|=|a_nz^n|\left(\frac{|w|}{|z|}\right)^n
\leq Mq^n,\qquad q=\frac{|w|}{|z|}<1.
$$
The <comparison test for series> with the convergent <geometric series> $\sum Mq^n$ yields <absolute convergence> at $w$, contradicting the definition of $R$. Thus <terms of a power series are unbounded outside its convergence disc>:
$$
\boxed{|z|>R\ \Longrightarrow\ (|a_nz^n|)_{n\geq0}\text{ is unbounded}.}
$$
For the given <power series>, the <ratio test> gives, when $z\ne0$,
$$
\frac{|z|^{n+1}/(n+1)^3}{|z|^n/n^3}
=|z|\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^3\longrightarrow |z|.
$$
Hence it converges absolutely for $|z|<1$ and its terms fail to tend to zero for $|z|>1$ (the ratios are eventually larger than some number greater than $1$). Therefore $\boxed{R=1}$. In fact, on $|z|=1$ it also converges absolutely by comparison with the convergent <p-series> $\sum n^{-3}$.

The <matrix trace> is the sum of the diagonal entries:
$$
\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii}.
$$
Using the definitions of <matrix multiplication> and <matrix transpose>,
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^TB)
=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ji}B_{ji}
=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n B_{ji}A_{ji}
=\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(B^TA)}.
$$
This is the symmetry of the real <Frobenius inner product>.

{parent=5a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <matrix trace> calculation in (i) gives
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^TA)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2\geq0.
$$
A sum of nonnegative real numbers is zero exactly when each is zero. Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(A^TA)=0\iff A=0}$, so the real <Frobenius inner product> is <positive-definite>.

Write $\alpha=\operatorname{tr}(A^TA)$, $\beta=\operatorname{tr}(B^TB)$ and $\gamma=\operatorname{tr}(A^TB)$. If $B\ne0$, then $\beta>0$, and applying the same nonnegativity to $A-tB$ gives
$$
0\leq\operatorname{tr}((A-tB)^T(A-tB))
=\alpha-2t\gamma+t^2\beta.
$$
Taking $t=\gamma/\beta$ yields $\alpha-\gamma^2/\beta\geq0$. If $B=0$, the claimed inequality is immediate. Hence the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> here is
$$
\boxed{\bigl(\operatorname{tr}(A^TB)\bigr)^2
\leq\operatorname{tr}(A^TA)\operatorname{tr}(B^TB).}
$$
When $B\ne0$, equality holds exactly when $\operatorname{tr}((A-(\gamma/\beta)B)^T(A-(\gamma/\beta)B))=0$, hence exactly when $A=(\gamma/\beta)B$. Together with the case $B=0$, the complete equality condition is $\boxed{A,B\text{ are linearly dependent}}$, including the case that either is zero.

The two coefficient <matrices> satisfy all three constraints. Their <linear independence> follows by looking first at entry $(1,1)$ and then at entry $(2,2)$. Therefore a <basis> for this <orthogonal complement> is

= Solution

In an oriented orthonormal coordinate system, the <dot product> and <cross product> are
$$
a\cdot b=\sum_{k=1}^3a_kb_k,\qquad
 a\times b=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1).
$$
The <scalar triple product> is $a\cdot(b\times c)$; equivalently it is the <determinant> of the <matrix> whose columns are $a,b,c$. The three vectors have <linear independence> when
$$
t_1a_1+t_2a_2+t_3a_3=0\ \Longrightarrow\ t_1=t_2=t_3=0.
$$

Let $U=(a_1\ a_2\ a_3)$ and $V=(b_1\ b_2\ b_3)$ be column <matrices>. Since $(U^TV)_{ij}=a_i\cdot b_j$, we have $S=U^TV$. Multiplicativity of the <determinant> and its invariance under <matrix transpose> yield
$$
\det S=\det(U^T)\det V=\det U\det V
=\boxed{\bigl(a_1\cdot(a_2\times a_3)\bigr)
\bigl(b_1\cdot(b_2\times b_3)\bigr)}.
$$
A square <matrix> has maximal <matrix rank> exactly when its <determinant> is nonzero, equivalently when its columns have <linear independence>. A product of two real numbers is nonzero exactly when both factors are nonzero. Hence $\boxed{\operatorname{rank}S=3\iff (a_i)_{i=1}^3\text{ and }(b_j)_{j=1}^3\text{ are both independent}}$.

The same argument works in every dimension. Set $U_n=(c_1\ \cdots\ c_n)$ and $V_n=(d_1\ \cdots\ d_n)$. Then
$$
T=U_n^TV_n,\qquad \det T=\det U_n\det V_n,
$$
so $\boxed{\operatorname{rank}T=n\iff(c_i)_{i=1}^n\text{ and }(d_j)_{j=1}^n\text{ are both independent}}$.

For the last construction, choose two <standard basis> vectors $e_1,e_2$ in $\mathbb R^n$ and take $c_j=e_1+je_2$ for $j=1,\ldots,n$. If $\alpha c_i+\beta c_j=0$ with $i\ne j$, its first two coordinates give $\alpha+\beta=0$ and $i\alpha+j\beta=0$, hence $\alpha=\beta=0$. Every pair therefore has <linear independence>. Every triple lies in the two-dimensional <linear span> of $e_1,e_2$ and has <linear dependence>. More explicitly, for distinct $i,j,k$,
$$
(j-k)c_i+(k-i)c_j+(i-j)c_k=0,
$$
a nontrivial dependence. Thus $\boxed{c_j=e_1+je_2\ (1\leq j\leq n)\text{ works for every }n>2}$.

{parent=7b}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For $t\ne0$, factor the <matrix> as
$$
A^{-1}-tI=A^{-1}(I-tA)=A^{-1}\bigl[-t(A-t^{-1}I)\bigr].
$$
The <determinant> of a scalar multiple of an $n\times n$ <matrix> gains the scalar's $n$th power. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi_{A^{-1}}(t)=(\det A)^{-1}(-1)^nt^n\chi_A(t^{-1}).}
$$
The right side extends to a <polynomial> at $t=0$: multiplying $\chi_A(t^{-1})$ by $t^n$ removes all negative powers. Its value there is $(\det A)^{-1}$, since the leading coefficient of $\chi_A(s)=\det(A-sI)$ is $(-1)^n$. This equals $\chi_{A^{-1}}(0)$, so the identity holds at zero with that interpretation.

For the relation between <eigenvalues> and the <characteristic polynomial>,
$$
\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A
\iff \ker(A-\lambda I)\ne\{0\}
\iff A-\lambda I\text{ is singular}
\iff \chi_A(\lambda)=0.
$$
The first equivalence is the definition of an <eigenvector>; the others use the <kernel of a linear map> and the criterion for an <invertible matrix> to have nonzero <determinant>. Since $\chi_A$ has degree $n$, the <fundamental theorem of algebra> gives $n$ roots over $\mathbb C$ counted with multiplicity. The multiplicity of a root is its <algebraic multiplicity>, and the <eigenspace> is exactly $\ker(A-\lambda I)$; its dimension is the <geometric multiplicity>, which need not equal the <algebraic multiplicity>.

Finally, factor $p(s)=s^4-\mu$ over $\mathbb C$ as $p(s)=\prod_{j=1}^4(s-\lambda_j)$, including repeated roots when $\mu=0$. Substituting $A$ into this <polynomial> is valid because the resulting factors commute. If $\mu$ is an <eigenvalue> of $A^4$, then
$$
0=\det(A^4-\mu I)=\prod_{j=1}^4\det(A-\lambda_jI).
$$
At least one factor must be zero, so its $\lambda_j$ is an <eigenvalue> of $A$ and satisfies $\lambda_j^4=\mu$. Thus the reverse direction of this instance of the <spectral mapping theorem> is
$$
\boxed{\mu\text{ an eigenvalue of }A^4
\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists\lambda\text{ an eigenvalue of }A\text{ with }\lambda^4=\mu.}
$$
This argument also covers $\mu=0$, when the conclusion is that $A$ is singular and has <eigenvalue> zero.

An <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $R^TR=I$, so it preserves the <Euclidean norm>. For a nonzero real <eigenvector> $v$,
$$
\|v\|^2=\|Rv\|^2=\|\lambda v\|^2=\lambda^2\|v\|^2.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\lambda=\pm1}$. Geometrically, <eigenvalue> $1$ fixes every vector on the <linear span> of $v$; <eigenvalue> $-1$ reverses every vector on that line. In either case the line is preserved as a set.

For $M$, solving $(M-I)v=0$ gives $y=x$, $z=y$, $x=z$. For $N$, the equations are $-2y-2z=0$ and $-2z=0$, hence $y=z=0$. For $P$, let $a=(1,1,1)^T$ and $J=aa^T$, so that $P=I-\frac23J$. Then $(P-I)v=0$ is equivalent to $x+y+z=0$. The resulting <eigenspaces> and their <bases> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
E_1(M)&=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,1)^T\},&\dim E_1(M)&=1,\\
E_1(N)&=\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0)^T\},&\dim E_1(N)&=1,\\
E_1(P)&=\operatorname{span}\{(1,-1,0)^T,(1,0,-1)^T\},&\dim E_1(P)&=2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Solving all the equations shows that these lists contain the maximum possible numbers of independent <eigenvectors> with <eigenvalue> $1$.

The columns of $M$ form an <orthonormal basis>, so $M$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. Also $\det M=1$. Its fixed axis is the line through $a$, and on the perpendicular plane it acts as a two-dimensional rotation. If $\theta$ denotes the rotation angle, its <matrix trace> is $1+2\cos\theta$. Since $\operatorname{tr}M=0$, we obtain $\cos\theta=-1/2$. To fix the orientation relative to $u=a/\sqrt3$, note that $Me_1=e_3$ and
$$
u\cdot(e_1\times Me_1)=u\cdot(e_1\times e_3)=-\frac1{\sqrt3}<0.
$$
The component of $e_1$ perpendicular to $u$ has squared length $2/3$, and this <scalar triple product> equals $(2/3)\sin\theta$. Hence $\sin\theta=-\sqrt3/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{M:\ \text{axis }\mathbb R(1,1,1),\quad
\theta=-\frac{2\pi}{3}\text{ about }\frac{(1,1,1)}{\sqrt3}.}
$$
Equivalently, its unsigned angle is $2\pi/3$, or its positive angle is $2\pi/3$ about the opposite oriented axis.

For $P$, the <reflection in a hyperplane> formula gives
$$
P=I-2\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}.
$$
It fixes the plane $a\cdot v=0$ pointwise and maps $a$ to $-a$, so $\boxed{P\text{ reflects in }x+y+z=0}$. These actions also show that $P$ is an <orthogonal matrix>. In contrast, the second column of $N$ has squared length $5$, so $N$ is not <orthogonal> and cannot represent either of these Euclidean motions.

A <diagonalizable matrix> must have a <basis> of <eigenvectors> over the field in question. For $M$, a direct <determinant> computation gives $\chi_M(t)=1-t^3$. Its <eigenvalues> are $1,\omega,\omega^2$, where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Only one is real, with one-dimensional <eigenspace>, so $M$ is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$. Over $\mathbb C$ the three <eigenvalues> are distinct, and the vectors $(1,\lambda,\lambda^2)^T$ for $\lambda=1,\omega,\omega^2$ give an <eigenbasis>. The upper triangular <matrix> $N$ has only <eigenvalue> $1$, of <algebraic multiplicity> $3$, but its <eigenspace> has dimension $1$ over either field. Hence $N$ is not diagonalizable over either field. For $P$, the two real plane vectors displayed above, together with $a$, form an <eigenbasis>, with <eigenvalues> $1,1,-1$. They remain independent over $\mathbb C$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}
&\mathbb R&\mathbb C\\\hline
M&\text{not diagonalizable}&\text{diagonalizable}\\
N&\text{not diagonalizable}&\text{not diagonalizable}\\
P&\text{diagonalizable}&\text{diagonalizable}
\end{array}}
$$

The <ratio test> says that a <series> $\sum u_n$ whose terms are eventually nonzero converges absolutely if $\limsup |u_{n+1}/u_n|<1$; in particular, it suffices to have $|u_{n+1}/u_n|\leq q<1$ eventually, since then its terms are bounded by a convergent <geometric series>. The <comparison test for series> states that, for nonnegative terms, comparison with a larger convergent series gives convergence, and comparison with a smaller divergent series gives divergence. For $u_n=n!/n^n$,
$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n.
$$
By the <binomial theorem>, $(1+1/n)^n\geq1+n(1/n)=2$. Thus $u_{n+1}/u_n\leq1/2$ for every $n\geq1$, and induction gives $0<u_n\leq2^{1-n}$. The <comparison test for series> with $\sum2^{1-n}=2$ proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{n!}{n^n}\text{ converges absolutely}.}
$$

= Solution

The <limit comparison test> says that if $u_n,v_n>0$ and $u_n/v_n\to L$ with $0<L<\infty$, then their <series> either both converge or both diverge. This follows because, eventually, $(L/2)v_n\leq u_n\leq(3L/2)v_n$, and the <comparison test for series> applies in both directions.

Here comparison with the <harmonic series> gives
$$
\frac{1/[n+(\log n)^2]}{1/n}
=\frac1{1+(\log n)^2/n}\longrightarrow1.
$$
For completeness, the <natural logarithm> grows slowly enough because for $x\geq1$,
$$
0\leq\log x=\int_1^x\frac{dt}{t}
\leq\int_1^x t^{-3/4}\,dt
=4(x^{1/4}-1)\leq4x^{1/4},
$$
so $(\log n)^2/n\leq16/\sqrt n\to0$. The <harmonic series> diverges: every block from $n=2^k$ to $2^{k+1}-1$ has $2^k$ terms at least $2^{-k-1}$ and therefore contributes at least $1/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}\frac1{n+(\log n)^2}\text{ diverges to }+\infty.}
$$

= Solution

The <alternating series test> states that if $b_n\geq0$ decreases to zero, then $\sum(-1)^nb_n$ converges. Indeed, its even and odd <partial sums> are monotone in opposite directions and bound each other, while their difference tends to zero. Here $b_n=1/(1+\sqrt n)$ decreases to zero, so the <series> converges.

It fails <absolute convergence>, since for $n\geq1$,
$$
\frac1{1+\sqrt n}\geq\frac1{2\sqrt n}.
$$
The <p-series> $\sum n^{-1/2}$ diverges. This also follows by comparison with the divergent <harmonic series>, because $n^{-1/2}\geq n^{-1}$. The <comparison test for series> therefore shows that $\sum b_n$ diverges. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{(-1)^n}{1+\sqrt n}\text{ converges conditionally}.}
$$

For $s=\pm1$, the identity $s/(2+s)=-1/3+(2/3)s$ gives
$$
\frac{(-1)^n}{n(2+(-1)^n)}
=-\frac1{3n}+\frac23\frac{(-1)^n}{n}.

$$
\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{(-1)^n}{n(2+(-1)^n)}
=-\frac13\sum_{n=1}^N\frac1n
+\frac23\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{(-1)^n}{n}
\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\text{the series diverges to }-\infty}$. Alternating signs alone do not satisfy the <alternating series test>: the magnitudes jump from $1/(3n)$ at even $n$ to $1/(n+1)$ at the next odd index.

One precise version of the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> is as follows. Let $n\geq0$, let $f$ and its <derivatives> through order $n$ be continuous on the closed interval between distinct points $a$ and $x$, and suppose $f^{(n+1)}$ exists throughout its interior. Then some $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k
+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}.}
$$
Here the endpoint <derivatives> are understood as restrictions of derivatives defined near the interval, or as their continuous one-sided extensions. No continuity of $f^{(n+1)}$ is required. For $x=a$ the equality is immediate with zero remainder.

To prove the formula, write the <Taylor polynomial> as $P_n(t)=\sum_{k=0}^n f^{(k)}(a)(t-a)^k/k!$ and define
$$
K=\frac{f(x)-P_n(x)}{(x-a)^{n+1}},\qquad
h(t)=f(t)-P_n(t)-K(t-a)^{n+1}.
$$
Then $h(a)=h(x)=0$, and $h^{(j)}(a)=0$ for $j=1,\ldots,n$. The <Rolle theorem> gives a zero $\xi_1$ of $h'$ strictly between $a$ and $x$. If $n\geq1$, apply the <Rolle theorem> to $h'$ between $a$ and $\xi_1$, using $h'(a)=0$, to get a zero $\xi_2$ of $h''$. Repeating produces a point $\xi_{n+1}$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ with $h^{(n+1)}(\xi_{n+1})=0$. Each application is justified by continuity on the relevant closed interval and differentiability in its interior; the same construction works when $x<a$.

Since $P_n$ has degree at most $n$,
$$
0=h^{(n+1)}(\xi_{n+1})
=f^{(n+1)}(\xi_{n+1})-(n+1)!K.
$$
Substituting this value of $K$ into its definition proves the displayed <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder>.

The assumed <differentiability> makes $e$ continuous. The identity $e'=e$ then implies inductively that $e$ has continuous <derivatives> of every order and
$$
e^{(k)}(t)=e(t)\quad(k\geq0),\qquad e^{(k)}(0)=1.
$$
Fix $x\ne0$. By the <extreme value theorem>, the <continuous function> $|e|$ is bounded on the closed interval between $0$ and $x$; let $M_x$ be its maximum. Applying the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> about $0$ gives some $\xi_n$ between $0$ and $x$ such that
$$
e(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{x^k}{k!}
+\frac{e(\xi_n)}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1},
\qquad
\left|e(x)-\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{x^k}{k!}\right|
\leq M_x\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$
For the last factor, successive ratios are $|x|/(n+2)$, which are at most $1/2$ once $n$ is sufficiently large. A <geometric sequence> bound therefore proves that $|x|^{n+1}/(n+1)!\to0$. The <partial sums> converge to $e(x)$, so
$$
\boxed{e(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
At $x=0$ the equality follows directly from $e(0)=1$. The proof obtains this <Taylor series> solely from the differential identity, continuity on a fixed finite interval, and the <Taylor remainder> estimate.

= Solution
{parent=11D}

For a bounded real function on $[a,\infty)$, convergence of its <improper integral> means that its restriction to every $[a,b]$, $b>a$, is <Riemann integrable> and that the finite limit
$$
\boxed{\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx
:=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\in\mathbb R}
$$
exists. Boundedness by itself does not guarantee either local <Riemann integrability> or existence of this limit.

A <monotone function> on $[a,b]$ is bounded between its endpoint values. Divide the interval into $m$ equal pieces with endpoints $x_j=a+j(b-a)/m$. If $f$ is nondecreasing, its upper and lower <Darboux sums> differ by
$$
U(f,P)-L(f,P)
=\frac{b-a}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m\bigl(f(x_j)-f(x_{j-1})\bigr)
=\frac{b-a}{m}\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr).
$$
For nonincreasing $f$, the same calculation with reversed endpoint values gives $(b-a)(f(a)-f(b))/m$. Thus in either case $U-L=(b-a)|f(b)-f(a)|/m\to0$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ one can choose $m$ to make this gap smaller than $\varepsilon$; the <Riemann integrability criterion> proves that $f$ is <Riemann integrable>.

Now let $f$ be decreasing on $[1,\infty)$ with limit zero. For each $x$, $f(x)\geq\lim_{y\to\infty}f(y)=0$. On $[n,n+1]$ its monotonicity gives
$$
f(n+1)\leq\int_n^{n+1}f(x)\,dx\leq f(n).
$$
Summing these bounds, with $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nf(n)$, yields
$$
\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx\leq S_N
\leq f(1)+\int_1^Nf(x)\,dx.
$$
Both the <partial sums> $S_N$ and the truncated integrals are nondecreasing because $f\geq0$. The inequalities show that one family is bounded above if and only if the other is. The <bounded monotone sequence theorem> then gives convergence of the sums exactly when the integer-endpoint integrals converge. For real $b$ between $N$ and $N+1$, the integral to $b$ lies between the integrals to these integer endpoints; the <squeeze theorem> supplies the same limit. If either family is unbounded, both tend to $+\infty$. This proves the <integral test for convergence> in the required form.

For $\alpha<0$, use $f(x)=x^\alpha$, which is decreasing and tends to zero. Its truncated <improper integral> is
$$
\int_1^b x^\alpha\,dx=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{b^{\alpha+1}-1}{\alpha+1},&\alpha\ne-1,\\[4pt]
\log b,&\alpha=-1.
\end{cases}
$$
This has a finite limit exactly when $\alpha<-1$. If $\alpha\geq0$, the terms $n^\alpha$ do not tend to zero, so the <term test for divergence> applies. Thus the full <p-series> criterion is
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}n^\alpha\text{ converges }\iff\alpha<-1.}
$$
For every $\alpha\geq-1$ its nonnegative <partial sums> tend to $+\infty$.

For $n=1$, <differentiability> at $x_0$ means existence of the finite two-sided <limit of a function> $\lim_{h\to0}(f(x_0+h)-f(x_0))/h$. More generally, <higher-order differentiability at a point> is defined recursively: for $n\geq2$, the <derivatives> $f',\ldots,f^{(n-1)}$ must exist throughout some open interval containing $x_0$, and $f^{(n-1)}$ must be differentiable at $x_0$. With $f^{(0)}=f$, the final condition is
$$
\boxed{f^{(n)}(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{f^{(n-1)}(x_0+h)-f^{(n-1)}(x_0)}h\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The neighbourhood requirement makes the numerator meaningful for every sufficiently small nonzero $h$. It does not require $f^{(n)}$ to exist at other points of that neighbourhood, or to be continuous at $x_0$.

The zeros of $\cos(\pi/x)$ occur at
$$
x_k=\frac{2}{2k+1},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Let $g(x)=\cos(\pi/x)$. At these nonzero points,
$$
g(x_k)=0,\qquad
 g'(x_k)=\frac\pi{x_k^2}\sin\frac\pi{x_k}
=\frac{\pi(-1)^k}{x_k^2}\ne0.
$$
The definition of the <derivative> gives $g(x_k+h)=g'(x_k)h+o(|h|)$, hence
$$
|g(x_k+h)|=\frac\pi{x_k^2}|h|+o(|h|).
$$
The <sign function> is constant sufficiently near the nonzero point $x_k$, and $(x_k+h)^2=x_k^2+O(h)$. As $f(x_k)=0$, multiplication yields
$$
f(x_k+h)=\pi\operatorname{sign}(x_k)|h|+o(|h|).
$$
Its right and left <one-sided derivatives> are therefore $\pi\operatorname{sign}(x_k)$ and $-\pi\operatorname{sign}(x_k)$ respectively. They differ, so $f$ is not differentiable at any $x_k$. In particular, $x_k\to0$ as $k\to+\infty$, proving
$$
\boxed{\text{Every neighbourhood of }0\text{ contains points where }f'\text{ does not exist}.}
$$
The <absolute value at a simple zero> mechanism creates unequal <one-sided derivatives>. It also shows why the existence of $f'(0)$ alone does not imply <higher-order differentiability at a point> of order two.

Away from zero and the zeros of $\cos(\pi/x)$, the <sign function> is locally constant and the <absolute value function> is differentiable at the value of the cosine. The <product rule> and <chain rule> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)
&=2x\operatorname{sign}(x)\left|\cos\frac\pi x\right|
+x^2\operatorname{sign}(x)\operatorname{sign}\left(\cos\frac\pi x\right)
\frac\pi{x^2}\sin\frac\pi x\\
&=2|x|\left|\cos\frac\pi x\right|
+\pi\operatorname{sign}(x)\operatorname{sign}\left(\cos\frac\pi x\right)
\sin\frac\pi x.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <triangle inequality> and the bounds on <sine> and <cosine> imply $|f'(x)|\leq2|x|+\pi$. Part (c) rules out differentiability at every nonzero cosine zero, and part (b) gives $f'(0)=0$, so this estimate covers every point where the <derivative> exists.

For a finite interval $I$, choose $L<\infty$ such that $|x|\leq L$ throughout $I$. Then
$$
\boxed{|f'(x)|\leq C_I:=2L+\pi\quad(x\in I\text{ where }f'(x)\text{ exists}).}
$$
The apparent $x^{-2}$ factor in the derivative of the oscillation cancels against the multiplying $x^2$, which is what makes a uniform bound possible even near zero.
