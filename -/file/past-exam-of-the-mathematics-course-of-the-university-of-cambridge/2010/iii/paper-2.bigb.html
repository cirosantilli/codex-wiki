Write $A=kG$ and $J=J(A)$, its <Jacobson radical>. First suppose the <characteristic> is $p>0$. We prove the counting statement through the <cocenter of a finite-dimensional algebra>
$$
C(A)=A/[A,A],
$$
where $[A,A]$ is the linear span of commutators, not an ideal. In a <group algebra>, this span is exactly the span of differences of conjugate group elements: $gh-hg$ is such a difference, and $xgx^{-1}-g=[x,gx^{-1}]$. Consequently $C(A)$ has one <basis vector> for each <conjugacy class>.

There is a well-defined <Frobenius map on an algebra cocenter>
$$
F:C(A)\longrightarrow C(A),\qquad [a]\longmapsto[a^p].
$$
Here $F$ is additive and $F(c[a])=c^pF([a])$. To prove additivity, expand $(a+b)^p$ into words of length $p$. Cyclic rotations of a word are equal modulo commutators. Every nonconstant word has a rotation orbit of length $p$, so its orbit contributes zero; only $a^p,b^p$ remain. Also
$$
[(xy-yx)^p]=[(xy)^p]-[(yx)^p]=0,
$$
because the two products are cyclic rotations. Additivity then shows that the whole commutator subspace maps to zero, proving well-definedness on the quotient.

Let $s$ be the number of <simple modules> up to isomorphism. The <semisimple algebra> $A/J$ is $\prod_{\nu=1}^sM_{d_\nu}(k)$: over an <algebraically closed field>, a finite-dimensional <division algebra> is the <field> itself, since the <minimal polynomial> of any element splits into linear factors and a <division algebra> has no zero divisors. In a <matrix algebra>, the commutator subspace is precisely the <traceless matrices>. Off-diagonal <matrix units> are commutators, and $E_{ii}-E_{jj}=[E_{ij},E_{ji}]$; these span that subspace in every characteristic. Thus
$$
C(A/J)\cong k^s.
$$
The map induced by $F$ on each coordinate is $z\mapsto z^p$, since $\operatorname{tr}(B^p)=(\operatorname{tr}B)^p$. This is bijective because $k$ is a <perfect field>.

The natural surjection $C(A)\twoheadrightarrow C(A/J)$ has <kernel> $(J+[A,A])/[A,A]$. Every <kernel> class has a representative in $J$. Since $J$ is nilpotent, $F^N$ kills that <kernel> for sufficiently large $N$. On the quotient it remains bijective, so its image has dimension exactly $s$. On the conjugacy-class basis, however,
$$
F^N([g])=[g^{p^N}].
$$
For large $N$, every $g^{p^N}$ is a <p-regular element>; moreover the $p^N$-power map permutes the p-regular conjugacy classes, because an inverse exponent can be chosen modulo the prime-to-$p$ part of the exponent of $G$. The stable image therefore has precisely those conjugacy classes as its basis. Comparing dimensions proves
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{simple }kG\text{-modules}\}
=\#\{\text{p-regular conjugacy classes of }G\}.}
$$
This argument uses dimensions of semilinear images: a bijective scalar Frobenius map preserves dimension, so the usual rank count applies.

In characteristic zero every group element is relevant. Averaging a linear projection onto a <submodule> over $G$ makes that projection $G$-equivariant, proving <Maschke's theorem> and semisimplicity of $kG$. The same matrix-algebra cocenter calculation now gives $s=\dim C(kG)$, the total number of conjugacy classes.

For the second request, $M$ is a <relative projective module> for $H$ when every $G$-equivariant epimorphism onto $M$ which splits after restriction to $H$ already splits over $G$. Equivalently $M$ is a <direct summand> of $kG\otimes_{kH}M$, the <induced representation> of the <restriction of a representation> to $H$. We can verify the defining splitting property directly: if $q:N\twoheadrightarrow M$ has an $H$-linear section $u$, choose representatives $T$ of the left <cosets> $G/H$ and set
$$
v(m)=\frac1{[G:H]}\sum_{t\in T}t\,u(t^{-1}m).
$$
The sum is independent of representatives by $H$-linearity. Reindexing $gt=t'h$ proves $G$-linearity, while $qv(m)=[G:H]^{-1}\sum_tm=m$. Hence **every $M$ is relatively $H$-projective when the index is invertible in $k$**.

For completeness the canonical induced-module epimorphism $\varepsilon(g\otimes m)=gm$ has the explicit $G$-linear section
$$
\boxed{\sigma(m)=\frac1{[G:H]}\sum_{t\in T}t\otimes t^{-1}m,\qquad \varepsilon\sigma=\operatorname{id}_M.}
$$
It exhibits the promised <direct summand> and is the <projectivity detected on a subgroup of invertible index> construction.

An <idempotent> $e$ gives a <projective module> $Ae$, since $A=Ae\oplus A(1-e)$. It is primitive when it cannot be written as a sum of two nonzero orthogonal <idempotents>. This is equivalent to indecomposability of $Ae$: a projection onto a <direct summand> is an <idempotent> of $\operatorname{End}_A(Ae)\cong(eAe)^{\mathrm{op}}$, and conversely such an <idempotent> splits $Ae$. Starting with $1$, split any nonprimitive summand repeatedly. The process terminates because each splitting increases the number of nonzero vector-space summands in a finite-dimensional algebra. Thus
$$
\boxed{1=e_1+\cdots+e_m,\qquad {}_AA=\bigoplus_{\nu=1}^mAe_\nu,}
$$
with orthogonal <primitive idempotents> and indecomposable <projective modules>. Each summand is generated by its <idempotent>.

Let $D=\operatorname{Hom}_k(-,k)$. A <Frobenius algebra> is a finite-dimensional algebra with $A\cong D(A)$ as left <modules> over $A$, where $(a\phi)(x)=\phi(xa)$. Equivalently there is a <linear functional> $\lambda:A\to k$ for which $(x,y)\mapsto\lambda(xy)$ is nondegenerate. A <symmetric algebra (Frobenius algebra)> satisfies the stronger <bimodule> isomorphism $A\cong D(A)$, with $(a\phi b)(x)=\phi(bxa)$; equivalently the functional can be chosen with $\lambda(xy)=\lambda(yx)$. This is the associative-algebra meaning of symmetric, rather than the <symmetric algebra> of a <vector space>.

For a finite-dimensional <module>,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Soc}M=\sum_{S\subseteq M\ \mathrm{simple}}S,\qquad
\operatorname{Rad}M=\bigcap_{N\subsetneq M\ \mathrm{maximal}}N.}
$$
The <socle> is its largest semisimple <submodule>, and the <radical of a module> gives its largest semisimple quotient. With $J=J(A)$ one has $\operatorname{Rad}M=JM$ and $\operatorname{Soc}M=\{m:Jm=0\}$: $A/J$ is semisimple, and $J$ annihilates every <simple module>.

Now let $P=Ae$ for a <primitive idempotent>, allowing the <field> $k$ to be nonsplit. Symmetry identifies $Ae$ with $D(eA)$ through the pairing
$$
eA\times Ae\longrightarrow k,\qquad (u,v)\longmapsto\lambda(uv).
$$
It is nondegenerate on these corners. If $v=ve\ne0$, choose $a$ with $\lambda(av)\ne0$; then $\lambda(eav)=\lambda(ave)=\lambda(av)\ne0$. The other variable is treated similarly. The pairing respects the left $A$-action, so
$$
\operatorname{Soc}(Ae)\cong\operatorname{Soc}D(eA)=D(eA/eAJ).
$$
The right-module quotient $eA/eAJ$ is simple. A <primitive idempotent> remains primitive modulo the nilpotent radical: <idempotents> lift through that radical, so a decomposition in the quotient would lift to one in $eAe$. In the <semisimple algebra> $A/J$, the left simple $Ae/JAe$ and the dual of the right simple $eA/eAJ$ belong to the same <matrix> block. There is exactly one left simple isomorphism class in that block, even over a nonalgebraically closed <field>. Hence the <head-socle identity for a symmetric algebra> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Soc}P\cong P/\operatorname{Rad}P.}
$$
This is an isomorphism of left <modules>, and need not be canonical.

For the modular examples put $r=(123)$, $s=(12)$, so $r^3=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. The coefficient-of-identity functional on the <group algebra> is symmetric and nondegenerate, so the identity just proved applies.

In characteristic two, the <central idempotents> are $b_0=1+r+r^2$ and $b_1=1-b_0$. The first block has basis $b_0,b_0s$ and is $kC_2\cong k[u]/(u^2)$. For the second, choose a primitive cube root $\omega$ and use
$$
r\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0\\0&\omega^2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The two $r$-eigenlines are interchanged by $s$, proving irreducibility. Polynomial interpolation in $r$ gives diagonal <matrix units>, and multiplying by $s$ gives the off-diagonal units. Thus the four-dimensional block maps onto $M_2(k)$ and is isomorphic to it:
$$
\boxed{kS_3\cong k[u]/(u^2)\oplus M_2(k)\quad(\operatorname{char}k=2).}
$$
There are two <simple modules>, the trivial one-dimensional <module> $T$ and the two-dimensional <module> $V$; the sign <module> equals $T$. Their <projective covers> are $P_T=k[u]/(u^2)$, with layers $T,T$, and $P_V=V$. The regular <module> is $P_T\oplus V^{\oplus2}$, and the <Cartan matrix of a group algebra> is $\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$. Every <module> is a sum of $T,P_T,V$: the first block is classified by <Jordan chains> for a <nilpotent linear map> of lengths one and two, while the <matrix> block is semisimple. Thus there are exactly three indecomposable isomorphism classes. This describes the <2-modular blocks of S3>.

In characteristic three put $u=r-1$. The ideal $I=uA$ satisfies $I^3=0$, and $A/I\cong kC_2\cong k\oplus k$, so $J(A)=I$. The only simples are the trivial and sign <modules> $S_+,S_-$. Put $e_\pm=(1\pm s)/2$. The projectives $P_\pm=Ae_\pm$ have dimension three. A convenient radical generator is $v=r-r^{-1}$:
$$
v^3=0,\qquad svs=-v,\qquad v=-u-u^2,\qquad v^2=u^2.
$$
The basis $e_\pm,ve_\pm,v^2e_\pm$ gives the radical layers
$$
\boxed{P_+:\ S_+,S_-,S_+;\qquad P_-:\ S_-,S_+,S_-.}
$$
Thus $A=P_+\oplus P_-$ and its <Cartan matrix of a group algebra> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The elements $e_-ve_+$ and $e_+ve_-$ give one arrow in each direction between the two simples. All paths of length three vanish, and the six paths of lengths zero, one and two are <linearly independent>. Hence $A$ is the <path algebra> of that two-vertex oriented cycle modulo the ideal of paths of length three. Its connected <quiver> also proves there is a single <block of S3 in characteristic three>.

Any <module> is a $\mathbb Z/2$-graded <vector space> given by the $s$-eigenspaces, with $v$ interchanging the grades and $v^3=0$. Constructing <Jordan chains> with homogeneous bases in the <kernel> and image filtrations and homogeneous lifts decomposes it into graded chains. Each chain has length $1,2$ or $3$ and is determined by its top sign. It is a <uniserial module> and hence indecomposable. Consequently there are exactly six indecomposables, the length-$\ell$ quotients $P_\pm/J^\ell P_\pm$, $\ell=1,2,3$.

For a finite-dimensional algebra over an <algebraically closed field>, <basic algebra> means $A/J(A)\cong k^n$, so its <simple modules> are one-dimensional and occur once in the semisimple quotient of the regular <module>. A <hereditary ring> has every <submodule> of a <projective module> projective; equivalently its <global dimension> is at most one.

Choose representatives $S_1,\ldots,S_n$ of the <simple modules>. Our <Ext quiver> convention gives one vertex $i$ for $S_i$ and
$$
\#\{i\to j\}=\dim_k\operatorname{Ext}^1_A(S_i,S_j).
$$
With primitive orthogonal <idempotents> $e_i$, put $P_i=Ae_i$ and $J=J(A)$. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_A(-,S_j)$ to $0\to JP_i\to P_i\to S_i\to0$ gives
$$
\operatorname{Ext}^1_A(S_i,S_j)
\cong\operatorname{Hom}_A(JP_i/J^2P_i,S_j).
$$
The restriction map from $\operatorname{Hom}(P_i,S_j)$ is zero since every map to a simple kills $JP_i$, and $P_i$ is projective. Thus the arrow multiplicity is $\dim e_j(J/J^2)e_i$. This specifies the orientation rather than silently replacing the <quiver> by its opposite.

Because $A$ is hereditary, $JP_i$ is projective. Its semisimple top has $a_{ij}$ copies of $S_j$, where $a_{ij}$ is the arrow multiplicity. Its <projective cover> therefore supplies an epimorphism
$$
\bigoplus_jP_j^{a_{ij}}\twoheadrightarrow JP_i.
$$
This splits by projectivity of $JP_i$. Its <kernel> is a summand contained in the radical of the source; projection onto that summand and <Nakayama lemma> force the <kernel> to be zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{JP_i\cong\bigoplus_jP_j^{a_{ij}},\qquad
\dim P_i=1+\sum_ja_{ij}\dim P_j.}
$$
Every arrow $i\to j$ forces $\dim P_i>\dim P_j$, so $Q$ has no directed cycles.

Choose lifts in $e_jJe_i$ of bases of the arrow spaces. Send the vertices and arrows of the <path algebra> to these <idempotents> and lifts, multiplying paths in the order $\beta\alpha$ for consecutive arrows $\alpha:i\to j$, $\beta:j\to l$. This defines an <algebra homomorphism> $kQ\to A$. It is surjective: vertices span $A/J$, arrows span $J/J^2$, and products show that paths of length $r$ span $J^r/J^{r+1}$. Nilpotence of $J$ then gives all of $A$.

Let $N_i$ be the number of directed paths starting at $i$, including the length-zero path. Acyclicity gives the same recursion $N_i=1+\sum_ja_{ij}N_j$, with value one at a sink. Induction from sinks gives $N_i=\dim P_i$. Therefore
$$
\dim kQ=\sum_iN_i=\sum_i\dim P_i=\dim A.
$$
The surjective homomorphism is an isomorphism. This proves the <basic hereditary algebra path-algebra theorem>:
$$
\boxed{A\cong kQ.}
$$

For each connected component $C$ of the underlying unoriented graph, $e_C=\sum_{i\in C}e_i$ is a <central idempotent>. Paths never connect distinct components, so $kQ=\bigoplus_Ce_CkQ$ as a <direct sum> of <two-sided ideals>. To prove each is a block, let $z$ be any <central idempotent>. Modulo the arrow ideal it has coordinates $c_i\in\{0,1\}$. Commuting $z$ with an arrow $i\to j$ and reducing modulo the square of that ideal gives $c_i=c_j$. Thus these coordinates are constant on each component. If they are zero on a component, the restriction of $z$ there is a nilpotent <idempotent>, hence zero; if they are one, apply that argument to $1-z$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{blocks of }A\ \longleftrightarrow\ \text{connected components of the underlying graph of }Q.}
$$

The <finite representation type of a quiver> condition means there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable finite-dimensional $kQ$-modules. Equivalently, every finite-dimensional <module> is a <direct sum> of members of a finite list of indecomposables.

For the $E_6$ case, we give the reflection argument underlying the <Gabriel theorem>, rather than using its conclusion as the proof. Since the underlying graph is a tree, every orientation is acyclic. At a sink $i$, write
$$
h:\bigoplus_{j\to i}V_j\longrightarrow V_i
$$
for the sum of the incoming arrow maps. If an indecomposable $V$ is not the simple $S_i$, then $h$ is surjective: a nonzero complementary subspace to its image at the sink would be a <direct summand> supported only at $i$. The <Bernstein–Gelfand–Ponomarev reflection functor> reverses the arrows and replaces $V_i$ by $\ker h$, with reversed maps the component projections. The source reflection replaces that <kernel> by the <cokernel> of its inclusion into $\bigoplus V_j$, recovering $V_i$. The inclusion is injective, so the reflected representation has no <direct summand> $S_i$ at the new source. If it decomposed, applying the inverse would decompose $V$. Thus reflection preserves indecomposability except that it annihilates $S_i$.

On a <dimension vector of a quiver representation>, it acts by the integral simple reflection
$$
(s_i d)_i=-d_i+\sum_{j\sim i}d_j,\qquad (s_i d)_j=d_j\quad(j\ne i).
$$
Let $B=2I-\operatorname{Adj}(E_6)$. These reflections preserve the <positive definite quadratic form> $d^{\mathsf T}Bd$. Positivity can be checked without a classification theorem: label the graph by the chain $1-2-3-4-5$ and attach vertex $6$ to $3$. The leading principal minors are $2,3,4,5,6,3$, all positive.

Choose a topological ordering so arrows point from smaller to larger indices. Reflect successively at sinks $6,5,\ldots,1$. This restores the orientation after one full cycle. The transformation of dimension vectors is the <Coxeter element> $C=s_1s_2\cdots s_6$. If $U$ has diagonal entries one and entry $-1$ above the diagonal at an arrow, direct multiplication of the reflection matrices gives
$$
C=-U^{-1}U^{\mathsf T},\qquad I-C=U^{-1}(U+U^{\mathsf T})=U^{-1}B.
$$
Thus $C$ has no fixed vector. It is an integral isometry of a positive definite form, so it has finite order $h$: the image of each lattice <basis vector> lies in a finite set of vectors of the same norm, giving only finitely many possible isometries. Since $C^h=I$ and $I-C$ is invertible,
$$
I+C+\cdots+C^{h-1}=0.
$$
If an indecomposable survived all $h$ reflection cycles, its dimension vectors $d,Cd,\ldots,C^{h-1}d$ would all be nonzero and componentwise nonnegative, contradicting their zero sum. Therefore it reaches a simple at one of the finitely many reflection steps and is then annihilated. Applying the inverse reflections uniquely reconstructs it from that simple. There are at most $6h$ such stopping positions, proving
$$
\boxed{kQ_3\text{ has finite representation type for every orientation of }E_6.}
$$
This supplies the requested finite-type proof sketch.

Take the <Kronecker quiver>, with two arrows $\alpha,\beta:1\to2$. There are no paths of length two, so its <path algebra> has basis $e_1,e_2,\alpha,\beta$ and dimension four.

For each $\lambda\in k$, take a <quiver representation> with one-dimensional spaces at both vertices and arrow maps $(1,\lambda)$. An endomorphism consists of scalars $a,b$ with $b=a$ from the first arrow; the second imposes no further condition. Its <endomorphism ring> is therefore $k$, which has no nontrivial <idempotent>. Thus the representation is indecomposable. An isomorphism to the representation $(1,\mu)$ again requires $b=a\ne0$, and the second arrow then gives $\lambda=\mu$. Because an <algebraically closed field> is infinite, this gives infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic indecomposables. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim_k kQ_2=4,\qquad kQ_2\text{ is not of finite representation type}.}
$$

For a left $\mathbb ZG$-module, <group cohomology> is
$$
\boxed{H^n(G,M)=\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathbb ZG}^n(\mathbb Z,M),}
$$
with $\mathbb Z$ the trivial <module>. Equivalently take a <projective resolution> $P_\bullet\to\mathbb Z$ and the cohomology of $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb ZG}(P_\bullet,M)$. This also describes the right-derived functors of the invariant-module functor $M\mapsto M^G$.

For an explicit model, the homogeneous bar <free resolution> has $P_n=\mathbb Z[G^{n+1}]$, with diagonal $G$-action and boundary the alternating sum of coordinate deletions. It is free over $\mathbb ZG$: each orbit has a unique representative with first coordinate $1$. The augmented complex is exact because inserting $1$ as a first coordinate supplies a contracting homotopy of underlying abelian groups; its lack of $G$-equivariance does not affect exactness. The associated inhomogeneous <group cochains> are functions $f:G^n\to M$, with
$$
(df)(g_1,\ldots,g_{n+1})=
g_1f(g_2,\ldots,g_{n+1})
+\sum_{j=1}^n(-1)^jf(g_1,\ldots,g_jg_{j+1},\ldots,g_{n+1})
+(-1)^{n+1}f(g_1,\ldots,g_n).
$$
For <group cochains> of degree zero, $(dm)(g)=gm-m$. Adjacent deletions cancel in pairs, giving $d^2=0$ and hence $H^n=\ker d/\operatorname{im}d$.

In degree zero this gives
$$
\boxed{H^0(G,M)=M^G=\{m:gm=m\text{ for all }g\}.}
$$
It is also $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb ZG}(\mathbb Z,M)$, since a map is determined by the invariant image of $1$.

In degree one the cocycle condition is $f(gh)=f(g)+gf(h)$, so
$$
\boxed{H^1(G,M)=
\{\text{crossed homomorphisms }G\to M\}/
\{g\mapsto gm-m\}.}
$$
These are <crossed homomorphisms> modulo <principal crossed homomorphisms>. For trivial action they reduce to ordinary <group homomorphisms>, so $H^1(G,M)=\operatorname{Hom}(G_{\mathrm{ab}},M)$.

There are two further useful descriptions. If $I=\ker(\mathbb ZG\to\mathbb Z)$ is the <augmentation ideal>, a <crossed homomorphism> gives an equivariant map $I\to M$ by $g-1\mapsto f(g)$, because $gh-1=(g-1)+g(h-1)$. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}(-,M)$ to $0\to I\to\mathbb ZG\to\mathbb Z\to0$ therefore gives
$$
H^1(G,M)\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb ZG}(I,M)/
\operatorname{res}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb ZG}(\mathbb ZG,M).
$$
Also a section of $M\rtimes G\to G$ has the form $g\mapsto(f(g),g)$ and is a <group homomorphism> exactly when $f$ is a <crossed homomorphism>. Conjugation of sections by an element of $M$ changes $f$ by a <principal crossed homomorphism>. Thus $H^1$ classifies the $M$-conjugacy classes of such sections, or equivalently of complements to $M$ in the <semidirect product>.

In degree two, a normalized <two-cocycle> $c:G\times G\to M$ satisfies
$$
gc(h,l)-c(gh,l)+c(g,hl)-c(g,h)=0,\qquad c(1,g)=c(g,1)=0.
$$
Define multiplication on $M\times G$ by
$$
(m,g)(n,h)=(m+gn+c(g,h),gh).
$$
The displayed cocycle identity is precisely associativity; normalization gives the identity and inverses exist. This produces a <group extension> $1\to M\to E_c\to G\to1$ with the prescribed action on $M$.

Conversely a set-theoretic section $s:G\to E$, $s(1)=1$, gives $c(g,h)=s(g)s(h)s(gh)^{-1}$ in the additive <kernel of a group homomorphism>. Associativity gives the cocycle condition. Replacing $s(g)$ by $b(g)s(g)$ changes $c$ by
$$
(db)(g,h)=gb(h)-b(gh)+b(g).
$$
The resulting extensions are equivalent through a change of coordinates in $M\times G$. Conversely any extension equivalence preserving both <kernel of a group homomorphism> and quotient yields such a change of section. Hence
$$
\boxed{H^2(G,M)\ \longleftrightarrow\
\text{equivalence classes of extensions of }G\text{ by }M
\text{ with the specified action}.}
$$
The zero class corresponds exactly to a split extension. With trivial action these are central <group extensions>; addition of classes corresponds to the <Baer sum>. This proves the <second group cohomology classifies group extensions> interpretation.

For $G=\mathbb Z^2$ and trivial coefficients $\mathbb Z$, put $R=\mathbb Z[x^{\pm1},y^{\pm1}]$. The <Koszul resolution for a rank-two free abelian group> is
$$
0\longrightarrow R\xrightarrow{d_2}R^2\xrightarrow{d_1}R
\xrightarrow{x,y\mapsto1}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0,
$$
where $d_1(a,b)=(x-1)a+(y-1)b$ and $d_2(c)=(-(y-1)c,(x-1)c)$. To check exactness at $R^2$, reduce $d_1(a,b)=0$ modulo $x-1$. Since $y-1$ is not a zero divisor in $\mathbb Z[y^{\pm1}]$, write $b=(x-1)c$, and cancellation gives $a=-(y-1)c$. Injectivity of $d_2$ follows because $R$ is a domain. The augmentation <kernel> is generated by $x-1,y-1$, proving the other exactness statement.

Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-,\mathbb Z)$ makes both differentials zero, since $x,y$ act trivially. Thus
$$
\boxed{H^0(\mathbb Z^2,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z,\quad
H^1(\mathbb Z^2,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z^2,\quad
H^2(\mathbb Z^2,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z,\quad H^n=0\ (n>2).}
$$
Degree-one classes assign arbitrary integers to the two generators. Geometrically these are also the cohomology groups of the <torus>, a <classifying space> for $\mathbb Z^2$; its cohomology ring is the <exterior algebra> on the two degree-one classes.

The integer $n$ in degree two has an explicit extension representative. On triples $(a,b,m)\in\mathbb Z^3$, set
$$
(a,b,m)(c,d,l)=(a+c,b+d,m+l+nad).
$$
The bilinear term is a <two-cocycle>. With generators $x=(1,0,0)$, $y=(0,1,0)$ and central $z=(0,0,1)$,
$$
\boxed{E_n=\langle x,y,z:\ z\text{ central},\ [x,y]=z^n\rangle.}
$$
Every central extension of $\mathbb Z^2$ by $\mathbb Z$ has this form: choose lifts of the two generators and the fixed generator of the <kernel of a group homomorphism>, then their <group commutator> determines $n$ and every element has a unique form $z^my^bx^a$. Changing the lifts by central elements leaves that commutator unchanged. Thus distinct integers give inequivalent extensions, and the cocycles $nad$ add with $n$. The case $n=0$ is $\mathbb Z^3$; $n=1$ is the integer Heisenberg group. This realizes the <integral central extensions of a rank-two free abelian group> concretely.

We first prove the simple Artinian case of the <Artin–Wedderburn theorem>. This part does not need finite dimension over $k$. Choose a minimal nonzero <left ideal> $L$, using the descending chain condition. Simplicity gives $LA=A$. Also $L^2\ne0$: otherwise $LAL=(LA)L=L$ would be zero, since each $lal'$ lies in $L^2$. Choose $x\in L$ for which right multiplication $r_x:L\to L$ is nonzero. By simplicity of $L$, it is an automorphism. Choose $e\in L$ with $ex=x$. Then $(e^2-e)x=0$, and injectivity of $r_x$ gives $e^2=e$. Thus $L=Ae$.

Right multiplication by $e$ splits the left regular <module> as $A=Ae\oplus A(1-e)$. Repeat within the remaining <left ideal>: if it contains a minimal <left ideal> $Af$ generated by an <idempotent>, the map $v\mapsto vf$ splits off $Af$. Each nonzero remaining complement is a strictly smaller <left ideal>, so the Artinian condition terminates the process. Therefore the regular <module> is a finite <direct sum> of minimal <left ideals>.

All these simple summands are isomorphic. For <primitive idempotents> $e,f$, simplicity gives $AeA=A$, so $fAe\ne0$; otherwise $fAeA=fA$ would be zero. A nonzero element of $fAe$ defines a nonzero homomorphism $Af\to Ae$, which is an isomorphism between <simple modules>. Thus $A\cong L^{\oplus n}$ as a left <module>. Put $\Delta=\operatorname{End}_A(L)$, a <division algebra> by the <Schur lemma>. Right multiplication gives
$$
A^{\mathrm{op}}\cong\operatorname{End}_A(A)
\cong M_n(\Delta).
$$
Taking opposites, with <matrix> transposition reversing products, proves
$$
\boxed{A\cong M_n(D),\qquad D=\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\text{ a division algebra}.}
$$
The construction is $k$-linear and embeds $k$ into the center of $D$.

A <central simple algebra> is a finite-dimensional simple $k$-algebra whose center is exactly $k$. The <Brauer group> consists of such algebras modulo <matrix> stabilization: $A,B$ represent the same class if $M_r(A)\cong M_s(B)$ for some positive integers $r,s$. Its operation and inverse are
$$
\boxed{[A][B]=[A\otimes_kB],\qquad 1=[k],\qquad [A]^{-1}=[A^{\mathrm{op}}].}
$$
The <tensor product> is central simple, and $A\otimes A^{\mathrm{op}}\cong\operatorname{End}_k(A)$ by $(a\otimes b)(x)=axb$, explaining the inverse. Both facts can be checked after a <splitting field of a central simple algebra> extension, where they are matrix-algebra identities, and descended by faithful scalar extension. Every class has a unique central division-algebra representative, by the regular-module description above.

To prove infinitude over $\mathbb Q$ explicitly, put $E=\mathbb Q(i)$ and, for $a\ne0$, let
$$
A_a=(-1,a)_{\mathbb Q}=E\oplus Ej,\qquad j^2=a,\quad jz=\overline z\,j.
$$
This is a <quaternion algebra>. It is central simple: the center is $\mathbb Q$, and after adjoining $i$ its generators have the faithful <matrix> model
$$
i\longmapsto\operatorname{diag}(i,-i),\qquad
j\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These generate $M_2(E)$; consequently a nonzero proper <two-sided ideal> over $\mathbb Q$ is impossible. The quaternion conjugation gives
$$
N(z+w j)=N_{E/\mathbb Q}(z)-aN_{E/\mathbb Q}(w).
$$
A nonzero element has an inverse exactly when this norm is nonzero. Thus $A_a$ is a <division algebra> when $a$ is not an $E/\mathbb Q$ norm; if $a$ is a norm it has a nonzero zero divisor and is $M_2(\mathbb Q)$ by the classification just proved.

For a prime $p\equiv3\pmod4$, every nonzero rational sum $x^2+y^2$ has even $p$-adic valuation. Divide out the minimum valuation of $x,y$; cancellation modulo $p$ is impossible because $-1$ is not a square modulo $p$. Therefore $p$ is not an $E/\mathbb Q$ norm, and $A_p$ is a nontrivial division-algebra Brauer class. This is the <quaternion norm obstruction at primes congruent to three modulo four>.

We also prove the product rule needed to distinguish classes. In $A_a\otimes A_b$, write the generators as $i_1,j_1,i_2,j_2$. The commuting subalgebras generated respectively by
$$
i_1,\ j_1j_2\qquad\text{and}\qquad i_1i_2,\ j_2
$$
are $A_{ab}$ and the split <quaternion algebra> $(1,b)_{\mathbb Q}\cong M_2(\mathbb Q)$. The latter has the <matrix> model $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ and $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&b\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Together the two subalgebras generate the whole <tensor product>, since they recover $i_2$ and $j_1$. Dimensions are sixteen on both sides, giving
$$
\boxed{A_a\otimes A_b\cong A_{ab}\otimes M_2(\mathbb Q),\qquad [A_a][A_b]=[A_{ab}].}
$$
Quaternion conjugation identifies $A_a$ with its opposite, so these classes have order at most two. For distinct primes $p,q\equiv3\pmod4$, the parameter $pq$ has odd valuation at $p$, so $A_{pq}$ is a <division algebra>. Thus $[A_p]\ne[A_q]$. There are infinitely many such primes: if their list were $p_1,\ldots,p_m$, the integer $4p_1\cdots p_m-1$ would have a prime factor congruent to three modulo four outside that list. We conclude
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb Q)\text{ is infinite}.}
$$
Indeed the same valuation argument for any nonempty finite product gives <independent quaternion classes over rational numbers>.

For $\mathbb R$, we prove the required division-algebra classification. Let $D$ be finite-dimensional and central over $\mathbb R$. If $D\ne\mathbb R$, choose a nonreal element; its irreducible real <minimal polynomial> is quadratic, so translating and rescaling it gives $i\in D$ with $i^2=-1$. The centralizer $D_+$ of $i$ is a finite-dimensional <division algebra> over $\mathbb C=\mathbb R(i)$, with that <field> central. Every element has a split <minimal polynomial> over $\mathbb C$, hence belongs to $\mathbb C$, so $D_+=\mathbb C$.

Conjugation by $i$ is an involution, giving $D=D_+\oplus D_-$, where $D_-=\{x:ix=-xi\}$. If $D_-=0$, then $D=\mathbb C$, contrary to its center being $\mathbb R$. Choose $j\ne0$ in $D_-$. For any $x\in D_-$, $xj^{-1}$ commutes with $i$, so $D_-=\mathbb Cj$. Also $j^2\in\mathbb C$ and commutes with $j$, forcing $j^2\in\mathbb R$. It cannot be positive, since $(j-\sqrt{j^2})(j+\sqrt{j^2})=0$, or zero, since $D$ is a <division algebra>. Rescale to $j^2=-1$. Consequently
$$
D=\mathbb R\oplus\mathbb Ri\oplus\mathbb Rj\oplus\mathbb Rij\cong\mathbb H.
$$
Conversely the positive definite quaternion norm makes $\mathbb H$ a <division algebra>. The two possible central division representatives are therefore $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb H$. They are not equivalent, and conjugation identifies $\mathbb H$ with its opposite, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb R)\cong\mathbb Z/2,\quad\text{generated by }[\mathbb H].}
$$
This uses the <finite-dimensional real division-algebra classification> directly, not as an unexplained theorem citation.

Finally let $k_s$ be a separable closure and $\Gamma=\operatorname{Gal}(k_s/k)$. The cohomological description is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(k)\cong H^2_{\mathrm{cont}}(\Gamma,k_s^\times)
=\varinjlim_{L/k\ \mathrm{finite\ Galois}}H^2(\operatorname{Gal}(L/k),L^\times).}
$$
Here $k_s^\times$ has the discrete topology and its natural Galois action. For a finite Galois splitting <field> $L$, identify $A\otimes_kL$ with a <matrix algebra>. Its descent action is implemented by matrices $u_\sigma$, unique up to scalars, so
$$
u_\sigma\,\sigma(u_\tau)\,u_{\sigma\tau}^{-1}=c(\sigma,\tau)^{-1}\in L^\times.
$$
The inverse convention here agrees with the crossed-product convention below. The semilinear operators $u_\sigma\sigma$ commute with the descended algebra $A$; their crossed product has class $[A]^{-1}$ by the commuting-factor decomposition of the full endomorphism algebra. Inverting its scalar cocycle therefore gives the class $[A]$. Associativity gives the <two-cocycle> identity, and changing the representatives changes $c$ by a coboundary. Conversely a <two-cocycle> defines the <crossed-product algebra of a Galois extension>, with $u_\sigma a=\sigma(a)u_\sigma$ and $u_\sigma u_\tau=c(\sigma,\tau)u_{\sigma\tau}$. It is central simple and split by $L$. <Galois descent> identifies coboundaries with split matrix-algebra classes and gives $H^2(\operatorname{Gal}(L/k),L^\times)\cong\ker(\operatorname{Br}(k)\to\operatorname{Br}(L))$. Every <central simple algebra> has a finite separable, hence finite Galois, splitting <field>, yielding the displayed limit.
