Label the six vertices $A,B,C,D,E,F$ clockwise, starting at the upper-left vertex. The arrows identify $AB$ with $ED$, $BC$ with $FE$, and $FA$ with $DC$, preserving the indicated directions. Consequently the <quotient space> has exactly two vertices,
$$
P=\{A,C,E\},\qquad Q=\{B,D,F\}.
$$
Orient its three <topological cells> by $a:A\to B$, $b:B\to C$, and $c:F\to A$. The clockwise boundary of the hexagonal <topological cell> traverses
$$
abc^{-1}a^{-1}b^{-1}c.
$$
Every <topological cell> of dimension one therefore occurs once with each sign. The <cellular chain complex>, with integral coefficients, is
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\partial_2=0}\mathbb Z^3
\xrightarrow{\partial_1}\mathbb Z^2\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
\partial_1=
\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&1\\1&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here the rows correspond to $P,Q$. The <image> of $\partial_1$ is the primitive subgroup generated by $Q-P$, and its <kernel> is freely generated by $a+b$ and $a+c$. Thus the <homology groups> are
$$
\boxed{H_j(X;\mathbb Z)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Z,&j=0,2,\\
\mathbb Z^2,&j=1,\\
0,&j>2.
\end{cases}}
$$
The two vertex classes are important: identifying all six vertices would give an incorrect <cellular chain complex>. The result agrees with the <homology> of a <torus>.

Write a point of the larger <sphere> as $(u,v)\in\mathbb R^{m+1}\times\mathbb R^{n-m}$. In the complement, $v\ne0$. There is a <homeomorphism>
$$
X\longrightarrow B^{m+1}_{\mathrm{open}}\times S^{n-m-1},
\qquad (u,v)\longmapsto\left(u,\frac{v}{\lVert v\rVert}\right),
$$
whose inverse sends $(u,w)$ to $(u,\sqrt{1-\lVert u\rVert^2}\,w)$. In particular the complement has a <deformation retraction> onto its transverse <sphere>. Explicitly,
$$
R_t(u,v)=\frac{((1-t)u,v)}{\sqrt{(1-t)^2\lVert u\rVert^2+\lVert v\rVert^2}},
\qquad 0\le t\le1.
$$
The denominator stays positive, the homotopy stays in the complement, and points with $u=0$ remain fixed. This establishes the <homology of an equatorial sphere complement>. By <homotopy invariance of homology>, its <homology> is therefore the <homology of a sphere> of dimension $d=n-m-1$:
$$
\boxed{H_j(X;\mathbb Z)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Z,&d\ge1\text{ and }j=0,d,\\
\mathbb Z^2,&d=0\text{ and }j=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
$$
In the codimension-one case, the complement has two contractible components; this is why degree-zero <homology> has rank two. Equivalently, in every case its only nonzero <reduced homology> is $\widetilde H_d(X;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z$.

The <torus> map acts on $H_1(T^2;\mathbb Z)$ by
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <determinant> is one, so it is an orientation-preserving <homeomorphism> and acts as the identity on both $H_0(T^2)$ and $H_2(T^2)$.

To calculate the <homology> of its <mapping torus>, cover the time <circle> by two arcs. Each inverse image has the <homotopy type> of $T^2$, while their intersection has two components of that <homotopy type>. The map in the <Mayer–Vietoris sequence> can be written
$$
H_q(T^2)\oplus H_q(T^2)\longrightarrow H_q(T^2)\oplus H_q(T^2),
\qquad (a,b)\longmapsto(a+f_*b,-a-b).
$$
Adding the output coordinates and using the input coordinate $a+b$ separates an identity map from $f_*-I$. Canceling the identity summands gives the <Wang sequence>, and hence
$$
0\longrightarrow\operatorname{coker}(f_*-I\mid H_q(T^2))
\longrightarrow H_q(X)
\longrightarrow\ker(f_*-I\mid H_{q-1}(T^2))\longrightarrow0.
$$
In degree one,
$$
A-I=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <image> and <kernel> are both generated by $(1,-1)$; its <cokernel> is $\mathbb Z$ since this image is primitive. Its <Smith normal form> is $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$, so its <torsion subgroup> is zero. On the other two nonzero <homology groups> of the <torus>, $f_*-I=0$. The displayed <short exact sequences> split because their right-hand groups are free. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H_j(X;\mathbb Z)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Z,&j=0,3,\\
\mathbb Z^2,&j=1,2,\\
0,&j>3.
\end{cases}}
$$
In particular, the <mapping torus> contributes an additional degree-one class from the time <circle>, as well as the invariant degree-one class in the fiber.

The <chain map> identity $d_Df=fd_C$ gives, for $(x,y)\in M_i$,
$$
d_f^2(x,y)=\left(d_C^2x,
(-1)^{i-1}f(d_Cx)+(-1)^id_Df(x)+d_D^2y\right)=(0,0).
$$
Thus $M$ is a <chain complex>; it is a sign convention for the algebraic <mapping cone>.

Put $E_i=C_{i-1}$ with differential $d_E=d_C$. Inclusion into the second summand and projection onto the first give a <short exact sequence of chain complexes>
$$
0\longrightarrow D\xrightarrow{\iota}M\xrightarrow{\rho}E\longrightarrow0.
$$
Its <connecting homomorphism> is particularly explicit. If $x\in C_{i-1}$ is a <chain cycle>, lift it to $(x,0)\in M_i$ and take its boundary. The second component is $(-1)^if(x)$, so the <long exact sequence in homology> contains
$$
\cdots\longrightarrow H_i(C)\xrightarrow{(-1)^{i+1}f_*}H_i(D)
\longrightarrow H_i(M)\longrightarrow H_{i-1}(C)
\xrightarrow{(-1)^if_*}H_{i-1}(D)\longrightarrow\cdots.
$$
If every $H_i(M)$ vanishes, the <exact sequence> makes every $f_*$ both injective and surjective. Conversely, if every $f_*$ is an <isomorphism>, the map $H_i(D)\to H_i(M)$ has zero image, while $H_i(M)\to H_{i-1}(C)$ has zero image and zero kernel; hence $H_i(M)=0$.

One can also see the converse directly on <chain cycles>. If $(x,y)\in M_i$ is a <chain cycle>, then $d_Cx=0$ and $d_Dy=-(-1)^if(x)$. Injectivity of the <induced map on homology> $f_*$ gives $x=d_Cz$. Subtracting the <chain boundary> $d_f(z,0)$ leaves $(0,t)$ with $d_Dt=0$. Surjectivity of the <induced map on homology> $f_*$ lets us choose a <chain cycle> $w\in C_i$ and $v\in D_{i+1}$ such that
$$
t=(-1)^{i+1}f(w)+d_Dv.
$$
Then $(0,t)=d_f(w,v)$. Thus every <chain cycle> in $M$ is a <chain boundary>, proving the <mapping cone acyclicity criterion>:
$$
\boxed{H_*(M)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f\text{ is a quasi-isomorphism}.}
$$

For each <prime number> $p$, reduction of the <mapping cone> is the <mapping cone> of the reduced <chain map>. Part (a), whose proof works over any coefficient ring, therefore gives
$$
H_i(M\otimes\mathbb F_p)=0\qquad\text{for every }i,p.
$$
Every $M_i$ is a finitely generated <free abelian group>. Multiplication by $p$ consequently gives a <short exact sequence of chain complexes>
$$
0\longrightarrow M\xrightarrow{p}M\longrightarrow M\otimes\mathbb F_p\longrightarrow0.
$$
The neighboring terms $H_{i+1}(M\otimes\mathbb F_p)$ and $H_i(M\otimes\mathbb F_p)$ in its <long exact sequence in homology> both vanish. Thus multiplication by $p$ on $H_i(M)$ is an <isomorphism> for every <prime number> $p$.

The group $H_i(M)$ is a <finitely generated abelian group>, since it is a quotient of a subgroup of $M_i$. Write it, using the <fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups>, as
$$
H_i(M)\cong\mathbb Z^{b_i}\oplus T_i,
$$
with $T_i$ finite. If $b_i>0$, multiplication by any <prime number> is not surjective. If $T_i\ne0$, choose a <prime number> dividing its order; multiplication by that prime is not injective. Both contradict the preceding <isomorphism>. Hence $H_i(M)=0$ for every $i$.

Applying part (a) integrally now proves the stronger natural conclusion
$$
\boxed{f_*:H_i(C;\mathbb Z)\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }H_i(D;\mathbb Z)\quad\text{for every }i.}
$$
This is <prime coefficient detection of quasi-isomorphisms>. The finite-generation hypothesis is used precisely when excluding nonzero groups on which every prime acts invertibly.

Choose <orientations> on the two <sphere> factors whose product is the specified <orientation> of $X$. Give the four factors of $Y=(S^2)^4$ the corresponding <orientations> in the order $1,2,3,4$. The <Künneth theorem> has no <Tor functor> terms here and gives
$$
\boxed{H_j(Y;\mathbb Z)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbb Z,&j=0,8,\\
\mathbb Z^4,&j=2,6,\\
\mathbb Z^6,&j=4,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
$$
Let $u_i\in H^2(Y;\mathbb Z)$ be the pullback of the positively normalized <cohomology> class from factor $i$. The <cohomology ring> is
$$
H^*(Y;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z[u_1,u_2,u_3,u_4]/(u_1^2,u_2^2,u_3^2,u_4^2),
\qquad |u_i|=2,
$$
with $\langle u_1u_2u_3u_4,[Y]\rangle=1$. Its generators commute by <graded commutativity of the cup product>.

Fix a point in each <sphere>. For each pair $i<j$, let $S_{ij}$ be the four-dimensional <submanifold> in which factors $i,j$ vary and the remaining factors equal their fixed points. Orient $S_{ij}$ by the ordered factors $i,j$. The six <homology classes>
$$
[S_{12}],\ [S_{13}],\ [S_{14}],\ [S_{23}],\ [S_{24}],\ [S_{34}]
$$
give a <coordinate sphere intersection basis> by the <homology cross product> description in the <Künneth theorem>. Moving a fixed point in one of the other two factors produces a disjoint homologous representative of each $S_{ij}$. Hence every basis element has zero <self-intersection number>.

More precisely, the <intersection pairing> is
$$
[S_{ij}]\mathbin{\cdot}[S_{kl}]=
\begin{cases}
1,&\{i,j\}\sqcup\{k,l\}=\{1,2,3,4\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
For complementary pairs the representatives intersect transversely at one point. The sign is positive because swapping two-dimensional factors contributes $(-1)^{2\cdot2}=1$. For pairs with a common index, a factor fixed in both representatives can be displaced to make them disjoint. In the paired order $(12,34),(13,24),(14,23)$, the <intersection form> is three copies of $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

For the <diagonal map> $\delta:X\to Y$, write $a,b\in H^2(X;\mathbb Z)$ for the two <sphere> generators. Then
$$
\delta^*u_1=\delta^*u_3=a,\qquad
\delta^*u_2=\delta^*u_4=b,
\qquad a^2=b^2=0,\quad\langle ab,[X]\rangle=1.
$$
The coefficient of $[S_{ij}]$ in $[\Delta]$ is $\langle u_iu_j,[\Delta]\rangle$. Pullback and the preceding <cup product> relations show that these coefficients are one for $12,14,23,34$ and zero for $13,24$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[\Delta]=[S_{12}]+[S_{14}]+[S_{23}]+[S_{34}].}
$$
There are exactly two complementary pairings among these four terms, each counted twice in the square. Thus
$$
\boxed{[\Delta]\mathbin{\cdot}[\Delta]=2(1+1)=4.}
$$
This computes both the explicit <homology class> and its <intersection pairing>, including all <orientation> signs.

Work with coefficients $\mathbb F_2$. For $n\ge1$, the <mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space> and the <Künneth theorem> give
$$
H^*(\mathbb{RP}^{2n};\mathbb F_2)=\mathbb F_2[z]/(z^{2n+1}),
\qquad
H^*(\mathbb{RP}^{n}\times\mathbb{RP}^{n};\mathbb F_2)
=\mathbb F_2[x,y]/(x^{n+1},y^{n+1}),
$$
where all three generators have degree one. Any <induced map on cohomology> has $f^*z=\alpha x+\beta y$, with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb F_2$. By naturality of the <cup product>, the only possible term in top degree is
$$
f^*(z^{2n})=(\alpha x+\beta y)^{2n}
=\binom{2n}{n}\alpha^n\beta^n x^ny^n.
$$
All other terms vanish because their exponent of $x$ or $y$ exceeds $n$. But
$$
\binom{2n}{n}=2\binom{2n-1}{n-1}
$$
is even for every $n\ge1$. Thus the displayed top-degree <induced map on cohomology> is zero for every $f$.

Both top-degree <homology groups> are one-dimensional over $\mathbb F_2$, and their dual <cohomology groups> pair with them nondegenerately. Naturality of this evaluation makes $f_*$ dual to $f^*$, so $f_*$ is also zero and cannot be an <isomorphism>. This is the <mod-two degree obstruction for equal projective factors>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{There is no such map for any positive }n.}
$$
If zero is included among the allowed values, $\mathbb{RP}^0$ is a point and the unique map is an <isomorphism> on $H_0$. In that convention, **the only value is $n=0$**.

Use the standard <orientations> on the three <spheres> and the complex <orientation> on <complex projective space>. Let $h\in H^2(\mathbb{CP}^3;\mathbb Z)$ satisfy $\langle h^3,[\mathbb{CP}^3]\rangle=1$. The <cohomology ring of complex projective space> is $\mathbb Z[h]/(h^4)$. The <Künneth theorem> gives
$$
H^*((S^2)^3;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z[a,b,c]/(a^2,b^2,c^2),
\qquad |a|=|b|=|c|=2,
\qquad \langle abc,[(S^2)^3]\rangle=1.
$$
Write the <induced map on cohomology> as $f^*h=pa+qb+rc$ with integers $p,q,r$. Naturality and <graded commutativity of the cup product> give
$$
f^*(h^3)=(pa+qb+rc)^3=6pqr\,abc.
$$
The <mapping degree> is therefore $d=6pqr$. This is the <factorial degree obstruction for products of two-spheres>. Every positive value is a positive multiple of six, so **the least possible positive degree is at least $6$**.

To attain it, identify each oriented <sphere> with $\mathbb{CP}^1$. Send three projective linear forms to the coefficients of their product:
$$
F:(\mathbb{CP}^1)^3\longrightarrow\mathbb{CP}^3,
\qquad ([a_1:b_1],[a_2:b_2],[a_3:b_3])\longmapsto[c_0:c_1:c_2:c_3],
$$
where
$$
\prod_{i=1}^3(a_i z+b_i w)=c_0z^3+c_1z^2w+c_2zw^2+c_3w^3.
$$
No product of nonzero linear forms is the zero polynomial. Rescaling any factor rescales all coefficients by the same nonzero scalar. The map is therefore well-defined and is a <holomorphic map> on the products of projective charts.

Choose a homogeneous cubic with three distinct roots, all in an affine chart. Its preimages are exactly the $3!=6$ orderings of its three projective linear factors. In root coordinates, the derivative of the elementary-symmetric coefficient map has <determinant> equal, up to sign, to the <Vandermonde determinant>, the product of the three pairwise root differences. This is nonzero for distinct roots, so all six preimages are regular. A nonsingular complex-linear derivative preserves the underlying real <orientation>, since its real <determinant> is the squared absolute value of its complex <determinant>. Each preimage consequently contributes $+1$ to the <mapping degree>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\deg F=6,\qquad d_{\min}=6.}
$$
This <polynomial multiplication map to complex projective space> provides the requested example without assuming that a prescribed <cohomology> homomorphism is realizable by a continuous map.

Let $D^n_+$ and $D^n_-$ be the two closed hemispheres of the <sphere>. Use $\gamma$ as the <clutching function> between the trivial bundles on these hemispheres. The <clutching construction> defines
$$
V_\gamma=
\bigl((D^n_+\times\mathbb R^k)\sqcup(D^n_-\times\mathbb R^k)\bigr)/\sim,
\qquad (u,v)_+\sim(u,\gamma(u)v)_-,\quad u\in S^{n-1}.
$$
The projection to $S^n$ is locally a <vector bundle>. In a collar of the equator, extend the transition function by keeping it constant in the normal coordinate; this gives explicit <vector bundle trivializations> across the seam. Since the transition matrices lie in the <special orthogonal group>, the standard fiber <orientations> and Euclidean <inner products> agree on overlaps. Thus this is an oriented rank-$k$ <vector bundle> with a <fiber metric>.

For $k\ge2$, choose a base point $u_0$ on the equator. A constant change of the negative hemisphere frame replaces $\gamma$ by $\gamma(u_0)^{-1}\gamma$, so we may suppose $\gamma(u_0)=I$. With $e_k$ the last standard unit vector, define the <section obstruction for a clutched vector bundle>
$$
\boxed{f_\gamma=[u\longmapsto\gamma(u)e_k]\in\pi_{n-1}(S^{k-1});\qquad r=n-1,\ s=k-1.}
$$
The crucial point is that a <nowhere-zero section> can be normalized using the <fiber metric>. It is then represented by continuous maps $\sigma_\pm:D^n_\pm\to S^{k-1}$ satisfying
$$
\sigma_-(u)=\gamma(u)\sigma_+(u)
$$
on the equator.

If $f_\gamma=0$, the <extension-null-homotopy criterion for a sphere> extends $u\mapsto\gamma(u)e_k$ to $D^n_-$. Take this extension as $\sigma_-$ and the constant map $e_k$ as $\sigma_+$. They glue to a <nowhere-zero section>.

Conversely, suppose such a section exists. Restricting a contraction of $D^n_+$ shows that $\sigma_+|_{S^{n-1}}$ is homotopic to a constant. The target <sphere> is path connected, so the constant can be moved to $e_k$. Multiplying this homotopy pointwise by $\gamma(u)$ gives a <homotopy> from $\gamma\sigma_+$ to $\gamma e_k$. The first of these maps equals $\sigma_-|_{S^{n-1}}$ and extends over $D^n_-$, hence is <null-homotopic>. So $\gamma e_k$ is <null-homotopic> too. A freely null-homotopic based sphere map represents zero in its <homotopy group>; any change of base point sends the zero class to zero. We have proved both directions:
$$
\boxed{f_\gamma=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V_\gamma\text{ has a nowhere-zero section}.}
$$
For $n=1$, the same argument reads as the extension criterion for a two-point boundary and uses the trivial set $\pi_0(S^{k-1})$ when $k\ge2$. For $k=1$, the <special orthogonal group> $SO(1)$ is trivial, so the <vector bundle> is already a <trivial vector bundle>; the corresponding constant map to $S^0$ has zero obstruction. These cases require no higher <homotopy group> calculation.

If $k>n$, then $n-1<k-1$ and the permitted vanishing of lower <homotopy groups> of a <sphere> makes $f_\gamma=0$. Normalize the resulting <nowhere-zero section> to a unit section $\sigma$, and set
$$
V'_x=\{v\in(V_\gamma)_x:\langle v,\sigma(x)\rangle=0\}.
$$
Local orthonormal frames show that these <orthogonal complements> form a rank-$(k-1)$ <vector bundle>. The explicit <vector bundle isomorphism>
$$
V'\oplus(S^n\times\mathbb R)\longrightarrow V_\gamma,
\qquad (x,v,t)\longmapsto v+t\sigma(x)
$$
has inverse $w\mapsto(w-\langle w,\sigma\rangle\sigma,\langle w,\sigma\rangle)$. Orient $V'$ so that a positive frame followed by $\sigma$ is positive in $V_\gamma$. This is <splitting a trivial line from a nowhere-zero section> and proves the oriented splitting
$$
\boxed{V_\gamma\cong V'\oplus\mathbb R\qquad(k>n).}
$$
Finally consider the <special orthogonal sphere fibration>
$$
SO(s)\longrightarrow SO(s+1)\xrightarrow{p}S^s,
\qquad p(A)=Ae_{s+1}.
$$
The fiber over $e_{s+1}$ consists exactly of $\operatorname{diag}(B,1)$ with $B\in SO(s)$. Near any unit vector, the <Gram-Schmidt process> continuously completes it to an oriented orthonormal frame, giving local sections of $p$ and local product <fiber bundle> charts. In particular $p$ has the <homotopy lifting property>.

Let $\alpha:S^r\to SO(s+1)$ be based and suppose $r<s$. The composite $p\alpha$ is a based <null-homotopic map> by the given vanishing of $\pi_r(S^s)$. Lift a based null-homotopy of $p\alpha$, beginning at $\alpha$, to a homotopy $G:S^r\times[0,1]\to SO(s+1)$. The lift might initially move its base point $u_0$, but $G(u_0,t)$ always fixes $e_{s+1}$. Right multiplication by $G(u_0,t)^{-1}$ corrects this: the homotopy
$$
\widetilde G(u,t)=G(u,t)G(u_0,t)^{-1}
$$
still covers the same sphere homotopy, remains based, and starts at $\alpha$. At its endpoint every matrix fixes $e_{s+1}$, so $\widetilde G(-,1)$ lies in the included $SO(s)$. Thus every <homotopy class> in the target is represented by one from $SO(s)$:
$$
\boxed{\pi_r(SO(s))\longrightarrow\pi_r(SO(s+1))\text{ is surjective for }r<s.}
$$
This proves <surjectivity of special orthogonal stabilization below the sphere dimension>. For $r=0$, this is the corresponding assertion about path components; the positive-rank <special orthogonal groups> are connected. The lifting argument also explains the zero term $\pi_r(S^s)$ in the <long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration>.
