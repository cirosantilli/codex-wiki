A <locally ringed space> is a <topological space> $X$ equipped with a <sheaf> of commutative <rings> $\mathcal O_X$ whose <stalk> $\mathcal O_{X,x}$ at every point is a <local ring>. A <morphism of locally ringed spaces> $(f,f^\#):(Y,\mathcal O_Y)\to(X,\mathcal O_X)$ consists of a <continuous map> $f:Y\to X$ and a <morphism of sheaves> of <rings> $f^\#: \mathcal O_X\to f_*\mathcal O_Y$, with every induced <stalk> map $\mathcal O_{X,f(y)}\to\mathcal O_{Y,y}$ a <local homomorphism>: the inverse image of the target <maximal ideal> is the source <maximal ideal>. Here $f_*\mathcal O_Y$ is the <direct image sheaf>, with sections $\mathcal O_Y(f^{-1}U)$ on $U$.

Let $\phi:A\to B$ be a <ring homomorphism>. Define the map between the <spectra of rings> by
$$
f(\mathfrak q)=\phi^{-1}(\mathfrak q).
$$
The inverse image is a <prime ideal>, and $f^{-1}(D(a))=D(\phi(a))$. Thus $f$ is a <continuous map>, because the <principal open subsets> form a basis. On these <principal open subsets>, define the <structure sheaf> map using the <localizations>
$$
A_a\longrightarrow B_{\phi(a)},\qquad \frac{b}{a^r}\longmapsto\frac{\phi(b)}{\phi(a)^r}.
$$
These maps commute with restrictions and consequently give a <morphism of sheaves>. At $\mathfrak q$, putting $\mathfrak p=\phi^{-1}\mathfrak q$, the <stalk> map is $A_{\mathfrak p}\to B_{\mathfrak q}$. An element $b/s$ belongs to its inverse image of $\mathfrak qB_{\mathfrak q}$ exactly when $b\in\mathfrak p$, so this is a <local homomorphism>. We have constructed a <morphism of locally ringed spaces>.

Conversely, take a <morphism of locally ringed spaces> $f:\operatorname{Spec}B\to\operatorname{Spec}A$. Its map on <global sections> gives a <ring homomorphism>
$$
\phi:A=\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X)\longrightarrow\Gamma(Y,\mathcal O_Y)=B.
$$
Locality of the <stalk> map implies, for every $a\in A$,
$$
a\in f(\mathfrak q)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a/1\in\mathfrak m_{f(\mathfrak q)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi(a)/1\in\mathfrak qB_{\mathfrak q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi(a)\in\mathfrak q.
$$
Hence the point map must be $\mathfrak q\mapsto\phi^{-1}\mathfrak q$. On $D(a)$, its <structure sheaf> map must send $b/1$ to $\phi(b)/1$ and the inverse of $a$ to the inverse of $\phi(a)$. Thus it is precisely the <localization> map constructed above. Agreement on this basis proves agreement of the <morphisms of sheaves>. In particular, **the inducing ring homomorphism exists and is unique**.

If $\phi$ is surjective, write $B=A/I$. The correspondence between <prime ideals> of $A/I$ and <prime ideals> of $A$ containing $I$ identifies $\operatorname{Spec}B$ homeomorphically with the closed subset $V(I)$: the <principal open subsets> correspond on both sides. Moreover, the <stalk> maps are the surjections $A_{\mathfrak p}\to(A/I)_{\mathfrak p/I}$, so $\mathcal O_X\to f_*\mathcal O_Y$ is surjective.

For the converse, it is important not to assume that <global sections> preserve an arbitrary surjection of <sheaves>. Here the <affine module-sheaf equivalence> supplies the extra information: on every $D(a)$,
$$
(f_*\mathcal O_Y)(D(a))=B_{\phi(a)}=B\otimes_AA_a.
$$
Thus $f_*\mathcal O_Y$ is the <quasi-coherent sheaf> associated with the $A$-<module> $B$, and the <cokernel sheaf> of $\mathcal O_X\to f_*\mathcal O_Y$ is associated with the <module> $C=\operatorname{coker}(A\to B)$, since <localization of a module> is exact. Surjectivity of the <morphism of sheaves> gives $C_{\mathfrak p}=0$ for every <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$. This implies $C=0$: if $0\ne c\in C$, its proper annihilator lies in a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$, and then $c/1\ne0$ in $C_{\mathfrak m}$. Therefore $A\to B$ is surjective. We have proved the <closed immersion criterion for affine schemes>:
$$
\boxed{\phi\text{ surjective}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ f\text{ is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset and }\mathcal O_X\twoheadrightarrow f_*\mathcal O_Y.}
$$

A <proper morphism> of <schemes> is a <separated morphism> of <finite type> which is a <universally closed morphism>: after every <base change>, the resulting map of underlying <topological spaces> takes closed subsets to closed subsets.

The <valuative criterion for properness>, in the Noetherian setting used here, says that a <finite type morphism> $f:X\to Y$ is a <proper morphism> if and only if the following existence and uniqueness condition holds. For every <valuation ring> $V$, its <fraction field> $K$, and compatible <morphisms of schemes> $a:\operatorname{Spec}K\to X$ and $b:\operatorname{Spec}V\to Y$ with $f\circ a=b|_{\operatorname{Spec}K}$, there is exactly one <morphism of schemes> $h:\operatorname{Spec}V\to X$ such that $h|_{\operatorname{Spec}K}=a$ and $f\circ h=b$. All <valuation rings> are allowed; no discreteness assumption is needed in this formulation. The following arguments also use that each of the three defining conditions for a <proper morphism> is preserved by <base change>.

A <finite morphism> is a <finite type morphism>. It is also a <separated morphism>: on an <affine scheme> chart $\operatorname{Spec}B\to\operatorname{Spec}A$, its <diagonal morphism> is represented by the surjective multiplication <ring homomorphism> $B\otimes_AB\to B$, and is therefore a <closed immersion> by Question 1.

We prove the lifting condition in the <valuative criterion for properness>. Choose an <affine open subset> of $Y$ containing the image of the closed point of $\operatorname{Spec}V$. Every point of $\operatorname{Spec}V$ is a generalization of its closed point, so the whole map factors through this <affine open subset>. Its inverse image under the <finite morphism> is $\operatorname{Spec}B$, with $B$ a finitely generated $A$-<module>. Every $b\in B$ is integral over $A$: apply the <determinant trick> to multiplication by $b$ on a finite set of <module> generators to obtain a monic polynomial which annihilates all generators and hence annihilates $1$.

In the given valuative square, we have compatible <ring homomorphisms> $A\to V$ and $B\to K$. The image in $K$ of each $b\in B$ satisfies a monic polynomial over $V$. To see directly why it belongs to $V$, suppose $x\in K\setminus V$. The defining property of a <valuation ring> gives $x^{-1}\in V$, and it lies in the <maximal ideal> since it cannot be a unit. A monic equation for $x$, multiplied by $x^{-n}$, would then express $1$ as a sum of elements in that <maximal ideal>, a contradiction. This proves that <valuation rings are integrally closed> and that $B\to K$ factors through $B\to V$. The factorization is unique because $V\hookrightarrow K$ is injective. Thus the lift exists uniquely, proving
$$
\boxed{\text{finite morphisms are proper}.}
$$

The composite of two <finite type morphisms> is of <finite type>: on suitable <affine open subsets>, take the algebra generators of the first algebra together with the images of the algebra generators of the second. To check <separatedness>, factor the <diagonal morphism> of the composite as
$$
X\xrightarrow{\Delta_f}X\times_YX\longrightarrow X\times_ZX.
$$
The first arrow is a <closed immersion>, and the second is the <base change> of the <closed immersion> $\Delta_g:Y\to Y\times_ZY$. Hence their composite is a <closed immersion>.

Finally, after any <base change> $Z'\to Z$, the composite is $X'\xrightarrow{f'}Y'\xrightarrow{g'}Z'$. Both arrows take closed subsets to closed subsets, so their composite does as well. This is <universal closedness>. All three conditions hold, and therefore
$$
\boxed{f,g\text{ proper}\ \Longrightarrow\ gf\text{ proper}.}
$$

\Gamma_f:X\longrightarrow X\times_ZY,\qquad x\longmapsto(x,f(x)).
$$
It is a <closed immersion>: the square with $\Delta_g:Y\to Y\times_ZY$ and the map $X\times_ZY\to Y\times_ZY$, $(x,y)\mapsto(f(x),y)$, is a <fibre product of schemes> square. The <separatedness> of $g$ makes $\Delta_g$ a <closed immersion>, and <base change> preserves a <closed immersion>.

The projection $\pi:X\times_ZY\to Y$ is the <base change> of the <proper morphism> $gf:X\to Z$, so it is a <proper morphism>. The <closed immersion> $\Gamma_f$ is a <finite morphism>, hence a <proper morphism> by part (i). Since $f=\pi\Gamma_f$, part (ii) proves
$$
\boxed{gf\text{ proper},\quad g\text{ separated}\ \Longrightarrow\ f\text{ proper}.}
$$
This argument supplies <finite type> for $f$ as well; it need not be separately assumed.

We first justify <surjectivity is preserved by base change> for <morphisms of schemes>. Given $y'$ over $y$ and a point $x$ over $y$, the <fiber> after the residue-<field> extension contains a point because
$$
\kappa(x)\otimes_{\kappa(y)}\kappa(y')\ne0.
$$
Indeed, tensoring a nonzero <vector space> over a <field> with a <field extension> remains nonzero; a nonzero commutative <ring> has a <prime ideal>, giving the required point. Thus every <base change> of $f$ is surjective.

Now make an arbitrary <base change> $Z'\to Z$. For a closed subset $C\subseteq Y'=Y\times_ZZ'$, its inverse image under $f':X'\to Y'$ is closed. The <proper morphism> $(gf)':X'\to Z'$ therefore has closed image on that inverse image. Surjectivity of $f'$ gives
$$
g'(C)=(g'f')\bigl((f')^{-1}(C)\bigr),
$$
so $g'(C)$ is closed. This proves that $g$ is a <universally closed morphism>. Together with the given <separatedness> and <finite type>, it proves
$$
\boxed{gf\text{ proper},\ f\text{ surjective},\ g\text{ separated and of finite type}\ \Longrightarrow\ g\text{ proper}.}
$$

Write $f$ as the <ring homomorphism> $A\to B$. By the permitted assumption, $B$ is a finitely generated $A$-algebra. We will prove that every element of $B$ is integral over $A$, including any nilpotent contribution. If $B=0$, there is nothing to prove.

We use the <integral closure as an intersection of valuation rings> in its general form: if an <integral domain> $R$ lies in a <field> $K$, then its <integral closure> inside $K$ is the intersection of all <valuation rings> of $K$ containing $R$. Here $K$ need not be the <fraction field> of $R$. One direction follows from <valuation rings are integrally closed>. For the other, if $0\ne x\in K$ is not integral, the <ideal> $x^{-1}R[x^{-1}]$ is proper: an expression for $1$ in that <ideal>, multiplied by a sufficiently large power of $x$, would be a monic equation for $x$. Choose a <maximal ideal> containing this <ideal>, and apply the <valuation domination lemma> to the corresponding <local ring> inside $K$. The resulting <valuation ring> $V$ contains $R$ and has $x^{-1}$ in its <maximal ideal>, so $x\notin V$. This proves the claimed intersection criterion. The <valuation domination lemma> is obtained by choosing, using <Zorn\'s lemma>, a maximal local overring dominating the given <local ring>; the usual alternative between adjoining $z$ and adjoining $z^{-1}$ forces this maximal overring to be a <valuation ring>.

The <Noetherian ring> $B$ has finitely many minimal <prime ideals> $\mathfrak q_1,\ldots,\mathfrak q_r$. Set
$$
D_i=B/\mathfrak q_i,\qquad K_i=\operatorname{Frac}(D_i),\qquad R_i=\operatorname{im}(A\to D_i).
$$
Take any <valuation ring> $V$ of $K_i$ containing $R_i$. The maps $B\to D_i\to K_i$ and $A\to R_i\to V$ form a square for the <valuative criterion for properness>. Its lift gives $B\to V$, whose composite with $V\hookrightarrow K_i$ is the original map. Consequently the image $D_i$ is contained in $V$. The intersection criterion proves that every element of $D_i$ is integral over $R_i$.

Fix $b\in B$. For each $i$, choose a monic polynomial $P_i(T)\in A[T]$ whose value at the image of $b$ in $D_i$ is zero; coefficients over $R_i$ can be lifted to $A$. The product $P(T)=\prod_iP_i(T)$ is monic and satisfies
$$
P(b)\in\bigcap_i\mathfrak q_i=\sqrt{(0)}.
$$
The <nilradical> of a <Noetherian ring> is nilpotent. Explicitly, choose finitely many generators $n_j$ with $n_j^{e_j}=0$; every product of $1+\sum_j(e_j-1)$ generators then contains a vanishing power. Thus $(\sqrt{(0)})^N=0$ for some $N$, and $P(T)^N$ is a monic polynomial annihilating $b$. Hence $B$ is integral over $A$.

Finally choose algebra generators $b_1,\ldots,b_s$, with monic equations of degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_s$. Reducing powers in these equations shows that the finitely many monomials
$$
b_1^{a_1}\cdots b_s^{a_s},\qquad 0\leq a_j<d_j,
$$
span $B$ as an $A$-<module>. Therefore $B$ is a finite $A$-<module>, proving
$$
\boxed{\text{a proper morphism between affine Noetherian schemes is finite}.}
$$

An $A$-<module homomorphism> $h:M\to N$ induces, by <localization of a module>, compatible maps
$$
M_a\longrightarrow N_a,\qquad m/a^r\longmapsto h(m)/a^r
$$
on all <principal open subsets> $D(a)$. These glue to an $\mathcal O_X$-linear <morphism of sheaves> $\widetilde h:\widetilde M\to\widetilde N$.

Conversely, taking <global sections> of an $\mathcal O_X$-linear <morphism of sheaves> gives an $A$-<module homomorphism> $h:M\to N$, since $\Gamma(X,\widetilde M)=M$ and $\Gamma(X,\widetilde N)=N$. On $D(a)$, compatibility with restriction determines the image of $m/1$, and $\mathcal O_X$-linearity determines the image of $a^{-r}m$. It must be $h(m)/a^r$. Thus the <morphism of sheaves> is uniquely recovered from $h$. Both constructions preserve addition and composition, proving the <affine module-sheaf equivalence> on morphisms:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O_X}(\widetilde M,\widetilde N)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_A(M,N).}
$$
No finite-generation assumption is used.

Use the local-presentation definition of a <quasi-coherent sheaf>: locally it admits a presentation $\mathcal O^{(I)}\to\mathcal O^{(J)}\to\mathcal F\to0$ by direct sums of copies of the <structure sheaf>, with no finiteness requirement on the index sets. If $M$ is an $A$-<module>, choose a free <module> presentation $A^{(I)}\to A^{(J)}\to M\to0$. Exactness of <localization of a module> gives the corresponding local presentation of $\widetilde M$. Hence a <sheaf> which is associated with a <module> on every <affine open subset> is a <quasi-coherent sheaf>.

For the converse, begin with a <quasi-coherent sheaf> and a local presentation on an <affine open subset> $V=\operatorname{Spec}R$. The free <sheaves> have <global sections> $R^{(I)}$ and $R^{(J)}$: a section has locally finite support, and quasi-compactness of $V$ makes its support finite globally. Part (i) identifies the presentation map with an $R$-<module homomorphism>. Exactness of <localization> then identifies its <cokernel sheaf> with $\widetilde M_V$ for the <module> cokernel $M_V$. Thus there is an <affine open cover> on which $\mathcal F$ is associated with <modules>.

It remains to show this on an arbitrary <affine open subset> $U=\operatorname{Spec}A$, rather than only the selected cover. Around each point of $U$, choose an affine neighborhood contained in $U$ with such a local presentation. Choose a finite cover $U=\bigcup_{i=1}^rD(s_i)$ refining these neighborhoods. On each $D(s_i)$ the restriction is associated with a <module>. Its restriction to $D(s_is_j)$ is therefore the corresponding <localization>, and the same is true after intersecting with $D(a)$. Put $M=\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)$. The <sheaf gluing axiom> gives
$$
M=\ker\left(\prod_i\Gamma(D(s_i),\mathcal F)\longrightarrow\prod_{i,j}\Gamma(D(s_is_j),\mathcal F)\right),
$$
where the arrow is the difference of the two restriction maps. <Localization of a module> commutes with kernels and finite products. Localizing this formula at $a$ therefore gives exactly the gluing formula for $D(a)$, covered by the $D(as_i)$. Consequently
$$
M_a\simeq\Gamma(D(a),\mathcal F),
$$
compatibly with restriction. These isomorphisms on the <principal open subsets> identify $\widetilde M$ with $\mathcal F|_U$. We have proved the <global-section localization for quasi-coherent sheaves> and, in particular,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F|_U\simeq\widetilde{\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)}\quad\text{for every affine open }U.}
$$

Work on an <affine open subset> $U=\operatorname{Spec}A$ and write $\mathcal F|_U=\widetilde M$ and $\mathcal E|_U=\widetilde N$ using part (ii). We first show that any section $e\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal E)$ lifts to $\Gamma(U,\mathcal G)$.

A surjection of <sheaves> supplies local lifts $g_i$ on a finite cover by <principal open subsets> $D(s_i)$. Their differences
$$
c_{ij}=g_i-g_j\in M_{s_is_j}
$$
satisfy $c_{ij}+c_{jk}=c_{ik}$ on triple intersections. We spell out why this <Čech cocycle> is a <coboundary>. Choose a common sufficiently large power $n$ so that $s_j^nc_{ij}$ can be represented in $M_{s_i}$ for every pair. Increasing $n$ also kills the finitely many <localization> errors in the cocycle identities: after extending $s_k^nc_{ik}$ to $M_{s_i}$ and $s_k^nc_{jk}$ to $M_{s_j}$, their difference in $M_{s_is_j}$ is $s_k^nc_{ij}$. This follows because any error which vanishes after localizing at $s_k$ is killed by a power of $s_k$; there are only finitely many indices.

The $D(s_i^n)$ still cover $U$, so the <ideal> generated by the $s_i^n$ is $A$. Choose $a_i\in A$ with $\sum_i a_is_i^n=1$ and put
$$
b_i=\sum_k a_ks_k^nc_{ik}\in M_{s_i},
$$
using the extended representatives just chosen. On an overlap, the cocycle identity gives
$$
b_i-b_j=\sum_k a_ks_k^nc_{ij}=c_{ij}.
$$
Thus $g_i-b_i$ agree and glue to a lift of $e$. This is the degree-one argument behind the <exactness of the unit-ideal localization Čech complex>.

The same reasoning applies to every $D(a)\subseteq U$. Hence we have short exact sequences of <modules> of sections on $U$ and on $D(a)$. Localize the first sequence at $a$ and compare them. The maps on the outer <modules> are the isomorphisms from part (ii):
$$
\begin{array}{ccccccccc}
0&\to&\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)_a&\to&\Gamma(U,\mathcal G)_a&\to&\Gamma(U,\mathcal E)_a&\to&0\\
&&\downarrow\simeq&&\downarrow&&\downarrow\simeq\\
0&\to&\Gamma(D(a),\mathcal F)&\to&\Gamma(D(a),\mathcal G)&\to&\Gamma(D(a),\mathcal E)&\to&0.
\end{array}
$$
An elementary diagram chase makes the middle map an isomorphism: subtract a lift from the right to reduce surjectivity to the left, and use injectivity on the left to prove injectivity. These isomorphisms identify $\mathcal G|_U$ with the <quasi-coherent sheaf> associated with $\Gamma(U,\mathcal G)$. Doing this on every <affine open subset> proves
$$
\boxed{\mathcal G\text{ is quasi-coherent}.}
$$
The proof even works without the Noetherian assumption.

Take the <projective line> $X=\mathbb P^1_k$ and the <coherent sheaves> $\mathcal F=\mathcal O_X$ and $\mathcal G=\mathcal O_X(-1)$, where the latter is a <twisting sheaf on projective space>. Both are <line bundles>, so each <stalk> is a free rank-one $\mathcal O_{X,x}$-<module> and therefore $\mathcal F_x\simeq\mathcal G_x$ at every point.

They are nevertheless not isomorphic. On the standard charts with coordinates $t$ and $t^{-1}$, choose frames $e_0,e_1$ of $\mathcal O(-1)$ with $e_1=t^{-1}e_0$. A <global section> would give polynomials $a(t)$ and $b(t^{-1})$ satisfying
$$
a(t)=t^{-1}b(t^{-1}).
$$
The left side has only nonnegative powers of $t$, and the right side only strictly negative powers. Thus both vanish, giving $\Gamma(X,\mathcal O(-1))=0$. For the <structure sheaf>, the corresponding equality is $a(t)=b(t^{-1})$, whose solutions are precisely the constants. Hence
$$
\boxed{H^0(X,\mathcal F)=k,\qquad H^0(X,\mathcal G)=0,\qquad\mathcal F\not\simeq\mathcal G.}
$$
A <morphism of sheaves> which is an isomorphism on all <stalks> would be an isomorphism; this example only asserts the existence of separate <stalk> isomorphisms, not that they come from one <morphism of sheaves>.

Recall that a <flasque sheaf> has surjective restriction maps on every inclusion of open subsets. We first prove the <flasque-kernel section-lifting lemma>: in an exact sequence $0\to\mathcal A\to\mathcal B\to\mathcal C\to0$ of <sheaves>, with $\mathcal A$ a <flasque sheaf>, every section of $\mathcal C$ on any open subset $V$ lifts to a section of $\mathcal B$ on $V$.

Fix such a section $c$. Order its lifts on open subsets $W\subseteq V$ by extension. A chain has an upper bound by the <sheaf gluing axiom>, so <Zorn\'s lemma> gives a maximal lift $b_W$. If $W\ne V$, choose a point outside $W$ and a neighborhood $T\subseteq V$ on which $c$ has a local lift $b_T$. On $W\cap T$, the difference $b_T-b_W$ is a section of $\mathcal A$. Extend it to $T$ by <flasqueness> and subtract it from $b_T$. The corrected lift now agrees with $b_W$ and glues on $W\cup T$, contradicting maximality. Thus $W=V$, proving the lemma.

It follows also that if both $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$ are <flasque sheaves>, then $\mathcal C$ is a <flasque sheaf>. Given a section of $\mathcal C$ on $U\subseteq V$, first lift it to $\mathcal B$ on $U$, extend that lift to $V$, and project to $\mathcal C$.

Next, an <injective sheaf of modules> $\mathcal I$ is a <flasque sheaf>. For $U\subseteq V$, let $j_U$ and $j_V$ denote the open inclusions into $X$. <Extension by zero> gives a monomorphism $j_{U!}\mathcal O_U\to j_{V!}\mathcal O_V$: on every <stalk> it is either $0\to\mathcal O_{X,x}$, the identity, or $0\to0$. By the adjunction for <extension by zero>,
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O_X}(j_{U!}\mathcal O_U,\mathcal I)=\Gamma(U,\mathcal I).
$$
Injectivity therefore makes $\Gamma(V,\mathcal I)\to\Gamma(U,\mathcal I)$ surjective. This proves <injective module sheaves are flasque>.

Take an <injective resolution of sheaves> of $\mathcal F$. Write $\mathcal C^0=\mathcal F$ and, successively,
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal C^r\longrightarrow\mathcal I^r\longrightarrow\mathcal C^{r+1}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The initial $\mathcal C^0$ is a <flasque sheaf>; each $\mathcal I^r$ is a <flasque sheaf>; and the quotient argument proves inductively that all $\mathcal C^r$ are <flasque sheaves>. The section-lifting lemma makes each displayed sequence exact after <global sections>. Consequently the complex of <global sections> of the <injective resolution of sheaves> has zero positive cohomology. Since that complex defines <sheaf cohomology>,
$$
\boxed{H^p(X,\mathcal F)=0\quad(p>0).}
$$

The <affine space> $X=\mathbb A^n_k$ is an <integral scheme>, hence its underlying <topological space> is irreducible. Every nonempty open subset of an irreducible <topological space> is irreducible and in particular connected. A section of the <constant sheaf> with value the <function field> $K=k(t_1,\ldots,t_n)$ is a <locally constant function>, so on any such open subset it is constant. The <constant sheaf> is therefore a <flasque sheaf>. It has its natural $\mathcal O_X$-<module> structure because a <regular function> on a nonempty open subset embeds in the <function field> and acts by multiplication. Part (i) now gives
$$
\boxed{H^0(X,\mathcal F)=K,\qquad H^p(X,\mathcal F)=0\quad(p>0).}
$$
This is an instance of <constant sheaves on irreducible spaces are flasque>; no restriction on $n$ is needed.

If $X$ is an <affine scheme>, each reduced <irreducible component> is a <closed subscheme>, hence an <affine scheme>. We prove the converse by explicit <affine gluing along closed subschemes>.

First suppose $X$ is the scheme-theoretic union of two <closed subschemes> which are <affine schemes> $Y$ and $Z$: their defining <ideal sheaves> have zero intersection. Their scheme-theoretic intersection $W$ is a <closed subscheme> of both, hence an <affine scheme>. Write
$$
Y=\operatorname{Spec}A,\qquad Z=\operatorname{Spec}B,\qquad W=\operatorname{Spec}C.
$$
The two maps $A\to C$ and $B\to C$ are surjective. Form the <fiber product of rings>
$$
R=A\times_CB=\{(a,b):a|_W=b|_W\}.
$$
Both projections are surjective. Let $I=\ker(R\to A)$ and $J=\ker(R\to B)$. Then
$$
I\cap J=IJ=0,\qquad R/I=A,\qquad R/J=B,\qquad R/(I+J)=C.
$$
For the last equality, the common image map $R\to C$ is surjective, and its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> consists exactly of pairs whose two coordinates map to zero. Therefore $\operatorname{Spec}R$ has closed pieces isomorphic to $Y$ and $Z$, with intersection $W$. They cover it because each <prime ideal> contains $I$ or $J$, as $IJ=0$.

A finite closed cover determines the topology: a subset is closed if and only if its intersections with both closed pieces are closed. The identifications of $Y,Z,W$ therefore give a homeomorphism between $X$ and $\operatorname{Spec}R$. To check the <structure sheaf>, on $X$ the elementary ideal-intersection sequence gives
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_X\longrightarrow i_*\mathcal O_Y\oplus j_*\mathcal O_Z\xrightarrow{(a,b)\mapsto a|_W-b|_W}k_*\mathcal O_W\longrightarrow0.
$$
It is exact on <stalks>, by $\mathcal I_Y\cap\mathcal I_Z=0$. The same sequence holds on $\operatorname{Spec}R$, using $I\cap J=0$. Under the homeomorphism, the two middle <sheaves> and their restriction maps identify, so their kernels identify as <sheaves> of <rings>. This identifies the <structure sheaves>, giving an isomorphism of <schemes> $X\simeq\operatorname{Spec}R$. The intersection $W$ need not be reduced.

Now a <Noetherian scheme> has finitely many <irreducible components> $Y_1,\ldots,Y_r$. Induct on $r$. For $r=1$, the reduced <scheme> is its single reduced <irreducible component>, so is an <affine scheme>. For $r>1$, let $Y=Y_1$ and let $Z$ be the reduced union of the remaining <irreducible components>. Its components are the corresponding $Y_i$, so $Z$ is an <affine scheme> by induction. The defining <ideal sheaves> of $Y$ and $Z$ have zero intersection: their intersection is the <ideal sheaf> of the reduced union of all components, which is zero because $X$ is reduced. The two-piece argument proves that $X$ is an <affine scheme>. The empty <scheme> is $\operatorname{Spec}0$ and causes no exception. Hence
$$
\boxed{X\text{ affine}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{every reduced irreducible component of }X\text{ is affine}.}
$$

Let $f:\mathbb P^1_k\to\operatorname{Spec}k$ be the structure map, a <projective morphism>. Let $D$ be the union of the two rational points $[0:1]$ and $[1:0]$. The homogeneous polynomial $t_0t_1$ defines this <effective Cartier divisor> and gives the exact sequence of <coherent sheaves>
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(-2)\xrightarrow{\ t_0t_1\ }\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_D\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <twisting sheaf on projective space> $\mathcal O(-2)$ has no nonzero <global sections>: the chart calculation of Question 3(iv) now requires $a(t)=t^{-2}b(t^{-1})$, again impossible for nonzero polynomials. We also have $\Gamma(\mathbb P^1,\mathcal O)=k$ and $\Gamma(D,\mathcal O_D)=k\oplus k$.

The <direct image sheaf> under $f$ is the <module> of <global sections> on the one-point <scheme>. Consequently the induced sequence is
$$
0\longrightarrow0\longrightarrow k\xrightarrow{c\mapsto(c,c)}k\oplus k\longrightarrow0.
$$
Its last nonzero map has <cokernel> isomorphic to $k$, since $(a,b)\mapsto b-a$ has the diagonal as its <kernel>. Thus **the induced sequence is not exact at $f_*\mathcal O_D$**, proving that <projective direct image need not preserve surjections>.

Put $P=\mathbb P^2_k$ and $S=k[t_0,t_1,t_2]$. A homogeneous polynomial $F$ of positive degree $d$ is nonzero, and $S$ is an <integral domain>. Multiplication by $F$ therefore gives an exact sequence of <coherent sheaves>
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_P(-d)\xrightarrow{\ F\ }\mathcal O_P\longrightarrow i_*\mathcal O_X\longrightarrow0,
$$
where $i:X\hookrightarrow P$ is the <closed immersion>.

We compute the necessary <cohomology of twisting sheaves on projective space>. The standard three <affine open subsets> $D_+(t_i)$ and all their intersections are <affine schemes>. For a <quasi-coherent sheaf>, <vanishing of quasi-coherent cohomology on an affine scheme> and the <acyclic cover theorem> identify its <sheaf cohomology> with the <Čech cohomology> of this cover. In degree $m$, the <Čech cochain complex> is
$$
\bigoplus_i(S_{t_i})_m\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i<j}(S_{t_it_j})_m\longrightarrow(S_{t_0t_1t_2})_m.
$$
Decompose it into subcomplexes indexed by Laurent monomials $t_0^{a_0}t_1^{a_1}t_2^{a_2}$ with $a_0+a_1+a_2=m$. Let $N=\{i:a_i<0\}$. Such a monomial occurs in the summand for a nonempty index set $J$ precisely when $N\subseteq J$, since only the variables in $J$ are inverted.

If $N$ is empty, the monomial contributes the cochain complex of a full two-simplex: its degree-zero cohomology is $k$ and its higher cohomology vanishes. If $|N|=1$, the dimensions in degrees $0,1,2$ are $1,2,1$; the first map has rank one and the second map has rank one with the same kernel as the first map's image. If $|N|=2$, there is one copy of $k$ in each of degrees $1$ and $2$ and the map between them is an isomorphism. These two cases are acyclic over every <field>, including characteristic two. If $N=\{0,1,2\}$, only the degree-two term occurs, contributing one copy of $k$. It follows that
$$
H^1(P,\mathcal O_P(m))=0\quad\text{for every }m,
$$
and that $H^0(P,\mathcal O_P)=k$, $H^0(P,\mathcal O_P(-d))=0$, and $H^2(P,\mathcal O_P)=0$. Moreover the <Laurent-monomial description of top cohomology on projective space> gives a basis of $H^2(P,\mathcal O_P(-d))$ consisting of
$$
t_0^{-b_0}t_1^{-b_1}t_2^{-b_2},\qquad b_i\geq1,\quad b_0+b_1+b_2=d.
$$
For $d\geq3$, subtracting one from each $b_i$ counts these as $\binom{d-1}{2}$. For $d=1,2$ there are no such triples, and the formula $(d-1)(d-2)/2$ is also zero.

Use the <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology> of the initial sequence, and <cohomology under a closed immersion> to identify the terms for $i_*\mathcal O_X$. The vanishings just proved give
$$
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\simeq k,\qquad H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)\simeq H^2(P,\mathcal O_P(-d)).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dim_kH^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=1,\qquad\dim_kH^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.}
$$
This proves the <cohomology of a projective plane hypersurface> without imposing smoothness, irreducibility or reducedness. In particular, the second number is an <arithmetic genus>, not a claim about the genus of a normalization.

Take the <affine scheme> $X=\mathbb A^1_k$, its <function field> $K=k(t)$, and the <constant sheaf> $\mathcal K$ with value $K$. By Question 4(ii), $\mathcal K$ is a <flasque sheaf>. At the two distinct closed rational points $p_0=0$ and $p_1=1$, let $S_0,S_1$ be <skyscraper sheaves> with value $K$: on an open subset $V$, $S_i(V)=K$ if $p_i\in V$ and is zero otherwise. Both are <flasque sheaves>.

There is a natural <morphism of sheaves>
$$
\rho:\mathcal K\longrightarrow S_0\oplus S_1,
$$
which sends a constant rational function to the same element in every summand present on the open subset. At $p_i$, its <stalk> map is the identity $K\to K$, and at any other point the target <stalk> is zero. Thus $\rho$ is a surjection of <sheaves>, although it is not surjective on <global sections>. Put $\mathcal F=\ker\rho$.

This can even be an example of <sheaves> of $\mathcal O_X$-<modules>: let <regular functions> act on each copy of $K$ by multiplication after embedding them in $K$. At the closed points this action is multiplication by a rational function, not evaluation at the point. With these actions, $\rho$ is $\mathcal O_X$-linear.

The exact sequence $0\to\mathcal F\to\mathcal K\to S_0\oplus S_1\to0$, together with <flasque> acyclicity, gives
$$
0\longrightarrow H^0(X,\mathcal F)\longrightarrow K\xrightarrow{a\mapsto(a,a)}K^2\longrightarrow H^1(X,\mathcal F)\longrightarrow0.
$$
Hence $H^0(X,\mathcal F)=0$ and $H^1(X,\mathcal F)=K^2/\Delta K\simeq K\ne0$. In contrast, use the one-member <affine open cover> $\mathcal U=\{X\}$. Its alternating <Čech cochain complex> has no positive-degree terms, so $\check H^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0$. We have the explicit discrepancy
$$
\boxed{\check H^1(\{X\},\mathcal F)=0\ne H^1(X,\mathcal F)\simeq k(t).}
$$
The <sheaf> $\mathcal F$ is not a <quasi-coherent sheaf>, as also follows from its nonzero higher <sheaf cohomology> on an <affine scheme>. Thus this example does not contradict the <acyclic cover theorem> for <quasi-coherent sheaves> on affine covers.
