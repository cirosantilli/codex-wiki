Two <absolute values on a field> are <equivalent absolute values> when they induce the same <topology>. For nontrivial <absolute values on a field> this is equivalent to
$$
|x|_2=|x|_1^c\qquad(x\in K)
$$
for a fixed real number $c>0$. The <trivial absolute value> is equivalent only to itself.

Restriction of a <non-Archimedean absolute value> plainly preserves the <ultrametric inequality>. Conversely, if the restriction is non-Archimedean, every integer, interpreted in either <field>, has <absolute value on a field> at most one. In particular every binomial <coefficient> has <absolute value on a field> at most one. The <triangle inequality> applied to the binomial expansion gives, for $x,y\in L$ and every positive integer $N$,
$$
|x+y|^N
\leq\sum_{j=0}^N\left|\binom Nj x^j y^{N-j}\right|
\leq (N+1)\max(|x|,|y|)^N.
$$
Raise both sides to the power $1/N$ and let $N\to\infty$. Since $(N+1)^{1/N}\to1$, this yields
$$
\boxed{|x+y|\leq\max(|x|,|y|).}
$$
This is the <bounded-integer criterion for a non-Archimedean absolute value>. The proof works for arbitrary <field extensions>, not just algebraic ones, and in positive characteristic as well.

First include the <trivial absolute value> if that convention is allowed. For a nontrivial <non-Archimedean absolute value> on $\mathbb Q$, every integer has <absolute value on a field> at most one, and some nonzero integer has <absolute value on a field> less than one: otherwise every nonzero <rational number>, being a quotient of two integers, would have <absolute value on a field> one. Factoring that integer shows that some <prime number> $p$ has $|p|<1$.

There cannot be two such <prime numbers>. If $|p|<1$ and $|q|<1$ for distinct <prime numbers>, choose integers $a,b$ with $ap+bq=1$. Then the <ultrametric inequality> gives
$$
1=|1|\leq\max(|a||p|,|b||q|)<1.
$$
Every other <prime number> therefore has <absolute value on a field> one. Factoring numerator and denominator of a <rational number> gives
$$
|x|=|p|^{v_p(x)}=|x|_p^c,\qquad
c=\frac{-\log |p|}{\log p}>0.
$$
Thus the nontrivial equivalence classes are precisely the <p-adic absolute values>, one for each <prime number>.

Now let $F$ be a <number field>, with <ring of integers of a number field> $A$. If the restriction of an <absolute value on a field> to $\mathbb Q$ is trivial, a <monic polynomial> over $\mathbb Q$ satisfied by $x\in F$ forces $|x|\leq1$: if $|x|>1$, its leading term would strictly dominate the other terms. Applying the same argument to $x^{-1}$ forces $|x|=1$ for $x\ne0$. Thus a nontrivial <absolute value on a field> defined on $F$ has a nontrivial restriction, associated with some <prime number> $p$.

Every $a\in A$ has $|a|\leq1$, by applying the same leading-term argument to its monic integral equation. Consequently
$$
\mathfrak p=\{a\in A:|a|<1\}
$$
is a proper <prime ideal>: it is an ideal by the <ultrametric inequality>, and $|ab|<1$ with $|a|,|b|\leq1$ implies that at least one factor has <absolute value on a field> less than one. It is nonzero because it contains $p$.

The <localization> $A_{\mathfrak p}$ lies in the <valuation ring> of the <absolute value on a field>: a denominator outside $\mathfrak p$ has <absolute value on a field> one. Since $A$ is a <Dedekind domain>, $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is a <discrete valuation ring>. Choose its <uniformizer> $\pi$. Each $x\in F^\times$ has a unique expression
$$
x=\pi^m u,\qquad m=v_{\mathfrak p}(x),\quad u\in A_{\mathfrak p}^{\times}.
$$
Both $u$ and $u^{-1}$ lie in the <valuation ring>, so $|u|=1$. Also $\pi$ belongs to its <maximal ideal>, hence $|\pi|<1$. Therefore $|x|=|\pi|^{v_{\mathfrak p}(x)}$.

Conversely each nonzero <prime ideal> of $A$ gives the <non-Archimedean absolute value> $|x|=c^{v_{\mathfrak p}(x)}$ for any $0<c<1$, using the <discrete valuation> of $A_{\mathfrak p}$. All choices of $c$ are equivalent, and different <prime ideals> are distinguished by the elements of $A$ having <absolute value on a field> less than one. We have proved <non-Archimedean absolute values on a number field>:
$$
\boxed{\{\text{nontrivial equivalence classes on }F\}
\longleftrightarrow
\{\text{nonzero prime ideals of }\mathcal O_F\}.}
$$
The trivial class is additional when trivial <absolute values on a field> are admitted.

An extending <absolute value on a field> is non-Archimedean by Question 1(a). We prove <finite-dimensional non-Archimedean norm equivalence over a complete field>, using completeness rather than <local compactness>.

Fix a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ of a finite-dimensional <vector space> over $K$, and let $\|\cdot\|$ be a <norm> homogeneous for the given <absolute value on a field> and satisfying the <ultrametric inequality>. Write $\|\sum a_i e_i\|_0=\max_i|a_i|$. The upper bound
$$
\|v\|\leq C\|v\|_0,\qquad C=\max_i\|e_i\|,
$$
is immediate. For the lower bound use induction on $d$. The one-dimensional case is homogeneity. For the induction step, the span $W$ of $e_1,\ldots,e_{d-1}$ is complete for its restricted <norm> by the induction comparison with the coordinate <norm> and completeness of $K$. A complete <vector subspace> of a <metric space> is closed, so
$$
\delta=\inf_{w\in W}\|e_d-w\|>0.
$$
For $v=w+ae_d$ with $a\ne0$, scaling by $a$ gives $\|v\|\geq |a|\delta$; the same <coefficient> bound is trivial when $a=0$. Also
$$
\|w\|\leq\max(\|v\|,|a|\|e_d\|)
\leq\max(1,\delta^{-1}\|e_d\|)\|v\|.
$$
The induction bound controls every coordinate of $w$ by a constant times $\|w\|$. Together with the bound on $a$, this proves $\|v\|_0\leq C'\|v\|$.

Two extending <absolute values on a field> on $L$ are <norms> of this kind on its finite-dimensional <vector space> over $K$. Hence there is $B>0$ with $|x|_2\leq B|x|_1$ for every $x\in L$. Apply this to $x^N$ and use multiplicativity:
$$
|x|_2\leq B^{1/N}|x|_1.
$$
Letting $N\to\infty$ gives $|x|_2\leq|x|_1$. Interchanging the two <norms> gives equality. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{There is at most one extending absolute value.}}
$$
The proof covers the trivial base <absolute value on a field> too. It proves uniqueness, without assuming an existence theorem or <compactness> of the coordinate sphere $\{v:\|v\|_0=1\}$.

Use the <strong form of Hensel lemma>: for $f\in\mathbb Z_p[T]$ and $a\in\mathbb Z_p$, if
$$
v_p(f(a))>2v_p(f'(a)),
$$
there is a unique <polynomial root> $b$ in the ball $v_p(b-a)>v_p(f'(a))$. In particular, a simple <polynomial root> modulo $p$ lifts uniquely in its residue class. Newton iteration gives $v_p(b-a)=v_p(f(a))-v_p(f'(a))$ when $f(a)\ne0$.

For odd $p\notin\{5,7,13\}$, the sets
$$
\{5x^2:x\in\mathbb F_p\},
\qquad
\{-13-7y^2:y\in\mathbb F_p\}
$$
each have $(p+1)/2$ elements, so they intersect. This gives a zero modulo $p$ with $z=1$. The derivative with respect to $z$ is $26$, nonzero modulo $p$, so <Hensel's lemma> lifts $z$ while holding $x,y$ fixed. This is the elementary proof of <isotropy of nondegenerate ternary quadratic forms over finite fields> followed by a simple-root lift.

At $p=5$, the residue vector $(0,1,1)$ is a zero, and the derivative $14y$ is nonzero. At $p=13$, use $(3,1,0)$, for which the value is 52 and the derivative $10x=30$ is nonzero modulo 13. Again a single-variable Hensel lift suffices.

At $p=2$, hold $x=2,z=1$ and use $f(Y)=7Y^2+33$. At $Y=1$, $f(1)=40$ has <valuation> 3, while $f'(1)=14$ has <valuation> 1. Since $3>2$, the strong Hensel inequality gives a <polynomial root> $y\in\mathbb Z_2$, indeed $y\equiv1\pmod4$. This yields a nonzero solution.

At $p=7$, suppose a nonzero solution exists and scale it so that all coordinates are in $\mathbb Z_7$ and at least one is a <unit>. Reduction modulo 7 gives $z^2=5x^2$. Since 5 is not a square modulo 7, both $x,z$ are divisible by 7. In the original equation their terms are then divisible by 49, so $7y^2$ is divisible by 49 as well, forcing $y$ divisible by 7. This contradicts the normalization. Thus <local isotropy of the five seven thirteen form> gives
$$
\boxed{\text{The unique obstructing prime is }p=7.}
$$

The intended statement uses a nontrivial <non-Archimedean absolute value>. Under that convention choose $t$ with $0<|t|<1$. A compact neighborhood $C$ of zero contains an open ball of some radius $r>0$. For sufficiently large $N$, $t^N\mathcal O_K$ is contained in that ball. The <valuation ring> is closed, so $t^N\mathcal O_K$ is a closed subset of $C$ and is compact. Scaling shows that $\mathcal O_K$ itself is compact.

Its <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m=\{x:|x|<1\}$ is open. The <residue field> $k=\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak m$ is therefore discrete and compact, hence finite: the open cover by its singleton sets has a finite subcover. Since $\mathfrak m$ has finite index, its complement is a finite union of open cosets. Thus $\mathfrak m$ is also closed and compact.

The continuous <absolute value on a field> attains its maximum on $\mathfrak m$. Nontriviality supplies a nonzero element there, so this maximum is $\rho=|\pi|$ for some $\pi\ne0$, with $0<\rho<1$. If $x\in\mathfrak m$, then $|x/\pi|\leq1$, so $\mathfrak m=\pi\mathcal O_K$.

For arbitrary $x\ne0$, choose the integer $n$ for which $\rho^{n+1}<|x|\leq\rho^n$. Then $\rho<|x/\pi^n|\leq1$. This element cannot lie in $\mathfrak m$, by maximality of $\rho$, so its <absolute value on a field> is one. Therefore $|x|=\rho^n$. This proves <discrete valuation from nontrivial local compactness>:
$$
\boxed{|K^\times|=\rho^{\mathbb Z},\qquad k\text{ finite}.}
$$

The <nontrivial valuation in the local compactness criterion> is essential. If the <trivial absolute value> is admitted, the displayed source claim is false: take $\mathbb Q$ with that <absolute value on a field>. It is complete and discrete, hence locally compact, but its <valuation ring> and <residue field> are both $\mathbb Q$, which is infinite. The intended nontrivial case has been proved above.

A <local field> is nondiscrete and locally compact. In the non-Archimedean case its <valuation ring> is compact by the scaling argument in part (b). Any <Cauchy sequence> has a tail in a translate of a compact ball, so has a convergent subsequence; the Cauchy property forces the whole sequence to converge. Thus the <field> is complete. Part (b) gives a <discrete valuation>, a <uniformizer> $\pi$, and a finite <residue field> $k=\mathbb F_q$, $q=p^f$.

We construct the <coefficient> <field> rather than presuming it exists. The <polynomial> $T^q-T$ has derivative $-1$ in characteristic $p$. Every residue element therefore has a unique <polynomial root> lift by <Hensel's lemma>. The set $S$ of these $q$ lifts is a <field>: qth powers preserve addition and multiplication, so sums and products remain <polynomial root> lifts; a nonzero lift has inverse $a^{q-2}$. Reduction identifies $S$ with $\mathbb F_q$. This is the <finite coefficient field in positive-characteristic local fields>.

For $x\in\mathcal O_K$, choose $a_0\in S$ with the same residue, write $x=a_0+\pi x_1$, and repeat with $x_1\in\mathcal O_K$. Completeness gives
$$
x=\sum_{j\ge0}a_j\pi^j,\qquad a_j\in S.
$$
The first nonzero <coefficient> determines the <valuation>, so the expansion is unique. Multiplying an arbitrary <field> element by a suitable power of $\pi$ reduces it to this case. Consequently substitution $T\mapsto\pi$ gives a valued-field isomorphism
$$
\boxed{K\cong\mathbb F_{p^f}((T)),\qquad f\ge1.}
$$
Addition and multiplication agree with those of <Laurent series> by convergence, and a nonzero series has a nonzero leading <coefficient>, establishing injectivity as well as surjectivity.

Conversely, $\mathbb F_q((T))$ is complete for its T-adic <absolute value on a field>. Its <valuation ring> $\mathbb F_q[[T]]$ is compact: fixing finitely many <coefficients> gives the finite partitions into balls, and the successive <coefficient> choices realize the <inverse limit> of the finite rings $\mathbb F_q[T]/(T^n)$. Equivalently it is the product of countably many finite discrete <coefficient> sets. Thus it is a non-Archimedean <local field>. Distinct $q$ give nonisomorphic valued <fields> because their <residue fields> have different sizes. Replacing $|T|$ by any number in $(0,1)$ gives an equivalent <absolute value on a field>.

For the finite <separable field extension> define the <inverse different>, or <codifferent>, by
$$
\mathfrak D_{L/K}^{-1}
=\{x\in L:\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}(x\mathcal O_L)
\subseteq\mathcal O_K\}.
$$
It is a <fractional ideal> of $\mathcal O_L$. The <different ideal> is its inverse <fractional ideal>.

Suppose the integer ring is generated by $\alpha$, with monic <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $g$ of degree $d$. We prove the derivative formula using the <coefficient pairing for a monogenic algebra>. If $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d$ are its distinct conjugate <polynomial roots>, <Lagrange interpolation> for a <polynomial> $h$ of degree less than $d$ gives
$$
h(T)=\sum_{i=1}^d h(\alpha_i)
\frac{g(T)}{(T-\alpha_i)g'(\alpha_i)}.
$$
Comparing <coefficients> of $T^{d-1}$ yields
$$
\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}\!\left(\frac{h(\alpha)}{g'(\alpha)}\right)
=[T^{d-1}]h(T).
$$
For any integral <polynomial>, first reduce modulo $g$; the <coefficients> stay integral because $g$ is monic over $\mathcal O_K$.

It follows that $g'(\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal O_L$ lies in the <codifferent>. To prove equality, consider the <matrix>
$$
A_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}
\left(\frac{\alpha^{i+j}}{g'(\alpha)}\right),
\qquad 0\le i,j\le d-1.
$$
All entries are integral. Entries with $i+j<d-1$ are zero, and those with $i+j=d-1$ are one. Reversing the column order makes the <matrix> triangular with diagonal ones; thus its <determinant> is a <unit> of $\mathcal O_K$.

Write an arbitrary $x\in L$ as $x=g'(\alpha)^{-1}\sum_j b_j\alpha^j$ with $b_j\in K$. Membership in the <codifferent> says $A(b_j)_j\in\mathcal O_K^d$. Since $A$ is invertible over $\mathcal O_K$, this forces every $b_j$ integral. Therefore
$$
\mathfrak D_{L/K}^{-1}=g'(\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal O_L,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathfrak D_{L/K}=(g'(\alpha)).}
$$
Separability ensures that $g'(\alpha)\ne0$; the <trace pairing> calculation also proves that no extra factor has been overlooked.

Let $L/K$ be a finite <Galois extension> of non-Archimedean <local fields>, with <Galois group> $G$, normalized <valuation> $v_L$, integer ring $\mathcal O_L$, and residue characteristic $p$. The <lower ramification numbering> is
$$
G_{-1}=G,\qquad
G_i=\{\sigma\in G:v_L(\sigma a-a)\ge i+1
\text{ for every }a\in\mathcal O_L\},\quad i\ge0.
$$
These <higher ramification groups> measure successively finer agreement with the identity. The <group> $G_0$ is the <inertia group>, the kernel of the action on the <residue field>. Each $G_i$ is normal in $G$: conjugation permutes integral elements and preserves their <valuations>. The <groups> eventually become trivial, since each nonidentity automorphism moves some integral element and its displacement has finite <valuation>.

Let $K_0=L^{G_0}$, the <maximal unramified subextension of a local field extension>. Then $G/G_0$ is the <Galois group> of the residue extension, cyclic and generated by a <Frobenius automorphism>. For a <uniformizer> $\pi$ of $L$, the <uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups> gives
$$
G_i=\{\sigma\in G_0:v_L(\sigma\pi-\pi)\ge i+1\}.
$$
To see why one <uniformizer> suffices, the extension $L/K_0$ is totally ramified and its integer ring is $\mathcal O_{K_0}[\pi]$. Elements of $G_0$ fix its <coefficients>. Differences of powers of $\pi$ are divisible by $\sigma\pi-\pi$, so the <valuation> bound for $\pi$ implies it for every integral element. The converse follows by testing $\pi$ itself.

The <tame inertia quotient> is seen explicitly from
$$
G_0\longrightarrow k_L^\times,\qquad
\sigma\longmapsto\overline{\sigma\pi/\pi}.
$$
It is a homomorphism because inertia acts trivially on the <residue field>, and its kernel is $G_1$. Thus $G_0/G_1$ is cyclic of order not divisible by $p$. For $i\ge1$, the homomorphism
$$
G_i\longrightarrow(k_L,+),\qquad
\sigma\longmapsto
\overline{\frac{\sigma\pi-\pi}{\pi^{i+1}}}
$$
has kernel $G_{i+1}$. Indeed composing two automorphisms adds the leading displacements, since $\sigma\pi/\pi\equiv1$ and inertia fixes residue <coefficients>. Hence each $G_i/G_{i+1}$ is an <elementary abelian p-group>. Eventual triviality makes $G_1$ a <p-group>; the prime-to-p quotient makes it the unique <Sylow subgroup> of $G_0$. This is the <wild inertia group>. <Tame ramification> means $G_1=1$.

The filtration also measures the <different exponent>. For the <totally ramified extension> over $K_0$, the <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $g$ of $\pi$ has <polynomial roots> $\sigma\pi$, $\sigma\in G_0$. The derivative formula gives
$$
v_L(g'(\pi))=\sum_{\sigma\in G_0\setminus\{1\}}
v_L(\pi-\sigma\pi).
$$
For a fixed $\sigma$, its displacement <valuation> is the number of indices $i\ge0$ for which it belongs to $G_i$. Interchanging the finite sums therefore gives <different exponent from ramification groups>:
$$
\boxed{\delta(L/K)=\sum_{i\ge0}(|G_i|-1).}
$$
The unramified part contributes zero. More generally <different in a tower> follows by taking products of <bases of a module> dual for the <trace pairing>, and gives
$$
\delta(L/K)=\delta(L/E)+e(L/E)\delta(E/K).
$$
Thus a tame extension has <different exponent> $e-1$, while nontrivial wild <groups> contribute additional positive terms.

The lower numbering restricts to <subgroups>: if $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)$, its <groups> are $H_i=H\cap G_i$, directly from the definition using the same top-field <valuation>. Quotients need a different indexing. For real $s\ge0$ put $G_s=G_{\lceil s\rceil}$ and define the <Herbrand function>
$$
\varphi(s)=\int_0^s\frac{dt}{[G_0:G_t]},\qquad
\psi=\varphi^{-1},\qquad G^u=G_{\psi(u)}.
$$
This <upper ramification numbering> stretches or compresses intervals according to the inertia indices. The quotient theorem says that for a <normal subgroup> $H$, <upper ramification groups commute with quotients>:
$$
(G/H)^u=G^uH/H.
$$
In particular the same upper index has a meaning compatible with passing to a smaller <Galois extension>, which lower indices generally lack.

For a tame <totally ramified extension> the picture is simple: $G_0=G$, all $G_i$ with $i\ge1$ are trivial, and the <different exponent> is $|G|-1$. The extension $\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_p)$ for odd $p$ illustrates this, giving exponent $p-2$.

A mixed-characteristic wild family is the <lower ramification filtration of a prime-power cyclotomic extension>. Take $L=\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_{p^r})$ with odd $p$ and $\pi=\zeta_{p^r}-1$. If $\sigma_a(\zeta)=\zeta^a$ and $a\ne1$ modulo $p^r$, then
$$
v_L(\sigma_a\pi-\pi)
=v_L(\zeta^{a-1}-1)=p^{v_p(a-1)}.
$$
The last equality follows because $\zeta^{a-1}$ has order $p^{r-v_p(a-1)}$ and its <uniformizer> <valuation> scales by the <ramification index> in that cyclotomic tower. The lower breaks are $0,p-1,p^2-1,\ldots,p^{r-1}-1$. Integrating the successive indices $(p-1),p(p-1),\ldots$ gives upper breaks $0,1,\ldots,r-1$.

In equal characteristic there is an equally explicit wild example. Start with $K=\mathbb F_q((t))$ and an <Artin–Schreier extension>
$$
y^p-y=t^{-m},\qquad m>0,\quad p\nmid m.
$$
There is no <polynomial root> in $K$: a negative-valuation element has $v(x^p-x)=p\,v(x)$, while the right side has <valuation> $-m$. Adjoining a <polynomial root> splits the <polynomial>, whose <polynomial roots> are $y+c$ for $c\in\mathbb F_p$, so the extension is cyclic of degree $p$. The <valuation> equation forces total ramification, $v_L(t)=p$ and $v_L(y)=-m$. Choose $1\le b\le p-1$ with $-bm\equiv1\pmod p$ and set $a=(1+bm)/p$. Then $\pi=t^a y^b$ is a <uniformizer>. For a nonidentity automorphism $y\mapsto y+c$,
$$
\frac{\sigma\pi}{\pi}=(1+c/y)^b,\qquad
v_L(\sigma\pi-\pi)=1+m;
$$
the first <binomial coefficient> $b$ is nonzero modulo $p$, and all subsequent terms have strictly higher <valuation>. Therefore
$$
G_0=\cdots=G_m=C_p,\qquad G_{m+1}=1,\qquad
\delta=(m+1)(p-1).
$$
This is the <ramification break of an Artin–Schreier pole>. It shows concretely that wild breaks, unlike tame inertia, can occur arbitrarily far out in the filtration.

**The lower <groups> measure integral displacements, the upper <groups> make quotient comparison possible, and the sizes of the <groups> determine the different.**

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <polynomial> is <Eisenstein> at every <prime number> $p$, so a <polynomial root> $\alpha$ generates a totally ramified cubic <field> $F/\mathbb Q_p$. The splitting-field <Galois group> is a transitive <subgroup> of $S_3$, hence $C_3$ or $S_3$. Its <polynomial discriminant> is
$$
\Delta=-p^2(4p+27).
$$
The product of the three <polynomial root> differences is a <square root> of $\Delta$, transformed by the sign of the <polynomial root> permutation. Thus the <group> is $C_3$ exactly when $\Delta$ is a square, and otherwise it is $S_3$. In the latter case the <splitting field> is
$$
L=F\,\mathbb Q_p(\sqrt{\Delta}).
$$
Indeed adjoining one <polynomial root> leaves a quadratic factor, whose <polynomial discriminant> is $\Delta/f'(\alpha)^2$, so the <square root> of $\Delta$ supplies its remaining <polynomial roots>.

Suppose first that $p>3$. The <unit> $-(4p+27)$ reduces to $-27$, and <Hensel's lemma> makes it a square exactly when $-3$ is a square modulo $p$. <Quadratic reciprocity> gives
$$
\left(\frac{-3}{p}\right)=
\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1\pmod3,\\-1,&p\equiv2\pmod3.\end{cases}
$$
If $p\equiv1\pmod3$, the <field> generated by $\alpha$ is already the cyclic <splitting field>, totally ramified of degree three. If $p\equiv2\pmod3$, the nonsquare <unit> gives an unramified quadratic <field> $E=\mathbb Q_p(\sqrt{\Delta})$. Since $F$ and $E$ have coprime degrees, $[FE:\mathbb Q_p]=6$. Its <residue degree> is at least two and its <ramification index> at least three; their product is six, so they are exactly two and three. Thus its <inertia group> is the normal $C_3$ inside $S_3$.

In both cases the <ramification index> is three and is not divisible by $p$. The <wild inertia group> is trivial, so all positive lower <groups> are trivial. As a different check, $f'(\alpha)=3\alpha^2+p$ has <valuation> two in $F$ when $p>3$, agreeing with tame <different exponent> $3-1$.

For $p=3$ the <polynomial discriminant> is $-351=-3^3\cdot13$, which is nonsquare. Hence $G=S_3$. The quadratic subfield can be written $E=\mathbb Q_3(\beta)$ with $\beta^2=-39$. It is ramified of degree two, by the <Eisenstein polynomial> $T^2+39$. Together with the totally ramified cubic <field> this forces $e(L/\mathbb Q_3)$ to be divisible by both two and three. Since $[L:\mathbb Q_3]=6$, the <splitting field> is totally ramified. In its normalized <valuation>,
$$
v_L(3)=6,\quad v_L(\alpha)=2,\quad
v_L(\beta)=3,\quad \pi=\beta/\alpha\text{ has }v_L(\pi)=1.
$$

All differences between distinct <polynomial roots> have the same <valuation>, since $S_3$ permutes the <polynomial root> pairs and preserves the <absolute value on a field>. Also
$$
f'(\alpha)=3(\alpha^2+1)
=(\alpha-\alpha_2)(\alpha-\alpha_3)
$$
has <valuation> six, so each <polynomial root> difference has <valuation> three. A nonidentity <three-cycle> fixes $\beta$, and therefore
$$
v_L(\sigma\pi-\pi)
=v_L\left(\frac{\beta(\alpha-\sigma\alpha)}
{\alpha\,\sigma\alpha}\right)
=3+3-2-2=2.
$$
The <transposition> fixing $\alpha$ negates $\beta$, so sends $\pi$ to $-\pi$ and has displacement <valuation> $v_L(2\pi)=1$. The other <transpositions> have the same value by conjugacy. The <uniformizer> criterion now determines every <group>:
$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
p&G&e&f&(G_0,G_1,G_2,\ldots)\\
p>3,\ p\equiv1\pmod3&C_3&3&1&(C_3,1,1,\ldots)\\
p>3,\ p\equiv2\pmod3&S_3&3&2&(C_3,1,1,\ldots)\\
p=3&S_3&6&1&(S_3,C_3,1,1,\ldots)
\end{array}}
$$
For the wild case the <different exponent> is $5+2=7$. Independently, the cubic subfield has <different exponent> $v_F(3(\alpha^2+1))=3$, and the quadratic extension $L/F$ is tame with exponent one. The tower formula gives $1+2\cdot3=7$, confirming the filtration. Its positive upper break is $1/2$, because $[G_0:G_1]=2$. This completes <ramification of the splitting field of X3 plus pX plus p> for every odd <prime number>.
