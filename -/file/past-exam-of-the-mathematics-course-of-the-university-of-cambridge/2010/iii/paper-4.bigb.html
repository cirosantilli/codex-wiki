A <nilpotent group> has a finite <central series>
$$
1=Z_0\le Z_1\le\cdots\le Z_c=G,\qquad [G,Z_{j+1}]\le Z_j.
$$
Equivalently the <upper central series>, defined by $Z_{j+1}/Z_j=Z(G/Z_j)$, reaches $G$.

We first prove the <normalizer condition for nilpotent groups>. If $H<G$, choose the least $j$ with $Z_j\not\subseteq H$. Then $Z_{j-1}\le H$, and an element $z\in Z_j\setminus H$ satisfies $[z,H]\le Z_{j-1}\le H$. Thus $z$ normalizes $H$, giving $H<N_G(H)$.

Let $P$ be a <Sylow subgroup> of a finite nilpotent $G$, and write $N=N_G(P)$. If $N<G$, the <normalizer> condition gives some $g\in N_G(N)\setminus N$. The <subgroups> $P$ and $P^g$ are both <Sylow subgroups> of $N$, so <Sylow theorem> supplies $n\in N$ with $P^{gn}=P$. But then $gn\in N_G(P)=N$, forcing $g\in N$, a contradiction. Therefore every <Sylow subgroup> is a <normal subgroup> of $G$.

For normal <Sylow subgroups> $P,Q$ at different primes, $[P,Q]\le P\cap Q=1$. They commute, have coprime orders, and their product has order $|G|$. Multiplication consequently gives an isomorphism from their external <direct product of groups> onto $G$.

Conversely, a nontrivial <finite p-group> has nontrivial <center of a group>: its <class equation> has every noncentral conjugacy-class size divisible by $p$, so $|Z(G)|$ is a positive multiple of $p$. Induction on order, applied to $G/Z(G)$, gives a central series for $G$. Finite direct products of these central series give a central series for the product, padding shorter series by the full factor. Thus the <finite nilpotent group decomposition> is
$$
\boxed{G\text{ nilpotent}\ \Longleftrightarrow\
G\cong\prod_{p\mid |G|}P_p.}
$$

For a finite group, define the <Frattini subgroup> by
$$
\Phi(G)=\bigcap_{M\text{ maximal in }G}M;
$$
the intersection is $G$ when there are no <maximal subgroups>, which for a finite group happens only for $G=1$. A second definition is the set of <non-generators>: an element $x$ such that $\langle S,x\rangle=G$ always implies $\langle S\rangle=G$.

If $x\in\Phi(G)$ and $H=\langle S\rangle<G$, put $H$ in a <maximal subgroup> $M$. Then $x\in M$, so $\langle S,x\rangle\le M<G$. Conversely, if $x\notin\Phi(G)$, some <maximal subgroup> $M$ omits it; maximality gives $\langle M,x\rangle=G$, although $M\ne G$. This proves equivalence.

Every <group automorphism> permutes <maximal subgroups>, so $\Phi(G)$ is a <characteristic subgroup>, in particular a <normal subgroup>. Also
$$
H\Phi(G)=G\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H=G:
$$
a proper $H$ would lie in a <maximal subgroup> containing both factors.

Set $F=\Phi(G)$ and take a <Sylow subgroup> $P$ of $F$. For $g\in G$, normality of $F$ makes $P^g$ a <Sylow subgroup> of $F$. Conjugating inside $F$ therefore gives the <Frattini argument> factorization $G=F N_G(P)$. The preceding generating property forces $N_G(P)=G$. Thus every <Sylow subgroup> of $F$ is normal, and part (i) shows that $F$ is a <nilpotent group>.

Now suppose $G$ is a <finite p-group>. The <normalizer> condition makes every <maximal subgroup> $M$ normal: $M<N_G(M)$ and maximality force $N_G(M)=G$. The quotient $G/M$ has no proper nontrivial <subgroup>, hence is cyclic of prime order; its p-power order makes that prime $p$. Consequently every such $M$ contains
$$
D=[G,G]G^p,
$$
where the two factors are the <commutator subgroup> and the <subgroup> generated by pth powers. Thus $D\le\Phi(G)$.

The quotient $G/D$ is an <elementary abelian group>, hence a <vector space> over $\mathbb F_p$. If $x\notin D$, extend the nonzero <vector> $xD$ to a <basis> and choose a <linear functional> nonzero on it. Its kernel pulls back to a <subgroup> of index $p$, a <maximal subgroup> omitting $x$. Therefore $x\notin\Phi(G)$, proving
$$
\boxed{\Phi(G)\text{ is characteristic and nilpotent},\qquad
\Phi(G)=[G,G]G^p\text{ for finite p-groups}.}
$$
The two factors in the last formula are multiplied; the superscripted expression in the TeX aid is not the formula printed in the PDF.

= Solution

For a subset $S$ of a <finite p-group>, its images span the <Frattini quotient> precisely when $\langle S\rangle\Phi(G)=G$. By the generating property proved in part (ii), this is equivalent to $\langle S\rangle=G$.

If $S$ is a <minimal generating set of a group>, its images must be an irredundant spanning set in the <vector space> $G/\Phi(G)$. A linear dependence would express one image using the others; those other images would still span, so the other elements would still generate $G$, a contradiction. Thus the images form a <basis>. Conversely lifts of a <basis> generate $G$, and deleting a lift destroys spanning and hence generation.

This proves the <Burnside basis theorem>, including equality of sizes for every inclusion-minimal <generating set of a group>:
$$
\boxed{|S|=\dim_{\mathbb F_p}G/\Phi(G)
=\log_p|G:\Phi(G)|.}
$$
Inclusion-minimal <generating sets of a group> need not have equal sizes in arbitrary finite groups; the elementary-abelian Frattini quotient is the essential p-group feature.

Use rightmost-first composition of <permutations>, and let
$$
a=(1\,2\,\cdots\,n),\qquad b=(n-1\,n-2\,\cdots\,1).
$$
For $n\ge2$, direct evaluation gives $ab=(1\,n)$. Conjugating this <transposition> by powers of $a$ yields the <transpositions> between consecutive letters around the cycle, including $(1\,2),(2\,3),\ldots,(n-1\,n)$.

Put $s_i=(i\,i+1)$. For $i<j$,
$$
(i\,j)=s_i s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-2}s_{j-1}s_{j-2}\cdots s_{i+1}s_i.
$$
Thus the adjacent <transpositions> generate every <transposition>, and <permutation cycle> decomposition shows that <transpositions> generate the <symmetric group>. Therefore $\langle a,b\rangle=S_n$.

For the final lower bound, associate a <graph> to a family of <transpositions>: its vertices are the letters and its edges are the swapped pairs. Every generator preserves every connected component, so generation of $S_n$ requires that <graph> to be connected. A connected <graph> on $n$ vertices needs at least $n-1$ edges: adding an edge can reduce the number of components by at most one, starting from $n$ isolated vertices. The adjacent <transpositions> supply exactly $n-1$ edges. Equivalently, <transpositions on a connected graph generate the symmetric group>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{The least number of transpositions generating }S_n\text{ is }n-1.}
$$

We prove the <primitive three-cycle criterion> through supports. First, suppose a <subgroup> contains $A_U$, the <alternating group> on a set $U$ of at least three letters, and a <three-cycle> with support $E$ meeting $U$.

If $E$ adds one letter, it has two letters in $U$. The group $A_U$ is transitive on unordered pairs of $U$: for three letters its cyclic action permutes the three pairs, and for at least four letters one can adjust the parity of a <permutation> sending one pair to another while preserving its target pair. Conjugating the new cycle, and using inverses, supplies every <three-cycle> on two old letters and the new letter. Together with $A_U$, these generate $A_{U\cup E}$.

If $E$ adds two letters, write its cycle as $\tau=(a\,b\,c)$ with $a\in U$. Choose $a'\in U\setminus\{a\}$ and conjugate within $A_U$ to obtain $\tau'=(a'\,b\,c)$. Then
$$
\tau(\tau')^{-1}=(a\,b\,a').
$$
The preceding case first adds $b$; applying it again to $\tau$ adds $c$. This proves <connected triple supports generate an alternating group>, by successively merging the intersecting triples of a connected support hypergraph. We used that <three-cycles> generate <alternating groups>: pairs of <transpositions> with a common letter are <three-cycles>, while a pair of disjoint <transpositions> is a product of two <three-cycles>.

Now take all conjugates in $G$ of the given <three-cycle>. Their support hypergraph is $G$-invariant, and its connected components form a <block system>. Transitivity makes their union the whole set. There is an edge, so a component has more than one vertex. A <primitive group action> forces a single component. The preceding merging argument proves
$$
\boxed{A_n\le G.}
$$

For the first family of counterexamples, let
$$
G=PGL_2(p)\curvearrowright\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_p),\qquad p>3.
$$
Its elements are <Möbius transformations> $x\mapsto(ax+b)/(cx+d)$, modulo nonzero scalar <matrices>. Any ordered triple of distinct <projective points> is the image of $(\infty,0,1)$ under exactly one such transformation: representatives of the first two target lines form a <basis>, and scaling the two columns sends their sum to the third target line. Thus the action is <sharply three-transitive>, hence <two-transitive> and primitive. Its degree is $p+1$ and order $p(p^2-1)$. It is divisible by $p$, but
$$
\frac{|A_{p+1}|}{|PGL_2(p)|}=\frac{(p-2)!}{2}>1,
$$
so it cannot contain $A_{p+1}$.

For the second example take
$$
\boxed{p=7,\quad G=PGL_2(8),\quad n=9,\quad |G|=504.}
$$
The same projective-line construction over $\mathbb F_8$ is <sharply three-transitive>. Its order is divisible by 7 and is less than $|A_9|=181440$, so it does not contain $A_9$. For a concrete field one can use $\mathbb F_8=\mathbb F_2[u]/(u^3+u+1)$.

Use the displayed formula as a right <group action>: applying $(a,b)$ and then $(c,d)$ gives $(ac,bd)$. It is transitive since $(1,t)$ sends the identity to $t$. An element fixing every $t$ must have $a=b$ by evaluating at the identity, and then $a^{-1}ta=t$ for every $t$. Its kernel is therefore $\{(z,z):z\in Z(T)\}$, which is trivial by the <center of a group> hypothesis.

The identity <stabilizer subgroup> is $\Delta=\{(a,a):a\in T\}$. For every <subgroup> $H$ containing $\Delta$, set
$$
N=\{b:(1,b)\in H\}.
$$
Conjugation by diagonal elements makes $N$ normal in $T$. Multiplying $(a,b)\in H$ by $(a,a)^{-1}$ shows $(1,a^{-1}b)\in H$, so
$$
H=\{(a,b):a^{-1}b\in N\}.
$$
Conversely each <normal subgroup> $N$ gives such an $H$. Thus the <subgroups> strictly between $\Delta$ and $T\times T$ correspond exactly to proper nontrivial <normal subgroups> of $T$.

For a transitive action, blocks containing a chosen point correspond to <subgroups> containing its <stabilizer subgroup>: a block is the orbit of that point under its setwise <stabilizer subgroup>, and conversely the orbit under an intermediate <subgroup> gives a block. Therefore the <diagonal action on a centerless group> is primitive exactly when $T$ is simple. A nontrivial centerless <simple group> cannot be abelian, because an abelian group's <center of a group> is the whole group. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{The action is faithful and transitive, and primitive iff }T
\text{ is nonabelian simple}.}
$$
This uses the usual nontrivial degree convention. If one allows a one-point action to count as primitive, $T=1$ is the additional degenerate exception.

For the requested order example use $T=A_5$. Its simplicity follows from its conjugacy-class sizes $1,15,20,12,12$: a <normal subgroup> is a union of classes containing the identity, and no proper nontrivial such sum divides 60. Set $p=59$, a prime. The primitive diagonal action has
$$
\boxed{n=60=p+1,\qquad G=A_5\times A_5,\qquad |G|=3600,\quad59\nmid3600.}
$$

Work in the finite-group setting. If $M$ is maximal in a nonabelian simple $G$, then $M\ne1$: otherwise every nonidentity element would generate $G$, forcing a cyclic group of prime order. Choose a <minimal normal subgroup> $N\ne1$ of $M$. Every <characteristic subgroup> of $N$ is normal in $M$, so minimality makes $N$ a <characteristically simple group>. Its <normalizer> contains $M$. By maximality it is $M$ or $G$, and the latter would make $N$ a nontrivial <normal subgroup> of the <simple group> $G$, impossible because $N\le M<G$. This proves the <maximal subgroup normalizer criterion>:
$$
\boxed{M=N_G(N)\text{ for some characteristically simple }N.}
$$

For $A_5$, <stabilizer subgroups> have order 12. <Stabilizer subgroups> of a two-element subset have order $|(S_2\times S_3)\cap A_5|=6$, and are isomorphic to $S_3$; for example $\langle(1\,2\,3),(1\,2)(4\,5)\rangle$. The <normalizer> of a Sylow 5-subgroup has order 10: its <normalizer> in $S_5$ has order $5\cdot4$, and exactly half its <permutations> are even, since a multiplier of order four on $\mathbb F_5$ is an odd four-cycle. This gives a <dihedral group> of order 10.

To prove completeness, an intransitive <subgroup> either fixes a point, or its orbits on five letters have sizes two and three. It therefore lies in one of the first two types. A transitive proper <subgroup> $H$ has order divisible by 5. Its number of Sylow 5-subgroups is one or six, since $A_5$ has six and the count is $1$ modulo 5. A count of six would make $30\mid |H|$, forcing an index-two <subgroup> and contradicting <simplicity of the alternating group A5>. Therefore $H$ normalizes its unique Sylow 5-subgroup and lies in the order-ten <normalizer>.

<Stabilizer subgroups> are maximal because their index is prime. The order-ten group is transitive and cannot have a larger proper transitive overgroup by the preceding argument. The order-six group has no fixed point, cannot lie in a <stabilizer subgroup>, and cannot lie in the order-ten type because 3 does not divide 10. Thus all three types are maximal. $A_5$ is transitive on points and on unordered pairs, while Sylow conjugacy handles the <normalizers>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{A_5:\text{ three maximal-subgroup classes, of orders }6,\ 10,\ 12.}
$$

Now let $G=GL_3(2)$. Choosing independent columns gives $|G|=7\cdot6\cdot4=168$. Its simplicity follows from the elementary projective-transvection proof in part 4(i), which does not use this <subgroup> classification.

A <stabilizer subgroup> and a plane <stabilizer subgroup> each have index seven and order 24. To construct the remaining type, identify $\mathbb F_2^3$ with $\mathbb F_8$ and take the <Singer cycle> $P$ of multiplications by nonzero field elements, of order seven. Any <linear map> commuting with a primitive multiplication commutes with all of $\mathbb F_8$, so its <centralizer> is precisely $P$. A <normalizer> sends a primitive element $a$ to another root of its minimum polynomial, namely $a,a^2,a^4$. The Frobenius map $x\mapsto x^2$ realizes those three choices. Hence
$$
N_G(P)=P\rtimes C_3,\qquad |N_G(P)|=21,\qquad n_7(G)=8.
$$

If $7\mid |H|$ for a proper $H<G$, its Sylow count is one or eight. Eight would make $56\mid|H|$, hence $|H|=56$. Its index-three <coset> action would inject the <simple group> $G$ into $S_3$: the kernel is normal and cannot be all of $G$. This is impossible. Thus $H$ normalizes a unique Sylow 7-subgroup and lies in the order-21 <normalizer>.

If $7\nmid |H|$, its order divides 24. In its action on seven nonzero <vectors>, an odd orbit has size one or three. A one-point orbit gives a fixed point. If a three-point orbit is collinear, it is the set of nonzero <vectors> of an invariant plane. If its three <vectors> are independent, their nonzero sum is fixed by $H$. Thus $H$ lies in a point or plane <stabilizer subgroup>.

The index-seven <stabilizer subgroups> are maximal, and any proper overgroup of the order-21 <normalizer> would again have a unique Sylow 7-subgroup and normalize that same <subgroup>, so the <normalizer> is maximal too. The point and plane <stabilizer subgroups> are not conjugate: a <stabilizer subgroup> fixes a point, whereas a plane <stabilizer subgroup> is transitive on its three internal points and its four external points, so fixes no point. Each type is one <conjugacy class>. We have proved <maximal subgroups of GL3 over F2>:
$$
\boxed{GL_3(2):\text{ three classes, of orders }21,\ 24,\ 24.}
$$

The <Fano plane> has as points the seven nonzero <vectors> of $\mathbb F_2^3$, because every one-dimensional <vector subspace> has just one nonzero <vector>. Its lines are the seven two-dimensional <vector subspaces>. Every line has three points, every point lies on three lines, two points determine the line $\{u,v,u+v\}$, and two lines meet in one point.

The two order-24 classes are therefore <stabilizer subgroups> of points and of lines. Each is isomorphic to $S_4$: fixing a <vector> gives the block group $\mathbb F_2^2\rtimes GL_2(2)$, the full affine group on four points; inverse transpose gives the dual line <stabilizer subgroup>. Their distinct fixed-point behavior in part (i) distinguishes the two <conjugacy classes>.

For a point $p$ and line $L$, $G_p\cap G_L$ stabilizes the ordered pair $(p,L)$. There are $7\cdot3=21$ incident pairs and $7\cdot4=28$ nonincident pairs. A change of <basis> takes any pair to a fixed model of its type, so each type is a single orbit. <Orbit-stabilizer theorem> gives the <Fano point-line stabilizer intersection>:
$$
\boxed{|G_p\cap G_L|=
\begin{cases}
8,&p\in L,\\
6,&p\notin L.
\end{cases}}
$$
Both occur: take $L=\langle e_1,e_2\rangle$ and choose $p=\langle e_1\rangle$ or $p=\langle e_3\rangle$.

\Image[paper-4-fano-plane.png]
{title=Fano plane with incident and nonincident point-line pairs}

For the extension, identify <vectors> and covectors by the standard pairing. The <inverse-transpose automorphism> exchanges a point with its orthogonal plane and exchanges the two order-24 classes. Thus it is outer. The <Fano plane duality group> $\overline G=G\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle$ has order 336 and acts on points and lines, preserving incidence while allowing their types to be swapped.

The <stabilizer subgroup> of an unordered point-line pair has twice the corresponding ordered-pair <stabilizer subgroup>: some element outside $G$ swaps the pair, since duality produces a pair of the same incidence type and $G$ is transitive on such pairs. Hence these <stabilizer subgroups> have orders 16 and 12. Also every outside conjugate of a Sylow 7-subgroup can be moved back by an element of $G$, giving
$$
|N_{\overline G}(P)|=2|N_G(P)|=42.
$$

We prove these exhaust the <maximal subgroups> other than $G$. Let $K$ be such a <maximal subgroup> and $H=K\cap G$. Since $K$ is not contained in $G$, $H$ is normal in $K$ and $|K:H|=2$. If $7\mid|H|$, part (i) gives a unique Sylow 7-subgroup $P$ of $H$. It is characteristic in $H$, hence normalized by $K$. Therefore maximality forces $K=N_{\overline G}(P)$.

Suppose $7\nmid|H|$ and $H\ne1$. The classification argument in part (i) says $H$ fixes a point or a line. Because $H$ is normal in $K$, an element outside $G$ swaps its fixed-point and fixed-line sets, so it fixes at least one of each. Write
$$
d=\dim V^H,\qquad d^*=\dim(V^*)^H.
$$
Fixed lines are kernels of fixed nonzero covectors, since the only nonzero scalar is one. Duality gives $2^d-1=2^{d^*}-1$, hence $d=d^*\in\{1,2\}$.

If $d=1$, there is a unique fixed point and unique fixed line; $K$ preserves their unordered pair. If $d=2$, put $W=V^H$ and $A=((V^*)^H)^\perp$. For every $h\in H$, $\ker(h-I)$ contains the plane $W$ and $\operatorname{im}(h-I)$ lies in the line $A$. Over $\mathbb F_2$ there is only one nonzero <linear map> $V/W\to A$, so $H$ has order two. Invertibility forces $A\subseteq W$: for $h=I+v f$ one needs $f(v)=0$. Thus $A$ and $W$ are its unique image point and kernel line. These are canonically swapped by duality, so $K$ again preserves their unordered incident pair.

If $H=1$, then $K$ has order two, generated by an involutory duality $u$. For any point $p$, the pair $\{p,u(p)\}$ is preserved by $u$. Hence $K$ lies in an order-12 or order-16 pair <stabilizer subgroup> and is not maximal. We have exhausted all cases.

The pair <stabilizer subgroups> are themselves maximal: a proper maximal overgroup is one of the just-listed types, and divisibility excludes orders 12 inside 16 or 42, and 16 inside 12 or 42. The order-42 <normalizer> is maximal by the unique-Sylow argument. Incidence types give one <conjugacy class> each; Sylow conjugacy gives one <normalizer> class. Thus, apart from the normal index-two <subgroup> $G$,
$$
\boxed{\overline G:\text{ three maximal-subgroup classes, of orders }12,\ 16,\ 42.}
$$

Let $F=\mathbb F_q$. The <general linear group over a finite field> consists of all invertible <linear maps> $F^n\to F^n$, and the <special linear group over a finite field> is its determinant-one <subgroup>. Counting the ordered <bases> gives
$$
|GL_n(q)|=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(q^n-q^i)
=q^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(q^j-1).
$$
The <determinant> map is onto $F^\times$, since $\operatorname{diag}(t,1,\ldots,1)$ has <determinant> $t$. Its kernel is $SL_n(q)$, so
$$
\boxed{|SL_n(q)|=\frac{|GL_n(q)|}{q-1}.}
$$

For $n>1$, commuting with all the <elementary transvection matrices> $I+tE_{ij}$ forces a <matrix> to be scalar. Therefore the <center of a group> of $SL_n(q)$ consists of $\lambda I$ with $\lambda^n=1$. The multiplicative group of a <finite field> is cyclic, so this <center of a group> has size $d=\gcd(n,q-1)$. The <projective special linear group over a finite field> consequently has order
$$
|PSL_n(q)|=\frac{q^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_{j=2}^n(q^j-1)}{\gcd(n,q-1)}.
$$

Here is a proof of simplicity which isolates all the required ingredients. First we prove the <Iwasawa simplicity lemma> in the form needed. Suppose a group $H$ acts faithfully and primitively, is a <perfect group>, and has an abelian <subgroup> $U$ normal in a <stabilizer subgroup> $H_\alpha$, whose conjugates generate $H$. For a nontrivial <normal subgroup> $N$, its orbits form a <block system>. Faithfulness prevents all these orbits from being singletons, so primitivity makes $N$ transitive. Thus $H=NH_\alpha$. Modulo $N$, each conjugate of $U$ has exactly the same image as $U$, because we may write its conjugating element as $nh$ with $n\in N$ and $h\in H_\alpha$. The quotient $H/N$ is generated by this one abelian image and hence is abelian. Since $H$ is a <perfect group>, $H/N=1$. Thus every nontrivial <normal subgroup> is $H$.

Apply this to the action on <projective points>, that is, one-dimensional <vector subspaces> of $F^n$. Any ordered pair of distinct lines can be sent to any other by a <linear map> obtained by extending representatives to <bases>. Multiplying one target <basis> <vector> by a suitable nonzero scalar adjusts the <determinant> to one without changing either target line. Thus $SL_n(q)$ is <two-transitive> on these points. A <linear map> fixing every line is scalar: it is diagonal on a fixed <basis>, and fixing the lines spanned by $e_i+e_j$ makes all diagonal entries equal. The projective quotient therefore acts faithfully, two-transitively, and primitively.

For the line $\langle e_1\rangle$, use
$$
U=\{I+e_1f:f\in (F^n)^*,\ f(e_1)=0\}.
$$
These <matrices> multiply by addition of $f$, so $U$ is abelian. Conjugating by a <matrix> preserving $\langle e_1\rangle$ preserves $U$, so it is normal in that <stabilizer subgroup>. Its conjugates include every <elementary transvection matrix> $I+tE_{ij}$.

These <elementary transvection matrices> generate $SL_n(q)$ by row elimination. For completeness, row addition is elementary, signed row interchange is elementary, and compensating diagonal scalings are elementary because, in a two-dimensional coordinate block,
$$
w(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-t^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\-t^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
w(t)w(-1)=\operatorname{diag}(t,t^{-1}).
$$
Elimination using these operations reduces a determinant-one <matrix> to the identity.

It remains to show that these groups are <perfect groups>. In the following <matrix> identities use $[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$; the alternative <group commutator> convention generates the same <commutator subgroup>. If $n\ge3$, distinct indices give the <group commutator> identity
$$
[I+aE_{ij},I+bE_{jk}]=I+abE_{ik}.
$$
Every elementary generator is therefore a <group commutator>. For $n=2$ and $q>3$, choose $\lambda\in F^\times$ with $\lambda^2\ne1$, and let $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$. Then
$$
[D,I+tE_{12}]=I+(\lambda^2-1)tE_{12}.
$$
As $t$ varies this supplies all upper <elementary transvection matrices>, and conjugation supplies the lower ones. Thus $SL_n(q)$, and its projective quotient, are perfect in the asserted cases. The preceding simplicity argument now applies.

The two exceptions are genuine: the projective actions identify $PSL_2(2)$ with $S_3$ and $PSL_2(3)$ with $A_4$, of orders 6 and 12 respectively. The first contains its <normal subgroup> of order 3; the second contains the normal Klein four group. We have proved
$$
\boxed{PSL_n(q)\text{ is simple for }n>1,\quad
(n,q)\notin\{(2,2),(2,3)\}.}
$$

Replace the <determinant> condition by preservation of a nondegenerate <alternating bilinear form> $B$ on $F^{2m}$. In a <symplectic basis> the <symplectic group over a finite field> is
$$
Sp_{2m}(q)=\{g\in GL_{2m}(q):g^{\mathsf T}Jg=J\},
\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_m\\-I_m&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
To count <symplectic bases>, choose the first <vector> $e$ in $q^{2m}-1$ ways, then choose $f$ with $B(e,f)=1$ in $q^{2m-1}$ ways. Their span is nondegenerate; its <symplectic orthogonal complement> has <dimension> $2m-2$ and supplies the rest of the <basis>. The resulting recurrence gives the <finite symplectic group order>
$$
\boxed{|Sp_{2m}(q)|=q^{m^2}\prod_{i=1}^m(q^{2i}-1).}
$$

The elementary replacements are <symplectic transvections>
$$
T_{v,c}(x)=x+cB(x,v)v,\qquad v\ne0,\ c\in F.
$$
Expanding $B(Tx,Ty)$ shows that the two cross terms cancel and the last term vanishes because $B(v,v)=0$. Thus these maps preserve $B$; their inverses are $T_{v,-c}$.

We need both generation and primitivity to use the argument from part (i). For nonzero <vectors> $v,w$ with $B(v,w)\ne0$, the <symplectic transvection> with direction $w-v$ and parameter $1/B(v,w)$ sends $v$ to $w$. When $B(v,w)=0$, choose $z$ nonorthogonal to both and use two such steps. Such $z$ exists because two proper <hyperplanes> cannot cover a <vector space>: if the two linear forms are independent prescribe both values to be one, and if dependent prescribe either a nonzero value. Hence <symplectic transvections> act transitively on nonzero <vectors>.

They generate the whole <symplectic group over a finite field>. Indeed first use them to make a given symplectic transformation fix $e_1$. Its image $f'$ of $f_1$ still satisfies $B(e_1,f')=1$. A <symplectic transvection> with direction $f_1-f'$ fixes $e_1$ and sends $f'$ to $f_1$ if the pairing is nonzero. If that pairing is zero, pass through $f'+ce_1$ for any $c\ne0$: it has nonzero pairing with both partners, and both required directions lie in $e_1^\perp$. We have now fixed the first <hyperbolic pair>, and induction applies in its nondegenerate orthogonal complement. The final two-dimensional case is exactly the elementary generation of $SL_2(q)=Sp_2(q)$ proved in part (i).

For a fixed <projective point> $\langle e\rangle$, the <symplectic transvections> $T_{e,c}$ form an abelian <subgroup> normal in its <stabilizer subgroup>; conjugation scales $c$ by the square of the scalar multiplying $e$. Their conjugates supply all <symplectic transvections>. A central element commutes with every <symplectic transvection>, so it preserves every direction line and must be scalar. Conversely scalars commute with the whole group. The kernel of the projective action consists of scalar <matrices>, and preservation of $B$ restricts the scalar to $\lambda^2=1$. Thus the <center of a group> has size $\gcd(2,q-1)$ and the <projective symplectic group over a finite field> acts faithfully.

For $m\ge2$ the <stabilizer subgroup> has precisely three orbits: its fixed point, the other points in $e^\perp$, and the points outside $e^\perp$. Transitivity on each follows by extending, respectively, an independent isotropic pair or a <hyperbolic pair> to a <symplectic basis>. Write
$$
N=\frac{q^{2m}-1}{q-1},\qquad
h=\frac{q^{2m-1}-1}{q-1}.
$$
A nontrivial proper block through the fixed point would be a union of these <stabilizer subgroup> orbits, hence would have size either $h$ or $q^{2m-1}+1$. A block size divides $N$. But $N-qh=1$, so $h>1$ cannot divide $N$, and $N/2<q^{2m-1}+1<N$, excluding the other size. This proves primitivity. For $m=1$ the projective action is the <two-transitive> action of $PSL_2(q)$.

Here is a direct check of <perfectness of finite symplectic groups>, including the small fields where the diagonal <group commutator> needs replacement. If $q>3$, every <symplectic transvection> lies in an embedded $SL_2(q)$ on a hyperbolic plane containing its direction, and that <subgroup> is perfect by part (i). Since the <symplectic transvections> generate, the whole <symplectic group over a finite field> is perfect.

For $q=3$ and $m\ge2$, all $T_{v,1}$ are conjugate, so their common image $z$ generates the <abelianization> and satisfies $3z=0$. Choose independent perpendicular <vectors> $e,f$. The four commuting <symplectic transvections> with directions $e,f,e+f,e-f$ and parameter one multiply to the identity: the sum of their rank-one <matrices> is zero in characteristic 3. Thus also $4z=0$, giving $z=0$.

For $q=2$ and $m\ge3$, use the seven nonzero <vectors> of a three-dimensional <isotropic subspace of a symplectic vector space>. Their <symplectic transvections> commute, and their product is the identity. To see this explicitly, write a <vector> in three isotropic <basis> coordinates: in the sum of the outer products $vv^{\mathsf T}$ each diagonal coefficient occurs four times and each off-diagonal coefficient twice, all zero in characteristic 2. The common <abelianization> image $z$ therefore satisfies $2z=7z=0$. Again the group is perfect.

The simplicity argument from part (i) now applies to the projective <symplectic group over a finite field>. The remaining exceptional group besides $Sp_2(2)$ and $PSp_2(3)$ is $Sp_4(2)$. It is not simple: it is isomorphic to $S_6$. One can verify this identification without assuming it. On $F_2^4$ set
$$
Q_0(x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2)=x_1y_1+x_2y_2,\qquad
Q_a(v)=Q_0(v)+B(a,v).
$$
These are all 16 <quadratic forms> with polar form $B$; the <Arf invariant of a quadratic form> gives $\operatorname{Arf}(Q_a)=Q_0(a)$. There are six with invariant one. The <symplectic group over a finite field> permutes these six forms. Differences of their parameter <vectors> span $F_2^4$: writing $e_i,f_i$ for the <symplectic basis>, the six parameters are
$$
e_1+f_1,\ e_1+f_1+e_2,\ e_1+f_1+f_2,\
e_2+f_2,\ e_2+f_2+e_1,\ e_2+f_2+f_1.
$$
Their pairwise differences include each of $e_1,e_2,f_1,f_2$. An element fixing all six forms therefore fixes all linear forms $B(a-b,-)$, hence is the identity. This gives a faithful action on six objects; the group order is 720, so its image is all of $S_6$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{|PSp_{2m}(q)|=\frac{q^{m^2}\prod_{i=1}^m(q^{2i}-1)}{\gcd(2,q-1)},\qquad
PSp_{2m}(q)\text{ simple except }(m,q)=(1,2),(1,3),(2,2).}
$$

Choose a complement $W$ to the fixed $k$-dimensional <vector subspace> $U$. With column <vectors> and the decomposition $V=U\oplus W$, its <parabolic stabilizer of a subspace> is
$$
P_k=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&D\end{pmatrix}:
A\in GL_k(q),\ D\in GL_{n-k}(q),\
B\in\operatorname{Hom}(W,U)\right\}.
$$
The arbitrary upper-right block forms a normal <elementary abelian group>. The block-diagonal <matrices> form a complement, acting on it by $B\mapsto ABD^{-1}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{P_k\cong \operatorname{Hom}(W,U)\rtimes
(GL_k(q)\times GL_{n-k}(q)),\qquad
|P_k|=q^{k(n-k)}|GL_k(q)||GL_{n-k}(q)|.}
$$
With row <vectors> the off-diagonal block is transposed; the <subgroup> description is the same after the convention change.

The <rank of a transitive permutation group> is the number of orbits of a <stabilizer subgroup>, not the rank of a <matrix>. For $Y\in X_k$, the invariant of its $P_k$-orbit is $r=\dim(U\cap Y)$. This invariant is complete. Choose a <basis> of $U\cap Y$, extend it to a <basis> of $U$, then add a <basis> of a complement of $U\cap Y$ in $Y$, and extend the resulting independent <vectors> to a <basis> of $V$. For $Y'$ with the same intersection <dimension> $r$, do the same. The map between these adapted <bases> preserves $U$ and sends $Y$ to $Y'$.

Each value $r=0,1,\ldots,k$ occurs: in a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ with $U=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_k\rangle$, take
$$
Y_r=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_r,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{2k-r}\rangle.
$$
The hypothesis $2k\le n$ guarantees all these <vectors> exist. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}(GL_n(q)\curvearrowright X_k)=k+1.}
$$

The <Grassmann graph> is connected, and its distance can be determined directly. Put $C=A\cap B$, $r=\dim C$, and $s=k-r$. Choose <vectors> so that
$$
A=C\oplus\langle a_1,\ldots,a_s\rangle,\qquad
B=C\oplus\langle b_1,\ldots,b_s\rangle.
$$
The <vectors> of a <basis> of $C$, followed by all the $a_i$ and $b_i$, are independent: a relation would identify a <vector> in the two chosen complements modulo $C$, contradicting $A\cap B=C$. For $0\le j\le s$ define
$$
A_j=C\oplus\langle b_1,\ldots,b_j,a_{j+1},\ldots,a_s\rangle.
$$
Each successive pair has an intersection of <dimension> $k-1$. Thus $A_0=A$ and $A_s=B$ are joined by a path of length $s$.

For the lower bound, if two vertices $U,V$ are adjacent, their common <vector subspace> $D$ has codimension one in each. Both $\dim(U\cap B)$ and $\dim(V\cap B)$ lie between $\dim(D\cap B)$ and $\dim(D\cap B)+1$. Thus one edge can change the <dimension> of the intersection with $B$ by at most one. It must change from $r$ to $k$ along a path from $A$ to $B$, requiring at least $k-r$ edges. Therefore
$$
\boxed{d(A,B)=k-\dim(A\cap B).}
$$

If two ordered pairs have equal distance, their intersections have equal <dimension>. Construct adapted <bases> for each pair as above and extend both to <bases> of the ambient <vector space>. The invertible <linear map> between them sends the first ordered pair to the second. It preserves intersection <dimensions>, so it is a <graph automorphism>. This proves that the <Grassmann graph> is <distance-transitive>, with the required simultaneous images. Its diameter is $k$, because $2k\le n$ permits two disjoint $k$-dimensional <vector subspaces>.

A finite <two-transitive group action> is transitive on ordered pairs of distinct points. Equivalently, the action is transitive and a point <stabilizer subgroup> is transitive on the remaining points. Its <rank of a transitive permutation group> is two, and the <orbit-stabilizer theorem> gives $n(n-1)\mid |G|$ for degree $n$. Such an action is primitive: a block containing a point and another point must, by the transitivity of the <stabilizer subgroup>, contain every point.

These groups illustrate two quite different sources of symmetry. The structural theorem for finite <two-transitive> groups says that they have either a regular elementary-abelian <normal subgroup>, giving the affine case, or a nonabelian simple <normal subgroup>, giving the almost simple case. The full classification of the second case depends on the classification of finite <simple groups>; the useful point here is to describe the constructions, actions, and representative families rather than treat two-transitivity as an abstract property of a group without a specified action.

In the affine case let $V$ be a finite <vector space> over $\mathbb F_p$ and let $H\le GL(V)$ act transitively on $V\setminus\{0\}$. The <affine two-transitive group>
$$
G=V\rtimes H,\qquad (v,h):x\mapsto v+hx,
$$
is transitive by translations, and the <stabilizer subgroup> of zero is exactly $H$, so it is <two-transitive>. Conversely, if a <two-transitive> group has an abelian minimal <normal subgroup> $V$, normal-subgroup orbits and primitivity make $V$ transitive. An abelian transitive faithful <permutation> <subgroup> is regular: an element fixing one point commutes with every element moving that point and hence fixes them all. Minimal normality makes $V$ elementary abelian, since its prime-primary <subgroups> and its <subgroup> of pth powers are characteristic. Conjugation by the <stabilizer subgroup> acts faithfully as linear automorphisms of $V$, and regularity gives $G=V\rtimes G_0$. This recovers precisely the construction above.

Many examples follow at once. The group $AGL_d(q)$ acts on the $q^d$ <vectors> of $\mathbb F_q^d$; $GL_d(q)$ sends any nonzero <vector> to any other by a <basis> map. For $d\ge2$, the <subgroup> $ASL_d(q)$ also gives a <two-transitive> action, since the <determinant> of a <basis> map can be adjusted on a second <basis> <vector> while retaining the prescribed first <vector>. The affine group with linear part $Sp_{2m}(q)$ gives another family: the nonzero-vector transitivity was proved using <symplectic transvections> in part 4(ii). Here the additive group of $\mathbb F_q^d$, for $q=p^f$, is an <elementary abelian p-group> of rank $fd$.

In particular $AGL_1(q)$ consists of $x\mapsto ax+b$ with $a\ne0$. Given distinct $x,y$ and distinct $u,v$, the equations $ax+b=u$, $ay+b=v$ have the unique solution $a=(v-u)/(y-x)$, $b=u-ax$. Thus this action is <sharply two-transitive>, of degree $q$ and order $q(q-1)$. Adding <field automorphisms> gives $A\Gamma L_1(q)$, still <two-transitive>, with elements $x\mapsto ax^{p^i}+b$.

An <almost simple group> lies between a nonabelian <simple group> $T$ and its automorphism group. One must still specify an action: a group can have several <permutation representations> with different degrees or different transitivity properties. The natural actions of $S_n$ for $n\ge2$ and $A_n$ for $n\ge4$ are the most familiar examples of a <two-transitive group action>. For $n\ge5$, the <simple group> $A_n$, a <normal subgroup>, places these groups in the almost simple case; the smaller two-transitive examples belong to the affine case. The <symmetric group> sends any ordered distinct pair to any other; for the <alternating group> one can adjust an odd map by swapping two letters outside the target pair. Thus both are <two-transitive> in the indicated ranges.

The projective linear examples are just as concrete. The groups $PSL_n(q)$ and $PGL_n(q)$ act two-transitively on
$$
|\mathbb P^{n-1}(\mathbb F_q)|=\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}
$$
points, by the <basis> and <determinant> adjustment argument of part 4(i). Their field-automorphism extensions are also <two-transitive>. On the <projective line>, $PGL_2(q)$ is <sharply three-transitive>: three distinct points determine a unique <Möbius transformation>. Its degree is $q+1$ and its order is $q(q^2-1)$. For even $q$, $PSL_2(q)=PGL_2(q)$; for odd $q$ the special projective group has half that order and remains <two-transitive>, but cannot be <three-transitive>. In projective <dimension> at least two, triples of distinct points may be collinear or noncollinear, so the natural action is not <three-transitive>.

The <projective special unitary group over a finite field> $PSU_3(q)$, for $q\ge3$, acts on the isotropic points of a space with a nondegenerate <Hermitian form> of <dimension> three over $\mathbb F_{q^2}$. Its degree and order are
$$
q^3+1,\qquad \frac{q^3(q^3+1)(q^2-1)}{\gcd(3,q+1)}.
$$
For two distinct isotropic lines the <Hermitian form> pairing is nonzero, since a three-dimensional nondegenerate Hermitian space has maximal totally isotropic <dimension> one. Scale their representatives to form a <hyperbolic pair> and extend to a <basis> adapted to the <Hermitian form>. The corresponding isometry sends one ordered pair to another, and a norm-one scalar on the orthogonal one-dimensional complement adjusts its <determinant> without moving the two lines. Thus the special projective unitary action is <two-transitive>.

Two further families of <simple groups> have analogous rank-two actions:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{family}&q&\text{degree}&\text{order}\\
Sz(q)&2^{2a+1}\ge8&q^2+1&q^2(q^2+1)(q-1)\\
{}^2G_2(q)&3^{2a+1}\ge27&q^3+1&q^3(q^3+1)(q-1)
\end{array}
$$
The <Suzuki group of Lie type> and the <Ree group of type G2> have <stabilizer subgroups> containing <subgroups> acting regularly on all the other points in these actions; this gives two-transitivity. For example $Sz(8)$ has degree 65 and order 29120. These are exceptional Lie-type families, distinct from the preceding linear and unitary constructions.

The <symplectic group over a finite field> in characteristic two has a particularly illuminating pair of <two-transitive> actions which are not their projective-vector actions. For $m\ge3$, $Sp_{2m}(2)$ acts on the two classes of <quadratic forms> whose polar form is the given <alternating bilinear form>, distinguished by the <Arf invariant of a quadratic form>. Their degrees are
$$
2^{m-1}(2^m+1)\quad\text{and}\quad2^{m-1}(2^m-1).
$$
Transitivity on each class follows by choosing <symplectic bases> giving the standard quadratic-form normal forms. Fix a form $Q$. Every other form with the same polar form is $Q_a(x)=Q(x)+B(a,x)$, and has the same <Arf invariant of a quadratic form> exactly when $Q(a)=0$. The <stabilizer subgroup> of $Q$, an <orthogonal group over a finite field>, is transitive on its nonzero <isotropic vectors>: extend such a <vector> to a <hyperbolic pair> and map adapted quadratic-form <bases>. It is therefore transitive on the other forms of the same type. This proves two-transitivity of both actions. In particular $Sp_6(2)$ has actions of degrees 28 and 36. The degree-six action of $Sp_4(2)\cong S_6$ in part 4(ii) is the smaller-dimensional precursor.

Finally the <Mathieu groups> supply exceptional examples with even higher transitivity:
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
G&M_{11}&M_{12}&M_{22}&M_{23}&M_{24}\\
\text{degree}&11&12&22&23&24\\
\text{transitivity}&4&5&3&4&5
\end{array}
$$
In particular all five natural actions are <two-transitive>. Together these examples exhibit affine spaces, projective and Hermitian geometries, symplectic <quadratic forms>, and exceptional finite <simple groups> as different sources of the same pair symmetry.

**The organizing distinction is affine versus almost simple; the action, its degree, and its <stabilizer subgroup> are essential data.** The explicitly verified affine, symmetric, alternating, projective, unitary, and symplectic actions already give many infinite families, while the exceptional families show why no single <matrix> construction covers every example.
