= Solution
{parent=i}

Assume a steady, locally plane-symmetric <galactic disk>, with negligible radial tilt term in the <vertical Jeans equation>. Write $\rho(z)$ for the stellar <mass density>, and $K_z=\partial_z\Phi$ for the upward gradient of the <Newtonian gravitational potential>; the actual vertical <gravitational acceleration> is $-K_z$. Constant vertical <velocity dispersion> gives
$$
\sigma^2\frac{d\rho}{dz}=-\rho K_z,\qquad
\rho(z)=\rho(0)\exp\left[-\frac{\Phi(z)-\Phi(0)}{\sigma^2}\right].
$$
Integrating the plane-parallel <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> from the symmetry plane gives $K_z(z)=4\pi G\int_0^z\rho(z')\,dz'$. Outside most of the mass, this tends to $2\pi G\Sigma$ for $z>0$, where the <surface density> includes both sides of the <galactic disk>. Treating this field as constant and integrating the <vertical Jeans equation> yields
$$
\rho(z)\propto e^{-|z|/h},\qquad
\boxed{h=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G\Sigma}}.
$$
**This exponential is the thin-sheet approximation or the outer tail, not the exact profile at every height.** To see the distinction without an additional assumption, set $\rho=\rho_0e^{-u}$ with $u=(\Phi-\Phi(0))/\sigma^2$. The <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> becomes $u''=(4\pi G\rho_0/\sigma^2)e^{-u}$, with $u(0)=u'(0)=0$, and is solved by
$$
u=2\log\cosh(z/z_0),\qquad
\rho=\rho_0\operatorname{sech}^2(z/z_0),\qquad
z_0^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi G\rho_0}.
$$
Since $\Sigma=2\rho_0z_0$, the exact <self-gravitating isothermal slab> has $z_0=\sigma^2/(\pi G\Sigma)$. At large $|z|/z_0$, $\operatorname{sech}^2(z/z_0)\sim4e^{-2|z|/z_0}$, recovering precisely the boxed <scale height>; near the plane its slope vanishes, whereas an exponential has a cusp.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the thin-sheet regime, the gas <scale height> is $h_g(R)=\sigma_g^2(R)/(2\pi G\Sigma_*(R))$. Hence radius-independent gas <velocity dispersion> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{h_g(R)}{h_g(R_0)}=\frac{\Sigma_*(R_0)}{\Sigma_*(R)}}.
$$
For an <exponential galactic disk>, $\Sigma_*(R)=\Sigma_{*,0}e^{-R/R_d}$, so
$$
\boxed{h_g(R)=\frac{\sigma_g^2}{2\pi G\Sigma_{*,0}}e^{R/R_d}}.
$$
The gas therefore exhibits <galactic disk flaring> as stellar <surface density> falls outward. Stellar <galactic disks>, by contrast, commonly have roughly constant thickness over their main optical region; applying the same <scale height> relation to the stars requires $\sigma_{z,*}^2(R)\propto\Sigma_*(R)$, or $\sigma_{z,*}\propto e^{-R/(2R_d)}$ for an <exponential galactic disk>. This comparison does not follow by imposing a radially constant stellar <velocity dispersion>: that extra assumption would predict stellar <galactic disk flaring> too. The constancy used in the vertical calculation is constancy with height locally, not a mandatory constancy with radius.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The thin-sheet field used above requires the gas to sample heights beyond most of the stellar mass, while the stellar <galactic disk> still dominates vertical gravity and is approximately locally plane-parallel. In inner regions a cold gas layer can lie inside the thicker stellar layer, where reflection symmetry gives $K_z\simeq\nu_z^2z$ rather than a constant field. The <vertical Jeans equation>, or gas <hydrostatic equilibrium>, then gives
$$
\rho_g(z)=\rho_g(0)\exp\left(-\frac{\nu_z^2z^2}{2\sigma_g^2}\right),\qquad H_g=\frac{\sigma_g}{\nu_z}.
$$
Here $H_g$ is the Gaussian width and $\nu_z$ is the <vertical epicyclic frequency>; in a locally flat <galaxy rotation curve>, $\nu_z^2\simeq4\pi G\rho_{\rm total}(R,0)$. A stellar layer of approximately fixed thickness then gives $H_g\propto\Sigma_*^{-1/2}$, rather than the inverse <surface density> law.

At large radii, the <dark-matter halo> or the gas's own gravity can exceed the dwindling stellar restoring force, so replacing the total force by $2\pi G\Sigma_*$ fails. A nearly spherical <dark-matter halo> instead gives $\nu_z\simeq v_c/R$ near its plane, and $H_g\simeq\sigma_gR/v_c$ for a flat <galaxy rotation curve>, much slower than exponential <galactic disk flaring>. Warps, radial gradients across a thick gas layer, non-equilibrium disturbances, and height-dependent <velocity dispersion> also invalidate the locally plane-parallel steady isothermal approximation. **The inverse stellar surface-density law applies only while its force and geometric assumptions apply.**

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Measure the number-density profile $n(z)$ and vertical <velocity dispersion> $\sigma_z(z)$ of a tracer <stellar population>. The <vertical Jeans equation> estimates the restoring field from
$$
K_z=-\frac1n\frac{\partial(n\sigma_z^2)}{\partial z}
-\frac1{Rn}\frac{\partial(Rn\overline{v_Rv_z})}{\partial R}.
$$
The second term accounts for the tilt of the <velocity ellipsoid>; an exponential tracer with constant <velocity dispersion> and negligible tilt gives $K_z=\sigma_z^2/h$ above the plane. The tracer need not supply all the gravitating mass. To infer the <dynamical surface density from vertical stellar motions>, integrate the axisymmetric <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>:
$$
\Sigma_{\rm dyn}(<|z|)=\frac1{2\pi G}\left[K_z(z)+\int_0^z\frac1R\partial_R(R\partial_R\Phi)\,dz'\right],\qquad z>0.
$$
For a nearly flat <galaxy rotation curve> with a small radial correction, this reduces to $K_z/(2\pi G)$. Estimate stellar <surface density> independently from counts, the <initial mass function> and faint objects, and add the gas <surface density> and <stellar remnants>. The residual
$$
\Sigma_{\rm excess}=\Sigma_{\rm dyn}-\Sigma_{\rm stars}-\Sigma_{\rm gas}
$$
limits the allowed unseen material within the same height interval. A fitted <dark-matter halo> contributes too: measurements at several heights help distinguish its extended density from a thin dark component in the <galactic disk>. Under a specified decomposition, **the disc dark-matter fraction is the inferred dark-disc surface density divided by the total disc surface density**, with uncertainty or an upper limit if the residual is consistent with zero. Selection effects, disequilibrium, the tilt term and uncertain baryonic counts must enter the error budget; an excess alone does not establish that all of it is in the <galactic disk>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $\psi(t)$ be the <star formation rate>, $M$ the remaining gas mass, and $S$ the mass put into stars. With zero returned bulk gas, pristine <galactic gas inflow>, and no <galactic outflow>, <mass conservation> gives
$$
\dot S=\psi,\qquad \dot M=-(1-\nu)\psi.
$$
A well-mixed gas reservoir loses existing metals at rate $Z\psi$ and gains newly synthesized metals at rate $y\psi$; the incoming gas brings none. Thus
$$
\frac{d(MZ)}{dt}=y\psi-Z\psi,\qquad
M\dot Z=(y-\nu Z)\psi.
$$
For $0<\nu<1$, eliminate time and integrate:
$$
\frac{dZ}{y-\nu Z}=-\frac{dM}{(1-\nu)M},\qquad
\frac{y-\nu Z}{y-\nu Z_0}=\left(\frac M{M_0}\right)^{\nu/(1-\nu)}.
$$
The <proportional-infall model of galactic chemical evolution> therefore gives, for initially pristine gas,
$$
\boxed{Z=\frac y\nu\left[1-\left(\frac M{M_0}\right)^{\nu/(1-\nu)}\right]}.
$$
**The displayed form requires $Z_0=0$**; more generally $Z=y/\nu+(Z_0-y/\nu)(M/M_0)^{\nu/(1-\nu)}$. Also $S=(M_0-M)/(1-\nu)$ for an initially star-free reservoir, so $M/M_0$ is not the <gas fraction of a galaxy> $M/(M+S)$. As a check, the $\nu\to0$ limit is the <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution> relation $Z=y\log(M_0/M)$; at $\nu=1$ the gas mass is constant and one must instead solve $\dot Z=(y-Z)\psi/M_0$. Retaining a nonzero <stellar yield> while setting bulk mass return to zero is the prescribed trace-metal approximation, not a literal claim that stellar ejecta have no mass.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $\nu=1/2$, the <gas-phase metallicity> relation reduces to $Z=2y(1-M/M_0)$. Elimination of $M$ from <mass conservation> gives
$$
M=M_0\left(1-\frac Z{2y}\right),\qquad
S=2(M_0-M)=\frac{M_0Z}{y}.
$$
With a fixed <initial mass function>, let $\kappa$ be the number of surviving low-mass tracer <stars> per unit formed stellar mass. Successive <stars> inherit the monotonically increasing <gas-phase metallicity>, so the cumulative and differential counts are respectively
$$
\boxed{N(<Z)=\frac{\kappa M_0}{y}Z,\qquad \frac{dN}{dZ}=\frac{\kappa M_0}{y}},\qquad 0<Z<Z_f,
$$
where $Z_f=2y(1-M_f/M_0)$ is the abundance at the epoch when star formation stops. Above $Z_f$ the cumulative count is constant, and the differential count vanishes. In the complete gas-exhaustion limit, $Z_f\to2y$ and $N_{\rm total}=2\kappa M_0$, so the normalized <metallicity distribution function> is $p_Z=1/(2y)$ on $0<Z<2y$; at a finite stopping epoch it is $1/Z_f$. **The distribution is flat in linear metallicity.** A <logarithmic metallicity distribution> instead satisfies $dN/d\log_{10}Z=(\ln10)Z\,dN/dZ$ and is not flat.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution> has $M=M_0e^{-Z/y}$ and $S=M_0-M$, giving $dN/dZ=(\kappa M_0/y)e^{-Z/y}$. To compare shapes without confusing different total stellar counts, the figure normalizes both <metallicity distribution functions> to unit integral after complete gas exhaustion and uses $x=Z/y$:
$$
p_{\rm closed}(x)=e^{-x}\quad(x>0),\qquad
p_{\rm infall}(x)=\begin{cases}1/2,&0<x<2,\\0,&x>2.\end{cases}
$$
The right panel also shows the corresponding cumulative fractions, $1-e^{-x}$ and $\min(x/2,1)$.

\Image[paper-60-metallicity.png]
{title=Normalized linear metallicity distributions and cumulative fractions for closed-box and half-rate pristine-infall models}

Pristine <galactic gas inflow> replenishes the fuel and dilutes freshly produced metals, so enrichment per unit newly formed stellar mass is slower and asymptotes to $2y$. More <stars> are formed at later abundances; the <proportional-infall model of galactic chemical evolution> makes twice as many <stars> as the closed box for the same initial gas mass in this limit. Consequently the normalized low-abundance fraction is smaller: for $Z\ll y$, it is $Z/(2y)$ rather than approximately $Z/y$. Their unnormalized $dN/dZ$ actually agree at $Z=0$ for the same $M_0$, which is why stating the normalization matters. At a finite stopping abundance $Z_f$, use $p_Z=1/Z_f$ for infall and $p_Z=e^{-Z/y}/[y(1-e^{-Z_f/y})]$ for the closed box, each truncated at its own chosen stopping point.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The <G-dwarf problem> is the observed shortage of metal-poor, long-lived disc <stars> relative to a simple <closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution>. In the fully evolved comparison, the half-rate <proportional-infall model of galactic chemical evolution> lowers the low-$Z$ fraction from $1-e^{-Z/y}$ to $Z/(2y)$, and prevents the closed-box tail from extending to arbitrarily high <stellar metallicity>. It therefore reduces the relative weight of the earliest metal-poor generation and the very metal-rich tail.

**This particular constant-proportional-infall model is an improvement, not a complete fit.** It still forms a metal-poor population immediately from its initially pristine reservoir, with nonzero constant $dN/dZ$ all the way down to zero, and predicts a broad linear distribution followed by an abrupt upper cutoff instead of a generally smooth, concentrated observed distribution. Comparison with observed abundance histograms must first use the same coordinate: its <logarithmic metallicity distribution> rises in proportion to $Z$ up to that cutoff, rather than remaining flat in logarithmic bins. https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9510101[Local G-dwarf measurements] exhibit a prominent logarithmic-abundance peak and a paucity of low-abundance stars; translating iron abundances into total $Z$ also requires an abundance-ratio model. More realistic histories can vary the <galactic gas inflow> and <star formation rate>, allow recycling and delayed enrichment, and account for migration and selection of different <stellar populations>; simply tuning a constant <stellar yield> does not remove all these shape deficiencies.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $R$ be a characteristic radius enclosing mass $M$, and let $\sigma$ denote a one-dimensional <velocity dispersion>. Gravity and random-motion support give a relation $GM/R=\eta\sigma^2$, where the dimensionless structure coefficient $\eta$ depends on the chosen radius and density profile. A crossing-based <galactic dynamical time> is therefore
$$
t_{\rm dyn}=\frac R\sigma=\frac{GM}{\eta\sigma^3},\qquad
\boxed{t_{\rm dyn}\sim\frac{GM}{\sigma^3}}.
$$
The scaling follows by equating acceleration scales $\sigma^2/R$ and $GM/R^2$, not by identifying a complete orbital period with a crossing time. An explicit scale-free example checks the coefficient: constant <circular speed> and <spherical symmetry> give $M(<r)=v_c^2r/G$ and $\rho=v_c^2/(4\pi Gr^2)$. With isotropic constant <velocity dispersion>, the <spherical Jeans equation> becomes
$$
\sigma^2\frac{d\rho}{dr}=-\rho\frac{v_c^2}{r},
$$
so $v_c^2=2\sigma^2$, $M(<R)=2\sigma^2R/G$, and $R/\sigma=GM/(2\sigma^3)$. This is the <singular isothermal sphere> scaling, valid over an interior radial range; a literal infinite model has no finite total mass, and a hard finite boundary needs a pressure term or an outer transition. A <free-fall time of a uniform sphere> or orbital period changes the numerical factor. The subsequent estimates use the nominal scale $GM/\sigma^3$, keeping these order-unity structural uncertainties explicit.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Using the given constants, the mass is $2\times10^{41}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and the <velocity dispersion> is $2\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The nominal <galactic dynamical time> is
$$
\frac{GM}{\sigma^3}=\frac{(7\times10^{-11})(2\times10^{41})}{(2\times10^5)^3}
=1.75\times10^{15}\,\mathrm s\simeq6\times10^7\,\mathrm{yr}.
$$
Thus **the collapse clock is a few tens of millions of years, of order $10^8$ years**, with $R/\sigma\simeq3\times10^7$ years in the interior <singular isothermal sphere> convention. The nominal radius $GM/\sigma^2\simeq3.5\times10^{20}\,\mathrm m\simeq12\,\mathrm{kpc}$ is a plausible galactic scale. A full stellar orbit takes several crossing times, giving order $10^8$ years near a galactic disc radius, whereas the age of the <Milky Way> is of order $10^{10}$ years. Many orbital passages therefore fit into the lifetime of the <galaxy>, as expected; a <galactic dynamical time> need not equal either its full orbital period or its age.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Putting the whole assumed gas reservoir into <stars> during the nominal <galactic dynamical time> gives
$$
\boxed{\langle\dot M_*\rangle\simeq\frac{10^{11}M_\odot}{6\times10^7\,\mathrm{yr}}\simeq2\times10^3\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}}.
$$
An order-unity shorter collapse clock would raise this to a few thousand <solar masses> per year. The <Milky Way>'s present <star formation rate> is only of order a few <solar masses> per year; for example, https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.1078[A determination combining Galactic measurements] finds about $1.7\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ with a specified <initial mass function>. **The extreme rapid-collapse rate is therefore roughly three orders of magnitude larger**, resembling an intense short-lived <starburst galaxy> phase rather than ongoing ordinary disc <star formation>. This estimate assumes all the quoted mass can form <stars>; it would be smaller if some mass were nonbaryonic <dark matter> or if the conversion efficiency were below unity.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For an isolated final system with isotropic one-dimensional <velocity dispersion> $\sigma$, the random <kinetic energy> is $K=\tfrac12M\langle v^2\rangle=\tfrac32M\sigma^2$. The <virial theorem> gives $W=-2K$ and total energy $E_f=K+W=-K$. Starting from a diffuse, almost unbound gas reservoir with $E_i\simeq0$, <conservation of energy> therefore requires the <radiative energy of virialized gravitational collapse>
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm rad}\simeq E_i-E_f\simeq\frac32M\sigma^2\simeq1\times10^{52}\,\mathrm J}.
$$
It is the decrease in total energy, not the whole final magnitude $|W|$, that is lost; half of the binding-energy magnitude remains as <kinetic energy>. Assuming radiation carries most of this loss, the average <luminosity> during the nominal <galactic dynamical time> is
$$
\boxed{\langle L\rangle\simeq\frac{1.2\times10^{52}}{1.75\times10^{15}}
\simeq7\times10^{36}\,\mathrm W\simeq2\times10^{10}L_\odot},
$$
using $L_\odot\simeq4\times10^{26}\,\mathrm W$. This is comparable in order of magnitude to the stellar <luminosity> of a characteristic $L_*$ <galaxy> in the <Schechter function>, often of order $10^{10}L_\odot$ with a band-dependent definition. The equality is an energy-scale comparison: ordinary present starlight is powered mainly by <stellar nucleosynthesis>, whereas this estimate concerns gravitational energy lost during formation. Appreciable initial binding energy, mass loss or external stresses alter the coefficient, and the finite-boundary <singular isothermal sphere> cannot be used as an isolated exact <virial equilibrium> without its boundary term.

= Solution
{parent=v}

The assumed central <black-hole> mass is $M_\bullet\sim10^8M_\odot\simeq2\times10^{38}\,\mathrm{kg}$. A mass alone does not fix radiated energy: specify a formation mechanism and its <radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion>. For radiatively efficient accretion, if a fraction $\epsilon$ of the supplied rest-mass energy escapes and the remainder increases the <black-hole> mass, then $M_\bullet=(1-\epsilon)M_{\rm supplied}$ and
$$
\boxed{E_\bullet=\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}M_\bullet c^2,qquad
\langle L_\bullet\rangle=\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\frac{M_\bullet c^2}{t_{\rm dyn}}}.
$$
Taking the customary order-of-magnitude efficiency $\epsilon\sim0.1$ gives
$$
E_\bullet\simeq2\times10^{54}\,\mathrm J,qquad
\langle L_\bullet\rangle\simeq10^{39}\,\mathrm W\simeq3\times10^{12}L_\odot.
$$
**The coeval accretion phase would be a quasar-scale source, hundreds of times brighter than a typical present $L_*$ galaxy.** Compared directly with the preceding gravitational-collapse estimate,
$$
\frac{E_\bullet}{E_{\rm rad}}\simeq\frac23\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
\frac{M_\bullet}{M}\left(\frac c\sigma\right)^2\sim10^2.
$$
Despite its small mass fraction, <black-hole accretion> extracts a fraction of rest-mass energy, while galactic collapse releases only an energy scale $M\sigma^2\ll Mc^2$. The numerical <luminosity> is conditional: a <radiatively inefficient accretion flow>, direct collapse or assembly by mergers need not radiate this amount over the galactic clock, and the question supplies no unique efficiency.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Older disc <stellar populations> generally have larger <velocity dispersions>, partly through accumulated <galactic disk heating>, so random-motion stresses supply more of their support against gravity. The radial <Jeans equation> then requires a smaller mean rotation than the <circular speed>, producing **larger stellar asymmetric drift** than in dynamically colder young <stars>.

= ii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Low-mass <main-sequence> <stars> live for much of the <Milky Way>'s age, retaining representatives of many generations, while <phase mixing> disperses their birth associations around their orbits. In an approximately axisymmetric <galactic disk>, a sufficiently complete local census can therefore sample the mixed annular <stellar population> at the Sun's radius despite its small volume. **This is a representative local-radius sample, not automatically an unbiased whole-disc census**: different vertical <scale heights>, selection effects and radial migration require corrections when inferring integrated population fractions.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Metal <absorption lines> depend on the <temperatures>, <ionization> states and luminosity weights of the contributing <stellar populations>, as well as their <stellar metallicity>. A young <stellar population> dominated by hot luminous <stars> can have weak metal <absorption lines> even at substantial abundance, so **weak lines alone do not establish low metallicity**, an aspect of the <age-metallicity degeneracy>.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Typical present <elliptical galaxies> are dominated by old <stellar populations>: their brightest short-lived <stars> have disappeared, leaving faint long-lived <stars> and <stellar remnants>, so luminosity per unit stellar mass is low. Ongoing <star formation> in many <spiral galaxies> and <irregular galaxies> supplies luminous young <stars>, giving **a smaller stellar mass-to-light ratio in a fixed band**; <dark matter>, the <initial mass function> and aperture must also be specified if the quoted ratio is dynamical.

= Solution
{parent=v}

Many <open clusters> are weakly bound and are dispersed by <two-body relaxation>, <stellar evaporation> and <tidal heating>, including encounters with <molecular clouds> in the <galactic disk>, so their present age histogram preferentially retains young survivors. More massive compact <globular clusters> can survive much longer, while the released low-mass <stars> remain as old field <stellar populations>: **disruption removes clusters from the catalogue without removing their stars from the disc**.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vi}

In the radial-residual convention $\delta r=a_4\cos4\theta$, **boxy isophotes have $a_4<0$ and disky isophotes have $a_4>0$**, since negative $a_4$ extends the diagonal directions and positive $a_4$ extends the fitted axes. Massive <elliptical galaxies> tend to have slowly rotating, anisotropic stellar structures associated with <dry galaxy mergers>, whereas rotating embedded <galactic disks>, often associated with dissipation in <gas-rich galaxy mergers>, favour disky structure in lower-mass <elliptical galaxies>. These are population trends, as in https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9610055[Central structure measurements], rather than a universal mass rule, and brightness-residual Fourier coefficients must first be converted to this radial convention.

= vii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vii}

The <neutral-hydrogen 21-centimeter line> traces cold gas far beyond the bright stellar <galactic disk>, where a <dark-matter halo> contributes strongly to the <galaxy rotation curve>. With relatively small random velocities, this gas approximately follows <circular orbits>, allowing its Doppler velocities to measure the <circular speed> after inclination and pressure corrections, whereas stellar dispersions require an orbital-anisotropy model. **Its extended radial reach and comparatively simple force tracing** make neutral gas especially useful for outer-halo constraints, although warps and noncircular motions can invalidate that simplicity.

= viii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=viii}

Many lower-luminosity <elliptical galaxies> have dense central stellar cusps, giving far more photons and kinematic tracers near a central <black hole> than the shallow depleted cores common in massive <elliptical galaxies>, as measured in https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9610055[Central galaxy profiles]. Thus **central <surface brightness> can make the smaller black hole easier to measure**, despite its smaller mass, whereas sparse central tracers and uncertain stellar orbital anisotropy weaken measurements in a large core. This is not a universal mass ordering: the <black-hole sphere of influence> $r_{\rm inf}\simeq GM_\bullet/\sigma^2$ must still be sufficiently resolved in angle, so distance, <velocity dispersion> and <angular resolution> also matter.
