An <integral curve of a vector field> $X$ through $p$ is a <smooth curve> $c:I\to M$, on an interval containing zero, satisfying $c(0)=p$ and $\dot c(t)=X_{c(t)}$. The <local flow> is the smoothly varying family of these <integral curves of a vector field>:
$$
\phi:D\longrightarrow M,\qquad \phi_t(p)=c_p(t),\qquad
\phi_0(p)=p,\quad \partial_t\phi_t(p)=X_{\phi_t(p)}.
$$
Here $D$ is an open neighborhood of $\{0\}\times M$; for the maximal <local flow>, its fibre over $p$ is the maximal existence interval of $c_p$. Local existence, uniqueness and smooth dependence for a smooth <ordinary differential equation> give this family. Uniqueness also gives $\phi_{t+s}(p)=\phi_t(\phi_s(p))$ whenever the relevant curves and compositions are defined, and $\phi_{-t}=\phi_t^{-1}$ on their common domains.

Reparametrizing the <integral curves of a vector field> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{2t}(p)=2X_{\phi_{2t}(p)}.
$$
Thus **the flow of $2X$ is $\boxed{\widetilde\phi_t=\phi_{2t}}$**, with domain $\{(t,p):(2t,p)\in D\}$.

Commutation of the two <local flows> means $\phi_t\psi_s=\psi_s\phi_t$ for all sufficiently small independent parameters $s,t$, on their common local domains. Differentiate this identity in $s$ at zero. The <chain rule> gives
$$
d\phi_t|_p(Y_p)=Y_{\phi_t(p)}.
$$
In a <coordinate chart>, differentiating again in $t$ at zero gives $DX_pY_p=DY_pX_p$. But the <Lie bracket of vector fields> has coordinate expression
$$
[X,Y]_p=DY_pX_p-DX_pY_p,
$$
so commuting <local flows> imply $[X,Y]=0$.

For the converse, suppose the <Lie bracket of vector fields> vanishes. Write $u(t)=\phi_t(p)$ in a <coordinate chart>, and put $J(t)=d\phi_t|_p$. Differentiating the <local flow> equation with respect to the initial point gives the variational <ordinary differential equation>
$$
\dot J(t)=DX_{u(t)}J(t),\qquad J(0)=I.
$$
Consequently $W(t)=Y_{u(t)}-J(t)Y_p$ satisfies
$$
\dot W(t)=DY_{u(t)}X_{u(t)}-DX_{u(t)}J(t)Y_p
=DX_{u(t)}W(t),\qquad W(0)=0,
$$
where the zero <Lie bracket of vector fields> was used in the second equality. Uniqueness for this linear <ordinary differential equation> gives $W=0$. This is a local argument and can be continued through successive <coordinate charts>, proving $d\phi_t(Y)=Y\circ\phi_t$ wherever the <local flow> is defined.

For fixed $t$, the curve $s\mapsto\phi_t(\psi_s(p))$ therefore has derivative $Y$ at its current point and starts at $\phi_t(p)$. Uniqueness of the <integral curve of a vector field> $Y$ identifies it with $s\mapsto\psi_s(\phi_t(p))$. Hence **$\boxed{\phi_t\psi_s=\psi_s\phi_t\ \Longleftrightarrow\ [X,Y]=0}$ locally**. No <complete vector field> hypothesis is needed.

Using the coordinate formula for the <Lie bracket of vector fields>, the two components are
$$
[X,Y]^x=X(0)-Y(y)=-\frac{x^2}{2},\qquad
[X,Y]^y=X(x^2/2)-Y(0)=xy.
$$
Thus $\boxed{[X,Y]=-(x^2/2)\partial_x+xy\partial_y}$, which is not the zero <vector field>.

The <integral curves of a vector field> $X$ solve $\dot x=y$, $\dot y=0$, whereas those of $Y$ solve $\dot x=0$, $\dot y=x^2/2$. Their <local flows> are
$$
\boxed{\phi_t(x,y)=(x+ty,y),\qquad \psi_s(x,y)=(x,y+sx^2/2).}
$$
Both formulas exist for all real parameters, so both are <complete vector fields>. Direct composition gives
$$
\phi_t\psi_s(x,y)=(x+ty+tsx^2/2,\ y+sx^2/2),\qquad
\psi_s\phi_t(x,y)=(x+ty,\ y+s(x+ty)^2/2).
$$
At $(1,0)$ their first coordinates differ by $ts/2$. Hence these <local flows> do not commute when $s,t$ are nonzero.

For the <Lie bracket of vector fields>, start at $(2,0)$. An <integral curve of a vector field> $[X,Y]$ is
$$
(x(t),y(t))=\left(\frac{2}{1+t},0\right),\qquad -1<t<\infty.
$$
It solves $\dot x=-x^2/2$, $\dot y=xy$, but $x(t)$ diverges as $t\downarrow-1$. It cannot extend through that finite time as a curve in $\mathbb R^2$. **Complete vector fields are therefore not closed under the Lie bracket.**

For the sum $X+Y$, its <integral curves of a vector field> obey $\dot x=y$, $\dot y=x^2/2$. Substitution of $x=(1+\alpha t)^{-2}$ and $y=\beta(1+\alpha t)^{-3}$ requires
$$
\beta=-2\alpha,\qquad -3\alpha\beta=\frac12,
\qquad \alpha^2=\frac1{12}.
$$
Choosing $\alpha=-1/(2\sqrt3)$ gives the <integral curve of a vector field>
$$
\gamma(t)=\left(\left(1-\frac{t}{2\sqrt3}\right)^{-2},
\frac1{\sqrt3}\left(1-\frac{t}{2\sqrt3}\right)^{-3}\right),\qquad t<2\sqrt3.
$$
It starts at $(1,1/\sqrt3)$ and escapes to infinity at the finite time $2\sqrt3$. **Complete vector fields are not closed under addition either.** Both failures use the same pair of <complete vector fields>.

A smooth rank-$r$ <vector bundle> over a <smooth manifold> $M$ consists of a smooth total space $E$, a smooth projection $\pi:E\to M$, and an $r$-dimensional real <vector space> structure on each fibre $E_p=\pi^{-1}(p)$. Each point has a neighborhood $U$ with a <vector bundle trivialization>
$$
\tau_U:\pi^{-1}(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}U\times\mathbb R^r
$$
that is a <diffeomorphism>, commutes with projection to $U$, and is a <linear isomorphism> on every fibre. The overlap maps have the form $(p,v)\mapsto(p,h(p)v)$ with $h:U\cap V\to GL_r(\mathbb R)$ smooth. For a complex <vector bundle>, replace $\mathbb R$ by $\mathbb C$ and require complex-linear fibre maps.

A <connection on a vector bundle> is an $\mathbb R$-linear map
$$
\nabla:\Gamma(E)\longrightarrow\Omega^1(M;E),\qquad
\nabla(fs)=df\otimes s+f\nabla s,
$$
where $\Gamma(E)$ is the <module of smooth sections>, $\Omega^1(M;E)$ consists of <vector-bundle-valued differential forms> of degree one, and $f$ is a <smooth function>. Equivalently, evaluating on a <vector field> $X$ gives operators $\nabla_X$ with $\nabla_{fX}s=f\nabla_Xs$ and $\nabla_X(fs)=X(f)s+f\nabla_Xs$. A <connection on a vector bundle> is local: its value at a point depends on the germ of the <section of a vector bundle>, so the same definition applies to local sections.

Realize the <pullback vector bundle> as
$$
\phi^*E=\{(p,v)\in M'\times E:\phi(p)=\pi(v)\},\qquad
\pi'(p,v)=p.
$$
Its fibre over $p$ is canonically $E_{\phi(p)}$, with the same <vector space> operations. If a <vector bundle trivialization> over $U\subset M$ is $\tau_U(v)=(\pi(v),\widehat v)$, give the <pullback vector bundle> the local trivialization
$$
T_U:(\pi')^{-1}(\phi^{-1}U)\longrightarrow\phi^{-1}U\times\mathbb R^r,
\qquad T_U(p,v)=(p,\widehat v).
$$
Declare these maps to be local homeomorphisms and use <coordinate charts> on $M'$ to obtain a <smooth atlas> on the total space. On overlaps the <smooth transition maps> are $(p,w)\mapsto(p,h(\phi(p))w)$, hence smooth and fibrewise linear. They satisfy the same cocycle identities as the original transition maps. This gives a well-defined smooth <vector bundle>, with smooth projection and rank $r$. It also gives the <subspace topology> inherited from $M'\times E$: inside each product $\phi^{-1}(U)\times\pi^{-1}(U)$ the defining condition is the graph of $\phi$ in the base coordinates. Thus the total space is Hausdorff and second countable, as required for a <smooth manifold>.

For a smooth <section of a vector bundle> $s$ over $U$, its pulled-back section is more precisely $p\mapsto(p,s(\phi(p)))$. If $s$ has component column $u:U\to\mathbb R^r$ in the original <vector bundle trivialization>, its component column in $T_U$ is $u\circ\phi$, which is smooth. **The pullback is a smooth rank-$r$ vector bundle, and every local section pulls back smoothly.**

The expression $d\phi(X)$ is in general a <vector field along a map>, rather than a <vector field> on $M$. Its intended meaning in the required formula is pointwise evaluation of the <vector-bundle-valued differential form> $\nabla s$:
$$
\bigl(\nabla'_{X}(\phi^*s)\bigr)_p
=(\nabla s)_{\phi(p)}(d\phi_pX_p),
$$
using the canonical identification $(\phi^*E)_p=E_{\phi(p)}$. This interpretation works for every <smooth map>, including a constant map or a map with self-intersections.

Choose a <frame of a vector bundle> over $U$, written as a row $e=(e_1,\ldots,e_r)$. Define its <connection one-form> $\Omega$ by
$$
\nabla(eu)=e(du+\Omega u)
$$
for component columns $u$. The pulled-back frame $e^\phi=(\phi^*e_1,\ldots,\phi^*e_r)$ is a <frame of a vector bundle> over $\phi^{-1}U$. For any component column $v$ of <smooth functions> on that open set, define
$$
\boxed{\nabla'(e^\phi v)=e^\phi\bigl(dv+(\phi^*\Omega)v\bigr).}
$$
Here the matrix entries of $\phi^*\Omega$ are ordinary <pullbacks of a differential form>. The displayed operator is linear and satisfies the <Leibniz rule>, hence is locally a <connection on a vector bundle>.

To prove these local operators glue, change the original <frame of a vector bundle> to $\widetilde e=eh$. Expanding $\nabla(eh u)$ gives the <change of frame of a vector-bundle connection>
$$
\widetilde\Omega=h^{-1}\Omega h+h^{-1}dh.
$$
The <pullback of a differential form> and the <chain rule> give
$$
\phi^*\widetilde\Omega
=(h\circ\phi)^{-1}(\phi^*\Omega)(h\circ\phi)
+(h\circ\phi)^{-1}d(h\circ\phi).
$$
This is precisely the required transformation law for the pulled-back frame $\widetilde e^\phi=e^\phi(h\circ\phi)$. Thus the definitions agree and give a global <pullback connection>.

For $s=eu$, substitute $v=u\circ\phi$ in the defining formula and use $d(u\circ\phi)(X)=du(d\phi(X))$. This proves the required identity. Conversely that identity fixes $\nabla'_X(\phi^*e_a)$; the <Leibniz rule> then fixes the derivative of every $\sum_a v^a\phi^*e_a$, proving uniqueness. If the identity is initially imposed only on global <sections of a vector bundle>, multiply each local frame section by a <smooth bump function> equal to one near the point in question and supported in $U$, and extend by zero. These global sections agree with the frame locally, so the same uniqueness argument applies. **The pullback connection exists and is unique.**

The <curvature form of a connection> is the endomorphism-valued <differential two-form> defined by
$$
R^\nabla(X,Y)s=\nabla_X\nabla_Ys-\nabla_Y\nabla_Xs-\nabla_{[X,Y]}s.
$$
It is alternating in $X,Y$. To see its <tensoriality>, replacing $X$ by $fX$ produces an extra $-Y(f)\nabla_Xs$ from the second term and an extra $+Y(f)\nabla_Xs$ from $[fX,Y]=f[X,Y]-Y(f)X$, so these cancel. Alternation gives linearity over <smooth functions> in $Y$ too. Replacing $s$ by $fs$ produces the additional coefficient $(X(Yf)-Y(Xf)-[X,Y]f)s=0$; all terms involving one derivative of $s$ also cancel. Thus $R^\nabla$ belongs to $\Omega^2(M;\operatorname{End}E)$.

In the coefficient-column convention of part (b), expanding the definition gives
$$
R^\nabla(X,Y)(eu)=e\Bigl(
X(\Omega(Y))-Y(\Omega(X))-\Omega([X,Y])
+\Omega(X)\Omega(Y)-\Omega(Y)\Omega(X)\Bigr)u.
$$
The first three terms are $d\Omega(X,Y)$, and the last two are $(\Omega\wedge\Omega)(X,Y)$. This proves the <Cartan curvature matrix equation> $F=d\Omega+\Omega\wedge\Omega$, with matrix multiplication combined with the <wedge product of differential forms>.

The <pullback connection> has matrix $\Omega'=\phi^*\Omega$. Since the <pullback of a differential form> commutes with the <exterior derivative> and the <wedge product of differential forms>, its <curvature form of a connection> has matrix
$$
F'=d(\phi^*\Omega)+(\phi^*\Omega)\wedge(\phi^*\Omega)
=\phi^*(d\Omega+\Omega\wedge\Omega)=\phi^*F.
$$
Changes of <frame of a vector bundle> conjugate both sides by the same pulled-back transition matrix, so this identity is intrinsic. In fibre notation the answer is
$$
\boxed{R^{\nabla'}_p(u,v)=R^\nabla_{\phi(p)}(d\phi_pu,d\phi_pv),
\qquad u,v\in T_pM'.}
$$
Both sides act on the same fibre $E_{\phi(p)}$. No claim that $d\phi$ preserves <Lie brackets of vector fields> for an arbitrary map is needed.

An <oriented atlas> is a <smooth atlas> whose <smooth transition maps> have positive <Jacobian determinants> at every point of their domains. Such an atlas supplies an <orientation of a vector space> on every <tangent space>, and these choices agree on overlaps.

Let $\omega$ be a nowhere-vanishing smooth top-degree <differential form>. In a <coordinate chart> write
$$
\omega=f(x)\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
$$
The coefficient $f$ is smooth and nowhere zero. Restrict the <coordinate chart> to connected neighborhoods; the <intermediate value theorem> makes the sign of $f$ constant on each. If $f$ is negative, replacing $x^1$ by $-x^1$ changes the sign of the coordinate <volume form>. Thus for $n\geq1$ we can choose a covering of <coordinate charts> in each of which the coefficient is positive.

On an overlap of two such <coordinate charts>, the <wedge product of differential forms> transforms by
$$
dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n
=\det\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)
dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n.
$$
Consequently $f_y=f_x\det(\partial x/\partial y)$. Both coefficients are positive, so the <Jacobian determinant> is positive, as is that of the inverse transition. **A nowhere-vanishing top form therefore determines an oriented atlas.** The zero-dimensional case has only zero-dimensional transitions, whose determinant is one, and is automatically orientable. Connectedness of $M$ is not needed for the positive-dimensional construction; only the local sign choice matters.

Use <coordinate charts> from the chosen <oriented atlas>. In each, the matrix $G_x=(g_{ij})$ of the <Riemannian metric> is smooth and <positive definite>, so $\det G_x>0$ and its positive square root is smooth. Define the local <Riemannian volume form> by
$$
\omega_{g,x}=\sqrt{\det G_x}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
$$
If $y$ is another positive <coordinate chart> and $J=\partial x/\partial y$, the transformation rule for the <metric tensor> is $G_y=J^TG_xJ$. Taking <determinants> gives
$$
\det G_y=(\det J)^2\det G_x,
\qquad \sqrt{\det G_y}=|\det J|\sqrt{\det G_x}.
$$
The <oriented atlas> has $\det J>0$, so the <wedge product of differential forms> transformation from part (a) gives
$$
\sqrt{\det G_y}\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n
=\sqrt{\det G_x}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
$$
The local expressions therefore glue to a global smooth top-degree <differential form>. Its local coefficient never vanishes, so it is a <volume form>. **The global answer is $\boxed{\omega_g=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n}$ in positive charts.** In an arbitrary negatively oriented chart the corresponding expression needs a minus sign; the orientation is essential to the form, although a positive metric volume density exists without it.

**The integral identity requires compactness of $M$, compact support of $X$, or suitable conditions eliminating flux at infinity.** The printed question does not state such a hypothesis. We prove the <Riemannian divergence theorem> for a <compact manifold>, and more generally for a <compactly supported> smooth <vector field> on an oriented <Riemannian manifold> with boundary.

Write $j:\partial M\hookrightarrow M$ for the inclusion. Give $\partial M$ the <outward-normal-first boundary orientation>, and let $N$ be the outward unit <normal vector>. The <Riemannian volume form> of the induced <Riemannian metric> is
$$
\omega_{\widetilde g}=j^*(\iota_N\omega_g).
$$
Indeed, if $(e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1})$ is a positively oriented <orthonormal basis> tangent to the boundary, $(N,e_1,\ldots,e_{n-1})$ is a positively oriented <orthonormal basis> in $M$. Both sides therefore evaluate to one on the boundary basis.

At a boundary point decompose $X=g(X,N)N+X^\top$, where $X^\top$ is tangent to $\partial M$. Evaluating on $n-1$ tangent vectors, the term with $X^\top$ vanishes: all $n$ arguments of the alternating <volume form> then lie in the $(n-1)$-dimensional boundary <tangent space>. Thus the <interior product of a differential form> satisfies
$$
j^*(\iota_X\omega_g)=g(X,N)\,\omega_{\widetilde g}.
$$
The general <Stokes theorem> for an oriented <manifold with boundary> states $\int_M d\eta=\int_{\partial M}j^*\eta$ for any smooth <differential form> $\eta$ of degree $n-1$ with <compact support>; compactness of $M$ makes the support condition automatic. Applying it to $\eta=\iota_X\omega_g$ and using the defining equation for the <divergence of a Riemannian vector field> proves
$$
\boxed{\int_M (\operatorname{div}X)\,\omega_g
=\int_{\partial M}g(X,N)\,\omega_{\widetilde g}.}
$$

For a concrete failure without the extra hypothesis, take $M=[0,\infty)$ with <Riemannian metric> $dx^2$ and $X=(\arctan x)\partial_x$. The <divergence of a Riemannian vector field> is $\operatorname{div}X=(1+x^2)^{-1}$, so
$$
\int_M\operatorname{div}X\,dx=\frac\pi2,
\qquad \int_{\partial M}g(X,N)\,\omega_{\widetilde g}=0,
$$
because $X$ vanishes at the only boundary point. Both integrals are finite. The missing $\pi/2$ is the limiting flux at infinity, so mere integrability of the divergence cannot repair the unrestricted assertion.

A subset $Z$ of an $m$-dimensional <smooth manifold> $M$ is an $r$-dimensional <embedded submanifold> if, for each $p\in Z$, some <coordinate chart> $x:U\to V\subset\mathbb R^m$ containing $p$ satisfies
$$
x(U\cap Z)=V\cap(\mathbb R^r\times\{0\}^{m-r}).
$$
The induced topology on $Z$ is the <subspace topology>, and the first $r$ coordinates restricted to $Z$ give its <smooth atlas>. To see their <smooth transition maps> agree, restrict the ambient smooth transitions to the coordinate planes; their inverses restrict in the same way. The inclusion then has injective <differential of a smooth map> and is a homeomorphism onto its image. Conversely this is the local coordinate description of a smooth <smooth embedding>. An <embedded submanifold> need not be a closed subset of $M$.

Put $m=\dim M$ and $n=\dim N$. The hypothesis says that $q$ is a <regular value> of $F$. Since its preimage is nonempty and the <differential of a smooth map> is surjective there, $m\geq n$.

We use the following finite-dimensional <inverse function theorem>: if a smooth map $H:O\to\mathbb R^m$, with $O\subset\mathbb R^m$ open, has invertible derivative at $a$, there are open neighborhoods of $a$ and $H(a)$ on which $H$ is a <diffeomorphism>, with smooth inverse.

Fix $p\in F^{-1}(q)$. Choose <coordinate charts> centered at $p$ and $q$, and shrink the source neighborhood so that its image under $F$ lies in the target chart. Its coordinate expression is $f=(f^1,\ldots,f^n)$ with $f(0)=0$ and derivative of rank $n$. Choose $n$ domain coordinates giving an invertible $n\times n$ minor, and relabel them as $x^1,\ldots,x^n$. Define
$$
H(x)=\bigl(f^1(x),\ldots,f^n(x),x^{n+1},\ldots,x^m\bigr).
$$
Its derivative at zero has block form
$$
DH_0=\begin{pmatrix}B&C\\0&I_{m-n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det B\ne0,
$$
so it is invertible. The <inverse function theorem> makes $u=H(x)$ a new <coordinate chart> near $p$. In this chart $F=q$ is exactly $u^1=\cdots=u^n=0$, because the target chart sends $q$ to zero. These are <slice charts for an embedded submanifold>; reordering coordinates if desired puts the free coordinates first. Giving the level set the <subspace topology>, these restricted charts are smoothly compatible, as in part (a). This proves the <regular level set theorem> rather than merely invoking it.

**The level set is an embedded submanifold of dimension $\boxed{m-n}$**. The same coordinates show $T_pZ=\ker dF_p$: tangent vectors to the slice have zero first $n$ components, which are exactly the components of $dF_p$. The empty-dimensional case $m=n$ gives a discrete zero-dimensional <embedded submanifold>.

Let $m=\dim M$, $n=\dim N$ and $k=\operatorname{codim}_N S$. Fix $p\in f^{-1}(S)$ and choose the allowed <slice chart for an embedded submanifold> on a neighborhood $U$ of $f(p)$, so that $S\cap U$ is given by $x^1=\cdots=x^k=0$. On $f^{-1}(U)$ define the <smooth map>
$$
G=(x^1,\ldots,x^k)\circ f:f^{-1}(U)\longrightarrow\mathbb R^k.
$$
The derivative $L=d(x^1,\ldots,x^k)$ at a point of $S\cap U$ is surjective and has kernel $T S$, by the slice description. The printed sum-of-spaces condition gives
$$
L\bigl(df_r(T_rM)\bigr)=\mathbb R^k\qquad
\text{for every }r\in f^{-1}(S\cap U).
$$
Indeed any target tangent vector is a sum of an image vector and a tangent vector to $S$, and $L$ kills the latter. By the <chain rule>, $dG_r=L\circ df_r$ is therefore surjective. This condition is precisely <transversality of a map to a submanifold>; the original sum need not be direct.

We have $G^{-1}(0)=f^{-1}(S)\cap f^{-1}(U)$. Part (b), applied to $G$, gives <slice charts for an embedded submanifold> of codimension $k$ around every point of this zero set. Since the construction is available around every $p\in f^{-1}(S)$, the charts give its global structure as an <embedded submanifold> with the <subspace topology>. **Its dimension is $\boxed{m-k=m-n+\dim S}$**. Moreover,

\boxed{T_p(f^{-1}(S))=\{v\in T_pM:df_p(v)\in T_{f(p)}S\}.}
$$
This follows either from the slice coordinates or from $T_pG^{-1}(0)=\ker dG_p$ and $\ker L=T_{f(p)}S$. No injectivity of $df$, closedness of $S$, or direct-sum hypothesis is required.

An <affine connection> $\nabla$ is a <metric connection> for the <Riemannian metric> $g$ when
$$
\boxed{X\bigl(g(Y,Z)\bigr)=g(\nabla_XY,Z)+g(Y,\nabla_XZ)
\quad\text{for all vector fields }X,Y,Z.}
$$
Equivalently the induced <covariant derivative> of the <metric tensor> satisfies $\nabla g=0$. This is <metric compatibility>; it imposes no condition on the <torsion tensor>. A <Levi-Civita connection> has the additional property of being a <torsion-free connection>.

= Solution

A <vector field along a map> $\gamma$ assigns $V(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}M$ smoothly to each parameter value. Equivalently, it is a <section of a vector bundle> $\gamma^*TM$. The <pullback connection> defines its <covariant derivative along a curve>, $D_tV$. To make the definition explicit, use <connection coefficients> with the convention
$$
\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\partial_k.
$$
For $V=V^k(t)\partial_k|_{\gamma(t)}$ the derivative is
$$
D_tV=\left(\frac{dV^k}{dt}
+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(\gamma(t))\dot\gamma^i(t)V^j(t)\right)\partial_k.
$$
The <change of frame of a vector-bundle connection> ensures this expression is independent of the <coordinate chart>. It remains meaningful when $\dot\gamma=0$ and does not require extending $V$ to one ambient <vector field>.

**The field is parallel exactly when $\boxed{D_tV=0}$**. A <geodesic> for $\nabla$, with its specified affine parameter, is a <smooth curve> whose own tangent is parallel:
$$
\boxed{D_t\dot\gamma=0,
\qquad \ddot\gamma^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(\gamma)\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^j=0.}
$$
This definition applies to any <affine connection>, whether or not it is a <metric connection>. A non-affine change of parameter generally gives a <pregeodesic> instead of this parametrized <geodesic>.

We use the following existence and uniqueness statement for <parallel transport>: for a smooth <affine connection>, a smooth curve $\gamma:[0,1]\to M$, an initial time $t_0$ and a vector $v\in T_{\gamma(t_0)}M$, there is a unique smooth <vector field along a map> $V$ on the whole curve satisfying $D_tV=0$ and $V(t_0)=v$. In a <frame of a vector bundle> this is the linear <ordinary differential equation> $\dot V=-\Gamma(\dot\gamma)V$. Its smooth coefficient matrix is bounded on every compact time subinterval in a trivializing neighborhood, and the linear existence theorem gives a solution throughout that subinterval. A finite subdivision of $[0,1]$ into such neighborhoods and uniqueness patch the solutions. No completeness hypothesis on $M$ is needed.

For arbitrary fields $U,V$ along a curve, the definition of the induced <covariant derivative> of the <metric tensor> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}g(U,V)
=(\nabla_{\dot\gamma}g)(U,V)+g(D_tU,V)+g(U,D_tV).
$$
This can also be checked by differentiating $g_{ij}(\gamma(t))U^i(t)V^j(t)$ and substituting the formula in part (a). If $\nabla$ is a <metric connection> and $V$ is parallel, it follows that $d(g(V,V))/dt=0$. Positivity of the <Riemannian metric> makes $|V|=\sqrt{g(V,V)}$ constant. Thus <parallel transport> preserves lengths.

Conversely assume every parallel field along every smooth curve has constant length. Fix $p\in M$ and arbitrary $x,v\in T_pM$. Choose a curve through $p$ with velocity $x$ at an interior time $t_0$. Explicitly, in a <coordinate chart> $z$ centered at $p$, take $\gamma(t)=z^{-1}(h(t)\,dz_p(x))$, where $h(t_0)=0$, $h'(t_0)=1$, and $h$ has sufficiently small amplitude and support near $t_0$. A <smooth bump function> produces such $h$, so the curve is defined on all of $[0,1]$ and remains inside the chart. By the stated <parallel transport> theorem there is a parallel field with $V(t_0)=v$. Evaluating the derivative of its squared length gives
$$
0=\left.\frac d{dt}g(V,V)\right|_{t_0}=(\nabla_xg)_p(v,v).
$$
For fixed $x$, the <bilinear form> $B_x(v,w)=(\nabla_xg)_p(v,w)$ is symmetric. Its diagonal values vanish, so <polarization identity> gives
$$
2B_x(v,w)=B_x(v+w,v+w)-B_x(v,v)-B_x(w,w)=0.
$$
Since $p,x,v,w$ were arbitrary, $\nabla g=0$. **A connection is metric-compatible if and only if all parallel fields have constant length.** Polarization also shows that its <parallel transport> preserves all <inner products>, not just lengths.

= Solution
{parent=c}

First verify the <difference of affine connections is a tensor>. Linearity over <smooth functions> in $X$ follows from the <affine connection> axioms. In $Y$, the two derivative terms cancel:
$$
A(X,fY)=\nabla_X(fY)-\nabla'_X(fY)=fA(X,Y).
$$
Thus $A$ is a smooth $(1,2)$-<tensor field>, and $A_p(u,v)$ depends only on the tangent vectors $u,v$ at $p$. For any <smooth curve>, its two covariant accelerations satisfy
$$
D_t\dot\gamma-D'_t\dot\gamma=A(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma).
$$
If $A(X,Y)=-A(Y,X)$, its diagonal values vanish. The two <geodesic equations> are therefore identical, and the connections have the same parametrized <geodesics>.

For necessity, the required local <geodesic> existence theorem is this: for every smooth <affine connection>, $p\in M$ and $v\in T_pM$, a unique <geodesic> exists on some interval about zero with $\gamma(0)=p$ and $\dot\gamma(0)=v$. Indeed the <geodesic equation> is the smooth first-order <ordinary differential equation> on position and velocity
$$
\dot x^k=v^k,\qquad \dot v^k=-\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x)v^iv^j,
$$
so local existence and uniqueness apply. If the two connections have the same parametrized <geodesics>, apply the acceleration identity to this <geodesic> at zero to obtain $A_p(v,v)=0$ for every $p,v$. Expanding $A_p(u+v,u+v)=0$ gives $A_p(u,v)+A_p(v,u)=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{same parametrized geodesics}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A(X,Y)=-A(Y,X).}
$$
This is the criterion that <parametrized geodesics determine the symmetric part of an affine connection>. It concerns equality with the same affine parameters. Agreement merely of unparametrized images is weaker: adding $\alpha(X)Y+\alpha(Y)X$ to a connection, for a smooth one-form $\alpha$, changes acceleration by a multiple of the velocity, which can be absorbed by reparametrization. Such a difference need not be antisymmetric.

Finally, since $\nabla'_XY=\nabla_XY-A(X,Y)$, expand the derivative of the <metric tensor>:
$$
(\nabla'_Xg)(Y,Z)
=X(g(Y,Z))-g(\nabla'_XY,Z)-g(Y,\nabla'_XZ)
=(\nabla_Xg)(Y,Z)+g(A(X,Y),Z)+g(Y,A(X,Z)).
$$
Under the assumed <metric compatibility> of $\nabla$, the first term is zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\nabla' g=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
g(A(X,Y),Z)=-g(Y,A(X,Z))\quad\text{for all }X,Y,Z.}
$$
Equivalently, for each fixed $X$, the endomorphism $A(X,\cdot)$ is skew-adjoint for the <Riemannian metric>. This metric criterion does not require the two connections to have the same <geodesics>.
