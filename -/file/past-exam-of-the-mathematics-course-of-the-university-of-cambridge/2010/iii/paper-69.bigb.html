The <solar constant> measures the incident power per area normal to the sunlight. A spherical <Earth> of radius $R$ intercepts sunlight over the disc $\pi R^2$, but radiates over its surface $4\pi R^2$. For uniform <surface albedo> $a$ in the global energy-budget approximation, absorbed power is $(1-a)S\pi R^2$. Treat the surface as a <blackbody>, assume efficient heat redistribution and neglect internal heating. The <Stefan–Boltzmann law> and the <planetary surface energy balance> then give
$$
(1-a)S\pi R^2=4\pi R^2\beta T_E^4,\qquad \boxed{T_E=\left(\frac{(1-a)S}{4\beta}\right)^{1/4}}.
$$
For a nonuniform surface the same power balance instead defines an effective <planetary equilibrium temperature>, $T_E^4=\langle T^4\rangle$; its fourth root is not generally the arithmetic mean of the local temperatures. Also, area-averaging the <surface albedo> is an approximation to the properly insolation-weighted averaging. These are the simplifications implicit in the naked-planet calculation.

Take $R=6.371\times10^6\,\mathrm m$, so $A_E=4\pi R^2=5.10\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The changed area is $A_i=10^{12}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, giving
$$
\Delta a=(0.10-0.80)\frac{A_i}{A_E}=-1.372\times10^{-3},\qquad a_1=0.298628.
$$
The initial <planetary equilibrium temperature> is $T_0=254.8\,\mathrm K$. Thus
$$
\Delta T=T_0\left[\left(\frac{1-a_1}{1-0.3}\right)^{1/4}-1\right]\simeq \boxed{+0.125\,\mathrm K}.
$$
The differential estimate $\Delta T\simeq-T_0\Delta a/[4(1-a_0)]$ gives the same result. This is the global equilibrium response of the specified model, not the immediate local Arctic warming or the full atmospheric response.

The exceptional 2007 <Arctic sea ice decline> is best understood as a weather event acting on an already vulnerable ice cover, with several reinforcing pathways. Warmer air increases downward long-wave heating, lengthens the melt season and delays autumn freezing. Changes in snowfall and the development of <melt ponds> also change the surface energy balance. Air temperature alone, however, does not identify the source of the observed bottom melt: heat must reach the underside through the ocean.

Warmer Atlantic-origin water supplies a larger subsurface <ocean heat content> after entering through Fram Strait and the Barents Sea. Its influence on <sea ice> depends on vertical exchange, because a cold, relatively fresh <halocline> normally separates it from the ice. Erosion of that <halocline>, through altered freshwater distributions, wind-driven mixing or changes in circulation, weakens <stable density stratification> and can increase upward <ocean heat flux>. A warmer Atlantic layer without this exchange need not produce immediate extra bottom melt. Conversely, warmer Pacific inflow through Bering Strait introduces heat into the shallower western Arctic; advection through the Chukchi Sea provides another route to ice retreat and ocean heat storage.

The <Arctic Oscillation> changes winds, ice motion and export. Positive phases during earlier years helped remove old, thick ice through Fram Strait and left a younger, thinner pack. Summer 2007 also had a pronounced pressure contrast between the Beaufort region and the Eurasian Arctic, associated with the <Arctic dipole anomaly>. Its winds promoted ice displacement and warm-air transport. The summer circulation should not be described merely as a particular <Arctic Oscillation> index: the spatial pattern and the preconditioning from earlier years both matter.

Long-term thinning reduces the <latent heat of fusion> needed to remove the remaining ice. Replacement of <multi-year sea ice> by <first-year sea ice> therefore makes a given summer heat input more effective, while mobile thin ice is more readily redistributed or exported. These dynamical changes can expose open water early enough for substantial solar absorption. Water and <melt ponds> have lower <surface albedo> than snow-covered ice, so absorbed short-wave energy rises, increasing upper-ocean heat storage and bottom melt. This <ice-ocean albedo feedback> also delays refreezing. In the Beaufort Sea in 2007, enhanced solar heating of the newly exposed upper ocean was an especially important explanation of extreme bottom melt. Thus thinning and wind-driven opening can initiate retreat, while solar heating amplifies it; changes in Atlantic or Pacific heat supply can further reinforce the process where circulation permits.

A further positive <climate feedback> involves terrestrial carbon. Loss of the insulating ice cover permits greater autumn and winter transfer of stored ocean heat to the atmosphere. The resulting regional warming can increase thaw of coastal and inland <permafrost>. Microbial decomposition of previously frozen organic material then releases <carbon dioxide>, and oxygen-poor wet environments also release <methane>. These <greenhouse gases> strengthen the <greenhouse effect>, increasing global warming and further thaw. The <permafrost carbon feedback> provides the causal chain from summer ice retreat to an additional global influence. An <Arctic methane feedback> involving subsea sources is also possible, but the amount actually reaching the atmosphere depends on oxidation and transport; an abrupt, wholesale release is not a necessary consequence of summer ice loss.

For the bottom-melt estimate, use $\rho_i=917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and the given $L_f=336000\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. Assume the ice at the melting interface is already at its local freezing temperature; there is then no separate sensible-heating term. Per square metre the <latent heat> requirement is
$$
E=\rho_i L_f h_i=917\times336000\times2=6.16\times10^8\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}.
$$
July through September has $92$ days, so the mean <ocean heat flux> to the ice is
$$
\boxed{\overline Q=\frac{E}{92\times86400}=77.5\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\approx80\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}}.
$$
Using $\rho_i\simeq900\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ instead gives about $76\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, within the intended accuracy. Around the date of this examination, the estimated net anthropogenic <radiative forcing> since preindustrial times was about $1.6\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$; doubling <carbon dioxide> gives about $3.7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. The calculated bottom flux is tens of times larger. There is no energy-budget contradiction: it is a local, summer ocean-to-ice heat transfer, supplied by absorbed sunshine, stored heat and transport, whereas <radiative forcing> is a global perturbation to the radiation budget. Redistribution and local absorption can produce much larger regional fluxes than the global forcing.

For an already ice-free $100\,\mathrm m$ water column, take $\rho_w=1025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and liquid-water <specific heat capacity> $c_w\simeq4000\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. Assuming all the same energy remains in that uniformly mixed column,
$$
\rho_wc_wH\Delta T_w=E,\qquad \boxed{\Delta T_w\simeq1.50\,\mathrm K,\quad T_{w,\mathrm{end}}=T_{w,\mathrm{start}}+1.50\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C}.
$$
If the water initially lies near the seawater freezing point, about $-1.8\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C$, its final temperature is approximately $\boxed{-0.3\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C}$. An absolute final temperature requires that additional initial condition; the energy calculation itself determines only the temperature increase. The given ice <specific heat capacity> is not the appropriate value for heating liquid seawater.

A <steric sea level change> is caused by a change of water volume at fixed ocean mass. Warming usually lowers seawater <mass density>, producing <thermosteric sea level change>; freshening also lowers density and produces a halosteric contribution. Both depend on the temperature, pressure and <salinity> dependence of the equation of state. Regional freshening or warming can therefore change local sea level even when the global ocean mass has not increased.

An <eustatic sea level change> concerns the ocean water amount or basin capacity. On the coming-century timescale, the principal positive mass contributions are melting mountain <glaciers> and <ice caps>, and loss of grounded ice from Greenland and Antarctica, including iceberg discharge. Terrestrial water storage also matters: groundwater depletion transfers water to the ocean, whereas reservoir filling retains it on land. Snowfall accumulation can partly compensate ice loss. Basin changes associated with geology matter over longer periods, and vertical land motion changes locally measured relative sea level. Melting floating <sea ice> has already largely displaced its eventual meltwater volume; its direct contribution is small compared with transfer of grounded ice into the ocean. Small density and salinity corrections do not alter that distinction.

For scale, a contemporary budget available in 2010 gave the following approximate contributions for 1993–2003, in $\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|r}
\text{Thermal expansion}&1.6\\
\text{Glaciers and ice caps}&0.77\\
\text{Greenland ice sheet}&0.21\\
\text{Antarctic ice sheet}&0.21\\\hline
\text{Sum of these estimates}&2.8\\
\text{Observed global mean rise}&3.1
\end{array}
$$
These are period-specific estimates with appreciable uncertainties, particularly for Antarctica, rather than exact closure of the budget. For comparison, over 1961–2003 the observed rate was about $1.8\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ and the estimated <thermal expansion> contribution about $0.4\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$. The shorter interval need not define a permanent acceleration by itself, but the magnitudes show that thermal expansion and land-ice loss are both essential.

With continued warming, increasing <ocean heat content> sustains <thermosteric sea level change>, including after surface warming slows because the deep ocean equilibrates gradually. Increased <ice ablation> generally increases glacier runoff, although small glaciers eventually lose much of their available ice. Surface melt and changes in outlet-glacier flow can increase the ice-sheet contribution; ocean-driven loss of <ice shelves> can accelerate discharge of grounded ice. Greater snowfall provides some compensation but cannot be assumed to cancel these losses. Thus, under continued substantial <greenhouse gas> emissions, \b[the global rate is likely to rise above its historical few millimetres per year], with an increasing contribution from the great ice sheets. The precise century-scale increase depends on emissions, ocean heat uptake and <ice sheet dynamics>; a single fixed rate extrapolated from one decade is inadequate.

For the idealized mixed-layer calculation, the stated density response gives
$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}=-\frac{1}{5}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}},\qquad \alpha=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}=\frac{0.2}{1025}=1.95\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}.
$$
At fixed column mass, $\rho H=(\rho+\Delta\rho)(H+\Delta\eta)$, so to first order
$$
\Delta\eta=-\frac{H\Delta\rho}{\rho}=H\alpha\Delta T=200\times1.95\times10^{-4}\times1,\qquad \boxed{\Delta\eta\simeq0.039\,\mathrm m=3.9\,\mathrm{cm}}.
$$
This is only the <thermosteric sea level change> from the prescribed layer and warming. The much larger total observed rise since the nineteenth century does not, by itself, prove deeper warming: part of that rise is <eustatic sea level change>. After independently allowing for the mass contribution, a larger remaining steric signal would require warming below $200\,\mathrm m$, a different temperature distribution, or corrections to the simplified equation of state. Direct temperature profiles do independently establish subsurface ocean warming. It reaches depth through winter mixing and <open-ocean convection>, especially in the North Atlantic and parts of the Southern Ocean; through subduction of surface waters into the interior; and through advection and turbulent mixing. Formation and spreading of intermediate and deep waters transmit surface properties far beyond the immediate region of heat uptake. Warm surface water need not simply sink locally: cooling, freshwater effects and <density stratification> govern the actual pathways.

Using $R=6.371\times10^6\,\mathrm m$, the ocean area is $A_o=0.70(4\pi R^2)=3.57\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. A liquid runoff volume $\Delta V=600\,\mathrm{km^3}=6.00\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\dot\eta_{\rm runoff}=\frac{\Delta V}{A_o}=1.68\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,yr^{-1}}\simeq1.7\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}}.
$$
This is the contribution from that input, before allowing for other exchanges in the <global ocean freshwater budget>.

For the sea-ice dilution estimate, the ocean volume is $V_o=A_o(4000\,\mathrm m)=1.428\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^3}$. Let its initial mean <salinity> be $S_0$; the question does not specify it, so use $S_0\simeq35$ psu for a numerical estimate. Interpret the $600\,\mathrm{km^3}$ as liquid freshwater-equivalent melt, with $\rho_f\simeq1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Salt mass remains fixed while liquid water mass increases, giving
$$
S_1=\frac{S_0\rho_wV_o}{\rho_wV_o+\rho_f\Delta V},\qquad \Delta S\simeq-S_0\frac{\rho_f\Delta V}{\rho_wV_o}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dot S\simeq-1.43\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{psu\,yr^{-1}}\approx-1.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{psu\,yr^{-1}}}.
$$
Approximating the densities as equal gives $-1.47\times10^{-5}$ psu per year. If the quoted volume instead denotes literal solid-ice volume, use $\rho_i\Delta V$ for the added mass; with $\rho_i=917\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ the result is $-1.32\times10^{-5}$ psu per year. Either convention gives the same order of magnitude. Floating-ice melt can change mean ocean <salinity> even though its direct sea-level effect is small: displaced volume and dissolved salt concentration are different budgets.

An observed net freshening is a statement about the sum of all freshwater exchanges, not a separate measurement of every positive contribution. Let $M$ be liquid ocean mass, $R$ net freshwater runoff from land, $I$ net global sea-ice melt minus formation, and $P-E$ net precipitation minus evaporation, all expressed as masses per unit time. The <global ocean freshwater budget> and salt conservation give
$$
\dot M=R+I+P-E,\qquad \frac{\dot S}{S}=-\frac{R+I+P-E}{M}.
$$
The runoff term must include non-ice river input as well, or those other exchanges must already have been netted out. If the measured freshening equals that predicted from $I$ alone, positive runoff is still possible provided
$$
\boxed{R+P-E=0}
$$
or an equivalent compensating term exists in the complete budget. For instance, excess evaporation can remove water at the same rate at which land-ice runoff supplies it; compensating net ice formation elsewhere must also be included if $I$ was initially only the Arctic contribution. Gross seasonal melt cannot be compared with an annual global trend without subtracting refreezing. Such compensation also means that the runoff-only sea-level increment need not equal the total retained increment. If runoff is additional, sea-ice melt is already net and global, and every other freshwater term is zero, the two contributions must add: under those stronger assumptions the alleged equality would be inconsistent. \b[The apparent paradox is resolved by closing the complete freshwater and salt budgets.]

A <polynya> is a persistent or recurrent area of open water or relatively thin ice within an otherwise ice-covered region. Persistence of the opening does not mean absence of ice formation: a <latent-heat polynya> can continuously make ice and export it. In contrast, a <sensible-heat polynya> remains open because ocean heat inhibits freezing. Both processes may occur at the same site.

At an Antarctic coast, cold air descending from the elevated continent produces strong <katabatic winds>. Their offshore component drives <sea ice> away from a fixed coastline, fast-ice edge or grounded-iceberg barrier. Open water exposed to the cold atmosphere loses heat rapidly; <frazil ice> forms, aggregates into <pancake ice> or consolidated new ice, and is exported offshore. The opening continues as long as export balances production. Headlands, the shelter provided by <fast ice> and the direction of the prevailing winds determine the location and size. Terra Nova Bay is a characteristic coastal example. Such <latent-heat polynyas> are ice factories rather than permanently warm holes in the ice pack.

At an <ice shelf> front the fixed boundary is floating shelf ice instead of land. Offshore winds blowing across the shelf can similarly remove newly formed sea ice, so an ice-front <polynya> need not have a different thermodynamic mechanism from a coastal one; the Ross Ice Shelf front provides an important wind-driven example. Shelf-front geometry, ice tongues, grounded icebergs and changes in calving alter the shelter and the supply of drifting pack ice. Some ice-front openings also receive significant sensible heat from currents, tidal mixing or upwelling, and heat carried out of an ice-shelf cavity can affect freezing. A <sensible-heat polynya> requires a sufficiently warm ocean supply, whereas the latent-heat mechanism requires continuing removal of the ice that does form. Consequently a strict identification of all coastal openings with one mechanism and all ice-shelf openings with the other would be incorrect.

The oceanic roles are substantial. Rapid heat loss and freezing produce <brine rejection>, raising shelf-water <salinity> and <mass density>. The resulting convection and circulation can carry dense shelf water into ice-shelf cavities; melting beneath an <ice shelf> modifies it into colder, fresher <ice-shelf water>. Dense shelf and cavity-modified waters can subsequently descend the continental slope, mix with adjacent waters and contribute to <Antarctic bottom water>. The outcome depends on bathymetry and the density after mixing: not all rejected brine reaches the abyss.

These localized regions can therefore have a disproportionate effect on deep-ocean ventilation, oxygen supply, carbon transport and the large-scale <ocean circulation>. They are also intense sites of ocean-to-atmosphere heat and moisture transfer. Seasonal sunlight arriving in open water supports high biological production where nutrients are available, with consequences for marine food webs and carbon cycling. \b[Antarctic polynyas connect wind-driven ice export, concentrated ice production and dense-water formation; ice-shelf fronts add a floating boundary and possible cavity-related heat and freshwater exchanges.]

Off northeast Greenland, the <Northeast Water Polynya> is organized by the interaction of local currents and winds with coastal geometry and relatively fixed ice barriers. A regional northward coastal current encounters the Norske Øer shelf-ice barrier; divergence and shelter on its lee side help create the southern opening. This local northward current should not be replaced in the explanation by assuming uniform southward flow along the entire Greenland coast. In summer, weak winds, the current and the barrier can maintain the opening, while solar heating and the seasonal heat budget allow it to expand northward without continuous new-ice formation.

Winter conditions differ. Strong northerly winds can remove newly formed <sea ice> from the northern region around Ob Bank, with the Ob Bank ice barrier and Kronprins Christian Land controlling shelter and divergence. Large air–sea temperature differences then sustain rapid freezing and export. Thus the recurring geographical opening has a changing balance of dynamical export and seasonal thermodynamics; long-lived does not mean identical formation conditions throughout the year. Ice barriers themselves can also change between years.

The <North Water Polynya>, or <Pikialasorsuaq>, occupies the Smith Sound and northern Baffin Bay region between Greenland and the Canadian Arctic. An ice arch across the narrow upstream channel restricts the arrival of thick drifting Arctic ice. Northerly winds and predominantly southward transport through the channel remove newly formed ice into Baffin Bay. The combination of blocked upstream ice supply and continuing downstream export maintains low ice concentration despite strong winter cooling. The location and stability of the arch therefore matter as well as the local wind. Warm Atlantic-origin water carried along the Greenland side can provide a sensible-heat contribution, with tidal and other mixing helping deliver heat upward; the entire opening should not be attributed either exclusively to warm-water convection or exclusively to wind.

Both regions permit much greater winter ocean-to-atmosphere heat transfer than nearby thick ice, and both can concentrate ice formation and <brine rejection>. The production of saline shelf water affects local <density stratification> and exchanges with surrounding waters; it is not automatically equivalent to deep or abyssal water formation. In spring and summer the early availability of light and open water promotes biological production and supports rich marine food webs. These persistent or recurrent openings also provide habitat and access of particular importance to Arctic animals and coastal communities. \b[The key Arctic mechanisms are ice-barrier control of supply, current- and wind-driven divergence, and a seasonally varying contribution from ocean heat.]

In the <Pease polynya model>, take a straight fixed coast at $x=0$ and an offshore polynya edge at $x=L(t)$. Let consolidated pack ice move offshore at speed $U>0$, let new ice be produced throughout the opening at equivalent thickness rate $F>0$, and let the collected ice at the edge have a prescribed thickness $H$. All fluxes below are per unit length of coastline. The new-ice volume production is $FL$, while the consolidated-ice export relative to the moving edge is $H(U-\dot L)$. Neglect storage of drifting <frazil ice> inside the opening, so conservation gives
$$
H(U-\dot L)=FL,\qquad \boxed{\dot L=U-\frac FH L}.
$$
This is the simplest Pease closure: newly formed ice is collected at the edge rapidly enough that production can be equated to the instantaneous edge flux. A model retaining frazil storage would instead include the local balance $\partial_t h+\partial_x(uh)=F$, with frazil thickness $h$ and transport velocity $u$, together with an edge condition; it should not silently be identified with the instantaneous-collection approximation.

For constant $U,F,H$, the stable equilibrium and relaxation time are
$$
\boxed{L_\infty=\frac{HU}{F},\qquad \tau=\frac HF},\qquad L(t)=L_\infty+[L(0)-L_\infty]e^{-t/\tau}.
$$
In particular, an initially closed opening has $L(t)=L_\infty(1-e^{-t/\tau})$. It initially opens at speed $U$, then slows as its larger area produces more ice. It reaches $63\%$ of its ultimate width after $\tau$ and $95\%$ after $-\tau\log0.05\simeq3\tau$; the exact equilibrium is approached asymptotically, not reached at a finite opening time.

The production rate is determined by the energy budget. With $Q_{\rm surf}$ positive for net upward surface heat loss and $Q_o$ the heat supplied from the underlying ocean, water at its local freezing temperature gives
$$
F=\frac{Q_{\rm surf}-Q_o}{\rho_iL_f}.
$$
The surface loss includes sensible and latent turbulent transfers and net radiation. For example, the sensible component is approximately $\rho_ac_{p,a}C_HV(T_f-T_a)$, where $V$ is wind speed and $C_H$ an exchange coefficient. Ice motion is commonly parameterized as a small fraction of the offshore wind speed, but currents and the direction relative to the coast also matter. Thus colder air and stronger net cooling increase $F$ and narrow the opening; faster export increases $U$ and widens it; thicker collected ice increases both $L_\infty$ and $\tau$. Stronger winds can increase both export and heat loss, so the width's dependence on wind can be much weaker than the formula with artificially fixed $F$ would suggest.

The assumptions are steady spatially uniform forcing; a one-dimensional straight coast; fixed collection thickness and drift speed; no alongshore import of pack ice; water already at freezing rather than an appreciable sensible-heat reservoir; and negligible residence time of newly formed ice. Real coastlines, time-dependent winds, ocean currents, atmospheric adjustment over warm open water and variable ice accumulation can all violate these assumptions. The model describes a <latent-heat polynya>, not an opening maintained principally by a warm deep-ocean heat supply.

If air temperature rises while wind, $H$ and the other external parameters are unchanged, the smaller air–sea temperature difference reduces net heat loss and hence $F$. For $0<F'<F$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{L_\infty'}{L_\infty}=\frac{\tau'}{\tau}=\frac{F}{F'}>1}.
$$
The opening becomes wider and takes longer to attain the same fraction of its new equilibrium width. Its initial opening speed remains $U$; the longer equilibration follows from weaker ice production, rather than slower initial export. If warming eliminates net freezing, $F=0$ gives $L=L(0)+Ut$ in the simple kinematic closure, with no finite freezing-balanced width. A negative $F$ represents melt and is outside the positive-production Pease model.

For the shelf-water calculation, a $30$-day month at $0.05\,\mathrm{m\,day^{-1}}$ produces $h_i=1.5\,\mathrm m$ of ice. Assume a well-mixed $D=100\,\mathrm m$ shelf column, no compensating advection or precipitation, and fixed ice and water densities $\rho_i=917$ and $\rho_w=1025\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. The frozen fraction of its initial liquid mass is
$$
f=\frac{\rho_i h_i}{\rho_wD}=0.01342.
$$
Let the liquid initially have <salinity> $S_0$, and newly formed ice retain $rS$ with $r=0.3$ and $S$ the instantaneous ambient value. For remaining liquid mass $M$, freezing removes salt with the departing ice, so <conservation of salt with salt-retaining ice> gives
$$
d(MS)=rS\,dM,\qquad M\,dS=-(1-r)S\,dM,\qquad S_1=S_0(1-f)^{-(1-r)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta S=S_0[(1-0.01342)^{-0.7}-1]\simeq0.00950S_0}.
$$
An initial shelf-water salinity is needed for an absolute answer. Taking $S_0=34$ psu gives $\boxed{\Delta S\simeq0.32\,\mathrm{psu}}$. At the accuracy of an equal-density thickness calculation, $f\simeq1.5/100$ and $\Delta S\simeq0.7S_0f$, giving about $\boxed{0.36\,\mathrm{psu}}$ for $S_0=34$ psu. These estimates describe the same modest increase. Treating ice as completely fresh would overestimate <brine rejection> by omitting the factor $0.7$.

The resulting water is denser and can leave the shelf and intrude beneath fresher surface water. It is therefore a physically plausible contribution to the <cold halocline>. Nevertheless, this one-month column budget yields only a few tenths of a psu, and the salinity contrast across the Arctic halocline can be several psu. If water from a shelf occupying one-third of the area were spread through an equally deep layer over the whole ocean, the average increase would be only about $0.1$ psu. This illustration is not a volume budget for the actual deep Arctic: shelf area fraction is not ocean water-volume fraction, and the exported water can be concentrated within a relatively thin halocline layer.

Consequently \b[this calculation supports a shelf contribution but does not establish its dominance]. Repeated seasonal ice production, enhanced production in <polynyas>, shelf-water export, freshwater dilution, and the thickness and residence time of the receiving layer would all be needed to test dominance. The assumption that the shelf initially becomes ice-free limits the first month's salt production; it does not bound an entire winter's cumulative production if new ice is continually removed.

Another pathway is winter cooling and limited convection of Atlantic-origin water as it enters the Arctic and encounters the ice-covered ocean. Cooling and local freezing can make it near-freezing water without requiring that it first be formed throughout the Siberian shelf. A subsequent fresh surface cap limits further convection; the cooled water is advected into the basin beneath that cap, producing a cold, salinity-stratified lower halocline. Pacific-origin waters also contribute to halocline structure in the western Arctic. Both formation and maintenance depend on circulation and freshwater supply, not simply on the presence of freezing somewhere. The strong <stable density stratification> then limits upward transfer of heat from the deeper Atlantic layer to <sea ice>.

\b[The case for anthropogenic global warming rests on a physically consistent combination of forcing, energy storage and observed response, rather than on the claim that every unusual event has the same cause.] For this examination, the observational comparison is the evidence available around 2010, and the two forecast horizons are approximately 2060 and 2110. Separating the robust mechanisms from uncertain regional details is essential to a coherent <attribution of recent climate change>.

The <greenhouse effect> follows from <radiative transfer>. Sunlight supplies energy, while the Earth loses energy mainly through long-wave radiation. <Carbon dioxide>, <methane> and other <greenhouse gases> absorb and emit in parts of that outgoing spectrum. Increasing their concentrations alters the levels and temperatures from which radiation escapes, initially reducing the net loss of energy for a given surface state. The surface and atmosphere must then warm until outgoing radiation again balances the input. Strong absorption near the centres of some bands does not eliminate the influence of additional gas: absorption in the wings and the changing height of emission still matter. For scale, the approximate <carbon dioxide> forcing law $\Delta F\simeq5.35\log(C/C_0)\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ gives about $3.7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ for doubling. It describes a forcing, not the ultimate temperature response, which additionally involves <climate feedbacks> and ocean heat uptake.

Measured increases in atmospheric <carbon dioxide> are consistent with fossil-fuel combustion and changes in land use. The carbon <isotope> signature and atmospheric oxygen consumption provide independent evidence of the combustion source, while the ocean and land absorb part of the emitted carbon. <Methane> and other climatically active gases add to the forcing. Aerosols exert partially compensating effects, so net human forcing is smaller than the sum of the warming gases alone. Uncertainty in that compensation affects the quantitative rate of warming without removing the greenhouse mechanism.

A sustained rise in global temperature is accompanied by increased <ocean heat content>, glacier retreat and <sea level rise>. This combination is harder to explain as an artefact of a few surface thermometers than a temperature series in isolation: the ocean is a large energy reservoir, and thermal expansion and melting have independent observational consequences. Internal variations of <ocean circulation> can move heat between regions and alter warming over particular decades, but cannot indefinitely supply a positive global energy accumulation without a change in the energy budget. Solar changes and volcanic eruptions also influence climate, and must be included in attribution; they do not reproduce the full recent warming pattern and sustained heat gain. Volcanic aerosol episodes chiefly produce temporary cooling. Broad <troposphere> warming together with <stratosphere> cooling is another useful fingerprint: additional <greenhouse gases> and ozone changes have a different vertical response from simply increasing solar input. No one fingerprint is unique to one cause, but their joint agreement with greenhouse forcing strengthens the attribution.

Polar observations are especially informative because they connect atmosphere, ocean and ice. In the Arctic, reduced summer <sea ice> extent, thinning and loss of <multi-year sea ice> indicate a diminished ice reservoir. Warmer air, earlier melt and delayed freeze-up contribute, while wind and current changes redistribute ice and export thick floes. Pacific heat inflow and changes in Atlantic-origin waters supply oceanic routes to melting. The protection afforded by the <cold halocline> means that warmer Atlantic water does not automatically heat the ice: the intervening mixing and <density stratification> must be considered. The 2007 retreat illustrates how long-term thinning, anomalous winds and greater solar absorption can combine. It neither proves that weather variability has ceased nor provides a reason to ignore the longer-term energy imbalance.

Loss of grounded ice has a different global implication. Increased surface melt and runoff on Greenland, and changes in outlet-glacier discharge, transfer water from land to the ocean. In Antarctica, warming of the Peninsula and disintegration of some <ice shelves> demonstrate strong regional change. Warm ocean water reaching shelf cavities can thin floating shelves from below; loss of <ice-shelf buttressing> can then speed grounded-ice discharge. The floating shelf's disappearance is not itself equivalent to adding its whole volume to sea level. The important subsequent response is increased loss from grounded <ice sheets>. Increased snowfall can compensate some losses, so a mass budget must distinguish accumulation, melting and ice flow.

The two polar regions should not be assumed to behave identically. Around 2010, Antarctic sea-ice changes included strong regional contrasts and an overall satellite-era behaviour quite different from Arctic summer retreat. Winds, ozone-related atmospheric changes, freshwater effects and the circulation of the Southern Ocean influence the Antarctic pattern. A large cold continent surrounded by an ocean is physically different from an ocean surrounded by land, and vigorous Southern Ocean heat uptake can delay surface warming. A regional increase in Antarctic sea ice is therefore compatible with global ocean heat gain and losses from some grounded Antarctic ice; it still needs its own dynamical and thermodynamic explanation. Credible attribution accounts for these differences instead of requiring uniform warming everywhere.

Several mechanisms produce <polar amplification>. The <ice-albedo feedback> is the most direct: retreating snow and ice expose darker surfaces that absorb more sunlight, causing further warming and melt. Much of the extra energy over newly open Arctic water is stored during summer and released to the atmosphere in autumn and winter, when an ice-covered ocean would be better insulated. Consequently the largest near-surface atmospheric response need not occur in the season of greatest solar input. Sea-ice loss changes both reflection and the timing of ocean heat release.

The atmospheric vertical structure also matters. In the tropics, convective adjustment often produces appreciable warming aloft, which increases radiative cooling. Stable polar boundary layers can concentrate warming near the surface, producing a different <lapse-rate feedback> and less compensating cooling from upper layers. Furthermore, the idealized <Planck feedback> scales as $4\sigma T^3$: colder emitting regions have a smaller increase of blackbody radiation per kelvin, although a quantitative atmospheric comparison needs full <radiative transfer>. Water-vapour and cloud changes modify both short-wave and long-wave fluxes, with cloud effects depending on season and surface properties. Changes in poleward heat and moisture transport provide further coupling. Thus <polar amplification> has several interacting causes; it is not simply the same local ice-albedo process repeated throughout the year.

Polar change affects the rest of the planet. Grounded-ice loss contributes to <eustatic sea level change>, while ocean warming contributes to <thermosteric sea level change>; together they increase coastal flooding and erosion risks. Redistribution of ice and freshwater changes <density stratification> and dense-water formation, potentially altering the <Atlantic meridional overturning circulation> and other oceanic heat and carbon transports. A weakening is physically plausible under warming and freshening, but an immediate complete shutdown is not a necessary prediction. <Permafrost carbon feedback> can release additional <carbon dioxide> and <methane>, increasing warming outside the Arctic. Habitat loss, changing primary production and altered food webs affect marine ecosystems and people, while a longer open-water season changes navigation, coastal exposure and resource access. These impacts are not exhausted by a single global-mean temperature number.

Forecasts require an emissions scenario as well as physical understanding. On the roughly fifty-year horizon, continued emissions imply further ocean and atmospheric warming, a longer Arctic melt season, less late-summer ice and a greater fraction of young seasonal ice. Mountain glaciers generally continue to lose mass, and sea level continues rising through thermal expansion and land-ice loss. Variability can interrupt a regional trend for several years, and the exact first nearly ice-free Arctic summer cannot be fixed from the 2007 minimum. Ocean heat storage and slow ice-sheet adjustment also mean that some further change is committed even if emissions are reduced.

By approximately 2110, sustained high emissions would imply a substantially warmer world, potentially several degrees above early twenty-first-century global temperatures, with stronger Arctic warming and likely recurrent nearly ice-free late summers. This is a seasonal prediction, not a claim that the entire Arctic Ocean must be ice-free in winter. Losses of grounded ice and thermal expansion would produce larger cumulative <sea level rise>, plausibly measured in several decimetres and potentially more if ice dynamics accelerate. The largest uncertainty is not whether adding heat expands water, but the future emissions trajectory and the response of major <ice sheets>. Complete loss of Greenland on this century-long horizon is not required by that prediction. With strong mitigation, substantially smaller late-century warming and ice loss are possible, although sea level can continue to rise beyond stabilization of surface temperature.

\b[Anthropogenic forcing explains the broad warming signal; feedbacks amplify polar change, while emissions and ice dynamics govern future outcomes.] This distinction permits both a firm physical conclusion and honest uncertainty about dates, regional details and the magnitude of rapid ice-sheet change.
