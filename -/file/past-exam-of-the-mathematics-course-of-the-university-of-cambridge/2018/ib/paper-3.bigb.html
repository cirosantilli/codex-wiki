{title2=Analysis II}

= Solution
{parent=2F}

Put $I=\int_0^1f(t)\,dt$. If $I\ne0$, take the <inner product> with the <unit vector> $u=I/\|I\|_2$. Then
$$
\|I\|_2=u\mathbin{\cdot}I=\int_0^1u\mathbin{\cdot}f(t)\,dt\leq\int_0^1\|f(t)\|_2\,dt
$$
by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>; the case $I=0$ is immediate. This is the <triangle inequality for a vector-valued integral>.

Equality in the Euclidean argument requires $u\mathbin{\cdot}f(t)=\|f(t)\|_2$ for every $t$: the nonnegative continuous difference has integral zero. Equality in <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> then says $f(t)=\lambda(t)u$ with $\lambda(t)\geq0$. Conversely, if $f(t)=\lambda(t)v$ for one fixed $v\in\mathbb R^m$ and a continuous $\lambda\geq0$, then for **every** <norm>,
$$
\left\|\int_0^1f\right\|=\left(\int_0^1\lambda\right)\|v\|=\int_0^1\|f(t)\|\,dt.
$$
Thus these nonnegative-ray functions, including $f\equiv0$, are exactly the required functions.

{title2=Metric and Topological Spaces}

= Solution
{parent=3E}

A <topological space> is <connected> if it is not the union of two disjoint nonempty <open sets>, or equivalently has no nontrivial subset that is both an <open set> and a <closed set>.

Let $K_x$ be the union of all connected subspaces containing $x$. A union of <connected subsets> with a common point is connected: if a <separation of a topological space> existed, every member containing $x$ would lie wholly in the same side. Hence $K_x$ is connected and contains every connected subspace through $x$, so it is the <connected component> of $x$.

If $K_x\cap K_y\ne\varnothing$, their union is connected and maximality gives $K_x=K_y$. Otherwise they are disjoint. Since every $x$ belongs to $K_x$, the connected components **partition $X$**.

The <hyperbolic lines> $AB$ and $CD$ are both perpendicular to $BC$. They are therefore <ultraparallel hyperbolic lines>, and $BC$ is their unique <common perpendicular of ultraparallel hyperbolic lines>.

The common perpendicular realizes the minimum <hyperbolic distance> between two ultraparallel lines. = 6B
{parent=Paper 3}
{scope}
{title2=Variational Principles}

= Solution
{parent=6B}

The <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\ddot\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=0,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}(\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi)=0.
$$
The coordinate $\phi$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, so its <conjugate momentum>
$$
l=\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi
$$
is conserved. Substitution into the first equation gives
$$
\ddot\theta=\frac{l^2\cos\theta}{\sin^3\theta}.
$$
Multiplying by $2\dot\theta$ shows that
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(\dot\theta^2+\frac{l^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right)=0.
$$
Thus $h$ is conserved. Since $l^2/\sin^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2$, **$h$ is the squared speed on the unit sphere, equivalently twice the kinetic energy**.

= 8B
{parent=Paper 3}
{scope}
{title2=Quantum Mechanics}

= Solution
{parent=8B}

A <Hermitian operator> $A$ satisfies $\langle\varphi,A\psi\rangle=\langle A\varphi,\psi\rangle$ on its <operator domain>. Here
$$
H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x).
$$
Two <integration by parts> operations, together with the boundary conditions in the Hamiltonian domain, show that the kinetic term is Hermitian; multiplication by the real function $V$ is Hermitian as well. Thus $H$ is Hermitian.

For a time-independent operator $A$, the <Schrodinger equation> and its adjoint give the <Ehrenfest theorem>
$$
\frac d{dt}\langle A\rangle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,A]\rangle.
$$
Using the <canonical commutation relation> $[x,p]=i\hbar$,
$$
[H,x]=-\frac{i\hbar}{m}p,
\qquad
[H,p]=i\hbar V'(x).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\langle x\rangle=\frac1m\langle p\rangle,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle V'(x)\rangle.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=10E}

The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> states that every square matrix satisfies its <characteristic polynomial>: if $\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)$, then $\chi_A(A)=0$.

For the proof, the <adjugate identity> gives
$$
(tI-A)\operatorname{adj}(tI-A)=\chi_A(t)I.
$$
Write $\operatorname{adj}(tI-A)=B_0+B_1t+\cdots+B_{n-1}t^{n-1}$ and compare coefficients. Multiplying the resulting recurrence by successive powers of $A$ and summing telescopically yields $\chi_A(A)=0$.

By <polynomial division>, $p=q\chi_A+r$ with $\deg r<n$. Therefore $p(A)=r(A)$ by Cayley-Hamilton, while $p(\lambda)=r(\lambda)$ for every <eigenvalue> $\lambda$, since $\chi_A(\lambda)=0$.

For the displayed matrix,
$$
\chi_A(t)=(t-2)(t^2+1).
$$
The remainder of $t^{1000}$ modulo this polynomial has the form $at^2+c$, because its values at $i$ and $-i$ are both $1$. The equations $-a+c=1$ and $4a+c=2^{1000}$ give
$$
r(t)=\frac{2^{1000}-1}{5}t^2+\frac{2^{1000}+4}{5}.
$$
Since $(A^2)_{11}=3$, the requested entry is
$$
\boxed{(A^{1000})_{11}=\frac{4\cdot2^{1000}+1}{5}.}
$$

<Eisenstein criterion> states that if
$$
f(X)=a_nX^n+\cdots+a_0\in\mathbb Z[X]
$$
has a <prime number> $p$ such that $p\nmid a_n$, $p\mid a_i$ for every $i<n$, and $p^2\nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

After dividing out the <polynomial content>, suppose for contradiction that the resulting primitive polynomial factors as $f=gh$ with positive-degree $g,h\in\mathbb Z[X]$, using <Gauss lemma for polynomials>. Reduction modulo $p$ gives $\bar f=a_nX^n$, so in the <polynomial ring> $\mathbb F_p[X]$ both $\bar g$ and $\bar h$ are monomials. Because both factors have positive degree, their constant coefficients are divisible by $p$. Their product $a_0$ is then divisible by $p^2$, a contradiction.

The sequence $(f_n)$ <converges uniformly> to $f$ on $A$ when, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $N$ such that
$$
n\geq N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon
$$
for **every** $x\in A$.

The function $f$ is <uniformly continuous> when, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $x,y\in A$ and $\|x-y\|<\delta$ imply $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The same $\delta$ works throughout the domain.

This is precisely <uniform convergence> of $f_n$ to $f$.

= Solution

The closed bounded set $A\subset\mathbb R^m$ is <compact> by the <Heine-Borel theorem>. Choose a <convergent subsequence> $x_{n_j}\to x_0$. For fixed $N$ and sufficiently large $j$, monotonicity gives
$$

= Solution
{parent=14G}

For a coordinate curve $v=v_0$, the length between $u_1$ and $u_2$ is
$$
L_v(u_1,u_2)=\int_{u_1}^{u_2}\sqrt{E(u,v_0)}\,du,
$$
where $E$ is a coefficient of the <first fundamental form>. Thus $\partial E/\partial v=0$ makes the two $u$-directed sides of every coordinate rectangle equally long. Similarly,
$$
L_u(v_1,v_2)=\int_{v_1}^{v_2}\sqrt{G(u_0,v)}\,dv,
$$
so $\partial G/\partial u=0$ makes the two $v$-directed sides equally long.

Conversely, suppose every pair of opposite sides has equal length. Fix $v_0,v_1$. Equality of the first pair for every interval $[u_1,u_2]$ gives
$$
\int_{u_1}^{u_2}\left(\sqrt{E(u,v_0)}-\sqrt{E(u,v_1)}\right)du=0.
$$
The <fundamental theorem of calculus> implies that the continuous integrand vanishes, so $E$ is independent of $v$. The same argument shows that $G$ is independent of $u$. Hence the stated equal-length property is equivalent to **$E_v=G_u=0$**.

= 16B
{parent=Paper 3}

{title2=Quantum Mechanics}

= Solution
{parent=16B}

The <expectation value> is the mean outcome over many identically prepared measurements of the <observable>. For real $\lambda$, positivity of the <norm> in the <Hilbert space> gives
$$
0\leq\|(Q+i\lambda P)\psi\|^2
=\langle Q^2\rangle+\lambda^2\langle P^2\rangle+i\lambda\langle[Q,P]\rangle.
$$
This real quadratic has nonpositive <quadratic discriminant>, so
$$
\langle Q^2\rangle\langle P^2\rangle\geq\frac14|\langle[Q,P]\rangle|^2.
$$
Apply this to the centered observables $Q-\langle Q\rangle$ and $P-\langle P\rangle$ to obtain the <Robertson uncertainty principle>
$$
\boxed{\Delta Q\,\Delta P\geq\frac12|\langle[Q,P]\rangle|.}
$$

For the <quantum harmonic oscillator>, $[x,p]=i\hbar$ and
$$
E=\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle}{2}
\geq\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}+\frac{m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2}{2}.
$$
The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> and $\Delta x\Delta p\geq\hbar/2$ now give
$$
\boxed{E\geq\omega\Delta x\Delta p\geq\frac12\hbar\omega.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=17C}

Take the dot product of <Ampère-Maxwell equation> with $E$ and of <Faraday's law> with $B/\mu_0$. The <divergence of a cross product> identity
$$
\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}(E\times B)=B\mathbin{\cdot}(\nabla\times E)-E\mathbin{\cdot}(\nabla\times B)
$$
then gives the local <Poynting theorem>
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)+J\mathbin{\cdot}E+\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}\left(\frac1{\mu_0}E\times B\right)=0.
$$
Integration and the <divergence theorem> produce the stated equation. Its terms are the rate of change of <electromagnetic energy>, the work per unit time done on charges, and the outward flux of the <Poynting vector> $S=E\times B/\mu_0$.

Let the plate separation be $d$ and their radius be $R$, so $A=\pi R^2$. Between the plates,
$$
E=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0A}\,\hat z.
$$
For a circular <Amperian loop> of radius $r<R$, the <displacement current> in the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> gives
$$
B_\phi(r)=\frac{\mu_0\varepsilon_0r}{2}\dot E
=-\frac{\mu_0\lambda q r}{2A}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{S=\frac{\lambda q^2r}{2\varepsilon_0A^2}\,\hat r,}
$$
which points radially outward. Its flux through the cylindrical side is $\lambda q^2d/(\varepsilon_0A)$, exactly $-dU/dt$ for the stored <capacitor energy> $U=q^2d/(2\varepsilon_0A)$.

Let $u(t)$ be the signed air speed along the straw towards the bubble. <Conservation of mass in a pipe> for the incompressible plug flow gives
$$
\pi\varepsilon^2u=\frac d{dt}\left(\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\right),
\qquad
u=\frac{4a^2\dot a}{\varepsilon^2}.
$$
The unsteady <Bernoulli equation> along the straw, whose open end is at atmospheric pressure, gives
$$
p_{\rm in}-p_{\rm atm}=-\rho L\dot u=\frac{2\gamma}{a}
$$
by the <Young-Laplace equation>. Substitution yields
$$
a^3\ddot a+2a^2\dot a^2=-\frac{\gamma\varepsilon^2}{2\rho L}=-C.
$$

Set $w=a^2\dot a$. Since $a\dot w=-C$, the <chain rule> gives $w\,dw/da=-Ca$. The initial condition $\dot a(0)=0$ implies
$$
a^4\dot a^2=C(a_0^2-a^2),
\qquad
a^2\dot a=-\sqrt{C(a_0^2-a^2)}.
$$
Writing $a=a_0\sin\theta$ and integrating gives the implicit solution
$$
t=\frac{a_0^2}{2\sqrt C}\left[\frac\pi2-\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta\right],
\qquad \theta=\sin^{-1}(a/a_0).
$$
At disappearance $a=0$ and $\theta=0$, so
$$
\boxed{t_*=\frac{\pi a_0^2}{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\rho L}{2\gamma}\right)^{1/2}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=19D}

Repeated <integration by parts> in
$$
\frac1{k!}\int_a^x(x-\theta)^kf^{(k+1)}(\theta)\,d\theta
$$
produces the first $k+1$ terms of the <Taylor polynomial> and leaves $f(x)$, proving the <integral remainder in Taylor theorem>
$$
R(x)=\frac1{k!}\int_a^x(x-\theta)^kf^{(k+1)}(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$
Apply the linear functional $\lambda_k$ to this identity. It annihilates the Taylor polynomial, and commuting it with integration gives the <Peano kernel theorem>
$$
\lambda_k[f]=\frac1{k!}\int_a^b\lambda_k[(x-\theta)^+_k]f^{(k+1)}(\theta)\,d\theta.
$$

For the <midpoint quadrature rule> on $[0,1]$, $e[1]=e[x]=0$, so $k=1$. Its <Peano kernel> is
$$
K(\theta)=\int_0^1(x-\theta)_+\,dx-(1/2-\theta)_+
=\begin{cases}
\theta^2/2,&0\leq\theta\leq1/2,\\
(1-\theta)^2/2,&1/2\leq\theta\leq1.
\end{cases}
$$
For $f(x)=x^2$, the formula gives
$$
e[f]=\int_0^1K(\theta)f''(\theta)\,d\theta=2\int_0^1K(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac1{12},
$$
agreeing with $\int_0^1x^2dx-(1/2)^2=1/3-1/4$.

= Solution
{parent=20H}

Under the <null hypothesis> that treatment and recovery are independent, estimate each expected cell count by
$$
E_{ij}=\frac{(\text{row total})(\text{column total})}{N}
$$
and form the <Pearson chi-squared test of independence> statistic $\chi^2=\sum(O-E)^2/E$. For a $2\times2$ table its asymptotic null law is a <chi-squared distribution> with one <degree of freedom>. A second-order <Taylor expansion> of the <generalized likelihood-ratio test> statistic $-2\log\Lambda$ around the fitted counts gives this Pearson statistic, which explains its asymptotic null distribution.

Writing the four residuals over their fitted counts and simplifying gives
$$
\boxed{\chi^2=\frac{(ad-bc)^2(a+b+c+d)}{(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)}.}
$$
For $(a,b,c,d)=(50,10,15,5)$,
$$
\chi^2=\frac{80}{117}\approx0.684,
$$
whose one-degree-of-freedom <p-value> is about $0.41$. There is no evidence of an effect. One fitted count is only $3.75$, however, so the chi-squared approximation is questionable; <Fisher's exact test> would be preferable.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <Lagrange sufficiency theorem> says that if a feasible point $x^*$ and nonnegative multipliers satisfy complementary slackness and $x^*$ globally maximizes the <Lagrangian function in constrained optimization>, then $x^*$ globally solves the constrained maximization problem. Indeed, for every feasible $x$, the signs of the constraints give $f(x)\leq L(x)\leq L(x^*)=f(x^*)$. Concavity and the <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> are a common way to verify the global Lagrangian maximum.

For fixed $y$, A maximizes
$$
F(x,y)=\sum_i u_i\frac{x_i-y_i}{x_i+y_i}
=\sum_i u_i-2\sum_i\frac{u_iy_i}{x_i+y_i}
$$
subject to $x_i\geq0$ and $\sum_i x_i=X$. This is a <concave function> of $x$. On every active coordinate, the multiplier equation is
$$
\frac{2u_iy_i}{(x_i+y_i)^2}=\lambda.
$$
Thus A's optimal allocation is given by the <water-filling algorithm>:
$$
\boxed{x_i=\left(\sqrt{\frac{2u_iy_i}{\lambda}}-y_i\right)_+,}
$$
where $\lambda>0$ is chosen so that $\sum_i x_i=X$. Coordinates with zero expression receive no resource. This is a <water-filling algorithm>. The formula gives the optimum when every $y_i>0$. If $y_i=0$, any $x_i>0$ already gives A the whole market $u_i$ in that region, so A assigns such coordinates arbitrarily small positive amounts and applies water filling to the remaining budget; without a convention for $0/0$, the resulting value can be a supremum rather than an attained maximum.

= ii
{parent=21h}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $U=\sum_i u_i$. The candidate strategies are
$$
\boxed{x_i^*=X\frac{u_i}{U},\qquad y_i^*=Y\frac{u_i}{U}.}
$$
At this pair, A's partial derivatives $2u_iy_i/(x_i+y_i)^2$ are independent of $i$, as are the negatives of B's partial derivatives. Since $F$ is concave in $x$ for fixed $y$ and convex in $y$ for fixed $x$, the <Lagrange sufficiency theorem> proves
$$
F(x,y^*)\leq F(x^*,y^*)\leq F(x^*,y)
$$
for all feasible $x,y$. Hence $(x^*,y^*)$ is a <saddle point> and gives the optimal strategies in this <zero-sum game>. Its game value is
$$
\boxed{F(x^*,y^*)=U\frac{X-Y}{X+Y}.}
$$
