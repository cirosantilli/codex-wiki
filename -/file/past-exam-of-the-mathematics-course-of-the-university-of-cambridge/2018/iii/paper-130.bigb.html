\b[Hales–Jewett and the arithmetic-progression consequence.] For a finite <alphabet> $[m]$ and a positive integer $r$, the <Hales-Jewett theorem> gives an integer $N$ such that every $r$-color <finite coloring> of $[m]^N$ contains a <monochromatic> <combinatorial line>. A <combinatorial line> has a nonempty active coordinate set: all those coordinates receive the same freely varying letter, and the remaining coordinates are fixed.

We first prove the <alphabet insensitivity lemma>. Fix two letters $a,b\in[m]$, with $m\geq2$, and a desired dimension $d$. There is a <combinatorial subspace> of dimension $d$ on which changing $a$ to $b$ in any variable coordinate, with all other variable coordinates arbitrary, preserves the color.

For the construction, divide the coordinates into $d$ consecutive blocks. Choose their lengths from left to right by
$$
P_i=\sum_{j<i}L_j,\qquad L_i=r^{\,m^{P_i+d-i}}.
$$
Process the blocks from right to left. When processing block $i$, the later blocks already form a $(d-i)$-dimensional <combinatorial subspace>. Color each word in block $i$ by the vector of its original colors for every prefix word of length $P_i$ and every assignment to those later variables. There are $m^{P_i+d-i}$ contexts, and at most $r^{m^{P_i+d-i}}=L_i$ such color vectors.

The $L_i+1$ words $a^j b^{L_i-j}$, $0\leq j\leq L_i$, contain two, indexed by $u<v$, with the same vector by the <pigeonhole principle>. Fix the first $u$ coordinates to $a$, the coordinates after $v$ to $b$, and make coordinates $u+1,\ldots,v$ the active set of one new variable. Its values $a,b$ now have identical colors for every prefix and later-variable context. The active set is nonempty. Insensitivity already obtained in later blocks survives, because it held for every unrestricted earlier prefix. After all $d$ blocks have been processed, changing $a,b$ in any of the $d$ variables preserves the color. This proves the lemma.

Now induct on $m$. For $m=1$, a one-coordinate <combinatorial line> is automatically <monochromatic>. For $m\geq2$, let $d$ be a length supplied by the theorem for <alphabet> $[m-1]$ and $r$ colors. Apply the <alphabet insensitivity lemma> for the letters $m-1,m$ to obtain a $d$-dimensional <combinatorial subspace>. Restrict its variables to $[m-1]$. Induction gives a <monochromatic> <combinatorial line> in those variables. Its values $1,\ldots,m-1$ have the same color, and its value $m$ has the same color as its value $m-1$ by successively switching the active variables. Lifting this line to the original coordinates proves
$$
\boxed{\text{every finite coloring of a sufficiently long word space has a monochromatic line}.}
$$

For the <Van der Waerden theorem>, fix a progression length $k\geq2$ and use the <alphabet> $\{0,\ldots,k-1\}$. Pull back an $r$-color <finite coloring> of $\{1,\ldots,1+(k-1)N\}$ along
$$
(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\longmapsto1+\sum_{j=1}^N x_j.
$$
A <combinatorial line> with active set $S$ maps to the <arithmetic progression> $a,a+q,\ldots,a+(k-1)q$, where $a\geq1$ and its <common difference> is $q=|S|>0$. The <Hales-Jewett theorem> makes this progression <monochromatic>. Length one is trivial, so every <finite coloring> of the <positive integers> contains <monochromatic> <arithmetic progressions> of every finite length.

\b[The active-coordinate obstruction.] Color a word red when its first coordinate is $1$, and blue otherwise. Any <combinatorial line> whose active coordinate set contains $1$ has a red word at variable value $1$ and a blue word at variable value $2$, since $m>1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\{S\subseteq[n]:1\in S\}\text{ is not adequate}.}
$$
Here adequacy is the <adequate family of active coordinate sets> property; an active coordinate set is always nonempty.

Let $\mathcal F$ be an <intersecting family>. For a word $w\in[m]^n$, put $S(w)=\{i:w_i=1\}$. Give $w$ the red color if $S(w)$ contains some member of $\mathcal F$, and the blue color otherwise.

Consider any <combinatorial line> with active set $A\in\mathcal F$. At variable value $1$, $A\subseteq S(w)$, so that word is red. At variable value $2$, $S(w)\cap A=\varnothing$. No member of $\mathcal F$ can be contained in this $S(w)$, because every member meets $A$. Thus that word is blue. No such line is <monochromatic>, proving
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\text{ intersecting }\Longrightarrow\mathcal F\text{ not adequate}.}
$$
If $\mathcal F$ is empty there is no permitted active set at all, so it is also not an <adequate family of active coordinate sets>.

Let $N$ be a word length supplied by the <Hales-Jewett theorem> for <alphabet> $[m]$ and two colors. For $n\geq2N$, embed $[m]^N$ into $[m]^n$ by the <coordinate duplication for combinatorial lines> map
$$
(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\longmapsto(x_1,x_1,x_2,x_2,\ldots,x_N,x_N,1,\ldots,1).
$$
Pull back any two-color <finite coloring> to this word space. The <Hales-Jewett theorem> gives a <monochromatic> <combinatorial line> with nonempty active set $S$. Its image is a <combinatorial line> whose active set is
$$
\bigcup_{j\in S}\{2j-1,2j\},
$$
and hence has size $2|S|$, a positive even integer. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\{A\subseteq[n]:|A|\text{ is even}\}\text{ is adequate for all }n\geq2N.}
$$
The empty set's presence in the family causes no difficulty: the line just constructed has at least two active coordinates.

\b[Rado's criterion.] Let $A$ be a <matrix> over the <rational numbers>, with columns $a_1,\ldots,a_q$. A <partition regular matrix> is one for which every <finite coloring> of the <positive integers> gives a <monochromatic> positive vector $x\in\mathbb N^q$ with $Ax=0$; repeated coordinates are allowed. The <columns condition> is the existence of an ordered <set partition> $B_1,\ldots,B_s$ of $[q]$ into nonempty blocks such that
$$
\sum_{i\in B_1}a_i=0,\qquad
\sum_{i\in B_j}a_i\in\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{a_i:i\in B_1\cup\cdots\cup B_{j-1}\}\quad(j>1).
$$
<Rado's theorem> states
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is partition regular if and only if it satisfies the columns condition}.}
$$

\b[Necessity.] Clear denominators so the columns are integer vectors. For each pair of disjoint index sets $I,B$, with $B\ne\varnothing$, for which $v_B=\sum_{i\in B}a_i$ is outside the <linear span> of the $I$-columns, choose an integer <linear functional> $\phi_{I,B}$ that vanishes on those columns and satisfies $\phi_{I,B}(v_B)\ne0$. Such a functional exists by extending a <basis> of the span to one also containing $v_B$, defining a functional on that <basis>, and clearing its rational denominators.

There are only finitely many such pairs. Choose a <prime number> $p$ dividing none of these nonzero integer evaluations. If there are no such pairs, any prime will do. Use the <last nonzero digit coloring>: write $x=p^{v_p(x)}u$, with $p\nmid u$, and color $x$ by $u\bmod p\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}$. Since $A$ is a <partition regular matrix>, this coloring supplies a <monochromatic> positive solution, so all its unit parts have a common nonzero residue $d$.

Group the coordinates by increasing <p-adic valuation>, obtaining blocks $B_1,\ldots,B_s$ with valuations $e_1<\cdots<e_s$. Suppose for some $j$ the sum $v_{B_j}$ lay outside the <linear span> of the earlier columns, with earlier index set $I$. Apply $\phi_{I,B_j}$ to $Ax=0$. Earlier terms vanish exactly. Divide the remaining integer equation by $p^{e_j}$ and reduce modulo $p$. Later terms vanish modulo $p$, while the current block gives
$$
0\equiv d\,\phi_{I,B_j}(v_{B_j})\pmod p,
$$
contrary to the choice of $p$. Thus every block sum lies in the earlier <linear span>, and for the first block this means it is zero. This proves the <columns condition> by the <finite separating-functional proof of the columns condition>.

\b[Sufficiency.] Suppose the <columns condition> holds. For $j>1$, choose rational coefficients $\lambda_{ij}$, indexed by earlier columns, such that
$$
\sum_{i\in B_j}a_i+\sum_{i\in B_1\cup\cdots\cup B_{j-1}}\lambda_{ij}a_i=0.
$$
Take a positive integer $c$ clearing their denominators and $p\geq1$ bounding all $|c\lambda_{ij}|$. We use the allowed <monochromatic m-p-c set theorem> with $s$ levels. Its <M-p-c set> is the full set
$$
D=\bigcup_{h=1}^s\left\{cz_h+\sum_{j>h}\mu_jz_j:\mu_j\in\mathbb Z,\ |\mu_j|\leq p\right\},
$$
where the generators make every displayed expression positive. Positivity is part of this convention, rather than a rule that discards nonpositive expressions. For $i\in B_h$, set
$$
x_i=cz_h+\sum_{j>h}c\lambda_{ij}z_j.
$$
All these numbers lie in the <monochromatic> set $D$. The coefficient of $z_j$ in $Ax$ is
$$
c\left(\sum_{i\in B_j}a_i+\sum_{i\in B_1\cup\cdots\cup B_{j-1}}\lambda_{ij}a_i\right)=0,
$$
with the first coefficient zero by $\sum_{i\in B_1}a_i=0$. Hence $Ax=0$. This <Rado solution inside an m-p-c set> proves sufficiency.

\b[The equality-or-inequality dichotomy.] If every <finite coloring> gives a <monochromatic> solution with $x_1=x_2$, the first alternative holds. Otherwise fix a <finite coloring> $\chi$ admitting no such solution. Given any other <finite coloring> $\psi$, use the <refinement of a finite coloring> $n\mapsto(\chi(n),\psi(n))$. Since $A$ is a <partition regular matrix>, it supplies a solution <monochromatic> for both colorings. Its first two coordinates cannot be equal, by the defining property of $\chi$, so it is the required unequal solution for $\psi$. Thus one of the two universal alternatives always holds. This is the <partition regularity dichotomy under an extra constraint>; the alternatives are not asserted to be mutually exclusive.

\b[Both restrictions can fail.] The Schur <matrix>
$$
A=(1\ \ 1\ \ {-1})
$$
is a <partition regular matrix>: blocks $\{1,3\}$ and $\{2\}$ satisfy the <columns condition>. Color $n$ by $v_2(n)\bmod2$, using the <2-adic valuation>. An equal-coordinate solution would have $x_3=2x_1$, and $v_2(2x_1)=v_2(x_1)+1$, so its coordinates cannot be <monochromatic>. This exhibits failure of the equality restriction.

Conversely, $A=(1\ \ {-1})$ satisfies the <columns condition> in one block. Every solution has $x_1=x_2$, so even the constant one-color <finite coloring> admits no unequal-coordinate solution. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{equality can be impossible for one coloring, and inequality can be impossible for every coloring}.}
$$

\b[The ultrafilter space.] An <ultrafilter> $U$ on $\mathbb N$ is a proper <filter on a set>: it is upward closed, closed under finite intersections, excludes $\varnothing$, and contains exactly one of $A,A^c$ for each $A\subseteq\mathbb N$. The <Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers> is the set of these <ultrafilters>, with basic sets
$$
\widehat A=\{U:A\in U\}.
$$
They form a basis since $\widehat A\cap\widehat B=\widehat{A\cap B}$ and $\widehat{\mathbb N}=\beta\mathbb N$. Moreover $(\widehat A)^c=\widehat{A^c}$, so each is a <clopen set>. The <principal ultrafilter> at $n$ embeds $\mathbb N$ in this space; every nonempty $\widehat A$ contains such a point, so the embedded copy is <dense>.

Distinct <ultrafilters> $U,V$ have some $A\in U$ with $A\notin V$. Then $A^c\in V$, and $\widehat A,\widehat{A^c}$ are disjoint <open sets> separating them. Thus the space is <Hausdorff>.

For <compactness>, any <open set> cover can be refined to a cover by basic sets. If $\{\widehat{A_i}\}$ has no finite subcover, the sets $A_i^c$ have the <finite intersection property>: otherwise finitely many $A_i$ cover $\mathbb N$, and their basic sets cover every <ultrafilter>. Extend the proper <filter on a set> generated by these complements to an <ultrafilter> $U$. This extension follows from <Zorn lemma>: unions of chains of proper filters remain proper, and a maximal proper filter decides every $A$ versus $A^c$. Indeed, if adding $A$ made the filter improper, some existing filter member would be disjoint from $A$, forcing $A^c$ into the filter. This $U$ contains every $A_i^c$ and misses every $\widehat{A_i}$, a contradiction. Hence
$$
\boxed{\beta\mathbb N\text{ is compact and Hausdorff}.}
$$
This proves the <compact Hausdorff topology on ultrafilters> directly.

\b[An idempotent.] The <addition on the Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers> can be defined by
$$
A\in U+V\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\{n:A-n\in V\}\in U,\qquad A-n=\{k:n+k\in A\}.
$$
Associativity is supplied. For fixed $V$, the inverse image of $\widehat A$ under $U\mapsto U+V$ is $\widehat{\{n:A-n\in V\}}$, so this translation is <continuous> directly from the definition. This makes a <left-topological semigroup> in the convention used here.

We prove the <Ellis–Numakura lemma> in this case. Among nonempty compact <subsemigroups> choose one, $K$, minimal by inclusion. It exists by <Zorn lemma>: a descending chain has nonempty intersection by <compactness>, and that intersection is again a compact <subsemigroup>. For $a\in K$, the set $K+a$ is a nonempty compact <subsemigroup> contained in $K$. <Compactness> follows from <continuity>; closure under addition follows from
$$
(x+a)+(y+a)=(x+a+y)+a\in K+a.
$$
Minimality gives $K+a=K$. Hence there is $b\in K$ with $b+a=a$. The set
$$
L=\{x\in K:x+a=a\}
$$
is nonempty, compact, and a <subsemigroup>: if $x,y\in L$, then $(x+y)+a=x+(y+a)=x+a=a$. Thus minimality gives $L=K$, and in particular $a+a=a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{there is an idempotent ultrafilter in }\beta\mathbb N.}
$$
This is an <idempotent ultrafilter on the natural numbers>.

\b[Isolating the sequence terms.] Now use the hypothetical <convergent sequence> of distinct <ultrafilters>. For each $n$, choose $C_n\in U_n$ with $C_n^c\in U$, possible since $U_n\ne U$. Convergence implies $C_n^c\in U_m$ for all sufficiently large $m$. Thus only finitely many indices $m\ne n$ have $C_n\in U_m$. Call this finite set $E_n$.

For each $m\in E_n$, choose $D_{n,m}\in U_n$ with $D_{n,m}\notin U_m$, using distinctness. Set
$$
A_n=C_n\cap\bigcap_{m\in E_n}D_{n,m}.
$$
Finite-intersection closure puts $A_n$ in $U_n$. For $m\ne n$, either $C_n\notin U_m$ or one of the chosen $D_{n,m}$ is absent from $U_m$; upward closure then prevents $A_n$ from belonging to $U_m$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A_n\in U_m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m=n.}
$$

Only finite intersections were used; no countable-intersection closure of an <ultrafilter> is being assumed.

Let $E=\bigcup_{j\geq1}B_{2j}$. For every even index $2j$, $B_{2j}\subseteq E$ and $B_{2j}\in U_{2j}$ imply $E\in U_{2j}$. For every odd index $2j-1$, disjointness gives $B_{2j-1}\subseteq E^c$, so $E^c\in U_{2j-1}$ and $E\notin U_{2j-1}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{E\in U_n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\text{ is even}.}
$$
The limit <ultrafilter> $U$ contains either $E$ or $E^c$. Whichever it contains, the corresponding basic <clopen set> $\widehat E$ or $\widehat{E^c}$ is a <neighbourhood> of $U$, but infinitely many sequence terms lie outside it. This contradicts convergence. Therefore pairwise distinct <ultrafilters> cannot form a <convergent sequence> in $\beta\mathbb N$, the key step in <no nontrivial convergent sequences in the Stone-Čech compactification>.

Let $(V_n)$ be a <convergent sequence> in the <Stone-Čech compactification of the natural numbers>, with limit $V$. Each fixed $W\ne V$ occurs only finitely often: the <Hausdorff> property gives a <neighbourhood> of $V$ excluding $W$, and eventually the sequence is in that <neighbourhood>.

If $(V_n)$ were not eventually equal to $V$, infinitely many terms would differ from $V$. They could not take only finitely many distinct values, because each such value occurs only finitely often. Recursively select a <subsequence> of pairwise distinct terms, all different from $V$. It still converges to $V$, contradicting the preceding parts. Hence
$$
\boxed{V_n=V\text{ for all sufficiently large }n.}
$$
This establishes <no nontrivial convergent sequences in the Stone-Čech compactification> for arbitrary sequences, including ones with repetitions.

\b[The two Ramsey properties and the topologies.] Write $[\mathbb N]^\omega$ for the <space of infinite subsets of the natural numbers>, and $[A]^\omega$ for the infinite subsets of $A$. The <positive integers> are used throughout. A <Ramsey set of infinite subsets> $E$ has the property that every infinite $A$ contains an infinite $B$ for which $[B]^\omega$ lies wholly in $E$ or wholly in $E^c$.

For finite $s$ and infinite $A$ above $\max s$, let
$$
[s,A]=\{s\cup B:B\in[A]^\omega\},\qquad\max\varnothing=0.
$$
These are the basic <neighbourhoods> of the <Ellentuck topology>, denoted $*$ in the question. They form a basis: if $X$ belongs to two such sets, their stems are comparable <initial segments> of $X$; take the longer stem and the intersection of the two tails beyond that stem. This intersection contains the infinite remaining tail of $X$ and yields a basic set contained in both.

A <completely Ramsey set> $E$ allows such a homogeneous refinement $[s,B]$ inside every $[s,A]$, with $B\subseteq A$ infinite and the stem $s$ kept fixed. Taking $s=\varnothing$ shows that complete Ramseyness implies Ramseyness. The <ordinary topology on infinite subsets> $\tau$ has basic cylinders
$$
[s]=\{X:s\text{ is an initial segment of }X\}
=[s,\{n:n>\max s\}].
$$
It is the usual <product topology> on increasing enumerations and is coarser than the <Ellentuck topology>.

\b[Examples distinguishing the properties.] A non-Ramsey example uses the <axiom of choice>. Let $\kappa=2^{\aleph_0}$ and enumerate all infinite sets as $(A_\alpha)_{\alpha<\kappa}$. Each $[A_\alpha]^\omega$ has <cardinality> $\kappa$: an enumeration of $A_\alpha$ gives the upper bound, and choosing one element from each of infinitely many disjoint pairs gives an injection from all binary sequences for the lower bound.

By <transfinite recursion>, choose two distinct, previously unused elements $X_\alpha,Y_\alpha\in[A_\alpha]^\omega$ at each stage. Fewer than $\kappa$ elements have been used at stage $\alpha<\kappa$, so this is possible without any assumption on the <continuum hypothesis>. Put $R=\{X_\alpha:\alpha<\kappa\}$. All the $Y_\alpha$ remain outside $R$, since subsequent choices also avoid previously chosen elements. Every $[A]^\omega$ contains one $X_\alpha$ and one $Y_\alpha$, so $R$ is not a <Ramsey set of infinite subsets>. This is the <non-Ramsey set from transfinite selection> construction.

Perform the same construction on $T=\mathbb N\setminus\{1\}$, giving a non-Ramsey set $R_T\subseteq[T]^\omega$, and put
$$
E=\{\{1\}\cup X:X\in R_T\}.
$$
For any infinite $A$, the infinite set $B=A\setminus\{1\}$ has $[B]^\omega\cap E=\varnothing$, so $E$ is a <Ramsey set of infinite subsets>. But no $[\{1\},B]$ with infinite $B\subseteq T$ is homogeneous, because $[B]^\omega$ meets both $R_T$ and its complement. Therefore $E$ is not a <completely Ramsey set>.

\b[Every star-open set is completely Ramsey.] We give the full <fusion proof for open Ellentuck sets>, without quoting an infinite Ramsey theorem. Fix a star-<open set> $O$ and a basic set $[s,A]$. For finite $a\subseteq A$ and an infinite tail $D\subseteq A$ above $\max a$, say $D$ accepts $a$ if $[s\cup a,D]\subseteq O$; it rejects $a$ if no infinite subset of $D$ accepts $a$. Both properties persist under passing to infinite subsets. Every infinite tail can be thinned to decide $a$: take an accepting subset if one exists, and otherwise the original tail rejects it.

First thin $A$ to decide $a=\varnothing$. If it accepts, the required homogeneous neighborhood has already been found. Otherwise keep a rejecting tail. We now fuse to an infinite set $B$ deciding every finite $a\subseteq B$, where the decision for $a$ is made by $B/a=\{b\in B:b>\max a\}$.

To do this, choose $b_1<b_2<\cdots$ successively from an infinite current tail. After choosing $b_j$, thin the tail above $b_j$ to decide each of the finitely many subsets of $\{b_1,\ldots,b_j\}$, one after another, before choosing the next element. For any finite $a\subseteq B$, at the stage when its largest element is selected, the current tail decides $a$ and contains every later selected element. Heredity therefore gives a decision on $B/a$. For $a=\varnothing$, rejection persists from the initial tail.

If $B/a$ rejects $a$, only finitely many $n\in B/a$ can have $B/n$ accepting $a\cup\{n\}$. Otherwise let $D$ be the infinite set of those $n$. Every member of $[s\cup a,D]$ has a first new element $n$, and its remaining tail lies in $B/n$. It therefore belongs to $O$ by acceptance of $a\cup\{n\}$. This would make $D$ accept $a$, contradicting rejection.

Thin $B$ a second time to $C=\{c_1<c_2<\cdots\}$ so that every finite $a\subseteq C$ is rejected. Start with the rejected empty set. At a finite stage, all subsets of the selected elements are rejected. For each of these finitely many subsets, avoid its finitely many next elements that would give an accepted extension, and choose the next $c_j$ beyond all excluded elements. Since $B$ decides every finite extension, each new extension is rejected. Induction gives the asserted property of $C$.

We claim $[s,C]\cap O=\varnothing$. If $X\in[s,C]\cap O$, openness provides a basic set $[t,D]\subseteq O$ containing $X$. Extend $t$, if necessary, to an <initial segment> $u$ of $X$ containing $s$. Write $u=s\cup a$ with finite $a\subseteq C$. The infinite tail $X\setminus u$ lies in $B/a$ and
$$
[s\cup a,X\setminus u]\subseteq[t,D]\subseteq O.
$$
It is therefore an accepting subtail for $a$, contradicting rejection. We have found either an accepting neighborhood or a neighborhood disjoint from $O$. Thus
$$
\boxed{O\text{ star-open }\Longrightarrow O\text{ completely Ramsey}.}
$$

\b[Basic neighborhoods are clopen.] Consider $[s,A]$ and a point $X$ outside it. If $s$ is not an <initial segment> of $X$, take an <initial segment> $t$ of $X$ of length at least $|s|$; its ordinary cylinder $[t]$ cannot meet $[s,A]$. Otherwise some element of $X\setminus s$ is outside $A$; take $t$ through that element, and again $[t]$ is disjoint from $[s,A]$.

Thus the complement of $[s,A]$ is already <open> in the <ordinary topology on infinite subsets>, and hence in the finer <Ellentuck topology>. A basic <neighbourhood> is <open> by definition, so
$$
\boxed{[s,A]\text{ is star-clopen, and is also }\tau\text{-closed}.}
$$

The answer is \b[no]. Let $X_0=\mathbb N$, regarded as one point of the <space of infinite subsets of the natural numbers>, and put
$$
O=[\mathbb N]^\omega\setminus\{X_0\}
=\bigcup_{j\geq1}[\varnothing,\mathbb N\setminus\{j\}].
$$
The displayed union makes $O$ an <open set> in the <Ellentuck topology>. On the other hand, every basic <neighbourhood> $[s,A]$ of $X_0$ contains a different infinite set: keep $s$ and remove one element from its infinite remaining tail. This other point belongs to $O$. Therefore $X_0$ is in the <closure> of $O$ but not in $O$, so
$$
\boxed{O\text{ is star-open but not star-closed}.}
$$
The fact that basic <neighbourhoods> are <clopen sets> does not make arbitrary unions of them closed.

Take $A=\{2,4,6,\ldots\}$ and
$$
O=[\varnothing,A]=[A]^\omega.
$$
This is a nonempty basic <open set> in the <Ellentuck topology>. It is a <closed set> in the <ordinary topology on infinite subsets>: an infinite set outside $O$ contains an odd integer, and its finite <initial segment> through that integer gives an ordinary cylinder disjoint from $O$.

Its ordinary <interior> is empty. In any nonempty ordinary cylinder $[s]$, choose an odd integer $b>\max s$ and continue $s\cup\{b\}$ by any infinite increasing tail. The resulting point belongs to $[s]$ and lies outside $O$. Indeed the smaller cylinder $[s\cup\{b\}]$ is disjoint from $O$. Since $O$ is ordinary-closed with empty ordinary <interior>, it is a <nowhere dense set> for $\tau$. Hence
$$
\boxed{[2\mathbb N]^\omega\text{ is nonempty and star-open, but }\tau\text{-nowhere-dense}.}
$$
