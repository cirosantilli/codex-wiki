Write $\alpha=1-1/r$. The <Erdős-Stone theorem> states that, for a fixed <graph> $F$ with <chromatic number> $\chi(F)=r+1\geq2$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ex}(n,F)=\left(1-\frac1r+o(1)\right)\binom n2.}
$$
Here $\operatorname{ex}$ is the <extremal number>, and copies are <subgraphs>, not necessarily <induced subgraphs>. We write $K_a(b)$ for the <balanced complete multipartite blow-up> with $a$ classes of $b$ <vertices> each. All asymptotic errors below concern $n\to\infty$ with the forbidden <graphs> fixed.

\b[The stability subgraph.] We prove the <high-minimum-degree multipartite stability subgraph> assertion directly from the <Erdős-Stone theorem>. For $r=1$, the edgeless spanning <subgraph> suffices. Suppose $r\geq2$.

First repeatedly remove a <vertex> whose <degree of a vertex> is less than $(\alpha-\varepsilon_n)$ times the current order, where $\varepsilon_n\to0$ sufficiently slowly. The <Erdős-Stone theorem> gives, uniformly for every $K_{r+1}(t)$-free <subgraph> $J$ of order at most $n$,
$$
e(J)\leq\frac\alpha2|J|^2+\eta_n n^2,\qquad \eta_n\to0.
$$
Indeed, apply the asymptotic bound at large orders; the contribution from bounded or sufficiently small orders is negligible relative to $n^2$. If $m$ <vertices> survive, the removed <edges> number at most
$$
(\alpha-\varepsilon_n)\sum_{j=m+1}^n j
=\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon_n}{2}(n^2-m^2)+O(n).
$$
Comparing with $e(G_n)=\alpha n^2/2+o(n^2)$ shows that $\varepsilon_n(n^2-m^2)=o(\varepsilon_n n^2)$, provided $\varepsilon_n$ dominates $\eta_n$, the initial relative error, and $1/n$. Consequently $m=n-o(n)$. The surviving <induced subgraph> $G'$ satisfies $\delta(G')\geq(\alpha-o(1))m$ and $e(G')=\alpha m^2/2+o(m^2)$.

By the <Erdős-Stone theorem>, $G'$ contains $K_r(L)$ for some $L=L_n\to\infty$ as slowly as necessary. This follows because $\alpha$ exceeds the <extremal number> density for each fixed $K_r(L)$ by the positive gap $1/(r(r-1))$. Choose $L$ so slowly that $rL=o(m)$ and $\binom Lt^r=o(m)$, and denote its root classes by $A_1,\ldots,A_r$.

An outside <vertex> having at least $t$ <neighbours of a vertex> in every $A_i$ determines one choice of a $t$-subset in each $A_i$. At most $t-1$ outside <vertices> can determine any fixed choice, since otherwise these <vertices> and the chosen root sets form $K_{r+1}(t)$. Thus at most $(t-1)\binom Lt^r=o(m)$ outside <vertices> have this property. Exclude those <vertices> and the root classes; put each remaining <vertex> in a class $C_i$ for which it has fewer than $t$ <neighbours of a vertex> in $A_i$.

Write $\delta(G')\geq(\alpha-\gamma_n)m$, with $\gamma_n\to0$. Counting missing incidences with $A_i$ gives
$$
(L-t+1)|C_i|\leq\sum_{u\in A_i}(m-d(u))\leq L(1/r+\gamma_n)m.
$$
Since $\sum_i|C_i|=m-o(m)$, it follows that $|C_i|=m/r+o(m)$ for every $i$. The total missing incidences from all root <vertices> to assigned <vertices> are at most $(1+r\gamma_n)Lm$. At least $(L-t+1)(m-o(m))$ of them are incidences with a <vertex>'s own assigned root class. Hence only $o(Lm)$ missing incidences go to other root classes.

Choose $\rho_n\to0$ slowly and discard the $o(m)$ assigned <vertices> with more than $\rho_n L$ missing incidences to other root classes. Each remaining <vertex> of $C_i$ is adjacent to all but at most $\rho_n L$ <vertices> of every $A_j$, $j\ne i$. If its class contained a <complete bipartite graph> $K_{t,t}$, the $2t$ <vertices> of this copy would have at least $L-2t\rho_n L\geq t$ common <neighbours of a vertex> in each other root class. These sets extend the copy to $K_{r+1}(t)$, a contradiction. By the bipartite case of the <Erdős-Stone theorem>, the number of internal <edges> in each surviving class is $o(m^2)$.

The surviving <graph> still has $\alpha m^2/2+o(m^2)$ <edges>, whereas the total possible cross-class <edges>, with these balanced classes, is $\alpha m^2/2+o(m^2)$. Thus only $o(m^2)$ cross-class <edges> are missing. Discard the $o(m)$ <vertices> missing more than $\sigma_n m$ cross-class <edges>, with $\sigma_n\to0$ slowly, and delete all internal <edges>. The resulting $r$-partite <subgraph> $H$ has classes $H_i$ of size $n/r+o(n)$ and
$$
\boxed{|H|=n-o(n),\qquad \delta(H)=(1-1/r+o(1))n.}
$$
For the lower bound, every surviving <vertex> misses only $o(n)$ <vertices> outside its class; the upper bound follows from the class sizes. This also records the uniform cross-class error that we need next.

\b[Minimum degree in an extremal graph.] Fix $F$ with $\chi(F)=r+1$. Choose $t$ large enough that $F$ embeds in $K_{r+1}(t)$. An extremal $F$-free <graph> $G$ is therefore $K_{r+1}(t)$-free, and its <edge> count has the required asymptotics by the <Erdős-Stone theorem>. For $r\geq2$ take $H$ as above.

For any <vertex> $v$, remove $v$ and introduce a new <vertex> $v^*$ whose <vertex neighbourhood> consists precisely of the <vertices> in $H_2\cup\cdots\cup H_r$ other than $v$. The new <degree of a vertex> is $\alpha n-o(n)$. This new <graph> remains $F$-free. Indeed, a new copy of $F$ must use $v^*$, and all its <neighbours of a vertex> in that copy lie in the other classes of $H$. There are only boundedly many of them. Each misses only $o(n)$ <vertices> in $H_1$, so an unused <vertex> $w\in H_1$ is adjacent to all of them. Replacing $v^*$ by $w$ would give $F$ in $G-v$, which is impossible.

Extremality now forces $d_G(v)\geq\alpha n-o(n)$, uniformly in $v$. The upper bound for the <minimum degree of a graph> follows from $\delta(G)\leq2e(G)/n$. For $r=1$, $e(G)=o(n^2)$ already gives $0\leq\delta(G)=o(n)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\delta(G)=(1-1/r+o(1))n.}
$$
This is the <minimum degree of an extremal forbidden-subgraph graph> principle.

\b[The exact extremal graph for disjoint cliques.] Define
$$
J_s(n)=K_{s-1}+T_r(n-s+1),\qquad
b_s(n)=\binom{s-1}{2}+(s-1)(n-s+1)+e(T_r(n-s+1)).
$$
The plus sign denotes the <join of graphs>. The <Turan graph> contains no $K_{r+1}$, so every such <clique> in $J_s(n)$ uses a <vertex> of $K_{s-1}$. Therefore $J_s(n)$ contains no $s$ vertex-disjoint such <cliques>, and the <extremal number> is at least $b_s(n)$.

Induct on $s$, with $s=1$ supplied by the <Turan theorem>, including its equality characterization. Let $s\geq2$ and let $G$ be extremal for $sK_{r+1}$. The preceding results give $\delta(G)\geq\alpha n-o(n)$ and the $r$-partite <subgraph> $H$. Keep its classes $H_i$ fixed and assign each of the remaining $o(n)$ <vertices> to a class in which it has fewest <neighbours of a vertex> in $H$. Write the resulting classes as $C_i$; all have size $n/r+o(n)$. Choose a positive $\tau_n\to0$ so slowly that $\tau_n n$ dominates the outside count, every cross-class error in $H$, and every error in the <minimum degree of a graph> bound.

Suppose some <vertex> $x\in C_i$ has at least $\tau_n n$ internal <neighbours of a vertex>. If $x\notin H$, the assignment rule shows that $x$ has at least $\tau_n n-o(n)$ <neighbours of a vertex> in every $H_j$. If $x\in H_i$, it has this many in $H_i$ and $n/r-o(n)$ in the other $H_j$. If $G-x$ contained $s-1$ disjoint $K_{r+1}$, we could choose one <vertex> in each $N(x)\cap H_j$, successively avoiding those copies and the $o(n)$ missing cross-neighbours of the already chosen <vertices>. This produces a further $K_{r+1}$ through $x$. For $r=1$, this just chooses an unused neighbour of $x$. Hence $G-x$ is $(s-1)K_{r+1}$-free, and induction gives
$$
e(G)\leq b_{s-1}(n-1)+(n-1)=b_s(n).
$$
Since the candidate attains $b_s(n)$, equality holds throughout: $x$ is a <universal vertex> and $G-x$ is the inductively unique extremal <graph>. Thus $G$ is isomorphic to $J_s(n)$.

It remains to show that this case must occur. Otherwise all internal <vertex degrees> are less than $\tau_n n=o(n)$. Together with the lower bound on $\delta(G)$ and $|C_i|=n/r+o(n)$, this implies that every <vertex> misses only $o(n)$ <vertices> outside its own class. If some $C_i$ contains a <matching in a graph> of $s$ <edges>, extend its <edges> one by one to $K_{r+1}$, choosing one <vertex> from each other class. At every step only $o(n)$ cross-neighbours and boundedly many used <vertices> are excluded. This gives $s$ disjoint forbidden <cliques>, a contradiction.

A <maximal matching> in each class therefore has at most $s-1$ <edges>, and its at most $2(s-1)$ endpoints form a <vertex cover> of the internal <edges>. Their internal <vertex degrees> are all $o(n)$, so the total internal <edge> count is $o(n)$. The cross-class <edge> count is at most $e(T_r(n))$, whence
$$
e(G)\leq e(T_r(n))+o(n).
$$
But the balanced part sizes of the <Turan graph> give
$$
b_s(n)-e(T_r(n))=\frac{s-1}{r}n+O(1),
$$
contradicting extremality for $s\geq2$. This argument also works for $r=1$. Consequently, for all sufficiently large $n$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ex}(n,sK_{r+1})=b_s(n),\quad G\cong K_{s-1}+T_r(n-s+1).}
$$
The uniqueness is up to <isomorphic graphs>, as usual for an <extremal graph for disjoint cliques>.

\b[Symmetrization for an arbitrary real coefficient.] Among <graphs> maximizing $f(G)=e(G)-c k_3(G)$, choose one maximizing
$$
Q(G)=\sum_{x\in V(G)}d(x)^2.
$$
For a <vertex> $x$, put $L(x)=d(x)-c e(G[N(x)])$. This is the contribution of <edges> and <triangles in a graph> containing $x$. If $u,v$ are nonadjacent, <Zykov symmetrization> replacing $u$ by a clone of $v$ changes $f$ by $L(v)-L(u)$; the opposite replacement changes it by $L(u)-L(v)$. Maximality implies $L(u)=L(v)$, so both changes preserve $f$.

Let $S=N(v)\setminus N(u)$ and $T=N(u)\setminus N(v)$. In the first replacement the <vertex degrees> in $S$ increase by one and those in $T$ decrease by one; in the opposite replacement these changes reverse. The changes to $d(u)^2+d(v)^2$ cancel when the two replacements are added. Since $(d+1)^2+(d-1)^2-2d^2=2$,
$$
\Delta_{u\gets v}Q+\Delta_{v\gets u}Q=2(|S|+|T|).
$$
If the two <vertex neighbourhoods> differ, one replacement increases $Q$, a contradiction. Thus every pair of nonadjacent <vertices> has the same <vertex neighbourhood>. Nonadjacency, with equality allowed, is an <equivalence relation>: if $u$ and $v$ are nonadjacent and $v$ and $w$ are nonadjacent, their identical <vertex neighbourhoods> forbid $uw$ as well. Its classes are <independent sets> and every cross-class <edge> is present. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{some maximizer of }e(G)-c k_3(G)\text{ is complete multipartite}.}
$$
This <edge-triangle symmetrization> works for either sign of $c$.

\b[The exact supporting line.] For $n\geq3$, write
$$
e_2=e(T_2(n)),\quad e_3=e(T_3(n)),\quad t_3=k_3(T_3(n)),\quad c_*=(e_3-e_2)/t_3.
$$
We prove the <triangle support line between bipartite and tripartite Turan graphs> by maximizing $e-c_*k_3$. First $c_*\geq2/n$. For $n=3,4,5$, its values are respectively $1,1/2,1/2$. For $n\geq6$, the exact balancing of the <Turan graph> gives $e_3\geq n^2/3-1/3$, while $e_2\leq n^2/4$ and the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> gives $t_3\leq n^3/27$. Hence
$$
n(e_3-e_2)-2t_3\geq\frac{n^3}{108}-\frac n3\geq0.
$$

By <edge-triangle symmetrization> a maximizing <graph> can be taken <complete multipartite graph>; among such maximizers use one with the fewest nonempty parts. If there are at least four parts, let $a,b$ be the two smallest sizes. Then $a+b\leq n/2$. Merging them loses $ab$ <edges> and $ab(n-a-b)$ <triangles in a graph>, so the objective changes by
$$
ab\{c_*(n-a-b)-1\}\geq0.
$$
This contradicts the choice of the number of parts. Thus at most three parts remain.

For three part sizes $a\geq b\geq d>0$, the objective is
$$
ad(1-c_*b)+b(a+d).
$$
If $1-c_*b\leq0$, merging the parts of sizes $a,d$ does not decrease it, again contradicting minimality. If $1-c_*b>0$ and $a-d\geq2$, transferring one <vertex> from the largest to the smallest part raises $ad$ by $a-d-1>0$ and strictly increases the objective. Thus a three-part maximizer has all sizes differing by at most one, and is $T_3(n)$. A maximizer with at most two parts has objective at most $e_2$, since it has no <triangles in a graph> and the <Mantel theorem> bounds its <edges>. Finally,
$$
e(T_3(n))-c_*k_3(T_3(n))=e_2.
$$
Therefore every <graph> satisfies $e(G)-c_*k_3(G)\leq e_2$. At the prescribed <edge> count this gives
$$
\boxed{k_3(G)\geq\theta k_3(T_3(n)).}
$$
For $n\leq2$, the conclusion is simply the nonnegative lower bound zero. All rounding in the <Turan graphs> has been retained.

\b[One edge above the bipartite threshold.] Write $n=2m$. We prove the <triangle lower bound one edge above the Mantel threshold> by induction on $m\geq2$, allowing at least $m^2+1$ <edges>. For $m=2$, five <edges> on four <vertices> give two <triangles in a graph>, and adding <edges> preserves this bound.

We will also use the following consequence of an inductive bound: on $2a$ <vertices>, at least $a^2+q$ <edges>, for an integer $q\geq1$, force at least $a+q-1$ <triangles in a graph>. Delete surplus <edges> first to leave exactly $a^2+q$. Repeatedly delete an <edge> in a <triangle in a graph> until $a^2+1$ <edges> remain. The <Mantel theorem> guarantees such a <triangle in a graph> at every step; each deletion destroys at least one distinct <triangle in a graph>. Apply the inductive bound to the remaining <graph>.

Now take exactly $m^2+1$ <edges>. If every <edge> lies in a <triangle in a graph>, the <edge-triangle incidence bound> gives $3k_3(G)\geq m^2+1$. This is at least $3m$ for $m\geq3$; the base case was handled separately.

Otherwise choose an <edge> $uv$ in no <triangle in a graph>. Its endpoints have disjoint <vertex neighbourhoods>, so $d(u)+d(v)\leq2m$. Deleting $u,v$ removes exactly $d(u)+d(v)-1$ <edges> and leaves at least $(m-1)^2+1$ <edges>. If $d(u)+d(v)\leq2m-1$, it leaves at least $(m-1)^2+2$ <edges>, and the strengthened inductive bound gives at least $m$ <triangles in a graph> already.

If $d(u)+d(v)=2m$, induction gives at least $m-1$ <triangles in a graph> after deletion. There must be an additional <triangle in a graph> containing $u$ or $v$. Otherwise both $N(u)$ and $N(v)$ are <independent sets>; since they are disjoint and cover all <vertices>, $G$ would be <bipartite graph>, contradicting $e(G)>m^2$ by the <Mantel theorem>. Hence
$$
\boxed{k_3(G)\geq m=n/2.}
$$
The bound is sharp: add one internal <edge> to one class of the <complete bipartite graph> $K_{m,m}$. Its <triangles in a graph> are precisely that <edge> together with each of the $m$ <vertices> in the other class.

\b[Statement and definitions.] For disjoint nonempty sets $A,B$ of <vertices>, their <edge density of a bipartite graph> is $d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(|A||B|)$. They form a <regular pair of vertex sets>, or an $\varepsilon$-uniform pair, if
$$
|d(X,Y)-d(A,B)|\leq\varepsilon
$$
whenever $X\subseteq A$, $Y\subseteq B$, $|X|\geq\varepsilon|A|$ and $|Y|\geq\varepsilon|B|$.

The <Szemerédi regularity lemma> states that for every $\varepsilon>0$ and positive integer $m_0$, there are integers $M,n_0$ such that every <graph> of order $n\geq n_0$ has a <set partition>
$$
V(G)=V_0\sqcup V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_k
$$
with $m_0\leq k\leq M$, $|V_0|\leq\varepsilon n$, equal nonzero sizes $|V_1|=\cdots=|V_k|$, and at most $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered pairs $(V_i,V_j)$, $1\leq i<j\leq k$, that fail to be $\varepsilon$-uniform. It is enough to prove this for $0<\varepsilon\leq1/2$, since decreasing the parameter strengthens the conclusion.

\b[Energy increment.] For the proof, regard each exceptional <vertex> as a singleton part and use the <equitable regularity energy>
$$
q(\mathcal P)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{A,B\in\mathcal P}|A||B|d(A,B)^2.
$$
The sum is over ordered pairs, including diagonal pairs; for those pairs define $d$ as the mean adjacency indicator on $A\times B$. Thus $0\leq q\leq1$. Under a refinement, the subrectangle densities have the original density as their weighted mean. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, equivalently the nonnegativity of their weighted <variance>, gives $q(\mathcal P')\geq q(\mathcal P)$.

Suppose there are more than $\varepsilon k^2$ irregular pairs, with main cell size $m$. For each such pair choose witnesses $X\subseteq V_i$, $Y\subseteq V_j$ of sizes at least $\varepsilon m$ and with $|d(X,Y)-d(V_i,V_j)|>\varepsilon$. Refine every main cell by all its witness subsets. Each splits into at most $2^{k-1}$ atoms, and we may use the larger bound $2^k$.

For an ordered irregular rectangle, write its refined densities as $d_{ab}$ and relative areas as $w_{ab}$. The energy gain on that rectangle is
$$
\frac{m^2}{n^2}\sum_{a,b}w_{ab}(d_{ab}-d(V_i,V_j))^2.
$$
The witness rectangle has relative area at least $\varepsilon^2$. Applying the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> to the subrectangles it contains shows that this expression is greater than $\varepsilon^4m^2/n^2$. There are two orientations of each irregular pair. Since $km=n-|V_0|\geq n/2$, the total gain is greater than
$$
2\varepsilon^5\frac{k^2m^2}{n^2}\geq\varepsilon^5/2.
$$

\b[Restoring equal sizes without losing energy.] Put $\ell=\lfloor m/4^k\rfloor$, assuming $m\geq2\cdot4^k$. Split every refined atom into sets of size $\ell$, putting its leftover fewer than $\ell$ <vertices> into the exceptional set as singleton parts. This is a further refinement, so the energy cannot decrease. The number of new exceptional <vertices> is at most
$$
k2^k\ell\leq km/2^k\leq n/2^k.
$$
The number $k'$ of new main cells satisfies
$$
k\leq k'\leq\frac{km}{\ell}\leq2k4^k.
$$
For the first inequality, in each old cell the leftovers have total size less than $2^k\ell<m$, so at least one full chunk survives. All previous exceptional singleton parts are kept. Treating leftovers as singleton parts is what makes the energy increment survive equitabilization exactly.

Take $S=\lceil2\varepsilon^{-5}\rceil+1$ and choose $k_0\geq m_0$ so large that $S2^{-k_0}\leq\varepsilon/2$. Start with $k_0$ equal main cells, with fewer than $k_0$ exceptional <vertices>. Define a finite bound $M$ by iterating $x\mapsto2x4^x$ a total of $S$ times, starting from $k_0$. Choose $n_0$ sufficiently large that $k_0\leq\varepsilon n/2$ and $n/(2M)\geq2\cdot4^M$ whenever $n\geq n_0$.

At each of the first $S$ refinements, the exceptional set has size at most
$$
k_0+S n2^{-k_0}\leq\varepsilon n,
$$
the cell count is between $k_0$ and $M$, and $m\geq n/(2M)$, so all the chunk sizes above are valid. But $S$ unsuccessful refinements would raise $q$ by more than $S\varepsilon^5/2>1$, which is impossible. Thus the procedure terminates at a partition satisfying the <Szemerédi regularity lemma>.

\b[Three linearly large uniform sets.] Apply the <Szemerédi regularity lemma> with
$$
\varepsilon=10^{-5},\qquad m_0=1000,\qquad d_0=10^{-3}.
$$
For $n\geq n_0$, delete all <edges> touching $V_0$, all internal main-cell <edges>, all <edges> of irregular pairs, and all <edges> of regular pairs whose <edge density of a bipartite graph> is less than $d_0$. The respective losses are bounded by
$$
\varepsilon n^2,\qquad \frac{n^2}{2m_0},\qquad \varepsilon n^2,\qquad \frac{d_0n^2}{2}.
$$
Thus the total loss is at most $0.00102n^2$, and the remaining <graph> has at least
$$
(0.255-0.00102)n^2=0.25398n^2>n^2/4
$$
<edges>. Form the <reduced graph of a regularity partition> $R$, joining two main cells precisely when they are $\varepsilon$-uniform and have <edge density of a bipartite graph> at least $d_0$. If their common size is $m$, the remaining <edge> count is at most $e(R)m^2$. Since $km\leq n$, we obtain $e(R)>k^2/4$. The <Mantel theorem> gives a <triangle in a graph> in $R$. Its three cells $U_1,U_2,U_3$ have the required pairwise density and are $10^{-3}$-uniform: an $\varepsilon$-uniform pair is uniform for any larger parameter.

Each selected cell has size $m\geq(1-\varepsilon)n/M$. Choose
$$
c=\min\left\{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2M},\frac1{n_0}\right\}>0.
$$
For $n<n_0$, the <Mantel theorem> gives a <triangle in a graph> in the original <graph>, since its <edge> count exceeds $n^2/4$; take its three singleton <vertices>. Their pairs have density one and are uniform for every positive parameter, and $1>cn$. Therefore in all cases
$$
\boxed{|U_i|>cn,\quad (U_i,U_j)\text{ is }10^{-3}\text{-uniform},\quad d(U_i,U_j)\geq10^{-3}.}
$$
The constant is absolute; the energy proof gives a very large bound for $M$, which does not affect its positivity.

Use natural logarithms. A $K_t$ <graph minor> is represented by $t$ disjoint nonempty <branch sets of a graph minor>, each inducing a <connected graph>, with an <edge> between every pair. The <complete graph minor density threshold> is defined over nonempty finite <graphs> by
$$
c(t)=\inf\{c:\text{for every nonempty finite }G,\ e(G)\geq c|G|\Longrightarrow G\succ K_t\}.
$$
It uses $e(G)/|G|$, half the average <degree of a vertex>; the nonempty convention excludes the vacuous density inequality for the empty <graph>. The PDF has the intended definition with a universal implication over $G$; the TeX transcription corrupts that definition.

\b[Probabilistic lower bound.] Put $N=\lfloor t\sqrt{\log t}/4\rfloor$ and take the <binomial random graph> $G(N,1/2)$. Its <edge> count has mean $N(N-1)/4$. The <Chernoff bound> implies
$$
\mathbb P(e(G)\geq N^2/8)\longrightarrow1.
$$
We show that the <probability> of a $K_t$ <graph minor> tends to zero. Ignore connectedness, which only enlarges the set of possible models, and assign each of the $N$ <vertices> one of $t$ branch labels or an unused label. There are at most $(t+1)^N$ assignments.

For any assignment with $t$ nonempty branch sets, at least $t/2$ of them have size at most $2N/t\leq\sqrt{\log t}/2$. Between any two such small sets $A,B$, the <probability> of no <edge> is
$$
2^{-|A||B|}\geq \exp\left(-\frac{\log2}{4}\log t\right)\geq t^{-1/4}.
$$
The absence or presence of <edges> between different pairs of branch sets depends on disjoint sets of random <edges>, so these events are independent. For this assignment the <probability> that all small pairs are adjacent is at most
$$
(1-t^{-1/4})^{\binom{\lfloor t/2\rfloor}{2}}
\leq\exp\left(-\binom{\lfloor t/2\rfloor}{2}t^{-1/4}\right).
$$
The <union bound> over assignments is therefore at most
$$
\exp\left(N\log(t+1)-\binom{\lfloor t/2\rfloor}{2}t^{-1/4}\right)\longrightarrow0,
$$
since the positive term is $O(t(\log t)^{3/2})$ and the negative term has order $t^{7/4}$. There is consequently a <graph> with no $K_t$ <graph minor> and with $e(G)/N\geq N/8$. Any constant having the universal forcing property must exceed this ratio. Since $N/8\geq t\sqrt{\log t}/64$ for large $t$,
$$
\boxed{c(t)\geq\beta t\sqrt{\log t}\quad\text{with }\beta=1/64.}
$$

\b[The auxiliary density lemma contains a typo.] Both the original PDF and the TeX print $e(G)\leq11k|G|$ in the hint. This cannot imply its conclusion: for $k\geq1$, an edgeless <graph> has only edgeless <graph minors>, and none satisfies $2\delta(H)\geq|H|+4k-1$. We use the intended <dense minor with bounded order and high minimum degree> lemma with the hypothesis $e(G)\geq11k|G|$ for nonempty $G$. The upper-bound argument below depends on that corrected supplied lemma; the literal printed hint is false.

\b[Probabilistic upper bound and the constant seven.] Suppose $G$ is nonempty and $e(G)\geq7t\sqrt{\log t}|G|$, and set
$$
k=\left\lfloor\frac7{11}t\sqrt{\log t}\right\rfloor,\qquad
\ell=\lceil\sqrt{\log t}\rceil.
$$
The corrected lemma gives a <graph minor> $H$ of order $N\leq11k+2$ with $2\delta(H)\geq N+4k-1$. Since $\delta(H)\leq N-1$, we also have $N\geq4k+1$. For large $k$, every <vertex> has at most $N/3$ nonneighbours, counting itself, because
$$
N-\delta(H)\leq N/2-2k+1/2\leq N/3;
$$
the last inequality uses $N\leq11k+2\leq12k-3$. Moreover any two distinct <vertices> have at least
$$
2\delta(H)-N\geq4k-1
$$
<common neighbours>.

Choose $2t$ disjoint random $\ell$-sets $A_1,\ldots,A_{2t}$ from $V(H)$, uniformly, which is possible since $2t\ell<4k-1\leq N$ for sufficiently large $t$. For a set $A$, let $U(A)$ be the set of <vertices> having no neighbour in $A$. For each <vertex>, the <probability> that all members of a uniformly sampled $\ell$-set are its nonneighbours is at most $3^{-\ell}$, even when sampling without replacement. By linearity of <expected value>,
$$
\mathbb E|U(A)|\leq N3^{-\ell}.
$$
Call $A$ good if $|U(A)|\leq N3^{-19\ell/20}$. The <Markov inequality> shows that a random set is bad with <probability> at most $3^{-\ell/20}=o(1)$. Thus the expected number of bad sets among our $2t$ samples is $o(t)$.

Conditional on a fixed good $A_i$, the marginal distribution of $A_j$ is uniform among $\ell$-sets outside $A_i$. For there to be no <edge> between them, all its members must lie in $U(A_i)$. Hence
$$
\mathbb P(A_i\text{ good and }e(A_i,A_j)=0)
\leq\left(\frac{N3^{-19\ell/20}}{N-\ell}\right)^\ell.
$$
Here $(N/(N-\ell))^\ell=\exp(O(\ell^2/N))=1+o(1)$. Consequently the expected number of nonadjacent pairs of good sets is at most
$$
\binom{2t}{2}(1+o(1))3^{-19\ell^2/20}
=O\left(t^{2-(19/20)\log3}\right)=o(t),
$$
since $(19/20)\log3>1$. Applying the <Markov inequality> to both counts shows that there exists a choice with fewer than $t$ bad sets and at most $t$ nonadjacent pairs of good sets. Select $t$ of the good sets; they still have at most $t$ missing adjacencies.

Make each selected set connected by fixing one of its <vertices> as a root and, for each of its other members, adding one unused <common neighbour> of that member and the root. This uses at most $t(\ell-1)$ additional <vertices>. Then for each pair with no original <edge>, choose one <vertex> in each set and add an unused <common neighbour> to one of the sets. The added <vertex> is joined to that set and to the other set, so it preserves connectedness and repairs their adjacency. At most $t$ such repairs are required.

Throughout this process the total number of occupied <vertices> is at most
$$
t\ell+t(\ell-1)+t=2t\ell<4k-1.
$$
Since every pair has at least $4k-1$ <common neighbours>, an unused choice always exists. The final sets are disjoint connected <branch sets of a graph minor> with every pair adjacent. They represent $K_t$ in $H$, and hence in $G$, by transitivity of <graph minors>. This <random branch-set construction of a complete graph minor> proves
$$
\boxed{c(t)\leq7t\sqrt{\log t}\quad\text{for sufficiently large }t.}
$$

\b[The sharp linear threshold for a four-vertex complete minor.] We prove by induction that a <graph> on $n\geq4$ <vertices> with at least $2n-2$ <edges> has a $K_4$ <graph minor>. The case $n=4$ is $K_4$ itself. If some <edge> $uv$ has at most one <common neighbour>, an <edge contraction> reduces the order by one and removes exactly $1+|N(u)\cap N(v)|\leq2$ <edges>. The contracted <graph> has at least $2(n-1)-2$ <edges>, so induction applies.

Otherwise every <edge> is in at least two <triangles in a graph>. Choose a <vertex> $v$ incident with an <edge>. Each <vertex> $u\in N(v)$ has at least two <neighbours of a vertex> in $N(v)$, since these are the <common neighbours> of $u,v$. Thus $G[N(v)]$ has <minimum degree of a graph> at least two. A longest path in this finite <graph> has an endpoint adjacent to an earlier nonconsecutive path <vertex>, and hence contains a <cycle in a graph>. Together with $v$, this <cycle in a graph> gives a <wheel graph> as a <subgraph>. Divide its rim into three consecutive nonempty connected arcs; these arcs and $\{v\}$ form four pairwise adjacent <branch sets of a graph minor>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{e(G)\geq2n-2\ \Longrightarrow\ G\succ K_4.}
$$

With $2n-3$ <edges> the answer is \b[no]. Take the <join of graphs> of $K_2$ and an <independent set> of $n-2$ <vertices>. It has $1+2(n-2)=2n-3$ <edges>. Among four disjoint putative <branch sets of a graph minor>, at most two can contain the two <vertices> of $K_2$. Any connected branch set avoiding them is a singleton in the <independent set>. At least two such singleton sets would therefore have no <edge> between them. This excludes a $K_4$ <graph minor> for every $n\geq4$ and proves sharpness.
