The <spectrum of a commutative ring> is the set
$$
\operatorname{Spec}R=\{\mathfrak p\subsetneq R:\mathfrak p\text{ is a prime ideal}\}.
$$
For an <ideal> $I\subseteq R$, set $V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}$. The <Zariski topology> declares precisely these subsets to be <closed sets>. Using arbitrary subsets instead of <ideals> gives the same definition, because a <prime ideal> contains a subset exactly when it contains the <ideal> generated by that subset.

We verify the <closed set> axioms directly. First, $V(0)=\operatorname{Spec}R$ and $V(R)=\varnothing$. For any family of <ideals>,
$$
\bigcap_{\lambda}V(I_\lambda)=V\left(\sum_{\lambda}I_\lambda\right),
$$
since containing the sum is equivalent to containing every summand. For two <ideals>,
$$
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
$$
Indeed, containing either $I$ or $J$ implies containing their <product of ideals>. Conversely, if a <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$ contains $IJ$ but does not contain $I$, choose $a\in I\setminus\mathfrak p$. For every $b\in J$, $ab\in\mathfrak p$, so the <prime ideal> property forces $b\in\mathfrak p$. Thus $J\subseteq\mathfrak p$.

The displayed identities establish arbitrary intersections and finite unions of <closed sets>, including their empty cases. Therefore \b[these sets define a topology on $\operatorname{Spec}R$].

The induced map on the <spectrum of a commutative ring> is <contraction of an ideal>:
$$
\boxed{f(\mathfrak q)=\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q).}
$$
To see that it is well defined, let $\mathfrak q$ be a <prime ideal> of $S$. Its inverse image is an <ideal> of $R$, and it is proper because $\varphi(1)=1\notin\mathfrak q$. If $ab\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)$, then $\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\in\mathfrak q$, whence $\varphi(a)\in\mathfrak q$ or $\varphi(b)\in\mathfrak q$. This proves that the inverse image is a <prime ideal>.

For any <ideal> $I\subseteq R$, its <extension of an ideal> to $S$ is $IS=\varphi(I)S$. We have
$$
f^{-1}(V_R(I))=\{\mathfrak q:I\subseteq\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)\}=V_S(IS).
$$
Thus inverse images of <Zariski-closed sets> are <Zariski-closed sets>, which proves that \b[$f$ is a continuous map].

We first establish the <nilradical as the intersection of prime ideals>:
$$
\operatorname{Nil}(A)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}A}\mathfrak p.
$$
Every <nilpotent element> belongs to every <prime ideal>. In the other direction, if $a$ is not nilpotent, the <multiplicative subset> $T=\{1,a,a^2,\ldots\}$ avoids zero. By the <Zorn lemma>, there is an <ideal> $J$ maximal among those disjoint from $T$. It is a <prime ideal>: if $uv\in J$ and neither $u$ nor $v$ belongs to $J$, then $J+(u)$ and $J+(v)$ each meet $T$. Multiplying elements in these two intersections gives an element of $T$ lying in $J$, a contradiction. Since $a\notin J$, this proves the identity. The identity also holds for the zero <ring>, with the intersection over an empty family interpreted as the whole <ring>.

A <Zariski-closed set> $V_R(I)$ contains the image of $f$ exactly when
$$
I\subseteq\bigcap_{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}S}\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)
=\varphi^{-1}(\operatorname{Nil}(S))
=\sqrt{\ker\varphi}.
$$
The last equality follows because $\varphi(r)$ is a <nilpotent element> exactly when $r^n\in\ker\varphi$ for some $n\geq1$. Consequently the smallest <Zariski-closed set> containing the image, namely its <closure>, is
$$
\overline{f(\operatorname{Spec}S)}=V_R(\sqrt{\ker\varphi})=V_R(\ker\varphi).
$$
This is all of $\operatorname{Spec}R$ precisely when the <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is contained in every <prime ideal> of $R$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f(\operatorname{Spec}S)\text{ is dense in }\operatorname{Spec}R
\iff\ker\varphi\subseteq\operatorname{Nil}(R).}
$$

Here the coefficient <fields> are $\mathbb R$ and $\mathbb C$. A <polynomial ring> in one variable over a <field> is a <Euclidean domain>, hence a <principal ideal domain>; its nonzero <prime ideals> are generated by <irreducible polynomials>. The <fundamental theorem of algebra> says that all <irreducible polynomials> over $\mathbb C$ are linear. Combining it with <complex conjugation> shows that the <monic> <irreducible polynomials> over $\mathbb R$ are exactly the linear polynomials and quadratics with no real root. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb R[x]
=\{(0)\}\cup\{(x-a):a\in\mathbb R\}
\cup\{((x-a)^2+b^2):a\in\mathbb R,\ b>0\}.}
$$
Every nonzero <prime ideal> in this list is a <maximal ideal>; $(0)$ is a <prime ideal> because $\mathbb R[x]$ is an <integral domain>. Likewise,
$$
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[x]=\{(0)\}\cup\{(x-z):z\in\mathbb C\}.
$$
The map is <contraction of an ideal> along the inclusion. It sends $(0)$ to $(0)$. For $z=a+ib$, the contraction of $(x-z)$ consists of the real polynomials vanishing at $z$. If $b=0$, the <factor theorem> gives the <ideal> $(x-a)$. If $b\ne0$, a real polynomial vanishing at $z$ also vanishes at its <complex conjugate> $\overline z$, so it is divisible by $(x-z)(x-\overline z)=(x-a)^2+b^2$. Division by this real quadratic gives the converse and hence
$$
\boxed{f((x-z))=
\begin{cases}
(x-a),&z=a\in\mathbb R,\\
((x-a)^2+b^2),&z=a+ib,\ b\ne0.
\end{cases}}
$$
Thus each real linear <prime ideal> has one preimage, each real quadratic <prime ideal> has the two conjugate preimages $(x-a-ib)$ and $(x-a+ib)$, and $(0)$ has only $(0)$ above it. In particular, $f$ is surjective.

Let $T=R\setminus P$. Since $P$ is a <prime ideal>, $T$ is a <multiplicative subset> containing $1$ and excluding $0$. The <localization at a prime ideal> is
$$
\boxed{R_P=T^{-1}R.}
$$
Concretely, its elements are equivalence classes of pairs $(r,s)\in R\times T$, written $r/s$, with
$$
\frac r s=\frac {r'}{s'}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u(s'r-sr')=0\text{ for some }u\in T.
$$
This extra multiplier is essential when $R$ has <zero divisors>. Addition and multiplication are
$$
\frac r s+\frac {r'}{s'}=\frac{rs'+r's}{ss'},\qquad
\frac r s\frac {r'}{s'}=\frac{rr'}{ss'}.
$$
The equivalence criterion is compatible with these operations: after multiplying by the multipliers witnessing changes of representatives, the corresponding numerators agree. The <commutative ring> axioms follow from those of $R$; the identity is $1/1$.

The canonical <ring homomorphism> $\iota:R\to R_P$, $r\mapsto r/1$, supplies the <algebra over a commutative ring> structure, with scalar action $a(r/s)=ar/s$. It also has the <universal property of localization>: whenever a <ring homomorphism> $\psi:R\to A$ sends $T$ to <units>, the unique extension is $r/s\mapsto\psi(r)\psi(s)^{-1}$.

For later use, $R_P$ is a <local ring> with <maximal ideal> $PR_P$. Reduction of numerators and denominators gives a surjective <ring homomorphism> $R_P\to\operatorname{Frac}(R/P)$ with kernel $PR_P$, so this <ideal> is proper and maximal. Every fraction outside it has numerator outside $P$ and is a <unit>, with inverse $s/r$. Thus it is the unique <maximal ideal>.

The equivalence relation defining the <localization at a prime ideal> gives
$$
\ker(R\to R_P)=\{r\in R:sr=0\text{ for some }s\notin P\}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{R\longrightarrow R_P\text{ is injective }
\iff\text{ every }s\in R\setminus P\text{ annihilates no nonzero element of }R.}
$$
Equivalently, all <zero divisors> of $R$ must lie in $P$. To prove necessity, if $s\notin P$ and $sr=0$ with $r\ne0$, then $r/1=0$ in $R_P$, contradicting the hypothesis that the map is <injective>. Conversely, if every denominator annihilates no nonzero element, the displayed <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is zero, so the map is <injective>.

An <integral domain> automatically satisfies this criterion at every <prime ideal>, but the criterion itself does not require $R$ to be an <integral domain>.

\b[No: $R$ cannot contain a nonzero nilpotent element.] We prove the needed principle, <localization detects zero elements>, rather than assume it.

Let $a\ne0$ in $R$. Its <annihilator> $\operatorname{Ann}(a)=\{r:ra=0\}$ is a proper <ideal>, because $1$ does not annihilate $a$. Every proper <ideal> is contained in a <maximal ideal>, by the <Zorn lemma>; choose such a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$ containing $\operatorname{Ann}(a)$. If $a/1=0$ in $R_{\mathfrak m}$, then some $s\notin\mathfrak m$ satisfies $sa=0$. This would put $s$ in $\operatorname{Ann}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak m$, a contradiction. Thus every nonzero element survives in at least one <localization at a prime ideal>.

Now suppose $a^n=0$. For every <prime ideal> $P$, $(a/1)^n=0$ in $R_P$. The assumed absence of nonzero <nilpotent elements> forces $a/1=0$ in every $R_P$. The preceding argument then forces $a=0$. In the terminology of <reduced rings>, we have proved
$$
\boxed{R_P\text{ reduced for every prime }P\ \Longrightarrow\ R\text{ reduced}.}
$$

\b[Yes: $R$ can have nonzero zero divisors.] Let $R=k\times k$, a <direct product of rings>, where $k$ is any <field>. The nonzero <idempotents> $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$ satisfy $e_1e_2=0$, so both are <zero divisors>.

There are exactly two <prime ideals>:
$$
P_1=0\times k,\qquad P_2=k\times0.
$$
Indeed, any <prime ideal> must contain $e_1$ or $e_2$, since their product is zero. If it contains $e_1$, it contains $k\times0$ and corresponds to a <prime ideal> of the <quotient ring> $k$, whose only <prime ideal> is zero; hence it equals $P_2$. The other case gives $P_1$.

For the <localization at a prime ideal> $P_1$, every denominator $(u,v)\notin P_1$ has $u\ne0$, and
$$
R_{P_1}\longrightarrow k,\qquad
\frac{(a,b)}{(u,v)}\longmapsto\frac a u
$$
is an isomorphism. It is surjective using constant first-coordinate fractions. It is injective because a numerator with first coordinate zero is annihilated by $e_1\notin P_1$, so its fraction is zero. Interchanging the coordinates proves $R_{P_2}\cong k$.

Thus the <localization at a prime ideal> is a <field> in every case, although $R$ has <zero divisors>. The obstruction to using the previous part's argument is that the two nonzero factors of a zero product can survive at different <prime ideals>.

Let $e_j$ be the sequence with $1$ in coordinate $j$ and zero elsewhere. In the <direct product of rings> $R=k^{\mathbb N}$, the <ideals>
$$
(e_1)\subsetneq(e_1,e_2)\subsetneq(e_1,e_2,e_3)\subsetneq\cdots
$$
form a strict ascending chain: every element of $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ vanishes after coordinate $n$, whereas $e_{n+1}$ does not. Hence \b[$R$ is not a Noetherian ring].

Nevertheless, each $a=(a_j)\in R$ has a coordinatewise generalized inverse $b=(b_j)$, defined by $b_j=a_j^{-1}$ when $a_j\ne0$ and $b_j=0$ otherwise. It satisfies
$$
a=a^2b.
$$
This is the defining property of a <von Neumann regular ring>. Fix any <prime ideal> $P$, and take $a\in P$. Since $ab\in P$, $1-ab\notin P$; but
$$
(1-ab)a=0.
$$
Consequently $a/1=0$ in the <localization at a prime ideal> $R_P$. All elements of $PR_P$ therefore vanish. By the <local ring> description in part (a), $PR_P$ is the unique <maximal ideal>, so $R_P$ is a nonzero <field>. A <field> has only the <ideals> zero and itself, and thus is <Noetherian>.

We have proved the stronger conclusion
$$
\boxed{R_P\text{ is a field for every prime ideal }P\subset R.}
$$
The argument applies to every <prime ideal>, without assuming that it comes from a coordinate projection.

A $P$-<primary ideal> is a proper <ideal> $Q$ with <radical of an ideal> $\sqrt Q=P$, such that $ab\in Q$ and $a\notin Q$ imply $b^n\in Q$ for some $n\geq1$. Equivalently, when $\sqrt Q=P$, the implication is $ab\in Q$, $b\notin P\Rightarrow a\in Q$.

\b[True for a nonempty finite family.] Let $Q=Q_1\cap\cdots\cap Q_m$, with each $Q_j$ a $P$-<primary ideal>. This <intersection of ideals> is proper. Its <radical of an ideal> is $P$: if $r\in P$, choose $n_j$ with $r^{n_j}\in Q_j$ and put $N=\max_j n_j$. Then $r^N\in Q$. Conversely, $r^N\in Q$ implies $r\in\sqrt{Q_j}=P$.

If $ab\in Q$ and $b\notin P$, the <primary ideal> property gives $a\in Q_j$ for every $j$, so $a\in Q$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Q_1\cap\cdots\cap Q_m\text{ is }P\text{-primary}\quad(m\geq1).}
$$
As usual, the statement about finite intersections excludes the empty intersection, which is the whole <ring> and is not a <primary ideal>.

\b[True, without a Noetherian hypothesis.] By definition, the <symbolic power> is the <contraction of an ideal>
$$
P^{(n)}=P^nR_P\cap R,
$$
where the intersection notation means inverse image under $R\to R_P$, even if this map is not <injective>. In the <local ring> $A=R_P$, put $\mathfrak m=PR_P$. Then $P^nR_P=\mathfrak m^n$, and $\sqrt{\mathfrak m^n}=\mathfrak m$: every element of $\mathfrak m$ has its $n$th power in $\mathfrak m^n$, whereas a <unit> cannot have a power in this proper <ideal>.

The <ideal> $\mathfrak m^n$ is $\mathfrak m$-primary. Indeed, if $uv\in\mathfrak m^n$ and $v\notin\mathfrak m$, then $v$ is a <unit>, so $u\in\mathfrak m^n$. Its <contraction of an ideal> is therefore $P$-primary: its <radical of an ideal> contracts to $P$, and the same implication applies to the images of any two elements of $R$. Thus $P^{(n)}$ is always $P$-primary, proving the reverse implication immediately when $P^n=P^{(n)}$.

For the other implication, suppose $P^n$ is $P$-primary. Membership in the contraction can be expressed by clearing denominators:
$$
r\in P^{(n)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
sr\in P^n\text{ for some }s\notin P.
$$
For completeness, if $r/1=a/t$ with $a\in P^n$ and $t\notin P$, equality of fractions gives $u(tr-a)=0$ for some $u\notin P$, so $(ut)r=ua\in P^n$; the converse follows by inverting $s$. Since $s\notin\sqrt{P^n}=P$, the <primary ideal> property forces $r\in P^n$. The inclusion $P^n\subseteq P^{(n)}$ always holds, giving
$$
\boxed{P^n\text{ is }P\text{-primary}\iff P^n=P^{(n)}.}
$$

\b[True.] An <irreducible ideal> $I$ is a proper <ideal> such that $I=J\cap K$ forces $I=J$ or $I=K$. Passing to the <quotient ring> $A=R/I$ turns this into the assertion that zero cannot be the intersection of two nonzero <ideals>. $$
\operatorname{Ann}(b)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}(b^2)\subseteq\cdots
$$
stabilize. Choose $n\geq1$ with $\operatorname{Ann}(b^n)=\operatorname{Ann}(b^{n+1})$. We claim
$$
(a)\cap(b^n)=0.

\b[False: existence holds, but uniqueness fails.] First, the <Lasker–Noether theorem> supplies existence; here is an argument from the preceding part. If some proper <ideal> in a <Noetherian ring> were not a finite intersection of <irreducible ideals>, the <ascending chain condition> would let us choose such an <ideal> $I$ maximal under inclusion. It is not irreducible, so $I=J\cap K$ with both $J$ and $K$ strictly larger than $I$. By maximality, both are finite intersections of <irreducible ideals>, and therefore so is $I$, a contradiction. Part (d) makes each factor a <primary ideal>. The whole <ring> is represented by the empty intersection if that convention is needed.

For a counterexample to uniqueness, work in $R=k[x,y]$ over a <field> $k$ and put $I=(x^2,xy)$. The <Hilbert basis theorem> makes $R$ a <Noetherian ring>. There are two distinct <minimal primary decompositions>:
$$
\boxed{(x^2,xy)=(x)\cap(x^2,y)=(x)\cap(x^2,y-x).}
$$
The <ideal> $(x)$ is a <prime ideal>, hence a <primary ideal>. Each of the other factors has <quotient ring> isomorphic to $k[x]/(x^2)$, by substituting respectively $y=0$ and $y=x$. As in part (b), each is $(x,y)$-primary.

To check both intersections, let $Q_c=(x^2,y-cx)$, for $c=0,1$. Substitution identifies $R/Q_c$ with $k[x]/(x^2)$. The class of $xg(x,y)$ is zero exactly when $g(0,0)=0$, equivalently when $g\in(x,y)$. Therefore $(Q_c:x)=(x,y)$ and
$$
(x)\cap Q_c=x(Q_c:x)=x(x,y)=I.
$$
The two $Q_c$ are different, since $y$ belongs to $Q_0$ but has nonzero class $x$ modulo $Q_1$. Both decompositions are irredundant: $x\notin Q_c$, while $y-cx\notin(x)$. Applying the <radical of an ideal> operation to the two components in either decomposition gives $(x)$ and $(x,y)$, respectively.

The <second uniqueness theorem for primary decomposition> explains the distinction: the component at an <isolated prime of a primary decomposition> is unique, but <embedded primary components> need not be. The counterexample varies an embedded component.

\b[A <ring> is a <Noetherian ring> if it satisfies the <ascending chain condition> on <ideals>]: every chain
$$
I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq I_3\subseteq\cdots
$$
eventually becomes constant. Equivalently, every <ideal> has a finite <generating set of an ideal>. To see this equivalence, if an <ideal> were not finitely generated, one could repeatedly choose an element outside the <ideal> generated by the preceding choices, producing a strictly ascending chain. Conversely, the union of an ascending chain is an <ideal>; if it is generated by finitely many elements, all of them lie in one term of the chain, and every later term equals that term.

\b[A <ring> is an <Artinian ring> if it satisfies the <descending chain condition> on <ideals>]: every chain
$$
I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq\cdots
$$
eventually becomes constant. These are stabilization conditions on all <ideals>, not merely on <principal ideals>.

\b[Yes: $R[[x]]$ is Noetherian.] We prove <Noetherianity of a formal power series ring> directly, using only the equivalence in part (a). Let $A=R[[x]]$, a <formal power series ring>, and fix an <ideal> $I\subseteq A$. For $n\geq0$, let $C_n$ be the set of coefficients of $x^n$ in elements of $I\cap x^nA$. It is an <ideal> of $R$, because coefficient extraction is additive and respects multiplication by constants. Multiplication by $x$ gives
$$
C_0\subseteq C_1\subseteq C_2\subseteq\cdots.
$$
Since $R$ is <Noetherian>, there is an $N$ with $C_n=C_N$ for all $n\geq N$. Each $C_n$, for $0\leq n\leq N$, has a finite <generating set of an ideal> $a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nr_n}$. Choose $f_{nj}\in I\cap x^nA$ whose coefficient of $x^n$ is $a_{nj}$. If $C_n=0$, use no generators at that index.

We claim that these finitely many $f_{nj}$ generate $I$ over $A$. Given $f\in I$, start with $g_0=f$ and recursively construct $g_n\in I\cap x^nA$. For $n<N$, express the coefficient of $x^n$ in $g_n$ as $\sum_jc_{nj}a_{nj}$, with $c_{nj}\in R$, and subtract $\sum_jc_{nj}f_{nj}$ to obtain $g_{n+1}\in I\cap x^{n+1}A$. For $n\geq N$, use $C_n=C_N$ and subtract
$$
\sum_jc_{nj}x^{n-N}f_{Nj}
$$
to cancel that coefficient. Every step involves a finite sum and leaves a residual still in $I$.

For each $j$, define the <formal power series>
$$
h_j(x)=\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}c_{nj}x^{n-N}\in A.
$$
The residual after the $n$th cancellation lies in $x^{n+1}A$, so comparison of each coefficient gives the exact identity
$$
f=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_jc_{nj}f_{nj}+\sum_jh_j(x)f_{Nj}.
$$
The right side is a finite $A$-linear combination of the chosen generators. No assertion that arbitrary <ideals> are closed under limits is being used: the equality is verified coefficient by coefficient, and the multipliers $h_j$ belong to $A$. Thus every <ideal> of $A$ is finitely generated, proving
$$
\boxed{R\text{ Noetherian}\ \Longrightarrow\ R[[x]]\text{ Noetherian}.}
$$

\b[A <Dedekind domain> is a <Noetherian ring> $D$ which is an <integral domain>, is an <integrally closed domain>, and has <Krull dimension> one]. Explicitly, every <ideal> is finitely generated; every element of the <fraction field> that is an <integral element> over $D$ already belongs to $D$; and every nonzero <prime ideal> is a <maximal ideal>, with $D$ itself not a <field>.

With this convention, an equivalent local characterization is that $D$ is a <Noetherian> <integral domain> that is not a <field> and whose <localization at a prime ideal> $D_P$ is a <discrete valuation ring> for every nonzero <prime ideal> $P$. The <integrally closed domain> condition rules out singularities such as those represented by the subring $k[t^2,t^3]\subset k[t]$, where $t$ is integral but missing.

Some conventions also include <fields> by allowing <Krull dimension> at most one; the conclusions about <ideals> below remain valid under that convention.

The <unique factorization of ideals in a Dedekind domain> states that every nonzero proper <ideal> $I$ of a <Dedekind domain> $D$ has a factorization
$$
\boxed{I=P_1^{n_1}\cdots P_r^{n_r},}
$$
where the $P_j$ are distinct nonzero <prime ideals> and the $n_j$ are positive integers. The <prime ideals> and their exponents are unique up to reordering. The unit <ideal> $D$ is the empty product; the zero <ideal> is excluded.

Write $v_P(I)$ for the <prime-ideal valuation in a Dedekind domain>, namely the exponent of $P$ in this factorization, with zero assigned when $P$ is absent. The associated containment rule is
$$
I\subseteq J\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_P(I)\geq v_P(J)\text{ for every nonzero prime ideal }P.
$$
Larger exponents give smaller <ideals>, just as higher divisibility gives smaller <principal ideals> in $\mathbb Z$. The theorem concerns unique factorization of <ideals>; it does not assert unique factorization of elements or that these <ideals> are principal.

Let $D$ be a <Dedekind domain>. The zero <ideal> is generated by $0$, and $D$ is generated by $1$, so consider a nonzero proper <ideal> $I$. Choose $0\ne a\in I$. We use these consequences of the <unique factorization of ideals in a Dedekind domain>: every nonzero <ideal> has finitely many nonzero <prime-ideal valuations in a Dedekind domain>; containment reverses the valuations as in part (b); and
$$
v_P(J+K)=\min\{v_P(J),v_P(K)\}.
$$
The last formula follows from the containment rule: $J+K$ is the smallest <ideal> containing both $J$ and $K$, and the product with the indicated minimum exponents is exactly that <ideal>.

Let $T$ be the finite set of <prime ideals> dividing $(a)$. For each $P\in T$, set $r_P=v_P(I)$. Since $(a)\subseteq I$, we have $r_P\leq v_P((a))$. The containment rule also gives $I\not\subseteq P^{r_P+1}$, so choose
$$
b_P\in I\setminus P^{r_P+1}.
$$
Distinct nonzero <prime ideals> of $D$ are distinct <maximal ideals> and hence <comaximal ideals>; their positive powers are also comaximal. Indeed, if $u+v=1$ with $u\in P$, $v\in Q$, for positive integers $m,n$ expanding $(u+v)^{m+n-1}$ shows $1\in P^m+Q^n$, since each term contains either at least $m$ factors of $u$ or at least $n$ factors of $v$. We use the <Chinese remainder theorem> in its ideal form: for pairwise comaximal <ideals> $J_1,\ldots,J_s$, the map $D\to\prod_jD/J_j$ is surjective. Apply it to the powers $P^{r_P+1}$ to choose $e_P\in D$ with
$$
e_P\equiv1\pmod{P^{r_P+1}},\qquad
e_P\equiv0\pmod{Q^{r_Q+1}}\quad(Q\in T,\ Q\ne P).
$$
Then $b=\sum_{P\in T}e_Pb_P$ belongs to $I$ and satisfies $b\equiv b_P\pmod{P^{r_P+1}}$ for every $P\in T$. Thus $b\notin P^{r_P+1}$, whereas $b\in I\subseteq P^{r_P}$, proving $v_P((b))=r_P$.

For $P\in T$, therefore,
$$
v_P((a,b))=\min\{v_P((a)),v_P((b))\}=r_P.
$$
For $P\notin T$, $v_P((a))=0$, and $(a)\subseteq I$ forces $v_P(I)=0$ as well. Hence $(a,b)$ and $I$ have the same valuation at every nonzero <prime ideal>. Uniqueness of their factorizations gives
$$
\boxed{I=(a,b).}
$$
This proves <two generators for an ideal in a Dedekind domain>; in fact the first nonzero generator $a\in I$ can be chosen in advance.

We justify that this is a <Dedekind domain>. It is an <integral domain> inside the <number field> $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})$. As an additive group it is free of rank two over $\mathbb Z$. The subgroup theorem for <finitely generated abelian groups> says that every subgroup of such a group is finitely generated. Thus every <ideal> has finitely many additive generators, and those same elements generate it as a $D$-ideal. This proves that $D$ is <Noetherian>.

We use the standard formula for the <ring of integers of a quadratic field>: for squarefree $d$, it is $\mathbb Z[(1+\sqrt d)/2]$ if $d\equiv1\pmod4$, and $\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]$ otherwise. Since $-5\equiv3\pmod4$, $D$ is the <ring of integers of a number field> $K$, meaning all elements of $K$ integral over $\mathbb Z$. We also use transitivity of <integral extensions>. If an element of $K$ is integral over $D$, it is integral over $\mathbb Z$ because $D$ is integral over $\mathbb Z$, and therefore belongs to $D$. Thus $D$ is an <integrally closed domain>.

Every nonzero <prime ideal> $P$ is maximal. Indeed, choose $0\ne\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-5}\in P$. Multiplying by its <algebraic conjugate> puts the nonzero integer $N(\alpha)=a^2+5b^2$ in $P$. Hence the <integral domain> $D/P$ is a quotient of the finite <ring> $D/N(\alpha)D$, and is finite. Every finite <integral domain> is a <field>, since multiplication by a nonzero element is injective and therefore surjective. The element $2$ has no inverse in $D$, since $1/2\notin\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, so $D$ is not a <field>. Consequently $(0)$ is not maximal, and a <maximal ideal> above it is nonzero. Together with the preceding result, this gives <Krull dimension> exactly one.

Finally, consider the <ideal>
$$
I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5}).
$$
Reduction modulo $2$ with $\sqrt{-5}\mapsto1$ identifies $D/I$ with $\mathbb F_2$, so the additive index $[D:I]$ is $2$. If $I$ were the <principal ideal> $(a+b\sqrt{-5})$, multiplication by its nonzero generator would have integer matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&-5b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}
$$
in the basis $1,\sqrt{-5}$. The index of its image is the absolute determinant, so
$$
2=[D:I]=a^2+5b^2.
$$
There are no integer solutions: $b\ne0$ makes the right side at least $5$, while $b=0$ would require $a^2=2$. Thus $I$ is a <nonprincipal ideal in the integers adjoined a square root of minus five>, and \b[$D$ is a Dedekind domain that is not a principal ideal domain].

For a subring $S\subseteq R$ with the same identity, $R$ is an <integral extension> of $S$ if every $r\in R$ is an <integral element> over $S$: there exist $n\geq1$ and $s_0,\ldots,s_{n-1}\in S$ such that
$$
\boxed{r^n+s_{n-1}r^{n-1}+\cdots+s_1r+s_0=0.}
$$
The requirement that the polynomial be <monic> is essential. Merely satisfying some polynomial equation over $S$ is insufficient: for instance $1/2\in\mathbb Q$ satisfies $2T-1=0$ over $\mathbb Z$, but is not integral over $\mathbb Z$. To see this directly, a hypothetical <monic> equation for $1/2$, multiplied by $2^n$, would say that an odd integer $1$ plus a sum of even integers is zero.

The degree and coefficients may depend on $r$. Integrality does not by itself assert that the entire <ring> $R$ is a finite $S$-<module>; it does imply that the subalgebra $S[r]$ is generated as an $S$-<module> by $1,r,\ldots,r^{n-1}$.

We use three theorems for an <integral extension> $S\subseteq R$, none requiring <Noetherian> hypotheses. The <lying-over theorem> says that each <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$ of $S$ is $\mathfrak q\cap S$ for some <prime ideal> $\mathfrak q$ of $R$. The <going-up theorem> says that if $\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak p'$ in $S$ and $\mathfrak q\cap S=\mathfrak p$, there is $\mathfrak q'\supseteq\mathfrak q$ with $\mathfrak q'\cap S=\mathfrak p'$. The <incomparability theorem for integral extensions> says that comparable <prime ideals> of $R$ with the same contraction to $S$ are equal.

Take any strict chain
$$
\mathfrak q_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak q_n
$$
in $R$. The <contraction of an ideal> operation gives $\mathfrak p_j=\mathfrak q_j\cap S$, which are <prime ideals> of $S$, by the proof in Question 1(b), and remain strictly increasing by the <incomparability theorem for integral extensions>. Thus every chain length in $R$ occurs in $S$, giving $\dim R\leq\dim S$.

Conversely, take any strict chain $\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_n$ in $S$. Use the <lying-over theorem> to choose $\mathfrak q_0$ over $\mathfrak p_0$, then repeatedly use the <going-up theorem> to obtain $\mathfrak q_0\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathfrak q_n$ with the prescribed contractions. Each inclusion must be strict, since its contractions are distinct. Hence every chain length in $S$ occurs in $R$, giving $\dim S\leq\dim R$.

Taking suprema proves <integral extensions preserve Krull dimension>:
$$
\boxed{\dim R=\dim S.}
$$
The argument works equally well when the dimensions are infinite, since it compares all finite chain lengths.
