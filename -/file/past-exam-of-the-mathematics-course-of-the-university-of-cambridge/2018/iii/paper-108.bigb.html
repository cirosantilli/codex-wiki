= Solution
{parent=1}

Use the probability-system convention $\mu(X)=1$. The <Birkhoff ergodic theorem>, also called the <pointwise ergodic theorem>, states that for a <measure-preserving system> and $f\in L^1(\mu)$,
$$
A_Nf(x)=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}f(T^nx)
\longrightarrow\mathbb E_\mu[f\mid\mathcal I](x)
\quad\text{almost everywhere},
$$
where $\mathcal I$ is the <invariant sigma-algebra>. The limit is integrable and has the same integral as $f$. On a probability space the convergence also holds in $L^1$, as in the allowed mean ergodic theorem. If $T$ is an <ergodic transformation>, $\mathcal I$ is trivial modulo null sets, giving
$$
\boxed{A_Nf(x)\longrightarrow\int_X f\,d\mu\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$

The <integer multiplication map on the circle> preserves <Lebesgue measure>: for any integrable $g$ on $[0,1)$,
$$
\int_0^1g(Kx\bmod1)\,dx
=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\int_{j/K}^{(j+1)/K}g(Kx-j)\,dx
=\int_0^1g(y)\,dy.
$$
To prove the <ergodicity of integer multiplication on the circle>, suppose $u\in L^2(m)$ satisfies $u\circ T_K=u$. Let $c_j$ be its <Fourier coefficients> in the <Fourier basis> $e_j(x)=e^{2\pi ijx}$. Since $e_j\circ T_K=e_{Kj}$, the <Fourier coefficients> of $u\circ T_K$ at index $j$ are zero if $K$ does not divide $j$, and are $c_{j/K}$ otherwise. This identity holds for all $L^2$ functions by approximation with <trigonometric polynomials> and the isometry $u\mapsto u\circ T_K$. Invariance gives
$$
c_j=\begin{cases}c_{j/K},&K\mid j,\\0,&K\nmid j.\end{cases}
$$
Every nonzero integer $j$ can be divided by $K$ only finitely often. Thus $c_j=0$ for every $j\ne0$, and completeness of the <Fourier basis> makes $u$ constant <almost everywhere>. Applying this to the <indicator function> of an invariant set gives measure zero or one, so
$$
\boxed{T_K\text{ is ergodic for }m\quad(K\geq2).}
$$

A <normal number> in base $K$ has every word $w=(w_1,\ldots,w_r)$ of $r$ base-$K$ digits occurring with limiting overlapping frequency $K^{-r}$. Use the expansion that is not eventually equal to $K-1$ when there are two expansions. The word $w$ corresponds to the half-open interval
$$
I_w=\left[\frac{j(w)}{K^r},\frac{j(w)+1}{K^r}\right),\qquad
j(w)=\sum_{i=1}^r w_iK^{r-i}.
$$
A word starting at position $n+1$ occurs exactly when $T_K^nx\in I_w$. The <Birkhoff ergodic theorem>, applied to $\mathbf1_{I_w}$, gives frequency $m(I_w)=K^{-r}$ <almost everywhere>. There are countably many pairs $(K,w)$, so their full-measure sets have a full-measure intersection. In particular,
$$
\boxed{m\{x\in[0,1):x\text{ is normal in every integer base }K\geq2\}=1.}
$$
These are <absolutely normal numbers>, so existence follows as well. This interval description also proves <normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication>: the base-$K$ intervals form arbitrarily fine grids, so their frequencies imply the correct frequency for every interval by approximation from inside and outside.

For the growth assertion, put $g=|f|$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, the <Tonelli theorem> gives the useful summability bound
$$
\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mu(g>\varepsilon n)
=\int_X\sum_{n=1}^\infty\mathbf1_{\{g(x)>\varepsilon n\}}\,d\mu(x)
\leq\frac{\|f\|_1}{\varepsilon}.
$$
Since $T$ is a <measure-preserving transformation>, $\mu(g\circ T^n>\varepsilon n)=\mu(g>\varepsilon n)$. The first <Borel-Cantelli lemma> shows that $g(T^nx)>\varepsilon n$ occurs only finitely often <almost everywhere>. Intersecting the resulting full-measure sets for $\varepsilon=1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$ proves the <linear growth bound for integrable observables>, $f(T^nx)/n\to0$. Multiplication by $n^{1-a}\leq1$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{f(T^nx)}{n^a}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost everywhere, for every }a\geq1.}
$$

The threshold is sharp. For $0<a<1$, choose $p$ with $1<p<1/a$, and take the <Bernoulli shift> on $X=(0,1)^{\mathbb Z}$ with the <product measure> of independent uniform coordinates. The left shift preserves that measure because it preserves the probability of every finite-coordinate event. Define $f(x)=x_0^{-1/p}$; it is integrable because
$$
\int_X f\,d\mu=\int_0^1u^{-1/p}\,du=\frac{p}{p-1}<\infty.
$$
The variables $f(T^nx)=x_n^{-1/p}$ are independent. For any fixed $M\geq1$,
$$
\mu\{f(T^nx)>Mn^a\}=M^{-p}n^{-ap},\qquad ap<1.
$$
The probability sum diverges, so the second <Borel-Cantelli lemma> makes these events occur infinitely often <almost surely>. Intersecting over positive integer $M$ even yields $\limsup_n f(T^nx)/n^a=\infty$. For $a\leq0$, the constant observable $f=1$ already fails to give limit zero. Thus the <sharpness of the linear growth bound for integrable observables> gives
$$
\boxed{\text{There is no }a<1\text{ for which the asserted limit holds in every system for every }f\in L^1.}
$$

For an increasing sequence $(n_k)$ of nonnegative integers, <full-density sequence> means that its range $S$ has <natural density> one:
$$
\frac{|S\cap\{0,\ldots,N-1\}|}{N}\longrightarrow1.
$$
For a sequence $(z_n)$ in a metric space, <convergence in density of a sequence> to $z$ means that, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\frac1N\bigl|\{0\leq n<N:d(z_n,z)\geq\varepsilon\}\bigr|\longrightarrow0.
$$
In particular, a bounded nonnegative scalar sequence converging to zero in density has averages tending to zero: the average is at most $\varepsilon$ plus its bound times the proportion of indices above $\varepsilon$.

Here is a <Hilbert space> form of the <Van der Corput lemma>, including the density version. Suppose $\|u_n\|\leq M$, and put
$$
c_h=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-h-1}\langle u_{n+h},u_n\rangle\right|\qquad(h\geq1).
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{\frac1H\sum_{h=1}^Hc_h\longrightarrow0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\left\|\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_n\right\|\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Since $0\leq c_h\leq M^2$, it suffices for $(c_h)$ to converge to zero in density. A frequently used special case is that the correlation average tends to zero for every fixed $h\geq1$.

To prove the lemma, extend the finite list $u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}$ by zero outside this range, and write $S_N=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}u_n$. For $1\leq H\leq N$,
$$
HS_N=\sum_{n=-H+1}^{N-1}\sum_{r=0}^{H-1}u_{n+r}.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for this sum, followed by expansion of the squared <Hilbert space> norm, gives
$$
H^2\|S_N\|^2
\leq(N+H-1)\left[
H\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|u_n\|^2
+2\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}(H-h)\operatorname{Re}\sum_{n=0}^{N-h-1}\langle u_{n+h},u_n\rangle
\right].
$$
The coefficient $H-h$ counts the pairs of shifts separated by $h$. Dividing by $H^2N^2$, bounding real parts by absolute values, and first taking the limit superior in $N$ for fixed $H$ yields
$$
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{S_N}{N}\right\|^2
\leq\frac{M^2}{H}
+\frac2H\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\left(1-\frac hH\right)c_h.
$$
The right side tends to zero as $H\to\infty$ under the stated hypothesis. This proves both the lemma and its density consequence.

For the polynomial application, fix a nonzero integer $m$ and let $u_n=e^{2\pi imP(n)}\in\mathbb C$. For each fixed $h\geq1$, the correlation phase is linear:
$$
P(n+h)-P(n)=2ahn+ah^2+bh.
$$
Thus, with $q_h=e^{2\pi i(2mah)}$,
$$
\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-h-1}u_{n+h}\overline{u_n}
=e^{2\pi im(ah^2+bh)}\frac{1-q_h^{N-h}}{N(1-q_h)}.
$$
The coefficient $2mah$ is irrational, so $q_h\ne1$. This <geometric series> is bounded in modulus by $2/(N|1-q_h|)$ and tends to zero. All $c_h$ vanish, so the <Van der Corput lemma> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi im(an^2+bn+c)}\longrightarrow0\qquad(m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}).}
$$

The <Weyl criterion> now shows that the fractional parts form an <equidistributed sequence>. For completeness, its sufficiency here follows directly: the displayed limits give the correct average for every <trigonometric polynomial>, including the constant term. The <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> extends this to every continuous function by uniform approximation. Approximating an interval's <indicator function> above and below by continuous functions gives its length as the limiting frequency. Hence
$$
\boxed{(an^2+bn+c)\bmod1\text{ is equidistributed in }\mathbb R/\mathbb Z.}
$$
This proves the <equidistribution of a quadratic polynomial with irrational leading coefficient> using the required correlation argument.

Let $\xi_0^{n-1}=\bigvee_{j=0}^{n-1}T^{-j}\xi$ denote the <join of measurable partitions> recording the first $n$ observations, and set $H_n=H_\mu(\xi_0^{n-1})$, with $H_0=0$. The <entropy of a countable measurable partition> is $H_\mu(\xi)=-\sum_{A\in\xi}\mu(A)\log\mu(A)$, using natural logarithms and $0\log0=0$. Null atoms can be discarded. Throughout, $\mu$ is a probability measure.

The stronger property needed for <monotonicity of normalized block entropy> is that its increments decrease. Define
$$
c_k=H_\mu\left(\xi\mathrel{\big|}\bigvee_{j=1}^kT^{-j}\xi\right),\qquad c_0=H_\mu(\xi).
$$
Since <conditioning reduces entropy>, $c_{k+1}\leq c_k$. The conditional entropy chain rule and measure preservation give
$$
H_{k+1}
=H_\mu\left(\bigvee_{j=1}^kT^{-j}\xi\right)+c_k
=H_k+c_k,
\qquad H_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_k.
$$
It follows that $c_n\leq H_n/n$, and consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{H_{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{H_n+c_n}{n+1}\leq\frac{H_n}{n}.}
$$
This uses stationarity as well as the entropy chain rule; subadditivity alone would not establish monotonicity of every successive ratio.

The <entropy rate of a measurable partition> and the <Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy> are, respectively,
$$
\boxed{h_\mu(T,\xi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1nH_\mu(\xi_0^{n-1})
=\inf_{n\geq1}\frac{H_n}{n},\qquad
h_\mu(T)=\sup_{\eta\text{ finite measurable partition}}h_\mu(T,\eta).}
$$
One may equivalently take the supremum over countable finite-entropy <measurable partitions>.

The <Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem> states that for a countable <measurable partition> with $H_\mu(\xi)<\infty$, the normalized information
$$
\frac1nI_\mu(\xi_0^{n-1})(x)
=-\frac1n\log\mu(\xi_0^{n-1}(x))
$$
converges <almost everywhere> and in $L^1$ to a $T$ invariant function $h_\xi(x)$ with integral $h_\mu(T,\xi)$. Here $\xi_0^{n-1}(x)$ is the atom containing $x$. If $T$ is an <ergodic transformation>, then $h_\xi(x)=h_\mu(T,\xi)$ <almost everywhere>. We prove the general form, including an explicit formula for its limit.

Let $\mathcal F_k$ be the <sigma-algebra> generated by $\bigvee_{j=1}^kT^{-j}\xi$, let $\mathcal F_0$ be trivial, and put $\mathcal F_\infty=\bigvee_{j=1}^\infty T^{-j}\sigma(\xi)$. For every atom $A$, the probabilities
$$
p_k^A=\mathbb E_\mu[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal F_k]
$$
form a bounded <conditional-expectation martingale>. The <martingale convergence theorem> gives $p_k^A\to p_\infty^A=\mathbb E_\mu[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal F_\infty]$ <almost everywhere> and in $L^1$. Each of these probabilities is positive <almost everywhere> on $A$: for example, integrating $\mathbf1_A$ over the $\mathcal F_\infty$ measurable set $\{p_\infty^A=0\}$ gives zero. Countability of $\xi$ lets us choose a common full-measure set for every atom.

The <conditional information functions>
$$
f_k=I_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal F_k)
=-\sum_{A\in\xi}\mathbf1_A\log p_k^A
$$
therefore converge <almost everywhere> to $f_\infty=I_\mu(\xi\mid\mathcal F_\infty)$. We need an integrable bound on this sequence; convergence of the probabilities alone would not supply one after taking logarithms. The allowed <maximal inequality for conditional information functions> gives, for $F=\sup_{k\geq0}f_k$,
$$
\mu(A\cap\{F>t\})\leq e^{-t}\qquad(A\in\xi,\ t\geq0).
$$
Combining this with the bound by $\mu(A)$ and the <tail integral formula for moments> yields
$$
\int F\,d\mu
\leq\sum_{A\in\xi}\int_0^\infty\min\{\mu(A),e^{-t}\}\,dt
=\sum_{A\in\xi}\mu(A)(1-\log\mu(A))
=1+H_\mu(\xi)<\infty.
$$
Thus $0\leq f_\infty\leq F$, and the <dominated convergence theorem> gives $f_k\to f_\infty$ in $L^1$. In particular,
$$
\int f_\infty\,d\mu=\lim_k c_k=h_\mu(T,\xi),
$$
because $H_n/n$ is the average of the decreasing sequence $(c_k)$.

The information chain rule, applied from the final observation backwards, gives the exact identity
$$
I_\mu(\xi_0^{n-1})(x)
=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{n-1-j}(T^jx).
$$
Indeed, the symbol at time $j$ is conditioned on times $j+1,\ldots,n-1$; pulling that conditional probability back by $T^j$ gives $f_{n-1-j}\circ T^j$. Measure preservation is sufficient for this pullback identity.

We now prove the required <triangular ergodic averaging lemma> in this instance. Put $g_M=\sup_{k\geq M}|f_k-f_\infty|$. Then $g_M\downarrow0$ <almost everywhere>, $g_M\leq2F$, and $\|g_M\|_1\to0$. Split the difference between the displayed triangular sum and $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_\infty(T^jx)$. Terms whose index is at least $M$ are bounded by $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}g_M(T^jx)$. The remaining terms are at most
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}|f_k-f_\infty|(T^{n-1-k}x).
$$
After division by $n$, each of these finitely many end terms tends to zero <almost everywhere> by the <linear growth bound for integrable observables> proved in Question 1. Applying the <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> to $g_M$, for every fixed $M$, therefore gives
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left|
\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(f_{n-1-j}-f_\infty)(T^jx)
\right|\leq\mathbb E_\mu[g_M\mid\mathcal I](x).
$$
These <conditional expectations> decrease to zero <almost everywhere>: they decrease, and their integrals $\int g_M\,d\mu$ tend to zero. Letting $M\to\infty$, then applying the <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> to $f_\infty$, proves
$$
\boxed{-\frac1n\log\mu(\xi_0^{n-1}(x))
\longrightarrow h_\xi(x)=\mathbb E_\mu[f_\infty\mid\mathcal I](x)
\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$

For $L^1$ convergence, measure preservation gives the direct estimate
$$
\left\|\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(f_{n-1-j}-f_\infty)\circ T^j\right\|_1
\leq\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|f_k-f_\infty\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$
Combine this with $L^1$ convergence of the ergodic averages of $f_\infty$. Finally, $\int h_\xi\,d\mu=\int f_\infty\,d\mu=h_\mu(T,\xi)$. In the ergodic case the <invariant sigma-algebra> is trivial, completing the familiar form of the theorem:
$$
\boxed{-\frac1n\log\mu(\xi_0^{n-1}(x))\longrightarrow h_\mu(T,\xi)
\quad\text{almost everywhere and in }L^1.}
$$

The <Rudolph measure rigidity theorem> has an essential ergodicity hypothesis. If a Borel probability measure $\mu$ on the <circle group> is invariant under both $T_2$ and $T_3$, is ergodic for the semigroup generated jointly by these maps, and either map has positive <Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy>, then
$$
\boxed{\mu=m.}
$$
Equivalently, a jointly ergodic common invariant measure other than <Lebesgue measure> has zero entropy for both maps. Joint ergodicity means that every set invariant modulo $\mu$ under both maps has measure zero or one. Positive entropy without this hypothesis is insufficient: $\tfrac12m+\tfrac12\delta_0$, with $\delta_0$ a <Dirac measure>, is a common invariant measure of positive entropy and is not $m$.

The <Host equidistribution theorem> states that if $p,q\geq2$ are relatively prime integers and $\nu$ is invariant and ergodic under $T_p$, with $h_\nu(T_p)>0$, then for $\nu$-almost every $x$ the sequence $(T_q^nx)$ is an <equidistributed sequence> for <Lebesgue measure>. Explicitly, for every continuous $g$ on the circle,
$$
\boxed{\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}g(T_q^nx)\longrightarrow\int g\,dm
\quad\text{for }\nu\text{-almost every }x.}
$$
The non-ergodic form assumes $T_p$ invariance and positive entropy for almost every component in the <ergodic decomposition> $\nu=\int\nu_\omega\,d\lambda(\omega)$. Applying the ergodic theorem of Host on each such component gives the same almost-everywhere conclusion for $\nu$. More generally, its conclusion holds on the part supported on positive-entropy components. A positive value of $h_\nu(T_p)$ alone does not eliminate zero-entropy components.

To deduce the joint version of the <Rudolph measure rigidity theorem>, suppose $h_\mu(T_2)>0$; if only $T_3$ has positive entropy, interchange the roles. Write the $T_2$ <ergodic decomposition> as $\mu=\int\nu\,d\tau(\nu)$. Since $T_3$ commutes with $T_2$, its <pushforward measure> sends a $T_2$ ergodic component $\nu$ to a $T_2$ ergodic component $(T_3)_*\nu$. On each component, $T_3$ is a <factor of a measure-preserving system> with fibres of size at most three. We use the standard <entropy preservation under a finite-to-one factor>:
$$
h_{(T_3)_*\nu}(T_2)=h_\nu(T_2).
$$
The reason for this standard entropy fact is that, conditional on a complete factor point, every finite orbit name has at most three possibilities; its conditional entropy is bounded by $\log3$, and division by the orbit length gives zero relative entropy.

The component at $T_3x$ is $(T_3)_*\nu_x$ <almost everywhere>; this follows from commutation and the componentwise ergodic averages. The component entropy function $r(x)=h_{\nu_x}(T_2)$ is therefore invariant under both $T_2$ and $T_3$. Joint ergodicity makes it constant <almost everywhere>, and <affinity of entropy under ergodic decomposition> identifies the constant as $h_\mu(T_2)>0$. Thus almost every $T_2$ component has positive entropy, exactly the condition required in the non-ergodic <Host equidistribution theorem>. It follows that $\mu$-almost every point equidistributes for $m$ under $T_3$.

For any continuous $g$, invariance under $T_3$ and the <dominated convergence theorem> now give
$$
\int g\,d\mu
=\int\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}g(T_3^nx)\,d\mu(x)
\longrightarrow\int g\,dm.
$$
Continuous functions determine Borel probability measures on the circle, so $\mu=m$, proving the deduction.

For the normal-number example, let $\omega_1,\omega_2,\ldots$ be independent fair binary digits and define
$$
\pi(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{2\omega_j}{3^j},\qquad
\nu=\pi_*\left(\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)^{\mathbb N}\right).
$$
This <Cantor Bernoulli measure> is supported on the middle-third <Cantor set> $C$. If $S$ is the <Bernoulli shift>, then $T_3\circ\pi=\pi\circ S$ on the circle. Consequently $\nu$ is $T_3$ invariant and ergodic: a $T_3$ invariant event pulls back to an $S$ invariant event, which has probability zero or one.

Take the ternary digit <measurable partition> $\eta=\{[0,1/3),[1/3,2/3),[2/3,1)\}$. Its block partition of length $n$ has, up to null endpoints, $2^n$ positive-measure atoms under $\nu$, each of measure $2^{-n}$, and all other atoms have measure zero. Hence
$$
H_\nu(\eta_0^{n-1})=n\log2,\qquad
h_\nu(T_3)\geq h_\nu(T_3,\eta)=\log2>0.
$$
Apply the <Host equidistribution theorem> with $p=3$, $q=2$. For $\nu$-almost every $x\in C$, the sequence $(T_2^nx)$ equidistributes for <Lebesgue measure>, so $x$ is a <normal number> in base $2$ by <normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication>.

The ternary expansion of $\nu$-almost every such $x$ contains only $0$ and $2$, so the frequency of digit $1$ is zero rather than $1/3$. The ambiguous ternary endpoints form a countable $\nu$ null set and can be removed. Therefore $x$ is not a <normal number> in base $3$. We have proved the stronger almost-everywhere existence statement
$$
\boxed{\nu\{x\in C\cap[0,1):x\text{ is normal in base }2\text{ and not normal in base }3\}=1.}
$$
