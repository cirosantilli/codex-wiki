Write $Z(u)$ for the <continuous-time Markov chain> and $\mathbb E_r$ for <expected value> conditional on $Z(0)=r$. The <occupation time of a continuous-time Markov chain> in state $s$ is the integral of its <indicator random variable>. By <Tonelli theorem>,
$$
\boxed{T_{rs}(t)=\mathbb E_r\!\left[\int_0^t\mathbf1\{Z(u)=s\}\,du\right]
=\int_0^t p_{rs}(u)\,du.}
$$
The interchange is valid because the integrand is nonnegative; indeed the random integral is bounded by $t$.

Let $N(u)$ count all jumps of the <continuous-time Markov chain>. Its <Markov-chain entry count> into state $s$ is
$$
N_s(t)=\sum_{0<u\leq t}\mathbf1\{Z(u-)\ne s,\ Z(u)=s\}
=\int_{(0,t]}\mathbf1\{Z(u)=s\}\,dN(u).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{E_{rs}(t)=\mathbb E_r\!\left[\int_{(0,t]}\mathbf1\{Z(u)=s\}\,dN(u)\right].}
$$
This is a <Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral> against a <counting process>, so each jump contributes one. The initial occupation of state $s$ is not counted as an entry. An ordinary time integral of an <indicator random variable> asserting a jump at exactly time $u$ would be zero, since individual jump times have zero <Lebesgue measure>. The requested integral must therefore use this counting-measure interpretation. Assume non-explosion and finite expected jump counts on bounded intervals, as holds for a finite-state <continuous-time Markov chain>.

For $i\ne s$, the <Markov property> and the definition of <transition intensity> give
$$
\mathbb P_r\{Z(u)=i,\text{ a jump }i\to s\text{ in }(u,u+du]\}
=p_{ri}(u)q_{is}\,du+o(du).
$$
Summing over the possible pre-jump states gives the density of the expected <Markov-chain entry count>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{du}\mathbb E_rN_s(u)=\sum_{i\ne s}p_{ri}(u)q_{is}.}
$$
For a finite-state <continuous-time Markov chain>, the chance of two or more jumps in this short interval is $o(du)$. There is no $q_{ss}$ contribution: diagonal entries of the <infinitesimal generator> encode exit rates, not jumps into the same state. With repeated entries possible, this density is an expected event rate rather than the <probability density function> of a single normalized random time.

Integrate the expected <Markov-chain entry count> density and use the <occupation time of a continuous-time Markov chain> formula:
$$
\boxed{E_{rs}(t)=\int_0^t\sum_{i\ne s}p_{ri}(u)q_{is}\,du
=\sum_{i\ne s}q_{is}T_{ri}(t).}
$$
Each term is a <transition intensity> multiplied by the expected exposure time to that transition. This counts every entry, including returns, rather than only the first visit. For countably many states the same nonnegative interchange follows from <Tonelli theorem>, provided the resulting expected count is finite. The sum excludes $i=s$.

$$
q_{12}(A)=a e^{\beta_{12}(A-50)},\qquad
q_{23}(A)=b e^{\beta_{23}(A-50)}.
$$
For a closed-form <panel-observed multi-state likelihood>, freeze the age covariate at the start of each observation interval. The <transition intensities> are then $(a,b)$ over the first interval and $(\lambda,\mu)=(ae^{5\beta_{12}},be^{5\beta_{23}})$ over the second. Conditional on the initial state, the <likelihood contribution> is
$$
\boxed{L(a,b,\beta_{12},\beta_{23})
=e^{-5a}\left[1-e^{-ae^{5\beta_{12}}}
-p_{12}\!\left(1\mid ae^{5\beta_{12}},be^{5\beta_{23}}\right)\right].}
$$
Indeed, $p_{11}(5)=e^{-5a}$ and $p_{13}(1)=1-p_{11}(1)-p_{12}(1)$. In this irreversible <continuous-time Markov chain>, the intermediate state has probability
$$
p_{12}(u\mid\lambda,\mu)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(e^{-\lambda u}-e^{-\mu u})}{\mu-\lambda},&\lambda\ne\mu,\\
\lambda u e^{-\lambda u},&\lambda=\mu.
\end{cases}
$$
The second expression is the continuous limit of the first and handles equal rates. The observed two-state jump across an interval is compatible with adjacent instantaneous transitions: an unobserved visit to state 2 occurs between the examinations.

The four-parameter <panel-observed multi-state likelihood> requires the following additional assumptions.

* \b[Irreversibility:] the <chronic disease> can progress but cannot improve, so $q_{21}=q_{32}=0$ and state 3 is absorbing. Merely permitting transitions between adjacent states does not impose this; allowing regression would require additional <transition intensities>.
* \b[Piecewise-constant age:] the current age is held at 50 and 55 over the respective observation intervals. Actual age changes continuously, and a five-year interval can be too long for this approximation when the age effect is substantial.
* \b[Log-linear age effects and common rates:] the specified age functions apply to the patient, without unmodelled heterogeneity or other changing covariates.
* \b[Observation mechanism:] disease states are recorded without misclassification, and examination times or dropout carry no additional information about the underlying process once the modelled history is given. A new visit only one year after a five-year gap might have been prompted by worsening symptoms, making the observation schedule informative.

If age is instead updated continuously, the model is time-inhomogeneous and the displayed homogeneous transition formula is not exact. For example, set
$$
\Lambda(u,v)=\int_u^v q_{12}(A)\,dA,\qquad M(u,v)=\int_u^v q_{23}(A)\,dA.
$$
Then the exact irreversible <likelihood contribution> is
$$
L=e^{-\Lambda(50,55)}\left[1-e^{-\Lambda(55,56)}
-\int_{55}^{56}e^{-\Lambda(55,u)}q_{12}(u)e^{-M(u,56)}\,du\right].
$$
Thus \b[the simple closed form uses an explicit frozen-age approximation]; adjacency alone and continuously evolving age do not determine that form.

Put $v_k=p_k(1-p_k)$ and assume $0<p_k<1$. For fixed $n$ and a fixed nonzero difference $\Delta=p_0-p_1$, the approximate <statistical power> of the <Wald test> increases as its noncentrality $|\Delta|/\sqrt V$ increases, where
$$
V=\frac{v_0}{n_0}+\frac{v_1}{n-n_0}.
$$
Differentiating with respect to the continuous allocation gives
$$
\frac{dV}{dn_0}=-\frac{v_0}{n_0^2}+\frac{v_1}{n_1^2}=0,\qquad
\frac{d^2V}{dn_0^2}=\frac{2v_0}{n_0^3}+\frac{2v_1}{n_1^3}>0.
$$
Hence the unique minimum and its associated minimum <variance> are
$$
\boxed{R^*=\frac{n_0}{n_1}=\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{p_1(1-p_1)}},\qquad
V_{\min}=\frac{(\sqrt{v_0}+\sqrt{v_1})^2}{n}.}
$$
This is <Neyman allocation>. Integer allocations use a feasible adjacent integer to the continuous optimum, comparing the resulting <variances>; $n_0,n_1\geq1$ must still hold. It is a large-sample <normal approximation> argument, not an exact finite-sample power theorem for arbitrary <response-adaptive randomization>. Under the null $p_0=p_1$, the approximate power is the <significance level> for any allocation, although the same allocation still minimizes the stated <variance>.

The difference of the <Bernoulli distribution> variances factors as

v_0-v_1=p_0(1-p_0)-p_1(1-p_1)
=(p_0-p_1)(1-p_0-p_1).

Therefore \b[<Neyman allocation> assigns more patients to treatment 1, the inferior treatment]. Minimizing the <variance> of a treatment comparison and maximizing the number of desirable responses are different objectives.

For the <randomized play-the-winner rule> RPW$(1,1)$, return the drawn ball and add one ball of the same treatment after a success or of the opposite treatment after a failure. Treatment 0 has three successes and two failures, and treatment 1 has one success and three failures. The urn therefore has $1+3+3=7$ treatment-0 balls and $1+1+2=4$ treatment-1 balls. Hence

\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{treatment }0)=\frac7{11}.}

For <dynamic programming> with only the final patient left, there is no future value from learning. $$
This is optimal for expected successes with no imposed lower bound on the <randomization> probabilities.

A second opportunity to reject raises the trial's overall <Type I error> above the nominal 10% in general. If $A$ and $B$ denote rejection at the first and second analyses, the overall error is
$$
\mathbb P_0(A)+\mathbb P_0(A^c\cap B).
$$
The cumulative-data <test statistics> are correlated, so neither 20% nor $1-0.9^2=19\%$ is the general answer. The latter requires independent tests, which is not the situation here.

\b[The rejection thresholds and the stopping/continuation rule must be calibrated jointly.] A prespecified <group sequential design> or <alpha-spending function> can allocate a total error budget of 10% across the analyses, using their joint null distribution. A conservative alternative planned in advance is two tests each at 5%, controlled by the <Bonferroni inequality>. An unplanned change to a design with a remaining error budget can be justified by the <conditional error principle>. Here, however, an original single-look test at 10% has no remaining conditional rejection probability after its final nonrejection. Keeping its entire original rejection region and adding a new positive-probability rejection opportunity cannot in general retain a 10% overall error rate. The two-look rule therefore needs prospective joint calibration; an already observed result cannot retrospectively create an unused error budget.

Let $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ be the <potential outcomes> for the same individual under well-defined interventions setting the binary <exposure> to 1 and 0. The <average treatment effect>, also called the average <causal effect> on the additive scale, is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ACE}=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)]=\mathbb E[Y(1)]-\mathbb E[Y(0)].}
$$
The average is over the target population. This compares hypothetical outcomes under two interventions, rather than the observed difference $\mathbb E[Y\mid X=1]-\mathbb E[Y\mid X=0]$, which can be distorted by <confounding>. For binary $Y$, the displayed contrast is a causal risk difference. <Consistency in causal inference> and a well-defined intervention, including an appropriate no-<interference in causal inference> assumption, give these <potential outcomes> their meaning.

An <instrumental variable> $G$ supplies variation in an <exposure> $B$ that can be used for <causal inference> about its effect on $Y$. Its three core requirements are:

* \b[<Instrument relevance>:] $G$ changes the distribution of $B$; in a simple linear model this requires a nonzero first-stage association.
* \b[<Instrumental-variable independence>:] $G$ is independent of unmeasured common causes of $B$ and $Y$, or independent of the relevant <potential outcomes> conditional on specified measured covariates.
* \b[<Exclusion restriction>:] interventions on $G$ affect $Y$ only through $B$, with no other causal pathway. In <potential outcomes> notation, $Y(b,g)=Y(b)$.

\b[Relevance, independence, and exclusion are the core instrumental-variable assumptions.] They do not by themselves identify an arbitrary population-average effect. For a binary instrument and treatment, <instrumental-variable monotonicity> and the usual <consistency in causal inference> assumptions can identify a <local average treatment effect>; stronger effect-homogeneity or structural-model assumptions are needed for other effect interpretations.

Under the displayed <causal directed acyclic graphs>, with nonzero effects along the relevant arrows and no exact cancellation of the effects on $B$, \b[the valid <instrumental variables> are scenarios (i), (ii), and (iv)]. The path arguments are:

* In (i), $G\to B$ supplies <instrument relevance>, and every directed path from $G$ to $Y$ goes through $B$. The unmeasured $U$ does not cause $G$, so <instrumental-variable independence> holds.
* In (ii), $G\to A\to B$ supplies <instrument relevance>. Although $A$ is unmeasured, every directed path from $G$ to $Y$ still passes through $B$. The path $G\to A\leftarrow U\to Y$ is blocked at its collider $A$ without conditioning, so it does not violate <instrumental-variable independence>.
* In (iii), $G\to C\to Y$ bypasses $B$, violating the <exclusion restriction>. There is also no directed effect of $G$ on $B$, so <instrument relevance> is absent in this graph.
* In (iv), both $G\to B$ and $G\to A\to B$ affect $B$, and all directed paths to $Y$ still pass through $B$. The unconditioned collider $G\to A\leftarrow U$ blocks the route to $U$, so both <instrumental-variable independence> and the <exclusion restriction> hold. Conditioning on $A$ could introduce <collider bias>, but $A$ is not being conditioned on here.
* In (v), $G\to C\to Y$ bypasses $B$, so the <exclusion restriction> fails despite the additional $G\to B$ arrow.
* In (vi), $G\to U\to Y$ bypasses $B$, and $G$ affects the unmeasured common cause $U$. Thus the <exclusion restriction> and the stated <instrumental-variable independence> requirement fail.

An upstream intermediate variable need not invalidate an <instrumental variable> if its routes to the outcome all pass through the target <exposure>; an outcome intermediate reached without that <exposure> does invalidate it.

For the electronic-record <observational study>, three strengths are:

* The large dataset improves precision of the estimated <statistical association> and allows detection of associations too small for a modest study.
* Adjusting for age, sex, and ancestry addresses specified measured sources of <confounding>.
* Routinely collected <hypercalcaemia> and <migraine> diagnoses give evidence from clinical practice, rather than depending solely on recalled <calcium intake>.

Three weaknesses are:

* Residual <confounding> remains possible: for example, other diseases, medicines, or health behaviours may affect both <serum calcium> and <migraine>.
* Co-occurrence does not establish temporal order, leaving <reverse causality> possible. <Migraine>, its management, or the decision to investigate it may alter recorded <serum calcium>.
* <Selection bias> and diagnostic misclassification can arise because patients enter health records and receive testing nonrandomly. Moreover, <hypercalcaemia> is not a direct measure of dietary <calcium intake>, limiting the claimed intervention's interpretation.

For <two-sample Mendelian randomization>, three strengths are:

* <Genetic variants> are inherited before adult <migraine> develops, making ordinary disease-to-genotype <reverse causality> implausible.
* Under <instrumental-variable independence>, genetic assignment reduces the environmental <confounding> that undermines a conventional <observational study>.
* The large outcome sample and combined <genetic risk score> improve precision, while concordant <weighted-median Mendelian randomization estimator> and <MR-Egger regression> results offer checks under different assumptions about <horizontal pleiotropy>.

Three weaknesses are:

* <Horizontal pleiotropy> can violate the <exclusion restriction>, while <population stratification> or inherited family effects can violate <instrumental-variable independence>. In the supplied figure, one variant has a much larger <serum calcium> association than the others, motivating a check for dependence on that single variant.
* The score explains only 1.25% of <serum calcium> <variance>, so instrument strength must be assessed rather than assumed. Different populations in the two samples can also undermine transport of genetic associations. A small variance fraction is not itself proof of a <weak instrument> in a large sample.
* A lifelong genetically influenced shift in <serum calcium> is not equivalent to changing dietary <calcium intake>. The robustness methods also need assumptions: the <weighted-median Mendelian randomization estimator> needs more than half of the weight from valid instruments, and <MR-Egger regression> needs the <InSIDE assumption>. The nonsignificant <MR-Egger regression> intercept is compatible with zero average directional <horizontal pleiotropy>, but with eight variants it cannot prove all instruments valid.

Thus \b[the agreement supports the hypothesis, but does not establish the effect of a dietary intervention].

The <survivor function> of a nonnegative event time is
$$
\boxed{F(t)=\mathbb P(T>t)=\int_t^\infty f_T(u)\,du.}
$$
Here the paper uses $F$ for the <survival function>, rather than for the <cumulative distribution function>; the latter is $1-F(t)$ for a continuous distribution. The <survivor function> is nonincreasing, starts at 1 when there is no atom at zero, and tends to zero for an almost surely finite event time.

With no <censoring> or new entry within the interval, all $r$ individuals can be classified at its end: $d$ have experienced the event and $r-d$ remain event-free. Thus the empirical conditional <survival probability> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat{\mathbb P}(T>t_R\mid T>t_L)=\frac{r-d}{r}=1-\frac dr.}

This assumes $r>0$ and that the observed individuals represent the group whose conditional <survival probability> is being estimated.

An individual is <right-censored> at $t$ when follow-up establishes that the event has not occurred through $t$, but does not reveal the subsequent event time. The observed information is $T>t$ for the continuous-time convention.

Knowing only the total number censored inside an interval does not specify the <risk sets> at the event times. For example, start with two individuals, one event and one censoring. If censoring precedes the event, the <Kaplan–Meier estimator> contributes $1-1/1=0$; if the event precedes censoring, it contributes $1-1/2=1/2$. The totals $r=2,d=1,c=1$ are identical.

Therefore \b[the timing of <right censoring> relative to the events is needed]. <Independent censoring> is also required for these changing <risk sets> to yield a valid estimate of the underlying <survivor function>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $d_j$ be the number of events at potential time $a_j$ and let $r_j$ be the size of the <risk set> immediately before that time, including observations censored at $a_j$. Define the discrete conditional event probability
$$
q_j=\mathbb P(T=a_j\mid T\geq a_j).
$$
Under <independent censoring>, and ignoring the separate censoring distribution, the <survival likelihood> factorizes as
$$
L(\{q_j\})\propto\prod_j q_j^{d_j}(1-q_j)^{r_j-d_j}.
$$
This follows by multiplying a survival factor for each individual at risk who does not fail at $a_j$, and an event factor for each failure. The log factor $d_j\log q_j+(r_j-d_j)\log(1-q_j)$ is maximized at $\widehat q_j=d_j/r_j$, with the corresponding boundary values when $d_j=0$ or $d_j=r_j$.

The <survival function> is the product of successive conditional survival probabilities. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\widehat F(t)=\prod_{a_j\leq t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{r_j}\right).}
$$
This is the <Kaplan–Meier estimator>. A product over no event times is 1. If everyone remaining experiences an event, the product becomes zero and stays zero; subsequent empty <risk sets> supply no additional factor.

Choose the <potential right-censoring times> to include every observed <right censoring> time up to $t$, and also the endpoints 0 and $t$. Then no individual is censored strictly inside $(c_{k-1},c_k)$. Use an explicit endpoint convention: let $r_k$ count individuals still event-free after any events at $c_k$, but before censoring at $c_k$. If $d_k$ events occur in $(c_{k-1},c_k]$, the number at the beginning of that interval, after censoring at its left endpoint, is $r_k+d_k$.

The no-censoring interval estimate from the preceding parts is therefore $r_k/(r_k+d_k)$. The possible event mass at zero contributes $r_0/(r_0+d_0)$. Multiplying yields
$$
\boxed{\widetilde F(t)=\frac{r_0}{r_0+d_0}\prod_{k=1}^h\frac{r_k}{r_k+d_k}.}
$$
For a continuous event-time distribution, normally $d_0=0$. Within interval $k$, put $b_k=r_k+d_k$ and let its successive event counts be $e_1,\ldots,e_m$. Its <Kaplan–Meier estimator> factors telescope:
$$
\prod_{j=1}^m\left(1-\frac{e_j}{b_k-\sum_{\ell<j}e_\ell}\right)
=\frac{b_k-\sum_j e_j}{b_k}=\frac{r_k}{r_k+d_k}.
$$
The same calculation at zero proves
$$
\boxed{\widetilde F(t)=\widehat F(t).}
$$
The paper's phrase “at risk at $c_k$” does not specify its side of an event at that endpoint. If it denotes the conventional pre-event <risk set> size $r_k^-$ instead, and $e_k$ events occur exactly at $c_k$, substitute $r_k=r_k^--e_k$. The interval factor is then $(r_k^--e_k)/(r_k^-+d_k-e_k)$; at zero it is $(r_0^--d_0)/r_0^-$. This keeps the events-before-censoring convention consistent. After an empty <risk set>, stop the product; if the fitted survival has reached zero, retain zero rather than creating a $0/0$ factor.

Define the event <counting process> and <at-risk process> by
$$
N_i(u)=v_i\mathbf1\{x_i\leq u\},\qquad Y_i(u)=\mathbf1\{x_i\geq u\},\qquad
N(u)=\sum_iN_i(u),\quad Y(u)=\sum_iY_i(u).
$$
Under a common <hazard function> and <independent censoring>, the conditional event rate is $Y(u)h(u)$. Equivalently, the <counting-process compensator> satisfies $\mathbb E[dN(u)\mid\mathcal F_{u-}]=Y(u)dH(u)$. Estimate a <cumulative hazard> increment by the observed event increment divided by the current <risk set>:
$$
d\widehat H(u)=\frac{dN(u)}{Y(u)}.
$$
With no ties, $dN$ has mass $v_i$ at $x_i$, and $Y(x_i)=\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}1$. Thus the <Nelson–Aalen estimator> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat H(t)=\int_0^t\frac{dN(u)}{Y(u)}
=\sum_{i:x_i\leq t}\frac{v_i}{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}1}.}
$$
Only event times contribute, so no empty <risk set> is divided into an event count. <Right-censored> observations contribute time to the <risk sets>, but no event increment.

Let $R_i=\{j:x_j\geq x_i\}$ be the <risk set> at an observed event time $x_i$. In the <Cox proportional-hazards model>, conditional on one member of this set failing then and on the preceding history, the probability that it is individual $i$ is
$$
\frac{e^{\beta z_i}h_0(x_i)}{\sum_{j\in R_i}e^{\beta z_j}h_0(x_i)}
=\frac{e^{\beta z_i}}{\sum_{j\in R_i}e^{\beta z_j}}.
$$
The <baseline hazard> cancels. Multiplying these conditional contributions gives the <Cox partial likelihood>
$$
\boxed{L_p(\beta)=\prod_{i:v_i=1}\frac{e^{\beta z_i}}{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}e^{\beta z_j}}.}
$$
The no-ties assumption avoids the need for a tied-event approximation. This construction assumes <independent censoring> conditional on the modelled covariates. The printed phrase calling $z$ a scalar parameter is read as a scalar observed covariate $z_i$; $\beta$ is the unknown regression parameter.

Taking logarithms of the <Cox partial likelihood> gives
$$
S(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n v_i\left[\beta z_i-\log\left(\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}e^{\beta z_j}\right)\right].
$$
Differentiate each term, using the <chain rule> for the logarithm:
$$
\boxed{S'(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n v_i\left[z_i-
\frac{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}z_j e^{\beta z_j}}{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}e^{\beta z_j}}\right].}
$$
This <score function> is the sum over failures of the observed covariate minus its current risk-weighted mean. Define that mean as $\overline z_\beta(x_i)$; observations with $v_i=0$ have no direct event term, although they occur in the relevant <risk sets>.

= Solution

Within the <risk set> $R_i$, the <Cox proportional-hazards model> makes the conditional failure probabilities proportional to $e^{\beta z_j}$. Thus
$$
\mathbb E_\beta[z_{\text{failing individual}}\mid\mathcal F_{x_i-},\text{one event at }x_i]
=\overline z_\beta(x_i)
=\frac{\sum_{j\in R_i}z_je^{\beta z_j}}{\sum_{j\in R_i}e^{\beta z_j}}.
$$
The <score function> sums observed minus expected covariates at event times. At a finite interior maximum of the <Cox partial likelihood>,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i:v_i=1}z_i=\sum_{i:v_i=1}\overline z_{\widehat\beta}(x_i).}
$$
The balance holds in aggregate, not separately at every event. The log <partial likelihood> is concave because
$$
S''(\beta)=-\sum_{i:v_i=1}\operatorname{Var}_\beta(z\mid R_i)\leq0.
$$
When there is informative covariate variation in the <risk sets>, this gives a unique finite solution if it exists. A completely separated event pattern can instead have its maximum only as $\beta\to\pm\infty$; the question's finite $\widehat\beta$ implicitly excludes that case.

In the <Cox proportional-hazards model>, replace the number at risk in the <Nelson–Aalen estimator> by the sum of relative hazards:
$$
W_\beta(u)=\sum_jY_j(u)e^{\beta z_j}.
$$
The total event <counting process> has conditional rate $W_\beta(u)h_0(u)$, so the estimated <baseline hazard> increment is $dN(u)/W_{\widehat\beta}(u)$. Integrating gives the <Breslow estimator>
$$
\boxed{\widehat H_0(t)=\sum_{i:x_i\leq t}\frac{v_i}{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_i}e^{\widehat\beta z_j}}.}

When $\widehat\beta=0$, all relative hazards are 1 and this reduces to the <Nelson–Aalen estimator>. The denominator is positive at each observed event time.

In the <martingale residual>
$$
y_i=v_i-e^{\widehat\beta z_i}\widehat H_0(x_i),
$$
$v_i$ is the observed event count up to the end of the individual's follow-up: 1 for a recorded event and 0 for <right censoring>. The second term is the fitted <counting-process compensator>, obtained by integrating that individual's fitted <hazard function> while they remain at risk:
$$
\int_0^{x_i}e^{\widehat\beta z_i}\,d\widehat H_0(u)
=e^{\widehat\beta z_i}\widehat H_0(x_i).
$$
Thus \b[the residual is observed events minus the fitted cumulative event intensity over follow-up]. The fitted intensity is not an event probability and can exceed 1. Positive values indicate more events than predicted over that observed exposure; negative values indicate fewer. A <right-censored> individual has a nonpositive residual. With true model parameters and <independent censoring>, the underlying observed-minus-compensator process is a <martingale>; fitted terminal residuals are not independent identically distributed errors.

Insert the <Breslow estimator> into the fitted cumulative intensities and interchange the finite sums:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^n e^{\widehat\beta z_i}\widehat H_0(x_i)
&=\sum_{i=1}^n e^{\widehat\beta z_i}
\sum_{\ell:x_\ell\leq x_i}\frac{v_\ell}{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_\ell}e^{\widehat\beta z_j}}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=1}^n
\frac{v_\ell\sum_{i:x_i\geq x_\ell}e^{\widehat\beta z_i}}
{\sum_{j:x_j\geq x_\ell}e^{\widehat\beta z_j}}
=\sum_{\ell=1}^n v_\ell.
\end{aligned}
$$
Every observed event contributes exactly one after summing its fitted contribution over its <risk set>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^n y_i=\sum_i v_i-\sum_i e^{\widehat\beta z_i}\widehat H_0(x_i)=0.}
$$
This identity is algebraic and holds for any finite coefficient used consistently in the relative hazards and the <Breslow estimator>; it does not require the <score equation> to be solved exactly. It can fail if the <baseline hazard> is estimated or smoothed by a different procedure.

Condition on the event time $T_A=u$. <Independence> makes the chance that $T_B$ is still later equal to its <survivor function> $F_B(u)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\pi_A=\mathbb P(T_A<T_B)=\int_0^\infty f_A(u)F_B(u)\,du.}
$$
The continuous distributions give zero probability of a tie. The complementary probability is $\pi_B=\int_0^\infty F_A(u)f_B(u)\,du=1-\pi_A$.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the <survivor functions>, $F_A'(u)=-f_A(u)$ and $F_B'(u)=-f_B(u)$. The <product rule> gives
$$
\frac{d}{du}[F_A(u)F_B(u)]=-f_A(u)F_B(u)-F_A(u)f_B(u).
$$
Integrating from 0 to $t$, using $F_A(0)=F_B(0)=1$, yields
$$
\boxed{\int_0^t f_A(u)F_B(u)\,du+\int_0^t F_A(u)f_B(u)\,du+F_A(t)F_B(t)=1.}
$$
The three terms partition the possible histories: A occurs first by time $t$, B occurs first by time $t$, or neither has occurred by $t$. The first two are the <cumulative incidence functions> for the two <competing risks>, while the third is the <survival probability> of their minimum. The initial value assumes nonnegative event times with no atom at zero.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $\pi_A=\mathbb P(T_A<T_B)$, and assume $0<\pi_A<1$ so that both conditional distributions are defined. By <independence>, the joint density of $T_A=u$ and $T_A<T_B$ is $f_A(u)F_B(u)$, whereas the joint density of $T_A=u$ and $T_B<T_A$ is $f_A(u)[1-F_B(u)]$. Normalizing gives
$$
\boxed{f_{T_A\mid T_A<T_B}(u)=\frac{f_A(u)F_B(u)}{\pi_A},\qquad
f_{T_A\mid T_B<T_A}(u)=\frac{f_A(u)[1-F_B(u)]}{1-\pi_A}.}
$$
Both are densities of the latent event time $T_A$. The second is not the density of the observed first event $T_B$ in cases where B wins. Their integrals are 1 by the definitions of $\pi_A$ and its complement.

The minimum exceeds $t$ exactly when both times exceed $t$. By <independence>,
$$
\mathbb P(X>t)=F_A(t)F_B(t).
$$
Differentiating its <survivor function> gives
$$
\boxed{f_X(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}[F_A(t)F_B(t)]
=f_A(t)F_B(t)+F_A(t)f_B(t).}
$$
The <tail integral formula for moments> then gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\int_0^\infty F_A(t)F_B(t)\,dt.}
$$
Equivalently, <integration by parts> in $\int_0^R t f_X(t)\,dt$ gives $-RF_A(R)F_B(R)+\int_0^R F_A(t)F_B(t)\,dt$. The assumed $tF_B(t)\to0$ makes the boundary term vanish, since $0\leq F_A\leq1$.

The stated tail condition does not itself guarantee a finite mean. For example, the proper <survivor functions>
$$
F_B(t)=\frac1{(1+t)\log(e+t)},\qquad
F_A(t)=\frac1{\log(e+\log(1+t))}
$$
satisfy $tF_B(t)\to0$, but their product behaves as $1/[t\log t\log\log t]$, whose integral diverges. Thus the displayed expectation is valid as an extended nonnegative value; finiteness needs integrability of the product. The <exponential distribution> in the next part guarantees that finiteness.

= Solution
{parent=v}

For the <exponential distribution> with mean $\mu$, $f_A(t)=F_A(t)/\mu$ and $F_A(t)=e^{-t/\mu}$. Therefore
$$
\mathbb P(T_A<T_B)=\frac1\mu\int_0^\infty F_A(t)F_B(t)\,dt
=\frac{\mathbb E X}{\mu}.
$$
Since ties have probability zero, $\mathbb P(X=T_B)=1-\mathbb E X/\mu$. Taking <expected values> in the definition of $U$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E U=\mathbb E X+\mu\mathbb P(X=T_B)
=\mathbb E X+\mu-\mathbb E X=\mu=\mathbb E T_A.}
$$
There is also an imputation interpretation. If A is observed first, $T_A=X$; if B censors A, <memorylessness of the exponential distribution> and <independence> imply that the remaining mean lifetime is $\mu$. Thus $U$ is the <conditional expectation> of $T_A$ given the observed minimum and censoring indicator. The <tower property of conditional expectation> gives the same equality. This does not say that $U$ and $T_A$ have the same distribution.
