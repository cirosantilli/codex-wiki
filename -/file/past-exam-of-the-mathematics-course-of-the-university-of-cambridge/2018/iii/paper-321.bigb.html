= Solution
{parent=a}

The <specific orbital energy> released in a <black-hole accretion> flow reaching an inner radius $r_{\rm in}$ is of order $GM/r_{\rm in}$. For a circular <Keplerian orbit>, the binding energy is more precisely $GM/(2r_{\rm in})$, with the other half of the potential-energy decrease retained as orbital <kinetic energy>. Multiplying by the <mass accretion rate> gives the <accretion luminosity> scale
$$
L_{\rm acc}\sim\frac{GM\dot M}{r_{\rm in}}.
$$
For an inner radius of order the <Schwarzschild radius>, $r_{\rm in}\sim2GM/c^2$, this becomes
$$
\boxed{L_{\rm acc}\sim\dot M c^2.}
$$
This is an energy-release scaling, not unit <radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion>. The actual inner orbit, stresses and advected energy determine the coefficient and the emitted luminosity; the <innermost stable circular orbit> of a <Schwarzschild black hole> lies outside its horizon.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $v>0$ to mean the magnitude of the inward radial velocity. In the <Gaussian units> used for the <magnetic pressure>, approximate the <surface density of a disk> by $\Sigma\simeq2H\rho$. The <mass accretion rate> and <free-fall speed> then give
$$
\rho\simeq\frac{\dot M}{4\pi rHv}=\frac{\dot M}{4\pi\epsilon r^2v},\qquad
v=\left(\frac{2GM}{r}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
At the <magnetospheric truncation radius>, balance the <magnetic pressure> of the <magnetic dipole field> with the specified <ram pressure>:
$$
\frac{\mu^2}{8\pi r_m^6}\simeq\frac12\rho v^2\simeq\frac{\dot M v}{8\pi\epsilon r_m^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
r_m^{7/2}\simeq\frac{\epsilon\mu^2}{\dot M\sqrt{2GM}},\qquad
r_m\simeq\left(\frac{\epsilon^2\mu^4}{2GM\dot M^2}\right)^{1/7}.
$$
Discarding the convention-dependent numerical factor gives
$$
\boxed{r_m\sim\left(\frac{\epsilon^2\mu^4}{GM\dot M^2}\right)^{1/7}.}
$$
The <magnetic pressure> grows inward as $r^{-6}$, faster than the <ram pressure> $\propto r^{-5/2}$, so the field disrupts the flow inside this radius. The numerical coefficient depends on the vertical averaging and on treating the inflow as radial <free fall>, as stipulated here; a detailed <thin disk> boundary model need not have the same coefficient.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <corotation radius> is where the stellar spin matches <Keplerian rotation>:
$$
r_{\rm co}=\left(\frac{GM}{\Omega_m^2}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
A parcel forced into corotation at $r_m$ has outward <centrifugal acceleration> $\Omega_m^2r_m$. This exceeds the inward <gravitational acceleration> when
$$
\boxed{r_m>r_{\rm co}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Omega_m>\Omega_K(r_m).}
$$
This is the onset condition for a centrifugal <propeller effect>. It does not by itself guarantee escape to infinity. For example, a parcel released with only azimuthal speed $\Omega_m r_m$ has nonnegative <specific orbital energy> only if
$$
\frac12\Omega_m^2r_m^2-\frac{GM}{r_m}\geq0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r_m\geq2^{1/3}r_{\rm co}.
$$
Additional magnetic work, radial motion or <pressure> can alter that escape condition.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <magnetic torque> transfers <angular momentum> from the faster rotator to the slower one. When $\Omega_m>\Omega_K(r_m)$, the <neutron star> supplies <angular momentum> to the inner <accretion disk> and possible <propeller flow>, so it tends to \b[spin down]. When the inner <accretion disk> rotates faster than the star, the magnetic connection and accreted <angular momentum> tend to \b[spin it up].

For slowly varying <mass accretion rate> and <magnetic dipole moment>, these tendencies favour a <magnetic spin equilibrium> with the <corotation radius> near the <magnetospheric truncation radius>:
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{\rm eq}\sim\left(\frac{GM}{r_m^3}\right)^{1/2},\qquad
P_{\rm eq}\sim\frac{2\pi\epsilon^{3/7}\mu^{6/7}}{(GM)^{5/7}\dot M^{3/7}}.}
$$
The precise balance depends on accreted and ejected <angular momentum> and on the extent of magnetic coupling across the <accretion disk>; corotation is a scaling estimate, not an exact zero-torque theorem.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Use $\dot M>0$ for inward <mass accretion rate>, and $T_m>0$ for <magnetic torque> adding <angular momentum> to the disk. Combining the <continuity equation> with <conservation of angular momentum> identifies the inward mass flux as $(dh/dr)^{-1}d\mathcal G/dr$. In a steady <Keplerian accretion disk>, this is the supplied constant $\dot M$, so
$$
\frac{d\mathcal G}{dr}=\dot M\frac{dh}{dr},\qquad
\mathcal G(r)=T_m+\dot M(h-h_{\rm in}),\qquad h=\sqrt{GMr}.
$$
The <boundary condition> is $\mathcal G(r_{\rm in})=T_m$. Since $d\Omega/dr=-3\Omega/(2r)$, the <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is $\mathcal G=3\pi\bar\nu\Sigma h$. Hence the <steady Keplerian accretion disk with an inner torque> has
$$
\boxed{\Sigma(r)=\frac{\dot M}{3\pi\bar\nu}\left[1+(\lambda-1)\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}r\right)^{1/2}\right],\qquad
\lambda=\frac{T_m}{\dot M h_{\rm in}}.}
$$
In particular, $\Sigma(r_{\rm in})=\lambda\dot M/(3\pi\bar\nu)$. For a physical steady solution with this <boundary condition>, $\lambda\geq0$.

For $0\leq\lambda\ll1$, the <surface density of a disk> rises from a small inner value toward $\dot M/(3\pi\bar\nu)$: this is the usual <Keplerian accretion disk> with an approximately <zero-torque inner boundary condition>. For $\lambda\gg1$, the inner <surface density of a disk> is large and decreases approximately as $r^{-1/2}$, giving a <torque-dominated accretion disk>. This approximation applies where $r/r_{\rm in}\ll(\lambda-1)^2$; sufficiently far out, any fixed finite $\lambda$ again approaches the same constant asymptote.

\Image[paper-321-surface-density.png]
{title=Steady surface-density profiles with weak and strong inner torques}

The constant net inward <angular-momentum flux> is
$$
\dot M h-\mathcal G=\dot M h_{\rm in}(1-\lambda).
$$
For $\lambda>1$, net <angular momentum> moves outward. The strong-torque profile is therefore <decretion disk>-like in its stress-dominated structure, but the specified mass flux remains inward: a genuine net <decretion disk> requires an outward mass flux, not merely large $\lambda$.

At $r_{\rm in}=r_m$, the <Maxwell stress tensor> estimate gives
$$
T_m\sim\frac{\epsilon\mu^2}{4\pi r_m^3},\qquad
\lambda\sim\frac{\epsilon\mu^2}{4\pi\dot M\sqrt{GM}\,r_m^{7/2}}.
$$
Using the <magnetospheric truncation radius> scaling in part (b), all dimensional parameters cancel:
$$
\boxed{\lambda\sim1\quad\text{up to geometric numerical factors}.}
$$
If the displayed $4\pi$ is retained and the scaling radius is assigned unit coefficient, the estimate is $1/(4\pi)$; retaining $\Sigma\simeq2H\rho$ and exact <free-fall speed> throughout gives $\sqrt2/(4\pi)$. Those prefactors are not controlled by the scaling argument. In particular, it does not generate a parametrically large $\lambda\gg1$.

= Solution

Distinguish the <cylindrical radius> $R$ from the spherical radius $r=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$. A <Taylor expansion> of the central <gravitational potential> for $|z|/R\ll1$ gives
$$
\Phi=-\frac{GM}{R}\left(1+\frac{z^2}{R^2}\right)^{-1/2}
=-\frac{GM}{R}+\frac{GMz^2}{2R^3}+O\left(\frac{GMz^4}{R^5}\right).
$$
Thus the usual <thin-disk expansion of the stellar gravitational potential> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi(R,z)\simeq-\frac{GM}{R}+\frac12\Omega_K^2(R)z^2,\qquad\Omega_K^2=\frac{GM}{R^3}.}
$$
The vertical acceleration is $-\partial_z\Phi=-\Omega_K^2z$. The first term can be omitted when considering only vertical forces at fixed $R$, but must be retained for radial dynamics.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $\gamma>1$ for the <ideal gas>. At fixed <cylindrical radius>, put $\mathcal R=k/(\mu m_p)$, where $k$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $\mu$ the <mean molecular weight> and $m_p$ the <proton> mass. Also write $a=9\alpha\Omega/4$. Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium>, <thermal equilibrium> and the <ideal gas> law require
$$
\frac{dP}{dz}=-\Omega^2z\rho,\qquad aP=AT^{1+\beta},\qquad P=\mathcal R\rho T.
$$
Consequently, with $K=A/a$,
$$
P=KT^{1+\beta},\qquad\rho=\frac K{\mathcal R}T^\beta,\qquad
(1+\beta)\mathcal R\frac{dT}{dz}=-\Omega^2z.
$$
Integrating from the midplane yields
$$
T=T_0-\frac{\Omega^2z^2}{2(1+\beta)\mathcal R},\qquad
H^2=\frac{2(1+\beta)\mathcal R T_0}{\Omega^2}.
$$
The <polytropic vertical structure in stellar gravity> therefore has, for $|z|\leq H$,
$$
\boxed{T=T_0\left(1-\frac{z^2}{H^2}\right),\quad
\rho=\rho_0\left(1-\frac{z^2}{H^2}\right)^\beta,\quad
P=P_0\left(1-\frac{z^2}{H^2}\right)^{1+\beta},}
$$
where
$$
P_0=\frac{4A}{9\alpha\Omega}T_0^{1+\beta},\qquad
\rho_0=\frac{P_0}{\mathcal R T_0}.
$$
This is a <polytropic equation of state> $P\propto\rho^{1+1/\beta}$ with <polytropic index> $\beta$. For $|z|>H$, set $\rho=P=0$; <temperature> has no physical gas value in that vacuum. Here $H$ is the finite semi-thickness, rather than an exponential <disk scale height>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The vertical acceleration in the <thin disk> is of order $\Omega^2H$. Balancing it against the characteristic <pressure> force gives
$$
\frac{P}{\rho H}\sim\Omega^2H,\qquad c_s\sim\Omega H,
$$
up to factors depending on the <adiabatic exponent> and the vertical profile. A displacement of size $\delta z$ has a restoring acceleration of order $\Omega^2\delta z$, so $\delta\ddot z\sim-\Omega^2\delta z$. Equivalently the <sound-crossing time> is $H/c_s$. Both estimates give the <vertical dynamical timescale of a disk>:
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm dyn}\sim\frac H{c_s}\sim\Omega^{-1}.}
$$
This is a mechanical response time, not an unconditional damping time. An inviscid stable layer can exhibit a <vertical breathing mode of an astrophysical disk> without settling; its adiabatic frequency satisfies $\omega_H^2=(\gamma+1)\Omega^2$. Moreover the profile in part (b) need not be convectively stable: its <specific entropy> satisfies
$$
s_{\rm gas}=c_v\log(P/\rho^\gamma)+\text{constant}
=c_v[1-\beta(\gamma-1)]\log T+\text{constant}.
$$
For $z>0$, $T$ decreases upward, so <convective stability> requires $\beta>1/(\gamma-1)$, with equality neutrally stratified. Thus interpreting the timescale as re-establishment of a stable equilibrium presupposes suitable stability and damping; the stated equations alone do not guarantee either.

= Solution

For <slow cooling of an astrophysical disk>, let $t_{\rm cool}\gg\Omega^{-1}$ and assume a vertically symmetric laminar column with no significant radial mass exchange. Its vertical velocity is of order $H/t_{\rm cool}$, so the vertical <material derivative> of that velocity is of order $H/t_{\rm cool}^2$. Relative to gravity or <pressure> acceleration $\sim\Omega^2H$, the inertial correction is $O[(\Omega t_{\rm cool})^{-2}]$.

The <hydrostatic approximation> therefore holds to leading order while the column slowly cools and contracts. Non-turbulent evolution removes the prescribed turbulent heating, but not the compressional work in the <ideal gas> energy equation:
$$
\boxed{\partial_zP=-\Omega^2z\rho,\qquad
\frac1{\gamma-1}\left(D_tP-\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}D_t\rho\right)=-AT^{1+\beta},\qquad
P=\mathcal R\rho T.}
$$
The <continuity equation> must still determine the slow vertical flow; $D_t$ is not generally $\partial_t$ at fixed height. This is a quasi-static approximation for a well-prepared, mechanically stable column, with fast free oscillations or growing <convection> excluded from the assumed slow solution.

= Solution
{parent=e}

At fixed <cylindrical radius>, let $w=u_z$ and use the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho+\partial_z(\rho w)=0$. For the <similarity solution> $z=\xi\eta(t)$ and $\rho=\widetilde\rho(\xi)/\eta$, the <continuity equation> reduces to
$$
\partial_\xi\left[\widetilde\rho(w-\dot\eta\xi)\right]=0.
$$
Here $\eta$ is dimensionless and $\xi$ labels the initial height. Midplane symmetry sets the integration constant to zero, so the flow is a <homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk>:
$$
w=\dot\eta\xi=\frac{\dot\eta}{\eta}z,\qquad
\partial_z=\eta^{-1}\partial_\xi,\qquad
\left.\partial_t\right|_z=\left.\partial_t\right|_\xi-\frac{\dot\eta}{\eta}\xi\partial_\xi,\qquad
D_t=\left.\partial_t\right|_\xi.
$$
The <hydrostatic approximation> and <ideal gas> law become
$$
\frac{d\widetilde P}{d\xi}=-\Omega^2\xi\widetilde\rho,\qquad
\widetilde P=\mathcal R\widetilde\rho\widetilde T.
$$
The <material derivatives> are $D_tP=\widetilde P\dot\eta$ and $D_t\rho=-\widetilde\rho\dot\eta/\eta^2$, so the energy equation gives
$$
\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\widetilde P\dot\eta=-A\widetilde T^{1+\beta}\eta^{2+2\beta}.
$$
<Separation of variables> requires a positive constant $K$ such that
$$
\dot\eta=-K\eta^{2+2\beta},\qquad
\widetilde P=\frac{(\gamma-1)A}{(\gamma+1)K}\widetilde T^{1+\beta}.
$$
Integrating with $\eta(0)=1$ gives the <slow cooling of an astrophysical disk> similarity factor
$$
\boxed{\eta(t)=(1+Ct)^{-1/(1+2\beta)},\qquad C=(1+2\beta)K>0.}
$$
A closed set of profile equations is therefore
$$
\boxed{\widetilde P'=-\Omega^2\xi\widetilde\rho,\qquad
\widetilde P=\mathcal R\widetilde\rho\widetilde T,\qquad
\widetilde P=\frac{(\gamma-1)(1+2\beta)A}{(\gamma+1)C}\widetilde T^{1+\beta}.}
$$
These have the same <polytropic vertical structure in stellar gravity> as part (b). Writing
$$
H_0^2=\frac{2(1+\beta)\mathcal R T_0}{\Omega^2},\qquad
\widetilde T=T_0\left(1-\frac{\xi^2}{H_0^2}\right),\qquad
\widetilde\rho=\rho_0\left(1-\frac{\xi^2}{H_0^2}\right)^\beta,\qquad
\widetilde P=P_0\left(1-\frac{\xi^2}{H_0^2}\right)^{1+\beta},
$$
with $P_0=\mathcal R\rho_0T_0$, fixes
$$
C=\frac{(\gamma-1)(1+2\beta)A T_0^{1+\beta}}{(\gamma+1)P_0}.
$$
Returning to physical height, the explicit profiles and evolving semi-thickness for $|z|\leq H(t)$ are
$$
\boxed{H(t)=H_0(1+Ct)^{-1/(1+2\beta)},\qquad
T(z,t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{2(1+\beta)\mathcal R}\left[H(t)^2-z^2\right],}
$$
and
$$
\rho(z,t)=\frac{\rho_0}{\eta(t)}\left[1-\frac{z^2}{H(t)^2}\right]^\beta,\qquad
P(z,t)=P_0\eta(t)\left[1-\frac{z^2}{H(t)^2}\right]^{1+\beta}.
$$
Outside this moving surface $\rho=P=0$. The <surface density of a disk> is conserved because $\rho\,dz=\widetilde\rho\,d\xi$. The column \b[cools and contracts without changing its scaled profile]: its midplane <temperature> falls as $\eta^2$, its <mass density> rises as $\eta^{-1}$ and its <pressure> falls as $\eta$.

The value $T_0$ determines $H_0$, but the <mass density> normalization also enters $C$. If the initial column is specifically the heated equilibrium of part (b), switching off its heating gives
$$
C=\frac{9\alpha\Omega}{4}\frac{(\gamma-1)(1+2\beta)}{\gamma+1}.
$$
Otherwise $P_0$, or equivalently the conserved column mass, is additional initial data. The contraction timescale grows with $1+Ct$, so sufficiently slow initial evolution remains slow relative to the fixed <vertical dynamical timescale of a disk>. This is an exact solution of the reduced <hydrostatic approximation>, rather than the full momentum equation: its omitted vertical acceleration is $\ddot\eta\xi$, with $\ddot\eta/(\eta\Omega^2)=2(1+\beta)C^2/[(1+2\beta)^2\Omega^2(1+Ct)^2]$, which remains small and decreases if $C\ll\Omega$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\boldsymbol\zeta=\nabla\times\boldsymbol u$ and $\boldsymbol\omega=\boldsymbol\zeta+2\Omega\boldsymbol e_z$ be the <relative vorticity> and <absolute vorticity>. The nonlinear acceleration identity rewrites the momentum equation as
$$
\partial_t\boldsymbol u-\boldsymbol u\times\boldsymbol\omega
=-\nabla\left(\frac P{\rho_0}+\frac12|\boldsymbol u|^2-\frac32\Omega^2x^2\right)-N^2\theta\boldsymbol e_x.
$$
Taking the <curl> removes the <pressure>, <kinetic energy> gradient and <shearing-sheet tidal potential>. Thus
$$
\partial_t\boldsymbol\omega-\nabla\times(\boldsymbol u\times\boldsymbol\omega)
=-N^2\nabla\theta\times\boldsymbol e_x.
$$
Both $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol u$ and $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega$ vanish. The <vorticity equation> is consequently
$$
\partial_t\omega_j+u_i\partial_i\omega_j-\omega_i\partial_i u_j
=-N^2(\nabla\theta\times\boldsymbol e_x)_j.
$$
For the <absolute-vorticity flux tensor> $T_{ij}=u_i\omega_j-\omega_i u_j$, use the flux convention $(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol T)_j=\partial_iT_{ij}$, with the first index denoting transport direction. This gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\boldsymbol\omega+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol T=-N^2\nabla\theta\times\boldsymbol e_x.}
$$
The scalar $\theta$ is a <buoyancy displacement variable>, so dimensional consistency gives it units of length; the printed potential-temperature terminology presupposes this normalization. Its <curl> source directly forces only the $y,z$ components of <absolute vorticity>. Hence <radial vorticity conservation in a shearing sheet> takes the local flux form
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\omega_x+\partial_i(u_i\omega_x-\omega_i u_x)=0.}
$$
Its volume integral is constant when the net boundary flux vanishes. However $D_t\omega_x=\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla u_x$ still permits <vortex stretching>; the radial component is not generally a materially conserved scalar in three dimensions. Using divergence on the second index of the stated tensor would reverse the transport sign, so the tensor convention matters.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the <Keplerian shearing sheet> background $\boldsymbol U=-3\Omega x\boldsymbol e_y/2$, the only spatial derivative is $\partial_xU_y=-3\Omega/2$. Since $U_x=0$ and all fields are independent of $y$, $\boldsymbol U\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol U=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol U=0$.

The <Coriolis acceleration> exactly balances the tidal term:
$$
-2\Omega\boldsymbol e_z\times\boldsymbol U=-3\Omega^2x\boldsymbol e_x.
$$
With constant <pressure> and $\theta=0$, the <buoyancy displacement variable> equation also has zero advection, zero source $U_x$ and zero diffusion. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol u=-\frac32\Omega x\boldsymbol e_y,\qquad\theta=0,\qquad P=\text{constant}}
$$
is a steady solution. Its <absolute vorticity> is $\boldsymbol\omega_0=(\Omega/2)\boldsymbol e_z$, since the background <relative vorticity> is $-3\Omega\boldsymbol e_z/2$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For an <axisymmetric vertical mode of a shearing sheet> in this <Boussinesq approximation>, the perturbations have no horizontal gradients, so background-shear advection vanishes. The linear <Coriolis acceleration> and perturbation advection of the background shear give
$$
su'_x-2\Omega u'_y=-N^2\theta',\qquad
su'_y+\frac\Omega2u'_x=0,\qquad
su'_z=-\frac{ik}{\rho_0}P',
$$
while <thermal conduction> and <incompressibility> give
$$
(s+\beta)\theta'=u'_x,\qquad iku'_z=0,\qquad \beta=\xi k^2>0.
$$
Here $\beta$ is the <thermal diffusion rate of a Fourier mode>, not the cooling exponent in Question 2. Since $k\ne0$, <incompressibility> forces $u'_z=0$, and the vertical momentum equation then forces $P'=0$ for this nonzero-<wavenumber> amplitude.

The three remaining amplitudes satisfy
$$
\begin{pmatrix}s&-2\Omega&N^2\\\Omega/2&s&0\\-1&0&s+\beta\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u'_x\\u'_y\\\theta'\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
A nontrivial solution requires the <determinant> to vanish, giving the <convective overstability> <dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{(s+\beta)(s^2+\Omega^2)+N^2s=0,\quad\text{or}\quad
s^3+\beta s^2+(N^2+\Omega^2)s+\beta\Omega^2=0.}
$$
No division by $s$ or $s+\beta$ was needed, so the <thermal energy mode of a shearing sheet> is retained. The notation $N^2$ denotes a signed <buoyancy frequency> squared and can be negative for an adverse stratification; it must not be restricted to the square of a real positive frequency.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Treat $n^2=N^2/\Omega^2$ as a signed small parameter, with $|n^2|\ll1$, as printed in the original PDF. At $n^2=0$, the <dispersion relation> factors as
$$
(s+\beta)(s^2+\Omega^2)=0.
$$
Thus the two <epicyclic motion> modes have $s_0=\pm i\Omega$, while the <thermal energy mode of a shearing sheet> has
$$
\boxed{s_{E,0}=-\beta=-\xi k^2.}
$$
It decays on the <thermal diffusion time> $\beta^{-1}$. For completeness, its next correction is $s_E=-\beta+\beta N^2/(\beta^2+\Omega^2)+O(\Omega n^4)$ at fixed $\beta/\Omega$.

For either <epicyclic motion> root, the <implicit function theorem> applied to the <dispersion relation> gives
$$
2s_0(s_0+\beta)s_1+\Omega^2s_0=0,\qquad
\boxed{s_1=-\frac{\Omega^2}{2(\beta+s_0)}.}
$$
Hence
$$
s_\pm=\pm i\Omega-\frac{N^2}{2(\beta\pm i\Omega)}+O(\Omega n^4),\qquad
\operatorname{Re}s_\pm=-\frac{N^2\beta}{2(\beta^2+\Omega^2)}+O(\Omega n^4).
$$
Since $\beta>0$, the oscillations undergo <overstability> precisely when
$$
\boxed{N^2<0.}
$$
The <convective overstability growth rate> tends to zero both for very slow diffusion and for very fast diffusion. Differentiating $\beta/(\beta^2+\Omega^2)$ shows that its maximum occurs at $\beta=\Omega$, corresponding to $k^2=\Omega/\xi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}s_{\rm max}=-\frac{N^2}{4\Omega}+O(\Omega n^4),\qquad\xi k_{\rm max}^2=\Omega.}
$$
This is the growth rate of the perturbation amplitude; a quadratic perturbation energy grows at twice that rate. The <convective overstability> arises when an adverse radial <specific entropy> gradient couples to <epicyclic motion> with a finite <thermal conduction> lag.
