For a discrete-time <martingale> $(M_n)$ and real $a<b$, let $U_N[a,b]$ be the <upcrossing count>: buy on the first observation at or below $a$, sell on the next observation at or above $b$, and repeat up to time $N$, counting only completed pairs. Writing $z^-=max(-z,0)$, the <Doob upcrossing inequality> is
$$
\boxed{(b-a)\mathbb E U_N[a,b]\leq\mathbb E(M_N-a)^-.}
$$
This convention permits the first purchase at time zero. Equivalently, the right side is $\mathbb E(M_N-a)^+-\mathbb E(M_0-a)$, because the <martingale> has constant <expected value>.

For completeness, let $H_k\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the trading rule holds one unit during $(k-1,k]$. This is a <predictable process>, so the <martingale transform> $G_N=\sum_{k=1}^NH_k(M_k-M_{k-1})$ has zero <expected value>. Every completed trade gains at least $b-a$, and an unfinished trade loses at most $(M_N-a)^-$. Thus $G_N\geq(b-a)U_N[a,b]-(M_N-a)^-$, giving the inequality.

The <martingale convergence theorem> says that if a discrete-time <martingale> $(M_n)$ satisfies $C=\sup_n\mathbb E|M_n|<\infty$, then there is an <integrable random variable> $M_\infty$ such that
$$
\boxed{M_n\longrightarrow M_\infty\quad\text{almost surely},\qquad \mathbb E|M_\infty|\leq C.}
$$
The hypothesis does not by itself ensure <convergence in L1>.

For each pair of <rational numbers> $a<b$, the <Doob upcrossing inequality> gives $\mathbb E U_N[a,b]\leq(C+|a|)/(b-a)$. The <monotone convergence theorem> therefore gives a finite <expected value> for $U_\infty[a,b]=\lim_NU_N[a,b]$, so this <upcrossing count> is finite <almost surely>. There are only countably many such pairs, hence all their <upcrossing counts> are simultaneously finite outside one event of probability zero.

If $\liminf_nM_n<\limsup_nM_n$, the <density of the rational numbers> supplies $a<b$ strictly between them, forcing infinitely many <upcrossings>. Consequently $M_n$ has a limit in the <extended real numbers> <almost surely>. By the <Fatou lemma>,
$$
\mathbb E\left[\liminf_n|M_n|\right]\leq\liminf_n\mathbb E|M_n|\leq C.
$$
A limit of either $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ would make this lower limit infinite, so the limit is finite <almost surely>, and the same inequality proves its <integrable random variable>[integrability].

$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> implies $\sup_n\mathbb E|M_n|\leq\sqrt V$. Applying the <martingale convergence theorem> from (b),
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}X_j\ \text{converges almost surely}.}

In fact the <L2 martingale convergence theorem> also gives <convergence in L2>. Independently, for $n>m$ the same <variance additivity for independent random variables> yields $\mathbb E|M_n-M_m|^2=\sum_{j=m+1}^n\mathbb E[X_j^2]\to0$, confirming that the partial sums form a <Cauchy sequence> in $L^2$.

Let $\xi_1,\xi_2,\ldots$ be <independent and identically distributed random variables> with the fair <Bernoulli distribution>, and put
$$
M_0=1,\qquad M_n=2^n\mathbf1_{\{\xi_1=\cdots=\xi_n=1\}}.
$$
Relative to $\mathcal F_n=\sigma(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$, this <fair-coin doubling martingale> satisfies $\mathbb E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$: while alive it doubles with probability $1/2$ and becomes zero otherwise. Its <expected value> is $\mathbb E M_n=2^n2^{-n}=1$.

The probability of an infinite run of ones is $\lim_n2^{-n}=0$, so $M_n$ is eventually zero <almost surely>. However,
$$
\boxed{M_n\to0\ \text{almost surely},\qquad \mathbb E|M_n-0|=1\ \text{for every }n.}
$$
Thus the <martingale> lacks <convergence in L1> to its limit from <almost sure convergence>. Nor can it have <convergence in L1> to any other limit: <convergence in L1> implies <convergence in probability>, whose limit is unique up to <almost sure equality>.

A sequence of <integrable random variables> $(X_n)$ has <uniform integrability> exactly when
$$
\boxed{\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq0}\mathbb E\left[|X_n|\mathbf1_{\{|X_n|>K\}}\right]=0.}
$$
The <uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem> states that a <uniformly integrable> discrete-time <martingale> $(M_n)$ has an <integrable random variable> $M_\infty$ with
$$
\boxed{M_n\to M_\infty\ \text{almost surely and in }L^1,\qquad M_n=\mathbb E[M_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n].}
$$
Indeed, <uniform integrability> implies $\sup_n\mathbb E|M_n|<\infty$, so the <martingale convergence theorem> gives <almost sure convergence>. The combination of <uniform integrability> and <almost sure convergence> gives <convergence in L1>. For $m\geq n$, the <martingale> identity $\mathbb E[M_m\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$ passes to the limit by the <L1 contraction of conditional expectation>.

Conversely, <uniform integrability of conditional expectations> shows that a <martingale> of the form $M_n=\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal F_n]$ for one <integrable random variable> $Y$ is <uniformly integrable>.

The original PDF has $\mathbb E|X_T|<\infty$ and $\mathbb E[|X_n|\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}]\to0$. The TeX transcription drops the <expected value> and absolute value in the first condition, and the absolute value in the second. The proof uses the PDF's conditions.

Because the <stopping time> $T$ is finite <almost surely>, $X_{n\wedge T}\to X_T$ <almost surely>. More strongly,
$$
\mathbb E|X_{n\wedge T}-X_T|
=\mathbb E\left[|X_n-X_T|\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}\right]
\leq\mathbb E\left[|X_n|\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}\right]
+\mathbb E\left[|X_T|\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}\right]\longrightarrow0.
$$
The first term tends to zero by hypothesis; the second does so by the <dominated convergence theorem>. Thus there is <convergence in L1>.

Here is the needed <L1 convergence implies uniform integrability> argument. For any <integrable random variables> $Z,Y$ and $K>0$, splitting according to $|Y|>K/2$ gives
$$
\mathbb E\left[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>K\}}\right]
\leq2\mathbb E|Z-Y|+\mathbb E\left[|Y|\mathbf1_{\{|Y|>K/2\}}\right].
$$
Take $Z=X_{n\wedge T}$ and $Y=X_T$. For large $n$, the first term is uniformly small by <convergence in L1>, and the second is small for large $K$ by <integrable random variable>[integrability]. The finitely many remaining $n$ are handled individually by <integrable random variable>[integrability]. Hence **the stopped process is <uniformly integrable>**. This proves the <stopped-martingale uniform integrability criterion>; the <martingale> assumption is not needed for this particular implication.

One can also pass directly by the <dominated convergence theorem>, since $|X_{n\wedge T}|\leq Y$.

The <sigma-algebras> $\mathcal G_n=\sigma(S_n,S_{n+1},\ldots)$ decrease with $n$, and (b) gives $S_n/n=\mathbb E[X_1\mid\mathcal G_n]$. The permitted <reverse martingale convergence theorem> therefore gives an <integrable random variable> $L$ such that $S_n/n\to L$ <almost surely> and with <convergence in L1>. In particular,
$$
\mathbb EL=\lim_n\mathbb E[S_n/n]=m.
$$
It remains to prove that $L$ is constant. We must not assume that $\bigcap_n\mathcal G_n$ is the <tail sigma-algebra> of the $X_j$; instead use <tail measurability of limits of sample averages> directly. Define the <random variable> valued in the <extended real numbers> $L^*=\limsup_n S_n/n$. For every fixed $N$,
$$
L^*=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_{N+1}+\cdots+X_n}{n},
$$
because $S_N/n\to0$ pointwise. Thus $L^*$ is measurable with respect to $\sigma(X_{N+1},X_{N+2},\ldots)$ for every $N$, and hence to the <tail sigma-algebra>. Also $L^*=L$ <almost surely>.

By the <Kolmogorov zero-one law>, each <tail event> $\{L^*\leq q\}$, for a <rational number> $q$, has probability zero or one. Since $L^*$ is finite <almost surely>, its <distribution function> can only be that of a constant $c$: taking $c=\inf\{q\in\mathbb Q:\mathbb P(L^*\leq q)=1\}$ and using the <density of the rational numbers> gives $L^*=c$ <almost surely>. Its <expected value> identifies $c=m$. This proves the <strong law of large numbers>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{S_n}{n}\longrightarrow m\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$

A standard one-dimensional <Brownian motion> is a real-valued <stochastic process> $(B_t)_{t\geq0}$ satisfying $B_0=0$ <almost surely>, having <continuous paths> <almost surely>, and having <independent increments> with
$$
\boxed{B_t-B_s\sim N(0,t-s)\qquad(0\leq s<t).}
$$
Here $N(0,t-s)$ is the <normal distribution> with zero <expected value> and <variance> $t-s$. Thus the increments are also <stationary increments>.

If <Brownian motion> is defined relative to a specified <filtration> $(\mathcal F_t)$, additionally require that it is an <adapted process> and that $B_t-B_s$ is <independence>[independent] of $\mathcal F_s$. For its <natural filtration>, this follows from <independent increments>. **These are the defining properties of standard <Brownian motion>.**

Fix an integer $k\geq1$ and consider the <independent increments>
$$
\Delta_{j,k}=B_{(j+1)/k}-B_{j/k},\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
For fixed $k$ these are <independent and identically distributed random variables> with the <normal distribution> $N(0,1/k)$, so $p_k=\mathbb P(|\Delta_{j,k}|>1)>0$. $$
\boxed{\mathbb P\bigl(B\text{ is uniformly continuous on }[0,\infty)\bigr)=0.}
$$
The event is measurable: for <continuous paths>, <uniform continuity> can be tested by countably many pairs of nonnegative <rational numbers> and tolerances $1/m$. **<Brownian motion> has <uniform continuity> on every compact time interval, but <almost surely> fails it on the whole half-line.**

The <Lévy continuity theorem> states that if <characteristic functions> $\varphi_n$ of <probability distributions> $\mu_n$ converge pointwise on $\mathbb R$ to $\varphi$, and $\varphi$ is continuous at zero, then $\varphi$ is the <characteristic function> of a unique <probability distribution> $\mu$ and
$$
\boxed{\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu.}
$$
Here the arrow denotes <weak convergence of probability measures>, equivalently <convergence in distribution> of associated <random variables>. Conversely, <weak convergence of probability measures> implies pointwise convergence of their <characteristic functions> to the <characteristic function> of the limit. The continuity-at-zero condition is essential to ensure that the pointwise limit is a <characteristic function>.

$$
No <integrable random variable>[integrability] hypothesis has been used. The conclusion is also <convergence in probability> by (d)'s <convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability>. If the word “constant” were to allow complex $a$, the <characteristic function> symmetry $\varphi(-t)=\overline{\varphi(t)}$ forces $a=\overline a$, so it is necessarily real.

= c
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Suppose $X_n\to X$ with <convergence in probability>. For real $t$ and $\delta>0$, the elementary inequality $|e^{iu}-e^{iv}|\leq\min(2,|u-v|)$ gives
$$
\left|\mathbb E e^{itX_n}-\mathbb E e^{itX}\right|
\leq |t|\delta+2\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\delta).
$$
First let $n\to\infty$, then let $\delta\downarrow0$. The <characteristic functions> converge pointwise to that of $X$, which is continuous at zero. The <Lévy continuity theorem> gives **<convergence in probability> implies <convergence in distribution>**.

For the converse, let $Z$ have the fair <Bernoulli distribution>, put $X=Z$, and set $X_n=1-Z$ for every $n$. All $X_n$ have the same <probability distribution> as $X$, so $X_n\xrightarrow dX$. But $|X_n-X|=1$ always, hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>1/2)=1\quad\text{for every }n.}
$$
There is no <convergence in probability> to $X$. In this example $X_n$ does have <convergence in probability> to $1-Z$: the counterexample concerns the converse with the specified limit $X$, as usual. If one interprets the question as denying <convergence in probability> to any limit, take $X_n=Z$ for even $n$ and $X_n=1-Z$ for odd $n$. Its <probability distribution> is still constant, while the two subsequences have distinct limits from <almost sure convergence>, ruling out any common limit in probability by <uniqueness of a limit in probability>.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $F_n$ be the <distribution function> of $X_n$. The limiting constant zero has <distribution function> $F(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $F(x)=1$ for $x\geq0$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, both $-\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon$ are continuity points of $F$. Thus <convergence in distribution> implies $F_n(-\varepsilon)\to0$ and $F_n(\varepsilon)\to1$. Therefore
$$
0\leq\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)
\leq F_n(-\varepsilon)+1-F_n(\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.
$$
This proves
$$
\boxed{X_n\xrightarrow d0\ \Longrightarrow\ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathbb P}0.}
$$
The same proof after subtracting a fixed real $c$ proves <convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability>. A deterministic limit fixes the coupling automatically; the counterexample in (c) exploits a nonconstant limit whose <probability distribution> alone does not fix that coupling.
