Let $\mathcal P$ be the <predictable sigma-algebra> on $\Omega\times[0,\infty)$, generated by $A\times\{0\}$ with $A\in\mathcal F_0$ and $A\times(s,u]$ with $A\in\mathcal F_s$. A <stochastic process> $H$ is <previsible> precisely when $(\omega,t)\mapsto H_t(\omega)$ is $\mathcal P$-measurable.

\b[Every left-continuous <adapted process> is <previsible>.] Indeed, on intervals $(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]$, put $H^{(n)}_t=H_{k2^{-n}}$, and put $H^{(n)}_0=H_0$. Each approximation is a <predictable process>, since its coefficient is $\mathcal F_{k2^{-n}}$-measurable; left continuity gives $H^{(n)}_t\to H_t$ for every $t$, so measurability passes to the limit.

\b[An <adapted> <càdlàg process> need not be <previsible>.] For a concrete example, let $\xi$ take values $0,1$ with equal probabilities, let $\mathcal F_t$ be trivial for $t<1$ and $\mathcal F_t=\sigma(\xi)$ for $t\geq1$, and set $H_t=\xi\mathbf1_{[1,\infty)}(t)$. This is an <adapted> <càdlàg process>, but $H_1=\xi$ is not $\mathcal F_{1-}$-measurable, where $\mathcal F_{1-}=\sigma(\bigcup_{s<1}\mathcal F_s)$. Sections at time $1$ of the generators of $\mathcal P$ all belong to $\mathcal F_{1-}$, so every <predictable process> has an $\mathcal F_{1-}$-measurable value there; this proves the counterexample directly.

Use the standard convention that the <finite-variation process> $X$ has <càdlàg> paths and that the <stochastic integral> starts from zero. Write $V(X)$ for its <total-variation process>. The necessary admissibility condition is $\int_{(0,t]}|H_s|\,dV_s(X)<\infty$ <almost surely> for each finite $t$; it holds, for example, for locally bounded $H$. Define the pathwise <Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral>
$$
\boxed{(H\mathbin\cdot X)_t=\int_{(0,t]}H_s\,dX_s,\qquad(H\mathbin\cdot X)_0=0.}
$$
An atom at $s>0$ contributes $H_s\Delta X_s$, so this convention also fixes the treatment of jumps.

Fix $t$. For a generator $H_s=\mathbf1_A\mathbf1_{(a,b]}(s)$ of the <predictable sigma-algebra>, with $A\in\mathcal F_a$, the <Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral> is
$$
\mathbf1_A\bigl(X_{b\wedge t}-X_{a\wedge t}\bigr).
$$
It vanishes when $t\leq a$ and is $\mathcal F_t$-measurable otherwise. The <total-variation process> is <adapted>: for a <càdlàg> path its value at $t$ is the <supremum> over a countable collection of <partitions of an interval> using rational interior points and endpoint $t$. Thus the positive and negative <Lebesgue-Stieltjes measures> $dX^\pm=(dV(X)\pm dX)/2$ have $\mathcal F_t$-measurable masses on the generator intervals.

The <monotone class theorem>, applied separately to these positive <random measures> on $[0,t]$, extends this measurability from the generators of the <predictable sigma-algebra> to all nonnegative <predictable processes>. Truncation and positive/negative decomposition then cover every admissible $H$. Consequently \b[$H\mathbin\cdot X$ is <adapted>.]

\b[The integrability condition is essential.] Taken literally, an arbitrary <previsible process> cannot always be integrated against a <finite-variation process>: with $X_t=t$ and deterministic $H_s=s^{-1}$ for $s>0$, $H_0=0$, the proposed value at every $t>0$ diverges. The usual <càdlàg> convention is also what identifies $dX$ by $dX((a,b])=X_b-X_a$.

For deterministic <step functions>, define directly from <Brownian motion> increments
$$
Z_t=\sum_{i=1}^n f_i\bigl(B_{t\wedge s_i}-B_{t\wedge s_{i-1}}\bigr),
$$
with $s_0$ the left endpoint of the first interval and $f=0$ outside the listed intervals. The printed endpoint list omits $s_0$; the intended convention is $0\leq s_0<s_1<\cdots<s_n$, usually $s_0=0$.

Given finitely many observation times, refine the deterministic <partition of an interval> to include all observation times and all $s_i$. Every $Z_{t_j}$ is a linear combination of the same finite family of <independent> centered <normal> <Brownian motion> increments. Thus every linear combination of $(Z_{t_1},\ldots,Z_{t_m})$ is <normal>, including possibly a degenerate constant: $Z$ is a <Gaussian process>. By <independence>, only increments in the common interval $[0,s\wedge t]$ contribute to the <covariance>, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EZ_t=0,\qquad C(s,t)=\int_0^{s\wedge t}f(u)^2\,du.}
$$
The same calculation for two <step functions> $f,g$ proves, rather than assumes, the <Itô isometry>
$$
\mathbb E|Z_t(f)-Z_t(g)|^2=\int_0^t|f(u)-g(u)|^2\,du.
$$
For $f\in L^2(\mathbb R_+)$, choose deterministic <step functions> $f_n\to f$ in the <L2 space>. This identity makes $Z_t(f_n)$ a <Cauchy sequence> in the <L2 space>; the <Riesz-Fischer theorem> defines $Z_t(f)$ by <mean-square convergence>, independently of the approximation. The <covariance> formula passes to the limit by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. For finitely many times and coefficients $a_j$, the <characteristic functions> converge to
$$
\mathbb E\exp\!\left(i\sum_j a_jZ_{t_j}(f)\right)
=\exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_{j,k}a_ja_k\int_0^{t_j\wedge t_k}f(u)^2\,du\right),
$$
so the extension remains a <Gaussian process>.

For completeness, the construction has a continuous version. Each approximating $Z(f_n)$ is a <martingale>, directly by the centered <independent increments> of <Brownian motion>; the <Doob L2 maximal inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb E\sup_{u\leq T}|Z_u(f_n)-Z_u(f_m)|^2\leq4\|f_n-f_m\|_{L^2(\mathbb R_+)}^2.
$$
To prove the inequality used here without assuming <Itô integral> properties, a continuous <martingale> $N$ satisfies $\lambda\mathbb P(N^*_T\geq\lambda)\leq\mathbb E(|N_T|\mathbf1_{\{N^*_T\geq\lambda\}})$, by conditioning at the first crossing on finite deterministic grids and then using continuity; integrating in $\lambda$ and using the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> yields $\|N^*_T\|_2\leq2\|N_T\|_2$. A sufficiently fast subsequence therefore converges uniformly <almost surely> on every compact time interval, giving the continuous version of the <stochastic integral>.

Since $h$ is smooth and compactly supported, $h'$ is bounded and globally <Lipschitz continuous>. Define the <stochastic exponential>
$$
D_t=\exp\!\left(\int_0^t h'(B_s)\,dB_s-\frac12\int_0^t h'(B_s)^2\,ds\right).
$$
The <Novikov condition> holds on every finite horizon $T$, because $\mathbb E\exp(\frac12\int_0^Th'(B_s)^2ds)\leq\exp(T\|h'\|_\infty^2/2)$. Thus $D$ is a true <martingale>. Under $d\mathbb Q=D_Td\mathbb P$, the <Girsanov theorem> makes $\widehat B_t=B_t-x-\int_0^th'(B_s)ds$ a standard <Brownian motion> for $t\leq T$. Consequently $B$ under $\mathbb Q$ solves the prescribed <stochastic differential equation>; the <existence and pathwise uniqueness theorem for a stochastic differential equation> identifies its law with that of $X$.

The <Itô formula> gives $\int_0^t h'(B_s)dB_s=h(B_t)-h(x)-\frac12\int_0^th''(B_s)ds$. Hence, for any bounded measurable $f$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_xf(X_t)=\mathbb E_x\!\left[\exp\!\left(h(B_t)-h(x)-\int_0^tV(B_s)\,ds\right)f(B_t)\right],\quad V=\frac12(h')^2+\frac12h''.}
$$
The finite-horizon argument applies separately to every $t\geq0$.

Let $X,Y$ be two continuous solutions on the same <filtered probability space>, driven by the same <Brownian motion> and with $X_0=Y_0$ <almost surely>. Write $D=X-Y$ and choose a common <Lipschitz constant> $L$ for $b,\sigma$. Introduce the <stopping time> $\tau_n=\inf\{t\geq0:|X_t|+|Y_t|\geq n\}$. On $\{\tau_n=0\}$ the stopped difference is zero; before $\tau_n$, both coefficients and the difference are bounded.

The <Itô formula> and the zero <expectation> of the stopped <square-integrable> <Itô integral> yield
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E D_{t\wedge\tau_n}^2
&=\mathbb E\int_0^t\mathbf1_{\{s<\tau_n\}}\left(2D_s(b(X_s)-b(Y_s))+(\sigma(X_s)-\sigma(Y_s))^2\right)ds\\
&\leq(2L+L^2)\int_0^t\mathbb E D_{s\wedge\tau_n}^2\,ds.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <Gronwall inequality> implies $\mathbb ED_{t\wedge\tau_n}^2=0$. Continuous solution paths are bounded on each compact time interval, so $\tau_n\uparrow\infty$ <almost surely>. Letting $n\to\infty$ proves $X_t=Y_t$ <almost surely> at each fixed time, and a countable dense set of times together with continuity gives <indistinguishability of stochastic processes>. Therefore \b[the <stochastic differential equation> has <pathwise uniqueness>.]

Multiplication by the integrating factor $e^{t/2}$ and the <Itô product rule> give the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process>
$$
X_t=e^{-t/2}X_0+\int_0^te^{-(t-u)/2}\,dB_u.
$$
The two summands are <independent> centered <normal> <random variables>, because $X_0$ is <independent> of the <Brownian motion> and the integrand is deterministic. By the <Itô isometry>, their <variances> sum to $e^{-t}+\int_0^te^{-(t-u)}du=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{X_t\sim N(0,1)\quad(t\geq0).}
$$
For $t\geq s$, split the <Itô integral> at $s$ to obtain $X_t=e^{-(t-s)/2}X_s+\int_s^te^{-(t-u)/2}dB_u$, where the latter term is centered and <independent> of $X_s$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_s)=e^{-|t-s|/2}.}
$$
In particular this initialization makes the <Gaussian process> a <stationary process>.

Use the standard definition of a <local martingale>, including an integrable initial value, and let $T_n\uparrow\infty$ be a <localizing sequence>. If $X\geq0$, the <Fatou lemma> gives $\mathbb EX_t\leq\liminf_n\mathbb EX_{t\wedge T_n}=\mathbb EX_0<\infty$. For $s\leq t$, the <conditional Fatou lemma> gives
$$
\mathbb E(X_t\mid\mathcal F_s)\leq\liminf_n\mathbb E(X_{t\wedge T_n}\mid\mathcal F_s)=\liminf_nX_{s\wedge T_n}=X_s.
$$
For clarity, the <conditional Fatou lemma> itself follows by setting $U_m=\inf_{n\geq m}U_n$ for nonnegative $U_n$, applying the conditional version of the <monotone convergence theorem> to $U_m\uparrow\liminf U_n$, and observing $\mathbb E(U_m\mid\mathcal F_s)\leq\inf_{n\geq m}\mathbb E(U_n\mid\mathcal F_s)$. Hence \b[a nonnegative <local martingale> is a <supermartingale>.]

If $|X_t|\leq C$ simultaneously for all $t$, with a deterministic $C<\infty$, the <dominated convergence theorem> applied to the stopped <martingale> identity gives, for each $A\in\mathcal F_s$,
$$
\mathbb E(\mathbf1_AX_t)=\lim_n\mathbb E(\mathbf1_AX_{t\wedge T_n})=\lim_n\mathbb E(\mathbf1_AX_{s\wedge T_n})=\mathbb E(\mathbf1_AX_s).
$$
This is exactly the <conditional expectation> identity, so \b[a bounded <local martingale> is a true <martingale>.] The same proof works for deterministic bounds on every finite time interval; merely having a finite random pathwise bound supplies no dominating integrable <random variable> and is a different convention.

Let $U_t=(1,0,0)+(B_t^1,B_t^2,B_t^3)$, with three <independent> standard <Brownian motions>, and set $R_t=|U_t|$ and $M_t=R_t^{-1}$. The radial <stochastic process> $R$ is a three-dimensional <Bessel process>, which never hits zero by the <hitting-zero classification for a Bessel process>. In three dimensions $u\mapsto|u|^{-1}$ is a <harmonic function> away from zero: direct differentiation gives $\Delta(|u|^{-1})=0$.

For the <stopping times> $T_n=\inf\{t:R_t\notin(1/n,n)\}$, $n\geq2$, the <Itô formula> gives
$$
M_{t\wedge T_n}=1-\sum_{j=1}^3\int_0^{t\wedge T_n}\frac{U_s^j}{R_s^3}\,dB_s^j.
$$
The coefficients are bounded by $n^2$, so each stopped <Itô integral> is a true <martingale> on every finite horizon. The <stopping times> form a <localizing sequence>; as permitted, its divergence need not be proved here. Thus $M$ is a nonnegative <continuous local martingale>.

To see that $M$ is not a true <martingale>, integrate the three-dimensional <multivariate normal density> of $U_t$ in <spherical coordinates>. The angular integral yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EM_t
&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\int_0^\infty\left(e^{-(r-1)^2/(2t)}-e^{-(r+1)^2/(2t)}\right)dr\\
&=2\Phi(1/\sqrt t)-1<1=\mathbb EM_0\qquad(t>0),
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution function>. Therefore \b[the reciprocal of a three-dimensional <Bessel process> is a <strict local martingale>.]

Put $\Delta B_h=B_{t+h}-B_t$ and $R_h=\int_t^{t+h}(H_s-H_t)dB_s$. The constant-in-time coefficient $H_t$ on $(t,t+h]$ is <previsible>, so the ratio minus $H_t$ is $R_h/\Delta B_h$. Since $|H|\leq C$, continuity and the <dominated convergence theorem> imply
$$
\eta_h=\mathbb E\sup_{t\leq s\leq t+h}|H_s-H_t|^2\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <Itô isometry> gives $\mathbb ER_h^2\leq h\eta_h$. For $\varepsilon,\delta>0$, use the <Chebyshev inequality> and the <normal distribution> of $\Delta B_h/\sqrt h$ to obtain
$$
\mathbb P\left(\left|\frac{R_h}{\Delta B_h}\right|>\varepsilon\right)
\leq\mathbb P(|N(0,1)|\leq\delta)+\frac{\eta_h}{\varepsilon^2\delta^2}.
$$
First let $h\downarrow0$, then $\delta\downarrow0$; the <normal distribution> has no atom at zero. This proves
$$
\boxed{\frac{\int_t^{t+h}H_s\,dB_s}{B_{t+h}-B_t}\xrightarrow[h\downarrow0]{\mathbb P}H_t.}
$$
No <independence> between $R_h$ and the denominator is needed.

The first estimate suggested by the hint is valid: <Brownian scaling> and the <Gamma function> give $\mathbb E|\Delta B_h|^{-1/2}=h^{-1/4}2^{-1/4}\Gamma(1/4)/\sqrt\pi$. The second hint requires care about the placement of its <expectation>: with $V_h=\int_t^{t+h}(H_s-H_t)^2ds$, the interpretation $\mathbb E|R_h|^{1/2}\leq(\mathbb EV_h)^{1/4}$ is correct by the <Jensen inequality> and the <Itô isometry>, whereas \b[the interpretation $\mathbb E|R_h|^{1/2}\leq\mathbb E(V_h^{1/4})$ is false with constant $1$]. For the latter interpretation, the <Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality> supplies a constant $C_{1/2}$ instead. One can see the obstruction with a <Brownian motion> stopped on exiting $(-1,1)$: if $\tau$ is that <Brownian exit time>, then $|B_\tau|=1$, $\mathbb E\tau=1$, and strict <Jensen inequality> gives $\mathbb E\tau^{1/4}<1=\mathbb E|B_\tau|^{1/2}$.

This obstruction also survives the continuity condition on $H$. On a long finite interval approximate $\mathbf1_{\{s<\tau\}}$ by a continuous <adapted process> equal to $1$ until $\tau$ and decreasing linearly to $0$ during the next $\epsilon$ units of time, and take its initial value to be $0$ by inserting a short continuous ramp before starting the exit experiment. The <Itô isometry> controls these approximations, and letting the interval length tend to infinity recovers the strict inequality above; hence some bounded continuous <adapted> example also violates the latter interpretation with constant $1$. The direct proof avoids relying on this ambiguous hint.

The usual <quadratic variation> convention includes $\langle M\rangle_0=0$. If $A,C$ are two continuous <finite-variation processes> with $A_0=C_0=0$ such that $M^2-A$ and $M^2-C$ are <continuous local martingales>, then $A-C$ is a <continuous local martingale> of <finite variation>. It must be constant, and its initial value is zero, so $A=C$ up to <indistinguishability of stochastic processes>.

Here is a proof of the fact used. Subtract the initial value of a <continuous local martingale> $N$ of <finite variation>, and stop so that both $|N|$ and its <total-variation process> are bounded by deterministic constants. The stopped <local martingale> is a bounded true <martingale>, so orthogonality of its increments gives, on any deterministic <partition of an interval> of $[0,t]$,
$$
\mathbb E(N_t^2)=\mathbb E\sum_i(\Delta_iN)^2\leq\mathbb E\left[\max_i|\Delta_iN|\,V_t(N)\right]\longrightarrow0.
$$
Continuity and the <dominated convergence theorem> justify the last limit. Remove stopping and use continuity on a countable dense set of times to conclude $N\equiv0$.

\b[As literally printed, uniqueness is false without normalization.] If $A$ works then $A+c$ also works for any deterministic constant $c$, since subtracting a constant preserves the <local martingale> property; this is a genuine omitted convention.

\b[With normalization, existence always holds for a <continuous local martingale>.] Its <quadratic variation> $[M]$, obtained as the limit in <probability> of squared increments, is continuous, increasing, starts at zero and satisfies $M^2-[M]$ is a <continuous local martingale>, so taking $\langle M\rangle=[M]$ supplies the required <finite-variation process>.

Interpret the <Stratonovich integral> in its standard extension to continuous <semimartingale> integrands:
$$
\int_0^tY_s\circ dX_s=\int_0^tY_s\,dX_s+\frac12[Y,X]_t.
$$
This extension is needed because $f'(X)$ is generally a <semimartingale> rather than a <local martingale>. Also the introductory prose in the PDF reverses integrand and integrator relative to its displayed definition; the displayed definition fixes the convention used here.

Apply the <Itô formula> to $f'(X)$, using $f\in C^3$:
$$
df'(X_s)=f''(X_s)\,dX_s+\frac12f'''(X_s)\,d\langle X\rangle_s.
$$
The continuous <finite-variation process> in the second term has zero <quadratic covariation> with $X$, while the <quadratic covariation> rule for an <Itô integral> gives
$$
[f'(X),X]_t=\int_0^tf''(X_s)\,d\langle X\rangle_s.
$$
A second application of the <Itô formula>, now to $f(X)$, therefore yields
$$
\boxed{f(X_t)-f(X_0)=\int_0^tf'(X_s)\circ dX_s.}
$$
All unbounded coefficients are handled by stopping $X$ on compact spatial intervals, then removing the <localizing sequence>.

Take $X=Y=B$, a standard <Brownian motion> with $B_0=0$. The <Itô formula> and $\langle B\rangle_t=t$ give
$$
\boxed{\int_0^tB_s\circ dB_s=\frac12(B_t^2-t)+\frac12t=\frac12B_t^2.}
$$
If this nonnegative <stochastic process>, which starts from zero, were a <local martingale>, question 4(i) would make it a <supermartingale> and force its <expectation> to be at most zero. But $\mathbb E(B_t^2/2)=t/2>0$ for $t>0$. Hence \b[a <Stratonovich integral> is in general not a <local martingale>.]

The limit is in <probability>, or more strongly in <uniform convergence on compacts in probability>; a pathwise limit for every <continuous local martingale> is not implicit. The original PDF uses $\lceil t2^n\rceil$, whereas the supplied TeX changes this to $\lfloor t2^n\rfloor$; both versions have the same limit by continuity.

Put $\delta=2^{-n}$ and $m=\lfloor t/\delta\rfloor$. For the completed intervals, the algebraic identity
$$
\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{Y_{i\delta}+Y_{(i-1)\delta}}2\,(X_{i\delta}-X_{(i-1)\delta})
=\sum_{i=1}^mY_{(i-1)\delta}\Delta_iX+\frac12\sum_{i=1}^m\Delta_iY\,\Delta_iX
$$
separates a left-point sum from a <quadratic covariation> sum.

The results from <stochastic calculus> used are these: predictable left-step approximations to a continuous <adapted> integrand converge in their <Itô integrals> in <uniform convergence on compacts in probability>; and products of increments of continuous <semimartingales> on deterministic <partitions of an interval> with vanishing mesh converge to their <quadratic covariation> in the same sense. The first follows after localization from the <Itô isometry> and the <Doob L2 maximal inequality>, since the integrand approximations converge uniformly on compact intervals; the second is the deterministic-partition characterization of <quadratic covariation>.

Thus the two sums converge to $\int_0^tY_s\,dX_s$ and $\frac12\langle Y,X\rangle_t$. For the ceiling convention, at most one additional full interval extends past $t$; on $[0,T+1]$ its absolute contribution is at most $\sup_{s\leq T+1}|Y_s|$ times the modulus of continuity of $X$ at scale $\delta$, which tends to zero <almost surely>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil t2^n\rceil}\frac{Y_{i2^{-n}}+Y_{(i-1)2^{-n}}}{2}\,(X_{i2^{-n}}-X_{(i-1)2^{-n}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb P}\int_0^tY_s\circ dX_s.}
$$

The <space-time Hermite polynomials> obtained by scaling the <probabilists' Hermite polynomials> are genuine <polynomials> in both variables, including at $t=0$:
$$
H_n(x,t)=n!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^j x^{n-2j}t^j}{2^j j!(n-2j)!}.
$$
This expansion follows by differentiating the defining Gaussian exponential, and shows that $H_n(x,0)=x^n$ extends smoothly through $t=0$. Consequently the <Itô formula> is applicable from time zero. The given backward <heat equation> cancels the <drift>, and the derivative identity gives
$$
dH_n(B_t,t)=nH_{n-1}(B_t,t)\,dB_t\qquad(n\geq1).
$$
The integrand is continuous and <adapted>, hence <previsible>, and stopping the <Brownian motion> and time makes it bounded. Thus \b[$H_n(B_t,t)$ is a <local martingale> for every $n\geq0$.] The $n=0$ case is the constant $H_0=1$; in fact Gaussian <moments> make the displayed <Itô integral> <square-integrable> on every finite horizon, so these are true <martingales> as well.

The first three <probabilists' Hermite polynomials> are $h_1(x)=x$, $h_2(x)=x^2-1$ and $h_3(x)=x^3-3x$. Hence
$$
\boxed{H_1(B_t,t)=B_t,\qquad H_2(B_t,t)=B_t^2-t,\qquad H_3(B_t,t)=B_t^3-3tB_t.}
$$
