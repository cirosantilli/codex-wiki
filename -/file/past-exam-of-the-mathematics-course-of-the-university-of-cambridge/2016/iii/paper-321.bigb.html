= Solution
{parent=i}

Write $\Sigma=\Sigma_0x^{-5/2}g$ because the prescribed <kinematic viscosity> is $\bar\nu=\bar\nu_0x^{5/2}$. Substituting $r=r_0x$ and $\partial_t=(3\bar\nu_0/4r_0^2)\partial_\tau$ into the <viscous evolution of an accretion disk> gives
$$
x^{-5/2}g_\tau=\frac4x\partial_x\left[x^{1/2}\partial_x(x^{1/2}g)\right].
$$
Hence the dimensionless <diffusion equation> is
$$
\boxed{g_\tau=4x^{5/2}g_{xx}+6x^{3/2}g_x.}
$$
For the useful inverse-radius coordinate $y=x^{-1/2}$, this becomes simply $g_\tau=y g_{yy}$. The positive factor in $\tau=1+3\bar\nu_0t/(4r_0^2)$ makes increasing $\tau$ correspond to increasing physical time.

There is a distinction between the named torque variable and the physical <viscous torque in an accretion disk>. For <Keplerian rotation>, the outward torque magnitude is
$$
\mathcal T=3\pi\bar\nu\Sigma\sqrt{GM_\star r}=3\pi\bar\nu_0\Sigma_0\sqrt{GM_\star r_0}\,x^{1/2}g.
$$
Here $M_\star$ denotes the central <mass>. The <zero-torque inner boundary condition> therefore requires $x^{1/2}g\to0$ as $x\to0$. Requiring $g\to0$ is stronger. The nontrivial finite-mass profile used below satisfies both; this distinction matters when counting integration constants.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $g=G(\xi)$ with $\xi=\tau^{-1}x^{-1/2}$. The derivatives of this <similarity solution> are
$$
g_\tau=-\frac\xi\tau G',\quad g_x=-\frac\xi{2x}G',\quad g_{xx}=\frac{\xi^2G''+3\xi G'}{4x^2}.
$$
The first-derivative terms cancel in the dimensionless <diffusion equation>, leaving $G''+G'=0$ for $\xi>0$. Thus
$$
G=A+B e^{-\xi}.
$$
At the inner boundary $x\to0$, $\xi\to\infty$. Interpreting the named variable as vanishing there sets $A=0$. Even if only the true <viscous torque in an accretion disk> $x^{1/2}g$ is required to vanish, a nonzero $A$ would give infinite total <mass> near $r=0$; finite <mass> again selects $A=0$. With nonnegative <surface density>, choose $B=C>0$; its value fixes the initial mass normalization. Therefore
$$
\boxed{g=C e^{-\xi},\qquad \frac\Sigma{\Sigma_0}=C x^{-5/2}\exp\left(-\frac1{\tau\sqrt x}\right).}
$$
The <surface density> vanishes exponentially at the origin and has an $x^{-5/2}$ tail at infinity. Its peak occurs at $x_{\max}=1/(25\tau^2)$, with $\Sigma_{\max}/\Sigma_0=C(5\tau)^5e^{-5}$. The peak moves inward and grows as time increases.

\Image[paper-321-surface-density.png]
{title=Surface-density profiles of the printed similarity solution at two times, showing an inward-moving growing peak and the outer power-law tail}

The plot uses $C=1$ and shows the actual <surface density>, with logarithmic radius to resolve both peaks. The later profile is larger at every fixed radius. Its total <mass> is finite; its total <angular momentum> is not, as the next calculation establishes.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For the finite-mass <similarity solution> above, the total disk <mass> is
$$
M_d=2\pi\Sigma_0r_0^2 C\int_0^\infty x^{-3/2}e^{-1/(\tau\sqrt x)}\,dx.
$$
Substitute $\xi=1/(\tau\sqrt x)$, so $x=\tau^{-2}\xi^{-2}$ and $x^{-3/2}|dx|=2\tau\,d\xi$. This gives
$$
\boxed{M_d=4\pi C\Sigma_0r_0^2\tau=M_d(0)+3\pi C\bar\nu_0\Sigma_0t.}
$$
The requested linear increase of <mass> is therefore correct. It is supplied by the infinite-radius boundary, rather than by a source-free isolated finite disk. The inward <mass accretion rate> is
$$
\dot M(r)=6\pi r^{1/2}\partial_r(r^{1/2}\bar\nu\Sigma)=3\pi C\bar\nu_0\Sigma_0(1+\xi)e^{-\xi},
$$
which tends to zero at the origin and to $3\pi C\bar\nu_0\Sigma_0$ at infinity.

For <Keplerian rotation>, the <specific angular momentum> is $\ell=\sqrt{GM_\star r}$. The total disk <angular momentum> would be
$$
J_d=2\pi C\Sigma_0\sqrt{GM_\star}\,r_0^{5/2}\int_0^\infty x^{-1}e^{-1/(\tau\sqrt x)}\,dx
=4\pi C\Sigma_0\sqrt{GM_\star}\,r_0^{5/2}\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\xi}}\xi\,d\xi.
$$
**This integral diverges at $\xi=0$, corresponding to infinite radius. There is no finite constant total angular momentum for the nonzero printed similarity solution.** The seemingly time-independent last expression is an infinite quantity; it cannot prove a finite <conservation law>.

The problem remains visible under a cutoff. With $r\le r_0R$, write $J_R=J_*E_1(1/(\tau\sqrt R))$, where $J_*=4\pi C\Sigma_0\sqrt{GM_\star}\,r_0^{5/2}$ and $E_1$ is the <exponential integral>. Then
$$
\frac{dJ_R}{d\tau}=\frac{J_*}{\tau}e^{-1/(\tau\sqrt R)}>0,\qquad \lim_{R\to\infty}[J_R(\tau)-J_R(1)]=J_*\log\tau.
$$
Even the finite change after subtracting a common logarithmic divergence is not zero. Direct integration of the disk <diffusion equation> gives the boundary identity
$$
\frac{dJ_d}{dt}=6\pi\sqrt{GM_\star}\left[r^{3/2}\partial_r(\bar\nu\Sigma)\right]_0^\infty;
$$
for this profile the outer expression has a nonzero limit, giving the same finite change rate. An inner zero torque alone does not impose zero outer <angular-momentum flux>.

More generally, the printed family $A+B e^{-\xi}$ can have finite <angular momentum> at both ends only if $A=0$ and $A+B=0$, hence it is trivial. Thus the request for a nonzero finite constant $J_d$ is incompatible with the stated viscosity exponent, similarity ansatz and infinite radial domain. A physical finite-domain or altered similarity problem would need different boundary data or assumptions; silently assigning a finite value to the divergent integral does not repair it.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The four equations describe, respectively, vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> in the central object's gravity, the vertical divergence of <radiative flux> balancing <viscous dissipation>, optically thick <radiative diffusion>, and the <equation of state> combining <ideal gas> <gas pressure> with <radiation pressure>. Here $\mu$ is <dynamic viscosity>, not the mean molecular weight $\mu_m$; $\kappa$ is the <opacity>, $\sigma$ the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>, and $\Omega$ the local orbital frequency.

In the radiation-pressure limit, $p=4\sigma T^4/(3c)$, so <radiative diffusion> gives
$$
\frac{dp}{dz}=\frac{16\sigma T^3}{3c}\frac{dT}{dz}=-\frac{\kappa\rho F}{c}.
$$
Compare this with vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium>. Wherever $\rho\ne0$,
$$
F(z)=\frac{c\Omega^2}{\kappa}z.
$$
At fixed radius, constant <opacity> makes $dF/dz=c\Omega^2/\kappa$. The <viscous dissipation> equation therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{4c}{9\kappa},}
$$
independent of height within the optically thick radiation-supported layer. The result assumes the supplied gravity, heating and diffusion equations; optically thin surface corrections are outside that approximation.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use characteristic, vertically averaged quantities at fixed radius. Let $H$ be a disk thickness, $\rho\sim\Sigma/H$, and let the <alpha disk> prescription give $\mu\sim\alpha p/\Omega$, up to a convention-dependent constant. From the preceding <radiation pressure> and constant-<opacity> balance, $\mu\sim c/\kappa$ is independent of $\Sigma$; thus
$$
p\sim\frac{c\Omega}{\alpha\kappa}.
$$
Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives $p\sim\rho\Omega^2H^2\sim\Sigma\Omega^2H$, hence
$$
H\sim\frac{c}{\alpha\kappa\Omega\Sigma}\propto\Sigma^{-1}.
$$
The vertically averaged <kinematic viscosity> satisfies $\bar\nu\Sigma=\int\mu\,dz\sim\mu H$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\bar\nu\Sigma\sim\frac{c^2}{\alpha\kappa^2\Omega}\,\Sigma^{-1}.}
$$
At fixed radius the omitted factors are independent of $\Sigma$, so this is the requested inverse-density scaling of the <viscous torque in an accretion disk>.

This is an order-of-magnitude vertical model, as requested. Imposing both exact pointwise $\mu=\text{constant}$ and exact pointwise $\mu\propto p(z)$ would force $p$ to be constant, contrary to nontrivial vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium>. The characteristic-pressure prescription supplies a scaling, not an exact simultaneous vertical solution to all those idealized equations.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Write $W(\Sigma)=\bar\nu\Sigma$. In a small radial patch $r=r_c+\chi$ with disturbance scale much smaller than $r_c$, the leading disk <diffusion equation> is $\partial_t\Sigma\simeq3\partial_\chi^2W$. Taking the <linearization> around a uniform <surface density> $\Sigma_c$ gives
$$
\partial_t\delta\Sigma=3W'(\Sigma_c)\partial_\chi^2\delta\Sigma.
$$
For a local <Fourier mode> $\delta\Sigma\propto e^{st+ik\chi}$,
$$
\boxed{s=-3k^2W'(\Sigma_c)>0,}
$$
because $W\propto\Sigma^{-1}$ has $W'<0$. Thus the effective density <diffusion coefficient> is negative and the disk has a <viscous instability of an accretion disk>.

A localized overdensity is a maximum of $\Sigma$ but a minimum of $W$. At its center $\partial_\chi^2W>0$, so $\partial_t\Sigma>0$: it grows. The local radial mass flux is proportional to $-\partial_\chi W$, directing matter toward that minimum from both sides. This is the physical feedback: an overdense patch has a smaller <viscous torque in an accretion disk> and attracts further accumulation. The arbitrarily rapid $k\to\infty$ growth in this ideal negative-diffusion model is not a resolved prediction below the validity scale of the thin-disk and local viscous closure.

= Solution

Let $\mathbf u=-(3/2)\Omega x\mathbf e_y+\mathbf v$ and $\rho=\rho_0(1+\delta)$, with $\delta$ and $\mathbf v$ small. Define the <sound speed> by $c^2=(dp/d\rho)_0$, assumed positive. The background <Keplerian shear> balances the radial <Coriolis force> and the <shearing-sheet tidal potential>. For disturbances preserving <axisymmetry> $\partial_y=0$, its advective derivative $\mathbf u_0\cdot\nabla$ vanishes, but $\mathbf v\cdot\nabla\mathbf u_0=-(3/2)\Omega v_x\mathbf e_y$ remains. The linearized <Euler equations> and <continuity equation> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\partial_t v_x-2\Omega v_y&=-c^2\partial_x\delta,\\
\partial_t v_y+\frac\Omega2v_x&=0,\\
\partial_t v_z&=-c^2\partial_z\delta,\\
\partial_t\delta+\partial_xv_x+\partial_zv_z&=0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The azimuthal coefficient is $(2-3/2)\Omega=\Omega/2$, not $2\Omega$: it combines the background velocity gradient with the <Coriolis force>. The uniform background has no buoyancy or vertical stratification in this stated model.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Substitute the axisymmetric <Fourier mode> into the linearized equations. With hats denoting amplitudes,
$$
\begin{aligned}
-i\omega\hat v_x-2\Omega\hat v_y&=-ik_xc^2\hat\delta,\\
-i\omega\hat v_y+\frac\Omega2\hat v_x&=0,\\
-i\omega\hat v_z&=-ik_zc^2\hat\delta,\\
\omega\hat\delta&=k_x\hat v_x+k_z\hat v_z.
\end{aligned}
$$
For a nondegenerate nonzero frequency, eliminate the azimuthal velocity and use
$$
(\omega^2-\Omega^2)\hat v_x=\omega k_xc^2\hat\delta,\qquad \omega\hat v_z=k_zc^2\hat\delta.
$$
Continuity then gives
$$
\omega^2(\omega^2-\Omega^2)=c^2\left[k_x^2\omega^2+k_z^2(\omega^2-\Omega^2)\right].
$$
Equivalently, taking the <determinant> of the original four amplitude equations avoids division by zero and gives the full <dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{\omega^4-(\Omega^2+c^2k^2)\omega^2+\Omega^2c^2k_z^2=0,\qquad k^2=k_x^2+k_z^2.}
$$
The two squared frequencies are
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\frac12\left[\Omega^2+c^2k^2\pm\sqrt{(\Omega^2+c^2k^2)^2-4\Omega^2c^2k_z^2}\right].
$$
The <discriminant> is $(\Omega^2-c^2k^2)^2+4\Omega^2c^2k_x^2\ge0$. For $c^2>0$, both squared frequencies are nonnegative. The high-frequency branch is predominantly acoustic and the low-frequency branch is an <inertial wave>. Zero-frequency limiting modes, including $k_z=0$, are retained by the determinant calculation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take $ck\gg\Omega$, retaining a finite low-frequency branch and a nonzero <wavevector>. In the <dispersion relation>, neglect $\omega^4$ against $c^2k^2\omega^2$ and neglect $\Omega^2$ against $c^2k^2$ in their coefficient. The <inertial wave> limit is
$$
\boxed{\omega^2\simeq\Omega^2\frac{k_z^2}{k^2}.}
$$
Choose the signed branches $\omega=\epsilon\Omega k_z/k$, $\epsilon=\pm1$. The <phase velocity> normal to the phase planes and the <group velocity> are
$$
\mathbf c_p=\frac\omega{k^2}\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf c_g=\epsilon\Omega\left(\frac{\mathbf e_z}{k}-\frac{k_z\mathbf k}{k^3}\right).
$$
Therefore $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf c_g=\epsilon\Omega(k_z/k-k_zk^2/k^3)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c_p\cdot\mathbf c_g=0.}
$$
The geometric reason is that this limiting <dispersion relation> is homogeneous of degree zero in $\mathbf k$: its frequency depends on direction, not magnitude. <Euler homogeneous function theorem> gives $\mathbf k\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega=0$. Exact orthogonality holds for this <inertial wave> limit; finite compressibility introduces corrections. At a zero-frequency degeneracy the usual propagating-wave interpretation needs a limiting treatment.

For the <polytropic equation of state> $p=K\rho^{1+1/n}$, take $K>0$ and $n>0$ for the physical model. The <specific enthalpy> relative to vacuum is
$$
Q=\int_0^\rho\frac{dp(\tilde\rho)}{\tilde\rho}=(n+1)K\rho^{1/n}=\frac{(n+1)p}{\rho}.
$$
Its gradient obeys $\nabla Q=\rho^{-1}\nabla p$, so the <Euler equations> become
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf u+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u=-\nabla Q-2\Omega\mathbf e_z\times\mathbf u-\nabla\Phi_t.}
$$
For the material derivative $D_t=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$, $D_tQ=(Q/n\rho)D_t\rho$. The <continuity equation> thus becomes
$$
\boxed{nD_tQ=-Q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u.}
$$
The choice $Q=0$ at zero <mass density> fixes the additive convention in this equation. Although the momentum equation alone is unchanged by adding a constant to $Q$, the polytropic relation and enthalpy evolution must use the same vacuum reference.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $\mathbf u=\alpha y\mathbf e_x-\beta x\mathbf e_y$, the velocity divergence is zero and $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u=-\alpha\beta(x\mathbf e_x+y\mathbf e_y)$. Substituting into the steady <Euler equations> yields
$$
Q_x=(\alpha\beta+3\Omega^2-2\Omega\beta)x,\qquad Q_y=\alpha(\beta-2\Omega)y.
$$
Integration gives
$$
Q=Q_0+\frac12(\alpha\beta+3\Omega^2-2\Omega\beta)x^2+\frac12\alpha(\beta-2\Omega)y^2.
$$
The steady enthalpy equation also requires $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla Q=0$. Its $xy$ coefficient is $\alpha(3\Omega^2+\alpha\beta-\beta^2)$, so, since $\alpha>0$,
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\beta^2-3\Omega^2}{\beta},\qquad Q=Q_0-\frac12(2\Omega-\beta)(\beta x^2+\alpha y^2).}
$$
For a bounded patch with positive <mass density> and a vacuum <fluid free boundary>, choose $Q_0>0$ and require both quadratic coefficients to confine the fluid. With $\Omega>0$ and $\alpha,\beta>0$, this means
$$
\boxed{\sqrt3\,\Omega<\beta<2\Omega.}
$$
The lower bound is imposed by $\alpha>0$; the upper bound by bounded positive $Q$. At $\beta=2\Omega$, $Q$ is constant and has no nontrivial finite vacuum boundary; $\beta\le\sqrt3\Omega$ violates or degenerates the stipulated elliptical flow.

For a <polytropic elliptical patch in a shearing sheet>, the physical <fluid free boundary> is $p=\rho=Q=0$. It is the <ellipse>
$$
\boxed{\beta x^2+\alpha y^2=\frac{2Q_0}{2\Omega-\beta},\qquad \frac{x^2}{a_x^2}+\frac{y^2}{a_y^2}=1,}
$$
where $a_x^2=2Q_0/[\beta(2\Omega-\beta)]$ and $a_y^2=2Q_0/[\alpha(2\Omega-\beta)]$. In the interior $\rho=[Q/((n+1)K)]^n$. The boundary is a material <streamline> because $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla(\beta x^2+\alpha y^2)=0$. Since $\alpha<\beta$, it is elongated in the azimuthal $y$ direction.

This is a pressure-supported fluid patch in the supplied tidal model; no additional planetary self-gravity potential was assumed.

At $x_n=0$ and $y_n=hn$, every radial pair displacement vanishes, so $f_x^n=0$. The particles with indices $n+l$ and $n-l$ give opposite azimuthal accelerations,
$$
\frac{Gm}{h^2}\frac{l}{|l|^3}\mathbf e_y,\qquad -\frac{Gm}{h^2}\frac{l}{|l|^3}\mathbf e_y,
$$
which cancel pairwise. The acceleration sum has <absolute convergence> because $\sum_{l\ge1}l^{-2}<\infty$. With zero velocities and $x_n=0$, both the <Coriolis force> and radial <tidal force> vanish. **The equally spaced row is therefore an equilibrium of the <infinite gravitating particle lattice>.** Its absolute gravitational potential would require an additional reference or regularization; the acceleration used here is well defined.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $\mathbf d_{jn}=h(j-n)\mathbf e_y$ and $\Delta\boldsymbol\xi=\boldsymbol\xi_j-\boldsymbol\xi_n$, with $\boldsymbol\xi_n=(x'_n,y'_n)$. Linearizing the <Newton's law of universal gravitation> uses
$$
\delta\left(\frac{\mathbf d}{|\mathbf d|^3}\right)=\frac1{|\mathbf d|^3}\left(I-3\frac{\mathbf d\mathbf d^T}{|\mathbf d|^2}\right)\Delta\boldsymbol\xi.
$$
For an azimuthal separation, the matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(1,-2)$. After canceling the equilibrium forces, the linearized <shearing-sheet> equations are
$$
\boxed{\ddot x'_n-2\Omega\dot y'_n=3\Omega^2x'_n+\frac{Gm}{h^3}\sum_{j\ne n}\frac{x'_j-x'_n}{|j-n|^3},}
$$
$$
\boxed{\ddot y'_n+2\Omega\dot x'_n=-\frac{2Gm}{h^3}\sum_{j\ne n}\frac{y'_j-y'_n}{|j-n|^3}.}
$$
The transverse gravitational response tends to restore a radial displacement, while the longitudinal response reinforces an azimuthal density concentration. Both sums converge for the bounded <Fourier modes> considered next.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Set $\xi=hk$. For the <discrete Fourier mode>, the displacement difference in the $l$th neighbor pair carries $e^{il\xi}-1$, and
$$
\sum_{l\ne0}\frac{e^{il\xi}-1}{|l|^3}=2\sum_{l=1}^\infty\frac{\cos(l\xi)-1}{l^3}=-F(\xi).
$$
Thus the infinite system reduces to
$$
\boxed{s^2X-2\Omega sY=3\Omega^2X-\frac{Gm}{h^3}F(\xi)X,\qquad s^2Y+2\Omega sX=2\frac{Gm}{h^3}F(\xi)Y.}
$$
The <gravitating lattice Fourier kernel> $F$ is nonnegative, even and $2\pi$-periodic, with $F(0)=0$. The <uniform convergence> and <absolute convergence> of its differentiated series gives
$$
F'(\xi)=2\sum_{l=1}^\infty\frac{\sin(l\xi)}{l^2}.
$$
To prove the maximum, it is not sufficient to maximize each cosine term separately: the even terms do not reach their individual maxima at $\pi$. Instead use the convergent integral representation $l^{-2}=\int_0^\infty t e^{-lt}dt$. Summing the <geometric series> for sines gives
$$
F'(\xi)=2\sin\xi\int_0^\infty\frac{t e^{-t}}{1-2e^{-t}\cos\xi+e^{-2t}}\,dt.
$$
For $0<\xi<\pi$, the denominator is positive and $\sin\xi>0$, so $F'>0$. For $\pi<\xi<2\pi$, $F'<0$. The interchange is justified by $\sum_l\int_0^\infty t e^{-lt}|\sin(l\xi)|dt\le\sum_l l^{-2}<\infty$. Hence
$$
\boxed{F\text{ has its global maxima exactly at }\xi=(2j+1)\pi.}
$$
Their value is
$$
\boxed{F(\pi)=4\sum_{l\text{ odd}}l^{-3}=\frac72\zeta(3),}
$$
where $\zeta$ is the <Riemann zeta function>. This maximizing <Fourier mode> alternates the signs of neighboring particle displacements.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Put $A=(Gm/h^3)F(hk)$. The two amplitude equations have coefficient matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}s^2-3\Omega^2+A&-2\Omega s\\2\Omega s&s^2-2A\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its determinant gives the <dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{s^4+(\Omega^2-A)s^2+2A(3\Omega^2-A)=0.}
$$
Writing $q=A/\Omega^2\ge0$, its squared growth rates are
$$
\boxed{\frac{s_\pm^2}{\Omega^2}=\frac{q-1\pm\sqrt{9q^2-26q+1}}2,}
$$
and the four growth rates are both <square roots> of each displayed value. Define
$$
q_- =\frac{13-4\sqrt{10}}9,\qquad q_+ =\frac{13+4\sqrt{10}}9.
$$
For $0<q<q_-$, the <discriminant> is positive, the sum of the squared rates is $q-1<0$, and their product is $2q(3-q)>0$; both squared rates are negative. These modes have purely imaginary $s$. For $q_-<q<q_+$, the squared rates form a nonreal <complex conjugate> pair; their <square roots> include roots with positive real part. This is <overstability>, meaning growing oscillations. For $q\ge q_+$, at least the plus squared rate is positive because $q>1$, so a real growing root exists. Consequently every $q>q_-$ is exponentially unstable.

Take the maximizing mode $hk=\pi$, so $q=(Gm/(h^3\Omega^2))F(\pi)$. It is unstable whenever
$$
\boxed{\frac{Gm}{h^3\Omega^2}>\frac{13-4\sqrt{10}}{9F(\pi)}.}
$$
This is also the threshold for the onset of exponential growth among all lattice modes, since $0\le F\le F(\pi)$. At equality the maximizing mode has repeated imaginary roots and can have algebraic growth; it is a marginal collision of oscillatory modes, not a strict-decay assertion. The $k=0$ mode has $F=0$, an <epicyclic motion> sector and a zero-frequency <epicyclic guiding center> sector with possible secular shear drift. These neutral or algebraic behaviors are distinct from the strict exponential instability established by the requested inequality.
