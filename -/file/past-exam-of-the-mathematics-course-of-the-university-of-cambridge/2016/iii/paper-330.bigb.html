= Solution
{parent=a}

The <WKB approximation> treats the vertical <wavenumber> as locally constant over one vertical <wavelength>. Write $w=\Re\{A(z)e^{ikx+i\int^z m(s)ds}\}$, with $|m'|\ll m^2$ and $|A'|\ll |mA|$. For the <stationary internal-wave WKB solution>, $A\propto m^{-1/2}$ away from singular or turning levels. A small terrain slope and small perturbations are also needed for <linearized Boussinesq equations>.

Let $p$ be the perturbation <fluid pressure> divided by reference <mass density>, and $b$ the <buoyancy perturbation>. The steady <linearized Boussinesq equations> and <incompressibility condition> are
$$
Uu_x+U'w=-p_x,\qquad Uw_x=-p_z+b,\qquad Ub_x+N^2w=0,\qquad u_x+w_z=0.
$$
For an $e^{ikx}$ component, elimination gives $u=-w'/(ik)$, $b=-N^2w/(ikU)$ and $p=(Uw'-U'w)/(ik)$. Substitution into vertical momentum gives the exact stationary <Taylor–Goldstein equation>
$$
w''+\left(\frac{N^2}{U^2}-\frac{U''}{U}-k^2\right)w=0.
$$
The leading <WKB approximation> therefore identifies
$$
\boxed{m^2=\frac{N^2}{U^2}-\frac{U''}{U}-k^2=\left(\frac{N^2}{k^2U^2}-\frac{U''}{k^2U}-1\right)k^2.}
$$
The <kinematic boundary condition> fixes the horizontal <wavenumber> to $k=k_T$ for the sinusoidal forcing.

For explicit heights assume a constant positive <buoyancy frequency> $N$, $0<kU_0<N$, and $|S|/N\ll1$. The latter is a large <gradient Richardson number>. The <linear shear flow> has $U''=0$, so the propagation condition is $|U|<N/k$.

For $S<0$, the first obstruction is the <critical level of an internal gravity wave> at
$$
\boxed{z_c=\frac{U_0}{|S|}.}
$$
As $z\uparrow z_c$, $m\sim N/U\to\infty$. The <critical-height approach of a stationary wave in linear shear> has upward <group velocity> $c_{gz}\sim kU^2/N$, so the travel time to $z_c$ diverges: the height is a supremum, approached in the ideal ray model. Here $|m'|/m^2\to |S|/N$, which can remain small; it would be incorrect to say that the short-<wavelength> <WKB approximation> necessarily fails solely because $m$ diverges. Nevertheless $w\propto U^{1/2}$ and <displacement> $w/(ikU)\propto U^{-1/2}$, so a finite disturbance eventually violates linearity. Dissipation and overturning also matter near this singular level.

For $S>0$, the <turning height of a stationary wave in linear shear> is
$$
\boxed{z_t=\frac{N/k-U_0}{S}.}
$$
At $z_t$, $m=0$ and the <WKB approximation> fails. An <Airy function> matching region describes reflection and an upper <evanescent wave>. Thus $z_t$ is the highest height of vertical propagation, rather than a sharp cut-off of every disturbance.

The problem initially allows $N(z)$ to vary. Without the extra constant-$N$ assumption, replace the explicit turning height by the first root of $N(z)=k|U_0+Sz|$ encountered on a propagating branch. A declining $N(z)$ can cause a turning level before the negative-shear critical level. **The two closed-form heights require the stated constant-stratification assumption.**

= Solution

Take a common constant <buoyancy frequency> $N$ and put $k=k_T>0$. Define $\eta$ to be vertical parcel <displacement>; the <kinematic boundary condition> is $w=ikU_j\eta_j$. In either uniform region the exact <Taylor–Goldstein equation> gives
$$
\eta_j''+m_j^2\eta_j=0,\qquad m_j^2=\frac{N^2}{U_j^2}-k^2.
$$
No <WKB approximation> is needed. If the earlier $N(z)$ is genuinely nonconstant, the exact regional equation is instead $\eta_j''+[N(z)^2/U_j^2-k^2]\eta_j=0$; its fundamental solutions, not plane exponentials, must be used. For example, let $C_1(0)=1$, $C_1'(0)=0$, $S_1(0)=0$, $S_1'(0)=1$, and let $f_2$ be an outgoing upper solution. Then $\eta_1=h_0C_1+D S_1$, $\eta_2=A f_2$, with $D,A$ determined by $h_0C_1(H)+DS_1(H)=Af_2(H)$ and $U_1^2[h_0C_1'(H)+DS_1'(H)]=U_2^2Af_2'(H)$. This gives the exact arbitrary-profile field implicitly, while a simple angle-only coefficient requires further stratification assumptions.

Under the common constant-$N$ assumption, propagating incident <internal gravity waves> require
$$
\boxed{0<U_1<\frac Nk.}
$$
For co-directed upper flow, the outgoing propagating branch exists for $0<U_2<N/k$ and has $m_2>0$. The <radiation condition> chooses $m_j>0$ because the laboratory vertical <group velocity> is positive on the negative <intrinsic frequency> branch. Define $\tan\theta_j=m_j/k$, so $\cos\theta_j=kU_j/N$.

At the <velocity> jump, the material-interface <jump conditions for stratified inviscid shear flow> require continuity of <displacement> and of <fluid pressure>. Vertical <velocity> itself is generally discontinuous: $w_j=ikU_j\eta$. In terms of <displacement> the pressure is $p_j=U_j^2\eta_j'$. The <displacement impedance for an internal wave at a velocity jump> is $Z_j=U_j^2m_j$. An upward incident component of complex <displacement> <amplitude> $I$ at $H$, a reflected component $RI$ and a transmitted component $TI$ therefore obey
$$
1+R=T,\qquad Z_1(1-R)=Z_2T.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\qquad T=\frac{2Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}.}
$$
For a one-pass incident <displacement> $h_0$, a real wave field is the imaginary part of
$$
\begin{aligned}
\eta_1(x,z)&=h_0e^{ikx}\left[e^{im_1z}+R e^{im_1(2H-z)}\right],&&0<z<H,\\
\eta_2(x,z)&=h_0T e^{i[kx+m_1H+m_2(z-H)]},&&z>H.
\end{aligned}
$$
The incident vertical-<displacement> <amplitude> is $h_0$, the reflected <amplitude> is $|R|h_0$ and the transmitted <amplitude> is $|T|h_0$; the sign of $R$ encodes a phase reversal. The other fields follow from $w=ikU\eta$, $u=-w'/(ik)$, $b=-N^2\eta$ and $p=U^2\eta'$. If a perfectly reflecting terrain boundary is enforced after the return wave arrives, replace the incident coefficient $h_0$ by $h_0/[1+Re^{2im_1H}]$. The one-pass <amplitudes> use the permitted neglect of repeated terrain reflections.

For common $N$, $Z_j=(N^2/k)\cos\theta_j\sin\theta_j$. The physical vertical-<displacement> transmission coefficient is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\eta_{2,\mathrm{vertical}}}{h_0}=\frac{2\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_1}{\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_1+\cos\theta_2\sin\theta_2}=\frac{2\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_1}{\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)}.}
$$
The printed coefficient has a different denominator and cannot be obtained for this standard <displacement> convention. For example, when $U_2=U_1$, there is no interface scattering and the vertical <amplitude> stays $h_0$, whereas the printed expression gives $h_0/\cos\theta_1$. A total <displacement> magnitude would instead be the vertical <amplitude> divided by $\cos\theta_2$; with unequal <velocities> it still does not give the printed expression. **The transmission formula needs an explicit correction or a different, presently unspecified <amplitude>/interface model.**

For $U_2>N/k$, choose $m_2=i\kappa$ with $\kappa=(k^2-N^2/U_2^2)^{1/2}$: the transmitted response decays as $e^{-\kappa(z-H)}$ and the same impedance equations give a complex $R,T$, with $|R|=1$. At $U_2=0$ the steady equation is singular. Oppositely directed upper flow formally supports propagating waves when $0<|U_2|<N/k$, but upward radiation then requires $m_2<0$ and signed $Z_2$; the positive-angle co-directed formula is not valid there. A real <velocity> jump can also have <Kelvin-Helmholtz instability>, which is excluded from this formal steady scattering calculation.

Consider the permanent <stationary topographic internal gravity wave> component of a localized source, with constant <buoyancy frequency> in each layer and positive $U_1,U_2$. A sufficiently broad obstacle spectrum is assumed. The <dispersion relation> on the upward branch in a uniform layer is
$$
\omega=kU-\frac{Nk}{(k^2+m^2)^{1/2}}.
$$
For a stationary wave, $K=(k^2+m^2)^{1/2}=N/U$. Differentiating before imposing stationarity gives the laboratory <group velocity>
$$
(c_{gx},c_{gz})=U(\cos^2\theta,\sin\theta\cos\theta),\qquad \cos\theta=k/K.
$$
The vertical component is at most $U/2$, attained at $\theta=\pi/4$. The <first arrival of a stationary internal wavefront at a layer boundary> is thus
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{2H}{U_1},\qquad x(T)=H.}
$$
If the obstacle spectrum lacks that <wavenumber>, the first arrival of its actual permanent wave packets is later. This statement concerns the stationary part; arbitrary long-wave startup transients are not bounded by this particular envelope.

In the lower layer an elapsed propagation time $t$ gives $x=U_1t\cos^2\theta_1$, $z=U_1t\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1$. Elimination gives the <causal envelope of stationary internal waves>
$$
\boxed{\left(x-\frac{U_1t}{2}\right)^2+z^2=\left(\frac{U_1t}{2}\right)^2,\qquad z\geq0.}
$$
Later-emitted packets fill the interior. At $t=T$ this semicircle just touches $H$ at $x=H$. At $2T$ its unobstructed continuation would reach $2H$, but rays that hit the interface must instead be refracted or reflected.

For a lower-layer ray labelled by $\theta_1$, its interface arrival time and point are
$$
\tau_1=\frac{H}{U_1\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1},\qquad x_H=H\cot\theta_1.
$$
The <refraction of a stationary internal-wave causal envelope> conserves horizontal <wavenumber>, so $\cos\theta_2=(U_2N_1)/(U_1N_2)\cos\theta_1$. If $\tau_1\leq t$ and $0<\cos\theta_2<1$, its transmitted front is parametrized by
$$
\boxed{x=x_H+(t-\tau_1)U_2\cos^2\theta_2,\qquad z=H+(t-\tau_1)U_2\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_2.}
$$
The reflected front instead has $x=U_1t\cos^2\theta_1$ and $z=2H-U_1t\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_1$, until it reaches the ground. At $2T$ that first return just touches the ground. If the $\pi/4$ incident ray is evanescent upstairs, upper-layer propagating arrival is delayed until an allowed ray reaches $H$; this does not change the first lower-layer arrival time $T$.

The <stationary phase of topographic internal waves> also gives the phase sketch. In a uniform lower layer the <Fourier transform> integrand has phase $kx+m(k)z$. The <stationary phase method> requires $x=kz/m$, giving phase $\Phi=(N_1/U_1)(x^2+z^2)^{1/2}$. Thus the dominant permanent phase lines are downstream circular arcs centred on the obstacle. Reflected phase arcs have the image source $(0,2H)$. Upstairs the ray arriving at $(x_H,H)$ has phase
$$
\Phi=kx_H+m_1H+k(x-x_H)+m_2(z-H),
$$
which is continuous at the interface and determines refracted constant-phase curves. Individual monochromatic components have straight phase lines of slope $-k/m_j$ instead; the arcs describe their localized-source superposition.

Use the steady <Boussinesq top-hat plume in a stratified ambient>, with negligible ambient vertical momentum and no externally added heat or particles. The <Batchelor entrainment hypothesis> supplies inward <velocity> $\alpha w$ across circumference $2\pi b$. <Volume conservation> gives $\pi Q_z=2\pi b\alpha w$, and $bw=\sqrt M$, hence
$$
\boxed{Q_z=2\alpha\sqrt M.}
$$
The <momentum flux> increases by the integrated <buoyancy> force. Writing $g'=g\Delta\rho/\rho_o$, the force per reference density and per $\pi$ is $g'b^2$. Since $F=g'Q$ and $b^2=Q^2/M$, this gives
$$
\boxed{M_z=g'b^2=\frac{FQ}{M}.}
$$
For the <buoyancy flux>, entrained material has the local environmental <mass density>. Let $\rho_p$ be the plume's averaged density. Its scalar <mass conservation> is $(\rho_pQ)_z=\rho_a Q_z$. Since $F=(g/\rho_o)(\rho_a-\rho_p)Q$, differentiating cancels both <entrainment> terms and gives
$$
\boxed{F_z=\frac g{\rho_o}\left[(\rho_aQ)_z-(\rho_pQ)_z\right]=Q\frac g{\rho_o}\rho_a'.}
$$
In <stable density stratification>, $\rho_a'<0$ and a rising plume loses <buoyancy flux>. These are kinematic fluxes with the common factor $\pi\rho_o$ removed, so no additional factor of $\pi$ belongs in the three differential equations.

= Solution

Define $K=N_s^2z_s^{-\beta}>0$. The <buoyancy frequency> definition gives $F_z=-KQz^\beta$. The powers in a <plume similarity in power-law stratification> satisfy
$$
q-1=\frac m2,\qquad m-1=f+q-m,\qquad f-1=q+\beta.
$$
Solving gives
$$
\boxed{q=3+\frac\beta2,\qquad m=4+\beta,\qquad f=4+\frac{3\beta}{2}.}
$$
A rising positive-buoyancy <turbulent plume> entrains, so $Q_z>0$; its <momentum flux> increases, so $M_z>0$; its <buoyancy flux> decreases in the stable ambient, so $F_z<0$. With positive coefficients, this requires $q>0$, $m>0$ and $f<0$. The strongest restrictions are
$$
\boxed{-4<\beta<-\frac83.}
$$
In this interval $1<q<5/3$, $0<m<4/3$ and $-2<f<0$.

Coefficient matching makes the positive <similarity solution> explicit:
$$
qQ_o=2\alpha\sqrt{M_o},\qquad mM_o^2=F_oQ_o,\qquad fF_o=-KQ_o,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{M_o=-\frac{4\alpha^2K}{fmq^2},\qquad Q_o=\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{M_o}}q,\qquad F_o=-\frac{KQ_o}f.}
$$
It is an ideal scaling for $z>0$, often a far-field description. As $z\to0$, the prescribed ambient stratification and this model's <buoyancy flux> are singular; a finite physical source would require a regularized near-source region.

Take logarithmic height $\xi=\ln(z/z_*)$ with an arbitrary fixed length $z_*$, so $d/d\xi=z\,d/dz$. This is dimensionally meaningful and has the same derivatives as the abbreviated $\ln z$. For example,
$$
\frac{d\widehat Q}{d\xi}=\frac{zQ_z}{Q_s}-q\widehat Q=\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{M_o}}{Q_o}z^{1+m/2-q}\widehat M^{1/2}-q\widehat Q.
$$
The exponent vanishes and the coefficient is $q$, by the <plume similarity in power-law stratification>. Likewise the momentum coefficient is $m$ and the buoyancy coefficient is $f$. The <logarithmic-height plume equations> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\widehat Q_\xi&=-q(\widehat Q-\widehat M^{1/2}),\\
\widehat M_\xi&=-m(\widehat M-\widehat F\widehat Q/\widehat M),\\
\widehat F_\xi&=-f(\widehat F-\widehat Q).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The reference <similarity solution> becomes the equilibrium $(1,1,1)$ of an <autonomous differential equation>. Normalizing each flux by its own height-dependent similarity value is essential; simply differentiating the unnormalized fluxes would miss the three subtraction terms.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Expand the square root and quotient in the <logarithmic-height plume equations>:
$$
\widehat M^{1/2}=1+\tfrac12\epsilon M'+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad\frac{\widehat F\widehat Q}{\widehat M}=1+\epsilon(F'+Q'-M')+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
The <linear stability analysis> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac d{d\xi}\begin{pmatrix}Q'\\M'\\F'\end{pmatrix}=J\begin{pmatrix}Q'\\M'\\F'\end{pmatrix},\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}-q&q/2&0\\m&-2m&m\\f&0&-f\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In particular, the second row contains $-2mM'$, since the reciprocal of $\widehat M$ contributes another $-M'$.

For the <growing mode of power-law plume similarity>, the <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
P(\lambda)=\lambda^3+(q+2m+f)\lambda^2+\left(\frac32qm+qf+2mf\right)\lambda+qmf.
$$
Within the physical interval $q,m>0$ and $f<0$, $P(0)<0$ while $P(\lambda)\to+\infty$ as $\lambda\to+\infty$. There is a positive real <eigenvalue>, so the similarity equilibrium is **unstable with increasing height**. Perturbations behave as $e^{\lambda\xi}\propto z^\lambda$; the result is not stability in physical time. Thus this exact positive-buoyancy similarity trajectory is not a generic attracting far field.

At the upper endpoint,
$$
\boxed{\beta\to-\frac83:\qquad f\to0^-,\quad q\to\frac53,\quad m\to\frac43.}
$$
The limiting polynomial is $\lambda(\lambda+1)(\lambda+10/3)$, reproducing the three stated <eigenvalues>. The positive <eigenvalue> tends to zero, with $\lambda\sim-2f/3$. The two negative modes decay as $z^{-1}$ and $z^{-10/3}$; the remaining mode is neutral at the limiting linear system. Its <eigenvector> is $(1,2,3)$ in relative-flux coordinates.

The <neutral buoyancy-flux mode of a pure plume> has a simple interpretation: a constant surviving <buoyancy flux> fixes the <amplitudes> of a <pure plume>, with $Q\propto F^{1/3}z^{5/3}$ and $M\propto F^{2/3}z^{4/3}$. A small fractional change in $F$ produces changes in $Q,M$ in the ratio $1:2:3$. It is retained rather than damped out.

There is a qualification at the exact endpoint. For fixed nonzero $K$ and $\beta=-8/3$, taking $Q\propto z^{5/3}$ gives $F_z\propto-z^{-1}$, not zero. The flux coefficients above also diverge as $f\to0$. Thus the constant-flux powers are the formal limiting scaling, not an exact constant-buoyancy solution in a nonzero endpoint stratification; cumulative ambient effects can require logarithmic corrections.

Seek a steady downslope solution with $u>0$ constant. The <volume conservation> equation reduces to $h_x=E$, so the <constant-speed entraining gravity current on a slope> has
$$
\boxed{h=h_0+Ex.}
$$
The conserved <buoyancy flux> per unit width is $B=ug'h$, giving
$$
\boxed{g'(x)=\frac{B}{u(h_0+Ex)}.}
$$
Substitute these expressions into the momentum equation. Its advective term is $Eu^2$ and its hydrostatic term is $\tfrac12E(B/u)\cos\theta$. The balance is therefore
$$
Eu^2+\frac{EB}{2u}\cos\theta=\frac Bu\sin\theta-cu^2,
$$
which gives
$$
\boxed{u=\beta_*B^{1/3},\qquad\beta_*^3=\frac{\sin\theta-\tfrac12E\cos\theta}{c+E}.}
$$
The assumed slope inequality makes $\beta_*>0$. The increasing thickness dilutes <reduced gravity> while leaving $g'h$ and the <buoyancy flux> constant. The denominator includes both bottom drag and the momentum needed to accelerate newly entrained ambient fluid.

= Solution

For identical suspended particles, <reduced gravity> is proportional to <particle volume fraction>. If particles settle through the bed at speed $v_s$ and are not resuspended, the downward <particle deposition flux> removes a buoyancy-equivalent amount $v_sg'$ per unit bed area. The depth-integrated scalar <mass conservation> equation is
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(g'h)+\partial_xB=-v_sg',\qquad B=ug'h.}
$$
For a steady <particle-laden gravity current>, $B_x=-v_sB/(uh)$. If $v_s/u\ll1$, with fixed positive $E$ and drag/slope coefficients, the locally adjusted leading solution of the momentum and volume equations is $u\sim\beta_*B^{1/3}$ and $h\sim h_0+E(x-x_0)$. The <weak-settling attenuation of a sloping gravity current> then gives
$$
\frac d{dx}B^{1/3}\sim-\frac{v_s}{3\beta_*[h_0+E(x-x_0)]},
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{B(x)\sim\left[B_o^{1/3}-\frac{v_s}{3\beta_*E}\ln\left(1+\frac{E(x-x_0)}{h_0}\right)\right]^3.}
$$
For an inlet at $x_0=0$ this has the requested logarithmic form. If the inlet is genuinely at $x_0\ne0$, the argument must contain $x-x_0$ in order to satisfy the specified inlet condition.

The speed factor must be the $\beta_*$ derived in the previous part. The printed $\beta$ omits the factor one-half multiplying $E\cos\theta$ and uses an undefined uppercase $C$ instead of the governing equation's $c$. Taking $C=c$ still leaves a nonzero leading momentum residual $-\tfrac12E(B/u)\cos\theta$ for the printed speed; this discrepancy cannot be repaired by a correction of order $v_s/u$. **With the given hydrostatic momentum equation, the logarithmic law is valid with $\beta_*$, not the printed speed factor.**

A controlled local approximation also needs $v_s/(Eu)\ll1$ if $E$ is allowed to become small. Indeed the exact volume balance is $h_x=E-hu_x/u$; under $u\sim\beta_*B^{1/3}$ its second term is $v_s/(3u)$. The neglected depth and acceleration corrections become significant as $B$ approaches zero.

= Solution

The <formal sedimentation runout of a sloping gravity current> is obtained when the bracket in the leading <weak-settling attenuation of a sloping gravity current> reaches zero. With the consistent speed factor,
$$
\boxed{x_{\max}-x_0\sim\frac{h_0}{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{3\beta_*E B_o^{1/3}}{v_s}\right)-1\right].}
$$
Setting $x_0=0$ gives the distance from the inlet. The corresponding formal expression with a stipulated independent speed coefficient is identical with that coefficient replacing $\beta_*$.

This is an extrapolated leading-model sedimentation distance. Since $u\sim\beta_*B^{1/3}$ tends to zero there, the weak-settling condition fails in the terminal region; the exponential value is not a uniformly controlled prediction of the fully coupled flow. In particular, disappearance of particle buoyancy need not coincide with instantaneous disappearance of every inertial motion.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a steady no-source <particle-laden gravity current>, $B_x=-v_sg'<0$. Simply entraining particle-free ambient fluid increases volume and dilutes <particle volume fraction>; it cannot create particle <buoyancy flux>.

A steady increase can occur if <sediment resuspension> or bed erosion adds more suspended particle buoyancy than settling removes, if a tributary or lateral particle source supplies a positive flux, or if the buoyancy per unit particle amount increases through a change of the surrounding fluid's density. Changes of particle properties or another buoyancy source can also alter the flux, but they go beyond the fixed-particle, fixed-reference model. In a model with a source $S_B$,
$$
\partial_t(g'h)+B_x=S_B-v_sg'.
$$
Thus the <buoyancy production in a particle-laden current> requires $S_B>v_sg'$ in a steady current. In an unsteady current, $B_x$ can be positive even without external addition: local depletion has $\partial_t(g'h)<-v_sg'$, so more buoyancy leaves a section than enters it while stored particles are being released. **An instantaneous spatial flux increase is not by itself proof of particle production.**

The conserved particle volume per unit horizontal area is
$$
\mathcal P=\phi_1(H-z_m)+\phi_2z_m.
$$
The <sediment mass determines final deposit thickness>: with the stipulated deposit <particle volume fraction> $\phi_{\max}=1$, all particle volume becomes bed thickness, so $h_c=\mathcal P$. For $z_m=H/2$,
$$
\boxed{\bar h=h_c=\frac H2(\phi_1+\phi_2),\qquad\bar\phi=\frac1H\int_0^H\phi\,dz=\frac{\phi_1+\phi_2}{2}=\frac{h_c}{H}.}
$$
The final <particle volume fraction> averaged over the entire container remains the initial average: clear fluid occupies $h_c<z<H$ and the deposit occupies $0<z<h_c$. Averaging over the deposit alone would instead give one, which is a different average.

= Solution

Take $z$ upward and $u_s>0$ downward. The <quadratic hindered-settling flux> is $j(\phi)=-u_s\phi(1-\phi)$. Particle <mass conservation> gives the <kinematic sedimentation> equation
$$
\phi_t+j(\phi)_z=0,\qquad \frac{dz}{dt}=j'(\phi)=u_s(2\phi-1),\qquad\frac{d\phi}{dt}=0
$$
on a smooth <characteristic curve>. Integrate the <scalar conservation law> across a moving discontinuity: $V[\phi]=[j]$. The <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives the <settling shock speed>
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{j(\phi_R)-j(\phi_L)}{\phi_R-\phi_L}=u_s(\phi_L+\phi_R-1),}
$$
where $L$ is the lower side and $R$ the upper side. Since $j''=2u_s>0$, a compressive <shock> has $\phi_L>\phi_R$ and its neighboring <characteristic curves> point into it.

Put $a=\phi_1$, so $\phi_2=2a$, with $0<a<1/4$. The <two-layer sedimentation with a compression shock> has
$$
\boxed{V_1=-u_s(1-a),\qquad V_2=-u_s(1-3a),\qquad V_3=2u_sa.}
$$
These respectively separate upper suspension from clear fluid, lower suspension from upper suspension, and deposit from lower suspension. Their initial positions are $H,z_m,0$. The <characteristic curves> in the two suspended states have slopes $u_s(2a-1)$ and $u_s(4a-1)$; clear-fluid and deposit <characteristic curves> have slopes $-u_s$ and $u_s$.

For $z_m=H/2$, $V_2$ and $V_3$ meet first. Their intersection gives
$$
\boxed{t_{c1}=\frac{z_m}{u_s(1-a)}=\frac{H}{2u_s(1-a)},\qquad h_{c1}=\frac{2az_m}{1-a}=\frac{aH}{1-a}.}
$$
The potential meeting of the upper two fronts would take $(H-z_m)/(2u_sa)$, which is later in the stated range. The new front separates <particle volume fraction> one from <particle volume fraction> $a$, so
$$
\boxed{V_4=u_sa.}
$$
It follows $z=h_{c1}+u_sa(t-t_{c1})$ until it meets $z=H-u_s(1-a)t$. The final mass-conserving intersection is
$$
\boxed{t_{c2}=\frac{H(1-a)-az_m}{u_s(1-a)},\qquad h_{c2}=a(H+z_m).}
$$
For $z_m=H/2$, these are $H(1-3a/2)/[u_s(1-a)]$ and $3aH/2$.

The PDF prints a plus sign before $az_m/H$ in the stopping-time numerator. That sign contradicts both the top-front trajectory and particle <mass conservation>; the correct sign is minus, as derived here. Once all particles are deposited, the remaining $1$-to-$0$ front is stationary because both limiting fluxes vanish.

Retain $a=\phi_1$, $\phi_2=2a$ and the three <settling shock speeds>. A <triple-shock sedimentation point> requires the meeting of $V_2$ with $V_3$ to coincide with the meeting of $V_1$ with $V_2$. Hence
$$
\frac{z_m}{u_s(1-a)}=\frac{H-z_m}{2u_sa}.
$$
Solving, and using the deposit-front position $2u_sa t$, gives
$$
\boxed{z_m=\frac{1-a}{1+a}H,\qquad t_c=\frac{H}{u_s(1+a)},\qquad h_c=\frac{2aH}{1+a}.}
$$
The common height also equals $a(H+z_m)$, independently checking <mass conservation>. The second panel of the compression-<shock> diagram shows $V_1,V_2,V_3$ meeting at this point. For $t>t_c$ the suspension is exhausted: deposit below $h_c$ and clear fluid above it are separated by a stationary <shock>, with zero flux on both sides.

Put $a=\phi_1$, $b=\phi_2=a/2$, $z_m=4H/5$, $L=H-z_m$ and $D=(1-b)z_m$. The <quadratic hindered-settling flux> is convex. At the initial internal interface its lower <particle volume fraction> is smaller than its upper <particle volume fraction>, so <characteristic curves> diverge: the <entropy solution> has a <settling rarefaction fan>, not a compression <shock>. Within this fan,
$$
\boxed{\phi(z,t)=\frac12\left(1+\frac{z-z_m}{u_st}\right),\qquad z_m+u_s(2b-1)t<z<z_m+u_s(2a-1)t.}
$$
The two bounding straight <shocks> initially have
$$
\boxed{V_1=-u_s(1-a),\qquad V_2=u_sb=\frac{u_sa}{2}.}
$$
The upper front's nominal meeting with the fan's upper edge occurs at
$$
\boxed{t_{c1}=\frac{L}{u_sa}=\frac{H}{5u_sa},\qquad h_{c1}=H-\frac{(1-a)L}{a}=\frac{H(6a-1)}{5a}.}
$$
The lower front meets the fan's lower edge at
$$
\boxed{t_{c2}=\frac{z_m}{u_s(1-b)}=\frac{4H}{5u_s(1-a/2)},\qquad h_{c2}=\frac{bz_m}{1-b}=\frac{4aH}{5(2-a)}.}
$$
The lower meeting is always physical in the stated range. The upper meeting need not happen before the suspension disappears; this distinction matters for sufficiently small $a$.

For the <curved settling fronts within a rarefaction fan>, the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives $\dot z_u=u_s(\phi-1)$ at the clear-fluid front and $\dot z_d=u_s\phi$ at the deposit front. Substitution of the fan <particle volume fraction> and matching to the straight sections gives
$$
\boxed{z_u=z_m-u_st+2\sqrt{u_saLt},\qquad z_d=z_m+u_st-2\sqrt{u_sDt}.}
$$
The first curve applies after $t_{c1}$ if that meeting occurs, and the second after $t_{c2}$ until it leaves the fan or deposition ends. If both fronts meet inside the fan, their intersection is
$$
\boxed{t_c=\frac{(\sqrt{aL}+\sqrt D)^2}{u_s},\qquad h_c=aL+bz_m=\frac35aH.}
$$
The <last surviving characteristic of a settling fan> joins $(z_m,0)$ to $(h_c,t_c)$. It has constant <particle volume fraction>
$$
\boxed{\phi_* =\frac{\sqrt{aL}}{\sqrt{aL}+\sqrt D},\qquad\frac{h_c-z_m}{t_c}=u_s(2\phi_*-1).}
$$
It is swallowed simultaneously by the clear-fluid and deposit <shocks> at final deposition. This requires $b\leq\phi_*\leq a$, which in the given geometry is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{a\geq a_*=1-\sqrt{\frac23}.}
$$
Equality places the last <characteristic curve> on the upper fan edge. In the generic fan-intersection regime $a>a_*$, both nominal meetings and the two curved fronts are realized.

For $0<a<a_*$ there is <early extinction of a settling rarefaction fan>. The deposit <shock> reaches the upper fan edge before the clear-fluid front does, at
$$
t_e=\frac{D}{u_s(1-a)^2},\qquad z_e=z_m+u_s(2a-1)t_e.
$$
It then travels through the remaining uniform <particle volume fraction> $a$ with speed $u_sa$. The upper front retains $V_1$ until the final intersection. Since the final bed height is still $h_c=3aH/5$, the actual stopping time is
$$
\boxed{t_c=\frac{H-h_c}{u_s(1-a)}=\frac{H(1-3a/5)}{u_s(1-a)},\qquad0<a<a_*.}
$$
In this regime $(h_{c1},t_{c1})$ is only an extrapolated intersection; for $a<1/6$ its extrapolated height is even negative. The line joining the initial internal interface to the final point would have a <particle volume fraction> greater than $a$, so it is not a <characteristic curve> emitted by that fan. **The single fan-<characteristic curve> stopping-time construction does not apply to the entire printed interval $0<a<1/2$; the missing subregime must be included.**

Finally, the <density inversion in a settling suspension> puts more particles, hence denser fluid, above lighter fluid. <Rayleigh-Taylor instability> and overturning can accompany this profile in a real container. They are excluded by the one-dimensional quiescent <kinematic sedimentation> model used for these calculations.
