= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Laurent polynomial ring> is a <localization of a ring>:
$$
R=k[X_1,X_2]_{X_1X_2}.
$$
The <Hilbert basis theorem> makes $k[X_1,X_2]$ a <Noetherian ring>, and <localization of a Noetherian ring> preserves this property. Thus $\boxed{R\text{ is Noetherian}}$.

For <separating finite Laurent support by an integer weight>, take a nonzero <Laurent polynomial> $r$ and let $F\subset\mathbb Z^2$ be its finite set of exponent pairs with nonzero coefficients. Choose an integer $N$ such that
$$
N>\max_{m,m'\in F}|m_1-m'_1|,
$$
where the maximum is zero if $F$ has only one member. Set $(a_1,a_2)=(1,N)$. If $m_1+Nm_2=m'_1+Nm'_2$, then
$$
N|m_2-m'_2|=|m_1-m'_1|<N,
$$
so $m_2=m'_2$ and then $m_1=m'_1$. Consequently all powers of $T$ in $\theta_{(1,N)}(r)$ are distinct. The <linear independence> of the Laurent <monomials> prevents cancellation, giving
$$
\boxed{\theta_{(1,N)}(r)\ne0.}
$$
The choice depends on the finite support of $r$; a single fixed substitution need not detect every nonzero element.

For <Noetherianity of subalgebras of a one-variable Laurent polynomial ring>, let $A$ be a unital $k$-<subalgebra> of $k[T,T^{-1}]$. If $A=k$, it is already a <Noetherian ring>. Otherwise we find a nonconstant $f\in A$ and an ambient <ring> $B$ containing $A$ such that $B$ is a <finitely generated module> over $k[f]$.

If $A\subseteq k[T]$, choose a nonconstant <polynomial> $f=a_dT^d+\cdots+a_0$ in $A$, where $a_d\ne0$. Then $T$ is an <integral element> over $k[f]$, since it satisfies the <monic polynomial> equation
$$
T^d+\frac{a_{d-1}}{a_d}T^{d-1}+\cdots+\frac{a_1}{a_d}T+\frac{a_0-f}{a_d}=0.
$$
Thus $B=k[T]$ is a <finitely generated module> over $k[f]$, generated by $1,T,\ldots,T^{d-1}$. The case $A\subseteq k[T^{-1}]$ is identical after replacing $T$ by $T^{-1}$.

In the remaining case $A$ is contained in neither of these two <polynomial rings>. There is an element with a positive exponent and an element with a negative exponent. If either already has both, use it as $f$. Otherwise their sum has both, since the positive powers of the first and the negative powers of the second cannot cancel. We obtain
$$
f=\sum_{j=-p}^{q}a_jT^j,\qquad p,q>0,\quad a_{-p}a_q\ne0.
$$
Multiplying this identity by $T^p$ gives a <polynomial equation> for $T$ with leading coefficient $a_q$:
$$
\sum_{j=-p}^{q}a_jT^{j+p}-fT^p=0.
$$
Dividing by $a_q$ makes it monic. Similarly, multiplication by $T^{-q}$ gives a monic equation for $T^{-1}$ after division by $a_{-p}$. Both generators are therefore <integral elements> over $k[f]$, so $B=k[T,T^{-1}]$ is a <finitely generated module> over $k[f]$.

In all three cases $k[f]$ is a <Noetherian ring>, as a quotient of a one-variable <polynomial ring>. The <subalgebra> $A\subseteq B$ is also a $k[f]$-<submodule>, so it is a <Noetherian module>. Every <ideal> of $A$ is a $k[f]$-<submodule> of $A$ and is therefore finitely generated over $k[f]$, hence over $A$. This proves
$$
\boxed{\text{Every }k\text{-subalgebra of }k[T,T^{-1}]\text{ is Noetherian}.}
$$

For the final request, take the following <subalgebra> of the two-variable <Laurent polynomial ring>:
$$
\boxed{A=k+X_1k[X_1,X_2]=k[X_1,X_1X_2,X_1X_2^2,\ldots].}
$$
Let $J=X_1k[X_1,X_2]$, an <ideal> of $A$. We have
$$
A/J\cong k,\qquad J^2=X_1^2k[X_1,X_2],\qquad J/J^2\cong X_1k[X_2]
$$
as $k$-<vector spaces>. In particular, the classes of $X_1X_2^j$, for $j\ge0$, are <linearly independent>. If $J$ had finitely many generators as an $A$-<module>, their classes would span $J/J^2$ over $A/J=k$, contradicting its infinite <dimension>. Hence $J$ is not finitely generated and $A$ is not a <Noetherian ring>. This illustrates why the preceding result is special to one Laurent variable.

The <Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz> states that if $k$ is an <algebraically closed field>, every <maximal ideal> of $k[Y_1,\ldots,Y_s]$ is
$$
\boxed{(Y_1-a_1,\ldots,Y_s-a_s)\quad\text{for a unique }(a_1,\ldots,a_s)\in k^s.}
$$
Equivalently, every proper <ideal> has a common zero. We prove the more general field statement, the <Zariski lemma>: a <field> finitely generated as a $k$-<algebra> is a <finite field extension> of $k$.

Write such a field as $L=k[b_1,\ldots,b_s]$. Choose a maximal set of <algebraically independent elements> $t_1,\ldots,t_r$ among these generators and denote the remaining generators by $c_1,\ldots,c_h$. Each $c_j$ is an <algebraic element> over $K=k(t_1,\ldots,t_r)$, so $L/K$ is a <finite field extension>. Clearing the denominators in their monic equations, choose a nonzero $d\in k[t_1,\ldots,t_r]$ such that each $c_j$ is an <integral element> over
$$
D=k[t_1,\ldots,t_r,d^{-1}].
$$
Because $L$ is a field and $d^{-1}\in L$, we have $L=D[c_1,\ldots,c_h]$. Thus $L$ is an <integral extension> of $D$.

A subring over which a field is integral must itself be a field. Indeed, for $0\ne a\in D$, an integral equation for $a^{-1}$ multiplied by $a^{m-1}$ expresses $a^{-1}$ as an element of $D$. But if $r>0$, the ring $D$ is not a field: choose an <irreducible polynomial> $q(t_1)\in k[t_1]$ which does not divide $d$. There are infinitely many monic <irreducible polynomials> in $k[t_1]$, by the usual product-plus-one argument, whereas only finitely many can divide the nonzero $d$. The <prime ideal> $(q)$ stays proper after inverting $d$, so $q$ has no inverse in $D$. Therefore $r=0$, proving that $L/k$ is a <finite field extension>, and in particular an <algebraic field extension>.

Apply the <Zariski lemma> to the <residue field> $k[Y_1,\ldots,Y_s]/\mathfrak m$ of a <maximal ideal>. When $k$ is an <algebraically closed field>, this field is $k$ itself. The images $a_i$ of $Y_i$ then give $\mathfrak m=(Y_1-a_1,\ldots,Y_s-a_s)$, proving the <Weak Hilbert Nullstellensatz>.

For the real <Laurent polynomial ring>, the same argument applies because it is the <finitely generated algebra>
$$
R\cong\mathbb R[U_1,V_1,U_2,V_2]/(U_1V_1-1,U_2V_2-1).
$$
Every <residue field> at a <maximal ideal> is consequently a <finite field extension> of $\mathbb R$, and so is $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. Here we use the <fundamental theorem of algebra>, which makes $\mathbb C$ the <algebraic closure> of $\mathbb R$ of degree two. The images of $X_1,X_2$ must be nonzero since these elements are <units>. It follows that the answer is
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak m_{\alpha,\beta}=\{f\in R:f(\alpha,\beta)=0\},\quad (\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb C^\times)^2,}
$$
with $(\alpha,\beta)$ and $(\overline\alpha,\overline\beta)$ defining the same <maximal ideal>. If both coordinates are real, evaluation has image $\mathbb R$; otherwise its image is $\mathbb C$. Thus every displayed <kernel> is maximal, and the <Zariski lemma> shows that this list is exhaustive.

For explicit <generating sets of an ideal>, there are three cases. For real nonzero $a,b$,
$$
\mathfrak m_{a,b}=(X_1-a,X_2-b).
$$
If $\alpha=a\in\mathbb R^\times$ and $\beta=s+it$ with $t\ne0$,
$$
\mathfrak m_{a,\beta}=(X_1-a,\ X_2^2-2sX_2+s^2+t^2).
$$
If $\alpha=u+iv$ with $v\ne0$ and $\beta=s+it$, then
$$
\mathfrak m_{\alpha,\beta}=\left(X_1^2-2uX_1+u^2+v^2,\ X_2-s-\frac{t}{v}(X_1-u)\right).
$$
The last two quotients are $\mathbb C$; the nonzero coordinate condition ensures that the Laurent inverses exist in them. There are no further coincidences in the classification: equal <kernels> induce an $\mathbb R$-<isomorphism> between the evaluation image fields. The only $\mathbb R$-<automorphisms> of $\mathbb C$ are the identity and <complex conjugation>, so the two pairs must agree or be conjugate.

To prove the intersection assertion, fix a <prime ideal> $P$ and an element $f\notin P$. The <integral domain> $B=R/P$ has nonzero image $\bar f$. Its <localization of a ring> $B[\bar f^{-1}]$ is nonzero and still a <finitely generated algebra> over $\mathbb R$, so it has a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak n$. By the <Zariski lemma>, the field $L=B[\bar f^{-1}]/\mathfrak n$ is finite over $\mathbb R$.

The image of $B$ in $L$ is a subring containing $\mathbb R$ and is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb R$-<vector space>. It is an <integral domain>, hence a field: multiplication by any nonzero element is an injective <linear map> of a finite-dimensional <vector space> and therefore surjective. The <kernel> of $B\to L$ is thus a <maximal ideal> which avoids $\bar f$, since $\bar f$ is invertible in $L$. Its inverse image in $R$ is a <maximal ideal> containing $P$ but avoiding $f$. As this works for every $f\notin P$,
$$
\boxed{P=\bigcap_{\substack{\mathfrak m\supseteq P\\\mathfrak m\text{ maximal}}}\mathfrak m.}
$$
This is the <Jacobson ring> property for this <Laurent polynomial ring>.

For a nonzero <finitely generated module> $M$, its set of <associated primes> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ass}_R(M)=\{\operatorname{Ann}_R(m):0\ne m\in M,\ \operatorname{Ann}_R(m)\text{ is a prime ideal}\}.}
$$
Here $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)=\{a\in R:am=0\}$ is the <annihilator> of the element. The <Laurent polynomial ring> $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, so the nonempty collection of <annihilators> of nonzero elements of $M$ has a maximal member, say $P=\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$. It is proper because $m\ne0$.

If $ab\in P$ and $b\notin P$, then $bm\ne0$ and
$$
P\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_R(bm),\qquad a\in\operatorname{Ann}_R(bm).
$$
Maximality forces $\operatorname{Ann}_R(bm)=P$, hence $a\in P$. Thus $P$ is a <prime ideal>, proving $\boxed{\operatorname{Ass}_R(M)\ne\varnothing}$. This argument in fact only needs that $R$ is <Noetherian>, not the finite generation of $M$.

A proper <ideal> $Q$ is $P$-<primary> when its <radical of an ideal> is $P$ and
$$
\boxed{ab\in Q,\ a\notin Q\ \Longrightarrow\ b^j\in Q\text{ for some }j\ge1.}
$$
Equivalently, $ab\in Q$ and $b\notin P$ imply $a\in Q$; equivalently, every <zero divisor> of $R/Q$ is <nilpotent>. These formulations distinguish the actual <primary ideal> property from the weaker assertion that there is only one <minimal prime ideal>.

For the final example, set $u=X_1-1$ and $v=X_2-1$. Then
$$
\boxed{I=(u^2,uv),\qquad P=(u).}
$$
Since $R/P\cong\mathbb R[X_2,X_2^{-1}]$ is an <integral domain>, $P$ is a <prime ideal>. Moreover $I\subseteq P$ and $u^2\in I$, so $\sqrt I=P$. Consequently $P$ is the unique <minimal prime ideal> over $I$.

Nevertheless $uv\in I$, while $u\notin I$ and no power of $v$ belongs to $I$. For the first assertion, reduce modulo $v$: the image of $I$ is $(u^2)$ in $\mathbb R[X_1,X_1^{-1}]$, and $u$ is not a multiple of $u^2$ because $u$ is a nonunit in this <integral domain>. For the second, $v\notin P$, so $v^j\notin P\supseteq I$ for every $j\ge1$. Thus $I$ is not a $P$-<primary ideal>: a <unique minimal prime does not imply primary>. In $R/I$, the nonzero class of $u$ is killed by $v$, a <zero divisor> which is not <nilpotent>.

Put $u=X_1-1$ and $v=X_2-1$. Evaluation at $(1,1)$ is surjective, and
$$
R/(u,v)\cong\mathbb Z,
$$
so its <kernel> is the <prime ideal> $\boxed{P=(u,v)}$. In particular $P/P^2$ is naturally an $R/P=\mathbb Z$-<module>, generated by the classes of $u,v$.

For integers $a,b$, including negative integers, reduction modulo $P^2$ gives
$$
(1+u)^a(1+v)^b\equiv1+au+bv\pmod{P^2}.
$$
For negative powers, this follows from $(1+u)^{-1}\equiv1-u$ and $(1+v)^{-1}\equiv1-v$; the mixed product $uv$ vanishes modulo $P^2$. Therefore a <Laurent polynomial> $f$ satisfies
$$
f\equiv\theta(f)+D_1(f)u+D_2(f)v\pmod{P^2},\qquad D_i(f)=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_i}\right|_{(1,1)}\in\mathbb Z.
$$
The <formal derivatives> obey the <product rule>, so $D_i(ab)=\theta(a)D_i(b)+\theta(b)D_i(a)$. If $a,b\in P$, both terms vanish. Hence both $D_i$ vanish on $P^2$ and define a map
$$
P/P^2\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^2,\qquad [f]\longmapsto(D_1(f),D_2(f)).
$$
It sends $[u]$ to $(1,0)$ and $[v]$ to $(0,1)$, and the displayed expansion gives its inverse. Thus the <conormal module> has the explicit basis
$$
\boxed{P/P^2\cong\mathbb Z^2,\quad [u],[v]\text{ a basis}.}
$$

Let $A=R_P$ be the <localization at a prime ideal>, with <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m=PR_P$. Every nonzero integer lies outside $P$ and is therefore inverted. The <residue field> is
$$
\boxed{A/\mathfrak m=\operatorname{Frac}(R/P)=\mathbb Q.}
$$
Indeed there is an explicit identification
$$
A\cong\mathbb Q[u,v]_{(u,v)}:
$$
inverting $1+u$ and $1+v$ has no effect in this <local ring>, and a polynomial denominator lies outside $(u,v)$ precisely when its constant term is nonzero. Conversely, clearing rational denominators turns every such fraction into a fraction from $R_P$.

For the <Krull dimension>, the <prime ideal correspondence for localization> preserves the strict chain
$$
(0)\subsetneq(u)\subsetneq(u,v)
$$
of <prime ideals> in $R$. They are prime because the successive quotients are the <integral domains> $R$, $\mathbb Z[X_2,X_2^{-1}]$, and $\mathbb Z$. They remain distinct after <localization at a prime ideal> because they are all contained in $P$. Hence $\dim A\ge2$.

For the upper bound, $R$ is a <Noetherian ring> by the <Hilbert basis theorem> and <localization of a Noetherian ring>. Since $P$ is generated by two elements, the <Krull height theorem> gives $\operatorname{ht}P\le2$. The <prime ideal correspondence for localization> identifies $\dim R_P$ with $\operatorname{ht}P$, so
$$
\boxed{\dim R_P=2.}
$$

A <Noetherian local ring> $(B,\mathfrak n)$ with <residue field> $\kappa$ is a <regular local ring> if
$$
\boxed{\dim_\kappa(\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2)=\dim B.}
$$
By the <Nakayama lemma>, the dimension on the left is the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak n$; this is also called the <embedding dimension>.

The <localization of a module> is exact and commutes with quotients and products of ideals. Applying it to the already computed <conormal module> gives the <cotangent space of a local ring>
$$
\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\cong(P/P^2)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Q\cong\mathbb Q^2.
$$
Its <dimension> is two, equal to $\dim A$. Therefore $\boxed{R_P\text{ is a regular local ring}}$. Notice that $P/P^2$ has a basis over $\mathbb Z$, whereas the localized <cotangent space of a local ring> has a basis over the <residue field> $\mathbb Q$.

An <integral domain> $A$ is an <integrally closed domain> if every element of its <fraction field> which is an <integral element> over $A$ belongs to $A$. Explicitly, if $z\in\operatorname{Frac}A$ satisfies
$$
z^d+a_{d-1}z^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0=0,\qquad a_i\in A,
$$
then $z\in A$. Thus $\boxed{A\text{ is integrally closed exactly when its integral closure in its fraction field is }A}$.

Suppose $A$ is a <unique factorization domain> and $z=a/b$ is integral, with $a,b\in A$, $b\ne0$, and with no common <irreducible element> as a factor. Multiplying its monic equation by $b^d$ gives
$$
a^d+a_{d-1}a^{d-1}b+\cdots+a_0b^d=0.
$$
Hence $b$ divides $a^d$. Every <irreducible element> dividing $b$ is a <prime element>, so it would divide $a$, contrary to the choice of the fraction. Therefore $b$ is a <unit>, and $z\in A$. We have proved
$$
\boxed{\text{Every unique factorization domain is integrally closed}.}
$$

The <going-down theorem> is the following. Let $A\subseteq B$ be an <integral extension> of <integral domains> with $A$ an <integrally closed domain>. If
$$
\mathfrak p_1\subseteq\mathfrak p_2\quad\text{in }A,\qquad
\mathfrak q_2\cap A=\mathfrak p_2\quad\text{for a prime ideal }\mathfrak q_2\subset B,
$$
then
$$
\boxed{\text{there is a prime ideal }\mathfrak q_1\subseteq\mathfrak q_2\text{ with }\mathfrak q_1\cap A=\mathfrak p_1.}
$$
We first prove this for a <module-finite ring extension> and then remove that extra hypothesis. We use the <lying-over theorem>, the <going-up theorem>, and the <incomparability theorem for integral extensions>: primes exist over every base prime, prime chains can be extended upwards, and distinct primes over the same base prime cannot contain one another. The <going-up theorem> follows by applying the <lying-over theorem> to the quotient by the lower prime. These results concern arbitrary <integral extensions>; none assumes the <going-down theorem>.

For the finite case, let $K=\operatorname{Frac}A$ and $L=\operatorname{Frac}B$. Since $B$ is module-finite, $L/K$ is a <finite field extension>. Take a finite <normal field extension> $E/K$ containing $L$, obtained as a <splitting field> of the minimal polynomials of finitely many field generators. The extension $E/K$ need not be a <separable field extension>. Let $C$ be the <integral closure> of $A$ in $E$. Then $B\subseteq C$, and $C$ is integral over both $A$ and $B$.

We need one auxiliary fact: the <finite group> $G=\operatorname{Aut}_K(E)$ acts transitively on the <prime ideals> of $C$ over any fixed <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$ of $A$. First observe that an element $w\in E$ fixed by all of $G$ is a <purely inseparable algebraic element> over $K$. Indeed, all $K$-embeddings of $K(w)$ into an <algebraic closure> extend to $K$-<automorphisms> of the <normal field extension> $E$. Hence the <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $w$ has only one distinct root. In <characteristic zero> this says $w\in K$; in characteristic $p>0$ it says $w^{p^e}\in K$ for some $e\ge0$.

Now let $Q,Q'$ be primes of $C$ over $\mathfrak p$, and suppose $Q$ is different from every $\sigma(Q')$, $\sigma\in G$. By the <incomparability theorem for integral extensions>, $Q$ is contained in none of these conjugate primes. The <prime avoidance> lemma supplies
$$
x\in Q\setminus\bigcup_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(Q').
$$
The product
$$
h=\prod_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(x)
$$
is fixed by $G$, is integral over $A$, and lies in $Q$ because one factor is $x$. Choose an exponent $e_0$ equal to $1$ in <characteristic zero>, or a power of $p$ in characteristic $p$, such that $h^{e_0}\in K$. Since $A$ is an <integrally closed domain>, $h^{e_0}\in A$. It also lies in $Q\cap A=\mathfrak p$, hence in $Q'$. Because $Q'$ is a <prime ideal>, some factor $\sigma(x)$ lies in $Q'$. This means $x\in\sigma^{-1}(Q')$, contradicting our choice. Thus $Q=\sigma(Q')$ for some $\sigma$, proving transitivity. The power $e_0$ accounts for the possible <purely inseparable field extension> and makes the argument valid in every characteristic.

Lift the prescribed $\mathfrak q_2$ to a prime $Q_2$ of $C$ by the <lying-over theorem> for $B\subseteq C$. Separately, choose a prime $Q'_1$ over $\mathfrak p_1$ and use the <going-up theorem> to find $Q'_2\supseteq Q'_1$ over $\mathfrak p_2$. Transitivity gives $\sigma\in G$ with $\sigma(Q'_2)=Q_2$. Then
$$
Q_1=\sigma(Q'_1)\subseteq Q_2,
\qquad Q_1\cap A=\mathfrak p_1.
$$
Contracting to $B$ gives $\mathfrak q_1=Q_1\cap B\subseteq\mathfrak q_2$ with the required contraction. This proves the <going-down theorem> for the module-finite case.

For a general <integral extension> $A\subseteq B$, consider $B_{\mathfrak q_2}$ and the <ideal>
$$
J=\mathfrak p_1B_{\mathfrak q_2}.
$$
We claim that $J$ is disjoint from $A\setminus\mathfrak p_1$. Otherwise, clearing the finitely many denominators in an expression for an element $a\in J\cap(A\setminus\mathfrak p_1)$ gives
$$
s a=\sum_{i=1}^{h}a_i b_i,\qquad
s\in B\setminus\mathfrak q_2,\quad a_i\in\mathfrak p_1,\quad b_i\in B.
$$
The <subalgebra> $B_0=A[s,b_1,\ldots,b_h]$ is a <module-finite ring extension> of $A$, since all its generators are <integral elements>. Apply the finite case to $\mathfrak q_2\cap B_0$: there is a prime $Q_1\subseteq\mathfrak q_2\cap B_0$ contracting to $\mathfrak p_1$. The displayed identity puts $sa$ in $Q_1$, but $a\notin Q_1$. Thus $s\in Q_1\subseteq\mathfrak q_2$, a contradiction.

Localize $B_{\mathfrak q_2}$ further at the <multiplicative subset> $A\setminus\mathfrak p_1$. The extension of $J$ is a proper <ideal>, by the disjointness just proved. Choose a <maximal ideal> containing it and contract back to a <prime ideal> of $B_{\mathfrak q_2}$. This prime contains $J$ and avoids $A\setminus\mathfrak p_1$. Its contraction $\mathfrak q_1$ to $B$ lies inside $\mathfrak q_2$ by the <prime ideal correspondence for localization>, and its contraction to $A$ is exactly $\mathfrak p_1$. This completes the proof of the <going-down theorem> without any finite generation assumption on $B$.

The <Artin-Rees lemma> states that, for a <Noetherian ring> $R$, an <ideal> $I$, a <finitely generated module> $M$, and a <submodule> $N\subseteq M$, there is $c\ge0$ such that
$$
\boxed{I^jM\cap N=I^{j-c}(I^cM\cap N)\quad\text{for every }j\ge c.}
$$
Thus the filtration on $N$ induced from the <I-adic filtration> of $M$ is eventually determined by one of its terms.

For the proof, form the <Rees ring> and its <Rees module>:
$$
\mathcal R(I)=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}I^jt^j\subseteq R[t],\qquad
\mathcal R_I(M)=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}I^jMt^j.
$$
Since $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>, write $I=(a_1,\ldots,a_s)$. Then $\mathcal R(I)=R[a_1t,\ldots,a_st]$, so it is a <Noetherian ring> by the <Hilbert basis theorem>. Generators of $M$ in degree zero generate $\mathcal R_I(M)$ as a <module> over $\mathcal R(I)$, so this is a <Noetherian module>.

The graded <submodule>
$$
L=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}(I^jM\cap N)t^j
$$
is therefore finitely generated. Choose homogeneous generators $z_\alpha t^{d_\alpha}$ with $d_\alpha\le c$ for some $c$. For $j\ge c$, taking degree-$j$ components gives
$$
I^jM\cap N=\sum_\alpha I^{j-d_\alpha}z_\alpha
=I^{j-c}\sum_\alpha I^{c-d_\alpha}z_\alpha.
$$
The same generators show that the final sum is exactly $I^cM\cap N$. This proves the <Artin-Rees lemma>. If $L=0$, one may simply take $c=0$.

Now let
$$
K=\bigcap_{j\ge1}I^jM.
$$
It is a <submodule> of the <Noetherian module> $M$, hence a <finitely generated module>. Apply the <Artin-Rees lemma> with $N=K$ and $j=c+1$. Since $K$ is contained in every $I^jM$ (including $I^0M=M$), its conclusion reduces to
$$
K=IK.
$$
Write generators as $y_1,\ldots,y_h$. There is a matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ with entries in $I$ such that $y_i=\sum_j a_{ij}y_j$. Multiplication by the adjugate of $I_h-A$ shows that
$$
\det(I_h-A)y_i=0\quad\text{for all }i.
$$
The <determinant> is congruent to $1$ modulo $I$, so it is $1+r$ for some $r\in I$. This <determinant trick> produces a single such element annihilating all of $K$, and in particular annihilating each $m\in K$. If $K=0$, use $r=0$.

Conversely, if $(1+r)m=0$ with $r\in I$, then $m=-rm$. Iterating gives $m=(-r)^jm\in I^jM$ for every $j\ge1$. Hence the exact description is
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_{j\ge1}I^jM=\{m\in M:\text{some }r\in I\text{ satisfies }(1+r)m=0\}.}
$$
In a <local ring> with $I$ contained in the <maximal ideal>, every $1+r$ is a <unit>, so this also yields the usual vanishing form of the <Krull intersection theorem>.

For a failure without the <Noetherian ring> hypothesis, let $D=\mathbb Z[1/2]_{\ge0}$ and use the <semigroup algebra>
$$
R=k[t^q:q\in D]=\bigcup_{h\ge0}k[t^{1/2^h}].
$$
Its elements are finite sums of formal <monomials> $t^q$, with multiplication $t^qt^{q'}=t^{q+q'}$. Each ring in the union is a <polynomial ring> and the inclusions are injective, so $R$ is an <integral domain>. It is not a <Noetherian ring>, since
$$
(t)\subsetneq(t^{1/2})\subsetneq(t^{1/4})\subsetneq\cdots.
$$
Indeed the generator on the left is the square of the next generator, while division in the opposite direction would require a negative exponent, which is unavailable in $R$.

Let $I$ be the <ideal> of elements with zero constant term, equivalently
$$
I=(t^q:q\in D,\ q>0).
$$
It is proper because taking the constant term gives $R/I\cong k$. Every generator $t^q$ is $(t^{q/2})^2$, with $t^{q/2}\in I$, so $I^2=I$. Thus this is a nonzero <idempotent ideal>, with $I^j=I$ for every $j\ge1$. Take
$$
\boxed{m=t\in\bigcap_{j\ge1}I^j.}
$$
For every $r\in I$, the constant term of $1+r$ is $1$, so $1+r\ne0$. Since $R$ is an <integral domain> and $t\ne0$, we have $(1+r)t\ne0$. This gives all three requested objects and proves that no element of the required form annihilates $m$.

= Solution
{parent=6}

For a <finitely generated module> $V=\bigoplus_dV_d$ over the <graded ring> $S=k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$, with each $X_i$ of degree one, its <Poincare series of a graded module> is
$$
P_V(t)=\sum_d\dim_k(V_d)t^d.
$$
This is also its <Hilbert series>. One usually takes $d\ge0$; allowing an integer grading also permits finitely many negative degrees. Homogeneous generators of degrees $a_1,\ldots,a_h$ give a surjection $\bigoplus_j S(-a_j)\to V$, where the <graded shift> is defined by $V(-a)_d=V_{d-a}$. Consequently every $V_d$ is a finite-dimensional <vector space> and $V_d=0$ for all sufficiently negative $d$.

The <Hilbert-Serre theorem> in the standard grading says
$$
\boxed{P_V(t)=\frac{Q(t)}{(1-t)^n},\qquad Q(t)\in\mathbb Z[t,t^{-1}].}
$$
For a nonnegatively graded $V$, the numerator is in $\mathbb Z[t]$. Equivalently, the only possible pole of this rational expression is at $t=1$, with order at most $n$.

We prove the <Hilbert-Serre theorem> by induction on the number of variables. For $n=0$, $S=k$ and $V$ is a finite-dimensional <graded module>; thus $P_V(t)$ is a Laurent <polynomial>. Suppose $n>0$ and put
$$
K=0:_VX_n=\{v\in V:X_nv=0\},\qquad C=V/X_nV.
$$
The <Hilbert basis theorem> makes $S$ a <Noetherian ring>, so $K$ is a <finitely generated module>, as is $C$. Both are <graded modules> killed by $X_n$, hence finitely generated over $S'=k[X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}]$.

The graded <exact sequence>
$$
0\longrightarrow K(-1)\longrightarrow V(-1)
\xrightarrow{\ X_n\ }V\longrightarrow C\longrightarrow0
$$
identifies the <kernel> and <cokernel> of multiplication by $X_n$. Taking the alternating sum of the finite-dimensional degree components yields
$$
tP_K(t)-tP_V(t)+P_V(t)-P_C(t)=0,
$$
so
$$
(1-t)P_V(t)=P_C(t)-tP_K(t).
$$
By induction the right side has denominator $(1-t)^{n-1}$, which proves the required denominator $(1-t)^n$. Since $(1-t)^nP_V(t)$ has no negative powers when $V$ is nonnegatively graded, its Laurent <polynomial> numerator is an ordinary <polynomial> in that case.

For $n\ge1$, this also implies the usual <Hilbert polynomial> consequence. Writing $Q(t)=\sum_jq_jt^j$ and using
$$
\frac1{(1-t)^n}=\sum_{d\ge0}\binom{d+n-1}{n-1}t^d
$$
gives, for all sufficiently large $d$,
$$
\dim_kV_d=\sum_jq_j\binom{d-j+n-1}{n-1},
$$
a <polynomial> in $d$ of degree at most $n-1$. For $n=0$, the graded pieces are eventually zero.

For the final request, a <free resolution> of $V$ is an <exact sequence>
$$
\cdots\longrightarrow F_2\xrightarrow{d_2}F_1\xrightarrow{d_1}F_0\longrightarrow V\longrightarrow0
$$
with each $F_i$ a <free module>. In the graded setting we take finite <direct sums> of shifts $S(-a)$ and maps preserving degree. The resolution has length at most $n$ if $F_i=0$ for every $i>n$. The <syzygy modules> are the successive <kernels> which record the relations among generators, then relations among those relations, and so on.

Put $\mathfrak m=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, the homogeneous <maximal ideal> with $S/\mathfrak m=k$. Choose a homogeneous $k$-basis of $V/\mathfrak mV$ and lift it to $V$. These lifts generate $V$ by the <graded Nakayama lemma>: a bounded-below <graded module> $W$ satisfying $W=\mathfrak mW$ must be zero, since a nonzero homogeneous element of least degree could not be a sum of variables times elements of lower degrees. Apply this to the quotient by the <submodule> generated by the chosen lifts. This gives a surjection $F_0\to V$ inducing an <isomorphism> modulo $\mathfrak m$.

Its <kernel> is finitely generated because $S$ is a <Noetherian ring>. Repeat the construction for that kernel, then for each subsequent kernel. We obtain a <minimal graded free resolution>, meaning
$$
d_i(F_i)\subseteq\mathfrak mF_{i-1}\qquad(i\ge1).
$$
Indeed, at each stage the free cover induces an <isomorphism> modulo $\mathfrak m$, so its kernel lies in $\mathfrak m$ times its source. The sequence is exact by construction, though it may at first appear infinite.

The <Koszul complex> on $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ is
$$
K_i=\bigwedge^i S^n\cong S(-i)^{\binom ni},\qquad 0\le i\le n,
$$
with basis vectors $e_j$ given degree one, and differential
$$
\partial(e_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_i})
=\sum_{a=1}^{i}(-1)^{a-1}X_{j_a}
 e_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{e_{j_a}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_i}.
$$
The hat means omission of that factor. Every pair of terms in $\partial^2$ cancels with opposite signs, so this is a <chain complex>. The sequence $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ is a <regular sequence>: after quotienting by the first $j-1$ variables, the next variable is a <non-zero-divisor> in the remaining <polynomial ring>.

The <Koszul complex> is consequently exact in positive degrees and has $H_0=k$, so it is a <Koszul resolution> of $k$ of length $n$. For completeness, one proves this by induction: for one variable it is $0\to S\xrightarrow{X_1}S\to S/(X_1)\to0$. Appending $X_j$ constructs the <mapping cone> of multiplication by $X_j$ on the previous <chain complex>. Its <homology> is zero in positive degrees because $X_j$ acts injectively on $S/(X_1,\ldots,X_{j-1})$; its degree-zero <homology> is the further quotient. Here a <mapping cone> combines a <chain complex> with a shifted copy and adds the given multiplication map to the differential, thereby measuring its <kernel> and <cokernel> on <homology>.

The <Tor functor> $\operatorname{Tor}_i^S(V,k)$ is defined as the degree-$i$ <homology> of $F_\bullet\otimes_S k$ for a <free resolution> $F_\bullet$ of $V$. It can equally be computed as the <homology> of $V\otimes_S K_\bullet$, using the <Koszul resolution> of $k$. To see the equality, form the <double complex> $F_\bullet\otimes_S K_\bullet$. Taking <homology> first along $F_\bullet$ leaves $V\otimes_S K_\bullet$, since each $K_i$ is a <free module>; taking <homology> first along $K_\bullet$ leaves $F_\bullet\otimes_S k$, since each $F_i$ is free. The finite sums along each total degree identify both with the <homology> of the total complex. This also explains the symmetry used in computing the <Tor functor>.

Since $K_i=0$ for $i>n$, we have $\operatorname{Tor}_i^S(V,k)=0$ for $i>n$. On the other hand, all differentials of the <minimal graded free resolution> become zero after tensoring with $k=S/\mathfrak m$, so
$$
\operatorname{Tor}_i^S(V,k)\cong F_i\otimes_S k=F_i/\mathfrak mF_i.
$$
Thus $F_i/\mathfrak mF_i=0$ for $i>n$, and the <graded Nakayama lemma> implies $F_i=0$ for those $i$. We have proved the required <Hilbert syzygy theorem>:
$$
\boxed{0\longrightarrow F_n\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow F_0\longrightarrow V\longrightarrow0,}
$$
where some initial $F_i$ may be zero. The argument supplies finitely generated graded <free modules>, which is stronger than merely giving an ungraded <free resolution>.
