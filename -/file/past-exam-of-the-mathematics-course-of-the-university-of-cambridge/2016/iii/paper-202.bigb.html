= Solution

Use a <filtered probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t),\mathbb P)$, and put $\mathcal F_{0-}=\mathcal F_0$. The **<predictable sigma-algebra>** on $\Omega\times[0,\infty)$ is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal P=\sigma\{A\times\{0\},\ A\in\mathcal F_0;\ A\times(s,t],\ A\in\mathcal F_s,\ 0\leq s<t<\infty\}.}
$$
Equivalently it is generated by left-continuous <adapted processes>. A real-valued **<previsible process>** is a function $(\omega,t)\mapsto H_t(\omega)$ that is <measurable> for $\mathcal P$ and the <Borel sigma-algebra> of $\mathbb R$.

A **<simple predictable process>** has the form
$$
H=h_{-1}\mathbf1_{\{0\}}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_i\mathbf1_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]},\qquad 0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n<\infty,
$$
where $h_{-1}$ is bounded and $\mathcal F_0$-<measurable>, and each $h_i$ is bounded and $\mathcal F_{t_i}$-<measurable>. Set $H=0$ after $t_n$; allowing a final infinite interval gives an equivalent dense class. The values may be restricted to finitely many values if desired, by approximating bounded <measurable functions> by simple functions. The initial-time term matters for arbitrary <finite measures> on $\mathcal P$: a measure can charge time zero, even though the <stochastic integral> against a continuous <martingale> does not.

For fixed $u>0$, define the <left-limit sigma-algebra> by $\mathcal F_{u-}=\sigma(\bigcup_{s<u}\mathcal F_s)$. The section at $u$ of a generating rectangle $A\times(s,t]$ of the <predictable sigma-algebra> is $A$ when $s<u\leq t$, and empty otherwise. In the first case $A\in\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_{u-}$. Sections of the time-zero generators are empty for $u>0$.

The collection of subsets of sample-time space whose section at $u$ belongs to $\mathcal F_{u-}$ is a <sigma-algebra>: sections commute with complements and countable unions. It therefore contains all of $\mathcal P$. Apply this to $\{(\omega,t):H_t(\omega)\in E\}$ for every <Borel set> $E$. Thus **<predictable sections at deterministic times> satisfy**
$$
\boxed{H_u\text{ is }\mathcal F_{u-}\text{-measurable}.}
$$
At $u=0$ the same argument gives $\mathcal F_0$-<measurability>, using the convention $\mathcal F_{0-}=\mathcal F_0$. The PDF asks for information strictly before $u$; the TeX aid drops the minus sign.

Let $V$ be the closure of the <simple predictable processes> in the <Hilbert space> $L^2(\mathcal P,\mu)$. Common refinement of deterministic partitions makes these processes a vector space. To prove **<density of simple predictable processes for finite measures>**, suppose $g\in V^\perp$. Since $\mu$ is a <finite measure>, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $g\in L^1(\mu)$, so
$$
\nu(E)=\int_Eg\,d\mu
$$
defines a finite signed <measure>. The indicator of each predictable generating rectangle, including each time-zero rectangle, is a <simple predictable process>; hence $\nu$ vanishes on these rectangles.

Finite intersections of the rectangles are again rectangles or empty. Add the whole space to this <pi-system>. Its $\nu$-mass is zero because $\mathbf1_{\Omega\times[0,n]}$ is simple and converges to $1$ in $L^2(\mu)$ by the <dominated convergence theorem>. The zero sets of $\nu$ form a <lambda-system> now that its total mass is zero. The <Pi-lambda theorem> therefore gives $\nu(E)=0$ for every $E\in\mathcal P$. Taking the sets where $g>0$ and $g<0$ gives $g=0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere.

Thus $V^\perp=\{0\}$ and the <orthogonal complement> criterion for a closed subspace of a <Hilbert space> yields
$$
\boxed{\overline{\mathcal S}^{\,L^2(\mathcal P,\mu)}=L^2(\mathcal P,\mu).}
$$
Omitting the time-zero term would make this conclusion false when $\mu$ is concentrated at time zero.

For the <simple predictable process> from part (a), define its **<stochastic integral>** by
$$
\boxed{(H\cdot M)_t=\sum_i h_i\bigl(M_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-M_{t\wedge t_i}\bigr).}
$$
The time-zero value contributes nothing. Write $D_i=M_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-M_{t\wedge t_i}$. If $i<j$ and $t>t_j$, then $h_iD_i h_j$ is $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-<measurable> and $\mathbb E[D_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_j}]=0$. If $t\leq t_j$, then $D_j=0$. Boundedness ensures <integrability>, so all off-diagonal terms vanish by <conditional expectation>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[(H\cdot M)_t^2]=\sum_i\mathbb E[h_i^2D_i^2].}
$$
The <quadratic variation> theorem says $M^2-[M]$ is a <local martingale>. A bounded continuous <martingale> is <square-integrable>, and localization gives the conditional second-moment identity
$$
\mathbb E[D_i^2\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]=\mathbb E\bigl([M]_{t\wedge t_{i+1}}-[M]_{t_i}\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}\bigr)\qquad(t\geq t_i).
$$
Multiplication by $h_i^2$ and summation therefore give the **<Itô isometry>**
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left|\int_0^t H_s\,dM_s\right|^2=\mathbb E\int_0^t H_s^2\,d[M]_s.}
$$
For <Brownian motion>, $[M]_s=s$. More generally the <Itô isometry> holds for every <predictable process> with finite right-hand side, against a continuous <square-integrable> <martingale>. Indeed, on a fixed horizon the <quadratic-variation measure> $\mu_M(E)=\mathbb E\int_0^t\mathbf1_E\,d[M]$ is finite. Part (c) approximates the integrand by <simple predictable processes>; the displayed equality makes the elementary integrals Cauchy in $L^2$ and defines their unique limit. The finite sum is exactly the elementary case of the <Itô isometry>.

On the same <measurable space>, **<absolute continuity of measures>** means
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\mathbb P}\ll\mathbb P\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb P(A)=0\implies\widetilde{\mathbb P}(A)=0\text{ for every }A\in\mathcal F.}
$$
The <Radon-Nikodym theorem> then supplies a nonnegative <measurable> density $Z$ with $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P}Z=1$ and $\widetilde{\mathbb P}(A)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P}[Z\mathbf1_A]$. The converse follows directly from this formula. Strict positivity of the <Radon-Nikodym derivative> gives equivalence of the two <probability measures>; <absolute continuity of measures> alone need not give equivalence.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a <continuous local martingale> $M$, its **<stochastic exponential>** is
$$
\boxed{Z_t=\mathcal E(M)_t=\exp\{M_t-M_0-\tfrac12[M]_t\}.}
$$
The <Itô formula> gives $dZ_t=Z_t\,dM_t$ and $Z_0=1$, so $Z$ is a strictly positive <local martingale>. The finite-horizon **<Girsanov theorem>** says: if $Z$ is a true <martingale> on $[0,T]$ and $d\widetilde{\mathbb P}=Z_T\,d\mathbb P$ on $\mathcal F_T$, then for every <continuous local martingale> $N$ under $\mathbb P$,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde N_t=N_t-[N,M]_t\text{ is a }\widetilde{\mathbb P}\text{-local martingale},\quad 0\leq t\leq T.}
$$
For an infinite-horizon <change of measure>, assume $Z$ is a <uniformly integrable> <martingale> and use its terminal density $Z_\infty$. In particular, if $M=\int\theta\,dB$, the process $\widetilde B=B-\int\theta\,ds$ is <Brownian motion> under the new <probability measure>, by the <Lévy characterization of Brownian motion>.

**The <quadratic variation> cannot change.** Adding the continuous <finite-variation process> $-[N,M]$ leaves $[\widetilde N]=[N]$. Also the same pathwise partition sums defining $[N]$ have the same limit under an absolutely continuous <change of measure>; convergence in <probability> transfers by integrability of the density. Thus the bracket of the unchanged process $N$, now a <semimartingale>, is also unchanged. The <local martingale> property itself need not survive. This is <quadratic covariation under an absolutely continuous measure change>.

= Solution

The **<Novikov condition> holds on every horizon**, since
$$
\mathbb E\exp\{\tfrac12[M]_T\}\leq e^{C/2}<\infty.
$$
Therefore $Z=\mathcal E(M)$ is a true <martingale> and the <Girsanov theorem> applies through each finite $T$.

The deterministic bound also gives a genuine infinite-horizon density. For any $p>1$, the <stochastic exponential> identity is
$$
Z_t^p=\mathcal E(pM)_t\exp\{\tfrac12p(p-1)[M]_t\}.
$$
A nonnegative <local martingale> is a <supermartingale>, so
$$
\boxed{\sup_t\mathbb E Z_t^p\leq\exp\{\tfrac12p(p-1)C\}.}
$$
This $L^p$ bound gives <uniform integrability>. Also $M$ is a <L2-bounded continuous martingale>, since localization and the <Itô isometry> give $\mathbb E M_t^2=\mathbb E[M]_t\leq C$. The <L2 martingale convergence theorem> gives a finite limit $M_\infty$; hence $Z_\infty=\exp(M_\infty-[M]_\infty/2)>0$ and $\mathbb E Z_\infty=1$. Weighting by $Z_\infty$ defines an equivalent <probability measure> on $\mathcal F_\infty$. This is the <bounded-bracket criterion for a stochastic exponential>.

The reference <Brownian motion> must start at $x$, and both laws must use the same stopped-path rule. Under the reference <probability measure> let $R_s=x+W_s$, let $\tau$ be its exit time from $(a,b)$, and put $T=t\wedge\tau$ and $c=(\delta-1)/2$. The **<Girsanov density for a stopped Bessel process>**, as a functional of the stopped reference path, is
$$
\boxed{D(R)=\exp\left\{c\int_0^T\frac{dW_s}{R_s}-\frac{c^2}{2}\int_0^T\frac{ds}{R_s^2}\right\}.}
$$
Use the <predictable process> $\theta_s=cR_s^{-1}\mathbf1_{\{s\leq\tau\}}$, with any harmless choice at the endpoint. On $[0,t]$, $\int\theta_s^2ds\leq c^2t/a^2$. The <Novikov condition> holds; under the density $D$, the <Girsanov theorem> makes $\widetilde W_s=W_s-\int_0^s\theta_u du$ a <Brownian motion>. Before exit, $dR_s=cR_s^{-1}ds+d\widetilde W_s$, which is the required <stochastic differential equation>. The drift is a <locally Lipschitz function> on $(0,\infty)$, so <pathwise uniqueness> and <uniqueness in law> hold up to this exit time.

For a version involving just the endpoint and an ordinary integral, the <Itô formula> for $\log R$ gives
$$
\int_0^T\frac{dW_s}{R_s}=\log\frac{R_T}{x}+\frac12\int_0^T\frac{ds}{R_s^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{D(R)=\left(\frac{R_T}{x}\right)^{(\delta-1)/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\delta-1)(\delta-3)}8\int_0^T\frac{ds}{R_s^2}\right\}.}
$$
The stopped density is <measurable> from the stopped path, so it is also the <Radon-Nikodym derivative> of the stopped-path laws. The stopping times in this formula are evaluated on the reference path, rather than taken from a different realization of $X$. A zero-start reference <Brownian motion> would have a different deterministic initial value and could not dominate this law.

A **<weak solution of a stochastic differential equation>** consists of a <filtered probability space>, an <adapted> continuous process $X$, and a <Brownian motion> $B$ relative to that <filtration>, with specified initial distribution, such that for every finite $t$ the coefficient integrals are finite and
$$
\boxed{X_t=X_0+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s)\,dB_s+\int_0^t b(X_s)\,ds\quad\text{almost surely for all }t.}
$$
In one dimension the integrability conditions are $\int_0^t\sigma(X_s)^2ds<\infty$ and $\int_0^t|b(X_s)|ds<\infty$. The noise integral is the <Itô integral>. A solution on a prescribed stochastic basis with prescribed $B$ is distinguished from this weak construction.

A **<strong solution of a stochastic differential equation>** uses the prescribed $B$ and initial variable, and is <adapted> to their augmented <natural filtration>; thus no additional randomness is needed. For deterministic $X_0=x$, each $X_t$ is determined by the driving <Brownian motion> through time $t$. Merely being <adapted> to a larger <filtration> is insufficient for this definition.

**<Uniqueness in law>** means that any two <weak stochastic solutions> with the same initial distribution have the same law of $X$ on continuous path space. **<Pathwise uniqueness>** means that two solutions on one stochastic basis, with the same $B$ and almost surely equal initial values, are <indistinguishable stochastic processes>. The latter compares paths under the same noise, rather than just comparing their <probability distributions>.

Fix $X_0=x$ and a finite horizon $T$. Begin under <Wiener measure> $\mathbb P$ with $X_t=x+W_t$, and let $K$ bound $|b|$. The bounded <progressively measurable process> $b(X)$ is an admissible <Itô integral> integrand. Put
$$
Z_T=\exp\left\{\int_0^T b(X_s)\,dW_s-\frac12\int_0^T b(X_s)^2ds\right\}.
$$
The <Novikov condition> follows from $\int_0^T b(X_s)^2ds\leq K^2T$. With $d\mathbb Q=Z_Td\mathbb P$, the <Girsanov theorem> says that $B=W-\int_0^\cdot b(X_s)ds$ is <Brownian motion>. Therefore **a <weak stochastic solution> exists**, since
$$
\boxed{X_t=x+B_t+\int_0^t b(X_s)ds.}
$$
Consistent finite-horizon densities on canonical continuous path space give a solution for all finite times. No uniform integrability at infinite time is needed for this extension.

For **<uniqueness in law>**, take any <weak stochastic solution> under a measure $\mathbb Q$ and reverse the <change of measure>:
$$
R_T=\exp\left\{-\int_0^T b(X_s)\,dB_s-\frac12\int_0^T b(X_s)^2ds\right\},\qquad d\mathbb P=R_Td\mathbb Q.
$$
Again the <Novikov condition> holds. Under $\mathbb P$, $W=B+\int b(X_s)ds=X-x$ is <Brownian motion>, so the law of $X$ is the fixed shifted <Wiener measure>. Substitution of $dB=dX-b(X)dt$ gives
$$
\boxed{R_T^{-1}=\exp\left\{\int_0^T b(X_s)\,dX_s-\frac12\int_0^T b(X_s)^2ds\right\}.}
$$
Under shifted <Wiener measure>, the <stochastic integral> on the right has a fixed <measurable> version as a functional of the canonical path. It can be defined by <simple predictable processes> converging in the integrand $L^2$ norm and an almost surely convergent subsequence of their integrals. Equivalence of the two finite-horizon measures preserves that version. Thus for every bounded <measurable> path functional $F$,
$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}F(X)=\mathbb E_{\text{shifted Wiener measure}}\left[F(X)\exp\left\{\int_0^T b(X_s)dX_s-\tfrac12\int_0^T b(X_s)^2ds\right\}\right].
$$
The right-hand side depends only on $b,x,T$, not on the chosen solution. This proves <uniqueness in law> on every finite horizon, and hence on the whole continuous path space. This is <weak existence and uniqueness in law for an additive-noise SDE with bounded drift>. For merely <measurable> $b$, one should not assume that naive deterministic left-endpoint samples of $b(X)$ converge; the predictable $L^2$ approximation is the appropriate definition.

**The <Girsanov theorem> construction proves a <weak stochastic solution>, but does not by itself establish a <strong stochastic solution>.** It constructs $X=x+W$ first, and then constructs a new driving <Brownian motion> $B=W-\int b(X_s)ds$. Adaptation of $X$ to the original <filtration> of $W$ does not by itself prove adaptation to the smaller <natural filtration> of $B$. Inverting this relation requires an additional result; <uniqueness in law> alone is insufficient in general.

**For the particular coefficients in part (b), however, the literal claim that a strong solution may fail is incorrect.** The <strong existence theorem for additive-noise SDEs with bounded measurable drift> gives <strong existence> and <pathwise uniqueness> for bounded Borel state-dependent $b$ and unit diffusion. Consequently every weak realization here is determined by its driving noise and initial value. This theorem is deeper than the <Girsanov theorem> argument and no proof is needed here. Examples with path-dependent drift or with a nonconstant diffusion coefficient do not contradict it.

The valid distinction is therefore: the argument in part (b) has established weak existence and <uniqueness in law>; strong existence requires an additional theorem, and in this class that theorem is available. A primary reference for the correction is \a[https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=sm&option_lang=eng&paperid=2601&wshow=paper][On strong solutions and explicit formulas for solutions of stochastic integral equations].

A **<continuous martingale>** is an <adapted process> with almost surely continuous paths, $\mathbb E|M_t|<\infty$ for every $t$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s\qquad(s\leq t).}
$$
A **<continuous local martingale>** is continuous and <adapted> and has a <localizing sequence> of <stopping times> $T_n\uparrow\infty$ almost surely such that every $M^{T_n}_t=M_{t\wedge T_n}$ is a true <martingale>. The initial value is normally integrable; the frequently used class $\mathcal M_{c,\mathrm{loc}}$ is normalized to start at zero.

The **<usual conditions for a filtration>** are completeness and right continuity. Completeness means that $\mathcal F_0$ contains every subset of each $\mathbb P$-null set in the ambient $\mathcal F$; right continuity means $\mathcal F_t=\bigcap_{u>t}\mathcal F_u$. These conditions concern the <filtered probability space>, whereas continuity of a <martingale> concerns its sample paths.

For a continuous vector <semimartingale> $X$ and $f\in C^{1,2}$, the **<Itô formula>** is
$$
\boxed{f(t,X_t)=f(0,X_0)+\int_0^t\partial_s f(s,X_s)ds+\sum_i\int_0^t\partial_i f(s,X_s)dX_s^i+\frac12\sum_{i,j}\int_0^t\partial_{ij}f(s,X_s)d[X^i,X^j]_s.}
$$
The **<Itô product rule>**, or stochastic <integration by parts>, is
$$
\boxed{X_tY_t=X_0Y_0+\int_0^tX_s\,dY_s+\int_0^tY_s\,dX_s+[X,Y]_t.}
$$
The additional second-order term is the <quadratic covariation>.

For general <semimartingales> with <càdlàg> paths, use left limits in the integrands. The **<Itô formula for semimartingales with jumps>**, for time-independent $f\in C^2$, is
$$
f(X_t)=f(X_0)+\sum_i\int_0^t\partial_i f(X_{s-})dX_s^i+\frac12\sum_{i,j}\int_0^t\partial_{ij}f(X_{s-})d[X^{i,c},X^{j,c}]_s+\sum_{0<s\leq t}\left(f(X_s)-f(X_{s-})-\sum_i\partial_i f(X_{s-})\Delta X_s^i\right).
$$
Here $X^c$ denotes the continuous <local martingale> part; the continuous bracket is that of these parts. For time-dependent $f\in C^{1,2}$ add $\int\partial_s f(s,X_{s-})ds$ and insert $s$ in the other evaluations of $f$. The <integration by parts> formula becomes
$$
X_tY_t=X_0Y_0+\int_0^tX_{s-}dY_s+\int_0^tY_{s-}dX_s+[X,Y]_t,\qquad [X,Y]_t=[X^c,Y^c]_t+\sum_{0<s\leq t}\Delta X_s\Delta Y_s.
$$
For continuous paths the jump sum vanishes, recovering the first two formulas.

Use two standard results: for every <continuous local martingale> $U$, there is a unique continuous <adapted> increasing <quadratic variation> $[U]$, starting at zero, such that $U^2-U_0^2-[U]$ is a <local martingale>; and a <continuous finite-variation local martingale is constant>.

Define the **<quadratic covariation>** by the <polarization identity>
$$
\boxed{[M,N]=\frac14\bigl([M+N]-[M-N]\bigr).}
$$
This is continuous, <adapted>, starts at zero, and is of <finite variation> on compact time intervals. Since $(M+N)^2-(M-N)^2=4MN$, subtraction of the two quadratic-variation identities shows that $MN-M_0N_0-[M,N]$ is a <continuous local martingale>. In the zero-start convention this is exactly $MN-[M,N]\in\mathcal M_{c,\mathrm{loc}}$.

If $A$ is another continuous <adapted> <finite-variation process> with $A_0=0$ and the same <local martingale> property, then $A-[M,N]$ is both a <continuous local martingale> and of <finite variation>. It is constant, and its initial value is zero, so it vanishes. Uniqueness is up to <indistinguishability of stochastic processes>.

**The normalization $[M,N]_0=0$ is essential.** If arbitrary nonzero initial constants are allowed in the <martingale> class and this normalization is omitted, adding a constant to the bracket preserves the claimed property. The usual definition of <quadratic covariation> always includes the zero initial value.

The **<Lévy characterization of Brownian motion>** states that an <adapted> continuous real process $M$ with $M_0=0$ is standard <Brownian motion> relative to $(\mathcal F_t)$ if and only if it is a <continuous local martingale> and $[M]_t=t$. The multidimensional version replaces the bracket identity by $[M^i,M^j]_t=\delta_{ij}t$.

For the converse direction, a standard <Brownian motion> is a continuous <martingale>, and its independent centered increments give <quadratic variation> $t$. Distinct independent coordinates have zero <quadratic covariation>.

For the substantive direction, fix a vector $\theta\in\mathbb R^d$. The <Itô formula> applied to
$$
E_t(\theta)=\exp\{i\theta\cdot M_t+\tfrac12|\theta|^2t\}
$$
shows that its drift is zero: the second-order term is $-\tfrac12|\theta|^2E_tdt$ and cancels the time derivative. Thus $E(\theta)$ is a complex <local martingale>. On $[0,T]$ its modulus is the deterministic value $e^{|\theta|^2t/2}\leq e^{|\theta|^2T/2}$; localization and the <dominated convergence theorem> make it a true <martingale>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[e^{i\theta\cdot(M_t-M_s)}\mid\mathcal F_s]=e^{-\frac12|\theta|^2(t-s)}.}
$$
This conditional <characteristic function> is the deterministic <characteristic function> of $N(0,(t-s)I)$. For $A\in\mathcal F_s$, multiplying by $\mathbf1_A$ and taking <expectations>, then using uniqueness of <characteristic functions>, proves that $M_t-M_s$ has this <multivariate normal distribution> and is independent of $\mathcal F_s$. Iterated conditioning gives independent increments with <Gaussian distributions>. Continuity, the zero initial value and these increment laws are the definition of standard <Brownian motion>. The case $d=1$ proves the requested statement; the vector form will also be used below.

Interpret $f$ as a <conformal bijection> onto $D'$. If it is merely an injective map into a larger target, the correct exit domain is $f(D)$ instead. Let $\zeta$ be the exit time from $D$, write $f=u+iv$, and identify <planar Brownian motion> with $B^1+iB^2$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $u_x=v_y$, $u_y=-v_x$, and both $u,v$ are <harmonic functions>. The <Itô formula>, localized on compact subsets of $D$, yields
$$
du(B_t)=u_x(B_t)dB_t^1+u_y(B_t)dB_t^2,\qquad dv(B_t)=v_x(B_t)dB_t^1+v_y(B_t)dB_t^2.
$$
Thus both coordinates are <continuous local martingales>, with
$$
[u(B)]_t=[v(B)]_t=A_t:=\int_0^{t}|f'(B_s)|^2ds,\qquad [u(B),v(B)]_t=\int_0^t(u_xv_x+u_yv_y)(B_s)ds=0\quad(t<\zeta).
$$
Since $f'$ never vanishes, $A$ is strictly increasing before $\zeta$. Let $\eta_s=\inf\{t:A_t>s\}$, defined for $s<A_{\zeta-}$. The <optional time-change theorem> gives <continuous local martingale> coordinates for $\beta_s=f(B_{\eta_s})$, with bracket matrix $sI$. The <Lévy characterization of multidimensional Brownian motion> makes $\beta$ a standard <planar Brownian motion> started at $f(z)$, up to its lifetime. Therefore **<conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion> takes the form**
$$
\boxed{f(B_t)=\beta_{A_t},\qquad A_t=\int_0^t|f'(B_s)|^2ds,\quad t<\zeta.}
$$
To identify the lifetime, exhaust $D$ by relatively compact open sets $D_n$ with nested closures. The images $f(D_n)$ exhaust $D'$ because $f$ is a homeomorphism onto $D'$. Each stopped transformed path exits $f(D_n)$ at precisely the clock value of its exit from $D_n$. Increasing these exit times gives the exit lifetime of <Brownian motion> from $D'$, including a possibly infinite lifetime. This identifies $A_{\zeta-}$ with that lifetime and needs no global extension of $f$ to the boundary.

Use the open strip $S=\{x+iy:0<y<\pi\}$; the printed closed strip denotes the region together with its exit boundary. Starting at $z=x+iy$, the <conformal bijection> $e^z$ sends $S$ onto the <complex upper half-plane> and the bottom edge $a\in\mathbb R$ onto $v=e^a>0$. The upper edge maps to the negative half-axis. The transformed initial point is $e^x\cos y+i e^x\sin y$.

By <conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion>, transform the given <Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane> and include the <Jacobian determinant> $dv/da=e^a$. The **<bottom-boundary harmonic measure of a strip>** is
$$
\boxed{\rho_z(a)=\frac{e^{a+x}\sin y}{\pi\bigl((e^a-e^x\cos y)^2+e^{2x}\sin^2y\bigr)}=\frac{\sin y}{2\pi\bigl(\cosh(a-x)-\cos y\bigr)}.}
$$
This is the unconditional exit density on the bottom edge, not the density conditional on using that edge. Its total mass is
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}\rho_z(a)da=1-\frac y\pi.}
$$
Indeed substitution $v=e^{a-x}$ gives the positive-half-axis mass of the <Poisson kernel>, equal to $\frac12+\pi^{-1}\arctan(\cot y)=1-y/\pi$ for $0<y<\pi$. To obtain the conditional bottom-edge density, divide by $1-y/\pi$. The top-edge density is $\sin y/[2\pi(\cosh(a-x)+\cos y)]$ and has mass $y/\pi$, a useful normalization check.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a <harmonic function> $u$ on $\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2$, the <Itô formula> gives
$$
u(B_t)=u(B_0)+\int_0^t\nabla u(B_s)\cdot dB_s+\frac12\int_0^t\Delta u(B_s)ds=u(B_0)+\int_0^t\nabla u(B_s)\cdot dB_s.
$$
Stop on leaving increasing discs and at increasing deterministic times. On each such interval the <gradient> is bounded, so the <stochastic integral> is a <martingale>. This proves that **$u(B)$ is a <continuous local martingale>**.

If $|u|\leq K$, this is a bounded <local martingale> and hence a true <martingale>. Start <Brownian motion> at an arbitrary $z$. Its <Gaussian heat kernel> gives
$$
u(z)=\mathbb E_z u(B_t)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}u(v)p_t(v-z)dv,\qquad p_t(v)=\frac1{2\pi t}e^{-|v|^2/(2t)}.
$$
For a unit vector $e$, $\partial_e p_t(v)=-(e\cdot v)p_t(v)/t$, so $\int|\partial_ep_t(v)|dv=\sqrt{2/\pi}/\sqrt t$. Integrating the directional derivative along the segment from $z$ to $w$ yields
$$
|u(z)-u(w)|\leq K\int|p_t(v-z)-p_t(v-w)|dv\leq K\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{|z-w|}{\sqrt t}.
$$
Letting $t\to\infty$ gives **the <harmonic Liouville theorem>**:
$$
\boxed{u(z)=u(w)\text{ for all }z,w\in\mathbb C;\quad u\text{ is constant}.}
$$
This <Gaussian heat-kernel proof of the harmonic Liouville theorem> uses the bounded <martingale> identity established above.

Let the <infinitesimal generator> be the second-order operator
$$
Lg(x)=\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^d a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}g(x)+\sum_{i=1}^d b_i(x)\partial_i g(x),
$$
where $a(x)$ is symmetric positive semidefinite and $b(x)$ is a vector. In the standard bounded-coefficient setting take $a,b$ bounded and <measurable>. An **<L-diffusion>** is a continuous <adapted process> $X$ for which, for every $g\in C_b^2(\mathbb R^d)$,
$$
\boxed{g(X_t)-g(X_0)-\int_0^t Lg(X_s)ds\text{ is a martingale}.}
$$
Here $C_b^2$ includes boundedness of the function and its derivatives through order two. This is the true-<martingale> version of the <diffusion martingale problem>. The matrix $a$ is the diffusivity, and $b$ is the drift. An <Itô diffusion> with coefficient $\sigma$ has $a=\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf T}$.

A more general local <martingale problem> may permit unbounded coefficients and require only a <local martingale>. That convention is weaker and must not silently replace the true <martingale> property used here and in part (b). The bounded-coefficient convention guarantees all finite-horizon integrability needed below; a sufficient more general replacement is $\mathbb E\int_0^T(\sum_i|b_i(X_s)|+\sum_i a_{ii}(X_s))ds<\infty$ for every $T$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

We prove the extension from spatial test functions to **<time-dependent test functions for a diffusion martingale problem>** directly, without assuming a driving <Brownian motion>. Fix $0\leq s<t$ and a deterministic partition $s=t_0<\cdots<t_n=t$. For each $j$, $g_j(x)=f(t_j,x)$ lies in $C_b^2$. The defining <martingale problem> gives
$$
\mathbb E\left[\mathbf1_A\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(f(t_j,X_{t_{j+1}})-f(t_j,X_{t_j})-\int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}}Lf(t_j,X_u)du\right)\right]=0\qquad(A\in\mathcal F_s).
$$
Call the sum $S_n$. Telescoping separates the spatial and temporal changes:
$$
S_n=f(t,X_t)-f(s,X_s)-\sum_j\int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}}\partial_u f(u,X_{t_{j+1}})du-\sum_j\int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}}Lf(t_j,X_u)du.
$$
The sample path $X$ is uniformly continuous on $[s,t]$ and has compact range. Joint continuity of $\partial_t f$, the spatial first derivatives, and the spatial second derivatives therefore gives, as the mesh tends to zero,
$$
S_n\longrightarrow f(t,X_t)-f(s,X_s)-\int_s^t(\partial_u+L)f(u,X_u)du=M_t^f-M_s^f
$$
almost surely. For bounded $a,b$ and bounded derivatives of $f$, both integrals are uniformly bounded on this finite horizon, as are the two endpoint terms. The <dominated convergence theorem> permits passage to the limit in the expectation against $\mathbf1_A$. Thus $M_t^f$ is integrable and $\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A(M_t^f-M_s^f)]=0$ for every $A\in\mathcal F_s$, proving
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_t^f\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s^f.}
$$
Adaptation and continuity follow from the formula, so **$M^f$ is a continuous <martingale>**. Under the more general integrability condition in part (a), the same proof uses an integrable bound proportional to $1+\int_s^t(\sum_i|b_i(X_u)|+\sum_i a_{ii}(X_u))du$; positive semidefiniteness bounds every off-diagonal entry by the diagonal ones. With only local assumptions this reasoning proves a <local martingale>, and an integrability hypothesis is needed to upgrade that conclusion.

The <Itô formula> for $f(t,x)=e^{x+at}$ gives the **<stochastic differential equation>**
$$
\boxed{dZ_t=Z_t\,dB_t+(a+\tfrac12)Z_t\,dt,\qquad Z_0=1.}
$$
Write $N_t=\int_0^t Z_u\,dB_u$ for its <continuous local martingale> part. Its clock is the <quadratic variation>
$$
A_t=[Z]_t=[N]_t=\int_0^t Z_u^2du.
$$
Because $Z_t>0$, $A$ is strictly increasing. For $s<A_\infty$, its inverse $\tau_s$ satisfies $A_{\tau_s}=s$ and $d\tau_s/ds=Z_{\tau_s}^{-2}$. The <Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem>, or the <Lévy characterization of Brownian motion> after the <optional time-change theorem>, makes $\beta_s=N_{\tau_s}$ a <Brownian motion> for the time-changed <filtration>. With $Y_s=Z_{\tau_s}$,
$$
Y_s=1+\beta_s+(a+\tfrac12)\int_0^sY_v^{-1}dv,
\qquad
\boxed{dY_s=d\beta_s+\frac{(2+2a)-1}{2Y_s}ds.}
$$
This is the <Bessel process> equation with **dimension $\boxed{2+2a}$**, on the positive stochastic interval. This establishes the <exponential Brownian-to-Bessel time change>.

The lifetime convention is important: the inverse is defined only for $s<A_\infty$. If $a<0$, the <strong law for Brownian motion> gives $B_t/t\to0$, so $Z_t\to0$ exponentially and $A_\infty<\infty$. Then $Y_s\to0$ as $s\uparrow A_\infty$, and this is its first hit of zero after continuous extension. If $a>0$ the clock diverges by the same strong law. If $a=0$ it diverges by <infinite occupation time of one-dimensional Brownian motion> on a bounded interval, since $e^{2B_t}$ is bounded below there. Thus the lifetime is infinite when $a\geq0$. Negative dimensions are interpreted by the same positive-state equation killed at zero; no post-zero <Bessel process> behavior is asserted.

= Solution

Work before the first zero of $Z$, with $Z_0=z>0$. For the <Bessel process> equation, the <Itô formula> applied to $x^r$ gives
$$
\boxed{dW_t=rZ_t^{r-1}dB_t+\frac{r(\delta+r-2)}2Z_t^{r-2}dt,\qquad W_0=z^r.}
$$
Its <quadratic variation> is
$$
C_t=[W]_t=r^2\int_0^tZ_u^{2r-2}du.
$$
This clock is strictly increasing on the positive-state interval. For $s$ before its terminal clock value, let $Y_s=W_{\sigma_s}$ and let $\beta_s=(\int_0^\cdot rZ_u^{r-1}dB_u)_{\sigma_s}$. The <Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem> makes $\beta$ a <Brownian motion> there. Since $d\sigma_s=ds/(r^2Z_{\sigma_s}^{2r-2})$, the drift changes to
$$
\frac{r(\delta+r-2)}2\frac{Z_{\sigma_s}^{r-2}}{r^2Z_{\sigma_s}^{2r-2}}=\frac{\delta+r-2}{2rY_s}.
$$
Consequently the **<power time change of a Bessel process>** gives
$$
\boxed{dY_s=d\beta_s+\frac{\delta'-1}{2Y_s}ds,\qquad\delta'=2+\frac{\delta-2}{r}.}
$$
The initial value is $z^r$, and this equation identifies a <Bessel process> of dimension $\delta'$ until its first zero. Localize both equations on compact subintervals of $(0,\infty)$ to justify the inverse and all <stochastic integrals> for arbitrary $r>0$.

For completeness, the inverse-clock lifetime really is the positive <Bessel process> lifetime: the map $z\mapsto z^r$ is a homeomorphism of $(0,\infty)$, and exits from nested compact intervals correspond under the clock. There is no finite explosion at infinity for a positive <Bessel process>, since the <Itô formula> for $Y^2$ gives $d(Y^2)=2Y\,d\beta+\delta'ds$; stopping at zero or level $R$ yields a probability of hitting $R$ before a fixed $T$ bounded by $(Y_0^2+\max(\delta',0)T)/R^2$. If the transformed lifetime were finite while $Y$ stayed in a compact positive interval, the inverse clock and the original solution could both be continued. Thus a finite lifetime is approach to zero. For $\delta<2$, also $\delta'<2$ and the positive-state <Bessel process> hits zero; for $\delta\geq2$ the transformed lifetime is infinite. No continuation through zero is needed.

If the initial value is already zero, the interval before its first zero is empty; the positive-state argument concerns starts $z>0$.
