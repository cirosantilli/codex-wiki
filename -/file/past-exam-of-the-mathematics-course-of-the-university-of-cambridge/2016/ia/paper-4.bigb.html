= Solution
{parent=1E}

The <extended Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
29=17+12,\qquad17=12+5,\qquad12=2\cdot5+2,\qquad5=2\cdot2+1.
$$
Back-substituting in these equations produces the <Bezout identity>
$$
1=5-2(12-2\cdot5)=5\cdot5-2\cdot12
=5\cdot17-7\cdot12=12\cdot17-7\cdot29.
$$
Thus **one integer solution** is $\boxed{x=12,\ y=-7}$. In fact, subtracting this <Bezout identity> from any other solution and using that $17$ and $29$ are <coprime integers> gives all solutions: $x=12+29t$, $y=-7-17t$, where $t\in\mathbb Z$.

For the <modular arithmetic> calculation, $29$ is a <prime number> and $17$ is <coprime> to it, so <Fermat little theorem> gives $17^{28}\equiv1\pmod{29}$. Since $138=5\cdot28-2$, and the preceding <Bezout identity> identifies $12$ as the <modular multiplicative inverse> of $17$, we obtain
$$
17^{138}\equiv17^{-2}\equiv12^2=144\equiv28\pmod{29}.
$$
Consequently **the least positive representative** is $\boxed{28}$.

= Solution
{parent=2E}

For a nonempty <set> $S\subseteq\mathbb R$, an <upper bound in a partially ordered set> is a real number $u$ such that $s\leq u$ for every $s\in S$. Its <supremum>, or least upper bound, is an upper bound $L$ with $L\leq u$ for every upper bound $u$. The <least-upper-bound property> asserts that every nonempty set of <real numbers> bounded above has a real <supremum>.

Let $(x_n)$ be a bounded, increasing <sequence>; here increasing allows $x_{n+1}=x_n$. Apply the <least-upper-bound property> to its set of terms and put $L=\sup\{x_n:n\geq1\}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, the number $L-\varepsilon$ cannot be an upper bound, so some $x_N$ satisfies $x_N>L-\varepsilon$. The <monotone sequence> property then gives
$$
L-\varepsilon<x_N\leq x_n\leq L\qquad(n\geq N).
$$
Hence $|x_n-L|<\varepsilon$ for all $n\geq N$. This proves the <monotone bounded sequence> result, with **limit** $\boxed{x_n\longrightarrow L}$.

For the two <series>, define their partial sums $A_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n$ and $B_N=\sum_{n=1}^N b_n$. Positivity makes both partial-sum sequences increasing, and termwise comparison gives $0<B_N\leq A_N$. Since $(A_N)$ is a <convergent sequence>, it is bounded above, and therefore so is $(B_N)$. Applying the <monotone bounded sequence> result already proved shows that $(B_N)$ converges. By the definition of a <convergent series>, **$\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n$ converges**. This is the <comparison test for series> for positive terms.

= Solution
{parent=4B}

In a <circular orbit>, $r=a$ is constant, so the radial <acceleration> vanishes. The radial equation requires
$$
\boxed{h^2=ka^{n+3}}.
$$
For every $a>0$, this equation permits a real nonzero <specific angular momentum>, $h=\pm\sqrt{ka^{n+3}}$, and hence a <circular orbit>. This chooses $h$ for the desired radius; it does not claim that one prescribed value of $h$ permits every radius.

To test <circular-orbit stability for a power-law central force>, keep that chosen <specific angular momentum> fixed and write $r=a+\rho$, with $|\rho|\ll a$. The radial equation becomes
$$
\ddot\rho=\frac{h^2}{(a+\rho)^3}-k(a+\rho)^n.
$$
Expanding to first order and using $h^2=ka^{n+3}$ gives
$$
\ddot\rho=-\left(\frac{3h^2}{a^4}+kn a^{n-1}\right)\rho+O(\rho^2)
=-(n+3)ka^{n-1}\rho+O(\rho^2).
$$
For $n>-3$, the linearized equation is the <harmonic oscillator equation>, with
$$
\boxed{\Omega_r^2=(n+3)ka^{n-1}>0}.
$$
The radial disturbance undergoes bounded <small oscillations>, rather than growing. Equivalently, the <effective potential> has a strict local minimum: its second derivative at $a$ is $(n+3)ka^{n-1}>0$. By the <effective potential stability criterion>, sufficiently small radial disturbances remain small, proving **stability for $n>-3$**. For comparison, $n<-3$ gives exponential growth. At $n=-3$, the balancing value $h^2=k$ makes the radial acceleration identically zero, so a small radial velocity produces drift rather than a restoring oscillation.

Suppose $f:S\to\mathcal P(S)$ were a <surjective function>. Form the <subset>
$$
D=\{s\in S:s\notin f(s)\}.
$$
Since $D$ belongs to the <power set>, surjectivity would give $D=f(d)$ for some $d\in S$. But then $d\in D$ holds if and only if $d\notin f(d)=D$, a contradiction. Thus no such function is surjective, and in particular **there is no <bijection> from $S$ to its <power set>**. This is <Cantor theorem>; the argument also applies when $S$ is empty.

For infinite <binary sequences>, use the <bijection>
$$
(x_n)_{n\geq1}\longmapsto\{n\in\mathbb N:x_n=1\}
$$
with $\mathcal P(\mathbb N)$, taking $\mathbb N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$. Conversely, a subset determines its sequence of membership indicators. The <power set> is infinite, since it contains all singletons, and <Cantor theorem> rules out a bijection with $\mathbb N$. Hence it is an <uncountable set>, as is the set of infinite <binary sequences>. Equivalently, a proposed enumeration $(x_n^{(j)})_{n\geq1}$ is defeated by the sequence $y_j=1-x_j^{(j)}$, which differs from its $j$th listed sequence at position $j$. This is the <Cantor diagonal argument>.

Write $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}$ and enumerate $B=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots\}$. Each <injective function> $f:A\to B$ is determined by the finite tuple $(f(a_1),\ldots,f(a_m))$. Thus these functions form a subset of the finite <Cartesian product> $B^m$. A <finite Cartesian power of a countable set> is countable: for example, group the index tuples by the maximum of their coordinates, obtaining a <countable union of countable sets> consisting of finite groups.

There are also infinitely many such <injective functions>, since
$$
f_j(a_i)=b_{j+i}\qquad(j\geq0,\ 1\leq i\leq m)
$$
defines pairwise distinct examples. Therefore **$S_1$ is a <countably infinite set>**.

Again let $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}$ with $m>1$, and enumerate $B=\{b_1,b_2,\ldots\}$. For each infinite <binary sequence> $x=(x_j)_{j\geq1}$, define a function $g_x$ by

The first $m$ values make every $g_x$ a <surjective function>, while the remaining values recover $x$ uniquely. Hence $x\mapsto g_x$ is an <injective function> from the uncountable set of <binary sequences> into $S_2$. This proves **$S_2$ is an <uncountable set>**, an instance of <uncountability of surjections onto a nontrivial finite set>.

= Solution

Multiplying the desired representation by $b_1b_2$ turns it into the integer equation
$$
a=a_1b_2+a_2b_1+nb_1b_2.
$$
Since $b_1$ and $b_2$ are <coprime integers>, the necessary <modular multiplicative inverses> exist. Choose the unique representatives
$$
a_1\equiv a b_2^{-1}\pmod{b_1},\quad0\leq a_1<b_1,\qquad
a_2\equiv a b_1^{-1}\pmod{b_2},\quad0\leq a_2<b_2.
$$
Then $a-a_1b_2-a_2b_1$ is divisible by both $b_1$ and $b_2$, hence by their product, because they are <coprime>. Defining
$$
n=\frac{a-a_1b_2-a_2b_1}{b_1b_2}
$$
therefore gives an integer and proves existence. Any other representation, reduced modulo $b_1$ and $b_2$, must have the same $a_1$ and $a_2$, since their allowed ranges contain exactly one representative of each residue. The integer equation then forces the same $n$. This proves uniqueness.

For the <prime-power fraction decomposition>, put $d_i=p_i^{n_i}$ and $B_i=b/d_i$. The distinct prime-power factors $d_i$ are pairwise <coprime>, and $B_i$ has a <modular multiplicative inverse> modulo $d_i$. Define
$$
q_i\equiv aB_i^{-1}\pmod{d_i},\qquad0\leq q_i<d_i.
$$
Every term $q_jB_j$ with $j\ne i$ is divisible by $d_i$, while $q_iB_i\equiv a\pmod{d_i}$. Thus $a-\sum_iq_iB_i$ is divisible by all the pairwise coprime $d_i$ and therefore by $b$. With $n=(a-\sum_iq_iB_i)/b$, division by $b$ yields the required representation. Reducing any such representation modulo each $d_i$ proves uniqueness exactly as above. This is the residue-solving mechanism of the <Chinese remainder theorem>.

For $315=9\cdot5\cdot7$, the complementary factors are $35,63,45$. The required <modular inverses> give
$$
q_9=8,\qquad q_5=2,\qquad q_7=5,
$$
because $35\cdot8\equiv1\pmod9$, $63\cdot2\equiv1\pmod5$, and $45\cdot5\equiv1\pmod7$. Their numerator sum is $280+126+225=631=1+2\cdot315$. Thus **the unique decomposition** is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{315}=\frac89+\frac25+\frac57-2}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=7E}

For finite sets $E_1,\ldots,E_s$, the <inclusion-exclusion principle> states
$$
\left|\bigcup_{j=1}^s E_j\right|
=\sum_{\varnothing\ne I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}}
(-1)^{|I|+1}\left|\bigcap_{j\in I}E_j\right|.
$$
It counts each element once: an element in exactly $q$ of the sets contributes $\sum_{j=1}^q(-1)^{j+1}\binom qj=1$, by the <binomial theorem>.

For the <cyclic digit run> problem, let $E_j$ mean that the three digits starting at position $j$ form a run, for $j=1,2,3,4$. Every run of length at least three contains one of these triples, so we need $|E_1\cup E_2\cup E_3\cup E_4|$. Introduce the five differences
$$
d_i\equiv a_{i+1}-a_i\pmod{10},\qquad i=1,\ldots,5.
$$
A starting digit and these five residues determine a unique string, including runs that cross $9$ and $0$. In these coordinates $E_j$ requires $(d_j,d_{j+1})=(1,1)$ or $(-1,-1)$.

Each single event fixes two differences, with two choices of sign; the initial digit and the other three differences are free. Thus $|E_j|=10\cdot2\cdot10^3=20000$. For intersections of two events, adjacent starting positions force three consecutive differences to have the same sign, giving $10\cdot2\cdot10^2=2000$. There are three such pairs. The other three pairs have disjoint constrained difference positions, with independent signs and one free difference, giving $10\cdot4\cdot10=400$ each. Consequently the sum of pair-intersection sizes is $3\cdot2000+3\cdot400=7200$.

For triple intersections, $E_1\cap E_2\cap E_3$ and $E_2\cap E_3\cap E_4$ each force four consecutive differences to have the same sign, leaving one free difference, and each has size $10\cdot2\cdot10=200$. The other two triples, $E_1\cap E_2\cap E_4$ and $E_1\cap E_3\cap E_4$, divide the five differences into two blocks with independent signs; each has size $10\cdot4=40$. The triple-intersection sum is therefore $480$. Finally, all four events force all five differences to have the same sign, giving $20$ strings.

The <inclusion-exclusion principle> now gives **the number of strings**
$$
\boxed{4\cdot20000-7200+480-20=73260}.
$$
Using differences makes the overlap conditions explicit and prevents double-counting long <cyclic digit runs>.

= Solution

First establish the <binomial theorem> needed throughout this question. For integers $0\leq m\leq n$, define the <binomial coefficient> $\binom nm$ to be the number of $m$-element <subsets> of an $n$-element set. Counting ordered selections and then forgetting their order gives
$$
\binom nm=\frac{n!}{m!(n-m)!},
$$
with the <factorial> convention $0!=1$. In the product of $n$ factors $(1+z)$, choose $z$ from exactly $m$ factors and $1$ from the others. Each choice contributes $z^m$, and there are $\binom nm$ choices. The distributive law therefore proves
$$
(1+z)^n=\sum_{m=0}^n\binom nm z^m
$$
for every <complex number> $z$, including $n=0$ by the empty-product convention.

To evaluate the even-indexed sum, use the <even part of a polynomial>: averaging the <binomial theorem> at $z$ and $-z$ cancels the odd powers. For $z=i\sqrt3$,
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{3n}(-3)^k\binom{6n}{2k}
=\frac{(1+i\sqrt3)^{6n}+(1-i\sqrt3)^{6n}}2.
$$
The two <complex numbers> satisfy $1\pm i\sqrt3=2e^{\pm i\pi/3}$. Their $6n$th powers are both $2^{6n}$, since $e^{\pm2\pi in}=1$. Thus **the sum is**
$$
\boxed{2^{6n}}.
$$

{parent=8e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate the finite <binomial theorem> and set $z=1$. For $n\geq1$ this gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{m=0}^n m\binom nm=n2^{n-1}}.
$$
For $n=0$ the sum is $0$ directly.

For the <alternating binomial moments>, it is useful to differentiate in a form that introduces <falling factorials>. Write $(m)_j=m(m-1)\cdots(m-j+1)$, with $(m)_0=1$. Differentiating the <binomial theorem> $j$ times and multiplying by $z^j$ yields, for $0\leq j\leq n$,
$$
\sum_{m=0}^n(m)_j\binom nm z^m=(n)_jz^j(1+z)^{n-j}.
$$
At $z=-1$, the right side vanishes when $j<n$, and equals $(-1)^nn!$ when $j=n$.

The <polynomials> $(X)_0,\ldots,(X)_r$ have degrees $0,\ldots,r$ and leading coefficient $1$, so successive cancellation of leading terms expresses $X^r$ as a linear combination of them. If $r<n$, every falling-factorial term has index $j<n$ and hence its alternating sum vanishes. If $r=n$, the coefficient of $(X)_n$ is $1$, and all lower-index terms still vanish. Therefore **the alternating moments are**
$$
\boxed{S_r(n)=0\quad(0\leq r<n),\qquad S_n(n)=(-1)^nn!}.
$$
For $n=0$, the latter identity uses the constant polynomial $X^0=1$, so its value at $X=0$ is $1$; thus $S_0(0)=1$ as well. This makes the edge-case convention explicit rather than treating $0^0$ as an unrelated numerical expression.

= Solution

Work in one fixed <inertial frame>, and take positive <velocity> in the rocket's direction. During $d\tau$, the rocket ejects the positive amount of <mass> $-m'(\tau)d\tau$. Its exhaust has inertial velocity $v(\tau)-u$, by the <Galilean transformation> of velocities. Once expelled, that material retains its velocity because it experiences no external <force>. The system's <momentum> is therefore the rocket momentum plus the sum of all these exhaust momenta:
$$
P(t)=m(t)v(t)-\int_0^t[v(\tau)-u]m'(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$
The minus sign comes from the positive expelled mass being $-m'(\tau)d\tau$.

Differentiate, applying the product rule to the rocket <momentum> and the <fundamental theorem of calculus> to the integral:
$$
P'=m'v+mv'-(v-u)m'=mv'+um'.
$$
The <momentum conservation> law thus gives **the nonrelativistic <rocket equation>**
$$
\boxed{mv'+um'=0}.
$$

The same bookkeeping for <kinetic energy> gives
$$
K(t)=\frac12m(t)v(t)^2-\frac12\int_0^t[v(\tau)-u]^2m'(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$
Its derivative is
$$
K'=mvv'+\frac12\bigl[v^2-(v-u)^2\bigr]m'
=mvv'+uvm'-\frac12u^2m'
=-\frac12u^2m',
$$
where the <rocket equation> cancels the first two terms. Thus **kinetic energy increases during mass ejection**, with $\boxed{K'=-u^2m'/2>0}$ when $u>0$ and $m'<0$. This does not violate <conservation of energy>: the increase comes from stored <internal energy>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Now $m$ denotes the instantaneous <rest mass> of the rocket, including its fuel and stored <internal energy>, and $v$ is measured in a fixed <inertial frame>. Assume the exhaust is expelled backwards with speed $0<u<c$ in the rocket's instantaneous rest frame. By the <relativistic velocity-addition formula>, material emitted at time $\tau$ has inertial velocity
$$
w(\tau)=\frac{v(\tau)-u}{1-u v(\tau)/c^2}.
$$
It coasts at this velocity after emission.

The crucial change is that the expelled material's rest mass is not simply $-dm$: some of the rocket's <rest energy> becomes exhaust motion. Instead, use <conservation of relativistic energy>. Put $q(t)=m(t)\gamma(t)$, so the rocket's energy is $c^2q(t)$. The exhaust emitted during $d\tau$ carries energy $-c^2q'(\tau)d\tau$. For material moving at velocity $w$, the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> gives momentum $wE/c^2$, since $E=\gamma_{\rm ex}\mu c^2$ and $p=\gamma_{\rm ex}\mu w$. Hence the modified total <momentum> is
$$
P(t)=q(t)v(t)-\int_0^t w(\tau)q'(\tau)\,d\tau.
$$
Meanwhile the total relativistic energy is $c^2[q(t)-\int_0^tq'(\tau)d\tau]=c^2q(0)$. These expressions consistently account for both <energy> and <momentum> of the expelled material.

Differentiate the momentum formula. The <momentum conservation> law gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d(m\gamma v)}{dt}
=\frac{v-u}{1-uv/c^2}\frac{d(m\gamma)}{dt}}.
$$
To simplify it, use
$$
qv'+(v-w)q'=0,\qquad
v-w=\frac{u}{\gamma^2(1-uv/c^2)},\qquad
q'=\gamma m'+m\gamma^3\frac{vv'}{c^2}.
$$
Multiplying by $\gamma^2(1-uv/c^2)$, the equation becomes
$$
m\gamma^3(1-uv/c^2)v'
+u\gamma m'+um\gamma^3vv'/c^2=0.
$$
The two terms containing $uv/c^2$ cancel. Dividing by $\gamma>0$ proves **the <relativistic rocket equation>**
$$
\boxed{m\gamma^2v'+um'=0}.
$$

= c
{parent=9b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For fixed finite velocities, the <nonrelativistic limit> $c\to\infty$ gives $\gamma^2=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1}\to1$. Hence the <relativistic rocket equation> reduces to
$$
\boxed{mv'+um'=0},
$$
which is the nonrelativistic <rocket equation>.

The conserved quantities called energy in the two calculations include different contributions. The nonrelativistic calculation considered only <kinetic energy>, which increases as fuel releases <internal energy>; total energy including that internal reservoir is still conserved. Relativistic energy includes <rest energy>, and therefore includes the fuel's stored internal energy through the rocket's <rest mass>. That rest mass decreases as energy is transferred into the exhaust. Thus <conservation of relativistic energy> and the nonconservation of kinetic energy express the same physical conversion.

= Solution

For unit <mass>, the <inverse-square force> has <potential energy> $-k/r$, with zero potential at infinity. Conservation of <angular momentum> gives $r^2\dot\theta=h$. Differentiate the polar equation of the <Kepler orbit>:
$$
\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\frac{\ell e\sin\theta}{(1+e\cos\theta)^2},
\qquad
\dot r=\frac{he\sin\theta}{\ell}.
$$
Consequently the total <mechanical energy> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
E&=\frac12\left(\dot r^2+\frac{h^2}{r^2}\right)-\frac{k}{r}\\
&=\frac{h^2}{2\ell^2}
\left[e^2\sin^2\theta+(1+e\cos\theta)^2-2(1+e\cos\theta)\right]\\
&=\boxed{\frac{h^2(e^2-1)}{2\ell^2}},
\end{aligned}
$$
where $k=h^2/\ell$. This also explains the sign distinction between an <elliptic orbit>, a <parabolic Kepler orbit>, and a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>.

At the original <periapsis>, the <parabolic Kepler orbit> has $e=1$, $r=d=\ell/2$, and purely tangential speed $V$. Thus
$$
\ell=2d,\qquad h=dV,\qquad V^2=\frac{2k}{d}.
$$
The outward radial <impulse> has zero moment about the Sun, so it preserves <angular momentum> and hence $\ell$. Its additional velocity is perpendicular to the original velocity, so the new <mechanical energy> is $E'=\alpha^2V^2/2$. Applying the energy formula gives
$$
\frac{V^2}{8}(e'^2-1)=\frac{\alpha^2V^2}{2},
\qquad
\boxed{e'=\sqrt{1+4\alpha^2}}.
$$
For $\alpha>0$, the new orbit is a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>; $\alpha=0$ leaves the parabola unchanged.

To fix the orientation of the sketch, put the Sun at the origin, the impulse point at $(d,0)$, and take the original motion there upwards. The new velocity is $(\alpha V,V)$. The <eccentricity vector> is
$$
\mathbf e'=\frac{\mathbf v'\times\mathbf h}{k}-\hat{\mathbf r}
=(1,-2\alpha).
$$
It points towards the new <periapsis>, which is rotated clockwise through $\beta=\arctan(2\alpha)$ from the original periapsis direction. Thus, measured using the original polar angle $\theta$, the new orbit is
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{2d}{1+\cos\theta-2\alpha\sin\theta}
=\frac{2d}{1+e'\cos(\theta+\beta)}}.
$$
In particular, the impulse point is already on the outgoing part of the new orbit and is not its new periapsis. The old parabola satisfies $x=d-y^2/(4d)$. Both orbits pass through $(d,0)$ and have the same focus, but the hyperbola's new periapsis axis is tilted.

= Solution
{parent=a}

In the Earth <inertial frame>, the return journey takes $2d/v$. Bob is at rest in this frame, while Alice's <proper time> is reduced by the <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. Neglecting the short turnaround interval, **their increases in age** are
$$
\boxed{\Delta\tau_B=\frac{2d}{v},\qquad
\Delta\tau_A=\frac{2d}{v}\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}}.
$$

The <twin paradox> is the apparent contradiction from applying reciprocal <time dilation> to claim that each twin must be younger than the other. Alice changes inertial frames at the turnaround, so a single comoving <inertial frame> cannot describe her whole journey; Bob uses one inertial frame throughout. The twins follow different <world lines>, and integrating <proper time> along those world lines gives the displayed, consistent ages. The change in Alice's notion of simultaneity at turnaround removes the apparent contradiction. Acceleration distinguishes the paths; its magnitude is not an additional local rule for aging.

= b
{parent=11b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The meetings occur where Bob's constant position $x=\alpha ct_0$ intersects Alice's positive-$x$ branch of the <hyperbolic motion>:
$$
t_\pm=\pm t_0\sqrt{\alpha^2-1}.
$$
Bob is at rest in this <inertial frame>, so **his elapsed <proper time>** is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\tau_B=2t_0\sqrt{\alpha^2-1}}.
$$

Parametrize Alice's <world line> by
$$
x=ct_0\cosh\eta,\qquad t=t_0\sinh\eta.
$$
Since $t$ increases with $\eta$, the <proper time> element is
$$
d\tau=\sqrt{dt^2-\frac{dx^2}{c^2}}
=t_0\sqrt{\cosh^2\eta-\sinh^2\eta}\,d\eta=t_0\,d\eta.
$$
At the meetings, $\cosh\eta=\alpha$, giving endpoints $\eta=\pm\operatorname{arcosh}\alpha$. Thus **Alice's elapsed proper time** is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\tau_A=2t_0\operatorname{arcosh}\alpha}.
$$
Here $\operatorname{arcosh}$ is the nonnegative real <inverse hyperbolic cosine>. As a check, if $\alpha=\cosh\eta_*$ with $\eta_*>0$, then Bob ages by $2t_0\sinh\eta_*$ and Alice by $2t_0\eta_*$; $\sinh\eta_*>\eta_*$ confirms that Bob again ages more.

= Solution
{parent=12B}

Use an Earth-centred <rotating reference frame>, with Earth's constant <angular velocity> vector $\boldsymbol\omega$. The vectors $\mathbf r$, $\mathbf v$, and $\mathbf a$ are the particle's <position>, <velocity>, and <acceleration> measured in that rotating frame. The vector $\mathbf g$ is the gravitational acceleration due to the actual gravitational force, expressed in the same axes; it excludes the separate <centrifugal acceleration>. The remaining term $-2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v$ is the <Coriolis acceleration>. A constant rotation axis and an unaccelerated Earth-centred origin account for the absence of an Euler term or a translational-origin term.

For the size estimates, Earth's angular speed is
$$
\omega=\frac{2\pi}{9\times10^4}\approx7.0\times10^{-5}\ {\rm s}^{-1}.
$$
With drop height $H=20\ {\rm m}$, unperturbed <free fall> gives **the fall time**
$$
\boxed{T=\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}=2\ {\rm s}},
$$
and final speed $gT=20\ {\rm m\,s}^{-1}$. Thus the gravitational term has size $10\ {\rm m\,s}^{-2}$, the <Coriolis acceleration> is at most about $2\omega gT\approx2.8\times10^{-3}\ {\rm m\,s}^{-2}$, and the <centrifugal acceleration> is at most $\omega^2R_0\approx2.9\times10^{-2}\ {\rm m\,s}^{-2}$. These are characteristic sizes $10^1$, $10^{-3}$, and $10^{-2}$ in acceleration units; geometric factors can reduce them, and the Coriolis term is zero at release.

For a suitable approximation to the <rotational deflection of a falling body>, take $\mathbf g$ constant over the short fall, start at rest relative to Earth, and use the zeroth-order velocity $\mathbf v^{(0)}(t)=\mathbf g\,t$ inside the small Coriolis term. In the centrifugal term replace $\mathbf r$ by $\mathbf R_0$, because $H/R_0\ll1$. Integrating the resulting acceleration twice, with initial position $\mathbf r_{\rm i}$, gives
$$
\mathbf r(t)\approx\mathbf r_{\rm i}+\frac12\mathbf g\,t^2
-\frac13\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf g\,t^3
-\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf R_0)t^2.
$$
Since $\mathbf r_{\rm i}+\mathbf gT^2/2=\mathbf R_0$, evaluating at the unperturbed fall time yields the printed first-iterate estimate
$$
\boxed{\mathbf R\approx\mathbf R_0
-\frac13\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf g\,T^3
-\frac12\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf R_0)T^2}.
$$

The approximation keeps the leading Coriolis contribution and the leading centrifugal contribution enhanced by Earth's large radius. Feeding the Coriolis velocity correction back into the equation produces terms of size $\omega^2gT^4$, smaller than the retained centrifugal displacement by a factor of order $H/R_0$. Replacing $\mathbf r$ by $\mathbf R_0$ in that term has the same relative error. Here $\omega T\approx1.4\times10^{-4}$ and $H/R_0\approx3.3\times10^{-6}$, so both approximations are well controlled. Strictly, the displayed vector is the position at time $T$: its small upward component is removed by the slightly longer actual fall time. That arrival-time correction changes the retained horizontal displacements only at higher order. Thus the horizontal components of the printed estimate give the landing point on the locally flat surface; its vertical component should not be interpreted as a landing above the ground.

Let $\hat{\mathbf e}_E,\hat{\mathbf e}_N,\hat{\mathbf e}_U$ point east, north, and vertically upwards. They form a right-handed basis, with $\hat{\mathbf e}_N\times\hat{\mathbf e}_U=\hat{\mathbf e}_E$. At latitude $\lambda$, in the spherical-Earth approximation,
$$
\boldsymbol\omega=\omega(\cos\lambda\,\hat{\mathbf e}_N+\sin\lambda\,\hat{\mathbf e}_U),
\quad \mathbf g=-g\hat{\mathbf e}_U,\quad
\mathbf R_0=R_0\hat{\mathbf e}_U.
$$
The required <cross products> are
$$
\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf g=-\omega g\cos\lambda\,\hat{\mathbf e}_E,
\qquad
\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf R_0)
=\omega^2R_0(\sin\lambda\cos\lambda\,\hat{\mathbf e}_N-\cos^2\lambda\,\hat{\mathbf e}_U).
$$
At $\lambda=45^\circ$, **the northerly and easterly displacements** are therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta N=-\frac{\omega^2R_0T^2}{4},\qquad
\Delta E=\frac{\omega gT^3}{3\sqrt2}}.
$$
The negative northerly component means a southward displacement. With the supplied values, these are approximately $2.9\ {\rm cm}$ south and $1.3\ {\rm mm}$ east. The eastward effect comes from <Coriolis acceleration>, while the leading southward effect here comes from <centrifugal acceleration>.
