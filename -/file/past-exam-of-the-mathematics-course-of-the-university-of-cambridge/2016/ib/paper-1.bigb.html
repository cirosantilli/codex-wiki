At the origin, the <Taylor series> $\sin z=z+O(z^3)$ and $1-\cos z=z^2/2+O(z^4)$ give
$$
f_1(z)=z^{-3}(1+O(z)),\qquad f_2(z)=2z^{-4}(1+O(z^2)).
$$
Thus **$f_1$ has a pole of order three and $f_2$ a pole of order four at zero**. For the third function, the <Laurent series>
$$
f_3(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k z^{1-2k}}{(2k+1)!}
$$
has infinitely many nonzero negative-power terms, so **zero is an essential singularity of $f_3$**.

To classify the point at infinity on the <Riemann sphere>, use the coordinate $w=1/z$. Then
$$
f_3(1/w)=w^{-2}\sin w=w^{-1}-\frac w6+O(w^3),
$$
so **$f_3$ has a simple pole at infinity**.

For the other two functions, an important qualification is necessary. The <poles> of $f_1$ at nonzero integer multiples of $\pi$, and those of $f_2$ at nonzero integer multiples of $2\pi$, approach infinity. Equivalently, their images in the $w$ coordinate accumulate at zero. Hence **infinity is a non-isolated singularity of $f_1$ and $f_2$**. Neither function is <holomorphic> on any punctured neighbourhood of infinity, so the <classification of isolated singularities> into removable singularities, poles and essential singularities does not apply there.

The <Poincare disc model> is the open <unit disc> $D=\{z=x+iy:|z|<1\}$ equipped with the <Riemannian metric>
$$
\boxed{ds^2=\frac{4(dx^2+dy^2)}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}=\frac{4|dz|^2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}.}
$$
This normalization gives <Gaussian curvature> $-1$. In particular, a Euclidean curve has <hyperbolic length> $\int 2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$; the boundary circle is infinitely far away in <hyperbolic distance>.

**A suitable <Poincare disc automorphism>** is the <Möbius transformation>
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}.}

It sends $a$ to zero and has inverse $(w+a)/(1+\overline a w)$. To verify the <isometry> directly, note that

These identities show both that $f$ maps the <unit disc> onto itself and that $2|df|/(1-|f|^2)=2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$, preserving the <Riemannian metric>.

A sequence of real <orthogonal polynomials> consists of real polynomials $p_n$ with <degree of a polynomial> $n$ and
$$
\langle p_m,p_n\rangle=0\quad(m\ne n),\qquad \langle p_n,p_n\rangle>0.
$$
Orthogonality is relative to the specified <inner product>, rather than to the ordinary coefficients. A <monic polynomial> has leading coefficient one, so a <monic orthogonal polynomial> has the form $p_n(x)=x^n+\text{terms of lower degree}$. In particular, the monic degree-zero polynomial is $p_0=1$.

= Solution

Since $xp_n-p_{n+1}$ has <degree of a polynomial> at most $n$, expand it in the <basis> $p_0,\ldots,p_n$. Multiplication by $x$ is <self-adjoint> for this weighted <inner product>: $\langle xp,q\rangle=\langle p,xq\rangle$. For $k\leq n-2$, the polynomial $xp_k$ has degree at most $n-1$, so
$$
\langle xp_n-p_{n+1},p_k\rangle=\langle p_n,xp_k\rangle=0.
$$
Only the $p_n$ and $p_{n-1}$ coefficients remain. Taking their <inner products> gives **the three-term recurrence**
$$
\boxed{p_{n+1}=(x-\alpha_n)p_n-\beta_n p_{n-1},\qquad \alpha_n=\frac{\langle xp_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle},\quad \beta_n=\frac{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_{n-1},p_{n-1}\rangle}>0\ (n\geq1).}
$$
For the second formula, $xp_{n-1}=p_n+$ a polynomial of lower degree, hence $\langle xp_n,p_{n-1}\rangle=\langle p_n,xp_{n-1}\rangle=\langle p_n,p_n\rangle$. At $n=0$, take $p_{-1}=0$, $\beta_0=0$ and the same formula for $\alpha_0$. Positivity of the weight ensures that all the squared <norms> in the denominators are nonzero.

= Solution
{parent=7H}

Let $S=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$. The <likelihood ratio> of rate two to rate one is $2^n e^{-S}$, which decreases strictly with $S$. By the <Neyman-Pearson lemma>, the **most powerful test rejects for small values of the sum**:
$$
\boxed{\text{reject if }S<c_\alpha,\qquad \mathbb P_{\lambda=1}(S<c_\alpha)=\alpha.}
$$
Under the null, $S$ has the <gamma distribution> with shape $n$ and rate one. Thus $c_\alpha$ is its $\alpha$-quantile, equivalently half the $\alpha$-quantile of the <chi-squared distribution> with $2n$ degrees of freedom. The <distribution> is continuous, so boundary randomization is unnecessary for $0<\alpha<1$.

For every fixed alternative $\lambda>1$, the <likelihood ratio> against rate one is $\lambda^n e^{-(\lambda-1)S}$, again strictly decreasing. Therefore the same region is most powerful against every such alternative. Moreover, if $G$ has the shape-$n$, rate-one <gamma distribution>, then $S$ at rate $\lambda$ has the same <distribution> as $G/\lambda$. It follows that $\mathbb P_\lambda(S<c_\alpha)=\mathbb P(G<\lambda c_\alpha)$ increases with $\lambda$, so the maximum rejection probability over $\lambda\leq1$ is $\alpha$ at one. Any competing size-$\alpha$ test for the composite null is also of level at most $\alpha$ at rate one, and is bounded in power by the <Neyman-Pearson lemma> for each alternative. **The test is uniformly most powerful for the composite hypotheses as well.** This is the <uniformly most powerful test for an exponential rate> based on the sample sum.

Let $p$ be the row player's <mixed strategy> and $q$ the column player's <mixed strategy>. The fourth row is strictly dominated by the first. A useful candidate mixes the first two rows so that their first and third column payoffs agree. If the first row has probability $t$, these payoffs are $7t-2$ and $2-7t$, giving $t=2/7$ and common value zero.

To prove that these are <optimal mixed strategies>, use the two explicit <mixed strategies>
$$
\boxed{p=(2/7,5/7,0,0)^T,\qquad q=(1/2,0,1/2)^T,\qquad v=0.}
$$
Their payoff vectors satisfy
$$
p^TA=(0,11/7,0),\qquad Aq=(0,0,-1/2,-1)^T.
$$
Thus the row player guarantees a payoff at least zero against every column <mixed strategy>, and the column player guarantees a payoff at most zero against every row <mixed strategy>. This matching pair proves that **the value of the zero-sum game is zero**.

These <optimal mixed strategies> are also unique. Since $Aq$ is strictly negative in rows three and four, any optimal row strategy gives them probability zero. Against columns one and three it must then satisfy both $7t-2\geq0$ and $2-7t\geq0$, so $t=2/7$. Similarly, $p^TA$ is strictly positive in column two, forcing an optimal column strategy to avoid that column; its constraints against rows one and two then force equal weights on columns one and three.

For <matrices> $A,B$ and scalars $r,s\in\mathbb F$, the definition of the <trace> gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(rA+sB)=\sum_i(ra_{ii}+sb_{ii})=r\operatorname{Tr}(A)+s\operatorname{Tr}(B).

Hence **the trace is a linear functional** on the <vector space> of $n\times n$ <matrices>.

The definition of <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}b_{ji}=\sum_{j,i}b_{ji}a_{ij}=\operatorname{Tr}(BA).

The middle equality uses commutativity of scalar multiplication in $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, together with interchange of finite sums. Thus **the trace is unchanged by cyclically moving the two factors**.

To prove that <trace is the unique normalized cyclic linear functional>, let $E_{ij}$ be the <matrix units>. For $i\ne j$, $E_{ii}E_{ij}=E_{ij}$ whereas $E_{ij}E_{ii}=0$, so the assumed identity for the <linear functional> gives $f(E_{ij})=0$. Also, $E_{ij}E_{ji}=E_{ii}$ and $E_{ji}E_{ij}=E_{jj}$, giving $f(E_{ii})=f(E_{jj})$. Write this common value as $c$. Since the <matrix units> form a <basis>,
$$
\boxed{f(A)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}f(E_{ij})=c\sum_i a_{ii}=c\operatorname{Tr}(A).}
$$
For $n=1$ the same conclusion follows immediately from the one-dimensional <basis> $E_{11}$. Finally, $f(I)=nc$; if this equals $n$, then $c=1$ over either specified field. **The normalization makes $f$ exactly the trace.**

Every <commutator> $[A,B]=AB-BA$ has zero <trace>, by the cyclic identity. Hence its linear span $W$ is contained in $\ker\operatorname{Tr}$. For the reverse inclusion, the <matrix units> satisfy
$$
E_{ij}=[E_{ii},E_{ij}]\quad(i\ne j),\qquad E_{ii}-E_{jj}=[E_{ij},E_{ji}].
$$
The off-diagonal <matrix units> and the differences $E_{ii}-E_{nn}$ for $i<n$ span all <traceless matrices>: a traceless diagonal part is $\sum_{i<n}a_{ii}(E_{ii}-E_{nn})$. Thus every element of the <kernel> is a linear combination of <commutators>, proving
$$
\boxed{W=\ker\operatorname{Tr}.}
$$
This establishes that <traceless matrices are spanned by commutators>. For $n=1$, both <vector subspaces> are zero. Notice that the argument needs a span of <commutators>; it does not assume that every such sum has already been written as a single commutator.

We prove <prime avoidance> by contradiction. Remove any <prime ideal> contained in another one on the list; this leaves the same union. If the <ideal> $I$ is contained in none of the remaining <prime ideals>, choose $a_i\in I\setminus P_i$. For every $j\ne i$, incomparability lets us choose $b_{ij}\in P_j\setminus P_i$. Set
$$
b_i=\prod_{j\ne i}b_{ij},\qquad c=\sum_i a_i b_i.

For a one-element list, use the empty product $b_1=1$. By the defining property of a <prime ideal>, $b_i\notin P_i$, and also $a_i b_i\notin P_i$. But $b_j\in P_i$ for every $j\ne i$. Thus all summands except $a_i b_i$ lie in $P_i$, and consequently $c\notin P_i$ for every $i$. On the other hand, $c\in I$ because each $a_i\in I$. \boxed{I\subseteq P_i\text{ for at least one }i.}
$$

A <Cauchy sequence> in a <metric space> satisfies: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that $d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon$ whenever $m,n\geq N$. Suppose a <subsequence> $x_{n_k}$ converges to $x\in X$. Choose $N$ so that the Cauchy bound is $\varepsilon/2$, and then choose $k$ with $n_k\geq N$ and $d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon/2$. For every $n\geq N$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
d(x_n,x)\leq d(x_n,x_{n_k})+d(x_{n_k},x)<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus **the whole Cauchy sequence converges to the subsequential limit**.

We argue by contradiction using the previous <subsequence> construction. If $X$ is not <complete>, choose a <Cauchy sequence> with no limit in $X$. None of its $d_m$ can be zero: if $d_m=0$, then $d(x_m,x_n)\to0$, giving a limit in $X$. The construction therefore produces pairwise distinct $y_j$ with

$$
\boxed{X\text{ is complete}.}
$$
This is the converse direction of <contraction property of closed subsets characterizes completeness>.

$$
\boxed{d_p(a,b)=|a-b|_p.}
$$
Factoring out the smaller power of $p$ from two rational numbers shows $v_p(q+r)\geq\min\{v_p(q),v_p(r)\}$, with the usual convention $v_p(0)=+\infty$. Consequently the <ultrametric inequality> gives
$$
d_p(a,b)=|(a-c)+(c-b)|_p\leq\max\{d_p(a,c),d_p(c,b)\}.
$$
Together with symmetry, positivity and separation of points, this verifies that $d_p$ is a <metric>.

For the given geometric partial sums, use the finite <geometric series> identity. Since $p$ does not divide $p-1$,
$$
a_n=\frac{p^n-1}{p-1},\qquad \left|a_n+\frac1{p-1}\right|_p=\left|\frac{p^n}{p-1}\right|_p=p^{-n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus **the limit is the rational number** $\boxed{1/(1-p)}$, even though the partial sums diverge in the ordinary real <metric>.

$$
\boxed{|a+b|_p=\max\{|a|_p,|b|_p\}\quad\text{when }|a|_p\ne|b|_p.}
$$
Finally, let $b$ be outside the <open ball> $B(a,\delta)$, so $d_p(a,b)\geq\delta$. If $d_p(b,c)<\delta$, the preceding unequal-norm identity gives $d_p(a,c)=d_p(a,b)\geq\delta$. <Convergence of a numerical method> on a fixed interval $[0,T]$ means that, as the step size tends to zero and the starting approximations tend to the exact initial values,
$$
\boxed{\max_{0\leq t_n\leq T}|y_n-y(t_n)|\longrightarrow0.}
$$
For a vector-valued <ordinary differential equation>, replace the absolute value by a <norm>. The interval remains fixed as $h\to0$; a small error at only one step is insufficient.

The <order of a numerical method> is measured by its defect on a smooth exact solution. With the unscaled residual convention for a <linear multistep method>, order at least $p$ means local residual $O(h^{p+1})$; equivalently, the residual divided by $h$ is $O(h^p)$. Order exactly $p$ means the next coefficient does not vanish in general. For a <zero-stable> method, under the usual smoothness and <Lipschitz continuity> assumptions and with starting errors $O(h^p)$, this yields <global error> $O(h^p)$ on the fixed interval. Local order by itself does not imply convergence without stability.

With $\rho_0=a$, $\rho_1=\theta$, $\rho_2=1$, the constant order condition gives $a=-1-\theta$. The next three <order conditions for a linear multistep method> give
$$
\theta+2=\sigma_0+\sigma_1+\sigma_2,\qquad \theta+4=2\sigma_1+4\sigma_2,\qquad \theta+8=3\sigma_1+12\sigma_2.
$$
Solving this linear system yields **the unique coefficients of order at least three**:
$$
\boxed{a=-1-\theta,\qquad \sigma_0=\frac{5\theta+4}{12},\quad \sigma_1=\frac{2(\theta+2)}3,\quad \sigma_2=\frac{4-\theta}{12}.}
$$
The characteristic polynomial controlling <zero-stability> factors as
$$
\rho(\zeta)=\zeta^2+\theta\zeta-1-\theta=(\zeta-1)(\zeta+1+\theta).
$$
The other root is $-1-\theta$. Requiring its modulus to be at most one gives $-2\leq\theta\leq0$, but $\theta=-2$ produces a repeated root at one and violates the <root condition for a multistep method>. At $\theta=0$, the roots $1,-1$ are both simple, so that endpoint is allowed. By the <Dahlquist equivalence theorem>, **the method converges precisely for**
$$
\boxed{-2<\theta\leq0.}
$$
The fourth defect coefficient is $C_4=\theta$, so the method has order exactly three for nonzero $\theta$. At $\theta=0$ it is the two-step Simpson method, of order four; thus the extra-order endpoint has correctly remained in the answer.

A <statistic> $T$ is a <sufficient statistic> for $\theta$ if the conditional <distribution> of the data given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$. For a dominated family with density or mass function $f_\theta(x)$, the <Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem> states that this is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{f_\theta(x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x),}
$$
where $h$ is nonnegative and independent of $\theta$, and $g_\theta$ is nonnegative and depends on the data only through $T$.

For a discrete sample space, suppose first that the factorization holds. If $\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)>0$, summing over the fibre gives $\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)=g_\theta(t)\sum_{T(x)=t}h(x)$, so
$$
\mathbb P_\theta(X=x\mid T=t)=\frac{h(x)}{\sum_{T(z)=t}h(z)}\quad(T(x)=t).
$$
This conditional <distribution> is independent of $\theta$, proving that $T$ is a <sufficient statistic>. Conversely, let $q_t(x)$ be the common conditional mass function guaranteed by the <sufficient statistic> property, for every fibre attainable under at least one parameter. Set $h(x)=q_{T(x)}(x)$ and $g_\theta(t)=\mathbb P_\theta(T=t)$. Then $\mathbb P_\theta(X=x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x)$. Fibres never attained under any parameter can be assigned any conditional distribution, since their multiplying probability is always zero. This proves both directions of the discrete criterion, including possible parameter-dependent supports.

The <Rao-Blackwell theorem> says that an unbiased finite-variance <estimator> $\delta(X)$ of a parameter function $\tau(\theta)$ can be improved using a <sufficient statistic> $T$. Define
$$
\delta^*(T)=\mathbb E[\delta(X)\mid T].
$$
Because the conditional <distribution> given $T$ is parameter-independent, this is a statistic computable without knowing $\theta$. The <tower property of conditional expectation> gives $\mathbb E_\theta\delta^*=\mathbb E_\theta\delta=\tau(\theta)$, so it remains unbiased. The <law of total variance> gives
$$
\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta)=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta^*)+\mathbb E_\theta[\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta\mid T)].
$$
Hence **the improved estimator has no larger variance or mean squared error**:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta^*)\leq\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta),\qquad \mathbb E_\theta[(\delta^*-\tau(\theta))^2]\leq\mathbb E_\theta[(\delta-\tau(\theta))^2].}
$$
Equality holds precisely when $\delta=\delta^*(T)$ almost surely under the relevant parameter. More generally, even without unbiasedness, <conditional Jensen inequality> shows that conditioning on a sufficient statistic cannot increase risk for any convex loss in the estimate, provided the expectations exist. The squared-loss result above is the usual variance form of the theorem.

Write $L=\min_iX_i$, $R=\max_iX_i$ and $M=\max_i|X_i|=\max\{-L,R\}$. The joint <probability density> factors as
$$
f_\theta(x)=(2\theta)^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta\geq\max_i|x_i|\}}=(2\theta)^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta\geq\max(-L,R)\}}.
$$
By the <Fisher-Neyman factorization theorem>, **$T=(L,R)$ is a <sufficient statistic>**. The same factorization shows that the smaller <statistic> $M$ is also a <sufficient statistic>. This gives the <sufficient statistic for a symmetric uniform sample>.

In fact, the <likelihood-ratio criterion for minimal sufficiency> identifies $M$ as a <minimal sufficient statistic>: two sample points with the same $M$ have identical likelihoods for all $\theta$; with different $M$, their likelihoods have different parameter supports, and so are not proportional as functions of $\theta$. Equivalently, the condition that $f_\theta(x)=c f_\theta(y)$ for every $\theta$ and some positive constant $c$ holds exactly when their absolute maxima agree.

To see rigorously that $T$ cannot be recovered from $M$ almost surely, fix any $\theta>0$. The sample has a <distribution> invariant under simultaneous sign reversal. If $T=h(M)$ almost surely, the same identity would hold for the sign-reversed sample on another probability-one set. Since $M$ is unchanged by sign reversal, it would follow that
$$
(L,R)=(-R,-L)\quad\text{almost surely},
$$
and hence $L=-R$ almost surely. But this event has probability zero for a finite sample from a continuous <uniform distribution>: it entails $X_i=-X_j$ for some pair of indices, including the possible case $X_i=0$. Therefore $T$ is not a function of the sufficient statistic $M$. **The pair of extrema is not minimally sufficient**, for every $n\geq1$, even though it is sufficient.

A <Markov chain> is <irreducible> if every state can reach every other state in finitely many steps with positive probability. It is a <recurrent Markov chain> if, starting at any state, it returns to that state with probability one; for an irreducible chain this property is shared by all states.

For the <simple symmetric random walk> on $\mathbb Z$, taking $|j-i|$ steps all in the appropriate direction reaches $j$ from $i$ with probability $2^{-|j-i|}>0$. Thus the chain is irreducible. Its return probabilities are
$$
p_{00}^{(2k)}=\binom{2k}{k}2^{-2k},\qquad p_{00}^{(2k+1)}=0.
$$
By <Stirling formula>, $p_{00}^{(2k)}\sim1/\sqrt{\pi k}$, so their sum diverges. The standard recurrence criterion $\sum_{n\geq0}p_{00}^{(n)}=\infty$ therefore proves **the walk is recurrent**.

The printed hint's constant is erroneous: the actual limit of $\sqrt{k}\,p_{00}^{(2k)}$ is $1/\sqrt\pi$. Its positivity is what the recurrence argument requires, but the reciprocal is necessary for the correct numerical value.

An irreducible <Markov chain> has <positive recurrent states> if the expected first return time to a state is finite; this then holds for every state. A recurrent chain with infinite mean return time has <null recurrent states>.

An irreducible positive recurrent chain admits a <stationary distribution>. If such a distribution $\pi$ existed for this walk, its stationarity equations would give
$$
\pi_j=\tfrac12(\pi_{j-1}+\pi_{j+1})\quad(j\in\mathbb Z).
$$
Thus $\pi_{j+1}-\pi_j=\pi_j-\pi_{j-1}$, so $\pi_j=A+Bj$. Nonnegativity for all positive and negative integers forces $B=0$. A constant sequence cannot sum to one over $\mathbb Z$, so no stationary probability distribution exists. Hence **the walk is null recurrent, not positive recurrent**: all its states are <null recurrent states>. The first-return generating function in part (c) also verifies directly that its mean return time is infinite.

Let $u_n=\mathbb P_0(X_n=0)$ and $f_n=\mathbb P_0(T=n)$. Decomposing at the first return and using the <Markov property> gives the <renewal equation>
$$
u_0=1,\qquad u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n f_k u_{n-k}\quad(n\geq1).
$$
For $0<s<1$, define the <probability generating functions> $U(s)=\sum_{n\geq0}u_ns^n$ and $F(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}f_ns^n=\mathbb E_0[s^T]$. Absolute convergence, or nonnegative summation, justifies the convolution identity $U=1+FU$. The <binomial series> gives
$$
U(s)=\sum_{k\geq0}\binom{2k}{k}\left(\frac{s^2}{4}\right)^k=\frac1{\sqrt{1-s^2}}.
$$
Solving the renewal identity gives **the <first-return generating function of the simple symmetric random walk>**
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_0[s^T]=F(s)=1-\sqrt{1-s^2},\qquad 0<s<1.}
$$
As consistency checks, $F(s)\to1$ as $s\uparrow1$, confirming recurrence, while $F'(s)=s/\sqrt{1-s^2}\to\infty$. By monotone convergence of $\sum_n n f_n s^{n-1}$, this last limit is $\mathbb E_0T=\infty$, confirming <null recurrent states>.
