= Solution
{parent=a}

The <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
100=79+21,\qquad79=3\cdot21+16,\qquad21=16+5,\qquad16=3\cdot5+1.
$$
Back substitution produces the <Bézout's identity>
$$
1=16-3(21-16)=4(79-3\cdot21)-3\cdot21
=19\cdot79-15\cdot100.
$$
Hence a positive <integer> solution is $\boxed{m=19,\ n=15}$. More generally, subtracting this identity from any other solution shows $79(m-19)=100(n-15)$. Since $79$ and $100$ are <coprime>, the <integer> solutions are $m=19+100k$, $n=15+79k$; both are positive precisely when $k\geq0$.

Divide the <linear congruence> by the common factor $3$, also dividing its modulus:
$$
237x\equiv21\pmod{300}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad79x\equiv7\pmod{100}.
$$
The <Bézout's identity> from part (a) makes $19$ the inverse of $79$ modulo $100$. Multiplication by $19$ therefore gives $x\equiv133\equiv33\pmod{100}$. Thus all <integer> solutions are $\boxed{x=33+100k,\ k\in\mathbb Z}$. Equivalently, the three solution classes modulo $300$ are $33,133,233$; using modulus $300$ must not discard two of these classes.

= Solution
{parent=2E}

For the odd-square sum, the initial case $n=1$ gives $1=(4-1)/3$. Assume the formula at $n$. Adding the next square gives
$$
\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}(2j-1)^2
=\frac{4n^3-n}{3}+(2n+1)^2
=\frac{4n^3+12n^2+11n+3}{3}
=\frac{4(n+1)^3-(n+1)}3.
$$
The initial case and this <induction> step prove $\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^n(2j-1)^2=(4n^3-n)/3}$ for every positive <integer> $n$.

For the <divisibility> assertion, $1^3+5\cdot1=6$. The change when $n$ is replaced by $n+1$ is
$$
(n+1)^3+5(n+1)-(n^3+5n)=3n(n+1)+6.
$$
One of the consecutive <integers> $n,n+1$ is even, so the displayed change is divisible by $6$. If $n^3+5n$ is divisible by $6$, its successor is too. Thus <induction> proves $\boxed{6\mid n^3+5n}$ for every $n\geq1$.

= Solution
{parent=3A}

Choose the positive direction along the incoming car's <velocity>. Treat each car as a translating body, take $m_1,m_2>0$, and neglect external impulse, rotation and <energy> losses during the short <elastic collision>. If $v_1,v_2$ are the final signed <velocities>, <conservation of momentum> and <conservation of energy> give
$$
m_1U_1=m_1v_1+m_2v_2,\qquad
m_1U_1^2=m_1v_1^2+m_2v_2^2.
$$
Factoring the second relation and using the first gives $U_1+v_1=v_2$ on the collision branch. The other algebraic branch, $v_1=U_1,v_2=0$, describes no impulse and cannot describe separating bodies after contact. Solving the collision branch yields
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac{R-1}{R+1}U_1,\qquad v_2=\frac{2R}{R+1}U_1,\qquad R=\frac{m_1}{m_2}.}
$$
For $0<R<1$ the first car rebounds and the second moves forwards. At $R=1$ the first stops and the second takes its original <velocity>. For $R>1$ both move forwards, with $v_2-v_1=U_1>0$, so they separate. In the limits $R\to0$ and $R\to\infty$, the first car's <velocity> approaches $-U_1$ and $U_1$, respectively.

For the deformable car, take $y=0$ at first contact. The resisting <force> does negative <work>. The <work-energy theorem> therefore gives
$$
\frac12Mv(y)^2=\frac12MV^2-\int_0^y\frac{ds}{\sqrt{L-s}}
=\frac12MV^2-2\bigl(\sqrt L-\sqrt{L-y}\bigr).
$$
At the first stop $v=0$, whence $\sqrt{L-y}=\sqrt L-MV^2/4$. The <speed> bound ensures that this right-hand side is positive, so squaring introduces no extraneous solution. The stopping compression is
$$
\boxed{y=L-\left(\sqrt L-\frac{MV^2}{4}\right)^2
=\frac{MV^2\sqrt L}{2}-\frac{M^2V^4}{16}.}
$$
For $0<V<2L^{1/4}/M^{1/2}$ this lies strictly between $0$ and $L$, as required before complete crushing. The coefficient in the stipulated <force> law is taken in its stated normalization.

= Solution
{parent=4A}

Let $z$ measure height above the release point, and use $\mathcal V=4\pi a^3/3$ for the bubble volume to avoid confusing it with a <speed>. For constant radius, <Newton's second law>, the specified <buoyancy> and the given <Stokes law> give
$$
\alpha\rho\mathcal V\dot u=\rho g\mathcal V-6\pi\mu au.
$$
With positive $\alpha,\rho,\mu,a$, define
$$
\tau=\frac{\alpha\rho\mathcal V}{6\pi\mu a}
=\frac{2\alpha\rho a^2}{9\mu},\qquad
U=\frac{\rho g\mathcal V}{6\pi\mu a}
=\frac{2\rho g a^2}{9\mu}.
$$
The <differential equation> becomes $\dot u+(u/\tau)=U/\tau$. Its solution with $u(0)=0$ is $u(t)=U(1-e^{-t/\tau})$. Integration using $z(0)=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{z(t)=U\left[t-\tau\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)\right],\qquad
\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t)=U=\frac{2\rho g a^2}{9\mu}.}
$$
An arbitrary initial height is added to this expression. The effective inertia changes the relaxation time $\tau$, but not the <terminal velocity> obtained by balancing <buoyancy> and drag.

For the dissolving bubble assume $\beta>0$ and use the specified instantaneous <terminal velocity>, rather than the constant-radius transient formula. The bubble disappears at $t_*=a_0^2/\beta$, and
$$
z(t)=\frac{2\rho g}{9\mu}\int_0^t(a_0^2-\beta s)\,ds
=\frac{2\rho g}{9\mu}\left(a_0^2t-\frac{\beta t^2}{2}\right),\qquad0\leq t\leq t_*.
$$
Its total rise is therefore $\boxed{z(t_*)=\rho g a_0^4/(9\mu\beta)}$. This second model neglects the initial <acceleration> and uses exactly the terminal-speed approximation stipulated for the shrinking bubble.

= Solution

Number the <natural numbers> from one. If the <power set> could be enumerated as $A_1,A_2,\ldots$, form $D=\{j:j\notin A_j\}$. For each $j$, membership of $j$ in $D$ is opposite to its membership in $A_j$, so $D\ne A_j$. This contradicts completeness of the enumeration. Thus the <Cantor diagonal argument> proves that the <power set> is an <uncountable set>.

For a finite <subset> $A$, define $b(A)=\sum_{j\in A}2^{j-1}$. This is a nonnegative <integer> and the map is injective: if two finite <subsets> differ, let $k$ be their largest differing element. Its contribution $2^{k-1}$ exceeds the sum $1+2+\cdots+2^{k-2}$ of all smaller possible contributions, so their codes differ. There are infinitely many singleton <subsets>. Hence the <countability of finite subsets of a countable set> here is countable infinitude.

To treat all <bijections>, associate to every <subset> $A\subseteq\mathbb N$ the <permutation> that swaps $2j-1$ and $2j$ when $j\in A$, and fixes both when $j\notin A$. These disjoint pair swaps define a <bijection> of $\mathbb N$. Different <subsets> give different <permutations>, so the map from the <power set> into $X$ is injective. Therefore $X$ is an <uncountable set>.

Every <finitely supported permutation> fixes all elements above some $k$. A <bijection> fixing that tail must permute $\{1,\ldots,k\}$: it cannot send a smaller element into the fixed tail without violating injectivity. There are exactly $k!$ such <permutations>. Thus
$$
Y=\bigcup_{k\geq1}\{\sigma:\sigma(j)=j\text{ for every }j>k\}
$$
is a <countable union> of finite <sets>. Enumerate first by $k$, then by one of the finitely many <permutations> at that $k$; duplicates can be omitted. Infinitely many distinct pair swaps belong to $Y$, so $Y$ is countably infinite. **The full family is uncountable, while the finitely supported family is countably infinite.**

= Solution
{parent=6E}

For a <prime number> $p$ and an <integer> $x$ <coprime> to $p$, multiplication by $x$ permutes the nonzero residue classes modulo $p$. Indeed, $ax\equiv bx\pmod p$ implies $a\equiv b\pmod p$, and none of the products is zero. Taking the product over the $p-1$ classes gives
$$
x^{p-1}(p-1)!\equiv(p-1)!\pmod p.
$$
The <factorial> is <coprime> to $p$ and can be cancelled, proving <Fermat little theorem>: $\boxed{x^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p}$.

Now $n=mp$ and $x$ is <coprime> to $n$, so it is <coprime> to $p$. Since $(n-1)-(m-1)=m(p-1)$, the proved theorem gives
$$
x^{n-1}=x^{m-1}(x^{p-1})^m\equiv x^{m-1}\pmod p.
$$
Thus the two specified <integer congruences> modulo $p$ are equivalent. In this particular step the additional hypothesis $(m,p)=1$ is not needed; it will hold for the squarefree factorization used next.

If $n$ is a <squarefree integer>, its distinct <prime factors> are pairwise <coprime>. Consequently $n$ divides $x^{n-1}-1$ exactly when every <prime factor> does. Applying the preceding equivalence with $m=n/p$ proves
$$
\boxed{x^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x^{(n/p)-1}\equiv1\pmod p\text{ for every }p\mid n.}
$$
Finally retain this squarefree hypothesis. If $p-1$ divides $n-1$, write $n-1=k_p(p-1)$. <Fermat little theorem> gives $x^{n-1}=(x^{p-1})^{k_p}\equiv1\pmod p$ for every <prime factor>. Their pairwise coprimality then gives $\boxed{x^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n}$. For composite $n$ this is the sufficient direction of the <Korselt criterion> for a <Carmichael number>; primality is not excluded by the requested implication.

= Solution
{parent=7E}

The <binomial polynomial> $P_r$ equals $1$ when $r=0$. For $n\geq0$, it has value $\binom nr$ if $r\leq n$ and value zero otherwise. These are <integers>. If $n=-k<0$, then
$$
P_r(-k)=(-1)^r\frac{k(k+1)\cdots(k+r-1)}{r!}
=(-1)^r\binom{k+r-1}{r}\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Hence each $P_r$ is an <integer-valued polynomial> on all of $\mathbb Z$, including negative arguments.

For $r\geq1$, factor the common product in the two <polynomials>:
$$
P_r(X)-P_r(X-1)
=\frac{(X-1)\cdots(X-r+1)}{r!}\bigl[X-(X-r)\bigr]
=P_{r-1}(X-1).
$$
For $r=1$ the common product is empty and equals $1$, so this argument also covers that boundary case.

The <polynomial> $P_r$ has <polynomial degree> $r$ and <leading coefficient> $1/r!$. For a <polynomial> $F$ of <polynomial degree> $d$ with <leading coefficient> $a_d\in\mathbb Q$, subtract $d!a_dP_d$; the remainder has smaller <polynomial degree>. Repeating gives the required expansion with <rational numbers> $c_r$. For uniqueness, suppose two expansions differ and let $s$ be the largest index with different coefficients. Their difference then has a nonzero term of <polynomial degree> $s$, with <leading coefficient> equal to that coefficient difference divided by $s!$. It cannot be the zero <polynomial>. Thus the expansion is unique.

Replacing $X$ by $X+1$ in the earlier identity gives $\Delta P_r(X)=P_{r-1}(X)$ for $r\geq1$, while $\Delta P_0=0$. By linearity of the <forward difference operator>,
$$
\boxed{\Delta F(X)=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}c_{r+1}(F)P_r(X).}
$$
An empty sum is zero when $d=0$. If $H=F-G$ were nonconstant, with <polynomial degree> $s\geq1$ and <leading coefficient> $a_s$, then $\Delta H$ would have <polynomial degree> $s-1$ and <leading coefficient> $sa_s\ne0$. Therefore $\Delta F=\Delta G$ <forces> $H$ to be constant.

There is a direct way to recover every coefficient. Since $P_0(0)=1$ and $P_j(0)=0$ for $j\geq1$, repeated application of the <forward difference operator> gives
$$
\boxed{c_r(F)=\Delta^r F(0)=\sum_{j=0}^r(-1)^{r-j}\binom rj F(j).}
$$
The last equality follows by expanding $(T-I)^r$, where $TF(X)=F(X+1)$; the commuting shifts obey the <binomial theorem>. If $F$ is an <integer-valued polynomial>, every summand on the right is an <integer>, proving that all $c_r(F)$ are <integers>. Together with the first paragraph this gives both directions of the <Newton series for an integer-valued polynomial sequence> description.

= Solution
{parent=8E}

For each $x\in X$, the product $\prod_{i=1}^m(1-g_i(x))$ is $1$ precisely outside every $X_i$, and is $0$ otherwise. It is therefore the <indicator function> of $Y$. Expanding the finite product gives
$$
\prod_{i=1}^m(1-g_i(x))
=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}}(-1)^{|S|}\prod_{i\in S}g_i(x).
$$
Multiply by $f(x)$ and sum over the finite <set> $X$. The product of <indicator functions> is the <indicator function> of the corresponding intersection, so
$$
\sum_{x\in Y}f(x)
=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}}(-1)^{|S|}
\sum_{x\in\bigcap_{i\in S}X_i}f(x).
$$
Here the empty intersection is $X$, and the empty product is $1$. Grouping the <subsets> $S$ by their <cardinality> gives the requested <weighted inclusion-exclusion principle>, with no contribution for $r>m$.

Let $\mathcal P$ be the finite <set> of distinct <prime factors> of $n$, and work in $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$. For $S\subseteq\mathcal P$ put $d_S=\prod_{p\in S}p$, including $d_\varnothing=1$. Then $d_S\mid n$, and an <integer> belongs to every $X_p$ for $p\in S$ exactly when it is a multiple of $d_S$. Thus the intersection has size $n/d_S$. The complement consists precisely of the residues <coprime> to $n$. Using weight $1$ gives
$$
\boxed{\varphi(n)=\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal P}(-1)^{|S|}\frac n{d_S}
=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right).}
$$
This proves the product formula for the <Euler totient function> without requiring $n$ to be squarefree.

For the weight $f(x)=x$, the sum over the multiples of any divisor $d$ in this interval is
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n/d-1}dj
=\frac{n^2}{2d}-\frac n2.
$$
The <weighted inclusion-exclusion principle> therefore gives
$$
\sum_{\substack{0<x<n\\(x,n)=1}}x
=\frac{n^2}{2}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal P}\frac{(-1)^{|S|}}{d_S}
-\frac n2\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal P}(-1)^{|S|}.
$$
Since $n>1$, $\mathcal P$ is nonempty and the last sum is $(1-1)^{|\mathcal P|}=0$. The first factors as before, giving
$$
\boxed{\sum_{\substack{0<x<n\\(x,n)=1}}x
=\frac{n^2}{2}\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)
=\frac{n\varphi(n)}2.}
$$
This <sum of reduced residues> also follows by pairing the <bijection> $x\mapsto n-x$; at $n=2$ its sole fixed residue causes no problem because the sum of both copies still equals $n\varphi(n)$.

= Solution
{parent=9A}

Use a <translating accelerated reference frame>; its axes do not rotate. If $\mathbf R=(\xi,\eta,\zeta)$ is the block's <position> relative to the table, then its inertial <position> is $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf A\sin\omega t$. Its relative <velocity> is $\dot{\mathbf R}$ and its inertial <acceleration> is $\ddot{\mathbf R}-\omega^2\mathbf A\sin\omega t$. Let $N$ be the upward <normal force>. During contact $\zeta=\dot\zeta=\ddot\zeta=0$, and the linear tangential resistance gives
$$
\boxed{m\ddot\xi+\lambda\dot\xi=mA_x\omega^2\sin\omega t,\qquad
m\ddot\eta+\lambda\dot\eta=0,\qquad
m\ddot\zeta=N-mg+mA_z\omega^2\sin\omega t.}
$$
These are <Newton's second law> transformed to the table's <reference frame>; the extra terms arise from the table's <acceleration>. The last equation gives $N=m(g-A_z\omega^2\sin\omega t)$ while contact persists. If contact is lost, both the contact resistance and $N$ vanish, and free flight must be described by the corresponding gravity-only equations.

For $A_z=0$, assume $\lambda>0$ and put $\gamma=\lambda/m$. Seek a periodic particular solution $\xi_p=b\sin\omega t+c\cos\omega t$. Substitution into the first <differential equation> gives
$$
-\omega^2b-\gamma\omega c=A_x\omega^2,\qquad
-\omega^2c+\gamma\omega b=0.
$$
Solving yields
$$
b=-\frac{A_x\omega^2}{\gamma^2+\omega^2},\qquad
c=-\frac{A_x\gamma\omega}{\gamma^2+\omega^2}.
$$
The homogeneous solutions are a constant and a multiple of $e^{-\gamma t}$. Choose the phase $0<\theta<\pi/2$ with $\tan\theta=\omega/\gamma=m\omega/\lambda$. Then $b=-A_x\sin^2\theta$ and $c=-A_x\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so
$$
\boxed{\xi(t)=\xi_0-A_x\sin\theta\cos(\omega t-\theta)+C e^{-\lambda t/m},\qquad
\sin^2\theta=\frac{m^2\omega^2}{\lambda^2+m^2\omega^2}.}
$$
Also $\eta(t)=\eta_\infty+C_\eta e^{-\lambda t/m}$. The decaying transients leave the stated steady orbit. Positive damping is essential to this late-time assertion; for $\lambda=0$ an additional constant drift may persist.

No attractive contact <force> is available, so $N$ cannot be negative. Over a whole oscillation its minimum is $m(g-\omega^2|A_z|)$. Thus **strictly positive contact loading throughout requires** $\boxed{\omega^2|A_z|<g}$, or the printed form with $A_z$ taken as a nonnegative vertical <amplitude>. If $\omega^2|A_z|>g$, contact cannot persist: it would require $N<0$ during part of the cycle. At equality, $N$ vanishes only at isolated instants and the ideal constrained motion can still remain in contact. Hence the strict inequality characterizes positive loading; mere nonnegative contact permits the marginal equality case.

= Solution
{parent=10A}

Write $k=GM$ and $r=|\mathbf r|$. The gravitational <central force> has zero <torque>, so the specific <angular momentum>
$$
\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r},\qquad
\dot{\mathbf h}=\mathbf r\times\ddot{\mathbf r}=0
$$
is constant. For a nonradial orbit $h=|\mathbf h|>0$, motion lies in the plane normal to $\mathbf h$, and plane polar coordinates give $r^2\dot\theta=h$. With $u=1/r$ and primes denoting $\theta$ derivatives,
$$
\dot r=-hu',\qquad \ddot r=-h^2u^2u'',\qquad r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3.
$$
The radial equation $\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-k/r^2$ is therefore the <Binet equation>
$$
u''+u=\frac{k}{h^2}.
$$
Its general solution, after choosing the polar origin of angle, is
$$
u=\frac{k}{h^2}(1+e\cos\theta),\qquad
\boxed{r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta},\quad p=\frac{h^2}{GM}.}
$$
The origin is a focus of this <conic section>. To identify which <conic section> occurs, use the conserved specific <energy>
$$
\varepsilon=\frac12(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-\frac{k}{r}
=\frac{h^2}{2}\left[(u')^2+u^2\right]-ku
=\frac{k^2}{2h^2}(e^2-1).
$$
A bound, nonradial orbit has $\varepsilon<0$, hence $0\leq e<1$ and is an <ellipse>. For example, putting $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ in $r+ex=p$ gives
$$
(1-e^2)\left(x+\frac{ep}{1-e^2}\right)^2+y^2=\frac{p^2}{1-e^2},
$$
an explicit <ellipse> equation. This is the intended low, bound <Kepler orbit>. The inverse-square equation alone does not imply an <ellipse>: an initially tangential <speed> larger than $\sqrt{2GM/r}$ gives $\varepsilon>0$, $e>1$ and a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>. Zero <angular momentum> instead gives radial motion. Those qualifications are necessary for the elliptic conclusion.

In a circular orbit, centripetal <acceleration> balances the gravitational <acceleration>:
$$
r_0\Omega^2=\frac{GM}{r_0^2},\qquad
\boxed{T=\frac{2\pi}{\Omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{GM}}.}
$$
Here $\Omega$ is the positive <angular speed>; either sense of circulation has this <period>.

With the stated drag <force>, use the full <angular momentum> $\mathbf L=m\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}$. Gravitational <torque> is still zero, while the drag <torque> is
$$
\dot{\mathbf L}=\mathbf r\times(-A\dot{\mathbf r})
=-\frac{A}{m}\mathbf L.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\mathbf L(t)=\mathbf L(0)e^{-At/m}}$ for constant $A>0$. Its direction remains fixed, and its magnitude has <exponential decay> with time constant $m/A$; the orbit remains planar although its shape is no longer a fixed <ellipse>.

= Solution
{parent=11A}

Taking the <scalar product> of the <Lorentz force> equation with the <velocity> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2\right)
=q\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot(\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf B)=0.
$$
The triple product vanishes because the <cross product> is perpendicular to its first factor. Thus **the <kinetic energy> is constant**, even if the <magnetic field> varies with <position>: <magnetic forces do no work>.

In <cylindrical coordinates>, the radial, angular and vertical equations are
$$
m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=qr\dot\theta B(r),\qquad
m(r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta)=-q\dot r B(r),\qquad
m\ddot z=0.
$$
For a nonstationary circular orbit with constant $r$ and $z$, the first equation gives $-m\dot\theta^2=qB(r)\dot\theta$. Hence its signed <angular velocity> is $\boxed{\dot\theta=-qB(r)/m}$. The sign gives the sense of circulation. The zero root describes a stationary transverse <position>, not a moving circular orbit. A constant vertical <velocity> can be superposed to produce a helix.

Define the mechanical <angular momentum> about the axis by $L=mr^2\dot\theta$. Multiplying the angular equation by $r$ gives
$$
\dot L=-qrB(r)\dot r.
$$
For the inverse-radius <magnetic field> this becomes $\dot L=-qB_0\dot r$. Integrating from an initial radius $r_0$ proves
$$
\boxed{L=L_0-qB_0(r-r_0).}
$$
Let $c=qB_0/m$ and $\ell=(L_0+qB_0r_0)/m$, so that $L/m=\ell-cr$. Since $\dot z$ is constant and the total <kinetic energy> is constant, the transverse <speed> squared
$$
v_\perp^2=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2
$$
is also constant. Substituting the <angular momentum> relation gives the required radial curve:
$$
\boxed{\dot r^2=W(r)=v_\perp^2-\left(\frac{\ell}{r}-c\right)^2
=v_\perp^2-c^2+\frac{2\ell c}{r}-\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}.}
$$
Only the region $W(r)\geq0$ is accessible. For $\ell\ne0$, $W(r)\to-\infty$ as $r\to0$, and its horizontal asymptote is $v_\perp^2-c^2$. Its derivative is $W'(r)=2\ell(\ell-cr)/r^3$. Thus, when $\ell c>0$, it has a single maximum $W=v_\perp^2$ at $r=\ell/c$. The positive roots of $W$ are the <turning points> obtained from $r=\ell/(c\pm v_\perp)$.

For nonzero $c$ and $\ell c>0$, write $r_c=|\ell/c|$. When $0<v_\perp<|c|$, two <turning points> occur at
$$
r_-=\frac{r_c}{1+v_\perp/|c|},\qquad
r_+=\frac{r_c}{1-v_\perp/|c|},
$$
and the radius is confined between them. At $v_\perp=|c|$, there is only the finite lower <turning point> $r_c/2$; $W$ tends to zero from above and an outgoing particle reaches arbitrarily large radius. For $v_\perp>|c|$, only the lower <turning point> remains and the outgoing radial <velocity> approaches $\sqrt{v_\perp^2-c^2}$. The sketch shows these three cases and omits inaccessible negative values of $\dot r^2$ from the physical interpretation.

\Image[paper-4-magnetic-radial-motion.png]
{title=Radial-speed curves and turning points in an inverse-radius axial magnetic field}

For completeness, if $\ell c<0$, $W$ rises monotonically from $-\infty$ to its asymptote; admissible motion then requires $v_\perp>|c|$ and is radially unbound after any inward branch turns. If $\ell=0$, $W=v_\perp^2-c^2$ is constant; equality gives a circular orbit rather than escape. Above threshold the outgoing branch escapes, whereas an incoming branch reaches the singular axis in finite time and cannot be continued through it without specifying a nonsingular core field. These cases explain why a borderline escape assertion needs an initial-direction qualification.

The transverse <escape velocity> threshold for <radial escape in an inverse-radius axial magnetic field> is
$$
\boxed{v_{\perp,\mathrm{esc}}=\frac{|qB_0|}{m}.}
$$
For a radial launch at $r_0$, $\ell=cr_0$, and equality permits marginal escape; for $\ell\ne0$, larger transverse <speed> guarantees radial escape in forward time after any initial inward leg turns. The singular incoming $\ell=0$ branch is the exception described above. More general launches at equality can instead give the circular exception $\ell=0$. A vertical <velocity> adds to the total <speed> but cannot pay the transverse escape-energy requirement; if escape merely means unbounded $z$, any nonzero $\dot z$ already achieves that. If $qB_0=0$, transverse motion is force-free and there is no positive escape-speed threshold.

= Solution
{parent=12A}

The <moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> is taken about its symmetry axis. Write its <mass density> as $\rho_c=m/(\pi a^2L)$. A coaxial shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ has <mass> $dm=2\pi\rho_c Lr\,dr$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I=\int_0^a r^2\,dm=2\pi\rho_c L\int_0^a r^3\,dr
=\frac12ma^2.}
$$
A hollow cylinder would have a different <moment of inertia>; uniform solid density is the intended assumption.

Let $s$ measure the centre's displacement uphill from its initial <position>, and choose signed <angular velocity> $\omega$ so that uphill <rolling without slipping> has $\dot s=a\omega$. Let $F$ be the signed contact <force> uphill. The translation and rotation equations are
$$
m\ddot s=-mg\sin\alpha+F,\qquad I\dot\omega=-aF.
$$
The minus sign in the <torque> equation follows from the chosen rolling sense: uphill contact <force> opposes the positive spin. Under <rolling without slipping>, $\ddot s=a\dot\omega$. Eliminating $F$ gives
$$
\left(m+\frac I{a^2}\right)\ddot s=-mg\sin\alpha,
\qquad \ddot s=-\frac23g\sin\alpha,
\qquad F=\frac13mg\sin\alpha.
$$
Using $s(0)=0$ and $\dot s(0)=V$, the no-slip motion and maximum vertical rise are
$$
\boxed{s(t)=Vt-\frac13g\sin\alpha\,t^2,\qquad
h_{\max}=\frac{3V^2}{4g}.}
$$
Indeed the centre turns at $t=3V/(2g\sin\alpha)$, reaching $s_{\max}=3V^2/(4g\sin\alpha)$, whose vertical component is the boxed height. <Conservation of energy> independently gives the same answer from initial <kinetic energy> $mV^2/2+I(V/a)^2/2=3mV^2/4$.

The available <static friction> is at most $\mu mg\cos\alpha$. It can supply the required uphill <force> exactly when $mg\sin\alpha/3\leq\mu mg\cos\alpha$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\tan\alpha>3\mu\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{slip starts}.}
$$
Interpret the stated friction magnitude as limiting <static friction> and as <kinetic friction> during slip, with the same coefficient. Take $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, $\mu\geq0$. In the slipping regime the contact point moves downhill relative to the slope, so <kinetic friction> acts uphill. To verify this direction rather than assume it unchecked, solve with $F=\mu mg\cos\alpha$. Define $b=g(\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha)>0$. The result is
$$
\boxed{s(t)=Vt-\frac12bt^2,\qquad
v(t)=\dot s=V-bt,\qquad
\omega(t)=\frac Va-\frac{2\mu g\cos\alpha}{a}t.}
$$
The centre's <speed> is $|V-bt|$, with the sign of $v$ specifying ascent or descent. The contact point's relative <velocity> is
$$
w(t)=v(t)-a\omega(t)=-gt(\sin\alpha-3\mu\cos\alpha)<0\qquad(t>0).
$$
Thus the assumed uphill <kinetic friction> is consistent throughout both ascent and descent, including when the centre's <velocity> changes sign. There is no later return to <rolling without slipping> before reaching the bottom. This is the characteristic <slipping solid cylinder on an incline> regime.

The centre turns at $t_*=V/b$, giving
$$
\boxed{h_{\max}=\frac{V^2\sin\alpha}{2g(\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha)}.}
$$
The formulas apply up to the positive return time $t_b=2V/b$. Substitution into the signed <velocity> and <angular velocity> gives
$$
\boxed{v(t_b)=-V,\qquad\text{speed at return}=V,\qquad
\omega(t_b)=\frac Va\frac{\sin\alpha-5\mu\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha}.}
$$
The return spin can have either sign and vanishes at $\tan\alpha=5\mu$. It reverses when $3\mu<\tan\alpha<5\mu$, although the contact point still slips downhill. The unchanged centre <speed> does not mean that no <energy> was dissipated: the rotational <kinetic energy> has decreased. Indeed integrating the frictional power $Fw<0$ gives
$$
K(0)-K(t_b)=\frac{2\mu mV^2\cos\alpha(\sin\alpha-3\mu\cos\alpha)}{(\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha)^2}\geq0,
$$
which agrees with the difference of the initial and final rotational <kinetic energies>.
