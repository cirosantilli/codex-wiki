Let $\mu$ be the vector of <expected returns> and $\Sigma$ the positive definite <covariance matrix> of risky returns. For risky <investment portfolio> weights $w$ satisfying $\mathbf1^Tw=1$, the <portfolio opportunity set> consists of
$$
(\sigma,m)=\bigl(\sqrt{w^T\Sigma w},\mu^Tw\bigr).
$$
<Short selling> permits negative weights. For a fixed <expected return>, the least possible <variance> is attained on the left boundary of this set. Its upper branch is the <mean-variance efficient frontier>: every other attainable point has either a larger <standard deviation> for the same <expected return> or a smaller <expected return> for the same risk. The lower boundary is therefore inefficient.

For example, writing $A=\mathbf1^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf1$, $B=\mathbf1^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu$, $C=\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu$ and $D=AC-B^2$, minimization of $w^T\Sigma w$ subject to its two linear constraints gives
$$
w=\Sigma^{-1}\left(\frac{C-Bm}{D}\mathbf1+\frac{Am-B}{D}\mu\right),\qquad
\sigma_{\min}^2(m)=\frac{Am^2-2Bm+C}{D}=\frac1A+\frac{A}{D}\left(m-\frac BA\right)^2.
$$
To obtain these weights, use <Lagrange multipliers>: the stationary equation is $\Sigma w=\lambda\mathbf1+\nu\mu$, and the budget and mean constraints determine $\lambda,\nu$. Positive definiteness makes this the unique constrained minimum. Thus the boundary is a hyperbola in risk/mean coordinates.

Adding the risk-free asset allows borrowing and lending at $r$. In the usual positive-premium case the <market portfolio> is the risky tangency portfolio, and combinations of it with the risk-free asset form the upper <capital market line>
$$
\boxed{m=r+\theta\sigma,\qquad \theta=\frac{m_M-r}{\sigma_M}.}
$$
The <Sharpe ratio> measures excess <expected return> per unit of <standard deviation>; its maximum is the <market price of risk> $\theta$. Points between the risk-free point and the tangency point lend some wealth; points beyond it borrow to leverage the <market portfolio>. The first panel illustrates the actual data here. The second anticipates the change in part (g), where the normalization of the tangency direction reverses its orientation.

Write $d=\mu-r\mathbf1$ and let $x$ denote the risky holdings as fractions of total wealth. The remaining fraction $1-\mathbf1^Tx$ is in the risk-free asset. Consequently their excess <expected return> is $d^Tx$ and their <variance> is $x^T\Sigma x$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> in the <inner product> defined by $\Sigma$ gives
$$
d^Tx=(\Sigma^{-1}d)^T\Sigma x
\leq\sqrt{d^T\Sigma^{-1}d}\sqrt{x^T\Sigma x}.
$$
Equality with positive excess return holds precisely for $x=cz$, $c>0$, where $z=\Sigma^{-1}d$. Thus solving $\Sigma z=d$ determines both the efficient risky direction and the upper <capital market line> slope. Provided $s=\mathbf1^Tz>0$, the normalized <market portfolio> and its <Sharpe ratio> are
$$
\boxed{w_M=\frac{z}{s},\qquad \theta=\sqrt{d^Tz}.}
$$
Here the excess means are $(7,14,21)^T$, so the required equations are
$$
4z_1+z_2+z_3=7,\quad z_1+9z_2+2z_3=14,\quad z_1+2z_2+16z_3=21.
$$
The leading principal minors of $\Sigma$ are $4$, $35$ and $539$, so <Sylvester's criterion> verifies positive definiteness and the solution is unique.

Indifference means equality of the risky <expected utility> and the utility of the sure reduced wealth. A second-order <Taylor expansion>, using $\mathbb E\phi=0$, gives
$$
\mathbb E U(W(1+\phi))=U(W)+\frac12W^2U''(W)\mathbb E\phi^2+o(\mathbb E\phi^2).
$$
For the sure alternative,
$$
U(W(1-\psi))=U(W)-WU'(W)\psi+O(\psi^2).
$$
Equating these and retaining the leading terms produces the <small multiplicative risk premium>
$$
\boxed{\psi\simeq-\frac12\frac{WU''(W)}{U'(W)}\operatorname{Var}(\phi).}
$$
For increasing concave <utility functions> this is positive. The approximation assumes genuinely small shocks with controlled Taylor remainders and positive final wealth; small <variance> alone would not justify it for rare large shocks.

= Solution

Constant <relative risk aversion coefficient> means that $\alpha=-WU''(W)/U'(W)$ does not depend on wealth, for $W>0$ and $U'(W)>0$. The defining differential equation integrates as
$$
\frac{d}{dW}\log U'(W)=-\frac{\alpha}{W},\qquad U'(W)=cW^{-\alpha},\quad c>0.
$$
A second integration therefore gives
$$
\boxed{U(W)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{cW^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}+d,&\alpha\ne1,\\ c\log W+d,&\alpha=1.\end{cases}}
$$
The additive constant and positive scale do not affect <expected utility> preferences. Since $U''(W)=-\alpha cW^{-\alpha-1}$, positive $\alpha$ gives <risk aversion>, zero gives <risk neutrality>, and negative $\alpha$ gives <risk seeking>. These are respectively concave, affine and convex <utility functions>. The usual <CRRA utility> class is the positive-$\alpha$ case.

Use $w$ for current wealth and $\pi=w-B$ for the dollar position in the risky asset. Its controlled dynamics have drift $rw+(\mu-r)\pi$ and diffusion coefficient $\sigma\pi$. Over a short time step $h$, the <dynamic programming principle> gives
$$
J(w,t)=\sup_\pi\mathbb E\left[\int_t^{t+h}e^{-\rho s}W_s^\gamma ds+J(W_{t+h},t+h)\mid W_t=w\right].
$$
Apply <Itô formula> to the second term, divide by $h$, and let $h\downarrow0$. The <Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation> is
$$
0=e^{-\rho t}w^\gamma+J_t+\sup_\pi\left\{[rw+(\mu-r)\pi]J_w+\frac12\sigma^2\pi^2J_{ww}\right\}.
$$
For the increasing concave value function, $J_w>0$ and $J_{ww}<0$, so the expression is a strictly concave quadratic in the risky position. Its derivative vanishes at
$$
\pi^*=-\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma^2}\frac{J_w}{J_{ww}},\qquad
\boxed{B^*=w+\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma^2}\frac{J_w}{J_{ww}}.}
$$
Substitution of this maximizer yields
$$
\boxed{J_t+rwJ_w-\frac12\left(\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}\right)^2\frac{J_w^2}{J_{ww}}+e^{-\rho t}w^\gamma=0,\qquad J(w,T)=w^\gamma.}
$$
There is a running utility reward but no consumption withdrawal in the wealth dynamics. The required concavity is also verified explicitly by the positive coefficient in part (d), so this stationary point really is the maximum.

= Solution

The <power-wealth investment with running utility> objective is homogeneous of degree $\gamma$ in wealth: scaling both wealth and positions by $a>0$ scales the reward by $a^\gamma$. Accordingly set $J(w,t)=A(t)w^\gamma$. Then
$$
\frac{J_w}{J_{ww}}=\frac{w}{\gamma-1},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{B^*}{w}=1-\frac{\mu-r}{(1-\gamma)\sigma^2},\qquad\frac{\pi^*}{w}=\frac{\mu-r}{(1-\gamma)\sigma^2}.}
$$
For completeness the remaining equation is $A'+\kappa A+e^{-\rho t}=0$, $A(T)=1$, where
$$
\kappa=\gamma r+\frac{\gamma(\mu-r)^2}{2(1-\gamma)\sigma^2},\qquad
A(t)=e^{\kappa(T-t)}+\int_t^T e^{\kappa(s-t)-\rho s}ds>0.
$$
This confirms $J_{ww}<0$. The constant optimal risky fraction also makes wealth a positive <geometric Brownian motion> when started positive.

Financially the <relative risk aversion coefficient> is $1-\gamma$. Increasing $\gamma$ reduces <risk aversion>, raises the risky fraction, and decreases the bond fraction: its derivative is $-(\mu-r)/[\sigma^2(1-\gamma)^2]<0$. If the risky fraction is below one, some wealth is lent; at one, none is invested in bonds; above one, $B^*<0$ represents borrowing to leverage the risky position. As $\gamma\uparrow1$ the risky fraction becomes unbounded, consistent with the absence of a finite unconstrained optimum for linear utility and positive excess drift. As $\gamma\downarrow0$ the limiting fraction is $(\mu-r)/\sigma^2$, the logarithmic-utility allocation obtained by the appropriately rescaled power-utility limit; the literal objective at $\gamma=0$ would be constant and would not select a unique strategy.

A process $M_n$ is a $\mathbb P$-<martingale> relative to the <filtration> $(\mathcal F_n)$ when it is adapted, each $M_n$ is integrable, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_{\mathbb P}[M_n\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}]=M_{n-1},\qquad 1\leq n\leq N.}
$$
Here adapted means $M_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, and integrable means $\mathbb E_{\mathbb P}|M_n|<\infty$. Thus its present value is the conditional mean of its next value given all present information.

= Solution

A <previsible> strategy selects $(\phi_n,\psi_n)$ using only $\mathcal F_{n-1}$, before the price changes from time $n-1$ to time $n$ occur. Thus these holdings cannot exploit future information.

The <self-financing portfolio> condition values both the old and newly rebalanced holdings at the same time-$n$ prices:
$$
\phi_nS_n+\psi_nB_n=\phi_{n+1}S_n+\psi_{n+1}B_n.
$$
Their equality says that rebalancing uses only the proceeds of selling existing assets: there is no external deposit or withdrawal. In particular, the changes in holdings have zero net cost at the current prices. The holdings for the following period may use the newly available information in $\mathcal F_n$.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At time $n-1$, the <self-financing portfolio> can be valued using the holdings about to be carried over the next period:
$$
V_{n-1}=\phi_nS_{n-1}+\psi_nB_{n-1}.
$$
Subtracting this from $V_n=\phi_nS_n+\psi_nB_n$ gives the gains identity
$$
\boxed{V_n-V_{n-1}=\phi_n(S_n-S_{n-1})+\psi_n(B_n-B_{n-1}).}
$$
Conversely, suppose the gains identity and the valuation $V_n=\phi_nS_n+\psi_nB_n$ hold. Rearranging the identity gives $V_{n-1}=\phi_nS_{n-1}+\psi_nB_{n-1}$. Comparing with the valuation using $(\phi_{n-1},\psi_{n-1})$ shows that rebalancing at $n-1$ costs nothing. Applying this at each rebalancing time recovers the <self-financing> condition.

Assume the <bank account> stays positive and write $\widetilde S_n=S_n/B_n$ and $\widetilde V_n=V_n/B_n$. Dividing the time-$n$ rebalancing equality by $B_n$ gives
$$
\widetilde V_n=\phi_n\widetilde S_n+\psi_n=\phi_{n+1}\widetilde S_n+\psi_{n+1}.
$$
The discounted <bank account> has constant price one. This is exactly the <self-financing> equality in the discounted market. Equivalently, subtract the corresponding valuation at $n-1$ to obtain the <discounted wealth equation in discrete time>
$$
\boxed{\widetilde V_n-\widetilde V_{n-1}=\phi_n(\widetilde S_n-\widetilde S_{n-1}).}
$$
This algebra uses only positivity of $B_n$, and does not require it to be deterministic.

The usual replication argument needs two hypotheses implicit in the pricing conclusion: the strategy must replicate the payoff, $V_N=X$, and its discounted gains must be integrable. Bounded <predictable> risky holdings suffice for the latter. Under these conditions, the <equivalent martingale measure> $\mathbb Q$ and part (c) give
$$
\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[\widetilde V_n-\widetilde V_{n-1}\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}]
=\phi_n\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[\widetilde S_n-\widetilde S_{n-1}\mid\mathcal F_{n-1}]=0.
$$
Thus discounted wealth is a <martingale>, and repeated conditioning gives
$$
\boxed{V_n=B_n\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[X/B_N\mid\mathcal F_n].}
$$
For a normalized initial <bank account>, $B_0=1$, deterministic terminal bank value, and trivial initial information, this reduces to the intended <risk-neutral pricing> formula
$$
\boxed{V_0=B_N^{-1}\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[X].}
$$
If $B_N$ is random it must remain inside the expectation. If a claim is not replicable, the existence of an <equivalent martingale measure> alone does not make its arbitrage-free price unique.

The <discounted wealth martingale integrability condition> cannot simply be dropped. For a concrete counterexample, set $B_n=1$, $S_0=3/2$, $S_1=1+U$, and $S_2=1+U+\varepsilon/2$, where $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$ and $\varepsilon$ an independent fair sign. These positive bounded prices form a <martingale> in the natural filtration. Start with zero holdings; at time one buy $1/U$ shares financed by borrowing $S_1/U$. This is <previsible> and <self-financing>, but its terminal wealth is $\varepsilon/(2U)$, whose absolute expectation is infinite. It is therefore not a <martingale>. The stated pricing proof applies to the standard integrable replication class, rather than all unconstrained previsible strategies.

For an invertible continuous <cumulative distribution function>, monotonicity gives
$$
\mathbb P(F^{-1}(U)\leq x)=\mathbb P(U\leq F(x))=F(x),\qquad U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1).
$$
Thus **each transformed observation has the desired distribution**. More generally use the <quantile function> $q(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geq u\}$; the same event identity proves <inverse transform sampling> even when there are atoms. Independent uniform inputs give independent transformed samples; mere uniform marginal distributions do not imply independence between different samples.

= Solution

One route is <inverse transform sampling>: if $\Phi$ is the standard normal <cumulative distribution function>, then $\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(U)$ has the <normal distribution> with mean $\mu$ and <variance> $\sigma^2$.

Alternatively the <Box-Muller transform> uses two independent uniform inputs $U_1,U_2\in(0,1)$:
$$
\boxed{Z_1=\sqrt{-2\log U_1}\cos(2\pi U_2),\qquad Z_2=\sqrt{-2\log U_1}\sin(2\pi U_2).}
$$
To verify this, set $R=\sqrt{-2\log U_1}$ and $\Theta=2\pi U_2$. The radial density is $re^{-r^2/2}$ for $r>0$, and the angle is independent and uniform. Their joint density is $re^{-r^2/2}/(2\pi)$. Changing to Cartesian coordinates divides by the <Jacobian determinant> $r$, giving
$$
f_{Z_1,Z_2}(x,y)=\frac1{2\pi}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}.
$$
This factors into two standard normal densities, proving both normality and independence. Then $\mu+\sigma Z_j$ provides the required means and variances. Implementations must avoid the zero input to the logarithm; ideal continuous uniforms hit it with probability zero.

Let $K$ denote the strike, $\tau=T-t$, and suppose $S>0$, $K>0$, $\tau>0$ and $\sigma>0$. Under the <Black-Scholes model> and the <risk-neutral measure>, $\log S_T$ is normal with
$$
m=\log S+(r-\sigma^2/2)\tau,\qquad v=\sigma\sqrt\tau.
$$
Applying <risk-neutral valuation> to the <European call option> payoff gives
$$
C=e^{-r\tau}\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}(S_T-K)^+
=e^{-r\tau}\int_{\log K}^{\infty}(e^u-K)\frac{e^{-(u-m)^2/(2v^2)}}{v\sqrt{2\pi}}\,du.
$$
Use $\xi=\Phi((u-m)/v)$, whose differential is precisely the normal density times $du$. The threshold is $\xi_K=\Phi((\log K-m)/v)$, so
$$
\boxed{C=\int_0^1g(\xi)d\xi,\qquad
 g(\xi)=e^{-r\tau}\left(e^{m+v\Phi^{-1}(\xi)}-K\right)^+.}
$$
Equivalently, rescale the tail interval to obtain $g_{\mathrm{tail}}(\eta)=e^{-r\tau}(1-\xi_K)\{\exp[m+v\Phi^{-1}(\xi_K+(1-\xi_K)\eta)]-K\}$ on $0<\eta<1$.

For independent uniform inputs, the <Monte Carlo estimator> is $\widehat C_N=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^Ng(U_j)$. It is unbiased, converges by the <strong law of large numbers>, and has <variance> $\operatorname{Var}(g(U))/N$. The payoff has finite second moment because the lognormal stock has finite moments. Its estimated standard error is the sample <standard deviation> divided by $\sqrt N$.

= Solution

Three useful methods are <antithetic variates>, a <control variate>, and <stratified sampling>. Their purpose is to reduce sampling <variance> at comparable computational cost, rather than merely to take more samples.

For <antithetic variates>, generate independent pairs $(U,1-U)$ and average both payoffs. Since the call integrand is increasing, the two values have nonpositive <covariance>. Indeed, for an independent copy $U'$, twice that covariance is the expectation of $[g(U)-g(U')][g(1-U)-g(1-U')]$, which is nonpositive pointwise. With $N=2m$ evaluations, the paired average has <variance>
$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(g(U))+\operatorname{Cov}(g(U),g(1-U))}{N}\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(g(U))}{N}.
$$
For a <control variate>, use the same simulated discounted stock value $Y=e^{-r\tau}S_T$, whose known mean is $S$. Replace the discounted call payoff $H$ by $H-\beta(Y-S)$, preserving its mean. Minimizing its quadratic variance gives
$$
\beta^*=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(H,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(H-\beta^*(Y-S))=\operatorname{Var}(H)(1-\operatorname{Corr}(H,Y)^2).
$$
A predetermined coefficient or an independent pilot estimate preserves exact unbiasedness; fitting the coefficient from the same observations requires care.

For <stratified sampling>, divide $[0,1]$ into $q$ equal intervals, sample independently and uniformly within each, and average their $q$ payoffs. If $v_j$ is the conditional <variance> in interval $j$, this unbiased estimator has <variance> $q^{-2}\sum_jv_j$. The <law of total variance> gives $\operatorname{Var}(H)\geq q^{-1}\sum_jv_j$, so this is at most the <variance> $\operatorname{Var}(H)/q$ of $q$ ordinary independent samples. Stratification removes randomness in how many samples fall in each region, which is particularly valuable when conditional payoff means vary strongly.

Take a unit-face <zero-coupon bond> with $P(T,T)=1$. Its continuously compounded <zero-coupon yield to maturity>, <instantaneous forward rate>, and <short rate> are
$$
\boxed{R(t,T)=-\frac{\log P(t,T)}{T-t},\qquad
f(t,T)=-\partial_T\log P(t,T),\qquad
r(t)=\lim_{T\downarrow t}R(t,T)=f(t,t).}
$$
The last equality assumes the appropriate maturity differentiability at the diagonal. Integration of the forward-rate definition and differentiation of the yield definition give
$$
P(t,T)=\exp\left[-\int_t^Tf(t,u)du\right]=e^{-(T-t)R(t,T)},\qquad
\boxed{R(t,T)=\frac1{T-t}\int_t^Tf(t,u)du,\quad f(t,T)=R(t,T)+(T-t)R_T(t,T).}
$$
At fixed observation time $t$, the <yield curve> plots this yield against maturity $T$; it describes today's discount prices at different horizons. Forward rates are extracted from these prices, not identified with the subsequent realized path of the <short rate>.

The common <short-rate market price of risk> follows from absence of <arbitrage>, with the securities driven by the same <Brownian motion>. Take two traded derivatives $V_1,V_2$ with drifts $m_1,m_2$ and diffusion coefficients $s_1,s_2$. At a given state hold $s_2$ units of the first and $-s_1$ of the second, and place $-s_2V_1+s_1V_2$ in the <bank account>. The portfolio has zero current value and zero diffusion exposure. Its instantaneous excess drift is
$$
s_2(m_1-rV_1)-s_1(m_2-rV_2).
$$
If this were nonzero, selecting its positive orientation and maintaining the local risk-cancelling <self-financing> hedge would generate a gain without risk from zero wealth. Under the usual trading and regularity assumptions this violates absence of <arbitrage>. Thus the expression vanishes, and at nonzero exposures
$$
\boxed{\frac{m_1-rV_1}{s_1}=\frac{m_2-rV_2}{s_2}=\theta(t,r).}
$$
At zero exposure the correct relation is $m-rV=\theta s=0$, without dividing by zero. This is a condition on traded securities: the physical drift of the nontraded <short rate> alone does not determine $\theta$.

For $V(t,r)$ sufficiently smooth, <Itô formula> under the physical measure gives
$$
dV=\left(V_t+bV_r+\frac12\sigma^2V_{rr}\right)dt+\sigma V_r\,dW.
$$
Hence $m=V_t+bV_r+\sigma^2V_{rr}/2$ and $s=\sigma V_r$. Substitute these into the common risk-price condition $m-rV=\theta s$ from part (b), obtaining the <Cox-Ross short-rate pricing equation>
$$
\boxed{V_t+(b-\sigma\theta)V_r+\frac12\sigma^2V_{rr}-rV=0.}
$$
For a terminal payoff $H(r_T)$ the final condition is $V(T,r)=H(r)$. Writing prices as functions of $(t,r)$ requires the relevant <Markov> state to be sufficient for the claim; a path-dependent payoff generally requires additional state variables. The negative sign in $b-\sigma\theta$ is the adjustment from physical to risk-neutral rate drift.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The unit <zero-coupon bond> pays one at maturity. Its <risk-neutral valuation> therefore is
$$
\boxed{P(t,T)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}\left[\exp\left(-\int_t^Tr_sds\right)\mid r_t=r\right].}
$$
No particular rate coefficients were specified, so this expectation is the general answer; evaluating it further requires a chosen <one-factor short-rate model>.

For the alternative derivation let $D_t=\exp(-\int_0^tr_sds)$ be the bank <discount factor>. For an integrable discounted payoff, pricing gives
$$
D_tV(t,r_t)=\mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[D_TX\mid\mathcal F_t],
$$
which is a <martingale> by the <tower property of conditional expectation>. For a payoff determined by the <Markov> rate state, this conditional expectation is represented by the indicated pricing function. A path-dependent claim requires an appropriately enlarged state, as in part (c). Now $dD_t=-r_tD_tdt$ has finite variation. Applying <Itô formula> and the product rule under the <risk-neutral measure>, whose rate drift is $b-\sigma\theta$, yields
$$
d(D_tV)=D_t\left[V_t+(b-\sigma\theta)V_r+\frac12\sigma^2V_{rr}-rV\right]dt+D_t\sigma V_r\,d\widetilde W_t.
$$
Under the smoothness and integrability conditions used above, the <martingale> has zero finite-variation drift. The bracket therefore vanishes, giving again
$$
\boxed{V_t+(b-\sigma\theta)V_r+\frac12\sigma^2V_{rr}-rV=0.}
$$
For the bond the terminal condition is $P(T,T)=1$. This argument also shows why stochastic discounting remains inside the conditional expectation.
