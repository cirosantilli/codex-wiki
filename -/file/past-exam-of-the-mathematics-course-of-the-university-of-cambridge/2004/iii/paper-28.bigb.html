= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $(Z_k,\mathcal F_k)_{0\leq k\leq n}$ be an <integrable> real <submartingale>, and put $Z_n^*=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}Z_k$. The refined <Doob maximal inequality> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda\,\mathbb P(Z_n^*\geq\lambda)
\leq\mathbb E[Z_n\mathbf1_{\{Z_n^*\geq\lambda\}}],\qquad\lambda>0.}
$$
In particular, for a nonnegative <submartingale> the right-hand side is at most $\mathbb EZ_n$, giving the usual weak maximal bound.

For the proof, partition $A=\{Z_n^*\geq\lambda\}$ by its first crossing time: $A_k=\{Z_j<\lambda\ (j<k),\ Z_k\geq\lambda\}\in\mathcal F_k$. The <submartingale> property gives $\mathbb E[Z_n\mid\mathcal F_k]\geq Z_k$, so
$$
\mathbb E[Z_n\mathbf1_{A_k}]
=\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{A_k}\mathbb E(Z_n\mid\mathcal F_k)]
\geq\mathbb E[Z_k\mathbf1_{A_k}]
\geq\lambda\mathbb P(A_k).
$$
Summing over the disjoint crossing events proves the refined inequality, including a possible crossing at time zero. No <independence> assumption is needed.

The strong form is the <Doob Lp maximal inequality>. For $p>1$ and a nonnegative <submartingale> with $Z_n\in L^p$,
$$
\boxed{\|Z_n^*\|_p\leq\frac p{p-1}\|Z_n\|_p.}
$$
Indeed, integrate the refined inequality against $p\lambda^{p-2}$ up to $K$. The <tail integral formula for moments> and the <Tonelli theorem> give
$$
\mathbb E[(Z_n^*\wedge K)^p]
\leq\frac p{p-1}\mathbb E[Z_n(Z_n^*\wedge K)^{p-1}].
$$
Apply the <Holder inequality>, divide by $\|Z_n^*\wedge K\|_p^{p-1}$ when it is nonzero, and let $K\to\infty$ by the <monotone convergence theorem>. For a <martingale> $M$, the process $|M_k|$ is a <submartingale> by the <conditional Jensen inequality>, giving the corresponding bound for $\max|M_k|$. On a bounded continuous-time interval the nonnegative, right-continuous version follows by applying these inequalities on refining finite time grids and taking limits.

Use $\mathcal F_m=\sigma(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_m)$ and $S_0=0$. <Independence> and zero <means> imply $\mathbb E[S_{m+1}\mid\mathcal F_m]=S_m$, so the partial sums form a <square-integrable> <martingale>. More explicitly,
$$
\mathbb E[S_{m+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_m]=S_m^2+\mathbb E\xi_{m+1}^2\geq S_m^2.
$$
Thus $S_m^2$ is a nonnegative <submartingale>. The <Doob maximal inequality> at threshold $x^2$ now proves the <Kolmogorov maximal inequality>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\max_{0\leq m\leq n}|S_m|\geq x\right)
=\mathbb P\left(\max_{m\leq n}S_m^2\geq x^2\right)
\leq\frac{\mathbb ES_n^2}{x^2}.}
$$
Finally, expanding the terminal square and using <independence> and the zero <means> gives $\mathbb ES_n^2=\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbb E\xi_j^2$. Identical distributions are not required.

Put $V=\mathbb EX_n^2$. For $c\geq0$, the process $(X_m+c)^2$ is a nonnegative <submartingale>, by the <conditional Jensen inequality>. A crossing of $x$ by $X$ implies a crossing of $(x+c)^2$ by this squared process. Since $\mathbb EX_n=\mathbb EX_0=0$, the <Doob maximal inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb P\left(\max_{m\leq n}X_m\geq x\right)
\leq\frac{\mathbb E(X_n+c)^2}{(x+c)^2}
=\frac{V+c^2}{(x+c)^2}.
$$
For $V>0$, <differentiation> of the last expression gives $2(cx-V)/(x+c)^3$, so its minimum over $c\geq0$ occurs at $c=V/x$. Substitution proves the <one-sided martingale maximal inequality>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\max_{0\leq m\leq n}X_m\geq x\right)
\leq\frac{V}{V+x^2}.}
$$
If $V=0$, then $X_n=0$ <almost surely> and $X_m=\mathbb E[X_n\mid\mathcal F_m]=0$ for every $m\leq n$, so the same conclusion holds. The constant is sharp: at one step take $X_1=x$ with probability $V/(V+x^2)$ and $X_1=-V/x$ otherwise. This has <mean> zero, <variance> $V$, and achieves equality.

Finite <second moment> implies $\mathbb EX<\infty$. For $\lambda$ near any positive $\lambda_0$, the <derivative> of $e^{-\lambda X}$ is bounded in absolute value by $X$, provided the neighbourhood stays in $(0,\infty)$. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore permits <differentiation> under the <expectation>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_X'(\lambda)=-\mathbb E[Xe^{-\lambda X}],\qquad\lambda>0.}
$$
For the expansion, put $r(z)=e^{-z}-1+z-z^2/2$. <Taylor expansion> gives $r(z)/z^2\to0$ as $z\downarrow0$. Also, for $z\geq0$,
$$
0\leq e^{-z}-1+z\leq\frac{z^2}{2},
\qquad |r(z)|\leq\frac{z^2}{2}.
$$
The inequalities follow by integrating $0\leq1-e^{-u}\leq u$ from zero to $z$. Consequently $r(\lambda X)/\lambda^2\to0$ pointwise and its absolute value is bounded by $X^2/2$. Another application of the <dominated convergence theorem> gives the <second-moment expansion of a Laplace transform>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_X(\lambda)=1-\lambda\mathbb EX
+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\mathbb EX^2+o(\lambda^2),\qquad\lambda\downarrow0.}
$$
No third moment is needed for the remainder estimate.

First the exit time has sufficiently many finite moments. Over any interval of length $a^2$, a <Brownian motion> increment has probability $q=\mathbb P(|B_{a^2}|>2a)>0$ of absolute size larger than $2a$. If the process is still inside $(-a,a)$, such an increment forces it to exit before the next interval endpoint. <Independence> of increments at deterministic times therefore gives
$$
\mathbb P(T>ka^2)\leq(1-q)^k.
$$
Thus $T<\infty$ <almost surely> and all its positive moments are finite. This justifies extracting moments from its transform rather than presupposing them.

For $\lambda>0$, put $\theta=\sqrt{2\lambda}$. The process
$$
M_t=e^{-\lambda t}\cosh(\theta B_t)
=\frac12\left(e^{\theta B_t-\theta^2t/2}+e^{-\theta B_t-\theta^2t/2}\right)
$$
is a <martingale>, being the average of two <exponential martingales for Brownian motion>. Apply the <optional stopping theorem> at the bounded time $T\wedge t$. Since $|B_{T\wedge t}|\leq a$, the stopped values are bounded by $\cosh(\theta a)$. Continuity gives $B_T\in\{-a,a\}$. Letting $t\to\infty$ by the <dominated convergence theorem> yields
$$
1=\mathbb E[e^{-\lambda T}\cosh(\theta B_T)]
=\cosh(a\sqrt{2\lambda})\,\mathbb Ee^{-\lambda T}.
$$
Hence the <Laplace transform of symmetric Brownian interval-exit time> is
$$
\boxed{\phi_T(\lambda)=\frac1{\cosh(a\sqrt{2\lambda})},\qquad\lambda\geq0.}
$$
The value at zero follows from almost-sure finiteness. Expanding the reciprocal gives
$$
\phi_T(\lambda)=1-a^2\lambda+\frac56a^4\lambda^2+O(\lambda^3).
$$
Compare with part (a), now applicable because of the geometric tail bound. The <Brownian symmetric interval-exit moments> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb ET=a^2,\qquad\mathbb ET^2=\frac53a^4,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac23a^4.}
$$

Use the natural <filtration> $\mathcal G_n=\sigma(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$. Define the deterministic tail $r_n=\sum_{j=n}^\infty\delta_j$ and put $Z_n=\xi_n+r_n$. This is nonnegative and <integrable>. Since $r_n=\delta_n+r_{n+1}$,
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal G_n]
\leq\xi_n+\delta_n+r_{n+1}=Z_n.
$$
Thus the <deterministic summable drift correction> produces a nonnegative <supermartingale>. The <almost sure supermartingale convergence theorem> gives a finite limit $Z_\infty$ <almost surely>. Its finiteness is also reflected in the bound $\mathbb EZ_n\leq\mathbb EZ_1$ and <Fatou's lemma>, which gives $\mathbb EZ_\infty\leq\mathbb EZ_1<\infty$. The convergence theorem follows from the <upcrossing> argument: bounded expected negative parts of $-Z$ rule out infinitely many crossings of rational intervals, and the <expectation> bound excludes an infinite limit.

Since $r_n\to0$, the required conclusion is $\boxed{\xi_n\longrightarrow Z_\infty\text{ almost surely, with }Z_\infty<\infty}$. The original sequence need not itself be a <supermartingale>; subtracting the vanishing deterministic tail recovers it from one that is.

Set $\inf\varnothing=\infty$. Since each $Y_j$ is $\mathcal F_j$-measurable, the event
$$
\{\tau_a\leq n\}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^nY_j>a\right\}
$$
is in $\mathcal F_n$, so $\tau_a$ is a <stopping time>. The <stopped predictable budget for adapted increments> is $A_n=a-U_{n\wedge\tau_a}$, for $n\geq1$.

If $\tau_a=k<\infty$, the sum before the overshooting increment is $U_k=\sum_{j<k}Y_j\leq a$. If $\tau_a>n$, the sum through time $n$ is at most $a$, and in particular $U_n\leq a$. Thus
$$
\boxed{0\leq A_n\leq a.}
$$
The process is adapted, and boundedness gives integrability even when the increments have no finite <expectations>. Directly from the one-step-lagged sum,
$$
U_{(n+1)\wedge\tau_a}-U_{n\wedge\tau_a}
=Y_n\mathbf1_{\{\tau_a>n\}}.
$$
This decrement is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, and $0\leq Y_n\mathbf1_{\{\tau_a>n\}}\leq a$. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[A_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]
=A_n-Y_n\mathbf1_{\{\tau_a>n\}}\leq A_n.
$$
Hence $A$ is a nonnegative <supermartingale>; in fact its paths are nonincreasing. At time zero one may set $U_0=0$ and $A_0=a$; then $A_1=a$ too. Stopping the sum including $Y_{\tau_a}$ would lose nonnegativity, which is why the shifted definition of $U$ matters.

Work from time one; a finite initial prefix does not affect convergence. Fix a positive integer $a$, and use the <stopping time> $\tau_a$ and budget $A_n$ from part (b). Define
$$
Z_n^{(a)}=X_{n\wedge\tau_a}+a-U_{n\wedge\tau_a}.
$$
It is nonnegative. For each finite $n$, the stopped variable $X_{n\wedge\tau_a}$ is <integrable>: it is bounded by the finite sum $X_1+\cdots+X_n$, and the budget is bounded by $a$.

The stopped increment of $X$ is
$$
X_{(n+1)\wedge\tau_a}-X_{n\wedge\tau_a}
=\mathbf1_{\{\tau_a>n\}}(X_{n+1}-X_n).
$$
Taking <conditional expectations>, the assumed drift bound gives
$$
\mathbb E[X_{(n+1)\wedge\tau_a}\mid\mathcal F_n]
\leq X_{n\wedge\tau_a}+Y_n\mathbf1_{\{\tau_a>n\}}.
$$
The last term is bounded by $a$; no integrability assumption on the unlocalized $Y_n$ has been used. Its contribution is exactly cancelled by the budget decrement from part (b), giving
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}^{(a)}\mid\mathcal F_n]\leq Z_n^{(a)}.
$$
Thus $Z^{(a)}$ is an <integrable> nonnegative <supermartingale>. By the <almost sure supermartingale convergence theorem> it has a finite almost-sure limit. The bounded decreasing budget $A_n$ has a finite pathwise limit as well, so $X_{n\wedge\tau_a}=Z_n^{(a)}-A_n$ converges to a finite limit <almost surely>.

Take the intersection of these probability-one convergence events over all positive integers $a$. On the further probability-one event $\sum_{j\geq1}Y_j<\infty$, choose an integer $a$ strictly larger than that total. Then $\tau_a=\infty$, so the stopped sequence is the original one. Consequently
$$
\boxed{X_n\text{ converges almost surely to a finite limit}.}
$$
This proves <supermartingale convergence with summable adapted drift> under almost-sure summability alone; it does not require $\mathbb E\sum_jY_j<\infty$.

Here is the one-parameter <Kolmogorov continuity theorem>. Suppose a real-valued <stochastic process> on $[0,1]$ satisfies, for some $p>0$, $q>0$ and finite $C$,
$$
\mathbb E|X_t-X_s|^p\leq C|t-s|^{1+q},\qquad s,t\in[0,1].
$$
Then it has a <continuous> <modification of a stochastic process> whose paths are <Hölder continuous> of every positive exponent $\gamma<q/p$. A modification <means> equality <almost surely> at each fixed time, not an a priori simultaneous equality at all times. Other compact time intervals follow by rescaling; the vector-valued version uses a <norm> in place of absolute value.

For the proof fix $0<\gamma<q/p$ and use the dyadic grids $D_n=\{j2^{-n}:0\leq j\leq2^n\}$. Let $D_n^*$ be the largest adjacent increment on this grid. The <union bound> and <Markov inequality> give
$$
\mathbb P(D_n^*>2^{-n\gamma})
\leq\sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}2^{n\gamma p}
\mathbb E|X_{(j+1)2^{-n}}-X_{j2^{-n}}|^p
\leq C2^{-n(q-p\gamma)}.
$$
The right-hand side is summable. The <Borel-Cantelli first lemma> shows that, outside a <null set>, an almost-surely finite random constant $K$ can absorb the finitely many early grids so that
$$
D_n^*\leq K2^{-n\gamma}\quad\text{for every }n.
$$

We now use <dyadic increment chaining>. For two distinct dyadic points $s,t$, choose $n$ with $2^{-(n+1)}<|t-s|\leq2^{-n}$. Approximate them from below by $s_j,t_j\in D_j$, for $j\geq n$. Each refinement moves by zero or one grid step, while $s_n,t_n$ are at most two grid steps apart. The sequences eventually equal the original dyadic points. Telescoping gives
$$
|X_t-X_s|
\leq 2K2^{-n\gamma}+2K\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}2^{-j\gamma}
\leq C_\gamma K|t-s|^\gamma,
$$
where a deterministic finite $C_\gamma$ absorbs the <geometric series> and $2^{-n}<2|t-s|$. Thus the sample function on the <dense> union of dyadic grids has a unique Hölder-continuous extension to $[0,1]$. Define this extension to be $\widetilde X$ on the probability-one event, and set it to zero on the exceptional <null set>.

For a fixed $t$, take dyadic $t_j\to t$. The moment condition gives $X_{t_j}\to X_t$ in probability, while the constructed extension gives $X_{t_j}\to\widetilde X_t$ <almost surely>. Uniqueness of a probability limit yields $\widetilde X_t=X_t$ <almost surely>. This proves the required modification property. Performing the argument for a <countable> increasing sequence of exponents tending to $q/p$ gives one version with all smaller positive <Hölder exponents>, since its extensions agree on the dyadic points.

For <Brownian motion>, <Gaussian> increments give, for any integer $r\geq2$,
$$
\mathbb E|B_t-B_s|^{2r}=(2r-1)!!\,|t-s|^r.
$$
Use $p=2r$ and $q=r-1$. The theorem gives every exponent $\alpha<(r-1)/(2r)=1/2-1/(2r)$. For any $0<\alpha<1/2$, choose $r$ large enough. Intersect probability-one events over integer $r$ and compact intervals $[0,N]$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\text{Brownian paths are locally Hölder continuous of every exponent }0<\alpha<\tfrac12\text{ almost surely}.}
$$
The usual <continuous> Brownian version has the same property: its <continuous> paths agree with the constructed version on a <countable> <dense> set and hence everywhere, outside one <null set>. The Hölder constant depends on the compact interval. This is the precise local meaning of <Brownian Hölder regularity>; it is not a uniform Hölder bound on the entire unbounded time axis.

The force from one location is defined <almost surely>, since the <uniform distribution> assigns probability zero to the origin. Symmetry of that <uniform distribution> makes its <characteristic function> real:
$$
q_n(t)=\mathbb E\exp\!\left(\frac{itm\operatorname{sign}(X_1)}{X_1^2}\right)
=\frac1n\int_0^n\cos\!\left(\frac{mt}{x^2}\right)\,dx
=1-\frac{I_n(t)}n,
\qquad I_n(t)=\int_0^n\left(1-\cos\!\left(\frac{mt}{x^2}\right)\right)\,dx.
$$
Near zero the integrand is bounded by $2$. For large $x$ it is at most $m^2t^2/(2x^4)$. The <monotone convergence theorem> therefore gives the finite <integral>
$$
I_n(t)\longrightarrow I(t)=\int_0^\infty\left(1-\cos\!\left(\frac{mt}{x^2}\right)\right)\,dx.
$$
<Independence> of the locations gives the <characteristic function> of the total force as $q_n(t)^n$. For fixed $t$, $I_n(t)$ is bounded, so <Taylor expansion> of the <logarithm> gives
$$
n\log\left(1-\frac{I_n(t)}n\right)=-I_n(t)+O(n^{-1})\longrightarrow-I(t).
$$
The <logarithm> is legitimate for all sufficiently large $n$.

For $t\ne0$, substitute $u=m|t|/x^2$. The <cosine integral for a symmetric stable exponent>, evaluated directly in part (b), gives
$$
I(t)=\frac{\sqrt{m|t|}}2\int_0^\infty\frac{1-\cos u}{u^{3/2}}\,du
=\sqrt{\frac{\pi m}{2}}\,|t|^{1/2}.
$$
At $t=0$ both sides vanish. The limiting <characteristic function> is <continuous> at zero, so the <Lévy continuity theorem> proves <weak convergence of random variables> to the <symmetric stable distribution> of index $1/2$ with
$$
\boxed{\phi(t)=\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{\pi m}{2}}\,|t|^{1/2}\right).}
$$
The coefficient reflects the spatial density $n/(2n)=1/2$. Symmetry was used in the <characteristic function>, without assuming that the singular force has a finite <mean>.

Interpret the signed inverse-power force as $m\operatorname{sign}(x)/|x|^p$, so noninteger $p$ is meaningful on either side of the origin. Write $\alpha=1/p\in(0,2)$. Exactly as in part (a), the one-star <characteristic function> is $1-I_{n,p}(t)/n$, where
$$
I_{n,p}(t)=\int_0^n\left(1-\cos\!\left(\frac{mt}{x^p}\right)\right)\,dx.
$$
The integrand is bounded near zero and is at most $m^2t^2/(2x^{2p})$ at infinity. The condition $2p>1$ thus makes its <integral> finite. <Independence> and the <logarithm> calculation in part (a) give the limiting <characteristic function> $\exp(-I_p(t))$, with
$$
I_p(t)=\alpha m^\alpha|t|^\alpha J_\alpha,
\qquad J_\alpha=\int_0^\infty(1-\cos u)u^{-1-\alpha}\,du.
$$
Here we substituted $u=m|t|/x^p$. The <integral> for $J_\alpha$ is finite because its integrand is $O(u^{1-\alpha})$ at zero and $O(u^{-1-\alpha})$ at infinity.

To determine the constant rather than leave it as an unevaluated <integral>, use the <Gamma function> representation
$$
u^{-1-\alpha}=\frac1{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\int_0^\infty v^\alpha e^{-vu}\,dv.
$$
The integrand multiplied by $1-\cos u$ is nonnegative, so the <Tonelli theorem> permits exchanging the <integrals>. For $v>0$ the inner <integral> is
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-vu}(1-\cos u)\,du
=\frac1v-\frac{v}{1+v^2}=\frac1{v(1+v^2)}.
$$
Consequently, substituting $z=v^2$ and using the <Euler beta function> and <Gamma reflection formula>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
J_\alpha
&=\frac1{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}\int_0^\infty\frac{v^{\alpha-1}}{1+v^2}\,dv\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha/2)\Gamma(1-\alpha/2)}{2\Gamma(1+\alpha)}
=\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma(1+\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $\Gamma(1+\alpha)=\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)$, this gives
$$
\boxed{c_p=\frac{\pi m^{1/p}}{2\Gamma(1/p)\sin(\pi/(2p))},\qquad
\phi(t)=e^{-c_p|t|^{1/p}}.}
$$
Every factor in $c_p$ is positive in the stated range. The <Lévy continuity theorem> gives <weak convergence of random variables> because this <characteristic function> is <continuous> at zero. The limit is strictly stable: if $Z_1,Z_2$ are <independent> copies of it, the <characteristic function> of $aZ_1+bZ_2$ is
$$
\exp\{-c_p(|a|^\alpha+|b|^\alpha)|t|^\alpha\},
$$
which is the <characteristic function> of $(|a|^\alpha+|b|^\alpha)^{1/\alpha}Z_1$. This proves the asserted index, as well as identifying the <symmetric stable distribution>. In particular $p=2$ gives $c_2=\sqrt{\pi m/2}$, while $p=1$ gives $c_1=\pi m/2$, the centered <Cauchy distribution> with that scale.

Take a <random variable> $Z$ with the <Rademacher distribution>, and set
$$
X_n=Z,\qquad Y_n=\begin{cases}Z,&n\text{ even},\\-Z,&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
$$
Both <marginal distributions> equal the law of $Z$ for every $n$. Hence $X_n$ and $Y_n$ each have <weak convergence of random variables> to $Z$; one may take $X=Y=Z$ on this <probability space>. But their sums alternate:
$$
X_n+Y_n=\begin{cases}2Z,&n\text{ even},\\0,&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}
$$
Indeed, at $t=\pi/2$ their <characteristic functions> are $-1$ for even $n$ and $1$ for odd $n$, so the sums have no weak limit at all. **Separate marginal weak convergence does not imply convergence of the sum.** The <marginal weak convergence does not control sums> counterexample changes the dependence while leaving the marginals fixed. Unlike <convergence in probability>, separate marginal <weak convergence of random variables> contains no information about the joint coupling.

Write the constant limit as $c$. First, <convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability>: the <Portmanteau theorem> applied to the closed set $\{y:|y-c|\geq\varepsilon\}$ gives
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(|Y_n-c|\geq\varepsilon)\leq0.
$$
Now compare <characteristic functions> without imposing <independence>. For fixed $t\in\mathbb R$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\mathbb Ee^{it(X_n+Y_n)}-e^{itc}\mathbb Ee^{itX_n}\right|
&\leq\mathbb E|e^{itY_n}-e^{itc}|\\
&\leq |t|\varepsilon+2\mathbb P(|Y_n-c|>\varepsilon).
\end{aligned}
$$
The second bound uses $|e^{iu}-e^{iv}|\leq\min\{2,|u-v|\}$. By <convergence in probability>, the limiting upper bound is $|t|\varepsilon$; then let $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Meanwhile $X_n$ has <weak convergence of random variables> to $X$, so $\mathbb Ee^{itX_n}\to\mathbb Ee^{itX}$. Thus the <characteristic function> of the sum tends to $e^{itc}\mathbb Ee^{itX}$, the <characteristic function> of $X+c$, which is <continuous> at zero. The <Lévy continuity theorem> proves
$$
\boxed{X_n+Y_n\ \Longrightarrow\ X+c.}
