= Solution
{parent=1}

Choose depth $z$ positive downwards and harmonic time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$. At normal incidence, <P-waves> and the two polarizations of <S-waves> are independent scalar problems: a <P-wave> uses the longitudinal modulus $\rho\alpha^2$, and an <S-wave> uses the <shear modulus> $\rho\beta^2$. In a uniform layer with <wave speed> $c$ and <seismic impedance> $q=\rho c$, write <velocity> and compressive <traction> as
$$
V=Ae^{i\omega z/c}+Be^{-i\omega z/c},\qquad
P=-T=q\left(Ae^{i\omega z/c}-Be^{-i\omega z/c}\right).
$$
At a perfectly bonded interface the <velocity> and <traction> are continuous. For incident <velocity> <wave amplitude> one from medium 1 into medium 2, these two conditions are $1+r_v=t_v$ and $q_1(1-r_v)=q_2t_v$. Thus
$$
\boxed{r_v=\frac{q_1-q_2}{q_1+q_2},\qquad t_v=\frac{2q_1}{q_1+q_2}.}
$$
Reflection is determined by <seismic impedance>, not by speed alone. A step to larger <seismic impedance> reverses the reflected <velocity>/<displacement field> waveform; the pressure <reflection coefficient> has the opposite sign. Its transmitted pressure coefficient is $t_p=2q_2/(q_1+q_2)$. Equal <seismic impedance> eliminates the reflection even when speed and <wavelength> change. At a free surface <traction> vanishes and <velocity> reflects with coefficient $+1$; at a rigid surface <velocity> reflects with coefficient $-1$.

The mean <elastic-wave energy flux> is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(PV^*)=q(|A|^2-|B|^2)/2$ for positive real $q$. Therefore the reflected and transmitted <energy> fractions are
$$
\mathcal R=|r_v|^2,\qquad \mathcal T=\frac{q_2}{q_1}|t_v|^2,\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1.}
$$
A <displacement field> <transmission coefficient> greater than one is compatible with <conservation of energy>; its flux must be weighted by the receiving <seismic impedance>.

For many layers, use the <seismic layer transfer matrix>. A layer of thickness $h_j$ has <wave phase> thickness $\delta_j=\omega h_j/c_j$ and
$$
\begin{pmatrix}V\\P\end{pmatrix}_{z+h_j}
=M_j\begin{pmatrix}V\\P\end{pmatrix}_{z},\qquad
M_j=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta_j&i\sin\delta_j/q_j\\iq_j\sin\delta_j&\cos\delta_j\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det M_j=1.
$$
This follows either by eliminating $A,B$ between the two faces or by integrating $V'=i\omega P/(\rho c^2)$ and $P'=i\omega\rho V$. For the complete stack put $M=M_N\cdots M_1=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}$. With upper and lower <half-space> impedances $q_a,q_b$, an outgoing lower wave satisfies $P_b=q_bV_b$. Solving this condition gives the input <seismic impedance>
$$
Z_{\rm in}=\frac{q_bA-C}{D-q_bB},\qquad
\boxed{R=\frac{q_a-Z_{\rm in}}{q_a+Z_{\rm in}},\quad
T=\frac{2q_a}{q_aD+q_bA-C-q_aq_bB}.}
$$
Here $T$ is lower-face <velocity> divided by upper-face incident <velocity>, including the propagation <wave phase>. For lossless layers and real <frequency>, $|R|^2+(q_b/q_a)|T|^2=1$. This supplies a sensitive check on any spreadsheet or numerical implementation; it is not correct simply to multiply the individual interface transmissions and discard the <elastic waves>.

The physical reason is repeated <wave interference>. In one layer between media 0 and 2, let $r_{ij},t_{ij}$ denote the <velocity> coefficients above and $\delta=\omega h/c_1$. The first transmitted wave has <wave amplitude> $t_{01}t_{12}e^{i\delta}$. Every extra round trip supplies $r_{10}r_{12}e^{2i\delta}$. Summing the <geometric series> gives
$$
T=\frac{t_{01}t_{12}e^{i\delta}}{1-r_{10}r_{12}e^{2i\delta}},\qquad
R=r_{01}+\frac{t_{01}t_{10}r_{12}e^{2i\delta}}{1-r_{10}r_{12}e^{2i\delta}}.
$$
In the time domain the successive arrivals are delayed by $2h/c_1$, producing reverberation. In the <frequency> domain the same delayed terms produce resonant maxima and minima. For a layer between identical <half-spaces> of impedance $q_0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T=\left[1+\frac14\left(\frac{q_1}{q_0}-\frac{q_0}{q_1}\right)^2\sin^2\delta\right]^{-1}.}
$$
At $\delta=n\pi$ the layer transmits all the incident <energy>: the internally <elastic waves> cancel the direct reflection. Halfway between these resonances, a large impedance contrast gives strong reflection. For unequal exterior impedances, a quarter-wave layer with $q_1^2=q_aq_b$ transforms its lower load to $Z_{\rm in}=q_1^2/q_b=q_a$, again giving zero reflection at its design <frequency>. This matching is narrow-band.

Periodic alternation produces a <periodic seismic multilayer stop band>. For a cell of two layers with total thickness $d$, the cell <matrix> has reciprocal <eigenvalues> $e^{\pm iKd}$, so
$$
\cos Kd=\frac12\operatorname{tr}(M_2M_1)
=\cos\delta_1\cos\delta_2-\frac12\left(\frac{q_1}{q_2}+\frac{q_2}{q_1}\right)\sin\delta_1\sin\delta_2.
$$
When the absolute value of this expression exceeds one, the infinite periodic medium has no propagating cell mode; a long finite stack has exponentially small transmission. The <energy> is reflected, not absorbed. Pass bands instead contain oscillatory transmission peaks, becoming more numerous as the stack length increases. Disorder removes the exact cell periodicity but not multiple scattering or frequency-selective interference.

For a concrete reproducible investigation, take $q_H/q_L=4$, equal one-way travel times $\tau$ in both layer types, exterior impedance $q_L$, and <frequency> coordinate $\nu=2\omega\tau/\pi$. At $\nu=1$ each layer is a quarter <wavelength>; one cell has diagonal <matrix> entries $-4,-1/4$. For $N$ cells,
$$
\mathcal T(1)=\frac4{(4^N+4^{-N})^2}.
$$
It is about $0.22145$ for one cell, $6.10\times10^{-5}$ for four cells and $9.31\times10^{-10}$ for eight cells. The infinite-cell stop band is $2\arcsin(0.8)/\pi<\nu<2-2\arcsin(0.8)/\pi$. The accompanying calculation also shows the first transmitted arrivals for a single high-impedance slab: their <wave amplitudes> are $0.64(0.36)^n$ at times $(2n+1)\tau$. Their sum is one, consistent with unit zero-frequency transmission. These calculations illustrate both the spectrum and the pulse train without assuming access to the course's spreadsheet.

\Image[paper-79-layer-transmission.png]{title=Original transfer-matrix calculations showing a periodic stack stop band and the transmitted reverberation train of one slab}

Finally, as $\omega\to0$ for a fixed finite stack, every $M_j\to I$, so the response approaches that of a direct interface between the exterior <half-spaces>. Equal exterior impedances give $R\to0,T\to1$. For <wavelengths> much longer than a repeating cell, expansion of $M_j$ to first order instead gives an effective medium with averaged <mass density> $\rho_{\rm eff}=\sum h_j\rho_j/d$ and harmonic-mean modulus $M_{\rm eff}^{-1}=\sum h_j/M_j^{\rm mod}/d$, where $M_j^{\rm mod}=\rho_jc_j^2$. Its speed is $\sqrt{M_{\rm eff}/\rho_{\rm eff}}$, not generally the arithmetic mean of the layer speeds. Attenuation makes the <energy> balance an inequality, while for a fixed oblique horizontal slowness the scalar SH calculation uses $q_j=\mu_jp_{\beta,j}$ and $\delta_j=\omega p_{\beta,j}h_j$. Oblique P-SV waves require coupled <displacement field> and <traction> <matrices> because of <mode conversion at a planar elastic interface>. Thick evanescent layers require scattering or impedance recursion rather than exponentially ill-conditioned transfer-matrix products.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Work in a meridional $R,z$ plane and denote the interface height by $Z(R)$, to distinguish it from an observation coordinate. Put
$$
s=z_0(\alpha-\alpha_f),\qquad d=\sqrt{\alpha^2-\alpha_f^2},\qquad
D(R)=\sqrt{s^2+d^2R^2}.
$$
The interface is $Z=(\alpha s+\alpha_fD)/d^2$, so $Z'=\alpha_fR/D$. Its unit normal into the fluid is proportional to $(-Z',1)$. Consequently the angle between this normal and the vertical satisfies
$$
\boxed{\tan\theta_z=Z'=\frac{\alpha_fR}{D}.}
$$
The outgoing source <P-wave> has ray direction proportional to $(R,Z)$, tilting outwards by $\arctan(R/Z)$ from the vertical, while the normal tilts inwards. Thus $\theta_P=\arctan(R/Z)+\theta_z$. The tangent addition formula gives
$$
\tan\theta_P=\frac{R+ZZ'}{Z-RZ'}.
$$
Direct substitution simplifies its denominator to $s(\alpha D+\alpha_fs)/(d^2D)$ and its numerator to $\alpha R(\alpha D+\alpha_fs)/(d^2D)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tan\theta_P=\frac{\alpha R}{s}=\frac{\alpha R}{z_0(\alpha-\alpha_f)}.}
$$
The denominator is positive, so these are the physical incidence angles in $[0,\pi/2)$; the transmitted <P-wave> reaches the fluid rather than propagating back into the solid.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The angles from part (i) have sines
$$
\sin\theta_P=\frac{\alpha R}{\sqrt{s^2+\alpha^2R^2}},\qquad
\sin\theta_z=\frac{\alpha_fR}{\sqrt{s^2+\alpha^2R^2}}.
$$
By <Snell law for elastic and acoustic waves>, $\sin\theta_f/\alpha_f=\sin\theta_P/\alpha$. Both angles lie in $[0,\pi/2)$, so **$\theta_f=\theta_z$**. The outgoing fluid ray tilts away from the inward-tilted normal by exactly its tilt from vertical; it therefore travels vertically upwards. This is <hyperbolic interface collimation of P-waves>.

The source-to-interface distance satisfies
$$
r(R)=\sqrt{R^2+Z(R)^2}=\frac{\alpha D+\alpha_fs}{d^2}.
$$
One can verify this by squaring, using $D^2-s^2=d^2R^2$. Consequently
$$
\frac r\alpha-\frac Z{\alpha_f}=-\frac{s}{\alpha\alpha_f}
=\frac{z_0}{\alpha}-\frac{z_0}{\alpha_f}.
$$
Adding the vertical fluid travel time from the interface to height $z$ gives
$$
\boxed{\tau(R,z)=\frac{r(R)}\alpha+\frac{z-Z(R)}{\alpha_f}
=\frac{z_0}\alpha+\frac{z-z_0}{\alpha_f}.}
$$
Thus surfaces of equal arrival time in the fluid are horizontal planes. Their leading <wave amplitude> may vary with $R$: a planar <wavefront> does not imply a spatially uniform plane-wave solution at every order.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Use local coordinates with the interface $z=0$, solid below and fluid above, and choose the <P-SV displacement potentials> convention $u_x=\phi_x-\psi_z$, $u_z=\phi_z+\psi_x$. Let the reflected P and SV potential <wave amplitudes> be $A,B$, while the transmitted P <wave amplitude> is $T_P$. All four waves have the same tangential slowness
$$
p=\frac{\sin\theta_P}{\alpha}=\frac{\sin\theta_S}{\beta}=\frac{\sin\theta_f}{\alpha_f},\qquad
q_P=\frac{\cos\theta_P}{\alpha},\quad q_S=\frac{\cos\theta_S}{\beta},\quad q_f=\frac{\cos\theta_f}{\alpha_f}.
$$
The incident and transmitted retarded times have negative $q_Pz,q_fz$, while <elastic waves> have positive $q_Pz,q_Sz$. At the interface all waveform <derivatives> therefore have the same argument. <displacement field> continuity gives
$$
q_P(1-A)+pB=q_fT_P. \qquad(1)
$$
With <Lamé parameters> $\mu=\rho\beta^2$ and $\lambda=\rho(\alpha^2-2\beta^2)$, the shear and normal <stresses> follow directly from differentiation:
$$
\sigma_{xz}=\mu(2\phi_{xz}+\psi_{xx}-\psi_{zz}),\qquad
\sigma_{zz}=\lambda\Delta\phi+2\mu(\phi_{zz}+\psi_{xz}).
$$
The fluid has no shear <traction>, so the coefficient of $f''$ in $\sigma_{xz}$ must vanish:
$$
\frac{\sin2\theta_P}{\alpha^2}(1-A)-\frac{\cos2\theta_S}{\beta^2}B=0. \qquad(2)
$$
The coefficient of $f''$ in the solid <stress> is $\rho[\cos2\theta_S(1+A)-\sin2\theta_SB]$; in the fluid it is $\rho_fT_P$. Thus
$$
\rho[\cos2\theta_S(1+A)-\sin2\theta_SB]=\rho_fT_P. \qquad(3)
$$
These are the three required matching conditions; tangential <displacement field> need not be continuous at a solid–inviscid-fluid boundary.

Put $C=\cos2\theta_S$ and $X=1-A$. From (2), $B=(\beta^2/\alpha^2)(\sin2\theta_P/C)X$. In (1), the coefficient of $X$ simplifies because $C+2(\beta^2/\alpha^2)\sin^2\theta_P=1$. Hence $X=(\alpha C\cos\theta_f)/(\alpha_f\cos\theta_P)T_P$. Substitute this into (3) to obtain
$$
2\rho C=\left[\rho_f+\frac{\rho\alpha\cos\theta_f}{\alpha_f\cos\theta_P}
\left(C^2+\frac{\beta^2}{\alpha^2}\sin2\theta_P\sin2\theta_S\right)\right]T_P.
$$
Therefore the <solid-fluid P-wave transmission coefficient> is
$$
\boxed{T_P=\frac{2\rho\alpha_f\cos2\theta_S\cos\theta_P}
{\rho_f\alpha_f\cos\theta_P+\rho\alpha\left[\cos^22\theta_S+(\beta^2/\alpha^2)\sin2\theta_P\sin2\theta_S\right]\cos\theta_f}.}
$$
Division by $C$ was only an elimination device; the final expression extends continuously to $C=0$. The normal-incidence value is $2\rho\alpha_f/(\rho\alpha+\rho_f\alpha_f)$. This is a potential coefficient; longitudinal <displacement field> transmission additionally multiplies it by $\alpha/\alpha_f$. The original PDF's bracket lies in the denominator and contains $\beta^2/\alpha^2$ and $\cos\theta_f$. The converted TeX misplaces the bracket, substitutes $\theta^2$ for $\beta^2$, and loses the final cosine.

= Solution

The leading source <displacement field> is
$$
\mathbf u_{\rm inc}=-\frac{\widehat{\mathbf r}}{\alpha r}f'(t-r/\alpha)
-\frac{\widehat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}f(t-r/\alpha).
$$
The second term is one <derivative> smoother at the leading arrival. At each surface point, use its tangent plane, the angles from (i)–(ii), and the local <solid-fluid P-wave transmission coefficient>. The incident potential coefficient is $1/r(R)$, so the transmitted leading potential coefficient is
$$
A_\phi(R)=\frac{T_P(R)}{r(R)}.
$$
Transport this coefficient along the vertical fluid rays. Their transverse area does not change with height, so <ray-tube conservation for a P-wave jump> gives no further geometrical spreading. The leading <displacement field> coefficient is consequently
$$
\boxed{u_z^{\rm lead}(R,z,t)=-A_u(R)f'(t-\tau),\qquad
A_u(R)=\frac{T_P(R)}{\alpha_fr(R)}.}
$$
This is the <transmitted jump at a collimating solid-fluid interface>. Radial <derivatives> of $A_\phi$ multiply $f$, and therefore do not contribute to the first <derivative> jump carried by $f'$. A varying transverse <wave amplitude> does generate smoother later terms, so this construction is a leading-front result, not a complete global plane-wave field.

Since the source is zero before $t=0$ and is $C^2$ there, $f(0)=f'(0)=f''(0)=0$. Thus <displacement field> itself does not jump. If $J=[f^{(3)}(0)]$ exists and is the first nonzero jump, the leading discontinuity is the acceleration jump $[\partial_t^2\mathbf u]=-A_u(R)J\mathbf e_z$. More generally, a first jump in $f^{(m)}$ gives $[\partial_t^{m-1}\mathbf u]=-A_u(R)[f^{(m)}]\mathbf e_z$. The supplied regularity alone does not guarantee a nonzero discontinuity: a source smooth to all orders may have none. The geometrical transfer factor $A_u$ is nevertheless determined.

At $R=0$, $r=z_0$ and all three angles vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{A_\phi(0)=\frac{2\rho\alpha_f}{z_0(\rho\alpha+\rho_f\alpha_f)},\qquad
A_u(0)=\frac{2\rho}{z_0(\rho\alpha+\rho_f\alpha_f)}.}
$$
For large $R$, put $C_\infty=1-2\beta^2/\alpha^2$ and retain $s=z_0(\alpha-\alpha_f)$, $d=\sqrt{\alpha^2-\alpha_f^2}$. The relevant limits are
$$
r=\frac{\alpha R}{d}+O(1),\quad
\cos\theta_P=\frac{s}{\alpha R}+O(R^{-3}),\quad
\cos\theta_f=\frac d\alpha+O(R^{-2}),\quad
\cos2\theta_S=C_\infty+O(R^{-2}).
$$
When $C_\infty\ne0$, the square-bracket factor in the denominator of $T_P$ is $C_\infty^2+O(R^{-1})$, so
$$
T_P=\frac{2\alpha_fs}{\alpha d C_\infty R}+O(R^{-2}),\qquad
\boxed{A_\phi(R)=\frac{2\alpha_fs}{\alpha^2C_\infty R^2}+O(R^{-3}),\quad
A_u(R)=\frac{2s}{\alpha^2C_\infty R^2}+O(R^{-3}).}
$$
These are signed waveform coefficients; their absolute values give <wave amplitude> magnitudes. The leading <displacement field> decays as $R^{-2}$ rather than $R^{-1}$ because grazing incidence adds a small transmission factor to spherical spreading. The leading coefficient is independent of fluid and solid <mass densities>, although the exact <wave amplitude> and its higher-order terms depend on them.

The assumptions do not exclude $\beta^2=\alpha^2/2$, where $C_\infty=0$ and the preceding division is invalid. In this special case $\cos2\theta_S=\cos^2\theta_P$ exactly, and the square-bracket factor is $\cos\theta_P+O(\cos^3\theta_P)$. Keeping both denominator terms gives
$$
\boxed{A_u(R)=\frac{2\rho s^2d}{\alpha^3(\rho_f\alpha_f+\rho d)R^3}+O(R^{-4}),\qquad A_\phi=\alpha_fA_u.}
$$
It has no $R^{-2}$ term. This exceptional case matters when requesting an asymptotic formula without an additional restriction on the elastic moduli.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write $\omega=u+iv$, with $v>0$, and a chosen square root $k_\beta=a+ib$. Squaring gives
$$
a^2-b^2=\frac{u^2-v^2}{\beta^2}-k^2,\qquad ab=\frac{uv}{\beta^2}.
$$
One cannot have $b=0$: the second relation would force $u=0$, and then the first would require the square of a real number to be negative. Consequently
$$
\operatorname{Re}p_\beta
=\operatorname{Re}\frac{a+ib}{u+iv}
=\frac{au+bv}{|\omega|^2}
=\frac{v}{|\omega|^2}\left(b+\frac{u^2}{\beta^2b}\right).
$$
The bracket has the sign of $b$, proving the <causal vertical wavenumber branch> equivalence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}k_\beta>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Re}p_\beta>0.}
$$
This selects the square root analytically in the upper <frequency> half-plane. At $\omega=iv$ it is $k_\beta=i\sqrt{v^2/\beta^2+k^2}$; continuation supplies the outgoing branch for other <frequencies>. The expression $\sqrt{\beta^{-2}-p^2}$ must use this inherited branch rather than an independently chosen square root. For positive real <frequency> its limit is positive real for a propagating wave and positive imaginary for an evanescent wave.

Let the <shear-horizontal wave> <displacement field> be $U(z)e^{ikx-i\omega t}$. Its <velocity> is $V=-i\omega U$, and its <traction> is $T=\mu U'$. The scalar shear-wave equation gives
$$
(\mu U')'+(\rho\omega^2-\mu k^2)U=0.
$$
With $p=k/\omega$ and $p_\beta^2=\beta^{-2}-p^2$, the first-order <velocity>–<traction> system is
$$
V'=-\frac{i\omega}{\mu}T,\qquad T'=-i\omega\mu p_\beta^2V.
$$
These equations remain valid for depth-dependent $\mu,\rho$; differentiating $\mu U'$ is essential and one must not incorrectly use $\mu U''$ when $\mu$ varies.

Where $V\ne0$, define the <SH input impedance> $Z=-T/V$. Differentiating $T=-ZV$ and substituting the two equations gives the <SH impedance Riccati equation>:
$$
\boxed{Z'=-i\omega\left(\frac{Z^2}{\mu}-\mu p_\beta^2\right),\qquad
V'=i\omega\frac Z\mu V.}
$$
The second relation is a one-way scalar equation once the complete <SH input impedance> is known. It does not discard reflections: $Z$ describes the actual superposition of both wave directions. At a zero of $V$, $Z$ may have a pole while the <velocity>–<traction> system stays regular.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Use the quadratic spectral flux $S_z=-\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(V^*T)$. For positive real material <mass density> and <shear modulus>, differentiation of the <velocity>–<traction> system gives
$$
S_z'=-\frac12\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{i\omega^*}{\mu}|T|^2-i\omega\mu p_\beta^2|V|^2\right].
$$
Because $k$ is real, $\mu p_\beta^2=\rho-\mu k^2/\omega^2$. If $v=\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$, then $\operatorname{Re}(i\omega^*)=v$ and $\operatorname{Re}(-i\omega\mu p_\beta^2)=v(\rho+\mu k^2/|\omega|^2)$. This proves <complex-frequency SH flux monotonicity>:
$$
\boxed{S_z'=-\frac v2\left[\frac{|T|^2}{\mu}
+\left(\rho+\frac{\mu k^2}{|\omega|^2}\right)|V|^2\right]<0.}
$$
Strictness holds for a nontrivial wavefield: if $V,T$ vanished together at one point, uniqueness of the first-order system would make the entire solution zero. The zero solution is the necessary exception to the literal strict inequality. Across a bonded material boundary $V,T$, and hence $S_z$, are continuous; integrating the negative <derivative> gives strict decrease across any positive-depth interval as well.

Where the <SH input impedance> is finite, $S_z=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}Z|V|^2$. If $\operatorname{Re}Z(z_1)>0$, then $S_z(z_1)>0$ and all shallower fluxes are larger and positive. They cannot have $V=0$, since that would make $S_z=0$. Thus **$\operatorname{Re}Z(z)>0$ for every $z<z_1$**. Likewise, negative real <SH input impedance> at $z_0$ gives negative flux throughout $z>z_0$, excludes <velocity> zeros there and implies **$\operatorname{Re}Z(z)<0$ for every $z>z_0$**.

In a uniform region let $q=\mu p_\beta$ on the <causal vertical wavenumber branch>. The constant Riccati solutions are $Z=\pm q$, and integrating the one-way equation gives
$$
\boxed{V(z)\propto e^{\pm i\omega p_\beta z}=e^{\pm ik_\beta z}.}
$$
Since $\operatorname{Re}q>0$, the plus solution carries positive downward flux and the minus solution negative upward flux. Writing $k_\beta=a+ib$ with $b>0$, their moduli are proportional to $e^{-bz}$ and $e^{bz}$ respectively: each decays in its direction of <energy> flow.

For complex <frequency> the displayed quadratic form is the spectral flux diagnostic specified in the question; a genuinely periodic time average is recovered on the real-frequency axis. The decrease here comes from the upper-half-plane convergence parameter, not from material dissipation. At real <frequency> in a lossless layer the corresponding flux is constant.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Let $z_r$ be either endpoint at which $Z_r$ is prescribed, and put $d_z=z-z_r$, $q=\mu p_\beta$ and $\kappa=\omega p_\beta$. Integrating the constant-coefficient <velocity>–<traction> system gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}V(z)\\T(z)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\kappa d_z&-i\sin(\kappa d_z)/q\\-iq\sin\kappa d_z&\cos\kappa d_z\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}V_r\\-Z_rV_r\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Taking the ratio $-T/V$ yields the <uniform-layer SH impedance update> and its accompanying <velocity>:
$$
\boxed{Z(z)=\frac{Z_r\cos\kappa d_z+iq\sin\kappa d_z}{\cos\kappa d_z+i(Z_r/q)\sin\kappa d_z},\qquad
V(z)=V_r\left[\cos\kappa d_z+i\frac{Z_r}{q}\sin\kappa d_z\right].}
$$
The initial value $V_r$ sets an arbitrary normalization; specifying impedance alone cannot specify the overall wave <wave amplitude>. Positive $d_z$ propagates data from $z_0$, while negative $d_z$ propagates a specified load from $z_1$, so the same expressions solve both requested initial-value problems.

Expanding sine and cosine gives
$$
\boxed{V(z)=\frac{V_r}{2}\left(1+\frac{Z_r}q\right)e^{i\kappa(z-z_r)}
+\frac{V_r}{2}\left(1-\frac{Z_r}q\right)e^{-i\kappa(z-z_r)}.}
$$
Absorbing the reference-point phases into the two constants gives the requested forward/backward exponential superposition. Equivalently, wherever the ratios are defined,
$$
\frac{Z(z)-q}{Z(z)+q}=\frac{Z_r-q}{Z_r+q}e^{-2i\kappa(z-z_r)}.
$$
The exceptional values $Z_r=q,-q$ give the pure directional solutions. If a denominator in the impedance formula vanishes, propagate the finite pair $(V,T)$ through that point; this is an impedance-chart pole, not a failure of the physical solution. The upper-half-plane assumptions ensure $q\ne0$.

= Solution
{parent=v}

In the lower uniform <half-space> the <causal vertical wavenumber branch> has $\operatorname{Im}k_{\beta,b}>0$. The downward factor $e^{ik_{\beta,b}(z-z_b)}$ decays as $z\to+\infty$, whereas the upward factor grows there and represents an incoming wave from the lower infinity. The outgoing <radiation condition> therefore retains only the downward factor and gives
$$
\boxed{Z(z_b)=q_b=\mu_bp_{\beta,b}.}
$$
For each uniform layer $j$ of thickness $h_j$, work upwards from its known bottom load $Z_{j,b}$. With $q_j=\mu_jp_{\beta,j}$ and $\delta_j=\omega p_{\beta,j}h_j$, part (iv) gives
$$
\boxed{Z_{j,t}=\frac{Z_{j,b}\cos\delta_j-iq_j\sin\delta_j}
{\cos\delta_j-i(Z_{j,b}/q_j)\sin\delta_j}.}
$$
<velocity> and <traction> are continuous across a bonded interface, so this top impedance becomes the bottom load of the next shallower layer. Repeat up to $z_a$ to obtain $Z_{\rm in}=Z(z_a)$. Poles can be handled by carrying $(V,T)$ or the reciprocal ratio. Since the lower load has positive real part, part (iii) actually ensures that the outgoing whole-stack impedance stays finite with positive real part at all shallower depths in the upper <frequency> half-plane.

The upper <half-space> has directional impedance $q_a=\mu_ap_{\beta,a}$. At $z_a$, an incident <velocity> $V_0$ and reflected <velocity> $RV_0$ give
$$
V(z_a)=V_0(1+R),\qquad T(z_a)=-q_aV_0(1-R).
$$
Taking their ratio and solving establishes <reflection from a layered SH impedance>:
$$
Z_{\rm in}=q_a\frac{1-R}{1+R},\qquad
\boxed{R=\frac{q_a-Z_{\rm in}}{q_a+Z_{\rm in}}.}
$$
Thus no individual deeper reflection coefficients are needed after $Z_{\rm in}$ is known: it contains all underlying interfaces and their coherent multiples. A matched load gives $R=0$; the real-frequency limits of zero <traction> and zero <velocity> give $R=+1$ and $R=-1$. For real <frequency> and propagating upper and lower <half-space> waves, the usual energy-flux balance provides a further check on the computed reflection and transmission. A stable implementation for thick layers uses the ratio update or its decaying-exponential version instead of multiplying growing transfer <matrices>.
