Let $P$ be the transition matrix of a finite <irreducible Markov chain> with at least two states, reversible with <stationary distribution> $\pi$. Its <Dirichlet form of a Markov chain> is
$$
\mathcal E(f,f)=\frac12\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)P(x,y)(f(x)-f(y))^2.
$$
The <discrete Poincare inequality> states that $\operatorname{Var}_\pi f\leq C\mathcal E(f,f)$ for every real $f$. Its optimal constant is $C=(1-\lambda_2)^{-1}$, where $\lambda_2$ is the second largest <eigenvalue> of $P$. Indeed, <detailed balance> makes $P$ <self-adjoint> on $L^2(\pi)$. Expand $f-\pi(f)=\sum_{j\geq2}a_jv_j$ in an <orthonormal eigenbasis>. Then $\operatorname{Var}_\pi f=\sum a_j^2$ and $\mathcal E(f,f)=\sum(1-\lambda_j)a_j^2$, proving the inequality and its sharpness.

The useful constructive version is the <canonical paths Poincare bound>. Choose transition <graph paths> $\gamma_{xy}$ between each ordered pair, write $Q(u,v)=\pi(u)P(u,v)$, and define
$$
\rho=\max_{(u,v):Q(u,v)>0}\frac1{Q(u,v)}
\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)\pi(y)|\gamma_{xy}|N_{uv}(\gamma_{xy}),
$$
where $N_{uv}$ counts occurrences of the directed transition. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> along each <path in a graph> gives $(f(x)-f(y))^2\leq|\gamma_{xy}|\sum_{\gamma_{xy}}(f(u)-f(v))^2$. Multiply by $\pi(x)\pi(y)/2$ and sum. The identity $\operatorname{Var}_\pi f=\frac12\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)\pi(y)(f(x)-f(y))^2$ proves $\operatorname{Var}_\pi f\leq\rho\mathcal E(f,f)$.

For a <lazy Markov chain> this also proves the <mixing time> estimate
$$
\|P^t(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\leq\frac1{2\sqrt{\pi(x)}}e^{-t/\rho}.
$$
To see this, the centred initial density has $L^2(\pi)$ norm at most $\pi(x)^{-1/2}$; laziness puts all nonconstant <eigenvalues> between zero and $1-\rho^{-1}$. Their powers contract that norm, and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> bounds <total variation distance> by half the density's $L^2(\pi)$ norm.

Here is a precise sufficient density condition for the permanent application: **every row and every column has more than $n/2$ ones**. Interpret the <permanent of a matrix> as the number $m_n$ of <perfect matchings> in its <bipartite graph>, with $n$ <vertices> per side. Write $m_k$ for the number of $k$-edge <matchings in a graph>.

First prove the <short augmenting paths in a dense bipartite graph> bound. Given a nonperfect <matching in a graph>, choose unmatched $u,v$ on opposite sides. If either has an unmatched <neighbour>, augment with one <edge>. Otherwise the matched partners of $N(u)$ and the <neighbours> $N(v)$ are subsets of the same $n$-element side whose total size exceeds $n$. Their intersection gives an alternating <path in a graph> $u,b,a,v$ of length three, with $ab$ a matching <edge>. Repeating proves $m_n>0$. Choose augmentations by a fixed deterministic rule. A reverse augmentation is specified by at most four <vertices>, so a next-size <matching in a graph> has at most $C_0=2n^4$ preimages. Therefore
$$
m_k\leq C_0m_{k+1},\qquad m_{n-j}\leq C_0^jm_n.
$$

Use the <matching generating function> $Z(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^nm_k\lambda^k$ and the <weighted matching Markov chain> on all <matchings in a graph>, with stationary weights $\pi_\lambda(M)=\lambda^{|M|}/Z(\lambda)$. It stays put with probability $1/2$; otherwise it chooses a <graph> <edge> uniformly and proposes its deletion, its addition if both endpoints are unmatched, or its exchange for the unique incident matching <edge> if precisely one endpoint is matched. Other proposals stay put. Accept with probability $\min(1,\lambda^{|M'|-|M|})$. Reverse proposals use the opposite exchanged <edge>, so <detailed balance> holds. Deletions connect every state to the empty <matching in a graph>.

For completeness, the <canonical paths for the weighted matching chain> give a polynomial bound without assuming a mixing theorem. The <symmetric difference> $I\triangle F$ consists of alternating <graph paths> and <graph cycles>. Order its components deterministically, orient open components from their smaller endpoint, and convert them from $I$ to $F$ by moving an unmatched endpoint with exchanges. If the first <edge> belongs to $I$, delete it first. For a <graph cycle>, delete one $I$-<edge>, move the unmatched endpoint around the opened cycle, then insert the last $F$-<edge>. Each intermediate state is a <matching in a graph>, and each entire transition <path in a graph> has length at most $4n$.

Fix a directed transition with initial state $M$. The complementary edge set $K=I\triangle F\triangle M$ agrees with one of the two <matchings in a graph> outside the active component and has at most two degree-two <vertices> on that component. Remove at most two offending <edges> to obtain a <matching in a graph> $K'$. Store those <edges> and one bit specifying the initial alternating colour of the active component. This encoding is injective for the fixed transition: it restores $K$ and $I\triangle F=M\triangle K$, identifies the active component from the changed <edges>, and recovers the two colours using the fixed component order. Completed components have $F$'s colour in $M$, pending components have $I$'s colour, and the stored bit resolves the active component. Common <edges> are $M\cap K$.

There are at most $2(m+1)^2$ auxiliary records per $K'$, where $m$ is the number of <graph> <edges>. If $d=|K|-|K'|\leq2$, then
$$
\pi_\lambda(I)\pi_\lambda(F)=\lambda^d\pi_\lambda(M)\pi_\lambda(K').
$$
Also $Q(M,M')\geq\pi_\lambda(M)/(2m\max(\lambda,\lambda^{-1}))$. Sum over the encodings and use the <path> length bound to obtain
$$
\rho\leq16nm(m+1)^2\max(1,\lambda^2)\max(\lambda,\lambda^{-1}).
$$
This is polynomial whenever $\lambda$ and $\lambda^{-1}$ are polynomially bounded. Starting at the empty <matching in a graph>, $\log(1/\pi_\lambda(\varnothing))=\log Z(\lambda)\leq m\log(1+\lambda)$, so the <discrete Poincare inequality> gives polynomial-time sampling to any prescribed <total variation distance>. The addition/deletion/exchange construction is also described in https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~sinclair/mcmc.pdf[Section 12.4 of Jerrum and Sinclair's survey].

It remains to turn sampling into a relative count; merely sampling <perfect matchings> would not do that. For $0<\varepsilon\leq1/2$, choose $\lambda_0=\varepsilon/(8m)$ and $\lambda_*=8C_0/\varepsilon$. The elementary bounds above give
$$
1\leq Z(\lambda_0)\leq(1+\lambda_0)^m\leq1+\varepsilon/4,
\qquad
1\leq\frac{Z(\lambda_*)}{m_n\lambda_*^n}
\leq\sum_{j=0}^n(C_0/\lambda_*)^j\leq1+\varepsilon/4.
$$
Choose a schedule $\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_J=\lambda_*$ whose successive ratios are at most $1+1/n$. It has $J=O(n\log(n/\varepsilon))$ stages. The <annealing ratios for a matching generating function> satisfy
$$
\frac{Z(\lambda_{j+1})}{Z(\lambda_j)}
=\mathbb E_{\pi_{\lambda_j}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j}\right)^{|M|}.
$$
Each observable lies in $[1,e]$, so the <Hoeffding inequality> gives relative accuracy $\eta=\varepsilon/(10J)$ with $O(\eta^{-2}\log(J/\delta))$ independent samples per stage. Run independent copies of the chain from the empty <matching in a graph>; make the <total variation distance> per sample at most $\delta/(3JK)$, where $K$ is that sample count. The <coupling characterization of total variation distance> and the <union bound> make the entire sample collection coincide with exact stationary samples except with probability at most $\delta/3$. Choose the sample-count constant so all stationary sample means are accurate except with probability at most $\delta/3$.

Let $\widehat R$ be the product of the sample means, and output $\widehat p=\widehat R/\lambda_*^n$. On the successful event its multiplicative error relative to $Z(\lambda_*)/Z(\lambda_0)$ lies between $e^{-\varepsilon/5}$ and $e^{\varepsilon/5}$. Combining the two endpoint bounds proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\bigl((1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{per}A\leq\widehat p\leq(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{per}A\bigr)\geq1-\delta.}
$$
Activities, sample counts, simulation lengths and rational bit lengths are polynomial in $n,\varepsilon^{-1},\log(\delta^{-1})$. Thus this is a <fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme>. Small dimensions can be evaluated directly.

For disjoint <vertex sets> $A,B$, define their <edge density of a bipartite graph> by $d(A,B)=e(A,B)/(|A||B|)$. They form a <regular pair> with parameter $\varepsilon$ if every $A'\subseteq A,B'\subseteq B$ with $|A'|\geq\varepsilon|A|$, $|B'|\geq\varepsilon|B|$ satisfies $|d(A',B')-d(A,B)|\leq\varepsilon$.

The <Szemerédi regularity lemma> says that for every $\varepsilon>0$ and minimum cell count $k_0$, some $K,n_0$ have the following property. Every <graph> on $n\geq n_0$ <vertices> admits
$$
V=V_0\sqcup V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_k,
\qquad k_0\leq k\leq K,
$$
with $|V_0|\leq\varepsilon n$, equal-sized $V_1,\ldots,V_k$, and at most $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered <irregular pairs>. It is enough to consider $0<\varepsilon<1/2$.

Prove this using <equitable regularity energy>. Treat exceptional <vertices> as singleton cells and set
$$
q(\mathcal P)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{A,B\in\mathcal P}|A||B|d(A,B)^2,
$$
with ordered pairs and the adjacency indicator also defining diagonal densities. Then $0\leq q\leq1$. The <refinement variance identity for regularity energy> proves refinement cannot decrease $q$:
$$
\sum_{a,b}|A_a||B_b|d(A_a,B_b)^2-|A||B|d(A,B)^2
=\sum_{a,b}|A_a||B_b|(d(A_a,B_b)-d(A,B))^2.
$$
An <irregular pair> supplies witness subsets of relative sizes at least $\varepsilon$ whose density differs by more than $\varepsilon$. Refining along those witnesses raises $q$ by more than $\varepsilon^4L^2/n^2$ per orientation, where $L$ is the common cell size. If there are more than $\varepsilon k^2$ <irregular pairs>, the increase is greater than $\varepsilon^5(kL/n)^2\geq\varepsilon^5/4$, provided the exceptional set has size at most $n/2$.

Refine each cell by all its witness subsets; this produces at most $2^k$ atoms per cell. Restore equal sizes by the <equalization with a controlled exceptional set>: cut each atom into blocks of size $\lfloor L/4^k\rfloor$ and declare its remainder exceptional, still retaining every such <vertex> as a singleton in the energy. This is another refinement, so no energy is lost. At most $k2^k\lfloor L/4^k\rfloor\leq n2^{-k}$ <vertices> become exceptional, and, when $L\geq2\cdot4^k$, at most $2k4^k$ new nonexceptional cells occur.

Put $T=\lceil4\varepsilon^{-5}\rceil$ and start with $k\geq k_0$ so large that $T2^{-k}\leq\varepsilon/2$. An initial equitable partition has at most $\varepsilon n/2$ exceptional <vertices> when $n$ is sufficiently large. Subsequent cell counts only increase, so the total added exceptional set has size at most $\varepsilon n/2$. The recurrence $k\mapsto2k4^k$, iterated at most $T$ times, supplies a finite bound $K$; choose $n_0$ large enough that every intermediate $L$ meets the size requirement. More than $T$ unsuccessful refinements would raise $q$ above one. The process must therefore stop with the required <regular pairs>. This proves the lemma.

Its usefulness comes from replacing an arbitrarily large <graph> by the bounded <reduced graph of a regularity partition>. For fixed positive cross-density, the <graph embedding lemma for regular pairs> lifts fixed configurations from the reduced <graph> to the original one. In particular, three sufficiently regular positive-density pairs contain many <triangles in a graph>. Deleting exceptional, intracell, irregular and sparse-pair <edges> then gives the <triangle removal lemma>: a <graph> with sufficiently few triangles can be made <triangle-free> by deleting $o(n^2)$ <edges>. A triangle surviving in the reduced <graph> would force a positive proportion of the original possible triangles, a contradiction. More generally this approach yields the <clique removal lemma> and the asymptotic <Erdős-Stone theorem>. Applied to the tripartite <graph> encoding $x+z=2y$, triangle removal also gives <Roth theorem> on three-term <arithmetic progressions>. The price is that the energy argument gives a very rapidly growing, iterated-exponential bound on $K(\varepsilon)$; the lemma is a structural tool, not a promise of small practical constants.

Let $t$ be the maximum number of positive <Euclidean distances> from one of the $n$ points to the others. It suffices to prove the stronger <Székely distinct-distance bound>, $t\geq cn^{4/5}$. Here $n\geq2$; enlarge constants to handle bounded $n$.

First prove the <crossing lemma for multigraphs>. In an optimal drawing of a loopless <multigraph> with $v$ <vertices>, $e$ <edges> and maximum parallel multiplicity $\mu$, crossings of incident <edges> can be removed by interchanging initial segments, so each remaining crossing involves four distinct <vertices>. In each parallel class choose one of its <edges> with probability $1/\mu$ per <edge>, selecting none with the remaining probability. Independently keep each <vertex> with probability $p$. The resulting <simple graph> has expected <edge> count $p^2e/\mu$, expected <vertex> count $pv$, and expected crossings $p^4X/\mu^2$, where $X$ is the original crossing count. Deleting one <edge> per crossing and using the allowed <planar graph edge bound> yields
$$
\frac{p^4X}{\mu^2}\geq\frac{p^2e}{\mu}-3pv.
$$
If $e\geq4\mu v$, take $p=4\mu v/e$ to obtain
$$
X\geq\frac{e^3}{64\mu v^2}.
$$
The case $\mu=1$ is the ordinary <crossing lemma>.

We also need the <rich line bound>. Given $L$ <lines>, connect consecutive incidence points on each <line> by straight <edges>. This <simple graph> has at least $I-L$ <edges>, where $I$ is the total number of incidences, and at most $L^2$ crossings. The <crossing lemma> gives $I=O((nL)^{2/3}+n+L)$, proving the <Szemerédi–Trotter theorem> here rather than assuming it. For <lines> containing at least $k$ points, $I\geq kL$, and absorbing the additive $L$ term gives
$$
L_{\geq k}=O(n^2/k^3+n/k),\qquad k\geq2.
$$
For bounded $k$, counting point pairs gives the same estimate with a larger absolute constant. Grouping rich <lines> into occupancy ranges $2^jk\leq s<2^{j+1}k$ and summing the resulting geometric series proves
$$
\sum_{\ell:\,|P\cap\ell|\geq k}|P\cap\ell|
=O(n^2/k^2+n\log n).
$$

Now form the <circular-arc graph for distinct distances>. Around every point draw each positive-distance <circle> centred there; there are at most $nt$ such <circles>. A <circle> with at least three of the original points contributes one <edge> between each consecutive pair around it. Discard the <circles> with at most two points. Since the total number of centre-to-point incidences is $n(n-1)$, the remaining loopless <multigraph> has
$$
e\geq n(n-1)-2nt.
$$
Its circular-arc drawing has $O(n^2t^2)$ crossings: two distinct <circles> have at most two intersections, and small perturbations remove tangencies and multiple crossings without changing this order.

The apparent difficulty is unbounded parallel multiplicity. The <rich-bisector deletion bound> controls it. Every centre supporting an arc between fixed endpoints lies on their <perpendicular bisector>. Distinct parallel arcs come from distinct centres, since their common endpoints determine the radius once the centre is fixed. Thus multiplicity at least $k$ makes that <line> contain at least $k$ of the points. For a <line> with $s$ centres, each of its at most $st$ <circles> has at most two consecutive-point arcs symmetric about that <line>, one at each end of its diameter along the <line>. The occupancy sum above therefore bounds the number of high-multiplicity arcs by
$$
O(tn^2/k^2+tn\log n).
$$

If $t\geq n^{4/5}$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $t\log n=o(n)$ and $t=o(n)$. Choose $k=\lceil K\sqrt t\rceil$ with a sufficiently large absolute constant $K$. The first deletion term is at most a small fixed fraction of $n^2$ and the second is $o(n^2)$. A remaining <multigraph> therefore has $e_1\geq c_1n^2$ <edges> and multiplicity at most $k$. For large $n$, $e_1\geq4kn$, so the <crossing lemma for multigraphs> gives
$$
Cn^2t^2\geq X_1\geq\frac{e_1^3}{64kn^2}
\geq c_2\frac{n^4}{\sqrt t}.
$$
Rearranging,
$$
\boxed{t^{5/2}\geq c_3n^2,\qquad t\geq cn^{4/5}.}
$$
The whole <distinct-distance set> contains those distances from the selected point, proving the requested bound. The circular-arc method and the stronger one-point conclusion are due to https://www.cs.tau.ac.il/~michas/szekely.pdf[Székely].

Let $\operatorname{CLIQUE}_{n,m}$ be the <monotone Boolean function> of the <edge> indicators asserting that an $n$-<vertex> <graph> contains an $m$-<clique>. We prove a <superpolynomial monotone clique lower bound> using a finite <lattice> of approximations. The only circuit theorem assumed is the <Razborov gate-by-gate approximation lemma>.

Fix integers $\ell,r\geq2$. Consider upward-closed <set families> $A$ of subsets of $[n]$ of size at most $\ell$. Impose the <Razborov closure> rule: if $W_1,\ldots,W_r\in A$ have pairwise intersections contained in $W$, adjoin $W$ whenever $|W|\leq\ell$. Also normalize a family containing a set of size zero or one to the full family, since its clique predicate is identically true. Represent it by
$$
\langle A\rangle=\{G:G[W]\text{ is complete for some }W\in A\}.
$$
Closed families form the <finite lattice approximation for monotone clique circuits>, with meet $A\cap B$ and join $\operatorname{cl}(A\cup B)$. An input <edge> is represented by the family of its supersets up to size $\ell$. The constant predicates have the empty and full families. The assumed approximation theorem says that a size-$S$ circuit's output disagreement is covered by at most $S$ local gate errors:
$$
\delta_\wedge(A,B)=(\langle A\rangle\cap\langle B\rangle)\setminus\langle A\cap B\rangle,
\qquad
\delta_\vee(A,B)=\langle\operatorname{cl}(A\cup B)\rangle\setminus(\langle A\rangle\cup\langle B\rangle).
$$

We need the <Erdős–Rado sunflower lemma>: an $s$-<uniform set family> with more than $s!(r-1)^s$ members contains $r$ members with a common pairwise intersection. Its short induction proves the bound. A maximal disjoint subfamily either has $r$ members, already the required empty-core <delta-system>, or its union has at most $s(r-1)$ points and meets every member. Some point then occurs in more than $(s-1)!(r-1)^{s-1}$ members. Delete it, apply the induction hypothesis, and restore it to the core. Thus an $r$-closed family has at most
$$
a_s\leq s!(r-1)^s
$$
inclusion-minimal $s$-sets: a sunflower among those members would force its proper core and contradict minimality. This is the <sunflower bound for minimal members of a Razborov-closed family>.

For positive inputs choose a uniform $m$-subset $Z$ and the <graph> $K_Z$ having exactly the <edges> of its <clique>. Put $q=\ell rm/n$ and suppose $q\leq1/2$. A nonuniversal family has no minimal member of size at most one, so it accepts at most
$$
\sum_{s=2}^\ell s!(r-1)^s\left(\frac mn\right)^s
\leq\sum_{s=2}^\ell q^s\leq2q^2
$$
of positive inputs. Here $\mathbb P(W\subseteq Z)=(m)_{|W|}/(n)_{|W|}\leq(m/n)^{|W|}$.

If $K_Z$ is in a meet-error set, it contains minimal witnesses $X\in A,Y\in B$. Their union must have size greater than $\ell$, since otherwise upward closure would put it in $A\cap B$. At least one witness therefore has size greater than $\ell/2$. The same counting and the <union bound> give the <positive clique error of a truncated lattice meet>:
$$
\mathbb P(K_Z\in\delta_\wedge(A,B))
\leq2\sum_{s>\ell/2}s!(r-1)^s(m/n)^s
\leq4q^{\ell/2}.
$$

For negative inputs independently assign one of $m-1$ colours to each <vertex>, joining differently coloured <vertices>. Every resulting <complete multipartite graph> is $m$-<clique>-free. Consider one forcing step adjoining $W$. If its <clique> is newly accepted, $W$ is rainbow while all witnesses $W_i$ are not. Condition on the colours of $W$. The sets $W_i\setminus W$ are disjoint, so their non-rainbow events are <conditionally independent>. For each witness, every possible collision either involves two random petal colours or one petal colour and one fixed core colour; its probability is at most
$$
\frac{\binom\ell2}{m-1}.
$$
When this is at most $1/2$, a step makes a negative error with probability at most $2^{-r}$. Upward closure itself introduces no extra accepted <graphs>, and normalization introduces no extra error beyond the forcing step that produced a set of size at most one. At most $\sum_{j=0}^\ell\binom nj\leq n^{\ell+1}$ sets can be forced. The <negative colouring error of a forced-set closure> is therefore
$$
\mathbb P(G_{\mathrm{colour}}\in\delta_\vee(A,B))\leq n^{\ell+1}2^{-r}.
$$

Set $m=r=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor$ and $\ell=\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor$. For sufficiently large $n$, both probability conditions hold and $2q^2<1/2$. If the output approximation is nonuniversal, it misses at least half the positive inputs, so the <Razborov gate-by-gate approximation lemma> forces $1/2\leq4Sq^{\ell/2}$. If it is universal, it accepts every negative colouring input, forcing $1\leq Sn^{\ell+1}2^{-r}$. Consequently
$$
S\geq\min\left\{\frac1{8q^{\ell/2}},\frac{2^r}{n^{\ell+1}}\right\}.
$$
Since $\log(1/q)\geq\frac12\log n-\log\ell$ and $r$ grows faster than $(\log n)^2$, both terms imply
$$
\boxed{S\geq\exp(c(\log n)^2)=n^{c\log n}}
$$
for an absolute positive $c$ and all sufficiently large $n$. This is superpolynomial in the $\binom n2$ input bits. The argument concerns AND/OR <monotone circuits>; it gives no such bound for circuits allowed negation.

The <Frankl-Wilson theorem> is a striking example of the <polynomial method in combinatorics>: an intersection restriction creates a family of <linearly independent> low-degree <polynomials>, whose dimension bounds the set family. Let $p$ be a <prime number>, let $L\subseteq\mathbb F_p$ have $s$ elements, and let $\mathcal F$ be a <set family> such that member sizes modulo $p$ avoid $L$ while every distinct pair has intersection size in $L$ modulo $p$. Its general modular form gives
$$
|\mathcal F|\leq\sum_{j=0}^s\binom nj.
$$
If all members have the same size $k$ and $s\leq k$, the uniform refinement gives **$|\mathcal F|\leq\binom ns$**.

For the proof, work over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_p$ and associate to $A\in\mathcal F$ the <modular intersection polynomial>
$$
P_A(x)=\prod_{\lambda\in L}\left(\sum_{i\in A}x_i-\lambda\right).
$$
At the <characteristic vector of a set> $B$, this equals zero for $B\ne A$ and is nonzero for $B=A$. A linear relation among the evaluation functions therefore has every coefficient zero. The <multilinear reduction on the Boolean cube>, replacing $x_i^a$ by $x_i$ for $a\geq1$, preserves these evaluations and the degree bound $s$. There are $\sum_{j=0}^s\binom nj$ possible square-free <monomials>, proving the general form.

For uniform members, the <low-degree evaluation rank on a uniform layer> supplies the sharper dimension bound. To verify this without division modulo $p$, form the integer incidence <matrix> with rows indexed by subsets $S$ of size at most $s$, columns indexed by $k$-subsets $B$, and entries $\mathbf1_{S\subseteq B}$. Over the <rational numbers>, a row with $|S|=j$ is $\binom{k-j}{s-j}^{-1}$ times the sum of all size-$s$ rows containing $S$. Its <matrix rank> is at most $\binom ns$. Every larger integer minor is consequently zero and remains zero modulo $p$, giving the same bound over $\mathbb F_p$. The nonzero diagonal evaluation <matrix> of the $P_A$ then forces $|\mathcal F|\leq\binom ns$.

The theorem, proved by Frankl and Wilson in 1981, extends the theme of the <Ray-Chaudhuri–Wilson theorem> from ordinary intersection sizes to residues. Modular restrictions can be weaker than exact intersection restrictions yet still force unexpectedly small families. Here are three consequences from one explicit <modular intersection graph>.

Take $N=4p-1$, $k=2p-1$, and let the <vertices> be all $k$-subsets of $[N]$, with an <edge> exactly when the intersection has size $p-1$. An <independent set> avoids the only possible off-diagonal intersection congruent to $k$ modulo $p$, so applying the <Frankl-Wilson theorem> with $L=\mathbb F_p\setminus\{p-1\}$ gives
$$
\alpha(G)\leq B:=\binom N{p-1}.
$$
A <clique> has one exact intersection size. Apply the uniform theorem modulo a prime larger than $k$, with $L=\{p-1\}$, to get $\omega(G)\leq N$. Since there are $v=\binom Nk$ <vertices>, this gives the explicit asymmetric <Ramsey number> bound **$R(N+1,B+1)>v$**. It illustrates how algebraic constructions produce graphs with simultaneously restricted <cliques> and <independent sets>.

For a geometric application, map each <vertex> to $x_A=\mathbf1_A/\sqrt{2p}$ in $\mathbb R^N$. Then $|x_A-x_B|^2=(k-|A\cap B|)/p$, so <edges> have unit <Euclidean distance>. Every proper colouring of Euclidean space therefore colours this finite <graph>, giving
$$
\chi(\mathbb R^N)\geq\chi(G)\geq\frac{\binom N{2p-1}}{\binom N{p-1}}.
$$
The <Stirling formula> gives the ratio as $\exp((\log2-H(1/4)+o(1))N)$, where $H(a)=-a\log a-(1-a)\log(1-a)$ is the <binary entropy function> in natural-log units. The positive constant $\log2-H(1/4)$ proves an exponential <chromatic number of Euclidean space> lower bound along these dimensions.

Finally, a quadratic embedding converts this same forbidden intersection into a forbidden <diameter> pair. Set $u_A=2\mathbf1_A-\mathbf1$ and $Q_A=u_Au_A^T$. The <quadratic sign-vector diameter formula> follows from the <Frobenius inner product>:
$$
\|Q_A-Q_B\|_F^2=2\bigl(N^2-(u_A\cdot u_B)^2\bigr),
\qquad
u_A\cdot u_B=4|A\cap B|-4p+3.
$$
For distinct members the smallest possible absolute inner product is one, attained exactly when the intersection is $p-1$. Thus maximum-distance pairs are exactly <edges> of $G$. The matrices have common diagonal; using their off-diagonal coordinates, multiplied by $\sqrt2$, realizes them isometrically in $D=\binom N2$ dimensions. They are distinct because equal outer products require $u_A=\pm u_B$, and complements have size $2p$, not $k$.

Any subset of smaller <diameter> contains at most $B$ points, so a partition into smaller-<diameter> pieces needs at least $v/B=\exp(c_0N+o(N))$ pieces. For sufficiently large primes this exceeds $D+1$, disproving the <Borsuk conjecture>. This is the mechanism behind the <Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture>: the number of required pieces grows exponentially in the original set-system parameter while dimension grows quadratically. Their https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9307229[1993 paper] establishes the resulting high-dimensional counterexample.
