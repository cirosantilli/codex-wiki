= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the curvature sign convention implicit in part (c): the <Jacobi field> equation is $D_t^2J+K_{\dot\gamma}J=0$, so the operator in a positively curved normal direction has positive <eigenvalue>. With the alternative convention $R_{\mathrm{std}}(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ used for the catalog's <Jacobi curvature operator>, the paper's tensor is $R=-R_{\mathrm{std}}$, and its operator is $K_v(x)=R_{\mathrm{std}}(x,v)v$. This fixes the sign throughout Q1.

Let $r(A,B,C,D)=\langle R(A,B)C,D\rangle$. Pair interchange for the <Riemann curvature tensor> gives
$$
\langle K_vx,y\rangle=r(v,x,v,y)=r(v,y,v,x)=\langle x,K_vy\rangle.
$$
Thus **$K_v$ is <self-adjoint>** with respect to the <Riemannian metric>. The curvature sign does not affect this conclusion. The <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> therefore supplies the real orthonormal <eigenbasis> used in part (b). Also $K_vv=0$, so the tangent direction is a zero-eigenvalue direction.

Write $D_t=\nabla_{\dot\gamma}$ and let $P_t:T_pM\to T_{\gamma(t)}M$ denote <parallel transport>. Since $\gamma$ is a <geodesic>, $D_t\dot\gamma=0$, while the transported vectors satisfy $D_te_i(t)=0$. The <locally symmetric Riemannian manifold> condition is $\nabla R=0$. Hence the <Levi-Civita connection> product rule yields
$$
D_t\bigl(R(\dot\gamma,e_i(t))\dot\gamma\bigr)
=(\nabla_{\dot\gamma}R)(\dot\gamma,e_i(t))\dot\gamma
+R(D_t\dot\gamma,e_i(t))\dot\gamma
+R(\dot\gamma,D_te_i(t))\dot\gamma
+R(\dot\gamma,e_i(t))D_t\dot\gamma=0.
$$
Both this vector field and $\lambda_i e_i(t)$ are parallel and agree at zero. Uniqueness of <parallel transport> therefore proves
$$
\boxed{K_{\dot\gamma(t)}e_i(t)=\lambda_i e_i(t),\qquad K_{\dot\gamma(t)}=P_tK_vP_t^{-1}.}
$$
The <eigenvalues> are independent of $t$. The original PDF has $K_{\dot\gamma(t)}$ here; the converted TeX omits the dot, which would incorrectly put a point rather than a tangent vector in the operator's subscript.

Expand a <Jacobi field> in the parallel orthonormal <eigenbasis>: $J(t)=\sum_i j_i(t)e_i(t)$. Part (b) reduces its equation to the scalar equations $j_i''+\lambda_i j_i=0$. Define
$$
S_\lambda(t)=\begin{cases}
\sin(\sqrt\lambda\,t)/\sqrt\lambda,&\lambda>0,\\
t,&\lambda=0,\\
\sinh(\sqrt{-\lambda}\,t)/\sqrt{-\lambda},&\lambda<0,
\end{cases}
\qquad C_\lambda(t)=S_\lambda'(t).
$$
For arbitrary initial values $J(0)=\sum_i a_ie_i$ and $D_tJ(0)=\sum_i b_ie_i$, the complete solution is
$$
J(t)=\sum_i\bigl(a_iC_{\lambda_i}(t)+b_iS_{\lambda_i}(t)\bigr)e_i(t).
$$
A point at parameter $T>0$ is a <conjugate point> of $p$ precisely when a nonzero <Jacobi field> vanishes at both $0$ and $T$. The first condition makes every $a_i=0$. The second requires $b_iS_{\lambda_i}(T)=0$ for every $i$, with at least one nonzero $b_i$. For $T>0$, the factors for zero and negative <eigenvalues> never vanish; positive <eigenvalues> give exactly the sine zeros. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{k\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda_i}},\qquad k\in\mathbb N_{>0},\quad\lambda_i>0.}
$$
The corresponding points are $\gamma(T)$. If several positive eigenspaces have a zero at the same parameter, the conjugate multiplicity is the sum of their dimensions. This also shows that a <locally symmetric Riemannian manifold> has no <conjugate points> along a given <geodesic> when that <geodesic>'s <Jacobi curvature operator> has no positive <eigenvalues>.

A <connected> <Riemannian manifold> is complete when its <Riemannian distance> is a complete <metric space>, meaning that every <Cauchy sequence> converges in the manifold. The <Hopf-Rinow theorem> says that for a <connected> finite-dimensional <Riemannian manifold>, this is equivalent to <geodesic completeness>, and also to <compactness> of every closed bounded subset. Under these equivalent conditions any two points are joined by a <minimizing geodesic>, and the <Riemannian exponential map> at every point is defined on its entire <tangent space>.

We use the usual connected, boundaryless-manifold convention for the global statements in Q2–Q5. Connectedness is essential, including for the diameter and group-growth conclusions. Without connectedness, the completeness and covering assertions apply to the target components met by the map; an unrelated incomplete component cannot be controlled by a <local isometry> into another component.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <local isometry> preserves the <Levi-Civita connection> and hence <geodesics>, as well as the lengths of lifted curves. We first show that a complete source permits finite-length paths to lift all the way to their endpoints. Begin lifting a piecewise smooth path $c:[0,1]\to N$ from any chosen point above $c(0)$, using local inverse maps. If its lift is defined only up to a maximal $b\leq1$, then
$$
d_M(\widetilde c(s),\widetilde c(t))\leq\operatorname{length}(c|_{[s,t]})\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{as }s,t\uparrow b.
$$
Completeness gives a limit point in $M$. A local inverse at that point continues the lift, including its endpoint. Thus such a finite obstruction is impossible. Connectedness of $N$ and piecewise smooth paths from $f(p)$ to any target point prove that $f$ is onto; no prior completeness of $N$ is used in this step.

Now lift the initial position and velocity of any <geodesic> in $N$. The resulting <geodesic> in $M$ exists for all real time by <Hopf-Rinow theorem>. Its image extends the original target <geodesic> by uniqueness of the <geodesic> initial-value problem. Hence $N$ is geodesically complete, and therefore complete.

**The converse is false for a general local <isometry>**, even an onto one. Give the interval $(0,4\pi)$ its ordinary metric and map it to the unit circle by $t\mapsto e^{it}$. This is an onto <local isometry> to a complete circle, but the sequence $t_j=1/j$ is Cauchy in the interval and has no limit there. It is not a <covering map>: the identity point of the circle has only one preimage, whereas nearby points have two.

**The converse holds for a Riemannian covering.** If $f$ is also a <covering map> and $N$ is complete, every target <geodesic> defined on $\mathbb R$ lifts globally with any specified initial point. A <local isometry> makes that lift a <geodesic>. Its initial velocity can be any prescribed source velocity because $df$ is an isomorphism. Thus all source <geodesics> extend for all time, and $M$ is complete by the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>.

= Solution

Surjectivity was proved in part (b). Fix $q\in N$ and choose a normal ball $B=B_N(q,\varepsilon)$ on which $\exp_q$ is a <diffeomorphism> from the tangent ball. For each $p\in f^{-1}(q)$ define a section
$$
s_p(\exp_qv)=\exp_p\bigl((df_p)^{-1}v\bigr),\qquad |v|<\varepsilon.
$$
Completeness of $M$ guarantees that the source <Riemannian exponential map> is defined. Preservation of <geodesics> and uniqueness of their initial-value problem give $f\circ s_p=\mathrm{id}_B$. Since $df$ is invertible, differentiation of this identity shows that $s_p$ is a local <diffeomorphism>; it is injective because it is a section. Its image $U_p$ is open, and $f:U_p\to B$ is a <diffeomorphism>.

These images are disjoint. If $s_p(y)=s_{p'}(y)$, lift the unique radial <geodesic> from $y$ back to $q$ starting at that common point. Both section constructions give this same lift, and uniqueness forces its endpoint to be both $p$ and $p'$. Conversely, for any $x\in f^{-1}(B)$, lift that reversed radial <geodesic> from $f(x)$ to $q$ starting at $x$. Completeness supplies its full finite interval; its endpoint is some $p\in f^{-1}(q)$. Reversing the lift shows $x=s_p(f(x))$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{f^{-1}(B)=\coprod_{p\in f^{-1}(q)}U_p,\qquad f|_{U_p}:U_p\longrightarrow B\text{ is a diffeomorphism}.}
$$
Every point has such an evenly covered neighborhood, proving that **the complete local <isometry> is a covering**. This argument does not assume a uniform positive source injectivity radius.

A <connected> complete <Riemannian manifold> is <disconnected at infinity> when the complement of some <compact> set has at least two unbounded <connected> components. Equivalently it has at least two ends. A <line in a Riemannian manifold> is a unit-speed <geodesic> $\ell:\mathbb R\to M$ satisfying $d(\ell(s),\ell(t))=|s-t|$ for every $s,t$.

Let $K$ be the <compact> separating set, enlarged inside a fixed <compact> metric ball if needed for bounds. Choose $x_j,y_j$ in two different unbounded components of $M\setminus K$, with both distances from $K$ tending to infinity. By the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>, join them by unit-speed <minimizing geodesics>. Every such segment meets $K$, since a path avoiding it cannot join its different components. Reparametrize a segment so that one crossing point is at time zero. Its parameter interval is $[-a_j,b_j]$, where $a_j,b_j\to\infty$ by the distances from the endpoints to $K$.

The <unit tangent bundle> over $K$ is <compact>, so a subsequence of the initial position/velocity pairs converges. Continuous dependence of the <geodesic equation> on its initial data, and completeness, give a limiting <geodesic> defined on all of $\mathbb R$. For any fixed $s<t$, the approximating segments are defined on $[s,t]$ for large $j$ and satisfy $d(\gamma_j(s),\gamma_j(t))=t-s$. Continuity of the distance passes this equality to the limit. Thus **the limiting <geodesic> is a line**, proving <a line from disconnection at infinity>.

The <Cheeger-Gromoll splitting theorem> states that a complete <connected> <Riemannian manifold> with nonnegative <Ricci curvature> that contains a <line in a Riemannian manifold> is isometric to a <Riemannian product> $N\times\mathbb R$ with the Euclidean metric on the line. The factor $N$ is complete and has nonnegative <Ricci curvature>. Under the <isometry> one can take the given line to $\{q\}\times\mathbb R$, up to translation and reversal. A line means global minimization between every pair of its points, not merely a complete <geodesic>.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that $X=M\times\mathbb R$ has a complete metric with zero <Ricci curvature>. Since $M$ is closed and <connected>, the complement of $M\times[-A,A]$ has two unbounded components in any complete metric on $X$: their closures are noncompact, while closed bounded sets are <compact> by the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>. Thus $X$ is <disconnected at infinity>, and part (a) supplies a line. The <Cheeger-Gromoll splitting theorem> gives an <isometry>
$$
X\cong N^3\times\mathbb R.
$$
The <Riemannian product> curvature formula makes $N$ complete and Ricci-flat. A three-dimensional <Ricci-flat Riemannian manifold> is flat: for any orthonormal triple, the three trace equations are $\operatorname{Ric}_{11}=K_{12}+K_{13}$ and their cyclic companions. Solving gives $K_{ij}=\operatorname{Ric}_{ii}+\operatorname{Ric}_{jj}-\operatorname{Scal}/2=0$. Every plane can occur in such a triple, so every <sectional curvature> vanishes, and hence so does the <Riemann curvature tensor>.

The complete <simply connected> <universal cover> of $N$ is therefore Euclidean $\mathbb R^3$, and that of $X$ is Euclidean $\mathbb R^4$. But the topological <universal cover> of $X$ is $\widetilde M\times\mathbb R$. Its <deformation retraction> onto $\widetilde M\times\{0\}$ shows that contractibility of this cover would imply contractibility of $\widetilde M$, contrary to the hypothesis. Therefore **no complete Ricci-flat metric exists on $M\times\mathbb R$**. Notice that no product assumption was made about the hypothetical original metric.

There is a normalization issue in the printed constant. Let $\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ denote the trace convention in the catalog's <Ricci curvature>, and write $\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}=\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}/(n-1)$ for <normalized Ricci curvature>, with $n\geq2$. The claimed $\pi/\sqrt k$ bound corresponds to $\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}\geq kg$, or equivalently $\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}\geq(n-1)kg$. We prove the estimate in both conventions.

Take a unit-speed <minimizing geodesic> of length $L>0$ and perpendicular parallel orthonormal fields $E_1,\ldots,E_{n-1}$. Set $V_i(t)=\sin(\pi t/L)E_i(t)$. The fields vanish at the endpoints. The allowed <second variation of geodesic energy> makes each <Riemannian index form> nonnegative. Summing them gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
0\leq\sum_i I(V_i,V_i)
&=\int_0^L\left((n-1)\frac{\pi^2}{L^2}\cos^2(\pi t/L)
-\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)\sin^2(\pi t/L)\right)dt\\
&\leq\frac{n-1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{L}-kL\right)
\end{aligned}
$$
under the normalized lower bound. Thus $L\leq\pi/\sqrt k$. Completeness and the <Hopf-Rinow theorem> supply a <minimizing geodesic> between any two points, so this bounds the diameter. A closed ball of that radius about any point contains the whole manifold and is <compact>, again by the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>.

Hence the intended <Bonnet-Myers theorem> conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{Ric}}\geq kg\ \Longrightarrow\ M\text{ compact},\quad\operatorname{diam}M\leq\frac\pi{\sqrt k}.}
$$
If the printed $\operatorname{Ric}\geq k$ means trace Ricci curvature instead, the same integral has lower curvature term $kL/2$ and yields
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}\geq kg\ \Longrightarrow\ \operatorname{diam}M\leq\pi\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{k}}.}
$$
The smaller bound is false under that convention when $n>2$: the round unit $S^n$ has trace Ricci tensor $(n-1)g$ and diameter $\pi$. Taking $k=n-1$ contradicts the printed $\pi/\sqrt k$ inequality. The <compactness> assertion remains true.

= Solution

**Pointwise positive Ricci curvature does not imply <compactness> or a finite diameter.** The <complete positively curved paraboloid> $z=x^2+y^2$ has induced metric
$$
g=(1+4r^2)dr^2+r^2d\theta^2
$$
and <Gaussian curvature>
$$
K(r)=\frac{4}{(1+4r^2)^2}>0.
$$
For example, this follows from the graph formula $K=\det D^2z/(1+|Dz|^2)^2$. The metric dominates the Euclidean plane metric, so it is complete by <upward stability of Riemannian completeness>. Its diameter is infinite since its distance dominates Euclidean distance. On a surface the <Ricci tensor> is $Kg$, so it is strictly positive everywhere, but has no uniform positive lower bound.

**There is no positive-Ricci metric on $S^2\times S^1$.** If one existed, <compactness> of the <unit tangent bundle> would give $\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}\geq cg$ for some $c>0$. The lifted metric on the <universal cover> is complete by Q2 and satisfies the same positive lower bound. Part (a) makes this cover <compact>. A covering fiber is closed and discrete and therefore finite, implying a finite <fundamental group>. But
$$
\pi_1(S^2\times S^1)\cong\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z,
$$
which is infinite. This is the <finite fundamental group from a uniform positive Ricci bound> obstruction; it rules out every possible metric, not just the standard product metric.

Parametrize the nonconstant <closed geodesic> at unit speed on $[0,L]$. Its <parallel transport> around the loop fixes the tangent $\dot\gamma(0)$. Since the manifold is orientable, it preserves orientation on the full <tangent space>, and hence has determinant one on the normal space. This normal space has odd dimension because the manifold has even dimension.

An <odd-dimensional special orthogonal transformation has a fixed vector>. Indeed, nonreal <eigenvalues> of a real orthogonal transformation occur in conjugate pairs with product one; the real <eigenvalues> are $\pm1$, and determinant one makes the number of $-1$ <eigenvalues> even. Odd dimension therefore requires a $+1$ <eigenvalue>. Transport a unit fixed normal vector around the <geodesic> to obtain a periodic parallel field $V$, with $D_tV=0$ and $V\perp\dot\gamma$.

The variation $\gamma_s(t)=\exp_{\gamma(t)}(sV(t))$ is defined uniformly for small $s$, because the image of the original loop is <compact>. Periodicity gives closed curves and a <homotopy> through loops. With the standard positive-sectional-curvature <Riemannian index form>, the <second variation of geodesic energy> is
$$
E''(0)=I(V,V)=\int_0^L\bigl(|D_tV|^2-K(\dot\gamma,V)\bigr)dt
=-\int_0^LK(\dot\gamma,V)\,dt<0.
$$
The first variation vanishes for a <closed geodesic>, since the endpoint terms cancel. Therefore $E(\gamma_s)<E(\gamma_0)=L/2$ for sufficiently small nonzero $s$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
\operatorname{length}(\gamma_s)^2\leq L\int_0^L|\dot\gamma_s|^2dt
=2LE(\gamma_s)<L^2.
$$
Thus **the <closed geodesic> is homotopic to a strictly shorter closed curve**. This proves the <instability of a closed geodesic in positive even-dimensional curvature>; completeness of the manifold is unnecessary for this compact-loop variation. Constant loops are excluded by the usual nonconstant meaning of a <closed geodesic> in this assertion.

Choose a finite symmetric generating set $S$ for $\Gamma$, let $B_S(m)$ be its <word metric> ball, and fix $p\in M$. Set $D=\max_{s\in S}d(p,sp)$. The <triangle inequality> and invariance of distance imply
$$
d(p,\gamma p)\leq Dm\qquad(\gamma\in B_S(m)).
$$
The <properly discontinuous group action> has a finite <point stabilizer> $H=\Gamma_p$. It also gives uniform separation of distinct orbit points. To see this, only finitely many <group> elements can send $p$ into a fixed <compact> ball about $p$, by proper discontinuity and the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>. The positive distances among this finite list, together with the radius of the ball for all other elements, have a positive lower bound $\delta$. Thus $d(\gamma p,\gamma'p)\geq\delta$ whenever the centers differ.

Take $0<\varepsilon<\delta/2$ and write $v=\operatorname{Vol}B(p,\varepsilon)>0$. Balls of radius $\varepsilon$ about distinct orbit points are disjoint and all have volume $v$, because the action is by <isometries>. Those corresponding to words of length at most $m$ lie in $B(p,Dm+\varepsilon)$. Each orbit point arises from at most $|H|$ <group> elements in $B_S(m)$, so
$$
\frac{|B_S(m)|}{|H|}\,v\leq\operatorname{Vol}B(p,Dm+\varepsilon)
\leq\omega_n(Dm+\varepsilon)^n.
$$
The final inequality is the allowed <Bishop-Gromov inequality> for nonnegative <Ricci curvature>. If the entire <group> fixes $p$, proper discontinuity already makes it finite and the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise the preceding separation argument applies. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|B_S(m)|\leq\frac{|H|\omega_n}{v}(Dm+\varepsilon)^n\leq C(1+m)^n.}
$$
This is <polynomial growth of a group> of degree at most $n$, proved by <polynomial group growth from orbit packing>. Neither freeness of the action nor <compactness> of the quotient was assumed.

Put $\theta(r)=\operatorname{Vol}B(p,r)/(\omega_nr^n)$. The <Bishop-Gromov inequality> says that $\theta$ is nonincreasing and at most one, while smoothness gives $\theta(r)\to1$ as $r\downarrow0$. The prescribed limit at infinity is also one. Hence $\theta(r)=1$ for every $r>0$.

Here is how equality gives the global <Euclidean rigidity of maximal asymptotic volume ratio>. Assume first $n\geq2$. In <geodesic polar coordinates> centered at $p$, let $c(\xi)$ be the cut time along unit direction $\xi$, and $J(r,\xi)$ the positive radial volume density before that time. The comparison giving the <Bishop-Gromov inequality> says $J(r,\xi)\leq r^{n-1}$, and
$$
\operatorname{Vol}B(p,R)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_0^{\min\{R,c(\xi)\}}J(r,\xi)\,dr\,d\xi.
$$
No cut time can be finite. If $c(\xi_0)<t$, then the radial segment at time $t$ is not minimizing, so $d(p,\exp_p(t\xi_0))<t$. This strict inequality persists for nearby directions by continuity, and their cut times are also at most $t$. At any radius $R>t$, that open set of directions omits all radial volume from $t$ to $R$. Even the maximal density $r^{n-1}$ cannot then give Euclidean volume, contradicting $\theta(R)=1$. Thus $c(\xi)=\infty$ for all directions. Equality of all ball volumes and continuity now force $J(r,\xi)=r^{n-1}$ everywhere.

Let $A(r)X=\nabla_X\partial_r$ on the distance sphere. This is the negative of the outward <shape operator> in the convention $S=-\nabla\nu$. Put $h=\operatorname{tr}A=\partial_r\log J$. The traced <radial Riccati equation for distance spheres> is
$$
h'+\operatorname{tr}(A^2)+\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\partial_r,\partial_r)=0.
$$
Since $h=(n-1)/r$, it gives
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^2)+\operatorname{Ric}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\partial_r,\partial_r)=\frac{n-1}{r^2}.
$$
But $\operatorname{tr}(A^2)\geq h^2/(n-1)=(n-1)/r^2$ by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, and the Ricci term is nonnegative. Equality therefore holds in both, so $A=r^{-1}I$. Writing the polar metric as $dr^2+g_r$, its evolution is $\partial_rg_r=2g_r/r$. Its small-radius Euclidean limit then gives
$$
g_r=r^2g_{S^{n-1}},\qquad g=dr^2+r^2g_{S^{n-1}}.
$$
The <Riemannian exponential map> is onto by the <Hopf-Rinow theorem>, has no conjugate points, and is injective because there is no finite cut time. Explicitly, two distinct minimizing radial <geodesics> reaching the same point would, after extending one past that point, create a length-minimizing broken curve with a genuine corner; smoothing the corner shortens it. That contradicts global minimization of every radial segment. Thus $\exp_p$ is a global <diffeomorphism>, and the displayed metric proves
$$
\boxed{(M,g)\cong(\mathbb R^n,g_{\mathrm{Euclidean}})\text{ isometrically}.}
$$
In dimension one, a <connected> complete manifold without boundary is a line or a circle. The circle has limiting volume ratio zero, so the hypothesis leaves only the Euclidean line.

Take the <orientation-preserving affine group of the real line>, written as
$$
G=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&1\end{pmatrix}:a>0,\ b\in\mathbb R\right\},\qquad
(a,b)(a',b')=(aa',b+ab').
$$
It is a <connected> <Lie group>, since its underlying manifold is $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$. We use the permitted alternative to part (a), a direct obstruction from the <adjoint representation of a Lie group>.

If a <bi-invariant Riemannian metric> existed, conjugation $h\mapsto ghg^{-1}$ would be an <isometry>, being a composition of a left and a right translation. Its differential at the identity would preserve the positive <inner product> on the <Lie algebra>. Let $T$ be the nonzero infinitesimal translation,
$$
T=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad g=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Direct multiplication gives $\operatorname{Ad}_gT=gTg^{-1}=2T$. Metric invariance would then imply $\|T\|^2=\|2T\|^2=4\|T\|^2$, impossible for a nonzero vector in a positive <inner product>. Therefore **this <connected> affine Lie <group> has no bi-invariant <Riemannian metric>**. This is the <adjoint dilation obstruction to a bi-invariant Riemannian metric>; allowing an indefinite metric would be a different question.
