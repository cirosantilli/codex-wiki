= Solution

Positive-energy one-particle <Poincare group> representations have labels **$(m,s)$** for $m>0$, and **$(0,h)$** for finite-helicity massless states; individual massive polarizations add $m_s=-s,\ldots,s$. An $\mathcal N=1$ <massive supermultiplet> is labelled by mass $m$ and <superspin> $j$, with constituent labels
$$
\boxed{(m,j),\ (m,j),\ (m,j+\tfrac12),\ (m,j-\tfrac12)\quad(j\ge\tfrac12);\qquad (m,0),(m,0),(m,\tfrac12)\quad(j=0).}
$$
A <massless supermultiplet> has constituent labels
$$
\boxed{(0,h),\ (0,h-\tfrac12).}
$$
A <CPT completion of a supermultiplet> additionally supplies $(0,-h),(0,-h+\tfrac12)$, with conjugated internal charges where applicable. Constituent spins/<helicities> are the labels here, not counts of polarizations.

= Solution
{parent=b}

TeV-scale softly broken <supersymmetry> addresses the <technical hierarchy problem>: equal bosonic and fermionic couplings cancel quadratic ultraviolet contributions to the Higgs mass. Soft mass splittings leave corrections of order $\delta m_H^2\sim g^2M_{\mathrm{soft}}^2\log(\Lambda/M_{\mathrm{soft}})/(16\pi^2)$, so a weak-scale soft spectrum protects the weak scale against a much higher cutoff. Unbroken four-dimensional <extended supersymmetry> is incompatible with the observed complex <chiral gauge spectrum>: charged <hypermultiplets> contain conjugate representations, while <vector multiplets> have real adjoint matter. It must therefore be broken to at most $\mathcal N=1$ before reproducing low-energy <Standard Model> chirality.

= Solution
{parent=c}

In a <supersymmetric Higgs mechanism>, an internal <gauge symmetry> breaks while <supersymmetry> can remain unbroken. A massless <vector multiplet> absorbs a <chiral multiplet> and becomes a massive multiplet with spins $(1,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$: the <gauge boson> acquires its longitudinal <particle polarization> from the Goldstone mode. In the <super-Higgs mechanism>, local <supersymmetry> itself breaks: the spin-$3/2$ <gravitino> absorbs the spin-$1/2$ <goldstino>, gaining two longitudinal polarizations and becoming massive. With only global <supersymmetry breaking>, the <goldstino> instead remains a physical massless <fermion>.

= Solution

Both <Kaluza-Klein theory> and the <brane-world scenario> use a higher-dimensional bulk. Ordinary Kaluza–Klein matter propagates in the compact directions, producing observable massive towers; absence of weak-scale charged modes requires roughly $R^{-1}\gtrsim\mathrm{TeV}$, or **$R\lesssim2\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm m$**, and traditional constructions often take Planck-size radii. Brane-world matter is localized while gravity explores the bulk, so large flat dimensions can reach the **submillimetre order of magnitude** discussed in the 2004 setting. For $n$ flat dimensions, $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2\sim M_D^{n+2}(2\pi R)^n$. These are model-dependent scales, not universal maxima: warped gravity-localizing models can have a noncompact extra dimension. The historical large-flat-dimension mechanism is described in https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9803315.

= Solution

Work with the local <Wilsonian effective action>, retaining the elementary <chiral superfield> and using a nonzero infrared cutoff. Its <superpotential> is holomorphic in both the field and chiral <spurions> representing the couplings. A convenient pair of <spurion> symmetries assigns charges
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
&\Phi&m&g&\theta&W\\\hline
U(1)&1&-2&-3&0&0\\
U(1)_R&3&-4&-7&1&2
\end{array}}
$$
Both couplings and $\Phi$ transform nontrivially under each displayed symmetry. Each <superpotential> monomial has ordinary charge zero and <R-charge> two, so the integral $\int d^2\theta\,W$ is invariant. This R assignment differs from the conventional $(1,0,-1)$ assignment for $(\Phi,m,g)$ by twice the ordinary charge, and describes the same two-dimensional charge space. These are formal <spurion> symmetries; fixing the couplings to numerical values need not leave them as actual symmetries of the fields alone.

For $m\ne0$, $m\Phi^2$ has the required charges and mass dimension three, while $z=g\Phi/m$ has zero charge under both symmetries and is dimensionless. Thus the allowed holomorphic form is
$$
\boxed{W_{\mathrm{eff}}=m\Phi^2F\left(\frac{g\Phi}{m}\right).}
$$
One can see its generality directly. A holomorphic monomial $\Phi^u m^v g^w$ must obey $u-2v-3w=0$ and $3u-4v-7w=2$. Solving gives $u=w+2$ and $v=1-w$, hence precisely $m\Phi^2(g\Phi/m)^w$. A field-independent constant is irrelevant to this global supersymmetric action and can be dropped.

The symmetries alone leave the function arbitrary. To determine its perturbative form, use regularity as well as the two limits. A regular weak-coupling expansion at $g=0$ has
$$
F(z)=\sum_{r\ge0}c_rz^r,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{r\ge0}c_r g^r m^{1-r}\Phi^{r+2}.
$$
The $g\to0$ limit is a free massive theory, so $c_0=1/2$. Since the elementary field has not been integrated out, the Wilsonian <superpotential> must remain regular term by term as $m\to0$ with the infrared cutoff fixed. Every term with $r\ge2$ would introduce a pole in $m$ and is excluded. Negative powers of $g$ were already excluded by perturbative regularity at $g=0$. Consequently $F=c_0+c_1z$.

At $m=0$, the only surviving allowed interaction is proportional to $g\Phi^3$. Its coefficient is first order in $g$, and matching the tree three-field vertex gives $c_1=1/3$. No closed-loop three-field diagram can be made from a single cubic vertex; extra interaction vertices would produce higher coupling order. Equivalently free superspace propagators join chiral to antichiral fields, so a closed loop would require additional vertices or conjugate couplings, which the holomorphic charge constraints exclude for this term. This fixes the coefficient without assuming the desired conclusion. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F(z)=\frac12+\frac z3,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{pert}}=\frac12m\Phi^2+\frac13g\Phi^3=W_{\mathrm{tree}}.}
$$
The regularity condition is essential: it concerns the local Wilsonian <superpotential> with the field retained, not a nonlocal one-particle-irreducible functional at zero infrared cutoff or a <superpotential> obtained by eliminating a massive field. Such different operations can generate inverse masses or infrared singularities without contradicting this proof.

The <Kähler potential> is a real <D-term>, not a holomorphic <F-term>, and is not protected by this argument. For example its quadratic part becomes $Z(g,\bar g,m,\bar m;\mu)\Phi^\dagger\Phi$, with higher real operators also allowed. Terms involving $|g|^2$ are compatible with the symmetries and produce <wave-function renormalization>. After the canonical rescaling $\Phi_c=Z^{1/2}\Phi$, the parameters in the <superpotential> expressed using canonical fields are $m_c=m/Z$ and $g_c=g/Z^{3/2}$, so physical couplings can run despite the holomorphic non-renormalization statement.

**<Holomorphy> is crucial.** Without it, neutral factors such as $1+c|g|^2$ could multiply either <superpotential> monomial while respecting both <spurion> symmetries. Excluding conjugate couplings, together with regularity and tree matching, is what turns the charge argument into the <non-renormalization theorem>.

For global four-dimensional $\mathcal N=1$ <supersymmetry>, use
$$
\{Q_\alpha,Q_\beta^\dagger\}=2\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu,\qquad
\{Q_\alpha,Q_\beta\}=0,\qquad [P_\mu,Q_\alpha]=0.
$$
Let $\Pi=(-1)^F$ be <fermion parity>. On a finite one-particle <supermultiplet> at fixed positive-energy momentum, the <supercharges> act within the multiplet and $\Pi Q_\alpha=-Q_\alpha\Pi$. Cyclicity of the finite-dimensional <trace> gives
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi Q_\alpha Q_\alpha^\dagger)
=\operatorname{Tr}(Q_\alpha^\dagger\Pi Q_\alpha)
=-\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi Q_\alpha^\dagger Q_\alpha).
$$
Thus the <trace> with $\Pi$ of every displayed diagonal <anticommutator> vanishes. Summing the two components, $\sum_\alpha\{Q_\alpha,Q_\alpha^\dagger\}=4E\mathbf1$, so
$$
0=4E\operatorname{Tr}\Pi=4E(N_B-N_F).
$$
Since $E>0$, this proves **$N_B=N_F$**, for both massive and massless one-particle multiplets. The restriction matters: a zero-momentum supersymmetric vacuum singlet, annihilated by every <supercharge>, need not contain a fermionic partner. The algebra does not imply equal counts for such a trivial zero-energy representation or for an arbitrary unregulated infinite-dimensional <trace>.

= Solution

The same <Super-Poincaré algebra> gives the operator identity
$$
H=\frac14\sum_{\alpha=1}^2\{Q_\alpha,Q_\alpha^\dagger\}.
$$
For a normalized physical state $|v\rangle$ in a positive-norm <Hilbert space>,
$$
\boxed{\langle H\rangle_v=\frac14\sum_{\alpha=1}^2\big(\|Q_\alpha v\|^2+\|Q_\alpha^\dagger v\|^2\big)\ge0.}
$$
An unbroken supersymmetric vacuum must be annihilated by every <supercharge> and its adjoint, hence has zero energy. Conversely, zero expectation of this positive sum forces each <norm> separately to vanish. Therefore a vacuum with **strictly positive energy necessarily breaks <supersymmetry>**. For an infinite-volume field theory the corresponding statement is about <vacuum energy density>, obtained with an appropriate finite-volume regulator. The zero of energy is fixed by the algebra; it cannot be arbitrarily shifted while preserving this identity. This is the global-supersymmetry result and does not assert positivity of the general local-supergravity scalar potential.

For the unheaded extended-algebra request, fix the central-charge normalization explicitly:
$$
\{Q_\alpha^A,(Q_\beta^B)^\dagger\}=2\delta^{AB}\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu,
\qquad
\{Q_\alpha^A,Q_\beta^B\}=2\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}Z^{AB},
\qquad Z^{AB}=-Z^{BA}.
$$
The central charges are scalar numbers on an irreducible massive representation. A <unitary skew-diagonalization of an antisymmetric matrix> puts $Z$ into two-by-two blocks $\begin{pmatrix}0&z_r\\-z_r&0\end{pmatrix}$ and, for odd $\mathcal N$, a leftover zero entry. Since this change of <supercharge> basis is unitary, it preserves the mixed <anticommutator>. Go to the rest frame $P^\mu=(M,0,0,0)$.

For one block set $q_\alpha=Q_\alpha^1$ and $r_\alpha=\epsilon_{\alpha\gamma}(Q_\gamma^2)^\dagger$, with $\epsilon_{12}=1$. Direct use of the algebra gives
$$
\{q_\alpha,q_\beta^\dagger\}=\{r_\alpha,r_\beta^\dagger\}=2M\delta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad
\{q_\alpha,r_\beta^\dagger\}=2z\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
$$
Write $z=|z|e^{i\varphi}$ and form the combinations suggested by the hint:
$$
A_\alpha=\frac{q_\alpha+e^{i\varphi}r_\alpha}{\sqrt2},\qquad
B_\alpha=\frac{q_\alpha-e^{i\varphi}r_\alpha}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Then
$$
\{A_\alpha,A_\beta^\dagger\}=2(M+|z|)\delta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad
\{B_\alpha,B_\beta^\dagger\}=2(M-|z|)\delta_{\alpha\beta},\qquad
\{A_\alpha,B_\beta^\dagger\}=0.
$$
Each diagonal <anticommutator> has nonnegative expectation because it is the sum of squared <norms> of an operator and its adjoint. Therefore $M-|z_r|\ge0$ for every block, proving
$$
\boxed{M\ge\max_r|z_r|.}
$$
If the algebra is instead written with $\{Q,Q\}=\epsilon Z$ without the factor two, the same proof gives $M\ge\tfrac12\max_r|Z_r|$. This is the normalization used by some definitions of the <BPS bound in supersymmetry>; the physical bound is unchanged by the naming of the central charge.

A massive <BPS state> saturates at least one block bound. At saturation $\{B_\alpha,B_\alpha^\dagger\}=0$, so both $B_\alpha$ and $B_\alpha^\dagger$ annihilate every state in its multiplet. The two complex inactive oscillators are four preserved real supersymmetries per saturated block. If $r$ blocks saturate, the preserved fraction is $r/\mathcal N$ and only $2\mathcal N-2r$ complex fermionic oscillators remain active. For a spin-$j$ <Clifford vacuum>,
$$
\boxed{\dim\mathcal H_{\mathrm{BPS}}=(2j+1)2^{\,2\mathcal N-2r},}
$$
compared with $(2j+1)2^{2\mathcal N}$ for a long massive multiplet. Thus <BPS states> have an exact mass-charge relation, preserve part of the <supersymmetry>, and belong to shortened representations. A physical CPT-invariant spectrum may additionally require the conjugate-charge multiplet. The short representation protects the saturated mass relation while it exists; it does not prohibit a decay at a threshold where central-charge phases align and the sum of daughter masses equals the bound.

= Solution

Here the sphere's “volume” is its intrinsic $(N-1)$-dimensional area, not the volume of the enclosed ball. Write $\Omega_{N-1}$ for the area of the unit sphere. The <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
I_N=\int_{\mathbb R^N}e^{-|x|^2}\,d^Nx=\left(\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-u^2}\,du\right)^N=\pi^{N/2}.
$$
For completeness the one-dimensional integral is positive and its square is $2\pi\int_0^\infty\rho e^{-\rho^2}d\rho=\pi$. In <hyperspherical coordinates>, the same integral is
$$
I_N=\Omega_{N-1}\int_0^\infty\rho^{N-1}e^{-\rho^2}\,d\rho
=\frac{\Omega_{N-1}}2\int_0^\infty u^{N/2-1}e^{-u}\,du
=\frac{\Omega_{N-1}}2\Gamma(N/2).
$$
The substitution is $u=\rho^2$, and the last integral defines the <Gamma function>. Equating the two evaluations and scaling lengths by $r$ proves
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{N-1}=\frac{2\pi^{N/2}}{\Gamma(N/2)},\qquad
V_{N-1}(r)=\frac{2\pi^{N/2}}{\Gamma(N/2)}r^{N-1}.}
$$

Take $D$ to mean spacetime dimension, so the number of spatial dimensions is $d=D-1$. This convention is needed when comparing five-dimensional spacetime with four-dimensional spacetime. For a point charge $q$, <Gauss's law> in $d$ spatial dimensions gives
$$
\Omega_{d-1}r^{d-1}E_r(r)=\frac q{\varepsilon_D}.
$$
Using $E_r=-\phi'(r)$ and choosing $\phi(\infty)=0$ for $d>2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\phi_D(r)=\frac q{\varepsilon_D(d-2)\Omega_{d-1}r^{d-2}}
=\frac{q\,\Gamma((D-1)/2)}{2(D-3)\pi^{(D-1)/2}\varepsilon_D\,r^{D-3}}\quad(D>3).}
$$
The <Green function of the Laplacian> with $-\nabla^2G_d=\delta^{(d)}$ is consequently $G_d(r)=1/[(d-2)\Omega_{d-1}r^{d-2}]$. Define the gravitational Poisson coupling $\kappa_D$ by $\nabla^2\Psi=\kappa_DM\delta^{(d)}$, and use acceleration $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Psi$. The same flux calculation gives
$$
\boxed{\Psi_D(r)=-\kappa_DM G_d(r)
=-\frac{\kappa_DM}{(D-3)\Omega_{D-2}r^{D-3}}.}
$$
This explicitly fixes the normalization of the gravitational coupling: in four dimensions $\kappa_4=4\pi G_4$, so $\Psi_4=-G_4M/r$. If a higher-dimensional Newton constant is instead defined by the force law $|\mathbf g|=G_DM/r^{D-2}$, then $\kappa_D=\Omega_{D-2}G_D$ in the displayed expression. In all conventions the force scales as $r^{-(D-2)}$ and the potential as $r^{-(D-3)}$. If “$D$ dimensions” denotes spatial dimensions instead, replace $d$ by $D$ and the potential exponent is $D-2$.

For $d=2$, integration gives $\phi=-q\log(r/r_0)/(2\pi\varepsilon_3)$ and $\Psi=\kappa_3M\log(r/r_0)/(2\pi)$ for the Poisson-normalized gravitational model; these logarithmic potentials have no zero-at-infinity convention. For $d=1$, a fundamental solution of the negative Laplacian is $G_1(x)=-|x|/2$, giving linear potentials up to an additive homogeneous solution.

Now compactify the fourth spatial coordinate $y$ on a circle of circumference $L=2\pi R$, retaining three noncompact coordinates with radial distance $r$. In uncompactified five-dimensional spacetime, $G_4(r,y)=1/[4\pi^2(r^2+y^2)]$. A periodic point source is therefore represented by its images:
$$
G_{\mathrm{circle}}(r,y)=\frac1{4\pi^2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac1{r^2+(y+nL)^2}.
$$
For $r/L\to\infty$, write the sum as $r^{-2}\sum_n[1+(nL/r)^2]^{-1}$ on the source slice $y=0$. The spacing $L/r$ tends to zero, so its Riemann sum approaches the integrable function's integral:
$$
\sum_n\frac1{r^2+n^2L^2}\sim\frac1{Lr}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{du}{1+u^2}=\frac\pi{Lr}.
$$
Thus the compactified Green function is asymptotic to $1/(4\pi Lr)$, already proving the four-dimensional inverse-distance law.

An exact expression makes the crossover transparent. The Fourier transform of $(r^2+y^2)^{-1}$ is $\pi e^{-r|k|}/r$, obtained by closing the integration contour around $y=ir$ or $-ir$. Applying the <Poisson summation formula> then gives, at $y=0$,
$$
\sum_n\frac1{r^2+n^2L^2}=\frac\pi{Lr}\left(1+2\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-2\pi nr/L}\right)
=\frac\pi{Lr}\coth\left(\frac{\pi r}L\right).
$$
The last equality sums a geometric series. Hence the <point-source potential with one compact spatial dimension> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(r,0)=\frac q{4\pi\varepsilon_5Lr}\coth\left(\frac{\pi r}L\right),\qquad
\Psi(r,0)=-\frac{\kappa_5M}{4\pi Lr}\coth\left(\frac{\pi r}L\right).}
$$
At short distances $r\ll L$, $\coth(\pi r/L)\sim L/(\pi r)$ and these reproduce the five-dimensional $1/r^2$ potentials. At long distances $r\gg L$, the hyperbolic factor is $1+O(e^{-2\pi r/L})$, giving the four-dimensional $1/r$ potentials with effective Poisson couplings $\varepsilon_4=L\varepsilon_5$ and $\kappa_4=\kappa_5/L$. Equivalently only the massless <Kaluza-Klein mode> survives at long distance, while the massive modes contribute exponentially suppressed terms.

= Solution

Start with the <Clifford algebra> $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}$. For the dimension count, complexify and rescale the timelike matrix so that the generators obey the Euclidean complex relation $\{\gamma_a,\gamma_b\}=2\delta_{ab}$. This changes neither the <complex representation> dimension nor its decomposition under the complexified even algebra. In $D=2k$, pair the generators into
$$
a_j=\frac{\gamma_{2j-1}+i\gamma_{2j}}2,\qquad
b_j=\frac{\gamma_{2j-1}-i\gamma_{2j}}2\quad(j=1,\ldots,k).
$$
Direct substitution gives $\{a_i,a_j\}=\{b_i,b_j\}=0$ and $\{a_i,b_j\}=\delta_{ij}$. The commuting operators $n_j=b_ja_j$ satisfy $n_j^2=n_j$, so their occupation <eigenvalues> are zero or one. An irreducible module contains an all-zero occupation state $|0\rangle$, obtained by lowering the occupied entries of a simultaneous <eigenvector>. The states
$$
b_1^{e_1}\cdots b_k^{e_k}|0\rangle,\qquad(e_1,\ldots,e_k)\in\{0,1\}^k,
$$
are nonzero and independent by their distinct joint occupation <eigenvalues>. They span an invariant module; irreducibility makes this the full module. Conversely this Fock construction supplies a representation on exactly these $2^k$ states. Products of occupation projectors and raising/lowering operators give its matrix units, so the generated algebra is the full <matrix algebra>. Therefore the minimal complex <Dirac spinor> has
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb C}S_{\mathrm{Dirac}}=2^k\quad(D=2k).}
$$

This module is irreducible under the full <Clifford algebra>, but the <spinor representation of the Lorentz group> is generated by the even bilinears $\Sigma^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac14[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]$. The <chirality matrix> in the Euclideanized basis is
$$
\gamma_*=i^{-k}\gamma_1\cdots\gamma_{2k},\qquad
\gamma_*^2=1,\quad\{\gamma_*,\gamma_a\}=0,\quad[\gamma_*,\Sigma^{\mu\nu}]=0.
$$
Its two eigenspaces therefore remain invariant under <Lorentz transformations>. Each has half the Fock states, since any <gamma matrix> bijects them. The even algebra connects every pair of occupation states of the same parity and has no further invariant subspaces within either one. Hence
$$
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{Dirac}}=S_+\oplus S_-,\qquad
\dim_{\mathbb C}S_\pm=2^{k-1}.}
$$
These are the irreducible <Weyl spinors>. Globally they are ordinary representations of $\operatorname{Spin}(D-1,1)$, the double cover; calling them representations of $SO(D-1,1)$ uses the conventional projective spinor terminology.

For $D=2k+1$, adjoin $\gamma_{2k+1}=\pm\gamma_*$. It anticommutes with the old matrices and gives a Clifford module of the same dimension $2^k$. The new even generators $[\gamma_a,\gamma_{2k+1}]$ interchange the two old chirality eigenspaces, so the odd-dimensional Lorentz spinor is **irreducible of complex dimension $2^k$**, with no Weyl splitting. The product of all odd-dimensional <gamma matrices> is central and acts as a scalar; it cannot be a new anticommuting chirality operator. Its two signs distinguish the two complex odd-Clifford modules, whose restrictions to <Spin group> are equivalent. <Majorana spinor> reality conditions, when the signature permits them, are additional conditions and should not be confused with complex chirality.

For the reduction, take the massless zero modes on a flat seven-torus with no flux or projections. The eleven-dimensional fields are the metric $G_{MN}$, a three-form $A_{MNP}$ and a Majorana <gravitino> $\Psi_M$. Split $M=(\mu,i)$ with $\mu=0,\ldots,3$ and $i=1,\ldots,7$. The metric supplies $G_{\mu\nu}$, seven vectors $G_{\mu i}$ and 28 <scalar fields> $G_{ij}=G_{ji}$. The three-form supplies a four-dimensional three-form $A_{\mu\nu\rho}$, seven two-forms $A_{\mu\nu i}$, $\binom72=21$ vectors $A_{\mu ij}$ and $\binom73=35$ scalars $A_{ijk}$.

A massless two-form in four dimensions has one physical <particle polarization> and can be dualized to a scalar by $H_3=*d\sigma$; the seven two-forms thus supply seven more scalars. The four-dimensional three-form has no local <particle polarization>, since its four-form field strength is locally a constant times the volume form. The total bosonic content is consequently
$$
\boxed{1\text{ graviton},\qquad28\text{ vectors},\qquad70\text{ real scalars}.}
$$
The eleven-dimensional <Majorana spinor> has 32 real components, decomposing into eight four-dimensional <Majorana spinors>. Accordingly $\Psi_\mu$ supplies eight <gravitini>, while $\Psi_i$ supplies $7\times8=56$ spin-$1/2$ fields after diagonalizing the <gravitino>/matter kinetic terms. The resulting theory has 32 real <supercharges>, hence is <four-dimensional N=8 supergravity>, with
$$
\boxed{8\text{ gravitini},\qquad56\text{ spin-}\tfrac12\text{ fermions}.}
$$
The on-shell counts check the reduction: the metric has $11(11-3)/2=44$ polarizations and the three-form has $\binom93=84$, totaling 128. In four dimensions $2+28\cdot2+70=128$ bosonic polarizations match $8\cdot2+56\cdot2=128$ fermionic ones. Higher Kaluza–Klein levels, fluxes or projections would be additional choices beyond this massless toroidal reduction.

= Solution

Work on the physical domain
$$
s=S+\bar S>0,\qquad t=T+\bar T-|C|^2>0,
$$
and abbreviate $A=ae^{-\alpha S}$, $W=C^3+A+b$, and $B=W-sW_S=C^3+b+(1+\alpha s)A$. There are no <vector multiplets> specified, hence no additional gauge <D-term>. In Planck units the <supergravity F-term potential> and upper-index <supergravity auxiliary fields> are
$$
V=e^K(K^{i\bar j}D_iW\,\overline{D_jW}-3|W|^2),\qquad
F^i=-e^{K/2}K^{i\bar j}\overline{D_jW},\qquad e^K=\frac1{st^3}.
$$
The overall auxiliary-field sign is a convention; its vanishing and <norm> are invariant statements.

The <Kähler covariant derivatives of a superpotential> here are
$$
D_SW=-\alpha A-\frac Ws=-\frac Bs,\qquad
D_TW=-\frac{3W}t,\qquad
D_CW=3C^2+\frac{3\bar C W}t.
$$
The <Kähler metric>, with rows $S,T,C$ and columns their barred partners, is
$$
K_{i\bar j}=\begin{pmatrix}
s^{-2}&0&0\\
0&3/t^2&-3C/t^2\\
0&-3\bar C/t^2&3/t+3|C|^2/t^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its $T,C$ block is positive definite because its leading diagonal and determinant $9/t^3$ are positive. In the inverse-tensor convention $K_{i\bar j}K^{k\bar j}=\delta_i^k$, the nonzero upper components are
$$
K^{S\bar S}=s^2,\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}K^{T\bar T}&K^{T\bar C}\\K^{C\bar T}&K^{C\bar C}\end{pmatrix}
=\frac t3\begin{pmatrix}t+|C|^2&\bar C\\C&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
In particular the conjugations in the off-diagonal components matter.

To verify the <no-scale supergravity> cancellation explicitly, temporarily write $u=D_TW=-3W/t$ and $v=D_CW=W_C+3\bar CW/t$. Their part of the metric contraction is
$$
\frac t3\{(t+|C|^2)|u|^2+\bar C\,u\bar v+C\,v\bar u+|v|^2\}
=3|W|^2+\frac t3|W_C|^2.
$$
The terms involving $C W_C\bar W$ and its conjugate cancel between the mixed and diagonal contributions. The remaining $3|W|^2$ cancels the universal negative term. Since $W_C=3C^2$, the exact <matter-logarithm no-scale potential> is therefore
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{|C^3+b+(1+\alpha s)ae^{-\alpha S}|^2}{st^3}
+\frac{3|C|^4}{st^2}\ge0.}
$$
It differs from a model where $|C|^2$ appears outside the logarithm in the <Kähler potential>.

Substitution into the auxiliary-field formula gives
$$
\boxed{F^S=e^{K/2}s\bar B,\qquad
F^T=e^{K/2}t(\bar A+\bar b),\qquad
F^C=-e^{K/2}t\bar C^2.}
$$
For example $F^T=e^{K/2}t(\bar W-\bar C\,\overline{W_C}/3)$, which reduces to the displayed expression for the cubic <superpotential>. To test unbroken <supersymmetry> at any finite point, $F^C=0$ first forces $C=0$. Then $F^T=0$ forces $A+b=0$, whereas $F^S=0$ forces $(1+\alpha s)A+b=0$. Subtracting gives $\alpha sA=0$. Because $s>0$, $\alpha>0$ and $e^{-\alpha S}\ne0$ at a finite point, all auxiliaries can vanish only if **$a=b=0$**. Thus every nontrivial choice of $(a,b)$ breaks <supersymmetry> at finite field values.

A finite zero-energy minimum requires both nonnegative terms of $V$ to vanish:
$$
\boxed{C=0,\qquad b+(1+\alpha s)ae^{-\alpha S}=0.}
$$
There cannot be a finite positive-energy stationary point: keeping $S,C$ fixed and varying the real part of $T$, equivalently $t$, gives
$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}=-\frac{3|B|^2}{st^4}-\frac{6|C|^4}{st^3},
$$
which is strictly negative unless $B=C=0$. Hence these zero-energy solutions are all the possible finite minima.

Because $a,b$ were allowed to be arbitrary, existence must be checked. If both are nonzero, put $x=\alpha s>0$ and $r=|b/a|$. The magnitude equation is
$$
r=(1+x)e^{-x/2}.
$$
The function on the right starts at one at the excluded boundary $x=0$, tends to zero as $x\to\infty$, and has derivative $\tfrac12(1-x)e^{-x/2}$. Its maximum is $2e^{-1/2}$ at $x=1$. Therefore a finite <cubic-matter no-scale vacuum with an exponential dilaton> exists exactly when
$$
\boxed{a,b\ne0\quad\text{and}\quad0<|b/a|\le2e^{-1/2}.}
$$
There is one finite positive root for $0<r\le1$, two for $1<r<2e^{-1/2}$, and one merged root at the maximum. The complex phase equation fixes $\operatorname{Im}S$ modulo $2\pi/\alpha$. If exactly one of $a,b$ vanishes, or the ratio exceeds the bound, no finite minimum exists; the potential instead has infimum zero along the runaway $t\to\infty$. A zero infimum at an infinite-field boundary is not an attained finite vacuum.

On a nontrivial finite branch, $W=A+b=-\alpha sA\ne0$, so
$$
\boxed{V_{\min}=0,\qquad F^S=F^C=0,\qquad
F^T=e^{K/2}t\bar W\ne0.}
$$
This proves <supersymmetry breaking> at the intended no-scale vacuum. Its zero energy is consistent with the negative gravitational term: $K_{T\bar T}|F^T|^2=3e^K|W|^2$, which cancels $3e^K|W|^2$ in $V$. It is not a counterexample to the global-supersymmetry positivity argument in question 3.

Both real components of $T$ are exactly flat on the vacuum family: $\operatorname{Im}T$ never enters the potential, and at $B=C=0$ every allowed positive $\operatorname{Re}T$ gives the same zero energy. Generically both components of $S$ are fixed. Although the matter field has no quadratic mass there, it is not an exact flat direction: for $S$ fixed at its root, $B=C^3$ and the leading positive potential is $3|C|^4/(st^2)$. At the special merged root $x=1$, the real-$S$ quadratic curvature also vanishes, but higher orders lift it; this does not add a continuous flat vacuum direction. If $a=b=0$, by contrast, $C=0$ is a supersymmetric zero-energy family with both complex $S$ and $T$ flat and all auxiliaries zero. This exception prevents an unconditional breaking assertion for arbitrary constants.

Finally the <gravitino mass from a superpotential> is
$$
\boxed{m_{3/2}=e^{K/2}|W|=\frac{|C^3+ae^{-\alpha S}+b|}{\sqrt{st^3}}.}
$$
At a nontrivial finite minimum it becomes
$$
\boxed{m_{3/2}=\frac{\alpha\sqrt s\,|ae^{-\alpha S}|}{t^{3/2}}
=\frac{\alpha\sqrt s\,|b|}{(1+\alpha s)t^{3/2}}>0.}
$$
Its value varies along the flat volume modulus; the classical potential does not select a unique supersymmetry-breaking or gravitino-mass scale. On the trivial supersymmetric branch $a=b=C=0$, it is zero.
