The <Frattini subgroup> is the intersection of the <maximal subgroups> of $G$, with $\Phi(1)=1$. A useful equivalent property is
$$
H\Phi(G)=G\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H=G\qquad(H\le G).
$$
Indeed, a proper <subgroup> $H$ lies in a <maximal subgroup>, which also contains $\Phi(G)$. The <Frattini subgroup> is a <characteristic subgroup>, since <automorphisms> permute the <maximal subgroups>.

The <Fitting subgroup> is the largest <nilpotent normal subgroup>. To prove its existence, first form the <p-core> $O_p(G)$: the product of all normal <p-subgroups>. For two such <subgroups>, their product is normal and has order $|AB|=|A||B|/|A\cap B|$, a power of $p$. There are only finitely many <subgroups>, so $O_p(G)$ exists. For distinct primes $p,r$,
$$
[O_p(G),O_r(G)]\le O_p(G)\cap O_r(G)=1.
$$
Thus the product of the <p-cores> is a <nilpotent normal subgroup>, a <direct product of groups> of its <Sylow subgroups>. Conversely, a finite <nilpotent normal subgroup> $K$ has unique <Sylow subgroups>, each a <characteristic subgroup> of $K$. Each is normal in $G$ and lies in the corresponding <p-core>. Consequently **the required largest <subgroup> exists**, and
$$
\boxed{F(G)=\prod_{p\mid |G|}O_p(G).}
$$

Put $\Phi=\Phi(G)$ and choose a <Sylow subgroup> $P$ of $\Phi$. Since $\Phi$ is normal, the <Frattini argument> gives $G=\Phi N_G(P)$: for $g\in G$, the <Sylow subgroups> $P^g$ and $P$ of $\Phi$ are conjugate by an element of $\Phi$, leaving an element of $N_G(P)$. The non-generator property of the <Frattini subgroup> proved above forces $N_G(P)=G$. Thus every <Sylow subgroup> of $\Phi$ is normal. Distinct such <Sylow subgroups> commute because their <group commutators> lie in their trivial intersection. Hence $\Phi$ is a <nilpotent normal subgroup>, and
$$
\boxed{\Phi(G)\le F(G).}
$$

Write $F=F(G)$, $C=C_G(F)$ and $D=C\cap F=Z(F)$. Both $C$ and $D$ are normal in $G$. Suppose $C>D$. Choose a <minimal normal subgroup> $K/D$ of $G/D$ contained in $C/D$. In a finite <soluble group>, such a <minimal normal subgroup> is <elementary abelian>: the last nontrivial term of its <derived series> is an abelian <characteristic subgroup>, so minimality makes the whole <subgroup> abelian; its primary components and <subgroup> of elements killed by $p$ then force one prime and exponent $p$.

It follows that $K'\le D$. Moreover $K\le C$ centralizes $D\le F$, so $K'\le Z(K)$. Therefore $K$ is a <nilpotent group> of class at most two. Since $K$ is normal in $G$, the definition of the <Fitting subgroup> gives $K\le F$. But then $K\le C\cap F=D$, a contradiction. This proves the <Fitting subgroup is self-centralizing in soluble groups>:
$$
\boxed{C_G(F(G))\le F(G),\qquad C_G(F(G))=Z(F(G)).}
$$

It suffices to centralize each <minimal normal subgroup> $M$. The <subgroup> $M\cap F(G)$ is normal in $G$, so it is either $1$ or $M$. In the first case
$$
[M,F(G)]\le M\cap F(G)=1.
$$
In the second case $M$ is a <nilpotent group>. Its nontrivial <group center> is a <characteristic subgroup> of $M$, hence normal in $G$, so minimality makes $M$ abelian. The primary components, which are <characteristic subgroups>, and the <subgroup> killed by a prime show that $M$ is <elementary abelian> of exponent $p$. Thus $M\le O_p(G)$.

The <finite p-group> $O_p(G)$ acts by <conjugation> on $M$. Its orbits have prime-power sizes; hence the number of fixed elements is congruent to $|M|=0$ modulo $p$. The identity is fixed, so there is a nonidentity fixed element. Therefore $M\cap Z(O_p(G))\ne1$. This intersection is normal in $G$, and minimality makes it $M$. The other <p-cores> commute with $O_p(G)$, and hence with $M$. Thus every factor of $F(G)$ centralizes $M$. Since the <group socle> is generated by these <minimal normal subgroups>,
$$
\boxed{F(G)\le C_G(\operatorname{Soc}(G)).}
$$

Put $\Phi=\Phi(G)$ and let $H$ be the full preimage of $F(G/\Phi)$ in $G$. The preceding part gives $\Phi\le F(G)$, so the normal nilpotent <image> $F(G)/\Phi$ lies in $F(G/\Phi)$.

For the reverse inclusion, choose a <Sylow subgroup> $P$ of $H$. Its <image> $P\Phi/\Phi$ is the unique <Sylow subgroup> of the <nilpotent group> $H/\Phi$, hence a <characteristic subgroup> there and normal in $G/\Phi$. Thus $P\Phi\trianglelefteq G$. The <subgroup> $P$ is also a <Sylow subgroup> of $P\Phi$: the p-part of $\Phi$ lies in $P$, because $P\cap\Phi$ is a <Sylow subgroup> of the <normal subgroup> $\Phi$ of $H$. Apply the <Frattini argument> to $P\Phi$:
$$
G=(P\Phi)N_G(P)=\Phi N_G(P).
$$
The <Frattini subgroup> property forces $N_G(P)=G$. Hence every <Sylow subgroup> of $H$ is normal, so $H$ is a <nilpotent group> and $H\le F(G)$. Together the inclusions prove
$$
\boxed{F(G/\Phi(G))=F(G)/\Phi(G).}
$$
This is <Frattini lifting of nilpotence>; simply calling an extension of <nilpotent groups> nilpotent would not establish it.

A <soluble group> is a <group> whose <derived series> $G^{(0)}=G$, $G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]$ terminates at $1$. Equivalently, it has a finite <subnormal series> with abelian factors. For the reverse implication, if $G/N$ has derived length $r$, then $G^{(r)}\le N$; if $N$ has derived length $s$, then $G^{(r+s)}=1$. Iterating this observation through the series proves the equivalence. For a finite <group>, refining the abelian factors to <composition factors> says that solubility is equivalent to all <composition factors> being cyclic of prime order.

<Subgroups> and <quotient groups> of <soluble groups> are soluble, since their <derived series> lie in, or are images of, the original series. The same calculation proves closure under <group extensions>. <Finite p-groups> are <nilpotent groups>, hence soluble: their nontrivial centers allow induction to construct a <central series>. Every finite <nilpotent group> is a <direct product of groups> of its <Sylow subgroups>. The converse fails: $S_3$ has the abelian-factor series $1<C_3<S_3$, but its order-two <Sylow subgroups> are not normal. The <symmetric group> $S_4$ is also soluble, with series $1<V_4<A_4<S_4$ and abelian factors. A finite <soluble group> has elementary abelian <minimal normal subgroups>, by the argument in 1(c). In contrast, a nonabelian <simple group> is a <perfect group> and is not soluble.

The <Fitting subgroup> collects the normal nilpotent structure. For a nontrivial finite <soluble group> it is nontrivial, since it contains an elementary abelian <minimal normal subgroup>. Part 1(c) proves $C_G(F)\le F$, so <conjugation> embeds $G/C_G(F)$ in $\operatorname{Aut}(F)$. This shows why controlling a <soluble group> through normal abelian or nilpotent layers is effective, even when the whole <group> is not a <nilpotent group>.

For a set of primes $\pi$, a <Hall subgroup> of type $\pi$ has order divisible only by primes in $\pi$ and index divisible only by primes outside $\pi$. The <Hall theorem for soluble groups> says that a finite <soluble group> has such <subgroups>, any two are conjugate, and every <subgroup> of $\pi$-order is contained in one. These assertions generalize the <Sylow theorems>, which treat a single prime. Here is a proof of all three assertions.

First we need <coprime splitting over an elementary abelian normal subgroup>. Suppose $V\trianglelefteq E$ is an elementary abelian p-group and $Q=E/V$ has order $h$ prime to $p$. A section $s:Q\to E$ defines an action on $V$ and an additive factor set $f(x,y)$ by $s(x)s(y)=f(x,y)s(xy)$. Associativity says
$$
f(x,y)+f(xy,z)=x f(y,z)+f(x,yz).
$$
Summing over $z\in Q$ and dividing by $h$ in $V$ gives $f(x,y)=b(x)+xb(y)-b(xy)$, where $b(x)=h^{-1}\sum_zf(x,z)$. Replacing $s(x)$ by $-b(x)s(x)$ removes the factor set, giving a complement. Two complements differ by a map $d$ satisfying $d(xy)=d(x)+xd(y)$. Averaging over $y$ gives $d(x)=b-xb$, so the complements are conjugate by an element of $V$. This proves both splitting and conjugacy, without assuming that an arbitrary extension splits.

Induct on $|G|$, taking an elementary abelian <minimal normal subgroup> $V$ of exponent $p$. Choose a quotient <Hall subgroup> $\bar H\le G/V$ and let $K$ be its preimage. If $p\in\pi$, then $K$ itself is a <Hall subgroup>. If $p\notin\pi$, the preceding splitting argument gives a complement $H$ to $V$ in $K$, and that complement is a <Hall subgroup> of $G$.

For conjugacy, the images of two <Hall subgroups> are quotient <Hall subgroups>, so induction allows them to have the same <image> and lie in the same $K$. If $p\in\pi$, every <Hall subgroup> contains $V$: its product with $V$ is a $\pi$-subgroup and cannot be larger by the subgroup-order formula. Both are then $K$. If $p\notin\pi$, both are complements to $V$ in $K$, so the averaging argument conjugates them.

Finally let $U\le G$ be any $\pi$-subgroup. By induction its <image> lies in a quotient <Hall subgroup>, so $U\le K$. If $p\in\pi$, $K$ is already a <Hall subgroup> containing $U$. Otherwise write $K=V\rtimes H$. In $VU$, both $U$ and the inverse <image> in $H$ of the <image> of $U$ are complements to $V$. Complement conjugacy therefore places $U$ in a conjugate of $H$. This proves embedding and completes the induction.

**Thus existence, conjugacy and containment all hold for every prime set in a finite <soluble group>.** The solubility hypothesis matters: $A_5$ has no <Hall subgroup> for $\pi=\{2,5\}$. Such a <subgroup> would have index three; its coset action would give a nontrivial <group homomorphism> $A_5\to S_3$, impossible because $A_5$ is a <simple group> and has order $60$. This example also distinguishes a <Hall subgroup> from a merely arbitrary <subgroup> whose order uses the chosen primes.

Since the <two-transitive group action> is primitive, the orbits of any nontrivial <normal subgroup> form only the single block $\Omega$. Thus $N$ is transitive. If $N$ is regular, identify $\Omega$ with $N$ by $x\mapsto x\alpha$. Then $G=NG_\alpha$, $N\cap G_\alpha=1$, and <conjugation> by $G_\alpha$ gives <automorphisms> of $N$. The <two-transitive group action> makes these <automorphisms> transitive on $N\setminus\{1\}$. All nonidentity elements therefore have the same order, which must be a prime $p$: a suitable power of an element of composite order would have a smaller nontrivial order. <Cauchy's theorem for finite groups> now shows $|N|=p^d$.

The <center of a group> of prime-power order is nontrivial and is a <characteristic subgroup>. The transitive <conjugation> action on nonidentity elements therefore forces $Z(N)=N$. Hence $N$ is an <elementary abelian group>, which we identify with the additive <vector space> $\mathbb F_p^d$. <Conjugation> by $G_\alpha$ is linear on this <vector space>. It is faithful: an element fixing $\alpha$ and centralizing $N$ fixes every $x\alpha$, hence is the identity permutation. Consequently
$$
\boxed{n=p^d,\qquad G=N\rtimes G_\alpha\le AGL_d(p).}
$$
Here the <general affine group> acts by translations followed by invertible <linear maps>.

The orbits of $N_\alpha\trianglelefteq G_\alpha$ on $\Omega\setminus\{\alpha\}$ have a common size $r$, because $G_\alpha$ is transitive there. Suppose $N$ has a <block system> of block size $b$, $1<b<n$. Since $r\mid n-1$ and $b\mid n$, $\gcd(r,b)=1$. Let $B$ be the block of $\alpha$ and $C\ne B$ another block. The union $N_\alpha C$ is a union of blocks and of r-element point-orbits, so its size is divisible by $br$; it is at most $br$. Equality means each of the b point-orbits meets $C$ once. Thus $N_{\alpha\beta}$ fixes $C$ pointwise for $\beta\in C$. Interchanging $\alpha,\beta$ also makes it fix $B$. All two-point stabilizers have order $|N_\alpha|/r$. Therefore $N_{\alpha\beta}=N_{\alpha\gamma}$ for every $\gamma\in B\setminus\{\alpha\}$. Varying $\beta$ outside $B$ shows this same <subgroup> fixes every point, so it is trivial. We have proved <uniform subdegrees force an imprimitive action to be Frobenius>: no nonidentity element of $N$ fixes two points.

Write $|N_\alpha|=h$. The distinct <point stabilizers> meet only at $1$, and $|N|=nh$. Thus the set $D$ of fixed-point-free elements of $N$ has size
$$
|D|=nh-\bigl(1+n(h-1)\bigr)=n-1.
$$
Count triples $(\alpha,\beta,x)$ with $x\in D$ and $x\alpha=\beta$: there are $n(n-1)$, exactly the number of ordered distinct pairs. <Conjugation> by the <two-transitive> <group> makes every pair occur, hence each pair has a unique such $x$. It follows that $G$ conjugates all elements of $D$ transitively.

Also $h\mid n-1$, since $N_\alpha$ acts freely on the remaining points. For each prime $p\mid n$, <Cauchy's theorem for finite groups> supplies an order-p element of $N$; it cannot fix a point, since $p\nmid h$. Conjugacy of $D$ forces all these primes to coincide, so $n=p^d$ and every element of $D$ has order $p$. A <Sylow subgroup> $P$ of $N$ has order $n$, because $p\nmid h$. Every nonidentity element of $P$ is fixed-point-free, so $P=\{1\}\cup D$. This uniquely described <subgroup> is normal in $G$. Minimal normality of $N$ gives $P=N$, and $|N|=n$. **Therefore $N$ is regular.** The proof establishes closure through a <Sylow subgroup>; it does not assume that a set of derangements is automatically a <subgroup>.

A finite <minimal normal subgroup> is a <characteristically simple group>, and has the <direct-product structure of a finite minimal normal subgroup>
$$
N=T_1\times\cdots\times T_k
$$
with isomorphic <simple groups> $T_i$. For completeness, choose a minimal nontrivial <normal subgroup> $T$ of $N$. Its distinct $G$-conjugates are <minimal normal subgroups> of $N$, so they centralize one another. Their product is normal in $G$, hence equals $N$. If $T$ is abelian the product is abelian and minimal primary <characteristic subgroups> make $N$ elementary abelian. Otherwise each $T$ has trivial center: its center is a <characteristic subgroup> of $T$, hence normal in $N$, and minimality would make $T$ abelian. Each new conjugate then meets the product of previous conjugates trivially, because it centralizes that product and is centerless. The product is direct. Every <normal subgroup> of a direct factor is normal in $N$, so minimality of that factor makes it simple.

Assume now that $N$ acts primitively and nonregularly. Any nontrivial <normal subgroup> of this faithful primitive action is transitive. Thus each $T_i$ is transitive. If $k\ge2$, the transitive commuting <groups> $T_1$ and $T_2$ are regular: an element of one fixing $\alpha$ commutes with the other and hence fixes its entire orbit. But the <centralizer> of a regular <group> has order $n$, as its elements are determined by the <image> of $\alpha$. Therefore all remaining factors lie in the order-n <centralizer> of $T_1$, and the transitive <group> $T_2$ already fills it. There can be only two factors, with $|N|=n^2$. Now $|N_\alpha|=n$, whereas its common nontrivial subdegree $r$ divides both $n$ and $n-1$. Hence $r=1$, making $N_\alpha$ trivial, a contradiction. Thus $k=1$, and $N$ is simple. An abelian transitive <group> is regular, so $N$ is nonabelian.

Finally $C_G(N)$ is normal in the primitive <group> $G$. If nontrivial it would be transitive, and commuting with transitive $N$ would force $N$ regular. Thus $C_G(N)=1$, and <conjugation> embeds $G$ in $\operatorname{Aut}(N)$. The centerless simple <group> $N$ is identified with its <inner automorphism group>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{N\text{ is nonabelian simple},\qquad N\le G\le\operatorname{Aut}(N).}
$$

By 3(a), identify $\Omega$ with $V=\mathbb F_p^d$ and let $H=G_0\le GL_d(p)$. Four-transitivity means $H$ is transitive on ordered triples of distinct nonzero <vectors>, and in particular on ordered pairs of distinct nonzero <vectors>.

If $p>2$ and $d\ge2$, the pairs $(u,au)$ with $a\ne0,1$ and $(u,v)$ with $u,v$ <linearly independent> cannot be interchanged by a <linear map>. Thus $d=1$ if $p>2$. Then $|H|\le p-1$, whereas pair-transitivity on the $p-1$ nonzero points requires $(p-1)(p-2)\le |H|$. This forces $p\le3$, incompatible with four-transitivity, whose degree is at least four.

Hence $p=2$. If $d\ge3$, the triples $(u,v,u+v)$ and $(u,v,w)$ for <linearly independent> $u,v,w$ cannot be interchanged: the first is <linearly dependent> and the second is not. Thus $d=2$. The <general affine group> $AGL_2(2)$ has order $4(4-1)(4-2)=24$. Four-transitivity on four points requires $24\mid |G|$, and $G\le AGL_2(2)\le S_4$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{n=4,\qquad G=AGL_2(2)\cong S_4.}
$$

For $i\ne j$, put $x_{ij}(a)=I+aE_{ij}$. When $a\ne0$ this is a <transvection>: it fixes the <hyperplane> $v_j=0$ pointwise and has rank-one difference from the identity. Its inverse is $x_{ij}(-a)$ and its <determinant> is one.

Left multiplication performs a row addition. For an invertible <matrix>, bring a nonzero entry into a pivot position by a signed interchange, then clear its column by row additions. A signed interchange on two coordinates is itself a product of row additions, because
$$
w(a)=x_{12}(a)x_{21}(-a^{-1})x_{12}(a)
=\begin{pmatrix}0&a\\-a^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad(a\ne0).
$$
Repeating the pivot construction and then clearing above the pivots reduces any determinant-one <matrix> to a diagonal <matrix> of <determinant> one. Finally
$$
w(a)w(-1)=\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1}).
$$
A determinant-one diagonal <matrix> is a product of such two-coordinate matrices, using the last coordinate to compensate each of the first $n-1$ diagonal entries. Each displayed <matrix> is a product of <elementary transvection matrices>. Hence reversing the elimination proves
$$
\boxed{SL_n(F)=\langle x_{ij}(a):i\ne j,\ a\in F\rangle.}
$$
This argument works over every <field>, including <characteristic> two.

Let $S=SL_n(F)$ and let $Z$ be its scalar center. Its action on the lines of $F^n$ has <kernel> exactly $Z$: a <linear map> fixing every line is diagonal in a basis, and fixing the lines spanned by $e_i+e_j$ makes all its diagonal entries equal. The action is two-transitive. Indeed an ordered pair of distinct lines can be represented by two independent <vectors> and extended to a basis. A <linear map> between two such bases can be adjusted to <determinant> one by scaling one target basis <vector>; this preserves both target lines. Therefore $S/Z$ has a faithful <primitive group action>.

Fix $L=Fv$. The maps
$$
A_L=\{I+vf:f\in(F^n)^*,\ f(v)=0\}
$$
form an abelian <subgroup>: all products of the rank-one terms vanish. It is normal in the line stabilizer, since <conjugation> replaces $v$ by a <scalar multiple> and transports $f$. Its conjugates contain every <transvection>, and hence generate $S$ by 4(b). The same statements hold for its <image> in $S/Z$.

It remains to prove perfectness rather than assume it. Use the <group commutator> convention $[x,y]=xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$. If $n\ge3$, distinct $i,j,k$ give
$$
[x_{ik}(a),x_{kj}(b)]=x_{ij}(ab).
$$
Every elementary generator is consequently in $S'$. If $n=2$ and $|F|>3$, choose $a\in F^*$ with $a^2\ne1$. There are at most two roots of $a^2=1$, so such a choice exists. For $h=\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1})$,
$$
[h,x_{12}(b)]=x_{12}((a^2-1)b).
$$
Varying $b$ gives every upper <transvection>; conjugating by $w(1)$ gives every lower one. Again $S'=S$, and its projective quotient is a <perfect group>.

Apply 4(a) to this faithful primitive projective action. Every nontrivial <normal subgroup> contains the <commutator subgroup>, which is the whole quotient. Thus
$$
\boxed{PSL_n(F)\text{ is simple if }n\ge3\text{ or if }n=2,\ |F|>3.}
$$
The restriction is real: $PSL_2(2)\cong S_3$ and $PSL_2(3)\cong A_4$ are not simple.

Replace $F^n$ by a <symplectic vector space> $(V,B)$ of dimension $2m$, and elementary row operations by <symplectic transvections>
$$
T_{v,c}(x)=x+cB(x,v)v.
$$
The cross terms in $B(Tx,Ty)$ cancel, and $B(v,v)=0$, so these maps preserve $B$. Their inverses are $T_{v,-c}$.

They generate $Sp(V)$ by the following <symplectic basis> elimination. If $B(u,v)\ne0$, a single <transvection> with direction $v-u$ and parameter $1/B(u,v)$ sends $u$ to $v$. If $B(u,v)=0$ and $u\ne v$, choose $w$ with both $B(u,w)$ and $B(w,v)$ nonzero: the union of two proper <hyperplanes> does not exhaust a <vector space>, even over $\mathbb F_2$. Two <transvections> then suffice. Thus one can make a form-preserving <linear map> fix the first basis <vector> $e$. Next align its partner $f$, while keeping $e$ fixed. Two partners have difference in $e^\perp$, so the preceding single-transvection construction uses a direction in $e^\perp$ whenever their mutual pairing is nonzero. If it is zero, the intermediate partner $f+ae$, $a\ne0$, has nonzero pairing with both. All these <transvections> fix $e$. Once both $e,f$ are fixed, recurse on their <nondegenerate> <symplectic orthogonal complement>. This proves generation over every <field>.

Use the action on the points of <projective space>, whose <kernel> is the scalar center $Z=\{\lambda I:\lambda^2=1\}$. For $m=1$ this is the $SL_2$ case. For $m\ge2$ the action is primitive, although it is generally not two-transitive. The stabilizer of a line $L$ has two orbits on other lines: those orthogonal to $L$ and those not orthogonal to $L$. This follows by extending a <hyperbolic pair>, or an independent isotropic pair, to a <symplectic basis>. A block containing $L$ and a second line $M$ contains the entire corresponding orbit of the stabilizer of $L$. The stabilizer of $M$ then supplies a line of the other type: choose a <vector> orthogonal to one of $L,M$ and not to the other. Nondegeneracy and independence guarantee such a <vector>, also in dimension four. Hence the block contains both orbits and is the whole set.

The <transvections> along $L$ form an abelian normal <subgroup> of its stabilizer; their conjugates generate the <group>. Thus the <Iwasawa simplicity lemma> applies as soon as perfectness is established. For $|F|>3$, every <transvection> lies in an embedded $SL_2(F)$ acting on a hyperbolic plane, and 4(c) proves that embedded <group> perfect.

For the remaining fields, the <perfectness of finite symplectic groups> has a short direct proof. Over $\mathbb F_3$ with $m\ge2$, all parameter-one <transvections> have the same class $t$ in the <abelianization>, and $3t=0$. In an isotropic plane with basis $u,v$, the four directions $u,v,u+v,u-v$ give commuting <transvections> whose product is $1$: their rank-one terms add to zero, since
$$
uu^{\mathsf T}+vv^{\mathsf T}+(u+v)(u+v)^{\mathsf T}+(u-v)(u-v)^{\mathsf T}=0
$$
over $\mathbb F_3$. Thus $4t=0$, giving $t=0$. Parameter-two <transvections> are inverses, so all generators vanish in the <abelianization>. Over $\mathbb F_2$ with $m\ge3$, use the seven nonzero <vectors> of an isotropic three-space. Their commuting <transvections> multiply to $1$, since each diagonal coordinate of $\sum_{0\ne v}vv^{\mathsf T}$ occurs four times and each off-diagonal coordinate twice. Their common abelianized class satisfies $2t=7t=0$, and again the <group> is perfect.

Consequently **the projective <symplectic group> is simple**, with the necessary exceptions
$$
\boxed{PSp_{2m}(F)\text{ simple, except }(m,F)=(1,\mathbb F_2),(1,\mathbb F_3),(2,\mathbb F_2).}
$$
The first two are the linear exceptions; the last is $Sp_4(2)\cong S_6$, proved in 6(c). The word “projective” matters: the unquotiented <symplectic group> may have the nontrivial scalar center $\{I,-I\}$.

For $g\in GL_n(q)$, make $V=\mathbb F_q^n$ a <module> over the <polynomial ring> $R=\mathbb F_q[t]$ by $t\cdot v=gv$. The <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> and <primary decomposition> give
$$
V\cong\bigoplus_{f\ne t}\bigoplus_{j\ge1}\bigl(R/(f^j)\bigr)^{m_j(f)}.
$$
Here $f$ ranges over <monic polynomials> that are <irreducible polynomials>, and $t$ is excluded because $g$ is invertible. Associate the <integer partition> $\lambda_f$ having $m_j(f)$ parts equal to $j$. It has finite support and satisfies $\sum_f\deg(f)|\lambda_f|=n$. Two matrices are conjugate exactly when their R-modules are isomorphic, so this gives a bijective parameterization of <conjugacy classes>.

The <centralizer> is the R-module <automorphism> <group>. Homomorphisms between distinct primary components vanish: coprimeness of their annihilating <polynomials> makes each such map zero. Fix one $f$ of degree $d$, put $Q=q^d$, and write $m_j=m_j(f)$. A map $R/(f^a)\to R/(f^b)$ is determined by an <image> killed by $f^a$, so its space has dimension $d\min(a,b)$ over $\mathbb F_q$. Consequently the <endomorphism> algebra $E$ has
$$
|E|=Q^{\sum_{a,b}\min(a,b)m_am_b}=Q^{\sum_i(\lambda'_i)^2},
$$
where $\lambda'$ is the <conjugate partition>.

Its semisimple quotient is $\prod_jM_{m_j}(\mathbb F_Q)$: reduce the maps between the equal-length summands modulo $f$ and discard the maps between unequal lengths. Composition through a different length acquires a factor of $f$ when it returns to the original length, so these reductions define an algebra homomorphism. It is onto, and its <kernel> is nilpotent. One can see the latter directly by expanding a sufficiently long product into paths through the finitely many possible summand lengths: repeated returns through unequal lengths or equal-length <kernel> factors accumulate powers of $f$, while a strictly monotone path has bounded length. Eventually every path vanishes in every summand.

An element is invertible exactly when its <image> in that quotient is invertible, since a nilpotent error is inverted by a finite <geometric series>. The fraction of units is therefore $\prod_j|GL_{m_j}(Q)|/Q^{m_j^2}=\prod_j\prod_{r=1}^{m_j}(1-Q^{-r})$. Multiplying the independent primary contributions gives the <primary matrix centralizer formula>:
$$
\boxed{|C_{GL_n(q)}(g)|=\prod_{f\ne t}q^{\deg(f)\sum_i(\lambda'_{f,i})^2}\prod_{j\ge1}\prod_{r=1}^{m_j(f)}(1-q^{-r\deg(f)}).}
$$

The <generating function> for <integer partitions> is $\sum_\lambda u^{|\lambda|}=\prod_{i\ge1}(1-u^i)^{-1}$, since independently choosing the multiplicity of each part size gives a <geometric series>. Applying the parameterization in 5(a),
$$
\sum_{n\ge0}k_n(q)t^n=\prod_{f\ne t}\prod_{i\ge1}(1-t^{i\deg(f)})^{-1}.
$$
Unique factorization of <monic polynomials> shows
$$
\prod_{f\ne t}(1-u^{\deg(f)})^{-1}
=1+\sum_{r\ge1}(q-1)q^{r-1}u^r
=\frac{1-u}{1-qu}.
$$
Indeed the left side counts <monic polynomials> with nonzero constant term; in degree $r\ge1$ there are $(q-1)q^{r-1}$ choices. Reordering the formal products is legitimate because only finitely many factors contribute to any fixed coefficient. Substituting $u=t^i$ for each $i$ gives the <conjugacy-class generating function for finite general linear groups>:
$$
\boxed{k_n(q)=[t^n]\prod_{i\ge1}\frac{1-t^i}{1-qt^i}.}
$$
The constant term corresponds to the trivial zero-dimensional <group>.

Translation $X\mapsto I+X$ is a <bijection> from <nilpotent matrices> to <unipotent matrices>. We can count the former without requiring a further partition identity. Let $a_n$ be their number, with $a_0=1$.

Every <linear operator> $T$ has a unique decomposition from the <Fitting lemma>
$$
V=\ker T^n\oplus\operatorname{im}T^n,
$$
on which it is respectively nilpotent and invertible. To justify the decomposition, kernels and images stabilize by step $n$; if $T^nv\in\ker T^n$, then $v\in\ker T^{2n}=\ker T^n$, so $T^nv=0$. The dimensions then show the sum is all of $V$, and stabilization makes $T$ invertible on the <image>. These two summands are determined by $T$, so there is no overcounting.

For a nilpotent summand of dimension $k$, the number of ordered complementary subspaces is $|GL_n(q)|/(|GL_k(q)||GL_{n-k}(q)|)$, by transporting two fixed bases. The restrictions of $T$ have $a_k$ and $|GL_{n-k}(q)|$ choices. Summing all $q^{n^2}$ <matrices> gives
$$
\frac{q^{n^2}}{|GL_n(q)|}=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{a_k}{|GL_k(q)|}.
$$
Write $\phi_n(q^{-1})=\prod_{i=1}^n(1-q^{-i})$, so $|GL_n(q)|=q^{n^2}\phi_n(q^{-1})$. Subtract the same equation for $n-1$:
$$
\frac{a_n}{|GL_n(q)|}=\frac1{\phi_n(q^{-1})}-\frac1{\phi_{n-1}(q^{-1})}
=\frac{q^{-n}}{\phi_n(q^{-1})}.
$$
Thus <counting nilpotent matrices by the Fitting decomposition> proves
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{unipotent elements of }GL_n(q)\}=a_n=q^{n^2-n}.}
$$
The supplied product identity is consistent with the resulting mass series: taking its variables to be $s=u$, $t=q^{-1}$ gives $\sum_na_nu^n/|GL_n(q)|=\prod_{i\ge1}(1-uq^{-i})^{-1}$.

For the fixed degree-two <irreducible polynomial> $f$, a <matrix> with <minimal polynomial> a power of $f$ has only one primary component. Its <conjugacy classes> correspond to <integer partitions> $\lambda$ of $m$. By the <primary matrix centralizer formula>, its <centralizer> has exactly the same order $c_\lambda(q^2)$ as the <centralizer> of the corresponding <unipotent matrix> in $GL_m(q^2)$.

Sum the <conjugacy class> sizes:
$$
\#\{g\in GL_{2m}(q):\mu_g=f^a\text{ for some }a\ge1\}
=|GL_{2m}(q)|\sum_{\lambda\vdash m}\frac1{c_\lambda(q^2)}.
$$
The same sum times $|GL_m(q^2)|$ is the number of <unipotent matrices> of $GL_m(q^2)$, which 5(c) shows is $(q^2)^{m^2-m}$. Hence <counting matrices with a fixed primary polynomial> gives
$$
\boxed{q^{2m^2-2m}\frac{|GL_{2m}(q)|}{|GL_m(q^2)|}.}
$$
In particular the exponent $a$ of the minimal polynomial is not fixed in advance: summing all <integer partitions> includes every possible largest block size. The usual statement takes $m\ge1$; dimension zero can instead be included with its identity convention.

Let $V$ have dimension $2m$ over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_q$, with a <nondegenerate> <alternating bilinear form> $B$. The <symplectic group over a finite field> is
$$
Sp(V,B)=\{g\in GL(V):B(gx,gy)=B(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\}.
$$
It acts simply transitively on ordered <symplectic bases>: a <linear map> between two such bases is uniquely specified and preserves all pairings, hence the whole <bilinear form>.

Count a first <hyperbolic pair> $(e_1,f_1)$. There are $q^{2m}-1$ choices for $e_1\ne0$. Nondegeneracy makes $B(e_1,-)$ a nonzero <linear functional>, so there are $q^{2m-1}$ choices of $f_1$ with $B(e_1,f_1)=1$. Their span is <nondegenerate>, and its <symplectic orthogonal complement> is a <symplectic vector space> of dimension $2m-2$. Recursing, with $|Sp_0(q)|=1$, yields
$$
\boxed{|Sp_{2m}(q)|=\prod_{i=1}^m(q^{2i}-1)q^{2i-1}
=q^{m^2}\prod_{i=1}^m(q^{2i}-1).}
$$
The construction also proves the existence of the required <symplectic bases>, in <characteristic> two as well as odd <characteristic>.

Represent a subset by the coordinate <vector> of its <indicator function> in $\mathbb F_2^n$. <Symmetric difference> then becomes coordinatewise addition, and the proposed pairing is the <dot product>
$$
B(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^nx_iy_i.
$$
It is a <symmetric bilinear form>, and it is <nondegenerate> because its <Gram matrix> in the singleton basis is the identity. Coordinate <permutations> preserve the sum, so the natural $S_n$ action preserves the <bilinear form>.

Let $j=(1,\ldots,1)$, the <vector> representing the full set. Then
$$
W=\langle j\rangle^\perp=\{x:\sum_ix_i=0\}
$$
is the even-weight subspace. For even $n$, $B(j,j)=n=0$ in $\mathbb F_2$, so $j\in W$. Moreover $B(x,x)=\sum_ix_i^2=\sum_ix_i=0$ for $x\in W$, making the restricted <bilinear form> alternating. Nondegeneracy on the ambient space implies $W^\perp=\langle j\rangle$, so the radical of $B|_W$ is exactly
$$
W\cap W^\perp=\langle j\rangle.
$$
Thus it descends to an <alternating bilinear form> on $W/\langle j\rangle$, independent of the representatives, and that quotient form has zero radical. **The induced form is <nondegenerate>**, and its dimension is $n-2$. This is the <symplectic quotient of the binary subset module>; at $n=2$ it is the zero-dimensional <nondegenerate> space.

Take $n=6$ in 6(b). The <symplectic quotient of the binary subset module> has dimension four, so the action gives a <group homomorphism>
$$
S_6\longrightarrow Sp_4(2).
$$
It is injective. If $\sigma$ acts trivially on the quotient, every two-element subset $A$ has $\sigma A+A\in\{\varnothing,\Omega\}$. Hence $\sigma A$ equals $A$ or its complement. The latter has size four, whereas $\sigma A$ has size two; therefore $\sigma$ fixes every two-element subset. Given $i$, choose distinct $j,k\ne i$. It then fixes the intersection $\{i,j\}\cap\{i,k\}=\{i\}$, so it fixes each point and is the identity.

Finally 6(a) gives
$$
|Sp_4(2)|=2^4(2^2-1)(2^4-1)=16\cdot3\cdot15=720=|S_6|.
$$
An injective <group homomorphism> between these <finite groups> of the same order is surjective. Thus
$$
\boxed{Sp_4(2)\cong S_6.}
$$
