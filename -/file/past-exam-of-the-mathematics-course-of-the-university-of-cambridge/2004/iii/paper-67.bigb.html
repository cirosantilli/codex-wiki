= Solution
{parent=a}

Take $c=1$ and normalize the <Big Bang> to $t=0$. The <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> for <pressureless matter> makes $C=8\pi G\rho_m a^3/3$ a positive constant. The flat <Friedmann equation> consequently reads
$$
\dot a^2=\frac Ca+\frac{\Lambda a^2}{3}.
$$
Putting $u=a^{3/2}$ gives $\dot u^2=\tfrac94(C+\Lambda u^2/3)$, which is directly integrable. The expanding <flat dust universe with a cosmological constant> has
$$
\boxed{
 a(t)=\begin{cases}
 (3C/\Lambda)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}(\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t/2),&\Lambda>0,\\
 (9C/4)^{1/3}t^{2/3},&\Lambda=0,\\
 (3C/|\Lambda|)^{1/3}\sin^{2/3}(\sqrt{3|\Lambda|}\,t/2),&\Lambda<0.
 \end{cases}}
$$
For negative <cosmological constant>, the last expression describes expansion and contraction on $0<t<2\pi/\sqrt{3|\Lambda|}$. Its maximum is $a_m=(3C/|\Lambda|)^{1/3}$ at $t_m=\pi/\sqrt{3|\Lambda|}$; it reaches zero again at $2t_m$. Positive or zero <cosmological constant> gives unbounded expansion and no finite maximum. All three have <infimum> zero at their initial singularity; negative <cosmological constant> also gives zero at the final singularity, with no positive minimum.

For positive <cosmological constant>, expanding the hyperbolic sine shows $a\sim(9C/4)^{1/3}t^{2/3}$ at early times: <pressureless matter> dominates. At late times,
$$
a\sim\left(\frac{3C}{4\Lambda}\right)^{1/3}e^{\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,t},\qquad H\longrightarrow\sqrt{\Lambda/3}.
$$
Thus the expansion changes from matter-dominated deceleration to exponential acceleration. Indeed $\ddot a/a=-C/(2a^3)+\Lambda/3$ changes sign at $a^3=3C/(2\Lambda)$.

Normalize today's <scale factor> to $a_0=1$ and use $c=1$. A source of bolometric <luminosity> $L$ emits <photons> whose <energies> arrive smaller by $1+z$, and whose arrival intervals are longer by $1+z$. Their wavefront has physical area $4\pi a_0^2r_1^2$. The received <radiative flux> is therefore
$$
F=\frac{L}{4\pi a_0^2r_1^2(1+z)^2}.
$$
By the definition of <luminosity distance>, $F=L/(4\pi d_L^2)$, so $d_L=(1+z)a_0r_1=r_1/a_{\rm em}$. The final equality assumes the stated normalization of the <scale factor>; it is not a formula invariant under an arbitrary rescaling of $a_0$.

A radial <null geodesic> of the <FRW metric> obeys $dt/a=dr/\sqrt{1-Kr^2}$. Using $1+z=1/a$ and $dz/dt=-(1+z)H(z)$ gives the radial <comoving distance>
$$
\chi(z)=\int_{t_{\rm em}}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a(t)}
=\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')}
=\int_0^{r_1}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{1-Kr^2}}.
$$
For $K>0$ this last integral is $\arcsin(\sqrt K r_1)/\sqrt K$, continued along the radial coordinate when necessary; for $K<0$ it is $\operatorname{arsinh}(\sqrt{|K|}r_1)/\sqrt{|K|}$. Inverting, with $\Omega_K=-K/H_0^2$, proves
$$
\boxed{d_L(z)=\frac{1+z}{H_0\sqrt{|\Omega_K|}}\,
S_K\!\left(H_0\sqrt{|\Omega_K|}\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')}\right).}
$$
Here $S_K$ is sine for positive <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>, hyperbolic sine for negative <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>, and its argument for zero <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>. The flat case is the continuous limit $d_L=(1+z)\chi$, not a literal division by zero. The original PDF has $H_0\sqrt{|\Omega_K|}$ in the denominator and $H=\dot a/a$; the converted TeX loses these details.

The <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> gives $\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$. In a flat universe the <Friedmann equation> then gives $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^\alpha$, where $\alpha=3(1+w)/2>0$. Writing $u=1+z$, the <angular diameter distance> obtained from the <luminosity distance> is
$$
d_A=\frac1{H_0u}\int_1^u v^{-\alpha}\,dv
=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{u^{-\alpha}-u^{-1}}{H_0(1-\alpha)},&\alpha\ne1,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{\log u}{H_0u},&\alpha=1.
\end{cases}
$$
It is positive for $u>1$, starts at zero and tends to zero as $u\to\infty$ for every $\alpha>0$, including $\alpha<1$ where the radial <comoving distance> itself is unbounded. Differentiation gives the <constant-equation-of-state angular diameter distance maximum>:
$$
\boxed{z_m=\alpha^{1/(\alpha-1)}-1,\quad \alpha=\frac32(1+w),\qquad z_m=e-1\text{ when }\alpha=1.}
$$
For $\alpha\ne1$ the stationary equation is $u^{\alpha-1}=\alpha$, which has exactly one solution greater than one. For $\alpha=1$ the <derivative> has the sign of $1-\log u$. Hence this stationary point is always the unique maximum.

To determine the sketch's shape, differentiate $\log(1+z_m)=\log\alpha/(\alpha-1)$:
$$
\frac{d\log(1+z_m)}{d\alpha}
=\frac{1-1/\alpha-\log\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^2}<0,
$$
with limiting value $-1/2$ at $\alpha=1$. The inequality follows because $\log\alpha-1+1/\alpha$ has its unique minimum zero at one. Thus $z_m$ decreases from infinity as $w\downarrow-1$ to $\sqrt3-1$ as $w\uparrow1$. Useful points are $z_m=e-1$ at $w=-1/3$, $5/4$ at $w=0$, and $1$ at $w=1/3$. The endpoint $w=-1$ is excluded: there $d_A=z/[H_0(1+z)]$ increases to an asymptote and has no finite turning point.

Normalize the region's reference <scale factor> to one. <Pressureless matter> has $\rho=\rho_0a^{-3}$, so the closed <Friedmann equation> is
$$
\dot a^2=H_0^2\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{a}-(\Omega_m-1)\right).
$$
The expanding branch can reach only $a\le a_m$, where **$a_m=\Omega_m/(\Omega_m-1)$**. Substitute $a=a_m(1-\cos\theta)/2$. Since $da/d\theta=a_m\sin\theta/2$ and $\dot a=H_0\sqrt{(\Omega_m-1)(a_m/a-1)}$, cancellation on $0<\theta<\pi$ gives
$$
\frac{dt}{d\theta}=\frac{\Omega_m}{2H_0(\Omega_m-1)^{3/2}}(1-\cos\theta).
$$
Integration from the <Big Bang> establishes
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{\Omega_m}{2(\Omega_m-1)}(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
H_0t=\frac{\Omega_m}{2(\Omega_m-1)^{3/2}}(\theta-\sin\theta).}
$$
The same parametrization continues through <spherical-collapse turnaround> at $\theta=\pi$ onto the contracting branch up to $\theta=2\pi$. Its maximum-expansion time is $t_m=\pi\Omega_m/[2H_0(\Omega_m-1)^{3/2}]$.

= Solution

The outer factor is explicitly $a_{\rm bg}/a_m=\tfrac14(6\pi t/t_m)^{2/3}$, the normalized <scale factor> of an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, with the same enclosed mass and initial bang time as the region. The correction describes a fractional reduction of the region's radius relative to this background:
$$
\frac{a_{\rm lin}}{a_{\rm bg}}=1-\epsilon(t),\qquad
\epsilon(t)=\frac1{20}\left(6\pi\frac{t}{t_m}\right)^{2/3}.
$$
For fixed mass, <density> varies as inverse radius cubed, so $\rho_{\rm region}/\rho_{\rm bg}=(1-\epsilon)^{-3}=1+3\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$. The growing linear <density contrast> is therefore
$$
\delta_{\rm lin}(t)=\frac3{20}\left(6\pi\frac{t}{t_m}\right)^{2/3},\qquad
\boxed{\delta_{\rm lin}(t_m)=\frac3{20}(6\pi)^{2/3}\simeq1.062.}
$$
The full <spherical collapse> reverses its expansion at $t_m$ and reaches zero radius at $t=2t_m$, corresponding to $\theta=2\pi$. In the ideal cold, pressureless model this makes the nonlinear <density contrast> infinite. Extrapolating the linear growing mode to that same time instead gives the finite <linear spherical-collapse threshold>
$$
\boxed{\delta_{\rm lin}(2t_m)=\delta_c=\frac3{20}(12\pi)^{2/3}\simeq1.686,\qquad
\delta_{\rm nonlin}(2t_m)\longrightarrow+\infty.}
$$
This is an extrapolation of a linear perturbation, not an accurate finite-radius approximation at the moment of collapse. <Virial equilibrium> requires relaxation of the cold coherent motion and replaces the ideal singular endpoint.

The regional <mass density> is $3H_0^2\Omega_m/(8\pi Ga^3)$, while the <Einstein-de Sitter universe> has $\rho_{\rm bg}=1/(6\pi Gt^2)$. Substituting the parametric <spherical collapse> from part (a) yields
$$
1+\delta_{\rm nonlin}=\frac{\rho_{\rm region}}{\rho_{\rm bg}}
=\frac92\frac{(\theta-\sin\theta)^2}{(1-\cos\theta)^3}.
$$
At <spherical-collapse turnaround>, $\theta=\pi$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\delta_{\rm nonlin}(t_m)=\frac{9\pi^2}{16}-1\simeq4.552.}
$$
For an isolated uniform sphere, the <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> is $U=-3GM^2/(5R)$. At <spherical-collapse turnaround> the coherent <kinetic energy> is zero, so <conservation of energy> fixes $E=U_m$. At <virial equilibrium>, the <virial theorem> gives $2T_v+U_v=0$, hence $E=U_v/2$. Equating these <energies> proves the <virial radius from turnaround energy>:
$$
\boxed{R_v=R_m/2.}
$$
This uses the same mass and gravitational structure coefficient before and after collapse and neglects boundary pressure, mass loss and external <energy> exchange.

If <virial equilibrium> is assigned to $2t_m$, the sphere's <density> is eight times its turnaround <density>, whereas the background <mass density> has decreased by four. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta_v\equiv\frac{\rho_v}{\rho_{\rm bg}(2t_m)}=18\pi^2\simeq177.65,
\qquad\delta_v=18\pi^2-1\simeq176.65.}
$$
The often quoted value $18\pi^2$ is a <density> ratio; the <density contrast> defined as $(\rho-\rho_{\rm bg})/\rho_{\rm bg}$ is smaller by one. This finite virialized estimate and the infinite cold-collapse contrast in part (b) describe different physical endpoints.

First fix a genuine normalization error in the original PDF: the Gaussian exponent uses $\sigma^2$, so $\sigma$ must be the <standard deviation>, the <variance> is $\sigma^2$, and the normalized <probability density function> is
$$
p_M(\delta)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma(M,z)}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta^2}{2\sigma^2(M,z)}\right).
$$
The printed prefactor has only $\sigma$ under its square root; its integral is $\sqrt{\sigma}$ rather than one. Use the normalized <normal distribution> above.

The one-scale probability of exceeding the <linear spherical-collapse threshold> is $P_M=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}[\delta_c/(\sqrt2\sigma)]$. In the <Press-Schechter formalism>, the mass fraction above $M$ is assigned to
$$
F(>M,z)=2P_M=\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\delta_c}{\sqrt2\sigma(M,z)}\right).
$$
The factor two is necessary to recover all the mass in the hierarchical limit $\sigma\to\infty$: the uncorrected one-point upper tail tends to only one half. A precise justification is available in the <excursion-set description of halo formation> with a <sharp-k smoothing filter>. The smoothed <Gaussian random field> then becomes <Brownian motion> in its variance $S$. Reflecting a trajectory after its first crossing of $\delta_c$ pairs trajectories which crossed but end below the threshold with those which end above it. The <Brownian reflection principle> therefore gives $\Pr(\max_{s\le S}\delta(s)>\delta_c)=2\Pr(\delta(S)>\delta_c)$.

This multiscale mass assignment is an extra modelling prescription: a Gaussian one-point probability and a collapse threshold alone do not mathematically determine a halo mass function. The “all matter” normalization additionally assumes arbitrarily large small-scale variance; a finite variance cutoff leaves an uncollapsed fraction.

Differentiate at fixed $z$ and fixed $\delta_c$:
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial M}
=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{\delta_c}{\sigma^2}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta_c^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial M}.
$$
For decreasing $\sigma(M,z)$, a mass interval contains the positive fraction $-F'(M)\,dM$. Its comoving mass is $\rho_{m,0}[-F'(M)]\,dM$, so division by halo mass gives the <Press-Schechter halo mass function>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{dn}{dM}=\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{\rho_{m,0}}M
\frac{\delta_c}{\sigma^2(M,z)}\left|\frac{\partial\sigma(M,z)}{\partial M}\right|
\exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta_c^2}{2\sigma^2(M,z)}\right).}
$$
The absolute value, or equivalently a minus sign multiplying $\partial_M\sigma$, is essential for a positive abundance. The original PDF correctly places absolute values around the abundance and the full <derivative> expression; these bars are lost in the converted TeX. The <density> is comoving, so the prefactor uses today's matter <mass density>, rather than the physical <density> at redshift $z$.

In the <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution>, each Cartesian component has mean-square velocity $k_BT_e/m_e$. Thus $\langle v^2\rangle=3k_BT_e/m_e$. The original PDF calls this quantity an rms velocity, but the rms speed is its square root. Also, its $n_\gamma$ must mean the dimensionless mean <occupation number> $n$, since its equilibrium value is a <Planck photon distribution>; it is not the frequency-integrated <photon number density>.

Take $x=h\nu/(k_BT_\gamma)$ at a fixed local radiation temperature. Because $x$ is proportional to <frequency>,
$$
\nu^2\partial_\nu^2n+4\nu\partial_\nu n
=x^2\partial_x^2n+4x\partial_xn
=x^{-2}\partial_x(x^4\partial_xn).
$$
The hot-electron <Kompaneets equation> therefore becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial n}{\partial y}=x^{-2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(x^4\frac{\partial n}{\partial x}\right),\qquad
dy=\frac{k_BT_e}{m_ec^2}n_e\sigma_Tc\,dt.}
$$
For traversal length $d\ell=c\,dt$, the <Compton y parameter> is
$$
\boxed{y=\frac{\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}\int n_ek_BT_e\,d\ell
=\frac{\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}\int P_e\,d\ell.}
$$
It measures electron <pressure> integrated along the <line of sight>, or the <Thomson scattering> <optical depth> weighted by $k_BT_e/(m_ec^2)$. In this hot-electron limit the mean fractional <energy> gain is approximately $4y$. The derivation assumes nonrelativistic electrons, Thomson-limit photon <energies> and $T_e\gg T_\gamma$. The original PDF uses $h\nu$, as required for ordinary <frequency>; the converted TeX incorrectly substitutes $\hbar\nu$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $n=n_0+y\mathcal L n_0+O(y^2)$, with $n_0=(e^x-1)^{-1}$ and $\mathcal L=x^{-2}\partial_x(x^4\partial_x)$. Differentiating the <Planck photon distribution> gives
$$
n_0'=-\frac{e^x}{(e^x-1)^2},\qquad
\mathcal L n_0=\frac{x e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}
\left[x\frac{e^x+1}{e^x-1}-4\right].
$$
Dividing by $n_0$ proves the first-order <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta n}{n_0}
=y\frac{x e^x}{e^x-1}\left[x\coth(x/2)-4\right].}
$$
In the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> limit, $xe^x/(e^x-1)\to1$ and $x\coth(x/2)\to2$, so **$\Delta n/n_0\to-2y$**. In the <Wien approximation>, the prefactor approaches $x$ and the bracket approaches $x-4$, so **$\Delta n/n_0\sim yx(x-4)\sim yx^2$**. The last limit is an asymptotic description of the first-order coefficient: a perturbative prediction at large $x$ still requires $yx^2\ll1$.

Hot electrons transfer <energy> to the <cosmic microwave background>, removing low-frequency <photons> and enhancing the high-frequency tail. The first-order distortion crosses zero where $x\coth(x/2)=4$, at $x\simeq3.830$; for today's <CMB> this is near $217\,\mathrm{GHz}$. Photon conservation follows directly from the boundary term
$$
\int_0^\infty x^2\Delta n\,dx=y[x^4n_0']_0^\infty=0.
$$
In contrast, <integration by parts> gives $\int x^3\Delta n\,dx=4y\int x^3n_0\,dx$, so radiation gains a fraction $4y$ of its original <energy>. The <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> is thus a spectral distortion, not simply a new <blackbody> temperature.

A <galaxy cluster> with bulk <peculiar velocity> along the <line of sight> additionally produces the <kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>. Define $v_\parallel>0$ for recession and $\tau=\int n_e\sigma_Td\ell$. To first order in velocity and <optical depth>, $\Delta T/T=-\tau v_\parallel/c$: receding gas gives a decrement and approaching gas an increment. Its <occupation number> perturbation is $(\Delta T/T)xe^x/(e^x-1)^2$, the spectrum of a small <blackbody> temperature change. It remains nonzero at the thermal null and therefore complicates measurements near that <frequency>.

There is a genuine flux typo in the original PDF. A received bolometric <radiative flux> must have inverse-square <luminosity distance>, so the physically consistent <thermal bremsstrahlung> expression is $S_X\propto d_L^{-2}\int n_e^2T_e^{1/2}\,dV$. The printed single power of $d_L$ cannot imply the requested <Hubble constant> scaling.

For an approximately uniform, spherical <galaxy cluster> let its depth be $\ell=q\theta d_A$, where $q$ is a fixed geometrical factor. With fixed profile coefficients $A,B$, the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> limit of the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> and the bolometric X-ray <radiative flux> give
$$
D\equiv|\Delta T_\gamma|=A n_eT_e\ell,\qquad
S_X=B\frac{n_e^2T_e^{1/2}\ell^3}{d_L^2}.
$$
Eliminate the electron <number density> and use $d_L=(1+z)^2d_A$:
$$
S_X=\frac B{A^2}\frac{D^2\ell}{T_e^{3/2}d_L^2}
=\frac{Bq}{A^2}\frac{D^2\theta}{T_e^{3/2}(1+z)^4d_A}.
$$
The <cluster distance from Sunyaev-Zeldovich and X-ray signals> is consequently
$$
\boxed{d_A=\frac{Bq}{A^2}\frac{D^2\theta}{S_XT_e^{3/2}(1+z)^4}.}
$$
At low <cosmological redshift>, $d_A\simeq cz/H_0$, so
$$
\boxed{H_0\simeq\frac{c z A^2}{Bq}\frac{S_XT_e^{3/2}(1+z)^4}{D^2\theta}
\ \propto\ (\Delta T_\gamma)^{-2}}
$$
when the other observables and profile parameters are held fixed. With the PDF's literal $d_L^{-1}$ instead, $S_X/D^2\propto\ell/(T_e^{3/2}d_L)=q\theta/[T_e^{3/2}(1+z)^2]$, independent of distance: this explicitly shows why the correction is necessary.

Real <galaxy clusters> are neither uniform nor generally spherical. The inferred depth depends on the ratio of <line of sight> depth to transverse extent; elongation and orientation selection therefore bias the distance. <density> clumps enhance X-ray emission, which weights $n_e^2$, more than the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>, which weights $n_e$. An unmodelled <gas clumping factor> greater than one makes the inferred distance too small and the inferred <Hubble constant> too large in this simple model. <Temperature> gradients and multiple thermal components bias the X-ray spectral temperature relative to the pressure-weighted <Compton y parameter>. Metals alter the cooling spectrum, and finite X-ray bands require a spectral and redshift correction to the bolometric approximation. Relativistic thermal corrections, the <kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>, radio sources and primary <cosmic microwave background anisotropy> contaminate the signal. Finally, calibration, angular-profile fitting and low-redshift <peculiar velocities> affect the observables and the relation between measured redshift and expansion distance.

Define neutral-hydrogen <column density> along a ray by $N=\int n_{\rm HI}\,d\ell$, in atoms per unit area. The <neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution> per redshift is
$$
f_z(N,z)=\frac{\partial^2\mathcal N}{\partial N\,\partial z},
$$
so $f_z\,dN\,dz$ is the expected number of absorbers in those intervals along one sightline. A logarithmic column bin instead has distribution $N\ln(10)f_z$; the bin convention must be specified.

Let $n_c$ be a constant <comoving number density> and $\sigma_p$ a constant physical interception area. The corresponding physical <number density> is $n_p=n_c(1+z)^3$. Proper light-path length satisfies $d\ell=c|dt|=c\,dz/[(1+z)H(z)]$. The expected intersection count, or first-order probability in infinitesimal $dz$, is therefore
$$
\frac{d\mathcal N}{dz}=n_p\sigma_p\frac{d\ell}{dz}
=\frac{c n_c\sigma_p(1+z)^2}{H(z)}.
$$
The <Friedmann equation>, the <constant-equation-of-state density scaling> for <pressureless matter>, and the present <cosmological density parameters> give
$$
H^2(z)=H_0^2\left[\Omega_m(1+z)^3+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_K(1+z)^2\right],
\qquad\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_K=1.
$$
Combining them yields the <absorber incidence and comoving number density> relation
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathcal N}{dz}\propto
\frac{(1+z)^2}{\sqrt{\Omega_m(1+z)^3+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_K(1+z)^2}}.}
$$
Here the constant cross-section is physical, as required for this result; a fixed comoving cross-section would instead decrease in physical area as $(1+z)^{-2}$. It is convenient to define <absorption distance> by $dX/dz=(H_0/H(z))(1+z)^2$ and $f_X=f_z/(dX/dz)$, so a population with these non-evolution assumptions has constant incidence per $X$.

A useful description of the low-column <Lyman-alpha forest> at $z\simeq3$ is a <power law> $f_X(N,z)=A(z)N^{-\beta}$, with $\beta$ around $1.4$–$1.5$ over the ordinary unsaturated forest range. The same column dependence holds for $f_z$ at a fixed redshift. It is not a universal unbroken <power law>: saturation, increased neutral fraction and changing absorber populations produce breaks. <Lyman limit systems> become optically thick at $N\simeq10^{17.2}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ because $N\sigma_{912}\simeq1$ with $\sigma_{912}\simeq6.3\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{cm}^2$. <Damped Lyman-alpha systems> have $N\gtrsim2\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ and strong radiative damping wings. They are rare in number but contain substantial neutral mass. The highest-column distribution steepens or cuts off rather than extending indefinitely.

\Image[paper-67-absorber-columns.png]{title=Schematic neutral-hydrogen column distribution showing the forest, optically thick Lyman-limit systems and damped systems}

This sketch shows representative shapes and transition scales, not an observational fit; its ordinate is distribution per unit column, not counts per logarithmic bin. In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, $H=H_0(1+z)^{3/2}$, so a fixed comoving population with fixed physical cross-section above a fixed column threshold has $d\mathcal N/dz\propto(1+z)^{1/2}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{(d\mathcal N/dz)_{z=4}}{(d\mathcal N/dz)_{z=1.5}}
=\left(\frac5{2.5}\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt2\simeq1.414.}
$$
The observed <redshift evolution of the Lyman-alpha forest> is much stronger for commonly selected forest lines. For example, \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0101005][Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico's absorption survey] finds an index about $2.19$ in $d\mathcal N/dz\propto(1+z)^\gamma$ for $10^{13.64}<N/\mathrm{cm}^{-2}<10^{16}$ over $1.5<z<4$, corresponding to an increase by approximately $2^{2.19}\simeq4.56$. This number is selection-dependent. Expansion changes the gas <density>, and the evolving <cosmic ionizing background> changes its neutral fraction through <photoionization equilibrium>, moving systems across a fixed neutral-column threshold. Structure also evolves. A non-evolving population with fixed physical cross-section is therefore not an adequate physical model of the observed forest.

Use <absorption distance> $X$ and its distribution $f_X$. Summing hydrogen columns per unit $X$ gives $\int N f_X\,dN$. The physical light-path length per $X$ is
$$
\frac{d\ell}{dX}=\frac{c}{H_0(1+z)^3}.
$$
The mean neutral-hydrogen <number density> is therefore $(H_0/c)(1+z)^3\int N f_X\,dN$. Divide by $(1+z)^3$ to obtain its comoving <density>, multiply by hydrogen atomic mass, and divide by today's mass <critical density> $\rho_{\rm crit,0}=3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$. This derives the <neutral gas density from an absorption distribution>:
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{\rm HI}(z)=\frac{H_0m_H}{c\rho_{\rm crit,0}}\int N f_X(N,z)\,dN.}
$$
If “neutral gas” includes <helium>, replace $m_H$ by $\mu_Hm_H$, with a stated abundance correction approximately $\mu_H\simeq1.3$. Equivalently, for the distribution per redshift the prefactor inside the integral is $m_H H(z)/[c\rho_{\rm crit,0}(1+z)^2]$ multiplying $Nf_z$. In the PDF's energy-density convention, both the mass <density> and <critical density> are multiplied by $c^2$, leaving their ratio unchanged. The usual quoted abundance uses comoving mass <density> relative to today's <critical density>; a ratio of physical <density> to the epoch's <critical density> instead multiplies this answer by $(1+z)^3H_0^2/H(z)^2$.

To infer total gas, one needs the neutral fraction $x_{\rm HI}=N_{\rm HI}/N_H$ for each absorber. The <ionized gas density from an absorption distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\Omega_g(z)=\frac{H_0\mu_Hm_H}{c\rho_{\rm crit,0}}
\int\frac{N}{x_{\rm HI}(N,z)}f_X(N,z)\,dN.}
$$
This is an ionization-corrected integral, not an inference available from the observed columns alone. For optically thin, almost fully ionized hydrogen, <photoionization equilibrium> gives $\Gamma n_{\rm HI}=\alpha(T)n_en_{\rm HII}\simeq\alpha(T)n_H^2$. If the absorbing depth $L$ is specified, $N=\alpha(T)n_H^2L/\Gamma$ and
$$
N_H=n_HL=\left(\frac{\Gamma NL}{\alpha(T)}\right)^{1/2}.
$$
Thus <photoionization rate>, <temperature> and depth are all needed.

One useful physical closure sets $L$ to a local <Jeans length>, $L\propto(f_gT/n_H)^{1/2}$, with local gas fraction $f_g$. Solving $N\propto[\alpha(T)/\Gamma](f_gT)^{1/2}n_H^{3/2}$ then gives the <Jeans-length ionization correction for the Lyman-alpha forest>:
$$
N_H\propto N^{1/3}\Gamma^{1/3}\left(\frac{f_gT}{\alpha(T)}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
\Omega_g\propto\Gamma^{1/3}\left(\frac{f_gT}{\alpha(T)}\right)^{1/3}\int N^{1/3}f_X\,dN.
$$
This illustrates why weak, highly ionized forest lines can dominate the total gas inventory even though <damped Lyman-alpha systems> dominate the neutral-column-weighted inventory. It is a model for locally confined optically thin gas; optically thick <Lyman limit systems> and <damped Lyman-alpha systems> require a separate <radiative transfer> treatment. The physical basis and observational application of this closure are developed in \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0104272][Schaye's absorption-based matter-density calculation].

= Solution
{parent=d}

No measured normalization of the <neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution>, ionizing field or gas <temperature> is supplied, so the paper's formulas do not determine numerical <densities> on their own. With observational data and suitable ionization corrections, the high-redshift <Lyman-alpha forest> gives a total gas <density> of order a few $10^{-2}$ relative to today's <critical density>, comparable to the <cosmological baryon density> $\Omega_b\sim0.04$–$0.05$ for $H_0\sim70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$. The conclusion is that a large fraction of baryons at $z\simeq3$ reside in diffuse ionized gas, rather than that every baryon has been individually accounted for. \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0104272][Schaye's analysis] and \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0101005][Kim and collaborators' survey] support that interpretation under their stated gas and ionizing-background assumptions.

The neutral-gas reservoir traced predominantly by <damped Lyman-alpha systems> is much smaller, of order $10^{-3}$ in the same convention. It is broadly comparable in order of magnitude to today's luminous stellar matter, whose <cosmic stellar mass density> is a few $10^{-3}$, but exact comparisons depend on cosmology, <helium> correction and which stellar components are counted. \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9608147][Storrie-Lombardi, McMahon and Irwin's neutral-gas census] finds especially close comparison with present stellar disks. **Most high-redshift gas is ionized; the smaller neutral reservoir has a mass of roughly the same order as today's visible stellar population.** This comparison does not imply that the measured neutral reservoir alone simply becomes all present-day stars: gas accretion, recycling, ionization changes and outflows continue during <galaxy> evolution.

The <neutral gas density from an absorption distribution> is comparatively robust for high columns where the hydrogen is mostly neutral, but saturated lines, dust-obscured quasars, incomplete surveys and small numbers of rare high-column systems affect the integral. The <ionized gas density from an absorption distribution> has additional systematic uncertainty in the <cosmic ionizing background>, <temperature>, absorber depth, <helium> treatment and small-scale <density> inhomogeneity. At low columns, line blending and incompleteness matter; at high columns, attenuation makes an optically thin ionization correction invalid. A single <power law> extrapolated outside the measured column range can also produce a misleading mass integral. Hot gas, molecular gas and gas missed by the selected absorption population are not included automatically. These are observational and modelling qualifications to a baryon census, not failures of the column-mass accounting itself.
