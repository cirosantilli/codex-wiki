An <inverse system> of <topological spaces> over a <directed set> $I$ consists of spaces $X_i$ and continuous maps $f_{ij}:X_j\to X_i$ for $i\leq j$, with $f_{ii}=1$ and $f_{ik}=f_{ij}f_{jk}$. Its <inverse limit> is a space $X$ with compatible continuous projections $\pi_i$ such that every compatible family of continuous maps $u_i:Y\to X_i$ factors uniquely through a continuous map $u:Y\to X$. To construct it, take
$$
X=\{(x_i)\in\prod_{i\in I}X_i:f_{ij}(x_j)=x_i\text{ whenever }i\leq j\}
$$
with the <subspace topology> inherited from the <product topology>. The map $u(y)=(u_i(y))$ has values in $X$ and is continuous by the definition of the <product topology>; its coordinates force uniqueness. If $X'$ is another <inverse limit>, the two universal properties give continuous maps $X\to X'$ and $X'\to X$ whose composites have all the same projections as the identity. Uniqueness makes these composites the identity. **The inverse limit exists and is unique up to a unique projection-preserving homeomorphism.** An arbitrary <inverse limit> may be empty; nonemptiness requires additional hypotheses.

Use the usual Hausdorff convention for a <topological group>. An <inverse limit> of finite discrete <groups> is a closed subgroup of their product: each compatibility condition is closed because the finite factors are <Hausdorff spaces>. The <Tychonoff theorem> gives <compactness>, and distinct tuples are separated by a clopen coordinate cylinder, so the limit is <totally disconnected>. Coordinatewise multiplication and inversion are continuous, giving a <profinite group>.

Conversely, let $G$ be a <compact Hausdorff space> and a <totally disconnected> <topological group>. We use the standard topological fact that a <compact Hausdorff space> that is <totally disconnected> has a basis of <clopen sets>. Given an identity neighbourhood, choose a clopen identity neighbourhood $U$ inside it. <Compactness> of $U$ and continuity of multiplication give a symmetric identity neighbourhood $V$ with $VU\subseteq U$: cover $U$ by finitely many neighbourhoods on which multiplication by a sufficiently small identity neighbourhood stays inside $U$, then intersect those identity neighbourhoods. Symmetry gives $vU=U$ for $v\in V$. Consequently $H=\{g:gU=U\}$ is a subgroup containing $V$, hence an <open subgroup>, and $H\subseteq U$ since $1\in U$. Its open <cosets> cover the compact group, so there are finitely many. The <normal core> $N=\bigcap_{g\in G}gHg^{-1}$ is a finite intersection of open subgroups, hence an <open normal subgroup> contained in $U$. Thus the open normal subgroups form an identity-neighbourhood basis and have trivial intersection.

The canonical <group homomorphism> $G\to\varprojlim_NG/N$ is continuous and injective. For a compatible tuple of quotient elements, its inverse-image cosets in $G$ are closed and have the <finite intersection property>: a finite family is refined by the quotient for the intersection of its normal subgroups. <Compactness> gives an element in all those cosets, proving surjectivity. A continuous bijection from a <compact space> to a <Hausdorff space> is a <homeomorphism>. **The compact totally disconnected and inverse-limit definitions of a profinite group agree.**

Index the finite nonempty sets $\mathcal P(N)$ by open normal subgroups, with the transition from $M$ to $N$ when $M\subseteq N$ given by image under $G/M\to G/N$. Images of <Sylow subgroups> under surjections of finite <groups> are <Sylow subgroups>. These transitions are surjective: if $S\leq G/N$ is a <Sylow subgroup>, choose a <Sylow subgroup> in its full inverse image in $G/M$. It maps onto $S$, and its order has the full $p$-part of $|G/M|$, so it is a <Sylow subgroup> of $G/M$ too. The transitions respect composition, giving an <inverse system> of finite sets.

Its <inverse limit> is nonempty. Indeed, finitely many compatibility conditions can be satisfied by choosing one <Sylow subgroup> in a quotient refining all the involved quotients and taking its images; <compactness> of the product of the finite sets then gives a compatible family $(P_N)_N$. Define
$$
P=\bigcap_N\pi_N^{-1}(P_N).
$$
This is a closed subgroup of $G$. Its image in $G/N$ is exactly $P_N$: for a prescribed $s\in P_N$, each finite collection of conditions defining $P$ together with $\pi_N(g)=s$ is satisfiable in a common refining quotient, because the transitions on the chosen <Sylow subgroups> are surjective. The <finite intersection property> again supplies $g\in P$. Hence $P\cong\varprojlim_NP_N$ is a <pro-p group> and $PN/N=P_N$ for every $N$.

Finally, if a <pro-p subgroup> $Q$ contains $P$, its image in every finite quotient is a <p-group> containing the <Sylow subgroup> $P_N$, so that image equals $P_N$. Thus every $q\in Q$ belongs to every $\pi_N^{-1}(P_N)$, whence $Q\subseteq P$. **The constructed $P$ is a maximal pro-p subgroup.**

A <topological group> is a group with a topology for which $(x,y)\mapsto xy$ and $x\mapsto x^{-1}$ are continuous; we use the Hausdorff convention. Translations and inversion are then <homeomorphisms>.

For the final generator-extraction assertion, use the <profinite Frattini subgroup>
$$
\Phi(G)=\overline{G^p[G,G]}.
$$
For a finitely generated <pro-p group>, the <Burnside basis theorem> says that $G/\Phi(G)$ is a finite-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb F_p$ and a subset topologically generates $G$ precisely when its image spans that <vector space>. In particular its dimension is the minimal number $d(G)$ of <topological generators>. A given $d$-element generating set shows $d(G)\leq d$. The image of any other generating set spans the same finite-dimensional <vector space>, so it contains a basis of $d(G)$ elements. Choose their preimages in that generating set and apply the <Burnside basis theorem> again. **Every generating set contains a generating subset of size $d(G)\leq d$.** This argument also applies to an infinite generating set.

For an <open subgroup> $H$ of a <profinite group> $G$, the open left <cosets> form a disjoint cover of a <compact space>. A finite subcover must include every coset, so $[G:H]$ is finite. Choose representatives $t_1,\ldots,t_m$ for these <cosets>. The <normal core> is
$$
N=\bigcap_{g\in G}gHg^{-1}=\bigcap_{j=1}^m t_jHt_j^{-1}.
$$
Each conjugate is open, and the intersection is finite, so $N$ is an <open normal subgroup> contained in $H$. **$[G:H]<\infty$ and $H$ contains an open normal subgroup of $G$.**

Choose a finite set $S$ of <topological generators> of $G$ and let $\Gamma=\langle S\rangle$ denote the abstract subgroup it generates. Then $\Gamma$ is dense in $G$. For an <open subgroup> $H$, the abstract subgroup $K=\Gamma\cap H$ is dense in $H$, since every nonempty open subset of $H$ is open in $G$. Every coset of $H$ meets $\Gamma$, so $[\Gamma:K]=[G:H]=m<\infty$.

Here is the needed <Schreier lemma> argument explicitly. Choose representatives $t$ in $\Gamma$ for the right <cosets> $K\backslash\Gamma$, including $1$ for $K$, and write $\overline\gamma$ for the representative of $K\gamma$. The finite set
$$
\{t s\,\overline{ts}^{-1}:t\text{ a representative},\ s\in S\cup S^{-1}\}
$$
is contained in $K$. For $k=s_1\cdots s_r\in K$, set $t_j=\overline{s_1\cdots s_j}$, with $t_0=t_r=1$. Then
$$
k=\prod_{j=1}^r(t_{j-1}s_jt_j^{-1}),
$$
and every factor belongs to the displayed finite set. Thus $K$ is finitely generated as an abstract <group>. Its finite generators topologically generate its closure $H$. **Every open subgroup of a finitely generated profinite group is finitely generated.**

Throughout this question $p$ is odd, $A^p$ means the subgroup generated by the $p$-th powers of elements of $A$, and the <commutator of group elements> convention is $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$. A subgroup $N$ is <powerfully embedded> in $G$ when $[N,G]\subseteq N^p$. In particular $N$ is normal, since $n^g=n[n,g]\in N$. A <finite p-group> $G$ is a <powerful p-group> when $[G,G]\subseteq G^p$.

First put $M=N^p$. It is normal in $G$. By the reduction permitted in the question, it suffices to prove $[M,G]\subseteq M^p$ after factoring out $[M,G,G]$. In that quotient, $[M,G]$ is central. For $n\in N$ and $g\in G$, put $c=[n,g]\in M$. Then $[c,n]$ is central, and the elementary identity $[ab,g]=[a,g]^b[b,g]$ gives, by induction,
$$
[n^p,g]=c^{n^{p-1}}c^{n^{p-2}}\cdots c
=c^p[c,n]^{p(p-1)/2}.
$$
Both $c$ and $[c,n]$ belong to $M$, and $p$ divides $p(p-1)/2$ because $p$ is odd. Hence $[n^p,g]\in M^p$. The subgroup $M^p$ is normal in $G$, so the commutator identity extends this inclusion from the generating elements $n^p$ to every element of $M$. Applying the allowed reduction proves **$N^p$ is powerfully embedded in $G$.**

For $H=\langle N,x\rangle$, normality of $N$ gives $H=N\langle x\rangle$. Its quotient by $N$ is cyclic. Modulo $[N,H]$, the image of $N$ is central and the remaining generator is $x$, so that quotient is abelian. Therefore
$$
[H,H]\subseteq[N,H]\subseteq[N,G]\subseteq N^p\subseteq H^p.
$$
Thus **$\langle N,x\rangle$ is powerful.**

The <lower p-series> is $G_1=G$, $G_{i+1}=G_i^p[G_i,G]$. For the later calculations write $P_j=G_{j+1}$, so $P_0=G$. Part (i) will show $P_{j+1}=P_j^p$ and $[P_j,G]\subseteq P_{j+1}$. A useful <power lifting in a powerful p-group> congruence is
$$
(xu)^p\equiv x^pu^p\pmod {P_{j+2}}\qquad(x\in G,\ u\in P_j).
$$
Indeed, modulo $P_{j+2}$ the <commutators> $[u,x]$ are central, lie in $P_{j+1}$ and have exponent dividing $p$. Collecting the $p$ copies of $xu$ therefore gives only the correction $[u,x]^{\binom p2}$, which is trivial. This argument also covers $j=0$. The congruence is the reason roots and coordinates can be corrected one power layer at a time.

$$
\boxed{G_{i+1}=G_i^p=\Phi(G_i),\qquad [G_i,G]\subseteq G_{i+1}.}
$$
Since the <Frattini subgroup> of a nontrivial finite <p-group> is proper, this series eventually reaches $1$. Thus $P_j=G^{p^j}$, with iterated power subgroups, terminates.

First, the images of $a_1^p,\ldots,a_n^p$ span $G_2/G_3$ by the surjective <group homomorphism> in (ii). Part (i) identifies $G_3$ with $\Phi(G_2)$, so the <Burnside basis theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{G^p=\langle a_1^p,\ldots,a_n^p\rangle.}
$$
This proves generation by the indicated powers; it does not yet prove that an arbitrary product of powers is itself a power.

For that stronger assertion, let $y\in P_1=G^p$. Part (ii) supplies $x$ with $x^p\equiv y\pmod {P_2}$. Suppose at some stage $x^p\equiv y\pmod {P_{j+1}}$, where $j\geq1$. Apply (ii) to the <powerful p-group> $P_j$: its first two power layers are $P_{j+1},P_{j+2}$. We can therefore choose $u\in P_j$ with
$$
u^p\equiv(x^p)^{-1}y\pmod {P_{j+2}}.
$$
The preliminary power congruence gives $(xu)^p\equiv y\pmod {P_{j+2}}$. Replacing $x$ by $xu$ improves the root by one layer. The <lower p-series> terminates, so finitely many corrections yield $x^p=y$ exactly. **Every element of $G^p$ is a $p$-th power in $G$.**

= Solution

For each $j\geq0$, the <elementary abelian group> $P_j/P_{j+1}$ is spanned by the images of $a_i^{p^j}$. We construct exponents $e_i$ so that $g$ equals the ordered product $a_1^{e_1}\cdots a_n^{e_n}$ to successively greater precision. Suppose this product agrees with $g$ modulo $P_j$; initially this is automatic for $j=0$. Its error modulo $P_{j+1}$ can be expressed using the images of $a_i^{p^j}$. Add the corresponding digits $p^jt_i$ to the exponents $e_i$. Since $[P_j,G]\subseteq P_{j+1}$, moving each new factor $a_i^{p^jt_i}$ past the other factors changes nothing modulo $P_{j+1}$. The new ordered product therefore agrees with $g$ modulo $P_{j+1}$. Termination of the <lower p-series> proves
$$
\boxed{G=\langle a_1\rangle\langle a_2\rangle\cdots\langle a_n\rangle.}
$$
No uniqueness of this factorization is asserted for a redundant generating tuple.

**The printed converse is false without an additional condition.** For any odd $p$, take the <Heisenberg group> $G=\operatorname{UT}_3(\mathbb F_p)$ with
$$
x=I+E_{12},\quad y=I+E_{23},\quad z=I+E_{13}=[x,y].
$$
Every element is uniquely $x^ay^bz^c$, with $a,b,c\in\mathbb F_p$, so $G$ is a product of three <cyclic subgroups>. For $A$ strictly upper triangular, $A^3=0$ and
$$
(I+A)^p=I+pA+\binom p2A^2=I
$$
in characteristic $p$, including $p=3$. Therefore $G^p=1$, whereas $[G,G]=\langle z\rangle\ne1$. This <finite p-group> is not powerful, disproving the literal request in the authoritative PDF.

A precise nearby correction is the <minimal cyclic factorization criterion for powerful p-groups>: require a product of $d(G)$ cyclic subgroups, where $d(G)$ is the minimal generator number. To prove it, put $d=d(G)$ and $H=G/G^p$. The quotient has exponent $p$ and still has minimal generator number $d$, because $G^p\subseteq\Phi(G)$. Each cyclic factor has image of order at most $p$, so $|H|\leq p^d$. But $|H/\Phi(H)|=p^d$ by the <Burnside basis theorem>. Consequently $\Phi(H)=1$, $H$ is an <elementary abelian group>, and $[G,G]\subseteq G^p$. **A product of exactly $d(G)$ cyclic subgroups is powerful.** A direct product of cyclic groups is also powerful, since it is abelian; neither stronger hypothesis appears in the printed converse.

= Solution

Put $U_0=G$ and $U_{j+1}=U_j^p$, where powers mean closed generated subgroups. The finite-quotient arguments of Question 3 pass to a <powerful pro-p group>: in particular $U_j$ is <powerfully embedded>, the power map is onto $U_{j+1}$, and
$$
[U_j,G]\subseteq U_{j+1},\qquad (xu)^p\equiv x^pu^p\pmod {U_{j+2}}\quad(u\in U_j).
$$
For the root assertion, apply the finite result to every finite quotient and use the <finite intersection property> on the closed sets of possible roots in the <compact group>. Each $U_j$ is finitely generated by the appropriate powers of a finite generating tuple, and $U_j/U_{j+1}$ is finite and <elementary abelian>. Thus the $U_j$ are open. They form an identity-neighbourhood basis: in any finite continuous quotient their images form a terminating <lower p-series>, so some $U_j$ is contained in the kernel. In particular $\bigcap_jU_j=1$. Collection also gives
$$
[U_a,U_b]\subseteq U_{a+b+1}.
$$
One can see the extra index directly by induction from $[U_a,G]\subseteq U_{a+1}$: collect $[v,w^p]$ for $w\in U_b$. Its leading factor $[v,w]^p$ advances one layer, and the remaining iterated <commutators>, with their binomial coefficients, lie at least as deep. Products and limits preserve the inclusion. This is the usual strongly central property of the <lower p-series>.

A <uniform pro-p group> is a finitely generated <powerful pro-p group> for which all the indices $|U_j:U_{j+1}|$ equal $|G:G^p|$. The preliminary congruence gives surjective linear maps
$$
\theta_j:U_j/U_{j+1}\longrightarrow U_{j+1}/U_{j+2},\qquad uU_{j+1}\longmapsto u^pU_{j+2}.
$$
If $G$ is uniform, these maps are bijective. If $x\ne y$, write $y=xu$ and choose the unique layer $j$ with $u\in U_j\setminus U_{j+1}$. Bijectivity of $\theta_j$ and the power congruence imply $y^p(x^p)^{-1}\in U_{j+1}\setminus U_{j+2}$, so $x^p\ne y^p$. Thus the power map is injective. A finite-order element of a <pro-p group> has $p$-power order; repeated injectivity of the power map excludes every nonidentity such element. Hence a uniform group is a <torsion-free group>.

Conversely, suppose some $\theta_j$ has a nonzero kernel. Choose $x\in U_j\setminus U_{j+1}$ with $x^p\in U_{j+2}$. At step $r\geq j+1$, if the current $x_r$ satisfies $x_r^p\in U_{r+1}$, surjectivity of $\theta_r$ supplies $u_r\in U_r$ with
$$
u_r^p\equiv(x_r^p)^{-1}\pmod {U_{r+2}}.
$$
Set $x_{r+1}=x_ru_r$. The power congruence gives $x_{r+1}^p\in U_{r+2}$. Since the corrections tend to $1$ in the complete <profinite group>, the $x_r$ converge to $z$, with $z^p=1$ and $z\equiv x\pmod {U_{j+1}}$. In particular $z\ne1$. A <torsion-free group> cannot have such an element, so every $\theta_j$ is injective and all layer indices are equal. This proves the <root correction criterion for uniformity> and
$$
\boxed{G\text{ is uniform}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ G\text{ is torsion-free}.}
$$
For a uniform group the power maps are continuous bijections $U_j\to U_{j+1}$, hence <homeomorphisms>. Their inverses define the unique roots used below. Comparison at the first differing layer also proves the <power congruences in a uniform pro-p group>:
$$
x\equiv y\pmod {U_b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^{p^a}\equiv y^{p^a}\pmod {U_{a+b}}.
$$

The <intrinsic Lie algebra of a uniform pro-p group> has the same underlying set as $G$, zero equal to the group identity, and operations
$$
x+_Ly=\lim_{n\to\infty}(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})^{p^{-n}},\qquad
[x,y]_L=\lim_{n\to\infty}[x^{p^n},y^{p^n}]^{p^{-2n}},\qquad
\lambda x=x^\lambda\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb Z_p).
$$
Here $x^\lambda$ is the limit of integral powers approaching $\lambda$. The first root is taken in $U_n$ and the second in $U_{2n}$; the commutator actually lies in $U_{2n+1}$. The following faithful algebra realization verifies convergence and the <Lie algebra> axioms, rather than assuming them as part of the definition.

Choose a minimal tuple $a_1,\ldots,a_d$ of <topological generators>. The layer isomorphisms $\theta_j$ show that the images of $a_i^{p^j}$ are bases of $U_j/U_{j+1}$. The ordered-coordinate argument of Question 3 therefore gives a continuous bijection
$$
\mathbb Z_p^d\longrightarrow G,\qquad (\lambda_i)\longmapsto a_1^{\lambda_1}\cdots a_d^{\lambda_d}.
$$
Existence follows by successive digits and <compactness>; uniqueness follows by the first digit at which the coordinates differ, because its images are linearly independent in the corresponding power layer. Thus this map is a <homeomorphism>.

Here are details of the norm needed on the <group algebra>. In $\mathbb Z_p[[G]]$ put $b_i=a_i-1$. Its elements have unique ordered expansions $\sum_\alpha c_\alpha b_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots b_d^{\alpha_d}$ with $c_\alpha\in\mathbb Z_p$. To verify this description, identify the ordered coordinates of $G$ with $\mathbb Z_p^d$. Elements of the <completed group algebra> are integral measures on this space; their values on the binomial polynomials $\prod_i\binom{\lambda_i}{\alpha_i}$ are precisely the coefficients $c_\alpha$. The finite-difference identity pairs the ordered monomial $b^\beta$ with this binomial polynomial as $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. The <Mahler expansion> of a continuous function therefore proves both existence and uniqueness of the measure with the specified coefficients. This also proves that the ordinary rational <group algebra> embeds in these expansions.

Choose $1/(p-1)<s<1$ and set
$$
v\left(\sum_\alpha c_\alpha b^\alpha\right)=\inf_\alpha\{v_p(c_\alpha)+s|\alpha|\}.
$$
This defines the <weighted group algebra of a uniform pro-p group>. Its multiplication is submultiplicative in the valuation convention, $v(AB)\geq v(A)+v(B)$. Indeed, $a_i^p-1=pb_i+\binom p2b_i^2+\cdots+b_i^p$ has valuation $1+s$; collection of ordered coordinates gives the same lower bound for $g-1$ when $g\in U_1$. Interchanging two adjacent $b_i$ in a product introduces a correction coming from $[a_i,a_j]-1$, of valuation at least $1+s>2s$. Iterating the interchange rule never lowers the total weight; the corrections have strictly increasing weight, so infinite collection converges. The same argument with $p^j$-th powers shows
$$
v(g-1)=s+j\quad\text{for }g\in U_j\setminus U_{j+1}.
$$
The equality follows from the first nonzero coordinate digit: its leading terms are $p^j\lambda_i' b_i$, at least one with unit $\lambda_i'$, and cannot cancel in the ordered expansion. Completing the rational <group algebra> in this norm produces a complete <normed algebra> $A$ in which $G$ embeds faithfully and with its original topology.

Since $v(g-1)\geq s>1/(p-1)$, the series
$$
\log g=\sum_{r\geq1}\frac{(-1)^{r+1}(g-1)^r}{r},\qquad
\exp X=\sum_{r\geq0}\frac{X^r}{r!}
$$
converge on the relevant balls in $A$ and are inverse there. Their convergence follows from $v_p(r!)\leq(r-1)/(p-1)$ and $v_p(r)\leq(r-1)/(p-1)$; formal one-variable identities prove that they are inverse. Put $X=\log x$ and $Y=\log y$. The <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> in $A$ now gives
$$
p^{-n}\log(x^{p^n}y^{p^n})\longrightarrow X+Y,
$$
because every term of degree at least two tends to zero after this rescaling. Similarly expansion of $\exp(-p^nX)\exp(-p^nY)\exp(p^nX)\exp(p^nY)$ gives
$$
p^{-2n}\log([x^{p^n},y^{p^n}])\longrightarrow XY-YX.
$$
The leading term is the indicated associative-algebra commutator and all terms of degree at least three tend to zero. These expressions are the logarithms of the unique roots in the intrinsic formulas. The image $\log G$ is compact and hence closed in $A$, so both limits belong to $\log G$, and the inverse logarithm takes the limits back to $G$. Also $\log(x^\lambda)=\lambda\log x$. Consequently $\log$ identifies the intrinsic operations with addition, scalar multiplication and commutation in $A$. The <Jacobi identity> follows by expanding the associative products; thus $L_G$ is indeed a <Lie algebra> over $\mathbb Z_p$.

The commutator roots in the intrinsic formula belong to $U_1$, so their limit gives $[L_G,L_G]\subseteq\log U_1=pL_G$. Iterating the power congruence gives $x^{p^n}y^{p^n}\equiv(xy)^{p^n}\pmod {U_{n+1}}$. Taking the unique roots shows that intrinsic addition modulo $U_1$ is the original group product modulo $U_1$. Consequently $L_G/pL_G$ is the original elementary abelian quotient $G/U_1$, and the images of the $a_i$ form a basis. Successively removing digits expresses every element uniquely as an additive sum $\sum_i\lambda_i a_i$: existence uses completeness, while a nonzero relation would have a first nonzero digit contradicting the basis in $U_j/U_{j+1}$. Hence $L_G$ is a <free module> of rank $d$ over the <p-adic integers> and is a <powerful Lie lattice>.

Conversely a <powerful Lie algebra over p-adic integers>, for odd $p$, is a finite free $\mathbb Z_p$-module $L$ with a bilinear alternating <Lie bracket> satisfying the <Jacobi identity> and $[L,L]\subseteq pL$. Define
$$
X*Y=\operatorname{BCH}(X,Y)
=X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]+\tfrac1{12}([X,[X,Y]]+[Y,[Y,X]])+\cdots.
$$
For completeness, integrality and convergence can be verified without guessing which individual denominators are units. In $\mathbb Q_p\otimes L$ set $M=p^{-1}L$. Then $[M,M]\subseteq M$, so its integral <universal enveloping algebra> has an ordered basis by the <Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem>. Its coefficientwise $p$-adic norm is submultiplicative, and $L=pM$ consists of elements of valuation at least one. The homogeneous degree-$r$ terms in $\log(\exp X\exp Y)$ have coefficients of valuation at least $-\lfloor(r-1)/(p-1)\rfloor$: expand the logarithm, use $v_p(k!)\leq(k-1)/(p-1)$ on each nonconstant exponential factor, and $v_p(\ell)\leq(\ell-1)/(p-1)$ on a product of $\ell$ such factors. Therefore evaluating degree $r$ on $X,Y\in pM$ gives valuation at least
$$
r-\left\lfloor\frac{r-1}{p-1}\right\rfloor,
$$
which tends to infinity and is at least two when $r\geq2$. Each homogeneous term is a rational <Lie polynomial> by the formal <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula>. The ordered enveloping-algebra basis shows that its intersection with $\mathbb Q_pM$ is exactly $M$, so the estimate places the sum in $L$, with the non-linear part in $pL$.

Associativity follows from the identity $\exp(X*Y)=\exp X\exp Y$ in the completed <universal enveloping algebra>. Its identity is $0$, its inverse is $-X$, and $X^{*m}=mX$, since the bracket of $X$ with itself is zero. The same coefficient estimate for the group commutator puts it in $pL$. Conjugation in the BCH group is $\exp(\operatorname{ad}X)$; each term $\operatorname{ad}(X)^r/r!$ preserves $p^jL$, because each bracket adds a factor of $p$ and $v_p(r!)<r$. Thus the submodules $p^jL$ are normal subgroups for $*$, their intersection is zero, and their finite quotients have $p$-power order. Thus $(L,*)$ is a <powerful pro-p group>, with
$$
(L,*)^{p^j}=p^jL,\qquad |p^jL:p^{j+1}L|=p^{\operatorname{rank}L}.
$$
A module basis topologically generates this group: it generates the quotient $L/pL$, and successive power digits lift that generation through all $p^jL$. **The BCH construction therefore gives a uniform pro-p group.**

In the <uniform pro-p group> $(L,*)$, the $p^n$-th power of $X$ is exactly $p^nX$, and its unique $p^n$-th root in the appropriate layer is division by $p^n$. The intrinsic addition is therefore
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}p^{-n}\operatorname{BCH}(p^nX,p^nY)=X+Y.
$$
The linear term is exact; every non-linear term tends to zero by the convergence estimates above. The intrinsic <Lie bracket> is
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}p^{-2n}\log\big(\exp(-p^nX)\exp(-p^nY)\exp(p^nX)\exp(p^nY)\big)=[X,Y],
$$
since the leading term is $p^{2n}[X,Y]$ and the terms of degree at least three vanish after rescaling. Finally the intrinsic scalar operation sends $(\lambda,X)$ to $\lambda X$. All underlying sets are identical, and all three operations agree. Thus
$$
\boxed{L_{(L,*)}=L.}
$$

Use the faithful logarithm realization from the root Solution: $\log:G\to A$ identifies $L_G$ with the <Lie algebra> $\log G$. For $x,y\in G$, the convergent exponential identity in $A$ gives
$$
\operatorname{BCH}(\log x,\log y)=\log(\exp(\log x)\exp(\log y))=\log(xy).
$$
Every <Lie polynomial> and its convergent sum are preserved by this realization of $L_G$. Therefore the BCH product on the underlying set of $L_G$ has exactly the same logarithm as the original product $xy$. Injectivity of the logarithm proves equality of the two products, with the same topology, identity and inverses:
$$
\boxed{(L_G,*)=G.}
$$
This proves the reverse direction of the <uniform pro-p group and powerful Lie lattice correspondence> directly; the forward identity alone would not imply it.

= Solution
{parent=5}

The proof of the <linearity of a uniform pro-p group> has three distinct steps: pass to a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra>, integrate a faithful representation on a sufficiently small open subgroup, and extend it to the entire group. Working first on a small subgroup is essential because a faithful <Lie algebra representation> need not send the original integral lattice into the convergence domain of the <matrix exponential>.

Let $G$ be a <uniform pro-p group> and let $L_G$ be its intrinsic <powerful Lie lattice>. Then
$$
\mathfrak g=\mathbb Q_p\otimes_{\mathbb Z_p}L_G
$$
is a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> over a <field> of <characteristic zero>. The <Ado theorem>, the classical Lie-theoretic ingredient in this proof, supplies a faithful finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation>
$$
\rho:\mathfrak g\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(V),\qquad \dim_{\mathbb Q_p}V=m<\infty.
$$
One must use a faithful representation rather than just the <adjoint representation>, which annihilates the <center of a Lie algebra>.

Choose a <p-adic lattice> $\Lambda\subset V$. Since $L_G$ is finitely generated as a module, the entries of the representing <matrices> of a module basis have bounded denominators. Hence for sufficiently large $k$,
$$
\rho(p^kL_G)\subseteq p\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb Z_p}(\Lambda).
$$
For odd $p$, the <matrix exponential> and <p-adic logarithm> converge and are inverse between $p\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$ and $1+p\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$. Put $H=G^{p^k}$; under the <uniform pro-p group and powerful Lie lattice correspondence> it is the BCH group on $p^kL_G$. Define
$$
\tau:H\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda),\qquad
\tau(\exp_G X)=\exp(\rho(X)).
$$
Here $\exp_G$ denotes the inverse of the intrinsic logarithm identification, not an assumed matrix representation of $G$. The <Lie algebra homomorphism> $\rho$ respects the convergent <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula>, so
$$
\exp(\rho(X*Y))=\exp(\rho(X))\exp(\rho(Y)).
$$
Thus $\tau$ is a continuous <group homomorphism>. If $\tau(\exp_G X)=1$, taking the <matrix logarithm> gives $\rho(X)=0$, whence $X=0$. Therefore $\tau$ is faithful and all its matrices preserve $\Lambda$. This proves linearity of the <open normal subgroup> $H$.

To finish, use the <extension of a faithful representation from an open normal subgroup>. Choose representatives $t_1=1,t_2,\ldots,t_r$ of the finite left <cosets> $G/H$, where $r=[G:H]$. On the finite free module $W=\bigoplus_{j=1}^r\Lambda$, define the action of $g\in G$ by writing uniquely
$$
gt_j=t_i h_{ij},\qquad h_{ij}\in H,
$$
and mapping the $j$-th block to the $i$-th block by $\tau(h_{ij})$. If $g't_j=t_\ell h'$ and $gt_\ell=t_i h$, then $gg't_j=t_i hh'$; this proves the representation identity because $\tau(hh')=\tau(h)\tau(h')$. Each block matrix is invertible over $\mathbb Z_p$, and the map is continuous because the finite coset permutation is locally constant and the remaining matrix entries are obtained continuously from $\tau$.

If $g\notin H$, its permutation of $G/H$ moves the identity coset, so its block representation is not the identity. If $g\in H$ and its block representation is the identity, the block corresponding to $t_1=1$ is $\tau(g)=1$, and faithfulness of $\tau$ gives $g=1$. Thus this <induced representation> is faithful. Choosing a basis of $W$ proves
$$
\boxed{G\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{mr}(\mathbb Z_p)}.
$$
The continuous injection is a topological embedding, since $G$ is compact and the target is Hausdorff. The trivial group is immediate. For uniform groups at $p=2$, the same proof uses the usual stronger powerfulness condition and the convergence ball $4\operatorname{End}(\Lambda)$ instead; the preceding questions concern odd $p$.
