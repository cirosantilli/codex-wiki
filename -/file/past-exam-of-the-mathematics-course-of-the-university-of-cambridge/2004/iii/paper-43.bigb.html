= Solution

Write the <expected value> as $\mu^T=(\mu_1^T,\mu_2^T)$ and assume the relevant <covariance matrix> $V_{22}$ is invertible. Remove the linear predictor from the first block by defining
$$
Y=X_1-\mu_1-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}(X_2-\mu_2).
$$
A <linear image of a multivariate normal vector> is again <multivariate normal>. Direct <covariance> calculation gives
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X_2)=V_{12}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{22}=0.
$$
Thus $Y$ and $X_2$ are <independent>: <uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent>. Expanding the <covariance matrix> of $Y$, using $V_{21}=V_{12}^T$, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(Y)
&=V_{11}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21}
 +V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{22}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21}\\
&=V_{11}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Conditioning on $X_2=x_2$ translates $Y$ by the fixed vector $\mu_1+V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2)$ and leaves its <covariance matrix> unchanged. Consequently the <conditional multivariate normal distribution> is
$$
X_1\mid X_2=x_2\sim N\!\left(\mu_1+V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2),\ \boxed{V_{11}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21}}\right).
$$
The <conditional covariance> is the <Schur complement> and does not depend on $x_2$.

Put $K=V^{-1}$, the <precision matrix>, with $X_1$ scalar and $X_2$ containing all remaining coordinates. Holding $X_2=x_2$ in the <multivariate normal density>, the terms involving $x_1$ in its exponent are
$$
-\frac12\left[K_{11}(x_1-\mu_1)^2+2(x_1-\mu_1)K_{12}(x_2-\mu_2)\right].
$$
Completing the square gives a <normal distribution> with mean $\mu_1-K_{11}^{-1}K_{12}(x_2-\mu_2)$ and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X_1\mid X_2=x_2)=\frac1{(V^{-1})_{11}}.}
$$
Equivalently, the block <matrix inverse> identity gives $K_{11}=(V_{11}-V_{12}V_{22}^{-1}V_{21})^{-1}$. The <Gaussian conditional variance from precision> is a reciprocal diagonal entry of the <precision matrix>, rather than the marginal <variance> $V_{11}$.

Write $\sigma_i^2=V_{ii}$ and $V_{ij}=\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$. Apply the <Schur complement covariance> to the first two coordinates, conditioning on the third. The <conditional covariance> and <conditional variances> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2\mid X_3=x_3)&=\sigma_1\sigma_2(\rho_{12}-\rho_{13}\rho_{23}),\\
\operatorname{Var}(X_1\mid X_3=x_3)&=\sigma_1^2(1-\rho_{13}^2),\\
\operatorname{Var}(X_2\mid X_3=x_3)&=\sigma_2^2(1-\rho_{23}^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Divide the <conditional covariance> by the product of the conditional <standard deviations>. The <conditional correlation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Corr}(X_1,X_2\mid X_3=x_3)=\frac{\rho_{12}-\rho_{13}\rho_{23}}{\sqrt{(1-\rho_{13}^2)(1-\rho_{23}^2)}}.}
$$
This is the <partial correlation> after removing the linear effect of $X_3$. For a nonsingular <multivariate normal distribution>, both residual <variances> are positive and the answer is independent of $x_3$. If a residual <variance> vanishes, the requested <correlation coefficient> is undefined, even though the residual <covariance> formula remains valid.

Use the usual <sample mean> and the divisor-$n-1$ <sample covariance matrix>,
$$
\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nx_j,\qquad S=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^n(x_j-\bar x)(x_j-\bar x)^T.
$$
The residual sum is zero, so expanding $x_j-\mu=(x_j-\bar x)+(\bar x-\mu)$ gives
$$
\sum_j(x_j-\mu)(x_j-\mu)^T=(n-1)S+n(\bar x-\mu)(\bar x-\mu)^T.
$$
Multiplying the independent <multivariate normal densities> and taking logarithms yields the <log-likelihood>
$$
\boxed{\ell(\mu,V)=-\frac{np}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log|V|-\frac{n-1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(V^{-1}S)-\frac n2(\bar x-\mu)^TV^{-1}(\bar x-\mu).}
$$
Here $|V|$ is the <determinant> and $\operatorname{tr}$ denotes the <trace>. The <maximum likelihood estimators> are
$$
\boxed{\widehat\mu=\bar x,\qquad \widehat V=\frac{n-1}{n}S.}
$$
If $S$ instead denotes the divisor-$n$ <sample covariance matrix>, replace $(n-1)S$ by $nS$ and then $\widehat V=S$. These formulas describe a nonsingular <maximum likelihood estimator> when the empirical scatter is <positive definite>; if it is singular, there is no maximizer over positive-definite <covariance matrices>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $n=\sum_{\nu=1}^gn_\nu$, $\bar x_\nu=n_\nu^{-1}\sum_jx_j^{(\nu)}$ and $\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_\nu n_\nu\bar x_\nu$. Define the within-group and between-group scatter <matrices> by
$$
\boxed{W=\sum_{\nu=1}^g\sum_{j=1}^{n_\nu}(x_j^{(\nu)}-\bar x_\nu)(x_j^{(\nu)}-\bar x_\nu)^T,\qquad B=\sum_{\nu=1}^gn_\nu(\bar x_\nu-\bar x)(\bar x_\nu-\bar x)^T.}
$$
The <within-group and between-group scatter decomposition> gives total scatter $T=W+B$: in each group the residuals about $\bar x_\nu$ sum to zero, so all cross terms vanish. Under unrestricted group <means>, the <maximum likelihood estimators> are $\widehat\mu_\nu=\bar x_\nu$ and $\widehat V=W/n$. Under the common-mean <null hypothesis>, they are $\widehat\mu=\bar x$ and $\widehat V_0=(W+B)/n$.

For either fitted scatter <matrix> $A$, substitution into the <log-likelihood> gives
$$
\ell_{\max}(A)=-\frac{np}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log|A/n|-\frac{np}{2},
$$
since $\operatorname{tr}[(A/n)^{-1}A]=np$. Thus the maximized <likelihood ratio> is
$$
\Lambda_{\mathrm{LR}}=\frac{\sup_{H_0}L}{\sup_{H_1}L}=\left(\frac{|W|}{|W+B|}\right)^{n/2}.
$$
A <generalized likelihood-ratio test> rejects for small $\Lambda_{\mathrm{LR}}$, equivalently
$$
\boxed{\log\frac{|W+B|}{|W|}>c.}
$$
The cutoff $c$ is chosen for the desired significance level. The determinant ratio $|W|/|W+B|$ itself is the <Wilks lambda statistic>. The derivation assumes $W$ is <positive definite>, which for continuous normal samples requires enough within-group degrees of freedom, $n-g\geq p$; singular $W$ does not support this ordinary determinant-based test without a modified model.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <within-group and between-group scatter decomposition> separates noise from group separation: $W$ measures variation about each group <sample mean>, and $B$ measures separation of those <means> about the overall <sample mean>. In <linear discriminant analysis>, seek a projection $a^Tx$ maximizing between-group relative to within-group variation,
$$
\frac{a^TBa}{a^TWa}.
$$
Normalize by $a^TWa=1$; a <Lagrange multiplier> calculation gives the <generalized eigenvalue problem> $Ba=\lambda Wa$. The leading <canonical discriminant directions> have the largest <eigenvalues>, and subsequent directions are orthogonal in the $W$ inner product. There are at most $\min(p,g-1)$ nonzero separating directions. Projecting observations onto these directions gives a low-dimensional display of group separation.

For classification, estimate the common <covariance matrix> by $S_p=W/(n-g)$ and use the <linear discriminant analysis> scores
$$
d_\nu(x)=x^TS_p^{-1}\bar x_\nu-\frac12\bar x_\nu^TS_p^{-1}\bar x_\nu+\log\pi_\nu,
$$
where $\pi_\nu$ are class prior probabilities. Assign to the largest score. **The same within-group scatter sets the noise scale, while between-group scatter selects the directions in which the group means separate most strongly.**

The first command reads a rectangular data file into `a`; `header=T` treats the first line as variable names. There are twelve observations, identified by student names, and nine binary attributes. A one records presence of an attribute and a zero records absence. This is a description of the encoding, not a claim that every presence is equally important for a scientific application.

`dist(a, metric="binary")` compares rows using <Jaccard distance>. If $n_{11}$ counts shared presences and $n_{10},n_{01}$ count mismatched presences, then
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=\frac{n_{10}+n_{01}}{n_{11}+n_{10}+n_{01}}.}
$$
Joint absences do not enter the denominator, unlike the <simple matching coefficient> or <normalized Hamming distance>. `dist2full(d)` expands the stored pairwise distances into a symmetric <dissimilarity matrix>; `round(...,2)` changes only its displayed precision. The clustering command still receives the original unrounded distances.

For example, Tom and Martin have six shared presences and only one mismatch, giving $d=1/7=0.142857\ldots$. Taeko and Luitgard have three shared presences and four mismatches, giving $d=4/7=0.571428\ldots$. LingChen and Mohammad share only the meat attribute and differ on four others, giving $d=4/5=0.8$. The diagonal is zero, and the entries are symmetric because exchanging the two observations exchanges $n_{10}$ and $n_{01}$.

`hclust(d, method="compact")` constructs an <agglomerative hierarchical clustering> object by <complete-linkage clustering>. Its distance between two current groups is
$$
d(A,B)=\max_{x\in A,\,y\in B}d(x,y).
$$
Start with singleton groups, merge a pair with smallest intergroup distance, update these maximum cross-pair distances, and repeat until one group remains. The object stores the merge structure, merge heights and a leaf order suitable for a <dendrogram>; assigning it to `h` does not itself display a graph. The historical https://sites.oxy.edu/lengyel/m150/Sueselbeck/helpfiles/hclust.html[S-Plus help] identifies `compact` with complete linkage and describes the plotting object.

The first merge is Tom with Martin at $1/7$. At the next height $1/5$, either LinYee with HuiChin or HuiChin with Meg may merge: the distances are tied. The following <dendrogram> shows one valid resolution, using original row order to resolve ties. In this tree, LinYee joins HuiChin at $1/5$, Pio joins Meg at $1/4$, and Nicolas joins Tom–Martin at $3/8$. The latter height is $\max\{d(\text{Nicolas},\text{Tom}),d(\text{Nicolas},\text{Martin})\}=\max\{3/8,2/7\}$, illustrating the complete-linkage update.

**`princomp()`: <principal component analysis>.** Supply observations in rows and numeric variables in columns. After centering, let $Z$ denote the resulting <matrix> and $C=Z^TZ/n$ its <sample covariance matrix>, with an explicitly stated normalization. Write
$$
Cq_j=\lambda_jq_j,\qquad q_i^Tq_j=\mathbf1_{\{i=j\}},\qquad \lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_p\geq0.
$$
The first direction maximizes the <Rayleigh quotient> $q^TCq$ over unit <vectors>; successive directions maximize it subject to orthogonality to the earlier directions. The <principal component scores> are $z_{ij}=q_j^T(x_i-\bar x)$, with <variances> $\lambda_j$ and zero pairwise <sample covariance>. Zero <covariance> alone does not imply <independence>. Each <principal component loading> describes a direction in variable space; its sign is arbitrary, and repeated <eigenvalues> leave the basis inside the corresponding <eigenspace> arbitrary.

The function returns <principal component loadings>, component <standard deviations> `sdev` and, when requested, <principal component scores>. By default it uses a <covariance matrix>; `cor=T` instead performs <principal component analysis on a correlation matrix>, standardizing nonconstant variables to <variance> one. This choice matters when variables have different measurement units. These interface details are documented in the https://sites.oxy.edu/lengyel/M150/Sueselbeck/helpfiles/princomp.html[S-Plus help for princomp].

A <scree plot> shows the ordered <eigenvalues>; a plot of the first two <principal component scores> can reveal groups, unusual observations or a dominant gradient. The first $k$ components explain $\sum_{j\leq k}\lambda_j/\sum_j\lambda_j$ of the total <variance>. Reconstructing each centered observation by its <orthogonal projection> onto these directions minimizes average squared reconstruction error among $k$-dimensional linear subspaces. Thus **PCA gives an optimal linear compression for squared reconstruction error, with scaling and the retained dimension chosen explicitly.** It is an <unsupervised learning> method, rather than a classification rule fitted to supplied labels.

**`tree()`: <classification trees> and <regression trees>.** A call such as `tree(response ~ predictors, data=dat)` fits a <recursive partitioning> model. At a current node, test numerical predictors against candidate thresholds, or divide the levels of a categorical predictor into two sets. Select a split that most improves the fitting criterion, then repeat within its children. Numerical splits give axis-aligned regions. A categorical response produces a <classification tree>; a numeric response produces a <regression tree>, as specified in the https://sites.oxy.edu/lengyel/m150/Sueselbeck/helpfiles/tree.html[S-Plus tree documentation].

For a <regression tree>, the constant prediction in a terminal region is its <sample mean>, minimizing the sum of squared residuals there. For a <classification tree>, estimate class probabilities by the observed proportions in a leaf and predict a most probable class under equal misclassification costs. The <classification-tree deviance> of a node is
$$
D(t)=-2\sum_r n_{tr}\log(n_{tr}/n_t),
$$
with $0\log0=0$; splitting maximizes the decrease from parent to children. This fitting criterion differs from the number of wrong classifications. The middle sketches show the same three-region partition as a binary tree: first split on variable one, then split the right-hand branch on variable two.

Growing to small leaves can overfit. Use <cost-complexity tree pruning>, balancing fitted loss $R(T)$ against leaf count,
$$
R_\alpha(T)=R(T)+\alpha|\mathcal L(T)|,
$$
and select complexity by <cross-validation>. Plotting the fitted tree and annotating nodes displays the rules; prediction routes each new observation through the fitted splits. **A tree gives an interpretable, piecewise-constant prediction rule; training fit alone does not measure prediction accuracy.**

**The `rpart()` alternative.** This also fits <classification and regression trees> by <recursive partitioning>, with squared-error splitting for ordinary numeric responses and, by default for classification, <Gini impurity> $1-\sum_r p_r^2$. Classification settings can instead use information-based splitting and specify prior probabilities and a loss <matrix>. It supports <surrogate splits> to approximate a node's primary left/right assignment when the primary predictor is missing. Its complexity-parameter path and cross-validated errors support <cost-complexity tree pruning>; for example, select a complexity parameter and apply `prune(fit, cp=...)`. The https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/rpart/html/rpart.html[maintainer's rpart documentation] describes the criteria and the distinction in handling surrogate predictors. The partition and tree sketches apply to either implementation; the fitted tree need not be the same because splitting criteria and controls can differ.

**`cmdscale()`: <classical multidimensional scaling>.** Its input is a <dissimilarity matrix> or stored distance object. It outputs coordinates for the observations in a chosen dimension, usually two for visualization. Let $D^{(2)}$ contain the squared entries of $D$, and put
$$
H=I-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1^T,\qquad G=-\frac12HD^{(2)}H.
$$
For centered Euclidean coordinates $X$, the distance identity $d_{ij}^2=\|x_i\|^2+\|x_j\|^2-2x_i^Tx_j$ gives $G=XX^T$: multiplying by $H$ removes the row and column norm terms. Diagonalize this <Gram matrix> and retain its $k$ largest positive <eigenvalues>. The coordinates are
$$
\boxed{X_k=U_k\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_k}).}
$$
Thus <classical scaling> exactly recovers Euclidean distances when all positive <eigenvalues> are retained. Reducing dimension approximates the centered <Gram matrix>; it does not in general minimize squared discrepancies in the original distances. Negative <eigenvalues> identify a failure of the <Euclidean distance matrix criterion>, so their size matters when interpreting a display. The historical https://sites.oxy.edu/lengyel/m150/Sueselbeck/helpfiles/cmdscale.html[S-Plus cmdscale help] describes the dimension argument, eigenvalue output and optional additive adjustment to dissimilarities.

Plot the two coordinate columns on equally scaled axes and label observations. Nearby plotted points suggest small dissimilarities only insofar as the dimension reduction fits the input well. The bottom sketches give an exact Euclidean example: a configuration and its independently recovered two-dimensional coordinates have identical pairwise distances, despite different coordinate axes. **Classical scaling converts distances into geometry; translations, rotations and reflections do not change the represented distances.** For Euclidean distances between observations, its coordinates agree with the corresponding <principal component scores> up to these harmless choices of axes.

= Solution

Under equal costs for either wrong assignment, the <Bayes classifier> chooses the class of greatest <posterior probability>. With positive prior probabilities, it assigns class one when $\pi_1f_1(x)>\pi_2f_2(x)$; ties can be resolved arbitrarily. For the two <multivariate normal densities> with common positive-definite <covariance matrix> $V$, their normalizing factors cancel. The log posterior odds are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{\pi_1f_1(x)}{\pi_2f_2(x)}
&=\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_2}-\frac12\big[(x-\mu_1)^TV^{-1}(x-\mu_1)-(x-\mu_2)^TV^{-1}(x-\mu_2)\big]\\
&=(\mu_1-\mu_2)^TV^{-1}x-\frac12(\mu_1^TV^{-1}\mu_1-\mu_2^TV^{-1}\mu_2)+\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore the <Gaussian Bayes classifier> has the required linear form, with
$$
\boxed{a=V^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2),\qquad b=\frac12(\mu_1+\mu_2)^TV^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)-\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_2}.}
$$
A larger prior probability for class one decreases $b$, expanding its decision region. The <prior-dependent Gaussian discriminant boundary> is the hyperplane $a^Tx=b$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For equal prior probabilities, $b=\tfrac12a^T(\mu_1+\mu_2)$. The projected score is <normal> under either class because it is a <linear image of a multivariate normal vector>, and its <variance> is
$$
a^TVa=(\mu_1-\mu_2)^TV^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)=\delta^2.
$$
Moreover $a^T\mu_1-a^T\mu_2=\delta^2$, so the midpoint threshold lies $\delta^2/2$ from either projected <mean>. Hence
$$
a^TX-b\mid C_1\sim N(\delta^2/2,\delta^2),\qquad a^TX-b\mid C_2\sim N(-\delta^2/2,\delta^2).
$$
Standardizing the two <normal distributions>, with $\Phi$ the <standard normal distribution function>, gives
$$
\boxed{P(\text{assign }C_2\mid C_1)=\Phi(-\delta/2)=P(\text{assign }C_1\mid C_2).}
$$
Thus the <equal-covariance Gaussian classification error> is $p(\delta)=\Phi(-\delta/2)$. The <Mahalanobis distance> $\delta$ between the class <means> is their separation in units of within-class noise. It yields $p\to1/2$ as $\delta\downarrow0$ and $p\to0$ as $\delta\to\infty$. The stipulated $\delta>0$ means the two class <means> are distinct.
