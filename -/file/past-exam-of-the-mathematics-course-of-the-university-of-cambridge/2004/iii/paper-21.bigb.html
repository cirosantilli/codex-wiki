= Solution
{parent=1}

The <determinant-one cyclic quotient of the affine plane> has the closed embedding
$$
\boxed{\mathbb A^2/\mu_n\cong\{(u,v,w)\in\mathbb A^3:uv=w^n\},\qquad
\pi(x,y)=(x^n,y^n,xy).}
$$
Here $\mu_n$ denotes the cyclic group generated by the given diagonal matrix. We establish the presentation of the <polynomial invariant ring> and verify that its fibres really are the <group orbits>.

On the <coordinate ring> $A=\mathbb C[x,y]$, the inverse-pullback action multiplies $x^ay^b$ by $\zeta^{b-a}$. Since these <monomials> are a basis, a <polynomial> is invariant precisely when each of its nonzero <monomials> satisfies $a-b\equiv0\pmod n$. If $a\ge b$, such a <monomial> is $(x^n)^{(a-b)/n}(xy)^b$; if $b\ge a$, it is $(y^n)^{(b-a)/n}(xy)^a$. Therefore
$$
A^{\mu_n}=\mathbb C[x^n,y^n,xy].
$$
These generators give a <surjective> <homomorphism> $\mathbb C[u,v,w]\to A^{\mu_n}$, and $uv-w^n$ lies in its <kernel of a ring homomorphism>. To prove there are no further relations, reduce any <monomial> using $uv=w^n$. The result is a linear combination of
$$
w^j,\qquad u^iw^j,\qquad v^iw^j\quad(i\ge1,\ j\ge0).
$$
Their images are respectively $x^jy^j$, $x^{ni+j}y^j$ and $x^jy^{ni+j}$. All have distinct exponent pairs, so they are linearly independent in $A$. Any <polynomial> in the <kernel of a ring homomorphism> has zero reduced expression and hence belongs to $(uv-w^n)$. Consequently
$$
A^{\mu_n}\cong\mathbb C[u,v,w]/(uv-w^n).
$$
This proves the claimed <closed subvariety> embedding of the affine quotient.

For surjectivity of $\pi$, if $u\ne0$ choose $x^n=u$ and set $y=w/x$. Then $y^n=w^n/u=v$. If $u=0$, the relation forces $w=0$; choose $x=0$ and any $n$th root of $v$ for $y$. If two points have the same image and $x\ne0$, equality of their $n$th powers gives $x'=\zeta^kx$ and equality of $xy$ gives $y'=\zeta^{-k}y$. If $x=0$, both first coordinates vanish, and the same argument using $y^n$ gives the orbit relation; the case $x=y=0$ has only that one point in its fibre. Thus every fibre is exactly one <group orbit>.

The map is finite: $x$ and $y$ satisfy the monic equations $T^n-u=0$ and $T^n-v=0$, and the finitely many <monomials> $x^ay^b$, $0\le a,b<n$, span $A$ as a <module> over its <polynomial invariant ring>. A <finite morphism> is closed when it is <surjective> as here and therefore gives the <quotient topology>. For a <principal open subset> defined by an invariant $h$, its regular functions pull back to $A_h$ and
$$
(A_h)^{\mu_n}=(A^{\mu_n})_h.
$$
Indeed, because $h$ is invariant, an invariant fraction with denominator $h^k$ has invariant numerator. These <principal open subsets> cover the <affine hypersurface>, proving the required local equality of invariant regular functions. Together with the orbit fibres, this verifies the <geometric quotient>, not merely a bijection of underlying sets.

Using the printed convention that the projective points represent lines of <binary quartics>, the locus of <GIT stable points> is
$$
\boxed{\{[f]:f\text{ has four distinct roots in }\mathbb P^1\}
=\{[f]:\operatorname{Disc}(f)\ne0\}.}
$$
The root at infinity is included. We determine stability by calculating every relevant <algebraic one-parameter subgroup> weight.

Use the natural action $(g\cdot f)(X,Y)=f(g^{-1}(X,Y))$. Every nontrivial <algebraic one-parameter subgroup> of $SL_2$ is conjugate to $\lambda_m(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t^m,t^{-m})$ for some integer $m>0$: its two integral <weights of a representation> sum to zero, and a basis of <weight vectors> can be rescaled to have determinant one. Write
$$
f(X,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^4a_jX^{4-j}Y^j,\qquad
\lambda_m(t)\cdot X^{4-j}Y^j=t^{m(2j-4)}X^{4-j}Y^j.
$$
If $r=\min\{j:a_j\ne0\}$, then $r$ is the <multiplicity of a root> at the projective point $[1:0]$: in the local coordinate $Y/X$, the <polynomial> begins with a nonzero multiple of $(Y/X)^r$.

The <Hilbert-Mumford criterion for projective stability>, with $\mu=-\min$(active weights), now gives
$$
\mu([f],\lambda_m)=m(4-2r).
$$
Being a <GIT stable point> requires this number to be strictly positive for every nontrivial subgroup. Conjugating the subgroup lets $[1:0]$ range over the entire <projective line>. Thus the <root-multiplicity criterion for stable binary forms> becomes $r_p(f)<2$ at every point $p$. A nonzero degree-four form over $\mathbb C$ has four projective roots counted with multiplicity, so this means precisely four distinct roots. This is equivalently nonvanishing of its <discriminant>.

The boundary cases show why a weak inequality would give the wrong answer. A triple or quadruple root can be moved to $[1:0]$, leaving only strictly positive weights; its affine representative tends to zero under that subgroup, so it is unstable. If all root multiplicities are at most two, all Hilbert-Mumford values are nonnegative and the point is a <GIT semistable point>. A double root gives a zero value for a suitable subgroup, so never represents a <GIT stable point>. More concretely, after putting that double root at $[1:0]$ one has
$$
f=Y^2(aX^2+bXY+cY^2),\qquad a\ne0,
$$
and $\lambda_1(t)\cdot f\to aX^2Y^2$. When there are three distinct roots, this limiting form, representing a <GIT semistable point> with two distinct roots, is outside the original <group orbit>, so that <group orbit> is not closed in the semistable locus. When there are two double roots, a change of coordinates gives $X^2Y^2$, which has the positive-dimensional diagonal torus as <stabilizer>. Neither case represents a <GIT stable point>.

For a form with four distinct roots, the projective <stabilizer> is finite: it permutes the four roots, and a <Möbius transformation> fixing three distinct points is the identity. Thus its image in $PGL_2$ injects into the finite <permutation group> on the roots. The <kernel of a group homomorphism> for $SL_2\to PGL_2$ is $\{\pm I\}$, also finite. This agrees with the finite-stabilizer condition in <geometric invariant theory>. The weight calculation already supplies the required strict stability condition for every conjugate subgroup.

Put $A=\mathbb C[V]=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}A_d$ and $R=A^G$. The linear action preserves <polynomial> degree, so $R=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}R_d$ with $R_0=\mathbb C$. We prove <Hilbert finite generation for reductive invariants> by combining finite ideal generation in $A$ with a degree-lowering argument in $R$.

Over $\mathbb C$, representations of a <reductive algebraic group> are completely reducible. On each finite-dimensional $A_d$, project its <isotypic component> for the <trivial representation> onto itself and its nontrivial summands onto zero. These projections together form a degree-preserving <Reynolds operator> $\mathcal R:A\to R$, with $\mathcal R|_R=\operatorname{id}$. The crucial additional property is
$$
\mathcal R(ah)=a\mathcal R(h)\qquad(a\in R,\ h\in A).
$$
Here is why it holds rather than being an arbitrary projection property. For a homogeneous invariant $a$, multiplication by $a$ is a $G$-equivariant linear map $A_d\to A_{d+\deg a}$. It sends invariant vectors to invariant vectors. On a nontrivial irreducible summand, its composition with projection onto the target's trivial summand is zero, since there is no nonzero equivariant map from a nontrivial <irreducible representation> to the <trivial representation>. It therefore commutes with the <Reynolds operators>. Summing the homogeneous components of $a$ and $h$ proves the displayed <module> property in general.

Let $R_+=\bigoplus_{d>0}R_d$ and form the ideal $I=AR_+$ in the ambient <polynomial> ring. The <Hilbert basis theorem> makes $A$ a <Noetherian ring>, so $I$ is generated by finitely many homogeneous positive-degree invariants $f_1,\ldots,f_s$. To justify choosing the generators from this particular set, start with any finite ideal generating set; each of its members is a finite sum of multiples of homogeneous members of $R_+$, and the union of those finitely many invariants then generates $I$. If $I=0$, there are no positive-degree invariants and the answer is simply $R=\mathbb C$.

Otherwise consider a homogeneous invariant $f$ of degree $d>0$. Since $f\in I$, write
$$
f=\sum_{i=1}^s h_i f_i,\qquad h_i\in A_{d-\deg f_i},
$$
with $h_i=0$ when that degree is negative. Taking the degree-$d$ component of any ideal expression gives exactly such an expression. Apply the <Reynolds operator> and its <module> property:
$$
f=\mathcal R(f)=\sum_{i=1}^s\mathcal R(h_i)f_i.
$$
Each coefficient $\mathcal R(h_i)$ is invariant and has degree strictly smaller than $d$, because every $f_i$ has positive degree. Induction on $d$, starting from $R_0=\mathbb C$, now shows that every homogeneous invariant lies in $\mathbb C[f_1,\ldots,f_s]$. An arbitrary invariant is a finite sum of invariant homogeneous components, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb C[V]^G=\mathbb C[f_1,\ldots,f_s].}
$$
This proves finite generation as an algebra. No Noetherian property of the <polynomial invariant ring> was assumed in obtaining the generators.

Let $C$ be the <smooth algebraic curve>. A nonzero regular section of a <line bundle> has an effective zero divisor, so its <degree of a line bundle> is nonnegative. Degrees add under tensor products and change sign on taking the dual. We use genuine <line subbundles>, so their quotients in $E$ are <locally free>. Both bounds can be proved constructively.

For the upper bound, take a rational frame of $E^*$ over the <function field> of $C$. Its two members are <rational sections> with only finitely many poles. Choose an <effective divisor> $D$ large enough to bound the pole orders of both <rational sections>. They become <global sections> of $E^*\otimes\mathcal O_C(D)$ and define a <sheaf> map
$$
E\longrightarrow\mathcal O_C(D)\oplus\mathcal O_C(D)
$$
which is an isomorphism at the <generic point>. It is therefore <injective> as a <sheaf> map: a section in its <kernel> would vanish generically in a <locally free sheaf>, which is a <torsion-free sheaf>. For any <line subbundle> $L\subset E$, at least one of its two component maps into $\mathcal O_C(D)$ is nonzero. Such a map is a nonzero <global section> of $\mathcal O_C(D)\otimes L^{-1}$. Its zero <divisor on an algebraic curve> is effective, so
$$
\boxed{\deg L\le\deg D.}
$$
This <upper degree bound for line subbundles on a smooth curve> depends only on a choice made for $E$, and applies to every one of its <line subbundles>.

For the lower-bound question we will actually construct subbundles $\mathcal O_C(-mp_0)$ for every sufficiently large integer $m$, where $p_0$ is a fixed point. First prove the <globally generated vector bundle> property rather than assuming that negative-degree inclusions automatically are subbundles.

Apply the upper-bound argument to the rank-two <vector bundle> $F=E^*\otimes K_C$, where $K_C$ is the <canonical bundle>. Let $B$ be its upper degree bound. Choose $m$ with $m-1>B$. For any point $p\in C$, a nonzero <global section> of $F(-mp_0+p)$ would give a nonzero map
$$
\mathcal O_C(mp_0-p)\longrightarrow F.
$$
Its image can be saturated to a <saturated line subbundle on a smooth curve> $M\subset F$. Locally the <local ring of a smooth algebraic curve> is a <discrete valuation ring>: dividing out the common power of a uniformizer makes the image a primitive rank-one summand, and its quotient is free. Thus the saturation is indeed a subbundle. The original map gives a nonzero <global section> of $M\otimes\mathcal O_C(-mp_0+p)$ and consequently $\deg M\ge m-1$, contradicting the upper bound. Hence $H^0(F(-mp_0+p))=0$ for every $p$. By <Serre duality>,
$$
H^1(E(mp_0-p))\cong H^0(E^*\otimes K_C(-mp_0+p))^*=0.
$$
The <exact sequence>
$$
0\longrightarrow E(mp_0-p)\longrightarrow E(mp_0)
\longrightarrow E(mp_0)|_p\longrightarrow0
$$
and its <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology> show that evaluation $H^0(E(mp_0))\to E(mp_0)|_p$ is onto for every $p$. This proves the <global generation of positive twists on a smooth curve> with one bound on $m$ that works at every point.

Write $W=H^0(E(mp_0))$ and $N=\dim W$. The evaluation map $W\otimes\mathcal O_C\to E(mp_0)$ is <surjective> with <kernel> bundle of rank $N-2$. Consider the incidence subset
$$
Z=\{(p,s)\in C\times W:s(p)=0\}.
$$
It is the total space of this <kernel> bundle, so $\dim Z=1+(N-2)=N-1$. Its projection into $W$ is closed because $C$ is proper, and its image has dimension at most $N-1<N$. Choose $s$ outside this proper closed subset. It vanishes nowhere. In a local trivialization at any point, one component of $s$ is a unit, so it can be completed to a basis. Therefore $\mathcal O_C\xrightarrow{s}E(mp_0)$ is a subbundle with a <locally free> rank-one quotient. Twisting back gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_C(-mp_0)\subset E,\qquad\deg\mathcal O_C(-mp_0)=-m\longrightarrow-\infty.}
$$
This proves the <unbounded negative degrees of line subbundles>. The nowhere-vanishing construction guarantees a subbundle at every point, including the zeros that might otherwise have occurred in a generic meromorphic inclusion. If the curve has several connected components, apply these constructions on each component and add the degrees; the same upper-bound and no-lower-bound conclusions follow.
