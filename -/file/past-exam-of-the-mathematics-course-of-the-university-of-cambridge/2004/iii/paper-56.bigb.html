= Solution
{parent=1}

Use units $c=1$ and take the initially expanding branch with ordinary positive matter or radiation density. In the <Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric>, $k=+1,0,-1$ respectively describe positive, zero and negative spatial curvature. Separately conserved <pressureless matter> has $\rho_M\propto a^{-3}$ and <radiation in cosmology> has $\rho_R\propto a^{-4}$, so either dominates the curvature and vacuum terms sufficiently far back on a hot Big Bang branch.

With negligible <cosmological constant>, a spatially flat radiation universe has $a\propto t^{1/2}$ and a flat matter universe has $a\propto t^{2/3}$; both expand forever while decelerating. An open universe also expands forever and eventually becomes approximately curvature dominated, with $a\propto t$. A closed universe containing only ordinary matter/radiation reaches a maximum size and recollapses. For example, dust gives $\dot a^2=C/a-k$ with $C>0$, so only $k=+1$ admits the positive turning point $a=C$.

**Curvature alone does not determine the fate when the cosmological constant is retained.** Positive $\Lambda$ can cause late accelerated expansion and prevent even a closed universe from recollapsing; whether a closed expanding branch turns around depends on the relative matter and vacuum terms. Negative $\Lambda$ can cause recollapse even for flat or open spatial sections. These statements follow by inspecting $\dot a^2=C/a-k+\Lambda a^2/3$ for dust; the curvature sign is a geometrical classification, not by itself a universal destiny criterion.

Define the <critical density> $\rho_c=3H^2/(8\pi G)$ and <cosmological density parameters> $\Omega_M=\rho_M/\rho_c$, $\Omega_R=\rho_R/\rho_c$, $\Omega_\Lambda=\Lambda/(3H^2)$ and $\Omega_k=-k/(a^2H^2)$. The <Friedmann equation> gives $\Omega_M+\Omega_R+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_k=1$. Dividing the <Friedmann acceleration equation> by $-H^2$ gives
$$
q=\frac{4\pi G}{3H^2}(\rho+3P)-\Omega_\Lambda
=\frac12\Omega_M+\Omega_R-\Omega_\Lambda.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{q=\frac12\Omega_M-\Omega_\Lambda\quad\text{for dust plus vacuum},\qquad q=\Omega_R-\Omega_\Lambda\quad\text{for radiation plus vacuum}.}
$$
The <deceleration parameter> measures the sign and size of the acceleration relative to $aH^2$: $q>0$ means deceleration, $q<0$ accelerated expansion and $q=0$ instantaneously zero acceleration. In particular a falling <Hubble parameter> alone does not imply deceleration, since $q=-1-\dot H/H^2$.

For conformal time $d\eta=dt/a$, the <conformal Hubble parameter> is $\mathcal H=aH$. Using matter conservation and differentiating $\Omega_M\propto\rho_M/H^2$,
$$
\frac{\dot\Omega_M}{\Omega_M}=-3H-2\frac{\dot H}{H}=H(2q-1),\qquad
\boxed{\Omega_M'=\mathcal H\Omega_M(\Omega_M-1-2\Omega_\Lambda).}
$$
This is the <matter density flow with a cosmological constant>. Neglecting $\Omega_\Lambda$ during matter domination reduces it to the requested relation
$$
\boxed{\Omega_M'=\mathcal H\Omega_M(\Omega_M-1).}
$$
That last equation is not exact with nonzero $\Lambda$. For example, a flat dust-plus-vacuum state with $(\Omega_M,\Omega_\Lambda)=(0.9,0.1)$ gives $\Omega_M'/\mathcal H=-0.27$, whereas the matter-only expression gives $-0.09$.

For the <flatness problem>, let $\Omega_{\rm tot}=\Omega_M+\Omega_R+\Omega_\Lambda$. Curvature satisfies $\Omega_{\rm tot}-1=k/(aH)^2$, so
$$
\frac{d\log|\Omega_{\rm tot}-1|}{d\log a}=2q.
$$
In decelerating near-flat radiation and matter eras, deviations therefore grow as $a^2$ and $a$ respectively. Maintaining small curvature at a late epoch requires very small early deviations unless a prior mechanism suppresses them. <Cosmic inflation>, with $q\simeq-1$, drives the deviation down approximately as $a^{-2}$. If vacuum energy is present, flatness means $\Omega_{\rm tot}=1$, not $\Omega_M=1$.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Work in <natural units> $k_B=\hbar=c=1$, initially neglecting helium and excited hydrogen states. The reaction $p+e^-\leftrightarrow H+\gamma$ is in <chemical equilibrium> when $\mu_p+\mu_e=\mu_H$, since equilibrium photons have zero chemical potential. Substitution of the nonrelativistic <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> for the three densities gives
$$
\frac{n_pn_e}{n_H}=\frac{g_pg_e}{g_H}\left(\frac{m_pm_eT}{2\pi m_H}\right)^{3/2}\exp\left[-\frac{m_p+m_e-m_H}{T}\right].
$$
For ground-state hydrogen use $g_e=g_p=2$, $g_H=4$, and $m_H\simeq m_p$ in the translational prefactor. Writing its <hydrogen binding energy> as $I$, this becomes $(m_eT/(2\pi))^{3/2}e^{-I/T}$. Charge neutrality gives $n_p=n_e=X_en_B$, while baryon conservation gives $n_H=(1-X_e)n_B$. Hence the <hydrogen-only Saha equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{1-X_e}{X_e^2}=n_B\left(\frac{2\pi}{m_eT}\right)^{3/2}e^{I/T}.}
$$
The photon <Bose-Einstein distribution> supplies $n_\gamma=2\zeta(3)T^3/\pi^2$, where $\zeta(3)$ is the usual <Riemann zeta function> value in the thermal number integral. With <baryon-to-photon ratio> $\eta_\gamma=n_B/n_\gamma$, substitution yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{1-X_e}{X_e^2}=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\eta_\gamma\left(\frac{2\pi T}{m_e}\right)^{3/2}e^{I/T}.}
$$
This reproduces the requested numerical coefficient, with the printed $\xi(3)$ interpreted as the thermal $\zeta(3)$ value. There is a normalization ambiguity in the PDF: it writes $\eta=n_B/s$ without defining $s$. To reproduce this coefficient, $s$ must mean photon number density. If $s$ has its usual <entropy density> meaning, the <Saha baryon-abundance normalization> instead gives
$$
s=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_{*s}T^3,\qquad \eta_s=\frac{n_B}{s},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{1-X_e}{X_e^2}=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_{*s}\eta_s\left(\frac{2\pi T}{m_e}\right)^{3/2}e^{I/T}.}
$$
The two forms agree through $\eta_\gamma=[\pi^4g_{*s}/(45\zeta(3))]\eta_s$, with the entropy sector specified. A baryon-to-entropy ratio cannot simply be inserted into the photon-normalized expression.

As the universe cools, the Boltzmann factor favors neutral atoms and <cosmological recombination> sharply lowers $X_e$. For an order-one ionized fraction the photon-normalized equation gives the balance
$$
\frac IT\simeq\log\left[\frac{\pi^2}{2\zeta(3)\eta_\gamma}\left(\frac{m_e}{2\pi T}\right)^{3/2}\right]
$$
up to an order-one ionization-fraction factor. The small baryon abundance means that there are very many photons per baryon: even a small high-energy photon tail can ionize most atoms. Thus the <recombination temperature> is far below the microscopic binding scale $I\simeq13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$, typically a few tenths of an eV for the standard abundance, rather than of order $I$.

<Photon decoupling> is a related but distinct kinetic event. The <Thomson scattering> rate $n_e\sigma_T$ falls relative to $H$ as free electrons disappear, and photons begin free streaming. The precise last-scattering epoch depends on integrated optical depth, not only the local equality of rates. At still lower density the effective recombination rate per electron also drops below the expansion rate. <Residual electron freeze-out> leaves a small nonzero ionized fraction, larger than the vanishingly small equilibrium Saha prediction; radiative atomic bottlenecks mean the entire history cannot be obtained from equilibrium alone.

The temperature comparison in the last request needs an interpretation. If “reionisation temperature” means the microscopic thermal ionization scale, the large photon-to-baryon ratio explains why cosmological recombination requires $T\ll I$. If it means later cosmic <reionization>, there is no universal thermal threshold: stars or other sources supply ionizing photons and can reheat gas. The cosmological photon background is actually colder at that later epoch, because $T_\gamma\propto a^{-1}$. Its temperature therefore cannot literally be higher than the background temperature at recombination merely because hydrogen is reionized.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For a homogeneous canonical <inflaton>, $\rho_\phi=\dot\phi^2/2+V$ and $P_\phi=\dot\phi^2/2-V$. <Vacuum domination by a scalar field> requires positive potential energy to dominate both kinetic energy and any competing material components. Then $P_\phi\simeq-\rho_\phi$ and the expansion is nearly vacuum-like. Exact scalar-driven acceleration requires $\rho_\phi+3P_\phi<0$, equivalently $\dot\phi^2<V$; potential domination is a stronger condition.

The <slow-roll approximation> additionally neglects $\ddot\phi$ compared with $3H\dot\phi$ in the <inflaton equation of motion>. Define the unreduced <Planck mass> $m_{\rm pl}=G^{-1/2}$; it differs from the <reduced Planck mass> by $\sqrt{8\pi}$. Sufficient potential-flatness conditions are
$$
\epsilon_V=\frac{m_{\rm pl}^2}{16\pi}\left(\frac{V'}V\right)^2\ll1,\qquad
|\eta_V|=\left|\frac{m_{\rm pl}^2}{8\pi}\frac{V''}V\right|\ll1,
$$
together with approach to the slow-roll attractor. The reduced equations are $H^2\simeq8\pi V/(3m_{\rm pl}^2)$ and $3H\dot\phi\simeq-V'$.

For $V=m^2\phi^2/2$, take $m>0$ and the positive-field rolling branch. Then
$$
H\simeq\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}}\frac{m\phi}{m_{\rm pl}},\qquad
\dot\phi\simeq-\frac{m^2\phi}{3H}=-\frac{mm_{\rm pl}}{2\sqrt{3\pi}}.
$$
The field velocity is constant to this order. Choosing the start at $t=0$ gives the <quadratic-potential slow-roll solution>
$$
\boxed{\phi(t)=\phi_i-\frac{mm_{\rm pl}}{2\sqrt{3\pi}}t.}
$$
The PDF puts $\pi$ inside this square root; the converted TeX incorrectly moves it outside. Next integrate $d\log a/d\phi=H/\dot\phi=-4\pi\phi/m_{\rm pl}^2$:
$$
\boxed{a(t)=a_0\exp\left[\frac{2\pi}{m_{\rm pl}^2}\bigl(\phi_i^2-\phi(t)^2\bigr)\right].}
$$
Thus the <number of e-folds> is the logarithmic expansion, not the expansion factor itself:
$$
\boxed{N=\log\frac{a_f}{a_i}=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}Hdt=\frac{2\pi}{m_{\rm pl}^2}(\phi_i^2-\phi_f^2).}
$$
Here $\epsilon_V=\eta_V=m_{\rm pl}^2/(4\pi\phi^2)$. The usual <quadratic-inflation endpoint estimate> sets $\epsilon_V\simeq1$, giving
$$
\boxed{\phi_{\rm end}\simeq\frac{m_{\rm pl}}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\qquad
N_{\rm total}\simeq\frac{2\pi\phi_i^2}{m_{\rm pl}^2}-\frac12.}
$$
This is an endpoint estimate, not an exact result from the full equations. Exact acceleration ends at the <Hubble slow-roll parameter> $\epsilon_H=1$, equivalently $\dot\phi^2=V$. Inserting the slow-roll velocity into that last condition instead gives $\phi\simeq m_{\rm pl}/\sqrt{6\pi}$; the different order-one coefficient illustrates the failing approximation at the endpoint. A precise value requires the full scalar and Friedmann evolution. The large initial-field contribution to $N$ is unaffected by that order-one uncertainty.

Finally $V(\phi_i)\sim m_{\rm pl}^4$ gives $\phi_i^2\sim2m_{\rm pl}^4/m^2$. The <quadratic-inflation expansion from Planck density> is therefore
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm total}\sim4\pi\left(\frac{m_{\rm pl}}m\right)^2,\qquad
\frac{a_{\rm end}}{a_0}\sim\exp\left[4\pi\left(\frac{m_{\rm pl}}m\right)^2\right].}
$$
For $m\ll m_{\rm pl}$ this is enormous and the initial field is in the slow-roll regime. No unique numerical factor follows without specifying $m/m_{\rm pl}$. Extrapolating the classical model from Planck density is only a formal estimate; it does not establish control of quantum-gravity corrections at that starting scale.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Assume standard radiation-dominated expansion, negligible chemical potential, one neutrino helicity and one antineutrino helicity, and a stable species $X$ with no later entropy injection. Use <natural units> and unreduced <Planck mass> $m_{\rm pl}\simeq1.22\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{GeV}$. The relativistic energy density is $\rho_R=\pi^2g_*T^4/30$. The <Friedmann equation> then gives
$$
H=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^3g_*}{45}}\frac{T^2}{m_{\rm pl}}\simeq1.66\sqrt{g_*}\frac{T^2}{m_{\rm pl}}.
$$
For <thermal decoupling in cosmology>, equate the interaction rate $G_A^2T_D^5$ to $H(T_D)$:
$$
\boxed{T_D\simeq\left(\frac{1.66\sqrt{g_*}}{G_A^2m_{\rm pl}}\right)^{1/3}
\simeq1.1\times10^2\,\mathrm{GeV}.}
$$
This order-of-magnitude estimate assumes the supplied rate law remains applicable there.

A decoupled relativistic species cools as $T_X\propto a^{-1}$, while annihilations heat the coupled bath. Treat the supplied high-temperature count $100$ as the remaining bath's entropy count, approximately equal to its energy count. <Cosmological entropy conservation> down to ordinary <neutrino decoupling> gives
$$
\frac{T_X}{T_\nu}=\left(\frac{10.75}{100}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
Subsequent <electron-positron annihilation in cosmology> heats photons but neither neutrino population. Therefore the <two-stage entropy dilution of an early-decoupled neutrino> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_X}{T_\gamma}=\left(\frac{10.75}{100}\frac4{11}\right)^{1/3}
=\left(\frac{43}{1100}\right)^{1/3}\simeq0.339.}
$$
The ordinary <cosmic neutrino background> has $T_\nu/T_\gamma=(4/11)^{1/3}$. If the rounded count $100$ instead includes the new two helicities, the remaining bath count is $98.25$; this changes the answer only slightly, to about $0.341$. Keeping the extra species' entropy separate is essential in either convention.

For the two relativistic fermion states, $g_{*s,X}=(7/8)2=7/4$. Photon and three standard neutrino species have present entropy degrees of freedom, referred to $T_\gamma$,
$$
g_{*s,\mathrm{std}}=2+\frac78\,6\left(\frac4{11}\right)=\frac{43}{11}.
$$
Writing $\theta=T_X/T_\gamma$, the <fractional entropy of a relativistically decoupled species> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{s_X}{s_{\rm total}}=
\frac{(7/4)\theta^3}{43/11+(7/4)\theta^3}
=\frac{7/4}{100+7/4}\simeq0.0172.}
$$
Thus approximately **1.7% of the thermal particle entropy is in the new neutrinos and antineutrinos**. If $100$ counts all states before the new decoupling, the fraction is $1.75\%$. These are the same rounded estimate. The question concerns the radiation/relic thermal entropy budget; it does not supply a model for entropy in later black holes or other gravitational structures.

If $X$ has a small mass but decoupled relativistically, its comoving particle number is still conserved. The <relativistic-neutrino number-density ratio> gives
$$
\frac{n_X}{n_\gamma}=\frac34\theta^3\simeq0.0293,\qquad
\frac{n_X}{n_{\nu,\mathrm{one\ standard\ species}}}=\frac{10.75}{100}=0.1075.
$$
Once nonrelativistic, $\rho_X\simeq m_Xn_X$. The <density bound on a relativistically decoupled massive relic> is therefore
$$
\boxed{m_X\leq\frac{\Omega_{X,\max}\rho_c}{n_X}.}
$$
Using the standard present photon abundance and critical-density normalization, this is approximately $\Omega_Xh^2\simeq m_X/(9\times10^2\,\mathrm{eV})$, where $h=H_0/(100\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}})$ is the present Hubble normalization, not a magnetic field. A stable relic must not exceed the permitted matter density; for example, if $\Omega_Mh^2=0.15$, even assigning all matter to it gives only an order-$10^2\,\mathrm{eV}$ mass ceiling.

A stronger restriction can follow from <neutrino free streaming> and the observed formation of <large-scale structure of the universe>: a light thermal population acts as hot rather than cold <dark matter>, erasing structure below its streaming scale. If at most a fraction $f_{\max}$ of the matter may be in this population, replace the overclosure ceiling by $m_X\lesssim9\times10^2 f_{\max}\Omega_Mh^2\,\mathrm{eV}$. The allowed fraction is observational input and is not supplied, so the paper does not determine a unique tighter mass limit. A species still relativistic today is radiation rather than matter; its contribution to the <effective number of neutrino species> is $\Delta N_{\rm eff}=(T_X/T_\nu)^4\simeq0.051$, and must instead satisfy the radiation-density constraints. These conclusions assume stable thermally populated states; decay or nonstandard entropy histories change the bounds.
