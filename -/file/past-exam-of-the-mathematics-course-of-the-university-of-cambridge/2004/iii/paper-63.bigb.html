= Solution
{parent=1}

After complete <ionization>, each <hydrogen> nucleus supplies one <ion> and one <electron>, while each <helium-4> nucleus supplies one <ion> and two <electrons>. Neglecting the small <metal mass fraction> $Z$ and putting $Y\simeq1-X$, the <mean molecular weight> therefore satisfies
$$
\frac1\mu=2X+\frac34Y\simeq\frac{3+5X}{4},\qquad \boxed{\mu\simeq\frac4{3+5X}}.
$$
Use <stellar homology> to compare models with common dimensionless profiles. The <stellar mass conservation equation>, <stellar hydrostatic equation> and <ideal gas law> give
$$
\rho_c\propto\frac M{R^3},\qquad P_c\propto\frac{GM^2}{R^4},\qquad T_c\propto\frac{\mu M}{R}.
$$
The <radiative diffusion in a star> equation then gives $L_{\rm rad}\propto RT_c^4/(\kappa_c\rho_c)$. Inserting the specified <opacity> yields $L_{\rm rad}\propto RT_c^7/(Z\rho_c^2)\propto\mu^7M^5/Z$. Independently, integrating the <stellar energy-generation rate> over the <mass> gives $L_{\rm nuc}\propto X^2\rho_cT_c^5M\propto X^2\mu^5M^7/R^8$. Equating these in <stellar thermal equilibrium> gives the <radiative homology with fifth-power hydrogen burning and inverse-cubic opacity>:
$$
R^8\propto X^2Z\mu^{-2}M^2,\qquad R\propto X^{1/4}Z^{1/8}\mu^{-1/4}M^{1/4},\qquad T_c\propto\frac{\mu^{5/4}M^{3/4}}{X^{1/4}Z^{1/8}}.
$$
At fixed <solar mass> these reduce to **$L\propto\mu^7/Z$ and $T_c\propto\mu^{5/4}/(X^{1/4}Z^{1/8})$.** The <stellar homology> approximation, rather than merely the local power laws, is what permits these relations between whole stars.

Equal <luminosities> imply $\mu_2/\mu_1=(Z_2/Z_1)^{1/7}=2^{-1/7}$. Thus
$$
3+5X_2=2^{1/7}(3+5X_1),\qquad X_2=\frac{6.5\,2^{1/7}-3}{5}=0.8353\ldots,
$$
so **$X_2=0.8$ to one significant figure**. Retaining the unrounded <hydrogen mass fraction> when comparing <central temperatures> gives
$$
\frac{T_{c,2}}{T_{c,1}}=2^{-3/56}\left(\frac{X_1}{X_2}\right)^{1/4}=0.9219\ldots<1.
$$
**The first model has the higher central temperature.**

For homogeneous <hydrogen burning> at fixed $M,Z$, fuel conservation gives $ME_0\dot X=-L$. Since $d\mu/dX=-5\mu^2/4$ and $L=L_0(\mu/\mu_0)^7$, the <homogeneous fuel-depletion luminosity feedback> obeys
$$
\dot\mu=\frac{5L_0}{4ME_0\mu_0^7}\mu^9,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\mu^{-8}=-\frac{10L_0}{ME_0\mu_0^7}.
$$
Integration with the initial <mean molecular weight> $\mu_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{L(t)=L_0\left(1-\frac{10\mu_0L_0t}{ME_0}\right)^{-7/8}}.
$$
This idealized <stellar evolution> law stops when $X=0$; it does not predict a physically attainable infinite <luminosity>. Indeed $\mu=4/3$ at fuel exhaustion, giving $t_H=ME_0[1-(3\mu_0/4)^8]/(10\mu_0L_0)$, strictly before the formal singularity.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Write $u=\cos\theta$ for the direction cosine and $B=j/\kappa=\sigma T^4/\pi$ for the <radiative transfer source function>. Integrating the <radiative transfer equation> $u\,\partial_\tau I=I-B$ over <solid angle> gives
$$
\frac{dF}{d\tau}=\int(I-B)\,d\Omega=4\pi(J-B).
$$
<Radiative equilibrium> makes the <radiative flux> $F$ constant, so **$J=B=j/\kappa$**.

For $I=A+Cu$, the <radiation-field moments> are
$$
J=\frac12\int_{-1}^1I\,du=A,\qquad F=2\pi\int_{-1}^1uI\,du=\frac{4\pi C}{3},\qquad cP_r=2\pi\int_{-1}^1u^2I\,du=\frac{4\pi A}{3}.
$$
Consequently the <Eddington closure approximation> holds. Substituting into the <radiative transfer equation>, with $B=J=A$, gives $uA'+u^2C'=Cu$. Equality for every $u$ requires $A'=C$ and $C'=0$, with $C=3F/(4\pi)$. The stipulated inward hemispheric <radiative flux> at the surface is
$$
F_{\rm in}=2\pi\int_{-1}^0u(A_0+Cu)\,du=-\pi A_0+\frac{2\pi C}{3}.
$$
Putting this equal to zero fixes $A_0=2C/3$, hence $A=C(\tau+2/3)$. Using $A=\sigma T^4/\pi$ and the <effective temperature> definition $F=\sigma T_e^4$ gives the <grey atmosphere> law
$$
\boxed{T^4=\frac34T_e^4\left(\tau+\frac23\right)},\qquad \boxed{T_0=2^{-1/4}T_e}.
$$
The <positivity limitation of a linear Eddington intensity> matters here: the formal surface intensity $I(0,u)=C(2/3+u)$ is negative for $u<-2/3$. Thus this angular ansatz gives the requested <Eddington surface boundary condition> for approximate moments; vanishing integrated inward flux is not an exact, nonnegative, pointwise no-incoming radiation field.

Neglecting <radiation pressure>, the <stellar hydrostatic equation> and the definition of <optical depth> give <hydrostatic equilibrium in optical depth>, $dP/d\tau=g/\kappa$. Since $T^4=T_0^4(1+3\tau/2)$, putting $v=T^4$ gives
$$
\frac{d(P^\alpha)}{dv}=\frac{2\alpha g}{3\kappa_0T_0^4}v^{\beta-1}.
$$
Taking $P(0)=0$ and $\alpha>0$, the <grey-atmosphere pressure with power-law opacity> is therefore
$$
\boxed{P^\alpha=\frac{2\alpha g}{3\beta\kappa_0T_0^4}\left(T^{4\beta}-T_0^{4\beta}\right)}\qquad(\beta\ne0).
$$
**The pressure formula printed in the PDF is missing the factor $1/\beta$.** Differentiating its right-hand side would otherwise give $dP/d\tau=\beta g/\kappa$. For $\beta=0$ the correct <limit> is $P^\alpha=2\alpha g\log(T^4/T_0^4)/(3\kappa_0T_0^4)$.

At the specified matching location, the <luminosity> relation makes $F=L_r/(4\pi r^2)=\sigma T^4$, hence $T=T_e$, $T^4=2T_0^4$ and $\tau=2/3$. Multiplying the corrected pressure relation by $\kappa_0T^{4-4\beta}/g$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{P\kappa}{g}=\frac{4\alpha}{3\beta}\left(1-2^{-\beta}\right)}.
$$
This agrees with the PDF's final <surface boundary condition for a power-law-opacity grey atmosphere>. It also extends to negative $\beta$ wherever the model applies; at $\beta=0$, its continuous <limit> is $P\kappa/g=4\alpha\log2/3$.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For <Roche-lobe overflow>, $R_2=R_L$. Cubing the <Roche lobe> approximation and using the donor's <mass-radius relation> gives
$$
a^3=\frac{R_2^3}{0.46^3}\frac M{M_2}=\frac{R_\odot^3}{0.46^3}\frac{M M_2^2}{M_\odot^3}.
$$
<Kepler's third law> therefore yields the <Roche-lobe period-mass relation for a linear donor radius law>:
$$
\boxed{\frac P{P_0}=\frac{M_2}{M_\odot}},\qquad P_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot^3}{0.46^3GM_\odot}}.
$$
The component distances from the <centre of mass> are $a_1=aM_2/M$ and $a_2=aM_1/M$. Summing their <angular momenta> gives the <circular-binary orbital angular momentum>
$$
\boxed{J=(M_1a_1^2+M_2a_2^2)\Omega=\frac{M_1M_2}{M}a^2\Omega=M_1M_2\sqrt{\frac{Ga}{M}}}.
$$
During <conservative binary mass transfer>, $\dot M=0$ and $\dot M_1=-\dot M_2$. Logarithmic differentiation at constant <angular momentum> gives $\dot a/a=-2(1-q)\dot M_2/M_2$. Thus the <Roche-lobe radius response exponent> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\dot R_L}{R_L}=\left(2q-\frac53\right)\frac{\dot M_2}{M_2}},\qquad \frac{\dot R_2}{R_2}=\frac{\dot M_2}{M_2}.
$$
Their difference is $d\log(R_L/R_2)/dt=(2q-8/3)\dot M_2/M_2$. Since the donor loses <mass>, this is positive for $q<4/3$: **the donor shrinks relative to its Roche lobe, so transfer shuts off without an external driver.** The <Roche lobe> itself expands only for $q<5/6$; for $5/6<q<4/3$ it shrinks more slowly than the donor.

<Gravitational-wave emission from a binary system> provides such a driver: the orbit emits <gravitational waves> carrying <energy> and <angular momentum>, and the shrinking <Roche lobe> can maintain contact. Allowing $\dot J<0$, the <binary mass-transfer contact equation> becomes
$$
\frac{\dot R_L}{R_L}=2\frac{\dot J}{J}+\left(2q-\frac53\right)\frac{\dot M_2}{M_2}=\frac{\dot M_2}{M_2},\qquad \boxed{\frac{\dot M_2}{M_2}=\frac{3}{4-3q}\frac{\dot J}{J}}.
$$
It gives a negative transfer rate for $q<4/3$.

For the <classical nova>, assume <isotropic re-emission from a binary star>: ejecta carry the accretor's <specific angular momentum> $j_1=a_1^2\Omega$. The eruption lasts many <orbital periods>, so the orbit responds approximately adiabatically and remains nearly circular; an instantaneous asymmetric kick would be a different model. To first order,
$$
\frac{\delta J}{J}=-\frac{j_1\delta m}{J}=-q\frac{\delta m}{M},\qquad \delta M_1=-\delta m,\qquad\delta M_2=0,\qquad\delta M=-\delta m.
$$
Differentiating the <circular-binary orbital angular momentum> formula during the ejection gives
$$
-q\frac{\delta m}{M}=-\frac{\delta m}{M_1}+\frac12\frac{\delta a}{a}+\frac12\frac{\delta m}{M},\qquad \boxed{\frac{\delta a}{a}=\frac{\delta m}{M}}.
$$
The accompanying <Roche lobe> change is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\delta R_L}{R_L}=\frac{\delta a}{a}+\frac13\frac{\delta m}{M}=\frac{4\delta m}{3M}}.
$$
The donor does not change <mass> in the eruption and moves inside its expanded <Roche lobe>: this is <nova-induced binary detachment>.

Put $\Gamma=-\dot J>0$ for the constant external loss. During detachment the component masses are fixed, so the <Roche lobe> shrinks at fractional rate $2\Gamma/J$. Closing the fractional gap $4\delta m/(3M)$ takes $t_d=2J\delta m/(3M\Gamma)$. During the subsequent <semidetached binary> phase, the <binary mass-transfer contact equation> gives $|\dot M_2|=3M_2\Gamma/[J(4-3q)]$, so accumulating the next eruption's <mass> takes $t_s=J(4-3q)\delta m/(3M_2\Gamma)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{t_d}{t_s}=\frac{2M_2}{M(4-3q)}=\frac{2q}{(4-3q)(1+q)}}.
$$
Both times are evaluated to first order in $\delta m/M$, with the same equilibrium donor <mass-radius relation>; changes of $q,J$ and the transfer rate within one cycle affect higher orders.

= Solution
{parent=4}

A $5M_\odot$ star of roughly solar <stellar composition> begins on the <zero-age main sequence> with a hot <convective core>, a mostly <radiative envelope>, and <hydrogen burning> dominated by the <CNO cycle>. The strong temperature dependence of the <CNO cycle> concentrates the <stellar energy-generation rate> near the centre, helping produce the <convective core>. Core mixing replenishes <hydrogen> within that region; it does not homogenize the entire star. The nuclear fuel supply gives a <stellar nuclear timescale> of order $10^8$ years. For a numerical comparison, Table 2 of https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.5049[Ekström et al.'s solar-metallicity models] gives core <hydrogen-burning> lifetimes of about $88$ and $109$ million years for nonrotating and rotating $5M_\odot$ models, respectively.

After central <hydrogen> exhaustion, an inert <helium> core contracts and heats through <Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction>, while a <hydrogen-burning shell> supplies the growing <luminosity>. The envelope expands and cools: the track crosses the <Hertzsprung gap> toward the <red giant> region. Its deepening <convective envelope> causes <first dredge-up>, bringing material altered by <hydrogen burning> to the surface. The rapid structural crossing has a thermal timescale, roughly $10^5$–$10^6$ years for a star of this <mass>, rather than the full <stellar nuclear timescale>; an estimate follows from the <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> $GM^2/(RL)$ with the evolving <radius> and <luminosity>.

The <helium> core reaches ignition before becoming strongly supported by <electron degeneracy pressure>. Thus a normal $5M_\odot$ star starts <core helium burning> without the <helium flash> characteristic of lower-mass stars. The <triple-alpha process> produces <carbon-12>, and <carbon-12 alpha capture> produces <oxygen-16>; these reactions build a <carbon-oxygen core>. There is a <convective core> and continued <hydrogen-burning shell> activity. Core <helium> burning lasts order $10^7$ years: the same https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.5049[model table] gives approximately $19$ and $18$ million years. The changing core and envelope structure can produce a <blue loop> in the <Hertzsprung-Russell diagram>; its extent depends on <stellar composition>, mixing and <stellar rotation>.

After central <helium> exhaustion, the <carbon-oxygen core> contracts and becomes increasingly supported by <electron degeneracy pressure>. A <helium-burning shell> forms above it, beneath the <hydrogen-burning shell>. The star returns to a cool, luminous <asymptotic giant branch> configuration. On this early branch, <helium-burning shell> activity is an important energy source; the overlying <hydrogen-burning shell> can weaken during readjustment and resume later. At this <mass>, a deepening <convective envelope> can cause <second dredge-up>. This shell-supported evolution to the first <AGB thermal pulse> takes order $10^6$ years, with model-dependent duration. As the <helium-burning shell> becomes sufficiently thin, the <thin-shell instability> ends steady shell burning and marks the onset of <AGB thermal pulses>.

\Image[paper-63-stellar-track.png]
{title=Schematic evolution of a five-solar-mass star to the first AGB thermal pulse}

The original sketch shows the direction of <stellar evolution>, with higher <effective temperature> to the left. It illustrates a possible <blue loop>; its coordinates and loop shape are schematic, rather than a computed stellar model. The stages before the first <AGB thermal pulse> are the requested track; the later remnant evolution is described below.

In the usual final evolution, <stellar winds> and an <AGB superwind> remove the envelope before the <carbon-oxygen core> can ignite substantial <carbon burning>. Once shell fuel is exhausted, the exposed core contracts and heats, potentially ionizing the expelled gas as a <planetary nebula>, and finally becomes a <carbon-oxygen white dwarf> that loses its stored thermal <energy> by cooling. The relevant competition is between the <AGB envelope-loss and core-growth timescales>, not just the total initial <mass>.

Much higher late-stage <mass loss> strips the envelope sooner, shortens the <asymptotic giant branch> phase, and allows less core growth, usually producing a smaller <carbon-oxygen white dwarf>. Much lower <mass loss> prolongs shell burning and permits greater core growth. In an extreme hypothetical case, retention of the envelope could let a degenerate <carbon-oxygen core> approach the <Chandrasekhar mass> and undergo explosive <carbon burning>: the proposed <type 1.5 supernova> channel. This is not an inevitable fate for a $5M_\odot$ star. https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.5431[Doherty et al.'s evolutionary calculations] emphasize the uncertainty of that channel and find that their adopted <mass loss> removes carbon-oxygen envelopes before such ignition. If <carbon burning> instead proceeds without disruption, the possible further evolution depends on the resulting core and continued envelope retention; lowering <mass loss> alone does not establish an <electron-capture supernova> outcome.
