= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i$. For $\Lambda>0$, the resulting <compound Poisson distribution> has
$$
\boxed{\text{count parameter }\Lambda,\qquad
F(x)=\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda}F_i(x).}
$$
Thus the merged <claim size> <probability distribution> is a rate-weighted <mixture distribution>, not an equally weighted <mixture distribution> unless the rates agree.

To prove this without any <moment-generating function> assumption, let $\chi_i(t)$ be the <characteristic function> of a claim of type $i$. Conditioning on its <Poisson distribution> count gives
$$
\mathbb Ee^{itS_i}=\exp\{\lambda_i(\chi_i(t)-1)\}.
$$
<Independence> of the aggregate risks therefore gives
$$
\mathbb Ee^{itS}
=\prod_i\exp\{\lambda_i(\chi_i(t)-1)\}
=\exp\left\{\Lambda\left(\sum_i\frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda}\chi_i(t)-1\right)\right\}.
$$
The weighted <characteristic function> inside is precisely that of the displayed <mixture distribution>. This is the transform of a sum of <independent> claims with <independent> count $\operatorname{Pois}(\Lambda)$, proving the claim by uniqueness of <characteristic functions>. This is <Poisson superposition of insurance portfolios>. If $\Lambda=0$, all risks make no claims <almost surely> and $S=0$; the severity law is then irrelevant.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the preceding construction with type-$i$ claims of deterministic size $a_i$. For $\Lambda=\sum_i\lambda_i>0$, the answer is
$$
\boxed{N\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\Lambda),\qquad
\mathbb P(X=a_i)=\frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda},\qquad
F_X(x)=\sum_{i:a_i\le x}\frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda}.}
$$
The <characteristic function> verifies this directly:
$$
\mathbb Ee^{itT}=\prod_i\exp\{\lambda_i(e^{ita_i}-1)\}
=\exp\{\Lambda(\mathbb Ee^{itX}-1)\}.
$$
Hence $T$ has the stated <compound Poisson distribution>. Distinctness of the $a_i$ makes the atomic <probabilities> unambiguous.

A zero coefficient is allowed. It corresponds to zero-sized claims in this representation and contributes nothing to $T$. If a representation using only positive claims is preferred, remove that category: its count parameter becomes $\Lambda_+=\sum_{i:a_i>0}\lambda_i$, with <probabilities> $\lambda_i/\Lambda_+$ on the positive values. The <zero claim sizes in a compound Poisson representation> explain why the count parameter need not be unique. If $\Lambda=0$, or if all positive-size categories have zero rate, the aggregate is identically zero.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Define $N_i=\sum_{k=1}^N\mathbf1_{\{X_k=a_i\}}$. Grouping equal <claim sizes> gives the pathwise identity
$$
\boxed{\widetilde T=\sum_{i=1}^m a_iN_i.}
$$
Conditional on $N=n$, the $n$ <independent> claims receive category labels with <probabilities> $p_i$. There are $n!/(n_1!\cdots n_m!)$ assignments producing prescribed category counts, and every assignment has <probability> $\prod_i p_i^{n_i}$. Thus the conditional <probability distribution> is a <multinomial distribution>:
$$
\mathbb P(N_1=n_1,\ldots,N_m=n_m\mid N=n)
=\frac{n!}{\prod_i n_i!}\prod_i p_i^{n_i}
$$
when the nonnegative counts sum to $n$, and is zero otherwise.

For $0\le z_i\le1$, condition first on $N$ and use the <probability generating function> of a <Poisson distribution>:
$$
\mathbb E\prod_i z_i^{N_i}
=\mathbb E\left(\sum_i p_i z_i\right)^N
=\exp\left\{\lambda\left(\sum_i p_i z_i-1\right)\right\}
=\prod_i\exp\{\lambda p_i(z_i-1)\}.
$$
This factorization is the joint <probability generating function> of <independent> <Poisson distribution> variables. Hence
$$
\boxed{N_i\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda p_i)\quad\text{independently}.}
$$
A category with $p_i=0$ has zero count <almost surely>. The <categorical thinning of a Poisson claim count> produces <independence> after averaging over the random total, even though the counts are constrained to sum to $n$ conditional on that total.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Let $M_X(t)=\mathbb Ee^{tX}$. Conditioning on $N\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)$ gives
$$
\mathbb Ee^{tS}=\mathbb E[M_X(t)^N]
=\exp\{\lambda(M_X(t)-1)\},\qquad
\boxed{K_S(t)=\lambda(M_X(t)-1).}
$$
Where the <moment-generating function> is finite around zero, differentiating the <cumulant-generating function> gives
$$
\boxed{\kappa_j(S)=K_S^{(j)}(0)=\lambda\mathbb E[X^j].}
$$
In particular $\mathbb ES=\lambda\mathbb EX$ and $\operatorname{Var}S=\lambda\mathbb EX^2$. These <compound Poisson cumulants> use raw claim <moments>. The first two formulas also follow by conditioning: $\mathbb E[S\mid N]=N\mathbb EX$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S\mid N)=N\operatorname{Var}X$, so
$$
\operatorname{Var}S=\mathbb E[N]\operatorname{Var}X+\operatorname{Var}N(\mathbb EX)^2
=\lambda\mathbb EX^2.
$$
This is the <law of total variance>. They therefore remain valid with finite first two <moments> even when no positive exponential <moment> exists.

Under per-claim <excess of loss reinsurance>, put $Y=\min(X,M)$ and let $W=(X-M)_+$ be the <positive part> of the excess. The insurer and reinsurer totals are sums of these payments over the same <Poisson distribution> count. Write $\overline F(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)$. Using the <tail integral formula for moments> on the tail <probabilities> of $Y$ and $W$ gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbb ES_I&=\lambda\int_0^M\overline F(x)\,dx,&
\operatorname{Var}S_I&=2\lambda\int_0^M x\overline F(x)\,dx,\\
\mathbb ES_R&=\lambda\int_M^\infty\overline F(x)\,dx,&
\operatorname{Var}S_R&=2\lambda\int_M^\infty(x-M)\overline F(x)\,dx.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For example, $\mathbb EY^2=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(Y^2>t)dt=2\int_0^M x\overline F(x)dx$ after $t=x^2$, and shifting by $M$ gives the reinsurer formula. The <moment> formulas are finite whenever the corresponding claim <moments> are finite.

For the specified <Pareto distribution>, with shape three and minimum $d>0$,
$$
\overline F(x)=\begin{cases}1,&0\le x\le d,\\d^3/x^3,&x\ge d,\end{cases}
\qquad\mathbb EX=3d/2,\quad\mathbb EX^2=3d^2.
$$
If $0\le M\le d$, every claim exceeds the <reinsurance retention>. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb ES_I&=\lambda M,&\operatorname{Var}S_I&=\lambda M^2,\\
\mathbb ES_R&=\lambda(3d/2-M),&\operatorname{Var}S_R&=\lambda(3d^2-3dM+M^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
If $M\ge d$, the tail integrals instead give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb ES_I&=\lambda\left(\frac{3d}{2}-\frac{d^3}{2M^2}\right),&
\operatorname{Var}S_I&=\lambda\left(3d^2-\frac{2d^3}{M}\right),\\
\mathbb ES_R&=\frac{\lambda d^3}{2M^2},&
\operatorname{Var}S_R&=\frac{\lambda d^3}{M}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently the requested sum of <variances> is
$$
V(M)=\lambda\begin{cases}
3d^2-3dM+2M^2,&0\le M\le d,\\
3d^2-d^3/M,&M\ge d.
\end{cases}
$$
On the first branch, $V'(M)=\lambda(4M-3d)$; it decreases until $M=3d/4$ and then increases. On the second branch $V'(M)=\lambda d^3/M^2>0$. For $\lambda>0$ the minimum is unique; if $\lambda=0$, all <variances> are zero and every <reinsurance retention> minimizes them. Thus
$$
\boxed{M^*=\frac{3d}{4},\qquad V(M^*)=\frac{15}{8}\lambda d^2.}
$$
The curve starts at $3\lambda d^2$, reaches this minimum, passes through $V(d)=2\lambda d^2$ with matching slope $\lambda d$ on both branches, and then increases concavely toward the horizontal <asymptote> $3\lambda d^2$. The <variance-minimizing retention for shape-three Pareto claims> is shown below.

\Image[paper-41-retention.png]
{title=Sum of insurer and reinsurer aggregate variances for shape-three Pareto claims, minimized at retention three quarters of the minimum claim size}

The two aggregate payouts are dependent. Conditioning on the common count gives <covariance> $\lambda\operatorname{Cov}(Y,W)+\lambda\mathbb EY\mathbb EW=\lambda\mathbb E[YW]=\lambda M\mathbb EW$, so $V(M)+2\operatorname{Cov}(S_I,S_R)=3\lambda d^2$ for every <reinsurance retention>, as required by $S_I+S_R=S$. The <covariance of two components of a compound Poisson sum> accounts for this distinction between a sum of <variances> and the <variance> of a sum.

There is a <moment> domain qualification for this heavy-tailed example. For every $t>0$, $\int_d^\infty e^{tx}3d^3x^{-4}dx=\infty$, and <moments> of order $j\ge3$ also diverge. Thus its positive <moment-generating function> is not finite and higher finite <cumulants> cannot be obtained by differentiating around zero. Its <mean> and <variance> calculations above are valid by conditioning or by the first two right derivatives at zero of the <Laplace transform of a nonnegative random variable>.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For nontrivial positive <claim sizes> in the <classical risk model> and $\lambda>0$, the <adjustment coefficient> is the unique positive solution
$$
\boxed{\lambda(M_X(R)-1)=cR,\qquad 0<R<r_\infty.}
$$
Indeed, $h(r)=\lambda(M_X(r)-1)-cr$ has $h(0)=0$, $h'(0)=\lambda\mu-c<0$, and is <strictly convex>. It becomes positive near the right endpoint of the <moment-generating function> domain. For a finite endpoint this follows from the assumed divergence; for an infinite endpoint, $\mathbb P(X\ge\varepsilon)>0$ for some $\varepsilon>0$ gives an exponential lower bound on $M_X(r)$, which eventually exceeds any linear function. <Convexity> gives existence and uniqueness of the positive crossing and $\lambda M_X'(R)-c>0$.

Put $q=\lambda\mu/c$ and $\overline F=1-F$. Substituting $\varphi=1-\psi$ into the supplied <survival renewal equation for a classical risk model>, and using $\int_0^\infty\overline F(x)dx=\mu$, yields
$$
\psi(u)=\frac{\lambda}{c}\int_u^\infty\overline F(x)\,dx
+\frac{\lambda}{c}\int_0^u\psi(u-x)\overline F(x)\,dx.
$$
Multiplying by $e^{Ru}$ gives the proper renewal-type equation
$$
\boxed{Z(u)=g_R(u)+\int_0^u Z(u-x)k_R(x)\,dx,}
\qquad
k_R(x)=\frac{\lambda}{c}e^{Rx}\overline F(x),\quad
g_R(u)=\frac{\lambda}{c}e^{Ru}\int_u^\infty\overline F(x)\,dx.
$$
The <exponential tilt of the ruin renewal kernel> turns a defective kernel into a <probability density function>, because the tail integration identity gives
$$
\int_0^\infty k_R(x)dx
=\frac{\lambda}{c}\frac{M_X(R)-1}{R}=1.
$$
This identity follows also by integrating $e^{RX}-1=R\int_0^X e^{Rx}dx$ and applying <Tonelli theorem>. The tilted <probability density function> defines a <nonarithmetic distribution>, even if the <claim size> <probability distribution> itself has atoms.

Differentiate $(M_X(R)-1)/R$ to calculate its <mean>:
$$
m_R=\int_0^\infty xk_R(x)dx
=\frac{\lambda}{c}\frac{RM_X'(R)-M_X(R)+1}{R^2}
=\frac{\lambda M_X'(R)-c}{cR}.
$$
It is finite and positive because $R$ is inside the finite <moment-generating function> domain. Next, reversing the order of integration gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty g_R(u)du
&=\frac{\lambda}{c}\int_0^\infty\overline F(x)\int_0^x e^{Ru}du\,dx\\
&=\frac{\lambda}{cR}\left(\frac{M_X(R)-1}{R}-\mu\right)
=\frac{c-\lambda\mu}{cR}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The forcing function is <directly Riemann integrable>. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $M_X(R+\varepsilon)<\infty$. <Markov's inequality> bounds $\overline F(x)$ by $M_X(R+\varepsilon)e^{-(R+\varepsilon)x}$, so $g_R(u)$ is bounded by a constant times $e^{-\varepsilon u}$. It is continuous; on finite intervals continuity controls its <Riemann sums>, and the exponential envelope controls the tails. These are sufficient conditions for <direct Riemann integrability>.

Iterating the <renewal equation> by <convolution> gives $Z=g_R*U_R$, where $U_R=\sum_{n\ge0}k_R^{*n}$ is the <renewal measure>. The remainder after $n$ iterations is bounded on each fixed interval by a constant times the chance that a sum of $n$ positive interarrivals remains in that interval, which tends to zero. The <key renewal theorem>, applicable to this <nonarithmetic distribution> of finite <mean> and the directly integrable forcing, now gives the <interior adjustment coefficient ruin prefactor>:
$$
\boxed{A=\lim_{u\to\infty}e^{Ru}\psi(u)
=\frac{\int_0^\infty g_R(u)du}{m_R}
=\frac{c-\lambda\mu}{\lambda M_X'(R)-c}.}
$$
Both numerator and denominator are positive, so $0<A<\infty$.

For the unit-rate shape-two <Erlang distribution>, $\mu=2$, $M_X(r)=(1-r)^{-2}$ and $M_X'(r)=2(1-r)^{-3}$. Write $\delta=\lambda/c$, so positive loading requires $0<\delta<1/2$. Dividing the <adjustment coefficient> equation by its nonzero root gives $\delta(2-R)=(1-R)^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=1-\frac{\delta+\sqrt{\delta^2+4\delta}}2,\qquad
A=\frac{1-2\delta}{2\delta(1-R)^{-3}-1}
=\frac{(1-R)(3-2R)}{3-R}.}
$$
The last simplification uses $\delta=(1-R)^2/(2-R)$; it is the <Erlang shape-two ruin prefactor>. Its value depends on the premium-to-arrival ratio, which is not specified numerically.

For the final exponential representation, evaluating at zero in the <survival renewal equation for a classical risk model> gives **$\lambda\mu/c=\psi(0)=a+b$**. If the slower term is present, necessarily $a>0$ and $e^u\psi(u)\to a$. Comparing with the finite positive asymptotic constant just proved yields
$$
\boxed{R=1,\qquad A=a,\qquad\lambda\mu/c=a+b\quad(a>0).}
$$
A smaller <adjustment coefficient> would make the limit zero and a larger one would make it infinite. Here the <leading exponential term determines a ruin adjustment coefficient>. The coefficient $b$ need not be assumed positive to identify the leading term.

The wording does not specify that $a$ is nonzero. If $a=0$ and $b>0$, the only surviving term instead gives **$R=6$, $A=b$ and $\lambda\mu/c=b$**. This case is realizable: claims with an <exponential distribution> of rate $\beta=6+\lambda/c$ have <ultimate ruin probability> $(\lambda/(c\beta))e^{-6u}$. Negative $a$ would make the <ultimate ruin probability> negative for large $u$, and both coefficients zero are incompatible with a nontrivial positive claim rate and <mean>. These observations specify the coefficient qualification needed for the usual $R=1$ answer.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Use the shape-rate convention for the <gamma distribution> $\theta\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)$, with <prior distribution> <probability density function> proportional to $\theta^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\theta}$. The observations are <independent> conditional on the common parameter $\theta$. Unconditionally a common nondegenerate <prior distribution> induces <covariance> $\alpha/\beta^2$ between distinct observations, so the <Bayesian inference> model requires conditional <independence>.

For unit-exposure counts, the <likelihood> is proportional to $\theta^{\sum_i x_i}e^{-n\theta}$. Multiplying by the <prior distribution> gives
$$
\theta\mid\mathbf x\sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left(\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^n x_i,\ \beta+n\right).
$$
The future count has <conditional expectation> $\theta$, so <posterior distribution> averaging gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathbf x]
=\frac{\alpha+\sum_i x_i}{\beta+n}
=\frac{n}{n+\beta}\overline x+\frac{\beta}{n+\beta}\frac\alpha\beta.}
$$
This is an exact <Bayesian credibility> estimate, with <credibility factor> $Z=n/(n+\beta)$ and collective <mean> $\alpha/\beta$.

For the <insurance exposure> and <claims inflation> calculation put $c_j=c(1+r)^j$, assuming $c>0$, $1+r>0$ and positive policy counts $n_j$. Let $K_j$ be the total claim count in year $j$. <Conditional independence> of the policy counts gives $K_j\mid\theta\sim\operatorname{Pois}(n_j\theta)$, and the observed average amount satisfies
$$
Y_j=\frac{c_jK_j}{n_j},\qquad k_j=\frac{n_jy_j}{c_j}.
$$
For possible observations, the recovered $k_j$ are nonnegative integers. If they are not, the data have zero <likelihood> under the model and no conditional <posterior distribution> is defined for those impossible values.

Write $W=\sum_{j=1}^n n_j$ and $K=\sum_{j=1}^n k_j$. Since $Y_j$ and $K_j$ determine each other, conditioning on the observed average amounts is equivalent to conditioning on these counts. The <independent> yearly count <likelihood> is
$$
\prod_{j=1}^n e^{-n_j\theta}\frac{(n_j\theta)^{k_j}}{k_j!}
\propto\theta^K e^{-W\theta}.
$$
The <Poisson-gamma conjugacy with unequal exposures> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid\mathbf y\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha+K,\beta+W).}
$$
For the future year, $\mathbb E[Y_{n+1}\mid\theta]=c_{n+1}\theta$; the number of future policies cancels because $Y_{n+1}$ is a per-policy average. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Y_{n+1}\mid\mathbf y]
=c_{n+1}\frac{\alpha+K}{\beta+W}
=Z\,m(\mathbf y)+(1-Z)m,}
$$
with
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
Z&=\frac{W}{W+\beta},\\
m&=c(1+r)^{n+1}\frac\alpha\beta,\\
m(\mathbf y)&=c_{n+1}\frac KW
=\frac1W\sum_{j=1}^n n_j(1+r)^{n+1-j}y_j.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The collective <mean> $m$ is the <prior distribution> expected claim amount per policy in the future year's monetary units. The experience <mean> $m(\mathbf y)$ first inflates each past per-policy amount to that same future year and then averages with policy-exposure weights. Equivalently, it estimates annual claim frequency by total observed claims divided by total past <insurance exposure> and converts that frequency to the future claim cost. This is <inflation-adjusted Poisson-gamma credibility>.

For fixed <prior distribution> parameters, $Z$ increases strictly with total past <insurance exposure>: $dZ/dW=\beta/(W+\beta)^2>0$, and **$Z\to1$ as $W\to\infty$**. The <prior distribution> weight decreases to zero; $\beta$ acts as equivalent <prior distribution> <insurance exposure>. With no past <insurance exposure> the appropriate weight is zero and the prediction is $m$, without defining an empirical experience <mean>.
