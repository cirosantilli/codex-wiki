A <chain complex> consists of <abelian groups> $C_q$ and <homomorphisms> $\partial_q:C_q\to C_{q-1}$ with $\partial_{q-1}\partial_q=0$. Its groups of <chain cycles> and <chain boundaries> are $Z_q=\ker\partial_q$ and $B_q=\operatorname{im}\partial_{q+1}$; the identity ensures $B_q\subseteq Z_q$. Its <homology groups> are
$$
H_q(C_*)=Z_q/B_q.
$$
For a nonnegative complex, take $C_q=0$ for $q<0$.

The <singular chain group> $C_q(X)$ is the <free abelian group> on <continuous maps> $\sigma:\Delta^q\to X$, called <singular simplices>. With $\delta_i$ the inclusion of the face opposite vertex $i$, define
$$
\partial\sigma=\sum_{i=0}^q(-1)^i\sigma\circ\delta_i.
$$
Deleting any two vertices in the two possible orders gives the same face with opposite signs, proving $\partial^2=0$. This defines the <singular chain complex> and its <singular homology>. A <continuous map> $f$ induces the <chain map> $f_\#(\sigma)=f\circ\sigma$; it commutes with the boundary because composition commutes with restriction to faces.

To prove <homotopy invariance of homology>, let $F:X\times I\to Y$ have endpoints $f,g$. Triangulate $\Delta^q\times I$ by the $q+1$ simplices with ordered vertices
$$
(v_0,0),\ldots,(v_i,0),(v_i,1),\ldots,(v_q,1),\qquad 0\le i\le q.
$$
Let $p_i:\Delta^{q+1}\to\Delta^q\times I$ be their affine parameterizations. The <singular prism operator> is
$$
P_q(\sigma)=\sum_{i=0}^q(-1)^iF\circ(\sigma\times1_I)\circ p_i.
$$
On taking boundaries, shared internal faces of consecutive prism simplices cancel. The top and bottom faces give $g_\#\sigma-f_\#\sigma$, and the remaining faces are $-P_{q-1}(\partial\sigma)$ with their induced signs. Thus
$$
\partial P+P\partial=g_\#-f_\#.
$$
For a <chain cycle> $z$, $g_\#z-f_\#z=\partial Pz$ is a <chain boundary>, so $f_*=g_*$ on <homology>. If $f$ is a <homotopy equivalence>, its <homotopy inverse> therefore induces an inverse to $f_*$, proving that <singular homology> depends only on <homotopy type>.

Use the standard <CW complex> for an orientable genus-three surface: one vertex, six edges $a_1,b_1,a_2,b_2,a_3,b_3$, and a two-cell whose attaching word is $\prod_{j=1}^3[a_j,b_j]$. The <cellular homology theorem> identifies this cellular calculation with <singular homology>. The boundary of every edge is zero, and each edge's total exponent in the attaching word is zero, so the two-cell boundary is zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{H_q(\Sigma_3;\mathbb Z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&q=0,2,\\\mathbb Z^6,&q=1,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Choose $C_1$ to be the <nonseparating curve> represented by $a_1$, and choose $C_2$ as a small circle bounding a disc in a surface patch. Then $[C_1]$ is a nonzero <primitive homology class>, whereas the inclusion of $C_2$ induces zero on $H_1$. If a <homotopy equivalence> carried $C_1$ onto $C_2$, its restriction would factor through $C_2$ and kill $[C_1]$, contradicting injectivity on first <homology>. This is the <homology obstruction to carrying surface curves onto one another>.

There is nevertheless such a <continuous map>. Map the edge $a_1$ homeomorphically onto $S^1$ and all other edges to its base point. The two-cell attaching word then has <winding number> zero in $S^1$, hence is <null-homotopic> and the map extends to $r:\Sigma_3\to S^1$. Compose $r$ with a <homeomorphism> $S^1\to C_2$ and the inclusion of $C_2$ in the surface. This gives $g(C_1)=C_2$. Thus **an arbitrary map can carry the first curve onto the second, but a <homotopy equivalence> cannot**.

For an unambiguous drawing, realize $\Sigma_3$ as the <double of a manifold> formed from a disc with three holes. Its two copies are glued along all four boundary circles. The red curve below follows an arc from the outer boundary to the first hole on the front copy and returns along the matching arc on the back copy; it is a <nonseparating curve>. The blue circle is entirely in a disc patch on the front copy and is <contractible>.

A <CW complex> is built by attaching q-dimensional discs to the $(q-1)$-skeleton along maps of their boundary <spheres>, with the closure-finite and weak-topology conditions. Write $X^q$ for its q-skeleton and $X^{-1}=\varnothing$. The <cellular chain group> is
$$
C_q^{\mathrm{cell}}(X)=H_q(X^q,X^{q-1})\cong\bigoplus_{\text{q-cells}}\mathbb Z.
$$
The <cellular boundary> is the composite of the connecting map and the relative quotient map:
$$
H_q(X^q,X^{q-1})\xrightarrow{\delta}H_{q-1}(X^{q-1})\longrightarrow H_{q-1}(X^{q-1},X^{q-2}).
$$
Consecutive maps in the relevant exact sequences compose to zero, so these maps make a <cellular chain complex>. Equivalently, a boundary coefficient is the degree of the attaching <sphere> followed by collapse onto the <sphere> of a selected lower cell. Its <cellular homology> is $\ker d_q/\operatorname{im}d_{q+1}$. The relative groups of successive skeleta vanish off their cell dimension; their exact sequences identify this <homology> with <singular homology>, the <cellular homology theorem>.

For $k\ge1$, the <sphere> $S^k$ has a zero-cell and a k-cell with zero boundary. The <complex projective space> $\mathbb{CP}^n$ has one cell in each dimension $0,2,\ldots,2n$ and no odd cells, so all cellular boundaries vanish. Consequently
$$
\boxed{H_q(S^k)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&q=0,k,\\0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad
H_q(\mathbb{CP}^n)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&q\in\{0,2,\ldots,2n\},\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
At $k=0$, the <sphere> consists of two points and $H_0(S^0)=\mathbb Z^2$; at $n=0$, projective space is a point.

Put $a=2n-2$ and $Z=S^a\times S^3$. Its product <CW complex> has cells in dimensions $0,3,a,a+3=2n+1$. For $n>3$, these positive dimensions are distinct and never consecutive, so all <cellular boundaries> vanish. Thus
$$
\boxed{H_q(Z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&q\in\{0,3,2n-2,2n+1\},\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
Its $2n$-skeleton is $S^a\vee S^3$. Collapsing it gives the quotient
$$
q:Z\longrightarrow S^a\wedge S^3\cong S^{2n+1}.
$$
It induces an isomorphism on top <homology>, with degree $+1$ after choosing the quotient <orientation>. Let $p:S^{2n+1}\to\mathbb{CP}^n$ be the <Hopf fibration> and set $\phi=pq$, the <Hopf projection of a product smash quotient>.

On <reduced homology>, the collapse kills the two lower-dimensional classes, and the remaining top class maps to zero because projective space has no odd-dimensional <homology>. Therefore $\phi_*=0$ in every reduced degree. On <homotopy groups>, $\pi_j(Z)\cong\pi_j(S^a)\oplus\pi_j(S^3)$ for $j\ge2$, with summands induced by the two factor inclusions. Both inclusions are collapsed by $q$, so $q_*$ and hence $\phi_*$ vanish on both summands. The domain is <simply connected> and connected, which also handles the lower groups.

It remains to prove that $\phi$ is not <null-homotopic>. The <Hopf fibration> has fibre $S^1$ and has the <homotopy lifting property> for the CW domain $Z$: a <homotopy> downstairs and a lift of its initial map have a lift with that specified initial value. If $\phi$ were homotopic to a constant, lift this <homotopy> starting at $q$. The ending map would land in the single fibre $S^1$. Every map $Z\to S^1$ is <null-homotopic>, since the <simply connected> domain permits its lift to the contractible <universal cover> $\mathbb R\to S^1$. Thus $q$ would be <null-homotopic> in $S^{2n+1}$, contradicting its nonzero top <homology> map. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi_*\text{ vanishes on all homotopy groups and reduced homology, but }\phi\not\simeq *.}
$$

An <orientation> of a topological n-manifold without boundary is a choice of generator $\mu_x$ of each local <homology> group $H_n(M,M\setminus\{x\};\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z$, compatible in coordinate neighbourhoods. Equivalently, local <orientations> of coordinate discs are chosen so their overlap changes have degree $+1$. This trivializes the <orientation local system>.

Here the printed $H^n_{\mathrm{ct}}$ denotes <compactly supported cohomology>, which we write $H_c^n$. We prove the cyclicity assertion without assuming <Poincare duality>. By the <compactly supported cohomology of Euclidean space>, an open n-disc has one copy of $\mathbb Z$ in degree $n$ and zero in all other degrees. The compact-support <Mayer-Vietoris sequence> for open sets contains
$$
H_c^n(U\cap V)\longrightarrow H_c^n(U)\oplus H_c^n(V)
\longrightarrow H_c^n(U\cup V)\longrightarrow H_c^{n+1}(U\cap V).
$$
Use the finite good cover allowed in the paper. Induction on the number of discs shows that any union of its discs has zero compact-support <cohomology> above degree $n$: at each step the intersection with the new disc is itself a union of fewer discs with a good cover, so the displayed sequence and its higher-degree terms apply.

The same induction presents $H_c^n(M)$ by a generator $u_i$ for each cover disc, with overlap relations $u_i=\epsilon_{ij}u_j$, where $\epsilon_{ij}=\pm1$ is its <orientation> transition sign. Indeed $H_c^n$ of the intersection is generated by its overlap discs; extension by zero sends each generator to the two disc generators with these signs. Passing through the compact-support <Mayer-Vietoris sequence> imposes exactly those relations. This is <top compactly supported cohomology from orientation signs>.

Since $M$ is connected, the overlap graph is connected: a disconnection would split the union into two disjoint nonempty open subsets. A spanning tree expresses every generator as $u$ or $-u$, so $H_c^n(M)$ is cyclic. Additional overlap relations can only impose $2u=0$. If $M$ is oriented, use its compatible local <orientations>; every relation becomes $u_i=u_j$, and the presentation is exactly one free generator. Thus
$$
\boxed{H_c^n(M)\text{ is cyclic};\qquad H_c^n(M)\cong\mathbb Z\text{ if }M\text{ is oriented}.}
$$
In fact a nonorientable connected manifold has an orientation-reversing loop in this transition graph and gives $\mathbb Z/2$. In dimension zero the connected manifold is a point and the assertion is immediate.

For a closed oriented n-manifold, <Poincare duality> says that its <fundamental class> $[M]\in H_n(M;\mathbb Z)$ gives isomorphisms by <cap product>
$$
H^q(M;\mathbb Z)\xrightarrow{\ \cap[M]\ }H_{n-q}(M;\mathbb Z)\qquad\text{for all }q.
$$
For an oriented manifold that is not necessarily compact, the corresponding ordinary-homology statement is $H_c^q(M;\mathbb Z)\cong H_{n-q}(M;\mathbb Z)$, using the locally finite <fundamental class>. The version without compact supports on <cohomology> pairs with locally finite <homology> instead. Orientability and the absence of boundary are essential to these untwisted formulas; for a manifold with boundary one needs <Poincare-Lefschetz duality>, and for a nonorientable manifold one uses the <orientation local system>.

For the following cases, $H_0=\mathbb Z$ forces connectedness. The <universal coefficient theorem for cohomology> gives
$$
H^1(M;\mathbb Z)\cong\operatorname{Hom}(H_1(M;\mathbb Z),\mathbb Z),
$$
since the Ext term from $H_0=\mathbb Z$ vanishes. Combining with duality yields the <third homology of a closed oriented four-manifold> constraint $H_3\cong\operatorname{Hom}(H_1,\mathbb Z)$.

For an oriented real <vector bundle> $\pi:E\to X$ of rank $d$, the <Thom isomorphism theorem> gives a unique <Thom class> $u_E\in H^d(D(E),S(E);\mathbb Z)$ restricting to the chosen generator in each fibre, and isomorphisms
$$
H^q(X;\mathbb Z)\xrightarrow{\ a\mapsto\pi^*a\smile u_E\ }H^{q+d}(D(E),S(E);\mathbb Z).
$$
Equivalently use the pair $(E,E\setminus X)$, identifying $X$ with the <zero section>. After forgetting relative supports, pull the <Thom class> back along the <zero section> $s$ to define the <Euler class>:
$$
\boxed{e(E)=s^*u_E\in H^d(X;\mathbb Z).}
$$
In this expression the relative class is first mapped to absolute <cohomology>.

A compact embedded <submanifold> equipped with a <coorientation> $Y\subset X$ has an oriented <normal bundle> $\nu_Y$ of rank $d$. A <tubular neighborhood> and <excision> identify its <Thom class> with a class $u_Y\in H^d(X,X\setminus Y)$. Its image in $H^d(X)$ is the <cohomology class of a cooriented submanifold> $\varepsilon_Y$. In particular it restricts to zero on $X\setminus Y$. If the ambient manifold is oriented, orient $Y$ by the normal-first convention. The class is its <Poincare dual>, characterized by
$$
\langle\varepsilon_Y\smile\alpha,[X]\rangle=\langle\alpha|_Y,[Y]\rangle
$$
for the appropriate complementary degree. This is the stated integration identity in cohomological notation.

First prove the <Lefschetz fixed-point theorem> for an oriented closed n-manifold $X$. Orient its diagonal by the identification $\Delta\cong X$, and choose the normal <orientation> so this agrees with the normal-first convention in the product. This produces the integral class $\varepsilon_\Delta$. To compute the part that contributes to the <trace>, tensor with $\mathbb Q$: the <trace> on a finitely generated integral <cohomology> group means its <trace> on the free quotient, equivalently on rational <cohomology>; torsion contributes nothing.

Choose a basis $e_{p,j}$ of $H^p(X;\mathbb Q)$ and its <Poincare duality pairing> dual basis $e_{p,j}^{\vee}\in H^{n-p}(X;\mathbb Q)$, normalized by $\langle e_{p,i}\smile e_{p,j}^{\vee},[X]\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. The rational image of the <cohomology class of the diagonal> is
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_\Delta=\sum_{p,j}(-1)^{np}\,e_{p,j}^{\vee}\times e_{p,j}.}
$$
Indeed, pairing this expression against $\alpha\times\beta$ with degrees $p,n-p$ picks out the p-summands. The cross-product multiplication contributes $(-1)^{p^2}$ and moving $\alpha$ past $e_{p,j}^{\vee}$ contributes $(-1)^{p(n-p)}$. Together with $(-1)^{np}$ their product is $+1$. The result is $\langle\alpha\smile\beta,[X]\rangle$, exactly the diagonal's defining identity. The rational <Kunneth theorem> and nondegeneracy of the duality pairing determine the class. An integral torsion component, if present, is not determined by this rational expression and does not affect any evaluation below.

For the graph map $\Gamma_f=(1,f):X\to X\times X$, write $f^*e_{p,j}=\sum_iA_{ij}e_{p,i}$. Pulling back the expression and evaluating gives the <graph-diagonal formula for the Lefschetz number>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\Gamma_f^*\varepsilon_\Delta,[X]\rangle
&=\sum_{p,j}(-1)^{np}\langle e_{p,j}^{\vee}\smile f^*e_{p,j},[X]\rangle\\
&=\sum_p(-1)^{np+p(n-p)}\operatorname{tr}A_p
=\sum_p(-1)^p\operatorname{tr}(f^*:H^p(X;\mathbb Q)\to H^p(X;\mathbb Q))=L(f).
\end{aligned}
$$
If $f$ has no fixed point, its graph lies in $(X\times X)\setminus\Delta$. The relative <Thom class> restricts to zero there, so $\Gamma_f^*\varepsilon_\Delta=0$ and $L(f)=0$. The contrapositive proves the required fixed-point assertion, without requiring smoothness of $f$ or transversality of its graph.

The printed hypothesis does not require $X$ to be orientable. For a nonorientable $X$, the diagonal's <normal bundle> is $TX$, so an ordinary integral coorientation cannot be assumed. We extend the proved theorem as follows, rather than apply the untwisted diagonal computation incorrectly. We first construct a <smooth embedding> in Euclidean space using finitely many charts $\chi_i:U_i\to\mathbb R^n$ and smooth functions $\rho_i$ supported in those charts, with at least one $\rho_i(x)>0$ at every point. The map $\Psi(x)=(\rho_i(x),\rho_i(x)\chi_i(x))_i$, with zero extensions, is injective: a positive first coordinate identifies a chart in which its remaining coordinates recover $x$. Its differential is injective as well, since $d\rho_i(v)=0$ and $d(\rho_i\chi_i)(v)=0$ force $d\chi_i(v)=0$ in a chart with $\rho_i>0$. Compactness makes this injective immersion a <homeomorphism> onto its image. This <finite-chart Euclidean embedding of a compact smooth manifold>, enlarged by zero coordinates if necessary, has positive codimension. A compact tubular disc neighbourhood $W$ is an oriented N-manifold with boundary, inheriting its <orientation> from the ambient Euclidean space. Its <double of a manifold> $M$ is closed and oriented. Projection of each copy of $W$ to $X$ agrees on their common boundary, giving $r:M\to X$; inclusion into the first copy gives $i:X\to M$ with $ri=1_X$.

Set $F=ifr$. A fixed point of $F$ must lie in $i(X)$, and then is exactly the image of a fixed point of $f$. Moreover $F^*=r^*f^*i^*$, and cyclicity of <trace> for maps between finite-dimensional rational <cohomology groups> gives
$$
\operatorname{tr}(r^*f^*i^*)=\operatorname{tr}(f^*i^*r^*)=\operatorname{tr}(f^*)
$$
in each degree, because $i^*r^*=1$. Thus $L(F)=L(f)$. The oriented theorem for $M$ now gives a fixed point of $F$, hence of $f$, whenever $L(f)\ne0$. This <oriented manifold retract for the Lefschetz theorem> proves the full printed result. The double construction also works componentwise if necessary.

For a <sphere> self-map, the <Lefschetz number> is $L(f)=1+(-1)^n\deg f$. At degree zero this is $1$, so the <Lefschetz fixed-point theorem> supplies $x$ with $f(x)=x$.

Let $A:S^n\to S^n$ be the <antipodal map>, $A(z)=-z$, of degree $(-1)^{n+1}$. The composite $Af$ also has degree zero, hence Lefschetz number one, so it has a fixed point $y$. The equation $A(f(y))=y$ is $f(y)=-y$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\exists x,y\in S^n:\ f(x)=x,\quad f(y)=-y.}
$$
The two points are distinct, since a unit vector is never its own negative.

Use the standard integral <cohomology ring> $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^2)=\mathbb Z[u]/(u^3)$, $|u|=2$. Any self-homeomorphism $h$ sends $u$ to $u$ or $-u$, and hence sends $u^2$ to $u^2$. Its <Lefschetz number> is therefore $1\pm1+1$, which is either $3$ or $1$. By the <Lefschetz fixed-point theorem>, every such <homeomorphism> has a fixed point.

Now $\mathbb{CP}^2$ is <simply connected>: its CW structure has no one-cells and only two- and four-cells. If it covered a connected space with degree $d\ge2$, it would be the <universal cover>. The usual <covering space> hypotheses hold for the base because it is locally homeomorphic to this manifold. Its <deck transformation group> would act transitively on every fibre and have $d$ elements. A nonidentity <deck transformation> has no fixed point: uniqueness of lifts makes one fixing a point the identity everywhere on the connected cover. This contradicts the preceding calculation. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb{CP}^2\text{ is not the total space of a covering of degree at least two}.}
$$
Thus the <complex projective plane has no nontrivial covering quotient>.

Over the <Grassmannian> $\operatorname{Gr}_k(\mathbb C^N)$, the <complex tautological bundle on a Grassmannian> is
$$
\gamma_k^N=\{(W,v):W\in\operatorname{Gr}_k(\mathbb C^N),\ v\in W\},\qquad (W,v)\longmapsto W.
$$
Its fibre at $W$ is the k-plane $W$ itself. A neighbourhood of a fixed plane consists of graphs of linear maps $A:W\to W^\perp$; $(A,w)\mapsto(\operatorname{graph}A,w+Aw)$ gives a <local trivialization>. The inclusions of coordinate spaces give the infinite <Grassmannian> and its tautological rank-k bundle $\gamma_k^\infty$.

Use the customary compact Hausdorff base convention for the <classification of complex vector bundles by a Grassmannian>; equivalently the argument uses finite subordinate partitions of unity. Give a rank-k <complex vector bundle> $E\to X$ a continuous <fiber metric> given by Hermitian <inner products>. Choose a finite trivializing cover with local orthonormal coordinates $t_i:E|_{U_i}\to U_i\times\mathbb C^k$, and a subordinate <partition of unity> $\rho_i$ with support contained in $U_i$. Define
$$
J_x(v)=\bigl(\sqrt{\rho_i(x)}\,t_{i,x}(v)\bigr)_i\in\mathbb C^{kr},
$$
extending each component by zero off its support. This is continuous and satisfies $\|J_x(v)\|^2=\sum_i\rho_i(x)\|v\|^2=\|v\|^2$, so it is a fibrewise embedding. The image planes vary continuously: in local frames, their <orthogonal projection matrix> is $J(J^*J)^{-1}J^*$. Thus they define a map $c_E:X\to\operatorname{Gr}_k(\mathbb C^{kr})$, and $v\mapsto(x,J_xv)$ identifies $E$ with $c_E^*\gamma_k^\infty$. This proves that every bundle is a tautological <pullback vector bundle>, by the <finite-dimensional embedding of a complex vector bundle>.

We next prove the two independence assertions that turn this construction into a bijection. First, homotopic maps have isomorphic <pullback vector bundles>. Pulling back along a <homotopy> gives a bundle over compact Hausdorff $X\times I$; embed that bundle in a finite <trivial vector bundle> by the construction just given. Its image has a continuous family of rank-k <orthogonal projection matrices> $P(x,t)$. <Compactness> gives a subdivision $0=t_0<\cdots<t_s=1$ with
$$
\|P(x,t_{j+1})-P(x,t_j)\|<1\qquad\text{for every }x.
$$
Restriction of $P(x,t_{j+1})$ to the image at $t_j$ is injective: if it killed $v$, then $v=(P(x,t_j)-P(x,t_{j+1}))v$, impossible by the strict norm bound unless $v=0$. Equal ranks make it an isomorphism, with continuous local inverses. Composing these bundle maps identifies the endpoint <vector bundles>. The fact that <close orthogonal projections identify their image bundles> therefore makes the pullback assignment well-defined on <homotopy> classes.

Conversely, suppose two classifying maps give isomorphic <vector bundles>. The infinite <Grassmannian> has its standard weak CW topology; the compact-subset property of <CW complexes> puts the compact images of the two maps in finite stages. Their tautological <pullback vector bundles> therefore give two finite-dimensional embeddings $J_0:E\to\mathbb C^{N_0}$ and $J_1:E\to\mathbb C^{N_1}$ of the same bundle, after using the <vector bundle isomorphism>. In orthogonal coordinate blocks interpolate by
$$
J_t(v)=\left(\cos\frac{\pi t}{2}\,J_0(v),\ \sin\frac{\pi t}{2}\,J_1(v)\right).
$$
At least one coefficient is nonzero, so every $J_t$ is injective and its image planes give a continuous <Grassmannian> <homotopy>. One endpoint uses the first block and the other the second block. Moving the latter coordinate copy to the standard one is a fixed unitary change of coordinates, connected to the identity by a path in the <unitary group>. Such a path exists by diagonalizing the <unitary matrix> and continuously varying its <eigenvalue> phases. Hence the original two maps are homotopic in the infinite <Grassmannian>. This also proves independence of all embedding and metric choices. Together the two directions establish
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbb C}^k(X)\longleftrightarrow[X,\operatorname{Gr}_k(\mathbb C^\infty)],\qquad[f]\longmapsto[f^*\gamma_k^\infty].}
$$
For arbitrary non-Hausdorff compact spaces without the required numerability, that usual bundle-classification statement needs additional hypotheses; the construction above specifies precisely the conventional setting being used.

Write $B=\operatorname{Gr}_k(\mathbb C^\infty)$. For the assumed <fiber bundle> $U_k\to\nu\to B$ with <contractible> total space, the <long exact sequence of homotopy groups of a fibration> has segment
$$
0=\pi_q(\nu)\longrightarrow\pi_q(B)\xrightarrow{\partial}\pi_{q-1}(U_k)\longrightarrow\pi_{q-1}(\nu)=0.
$$
For $q\ge2$ this gives group isomorphisms. For $q=1$, the relevant pointed-set part and connectedness of $U_k$ give $\pi_1(B)=0$, corresponding to the single component $\pi_0(U_k)$. Thus the claimed formula includes its endpoint:
$$
\boxed{\pi_q(B)\cong\pi_{q-1}(U_k)\quad(q\ge1),\qquad\pi_1(B)=0.}
$$
Finally the determinant gives a <fiber bundle> $SU_2\to U_2\to S^1$. Since $\pi_3(S^1)=\pi_4(S^1)=0$, its exact sequence yields $\pi_3(U_2)\cong\pi_3(SU_2)$. The given identification $SU_2\cong S^3$ and the degree calculation $\pi_3(S^3)=\mathbb Z$ therefore give
$$
[S^4,B U_2]=\pi_4(B U_2)\cong\pi_3(U_2)\cong\mathbb Z.
$$
The equality of free and based <homotopy> classes uses the simple connectedness just proved. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbb C}^2(S^4)\cong\mathbb Z.}
$$
The classification of <rank-two complex vector bundles over the four-sphere> is a bijection of isomorphism classes, labelled by the integer <clutching function> <homotopy> class after choosing a generator. It is not an assertion that <Whitney sum> gives a group operation on bundles of fixed rank two.
