= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Hilbert basis theorem> says that if $B$ is a commutative <Noetherian ring>, then $B[t]$ is <Noetherian>; iteration treats any finite number of variables. Here is a complete leading-coefficient proof. For an <ideal> $I\subseteq B[t]$, let $L_d$ be the set of degree-$d$ coefficients of its polynomials of degree at most $d$, including zero coefficients. These are <ideals> of $B$, and multiplication by $t$ gives $L_d\subseteq L_{d+1}$. The <ascending chain condition> makes the chain stationary from some $N$ onward. Choose finite generators for $L_0,\ldots,L_N$ and lift them to polynomials $f_{dj}\in I$ of degree at most $d$. If $f\in I$ has degree $m$, set $d=\min(m,N)$ and express its leading coefficient as $\sum_j a_{dj}b_j$, where $a_{dj}$ is the chosen degree-$d$ coefficient. Then $f-\sum_j b_jt^{m-d}f_{dj}$ has smaller degree. Induction proves that the finitely many lifts generate $I$. This proves the theorem, including coefficient <rings> with <zero divisors>.

Put $A=\mathbb CG$ and $L=\mathbb C[X,X^{-1}]$. The <infinite dihedral group> has normal forms $X^m$ and $X^mY$. The relations move every $Y$ to the right and reduce its exponent modulo two; the action $X:n\mapsto n+1$, $Y:n\mapsto-n$ on $\mathbb Z$ shows that these forms are distinct. Thus
$$
A=L\oplus LY,\qquad Yf(X)=f(X^{-1})Y.
$$
The <Hilbert basis theorem> makes $\mathbb C[X]$ <Noetherian>, and its <localization> $L$ is <Noetherian>: an <ideal> of a <localization> is generated by the images of the finite generators of its contraction. Since $A$ is free of rank two over $L$ on the left, it is a <Noetherian module> over $L$. Every <left ideal> of $A$ is an $L$-<submodule>, so all its ascending chains stabilize. Therefore **$\mathbb CG$ is left Noetherian**.

For the <center of the infinite dihedral group algebra>, write an element as $f(X)+g(X)Y$. Commuting it with $X$ gives $g(X)(X^{-1}-X)=0$ in the Laurent <integral domain>, forcing $g=0$. Commuting $f(X)$ with $Y$ then gives $f(X)=f(X^{-1})$. A symmetric <Laurent polynomial> is a linear combination of $1$ and $X^j+X^{-j}$, $j\ge1$. With $z=X+X^{-1}$, the recursion
$$
S_0=2,\quad S_1=z,\quad S_{j+1}=zS_j-S_{j-1}
$$
expresses $S_j=X^j+X^{-j}$ as a polynomial in $z$. Conversely every polynomial in $z$ is central. The highest Laurent exponent of a nonzero polynomial in $z$ shows that $z$ is transcendental. Hence
$$
\boxed{R=Z(A)=\mathbb C[z],\qquad z=X+X^{-1}.}
$$
The identities $X^{-1}=z-X$ and $X^2-zX+1=0$ give $L=R+RX$. Independence follows by applying $X\mapsto X^{-1}$ to a relation $f(z)+Xg(z)=0$ and subtracting: $(X-X^{-1})g(z)=0$, so $g=f=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{A=R\oplus RX\oplus RY\oplus RXY}
$$
is a free $R$-<module> of rank four.

Let $M$ be a nonzero <simple module> on the left. For any $m\ne0$, $M=Am$, so it is finite over $R$. Every central $r\notin P=\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$ acts bijectively: its kernel and image are $A$-<submodules>, and simplicity forces kernel zero and image $M$. To prove that $P$ is maximal, not merely prime, put $B=R/P$; then $M$ is a faithful finite $B$-<module>. For $0\ne b\in B$, $bM=M$. If $m_1,\ldots,m_d$ generate $M$, write $m_i=b\sum_jc_{ij}m_j$. The <adjugate matrix> identity for $I-bC$ shows that $\det(I-bC)$ annihilates every generator and hence $M$. Faithfulness makes this determinant zero in $B$. Its constant term is one, so it has form $1-bq$, proving $bq=1$. Thus $B$ is a <field>, and **$P$ is a <maximal ideal> of $R$**. This proves the general principle for <simple modules over an algebra finite over its center> in this instance.

Every maximal <ideal> of $\mathbb C[z]$ is $(z-\lambda)$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb C$, by factorization of complex polynomials. The <module> $M=Am$ is therefore a quotient of the four-dimensional <algebra> $A/(z-\lambda)A$. In particular **$\dim_{\mathbb C}M\le4<\infty$**, as required.

The actual simple dimensions are at most two. If $\lambda\ne\pm2$, choose $u\ne\pm1$ with $u+u^{-1}=\lambda$. The matrices $X=\operatorname{diag}(u,u^{-1})$ and $Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ give a representation of $A/(z-\lambda)A$ onto $M_2(\mathbb C)$: polynomial functions of $X$ give the two diagonal matrix units, and multiplication by $Y$ gives the off-diagonal ones. Both <algebras> have dimension four, so this is an isomorphism. If $\lambda=\pm2$, the element $X\mp1$ has square zero and is conjugated to its negative by $Y$; its generated two-sided <ideal> is square zero. It annihilates every <simple module>, since its action on a <simple module> is either zero or the whole <module>, and the latter contradicts its square being zero. The remaining quotient is $\mathbb C[C_2]$, whose two <simple modules> have $Y=\pm1$. Thus the generic <simple modules> have dimension two, and the four exceptional <one-dimensional characters> have $X=\pm1$, $Y=\pm1$.

If $M=0$, all its <localizations> are zero. Conversely, take $0\ne m\in M$. Its <annihilator> $I=\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$ is a proper <ideal>, so it is contained in a maximal <ideal> $P$, which is prime. The zero criterion for <module localization> says that $m/1=0$ in $M_P$ only if some $s\notin P$ satisfies $sm=0$. This would put $s\in I\subseteq P$, a contradiction. Therefore $M_P\ne0$, proving
$$
\boxed{M=0\iff M_P=0\text{ for every prime ideal }P.}
$$
In fact maximal <ideals> suffice, and neither finite generation nor <Noetherianity> is needed for this implication.

Set $C=N/\varphi(M)$. The natural map $N_P\to C_P$ is onto. Its kernel is the image of $\varphi_P$: a fraction $n/s$ maps to zero precisely when $tn\in\varphi(M)$ for some $t\notin P$. If $tn=\varphi(m)$, then $n/s=\varphi_P(m/(ts))$. This proves the localized <cokernel> identity directly:
$$
\operatorname{coker}(\varphi_P)\cong C_P.
$$
Thus $\varphi_P$ is a <surjection> for every $P$ exactly when $C_P=0$ for every $P$. Part (i) makes this equivalent to $C=0$, or to $\varphi$ being a <surjection>. Hence **surjectivity is detected at all prime <localizations>**, and again maximal <ideals> alone would suffice.

Let $S=R\setminus P$. If $Q\subseteq P$ is a <prime ideal>, then $Q\cap S=\varnothing$. Its extension is
$$
QR_P=\{q/s:q\in Q,\ s\in S\}.
$$
It is proper: $1=q/s$ would give $u(s-q)=0$ for some $u\in S$, and hence $us=uq\in Q$, impossible because $u,s\notin Q$. If a product $(a/s)(b/t)$ lies in $QR_P$, clearing a denominator gives $uab\in Q$ for some $u\in S$. Primeness and $u\notin Q$ imply $a\in Q$ or $b\in Q$. Thus $QR_P$ is prime. Its contraction is $Q$, since $r/1\in QR_P$ implies $ur\in Q$ for some $u\in S$, forcing $r\in Q$.

Conversely, contract a <prime ideal> $\mathfrak q$ of $R_P$ to $Q=\{r:r/1\in\mathfrak q\}$. This is a <prime ideal> of $R$. It contains no element of $S$, since such an element becomes a <unit> and cannot belong to the proper <ideal> $\mathfrak q$. Hence $Q\subseteq P$. Also $r/s\in\mathfrak q$ exactly when $r/1\in\mathfrak q$, because $s/1$ is a <unit>, so $\mathfrak q=QR_P$. These extension and contraction operations are inverse, giving the <prime ideal correspondence for localization>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Spec}(R_P)\longleftrightarrow\{Q\in\operatorname{Spec}R:Q\subseteq P\},\qquad Q\mapsto QR_P.}
$$

An <associated prime of a module> is an <annihilator> of a nonzero element. Suppose $Q=\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$ is prime and $Q\subseteq P$. Then $m/1\ne0$ in $M_P$: a denominator $s\notin P$ killing $m$ would belong to $Q\subseteq P$. Moreover
$$
\operatorname{Ann}_{R_P}(m/1)=QR_P.
$$
Indeed, if $(a/s)(m/1)=0$, some $t\notin P$ has $tam=0$, so $ta\in Q$; primeness and $t\notin Q$ imply $a\in Q$. The reverse containment is immediate. This proves one inclusion in the <localization of associated primes> formula.

For the other, let $\mathfrak q=\operatorname{Ann}_{R_P}(m/s)$ be an <associated prime> of $M_P$ and contract it to $Q$. By part (iii), $Q$ is prime, $Q\subseteq P$ and $QR_P=\mathfrak q$. Since $s$ is invertible, $\mathfrak q$ is also the <annihilator> of $m/1$. The <Noetherian ring> hypothesis makes $Q=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$ finitely generated. For each $a_i$, the equality $(a_im)/1=0$ supplies $t_i\notin P$ with $t_ia_im=0$. Put $t=\prod_i t_i$, taking $t=1$ for the zero <ideal>. Then $Q$ annihilates $tm$, while $tm/1\ne0$ because $t/1$ is a <unit> and $m/1\ne0$.

If $b(tm)=0$, then $b/1$ annihilates $m/1$, so $b\in Q$. Therefore $\operatorname{Ann}_R(tm)=Q$, and $Q\in\operatorname{Ass}_R(M)$. We conclude
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ass}_{R_P}(M_P)=\{QR_P:Q\in\operatorname{Ass}_R(M),\ Q\subseteq P\}.}
$$
The single common denominator $t$ is the key point: one must turn a localized <annihilator> into the <annihilator> of one actual nonzero element of $M$, not merely assert that <annihilators> commute with <localization>.

Use generators $x,D$ with $Dx-xD=1$. The ordered <monomials> $x^iD^j$ form a <basis>: construct the <Weyl algebra> as the differential <Ore extension> of $\mathbb C[x]$ with $Df=fD+f'$, which has unique ordered polynomial expressions. Giving both generators degree one produces the <Bernstein filtration>, whose <associated graded ring> is $\mathbb C[x,\xi]$. If $a,b\ne0$, their nonzero leading symbols have nonzero product in this polynomial <integral domain>. Thus $ab$ has nonzero leading symbol and cannot vanish. Hence **$A_1(\mathbb C)$ has no nontrivial <zero divisors>**.

Here is the left-handed <Ore theorem>. Let $S$ be a multiplicative set of <regular elements of a ring>, containing $1$ and excluding $0$. There is an injective <Ore localization> whose elements are left fractions $s^{-1}a$ if and only if
$$
\boxed{\text{for every }a\in A,s\in S,\text{ there are }t\in S,b\in A\text{ with }ta=bs.}
$$
This is the left <Ore condition>. For a <noncommutative domain>, taking $S=A\setminus\{0\}$ gives a <division ring> exactly when this condition holds. With zero-divisor denominators a further left reversibility condition is needed; the regular-denominator statement is the one used here.

Necessity follows by writing $as^{-1}=t^{-1}b$ in the quotient and multiplying by $t$ and $s$, giving $ta=bs$ in the embedded original <ring>. For a construction proving sufficiency, use the <left Ore fraction construction>: pairs $(s,a)$ represent $s^{-1}a$, and $(s,a)$ and $(t,b)$ are equivalent when
$$
us=vt=w\in S,\qquad ua=vb
$$
for suitable $u,v\in A$. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate. For transitivity, combine $us=vt=w$ and $u't=v'q=w'$ by a common left multiple $hw=kw'\in S$. Then $(hv-ku')t=0$, so regularity gives $hv=ku'$, which combines the numerator equalities and proves transitivity.

Addition uses the common denominator $w$, with numerator $ua+vb$. To multiply $s^{-1}a$ by $t^{-1}b$, choose $v\in S,c\in A$ with $va=ct$; set
$$
(s^{-1}a)(t^{-1}b)=(vs)^{-1}cb.
$$
The <Ore condition> supplies a common refinement whenever a representative or an Ore relation is changed, and regularity cancels the shared denominator, proving independence of choices. The same refinements verify the <ring> axioms; the formulas are simply addition and multiplication after clearing left denominators. The map $a\mapsto(1,a)$ is injective, since equivalence to zero would give $ua=0$ with $u\in S$. Each $s\in S$ becomes invertible. A homomorphism inverting $S$ extends uniquely by $(s,a)\mapsto f(s)^{-1}f(a)$, giving the universal property. For all nonzero denominators in a <noncommutative domain>, a nonzero fraction has inverse $(s^{-1}a)^{-1}=a^{-1}s$, so the quotient is a <division ring>. This is the requested proof sketch, with the fraction equivalence and multiplication made explicit.

It remains to check the left <Ore condition> for $A_1(\mathbb C)$. Its graded polynomial <ring> is <Noetherian> by the <Hilbert basis theorem>. Lifting homogeneous generators of a graded <left ideal> and successively cancelling leading symbols proves that the filtered <algebra> is a <left Noetherian ring>: each subtraction lowers the nonnegative degree, so it terminates.

In any left Noetherian <noncommutative domain>, nonzero principal <left ideals> intersect. Otherwise $Ab\cap Aa=0$ with $a,b\ne0$ would make
$$
Ab\oplus Aba\oplus Aba^2\oplus\cdots
$$
an infinite direct sum of nonzero <left ideals>. To verify directness, reduce a finite relation modulo $Aa$ to make its first summand zero, then cancel $a$ on the right and repeat. Its increasing finite partial sums contradict the <ascending chain condition>. A nonzero element of $Aa\cap As$ therefore has the form $ta=bs\ne0$, with $t\ne0$, proving the required condition. Consequently **$A_1(\mathbb C)$ has a classical <ring> of left quotients, and it is a division <ring>**.

For $n\ge1$, the <Weyl algebra> $A=A_n(\mathbb C)$ is generated by $x_1,\ldots,x_n,D_1,\ldots,D_n$ with $[D_i,x_j]=\delta_{ij}$ and the other generator <commutators> zero. Iterating the differential <Ore extension> construction gives the ordered <basis> $x^\alpha D^\beta$. In the <Bernstein filtration>, both kinds of generator have degree one, and
$$
F_jA=\operatorname{span}\{x^\alpha D^\beta:|\alpha|+|\beta|\le j\},\qquad
\dim F_jA=\binom{j+2n}{2n},\qquad
\operatorname{gr}A\cong\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n,\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n].
$$
Leading symbols show that $A$ is a <noncommutative domain>. The <Hilbert basis theorem> and homogeneous-generator lifting by decreasing degree make it both left and right <Noetherian>.

It is also a <simple ring>. In a nonzero two-sided <ideal>, choose a nonzero element and a <monomial> $x^{\alpha_0}D^{\beta_0}$ of maximal total degree with nonzero coefficient $c$. Applying $(\operatorname{ad}D)^{\alpha_0}(\operatorname{ad}x)^{\beta_0}$ eliminates every lower-degree term and every different term of that maximal degree. The result is the nonzero <scalar> $(-1)^{|\beta_0|}\alpha_0!\beta_0!c$. Thus the <ideal> contains $1$. Here the <scalar extraction by iterated Weyl commutators> uses characteristic zero. Despite simplicity, $A$ has no nonzero finite-dimensional <modules>: in such a representation $[D_1,x_1]=I$ would have <trace> $0=\dim M$, impossible over $\mathbb C$. Its interesting <module> theory is necessarily infinite-dimensional.

For a finitely generated left <module> $M$, choose a finite-dimensional generating subspace $M_0$ and set $M_j=F_jA\,M_0$. This is a <good filtration of a module>. Its <associated graded module> is finite over the graded polynomial <ring>, so its cumulative Hilbert function is eventually a polynomial,
$$
\dim M_j=\frac{e(M)}{d(M)!}j^{d(M)}+O(j^{d(M)-1})\quad(M\ne0),
$$
with positive integer multiplicity $e(M)$. These are the <Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module> and its <multiplicity of a filtered module>. Two good filtrations bound each other after fixed shifts: lift finite homogeneous generators in one filtration, bound their degrees in the other, and use compatibility with $F_jA$; then reverse the roles. The growth degree and leading coefficient therefore do not depend on the <good filtration of a module>. The degree is also the <Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module> and the dimension of the <characteristic variety of a Weyl algebra module>, the support of $\operatorname{gr}M$. Since $M$ is a quotient of a finite sum of copies of $A$, $d(M)\le2n$.

The essential lower bound is the <Bernstein inequality for Weyl algebra modules>:
$$
\boxed{M\ne0\text{ finitely generated}\Longrightarrow d(M)\ge n.}
$$
Here is a full proof. Multiplication gives a linear map $F_jA\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C}(M_j,M_{2j})$. Prove it injective by induction on $j$. At $j=0$ a <scalar> killing $M_0\ne0$ must vanish. If $a\in F_jA$ kills $M_j$, then for every generator $z$, its <commutator> $[a,z]\in F_{j-1}A$ kills $M_{j-1}$: both $azm$ and $zam$ are zero for $m\in M_{j-1}$. Induction gives $[a,z]=0$ for all generators. In the ordered <basis>, $[D_i,a]$ differentiates the $x_i$ exponents and $[x_i,a]$ differentiates the $D_i$ exponents with a minus sign. Vanishing of all these <commutators> in characteristic zero forces $a$ to be a <scalar>. It then vanishes because it kills $M_0$. This proves the <faithful finite-step action of a Weyl algebra> and hence
$$
\binom{j+2n}{2n}\le(\dim M_j)(\dim M_{2j})\le(\dim M_{2j})^2.
$$
Comparison of degrees gives $2n\le2d(M)$, proving the inequality. Equally, without invoking eventual polynomiality, this estimate directly bounds the <Gelfand–Kirillov dimension of a module> below by $n$. Right <modules> satisfy the same inequality through the filtration-preserving isomorphism $A\cong A^{\mathrm{op}}$, $x_i\mapsto x_i$, $D_i\mapsto-D_i$.

A nonzero finitely generated <module> with $d(M)=n$ is a <holonomic Weyl algebra module>; the zero <module> is conventionally included. This minimal-growth class is stable under <submodules>, <quotient modules> and extensions. For $0\to N\to M\to Q\to0$, take the induced and quotient filtrations. Their graded sequence is exact; <Noetherianity> of $\operatorname{gr}A$ makes the induced filtrations good. Thus the Hilbert functions add and
$$
d(M)=\max(d(N),d(Q));
$$
if both nonzero terms have the top dimension, their multiplicities add. For a <holonomic Weyl algebra module>, every nonzero subquotient has dimension $n$, by the upper bound inherited from $M$ and Bernstein's lower bound. Each nonzero factor in a strict chain therefore consumes positive integral multiplicity. This proves the <multiplicity bounds the length of a holonomic Weyl module>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{length}_A(M)\le e(M).}
$$
In particular a holonomic <module> is both <Noetherian> and <Artinian> and has finite length, although its vector-space dimension is infinite. This distinction separates finite-dimensional representation theory from finite-length Weyl <module> theory.

The polynomial <module> $\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, with $x_i$ acting by multiplication and $D_i$ by differentiation, is generated by $1$. Its $M_j$ consists of polynomials of degree at most $j$, with dimension $\binom{j+n}{n}$, so $d=n$ and $e=1$. It is simple: differentiating a maximal-degree <monomial> of a nonzero polynomial isolates a nonzero constant, and multiplication by polynomials then generates the whole <module>. A second basic example is the delta <module>
$$
A\Big/\sum_iAx_i.
$$
Using the reverse ordered <basis> $D^\beta x^\alpha$, its <vector space> is $\mathbb C[D_1,\ldots,D_n]$; $D_i$ acts by multiplication and $x_i$ by negative differentiation. The same argument proves simplicity and holonomicity. These two examples are exchanged by the <automorphism> $x_i\mapsto D_i$, $D_i\mapsto-x_i$. Free <modules>, in contrast, have growth dimension $2n$ and are not holonomic. Thus the central organizing invariants are <good filtration of a module>, growth dimension, characteristic support and multiplicity, with Bernstein's estimate explaining why dimension $n$ is the natural smallest nonzero size.

Specify a commutative ground <ring> $k$ and a unital associative $k$-<algebra> $R$. For an $R$-<bimodule> $M$, the <Hochschild cochain complex> has
$$
C^q(R,M)=\operatorname{Hom}_k(R^{\otimes_kq},M),\qquad C^0(R,M)=M,
$$
and coboundary
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\delta f)(a_1,\ldots,a_{q+1})={}&a_1f(a_2,\ldots,a_{q+1})\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{q}(-1)^if(a_1,\ldots,a_ia_{i+1},\ldots,a_{q+1})\\
&+(-1)^{q+1}f(a_1,\ldots,a_q)a_{q+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <bimodule> identities and <associativity> cancel the terms of $\delta^2$ in pairs. The <Hochschild cohomology> is $HH_k^q(R,M)=\ker\delta_q/\operatorname{im}\delta_{q-1}$; the paper's $H^q(R,R)$ uses the regular <bimodule> as coefficients.

Over a <field>, the <bar resolution of an associative algebra> explains its homological meaning. Put $R^e=R\otimes_kR^{\mathrm{op}}$. The degree-$q$ bar term is $R^{\otimes_k(q+2)}$, with the outer factors supplying the $R^e$ action, boundary obtained by multiplying adjacent factors with alternating signs, and augmentation multiplication $R\otimes R\to R$. Inserting $1$ at the left gives a $k$-linear contracting homotopy, proving exactness. The terms are free $R^e$-<modules> after choosing a $k$-basis for the inner tensor factors. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_{R^e}(-,M)$ gives precisely the displayed cochain complex and therefore
$$
HH_k^q(R,M)\cong\operatorname{Ext}_{R^e}^q(R,M).
$$
This <ground-ring qualification in Hochschild cohomology> matters for an arbitrary <ring>: one can take $k=\mathbb Z$, but the bar terms need not be projective over $R^e$. The ordinary cochain complex then computes relative <Hochschild cohomology>; the absolute Ext identification needs, for example, $R$ projective over $k$. Likewise the extension classification below applies to ground-ring-linearly split extensions. For algebras over a <field> these qualifications are automatic.

In degree zero, $(\delta m)(a)=am-ma$, so **$HH^0(R,R)=Z(R)$**. In degree one the cocycle equation is
$$
a\,d(b)-d(ab)+d(a)b=0,
$$
exactly the Leibniz rule for a <derivation of an algebra>. The coboundaries are the <inner derivations> $a\mapsto am-ma$. Thus the <first Hochschild cohomology as outer derivations> is
$$
\boxed{HH^1(R,R)=\operatorname{Der}_k(R)/\operatorname{Inn}(R).}
$$
Its significance is infinitesimal <automorphisms> modulo inner ones. Over the <dual numbers> $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, the map $a\mapsto a+\varepsilon d(a)$ is multiplicative precisely when $d$ satisfies the Leibniz rule. Conjugation by $1+\varepsilon m$ gives $a\mapsto a+\varepsilon(ma-am)$, an inner <derivation> with the opposite sign convention. The quotient therefore measures infinitesimal symmetry not arising from conjugation.

Degree two controls extensions and changes of multiplication. A $k$-linear split <square-zero extension of an algebra> with prescribed kernel <bimodule> $M$ can be written as $E=R\oplus M$. Choose a unital section and let $f(a,b)$ be the difference between its product and its value on $ab$. Then
$$
(a,u)(b,v)=(ab,av+ub+f(a,b)).
$$
Computing both parenthesizations of a triple gives the <associativity> condition
$$
a f(b,c)-f(ab,c)+f(a,bc)-f(a,b)c=0,
$$
namely $\delta f=0$. A unital product has $f(1,a)=f(a,1)=0$, so one uses the <normalized Hochschild cochain complex>. Changing section by $g:R\to M$ gives $f\mapsto f+\delta g$. Conversely the displayed product for any normalized two-cocycle constructs such an extension, and a section change gives the corresponding extension isomorphism. This proves that <second Hochschild cohomology classifies split square-zero extensions>:
$$
\boxed{HH^2(R,M)\text{ classifies these extensions, up to equivalence fixing }R,M.}
$$
The zero class is the split algebra extension with no extra product term. With $M=R$, this gives one meaning of the requested $H^2(R,R)$.

For a <first-order associative deformation>, retain the underlying $k$-<module> and put $a*b=ab+\varepsilon f(a,b)$. Expanding $(a*b)*c=a*(b*c)$ modulo $\varepsilon^2$ gives exactly $\delta f=0$. A coordinate change $T=1+\varepsilon g$ transports the product to $T^{-1}(T(a)*T(b))$, whose first-order coefficient is $f+\delta g$. Hence
$$
\boxed{HH^2(R,R)\text{ is the space of inequivalent first-order changes of multiplication.}}
$$
This classification is of infinitesimal deformations, not a guarantee that all classes extend to every order. For $a*b=ab+tf(a,b)+t^2\mu_2(a,b)$ modulo $t^3$, the next <associativity> equation is
$$
\delta\mu_2(a,b,c)=f(f(a,b),c)-f(a,f(b,c)).
$$
Substitution of $\delta f=0$ shows the right-hand side is a three-cocycle. Its class in $HH^3(R,R)$ is the <second-order obstruction to an associative deformation>. It must vanish for a second-order extension to exist. At higher orders the same expansion gives further obstruction cocycles. If $HH^2(R,R)=0$, every first nonzero coefficient of a <formal associative deformation> is a coboundary and can be removed by a coordinate change. Repeating this order by order gives <formal rigidity from vanishing second Hochschild cohomology>, with the transformations converging in the $t$-adic topology. Vanishing of $HH^3$ instead removes these extension obstructions; these are distinct roles.

Examples make the two low degrees concrete. For $R=k[x]$ over a <field>, <inner derivations> vanish and every <derivation> is $h(x)\partial_x$, determined by its value on $x$. The <bimodule> resolution
$$
0\longrightarrow k[x]\otimes k[x]
\xrightarrow{\,x\otimes1-1\otimes x\,}
 k[x]\otimes k[x]\longrightarrow k[x]\longrightarrow0
$$
is exact: the middle tensor <ring> is the polynomial <integral domain> $k[u,v]$, multiplication identifies its quotient by $u-v$, and multiplication by $u-v$ is injective. Applying <bimodule> Hom gives zero differential. Consequently the <Hochschild cohomology of a polynomial algebra in one variable> is
$$
HH^0(k[x])=k[x],\qquad HH^1(k[x])=k[x]\partial_x,\qquad HH^q(k[x])=0\quad(q\ge2).
$$
This algebra has many infinitesimal <automorphisms>, while its ordinary associative multiplication is formally rigid.

For $R=k[x,y]$ in characteristic zero, the bilinear map $f(a,b)=(\partial_ya)(\partial_xb)$ is a two-cocycle: substitution in $\delta f$ and the two product rules cancel its four terms. It is not a coboundary, because every $\delta g$ on a commutative <algebra> is symmetric in its two inputs, whereas $f(y,x)-f(x,y)=1$. This class integrates to the <Weyl deformation of a polynomial algebra in two variables>, with $yx-xy=t$. The differential <Ore extension> construction supplies the ordered <basis> $x^iy^j$ over $k[t]$, so the $t$-adic completion has the fixed underlying <module> $k[x,y][[t]]$. Its first multiplication correction is exactly this $f$. This displays a genuinely noncommutative <formal associative deformation> detected by $HH^2$.

Finally, the <Hochschild cup product> $(f\smile g)(a_1,\ldots,a_{p+q})=f(a_1,\ldots,a_p)g(a_{p+1},\ldots,a_{p+q})$ descends to the graded cohomology algebra, and the <Gerstenhaber bracket> organizes the interaction of <derivations> and <formal associative deformation> cocycles. The calculations above explain why the first two positive degrees are especially useful: $HH^1$ measures outer infinitesimal symmetries, while $HH^2$ measures split extensions and infinitesimal associative structures, with degree three detecting the first obstruction to continuing them.
