= Solution
{parent=a}

Suppose $F:X\to\mathcal P(X)$ were a <surjection> onto the <power set> of the <set> $X$. Form the <subset>
$$
D=\{x\in X:x\notin F(x)\}.
$$
Surjectivity would give $a\in X$ with $F(a)=D$. But then $a\in D$ holds exactly when $a\notin F(a)=D$, a <contradiction>. Hence no such <surjection> exists, and in particular **there is no bijection between a set and its power set**. This is the diagonal construction in <Cantor's theorem>; it also covers the <empty set>, whose <power set> has one element.

= b
{parent=1e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

**No such set exists.** If $R$ had precisely the <sets> $x$ satisfying $x\notin x$ as its elements, applying that membership criterion to $R$ itself would give
$$
R\in R\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R\notin R,
$$
a <contradiction>. This is <Russell's paradox>. It rules out this unrestricted universal collection; a <set> whose elements happen not to contain themselves is perfectly possible, for example the <empty set>.

= Solution
{parent=2E}

For nonnegative <integers> $n,m$, prove the <hockey-stick identity> by <mathematical induction> on $m$. The <induction base case> $m=0$ is $\binom n0=\binom{n+1}0=1$. If the formula holds through $m-1$, <Pascal's identity> gives
$$
\sum_{j=0}^m\binom{n+j}j
=\binom{n+m}{m-1}+\binom{n+m}m
=\binom{n+m+1}m.
$$
This supplies the <inductive step>.

A <binary string> containing exactly $n$ zeroes and $j$ ones has length $n+j$. Choosing its $j$ one-positions gives $\binom{n+j}j$ possibilities. Different values of $j$ give disjoint collections, so <counting binary strings with bounded ones> yields
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=0}^m\binom{n+j}j=\binom{n+m+1}m.}
$$
This includes $n=0$: the $m+1$ strings are the empty string and the all-one strings of lengths one through $m$.

= 3A
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=3A}

For an attractive <central force>, the transverse <acceleration> vanishes. In <polar coordinates>,
$$
2\dot r\dot\theta+r\ddot\theta=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0.
$$
Thus the <specific angular momentum> $h=r^2\dot\theta$ is constant. Assume $h\ne0$, so angle can parameterize the <orbit>, and put $u=1/r$. Primes denote angle <derivatives>. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\dot\theta=hu^2,\qquad
\dot r=-hu',\qquad
\ddot r=-h^2u^2u'',\qquad
r\dot\theta^2=h^2u^3.
$$
The outward radial component of the attractive <force> is $-f(u)$. Its <Newton's second law> equation is therefore $m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=-f(u)$, giving the <Binet equation>
$$
\boxed{u''+u=\frac{f(u)}{mh^2u^2}.}
$$
The sign reflects $f$ being the positive inward force magnitude. Zero <angular momentum> produces radial motion and is outside this angular parametrization.

= 4A
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=4A}

Put $M=m_1+m_2$, define the <center of mass> $\mathbf R=(m_1\mathbf x_1+m_2\mathbf x_2)/M$, and let $\mathbf r=\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_2$ be relative <position>. Adding the two <Newton's second law> equations gives
$$
M\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf F_1+\mathbf F_2=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf R(t)=\mathbf R(0)+t\dot{\mathbf R}(0).}
$$
The <center of mass> has constant <velocity>, by conservation of total <momentum>.

Subtracting the particle <accelerations> instead gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}=\frac{\mathbf f(\mathbf r)}{m_1}+\frac{\mathbf f(\mathbf r)}{m_2}
=\frac{\mathbf f(\mathbf r)}\mu,
\qquad
\boxed{\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf f(\mathbf r),\quad
\mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.}
$$
Thus relative motion is a one-particle problem with the <reduced mass> $\mu$, while <center-of-mass motion> is uniform. The same <reduced mass> appears in the <kinetic energy> decomposition $T=\tfrac12M|\dot{\mathbf R}|^2+\tfrac12\mu|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a <prime number> $p$, the nonzero <residue classes> form the multiplicative <group> of the <finite field> $\mathbb F_p$. Every class has a unique <multiplicative inverse>. A class equals its own inverse precisely when $x^2=1$, so $(x-1)(x+1)=0$ in the <field> and $x=1$ or $x=-1$.

For an <odd prime>, pair each remaining class with its distinct inverse. Each pair contributes one to the product, leaving
$$
\boxed{(p-1)!\equiv1\cdot(-1)=-1\pmod p.}
$$
For $p=2$, the same congruence is $1\equiv-1\pmod2$. This proves <Wilson's theorem>.

= b
{parent=5e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $r=(p-1)/2$, $O=1\cdot3\cdots(p-2)$ and $E=2\cdot4\cdots(p-1)$. The residues $p-2,p-4,\ldots,p-2r$ are exactly the odd factors of $O$, in reverse order. Hence
$$
O\equiv(-1)^rE\pmod p,
\qquad OE=(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p
$$
by <Wilson's theorem>. It follows that the <odd-residue squared product modulo a prime> is
$$
\boxed{1^2\cdot3^2\cdots(p-2)^2=O^2\equiv(-1)^{r+1}=(-1)^{(p+1)/2}\pmod p.}
$$
Thus it is $-1$ for $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $1$ for $p\equiv3\pmod4$.

{parent=Paper 4}

= Solution
{parent=6E}

For finite <sets> $A_1,\ldots,A_s$, the <inclusion-exclusion principle> states
$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^sA_i\right|
=\sum_{\varnothing\ne J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}}(-1)^{|J|+1}
\left|\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right|.
$$
To prove it, count the contribution of an element lying in exactly $r\ge1$ of the <sets>. Its total coefficient is
$$
\sum_{j=1}^r(-1)^{j+1}\binom rj=1-(1-1)^r=1,
$$
by the <binomial theorem>. An element in none contributes zero. Summing these elementwise counts proves the formula. Applying the same argument to <indicator functions> and taking <expectations> proves the corresponding probability formula.

Factor $4199=13\cdot17\cdot19$. An integer is <coprime> to this product precisely when none of its three <prime factors> divides it. <Inclusion-exclusion> applied to the multiples of those <prime numbers> among $1,\ldots,4199$ gives <Euler's totient function>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(4199)
&=4199-(323+247+221)+(19+17+13)-1\\
&=4199\left(1-\frac1{13}\right)\left(1-\frac1{17}\right)\left(1-\frac1{19}\right)\\
&=12\cdot16\cdot18=\boxed{3456}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For the survey, let $H,D,S$ be the three detestation <events> and use proportions as <probabilities>. The union $H\cup D$ is the reported total union with the only-$S$ group removed, so $\mathbb P(H\cup D)=0.90-0.27=0.63$. The two-set <inclusion-exclusion principle> gives $\mathbb P(H\cap D)=0.45+0.28-0.63=0.10$. Removing the all-three group leaves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P((H\cap D)\setminus S)=0.10-0.06=0.04.}
$$
The required proportion is **4%**. The separately reported total $S$ proportion is consistent with, but unnecessary for, this calculation.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}

If $p$ is a <prime number> and $p\nmid a$, multiplication by $a$ permutes the nonzero <residue classes> modulo $p$: $ai\equiv aj$ implies $i\equiv j$ because $a$ has a <multiplicative inverse>. Multiplying all the permuted classes gives
$$
a^{p-1}(p-1)!\equiv(p-1)!\pmod p.
$$
The <factorial> is a nonzero element of the <finite field> $\mathbb F_p$, so cancellation is valid. Therefore
$$
\boxed{a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p,}
$$
which is <Fermat's little theorem>.

= b
{parent=7e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $d$ be the <multiplicative order> of $a$ modulo $p$. Use <Euclidean division> to write $x=qd+r$, $0\le r<d$. Since $a^d\equiv1$,
$$
a^x\equiv a^r\pmod p.
$$
If $a^x\equiv1$, minimality of $d$ forces $r=0$, so **$d$ divides $x$**. Conversely, $d\mid x$ immediately gives $a^x\equiv1$. For negative <integers> $x$, powers are interpreted using the <multiplicative inverse> and the same argument applies.

Apply the result to $x=p-1$, using <Fermat's little theorem>. Then $d\mid p-1$, equivalently
$$
\boxed{p\equiv1\pmod d.}
$$

= c
{parent=7e}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

If $x^2\equiv-1\pmod p$ with $p$ an <odd prime>, then $x^4\equiv1$ but $x^2\not\equiv1$. The <multiplicative order> consequently divides four and is neither one nor two, so it is **four**. The preceding divisibility result makes $4\mid p-1$ necessary.

For sufficiency, suppose $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and put $r=(p-1)/2$, an <even integer>. Pair opposite factors in the <factorial>:
$$
(p-1)!=\prod_{j=1}^rj(p-j)\equiv(-1)^r(r!)^2=(r!)^2\pmod p.
$$
<Wilson's theorem> gives $(r!)^2\equiv-1\pmod p$, explicitly producing a <square root of minus one modulo a prime>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{For odd }p,\quad x^2\equiv-1\pmod p\text{ is soluble }\Longleftrightarrow p\equiv1\pmod4.}
$$

{parent=Paper 4}

= Solution
{parent=8E}

<Mathematical induction> consists of an <induction base case> $P(1)$ and an <inductive step> $P(n)\Rightarrow P(n+1)$ for every <positive integer> $n$. To derive it from the <well-ordering principle>, suppose the <set> of counterexamples were nonempty and take its least element $n$. The base case excludes $n=1$. If $n>1$, minimality gives $P(n-1)$, and the step then gives $P(n)$, a <contradiction>. Hence there are no counterexamples.

For the stated <integer congruence>, the base case is $9\equiv2\pmod7$. If $9^n\equiv2^n\pmod7$, multiplication and $9\equiv2\pmod7$ give
$$
9^{n+1}\equiv9\cdot2^n\equiv2^{n+1}\pmod7,
$$
completing the induction.

The purported shifted-sum proof has a correct conditional successor calculation, but **the base case is missing and false**: at $n=1$ it would assert $1=1+126$. An <inductive step> without a true <induction base case> cannot establish any instance.

{parent=Paper 4}

= Solution
{parent=9A}

The <equation of motion in a rotating frame> with constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\omega$ is
$$
m\left[\ddot{\mathbf x}+2\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}
+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf x)\right]=\mathbf F.
$$
With $\mathbf F=-4m\omega^2\mathbf x$ and $\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,\omega)$, this becomes
$$
\ddot x-2\omega\dot y+3\omega^2x=0,\qquad
\ddot y+2\omega\dot x+3\omega^2y=0,\qquad
\ddot z+4\omega^2z=0.
$$
The given initial data make $z=0$. For the <complex coordinate> $\zeta=x+iy$, the planar equations combine into
$$
\ddot\zeta+2i\omega\dot\zeta+3\omega^2\zeta=0.
$$
Its <characteristic roots> are $i\omega$ and $-3i\omega$. Applying $\zeta(0)=1$, $\dot\zeta(0)=0$ gives $\zeta=\tfrac34e^{i\omega t}+\tfrac14e^{-3i\omega t}$. The triple-angle identities reduce this <astroid motion of a harmonic oscillator in a rotating frame> to
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(\cos^3\omega t,\sin^3\omega t,0).}
$$
It traces an <astroid>. Differentiating gives the relative <speed>
$$
|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2
=9\omega^2\sin^2\omega t\cos^2\omega t
=\frac94\omega^2\sin^2(2\omega t).
$$
For $\omega>0$, its maxima occur at $t=(2n+1)\pi/(4\omega)$, with
$$
\boxed{|\dot{\mathbf x}|_{\max}=\frac{3\omega}{2}.}
$$
This is speed measured in the specified <rotating frame>. The same physical motion has inertial complex coordinate $e^{i\omega t}\zeta=\cos(2\omega t)+(i/2)\sin(2\omega t)$, an <ellipse>; its initial <velocity> is the frame-rotation contribution even though the initial relative <velocity> is zero.

{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=10A}

Let $m=4\pi\rho a^3/3$ be the drop's <mass>, with constant <mass density> $\rho$. Its volume-growth rule gives $4\pi a^2\dot a=c\pi a^2v$, hence $\dot a=cv/4$ and $\dot m=\rho c\pi a^2v$. The incoming mist is stationary, so the <variable-mass system> momentum balance is
$$
\frac d{dt}(mv)=mg-k\rho\pi a^2v^2.
$$
Expanding the <derivative> and substituting the accretion rate gives the <accreting raindrop with quadratic drag> equations
$$
\boxed{\dot a=\frac c4v,\qquad
\dot v=g-\frac34(c+k)\frac{v^2}a.}
$$
The extra $c$ contribution is the <momentum> needed to accelerate collected matter from rest; omitting it would treat the growing drop as a constant-mass particle.

Set $q=v^2/a$ and $B=7c/8+3k/4$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot q
&=\frac{2v\dot v}a-\frac{v^2\dot a}{a^2}\\
&=\frac va\left[2g-\left(\frac74c+\frac32k\right)q\right]
=\boxed{\frac{2v}a(g-Bq)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
To justify the limiting value as well as its stability sign, use $c>0$, as appropriate to volume growth. Divide by $\dot a=cv/4$ and integrate the resulting first-order equation:
$$
\boxed{q(a)=\frac gB+\left(q(a_0)-\frac gB\right)
\left(\frac{a_0}a\right)^{7+6k/c}.}
$$
Thus $q$ remains between two positive constants, its initial value and $g/B$. Since $d\sqrt a/dt=c\sqrt q/8$, the radius is defined for all increasing time and tends to infinity. The correction vanishes, proving the <speed-radius attractor of an accreting raindrop>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{v^2}a\longrightarrow\frac g{7c/8+3k/4},\qquad
\dot v\longrightarrow g-\frac{3(c+k)}4\frac gB
=\frac{cg}{7c+6k}<\frac g7.}
$$
The strict inequality uses $k>0$. The growing radius prevents a finite terminal <velocity>; instead the <acceleration> approaches this positive constant.

= 11A
{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=11A}

Write $h=r^2\dot\theta$ for the conserved <specific angular momentum>. Zero <specific orbital energy> in the inverse-square <Newtonian gravitational field> gives
$$
\frac12\left(\dot r^2+\frac{h^2}{r^2}\right)-\frac{GM}r=0.
$$
Define $r_0=h^2/(2GM)$, the <periapsis> radius for a nonradial <parabolic trajectory>. Then
$$
\dot r^2=\frac{2GM(r-r_0)}{r^2}.
$$
On the outgoing branch, integrate from the <periapsis> time $t_0$ and put $s=\sqrt{r-r_0}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
t-t_0
&=\frac1{\sqrt{2GM}}\int_{r_0}^r\frac{r'\,dr'}{\sqrt{r'-r_0}}\\
&=\frac2{\sqrt{2GM}}\left(\frac{s^3}3+r_0s\right)
=\frac{\sqrt2}{3\sqrt{GM}}(r+2r_0)\sqrt{r-r_0}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The incoming branch has the opposite sign. Squaring gives the <radius-time relation for a parabolic Kepler orbit>, a version of the <Barker equation>:
$$
\boxed{(r-r_0)(r+2r_0)^2=\frac92GM(t-t_0)^2.}
$$
Here $r_0$ and $t_0$ are fixed by <angular momentum> and the time origin at <periapsis>. The relation applies on both branches, with $r\ge r_0$; it is not necessary to solve for the polar angle.

{parent=Paper 4}
{scope}
{title2=Dynamics}

= Solution
{parent=12A}

A coaxial shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ has <mass> $dm=2\pi\rho l r\,dr$. Integrating squared radius against <mass>, the <moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder> about its axis is
$$
I=\int_0^a r^2dm=2\pi\rho l\int_0^a r^3dr
=\frac{\pi\rho l a^4}2=\boxed{\frac12Ma^2},
\qquad M=\pi\rho a^2l.
$$
On the smooth incline, no tangential contact <force> produces spin, so the initially stationary cylinder simply slides with <acceleration> $g\sin\theta$. Therefore $t_{\rm slide}=\sqrt{2L/(g\sin\theta)}$.

For <rolling without slipping>, let $F$ be uphill <static friction> and $A$ the downward translational <acceleration>. <Newton's second law>, the rotational <torque> equation, and the contact constraint give
$$
MA=Mg\sin\theta-F,\qquad
Fa=I\alpha,\qquad A=a\alpha.
$$
With $I=Ma^2/2$, these give $F=MA/2$ and $A=2g\sin\theta/3$. The <rolling-time factor on a straight incline> is consequently
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm roll}=\sqrt{\frac{3L}{g\sin\theta}}
=\sqrt{\frac32}\,t_{\rm slide}.}
$$
The required friction is $Mg\sin\theta/3$; the stated no-slip motion presupposes sufficient <static friction>. At an instantaneously stationary contact point it does no work. Gravitational <potential energy> is shared between translational and rotational <kinetic energy>, explaining why rolling accelerates more slowly than the smooth slide.
