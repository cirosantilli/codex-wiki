= Solution
{parent=1C}

Using the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols>, the $i$th component of the <vector triple product> is
$$
\epsilon_{ijk}a_j\epsilon_{klm}b_lc_m
=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})a_jb_lc_m
=b_i(a_jc_j)-c_i(a_jb_j).
$$
Thus **the triple-product identity is** $a\times(b\times c)=(a\cdot c)b-(a\cdot b)c$.

Apply this with the unit vector $n$ to obtain $\Pi(x)=x-(n\cdot x)n$. <Dot products> and scalar multiplication are linear in $x$, so $\Pi(sx+ty)=s\Pi(x)+t\Pi(y)$. Its <matrix representation> is
$$
\boxed{P=I-nn^T,\qquad P_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-n_in_j.}
$$
The fixed-vector equation is equivalent to $(n\cdot x)n=0$. Since $n\ne0$, **all its solutions are** $x\in n^\perp$. Also $\boxed{\Pi(n)=0}$. Every vector decomposes as $x=\Pi(x)+(n\cdot x)n$, with $\Pi(x)$ orthogonal to $n$. Hence $\Pi$ is the <orthogonal projection> onto the plane through the origin normal to $n$: it fixes that plane and removes the perpendicular component.

A family $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ is <linearly independent> if $\sum_{i=1}^n a_ix_i=0$ forces $a_1=\cdots=a_n=0$. For the three specified vectors, direct substitution gives
$$
\boxed{4x_1-3x_2-2x_3=0.}
$$
The coefficients are not all zero, so **the vectors are linearly dependent**.

For the implication question, using the usual interpretation that a <basis> contains the listed family as independent entries, the answers in order (i)–(x) are
$$
\boxed{\mathrm T,\ \mathrm T,\ \mathrm F,\ \mathrm F,\ \mathrm T,\ \mathrm F,\ \mathrm T,\ \mathrm T,\ \mathrm T,\ \mathrm T.}
$$
The key distinctions are that the unrestricted zero combination always exists, whereas <linear dependence> requires a nontrivial combination; and dependence does not necessarily let the specified third vector be solved in terms of the first two. For example $x=y=e_1$, $z=e_2$ makes (iii) and (iv) false, while $x=e_1,y=e_2,z=e_3$ disproves (vi).

There is a set-versus-family qualification in (v). If “contains” is interpreted as literal set containment, repeated nonzero vectors need only occur once in the <basis>. Then $x=y=e_1,z=e_2$ is dependent as a family but all three belong to the basis $\{e_1,e_2\}$, so (v) becomes false. With three distinct vectors, or with family-containment as above, <basis extension> gives (v) true. Implication (x) remains true on either reading.

The definition of a <convergent sequence> is
$$
\boxed{\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n\ge N:\ |u_n-l|<\epsilon.}
$$
To prove the product rule, convergence first gives an eventual bound $|u_n|\le |l|+1=:M$. Then
$$
|u_nv_n-lk|=|u_n(v_n-k)+k(u_n-l)|\le M|v_n-k|+|k||u_n-l|.
$$
Choose $n$ sufficiently large that $|v_n-k|<\epsilon/(2M)$ and $|u_n-l|<\epsilon/[2(|k|+1)]$, as well as the bound on $u_n$. The right side is less than $\epsilon$, proving **$u_nv_n\to lk$** directly from the <limit of a sequence> definition.

If $l\ne0$, eventually $|u_n-l|<|l|/2$, so the <reverse triangle inequality> gives $|u_n|>|l|/2$. Consequently
$$
\left|\frac1{u_n}-\frac1l\right|=\frac{|u_n-l|}{|u_n||l|}\le\frac{2|u_n-l|}{|l|^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus **$1/u_n\to1/l$**. The condition $u_n\ne0$ ensures the reciprocal sequence is defined at every index; the nonzero limit supplies the eventual uniform bound away from zero. Finally $\boxed{u_n=1/(n+1)\to0}$ has every term nonzero, showing that this termwise property alone does not prevent a zero limit.

= Solution
{parent=4D}

Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$ and satisfies $f(a)=f(b)$, with $a<b$. Apply the <extreme value theorem> to both $f$ and $-f$ to obtain a maximum and a minimum. If $f$ is constant, every interior derivative is zero. Otherwise some value differs from the common endpoint value, so either a maximum or a minimum occurs at an interior point $c$. At an interior maximum, difference quotients are nonpositive from the right and nonnegative from the left; since the <derivative> exists, it must be zero. The minimum case is identical with the inequalities reversed. This proves **Rolle's theorem:** $\boxed{f'(c)=0}$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

For the <mean value theorem>, subtract the endpoint chord:
$$
g(t)=f(t)-f(a)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(t-a).
$$
Then $g(a)=g(b)=0$, so <Rolle's theorem> supplies $c\in(a,b)$ with $g'(c)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.}
$$
If $f'(t)>0$ everywhere, applying the <mean value theorem> to any $x<y$ gives $f(y)-f(x)=f'(c)(y-x)>0$. Thus $f$ is <strictly increasing>. **The converse is false:** $\boxed{f(t)=t^3}$ is <strictly increasing> but $f'(0)=0$.

To find the <kernel of a linear map>, perform the row operations $R_2\leftarrow R_2-R_1$ and $R_3\leftarrow R_3-(1+\alpha)R_1$, followed by $R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_2$. The equivalent homogeneous equations are
$$
x-y+(2\alpha+1)z=0,\qquad \alpha y-2\alpha z=0,\qquad \alpha(3-\alpha)z=0.
$$
The resulting <determinant> is $\alpha^2(3-\alpha)$. If $\alpha\notin\{0,3\}$ all coordinates vanish. At $\alpha=0$ only $x-y+z=0$ remains; at $\alpha=3$ the equations give $y=2z$, $x=-5z$. Therefore **kernel bases are**
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\varnothing,&\alpha\notin\{0,3\},\\
\{(1,1,0)^T,(-1,0,1)^T\},&\alpha=0,\\
\{(-5,2,1)^T\},&\alpha=3.
\end{cases}}
$$
The empty family is the <basis> of the zero <vector subspace>.

For the <change of basis>, let $B_0,C_0$ be the matrices whose columns are the two ordered bases in standard coordinates. Then $v=B_0[v]_B=C_0[v]_C$, so $[v]_B=S[v]_C$ with $S=B_0^{-1}C_0$. If $A$ and $A'$ represent the same <linear map> in the two bases, $[\Phi(v)]_B=A[v]_B$ and also $[\Phi(v)]_B=SA'[v]_C$. Therefore $SA'=AS$, and
$$
\boxed{A'=S^{-1}AS,\qquad S=B_0^{-1}C_0.}
$$
For the concrete bases, their vectors satisfy $c_1=b_1+b_2$, $c_2=b_2+b_3$, $c_3=b_1+b_2+b_3$. These coordinate columns give
$$
\boxed{S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\1&1&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\\-1&1&0\\1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
At $\alpha=0$, every row of $A_0$ is $(1,-1,1)$, so every row of $A_0S$ is $(0,0,1)$. Multiplying on the left by the displayed inverse gives
$$
\boxed{S^{-1}A_0S=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The original PDF asks for this full product; its final factor $S$ is missing from the converted TeX.

If $Ax_p=b$, then $Ax=b$ is equivalent to $A(x-x_p)=0$. Thus the solution set is the <affine solution space of a linear equation> $\boxed{x_p+\ker A}$, and every vector of that form solves the equation.

For the <symmetric matrix> case, let $Au_i=\lambda_i u_i$. Symmetry gives
$$
\lambda_i(u_i\cdot u_j)=(Au_i)\cdot u_j=u_i\cdot(Au_j)=\lambda_j(u_i\cdot u_j).
$$
Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore imply $u_i\cdot u_j=0$. Normalizing the three vectors gives an <orthonormal basis>. Expand $x=\sum_i t_i u_i$ and $b=\sum_i b_i u_i$, with $b_i=b\cdot u_i$. The equation $(A-\lambda_kI)x=b$ becomes $(\lambda_i-\lambda_k)t_i=b_i$ for every $i$. Thus solvability is equivalent to $b_k=0$, and all solutions are
$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{i\ne k}\frac{b\cdot u_i}{\lambda_i-\lambda_k}u_i+\beta u_k,\qquad \beta\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This proves the claimed orthogonality condition and the full solution family rather than only a particular solution.

For the numerical <symmetric matrix>, direct multiplication gives $Au_1=0$ for $u_1=(1,1,1)^T/\sqrt3$, and $Au_2=-\sqrt2\,u_2$ for $u_2=(1,-1,0)^T/\sqrt2$. Their orthogonal complement is spanned by $u_3=(1,1,-2)^T/\sqrt6$, and multiplication gives $Au_3=\sqrt3\,u_3$. The supplied right side has $b\cdot u_1=0$, so the <linear system> is solvable. Its other coefficients are $b\cdot u_2=2$ and $b\cdot u_3=3\sqrt2$. Hence
$$
x=-\sqrt2\,u_2+\sqrt6\,u_3+\beta u_1
=(0,2,-2)^T+\frac\beta{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)^T.
$$
Equivalently, **all solutions are**
$$
\boxed{x=(0,2,-2)^T+t(1,1,1)^T,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$

Complete the square in the real-valued <complex-plane> equation:
$$
\alpha\left|z-\frac\beta\alpha\right|^2=\frac{|\beta|^2-\alpha\gamma}{\alpha}.
$$
Dividing by $\alpha$ gives **center and radius**
$$
\boxed{c=\frac\beta\alpha,\qquad r=\frac{\sqrt{|\beta|^2-\alpha\gamma}}{|\alpha|}.}
$$
If the radicand is zero, the locus is the single point $c$, a degenerate <circle>. If it is negative, the locus is empty. The absolute value of $\alpha$ is essential even when $\alpha<0$.

For the requested sketch, the five centers and radii are respectively $(-1/2,\sqrt3/2)$, $(-1,\sqrt2)$, $(-2,\sqrt6)$, $(2,\sqrt2)$ and $(1,0)$, all centers on the real axis.

\Image[paper-1-complex-circles.png]
{title=Five complex-plane circle loci, including the radius-zero point at alpha equals one}

The original PDF's map is $f(z)=(\beta/\bar\beta)\bar z$; the converted TeX drops the conjugate on its denominator. Write $\beta=|\beta|e^{i\theta}$, so $f(z)=e^{2i\theta}\bar z$. This is the <reflection symmetry of a complex circle equation>. It fixes $c=\beta/\alpha$ and satisfies
$$
|f(z)-c|=|f(z)-f(c)|=|z-c|,\qquad f(f(z))=z.
$$
Thus it maps every point of the <circle> into the same <circle>. Conversely, for any point $w$ on that <circle>, $z=f(w)$ also lies on it and $f(z)=w$. This proves **$f(C)=C$**, including the surjectivity required by the question.

For any three nonempty <circles>, fix $u\in C_3$. The <triangle inequality> gives, for every $z\in C_1,w\in C_2$,
$$
|z-w|\le|z-u|+|u-w|\le m(C_1,C_3)+m(C_2,C_3).
$$
Taking the maximum over $z,w$ proves **the requested inequality**.

For centers $c_1,c_2$ and radii $r_1,r_2$, the <maximum separation of two circles> is bounded above by $|c_1-c_2|+r_1+r_2$. If the centers differ, choose $z=c_1+r_1v$ and $w=c_2-r_2v$, where $v=(c_1-c_2)/|c_1-c_2|$, to attain the bound. If the centers coincide, any unit $v$ gives opposite points attaining $r_1+r_2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{m(C_{\alpha_1\beta_1\gamma_1},C_{\alpha_2\beta_2\gamma_2})
=\frac{|\alpha_1\beta_2-\alpha_2\beta_1|}{|\alpha_1\alpha_2|}
+\frac{\sqrt{|\beta_1|^2-\alpha_1\gamma_1}}{|\alpha_1|}
+\frac{\sqrt{|\beta_2|^2-\alpha_2\gamma_2}}{|\alpha_2|}.}
$$
The function $m$ is not a <metric> on positive-radius <circles>, since $m(C,C)=2r$, even though it satisfies this triangle-type inequality.

The line through the origin is $l_0=\operatorname{span}(u)$, containing zero and closed under addition and scalar multiplication, so it is a <vector subspace>. If $l_{x_1}=l_{x_2}$, then $x_1\in l_{x_2}$ and $x_1=x_2+\lambda u$ for some real $\lambda$. Conversely that equality gives the same set after shifting the line parameter. Thus
$$
\boxed{l_{x_1}=l_{x_2}\iff x_1-x_2\in\operatorname{span}(u).}
$$
Changing representatives to $x'=x+su$, $y'=y+tu$ changes their sum by $(s+t)u$ and a scalar multiple by $\alpha su$. The line classes therefore do not change, proving both operations well-defined. Associativity follows from $l_{(x+y)+z}=l_{x+(y+z)}$. The zero vector of this <quotient vector space> is **the entire line $l_0$**, because $l_x+l_0=l_x$.

For a <basis> $(u,b_1,b_2)$ of $\mathbb R^3$, every vector is $x=su+tb_1+vb_2$, so $l_x=t l_{b_1}+v l_{b_2}$. If this combination equals $l_0$, then $tb_1+vb_2=su$; independence of the three original <basis vectors> forces $t=v=s=0$. Hence **$(l_{b_1},l_{b_2})$ is a basis** of the space of <parallel lines as a quotient vector space>.

For the specified direction, add $zu$ to $(x,y,z)^T$ to obtain the representative $(x+z,y+3z,0)^T$. Thus its coordinates in the two standard line classes are $(x+z,y+3z)^T$. The two supplied image classes have coordinates $(1,1)^T$ and $(-3,1)^T$, while their input coordinates are $(1,-1)^T$ and $(1,1)^T$. If $a_1,a_2$ are the matrix columns, then $a_1-a_2=(1,1)^T$ and $a_1+a_2=(-3,1)^T$. Solving gives
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

= Solution

Let $M$ bound all $|a_nw^n|$ and put $q=|z|/|w|<1$. Then
$$
|a_nz^n|=|a_nw^n|q^n\le Mq^n.
$$
The <geometric series> $\sum_{n=0}^\infty Mq^n$ converges, so the <comparison test for series> proves **absolute convergence** of $\sum a_nz^n$. This is the <bounded power-series terms force interior absolute convergence> principle.

Define the <radius of convergence> by
$$
\boxed{\rho=\sup\{|w|:\ \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nw^n\text{ converges}\},}
$$
allowing $\rho=0$ and $\rho=\infty$. If $|z|<\rho$, the supremum supplies a point of convergence $w$ with $|w|>|z|$; convergence makes its terms bounded, so the argument above gives <absolute convergence> at $z$. If $|z|>\rho$, convergence is impossible by definition. At $|z|=\rho$, no general conclusion follows. Thus the convergence region has a circular interior centered at zero, with boundary behavior depending on the <power series>.

= Solution

All three examples below have <radius of convergence> one, because the $n$th roots of their coefficient moduli tend to one.

For convergence everywhere on the boundary, take **$\sum_{n=1}^\infty z^n/n^2$**. On $|z|=1$ it converges absolutely: for $n\ge2$, $1/n^2\le1/[n(n-1)]=1/(n-1)-1/n$, whose sum telescopes to a finite value.

For divergence everywhere on the boundary, take **$\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n$**. If $|z|=1$, every term has modulus one, so the <term test for divergence> applies.

For mixed boundary behavior, take **$\sum_{n=1}^\infty z^n/n$**. At $z=1$ it is the divergent <harmonic series>; each successive dyadic block contributes at least $1/2$. At every other point $|z|=1$, <summation by parts> proves convergence. Indeed put $B_k=\sum_{j=m}^kz^j$, so the finite <geometric series> gives $|B_k|\le2/|1-z|=:C_z$. Then
$$
\sum_{n=m}^N\frac{z^n}{n}=\frac{B_N}{N}+\sum_{n=m}^{N-1}B_n\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right),\qquad
\left|\sum_{n=m}^N\frac{z^n}{n}\right|\le\frac{C_z}{m}.
$$
Thus the partial sums form a <Cauchy sequence> and converge. This derives the relevant <Dirichlet test> rather than only naming it.

To prove the <intermediate value theorem> in this setting, let $A=\{t\in[a,b]:f(t)<v\}$ and $c=\sup A$. The endpoint inequalities and <continuity> give an interval immediately to the right of $a$ contained in $A$ and an interval immediately to the left of $b$ disjoint from $A$. Thus $a<c<b$. Choose points of $A$ tending to $c$; <continuity> gives $f(c)\le v$. If $f(c)<v$, <continuity> would place some point larger than $c$ in $A$, contradicting the supremum. Hence **$\boxed{f(c)=v}$** at an interior point.

For a continuous self-map of $[a,b]$, define $g(t)=f(t)-t$. The range condition implies $g(a)\ge0$ and $g(b)\le0$. If either is zero, its endpoint is a <fixed point>. Otherwise apply the just-proved <intermediate value theorem> to $-g$ with target zero to obtain $d\in(a,b)$ and $g(d)=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Every continuous self-map of }[a,b]\text{ has a fixed point.}}
$$
This proves the <fixed-point property of a closed interval> without requiring $f$ itself to be monotone.

= Solution

By <continuity> of $g''$ at $\alpha$, for any $\epsilon>0$ choose $\delta(\epsilon)>0$ such that $|g''(s)|\le |g''(\alpha)|+\epsilon=:M$ whenever $|s-\alpha|<\delta(\epsilon)$. Twice using the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives the remainder as
$$
g(t)-g(\alpha)-g'(\alpha)(t-\alpha)=\int_\alpha^t[g'(s)-g'(\alpha)]\,ds.
$$
The inner difference is $\int_\alpha^s g''(r)\,dr$ and has modulus at most $M|s-\alpha|$. Integrating this bound over the interval between $\alpha$ and $t$ proves, for either sign of $t-\alpha$,
$$
\boxed{|g(t)-g(\alpha)-g'(\alpha)(t-\alpha)|\le L|t-\alpha|^2,\qquad L=(|g''(\alpha)|+\epsilon)/2.}
$$
This is the local quadratic <Taylor remainder> estimate. The given $\epsilon$ may enter the bound on the second derivative; no stronger differentiability assumption is needed.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <intermediate value theorem> gives a root $r\in(a,b)$, unique because $f'>0$ makes $f$ <strictly increasing>. We show that <Newton root-finding iteration>, started at $b$, always stays to the right of $r$. If $x>r$, the <mean value theorem> gives $f(x)=f'(\xi)(x-r)$ for some $\xi\in(r,x)$. Since $f''>0$, $f'$ is <strictly increasing>, so $0<f'(\xi)<f'(x)$. It follows that
$$
0<\frac{f(x)}{f'(x)}<x-r,\qquad \boxed{r<F(x)<x.}
$$
Induction gives $r<x_{n+1}<x_n\le b$, so the <bounded monotone sequence theorem> supplies a limit $\alpha\ge r$. The continuous positive $f'$ is bounded away from zero on $[a,b]$, making $F$ continuous. Passing to the limit in $x_{n+1}=F(x_n)$ gives $\alpha=F(\alpha)$ and hence $f(\alpha)=0$. Uniqueness implies **$\boxed{\alpha=r}$**.

For the rate, apply the <Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder> to $f$ between $r$ and $x_n$:
$$
0=f(r)=f(x_n)-f'(x_n)(x_n-r)+\frac12f''(\xi_n)(x_n-r)^2,\qquad \xi_n\in(r,x_n).
$$
With $e_n=x_n-r>0$, rearrangement gives
$$
\boxed{e_{n+1}=\frac{f''(\xi_n)}{2f'(x_n)}e_n^2,\qquad
\frac{e_{n+1}}{e_n^2}\longrightarrow\frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)}>0.}
$$
Thus the <monotone Newton convergence for a strictly convex function> has **quadratic convergence**. More concretely, if $K=\max_{[a,b]}f''/[2\min_{[a,b]}f']$, then $e_{n+1}\le Ke_n^2$. Once $Ke_N<1$, induction gives $e_{N+j}\le K^{-1}(Ke_N)^{2^j}$, exhibiting the repeated squaring of small errors.

= Solution
{parent=12d}

For a bounded function on a nondegenerate compact interval $[a,b]$, a partition $P$ has <Darboux sums>
$$
L(f,P)=\sum_i\inf_{[t_{i-1},t_i]}f\,(t_i-t_{i-1}),\qquad
U(f,P)=\sum_i\sup_{[t_{i-1},t_i]}f\,(t_i-t_{i-1}).
$$
The <Riemann integrability criterion> is
$$
\boxed{\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists P:\ U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\epsilon.}
$$
Equivalently the supremum of lower sums and infimum of upper sums agree; their common value is the <Riemann integral>. A bounded nonintegrable example is the <Dirichlet function> $\boxed{f=1_{\mathbb Q}}$. Every positive-length interval contains rational and irrational points, so every lower sum is zero and every upper sum is $b-a>0$.

Suppose the continuous nonnegative function has $f(t_0)>0$ somewhere. <Continuity> supplies a positive-length interval within $[a,b]$ on which $f\ge f(t_0)/2$; at an endpoint use a one-sided interval. By <monotonicity of the Riemann integral>, its contribution to the integral is strictly positive, a contradiction. Therefore **$\boxed{f\equiv0}$** under the stated continuous-function assumptions.

For the general integrable case, choose $c\in(a,b)$ and let $f(c)=1$ while $f(t)=0$ for $t\ne c$. This nonnegative <indicator function> is <Riemann integrable> with integral zero: isolate $c$ in partition intervals of arbitrarily small total length, making the upper sum arbitrarily small while the lower sum stays zero. Yet $f(c)=1$. This is the <single-point changes preserve Riemann integrals> phenomenon and supplies the counterexample.

= Solution

Put $G(t)=\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$. At an interior point and for $h\ne0$ small enough,
$$
\frac{G(t+h)-G(t)}h-f(t)=\frac1h\int_t^{t+h}[f(x)-f(t)]\,dx.
$$
By <continuity> at $t$, the modulus is bounded by the supremum of $|f(x)-f(t)|$ between $t$ and $t+h$, which tends to zero. Thus **the fundamental theorem of calculus gives**
$$
\boxed{G'(t)=f(t).}
$$
At the interval endpoints the same argument supplies the corresponding one-sided derivatives.

For an integrable discontinuous function, the single-point spike from part (i) has $G\equiv0$, so $G'(c)=0\ne f(c)=1$; this disproves the derivative identity even when the primitive is differentiable. To see that differentiability itself may fail, take the <step function> $f(x)=0$ for $x<c$ and $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge c$. It is <Riemann integrable>, but its primitive is $G(t)=\max(0,t-c)$ and has left derivative zero and right derivative one at $c$. Hence the full assertion is false for general <Riemann integrable functions>.
