Let $D:I\to\mathcal C$ be a finite diagram. Form the finite <categorical products>
$$
P=\prod_{i\in\operatorname{Ob}I}D(i),\qquad Q=\prod_{u:i\to j\in\operatorname{Mor}I}D(j).
$$
There are two <morphisms> $s,t:P\rightrightarrows Q$: their components at $u:i\to j$ are $D(u)\pi_i$ and $\pi_j$, respectively. Let $e:L\to P$ be their <equaliser>. Then $\ell_i=\pi_i e$ satisfy $D(u)\ell_i=\ell_j$, so they constitute a <cone over a diagram>.

Any other cone $x_i:X\to D(i)$ uniquely determines $x:X\to P$. Its cone equations say precisely that $sx=tx$. The <equaliser> therefore gives a unique $h:X\to L$ with $eh=x$, and thus $\ell_i h=x_i$. Conversely these component equations force $eh=x$ by the <categorical product> <universal property>, so there is exactly one such mediator. This proves that $(L,\ell_i)$ is a <categorical limit> of $D$.

The empty <categorical products> are included: if $I$ is empty, $P$ and $Q$ are terminal and the construction gives the empty <categorical limit>. Thus **finite <categorical products> and <equalisers> give every finite <categorical limit>**, as a special case of the <construction of small limits from products and equalizers>.

Let $1$ be a <terminal object>. The <pullback> of the unique arrows $A\to1\leftarrow B$ is a binary <categorical product>: maps $X\to A$ and $X\to B$ automatically have equal composites into $1$, so the <pullback> <universal property> is exactly the <categorical product> <universal property>.

For parallel arrows $f,g:A\rightrightarrows B$, form $B\times B$ and take the <pullback> of $\langle f,g\rangle:A\to B\times B$ along the diagonal $\delta:B\to B\times B$. Write the resulting projections as $e:E\to A$ and $b:E\to B$. The <pullback> equation gives
$$
fe=b=ge.
$$
If $u:X\to A$ satisfies $fu=gu$, the pair $(u,fu)$ is a <pullback> cone, producing a unique $v:X\to E$ with $ev=u$ and $bv=fu$. The second condition is forced by the first, since $b=fe$. Thus $e$ is the <equaliser> of $f,g$.

The <terminal object> and iterated binary <categorical products> give all finite <categorical products>, including the nullary one. Part (a) therefore proves **<pullbacks> and a <terminal object> give all finite <categorical limits>**.

For a diagram shape $I$, a <functor> $F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D$ preserves <categorical limits> of shape $I$ if every limiting cone $(L,\ell_i)$ over every diagram $D:I\to\mathcal C$ is carried to a limiting cone $(FL,F\ell_i)$ over $FD$. It reflects <categorical limits> of that shape if a cone in $\mathcal C$ is limiting whenever its image cone is limiting. These properties concern cones which are given; reflection is not an assertion that every cone in the target lifts.

Suppose $F$ is a <full and faithful functor> and the image of $(L,\ell_i)$ is limiting. A cone $(X,x_i)$ in $\mathcal C$ becomes a cone in $\mathcal D$, so there is a unique $h:FX\to FL$ with $(F\ell_i)h=Fx_i$. Fullness writes $h=Fk$ for some $k:X\to L$. Faithfulness gives $\ell_i k=x_i$, and also ensures uniqueness: two such $k$ would have the same image, by uniqueness of $h$. Hence the original cone is limiting. This proves **every <full and faithful functor> reflects <categorical limits>**, the <limits reflected by full and faithful functors> criterion, with no essential-surjectivity requirement.

ph=arh=au=z,

where $z:Z\to X$ is the structure map. Thus $h$ is automatically a slice <morphism>, and underlying uniqueness gives uniqueness in the slice. Hence **$U_X$ reflects <pullbacks>**.

Write $H_c=\mathcal C(-,c)$. The <category of elements> $\int P$ of a <categorical presheaf> $P$ has objects $(c,x)$ with $x\in P(c)$; a <morphism> $(c,x)\to(d,y)$ is $u:c\to d$ satisfying $P(u)y=x$. It is a <small category> because $\mathcal C$ is small and each $P(c)$ is a <set>. Consider the diagram sending $(c,x)$ to $H_c$ and $u$ to the <natural transformation> given by postcomposition with $u$.

There is a canonical cocone $\alpha_{c,x}:H_c\to P$ with
$$
(\alpha_{c,x})_a(v)=P(v)x\qquad(v:a\to c).
$$
Functoriality makes it a <natural transformation>, and the equation $P(u)y=x$ makes these transformations a cocone.

To prove its <universal property>, let $Q$ be any <categorical presheaf> with a compatible cocone $\beta_{c,x}:H_c\to Q$. Define
$$
t_c(x)=(\beta_{c,x})_c(1_c).
$$
For $u:c\to d$ and $y\in P(d)$, compatibility with the arrow $(c,P(u)y)\to(d,y)$ gives
$$
t_c(P(u)y)=(\beta_{d,y})_c(u)=Q(u)(\beta_{d,y})_d(1_d)=Q(u)t_d(y).
$$
Thus $t:P\to Q$ is natural. <Naturality> of each $\beta_{c,x}$ shows $t\alpha_{c,x}=\beta_{c,x}$. Conversely any transformation with these equations must have the displayed values $t_c(x)$, so it is unique. We have proved the <canonical colimit presentation of a presheaf>:
$$
\boxed{P\cong\operatorname{colim}_{(c,x)\in\int P}H_c.}
$$

A <cartesian closed category> has finite <categorical products> and, for every $P,Q$, an <exponential object> $Q^P$ with an evaluation <morphism> $\operatorname{ev}:Q^P\times P\to Q$ such that composition with evaluation gives <natural bijections>
$$
\mathcal E(R,Q^P)\cong\mathcal E(R\times P,Q).
$$
Finite <categorical products> in the <presheaf category> are pointwise; in particular its <terminal object> is the constant singleton <categorical presheaf>. For <categorical presheaves> $P,Q$ define
$$
\boxed{(Q^P)(c)=\operatorname{Nat}(H_c\times P,Q).}
$$
For $u:d\to c$, restriction sends $\alpha$ to $\alpha\circ(H_u\times1_P)$. Composition and identity laws follow from those of the <Yoneda embedding>, so this is a <categorical presheaf>.

Define evaluation at $c$ by $\operatorname{ev}_c(\alpha,x)=\alpha_c(1_c,x)$. For $u:d\to c$, <naturality> of $\alpha$ gives $Q(u)\alpha_c(1_c,x)=\alpha_d(u,P(u)x)$, which is the evaluation of the restricted pair. Hence evaluation is a <natural transformation>.

Given $t:R\times P\to Q$, its <currying> sends $z\in R(c)$ to the transformation whose component at $a$ is
$$
(\widehat t_c(z))_a(v,x)=t_a(R(v)z,x)\qquad(v:a\to c,\ x\in P(a)).
$$
<Naturality> in $a$ follows from that of $t$, and <naturality> in $c$ follows by composing $v$ with the relevant arrow. Evaluating $\widehat t$ at $(1_c,x)$ recovers $t_c(z,x)$. Conversely, if $s:R\to Q^P$, <naturality> of $s$ along $v:a\to c$ says that evaluating $s_a(R(v)z)$ at $(1_a,x)$ gives $(s_c(z))_a(v,x)$. Thus <currying> the evaluation of $s$ recovers $s$. These constructions are inverse and natural, proving the <universal property>. This is the <exponential in a presheaf category>, and proves **the <presheaf category> is <cartesian closed>**.

\operatorname{Nat}(H_c,P)\cong P(c),\qquad \alpha\longmapsto\alpha_c(1_c).

A <poset> becomes a <category> whose objects are its elements and whose <hom-set> $X(x,y)$ is a singleton if $x\le y$ and empty otherwise. Reflexivity supplies identity arrows and transitivity supplies composition; all possible composites are unique, so the <category> axioms hold. Antisymmetry says that isomorphic objects are equal. A <functor> between such <categories> is exactly an <order-preserving function>: an inequality must be sent to an inequality, and there is no further choice of arrow map.

Since the <hom-sets> in these <categories> are either empty or singleton, an <adjunction> $f\dashv g$ is precisely the condition
$$
\boxed{f(x)\le y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\le g(y)\qquad(x\in X,\ y\in Y).}
$$
The <hom-set> <bijections> are then the unique possible ones, and <naturality> is automatic. This is a <Galois connection> between the two <posets>.

Equivalently, the monotone <functions> satisfy the unit and counit inequalities $x\le gf(x)$ and $fg(y)\le y$. The equivalence implies these by taking $y=f(x)$ and $x=g(y)$. Conversely, if the inequalities hold, $f(x)\le y$ implies $x\le gf(x)\le g(y)$, while $x\le g(y)$ implies $f(x)\le fg(y)\le y$.

For $X\subseteq A$, define
$$
\exists_p(X)=p[X],\qquad \forall_p(X)=\{b\in B:p^{-1}\{b\}\subseteq X\}=B\setminus p[A\setminus X].
$$
Both are monotone maps $PA\to PB$. For $Y\subseteq B$,
$$
p[X]\subseteq Y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X\subseteq p^{-1}[Y],
$$
so <direct image> is a <left adjoint> to inverse image. Also,
$$
p^{-1}[Y]\subseteq X\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y\subseteq\{b:p^{-1}\{b\}\subseteq X\},
$$
so the fiberwise universal condition gives the <right adjoint>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\exists_p\dashv p^*\dashv\forall_p.}
$$
An empty fiber satisfies the universal condition vacuously, so every point outside $p[A]$ belongs to $\forall_p(X)$, regardless of $X$. This is essential when $p$ is not surjective.

$$

$$
t_U=t_{\varnothing}\circ\operatorname{res}^{U}_{\varnothing}

for every open $U$, since the restrictions of $\Delta A$ are identities. Thus it is determined by the <function> $P(\varnothing)\to A$. Conversely such a <function> defines all $t_U$ by that equation, and the <categorical presheaf> restriction laws make the components compatible. Hence
$$
\operatorname{Nat}(P,\Delta A)\cong\operatorname{Set}(P(\varnothing),A).
$$

$$
s_U=\operatorname{res}^{S}_U\circ s_S.
$$
Every <function> $A\to P(S)$ supplies compatible components by this formula. Therefore $\operatorname{Nat}(\Delta A,P)\cong\operatorname{Set}(A,P(S))$, giving the <adjoints to constant presheaves on open sets>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ev}_{\varnothing}\dashv\Delta\dashv\operatorname{ev}_S.}

These are adjoints of the stated constant-presheaf <functor>, with its value at every <open set> including the empty one; no sheafification is involved.

The <Freyd general adjoint functor theorem> is as follows. Let $G:\mathcal D\to\mathcal C$, with $\mathcal D$ a <complete category> which is <locally small>. Then $G$ has a <left adjoint> if and only if it preserves all small <categorical limits> and satisfies the <solution-set condition>: for each $c\in\mathcal C$, there is a set-indexed family
$$
u_i:c\to Gd_i
$$
such that every $u:c\to Gd$ can be written $u=G(h)u_i$ for some $i$ and some $h:d_i\to d$. The target <category> need not be complete. We prove all the existence steps.

First establish the <initial-object lemma for complete categories with a weakly initial set>. Let $\mathcal E$ be <locally small> and complete, with a <weakly initial set> $\{w_i\}$, meaning every object receives a map from at least one $w_i$. Its <categorical product> $w=\prod_iw_i$ is weakly initial: compose a <categorical product> projection with the map from the appropriate $w_i$. Local smallness makes $\mathcal E(w,w)$ a <set>. Let $e:v\to w$ be the simultaneous <equaliser> of every <endomorphism> of $w$ and the identity. Explicitly it is the <equaliser> of the two arrows
$$
w\rightrightarrows\prod_{k\in\mathcal E(w,w)}w
$$
with components $k$ and $1_w$. Thus $ke=e$ for every <endomorphism> $k$, and $e$ is monic.

Every object $x$ receives a <morphism> from $v$, by composing $e$ with a map $w\to x$. To prove uniqueness, take parallel maps $a,b:v\to x$ and their <equaliser> $m:y\to v$. Weak initiality of $w$ gives $t:w\to y$. The composite $emt$ is an <endomorphism> of $w$, so $(emt)e=e$. Cancelling the <monomorphism> $e$ gives $mte=1_v$. Hence $m$ is both monic and a <split epimorphism>, and is therefore invertible. Since $am=bm$, this forces $a=b$. We have proved $v$ is initial, without using a class-sized <categorical product> or <equaliser>.

For sufficiency in the theorem, fix $c$ and consider the <comma category> $(c\downarrow G)$. Its objects are pairs $(d,u:c\to Gd)$; a <morphism> $h:(d,u)\to(d',u')$ satisfies $G(h)u=u'$. This <category> is <locally small> because each <hom-set> is a subset of $\mathcal D(d,d')$. It is complete: for a small diagram of such pairs, take the underlying <categorical limit> $d$ in $\mathcal D$. The arrows from $c$ form a cone to its image under $G$, and preservation of the <categorical limit> gives a unique $u:c\to Gd$ inducing them. The same <universal property> gives all mediators in the <comma category>. The solution-set family is a <weakly initial set> in this <comma category>, so the lemma supplies an initial pair
$$
(Fc,\eta_c:c\to GFc).
$$
For $f:c\to c'$, initiality defines a unique $Ff:Fc\to Fc'$ satisfying $G(Ff)\eta_c=\eta_{c'}f$. Uniqueness proves the identity and composition laws, making $F$ a <functor>. Initiality also gives <bijections>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal D(Fc,d)\cong\mathcal C(c,Gd),\qquad h\longmapsto G(h)\eta_c.}
$$
The definition of $Ff$ proves <naturality> in $c$, and postcomposition proves <naturality> in $d$. These <bijections> are the required <adjunction> $F\dashv G$.

For necessity, if $F\dashv G$, the single arrow $\eta_c:c\to GFc$ is a solution <set>: the transpose of any $u:c\to Gd$ gives its factorization through $\eta_c$. If $d$ is a limiting cone vertex of a small diagram $D_j$ in $\mathcal D$, then, naturally in $c$,
$$
\mathcal C(c,Gd)\cong\mathcal D(Fc,d)
\cong\lim_j\mathcal D(Fc,D_j)
\cong\lim_j\mathcal C(c,GD_j).
$$
These are the canonical cone-factorization <bijections>, so $Gd$ with its image projections is a <categorical limit> of $GD_j$. Thus $G$ preserves all small <categorical limits>, completing both directions of the theorem.

A <monad> on $\mathcal C$ consists of an <endofunctor> $T:\mathcal C\to\mathcal C$ and <natural transformations> $\eta:1_{\mathcal C}\Rightarrow T$ and $\mu:T^2\Rightarrow T$ satisfying, at every object $A$,
$$
\boxed{\mu_A T\eta_A=1_{TA}=\mu_A\eta_{TA},\qquad
\mu_A T\mu_A=\mu_A\mu_{TA}.}
$$
These are the unit and associativity laws for the <unit and multiplication of a monad>.

An <algebra for a monad> is a pair $(A,a)$ with $a:TA\to A$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{a\eta_A=1_A,\qquad a\mu_A=aT a.}
$$
A <monad algebra morphism> $f:(A,a)\to(B,b)$ is a <morphism> $f:A\to B$ with $fa=bT f$. Identities satisfy this condition, and composing two such maps preserves it by functoriality of $T$. The resulting <Eilenberg-Moore category> is denoted $\mathcal C^T$. Its <forgetful functor> $U:\mathcal C^T\to\mathcal C$ sends $(A,a)$ to $A$ and leaves the underlying arrows unchanged.

Applying $G$ yields

Define the <free algebra functor> by
$$
F^T(X)=(TX,\mu_X),\qquad F^T(f)=Tf.
$$
The <monad> laws say precisely that $\mu_X$ defines an <monad algebra> structure on $TX$, while <naturality> of $\mu$ makes $Tf$ an <monad algebra> <morphism>. We construct the <adjunction> explicitly.

For an <monad algebra> $(A,a)$ and a <morphism> $f:X\to A$, put $\overline f=aTf:TX\to A$. It is an <monad algebra> <morphism> because
$$
\overline f\mu_X=aTf\mu_X=a\mu_A T^2f
=aTaT^2f=aT\overline f.
$$
Conversely, from an <monad algebra> <morphism> $h:(TX,\mu_X)\to(A,a)$ obtain $h\eta_X:X\to A$. These operations are inverse: <naturality> of $\eta$ and the <monad algebra> unit law give
$$
aTf\eta_X=a\eta_A f=f,
$$
and the algebra-morphism equation together with the <monad> unit law gives
$$
aT(h\eta_X)=aThT\eta_X=h\mu_XT\eta_X=h.
$$
The formulas commute with precomposition in $X$ and composition with <monad algebra> <morphisms> in $(A,a)$, so the <bijections> are natural. Therefore the <free-forgetful Eilenberg-Moore adjunction> is
$$
\boxed{F^T\dashv U,\qquad\mathcal C^T(F^TX,(A,a))\cong\mathcal C(X,A).}
$$
Its unit is $\eta_X$ and its counit at $(A,a)$ is the <monad algebra> action $a$.

For each <algebra for a monad> $(A,a)$, consider the specific pair in $\mathcal D$
$$
FTA\mathrel{\substack{\xrightarrow{Fa}\\\xrightarrow[\varepsilon_{FA}]{} }}FA.
$$
Then the exact condition is
$$
\boxed{K\text{ has a left adjoint}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{each of these pairs has a coequalizer in }\mathcal D.}
$$
Each pair is reflexive, with common section $F\eta_A$: the two composite identities follow from $a\eta_A=1_A$ and $\varepsilon_{FA}F\eta_A=1_{FA}$. Thus existence of all <reflexive coequalizers> is sufficient, but the displayed specified pairs give the necessary-and-sufficient condition.

To prove it, take $h:FA\to d$ and its adjoint transpose $x=Gh\eta_A:A\to Gd$. The transpose of $hFa$ is $xa$, by <naturality> of $\eta$. The transpose of $h\varepsilon_{FA}$ is
$$
GhG\varepsilon_{FA}\eta_{TA}=Gh\mu_A\eta_{TA}=Gh.
$$
Moreover counit <naturality> and a triangle identity give
$$
G\varepsilon_d T x
=G\varepsilon_d,GF(Gh\eta_A)
=Gh,G\varepsilon_{FA},GF\eta_A=Gh.
$$
Because transposition is bijective, the coequalizing equation is therefore equivalent to
$$
hFa=h\varepsilon_{FA}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad xa=G\varepsilon_d T x.
$$
The right side says exactly that $x:(A,a)\to Kd$ is a <monad algebra morphism>.

For sufficiency, choose a <coequalizer> $q_a:FA\to L(A,a)$ for every <monad algebra>. Its <universal property> and the equivalence just proved give <natural bijections>
$$
\mathcal D(L(A,a),d)\cong
\{h:FA\to d:hFa=h\varepsilon_{FA}\}
\cong\mathcal C^T((A,a),Kd).
$$
To see explicitly that the chosen objects form a <functor>, an <monad algebra> <morphism> $u:(A,a)\to(B,b)$ makes $q_bFu$ coequalize the pair for $a$: use $ua=bTu$ and <naturality> of the counit. Hence there is a unique $Lu$ with $Lu\,q_a=q_bFu$. Uniqueness proves its identity and composition laws and the <naturality> of the displayed <bijections>. Thus $L\dashv K$.

Conversely, suppose $L\dashv K$ with unit $\lambda_{(A,a)}:(A,a)\to KL(A,a)$. Transpose its underlying arrow $A\to GL(A,a)$ through $F\dashv G$ to obtain $q_a:FA\to L(A,a)$. Since the unit is an <monad algebra> <morphism>, the equivalence above shows that $q_a$ coequalizes the specified pair. Any coequalizing $h:FA\to d$ transposes to an <monad algebra> map $(A,a)\to Kd$, which factors uniquely through the unit by $L\dashv K$. Transposing back gives a unique factorization of $h$ through $q_a$. Thus $q_a$ is the required <coequalizer>. This proves both directions of the <left adjoint to the Eilenberg-Moore comparison functor> criterion.

Assume the <coequalizers> from part (b) exist and write them $q_a:FA\to L(A,a)$. The underlying arrow of the comparison-adjunction unit is
$$
\lambda_a=Gq_a\eta_A:A\to GL(A,a).
$$
We first relate it to the <monad algebra> action. <Naturality> of $\eta$, the <coequalizer> equation and a <monad> unit law give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_a a
&=Gq_a\eta_A a=Gq_a,Ta\,\eta_{TA}\\
&=G(q_aFa)\eta_{TA}=G(q_a\varepsilon_{FA})\eta_{TA}\\
&=Gq_a\mu_A\eta_{TA}=Gq_a.
\end{aligned}
$$
In $\mathcal C$, $a:TA\to A$ is itself a <split coequalizer> of $Ta,\mu_A:T^2A\rightrightarrows TA$. Indeed,
$$
aTa=a\mu_A,\quad a\eta_A=1_A,\quad
\mu_A\eta_{TA}=1_{TA},\quad Ta\eta_{TA}=\eta_Aa.
$$
For an explicit universal-property proof, if $r:TA\to X$ satisfies $rTa=r\mu_A$, then $s=r\eta_A$ obeys
$$
sa=r\eta_Aa=rTa\eta_{TA}=r\mu_A\eta_{TA}=r.
$$
Its uniqueness follows by composing any equation $sa=r$ with $\eta_A$.

Now $Gq_a$ equalizes $Ta$ and $\mu_A$. If it is a <coequalizer>, then $a$ and $Gq_a$ are <coequalizers> of the same pair. Their unique comparison map is $\lambda_a$, since $\lambda_a a=Gq_a$, and is therefore invertible. Conversely, if $\lambda_a$ is invertible, $Gq_a=\lambda_a a$ is an isomorphic copy of the <coequalizer> $a$, so it is a <coequalizer> too.

The <forgetful functor> $\mathcal C^T\to\mathcal C$ reflects <isomorphisms>: if an <monad algebra> <morphism> $u$ has an inverse as an underlying arrow, the equation $ua=bTu$ rearranges to $u^{-1}b=aT(u^{-1})$, making that inverse an <monad algebra> <morphism>. Thus underlying invertibility here is exactly invertibility of the unit component. For all <monad algebras> together, the <comparison-adjunction unit criterion> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda\text{ is invertible}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
G\text{ preserves every specified coequalizer }q_a.}
$$
Equivalently, the necessary-and-sufficient conditions for a <left adjoint> with invertible unit are existence of the <coequalizers> in part (b) and their preservation by $G$. No preservation of arbitrary <coequalizers> is required.
