For the <central limit theorem>, let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be <independent and identically distributed random variables> with <expectation> $\mu$ and finite positive <variance> $\sigma^2$. Then
$$
\boxed{\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).}
$$
Thus the centered, normalized sum has <convergence in distribution> to the standard <normal distribution>. The positive-<variance> assumption is needed for this normalization; if $\sigma^2=0$, each summand equals $\mu$ <almost surely> and the centered sum is identically zero.

Here is a <characteristic function> proof using only the finite second moment. Put $U=(X_1-\mu)/\sigma$, so $\mathbb EU=0$ and $\mathbb EU^2=1$. The elementary integral remainder identity gives $|e^{iz}-1-iz|\leq z^2/2$ for real $z$. Consequently
$$
\frac{e^{itU}-1-itU}{t^2}\longrightarrow-\frac{U^2}{2},\qquad
\left|\frac{e^{itU}-1-itU}{t^2}\right|\leq\frac{U^2}{2}.
$$
By the <dominated convergence theorem>, the <characteristic function> satisfies $\varphi_U(t)=1-t^2/2+o(t^2)$ as $t\to0$. For a fixed real $t$, <independence> therefore gives
$$
\varphi_{(U_1+\cdots+U_n)/\sqrt n}(t)
=\varphi_U(t/\sqrt n)^n
=\left(1+\frac{c_n}{n}\right)^n,\qquad c_n\longrightarrow-t^2/2.
$$
The allowed complex-number limit makes this tend to $e^{-t^2/2}$, the <characteristic function> of the standard <normal distribution>. It is continuous at zero, so the <Lévy continuity theorem> proves the asserted <convergence in distribution>. No third moment or boundedness of the summands was used.

For $t\geq0$, <independence> factors the <survival function> of the minimum:
$$
\mathbb P(M>t)=\prod_{j=1}^n\mathbb P(X_j>t)
=\prod_{j=1}^ne^{-\lambda t}=e^{-n\lambda t}.
$$
The minimum is nonnegative, and this is exactly the <survival function> of an <exponential distribution>. Hence
$$
\boxed{M\sim\operatorname{Exp}(n\lambda).}
$$
The parameter here is the rate, rather than the mean. The calculation is the simplest example of <competing exponential clocks>.

The <exponential distribution> has no atoms, so all sample values are distinct <almost surely>, and $M_i$ is the $i$th <order statistic>. Set $M_0=0$ and $D_i=M_i-M_{i-1}$. Part (b) gives $D_1\sim\operatorname{Exp}(n\lambda)$.

To justify induction and the required <independence>, let $J$ be the label attaining the minimum. Conditional on its time and label, the remaining clocks are fresh <independent> rate-$\lambda$ <exponential random variables>, by the <memoryless property>. This conditional claim can be checked directly: for a specified label $j$, first ringing time $u$, and residual lifetimes $y_k>0$ for $k\ne j$, the <joint probability density> is
$$
\lambda^n e^{-\lambda(nu+\sum_{k\ne j}y_k)}
=\frac1n\left(n\lambda e^{-n\lambda u}\right)
\prod_{k\ne j}\left(\lambda e^{-\lambda y_k}\right).
$$
Thus the first minimum, its uniform winning label and the surviving residual clocks are <independent>. Repeat this argument after each minimum. At the $i$th step there are $n-i+1$ clocks, so the <independent exponential order-statistic spacings> satisfy
$$
D_i\sim\operatorname{Exp}((n-i+1)\lambda),\qquad D_1,\ldots,D_n\text{ independent}.
$$
Since $Y_k/k$ has rate $k\lambda$ whenever $Y_k\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$, summing the gaps proves
$$
\boxed{M_i\overset d=\sum_{k=n-i+1}^n\frac{Y_k}{k}.}
$$
In particular the maximum is $M^*=M_n$. By <variance additivity for independent random variables> and the <Laplace transform of an exponential distribution>, its requested quantities are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EM^*=\frac1\lambda\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(M^*)=\frac1{\lambda^2}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{k^2},\qquad
\mathbb E e^{-sM^*}=\prod_{k=1}^n\frac{k\lambda}{k\lambda+s}\quad(s\geq0).}
$$
The product follows from <independence> of the gaps, not merely from their marginal <probability distributions>.

This is the <simple symmetric random walk> on the integers, starting at zero. It is recurrent: it returns to zero infinitely often <almost surely>, and in fact visits every integer infinitely often. To see the two-sided unboundedness explicitly, <gambler's ruin> gives, for positive integers $a,b$,
$$
\mathbb P_0(T_b<T_{-a})=\frac{a}{a+b}.
$$
Letting $a\to\infty$ shows that $b$ is hit with probability one; the analogous argument hits every negative integer. From any state the same argument gives return to zero with probability one. Repeated use of the <strong Markov property> then gives infinitely many returns and visits. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n=+\infty,\qquad
\liminf_{n\to\infty}X_n=-\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
In particular $X_n$ has no limit <almost surely>, and $|X_n|$ does not tend to infinity because of the returns to zero. In contrast, the <strong law of large numbers> gives $X_n/n\to0$ <almost surely>, while the <central limit theorem> gives $X_n/\sqrt n\xrightarrow dN(0,1)$. These distinguish oscillation of the path from scaling limits of its <probability distribution>.

Write $c=1/(a-1)$. If $X_0=x>c$, then for either possible sign,
$$
X_{n+1}\geq aX_n-1,\qquad
X_{n+1}-X_n\geq(a-1)X_n-1>0
$$
as long as $X_n>c$. Thus the process stays above $c$, is strictly increasing, and induction gives $X_n\geq c+a^n(x-c)\to\infty$. If $x<-c$, the symmetric argument makes the process strictly decreasing to $-\infty$. This proves the first assertion pathwise, with the strict sufficient threshold $|x|>c$.

For an arbitrary starting point, a proof is needed that the process actually reaches such a region. Fix $L>c$ and choose an integer $m$ so
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}a^j=\frac{a^m-1}{a-1}>L.
$$
At a block starting at time $km$, if $X_{km}\geq0$, the next $m$ signs all equal to $+1$ give $X_{(k+1)m}>L$. If $X_{km}<0$, all $-1$ signs instead give $X_{(k+1)m}<-L$. Conditional on the current <natural filtration>, the selected block has probability $\varepsilon=2^{-m}$, by <independence> of future signs. If $\tau=\inf\{n:|X_n|>L\}$, iterated <conditional expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb P_x(\tau>km)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^k\longrightarrow0.
$$
This is a <geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability>, so $\tau<\infty$ <almost surely>. Once outside, the sign is preserved and, for every future noise sequence,
$$
|X_{\tau+j}|\geq c+a^j(|X_\tau|-c)\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
Consequently the <explosive affine recursion with symmetric bounded noise> has
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_x\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}|X_n|=\infty\right)=1\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The block argument closes the gap between escape from a large initial value and escape from an arbitrary one; no assertion about the absence of atoms in an infinite random series is necessary.

A discrete-time <martingale> relative to a <filtration> $(\mathcal F_n)$ is an <adapted process> $(M_n)$ with $\mathbb E|M_n|<\infty$ and
$$
\mathbb E[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Iterating <conditional expectation> also gives $\mathbb E[M_m\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$ for $m\geq n$. It is bounded in $L^2$ when each $M_n$ is square integrable and
$$
\boxed{\sup_{n\geq0}\mathbb E|M_n|^2<\infty.}
$$
This is a uniform bound on the second moments, not a pathwise bound on all sample values.

The <optional stopping theorem> for bounded <stopping times> says that, if $\sigma\leq\tau\leq K$ for a deterministic finite $K$, then
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_\tau\mid\mathcal F_\sigma]=M_\sigma,\qquad
\mathbb EM_\tau=\mathbb EM_0.}
$$
Here $\mathcal F_\sigma$ is the <stopping-time sigma-algebra>. The second assertion follows by taking $\sigma=0$ and then <expectations>. An $L^2$ bound is not needed: integrability at the finitely many deterministic times suffices.

For a proof, both stopped values are integrable since $|M_\tau|,|M_\sigma|\leq\sum_{j=0}^K|M_j|$. Expand
$$
M_\tau-M_\sigma=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbf1_{\{\sigma\leq k<\tau\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k).
$$
If $A\in\mathcal F_\sigma$, then $A\cap\{\sigma\leq k\}\in\mathcal F_k$ by the definition of the <stopping-time sigma-algebra>, and $\{\tau>k\}\in\mathcal F_k$ because $\tau$ is a <stopping time>. Thus the indicator of $A\cap\{\sigma\leq k<\tau\}$ is $\mathcal F_k$-measurable. Multiplying the sum by $\mathbf1_A$, taking <expectations>, and conditioning each summand on $\mathcal F_k$ gives zero, by the <martingale> increment property. Therefore $\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AM_\tau]=\mathbb E[\mathbf1_AM_\sigma]$ for every $A\in\mathcal F_\sigma$. Since $M_\sigma$ is $\mathcal F_\sigma$-measurable, this is exactly the claimed <conditional expectation> identity.

**The global drift hypothesis as printed is impossible on a finite state space.** Indeed, let $x_*$ minimize $f$. Every possible value of $f(X_{n+1})$ is at least $f(x_*)$, so
$$
\mathbb E[f(X_{n+1})\mid X_n=x_*]\geq f(x_*),
$$
contradicting a decrease by $\delta>0$. Equivalently, its formal iteration would give $\mathbb E_xf(X_n)=f(x)-n\delta$, whereas $f$ is bounded on the finite state space. This is the <constant nonzero drift is impossible on a finite Markov chain> obstruction. Thus there is no genuine chain satisfying all the literal premises.

The meaningful intended version imposes the drift only on $S\setminus D$, and stops at $D$. In the <natural filtration> $\mathcal F_n=\sigma(X_0,\ldots,X_n)$, the <Markov property> then gives, on $\{T>n\}$,
$$
\mathbb E[f(X_{n+1})-f(X_n)\mid\mathcal F_n]=-\delta.
$$
The correct <stopped martingale> is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde Y_n=f(X_{n\wedge T})+\delta(n\wedge T).}
$$
It is adapted and integrable, since $f$ is bounded and $n\wedge T\leq n$. Its increment equals
$$
\widetilde Y_{n+1}-\widetilde Y_n
=\mathbf1_{\{T>n\}}\bigl(f(X_{n+1})-f(X_n)+\delta\bigr),
$$
whose <conditional expectation> is zero. Taking <expectations> yields
$$
\mathbb E_x f(X_{n\wedge T})+\delta\mathbb E_x(n\wedge T)=f(x).
$$
Since $T<\infty$ <almost surely> and $f(X_T)=0$, the <bounded convergence theorem> gives $\mathbb E_x f(X_{n\wedge T})\to0$. The <monotone convergence theorem> gives $\mathbb E_x(n\wedge T)\uparrow\mathbb E_xT$, without presupposing its finiteness. Consequently the <constant-drift hitting-time identity> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_xT=\frac{f(x)}\delta<\infty.}
$$
For $x\in D$, both sides are zero. Under the repaired hypothesis the unstopped $f(X_n)+n\delta$ need not be a <martingale> after hitting $D$; it is the displayed <stopped martingale> that proves the result.

**The printed hitting-time formula is false for the stated transition probabilities.** For example, take $N=2$ and $q=3/4$, put $p=1-q$, and write $m_i=\mathbb E_iT$. A first step gives
$$
m_1=1+pm_2,\qquad m_2=1+m_1,
$$
so $m_2=2/q=8/3$. The printed expression gives $3$, not $8/3$. This discrepancy is present in the original PDF, not just in its converted text. The quantifier mentioning every positive integer must also be restricted to positive starting states $1,\ldots,N$; states larger than $N$ are not in $S$.

Here is the exact solution for the chain as specified. Until $T$, only $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ is visited. From any such state, a sequence of downward steps reaches zero within $N$ steps with probability at least $q^N>0$; the forced step at $N$ only increases that probability. The <Markov property> therefore gives
$$
\mathbb P_i(T>kN)\leq(1-q^N)^k.
$$
Thus $T$ is finite <almost surely> and has finite <expectation>, without needing the supplied transience assumption. Set $m_0=0$. The <first-step analysis> gives
$$
m_i=1+qm_{i-1}+pm_{i+1}\quad(1\leq i<N),\qquad m_N=1+m_{N-1}.
$$
Put $d=q-p=2q-1>0$, $r=p/q$, and $D_i=m_i-m_{i-1}$. The equations become
$$
qD_i-pD_{i+1}=1,\qquad D_N=1.
$$
Backward iteration, or substitution into this recursion, gives
$$
D_i=\frac1d-\frac{2p}{d}r^{N-i}.
$$
Summing from $1$ to $i$ gives the corrected <hitting time of a downward-biased walk reflected at an upper barrier>:
$$
\boxed{m_i=\frac{i}{d}-\frac{2pq}{d^2}r^{N-i}(1-r^i),\quad 0\leq i\leq N,}
$$
and in particular
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_NT=\frac N{2q-1}
-\frac{2q(1-q)}{(2q-1)^2}\left[1-\left(\frac{1-q}{q}\right)^N\right].}
$$
These values also supply the appropriate test function for part (b): $f(i)=m_i$, $f(0)=0$, and $Pf=f-1$ at every state before absorption. The finite-state stopped argument is applied on $\{0,\ldots,N\}$, with zero made absorbing.

For $N\geq3$, the printed hint cannot produce constant drift by choosing its single constant $c$. With $f(i)=i$ for $i<N$ and $f(N)=N+c$, the expected downward drift is $d$ at ordinary interior states, $d-pc$ at $N-1$, and $1+c$ at $N$. Making the top drift $d$ requires $c=-2p$, but then the drift at $N-1$ is $d+2p^2$, not $d$. For $N=2$ there is no ordinary interior state; a different constant drift can be arranged, but still gives $2/q$ rather than the printed formula. This identifies precisely the missing correction at the neighboring state for the intended drift $d$; the geometric term in the correct test function repairs it throughout the interval.

$$
\boxed{J_i\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda_i),\qquad
\mathbb P_i(X_{J_i}=i-1)=\mathbb P_i(X_{J_i}=i+1)=\frac12.}
$$
The <holding time> is <independent> of the direction of this jump: the conditional probability of destination $j$ is the rate $q_{ij}$ divided by the total rate $\lambda_i$, independently of the elapsed holding duration. This is the exponential-clock construction of a <continuous-time Markov chain> with the given <infinitesimal generator matrix>.

At $i=0$ or $i=N$, all rates out are zero. These are <absorbing states>; the path remains at its initial endpoint and $J_i=\infty$ <almost surely>. There is no next visited state, so $X_{J_i}$ is not a finite-time jump value at either endpoint. If one separately uses $X_\infty$ for the limiting state, that limit is the same endpoint.

The embedded jump chain in the interior is a <simple symmetric random walk> on $\{0,\ldots,N\}$, stopped at the endpoints. From any interior state it has probability at least $2^{-N}$ of hitting a boundary in the next $N$ jumps, by following successive leftward choices. Thus its number of jumps before absorption is finite <almost surely>. Each of this finite number of <holding times> is finite <almost surely>, so absorption also occurs in finite continuous time. The finite set of positive interior rates causes no explosion; in particular their maximum bounds the total jump rate. When $N=1$, the chain starts at an already absorbing endpoint.

After a departure to $i+1$, the analogous probability of reaching $N$ before returning is $1/(N-i)$. The departure is equally likely in either direction, so the probability of absorption before a return is
$$
p_i=\frac12\left(\frac1i+\frac1{N-i}\right)
=\frac{N}{2i(N-i)}.
$$
At every return to $i$, the <strong Markov property> resets these alternatives. Hence the <geometric return count before absorption> gives $K_i\sim\operatorname{Geom}(p_i)$ on positive integers. One may construct the chain from its embedded jump chain and a separate sequence of fresh <independent> exponential clocks. Then its successive <holding times> at $i$ are <independent and identically distributed> rate-$\lambda_i$ <exponential random variables>, independently of $K_i$, and
$$
I=\sum_{j=1}^{K_i}E_j^{(i)}.
$$
Using the first calculation gives the <occupation time at an interior state of a variable-rate symmetric walk>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_iI=\frac1{p_i\lambda_i}
=\frac{2i(N-i)}{N\lambda_i}.}
$$
For $N=2,i=1$, $p_i=1$ and the only visit is the initial one; the degenerate positive-integer <geometric distribution> at one handles this case. From the masses of the <Poisson distribution>, its <probability generating function> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[z^Z]=\sum_{k=0}^\infty z^ke^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
=e^{\lambda(z-1)}\qquad(0\leq z\leq1).}
$$
At $z=0$, the term $z^0$ is interpreted as one, so this also gives $\mathbb P(Z=0)=e^{-\lambda}$.

One constructive definition of a rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson process> uses <independent and identically distributed> <exponential random variables> $E_j$ of rate $\lambda$. Set $T_0=0$, $T_k=\sum_{j=1}^kE_j$ and
$$
N_t=\max\{k\geq0:T_k\leq t\}.
$$
The <strong law of large numbers> gives $T_k/k\to1/\lambda$ <almost surely>, so the maximum is finite at every finite $t$. The process starts at zero, has right-continuous nondecreasing integer paths, and jumps by one at the arrival times.

For $k\geq1$, exactly $k$ arrivals by $t$ means $T_k\leq t<T_{k+1}$. Integrating the first $k$ exponential gaps and the survival probability of the next gap gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(N_t=k)
&=\int_{\substack{e_1,\ldots,e_k>0\\e_1+\cdots+e_k\leq t}}
\lambda^ke^{-\lambda(e_1+\cdots+e_k)}
 e^{-\lambda(t-e_1-\cdots-e_k)}\,de_1\cdots de_k\\
&=e^{-\lambda t}\lambda^k\frac{t^k}{k!}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last factor is the volume of the $k$-dimensional simplex, obtained for example by iterated integration. For $k=0$, directly $\mathbb P(N_t=0)=\mathbb P(E_1>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_t\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t).}
$$
At a fixed time $s$, conditional on the counting history, the residual time until the next arrival has rate-$\lambda$ <exponential distribution>, independently of that history, by the <memoryless property>. After that arrival all subsequent gaps are fresh <independent> exponentials. Thus the process of future counts $N_{s+u}-N_s$ has the same law as $N_u$ and is <independent> of the past. This proves stationary <independent increments>, the equivalent usual defining properties of the <Poisson process>, and gives
$$
\boxed{N_t-N_s\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s)),\qquad
\mathbb E(N_t-N_s)=\lambda(t-s)\quad(0\leq s<t).}
$$

As in the intended independent-mark construction, take the entire sequence $(X_j)$ <independent> of the <Poisson process>, not merely independent of one fixed count. Then $Y$ is a <compound Poisson process>. For deterministic $s<t$, its increment $Y_t-Y_s$ is <independent> of $\mathcal F_s=\sigma(Y_u:0\leq u\leq s)$. To justify this carefully, first condition on $N_s=k$. The past of $Y$ uses only the counting history up to $s$ and marks among $X_1,\ldots,X_k$. The future count has <independent> rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson process> increments, and future marks $X_{k+1},X_{k+2},\ldots$ are fresh with the common law. Their joint conditional law is the same for every $k$ and every past history, so the increment is independent even of this larger marked history, hence of $\mathcal F_s$. Marks equal to zero do not cause a problem: $\mathcal F_s$ may then reveal less than the full counting history.

By part (b), with Poisson parameter $\lambda(t-s)$,
$$
\mathbb E e^{-q(Y_t-Y_s)}
=\exp\{\lambda(t-s)[\phi(q)-1]\}.
$$
The proposed process is adapted to $\mathcal F_t$, nonnegative, and
$$
\mathbb E|Z_t|=e^{\lambda t[1-\phi(q)]}\mathbb E e^{-qY_t}=1<\infty.
$$
In particular, no finite <expectation> of a mark is required. Also $Z_s>0$, and
$$
\frac{Z_t}{Z_s}
=\exp\{-q(Y_t-Y_s)+\lambda(t-s)[1-\phi(q)]\}
$$
is <independent> of $\mathcal F_s$ and has mean one. Therefore the <exponential Laplace martingale of a compound Poisson process> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[Z_t\mid\mathcal F_s]
=Z_s\,\mathbb E[Z_t/Z_s]=Z_s\qquad(0\leq s<t).}
$$
This proves every condition for a <continuous-time martingale> in the requested <filtration>. When $q=0$, $\phi(0)=1$ and the same conclusion reduces to the constant <martingale> $Z_t=1$.
