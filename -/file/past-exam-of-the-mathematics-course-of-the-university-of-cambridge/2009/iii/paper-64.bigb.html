= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\mathbf x'=\mathbf x-\mathbf vt$, $t'=t$ for a constant relative <velocity> $\mathbf v$. The <Galilean transformation> gives $\nabla'=\nabla$ and $\partial_{t'}=\partial_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla$. In the <magnetic Galilean limit of the pre-Maxwell equations>, take
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B'=\mathbf B,\qquad \mathbf J'=\mathbf J,\qquad \mathbf E'=\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B.}
$$
The <Ampère's law> without <displacement current> and the solenoidal constraint are immediately unchanged. For <Faraday's law>, the constant-<velocity> vector identity is $\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf v\nabla\cdot\mathbf B-(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B$. Thus
$$
-\nabla'\times\mathbf E'=-\nabla\times\mathbf E+(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B=\partial_{t'}\mathbf B'.
$$
These are transformations of the magnetic nonrelativistic approximation. The exact charge-current transformation also contains the convective term $-\rho_e\mathbf v$ in $\mathbf J'$; ignoring it requires the quasineutral magnetic limit. They are not exact transformations of the full <Maxwell equations>.

Since $\mathbf u'=\mathbf u-\mathbf v$, the combination $\mathbf E'+\mathbf u'\times\mathbf B'$ equals $\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$. For an ideally conducting fluid the rest-frame <electric field> vanishes, so the ideal <moving-conductor Ohm law> is $\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B=0$. Substitution in <Faraday's law> gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf B=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B),\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0.}
$$
The <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation> requires nonrelativistic fluid motion, negligible <displacement current>, and negligible nonideal terms in the electric-field relation. In particular, <magnetic diffusion> must be slow compared with advection: the <magnetic Reynolds number> $\mathrm{Rm}=UL/\eta_m\gg1$, where $\eta_m=(\mu_0\sigma)^{-1}$ is the <magnetic diffusivity>. The single-fluid <moving-conductor Ohm law> must apply on the scales considered, with corrections to that relation negligible; viscosity is not itself excluded by this induction equation.

For a smooth ideal evolution, <magnetic flux freezing> states that the <magnetic flux> through every material surface is conserved and that <magnetic field lines> are carried with the fluid. Their connectivity is preserved: ideal smooth advection does not reconnect them. These implications are stated here without proof, as requested.

Put $D_t=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$. The <continuity equation> and the expanded <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation> are
$$
D_t\rho=-\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u,\qquad D_t\mathbf B=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-\mathbf B\nabla\cdot\mathbf u,
$$
so $D_t(B_i/\rho)=(B_j/\rho)\partial_ju_i$. Because $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, the induction equation permits the <magnetic vector potential> equation $\partial_t\mathbf A=\mathbf u\times\mathbf B-\nabla\varphi$, with an arbitrary scalar electric potential $\varphi$ carrying the gauge choice. Using $(\mathbf u\times\nabla\times\mathbf A)_i=u_j\partial_iA_j-u_j\partial_jA_i$ yields
$$
D_tA_i=\partial_i(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)-A_j\partial_i u_j.
$$
Contracting with $B_i/\rho$ and differentiating that factor gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_t\left(\frac{A_iB_i}{\rho}\right)
&=\frac{B_i}{\rho}\partial_i(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)-\frac{B_iA_j}{\rho}\partial_i u_j+\frac{A_iB_j}{\rho}\partial_j u_i\\
&=\frac{\mathbf B}{\rho}\cdot\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last two terms cancel by exchanging their dummy indices. Thus the requested <material derivative> is
$$
\boxed{D_t(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B/\rho)=\frac{\mathbf B}{\rho}\cdot\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi).}
$$
This is <material transport of magnetic helicity density>.

For a material volume, <mass conservation> gives $d\int_{V(t)}\rho h\,dV/dt=\int_{V(t)}\rho D_th\,dV$. Apply this with $h=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B/\rho$, and use $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and the <divergence theorem>:
$$
\frac{dH_m}{dt}=\int_{V(t)}\mathbf B\cdot\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)\,dV=\int_{\partial V(t)}(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
If the <magnetic field lines> do not penetrate the boundary, $\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n=0$, so $\boxed{dH_m/dt=0}$.

For uniqueness under a regular, single-valued <gauge transformation> $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\Lambda$,
$$
\Delta H_m=\int_V\nabla\Lambda\cdot\mathbf B\,dV=\int_{\partial V}\Lambda\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=0.
$$
Consequently this <magnetic helicity> is both conserved and gauge independent. As usual, vector potentials are understood to be globally regular potentials for the same field, rather than unrelated harmonic-circulation choices on a multiply connected restricted domain.

For $ab\ne0$, a <magnetic field line> stays at fixed $R$ and satisfies $d\phi/dz=B_\phi/(RB_z)=a/b$. It is therefore a helix with axial pitch $2\pi b/a$; the sign of $a/b$ fixes its handedness. The axial <magnetic flux> is $\pi bR_0^2$, and the <toroidal magnetic field> supplies the twist. If $a=0$ the field is untwisted; if $b=0$ it consists of azimuthal circles rather than helical lines carrying axial flux.

A convenient globally regular <magnetic vector potential> has $A_R=0$ and
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_\phi&=\begin{cases}bR/2,&R<R_0,\\bR_0^2/(2R),&R>R_0,\end{cases}\\
A_z&=\begin{cases}C-aR^2/2,&R<R_0,\\C-aR_0^2/2,&R>R_0.\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed, the cylindrical <curl> gives $B_z=R^{-1}d(RA_\phi)/dR$ and $B_\phi=-dA_z/dR$, with $B_R=0$. The tangential potential components are continuous at $R_0$, so their derivatives introduce no unwanted delta-function <magnetic field> at the tube boundary. The $C$ term is a constant axial potential.

The most general regular potential is this one plus $\nabla\Lambda(R,\phi,z)$ for an arbitrary single-valued gauge function. A putative extra $C_\phi/R$ term in the inner $A_\phi$ is excluded by regularity at the axis; the exterior $1/R$ circulation is already fixed by the enclosed <magnetic flux>. Since the potential is globally defined across the axis and exterior, any remaining difference is a <curl-free vector field>, hence a gradient. The constant $C$ can itself be absorbed into a gauge function proportional to $z$.

For the displayed gauge family, inside the tube
$$
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=\frac{bR}{2}(aR)+b\left(C-\frac{aR^2}{2}\right)=bC,
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{H_m}{L}=\int_0^{R_0}2\pi R\,bC\,dR=\pi bCR_0^2.}
$$
For example, requiring $A_z=0$ outside sets $C=aR_0^2/2$ and gives $\boxed{H_m/L=\pi abR_0^4/2}$. The equally regular choice $C=0$ gives zero. More generally, for a length-$L$ segment the <gauge transformation> contributes
$$
\Delta H_m= b\int_{R<R_0}[\Lambda(R,\phi,L)-\Lambda(R,\phi,0)]\,dS.
$$
In particular, $\Lambda=\Delta C\,z$ shifts $H_m/L$ by $\pi bR_0^2\Delta C$. This <gauge dependence of helicity in an open magnetic flux tube> is possible because field lines penetrate the end caps when $b\ne0$. There is no contradiction with part (b), whose no-penetration condition fails at those caps. A gauge or end-cap convention is needed to give a unique value per unit length. For $b=0$ the ordinary helicity is zero and this end-cap ambiguity disappears.

For the <isothermal equation of state>, $p=c_s^2\rho$, with constant <isothermal sound speed> $c_s$. A nonzero steady plane-parallel flow has constant mass flux $\rho u=j$. Choose the positive flow direction for convenience. The <continuity equation> then gives $\rho'/\rho=-u'/u$, and the steady <Euler momentum equation> becomes
$$
uu'=-c_s^2\frac{\rho'}{\rho}-\Phi'\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(u-\frac{c_s^2}{u}\right)u'=-\Phi'.
$$
Integrating and absorbing the reference <velocity> into the constant yields, for the <Mach number> $\mathcal M=u/c_s$,
$$
\boxed{\tfrac12\mathcal M^2-\ln\mathcal M+\Phi/c_s^2=C.}
$$
For the opposite flow direction use $\mathcal M=|u|/c_s$; the same relation holds.

A smooth <sonic point> has $u=c_s$, so its momentum equation requires $\Phi'=0$. Moreover $f(\mathcal M)=\mathcal M^2/2-\ln\mathcal M$ satisfies $f'(1)=0$, $f''(1)=2$ and has its minimum $f(1)=1/2$. Thus, if the flow reaches $\mathcal M=1$ at $x_*$,
$$
f(\mathcal M)-\tfrac12=\frac{\Phi(x_*)-\Phi(x)}{c_s^2}\geq0.
$$
A sonic crossing is therefore possible at a maximum of the potential, with
$$
\boxed{C=\tfrac12+\Phi_{\max}/c_s^2.}
$$
At a nondegenerate maximum, the expansions give $(\mathcal M-1)^2=-\Phi''(x_*)(x-x_*)^2/(2c_s^2)+\cdots$. Choosing either smooth sign gives a crossing slope $\mathcal M'(x_*)=\pm\sqrt{-\Phi''(x_*)/(2c_s^2)}$, joining a <subsonic flow> to a <supersonic flow>, or the reverse. This establishes the <plane-parallel isothermal sonic transition at a potential maximum>. For a globally defined crossing, the same maximum must also be high enough for the Bernoulli relation to remain real over the intended domain.

Decompose $\mathbf u=\mathbf u_p+u_\phi\mathbf e_\phi$ and $\mathbf B=\mathbf B_p+B_\phi\mathbf e_\phi$. In a steady <axisymmetric vector field>, $\nabla\times\mathbf E=0$ and $\mathbf E=-\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$. The radial and vertical components of its <curl> are
$$
-\partial_zE_\phi=0,\qquad R^{-1}\partial_R(RE_\phi)=0.
$$
Therefore $RE_\phi=C$ is a constant. Regularity at the symmetry axis sets $C=0$; equivalently, one imposes no toroidal loop <electromotive force>, $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=2\pi C=0$. Under this usual condition,
$$
0=E_\phi=-(\mathbf u_p\times\mathbf B_p)_\phi,
$$
which implies
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u_p=\alpha\mathbf B_p}
$$
where $\mathbf B_p\ne0$. This is the <regular-axis alignment of steady poloidal ideal flow>. It allows nonzero toroidal components as well.

The axis or zero-loop-voltage condition is necessary for the unqualified conclusion. On an annular domain, $\mathbf B=(B_0/R)\mathbf e_R$ and $\mathbf u=U_0\mathbf e_z$ give $\mathbf E=-(U_0B_0/R)\mathbf e_\phi$ with zero curl, yet the poloidal vectors are perpendicular. This can satisfy the remaining steady equations as well: choose constant <mass density> $\rho_0$, $\Phi=\pi G\rho_0R^2$ and $p=p_0-\pi G\rho_0^2R^2>0$ on the annulus. The field has zero <curl>, radial <pressure> balances gravity, the flow advects no equilibrium quantity, and $\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho_0$. It carries a nonzero loop voltage. Steady axisymmetry alone on an annulus therefore does not exclude such cross-field flow.

Use the aligned poloidal flow from part (i), and define the <magnetohydrodynamic mass loading> $\eta=\rho\alpha$, so $\rho\mathbf u_p=\eta\mathbf B_p$. Axisymmetric <mass conservation> and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B_p=0$ give $\mathbf B_p\cdot\nabla\eta=0$. Introduce the <poloidal magnetic flux function> $\Psi$ by $\mathbf B_p=R^{-1}\nabla\Psi\times\mathbf e_\phi$, and write $u_\phi=R\Omega$. Then
$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf B=(u_\phi-\alpha B_\phi)\mathbf e_\phi\times\mathbf B_p=\left(\Omega-\frac{\eta B_\phi}{\rho R}\right)\nabla\Psi.
$$
The steady induction equation says the curl of this expression is zero. Hence $\nabla\Omega_F\times\nabla\Psi=0$, where
$$
\Omega_F=\Omega-\frac{\eta B_\phi}{\rho R};
$$
the <field-line angular velocity> $\Omega_F$ is constant on each connected magnetic surface and along its field lines.

To control the difference between matter rotation and field-line rotation, use the azimuthal component of the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation>. The inertial and magnetic terms are respectively $\rho R^{-1}\mathbf u_p\cdot\nabla(Ru_\phi)$ and $(\mu_0R)^{-1}\mathbf B_p\cdot\nabla(RB_\phi)$; <pressure> and gravity have no azimuthal derivative. Multiplying by $R$ and using constant mass loading gives
$$
\mathbf B_p\cdot\nabla\left(R^2\Omega-\frac{RB_\phi}{\mu_0\eta}\right)=0.
$$
Thus the <magnetohydrodynamic angular-momentum invariant> $L=R^2\Omega-RB_\phi/(\mu_0\eta)$ is constant along the line when $\eta\ne0$. Let $v_{Ap}=|\mathbf B_p|/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}$ and define $M_{Ap}^2=|\mathbf u_p|^2/v_{Ap}^2=\mu_0\eta^2/\rho$. Eliminating $B_\phi$ between the two invariants gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\frac{\Omega_F-M_{Ap}^2L/R^2}{1-M_{Ap}^2},\qquad \Omega-\Omega_F=\frac{M_{Ap}^2}{1-M_{Ap}^2}\left(\Omega_F-\frac{L}{R^2}\right).}
$$
For a highly sub-Alfvénic poloidal flow, $M_{Ap}^2\ll1$, this is $\Omega\simeq\Omega_F$ along a nonsingular field-line segment. This proves <sub-Alfvénic isorotation in a steady axisymmetric wind>, with the correction explicitly displayed. If the poloidal flow is exactly zero, the induction equation directly gives $\mathbf B_p\cdot\nabla\Omega=0$, namely <Ferraro's law of isorotation>. The approximate claim presumes regular invariants: small $M_{Ap}$ alone is not a uniform relative bound if $L/R^2$ is allowed to become arbitrarily large.

Matter corotating with the footpoint has fixed <angular velocity> $\Omega_0=(GM/R_0^3)^{1/2}$. Its <effective potential> in that rotating frame is the sum of <Newtonian gravitational potential> and centrifugal potential,
$$
\Phi_{\rm eff}(R,z)=-\frac{GM}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}-\frac12\Omega_0^2R^2.
$$
Along an outward-inclined straight <magnetic field line>, write $R=R_0+s\sin i$ and $z=s\cos i$, where $s$ is distance from the footpoint. The exact potential along it is
$$
\Phi_{\rm eff}(s)=-\frac{GM}{\sqrt{R_0^2+2R_0s\sin i+s^2}}-\frac{GM}{2R_0^3}(R_0+s\sin i)^2.
$$
Its first <derivative> at $s=0$ is zero by the <Keplerian rotation> balance. At the footpoint the meridional <Hessian> is $\operatorname{diag}(-3\Omega_0^2,\Omega_0^2)$ with zero mixed derivative. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm eff}(s)=-\frac{3GM}{2R_0}+\frac12\Omega_0^2(1-4\sin^2i)s^2+O(\Omega_0^2s^3/R_0).}
$$
When $0\leq i<30^\circ$, the potential initially rises and a cold parcel must overcome a barrier. When $i>30^\circ$, its quadratic curvature is negative, so the footpoint is a local maximum along the field and a small outward <displacement> is accelerated further outward:
$$
\boxed{-\frac{d\Phi_{\rm eff}}{ds}=\Omega_0^2(4\sin^2i-1)s+O(\Omega_0^2s^2/R_0)>0\quad(s>0\text{ small},\ i>30^\circ).}
$$
This is the <thirty-degree magnetocentrifugal launching criterion>. It describes the lack of a local cold-launch barrier, not a nonzero force on a parcel exactly at rest at $s=0$. At exactly $30^\circ$ the quadratic term vanishes; for the specified straight line the next term is $-7\Omega_0^2s^3/(16R_0)$, as in <marginal straight-line launch at thirty degrees>. Global wind escape additionally depends on conditions farther along the line.

Work in a gas region with $\rho,p>0$ and physical adiabatic exponent $\gamma>1$. With $\xi=x/t$, each time derivative is $-\xi/t$ times differentiation in $\xi$, and each spatial derivative is $1/t$ times it. The <continuity equation>, <Euler momentum equation> and the <entropy advection equation> for pressure reduce to
$$
(u-\xi)\rho'+\rho u'=0,\qquad (u-\xi)u'+p'/\rho=0,\qquad (u-\xi)p'+\gamma p u'=0.
$$
Their coefficient determinant is $(u-\xi)[(u-\xi)^2-c^2]$, where the <adiabatic sound speed> is $c=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$. If the determinant is nonzero, all derivatives vanish and the solution is uniform.

The apparent third characteristic $u=\xi$ does not give a smooth fan: on such an interval $u'=1$, whereas continuity requires $\rho u'=0$, impossible for $\rho>0$. It cannot support an isolated smooth <entropy> variation either. Subtracting $\gamma$ times the logarithmic density equation from the logarithmic <pressure> equation gives $(u-\xi)(\ln(p/\rho^\gamma))'=0$. A nonzero continuous <entropy> derivative would force $u=\xi$ on an interval and give the same contradiction. Thus the <specific entropy> is constant throughout each smooth branch.

Every nonconstant branch therefore lies in one acoustic characteristic family. Let $\sigma=\pm1$ denote it, with $\xi=u+\sigma c$, so $u-\xi=-\sigma c$. The constant <entropy> means $p=K\rho^\gamma$, $K>0$, and $c'/c=(\gamma-1)\rho'/(2\rho)$. Continuity gives $u'=\sigma c\rho'/\rho=2\sigma c'/(\gamma-1)$. Differentiating $\xi=u+\sigma c$ then proves
$$
\boxed{u'=\frac2{\gamma+1},\qquad c'=\sigma\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1},\qquad s=s_0+c_v\ln K=\text{constant}.}
$$
In particular, $u-2\sigma c/(\gamma-1)=J$ is a constant <Riemann invariant>. All nonconstant smooth local solutions have the explicit form
$$
\boxed{u(\xi)=\frac{2\xi+(\gamma-1)J}{\gamma+1},\quad c(\xi)=\frac{\sigma(\gamma-1)(\xi-J)}{\gamma+1},\quad\rho=\left(\frac{c^2}{\gamma K}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)},\quad p=K\rho^\gamma,}
$$
on an interval where $c>0$. Along the left-going family ($\sigma=-1$) sound speed decreases with $\xi$; along the right-going family ($\sigma=+1$) it increases. These are the two <self-similar ideal-gas rarefaction> branches. Together with arbitrary uniform positive states they exhaust the smooth local solutions. A global solution can join them piecewise at fan edges, where derivatives generally jump, and can also have discontinuities; those do not constitute additional smooth branches.

The initial-value problem is a <Riemann problem>. Both the one-dimensional <Euler equations> and the two constant initial states are unchanged under the simultaneous scaling $(x,t)\mapsto(\lambda x,\lambda t)$ for $\lambda>0$. There is no imposed length or time scale. Rescaling a physically admissible <entropy solution> therefore gives another solution with exactly the same initial data. Uniqueness of the physical Riemann solution makes the two coincide, so
$$
\boxed{(\rho,u,p)(x,t)=(\rho,u,p)(x/t).}
$$
The smooth pieces are consequently those in part (a), while shocks and contacts lie on rays $x=st$. Across shocks one uses the conservative mass, momentum and total-<energy> equations and the <entropy> admissibility condition, rather than extending the smooth <entropy>-advection equation through the discontinuity. Scale invariance by itself, without selecting the unique admissible solution, would not justify the uniqueness step.

Let $c_L=(\gamma p_L/\rho_L)^{1/2}$ and $c_R=(\gamma p_R/\rho_R)^{1/2}$, with positive pressures and densities. A nontrivial single <rarefaction wave> has constant <specific entropy> and constant <Riemann invariant>, so for one family label $\sigma=\pm1$ the necessary matching conditions are
$$
\boxed{\frac{p_L}{\rho_L^\gamma}=\frac{p_R}{\rho_R^\gamma}=K,\qquad u_R-u_L=\frac{2\sigma(c_R-c_L)}{\gamma-1},\qquad \sigma(c_R-c_L)>0.}
$$
The final inequality says that the fan opens rather than compresses. Its edge speeds are $\xi_L=u_L+\sigma c_L$ and $\xi_R=u_R+\sigma c_R$, and their difference is $\sigma(c_R-c_L)(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)>0$. The profiles in part (a) then interpolate the two end states and supply a sufficient construction. For the left family, $c_R<c_L$, hence $\rho_R<\rho_L$ and $p_R<p_L$; for the right family all three inequalities reverse. In both cases $u_R>u_L$. These are the <single-rarefaction matching conditions for an ideal-gas Riemann problem>.

For a concrete nontrivial example, take $\gamma=5/3$ and
$$
(\rho_L,p_L,u_L)=(1,3/5,0),\qquad (\rho_R,p_R,u_R)=(1/8,3/160,3/2).
$$
The <adiabatic sound speeds> are $c_L=1$ and $c_R=1/2$, with common $K=3/5$ and left-family invariant $u+3c=3$. The fan edges are $\xi_L=-1$ and $\xi_R=1$. The complete <self-similar solution> is uniform at these stated left and right values outside the fan, while inside $-1<\xi<1$,
$$
\boxed{u=\frac34(\xi+1),\qquad c=\frac{3-\xi}{4},\qquad \rho=c^3,\qquad p=\frac35c^5.}
$$
It matches both states continuously, has $u'=3/4=2/(\gamma+1)$, and has constant <specific entropy>. For fixed $x\ne0$, its $t\downarrow0$ limit is the required initial step.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A single <rarefaction wave> enforces two matching equalities: equal <specific entropy> and one common <Riemann invariant>. The inequality $u_R>u_L$ does not enforce either. If the entropies differ, one smooth isentropic fan cannot connect them; even if they agree, arbitrary end velocities and pressures need not satisfy either acoustic-family invariant.

The general positive-gas <Riemann problem> has a left acoustic wave, a middle <contact discontinuity>, and a right acoustic wave. Across the contact the two intermediate states have the same <velocity> $u_*$ and <pressure> $p_*$, but can have different <mass densities> and entropies. Each acoustic wave is a <rarefaction wave> or a <shock wave>, depending on the required intermediate <pressure> relative to its adjacent initial state. Shocks satisfy the conservative jump conditions and increase <entropy>. A sufficiently large <pressure> contrast can produce a shock on one side even when the initial velocities obey $u_R>u_L$.

For sufficiently rapid separation, the two rarefactions instead exhaust the gas and create vacuum between them. Their vacuum-edge velocities are $u_L+2c_L/(\gamma-1)$ and $u_R-2c_R/(\gamma-1)$, so a finite vacuum gap occurs when
$$
\boxed{u_R-u_L>\frac{2(c_L+c_R)}{\gamma-1}.}
$$
Equality is the limiting case in which the vacuum edges meet. Thus the additional features can be two acoustic waves, a contact carrying an <entropy> jump, shocks, or a vacuum region, rather than one fan joining the two original uniform states.

= Solution

The equilibrium quantities $\rho,p,\mathbf B,\Phi$ are real, and the gravitational <Hessian> $\Phi_{,ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ is symmetric. The crucial <tensor> property is
$$
\boxed{V_{ijkl}=V_{klij}.}
$$
This follows directly from the displayed expression for $V$: the products $\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}$ and $\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}$ are unchanged by interchanging the pairs $(i,j)$ and $(k,l)$; $B_jB_l\delta_{ik}$ is also unchanged; and the two terms involving $B_iB_j\delta_{kl}$ and $B_kB_l\delta_{ij}$ exchange with each other. All coefficients are real.

Multiply $F\boldsymbol\xi$ by $\rho\boldsymbol\eta^*$ and integrate by parts over all space. For admissible <displacement> fields, the boundary contribution at infinity vanishes: the fluid <pressure> and density are confined to the body, and the magnetic stress decays in the surrounding conducting medium. The conducting exterior is included in the integration. For a piecewise description, the usual <displacement> and traction matching conditions cancel the two opposite interface contributions; alternatively, take a smooth transition and then its limit. Thus
$$
\langle\boldsymbol\eta,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=-\int\rho\eta_i^*\Phi_{,ij}\xi_j\,dV-\int(\partial_j\eta_i^*)V_{ijkl}(\partial_l\xi_k)\,dV.
$$
The first term is a Hermitian pairing because $\Phi_{,ij}=\Phi_{,ji}$, and the second is Hermitian because $V_{ijkl}=V_{klij}$. Taking the complex conjugate of the same expression with $\boldsymbol\eta$ and $\boldsymbol\xi$ interchanged and relabelling $(i,j)\leftrightarrow(k,l)$ reproduces it. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle\boldsymbol\eta,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=\langle F\boldsymbol\eta,\boldsymbol\xi\rangle.}
$$
This establishes the required <self-adjoint operator> identity in the mass-weighted <inner product>, on the physical <displacement> domain with the stated decay and interface conditions. The negligible-density exterior can be treated as a small-positive-density conducting limit when defining this weighted pairing; its magnetic contribution is retained even as its inertia becomes negligible.

= Solution

Self-adjointness gives $\langle\boldsymbol\xi,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=\langle F\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol\xi,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle^*$, so
$$
\boxed{W[\boldsymbol\xi]\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The integrated expression from part (a) also writes it explicitly as
$$
W[\boldsymbol\xi]=\frac12\int\left[\rho\xi_i^*\Phi_{,ij}\xi_j+(\partial_j\xi_i^*)V_{ijkl}(\partial_l\xi_k)\right]dV.
$$
This is the quadratic change in <potential energy> associated with the perturbation, including <pressure>, magnetic and imposed-gravity restoring effects. Its sign need not be positive term by term.

The perturbation <kinetic energy> is $T=\tfrac12\langle\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\rangle$, and the total quadratic perturbation <energy> is
$$
\boxed{E=T+W=\tfrac12\langle\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\rangle-\tfrac12\langle\boldsymbol\xi,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle.}
$$
Since the equilibrium and $F$ are time independent, differentiating and using the <self-adjoint operator> identity gives
$$
\frac{dE}{dt}=\operatorname{Re}\langle\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}\rangle-\operatorname{Re}\langle\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{dE/dt=0}$ along the linearized motion. This conserved kinetic-plus-restoring <energy> is the basis of the <magnetohydrodynamic energy principle>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

First suppose $W[\boldsymbol\xi]>0$ for every nonzero admissible <displacement>. The conserved <energy> from part (b) then bounds both $T$ and $W$ by $E$. In particular,
$$
\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}(t)\|_\rho^2\leq2E,\qquad 2W[\boldsymbol\xi(t)]\leq2E,
$$
where $\|\boldsymbol\xi\|_\rho^2=\langle\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol\xi\rangle$. The positive quadratic form $\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|_\rho^2+2W[\boldsymbol\xi]$ defines the perturbation <energy> norm and is conserved. Small perturbations in this norm remain small, proving energetic linear stability. Also $\|\boldsymbol\xi(t)\|_\rho\leq\|\boldsymbol\xi(0)\|_\rho+\sqrt{2E}\,t$, excluding exponential <displacement> growth. For any admissible <normal mode> $\boldsymbol\xi=\boldsymbol\zeta e^{-i\omega t}$, multiplication of $-\omega^2\boldsymbol\zeta=F\boldsymbol\zeta$ by $\boldsymbol\zeta^*$ gives directly
$$
\omega^2=\frac{2W[\boldsymbol\zeta]}{\|\boldsymbol\zeta\|_\rho^2}>0,
$$
so all such frequencies are real. No variational principle has been assumed.

To prove instability, suppose some admissible $\boldsymbol\xi_0$ has $W_0=W[\boldsymbol\xi_0]<0$, and put $N_0=\|\boldsymbol\xi_0\|_\rho^2>0$. Choose the initial <velocity>
$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}(0)=\alpha\boldsymbol\xi_0,\qquad\alpha=\sqrt{-2W_0/N_0}>0.
$$
Its <kinetic energy> exactly cancels $W_0$, so $E=0$ for the entire subsequent motion. Write $N(t)=\|\boldsymbol\xi(t)\|_\rho^2$. Since $F$ is self-adjoint,
$$
N'=2\operatorname{Re}\langle\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\rangle,\qquad N''=2\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|_\rho^2+2\langle\boldsymbol\xi,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle.
$$
Zero <energy> gives $\langle\boldsymbol\xi,F\boldsymbol\xi\rangle=\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|_\rho^2$, and hence $N''=4\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|_\rho^2\geq0$. Initially $N'(0)=2\alpha N_0>0$, so $N$ stays positive and its logarithm is defined. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> now gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\ln N)''&=\frac{NN''-(N')^2}{N^2}\\
&=\frac{4\left[N\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|_\rho^2-(\operatorname{Re}\langle\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\rangle)^2\right]}{N^2}\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore $(\ln N)'(t)\geq(\ln N)'(0)=2\alpha$, and integration proves
$$
\boxed{N(t)\geq N_0e^{2\alpha t},\qquad\|\boldsymbol\xi(t)\|_\rho\geq\|\boldsymbol\xi_0\|_\rho e^{\alpha t}.}
$$
This is an exponentially growing perturbation. Scaling the initial <displacement> and <velocity> down leaves $\alpha$ unchanged, so arbitrarily small initial perturbations display the same linear growth mechanism. It proves that a <negative-energy direction gives exponential growth in a self-adjoint wave equation>, without assuming a discrete spectrum or quoting a trial-function variational theorem.

**Positive $W$ on nonzero displacements gives energetic stability; a negative direction gives instability.** For mathematical precision, a uniform bound on the mass-weighted <displacement> norm follows if $W\geq\tfrac12\omega_{\min}^2\|\boldsymbol\xi\|_\rho^2$ for some $\omega_{\min}>0$, giving $\|\boldsymbol\xi\|_\rho^2\leq2E/\omega_{\min}^2$. Strict positivity alone in an infinite-dimensional space need not supply such a uniform <spectral gap>; the standard <magnetohydrodynamic energy principle> asserts energetic stability and absence of growing modes. Cases with zero directions are marginal and require separate consideration.
