= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the simple-root form of <Hensel lemma>. Let $R$ be a <complete discrete valuation ring>, let $\pi$ be a <uniformizer>, and let $F\in R[T]$. If $\bar a\in R/(\pi)$ is a root of $\bar F$ with $\bar F'(\bar a)\ne0$, then **there is exactly one root $a\in R$ reducing to $\bar a$.**

Choose a lift $a_1$ of $\bar a$. Suppose $F(a_r)\in\pi^rR$. To construct a lift satisfying the next congruence, write $a_{r+1}=a_r+\pi^r t_r$. The <polynomial> expansion gives
$$
F(a_r+\pi^r t_r)\equiv F(a_r)+\pi^r t_rF'(a_r)\pmod{\pi^{r+1}}.
$$
Since $F'(a_r)$ is a <unit>, its residue is invertible in the <residue field>. There is a unique residue class of $t_r$ making this zero modulo $\pi^{r+1}$. Thus $a_{r+1}\equiv a_r\pmod{\pi^r}$ and $F(a_{r+1})\equiv0\pmod{\pi^{r+1}}$. This produces a <Cauchy sequence> in the <complete discrete valuation ring>. Its limit $a$ satisfies $F(a)=0$ by <continuity> of <polynomial> evaluation, and $a\equiv a_1\pmod\pi$.

For uniqueness, suppose $a,b$ are two such roots. Polynomial division of their difference gives
$$
F(b)-F(a)=(b-a)\bigl(F'(a)+(b-a)H(a,b)\bigr),\qquad H\in R[S,T].
$$
Because $b-a\in\pi R$, the second factor is a <unit>. Hence $b-a=0$. The simple-root hypothesis is used both for constructing successive lifts and for this uniqueness argument.

First reduce the <polynomial> modulo seven. At residues $0,1,\ldots,6$, its values are respectively $4,2,6,1,0,2,6$. Its only residue root is therefore $4$, and $f'(4)=45\equiv3\pmod7$ is a <unit>. By <Hensel lemma>, this residue root lifts uniquely to the <7-adic integers>; any other root would have to reduce to the same residue root. Thus **there is exactly one root in $\mathbb Z_7$, congruent to $4$ modulo seven.**

Modulo five the values at $0,1,\ldots,4$ are $4,2,1,2,1$, so there is no residue root and hence **no root in $\mathbb Z_5$.** Modulo three the only possible residue root is $-1$. But writing $x=-1+3t$ gives
$$
f(-1+3t)=6-27t^2+27t^3\equiv6\pmod9.
$$
Thus that residue root cannot lift even modulo nine, and **there is no root in $\mathbb Z_3$.** A repeated residue root alone would not establish this obstruction; the second congruence is needed.

Modulo two both residues are roots. The even residue is simple because $f'(0)=-3$ is a <2-adic unit>, so <Hensel lemma> gives exactly one even root. For any odd <2-adic integer>, $x^3\equiv x\pmod4$, whence $f(x)\equiv-2x\equiv2\pmod4$. No odd root can exist. Consequently the full answer is
$$
\boxed{\#\{x\in\mathbb Z_2:f(x)=0\}=1.}
$$

The two cosets of $2\mathbb Z_2$ are <clopen sets> in the <2-adic integers>. The function is constant on each, so it is a <locally constant function> and therefore <continuous>. Its values on the nonnegative integers are $f(j)=(-1)^j$.

The <Mahler coefficient> at index $n$ is the nth <forward difference operator> applied at zero. Here
$$
a_n=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^{n-j}\binom nj(-1)^j=(-1)^n\sum_{j=0}^n\binom nj=(-2)^n.
$$
Thus the <Mahler expansion of the parity function> is
$$
\boxed{f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-2)^n\binom xn,\qquad x\in\mathbb Z_2.}
$$
To verify the expansion directly, each <binomial polynomial> $\binom xn$ takes values in $\mathbb Z_2$: it does so at nonnegative integers, which form a <dense subset>, and polynomial evaluation is <continuous> into $\mathbb Q_2$. Hence the nth summand has <2-adic absolute value> at most $2^{-n}$ throughout the domain. The <ultrametric inequality> proves <uniform convergence>, and the sum is <continuous>. At each nonnegative integer $m$, the series becomes the finite <binomial theorem> identity $(1-2)^m=(-1)^m$. Equality on a <dense subset> and <continuity> prove equality everywhere. Finally, the coefficients in any such expansion are determined successively by its values at $0,1,2,\ldots$, since $\binom mn=0$ for $n>m$ and $\binom mm=1$. This also verifies uniqueness without needing the general <Mahler theorem>.

All the <local fields> here carry normalized <discrete valuations>. Write $\mathcal O_F$ for the <valuation ring>, $\mathfrak m_F$ for its <maximal ideal>, and $k_F$ for its <residue field>. We use the basic degree identity $[B:A]=e(B/A)f(B/A)$ for finite extensions of non-Archimedean <local fields>, with $e$ the <ramification index> and $f$ the <residue degree>.

First construct the <maximal unramified subextension of a local field extension> inside an arbitrary finite extension $L/K$. Choose a <primitive element of a field extension> $\bar z$ for the finite <residue field> extension $k_L/k_K$. Let $\bar h$ be its monic irreducible <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element>, of degree $f$, and lift its coefficients to obtain a monic $h\in\mathcal O_K[T]$ of the same degree. Finite <finite fields> are <perfect fields>, so $\bar h'(\bar z)\ne0$. By <Hensel lemma> applied in $L$, there is $z\in\mathcal O_L$ reducing to $\bar z$ with $h(z)=0$. For $U=K(z)$ we have
$$
f=[k_L:k_K]\leq[k_U:k_K]\leq[U:K]\leq\deg h=f.
$$
Every inequality is therefore an equality. In particular $U/K$ is <unramified>, $k_U=k_L$, and $h$ is the <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $z$.

If $L/K$ is <unramified>, then $[L:K]=f$, so this construction gives $U=L$. Taking $x=z$ supplies the required generator, and $h'(x)$ is a <unit>. Conversely suppose $L=K(x)$ with $x$ integral and its monic <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $g$ satisfying $g'(x)\notin\mathfrak m_L$. Its coefficients lie in $\mathcal O_K$, since all conjugates of an integral element are integral and $K$ is complete. In the constructed <unramified extension> $U$ the element $\bar x\in k_U=k_L$ is a simple root of $\bar g$. <Hensel lemma> in $U$ produces a root $x'\in U$ with residue $\bar x$. In $L$ both $x$ and $x'$ lift this same simple residue root, so Hensel uniqueness gives $x=x'$. Thus $L\subseteq U\subseteq L$, proving
$$
\boxed{L/K\text{ is unramified}\iff L=K(x),\ x\in\mathcal O_L,\ g'(x)\in\mathcal O_L^\times.}
$$

For <lifting residue-field embeddings of unramified extensions>, use the generator $z$ and polynomial $h$ just constructed when $L/K$ is <unramified>. Given a $k_K$-<field homomorphism> $\iota:k_L\to k_M$, its value $\iota(\bar z)$ is a simple root of $\bar h$. <Hensel lemma> in $M$ produces the unique root $z'\in\mathcal O_M$ with that residue. The assignment $z\mapsto z'$ defines a $K$-<field homomorphism> $K[T]/(h)\to M$; it induces $\iota$ because $\bar z$ generates $k_L$. Conversely any such <field homomorphism> preserves the unique extended <valuation>, hence induces a residue <field homomorphism>, and its image of $z$ is uniquely forced by Hensel uniqueness. This proves the asserted bijection. The same proof only requires that $M$ be a non-Archimedean <local field> containing $K$ with its compatible valuation; its finiteness over $K$ is not essential.

Now set $M=L$. The finite <residue field> extension has exactly $f=[L:K]$ automorphisms, cyclically generated by $a\mapsto a^{|k_K|}$. Their unique lifts give $[L:K]$ automorphisms of $L$. Consequently $L/K$ is a <Galois extension>, and reduction identifies its <Galois group> with that cyclic residue <Galois group>. Its generator is the <arithmetic Frobenius>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\simeq\operatorname{Gal}(k_L/k_K)\simeq\mathbb Z/f\mathbb Z.}
$$

For the compositum, choose a <uniformizer> $\pi_M$ of the <totally ramified extension> $M/K$. The <Eisenstein generator of a totally ramified extension> characterization, proved in the next solution, says that $M=K(\pi_M)$ and its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> is Eisenstein over $\mathcal O_K$. A <uniformizer> of $K$ remains a <uniformizer> in the <unramified extension> $L$. All its Eisenstein coefficient conditions therefore remain true over $\mathcal O_L$. Thus the same <Eisenstein polynomial> is irreducible over $L$ and generates $LM=L(\pi_M)$ as a <totally ramified extension>. This proves **$LM/L$ is totally ramified, of degree $[M:K]$.**

Here is an explicit example showing that the converse decomposition of an arbitrary extension can fail. Take $K=\mathbb Q_3$ and $L=K(i)$ with $i^2=-1$. Since $T^2+1$ is irreducible and separable modulo three, the degree and residue-degree argument above shows that $L/K$ is <unramified> quadratic. Put
$$
\alpha^2=3(1+i),\qquad N=L(\alpha).
$$
The element $1+i$ is a <unit> in $\mathcal O_L$, so $T^2-3(1+i)$ is Eisenstein over $L$. Hence $[N:L]=2$, $e(N/K)=2$, and $f(N/K)=2$. Moreover $i=\alpha^2/3-1$ gives $N=K(\alpha)$, with
$$
\alpha^4-6\alpha^2+18=0.
$$
We verify its <Galois group>, since that is what rules out a totally ramified complement. By <Hensel lemma> there is $r\in K$ with $r^2=-2$: the residue root $1$ of $T^2+2$ is simple modulo three. Set $\beta=(1+i)/r$. Then
$$
\beta^2=-i,\qquad \beta\bar\beta=-1.
$$
The assignments $\sigma(i)=-i$ and $\sigma(\alpha)=\beta\alpha$ respect the defining relation, because $(\beta\alpha)^2=-3i(1+i)=3(1-i)$. They therefore define a $K$-automorphism of $N$. Its square satisfies $\sigma^2(i)=i$ and $\sigma^2(\alpha)=\bar\beta\beta\alpha=-\alpha$, so $\sigma$ has order four. There are thus four automorphisms of the degree-four extension, and
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(N/K)=\langle\sigma\rangle\simeq C_4.
$$
This is the <cyclic quartic local extension without a totally ramified complement>. Its unique quadratic subfield is $L$, fixed by $\sigma^2$, and is unramified. If $N=L'M'$ with $L'/K$ unramified and $M'/K$ totally ramified, then $[L':K]\leq f(N/K)=2$ and $[M':K]\leq e(N/K)=2$. To obtain the full degree four both would have degree two, forcing $M'$ to be a totally ramified quadratic subfield. There is no such subfield. Thus **$N/\mathbb Q_3$ is the required counterexample.**

Let $d=[L:K]$, with normalized <discrete valuations> $v_K,v_L$. Suppose first that $L/K$ is <totally ramified>, so $e(L/K)=d$. Choose a <uniformizer> $x$ of $L$. The elements $1,x,\ldots,x^{d-1}$ are linearly independent over $K$: the nonzero terms $c_jx^j$ in any proposed relation have <valuations> $d\,v_K(c_j)+j$, distinct modulo $d$, and a sum with a unique least valuation cannot vanish by the <ultrametric inequality>. Hence $[K(x):K]\geq d$, proving $L=K(x)$.

Extend $v_K$ to an <algebraic closure> with values in $\mathbb Q$. Every conjugate of $x$ has valuation $1/d$, by uniqueness of the extended <valuation> on finite extensions of the complete field $K$. In its monic <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $g(T)=T^d+a_{d-1}T^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0$, the coefficient $a_{d-j}$ is a sum of products of $j$ conjugates, counted with multiplicity. Thus $v_K(a_{d-j})\geq j/d>0$ for $j\geq1$, so every nonleading coefficient is in $\mathfrak m_K$. The constant coefficient is the product of all $d$ conjugates, so has valuation exactly one. This proves that $g$ is an <Eisenstein polynomial>. This argument also works for inseparable finite extensions, since the conjugates can be counted with their multiplicities.

Conversely let $g$ be an <Eisenstein polynomial> of degree $d$, with root $x$ and $L=K(x)$. The <Eisenstein criterion> proves irreducibility. For completeness, monic factors would have integral coefficients; their reductions would be positive-degree powers of $T$, so both constant coefficients would be divisible by the <uniformizer> of $K$, contradicting $v_K(g(0))=1$. To determine the root valuation, put $t=v_K(x)$ in the extended valuation. If $t<0$, the leading term is the unique term of least valuation; if $t=0$, the leading term again has uniquely least valuation zero. Both are impossible. If $t>0$, every nonconstant lower term has valuation at least $1+jt>1$. Cancellation in $g(x)=0$ therefore requires the leading term and constant term to have equal valuation:
$$
dt=1.
$$
Since $t=v_L(x)/e(L/K)$ and $v_L(x)$ is a positive integer, $e(L/K)=d\,v_L(x)\geq d$. The opposite inequality follows from $[L:K]=e(L/K)f(L/K)=d$. Hence $e=d$, $f=1$, and $v_L(x)=1$. We have proved both the equivalence and the uniformizer assertion:
$$
\boxed{L/K\text{ is totally ramified}\iff L=K(x)\text{ with }g_x\text{ Eisenstein};\quad v_L(x)=1.}
$$

Now take $q=p^a$ with $a\geq1$ and $\zeta=\zeta_{p^a}$. The translated <cyclotomic polynomial> is
$$
\Phi_{p^a}(1+T)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(1+T)^{jp^{a-1}}.
$$
In characteristic $p$, putting $U=T^{p^{a-1}}$ reduces this to $\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(1+U)^j=U^{p-1}=T^{p^{a-1}(p-1)}$. Its constant coefficient over the integers is $p$. It is therefore Eisenstein at $p$, of degree $d=p^{a-1}(p-1)$. Consequently **$\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_{p^a})/\mathbb Q_p$ is totally ramified of degree $d$, with uniformizer $\zeta_{p^a}-1$.** If $q=1$, the field is simply $\mathbb Q_p$, a trivial totally ramified extension.

For a general positive integer $n=p^a m$ with $p\nmid m$, the field generated by the prime-to-$p$ <root of unity> $\zeta_m$ is unramified. Indeed $T^m-1$ has distinct roots modulo $p$. In a finite unramified field whose <residue field> contains those roots, <Hensel lemma> lifts all of them uniquely. Reduction preserves their orders: a prime-to-$p$ root reducing to one must itself be one, by Hensel uniqueness at the root one of $T^m-1$. The <arithmetic Frobenius> therefore acts as $\zeta_m\mapsto\zeta_m^p$, and the extension degree is the multiplicative order of $p$ modulo $m$, with degree one when $m=1$.

The identity
$$
\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_n)=\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_{p^a},\zeta_m)
$$
follows by taking powers of a primitive nth <root of unity> and using the coprimality of $p^a,m$ in the opposite direction. The prime-to-$p$ field is <unramified> and the $p$-power field is <totally ramified>; <unramified base change preserves total ramification> proves that their compositum has the same <residue degree> as the prime-to-$p$ field. Thus the <total ramification criterion for local cyclotomic extensions> gives the full classification:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q_p\text{ is totally ramified}\iff n=p^a m,\quad a\geq0,\quad m\mid p-1.}
$$
This includes trivial extensions when $a=0$. For $p=2$ only $m=1$ is possible.

For a finite totally ramified <Galois extension> $L/K$ with <Galois group> $G$, define its <lower ramification numbering> by
$$
G_{-1}=G,\qquad G_i=\{\sigma\in G:v_L(\sigma(b)-b)\geq i+1\text{ for every }b\in\mathcal O_L\}\quad(i\geq0).
$$
Here $G_0$ is the <inertia group>, which equals $G$, and $G_1$ is the <wild inertia group>. For a <uniformizer> $\pi$ the condition is equivalent to $v_L(\sigma(\pi)-\pi)\geq i+1$. To justify this <uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups>, expand every $b\in\mathcal O_L$ as a convergent series $\sum_{j\geq0}c_j\pi^j$ with the coefficients $c_j$ chosen from representatives of $k_K$ in $\mathcal O_K$. Such an expansion is obtained by successive subtraction of a residue representative and division by $\pi$, since total ramification gives $k_L=k_K$. The automorphism fixes each $c_j$, and $\sigma(\pi)^j-\pi^j$ is divisible by $\sigma(\pi)-\pi$. Thus the required valuation bound for $\pi$ implies it for every $b$, including the limit by <completeness>.

For $L=\mathbb Q_p(\zeta_{p^a})$, all primitive conjugates lie in $L$ and the Eisenstein degree is $\varphi(p^a)$. Hence
$$
G\simeq(\mathbb Z/p^a\mathbb Z)^\times,\qquad \sigma_b(\zeta)=\zeta^b.
$$
If $\sigma_b\ne1$, put $s=v_p(b-1)$, so $0\leq s<a$. The element $\zeta^{b-1}$ is a primitive $p^{a-s}$th <root of unity>. Applying the already proved uniformizer result to its field, and multiplying its valuation by the <ramification index> of $L$ over that field, gives
$$
v_L\bigl(\sigma_b(\zeta-1)-(\zeta-1)\bigr)=v_L(\zeta^{b-1}-1)
=\frac{\varphi(p^a)}{\varphi(p^{a-s})}=p^s.
$$
The identity automorphism has infinite valuation. Therefore the <lower ramification filtration of a prime-power cyclotomic extension> is explicitly
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
G_{-1}=G_0&=(\mathbb Z/p^a\mathbb Z)^\times,\\
G_i&=\{b:b\equiv1\pmod{p^j}\},&&p^{j-1}\leq i\leq p^j-1,\quad1\leq j<a,\\
G_i&=\{1\},&&i\geq p^{a-1}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The order of the middle group is $p^{a-j}$. At odd $p$, $G_0/G_1$ has order $p-1$; at $p=2$, the condition $b\equiv1\pmod2$ is automatic, so $G_0=G_1$ when $a\geq2$. The case $p=2,a=1$ has trivial <Galois group>, and the displayed formula remains valid. For $a=0$ all groups are trivial. The valuation calculation also provides the <upper ramification numbering>, if desired, by integrating the reciprocal indices with the <Herbrand function>; no change of numbering is implicit in the displayed answer.

Let $\pi\in K$ be a <uniformizer>. Because $L/K$ is <unramified>, the same $\pi$ is a <uniformizer> of $L$, and $v_L|_K=v_K$. The conjugates of $z\in L^\times$ all have the same <valuation>, so
$$
v_K(N_{L/K}z)=n\,v_L(z).
$$
This proves that every <field norm> has valuation divisible by $n$. Conversely $N_{L/K}(\pi^r)=\pi^{nr}$ for every integer $r$, so it remains to prove <surjectivity of unit norms in unramified extensions>.

Write $|k_K|=Q$, so $|k_L|=Q^n$. Reduction of the <field norm> on units is the finite-field norm
$$
k_L^\times\longrightarrow k_K^\times,\qquad a\longmapsto a^{1+Q+\cdots+Q^{n-1}}.
$$
The <multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic>. The displayed power map on a group of order $Q^n-1$ has image of order $Q-1$, and is therefore onto $k_K^\times$. Given a unit $u\in\mathcal O_K^\times$, first choose $b_1\in\mathcal O_L^\times$ with $N(b_1)\equiv u\pmod\pi$.

We can correct the norm at each subsequent power of $\pi$. For $r\geq1$ and $a\in\mathcal O_L$, expanding the product over the cyclic <Galois group> gives
$$
N(1+\pi^r a)\equiv1+\pi^r\operatorname{Tr}_{k_L/k_K}(\bar a)\pmod{\pi^{r+1}}.
$$
All products involving at least two correction terms are divisible by $\pi^{2r}$, hence by $\pi^{r+1}$. The residue <field trace> is onto even if the residue characteristic divides $n$. Indeed its <polynomial> $T(X)=X+X^Q+\cdots+X^{Q^{n-1}}$ is nonzero of degree $Q^{n-1}<Q^n$; it cannot vanish at every element of $k_L$. Its values lie in $k_K$, since raising $T(a)$ to $Q$ cyclically permutes the summands. Thus it is a nonzero $k_K$-linear map to the one-dimensional space $k_K$, and is surjective.

Suppose $N(b_r)\equiv u\pmod{\pi^r}$. Choose $\bar a_r$ whose trace is the coefficient of $\pi^r$ in $u/N(b_r)-1$, and put $b_{r+1}=b_r(1+\pi^r a_r)$. Then $N(b_{r+1})\equiv u\pmod{\pi^{r+1}}$, and $b_{r+1}\equiv b_r\pmod{\pi^r}$. By <completeness> the units $b_r$ converge to a unit $b$. The <field norm> is <continuous>, being a polynomial in coordinates of multiplication, so $N(b)=u$. Multiplying such a unit by a power of $\pi$ proves
$$
\boxed{N_{L/K}(L^\times)=\{x\in K^\times:v_K(x)\equiv0\pmod n\}.}
$$
The proof uses no local class field theory, and works in either characteristic.

The multiplicative <Hilbert theorem 90> states that, for a finite <Galois extension> $L/K$ with <Galois group> $G$, every multiplicative <cocycle> $c:G\to L^\times$ satisfying $c_{\sigma\tau}=c_\sigma\sigma(c_\tau)$ has the form $c_\sigma=\sigma(y)/y$ for some $y\in L^\times$. In the cyclic case $G=\langle\sigma\rangle$, it says
$$
\boxed{N_{L/K}(x)=1\iff x=\sigma(y)/y\text{ for some }y\in L^\times.}
$$

Here is the full existence argument. Distinct <field homomorphisms> $\sigma:L\to L$ are linearly independent as functions over $L$. To see this <Artin independence theorem> directly, assume a nonzero identically vanishing relation with the fewest nonzero coefficients. There are at least two terms. Choose two participating embeddings $\sigma_1,\sigma_j$ and an element $a$ with $\sigma_1(a)\ne\sigma_j(a)$. Evaluate the relation at $at$ and subtract $\sigma_j(a)$ times its value at $t$. The $j$th term disappears while the first remains nonzero, giving a shorter vanishing relation, a contradiction.

Apply this independence to
$$
A(t)=\sum_{\sigma\in G}c_\sigma^{-1}\sigma(t).
$$
The coefficients are nonzero, so $A$ is not identically zero; choose $t$ with $y=A(t)\ne0$. The <cocycle> identity gives $\tau(c_\sigma^{-1})=c_{\tau\sigma}^{-1}c_\tau$, and hence
$$
\tau(y)=\sum_{\sigma\in G}\tau(c_\sigma^{-1})\tau\sigma(t)
=c_\tau\sum_{\rho\in G}c_\rho^{-1}\rho(t)=c_\tau y.
$$
This proves the general statement. In the cyclic case, given $N(x)=1$, set $c_1=1$ and $c_{\sigma^j}=x\sigma(x)\cdots\sigma^{j-1}(x)$ for $1\leq j<n$. The norm-one identity makes this definition compatible with $\sigma^n=1$, and the <cocycle> identity follows by concatenating the products. The argument above gives $\sigma(y)/y=x$. Conversely that quotient has norm one because its conjugate product telescopes.

Finally let $L/K$ be <unramified>, and take its <arithmetic Frobenius> $\phi$. For a unit $x$ of norm one, the cyclic theorem gives $b\in L^\times$ with $\phi(b)/b=x$. Write $r=v_L(b)$ and choose the common <uniformizer> $\pi\in K$. Then $y=\pi^{-r}b$ has valuation zero, and $\phi(\pi)=\pi$ implies $\phi(y)/y=\phi(b)/b=x$. Thus the <unit form of Hilbert theorem 90> is
$$
\boxed{x\in\mathcal O_L^\times,\ N_{L/K}(x)=1\Longrightarrow\exists y\in\mathcal O_L^\times:\ \phi(y)/y=x.}
$$
The unramified hypothesis is what permits shifting an arbitrary integer valuation of $b$ by a power of a uniformizer belonging to $K$.

Fix a non-Archimedean <local field> $K$, write $Q=|k_K|$, and use the **arithmetic Frobenius normalization** throughout: a <uniformizer> maps to the automorphism inducing $a\mapsto a^Q$ on the <maximal unramified extension>. The <local Artin map>, or <local reciprocity map>, is the canonical continuous <group homomorphism>
$$
\operatorname{Art}_K:K^\times\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(K^{\mathrm{ab}}/K),
$$
where $K^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is the <maximal abelian extension> inside a fixed <algebraic closure> with its separable part understood. Its finite projection to every finite abelian extension $L/K$ is onto and has kernel exactly the <norm subgroup of a local field extension>:
$$
\boxed{K^\times/N_{L/K}(L^\times)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{Gal}(L/K).}
$$
In particular, the quotient has order $[L:K]$. This is how <local class field theory> turns abelian extension problems into computations inside a multiplicative group.

One precise definition of the finite reciprocity map uses the <cup-product construction of local reciprocity>. For a finite <Galois extension> with group $G$, the <local fundamental class> $u_{L/K}\in H^2(G,L^\times)$ is specified by <local Brauer invariant> $1/[L:K]$. <Cup product> gives an isomorphism
$$
\widehat H^{-2}(G,\mathbb Z)=G^{\mathrm{ab}}
\xrightarrow{\ \smile u_{L/K}\ }
\widehat H^0(G,L^\times)=K^\times/N_{L/K}(L^\times).
$$
The finite <local Artin map> is its inverse, composed with the quotient map from $K^\times$. For abelian $G$ the target is $G$ itself. Taking the compatible <inverse limit> over finite abelian extensions defines $\operatorname{Art}_K$.

The essential inputs to this cohomological construction have concrete arithmetic content. The <local Brauer invariant> identifies $\operatorname{Br}(K)$ with $\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$; restriction to $L$ multiplies the invariant by $[L:K]$, so its kernel is cyclic of that order and has the distinguished generator $u_{L/K}$. For every subgroup $H\subseteq G$, <Hilbert theorem 90> gives $H^1(H,L^\times)=0$, while the same invariant calculation over the fixed field gives $H^2(H,L^\times)$ cyclic of order $|H|$. These are the hypotheses of the algebraic cup-product isomorphism in <Tate cohomology of a finite group>. It is obtained by <dimension shifting in group cohomology> with a <projective resolution> of the trivial module and the extension representing the fundamental class. Thus the norm-kernel statement comes from the degree-zero definition of <Tate cohomology of a finite group>, rather than from an arbitrary assignment of an automorphism to each element. In an <unramified extension> of degree $d$, the fundamental class is the <cyclic algebra> with <arithmetic Frobenius> generator and parameter a <uniformizer>; the isomorphism sends that Frobenius to the class of the uniformizer. This explains the chosen sign of reciprocity.

Several properties follow immediately from this construction. If $L\supseteq E\supseteq K$ are finite abelian extensions, restricting $\operatorname{Art}_{L/K}(a)$ to $E$ gives $\operatorname{Art}_{E/K}(a)$. The <local Artin map> is therefore compatible with the finite projections defining its infinite target. For any finite separable extension $E/K$ and $a\in E^\times$, its norm compatibility is
$$
\operatorname{Art}_K(N_{E/K}a)=\operatorname{Art}_E(a)|_{K^{\mathrm{ab}}}.
$$
Here $K^{\mathrm{ab}}E/E$ is abelian, so the displayed restriction makes sense. An outline of the compatibility proof is to use the <corestriction map in group cohomology>: on multiplicative coefficients its degree-zero map is the norm. The cup-product projection formula, together with restriction and corestriction of the <local fundamental class>, gives exactly the commuting reciprocity diagram. The compatibility is an arithmetic instance of the functoriality of the cohomological definition.

The <local Artin map> has dense image, because every finite abelian quotient receives a surjective map. Its kernel is trivial, and it extends to an isomorphism
$$
\boxed{\widehat{K^\times}\simeq\operatorname{Gal}(K^{\mathrm{ab}}/K),}
$$
where this <profinite completion> uses the open finite-index subgroups of the topological multiplicative group. The original map is generally not onto: on the unramified quotient it sends the integer valuation into $\widehat{\mathbb Z}$, rather than producing every profinite integer. Its units map onto the abelian <inertia group>. For a finite abelian extension, the corresponding unit image is its <inertia group>, while the valuation accounts for its residue extension. Indeed $v_K(Nz)=f(L/K)v_L(z)$, so the valuation quotient has order $f$; the full norm quotient has order $ef$, leaving unit image of order $e$. Since unit images act trivially on the residue field, they give precisely the <inertia group>. More finely, the image of the <higher principal-unit group> $1+\mathfrak m_K^r$ in a finite abelian extension is its rth upper <ramification group>, for each integer $r\geq1$. The use of <upper ramification numbering> is essential for compatibility with quotients.

The <existence theorem of local class field theory> states that every open finite-index subgroup $H\subseteq K^\times$ is $N_{L/K}(L^\times)$ for a unique finite abelian extension $L/K$. Inclusions are reversed: larger extensions have smaller norm subgroups. In the established reciprocity isomorphism, the proof is simply to take the fixed field of the open subgroup corresponding to $H$. Conversely a finite extension gives an open kernel of a finite continuous projection. Uniqueness follows because that kernel specifies its fixed field. The substantive existence problem is therefore to prove that the reciprocity isomorphism realizes all open finite-index subgroups; it is not just this final fixed-field argument.

The <Lubin–Tate description of local reciprocity> provides an explicit route to that existence problem. Choose a <uniformizer> $\pi$ and a <Lubin–Tate series> $f(T)=\pi T+T^Q$, with its associated <Lubin–Tate formal group> $F$. Solving $f(F(X,Y))=F(f(X),f(Y))$ successively by total degree constructs the law with linear part $X+Y$; the analogous equations construct the endomorphisms $[a]$ with linear coefficient $a$. The congruence $f(T)\equiv T^Q\pmod\pi$ supplies the divisibility needed at each step. These endomorphisms satisfy $[a][b]=[ab]$ and make the torsion an $\mathcal O_K$-module.

If $f_r$ denotes the rfold iterate, the primitive level-r <Lubin–Tate torsion> points are roots of
$$
\frac{f_r(T)}{f_{r-1}(T)}=\pi+f_{r-1}(T)^{Q-1}.
$$
Reduction modulo $\pi$ is $T^{Q^{r-1}(Q-1)}$, and the constant term is $\pi$, so this is an <Eisenstein polynomial>. Its roots therefore generate a <totally ramified extension> $K_{\pi,r}$ of degree $(Q-1)Q^{r-1}$. A primitive point generates the entire torsion module, and all points are its images under the integral convergent endomorphisms $[a]$. The torsion field is therefore a <Galois extension> of $K$. The action map into $(\mathcal O_K/\pi^r)^\times$ is injective, and the computed degree equals the order of this unit group, proving it is an isomorphism. Passing to the <inverse limit> gives $\operatorname{Gal}(K_\pi/K)\simeq\mathcal O_K^\times$ for the <Lubin–Tate tower> $K_\pi$.

The unramified tower and $K_\pi$ are <linearly disjoint field extensions>: a finite common subextension would be both unramified and totally ramified, hence trivial. The <Lubin–Tate description of local reciprocity> on their compositum is
$$
\operatorname{Art}_K(\pi^m u)|_{K^{\mathrm{nr}}}=\operatorname{Frob}_K^m,\qquad
\operatorname{Art}_K(\pi^m u)(\lambda)=[u^{-1}](\lambda)\quad(\lambda\in F[\pi^r]).
$$
The inverse on the unit action goes with the arithmetic Frobenius convention; changing to geometric Frobenius inverts the whole reciprocity map. The reciprocity theorem identifies this explicit action with the finite cup-product maps, independently of the chosen uniformizer. Its proof compares the fundamental class with the norm relations between consecutive torsion fields. The construction makes the possible finite kernels concrete: on the compositum of the degree-d unramified field with $K_{\pi,r}$ the kernel is
$$
\pi^{d\mathbb Z}(1+\pi^r\mathcal O_K).
$$
These subgroups are cofinal among open finite-index subgroups. Indeed any such $H$ contains $1+\pi^r\mathcal O_K$ for sufficiently large $r$, and contains $\pi^d$ for some positive $d$, since the class of $\pi$ in $K^\times/H$ has finite order. Taking the appropriate fixed subgroup in this explicit finite compositum realizes $H$. This proves the open-subgroup part of existence once the norm compatibility of the explicit and cohomological constructions is established. Together with the finite reciprocity maps it also shows that $K^{\mathrm{ab}}=K^{\mathrm{nr}}K_\pi$.

As a direct check, on a finite unramified extension the construction reduces to
$$
\operatorname{Art}_{L/K}(a)=\phi_{L/K}^{\,v_K(a)}.
$$
The earlier elementary unit-norm proof shows its kernel is exactly $\pi^{d\mathbb Z}\mathcal O_K^\times$. At $K=\mathbb Q_p$, the <Lubin–Tate cyclotomic example> takes $[p](T)=(1+T)^p-1$ and $F(X,Y)=X+Y+XY$. Its torsion is $\zeta-1$, so a unit $u$ acts by $\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{u^{-1}}$. These examples exhibit both the valuation and unit parts of local reciprocity with consistent signs.
