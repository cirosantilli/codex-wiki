= Solution
{parent=1}

Throughout, <rings> are commutative with identity and <ring homomorphisms> preserve identity. The <spectrum of a commutative ring> is $\operatorname{Spec}A=\{\mathfrak p\subset A:\mathfrak p\text{ is a prime ideal}\}$. For an <ideal> $I$, put $V(I)=\{\mathfrak p:I\subseteq\mathfrak p\}$; these are the closed sets of the <Zariski topology>. Its axioms follow from
$$
V(0)=\operatorname{Spec}A,\qquad V(A)=\varnothing,\qquad \bigcap_\lambda V(I_\lambda)=V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right),\qquad V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
$$
For the last equality, a <prime ideal> containing $IJ$ must contain $I$ or $J$: if it contained neither, choose $a\in I\setminus\mathfrak p$ and $b\in J\setminus\mathfrak p$, contradicting $ab\in\mathfrak p$. The other equalities follow directly from containment. Thus arbitrary intersections and finite unions of closed sets are closed, proving that this is a <topology>.

The <Zorn lemma> says that a nonempty <partially ordered set> in which every chain has an upper bound has a maximal element. For $A\ne0$, apply it to proper <ideals> ordered by inclusion. The zero <ideal> is proper. The union of a nonempty chain is an <ideal> and is proper, because containing one would put one in a member of the chain; the empty chain has the zero <ideal> as an upper bound. There is therefore a <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. If $a\notin\mathfrak m$, then $\mathfrak m+(a)=A$, so the class of $a$ in $A/\mathfrak m$ is invertible. This <quotient ring> is a <field>, making $\mathfrak m$ a <prime ideal>. Hence **$A\ne0\implies\operatorname{Spec}A\ne\varnothing$**.

For a <ring homomorphism> $f:A\to B$, define $f^*:\operatorname{Spec}B\to\operatorname{Spec}A$ by $f^*(\mathfrak q)=f^{-1}(\mathfrak q)$. The inverse image is proper because $f(1)=1$, and primality follows from $f(ab)=f(a)f(b)$. Moreover
$$
(f^*)^{-1}(V_A(I))=V_B(IB),
$$
where $IB$ is the <ideal> generated by $f(I)$. This proves <continuity>. Inverse images also give $(g\circ f)^*=f^*\circ g^*$ and $\mathrm{id}^*=\mathrm{id}$. Thus **$\operatorname{Spec}$ is a contravariant <functor> from rings to topological spaces**.

For the specified inclusion, the <fundamental theorem of algebra> gives the points $0$ and $(x-z)$ of $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[x]$. Over $\mathbb R$, the nonzero <prime ideals> are generated either by $x-r$ with $r\in\mathbb R$, or by $(x-a)^2+b^2$ with $a\in\mathbb R$, $b>0$; all irreducible real <polynomials> have degree one or two. Contraction gives the <spectrum map for the complexification of a real polynomial ring>:
$$
\boxed{0\mapsto0,\qquad (x-z)\mapsto\begin{cases}(x-z),&z\in\mathbb R,\\((x-\operatorname{Re}z)^2+(\operatorname{Im}z)^2),&z\notin\mathbb R.\end{cases}}
$$
Indeed, a real <polynomial> vanishing at a nonreal $z$ also vanishes at $\bar z$, and is divisible by their quadratic factor; the converse is immediate. This map is surjective. Its fibers contain one point over a real linear factor, two conjugate points over an irreducible quadratic factor, and only the <generic point> over zero. It identifies complex-conjugate pairs. Since each proper <Zariski-closed set> in either one-variable <polynomial ring> is a finite set of closed points, images of closed sets are closed; hence the <Zariski topology> on the target is the <quotient topology> for this identification.

Let $S$ be a <multiplicative subset>, containing one and closed under products. On $A\times S$, use
$$
(a,s)\sim(b,t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u(ta-sb)=0\text{ for some }u\in S.
$$
The relation is reflexive and symmetric. For transitivity, if $u(ta-sb)=0$ and $v(rb-tc)=0$, then multiplication and addition give $uvt(ra-sc)=0$. Thus it is an <equivalence relation>. Write $a/s$ for the equivalence class. The <localization of a ring> $A_S=S^{-1}A$ has operations
$$
\frac as+\frac bt=\frac{at+bs}{st},\qquad \frac as\frac bt=\frac{ab}{st},\qquad -\frac as=\frac{-a}{s}.
$$
They are well defined. For example, replacing $a/s$ by $b/t$ in its sum with $c/r$ gives cross-numerator difference $r^2(ta-sb)$, and in its product with $c/r$ gives $rc(ta-sb)$. The same annihilating element $u$ kills both differences. Replacing the other input works identically. Associativity and distributivity then follow by putting all terms over the product of their denominators and using the <ring> identities in $A$. Addition and multiplication are commutative, with identities $0/1$ and $1/1$, and the displayed negative is an additive inverse. Hence **$S^{-1}A$ is a commutative ring**. If $0\in S$, the fraction relation identifies all elements, producing the zero <ring>.

The canonical <ring homomorphism> $\iota:A\to S^{-1}A$, $a\mapsto a/1$, makes each $s\in S$ a <unit>, with inverse $1/s$. The <universal property of localization> states that any <ring homomorphism> $h:A\to C$ sending $S$ into the <units> factors uniquely through $\iota$. Define the factor by
$$
\boxed{\widetilde h(a/s)=h(a)h(s)^{-1}.}
$$
If $u(ta-sb)=0$, then $h(u)$ is invertible, so $h(t)h(a)=h(s)h(b)$, proving well-definedness. The fraction operations prove that $\widetilde h$ is a <ring homomorphism>. Its value is forced by $a/s=\iota(a)\iota(s)^{-1}$, so it is unique. This property characterizes the <localization> up to a unique compatible <isomorphism>.

For a <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$, its complement is a <multiplicative subset>, and the <localization at a prime ideal> is **$A_{\mathfrak p}=(A\setminus\mathfrak p)^{-1}A$**. Fractions with numerator outside $\mathfrak p$ are <units>; those with numerator in $\mathfrak p$ form the unique <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}$. Thus it is a <local ring>.

For the nilpotent question, suppose $a\ne0$ and $a^n=0$. Its <annihilator> is a proper <ideal>, so it lies in some <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$. The fraction criterion says $a/1=0$ in $A_{\mathfrak m}$ only if $sa=0$ for some $s\notin\mathfrak m$, which is impossible by the choice of $\mathfrak m$. Thus $a/1$ is a nonzero <nilpotent element> in that <localization>. This proves <reducedness is detected by prime localizations>: **if every prime localization is reduced, $A$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements**.

For the domain question take $A=k\times k$ with $k$ a <field>. Its only <prime ideals> are $\mathfrak p_1=0\times k$ and $\mathfrak p_2=k\times0$: a <prime ideal> must contain one of the orthogonal <idempotents> $(1,0),(0,1)$, and the corresponding quotient is $k$. Localizing at $\mathfrak p_1$ makes $(1,0)$ invertible and kills $(0,1)$, giving $A_{\mathfrak p_1}\cong k$; similarly $A_{\mathfrak p_2}\cong k$. But $(1,0)(0,1)=0$ with both factors nonzero. Therefore **domain prime localizations do not force $A$ to be an integral domain**, as expressed by <domain prime localizations do not imply a domain>.

A <Noetherian module> satisfies the <ascending chain condition> on its <submodules>; equivalently every <submodule> is a <finitely generated module>. If the latter condition holds, the union of an ascending chain has a finite generating set lying in one member, so the chain stabilizes. Conversely, a submodule with no finite generating set allows successive choices outside the submodule generated so far, producing a strictly ascending chain. A <Noetherian ring> is a ring that is <Noetherian> as a module over itself, equivalently one whose <ideals> are all finitely generated.

The <formal power series ring> consists of sequences written $\sum_{i\ge0}a_ix^i$, with coefficientwise addition and the <Cauchy product>:
$$
\left(\sum_{i\ge0}a_ix^i\right)\left(\sum_{j\ge0}b_jx^j\right)=\sum_{n\ge0}\left(\sum_{i=0}^na_ib_{n-i}\right)x^n.
$$
Every coefficient uses a finite sum; no analytic convergence is involved. Evaluation at zero gives $A[[x]]/(x)\cong A$. A <quotient ring> of a <Noetherian ring> is <Noetherian>, because its ascending chains of <ideals> lift to such chains upstairs. Thus one direction of <Noetherianity of a formal power series ring> is immediate.

For the other direction suppose $A$ is <Noetherian>, and let $I\subseteq A[[x]]$ be an <ideal>. Define the <coefficient ideals of a formal power series ideal>
$$
I_r=\{[x^r]g:g\in I\cap x^rA[[x]]\}\subseteq A.
$$
Each is an <ideal>, and multiplication by $x$ gives $I_r\subseteq I_{r+1}$. Choose $N$ with $I_N=I_{N+1}=\cdots$. For $0\le r\le N$, choose generators $a_{r1},\ldots,a_{r m_r}$ for $I_r$, and choose series $g_{rj}\in I\cap x^rA[[x]]$ whose $x^r$ coefficients are those generators. Empty generating sets are allowed for zero <ideals>.

These finitely many series generate $I$. To prove this without assuming that $I$ is closed, start with $g\in I$ and cancel its coefficients successively. If the residual $h_r$ lies in $I\cap x^rA[[x]]$, its $x^r$ coefficient lies in $I_r$. Put $s=\min(r,N)$; since $I_r=I_s$ when $r\ge N$, write that coefficient as $\sum_jc_{rj}a_{sj}$ and subtract $\sum_jc_{rj}x^{r-s}g_{sj}$. The new residual belongs to $I\cap x^{r+1}A[[x]]$.

For $s<N$, the generators $g_{sj}$ are used only at the single step $r=s$. For $s=N$, their accumulated multipliers are the genuine <formal power series> $\sum_{r\ge N}c_{rj}x^{r-N}$. Thus the construction yields the exact coefficientwise identity
$$
g=\sum_{s<N}\sum_jc_{sj}g_{sj}+\sum_j\left(\sum_{r\ge N}c_{rj}x^{r-N}\right)g_{Nj}.
$$
Every coefficient of the residual vanishes after finitely many steps. This is an ordinary finite generating expression in $A[[x]]$, not merely an approximation by elements of a smaller <ideal>. Therefore every <ideal> is finitely generated, proving
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is Noetherian}\iff A[[x]]\text{ is Noetherian}.}
$$
Finally, for the surjective <module endomorphism> $f$, the kernels $\ker f\subseteq\ker f^2\subseteq\cdots$ stabilize because $M$ is a <Noetherian module>. Take $N$ with $\ker f^N=\ker f^{N+1}$. If $f(m)=0$, surjectivity of $f^N$ gives $u$ with $f^N(u)=m$. Then $f^{N+1}(u)=0$, so $u\in\ker f^N$ and $m=0$. Hence $f$ is injective as well as surjective; its inverse is automatically $A$-linear. This proves **$f$ is an isomorphism**, the statement that <Noetherian modules are Hopfian>.

An <algebraic line bundle> over $A$ is an <invertible module>: a <finitely generated projective module> $L$ such that $L_{\mathfrak p}\cong A_{\mathfrak p}$ for every <prime ideal> $\mathfrak p$. Define the <Picard group of a ring> as the set of its isomorphism classes, with
$$
[L][K]=[L\otimes_AK].
$$
The <tensor product of modules> of two finite <projective modules> is finite projective: write each as a direct summand of a finite <free module> and tensor the two decompositions. Localization gives $(L\otimes_AK)_{\mathfrak p}\cong A_{\mathfrak p}$, so this multiplication stays within the set of <invertible modules>. It is well defined on isomorphism classes. The associativity and symmetry isomorphisms of the <tensor product> give associativity and commutativity, and $[A]$ is the identity.

Let $L^*=\operatorname{Hom}_A(L,A)$ be the <dual module>. A finite direct-summand presentation shows that $L^*$ is finite projective and that localization commutes with this dual. Since $L_{\mathfrak p}$ is free of rank one, so is $(L^*)_{\mathfrak p}$. The <evaluation homomorphism>
$$
L\otimes_AL^*\longrightarrow A,\qquad \ell\otimes\lambda\longmapsto\lambda(\ell)
$$
is an <isomorphism> after every prime localization. Its kernel and cokernel are therefore zero, by <exactness of localization> and <localization detects zero elements> for modules. Thus **$[L]^{-1}=[L^*]$**, proving that $\operatorname{Pic}(A)$ is an <abelian group>.

For a <ring homomorphism> $f:A\to B$, tensoring a split presentation $L\oplus L'\cong A^r$ gives $(B\otimes_AL)\oplus(B\otimes_AL')\cong B^r$, so $B\otimes_AL$ is finite projective. For $\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}B$ put $\mathfrak p=f^{-1}(\mathfrak q)$. Every element of $A\setminus\mathfrak p$ becomes a <unit> in $B_{\mathfrak q}$, and
$$
(B\otimes_AL)_{\mathfrak q}\cong B_{\mathfrak q}\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak p}}L_{\mathfrak p}\cong B_{\mathfrak q}.
$$
This proves <base change of invertible modules>. Moreover, the canonical map
$$
(B\otimes_AL)\otimes_B(B\otimes_AK)\longrightarrow B\otimes_A(L\otimes_AK),\qquad (b\otimes\ell)\otimes(b'\otimes k)\longmapsto bb'\otimes(\ell\otimes k)
$$
is an <isomorphism>, with inverse $b\otimes(\ell\otimes k)\mapsto(b\otimes\ell)\otimes(1\otimes k)$. It shows that $[L]\mapsto[B\otimes_AL]$ is a <group homomorphism>. Identity maps induce the identity, and for $A\to B\to C$, the associativity isomorphism $C\otimes_B(B\otimes_AL)\cong C\otimes_AL$ proves composition compatibility. Therefore **$\operatorname{Pic}$ is a covariant functor to abelian groups**.

The <polynomial ring> $\mathbb C[x]$ is a <principal ideal domain> by division with remainder. A submodule $N$ of a finite free module over a <principal ideal domain> is free: induct on the ambient rank, project onto the first coordinate, and note that the image is a principal <ideal>, hence either zero or a free rank-one module. This gives a split exact sequence with that image as quotient; the kernel lies in a free module of one smaller rank, completing the induction. A finite <projective module> is such a submodule, and so is free. An <invertible module> has rank one after passing to the <fraction field>, so its free rank is exactly one. Hence every <algebraic line bundle> over $\mathbb C[x]$ is trivial and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Pic}(\mathbb C[x])=0.}
$$

Here the usual <homological algebra> convention is that the right-exact <functor> $F$ is additive. Module categories have enough <projective modules>, because any <module> is a quotient of a <free module>. Choose a <projective resolution>
$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{d_3}P_2\xrightarrow{d_2}P_1\xrightarrow{d_1}P_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0.
$$
Apply $F$ to the unaugmented <chain complex> and define the <left derived functors> by
$$
\boxed{L_iF(M)=H_i(F(P_\bullet))=\frac{\ker F(d_i)}{\operatorname{im}F(d_{i+1})}\quad(i>0),\qquad L_0F(M)=\operatorname{coker}F(d_1).}
$$
Additivity ensures that $F$ preserves zero maps, sums of maps and homotopy identities, so the transformed differentials still compose to zero.

For a <module homomorphism> $u:M\to N$, lift it to a <chain map> between chosen <projective resolutions>. In degree zero, lift through $Q_0\twoheadrightarrow N$ using projectivity of $P_0$. In each higher degree, the already constructed map lands in the next kernel; exactness of $Q_\bullet$ and projectivity of $P_i$ supply the next lift. Any two lifts are <chain homotopic>: their degree-zero difference lands in $\ker(Q_0\to N)=\operatorname{im}d_1^Q$, so it lifts to $h_0:P_0\to Q_1$. Inductively lift the remaining difference after subtracting the preceding homotopy term. This gives
$$
u_i-v_i=d_{i+1}^Qh_i+h_{i-1}d_i^P,\qquad h_{-1}=0.
$$
Applying $F$ preserves this identity, so the lifts induce the same <homology> map. Identity lifts and composable lifts show that $L_iF$ is a <functor>. Comparison lifts between two resolutions over the identity of $M$ are inverse up to <chain homotopy>; hence the construction is independent of the chosen resolution up to its canonical natural <isomorphism>.

Right exactness identifies $L_0F(M)=\operatorname{coker}(F(P_1)\to F(P_0))$ naturally with $F(M)$. For a <projective module> $P$, the length-zero resolution gives $L_iF(P)=0$ for $i>0$. For a <short exact sequence> $0\to M'\to M\to M''\to0$, the <horseshoe lemma> builds a degreewise split short exact sequence of <projective resolutions>. Concretely, lift the projective generators covering $M''$ into $M$ and combine them with generators covering $M'$; repeat on the kernels. The middle projective term in each degree is the direct sum of the two outer ones. An <additive functor> preserves these split sequences, and lifting cycles, taking their differentials and returning to the subcomplex produces the connecting maps in
$$
\cdots\to L_2F(M'')\to L_1F(M')\to L_1F(M)\to L_1F(M'')\to F(M')\to F(M)\to F(M'')\to0.
$$
This is the natural long exact derived-functor sequence. In particular, if $0\to K\to P\to M\to0$ has $P$ projective, then $L_iF(M)\cong L_{i-1}F(K)$ for $i\ge2$, and $L_1F(M)=\ker(F(K)\to F(P))$. These are dimension-shifting formulas. Positive degrees can be killed by the surjection $P\to M$. If $F$ is exact, it preserves every resolution's exactness and all its positive <left derived functors> vanish. Conversely, if $L_1F$ vanishes on all modules, the displayed long exact sequence makes $F$ exact. A natural transformation between additive right-exact <functors> induces transformations on the <left derived functors> by applying it termwise.

For <extension of scalars> $F(M)=B\otimes_AM$, these derived functors are the <Tor functors> $\operatorname{Tor}_i^A(B,M)$. When $B=A/(a)$ and $a$ is a <non-zero-divisor>, there is the length-one <free resolution>
$$
0\longrightarrow A\xrightarrow{\ a\ }A\longrightarrow B\longrightarrow0.
$$
Although the construction above resolves $M$, one may instead use this resolution of $B$. To justify that calculation, tensor a free resolution of $M$ with the two-term resolution of $B$ to form a double complex. Taking homology first along the resolution of $B$ leaves $B\otimes_AP_\bullet$; taking it first along the resolution of $M$ leaves $M\xrightarrow{a}M$. The free terms preserve exactness under tensoring. The double complex is first-quadrant with finitely many terms on each total diagonal, so both filtrations compute the same total homology. This proves the required balancing directly.

The kernel and cokernel of multiplication by $a$ now give <Tor for a principal non-zero-divisor quotient>:
$$
\boxed{L_0F(M)=M/aM,\qquad L_1F(M)=\{m\in M:am=0\},\qquad L_iF(M)=0\quad(i\ge2).}
$$
These are naturally $B$-modules. For $M=A$, the degree-one group is zero; for $M=B$, multiplication by $a$ is zero and both degrees zero and one are $B$. Thus, when $B\ne0$, degree one explicitly detects the failure of this base-change <functor> to be exact.

Choose a surjection $P_0=A^{r_0}\twoheadrightarrow M$. Since $A$ is a <Noetherian ring>, a finite <free module> is a <Noetherian module>, and its kernel $K_1$ is a <finitely generated module>. Choose a finite free surjection $P_1\twoheadrightarrow K_1$ and repeat. This constructs a <resolution by finitely generated free modules>
$$
\cdots\longrightarrow P_2\longrightarrow P_1\longrightarrow P_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0,
$$
whose terms are, in particular, <finitely generated projective modules>. It may be infinite; the request is finite generation of every term, not finite length.

Now suppose the nonzero <finitely generated module> $M$ has finite <projective dimension> $n$. For $n\ge1$, its $n$th syzygy $K_n$ in this free resolution is projective. Indeed, dimension shifting along the successive kernel sequences gives
$$
\operatorname{Ext}_A^1(K_n,N)\cong\operatorname{Ext}_A^{n+1}(M,N)=0
$$
for every <module> $N$. The vanishing follows by computing the latter <Ext functor> from any length-$n$ <projective resolution>. Vanishing of $\operatorname{Ext}^1(K_n,-)$ makes every short exact sequence ending in $K_n$ split: apply the <long exact sequence of Ext groups> to a free surjection onto $K_n$ to lift its identity. Thus $K_n$ is a <projective module>. It is finitely generated by the preceding construction. Truncating gives
$$
0\longrightarrow P_n\xrightarrow{d_n}P_{n-1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow P_0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow0
$$
with every term finite projective.

Apply the <Hom functor> $\operatorname{Hom}_A(-,A)$ and write $P_i^*=\operatorname{Hom}_A(P_i,A)$. At the top degree,
$$
\operatorname{Ext}_A^n(M,A)=\operatorname{coker}\bigl(d_n^*:P_{n-1}^*\to P_n^*\bigr).
$$
If this group were zero, $d_n^*$ would be surjective. The <dual module> of a <finite projective module> is finite projective, so this surjection has a section $s:P_n^*\to P_{n-1}^*$. Finite projective modules identify with their <double dual modules>: the evaluation map is an <isomorphism> for a finite free module and passes to its direct summands. Dualizing $d_n^*s=\mathrm{id}$ therefore gives $s^*d_n=\mathrm{id}_{P_n}$. Hence $d_n$ splits, and $P_{n-1}/d_n(P_n)$ is projective. Replacing the last two terms by this quotient shortens the <projective resolution> to length $n-1$, contradicting minimality. This proves <top Ext detects finite projective dimension>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ext}_A^n(M,A)\ne0.}
$$
For $n=0$, $M$ is a nonzero finite projective module and embeds as a summand of $A^r$. Some coordinate projection restricts nontrivially to $M$, proving $\operatorname{Hom}_A(M,A)\ne0$ as required.

For infinite <projective dimension>, take the <dual-number algebra> $A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ and $M=k=A/(\varepsilon)$. Multiplication by $\varepsilon$ has image and kernel both equal to $(\varepsilon)$, giving the <periodic resolution over dual numbers>
$$
\cdots\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}A\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}A\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}A\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow0.
$$
Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_A(-,k)$ makes every differential zero, so $\operatorname{Ext}_A^i(k,k)\cong k$ for every $i\ge0$. A finite <projective resolution> would force all sufficiently high <Ext functors> to vanish. Thus **$\operatorname{pd}_{k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)}k=\infty$**.

The finite-generation hypothesis is essential if the middle sentence's “a module” is interpreted as an unrestricted new module. A concrete <vanishing top Ext without finite generation> occurs for $A=k[[t]]$ and $K=A[1/t]=k((t))$. This <Noetherian ring> has the exact free presentation
$$
0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i\ge0}Ae_i\xrightarrow{d}\bigoplus_{i\ge0}Ae_i\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow0,\qquad d(e_i)=e_i-te_{i+1},\quad e_i\longmapsto t^{-i}.
$$
Injectivity of $d$ follows by examining the largest index of a finitely supported input, since $t$ is a <non-zero-divisor>. For exactness in the middle, use the relations to move a finite sum to its largest index $N$; its remaining coefficient is $\sum_{i=0}^Na_it^{N-i}$, which is zero precisely when its image in $K$ is zero. The map onto $K$ is surjective because every Laurent series has a lower bound on its exponents.

Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_A(-,A)$ gives on products the map $(b_i)\mapsto(b_i-tb_{i+1})$. It is surjective: for any $(c_i)$, set $b_i=\sum_{j\ge0}t^jc_{i+j}$. These are well-defined <formal power series>, because each coefficient receives only finitely many contributions, and $b_i-tb_{i+1}=c_i$. Consequently $\operatorname{Ext}_A^1(K,A)=0$. But $\operatorname{Hom}_A(K,A)=0$: the image of every $t^{-i}$ must lie in $\bigcap_{r\ge0}t^rA=0$, since it is divisible by every power of $t$. A nonzero <projective module> is a summand of a free module and therefore has a nonzero coordinate map to $A$. Thus $K$ is not projective, while its displayed resolution has length one. In particular,
$$
\operatorname{pd}_{k[[t]]}k((t))=1,\qquad\operatorname{Ext}_{k[[t]]}^1(k((t)),k[[t]])=0.
$$
The nonvanishing conclusion proved above retains the finitely generated $M$ introduced at the start of the question.
