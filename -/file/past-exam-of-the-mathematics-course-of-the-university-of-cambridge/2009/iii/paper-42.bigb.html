= Solution
{parent=1}

The <canonical anticommutation relations> realize the internal states as a <fermionic Fock space> $\Lambda^\bullet\mathbb C^3$. Choosing a <Clifford vacuum> $|0\rangle$ annihilated by all $\psi_a$, the states are functions multiplying $|0\rangle$, $\psi_a^\dagger|0\rangle$, $\psi_a^\dagger\psi_b^\dagger|0\rangle$ with $a<b$, or $\psi_1^\dagger\psi_2^\dagger\psi_3^\dagger|0\rangle$. Thus the four <fermion-number sectors in supersymmetric quantum mechanics> are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H_n=L^2(\mathbb R^3)\otimes\Lambda^n\mathbb C^3,\quad n=0,1,2,3,}
$$
with internal dimensions $1,3,3,1$. The <fermion number operator> $N=\sum_a\psi_a^\dagger\psi_a$ has value $n$ on $\mathcal H_n$. The sectors $n=0,2$ have even <fermion parity>, and $n=1,3$ have odd <fermion parity>.

Write $q=\sum_a\psi_a^\dagger A_a$, so that the <supercharge> is $Q=q+q^\dagger$. The <commutator> $[A_a,A_b]$ is zero because the two <mixed partial derivatives> of $\chi$ agree. The <canonical anticommutation relations> then imply $q^2=(q^\dagger)^2=0$ and
$$
H=qq^\dagger+q^\dagger q,\qquad [H,N]=[H,q]=[H,q^\dagger]=[H,Q]=0.
$$
The <Hamiltonian operator> preserves each sector, while $q$ raises and $q^\dagger$ lowers its degree by one. For a state in its operator domain, $\langle v,Hv\rangle=\|qv\|^2+\|q^\dagger v\|^2\geq0$, so the <spectrum> is nonnegative. If $Hv=Ev$ with $E>0$, then $Qv/\sqrt E$ is a state of the same energy and opposite <fermion parity>, and $Q(Qv/\sqrt E)=\sqrt E v$. This is an <odd involution pairing bosonic and fermionic states> and proves equal even and odd multiplicities at every positive <eigenvalue>.

There is also a more precise adjacent-sector pairing. On the positive-energy <eigenspace>,
$$
v=E^{-1}qq^\dagger v+E^{-1}q^\dagger qv.
$$
The two summands are <orthogonal> since $q^2=(q^\dagger)^2=0$. A state $u$ in the image of $q^\dagger$ obeys $q^\dagger u=0$, $q^\dagger qu=Eu$, so $q/\sqrt E$ maps it to a normalized partner in the next sector, with inverse $q^\dagger/\sqrt E$. Thus positive-energy doublets lie in $\mathcal H_0\oplus\mathcal H_1$, $\mathcal H_1\oplus\mathcal H_2$, or $\mathcal H_2\oplus\mathcal H_3$. In particular, every positive-energy state of $\mathcal H_0$ has a partner in $\mathcal H_1$, and every one of $\mathcal H_3$ has a partner in $\mathcal H_2$; the remaining middle-sector states pair with each other. There is no requirement that all four individual sector spectra coincide. Zero-energy states satisfy $qv=q^\dagger v=Qv=0$ and can be unpaired. No confinement assumption was given: a continuous <spectrum> is possible, with the same even/odd pairing on its positive spectral subspaces.

For real $\chi$, the <adjoint operator> is $A_a^\dagger=-\partial_a-\partial_a\chi$. In $\mathcal H_0$, every $\psi_a^\dagger\psi_b$ vanishes; in the one-dimensional internal space of $\mathcal H_3$, it equals $\delta_{ab}$. The two scalar <Hamiltonian operators> are consequently
$$
\boxed{H_0=\sum_a A_a^\dagger A_a=-\Delta+|\nabla\chi|^2+\Delta\chi,\qquad H_3=\sum_a A_aA_a^\dagger=-\Delta+|\nabla\chi|^2-\Delta\chi.}
$$
These are the extreme sectors of the <gradient superpotential Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics>.

For radial $\chi$ and a normalized state $v_0=f(r)|0\rangle$ with $E>0$, $q^\dagger v_0=0$ and $A_af=(x_a/r)(f'-\chi'f)$. The <radial superpartner in three-dimensional supersymmetric quantum mechanics> therefore has the simple form
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac{f'(r)-\chi'(r)f(r)}{\sqrt E}\,\psi_r^\dagger|0\rangle,\qquad \psi_r^\dagger=\sum_a\frac{x_a}{r}\psi_a^\dagger.}
$$
It belongs to $\mathcal H_1$; $Hv_1=Ev_1$ follows from $[H,Q]=0$, and its <wavefunction normalization> follows from $\|Qv_0\|^2=\langle v_0,Hv_0\rangle=E$. Also $Qv_1=\sqrt E v_0$. At the origin the formula is interpreted by regular extension; its degree-one components carry the radial <unit vector> rather than being three independent radial scalars.

For a <zero mode in the fermion-vacuum sector>, the factorization of $H_0$ gives $0=\sum_a\|A_af\|^2$. Hence $\partial_a f=(\partial_a\chi)f$, or $\partial_a(e^{-\chi}f)=0$. The requested <wavefunction> is
$$
\boxed{f(r)=Ce^{\chi(r)},\qquad |C|^{-2}=4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2e^{2\chi(r)}\,dr.}
$$
A nonzero <normalizable wavefunction> exists only if this integral is finite. The positive sign in the exponential is fixed by the given convention $A_a=\partial_a-\partial_a\chi$.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Odd elements of a <Grassmann algebra> anticommute, and in particular $\psi_i^2=0$. For variations with fixed endpoints, move each odd variation to the left before applying <integration by parts>. For example,
$$
\delta(\psi\dot\psi)=\delta\psi\dot\psi+\psi\delta\dot\psi
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta\int\psi\dot\psi\,dt=2\int\delta\psi\dot\psi\,dt.
$$
The interaction varies as $\delta_{\psi_1}(\psi_1\psi_2)=\delta\psi_1\psi_2$ and $\delta_{\psi_2}(\psi_1\psi_2)=-\delta\psi_2\psi_1$. Thus variation of the <action> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equations>
$$
\boxed{\ddot x=UU'+U''\psi_1\psi_2,\qquad \dot\psi_1=U'\psi_2,\qquad \dot\psi_2=U'\psi_1.}
$$
All products of $U$ and its derivatives commute with the odd variables. These signs are essential for this variant of <Grassmann-valued classical supersymmetric mechanics>.

The fermion bilinear is constant along a solution:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\psi_1\psi_2)=\dot\psi_1\psi_2+\psi_1\dot\psi_2=U'(\psi_2^2+\psi_1^2)=0.
$$
For the <energy>, direct differentiation now gives
$$
\dot E=\dot x(\ddot x-UU'-U''\psi_1\psi_2)-U'\frac{d}{dt}(\psi_1\psi_2)=0.
$$
Similarly the given <supercharge>, denoted $Q_1$, satisfies
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot Q_1&=\ddot x\psi_1+\dot x\dot\psi_1-U'\dot x\psi_2-U\dot\psi_2\\
&=(\ddot x-UU')\psi_1=U''\psi_1\psi_2\psi_1=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
A second conserved <supercharge> is
$$
\boxed{Q_2=\dot x\psi_2-U\psi_1,}
$$
since $\dot Q_2=(\ddot x-UU')\psi_2=U''\psi_1\psi_2\psi_2=0$. The two charges are independent functions on the unrestricted <phase space>: their coefficient matrix on $(\psi_1,\psi_2)$ has <determinant> $\dot x^2-U^2$, and is invertible wherever its even body is nonzero. They can become dependent on a restricted family of trajectories.

Under $\dot x=U$ and $\psi_2=-\psi_1$, the bilinear $\psi_1\psi_2$ vanishes. Differentiating the first-order equation gives $\ddot x=U'\dot x=UU'$, which is the bosonic <Euler-Lagrange equation>. The first fermionic equation becomes $\dot\psi_1=-U'\psi_1$; differentiating $\psi_2=-\psi_1$ gives $\dot\psi_2=U'\psi_1$, so the second fermionic equation holds too. This establishes the <first-order zero-energy solution in Grassmann supersymmetric mechanics> without imposing an additional equation.

Let $x_0=x(0)$ and $\psi_{10}=\psi_1(0)$. For $U=1+cx$ and $c\ne0$, solve $d(x+1/c)/dt=c(x+1/c)$ and $\dot\psi_1=-c\psi_1$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{x(t)=e^{ct}x_0+\frac{e^{ct}-1}{c},\qquad \psi_1(t)=e^{-ct}\psi_{10},\qquad \psi_2(t)=-e^{-ct}\psi_{10}.}
$$
For $c=0$, the nonsingular limiting answer is $\boxed{x(t)=x_0+t,\ \psi_1(t)=\psi_{10},\ \psi_2(t)=-\psi_{10}}$. These formulas also hold for even initial positions in the <Grassmann algebra> by ordinary polynomial evaluation. Finally, $U(x(t))=(1+cx_0)e^{ct}$, so the conserved quantities are
$$
\boxed{E=0,\qquad Q_1=2(1+cx_0)\psi_{10},\qquad Q_2=-2(1+cx_0)\psi_{10}.}
$$
The individual odd <supercharges> generally do not vanish, although their sum vanishes on this restricted solution.

Use <left Grassmann derivatives> and define the <supersymmetric covariant derivatives> $D_\pm=\partial_{\theta^\pm}-i\theta^\pm\partial_\pm$. Multiplication in the indicated order gives exactly the printed differential operator:
$$
iD_-D_+=i\partial_{\theta^-}\partial_{\theta^+}+\theta^-\partial_{\theta^+}\partial_- -\theta^+\partial_{\theta^-}\partial_+ -i\theta^-\theta^+\partial_-\partial_+.
$$
Thus the equation is $iD_-D_+\Phi=W(\Phi)$ in <two-dimensional N=(1,1) superspace>.

Under a <Lorentz boost>, take $x^\pm\mapsto e^{\pm\omega}x^\pm$ and $\theta^\pm\mapsto e^{\pm\omega/2}\theta^\pm$. The derivatives $\partial_\pm$ have boost weights $\mp1$, and $D_\pm$ have weights $\mp1/2$. Consequently $D_-D_+$ has weight zero, and is a <Lorentz scalar>. Both $\Phi$ and the ordinary function $W(\Phi)$ are scalar <superfields>, so the equation is <Lorentz invariant>.

For <supersymmetry>, $\{\partial_\theta,\theta\}=1$ and anticommutation of distinct odd coordinates give
$$
\{D_s,Q_t\}=0\quad(s,t\in\{+,-\}),\qquad Q_\pm^2=i\partial_\pm,\qquad \{Q_+,Q_-\}=0.
$$
Moving either <supercharge> through the two odd derivatives introduces two minus signs, hence $[Q_t,D_-D_+]=0$. If $\delta\Phi=\epsilon^+Q_+\Phi+\epsilon^-Q_-\Phi$, with odd constant parameters, the <chain rule> for an even scalar <superfield> gives
$$
\delta\bigl(iD_-D_+\Phi-W(\Phi)\bigr)=\sum_{t=\pm}\epsilon^tQ_t\bigl(iD_-D_+\Phi-W(\Phi)\bigr).
$$
Therefore a solution is mapped to a solution, proving <supersymmetry> invariance.

To compute the <supersymmetric Liouville equation>, order the odd coordinates as $\theta^-\theta^+$ and anticommute the fermionic component fields with both coordinates. In particular, $\partial_{\theta^+}(\theta^-\theta^+)=-\theta^-$. Direct application of the operator gives
$$
iD_-D_+\Phi=F+i\theta^-\partial_-\psi_- -i\theta^+\partial_+\psi_+ -i\theta^-\theta^+\partial_-\partial_+\phi.
$$
Let $N=\Phi-\phi$. Only the two products of its degree-one terms survive in $N^2$; for example, $(i\theta^-\psi_+)(i\theta^+\psi_-)=\theta^-\theta^+\psi_+\psi_-$. Hence $N^2=2\theta^-\theta^+\psi_+\psi_-$ and $N^3=0$. The <exponential function> expands exactly as
$$
e^\Phi=e^\phi\left[1+i\theta^-\psi_++i\theta^+\psi_-+\theta^-\theta^+(iF+\psi_+\psi_-)\right].
$$
Equating the constant terms fixes the <auxiliary field>, $\boxed{F=e^\phi}$. The linear and quadratic terms then give the requested component field <equations of motion>:
$$
\boxed{\partial_-\psi_-=e^\phi\psi_+,\qquad \partial_+\psi_+=-e^\phi\psi_-,\qquad \partial_-\partial_+\phi=-e^{2\phi}+ie^\phi\psi_+\psi_-.}
$$
The signs here follow from the printed operator, the printed component expansion, and <left Grassmann derivatives>; no convention change has been made in eliminating the <auxiliary field>.

A two-component <Weyl spinor> is a complex spinor transforming in one of the two inequivalent <group representations> $(1/2,0)$ and $(0,1/2)$ of the <Lorentz spinor double cover> $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$. An undotted left-handed field $\chi_\alpha$ transforms by a matrix $M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$; its <complex conjugate> $\bar\chi_{\dot\alpha}$ transforms by $M^*$. Dotted and undotted indices distinguish the two <chirality> representations. Raising a dotted index uses the invariant epsilon tensor and converts the <complex conjugate> transformation into the equivalent inverse-adjoint form. Rotations act by the same <SU(2)> generators on the two chiralities, while boosts have opposite generators, so the two complex representations cannot be identified by a complex-linear <Lorentz invariant> map.

The antisymmetric <invariant tensors> $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\epsilon_{\dot\alpha\dot\beta}$ raise and lower spinor indices; $M^T\epsilon M=\epsilon$ proves invariance of the contracted bilinear $\chi\psi=\chi^\alpha\psi_\alpha$. The components of a fermionic <Weyl spinor> are odd <Grassmann variables>, so this bilinear is symmetric under exchange of the two spinors: the antisymmetric epsilon sign cancels the Grassmann sign. In particular, choosing $\epsilon_{12}=1$ and $\epsilon^{12}=-1$ gives $\chi\chi=2\chi_1\chi_2$, generally nonzero. This <Weyl spinor bilinear exchange identity> permits a <Majorana mass term> for a single suitable <Weyl spinor>. With mostly-minus <metric signature> and $\bar\sigma^\mu=(I,-\boldsymbol\sigma)$, the free massless kinetic term is $i\chi^\dagger\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\chi$, and its field <equation of motion> is $\boxed{\bar\sigma^\mu\partial_\mu\chi=0}$.

= Solution

Use mostly-minus <metric signature>, $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. The <Weyl sigma matrices> are $\sigma^\mu=(I,\boldsymbol\sigma)$ and $\bar\sigma^\mu=(I,-\boldsymbol\sigma)$, with the three <Pauli matrices>
$$
\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <Pauli matrix multiplication law> $\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$ immediately gives $\sigma^\mu\bar\sigma^\nu+\sigma^\nu\bar\sigma^\mu=2\eta^{\mu\nu}I$: the $00$ case is $2I$, the mixed cases cancel, and the spatial cases give $-2\delta_{ij}I$. The spinor indices of $\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}$ express the correspondence between a <Lorentz four-vector> and a Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix. For $X=x^0I+x^i\sigma_i$, $\det X=(x^0)^2-|\mathbf x|^2$; $X\mapsto MXM^\dagger$ preserves this <determinant> for $M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb C)$, yielding the <Lorentz spinor double cover>.

In <four-dimensional N=1 supersymmetry>, the odd <supercharges> $Q_\alpha$ and $\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}=Q_\alpha^\dagger$ transform as conjugate <Weyl spinors>. Their <Super-Poincaré algebra> is
$$
\boxed{\{Q_\alpha,\bar Q_{\dot\beta}\}=2\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_\mu,\qquad \{Q_\alpha,Q_\beta\}=\{\bar Q_{\dot\alpha},\bar Q_{\dot\beta}\}=0,\qquad [P_\mu,Q_\alpha]=[P_\mu,\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}]=0.}
$$
The ordinary translation and Lorentz generators satisfy the <Poincaré algebra>, and the Lorentz generators act on the <supercharges> by the corresponding <group representations> on <Weyl spinors>. Tracing the mixed <anticommutator> gives $P_0=\frac14\sum_\alpha\{Q_\alpha,Q_\alpha^\dagger\}$, proving <energy positivity in global supersymmetry>. For a massive rest-frame state, $P_\mu=(M,0,0,0)$, the rescaled charges $Q_\alpha/\sqrt{2M}$ obey the <canonical anticommutation relations>. Acting with their adjoints builds a <supermultiplet> containing states of alternating <fermion parity>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For two independent left-handed <Weyl spinors> $\chi$ and $\eta$, a <Dirac mass term> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_D=-m\chi^\alpha\eta_\alpha-m^*\bar\chi_{\dot\alpha}\bar\eta^{\dot\alpha}.}
$$
The four-component <Dirac field> $\Psi_D=(\chi_\alpha,\bar\eta^{\dot\alpha})^T$ has independent particle and antiparticle degrees of freedom; after a phase redefinition making $m$ real, the same term is $-m\bar\Psi_D\Psi_D$. The two left-handed spinors can carry opposite charges under a conserved <U(1)> symmetry, $\chi\mapsto e^{iq\omega}\chi$, $\eta\mapsto e^{-iq\omega}\eta$. A gauge-invariant <Dirac mass term> contracts conjugate <gauge group representations>.

For one left-handed <Weyl spinor>, a <Majorana mass term> instead has the form
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_M=-\frac12\left(m\chi^\alpha\chi_\alpha+m^*\bar\chi_{\dot\alpha}\bar\chi^{\dot\alpha}\right).}
$$
Its self-contraction need not vanish, by the <Weyl spinor bilinear exchange identity>. The factor $1/2$ avoids double counting identical fields in variation. The associated four-component <Majorana fermion> $\Psi_M=(\chi_\alpha,\bar\chi^{\dot\alpha})^T$ satisfies $\Psi_M^c=\Psi_M$, so its particle is its own antiparticle. A <Majorana mass term> breaks continuous fermion-number phase symmetry for $\chi$, and is allowed only if its gauge indices admit an invariant symmetric contraction, as for a neutral field or a real <gauge group representation>. An isolated field of nonzero <U(1)> charge cannot have it. More generally, the mass matrix of several left-handed <Weyl spinors> is symmetric, since $\chi_i\chi_j=\chi_j\chi_i$.

The barred notation denotes the antichiral <supersymmetric covariant derivative>. Write barred spinor indices as dotted indices, and use the stated plus-sign convention with <left Grassmann derivatives>:
$$
\bar D_{\dot\alpha}=\partial_{\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}}+i\theta^\beta\sigma^\mu_{\beta\dot\alpha}\partial_\mu,\qquad
D_\alpha=\partial_{\theta^\alpha}+i\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^{\dot\beta}\partial_\mu.
$$
Their <supercovariant derivative algebra with left derivatives> gives $\{\bar D_{\dot\alpha},\bar D_{\dot\beta}\}=0$ and $\{D_\alpha,\bar D_{\dot\beta}\}=2i\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}\partial_\mu$. The constraint $\boxed{\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi=0}$ defines a <chiral superfield>, also called an <L-superfield>.

To solve the constraint, put $y^\mu=x^\mu+i\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta$. For a <left Grassmann derivative>, $\partial_{\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}}(\theta\sigma^\mu\bar\theta)=-\theta^\beta\sigma^\mu_{\beta\dot\alpha}$. Therefore $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}y^\mu=0$ and $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\theta^\beta=0$, so $\bar D$ becomes ordinary differentiation with respect to $\bar\theta$ at fixed $y$. Its general solution is the <chiral-superfield component expansion>
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\Phi(y,\theta)=A(y)+\sqrt2\theta^\alpha\chi_\alpha(y)+\theta\theta F(y).}
$$
Here $A$ is a complex scalar, $\chi$ a <Weyl spinor>, and $F$ a complex <auxiliary field>. Before imposing field <equations of motion> this is four real bosonic and four real fermionic components. Chirality means independence of $\bar\theta$ at fixed $y$, rather than at fixed $x$. The differential <supercharges> $Q_\alpha=\partial_{\theta^\alpha}-i\sigma^\mu_{\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^{\dot\beta}\partial_\mu$ and $\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}=\partial_{\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}}-i\theta^\beta\sigma^\mu_{\beta\dot\alpha}\partial_\mu$ anticommute with both covariant derivatives, so a <supersymmetry> transformation preserves the chiral constraint.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Here <L-superfield> means a left-<chiral superfield>: $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi=0$, and its <complex conjugate> is an <antichiral superfield>, $D_\alpha\Phi^*=0$. Interpret the two barred derivatives as the contracted scalar $\bar D^2=\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^{\dot\alpha}$. The key point is <chiral projection by squared supercovariant derivatives>. Since the two components of $\bar D$ anticommute, any product of three barred derivatives repeats a component and is zero. Thus
$$
\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^2X=0
$$
for every <superfield> $X$, even when $X$ is antichiral. In particular, $\bar D^2\Phi^*$ is chiral.

The right side is chiral too. Because $\Phi$ is even, the <graded derivation> rule gives $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}(\Phi^2)=(\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi)\Phi+\Phi(\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Phi)=0$. Constant $m$ and $\lambda$ do not contribute derivatives. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\bar D_{\dot\alpha}(\bar D^2\Phi^*)=\bar D_{\dot\alpha}(4m\Phi+4\lambda\Phi^2)=0,}
$$
so applying the chiral constraint to the equation imposes no additional incompatible condition. This is the <holomorphic closure of chiral superfields>; a generic nonholomorphic right side would not satisfy it.

Both sides have zero <Grassmann parity> and are <Lorentz scalars>: the two dotted derivative indices are contracted with the invariant epsilon tensor, and $\Phi$ is a scalar <superfield>. Moreover, each covariant derivative anticommutes with every <supercharge>, so its squared contraction commutes with the <supercharges>. Hence the equation transforms covariantly under <supersymmetry>. It is the chiral field <equation of motion> of the <Wess–Zumino model> with <superpotential> $\mathcal W(\Phi)=m\Phi^2/2+\lambda\Phi^3/3$, for which $\mathcal W'=m\Phi+\lambda\Phi^2$, using the normalization in the PDF. With <mass dimensions> $[\Phi]=1$, $[\bar D]=1/2$, $[m]=1$ and $[\lambda]=0$, each side has dimension two. No component field <equations of motion> are needed for this consistency argument.
