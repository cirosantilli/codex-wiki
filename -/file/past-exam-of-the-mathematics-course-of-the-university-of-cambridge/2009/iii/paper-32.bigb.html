= Solution
{parent=1}

A one-period <self-financing strategy> is a deterministic holdings vector $h=(h^0,\ldots,h^d)$, with initial cost $h\cdot S_0$ and terminal wealth $h\cdot S_1$. In the zero-initial-cost convention, an <arbitrage> satisfies
$$
h\cdot S_0=0,\qquad h\cdot S_1\ge0\text{ a.s.},\qquad\mathbb P(h\cdot S_1>0)>0.
$$
The convention allowing a nonpositive initial cost is equivalent here: invest any initial proceeds in the strictly positive <numéraire> to obtain a zero-cost <portfolio> with nonnegative terminal wealth.

Suppose a strictly positive <state-price density> $\rho$ satisfies the asset-pricing equations, with the indicated products integrable. By linearity,
$$
\mathbb E[\rho(h\cdot S_1)]=h\cdot S_0.
$$
For an <arbitrage>, the right-hand side is zero while the left-hand side is strictly positive, since the integrand is nonnegative and is positive with positive probability. This contradiction proves **absence of <arbitrage>**.

An <attainable European contingent claim> $X$ is a terminal random payoff for which some holdings vector $h$ satisfies $h\cdot S_1=X$ almost surely; its replication cost is $h\cdot S_0$. To prove uniqueness in a <complete market>, suppose $\rho'$ is another positive <state-price density>. For every event $A$, attainability gives a <portfolio> replicating $S_1^{(0)}\mathbf1_A$. Its initial cost has both representations, so
$$
\mathbb E[(\rho-\rho')S_1^{(0)}\mathbf1_A]=0\qquad\text{for every }A.
$$
The signed random variable here is integrable, because $\mathbb E[\rho S_1^{(0)}]=\mathbb E[\rho'S_1^{(0)}]=S_0^{(0)}<\infty$. Take $A$ to be the event where it is positive and then where it is negative. Both its positive and negative parts have expectation zero, and hence vanish almost surely. Since $S_1^{(0)}>0$,
$$
\boxed{\rho=\rho'\quad\text{a.s.}}
$$
This proves that <complete markets have unique state-price densities> without requiring an extra lower bound on the random terminal <numéraire>.

For the finite-state example, order the states from top to bottom in the diagram and write the density values as $\rho_1,\rho_2,\rho_3$. The two pricing equations are
$$
3\rho_1+5\rho_2+6\rho_3=12,\qquad6\rho_1+4\rho_2+3\rho_3=15.
$$
Adding gives $\rho_1+\rho_2+\rho_3=3$. Put $t=\rho_3$; substitution then gives
$$
\boxed{(\rho_1,\rho_2,\rho_3)=\left(\frac{3+t}{2},\frac{3(1-t)}2,t\right),\qquad0<t<1.}
$$
These are all the strictly positive solutions, and each satisfies both equations. The parameter is a state-price-density value, not a probability.

For a concrete unattainable <contingent claim>, take the payoff vector $(0,1,0)$. A replicating <portfolio> $(a,b)$ would have to satisfy $3a+6b=0$ and $6a+3b=0$ in the first and third states. These imply $a=b=0$, contradicting the required payoff one in the middle state. Thus **the market is an <incomplete market>**, despite having strictly positive <state-price densities>.

= Solution

Use the natural filtration $\mathcal F_t=\sigma(Z_1,\ldots,Z_t)$ and the usual integrable definition of the <Snell envelope>. Since the factors are positive, its terminal value is $U_T=Y_T$. If $U_{t+1}=Y_{t+1}c_{t+1}$, <independence of random variables> gives
$$
\mathbb E[U_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]=Y_tc_{t+1}\mathbb E Z_{t+1}.
$$
The backward recursion therefore becomes
$$
\boxed{U_t=Y_tc_t,\qquad c_T=1,\qquad c_t=\max\{1,c_{t+1}\mathbb E Z_{t+1}\}.}
$$
This proves the assertion by backward induction. All constants are finite and at least one. More explicitly,
$$
c_t=\max_{t\le u\le T}\prod_{j=t+1}^u\mathbb E Z_j,
$$
where the empty product is one. This is the <Snell envelope of a multiplicative process>. The standard <Snell envelope> hypothesis includes integrable rewards; <independence of random variables> and positivity then give finite first moments of the factors. If that hypothesis is dropped, an infinite-mean factor gives an infinite continuation value, so finite constants need not exist.

For the <discrete-time binomial market>, write $p=(1+r/\varepsilon)/2$. The assumptions give $0<p<1$, positive <bank account> and <stock> prices, and
$$
\mathbb E R_{t+1}=p\varepsilon-(1-p)\varepsilon=r.
$$
Thus
$$
\mathbb E\left[\frac{S_{t+1}}{B_{t+1}}\mid\mathcal F_t\right]=\frac{S_t}{B_t}.
$$
The finite discrete-time <fundamental theorem of asset pricing> states that a market with a strictly positive <numéraire> has no <arbitrage> if there is an equivalent probability measure under which all numéraire-denominated asset prices are <martingales>. The original probability measure is already such an <equivalent martingale measure>, proving **no <arbitrage>**.

The complete-market American-option pricing theorem identifies the discounted replication or superhedging value with the <Snell envelope> of the discounted exercise rewards. Here
$$
Y_t=\frac{S_t^2}{(1+r)^t}=S_0^2\prod_{j=1}^t\frac{(1+R_j)^2}{1+r}.
$$
The independent factors have common mean
$$
\lambda=\frac{\mathbb E(1+R_j)^2}{1+r}=\frac{1+2r+\varepsilon^2}{1+r}=1+\frac{r+\varepsilon^2}{1+r}>1.
$$
The <Snell envelope> recursion consequently gives $c_t=\lambda^{T-t}$. At every time before $T$, the continuation value strictly exceeds immediate exercise, so **optimal exercise is at maturity**. The undiscounted value process is $V_t=S_t^2\lambda^{T-t}$, and the time-zero replication cost is
$$
\boxed{V_0=S_0^2\left(\frac{1+2r+\varepsilon^2}{1+r}\right)^T.}
$$
In fact $V_t/B_t$ is a <martingale>, not just a <supermartingale>, because the <Snell envelope> has no exercise compensator before maturity.

To hedge the optimally exercised payoff, subtract the two next-state <portfolio> equations. The first-period <delta hedge> is
$$
\Delta_0=\frac{S_0^2(1+\varepsilon)^2\lambda^{T-1}-S_0^2(1-\varepsilon)^2\lambda^{T-1}}{S_0(1+\varepsilon)-S_0(1-\varepsilon)}
=\boxed{2S_0\lambda^{T-1}}.
$$
The corresponding <bank account> holding, since $B_0=1$, is $V_0-\Delta_0S_0=S_0^2\lambda^{T-1}(\lambda-2)$. Substitution into either next-state equation verifies both terminal values at time one. Repeating this two-state calculation at every node gives a <self-financing strategy> replicating the maturity payoff. It also covers earlier exercise: $V_t\ge S_t^2$ at all dates, so the seller can meet any earlier payment with the available wealth.

On a fixed finite horizon, a <self-financing strategy> consists of predictable <stock> holdings $h^i$, integrable against the price processes, and a <bank account> holding $h^0$. Its wealth and gains satisfy
$$
X_t=h_t^0B_t+\sum_{i=1}^dh_t^iS_t^i,\qquad dX_t=h_t^0dB_t+\sum_{i=1}^dh_t^idS_t^i.
$$
An <admissible trading strategy> has discounted wealth $X_t/B_t$ bounded below by a deterministic constant throughout the horizon. With the bounded <short rate> in this part, an undiscounted deterministic lower bound is equivalent after adjusting the constant. An <arbitrage> is an admissible zero-initial-cost strategy with $X_T\ge0$ almost surely and $\mathbb P(X_T>0)>0$.

Put $\mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1)^\top$ and define the <market price of risk>
$$
\lambda_t=\sigma_t^{-1}(\mu_t-r_t\mathbf1).
$$
The bounded coefficients and bounded inverse make $\lambda$ bounded. Define the <stochastic exponential>
$$
L_t=\exp\left(-\int_0^t\lambda_s\cdot dW_s-\frac12\int_0^t\lVert\lambda_s\rVert^2ds\right).
$$
The <Novikov condition> states that $\mathbb E\exp(\tfrac12\int_0^T\lVert\lambda_s\rVert^2ds)<\infty$ makes this exponential a true <martingale> with expectation one. Boundedness verifies the condition. Therefore $dQ=L_TdP$ defines an equivalent probability measure. The <Girsanov theorem> states that
$$
W_t^Q=W_t+\int_0^t\lambda_sds
$$
is a $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion> under $Q$. Substituting into the asset equations gives
$$
\frac{dS_t^i}{S_t^i}=r_tdt+\sum_j\sigma_t^{ij}dW_t^{Q,j},\qquad
\frac{d(S_t^i/B_t)}{S_t^i/B_t}=\sum_j\sigma_t^{ij}dW_t^{Q,j}.
$$
Thus $Q$ is an <equivalent local martingale measure>. The sufficient direction of the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing> says that such a measure excludes <arbitrage> among admissible <self-financing strategies>. Here the reason can be seen directly: $X/B$ is a <local martingale> under $Q$, and its lower bound makes it a <supermartingale>. Consequently $X_0=0$ implies $\mathbb E_Q[X_T/B_T]\le0$, incompatible with the nonnegative terminal payoff of an <arbitrage>. Therefore **the bounded-inverse market has no <arbitrage>**.

For the requested <law of one price> counterexample, take a broader continuous-time local-martingale model on $[0,T]$. Let $X$ be a <Brownian motion>, let $\tau=\inf\{u\ge0:X_u=-1\}$, and set $q(t)=t/(T-t)$ for $t<T$. Define
$$
\boxed{B_t=1,\qquad S_t=2+X_{q(t)\wedge\tau}\ (t<T),\qquad S_T=1.}
$$
The given hitting-time fact gives $\tau<\infty$ almost surely. The corresponding physical hitting time is $T\tau/(1+\tau)<T$, so $S$ reaches one and then stays one. In particular it is continuous at maturity and $S_t\ge1$.

For a rigorous closed-horizon localization, stop additionally when $S$ hits $n+2$, or at $T$ if it never does. The stopped process is <Brownian motion> stopped on first exiting $(-1,n)$ and subjected to the deterministic clock; it is a bounded <martingale>, including at $T$. The stopping times increase to $T$, since every Brownian path on the finite random interval $[0,\tau]$ has a finite maximum. Thus $S$ is a positive <local martingale> on the whole horizon. With the time-changed Brownian filtration, the original probability measure is an <equivalent local martingale measure>. The admissible-wealth <supermartingale> argument above proves **no admissible <arbitrage>**.

Nevertheless $S_T=B_T=1$ while $S_0=2\ne B_0=1$, so **the <law of one price> fails**. Shorting one share and buying two units of cash has zero initial cost and terminal gain one, but its wealth is $2-S_t$. It has no deterministic lower bound: for every $n$, the probability of <Brownian motion> hitting $n$ before $-1$ is $1/(n+1)>0$, obtained by optional stopping of the bounded stopped <Brownian motion>. That apparently profitable strategy is therefore inadmissible. The counterexample deliberately uses a <strict local martingale>; the bounded-coefficient class in the first part has true discounted asset <martingales> and does satisfy the <law of one price>.

= Solution

The boundedness of $a'$ makes $a$ globally Lipschitz, so the <stock> <stochastic differential equation> has a nonexplosive strong solution. Since $a$ is bounded, $S_t-S_0=\int_0^ta(S_u)dW_u$ is a square-integrable <martingale> on every finite horizon. Apply <Itô formula> to the option price:
$$
dV(t,S_t)=\left(V_t+\frac12a(S_t)^2V_{SS}\right)dt+a(S_t)V_S(t,S_t)dW_t
=V_S(t,S_t)dS_t.
$$
Thus both the <stock> and the option are <local martingales> under the original probability measure, while cash is constant. The relevant no-arbitrage theorem is the sufficient direction of the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing>: a common equivalent measure under which all numéraire-denominated traded prices are <local martingales> excludes <arbitrage> by <admissible trading strategies>. In this case the original measure itself is an <equivalent local martingale measure>. Hence **the augmented market has no admissible <arbitrage>**. The nonnegative option-value assumption also makes the replicating wealth below admissible.

Set
$$
\boxed{U(t,S)=V_S(t,S),\qquad\pi_t=U(t,S_t).}
$$
Differentiating the pricing equation in the stock-price variable gives
$$
U_t+a(S)a'(S)U_S+\frac12a(S)^2U_{SS}=0.
$$
The limiting terminal derivative is $\mathbf1_{\{S>K\}}$ away from the strike. Its value at the strike may be assigned as the stated $\mathbf1_{\{S\ge K\}}$: the smooth positive diffusion coefficient gives a continuous terminal distribution, so that point has probability zero. This is the <call delta equation for a driftless local volatility diffusion>.

Hold $\pi_t$ shares and $\eta_t=V(t,S_t)-\pi_tS_t$ units of cash. The <portfolio> value is $V(t,S_t)$, and the preceding Itô calculation gives $dV=\pi_t dS_t+\eta_t dB_t$, with $dB_t=0$. It is therefore a <self-financing portfolio>, with initial wealth $V(0,S_0)$ and terminal wealth $(S_T-K)^+$. This proves **replication by the stated <delta hedge>**.

A further application of <Itô formula>, now to $U$, yields
$$
d\pi_t=-a(S_t)a'(S_t)U_S(t,S_t)dt+a(S_t)U_S(t,S_t)dW_t.
$$
The density process is the strictly positive <stochastic exponential>
$$
Z_t=\exp\left(\int_0^ta'(S_u)dW_u-\frac12\int_0^ta'(S_u)^2du\right).
$$
Its integrand is bounded, so the <Novikov condition> proves that it is a true <martingale>. The product formula for $M=Z\pi$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
dM_t&=Z_td\pi_t+\pi_tdZ_t+d[Z,\pi]_t,\\
d[Z,\pi]_t&=Z_ta'(S_t)a(S_t)U_S(t,S_t)dt.
\end{aligned}
$$
The drift in $Z\,d\pi$ cancels this <quadratic covariation> term, leaving
$$
\boxed{dM_t=Z_t\bigl(a(S_t)U_S(t,S_t)+a'(S_t)U(t,S_t)\bigr)dW_t.}
$$
Thus $M$ is a <local martingale>; the expression also identifies its <stochastic integral> integrand explicitly.

Under the stated assumption that $M$ is a true <martingale> on $[0,T]$, its terminal value gives
$$
Z_t\pi_t=\mathbb E[Z_T\mathbf1_{\{S_T\ge K\}}\mid\mathcal F_t].
$$
Since $0\le Z_T\mathbf1_{\{S_T\ge K\}}\le Z_T$ and $\mathbb E[Z_T\mid\mathcal F_t]=Z_t>0$, division gives
$$
\boxed{0\le\pi_t\le1\quad\text{a.s.}}
$$
This is the <derivative-weighted call delta martingale> argument. It uses true martingality through maturity, not just local martingality before the terminal date.

For $v>0$ and $m>0$, let $\Phi$ and $\phi$ be the <standard normal distribution function> and density, and put
$$
d_1=\frac{-\log m+v/2}{\sqrt v},\qquad d_2=\frac{-\log m-v/2}{\sqrt v}=d_1-\sqrt v.
$$
The exercise event is $Z>(\log m+v/2)/\sqrt v=-d_2$. Complete the square in the first truncated expectation:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[e^{-v/2+\sqrt vZ}\mathbf1_{\{Z>-d_2\}}]
&=\int_{-d_2}^\infty e^{-v/2+\sqrt vz}\phi(z)dz\\
&=\int_{-d_2}^\infty\phi(z-\sqrt v)dz=\Phi(d_1).
\end{aligned}
$$
The probability of exercise is $\Phi(d_2)$. Hence the <normalized Black-Scholes call function> is
$$
\boxed{F(v,m)=\Phi(d_1)-m\Phi(d_2).}
$$
At $v=0$ it is $(1-m)^+$. If $m\le0$, the positive underlying always exceeds $m$, so $F(v,m)=1-m$; the reciprocal identity below is understood on its natural domain $m>0$.

For the reciprocal strike, $d_1(v,1/m)=-d_2(v,m)$ and $d_2(v,1/m)=-d_1(v,m)$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
1-m+mF(v,1/m)
&=1-m+m\Phi(-d_2)-\Phi(-d_1)\\
&=1-m+m(1-\Phi(d_2))-(1-\Phi(d_1))\\
&=\Phi(d_1)-m\Phi(d_2)=F(v,m).
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{F(v,m)=1-m+mF(v,1/m),\qquad v\ge0,\ m>0.}
$$
The $v=0$ case follows either by continuity or by checking the two positive parts directly.

For the <stock> model, the original probability measure is already risk-neutral: $e^{-rt}S_t=S_0e^{\sigma W_t-\sigma^2t/2}$ is a true <martingale>. Write $\tau=T-t$. Conditional on $\mathcal F_t$, the <stock> at maturity is
$$
S_T=S_te^{r\tau}\exp\left(-\frac12\sigma^2\tau+\sigma\sqrt\tau Z\right),
$$
with $Z$ independent of current information and <standard normal distribution>. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[e^{-r\tau}(S_T-K)^+\mid\mathcal F_t]=S_tF\left(\sigma^2\tau,\frac{Ke^{-r\tau}}{S_t}\right)=C_t(T,K).
$$
Equivalently,
$$
e^{-rt}C_t=\mathbb E[e^{-rT}(S_T-K)^+\mid\mathcal F_t],
$$
which is a true <martingale>, since the payoff is integrable. The sufficient direction of the <fundamental theorem of asset pricing> says that an equivalent measure making all discounted traded prices <local martingales> excludes <arbitrage> by <admissible trading strategies>. The original measure works simultaneously for the <bank account>, <stock> and call. Thus **the call-augmented market has no <arbitrage>**.

For the put, hold one call, short one share, and hold $Ke^{-rT}$ units of the <bank account>. This <self-financing portfolio> has current value $C_t-S_t+Ke^{-r\tau}$ and terminal payoff
$$
(S_T-K)^+-S_T+K=(K-S_T)^+.
$$
Its discounted value is also the <conditional expectation> of that payoff. Thus the <put-call parity> price
$$
\boxed{P_t=Ke^{-r\tau}-S_t+C_t}
$$
adds another discounted <martingale> to the same market, proving **no <arbitrage> with both call and put included**.

Now put $m=Ke^{-r\tau}/S_t$. The reciprocal identity gives
$$
P_t=S_t(F(\sigma^2\tau,m)-1+m)=S_tmF(\sigma^2\tau,1/m).
$$
The resulting <put-call symmetry in the Black-Scholes model> is
$$
\boxed{P_t(T,K)=Ke^{-r(T-t)}F\left((T-t)\sigma^2,\frac{S_te^{r(T-t)}}K\right).}
$$
**The final formula printed in the PDF has two errors:** its discount-rate symbol is undefined, and the reciprocal argument has the wrong sign in the interest-rate exponential. Both are resolved by the displayed calculation. Even replacing the undefined symbol by $r$ leaves an incorrect expression with $e^{-r\tau}$ inside $F$. For $\tau>0$, $r>0$ and $\sigma>0$, $F(v,m)$ is strictly decreasing in $m$, because $\partial_mF=-\mathbb P(e^{-v/2+\sqrt vZ}>m)<0$. The printed argument is smaller than the reciprocal argument and hence gives a strictly larger put price. Thus the literal printed symmetry cannot be established; the boxed expression is the corrected identity.

= Solution

A unit-face-value <zero-coupon bond> pays one unit of currency at its maturity $T$ and makes no earlier payments. Write its time-$t$ price as $P(t,T)$, with $P(T,T)=1$. The <instantaneous forward rate> is related to the bond price by
$$
\boxed{f_t(T)=-\partial_T\log P(t,T),\qquad P(t,T)=\exp\left(-\int_t^T f_t(u)du\right).}
$$
The <short rate> is $r_t=f_t(t)$, and the money-market account is $B_t=\exp(\int_0^tr_sds)$.

Apply <Itô formula> to $f_t(T)=F(T-t,r_t)$, holding maturity fixed:
$$
df_t(T)=\left(-F_\theta+a(r_t)F_r+\frac12b(r_t)^2F_{rr}\right)dt+b(r_t)F_r\,dW_t.
$$
The supplied equation identifies its volatility and drift as
$$
\sigma_t(T)=b(r_t)F_r(T-t,r_t),\qquad\alpha_t(T)=\sigma_t(T)\int_t^T\sigma_t(u)du.
$$
This is exactly the one-factor <Heath-Jarrow-Morton model> drift restriction with zero market price of risk. The initial condition also verifies $f_t(t)=F(0,r_t)=r_t$.

To verify absence of <arbitrage> directly, without merely naming the drift restriction, put
$$
G(\theta,r)=\int_0^\theta F(s,r)ds,\qquad\Sigma_t(T)=b(r_t)G_r(T-t,r_t).
$$
Integrate the equation in its first variable. Since $F(0,r)=r$ and
$$
\int_0^\theta F_r(s,r)\left(\int_0^sF_r(u,r)du\right)ds=\frac12G_r(\theta,r)^2,
$$
we obtain
$$
F(\theta,r)-r=a(r)G_r+\frac12b(r)^2G_{rr}-\frac12b(r)^2G_r^2.
$$
The bond price from the forward curve is $P(t,T)=e^{-G(T-t,r_t)}$. Its logarithm therefore satisfies
$$
d\log P(t,T)=\left(F-aG_r-\frac12b^2G_{rr}\right)dt-bG_r\,dW_t
=\left(r_t-\frac12\Sigma_t(T)^2\right)dt-\Sigma_t(T)dW_t.
$$
A second application of <Itô formula> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dP(t,T)}{P(t,T)}=r_tdt-\Sigma_t(T)dW_t,\qquad\frac{d(P(t,T)/B_t)}{P(t,T)/B_t}=-\Sigma_t(T)dW_t.}
$$
Thus every discounted bond price is a positive <local martingale> under the original measure. The sufficient no-arbitrage theorem is: if a common equivalent probability measure makes all discounted traded prices <local martingales>, then there is no <arbitrage> using <self-financing strategies> whose discounted wealth has a deterministic lower bound. Its proof is the <supermartingale> bound on such wealth. Apply it to any finite collection of bond maturities, using the original measure itself. This establishes **no admissible <arbitrage>** in the bond market, on horizons where the stated <short rate> model and smooth forward curve are defined. It is the <forward-rate equation for a Markov short-rate diffusion> construction.

For the constant-coefficient case, substitute $F(\theta,r)=A(\theta)r+B(\theta)$. The initial condition requires $A(0)=1$ and $B(0)=0$. Since $F_r=A$ and $F_{rr}=0$, the equation becomes
$$
A'(\theta)r+B'(\theta)=a_0A(\theta)-b_0^2A(\theta)\int_0^\theta A(s)ds.
$$
The right-hand side does not depend on $r$, so $A'=0$ and $A=1$. It follows that $B'=a_0-b_0^2\theta$, giving
$$
\boxed{A(\theta)=1,\qquad B(\theta)=a_0\theta-\frac12b_0^2\theta^2,\qquad f_t(T)=r_t+a_0(T-t)-\frac12b_0^2(T-t)^2.}
$$
The corresponding <zero-coupon bond> price is
$$
\boxed{P(t,T)=\exp\left(-(T-t)r_t-\frac{a_0}2(T-t)^2+\frac{b_0^2}6(T-t)^3\right).}
$$
It has discounted volatility $-b_0(T-t)$, which is deterministic and square-integrable on finite horizons. Its discounted price is consequently a true <martingale>, so the no-arbitrage conclusion follows again. This is the <Gaussian short-rate model with constant coefficients>.

As a separate check of the signs, conditional on $\mathcal F_t$,
$$
\int_t^T r_sds=(T-t)r_t+\frac{a_0}2(T-t)^2+b_0\int_t^T(W_s-W_t)ds.
$$
The final integral is centered Gaussian with variance $(T-t)^3/3$, obtained by integrating $\operatorname{Cov}(W_s-W_t,W_u-W_t)=\min(s-t,u-t)$. Its negative exponential expectation gives exactly the positive $b_0^2(T-t)^3/6$ term in the bond-price exponent above.
