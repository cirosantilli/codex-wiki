{title2=Linear Algebra}

= Solution
{parent=1G}

The <monomials> $x^iy^j$ with $i,j\ge0$ and $i+j\le n$ form a <basis> of the given <vector space>. There are $k+1$ of <total degree> $k$, so
$$
\boxed{\dim V=\sum_{k=0}^n(k+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2.}
$$
On that <basis> the <linear operator> is diagonal:
$$
S(x^iy^j)=\bigl(i(i-1)+j(j-1)\bigr)x^iy^j.
$$
Both summands of the <eigenvalue> are nonnegative integers. Their sum is zero precisely when $i,j\in\{0,1\}$. Thus **the <kernel> is the span of those members of $1,x,y,xy$ whose <total degree> is at most $n$**. In particular it is $\operatorname{span}\{1,x,y\}$ for $n=1$ and $\operatorname{span}\{1,x,y,xy\}$ for $n\ge2$; for $n=0$ it is the constants. **The <image of a linear map> is the span of all the other permitted <monomials>**, since each has a nonzero diagonal <eigenvalue> and hence is the image of its reciprocal multiple. No excluded <monomial> can occur in an image because its diagonal coefficient is zero.

The <trace> is the sum of these <eigenvalues>. At degrees zero and one all contributions vanish. At degree two the contributions are $2,0,2$, summing to four; at degree three they are $6,2,2,6$, summing to sixteen; at degree four they are $12,6,4,6,12$, summing to forty. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}S=0,4,20,60\quad\text{for }n=1,2,3,4\text{ respectively}.}
$$
This is the <diagonal monomial action of a second-order Euler operator>.

{title2=Groups, Rings and Modules}

= Solution
{parent=2F}

For a finite <group> of order $p^am$ with $p$ prime and $p\nmid m$, the <Sylow theorems> assert that a <subgroup> of order $p^a$ exists; every <subgroup> whose order is a power of $p$ is contained in one such <Sylow subgroup>; all <Sylow subgroups> for that prime are conjugate; and their number $n_p$ divides $m$ and is congruent to one modulo $p$. A unique <Sylow subgroup> is a <normal subgroup>, since conjugation cannot move it to another one.

Here $56=7\cdot8$, so $n_7$ divides eight and is congruent to one modulo seven. Hence $n_7=1$ or $8$. In the first case the <Sylow subgroup> of order seven is normal. In the second case, any two distinct order-seven <subgroups> intersect only at the identity, because a nontrivial intersection would generate both prime-order <subgroups>. Their nonidentity elements therefore account for $8\cdot6=48$ elements.

Exactly eight elements of the <group> remain. Every <Sylow subgroup> of order eight consists entirely of these remaining elements: a nonidentity element in it has order a power of two and cannot be one of the order-seven elements already counted. Each such <Sylow subgroup> must consequently equal the remaining eight-element set. It is unique and hence normal. Thus **a <group> of order 56 has a <normal subgroup> of order seven or of order eight**.

= Solution
{parent=3E}

The <contraction mapping theorem> states that a <contraction mapping> $T:X\to X$ on a nonempty <complete metric space> has a unique <fixed point>. Here contraction means that some $0\le q<1$ satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y)$ for all $x,y$.

For a proof, choose $x_0$ and iterate $x_{r+1}=Tx_r$. Induction gives $d(x_{r+1},x_r)\le q^rd(x_1,x_0)$. For $m>n$, the triangle inequality and a <geometric series> give
$$
d(x_m,x_n)\le\sum_{r=n}^{m-1}q^rd(x_1,x_0)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).
$$
Thus the iterates form a <Cauchy sequence>, and <completeness> supplies a limit $a\in X$. A <contraction mapping> is continuous, so $Ta=\lim Tx_n=\lim x_{n+1}=a$. If $b$ is another <fixed point>, $d(a,b)=d(Ta,Tb)\le qd(a,b)$, forcing $d(a,b)=0$. This proves existence and uniqueness.

For the exponential problem, use $h=f\circ f$ on the complete <metric space> $\mathbb R$. Its <derivative> is
$$
h'(x)=e^{-x}e^{-e^{-x}}.
$$
Putting $t=e^{-x}>0$, the function $te^{-t}$ has maximum $1/e$ at $t=1$, as its <derivative> is $(1-t)e^{-t}$. By the <mean value theorem>, $|h(x)-h(y)|\le e^{-1}|x-y|$, so $h$ is a <contraction mapping> on all of $\mathbb R$. Let $a$ be its unique <fixed point>. The compositions commute, so $h(f(a))=f(h(a))=f(a)$; uniqueness of the <fixed point> of $h$ forces $f(a)=a$. Conversely every <fixed point> of $f$ is fixed by $h$. Hence **there is exactly one real solution of $x=e^{-x}$**. It lies in $(0,1)$ because $a=e^{-a}>0$ and then $e^{-a}<1$. This uses the <iterated contraction> argument without claiming that $f$ itself contracts on all of $\mathbb R$.

{title2=Metric and Topological Spaces}

= Solution
{parent=4F}

A <Hausdorff space> is a <topological space> in which any two distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods. Let $K$ be a <compact subset> of a <Hausdorff space> and $x\notin K$. For each $y\in K$, choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U_y$ of $y$ and $W_y$ of $x$. The $U_y$ cover $K$, so <compactness> gives a finite subcover $U_{y_1},\ldots,U_{y_m}$. Their paired intersection $W=\bigcap_{r=1}^mW_{y_r}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$ disjoint from $K$. Thus every point of its complement has an open neighborhood in the complement, proving **every <compact subset of a Hausdorff space> is closed**. The empty set case is immediate.

In the <cocountable topology> on an uncountable set, any two nonempty open sets intersect: their combined complements are countable and cannot exhaust $X$. Thus **the space is not <Hausdorff>**.

Nevertheless **every <compact subset> is closed** here. In fact only finite subsets are compact. To prove this, suppose $K$ is infinite and choose distinct points $d_1,d_2,\ldots\in K$. The open sets
$$
U_n=X\setminus\{d_m:m\ge n\}
$$
form an increasing <open cover> of $X$, and hence of $K$. Any finite selection is contained in $U_N$ for its largest index $N$, which misses $d_N\in K$. So $K$ is not compact. Finite sets are compact in any <topological space>, and in this <cocountable topology> they are closed because their complements are open. This proves the claimed behavior of <compact subsets of a cocountable space>.

= Solution
{parent=5B}

The <Fourier sine series> coefficients of $x$ on $(0,\pi)$ are
$$
b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n,
$$
by <integration by parts>. The odd periodic extension is piecewise smooth, so the <Fourier series> converges to $x$ at every interior point. Hence
$$
\boxed{x=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx),\qquad 0<x<\pi.}
$$
To justify integration from zero, the <Fourier sine series> partial sums converge to $x$ in <L2 norm>. By the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, integration from zero to any $x\in[0,\pi]$ has error at most $\sqrt\pi$ times that <L2 norm> error, uniformly in $x$. Integrating the partial sums and taking the limit therefore gives
$$
\frac{x^2}{2}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}(1-\cos nx).
$$
The integrated <series> is absolutely and uniformly convergent, since its terms are bounded by $4/n^2$. Thus the resulting <Fourier cosine series> has
$$
\boxed{a_n=\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\quad(n\ge1),\qquad a_0=8\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^2}.}
$$
Independently, the constant coefficient is
$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2\,dx=\frac{2\pi^2}{3}.
$$
Comparison yields the requested exact sum
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}.}
$$

In SI units, <Gauss's law> relates the outward <electric flux> through a closed surface to its enclosed charge:
$$
\boxed{\int_S\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}.}
$$
The outward unit normal specifies the sign of the <electric flux>.

= Solution

The uniform <charge density> is $\rho_e=3Q/(4\pi R^3)$. Spherical symmetry makes the <electric field> radial and constant in magnitude on each concentric sphere. A sphere of radius $r<R$ encloses charge $Q(r/R)^3$. <Gauss's law> therefore gives $4\pi r^2E_r=Qr^3/(\varepsilon_0R^3)$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}\mathbf r,\qquad |\mathbf r|<R.}
$$
This also defines the zero <electric field> at the centre by continuity.

= b
{parent=6a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take the upper hemisphere to have $z>0$. Integrate the force $\mathbf E\,dq$ using the total interior <electric field> from part (a). This computes the net external force on the upper hemisphere, since the forces between pairs of its own charge elements cancel by antisymmetry of the Coulomb force. Horizontal components cancel by symmetry. The vertical component is
$$
F_z=\frac{\rho_e^2}{3\varepsilon_0}\int_{z>0,\,r<R}z\,dV.
$$
A horizontal section at height $z$ has area $\pi(R^2-z^2)$, so
$$
\int z\,dV=\pi\int_0^R z(R^2-z^2)\,dz=\frac{\pi R^4}{4}.
$$
Thus **the lower hemisphere repels the upper one vertically upward**, with
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=\frac{\pi\rho_e^2R^4}{12\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf z}=\frac{3Q^2}{64\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The result is the same for either sign of $Q$, as expected for the <force between hemispheres of a uniformly charged ball>.

{title2=Special Relativity}

= Solution
{parent=7C}

For a standard collinear <Lorentz transformation>, put $\beta=v/c$ and $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$, with $|v|<c$. Then $x'=\gamma(x-vt)$ and $ct'=\gamma(ct-\beta x)$. Define the <rapidity> $\phi=\operatorname{artanh}\beta$. The identities $\cosh^2\phi-\sinh^2\phi=1$ and $\tanh\phi=\beta$, with $\cosh\phi>0$, give $\cosh\phi=\gamma$ and $\sinh\phi=\gamma\beta$. Substitution proves the desired hyperbolic form.

Adding and subtracting those transformed coordinates uses $\cosh\phi\mp\sinh\phi=e^{\mp\phi}$ and yields
$$
\boxed{x'+ct'=e^{-\phi}(x+ct),\qquad x'-ct'=e^{\phi}(x-ct).}
$$
A second collinear <Lorentz transformation> of <rapidity> $\phi'$ multiplies these two null coordinates by $e^{-\phi'}$ and $e^{\phi'}$. Their total factors are therefore $e^{-(\phi+\phi')}$ and $e^{\phi+\phi'}$, proving
$$
\boxed{\phi''=\phi+\phi'.}
$$
The hyperbolic tangent addition formula now gives the velocity composition law
$$
\boxed{v''=c\tanh(\phi+\phi')=\frac{v+v'}{1+vv'/c^2}.}
$$
All velocities here are signed along the same axis; the speed is the absolute value if either frame moves in the opposite direction.

= Solution
{parent=8D}

Use the ideal steady incompressible-flow model, neglecting height change across the smooth nozzle. <Flow conservation> gives inlet and outlet speeds $u_0=Q/A_0$ and $u_1=Q/A_1$. Set atmospheric pressure to zero. The <Bernoulli equation> between inlet and outlet gives
$$
\boxed{p_0=\frac\rho2(u_1^2-u_0^2)=\frac{\rho Q^2}{2}\left(\frac1{A_1^2}-\frac1{A_0^2}\right).}
$$
Choose a control volume containing the fluid in the nozzle, with the positive axis pointing toward the outlet. The <integral momentum equation> gives
$$
p_0A_0+F_{\mathrm{wall\ on\ fluid}}=\rho Q(u_1-u_0).
$$
Thus the fluid's force on the nozzle is $p_0A_0-\rho Q(u_1-u_0)$, in the outlet direction. Substitute the pressure and velocities:
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{\mathrm{fluid\ on\ nozzle}}
&=\rho Q^2\left[\frac{A_0}{2A_1^2}-\frac1{A_1}+\frac1{2A_0}\right]\\
&=\frac{\rho Q^2}{2A_0}\left(\frac{A_0}{A_1}-1\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The fireman's holding force is equal and opposite. Therefore **the required force points upstream and has magnitude**
$$
\boxed{F=\frac{\rho Q^2}{2A_0}\left(\frac{A_0}{A_1}-1\right)^2.}
$$
This is the <holding force on a contracting nozzle>; retaining the inlet pressure force is essential to its sign and value.

Label the towns $L,M,R$ from left to right, and the treatment plants $A,B,C,D$ as upper-left, upper-right, lower-left and lower-right. Model each <treatment capacity as a sink arc> to the sea. Incoming untreated sewage can be forwarded, so the numbers inside the plant circles constrain treatment, not total throughput.

The sum of treatment capacities is $16+20+14+12=62$, so no feasible <flow network> can handle more than 62. Here is a feasible routing attaining that upper bound. Send town inflows
$$
L\to A:6,\quad L\to C:9;\qquad M\to A:5,\quad M\to B:7,\quad M\to C:10,\quad M\to D:3;\qquad R\to B:11,\quad R\to D:11.
$$
Every town-to-plant flow respects its directed pipe capacity. Send an additional five units from $C$ to $A$ through their bidirectional pipe, whose capacity is twelve, and two from $D$ to $B$ through their bidirectional pipe, whose capacity is fourteen. Use neither horizontal plant-to-plant pipe.

Now $A$ receives $6+5+5=16$ and treats all of it. Plant $C$ receives $9+10=19$, forwards five and treats fourteen. Plant $B$ receives $7+11+2=20$ and treats all of it. Plant $D$ receives $3+11=14$, forwards two and treats twelve. Thus <flow conservation> holds at every plant and all treatment capacities are saturated. The routing proves the upper bound is achievable:
$$
\boxed{\text{maximum total}=62,\qquad (L,M,R)=(15,25,22).}
$$
This is one optimal assignment, not a uniqueness assertion. The total bound comes from the cut consisting of all treatment-to-sea arcs; the explicit routing supplies a matching <maximum flow> certificate.

= Solution

The ascending chain $\ker T\subseteq\ker T^2\subseteq\cdots$ must stabilize because $V$ is finite-dimensional. Once $\ker T^l=\ker T^{l+1}$, it stays stable: if $T^{l+2}v=0$, then $Tv\in\ker T^{l+1}=\ker T^l$, so $v\in\ker T^{l+1}=\ker T^l$; induction repeats the argument. Choose a positive such $l$, giving $\ker T^{2l}=\ker T^l$.

If $v\in\ker T^l\cap\operatorname{im}T^l$, write $v=T^lw$. Then $T^{2l}w=0$, so $w\in\ker T^l$ and $v=0$. By the <rank-nullity theorem>, the dimensions of those two subspaces sum to $\dim V$. Hence
$$
\boxed{V=\ker T^l\oplus\operatorname{im}T^l.}
$$
This proves the finite-dimensional form of the <Fitting lemma>.

If $\det T=0$, the <kernel> contains a nonzero <vector> $u$, and $\operatorname{im}T$ is a proper <vector subspace>. Extend a <basis> of that <image of a linear map> to a <basis> of $V$ and choose a nonzero <linear functional> $\lambda$ which vanishes on the former <basis>. Define $S(v)=\lambda(v)u$. It is a nonzero <endomorphism>, since $\lambda$ takes a nonzero value somewhere, and
$$
TS(v)=\lambda(v)Tu=0,\qquad ST(v)=\lambda(Tv)u=0.
$$
Thus **a <singular endomorphism has a nonzero two-sided annihilator>**, as required.

For an <idempotent linear map> $T$, the decomposition $v=(v-Tv)+Tv$ shows $V=\ker T\oplus\operatorname{im}T$: the first term is killed by $T$, the second is fixed, and a <vector> both fixed and killed is zero. An adapted <basis> makes its <matrix> $\operatorname{diag}(0,I_r)$, with $r=\operatorname{rank}T$. Consequently equal ranks give identical adapted <matrices> and therefore a change of <basis> relating $T_1,T_2$. Conversely, similar <matrices> have the same rank because pre- and postmultiplication by <invertible matrices> preserve rank. Hence **$T_1$ and $T_2$ are similar exactly when their ranks agree**, proving the <similarity classification of idempotent linear maps>.

= Solution

The <centre of a group> is $Z(G)=\{z\in G:zg=gz\text{ for every }g\in G\}$. It is a <normal subgroup>. For $|G|=p^r$ with $r\ge1$, let $G$ act on itself by conjugation. The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> makes the size of the conjugacy class of $g$ equal to $[G:C_G(g)]$, a power of $p$. A class has size one exactly when $g\in Z(G)$; every other class size is divisible by $p$. The <class equation> therefore gives
$$
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{\text{noncentral classes}}[G:C_G(g)],
$$
so $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$. Since the identity belongs to the <centre of a group>, its size is a positive multiple of $p$. This proves the <nontrivial center of a finite p-group>.

If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, choose a representative $a$ of a generator. Every element is $a^mz$ for an integer $m$ and $z\in Z(G)$. Any two such elements commute, since their central factors commute with everything and their remaining factors are powers of the same element. Thus $G$ is an <abelian group>, $Z(G)=G$, and **the cyclic quotient by the <centre of a group> is trivial**.

For a non-<abelian group> of order $p^3$, the <centre of a group> has order $p$, $p^2$ or $p^3$ by the proved result and <Lagrange's theorem>. The last would make the <group> abelian. The middle would make $G/Z(G)$ have prime order and hence cyclic, also making $G$ abelian by the preceding argument. Therefore **its centre has order $p$**.

Finally write the given upper-unitriangular <matrix> as $M(a,b,c)$. Direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
M(a,b,c)M(a',b',c')=M(a+a',\,b+b'+ac',\,c+c').
$$
It commutes with every $M(a',b',c')$ exactly when $ac'=a'c$ for all $a',c'\in F$. Taking $(a',c')=(0,1)$ forces $a=0$, and taking $(1,0)$ forces $c=0$; those conditions also suffice. Thus the <centre of a group> in this <Heisenberg group over a prime field> is
$$
\boxed{Z(G)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&b\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}:b\in F\right\}.}
$$

For <stereographic projection>, choose the north pole $N=(0,0,1)$ of the <unit sphere> and identify the plane $Z=0$ with the <complex plane> by $z=X+iY$. Project a point $P=(X,Y,Z)\ne N$ along the line joining it to $N$; adjoin $z=\infty$ for the north pole to identify the whole <sphere> with the <Riemann sphere>. A point of that line is $N+t(P-N)$. Its last coordinate vanishes when $t=1/(1-Z)$, giving
$$
\boxed{z=\frac{X+iY}{1-Z}.}
$$
Using $X^2+Y^2+Z^2=1$, or solving this line's second intersection with the <sphere>, gives the inverse
$$
\boxed{X=\frac{2\operatorname{Re}z}{1+|z|^2},\quad Y=\frac{2\operatorname{Im}z}{1+|z|^2},\quad Z=\frac{|z|^2-1}{1+|z|^2}.}
$$
A <great circle> lies in a plane $h_1X+h_2Y+h_3Z=0$ through the origin. Its projected equation is
$$
2h_1\operatorname{Re}z+2h_2\operatorname{Im}z+h_3(|z|^2-1)=0.
$$
It is a straight line only if $h_3=0$, meaning the plane contains the north pole and the south pole. Thus if all three sides of a nondegenerate <spherical triangle> projected to straight sides of a Euclidean triangle, all three distinct side planes would contain both poles. Each pair of side planes could then meet on the <sphere> only at those same two poles, which cannot supply three distinct triangle vertices. **A nondegenerate <spherical triangle> cannot project to a Euclidean triangle.** If a side passes through the projection pole, its image is unbounded and also cannot be a finite triangle side.

A nonidentity <rotation in three dimensions> has an axis through the origin: an orthogonal three-by-three <matrix> of determinant one has an <eigenvalue> one, since nonreal <eigenvalues> pair as conjugates, real <eigenvalues> are $\pm1$, and their product is one. Its axis meets the <unit sphere> in an <antipodal pair>, which is fixed. In the identity case every point is fixed, so an <antipodal pair> can again be selected. Projecting $P$ and $-P$ gives
$$
p=\frac{X+iY}{1-Z},\qquad q=-\frac{X+iY}{1+Z},\qquad p\overline q=-\frac{X^2+Y^2}{1-Z^2}=-1
$$
whenever both points are finite. Thus the associated <Möbius transformation> fixes an <antipodal pair> satisfying the claimed relation. **The universally valid relation is $q=-1/\overline p$ on the <Riemann sphere>**, with $0$ and $\infty$ paired. A rotation about the projection axis fixes exactly that pair, so the PDF's literal finite product needs this extended-coordinate convention. This is the <antipodal stereographic coordinate relation>.

= Solution

Reflection of the <sphere> in the plane $Y=0$ sends $(X,Y,Z)$ to $(X,-Y,Z)$. It preserves <dot products>, hence the <spherical distance> between every pair of points. Its stereographic expression is
$$
\boxed{z\longmapsto\overline z.}
$$
This is an <isometry> of the <sphere> but is not a <Möbius transformation>: at any finite point, its complex difference quotient is one along real increments and minus one along imaginary increments, so it is not <holomorphic>. A <Möbius transformation> is <holomorphic> away from its pole. The geometric distinction is that this reflection reverses orientation, whereas a <Möbius transformation> preserves it.

= Solution
{parent=13E}

A subset $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is an <open set> when every $x\in U$ has some radius $r>0$ for which $B(x,r)\subseteq U$. If a <continuous function> takes only the values zero and one, continuity at $x$ with tolerance $1/2$ gives a neighborhood on which $|f(y)-f(x)|<1/2$. The two possible values differ by one, so $f(y)=f(x)$ throughout that neighborhood. Thus $f$ is <locally constant>, and the zero <linear map> is its <derivative>, since the difference $f(x+h)-f(x)$ is exactly zero for all sufficiently small $h$. Hence **$Df_x=0$ everywhere**.

For the bound on $g$, parametrize the segment by $\gamma(t)=b+t(a-b)$, $0\le t\le1$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
(g\circ\gamma)'(t)=(Dg)_{\gamma(t)}(a-b),\qquad |(g\circ\gamma)'(t)|\le M\|a-b\|
$$
by the definition of <operator norm>. The <mean value theorem> applied to the real function $g\circ\gamma$ proves
$$
\boxed{|g(a)-g(b)|\le M\|a-b\|.}
$$
If the endpoints agree the estimate is immediate. No continuity of the <derivative> is required.

For the finite-line complement, each removed line $\ell_i$ is closed, so $V$ is open. Because $a,b\notin\ell_i$, there is a unique affine plane $P_i$ containing $a$ and $\ell_i$, and a unique affine plane $Q_i$ containing $b$ and $\ell_i$. Choose $c$ outside the union of these finitely many planes, using the permitted fact about finite plane covers. In particular $c\in V$. If $[a,c]$ met $\ell_i$ at a point $d$, then $d\ne a$, and the line through $a,d$ lies in $P_i$. Since $c$ is on that same line, it would belong to $P_i$, a contradiction. The same argument using $Q_i$ applies to $[c,b]$. Thus **$a$ and $b$ can be joined in $V$ by these two segments**, proving <polygonal connectivity of a finite-line complement>.

Finally a continuous $f:V\to\{0,1\}$ has zero <derivative> by the first argument. Apply the proved segment estimate with $M=0$ separately on $[a,c]$ and $[c,b]$. It gives $f(a)=f(c)=f(b)$. Since $a,b$ were arbitrary, **$f$ is constant on $V$**.

For the <Joukowski map> $F(z)=z+b^2/z$, the <derivative> is $F'(z)=1-b^2/z^2$. Thus it is a <conformal map> at every finite $z$ except its pole at zero and its critical points
$$
\boxed{z=\pm b,\qquad \zeta=F(z)=\pm2b.}
$$
The pole maps to $\infty$; as a map of <Riemann spheres> it is a simple pole and is not a branch point. Neither is the point at infinity, where the map behaves like $z$.

An appropriate domain is the upper exterior half-disc
$$
\boxed{D=\{z:\operatorname{Im}z>0,\ |z|>b\}.}
$$
For $z=x+iy$, $\operatorname{Im}F(z)=y(1-b^2/|z|^2)>0$ on $D$. To prove surjectivity and injectivity, solve $F(z)=\zeta$ as $z^2-\zeta z+b^2=0$. Its two roots have product $b^2$. For $\operatorname{Im}\zeta>0$, neither root lies on the real axis or on $|z|=b$, since either boundary maps to the real axis. Exactly one root therefore has modulus greater than $b$. The imaginary-part identity forces that root to have positive imaginary part. It is the unique root in $D$, so **$F$ maps $D$ conformally and bijectively onto the upper half-plane**.

For small transverse displacement, the <wave equation on a string> is
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c^2=T/\rho,}
$$
where $T$ is the tension and $\rho$ is mass per unit length. Using <separation of variables>, put $y=X(x)F(t)$, the ratios $X''/X=F''/(c^2F)$ equal a constant. The fixed-end conditions $X(0)=X(L)=0$ exclude nontrivial solutions for nonnegative separation constants; for $-\kappa^2$ they give $X=\sin(\kappa x)$ and $\kappa=n\pi/L$, $n\ge1$. The associated angular frequencies are $\omega_n=n\pi c/L$. Thus the general superposition of separated <normal modes> is
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\sum_{n\ge1}\left[A_n\cos(\omega_nt)+B_n\sin(\omega_nt)\right]\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}.}
$$
The string is initially straight and at rest before the impulse, so $A_n=0$. Assume the hammer interval lies inside $[0,L]$. The imposed velocity $g(x)=y_t(x,0)$ has <Fourier sine series> coefficient
$$
g_n=\frac{2v}{L}\int_{l-a/2}^{l+a/2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx
=\frac{4v}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi l}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi a}{2L}.
$$
Therefore $B_n=g_n/\omega_n$, giving the amplitude of every excited <normal mode>.

The string energy is $\frac\rho2\int_0^L(y_t^2+c^2y_x^2)\,dx$. At the impulse it is $E_{\mathrm{total}}=\rho av^2/2$. Orthogonality of the sine modes gives $E_n=\rho Lg_n^2/4$ initially; each mode subsequently exchanges kinetic and elastic energy while retaining that total. Thus the <modal energy of a localized velocity impulse on a string> has fractions
$$
\boxed{\frac{E_n}{E_{\mathrm{total}}}=\frac{8L}{a\pi^2n^2}\sin^2\frac{n\pi l}{L}\sin^2\frac{n\pi a}{2L}.}
$$
For $l=L/3$ and $a=L/10$, a mode is absent exactly when $\sin(n\pi/3)=0$ or $\sin(n\pi/20)=0$. Hence **the unexcited modes are precisely**
$$
\boxed{n\in3\mathbb N_{>0}\ \cup\ 20\mathbb N_{>0}.}
$$

{title2=Quantum Mechanics}

= Solution
{parent=16B}

For a one-dimensional <wavefunction>, the <probability density> and <probability current> are
$$
\rho=|\psi|^2,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\psi_x-\psi\psi_x^*\right)=\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi_x).
$$
The <time-dependent Schrödinger equation> with a real potential is $i\hbar\psi_t=-\hbar^2\psi_{xx}/(2m)+V\psi$. Multiply it and its <complex conjugate> by $\psi^*$ and $\psi$, respectively. Subtracting cancels the real-potential terms and gives
$$
\rho_t=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\psi^*\psi_{xx}-\psi\psi_{xx}^*)=-j_x.
$$
This derives the <continuity equation> $\rho_t+j_x=0$.

For $k>0$, the specified free-region <wavefunction> combines a right-moving incoming wave of unit amplitude and a left-moving reflected wave with complex reflection amplitude $R$. Applying the free Schrödinger operator to either plane wave gives $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. Its time factor has modulus one, so
$$
\rho=1+|R|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(Re^{-2ikx}),\qquad \rho_t=0,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar k}{m}(1-|R|^2).
$$
The density includes the stationary interference pattern; $|R|^2$, rather than $R$, is the reflection probability.

For the <finite square well>, let $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ outside and $q=\sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}/\hbar$ inside. Write the stationary parts as $e^{ikx}+Re^{-ikx}$ to the left, $Ae^{iqx}+Be^{-iqx}$ inside, and $Te^{ikx}$ to the right. At both finite potential steps, the <wavefunction> and its first spatial <derivative> are continuous. Integrating the stationary <Schrödinger equation> across a shrinking step proves derivative continuity; a jump of the <wavefunction> would create an unmatched distributional derivative and is excluded.

Propagate the boundary values at $a$ back through the well. The values at zero are
$$
\chi(0)=Te^{ika}\left(\cos qa-i\frac kq\sin qa\right),\qquad
\chi'(0)=Te^{ika}\left(q\sin qa+ik\cos qa\right).
$$
Matching them to $1+R$ and $ik(1-R)$, and adding the two equations after division of the second by $ik$, gives
$$
2=Te^{ika}\left[2\cos qa-i\left(\frac kq+\frac qk\right)\sin qa\right].
$$
Since the exterior wave numbers agree, the <transmission probability> is the current ratio $|T|^2$, so
$$
|T|^2=\left[1+\frac{(q^2-k^2)^2}{4k^2q^2}\sin^2qa\right]^{-1}.
$$
When $V_0=3E$, $q=2k=\sqrt{8mE}/\hbar$, yielding
$$
\boxed{|T|^2=\left[1+\frac9{16}\sin^2\frac{a\sqrt{8mE}}{\hbar}\right]^{-1}.}
$$
Classically an incoming particle of positive energy crosses the attractive well and emerges with its original energy, so transmission is one. The quantum result agrees exactly when $qa$ is an integer multiple of $\pi$:
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{n\pi\hbar}{\sqrt{8mE}},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
These are widths with <resonant transmission through a square well>; the degenerate width zero also has unit transmission.

= Solution
{parent=17A}

The vacuum <Maxwell equations> are $\nabla\cdot E=\nabla\cdot B=0$, $\nabla\times E=-B_t$ and $\nabla\times B=E_t/c^2$. Taking the curl of Faraday's equation and using $\nabla\times(\nabla\times E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot E)-\nabla^2E$ gives $-\nabla^2E=-E_{tt}/c^2$. The same calculation for Ampère's equation gives $\nabla^2B=B_{tt}/c^2$. Hence **each Cartesian component satisfies the <electromagnetic wave equation>**.

Model the walls as a <perfect conductor>, with no oscillating fields in its interior. The tangential <electric field> is zero at the surface, otherwise it would drive unbounded conductor currents. The normal <magnetic field> is zero, since the divergence-free field has a continuous normal component and the interior oscillating field is zero. Thus $n\times E=0$ and $n\cdot B=0$. Normal $E$ and tangential $B$ may be nonzero because surface charge and surface current support their jumps.

The PDF writes the full <magnetic field> as $B=B_0(x,y)\hat z e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$. Taken literally, this cannot be a nonzero travelling wave: $\nabla\cdot B=ikB_z$, so for $k\ne0$ it forces $B_z=0$. The subsequent requests require transverse components. The consistent intended reading is **$B_z=B_0(x,y)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$**, with transverse fields determined by the <Maxwell equations>. Use that reading below.

All fields have the same $z,t$ factor. Put $\Delta=(\omega/c)^2-k^2$. The harmonic <Maxwell equations> are $\nabla\times E=i\omega B$ and $\nabla\times B=-i\omega E/c^2$. Their $x$ and $y$ components give
$$
E_y=-\frac{c^2k}{\omega}B_x-\frac{ic^2}{\omega}\partial_xB_z,\qquad
E_x=\frac{c^2k}{\omega}B_y+\frac{ic^2}{\omega}\partial_yB_z,
$$
together with $\partial_yE_z-ikE_y=i\omega B_x$ and $ikE_x-\partial_xE_z=i\omega B_y$. Substitution and solving, for $\Delta\ne0$, gives
$$
\boxed{B_x=\frac i\Delta\left(k\partial_xB_z-\frac\omega{c^2}\partial_yE_z\right),\qquad
B_y=\frac i\Delta\left(k\partial_yB_z+\frac\omega{c^2}\partial_xE_z\right).}
$$
The corresponding electric components are
$$
E_x=\frac i\Delta(k\partial_xE_z+\omega\partial_yB_z),\qquad
E_y=\frac i\Delta(k\partial_yE_z-\omega\partial_xB_z).
$$
For a <transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide>, $E_z=0$. The <electromagnetic wave equation> becomes
$$
(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\Delta)B_0=0.
$$
At $x=0,a$, the tangential component $E_y=-i\omega\partial_xB_z/\Delta$ vanishes, so $\partial_xB_0=0$; at $y=0,b$, the corresponding condition $E_x=0$ gives $\partial_yB_0=0$. These are <Neumann boundary conditions>, and the normal-$B$ conditions give the same conclusions through the displayed transverse magnetic components. Separation gives
$$
B_0=C_{mn}\cos\frac{m\pi x}{a}\cos\frac{n\pi y}{b},\qquad
\Delta=\kappa_{mn}^2=\left(\frac{m\pi}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^2,
$$
where $m,n\ge0$ are integers and $(m,n)\ne(0,0)$. The constant candidate is not a physical oscillating mode: its transverse derivatives vanish, so it has $E=0$ and Faraday's equation forces $B=0$ for $\omega\ne0$. More generally a hollow single-conductor guide has no transverse-electromagnetic zero-cutoff mode: its transverse electrostatic potential is harmonic with the same constant boundary value, hence constant, leaving zero transverse field.

The dispersion relation is $\omega^2=c^2(k^2+\kappa_{mn}^2)$. The <waveguide cutoff frequency> for this mode is $\omega_{mn}=c\kappa_{mn}$; below it $k$ is imaginary and the field is evanescent. Since $a\ge b$, the lowest cutoff occurs at $(m,n)=(1,0)$:
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{\pi c}{a},\qquad f_c=\frac c{2a}.}
$$
For a mode propagating in the positive $z$ direction ($k>0$), above its own cutoff $\omega_{mn}$, the <phase velocity> and <group velocity> are
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac\omega k=\frac c{\sqrt{1-(\omega_{mn}/\omega)^2}}>c,\qquad
v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=c\sqrt{1-(\omega_{mn}/\omega)^2}<c.}
$$
These <waveguide phase and group velocities> have product $c^2$. The superluminal phase speed describes motion of a constant phase, while a narrow wave packet travels at the subluminal <group velocity>; it does not transmit information faster than light. At cutoff $k=0$ there is no travelling mode with nonzero longitudinal flux.

A random <vector> $X$ has <multivariate normal distribution> $N_d(\mu,\Sigma)$ if every real linear combination $t^TX$ is normally distributed, with mean $t^T\mu$ and variance $t^T\Sigma t$. The <covariance matrix> is symmetric positive semidefinite; zero variances permit degenerate normals. Its <moment-generating function> is $M_X(t)=\exp(t^T\mu+t^T\Sigma t/2)$, including the degenerate cases.

For the given $X$, let $t_j\in\mathbb R^{d_j}$. The joint <moment-generating function> of the $Y_j=A_jX$ is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\exp\left(\sum_jt_j^TY_j\right)
&=M_X\left(\sum_jA_j^Tt_j\right)\\
&=\exp\left[\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\sum_{i,j}t_j^TA_jA_i^Tt_i\right]\\
&=\prod_j\exp\left[\frac{\sigma^2}{2}t_j^TA_jA_j^Tt_j\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality uses the assumed orthogonality of the row spaces. Its factors are exactly the individual <moment-generating functions>, so the hint implies **the random <vectors> $Y_j$ are independent**. Also every linear combination of the stacked <vector> $Y$ is a linear combination of $X$, hence normally distributed. Consequently
$$
\boxed{Y\sim N_{\sum_jd_j}\left(0,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2A_1A_1^T,\ldots,\sigma^2A_JA_J^T)\right).}
$$
This establishes both claims without excluding singular block covariances.

For the <sample mean> and <sample variance> result, assume $n\ge2$ and set $W=(Z_1-\mu,\ldots,Z_n-\mu)^T$. Independence of the observations gives $W\sim N_n(0,\sigma^2I)$. Let $u=(1,\ldots,1)^T$, $A_1=u^T/n$, and $A_2=I-uu^T/n$. Then $A_1A_2^T=0$. The first transformed <vector> is the scalar $\overline Z-\mu$, and the second is the residual <vector> $(Z_i-\overline Z)_i$. They are independent by the just-proved result. Applying the measurable function $v\mapsto\|v\|^2/(n-1)$ to the residual <vector> preserves independence from the <sample mean>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\overline Z\ \text{and}\ S_{ZZ}\ \text{are independent}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=20H}

The modified <transition matrix> $\widetilde P$ describes the original <Markov chain> until it hits $B$, and then keeps it at the first state hit. Each state of $B$ is therefore an <absorbing state>, and
$$
(\widetilde P^{\,n}\mathbf1_B)_i=\mathbb P_i(\tau\le n).
$$
For a <nonnegative superharmonic function for a Markov chain> $g$ satisfying the boundary condition, part (c) and positivity of the <transition matrix> give by induction $g\ge\widetilde P^{\,n}g$. Also $g\ge\mathbf1_B$. Consequently
$$
g_i\ge(\widetilde P^{\,n}g)_i\ge(\widetilde P^{\,n}\mathbf1_B)_i=\mathbb P_i(\tau\le n).
$$
Let $n\to\infty$ to obtain $g_i\ge h_i$. Since $h$ satisfies the conditions by parts (a),(b), **$h$ is their pointwise minimal solution**. This extends the <hitting probability is the minimal nonnegative harmonic extension> principle to the superharmonic inequality. Nonnegative sums justify the argument on a countable state space as well as a finite one.

Now suppose the <Markov chain> is irreducible and recurrent, and let $g$ satisfy condition (a). In such a chain every state is hit almost surely from every other state. To see this directly, recurrence gives infinitely many visits to a starting state $i$. Irreducibility supplies a finite path from $i$ to any target $j$ with positive probability $\alpha$, chosen with no return to $i$ before reaching $j$. At each successive return to $i$, the <strong Markov property> gives another trial with conditional success probability at least $\alpha$. The chance of avoiding $j$ through $N$ such trials is at most $(1-\alpha)^N$, which tends to zero.

Apply the absorbing modification at the singleton $\{j\}$. Part (c) holds for arbitrary $g$ satisfying (a), so
$$
g_i\ge(\widetilde P^{\,n}g)_i\ge g_j\mathbb P_i(\tau_j\le n).
$$
Since $g_j\ge0$ and the probability tends to one, $g_i\ge g_j$. Interchanging $i,j$ yields equality. Thus **every nonnegative superharmonic function is constant** on an irreducible recurrent <Markov chain>.

Conversely, if an irreducible <Markov chain> is not recurrent, choose a state $j$ with return probability $r_j=\mathbb P_j(\tau_j^+<\infty)<1$. Its <hitting probability> $h$ for $\{j\}$ satisfies condition (a), and $h_j=1$. The first-step formula for a return after time zero gives $(Ph)_j=r_j<1$. Therefore $h$ cannot be constant: a constant function with value one at $j$ would have $(Ph)_j=1$. This supplies a nonconstant nonnegative superharmonic solution. Hence
$$
\boxed{P\text{ is recurrent}\iff\text{every solution of (a) is constant}.}
$$
The admissible constant solutions are precisely the nonnegative constants. This is <recurrence characterized by constant nonnegative superharmonic functions>.
