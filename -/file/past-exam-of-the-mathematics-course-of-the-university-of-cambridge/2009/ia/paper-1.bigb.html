Write $z=x+iy$ and $\alpha=p+iq$. The <complex conjugates> in the PDF give
$$
z\overline\alpha+\overline z\alpha=2\operatorname{Re}(z\overline\alpha)=2(px+qy).
$$
Since $(p,q)\ne(0,0)$, **the locus is the <straight line> through the origin perpendicular to the position <vector> of $\alpha$** in the <complex plane>. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{z=it\alpha,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$

{parent=1c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $z=x+iy$. Dividing by two gives
$$
\sqrt{(x-a)^2+y^2}=x+a.
$$
The left side is nonnegative, so squaring is reversible only when $x+a\ge0$. Squaring yields $y^2=4ax$.

If $a>0$, this equation implies $x\ge0$, so the sign condition holds automatically. **The locus is the <parabola>**
$$
\boxed{y^2=4ax,\qquad a>0,}
$$
with vertex at the origin, <focus of a conic> $(a,0)$ and <directrix of a conic> $x=-a$. Indeed the original equation equates distance from the <focus of a conic> with distance from the <directrix of a conic> on its right side.

If $a<0$, the squared equation forces $x\le0$, making $x+a<0$. This violates the sign condition. Thus **the locus is empty for $a<0$**; squaring without checking that condition would introduce a spurious <parabola>.

= Solution

A <branch of the complex logarithm> must be specified to obtain a unique locus. First use the <principal complex logarithm>. Away from the negative real axis, write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r>0$ and $-\pi<\theta<\pi$. Then the <complex logarithms> of $z$ and its <complex conjugate> are $\log r+i\theta$ and $\log r-i\theta$. Equating real and imaginary parts gives $\log r=\theta$. Consequently **the principal-branch locus is the open segment of a <logarithmic spiral>**
$$
\boxed{z=e^{(1+i)\theta},\qquad -\pi<\theta<\pi.}
$$
The origin is excluded because its <complex logarithm> does not exist. If the principal value is assigned argument $\pi$ on the negative real axis, a point there equals its own <complex conjugate>, so its principal <complex logarithm> would have to equal $i$ times itself. That forces the <complex logarithm> to be zero and cannot hold there; the endpoints remain excluded.

If the printed equation instead means that some values of the multivalued <complex logarithms> satisfy it, write those values as $\log r+it$ and $\log r+iu$, with $e^{it}=z/r$ and $e^{iu}=\overline z/r$. Equality gives $\log r=-u=t$, hence $u=-t$, which is compatible with conjugation. Every real $t$ is allowed. Under this interpretation **the locus is the entire <logarithmic spiral> $z=e^{(1+i)t}$, $t\in\mathbb R$**. The PDF does not choose between these branch conventions.

= Solution

For a complex <matrix>, its <Hermitian conjugate> is obtained by transposing and taking entrywise <complex conjugates>:
$$
(A^\dagger)_{ij}=\overline{A_{ji}},\qquad A^\dagger=\overline A^{\,T}.
$$
The defining conditions are
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is Hermitian}\iff A^\dagger=A,\qquad A\text{ is unitary}\iff A^\dagger A=AA^\dagger=I.}
$$
For a square <matrix>, either one of the two inverse identities in the <unitary matrix> condition implies the other. A <Hermitian matrix> may have complex entries, but its diagonal entries are real.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Since $A$ is an <invertible matrix>, so is its <Hermitian conjugate>, and
$$
B^\dagger=A(A^\dagger)^{-1},\qquad BB^\dagger=A^{-1}A^\dagger A(A^\dagger)^{-1}.
$$
If $A$ is a <normal matrix>, substitute $A^\dagger A=AA^\dagger$ into the second identity to obtain $BB^\dagger=I$, so $B$ is a <unitary matrix>. Conversely, if $B$ is a <unitary matrix>, multiply $BB^\dagger=I$ on the left by $A$ and on the right by $A^\dagger$. This gives $A^\dagger A=AA^\dagger$, the defining <normal matrix> identity. Thus **$B$ is a <unitary matrix> exactly when $A$ is a <normal matrix>**.

Both sides are nonnegative. Therefore **$\|Cx\|=0$ if and only if $\|C^\dagger x\|=0$**. This proves the stronger reusable fact that <normal matrices have equal adjoint norms>.

= Solution
{parent=4E}

The key fact is that convergence of a <power series> at one nonzero point forces <absolute convergence> at every smaller modulus. Indeed, if $\sum a_nw^n$ converges and $w\ne0$, its terms form a <bounded sequence>, say $|a_nw^n|\le M$. For $|z|<|w|$,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_nz^n|\le M\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|\frac zw\right|^n<\infty
$$
by convergence of a <geometric series>.

Let $S$ be the set of moduli of points at which the <power series> converges, and put $R=\sup S$, allowing $R=\infty$. The set contains zero, so its <supremum> is defined in $[0,\infty]$. Whenever $|z|<R$, the definition of <supremum> provides a convergence point $w$ with $|w|>|z|$. The preceding estimate gives <absolute convergence> at $z$. Whenever $|z|>R$, convergence at $z$ would put $|z|$ in $S$, contradicting its upper bound. This proves the asserted <radius of convergence> property, including $R=0$ and $R=\infty$. Nothing in the argument decides convergence on $|z|=R$.

For the requested boundary behavior, take the <power series>
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2k}}k.}
$$
Its coefficients are $a_{2k}=1/k$ for $k\ge1$, $a_{2k+1}=0$ and $a_0=0$. At either $z=1$ or $z=-1$ it is the divergent <harmonic series>. At either $z=i$ or $z=-i$ it is $\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^k/k$, which converges by the <alternating series test>. In fact its <radius of convergence> is one, since inside the unit circle the terms are bounded by a <geometric series>, and outside it they do not tend to zero.

= Solution
{parent=5C}

Use <suffix notation>, summing repeated indices from one to three. The contraction identity for the <Levi-Civita symbol> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(a\times b)\cdot(a\times c)
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{i\ell m}a_jb_ka_\ell c_m\\
&=(\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell})a_jb_ka_\ell c_m\\
&=(a\cdot a)(b\cdot c)-(a\cdot c)(b\cdot a)\\
&=b\cdot c-(a\cdot b)(a\cdot c).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $a\cdot a=1$ because $a$ is a <unit vector>. For the second <cross product> identity, use $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{j\ell m}=\delta_{im}\delta_{k\ell}-\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{km}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
[(a\times b)\times(a\times c)]_i
&=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{j\ell m}\epsilon_{knp}a_\ell b_ma_nc_p\\
&=b_i\epsilon_{\ell np}a_\ell a_nc_p-a_i\epsilon_{mnp}b_ma_nc_p\\
&=0-a_i\,b\cdot(a\times c)\\
&=[a\cdot(b\times c)]a_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first term vanishes because $a_\ell a_n$ is symmetric while the <Levi-Civita symbol> is antisymmetric in those indices. The last equality uses cyclicity and antisymmetry of the <scalar triple product>.

On the <unit sphere>, a <great circle> has radius one, so the <spherical distance> is its shorter arc length, equal to its central angle in $[0,\pi]$. The <dot product> of its endpoint <unit vectors> is the cosine of that angle. Hence
$$
\boxed{\cos\delta(A,B)=a\cdot b.}
$$
For the angular formulas, take the ordinary nondegenerate convex minor <spherical triangle>: no pair of vertices is antipodal and the three vertices do not lie on one <great circle>. These are needed for the displayed denominators and interior angles to be defined. Merely requiring three distinct points, as the PDF does, does not exclude degeneracy.

Put $p=\delta(A,B)$, $q=\delta(A,C)$ and $r=\delta(B,C)$. The outgoing unit tangent <vectors> at $A$ are
$$
u=\frac{b-(a\cdot b)a}{\sin p},\qquad v=\frac{c-(a\cdot c)a}{\sin q}.
$$
Their <dot product> is $\cos\alpha$. Equivalently, they are obtained from the normalized plane normals $a\times b$ and $a\times c$ by the same right-angle rotation in the tangent plane: $u=(a\times b)\times a/|a\times b|$, and similarly for $v$. Rotation preserves the <dot product>, giving
$$
\cos\alpha=\frac{(a\times b)\cdot(a\times c)}{|a\times b|\,|a\times c|}.
$$
Since $|a\times b|=\sin p$ and $|a\times c|=\sin q$, the first proved identity becomes the <spherical law of cosines>:
$$
\boxed{\cos r=\cos p\cos q+\sin p\sin q\cos\alpha.}
$$
For the <spherical law of sines>, take norms in the second proved identity. Writing $\tau=a\cdot(b\times c)$, and using $0<\alpha<\pi$, gives
$$
|\tau|=\sin p\sin q\sin\alpha.
$$
Cyclic permutation gives the corresponding formulas at $B$ and $C$. Dividing each by the positive product $\sin p\sin q\sin r$ proves
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin r}=\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin q}=\frac{\sin\gamma}{\sin p}=\frac{|\tau|}{\sin p\sin q\sin r}.}
$$
Finally, if all sides equal $s$, the <spherical law of cosines> gives
$$
\cos\alpha=\frac{\cos s-\cos^2s}{\sin^2s}=\frac{\cos s}{1+\cos s}<\frac12,
$$
because $0<s<\pi$ makes $1+\cos s>0$ and $\cos s<1$. Cosine is strictly decreasing on $(0,\pi)$, so **every angle of a nondegenerate equilateral <spherical triangle> is greater than $\pi/3$**.

= Solution
{parent=6B}

For any <linear system> $Ax=c$, a solution exists exactly when $c$ belongs to the <image of a linear map> represented by $A$. If $x_0$ is one solution, all others are exactly
$$
x=x_0+v,\qquad v\in\ker A,
$$
since $A(x-x_0)=0$, and conversely adding a <kernel> <vector> preserves the equation. Thus there are no solutions if $c\notin\operatorname{im}A$, one if $c\in\operatorname{im}A$ and $\ker A=\{0\}$, and infinitely many if $c\in\operatorname{im}A$ and the <kernel> is nontrivial: every real multiple of a nonzero <kernel> <vector> gives a distinct solution. Any two solutions differ by a <kernel> <vector>. Equivalently, consistency means the augmented <matrix> and $A$ have equal <rank of a matrix>; a consistent square <linear system> is unique exactly when its <determinant> is nonzero.

For the given <matrix>, set $d=b-a$ and $s=2a+b$. Perform the <column operation> $C_1\leftarrow C_1+C_2+C_3$, then the <row operations> $R_2\leftarrow R_2-R_1$ and $R_3\leftarrow R_3-R_1$. Factoring the first column gives
$$
\det A=s\det\begin{pmatrix}1&a&b\\0&0&a-b\\0&b-a&a-b\end{pmatrix}
=\boxed{(2a+b)(b-a)^2}.
$$
These operations are determinant-preserving, and the displayed polynomial identity remains valid even when a factored expression vanishes.

To find all <kernels> and <images of a linear map>, put
$$
u=(1,1,1)^T,\qquad H=\{x:x_1+x_2+x_3=0\},\qquad P=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
If $J$ is the all-ones <matrix>, then $A=aJ+dP$. The <direct sum> $\mathbb R^3=\mathbb Ru\oplus H$ is invariant under $A$: on $\mathbb Ru$ it acts by multiplication by $s$, and on $H$ by $dP$. Since $P^3=I$, its restriction to $H$ is invertible.

For $d\ne0$ and $s\ne0$, the <kernel> is $\{0\}$ and the <image of a linear map> is all of $\mathbb R^3$. Decompose the right-hand side as
$$
(1,c,1)^T=\frac{c+2}{3}u+\frac{c-1}{3}(-1,2,-1)^T.
$$
Applying the inverse on each invariant summand gives the unique solution
$$
\boxed{x=\frac{c+2}{3s}u+\frac{c-1}{3d}(2,-1,-1)^T.}
$$
For $d\ne0$ and $s=0$, the <kernel> is $\mathbb Ru$ and the <image of a linear map> is $H$. A solution exists exactly when $c+2=0$. For $c=-2$, all solutions are
$$
\boxed{x=-\frac1d(2,-1,-1)^T+t u,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
For $d=0$ and $a\ne0$, so $a=b$, the <kernel> is $H$ and the <image of a linear map> is $\mathbb Ru$. A solution exists exactly when $c=1$. All such solutions are
$$
\boxed{x=(r,t,1/a-r-t)^T,\qquad r,t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Finally, for $a=b=0$, the <kernel> is $\mathbb R^3$ and the <image of a linear map> is $\{0\}$. The right-hand side has first entry one, so **there is no solution for any $c$**. These four cases exhaust all parameter values.

Let $v\ne0$ be an <eigenvector> of the <Hermitian matrix> $A$, with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$. Multiplying $Av=\lambda v$ by $v^\dagger$ gives
$$
\lambda=\frac{v^\dagger Av}{v^\dagger v}.
$$
The denominator is the positive real number $\|v\|^2$, while
$$
\overline{v^\dagger Av}=v^\dagger A^\dagger v=v^\dagger Av.
$$
Thus the numerator is real, and **every <eigenvalue> of a <Hermitian matrix> is real**. This argument does not require distinct <eigenvalues>.

Take <eigenvectors> $v_i,v_j$ for distinct <eigenvalues> $\lambda_i,\lambda_j$. Since the <eigenvalues> are real and $A$ is a <Hermitian matrix>,
$$
\lambda_jv_i^\dagger v_j=v_i^\dagger Av_j=(Av_i)^\dagger v_j=\lambda_i v_i^\dagger v_j.

Hence $(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)v_i^\dagger v_j=0$, so **<eigenvectors> for distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal vectors>**. They are therefore <linearly independent>; after normalization the given $n$ <eigenvectors> form an <orthonormal basis> of $\mathbb C^n$.

Use the convention

P_A(t)=\det(tI-A)=\sum_{r=0}^n a_rt^r
$$
for the <characteristic polynomial>. Choosing $\det(A-tI)$ instead multiplies it by $(-1)^n$ and leaves the requested zero identity unchanged. For each <eigenvector> $v_j$, induction gives $A^rv_j=\lambda_j^rv_j$, including $r=0$. Thus

\left(\sum_{r=0}^n a_rA^r\right)v_j=P_A(\lambda_j)v_j=0,

because $\lambda_jI-A$ has the nonzero <kernel> <vector> $v_j$ and therefore has zero <determinant>. Part (a) supplies a <basis> consisting of these $n$ <eigenvectors>. A <linear map> vanishing on every <basis> <vector> vanishes everywhere. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\sum_{r=0}^n a_rA^r=0.}
$$
This proves the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> identity directly in the distinct-<eigenvalue> case assumed here.

Expand $x=\sum_j c_jv_j$ in the <orthonormal basis> from part (a). Then
$$
\frac{x^\dagger Ax}{x^\dagger x}=\frac{\sum_j\lambda_j|c_j|^2}{\sum_j|c_j|^2}.

For $x\ne0$, the weights $|c_j|^2/\sum_k|c_k|^2$ are nonnegative and sum to one. The <Rayleigh quotient> therefore lies between the smallest and largest <eigenvalues>. Conversely, normalized extreme <eigenvectors> $v_{\min},v_{\max}$ give, for $0\le t\le1$,

x=\sqrt{1-t}\,v_{\min}+\sqrt t\,v_{\max},\qquad \frac{x^\dagger Ax}{x^\dagger x}=(1-t)\lambda_{\min}+t\lambda_{\max}.
$$
Thus **the exact <Hermitian Rayleigh quotient range> is**
$$
\boxed{[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}].}
$$
When $n=1$, the range is the singleton containing the sole <eigenvalue>.

Preserving the Euclidean <scalar product> means $(Ax)^T(Ay)=x^Ty$ for every pair of real <vectors> $x,y$. Since these bilinear expressions agree on all pairs of <basis> <vectors>, this is equivalent to $A^TA=I$: the columns of $A$ form an <orthonormal basis>.

The first column is a <unit vector>, so it is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$ for some real $\theta$. A perpendicular <unit vector> is one of the two choices $\varepsilon(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^T$, where $\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}$. Hence **all the required <orthogonal matrices> are**
$$
\boxed{A=R(\theta)D_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\varepsilon\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\varepsilon\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad D_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(1,\varepsilon),\quad\theta\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Here $R(\theta)$ is a <planar rotation>, and $\theta$ is determined modulo $2\pi$. To check the <group> laws explicitly, $D_\varepsilon R(\phi)=R(\varepsilon\phi)D_\varepsilon$, so
$$
(R(\theta)D_\varepsilon)(R(\phi)D_\eta)=R(\theta+\varepsilon\phi)D_{\varepsilon\eta}.
$$
This proves closure. The identity is $R(0)D_1=I$, the inverse of $R(\theta)D_\varepsilon$ is $R(-\varepsilon\theta)D_\varepsilon$, and associativity is inherited from <matrix multiplication>. Thus the set is the <orthogonal group> $O(2)$.

For the <Minkowski metric> $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, preservation means $(Ax)^TJ(Ay)=x^TJy$ for all real <vectors> $x,y$. This is equivalent to $A^TJA=J$, or, entrywise,
$$
a^2-c^2=1,\qquad b^2-d^2=-1,\qquad ab-cd=0.
$$
The restriction $a>0$ puts the first column on the right branch of the unit <hyperbola>, so uniquely $a=\cosh t$, $c=\sinh t$ for a real parameter $t$. The third equation makes the second column a scalar multiple of $(\sinh t,\cosh t)^T$: this also covers $t=0$, when $b=0$. The second equation makes that multiple $\varepsilon=\pm1$. Thus **all the required <matrices> are**
$$
\boxed{A=H(t)D_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh t&\varepsilon\sinh t\\\sinh t&\varepsilon\cosh t\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\mathbb R,\quad\varepsilon=\pm1.}
$$
The <hyperbolic function> addition formulas give $H(t)H(s)=H(t+s)$, while $D_\varepsilon H(s)=H(\varepsilon s)D_\varepsilon$. Consequently
$$
(H(t)D_\varepsilon)(H(s)D_\eta)=H(t+\varepsilon s)D_{\varepsilon\eta}.
$$
The product remains of the required form and its upper-left entry is positive. The identity is $H(0)D_1$, and the inverse is $H(-\varepsilon t)D_\varepsilon$. Together with associativity of <matrix multiplication>, these identities establish the <group> property. This is the <time-orientation-preserving Lorentz group in one spatial dimension>.

Put $s=\sin x$, so $-1\le s\le1$. The printed summand is $(3s^n+s^{2n})/n$. If $|s|<1$, its absolute value is at most $3|s|^n+|s|^{2n}$, and the two <geometric series> converge. Thus there is <absolute convergence>.

For $s=1$ the <series> becomes $\sum4/n$, a divergent multiple of the <harmonic series>. For $s=-1$ it becomes
$$
3\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n}+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1n.
$$
The first <series> converges by the <alternating series test>, while the second diverges to $+\infty$, so their partial sums tend to $+\infty$. In particular, alternation of some terms does not imply convergence here. The complete classification is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}x\notin\{\pi/2+k\pi:k\in\mathbb Z\}:&\text{absolute convergence},\\x\in\{\pi/2+k\pi:k\in\mathbb Z\}:&\text{divergence to }+\infty.\end{array}}
$$
**There are no conditionally convergent cases.**

The <intermediate value theorem> says that if $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is a <continuous function>, every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ is attained at some point of $[a,b]$.

For a proof, endpoint values are immediate, and replacing $f$ by $-f$ reduces the remaining case to $f(a)<y<f(b)$. The set $S=\{x\in[a,b]:f(x)\le y\}$ is nonempty and bounded above; let $c=\sup S$ by the <least-upper-bound property>. <Continuity> and the strict endpoint inequalities ensure $a<c<b$: near $a$ there are points in $S$, and near $b$ there are none. If $f(c)>y$, <continuity> gives a neighborhood of $c$ containing no points of $S$, contradicting the defining approximation property of the <supremum>. If $f(c)<y$, <continuity> gives points to the right of $c$ still in $S$, contradicting its being an upper bound. Hence $f(c)=y$, proving the <intermediate value theorem>.

For the distance problem, define the <continuous function>
$$
d(x)=\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(f(x)-b)^2}.
$$
It satisfies $d(x)\ge|x-a|$, so it is a <coercive function>: $d(x)\to\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$. Choose $R>|a|+d(0)+1$. Outside $[-R,R]$, the distance is greater than $d(0)$. On $[-R,R]$, the <extreme value theorem> gives a minimizer $x_0$, with $A=d(x_0)\le d(0)$. It is therefore a global minimizer and $A\ge0$.

For any $y>A$, choose $X>x_0$ so large that $d(X)>y$. Apply the <intermediate value theorem> to $d$ on $[x_0,X]$ to obtain a point whose distance is $y$. The distance $A$ itself is attained at $x_0$, and no smaller value is possible. This is the <range of a continuous coercive real function>. Thus **the distance set is exactly**
$$
\boxed{[A,\infty),\qquad A=\min_{x\in\mathbb R}\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(f(x)-b)^2}.}
$$
In particular $A=0$ precisely when the fixed point lies on the graph.

<Rolle's theorem> states that a <continuous function> $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$, differentiable on $(a,b)$ with $a<b$ and $f(a)=f(b)$, has $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$. If $f$ is constant this is immediate. Otherwise the <extreme value theorem> supplies a maximum or minimum with value different from the common endpoint value, so one such extremum occurs in $(a,b)$. At an interior maximum, the difference quotients for positive increments are nonpositive and those for negative increments are nonnegative. Their common limit, the <derivative>, must be zero. At a minimum the inequalities reverse, with the same conclusion. This proves <Rolle's theorem>.

For the two-function identity, write $\Delta f=f(b)-f(a)$ and $\Delta g=g(b)-g(a)$, and set $\phi(x)=g(x)\Delta f-f(x)\Delta g$. This is a <continuous function> on the closed interval and is differentiable inside it. Its endpoint values agree:
$$
\phi(a)=\phi(b)=g(a)f(b)-f(a)g(b).
$$
They need not be zero, but <Rolle's theorem> only requires equality. At the resulting point $c$,
$$
0=\phi'(c)=g'(c)\Delta f-f'(c)\Delta g,
$$
which proves the <Cauchy mean value theorem> identity
$$
\boxed{f'(c)(g(b)-g(a))=g'(c)(f(b)-f(a)).}
$$
For the final endpoint limit, first state the denominator convention explicitly. In the usual zero-over-zero version of <L'Hopital rule>, $g'(x)\ne0$ on some whole interval $(0,\delta)$; this is understood if the displayed derivative quotient is to be a function defined throughout a deleted right neighborhood of zero. Define $f(0)=g(0)=0$, making both functions continuous at zero. For $0<x<\delta$, <Rolle's theorem> implies $g(x)\ne0$, since otherwise $g$ would have equal zero values at $0$ and $x$ and a zero <derivative> between them. Apply the proved <Cauchy mean value theorem> on $[0,x]$ to obtain $c_x\in(0,x)$ with
$$
f(x)g'(c_x)=f'(c_x)g(x),\qquad \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f'(c_x)}{g'(c_x)}.
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, the hypothesis on the derivative quotient supplies $\eta>0$ such that its distance from $\ell$ is less than $\varepsilon$ whenever $0<t<\eta$. If $0<x<\min(\eta,\delta)$ then $0<c_x<x<\eta$, so the same bound holds for $f(x)/g(x)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\ell.}
$$
No continuity of the <derivatives> is needed.

The PDF does not explicitly state the nonvanishing condition on $g'$. If “the limit exists” permits a limit only along the natural domain where $g'\ne0$, the assertion is false, even with $g>0$ everywhere. The distinction concerns <derivative quotient limits on partial domains>. Here is a concrete counterexample to that weaker reading. Put $x_n=2^{-n}$ and $h_n=4^{-n}$ for $n\ge0$. On each interval $[x_n/2,x_n]$, split off the plateau $[3x_n/4,x_n]$. On the plateau set
$$
g(x)=h_n,\qquad f(x)=h_nq\left(\frac{x-3x_n/4}{x_n/4}\right),\qquad q(u)=16u^2(1-u)^2.
$$
On the transition interval $[x_n/2,3x_n/4]$ set
$$
g(x)=h_{n+1}+(h_n-h_{n+1})S\left(\frac{x-x_n/2}{x_n/4}\right),\qquad f(x)=0,\qquad S(u)=3u^2-2u^3.
$$
Extend $g=1,f=0$ for $x\ge1$. The endpoints fit because $S(0)=0$, $S(1)=1$, while both $S'$ and $q'$ vanish at zero and one and $q$ also vanishes there. Thus $f,g$ are differentiable for all $x>0$ and tend to zero at zero. The <derivative> $g'$ is nonzero exactly inside the transition intervals, where $f'=0$, so the derivative quotient has natural-domain limit zero. But $f(x_n)/g(x_n)=0$, whereas $f(7x_n/8)/g(7x_n/8)=1$. Hence $f/g$ has no limit. The preceding qualified proof is the intended valid form of <L'Hopital rule>.

A <Riemann integrable> function on a closed interval is bounded and has equal <upper and lower Darboux integrals>. More explicitly, for a <partition of an interval> $P:a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=b$, define the <Darboux sums>
$$
L(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^m\inf_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f\,(x_j-x_{j-1}),\qquad U(f,P)=\sum_{j=1}^m\sup_{[x_{j-1},x_j]}f\,(x_j-x_{j-1}).
$$
Then **<Riemann integrability> means**
$$
\boxed{\sup_P L(f,P)=\inf_P U(f,P),}
$$
and this common value is the <Riemann integral>. Equivalently, the <Riemann integrability criterion> requires that for every $\varepsilon>0$ some <partition of an interval> satisfies $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. Indeed, the common <partition refinement> of two partitions increases each lower <Darboux sum> and decreases each upper <Darboux sum>, so equality of the two extremal values is equivalent to arbitrarily small gaps.

Let $|f|\le M$. If $M=0$ the result is immediate, so take $M>0$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $0<\delta<\varepsilon/(4M)$ and the finitely many intervals supplied by the hypothesis. On each $[a_i,b_i]$, choose a <partition of an interval> whose <Darboux sum> gap is less than $\varepsilon/(2n)$, using <Riemann integrability> there.

Combine all these partitions with $0,1$ and all interval endpoints to obtain a global <partition of an interval> $P$. On the chosen intervals the gaps add to less than $\varepsilon/2$. The complementary intervals have total length
$$
1-\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i-a_i)\le\delta,
$$
and on each the difference between the supremum and infimum is at most $2M$. Consequently
$$
U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\frac\varepsilon2+2M\delta<\varepsilon.
$$
The <Riemann integrability criterion> proves **$f$ is <Riemann integrable> on $[0,1]$**. Shared endpoints cause no problem: the <Darboux sums> use closed intervals, and the complementary interval estimate includes every endpoint value. This proves <Riemann integrability from almost full interval coverage>.

Let $K$ be the set where the <indicator function> $f$ equals one. Fix a positive integer $N$. Each permitted length-$N$ decimal prefix $w=(w_1,\ldots,w_N)\in\{2,7\}^N$ gives the interval
$$
I_w=[t_w,t_w+10^{-N}],\qquad t_w=\sum_{j=1}^Nw_j10^{-j}.
$$
There are $2^N$ such intervals, each of length $10^{-N}$, and they cover $K$. Every point of $K$ is strictly inside the interval for its prefix, since its remaining tail lies between $2/(9\cdot10^N)$ and $7/(9\cdot10^N)$. Thus none of the interval endpoints belongs to $K$. The intervals are disjoint and separated: their distinct prefix integers cannot be consecutive because their final digits are only $2$ and $7$.

The complement consists of finitely many closed intervals on which $f=0$, of total length $1-2^N10^{-N}=1-5^{-N}$. Part (b) therefore already proves <Riemann integrability>, since this length tends to one.

To compute the <Riemann integral> directly, make a <partition of an interval> using $0,1$ and all the endpoints of the $I_w$. On complementary cells the upper <Darboux sum> contribution is zero, and on the $I_w$ it is at most their total length. Moreover every nonempty cell contains a terminating decimal, which is not in $K$: neither its terminating expansion with a zero tail nor its alternative expansion with a nine tail consists entirely of twos and sevens. Such decimals are dense, so every cell has infimum zero. Therefore
$$
0=L(f,P_N)\le\int_0^1f(x)\,dx\le U(f,P_N)\le5^{-N}.
$$
Letting $N\to\infty$ gives **the exact answer**
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1f(x)\,dx=0.}
$$
This is an example of an <indicator of a restricted-digit decimal set>: infinitely many, indeed uncountably many, points can support an <indicator function> whose <Riemann integral> is zero.
