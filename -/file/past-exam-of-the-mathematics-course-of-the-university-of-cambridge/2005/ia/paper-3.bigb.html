= Solution
{parent=1D}

By the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>, choose an <orthonormal eigenbasis> $u_1,u_2,u_3$ with $Au_i=\lambda_i u_i$. If $x=\sum_i y_i u_i$, then
$$
1=x^TAx=\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2
\leq\lambda_1\sum_i y_i^2=\lambda_1|x|^2.
$$
Thus every point of the <ellipsoid> has distance at least $\lambda_1^{-1/2}$ from the origin. Equality holds precisely when
$$
(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)y_2^2+(\lambda_1-\lambda_3)y_3^2=0.
$$
Both coefficients are strictly positive, so $y_2=y_3=0$. The surface equation then gives $y_1=\pm\lambda_1^{-1/2}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\frac1{\sqrt{\lambda_1}},\qquad
x=\pm\frac{u_1}{\sqrt{\lambda_1}}.}
$$
**Exactly two points attain the minimum.** The strict ordering makes the largest-eigenvalue <eigenspace> one-dimensional; this is the equality case in <nearest points to the origin on a positive-definite ellipsoid>.

= Solution
{parent=2D}

A <cyclic group> is a <group> generated by a single element: there is $g\in G$ such that every element is $g^n$ for some integer $n$. For a <finite group> of prime order $p$, choose $g\ne e$. Its <cyclic subgroup> $\langle g\rangle$ has order dividing $p$ by <Lagrange's theorem>, and its order is greater than one. Hence $|\langle g\rangle|=p$, and $\langle g\rangle=G$. Thus **every <group> of prime order is cyclic**.

A <group homomorphism> from $C_{11}=\langle a\rangle$ is determined by $h=\varphi(a)$, since $\varphi(a^j)=h^j$. Its image generator must satisfy $h^{11}=e$, so its <order of a group element> divides $11$. As an element of $C_{14}$ its order also divides $14$. These integers are coprime, so the only possible order is one. Thus $h=e$, and
$$
\boxed{\varphi(a^j)=e\quad\text{for every }j.}
$$
This map is indeed a <group homomorphism>, so **there is exactly one**, the trivial homomorphism.

= Solution
{parent=3A}

Let $e=\tfrac12(|A|^2+|B|^2)$. The time equations and the <dot product> <differentiation> rule give the local identity
$$
\partial_t e=A\cdot(\nabla\times B)-B\cdot(\nabla\times A).
$$
The <divergence of a cross product> is
$$
\nabla\cdot(A\times B)=B\cdot(\nabla\times A)-A\cdot(\nabla\times B).
$$
Combining them yields $\partial_t e+\nabla\cdot(A\times B)=0$. On a fixed bounded region with a <boundary> regular enough for the <divergence theorem>, differentiating under the <integral> and applying that theorem gives
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\left[\frac12\int_V(|A|^2+|B|^2)\,dV\right]
=-\int_{\partial V}(A\times B)\cdot n\,dS.}
$$
Here $n$ is the outward <unit normal>, so the sign expresses loss of the interior quadratic quantity by outward flux. No vanishing-boundary assumption is required.

= Solution
{parent=4A}

For the unit-speed <curve>, the <unit tangent vector>, <curvature of a space curve>, <principal normal vector> and <binormal vector> are
$$
\boxed{t=\gamma',\qquad \kappa=|\gamma''|,\qquad
p=\frac{\gamma''}{|\gamma''|},\qquad b=t\times p.}
$$
Differentiating $t\cdot t=1$ gives $t\cdot\gamma''=0$, so $t,p,b$ form a positively oriented <orthonormal basis>. The hypothesis $\gamma''\ne0$ makes the <principal normal vector> well-defined.

To determine the sign of the <torsion of a space curve> without relying on a pictorial handedness convention, take $R>0$, $h\ne0$, $L=\sqrt{R^2+h^2}$ and use the unit-speed <circular helix>
$$
\gamma_h(s)=\bigl(R\cos(s/L),\,R\sin(s/L),\,hs/L\bigr).
$$
Writing $u=s/L$, direct <differentiation> gives its <Frenet frame>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
t&=\frac1L(-R\sin u,R\cos u,h),&
p&=(-\cos u,-\sin u,0),\\
b&=\frac1L(h\sin u,-h\cos u,R),&
b'&=\frac h{L^2}(\cos u,\sin u,0).
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\frac R{R^2+h^2},\qquad
\tau=-b'\cdot p=\frac h{R^2+h^2}.}
$$
At $s=0$ the marked point is $(R,0,0)$, with $t=(0,R/L,h/L)$, $p=(-1,0,0)$, $b=(0,-h/L,R/L)$ and $b'=(h/L^2,0,0)$. For $h>0$, $b'$ points opposite to $p$ and the <torsion of a space curve> is positive. Replacing $h$ by $-h$ reflects the <circular helix> across the horizontal plane and makes the <torsion of a space curve> negative; then $b'$ points in the same direction as $p$. The direction of increasing $s$ is fixed in both panels.

= Solution
{parent=5D}

A <group action> is a map $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$ from $G\times X$ to $X$ satisfying $e\cdot x=x$ and $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$. Define the <orbit of a group action> $Gx=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$ and the <stabilizer subgroup> $G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}$. The latter is a <subgroup>: the identity fixes $x$, products of elements fixing $x$ fix $x$, and inverses do too.

For finite $G$, the <orbit-stabilizer theorem> states
$$
\boxed{|Gx|=\frac{|G|}{|G_x|}.}
$$
To prove it, send a left <coset> $gG_x$ to $g\cdot x$. If $gG_x=hG_x$, then $h^{-1}g\in G_x$ and $g\cdot x=h\cdot x$. Conversely, equality of the two images implies $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, hence equality of the <cosets>. Every point of the <orbit of a group action> has such a preimage. This is therefore a <bijection>, and each <coset> has $|G_x|$ elements, proving the formula.

For the action on <subgroups>, conjugation maps a <subgroup> $K$ to a <subgroup> $gKg^{-1}$ because it preserves products and inverses. Furthermore $eKe^{-1}=K$ and
$$
(gh)K(gh)^{-1}=g(hKh^{-1})g^{-1}.
$$
Thus this is the <conjugation action>. The <stabilizer subgroup> of $H$ is its <normalizer> $N_G(H)$, and $H\subseteq N_G(H)$ since each $h\in H$ conjugates $H$ onto itself. Hence the number $k$ of distinct <conjugate subgroups> is
$$
k=\frac{|G|}{|N_G(H)|}\leq\frac{|G|}{|H|}.
$$
Now suppose $H$ is proper. Put $m=|G|/|H|\geq2$ and list the $k$ distinct <conjugate subgroups> as $H_1,\ldots,H_k$. Each has $|H|$ elements and all contain the same identity. Counting the identity just once, even if other elements overlap, gives
$$
\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^kH_i\right|
\leq1+k(|H|-1)
\leq1+m(|H|-1)
=|G|-m+1<|G|.
$$
Thus **some element of $G$ lies in no conjugate of $H$**. This proves <conjugates of a proper subgroup do not cover a finite group> by an explicit count; it does not require the conjugates to intersect only at the identity.

= Solution
{parent=6D}

Represent the <Riemann sphere> by the complex <projective line>, with finite $z$ represented by $[z:1]$ and infinity by $[1:0]$. An invertible <matrix> $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ acts by $[u:v]\mapsto[au+bv:cu+dv]$, giving the stated fractional map on finite points. Applying $B$ and then $A$ is exactly <matrix multiplication> $AB$, including at poles and infinity. Therefore
$$
\theta(AB)=\theta(A)\circ\theta(B),
$$
so $\theta$ is a <group homomorphism> from the <special linear group> to the <Möbius group>.

Every <Möbius transformation> has an invertible complex <matrix> representative $A$. Choose $\rho\in\mathbb C$ with $\rho^2\det A=1$. Such a nonzero <square root> exists in $\mathbb C$, and $\rho A$ has <determinant> one while representing the same projective map. This proves surjectivity. If the map is the identity, fixing zero and infinity forces $b=c=0$, and fixing one forces $a=d$. The determinant-one condition gives $a^2=1$. Conversely both <scalar matrices> $I$ and $-I$ act trivially. Hence
$$
\boxed{\ker\theta=\{I,-I\},\qquad
\mathcal M\cong SL_2(\mathbb C)/\{I,-I\}.}
$$
For the normal forms, use the fact that a complex $2\times2$ <matrix> has a <Jordan normal form>: either it is diagonalizable or it has one nontrivial <Jordan block>. For a determinant-one representative, distinct <eigenvalues> are $\lambda,\lambda^{-1}$. Diagonalizing gives $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$, whose projective action is $z\mapsto\lambda^2z$. Distinctness means $\lambda^2\ne1$, so $\mu=\lambda^2$ is neither zero nor one.

If the <eigenvalue> is repeated, the <determinant> condition gives $\lambda=\pm1$. A diagonalizable representative would be $\lambda I$ and would give the identity, excluded here. A nontrivial <Jordan block> is similar to $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, giving $z\mapsto z+1/\lambda=z\pm1$. Any invertible conjugating <matrix> gives a <Möbius transformation>; rescaling it to <determinant> one does not change that map. Thus these <matrix similarities> are genuine conjugacies in the <Möbius group>, proving the requested <Möbius conjugacy normal forms>.

Conjugacy preserves <fixed points> bijectively. A nonidentity scaling has exactly the two <fixed points> zero and infinity, while either translation has exactly one, infinity. The identity fixes every point. This proves all three possibilities and excludes any further finite number of <fixed points>.

Finally, suppose $P\circ T\circ P^{-1}=S$, with $S(z)=z+\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon=\pm1$. Then $S^n(z)=z+n\varepsilon\to\infty$ for every finite $z$ in the topology of the <Riemann sphere>; infinity remains infinity. Since $P^{-1}$ is continuous on that <sphere>,
$$
\boxed{T^n(z)=P^{-1}\bigl(S^n(P(z))\bigr)\longrightarrow
P^{-1}(\infty)=z_0\quad\text{for every }z.}
$$
This is <iteration of a one-fixed-point Möbius transformation>. The limiting statement includes the unique <fixed point> itself and uses spherical convergence, which also handles intermediate iterates equal to infinity.

= Solution
{parent=7D}

The identity belongs to the <center of a group>. If $a,b$ are central, then for every $g\in G$,
$$
g(ab^{-1})=a\,gb^{-1}=ab^{-1}g,
$$
so the center is a <subgroup>. For a central $a$, $gag^{-1}=a$ for every $g$, giving $gZ(G)g^{-1}=Z(G)$. Thus **the center is a <normal subgroup>**.

Write a <matrix> of the <Heisenberg group> as $h(x,y,z)$. Direct <matrix multiplication> and inversion give
$$
h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c)=h(x+a,y+b+xc,z+c),
\qquad
h(x,y,z)^{-1}=h(-x,xz-y,-z).
$$
The identity is $h(0,0,0)$ and each <matrix> has <determinant> one, so these formulas show closure under products and inverses inside $GL_3(\mathbb R)$. Hence $H$ is a <subgroup>.

The two products $h(x,y,z)h(a,b,c)$ and $h(a,b,c)h(x,y,z)$ differ only in their upper-right entries, by $xc-az$. Commutation with every $h(a,b,c)$ therefore requires $xc=az$ for all real $a,c$. Setting $a=0,c=1$ forces $x=0$; setting $a=1,c=0$ forces $z=0$. These conditions are also sufficient. Thus
$$
\boxed{Z(H)=\{h(0,y,0):y\in\mathbb R\}.}
$$
Define the surjective <group homomorphism> $F:H\to(\mathbb R^2,+)$ by $F(h(x,y,z))=(x,z)$. The multiplication formula gives $F(hh')=F(h)+F(h')$, and its <kernel of a group homomorphism> is precisely $Z(H)$. The <first isomorphism theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{H/Z(H)\cong(\mathbb R^2,+),\qquad
h(x,y,z)Z(H)\longmapsto(x,z).}
$$
This <center quotient of the real Heisenberg group> can also be checked directly: two elements have the same $(x,z)$ coordinates exactly when they differ by multiplication by a central element, and quotient multiplication adds these coordinates.

= Solution
{parent=8D}

First $E=\ker(A+I)$ is a <linear subspace>. To prove it is nonzero, use <orthogonality> and $\det A=-1$:
$$
\det(A+I)=\det A\,\det(I+A^{-1})
=-\det(I+A^T)=-\det(A+I).
$$
Hence $\det(A+I)=0$. If $u\in E$ then $Au=-u$ also implies $A^Tu=-u$. For $x\perp E$,
$$
\langle Ax,u\rangle=\langle x,A^Tu\rangle=-\langle x,u\rangle=0,
$$
so $E^\perp$ is invariant. If $E$ had dimension two, its one-dimensional <orthogonal complement> would be invariant and $A$ would act there as a scalar $\eta=\pm1$. The <determinant> would be $(-1)^2\eta=-1$, so $\eta=-1$, forcing $A=-I$. Dimension three also forces $A=-I$. Both contradict the hypothesis. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim E=1.}
$$
Choose an <orthonormal basis> beginning with a <unit vector> in $E$ and followed by a basis of $\Pi=E^\perp$. In this basis the <orthogonal matrix> has block form
$$
A\sim\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&R\end{pmatrix},
\qquad R^TR=I_2,\qquad \det R=1.
$$
The first column of $R$ is some $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$. Its perpendicular unit second column with positive orientation must be $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^T$. Thus the restriction is the plane <rotation>
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
<Trace> invariance gives $\operatorname{tr}A=-1+2\cos\theta$, proving
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta=\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}A+1).}
$$
A <Euclidean reflection> in $\Pi$ fixes that plane pointwise and reverses $E$, so it occurs exactly when $R=I_2$. This is equivalent to $\cos\theta=1$, hence **exactly to $\operatorname{tr}A=1$**. Finally,
$$
\det(A-I)=(-2)\det(R-I_2)
=-2\bigl((\cos\theta-1)^2+\sin^2\theta\bigr)
=\boxed{4(\cos\theta-1)}.
$$
This exhibits the <three-dimensional improper orthogonal transformation> as a <rotation> about the axis $E$ composed with <Euclidean reflection> in $\Pi$.

= Solution
{parent=9A}

Assume sufficient smoothness and a regular <boundary> for the <integrations> below, with $n$ outward. Put $\delta=w-\phi$, so $\delta=0$ on $S$. Expanding the <Dirichlet energy> gives
$$
\int_V|\nabla w|^2
=\int_V|\nabla\phi|^2
+2\int_V\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\delta+\int_V|\nabla\delta|^2.
$$
By <Green's first identity>, the middle <integral> is
$$
\int_V\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\delta
=\int_S\delta\,\partial_n\phi-\int_V\delta\,\Delta\phi=0.
$$
Thus the <Dirichlet principle> follows with an explicit remainder:
$$
\boxed{\int_V|\nabla w|^2-\int_V|\nabla\phi|^2
=\int_V|\nabla(w-\phi)|^2\geq0.}
$$
Equality means $w-\phi$ is constant on each <connected component>; its zero <boundary> value forces that constant to be zero.

Now let $w$ solve the <heat equation> and set $E(t)=\int_V|\nabla w|^2\,dV$. <Differentiation> followed by <Green's first identity> gives
$$
E'(t)=2\int_V\nabla w\cdot\nabla w_t
=2\int_Sw_t\,\partial_nw-2\int_Vw_t\Delta w.
$$
Under time-independent <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $w_t=0$ on $S$, while $w_t=\Delta w$ in $V$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E'(t)=-2\int_V(\Delta w)^2\,dV\leq0.}
$$
If equality holds at a particular time, <continuity> makes $\Delta w=0$ throughout $V$ at that time. Its <boundary> values are still $f$, so uniqueness of the <Dirichlet problem> implies $w=\phi$. Conversely $w=\phi$ has $\Delta w=0$ and gives equality. This proves the full equality criterion for <Dirichlet energy dissipation for the heat equation>.

For the <dynamic boundary condition for the heat equation>, substitute $w_t=-\alpha\partial_nw$ directly into the <boundary term>. The interior equation is unchanged, so
$$
\boxed{E'(t)=-2\int_Vw_t^2\,dV
-2\int_S\alpha(\partial_nw)^2\,dS\leq0.}
$$
This is <Dirichlet energy dissipation with a dynamic heat boundary condition>. It remains valid when $\alpha$ vanishes, because no division by $\alpha$ is used. The last part preserves energy monotonicity; its stationary states need not have the earlier prescribed <Dirichlet data>. For example, with strictly positive $\alpha$ on a connected <boundary>, a stationary harmonic state has zero outward <normal derivative> and is constant, as follows by applying <Green's first identity> to that state itself.

= Solution

For an oriented piecewise <smooth surface> $S$ and a <continuously differentiable> <vector field>, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\int_S(\nabla\times B)\cdot n\,dS
=\oint_{\partial S}B\cdot dr,
$$
where the <boundary orientation> is induced by $n$ through the <right-hand rule>. Choose the upward orientation of the <elliptic paraboloid>. Its band has <cylindrical radius> $1/2\leq r\leq1$, and a parametrization is
$$
X(r,\varphi)=(r\cos\varphi,r\sin\varphi,r^2),
\qquad 0\leq\varphi<2\pi.
$$
Its <oriented surface element> and scalar area element are
$$
\boxed{d\mathbf S=(X_r\times X_\varphi)\,dr\,d\varphi
=(-2r^2\cos\varphi,-2r^2\sin\varphi,r)\,dr\,d\varphi,}
$$
$$
dS=r\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,dr\,d\varphi.
$$
The requested sketch shows the open band, not a capped solid.

\Image[paper-3-paraboloid.png]
{title=Upward-oriented annular paraboloid band, with counterclockwise outer boundary and clockwise inner boundary}

For the specified <vector field>, direct <differentiation> gives $\nabla\times B=(0,0,3x^2+3y^2)$. Hence
$$
I=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{1/2}^1 3r^3\,dr\,d\varphi
=\boxed{\frac{45\pi}{32}}.
$$
To check this by <Stokes theorem>, parametrize a counterclockwise circle of radius $\rho$ at height $\rho^2$ by $r_\rho(\varphi)=(\rho\cos\varphi,\rho\sin\varphi,\rho^2)$. Along it $dz=0$, and
$$
B\cdot dr_\rho
=\rho^4(\sin^4\varphi+\cos^4\varphi)\,d\varphi.
$$
Using $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^4\varphi\,d\varphi =\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^4\varphi\,d\varphi=3\pi/4$ gives $3\pi\rho^4/2$. The induced outer circle is counterclockwise as viewed from above, and the inner circle is clockwise. Therefore
$$
I=\oint_{C_1}B\cdot dr-\oint_{C_{1/2}}B\cdot dr
=\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(1-\frac1{16}\right)
=\boxed{\frac{45\pi}{32}}.
$$
This verifies the direct <surface integral> and the <Stokes flux through an annular paraboloid>. Reversing the chosen surface orientation reverses the final sign.

= Solution
{parent=11A}

At a <critical point> $p$ of a twice <continuously differentiable> real function, $\nabla f(p)=0$ and the <Taylor expansion> is
$$
f(p+h)=f(p)+\tfrac12h^THh+o(|h|^2),
\qquad H=D^2f(p).
$$
The real symmetric <Hessian matrix> has an <orthonormal eigenbasis>, so writing $h=\sum_i a_i u_i$ gives $h^THh=\sum_i\lambda_i a_i^2$. If every <eigenvalue> is positive, this quadratic form is at least $\lambda_{\min}|h|^2$, and the remainder cannot change its sign for sufficiently small nonzero $h$. Thus $p$ is a strict <local minimum>. If every <eigenvalue> is negative, the analogous argument gives a strict <local maximum>. If positive and negative <eigenvalues> both occur, displacement along the corresponding <eigenvectors> gives values above and below $f(p)$, so $p$ is a <saddle point of a scalar function>.

The <second-derivative test> can be inconclusive when the <Hessian matrix> is singular and semidefinite: higher-order terms along directions in the <kernel of a linear map> matter. For example $x^2+y^4$ and $x^2-y^4$ have the same Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(2,0)$ at zero, but the first has a strict minimum and the second a <saddle point of a scalar function>. A zero <eigenvalue> does not prevent a saddle conclusion if other <eigenvalues> already have both signs.

For the given function write $r^2=x^2+y^2$. Its first <derivatives> are
$$
f_x=y e^{-\alpha r^2}(1-2\alpha x^2),\qquad
f_y=x e^{-\alpha r^2}(1-2\alpha y^2).
$$
The origin is a <critical point> for every real $\alpha$, with
$$
D^2f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $1,-1$, so it is always a <saddle point of a scalar function>. If one coordinate at a <critical point> is zero, the other must also be zero. If both are nonzero, the <derivative> equations force $x^2=y^2=1/(2\alpha)$, possible only for $\alpha>0$. Hence for $\alpha\leq0$ the origin is the only <critical point>.

For $\alpha>0$ there are four additional <critical points>,
$$
(x,y)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\alpha}}(\varepsilon,\eta),
\qquad \varepsilon,\eta\in\{-1,1\}.
$$
At such a point,
$$
f=\frac{\varepsilon\eta}{2\alpha e},\qquad
D^2f=-4\alpha xy\,e^{-1}I_2
=-\frac{2\varepsilon\eta}{e}I_2.
$$
Thus **the two equal-sign points are strict maxima, and the two opposite-sign points are strict minima**. They are global: $2|xy|\leq r^2$ implies
$$
|f(x,y)|\leq\frac{r^2}{2}e^{-\alpha r^2}
\leq\frac1{2\alpha e},
$$
where the last maximum occurs at $r^2=1/\alpha$ and equality also requires $x^2=y^2$.

As $\alpha\downarrow0$, the four nonzero <critical points> have distance $\alpha^{-1/2}$ from the origin and **escape to infinity**, rather than merging with the origin. Their values diverge as $\pm1/(2e\alpha)$. As $\alpha\uparrow0$ from below there are no extra <critical points>, while the origin stays a saddle throughout. This is <critical points escaping to infinity in a Gaussian-weighted bilinear function>.

= Solution
{parent=12A}

The substitution is nonsingular in the interior $x>0$, $0<y<1$, $0<z<x$. Its inverse is
$$
x=\sqrt{\alpha\beta},\qquad
y=\sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha},\qquad
z=\gamma\sqrt{\frac\alpha\beta}.
$$
Thus $y<1$ is equivalent to $\beta<\alpha$, while $z<x$ is equivalent to $\gamma<xy=\beta$. Positivity gives the full transformed domain
$$
\boxed{0<\gamma<\beta<\alpha<\infty.}
$$
The forward <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\frac{\partial(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}{\partial(x,y,z)}
=\det\begin{pmatrix}
1/y&-x/y^2&0\\
y&x&0\\
0&z&y
\end{pmatrix}
=2x>0.
$$
Therefore $x\,dx\,dy\,dz=\tfrac12\,d\alpha\,d\beta\,d\gamma$. The <boundary> faces where the formula fails have measure zero. The <change of variables formula> gives
$$
I=\frac12\int_0^\infty d\alpha\int_0^\alpha d\beta\int_0^\beta d\gamma\,
e^{-A\alpha-B\beta-C\gamma}.
$$
The integrand is nonnegative, so <Tonelli's theorem> permits reordering the <integration> even before convergence has been established. Integrating first in $\alpha$, then in $\beta$, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\frac12\int_0^\infty e^{-C\gamma}\,d\gamma
\int_\gamma^\infty e^{-B\beta}\,d\beta
\int_\beta^\infty e^{-A\alpha}\,d\alpha\\
&=\frac1{2A}\int_0^\infty e^{-C\gamma}\,d\gamma
\int_\gamma^\infty e^{-(A+B)\beta}\,d\beta\\
&=\frac1{2A(A+B)}
\int_0^\infty e^{-(A+B+C)\gamma}\,d\gamma\\
&=\boxed{\frac1{2A(A+B)(A+B+C)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The assumptions $A,B,C>0$ make all three integrals of <exponential functions> converge. Equivalently, the <ordered-cone substitution by products and ratios> can be followed by the increment variables $\alpha-\beta$, $\beta-\gamma$, $\gamma$, all independently positive and with <Jacobian determinant> one; the exponent then separates into three linear terms with coefficients $A$, $A+B$ and $A+B+C$.
