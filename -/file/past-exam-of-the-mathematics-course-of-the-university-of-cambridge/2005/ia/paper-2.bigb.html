= Solution
{parent=1B}

An <integrating factor> is
$$
\mu(x)=\exp\left(\int3x^2\,dx\right)=e^{x^3}.
$$
Multiplication by this <integrating factor> turns the <first-order linear differential equation> into $(e^{x^3}y)'=x^2e^{x^3}$. Integrating from zero to $x$ gives
$$
e^{x^3}y(x)-a=\frac13(e^{x^3}-1).
$$
Thus the <initial-value problem> has the solution
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac13+\left(a-\frac13\right)e^{-x^3},\qquad \lim_{x\to+\infty}y(x)=\frac13.}
$$
Indeed, direct <differentiation> gives $y'=-3x^2(a-1/3)e^{-x^3}$, which verifies both the <differential equation> and the <initial condition>.

= Solution
{parent=2B}

Work first on $x>0$, where the proposed <logarithm> is defined. Substitution of $y=x^m$ into the <Cauchy-Euler differential equation> gives the <indicial equation>
$$
m(m-1)+m+1=m^2+1=0.
$$
Its distinct <indicial roots> are $m=\pm i$. Using $x^{\pm i}=e^{\pm i\log x}$ and <Euler's formula>, a real <basis> of solutions is $\cos(\log x),\sin(\log x)$. Their <Wronskian> is $1/x$, so they are <linearly independent> and span the two-dimensional <solution space of a homogeneous linear differential equation>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=A\cos(\log x)+B\sin(\log x),\qquad x>0.}
$$
For the requested <change of variables>, set $Y(z)=y(e^z)$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
y'=\frac{Y'}x,\qquad y''=\frac{Y''-Y'}{x^2}.
$$
The transformed <second-order linear differential equation> is $Y''+Y=0$. Its <characteristic roots> are again $\pm i$, giving $Y(z)=A\cos z+B\sin z$. Substituting $z=\log x$ recovers precisely the previous <general solution>.

If solutions on $x<0$ are wanted, the same calculation uses $z=\log|x|$ and gives independent constants on that interval. Zero is a <singular point of a differential equation>; the logarithmic oscillations do not define a nonzero continuous solution through it.

= Solution
{parent=3F}

Normalize the <probability density function> using $u=t^c$:
$$
1=A_c\int_0^\infty t^{c-1}e^{-t^c}\,dt=\frac{A_c}{c}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,du,
\qquad \boxed{A_c=c.}
$$
The corresponding <Weibull distribution> has <survival function>
$$
S_c(v)=P(X_c\geq v)=\int_v^\infty ct^{c-1}e^{-t^c}\,dt=e^{-v^c},\qquad v\geq0.
$$
Since the distribution is continuous, the choices $>$ and $\geq$ have the same <probabilities>. The <conditional probability> is consequently
$$
P(X_c\geq s+t\mid X_c\geq t)=\frac{S_c(s+t)}{S_c(t)}=e^{-[(s+t)^c-t^c]}.
$$
For $s,t>0$ and $c>1$, the supplied inequality, applied to $s/t$, gives $(s+t)^c>t^c+s^c$. Taking exponentials yields
$$
\boxed{P(X_c\geq s+t\mid X_c\geq t)<e^{-s^c}=P(X_c\geq s).}
$$
This expresses the increasing <hazard function> $h_c(t)=ct^{c-1}$: surviving to a later age shortens the remaining lifetime in this <conditional distribution>. At $c=1$ equality holds, as expected from the <memoryless property> of the <exponential distribution>.

= Solution
{parent=4F}

A <Poisson distribution> with parameter $\lambda>0$ assigns <probability mass function>
$$
P(Z=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
The <exponential series> makes these probabilities sum to one. Shifting the indices in the absolutely convergent sums gives
$$
E Z=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{k\lambda^k}{k!}=\lambda,
\qquad E[Z(Z-1)]=\lambda^2.
$$
Hence $E Z^2=\lambda^2+\lambda$ and
$$
\boxed{E Z=\operatorname{Var}Z=\lambda.}
$$
For <independent random variables> $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ of parameter one, let $S_n=\sum_jZ_j$. Summing their product <probability mass function> over all nonnegative $k_1+\cdots+k_n=k$ gives
$$
P(S_n=k)=e^{-n}\sum_{k_1+\cdots+k_n=k}\frac1{k_1!\cdots k_n!}
=e^{-n}\frac{n^k}{k!}.
$$
The final equality follows from the <multinomial theorem> applied to $(1+\cdots+1)^k$. Thus **$S_n$ has the Poisson distribution of parameter $n$**.

Each $Z_j$ has <expectation> and <variance> one, so the <central limit theorem> gives <convergence in distribution>
$$
\frac{S_n-n}{\sqrt n}\ \Longrightarrow\ N(0,1).
$$
Zero is a continuity point of the <standard normal cumulative distribution function>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{e^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{n^k}{k!}=P(S_n\leq n)
=P\left(\frac{S_n-n}{\sqrt n}\leq0\right)\longrightarrow\frac12.}
$$
The discrete atom at $S_n=n$ causes no difficulty: the <central limit theorem> applies to the entire event with threshold zero.

= Solution
{parent=5B}

The <characteristic equation of a linear recurrence> is
$$
r^2-2\cos\theta\,r+1=0,
\qquad r=e^{\pm i\theta}.
$$
For $0<\theta<\pi$, these <characteristic roots> are distinct. Two real solutions of the <second-order difference equation> are $\cos(n\theta)$ and $\sin(n\theta)$. Their values at $n=0,1$ form a <matrix> with <determinant> $\sin\theta\ne0$, proving <linear independence>. Consequently they span the two-dimensional <vector space> of recurrence solutions.

At $\theta=0$ the two roots coalesce at one. The <linear recurrence> becomes $X_{n+2}-X_{n+1}=X_{n+1}-X_n$, so its successive differences are constant. A <basis> is therefore **$1,n$**, and every solution is $A+Bn$.

For the specified <initial conditions>, the zero-angle solution is $X_n(0)=n$. For nonzero angle, it is convenient to center the <basis> at $n=1$:
$$
\boxed{X_n(\theta)=\cos((n-1)\theta)
+\frac{2-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\sin((n-1)\theta),\qquad 0<\theta<\pi.}
$$
This gives $X_1=1$ and $X_2=\cos\theta+2-\cos\theta=2$, and each summand obeys the <linear recurrence>. For every fixed integer $n\geq1$, the elementary <limit> $\sin z/z\to1$ gives
$$
\frac{\sin((n-1)\theta)}{\sin\theta}\longrightarrow n-1,
\qquad \boxed{X_n(\theta)\longrightarrow1+(n-1)=n=X_n(0).}
$$
The convergence here is for fixed $n$; it does not assert uniform convergence over all indices.

{parent=Paper 2}

= Solution
{parent=6B}

Substitution of the <power series>, with termwise <differentiation>, gives the coefficient identity
$$
-(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}+2na_n=Ea_n,
\qquad a_{n+2}=\frac{2n-E}{(n+2)(n+1)}a_n.
$$
Thus $a_0$ and $a_1$ are arbitrary, and all <power series> solutions of the <Hermite differential equation> are
$$
\boxed{W(x)=a_0\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{\prod_{r=0}^{j-1}(4r-E)}{(2j)!}x^{2j}
+a_1\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{\prod_{r=0}^{j-1}(4r+2-E)}{(2j+1)!}x^{2j+1}.}
$$
An empty product is one. For a nonterminating branch the ratio of consecutive terms in its own parity subsequence is asymptotic to $|x|^2/j$, hence tends to zero for every fixed $x$. The <ratio test> proves convergence everywhere; the resulting <power series> can be differentiated termwise and indeed solve the <differential equation>. A terminating branch is already a <polynomial>.

The <boundary condition> $W(0)=0$ forces $a_0=0$; the solution is odd. Apart from the zero solution, which works for every $E$, a <polynomial> occurs exactly when an odd index $d$ satisfies $2d-E=0$. Before that index the odd coefficients are nonzero, and after it they vanish. Thus the <odd polynomial solutions of the Hermite differential equation> have
$$
\boxed{E=4j+2,\quad \deg W=2j+1,\quad j=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Equivalently, comparison of leading <polynomial> coefficients gives $E=2\deg W$, proving that no other nonzero polynomial is possible. Normalizing by $a_1=1$, the <polynomials> of degree below six are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
E&W(x)\\\hline
2&x\\
6&x-\frac23x^3\\
10&x-\frac43x^3+\frac4{15}x^5.
\end{array}
$$
Each can be multiplied by an arbitrary nonzero constant; these are normalized odd <Hermite polynomials>.

Let $L=-d^2/dx^2+2x\,d/dx$. The forcing in the final <inhomogeneous linear differential equation> is precisely the displayed degree-five <eigenfunction> $P_5$, with $LP_5=10P_5$. Hence
$$
\boxed{W(x)=\frac1{10}P_5(x)=\frac{x}{10}-\frac{2x^3}{15}+\frac{2x^5}{75}.}
$$
Direct <differentiation> gives $LW=x-4x^3/3+4x^5/15$, and $W(0)=0$. Among <polynomial> solutions this choice is unique: a polynomial in the <kernel> of $L$ must be constant, and its value at zero eliminates that constant.

{parent=Paper 2}

{title2=Differential Equations}

= Solution
{parent=7B}

For $r>0$, the <polar coordinates> satisfy
$$
\dot r=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{r}=r(1-r^2),
\qquad \dot\theta=\frac{x\dot y-y\dot x}{r^2}=1.
$$
A positive constant radius must therefore be one. The nonconstant <periodic solutions> are
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\cos(t+\theta_0),\qquad y(t)=\sin(t+\theta_0),}
$$
with least <period> $2\pi$ and counterclockwise orientation. The origin is also a stationary solution, hence periodic in the degenerate sense of a constant function; it is a point rather than a nontrivial closed <orbit>. There are no other constant-radius solutions.

For $r_0>0$, put $v=r^2$. Then $\dot v=2v(1-v)$, a <logistic differential equation>. Solving this <separable differential equation> gives
$$
\boxed{r(t)=\left[1+(r_0^{-2}-1)e^{-2t}\right]^{-1/2},
\qquad \theta(t)=\theta_0+t,\qquad t\geq0.}
$$
The denominator is positive for every forward time. When $0<r_0<1$, the radius increases monotonically to one; when $r_0>1$, it decreases monotonically to one; and when $r_0=1$ it stays there. Thus every nonzero <orbit> approaches the unit circle while continuing to rotate. More precisely, its distance from the phase-matched circular solution tends to zero; its Cartesian coordinates do not converge to a single point. For $r_0=0$, the solution stays at the origin.

The origin is the only <equilibrium point>. Its <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J(0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose <eigenvalues> are $1\pm i$. It is an unstable <focus>. The unit circle is an attracting <limit cycle>, rather than a curve of equilibria: on it the vector field remains nonzero. Linearizing the radial equation at $r=1$ gives $\dot{\delta r}=-2\delta r+O(\delta r^2)$, confirming radial attraction. All other radii are strictly monotone, so there are no additional nontrivial <periodic orbits>.

= Solution
{parent=8B}

On an interval where $p,q$ are continuous, define the <Wronskian>
$$
W[u_1,u_2]=\det\begin{pmatrix}u_1&u_2\\u_1'&u_2'\end{pmatrix}
=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2.
$$
Using the two <second-order linear differential equations> to eliminate the second derivatives gives
$$
W'=u_1u_2''-u_1''u_2=-pW.
$$
The <integrating factor> yields the <Abel identity>
$$
\boxed{W(x)=W(x_0)\exp\left(-\int_{x_0}^x p(s)\,ds\right).}
$$
Thus the <Wronskian> either vanishes identically or is nonzero everywhere on the interval. If $u_1,u_2$ are <linearly dependent>, it is identically zero. Conversely, if $W(x_0)=0$, the two initial-data vectors are dependent, so some nontrivial linear combination has both value and first derivative zero at $x_0$. The <uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations> makes that combination identically zero. Hence **the solutions are linearly independent exactly when their Wronskian is nonzero at one, and therefore every, point**. Continuity and the common differential equation are essential to this converse.

For the particular equation, divide by $x^2$ on either interval $x>0$ or $x<0$. The coefficient of $y'$ is zero, so its <Wronskian> is constant there. The proposed $y_1=x^2$ satisfies $x^2y_1''-2y_1=2x^2-2x^2=0$. To construct a second solution by <reduction of order>, impose
$$
x^2y_2'-2xy_2=C\ne0.
$$
Dividing by $x^4$ shows that $(y_2/x^2)'=C/x^4$, giving
$$
y_2=Dx^2-\frac{C}{3x}.
$$
Taking $C=-3,D=0$ produces
$$
\boxed{y_2(x)=x^{-1},\qquad W[x^2,x^{-1}]=-3.}
$$
The nonzero <Wronskian> proves <linear independence>; hence the <general solution> on either nonsingular interval is $y=Ax^2+B/x$. Zero is a <singular point of a differential equation> and is excluded from this construction.

{parent=Paper 2}

= Solution
{parent=9F}

The <expectation> of a complex random quantity is defined through its real and imaginary parts. Both $\cos X$ and $\sin X$ are bounded, so all expectations below exist. Expand the addition formulae and apply the <expectation of a product of independent random variables> identity:
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\cos(X+Y)&=E\cos X\,E\cos Y-E\sin X\,E\sin Y,\\
E\sin(X+Y)&=E\sin X\,E\cos Y+E\cos X\,E\sin Y.
\end{aligned}
$$
Combining real and imaginary parts proves
$$
\boxed{E e^{i(X+Y)}=(E e^{iX})(E e^{iY}).}
$$
For the <simple symmetric random walk>, write $W_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\xi_j$, with independent increments satisfying $P(\xi_j=1)=P(\xi_j=-1)=1/2$. The usual casino model uses these independent, or conditionally fair, increments. Their <characteristic function> is $(e^{iu}+e^{-iu})/2=\cos u$, so repeated factorization gives
$$
\boxed{\phi(u)=E e^{iuW_n}=(\cos u)^n.}
$$
This is real for real $u$. Equivalently, the <distribution> of $W_n$ is symmetric, making $E\sin(uW_n)=0$.

For any integer $k$, <Fourier orthogonality> gives
$$
\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{iuk}\,du=\mathbf1_{\{k=0\}}.
$$
The <random walk> has finite support, so we may interchange its finite <expectation> with this integral. Therefore
$$
P(W_n=0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(\cos u)^n\,du.
$$
For even $n$, evenness and reflection symmetry about $\pi/2$ reduce this to
$$
\boxed{P(W_n=0)=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos u)^n\,du,\qquad \gamma=\frac2\pi.}
$$
For odd $n$, $W_n$ has odd parity and cannot be zero, so **$P(W_n=0)=0$**. As a separate combinatorial check, for $n=2m$ the return <probability> is $\binom{2m}{m}/2^{2m}$, since exactly $m$ positive increments are required.

{parent=Paper 2}

{title2=Probability}

= Solution
{parent=10F}

Use the intended model of <independent random variables> $A,B$ with <exponential distributions> of rates $\alpha,\beta>0$. For $M=\min(A,B)$, independence gives the <survival function>
$$
P(M>t)=P(A>t)P(B>t)=e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t},\qquad t\geq0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{M\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\alpha+\beta),\qquad
f_M(t)=(\alpha+\beta)e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}\ (t>0),\qquad
\operatorname{sd}(M)=\frac1{\alpha+\beta}.}
$$
The <variance> is $1/(\alpha+\beta)^2$, obtained from the standard first two exponential moments, $E M=1/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $E M^2=2/(\alpha+\beta)^2$.

For these <competing exponential clocks>, ties have <probability> zero. Integrating over Alice's firing time gives
$$
\boxed{P(A<B)=\int_0^\infty\alpha e^{-\alpha t}e^{-\beta t}\,dt
=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}.}
$$
Let $H$ denote the event that the next shot hits, and use the stated success probability as $P(H\mid A<B)=1/2$. Bill's corresponding conditional success probability is one. The <law of total probability> gives
$$
P(H)=\frac12\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.
$$
The <Bayes theorem> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{P(A<B\mid H)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2\beta}.}
$$

The printed assumptions specify the two marginal <exponential distributions> but do not explicitly specify their independence. The answers above require that extra assumption; marginals alone do not determine the race. For a concrete counterexample, take one $Z\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1)$ and set $A=Z/\alpha$, $B=Z/\beta$. These have the required marginals, but $M\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\max(\alpha,\beta))$; if $\alpha>\beta$, Alice always fires first and $P(A<B\mid H)=1$. Thus the independence qualification changes actual answers, rather than merely their derivation.

{parent=Paper 2}

{title2=Probability}

= Solution
{parent=11F}

All the following <distributions> are conditional on the point lying in the unit disk. Initially the <joint probability density> of $(S,T)$ is $1/4$ on the square. The disk has area $\pi$, so the conditioning event has <probability> $\pi/4$. Dividing by this probability gives the constant conditional <density> $1/\pi$ on the disk, proving its <uniform distribution> there.

The <polar coordinates> $S=r\cos\theta,T=r\sin\theta$ have <Jacobian determinant> $r$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac r\pi,\qquad 0<r<1,\quad0\leq\theta<2\pi.}
$$
Integrating the <joint probability density> gives $f_R(r)=2r$ and $f_\Theta(\theta)=1/(2\pi)$. Since the joint density equals the product of these marginal densities on a product support, **$R$ and $\Theta$ are independent**. In particular $U=R^2$ has the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$.

The second <change of variables> preserves the angle and changes the radius to
$$
\boxed{Q=\sqrt{-2\log(R^2)}=\sqrt{-2\log U},\qquad\Psi=\Theta.}
$$
The event $R=0$ has probability zero, so arbitrary definitions at that point have no effect on the distribution. For $q\geq0$, monotonicity of the <logarithm> gives
$$
P(Q\leq q)=P(U\geq e^{-q^2/2})=1-e^{-q^2/2},
\qquad f_Q(q)=q e^{-q^2/2}\quad(q>0).
$$
Since $Q$ is a function of $R$, it remains <independent> of the uniform angle. Hence the new <joint probability density> is
$$
\boxed{f_{Q,\Psi}(q,\psi)=\frac{q}{2\pi}e^{-q^2/2},\qquad q>0,\quad0\leq\psi<2\pi.}
$$
Finally, $(X,Y)=(Q\cos\Psi,Q\sin\Psi)$ has polar <Jacobian determinant> $q$. Dividing the preceding density by $q$ gives the Cartesian <joint probability density>
$$
f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac1{2\pi}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}
=\left(\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)
\left(\frac{e^{-y^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right).
$$
This factors into two standard <normal distribution> densities on $\mathbb R^2$. Thus **$X$ and $Y$ are independent, each with mean zero and variance one**. This derivation is the radial form of the <Box-Muller transform>.

= 12F
{parent=Paper 2}
{scope}
{title2=Probability}

= Solution
{parent=12F}

For each $i$, let $R_i$ be the relative rank of $a_i$ among $a_1,\ldots,a_i$, with the smallest value assigned rank one. Then $Y_i=\mathbf1_{\{R_i=1\}}$. The <independent relative ranks of a uniform random permutation> can be proved directly: for any choices $r_i\in\{1,\ldots,i\}$, insert the index $i$ at position $r_i$ in the list of previous indices ordered by their values. After all insertions, assign the values $1,\ldots,n$ along that list. This is a unique <permutation> with those relative ranks, and conversely every permutation yields one such insertion sequence.

There are $\prod_{i=1}^ni=n!$ possible insertion sequences, all equally likely because the <uniform random permutation> is uniform. Thus
$$
P(R_1=r_1,\ldots,R_n=r_n)=\frac1{n!}=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac1i.
$$
The ranks are <independent> and each is uniform on its $i$ possible values. It follows that the <independent record indicators> have
$$
\boxed{P(Y_i=1)=\frac1i,\quad P(Y_i=0)=1-\frac1i,\qquad Y_i\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/i).}
$$
The first indicator is the constant one, which is also independent of the others. For $K_n=\sum_{i=1}^nY_i$, additivity of <expectation> and <variance additivity for independent random variables> give
$$
\boxed{E K_n=H_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1i,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}K_n=H_n-\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{i^2}.}
$$
Here $H_n$ is the <harmonic number>.

Let $T$ be the year index of the second record. For $2\leq i\leq n$, independence gives
$$
\boxed{P(T=i)=\left[\prod_{j=2}^{i-1}\left(1-\frac1j\right)\right]\frac1i
=\frac1{i(i-1)}.}
$$
The empty product for $i=2$ is one. There is no second record in the first $n$ years with <probability>
$$
P(T>n)=\prod_{j=2}^n\frac{j-1}{j}=\frac1n.
$$
This exposes a finite-horizon qualification in the final request: a permutation of length $n$ alone does not specify when a record outside those years will occur. Under the usual continuation by an infinite sequence of independent, identically distributed observations with a continuous distribution, the same relative-rank argument holds for every finite prefix. Then $P(T=i)=1/[i(i-1)]$ for every $i\geq2$, and $P(T>m)=1/m$. These probabilities sum to one, so the second record occurs almost surely, but
$$
\boxed{E T=\sum_{i=2}^\infty\frac1{i-1}=\infty.}
$$
The expected number of additional years after year one, $E(T-1)$, is also infinite. This is the first-sample case of the <waiting time for a new record after a fixed sample>.

If instead one asks for the mean year conditional on the second record occurring within the stated $n$ years, then, for $n\geq2$,
$$
\boxed{E[T\mid T\leq n]=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^n1/(i-1)}{1-1/n}
=\frac{n}{n-1}H_{n-1}.}
$$
The mean additional wait under that conditioning is this value minus one. These are distinct questions: assigning $T=\infty$ whenever no second record occurs in the finite horizon also gives an infinite unconditional mean, whereas a censored observation $\min(T,n)$ has finite <expectation> $1+H_{n-1}$ by the <tail-sum formula for expectation>.
