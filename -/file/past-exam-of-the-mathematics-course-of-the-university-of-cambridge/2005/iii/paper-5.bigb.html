= Solution
{parent=1}

For <conjugate exponents> $p,q\in[1,\infty]$, $1/p+1/q=1$, <Hölder's inequality> states that $\int|fg|\leq\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$. If $r=\infty$ in the requested product estimate, every $p_i=\infty$ and multiplication of essential bounds proves it directly. Suppose $r<\infty$. Factors with $p_i=\infty$ can again be bounded pointwise by their <essential suprema>.

Here is an explicit finite-exponent proof of the remaining <generalized Holder inequality>. Put $q_i=p_i/r$, so $\sum_i1/q_i=1$, and normalize $h_i=|f_i|^r/\|f_i\|_{p_i}^r$. If one <norm> is zero the product vanishes <almost everywhere>. Otherwise $\int h_i^{q_i}=1$. The weighted <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality>, applied to $h_i^{q_i}$ with weights $1/q_i$, gives
$$
\prod_i h_i\leq\sum_i\frac{h_i^{q_i}}{q_i}.
$$
Integrating gives $\int\prod_i h_i\leq1$. If only one finite exponent remains, its weight is one and the same argument is an equality. Restoring the normalization and taking the $r$th root proves
$$
\boxed{\|f_1\cdots f_n\|_r\leq\prod_{i=1}^n\|f_i\|_{p_i}.}
$$
In particular the product belongs to $L^r$.

For the projection estimate, use the three-dimensional <functional Loomis-Whitney inequality>
$$
\int_{\mathbb R^3}
\bigl(g_1(x_2,x_3)g_2(x_1,x_3)g_3(x_1,x_2)\bigr)^{1/2}\,dx
\leq\prod_{j=1}^3\left(\int_{\mathbb R^2}g_j\right)^{1/2}.
$$
This permitted three-dimensional result also follows directly by <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>: integrate the <square root> of $g_1g_2$ in $x_3$, obtaining the <square root> of the product of their marginal <integrals>, and then apply <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> in $(x_1,x_2)$ against $\sqrt{g_3}$.

Let $f_j$ be any nonnegative <integrable function> of the three coordinates excluding $x_j$. For $j=1,2,3$, define $G_j$ by integrating $f_j$ in $x_4$. First <Hölder's inequality> with three equal exponents yields
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\prod_{j=1}^3 f_j(\widehat x_j)^{1/3}\,dx_4
\leq\prod_{j=1}^3G_j^{1/3}.
$$
The fourth factor $f_4(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ is independent of $x_4$. A further <Hölder's inequality> with exponents $3$ and $3/2$, followed by the three-dimensional estimate, therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R^4}\prod_{j=1}^4f_j(\widehat x_j)^{1/3}\,dx
&\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb R^3}f_4\right)^{1/3}
\left(\int_{\mathbb R^3}\prod_{j=1}^3G_j^{1/2}\right)^{2/3}\\
&\leq\prod_{j=1}^4\left(\int_{\mathbb R^3}f_j\right)^{1/3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The marginal-integral identifications use <Tonelli's theorem>. Now take $f_j=\mathbf1_{K_j}$. The projections are compact and hence measurable with <finite measure>. Every $x\in K$ has all its projected coordinates in the corresponding $K_j$, so $\mathbf1_K(x)\leq\prod_j\mathbf1_{K_j}(\widehat x_j)^{1/3}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda_4(K)\leq\left(\prod_{j=1}^4\lambda_3(K_j)\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
This includes zero-measure projections and is sharp for axis-parallel rectangular boxes.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Read equality of the two positive <series> as equality of a finite total $M$, so the sequences are summable. This is the <majorization of summable sequences> setting. If equality is instead allowed to mean $\infty=\infty$, the assertion is false: the strictly decreasing sequences $x_i=1+1/(i+1)$ and $y_i=2+1/(i+1)$ satisfy every partial-sum inequality and have divergent equal extended totals. But a normalized nonnegative row of any <doubly stochastic matrix> averages numbers all at least $2$, and cannot give $x_i<2$. We prove the intended finite-total assertion constructively.

Since $y_j\to0$ and $0<x_1\leq y_1$, there is a finite $k$ such that $y_k\geq x_1>y_{k+1}$. Put
$$
t=\frac{x_1-y_{k+1}}{y_k-y_{k+1}}\in(0,1],
\qquad u=y_k+y_{k+1}-x_1.
$$
The <two-coordinate stochastic averaging> <matrix> sends $(y_k,y_{k+1})$ to $(x_1,u)$. Move the first of these values to the front by a finite <permutation>, leaving the other values in decreasing order. The tail is
$$
z=(y_1,\ldots,y_{k-1},u,y_{k+2},y_{k+3},\ldots),
$$
which is decreasing and strictly positive because $y_{k+1}\leq u\leq y_k$.

We need the residual <majorization> in order to repeat the construction. For $n<k$, the first $n$ values of $z$ are at least $x_1$, while every target-tail value is at most $x_1$. Thus
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n z_j\geq nx_1\geq\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}x_i.
$$
For $n\geq k$, the residual initial sum is the old initial sum with $x_1$ removed:
$$
\sum_{j=1}^nz_j=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}y_j-x_1
\geq\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}x_i.
$$
The tail totals also agree, both equalling $M-x_1$. Therefore the same step applies to $(x_2,x_3,\ldots)$ and $z$.

Iterating, let $P^{(m)}$ be the product of the first $m$ finite <permutations> and embedded averaging <matrices>. Each $P^{(m)}$ is a <doubly stochastic matrix>, each row is a <finitely supported sequence>, and the first $m$ entries of $P^{(m)}y$ are $x_1,\ldots,x_m$. Later operations act only on tail rows, so row $i$ becomes permanently fixed once $m\geq i$. Define $p_{ij}$ to be that stabilized row. Then
$$
p_{ij}\geq0,\qquad \sum_jp_{ij}=1,\qquad
x_i=\sum_jp_{ij}y_j.
$$
For every fixed column $j$ and every $m$, its first $m$ stabilized entries sum to at most one, since they are entries of column $j$ in $P^{(m)}$. Consequently $s_j=\sum_i p_{ij}\leq1$. By <Tonelli's theorem>,
$$
M=\sum_i x_i=\sum_j y_j s_j,\qquad
0=\sum_j y_j(1-s_j).
$$
All $y_j$ are strictly positive and every summand in the last expression is nonnegative, so $s_j=1$ for every $j$. Thus **both the row sums and the column sums are one**, and
$$
\boxed{x=Py\quad\text{with }P\text{ doubly stochastic}.}
$$
The finite-total argument is what prevents loss of column mass in the infinite construction. This completes the <doubly stochastic realization of summable-sequence majorization>.

A nonnegative <sublinear operator> satisfies $S(f+g)\leq S(f)+S(g)$ and $S(cf)=|c|S(f)$ <almost everywhere>. It has <weak type (1,1)> if there is a finite constant $C$, independent of the function and threshold, such that
$$
\boxed{\lambda\{x:S(f)(x)>\alpha\}\leq
\frac{C}{\alpha}\|f\|_{L^1(\mathbb R)}
\quad\text{for every }f\in L^1(\mathbb R),\ \alpha>0.}
$$
For a signed or complex output use $|S(f)|$ in the <level set>. The bound controls the distribution of large values; it does not assert that $S(f)$ is integrable.

The domination hypothesis includes $r=0$. For any approximation error $h$, <linearity> and the <triangle inequality> therefore give the uniform estimate
$$
|T_rh-T_0h|\leq|T_rh|+|T_0h|\leq2S(h).
$$
For a general input $g$ and an approximating $f$, this implies
$$
|T_rg-T_0g|
\leq|T_rf-T_0f|+2S(g-f).
$$
The first term converges on the dense class, while the <weak type (1,1)> estimate controls the exceptional set where the second is large.

The printed symbol $E$ is not defined. Take $E=L^1(\mathbb R)$, or more generally an $L^1$ subspace with its induced $L^1$ <norm>; density must be in that <norm>. Given $g\in E$, choose a sequence $f_n\in F$ with
$$
\|g-f_n\|_1\leq2^{-2n}.
$$
By the assumed convergence on $F$, each $T_rf_n\to T_0f_n$ holds outside a <null set> $N_n$. Their <countable union> $N=\bigcup_nN_n$ is still null. Thus outside $N$ the convergence statements for every member of this single approximation sequence hold simultaneously. No assertion of simultaneous convergence for the entire uncountable dense class is needed.

= Solution
{parent=Paper 5/3/ii}

Use the approximants just chosen. The <weak type (1,1)> bound gives
$$
\lambda\{S(g-f_n)>2^{-n}\}
\leq C\,2^n\|g-f_n\|_1\leq C2^{-n}.
$$
The <measures> of these exceptional sets are summable. Indeed, the <measure> of their union for $n\geq m$ is at most $C\sum_{n\geq m}2^{-n}\to0$. Hence their limsup is null; this is the <Borel-Cantelli first lemma>. Outside it, $S(g-f_n)\to0$.

Remove also the countable <null set> from part (b). At each remaining point and for every $n$, the estimate in part (a) yields
$$
\limsup_{r\downarrow0}|T_rg-T_0g|
\leq2S(g-f_n).
$$
Letting $n\to\infty$ makes the right side zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{T_rg\longrightarrow T_0g\quad\text{almost everywhere as }r\downarrow0.}
$$
This proves <almost-everywhere convergence from weak-type domination>. The argument uses countably many measurable exceptional sets and does not assume that a supremum over all real parameters is measurable.

Let $U_\alpha=\{x:m_u(f)(x)>\alpha\}$. It is open: it is the union of all <open intervals> $I$ for which $\int_I|f|>\alpha|I|$. This also proves <measurability> of the <uncentered maximal function>. <Triangle inequality> inside each average proves sublinearity and absolute homogeneity.

Take a <compact subset> $K\subset U_\alpha$, and choose finitely many such intervals covering it. The one-dimensional <Wiener covering lemma> selects pairwise disjoint intervals $I_1,\ldots,I_N$ whose concentric triples cover $K$. For a finite family this follows by choosing a longest remaining interval and discarding all intervals intersecting it: each discarded interval has no greater length and lies in the triple of the chosen one. Thus
$$
\lambda(K)\leq3\sum_{j=1}^N|I_j|
<\frac3\alpha\sum_{j=1}^N\int_{I_j}|f|
\leq\frac3\alpha\|f\|_1.
$$
Taking the supremum over <compact subsets>, by <inner regularity of Lebesgue measure>, gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda\{m_u(f)>\alpha\}\leq
\frac3\alpha\|f\|_1.}
$$
This establishes <weak type (1,1)>. It is the one-dimensional <uncentered maximal weak-type inequality> for the <finite measure> $|f(t)|\,dt$; the constant need not be optimal for this question.

= Solution

For $r>0$, define the forward and backward <one-sided interval averaging operators>
$$
A_r^+f(x)=\frac1r\int_x^{x+r}f(t)\,dt,\qquad
A_r^-f(x)=\frac1r\int_{x-r}^{x}f(t)\,dt,
$$
and set $A_0^\pm f=f$. Each positive-radius average is bounded in <absolute value> by $m_u(f)(x)$. Although $x$ is an endpoint of its averaging interval, enlarge that interval slightly so that $x$ is interior, apply the maximal bound and let the enlargement shrink to zero.

To dominate the limiting identity too without assuming the <differentiation> result we are proving, use the <sublinear operator>
$$
S(f)=m_u(f)+|f|.
$$
The maximal estimate and <Chebyshev inequality> imply
$$
\lambda\{S(f)>\alpha\}
\leq\lambda\{m_u(f)>\alpha/2\}
+\lambda\{|f|>\alpha/2\}
\leq\frac8\alpha\|f\|_1.
$$
Thus $S$ has <weak type (1,1)> and dominates every $A_r^\pm$, including $r=0$.

Smooth <compactly supported> functions are dense in $L^1$, obtained by truncating and then smoothing with a <compactly supported> <approximate identity>. For each such <continuous function> both averages tend to its value at every point. Applying part (ii) to the two families, and intersecting their full-measure convergence sets, gives $A_r^\pm f(x)\to f(x)$ <almost everywhere>.

The forward <difference quotient> of the primitive is $A_r^+f(x)$, and the backward quotient is $A_r^-f(x)$:
$$
\frac{F(x+r)-F(x)}r=A_r^+f(x),\qquad
\frac{F(x-r)-F(x)}{-r}=A_r^-f(x).
$$
They have the same limit, proving **<differentiability> <almost everywhere>**, with the stronger conclusion
$$
\boxed{F'(x)=f(x)\quad\text{almost everywhere}.}
$$
This proves <differentiation of an indefinite Lebesgue integral> directly from the maximal estimate and density, without assuming $m_u(f)\geq|f|$ as an unproved prerequisite.

The interpolated exponent is strictly between $p_0$ and $p_1$. Apply <Hölder's inequality> to $|f|^p=|f|^{p(1-\theta)}|f|^{p\theta}$ with <conjugate exponents> $p_0/[p(1-\theta)]$ and $p_1/(p\theta)$. Their reciprocals sum to one, so
$$
\boxed{\|f\|_p\leq\|f\|_{p_0}^{1-\theta}\|f\|_{p_1}^{\theta}.}
$$
This gives $L^{p_0}\cap L^{p_1}\subseteq L^p$.

For the other inclusion take any $a>0$ and use the <threshold decomposition between Lp spaces>
$$
g=f\mathbf1_{\{|f|>a\}},\qquad
h=f\mathbf1_{\{|f|\leq a\}}.
$$
Because $p_0-p<0<p_1-p$,
$$
\int|g|^{p_0}\leq a^{p_0-p}\int|f|^p,\qquad
\int|h|^{p_1}\leq a^{p_1-p}\int|f|^p.
$$
Thus $g\in L^{p_0}$, $h\in L^{p_1}$ and $f=g+h$, proving $L^p\subseteq L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}$. Raising the two <norm> bounds to powers $1-\theta$ and $\theta$ gives a power of $a$ equal to
$$
(1-\theta)(1-p/p_0)+\theta(1-p/p_1)=0,
$$
and a power of $\|f\|_p$ equal to one. Hence
$$
\boxed{\|g\|_{p_0}^{1-\theta}\|h\|_{p_1}^{\theta}\leq\|f\|_p.}
$$
For later use, choose $a=\|f\|_p$ when $f\ne0$. Each endpoint <norm> is then at most $\|f\|_p$, so $\|f\|_{L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}}\leq2\|f\|_p$ for the <sum of Lp spaces> <norm>. If $f=0$, take $g=h=0$.

= Solution

Put $C=M_0^{1-\theta}M_1^\theta$. Since $p<\infty$, choose <simple functions> $f_n$ of finite-measure support with $f_n\to f$ in $L^p$. These belong to both endpoint domain spaces. The given simple-function estimate applies also to their differences, so
$$
\|Tf_n-Tf_m\|_q\leq C\|f_n-f_m\|_p.
$$
By <completeness of Lp spaces>, including $L^\infty$, there is $u\in L^q$ with $Tf_n\to u$ in $L^q$. Taking limits of the simple-function bound yields $\|u\|_q\leq C\|f\|_p$.

It remains to identify $u$ with the already defined $Tf$, rather than just define an unrelated extension. Write $X=L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}$ and $Y=L^{q_0}+L^{q_1}$ with their sum <norms>. The endpoint estimates imply
$$
\|Tv\|_Y\leq\max(M_0,M_1)\|v\|_X.
$$
Indeed, split $v=v_0+v_1$, bound the two images in their endpoint <norms>, and take the infimum over all splits. Part (i) gives $\|f_n-f\|_X\leq2\|f_n-f\|_p$, hence $Tf_n\to Tf$ in $Y$.

The inclusion $L^q\subseteq Y$ is also continuous. For distinct finite endpoints this is the same threshold argument as part (i), after ordering the endpoints. If the larger endpoint is infinity, splitting at $a=\|v\|_q$ bounds both the lower endpoint <norm> of the large-value part and the <supremum norm> of the small-value part by $\|v\|_q$. If the endpoints coincide, the inclusion is immediate. Thus $Tf_n\to u$ also in $Y$. Uniqueness of limits in the sum <norm> gives $u=Tf$ <almost everywhere>. Consequently the <Riesz-Thorin theorem> estimate extends as
$$
\boxed{\|Tf\|_q\leq M_0^{1-\theta}M_1^\theta\|f\|_p
\quad\text{for every }f\in L^p.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Consider the complex-linear two-coordinate map $H(x,y)=(x+y,x-y)$. The <triangle inequality> shows that its <operator norm> from $\ell^1$ to $\ell^\infty$ is one; equality is attained at $(1,0)$. The complex parallelogram identity
$$
|x+y|^2+|x-y|^2=2(|x|^2+|y|^2)
$$
shows that its <norm> from $\ell^2$ to $\ell^2$ is $\sqrt2$. Interpolate these endpoint bounds by the <Riesz-Thorin theorem> with parameter $\eta=2/p$. Then
$$
\frac1{p'}=1-\frac{\eta}{2},\qquad
\frac1p=\frac{\eta}{2},\qquad
\|H:\ell^{p'}\to\ell^p\|\leq(\sqrt2)^\eta=2^{1/p}.
$$
Dividing the output <norm> by $2^{1/p}$ proves the scalar estimate:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{|x+y|^p+|x-y|^p}{2}\right)^{1/p}
\leq(|x|^{p'}+|y|^{p'})^{1/p'}.}
$$
Apply it pointwise to $f,g$ and integrate its $p$th power. With $r=p/p'=p-1>1$, this gives
$$
\left(\frac{\|f+g\|_p^p+\|f-g\|_p^p}{2}\right)^{1/p}
\leq\bigl\||f|^{p'}+|g|^{p'}\bigr\|_r^{1/p'}.
$$
By <Minkowski inequality>, the expression on the right is at most
$$
\left(\bigl\||f|^{p'}\bigr\|_r+
\bigl\||g|^{p'}\bigr\|_r\right)^{1/p'}
=\left(\|f\|_p^{p'}+\|g\|_p^{p'}\right)^{1/p'}.
$$
Therefore **the requested function inequality holds**, namely the <dual-exponent Clarkson inequality>.

= Solution
{parent=5}

First use independent fair signs $\varepsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}$, the <Rademacher distribution> convention for <Bernoulli random variables> in this setting. Let $\Omega=\{-1,1\}^d$ with uniform <probability>, write $s(\omega)=\sum_i\omega_i a_i$ and $F(\omega)=\|s(\omega)\|$. We prove the <sharp Rademacher second-moment inequality> by a complete finite-cube calculation.

For a function on this cube define the <coordinate-flip generator on a hypercube>
$$
Lu(\omega)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^d
\bigl(u(\omega^{(i)})-u(\omega)\bigr),
$$
where $\omega^{(i)}$ flips just coordinate $i$. The <Walsh characters> $\chi_A(\omega)=\prod_{i\in A}\omega_i$ form an <orthonormal basis>: distinct characters have expectation-zero product, and there are $2^d$ of them. Flipping a coordinate in $A$ changes its sign and other flips leave it unchanged, so $L\chi_A=-|A|\chi_A$. Expand the real function $F$ as $\sum_A\widehat F(A)\chi_A$. Since $F(-\omega)=F(\omega)$, changing variables $\omega\mapsto-\omega$ shows that $\widehat F(A)=0$ whenever $|A|$ is odd. Thus
$$
-\mathbb E[FLF]=\sum_A|A|\widehat F(A)^2
\geq2\sum_{A\ne\varnothing}\widehat F(A)^2
=2\operatorname{Var}(F).
$$
This derives the <even-function spectral gap on a hypercube>, rather than assuming it.

At each vertex with $s(\omega)\ne0$, choose a <norming functional> $\ell_\omega$ of <norm> one satisfying $\ell_\omega(s(\omega))=F(\omega)$, supplied by the <Hahn-Banach theorem>. For complex $E$ take <real parts> in the following inequalities. At a vertex with $s(\omega)=0$, use the zero functional. The supporting inequality for the <norm> gives
$$
F(\omega^{(i)})-F(\omega)
\geq\operatorname{Re}\ell_\omega
\bigl(s(\omega^{(i)})-s(\omega)\bigr).
$$
But $Ls=-s$, since every coefficient is a degree-one <Walsh character>. Summing the preceding inequalities with factor $1/2$ therefore proves $LF(\omega)\geq-F(\omega)$. As $F\geq0$,
$$
2\bigl(\mathbb EF^2-(\mathbb EF)^2\bigr)
\leq-\mathbb E[FLF]\leq\mathbb EF^2.
$$
Rearranging and taking <square roots> yields
$$
\boxed{\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i\right\|_{L^2(E)}
\leq\sqrt2\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i\right\|_{L^1(E)}.}
$$
No completeness of $E$ is needed. The constant is sharp: with two identical nonzero scalar coefficients, the absolute sum is zero with <probability> $1/2$ and twice the coefficient magnitude with <probability> $1/2$.

If Bernoulli instead means fair $\{0,1\}$ variables $b_i$, write $b_i=(1+\varepsilon_i)/2$ and $a=\sum_i a_i$. Add an independent fair sign $\varepsilon_0$ and apply the proved estimate to $Y=\varepsilon_0a+\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i$. Conditional on either sign of $\varepsilon_0$, $\|Y\|$ has the same distribution as $\|a+\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i\|$, by symmetry of the sign sum. Hence $\|Y\|_{L^p(E)}=2\|\sum_i b_i a_i\|_{L^p(E)}$ for $p=1,2$, proving the same bound in that convention.

The constant is documented in \a[https://www.impan.pl/en/publishing-house/journals-and-series/studia-mathematica/all/109/1/108398/on-the-best-constant-in-the-khinchin-kahane-inequality][the primary sharp-constant study].

Fairness is essential. For a general $\{0,1\}$ <Bernoulli random variable> with success <probability> $0<t<1/2$, a single nonzero scalar coefficient gives $L^2/L^1=1/\sqrt t>\sqrt2$. Thus the printed term cannot be read as permitting arbitrary Bernoulli <probabilities>.
