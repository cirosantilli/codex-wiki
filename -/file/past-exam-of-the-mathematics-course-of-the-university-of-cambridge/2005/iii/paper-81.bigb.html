= Solution
{parent=i}

Take positive <volumetric flow rate> in the direction of increasing $x$. For an <arterial pressure wave>, the <characteristic admittance of an arterial wave> gives
$$
\boxed{q_B=Y(P_BE^--P_{BR}E^+).}
$$
The backward wave contributes the opposite flow sign. Write $p_j$ for each junction's complex <pressure> amplitude, suppressing the common $e^{i\omega t}$ factor, and put $r=RY$, $C_\beta=\cos\beta$, $S_\beta=\sin\beta$. The topology is the two paths $K\to L\to N$ and $K\to M\to N$; their intermediate and common distal junctions each have a <hydraulic resistance>. The coordinate convention in the source puts the upstream end at $-\ell$ and the downstream end at zero in each segment. Consequently the <wave transfer matrix of an arterial segment> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}p_u\\q_u\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}C_\beta&iS_\beta/Y\\iYS_\beta&C_\beta\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}p_d\\q_d\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Equivalently $q_u=Y(C_\beta p_u-p_d)/(iS_\beta)$ and $q_d=Y(p_u-C_\beta p_d)/(iS_\beta)$, with their continuous limits understood when $S_\beta=0$.

Symmetry gives $p_L=p_M=p_s$. Distal <mass conservation> and the resistive relation $Q_N=p_N/R$ give
$$
2\frac{Y}{iS_\beta}(p_s-C_\beta p_N)=\frac{p_N}{R},\qquad
p_s=\left(C_\beta+\frac{iS_\beta}{2r}\right)p_N.
$$
At $L$, the incoming flow supplies both its bed and segment $D$, so
$$
p_K=\left(2C_\beta+\frac{iS_\beta}{r}\right)p_s-p_N.
$$
Choose the incident amplitude $P_A$ at $K$. If $P_{AR}$ is the reflected amplitude in vessel $A$, <pressure> <continuity equation> gives $p_K=P_A+P_{AR}$, while inlet flow is $2Y(P_A-P_{AR})=2Y(2P_A-p_K)$. Equating this to the two outgoing segment flows yields
$$
p_s=e^{i\beta}p_K-2iS_\beta P_A.
$$
Eliminating $p_s,p_K$ from these three relations gives the <wave transmission through a resistively loaded arterial loop> result
$$
\boxed{Q_N=P_A RY^2\frac{8e^{-i\beta}}{e^{i\beta}(2RY+1)^2+e^{-i\beta}(2RY-1)}.}
$$
For large $r$ at fixed phase, $p_N=2P_Ae^{-2i\beta}+O(r^{-1})$ and $p_M=C_\beta p_N+O(r^{-1})$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Q_N\sim\frac{2P_A}{R}e^{-2i\beta},\qquad Q_M=Q_L\sim\frac{2P_A}{R}e^{-2i\beta}\cos\beta.}
$$
These leading terms must not be used as nonzero denominators near a phase at which the normal intermediate flow vanishes.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Once $C$ is removed, the remaining connected segments form the chain $K\to L\to N\to M$, of total length $3\ell$. For $RY\gg1$, each <hydraulic resistance> draws a small flow, so to leading order the far end at $M$ is reflecting, with zero segment flow there. Successive <wave transfer matrices of an arterial segment> give
$$
p_N=\cos\beta\,p_M,\qquad p_L=\cos2\beta\,p_M,\qquad
p_K=\cos3\beta\,p_M,\qquad q_K=iY\sin3\beta\,p_M.
$$
The incident-plus-reflected inlet relation remains $q_K=2Y(2P_A-p_K)$. Therefore, defining $D=3e^{3i\beta}+e^{-3i\beta}$,
$$
\boxed{Q_M^{\rm blocked}\sim\frac{8P_A}{RD},\quad
Q_N^{\rm blocked}\sim\frac{8P_A\cos\beta}{RD},\quad
Q_L^{\rm blocked}\sim\frac{8P_A\cos2\beta}{RD}.}
$$
The modulus of $D$ equals $\gamma=|3e^{4i\beta}+e^{-2i\beta}|$, because the expressions differ by a unit-modulus factor. Comparison with the normal flows gives the amplitude ratios
$$
\boxed{\frac{|Q_N^{\rm blocked}|}{|Q_N^{\rm normal}|}\sim\frac{4|\cos\beta|}{\gamma},\qquad
\frac{|Q_M^{\rm blocked}|}{|Q_M^{\rm normal}|}\sim\frac4{\gamma|\cos\beta|},\qquad
\frac{|Q_L^{\rm blocked}|}{|Q_L^{\rm normal}|}\sim\frac{4|\cos2\beta|}{\gamma|\cos\beta|}.}
$$
For small phases the cosines are positive and the absolute values can be removed. The original PDF prints an additional $\cos(\beta/2)$ in the middle numerator. That factor does not follow from the displayed equal-length network: the transfer relation from $M$ to $K$ above fixes $p_M=8P_A/D$, with no such factor. The corrected middle ratio is shown here.

Since $\gamma^2=10+6\cos6\beta$, one has $\gamma=4[1-27\beta^2/8+O(\beta^4)]$. Thus the three corrected ratios, in the order $N,M,L$, are
$$
1+\frac{23}{8}\beta^2+O(\beta^4),\qquad
1+\frac{31}{8}\beta^2+O(\beta^4),\qquad
1+\frac{15}{8}\beta^2+O(\beta^4).
$$
**All three exceed one for sufficiently small nonzero $\beta$; they equal one at zero <frequency>.** This is an oscillatory <arterial wave reflection> effect, not an increase in steady conductance. Removing a branch changes the <standing wave> pattern and the total inlet <pressure> associated with a fixed incident wave. Reflected waves can enhance junction <pressures> and hence peripheral flow amplitudes. Neither total inlet <pressure> nor total inlet flow was held fixed, and no extra energy source is implied.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The first equation is <mass conservation> for an <incompressible flow> whose area may change: local area storage balances the <divergence> of the volume flux $\alpha u$. The two left-hand momentum terms are local acceleration and <convective acceleration>, while $-p_x$ is the driving <pressure gradient> and $-R(\alpha)u$ models viscous drag per unit mass. The <pressure-area law of a collapsible tube> expresses transverse wall balance using internal minus prescribed external <pressure>.

For example, choose a reference area $\mathcal A_*$, length $L$ and <velocity> $U_*$. If dimensional variables are $\mathcal A,s,t_d,v,P$, take
$$
\alpha=\mathcal A/\mathcal A_*,\quad x=s/L,\quad t=U_*t_d/L,\quad
u=v/U_*,\quad p=P/(\rho U_*^2).
$$
The dimensionless external <pressure> uses the same scale. A dimensional friction rate $r_f(\mathcal A)$ becomes $R(\alpha)=Lr_f(\mathcal A_*\alpha)/U_*$, and the dimensional wall <pressure> law is divided by $\rho U_*^2$. These choices make both inertia and the <pressure>-gradient coefficient unity; other equivalent reference scales are possible. On the mechanically stable wall branch, increasing area requires increased transmural <pressure>, whereas an enlarged tube reduces resistance. Thus **$\boxed{\widetilde P'(\alpha)>0,\qquad R'(\alpha)<0}$**.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Steady <mass conservation> makes $\alpha u=Q$. For uniform area, $u=U=Q/\alpha_0$ is constant and the <pressure-area law of a collapsible tube> gives $p_x=p'_e(x)$. The acceleration vanishes, so momentum balance requires
$$
\boxed{p'_e(x)=-R(\alpha_0)\frac{Q}{\alpha_0}.}
$$
The external <pressure> must therefore decrease linearly along a positive-flow tube. Its additive constant is free; the constant transmural <pressure> is $\widetilde P(\alpha_0)$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Put $U=Q/\alpha_0$, $R_0=R(\alpha_0)$, $c_0^2=\alpha_0\widetilde P'(\alpha_0)$ and $\nu=\omega-kU$. Let the area and <velocity> perturbations have amplitudes $a$ and $w$. Linearizing <mass conservation> and momentum, and using $R'_0=-nR_0/\alpha_0$, gives
$$
-i\nu a+ik\alpha_0w=0,\qquad
(R_0-i\nu)w=-ik\frac{c_0^2}{\alpha_0}a+\frac{nR_0U}{\alpha_0}a.
$$
For nonzero $k$, eliminate $w=\nu a/(k\alpha_0)$ to obtain the <friction-induced instability of a collapsible-tube flow> relation
$$
\boxed{\nu^2+iR_0\nu-k^2c_0^2-inkR_0U=0,\qquad\nu=\omega-kU.}
$$
At the stated threshold $nU=c_0$, it factors as
$$
(\nu-kc_0)(\nu+kc_0+iR_0)=0.
$$
Therefore **one mode has real $\boxed{\omega=k(U+c_0)}$**, while the other has $\omega=k(U-c_0)-iR_0$ and decays. The PDF's real-<frequency> statement is valid for the neutral branch, not for both roots. The Fourier convention $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ makes positive imaginary <frequency> mean growth.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Complete the square in the <dispersion relation>:
$$
\nu=-\frac{iR_0}{2}\pm\sqrt{k^2c_0^2-\frac{R_0^2}{4}+inkR_0U}.
$$
If the imaginary part of the square root has magnitude $b$, then
$$
b^2=\frac12\left[\sqrt{\left(k^2c_0^2-\frac{R_0^2}{4}\right)^2+n^2k^2R_0^2U^2}-\left(k^2c_0^2-\frac{R_0^2}{4}\right)\right].
$$
One root grows exactly when $b>R_0/2$. For $k\ne0$, squaring the equivalent positive inequality gives $n^2U^2>c_0^2$. Thus **positive flow is unstable whenever $\boxed{U>c_0/n}$**. This proves the entire stated range, not just a small neighborhood of threshold. Zero <wavenumber> has only a neutral uniform-area change and a damped <velocity> mode.

Near threshold write $nU=c_0(1+\delta)$ and follow the neutral branch as $\nu=kc_0+\delta\nu_1+O(\delta^2)$. Substitution yields
$$
(2kc_0+iR_0)\nu_1=ikR_0c_0.
$$
Taking its imaginary part gives the growth rate
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}\omega=\frac{\delta R_0k^2c_0^2}{2(k^2c_0^2+R_0^2/4)}+O(\delta^2).}
$$
Area increases reduce friction, causing a <velocity> increase that feeds the area disturbance through <mass conservation>. The <pressure>-driven base flow supplies the energy; the positive friction coefficient does not by itself prevent instability of that maintained flow.

= Solution
{parent=v}

In the travelling coordinate $X=x-ct$, <mass conservation> becomes $[\alpha(u-c)]'=0$. Thus the conserved relative flux is $Q$ and
$$
\boxed{u=c+Q/\alpha.}
$$
For a periodic <peristaltic pumping> solution, the neglected-inertia momentum equation is
$$
\widetilde P'(\alpha)\alpha'+\epsilon kP_e\cos kX=-R(\alpha)(c+Q/\alpha).
$$
Take $\alpha_0$ to be the spatial mean area, so $\langle\alpha_1\rangle=\langle\alpha_2\rangle=0$. This normalization, and zero net <pressure> drop over a period, are implicit in the specified periodic forcing. The constant leading state has no <pressure gradient>, giving $Q_0=-\alpha_0c$. Put $B=\widetilde P'_0$ and $d=R_0c/\alpha_0$. At first order,
$$
B\alpha_1'+d\alpha_1=-kP_e\cos kX-R_0Q_1/\alpha_0.
$$
Period averaging gives $Q_1=0$, and the zero-mean solution is
$$
\alpha_1=-\frac{P_e[(d/k)\cos kX+B\sin kX]}{B^2+(d/k)^2},\qquad
\langle\alpha_1^2\rangle=\frac{P_e^2}{2[B^2+(d/k)^2]}.
$$
For the second-order mean balance, expand the <velocity> and friction:
$$
u_1=c\alpha_1/\alpha_0,\qquad
u_2=c\alpha_2/\alpha_0-c\alpha_1^2/\alpha_0^2+Q_2/\alpha_0,\qquad
R_1=-nR_0\alpha_1/\alpha_0.
$$
Averaging the exact <pressure> derivative gives zero, so $\langle R_0u_2+R_1u_1\rangle=0$. Hence the <weak peristaltic pumping of a collapsible tube> result is
$$
\boxed{Q_0=-\alpha_0c,\qquad Q_1=0,\qquad Q_2=\frac{c(n+1)}{2\alpha_0}\frac{P_e^2}{\widetilde P_0'^2+(R_0c/(k\alpha_0))^2}.}
$$
The mean laboratory <volumetric flow rate> is $c\langle\alpha\rangle+Q=\epsilon^2Q_2+O(\epsilon^3)$, forward with the wave when $c>0$. The negative leading relative flux is therefore consistent with positive mean pumping.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use the <pressure> scale $\rho\widehat U^2$, and denote the scaled transverse <velocity> by $v$. The steady <incompressible flow> equations under the prescribed coordinate scaling are
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x+v_y&=0,\\
uu_x+vu_y&=-p_x+\frac\lambda R\left(u_{yy}+\lambda^{-2}u_{xx}\right),\\
\lambda^{-2}(uv_x+vv_y)&=-p_y+\frac1{\lambda R}\left(v_{yy}+\lambda^{-2}v_{xx}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <no-slip boundary condition> is $u=v=0$ at $y=1$ and at the lower wall $y=\epsilon F(x)$, taking $F=0$ upstream. Far upstream, $u\to U_0=6y(1-y)$, $v\to0$, and $p_x\to-12\lambda/R$. These are the dimensional <Navier-Stokes equations> divided by the streamwise and transverse inertial scales, respectively; the transverse equation's $\lambda^{-2}$ is essential.

A consistent nonlinear wall-layer regime can be chosen as
$$
\boxed{\frac\lambda R=\epsilon^3,\qquad R^{1/7}\ll\lambda\ll R,\qquad\epsilon\ll1.}
$$
Near a wall the oncoming <plane Poiseuille flow> is linear in distance, so a layer of thickness $\epsilon$ has $u=O(\epsilon)$ and $v=O(\epsilon^2)$. Its inertia is $O(\epsilon^2)$; its transverse viscous term is $(\lambda/R)O(\epsilon^{-1})=O(\epsilon^2)$, explaining the distinguished balance. The resulting <pressure> perturbation is $O(\epsilon^2)$. In the core the induced transverse <pressure> variation is $O(\epsilon/\lambda^2)$, negligible compared with that <pressure> perturbation when $\lambda^2\epsilon\gg1$, equivalently $\lambda\gg R^{1/7}$. Also $\lambda\ll R$ makes both the indentation and the <viscous boundary layer> thin. Streamwise viscous derivatives are smaller still. Thus the core perturbation is inviscid at first order and <pressure> is common to the two wall layers at their leading order.

Write $u=U_0+\epsilon u_1+\cdots$ and $v=\epsilon v_1+\cdots$. The core <pressure gradient> only enters at order $\epsilon^2$, so first-order <continuity equation> and streamwise momentum give
$$
u_{1x}+v_{1y}=0,\qquad U_0u_{1x}+U'_0v_1=0.
$$
Eliminating $u_{1x}$ shows $(v_1/U_0)_y=0$. Let that ratio be $-A'(x)$; integration in $x$, with the unperturbed upstream condition, then gives
$$
\boxed{u=U_0(y)+\epsilon A(x)U'_0(y)+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
v=-\epsilon A'(x)U_0(y)+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
This displacement form automatically has zero first-order change in total core flux, because $\int_0^1 U'_0\,dy=0$.

For the lower <boundary layer>, use
$$
y=\epsilon[z+F(x)],\quad u=\epsilon U(x,z),\quad
v=\epsilon^2[V(x,z)+F'(x)U(x,z)],\quad p=p_0(x)+\epsilon^2P(x),
$$
where $p'_0=-12\epsilon^3$ is the smaller upstream <pressure gradient>. The extra term in $v$ removes the <velocity> induced by the moving coordinate surface. The chain rule gives $\partial_x|_y=\partial_x|_z-F'\partial_z$ and $\partial_y=\epsilon^{-1}\partial_z$; the wall-slope terms cancel from both <continuity equation> and convection. At leading order the <Prandtl boundary-layer equation> is
$$
\boxed{U_x+V_z=0,\qquad UU_x+VU_z=-P'(x)+U_{zz},\qquad U=V=0\text{ at }z=0.}
$$
Matching to the core near the lower wall gives $U\sim6[z+F+A]$, so $H_{\rm lower}=F+A$.

For the upper <boundary layer>, set $y=1-\epsilon z$, $u=\epsilon U$, $v=-\epsilon^2V$. The same equations, wall data and common $P(x)$ result, but the core matching gives $U\sim6[z-A]$, so $H_{\rm upper}=-A$. The two <pressure>-driven wall-layer problems have the same upstream shear, wall conditions and <pressure> forcing. Under the question's uniqueness assumption their solutions coincide, and their displacement limits must coincide. Thus the <symmetric viscous interaction in an indented Poiseuille channel> gives
$$
F+A=-A,\qquad \boxed{A=-F/2,\qquad H_{\rm lower}=H_{\rm upper}=F/2.}
$$

For $F=bx^{1/3}$, one has $A=-(b/2)x^{1/3}$ and $H=(b/2)x^{1/3}$. Matching $U\sim6[z+H(x)]$ in a <similarity solution> $U=x^\beta G'(zx^{-\gamma})$ requires $\beta=\gamma=1/3$. Equating the powers in convection $x^{2\beta-1}$, viscosity $x^{\beta-2\gamma}$ and <pressure gradient> $x^{\sigma-1}$ gives $\sigma=2/3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\frac13,\qquad\sigma=\frac23,\qquad\bar a=-b/2.}
$$
Use the <stream function> $\psi=x^{2/3}G(\eta)$, with $\eta=zx^{-1/3}$. Then
$$
U=x^{1/3}G',\qquad V=-x^{-1/3}\left(\frac23G-\frac13\eta G'\right).
$$
Direct differentiation gives $UU_x+VU_z=x^{-1/3}[(G')^2/3-2GG''/3]$, whereas $-P'+U_{zz}=x^{-1/3}[-2\bar p/3+G''']$. The <one-third-power similarity of an indented channel boundary layer> is therefore governed by
$$
\boxed{G'''+\frac23GG''-\frac13(G')^2=\frac23\bar p,\qquad
G(0)=G'(0)=0,\qquad G'(\eta)\sim6\eta+3b.}
$$
The original PDF has $(G')^2$ in this equation; the converted TeX loses the derivative and gives $G^2$. Existence remains conditional on a value of $\bar p$ for which this <boundary value problem> has a solution, as stipulated in the question. The similarity describes the stated thin-indentation regime on $x>0$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Measure $z$ from the rigid tube wall towards the air core, so $0<z<h$. To leading order in $h_0/a$ and small interface slope, the <curvature> entering the <Young–Laplace equation> is
$$
\kappa=\frac1{a-h}+h_{xx}+\cdots,\qquad
p=p_{\rm air}-\sigma_0\kappa.
$$
The air <pressure> is spatially uniform at the order retained. Thus the linear liquid <pressure> perturbation associated with thickness amplitude $H$ is $p_1=-\sigma_0(a^{-2}-k^2)H$. The circumferential <curvature> favors growth at long <wavelength>; the <curvature> opposes it at short <wavelength>.

The <lubrication theory> momentum equation is $\mu u_{zz}=p_x$. With the wall <no-slip boundary condition> $u(0)=0$ and zero surface <shear stress>, $u_z(h)=0$, its solution and flux are
$$
u=\frac{p_x}{2\mu}(z^2-2hz),\qquad
q=\int_0^h u\,dz=-\frac{h^3}{3\mu}p_x.
$$
Leading thin-film <mass conservation> is $h_t+q_x=0$. For a mode $e^{\beta t+ikx}$, this gives the <capillary instability of an annular liquid lining>
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{\sigma_0h_0^3}{3\mu}k^2(a^{-2}-k^2).}
$$
Therefore **the unstable band is $\boxed{0<k^2<a^{-2}}$**. Maximization with respect to $k^2$ gives $k_*^2=1/(2a^2)$ and
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\max}=\frac{\sigma_0h_0^3}{12\mu a^4}.}
$$
These <curvature> coefficients use $a$ instead of $a-h_0$ consistently at leading order in the thin-lining approximation.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let the positive undisturbed <surface tension> be $\bar\sigma=\sigma_0-A\Gamma_0>0$. An <insoluble surfactant> perturbation $g$ produces $\sigma_1=-Ag$. The tangential <Marangoni stress> changes the surface condition to $\mu u_z(h)=\sigma_x$. Solving the <lubrication theory> momentum equation now gives
$$
u=\frac{p_x}{2\mu}(z^2-2hz)+\frac{\sigma_x}{\mu}z,\qquad
q=-\frac{h^3}{3\mu}p_x+\frac{h^2}{2\mu}\sigma_x,\qquad
u_s=-\frac{h^2}{2\mu}p_x+\frac h\mu\sigma_x.
$$
At leading order, <conservation of insoluble surfactant on a moving interface> reduces to $\Gamma_t+(\Gamma u_s)_x=0$. Corrections from the changing circumference are smaller by $h_0/a$. Likewise the normal-<pressure> contribution $-\sigma_1/a$ gives a coupling smaller by $h_0/a$ than the retained tangential <Marangoni stress>, so it is omitted at the same order.

For thickness and concentration amplitudes $H,g$ proportional to $e^{\beta t+ikx}$, put $D=a^{-2}-k^2$ and use $p_1=-\bar\sigma DH$. The two conservation equations become
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\beta-\frac{h_0^3\bar\sigma}{3\mu}k^2D\right)H+\frac{h_0^2A}{2\mu}k^2g&=0,\\
-\frac{\Gamma_0h_0^2\bar\sigma}{2\mu}k^2D\,H+\left(\beta+\frac{h_0A\Gamma_0}{\mu}k^2\right)g&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Set $s=k^2a^2$, $\lambda=h_0^3\bar\sigma/(3\mu a^4)$ and $\alpha=h_0A\Gamma_0/(\mu a^2)$. The uncoupled capillary growth and elastic relaxation rates are $L=\lambda s(1-s)$ and $S=\alpha s$. The off-diagonal product is $3LS/4$, so the <determinant> is $(\beta-L)(\beta+S)+3LS/4$. Thus the <surfactant stabilization of an annular liquid lining> relation is
$$
\boxed{\beta^2+\beta s[\lambda(s-1)+\alpha]+\frac{\alpha\lambda}{4}s^2(s-1)=0.}
$$
This proves the <dispersion relation> before its lettered deductions. The physical assumption $\bar\sigma>0$ is needed for the claimed capillary instability band.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For positive $\lambda,\alpha$ and $0<s<1$, the constant term of the <quadratic equation> is negative. Its roots are therefore real with opposite signs, so one has positive growth. At $s>1$, both the coefficient of $\beta$ and the constant term are positive. If the roots are real they are both negative; if complex they have negative real parts. Thus **the growing band remains $\boxed{0<k^2a^2<1}$**. At $s=0$ or $1$ there is a neutral root. If $\alpha=0$, the result reduces continuously to the clean-film capillary branch with a passive zero root.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $\alpha/\lambda\ll1$, follow the unstable root near the clean-film value $L=\lambda s(1-s)$. Write $\beta=L+\Delta$ in $(\beta-L)(\beta+S)+3LS/4=0$. At first order in $S$, $L\Delta+3LS/4=0$, giving
$$
\beta=\lambda s(1-s)-\frac34\alpha s+O(\alpha^2/\lambda).
$$
This expansion is uniform near the maximizing <wavenumber>. Differentiation gives $s_*=1/2-3\alpha/(8\lambda)+O((\alpha/\lambda)^2)$. Evaluating the growth there yields
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\max}=\frac\lambda4-\frac{3\alpha}{8}+O(\alpha^2/\lambda).}
$$
The change in maximizing <wavenumber> only affects the maximum at second order. The first-order decrease therefore has exactly the requested coefficient, relative to the clean-film rate with its <surface tension> replaced by $\bar\sigma$.

{parent=ii}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The quarter-rate limit is the strong-elasticity limit $\lambda/\alpha\ll1$, not the weak-elasticity limit. The original PDF repeats $\alpha/\lambda\ll1$ here; that is inconsistent with the preceding small-$\alpha$ result. In fact at $\alpha=0$ the maximum is $\lambda/4$, directly contradicting a quarter of that value in the same limit. The converted TeX repairs this particular ratio.

For $\alpha\gg\lambda$ and <wavenumbers> near the maximum, $S=\alpha s\gg L$. In the <quadratic equation>, the growing root is $O(\lambda)$, so its square and the $-L\beta$ term are small compared with $S\beta$. The leading balance is $S\beta-LS/4=0$. Consequently
$$
\beta\sim\frac\lambda4s(1-s),\qquad
\boxed{s_*\to\frac12,\qquad\beta_{\max}\sim\frac\lambda{16}.}
$$
The second root is a rapidly damped <surfactant>-relaxation mode. Thus the stated quarter-rate result is correct after reversing the printed small-parameter ratio.

{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Without <surfactant>, <capillary pressure> drives a surface flow towards a growing bulge. <Advection> compresses the <insoluble surfactant> in converging parts of that motion, increasing concentration and lowering <surface tension>. The resulting gradient supplies a tangential <Marangoni stress> that opposes the surface motion. This additional resistance slows capillary-driven redistribution of liquid even after the base-tension decrease has already been accounted for.

In the strong-elasticity limit, concentration gradients suppress tangential interface motion. The surface behaves as effectively immobile at leading order. Setting $u_s\approx0$ in the <velocity> formula gives $\sigma_x\approx hp_x/2$, hence
$$
q\approx-\frac{h^3}{12\mu}p_x,
$$
one-quarter of the clean shear-free mobility $h^3/(3\mu)$. **This mobility reduction explains the quarter-rate limit; it slows the unstable capillary mode without removing the long-wave unstable band.**
