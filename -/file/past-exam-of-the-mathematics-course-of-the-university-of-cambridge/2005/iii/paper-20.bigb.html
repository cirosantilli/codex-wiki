= Solution
{parent=1}

The central problem of <spectral geometry> is how much of a <Riemannian metric> is determined by the <eigenvalues>, with <multiplicities>, of its <Laplace-Beltrami operator>. For a membrane with fixed <boundary>, the squared vibration frequencies are proportional to the <Dirichlet eigenvalues>. Kac's \a[https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~saito/courses/ACHA.READ.F03/kac-drum.pdf][1966 paper] brought this inverse problem into focus: the full sequence of frequencies might encode a domain's shape, but recovering a few geometric quantities is much weaker than recovering an <isometry> class.

The first major line of development extracts geometric information from spectral sums. On a smooth <closed> $d$-dimensional <manifold>, the <heat trace> has the short-time expansion
$$
Z(t)=\sum_j e^{-t\lambda_j}\sim(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\left(\operatorname{Vol}(M)+\frac t6\int_M R\,dV+\cdots\right),
$$
where $\lambda_j$ are the nonnegative <eigenvalues> of $P=-\Delta$ and $R$ is <scalar curvature>. The coefficients are <heat invariants>, formed by integrating local curvature expressions. They recover dimension, <Riemannian volume> and total <scalar curvature>. For smooth planar domains with the <Dirichlet boundary condition>,
$$
Z(t)=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)}{4\pi t}-\frac{\operatorname{Length}(\partial\Omega)}{8\sqrt{\pi t}}+O(1).
$$
Thus <area> and <boundary> length are audible. The constant term also contains topological information through <Gauss-Bonnet theorem>, with corner corrections when the <boundary> is polygonal. The <Weyl law> is the corresponding leading eigenvalue-counting result. Neither the leading term nor the whole list of local heat coefficients automatically reconstructs global geometry.

In the 1970s, the <wave trace> introduced a complementary link with global <closed geodesics>. The <distribution>
$$
W(t)=\sum_j e^{it\sqrt{\lambda_j}}
$$
is spectral, while its singularities are governed by periodic <geodesic> motion. The \a[https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01405172][1975 Duistermaat-Guillemin work] made this relationship precise. Its <trace> formula places singularities at <geodesic> lengths and computes their coefficients under suitable clean or nondegenerate hypotheses. When a coefficient is nonzero, the corresponding length is audible. Possible cancellation must be addressed before identifying the entire <length spectrum> with the singular support. This approach led to <spectral rigidity> questions, rather than just the recovery of integral curvature quantities.

The second main line constructs counterexamples to metric determination. A precedent was the \a[https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.51.4.542][1964 flat-torus example]: different sixteen-dimensional <Euclidean lattices> could give the same <Laplacian eigenvalues>. Subsequent lattice constructions lowered the dimension; \a[https://academic.oup.com/imrn/article-abstract/1992/4/93/660616][Conway and Sloane's 1992 work] exhibited four-dimensional <Euclidean lattices> with matching <lattice theta series>. For a <flat torus> $\mathbb R^d/\Lambda$, the spectrum is the multiset $4\pi^2|\xi|^2$ for $\xi\in\Lambda^*$, so equality of <lattice theta series> of the <dual lattices> gives <isospectrality> even when the <Euclidean lattices> are not related by an <orthogonal transformation>.

A systematic geometric construction arrived with the \a[https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1985/121-1/p04][Sunada theorem in 1985]. For a common Riemannian cover with finite <isometry> <group> $T$, <almost conjugate subgroups> have the same number of invariant vectors in each <Laplacian> <eigenspace>. Their freely acting quotients are therefore <isospectral manifolds>. This reduces an analytic equality to a finite-group representation identity. It produces many examples on surfaces and in higher dimensions, but nonconjugacy of the <subgroups> must be supplemented by an argument excluding extra quotient <isometries>. <Transplantation theorem> provides an explicit version: piecewise <eigenfunctions> are recombined by a fixed invertible linear transformation that respects the gluing and <boundary conditions>.

The planar problem itself was answered negatively by \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9207215][Gordon, Webb and Wolpert in 1992]: noncongruent simply connected polygonal plane domains have equal Dirichlet spectra. This is stronger than counterexamples among abstract <manifolds> or <flat tori>, since these are genuine two-dimensional planar membranes. It does not settle every restricted version, such as asking for two domains within a specified smooth or convex class.

By the 1990s, <isospectrality> was also known to coexist with continuous metric variation and changes in local geometry. For example, \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/dg-ga/9710004][a 1997 construction] gives continuous isospectral families on products of spheres and <tori> whose members need not be locally <isometric>; in some examples the maximum <scalar curvature> changes. Conversely, rigidity and <compactness> results show that an isospectral class is not unrestricted. The \a[https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1236(88)90071-7][1988 surface compactness theorem] makes fixed-spectrum sets of <closed> orientable surface metrics <compact> modulo <diffeomorphisms>. <Compactness> does not mean there is only one metric, or even finitely many metrics. By 2005, the subject therefore combined local <heat invariants>, global <geodesic> information, group-theoretic constructions, explicit <transplantation> and geometric rigidity. **The spectrum determines substantial geometry, while generally failing to determine the full metric.**

For a <smooth function> $f$ and the <Levi-Civita connection> $\nabla$, the <Riemannian Hessian> is the covariant two-tensor
$$
\operatorname{Hess}_g f(X,Y)=(\nabla_Xdf)(Y)=X(Yf)-df(\nabla_XY).
$$
The connection rules show that this is linear over <smooth functions> in both vector-field arguments, so it is a <tensor>. Torsion-freeness gives its symmetry:
$$
\operatorname{Hess}_g f(X,Y)-\operatorname{Hess}_g f(Y,X)
=[X,Y]f-df(\nabla_XY-\nabla_YX)=0.
$$
Its <metric trace> defines the paper's <Laplace-Beltrami operator>, $\Delta=\operatorname{div}_g\operatorname{grad}_g$; its <eigenvalues> on a <closed manifold> are nonpositive. We use $P=-\Delta$ when listing nonnegative <eigenvalues> or writing $e^{-tP}$.

At $p$, choose an <orthonormal basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_d$ of the <tangent space> and unit-speed <geodesics> $\gamma_i(t)=\exp_p(te_i)$. Differentiating twice along a curve gives
$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}f(\gamma_i(t))
=\operatorname{Hess}_g f(\dot\gamma_i,\dot\gamma_i)+df(\nabla_{\dot\gamma_i}\dot\gamma_i).
$$
The covariant acceleration vanishes on a <geodesic>. Taking the <trace> at $t=0$ proves the <geodesic trace formula for the Laplace-Beltrami operator>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta f(p)=\sum_{i=1}^d(f\circ\gamma_i)''(0)
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^d[f(\exp_p(he_i))+f(\exp_p(-he_i))-2f(p)]}{h^2}.}
$$
The last equality is Taylor's formula along the <geodesics>, and expresses the <Laplacian> entirely through nearby function values.

For stabilization, let $X_1,X_2$ be the assumed isospectral nonisometric four-dimensional <tori>, and let $k=n-4>0$. Take the same small <flat torus> $F_\varepsilon=\mathbb R^k/\varepsilon\mathbb Z^k$ as an extra factor. On a <Riemannian product>,
$$
P_{X_i\times F_\varepsilon}=P_{X_i}\otimes I+I\otimes P_{F_\varepsilon}.
$$
If $\phi_j$ and $\psi_\ell$ are <eigenfunctions> with <eigenvalues> $\lambda_j$ and $\mu_\ell$, then $\phi_j\psi_\ell$ has <eigenvalue> $\lambda_j+\mu_\ell$. Products of <orthonormal eigenbases> are complete in the product $L^2$ space, so this lists all <eigenvalues> with <multiplicities>, including coincidences between different sums. Consequently the two products are isospectral.

One must also prove that multiplying by a common factor has not accidentally made the two <manifolds> <isometric>. Choose
$$
0<\varepsilon<\delta<\min\{\operatorname{inj}(X_1),\operatorname{inj}(X_2)\}.
$$
The injectivity radii are positive because the factors are <compact>. A <closed> product <geodesic> of length less than $\delta$ projects to a <closed geodesic> of length less than $\delta$ in $X_i$. Such a projected <geodesic> must be constant: otherwise its initial vector over one period would be a nonzero vector of norm below the <injectivity radius> mapping back to its starting point under the <exponential map>. Thus all sufficiently short <closed geodesics> lie in the $F_\varepsilon$ directions. Conversely the $k$ coordinate circles, of length $\varepsilon$, pass through every point and span those directions.

It follows that the <smooth distribution> tangent to the small flat-torus factor is characterized intrinsically by the span of tangent vectors to <closed geodesics> of length below $\delta$. A hypothetical product <isometry> would preserve this <smooth distribution> and its <orthogonal complement>. The latter has leaves $X_i\times\{q\}$; the <isometry> and its inverse take whole leaves to whole leaves, thereby giving an <isometry> $X_1\cong X_2$, a contradiction. This proves <isospectral stabilization by a small flat torus>:
$$
\boxed{X_1\times F_\varepsilon\text{ and }X_2\times F_\varepsilon\text{ are isospectral nonisometric }n\text{-tori for every }n>4.}
$$
This argument works even without assuming that the original four-dimensional <torus> metrics are flat.

Work on a <closed> <compact> smooth <Riemannian manifold> $M$ without <boundary>, as in the usual discrete spectral setting here. Use the paper's $\Delta=\operatorname{tr}_g\operatorname{Hess}_g$, and put $P=-\Delta$. The <Riemannian heat kernel> $H(t,x,y)$ is the smooth kernel, for $t>0$, representing the solution of
$$
\partial_tu=\Delta u,\qquad u(0,\cdot)=f,\qquad
u(t,x)=\int_M H(t,x,y)f(y)\,dV(y).
$$
Thus $(\partial_t-\Delta_x)H=0$ and its distributional initial value in $x$ is the <Dirac delta distribution> at $y$. The <heat trace> is
$$
Z_M(t)=\int_M H(t,x,x)\,dV(x)=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-tP}).
$$
The <heat invariants> are the coefficients $a_r(M)$ in the short-time <heat trace> expansion specified below.

To derive its spectral form, take an <orthonormal eigenbasis> $P\phi_j=\lambda_j\phi_j$, with $0=\lambda_0\le\lambda_1\le\cdots$, repeating <eigenvalues> according to <multiplicity>. For the initial datum $\phi_j$, the function $e^{-t\lambda_j}\phi_j$ solves the <heat equation>. Uniqueness identifies it with the heat-kernel solution. Expanding arbitrary initial data in this basis therefore gives the <spectral expansion of the Riemannian heat kernel>:
$$
H(t,x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty e^{-t\lambda_j}\phi_j(x)\overline{\phi_j(y)}.
$$
Standard elliptic estimates and <eigenvalue> growth imply smooth convergence for $t$ bounded away from zero: polynomial bounds on derivatives of <eigenfunctions> are dominated by the exponential factors. On the diagonal all summands are nonnegative, so <monotone convergence> also justifies integration without needing an interchange of conditionally convergent terms. Since each <eigenfunction> has unit $L^2$ norm,
$$
\boxed{Z_M(t)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty e^{-t\lambda_j}.}
$$
Equivalently, if the paper's <Laplacian eigenvalues> are written $\nu_j=-\lambda_j$, the formula is $\sum_j e^{t\nu_j}$; a growing exponential is not the <heat semigroup>.

Here are the needed <heat kernel expansion> facts, stated without proof. On a <closed> $d$-dimensional smooth <Riemannian manifold>,
$$
H(t,x,x)\sim(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\sum_{r=0}^\infty u_r(x)t^r,\qquad u_0(x)=1,
$$
with the diagonal remainder uniform in $x$ after any finite truncation. The coefficients are smooth local geometric quantities; $u_1=R/6$. <Compactness> therefore allows integration term by term:
$$
Z_M(t)\sim(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\sum_{r=0}^\infty a_r(M)t^r,
\qquad a_r(M)=\int_Mu_r(x)\,dV(x),\qquad a_0(M)=\operatorname{Vol}(M)>0.
$$
These integrated coefficients are the <heat invariants>. The uniform leading remainder in particular gives $Z_M(t)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}(\operatorname{Vol}(M)+O(t))$.

<Isospectral manifolds> have identical <heat traces> by the derived spectral formula. If their dimensions were $d<e$, multiplying the common <trace> by $t^{d/2}$ would give a finite positive limit on the $d$-dimensional <manifold> but divergence on the $e$-dimensional one. Thus the dimensions are equal, and equality of the leading coefficient gives equal <Riemannian volume>. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{d=2\lim_{t\downarrow0}\frac{\log Z_M(t)}{\log(1/t)},\qquad
\operatorname{Vol}(M)=\lim_{t\downarrow0}(4\pi t)^{d/2}Z_M(t).}
$$
The absence of a <boundary> fixes this particular integer-power expansion. With a <boundary> one must first specify <boundary conditions> and include the appropriate half-integer <boundary> terms; the leading dimension-and-volume conclusion remains valid in the usual <compact> smooth setting.

Let $p:N\to M=U\backslash N$ be a finite normal Riemannian covering, so $U$ acts freely by deck <isometries>. With $x,y$ any lifts of $\bar x,\bar y$, the <heat kernel on a finite isometric quotient> satisfies
$$
\boxed{H_M(t,\bar x,\bar y)=\sum_{u\in U}H_N(t,x,uy).}
$$
There is no factor $1/|U|$ in this kernel formula. Simultaneous invariance of $H_N$ under <isometries> proves independence of the lifts, by reindexing $U$. Each term solves the lifted <heat equation>. If $F_U$ is a <fundamental domain> and $f$ a function on $M$, the integral of the right side against $f(\bar y)$ over $F_U$ equals
$$
\int_NH_N(t,x,y)f(p(y))\,dV(y),
$$
because the translates $uF_U$ tile $N$ up to sets of measure zero. This converges to $f(p(x))$ as $t\downarrow0$, proving the initial condition. Uniqueness of the <heat equation> solution on the <closed manifold> gives the formula. For a noncompact finite cover, use the minimal <heat kernels>, or compatible Friedrichs heat realizations. The normalized pullback $f\mapsto |U|^{-1/2}f\circ p$ is unitary onto the deck-invariant $L^2$ subspace and preserves the <Dirichlet energy>, so it intertwines the <heat semigroups> and yields the same kernel formula. No uniqueness of unrestricted noncompact heat-equation solutions is needed.

Set $I_t(g)=\int_N H_N(t,x,gx)\,dV(x)$ for an <isometry> $g$. Diagonal integration and the covering degree give
$$
\boxed{Z_{U\backslash N}(t)=\frac1{|U|}\sum_{u\in U}I_t(u).}
$$
Unlike the kernel formula, the <trace> formula has an averaging factor: integration of a quotient function over $N$ is $|U|$ times integration over $U\backslash N$.

Suppose now $U\le T$, with $T$ any larger <group> of <isometries> of the <closed manifold> $N$. Change variables $x=hy$ and use simultaneous heat-kernel invariance to obtain
$$
I_t(hgh^{-1})=\int_NH_N(t,hy,hgy)\,dV(y)=I_t(g).
$$
Thus $I_t$ is a <class function>, and the <Sunada orbital heat-trace formula> can be grouped by <conjugacy classes> $C$ of $T$:
$$
\boxed{Z_{U\backslash N}(t)=\sum_{C\subseteq T}\frac{|U\cap C|}{|U|}I_t(c_C),\qquad c_C\in C.}
$$
Only classes meeting the finite <subgroup> $U$ contribute, so this class sum is finite even if the larger <isometry> <group> $T$ is infinite.

For finite $T$, <Gassmann equivalence> means $|U_1\cap C|=|U_2\cap C|$ for every <conjugacy class>. Summing these counts gives $|U_1|=|U_2|$, so the displayed formula gives identical <heat traces>. Their spectra, with <multiplicities>, agree: as $t\to\infty$, the constant limit identifies the <multiplicity> of zero; after subtracting it, the slowest-decaying exponential identifies the least positive <eigenvalue> and its <multiplicity>. Repeating determines every subsequent <eigenvalue>. This proves the <Sunada theorem> in the stated covering situation.

There is an important convention when the <universal cover> $N$ has an infinite deck <group> $T$. For <compact> quotient <manifolds>, the <subgroups> must have finite index; the appropriate generalized Gassmann condition is equality of the characters of the finite coset <permutation representations>, not equality of possibly infinite cardinalities of conjugacy-class intersections. Let $K$ be the intersection of the two <subgroup> cores. Each core is the kernel of the corresponding finite coset action, so $K$ is normal and of finite index in $T$, and $K\subseteq U_1\cap U_2$. The <manifold> $X=K\backslash N$ is then a <compact> finite normal cover of $M_0$. The <finite group> $G=T/K$ acts on $X$, and $H_i=U_i/K$ have equal coset <permutation characters> in $G$. For a <finite group>, the fixed-coset count is
$$
\chi_{G/H_i}(g)=\frac{|C_G(g)|}{|H_i|}\,|H_i\cap[g]|.
$$
Equality at the identity gives equal <subgroup> orders, and then equality for all $g$ gives almost-conjugacy. Applying the proved finite theorem to $H_i\backslash X=U_i\backslash N$ establishes <isospectrality> also in this finite-index interpretation. It avoids taking the divergent <heat trace> of a noncompact <universal cover>.

To realize a <finitely presented group> $T=\langle a_1,\ldots,a_r\mid R_1,\ldots,R_s\rangle$, begin with the <closed> smooth four-manifold $B=\#_{j=1}^r(S^1\times S^3)$, whose <fundamental group> is free on the $a_j$. Represent the relator words by disjoint smoothly embedded circles; general position permits this in dimension four, including separating self-intersections of representatives. Their oriented normal rank-three bundles are trivial, so perform framed surgeries replacing $S^1\times D^3$ by $D^2\times S^2$. Removing the circles' tubular neighborhoods leaves the <fundamental group> unchanged, by codimension-three general position. The <Seifert-van Kampen theorem> shows that each replacement kills exactly the normal closure of its relator. The resulting <closed> smooth $M_0$ has $\pi_1(M_0)\cong T$. This gives a concrete <closed-manifold realization of a finitely presented fundamental group>. Equip it with any smooth <Riemannian metric> and lift that metric to the universal and intermediate covers; all <covering maps> become <local isometries>.

To ensure nonisometry, one must choose nonconjugate <subgroups>. If $U_1=U_2$, or they are conjugate, the quotients are <isometric> for every lifted metric, so <Gassmann equivalence> alone cannot imply nonisometry. For nonconjugate <subgroups>, choose the base metric to have no nonidentity <local isometry> between open sets, using the <Sunada local isometry lemma> in dimension at least two. If $F:M_1\to M_2$ were an <isometry>, a local inverse of the first covering followed by $F$ and the second covering would be a <local isometry> of $M_0$, hence the identity. Thus the <covering maps> satisfy $p_2F=p_1$ everywhere. Classification of connected covers then forces $U_1,U_2$ to be conjugate in $\pi_1(M_0)=T$, a contradiction. Therefore **nonconjugate almost-conjugate <subgroups> and a locally rigid base metric give isospectral nonisometric quotients**.

Use an oriented <geodesic> hyperbolic $2s$-gon $D$ with paired edges of equal length. Identify each pair by a length-preserving map reversing the <boundary> directions, to make an oriented base surface $M_0$ with possibly singular vertices. Assign to the $s$ edge pairs generators $A_1,\ldots,A_s$ of $T$, with inverse labels for reversed crossings. For each $g\in T$, take a copy $D_g$ and glue its positively labelled edge of type $k$ to the corresponding mate in $D_{gA_k}$. The generators make the glued surface $M$ connected. Left multiplication $D_g\mapsto D_{hg}$ commutes with these right-labelled gluings and gives the $T$ action with quotient $M_0$.

Existence for an arbitrary finite <group> can even be arranged with no cone singularities. If $T$ has $d$ generators, take $h\ge\max\{2,d\}$ and a regular <hyperbolic polygon> with $4h$ edges, all angles $\pi/(2h)$, paired with side word $\prod_{i=1}^h a_ib_ia_i^{-1}b_i^{-1}$. Its vertices form one cycle of angle $2\pi$, giving a smooth base of <genus> $h$. Label the $a_i$ by the generators of $T$, padded by identities, and every $b_i$ by the identity. The single vertex word is $\prod_i[A_i,1]=1$, so every lifted vertex also has angle $2\pi$. The resulting connected ordinary <normal covering map> has degree $|T|$ and deck <group> $T$.

This is the <paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers>. Interior and open-edge points are regular hyperbolic points; possible singularities occur only at vertices. When the base has cone points, the map is an <orbifold> covering and may be branched as a map between the underlying topological surfaces. It should not be called an unbranched <manifold> covering at those branch points.

A <vertex cycle of a paired polygon> is the collection of vertices identified to one point on $M_0$. Let $r$ be the number of cycles, $\theta_j$ the sum of the polygon angles in cycle $j$, and $C_j\in T$ the gluing word recorded on going around it. If $q_j=|C_j|$ is the order of that element, a lift closes after $q_j$ turns. It has angle $q_j\theta_j$, and there are $|T|/q_j$ such lifted vertices. The base and lifted cone orders, when they are integer <orbifold> orders, are
$$
\boxed{m_j=\frac{2\pi}{\theta_j},\qquad \widetilde m_j=\frac{2\pi}{q_j\theta_j}=\frac{m_j}{q_j}.}
$$
An <orbifold> homomorphism requires $q_j\mid m_j$. The lifted point is smooth exactly when $q_j\theta_j=2\pi$, equivalently $q_j=m_j$ in that case. For arbitrary cone metrics the angle formulas remain valid even when an angle does not correspond to an integer <orbifold> order.

Counting faces, edges and vertices gives the <Euler characteristic of a paired-polygon surface cover>:
$$
\boxed{\chi(M_0)=1-s+r,\qquad \chi(M)=|T|\left(1-s+\sum_{j=1}^r\frac1{q_j}\right).}
$$
These are ordinary topological Euler characteristics, rather than <orbifold> Euler characteristics. If the lifted surface is smooth, the equivalent <orbifold> formula is
$$
\chi(M)=|T|\chi_{\rm orb}(M_0),\qquad
\chi_{\rm orb}(M_0)=\chi(M_0)-\sum_j\left(1-\frac1{m_j}\right).
$$
For a <closed> oriented smooth surface, $\chi=2-2g$ defines its <genus>. These formulas also follow from the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> by subtracting the ramification deficits $|T|-|T|/q_j$ over each base vertex.

For $U\le T$, the quotient map $M\to M_1=U\backslash M$ is an ordinary normal covering if and only if $U$ acts freely. All possible point stabilizers in this construction are conjugates of $\langle C_j\rangle$, so the concrete necessary and sufficient condition is
$$
\boxed{U\cap g\langle C_j\rangle g^{-1}=\{1\}\quad\text{for every }j\text{ and every }g\in T.}
$$
This is the <freeness criterion for a subgroup of a polygon-cover deck group>. Under it the deck <group> is $U$, the degree is $|U|$, and
$$
\boxed{\chi(M_1)=\frac{\chi(M)}{|U|}.}
$$
There is no requirement $U\triangleleft T$ for this map. That different condition characterizes normality of the intermediate cover $M_1\to M_0$. If fixed points are retained and one instead speaks of an <orbifold> covering, the quotient is regular as an <orbifold> cover, but ordinary <Euler characteristic> need not divide by $|U|$; <orbifold Euler characteristic> does.

For the low-genus examples take
$$
T=\operatorname{GL}(3,\mathbb F_2)=\operatorname{PSL}(3,2),\qquad |T|=(8-1)(8-2)(8-4)=168.
$$
Let $U_1$ be a nonzero-vector stabilizer and $U_2$ a plane stabilizer. Their actions have seven points, so $|U_1|=|U_2|=24$ and $[T:U_i]=7$. They are <almost conjugate subgroups>: the numbers of fixed nonzero vectors for a matrix $A$ and its dual action $A^{-T}$ are both $2^{\dim\ker(A-I)}-1$. Thus the two coset <permutation characters> agree, which is equivalent to <Gassmann equivalence>. They are not conjugate: a point stabilizer has a common fixed nonzero vector, whereas a plane stabilizer fixes no nonzero vector globally. In particular, since $7\nmid24$, neither <subgroup> contains any nonidentity element of a cyclic <subgroup> of order seven. All cone monodromies of exact order seven therefore act freely on the intermediate covers.

For <genus> three, start with a <hyperbolic triangle> whose three angles are $\pi/7$ and double it across one edge. The resulting quadrilateral $D$ has angles
$$
\boxed{\pi/7,\ 2\pi/7,\ \pi/7,\ 2\pi/7.}
$$
The side pairings produce three base vertex cycles, each of total angle $2\pi/7$, and an underlying sphere. Choose generators $A,B$ with $|A|=|B|=|AB|=7$; the three vertex words are $A,B,(AB)^{-1}$. Hence $s=2$, $r=3$ and all $q_j=7$. The full cover is smooth, and
$$
\chi(M)=168\left(-1+\frac37\right)=-96,\qquad
\chi(U_i\backslash M)=-96/24=-4,\qquad\boxed{g_i=3.}
$$
The <hyperbolic polygon area> of $D$ is $8\pi/7$, so the seven-sheeted surfaces have <area> $8\pi$, agreeing with Gauss-Bonnet. Their equal spectra follow from the Sunada theorem. For complete numerical generator data, one possible pair is
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2.
$$
These generate all of $T$ and have the required orders. This construction establishes the requested <isospectrality>; it does not establish nonisometry. In the constant-curvature triangle example a lifted reflection can identify the point and plane covers, as in <reflection intertwining of triangle-cover coset actions>. Breaking that symmetry with a generic metric gives nonisometric genus-three surfaces, but then the metric need not have constant curvature. Those are distinct assertions.

For <genus> four, use the <cone-torus construction of genus-four Sunada surfaces>. Take a regular hyperbolic quadrilateral with four angles
$$
\boxed{\pi/14,\ \pi/14,\ \pi/14,\ \pi/14}
$$
and pair opposite sides in the usual <torus> pattern $a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}$. All four vertices belong to one cycle, whose total angle is $2\pi/7$; the underlying base is a <torus> with one <cone point>. Choose $A,B$ of order four generating $T$, with commutator $[A,B]$ of order seven. For example,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
|A|=|B|=4,\quad |[A,B]|=7.
$$
Only the commutator is a cone monodromy, so the order-four side labels do not create cone points of order four. Here $s=2$, $r=1$, $q_1=7$, giving
$$
\chi(M)=168\left(-1+\frac17\right)=-144,\qquad
\chi(U_i\backslash M)=-144/24=-6,\qquad\boxed{g_i=4.}
$$
The quadrilateral has <area> $12\pi/7$, and each seven-sheeted smooth quotient has <area> $12\pi$. Again the order-seven cone stabilizer meets neither $U_i$ nontrivially, so the metrics are smooth and the Sunada theorem gives identical spectra. The required <group> and <subgroup> orders are therefore **168 and 24 in both constructions**; the genus-three side-generator orders are **7 and 7**, and the displayed genus-four side-generator orders are **4 and 4**, with cone-word order **7** in both cases.
