= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the <resistive induction equation> with constant <magnetic diffusivity>,
$$
\partial_t\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}}=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}})+\eta\nabla^2\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}},\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}}=0.
$$
Seek $\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}}=B_0\mathbf e_z+B(s,z,t)\mathbf e_\phi$. The axisymmetric velocity $\mathbf u=s\omega(s,z)\mathbf e_\phi$ is incompressible, and the proposed field is <solenoidal>. The <toroidal magnetic field> is parallel to the velocity, so it does not contribute to their <cross product>. The remaining term is
$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}}=s\omega B_0\mathbf e_s,\qquad\nabla\times(s\omega B_0\mathbf e_s)=sB_0\partial_z\omega\,\mathbf e_\phi.
$$
The uniform axial field has zero vector <Laplacian>. For the azimuthal part, differentiation of the cylindrical basis gives $\partial_\phi^2\mathbf e_\phi=-\mathbf e_\phi$. Consequently
$$
\nabla^2(B\mathbf e_\phi)=\left(\partial_s^2B+\frac1s\partial_sB+\partial_z^2B-\frac B{s^2}\right)\mathbf e_\phi.
$$
There is no source for a radial or axial perturbation, so zero initial values of those components remain zero. Equating the toroidal components proves
$$
\boxed{\partial_tB=s(\mathbf B_0\cdot\nabla)\omega+\eta\left(\nabla^2-\frac1{s^2}\right)B.}
$$
The last Laplacian is the scalar axisymmetric <Laplacian>. The additional $-B/s^2$ term is essential for a vector component in a rotating basis. The source is the <Omega effect>: rotation that varies along an axial field line winds it into a <toroidal magnetic field>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

With $s=r\sin\theta$ and $z=r\cos\theta$, radial <differential rotation> gives $\partial_z\omega=\cos\theta\,\omega'(r)$. Thus the source is $B_0r\omega'(r)\sin\theta\cos\theta$. Put $B=f(r)g(\theta)$ with $g=\sin\theta\cos\theta$. The toroidal diffusion operator is
$$
\left(\nabla^2-\frac1{s^2}\right)(fg)=\left(f''+\frac2rf'\right)g+\frac f{r^2}\left[\frac1{\sin\theta}\frac d{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{dg}{d\theta}\right)-\frac g{\sin^2\theta}\right].
$$
Direct differentiation of $g$ makes the bracket $-6g$. Therefore the steady radial equation is
$$
\eta\left(f''+\frac2rf'-\frac6{r^2}f\right)=-B_0r\omega'(r).
$$
Define $I(r)=\int_0^r x^4\omega(x)\,dx$ and $h=I/r^3$. Since $I'=r^4\omega$,
$$
h'=r\omega-\frac{3I}{r^4},\qquad h''=r\omega'-2\omega+\frac{12I}{r^5},
$$
so $h''+2h'/r-6h/r^2=r\omega'$. Taking $f=-B_0h/\eta$ proves the <steady toroidal induction by spherical differential rotation>:
$$
\boxed{B(r,\theta)=-\frac{B_0}{\eta}\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{r^3}\int_0^rx^4\omega(x)\,dx.}
$$
Near zero, $I(r)=\omega(0)r^5/5+O(r^6)$, hence $B=O(r^2)\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and is regular. Its leading <vector field> is proportional to $z(-y,x,0)$, so the apparent azimuthal-basis singularity on the axis is harmless. The rapid decay of $\omega$ makes $I_\infty=\int_0^\infty x^4\omega(x)\,dx$ finite, giving $B\sim-(B_0I_\infty/\eta)r^{-3}\sin\theta\cos\theta$ at large radius. The homogeneous radial solutions are $r^2$ and $r^{-3}$; regularity at zero excludes the latter, and decay at infinity excludes the former. Thus these boundary requirements also single out the displayed solution in this angular sector.

Let $\ell$ be the characteristic radius over which the <differential rotation> changes, with <angular velocity> of order $\Omega$. The winding source has magnitude $F\sim B_0\Omega$. For times too short for appreciable <magnetic diffusion>, the zero initial <toroidal magnetic field> therefore grows as $B\sim B_0\Omega t$. In the steady balance, $F$ is opposed by $\eta B/\ell^2$, giving $B_{\mathrm{steady}}\sim B_0\Omega\ell^2/\eta$. Comparing these amplitudes gives the <diffusive establishment time of a wound toroidal field>:
$$
\boxed{t_{\mathrm{est}}\sim t_\eta=\frac{\ell^2}{\eta}=\mu_0\sigma\ell^2.}
$$
With $R_m=\Omega\ell^2/\eta$, this is $R_m$ rotation times and the steady toroidal amplitude is of order $R_mB_0$. Thus high <electrical conductivity> makes establishment very slow even though winding begins immediately.

More precisely, if $\mathcal L=\nabla^2-s^{-2}$ on regular <toroidal magnetic fields>, the solution with zero initial value satisfies $B(t)=B_{\mathrm{steady}}-e^{\eta t\mathcal L}B_{\mathrm{steady}}$. The residual relaxes diffusively. In the unbounded domain, $\ell^2/\eta$ estimates equilibration of the rotating region; exact establishment at every radius is only an asymptotic statement, with diffusion on scale $r$ taking order $r^2/\eta$. As $\eta\to0$, the time diverges and the prescribed-flow ideal solution continues its secular winding. In a real fluid, <magnetic tension> and <Lorentz force> feedback can modify the <differential rotation> before the very large kinematic steady amplitude is attained.

= Solution

A field in a circulation plane has components crossing circular streamlines. <Magnetic flux freezing> carries those lines with <fluid elements> at different <angular speeds>: the lines become spirals, their azimuthal components are stretched, and neighboring turns acquire increasingly small separations. This is a nonaxisymmetric winding and phase-mixing problem.

For illustration, in the ideal limit take the initial horizontal field along $x$ and fix a horizontal plane. With $\chi=\phi-\omega(s,z)t$, the induction equations give
$$
B_s=B_0\cos\chi,\qquad B_\phi=-B_0\sin\chi+s\,t\,\partial_s\omega\,B_0\cos\chi,\qquad B_z=0.
$$
The first term describes advection around a circle; the term proportional to $t\partial_s\omega$ is the stretching by <differential rotation>. Spatial variation of $\omega$ also produces phase gradients proportional to $t\nabla\omega$. A rigidly rotating region merely turns the horizontal field, whereas a shearing region progressively winds it up.

Even small <magnetic diffusivity> becomes important once these gradients are large. Locally a phase mode of azimuthal order $m$ has damping rate of order $\eta m^2|\nabla\omega|^2t^2$, leading to attenuation of order $\exp[-\eta m^2|\nabla\omega|^2t^3/3]$. The associated <magnetic phase mixing under differential rotation> time is
$$
t_{\mathrm{mix}}\sim[\eta m^2|\nabla\omega|^2]^{-1/3}.
$$
For a horizontal uniform seed, $m=1$; if $|\nabla\omega|\sim\Omega/\ell$, this is $\Omega^{-1}R_m^{1/3}$, much shorter than the ordinary diffusion time at large $R_m$. Resistive smoothing cancels the closely spaced oppositely directed field and, in persistent circulation regions with an imposed surrounding field, drives <flux expulsion> from the interiors toward their boundaries. The detailed final distribution depends on the geometry and boundary forcing. Winding alone does not supply a self-sustaining poloidal–toroidal regeneration loop, and magnetic feedback can again limit the kinematic process.

= Solution

In <mean-field electrodynamics>, use <Reynolds averaging> to write $\mathbf U_{\mathrm{tot}}=\overline{\mathbf U}+\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf B_{\mathrm{tot}}=\overline{\mathbf B}+\mathbf b$, with zero-mean fluctuations. Assume the averaging operation commutes with the <derivatives> and that resolved quantities vary little across an averaging region. Averaging the induction equation then gives
$$
\partial_t\overline{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times(\overline{\mathbf U}\times\overline{\mathbf B}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal E})+\eta\nabla^2\overline{\mathbf B},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}=\langle\mathbf u\times\mathbf b\rangle.
$$
This is the <mean-field electromotive force>, a vector correlation with units of velocity times <magnetic field>. The closure problem is to express this correlation in terms of the resolved field.

In the kinematic regime the flow statistics are prescribed, so the response is linear in the mean <magnetic field>, after independently excited fluctuation transients have decayed. If the mean-field scale $L$ is much greater than the turbulent scale $\ell$, and its evolution is slow compared with the response memory, expand that response in the local mean <magnetic field> and its gradients:
$$
\mathcal E_i=\alpha_{ij}\overline B_j+c_{ijk}\partial_k\overline B_j+\cdots.
$$
Without scale separation one generally has a spatially nonlocal and temporally retarded response rather than constant local coefficients.

For <homogeneous turbulence> the coefficients are independent of position. Invariance under proper rotations makes the rank-two response proportional to $\delta_{ij}$, and the isotropic rank-three gradient response proportional to the alternating tensor. Choosing the <diffusivity> sign convention gives
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}=\alpha\overline{\mathbf B}-\beta\nabla\times\overline{\mathbf B}+\cdots.}
$$
The <alpha effect> can regenerate a mean <magnetic field>, while $\beta$ supplies <turbulent magnetic diffusivity>. For constant positive $\beta$, $\nabla\times(-\beta\nabla\times\overline{\mathbf B})=\beta\nabla^2\overline{\mathbf B}$ because the <magnetic field> is <solenoidal>. Thus the total <diffusivity> is $\eta+\beta$.

The electromotive vector is a <polar vector>, whereas the <magnetic field> is an <axial vector>. Therefore $\alpha$ is a <pseudoscalar> and changes sign under reflection; $\beta$ is an ordinary scalar. A nonzero isotropic <alpha effect> requires broken reflection symmetry, such as nonzero <kinetic helicity>. If “isotropic” includes reflection symmetry as well as rotations, $\alpha$ must vanish. The dimensions are $[\alpha]=L/T$ and $[\beta]=L^2/T$.

= Solution

Take zero mean flow locally and incompressible fluctuations. Subtracting the averaged induction equation gives
$$
(\partial_t-\eta\nabla^2)\mathbf b=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\overline{\mathbf B})+\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf b-\langle\mathbf u\times\mathbf b\rangle).
$$
The <first-order smoothing approximation> omits the second term on the right when solving for $\mathbf b$, but retains $\langle\mathbf u\times\mathbf b\rangle$ in the mean equation. For a locally uniform, slowly changing mean <magnetic field> the forcing becomes $(\overline{\mathbf B}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u$.

There are two controlled ways to make the omitted fluctuation term small. At small eddy <magnetic Reynolds number> $R_m=u_{\mathrm{rms}}\ell/\eta$, diffusion gives $b/B\sim R_m$, so the omitted $ub/\ell$ is smaller than the retained $uB/\ell$ by $R_m$. Alternatively, if $u_{\mathrm{rms}}\tau/\ell\ll1$ for the fluctuation response time $\tau$, then $b/B\sim u_{\mathrm{rms}}\tau/\ell$ and the short-memory response is perturbative. Mean-field scale separation is additionally required. Merely calling the <turbulence> weak or assuming that the mean <magnetic field> is small does not justify the omission.

With homogeneous initial transients removed, the causal linear solution is
$$
b_i(t)=\overline B_j\int_0^\infty e^{\eta\tau\nabla^2}\partial_j u_i(t-\tau)\,d\tau.
$$
Insert this into the electromotive correlation. <Isotropy> gives $\alpha_{ij}=\alpha\delta_{ij}$, so taking the <trace> yields the <helicity formula for isotropic first-order smoothing>:
$$
3\alpha=\int_0^\infty\epsilon_{imn}\left\langle u_m(t)\partial_i e^{\eta\tau\nabla^2}u_n(t-\tau)\right\rangle d\tau.
$$
Using the alternating-tensor sign gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-\frac13\int_0^\infty\left\langle\mathbf u(t)\cdot\nabla\times e^{\eta\tau\nabla^2}\mathbf u(t-\tau)\right\rangle d\tau.}
$$
The minus sign follows from $\epsilon_{imn}=-\epsilon_{min}$. This derivation specifies the diffusion and correlation weighting, rather than asserting that alpha is proportional to instantaneous helicity for arbitrary <turbulence>.

If $\eta\tau/\ell^2\ll1$, diffusion over a <correlation time> is negligible. Define the helicity <correlation time> by
$$
\tau_H\langle\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\varpi\rangle=\int_0^\infty\langle\mathbf u(t)\cdot\boldsymbol\varpi(t-\tau)\rangle d\tau,\qquad\boldsymbol\varpi=\nabla\times\mathbf u.
$$
For an ordinary decaying correlation with nonzero mean <kinetic helicity>, this gives the familiar estimate
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-\frac{\tau_H}{3}\langle\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\varpi\rangle.}
$$
It is the <kinetic helicity>, not <magnetic helicity>. Opposite flow handedness reverses the <alpha effect>. The time-integral formula remains meaningful if the equal-time helicity vanishes or its correlation changes sign, when replacing it by a single positive <correlation time> may fail. In the same weak-diffusion short-memory approximation the gradient forcing $-(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf B}$ gives $\beta=\frac13\int_0^\infty\langle\mathbf u(t)\cdot\mathbf u(t-\tau)\rangle d\tau\simeq\tau_Uu_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/3$; the two <correlation times> need not be equal.

In the small-$R_m$, slowly varying quasistatic regime, diffusion is instead essential. The response gives
$$
\alpha=-\frac1{3\eta}\left\langle\mathbf u\cdot(-\nabla^2)^{-1}\boldsymbol\varpi\right\rangle.
$$
For velocity concentrated at wavenumber $k_0$, this becomes $\alpha=-\langle\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\varpi\rangle/(3\eta k_0^2)$. Its effective memory is the <magnetic diffusion> time on the eddy scale. The controlled low-$R_m$ setting is developed in \a[https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/hkm2/PDFs/Moffatt_1970_JFM_41_435.pdf][Moffatt's analysis of turbulent dynamo action]; the causal-response calculation above also covers short-memory <turbulence> without assuming that diffusion is always negligible.

= Solution

For constant isotropic $\alpha,\beta$ and zero mean flow, the mean induction equation is
$$
\partial_t\overline{\mathbf B}=\alpha\nabla\times\overline{\mathbf B}+\eta_T\nabla^2\overline{\mathbf B},\qquad\eta_T=\eta+\beta>0.
$$
Let $\overline{\mathbf B}=\widehat{\mathbf B}e^{\sigma t+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$, with $\mathbf k\cdot\widehat{\mathbf B}=0$. On this two-dimensional transverse space, $i\mathbf k\times$ has eigenvalues $\pm k$. For example, if $\mathbf k=k\mathbf e_z$, the vectors $\mathbf e_x\pm i\mathbf e_y$ have these <curl> eigenvalues. The <homogeneous alpha-squared dynamo growth criterion> follows from
$$
\boxed{\sigma_\pm=\pm\alpha k-\eta_Tk^2,\qquad\text{growth iff some admissible }k>0\text{ satisfies }|\alpha|>\eta_Tk.}
$$
Choose the <circular polarization> of the mode to match the sign of $\alpha$. This is an <alpha-squared dynamo>: the <alpha effect> supplies both parts of the regeneration loop without <differential rotation>.

If arbitrarily long wavelengths are permitted, every nonzero alpha has a growing band $0<k<|\alpha|/\eta_T$. Maximizing the quadratic gives
$$
k_* =\frac{|\alpha|}{2\eta_T},\qquad\sigma_{\max}=\frac{\alpha^2}{4\eta_T},
$$
provided $k_*\ell\ll1$ so the mean-field approximation is valid. A uniform field has $k=0$, zero <curl> and zero growth; a constant alpha cannot amplify that uniform mode. In a <periodic domain> of size $L$, the lowest nonzero wavenumber is $2\pi/L$, giving $|\alpha|L/\eta_T>2\pi$. Other boundaries and geometries give different critical constants, but the general scale criterion is $|\alpha|L/\eta_T$ sufficiently large. Nonzero <kinetic helicity> alone is therefore not a sufficient finite-domain dynamo criterion: its induced alpha must overcome diffusion on an available mean-field scale.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The <geodynamo> operates in the electrically conducting liquid of <Earth's outer core>. <buoyancy> from thermal and <compositional convection> supplies motion, while the <Coriolis force> organizes rotating flow. Rotation together with radial stratification permits <kinetic helicity>, typically with opposite signs in the two hemispheres. In mean-field language this allows an <alpha effect> that converts poloidal and toroidal components into one another. A large-scale <alpha-squared dynamo> is therefore a plausible regeneration mechanism even without dominant <differential rotation>.

The growth condition compares this regeneration with the sum of molecular and turbulent <magnetic diffusion> on the core scale. A spherical model must impose the appropriate magnetic matching at the core boundaries and permit spatially varying, generally anisotropic alpha and <diffusivity> tensors. Rotation selects an axis, and stratification and boundaries select positions, so homogeneous <isotropic turbulence> is only a local idealization. Such a model can favor a predominantly axial dipole, but the preferred parity and threshold must be found from its global eigenproblem rather than deduced from the plane-wave formula alone. If mean shear is significant, alpha–Omega regeneration can also contribute.

The first-order-smoothing helicity formula is a useful guide to the sign and magnitude of alpha, not an automatically controlled numerical closure for the core. The important convective motions need not have small eddy <magnetic Reynolds number> or short enough <correlation time>; scale separation can also be limited. Furthermore, the <Lorentz force> modifies the flow and its correlations once the field grows. Saturation therefore requires nonlinear feedback, including <dynamo quenching> and magnetic-helicity constraints where relevant.

The observed field varies and occasionally reverses. A constant-coefficient growing kinematic eigenmode does not itself explain these reversals or select a saturated amplitude. Spatial and temporal variations, competing modes, and nonlinear flow–field interactions must be included. **<mean-field dynamo theory> supplies a physical regeneration mechanism and a testable threshold, while the geodynamo requires a rotating, bounded, nonlinear realization of that mechanism.**

= Solution
{parent=3}

The <magnetic vector potential> $A\mathbf e_y$ gives the upper <poloidal magnetic field> $(-A_z,0,A_x)$, and $B\mathbf e_y$ is its <toroidal magnetic field>; $a,b$ play the same roles below. This representation automatically satisfies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Diffusion acts in both regions. Above the interface the <alpha effect> converts <toroidal magnetic field> into <poloidal magnetic field>, while below it a mean shear $U'(z)=V$ converts the poloidal component $a_x$ into <toroidal magnetic field>. Separating these source regions still allows an <alpha-Omega dynamo> through diffusion across the interface.

For electromagnetic matching, keep the closure actually represented by these equations. Its retained turbulent <mean-field electromotive force> is $\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}=\alpha B\mathbf e_y$ above and zero below: only toroidal-to-poloidal alpha generation is retained. Let the mean velocity be $U(z)\mathbf e_y$, continuous at the interface, and write the mean <electric field> as
$$
\mathbf E=-U\mathbf e_y\times\mathbf B-\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}+\eta\nabla\times\mathbf B.
$$
This yields the specified scalar equations with $U'=0$ above and $U'=V$ below. In particular, the upper tangential electric component is
$$
E_x=-U\partial_x A-\eta\partial_zB,
$$
where $B$ is the scalar toroidal-field amplitude. The other <tangential component> is $E_y=-\alpha B-\eta\nabla^2A=-\partial_tA$.

Assume equal permeability, no imposed singular surface current and ordinary electromagnetic matching. <Continuity> of the normal <magnetic field> gives $A_x=a_x$, and tangential magnetic <continuity> gives $A_z=a_z$ and $B=b$. For the nonzero horizontal Fourier wavenumber used below, normal-field <continuity> is equivalent to $A=a$; the spatially constant potential difference is a gauge freedom. <Continuity> of $E_x$, with continuous $U$ and $A_x$, gives the final condition. Thus <electromagnetic matching for an alpha-Omega interface> is
$$
\boxed{A=a,\qquad A_z=a_z,\qquad B=b,\qquad\eta\partial_zB=\eta\partial_zb\quad(z=0).}
$$
<Continuity> of $E_y$ follows from $A=a$ and their time evolution, rather than supplying a fifth independent amplitude condition. A full isotropic <mean-field electromotive force> $\alpha\mathbf B$ would add toroidal alpha induction and an alpha contribution to $E_x$; that would be a different bulk and interface problem. Retaining it only in a boundary condition would be inconsistent with the equations being solved here.

Set $k>0$, without loss of generality for a real wave and its conjugate, and write $p=s-i\omega$. Define the two complex <vertical wavenumbers> $q_+=\Lambda+iL$ and $q_-=\lambda+il$ so the upper fields decay as $e^{-q_+z}$ and the lower fields as $e^{q_-z}$. The original PDF has a lowercase $l$ in the lower phase. The homogeneous upper $B$ and lower $a$ equations give
$$
p=\eta(q_+^2-k^2)=\eta(q_-^2-k^2).
$$
Both $q_+$ and $q_-$ have positive <real part>. Since their squares agree, they must be equal, rather than opposite. Put
$$
q=q_+=q_-,\qquad q^2=k^2+\frac p\eta,
$$
so $\Lambda=\lambda$ and $L=l$ follow from the equations rather than being assumed in advance.

Suppress the common factor $e^{pt+ikx}$ and write
$$
A=(A_0+Cz)e^{-qz},\quad B=B_0e^{-qz},\qquad a=a_0e^{qz},\quad b=(b_0+cz)e^{qz}.
$$
The identity $[p-\eta(\partial_z^2-k^2)](ze^{-qz})=2\eta q e^{-qz}$ gives the upper forcing equation, while the same operator on $ze^{qz}$ gives $-2\eta q e^{qz}$. Hence
$$
2\eta qC=\alpha B_0,\qquad-2\eta qc=ikVa_0.
$$
The first and third <interface dynamo matching> give $a_0=A_0$, $b_0=B_0$. The <derivative> conditions then give
$$
C-qA_0=qA_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C=2qA_0,
$$
$$
-qB_0=c+qb_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=-2qB_0.
$$
Thus $4\eta q^2A_0=\alpha B_0$ and $4\eta q^2B_0=ikVA_0$. Eliminating the nonzero amplitudes produces the <dispersion relation of an equal-diffusivity interface dynamo>:
$$
\boxed{16\eta^2q^4=ikV\alpha.}
$$
In terms of $D=V\alpha/(\eta^2k^3)>0$,
$$
\left(1+\frac p{\eta k^2}\right)^2=\frac{iD}{16},\qquad p=\eta k^2\left[-1\pm(1+i)\sqrt{\frac D{32}}\right].
$$
Each branch has a <square root> $q$ with positive <real part> and is therefore compatible with vertical localization. The branch with the plus sign is the potentially growing one:
$$
\boxed{s=\eta k^2\left(\sqrt{\frac D{32}}-1\right),\qquad\omega=-\eta k^2\sqrt{\frac D{32}},\qquad s>0\iff D>32.}
$$
The minus branch has $s=-\eta k^2(1+\sqrt{D/32})<0$. At $D=32$ the first branch is neutrally oscillatory. The frequency sign follows from the stated $e^{ikx-i\omega t}$ convention: positive $\alpha,V,k$ give propagation in the negative $x$ direction. Reversing the coupling sign reverses propagation, while the threshold for the conjugate sign depends on the magnitude of the dynamo number.

{title=Separated interface-dynamo source layers and the growth threshold at dynamo number 32}

The <equal-diffusivity interface dynamo> idealizes a solar configuration in which helical <convection> regenerates <poloidal magnetic field> above the <tachocline> and <differential rotation> generates strong <toroidal magnetic field> in or below it. Diffusion communicates the fields between the two regions, closing the feedback loop. This separation is the mechanism emphasized by \a[https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930052418][Parker's interface-wave model]. An oscillatory traveling mode gives migrating magnetic patterns and polarity reversals; buoyant toroidal flux can appear as <sunspot> belts. If $x$ is a local latitude coordinate, the direction of their migration depends on the alpha–shear sign and the coordinate orientation.

The magnetic period of this branch is $2\pi/|\omega|$; polarity reverses after half a period, and activity that depends on field strength rather than sign can repeat on that half-period. This is the qualitative relation between the magnetic <solar cycle> and its activity cycle. The model does not itself predict the observed period or guarantee equatorward migration: those require actual geometry, transport coefficients and shear signs. Real stars also have <diffusivity> contrasts, meridional transport, spatially varying regeneration and nonlinear feedback. The kinematic exponential mode describes onset, while saturation and a maintained finite-amplitude cycle require further physics.
