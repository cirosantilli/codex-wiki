Let $C_*(X)$ be the integral <singular chain complex>. Augment it by the homomorphism $C_0(X)\to\mathbb Z$ sending every point simplex to one. The <homology> of this augmented complex is <reduced homology>: for a nonempty space, it agrees with ordinary <homology> in positive degrees and $\widetilde H_0(X)$ is the kernel of $H_0(X)\to\mathbb Z$. The <relative homology> $H_*(X,A)$ is the <homology> of $C_*(X)/C_*(A)$; a relative cycle has boundary in $A$, and chains differing by a boundary or a chain in $A$ represent the same class.

<Excision theorem>: if $U\subset A\subset X$ and $\overline U\subset\operatorname{int}_X A$, the inclusion $(X\setminus U,A\setminus U)\hookrightarrow(X,A)$ induces isomorphisms on all <relative homology> groups. In particular, for a nonempty <CW subcomplex> $A$, choose a neighborhood $N$ deforming onto $A$, with the deformation fixing $A$. Such neighborhoods exist for <CW pairs>. Then $N/A$ is contractible. The <long exact sequence of a pair> gives $H_*(X,A)\cong H_*(X,N)$ and $H_*(X/A,\{*\})\cong H_*(X/A,N/A)$. Excision removes $A$ and the collapsed point respectively; the resulting pairs $(X\setminus A,N\setminus A)$ and $((X/A)\setminus\{*\},(N/A)\setminus\{*\})$ are homeomorphic. Consequently
$$
\boxed{H_*(X,A;\mathbb Z)\cong\widetilde H_*(X/A;\mathbb Z).}
$$
Here the nonempty-subcomplex condition is the usual quotient convention in this statement. If $A=\varnothing$, use the pointed cofiber $X_+$ with a disjoint basepoint; the literal unpointed quotient $X/\varnothing=X$ would not give the asserted degree-zero identity.

For one embedded circle, its <long exact sequence of a pair> reduces to
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z\longrightarrow H_2(\Sigma_g,A)\longrightarrow\mathbb Z
\xrightarrow{i_*}\mathbb Z^{2g}\longrightarrow H_1(\Sigma_g,A)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad H_0(\Sigma_g,A)=0.
$$
A separating circle bounds a subsurface, so its <homology class> is zero. A nonseparating circle has a transverse closed curve meeting it once, showing that its class is nonzero and primitive. Thus $i_*$ is either zero or an injection with torsion-free cokernel. The resulting possibilities are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &H_2(\Sigma_g,A)&H_1(\Sigma_g,A)\\\hline
[A]=0&\mathbb Z^2&\mathbb Z^{2g}\\
[A]\ne0&\mathbb Z&\mathbb Z^{2g-1}
\end{array}}
$$
with all other relative groups zero. These ranks determine whether the circle is <null-homologous>.

For $g\geq2$, a disk boundary and a separating circle bounding a once-punctured torus have the same relative groups. The first is <null-homotopic>; the second is not. For a direct verification, use the surface-group presentation $\langle a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_g,b_g\mid\prod_i[a_i,b_i]=1\rangle$. Send $a_1,b_1$ to free generators $u,v$, send $a_2,b_2$ to $v,u$, and all remaining generators to one. The relation maps to $[u,v][v,u]=1$, so this defines a homomorphism to the <free group>. The separating circle represents $[a_1,b_1]$ and maps to the nonempty reduced word $uvu^{-1}v^{-1}$; it is therefore not null-homotopic. This proves the requested failure to detect nullhomotopy. There is a necessary low-genus qualification: **on $\Sigma_0$ or $\Sigma_1$, an embedded circle is nullhomologous exactly when it is nullhomotopic**. On the sphere every circle bounds a disk; on the torus every essential embedded circle is nonseparating. The printed nondetection assertion therefore needs $g\geq2$.

For two disjoint embedded circles let $r$ be the rank of their image in $H_1(\Sigma_g)$, and let $c$ be the number of connected components of the cut surface. Removing open annular neighborhoods and applying <excision> gives the compact cut surface $W$ relative to its boundary. <Poincare-Lefschetz duality> gives $H_2(W,\partial W)\cong H^0(W)=\mathbb Z^c$ and $H_1(W,\partial W)\cong H^1(W)$, which is free. Thus there is no hidden torsion. The long exact sequence gives $c=3-r$, and the <Euler characteristic> of the pair is $2-2g$, giving
$$
\boxed{H_2(\Sigma_g,A)=\mathbb Z^{3-r},\qquad H_1(\Sigma_g,A)=\mathbb Z^{2g+1-r},\qquad H_j=0\ (j\ne1,2).}
$$
The possibilities are $r=0,1,2$: two separating circles give zero; a nonseparating circle with a disk boundary, or two parallel essential circles, give one; circles around two different handles give two. For genus zero only $r=0$ occurs; for genus one $r=0,1$ occur; for every genus at least two all three occur. This is <relative homology of a surface modulo disjoint circles>.

For integral <singular cochains> $a\in C^p(X)$ and $b\in C^q(X)$, define the <cup product> on an ordered simplex by
$$
(a\smile b)(\sigma)=a(\sigma[0,\ldots,p])\,b(\sigma[p,\ldots,p+q]).
$$
The same construction works over any commutative coefficient ring. Expanding the <coboundary> as the alternating sum of faces gives the Leibniz identity
$$
\delta(a\smile b)=\delta a\smile b+(-1)^p a\smile\delta b.
$$
Indeed faces deleting vertices before the split occur in the first sum, faces after the split occur in the second, and the two terms at the common vertex cancel with opposite signs. Thus the product of <cocycles> is a <cocycle>. Replacing $a$ by $a+\delta c$ changes the product with a <cocycle> $b$ by $\delta(c\smile b)$; replacing $b$ by $b+\delta d$ changes the product with a <cocycle> $a$ by $(-1)^p\delta(a\smile d)$. Hence the product is independent of representatives and defines a **well-defined product on <cohomology classes>**.

With $\mathbb F_2=\mathbb Z_2$ coefficients, the real-projective-space <cohomology ring> is
$$
H^*(\mathbb{RP}^r;\mathbb F_2)=\mathbb F_2[t]/(t^{r+1}),\qquad |t|=1.
$$
For $n>m\geq1$, the pullback of the degree-one generator is either zero or $t$. The second choice would send the zero class $t^{m+1}$ to the nonzero class $t^{m+1}\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb F_2)$, contradicting the naturality of the <cup product>. Thus the generator pulls back to zero, and so do all its positive powers. For $m=0$ the target's reduced <cohomology> is already zero. Therefore **every such map induces zero on reduced mod-two <cohomology>**.

Suppose a continuous $f:S^n\to\mathbb R^n$ had $f(x)\ne f(-x)$ everywhere. Then
$$
h(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{|f(x)-f(-x)|}
$$
is a continuous odd map to $S^{n-1}$ and descends to $\bar h:\mathbb{RP}^n\to\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$. For $n\geq2$, a path from $x$ to $-x$ projects to a loop detected by the degree-one generator of the double cover. Its image lifts to a path from $h(x)$ to $-h(x)$, so is detected by the target's double cover as well. Thus $\bar h^*$ is nonzero in degree one, contradicting the preceding calculation. For $n=1$, an odd continuous map from the connected circle to $S^0$ is already impossible. The case $n=0$ is immediate. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{There exists }x\in S^n\text{ with }f(x)=f(-x).}
$$
This proves the <Borsuk-Ulam theorem> rather than assuming it.

For a closed cover $A_1,\ldots,A_{n+1}$, assume no member contains an <antipodal pair>. Apply the just-proved theorem to $F(x)=(d(x,A_1),\ldots,d(x,A_n))$, using any metric on the sphere. Empty sets can be assigned a constant positive coordinate. It gives equal distances at $x,-x$. If either point belongs to an $A_i$ with $i\leq n$, zero distance and closedness place both points in it, a contradiction. Hence neither lies in any of the first $n$ sets, and both must lie in $A_{n+1}$, another contradiction. This proves the <Lusternik-Schnirelmann-Borsuk theorem> for <closed sets>.

**Four <closed sets> on $S^2$ need not contain an <antipodal pair>.** Take the four unit vertices $v_i$ of a regular tetrahedron centered at zero and define $C_i=\{x:x\cdot v_i\geq x\cdot v_j\text{ for every }j\}$. These <closed sets> cover the sphere by maximizing the four scalar products. If $x$ and $-x$ belonged to $C_i$, both inequalities would force all four scalar products to be equal. Since $\sum v_i=0$, they would all be zero, and because the vertices span $\mathbb R^3$, $x$ would be zero. This is impossible on the sphere. Thus these four sets give the <antipodal-free closed cover from simplex Voronoi cells> counterexample.

For a locally compact space, <compactly supported cohomology> is
$$
H_c^q(X;\mathbb Z)=\varinjlim_{K\subset X\text{ compact}}H^q(X,X\setminus K;\mathbb Z),
$$
with transition maps induced by enlarging supports. In $\mathbb R^n$ closed balls are cofinal; their complements retract onto $S^{n-1}$. The relative <cohomology> long exact sequence and contractibility of $\mathbb R^n$ give
$$
\boxed{H_c^q(\mathbb R^n;\mathbb Z)=\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&q=n,\\0,&q\ne n.\end{cases}}
$$
This is <compactly supported cohomology of Euclidean space>; for $n=0$ it is simply the <cohomology> of a point.

For an oriented $n$-manifold without boundary, <Poincare duality> in its compact-support form states that capping with the locally finite <fundamental class> gives $H_c^q(M;\mathbb Z)\cong H_{n-q}(M;\mathbb Z)$. If $M$ is closed, the ordinary <fundamental class> $[M]\in H_n(M;\mathbb Z)$ gives $H^q(M;\mathbb Z)\cong H_{n-q}(M;\mathbb Z)$ and a perfect complementary-degree pairing over $\mathbb Q$.

Choose an oriented embedded closed ball $B$ in a connected closed oriented $M$. Collapse $M\setminus\operatorname{int}B$ to a point, identifying $B/\partial B$ with $S^n$ using its orientation. The quotient map is continuous. By <excision>, the <fundamental class> maps to the local oriented generator in $H_n(B,\partial B)$, and then to the generator of $H_n(S^n)$. Hence **the collapse map has $\boxed{\deg f=1}$**.

The reverse direction is not always possible. More generally a degree-one map $f:N\to M$ between closed oriented manifolds induces an injective map on rational <cohomology>: if $\alpha\ne0$, duality supplies $\beta$ with $\langle\alpha\smile\beta,[M]\rangle\ne0$, and
$$
\langle f^*\alpha\smile f^*\beta,[N]\rangle
=\deg(f)\langle\alpha\smile\beta,[M]\rangle\ne0.
$$
In particular **there is no degree-one map $S^2\to T^2$**, since $H^1(T^2;\mathbb Q)=\mathbb Q^2$ but $H^1(S^2;\mathbb Q)=0$. This is a counterexample to an unconditional reverse assertion.

Finally let $p:\mathbb{CP}^3\to S^4$ be the given bundle projection. The standard <cohomology ring of complex projective space> is $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^3;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z[h]/(h^4)$, $|h|=2$; the integral generator $u$ of $H^4(S^4)$ has $p^*u=mh^2$ for some integer $m$. If a <section of a fiber bundle> $s$ existed, $p\circ s=1$ would give $s^*p^*=1$ by functoriality. But $H^2(S^4)=0$, so $s^*h=0$ and $s^*(mh^2)=0$, contradicting $s^*p^*u=u\ne0$. The facts used here are the sphere <cohomology> groups, the projective-space ring just stated, and multiplicative functoriality of <cohomology>. Thus **the bundle has no section**, the <cohomological obstruction to a section of the twistor bundle>.

Let $\pi:E\to B$ be an oriented real <vector bundle> of rank $r$ over a paracompact base. A <Thom class> is a class $U\in H^r(E,E\setminus B;\mathbb Z)$ restricting on every oriented fiber pair $(\mathbb R^r,\mathbb R^r\setminus0)$ to its positive generator. The <Thom isomorphism theorem> states that this class exists uniquely with those fiber restrictions and that
$$
\boxed{H^q(B;\mathbb Z)\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }H^{q+r}(E,E\setminus B;\mathbb Z),\qquad
 a\longmapsto\pi^*a\smile U.}
$$
Here $B$ is identified with the zero section. Equivalently use the disk/sphere bundle pair. The compact-support version is $H_c^q(B;\mathbb Z)\cong H_c^{q+r}(E;\mathbb Z)$, with fiberwise compact support in the Thom construction. If the bundle is not oriented, the integral assertion requires the corresponding <orientation local system>.

For a closed co-oriented embedded submanifold $Y$ of codimension $r$, co-orientation means an orientation of its normal bundle. A <tubular neighborhood> identifies a neighborhood of $Y$ with that bundle. Its <Thom class>, transferred by excision, is a class in $H^r(M,M\setminus Y;\mathbb Z)$. For compact $Y$ this maps into the direct limit defining <compactly supported cohomology>, giving $\boxed{\varepsilon_Y\in H_c^r(M;\mathbb Z)}$. The construction is independent of the tubular neighborhood and uses the chosen co-orientation. In an oriented ambient manifold it is the <Poincare dual> of the oriented submanifold. Here “closed submanifold” has the usual compact-without-boundary meaning; if it meant merely a closed subset and $Y$ were noncompact, the supported relative class would still exist but need not have compact support.

We next prove the fixed-point implication without imposing an unprinted orientability assumption on $M$. Set
$$
L(f)=\sum_k(-1)^k\operatorname{tr}(f^*:H^k(M;\mathbb Q)\to H^k(M;\mathbb Q)).
$$
Use a finite <triangulation> of the closed smooth manifold. The elementary <Hopf trace identity> says that the alternating trace of a chain endomorphism equals its alternating trace on <homology>. To verify it, split each finite-dimensional chain space as boundaries, a complement representing <homology>, and a complement mapping isomorphically onto the preceding boundaries. The traces on these two boundary summands cancel in adjacent degrees, leaving exactly the <homology> traces. The <cohomology> traces equal the <homology> traces because the induced maps are dual over $\mathbb Q$.

If $f$ has no fixed points, compactness gives $\delta=\min_x d(x,f(x))>0$. Choose a <triangulation> $K$ of mesh less than $\delta/4$ and a sufficiently fine subdivision $K'$ admitting a simplicial approximation $F:K'\to K$ uniformly within $\delta/4$ of $f$. The subdivision <chain map> $s:C_*(K)\to C_*(K')$ sends each simplex to simplices inside it. For a vertex $v$ of such a small simplex, $d(v,F(v))>3\delta/4$, so $F(v)$ cannot lie in the original coarse simplex. Consequently $F_\#s$ has zero diagonal coefficient on every coarse simplex. Its trace vanishes in every degree, while it induces $f_*$ on <homology>. The trace identity yields $L(f)=0$. Taking the contrapositive proves
$$
\boxed{L(f)\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ f\text{ has a fixed point}.}
$$
This is the <Lefschetz fixed-point theorem>, established here rather than invoked. For oriented $M$ the same identity is the <graph-diagonal formula for the Lefschetz number>: $L(f)=\langle(1,f)^*\varepsilon_\Delta,[M]\rangle$, which vanishes when the graph misses the diagonal. Finally, homotopic maps induce the same <cohomology> maps, so their <Lefschetz numbers> agree.

Take the double cover of $S^2$ branched at $2g+2$ points, with deck <involution> $\iota$. One explicit construction cuts the sphere along $g+1$ disjoint arcs pairing the branch points and cross-glues two copies along the cuts. Its <Euler characteristic> is $2\chi(S^2)-(2g+2)=2-2g$, so it is an oriented closed surface of genus $g$. Locally at a branch point the projection is $z\mapsto z^2$ and the <involution> is $z\mapsto-z$; thus the <involution> is nonidentity and orientation-preserving, also when $g=0$.

The invariant rational <cohomology> of a finite surface action identifies with the <cohomology> of its quotient. For this action, triangulate the quotient with the branch points as vertices and lift the <triangulation>. Quotient <cochains> identify with invariant <cochains>, and averaging over the two deck transformations makes taking invariants exact over $\mathbb Q$. Hence the invariant part of $H^1(S;\mathbb Q)$ is $H^1(S^2;\mathbb Q)=0$. Since $\iota^2=1$, its eigenvalues are $\pm1$ and it is therefore $-I$ on the $2g$-dimensional $H^1$. Its actions on $H^0$ and $H^2$ are both the identity. Thus
$$
\boxed{L(\iota)=1-(-2g)+1=2g+2\ne0.}
$$
<Homotopy> invariance and the result just proved show that **every map homotopic to this <involution> has fixed points**, for every genus.

Let $P$ be a sphere with $g+1$ disjoint open disks removed, and form its double $S$ by gluing two copies along the boundary. Then $\chi(S)=2\chi(P)=2(1-g)$, so $S$ is an oriented closed surface of genus $g$. The <involution> $\iota$ swapping the two halves fixes precisely the $g+1$ seam circles.

Use disjoint collars with coordinates $(s,t)\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\times(-\eta,\eta)$, where $\iota(s,t)=(s,-t)$. Choose a smooth even bump function $\chi(t)$ supported inside the collar, with $\chi(0)=1$, and choose $0<\epsilon<1$. Define an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism $R$ on each collar by $R(s,t)=(s+\epsilon\chi(t),t)$ and as the identity outside the collars. Scaling $\epsilon$ from zero gives an isotopy from the identity to $R$, so $F=R\circ\iota$ is homotopic to $\iota$.

A fixed point of $F$ in a collar would satisfy $-t=t$, hence $t=0$. Its other coordinate would then require $s+\epsilon=s$ modulo one, impossible for the chosen $\epsilon$. Outside the collars, $F=\iota$ exchanges the interiors of the halves and has no fixed points. Therefore **$\iota$ has fixed circles but is homotopic to the fixed-point-free map $F$**. This is the <fixed-point-free perturbation of a doubled-surface reflection>; the construction includes genus zero, where the double is a sphere.

For a based space $(X,x_0)$ and $k\geq1$, the <homotopy group> $\pi_k(X,x_0)$ is the set of based <homotopy> classes of maps $(I^k,\partial I^k)\to(X,x_0)$, equivalently based maps $S^k\to X$. Concatenating along one coordinate defines its group operation; reversing that coordinate gives the inverse. The group is abelian for $k\geq2$. The symbol $\pi_0(X)$ records path components and is generally only a pointed set. An <Eilenberg–MacLane space> $K(G,n)$ is a connected CW space with $\pi_n=G$ and all other positive <homotopy groups> zero; for $n>1$ the group $G$ must be abelian.

Projection onto coordinates gives
$$
\pi_k(X\times Y)\cong\pi_k(X)\times\pi_k(Y):
$$
a map to a product is exactly a pair of maps, and a based <homotopy> to the product is exactly a pair of based homotopies. These identifications respect concatenation. Thus $K(G,n)\times K(H,n)$ has exactly the <homotopy groups> required of $K(G\times H,n)$. Taking CW models and using the allowed uniqueness of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces gives
$$
\boxed{K(G,n)\times K(H,n)\simeq K(G\times H,n).}
$$

Let $p$ be prime. Take $S^\infty$ in complex coordinates, with $C_p$ acting by scalar multiplication by a primitive $p$th root of unity. This is a free action. Every map from a finite-dimensional sphere lands in a finite stage and becomes null-homotopic in a sufficiently higher sphere, so $S^\infty$ is weakly contractible; the <Whitehead theorem> for <CW complexes> makes it contractible. Therefore the infinite lens space $S^\infty/C_p$ has contractible <universal cover>, <fundamental group> $C_p$, and no higher <homotopy>: it is a model of $K(C_p,1)$.

Here is the <group cohomology> computation, including the ring structure. For the cyclic group generated by $g$, the standard free resolution alternates the maps $g-1$ and $N=1+g+\cdots+g^{p-1}$. Applying $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z[C_p]}(-,\mathbb F_p)$ makes both maps zero, so each <cohomology> degree is one-dimensional. The period-two identity <chain map> represents a class $b$ in degree two, and cup multiplication by this periodicity class is an isomorphism from each degree to the degree two higher. For odd $p$, a nonzero degree-one class $a$ has $a^2=0$ by graded commutativity, while $b^j$ and $ab^j$ are nonzero by periodicity. For $p=2$ the model is <homotopy> equivalent to $\mathbb{RP}^\infty$, whose ring is the infinite version of that computed in question 2. Consequently the <mod-p cohomology ring of a cyclic group> is
$$
\boxed{H^*(K(C_p,1);\mathbb F_p)=
\begin{cases}\mathbb F_2[t],&p=2,\quad |t|=1,\\
\Lambda(a)\otimes\mathbb F_p[b],&p\text{ odd},\quad |a|=1,\ |b|=2.
\end{cases}}
$$
The standard results used are the contractible-cover description of a $K(G,1)$, its identification of singular <cohomology> with group <cohomology>, and the cyclic periodic resolution with cup-periodicity just stated.

The product equivalence and the assumed <Künneth theorem> over the <field> give
$$
\boxed{H^*(K(C_p\times C_p,1);\mathbb F_p)=
\begin{cases}\mathbb F_2[t_1,t_2],&p=2,\\
\Lambda(a_1,a_2)\otimes\mathbb F_p[b_1,b_2],&p\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The tensor product is graded: for odd $p$, $a_1a_2=-a_2a_1$ and each $a_i^2=0$. The dimensions in degree $d$ are $d+1$: for $p=2$ count $t_1^it_2^{d-i}$; for odd $p$ the generating series is $(1+t)^2/(1-t^2)^2=(1-t)^{-2}$.

If an $n$-dimensional <CW complex> has exactly one cell in dimension $n+1$, then $C^{n+1}(X;\mathbb F_p)=\mathbb F_p$. <Cellular cohomology> is the quotient of the kernel of its outgoing <coboundary> by the image of the incoming one, a quotient of a subspace of this one-dimensional vector space. Thus
$$
\boxed{H^{n+1}(X;\mathbb F_p)\text{ is either }0\text{ or }\mathbb F_p.}
$$
This <one-cell bound on next-degree cohomology> is valid even with infinitely many higher cells; the printed finite-per-dimension hypothesis is more than it needs.

Now a free finite-group action makes $S^n\to M=S^n/G$ a covering. For $n>1$ the sphere is simply connected, so $\pi_1(M)=G$, $\pi_i(M)=0$ for $1<i<n$, and $\pi_n(M)=\mathbb Z$. The covering also identifies every higher <homotopy group> with that of $S^n$. Use the <homotopy-group effect of attaching higher cells>: attaching $(i+1)$-cells, $i\geq2$, preserves <homotopy groups> below $i$ and quotients $\pi_i$ by the subgroup generated by the attaching classes and their fundamental-group translates. Attach a single $(n+1)$-cell along a generator of $\pi_n(M)$; its translates do not require additional cells since the original generator already generates the underlying group. This kills $\pi_n$ without changing $\pi_1$ or the lower groups.

Successively in each higher degree attach cells representing generators of the remaining <homotopy group>. In the union every higher <homotopy group> vanishes, since a map and its relevant <homotopy> have compact domain and are handled at a finite stage. The result is a $K(G,1)$ with exactly one $(n+1)$-cell. No finiteness assumption on the number of later cells is needed for the preceding <cochain> bound. If desired, one can retain finitely many per degree using the <Serre finiteness theorem for homotopy groups>: a simply connected <CW complex> of finite type has finitely generated <homotopy groups>. At each finite stage the <universal cover> is simply connected and finite type, since $G$ is finite; choosing finitely many generators then preserves finite type inductively. The first stage needs just the stated $\pi_n(S^n)=\mathbb Z$.

If $G=C_p\times C_p$, the resulting $K(G,1)$ would have $\dim H^{n+1}\leq1$ by its single cell, but its already computed <cohomology> has dimension $n+2>1$ in that degree. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{G\not\cong C_p\times C_p\qquad\text{for every prime }p.}
$$
This is the <free sphere actions exclude elementary abelian subgroups of rank two> obstruction.
