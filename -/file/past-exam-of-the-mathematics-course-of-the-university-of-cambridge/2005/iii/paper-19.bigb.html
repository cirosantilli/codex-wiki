We use $[a,b]=a^{-1}b^{-1}ab$. To obtain a <Wirtinger presentation>, choose an oriented regular <knot diagram>, cut its strands at undercrossings, and give each resulting arc a positively oriented <meridian of a knot>. The complement of the arcs above the projection plane has a free <fundamental group> on these meridians. At a crossing, pushing an understrand meridian past the overstrand conjugates it by the overstrand meridian. Thus, if $a$ is the overstrand generator and $b,c$ are the incoming and outgoing understrand generators, the relation is $c=a^{\epsilon}ba^{-\epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is determined by the crossing orientation. Applying the <Seifert-van Kampen theorem> while adjoining the crossing neighborhoods gives one such relation per crossing. The remaining three-dimensional region adds no fundamental-group relation. For a connected diagram one crossing relation is redundant. This derives a <group presentation> from any tame knot diagram, rather than just specifying a recipe without its topological justification.

For the <Borromean rings>, choose three meridians $x,y,z$ and call the other three arc meridians $x',y',z'$. Base paths and orientations can be chosen so the six crossing relations read
$$
\begin{aligned}
x'&=zxz^{-1},&y'&=xyx^{-1},&z'&=yzy^{-1},\\
x'&=z'x(z')^{-1},&y'&=x'y(x')^{-1},&z'&=y'z(y')^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Eliminate the primed generators with the first row. The second row says respectively that $x$ commutes with $(z')^{-1}z=[y^{-1},z]$, that $y$ commutes with $(x')^{-1}x=[z^{-1},x]$, and that $z$ commutes with $(y')^{-1}y=[x^{-1},y]$. The last relation is redundant, just as one crossing relation is redundant in the <Wirtinger presentation>. Alternatively the <Hall-Witt identity>, after conjugating its three factors, shows that any two of these cyclic relations imply the third. Hence the <Borromean link group> is
$$
\boxed{G=\langle x,y,z\mid [x,[y^{-1},z]]=1,\ [y,[z^{-1},x]]=1\rangle.}
$$
Both relators have zero exponent sum in each generator. Passing from the free <group> to its <abelianization> therefore imposes no relations beyond commutation, and
$$
\boxed{G/[G,G]\cong\mathbb Z^3.}
$$
Equivalently the three linking-number maps to $\mathbb Z$ independently detect the three meridians.

Here is an explicit representation, followed by a faithfulness argument:
$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Y=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2i&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Z=\begin{pmatrix}i&1\\2i&2-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho(x)=X,\quad\rho(y)=Y,\quad\rho(z)=Z.
$$
Each <determinant> is one. Direct multiplication verifies the two relators. For example $[Y^{-1},Z]=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2i\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, which commutes with $X$. Merely checking those relators would not prove faithfulness, so we identify the image's complete <group presentation>.

Write $B=PSL_2(\mathbb Z[i])$, the <Gaussian Bianchi group>, and use its four generators $T,U,L,A$ from the allowed presentation. In $B$ the three proposed generators are
$$
X=T,\qquad Y=AU^{-2}A^{-1},\qquad Z=AT^{-2}A^{-1}LU^{-1}.
$$
The last equality is in $PSL_2$, so an overall minus sign in matrix multiplication is immaterial. Reduce matrices modulo $4$ in $\mathbb Z[i]$, and then quotient by $\{\pm I\}$. The finite image $Q$ has order $1536$: the residue field is $\mathbb F_2$, $SL_2(\mathbb F_2)$ has order six, and each of the three subsequent local-ring layers has a kernel of order $2^3$, before the final quotient by $\{\pm I\}$. Elementary elimination over this local ring shows that the images of $T,U,A$ generate it. The subgroup $\bar H=\langle\bar X,\bar Y,\bar Z\rangle$ has order $64$, as multiplication in this finite ring verifies. Let $K$ be its inverse image in $B$; then $[B:K]=24$.

For completeness the following gives the coset computation needed to check the subgroup presentation explicitly. Cosets are right cosets $K r$; columns record right multiplication by $T,U,L,A$. Representatives are words in the allowed generators.
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|rrrr}
j&r_j&T&U&L&A\\\hline
0&1&0&1&2&3\\
1&U&1&0&4&5\\
2&L&2&4&0&6\\
3&A&7&8&6&0\\
4&UL&4&2&1&9\\
5&UA&10&11&9&1\\
6&LA&12&13&3&2\\
7&AT&9&14&10&15\\
8&AU&14&3&13&16\\
9&ULA&15&16&5&4\\
10&UAT&6&17&7&12\\
11&UAU&17&5&16&13\\
12&LAT&5&18&15&10\\
13&LAU&18&6&8&11\\
14&ATU&16&7&17&19\\
15&ATA&3&20&12&7\\
16&AUA&20&9&11&8\\

19&ATUA&19&22&21&14\\

\end{array}
$$
Apply the <Reidemeister-Schreier theorem>: for a transition $j\mathrel{\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{g}}k$, use generator $r_jg r_k^{-1}$, omitting the 23 tree transitions. Rewrite each of the eight allowed ambient relators starting at each of the 24 rows. There are 73 remaining Schreier generators. Adjoin the names $x=T$, $y=AU^{-2}A^{-1}$, $z=AT^{-2}A^{-1}LU^{-1}$ and eliminate the Schreier generators using relations in which they occur once. The remaining relations, with an uppercase letter denoting the inverse of the corresponding lowercase one, are the words
$$
\begin{aligned}
p&=ZYxyXzxYXy,\\
q&=ZYzXZxyXzx,\\
w&=ZxYXzXZxyXzxYxyX.
\end{aligned}
$$
This table and the rewriting rule determine every relation without an appeal to a guessed presentation. In this notation cyclically reduced versions of the two desired relators are $r=ZYzXZyzYxy$ and $q$. Elementary substitutions of relator segments, with cyclic reduction at each step, give
$$
p\ \longrightarrow\ r\ \longrightarrow\ 1,\qquad
w\ \longrightarrow\ ZYzXXZxyXzxYxy\ \longrightarrow\ q\ \longrightarrow\ 1
$$
using $r,q$; conversely $r\longrightarrow p\longrightarrow1$ using $p,q$. These substitutions show equality of the two normal closures. For example the first substitution replaces $xyX$ in the cyclic rotation of $p$ by $zxZyzXZ$, as allowed by $q$, and then the initial $ZYz$ is replaced by $YXyZYzx$ using $r$. Thus $K$ has exactly the presentation of $G$, with $x,y,z$ represented by $X,Y,Z$. In particular these three matrices generate $K$, and
$$
\boxed{\rho:G\hookrightarrow PSL_2(\mathbb C)\text{ is faithful and has discrete image of index }24\text{ in }B.}
$$

To identify the actual link complement as a <hyperbolic three-manifold>, rather than only on a manifold with an isomorphic <group>, cut along the projection-plane regions, making the usual small crossing detours above and below. The octahedral projection graph has eight triangular regions. The upper and lower pieces are ideal octahedra; corresponding faces are matched with one-third-turn corner permutations, with opposite directions on adjacent checkerboard faces. Reversing these cuts reconstructs the link complement. Give both pieces the regular ideal <hyperbolic space> octahedron metric. All triangular face maps are <isometries> and each edge cycle has four dihedral angles $\pi/2$, so the metric has no edge singularity. Equal horospherical truncations match in Euclidean squares and assemble into three flat <tori>, giving complete <torus> cusps. The compact truncated remainder and these cusps show completeness and finite volume. Thus **the Borromean complement is a complete finite-volume hyperbolic three-manifold**. This also supplies a geometric discrete faithful holonomy, independently of the subgroup calculation.

Start with the <prime decomposition of a closed orientable three-manifold>. Its <fundamental group> is a free product of the prime-summand <groups>. A nontrivial <abelian group> cannot be a free product of two nontrivial <groups>. The same is true of a nontrivial finitely generated <nilpotent group>: it has nontrivial center, whereas such a free product has trivial center. Thus all nontrivial <fundamental group> comes from a single prime summand; simply connected summands do not affect the argument.

A prime but nonirreducible orientable summand is $S^2\times S^1$, giving $\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z$. Otherwise the infinite-group prime summand is an <irreducible three-manifold>. The <sphere theorem for three-manifolds> gives $\pi_2=0$. Its simply connected <universal cover> is noncompact, so its top ordinary homology vanishes; its first and second homology also vanish by simple connectivity and the <Hurewicz theorem>. It is therefore acyclic and contractible by the <Whitehead theorem>. The summand is <aspherical>, and its closed orientable three-dimensional classifying space gives <integral cohomological dimension of a group> equal to three: the cellular resolution gives the upper bound and top <Poincare duality> gives the lower bound.

If $\Gamma$ is torsion-free abelian, finite generation makes it $\mathbb Z^r$. Its <integral cohomological dimension of a group> is $r$, as one sees from the standard $r$-torus. The aspherical case therefore has $r=3$. We obtain
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbb Z^3.}
$$
Both occur, on $S^2\times S^1$ and the three-torus.

For the infinite torsion-free nilpotent case, the same reduction gives either $\mathbb Z$ or an aspherical <group> of cohomological dimension three. By the <Malcev completion of a torsion-free nilpotent group>, the latter is a lattice in a simply connected three-dimensional nilpotent <Lie group>. A nonabelian three-dimensional <nilpotent Lie algebra> has <abelianization> of dimension at least two: a nilpotent algebra generated modulo its derived algebra by one element would be generated by that element and thus be abelian. Its derived algebra is therefore one-dimensional, and nilpotence makes that line central. Hence it has a basis with a single nonzero bracket $[Y,T]=X$; the other possibility is an abelian algebra. In the nonabelian case the lattice's center is an isolated infinite cyclic subgroup, generated by $x$, and the quotient by it is $\mathbb Z^2$. Choose lifts $y,t$ of a basis of that quotient. Their commutator is $x^b$ for a nonzero integer $b$, and normal forms give
$$
\Gamma_b=\langle x,y,t\mid[x,y]=[x,t]=1,\ tyt^{-1}=x^by\rangle.
$$
The subgroup $A=\langle x,y\rangle\cong\mathbb Z^2$ is normal and $\Gamma_b/A\cong\mathbb Z$. In its ordered basis $x,y$, conjugation by $t$ acts by
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is the <unipotent torus-bundle group>. Indeed, its normal forms multiply according to
$$
(x^my^nt^k)(x^{m'}y^{n'}t^{k'})=x^{m+m'+bkn'}y^{n+n'}t^{k+k'}.
$$
Projection to the $n,k$ coordinates, followed by inspection of the central $m$ coordinate, proves torsion-freeness. All commutators lie in $\langle x^b\rangle$, which is central, proving nilpotence. The <mapping torus> of the <torus> automorphism with this matrix realizes every integer $b$ as a closed orientable <three-manifold>; $b=0$ gives $\mathbb Z^3$, while $b\ne0$ gives the Nil cases. Thus the complete list of <groups> under the nilpotent hypotheses is $\mathbb Z$ and the <groups> $\Gamma_b$.

Here is the center argument, with its literal qualification. If a nilpotent <group> with abelian quotient $\Gamma/A$ were nonabelian, the last nonzero term $\gamma_c\Gamma$ of its <lower central series> would satisfy
$$
1\ne\gamma_c\Gamma\subseteq[\Gamma,\Gamma]\subseteq A,\qquad
\gamma_c\Gamma\subseteq Z(\Gamma).
$$
Consequently $Z(\Gamma)\cap A=1$ forces the <group> to be abelian. **For the specific nontrivial rank-two $A$ in the printed family the hypothesis cannot actually occur:** when $b\ne0$, $x\in Z(\Gamma_b)\cap A$; when $b=0$, the intersection is all of $A$. The implication is valid, but should not be used to invent an example satisfying this impossible extra condition.

A <torsion-free finite extension of a lattice> with quotient $C_4$ is realized by the <quarter-turn flat three-manifold>. On $\mathbb R^3$ let
$$
a(u,v,w)=(u+1,v,w),\quad b(u,v,w)=(u,v+1,w),\quad
s(u,v,w)=(-v,u,w+1/4).
$$
Let $c$ be unit translation in the third coordinate. Then $sas^{-1}=b$, $sbs^{-1}=a^{-1}$, $scs^{-1}=c$, and $s^4=c$. The lattice $\Lambda=\langle a,b,c\rangle\cong\mathbb Z^3$ is normal and the quotient is $C_4$. A representative $a^mb^nc^ks^j$, $0\le j<4$, with $j\ne0$ has third-coordinate displacement $k+j/4\ne0$, so has no fixed point; for $j=0$ a nonidentity element is a nonzero translation. This proves torsion-freeness. The action is properly discontinuous, cocompact and orientation preserving, so it gives a closed orientable flat <three-manifold> with precisely the required extension.

For faithful linear holonomy on the maximal translation lattice, the <holonomy groups of closed orientable flat three-manifolds> are
$$
\boxed{1,\quad C_2,\quad C_3,\quad C_4,\quad C_6,\quad C_2\times C_2.}
$$
The cyclic examples replace the quarter-turn by a planar lattice rotation of order $m=2,3,4,6$ and a translation of $1/m$ along its axis. The square and triangular plane lattices realize these orders; the trace condition $2\cos(2\pi/m)\in\mathbb Z$ explains the crystallographic restriction. The remaining case is obtained from
$$
\alpha(u,v,w)=(u+1/2,-v,-w),\qquad
\beta(u,v,w)=(-u,v+1/2,-w+1/2).
$$
Their squares translate by one in the first and second directions, and $(\alpha\beta)^2$ translates by minus one in the third. Their linear parts generate $C_2\times C_2$. The three nontranslation cosets have half-integral displacement along their respective fixed axes, so every element acts without a fixed point. These constructions realize every member of the orientable three-dimensional Euclidean holonomy classification.

The maximality/faithfulness restriction is essential. Taken literally as a question about arbitrary <finite group> quotients, the wording has more answers: $\mathbb Z$ has every finite cyclic quotient, and $\mathbb Z^3$ has, for example, quotient $C_5$. An exact criterion for the literal extension question is as follows. Choose an action $Q\to\operatorname{Aut}(A)$ and an extension class $e\in H^2(Q,A)$, where $A=\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb Z^3$. For every subgroup $\langle q\rangle$ of prime order $p$, require
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{res}_{\langle q\rangle} e\ne0\ \text{ in }\ A^q/(1+q+\cdots+q^{p-1})A.}
$$
To prove this, lift $q$ to $g$. Its $p$th power is in $A^q$, and replacing $g$ by $ag$ changes that power by the displayed norm of $a$. Thus a lift of order $p$ exists exactly when this restriction is zero. Any torsion element has a prime-order power, so the condition is both necessary and sufficient. For an oriented flat realization in rank three the linear action must also preserve orientation. This distinguishes a complete extension criterion from the finite faithful-holonomy list.

First correct the group-ring condition in the PDF. For an infinite <group>, a left-invariant element of $\mathbb Z\Gamma$ would have constant coefficients on the whole <group>; finite support then forces it to be zero. Hence $H^0(\Gamma,\mathbb Z\Gamma)=0$. For a <Poincare duality group> of positive dimension, the correct condition is
$$
\boxed{H^k(\Gamma,\mathbb Z\Gamma)=0\ (k\ne n),\qquad
H^n(\Gamma,\mathbb Z\Gamma)=D\cong\mathbb Z\ \text{as an abelian group}.}
$$
It must be accompanied by the finiteness condition: the trivial module has a finite-length resolution by <finitely generated projective modules>. The module $D$ has a right <group> action, possibly an orientation-sign action. The degree-zero exception only applies to the trivial $PD^0$ <group>, not simultaneously to positive-dimensional <groups>.

Here is how the duality follows. Put $R=\mathbb Z\Gamma$ and take a length-$n$ <projective resolution> $P_*\to\mathbb Z$. Dualize to $P^* =\operatorname{Hom}_R(P_*,R)$. Its cohomology is zero except in degree $n$, where it is $D$. Reversing degrees therefore makes $Q_j=P_{n-j}^*$ a projective right-$R$ resolution of $D$. Finite generation and projectivity give, for every left $R$-module $V$, an isomorphism of cochain complexes
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_R(P_*,V)\cong P^*\otimes_R V.
$$
Taking cohomology and reversing the grading yields
$$
H^k(\Gamma,V)\cong\operatorname{Tor}^R_{n-k}(D,V).
$$
Since $D$ has underlying abelian <group> $\mathbb Z$, its action is a sign character $\omega:\Gamma\to\{\pm1\}$. Twisting the resolution by this character identifies the right side with <group homology> having coefficient module $D\otimes_{\mathbb Z}V$, with diagonal left action $\gamma(d\otimes v)=\omega(\gamma)d\otimes\gamma v$. Thus
$$
\boxed{H^k(\Gamma,V)\cong H_{n-k}(\Gamma,D\otimes V).}
$$
For an orientable <Poincare duality group>, $D$ is trivial and this is untwisted <Poincare duality>. The natural isomorphism can equivalently be realized by <cap product> with the <group> fundamental class. The reversed finite projective complex explains why the top group-ring condition produces duality for every coefficient module, rather than just matching integral Betti numbers.

Now let $H$ have finite index in $\Gamma$. There is an isomorphism of left $\Gamma$-modules
$$
\mathbb Z\Gamma\cong\operatorname{Coind}_H^\Gamma(\mathbb ZH).
$$
One explicit map sends $r\in\mathbb Z\Gamma$ to the $H$-linear function $g\mapsto\operatorname{pr}_H(gr)$, where $\operatorname{pr}_H$ retains only terms in $H$. Finite index ensures that all such functions arise in this way. <Shapiro's lemma> gives the isomorphism
$$
H^k(\Gamma,\mathbb Z\Gamma)\cong H^k(H,\mathbb ZH)
$$
as abelian <groups>. If $\Gamma$ is a <Poincare duality group>, restricting its finite <projective resolution> to $H$ preserves projectivity and finite generation: the <group> ring is finite free over $\mathbb ZH$. This proves that $H$ is $PD^n$.

For the converse one needs the usual ambient torsion-free hypothesis. The standard finite-index resolution lemmas say that type $FP_\infty$ is preserved under finite-index passage, and that a torsion-free <group> of finite virtual cohomological dimension has the same integral cohomological dimension as a finite-index subgroup. Thus if $H$ is $PD^n$ and $\Gamma$ is torsion-free, $\Gamma$ has a finite resolution by finitely generated projective modules of length $n$. The preceding explicit coinduction and Shapiro calculation transfers exactly the required group-ring cohomology, and proves the <finite-index invariance of Poincare duality for torsion-free groups>.

Without that hypothesis the claimed converse is false. Take $\Gamma=\mathbb Z\times C_2$ and $H=\mathbb Z\times1$. Then $H$ is index two and is $PD^1$, while $\Gamma$ contains torsion. Its <integral cohomological dimension of a group> is infinite: restriction of any putative finite <projective resolution> to $C_2$ would contradict the nonzero cohomology $H^{2j}(C_2,\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z/2$ for every $j\ge1$. Also, orientability need not ascend: the <torus> subgroup of the Klein-bottle <group> has trivial orientation action, whereas the full <group> has a nontrivial one. The invariant theorem concerns the possibly twisted notion of $PD^n$.

Finally, a closed aspherical <surface> has a two-dimensional contractible <universal cover> and exactly this compact-support duality, so its <surface group> is $PD^2$. Conversely the algebraic condition forces the same local duality dimension and, in the orientable case, a nonsingular skew pairing on first cohomology, the familiar algebra of a closed orientable <surface>. Infinite-index subgroups behave like noncompact <surfaces> rather than closed ones. These are strong reasons to expect a <surface group>; in fact the $PD^2$ classification theorem confirms that every integral $PD^2$ <group> is the <fundamental group> of a closed aspherical <surface>. This includes the <torus> and, with orientation twist, the Klein bottle; the sphere and projective plane do not give $PD^2$ <groups>.

The <Loop theorem> converts a nonzero kernel of $\pi_1(F)\to\pi_1(M)$, for a boundary <surface> $F$, into a properly embedded <compression disk>. It is stronger than simply producing a singular nullhomotopy, and does not assert that every original self-intersecting loop is the boundary of the final disk.

The proof outline uses a strengthened version: keep the boundary class outside a prescribed <normal subgroup> $N\triangleleft\pi_1(F)$. Begin with a singular disk in general position. Local cut-and-paste removes branch points; when a disk splits, at least one boundary stays outside $N$, since otherwise their product would lie in $N$. Take a regular neighborhood of its image and lift the disk through connected double covers of successive neighborhoods. Each lift decreases a finite lexicographic singularity complexity, so the tower terminates. At the top no double cover remains; mod-two duality forces its boundary components to be spheres. A boundary curve of the disk neighborhood lying outside the pulled-back $N$ then bounds an embedded disk on such a sphere, pushed inward. Project down one stage at a time. Exchange disk sectors along double curves, retaining a boundary outside $N$, to remove the projection's double intersections. Repeating yields an embedded disk downstairs. Taking $N=1$ proves the <Loop theorem>; controlling a prescribed simple boundary gives <Dehn's lemma>. The key points are termination of the covering tower and preservation of an essential boundary during surgery, not an unjustified removal of all intersections by general position alone.

Two classification applications are particularly useful. First, a two-sided embedded <surface> is incompressible precisely when its inclusion is fundamental-group injective, with the usual exclusion of inessential sphere components. A <compression disk> immediately supplies a nontrivial kernel element. In the other direction, cut along the <surface> and use the <Seifert-van Kampen theorem>: if both boundary-copy maps were injective, the amalgamated-product or HNN normal-form theorem would make the original inclusion injective. Hence a nontrivial kernel appears on one cut side, and the <Loop theorem> produces a <compression disk>. This is what makes algebraically detected essential <surfaces> usable in three-manifold splitting and incompressible hierarchies.

Second, consider a compact orientable <irreducible three-manifold> with one <torus> boundary. If that boundary is not fundamental-group injective, the <Loop theorem> supplies a <compression disk>. An essential simple curve on a <torus> is nonseparating, so compression changes the <torus> into a sphere. Pushing this sphere into the manifold, irreducibility gives a ball on the side away from the original <torus> boundary; the region between the sphere and the <torus> is the compression neighborhood. Undoing compression therefore attaches one one-handle to a ball. This proves the <solid-torus recognition by a compression disk> criterion:
$$
\boxed{\text{irreducible compact orientable manifold with compressible torus boundary}\ \cong D^2\times S^1.}
$$
As a concrete consequence, a knot with <knot group> $\mathbb Z$ is the unknot. Its exterior is irreducible: a sphere disjoint from a connected knot bounds a ball on the side not containing the knot. The map from its boundary <group> $\mathbb Z^2$ to $\mathbb Z$ cannot be injective, so its exterior is a solid <torus>. The boundary of a meridian disk of this exterior lies in the kernel of the linking-number map; it is therefore the primitive preferred longitude. Joining that longitude to the knot through its tubular neighborhood makes an embedded spanning disk for the knot. This proves **the cyclic-knot-group unknot criterion**, rather than merely identifying a familiar consequence by name.

A smooth manifold alone has no distinguished notion of an <isometry>; one must first choose a <Riemannian metric> $g$. We therefore interpret the essay as concerning a smooth connected <Riemannian manifold> $(M,g)$. Its <isometry group> consists of diffeomorphisms $f$ with $f^*g=g$. In coordinates this is
$$
g_{ab}(f(x))\,\partial_i f^a\,\partial_j f^b=g_{ij}(x).
$$
An <isometry> preserves lengths, distances, the Levi-Civita connection, <geodesics> and all curvature tensors. The <Myers-Steenrod theorem> ensures that the <group> is a finite-dimensional <Lie group> acting smoothly, and that even distance-preserving bijections have the required smoothness.

The crucial rigidity is determination by one first jet. An <isometry> fixing $p$ satisfies
$$
f(\exp_p v)=\exp_p(df_pv)
$$
wherever the exponential chart is defined. If also $df_p=I$, it is the identity on a neighborhood. Continuing this equality through overlapping exponential neighborhoods proves it on connected $M$. Therefore the <isotropy group> at $p$ embeds in $O(T_pM,g_p)$. Its dimension is at most $n(n-1)/2$, while an orbit has dimension at most $n$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim\operatorname{Isom}(M,g)\le\frac{n(n+1)}2;\qquad n=3\text{ gives }\dim\operatorname{Isom}(M,g)\le6.}
$$
On a <compact manifold> the <group> is compact. Indeed distance preservation makes a family of <isometries> equicontinuous; compactness and the same argument for their inverses give subsequential limits which are inverse distance-preserving bijections. Smoothness and the Lie-group structure then follow from <Myers-Steenrod theorem>.

Infinitesimal <isometries> are <Killing vector fields>. Differentiating $f_t^*g=g$ at zero gives
$$
\mathcal L_Xg=0,\qquad \nabla_iX_j+\nabla_jX_i=0.
$$
On a complete <Riemannian manifold> these fields have complete flows and constitute the <Lie algebra> of the <isometry group>; on an incomplete manifold one must retain only fields whose flows are globally defined. A <Killing vector field> is fixed by its value and its skew derivative at one point, consistent with the same dimension bound. Along a <geodesic>, its restriction is a Jacobi field, so local infinitesimal data propagate rigidly.

In dimension three there is no independent Weyl curvature tensor: the <Riemann curvature tensor> is determined by the <Ricci tensor>. Large isotropy therefore strongly constrains the geometry. If the full dimension six is attained, the <group> is transitive with three-dimensional isotropy. That isotropy rotates all tangent two-planes transitively, forcing equal sectional curvatures at each point; transitivity makes the value constant. The simply connected complete models are the round $S^3$, Euclidean $\mathbb R^3$ and hyperbolic $\mathbb H^3$. Their <groups> are respectively $O(4)$, $\mathbb R^3\rtimes O(3)$ and $O^+(3,1)$, all of dimension six. The orientation-preserving hyperbolic <group> is $PSL_2(\mathbb C)$.

The standard <Thurston geometries> show how symmetry drops when a preferred direction is present:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{model}&\dim\operatorname{Isom}&\dim\text{continuous isotropy}\\\hline
S^3,\ \mathbb R^3,\ \mathbb H^3&6&3\\
S^2\times\mathbb R,\ \mathbb H^2\times\mathbb R&4&1\\
\mathrm{Nil},\ \widetilde{SL_2(\mathbb R)}&4&1\\
\mathrm{Sol}&3&0
\end{array}
$$
For the product models, the <surface> factor has a three-dimensional <isometry group>, and translations along the line add one parameter. For Nil and the universal covering of $SL_2(\mathbb R)$ with their standard geometric metrics, a three-dimensional transitive <group> is enlarged by a circle of isotropy. The Sol metric
$$
ds^2=e^{2w}du^2+e^{-2w}dv^2+dw^2
$$
is preserved by $(u,v,w)\mapsto(u_0+e^{-t}u,v_0+e^tv,w+t)$, giving three continuous parameters; its continuous isotropy is trivial because the distinguished expanding and contracting directions cannot be rotated into each other. Additional discrete symmetries may still occur. These dimensions refer to the model metrics, not arbitrary left-invariant perturbations.

A quotient generally has fewer global <isometries> than its local model. If $X$ is the complete simply connected <universal cover> and $M=X/\Gamma$, the <isometry group of a Riemannian quotient> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Isom}(M)=N_{\operatorname{Isom}(X)}(\Gamma)/\Gamma.}
$$
To prove this, lift an <isometry> to the <universal cover>. Conjugating a deck transformation by the lift again gives a deck transformation, so the lift normalizes $\Gamma$. Conversely a normalizing <isometry> descends, and two lifts differ by a deck transformation. A flat three-torus retains its three-dimensional translation <group>, with additional discrete lattice symmetries. A round spherical quotient retains only the normalizer of its finite deck <group>; the full six-dimensional <group> does not automatically descend.

For a closed <hyperbolic three-manifold>, there are no nonzero <Killing vector fields>. The integrated Killing identity is
$$
\int_M|\nabla X|^2\,dV=\int_M\operatorname{Ric}(X,X)\,dV=-2\int_M|X|^2\,dV.
$$
Both nonnegative terms must vanish. The compact <isometry group> is therefore zero-dimensional and hence finite. The <Mostow rigidity theorem> additionally says that isomorphisms between the <fundamental groups> of complete finite-volume hyperbolic three-manifolds are induced by unique hyperbolic <isometries>; this fixes the complete hyperbolic metric up to <isometry> and contrasts with the moduli of flat <tori>. An arbitrary smooth three-manifold need not admit a single homogeneous metric: prime decomposition and <torus> splitting may lead to several geometric pieces. Thus **local homogeneous symmetry, global quotient symmetry and the chosen metric must be distinguished** when discussing <isometries> in dimension three.
