Write $B=\beta$ for the driving <Brownian motion> throughout; the two symbols in the displayed formulas refer to the same process. For $t<1$ the deterministic integrand is square-integrable on $[0,t]$, so
$$
I_t=\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s},\qquad X_t=(1-t)I_t
$$
is well defined. The <Itô product rule>, with zero <quadratic covariation> against the deterministic factor, gives
$$
dX_t=-I_t\,dt+(1-t)\frac{dB_t}{1-t}
=dB_t-\frac{X_t}{1-t}\,dt,\qquad X_0=0.
$$
For the other form, apply the same <Itô product rule> to $B_t/(1-t)$:
$$
\frac{B_t}{1-t}=\int_0^t\frac{dB_s}{1-s}+\int_0^t\frac{B_s}{(1-s)^2}\,ds.
$$
Multiplying by $1-t$ gives the <stochastic integral representation of a Brownian bridge>
$$
\boxed{X_t=B_t-(1-t)\int_0^t\frac{B_s}{(1-s)^2}\,ds},\qquad t<1.
$$
The drift coefficient is singular only at the terminal endpoint, so this calculation is performed on compact subintervals of $[0,1)$.

For times $t_1,\ldots,t_n<1$ and real coefficients $a_i$, the <linear combination> of coordinates is
$$
\sum_i a_iX_{t_i}=\int_0^{\max_i t_i}\left(\sum_i\frac{a_i(1-t_i)\mathbf1_{[0,t_i]}(s)}{1-s}\right)dB_s.
$$
The <Gaussianity of deterministic Brownian stochastic integrals> makes this a centered <normal random variable>. Indeed step-function approximations give <linear combinations> of independent normal increments, and the <Itô isometry> gives convergence in $L^2$; their <characteristic functions> converge to that of a normal variable with the limiting variance. Since every <linear combination> is normal, the coordinate vector is a <Gaussian random vector>. Appending the deterministic coordinate $X_1=0$ preserves that property, so $X$ is a centered <Gaussian process> on $[0,1]$.

For $0\le s\le t<1$, the <covariance> version of the <Itô isometry> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X_sX_t]=(1-s)(1-t)\int_0^s\frac{du}{(1-u)^2}
=(1-s)(1-t)\frac{s}{1-s}=s(1-t).
$$
At $t=1$ the <covariance> is zero because $X_1=0$, agreeing with the formula. Thus the <Brownian bridge covariance kernel> is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(s,t)=\min(s,t)-st,\qquad\mathbb EX_t=0}.
$$
Its finite-dimensional distributions identify it as a <Brownian bridge>; continuity at the terminal endpoint is established next.

The endpoint value printed in this part is erroneous. The correct conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\lim_{t\uparrow1}X_t=0\quad\text{almost surely}}.
$$
Already $\mathbb EX_t^2=t(1-t)\to0$ gives <convergence in probability> to zero and rules out an almost sure limit of one.

To prove the stronger endpoint convergence, use <time reversal of a Brownian bridge>. Set $Y_t=X_{1-t}$, including $Y_0=X_1=0$. Both $X$ and $Y$ are centered <Gaussian processes>, and for $s\le t$,
$$
\mathbb E[Y_sY_t]=(1-t)\bigl(1-(1-s)\bigr)=s(1-t).
$$
They therefore have the same finite-dimensional distributions. The path of $X$ is continuous at zero, since its <stochastic integral> is continuous there. In particular its values at positive rational times tend to zero at zero with probability one. Equality of the laws on the countable rational coordinates transfers this event to $Y$. The path of $Y$ is continuous at every time in $(0,1]$, so its supremum on any interval bounded away from zero is determined by rational times. The rational endpoint condition consequently implies $Y_t\to0$ as $t\downarrow0$ along all real times. Hence $X_t\to0$ as $t\uparrow1$, and setting $X_1=0$ gives a continuous version on the whole closed interval. This proof uses equality of coordinate laws, without assuming terminal continuity in advance.

Use the standard global convention that $\mathcal M_c^2$ consists of <L2-bounded continuous martingales>, here starting at zero. This matters for arbitrary unbounded <stopping times> in part (iv); square-integrability at each separate deterministic time alone is weaker. Such <martingales> have terminal $L^2$ limits and satisfy the <optional sampling theorem> at arbitrary finite <stopping times>. Infinite <stopping times> can also be included by evaluating at the terminal limit. The <weakly orthogonal continuous martingales> condition compares cross-time <expectations>. The following four parts prove its equivalence with the equal-time, bracket-mean and forward-stopping tests.

If $M,N$ are <weakly orthogonal continuous martingales>, taking the two times equal gives $\mathbb E[M_sN_s]=0$. Conversely suppose this equal-time condition holds. For $s\le t$, the <martingale> property and <conditional expectation> give
$$
\mathbb E[M_sN_t]=\mathbb E[M_s\mathbb E(N_t\mid\mathcal F_s)]
=\mathbb E[M_sN_s]=0.
$$
For $t\le s$, condition the other factor at time $t$ instead. Products are integrable by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Thus the equal-time condition implies the full cross-time definition, proving (i) equivalent to (ii).

The <polarization identity> for <quadratic covariation>, together with $\mathbb E[M\pm N]_s=\mathbb E(M_s\pm N_s)^2$, yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M,N]_s=\mathbb E[M_sN_s]}.
$$
The bracket identity for each zero-starting square-integrable <martingale> is the <Itô isometry> for integration of the constant integrand against that <martingale>. Polarizing both identities gives the displayed <covariance identity for continuous square-integrable martingales>. The bracket is integrable: its total variation is at most $\sqrt{[M]_s[N]_s}$ by the <Kunita-Watanabe inequality>, whose <expectation> is finite by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Therefore condition (iii) is equivalent to (ii).

For a <stopping time> $T\ge s$, the <optional sampling theorem> for the <L2-bounded continuous martingale> $M$ gives $\mathbb E[M_T\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s$. Consequently
$$
\mathbb E[M_TN_s]=\mathbb E[M_sN_s].

If $N_s$ is unbounded, first multiply by a bounded truncation and then remove it using $L^2$ bounds and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Thus (ii) implies (iv). Taking $T=s$ proves the converse. For $T=\infty$, the same calculation uses the $L^2$ terminal value.

For a <stopping time> $T\le s$, <optional sampling> for $N$ gives $\mathbb E[N_s\mid\mathcal F_T]=N_T$, hence

\mathbb E[M_TN_s]=\mathbb E[M_TN_T].

The <characterization of a martingale by bounded continuous-time stopped expectations> therefore makes $MN$ a <martingale>. Thus **orthogonality is equivalent to the backward-stopping <expectation> test**. In contrast to <weakly orthogonal continuous martingales>, this tests the product at random times, not only deterministic times.

Take a standard <Brownian motion> $B$ and define
$$
\boxed{M_t=B_{t\wedge1},\qquad N_t=B_{t\wedge1}^2-(t\wedge1)}.
$$
The <Itô formula> gives $N_t=2\int_0^{t\wedge1}B_s\,dB_s$, so both are <continuous martingales> starting at zero. Their second moments are $\mathbb EM_t^2=t\wedge1$ and $\mathbb EN_t^2=2(t\wedge1)^2$, making them <L2-bounded continuous martingales>. At equal times, the odd moments of a centered <normal random variable> vanish, so
$$
\mathbb E[M_tN_t]=\mathbb E[B_{t\wedge1}^3]-(t\wedge1)\mathbb EB_{t\wedge1}=0.
$$
They are consequently <weakly orthogonal continuous martingales> by <a/i/solution>.

Their product, for $t<1$, is $B_t^3-tB_t$. The <Itô formula> gives
$$
d(M_tN_t)=(3B_t^2-t)\,dB_t+2B_t\,dt.
$$
The continuous finite-variation term $2\int_0^tB_s\,ds$ is not identically zero: it has variance $4t^3/3$ at every $t>0$. Uniqueness of the continuous semimartingale decomposition therefore shows that $MN$ is not even a <local martingale>. Equivalently $[M,N]_t=2\int_0^{t\wedge1}B_s\,ds$ is not zero, so the pair is not <orthogonal continuous local martingales>.

For the <integrable terminal bracket criterion>, assume $M_0=0$, or more generally $M_0\in L^2$. This initial-value assumption is necessary if it is not already part of the notation: an integrable but non-square-integrable random constant process has zero <quadratic variation> and still fails square-integrability.

For the zero-starting case, choose a <localizing sequence> $\tau_n$ bounding both $|M|$ and its <quadratic variation>. The stopped processes are true square-integrable <martingales>, and the <Itô isometry> gives
$$
\mathbb E[M_{t\wedge\tau_n}^2]=\mathbb E[M]_{t\wedge\tau_n}\le C:=\mathbb E[M]_\infty<\infty.
$$
For each fixed time these variables are <uniformly integrable> by their bounded second moments. Almost sure convergence as $n\to\infty$ is therefore convergence in $L^1$. Passing to the limit in the stopped conditional-expectation identity gives $\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s$: the <local martingale> is a true <martingale>. The <Fatou lemma> also gives
$$
\boxed{\sup_{t\ge0}\mathbb EM_t^2\le\mathbb E[M]_\infty<\infty},
$$
so $M$ belongs to the global $\mathcal M_c^2$ space. For general square-integrable $M_0$, apply this argument to $M-M_0$ and use $|M_t|^2\le2|M_0|^2+2|M_t-M_0|^2$.

The <existence and pathwise uniqueness theorem for a stochastic differential equation> states that <locally Lipschitz> drift $b$ and diffusion $\sigma$ on an open domain give a pathwise unique <strong solution of a stochastic differential equation> up to a maximal lifetime $\xi$. The initial value is measurable with respect to the initial sigma-algebra, and the filtration satisfies the <usual conditions for a filtration>. On $\mathbb R^d$, if $T_n$ are exits from increasing balls, then $T_n\uparrow\xi$; a finite lifetime entails escape from every compact subset. The coefficients need not satisfy a global growth bound for this local result.

One construction truncates the coefficients outside each compact region to <globally Lipschitz> coefficients, solves the resulting global equations, and patches their solutions until the exits. <Pathwise uniqueness> makes solutions from larger truncations agree on the smaller stopped interval. Their union is the <maximal local solution of a stochastic differential equation>, and any extension would contradict the exhaustion of the domain by the exit times.

= Solution

Use the <Itô formula> up to $T_n$:
$$
X_{t\wedge T_n}^2
=2\int_0^{t\wedge T_n}X_s\sigma(X_s)\,dB_s
+\int_0^{t\wedge T_n}\bigl(2X_sb(X_s)+\sigma^2(X_s)\bigr)ds.
$$
For fixed $n$, the stopped stochastic integrand is bounded on each finite horizon, so its integral has <expectation> zero. The <linear growth condition for an SDE> and $2xb(x)\le x^2+b(x)^2$ give
$$
2xb(x)+\sigma^2(x)\le K+(K+1)x^2.
$$
Writing $u_n(t)=\mathbb EX_{t\wedge T_n}^2$, and bounding the active-time integrand by the stopped square, yields
$$
u_n(t)\le Kt+(K+1)\int_0^tu_n(s)\,ds.
$$
The <Gronwall inequality> gives a bound independent of $n$:
$$
u_n(t)\le\frac{K}{K+1}\bigl(e^{(K+1)t}-1\bigr)=C_t.
$$
On $\{T_n\le t\}$, continuity gives $|X_{t\wedge T_n}|=n$. The <Markov inequality> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(T_n\le t)\le C_t/n^2\longrightarrow0}.
$$
Since $T_n\uparrow\xi$, the event $\{\xi\le t\}$ is the intersection of these decreasing events and has probability zero. Taking integer $t$ proves $\xi=\infty$ almost surely. This proves global existence with locally, rather than globally, Lipschitz coefficients under the stated growth bound.

Put $A_t=[M]_t$. Its continuous, strictly increasing paths start at zero and tend to infinity. Thus the inverse $T_s$ is finite and continuous, and
$$
\{T_s\le u\}=\{A_u\ge s\}\in\mathcal F_u,
$$
so each $T_s$ is a <stopping time>. Set $\mathcal G_s=\mathcal F_{T_s}$ and $\beta_s=M_{T_s}$. This is the inverse-clock construction in the <Dambis-Dubins-Schwarz theorem>.

To verify its <martingale> property, stop $M$ at times $\tau_n$ bounding its path, its bracket and time itself. The stopped $M$ and $M^2-A$ are bounded <martingales>. <Optional sampling> at $T_s\wedge\tau_n$ and $T_t\wedge\tau_n$ shows that the time-changed process, stopped at $A_{\tau_n}$, is a <martingale> and that its square minus the stopped clock $s\wedge A_{\tau_n}$ is a <martingale>. These clock <stopping times> tend to infinity because $A_\infty=\infty$. Therefore $\beta$ is a <continuous local martingale> with $\beta_0=0$ and <quadratic variation> $[\beta]_s=s$. The <Lévy characterization of Brownian motion> identifies it as a $\mathcal G_s$-Brownian motion.

Strict monotonicity and continuity give $A_{T_s}=s$ and $T_{A_t}=t$, so
$$
\boxed{M_t=\beta_{[M]_t}}.
$$
The inverse-clock argument proves the asserted representation; the random clock need not be independent of the <Brownian motion> it produces.

The terminal bracket value printed as zero is incompatible with a strictly increasing bracket starting at zero. If the intended value is infinity, <4/a/solution> applies directly. In fact the substantive conclusion holds for every deterministic continuous bracket $a(t)=[M]_t$, whether its terminal value is finite or infinite, and without strict increase.

For $u\in\mathbb R$, the <Itô formula> makes
$$
Z_t=\exp\bigl(iuM_t+\tfrac12u^2a(t)\bigr)
$$
a complex <local martingale>. Its modulus is the deterministic value $e^{u^2a(t)/2}$, bounded on every finite horizon, so its real and imaginary parts are true <martingales> there. Conditioning between $s$ and $t$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[e^{iu(M_t-M_s)}\mid\mathcal F_s]
=\exp\bigl(-\tfrac12u^2(a(t)-a(s))\bigr)}.
$$
This deterministic <conditional characteristic function> shows that the increment is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ and is normal with mean zero and variance $a(t)-a(s)$. Induction over ordered times gives independent Gaussian increments and hence jointly Gaussian coordinate vectors. Since $a(t)$ is finite at every finite time, the <integrable terminal bracket criterion> applied on each fixed horizon also upgrades $M$ to a true square-integrable <martingale>. This proves the <deterministic quadratic variation characterizes a Gaussian continuous local martingale> result in a form covering the corrected question.

Write the <diffusion process> as $dX_t=b(X_t)dt+\sigma(X_t)dB_t$, with $a=\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf T}$. Its <diffusion generator> is
$$
L\varphi=\frac12\sum_{i,j}a^{ij}\partial_{ij}\varphi+\sum_i b^i\partial_i\varphi.
$$
The <Itô formula> says that $\varphi(X_t)-\varphi(X_0)-\int_0^tL\varphi(X_s)ds$ is a <local martingale>, and is a true <martingale> when the stopped terms are integrable. Taking <expectations> gives <Dynkin formula for a diffusion>. This is the bridge from infinitesimal stochastic dynamics to differential equations.

For the <transition semigroup> $P_tg(x)=\mathbb E_xg(X_t)$, the <Markov property> gives $P_{t+s}=P_tP_s$. On a smooth generator domain where differentiation is valid, differentiating the semigroup identity yields the <Kolmogorov backward equation> $\partial_tP_tg=L(P_tg)$, with initial value $g$. If a transition density $p(t,x,y)$ exists, integration against smooth compactly supported tests and integration by parts give the <Fokker-Planck equation>
$$
\partial_tp=L_y^*p,
\qquad
L^*p=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}(a^{ij}p)-\sum_i\partial_i(b^ip).
$$
The backward equation acts on the starting point, while this forward equation describes the evolving probability density.

Here is a full representation proof with a source and potential. Suppose $X$ is nonexplosive, $V\ge0$ is bounded, $f,g$ are bounded, and $u$ is a bounded <classical solution> on a finite slab of
$$
u_t+Lu-Vu=-f,\qquad u(T,x)=g(x).
$$
For a process started from $x$ at time $t$, put $D_s=\exp(-\int_t^sV(X_r)dr)$. The <Itô product rule> gives
$$
d(D_su(s,X_s))=-D_sf(s,X_s)ds+D_s\nabla u(s,X_s)\sigma(X_s)dB_s.
$$
Therefore $D_su(s,X_s)+\int_t^sD_rf(r,X_r)dr$ is a <local martingale>. It is bounded in absolute value by $\|u\|_\infty+(T-t)\|f\|_\infty$, so it is a true <martingale>, without requiring a global derivative bound. Its endpoint <expectations> give the <parabolic Feynman-Kac formula with a source>
$$
\boxed{u(t,x)=\mathbb E_{t,x}\left[e^{-\int_t^TV(X_r)dr}g(X_T)
+\int_t^Te^{-\int_t^sV(X_r)dr}f(s,X_s)ds\right]}.
$$
This proves uniqueness in the bounded classical class. Setting $V=f=0$ recovers the backward heat or diffusion representation; the minus sign in the killing term produces the negative exponential.

For an elliptic problem, stop at the first exit $\tau$ from a bounded regular domain $D$. Suppose $\mathbb E_x\tau<\infty$, $u\in C^2(D)\cap C(\bar D)$ is bounded and solves $(L-V)u=-f$ with boundary data $g$. The same calculation, stopped at $t\wedge\tau$, gives a <martingale> dominated by $\|u\|_\infty+\|f\|_\infty\tau$. Continuity puts $X_\tau$ on the boundary. Taking the limit and using <dominated convergence> gives the <elliptic Feynman-Kac formula>
$$
\boxed{u(x)=\mathbb E_x\left[e^{-\int_0^\tau V(X_r)dr}g(X_\tau)
+\int_0^\tau e^{-\int_0^sV(X_r)dr}f(X_s)ds\right]}.
$$
For $V=f=0$, harmonic functions for $L$ give stopped <martingales> and are averages of their exit-boundary values. For <Brownian motion> $L=\tfrac12\Delta$, this is the probabilistic solution of the <Dirichlet problem>. Suitable smooth coefficients and uniform ellipticity give familiar settings where the required diffusion, exit-time integrability and <classical solutions> exist. The representation itself follows from the stated regularity and integrability conditions, rather than claiming classical solvability for arbitrary coefficients.

At the unscaled state $(x,y)$, write the <Lévy kernel of a Markov jump process> on jump displacements $z$ as
$$
\boxed{K((x,y),dz)=\lambda x\,\delta_{(1,0)}(dz)
+\frac{\mu xy}{N}\delta_{(-1,0)}(dz)
+\lambda y\,\delta_{(0,1)}(dz)
+\frac{\mu xy}{N}\delta_{(0,-1)}(dz)}.
$$
The destination-kernel convention translates each Dirac mass by $(x,y)$. The total holding rate is the mass of this kernel. At either zero population the corresponding death rates vanish, so no negative states are reached. Births in the total population are bounded by a <Yule process> with per-individual rate $\lambda$, while deaths only decrease it; this also gives nonexplosion.

Put $Z^N=(X/N,Y/N)$. This is a <density-dependent Markov jump process>, with jumps of size $1/N$ and rates of order $N$. Its drift gives the deterministic approximation
$$
\boxed{\dot x=\lambda x-\mu xy,\qquad\dot y=\lambda y-\mu xy,\qquad(x(0),y(0))=(1,1)}.
$$
Uniqueness preserves the diagonal $x=y=z$. For positive $\lambda,\mu$, the resulting <logistic differential equation> has solution
$$
\boxed{z(t)=\frac{\lambda}{\mu+(\lambda-\mu)e^{-\lambda t}}}.
$$
If $\lambda<\mu$, it decreases from one to $\lambda/\mu$; if $\lambda>\mu$, it increases to that same equilibrium; equality gives the constant solution one. In particular positive rates do not give extinction on this exactly symmetric deterministic trajectory. Transverse perturbations behave differently: subtracting the equations gives $(x-y)'=\lambda(x-y)$, so the positive symmetric equilibrium has an unstable difference direction. The <symmetric competition model> distinguishes diagonal logistic relaxation from this symmetry-breaking instability.

\Image[paper-38-competing-populations.png]
{title=Symmetric population relaxation and transverse instability}

For an explicit error estimate, use the <martingale decomposition of a density-dependent jump process>:
$$
Z_t^N=(1,1)+\int_0^tF(Z_s^N)ds+M_t^N,
\qquad F(x,y)=(\lambda x-\mu xy,\lambda y-\mu xy).
$$
Fix $t_0,\delta>0$ and choose $R>\sup_{t\le t_0}z(t)+\delta$. Stop at the first exit $\sigma_R$ from the box $[0,R]^2$. On that box, $F$ has Lipschitz constant $L_R=\lambda+2\mu R$ in the <supremum norm>. Each coordinate of $M^N$ has jumps bounded by $1/N$ and <predictable quadratic variation> at most
$$
\langle(M^{N,i})^{\sigma_R}\rangle_{t_0}\le\frac{C_Rt_0}{N},\qquad C_R=\lambda R+\mu R^2.
$$
Indeed a coordinate's positive and negative jump rates are $N\lambda x$ and $N\mu xy$, and the squared jump size is $1/N^2$. Independent jump clocks give zero cross-bracket.

The <Bernstein bound for bounded-jump martingales> gives, for each coordinate,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\sup_{t\le t_0}|M^{N,i}_{t\wedge\sigma_R}|\ge\eta\right)
\le2\exp\left(-\frac{N\eta^2}{2(C_Rt_0+\eta/3)}\right).
$$
The <Gronwall inequality> applied up to $\sigma_R$ gives
$$
\sup_{t\le t_0\wedge\sigma_R}\|Z_t^N-(z(t),z(t))\|_\infty
\le e^{L_Rt_0}\sup_{t\le t_0}\|M^N_{t\wedge\sigma_R}\|_\infty.
$$
An exit from the box already entails an error larger than $\delta$, by the choice of $R$, so the stopped estimate controls the event of any error on the full interval. Take $\eta=\delta e^{-L_Rt_0}$ and use the <union bound> on the two coordinates:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\sup_{t\le t_0}\|Z_t^N-(z(t),z(t))\|_\infty>\delta\right)
\le\min\left\{1,4\exp\left(-\frac{N\delta^2e^{-2L_Rt_0}}{2(C_Rt_0+\delta e^{-L_Rt_0}/3)}\right)\right\}}.
$$
For fixed time horizon and tolerance this is exponentially small in $N$, proving a <fluid limit>. The transverse instability explains why this finite-horizon estimate should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily long times without changing its constants.
