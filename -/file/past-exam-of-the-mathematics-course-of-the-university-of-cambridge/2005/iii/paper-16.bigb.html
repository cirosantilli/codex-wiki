= Solution

A <fixed point> of the <left shift> satisfies $x_{k+1}=x_k$ for every $k\in\mathbb Z$, so its entire two-sided <sequence> is constant. The constant <sequence> with value $i$ belongs to the <subshift of finite type> precisely when $A_{ii}=1$: this is exactly the requirement that its repeated transition be allowed by the <symbolic transition matrix>. There is one such <fixed point> for each diagonal entry equal to one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\#\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^m A_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}A.}
$$
This also covers an empty <subshift of finite type>, for then no permitted constant <sequence> exists.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $W_n(i,j)$ for the number of <locally admissible words> $(x_0,\ldots,x_n)$ with $x_0=i$ and $x_n=j$. Here local admissibility means $A_{x_rx_{r+1}}=1$ for each consecutive pair; length counts symbols, so there are $n$ transitions. For $n=0$, the single-symbol word gives $W_0(i,j)=\delta_{ij}=(A^0)_{ij}$, and for $n=1$ we have $W_1(i,j)=A_{ij}$.

To append the last symbol $j$, choose the penultimate symbol $k$. Every choice gives a <locally admissible word> exactly when its length-$n$ prefix is locally admissible and $A_{kj}=1$. Different prefixes or penultimate symbols give different words, and every admissible word is obtained once. Therefore
$$
W_{n+1}(i,j)=\sum_{k=1}^m W_n(i,k)A_{kj}.
$$
This is precisely the rule for <matrix multiplication>. <Mathematical induction> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{W_n(i,j)=(A^n)_{ij}.}
$$
Equivalently, expanding the <matrix> entry sums the products $A_{ix_1}A_{x_1x_2}\cdots A_{x_{n-1}j}$ over all intermediate symbols; each product is the <indicator function> of one allowed transition chain.

**The intended meaning of “allowed” here is local admissibility.** If instead it means a word that actually occurs in some two-sided <sequence> in $\Sigma_A$, the assertion needs an additional extension assumption. For example, $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ permits the word $(1,2)$ and has $(A)_{12}=1$, but $\Sigma_A$ is empty because no symbol can continue after $2$. Having a nonzero entry in every row and every column is sufficient: one can successively choose a predecessor and a successor to extend every <locally admissible word> indefinitely in both directions. No such assumption is needed for the local counting formula.

= Solution

The equation $\sigma^n x=x$ says $x_{r+n}=x_r$ for every $r\in\mathbb Z$. Thus a solution is determined uniquely by the block $(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1})$, repeated in both directions. It belongs to the <subshift of finite type> exactly when all transitions inside the block, and the closing transition from $x_{n-1}$ to $x_0$, are allowed. Hence these <fixed points> of the $n$th <iteration of a map> are in <bijection> with the closed transition chains of $n$ steps with a specified symbol at time zero. By the counting argument in part (b), the number starting at $i$ is $(A^n)_{ii}$. Summing over $i$ proves the <trace formula for periodic points of a subshift>:
$$
\boxed{\#\operatorname{Fix}(\sigma^n)=\operatorname{tr}(A^n).}
$$
Every closed transition chain extends by periodic repetition, so the extension issue in part (b) does not affect this formula. We count points, not <periodic orbits>: a least-period-$d$ <periodic orbit> contributes its $d$ distinct points.

**Here period $n$ means fixed by $\sigma^n$, so the least period may divide $n$.** If $P_d$ denotes the number of points of least period exactly $d$, division with remainder shows that a point is fixed by $\sigma^n$ exactly when its least period divides $n$. Consequently
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A^n)=\sum_{d\mid n}P_d,qquad
P_n=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu_{\mathrm M}(n/d)\operatorname{tr}(A^d),
$$
where $\mu_{\mathrm M}$ is the <Möbius function> and the second equality is <Möbius inversion>. For example, $A=(1)$ has one point fixed by every power but no point of least period two; interpreting the printed formula as an exact-period count would therefore be false.

The convention intended by this question is **existence of a dense forward orbit**, also called <point transitivity>. On a nonempty <compact metric space>, this means that some $x\in X$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\overline{\{f^n(x):n\geq0\}}=X.}
$$
Equivalently, that forward <orbit> visits every nonempty <open set>.

The other common definition of <topological transitivity> uses intersections of iterated <open sets>, as in part (b). The distinction matters on spaces with <isolated points>: parts (b) and (c) ask precisely how the two conventions are related. We use <point transitivity> for the dense-orbit convention throughout this solution. As usual for this definition, the <state space> is assumed nonempty; on an empty space the open-set condition is vacuous and there is no point with a forward <orbit>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <compact metric space> is <second countable>. To see this concretely, for each positive integer $r$ choose a finite cover by balls of radius $1/r$. Their centres, over all $r$, form a countable <dense subset>; balls centred there with positive rational radii form a countable <basis of a topology>. Enumerate its nonempty members as $(V_j)_{j\geq1}$, repeating members if the basis is finite.

For each $j$, put
$$
G_j=\bigcup_{n\geq1}f^{-n}(V_j).
$$
Each $G_j$ is an <open set>, because all <iterations of a map> $f^n$ are <continuous>. It is <dense>: if $U$ is any nonempty <open set>, the hypothesis supplies $n\geq1$ and $u\in U$ with $f^n(u)\in V_j$, so $u\in U\cap G_j$.

A <compact metric space> is a <complete metric space>, hence a <Baire space>. The <Baire category theorem> implies that $G=\bigcap_{j\geq1}G_j$ is <dense>, and in particular nonempty. For any $x\in G$, the forward <orbit> of $x$ meets every $V_j$, and therefore every nonempty <open set>. Its <orbit closure> is $X$. Thus **the open-set intersection hypothesis implies point transitivity**, and in fact the set of points with a dense forward <orbit> contains the dense intersection $G$.

= Solution

Let $x$ have a dense forward <orbit> $O=\{f^j(x):j\geq0\}$, and suppose that $f$ is <surjective>. Every tail $O_k=\{f^j(x):j\geq k\}$ is then <dense>. Indeed, <continuity> gives
$$
f^k(\overline O)\subseteq\overline{f^k(O)}=\overline{O_k},
$$
and the left side equals $f^k(X)=X$ by <surjectivity>.

Given nonempty <open sets> $U,V$, choose $k\geq0$ with $f^k(x)\in U$. The dense tail $O_{k+1}$ supplies $j\geq k+1$ with $f^j(x)\in V$. Taking $n=j-k\geq1$ gives $f^j(x)\in f^n(U)\cap V$. This proves the required positive-time intersection property.

**The result can fail without <surjectivity>.** Take the <compact metric space>
$$
X=\{0\}\cup\{1/j:j\geq1\},qquad
f(0)=0,qquad f(1/j)=1/(j+1),
$$
with the <metric> induced from the <real line>. All nonzero points are <isolated>, so <continuity> there is automatic; at zero, $f(1/j)\to0=f(0)$ proves <continuity>. The forward <orbit> of $1$ is $\{1,1/2,1/3,\ldots\}$, whose <closure> is $X$, so this is <point transitivity>. But $f(X)=X\setminus\{1\}$. The singletons $U=\{1/2\}$ and $V=\{1\}$ are nonempty <open sets>, and for every $n\geq1$,
$$
f^n(U)=\{1/(n+2)\},qquad f^n(U)\cap V=\varnothing.
$$
The isolated initial point can be visited only at the start of the dense forward <orbit>; deleting that initial segment destroys density.

Use the separated-set definition of <topological entropy>. For a <compatible metric> $d$ and $n\geq1$, define the <Bowen metric> and separated-set count by
$$
d_n(x,y)=\max_{0\leq k<n}d(f^kx,f^ky),\qquad
s_n(d,\epsilon)=\max\{|E|:d_n(x,y)\geq\epsilon\text{ for distinct }x,y\in E\}.
$$
For fixed $n$, the <metric> $d_n$ generates the original <topology>: it includes the $k=0$ term and all finitely many <iterations of a map> are <continuous>. Thus its space is compact and $s_n(d,\epsilon)$ is finite. With natural logarithms, set
$$
h_d(f,\epsilon)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log s_n(d,\epsilon)}n,qquad
h_d(f)=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}h_d(f,\epsilon).
$$
The last limit exists, possibly with value infinity, because decreasing $\epsilon$ can only increase separated-set counts.

Let $d'$ be another <compatible metric>. The identity $(X,d)\to(X,d')$ is a <continuous function> on a compact domain, hence <uniformly continuous>. For every $\epsilon>0$, choose $\delta>0$ such that $d(x,y)<\delta$ implies $d'(x,y)<\epsilon$. The same implication at every iterate gives
$$
d_n(x,y)<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ d'_n(x,y)<\epsilon.
$$
By its contrapositive, every $\epsilon$-<separated set> for $d'_n$ is $\delta$-separated for $d_n$. Consequently $s_n(d',\epsilon)\leq s_n(d,\delta)$ for every $n$, and
$$
h_{d'}(f,\epsilon)\leq h_d(f,\delta)\leq h_d(f).
$$
Taking $\epsilon\downarrow0$ gives $h_{d'}(f)\leq h_d(f)$. Interchanging the two <compatible metrics> gives the reverse inequality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{h_{d'}(f)=h_d(f)=h_{\mathrm{top}}(f).}
$$
<Compactness> is used for <uniform continuity>, not merely for pointwise equivalence of the two <metrics>.

Let $H:X\to Y$ be a <homeomorphism> with $H\circ f=g\circ H$. This is <topological conjugacy>. Choose a <compatible metric> $e$ on $Y$ and pull it back to $X$:
$$
d_H(x,x')=e(Hx,Hx').
$$
Because $H$ is a <homeomorphism>, $d_H$ is a <compatible metric>. The conjugacy identity implies $Hf^k=g^kH$ for every $k\geq0$, so the corresponding <Bowen metrics> satisfy
$$
(d_H)_n(x,x')=\max_{0\leq k<n}e(g^kHx,g^kHx')=e_n(Hx,Hx').
$$
The <bijection> $H$ therefore transports <separated sets> in either direction without changing their separation, and $s_n(d_H,\epsilon)=s_n(e,\epsilon)$. Their exponential growth rates, and then their limits as $\epsilon\downarrow0$, agree. Part (a) lets us replace $d_H$ by any <compatible metric> on $X$. Hence
$$
\boxed{h_{\mathrm{top}}(f)=h_{\mathrm{top}}(g).}
$$

Write the <hyperbolic toral automorphism> as $T_A([v])=[Av]$ on $\mathbb T^2=\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2$, where $A\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb Z)$. Its <determinant> is $\pm1$. The <eigenvalues> cannot be a nonreal conjugate pair: their product would have <absolute value> one, forcing each to have <absolute value> one, contrary to hyperbolicity. The same product condition and hyperbolicity imply two distinct real <eigenvalues>, labelled so that
$$
0<|\lambda_s|<1<|\lambda_u|,qquad |\lambda_s\lambda_u|=1.
$$
We will prove
$$
\boxed{h_{\mathrm{top}}(T_A)=\log|\lambda_u|.}
$$
This is the <entropy of a hyperbolic toral automorphism>; the <absolute value> is important when the expanding <eigenvalue> is negative.

Choose real <eigenvectors> $v_s,v_u$ and the adapted <norm> $\|a v_s+b v_u\|_*=\max(|a|,|b|)$. Its quotient <metric> on the <torus> is
$$
d([v],[w])=\min_{\ell\in\mathbb Z^2}\|v-w-\ell\|_*.
$$
It is a <compatible metric>, so part (a) allows its use. Let $\rho=\min_{0\ne\ell\in\mathbb Z^2}\|\ell\|_*>0$ and $D=\|A\|_*=|\lambda_u|$. Fix $\epsilon>0$ small enough that $2\epsilon<\rho$ and $(D+1)\epsilon<\rho$.

If $[v]$ lies in the <Bowen ball> $B_n(0,\epsilon)$, each $T_A^k[v]$ has a unique lift $z_k$ with $\|z_k\|_*<\epsilon$. Uniqueness follows because two such lifts differ by a lattice vector of <norm> less than $2\epsilon<\rho$. Moreover, $z_{k+1}-Az_k$ is a lattice vector, and
$$
\|z_{k+1}-Az_k\|_*<(D+1)\epsilon<\rho.
$$
It must be zero. Thus $z_k=A^kz_0$: there is no hidden lattice jump between successive lifted iterates.

Write $z_0=a v_s+b v_u$. The simultaneous bounds $\|A^kz_0\|_*<\epsilon$ for $0\leq k<n$ are exactly
$$
|a|<\epsilon,qquad |b|<\epsilon|\lambda_u|^{-(n-1)}.
$$
The stable coordinate is largest at time zero and the unstable coordinate at time $n-1$. Conversely, every vector in this rectangle satisfies all the <Bowen ball> bounds. The quotient is injective on this rectangle by $2\epsilon<\rho$. If $J=|\det(v_s,v_u)|$, its normalized <Haar measure> is therefore
$$
\nu(B_n(0,\epsilon))=4J\epsilon^2|\lambda_u|^{-(n-1)}.
$$
The quotient <metric> is translation invariant and $T_A$ is linear, so $d_n(x,y)=d_n(0,y-x)$; every <Bowen ball> has this same <measure>, independently of its centre.

Take an $\epsilon$-<separated set> of largest cardinality $s_n(d,\epsilon)$. It is maximal under inclusion, so its radius-$\epsilon$ <Bowen balls> cover the <torus>: a point outside all these balls could be added. The covering bound gives
$$
s_n(d,\epsilon)\geq\frac1{4J\epsilon^2}|\lambda_u|^{n-1}.
$$
Its radius-$\epsilon/2$ <Bowen balls> are disjoint, by the <triangle inequality> for $d_n$. Their measures sum to at most one, giving
$$
s_n(d,\epsilon)\leq\frac1{J\epsilon^2}|\lambda_u|^{n-1}.
$$
Taking logarithms, dividing by $n$, and letting $n\to\infty$ yields $h_d(T_A,\epsilon)=\log|\lambda_u|$ for every sufficiently small $\epsilon$. The limit as $\epsilon\downarrow0$ proves the answer. Contraction in the stable direction contributes no exponential growth; only expansion in the unstable direction does.

Let $(X,\mathcal B,\mu)$ be a <probability space>, let $T$ be a <measure-preserving transformation>, and let $g\in L^1(\mu)$. The <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> asserts that
$$
A_ng(x)=\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(T^kx)
\longrightarrow g^*(x)=\mathbb E_\mu[g\mid\mathcal I](x)
$$
for almost every $x$, where $\mathcal I=\{B\in\mathcal B:\mu(T^{-1}B\mathbin{\triangle}B)=0\}$ is the <invariant sigma-algebra>. The limit is integrable, satisfies $g^*\circ T=g^*$ almost everywhere, and has $\int g^*\,d\mu=\int g\,d\mu$. On a <probability space> the convergence also holds in $L^1$. No invertibility of $T$ is required.

If $T$ is <ergodic>, its <invariant sigma-algebra> is trivial modulo null sets, so its <conditional expectation> is constant. In that case the concise form is
$$
\boxed{A_ng(x)\longrightarrow\int_Xg\,d\mu\quad\text{for almost every }x.}
$$

= Solution

Identify the <circle group> with $[0,1)$ modulo its endpoints, and let $\lambda$ be normalized <Lebesgue measure>. We first check invariance. On the $r$th inverse branch, $x=(y+r)/m$ for $0\leq r<m$. For an integrable <function> $g$, substitution gives
$$
\int_0^1g(E_mx)\,dx
=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\frac1m\int_0^1g(y)\,dy
=\int_0^1g(y)\,dy.
$$
In particular, testing <indicator functions> shows that the <integer multiplication map on the circle> preserves $\lambda$.

To prove <ergodicity>, let $B$ be a measurable set with $\lambda(E_m^{-1}B\mathbin{\triangle}B)=0$ and put $h=\mathbf1_B$. Then $h(E_mx)=h(x)$ almost everywhere. Its <Fourier coefficients> are
$$
c_k=\int_0^1h(x)e^{-2\pi ikx}\,dx\qquad(k\in\mathbb Z).
$$
Using invariance of $h$ and substituting separately on the same $m$ branches gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_k
&=\int_0^1h(E_mx)e^{-2\pi ikx}\,dx\\
&=\frac1m\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}e^{-2\pi ikr/m}
  \int_0^1h(y)e^{-2\pi i(k/m)y}\,dy\\
&=\begin{cases}0,&m\nmid k,\\c_{k/m},&m\mid k.\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
In the last line, the finite <geometric series> of $m$th <roots of unity> is zero unless $m$ divides $k$, when it equals $m$. For any nonzero integer $k$, divide repeatedly by $m$ until the resulting integer is no longer divisible by $m$. The recurrence then implies $c_k=0$.

Since the <Fourier basis> is a complete <orthonormal basis> of $L^2([0,1),\lambda)$, the absence of every nonconstant <Fourier coefficient> implies $h=c_0=\lambda(B)$ almost everywhere. An <indicator function> takes only the values zero and one, so $\lambda(B)$ must be zero or one. This proves
$$
\boxed{E_m\text{ is ergodic for Lebesgue measure for every integer }m\geq2.}
$$

Outside the <countable set> of dyadic rational numbers, the <binary expansion> $x=\sum_{j\geq1}\epsilon_j(x)2^{-j}$ is unique. Multiplication by two modulo one deletes the first binary digit, so
$$
\epsilon_j(x)=\mathbf1_{[1/2,1)}(E_2^{j-1}x).
$$
Let $g=\mathbf1_{[1/2,1)}$. By part (b), the <doubling map> is a <measure-preserving transformation> and is <ergodic> for <Lebesgue measure>. Applying the <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> to this <indicator function> gives
$$
\frac1n\#\{1\leq j\leq n:\epsilon_j(x)=1\}
=\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(E_2^kx)
\longrightarrow\int_0^1g(x)\,dx=\frac12
$$
for almost every $x$. The dyadic rationals have <Lebesgue measure> zero, so the choice of their two possible <binary expansions> does not affect the conclusion. Thus **the limiting frequency of the digit one is $1/2$ almost everywhere**.

= Solution

We first prove invariance, before using any assertion that requires it. Write $A_n\varphi(x)=n^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\varphi(f^kx)$. Choose a point whose forward <orbit> has the prescribed convergence against every <continuous function>; the hypothesis gives a set of such points of full <measure>. For each <continuous function> $\varphi$, telescoping gives
$$
A_n(\varphi\circ f)(x)-A_n\varphi(x)
=\frac{\varphi(f^nx)-\varphi(x)}n\longrightarrow0,
$$
because a <continuous function> on a <compact metric space> is bounded. Both test functions are continuous, so their averages converge to their prescribed integrals. Hence
$$
\int_X\varphi\circ f\,d\mu=\int_X\varphi\,d\mu
$$
for every <continuous function> $\varphi$. The continuous-test criterion for an <invariant measure> therefore gives **$f_*\mu=\mu$**. The points in the hypothesis can now be called <generic points for an invariant measure>.

For <ergodicity>, let $B$ be a <Borel set> invariant modulo null sets, and set $h=\mathbf1_B$, $p=\mu(B)$. Then $h\circ f=h$ almost everywhere. Since invariance preserves null preimages, deleting the union of all iterated preimages of the exceptional <null set> gives $h(f^kx)=h(x)$ for every $k\geq0$ on one full-measure set. Thus $A_nh=h$ almost everywhere for every $n$.

Given $\epsilon>0$, the <continuous approximation of Borel indicators on a compact metric space>, proved in the supporting steps below, supplies $\varphi\in C(X)$ with $0\leq\varphi\leq1$ and $\|h-\varphi\|_{L^1(\mu)}<\epsilon$. By the hypothesis, $A_n\varphi\to\int\varphi\,d\mu$ almost everywhere. Since $0\leq A_n\varphi\leq1$, the <dominated convergence theorem> also gives convergence in $L^1(\mu)$. Invariance and the <triangle inequality> give the uniform estimate
$$
\|A_n(h-\varphi)\|_{L^1(\mu)}
\leq\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_X|h-\varphi|\circ f^k\,d\mu
=\|h-\varphi\|_{L^1(\mu)}<\epsilon.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|h-p\|_{L^1(\mu)}
&\leq\|A_nh-A_n\varphi\|_{L^1(\mu)}
 +\left\|A_n\varphi-\int\varphi\,d\mu\right\|_{L^1(\mu)}
 +\left|\int\varphi\,d\mu-p\right|\\
&<2\epsilon+\left\|A_n\varphi-\int\varphi\,d\mu\right\|_{L^1(\mu)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Letting $n\to\infty$ and then $\epsilon\downarrow0$ proves $\|h-p\|_1=0$. Explicitly,
$$
\|\mathbf1_B-p\|_1=p(1-p)+(1-p)p=2p(1-p),
$$
so $p\in\{0,1\}$. Every invariant <Borel set> has <measure> zero or one, which proves
$$
\boxed{\mu\text{ is invariant and ergodic}.}
$$
This proof controls measurable-set averages through a uniform $L^1$ estimate; convergence only against continuous test functions is not silently assumed to include <indicator functions>.

Here is an alternative route for the ergodicity step using the <Birkhoff ergodic theorem>. After invariance has been proved, the theorem identifies the almost-everywhere limit of $A_n\varphi$ with $\mathbb E_\mu[\varphi\mid\mathcal I]$. The hypothesis therefore gives
$$
\mathbb E_\mu[\varphi\mid\mathcal I]=\int\varphi\,d\mu
$$
for every <continuous function> $\varphi$. For an invariant <Borel set> $B$, approximate $h=\mathbf1_B$ in $L^1$ by such a $\varphi$. Since $h$ is measurable with respect to the <invariant sigma-algebra>, its <conditional expectation> is $h$ itself. The <L1 contraction of conditional expectation> gives
$$
\left\|h-\mu(B)\right\|_1
\leq\left\|\mathbb E_\mu[h-\varphi\mid\mathcal I]\right\|_1
 +\left|\int(\varphi-h)\,d\mu\right|
\leq2\|h-\varphi\|_1.
$$
Arbitrarily accurate continuous approximation forces $h=\mu(B)$ almost everywhere. Thus this use of the theorem reaches the same zero-or-one criterion as the direct averaging proof above, without assuming in advance that $\mu$ is <ergodic>.

For a <Borel set> $B$ and $\epsilon>0$, <inner regularity> supplies a <compact set> $K\subseteq B$ with $\mu(B)-\mu(K)<\epsilon/2$. Independently, <outer regularity> supplies an <open set> $U\supseteq B$ with $\mu(U)-\mu(B)<\epsilon/2$. Because $K\subseteq U$ and the <measure> is finite,
$$
\mu(U\setminus K)=\mu(U)-\mu(K)<\epsilon.
$$
This creates an arbitrarily small measurable transition region between a compact inner approximation and an open outer approximation. The cutoff in the next step then approximates $\mathbf1_B$, rather than merely approximating the number $\mu(B)$.

= Solution

For the <compact set> $K\subseteq B\subseteq U$ chosen in the preceding step, the <Urysohn lemma> gives a <continuous function> $0\leq\varphi\leq1$ equal to one on $K$ and zero on $X\setminus U$. In a <metric space> this cutoff can also be written explicitly, when $K$ and $X\setminus U$ are both nonempty:
$$
\varphi(x)=\frac{d(x,X\setminus U)}{d(x,K)+d(x,X\setminus U)}.
$$
The two sets are disjoint and closed, so the denominator never vanishes, and their distance functions are <continuous>. If $K$ is empty, take $\varphi=0$; if $U=X$ and $K$ is nonempty, take $\varphi=1$. These choices satisfy the required boundary conditions in the exceptional cases.

The difference $\varphi-\mathbf1_B$ vanishes on $K$ and outside $U$, and its <absolute value> is at most one everywhere. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|\varphi-\mathbf1_B\|_{L^1(\mu)}\leq\mu(U\setminus K)<\epsilon.}
$$
This is the precise continuous approximation required by both proofs of <ergodicity>, and also gives $|\int\varphi\,d\mu-\mu(B)|<\epsilon$.
