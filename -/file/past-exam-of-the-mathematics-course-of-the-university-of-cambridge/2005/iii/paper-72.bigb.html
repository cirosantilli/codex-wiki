= Solution

Both models obtain their density by integrating a <galactic distribution function> over <velocity>. The planar model integrates one vertical <velocity>, whereas the spherical model integrates all three components. In each case the resulting density must also generate its own <gravitational potential> through the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>. The two printed numerical subparts are treated separately below.

= 1
{parent=Paper 72/1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Gaussian integral> over the full vertical <velocity> line is $\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_z$. Thus the <mass density> of the <self-gravitating isothermal slab> is
$$
\rho(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,dv_z=\rho_0e^{-\Phi(z)/\sigma_z^2}.
$$
Neglecting radial derivatives makes the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> $\Phi_{zz}=4\pi G\rho(z)$. Substituting $\Phi=\sigma_z^2\phi$ and $z=z_0\zeta$, with $z_0^2=\sigma_z^2/(8\pi G\rho_0)$, gives
$$
\boxed{2\phi''=e^{-\phi}.}
$$
Multiply by $\phi'$ and integrate. The central value and <symmetry> under $z\mapsto-z$ fix the <constant of integration>:
$$
(\phi')^2=1-e^{-\phi}.
$$
On the positive-$\zeta$ side, <Newtonian gravity> makes $\phi'\geq0$. Put $y=e^{\phi/2}$; then $4(y')^2=y^2-1$ with $y(0)=1$. Integration gives $2\operatorname{arcosh}y=\zeta$, and even reflection supplies the other side. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\phi(\zeta)=2\log\cosh(\zeta/2),\qquad \rho(z)=\rho_0\operatorname{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{z}{2z_0}\right).}
$$
Direct <differentiation> verifies both the equation and central conditions. Uniqueness of the smooth <initial value problem> rules out other branches meeting those conditions. The <surface density> is $4\rho_0z_0$, and the conventional slab <scale height> is $2z_0$; the factor of two is only a definition of the length scale.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Use the <relative potential> $\Psi=\Phi_*-\Phi$ and integrate the stated, untruncated <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> over $\mathbb R^3$. The normalization cancels its three <Gaussian integrals>:
$$
\boxed{\rho(r)=\rho_1e^{\Psi(r)/\sigma^2}.}
$$
The <gravitational potential> sign reverses under the relative-potential convention, so the spherical <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d\Psi}{dr}\right)=-4\pi G\rho_1e^{\Psi/\sigma^2}.}
$$
Also $\Psi'=-Gm(r)/r^2$, with $m'=4\pi r^2\rho$. Differentiating the density relation yields
$$
\sigma^2\rho'=-\rho\frac{Gm(r)}{r^2}.
$$
An <ideal gas> with constant <temperature> has $P=\rho k_BT/m_0$. Its <hydrostatic equilibrium> equation is precisely the same when
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2=\frac{k_BT}{m_0}.}
$$
Together with the same enclosed-mass equation, central density and <boundary conditions>, this proves the <isothermal stellar and gas density equivalence>. In dimensionless terms, $y=-[\Psi-\Psi(0)]/\sigma^2$ and $x=r\sqrt{4\pi G\rho(0)}/\sigma$ obey $x^{-2}(x^2y')'=e^{-y}$ with $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ in both systems.

This calculation uses the distribution as printed for all <velocities>. Imposing an additional cutoff at positive <relative energy> changes the integral to $\rho_1[e^W\operatorname{erf}\sqrt W-2\sqrt W/\sqrt\pi]$, where $W=\Psi/\sigma^2\geq0$, and no longer gives an exactly isothermal gas profile. An untruncated self-gravitating isothermal sphere also needs an outer modification for finite total <mass>. The function $\operatorname{erf}$ here is the <error function>. Finally, the half-line <Gaussian integral> printed in the note lacks a factor: the correct value is **$\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx=\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$**.

= Solution

Take $V=|\mathbf V|$ to be the test body's instantaneous <speed> relative to the nonstreaming background, rather than an ensemble <root mean square> <speed>. Use a local isotropic <galactic distribution function> $f(v)$ normalized as a number distribution, and assume independent encounters and a large, approximately constant <Coulomb logarithm in stellar dynamics>.

For a background particle with relative incoming <velocity> $\mathbf w=\mathbf V-\mathbf v$, a <gravitational scattering angle> $\chi$ obeys
$$
\tan(\chi/2)=\frac{G(M+m)}{bw^2}=\frac{b_{90}}b.
$$
In the <centre-of-momentum frame>, the test body's share of the relative <velocity> is $m/(M+m)$. The azimuthal average cancels transverse changes, leaving a change parallel to the incoming relative <velocity>,
$$
\Delta\mathbf V_\parallel=-\frac{m}{M+m}(1-\cos\chi)\mathbf w=-\frac{2m}{M+m}\frac{\mathbf w}{1+b^2/b_{90}^2}.
$$
The number of encounters per unit time in $d^3v$ and impact interval $db$ is $f(v)d^3v\,w\,2\pi b\,db$. Integrating the longitudinal change from $b=0$ to $b_m$ gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf V}=-2\pi G^2m(M+m)\int f(v)\frac{\mathbf w}{w^3}\log\!\left(1+\frac{b_m^2w^4}{G^2(M+m)^2}\right)d^3v.
$$
At leading logarithmic order replace the logarithm by $2\ln\Lambda$, evaluated at a representative <speed>. For isotropic $f$, the <velocity-shell identity for dynamical friction> is
$$
\int f(v)\frac{\mathbf V-\mathbf v}{|\mathbf V-\mathbf v|^3}\,d^3v=\frac{4\pi\mathbf V}{V^3}\int_0^Vf(v)v^2\,dv.
$$
One proof takes the divergence with respect to $\mathbf V$: the inverse-square <vector field> has divergence $4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf V-\mathbf v)$, whose velocity integral is $4\pi f(V)$, and <rotational symmetry> plus the <divergence theorem> determines the radial field. Exterior <velocity> shells give zero field. Consequently the systematic <Chandrasekhar dynamical friction> is antiparallel to motion, with
$$
\dot V=-\frac{16\pi^2G^2m(M+m)\ln\Lambda}{V^2}\int_0^Vf(v)v^2\,dv.
$$
In the massive-test-body approximation $M\gg m$, this becomes
$$
\boxed{\dot V=-\frac{16\pi^2G^2mM\ln\Lambda}{V^2}\int_0^Vf(v)v^2\,dv,\qquad \Lambda=\frac{b_mV^2}{GM}.}
$$
The <finite-test-mass dynamical friction coefficient> shows the needed qualification: $M>m$ alone does not make $M+m=M$. The quoted expression also neglects diffusion and the <velocity> dependence of the logarithmic cutoff. For the <lowered Maxwellian velocity distribution>, set $f=0$ for $v>v_e$; continuing the printed subtraction beyond escape would give a negative distribution. If $V>v_e$, the integral stops at $v_e$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The stated relation follows for an isolated uniform sphere ending at $r_0$, with the <gravitational potential> zero at infinity. Uniform density in a core alone does not fix the <escape velocity>: exterior shells add a constant interior <gravitational potential> while leaving the interior force unchanged.

Under the isolated-sphere assumption, the enclosed <mass> and <circular speed> are
$$
m(r)=\frac{4\pi}3\rho_0r^3,\qquad V_c^2(r)=\frac{Gm(r)}r=\frac{4\pi G\rho_0}3r^2.
$$
The boundary value is $\Phi(r_0)=-Gm(r_0)/r_0$. Integrating $\Phi'=Gm(r)/r^2$ inwards gives
$$
\Phi(r)=-\frac{2\pi G\rho_0}3(3r_0^2-r^2),\qquad v_e^2(r)=-2\Phi(r).
$$
Thus the <escape and circular speeds inside an isolated uniform sphere> satisfy
$$
\boxed{v_e^2(r)=V_c^2(r)\left(\frac{3r_0^2}{r^2}-1\right),\qquad 0<r\leq r_0.}
$$
The finite central limit is $v_e^2(0)=4\pi G\rho_0r_0^2$, although $V_c(0)=0$. Adding an exterior spherical shell of <mass> $m_s$ at radius $s>r_0$ raises $v_e^2$ by $2Gm_s/s$ and is a direct counterexample to deducing the boxed relation from a uniform core alone.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Inside the uniform sphere, $V_c^2=3\sigma^2r^2/r_0^2$. Thus, with $X=V_c/(\sqrt2\sigma)$, part (b) gives
$$
\frac{v_e^2}{2\sigma^2}=\frac92-X^2,\qquad 0\leq X\leq\sqrt{3/2}.
$$
The <Gaussian integral> needed for the <lowered Maxwellian velocity distribution> is
$$
\int_0^X y^2e^{-y^2}\,dy=\frac{\sqrt\pi}4\operatorname{erf}X-\frac X2e^{-X^2}.
$$
Substitution $v=\sqrt2\sigma y$ therefore yields
$$
\int_0^{V_c}f(v)v^2\,dv=n_0I(X),\qquad I(X)=\frac1{4\pi}\left[\operatorname{erf}X-\frac{2X}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-X^2}-\frac{4X^3}{3\sqrt\pi}e^{X^2-9/2}\right].
$$
Since $V_c<v_e$ throughout the core, the integrand is positive and so is $I(X)$ for $X>0$. Using $mn_0=\rho_0$, part (a)'s massive-test-body drag magnitude is
$$
D=-\dot V\big|_{\rm drag}=\frac{16\pi^2G^2M\rho_0\ln\Lambda}{V_c^2}I(X).
$$
The slow drift of a <circular orbit> is determined by <torque>, not by equating its changing <circular speed> to the tangential drag. Here $V_c=\omega r$, with constant $\omega=\sqrt{4\pi G\rho_0/3}$. The <specific angular momentum> $j=rV_c=\omega r^2$ obeys
$$
\dot j=2V_c\dot r=-rD,\qquad \dot r=-\frac{rD}{2V_c}<0.
$$
Hence the positive local decay timescale is
$$
\boxed{\tau_+\equiv\frac{r}{2|\dot r|}=\frac{V_c}{D}=\frac{V_c^3}{16\pi^2G^2M\rho_0\ln\Lambda\,I(X)}.}
$$
There is a genuine factor-of-two discrepancy with the requested denominator $32\pi^2$. That denominator would give $V_c/(2D)$, which is the timescale for a flat <galaxy rotation curve>, not this uniform-density core. It also follows from the incorrect replacement $\dot V_c=-D$, which drops the $V_c\dot r$ term in $d(rV_c)/dt$. The literal signed definition $r/(2\dot r)$ is negative for an inward drift; it equals $-\tau_+$. A positive decay time requires the absolute value or a minus sign.

This is the <slow circular inspiral under tangential drag> calculation. Its assumptions require $D$ small enough for nearly circular adjustment and a meaningful large <Coulomb logarithm in stellar dynamics>; the leading-log approximation is not uniform at $V_c\to0$. Also, integrating the truncated distribution all the way to $v_e$ does not give exactly $n_0$. The model uses $n_0$ as the stated core normalization; exact self-consistency of a sharply truncated uniform core with that distribution is not implied.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Interpret the barred <velocities> as <second moments> of a steady nonstreaming <collisionless stellar system>, and write $\sigma_r^2=\overline{v_r^2}$. In an isotropic model the three component dispersions agree, so the <spherical Jeans equation> becomes
$$
\frac{d(\rho\sigma_r^2)}{dr}=-\frac{\rho GM(r)}{r^2}.
$$
Put $A=\rho_0r_0^k$, so $\rho=Ar^{-k}$. If this density supplies all of the <Newtonian gravity> and there is no central point <mass>, finite enclosed <mass> requires $k<3$ and
$$
M(r)=\frac{4\pi A}{3-k}r^{3-k}.
$$
Integrate to an outer radius $b$ at which the pressure is $P_b=\rho(b)\sigma_r^2(b)$. The exact Jeans-moment solution within the power-law region is
$$
\rho\sigma_r^2=P_b+\frac{4\pi GA^2}{3-k}\int_r^b s^{1-2k}\,ds,
$$
and, for $k\ne1$,
$$
\boxed{\sigma_r^2(r)=\frac{P_b}{A}r^k+\frac{2\pi GA}{(3-k)(1-k)}r^k\left(b^{2-2k}-r^{2-2k}\right).}
$$
For $k<1$ and $r\ll b$, the integral is dominated by outer radii. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sigma_r^2\sim Cr^k,\quad C=\frac{P_b}{A}+\frac{2\pi GA}{(3-k)(1-k)}b^{2-2k}.}
$$
The coefficient cannot be determined from the local density cusp alone. Extending the power law to infinity gives a divergent pressure integral when $k<1$, so an outer scale or boundary condition is essential. For the usual shallow cusp $0<k<1$, the central <velocity dispersion> tends to zero even though the density diverges.

For $1<k<3$, inner radii dominate as $r/b\to0$; equivalently one can send $b\to\infty$ with vanishing boundary pressure to obtain
$$
\boxed{\sigma_r^2\sim\frac{2\pi G\rho_0r_0^k}{(3-k)(k-1)}r^{2-k}=\frac{V_c^2(r)}{2(k-1)}.}
$$
Thus the central <velocity dispersion> decreases for $1<k<2$, is constant at $k=2$, and diverges for $2<k<3$. At the omitted threshold $k=1$, $\sigma_r^2=(P_b/A)r+2\pi GA r\ln(b/r)$. For $k\geq3$ the assumed central density law has infinite enclosed <mass>, so the same unmodified model cannot determine a finite <velocity dispersion>. These boundary and domain restrictions are the <boundary dependence of an isotropic self-gravitating cusp>; specifying density without them does not specify a physical finite <galaxy>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $p(r)=\rho(r)\sigma_r^2(r)$ and use the <velocity-anisotropy parameter> $\beta=1-\sigma_\theta^2/\sigma_r^2$, with $\sigma_\theta^2=\sigma_\phi^2$. Here the tangential variance is per component; a definition using the sum of both tangential variances instead has an extra factor of two. The original PDF has this correct per-component ratio, which the TeX corrupts.

At a point along the <line of sight>, $r^2=R^2+z^2$. The radial direction makes an angle with the sightline satisfying $\sin^2\chi=R^2/r^2$. <Spherical symmetry> eliminates mixed moments, and projection gives
$$
\overline{v_{\rm los}^2}=\sigma_r^2\cos^2\chi+\sigma_\theta^2\sin^2\chi=\sigma_r^2\left(1-\beta\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right).
$$
Using both sides of the <galaxy> and $dz=r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2-R^2}$,
$$
I(R)\sigma_p^2(R)=2\int_R^\infty\left(1-\beta\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)p(r)\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-R^2}}.
$$
For this formula use $I$ as the projected density of the same tracer represented by $\rho$. If $\rho$ is a <mass density> and $I$ an observed <surface brightness>, a constant <mass-to-light ratio> rescales $I$ by that factor; otherwise replace $\rho$ by the <luminosity density> throughout the tracer equation. The gravitating $M(r)$ remains the total <mass>, not necessarily the tracer's <mass>.

The <spherical Jeans equation> is $p'+2\beta p/r=-\rho GM/r^2$. Eliminate $\beta p=-r[p'+\rho GM/r^2]/2$ from the projected integrand:
$$
2\left(1-\beta\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)p=2p+\frac{R^2}{r}p'+\frac{R^2\rho GM}{r^3}.
$$
Rearranging proves
$$
\boxed{I\sigma_p^2-R^2\int_R^\infty\frac{\rho GM(r)}{r^2\sqrt{r^2-R^2}}\,dr=\int_R^\infty\left(2p+\frac{R^2}{r}p'\right)\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2-R^2}}.}
$$
No constancy of $\beta$ was needed. Convergence of the projected moments and the steady spherical assumptions are required. This is the integral form of the <projected spherical Jeans residual>.

Although $\beta$ has disappeared explicitly, the unknown radial pressure $p$ still carries the orbital information. Thus the identity does not remove the <mass--anisotropy--density degeneracy>: projected brightness and one projected <second moment> do not uniquely determine both $M(r)$ and the radial/tangential partition. A central rise in <velocity dispersion> can reflect a compact central <mass>, radial orbital bias, or a changing <mass-to-light ratio>; flattening and departures from equilibrium add further ambiguities.

For example, within a point-mass-dominated region with tracer $\rho\propto r^{-k}$ and constant $\beta$, direct solution gives $\sigma_r^2=GM_\bullet/[(k+1-2\beta)r]$. For $k>1$ the <projected velocity dispersion of a Keplerian cusp> is
$$
\sigma_p^2(R)=\frac{GM_\bullet}{(k+1-2\beta)R}\frac{B(k/2,1/2)}{B((k-1)/2,1/2)}\left(1-\frac{\beta k}{k+1}\right).
$$
The <beta function> factors come from setting $r=R\sec\chi$ in the integrals along the <line of sight>. Different allowed $\beta$ generally change the <mass> needed to fit the same normalization; $k=2$ is a special cancellation, not the general case. Resolving the central <black-hole sphere of influence> and constraining orbital structure with higher <velocity> moments or additional kinematic directions strengthens a compact-mass inference. **Brightness and <velocity dispersion> profiles alone do not establish a unique central black-hole <mass>, still less an <event horizon>.**

= Solution

A <constant of motion> is a quantity preserved along the evolution, possibly with explicit time dependence. For $C(\mathbf x,\mathbf v,t)$ in a <gravitational potential> $\Phi$, the preservation condition is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dC}{dt}=\partial_tC+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_xC-\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla_vC=0.}
$$
For free motion, $\mathbf x-\mathbf v t$ is a useful explicitly time-dependent example. Such initial-position labels should not be counted as stationary <isolating integrals> of a galactic <orbit>.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the distinction relevant here, an <integral of motion> is a single-valued function $I(\mathbf x,\mathbf v)$ of instantaneous <phase space>, with no explicit time dependence, constant along the <orbit>. In an autonomous <Hamiltonian> system this is **$\{I,H\}=0$**, where the bracket is the <Poisson bracket>. An integral must be defined consistently on the <phase space> region under discussion; an arbitrary multivalued formula obtained by solving one trajectory does not supply a global integral. Some conventions call explicitly time-dependent conserved functions integrals too; the stationary interpretation is needed for the following counts.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

An <isolating integral> restricts the accessible <phase space> to a lower-dimensional regular level set. For <functionally independent> <integrals of motion> $I_1,\ldots,I_N$, the differentials $dI_j$ must be independent, and $I_j=c_j$ impose $N$ independent restrictions. In six-dimensional <phase space> the common regular level set has dimension **$6-N$**. Nonisolating <orbit> labels or multivalued constants do not further reduce the closure explored by the motion. This is the <isolating integrals and orbital closure dimension> distinction.

= Solution

**Zero stationary <isolating integrals> are possible when time dependence removes <conservation of energy> and no other symmetry or invariant survives.** For instance, a generic time-dependent nonsymmetric <gravitational potential> need not have a stationary integral. This is not possible in an ordinary time-independent galactic <gravitational potential>: $E=\tfrac12v^2+\Phi$ is already an <isolating integral> on a regular <specific orbital energy> surface. An explicitly time-dependent constant that encodes an initial condition does not contradict the stationary count.

= Solution

**One <isolating integral> occurs when <specific orbital energy> is the only independent stationary invariant.** An autonomous chaotic <orbit> that densely explores a connected five-dimensional <specific orbital energy> surface is the standard case. Every continuous <integral of motion> must then be constant on that surface, so it is locally a function of <specific orbital energy> rather than an additional independent integral. Irregular motion need not mean zero integrals; <specific orbital energy> still isolates the <orbit> from other <specific orbital energy> surfaces.

= Solution

**More than three occur when symmetries or frequency degeneracies reduce the <orbit>'s invariant closure below a generic three-dimensional <invariant torus>.** For a nonradial <orbit> in a spherical <gravitational potential>, <specific orbital energy> and the three components of <specific angular momentum> are generically four independent <isolating integrals>. Their values fix the orbital plane, <angular momentum> magnitude and radial <specific orbital energy>, but a nonclosed rosette still fills a two-dimensional <invariant torus>. Thus more than three does not by itself imply closure. <Orbital resonances> can further reduce the number of independent orbital phases. The count concerns independent integrals, not arbitrarily many functions of the same invariants.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=vii}

**Five independent isolating <orbit> labels describe a regular closed, nonstationary <orbit> in three-dimensional space:** its <phase space> closure is one-dimensional. Five is the maximum at a nonzero flow vector, since every conserved differential annihilates that vector in the six-dimensional tangent space. A global <superintegrable Hamiltonian system> example is the <Kepler orbit>, where <specific orbital energy>, <angular momentum> and the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> have two vector/scalar relations leaving five independent invariants. A circular or radial <orbit> requires a separate treatment of any rank-degenerate formula for these invariants. Five integrals do not force an escaping trajectory to be periodic: unbound <Kepler orbits> also have the conserved apsidal vector. The closure criterion concerns recurrent bound motion.

Closure of one resonant <orbit> need not imply five smooth independent integrals on an open neighborhood of all nearby <orbits>. The orbit-wise count and global superintegrability must be distinguished; the distinction matters in part (b).

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $\mu=GM>0$ and interpret $L$ in the displayed equation as <specific angular momentum>, $L=r^2\dot\psi$, rather than total <angular momentum>. The <gravitational potential> is per unit <mass> and $a$ has dimensions of length. <Spherical symmetry> fixes the orbital plane and conserves the vector <angular momentum>, while the autonomous <specific orbital energy> is also conserved.

For the <orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction>, define
$$
\nu^2=1-\frac{2\mu a}{L^2}.
$$
A regular nonradial bounded oscillation requires $L\ne0$ and $L^2-2\mu a>0$. Solving the linear <Binet equation> then gives
$$
\boxed{u(\psi)=\frac{\mu}{L^2-2\mu a}\left[1+e\cos\bigl(\nu(\psi-\psi_0)\bigr)\right].}
$$
For a noncircular bound <orbit> $0<e<1$; its <specific orbital energy> is $E=\mu^2(e^2-1)/[2(L^2-2\mu a)]$. The <effective potential> $[L^2-2\mu a]/(2r^2)-\mu/r$ has a <centrifugal barrier> and a minimum in this case. If $L^2-2\mu a\leq0$, the oscillator frequency is zero or imaginary and there is no regular finite-radius bound oscillation: the attractive centre lacks a <centrifugal barrier>. Unbound branches with $e\geq1$ are not closed ellipselike trajectories.

Successive pericentres differ in angle by $2\pi/\nu$. The full <phase space> state returns after $p$ radial oscillations and $q$ revolutions precisely when
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac pq\in\mathbb Q_{>0},\qquad L^2=\frac{2\mu a}{1-p^2/q^2},}
$$
where $p,q$ are coprime positive integers; the second formula applies when $a\ne0$. Thus $a>0$ requires $0<p/q<1$ and $L^2>2\mu a$, whereas $a<0$ requires $p/q>1$. An irrational $\nu$ advances the apsidal direction through a dense set of angles and gives a nonclosed rosette, with only the four regular spherical invariants needed to label its two-dimensional closure.

\Image[paper-72-resonant-orbits.png]
{title=Closed rational-frequency orbits and a nonclosed irrational-frequency rosette in an inverse-cube-perturbed central force}

To see the additional resonant label explicitly, let $u_c=\mu/(L^2-2\mu a)$ and $w=u-u_c-i u'/\nu$. Then $w=C e^{i\nu(\psi-\psi_0)}$, so on a family with fixed resonant $L$,
$$
\boxed{w^q e^{-ip\psi}=C^q e^{-ip\psi_0}.}
$$
Its argument fixes the remaining apsidal phase modulo the symmetry of the closed curve; its magnitude is already determined by <specific orbital energy> and $L$. Choosing reference axes in the fixed orbital plane makes this single-valued under $\psi\mapsto\psi+2\pi$. This is the fifth independent <orbit> label on the resonant family described by <closed orbits with an inverse-cube perturbation>.

For $a\ne0$, $\nu$ varies with $L$. The <orbital resonance> condition holds only on selected <angular momentum> surfaces, so this construction is not a fifth global smooth integral on an open set of arbitrary nearby $L$. If the question asks for a maximally <superintegrable Hamiltonian system> on an open region of noncircular bound orbits, the required case is **$a=0$**: then $\nu=1$ for every $L$, and the conserved <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> $\mathbf A=\mathbf v\times\mathbf L-\mu\widehat{\mathbf r}$ fixes the apsidal direction globally. The relations $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf L=0$ and $A^2=\mu^2+2EL^2$ leave five independent constants. For the usual orbit-wise interpretation, noncircular bound <orbits> with rational $\nu$ have five isolating labels; irrational $\nu$ has four. <Circular orbits> are closed for any allowed $\nu$ but have no independent apsidal phase, so the generic noncircular count cannot simply be applied to them.

For completeness, regular unbound branches are different. When $e\geq1$ and $\nu>0$, positive radius restricts the radial phase to $|\nu(\psi-\psi_0)|<\arccos(-1/e)\leq\pi$. The <apsidal invariant on an unbound central-force branch> is
$$
\exp\!\left(i\psi-\frac{i}{\nu}\operatorname{Arg}w\right)=e^{i\psi_0}.
$$
The principal argument is continuous on this scattering branch because it never reaches an apocentre across the negative-real-axis cut. Thus the periapsis direction can be a fifth single-valued scattering label for any positive $\nu$, including irrational $\nu$; the rationality condition applies to the noncircular bound case. For plunging or collision branches with $L^2-2\mu a\leq0$, local trajectory labels can still be defined away from the singular centre, but the preceding periodic-orbit criterion is inapplicable and no smooth continuation through $r=0$ is asserted.
