= Solution
{parent=1}

Write $K_j(t)$ for a <balanced complete multipartite blow-up>, with $j$ parts each containing $t$ <vertices>. Its occurrence is a <subgraph> condition, so additional internal <edges> in the host are harmless. The coefficient multiplying $\log n$ is essential: the PDF has $t=\lfloor d\log n\rfloor$, although it is lost in the converted TeX.

First prove the <common neighbourhood from bipartite density> estimate. Suppose a <bipartite graph> has parts of sizes $m,N$ and at least $\beta mN$ <edges>. For an integer $s\le\beta m/2$, count incidences between $s$-subsets of the first part and their <common neighbours> in the second. The discrete <convexity> of $k\mapsto\binom ks$, with value zero for $k<s$, gives
$$
\max_{|S|=s}|N(S)|\binom ms\ge\sum_{v}\binom{\deg(v)}s\ge N\binom{\lfloor\beta m\rfloor}s.
$$
The <convexity> assertion follows directly from $\binom{k+1}s-\binom ks=\binom{k}{s-1}$, which is increasing in $k$; smoothing two unequal degrees cannot increase the sum. In the binomial ratio every factor is at least $\beta/2$, because $\lfloor\beta m\rfloor-s+1\ge\beta m-s\ge\beta m/2$. Consequently some $S$ satisfies
$$
|N(S)|\ge N(\beta/2)^s.
$$

We prove the <logarithmic clique blow-up from minimum degree> by induction on $r$. When $\varepsilon\ge1/r$, the degree hypothesis is impossible for a loopless <graph>, so any positive $d$ works vacuously. Assume $0<\varepsilon<1/r$. For $r=1$, take two copies of the <vertex set> and join the copies of adjacent <vertices>. This <bipartite graph> has <edge density of a bipartite graph> at least $\varepsilon$. Choose
$$
d(1,\varepsilon)\le\frac1{2\log(2/\varepsilon)}.
$$
For sufficiently large $n$, $t=\lfloor d\log n\rfloor\le\varepsilon n/2$, and the estimate supplies at least $\sqrt n$ <common neighbours>. Choose $t$ of them. The two selected sets are disjoint in the original <graph>, since a selected vertex cannot be adjacent to itself. They give $K_2(t)$.

For $r\ge2$, put $\varepsilon'=\varepsilon+1/(r-1)-1/r$. The same <minimum degree of a graph> exceeds $(1-1/(r-1)+\varepsilon')n$. By induction there is a $K_r(m)$ with $m=\lfloor d'\log n\rfloor$, where $d'=d(r-1,\varepsilon')>0$. Pair the <vertices> of its $r$ parts into $m$ disjoint transversal <cliques> $C_1,\ldots,C_m$. Each has at least
$$
n-r(n-\delta(G))\ge r\varepsilon n
$$
<common neighbours>, by bounding the union of the non-neighbour sets of its $r$ <vertices>. Set $\beta=\min\{r\varepsilon,1/2\}$. Form the <bipartite graph> whose first part is these $m$ <cliques>, with $C_i$ joined to a vertex when that vertex is a <common neighbour> of all of $C_i$. It has density at least $\beta$. Choose
$$
0<d\le\min\left\{\frac{\beta d'}4,\frac1{2\log(2/\beta)}\right\}.
$$
For large $n$, $m\ge d'\log n/2$ and $t=\lfloor d\log n\rfloor\le\beta m/2$. The estimate gives $t$ selected <cliques> and at least $\sqrt n\ge t$ common <vertices>. The selected <cliques> supply $t$ <vertices> in each of the original $r$ parts, and their common <vertices> supply the final part. No common vertex belongs to a selected <clique>, again because there are no loops. All required cross-part <edges> are present. Increasing $n_0(r,\varepsilon)$ to satisfy these finitely many inequalities completes the induction:
$$
\boxed{K_{r+1}(\lfloor d(r,\varepsilon)\log n\rfloor)\subseteq G.}
$$

The <Erdős-Stone theorem> states that for a fixed <graph> $H$ with <chromatic number> $r+1\ge2$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ex}(n,H)=\left(1-\frac1r+o(1)\right)\binom n2.}
$$
The lower bound comes from the <Turan graph> $T_r(n)$, which cannot contain an $(r+1)$-chromatic <subgraph>. To obtain the upper bound from our minimum-degree result, suppose $e(G)\ge(a+\eta)n^2/2$, where $a=1-1/r$ and $0<\eta<1-a$. Repeatedly delete a vertex of degree less than $b$ times the current order, where $b=a+\eta/2$, until no such vertex remains. If the remaining order is $m$, then
$$
e(G)\le\frac b2(n^2-m^2+n)+\frac{m^2}2,
\qquad
(1-b)m^2\ge\frac\eta2n^2-bn.
$$
Thus $m\ge c_\eta n$ for large $n$, with $c_\eta>0$, and the remaining <subgraph> has <minimum degree of a graph> at least $(a+\eta/2)m$. It contains $K_{r+1}(\lfloor d\log m\rfloor)$. A proper <graph colouring> of $H$ places its <vertices> in the $r+1$ parts once their size exceeds $|V(H)|$. This contradicts $H$-freeness. Adjusting a fixed positive density margin absorbs the difference between $n^2/2$ and $\binom n2$, proving the upper bound.

The six-vertex forbidden <graph> is the <octahedral graph> $K_{2,2,2}$: the deleted <matching in a graph> gives its three independent pairs. Its <chromatic number> is three, so
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{ex}(n,F)}{\binom n2}=\frac12.}
$$
The printed notation uses $H$ for the extremal-number argument while its defining condition forbids $F$; the answer uses that explicitly defined forbidden <graph>. For the strict finite inequality, take $T_2(n)$ and add one <edge> inside its larger part, which has at least two <vertices> for $n\ge3$. Every <triangle in a graph> then uses this added edge. However, $K_{2,2,2}$ contains two vertex-disjoint <triangles in a graph>, obtained by taking one vertex from each pair and then the other three. It therefore cannot occur in the constructed <graph>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ex}(n,F)\ge t_2(n)+1>t_2(n)\qquad(n\ge3).}
$$

Use the equitable form of the <Szemerédi regularity lemma>. For $0<\varepsilon<1/2$ and a prescribed lower bound $m_0$, there are $M,n_0$ such that every sufficiently large <graph> admits a <set partition>
$$
V(G)=V_0\sqcup V_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_k,
\quad m_0\le k\le M,\quad |V_0|\le\varepsilon n,\quad |V_1|=\cdots=|V_k|,
$$
with at most $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered <irregular pairs> among the nonexceptional cells. A <uniform pair> here means a <regular pair of vertex sets>. Larger values of $\varepsilon$ follow from a smaller parameter, so the stated restriction loses nothing.

For the proof, retain the exceptional <vertices> as singleton cells in an auxiliary <set partition> $\mathcal P$. Define its <regularity energy> by the ordered sum
$$
q(\mathcal P)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{A,B\in\mathcal P}|A||B|d(A,B)^2.
$$
For diagonal rectangles the <adjacency matrix of a graph> defines $d(A,A)$; diagonal terms are included. Thus $0\le q\le1$. If $A\times B$ is split into rectangles of densities $d_{uv}$ and sizes $w_{uv}$, their weighted mean is $d=d(A,B)$. Expanding squares proves the <refinement variance identity for regularity energy>:
$$
\sum_{u,v}w_{uv}d_{uv}^2-|A||B|d^2
=\sum_{u,v}w_{uv}(d_{uv}-d)^2\ge0.
$$
Every refinement therefore increases or preserves energy.

Suppose equal cells $V_i,V_j$ of size $m$ are $\varepsilon$-irregular. Choose witnesses $X\subseteq V_i$, $Y\subseteq V_j$ of sizes at least $\varepsilon m$, whose density differs from $d(V_i,V_j)$ by more than $\varepsilon$. Refine the cells so that these witnesses are unions of refined atoms. Applying the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> inside $X\times Y$ gives an increase, for this oriented rectangle, greater than
$$
\frac{|X||Y|}{n^2}|d(X,Y)-d(V_i,V_j)|^2
\ge\frac{\varepsilon^4m^2}{n^2}.
$$
Choose witnesses simultaneously for every <irregular pair>. Each original cell is cut by at most $k-1$ witness sets and has at most $2^k$ atoms. If more than $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered pairs are irregular, both orientations contribute, giving
$$
\Delta q>2\varepsilon^5\left(\frac{km}{n}\right)^2\ge\frac{\varepsilon^5}{2},
$$
as long as the exceptional set has at most $n/2$ <vertices>.

To restore equal sizes without sacrificing this increase, use <equalization with a controlled exceptional set>. Cut every witness atom into blocks of size $\ell=\lfloor m/4^k\rfloor$, leaving fewer than $\ell$ unused <vertices> in each atom. Make each unused vertex a singleton exceptional cell. This is a further refinement, so there is **no energy loss**. The number newly exceptional is at most
$$
k2^k\ell\le km2^{-k}\le n2^{-k}.
$$
When $m\ge2\cdot4^k$, the new nonexceptional cell count $k'$ is at most $4k4^k$: indeed $\ell\ge m/(2\cdot4^k)$ and $n/m\le2k$. It is at least $k$, since each old cell supplies at least $4^k-2^k\ge1$ blocks.

Let $L=\lceil2\varepsilon^{-5}\rceil+1$. Choose the initial count $k_0\ge m_0$ so large that $L2^{-k_0}\le\varepsilon/2$, and begin with $k_0$ equal cells and fewer than $k_0$ leftover <vertices>. Take $n_0$ large enough that these initial leftovers are at most $\varepsilon n/2$. Iterate the bound $k\mapsto4k4^k$ for $L$ rounds to obtain a finite number $M$. Increase $n_0$ further to ensure $n\ge4M4^M$. Then every cell size encountered satisfies $m\ge n/(2M)\ge2\cdot4^k$, so every equalization is legitimate. The total exception budget is at most $\varepsilon n$, because the initial losses use half the budget and all later losses sum to at most $Ln2^{-k_0}$. Energy cannot increase by more than one, so $L$ failing rounds are impossible. The process must stop with at most $\varepsilon k^2$ irregular pairs. This proves the <Szemerédi regularity lemma>, including the bounded cell count, equitability and exceptional-set control.

For the dense-pair application, apply this proof with $\eta=1/100$ and $m_0=100$. Suppose every $\eta$-<regular pair> of nonexceptional cells has density at most $1/4$. <Edges> touching $V_0$ number at most $\eta n^2$. <Edges> inside nonexceptional cells number at most $km^2/2\le n^2/(2m_0)$. The at most $\eta k^2$ irregular cell pairs contribute at most $\eta n^2$. Finally the regular cross-cell pairs contribute at most $k(k-1)m^2/8\le n^2/8$. Therefore
$$
e(G)\le\left(\frac18+2\eta+\frac1{2m_0}\right)n^2
=\frac3{20}n^2<\frac16n^2,
$$
a contradiction. Some regular pair $(U,W)$ consequently has density greater than $1/4$. It is also a $1/8$-<uniform pair>, since subsets of relative size at least $1/8$ qualify in the stronger $\eta$-regularity condition. The common cell size satisfies $m\ge(1-\eta)n/M\ge n/(2M)$. Thus the <dense regular pair from edge surplus> gives
$$
\boxed{|U|=|W|\ge cn,\qquad d(U,W)>1/4,\qquad (U,W)\text{ is }1/8\text{-uniform}.}
$$
This first proves the assertion for $n\ge n_0$. For the finitely many smaller possible orders, choose the endpoints of any edge as singleton sets: the pair has density one and is regular for every positive parameter. Taking $c=\min\{1/(2M),1/n_0\}>0$ includes these cases as well.

Both alternatives can be proved. For the <oriented tree> alternative take $n\ge2$; at $n=1$ the displayed <tournament> order is zero, so that unqualified boundary case cannot be intended.

Choose a <median order of a tournament> $v_1,\ldots,v_N$, maximizing its number of forward <directed edges>. Every interval inherits a <median order>, since improving that interval would improve the whole order. If $v_i$ had fewer outneighbours than inneighbours in $v_{i+1},\ldots,v_j$, moving it just past $v_j$ would increase the number of forward <directed edges>. Hence it has at least half the <vertices> of that later interval as outneighbours.

Call a <graph embedding> half-sparse if every proper final interval $I$ satisfies $|f(T)\cap I|<|I|/2+1$. This <half-sparse median-order embedding> admits a useful extension. Suppose an outward leaf is attached to a vertex mapped to $v_i$, and reserve two extra <vertices> at the end of the <tournament> order. In the old interval after $v_i$, half-sparsity bounds the number of occupied <vertices> by less than half its size plus one. The interval extended by two <vertices> has at least that half-size plus one outneighbours of $v_i$. Thus at least one outneighbour is free; map the new leaf there. Every old final interval has gained two available places and at most one new image, so its strict inequality persists. Final intervals wholly within the two new places contain at most one image, so they also satisfy the inequality. This proves the extension, not just an existence assertion about degrees.

Starting with an <directed edge> in a two-vertex <tournament> and repeatedly attaching outward leaves proves that every $h$-vertex <arborescence> embeds half-sparsely in every $(2h-2)$-vertex <tournament> for $h\ge2$. The root-first construction supplies the induction. An unused suffix can be appended without destroying the inequality; for a suffix which contains the whole old order the extra place supplies the needed strictness. A singleton embeds half-sparsely in the first vertex of a two-vertex order. Reversing the <oriented tree> and <tournament> gives ordinary embeddings of reversed <arborescences> in $2h-2$ <vertices> as well.

Here is the stronger invariant needed for an arbitrary <oriented tree>. Root it at a source. Classify each <directed edge> as forward or backward according to whether it points away from or towards the root in the underlying <rooted tree>. Let $b$ be the number of backward <directed edges> and $c$ the number of nonempty connected components made of these <directed edges>, ignoring isolated <vertices>. Put $d=b-c$. We claim that the <oriented tree> has a <half-sparse embedding> in every median-ordered <tournament> of order $2n+2d$.

Induct on the <oriented tree> order. If it has a forward leaf, delete that leaf, use the induction hypothesis and apply the two-place extension. Neither $b$ nor $c$ changes. If there are no backward <directed edges>, use the <arborescence> construction. Otherwise first remove forward leaves as above, so every remaining leaf is backward. Choose a backward component furthest from the root. It must be a pendant subtree: a forward continuation below it would either reach another backward component, contrary to its choice, or end at a forward leaf. Let this component have $h\ge2$ <vertices> and top vertex $y$. Since the root is a source, $y$ is not the root; its joining <directed edge> from its parent $x$ is forward. The component is an reversed <arborescence> rooted at $y$.

Delete the component. The remaining <oriented tree> has parameters
$$
n'=n-h,\qquad b'=b-h+1,\qquad c'=c-1,\qquad d'=d-h+2.
$$
Its required <tournament> order is $2n+2d-4h+4$. Embed it in the initial interval of this length. Temporarily attach $2h-2$ outward leaves at $x$, using the extension lemma repeatedly. This consumes exactly $4h-4$ extra places and gives a <half-sparse embedding> of the enlarged <oriented tree> in the original <tournament>. The $2h-2$ temporary images all lie in the outneighbourhood of $f(x)$; among them embed the deleted reversed <arborescence>. Its root automatically receives the joining <directed edge> from $f(x)$. Replace the temporary leaves by this component. The final image is a subset of the temporary enlarged image, so half-sparsity survives. This proves the invariant with the precise count, including the case when the remaining <oriented tree> is a singleton.

Unless the <oriented tree> is already an in- or out-arborescence, every rooting has at least one backward component. Choose one orientation, reversing all <directed edges> if necessary, so that some rooting has at most $(n-1)/2$ backward <directed edges>. It then has $d\le(n-3)/2$. Within this fixed orientation choose the root to minimize $(d,b)$ lexicographically. If a neighbour pointed into the root, move the root across that <directed edge>. Only this <directed edge> changes its forward/backward classification. Removing it from the backward forest decreases $b$ by one and decreases $c$ by at most one; hence it cannot increase $d$, and when $d$ stays equal it strictly decreases $b$. This contradicts the chosen minimum. Thus the chosen root is a source, while its $d$ retains the required bound. Reversal does not affect a universal tournament-embedding assertion, because one reverses the host <tournament> too. The invariant proves the <three-times-order tournament bound for oriented trees>:
$$
\boxed{T\subseteq R\quad\text{whenever }|T|=n\ge2,\ |R|=3n-3.}
$$
For the lower obstruction, take the outward <graph star> with $n-1$ leaves. There is a <regular tournament> on $2n-3$ <vertices>: on residues modulo $2n-3$, direct $i\to j$ when $j-i$ is one of $1,\ldots,n-2$. Every vertex has outdegree $n-2$, so none can be the centre of that outward star. Thus **the universal bound cannot be reduced to $2n-3$**.

For the <strongly connected directed graph> alternative, the correct universal assertion is an upper bound by the <independence number>, together with examples needing that many <directed circuits>. The literal assertion that every such <graph> needs $\alpha(D)$ <directed circuits> is false: a directed four-cycle has <independence number> two and is spanned by one <directed circuit>. Work with a nontrivial strongly connected digraph; a loopless singleton has no <directed circuit>.

Use the permitted <coherent cyclic order of a digraph>. Representatives differ by cyclic rotations and swaps of consecutive nonadjacent <vertices>. In every representative, a backward <directed edge> $v_j\to v_i$ has a forward <directed path> from $v_i$ to $v_j$. A <directed circuit> with one backward <directed edge> winds once around the order. Every vertex belongs to such a <directed circuit>, because every vertex has an outgoing <directed edge> and coherence puts that <directed edge> on a once-winding <directed circuit>. Let $k$ be the <cyclic stability of a digraph order>: the largest size of an <independent set> consecutive in an equivalent order. Then $k\le\alpha(D)$.

We need a short ordering lemma. If a set $X$ has no forward path between distinct members, then $|X|\le k$. Among equivalent representatives retaining this property, minimize the span from its first to its last member. Suppose there is a vertex in a gap. If some gap vertex $y$ cannot be reached from an earlier member of $X$ by a forward path, take the first such $y$. Every preceding gap vertex in the span is reachable from an earlier member of $X$. It follows that $y$ has no forward incoming <directed edge> from anywhere in that part of the span; a backward outgoing <directed edge> to it is also impossible, since coherence would supply the same forbidden forward reachability. Thus $y$ is nonadjacent to every intervening vertex and can be moved before the first member of $X$, shortening the span. Otherwise every gap vertex is reachable from an earlier member of $X$. Take the last one. It cannot have a forward path to a later member of $X$, and coherence consequently rules out <directed edges> in either direction to the following members of $X$. Move it past them, again shortening the span. Swaps of nonadjacent <vertices> do not change the forward-edge <graph>, so these moves preserve the hypothesis. Both cases contradict minimality. Therefore $X$ is consecutive. It is independent: a forward <directed edge> is forbidden by hypothesis, and a backward <directed edge> would imply a forward path by coherence. Rotate it to the start to obtain $|X|\le k$.

Choose a representative starting with a stable block $S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_k\}$. Add sink copies $s'_1,\ldots,s'_k$, retain all forward <directed edges> of $D$, and add $v\to s'_i$ for each original <directed edge> $v\to s_i$. This is an acyclic auxiliary <directed graph> $A$. A once-winding <directed circuit> through $s_i$ cannot visit another block member, since no forward path can join two members of this initial independent block. Opening this <directed circuit> gives a path from $s_i$ to $s'_i$ in $A$.

The <partial order> defined by reachability in $A$ has width exactly $k$. The sink copies already form an <antichain> of size $k$. For the reverse inequality, let $X$ be an <antichain> and let $J$ index its sink copies. Permute the independent initial block so that these labels come first, then rotate that part of the block to the end. The set
$$
Y=(X\cap V(D))\cup\{s_j:j\in J\}
$$
has size $|X|$: a vertex and its own copy cannot both belong to $X$, since they are comparable by the opened <directed circuit>. There is no forward path between distinct members of $Y$ in the rotated order. A path between original members would give comparability in $A$; a path from an original member to $s_j$ would become a path to its sink copy, also forbidden. No forward path leaves the final stable block. The ordering lemma therefore gives $|X|=|Y|\le k$.

Apply the <Dilworth theorem> to partition this reachability order into $k$ <chains in a partial order>. Each chain contains exactly one original block vertex and exactly one sink copy, since each of these two <antichains> has size $k$; these are its first and last elements. Expand consecutive chain relations into <directed paths> in $A$. The paths cover all <vertices>, though intervening <vertices> can occur on several paths. Label their heads $s_i$ and their tails $s'_{\sigma(i)}$; the tail labels define a <permutation> $\sigma$.

Among all such covering path families maximize the number of cycles of $\sigma$. If two paths whose labels lie in the same <permutation> cycle meet at a vertex, exchange their suffixes there. The paths remain simple, since the auxiliary <graph> is acyclic, and their union still covers the same <vertices>. Exchanging the two tail labels splits that <permutation> cycle into two, a contradiction. Thus paths in the same <permutation> cycle are disjoint. Identify every $s'_i$ with $s_i$ and concatenate the paths around each cycle of $\sigma$. Each concatenation is a genuine <directed circuit>, with no repeated vertex except its closing vertex. Together these <directed circuits> cover $D$, and there are at most $k\le\alpha(D)$ of them. This proves the <circuit-cover bound by independence number>:
$$
\boxed{D\text{ has a spanning circuit family of size at most }\alpha(D).}
$$
Finally, form $k$ directed triangles sharing just one central vertex, with no <directed edges> between their other <vertices>. This <strongly connected directed graph> has <independence number> $k$, by taking one outer vertex from each triangle. A simple <directed circuit> can use only one triangle, since visiting a second requires repeating the centre. Exactly $k$ <directed circuits> are therefore necessary. **The bound $\alpha(D)$ is sharp in general; $\alpha(D)-1$ is not a universal bound.**

The unrestricted identity needs qualification. A <Fano plane> has seven <vertices of a hypergraph>, so the complete three-<uniform hypergraph> on six <vertices of a hypergraph> avoids it and has twenty edges, whereas the displayed balanced-part count is eighteen. On five <vertices of a hypergraph> the corresponding numbers are ten and nine. Thus the intended extremal theorem is the sufficiently-large-$n$ <Fano-plane Turán theorem>. We describe its stability proof, including the exact counting step that upgrades an asymptotic result to equality.

Identify the Fano points with the nonzero vectors of $\mathbb F_2^3$, and its <hyperedges> with triples summing to zero. It has no <Property B>. Indeed, a line-free set has at most four points: if a set $A$ of nonzero vectors contains no zero-sum triple and $a\in A$, the sets $A\setminus\{a\}$ and $a+(A\setminus\{a\})$ are disjoint subsets of the six nonzero vectors other than $a$, so $|A|\le4$. Moreover, if $|A|=4$, those two three-element sets partition those six vectors. Write $A=\{a,b,c,d\}$. The sum $b+c$ lies outside $A$, so belongs to $a+(A\setminus\{a\})$. It is neither $a+b$ nor $a+c$, and must therefore equal $a+d$. Thus $a+b+c+d=0$. The other three points have sum zero because the sum of all seven nonzero vectors is zero. They therefore form a line. In any two-colouring one colour has at least four points: if it contains a line we are done, and otherwise the other colour contains a line. This proves non-two-colourability.

Partition the host <vertices of a hypergraph> into sizes $a,b$ and take all triples meeting both parts. This <bipartite three-uniform hypergraph> has <Property B> and is consequently Fano-free. Its edge count is
$$
a\binom b2+b\binom a2=\frac{ab(n-2)}2.
$$
The product $ab$ is maximized at balanced integer sizes. Writing
$$
b(n)=\binom n3-\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}3-\binom{\lceil n/2\rceil}3,
$$
we have the exact lower bound $\operatorname{ex}(n,F)\ge b(n)$, and $b(n)=(3/4+o(1))\binom n3$.

The structural step is <Fano stability>: a Fano-free <hypergraph> with density sufficiently close to $3/4$ has a bipartition with at most $\rho n^3$ internal triples, for any prescribed $\rho>0$. Its proof studies four <hypergraph link graphs> around a complete four-vertex three-uniform configuration. Colour a pair by the link <vertices of a hypergraph> containing it. Three perfect <matchings in a graph> on four other <vertices of a hypergraph>, with distinct colours corresponding to a triple of the configuration, would complete a Fano copy. Thus these rainbow patterns are forbidden. The near-extremal total link degree forces, after removing a small exceptional set, an almost balanced pattern with pair multiplicity two within the two classes and four between them. Internal triple density bounded away from zero produces many complete tripartite six-vertex templates; the nearly complete links then supply three paired neighbours and complete a Fano plane. Consequently the internal triple density tends to zero. These are the main ingredients of the stability argument: link degree counting supplies the approximate partition, and the forbidden completion templates eliminate positive internal density.

To see concretely why stability gives the **exact** bound, suppose $e(H)\ge b(n)$, and first obtain high minimum <hypergraph vertex degree>. Delete any vertex of degree at most $3m^2/8-2m$ when the current order is $m$. Directly from the formula for $b$, for large $m$,
$$
b(m)-b(m-1)>3m^2/8-2m+1.
$$
After deletions down to order $m$, the remaining <hypergraph> therefore has at least $b(m)+n-m$ edges. It cannot reach $m=\lceil n^{1/3}\rceil$, since this exceeds $\binom m3$ for large $n$. The process stops at an order still tending to infinity, with <minimum degree of a graph> greater than $3m^2/8-2m$. If the high-minimum-degree case has at most $b(m)$ edges, any deletion gives a contradiction. Thus it suffices to treat that case; rename its order $n$.

Apply stability with a very small constant, for example $\rho=10^{-12}$, and choose a partition $A\sqcup B$ minimizing the number of internal <hyperedges>. Its part sizes are close to $n/2$, since its cross-part capacity is $|A||B|(n-2)/2$, and $e(H)\ge b(n)$ leaves at most $\rho n^3$ available compensation for any imbalance. In particular $||A|-n/2|,||B|-n/2|\le2\sqrt\rho n$ for large $n$. Optimality also gives
$$
e(L_H(x)[A])\le e(L_H(x)[B])\quad(x\in A),
$$
with the analogous inequality for $x\in B$: otherwise moving $x$ would reduce the internal count.

Suppose some $x\in A$ has more than $n^2/100$ edges in its same-side link. Its opposite-side link then has at least as many. Each link has a <matching in a graph> of size at least $\lfloor n/300\rfloor$: the endpoints of a maximal <matching in a graph> cover every edge, so a <matching in a graph> of size $s$ bounds the link edge count by $2sn$. For each choice of one <matching in a graph> edge in $A$ and two <matching in a graph> edges in $B$, the six <vertices of a hypergraph> plus $x$ would form a Fano plane if all transversal triples through the three pairs were present. Specifically take the three triples through $x$ and the four even-parity transversal triples. Hence at least one of these cross-part triples is missing. The <matchings in a graph> are disjoint, so different choices give different missing triples. There are at least
$$
s\binom s2\quad\text{such missing triples},\qquad s=\lfloor n/300\rfloor.
$$
For large $n$ this exceeds $\rho n^3$, contradicting the edge count: the missing cross triples already outweigh every internal triple. Thus every same-side link has at most $n^2/100$ edges.

If an internal triple $xyz\subseteq A$ remains, high <minimum degree of a graph> gives, for each of its <vertices of a hypergraph>,
$$
e(L_H(x)[B])>\frac{3n^2}{8}-2n-|A||B|-\frac{n^2}{100}
\ge\left(\frac18-\frac1{100}\right)n^2-2n.
$$
The intersection of the three opposite-side links therefore has at least their total edge count minus $2\binom{|B|}2$. Using $|B|\le(1/2+2\sqrt\rho)n$, this exceeds $|B|^2/3$ for large $n$. By the quadratic <Turan theorem> bound, their intersection contains $K_4$. Its three perfect <matchings in a graph>, assigned to $x,y,z$, together with $xyz$ are the seven edges of a Fano plane. This is precisely <Fano completion through three matchings>, and gives a contradiction. The same argument excludes internal triples in $B$.

Thus the high-minimum-degree <hypergraph> is genuinely bipartite, not merely approximately so, and has at most $b(n)$ edges. The deletion argument proves the same bound for the original <hypergraph>. Combining with the balanced construction yields
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ex}(n,F)=\binom n3-\binom{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}3-\binom{\lceil n/2\rceil}3\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.}
$$
This proof description keeps the essential roles distinct: non-two-colourability gives the lower construction, link-graph stability gives an approximate partition, and the minimum-degree/<matching in a graph> argument removes the last internal edges.
