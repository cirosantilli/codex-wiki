Let $\zeta_n$ be a primitive nth <root of unity>. The <cyclotomic polynomial> is
$$
\Phi_n(X)=\prod_{\substack{1\le a\le n\\(a,n)=1}}(X-\zeta_n^a),\qquad X^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(X).
$$
Induction on $n$ shows that $\Phi_n$ is monic with integer coefficients. Here is the essential <irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials> argument. Let $f$ be the <minimal polynomial> of $\zeta_n$ and let $\ell\nmid n$ be a prime. If $\zeta_n^\ell$ had a different monic <irreducible polynomial> $g$, then $f\mid g(X^\ell)$. Reducing modulo $\ell$ gives $\bar f\mid\bar g^\ell$, so $\bar f$ and $\bar g$ have a common factor. As $fg\mid\Phi_n$, this would give a repeated factor of $X^n-1$ modulo $\ell$, impossible because its <derivative> is $nX^{n-1}$. Thus $\zeta_n^\ell$ is conjugate to $\zeta_n$. Every positive integer prime to $n$ is a product of such primes, so all primitive nth <roots of unity> are conjugate. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\zeta_n):\mathbb Q]=\varphi(n),\qquad\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q)\cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times,\quad \sigma_a(\zeta_n)=\zeta_n^a.}
$$
In particular the <cyclotomic field> is an <abelian extension>. <Complex conjugation> corresponds to $-1$; for $n>2$ its fixed field is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n+\zeta_n^{-1})$, the maximal real subfield, of degree $\varphi(n)/2$.

The <ring of integers> is $\mathbb Z[\zeta_n]$. One way to see that there is no missing index is to work at each prime $p$. Write $n=p^a m$ with $p\nmid m$. The prime-to-$p$ <roots of unity> have separable reduction; their local factors generate <unramified extensions> with full integer rings. Over each such factor, adjoining $\zeta_{p^a}$ is generated by the <Eisenstein polynomial> $\Phi_{p^a}(1+T)$, with <uniformizer> $\zeta_{p^a}-1$, and hence gives the full integer ring of that totally ramified factor. The coprimality of $p^a$ and $m$ means that $\zeta_n$ generates both sets of <roots of unity>. Thus $\mathbb Z_p[\zeta_n]$ is maximal at every $p$, proving the integral assertion.

The same local description computes <ramification> and splitting. For $p\nmid n$, the <ramification index> is one, the <residue degree> is the multiplicative order of $p$ modulo $n$, and there are $\varphi(n)/f$ primes above $p$. If $p\mid n$, put $m=n/p^a$; then
$$
e_p=\varphi(p^a),\qquad f_p=\operatorname{ord}_m(p),\qquad g_p=\frac{\varphi(m)}{f_p},
$$
where $\operatorname{ord}_1(p)=1$. Indeed, the prime-to-$p$ part is <unramified>, and the $p$-power part is totally ramified by the preceding <Eisenstein polynomial>. Notice the exception $p=2,a=1$: its <ramification index> is one, consistently with $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{2m})=\mathbb Q(\zeta_m)$ for odd $m$. Thus finite <ramification> occurs only at the indicated conductor primes, with that exception.

We now prove <cyclotomic Artin reciprocity over the rational numbers>, using arithmetic <Frobenius automorphisms>. If $\ell\nmid n$ and $\mathfrak L$ lies over $\ell$, then <Frobenius> sends the reduction of $\zeta_n$ to its $\ell$th power. Distinct nth <roots of unity> remain distinct modulo $\mathfrak L$, since $X^n-1$ has separable reduction. Hence the <Artin symbol> is exactly
$$
\operatorname{Art}((\ell))=\sigma_\ell.
$$
Extend multiplicatively to the <group> $I(n)$ of rational <fractional ideals> prime to $n$. Such an <ideal> has a unique positive rational generator $q$ whose numerator and denominator are prime to $n$. The formula is
$$
\operatorname{Art}((q))=\sigma_{q\bmod n}.
$$
Every unit residue modulo $n$ has a positive integer representative prime to $n$, so this <Artin map> is surjective. Its <kernel> is precisely the subgroup $P_{n\infty}$ generated by positive $q\equiv1\pmod n$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I(n)/P_{n\infty}\ \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q).}
$$
This proves the <Artin reciprocity law> for this extension, including its principal-ray kernel. The infinite component of the <modulus> records positivity and therefore the possible action of <complex conjugation>.

The <Kronecker–Weber theorem> says that **every finite abelian extension of $\mathbb Q$ is contained in a cyclotomic field**. We prove its quadratic case by identifying the <ramification> that a <quadratic number field> must have.

For an odd prime $\ell$, the cyclic <Galois group> of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_\ell)/\mathbb Q$ has exactly one subgroup of index two. Its fixed field $F_\ell$ is quadratic and is <unramified> at every finite prime other than $\ell$. For a squarefree integer $d$, the <discriminant> of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)$ is $d$ if $d\equiv1\pmod4$, and $4d$ otherwise. A nontrivial quadratic <discriminant> supported on the odd prime $\ell$ must therefore be
$$
\ell^*=(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\ell.
$$
Indeed $d=\pm1$ cannot give a nontrivial extension unramified at two, whereas exactly one of $\ell,-\ell$ is congruent to one modulo four. Thus
$$
F_\ell=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{\ell^*})\subseteq\mathbb Q(\zeta_\ell).
$$
This identifies the <quadratic subfield of a prime cyclotomic field> using <ramification>, without a Gaussian-sum computation.

Now let $F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ squarefree. Multiply $d$ by $\prod_{\ell\mid d,\ \ell\text{ odd}}\ell^*$. Every odd prime then occurs with even exponent, so its <square class> is represented by one of $1,-1,2,-2$. Thus adjoining the square roots of the $\ell^*$ removes all possible odd-prime <ramification>; the remaining quadratic factor is contained in
$$
\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2),\qquad \sqrt2=\zeta_8+\zeta_8^{-1}.
$$
The original $\sqrt d$ is a rational multiple of a product of these square roots. It belongs to the <compositum> of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)$ and the prime <cyclotomic fields> just used. This <compositum> is itself contained in a <cyclotomic field>, proving the theorem for $F$.

The sharper conductor statement follows from the same <ramification> calculation. For the fundamental <discriminant> $D$ of $F$, put
$$
D=D_2\prod_{\substack{\ell\mid D\\\ell\text{ odd}}}\ell^*,\qquad D_2\in\{1,-4,8,-8\}.
$$
The possibilities for $D_2$ follow immediately from the quadratic <discriminant> formula. The corresponding fields are trivial, $\mathbb Q(i)$, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2})$, lying in the <cyclotomic fields> of conductors $1,4,8,8$. Their conductors are coprime to the odd primes in the product. Hence <quadratic Kronecker–Weber theorem by ramification> gives the explicit conclusion
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)\subseteq\mathbb Q(\zeta_{|D|}).}
$$

Write $\mathcal O$ for the <valuation ring> of $K$, $\pi$ for a <uniformizer>, $v(\pi)=1$, and $k=\mathbb F_q$ for the <residue field>. A useful <strong form of Hensel lemma> is the following: if $f\in\mathcal O[X]$ and $a_0\in\mathcal O$ satisfy
$$
v(f(a_0))>2v(f'(a_0)),
$$
then there is a root $a\in\mathcal O$ with $v(a-a_0)>v(f'(a_0))$, unique among roots in that ball. If $f(a_0)\ne0$, its distance satisfies $v(a-a_0)=v(f(a_0))-v(f'(a_0))$.

Here is a proof by successive correction. Put $s=v(f'(a_0))$, $d_j=v(f(a_j))$, and
$$
a_{j+1}=a_j-\frac{f(a_j)}{f'(a_j)}.
$$
Stop if a root has already been reached. Otherwise the correction has <valuation> $d_j-s>s$. Integral Taylor expansions show that $v(f'(a_{j+1}))=s$ and
$$
d_{j+1}\ge2(d_j-s),\qquad d_{j+1}-2s\ge2(d_j-2s).
$$
Thus $d_j-2s$ grows at least geometrically, the corrections tend to zero, and <completeness> gives a limit $a$ with $f(a)=0$. The first correction has strictly smaller <valuation> than every later nonzero correction, proving the asserted distance. If $x,y$ are in the ball $v(x-a_0),v(y-a_0)>s$, then
$$
f(x)-f(y)=(x-y)\bigl(f'(a_0)+c\bigr),\qquad v(c)>s.
$$
The second factor cannot vanish. Two roots in the ball must therefore coincide. This proves <Hensel lemma>; in particular a simple root modulo $\pi$ has a unique lift with that residue.

We use this to establish <existence and uniqueness of unramified local extensions>. For each integer $d\ge1$, choose a monic <irreducible polynomial> $\bar f\in k[X]$ of degree $d$ and lift it to a monic $f\in\mathcal O[X]$. Reduction and <Gauss lemma> show that $f$ remains irreducible over $K$. Let
$$
A=\mathcal O[X]/(f),\qquad L=K[X]/(f).
$$
The <ring> $A$ is finite free and complete over $\mathcal O$, and $A/\pi A\cong\mathbb F_{q^d}$. An element outside $\pi A$ is invertible: lift an inverse from the <residue field> and invert the remaining $1+\pi z$ by a convergent geometric series. Every nonzero element of $A$ is $\pi^t$ times such a unit, since $\bigcap_t\pi^t A=0$ in a finite free <module>. Thus $A$ is a <discrete valuation ring>, its fraction field is $L$, and
$$
[L:K]=d=[k_L:k],\qquad e(L/K)=1.
$$
This constructs an <unramified extension> of each degree.

Conversely, let $M/K$ be an <unramified extension> of degree $d$. Its <residue field> is the unique degree-$d$ <finite field extension> $\mathbb F_{q^d}/\mathbb F_q$. Choose a residue root of $\bar f$ in it. The root is simple, so <Hensel lemma> lifts it to a root of $f$ in $M$. This embeds $L$ into $M$, and equality of degrees makes it an isomorphism. All $d$ residue conjugates lift in $L$ itself; hence $L/K$ is <Galois>, and reduction identifies its <Galois group> with the cyclic <Galois group> of the <residue field> extension. Its distinguished <Frobenius automorphism> reduces to $x\mapsto x^q$.

**There is exactly one unramified extension $K_d$ of each degree inside a fixed algebraic closure**, and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(K_d/K)\cong C_d,\qquad K_d=K(\mu_{q^d-1}),\qquad K_a\subseteq K_b\iff a\mid b.}
$$
For the middle equality, every nonzero element of $\mathbb F_{q^d}$ lifts uniquely to a $(q^d-1)$st <root of unity>, because that exponent is prime to $p$. A primitive such residue generates the <finite field extension>, so its lift generates $K_d$. Taking the union gives the <maximal unramified extension>, with <Galois group> $\widehat{\mathbb Z}$.

For a <local field> $F$ containing the Nth <roots of unity>, fix the <local Artin map> with arithmetic <Frobenius> convention. The <Hilbert norm residue symbol> can be defined by
$$
(a,b)_{F,N}=\frac{\operatorname{rec}_F(b)(a^{1/N})}{a^{1/N}}\in\mu_N.
$$
It is independent of the chosen root, continuous and bimultiplicative, factors through $F^\times/(F^\times)^N$ in each variable, and gives a nondegenerate pairing on that finite <group>. It satisfies $(a,b)_{F,N}=(b,a)_{F,N}^{-1}$ and $(a,1-a)_{F,N}=1$ for $a\ne1$. Its <norm> interpretation is
$$
(a,b)_{F,N}=1\iff b\in N_{F(a^{1/N})/F}F(a^{1/N})^\times.
$$
For $N=2$ the <quadratic Hilbert symbol> is symmetric and sign-valued. It is also characterized by the existence of a nonzero solution of $z^2=ax^2+by^2$. Moreover $(a,-a)_F=1$, and therefore $(a,a)_F=(a,-1)_F$. We now compute both the <square-class groups> and the pairing explicitly.

For odd $p$, write $a=p^\alpha u$ with $u\in\mathbb Z_p^\times$. An element is a square exactly when $\alpha$ is even and the residue of $u$ is a square in $\mathbb F_p^\times$. Necessity follows by reduction; sufficiency follows from the simple-root form of <Hensel lemma> applied to $X^2-u$, since $2x$ is then a unit. If $\eta$ is any unit with nonsquare residue, this gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q_p^\times/(\mathbb Q_p^\times)^2\cong C_2\times C_2,\qquad\text{representatives }1,\eta,p,\eta p\quad(p\text{ odd}).}
$$
The nonsquare unit defines an <unramified> quadratic extension. Its <field norms> include every unit and have even <valuation>, as follows from the local norm calculation below. Thus $(\eta,u)_p=1$ for every unit, but $(\eta,p)_p=-1$. Finally $(p,p)_p=(p,-1)_p=(-1/p)$, by symmetry and the preceding unit calculation. Bimultiplicativity now proves <explicit quadratic Hilbert symbols over the p-adic numbers>:
$$
\boxed{(p^\alpha u,p^\beta v)_p=(-1)^{\alpha\beta(p-1)/2}\left(\frac up\right)^\beta\left(\frac vp\right)^\alpha\quad(p\text{ odd}).}
$$
Here the factors are <Legendre symbols> of the nonzero residues. The formula works for arbitrary integer <valuations> $\alpha,\beta$, since only their parity matters.

At $p=2$, the square of every odd integer is one modulo eight. Conversely, for an odd $u\equiv1\pmod8$, apply the <strong form of Hensel lemma> to $X^2-u$ at $1$: the error has <valuation> at least three, while the <derivative> has <valuation> one. Thus the <unit square classes of the p-adic integers> are precisely their four residues modulo eight. Combining this with parity of the <valuation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q_2^\times/(\mathbb Q_2^\times)^2\cong C_2^3,\qquad\text{basis }[-1],[2],[5],\quad\text{representatives }\pm1,\pm2,\pm5,\pm10.}
$$
For an odd unit put
$$
\epsilon(u)=\frac{u-1}{2}\pmod2,\qquad\omega(u)=\frac{u^2-1}{8}\pmod2.
$$
Inspection of the residues $1,3,5,7$ gives $[u]=[-1]^{\epsilon(u)}[5]^{\omega(u)}$. It remains to compute six basis pairings. Explicit <field norms> give
$$
(-1,2)_2=(-1,5)_2=(2,2)_2=(5,5)_2=1:
$$
indeed $2=1^2+1^2$, $5=1^2+2^2$, $2=2^2-2\cdot1^2$, and $5=5^2-5\cdot2^2$.

In contrast $(-1,-1)_2=-1$. To justify the obstruction even for rational 2-adic coordinates, write $x=2^t x_0$, $y=2^t y_0$ with $x_0,y_0$ integral and at least one odd. Then $v_2(x_0^2+y_0^2)$ is zero if exactly one is odd, and one if both are odd. A sum of squares equal to the unit $-1$ consequently forces $t=0$ and exactly one coordinate odd. Its residue modulo four would then be one, whereas $-1$ is three.

Similarly $(2,5)_2=-1$. If $x^2-2y^2$ is a unit, the <valuations> of the two terms have opposite parity, so cannot cancel. It follows that $x$ is an odd integral element and $y$ is integral. Modulo eight its <norm> is then $1$ or $7$, never $5$. These signs, symmetry, and bimultiplicativity give
$$
\boxed{(2^\alpha u,2^\beta v)_2=(-1)^{\epsilon(u)\epsilon(v)+\alpha\omega(v)+\beta\omega(u)}.}
$$
The two boxed formulas determine the <quadratic Hilbert symbol> on all nonzero elements, including every rational prime $p$.

We give the full local-field norm-index argument. Let $G=\langle\sigma\rangle$ be cyclic of order $n$. On an additive $G$-<module> set $D=\sigma-1$ and $N=1+\sigma+\cdots+\sigma^{n-1}$. The two cyclic <Tate cohomology> <groups> are
$$
\widehat H^0(G,M)=M^G/NM,\qquad\widehat H^{-1}(G,M)=\ker N/DM.
$$
When these are finite, the <Herbrand quotient> is $h_G(M)=|\widehat H^0(G,M)|/|\widehat H^{-1}(G,M)|$. The periodic complex alternating $D$ and $N$ yields a six-term periodic exact sequence from a short exact sequence of <modules>. Taking alternating products of orders in that exact sequence proves
$$
h_G(M)=h_G(M')h_G(M'')\qquad(0\to M'\to M\to M''\to0).
$$
For a finite <module>, $|NM|=|M|/|\ker N|$ and $|DM|=|M|/|\ker D|$ show that the two <Tate cohomology> <groups> have equal order. Thus the <Herbrand quotient of a finite module> is one. For the trivial integral <module> $\mathbb Z$, the two groups are $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ and zero, giving $h_G(\mathbb Z)=n$.

We also need <cyclic cohomology of a regular lattice>. In $R[G]$, fixed coefficient vectors are constant, and every such vector is a norm of a basis vector times its coefficient. The <kernel> of $N$ consists of vectors whose coefficient sum is zero. For such a vector $(a_i)$, solve $b_{i-1}-b_i=a_i$ successively around the cycle; the final equation holds precisely because $\sum_i a_i=0$. Thus $\ker N=DR[G]$. Both <Tate cohomology> <groups> vanish, so $h_G(R[G])=1$, for $R=\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb Z_p$, and likewise for finite direct sums.

Now take a cyclic extension $L/K$ of <p-adic fields> of degree $n$. Write $U_L^r=1+\mathfrak p_L^r$. For $r>v_L(p)/(p-1)$, the convergent <p-adic logarithm> and <p-adic exponential> give mutually inverse $G$-equivariant <group isomorphisms>
$$
U_L^r\cong\mathfrak p_L^r,
$$
where the right side is additive. The convergence assertion follows from the estimates $v_L(x^j/j)\ge jv_L(x)-v_L(p)v_p(j)$ and $v_L(x^j/j!)\ge jv_L(x)-v_L(p)j/(p-1)$; formal inverse identities therefore hold on this ball.

By the <normal basis theorem>, choose $\theta$ so that $\{\sigma^i\theta\}$ is a $K$-<basis> of $L$. The lattice $A=\sum_i\mathcal O_K\sigma^i\theta$ is a regular $\mathcal O_K[G]$-<module>. It is a finite direct sum of regular $\mathbb Z_p[G]$-<modules>, hence its <Tate cohomology> vanishes. The lattices $A$ and $\mathfrak p_L^r$ are commensurable: their intersection has finite index in both. The exact sequences with these finite quotients establish finiteness of the <Tate cohomology> <groups> and show $h_G(\mathfrak p_L^r)=1$. Thus $h_G(U_L^r)=1$. Since $\mathcal O_L^\times/U_L^r$ is finite, $h_G(\mathcal O_L^\times)=1$ as well.

The $G$-equivariant <valuation> exact sequence is
$$
1\longrightarrow\mathcal O_L^\times\longrightarrow L^\times\xrightarrow{v_L}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
$$
Its last term has trivial $G$-action. The multiplication rule gives the <Herbrand quotient of the local multiplicative group>:
$$
h_G(L^\times)=n.
$$
All the <Tate cohomology> <groups> here are finite by the lattice argument and the exact sequence; finiteness of the norm index has not been assumed.

For completeness we prove the needed <Hilbert theorem 90>. If $a\in L^\times$ has <field norm> one, put $A_0=1$ and $A_i=\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\sigma^j(a)$. The linear map $c\mapsto b=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}A_i\sigma^i(c)$ is not zero by <linear independence of distinct field embeddings>. Choose $c$ with $b\ne0$. The relation $A_n=1$ gives $a\sigma(b)=b$, so $a=b/\sigma(b)$. This proves $\widehat H^{-1}(G,L^\times)=0$. The zeroth <Tate cohomology> <group> is $K^\times/N_{L/K}L^\times$. Consequently the <cyclic local norm index> is
$$
\boxed{[K^\times:N_{L/K}L^\times]=[L:K]=n.}
$$
Writing $n=ef$, the <valuation> of a <field norm> is $v_K(Nx)=f v_L(x)$. The valuation quotient has order $f$, and a norm with <valuation> zero comes from a unit. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[\mathcal O_K^\times:N_{L/K}\mathcal O_L^\times]=e.}
$$
In particular every unit is a norm in an <unramified extension>. These results give the norm assertions used in the <quadratic Hilbert symbol> computation.

We finish with the <Hasse norm theorem>: **for a cyclic extension of number fields, an element is a global norm if and only if it is a norm at every place**. More precisely the local norm is the norm from the product $L\otimes_K K_v$; in a <Galois extension> its factors have the same norm subgroup. The forward implication is immediate. For the converse associate to $a\in K^\times$ the <cyclic algebra> $(L/K,\sigma,a)$. The <splitting criterion for a cyclic algebra>, proved below, identifies its splitting with $a\in N_{L/K}L^\times$. At a place where the tensor product of fields has several factors, this <algebra> is a matrix algebra over the <cyclic algebra> of one decomposition-field factor, with the generator restricted to its cyclic decomposition subgroup. Thus the local norm assumption makes its <Brauer class> zero at every place. Injectivity in the <Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem> makes the global <Brauer class> zero, giving the desired global norm. This is the required proof outline; cyclicity is essential to this reduction.

A <central simple algebra> over $K$ is a finite-dimensional associative $K$-<algebra> with no nonzero proper two-sided <ideals> and with center $K$. The <Artin–Wedderburn theorem> writes it uniquely as $M_r(D)$ with $D$ a central <division algebra>. After a finite separable <splitting field> extension it becomes $M_d$, so its dimension is $d^2$, and $d$ is its <degree of a central simple algebra>. Passing from $D$ to a <matrix algebra> over $D$ does not change its <Brauer class>.

The <Brauer group> consists of these classes, with product induced by <tensor product>. Extending to a common <splitting field> shows that a <tensor product of central simple algebras> is again central simple; its two-sided <ideals> and center descend from the corresponding full <matrix algebra>. The neutral class is $[K]$, and the inverse of $[A]$ is $[A^{\mathrm{op}}]$, because
$$
A\otimes_K A^{\mathrm{op}}\cong\operatorname{End}_K(A),\qquad a\otimes b\mapsto(x\mapsto axb).
$$
The displayed map becomes the standard isomorphism for matrices over a <splitting field>, so it is already an isomorphism over $K$. Thus <Brauer classes> measure precisely the obstruction to being a split <matrix algebra>.

An explicit family is supplied by <cyclic algebras>. Let $L/K$ be cyclic of degree $n$, with generator $\sigma$, and let $a\in K^\times$. Define
$$
A=(L/K,\sigma,a)=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n-1}Lu^i,\qquad ux=\sigma(x)u,\qquad u^n=a.
$$
This is an associative <algebra>: form the skew polynomial ring with relation $ux=\sigma(x)u$ and quotient by the central relation $u^n-a$. Reduction of powers gives the displayed $n^2$-dimensional $K$-<basis>. An element commuting with every $x\in L$ has no $u^i$ coefficient for $i\ne0$, since $\sigma^i$ is not the identity. Commuting also with $u$ forces the remaining coefficient into $K$. Hence the center is $K$.

Here is an explicit splitting construction. On $L^n$, with <basis> $e_0,\ldots,e_{n-1}$, put
$$
D(x)e_j=\sigma^j(x)e_j,\qquad Ue_j=e_{j-1}\ (j\ge1),\qquad Ue_0=a e_{n-1}.
$$
Then $UD(x)=D(\sigma x)U$ and $U^n=aI$. After extending scalars to $L$, the separable decomposition $L\otimes_KL\cong\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}L$ supplies all diagonal matrices, and their products with powers of $U$ supply all <matrix units>. Therefore
$$
A\otimes_KL\cong M_n(L).
$$
Any nonzero proper two-sided <ideal> of $A$ would extend to one of $M_n(L)$, which is impossible. Thus this construction really gives a <central simple algebra>, with <splitting field> $L$.

Its central arithmetic feature is the <splitting criterion for a cyclic algebra>:
$$
\boxed{(L/K,\sigma,a)\text{ splits}\iff a\in N_{L/K}L^\times.}
$$
If $a=Nc$, let $L$ act by multiplication on the $K$-<vector space> $L$, and let $u$ act as $c\sigma$. Its nth power is multiplication by $Nc=a$, so this gives a representation $A\to\operatorname{End}_K(L)$. It is injective by simplicity and an isomorphism by equal dimension. Conversely, if $A\cong\operatorname{End}_K(V)$ with $\dim_K V=n$, its embedded field $L$ makes $V$ one-dimensional over $L$. The relation for $u$ says that $u$ is $\sigma$-semilinear. In an $L$-<basis> it must have the form $c\sigma$, whose nth power is $Nc$. The relation $u^n=a$ forces $a=Nc$.

More generally the <crossed-product algebra of a Galois extension> attaches an <algebra> to a multiplicative two-cocycle. Changing the cocycle by a coboundary rescales its basis and preserves the <Brauer class>. Descent of a split <matrix algebra> identifies
$$
\ker\bigl(\operatorname{Br}(K)\to\operatorname{Br}(L)\bigr)\cong H^2(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K),L^\times).
$$
For cyclic $L/K$, cyclic <Tate cohomology> identifies this second <cohomology group> with $K^\times/NL^\times$. In a crossed product, rescale successive cyclic basis elements to powers of a single $u$; the remaining datum is $u^n=a$, changed by a <field norm> when $u$ is rescaled. This explains why <cyclic algebras> give every class split by a cyclic extension, and why multiplication of their parameters is addition of their <Brauer classes>.

For a <finite field> $K=\mathbb F_q$, every finite <splitting field> $L=\mathbb F_{q^n}$ is cyclic over $K$. The <field norm> is the power map $x\mapsto x^{(q^n-1)/(q-1)}$ on its cyclic multiplicative <group>, and is onto $\mathbb F_q^\times$. Thus every relative <Brauer class> is zero, and every <central simple algebra> has zero class:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb F_q)=0.}
$$

For a non-Archimedean <local field> $K$, the valuation classification of central <division algebras> shows that every <Brauer class> splits over a finite <unramified extension>. For the degree-$n$ <unramified extension> $L/K$ with arithmetic <Frobenius> $\sigma$, units are norms and norm <valuations> are multiples of $n$. Hence the cyclic relative <Brauer group> is $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$, represented by $a=\pi^r$. The compatible <local Brauer invariant> is
$$
\operatorname{inv}_K[(L/K,\sigma,a)]=\frac{v_K(a)}n\pmod{\mathbb Z}.
$$
The union over all $n$ gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(K)\cong\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z.}
$$
A class with invariant $r/n$ in lowest terms has a central <division algebra> representative of <degree of a central simple algebra> $n$. This is the local valuation classification used above; the explicit <cyclic algebras> exhibit all of its invariants. At the Archimedean places, $\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb C)=0$, while $\operatorname{Br}(\mathbb R)=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$: its nontrivial class is the <quaternion algebra> $(\mathbb C/\mathbb R,\text{conjugation},-1)$, since complex <norms> are positive.

For a <number field>, the <Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorem> describes the <Brauer group> by the exact sequence
$$
0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Br}(K)\longrightarrow\bigoplus_v\operatorname{Br}(K_v)\xrightarrow{\ \sum_v\operatorname{inv}_v\ }\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus its classes are exactly finite-support families of local invariants with sum zero; real invariants are $0$ or $1/2$ and complex invariants are zero. Every global <Brauer class> has a cyclic splitting extension, so <cyclic algebras> also realize all global classes. If $\chi(\sigma)=1/n$, their local invariants are $\chi_v(\operatorname{rec}_{K_v}(a))$, with $\chi_v$ restricted to the local decomposition subgroup. The sum-zero relation for a principal parameter is the invariant form of <Artin reciprocity>. Injectivity supplies the local-to-global splitting test, which yields the <Hasse norm theorem> for cyclic extensions as explained in the preceding solution. Here the local valuation classification and the global invariant theorem are the structural theorems used to describe the <Brauer groups>; the construction and norm-splitting calculation have been proved explicitly.

Fix a prime $\mathfrak q$ of $E$ over $\mathfrak p$, and a prime $\mathfrak r$ of $LE$ over $\mathfrak q$. Let $\mathfrak P$ be its restriction to $L$. Inside the completed compositum, put
$$
F=K_{\mathfrak p},\qquad J=E_{\mathfrak q},\qquad U=L_{\mathfrak P}.
$$
The assumption means $U/F$ is an <unramified extension>. Its <residue degree> is some $f$; the completed extension $(LE)_{\mathfrak r}/J$ is the corresponding compositum $UJ/J$. It suffices to prove that this is <unramified>, regardless of the <ramification> of $J/F$.

Here is a proof of <unramified extensions remain unramified under base change> using the classification already established. Choose an integral generator $\alpha$ of $U/F$ whose monic <minimal polynomial> $g\in\mathcal O_F[X]$ has irreducible separable reduction of degree $f$. Write $k_F=\mathbb F_q$ and $k_J=\mathbb F_{q^r}$. Let $J_f/J$ be the degree-$f$ <unramified extension>. Its <residue field> $\mathbb F_{q^{rf}}$ contains $\mathbb F_{q^f}$, so $\bar g$ has a simple root there. <Hensel lemma> lifts this root to a root of $g$ in $J_f$. This gives an $F$-embedding $U\hookrightarrow J_f$.

An <unramified extension> of a <local field> is <Galois>, so all $F$-embeddings of $U$ into a fixed algebraic closure have the same image. The original $U$ therefore lies in $J_f$, and $UJ$ is an intermediate field of the <unramified extension> $J_f/J$. Its <ramification index> is one. Since $\mathfrak q$ and $\mathfrak r$ were arbitrary,
$$
\boxed{e(\mathfrak r/\mathfrak q)=1\text{ for every prime above }\mathfrak p.}
$$
This proof also explains why no linear-disjointness assumption on $L$ and $E$, and no assumption that $E/K$ is <unramified>, is needed.

A <modulus of a number field> is $\mathfrak m=\mathfrak m_0\mathfrak m_\infty$, with $\mathfrak m_0$ a nonzero integral <ideal> and $\mathfrak m_\infty$ a subset of the real places. Let $I_{\mathfrak m}$ be the <group> of <fractional ideals> prime to $\mathfrak m_0$, and let $P_{\mathfrak m}$ consist of principal <ideals> $(a)$ with $a\equiv1$ at each prime power in $\mathfrak m_0$ and $a>0$ at each selected real place. The <ray class group> is
$$
\operatorname{Cl}_{\mathfrak m}(K)=I_{\mathfrak m}/P_{\mathfrak m}.
$$
The <ray class field> $K_{\mathfrak m}$ is the finite <abelian extension> for which the <Artin map> has <kernel> $P_{\mathfrak m}$ and induces an isomorphism $\operatorname{Cl}_{\mathfrak m}(K)\cong\operatorname{Gal}(K_{\mathfrak m}/K)$. Equivalently it is the maximal <abelian extension> whose conductor divides $\mathfrak m$.

To compute its order use the <ray class exact sequence>, including signatures:
$$
\mathcal O_K^\times\longrightarrow (\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak m_0)^\times\times\{\pm1\}^{|\mathfrak m_\infty|}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}_{\mathfrak m}(K)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(K)\longrightarrow1.
$$
The middle map lifts a residue and sign tuple to an element of $K$ by <weak approximation theorem>, then takes its principal <ideal> as a ray class. Different lifts differ by a principal ray factor. Its <kernel> consists exactly of tuples represented by global units. Every ordinary <ideal class> has a representative prime to the finite modulus, again by <weak approximation theorem>; this gives surjectivity of the last map. If $U_{\mathfrak m}$ denotes the <ray units>, the kernel of the unit map is $U_{\mathfrak m}$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> and counting units in each prime-power quotient give the <ray class number formula>
$$
\boxed{|\operatorname{Cl}_{\mathfrak m}(K)|=\frac{h_K\,2^{|\mathfrak m_\infty|}\,\Phi_K(\mathfrak m_0)}{[\mathcal O_K^\times:U_{\mathfrak m}]},\qquad \Phi_K(\mathfrak m_0)=N\mathfrak m_0\prod_{\mathfrak p\mid\mathfrak m_0}\left(1-\frac1{N\mathfrak p}\right).}
$$

First take $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$. Its <ring of integers> is $\mathbb Z[\omega]$, where $\omega=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ and $\omega^2+\omega+1=0$. The <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> is $(2/\pi)\sqrt3<2$, so every <ideal class> has a representative of norm one and $h_K=1$. Its units are the six <roots of unity>. Modulo seven, the polynomial $X^2+X+1$ has distinct roots $2,4$, and therefore
$$
\mathcal O_K/(7)\cong\mathbb F_7\times\mathbb F_7,\qquad \omega\mapsto(2,4).
$$
The <unit group> of this quotient has order $36$. The unit $1+\omega$ maps to $(3,5)$, which has order six; consequently unit reduction is injective and $U_{(7)}=\{1\}$. The <ray class number formula> gives $36/6=6$.

We also identify the quotient explicitly. Conjugation interchanges the two factors, so the residue <norm> is $(x,y)\mapsto xy$. Its <kernel> has order six and equals the image of the six units, since $3\cdot5=1\pmod7$. Hence
$$
\operatorname{Cl}_{(7)}(K)\cong\mathbb F_7^\times\cong C_6.
$$
The field $M=K(\zeta_7)=\mathbb Q(\zeta_{21})$ has degree six over $K$, since the degrees over $\mathbb Q$ are twelve and two. It is <unramified> outside seven by the cyclotomic computation and the base-change argument above. At primes over seven, $K$ has completion $\mathbb Q_7$, and $M/K$ is the tame degree-six cyclotomic extension; its conductor exponent is one. Its relative arithmetic <Artin symbol> at an <ideal> $\mathfrak a$ prime to seven acts by
$$
\zeta_7\longmapsto\zeta_7^{N\mathfrak a\bmod7}.
$$
If $\mathfrak a=(a)$ with $a\equiv1\pmod{7\mathcal O_K}$, then $N_{K/\mathbb Q}(a)\equiv1\pmod7$. Thus the <Artin map> kills the principal ray subgroup. It factors through the order-six <ray class group>; the residue <norm> quotient computed above shows that it is onto the degree-six <Galois group>, so the fields coincide. This proves the explicit <ray class field of Q of square root minus three modulo seven>:
$$
\boxed{K_{(7)}=K(\zeta_7)=\mathbb Q(\zeta_{21}),\qquad[K_{(7)}:K]=6.}
$$

Now take $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-6})$, and write $s=\sqrt{-6}$. Its <ring of integers> is $\mathbb Z[s]$, its <discriminant> is $-24$, and its units are $\pm1$. The quotient by $(s)$ is
$$
\mathbb Z[s]/(s)\cong\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z,
$$
whose two units are both images of $\pm1$. The <ray class exact sequence> therefore gives $\operatorname{Cl}_{(s)}(K)\cong\operatorname{Cl}(K)$.

For its <class number>, the <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> is $(2/\pi)\sqrt{24}<4$, so each <ideal class> contains an integral <ideal> of norm at most three. The primes of norms two and three are
$$
\mathfrak p_2=(2,s),\qquad\mathfrak p_3=(3,s),\qquad\mathfrak p_2^2=(2),\quad\mathfrak p_3^2=(3),\quad\mathfrak p_2\mathfrak p_3=(s).
$$
These are the only nontrivial possibilities within the bound, and their <ideal classes> coincide and have order at most two. The <ideal> $\mathfrak p_2$ is not principal: its being principal would give an integer solution to $x^2+6y^2=2$, which is impossible. Thus $h_K=2$, the <ideal class group> is $C_2$, and the ray field is the <Hilbert class field>.

We verify its actual identity rather than stopping at the degree. Put
$$
H=K(\sqrt2)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3}),\qquad b=\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}2.
$$
The two quadratic fields $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$ are distinct, so $[H:\mathbb Q]=4$ and $[H:K]=2$. The integral elements $1,\sqrt2,b,\sqrt2b$ are a rational <basis>. Their trace matrix is the <Kronecker product> of the two quadratic trace matrices, with <discriminants> $8$ and $-3$. Its <determinant> is therefore $8^2(-3)^2=576$. The <discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice> shows that the field <discriminant> divides $576$. On the other hand the relative <discriminant ideal> formula gives
$$
|D_H|=|D_K|^{[H:K]}N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\mathfrak d_{H/K})=576\,N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\mathfrak d_{H/K}).
$$
The norm on the right is a positive integer, so equality is forced. Thus the relative <discriminant ideal> is the unit ideal, and $H/K$ is <unramified> at every finite prime. There are no real places of $K$. The extension is quadratic and therefore <abelian>; its degree equals $h_K$, identifying it with the <Hilbert class field of Q of square root minus six>. Hence the <ray class field of Q of square root minus six modulo square root minus six> is
$$
\boxed{K_{(\sqrt{-6})}=K(\sqrt2)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3}),\qquad[K_{(\sqrt{-6})}:K]=2.}
$$
