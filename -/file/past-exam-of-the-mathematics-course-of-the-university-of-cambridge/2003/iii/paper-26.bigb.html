= Solution
{parent=a}

The standard <CW complex> structure on <complex projective space> $\mathbb{CP}^3$ has one cell in each dimension $0,2,4,6$ and no other cells. Its <cellular chain complex> therefore has zero differentials: each boundary has an odd-dimensional target group, which is zero. The product of the usual two-cell structures on $S^2$ and $S^4$ likewise has exactly one cell in dimensions $0,2,4,6$. Thus <cellular homology> gives, for both spaces,
$$
\boxed{H_i(-;\mathbb Z)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb Z,&i=0,2,4,6,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
The <cohomology rings> detect more than these additive groups. Let $c\in H^2(\mathbb{CP}^3;\mathbb Z)$ be the hyperplane class. The <cohomology ring of complex projective space> is
$$
H^*(\mathbb{CP}^3;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z[c]/(c^4),\qquad |c|=2.
$$
For the normalization, a hyperplane is <Poincare dual> to $c$; intersecting three generic hyperplanes gives one point with positive complex <orientation>, so $\langle c^3,[\mathbb{CP}^3]\rangle=1$. Together with the computed additive groups this makes $c,c^2,c^3$ generators in their respective degrees.

Let $u$ and $v$ be the pullbacks of the <orientation> classes of $S^2$ and $S^4$. The <Künneth theorem> and naturality of the <cup product> give
$$
H^*(S^2\times S^4;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z[u,v]/(u^2,v^2),\qquad |u|=2,\quad |v|=4,
$$
where $uv$ generates degree six. In particular, every degree-two class on the product has square zero, whereas $c^2\ne0$. Any graded ring isomorphism would send $c$ to $\pm u$, contradicting this product relation. A <homotopy equivalence> induces an isomorphism of <cohomology rings>, so **the spaces have isomorphic integral <homology> but are not <homotopy> equivalent**.

For a <continuous map> $f:S^2\times S^4\to\mathbb{CP}^3$, use the classes from part (a). Since $H^2(S^2\times S^4;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Zu$, there is an <integer> $k$ with $f^*c=ku$. Naturality of the <cup product> and $u^2=0$ yield
$$
f^*(c^3)=(f^*c)^3=k^3u^3=0.
$$
With the product <orientation> on the source and the complex <orientation> on the target, the <mapping degree> $d$ is defined by $f_*[S^2\times S^4]=d[\mathbb{CP}^3]$. Evaluation on the <fundamental class> gives
$$
d=\langle c^3,f_*[S^2\times S^4]\rangle
=\langle f^*(c^3),[S^2\times S^4]\rangle=0.
$$
Thus **the induced <group homomorphism> on $H_6$ is zero**. This is a <cup-power obstruction to nonzero degree>, rather than a consequence of the additive <homology groups> alone.

A <simply connected> manifold is connected and orientable: its <orientation> character is a <group homomorphism> $\pi_1(X)\to\{\pm1\}$, and this group is trivial. Choose an <orientation>. The <Hurewicz theorem> in degree one gives $H_1(X;\mathbb Z)=0$. The <universal coefficient theorem for cohomology> then gives $H^1(X;\mathbb Z)=0$ and
$$
H^2(X;\mathbb Z)\cong\operatorname{Hom}(H_2(X;\mathbb Z),\mathbb Z),
$$
because the possible $\operatorname{Ext}(H_1(X),\mathbb Z)$ term is zero.

The <homology groups> are finitely generated for a closed manifold. The displayed dual group is therefore free abelian. <Poincare duality> identifies $H_2(X;\mathbb Z)$ with $H^2(X;\mathbb Z)$, so $H_2$ itself has no torsion. Since the second <Betti number> is one, both groups are isomorphic to $\mathbb Z$. This torsion argument is essential: rank one by itself would not rule out finite summands.

Again by <Poincare duality>, $H^3(X;\mathbb Z)\cong H_1(X;\mathbb Z)=0$, and $H^4(X;\mathbb Z)\cong H_0(X;\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Z$. Thus the only nonzero <cohomology groups> are $\mathbb Z$ in degrees $0,2,4$.

Let $u$ generate $H^2$. The integral <intersection form>
$$
Q(a,b)=\langle a\smile b,[X]\rangle
$$
is unimodular. Indeed, cap product with $[X]$ is the <Poincare duality> isomorphism to $H_2$, and the preceding universal-coefficient identification makes evaluation the full integral dual. Composing these isomorphisms identifies the pairing with an isomorphism $H^2\to\operatorname{Hom}(H^2,\mathbb Z)$. In rank one its matrix must therefore be $[1]$ or $[-1]$. Consequently $\langle u^2,[X]\rangle=\pm1$, so $u^2$ generates $H^4$.

All products in degrees above four vanish. Hence the assignment of the degree-two generator gives
$$
\boxed{H^*(X;\mathbb Z)\cong\mathbb Z[u]/(u^3),\qquad |u|=2,}
$$
which is the <cohomology ring of complex projective space> for $\mathbb{CP}^2$. If the intersection form is negative, this ring isomorphism reverses the chosen top <orientation> generator; preserving that additional <orientation> is not part of the requested ring isomorphism.

= Solution

Use integral <singular homology>. The <singular chain group> $C_n(B)$ is the <free abelian group> on continuous maps $\Delta^n\to B$, and its <boundary operator> is
$$
\partial\sigma=\sum_{j=0}^n(-1)^j\sigma|_{[0,\ldots,\widehat j,\ldots,n]}.
$$
For $i<j$, deleting vertex $j$ and then vertex $i$ has sign $(-1)^{i+j}$, while deleting $i$ and then the shifted vertex $j-1$ has sign $(-1)^{i+j-1}$. Both yield the same codimension-two face. Pairing these terms proves $\partial^2=0$. The inclusion $A\subset B$ identifies $C_n(A)$ with the subgroup spanned by those simplices having image in $A$, so it is injective and preserved by $\partial$.

The <relative chain complex> is $C_n(B,A)=C_n(B)/C_n(A)$ with induced boundary $\partial\bar b=\overline{\partial b}$. Thus there is a degreewise <short exact sequence of chain complexes>
$$
0\longrightarrow C_*(A)\xrightarrow{i}C_*(B)\xrightarrow{q}C_*(B,A)\longrightarrow0.
$$
Inclusion and quotient take <homological cycles> to <homological cycles> and boundaries to boundaries, hence define the <group homomorphisms> $i_*$ and $q_*$ on <homology>. We construct and prove exactness of the resulting sequence directly, rather than invoke an unproved algebraic lemma:
$$
\cdots\longrightarrow H_{n+1}(B,A)\xrightarrow{\delta}H_n(A)\xrightarrow{i_*}H_n(B)\xrightarrow{q_*}H_n(B,A)\xrightarrow{\delta}H_{n-1}(A)\longrightarrow\cdots
$$
ending in $H_0(A)\to H_0(B)\to H_0(B,A)\to0$.

For the <relative homology connecting homomorphism>, lift a <relative cycle> $\bar b\in C_n(B,A)$ to $b\in C_n(B)$. Since $\partial\bar b=0$, the chain $\partial b$ belongs to $C_{n-1}(A)$; it is a <homological cycle> because $\partial^2=0$. Define
$$
\delta[\bar b]=[\partial b]\in H_{n-1}(A).
$$
Changing the lift by $a\in C_n(A)$ changes the boundary by $\partial a$, hence does not change its <homology> class. More generally, if a different <relative cycle> represents the same <relative homology class>, lifts satisfy $b'-b=\partial c+a$ for some $c\in C_{n+1}(B)$ and $a\in C_n(A)$. Then $\partial b'-\partial b=\partial a$. This proves independence both of the lift and of the <homology> representative. Lifting a sum by the sum of lifts proves additivity, so $\delta$ is a well-defined <group homomorphism>.

At $H_n(A)$, an element in the image of $\delta$ is represented by $\partial b$ for some $b\in C_{n+1}(B)$, so its image in $H_n(B)$ is zero. Conversely, if a <homological cycle> in $A$ $a$ has $i_*[a]=0$, then $a=\partial b$ for a $B$-chain $b\in C_{n+1}(B)$. Its quotient $\bar b$ is a <relative cycle> and $\delta[\bar b]=[a]$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}\bigl(\delta:H_{n+1}(B,A)\to H_n(A)\bigr)=\ker i_*.}
$$
For completeness, the other terms are exact by the same elementary chain argument. At $H_n(B)$, clearly $q_*i_*=0$. If a <homological cycle> in $B$ $b$ has zero relative class, then $\bar b=\partial\bar c$ for a quotient chain $\bar c$. Lifting $c$ gives $a=b-\partial c\in C_n(A)$, and $\partial a=0$. Hence $[b]=i_*[a]$, proving $\ker q_*=\operatorname{im}i_*$. At $H_n(B,A)$, absolute <homological cycles> have zero connecting image. Conversely, if $\delta[\bar b]=0$, then $\partial b=\partial a$ for some $a\in C_n(A)$; the chain $b-a$ is an absolute <homological cycle> and maps to $[\bar b]$. Therefore $\ker\delta=\operatorname{im}q_*$. In degree zero every quotient chain lifts to an absolute <homological cycle>, because the differential to degree $-1$ is zero, so $q_*:H_0(B)\to H_0(B,A)$ is surjective. These proofs establish the whole <long exact sequence in relative homology> and all the algebraic facts used in its construction.

Here closed oriented surfaces are connected, as in the usual single-genus convention. Let $f:X\to Y$ have nonzero <mapping degree> $d$. Work over $\mathbb Q$, so multiplication by $d$ is invertible and cannot vanish.

The <Poincare duality pairing> on $H^1(Y;\mathbb Q)$ is nondegenerate. Thus for every $0\ne a\in H^1(Y;\mathbb Q)$ there is $b\in H^1(Y;\mathbb Q)$ with $\langle a\smile b,[Y]\rangle\ne0$. Naturality of the <cup product> and evaluation on the <fundamental class> give
$$
\langle f^*a\smile f^*b,[X]\rangle
=\langle f^*(a\smile b),[X]\rangle
=\langle a\smile b,f_*[X]\rangle
=d\langle a\smile b,[Y]\rangle\ne0.
$$
In particular $f^*a\ne0$, so $f^*:H^1(Y;\mathbb Q)\to H^1(X;\mathbb Q)$ is injective. The <cohomology ring of a closed oriented surface> has $\dim H^1(\Sigma_g;\mathbb Q)=2g$. Comparing dimensions now gives
$$
2g(Y)\le2g(X),\qquad\boxed{g(X)\ge g(Y).}
$$
This proves <genus monotonicity under nonzero-degree surface maps> by <rational cohomology injectivity of a nonzero-degree map>, without requiring $f$ to be a covering map or a smooth map.

A <vector field> on the <sphere> means a tangent <vector field>. For $S^{2m}\subset\mathbb R^{2m+1}$ with $m\ge1$, suppose $V(x)$ were continuous and nowhere zero. Normalize it to $w(x)=V(x)/\|V(x)\|$. Tangency means $\langle x,w(x)\rangle=0$, and both vectors have norm one. Therefore
$$
H(x,t)=\cos(\pi t)x+\sin(\pi t)w(x),\qquad 0\le t\le1,
$$
has squared norm $\cos^2(\pi t)+\sin^2(\pi t)=1$. It defines a <homotopy> through self-maps of the <sphere> from the identity to the <antipodal map>.

The identity has <mapping degree> $1$. The antipodal map on $S^n$ has degree $(-1)^{n+1}$: it is the boundary restriction of $-I$ on the oriented $(n+1)$-ball, and this <linear map> changes <orientation> by $\operatorname{sgn}\det(-I)=(-1)^{n+1}$. For $n=2m$ its degree is $-1$. <Homotopy invariance of mapping degree> would force $1=-1$, a contradiction. Thus **every continuous tangent field on an even-dimensional <sphere> vanishes somewhere**. For $S^0$ the tangent spaces are zero, so the conclusion holds directly. This proves the <hairy ball theorem> using degree, rather than assuming it.

Identify the ambient <Euclidean space> $\mathbb R^{2m+2}$ with $\mathbb C^{m+1}$ and let $J$ be multiplication by $i$. Equivalently, in real coordinates,
$$
J(x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_{2m},x_{2m+1})=(-x_1,x_0,\ldots,-x_{2m+1},x_{2m}).
$$
This <linear map> preserves the norm and satisfies $\langle x,Jx\rangle=0$. On the unit <sphere> $S^{2m+1}$ define
$$
\boxed{V(x)=Jx.}
$$
It is continuous, tangent because it lies in $x^\perp=T_xS^{2m+1}$, and has norm one everywhere. It is therefore a <nowhere-zero section> of the <tangent bundle>, including when $m=0$ and the <sphere> is $S^1$. This supplies a <nonvanishing tangent field on an odd-dimensional sphere> explicitly.

An <ellipse> here is a <nondegenerate ellipse>, hence a smooth embedded circle. Write the first <ellipse> as
$$
\gamma(t)=c+A\begin{pmatrix}\cos t\\\sin t\end{pmatrix},\qquad t\in\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z),
$$
where $A$ is invertible. Write the second as $Q(x)=0$, where
$$
Q(x)=(x-d)^TB(x-d)-1
$$
for a positive-definite symmetric matrix $B$. Its gradient is nonzero on the <ellipse>. Let $g(t)=Q(\gamma(t))$. At an intersection, <transversality> says the tangent vector $\gamma'(t)$ is not tangent to $Q=0$, so
$$
g'(t)=\nabla Q(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)\ne0.
$$
Every intersection is thus a simple zero of $g$, and $g$ changes sign there. The intersection set is finite: it is closed and compact, and <transversality> makes each of its points isolated. Start at a parameter with $g\ne0$ and traverse one full period. Returning to the same sign requires an even number of sign changes. Therefore **the number of intersection points is even**, including zero when the <ellipses> are disjoint.

For the upper bound, substituting the parametrization into the quadratic gives the <trigonometric polynomial>
$$
g(t)=a_0+a_1\cos t+b_1\sin t+a_2\cos2t+b_2\sin2t.
$$
It is not identically zero: otherwise the first <ellipse> would lie on the second, and its tangent would be tangent to the second everywhere, violating <transversality>. Put $z=e^{it}$. Multiplying by $z^2$ turns this function into the <polynomial>
$$
P(z)=\frac{a_2-ib_2}{2}z^4+\frac{a_1-ib_1}{2}z^3+a_0z^2+\frac{a_1+ib_1}{2}z+\frac{a_2+ib_2}{2}.
$$
It is nonzero and has degree at most four, so it has at most four distinct roots. Distinct points on the first <ellipse> correspond to distinct parameters modulo $2\pi$ and hence distinct points $z$ on the unit circle. Thus
$$
\boxed{|E_1\cap E_2|\in\{0,2,4\}.}
$$
Both the parity and the upper bound follow without using an algebraic intersection theorem. The four-point bound is attained by a unit circle and a concentric <ellipse> whose semiaxes lie on opposite sides of one.
