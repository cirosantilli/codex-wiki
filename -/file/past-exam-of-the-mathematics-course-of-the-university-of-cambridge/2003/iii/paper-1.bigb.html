Put $A_k=x\otimes x^{3-k}y^k$ and $B_k=y\otimes x^{3-k}y^k$ for $0\le k\le3$. These eight vectors are a <basis> of $U$; their weights are $4-2k$ and $2-2k$. Thus the <weight-space decomposition> and a <weight basis> for each summand are

4&A_0&1\\
2&A_1,\ B_0&2\\
0&A_2,\ B_1&2\\
-2&A_3,\ B_2&2\\
-4&B_3&1
\end{array}
$$
In particular $\boxed{U=U_4\oplus U_2\oplus U_0\oplus U_{-2}\oplus U_{-4}}$, with dimensions summing to eight. Here the subscripts are the eigenvalues of the chosen Cartan element $h$.

In the notation of part (i), the tensor <raising operator> and <lowering operator> act by
$$
eA_k=kA_{k-1},\quad eB_k=A_k+kB_{k-1},\qquad fA_k=B_k+(3-k)A_{k+1},\quad fB_k=(3-k)B_{k+1},
$$
with terms beyond the stated index range omitted. Both $v_4=A_0$ and $v_2=B_0-A_1$ are killed by $e$, and have respective weights four and two. They are the desired <highest-weight vectors>.

For $v_4$, successive lowering gives
$$
fv_4=3A_1+B_0,\quad f^2v_4=6(A_2+B_1),\quad f^3v_4=6(A_3+3B_2),\quad f^4v_4=24B_3,\quad f^5v_4=0.
$$
Thus a <basis> of the five-dimensional summand, in descending weight order, is
$$
\boxed{L(4)=\operatorname{span}\{A_0,\ 3A_1+B_0,\ A_2+B_1,\ A_3+3B_2,\ B_3\},\quad v_4=A_0.}
$$
For $v_2$, the successive vectors are $fv_2=2(B_1-A_2)$, $f^2v_2=2(B_2-A_3)$ and $f^3v_2=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{L(2)=\operatorname{span}\{B_0-A_1,\ B_1-A_2,\ B_2-A_3\},\quad v_2=B_0-A_1.}
$$
These spans are invariant under $h,e,f$. The <sl2 highest-weight lowering formula> gives $ef^rv_n=r(n-r+1)f^{r-1}v_n$, so all interior raising coefficients in either chain are nonzero. Any nonzero submodule therefore contains the highest vector and then the whole chain; each span is irreducible.

At the shared weights $2,0,-2$, the two displayed summand vectors are independent: their coefficient determinants in the original weight bases are $4,2,4$. At weights $4,-4$ only the five-dimensional summand occurs. Consequently the eight displayed vectors form a basis of $U$, proving that these are complementary submodules, not only modules with the right dimensions.

Define an equivariant contraction $C:V^*\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^2V\to V$ by
$$
C(\varphi\otimes uv)=\varphi(u)v+\varphi(v)u.
$$
This uses the natural dual pairing, so it commutes with the <Lie algebra representation>. Also define $s(v)=\sum_i e_i^*\otimes e_iv$. The identity tensor $\sum_i e_i^*\otimes e_i$ is invariant, making $s$ equivariant. Direct contraction gives $C(s(v))=3v+v=4v$. Thus $s$ is injective and $C$ is surjective, and
$$
w=\left(w-\frac14s(Cw)\right)+\frac14s(Cw)

is a direct decomposition into $\ker C$ and $s(V)$. In particular $\dim\ker C=18-3=15$ and $s(V)$ is a defining irreducible module of <highest weight> $\omega_1$.

For the usual upper-triangular choice of positive roots, $T_3^{11}=z^*\otimes x^2$ lies in the kernel and is killed by both simple raising operators $E_{12},E_{23}$. The nine one-dimensional weight spaces from part (i) all lie in $\ker C$, because the dual index differs from every polynomial index. Their bases remain $T_i^{jj}$ at weight $2\epsilon_j-\epsilon_i$ for $i\ne j$, and $T_i^{jk}$ at weight $-2\epsilon_i$ for three distinct indices $i,j,k$.

At weight $\epsilon_i$, the full space has basis $T_i^{ii},T_j^{ij},T_k^{ik}$, where $j,k$ are the two other indices. Their contractions are $2e_i,e_i,e_i$. Thus the contraction kernel has the independent basis $K_{ij}=2T_j^{ij}-T_i^{ii}$ and $K_{ik}=2T_k^{ik}-T_i^{ii}$. Explicitly the <weight basis of the trace-free symmetric-square dual tensor module> is completed by
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{weight}&\text{basis in }\ker C\\\hline
\epsilon_1&K_{12}=2T_2^{12}-T_1^{11},\quad K_{13}=2T_3^{13}-T_1^{11}\\
\epsilon_2&K_{21}=2T_1^{12}-T_2^{22},\quad K_{23}=2T_3^{23}-T_2^{22}\\
\epsilon_3&K_{31}=2T_1^{13}-T_3^{33},\quad K_{32}=2T_2^{23}-T_3^{33}
\end{array}
$$
Together with the nine single vectors, these give fifteen independent vectors. They are precisely the $T$ and $K$ labels on the fifteen-dimensional weight diagram.

The defining summand has only the following three weight spaces:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\epsilon_1&S_1=T_1^{11}+T_2^{12}+T_3^{13}\\
\epsilon_2&S_2=T_1^{12}+T_2^{22}+T_3^{23}\\
\epsilon_3&S_3=T_1^{13}+T_2^{23}+T_3^{33}

Each $S_i=s(e_i)$ is nonzero, has its indicated weight and satisfies $C(S_i)=4e_i$. These are the labels on the three-dimensional diagram. The <highest-weight vectors> in the two summands are

\boxed{z^*\otimes x^2=T_3^{11}\text{ in }L(2,1),\qquad S_1=s(x)\text{ in }L(1,0).}

At each repeated weight, the two kernel vectors together with $S_i$ span the original three-dimensional weight space, since the kernel has dimension two and $C(S_i)\ne0$. Thus the displayed diagrams specify all submodule weight-space bases.

Here the <Cartan subalgebra> of a finite-dimensional complex <semisimple Lie algebra> is a maximal toral subalgebra: it is abelian, its adjoint operators are simultaneously diagonalizable, and its centralizer is itself. These standard properties give the <root-space decomposition>
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha,\qquad \mathfrak g_\alpha=\{X\in\mathfrak g:[H,X]=\alpha(H)X\ \forall H\in\mathfrak h\}.}
$$
The <roots of a root system> $\alpha$ are the nonzero <linear functionals> in $\mathfrak h^*$ for which $\mathfrak g_\alpha\ne0$; $\Phi$ is their finite set. Each <root space> is one-dimensional. The zero <weight space> is the centralizer of $\mathfrak h$, hence exactly $\mathfrak h$. Thus the displayed sum is a direct sum of simultaneous eigenspaces of the <adjoint representation>. It is the Cartan root-space decomposition meant here.

The bracket respects weight addition: $[\mathfrak g_\alpha,\mathfrak g_\beta]\subseteq\mathfrak g_{\alpha+\beta}$, where $\mathfrak g_0=\mathfrak h$ and a missing weight means the zero space. This follows by applying the <Jacobi identity> with $H\in\mathfrak h$ to the bracket of two root vectors. It explains how the vector-space decomposition encodes the <Lie algebra> structure.

The <Killing form> is the bilinear form
$$
\boxed{B(X,Y)=\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}X\operatorname{ad}Y),\qquad\operatorname{ad}X(Z)=[X,Z].}
$$
The <adjoint representation> is linear, giving bilinearity, and the cyclic property of <trace> gives symmetry. To prove invariance, abbreviate the adjoint matrices of $X,Y,Z$ by $A_X,A_Y,A_Z$. The <Jacobi identity> gives $A_{[X,Y]}=[A_X,A_Y]$, and cyclic trace then gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
B([X,Y],Z)&=\operatorname{tr}\bigl((A_XA_Y-A_YA_X)A_Z\bigr)\\
&=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(A_X(A_YA_Z-A_ZA_Y)\bigr)=B(X,[Y,Z]).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $\boxed{B([X,Y],Z)=B(X,[Y,Z])}$, equivalently $B([X,Y],Z)+B(Y,[X,Z])=0$. This is infinitesimal invariance of the <Killing form>, with no choice of basis affecting the trace.

First show that the <Cartan subalgebra> is orthogonal, for the <Killing form>, to every nonzero <root space>. For $H\in\mathfrak h$ and $X\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$, choose $H_0\in\mathfrak h$ with $\alpha(H_0)\ne0$. Invariance and the abelian nature of $\mathfrak h$ give
$$
\alpha(H_0)B(H,X)=B(H,[H_0,X])=B([H,H_0],X)=0.
$$
Hence $B(H,X)=0$.

Now let $H$ be in the radical of the restriction to $\mathfrak h$, so $B(H,H')=0$ for every $H'\in\mathfrak h$. The preceding argument also gives $B(H,\mathfrak g_\alpha)=0$ for every root. By the <root-space decomposition>, $H$ is orthogonal to all of $\mathfrak g$. The assumed nondegeneracy on $\mathfrak g$ forces $H=0$. Therefore \b[the Killing form restricted to the Cartan subalgebra is nondegenerate].

For the embedded <Lie group>, the <tangent space> $T_eG$ consists of velocities $\gamma'(0)$ of smooth curves in $G$ satisfying $\gamma(0)=e$. The ambient coordinates identify these velocities with vectors in $\mathbb R^n$, but the tangent space itself is intrinsic. Let $c_g(a)=gag^{-1}$. Since this conjugation map fixes $e$, its derivative defines the <adjoint representation of a Lie group>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Ad}_g=(dc_g)_e:T_eG\to T_eG.}
$$
The inverse is $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}$, and $c_{gh}=c_g\circ c_h$ gives $\operatorname{Ad}_{gh}=\operatorname{Ad}_g\operatorname{Ad}_h$. Thus this is a smooth group homomorphism into the linear automorphisms of $T_eG$.

Differentiate that representation at the identity. The tangent space of $GL(T_eG)$ at its identity is $\operatorname{End}(T_eG)$, so $\operatorname{ad}=(d\operatorname{Ad})_e$ is a linear map from $T_eG$ into those endomorphisms. The <Lie bracket> is
$$
\boxed{[X,Y]=\operatorname{ad}(X)Y=\left.\frac d{ds}\right|_{s=0}\operatorname{Ad}_{\gamma(s)}Y,\quad\gamma(0)=e,\quad\gamma'(0)=X.}
$$
Equivalently, if $\eta(0)=e$ and $\eta'(0)=Y$, this is the mixed derivative at $(0,0)$ of $\gamma(s)\eta(t)\gamma(s)^{-1}$ in ambient coordinates. For a matrix <Lie group>, conjugation is ordinary matrix conjugation and this formula reduces to $[X,Y]=XY-YX$.

The checks required are that the derivative is independent of the representing curves, that it is bilinear and alternating, and that it satisfies the <Jacobi identity>. One must also check that each $\operatorname{Ad}_g$ preserves this bracket. Equivalently identify $X$ with its left-invariant vector field $X^L(g)=(dL_g)_eX$; the bracket above agrees with the <Lie bracket of vector fields> at $e$. These checks make $T_eG$ a <Lie algebra> and upgrade the adjoint maps to its Lie algebra automorphisms.

Use the finite-dimensional complex-module setting of the course. We prove the <Weyl complete reducibility theorem> by <Haar averaging produces invariant complements>, defining its constructions rather than invoking complete reducibility itself.

Choose a <compact real form> $\mathfrak k\subset\mathfrak g$: it is a real Lie subalgebra with $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak k\oplus i\mathfrak k$, and its simply connected Lie group $K$ is compact. Existence of this form and compact group is a standard fact for complex <semisimple Lie algebras>. Restrict the representation to $\mathfrak k$; <integration of a Lie-algebra representation> gives a group representation $R:K\to GL(V)$. Since $K$ is connected and generated by exponentials, the $\mathfrak g$-invariant subspace $W$ is also $K$-invariant.

Choose any positive-definite <Hermitian inner product> on $V$. With the <Haar measure> of $K$ normalized to mass one, define
$$
\langle v,w\rangle_K=\int_K\langle R(k)v,R(k)w\rangle_0\,dk.
$$
This is a Hermitian inner product, and it is positive-definite: for $v\ne0$ every $R(k)v$ is nonzero, so the continuous positive integrand has positive integral. Right invariance of Haar measure gives $\langle R(a)v,R(a)w\rangle_K=\langle v,w\rangle_K$ for $a\in K$.

Let $W'=W^\perp$ for this averaged inner product. Finite-dimensional inner-product linear algebra gives $V=W\oplus W'$. If $v\in W'$ and $w\in W$, then
$$
\langle R(a)v,w\rangle_K=\langle v,R(a^{-1})w\rangle_K=0,
$$
because $R(a^{-1})w\in W$. Thus $W'$ is $K$-invariant. Differentiating makes it $\mathfrak k$-invariant, and since it is a complex vector subspace, $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak k+i\mathfrak k$ also preserves it. Therefore
$$
\boxed{W'=W^{\perp_K}\text{ is a }\mathfrak g\text{-submodule and }V=W\oplus W'.}
$$
The zero or whole-space submodule cases are included. This proves the required complement theorem without circularly assuming it.

The finite-dimensional hypothesis is essential to the statement. The <nonsplit zero-weight Verma extension for sl2> is a counterexample for unrestricted modules: $M(0)$ has basis $v_n$ with $hv_n=-2nv_n$, $fv_n=v_{n+1}$ and $ev_n=n(1-n)v_{n-1}$. Its submodule spanned by $v_n$ for $n\ge1$ has trivial one-dimensional quotient. A complement would be trivial and contained in the zero-weight line $\mathbb Cv_0$, yet $fv_0=v_1\ne0$. Hence no such complement exists in that infinite-dimensional case. The paper's finite-dimensional representation setting supplies the necessary hypothesis.

For a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> $V$, a <weight of a representation> relative to $\mathfrak h$ is a linear functional $\mu\in\mathfrak h^*$ for which the simultaneous eigenspace
$$
V_\mu=\{v\in V:H\cdot v=\mu(H)v\text{ for every }H\in\mathfrak h\}
$$
is nonzero. Its elements are <weight vectors>, and $\dim V_\mu$ is the <weight multiplicity>. The set of weights is $\boxed{\Lambda_V=\{\mu:V_\mu\ne0\}}$. For a complex <semisimple Lie algebra>, the Cartan action on a finite-dimensional module is simultaneously diagonalizable, giving $V=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\Lambda_V}V_\mu$. The set of weights and their multiplicities are distinct information: several basis vectors can occupy the same point of a <weight diagram>.

Let $E$ be the real span of the roots in $\mathfrak h^*$. The <weight lattice> is
$$
\boxed{\Lambda_W=P=\{\mu\in E:\mu(h_\alpha)\in\mathbb Z\text{ for every root }\alpha\},}
$$
where $h_\alpha$ is the <coroot> in the root's <sl2 subalgebra associated with a root>, normalized by $\alpha(h_\alpha)=2$. It suffices to test the simple coroots. Equivalently, if $\omega_i(h_{\alpha_j})=\delta_{ij}$ defines the <fundamental weights>, then $P=\bigoplus_i\mathbb Z\omega_i$.

To prove <integrality of representation weights on coroots>, restrict a finite-dimensional module $V$ to the root's <sl2 Lie algebra>. By the <classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations>, the eigenvalues of its Cartan element $h_\alpha$ are integers. Thus every $\mu\in\Lambda_V$ has $\mu(h_\alpha)\in\mathbb Z$. The simple coroots form a basis of the real Cartan space, so these integral values also put $\mu$ in $E$. Therefore $\boxed{\Lambda_V\subseteq\Lambda_W}$ for every finite-dimensional representation in this setting. The weight lattice need not coincide with the smaller <root lattice>.

Choose a real linear functional $\ell:E\to\mathbb R$ nonzero on every root. Its positive system is $\boxed{\Phi^+=\{\alpha\in\Phi:\ell(\alpha)>0\}}$. The other roots form $\Phi^-=-\Phi^+$. Thus exactly one of $\alpha,-\alpha$ is positive, and a sum of positive roots that is a root is again positive because $\ell$ is additive.

The <simple roots> are the positive roots not expressible as a sum of two positive roots. They form a <fundamental system of a root system>; every positive root is a nonnegative integral combination of them. Positivity is a choice of ordering, rather than a property of a root independent of that choice.

For the chosen simple roots $\Delta$, the open <fundamental chamber of a root system> is
$$
\boxed{C=\{\lambda\in E:(\lambda,\alpha)>0\text{ for every }\alpha\in\Delta\}.}
$$
Equivalently $\lambda(h_\alpha)>0$ for every simple coroot. Since all positive roots are nonnegative combinations of simple roots, requiring the inequality for all positive roots gives the same chamber. It is a connected component of the complement of the root reflection hyperplanes; the <Weyl group> permutes those chambers simply transitively.

The <closed dominant Weyl chamber> is obtained by replacing the inequalities by $\ge0$. It is the cone generated by the <fundamental weights>. When the fundamental Weyl chamber is taken to include its walls, this closed convention is intended; both conventions determine the same positive system and boundary rays.

For the usual diagonal <Cartan subalgebra>, write $\epsilon_i(H)=H_{ii}$. The roots are $\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j$ for $i\ne j$. The three diagonal entries of the specified $H_0$ have ordering
$$
1>\sqrt2-1>-\sqrt2.

Therefore the <positive roots> selected by $\ell(\mu)=\mu(H_0)$ are
$$
\boxed{\Phi^+=\{\epsilon_2-\epsilon_3,\ \epsilon_3-\epsilon_1,\ \epsilon_2-\epsilon_1\}.}
$$
Their positive evaluations are respectively $2-\sqrt2$, $2\sqrt2-1$ and $1+\sqrt2$. For this nonstandard ordering the simple roots are $\alpha_1=\epsilon_2-\epsilon_3$ and $\alpha_2=\epsilon_3-\epsilon_1$; the third positive root is their sum. These simple roots differ from the usual choice used in Question 1.

Identify the real weight plane with triples $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ of sum zero. Pairing with these simple coroots gives the differences $u_2-u_3$ and $u_3-u_1$. Consequently the <fundamental chamber of a root system> is
$$
\boxed{C=\{u_1+u_2+u_3=0:\ u_2>u_3>u_1\}.}
$$
Its closure replaces the inequalities by weak inequalities. The fundamental weights for this ordering are $\omega_1=\epsilon_2$ and $\omega_2=-\epsilon_1$, represented by $(-1/3,2/3,-1/3)$ and $(-2/3,1/3,1/3)$. Their simple-coroot pairings are $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, so the two boundary rays are
$$
\boxed{\overline C=\mathbb R_{\ge0}\epsilon_2+\mathbb R_{\ge0}(-\epsilon_1).}
$$
The sketch uses $\epsilon_1=(1,0)$, $\epsilon_2=(-1/2,\sqrt3/2)$ and $\epsilon_3=(-1/2,-\sqrt3/2)$ in a Euclidean plane. The shaded sector is the sixty-degree closed chamber; its interior is the open chamber. The ordering vector $H_0$, identified with its dual direction, lies inside it.

= Solution

In the semisimple Lie algebra convention, an abstract <root system> is a finite subset $\Phi$ of a finite-dimensional real Euclidean space $E$ with the following properties: it spans $E$, does not contain zero, is reduced, is invariant under each of its root reflections, and satisfies crystallographic integrality. More explicitly, for every $\alpha,\beta\in\Phi$,
$$
\boxed{\Phi\cap\mathbb R\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\},\qquad s_\alpha(\beta)=\beta-\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha\in\Phi,\qquad\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Here $(\ ,\ )$ is a positive-definite inner product. The first condition is reducedness, the second gives closure under <root reflections>, and the last number is a <Cartan integer>. The <rank> is $\dim E$. A <simple root> basis is a basis of $E$ consisting of roots such that each root has integral coordinates that are either all nonnegative or all nonpositive. The two signs distinguish positive and negative roots. The diagrams in this problem concern these reduced <crystallographic root systems>; broader definitions sometimes omit one of these conditions.

The drawn simple roots have $\|\alpha\|^2=1$, $\|\beta\|^2=2$ and $\alpha\cdot\beta=-1$. Their <Cartan integers> are therefore $\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle=-2$ and $\langle\alpha,\beta^\vee\rangle=-1$, identifying a <B2 root system>. The corresponding <root reflections> act on coordinates by
$$
s_\alpha(u,v)=(-u,v),\qquad s_\beta(u,v)=(v,u).
$$
In particular $s_\beta(\alpha)=\alpha+\beta=(0,1)$ and $s_\alpha(\beta)=2\alpha+\beta=(1,1)$, so both new positive roots are forced. Reflection in any root also forces its negative. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Phi^+=\{\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta,2\alpha+\beta\},\qquad\Phi=\{\pm(1,0),\pm(0,1),\pm(-1,1),\pm(1,1)\}.}
$$
These eight roots are drawn below, with the given simple pair highlighted.

\Image[paper-1-b2-roots.png]
{title=All roots forced by the given simple roots of the B2 root system}

For completeness, no further roots can occur. In a finite reduced root system, every root is a <Weyl group> translate of a simple root: reflect a nonsimple positive root in a simple root with positive inner product to decrease its height, and iterate. This standard simple-root descent may also be quoted here. The eight-vector set just listed is stable under both generating reflections, and the orbits of the two simple roots already fill it. Hence no simple-root orbit, and therefore no root, lies outside that set. This establishes both the necessity of every drawn root and exhaustion of the root system.

The double-bond arrow points to the middle node in the PDF. Since $\|\alpha\|=1$ and $\|\beta\|=\sqrt2$, the short root $\alpha$ must occupy that middle node and the long root $\beta$ the left node. The right node $\gamma$, joined to $\alpha$ by a single bond, is another short root. Thus the diagram is <C3 root system> type, labelled as follows.

\Image[paper-1-c3-dynkin.png]
{title=Labelling the C3 Dynkin diagram with the given short and long roots}

The bonds require $\gamma\cdot\beta=0$, $\gamma\cdot\alpha=-1/2$ and $\|\gamma\|^2=1$. Writing $\gamma=(a,b,c)$ gives $a=-1/2$, $b=a$, and $c^2=1/2$. One choice is therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\left(-\frac12,-\frac12,\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
The opposite choice of the last coordinate reflects the entire realization across the first two coordinate axes' plane and is equally valid.

To enumerate exhaustively, set $f_1=(0,0,1)$, $f_2=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$ and $f_3=(-1,1,0)/\sqrt2$. These are orthonormal, and
$$
\gamma=(f_1-f_2)/\sqrt2,\qquad\alpha=(f_2-f_3)/\sqrt2,\qquad\beta=\sqrt2f_3.
$$
The complete <C3 root system> is $\{\pm\sqrt2f_i,\ (\pm f_i\pm f_j)/\sqrt2:i<j\}$, with eighteen roots. Its nine roots with nonnegative coefficients in this simple basis are exactly
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{positive root}&\text{coordinates}\\\hline
\alpha&(1,0,0)\\
\beta&(-1,1,0)\\
\gamma&(-1/2,-1/2,1/\sqrt2)\\
\alpha+\beta&(0,1,0)\\
\alpha+\gamma&(1/2,-1/2,1/\sqrt2)\\
\alpha+\beta+\gamma&(-1/2,1/2,1/\sqrt2)\\
2\alpha+\beta&(1,1,0)\\
2\alpha+\beta+\gamma&(1/2,1/2,1/\sqrt2)\\
2\alpha+\beta+2\gamma&(0,0,\sqrt2)
\end{array}
$$
There are six short and three long positive roots, and the other nine roots are their negatives.

The single-bond rank-two diagram is <A2 root system> type. Three suitable positive simple pairs for its <root subsystems> are
$$
\boxed{(\alpha,\gamma),\qquad(\alpha,\alpha+\beta+\gamma),\qquad(\alpha+\beta,\gamma).}
$$
Each pair $p,q$ has squared lengths one and inner product $-1/2$, so its angle is $120$ degrees. Its plane meets the full root set in $\pm p,\pm q,\pm(p+q)$, with $p+q$ respectively $\alpha+\gamma$, $2\alpha+\beta+\gamma$ and $\alpha+\beta+\gamma$. These exhaust the roots of the plane, and their coordinates in the pair have one sign. Thus each pair is genuinely a simple basis of an $A_2$ subsystem.

The double-bond diagram is rank-two <B2 root system> type, equivalently $C_2$. Three suitable pairs, written short root first, are
$$
\boxed{(\alpha,\beta),\qquad(\alpha+\gamma,\beta),\qquad(\gamma,2\alpha+\beta).}
$$
For each pair $s,l$, $\|s\|^2=1$, $\|l\|^2=2$ and $s\cdot l=-1$. The remaining positive roots in its plane are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
(s,l)&s+l&2s+l\\\hline
(\alpha,\beta)&\alpha+\beta&2\alpha+\beta\\
(\alpha+\gamma,\beta)&\alpha+\beta+\gamma&2\alpha+\beta+2\gamma\\
(\gamma,2\alpha+\beta)&2\alpha+\beta+\gamma&2\alpha+\beta+2\gamma
\end{array}
$$
Together with their negatives these are precisely the eight plane roots, and all four positive roots have nonnegative integral pair-coordinates. Hence the displayed pairs are simple bases of the required subsystems. The <rank-two subsystems of a C3 root system> description explains these as the three coordinate-plane subsystems in the orthonormal $f_i$ realization; the chosen $A_2$ pairs lie in three different diagonal planes.
