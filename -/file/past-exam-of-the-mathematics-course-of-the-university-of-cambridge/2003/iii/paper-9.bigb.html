= Solution
{parent=a}

Put $\alpha=-\log|c|>0$. Since $f$ has no zeros and the <unit disc> is <simply connected>, it has a <holomorphic logarithm>. Thus $F=-\log f$ is <holomorphic> with $\operatorname{Re}F>0$ and $\operatorname{Re}F(0)=\alpha$. The <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk> gives
$$
\phi(z)=\frac{F(z)-F(0)}{F(z)+\overline{F(0)}}\in\mathbb D,\qquad \phi(0)=0.
$$
The <Schwarz lemma> yields $|\phi'(0)|\le1$, so the <Carathéodory derivative bound for the right half-plane> gives $|F'(0)|\le2\alpha$. Since $f'=-F'f$, the sharp answer is
$$
\boxed{\max |f'(0)|=2|c|\log\frac1{|c|}.}
$$
Equality in the <Schwarz lemma> holds exactly when $\phi(z)=\omega z$, $|\omega|=1$. Solving for $F$ and exponentiating gives exactly the extremizers
$$
\boxed{f(z)=c\exp\left(-\frac{2\alpha\omega z}{1-\omega z}\right),\qquad |\omega|=1.}
$$
Their logarithmic real part is $\alpha\operatorname{Re}[(1+\omega z)/(1-\omega z)]>0$, so each is indeed a zero-free map into the <unit disc> with the prescribed value at zero.

For a fixed $w\in\mathbb D$, put $r=|w|$. Apply the <Harnack inequality on the unit disk> to the positive <harmonic function> $\operatorname{Re}F$ and then exponentiate its negative. The <zero-free Schwarz lemma> gives
$$
\boxed{|c|^{(1+r)/(1-r)}\le |f(w)|\le |c|^{(1-r)/(1+r)}.}
$$
For $w\ne0$ both endpoints are attained: take $\omega w=r$ for the lower endpoint and $\omega w=-r$ for the upper endpoint in the displayed extremizers. At zero both are $|c|$. If the question is interpreted as allowing $w$ to vary as well, the sharp <infimum> and <supremum> over all admissible pairs $(f,w)$ are respectively $0$ and $1$, approached as $r\uparrow1$; neither value is attained inside the <unit disc>. An individual function need not have these global extremal values: the constant function $f=c$ is a simple example.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Harnack inequality on the unit disk> states that a nonnegative <harmonic function> satisfies
$$
\frac{1-|z|}{1+|z|}u(0)\le u(z)\le\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|}u(0).
$$
One proof applies the <Poisson integral on the unit disk> on a circle of radius $R>|z|$: the <Poisson kernel on the circle> lies between $(R-|z|)/(R+|z|)$ and its reciprocal. The <mean value property for harmonic functions> identifies the mean boundary value with $u(0)$; let $R\uparrow1$.

A <harmonic conjugate> on the <simply connected> <unit disc> makes $H=u+iv$ <holomorphic>. The <Carathéodory derivative bound for the right half-plane> gives $|H'(0)|\le2u(0)$. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $|H'|=|\nabla u|$, so $|\nabla u(0)|\le2u(0)$. For a fixed $z$, use the <disk automorphism>
$$
\psi(\zeta)=\frac{z+\zeta}{1+\overline z\zeta},\qquad \psi(0)=z,\qquad |\psi'(0)|=1-|z|^2.
$$
The <chain rule> and <conformality> multiply the <gradient> length by $|\psi'(0)|$. Applying the estimate at zero to $u\circ\psi$ proves the <sharp gradient bound for positive harmonic functions>:
$$
\boxed{|\nabla u(z)|\le\frac{2}{1-|z|^2}u(z),\qquad c_{\rm best}(z)=\frac{2}{1-|z|^2}.}
$$
Sharpness follows from $u(\zeta)=\operatorname{Re}[(1+\zeta)/(1-\zeta)]$, which has equality at zero, and then composing with $\psi^{-1}$. These extremizers are positive multiples of the <Poisson kernel on the circle> with its boundary pole fixed at one point.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Write a <Blaschke product> as $B(z)=\lambda z^m\prod_n b_{a_n}(z)$, where $a_n\ne0$, $|\lambda|=1$, and the zeros are counted with <multiplicity>. With normalized angular measure $dm=d\theta/(2\pi)$, <Jensen's formula> for each <Blaschke factor> gives
$$
\int\log|b_a(re^{i\theta})|\,dm=\log\max(r,|a|).
$$
Indeed, the mean of $\log|a-re^{i\theta}|$ is $\log\max(r,|a|)$, while $\log|1-\overline a re^{i\theta}|$ has mean zero by the <mean value property for harmonic functions>. Each factor has modulus at most one. Applying the <monotone convergence theorem> to the negative logarithms of the finite products yields the <radial logarithmic mean of a Blaschke product>:
$$
\int\log|B(re^{i\theta})|\,dm=m\log r+\sum_n\log\max(r,|a_n|).
$$
The <Blaschke condition> implies $\sum_n-\log|a_n|<\infty$: only finitely many zeros have modulus below $1/2$, and $-\log t\le2(1-t)$ for $1/2\le t<1$. Every summand tends to zero and is bounded in absolute value by $-\log|a_n|$. The <dominated convergence theorem> for this series, together with $m\log r\to0$, therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{r\uparrow1}\int\log|B(re^{i\theta})|\,dm=0.}
$$
This also covers finite products and constant unimodular products. Zeros on a circle create integrable logarithmic singularities and do not change the conclusion.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the absolute value around the logarithm in the original PDF. Set $v(z)=\log^+|f(z)|=\max(0,\log|f(z)|)$. This is a nonnegative <subharmonic function>, including at zeros by its continuous extension. Its radial means are nondecreasing: <Poisson integral> comparison on a larger concentric circle bounds the smaller mean by the larger one. The hypothesis gives
$$
0\le T(r)=\int\log^+|f(re^{i\theta})|\,dm
\le\int\big|\log|f(re^{i\theta})|\big|\,dm\longrightarrow0.
$$
Consequently $T(r)=0$ for every $r$, and continuity of $v$ implies $v=0$ throughout the <unit disc>. Thus **$|f|\le1$ everywhere**, which is stronger than the requested conclusion. In particular $f$ has <bounded characteristic>, because $\sup_rT(r)<\infty$; it belongs to the <Nevanlinna class> and to <H-infinity space>. The hypothesis also excludes the identically zero function, whose absolute logarithmic integral is infinite.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $m$ be the <multiplicity> at zero, and write $f(z)=z^m h(z)$ with $h(0)\ne0$. The proof in <2/a/solution> gives $|f|\le1$. For radii without boundary zeros, <Jensen's formula> says
$$
\int\log|f(re^{i\theta})|\,dm
=m\log r+\log|h(0)|+\sum_{0<|a_n|<r}\log\frac r{|a_n|}.
$$
The absolute logarithmic hypothesis makes the left side tend to zero. The nonnegative zero sum increases to $\sum_n\log(1/|a_n|)$ by the <monotone convergence theorem>, so this series is finite. Hence $\sum_n(1-|a_n|)<\infty$, and the <Blaschke condition> supplies a <Blaschke product> $B$ with exactly these zeros and their <multiplicities>.

The quotient $g=f/B$ extends <holomorphically> across every zero because the <multiplicities> agree, and the extensions have nonzero values. Thus **$f=Bg$ with $g$ zero-free and holomorphic**. We will also need the stronger <Blaschke factorization of a bounded holomorphic function>, namely $|g|\le1$. Let $B_N$ contain the factor $z^m$ and the first $N$ other zero factors. The quotient $f/B_N$ is <holomorphic>. On $|z|=R$ enclosing those finitely many zeros, its modulus is at most $1/\min_{|z|=R}|B_N(z)|$. The <maximum modulus principle> transfers this bound to the interior. Since the finite <Blaschke product> has boundary modulus one continuously, letting $R\uparrow1$ gives $|f/B_N|\le1$. Now let $N\to\infty$ using <locally uniform convergence> of the <Blaschke product>, first off the zeros and then by <continuity> at them. This proves $|g|\le1$.

= Solution

The zero-free quotient $g$ from <2/b/solution> has a <holomorphic logarithm>, so $\log|g|$ is <harmonic>. Its radial mean is the constant $\log|g(0)|$. Subtract the radial logarithmic means of $f$ and $B$. The first tends to zero by the absolute logarithmic hypothesis; the second tends to zero by <2/solution>. It follows that
$$
\log|g(0)|=\int\log|f(re^{i\theta})|\,dm-\int\log|B(re^{i\theta})|\,dm=0.
$$
Since $|g|\le1$ and $|g(0)|=1$, the <maximum modulus principle> makes $g$ a constant of modulus one. Absorb that constant into the unimodular factor of $B$. **Therefore $f$ itself is a <Blaschke product>.** This proves the <vanishing absolute logarithmic mean characterization of Blaschke products>; conversely a <Blaschke product> satisfies the absolute version too, since $\log|B|\le0$.

= Solution

A <nontangential limit> at $\xi\in\partial\mathbb D$ restricts approach to a fixed <Stolz region> $\Gamma_A(\xi)=\{z\in\mathbb D:|z-\xi|<A(1-|z|)\}$, where $A>1$. Radial approach is a special case. The <Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions> says that every $f$ in <H-infinity space> has a finite boundary value $f^*(\xi)$ for almost every $\xi$, with convergence through every fixed <Stolz region> and $|f^*|\le\|f\|_\infty$ <almost everywhere>.

Here is a proof outline. Write $f=u+iv$ with bounded real <harmonic functions> $u,v$. Take a sequence $R_j\uparrow1$. The bounded functions $u(R_j\xi)$ have a subsequence converging in the <weak-star topology> of $L^\infty(\partial\mathbb D)$ by the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> and <weak-star metrizability of the dual ball>; its $L^1$ predual is <separable>. Denote the limit by $U$. For $|z|<R_j$, the <Poisson integral on the unit disk> gives
$$
u(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb D}P_{z/R_j}(\xi)u(R_j\xi)\,dm(\xi).
$$
The <Poisson kernels> converge uniformly for this fixed interior point and the boundary functions stay uniformly bounded, so the <weak-star topology> permits passage to the limit:
$$
u(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb D}\frac{1-|z|^2}{|\xi-z|^2}U(\xi)\,dm(\xi).
$$
Repeat for $v$, obtaining $V$. Thus $f$ is the <Poisson integral> of $F=U+iV\in L^\infty$.

The <Lebesgue differentiation theorem> makes almost every boundary point a <Lebesgue point> of $U$ and $V$. The lemma that a <Poisson integral converges nontangentially at Lebesgue points> then gives $f(z)\to F(\xi_0)$ in every fixed <Stolz region>. To see why, put $\delta=1-|z|$ and let $d(\xi,\xi_0)$ be the shorter angular distance. In $\Gamma_A(\xi_0)$ the kernel satisfies
$$
P_z(\xi)\le C_A\frac{\delta}{\delta^2+d(\xi,\xi_0)^2}.
$$
Split the circle into a central arc of radius comparable to $\delta$ and annuli with radii $2^k\delta$. For a <Lebesgue point>, averages of $|F-F(\xi_0)|$ on sufficiently small arcs are arbitrarily small. The annular contributions are bounded by these averages times a summable geometric sequence $C_A2^{-k}$. On the remaining fixed distant arc the kernel is $O(\delta)$, so its contribution vanishes. This proves the claimed <nontangential limit>. The same <Lebesgue points> work for every integer aperture $A\ge2$, hence for all finite apertures.

These boundary values recover the interior by the <Poisson integral>, so they determine $f$ uniquely. They satisfy $\|f^*\|_{L^\infty}=\|f\|_\infty$: one inequality follows by taking limits of the bounded interior values, and the other from positivity and unit integral of the <Poisson kernel>. The <dominated convergence theorem> also gives $f(r\xi)\to f^*(\xi)$ in every finite $L^p$ norm. Its negative <Fourier coefficients> vanish, because the <Taylor series> of $f$ has only nonnegative powers. This explains the boundary realization of analytic <Hardy spaces>.

The exceptional set cannot generally be removed. For example, $f(z)=\exp(i\operatorname{Log}(1-z))$ is bounded and <holomorphic> on the <unit disc>, using the <holomorphic logarithm> on the right half-plane: $|f(z)|=e^{-\arg(1-z)}$ lies between $e^{-\pi/2}$ and $e^{\pi/2}$. At the boundary point one its radial values $e^{i\log(1-r)}$ oscillate without a limit. Nor does the theorem assert convergence along arbitrary tangential paths or a continuous boundary extension. Its strength is almost-everywhere control on every fixed approach cone.

= Solution
{parent=4}

First construct a <holomorphic function> without requiring boundedness. Separate the finitely many zeros at zero into $z^m$, and enumerate the remaining prescribed zeros as $a_n$. Since $|a_n|\to1$, there is no interior accumulation point and every prescribed point has finite <multiplicity>. Using the <Weierstrass elementary factors>, set
$$
F(z)=z^m\prod_{n\ge1}E_n(z/a_n),\qquad
E_n(t)=(1-t)\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{t^k}{k}\right).
$$
For a compact disc $|z|\le r<1$, choose $q$ with $r<q<1$. Eventually $|z/a_n|\le q$. The <power series> identity
$$
\log E_n(t)=-\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty\frac{t^k}{k},\qquad
|\log E_n(t)|\le\frac{q^{n+1}}{(n+1)(1-q)}
$$
gives an absolutely summable uniform bound. The <infinite product convergence from logarithmic tails> therefore makes the tail <holomorphic> and nonvanishing on this compact set. The finitely many initial factors have exactly their prescribed zeros, with the prescribed <multiplicities>. Hence $F$ has exactly the required zero sequence. This is a direct <canonical product construction for zeros escaping to the disk boundary>.

For a nonzero bounded <holomorphic function>, <Jensen's formula> bounds $\sum_n\log(1/|a_n|)$ after separating the zero at zero. Thus the <Blaschke condition> is necessary; the <Blaschke product> construction makes it sufficient. Use the curvature-minus-one convention for <hyperbolic distance in the Poincare disc>:
$$
\rho(0,z)=\log\frac{1+|z|}{1-|z|},\qquad
e^{-\rho(0,z)}=\frac{1-|z|}{1+|z|}.
$$
The latter lies between $(1-|z|)/2$ and $1-|z|$. Consequently the precise bounded-function criterion is
$$
\boxed{\sum_n e^{-\rho(0,z_n)}<\infty.}
$$
This counts <multiplicities>; the finitely many occurrences at zero cause no problem.

For the requested <hyperbolic zero estimate for a Blaschke product>, the <pseudohyperbolic distance> identity is
$$
|b_a(w)|=\left|\frac{w-a}{1-\overline a w}\right|
=\tanh\frac{\rho(w,a)}2=\frac{1-t}{1+t},\qquad t=e^{-\rho(w,a)}.
$$
If $w$ is not a zero, $0<t<1$. The real function $L(t)=\log[(1+t)/(1-t)]-2t$ has $L(0)=0$ and $L'(t)=2t^2/(1-t^2)\ge0$, so $L(t)\ge0$. Thus $\log|b_a(w)|\le-2e^{-\rho(w,a)}$. Apply this to each factor, including factors at zero, and then pass from finite products to the <Blaschke product> by <locally uniform convergence>:
$$
\boxed{|B(w)|\le\exp\left(-2\sum_n e^{-\rho(w,z_n)}\right).}
$$
At a zero of $B$ the left side is zero, so the inequality is immediate; the logarithmic calculation at $t=1$ is unnecessary. The unimodular constant contributes nothing. Away from the zeros the product is nonzero, so the same inequality also bounds the sum and shows it is finite there.

We prove necessity of the geometric condition from the embedding condition. Use normalized angular measure $m$ and the <H1 space on the unit disk> norm $\|f\|_1=\sup_{r<1}\int|f(r\xi)|\,dm(\xi)$. Testing $f=1$ first gives $\mu(\mathbb D)\le N(\mu)$.

For a short arc $I$ of normalized length $\ell=m(I)$ centered at $\xi_0$, put $a=(1-\ell)\xi_0$ and use the <normalized squared Cauchy kernel>
$$
f_a(z)=\frac{1-|a|^2}{(1-\overline a z)^2}.
$$
Its boundary modulus is the <Poisson kernel on the circle>, so $\|f_a\|_1=1$. The <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison> gives $|z-\xi_0|\le C\ell$ for $z\in Q(I)$. Therefore $|1-\overline a z|\le C'\ell$, whereas $1-|a|^2=2\ell-\ell^2\ge\ell$. Thus $|f_a(z)|\ge c/\ell$ throughout the <hyperbolic geodesic cap>. The embedding bound gives
$$
\frac c\ell\mu(Q(I))\le\int_{\mathbb D}|f_a|\,d\mu\le N(\mu).
$$
For arcs longer than a fixed small threshold, the total-mass estimate gives the same form after changing the absolute constant. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mu(Q(I))\le C N(\mu)m(I).}
$$
This is precisely the <Carleson measure> condition, in its geodesic-cap version.

= Solution
{parent=b}

We prove sufficiency by a <non-tangential maximal function> and the <layer-cake representation>. Write $K=C(\mu)$. First $\mu(\mathbb D)\le C K$: three <hyperbolic geodesic caps> corresponding to arcs of normalized length $2/3$, with equally spaced centers, cover the <unit disc>. This handles interior points and large arcs, not only a thin boundary strip.

Fix aperture two and set $Nf(\xi)=\sup_{z\in\Gamma_2(\xi)}|f(z)|$. The crucial <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> is
$$
\int_{\partial\mathbb D}Nf\,dm\le C\|f\|_1.
$$
Here is its endpoint proof. For $0<R<1$ let $f_R(z)=f(Rz)$, which is <holomorphic> across the closed <unit disc>. Since <modulus powers of holomorphic functions are subharmonic>, Poisson comparison gives
$$
|f_R(z)|^{1/2}\le P[g_R](z),\qquad g_R(\xi)=|f(R\xi)|^{1/2}.
$$
The estimate that a <Poisson maximal function is dominated by the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function> yields $(Nf_R)^{1/2}\le C M g_R$. The strong $L^2$ <Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality> now gives
$$
\int Nf_R\,dm\le C\int(Mg_R)^2\,dm
\le C\int|f(R\xi)|\,dm\le C\|f\|_1.
$$
For each interior point $f_R(z)\to f(z)$, hence $Nf\le\liminf_{R\uparrow1}Nf_R$. The <Fatou lemma> proves the maximal inequality. This uses a genuine $L^2$ maximal bound; the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function> is not strongly bounded on $L^1$.

For $t>0$, the set $O_t=\{\xi:Nf(\xi)>t\}$ is open, since each interior point witnessing this strict inequality has an open boundary shadow. Write its disjoint arc components as $I_j$. If $|f(z)|>t$ and $\delta=1-|z|$, the arc centered at $z/|z|$ with angular half-width $\delta$ lies in $O_t$, because $|z-\xi|<2\delta$ there. Thus it lies in one component $I_j$, and $z$ has angular projection in $I_j$ with $\delta\le\pi m(I_j)$. The <geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison> puts all such points in $Q(\kappa I_j)$ for a fixed enlargement factor $\kappa$ whenever $I_j$ is small. For large $I_j$, use $\mu(\mathbb D)\le C K\le C'K m(I_j)$. If $z=0$ or $O_t$ is the entire circle, that same total-mass estimate applies. Summing, without needing the enlarged caps to be disjoint, gives
$$
\mu\{z:|f(z)|>t\}\le C K\sum_jm(I_j)=C K m(O_t).
$$
The <layer-cake representation> and the proved <H1 non-tangential maximal inequality on the disk> finish the argument:
$$
\int_{\mathbb D}|f|\,d\mu
=\int_0^\infty\mu\{|f|>t\}\,dt
\le C K\int_0^\infty m\{Nf>t\}\,dt
\le C K\|f\|_1.
$$
Thus **the two conditions are equivalent**, with their least admissible constants comparable: $C(\mu)\le C_1N(\mu)$ and $N(\mu)\le C_2C(\mu)$. This is the <Carleson embedding theorem on the disk>, proved at the analytic $H^1$ endpoint.
