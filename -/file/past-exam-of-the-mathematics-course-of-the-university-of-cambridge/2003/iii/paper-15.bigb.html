= Solution

We work over an <algebraically closed field> $k$, so the assertion concerns a geometric <projective line>. Let $V=k^4$ and let $W$ be the <vector space> of homogeneous cubic forms on $V$. It has dimension $\binom63=20$, so the parameter space of cubic equations up to nonzero scalar is $\mathbb P(W)=\mathbb P^{19}$. The <Grassmannian> $G=\operatorname{Gr}(2,V)$ parametrizes lines in $\mathbb P^3$. Its standard graph charts are four-dimensional <affine spaces>, so its dimension is four. The <Plücker embedding> realizes it as a closed quadric in $\mathbb P^5$, proving projectivity. The <general linear group> $\operatorname{GL}_4$ acts transitively on two-planes and is an irreducible open subset of the affine space of matrices; its orbit map proves that $G$ is irreducible.

Consider the incidence variety
$$
\mathcal I=\{(\ell,[F])\in G\times\mathbb P(W):F|_\ell=0\}.
$$
Restriction of a cubic to a fixed line is surjective onto the four-dimensional space of binary cubic forms: after choosing coordinates with that line given by $x_2=x_3=0$, extend any binary cubic using $x_0,x_1$ alone. Thus its kernel has dimension sixteen. On each <Grassmannian> chart these restriction maps depend algebraically on the line parameters and have constant rank four. Their kernels form a rank-sixteen <vector bundle>, and $\mathcal I$ is its <projective bundle>, with fibre $\mathbb P^{15}$. In particular, **$\mathcal I$ is irreducible of dimension $4+15=19$**. It is closed in the product, since restriction vanishes precisely when four polynomial coefficients vanish.

The projection $\pi:\mathcal I\to\mathbb P(W)$ is a <projective morphism>, hence its image is closed. It remains to prove that this image has dimension nineteen. Equal dimensions alone would not suffice: we must control a fibre locally.

Take
$$
F_0=x_0^2x_2+x_1^2x_3,\qquad \ell_0=\{x_2=x_3=0\}.
$$
In the open <Grassmannian> chart around $\ell_0$, every line is uniquely a graph
$$
x_2=a_0x_0+a_1x_1,\qquad x_3=b_0x_0+b_1x_1.
$$
Restriction of $F_0$ to that line is
$$
a_0x_0^3+a_1x_0^2x_1+b_0x_0x_1^2+b_1x_1^3.
$$
The four <monomials> are linearly independent over every field. Hence the only line in this chart lying on $F_0$ has $a_0=a_1=b_0=b_1=0$. The fibre $\pi^{-1}([F_0])$ therefore has local dimension zero at $(\ell_0,[F_0])$. We do not need $F_0$ to define a smooth surface, nor do we claim its entire fibre is finite.

The <fiber dimension theorem>, in its local dimension inequality, gives
$$
0=\dim_{(\ell_0,[F_0])}\pi^{-1}([F_0])\ge\dim\mathcal I-\dim\pi(\mathcal I).
$$
Thus the closed image has dimension at least nineteen. A proper closed subset of the irreducible $\mathbb P^{19}$ has smaller dimension, so $\pi(\mathcal I)=\mathbb P^{19}$. Every cubic equation consequently has a line in its zero set. In particular, **every nonsingular cubic surface contains a line**. The proof in fact establishes existence for arbitrary <cubic surfaces> over an <algebraically closed field>; it does not assert a rational line over an arbitrary nonclosed ground field.

Use the graded <ring homomorphism>
$$
\varphi:k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]\longrightarrow k[s,t],\qquad x_i\longmapsto s^{3-i}t^i,
$$
where source degree $m$ maps to target degree $3m$. The <homogeneous ideal> of the <twisted cubic> is $\ker\varphi$: a <homogeneous polynomial> vanishes on the parametrized curve precisely when its substitution vanishes identically. Here $k$ is infinite, being an <algebraically closed field>, so vanishing at all parameter points implies the polynomial identity.

Let $J$ be generated by
$$
q_1=x_0x_3-x_1x_2,\qquad q_2=x_0x_2-x_1^2,\qquad q_3=x_1x_3-x_2^2.
$$
Their substitutions vanish, hence $J\subseteq\ker\varphi$. To prove the reverse inclusion as an equality of ideals, we need more than a set-theoretic description of the zero locus.

Modulo $J$, replace $x_1^2$ by $x_0x_2$, $x_2^2$ by $x_1x_3$, and $x_1x_2$ by $x_0x_3$. Each replacement strictly decreases the sum of the exponents of $x_1$ and $x_2$, so the reduction terminates. It shows that the degree-$m$ quotient is spanned by the <standard monomials of the twisted cubic>
$$
x_0^ax_3^b\quad(a+b=m),\qquad
x_0^ax_1x_3^b\quad(a+b=m-1),\qquad
x_0^ax_2x_3^b\quad(a+b=m-1).
$$
For $m\ge1$ there are $(m+1)+m+m=3m+1$ of these. For $m=0$ the sole monomial is $1$. Their substituted images have $t$-exponents respectively $3b$, $3b+1$ and $3b+2$, so they are exactly the distinct <monomials>
$$
s^{3m-j}t^j,\qquad 0\le j\le3m.
$$
These are linearly independent. Consequently any degree-$m$ polynomial in $\ker\varphi$, after reduction modulo $J$, has all its reduced coefficients zero. It belongs to $J$. Applying this to each homogeneous component gives
$$
\boxed{I(X)=\ker\varphi=(x_0x_3-x_1x_2,\ x_0x_2-x_1^2,\ x_1x_3-x_2^2).}
$$
This also proves that the quotient embeds in the domain $k[s,t]$, so the displayed <ideal> is prime; no radicalization or saturation correction is concealed in the argument. Its image is the third <Veronese subring> $\bigoplus_{m\ge0}k[s,t]_{3m}$, and the calculation gives the <Hilbert polynomial> $3m+1$, consistent with degree three and genus zero.

The usual <field norm> $N_{L/K}$ is defined for a finite field extension. The printed hypothesis “algebraic” alone allows an infinite extension, for which this determinant norm is not defined. We therefore assume $[L:K]<\infty$. For an arbitrary algebraic extension the meaningful elementwise statement is instead $N_{K(b)/K}(b)\in A$, and we prove this as well.

First a <unique factorization domain> is an <integrally closed domain>. Indeed, write an integral fraction as $u/v$ with relatively prime numerator and denominator. A monic integral equation, after multiplication by $v^r$, shows that $v$ divides $u^r$. Unique factorization forces $v$ to be a unit.

Let $m_b(T)=T^r+a_{r-1}T^{r-1}+\cdots+a_0\in K[T]$ be the monic <minimal polynomial> of $b$. Because $b$ is an <integral element> over $A$, it satisfies a monic polynomial $f\in A[T]$, and $m_b$ divides $f$ in $K[T]$. Every root of $m_b$ in an algebraic closure is therefore integral over $A$. This assertion includes repeated roots when the extension is inseparable. The coefficients of $m_b$ are elementary symmetric expressions in these roots. The <integral elements> form a ring: finitely many integral elements generate a finite $A$-module algebra, and the determinant trick shows every element of that algebra is integral. Thus all $a_i$ are integral over $A$. Since $a_i\in K$ and $A$ is integrally closed, $a_i\in A$.

Multiplication by $b$ on $K(b)$, in the basis $1,b,\ldots,b^{r-1}$, has the companion matrix of $m_b$, whose determinant is $(-1)^ra_0$. If $e=[L:K(b)]$, take a $K(b)$-basis of $L$. Multiplication by $b$ preserves each summand in the resulting <direct sum> of $e$ copies of $K(b)$, so its determinant over $K$ is
$$
\boxed{N_{L/K}(b)=\bigl((-1)^ra_0\bigr)^e\in A.}
$$
In particular $N_{K(b)/K}(b)=(-1)^ra_0\in A$. The argument requires no separability; in fact it holds for every <integrally closed domain>, not just a <unique factorization domain>.

For the connection with dimension, let $B$ be the coordinate ring of an irreducible <affine variety>. By <Noether normalization> there is a finite integral inclusion $A=k[t_1,\ldots,t_d]\subset B$, with $d=\dim B$. Their fraction fields form a finite extension. For any $0\ne b\in B$, its minimal-polynomial constant term $a_0$ is nonzero and lies in $A$. Its equation gives
$$
a_0=-b\bigl(b^{r-1}+a_{r-1}b^{r-2}+\cdots+a_1\bigr)\in bB\cap A.
$$
Thus a nonzero function on the normalized variety produces a nonzero function on the base that vanishes wherever $b$ vanishes. Up to sign, the <field norm> is a power of this $a_0$, so it too belongs to $bB\cap A$.

Under the <finite morphism> to $\mathbb A^d$, the image of $V_B(b)$ is therefore contained in the proper <Zariski-closed set> $V_A(a_0)$ of dimension at most $d-1$. <Finite morphisms> preserve the dimension of a closed subvariety and its image. Applying this to a nonzero element of the ideal of any proper closed subvariety proves **proper closed subvarieties have strictly smaller dimension**. This is the useful role of the norm argument: nonzero equations upstairs give nonzero equations downstairs under a finite normalization.

Let $X$ be a separated <Noetherian scheme> over a field $k$ and $\mathcal F$ a <coherent sheaf>. <Coherent cohomology> studies the groups $H^q(X,\mathcal F)$: <global sections> in degree zero and the obstructions to gluing local sections in higher degrees. Coherence means finite generation locally, and on a Noetherian scheme it is preserved by kernels, cokernels and extensions. This makes <coherent sheaves> suitable for exact-sequence calculations.

For an ordered open cover $\mathcal U=(U_i)$, the <Čech cochain groups> are
$$
\check C^q(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=\prod_{i_0<\cdots<i_q}\mathcal F(U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_q}),
$$
with differential
$$
(\delta c)_{i_0\ldots i_{q+1}}
=\sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(-1)^j\left.c_{i_0\ldots\widehat{i_j}\ldots i_{q+1}}\right|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_{q+1}}}.
$$
Each double omission occurs twice with opposite signs, so $\delta^2=0$. The <Čech cohomology> of the cover is $\check H^q=\ker\delta/\operatorname{im}\delta$, and taking the direct limit over refinements defines cover-independent Čech cohomology. In degree zero the <sheaf gluing axiom> identifies it with $\Gamma(X,\mathcal F)$.

The comparison with derived-functor <sheaf cohomology> is particularly effective algebraically. Positive-degree cohomology of a <quasi-coherent sheaf> on an <affine scheme> vanishes. A separated scheme has affine intersections of affine opens, so a finite affine cover is acyclic for $\mathcal F$. Its <Čech cochain complex> consequently computes $H^q(X,\mathcal F)$ directly. For a <short exact sequence of sheaves>, sections over each affine intersection are exact on quasi-<coherent sheaves>. The resulting short exact sequence of Čech complexes gives the <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology>. In particular, the connecting map measures the failure of a global section of the quotient to lift globally.

Several central projective results make the theory finite and computable. On a <projective variety> over $k$, every coherent cohomology group is finite-dimensional. The <Grothendieck vanishing> theorem gives $H^q(X,\mathcal F)=0$ for $q>\dim X$. If $\mathcal O_X(1)$ is ample, <Serre vanishing> gives $H^{q>0}(X,\mathcal F(m))=0$ for sufficiently large $m$, and these large twists are <globally generated sheaves>. On <projective space> a <coherent sheaf> has a <finite twisting resolution of a coherent sheaf on projective space>: finite sums of $\mathcal O(a)$ resolve it. The explicit line-bundle cohomology below and the long exact sequences prove finiteness and positive-twist vanishing. For a closed embedding $i:X\hookrightarrow\mathbb P^n$, $i_*\mathcal F$ is coherent and $H^q(X,\mathcal F)=H^q(\mathbb P^n,i_*\mathcal F)$, reducing these results to <projective space>. A sufficiently positive twist of a finite presentation by sums of $\mathcal O(-a)$ also proves global generation.

The <Euler characteristic of a coherent sheaf>
$$
\chi(X,\mathcal F)=\sum_q(-1)^q\dim_kH^q(X,\mathcal F)
$$
is therefore defined, and the long exact sequence makes it additive. With a fixed projective embedding, $\chi(X,\mathcal F(m))$ is its <Hilbert polynomial>, and for sufficiently large $m$ it equals $h^0(X,\mathcal F(m))$. On a smooth projective variety of dimension $r$, <Serre duality> gives, for a <vector bundle> $E$,
$$
H^q(X,E)^*\cong H^{r-q}(X,E^*\otimes\omega_X).
$$
For an arbitrary <coherent sheaf> the correct counterpart is $H^q(X,\mathcal F)^*\cong\operatorname{Ext}^{r-q}_X(\mathcal F,\omega_X)$; replacing this Ext group indiscriminately by the cohomology of the ordinary sheaf dual would lose local extension information.

We now calculate the basic examples. For $n\ge1$, use the standard cover $U_i=D_+(x_i)$ of $\mathbb P^n$. Sections of $\mathcal O(m)$ on an intersection are degree-$m$ <Laurent monomials> in which negative exponents are allowed only for coordinates inverted on that intersection. Decompose the <Čech cochain complex> by exponent vectors $(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$ with sum $m$. If no exponent is negative, the corresponding simplex complex contributes only in degree zero. If all exponents are negative, the monomial occurs only on the full intersection and contributes only in degree $n$. When the negative-index set is nonempty and proper, inserting any index outside it gives a contracting homotopy, so the monomial complex is acyclic. Hence the <cohomology of twisting sheaves on projective space> is
$$
H^q(\mathbb P^n,\mathcal O(m))\cong
\begin{cases}
k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]_m,&q=0,\ m\ge0,\\
k^{\binom{-m-1}{n}},&q=n,\ m\le-n-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
In the top group a basis consists of the <Laurent monomials> with every exponent at most $-1$. Writing $a_i=-1-b_i$ reduces the count to nonnegative solutions of $\sum b_i=-m-n-1$, giving the displayed binomial coefficient. In particular, all intermediate cohomology vanishes, and $\omega_{\mathbb P^n}=\mathcal O(-n-1)$ agrees with <Serre duality>. For $\mathbb P^0$ separately, every twist is trivial and $H^0=k$.

On the <projective line> this gives
$$
h^0(\mathcal O(m))=\max(m+1,0),\qquad h^1(\mathcal O(m))=\max(-m-1,0),\qquad\chi(\mathcal O(m))=m+1.
$$
Thus positive and negative degrees produce quite different groups, while their alternating difference is a single polynomial. The <twisted cubic> is isomorphic to $\mathbb P^1$ and its hyperplane bundle corresponds to $\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(3)$. Consequently $h^0(X,\mathcal O_X(m))=3m+1$ for $m\ge0$, agreeing with Q2, and $h^1(X,\mathcal O_X(m))=\max(-3m-1,0)$.

For a smooth plane curve $C$ of degree $d\ge1$, the <ideal-sheaf sequence of a projective hypersurface> is
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}(-d)\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^2}\longrightarrow i_*\mathcal O_C\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology> and the preceding formula give $H^0(C,\mathcal O_C)=k$ and $H^1(C,\mathcal O_C)\cong H^2(\mathbb P^2,\mathcal O(-d))$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{g(C)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.}
$$
For $d=1,2$ this is zero; for a plane cubic it is one. Twisting the same exact sequence gives $\chi(C,\mathcal O_C(m))=dm+1-g$. These examples illustrate how <Čech cohomology>, vanishing, <Serre duality> and exact sequences convert local equations into global numerical invariants.

Use algebraic <Chern classes> in the <Chow ring> of a nonsingular <algebraic variety> $Y$. For a <vector bundle> $E$ of rank $r$, the classes $c_i(E)\in\operatorname{CH}^i(Y)$ are invariant under bundle isomorphism, have $c_0(E)=1$, vanish for $i>r$, and commute with pullback. Their <total Chern class> is $c(E)=\sum_i c_i(E)$. The <Whitney sum formula for Chern classes> applies to every short exact sequence $0\to E'\to E\to E''\to0$:
$$
c(E)=c(E')c(E'').
$$
Normalization for a <line bundle> is $c(\mathcal O_Y(D))=1+[D]$, where $[D]$ is the divisor class. In particular, $c_1(L\otimes M)=c_1(L)+c_1(M)$, $c_1(L^{-1})=-c_1(L)$, and the trivial bundle has total class one. These properties determine the classes: pull back to the iterated <projective bundle> where $E$ has a filtration by <line bundles>. The <projective bundle formula for Chow groups> makes this pullback injective, and Whitney multiplication expresses $c_i(E)$ as the elementary symmetric polynomial of the first classes of the line factors. This also gives $c_i(E^*)=(-1)^ic_i(E)$.

Let $X$ be the smooth degree-$d$ <projective hypersurface> and set $h=c_1(\mathcal O_X(1))$. The <algebraic cotangent bundle> has rank $n-1$. Restriction of the <cotangent Euler sequence in homogeneous coordinates> remains exact, because its terms and quotient are locally free. Thus
$$
0\longrightarrow\Omega^1_{\mathbb P^n}|_X\longrightarrow\mathcal O_X(-1)^{\oplus(n+1)}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_X\longrightarrow0
$$
and Whitney multiplication gives
$$
c(\Omega^1_{\mathbb P^n}|_X)=(1-h)^{n+1}.
$$
The defining equation has degree $d$, so the <conormal sheaf> is $\mathcal O_X(-d)$. Smoothness makes its differential map injective with locally free quotient, giving the <conormal exact sequence for Kähler differentials>
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_X(-d)\longrightarrow\Omega^1_{\mathbb P^n}|_X\longrightarrow\Omega^1_X\longrightarrow0.
$$
A second application of the <Whitney sum formula for Chern classes> yields
$$
\boxed{c(\Omega^1_X)=\frac{(1-h)^{n+1}}{1-dh}.}
$$
The inverse is the finite geometric series in the graded <Chow ring>: terms in codimension greater than $n-1$ vanish. Expanding gives every requested class,
$$
\boxed{c_i(\Omega^1_X)=\left(\sum_{j=0}^i(-1)^j\binom{n+1}{j}d^{i-j}\right)h^i\quad(0\le i\le n-1),\qquad c_i=0\ (i>n-1).}
$$
For example,
$$
c_1(\Omega^1_X)=(d-n-1)h,\qquad
c_2(\Omega^1_X)=\left(d^2-(n+1)d+\binom{n+1}2\right)h^2
$$
when the corresponding ranks and codimensions exist. The first expression agrees with the <canonical bundle of a smooth projective hypersurface> $\det\Omega_X^1=\mathcal O_X(d-n-1)$. For a plane curve, $\deg c_1=d(d-3)=2g-2$, matching Q4. For a quartic surface in $\mathbb P^3$, $c_1=0$ and $c_2=6h^2$, whose degree is $24$. These checks also confirm the sign in the cotangent, rather than tangent, formula.
