= Solution

Let $b\simeq2l_p$ be the <Kuhn length>, $N=L/b$ the number of statistical segments, and $v$ their effective <excluded volume>. We first assume a good-solvent, strongly confined <self-avoiding walk>, rather than an <ideal chain>. A confinement blob contains $g$ links and has diameter $D\sim bg^{3/5}$ in the simple $v\sim b^3$ scaling model. Therefore $g\sim(D/b)^{5/3}$. Distant blobs cannot freely pass through one another in a narrow tube, so the $N/g$ blobs form an axial string. The <de Gennes confinement scaling> is consequently
$$
\boxed{X\sim\frac NgD\sim L\left(\frac bD\right)^{2/3}\sim L\left(\frac{l_p}{D}\right)^{2/3}.}
$$
The <free energy> is of order $k_BT$ per blob, not the <energy> of stretching a straight elastic rod.

The role of solvent and chain thickness can be made explicit by a <Flory approximation>: an axial extension $X$ costs an entropic term $k_BTX^2/(Nb^2)$, while the confined number density gives an interaction term $k_BTvN^2/(XD^2)$. Minimizing their sum gives $X^3\sim vN^3b^2/D^2$, hence
$$
\boxed{X\sim L\left(\frac{v}{bD^2}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
For a thin statistical segment of effective diameter $d$, $v\sim b^2d$ instead gives $X\sim L(bd/D^2)^{1/3}$. Thus the simpler answer implicitly fixes the <excluded volume>; the <DNA> thickness and ionic environment can matter quantitatively.

Both blob arguments require $b\ll D\ll R_{\rm free}$ and $N/g\gg1$, where $R_{\rm free}\sim bN^{3/5}$ in the simple good-solvent model. The printed comparison with $L$ alone does not ensure confinement relative to the unconfined coil. If $D\gg R_{\rm free}$, the tube barely perturbs the coil. An <ideal chain> without <excluded volume> has axial spread of order $\sqrt{Lb}$ and does not acquire the extensive extension of a nonoverlapping blob string. These are distinct physical regimes, not changes to a numerical prefactor.

A slit of height $D$ leaves two unrestricted directions. Its three-dimensional blobs now form a planar <self-avoiding walk>, whose size exponent is $3/4$. The <polymer coil in slit confinement> therefore has in-plane size
$$
\boxed{R_\parallel\sim D(N/g)^{3/4}\sim bN^{3/4}(b/D)^{1/4}.}
$$
There is no uniquely preferred axial direction, and the chain is a coil rather than the one-dimensional blob string of a tube. Including a segment <excluded volume> $v$, the <Flory approximation> is $\mathcal F/(k_BT)\sim R_\parallel^2/(Nb^2)+vN^2/(R_\parallel^2D)$. Its minimum satisfies $R_\parallel^4\sim vN^3b^2/D$, agreeing with the displayed scaling when $v\sim b^3$.

If “two-dimensional channel” instead means an intrinsically planar chain confined to a strip of width $D$, a blob is already two-dimensional: $D\sim bg^{3/4}$, and the blobs form a one-dimensional string. That different geometry gives **$X\sim L(b/D)^{1/3}$**. Stating which transverse direction is removed is essential. In either geometry the blob description requires many blobs and a width below the free-coil size.

Approximate the gap by steady <Couette flow>, neglecting edge effects and additional losses from membrane deformation. Its <velocity> gradient is $U/h$, so the <shear stress> is $\eta U/h$. The <force> on the <gliding bacterium> and the rate of <viscous dissipation> are
$$
F_d=\eta A\frac Uh,\qquad P=F_dU=\eta A\frac{U^2}{h}.
$$
The same power follows by integrating $\eta(U/h)^2$ over gap volume $Ah$. In SI units, $A=10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $h=10^{-7}\,\mathrm m$, and $U=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\dot\gamma=10\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\quad F_d=10^{-12}\,\mathrm N,\quad P=10^{-18}\,\mathrm W.}
$$
An <energy> needs a duration or distance: **$E=10^{-18}\Delta t\,\mathrm J$ for $\Delta t$ in seconds**, equivalently $E=F_ds$ for travel distance $s$. Moving one micrometre takes one second and dissipates $10^{-18}\,\mathrm J$. This is the mechanical cost of the sheared film. A numerical biological efficiency would additionally require the motor's input power and the losses associated with its peristaltic actuation.

Let $a_c\ll R$ be the contact radius of the adhering <neutrophil>. Indentation is of order $a_c^2/R$, so the elastic <strain> is of order $a_c/R$ within a region of volume $a_c^3$. The elastic <energy> is consequently $G a_c^5/R^2$, while adhesion lowers the <energy> by order $Ja_c^2$. Balancing these terms gives
$$
a_c\sim\left(\frac{JR^2}{G}\right)^{1/3},\qquad \frac{a_c}{R}\sim\left(\frac{J}{GR}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
This estimate requires weak adhesion, $J\ll GR$.

For an <adhesive-cell rolling threshold>, assume peeling the rear bonds dissipates work of order $J$ per unit contact area. Rolling through angle $d\theta$ sweeps contact area of order $a_cR\,d\theta$, giving resisting moment $M_r\sim Ja_cR$. A surrounding fluid of <dynamic viscosity> $\eta$ supplies a moment of order $\eta\dot\gamma R^3$. Equating moments yields
$$
\boxed{\dot\gamma_c\sim\frac{Ja_c}{\eta R^2}\sim\frac{J^{4/3}}{\eta G^{1/3}R^{4/3}}.}
$$
A dimensionless numerical coefficient depends on the contact and near-wall <Stokes flow>. Fluid viscosity is indispensable to a rate estimate, even though the contact model alone specifies only $R,G,J$.

A perfectly reversible elastic adhesive contact does not lose net <energy> when an unchanged contact footprint translates. It therefore does not, by itself, justify a finite rolling threshold. The above estimate assumes adhesive hysteresis or irreversible bond peeling. If equilibrium adhesion is $J$ but the work lost during peeling and rebinding is $\Delta J$, retain $a_c\sim(JR^2/G)^{1/3}$ and replace $J$ by $\Delta J$ only in the resisting moment: $\dot\gamma_c\sim\Delta J\,a_c/(\eta R^2)$.

Write $B$ for the <filament bending modulus> and $C$ for the <filament torsional stiffness>, so that the <energy> per unit length is $(B\kappa^2+C\Omega^2)/2$. For one circular filament,
$$
B_1=EI_1=\frac{\pi Er^4}{4},\qquad C_1=G_sJ_1=\frac{\pi G_sr^4}{2},\qquad G_s=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},
$$
where $I_1$ is the <second moment of area>, $J_1$ the polar moment, $G_s$ the <shear modulus>, and $\nu$ <Poisson's ratio>. Numerical twisting constants cannot be fixed by <Young's modulus> alone without specifying $\nu$.

For a filled sliding bundle, $N\sim(R/r)^2$. Filaments share the centerline curvature but slide instead of accumulating the additional axial strains of a bonded cross-section. Their bending energies add: **$B\sim NB_1\sim Er^2R^2$**. If end constraints impose the same material twist on every filament, their torsional energies also add: **$C\sim G_sr^2R^2$**.

For a bonded, one-filament-thick circular tube, the wall thickness $h$ is of order $r$. Its moments are $I\sim\pi R^3h$ and $J_p\sim2\pi R^3h$, giving **$B\sim ErR^3$ and $C\sim G_srR^3$**. For a bonded filled rod, $I\sim\pi R^4/4$ and $J_p\sim\pi R^4/2$, giving **$B\sim ER^4$ and $C\sim G_sR^4$**. Packing fractions and the convention for tube radius affect constants, not these powers.

There is an important qualification to <bending and torsion of filament bundles>: free axial sliding does not specify whether each filament can also spin freely. If both spin and slip are unconstrained, the common-material-twist calculation is not forced by the geometry. A helix of small twist rate has centerline curvature of order $\rho\Omega^2$, so its bending <energy> starts at $\Omega^4$, not $\Omega^2$. A nonzero linear collective torsional stiffness then requires additional end, contact, or spin constraints.

= Solution

The compressive tip <force> is limited by <Euler buckling of an elastic filament>. With unsupported length $\ell$ and <filament bending modulus> $B=EI$,
$$
\boxed{F_c\sim\frac B{\ell^2}\sim\frac{Er^4}{\ell^2}.}
$$
For a hollow <microtubule>, use $I=\pi(r_o^4-r_i^4)/4$ rather than the solid-cylinder value. For a clamped seed and a moment-free tip free to slide along the wall, linearized angle balance is $B\theta''+F\theta=0$, with $\theta(0)=0$ and $\theta'(\ell)=0$. Its first nonzero solution has $\sqrt{F/B}\,\ell=\pi/2$, hence $F_c=\pi^2B/(4\ell^2)$. A different tip constraint changes this coefficient.

Increasing the unsupported length decreases that threshold. For growth against a wall at fixed projected gap $d$, <polymerization-driven cantilever postbuckling> gives a more direct quantitative statement. The inextensible elastica obeys $B\theta''+F\sin\theta=0$. Multiplying by $\theta'$ and using the zero tip moment gives
$$
\frac B2(\theta')^2=F(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_t).
$$
Put $k=\sin(\theta_t/2)$ and substitute $\sin(\theta/2)=k\sin\phi$. Integrating arc length and its horizontal projection gives
$$
\ell=\sqrt{B/F}\,K(k),\qquad d=\sqrt{B/F}\,[2E(k)-K(k)],
$$
where the complete <elliptic integrals> are $K(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-k^2\sin^2\phi)^{-1/2}d\phi$ and $E(k)=\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-k^2\sin^2\phi)^{1/2}d\phi$. Using $K=(\pi/2)(1+k^2/4+\cdots)$ and $E=(\pi/2)(1-k^2/4+\cdots)$ gives $\ell/d=1+k^2+O(k^4)$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{F=\frac{\pi^2B}{4d^2}\left[1-\frac32\frac{\ell-d}{d}+O\left(\frac{(\ell-d)^2}{d^2}\right)\right].}
$$
Thus the initial postbuckling <force> decreases as more contour is inserted. This result describes the weakly buckled branch before overhang, further wall contact, or altered tip constraints invalidate the model. It is a mechanical <force> law; a chemical <force> limit from <polymerization> can impose a smaller <force>.

Set $v=\dot x$, $\zeta=6\pi\mu a$, and $\tau=m/\zeta$. To keep dimensions and the thermal amplitude explicit, write the <force> covariance as $\langle F(t)F(s)\rangle=A\delta(t-s)$. The printed unit coefficient is a choice of noise normalization, not a general dimensional thermal <force>. Assume the initial <velocity> is independent of the future <white noise>. The <underdamped Langevin dynamics> has integrating-factor solution
$$
v(t)=v_0e^{-t/\tau}+\frac1m\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)/\tau}F(s)\,ds.
$$
Taking its <expectation> and using the delta covariance in the double integral gives
$$
\boxed{\langle v(t)\rangle=\langle v_0\rangle e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \langle v(t)^2\rangle=\langle v_0^2\rangle e^{-2t/\tau}+\frac{A}{2m\zeta}(1-e^{-2t/\tau}).}
$$
Here the noise contribution is $A m^{-2}\int_0^t e^{-2(t-s)/\tau}ds$. The <equipartition theorem> requires the long-time value $\langle mv^2/2\rangle=k_BT/2$. Consequently the <fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle> is
$$
\boxed{A=2\zeta k_BT,\qquad \langle F(t)F(s)\rangle=2\zeta k_BT\delta(t-s).}
$$
With an initially thermal <velocity>, $\langle v_0\rangle=0$ and $\langle v_0^2\rangle=k_BT/m$, so the <velocity> variance is stationary. If one insists on the printed coefficient $A=1$ in dimensional units, the model has <temperature> fixed by $k_BT=1/(2\zeta)$ in those units; it cannot describe arbitrary <temperature> while retaining that coefficient.

= Solution

Take $x(0)=0$. Integrating the <velocity> solution gives
$$
x(t)=v_0\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})+\frac1\zeta\int_0^t[1-e^{-(t-s)/\tau}]F(s)\,ds.
$$
The initial/noise cross term vanishes under the independence assumption. Evaluating the remaining integral yields the general <integrated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck displacement>
$$
\langle x(t)^2\rangle=\langle v_0^2\rangle\tau^2(1-e^{-t/\tau})^2+\frac A{\zeta^2}\left[t-2\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})+\frac\tau2(1-e^{-2t/\tau})\right].
$$
Use the thermal initial <velocity> and the <fluctuation-dissipation relation for a Langevin particle>. With <diffusion coefficient> $D=k_BT/\zeta$, this simplifies to
$$
\boxed{\langle x(t)^2\rangle=2D\left[t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})\right].}
$$
For $t\ll\tau$, expansion gives $\langle x^2\rangle=(k_BT/m)t^2+O(t^3)$: displacement is initially $v_0t$, so the particle is **ballistic**. For $t\gg\tau$, $\langle x^2\rangle=2Dt-2D\tau+o(1)$, hence the leading behavior is **one-dimensional <diffusion> with $D=k_BT/(6\pi\mu a)$**.

The ballistic conclusion depends on the initial state. For a particle prepared exactly at rest, $v_0=0$, the first expression gives $\langle x^2\rangle=A t^3/(3m^2)+O(t^4)$ at very short times. A thermal initial <velocity>, or a nonzero initial <velocity> variance, is required for the stated $t^2$ mean-square law. It should not be replaced silently by a zero-<velocity> initial condition.

The numerical contour length is $L=Nb_0=10^{10}\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m=100\,\mathrm m$, and $k_BT=4.14\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm J$. Begin with a three-dimensional <freely jointed chain> in which each chemical <monomer> is also an independent statistical link of length $b_0$. This neglects <excluded volume>, hydrodynamic effects on equilibrium statistics, and stretching of the chemical backbone.

An applied <force> $F$ gives a one-link <canonical partition function>
$$
Z_1=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\xi u}du=4\pi\frac{\sinh\xi}{\xi},\qquad \xi=\frac{Fb_0}{k_BT}.
$$
The mean extension is $z=k_BT\partial_F\log Z_1^N=Nb_0(\coth\xi-1/\xi)$. Expanding the <Langevin function> at small <force> gives $z=Nb_0^2F/(3k_BT)+O(F^3)$. The <three-dimensional entropic chain stiffness> is thus
$$
\boxed{k_s=\frac{3k_BT}{Nb_0^2}=1.242\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
The unforced root-mean-square end-to-end distance is $\sqrt N b_0=10^{-3}\,\mathrm m$, not the contour length. This response is entropic straightening of a fluctuating coil. Axial stretching of a straight material rod instead has stiffness $EA/L$, and is a different phenomenon. Aqueous solvent does not by itself prove ideal-chain statistics: a good-solvent <self-avoiding walk> has a different coil size and susceptibility. The estimate above states explicitly the model under which the supplied link data suffice.

\boxed{E=\frac{64k_BT l_p}{\pi d^4}=1.69\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}.}

This is an effective <Young's modulus> within the given continuum cross-sectional model; the numerical diameter and persistence length must not be replaced by unrelated typical biological values.

An <optical tweezers> <force> requires the change in optical momentum. Define the <optical momentum efficiency> $Q$ by $F=QP/c$, with all ray geometry and medium-index factors absorbed in $Q$ and $c$ the vacuum light speed. The specified relative refractive index does not determine $Q$ without bead size and the beam's convergence, waist, and ray directions. A unique exact <force> is therefore not supplied by these data.

Retaining the entropic stiffness from part (a), the parameterized answer is
$$
F=3.333\times10^{-15}Q\,\mathrm N,\qquad \boxed{z=F/k_s=0.2684Q\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Using the persistence-length-corrected <worm-like chain> instead gives **$z=1.074Q\,\mathrm m$** in its small-<force> regime.

For a definite scale estimate, take the weak-contrast optical momentum change to be $Q\sim m_r-1=0.2$. This gives $F\sim6.67\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm N$, **$z\sim5.37\,\mathrm{cm}$** for part (a)'s model, or **$z\sim21.5\,\mathrm{cm}$** for the corrected chain. These are estimates with an explicitly chosen ray-momentum efficiency, not a universal formula for a transparent trapped sphere.

Another illustrative optical model makes the uncertainty visible: normal-incidence Fresnel reflectance is $\mathcal R=[(m_r-1)/(m_r+1)]^2=1/121$. An effective reflector with $Q=2\mathcal R$ in the simple $P/c$ momentum convention gives $F=5.51\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm N$ and extensions $4.44\,\mathrm{mm}$ or $17.7\,\mathrm{mm}$, respectively. A refracting bead in a focused beam is not just that reflector; entry and exit rays also transfer momentum. Neither estimate eliminates the missing optical geometry.

The viscosity converts to $\eta=0.01\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, and the translation speed is $U=10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Mechanical power is $P_{\rm motor}=\zeta_{\rm chain}U^2$. The <hydrodynamic drag models for a polymer> show why a conformation assumption is necessary.

For a largely aligned slender filament translating along its axis in unbounded fluid, use
$$
\zeta_\parallel\simeq\frac{2\pi\eta L}{\log(2L/a)-1/2},\qquad a=d/2=5\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m.
$$
With $L=100\,\mathrm m$, $\log(2L/a)=\log(4\times10^{11})=26.715$, so $\zeta_\parallel\simeq0.240\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$. The required <force> is about $2.4\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm N$, and
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm motor}\simeq2.4\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm W\quad\text{for aligned axial translation}.}
$$
Transverse translation has roughly twice the leading logarithmic drag. This estimate treats the entire contour as an aligned slender body, assumes creeping flow, and omits bead drag, wall corrections, and additional motor losses. It is not a drag formula for an unstretched coil.

For an alternative nondraining coil, approximate the hydrodynamic radius by its <radius of gyration>, $R_h\sim R_g\simeq\sqrt{Ll_p/3}=8.16\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$. The <Stokes drag law> gives $\zeta\sim6\pi\eta R_h=1.54\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$ and $P\sim1.54\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm W$. A freely draining toy model that sums $N$ spherical-monomer drags of radius $a$ instead gives $P=N6\pi\eta aU^2\simeq9.42\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm W$. Hydrodynamic interactions are suppressed in this last model. These distinct answers explain the need to specify conformation and hydrodynamic coupling; the stated data do not select a unique one.

The printed diagram and the word “parallel” describe a <Kelvin-Voigt model>, not a series <Maxwell fluid>. In the depicted model both elements have displacement $x$ and their <forces> add:
$$
\boxed{F=kx+\mu\dot x.}
$$
For $x=ae^{i\omega t}$ and the convention $G(\omega)=F_{\rm amplitude}/x_{\rm amplitude}$,
$$
\boxed{G(\omega)=k+i\omega\mu,\qquad G'=k,\quad G''=\omega\mu.}
$$
The <storage modulus> and <loss modulus> here are element stiffnesses with units $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$; conversion to stress/<strain> moduli requires a geometric factor. If “response” instead denotes displacement per <force>, it is the compliance $1/(k+i\omega\mu)$. Specifying which ratio is used removes that convention ambiguity. The mean dissipated power is $\mu\omega^2|a|^2/2$, confirming the positive loss sign for $e^{i\omega t}$.

For completeness, the actual series <Maxwell fluid> analogue has a common <force> and additive <spring>/<dashpot> extensions. Thus $\dot x=\dot F/k+F/\mu$. With $\tau=\mu/k$,
$$
G_M(\omega)=\frac{k i\omega\tau}{1+i\omega\tau},\qquad G_M'=\frac{k\omega^2\tau^2}{1+\omega^2\tau^2},\qquad G_M''=\frac{k\omega\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}.
$$
These different functions cannot both describe the printed parallel arrangement. The distinction also changes the <elastic beam>'s response to a constant load.

= Solution

Let $y(x,t)$ be downward deflection, $0\le x\le\ell$, with $y(0,t)=y_x(0,t)=0$. Take a uniform circular <elastic beam>, $I=\pi r^4/4$, small slopes, and linear material response. Write $E$ for its axial elastic modulus and $\eta_s$ for its axial viscous coefficient; these are material quantities, not the solvent viscosity. The extension of the printed parallel model has moment law
$$
M=EIy_{xx}+\eta_sIy_{xxt},\qquad \tau=\eta_s/E.
$$
With <mass> per unit length $\rho A$, transverse momentum balance in the interior is
$$
\boxed{\rho Ay_{tt}+EIy_{xxxx}+\eta_sIy_{xxxxt}=0.}
$$
The free-tip conditions are $M(\ell,t)=0$ and $M_x(\ell,t)=-F(t)$ with this downward-positive convention; the clamp supplies the remaining reactions.

Neglecting <inertia> gives $M=F(t)(\ell-x)$. Twice integrating the moment law with the clamp conditions gives
$$
y+\tau y_t=\frac{F(t)x^2(3\ell-x)}{6EI},\qquad \delta+\tau\dot\delta=\frac{F(t)\ell^3}{3EI},\quad\delta=y(\ell,t).
$$
For a step $F_0$ and an initially relaxed <elastic beam>, <Kelvin-Voigt cantilever creep> is
$$
\boxed{\delta(t)=\frac{F_0\ell^3}{3EI}(1-e^{-t/\tau}),\qquad t\ge0.}
$$
Thus the equivalent tip <spring> and <dashpot> are $k_{\rm eff}=3EI/\ell^3$ and $\mu_{\rm eff}=3\eta_sI/\ell^3$.

If the intended material is instead a true series <Maxwell fluid>, its moment satisfies $\dot M+M/\tau=EIy_{xxt}$. Together with momentum balance $\rho Ay_{tt}+M_{xx}=0$, this gives the full interior equation $\rho A(y_{ttt}+y_{tt}/\tau)+EIy_{xxxxt}=0$, with the moment law retained for boundary and initial conditions. In the quasistatic limit,
$$
\dot\delta=\frac{\ell^3}{3EI}\left(\dot F+\frac F\tau\right),\qquad \boxed{\delta(t)=\frac{F_0\ell^3}{3EI}(1+t/\tau)\quad(t>0).}
$$
This <Maxwell cantilever creep> has an initial elastic jump followed by linear creep, not a finite plateau. The jump is the ideal massless step-load limit; <inertia> resolves the transient. A two-element series model has no nonzero relaxed modulus, so “Maxwell solid” is not an additional specification of a solid with finite long-time stiffness. Such a solid would need an additional <spring>. The small-deflection approximation requires $\delta\ll\ell$ and eventually fails for unbounded creep.

Model the <polymerization Brownian ratchet> by a rigid tip and a Brownian load. Let $a$ be the elementary load advance, $\zeta_L$ its drag coefficient, and $D=k_BT/\zeta_L$ its <diffusion coefficient>. For a spherical load, $\zeta_L=6\pi\mu R_L$, but no load radius is specified, so the rate is naturally expressed in terms of $D$. Assume addition is effectively instantaneous once the gap $h$ reaches $a$, and removal is negligible. The gap reflects at zero, first arrival at $a$ causes insertion, and insertion resets it to zero.

With no opposing <force>, the mean <first-passage time> satisfies $DT''=-1$, $T'(0)=0$, $T(a)=0$. Integration gives $T(h)=(a^2-h^2)/(2D)$. One advance per passage gives
$$
\boxed{T(0)=\frac{a^2}{2D},\qquad V_0=\frac a{T(0)}=\frac{2D}{a}.}
$$
The advance-event rate is $2D/a^2$, while $V_0$ is a length per time.

A constant resisting <force> $F\ge0$ produces drift $-u_F=-DF/(k_BT)$. The <diffusion-limited Brownian ratchet with constant load> now satisfies
$$
DT''-u_FT'=-1,\qquad T'(0)=0,\quad T(a)=0.
$$
An integrating factor gives $T'(h)=[1-e^{u_Fh/D}]/u_F$. Integrating again from $h$ to $a$ gives $T(0)=D(e^z-1-z)/u_F^2$, where $z=Fa/(k_BT)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V(F)=\frac Da\frac{z^2}{e^z-1-z},\qquad \frac{V(F)}{V_0}=\frac{z^2}{2(e^z-1-z)}.}
$$
At weak load the ratio is $1-z/3+O(z^2)$; at large load $V\sim(D/a)z^2e^{-z}$. Replacing the whole <force> dependence by $e^{-z}$ would discard the first-passage kinetics of this regime.

For two staggered strands that alternately advance the leading tip by half a monomer, $a=\delta/2$ and $V_0=4D/\delta$. Two aligned strands, as schematically drawn, do not automatically have that half-step: an advance may be $a=\delta$, with the other strand filling the same layer afterward. Use the same geometrical step in the passage problem and in converting event rate to <velocity>. The limiting formula assumes negligible removal, rigid tips, an overdamped load with constant drag, and fast insertion compared with $a^2/D$. With appreciable removal or additional internal strand states, the renewal reset model must be replaced by a coupled gap/reaction process.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $u$ for the effective unloaded addition rate and $w$ for the removal rate of the same elementary tip step $a$. If the supplied on-rate is a bimolecular rate constant, then $u=k_{\rm on}c_m$, with monomer concentration $c_m$; if it already denotes an event rate, use $u=k_{\rm on}$. Assume gap <diffusion> is much faster than chemical events, removal is <force>-independent, and the monomer bath is maintained.

Under a positive constant resisting <force>, the load equilibrates in the potential $Fh$ above a reflecting tip. The normalized <Boltzmann distribution> is $p(h)=\beta F e^{-\beta Fh}$ for $h\ge0$, where $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. The fraction of time with room to insert is
$$
\int_a^\infty p(h)\,dh=e^{-\beta Fa}.
$$
Steric gating reduces addition to $ue^{-\beta Fa}$; each addition advances by $a$ and each removal retreats by $a$. The <reaction-limited polymerization Brownian ratchet> therefore has
$$
\boxed{V(F)=a\left(ue^{-Fa/(k_BT)}-w\right),\qquad V(0)=a(u-w).}
$$
For $u>w>0$, the stall <force> is **$F_s=(k_BT/a)\log(u/w)$**. Equivalently, the available chemical free <energy> per effective step is $\Delta\mu=k_BT\log(u/w)$, and stall balances $F_sa=\Delta\mu$. A half-monomer step requires rates defined for that half-step, not automatically single-strand monomer rates with a gratuitous factor of two.

At exactly zero <force> a load on an unbounded half-line has no normalizable equilibrium gap density. The unloaded expression is the $F\downarrow0$ limit, or the usual freely available-gap approximation with a weak positional confinement. For a positive load the equilibration time must remain short compared with reaction times. Load <inertia>, filament bending, monomer depletion, and <force>-dependent bond rupture have been excluded. For arbitrary finite reaction and <diffusion> rates one must solve the joint gap dynamics rather than attach $-aw$ to the <diffusion>-limited first-passage formula: a removal changes the gap and the distribution from which the next addition starts.
