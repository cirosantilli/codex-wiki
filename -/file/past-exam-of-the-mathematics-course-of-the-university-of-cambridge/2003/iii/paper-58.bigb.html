= Solution
{parent=i}

Use <cosmic time> $t$ here; derivatives with respect to <conformal time> will be used from Question 2 onward. Conservation of <pressureless matter> and <radiation in cosmology> makes $\rho_m a^3$ and $\rho_r a^4$ constant. Define
$$
M=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_m a^3,\qquad Q=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_r a^4,\qquad \omega^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}|\rho_\Lambda|.
$$
The <Friedmann equation> is a mechanical energy equation,
$$
a_t^2+V(a)=-k,\qquad V(a)=-\frac{M}{a}-\frac{Q}{a^2}+\omega^2a^2.
$$
For nonnegative dust and radiation, at least one nonzero, $V$ tends to $-\infty$ as $a\to0$ and to $+\infty$ as $a\to\infty$, while
$$
V'(a)=\frac{M}{a^2}+\frac{2Q}{a^3}+2\omega^2a>0.
$$
Thus every energy line meets the potential once. An expanding solution reaches this <turning point> and then contracts: $a_{tt}=-V'(a)/2<0$. The <Friedmann effective potential for matter, radiation and vacuum> is sketched below, alongside the positive-vacuum case.

\Image[paper-58-effective-potentials.png]
{title=Friedmann effective potentials for negative and positive vacuum density}

To bound the full <cosmic time> from the initial zero of $a$ to its final zero, use the <Friedmann acceleration equation>:
$$
a_{tt}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho_m+2\rho_r+2|\rho_\Lambda|)a\leq-\omega^2a.
$$
Put maximum expansion at $t=0$, with $a(0)=a_{\max}$ and $a_t(0)=0$. The forced-oscillator identity is
$$
a(t)=a_{\max}\cos(\omega t)+\frac1\omega\int_0^t\sin[\omega(t-s)]\,[a_{ss}(s)+\omega^2a(s)]\,ds.
$$
Until the first zero and for $0\leq t\leq\pi/(2\omega)$, the kernel is nonnegative and the bracket is nonpositive. Hence $a(t)\leq a_{\max}\cos(\omega t)$, so contraction cannot remain at positive $a$ beyond $\pi/(2\omega)$. Applying the same argument backward from maximum expansion bounds the expanding half. Therefore the <negative-vacuum Friedmann lifetime bound> is
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm life}\leq\frac{\pi}{\omega}=\sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{8G|\rho_\Lambda|}}.}
$$
At either endpoint, nonzero dust or radiation has divergent density. If both are absent, the same estimate instead bounds the open <anti-de Sitter spacetime> coordinate patch; its coordinate zeros are not a finite lifetime of the maximally extended vacuum spacetime.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Set $L=8\pi G\rho_\Lambda/3>0$. The <Friedmann effective potential for matter, radiation and vacuum> now reads
$$
V(a)=-\frac{M}{a}-\frac{Q}{a^2}-La^2,\qquad a_t^2+V(a)=E,\qquad E=-k.
$$
With nonzero nonnegative dust or radiation, $V$ tends to $-\infty$ at both ends. Its stationary point satisfies
$$
2La_E^4-Ma_E-2Q=0.
$$
Equivalently $V'(a)=0$ when $2L=M/a^3+2Q/a^4$; the right-hand side decreases strictly, so there is exactly one stationary point. It is a maximum because
$$
V''(a)=-\frac{2M}{a^3}-\frac{6Q}{a^4}-2L<0.
$$
The energy line in the preceding sketch therefore gives **five broad types of history**:

* **Monotonic expansion.** For $E>V(a_E)$, the universe expands from $a=0$ to arbitrarily large $a$.
* **Monotonic contraction.** Reversing <cosmic time> gives the corresponding contracting history.
* **Recollapse.** For $E<V(a_E)$, the inner allowed interval ends at $a_-$: the universe expands from zero to $a_-$ and contracts back to zero.
* **Bounce.** The outer allowed interval starts at $a_+$. A universe contracting from large $a$ reaches $a_+$ and re-expands. Here $V'(a_+)<0$, so $a_{tt}>0$ at the <turning point>.
* **Static equilibrium.** For $E=V(a_E)$, $a=a_E$ and $a_t=0$ give the <Einstein static universe>.

At the critical energy there are also marginal branches within the monotonic classes: expansion from zero can approach $a_E$ asymptotically, or expansion can start asymptotically at $a_E$ and proceed to infinity; contraction gives their reversals. They must not be mistaken for reaching the static solution in finite time. Indeed near the maximum,
$$
E-V(a)\simeq-\tfrac12V''(a_E)(a-a_E)^2,
$$
so $dt\simeq da/[\sqrt{-V''(a_E)/2}\,|a-a_E|]$ diverges logarithmically. The static equilibrium is unstable. Since $V(a_E)<0$, the recollapsing, bouncing and static cases require positive spatial curvature $k>0$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For an <Einstein static universe>, both $a_t$ and $a_{tt}$ vanish. The <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives
$$
0=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho_m+2\rho_r-2\rho_\Lambda)a_E,
$$
and $a_E>0$ therefore implies
$$
\boxed{\rho_m+2\rho_r=2\rho_\Lambda.}
$$
The <Friedmann equation> additionally fixes the spatial curvature:
$$
\frac{k}{a_E^2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}(\rho_m+\rho_r+\rho_\Lambda)>0.
$$
Thus the acceleration balance is necessary but must be accompanied by the static energy constraint.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Without radiation, the <Friedmann acceleration equation> changes sign when $\rho_m=2\rho_\Lambda$. The <cosmological redshift> satisfies $1+z=a_0/a$, and conservation of <pressureless matter> gives $\rho_m(z)=\rho_{m,0}(1+z)^3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm acc}=\left(\frac{2\rho_\Lambda}{\rho_{m,0}}\right)^{1/3}-1
=\left(\frac{2\Omega_{\Lambda,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}\right)^{1/3}-1.}
$$
The expanding spatially flat model is decelerating above this redshift and accelerating below it. Positive $\rho_\Lambda$ is required for such a transition; a negative value gives no accelerated expanding epoch. If the displayed redshift is negative, the transition lies in our future.

= Solution

Write $\mathcal H=a'/a$, where primes denote <conformal time>. In the passive convention for a <cosmological gauge transformation>, retain terms linear in $h$ or $\xi$ and discard their products. The <linear metric gauge-transformation law> then gives
$$
\Delta h_{\mu\nu}=-\eta_{\mu\alpha}\partial_\nu\xi^\alpha
-\eta_{\nu\alpha}\partial_\mu\xi^\alpha
-2\mathcal H\eta_{\mu\nu}\xi^0.
$$
For a single nonzero <Fourier mode>, suppress the common factor $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$. Its derivatives are
$$
\partial_0\xi^0=T',\quad \partial_i\xi^0=ik\widehat k_iT,\quad
\partial_0\xi^i=i\widehat k_iL',\quad
\partial_j\xi^i=-k\widehat k_i\widehat k_jL.
$$
Using $\eta_{00}=-1$ and $\eta_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Delta h_{00}&=2(T'+\mathcal HT),\\
\Delta h_{0i}&=i\widehat k_i(kT-L'),\\
\Delta h_{ij}&=2kL\widehat k_i\widehat k_j-2\mathcal HT\delta_{ij}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The term proportional to $\mathcal HT$ comes from the time dependence of the background <scale factor>; omitting it would transform only the flat reference metric.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the <synchronous-to-Newtonian scalar Fourier transformation>, the coefficient of $\widehat k_i\widehat k_j$ in the transformed spatial <metric perturbation> must vanish. Thus $h_s+2kL=0$. Vanishing shift then gives $L'=kT$. For $k\ne0$ these conditions fix
$$
\boxed{L=-\frac{h_s}{2k},\qquad T=-\frac{h_s'}{2k^2},\qquad
\xi^\mu=\left(-\frac{h_s'}{2k^2},-\frac{i\widehat k_i h_s}{2k}\right)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}.}
$$
The lapse in <conformal Newtonian gauge> is $h_{00}^{\rm N}=-2\Phi$, so
$$
\Phi=-(T'+\mathcal HT)=\boxed{\frac{h_s''+\mathcal Hh_s'}{2k^2}}.
$$
The remaining coefficient of $\delta_{ij}$ is
$$
\frac{h-h_s}{3}-2\mathcal HT=-2\Psi,
$$
giving
$$
\boxed{\Psi=-\frac{h-h_s}{6}-\frac{\mathcal Hh_s'}{2k^2}.}
$$
The homogeneous $k=0$ perturbation requires separate background-coordinate choices; the inverse powers of $k$ do not define a transformation for it.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Preserving <synchronous gauge> means $T'+\mathcal HT=0$ and $L'=kT$. The first equation is $(aT)'=0$, and integration of the second gives the complete residual scalar transformation for each nonzero <Fourier mode>:
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{f}{a},\qquad L=kf\int^\tau\frac{ds}{a(s)}+g.}
$$
Changing the lower limit only changes $g$. Taking the trace and trace-free longitudinal part of $\Delta h_{ij}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta h=2kL-6\mathcal HT,\qquad \Delta h_s=2kL.}
$$
Consequently $\Delta h_s'=2k^2T$ and $\Delta h_s''=2k^2T'$. The <cosmological Newtonian potential> changes by
$$
\Delta\Phi=\frac{\Delta h_s''+\mathcal H\Delta h_s'}{2k^2}=T'+\mathcal HT=0,
$$
while its spatial counterpart changes by
$$
\Delta\Psi=-\frac{\Delta h-\Delta h_s}{6}-\frac{\mathcal H\Delta h_s'}{2k^2}
=\mathcal HT-\mathcal HT=0.
$$
Thus **both potentials are invariant under residual synchronous-gauge freedom**, although $h$ and $h_s$ individually are not.

= Solution
{parent=a}

During <matter domination>, the spatially flat background has $a\propto\tau^2$ and $\mathcal H=2/\tau$. Let $\theta_m=\nabla\cdot\mathbf v_m$ denote the scalar velocity divergence. Linear <stress-energy conservation> in <synchronous gauge> gives
$$
\delta_m'=-\theta_m-\frac12h',\qquad \theta_m'+\mathcal H\theta_m=0.
$$
The first equation follows from the perturbed volume element $a^3(1+h/2)$; the second states that peculiar dust velocity redshifts as $a^{-1}$. Differentiating the first and substituting the trace of the <Einstein field equations>, with $w_m=0$ and $\Omega_m=1$, yields
$$
\delta_m''+\mathcal H\delta_m'-\frac32\mathcal H^2\delta_m
=-(\theta_m'+\mathcal H\theta_m)=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\delta_m''+\frac2\tau\delta_m'-\frac6{\tau^2}\delta_m=0.
$$
Trying $\delta_m\propto\tau^p$ gives $p(p-1)+2p-6=(p-2)(p+3)=0$. The complete solution of this density equation is
$$
\boxed{\delta_m(\mathbf x,\tau)=A(\mathbf x)\tau^2+B(\mathbf x)\tau^{-3}.}
$$
These are the <matter-era growing and decaying density modes>, proportional to $a$ and $a^{-3/2}$. In a dust-comoving <synchronous gauge>, $\theta_m=0$ and $h'=-2\delta_m'$. That rest-frame choice distinguishes physical mode coefficients from changes of density induced by <residual synchronous-gauge freedom>.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Define the <matter power spectrum> by the <Fourier transform> convention
$$
\delta(\mathbf x,\tau)=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\delta_{\mathbf k}(\tau)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x},\qquad
\langle\delta_{\mathbf k}\delta_{\mathbf k'}^*\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')P_\delta(k,\tau).
$$
Statistical isotropy then gives
$$
\langle\delta^2\rangle=\int d\log k\,\frac{k^3P_\delta(k,\tau)}{2\pi^2}.
$$
For the growing mode found above, write $P_\delta(k,\tau)=\tau^4P_A(k)$. At <cosmological horizon entry>, $\tau_H\propto k^{-1}$, so the variance contribution per logarithmic interval is proportional to $k^3k^{-4}P_A(k)=P_A(k)/k$. Making it independent of $k$ requires $P_A(k)\propto k$. Hence
$$
\boxed{P_\delta(k,\tau)\propto\tau^4k.}
$$
This is the <Harrison-Zeldovich spectrum>. Scale invariance applies at horizon entry, not to the unprocessed density variance on a common late-time slice. In the notation $\delta_{\mathbf k}\propto k^{n/2}\tau^2$, it means **$n=1$**, with random Fourier coefficients supplying the ensemble statistics.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Neglecting the rapidly oscillating radiation source on subhorizon scales, <stress-energy conservation> and the trace of the <Einstein field equations> give
$$
\delta_m''+\mathcal H\delta_m'-4\pi Ga^2\rho_m\delta_m=0.
$$
Equivalently in <cosmic time>, $\delta_{m,tt}+2H\delta_{m,t}-4\pi G\rho_m\delta_m=0$. Radiation still contributes to the background expansion, even when its perturbation source is averaged away.

Put $\eta=a/a_{\rm eq}$ and let $\rho_{\rm eq}$ be the density of either component at <matter-radiation equality>. Then $\rho_m=\rho_{\rm eq}\eta^{-3}$ and $\rho_r=\rho_{\rm eq}\eta^{-4}$. With $C=(8\pi G/3)\rho_{\rm eq}a_{\rm eq}^2$, the <Friedmann equation> gives
$$
\mathcal H^2=C\frac{1+\eta}{\eta^2},\quad
(\eta')^2=C(1+\eta),\quad \eta''=\frac C2,\quad
4\pi Ga^2\rho_m=\frac{3C}{2\eta}.
$$
Apply the <chain rule> to the density equation:
$$
C(1+\eta)\delta_{\eta\eta}+C\left(\frac12+\frac{1+\eta}{\eta}\right)\delta_\eta-\frac{3C}{2\eta}\delta=0.
$$
Thus the <Mészáros equation> is
$$
\boxed{2\eta(1+\eta)\delta_{\eta\eta}+(2+3\eta)\delta_\eta-3\delta=0.}
$$
Substituting $D_+=1+3\eta/2$ gives $(2+3\eta)(3/2)-3(1+3\eta/2)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\delta_m=A(\mathbf x)(1+3\eta/2)}
$$
is indeed a solution. It is approximately constant during <radiation domination> and grows as $a$ during <matter domination>. The second independent solution is
$$
D_-=(1+3\eta/2)\log\frac{\sqrt{1+\eta}+1}{\sqrt{1+\eta}-1}-3\sqrt{1+\eta},
$$
which behaves as $\log(4/\eta)-3$ at small $\eta$. Thus smoothing radiation does not eliminate every logarithmic solution; matching through actual horizon entry fixes the combination excited.

Modes with $k\ll k_{\rm eq}$ enter after equality and retain the $P(k)\propto k$ shape. Modes with $k\gg k_{\rm eq}$ enter during radiation domination: their horizon-entry spectrum is $P_H\propto k^{-3}$ and they gain little growth until equality. The late <matter power spectrum> therefore turns over near $k_{\rm eq}$ and has **a large-scale $k$ branch and a small-scale $k^{-3}$ branch**, with the more accurate radiation-era matching giving
$$
P(k)\propto k^{-3}\log^2(k/k_{\rm eq})\quad(k\gg k_{\rm eq}).
$$
Equivalently the <cold-dark-matter transfer function> falls approximately as $(k_{\rm eq}/k)^2$, up to the logarithm. The turnover is the consequence of the <Mészáros effect>; it is not a change of the primordial scale-invariance condition.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The emission term comes from the local <photon> temperature. A blackbody radiation density is proportional to $T^4$, so linearization gives $\delta T/T=\delta_\gamma/4$. It is evaluated at last scattering, with the source position on the observed ray.

The velocity term is the <Doppler CMB anisotropy>: motion of the emitting plasma changes the measured photon frequency by the component of its peculiar velocity along the line of sight. Its sign here follows the paper's choice of the sky direction $\widehat{\mathbf n}$.

The metric integral follows the <null geodesic> energy equation in <synchronous gauge>. Time dependence of the spatial <metric perturbation> changes the photon energy by $-h_{ij}'n_in_j/2$ along the ray. It includes gravitational redshift endpoint terms as well as the part due to evolving gravitational potentials. In particular, **the entire synchronous metric integral is not just the integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect**. Integrating by parts to express it in <conformal Newtonian gauge> separates the ordinary <Sachs-Wolfe effect> from the evolving-potential contribution.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use one <Fourier mode>, with $\mu=\widehat{\mathbf k}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}$ and unperturbed ray $\mathbf x(\tau)=\widehat{\mathbf n}(\tau_0-\tau)$. Put $E(\tau)=e^{ik\mu(\tau_0-\tau)}$, and let $\tau_*$ denote last scattering. In the supplied branch $h'=h_s'$, the scalar decomposition gives
$$
h_{ij}'n_in_j=h'/3+h_s'(\mu^2-1/3)=\mu^2h_s'.
$$
Since $E''=-k^2\mu^2E$, two <integrations by parts> give the exact endpoint decomposition of the metric contribution:
$$
-\frac{\mu^2}{2}\int_{\tau_*}^{\tau_0}h_s'E\,d\tau
=\frac{[h_s'E'-h_s''E]_{\tau_*}^{\tau_0}}{2k^2}
+\frac1{2k^2}\int_{\tau_*}^{\tau_0}h_s'''E\,d\tau.
$$
For the matter-era growing mode, $h_s$ is quadratic in $\tau$, so the last integral vanishes. With $E'=-ik\mu E$, the emission endpoint is
$$
E_*\left(\frac{h_s''}{2k^2}+\frac{i\mu h_s'}{2k}\right)_*,
$$
and the observer endpoint is $-h_s''(\tau_0)/(2k^2)-i\mu h_s'(\tau_0)/(2k)$. The observer terms are only a <angular monopole> and <angular dipole>; they are removed when studying the anisotropy multipoles $\ell\geq2$.

For an adiabatic growing mode outside the horizon at last scattering, $\delta_\gamma=4\delta_m/3$, $\delta_m=-h/2$ after fixing the constant trace convention, and $h\propto\tau^2$. Hence the intrinsic emission term is smaller than $h_s''/(2k^2)$ by order $(k\tau_*)^2$. The derivative emission endpoint is smaller by order $k\tau_*$. <Stress-energy conservation> also makes the peculiar emission velocity subleading; its leading synchronous contribution cancels in $\delta_\gamma'+2h'/3$, with gradients supplying the remaining small term. Keeping the Fourier phase, which need not be small over the observer's distance, gives
$$
\boxed{\Theta(\widehat{\mathbf n})\simeq\frac{h_s''(\tau_*)}{2k^2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\chi_*},\qquad \chi_*=\tau_0-\tau_*.}
$$
Here $\Theta=\delta T/T$, and the sum over modes is understood. The derivative is the **second derivative of $h_s$**, as printed in the original PDF. This approximation assumes matter-era growing adiabatic modes and neglects late evolving potentials; large wavelength alone does not remove the last integral in a general cosmology. The equality $h'=h_s'$ is used as the supplied synchronous condition, rather than as a general identity for every fluid solely from vanishing anisotropic stress.

= Solution

Write the growing <density contrast> as $\delta_{m,\mathbf k}=A_{\mathbf k}\tau^2$, with $A_{\mathbf k}$ having the indicated $k^{n/2}$ spectral amplitude. In the dust-comoving <synchronous gauge>, $h'=-2\delta_m'$, and the supplied $h_s'=h'$ therefore implies $h_s''=-4A_{\mathbf k}$. On the same comoving density slicing, the <cosmological Poisson equation> is
$$
-k^2\Phi_{\mathbf k}=4\pi Ga^2\rho_m\delta_{m,\mathbf k}
=\frac32\mathcal H^2\delta_{m,\mathbf k}=6A_{\mathbf k},
$$
because $\mathcal H=2/\tau$. This is the comoving-density form of the relativistic scalar constraint, so it can be used on these large scales; replacing its density by the Newtonian-gauge density would not be justified there. Combining the two results gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{h_s''}{2k^2}=-\frac{2A_{\mathbf k}}{k^2}=\frac13\Phi_{\mathbf k}.}
$$
Thus the large-angle <Sachs-Wolfe effect> has source $\Phi/3$. The potential is constant for this matter-era growing mode, and its <power spectrum> obeys $P_\Phi(k)\propto k^{n-4}$.

\boxed{a_{\ell m}=\frac{4\pi i^\ell}{3}\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}

If $A_{\mathbf k}=A\,k^{n/2}$ denotes a mode coefficient with its random amplitude and phase included in $A$, substitution of $\Phi_{\mathbf k}=-6A_{\mathbf k}/k^2$ gives the equivalent factor $-8\pi i^\ell A_{\mathbf k}/k^2$ inside the Fourier integral. = Solution
{parent=e}

Let $\langle\Phi_{\mathbf k}\Phi_{\mathbf k'}^*\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')P_\Phi(k)$. Statistical isotropy and $\int|Y_{\ell m}|^2d\Omega=1$ applied to the preceding expression give the <CMB angular power spectrum>
$$
C_\ell=\frac{2}{9\pi}\int_0^\infty k^2P_\Phi(k)j_\ell(k\chi_*)^2\,dk.
$$
For $n=1$, define the constant dimensionless potential power $\mathcal A_\Phi=k^3P_\Phi(k)/(2\pi^2)$. Then
$$
C_\ell=\frac{4\pi\mathcal A_\Phi}{9}\int_0^\infty j_\ell(x)^2\frac{dx}{x}.
$$
The needed <logarithmic spherical Bessel square integral> can be proved directly. Put $I_\ell=\int_0^\infty j_\ell^2\,dx/x$ and $J_\ell=\int_0^\infty j_\ell j_{\ell-1}\,dx$. From the <spherical Bessel function> recurrences,
$$
j_\ell'=j_{\ell-1}-\frac{\ell+1}{x}j_\ell,\qquad
j_{\ell-1}'=\frac{\ell-1}{x}j_{\ell-1}-j_\ell,
$$
integration of the squares' derivatives gives $J_\ell=(\ell+1)I_\ell$, and for $\ell>1$ also $J_\ell=(\ell-1)I_{\ell-1}$. At $\ell=1$, $j_0(0)=1$ and $j_0(\infty)=0$ give $J_1=1/2$, hence $I_1=1/4$. Induction now proves $I_\ell=1/[2\ell(\ell+1)]$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{C_\ell=\frac{2\pi\mathcal A_\Phi}{9\ell(\ell+1)},\qquad
\ell(\ell+1)C_\ell=\frac{2\pi\mathcal A_\Phi}{9}\quad(\ell\geq2).}
$$
This is the flat large-angle <Sachs-Wolfe plateau>. The useful integral printed in the PDF is missing the square on $j_\ell$: without that square the formula is false, since at $\ell=1$ its left-hand side is $\pi/4$, whereas its asserted value would be $1/4$. The squared identity above is the one required by the variance calculation. The plateau describes the matter-era large-angle approximation, not acoustic peaks or late integrated-potential corrections.

= Solution

Introduce <conformal time>
$$
\eta=\int_0^t\frac{ds}{\cosh s}=2\arctan[\tanh(t/2)]=\arcsin(\tanh t),
\qquad -\frac\pi2<\eta<\frac\pi2.
$$
It satisfies $d\eta/dt=\operatorname{sech}t$ and $\cos\eta=\operatorname{sech}t$, so the metric becomes $ds^2=\sec^2\eta(-d\eta^2+d\xi^2)$. Choose <null coordinates>
$$
\boxed{u=\xi-\eta,\qquad v=\xi+\eta,\qquad
ds^2=\sec^2\!\left(\frac{v-u}{2}\right)du\,dv.}
$$
Thus $f(u,v)=\sec[(v-u)/2]$ is positive on $-\pi<v-u<\pi$. Choosing the two null coordinates with these orientations gives the requested positive coefficient of $du\,dv$.

There is a global-topology distinction in the source: unrestricted $\xi\in\mathbb R$ gives the <universal cover of two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime>. The usual <two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime> has $\xi$ periodic modulo $2\pi$. Indeed its standard embedding is $X^0=\sinh t$, $X^1=\cosh t\cos\xi$, $X^2=\cosh t\sin\xi$, and is unchanged by $\xi\mapsto\xi+2\pi$. The local metric and the null-coordinate calculation apply to either choice.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The positive <conformal factor> leaves the <causal structure> unchanged. In the strip $-\pi/2<\eta<\pi/2$, a future-directed <causal curve> obeys
$$
\left|\frac{d\xi}{d\eta}\right|\leq1,
$$
and <null geodesics> have slopes $\pm1$. Its past and future conformal boundaries $\eta=\mp\pi/2$ are spacelike. A finite <conformal time> interval limits the range of light signals: the observer at $\xi=0$ can receive a signal from $(\eta,\xi)$ before future infinity exactly when $|\xi|<\pi/2-\eta$. Equality describes its observer-dependent <cosmological event horizon>.

For a finite <Penrose diagram> of the unwrapped spatial coordinate, set $U=\arctan u$, $V=\arctan v$, $X=(U+V)/2$, and $Y=(V-U)/2$. Then the metric is conformal to $-dY^2+dX^2$; light rays remain at $45$ degrees, and the boundaries are the images of $v-u=\pm\pi$. The figure also shows the spatially periodic de Sitter cylinder so that the global distinction is explicit.

\Image[paper-58-causal-diagram.png]
{title=Causal compactification of the unwrapped de Sitter strip and the spatially periodic de Sitter cylinder}

**Both the covering spacetime and the de Sitter cylinder are globally hyperbolic.** To see this directly in the covering spacetime, suppose an inextendible future-directed <causal curve> had a future limiting conformal time strictly below $\pi/2$. The slope bound makes $\xi$ Cauchy as that time is approached, so the curve has a limiting point inside the spacetime and can be extended, a contradiction. The past endpoint similarly approaches $-\pi/2$. Thus every constant-$\eta$ line is crossed exactly once and is a <Cauchy hypersurface>. On the cylinder the same argument uses compactness of the spatial circle. Equivalently causal diamonds between finite events are bounded and closed within the strip, hence compact.

Initial field data on a complete <Cauchy hypersurface> determine a unique solution for a well-posed hyperbolic <Cauchy problem>, with the relevant constraints imposed for constrained systems. Spacelike conformal infinity does not require additional timelike-boundary conditions. An observer's cosmological horizon limits which data that observer can see; it does not destroy global uniqueness from data on the whole hypersurface.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Vary the action with compactly supported variations and integrate by parts. Its overall normalization does not affect the equation of motion:
$$
\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu\phi)=0.
$$
Here $\sqrt{-g}=e^{3t}$, so the <massless scalar field> obeys
$$
\phi_{tt}+3\phi_t-e^{-2t}\nabla^2\phi=0.
$$
Separate a spatial <Fourier mode> as $\phi(t,\mathbf x)=F_k(t)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$. Its mode equation is $F_{k,tt}+3F_{k,t}+k^2e^{-2t}F_k=0$. With <conformal time> $\tau=-e^{-t}$, $a=-1/\tau$ and $\mathcal H=-1/\tau$, it becomes
$$
F_k''-\frac2\tau F_k'+k^2F_k=0.
$$
For $k>0$, put $z=-k\tau=ke^{-t}$ and $F_k=z^2G(z)$. Direct differentiation reduces the equation to
$$
G_{zz}+\frac2zG_z+\left(1-\frac2{z^2}\right)G=0,
$$
the <spherical Bessel function> equation with order one. Hence the complete classical mode basis is
$$
\boxed{F_k(t)=z^2[C_{\mathbf k}j_1(z)+D_{\mathbf k}y_1(z)],\qquad z=ke^{-t}.}
$$
Since $z^2j_1(z)=\sin z-z\cos z$ and $z^2y_1(z)=-\cos z-z\sin z$, an equivalent complex basis is
$$
\boxed{F_k(\tau)=A_{\mathbf k}(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau}
+B_{\mathbf k}(1-ik\tau)e^{ik\tau}.}
$$
The classical action does not select one frequency branch or a quantum vacuum. A real field has conjugate-related coefficients at opposite wavevectors. The zero mode, which is not obtained by treating these two $k>0$ basis functions as independent at $k=0$, is
$$
\boxed{F_0(t)=C_0+D_0e^{-3t}.}
$$
At late times one independent solution freezes and the other decays as $e^{-3t}$. These are minimally coupled massless modes; the supplied action has no curvature-coupling term, so it should not be replaced by the conformally coupled wave equation.

Normalize a point source by $\int\rho\,d^3x\,dz=q_1$ on the compact spatial direction. The periodic delta has the <Fourier series>
$$
\delta_{2\pi R}(z)=\frac1{2\pi R}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{inz/R}.
$$
Writing the potential as $\phi(r,z)=\sum_n\phi_n(r)e^{inz/R}$ reduces the <Poisson equation> to
$$
\left(\nabla_3^2-\frac{n^2}{R^2}\right)\phi_n=-2q_1\delta^3(\mathbf x).
$$
Away from the source, the decaying radial solution is proportional to $e^{-|n|r/R}/r$. Its distributional normalization follows from integrating the Laplacian over a small sphere: the radial flux of $1/r$ is $-4\pi$, so
$$
\phi_n(r)=\frac{q_1}{2\pi r}e^{-|n|r/R}.
$$
This is the contribution of the $n$th <Kaluza-Klein mode>, with inverse range $|n|/R$. At equal compact coordinate, the sum is a geometric series,
$$
\phi(r,0)=\frac{q_1}{2\pi r}\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-nr/R}\right)
=\frac{q_1}{2\pi r}\coth\frac{r}{2R}.
$$
Therefore the pair interaction energy, without the individual self-energies, is
$$
\boxed{U(r)=\frac{q_1q_2}{2\pi r}\coth\frac{r}{2R}.}
$$
Its normalization is fixed by the stated $-4\pi R\rho$ source, rather than by importing another convention for <Coulomb's law>. More generally summing the phases gives the <periodic electrostatic Green function with one compact dimension>,
$$
\phi(r,z)=\frac{q_1}{2\pi r}\frac{\sinh(r/R)}{\cosh(r/R)-\cos(z/R)}.
$$
An independent check uses the <method of images> in four spatial dimensions. Since $\nabla_4^2s^{-2}=-4\pi^2\delta^4$, its periodic image sum is $Rq_1\sum_n[r^2+(z+2\pi nR)^2]^{-1}/\pi$, with the same normalization and short-distance singularity.

For $r\gg R$, only the zero <Kaluza-Klein mode> survives:
$$
\boxed{U(r)=\frac{q_1q_2}{2\pi r}\left[1+2e^{-r/R}+O(e^{-2r/R})\right].}
$$
For $r\ll R$, $\coth x=x^{-1}+x/3+O(x^3)$ gives
$$
\boxed{U(r)=\frac{q_1q_2R}{\pi r^2}+\frac{q_1q_2}{12\pi R}+O(r^2/R^3).}
$$
The leading potential thus changes from inverse distance to inverse squared distance. Differentiating gives the signed outward force
$$
F_r=\frac{q_1q_2}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\coth(r/(2R))}{r^2}
+\frac{\operatorname{csch}^2(r/(2R))}{2Rr}\right].
$$
It scales as $r^{-2}$ at large distance and as $2q_1q_2R/(\pi r^3)$ at small distance. **A precision inverse-square-law test over separations approaching $R$ would reveal the extra dimension** through this change of force law or its exponential corrections. The model assumes localized sources at equal compact coordinate and an electromagnetic field able to explore the circle; a field confined to ordinary spacetime would not show this effect. Finite source size and ordinary screening must be controlled when comparing a real force measurement with this point-source prediction.
