= Solution
{parent=1}

Assume first that the <topological group> is Hausdorff. This separation condition is necessary: an indiscrete group with two elements has the one-element <neighbourhood basis> consisting of the whole group, but its <topology> cannot come from a <metric>.

Translations are <homeomorphisms>, since multiplication by a fixed element is continuous and its inverse is multiplication by the inverse element. Inversion is likewise a <homeomorphism>. If $W$ is an <neighbourhood> of the <identity element>, continuity of the triple product at $(e,e,e)$ supplies <neighbourhoods> of the <identity element> $A,B,C$ with $ABC\subset W$. Their intersection with their inverses contains a symmetric open <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> $V$, and $V^3\subset W$. This proves the <neighbourhood> shrinking property needed below rather than assuming it.

Let $(B_n)$ be a countable <neighbourhood basis> at the <identity element>, choosing open representatives inside its members. Starting with $U_0=G$, recursively choose symmetric open <neighbourhoods> of the <identity element> such that
$$
U_n\subset B_n\cap U_{n-1},\qquad U_n^3\subset U_{n-1}\quad(n\ge1).
$$
They are still a basis. Their intersection is $\{e\}$: for $x\ne e$, the Hausdorff condition gives an <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> not containing $x$, and some $U_n$ lies inside it.

Here is the <dyadic product estimate for group neighbourhoods>. If $g_j\in U_{n_j}$ and $\sum_j2^{-n_j}\le2^{-n}$, then the ordered product lies in $U_{n-1}$, for $n\ge1$. Prove this by induction on the number of factors. A single factor has $n_j\ge n$ and hence belongs to $U_n\subset U_{n-1}$. For a longer word, choose the factor crossing the half-total-cost point. The words preceding and following it each have cost at most half the total, hence at most $2^{-n-1}$. The induction hypothesis puts both in $U_n$, and the middle factor also lies in $U_n$. Their threefold product is in $U_{n-1}$. Empty side words cause no problem because $e\in U_n$.

Give an edge from $x_{j-1}$ to $x_j$ cost $2^{-n_j}$ whenever $x_{j-1}^{-1}x_j\in U_{n_j}$, and define
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^m2^{-n_j}:x_0=x,\ x_m=y,\ x_{j-1}^{-1}x_j\in U_{n_j}\right\}}.
$$
Chains exist because $U_0=G$. Reversing a chain proves symmetry, concatenating chains proves the <triangle inequality>, and translating every vertex on the left leaves all edge differences unchanged. Thus $d$ is left invariant. The product estimate also gives
$$
d(x,y)<2^{-n-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ x^{-1}y\in U_n,
\qquad x^{-1}y\in U_n\ \Longrightarrow\ d(x,y)\le2^{-n}.
$$
The first implication follows by choosing a chain with total cost below the bound and applying the estimate with index $n+1$. If $d(x,y)=0$, the first implication puts $x^{-1}y$ in every $U_n$, so $x=y$. Hence $d$ is a genuine <left-invariant group metric>. These two inclusions show that its balls and the original <neighbourhoods> of the <identity element> refine one another; translations then show equality of the two <topologies> everywhere. More explicitly, every <metric> ball contains a sufficiently small translated $U_n$, and every open <neighbourhood> contains a sufficiently small <metric> ball. This proves the sufficient direction of the <Birkhoff-Kakutani theorem>.

Conversely, if a compatible <metric> exists, the balls $B_d(e,1/n)$ form a countable <neighbourhood basis> at the <identity element>. Invariance is not even needed for this direction. Thus, with the necessary Hausdorff convention, **a compatible left-invariant <metric> exists exactly when the <identity element> has a countable <neighbourhood basis>**.

For the requested counterexample, take the <orientation-preserving affine group of the real line>, represented by $(a,b)$ with $a>0$ and product $(a,b)(a',b')=(aa',b+ab')$. Its <topology> is metrizable, for instance by the ordinary Euclidean distance in coordinates $(\log a,b)$, and the product and inverse are continuous. Let $D_a=(a,0)$ and $T_b=(1,b)$. Direct multiplication gives $D_aT_bD_a^{-1}=T_{ab}$. A compatible <bi-invariant group metric> would make every <conjugation> an <isometry> fixing $e$. Therefore
$$
d(e,T_{1/n})=d(e,D_nT_{1/n}D_n^{-1})=d(e,T_1)>0.
$$
But $T_{1/n}\to e$ in the group's <topology>, a contradiction. This is the <affine conjugation obstruction to a bi-invariant group metric>: the group is metrizable, and even admits a compatible left-invariant <metric>, but **admits no compatible <metric> invariant on both sides**.

= Solution

There is an essential qualification to the printed hypothesis. If <compactly generated group> means merely generated algebraically by a <compact subset>, the claim is false. In the additive group $\mathbb Q$ with its usual <topology>,
$$
K=\{0\}\cup\{1/n!:n\ge1\}
$$
is compact, being a convergent sequence together with its limit, and generates every rational number since any integer denominator divides some factorial. The group is metrizable. However, an invariant <Radon measure> has a common mass $c$ on each singleton. If $c>0$, the <compact set> $K$ has infinite measure, contradicting finiteness on <compact sets>. If $c=0$, countable additivity makes the entire countable group have measure zero. Thus it has no nonzero <Haar measure>. This proves that <compact generation does not imply local compactness> and disproves the literal unrestricted statement.

The intended existence statement is valid when local <compactness> is included, either explicitly or in the convention that the group has a compact generating <neighbourhood>. Here is a construction for a locally compact, compactly generated, metrizable Hausdorff group. No invariant measure is assumed during its construction.

Enlarge a compact generating set to a symmetric compact <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> $S$. Then $G=\bigcup_nS^n$, and each $S^n$ is compact. Moreover $S^n$ lies in the interior of $S^{n+1}$, since multiplication by the open <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> contained in $S$ supplies an open <neighbourhood> of every point of $S^n$. This gives a compact exhaustion. Every compact <metric> space has a countable <dense subset>: take finite $1/k$-nets and unite them. Their union over this exhaustion is dense in $G$, so the <metric> space is separable and has a countable open basis.

We use nonnegative <compactly supported> <continuous functions> and cutoffs equal to one on specified <compact sets>. Their existence is covered by the source's permission concerning auxiliary functions. Fix a nonzero such function $h$. For nonzero $u\ge0$ define the weighted covering quantity
$$
(f:u)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^m c_j:f\le\sum_{j=1}^m c_jT_{x_j}u,\ c_j\ge0\right\},\qquad
T_xu(y)=u(x^{-1}y),\qquad I_u(f)=\frac{(f:u)}{(h:u)}.
$$
The quantities are finite: an <open set> where $u$ is bounded below has translates covering the <compact support> of $f$, so finitely many sufficiently large weighted translates dominate it. The denominator is positive, since evaluation at a point where $h>0$ gives $(h:u)\ge h(y)/\|u\|_\infty>0$.

Each $I_u$ is nonnegative, homogeneous, monotone, left invariant and subadditive; these follow respectively by rescaling, comparing or combining covers, and translating their centers. Also $I_u(h)=1$. Composing a cover of $f$ by translates of $h$ with a cover of $h$ by translates of $u$ gives
$$
(f:u)\le(f:h)(h:u),\qquad 0\le I_u(f)\le(f:h).
$$
This is a bound independent of $u$. On functions supported in a fixed <compact set> $C$, choose a cutoff $k\ge1$ on $C$. Monotonicity and subadditivity imply
$$
|I_u(f)-I_u(g)|\le\|f-g\|_\infty I_u(k)\le\|f-g\|_\infty(k:h).
$$
Thus the covering functionals are uniformly Lipschitz on each fixed support.

Choose a nonzero $u_n\ge0$ with support in an <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> shrinking to $e$. The nonnegative functions supported in each compact member of the exhaustion form a separable <metric> cone in the <supremum norm>, as a subspace of <continuous functions> on that compact <metric> space. Select a countable dense family from each cone and include $h$. The uniform scalar bounds permit a diagonal subsequence along which $I_{u_n}$ converges on their countable union. The displayed Lipschitz estimate extends convergence to every nonnegative member of $C_c(G)$. Denote the limit by $I$. It inherits positivity, homogeneity, monotonicity, subadditivity, invariance and $I(h)=1$.

The crucial remaining step is additivity. Fix $f_1,f_2\ge0$ with combined support $C$, and fix a compact <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> $V_0$. Choose a cutoff $k$ equal to one on $CV_0^{-1}V_0$. For $\delta>0$, put
$$
F=f_1+f_2+\delta k,\qquad r_i=f_i/F,
$$
setting the ratio to zero off the denominator's nonzero set. These ratios are continuous: $k=1$ on a <neighbourhood> of the supports, and off those supports the numerator vanishes. Their sum is at most one. <Compactness> and continuity give, for each $\eta>0$, an <neighbourhood> of the <identity element> $V$ small enough that each $r_i$ oscillates by at most $\eta$ on any translate of $V$ meeting $C$. To justify this uniform choice, such translates lie inside the fixed <compact set> $CV_0^{-1}V_0$ when $V\subset V_0$; continuity of $(y,v)\mapsto r_i(yv)$ and a finite cover of that <compact set> give a single small right-increment <neighbourhood>, then choose $V^{-1}V$ inside it.

If $u$ has support in $V$ and $F\le\sum_j c_jT_{x_j}u$, discard translates not meeting $C$ when covering $f_i$. Let $a_{ij}$ be the supremum of $r_i$ on the remaining translate's support. Then $f_i\le\sum_j a_{ij}c_jT_{x_j}u$, and comparing both suprema with the ratios at one common point gives $a_{1j}+a_{2j}\le1+2\eta$. Taking covering infima yields
$$
I_u(f_1)+I_u(f_2)\le(1+2\eta)I_u(F)
\le(1+2\eta)[I_u(f_1+f_2)+\delta I_u(k)].
$$
Along the subsequence this holds eventually, since its supports shrink. Pass to $I$, then let $\eta\downarrow0$ and $\delta\downarrow0$; the uniform bound on $I_u(k)$ gives $I(f_1)+I(f_2)\le I(f_1+f_2)$. Subadditivity gives the reverse inequality. Thus $I$ is additive.

Extend $I$ linearly to real functions using positive and negative parts, and then complex-linearly to $C_c(G)$. The compact-support bounds above make it a positive, locally bounded functional. The <Riesz representation on compactly supported continuous functions> gives a <Radon measure> $\mu$ with $I(f)=\int f\,d\mu$. Translation invariance of $I$ and uniqueness in that representation give $\mu(xE)=\mu(E)$ for Borel $E$. It is nonzero because $I(h)=1$, and finite on <compact sets> because a cutoff dominating their indicators has finite integral. Finally every nonempty <open set> has positive measure: otherwise its translates cover the <compact support> of $h$, a finite subcover would give that support measure zero, contradicting $I(h)=1$. Hence **$\mu$ is a <Haar measure> under the stated local-compactness qualification**. This is <Haar measure from normalized covering functionals>, with the limiting additivity proved explicitly.

Let $G$ be locally compact, Abelian and Hausdorff, and fix a <Haar measure>. Write $T_xf(y)=f(x^{-1}y)$ and use <convolution>
$$
(f*g)(y)=\int_G f(x)g(x^{-1}y)\,dx.
$$
The <L1 group algebra> is a commutative <Banach algebra>. Here a <group character> means a continuous <homomorphism> $\chi:G\to\mathbb T$, not the trace of an arbitrary group representation. A <Gelfand character> is a nonzero multiplicative complex-linear functional on the <convolution> algebra. We use the convention
$$
\boxed{\Phi_\chi(f)=\int_G f(x)\chi(x)\,dx}.
$$
With the alternative standard Fourier convention involving $\overline\chi$, one simply conjugates every <group character> in this identification.

For such $\chi$, the functional is bounded by $\|f\|_1$, and <Fubini's theorem>, the substitution $y=xz$, and the <homomorphism> law give
$$
\Phi_\chi(f*g)=\int\!\int f(x)g(z)\chi(xz)\,dz\,dx
=\Phi_\chi(f)\Phi_\chi(g).
$$
It is nonzero: if $u\ge0$ is nonzero in $C_c(G)$, then $f=\overline\chi u$ has $\Phi_\chi(f)=\int u>0$.

Conversely let $\Phi$ be an <algebra character>. It is automatically bounded, with $|\Phi(f)|\le\|f\|_1$. Indeed, extend it to the <unitization> by $\widetilde\Phi(f+\lambda1)=\Phi(f)+\lambda$. If $|\Phi(f)|>\|f\|_1$, the <Neumann series> makes $f-\Phi(f)1$ invertible, whereas its character value is zero, contradicting multiplicativity of an invertible element and its inverse.

Choose $g$ with $\Phi(g)\ne0$ and put $\chi(x)=\Phi(T_xg)/\Phi(g)$. Commutativity of the group gives $(T_xf)*g=f*(T_xg)$; applying $\Phi$ yields
$$
\Phi(T_xf)=\chi(x)\Phi(f)\quad\text{for every }f.
$$
Thus the ratio is independent of the chosen $g$. Applying the relation successively to $T_xT_yg=T_{xy}g$ gives $\chi(xy)=\chi(x)\chi(y)$, and $\chi(e)=1$. In particular it never vanishes.

The translation map $x\mapsto T_xg$ is continuous in $L^1$. For $g\in C_c(G)$, translated supports near a fixed $x$ lie in one <compact set>; continuity on that set and a finite cover give <uniform convergence> of the translated values, hence $L^1$ convergence because the <compact set> has finite <Haar measure>. For general $g$, approximate by $C_c(G)$ and use $\|T_xg\|_1=\|g\|_1$. Therefore $\chi$ is continuous. For all integers $n$,
$$
|\chi(x)|^n=\frac{|\Phi(T_{x^n}g)|}{|\Phi(g)|}
\le\frac{\|g\|_1}{|\Phi(g)|}.
$$
Positive powers exclude modulus greater than one; negative powers exclude modulus less than one. Hence $|\chi(x)|=1$.

Finally, the $L^1$-valued <convolution> formula $f*g=\int f(x)T_xg\,dx$ and boundedness of $\Phi$ give
$$
\Phi(f)\Phi(g)=\Phi(f*g)=\int f(x)\Phi(T_xg)\,dx
=\Phi(g)\int f(x)\chi(x)\,dx.
$$
Cancel $\Phi(g)$ to obtain the displayed formula for every $f$. Different characters give different functionals: if their continuous difference is nonzero at a point, integration against a suitably phased compactly supported test function in a small <neighbourhood> detects that difference. Thus the correspondence is bijective.

It also identifies the <character space of an Abelian L1 group algebra> topologically with the <Pontryagin dual group>. For a net converging uniformly on <compact subsets> in $\widehat G$, convergence of the functionals follows first for compactly supported $f$, then for all $L^1$ functions using the common <norm> bound one. Conversely suppose the functionals converge in the <Gelfand topology>. Choose $g$ whose limiting character value is nonzero; its values are eventually bounded away from zero. For compact $K\subset G$, the set $\{T_xg:x\in K\}$ is compact in $L^1$. Convergence of uniformly bounded <linear functionals> is uniform on this <compact set>: a finite norm-net reduces it to convergence at finitely many functions, with the common bound controlling the approximation error. The ratio formula then gives <uniform convergence> of the <group characters> on $K$. This proves equality with the <compact-open topology>, without assuming first countability of $G$.

For later use, these transforms form a self-adjoint subalgebra dense in $C_0(\widehat G)$. The set of <algebra characters> together with the zero functional is weak-star compact by <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> and the closed multiplicativity equations. Removing zero gives a locally compact Hausdorff <character space>; each evaluation $\Phi\mapsto\Phi(f)$ vanishes at infinity, since its modulus-at-least-$\epsilon$ set is compact and avoids zero. Transforms separate characters, and none vanishes identically at a given character. They are closed under <conjugation> because $f^*(x)=\overline{f(x^{-1})}$ satisfies $\Phi_\chi(f^*)=\overline{\Phi_\chi(f)}$. The locally compact <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> therefore gives the claimed density.

= Solution

For a locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group $G$, <Bochner's theorem> states that a continuous <positive-definite function> $p$ has a unique representation
$$
\boxed{p(x)=\int_{\widehat G}\chi(x)\,d\nu(\chi),\qquad
\nu\ge0,\quad\nu(\widehat G)=p(e)},
$$
where $\nu$ is a finite <Radon measure> on the <Pontryagin dual group>. Conversely every such measure gives a continuous <positive-definite function>. Here positive definiteness means $\sum_{i,j}c_i\overline{c_j}p(x_ix_j^{-1})\ge0$ for all finite choices. The Abelian and continuity hypotheses are part of the theorem; the representation is not claimed for arbitrary non-Abelian groups or discontinuous functions.

First prove existence without presupposing the representation. The elementary consequences of positive definiteness give $p(x^{-1})=\overline{p(x)}$, $p(e)\ge0$ and $|p(x)|\le p(e)$. If $p(e)=0$, then $p=0$ and take $\nu=0$. Otherwise form finite formal linear combinations of symbols $[x]$, with sesquilinear form
$$
\langle[x],[y]\rangle=p(xy^{-1}),
$$
linear in its first variable. Positive definiteness makes this form <positive semidefinite>. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for such a form shows that null vectors are orthogonal to everything, so quotienting by them gives an <inner-product space>. Complete it to a <Hilbert space> $H$. Since $G$ is Abelian, translation $U_a[x]=[ax]$ preserves the form and extends to a <unitary operator>. Its inverse is $U_{a^{-1}}$, so $(U_a)$ is a <unitary representation>. With $v=[e]$,
$$
p(x)=\langle U_xv,v\rangle,\qquad\|v\|^2=p(e).
$$
The translated copies of $v$ span a dense subspace. Moreover
$$
\|U_a[y]-[y]\|^2=2[p(e)-\operatorname{Re}p(a)]\longrightarrow0\quad(a\to e).
$$
Finite sums and density, together with $\|U_a\|=1$, give <strong continuity> on all of $H$. This constructs the <cyclic unitary representation of a positive-definite function> directly.

For $f\in L^1(G)$ define the <integrated unitary representation> $\pi(f)=\int f(x)U_x\,dx$, with the integral applied to each vector. Its <norm> is at most $\|f\|_1$. Fubini gives $\pi(f*g)=\pi(f)\pi(g)$, and inversion in the Abelian group gives $\pi(f^*)=\pi(f)^*$. Thus
$$
\mathcal A=\overline{\pi(L^1(G))}^{\|\cdot\|}
$$
is a commutative <C-star algebra>. It is nondegenerate: choose nonnegative compactly supported functions $u_V$ of Haar integral one with supports shrinking to the <identity element>. <Strong continuity> implies
$$
\|\pi(u_V)w-w\|\le\sup_{x\in V}\|U_xw-w\|\longrightarrow0
$$
for every $w\in H$. These form an <approximate identity> in the representation. <Neighbourhoods> may form a net, since no metrizability is being assumed here.

The <commutative Gelfand--Naimark theorem> identifies $\mathcal A$ with $C_0(X)$, where $X$ is its <character space>. The positive vector functional $\omega(B)=\langle Bv,v\rangle$ becomes a bounded positive functional on $C_0(X)$, so the <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> supplies a finite positive <Radon measure> $\rho$ with
$$
\omega(B)=\int_X\theta(B)\,d\rho(\theta).
$$
These general representation theorems are applied to the explicitly constructed algebra; they are not an assumption of Bochner's conclusion.

For each $\theta\in X$, the functional $f\mapsto\theta(\pi(f))$ is a nonzero <algebra character> of $L^1(G)$, since $\pi(L^1(G))$ is dense in $\mathcal A$. By Question 3 it has the form $\int f(x)\chi_\theta(x)\,dx$. The resulting map $\iota:X\to\widehat G$ is continuous: pointwise convergence of $\theta$ on $\mathcal A$ gives convergence on all $\pi(f)$, and the topological character identification in Question 3 gives compact-open convergence of $\chi_\theta$. Push $\rho$ forward under $\iota$ to obtain $\nu$. This <finite measure> is Radon: <compact subsets> approximating the measure of any inverse-image <Borel set> have compact images, giving inner regularity; finiteness and complements then give outer regularity.

To recover $p(x)$, note that $U_x\pi(u_V)=\pi(T_xu_V)\in\mathcal A$. Hence
$$
\langle U_x\pi(u_V)v,v\rangle
=\int_X\chi_\theta(x)\Phi_{\chi_\theta}(u_V)\,d\rho(\theta).
$$
The left side tends to $p(x)$. On the right, $|\Phi_\chi(u_V)|\le1$ and $\Phi_\chi(u_V)\to1$ uniformly on <compact subsets> of $\widehat G$. For completeness, the evaluation $(\chi,x)\mapsto\chi(x)$ is jointly continuous: near $x_0$ restrict $x$ to a compact <neighbourhood>, control the character uniformly there, and use continuity of the fixed character. A finite cover of a <compact set> of characters then makes those characters equicontinuous at $e$, proving the stated <uniform convergence>. Continuity of $\iota$ gives <uniform convergence> on <compact subsets> of $X$, and the finite <Radon measure> $\rho$ makes the remaining tails arbitrarily small. Thus passage to the limit is valid even for a net, and
$$
p(x)=\int_X\chi_\theta(x)\,d\rho(\theta)=\int_{\widehat G}\chi(x)\,d\nu(\chi).
$$
Taking $x=e$ also gives the required total mass $p(e)$.

Conversely, a finite positive <Radon measure> gives
$$
\sum_{i,j}c_i\overline{c_j}p(x_ix_j^{-1})
=\int_{\widehat G}\left|\sum_i c_i\chi(x_i)\right|^2d\nu(\chi)\ge0.
$$
Its transform is continuous. Indeed, choose a <compact set> of characters with arbitrarily small measure outside it; joint continuity of evaluation gives uniform continuity in $x$ near the specified point on that <compact set>, while the outside contribution is bounded by twice its measure. This proves continuity without using an unjustified dominated-convergence assertion for arbitrary nets.

Finally suppose two finite <Radon measures> have the same transform. Multiply by $f\in L^1(G)$ and integrate over $G$. Fubini shows that the measures agree on every function $\chi\mapsto\Phi_\chi(f)$. Question 3 showed that these functions are uniformly dense in $C_0(\widehat G)$. Boundedness of both measure functionals extends equality to all of $C_0(\widehat G)$; uniqueness in the Riesz representation theorem gives equality of the measures. This proves existence, uniqueness and the converse, completing Bochner's theorem. In particular **$p(e)=1$ corresponds exactly to a <probability measure>** on the dual group.
