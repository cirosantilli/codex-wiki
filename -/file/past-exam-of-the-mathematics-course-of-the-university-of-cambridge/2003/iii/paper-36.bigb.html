= Solution
{parent=1}

For a <linear code> $X\leq\mathbb F^n$, its <dual code> is
$$
X^\perp=\{y\in\mathbb F^n:\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i=0\text{ for every }x\in X\}.
$$
The standard <bilinear form> is nondegenerate. The map $\mathbb F^n\to X^*$ obtained by restricting the dot-product functional to $X$ is <surjective>: extend a functional from a <basis> of $X$ to a <basis> of $\mathbb F^n$, then use the nondegenerate <dot product> to represent it. Its <kernel> is $X^\perp$. The <rank-nullity theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\dim X^\perp=n-\dim X.}
$$

The first proposed description of the <dual code> is **false without self-orthogonality**. At length three take $X=\langle(1,0,0)\rangle$. It has the specified <dimension>, but
$$
X^\perp=\{(0,a,b):a,b\in\mathbb F_2\},
$$
whereas $\langle X,(1,1,1)\rangle$ contains $(1,0,0)$ and $(1,1,1)$, neither of which is in $X^\perp$. The natural corrected statement is the <odd-length binary self-orthogonal dual extension>: if also $X\subseteq X^\perp$, every word of $X$ has even <Hamming weight>, the all-ones word lies in $X^\perp$ but not in $X$, and <dimensions> then give $X^\perp=X\oplus\langle\mathbf1\rangle$.

For a binary <self-dual code>, $x\cdot x=0$ for every $x\in X$. In $\mathbb F_2$, this equals $\sum_i x_i$, so every <codeword> has even <Hamming weight>. Thus $\mathbf1\cdot x=0$ for all $x$, giving $\mathbf1\in X^\perp=X$. The <dimension> identity yields $2\dim X=n$, so $n$ is even. Alternatively $\mathbf1\in X$ and $\mathbf1\cdot\mathbf1=0$ itself gives evenness. Hence **the second assertion is true**.

For every $n=2m$, use
$$
X=\{(u,u):u\in\mathbb F_2^m\},\qquad G=(I_m\mid I_m).
$$
This <linear code> has <dimension> $m$, and $(u,u)\cdot(v,v)=2(u\cdot v)=0$. Therefore $X\subseteq X^\perp$; both have <dimension> $m$, so equality holds. This constructs a binary <self-dual code> at every even length. Conversely the <dimension> identity already proves **every self-dual length-$n$ <linear code> has <dimension> $n/2$**.

For a nonbinary example, over $\mathbb F_5$ take $X=\langle(1,2)\rangle$. Its generator has self-product $1+2^2=0$ in $\mathbb F_5$, so $X\subseteq X^\perp$. Both are one-dimensional, proving
$$
\boxed{\langle(1,2)\rangle\leq\mathbb F_5^2\text{ is self-dual}.}
$$

A <finite field> is a <field> with finitely many elements. Its characteristic is the least positive integer $p$ for which $p\cdot1=0$; such an integer exists because the additive group is finite. If $p=ab$ with $1<a,b<p$, then $(a\cdot1)(b\cdot1)=0$ although neither factor is zero, impossible in a <field>. Hence $p$ is prime, and the multiples of $1$ form its <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$. The whole <field> is a finite-dimensional <vector space> over this subfield, say of <dimension> $s\geq1$, so its cardinality is $p^s$. Thus **$q=p^s$ and its characteristic is $p$**.

For existence, let $K$ be a <splitting field> over $\mathbb F_p$ of $P(T)=T^{p^s}-T$. Since $P'(T)=-1$, it has exactly $p^s$ distinct roots. Its root set $F$ contains $0,1$ and is closed under addition and multiplication, because iterated <Frobenius endomorphism> gives
$$
(a+b)^{p^s}=a^{p^s}+b^{p^s},\qquad (ab)^{p^s}=a^{p^s}b^{p^s}.
$$
It is also closed under additive inverses, and for $a\ne0$, $(a^{-1})^{p^s}=(a^{p^s})^{-1}=a^{-1}$. Thus $F$ is a <field> with exactly $p^s$ elements. Since it contains every root, it is the entire <splitting field>. This proves existence.

For uniqueness, let $L$ be any <field> with $p^s$ elements. Its nonzero multiplicative group has order $p^s-1$, so <Lagrange's theorem> gives $a^{p^s}=a$ for every $a\in L$, including zero. Hence $L$ is a <splitting field> of the same <polynomial>. Here is the embedding argument giving uniqueness, rather than merely invoking it by name. Extend the identity on $\mathbb F_p$ from one root at a time. If an embedding is already defined on $E$ and a new root $\alpha$ has <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $h$ over $E$, the transported <polynomial> divides $P$ and therefore has a root $b$ in $L$. Evaluation at $b$ induces an embedding $E[T]/(h)\to L$, extending the existing embedding to $E(\alpha)$. Repeating for the finitely many roots embeds the constructed <field> $F$ in $L$. They have the same finite cardinality, so this embedding is an <isomorphism>. Thus **the <field> of order $p^s$ is unique up to <isomorphism>**.

We prove cyclicity of the multiplicative group, also establishing the more general <finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic> result. Let $H$ be a finite subgroup of a <field>'s multiplicative group, and let $e$ be the least common multiple of all element orders. For every prime $\ell$ dividing $e$, choose an element whose order has the maximal $\ell$-power $\ell^{a_\ell}$; raising it to the factor of its order coprime to $\ell$ gives an element of order $\ell^{a_\ell}$. Since the group is abelian, the product of these elements has order $\prod_\ell\ell^{a_\ell}=e$: for commuting elements of coprime orders, an equation $(ab)^t=1$ forces $a^t=b^{-t}$ into the intersection of their cyclic subgroups, which is trivial, so both orders divide $t$. Every member of $H$ is a root of $T^e-1$. The <Lagrange root bound over a field> gives $|H|\leq e$, while the element just constructed has order $e$ and gives $e\leq|H|$. Thus equality holds and that element generates $H$. In particular
$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_{p^s}^{\times}\cong\mathbb Z/(p^s-1)\mathbb Z.}
$$

For the <field with nine elements>, use $\mathbb F_3[u]/(u^2+1)$. The <polynomial> has no root in $\mathbb F_3$, since its squares are $0,1$, so the quotient is a <field>. In the <basis> $(1,u)$, a vector $(a,b)$ represents $a+bu$, and

(a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d),\qquad(a,b)(c,d)=(ac-bd,ad+bc)

A <root of unity over a finite field> is an element $a\in\overline{\mathbb F}_q$ satisfying $a^n=1$, with $n\geq1$; the <algebraic closure> is essential because all roots need not lie in the base <field>. Write $p=\operatorname{char}\mathbb F_q$ and $n=p^a m$, where $p\nmid m$. In characteristic $p$,
$$
T^n-1=(T^m-1)^{p^a}.
$$
The latter <polynomial> has $m$ distinct roots, since the derivative $mT^{m-1}$ is nonzero at every root. Those roots are closed under multiplication and inversion and contain $1$, so $E(n,q)$ is a finite multiplicative subgroup of a <field>. The proof in the preceding solution makes it a <cyclic group>. In particular
$$
\boxed{|E(n,q)|=m,\qquad |E(n,q)|=n\text{ if }\gcd(n,q)=1.}
$$
This also explains why the coprimality qualification is needed.

The <multiplicative group of a finite field> $\mathbb F_{q^s}^{\times}$ is cyclic of order $q^s-1$. It contains the entire group of $m$th roots precisely when $m\mid q^s-1$. For necessity, a generator of $E(n,q)$ has order $m$ and <Lagrange's theorem> applies. For sufficiency, a <cyclic group> whose order is divisible by $m$ contains $m$ roots of $T^m-1$; since there are exactly $m$ roots in the <algebraic closure>, these are all of them. Hence the least positive extension degree is
$$
\boxed{s=\operatorname{ord}_m(q)\quad(m>1),\qquad s=1\quad(m=1).}
$$
Here $\operatorname{ord}_m(q)$ is the <multiplicative order> of the residue class of $q$ modulo $m$. If $n$ is coprime to $q$, replace $m$ by $n$. Existence of this order follows because $q$ is a unit modulo $m$.

A primitive $n$th <root of unity over a finite field> has <multiplicative order> exactly $n$. If $p\mid n$, no such element exists, since all roots have order dividing $m<n$. If $\gcd(n,q)=1$, choose a generator $\zeta$ of $E(n,q)$. The element $\zeta^j$ has order $n/\gcd(n,j)$, so it is primitive precisely when $\gcd(n,j)=1$. Therefore **there are $\varphi(n)$ primitive roots**, with $\varphi$ the <Euler totient function>.

If $\omega$ is primitive of order $n$, then $\omega\in\mathbb F_{q^\ell}$ exactly when $\omega^{q^\ell}=\omega$, equivalently $n\mid q^\ell-1$. The implication from that equality to membership follows because the roots of $T^{q^\ell}-T$ form $\mathbb F_{q^\ell}$, as proved in the preceding solution. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\min\{\ell\geq1:\omega\in\mathbb F_{q^\ell}\}=\operatorname{ord}_n(q)}
$$
for $n>1$, and the degree is one for $n=1$. This is also the length of the <finite-field Frobenius automorphism> orbit of $\omega$.

Using the same $(1,u)$ <basis> of $\mathbb F_9$ as before, the fourth roots of unity are
$$
\boxed{E(4,9)=\{1,-1,u,-u\}=\{(1,0),(2,0),(0,1),(0,2)\}.}
$$
There are four, they are already in $\mathbb F_9$, and the primitive fourth roots are the two vectors $(0,1)$ and $(0,2)$.

= Solution
{parent=4}

A <cyclic code> of length $n$ over $\mathbb F_q$ is a <linear code> invariant under $(c_0,\ldots,c_{n-1})\mapsto(c_{n-1},c_0,\ldots,c_{n-2})$. Associate the word with $c(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_iT^i$. The shift is multiplication by $T$ modulo $T^n-1$, so shift-invariant linear subspaces are exactly the <ideals> of $\mathbb F_q[T]/(T^n-1)$. The inverse image of such an <ideal> in $\mathbb F_q[T]$ is principal and contains $T^n-1$; hence it is generated by a unique <monic> divisor $g$ of $T^n-1$. The code has <basis> $g,Tg,\ldots,T^{n-\deg g-1}g$, and <dimension> $n-\deg g$.

The <defining zeros of a cyclic code> are the roots of its generator $g$ in a <splitting field>. Since $g\mid T^n-1$, each is a <root of unity over a finite field>. Every <codeword> vanishes at each such root. If the characteristic divides $n$, root multiplicities must additionally be retained; in the Hamming construction below the modulus has no repeated roots.

**The unrestricted ternary cyclic-equivalence assertion is false.** At $s=2$ it would give a cyclic $[4,2,3]_3$ code. But
$$
T^4-1=(T-1)(T+1)(T^2+1)
$$
over $\mathbb F_3$, with the quadratic irreducible. A dimension-two <cyclic code> needs a degree-two generator, so its only possibilities are $T^2-1$ and $T^2+1$. Each is a weight-two <codeword>. Therefore neither has minimum distance three, and no code equivalent to the ternary $[4,2,3]$ Hamming code can be cyclic. A valid general version of the asserted construction assumes **odd $s$**. The usual distance-three Hamming parameters also require $s\geq2$; when $s=1$ the code is the length-one zero code.

Here is the construction with its precise qualification. A <ternary Hamming code> has a <parity-check matrix> whose columns represent each one-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb F_3^s$ once. There are $n=(3^s-1)/2$ such subspaces. Its <rank> is $s$, because the coordinate lines are included. Its minimum distance is at least three, because no column is zero and no two are proportional. For $s\geq2$, two independent vectors and their sum determine three distinct columns, and, after adjusting their nonzero representative scalars, give a weight-three linear relation. Thus its parameters are $[n,n-s,3]_3$. Permuting columns and changing their nonzero representatives produces a <monomial equivalence of linear codes>.

Take odd $s\geq3$ and choose $\alpha\in\mathbb F_{3^s}^{\times}$ of order $n$. Since $n$ is odd, $-1$ is not in the cyclic subgroup generated by $\alpha$. Hence $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1}$ lie in distinct one-dimensional $\mathbb F_3$-subspaces: a proportional pair would have ratio $1$ or $-1$, neither possible for two distinct powers in this list. By their number they represent all <projective points>. Consequently
$$
C=\{c\in\mathbb F_3^n:\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_i\alpha^i=0\}
$$
is monomially equivalent to the <ternary Hamming code>. Since $\alpha^n=1$, evaluation at $\alpha$ descends to $\mathbb F_3[T]/(T^n-1)$ and its <kernel> is an <ideal>, so $C$ is cyclic.

The degree of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb F_3$ is $s$. If it were $d<s$, its generated <field> would have $3^d$ elements and its element order would give
$$
n\leq3^d-1\leq3^{s-1}-1<\frac{3^s-1}{2}=n,
$$
a contradiction. Its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> therefore has degree $s$. Applying Frobenius to a <polynomial> relation shows that $\alpha,\alpha^3,\ldots,\alpha^{3^{s-1}}$ are its roots; they are distinct by the same field-degree argument. Their product <polynomial> is the <monic> <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element>, and it divides $T^n-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{g(T)=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}(T-\alpha^{3^j}),\qquad Z(C)=\{\alpha^{3^j}:0\leq j<s\}.}
$$
This identifies all the defining zeros. For $s=3$, $n=13$, the defining exponents are $1,3,9$ modulo $13$.

There is also a uniform construction for all $s\geq2$, explaining the missing qualification. Let $\beta$ generate $\mathbb F_{3^s}^{\times}$, which has order $2n$. The powers $1,\beta,\ldots,\beta^{n-1}$ represent all <projective points>, since proportional powers differ by exponent $0$ or $n$ modulo $2n$. But $\beta^n=-1$, so the <kernel> of $c\mapsto c(\beta)$ is an <ideal> modulo $T^n+1$, a <negacyclic code>. Multiplication by $T$ gives $(-c_{n-1},c_0,\ldots,c_{n-2})$, preserving its <kernel>. This code is always equivalent to the <ternary Hamming code>. If $s$ is odd, $n$ is odd too, and the substitution $T\mapsto-T$, or coordinate scaling $c_i\mapsto(-1)^ic_i$, changes this negacyclic construction into the cyclic one. In fact $\alpha=-\beta$ then has order $n$. These explicit constructions prove the qualified assertion rather than relying on a theorem name.

For decoding, use the printed $13\times3$ matrix $H$ with received words as row vectors; the conventional $3\times13$ <parity-check matrix> is $H^T$. The received word's <syndrome> is
$$
s=rH=(2,0,2)\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_3^3.
$$
A single-symbol error of value $a$ at position $i$ has <syndrome> $a h_i$, where $h_i$ is row $i$ of the printed matrix. Here
$$
(2,0,2)=2(1,0,1)=2h_6.
$$
Therefore subtract $2$ at the sixth coordinate, changing its received value $1$ to $2$ modulo three. The decoded <codeword> is
$$
\boxed{(2,1,2,0,1,2,0,0,2,1,1,2,0).}
$$
Its <syndrome> is zero. The thirteen projective rows and the two nonzero scalars give all $26$ nonzero <syndromes> exactly once, so this is the unique <codeword> at distance one. It is the transmitted word under the usual assumption of at most one symbol error. If positions $1,2,5$ are designated check positions, their independent unit rows make the other ten symbols systematic data, namely $(2,0,2,0,0,2,1,1,2,0)$.
