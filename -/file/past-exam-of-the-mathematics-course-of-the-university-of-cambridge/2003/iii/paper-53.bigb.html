= Solution
{parent=1}

Take $\{\gamma^a,\gamma^b\}=2\eta^{ab}$ and $\gamma^{ab}=\gamma^{[a}\gamma^{b]}=[\gamma^a,\gamma^b]/2$, with unit-weight antisymmetrization. The <Clifford algebra> first gives
$$
\gamma_a\gamma^m\gamma^a=(2-D)\gamma^m,\qquad
\gamma_a\gamma_b\gamma^a=(2-D)\gamma_b.
$$
Expand the two antisymmetrized <gamma matrices> using $\gamma_{ab}=\gamma_a\gamma_b-\eta_{ab}$:
$$
\gamma_{ab}\gamma^m\gamma^{ab}
=\gamma_a\gamma_b\gamma^m\gamma^a\gamma^b-D\gamma^m.
$$
Anticommuting $\gamma^m$ past the next contracted factor gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_a\gamma_b\gamma^m\gamma^a\gamma^b
&=-\gamma_a\gamma_b\gamma^a\gamma^m\gamma^b+2\gamma^m\gamma_b\gamma^b\\
&=-(2-D)\gamma_b\gamma^m\gamma^b+2D\gamma^m\\
&=\bigl[2D-(D-2)^2\bigr]\gamma^m.
\end{aligned}
$$
Subtracting the remaining $D\gamma^m$ gives the <gamma-matrix vector sandwich>
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-(D-1)(D-4).}
$$
In particular it vanishes in four dimensions. The result is independent of the choice of Lorentz signature when all indices are contracted consistently. Defining the bivector without its factor $1/2$ would multiply $\alpha$ by four.

Choose the <charge conjugation> matrix so that $C^T=-C$ and $(\gamma^m)^T=-C\gamma^mC^{-1}$, and write $\bar\lambda=\lambda^TC$. Since the components are odd <Grassmann variables>,
$$
\lambda^TM\lambda=\frac12\lambda^T(M-M^T)\lambda.
$$
Thus a <symmetric matrix> gives zero, whereas a nonzero <antisymmetric matrix> can give a nonzero bilinear. The <four-dimensional Majorana bilinear vanishing criterion> follows by transposing an antisymmetrized product and reversing its $n$ factors:
$$
\bigl(C\gamma^{m_1\ldots m_n}\bigr)^T
=-(-1)^{n(n+1)/2}C\gamma^{m_1\ldots m_n}.
$$
For $n=0,1,2,3$, the symmetry types are respectively antisymmetric, symmetric, symmetric and antisymmetric. These are the same-spinor selection rules of the <Majorana spinor>; they would be reversed for a commuting spinor. **The vanishing choices are (a) and (c).**

This is the vector case of the <four-dimensional Majorana bilinear vanishing criterion>.

The matrix $C$ is antisymmetric, so the scalar <Majorana spinor> bilinear is **not identically zero**. For example, choose two independent odd <Grassmann variables> $\lambda_\alpha,\lambda_\beta$ at a pair of indices with $C_{\alpha\beta}\ne0$. Their contribution is $2C_{\alpha\beta}\lambda_\alpha\lambda_\beta$, which is nonzero as a Grassmann polynomial. This is why a <Majorana mass term> is possible.

Four-dimensional <Hodge duality> identifies $\gamma^{mn}\gamma_5$ with a nonzero convention-dependent multiple of $\epsilon^{mnpq}\gamma_{pq}$. Every $C\gamma_{pq}$ is symmetric by the <four-dimensional Majorana bilinear vanishing criterion>; taking this linear combination preserves symmetry. Hence
$$
\boxed{\bar\lambda\gamma^{mn}\gamma_5\lambda=0.}
$$
The phase in the duality formula depends on the definition of $\gamma_5$ and orientation, but does not affect the vanishing result.

Here a supersymmetry-preserving combination means a real combination of Hermitian <Majorana spinor> <supercharges>, in a positive-norm <unitary representation> of the ordinary <Super-Poincaré algebra>. This reality qualification is essential. Let $|\psi\rangle$ be normalized and in the charge domains, and define the real <symmetric matrix>
$$
M_{ab}=\frac12\langle\psi|\{Q_a,Q_b\}|\psi\rangle.
$$
For real $u$, the Hermitian operator $q_u=u^aQ_a$ satisfies
$$
\|q_u\psi\|^2=\langle\psi|q_u^2|\psi\rangle=u^TMu.
$$
Therefore $M$ is a <positive semidefinite matrix>, and
$$
q_u\psi=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\in\ker M.
$$
The reverse implication uses positivity: diagonalize $M$, so a zero quadratic form has zero component in every positive-eigenvalue direction. This converts the problem into <nullity of real N=1 supercharges>, not just a condition on the determinant of an operator-valued expression.

In the equivalent <Weyl spinor> form, $\{q_\alpha,q_\beta^\dagger\}=2\sigma^m_{\alpha\dot\beta}P_m$. After a fixed invertible change of real charge basis, $M$ is the realification of the Hermitian two-by-two matrix
$$
B=E I+\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,\qquad
E=\langle P^0\rangle,\quad\mathbf p=\langle\mathbf P\rangle,
$$
up to the harmless spatial-sign convention. A complex <Hermitian matrix> has the same <eigenvalues> in its realification, each repeated twice. The <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives $(\mathbf p\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2=|\mathbf p|^2I$, so these <eigenvalues> are $E+|\mathbf p|$ and $E-|\mathbf p|$, each twice. Positivity forces $E\geq|\mathbf p|$. If one nonzero real $u$ annihilates the state, $M$ has a kernel: either $E=|\mathbf p|>0$, with exactly two zero <eigenvalues>, or $E=|\mathbf p|=0$, with four. Thus
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb R}\{u:(u\cdot Q)|\psi\rangle=0\}=2\ \text{or}\ 4.}
$$
For a momentum eigenstate these are the null-momentum and zero-momentum cases; the expectation-matrix proof also covers states not assumed to have definite momentum. There are no massive states preserving a real charge in this unextended algebra.

If “linear combination” instead allows arbitrary complex coefficients, the unrestricted claim is false. In a massive <fermionic Fock space> with two lowering operators $a_1,a_2$ and a spin-half <Clifford vacuum> spectator, take
$$
|\psi\rangle=\frac{|00\rangle\otimes|\uparrow\rangle+
|01\rangle\otimes|\downarrow\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Then $a_1\psi=0$. The three vectors $a_2\psi$, $a_2^\dagger\psi$ and $a_1^\dagger\psi$ are linearly independent, by their occupation numbers and spectator <spin> labels. The complex annihilator is therefore only one-dimensional. Such a lowering operator is not a Hermitian generator of preserved real <supersymmetry>. The boxed conclusion applies to the physical real-charge interpretation; it also assumes no <domain-wall charge in N=1 supersymmetry>.

= Solution

In a massive <four-dimensional N=1 supersymmetry> representation, the two normalized raising <supercharges> transform as a spin-half doublet. Start with a <Clifford vacuum> of <superspin> $j=1/2$. At occupation levels zero, one and two, the ordinary <spins> are
$$
\frac12,\qquad \frac12\otimes\frac12=0\oplus1,\qquad \frac12.
$$
The two-creation state has a spin-singlet oscillator factor because the creation operators anticommute. Thus the <massive N=1 vector multiplet> contains
$$
\boxed{1\ \oplus\ \frac12\ \oplus\ \frac12\ \oplus\ 0.}
$$
There are four bosonic polarizations, $3+1$, and four fermionic polarizations, $2+2$, as required by <boson-fermion degeneracy in a supermultiplet>.

In the massless limit the <helicity> multiplicities become $+1,-1$, two copies each of $+1/2,-1/2$, and two zero-helicity states. They separate into the four irreducible <helicity> pairs
$$
\boxed{(1,\tfrac12),\quad(\tfrac12,0),\quad(0,-\tfrac12),\quad(-\tfrac12,-1).}
$$
With the lower-helicity convention for <superhelicity>, a pair $(\kappa,\kappa+1/2)$ has labels $\kappa=1/2,0,-1/2,-1$, respectively. A midpoint convention shifts each label by $1/4$; the explicit <helicity> pairs remove any naming ambiguity.

Pairing under the <CPT theorem>, the first and last pairs form a massless <vector multiplet>, and the middle two form a massless <chiral multiplet>. This is the <massless limit of a massive N=1 vector multiplet>. The longitudinal vector polarization supplies one of the <chiral multiplet>'s two real scalar modes; the original spin-zero state supplies the other. Simply discarding the longitudinal polarization would lose a physical degree of freedom and give the wrong decomposition.

A <scalar superfield transformation> is a pullback under a <supertranslation>. Since that transformation mixes $x$ and $\theta$, an odd-coordinate derivative acquires an extra spacetime-derivative term under the coordinate Jacobian. It therefore does not transform as a spinor <superfield> merely by differentiating the scalar transformation law. This is <partial derivatives and superfield covariance>.

For explicit signs, split the <Majorana spinor> coordinates into $\theta^\alpha,\bar\theta^{\dot\alpha}$ and use <left Grassmann derivatives>. Choose the differential generators
$$
Q_\alpha=\partial_\alpha-i\sigma^m_{\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^{\dot\beta}\partial_m,
\qquad
\bar Q_{\dot\alpha}=-\bar\partial_{\dot\alpha}
+i\theta^\beta\sigma^m_{\beta\dot\alpha}\partial_m.
$$
For example, $\{\partial_\alpha,\bar Q_{\dot\beta}\}=i\sigma^m_{\alpha\dot\beta}\partial_m$, which is nonzero. Commuting the even <supersymmetry> variation through $\partial_\alpha$ consequently gives the unwanted translation term.

Replace the ordinary odd-coordinate derivatives by the <supersymmetric covariant derivatives>
$$
\boxed{D_\alpha=\partial_\alpha+i\sigma^m_{\alpha\dot\beta}\bar\theta^{\dot\beta}\partial_m,
\qquad
\bar D_{\dot\alpha}=-\bar\partial_{\dot\alpha}
-i\theta^\beta\sigma^m_{\beta\dot\alpha}\partial_m.}
$$
The graded product rule cancels the mixed coordinate/derivative terms, giving $\{D,Q\}=\{D,\bar Q\}=\{\bar D,Q\}=\{\bar D,\bar Q\}=0$. Thus $D_\alpha V$ and $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}V$ transform as spinor <superfields>. Their own mixed algebra is $\{D_\alpha,\bar D_{\dot\beta}\}=-2i\sigma^m_{\alpha\dot\beta}\partial_m$, with equal-chirality <anticommutators> zero. This covariant differentiation is also what makes a <chiral superfield> constraint $\bar D_{\dot\alpha}Z=0$ <supersymmetry> invariant. Negating every barred derivative is another convention, but changes the displayed mixed-algebra sign.

= Solution

In the ordinary local two-derivative matter class, the general ungauged <supersymmetric action> for a <chiral superfield> is
$$
\boxed{S=\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,K(Z,\bar Z)
+\left[\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,W(Z)+\mathrm{h.c.}\right].}
$$
The <Kähler potential> $K$ is real, and the <superpotential> $W$ is a <holomorphic function>. Adding $f(Z)+\bar f(\bar Z)$ to $K$ changes the <action> only by a <boundary term>: full <superspace integration> of a chiral or antichiral function vanishes modulo that term. This is a <Kähler transformation>. A positive <Kähler metric> $G=K_{z\bar z}$ is required for a nonsingular positive-norm kinetic term, not just for derivative counting.

To see the differential order, the bosonic component Lagrangian in mostly-plus signature contains
$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{bos}}=-G\partial_mz\partial^m\bar z
+G F\bar F+W_zF+\overline{W_z}\bar F.
$$
The <auxiliary field> equation is algebraic: $F=-G^{-1}\overline{W_z}$ on a purely bosonic configuration. It gives the <scalar potential> $U=G^{-1}|W_z|^2$. The scalar equations have at most two spacetime derivatives, the <fermion> equations from the sigma-model kinetic term have one, and the remaining interaction terms do not raise this order. The fermionic corrections to the auxiliary equation are still algebraic.

For four-dimensional <renormalizability>, assign <engineering dimensions> $[Z]=1$, $[d^4\theta]=2$, and $[d^2\theta]=1$. Hence, up to a <Kähler transformation> and a constant field normalization, the restrictions are
$$
\boxed{K=\bar Z Z,\qquad
W=w_0+aZ+\frac m2Z^2+\frac g{3!}Z^3.}
$$
A mixed higher-degree term in $K$ or a term of degree above three in $W$ introduces a negative-mass-dimension coupling. The constant $w_0$ has no local effect in global <supersymmetry>, as in the <constant superpotential in global supersymmetry>.

The boxed general-action statement uses the conventional two-derivative matter interpretation. It is not a classification of all derivative-dependent effective <superfield> <actions> by the order of their bosonic equations alone. For example, a scalar Lagrangian quartic in first derivatives still yields a second-order Euler–Lagrange equation, and <supersymmetric> derivative-dependent interactions can contain such terms. Their auxiliary and fermionic equations require separate checking. This distinction is explicit in \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/1012.3748][the original construction of supersymmetric derivative-dependent scalar actions]. Such <actions> are outside the ordinary $K+W$ class used for the renormalizable answer here.

Use the <supertranslation-invariant superspace coframe>
$$
\boxed{\Pi^m=dX^m+i\bar\theta\gamma^m d\theta,
\qquad\Psi^\alpha=d\theta^\alpha.}
$$
It is a basis because the change from $(dX,d\theta)$ is triangular with identity diagonal blocks. Under a rigid <supertranslation>, $\delta\theta=\epsilon$, $\delta X^m=-i\bar\epsilon\gamma^m\theta$ and $d\epsilon=0$. Therefore $\delta\Pi^m=-i\bar\epsilon\gamma^m d\theta+i\bar\epsilon\gamma^m d\theta=0$, and $\delta d\theta=0$.

Writing wedge products explicitly, the three-form is
$$
H_3=\Pi^m\wedge d\bar\theta\gamma_m d\theta.
$$
Both factors are <supertranslation> invariant. Under a <Lorentz transformation>, $\Pi^m$ and the bilinear in $d\theta$ transform as vectors, so their contraction is invariant. Thus $H_3$ is invariant under the <Super-Poincaré group>.

The <Maurer-Cartan equation> is $d\Pi^m=i\,d\bar\theta\gamma^m d\theta$. Since the other factor is closed,
$$
dH_3=i(d\bar\theta\gamma^m d\theta)
\wedge(d\bar\theta\gamma_m d\theta).
$$
The required four-dimensional <Fierz rearrangement> identity, which may be stated without proof, is
$$
(C\gamma^m)_{(\alpha\beta}(C\gamma_m)_{\gamma\delta)}=0.
$$
Differentials of odd <Grassmann variables> commute in the graded exterior algebra. Thus the coefficient of the product of four $d\theta$ factors is the fully symmetric spinor-index part appearing in this identity. It vanishes, proving
$$
\boxed{dH_3=0.}
$$
This is the <closed three-form on four-dimensional superspace>; it should not be confused with the different closed four-form used for a membrane coupling in Question 10.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Spontaneous <supersymmetry breaking> preserves the <action> and conserved <supercharges>, but the vacuum fails to be invariant. This differs from explicit breaking, which changes the symmetry of the <action> itself. In global <four-dimensional N=1 supersymmetry>, summing diagonal entries of the <Super-Poincaré algebra> writes the Hamiltonian as a sum of positive charge <anticommutators>. Its expectation is a sum of squared norms. Consequently **a zero-energy vacuum is <supersymmetric>, whereas an existing vacuum with broken global <supersymmetry> has positive energy density**. The energy zero is fixed by the algebra. Neither this positivity statement nor the particle-multiplet mass relations should be transferred without qualification to <supergravity>.

For <supersymmetric quantum mechanics>, let $H=\{q,q^\dagger\}/2$, $q^2=0$, and let <fermion parity> anticommute with both charges. On a positive-energy eigenspace, $(q+q^\dagger)/\sqrt{2E}$ is an odd involution: its square is one, it preserves energy, and it reverses parity. Thus every positive-energy bosonic state has a fermionic partner and conversely. Their contributions cancel in the <Witten index>
$$
I(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}((-1)^F e^{-\beta H})
=n_B^{(0)}-n_F^{(0)}.
$$
For a discrete spectrum with a well-defined trace, this proves independence of $\beta$. Continuous changes of parameters also preserve the index if the trace stays well defined and no state escapes to infinity or crosses an unregulated continuum threshold. **A nonzero index guarantees an unbroken <supersymmetric> ground state; a zero index is inconclusive.** For example, de Rham <supersymmetric quantum mechanics> on a circle has a bosonic constant zero-form and a fermionic constant one-form, both normalizable zero modes, so it is unbroken with index zero.

A more explicit one-dimensional family illustrates how normalizability determines the answer. Define
$$
A=\frac d{dx}+w(x),\quad A^\dagger=-\frac d{dx}+w(x),\quad
H_-=\frac12A^\dagger A,\quad H_+=\frac12AA^\dagger.
$$
Positivity of these <Hamiltonian operators> shows that a zero mode must solve $A\psi_-=0$ or $A^\dagger\psi_+=0$. Thus the only candidates are
$$
\psi_-(x)\propto e^{-\int^x w(s)ds},\qquad
\psi_+(x)\propto e^{+\int^x w(s)ds}.
$$
For $w(x)=x$, the Gaussian minus-sector <wavefunction> is normalizable and its reciprocal is not, giving an unbroken model with $I=1$ when the minus sector is assigned even parity. For $w(x)=x^2+a$, $a>0$, the exponent $x^3/3+ax$ tends to opposite infinities at the two ends, so neither candidate is normalizable. Both partner potentials $[w^2\mp w']/2$ grow quartically; their spectra are discrete and their lowest energies cannot be zero. The lowest states are therefore paired at positive energy, $I=0$, and <supersymmetry> is spontaneously broken. This is <supersymmetric factorization and zero-mode normalizability>. In quantum mechanics the paired ground states illustrate the charge algebra; a relativistic massless-particle conclusion requires field-theoretic hypotheses.

In relativistic global field theory, <Goldstone's theorem> has a fermionic version. If a conserved <supercurrent> charge does not annihilate the vacuum, its <Ward identity> has a nonzero vacuum contact term. Fourier transforming, the divergence of the current correlator cannot tend to zero smoothly at zero momentum. Its spectral representation therefore contains a massless fermionic pole: the <Goldstino>, with <spin> one-half. This statement assumes a Poincaré-invariant vacuum and the usual local unitary field-theory conditions. It does not require a bosonic Goldstone particle for the fermionic symmetry. In <supergravity> the <Goldstino> can instead supply the longitudinal polarization of a massive gravitino.

For canonical <chiral superfields> $\Phi_i$, elimination of their <auxiliary fields> gives
$$
F_i=-\overline{W_i},\qquad U=\sum_i|W_i|^2.
$$
A <supersymmetric vacuum> solves every $W_i=0$. At a stationary broken vacuum,
$$
0=\partial_iU=\sum_j W_{ij}\overline{W_j}.
$$
The same matrix $W_{ij}$ is the chiral-fermion mass matrix, so the nonzero vector $\overline{W_j}$ is a zero mode. This derives the <Goldstino zero mode from vacuum stationarity>. The transformation $\delta\psi_i=\sqrt2\epsilon F_i+\cdots$ independently identifies that direction through its inhomogeneous shift.

For one canonical chiral field, $W=fZ$, $f\ne0$, has $U=|f|^2$ everywhere, a massless <fermion> and a classically flat scalar direction. It is <flat F-term breaking with a linear superpotential>, rather than an isolated vacuum. In contrast, a nonconstant polynomial $W'$ of positive degree has a complex root and hence a <supersymmetric vacuum>; in particular a generic quadratic or cubic single-field <superpotential> cannot remove all such roots. This argument assumes canonical nonsingular kinetic terms and does not claim that every runaway or nonpolynomial model has a vacuum.

A renormalizable three-field <O'Raifeartaigh model> gives a less degenerate illustration:
$$
W=fX+\frac h2X\Phi_1^2+m\Phi_1\Phi_2,\qquad fm\ne0.
$$
The auxiliary equations require $W_2=m\phi_1=0$, then $W_1=hX\phi_1+m\phi_2=0$, leaving $W_X=f\ne0$. They cannot vanish simultaneously. For real positive $f,h,m$ with $m^2>fh$, the potential obeys
$$
\begin{aligned}
U&=|f+h\phi_1^2/2|^2+|hX\phi_1+m\phi_2|^2+m^2|\phi_1|^2\\
&\geq f^2+(m^2-fh)|\phi_1|^2+\frac{h^2}{4}|\phi_1|^4.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore its global tree-level minimum has $\phi_1=\phi_2=0$, arbitrary $X$, and energy $f^2$. The massless $\psi_X$ is the <Goldstino>, while $X$ is a <pseudomodulus>. At $X=0$, the scalar mass-squared spectrum is $0,0,m^2,m^2,m^2+fh,m^2-fh$, and the <fermion> masses are $0,m,m$. This exhibits both the stability condition and the loss of ordinary <supermultiplet> mass degeneracy in a broken vacuum. Quantum corrections can lift the <pseudomodulus> and must be analysed rather than assumed away; the noncompact classical valley also prevents an unqualified finite-volume <Witten index> trace argument. The index, the vacuum equations and the <Goldstino> theorem consequently answer complementary questions.

= Solution

Ordinary <electromagnetism> uses a one-form <gauge potential>, its <gauge transformation> and its curvature. On <superspace> the corresponding object is a <superspace gauge connection>, with both vector and spinor components. Write $\nabla_A=D_A+i\mathcal A_A$. Its graded commutator contains the flat-frame torsion plus supercurvature. The conventional constraints set the two undotted-spinor curvatures, the two dotted-spinor curvatures and the conventional mixed spinor curvature to zero; the mixed derivative algebra then determines the vector connection. The remaining independent gauge curvature is packaged into a <chiral field-strength superfield>. A real <vector superfield> $V=V^\dagger$ is a prepotential solving these constraints, rather than four unrelated ordinary <gauge potentials>.

Use the left-derivative conventions of Question 5, with $D^2=D^\alpha D_\alpha$ and $\bar D^2=\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar D^{\dot\alpha}$. An Abelian <supergauge transformation> has a chiral parameter $\Lambda$, and one consistent normalization is
$$
V'=V+i(\Lambda-\bar\Lambda),\qquad
\Phi'=e^{-2iq\Lambda}\Phi,\qquad\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\Lambda=0.
$$
The parameter is chiral so that the transformed matter is still a <chiral superfield>; its complex components remove more of the prepotential than an ordinary real gauge parameter. In <Wess-Zumino gauge>, one convenient expansion is
$$
V=-\theta\sigma^m\bar\theta A_m+i\theta^2\bar\theta\bar\lambda
-i\bar\theta^2\theta\lambda+\frac12\theta^2\bar\theta^2D.
$$
Its components are the <gauge potential> $A_m$, the <gaugino> $\lambda$ and the real <auxiliary field> $D$. <Wess-Zumino gauge> removes the extra scalar and spinor components of a general real <superfield>. <Supersymmetry> transformations usually leave that gauge slice and require a compensating <supergauge transformation>. For a residual chiral parameter with real lowest component $a(x)$, the expansion gives $A_m'=A_m+2\partial_ma$ and $\phi'=e^{-2iqa}\phi$; thus $\alpha=2a$ is the ordinary Maxwell gauge parameter.

The Abelian field strength is the <Abelian field-strength chiral projection>
$$
\boxed{W_\alpha=-\frac14\bar D^2D_\alpha V.}
$$
Three barred derivatives vanish because there are only two anticommuting dotted components, so $\bar D_{\dot\beta}W_\alpha=0$. Gauge invariance follows directly: $D_\alpha\bar\Lambda=0$ by antichirality, while the mixed derivative algebra gives $\bar D^2D_\alpha\Lambda=0$ for chiral $\Lambda$. In chiral coordinates its component structure is
$$
W_\alpha=-i\lambda_\alpha+\theta_\alpha D
-i(\sigma^{mn}\theta)_\alpha F_{mn}
+\theta^2(\sigma^m\partial_m\bar\lambda)_\alpha,
\qquad F_{mn}=\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m,
$$
where $\sigma^{mn}=(\sigma^m\bar\sigma^n-\sigma^n\bar\sigma^m)/4$ fixes the tensor normalization. Overall <gaugino> phase choices do not change the gauge-invariant field strength or its Bianchi constraint.

Reality of $V$ and the derivative algebra imply the <superspace Yang-Mills Bianchi identity>
$$
\boxed{D^\alpha W_\alpha=\bar D_{\dot\alpha}\bar W^{\dot\alpha}.}
$$
Indeed, the two sides are the same differential operator on $V$, because $D^\alpha\bar D^2D_\alpha=\bar D_{\dot\alpha}D^2\bar D^{\dot\alpha}$. This is the superspace counterpart of $dF=0$, and its components contain $\partial_{[m}F_{np]}=0$. It is a defining curvature constraint, not the <Maxwell equations>. Varying the pure gauge <action> additionally sets the common real <superfield> $D^\alpha W_\alpha$ to zero; matter supplies a current instead.

The <supersymmetric Yang-Mills action> and minimal matter coupling are
$$
S=\frac1{4g^2}\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,W^\alpha W_\alpha+\mathrm{h.c.}
+\int d^4x\,d^4\theta\,\bar\Phi e^{2qV}\Phi
+\left[\int d^4x\,d^2\theta\,\mathcal W(\Phi)+\mathrm{h.c.}\right].
$$
The chiral phase factors cancel in $\bar\Phi e^{2qV}\Phi$. The component matter derivative is $(\partial_m+iqA_m)\phi$, which transforms covariantly under the residual <gauge transformation> just derived. The same superspace term generates the associated matter–<gaugino> <Yukawa coupling> and $qD|\phi|^2$ auxiliary coupling, so minimal coupling is not only replacing an ordinary derivative in the scalar <action>. The gauge <action> contains $-F_{mn}F^{mn}/(4g^2)$, the <gaugino> kinetic term and $D^2/(2g^2)$. Rescaling the <vector multiplet> to canonical kinetic normalization gives the physical coupling $qg$. The holomorphic <superpotential> must be gauge invariant; a single field of nonzero continuous Abelian charge admits no nonconstant polynomial <superpotential> without additional fields. For several fields, invariance requires $\sum_iq_i\Phi_i\mathcal W_i=0$. A <Fayet–Iliopoulos term> is additionally possible for an Abelian factor, shifting the algebraic $D$ equation.

For a non-Abelian <gauge group>, $V$ and $\Lambda$ are Lie-algebra-valued, and multiplication cannot be reordered. For matter in a specified representation, set
$$
\Phi'=e^{-2i\Lambda}\Phi,\qquad
\boxed{e^{2V'}=e^{-2i\Lambda^\dagger}e^{2V}e^{2i\Lambda}.}
$$
The same matter quadratic form remains invariant. Its curvature is
$$
\boxed{W_\alpha=-\frac18\bar D^2\bigl(e^{-2V}D_\alpha e^{2V}\bigr),
\qquad W_\alpha'=e^{-2i\Lambda}W_\alpha e^{2i\Lambda}.}
$$
At first order in an Abelian $V$, this reduces to $-\bar D^2D_\alpha V/4$, fixing the factor $1/8$. In the chiral gauge frame, $\bar\nabla=\bar D$ and $\nabla_\alpha=e^{-2V}D_\alpha e^{2V}$ as an operator. The gauge-covariant Bianchi identity is $\nabla^\alpha W_\alpha=\bar\nabla_{\dot\alpha}\widetilde{\bar W}^{\dot\alpha}$, where $\widetilde{\bar W}=e^{-2V}\bar W e^{2V}$ is the conjugate curvature expressed in that same frame. It follows from the <graded Jacobi identity> of the <covariant derivatives>. Taking a trace of $W^\alpha W_\alpha$ makes the chiral gauge <action> invariant. Its bosonic curvature becomes the ordinary Yang-Mills curvature, and the <gaugino> transforms in the <adjoint representation>. A constant Fayet–Iliopoulos coefficient cannot be inserted for a simple non-Abelian group, since it would need an invariant linear functional annihilating every Lie bracket. These constructions connect the constrained superspace connection, its prepotential and its component gauge multiplet.

= Solution
{parent=10}

A canonical <Wess–Zumino model> with a cubic <superpotential> already supplies a microscopic membrane-like object. Choose positive real $\mu,g$ and
$$
W(\Phi)=\frac{\mu^2}{g}\Phi-\frac g3\Phi^3,
\qquad U(\phi)=\left|\frac{\mu^2}{g}-g\phi^2\right|^2.
$$
There are two isolated <supersymmetric vacua>, $\phi_\pm=\pm\mu/g$. A planar <domain wall> has a static scalar profile depending on the transverse coordinate $z=X^3$, with one vacuum at either end. Its energy per unit area is
$$
T=\int dz\,(|\phi'|^2+|W'|^2)
=\int dz\,|\phi'-e^{i\alpha}\overline{W'}|^2
+2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(e^{-i\alpha}\Delta W\bigr).
$$
Choosing $\alpha=\arg\Delta W$ gives $T\geq2|\Delta W|$. The real increasing solution saturates the bound:
$$
\phi'=\frac{\mu^2}{g}-g\phi^2,
\qquad
\boxed{\phi(z)=\frac\mu g\tanh(\mu(z-z_0)),\qquad
T=\frac{8\mu^3}{3g^2}.}
$$
The tension follows either from integrating $2(\phi')^2$ or from $W(\mu/g)-W(-\mu/g)=4\mu^3/(3g^2)$. This is a <supersymmetric domain wall>, and the first-order equation also implies the second-order scalar equation. Its thickness is of order $1/\mu$.

\Image[paper-53-domain-wall.png]
{title=Derived Wess-Zumino double-well potential, domain-wall profile and localized energy density}

The wall breaks transverse translations: changing $z_0$ gives a normalizable scalar zero mode proportional to $\phi'(z)$. It also preserves only half the four real <supercharges>. In the chiral-fermion transformation, $F=-\overline{W'}=-\phi'$ and the derivative term combine into a rank-half condition on the constant <supersymmetry> parameter, for example $\epsilon=i\sigma^3\bar\epsilon$ in a compatible Weyl convention. The orthogonal two real parameters are broken and generate localized <fermion> zero modes. One such profile satisfies $(\partial_z+2g\phi)f=0$, giving $f\propto\operatorname{sech}^2(\mu(z-z_0))$. The scalar and a three-dimensional Majorana <fermion> therefore form the low-energy wall multiplet. The <domain-wall charge in N=1 supersymmetry> is a boundary/tensor charge contributing to the extended algebra; the preserved combinations saturate $T=2|\Delta W|$. This avoids falsely applying the unextended particle-algebra argument of Question 3 to a wall with nonzero tension.

Promote the translation collective coordinate to a slowly varying <worldvolume> field $Y(\xi^0,\xi^1,\xi^2)$. At wavelengths long compared with the wall thickness, its leading relativistic geometric <action> is the <Dirac membrane action>
$$
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{mem}}=-T\int d^3\xi\sqrt{-\det h_{ij}},
\qquad h_{ij}=\partial_iX^m\partial_jX^n\eta_{mn}.}
$$
It is invariant under <worldvolume> reparametrizations and the target <Poincaré group>. In static gauge $X^i=\xi^i$, $X^3=Y$, the <matrix determinant lemma> gives
$$
S_{\mathrm{mem}}=-T\int d^3\xi\sqrt{1+\partial_iY\partial^iY}
=-T\int d^3\xi\left(1+\tfrac12\partial_iY\partial^iY+\cdots\right).
$$
Directly substituting the leading collective-coordinate profile into the scalar kinetic term gives the same coefficient, since $\int dz\,(\phi')^2=T/2$. Target Lorentz symmetry fixes the leading first-derivative nonlinear completion to the invariant area; finite-thickness physics can add higher-curvature terms. The area <action> is therefore a long-wavelength <effective field theory>, not an exact replacement for the microscopic wall at arbitrary momenta. Its embedding equation is $\partial_i(\sqrt{-h}\,h^{ij}\partial_jX^m)=0$.

For the <four-dimensional supermembrane>, extend the embedding to $(X^m(\xi),\theta^\alpha(\xi))$ with a four-real-component <Majorana spinor>. Pull back the invariant coframe of Question 7:
$$
\Pi_i^m=\partial_iX^m+i\bar\theta\gamma^m\partial_i\theta,
\qquad h_{ij}=\Pi_i^m\Pi_j^n\eta_{mn}.
$$
Replacing $\partial X$ by $\Pi$ makes the area term invariant under rigid target <supersymmetry>, but does not yet give the appropriate fermionic gauge symmetry. Add a <Wess-Zumino brane coupling> to a superspace three-form potential $C_3$. In one orientation and matching normalization,
$$
S=-T\int d^3\xi\sqrt{-h}+T\int_{\Sigma_3}Z^*C_3,
\qquad
G_4=dC_3=\frac i2\Pi^m\wedge\Pi^n\wedge d\bar\theta\gamma_{mn}d\theta.
$$
The <supermembrane closed four-form> uses the distinct four-dimensional Fierz identity $(C\gamma_{mn})_{(\alpha\beta}(C\gamma^n)_{\gamma\delta)}=0$. Differentiating $G_4$ reduces its coefficient to this symmetric tensor, so $dG_4=0$. A local potential therefore exists. Since the curvature is <supertranslation> invariant, the potential can vary by an exact form; its pullback integral is invariant modulo <boundary terms>. This is why the three-form $H_3$ of Question 7 is not itself the membrane field strength: integrating a membrane potential requires three-form degree and hence four-form curvature.

The required additional local fermionic gauge invariance is <kappa symmetry>. Define the <worldvolume> <gamma matrices> $\gamma_i=\Pi_i^m\gamma_m$ and
$$
\Gamma_\kappa=\frac{\epsilon^{ijk}}{3!\sqrt{-h}}\gamma_{ijk},
\qquad
\boxed{\delta_\kappa\theta=(1+\Gamma_\kappa)\kappa(\xi),
\quad\delta_\kappa X^m=-i\bar\theta\gamma^m\delta_\kappa\theta.}
$$
With mostly-plus target signature, a local orthonormal <worldvolume> frame gives $\Gamma_\kappa=\gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2$, so $\Gamma_\kappa^2=1$ and $\operatorname{tr}\Gamma_\kappa=0$. Thus $(1\pm\Gamma_\kappa)/2$ are rank-two <kappa symmetry projectors>. The embedding variation gives $\delta\Pi_i^m=2i\delta\bar\theta\gamma^m\partial_i\theta$. The area variation is consequently proportional to $-2iT\sqrt{-h}\delta\bar\theta\gamma^i\partial_i\theta$. Contracting the displayed $G_4$ with the fermionic variation and using $\epsilon^{ijk}\gamma_{ij}=2\sqrt{-h}\Gamma_\kappa\gamma^k$ gives the opposite Wess-Zumino contribution with an extra $\Gamma_\kappa$. Their sum is proportional to
$$
\delta\bar\theta(1-\Gamma_\kappa)\gamma^i\partial_i\theta.
$$
For the boxed kappa variation, $\delta\bar\theta=\bar\kappa(1+\Gamma_\kappa)$, so it vanishes by $(1+\Gamma_\kappa)(1-\Gamma_\kappa)=0$. Reversing membrane orientation reverses the Wess-Zumino sign and the matching projector. The cancellation fixes the relative charge magnitude to the tension; rigid <supersymmetry> alone would not fix it.

<Worldvolume> reparametrizations remove three of the four embedding scalars, leaving one transverse mode. <Kappa symmetry> removes half the four real embedding spinor components, leaving a two-component three-dimensional Majorana field, whose first-order equation has one on-shell fermionic polarization. **The physical <worldvolume> modes are one scalar and one Majorana <fermion>**, matching the wall zero modes. Without <kappa symmetry> the fermionic degrees would be doubled. A planar configuration preserves the target supersymmetries that can be compensated by the kappa projector, reproducing the wall's half-supersymmetry condition. This is the four-dimensional effective domain-wall interpretation of a supermembrane, rather than the eleven-dimensional M2-brane particle-content problem.
