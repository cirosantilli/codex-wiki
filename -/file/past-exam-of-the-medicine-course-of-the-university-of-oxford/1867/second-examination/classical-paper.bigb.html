== Latin translation
{scope}

**Original question.**

Translate :—

Celeres enim, vel acutae passiones, etiam sponte solvuntur, et nunc fortunâ nunc naturâ favente. Quo fit ut ignari homines elati saepe medicos fugiant, et hos proventus incantationibus novis ac ligamentis adscribant, aut fortunae; pelluntur enim (celeres passiones) aut fluore sudoris, aut sanguinis per nares, aut ventris. Chroniae autem, vel tardae passionis morbi, qui jam praejudicio quodam corpora possederint, solius medici peritiam poscunt, cum neque naturâ, neque fortunâ solvantur.

*e Caelii Aureliani Morb. Chron. Praefatione.*

=== Solution

**Translation.** For rapidly developing, or <acute diseases>, also resolve of their own accord, sometimes through favourable chance and sometimes through nature's favour. Consequently, ignorant people, carried away by this, often avoid <physicians> and attribute these favourable outcomes to new incantations and amulets, or to chance; for these rapidly developing <diseases> are driven out either by a flow of <sweat>, by <blood> discharged through the nose, or by a discharge from the <intestines>. But <chronic diseases>, or <diseases> of slow development, which have already gained possession of the body with a kind of prior claim, call for the skill of the <physician> alone, since they are resolved neither by nature nor by chance.

**Interpretation.** <Caelius Aurelianus> contrasts spontaneous resolution in <acute disease> with the entrenched course of <chronic disease>. Here *passiones* means affections or <diseases>, not emotions; *ligamenta* means protective bindings or amulets, not anatomical <ligaments>; *proventus* means favourable outcomes. The metaphor *praejudicio quodam* gives the established <disease> something like a prior claim on the body. The final assertion is the author's defence of professional <medicine>, not a present-day law that every <chronic disease> requires effective medical <treatment> or that none can remit spontaneously. The account of <sweating>, <nosebleeds> and <intestine> discharge describes his historical explanation, not evidence that deliberately producing these discharges cures <acute disease>. <Examination papers for the Degree of Bachelor of Medicine by the University of Oxford (1864–1867)>[PDF volume p. 101, Latin extract attributed to the preface to Caelius Aurelianus's chronic diseases]{ref}

== Greek translation
{scope}

**Original question.**

Translate :—

*Κοιλίη, σπλάγχνον πεπτήριον, κάμνει τὴν πέψιν, ὁκότε διάῤῥοια τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἴσχει· καὶ ἔστιν ὑγρῆς τροφῆς ἀπέπτου ἡ διάῤῥοια· ἢν μὴ ἐπὶ σχεδίῳ τῆς αἰτίης γίγνοιτο τόδε ἐς μίαν ἢ δευτέρην ἡμέρην μούνην. Ὅλος ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἀσθενέει, ἀτροφίῃ τοῦ σκήνεος, χρονίη νοῦσος ἡ κοιλιακὴ γίγνεται, ἀτονίῃ τοῦ πέψοντος θερμοῦ, καὶ ψύξει τῆς κοιλίης· εὖτε λύεται μὲν ἐς θερμὸν ἡ τροφὴ, ἀλλ᾽ οὐκ ἐκπέσσει θερμὸν, οὐδὲ ἐς χυμὸν οἰκεῖον τρέπει, ἡμιτελέα δὲ λείπε-*

*\[Printed page break; the preceding word is incomplete in the source.\]*

*ἀδρανείῃ τοῦ τέλεος· ἡ δὲ ἀφεθεῖσα τῆς ἐργασίης ἐς κακὸν ἀλλοιοῦται καὶ χροιῇ, καὶ ὀδμῇ, καὶ συστάσι. Λευκὴ μὲν γὰρ καὶ ἄχολος ἡ χροιή· κάκοδμα δὲ καὶ βορβορώδεα· ὑγρὴ δὲ καὶ ἀσύστατος ἀπραγίῃ, μούνον δὲ ἀρετὴν ἴσχουσα τῆς πέψιος τὴν ἀρχήν· διὰ τόδε φυσώδεες ἐς τὴν γαστέρα ἐρυγαὶ ξυνεχέες κακώδεες.*

ex Aretaei de causis et signis morborum diuturnorum Libri Sec. cap. 7ᵐᵒ.

=== Solution

**Translation.** The <stomach>, the internal <organ> responsible for <digestion>, performs <digestion> poorly whenever <diarrhoea> takes hold of a person; and the <diarrhoea> consists of liquid, undigested <food>. Unless this happens from a temporary cause for only one or two days, the whole person becomes weak through wasting of the body, and the <coeliac affection of Aretaeus> becomes a <chronic disease>, through weakness of the <digestive heat> and chilling of the <stomach>. Then the <food> is indeed broken down under the action of <heat>, but the <heat> does not digest it completely or turn it into an appropriate nutritive fluid: it leaves it half-finished (the printed word is incomplete), through failure of the completing power. The <food>, abandoned by the process, changes for the worse in colour, smell and consistency. Its colour is white and lacking <bile>; its smell is foul and dung-like; and it is liquid and without cohesion because the process has failed, retaining only the beginning of <digestion> as its one good quality. Hence there is gaseous, continual, foul-smelling <belching> in the <stomach>.

**Text and medical meaning.** The scan ends PDF volume p. 101 with *λείπε-* and begins p. 102 with *ἀδρανείῃ*. The translation marks this defective word rather than silently pretending that the missing ending is visible. The corresponding edited Greek has *λείπει*, “leaves”, which supplies the sense used here. The Oxford extract also separates the conditional clause from the following sentence more abruptly than that edition; the translation carries its evident qualification forward. <On the Causes and Signs of Chronic Diseases by Aretaeus of Cappadocia>[II.7, opening paragraph: λείπει ἀδρανείῃ τοῦ τέλεος; the edited text also has ἀλλ᾽ and πρὸς δὲ καὶ linking the conditional construction]{ref}

<Aretaeus of Cappadocia> uses *κοιλίη* for the <stomach> or digestive belly and *σκῆνος*, literally a tent or dwelling, for the body. *ἀτροφίη* indicates wasting or failure of nourishment; it need not denote the modern histological concept of <atrophy>. “Appropriate nutritive fluid” renders *χυμὸν οἰκεῖον*: the product that the author thinks suitable for bodily nourishment, rather than a modern chemical identification. The pale, foul, loose output is an observed feature; the explanation by weak <digestive heat> is the author's theory. It is not a modern account of <digestion>. <Examination papers for the Degree of Bachelor of Medicine by the University of Oxford (1864–1867)>[PDF volume pp. 101–102, Greek extract attributed to Aretaeus, Causes and Signs of Chronic Diseases II.7]{ref}

**The historical <coeliac affection of Aretaeus> cannot establish a modern diagnosis of <coeliac disease>.** Modern <coeliac disease> involves an <immune response> to <gluten> that damages the <small intestine>; the ancient symptom description alone cannot identify that mechanism. The diagnosis should therefore not be read back into the passage as certain. <Celiac Disease by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases>[Definition and Facts: chronic digestive and immune disorder triggered by gluten, damaging the small intestine]{ref}

== History of bloodletting
{scope}

**Original question.**

Give a brief account of the History of Blood-letting from ancient to modern times. Explain the changes of opinion in respect of it.

=== Solution

**By 1867 the central change was from confidence in <bloodletting> as a general remedy to a disputed, increasingly restricted practice tested against clinical outcomes.** <Bloodletting> includes opening a <vein>, local <wet cupping>, and the application of <medicinal leeches>. The history must explain both the theories that supported it and why clinical comparisons weakened that support.

**Ancient explanations and qualifications.** In the <Hippocratic Corpus>, <disease> could be explained through disturbances of bodily fluids. The four-fluid account underlying <humorism> named <blood>, <humoral phlegm>, yellow <bile> and <black bile>; restoring their proper mixture offered a rationale for evacuation. This was a historical explanatory system, not a modern description of <blood> composition. Nor was ancient practice uniformly indiscriminate. <Aulus Cornelius Celsus> describes <bloodletting> as a powerful intervention whose suitability depends on bodily strength and whether the condition represents excess rather than exhaustion. <Galen> likewise distinguishes whether evacuation is needed, whether it should be by <venesection>, and whether the patient can tolerate it; he discusses the site, quantity and repetition. Thus the ancient debate already included selection and restraint. <On the Nature of Man in the Hippocratic Corpus>[Selection 1, The Elements of Life, paragraphs 1–2: four distinct fluids; translated primary excerpt]{ref} <On Medicine by Aulus Cornelius Celsus>[II.10.1–8, strength, depletion and the risks of bloodletting]{ref} <On Treatment by Bloodletting by Galen>[§1, successive questions governing evacuation, tolerance, site and amount]{ref}

**Medieval continuity and the instruments.** The <Canon of Medicine by Ibn Sina> preserves a carefully qualified doctrine of <venesection>, including cases where removing good <blood> would leave harmful fluids behind. Its reasoning belongs to <humorism>; its distinctions should not be mistaken for verified modern indications. In late medieval Europe, <bloodletting> was also organized by calendars: a surviving Augsburg calendar for 1472 specifies supposedly favourable times according to the patient and the <Moon>. This documents an additional astrological framework, not evidence of therapeutic efficacy. <Canon of Medicine by Ibn Sina>[Book I, translation §§1019–1022, contraindications, timing and choice of vessels]{ref} <Bloodletting calendar for Augsburg (1472)>[Princeton copy and catalogue description: days selected by lunar phases, patient age, affected part and temperament]{ref}

The material distinction between general and local removal mattered. <Venesection> opened a <vein> with a sharp instrument. <Wet cupping> combined superficial cuts in the <skin> with suction under a cup; <Celsus> describes bronze cups in which burning material reduced the enclosed air's <pressure>, and horn cups from which air was sucked through a small opening. <Dry cupping> did not include the cuts and therefore did not itself remove <blood>. <Medicinal leeches> provided another means of local removal. These instruments made depletion visibly demonstrable, but the visible removal of <blood> did not establish that a <disease> had been removed. <On Medicine by Aulus Cornelius Celsus>[II.11.1–6, bronze and horn cups, scarified versus intact skin, and local versus general depletion]{ref}

**New <physiology> did not immediately settle <treatment>.** <William Harvey>'s 1628 account of <blood circulation> undermined the older picture of <blood> simply being supplied and consumed in the parts. His observations of <venous valves> and experiments with arm ligatures showed directional movement: filling below a ligature and the behaviour of compressed <veins> helped demonstrate return towards the <heart>. Nevertheless, a circulating <blood> supply could still be invoked to explain relief through depletion or redistribution. Establishing <blood circulation> and establishing the benefit of <bloodletting> were different questions. <On the Motion of the Heart and Blood in Animals by William Harvey>[chapters 8–9, quantitative argument for circulation; chapter 13, ligatures and venous valves]{ref}

In the early nineteenth century, <François-Joseph-Victor Broussais>'s emphasis on local irritation and <inflammation> encouraged extensive use of <medicinal leeches>. This illustrates why changing theory could increase <bloodletting>: a local inflammatory explanation supplied a fresh reason to remove <blood> near the supposed seat of <disease>. The accompanying claims of efficacy still needed comparison with what happened under other <treatments>. <Re-imagining Bleeders by Robert G. W. Kirk and Neil Pemberton>[section discussing Broussais, local irritation, leeching, and the claim that leeches offered a relatively gentle intervention]{ref}

**Comparative evidence weakened general claims.** In 1835 <Pierre Charles Alexandre Louis> asked whether comparable patients with <pneumonia> did better when first subjected to <bloodletting> during days 1–4 than when first bled later. He explicitly considered age and other differences. This <numerical method in medicine> shifted attention from selected recoveries to outcomes in defined groups. His work restricted confidence in a large, general benefit; it should not be presented as a <randomized controlled trial> proving that every use was ineffective. In modern terms, these were <observational studies>: <confounding by indication> could make the earlier-treated group differ in severity, and surviving long enough to enter the later-treated group could introduce <selection bias>. Counting cases was an advance, but did not automatically remove these problems. <Research on the Effects of Bloodletting by Pierre Charles Alexandre Louis>[1835, pp. 70–76; primary passages reproduced by the James Lind Library on timing, age and comparisons between groups]{ref}

**The dispute still alive in 1867.** <William Pulteney Alison> defended earlier experience of benefit, while <John Hughes Bennett> opposed routine depletion and developed a restorative approach. One defence of the altered practice was the <change-of-type theory of disease>: formerly vigorous or “sthenic” inflammatory illness was said to have become weak or “asthenic”, so that measures once borne well were now inappropriate. The rival explanation was that understanding of <pathology> and the evidence about <treatment> had improved. <Alison>'s 1856 argument and the contemporary 1858 review show that this was an active disagreement, not a completed unanimous rejection. <Reflections on the Results of Experience as to the Symptoms of Internal Inflammations by William Pulteney Alison>[1856, pp. 769–788, opening response to Bennett and defence of previous clinical experience]{ref} <The Bloodletting Controversy (1858 review)>[opening pp. 1–3, contrasting altered disease type with altered pathological understanding]{ref}

<Bennett>'s 1864 discussion made the theoretical contrast explicit: an account centred on altered <blood> and vessels favoured depletion, whereas his account of tissue irritation and <inflammatory exudation> led him towards the opposite practice. That historical account of <inflammation> should itself be evaluated historically, rather than adopted as current <pathology>. The durable methodological lesson is that recovery after <treatment> is insufficient evidence of recovery because of it: <acute disease> may resolve naturally, as even the Latin extract acknowledges. Changes in theory, recognition of the patient's limited reserve, and comparative <clinical observation> together explain the retreat from routine <bloodletting>. <Treatment of Inflammation by John Hughes Bennett>[1864, p. 142, opening comparison of vascular and extravascular accounts of inflammation and their therapeutic consequences]{ref}

**Present-day distinction.** Contemporary <venesection> has specific indications: in <haemochromatosis>, removal of <red blood cells> helps reduce excess <iron>; in <polycythaemia vera>, it can reduce an excessive <red blood cell> burden. Those uses concern defined abnormalities, rather than correction of historical <humorism> or a general remedy for <fever> and <inflammation>. They are a modern explanatory coda, not knowledge attributed to the 1867 candidate. <Haemochromatosis treatment by the NHS>[Venesection: removal of iron-containing red blood cells and use of iron to replace them]{ref} <Erythrocytosis by the NHS>[Treatment, Venesection: use in polycythaemia vera and selected other circumstances]{ref}
