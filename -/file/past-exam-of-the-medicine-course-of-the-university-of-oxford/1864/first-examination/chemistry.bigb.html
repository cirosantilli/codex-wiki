**Original question.**

What are the proportions respectively required of Carbonate of Potash, Carbonate of Soda, and Caustic Magnesia to neutralize a given quantity of Sulphuric Acid ?

=== Solution

**For complete neutralization, the required masses of anhydrous potassium carbonate, anhydrous sodium carbonate and magnesium oxide are in the ratio $138:106:40=69:53:20$.** Here “carbonate of potash” means <potassium carbonate>, “carbonate of soda” means <sodium carbonate>, and “caustic magnesia” means <magnesium oxide>, rather than <magnesium hydroxide> or a <magnesium carbonate> preparation. The <hydration state> of the <sodium carbonate> matters considerably.

Each <formula unit> of either <carbonate> consumes two <protons>; each <formula unit> of <magnesium oxide> also consumes two <protons>. Thus one <mole> of each <base> neutralizes one <mole> of <sulfuric acid> when the final <salts> are the normal <sulfates>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{K_2CO_3+H_2SO_4}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{K_2SO_4+CO_2+H_2O},\\
\mathrm{Na_2CO_3+H_2SO_4}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Na_2SO_4+CO_2+H_2O},\\
\mathrm{MgO+H_2SO_4}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{MgSO_4+H_2O}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <carbon dioxide> evolved in the first two <chemical reactions> escapes, whereas <magnesium oxide> produces no <gas>. The <stoichiometric coefficients>, rather than the visibly different behaviour of the <bases>, determine the required amounts.

Let $m$ be the <mass> of pure $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$ in the given <chemical solution>. Using the rounded <relative atomic masses> $\mathrm{H}=1$, $\mathrm{C}=12$, $\mathrm{O}=16$, $\mathrm{Na}=23$, $\mathrm{Mg}=24$ and $\mathrm{K}=39$ gives
$$
\boxed{m_{\mathrm{K_2CO_3}}=\frac{138}{98}m,\qquad m_{\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}}=\frac{106}{98}m,\qquad m_{\mathrm{MgO}}=\frac{40}{98}m.}
$$
Thus 98 mass units of pure <sulfuric acid> require respectively 138, 106 and 40 mass units. Present <atomic weights> slightly change these rounded values, without changing the one-to-one <mole> ratios. If $m_s$ denotes the <mass> of the supplied <acid> and $w$ its <mass fraction> of $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$, substitute $m=wm_s$. A <volume> alone is insufficient without its <concentration> or an equivalent <density> and composition.

If “carbonate of soda” is instead the crystalline decahydrate, <sodium carbonate decahydrate>, its rounded <molar mass> is $286\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, so the corresponding factor is $286/98$, rather than $106/98$. Likewise an impure <base> requires correction for its assayed content. The unspecified given quantity can therefore be handled exactly by proportional reasoning; no missing numerical measurement is needed.

**Original question.**

What formula would you assign to a substance which gave the following results to analysis :—

|| Constituent
|| Analysis

| Carbon
| 54.35

| Hydrogen
| 9.11

| Oxygen
| 36.54

| Total
| 100.00

and of which the vapour density was 3.05.

=== Solution

**The molecular formula is $\boxed{\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}}$, interpreting the vapour density as relative to air.** The <organic elemental analysis> first determines a simpler <empirical formula>; the <vapor-density molar mass measurement> then fixes its multiple.

Take 100 mass units of the <chemical substance> and divide each elemental contribution by its rounded <relative atomic mass>:
$$
 n_{\mathrm C}:n_{\mathrm H}:n_{\mathrm O}
 =\frac{54.35}{12}:\frac{9.11}{1}:\frac{36.54}{16}
 =4.5292:9.1100:2.2838
 \simeq 2:4:1.
$$
The <empirical formula> is therefore $\mathrm{C_2H_4O}$, with <empirical formula mass> approximately 44. Its calculated <mass percentages> are 54.55% <carbon>, 9.09% <hydrogen> and 36.36% <oxygen>. The small discrepancies from the given <organic elemental analysis> are consistent with rounded experimental data; they should not be converted into large, meaningless integer subscripts.

For equal <volumes> of <ideal gases> at equal <temperature> and <pressure>, the <density> ratio equals the <molar mass> ratio. On the <air> convention,
$$
M\simeq 3.05\times28.97\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}=88.36\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
\frac{M}{44\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}\simeq2.
$$
Doubling $\mathrm{C_2H_4O}$ gives $\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}$; its rounded <relative molecular mass> of 88 predicts an <air>-relative <vapor density> of approximately $88/28.97=3.04$, close to 3.05. If the stated <vapor density> were instead relative to <hydrogen>, it would imply a <relative molecular mass> near 6.1, smaller than the <empirical formula mass> and incompatible with the analysis. That interpretation cannot describe a pure, undecomposed molecular <vapor> with the stated composition.

Under the older <equivalent weight> convention $\mathrm C=6$, $\mathrm O=8$, $\mathrm H=1$, the same composition and formula weight could be written $\mathrm{C_8H_8O_4}$. This is a change of notation, not eight modern <carbon> <chemical atoms> in the <molecule>. The result specifies composition and <molar mass>; it does not distinguish among <structural isomers>, such as <ethyl acetate> and <butyric acid>.

**Original question.**

What observations are required for the accurate comparison of the volumes of two gases, and for what reasons ?

=== Solution

**Observe each gas volume, temperature, pressure and moisture condition, and reduce both volumes to the same temperature, pressure and dry-gas basis.** Equal scale readings alone do not establish equal amounts of <gas>.

Read the calibrated <volume> of each <gas> in a graduated tube or <eudiometer>, with the eye at the <meniscus> to avoid <parallax>. Allow the <gas> and the surrounding bath to reach <thermal equilibrium>, then read a <thermometer> close to the <gas>. Heating expands a fixed amount of <gas>, so a larger reading could reflect a higher <temperature> rather than more <gas>. The <temperature> used in a <gas law> must be an <thermodynamic temperature>.

Read the atmospheric <pressure> with a <barometer>. If a <gas> is confined over a <liquid>, also observe the difference between the inside and outside <liquid> levels. With $h$ the internal surface height above the external surface, the total internal <pressure> is
$$
p_g=p_{\mathrm{atm}}-\rho_\ell gh.
$$
Here $\rho_\ell$ is the confining <density> of the <liquid> and $g$ the <gravitational acceleration>; the sign reverses automatically when $h$ is negative. Equalizing the levels before taking the <volume> reading removes this <hydrostatic pressure> correction. A <mercury barometer> can itself require <temperature> and calibration corrections for accurate work.

If the <gas> is saturated with <water vapor>, subtract the <vapor pressure> of <water> at its measured <temperature>:
$$
p_{\mathrm{dry}}=p_g-p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}(T).
$$
The observed <pressure> includes both the test <gas> and <water vapor>; counting it all as test <gas> overestimates the amount. The saturation assumption needs time for equilibration. For a dry <gas>, no <water vapor> subtraction is made; another collecting <liquid> may require its own <vapor pressure> correction. A collecting <liquid> must also be chosen so that it neither appreciably dissolves nor reacts with the <gas>: <water>, for instance, is unsuitable for preserving the whole amount of freely soluble <ammonia>.

For dilute <gases> obeying the <ideal gas law>, the <volume> $V_0$ at a common chosen $T_0,p_0$ is
$$
\boxed{V_0=V\frac{p_{\mathrm{dry}}}{p_0}\frac{T_0}{T}.}
$$
Consequently the corrected comparison is
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{1,0}}{V_{2,0}}=\frac{V_1}{V_2}\frac{p_{1,\mathrm{dry}}}{p_{2,\mathrm{dry}}}\frac{T_2}{T_1}.}
$$
This is also the ratio of <amounts of substance> in the <ideal gas> approximation. Check for leaks, trapped <air>, changing composition and incomplete transfer. At high <pressure> or near <condensation>, <real gas> behaviour may require <compressibility factors>; with $pV=ZnRT$, the inferred amount ratio contains the further factor $Z_2/Z_1$.

**Original question.**

Give the Chemical Formulæ of the following substances :—Hydrocyanic Acid, Formic Acid, Oxalic Acid, Calomel, Corrosive Sublimate, Arsenious Acid, Tartar-Emetic, Chlorate of Potash.

=== Solution

**In modern notation the formulas are HCN, HCOOH, $\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4}$, $\mathrm{Hg_2Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{HgCl_2}$, $\mathrm{As_2O_3}$, a hydrated potassium antimony tartrate, and $\mathrm{KClO_3}$, respectively.** Several historical names need qualification.

* <Hydrocyanic acid> is <hydrogen cyanide>, $\boxed{\mathrm{HCN}}$. The pharmaceutical name normally concerns an aqueous <chemical solution>; its <concentration> is not encoded by the molecular <chemical formula>.
* <Formic acid> is $\boxed{\mathrm{HCOOH}}$, equivalently $\mathrm{CH_2O_2}$.
* Anhydrous <oxalic acid> is $\boxed{\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4}}$, or $\mathrm{(COOH)_2}$. Its common crystalline dihydrate is $\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4\cdot2H_2O}$; the <water of crystallization> must be included when calculating a weighed quantity.
* <Calomel> is <mercury(I) chloride>, $\boxed{\mathrm{Hg_2Cl_2}}$. The formula $\mathrm{HgCl}$ gives only the simplest composition ratio and conceals the paired <mercury(I) ion>.
* <Corrosive sublimate> is <mercury(II) chloride>, $\boxed{\mathrm{HgCl_2}}$. It is chemically distinct from <calomel>.
* The solid historically sold as <arsenious acid> is normally <arsenic trioxide>, $\boxed{\mathrm{As_2O_3}}$, often written $\mathrm{AsO_3}$ in older equivalent notation. The genuinely hydrated molecular <arsenous acid> is $\mathrm{H_3AsO_3}$, also written $\mathrm{As(OH)_3}$. One should not quietly assign the hydrated formula to the dry commercial <oxide>.
* <Tartar emetic>, or <potassium antimony tartrate>, is conventionally represented as $\boxed{\mathrm{K(SbO)C_4H_4O_6\cdot\tfrac12H_2O}}$. A structurally more informative doubled formula is $\mathrm{K_2Sb_2(C_4H_2O_6)_2\cdot3H_2O}$: the complete elemental totals agree. The older “antimonyl tartrate” formula is a compositional convention and does not imply isolated $\mathrm{SbO^+}$ units in the crystal.<Antimony Potassium Tartrate in PubChem>[compound 73415808, elemental formula C8H10K2O15Sb2]{ref}
* <Chlorate of potash> is <potassium chlorate>, $\boxed{\mathrm{KClO_3}}$.

The <chemical formulas> above use modern <atomic weights> and distinguish <molecular formulas>, <formula units> and <hydrates>. Historical equivalent notation sometimes halves the subscripts attached to divalent <chemical elements> or writes <water> as $\mathrm{HO}$. Such formulas must be read together with their contemporary <atomic weights>, rather than treated as inconsistent modern <molecular formulas>.

**Original question.**

Explain briefly how each of the substances named in the preceding question may be best prepared.

=== Solution

**A suitable historical answer gives both the chemical transformation and the means of isolating a reasonably pure product.** The following are preparative principles, with modern <chemical formulas>; they are not instructions for self-preparation of medicines. In particular, <hydrogen cyanide>, <mercury compounds> and <arsenic compounds> present serious <toxicity> hazards. <Hydrogen cyanide> is extremely volatile, can be absorbed by several routes, and its odour is an unreliable warning.<Hydrogen cyanide emergency response information by NIOSH>[Agent Characteristics; Routes of Exposure; warning that smell does not provide adequate protection]{ref}

For <hydrocyanic acid>, a customary early nineteenth-century route is the controlled <distillation> of <potassium ferrocyanide> with dilute <sulfuric acid> and <water>. <Hydrogen cyanide> passes into the distillate; nonvolatile <salts> remain behind. A generating flask, efficient <laboratory condenser> and chilled receiver are needed because <hydrogen cyanide> is readily lost. The contemporary account of <hydrocyanic acid> preparation in <Pharmacopoeia Castrensis Ruthenica in The British and Foreign Medical Review (1842)> specifically describes the <ferrocyanide>–<sulfuric acid> method and its losses on imperfect condensation. The <concentration> must be determined analytically before any defined dilution: a nominal starting mixture does not establish the final strength.<Pharmacopoeia Castrensis Ruthenica in The British and Foreign Medical Review (1842)>[Processes for obtaining Hydrocyanic Acid]{ref} At the level of the released <cyanide>, the essential <acid-base reaction> is $\mathrm{CN^-+H^+\rightarrow HCN}$. This equation alone is not a complete equation for decomposing the stable <ferrocyanide> <coordination complex>. Concentrated <sulfuric acid> can lead to different decomposition chemistry, so the specified dilution is meaningful.

For <formic acid>, gently heat <oxalic acid> with <glycerol> and some <water>, then separate the aqueous <formic acid> by <distillation>. The useful net transformation is
$$
\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4\longrightarrow HCOOH+CO_2}.
$$
The <glycerol> participates through intermediate <esters> and is regenerated rather than consumed in this net equation. <Marcellin Berthelot>'s 1856 account describes the <carbon dioxide> evolution, subsequent <water> addition and <distillation>, and reuse of the residual <glycerol>. Overheating is disadvantageous because the <formic acid> can decompose further to <carbon monoxide> and <water>. The method therefore separates a volatile <acid> from a much less volatile carrier; it is not merely the dry destructive heating of <oxalic acid>.<Verfahren zur Darstellung der Ameisensäure by Marcellin Berthelot>[Polytechnisches Journal 140 (1856), p.78, report of the March 1856 communication]{ref}

For <oxalic acid>, oxidize a <carbohydrate>, such as <sucrose> or <starch>, with <nitric acid>; concentrate the resulting <chemical solution> appropriately and cool it to obtain crystals, followed by <recrystallization>. <Nitrogen oxides> evolve, and excessive <oxidation> can carry <carbon> onward to <carbon dioxide>, so an idealized single-product equation would conceal losses. The use of <starch> and <nitric acid> for <oxalic acid> production is explicitly documented in an 1850 account of <nitric oxide> recovery.<Polytechnisches Journal volume 117 (1850)>[p.466, “Verfahren das Salpetergas in Salpetersäure zu verwandeln”, opening example]{ref} An alternative already established before 1864 is alkaline treatment of <sawdust>, producing an <oxalate> which can be purified through sparingly soluble <calcium oxalate>.<Polytechnisches Journal volume 162 (1861)>[report on chemical manufacture in South Lancashire, Oxalsäure section: alkaline sawdust route]{ref} Decomposing the isolated <calcium oxalate> with <sulfuric acid> separates much of the <calcium> as <calcium sulfate>:
$$
\mathrm{CaC_2O_4+H_2SO_4\longrightarrow H_2C_2O_4+CaSO_4}.
$$
Filter off the <calcium sulfate> and crystallize the <oxalic acid> from the filtrate. The usual crystals contain two <water of crystallization>; a calculation for the anhydrous <acid> must not treat their entire <mass> as $\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4}$.

For <calomel>, intimately combine <mercury(II) sulfate>, metallic <mercury> and dry <sodium chloride>, then collect the volatile <mercury(I) chloride> in a cooler receiving chamber. The net change is
$$
\mathrm{HgSO_4+Hg+2NaCl\longrightarrow Hg_2Cl_2+Na_2SO_4}.
$$
The extra metallic <mercury> lowers the average <oxidation state> from +2 to +1. The product is purified by appropriate <sublimation> and washing to remove soluble contamination, particularly <mercury(II) chloride>. Both the <chemical composition> and the fine subdivision of the product matter: condensation through a chamber can give a powder rather than an adherent crystalline crust. This physical part of <calomel> preparation is described in the 1843 primary account in <Polytechnisches Journal volume 87 (1843)>; the account's historical therapeutic claims are not adopted here.<Polytechnisches Journal volume 87 (1843)>[pp.209 onward, “Ueber Darstellung des Calomels als höchst zartes Pulver”, condensation and collecting chamber]{ref}

For <corrosive sublimate>, heat dry <mercury(II) sulfate> with <sodium chloride> and collect the <mercury(II) chloride> in the cooler part of a suitable <sublimation> apparatus:
$$
\mathrm{HgSO_4+2NaCl\longrightarrow HgCl_2+Na_2SO_4}.
$$
Here no metallic <mercury> is added. Nonvolatile <sodium sulfate> stays behind, while <mercury(II) chloride> is transferred and condensed. Further <sublimation> or <recrystallization> separates impurities. <Dictionary of Arts, Manufactures and Mines by Andrew Ure>[Mercury, p.141, bichloride preparation and the following calomel account]{ref} The crucial distinction from <calomel> preparation is whether <mercury> remains in the +2 <oxidation state> or is converted to +1.

For the solid called <arsenious acid>, roast an <arsenic>-bearing <ore> in <air> and collect the volatile <arsenic trioxide> in cooler chambers; purify by resublimation. The essential <oxidation> is represented by
$$
\mathrm{4As+3O_2\longrightarrow2As_2O_3}.
$$
Actual <ores> also contain other <chemical elements>, so their roasting equations and gaseous by-products depend on composition. A contemporary 1860 report describes roasting arseniferous <ores>, collecting the volatile <arsenic trioxide> in chambers and refining it by another <sublimation>. It also demonstrates that apparently good material could contain <antimony(III) oxide>: physical appearance alone does not establish purity.<Polytechnisches Journal volume 156 (1860)>[p.77, report on the antimony content of glassy arsenious acid from Andreasberg]{ref} The product intended here is dry $\mathrm{As_2O_3}$; dissolving it does not make the bottled solid a pure crystalline hydrate $\mathrm{H_3AsO_3}$.

For <tartar emetic>, react <antimony(III) oxide> with <potassium hydrogen tartrate> in <water>. A small amount of <water> first gives intimate contact in a paste; subsequent heating with more <water> completes formation of the soluble <potassium antimony tartrate>. Filter away unreacted insoluble matter and cool the clear filtrate to crystallize the product. This sequence is directly described in a January 1864 discussion of the newly issued <British Pharmacopoeia>, rather than inferred from its later editions.<Chemical News volume 9 (1864)>[30 January 1864, p.53, Antimonium Tartaratum: paste, standing, boiling and the oxide–bitartrate route]{ref} In the traditional antimonyl notation the underlying reaction, before assigning crystal <water of crystallization>, is
$$
\mathrm{Sb_2O_3+2KHC_4H_4O_6\longrightarrow2K(SbO)C_4H_4O_6+H_2O}.
$$
This notation expresses the overall composition; <tartrate> <coordination complexes> and <water of crystallization> need the qualification given in <4>. The historical pharmaceutical use of <tartar emetic> does not make it a safe contemporary emetic.<Antimony potassium tartrate by the National Poisons Information Service>[Toxicity; gastrointestinal effects and systemic poisoning]{ref}

For <potassium chlorate>, pass <chlorine> into hot concentrated <potassium hydroxide>. The <disproportionation> gives <potassium chloride> together with <potassium chlorate>:
$$
\mathrm{3Cl_2+6KOH\longrightarrow KClO_3+5KCl+3H_2O}.
$$
Concentration and cooling permit <fractional crystallization> of the much less soluble cold-water <potassium chlorate>, leaving much of the <potassium chloride> in the mother liquor; <recrystallization> improves purity. Cold dilute <alkali> instead favours <hypochlorite>, so the heating condition is essential. An economical contemporary manufacturing alternative uses hot <calcium hydroxide> and <potassium chloride>; the overall accounting is
$$
\mathrm{6Cl_2+6Ca(OH)_2+2KCl\longrightarrow2KClO_3+6CaCl_2+6H_2O}.
$$
The 1850 account in <Polytechnisches Journal volume 118 (1850)> describes a steam-heated stirred vessel and recovery by cooling, rather than an electrical manufacturing process introduced retrospectively.<Polytechnisches Journal volume 118 (1850)>[pp.424 onward, “Die neue Bereitungsart des chlorsauren Kalis”]{ref}

**Original question.**

What are the constituents of cows' milk, and how may they be separated ?

=== Solution

**<Cow's milk> contains <water>, dispersed <fat>, <casein>, soluble <milk proteins>, <lactose> and <mineral> constituents, with smaller amounts of other substances.** These constituents occupy different physical states, which makes their separation possible: the <fat> is present as an <emulsion>, much of the <casein> as <colloidal> <casein micelles>, and the <lactose> and many <salts> in <chemical solution>. <Vitamins>, <enzymes> and small nitrogenous constituents accompany these main fractions. The proportions vary between samples and are not fixed by the name <cow's milk>.<The Chemistry of Milk in the Dairy Processing Handbook>[chapter 2, principal constituents and Tables 2.1–2.2]{ref}

First let fresh unhomogenized <milk> stand so that the less dense <fat globules> rise as <cream>; skimming gives a <fat>-enriched layer and <skimmed milk> beneath. This separates fractions rather than pure <chemical substances>: <cream> still contains <water> and other constituents. Churning <cream> coalesces the <fat globules> into butter; extraction of a separately prepared dried portion with a suitable organic <solvent> provides another way to isolate <fat>. A modern <centrifugal separator> accelerates the same density-based separation, but it is not needed to explain an 1864 bench separation.

Next add dilute <acetic acid> carefully to <skimmed milk>. Lowering its <pH> toward the <isoelectric point> of <casein>, about 4.6, produces a curd that can be collected by <filtration> and washed. Excess <acid>, retained <fat>, and shifts of <calcium phosphate> between curd and liquid limit purity. <Rennet> offers another route to a curd, but its <enzyme>-catalysed action and the <mineral> content of the resulting curd differ from simple <acid precipitation>; the two should not be treated as identical mechanisms.<The Chemistry of Milk in the Dairy Processing Handbook>[chapter 2, Casein, precipitation and acidification]{ref}

The liquid after removal of the curd is <whey>. Heat this fraction under suitable acidity to induce <protein denaturation> and aggregate much of its residual <whey protein>; collect the coagulum by <filtration>. Older descriptions commonly grouped this fraction under “albumin”, but modern <milk chemistry> distinguishes several <proteins>. Heating alone does not make every minor constituent fall into a single pure fraction.<Milk and Whey Fractionation in the Dairy Processing Handbook>[chapter 18, Recovery of denatured whey proteins]{ref}

Concentrate the deproteinized <whey> and cool it to crystallize <lactose>. Filter and wash the crystals, then purify them by <recrystallization>; dissolved <salts> and residual <organic matter> remain substantially in the mother liquor. This provides a separation from most <protein>, not an automatic complete separation from all <minerals>.<Milk and Whey Fractionation in the Dairy Processing Handbook>[chapter 18, Lactose recovery; Crystallisation; Lactose separation]{ref}

Finally use separate measured portions for <water> and <mineral> determinations. Controlled drying gives an operational <total solids> measurement; subtraction from the initial <mass> estimates the <water> lost. Ignition of a dried portion destroys most <organic matter> and leaves <ash> for <mineral analysis>. <Ash> is a transformed residue: it does not preserve all original <salts> unchanged, and some constituents can volatilize. <Calcium>, <phosphorus>, <sodium>, <potassium> and <chloride> are among the important <mineral> constituents. **Skimming, curd formation, heat coagulation, lactose crystallization, drying and ashing separate or determine the principal fractions; none alone constitutes a complete pure-component analysis.**

**Original question.**

How may Glucose be prepared and detected ?

=== Solution

**<Glucose> can be obtained by <hydrolysis> of <starch> and detected as a <reducing sugar> by a heated alkaline <copper(II)> test. A positive copper test alone does not uniquely identify <glucose>.**

Suspend <starch> in <water>, warm it to make a paste, and heat with a dilute <acid>, such as dilute <sulfuric acid>, to cause <hydrolysis> of the <glycosidic bonds>. The <chemical reaction> passes through shorter <carbohydrates>; sufficient <hydrolysis> ultimately gives <glucose>. The usual repeat-unit accounting is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{(C_6H_{10}O_5)_n+nH_2O\longrightarrow nC_6H_{12}O_6}.}
$$
This idealized <polymer> formula omits end-group details. The <acid> catalyses cleavage and is not consumed in the displayed equation. Merely using <amylase> from malt should not be described as necessarily producing pure <glucose>; it can leave <maltose> and shorter <polysaccharides>.

When <sulfuric acid> is used, neutralization with <calcium carbonate> removes the acidity and much of the <sulfate> as <calcium sulfate>. Filter out the insoluble residue, then concentrate the <chemical solution> gently and crystallize the <glucose>; further <recrystallization> is required to distinguish a reasonably pure <chemical substance> from a crude syrup. Overheating and unnecessarily prolonged strong <acid> treatment degrade the <sugar>. <Hydrolysis> of <sucrose> is a less selective preparative answer because it gives <fructose> alongside <glucose>.

For the historical copper test, use freshly mixed <Fehling's reagent>: an aqueous <copper(II) sulfate> portion and an alkaline <tartrate> portion. The <tartrate> keeps <copper(II)> in <coordination complexes> instead of allowing immediate bulk precipitation of <copper(II) hydroxide>. Add a suitably prepared sample to a small test portion and warm in a hot <water bath>. A yellow-orange to brick-red <precipitate> of <copper(I) oxide> indicates <reduction> of the <copper(II)> reagent. Run a known <glucose> sample and a <water> blank alongside it. In a simple sample, a visible difference between these controls helps distinguish a true positive from a deteriorated reagent or contamination.<Estimation of Glucose by Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham>[Theory, uses of Fehling's solution and formation of red copper(I) oxide]{ref}

The key <redox> accounting on the <copper> side is
$$
\mathrm{2Cu^{2+}+2OH^-+2e^-\longrightarrow Cu_2O+H_2O}.
$$
The required <electrons> come from <oxidation> of the <sugar>. This half-equation deliberately omits the changing <tartrate> <ligands> and does not assign a unique organic product. The often printed claim that hot alkaline <glucose> simply becomes <gluconate> in this test is too simple: competing <isomerization>, <oxidation> and fragmentation make the organic chemistry more complicated.<The Iodine Test for Reducing Sugars by Holger Fleischer>[§2.3, interpretation of copper tests and product complexity]{ref}

<Fructose>, <lactose>, <maltose> and various other <reducing substances> can also give a positive <Fehling's test>. In particular, <fructose> can form <aldoses> by <tautomerization> in <alkaline solution>; an initial <ketone> functional group does not make it reliably negative.<Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/25.6 Reactions of Monosaccharides>[Oxidation of Monosaccharides; fructose rearrangement]{ref} Fermentation by suitable <yeast>, with <carbon dioxide> formation, and <optical rotation> can supply further historical evidence, but neither is individually specific to <glucose>. These are anticipated diagnostic observations, not measurements supplied by the paper. In present laboratory practice an appropriately validated <enzymatic glucose assay> can establish <glucose> more selectively; the old reducing-sugar test is not by itself a diagnosis of <diabetes>.<Diagnosis of Diabetes in PDB-101>[Monitoring: Diagnosis, blood tests and glucose-oxidase assay explanation]{ref}

**Original question.**

Give the ratio which equal volumes of the following gases bear to the volumes of the elements of which they consist :—Hydrochloric Acid, Water, Protoxide of Nitrogen, Binoxide of Nitrogen, Ammonia.

=== Solution

**At the same temperature and pressure, one volume of HCl corresponds to $\tfrac12$ volume of hydrogen and $\tfrac12$ volume of chlorine; one of steam to one of hydrogen and $\tfrac12$ of oxygen; one of $\mathrm{N_2O}$ to one of nitrogen and $\tfrac12$ of oxygen; one of NO to $\tfrac12$ of each element; and one of ammonia to $\tfrac12$ of nitrogen and $\tfrac32$ of hydrogen.** All elemental <volumes> here refer to their ordinary diatomic <gases>.

The simplest way to avoid an erroneous factor of two is to balance each <chemical reaction> using $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$ and $\mathrm{O_2}$. Under <Avogadro's law>, their <stoichiometric coefficients> are also <gas volume> ratios, provided all <gases> are compared at the same <temperature> and <pressure> and <ideal gas> behaviour is a good approximation.

* For gaseous <hydrogen chloride>, $\mathrm{H_2+Cl_2\rightarrow2HCl}$. Thus $\boxed{V_{\mathrm{HCl}}:V_{\mathrm{H_2}}:V_{\mathrm{Cl_2}}=2:1:1}$. There is no change in total <gas volume> in this idealized combination.
* For <water vapor>, $\mathrm{2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O(g)}$. Thus $\boxed{V_{\mathrm{H_2O(g)}}:V_{\mathrm{H_2}}:V_{\mathrm{O_2}}=2:2:1}$: three reactant <volumes> give two product <volumes>.
* “Protoxide of nitrogen” is <nitrous oxide>, $\mathrm{N_2O}$. The composition equation $\mathrm{2N_2+O_2\rightarrow2N_2O}$ gives $\boxed{V_{\mathrm{N_2O}}:V_{\mathrm{N_2}}:V_{\mathrm{O_2}}=2:2:1}$. This equation expresses composition; it does not assert that direct mixing gives an easy preparative reaction.
* “Binoxide of nitrogen” in the historical equivalent nomenclature is <nitric oxide>, modern $\mathrm{NO}$, rather than modern <nitrogen dioxide>. The equation $\mathrm{N_2+O_2\rightarrow2NO}$ gives $\boxed{V_{\mathrm{NO}}:V_{\mathrm{N_2}}:V_{\mathrm{O_2}}=2:1:1}$. Older $\mathrm{NO_2}$ notation with <oxygen> equivalent 8 describes the same oxygen-to-nitrogen mass ratio as modern $\mathrm{NO}$ with <oxygen> atomic weight 16; reading its subscript by modern rules changes the substance.<Review of Chemistry for Students by John G. Murphy>[Nitrogen, p.115, “Binoxide of Nitrogen; Nitric Oxide”, older formula and equal elemental volumes]{ref}
* For <ammonia>, $\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\rightarrow2NH_3}$ gives $\boxed{V_{\mathrm{NH_3}}:V_{\mathrm{N_2}}:V_{\mathrm{H_2}}=2:1:3}$: four reactant <volumes> correspond to two product <volumes>.

If the common chosen <volume> of each compound is $V$, the corresponding ordered elemental <volumes> are therefore $(V/2,V/2)$, $(V,V/2)$, $(V,V/2)$, $(V/2,V/2)$ and $(V/2,3V/2)$. The ratios concern molecular counts and hypothetical free elemental <gases>, not separate compartments of elemental <gas> still present inside a compound.

“Hydrochloric acid” must here mean gaseous <hydrogen chloride>, rather than its aqueous <chemical solution>; <water> must remain as <steam>. If <water> condenses or <ammonia> dissolves in the collecting <liquid>, measured <volumes> no longer show these simple ratios. For an experimental comparison, dry-gas and <hydrostatic pressure> corrections are those discussed in <3>.
