== Latin translation
{scope}
{c}

**Original question.**

Translate:—

Eques erat ornatissimus, annos natus quinque et sexaginta, sed robustis membris et proba humorum temperie, nisi quod ante annos aliquot diuturnis et contumacibus crurum ulceribus laboraverat : quae pertaesus remediis qua internis qua externis tandem persanaverat. Is novissime rheumaticis doloribus subinde obnoxius, sed levibus adeo ut qui domo exire non prohiberent, quod et pridie fecerat quam moreretur, iis acerbius prehensus est V. Nonas Junias ad sternum quidem et brachia, non sine capitis perturbatione. Quibus per ea quae sunt visa magis idonea cum occursum esset, jam circa meridiem magna ex parte relevatus, quamvis infirmo pulsu, alacriter se gerebat. A prandio cum quievisset, expergefactus, eosdem quos mane dolores sensit. Quapropter inambulare in conclavi coepit ; sic enim illos facilius ferebat. Sed postquam satis id fecerat, cum lectum repetiisset, querebatur de fumis ad caput ascendentibus, et de pectoris angustiis, inquietusque sese huc illucque vertebat. Quod cum spatio temporis non brevi fecisset, repente se mori sensit, simulque pallens et se agitans illico interiit. Abdomine diducto, inventa sunt omnia secundum naturam. Pectore autem aperto, et pulmonibus, qui antrorsum compulsi erant, dimotis, pericardium deprehensum est nigro, concretoque sanguine distentum. Is e sinistro cordis ventriculo exierat per scissuram longam unciae dimidium et secundum illius longitudinem ductam, circa quam cordis fibrae non recenti corrosione exesae conspiciebantur.

*Morgagni.* De Sed. Mor. II. 27, 8.

=== Solution

There was a very distinguished knight, sixty-five years old, but with sturdy limbs and a sound balance of the <humoral fluids>, except that some years earlier he had suffered from long-lasting, obstinate <ulcers> of the legs. Weary of these, he had at last completely healed them by remedies both internal and external. More recently he had occasionally been subject to rheumatic <pains>, but <pains> so slight that they did not prevent him from leaving home, as he had indeed done on the day before his death. On 1 June these seized him more severely, at the <sternum> and in the arms, accompanied by disturbance of the head. After they had been treated with the measures judged most suitable, he was already largely relieved by about midday and behaved cheerfully, although his <pulse> was weak.

After resting following dinner, he awoke and felt the same <pains> as in the morning. He therefore began walking about his room, for in this way he bore them more easily. But after doing this sufficiently, he returned to bed and complained of vapours rising to his head and of tightness in his chest; in his restlessness he turned this way and that. After doing so for a considerable time, he suddenly felt that he was dying and, at the same moment, grew pale, struggled, and died immediately.

When the abdomen was opened, everything was found in its natural condition. But when the chest was opened and the <lungs>, which had been pushed forwards, were moved aside, the <pericardium> was found distended with dark, clotted <blood>. This had escaped from the left <cardiac ventricle> through a tear half an inch long, running in the direction of its length. Around it the fibres of the <heart> were visibly eaten away by an erosion that was not recent.

**The anatomical finding is rupture of the left ventricular wall into the <pericardium>.** This is <Morgagni>'s account in <The Seats and Causes of Diseases Investigated by Anatomy by Giovanni Battista Morgagni>, Book II, Letter XXVII, article 8. The language of balanced <humoral fluids>, rheumatic <pain>, rising vapours and erosion belongs to the account's historical descriptive framework. It does not establish a modern rheumatological diagnosis or a microscopic cause of rupture. The Roman date is the fifth day before the Nones of June, counted inclusively: 1 June. The translation retains the historical half-inch measure without assigning an unjustified modern measurement to the specimen. <The Seats and Causes of Diseases Investigated by Anatomy by Giovanni Battista Morgagni>[II.27.8; Latin extract on original Oxford PDF-volume p. 25]{ref}

== Similar case and cardiac muscle fibres
{scope}

**Original question.**

Relate any similar case which you may have seen or heard of: and state the probable condition of the Cardiac Muscular Fibres.

=== Solution

**A suitable published comparison is <J. B. Junor>'s 1858 report of <cardiac rupture> with a soft, fatty ventricular wall and <blood> in the <pericardium>.** The comparison comes from the published clinical record.

The patient was a woman of about seventy with repeated <vomiting> and an initially weak <pulse>. She deteriorated after a severe episode of <vomiting>, her radial <pulse> becoming almost imperceptible, and died a few hours later. At examination after death, a large <gallstone> was found impacted in the <ileum>. The <pericardium> contained much <blood>. The small, atrophied <heart> had an extremely soft and fatty right <cardiac ventricle>; when it was raised, <blood> emerged through several small fissures, and its tissue broke under very gentle handling. <Gallstone ileus with rupture of the heart by J. B. Junor>[Edinburgh Medical Journal, May 1858, pp. 1004–1005, clinical course and post-mortem findings]{ref}

The comparison is anatomical and pathological: a fragile ventricular wall, escape of <blood> and death. There are also important differences. <Morgagni>'s tear is in the left <cardiac ventricle>; <J. B. Junor>'s reported fissures concern the right <cardiac ventricle> and were described during handling of a very friable <heart>. The latter account includes intestinal obstruction and does not reproduce <Morgagni>'s sternal and arm <pain>. It supports the possibility of severe muscular degeneration in a ruptured <heart>, without establishing that the two patients had identical disease or that every observed fissure opened before death.

**Probable muscular condition in an 1864 answer.** The fibres around the tear were probably structurally weakened and softened; <fatty degeneration of the heart> is a strong historical pathological suggestion. This means alteration within damaged muscular fibres, rather than merely an abundance of <adipose tissue> outside them. In <Richard Quain>'s account, reproduced before the examination, microscopic fatty change involved loss of the normal transverse markings and refractile fatty particles within the fibre sheath. Extensive change could accompany a pale, mottled, flabby <myocardium>. Those are findings one would look for, not observations supplied by the Oxford passage. <On Fatty Diseases of the Heart by Richard Quain>[1850 account reproduced in 1852, pp. 120–123, distinction between fatty growth and degeneration within muscular fibres]{ref}

An appropriate investigation would compare thin preparations from the tear's margin with apparently unaffected <myocardium> under a <microscope>, looking at the cross-striation, continuity of the muscular structure and position of the fatty particles. Fat lying between bundles is different from particles lying within damaged fibres. The gross appearance, the microscopic preparation and the sampling site must be reported separately. The word “erosion” does not by itself supply these microscopic findings. <On Fatty Diseases of the Heart by Richard Quain>[1852 reproduction, pp. 123–126, microscopic appearances, friability and examination of fresh samples from different regions]{ref}

**Modern interpretation and its limit.** Rupture of a left ventricular free wall can follow <myocardial infarction>, where a zone of damaged <myocardium> becomes friable. That is a plausible modern differential for the combination of chest and arm <pain>, weak <pulse> and a tear, but the historical record does not supply coronary anatomy or <histology> sufficient to prove it. A sudden collection of <blood> in the <pericardium>, or <haemopericardium>, can impair cardiac filling and cause <cardiac tamponade>. This provides a mechanism compatible with the abrupt pallor and collapse; pericardial pressure was not measured in the reported case. Present-day assessment could use urgent <echocardiography>; that technology belongs to the later comparison. <Mechanical Complications of Acute Myocardial Infarction by the American Heart Association>[Free wall rupture: friable infarct zone, collapse, tamponade and bedside echocardiography]{ref}

The defensible conclusion is **a ruptured, locally weakened <myocardium>, with <fatty degeneration of the heart> a probable nineteenth-century interpretation and infarct-related damage a modern possibility requiring additional evidence**.

== Greek translation
{scope}
{c}

**Original question.**

Translate:—

Μανίης τρόποι εἴδεσι μὲν μυρίοι, γένει δὲ μοῦνος εἷς· ἔκστασις γὰρ ἐστι τὸ σύμπαν χρόνιος, ἄνευθεν πυρετοῦ· εἰ γάρ κοτε καὶ πυρετὸς ἐπιλάβοι, οὐκ ἀπὸ μανίης ἂν ἴδιος γίγνοιτο, ἀλλ’ ἐκ συντυχίης ἄλλης· ἐκφλέγει γὰρ καὶ οἶνος ἐς παραφορὴν ἐν μέθῃ· ἐκμαίνει δὲ καὶ τῶν ἐδεστῶν μετεξέτερα, ἡ μανδραγόρη, ἡ ὑοσκύαμος, ἀλλ’ οὔ τί πω μανίη τάδε κικλήσκεται. ἐπὶ γὰρ σχεδίου γιγνόμενα καθίσταται θᾶττον· τὸ δὲ ἔμπεδον ἡ μανίη ἴσχει· τῇδε τῇ μανίῃ οὐδέν τι ἴκελοι ἡ λήρησις, γήραος ἡ ξυμφορή. αἰσθήσιος γὰρ ἐστι νάρκη, καὶ γνώμης νάρκωσις ἠδὲ τοῦ νοῦ ὑπὸ ψύξιος· μανίη δὲ θερμόν τι καὶ ξηρὸν τῇ αἰτίῃ, καὶ ταραχῶδες τῇσι πρήξεσι· ἡ μὲν γὰρ λήρησις ἀρχομένη ἀπὸ γήραος οὔτε διαλείπει, καὶ ξυναποθνήσκει· μανίη δὲ καὶ διαλείπει καὶ μελεδῶνι ἐς τέλος ἀποπαύεται· διάλειψις δὲ ἀτελής, ἢν τῷ τῆς μανίης λόγῳ γίγνηται, οὐκ εἰκότως ἀκεομένου τοῦ κακοῦ ἰητρείῃ, ἢ τῆς ὥρης εὐκρασίῃ. μετεξετέρους γὰρ δοκέοντας ἀσινέας ἔμμεναι, ἡ ὥρη τὸ ἔαρ, ἢ ἁμαρτωλὴ διαίτης, ἢ ὀργὴ ἐκ συντυχίης ἐς ἀνάκλησιν ἤγαγε.

Aretæus. Χρον. Παθ. Α. ϛʹ.

=== Solution

The forms of <ancient mania> are innumerable in their varieties, but there is only one general kind: taken as a whole, it is a lasting derangement of the mind without <fever>. For if <fever> should at some time also occur, it would not arise as a feature peculiar to the <ancient mania>, but from some other coinciding cause. Thus <wine> also inflames the mind into delirious behaviour in <drunkenness>, and some things that are eaten, such as <mandrake> and <henbane>, drive people out of their minds. Yet these conditions are not called <ancient mania>: arising from a temporary cause, they settle more quickly, whereas <ancient mania> has persistence.

The <senile dotage> that is a misfortune of old age is quite unlike this <ancient mania>. It is a torpor of sensation and a numbing of judgement and intellect through coldness; <ancient mania>, by contrast, has something hot and dry in its cause and is turbulent in its actions. For <senile dotage> beginning in old age neither remits nor leaves the sufferer before death, whereas <ancient mania> does remit and, with care, can come to a complete end.

But a remission is incomplete when it occurs in keeping with the course of the <ancient mania>, without the illness having been properly cured by treatment or by the mildness of the season. For in some people who seemed to be unharmed, the spring season, an error in their way of life, or anger arising from some incident has brought the condition back.

**<Aretaeus of Cappadocia> distinguishes persistent mental illness, transient intoxication and the cognitive decline attributed to old age.** The opening of <On the Causes and Signs of Chronic Diseases by Aretaeus of Cappadocia>, I.6, organizes these distinctions by persistence, attributed cause and course, ending with examples of relapse. <On the Causes and Signs of Chronic Diseases by Aretaeus of Cappadocia>[I.6, opening paragraph, from the classification of mania through relapse in spring]{ref}

Several terms need historical interpretation. “Derangement” renders a displacement from one's ordinary mental state, not a diagnosis based on a modern symptom checklist. “Way of life” includes <diet> and regimen; it is wider than a single meal. The sentence about incomplete remission concerns an apparent improvement within the illness's course, distinguished from a thorough cure through treatment or favourable seasonal conditions. A quiet interval can therefore be followed by recurrence. The hot, dry and cold explanations are historical physiology, not established modern causes.

The scope of <ancient mania> is broader than present-day <mania>. Current assessment examines the course of mood and activity changes, symptoms between episodes, medication and substance exposure, physical illness and alternative diagnoses; it does not define the disorder simply as every persistent mental disturbance without <fever>. Nor should historical <senile dotage> be equated with a precise diagnosis of one modern <dementia>, or the passage's assertions about old age be accepted as a modern prognosis. <Bipolar disorder: assessment and management by NICE>[CG185 §§1.3.2–1.3.3: longitudinal assessment and consideration of substance-related, physical and other psychiatric explanations]{ref}

== Greek aphorism translation
{scope}
{c}

**Original question.**

Translate:—

Ἐν τροφῇ γὰρ κείσεται τὰ φάρμακα.

Ib.

=== Solution

**“For the remedies will be found in <food>.”** More literally, the sentence says that the remedies “will lie in nourishment”. The connective “for” is retained, and the future tense expresses where the therapeutic resource is to be sought. The statement allows <diet> to act as treatment; it does not say that every illness can be cured by ordinary eating.

The attribution is to <Aretaeus of Cappadocia>. The sentence also occurs in the dietary discussion of <pleurisy> in <On the Treatment of Acute Diseases by Aretaeus of Cappadocia>, I.10. Thus the printed “Ib.” need not place this separate aphorism inside the preceding chapter on <ancient mania>. The wider dietary recommendations there belong to ancient medicine; the short maxim can be illustrated without endorsing all of them. <On the Treatment of Acute Diseases by Aretaeus of Cappadocia>[I.10, dietary paragraph beginning with the discussion of nourishment; the Greek has the same future-tense verb]{ref}

== Aphorism illustration
{scope}

**Original question.**

Illustrate this aphorism from modern experience.

=== Solution

**<Scurvy> supplies a clear illustration available to an 1864 candidate: an appropriate change in <diet> can act as a remedy.** In <A Treatise of the Scurvy by James Lind>, <James Lind> described a shipboard comparison begun on 20 May 1747. Twelve affected sailors lived in the same sick quarters and shared a common basic <diet>, while pairs received different additions. The additions included cider, acidic preparations, sea water, a compound preparation, and fresh oranges and lemons. Thus the comparison asked whether a particular addition helped, rather than merely observing that patients ate while recovering.

The pair given fresh citrus each received two oranges and one lemon daily. Supplies lasted six days. <James Lind> reported that one was then fit for duty, while the other had recovered sufficiently to help nurse the remaining sick. The exact contrast matters: two recoveries are not proof that every sour substance is equivalent to citrus, and <James Lind>'s physiological theories should not be confused with the observed result. The record is also a small comparison with incompletely specified allocation, rather than evidence for a modern randomized design. It nevertheless supplies a concrete, pre-1864 illustration of a therapeutic resource carried in <food>. <A Treatise of the Scurvy by James Lind>[1753, pp. 191–196, common diet, six paired treatments and the course of the citrus recipients; original pages reproduced by the James Lind Library]{ref}

**Present explanation.** <Scurvy> is caused by deficient <vitamin C> intake. Humans require a dietary source of <vitamin C>; it is needed for normal <collagen> formation. Deficiency weakens <connective tissue> and can produce fragile small <blood vessels>, bleeding gums and poor <wound healing>. Suitable <food> can therefore correct the deficiency, not merely disguise its symptoms. This explanation is later knowledge: an 1864 candidate could cite the effect of fresh antiscorbutic <food> without knowing the chemical identity of <vitamin C>. The effective factor is not simply acidity, nor is the claim that more <vitamin C> cures every unrelated disease. <Vitamin C Fact Sheet for Health Professionals by NIH>[Introduction, Vitamin C Deficiency and Food Sources: dietary requirement, collagen, scurvy and citrus sources]{ref}

There is also a broader nutritional sense. In <malnutrition>, adequate <food> supplies energy and <protein> needed for recovery, and the composition and route of nutritional support depend on the person's capacity to eat and absorb it. Current guidance distinguishes ordinary enriched <food> and oral supplements from enteral or parenteral support when required. This extends the principle of using nourishment therapeutically, while distinguishing it from a specific cure such as correcting <vitamin C> deficiency. These present-day care pathways are not attributed to ancient practice or to the 1864 examiner. <Nutrition support for adults by NICE>[CG32 §§1.3–1.4 and 1.6–1.8: indications, prescription and routes of nutrition support]{ref}

The illustration therefore supports **a specific relation between nutritional need, a suitable <food>, and a measurable clinical effect**. It gives no warrant for replacing every other form of treatment with a general dietary slogan.
