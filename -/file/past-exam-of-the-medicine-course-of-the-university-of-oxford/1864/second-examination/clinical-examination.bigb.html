{scope}

**Original question.**

*At the Museum.*

Examine (writing a methodical account of your plan of examination) the Urine marked A.

=== Solution

**Examine the specimen systematically: establish its condition, inspect it, assess reaction and concentration, investigate important solutes, examine the sediment, and interpret the findings together.** This is a written plan; the particular appearances and analytical results for A are not supplied.

The physical, chemical and microscopical divisions are complementary. <Urinalysis in Clinical Methods by David M. Roxe> supplies a modern framework for them; its 1990 account includes <urine reagent strips>, which must not be attributed to an 1864 examination. A period account instead uses visual examination, indicator paper, a <urinometer>, separate wet-chemical tests and an <optical microscope>. Microscopy of urinary deposits was already established before this paper.<Urinary Deposits (review, 1853)>[pp. 128–129: microscopical examination even when no deposit is visible to the unaided eye]{ref}

**Check the sample before testing it.** Record the label, collection time, whether it is a single void or part of a timed collection, and any known contamination or storage. The volume in one bottle does not establish daily <urine> output. Retain an unchanged portion and use separate clean vessels for different tests. Examine promptly: standing changes the specimen through microbial growth, chemical change and the formation or disappearance of particles. A current investigation would use appropriate collection and preservation procedures; a fresh sample and one altered by prolonged storage are not interchangeable.<EFLM European Urinalysis Guideline (2023)>[Executive summary, Patient preparation and Specimen collection; preanalytical sample quality and preservation]{ref}

**Describe what is visible.** Note colour, clarity, apparent sediment and any surface film. Compare a well-mixed portion with material allowed to settle. Cloudiness may reflect <urinary crystals>, <red blood cells> or <leukocytes>, microorganisms or contamination, so it should be described before a cause is assigned. The plan does not infer “pus” from opacity or <jaundice> from colour alone. Record actual observations and the conditions under which they were made; neither tasting nor deliberate inhalation is a necessary diagnostic step.

**Assess reaction and concentration.** Indicator paper such as <litmus> supplies a period-appropriate assessment of acidity or alkalinity; modern <pH> measurement gives a numerical result. Measure <specific gravity> with a freely floating <urinometer> in sufficient fluid, avoiding contact with the vessel and following its calibration and <temperature> requirements. Report the instrument and conditions. <Specific gravity> concerns dissolved material as well as <water>, so a dense specimen is not by itself proof of either normal renal function or <glycosuria>.<Urinalysis in Clinical Methods by David M. Roxe>[Basic Science: specific gravity, urinometer temperature correction and the effects of dissolved constituents]{ref}

**Investigate protein on a separate portion.** For a historical account, a controlled heat-coagulation test with attention to the reaction of the fluid, together with an appropriate acid-precipitation test, provides a way to investigate the older category “albumen.” Compare any turbidity before and after the specified reagent, since heating can also bring inorganic salts out of solution. In a present laboratory, a <urine reagent strip> result is followed, where appropriate, by quantitative <albumin> or total <protein> measurement. These tests have different sensitivities and chemical selectivities; one negative screen does not exclude every urinary <protein>.<Proteinuria in Clinical Methods>[Technique: heat and precipitation methods, strip selectivity, confirmation and quantification]{ref}

**Investigate sugar independently.** A period chemical approach tests reduction of an alkaline <copper(II)> reagent, while a modern enzyme-based method tests <glucose> more selectively. A positive reducing reaction does not identify <glucose> uniquely, and the amount of reduction is not a diagnosis of <diabetes>. Confirm the substance sought and relate confirmed <glycosuria> to <blood glucose> and renal handling before drawing a clinical conclusion. A sample can contain other reducing substances, and impaired tubular recovery can permit <glycosuria> without persistent <hyperglycaemia>.<Glucosuria in Clinical Methods>[Technique and Basic Science: copper reduction versus glucose-specific methods; filtered load and renal threshold]{ref} A <reagent blank> and a suitable known reference help distinguish specimen behaviour from an unsuccessful or contaminated test.

**Examine the formed elements.** Keep an unmodified portion for <urine microscopy>. Let a suitable portion settle, or in a modern standardised laboratory prepare a measured sediment by controlled <centrifugation>. Place a drop under a coverslip, survey several fields at low magnification, then use higher magnification to characterise cells, casts and crystals. Record the method and magnification so that reported abundance has a meaning.<EFLM European Urinalysis Guideline (2023)>[Particle analysis and microscopy procedures; sample preparation and reporting]{ref} Do not acidify the entire specimen merely to clear it before recording its original particles: the treatment may change the structures being sought.

Distinguish <red blood cells>, <leukocytes>, epithelial elements, <urinary casts>, <urinary crystals>, microorganisms and extraneous fibres or other artefacts. A cast's shape and contents provide different information from free cells. Hyaline casts are not invariably pathological; a red-cell cast is more significant for a renal source of bleeding than a few free <red blood cells>. White-cell casts indicate renal inflammation but are not uniquely diagnostic of bacterial <pyelonephritis>.<Urinary Casts by Merck Manual>[Hyaline, red-cell and white-cell casts; interpretation and pseudocasts]{ref} Crystal morphology, the specimen's reaction and storage conditions should be considered together; crystals in a bottle do not alone demonstrate a <urinary stone>.

**Confirm and report the pattern.** If the findings raise a question about <bilirubin>, blood pigment, infection or another constituent, perform the appropriate discriminating test on a fresh separate portion. Modern blood-pigment, <ketone> and leukocyte/nitrite strip tests are later additions, not an 1864 instrument list. <Urine culture> is a separate modern investigation when infection is suspected. Neither <pyuria> nor organisms seen in a possibly contaminated specimen settle that diagnosis on their own.<Bacterial Urinary Tract Infections by Merck Manual>[Urine testing: limitations of microscopy, contamination, culture and leukocyte findings]{ref}

The final account should distinguish the observations, the tests and their limitations, and the physiological or pathological interpretation. Reconcile apparently conflicting results, repeat a doubtful test on a fresh portion, and identify what further clinical information is needed. **The deliverable here is the examination plan; no colour, density, chemical result, sediment finding or diagnosis is assigned to A.**
