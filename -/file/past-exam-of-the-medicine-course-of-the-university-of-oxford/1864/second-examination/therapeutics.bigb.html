**Original question.**

Give an outline of the mode of treatment you would employ in a case of acute Rheumatic Fever.

=== Solution

**Support the acute illness, relieve painful <arthritis>, and repeatedly assess the <heart>; modern treatment also eradicates the antecedent <streptococcal infection> and prevents recurrence.** <Acute rheumatic fever> is an inflammatory consequence of preceding <group A streptococcal infection>, rather than infection of each painful <anatomical joint> by the organism. The distinction matters because an <antibiotic> and an <anti-inflammatory drug> have different purposes.<Clinical Guidance for Acute Rheumatic Fever by CDC>[Cause, clinical features, treatment and secondary prevention]{ref}

In an 1864 answer, the defensible foundation is rest during the febrile illness, attentive nursing, adequate drinks and food, comfortable support of painful <anatomical joints>, and relief of <pain>. The physician would follow the <temperature>, <pulse>, breathing, ability to drink and <urine> output. Examination with a <stethoscope> should be repeated for a new <heart murmur>, a <pericardial friction rub> or signs of <heart failure>; improvement of the <arthritis> does not prove that the <rheumatic carditis> has ended. Historical alkaline treatment with <potassium bicarbonate> or other alkalis was intended to counter a supposed acid state. <Opium> was used for severe <pain> and sleeplessness, with risks of <constipation> and <respiratory depression>. Neither that acid theory nor abolition of <pain> establishes prevention of damage to the <heart valves>. Contemporary discussion already disputed whether the apparent benefit of alkalis exceeded that of rest and nourishment, and recognised the difficulty of comparing unlike cases.<Treatment of acute rheumatism in the Edinburgh Medical Journal (1862)>[Practice of Medicine, pp. 179–183: alkaline treatment and discussion of case comparisons]{ref}

Current assessment includes an <electrocardiogram> and <echocardiography>, since <valvulitis> may be present without an obvious <heart murmur>. A suitable <penicillin> regimen eradicates residual <group A streptococcal infection>; subsequent <secondary antibiotic prophylaxis> reduces further attacks. An <anti-inflammatory drug> such as <naproxen> or <ibuprofen> treats the established inflammatory symptoms, with choice and monitoring appropriate to age and illness. These measures should not be described as reversing an already scarred <heart valve>. <Heart failure> from severe <rheumatic carditis> needs specialist management; severe <Sydenham's chorea> may require specific treatment as well as protection, rest and support. Activity is adjusted to the clinical and cardiac findings rather than prescribed as indefinite immobility. <Bloodletting>, deliberately induced <dehydration> and historical <counterirritation> provide no substitute for these measures.<Australian guideline for acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease (2025)>[Chapter 7, especially Table 7.1: acute manifestations, carditis, chorea and continuing care]{ref}

The treatment outline therefore has two time scales: control the present inflammatory illness and its complications, then prevent another immune-mediated attack. Modern <salicylate> treatment should not be projected backwards as the settled Oxford practice of December 1864.

**Original question.**

How would you treat a case of Pneumonia supervening upon Delirium Tremens, and what would probably be the result of your treatment ?

=== Solution

**This combination warrants a grave prognosis and closely supervised treatment of both the <pneumonia> and the <delirium tremens>; recovery cannot be promised or ruled out from the diagnosis alone.** The immediate conflict is that the agitation and sleeplessness of <alcohol withdrawal> call for effective control, while <pneumonia> reduces respiratory reserve. Excessive sedation can then worsen <hypoventilation> and <aspiration>, whereas uncontrolled <delirium tremens> can itself be life-threatening.

An historically reasoned 1864 approach would emphasise continuous nursing, a quiet environment, nourishment and fluids that the person could safely swallow, and repeated examination of the <thorax> and <circulation>. Suppressing a cough or inducing sleep would not demonstrate resolution of the <pneumonia>. The traditional strongly depleting approach to inflammation, including <bloodletting> and large doses of <tartar emetic>, would be especially hazardous in an exhausted person. <Opium> and alcoholic stimulation were debated contemporary remedies; escalating <opium> simply because sleep had not occurred could deepen <respiratory depression>. Period discussion of <delirium tremens> explicitly recognised that treatment response varied and that intercurrent disease altered the outlook.<Practical Notes on Diagnosis, Prognosis, and Treatment in Cases of Delirium Tremens by Thomas Laycock>[Medicinal-treatment discussion, pp. 411–413, and discussion of complications and prognosis]{ref} These are historical treatment controversies, not present instructions to administer alcohol or <opium>.

Modern management is in hospital, with an appropriate level of observation and early critical-care assessment if breathing or <consciousness> deteriorates. Assess the <airway>, <respiratory rate>, <oxygen saturation>, <blood pressure>, <temperature>, <blood glucose> and <electrolytes>. Establish the severity and likely cause of the <pneumonia>, including possible <aspiration>; a <chest radiograph> and microbiological samples are used as indicated. Give timely <antibiotics> selected for the setting, severity, likely organisms and local resistance patterns. Provide <oxygen therapy> or more advanced respiratory support when indicated, while reassessing the response.<Pneumonia: diagnosis and management by NICE>[NG250, §§1.4–1.7 and 1.9: investigations, antibiotic treatment and respiratory support]{ref}

Treat the <delirium tremens> using an appropriate <benzodiazepine> regimen with frequent reassessment; the respiratory illness makes skilled monitoring particularly important, rather than making untreated withdrawal acceptable. Provide <thiamine>, with the parenteral route when indicated by malnutrition or suspected <Wernicke encephalopathy>, and correct relevant deficiencies. <Hypoglycaemia> requires prompt treatment. <Antipsychotics> are not a replacement for treatment of the underlying <alcohol withdrawal>.<Alcohol-use disorders: diagnosis and management of physical complications by NICE>[CG100, §§1.1.4 and 1.2: delirium tremens and thiamine]{ref}

Historically the absence of effective <antibiotics> made the additional <pneumonia> particularly ominous. Today the outlook depends on respiratory failure, <sepsis>, coexisting organ disease and treatment response. No severity findings, age or organ-function results are supplied, so neither a mortality percentage nor an inevitable fatal outcome follows. A poor result would also not, by itself, prove that a particular historical remedy caused or prevented death.

**Original question.**

What steps are required by the law of England to be taken before a person of unsound mind can be put under bodily restraint ?

=== Solution

**For ordinary private asylum reception in England in 1864, the central safeguards were a proper reception order and two independent medical certificates.** The legal question concerns deprivation of liberty under the contemporary lunacy legislation. It must also be distinguished from the separate justification for applying mechanical <physical restraint> to a person already in care. The relevant framework is the <Lunacy Act 1845>, amended by the two 1853 Acts and the <Lunacy Acts Amendment Act 1862>, rather than later Victorian legislation.

For a private patient entering a licensed house or registered hospital, obtain the prescribed order and accompanying particulars, normally signed by a relative or other responsible person, and two certificates from qualified, independent medical practitioners. Each practitioner must examine the person separately within seven clear days before reception. Each certificate distinguishes personally observed facts from information supplied by others, and certifies both unsoundness of mind and suitability for detention for care and treatment. Financial and professional conflicts disqualify specified signatories. Comparable documentary safeguards cover a paid single patient in an unlicensed house. Exceptionally, special circumstances stated with the order permit initial reception on one certificate; two further independent certificates, from two other practitioners, must follow within three clear days. That exception is not indefinite authority to continue confinement.<Lunatics Care and Treatment Act 1853>[16 & 17 Vict. c.96, §§4–5, 8, 10, 12–13 and Schedule A]{ref}

The 1862 amendments add important timing and conflict safeguards. The order's signer must have seen the person within the preceding month and state when and where; the order ceases to authorise reception after one calendar month. The legislation further excludes specified financially interested persons and medical attendants from signing private reception documents. A relative's name and address should be entered where possible. The required admission documents go to the Commissioners within one clear day, subject to the Act's separate timing for the subsequent medical statement. That notification follows reception and is not a certificate that must already have been returned before it.<Lunacy Acts Amendment Act 1862>[§§23–25 and 28]{ref}

There are distinct statutory routes for pauper patients, involving the appropriate justice's order and medical evidence, and for persons already found of unsound mind by inquisition. Therefore the two-certificate rule should not be asserted as the sole route for every legal category.<Lunatic Asylums Act 1853>[§67 and Schedule F: pauper reception procedure]{ref}<Lunacy Acts Amendment Act 1862>[§22: order of the appointed committee after inquisition]{ref}

Finally, a reception order is **not permission to tie down a patient merely because a certificate exists**. The contemporary inspection records specifically called for the occasions, means, duration and reasons for restraint and seclusion to be recorded. Immediate protection during dangerous behaviour and continued institutional detention are different questions; a medical opinion alone cannot erase that distinction.<Lunatics Care and Treatment Act 1853>[Schedule D, §25: matters for the Patients' Book, including restraint and seclusion]{ref} This describes historical <historical English asylum admission>, not the law governing present-day detention.

**Original question.**

What would be your line of treatment in a well marked case of Pyæmia ?

=== Solution

**Seek and control the infective source, drain accessible collections, support failing organs, and recognise that established <pyaemia> had a very poor outlook before effective <antibiotics>.** The historical term commonly described severe systemic illness, often after a wound or operation, with recurrent rigors and secondary <abscesses>. Modern explanations include <bacteraemia>, an infected <thrombus> producing <septic embolism>, and <metastatic infection>. It is misleading to reduce the condition to an inert quantity of <pus> freely floating through the <blood>.

In the 1864 setting, examine the original wound and any painful swollen region for retained infected material, an undrained <abscess>, dead tissue or obstructed drainage. An accessible collection calls for surgical drainage; a superficially healed wound should not conceal an undrained deeper focus. Remove obvious sources of continuing contamination, provide careful dressings, cleanliness, ventilation, nursing and tolerable nourishment, and relieve <pain>. Period practice used <quinine> and alcoholic stimulants as supportive remedies. A surgical report covering July 1863 to October 1864 records these measures in the Royal Infirmary; its publication in 1865 documents the period rather than supplying an Oxford model answer.<Royal Infirmary surgical ward report (1863–1864)>[p. 111: wound cleanliness, local applications and quinine and stimulants in pyaemia]{ref} Such measures did not reliably sterilise the <blood> or inaccessible secondary <abscesses>. Neither repeated purging nor <bloodletting> has a sound role in an already depleted, seriously infected person. Later organised surgical <antisepsis> should not be presented as an established system available at this examination.

Current care treats the presentation as possible <sepsis> with disseminated infection. Assess <airway>, breathing, <circulation>, mental state and organ function urgently. Obtain appropriate <blood cultures> and source samples without delaying necessary <antibiotic> treatment; investigate the original wound and likely secondary sites. <Ultrasonography> or other imaging can locate a collection for drainage. Persistent <bacteraemia>, embolic manifestations or a new <heart murmur> should prompt assessment for <infective endocarditis>, often including <echocardiography>. Source control may require drainage, removal of infected material or <debridement>. Initial <antibiotics> are chosen for the likely source and resistance risks, then narrowed using microbiological evidence.

Provide <oxygen therapy> when needed and carefully reassessed <intravenous fluids>; persistent circulatory failure requires critical-care treatment rather than indiscriminate fluid loading. Monitor <urine> output, <renal function>, <blood pressure> and response to treatment. The modern prognosis depends on source, organ injury and speed and adequacy of treatment, so the historical description alone does not support a numerical prediction.<Suspected sepsis in people aged 16 or over by NICE>[NG253, recognition, source investigation and antibiotic, intravenous-fluid and oxygen recommendations]{ref}

**Original question.**

Bearing in mind the several causes of Uterine Hæmorrhage, what ought to be the treatment of the various forms of this affection ?

=== Solution

**Resuscitate significant <haemorrhage> while identifying its cause; pregnancy, timing in relation to birth, and the condition of the <uterus> determine the definitive treatment.** A vaginal loss need not originate in the <uterus>, and the visible volume may underestimate concealed bleeding. Assess <consciousness>, <pulse>, <blood pressure> and ongoing loss; serious bleeding calls for urgent obstetric or gynaecological help, <intravenous access>, blood sampling and access to <blood transfusion>. Investigations must not delay control of a life-threatening source.

Outside pregnancy, distinguish excessive menstrual bleeding from irregular, postcoital or postmenopausal bleeding. Causes include <uterine fibroids>, <endometrial polyps>, disturbed <ovulation>, <coagulopathy> and <cancer>. A <speculum> examination may reveal a cervical or vaginal source. History, examination, <blood count>, and appropriately selected <ultrasonography>, <hysteroscopy> or tissue sampling establish the cause. Current treatment for <heavy menstrual bleeding> may use <tranexamic acid> or hormonal treatment where suitable; removal of a <polyp> or treatment of a <uterine fibroid> addresses a structural cause. Suspected <cancer> needs a diagnostic and specialist pathway, rather than repeated empirical suppression of bleeding.<Heavy menstrual bleeding by NICE>[NG88, investigation according to history and examination, and cause-directed treatment]{ref}

In early pregnancy, consider threatened or established <miscarriage> and retained pregnancy tissue. Establish the location and viability of the pregnancy with <ultrasonography> and, where appropriate, serial <human chorionic gonadotropin> measurements. Stable cases permit a choice among expectant, medical or surgical management according to the diagnosis and circumstances; heavy ongoing bleeding or instability changes the urgency. An <ectopic pregnancy> may cause vaginal bleeding together with dangerous internal <haemorrhage>, even though the bleeding pregnancy is outside the <uterus>. Pain, collapse or instability therefore demands urgent assessment rather than an assumption that all bleeding represents a harmless menstrual or uterine event.<Ectopic pregnancy and miscarriage by NICE>[NG126, §§1.3–1.7: emergency assessment, diagnosis and management]{ref}

Later pregnancy requires distinction between <placenta praevia>, <placental abruption> and other genital-tract sources. <Placenta praevia> may cause heavy painless bleeding; <placental abruption> may involve pain, a tense <uterus> and concealed loss. Stabilise the mother, assess the fetus and locate the <placenta> by <ultrasonography>. Avoid a digital examination that could disturb a covering <placenta> before <placenta praevia> has been excluded. The timing and route of birth depend on severity, placental position, gestation and maternal and fetal condition. A <placenta> covering the cervical opening usually requires <Caesarean section>; the diagnosis is not an instruction to apply a vaginal plug and wait through major continuing loss.<Placenta praevia, placenta accreta and vasa praevia by RCOG>[Bleeding, ultrasound assessment and planning birth]{ref}<Antepartum haemorrhage by RCOG>[Green-top Guideline 63, §7.3: digital examination precaution]{ref}

During and after birth, organise <postpartum haemorrhage> causes as poor <uterine contraction>, retained tissue, genital-tract trauma and defective clotting. A <uterine rupture> is a surgical emergency; a contracting <uterus> does not exclude a traumatic source. For <uterine atony>, uterine massage and a suitable <uterotonic> accompany resuscitation; emptying a distended <urinary bladder> may help contraction. Examine for tears and repair them; deal with retained <placental tissue> with skilled obstetric care, and do not forcibly detach an abnormally adherent <placenta>. Correct relevant <coagulopathy> and use <blood products> as indicated. Current early treatment includes <tranexamic acid> within the recommended interval. Continuing bleeding may require balloon tamponade or surgery; a <uterine inversion> requires urgent skilled replacement and resuscitation, not treatment as simple atony. Delayed bleeding after birth suggests retained tissue or infection and requires renewed assessment.<Consolidated guidelines for the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of postpartum haemorrhage by the World Health Organization (2025)>[First-response treatment bundle and cause-directed management of refractory bleeding]{ref}

An 1864 practitioner could recognise miscarriage, abnormal placental attachment or separation, retained <placenta>, atony and traumatic bleeding, and could attempt pressure, manual measures and delivery appropriate to the cause. Cold applications and vaginal plugging appeared in period practice, but were not interchangeable cures. Contemporary obstetric discussion already distinguished several mechanisms of bleeding.<Quarterly Report on Midwifery (April 1864)>[pp. 549–556: uterine disease, pregnancy and obstetric complications]{ref} In particular, a <uterotonic> cannot repair a tear, and obstructing the outflow does not establish that internal <haemorrhage> has ceased. The modern diagnostic and resuscitative measures above explain why treatment must follow the mechanism rather than the symptom alone.

**Original question.**

What steps would you take if a woman previously healthy, were seized with convulsions during labour ?

=== Solution

**Treat new convulsions during labour as possible <eclampsia>: protect breathing, terminate and prevent seizures, control severe <hypertension>, and plan birth after stabilisation.** Previous good health does not exclude <pre-eclampsia> or <eclampsia>. Also consider <epilepsy>, <hypoglycaemia>, intracranial disease and other causes if the clinical findings suggest them; neither an invented history nor a fabricated <urine> result is needed to recognise the emergency.

Summon obstetric, anaesthetic and neonatal assistance. Protect the woman from injury, position her safely on her side when feasible, maintain the <airway>, and assess breathing and <oxygen saturation>. Do not force an object into her mouth or attempt to suppress a fit by forcefully holding down her limbs. Obtain <intravenous access>, check <blood glucose>, and measure <blood pressure>; assess <platelets>, <renal function>, liver tests and <proteinuria> without delaying essential treatment. Review the fetal condition once maternal resuscitation is under way.

Current first-line anticonvulsant treatment is <magnesium sulfate>. NICE describes an intravenous loading dose of 4 g over 5–15 minutes, followed by 1 g per hour, with continued treatment for 24 hours after the last fit. Recurrent fits require additional treatment under the emergency protocol. Monitor breathing, tendon reflexes and <urine> output because impaired <renal function> increases the risk of <magnesium> accumulation. A <benzodiazepine> is not an interchangeable routine substitute for <magnesium sulfate> in this setting. Severe <hypertension> requires prompt suitable treatment, for example <labetalol>, <nifedipine> or <hydralazine>, with continuing reassessment.<Hypertension in pregnancy by NICE>[NG133, §§1.8.1–1.8.6: anticonvulsants and immediate treatment of severe hypertension]{ref}

Once stabilised, the woman needs a plan for birth. Cervical findings, progress of labour, gestation and maternal and fetal condition determine whether vaginal birth or <Caesarean section> is appropriate. Performing an immediate instrumental delivery during an uncontrolled fit is not a general rule, nor does the occurrence of one fit automatically establish the route of birth. Observation and treatment continue after delivery because further fits in <eclampsia> can occur.<Hypertension in pregnancy by NICE>[§1.8.13: mode of birth according to clinical circumstances; §1.8.4: magnesium regimen after a fit]{ref}

Historically, <bloodletting>, purgation, <chloroform> and expedited delivery were advocated in different combinations. An actual 1864 case report records such treatment and continuing convulsions despite delivery. It illustrates the historical problem, not proof that removal of blood or pressure on pelvic <nerves> explains or cures <eclampsia>.<Report of a Case of Puerperal Convulsions (1864)>[pp. 411–413: bloodletting, chloroform, delivery and subsequent course]{ref} Modern <eclampsia> care replaces those obsolete theories with specific seizure treatment, blood-pressure control and maternal resuscitation.

**Original question.**

What measures would you adopt in treating a case of Spasmodic Stricture of the Urethra?

=== Solution

**Relieve <urinary retention> promptly and gently, while distinguishing transient <spasm> from a fixed <urethral stricture> or another obstruction.** The historical label <spasmodic urethral stricture> describes a functional component of obstruction; it does not prove that the <urethra> contains a permanent fibrotic narrowing.

Ask about the onset, previous attacks, instrumentation, injury, discharge, medications and previous difficulty passing <urine>. Examine for a distended painful <urinary bladder>, infection and trauma. Current <bladder ultrasonography> can confirm retention and estimate distension without repeatedly attempting instrumentation. A history of easy passage at other times may support a transient component, but neither that history nor failure to pass a catheter excludes an underlying anatomical lesion.

For the 1864 therapeutic framework, reassurance, warmth, a warm bath and relief of <pain> were intended to relax muscular <spasm>. An earlier clinical account documents <opium> and the warm bath, while warning that the bath was unreliable.<Diseases of the Urinary Organs (1842)>[Discussion of acute retention and spasmodic obstruction]{ref} Period use of <opium> belongs to that historical account; it must not be assumed to be harmless, since <opioids> can themselves contribute to <urinary retention> and sedation. Such measures should not postpone drainage of a persistently overfilled <urinary bladder>. A lubricated catheter, handled by a skilled operator, should be advanced gently rather than forced through resistance. Producing bleeding or a false passage can convert an initially functional problem into substantial injury.

Current guidance calls for immediate catheterisation in acute <urinary retention>, with attention to contraindications and the cause. Suspected <urethral trauma>, major resistance or repeated failure calls for specialist help, not escalating force. Depending on the findings, guided urethral catheterisation or <suprapubic catheterisation> may be appropriate. The latter requires a suitably localised distended <urinary bladder> and assessment of contraindications; it is not an instruction for blind puncture.<Lower urinary tract symptoms in men by NICE>[CG97, §1.7.1: immediate catheterisation for acute retention]{ref}<Indwelling catheterisation in adults by EAUN (2024)>[§§4.3–4.6: urethral risks and suprapubic indications and contraindications]{ref}

After decompression, address the precipitant: infection, painful local inflammation, medication effects, a fixed <urethral stricture> or another outlet disorder. Recurrent attacks merit investigation rather than repeated labelling as <spasm>. An incision or a course of repeated forceful dilatation is not justified merely by the word spasmodic. <Diseases of the Urinary Organs (1842)>[Discussion of retention: opium and the warm bath, with contemporary reservations about the bath]{ref} The principle common to the historical and modern reasoning is to remove the obstruction with the least injury; modern assessment makes the anatomical and infective distinctions more reliable.

**Original question.**

What are the indications for the use of, and contra-indications against the use of, Elaterium, Digitalis, Alo\"es, and Copaiba, severally in various diseases ?

=== Solution

The four agents have different proposed purposes. **Their indications must be tied to a mechanism and a diagnosis, and their historical use must not be converted into a general modern recommendation.** In particular, a historical effect on a discharge, pulse or bowel movement is not proof of cure of the disease causing it.

<Elaterium> was a drastic hydragogue <purgative>, the dried deposit from the juice of squirting cucumber fruit. The intended use was to remove fluid through the <intestine> in severe <dropsy>, particularly some cardiac cases. The same action explains the danger: violent <diarrhoea>, abdominal irritation, <dehydration> and collapse. Frailty, existing fluid depletion, inflammatory disease of the <intestine>, suspected <bowel obstruction> and pregnancy make such treatment particularly objectionable. Variable potency further undermines a fixed-dose rule.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Cucurbitaceae, Elaterium: preparation, hydragogue action, dropsy and irritant effects]{ref} In current care, identify the cause of <oedema>; cardiac fluid overload may require monitored <diuretic> treatment, not deliberate drastic purging. <Elaterium> is not a substitute for a modern <diuretic>.

<Digitalis preparations>, historically obtained from foxglove leaves, were used to slow and strengthen cardiac action and promote <diuresis> in selected forms of cardiac <dropsy>. An appropriate indication therefore requires assessment of the <heart> and <circulation>, not the mere presence of swelling. Modern <digoxin> is a standardised <cardiac glycoside>, used in selected <heart failure> and <atrial fibrillation>; it is not equivalent to an arbitrary amount of crude leaf. Important contraindications include certain <atrioventricular blocks>, <ventricular tachycardia>, <ventricular fibrillation> and particular accessory-pathway arrhythmias. <Renal impairment>, <hypokalaemia> and interacting medicines increase the risk of toxicity and demand attention to dosing and monitoring. A very slow <pulse>, new <cardiac arrhythmia>, nausea or visual disturbance during treatment calls for assessment, not additional doses to obtain more <diuresis>.<Digoxin tablets summary of product characteristics (emc)>[§§4.1–4.5: indications, conduction disease, arrhythmias, renal function, electrolytes and interactions]{ref}

<Aloes> means the dried aloe leaf juice used as a stimulant <laxative>, not the relatively different leaf gel. Historically it was used for sluggish bowel action and sometimes as an <emmenagogue>, on theories of pelvic stimulation. The latter is not a present treatment for an unexplained absent period. The recognised current herbal indication is short-term occasional <constipation>, with limits on duration and age. Contraindications include <bowel obstruction>, inflammatory bowel disease, unexplained abdominal <pain>, severe <dehydration>, pregnancy and breastfeeding. Repeated purgation can disturb water and <electrolyte> balance; constipation caused by a serious obstruction should not be treated by progressively stronger <laxatives>.<Aloes folii succus siccatus by the European Medicines Agency>[Public summary: dried leaf juice, short-term use and contraindications]{ref}

<Copaiba> is an <oleoresin>, historically given for <gonorrhoea> and persistent urethral discharge, and sometimes other mucosal complaints. Period selection tended to favour a less acutely inflamed stage; irritation of the <stomach> and urinary tract, nausea, <diarrhoea> or a rash argued against continuing it. The contemporary monograph also warns against its use in febrile states. Existing marked urinary irritation is poor ground for an irritant empirical remedy.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Cisalpinae, Copaiba: balsam and oil, urinary uses and unwanted effects]{ref} Modern <gonorrhoea> requires appropriate microbiological diagnosis, effective <antibiotic> treatment and partner management. Current CDC guidance uses <ceftriaxone>-based treatment; <copaiba> is not an alternative to eradication of the infection. Apparent reduction of discharge cannot establish microbiological cure.<Gonococcal infections among adolescents and adults by CDC>[Diagnostic considerations, recommended regimens and management of partners]{ref}

Thus the period shorthand is fluid removal by <elaterium>, selected cardiac treatment by <digitalis preparations>, bowel stimulation by <aloes>, and a disputed treatment of mucosal discharge by <copaiba>. Present interpretation preserves the useful distinction between actions while rejecting the assumption that all four historical indications remain valid.

**Original question.**

What are poisonous doses of the officinal preparations of Opium respectively? How do you treat deep narcotism from that drug ?

=== Solution

**There is no single reliably poisonous dose for each <opium> preparation. Identify the preparation and exposure, but diagnose and treat poisoning from its effects, especially depressed breathing.** Toxicity depends on the actual alkaloid content, route, age, <opioid tolerance>, coexisting illness and other depressants. A quantity tolerated by one person may produce dangerous <respiratory depression> in another. In particular, neither a historical ordinary dose nor a published fatal quantity defines a boundary below which every person is safe.<Opioid overdose by WHO>[Risk factors and the characteristic combination of impaired consciousness, depressed breathing and pinpoint pupils]{ref}

For the historical numerical part, separate formulation strength from a poisonous threshold. The relevant British formulations are those associated with the <British Pharmacopoeia by the General Medical Council (1864)>, not a later edition or the bracketed American recipes in <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>. In the following comparisons, a <grain> is a mass unit and a <minim> a volume unit; a fluid ounce is 480 <minims>. These comparisons describe period preparations and are not administration instructions.

* <Opium tincture> and the 1864 <Vinum Opii> are each described as containing the soluble constituents of one grain of dry <opium> in approximately 14½ minims. <Tinctura Camphorae cum Opio> contains one grain in half a fluid ounce, or 240 minims. Confusing these liquids therefore creates a large error in exposure.
* <Extractum Opii> is an aqueous extract with potency dependent on extraction and starting material. The contemporary comparison gives about half a grain of dry extract as equivalent to one grain of raw <opium>. <Extractum Opii Liquidum> contains about one grain of extract in 22 minims. This is an approximate raw-drug equivalence, not an assay of <morphine>.
* <Pilula Opii> contains one part <opium> in five parts pill mass; <Pilula Plumbi cum Opio> one in eight. The latter also carries the independent hazard of a <lead> compound.
* <Pulvis Ipecacuanhae cum Opio>, <Pulvis Kino cum Opio> and <Pulvis Cretae Aromaticus cum Opio> contain one part <opium> in ten, twenty and forty parts respectively. Other ingredients do not abolish <opioid> toxicity.

These strengths make clear how to compare exposures once their quantities are known. For example, if a preparation contains a mass fraction $f$ of dry <opium> and a mass $m$ of it was taken, its nominal raw-drug content is
$$
\boxed{m_{\mathrm{opium}}=fm.}
$$
That equation cannot supply an unknown toxic threshold or account for all differences in absorption.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Posological Table, final Opium comparison: British strengths and approximate extract equivalence]{ref}

Two entries require explicit textual caution. The monograph for <Trochisci Opii> specifies 72 grains of <Extractum Opii> divided into 720 lozenges, hence **one tenth of a grain of extract per lozenge**; its summary table instead says one tenth of a grain of <opium>. Those are not interchangeable descriptions. Similarly, the <Enema Opii> recipe specifies 30 minims of <opium tincture> with two fluid ounces of starch decoction, whereas the summary table describes one grain of <opium> in two fluid ounces. At the stated tincture strength the recipe contributes about two grains to the mixture, so the discrepancy should not be silently turned into a precise rectal poisonous dose.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, Parts 2–3: Enema Opii recipe and Trochisci Opii recipe; compare Posological Table]{ref}

The external preparations also belong to the officinal list: <Emplastrum Opii>, <Linimentum Opii> and <Unguentum Gallae cum Opio>. The plaster contains one part powdered <opium> to nine parts resin plaster; the liniment mixes equal volumes of <opium tincture> and soap liniment. The galls ointment is described as approximately one part <opium> in 14½ parts of ointment. These are not oral medicines. Exposure over damaged skin, contact with mucosa or accidental swallowing cannot be evaluated by assigning a universal number of spoonfuls or applications.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, Part 2: plaster and liniment; Part 3: galls and opium ointment]{ref}

If preparations derived from the purified alkaloid are included, distinguish them from raw <opium>: <Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis> contains four grains of <morphine hydrochloride> per fluid ounce; <Suppositoria Morphiae> contain a quarter grain each; <Trochisci Morphiae> and <Trochisci Morphiae et Ipecacuanhae> contain one thirty-sixth of a grain of the salt per lozenge. A raw-drug fraction is not the same thing as a mass of purified <morphine hydrochloride>.<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, Part 3, Of Hydrochlorate of Morphia: solution, suppositories and the two lozenges]{ref}

Deep historical narcotism means profound <opioid poisoning>, with depressed <consciousness> and dangerously slow or ineffective breathing, often with small <pupils>. Current priorities are emergency help, an open <airway>, assisted ventilation and <oxygen therapy> as required, and prompt <naloxone>. If there is <cardiac arrest>, start appropriate <cardiopulmonary resuscitation>. Repeat <naloxone> or use an infusion when clinically required, and continue observation because the <opioid> may outlast the reversal. A transient awakening is not proof of sustained recovery.<Opioid overdose by WHO>[Emergency responses: basic life support and timely naloxone]{ref}<Responding to a Suspected Opioid Overdose by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[Emergency response, naloxone and continuing assistance]{ref}

Older attempts to empty the <stomach> or to rouse an unconscious person must not displace respiratory support. Do not give oral drinks or an <emetic> to an unconscious person, and do not perform unprotected gastric instrumentation: <aspiration> can add a second cause of respiratory failure. The concise therapeutic answer is therefore **support ventilation and reverse the <opioid> effect, rather than waiting for a calculated poisonous dose to be exceeded**.
