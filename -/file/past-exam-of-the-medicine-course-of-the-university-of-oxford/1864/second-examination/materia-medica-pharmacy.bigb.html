**Original question.**

What officinal preparations of the British Pharmacopœia contain Opium, and in what proportions ?

=== Solution

**The 1864 preparations contain either crude <opium> or its aqueous <Extractum Opii>; those quantities must be distinguished from a quantity of isolated <morphine>.** The following are the officinal <opium> preparations. Ratios for solids mean the <mass fraction> in the compounded preparation; liquid strengths specify <mass> per <volume> or the actual ingredients used.

* <Emplastrum Opii>: powdered <opium> in resin plaster: **1 in 10**.
* <Enema Opii>: <opium tincture> 30 <minims> with starch <decoction> 2 <fluid ounces>. Thus the tincture is **1 volume to 32 volumes of vehicle**, before mixing.
* <Extractum Opii>: aqueous <solvent extraction> of 1 <avoirdupois pound> of sliced <opium> with 6 <Imperial pints> of <distilled water>, in three successive portions, followed by <evaporation>. The prescribed starting quantities do not impose a fixed yield or a universal crude-drug-to-extract potency ratio.
* <Extractum Opii Liquidum>: <Extractum Opii> 1 <avoirdupois ounce>, <distilled water> 17 <fluid ounces>, <rectified spirit> 3 <fluid ounces>: **1 ounce of extract to the pint of liquid ingredients**.
* <Linimentum Opii>: equal <volumes> of <opium tincture> and <soap liniment>: **half the tincture's nominal <opium> strength**.
* <Pilula Opii>: powdered <opium> with hard <soap>: **1 in 5** of the nominal pill ingredients.
* <Pilula Plumbi cum Opio>: powdered <opium> with <lead acetate> and confection of roses: **1 in 8**.
* <Pulvis Cretae Aromaticus cum Opio>: powdered <opium> with aromatic chalk powder: **1 in 40**.
* <Pulvis Ipecacuanhae cum Opio>: powdered <opium> with <ipecacuanha> and <potassium sulfate>: **1 in 10**.
* <Pulvis Kino cum Opio>: powdered <opium> with <kino> and <cinnamon>: **1 in 20**.

<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, part 2, British formulas outside the bracketed American additions; underlying revision dated May 1864]{ref}

* <Tinctura Camphorae cum Opio>: <opium> 40 <grains> to 1 <Imperial pint> of <proof spirit>, with <benzoic acid>, <camphor> and <anise oil>: **2 grains of <opium> per <fluid ounce>**.
* <Opium tincture>, *Tinctura Opii*: powdered <opium> 1½ <avoirdupois ounces> to 1 <Imperial pint> of <proof spirit>. Nominally this is **32.8125 grains per fluid ounce**, or approximately 1 <grain> per 14.63 <minims>.
* <Trochisci Opii>: 72 <grains> of <Extractum Opii> in 720 lozenges: **one tenth of a grain of extract per lozenge**.
* <Unguentum Gallae cum Opio>: powdered <opium> 32 <grains> added to 1 <avoirdupois ounce> of gall ointment. The actual nominal total is $437.5+32=469.5$ <grains>, giving **32/469.5 by mass, approximately 1 in 14.67**; “32 grains to the ounce of ointment taken” is not 32 <grains> per final ounce.
* <Vinum Opii>: powdered <opium> 1½ <avoirdupois ounces>, <sherry> 1 <Imperial pint>: **the tincture's nominal <opium> proportion**, in a different solvent. This formula uses crude <opium>, not <Extractum Opii>.

<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, part 3, British tincture, lozenge, ointment and wine formulas]{ref}

The arithmetic uses the new <avoirdupois ounce> of 437.5 <grains>, not the old apothecaries' ounce of 480 <grains>. These are historical formulation proportions, not modern assays of <morphine> delivered after <solvent extraction>. <Confectio Opii> was omitted in 1864, and this edition's <Vinum Opii> contains no spices. The separate <morphine hydrochloride> preparations—<Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis>, <Suppositoria Morphiae>, <Trochisci Morphiae>, and <Trochisci Morphiae et Ipecacuanhae>—contain an isolated <alkaloid> salt rather than crude <opium>. <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[pp. 11–12, 39 and 75–76]{ref}

**Original question.**

Mention the chief Diuretics used in England, and give the doses of their several preparations.

=== Solution

**The principal period groups were <digitalis preparations>, <squill>, <broom>, <juniper oil>, <buchu>, <nitrous ether spirit>, and several soluble <potassium salts>.** They were grouped by observed promotion of <urine> flow, rather than by one common mechanism. The following are historical adult quantities per administration, expressed in the contemporary units; they are not present-day prescriptions.

* <Digitalis preparations>: powdered leaves, ½–2 <grains>; <Infusum Digitalis>, 2–4 <fluid drachms>; <Tinctura Digitalis>, 5–40 <minims>; <digitalin>, 1/40–1/10 <grain> in the period table. Historical <digitalin> was not simply a modern pure <digoxin> product.
* <Squill>: powder, 1–2 <grains>; <Pilula Scillae Composita>, 5–10 <grains>; <Tinctura Scillae>, 10–20 <minims>; <Syrupus Scillae>, ½–1½ <fluid drachms>.
* <Broom>: <Decoctum Scoparii>, 1–3 <fluid ounces>; <Succus Scoparii>, 1–4 <fluid drachms>.
* <Buchu>: powdered leaves, 20–40 <grains>; <Infusum Bucco>, 1–2 <fluid ounces>; <Tinctura Bucco>, 1–2 <fluid drachms>.
* <Juniper oil>: <Spiritus Juniperi>, 10–30 <minims> in the 1864 commentary. This unusually concentrated spirit contains one volume of <juniper oil> in ten; its dose must not be borrowed from a later, weaker formula.
* <Nitrous ether spirit>, *Spiritus Aetheris Nitrosi*: 30 <minims> to 2 <fluid drachms>.
* <Potassium acetate>: 10–20 <grains>; <potassium citrate>: 20–60 <grains>; <potassium nitrate>: 5–20 <grains>; <potassium tartrate>: 10–60 <grains>; <potassium hydrogen tartrate>: 20–60 <grains>.
* <Ammonium acetate solution>, *Liquor Ammoniae Acetatis*: 10–40 <minims> of the concentrated British preparation, diluted for administration.

<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Posological table: Digitalis, Infusum Digitalis, Scoparius preparations, Bucco preparations, Spiritus Aetheris Nitrosi, potassium salts and Liquor Ammoniae Acetatis; Liliaceae: squill preparations]{ref} <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[p. 61, Spiritus Juniperi, 1 in 10 and 10–30 minims]{ref}

Other recognised period agents included <dandelion> and <copaiba>. The <dandelion> <decoction> was given in quantities of 1–2 <fluid ounces>, its soft extract in quantities of 10–30 <grains>, and its preserved <juice> in quantities of ½–2 <fluid drachms>. <Copaiba> was given in quantities of 15–30 <minims>, and <copaiba oil> in quantities of 5–20 <minims>. Their historical inclusion does not establish that they are suitable substitutes for current <diuretic therapy>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Posological table, Taraxacum preparations, Copaiba and Copaibae Oleum]{ref}

The distinction between preparations matters more than a single “dose of the plant”: an <infusion>, <tincture>, <juice> and powder have different <concentrations>. Likewise, a salt's small historical <diuretic> dose is not its larger <purgative> dose. A modern explanation also separates a direct effect on <renal tubules> from improved <cardiac output> in appropriate <heart failure>: <digitalis preparations> cannot be understood simply as agents that make every <kidney> excrete more <water>. Modern <digoxin> is used for selected cardiac indications, rather than as a general treatment for low <urine> output. <Digoxin by MedlinePlus>[Why is this medication prescribed?]{ref}

**Original question.**

Enumerate the “mixtures” for which directions are given in the British Pharmacopœia and state their composition.

=== Solution

**The seven named <mixtures> of the 1864 <British Pharmacopoeia> are <Mistura Ammoniaci>, <Mistura Amygdalae>, <Mistura Creasoti>, <Mistura Cretae>, <Mistura Ferri Composita>, <Mistura Guaiaci> and <Mistura Scammonii>.** Their compositions are as follows; <avoirdupois ounces> are used for solids and <fluid ounces> for liquids.

* <Mistura Ammoniaci>: <ammoniacum> ¼ <avoirdupois ounce>, <distilled water> 8 <fluid ounces>, rubbed together and strained. Its own <gum> helps disperse its <resin>; <ammoniacum> is not <ammonia>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Umbelliferae, part 2, Ammoniacum]{ref}
* <Mistura Amygdalae>: compound almond powder 2½ <avoirdupois ounces>, <distilled water> 1 <Imperial pint>. The compound powder is made from sweet <almonds> 8 parts, <sugar> 4 parts and <gum arabic> 1 part by <mass>. Trituration produces an <emulsion>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Rosaceae, continued, almond preparations]{ref}
* <Mistura Creasoti>: <creosote> 16 <minims>, <glacial acetic acid> 16 <minims>, <Spiritus Juniperi> ½ <fluid drachm>, <syrup> 1 <fluid ounce>, <distilled water> 15 <fluid ounces>. Here the spirit has the particularly strong 1864 <juniper oil> content. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Hydrocarbons from the destruction of wood by fire, Mistura Creosoti]{ref}
* <Mistura Cretae>: prepared <chalk> and powdered <gum arabic>, ¼ <avoirdupois ounce> each; <syrup> ½ <fluid ounce>; <cinnamon water> 7½ <fluid ounces>. The <chalk> is suspended, not dissolved. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Calcis Carbonas Praecipitata, Creta Praeparata, British mixture]{ref}
* <Mistura Ferri Composita>: powdered <myrrh> 60 <grains>, <potassium carbonate> 25 <grains>, <rose water> 8 <fluid ounces>, <ferrous sulfate> 30 <grains>, <Spiritus Myristicae> 1 <fluid drachm>, <sugar> 60 <grains>. Triturate the <myrrh> with the spirit and <potassium carbonate>, then add the <rose water> with <sugar>, followed by <ferrous sulfate>; keep the resulting preparation stoppered. Formation of <ferrous carbonate> and its subsequent <oxidation> explain why the order and storage matter. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Ferri Carbonas Saccharata, British Mistura Ferri Composita formula]{ref}
* <Mistura Guaiaci>: powdered <guaiacum resin> ½ <avoirdupois ounce>, <sugar> ½ <avoirdupois ounce>, powdered <gum arabic> ¼ <avoirdupois ounce>, <cinnamon water> 1 <Imperial pint>. The <gum> assists dispersion of the poorly water-soluble <resin>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Zygophyllaceae, resin preparations]{ref}
* <Mistura Scammonii>: <scammony resin> 4 <grains> with unskimmed <milk> 2 <fluid ounces>, triturated into a uniform dispersion. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Convolvulaceae, Scammonium, Mistura Scammonii]{ref}

The category is an official preparation name, not every possible bedside combination of medicines. Camphor water and acacia <mucilage> are not additional entries under *Mistura*: the 1864 names are *Aqua Camphorae* and *Mucilago Acaciae*. <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[pp. 30 and 52–53, mixture names and alterations]{ref}

**Original question.**

What is the composition of the Pulvis Jalapæ compositus, of the Pilula Scillæ composita, of the Liquor Morphiæ hydrochloratis, and of the Liquor Strychniæ of the British Pharmacopœia ?

=== Solution

**The compositions must be specified ingredient by ingredient; the two alkaloidal solutions contain 4 grains per nominal fluid ounce, but one contains a <morphine> salt and the other <strychnine>.**

<Pulvis Jalapae Compositus> contains powdered <jalap> 5 <avoirdupois ounces>, <potassium hydrogen tartrate> 9 <avoirdupois ounces>, and powdered <ginger> 1 <avoirdupois ounce>. Thus the <mass> proportions are **5:9:1**, and <jalap> supplies one third of the powder's <mass>. This is a compound <purgative>, not pure <jalap resin>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Convolvulaceae, Jalapa, compound powder]{ref}

<Pilula Scillae Composita> contains powdered <squill> 1¼ <avoirdupois ounces>, powdered <ginger>, <ammoniacum> and hard <soap> 1 <avoirdupois ounce> each, with <treacle> 2 <avoirdupois ounces>, or sufficient to make a pill mass. Using the specified 2 <avoirdupois ounces> of <treacle>, the whole is 6¼ <avoirdupois ounces> and contains **one fifth <squill> by mass**. The allowance of sufficient <treacle> makes consistency a compounding requirement, not a claim that every moist pill batch has an analytically exact 20% <squill> content. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Liliaceae, British compound squill pill, distinguished from bracketed American formula]{ref}

<Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis> contains <morphine hydrochloride> 4 <grains>, dilute <hydrochloric acid> 8 <minims>, <rectified spirit> 2 <fluid drachms> and <distilled water> 6 <fluid drachms>. Its conventional strength is **½ grain of <morphine hydrochloride> per fluid drachm**, not ½ <grain> of free <morphine>. The small quantity of <hydrochloric acid> maintains an acid solution. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Papaverceae, part 3, Liquor Morphiae Hydrochloratis]{ref}

<Liquor Strychniae> contains <strychnine> 4 <grains>, dilute <hydrochloric acid> 6 <minims>, <rectified spirit> 2 <fluid drachms> and <distilled water> 6 <fluid drachms>. Dissolve the <strychnine> in the acid mixed with part of the <water>, using the prescribed assistance of heat, then add the spirit and remaining <water>. Its conventional strength is **½ grain of <strychnine> per fluid drachm**. Acid promotes <solubility> by forming a soluble <salt> of the <alkaloid>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Loganiaceae, Liquor Strychniae]{ref}

For the last two solutions, the stated historical strengths use the nominal 8 <fluid drachms> of spirit and <water>. The extra few <minims> of dilute acid must still appear in the actual recipe; treating all ingredient <volumes> as perfectly additive would not give an exact modern analytical <concentration>.

**Original question.**

Name the medicines which impart colouring matter to the excretions.

=== Solution

**The chief historical examples are <rhubarb>, <senna>, <santonin> and <logwood> for <urine>; <iron preparations>, <bismuth preparations> and <charcoal> for darkening the <faeces>.** There are two different processes: a substance absorbed and subsequently excreted can colour a secretion, while material remaining in the <intestinal lumen> can colour the stools without demonstrating systemic <drug absorption>.

* <Rhubarb> colours <urine> yellow to yellow-brown; the colour becomes redder in an <alkaline solution>. The contemporary account also reports coloration of <sweat> and <breast milk>. This is a historical observation about passage of colouring matter, not a claim that every constituent passes unchanged into every secretion. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Labiatae, continued, Rheum: colouring constituent, alkaline solutions, and coloration of urine, perspiration and milk]{ref}
* <Senna> can give <urine> a yellow-brown or red-brown colour. The effect is still recognised in current medicinal information. <Senna by the NHS>[Side effects, coloration of urine]{ref}
* <Santonin>, used historically as an <anthelmintic>, can make <urine> conspicuously yellow. This must be distinguished from the drug's separate effect on colour vision: a yellow-looking visual field is not itself excretion of a yellow pigment. <A Manual of Practical Therapeutics by Edward John Waring (1865)>[§2416, physiological effects and yellow urine]{ref}
* <Logwood> can give <urine> a pink or reddish colour, particularly when it is an <alkaline solution>; the historical description notes that addition of <ammonia> brings out the colour in a previously acid specimen. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Cisalpinae, Haematoxylum, urine coloration]{ref}
* <Iron preparations> can darken <faeces>; <bismuth preparations> also cause dark or black stools. In these examples the result need not be a pre-existing vegetable dye simply passing unchanged through the body. Current information confirms black stools with oral <iron> and with the modern <bismuth> medicine <bismuth subsalicylate>; the latter is corroboration of the colour effect, not an identification of that modern formulation with the 1864 bismuth articles. <Bleeding from the bottom by the NHS>[Dark stools and iron tablets]{ref} <Pepto-Bismol by the NHS>[Side effects: black stools and black tongue]{ref}
* <Charcoal> supplies a black material directly to the bowel contents. Its appearance in the stools does not require its <absorption> and subsequent <renal excretion>.

**Colour alone does not identify either the drug or a disease.** A medicinal pigment can resemble <blood> or a <bile pigment>; <pH>, timing after administration and appropriate chemical or microscopic examination distinguish possibilities. Conversely, a history of taking a colouring medicine does not prove that an observed specimen contains no <blood>. These examples describe pharmacological and historical observations, not results from an unseen specimen.

**Original question.**

What are the preparations of Iron in the British Pharmacopœia ? For what purpose is Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum used ? How is it ordered to be made ?

=== Solution

**The 1864 iron list includes metallic, ferrous, ferric and compound preparations; its wet <Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum> was used historically as an <antidote> to <arsenic poisoning>.** It must be distinguished from dried <Ferri Peroxidum> and from later editions' reassignment of similar Latin names.

The officinal articles and preparations are:

* metallic forms: <iron wire>, *Ferrum*, and <Ferrum Redactum>;
* simple salts and oxides: <Ferri Arsenias>, <Ferri Carbonas Saccharata>, <Ferri Iodidum>, <Ferri Oxidum Magneticum>, <Ferri Peroxidum>, <Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum>, <Ferri Phosphas>, <Ferri Sulphas>, <Ferri Sulphas Exsiccata> and <Ferri Sulphas Granulata>;
* complex salts: <Ferri et Ammoniae Citras>, <Ferri et Quiniae Citras> and <Ferrum Tartaratum>;
* solutions: <Liquor Ferri Perchloridi>, <Liquor Ferri Pernitratis> and the preparative <Liquor Ferri Persulphatis>;
* compounded forms: <Mistura Ferri Composita>, <Pilula Ferri Carbonatis>, <Pilula Ferri Iodidi>, <Syrupus Ferri Iodidi>, <Syrupus Ferri Phosphatis>, <Tinctura Ferri Perchloridi> and <Vinum Ferri>.

<The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Iron and successive British iron monographs; Solution of Persulphate of Iron, Appendix A; British compounds distinguished from American additions and older London preparations]{ref} <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[pp. 43–45, iron additions, omissions and names; pp. 55, 63, 67 and 72, compound forms]{ref}

**Prepare the hydrated peroxide by inducing <precipitation> from the ferric solution with <sodium hydroxide>, collecting and washing the wet precipitate, and preserving it without drying in a covered vessel.** The starting solution is <Liquor Ferri Persulphatis>, whose “persulphate” here means ferric sulfate, not a modern peroxydisulfate salt. Add the appropriately diluted ferric solution to the alkaline liquor with stirring, allow the <precipitate> to settle, collect it on a calico <filter>, and wash with <distilled water> until the washings no longer give a <precipitate> with <barium chloride>. This tests removal of soluble <sulfate>. The resulting fresh, hydrated material is kept wet; drying gives the separate dried preparation. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Ferri Peroxidum Hydratum; Ferri Peroxidum; Solution of Persulphate of Iron]{ref}

In modern chemical notation the precipitation can be idealised as
$$
\mathrm{Fe^{3+}+3OH^-\longrightarrow Fe(OH)_3(s)}.
$$
The fresh solid is a hydrated ferric oxide material with variable water content; the equation is not a claim that every pharmaceutical batch is one pure, crystalline <iron hydroxide>. Its historical use depended on binding dissolved <arsenic> into less available material in the digestive tract, especially the freshly precipitated, wet state. It was not a universal antidote to every metal or every stage of absorbed <arsenic poisoning>. Contemporary discussion explicitly identified the new hydrated peroxide as an arsenic antidote. <The British Pharmacopoeia in Chemical News (1864)>[6 February 1864, p. 65, iron preparations and freshly precipitated antidote]{ref} <A Manual of Practical Therapeutics by Edward John Waring (1865)>[§1231, preparation, washings tested with barium chloride, preservation without drying and recent preparation]{ref}

Modern treatment of significant <arsenic poisoning> concerns systemic toxicity and may involve specialist-directed <chelation therapy>; a nineteenth-century ferric paste is not the modern treatment standard. <Clinician Brief: Arsenic by ATSDR>[Patient management, acute exposure and specialist consultation for chelation]{ref}

**Original question.**

What classes of medicines are fitted for preparation by means of decoction and of infusion respectively ? How are liquid extracts made ?

=== Solution

**<Decoction> suits material whose useful constituents are water-soluble and withstand boiling; <infusion> avoids sustained boiling and can better preserve delicate or volatile constituents.** The choice depends on both the plant's structure and the chemistry of the constituent being extracted.

Hard <bark>, <wood> and many kinds of <root (botany)> need comminution and more prolonged contact with hot <water> to penetrate their tissues; a <decoction> can assist extraction when the desired material is stable. Boiling is unsuitable when it drives off a needed <volatile oil>, decomposes the active constituent, or extracts an unwanted excess of <starch> or other material. A hard <root (botany)> is therefore not automatically an instruction to boil. Period examples suitable for <decoction> include <oak bark>, <logwood> and <pareira root>; appropriate <infusions> include aromatic <cloves> and <chamomile>. Cold <infusion> of <calumba> avoids dissolving its <starch>, illustrating that suitability depends on the desired selectivity of <solvent extraction>, not merely on whether a drug is a leaf or a root. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Relation of measures to weights, part 2, Infusa and cold calumba; corresponding British plant monographs]{ref}

For an <infusion>, the appropriately divided drug is left in a measured solvent for a prescribed time, commonly in a covered vessel. Hot <water> assists extraction, while a cover reduces loss of volatile matter. Cold <infusion> can be chosen when heat is undesirable. The result must be strained or filtered; time, temperature, particle size and solvent quantity affect its strength. Neither method guarantees that every constituent is extracted in the same proportion.

**A <liquid extract> is made by extracting the specified drug with a suitable solvent, separating the liquid from the exhausted material, and bringing the extract to its prescribed concentration and volume.** <Maceration> can precede <percolation>; expression or washing recovers retained liquid. A percolator passes fresh solvent through a packed bed, rather than indefinitely soaking the drug in an increasingly saturated liquid. Where concentration is necessary, controlled evaporation limits damage; an alcoholic fraction can be reserved or recombined as the monograph directs. Different <liquid extracts> can use aqueous, alcoholic or ethereal processes. For example, the <liquid extract> of <male fern> is an ethereal extraction of a resinous and oily fraction, whereas the <liquid extract> of <opium> is compounded from a previously made aqueous extract. These are chemically different operations. There is no warrant for assigning all 1864 extracts one modern universal drug-to-extract ratio. <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[pp. 20–21 and 42–43, differing liquid extracts and strengths; p. 15, percolation]{ref}

**Original question.**

What is the temperature at which Linseed, Mustard, Charcoal, and Yeast Poultices are to be made ?

=== Solution

**For the contemporary formulary, use boiling water for the <linseed>, <charcoal> and <mustard> <poultices>, and water at 100°F for the <yeast> <poultice>.** These are preparation temperatures, not temperatures at which boiling material is placed on <skin>.

For <Cataplasma Lini>, hot <water> hydrates the ground <linseed> into a cohesive moist mass; the contemporary account also notes the addition of <olive oil> when oil has been removed from the meal. For <Cataplasma Carbonis>, the hot bread-and-<linseed> basis is made with boiling <water>, then the <charcoal> is incorporated and part used at the surface. At ordinary atmospheric pressure the boiling-water preparation temperature is approximately $\boxed{212\,{}^\circ\mathrm{F}=100\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}}$. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Relation of measures to weights of the British Pharmacopoeia, Cataplasmata; Carbo Ligni, charcoal cataplasm]{ref}

For <Cataplasma Fermenti>, the stated $\boxed{100\,{}^\circ\mathrm{F}\approx37.8\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}}$ permits the <yeast> to ferment the flour mixture. Boiling would injure its fermenting activity. The flour, <yeast> and warm <water> are mixed and kept warm while the mass rises. This is a historical compounding process, not evidence that a fermented dressing treats every <wound>. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Cataplasmata, exception for Cataplasma Fermenti]{ref}

The <mustard> entry requires a historical qualification. The contemporary British recipe reproduced by the text directs powdered <mustard> and <linseed> to be mixed, then stirred into boiling <water>; the same text observes that excessive heat impairs development of the pungent <volatile oil>. Thus **“boiling” is the contemporary formula's instruction, while lukewarm preparation better preserves development of mustard's pungent <volatile oil>**. These are different claims. The later practical preference for lukewarm <water> should not silently replace the 1864 recipe, nor should that recipe be mistaken for optimal modern biochemical practice. <The Essentials of Materia Medica and Therapeutics by Alfred Baring Garrod (1865)>[Cruciferae or Brassicaceae, Cataplasma Sinapis and effect of excessive heat]{ref}

**Original question.**

Explain the principle of Volumetric Analysis.

=== Solution

**<Volumetric analysis> determines an amount of substance from the measured volume of a reagent of known strength that reacts with it in a known proportion.** A measured <aliquot> is placed in a suitable vessel, the <standard solution> is delivered from a <burette>, and the volume required to reach the chosen <titration endpoint> is read. A <pipette> measures the <aliquot>; a <volumetric flask> prepares or dilutes the <standard solution>.

For a reaction $aA+bB\longrightarrow\text{products}$, if the <concentration> of the <titrant> $B$ is $c_B$ and the corrected volume delivered is $V_B$, then
$$
\boxed{n_A=\frac{a}{b}c_BV_B}.
$$
With volume in <litres> and concentration in <moles> per litre, the answer is an amount in <moles>. The corresponding <mass> is $m_A=M_A n_A$, using the appropriate <molar mass>. For an unknown solution of aliquot volume $V_A$,
$$
\boxed{c_A=\frac{a\,c_BV_B}{b\,V_A}}.
$$
These are modern symbols for the same proportional reasoning that a nineteenth-century analyst could express with equivalent weights and a specified standard solution.

<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[titration; volumetric titration, terms T06387 and 09055]{ref}

A suitable reaction is sufficiently rapid, complete and selective, with a recognisable endpoint. In an <acid–base titration>, an <indicator> can change colour near <neutralization>; other reactions use <precipitation>, <redox> or a colour intrinsic to a reagent. The observed <titration endpoint> must represent the required <equivalence point> closely enough. The reagent's nominal preparation alone is insufficient if its <concentration> changes in storage; <standardisation of a solution>, a <reagent blank> where needed, and correct reading of the <meniscus> control <systematic error>. A <titration> measures the intended <analyte> only insofar as interfering substances have been excluded or accounted for.

**Original question.**

Give the equivalents of the weights and measures employed in the British Pharmacopœia, in French weights and measures.

=== Solution

**The 1864 solid units are the <avoirdupois pound>, <avoirdupois ounce> and <grain>; the liquid units are the Imperial measures.** The <grain> is unchanged, while the old apothecaries' pound and ounce are not the units of the new formulary. <The Prescriber's Analysis of the British Pharmacopoeia by John Birkbeck Nevins (1864)>[pp. 16–17, change of weights and unchanged liquid measures]{ref}

The relations for solids are
$$
1\,\mathrm{lb}=16\,\mathrm{oz}=7000\,\mathrm{gr},\qquad
1\,\mathrm{oz}=437.5\,\mathrm{gr}.
$$
Their approximate French metric equivalents are:

* one <grain>: $\boxed{0.0648\,\mathrm{g}}$, about $64.8\,\mathrm{mg}$;
* one <avoirdupois ounce>: $\boxed{28.35\,\mathrm{g}}$;
* one <avoirdupois pound>: $\boxed{453.59\,\mathrm{g}}$.

For liquids,
$$
1\,\mathrm{gal}=8\,\mathrm{pt},\qquad
1\,\mathrm{pt}=20\,\mathrm{fl\,oz},\qquad
1\,\mathrm{fl\,oz}=8\,\mathrm{fl\,dr},\qquad
1\,\mathrm{fl\,dr}=60\,\mathrm{min}.
$$
The approximate metric volumes are:

* one <minim>: $\boxed{0.0592\,\mathrm{mL}}$;
* one <fluid drachm>: $\boxed{3.552\,\mathrm{mL}}$;
* one <fluid ounce>: $\boxed{28.41\,\mathrm{mL}}$;
* one <Imperial pint>: $\boxed{568.26\,\mathrm{mL}}$;
* one <Imperial gallon>: $\boxed{4.5461\,\mathrm{L}}$.

A <millilitre> is a cubic centimetre, and a <litre> is a cubic decimetre. These displayed equivalents are rounded; small differences from a period table's last digits reflect rounding and historical calibration conventions. A <fluid ounce> is a <volume> and an <avoirdupois ounce> is a <mass>. They cannot be interchanged for an arbitrary liquid without its <density>. Likewise, a drop is not a defined <minim>: its volume depends on the liquid and the dropper.
