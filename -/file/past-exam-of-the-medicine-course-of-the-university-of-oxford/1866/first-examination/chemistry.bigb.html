**Original question.**

In what relation do the chemical elements stand to the other kinds of matter on the earth's surface ? Why are air and water, respectively, no longer regarded as elements ?

=== Solution

**<Chemical elements> are the constituents from which ordinary <chemical compounds> are built; many familiar materials are <mixtures> of those <chemical compounds> and sometimes free <chemical elements>.** In the historical operational definition, a <chemical element> could not be resolved into simpler substances by chemical means. In the modern definition, its <chemical atoms> all have the same <atomic number>. Ordinary <chemical reactions> rearrange those <chemical atoms> rather than creating a new <chemical element>.

<Air> is a <mixture>, chiefly of <nitrogen> and <oxygen>, whose components can be separated or selectively consumed. For example, burning <phosphorus> in a suitable enclosed sample removes most of the <oxygen> and leaves chiefly <nitrogen>; the residual <gas> has different properties from the original <air>. <Air> also contains <carbon dioxide>, variable <water vapour> and smaller amounts of other gases. Its variable composition is incompatible with treating it as one elementary substance.

<Water> is a <chemical compound>: <electrolysis> produces <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen>, approximately in the <volume> ratio $2:1$ at the same <temperature> and <pressure>. Their recombination produces <water> again:
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm H_2+\mathrm O_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.}
$$
Thus <water> has fixed composition but is chemically decomposable, whereas <air> has no fixed single <chemical formula>. Neither belongs with the <chemical elements> merely because it is common and widespread on the Earth's surface.

**Original question.**

By what methods may oxygen be prepared ?

=== Solution

**<Oxygen> can be obtained by decomposing an oxygen-rich <chemical compound>, or by separating it from <air>.** For the historical laboratory, heating <potassium chlorate>, heating certain <metal oxides>, and <electrolysis> of <water> are useful examples. The substance collected is <molecular oxygen>, $\mathrm O_2$.

A common preparation heats <potassium chlorate>, often with <manganese dioxide> as a <catalyst>:
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm{KClO}_3\xrightarrow{\Delta}2\mathrm{KCl}+3\mathrm O_2.}
$$
A heat-resistant vessel and delivery tube conduct the evolved <gas> to an inverted collecting vessel over <water>, after the initial displaced <air> has been excluded. <Oxygen> is only slightly soluble in <water>, making this collection principle useful. The <manganese dioxide> changes the reaction rate and required heating conditions; it is not the net source of the <oxygen> in this catalysed balance. Without the <catalyst>, <potassium perchlorate> can occur as an intermediate.

Two older preparations use heated <metal oxides>:
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm{HgO}\xrightarrow{\Delta}2\mathrm{Hg}+\mathrm O_2,}
$$
$$
\boxed{4\mathrm{MnO}_2\xrightarrow{\text{strong heating}}2\mathrm{Mn}_2\mathrm O_3+\mathrm O_2.}
$$
The first explains the historical decomposition of <mercury(II) oxide>, with metallic <mercury> deposited on cooler parts of the apparatus. It is historical chemistry, not a preferred present-day teaching preparation. The second is one stage in the reduction of <manganese dioxide>; at still higher <temperature> further oxygen loss can give $\mathrm{Mn}_3\mathrm O_4$, so the oxide residue depends on the conditions. Strong heating of <potassium nitrate> provides another net balance:
$$
2\mathrm{KNO}_3\longrightarrow2\mathrm{KNO}_2+\mathrm O_2.
$$
In <electrolysis>, suitable separated <electrodes> collect <oxygen> at the <anode> and <hydrogen> at the <cathode>. With a suitable electrolyte and electrodes, the net <chemical equation> is
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\longrightarrow2\mathrm H_2+\mathrm O_2.}
$$
The gases must be kept in separate collecting spaces if the experiment is to demonstrate their separate identities and the approximately $2:1$ <volume> ratio. Choice of electrolyte matters: a chloride-containing electrolyte can give <chlorine> instead of clean <oxygen> evolution.

For a modern small-scale comparison, catalytic decomposition of <hydrogen peroxide> gives
$$
2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O+\mathrm O_2.
$$
This is a different feedstock from <potassium chlorate>. Large-scale separation of <oxygen> from <air> by cryogenic fractionation belongs to later industrial practice and should not be substituted for an 1866 laboratory answer. A collected sample can be tested by its vigorous support of <combustion>, conventionally the relighting of a glowing splint; this observation identifies a property, while the preparation and exclusion of interfering gases establish the sample's provenance.

**Original question.**

What are the sources and properties of ammonia ?

=== Solution

**<Ammonia> is the volatile <nitrogen>–<hydrogen> <chemical compound> $\mathrm{NH}_3$, obtained historically from ammoniacal salts and from the decomposition of nitrogen-containing organic matter.** Sources include the decay of animal and vegetable material, the conversion of <urea> in stale urine, and the destructive distillation of <coal>, bones or horn. In gasworks, much of the <ammonia> was captured in the aqueous <ammoniacal liquor> during cooling and washing of crude <coal gas>. These sources do not mean that nitrogen-free constituents of <coal> spontaneously produce <nitrogen>.

An <ammonium> salt releases <ammonia> when heated with a suitable <chemical base>. For <ammonium chloride> and <slaked lime>,
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}+\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm{NH}_3+\mathrm{CaCl}_2+2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.}
$$
The ionic principle is $\mathrm{NH}_4^++\mathrm{OH}^-\to\mathrm{NH}_3+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$. A generating vessel supplies a drying stage and then an inverted dry collecting vessel in which the lighter <ammonia> displaces <air>. It is unsuitable for ordinary collection over <water> because it dissolves so readily. <Quicklime> can serve as a drying agent; a strongly acidic drying material would instead consume the <ammonia>.

<Ammonia> is a colourless <gas> with a penetrating, irritating odour. Its approximate <relative density> against <air> is $17.03/28.97\simeq0.59$. It is readily liquefied under suitable cooling or <pressure>, and its dissolution in <water> releases <heat>. It is toxic and corrosive at sufficient exposure; direct smelling is not an analytical requirement.<Ammonia in the NIOSH Pocket Guide>[Physical Description, Molecular Weight, Solubility and Symptoms]{ref}

The aqueous <chemical equilibrium>
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{NH}_3+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{NH}_4^++\mathrm{OH}^-}
$$
explains the alkaline reaction and the change of moist red <litmus> to blue. The historical name “ammonium hydroxide” for the solution should not imply that every dissolved molecule has become a discrete molecule of $\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH}$. With <hydrochloric acid>, <ammonia> forms <ammonium chloride>:
$$
\mathrm{NH}_3+\mathrm{HCl}\longrightarrow\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}.
$$
The familiar white cloud where the gases meet consists of fine solid salt particles, rather than liquid <water> alone. In solution, <ammonia> also acts as a <ligand>; an excess can produce a deep-blue copper–ammine <coordination complex> from suitable <copper(II)> solutions. Although <ammonia> does not readily sustain an ordinary flame in <air>, it is not intrinsically incapable of <combustion>; sufficiently favourable mixtures burn.

**Original question.**

In what metallic solutions does sulphuretted hydrogen produce precipitates in presence of an acid ? How is this gas prepared ?

=== Solution

**In a suitably dilute, nonoxidizing <chemical acid>, <hydrogen sulfide> precipitates the sufficiently insoluble sulfides, including those of <copper>, <lead>, <mercury>, <bismuth>, <cadmium>, <arsenic>, <antimony> and <tin>.** “Sulphuretted hydrogen” is the historical name for <hydrogen sulfide>, $\mathrm H_2\mathrm S$. Oxidation state and acidity must be specified: the statement is not independent of every possible <chemical solution> condition.

\Table[
\Tr[\Th[Representative dissolved species]\Th[Principal precipitate]\Th[Usual appearance]]
\Tr[\Td[<Copper(II)>]\Td[$\mathrm{CuS}$]\Td[Black]]
\Tr[\Td[<Lead(II)>]\Td[$\mathrm{PbS}$]\Td[Black]]
\Tr[\Td[<Mercury(II)>]\Td[$\mathrm{HgS}$]\Td[Black when freshly precipitated]]
\Tr[\Td[<Bismuth(III)>]\Td[$\mathrm{Bi}_2\mathrm S_3$]\Td[Brown-black]]
\Tr[\Td[<Cadmium(II)>]\Td[$\mathrm{CdS}$]\Td[Yellow]]
\Tr[\Td[<Arsenic(III)>]\Td[$\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm S_3$]\Td[Yellow]]
\Tr[\Td[<Antimony(III)>]\Td[$\mathrm{Sb}_2\mathrm S_3$]\Td[Orange]]
\Tr[\Td[<Tin(II)>]\Td[$\mathrm{SnS}$]\Td[Brown or brown-black]]
\Tr[\Td[<Tin(IV)>]\Td[$\mathrm{SnS}_2$]\Td[Yellow]]
]

<Arsenic> is now classed as a <metalloid>; the historical analytical grouping was not a rigorous modern division into metals and nonmetals. Dissolved <silver> also gives black $\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm S$ in acidic conditions. It is often absent from the conventional second analytical group because a preceding <hydrochloric acid> treatment has already removed it as <silver chloride>. Suitable <gold> and <platinum> solutions can likewise yield dark sulfide precipitates, although their speciation and reduction chemistry make a simple universal colour rule inappropriate. Thus an analytical group is a separation convention, not a list of every ion that can possibly react.

For a divalent <metal ion>, a useful net balance is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm M^{2+}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm S\longrightarrow\mathrm{MS}\downarrow+2\mathrm H^+.}
$$
The selectivity follows from two coupled <chemical equilibrium> conditions. <Hydrogen sulfide> is a <weak acid>; increasing $[\mathrm H^+]$ suppresses its ionization. Approximately,
$$
[\mathrm S^{2-}]=\frac{K_{a1}K_{a2}[\mathrm H_2\mathrm S]}{[\mathrm H^+]^2}.
$$
Even this small sulfide activity can exceed the <solubility product> of very insoluble sulfides. More soluble sulfides, such as those of <zinc> and <manganese>, generally require less acidic or alkaline conditions. Consequently an excessively strong <chemical acid>, complex formation or an oxidizing medium can change the result. Colours alone are preliminary indications, not final identification.<Qualitative Analysis of Metal Ions in Solution by Williams College>[Experiment 2–3, Introduction and Group II discussion, printed II & III-1–3 and II & III-9–10]{ref}

The traditional preparation treats <iron(II) sulfide> with a dilute nonoxidizing <chemical acid>:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{FeS}+2\mathrm{HCl}\longrightarrow\mathrm{FeCl}_2+\mathrm H_2\mathrm S.}
$$
A gas-generating flask with controlled acid entry and a delivery outlet conducts the product to the test solution. The apparatus must permit gas flow and contain the effluent rather than accumulating <pressure> in a sealed vessel. <Sulfuric acid> in dilute form can replace <hydrochloric acid>; concentrated oxidizing acids are unsuitable for this simple preparation. <Hydrogen sulfide> is acutely toxic, and loss of odour perception makes smell an unreliable warning: the historical apparatus describes a laboratory principle, not a household test.<Hydrogen sulfide by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[Physical Description, odour fatigue, Symptoms and Target Organs]{ref}

**Original question.**

What are the principal constituents of coal gas ?

=== Solution

**Purified <coal gas> is mainly <molecular hydrogen>, <methane> and <carbon monoxide>, with smaller quantities of other <hydrocarbons> and diluent gases.** It is made by heating <coal> in retorts with little or no admitted <air>, not by completely burning the <coal>.

The hot products include permanent gases, condensable organic vapours and <water vapour>, leaving <coke> in the retort. Cooling removes much of the <coal tar> and condenses <water>, which captures <ammonia> as <ammoniacal liquor>. Further washing and purification remove much of the remaining <ammonia>, <hydrogen sulfide> and other objectionable constituents before the <gas> is distributed. A crude retort product and purified household <coal gas> therefore have different compositions.

<Molecular hydrogen> is commonly the largest component by <volume>. <Methane> is another major combustible constituent, while <carbon monoxide> contributes fuel value and serious toxicity. <Ethylene> and other unsaturated <hydrocarbons>, together with vapours such as <benzene>, contribute strongly to the luminous flame. Their thermal breakdown can produce incandescent <soot>, explaining why a small fraction of heavier <hydrocarbons> can disproportionately affect lighting quality. Smaller or variable quantities of <carbon dioxide> and <molecular nitrogen> dilute the combustible mixture. <Molecular oxygen> is not an intended major constituent; air ingress can change the mixture.

There is no single balanced <chemical equation> for making <coal gas>: <coal> is a complex feed and produces many products simultaneously. Nor is one exact percentage composition universal; it depends on the <coal>, retort <temperature>, operating conditions and purification. The main distinction is between the light combustible gases, the smaller illuminating hydrocarbon fraction, and contaminants or diluents. Modern natural gas, usually rich in <methane>, should not be used as a synonym for historical <coal gas>.

**Original question.**

Describe the mode of preparation and properties of iodide of potassium.

=== Solution

**<Potassium iodide> is prepared by converting <iodine> into <iodide>, then obtaining a pure crystalline <potassium> salt.** One chemical route uses <potassium hydroxide>; an alternative first forms <iron(II) iodide>. The account here describes the chemical operations and their balances, without assigning a later pharmacopoeial formula to 1866.

In the alkaline route, <iodine> reacts with hot <potassium hydroxide> to give a mixture of <potassium iodide> and <potassium iodate>:
$$
\boxed{3\mathrm I_2+6\mathrm{KOH}\longrightarrow5\mathrm{KI}+\mathrm{KIO}_3+3\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.}
$$
This is <disproportionation>: <iodine> goes from <oxidation state> $0$ to both $-1$ and $+5$. Merely evaporating the solution would leave the unwanted <iodate> with the <iodide>. Heating the dried salts with <charcoal> reduces the <iodate> to <iodide>. A limiting balance with <carbon monoxide> as the carbon oxide product is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{KIO}_3+3\mathrm C\longrightarrow\mathrm{KI}+3\mathrm{CO}.}
$$
The carbon oxides can depend on the reaction conditions; the indispensable chemical point is removal of the iodate's <oxygen> without losing its <iodine>. Extraction with <water>, filtration of insoluble residue, concentration and crystallization then recover <potassium iodide>. A modern USDA technical evaluation explicitly describes this same reaction sequence; it is corroboration of the chemistry, not evidence that its modern document was the 1866 official recipe.<Potassium Iodide technical evaluation by United States Department of Agriculture>[2011 report, printed pp. 5–6, Evaluation Question 1, method (a)]{ref}

In the alternative iron route,
$$
\mathrm{Fe}+\mathrm I_2\longrightarrow\mathrm{FeI}_2,
$$
followed by
$$
\mathrm{FeI}_2+\mathrm K_2\mathrm{CO}_3\longrightarrow2\mathrm{KI}+\mathrm{FeCO}_3\downarrow.
$$
The insoluble iron-containing material is separated and the <potassium iodide> recovered from the liquid. Oxidation of the ferrous precipitate during working can alter its final form, so the displayed <chemical equation> is the ideal metathesis balance rather than a guarantee that every collected particle remains pure <iron(II) carbonate>.

Pure <potassium iodide> is a colourless or white crystalline ionic <solid>, commonly in cubic crystals, readily soluble in <water> and less soluble in <ethanol>. It has no intrinsic brown iodine colour. Its aqueous solution contains <potassium> <ions> and <iodide> <ions>. Free <iodine> dissolves in the presence of <iodide> through <triiodide> formation,
$$
\mathrm I_2+\mathrm I^-\rightleftharpoons\mathrm I_3^-.
$$
With <silver nitrate>, <iodide> gives yellow <silver iodide>, sparingly soluble in <water> and resistant to ordinary aqueous <ammonia>:
$$
\mathrm{Ag}^++\mathrm I^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{AgI}\downarrow.
$$
A little <chlorine> liberates <iodine>, which gives the characteristic colour with <starch>:
$$
\mathrm{Cl}_2+2\mathrm I^-\longrightarrow2\mathrm{Cl}^-+\mathrm I_2.
$$
By contrast, <iodate> contamination can liberate <iodine> simply on acidification in the presence of <iodide>:
$$
\mathrm{IO}_3^-+5\mathrm I^-+6\mathrm H^+\longrightarrow3\mathrm I_2+3\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.
$$
That distinction explains why removal of the <iodate> is part of preparing a well-defined product, rather than an optional cosmetic step. These are chemical properties and recognition principles, not advice to administer the salt.

**Original question.**

How is phosphorus obtained ?

=== Solution

**The relevant historical route starts with phosphate-rich bone ash, converts it into a reducible phosphate preparation, and heats that preparation with <carbon> to obtain elemental <phosphorus>.** Bone contains <phosphorus> in <phosphate>, not as free pieces of <phosphorus> awaiting extraction.

Burning cleaned bones removes most organic matter and leaves <bone ash>, rich in calcium phosphates. Although natural bone mineral is not pure $\mathrm{Ca}_3(\mathrm{PO}_4)_2$, that formula is a useful historical reaction model. Treatment with <sulfuric acid> forms sparingly soluble <calcium sulfate> and a soluble acid phosphate fraction. With partial removal of <calcium>, an idealized balance is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Ca}_3(\mathrm{PO}_4)_2+2\mathrm H_2\mathrm{SO}_4\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ca(H}_2\mathrm{PO}_4)_2+2\mathrm{CaSO}_4.}
$$
Separate the sulfate-rich residue, concentrate the phosphate-bearing liquor, and heat the resulting material with <charcoal> in a retort. Dehydration and high-temperature <reduction> produce phosphorus vapour together with carbon oxides, while a calcium phosphate residue remains. One overall balance for the acid-phosphate route is
$$
\boxed{3\mathrm{Ca(H}_2\mathrm{PO}_4)_2+10\mathrm C\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ca}_3(\mathrm{PO}_4)_2+\mathrm P_4+10\mathrm{CO}+6\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.}
$$
This is a summed process balance; it does not imply that the hydrated phosphate survives unchanged until one elementary reaction suddenly occurs. It also makes clear that the partial-acid route leaves some of the original <phosphorus> in the calcium phosphate residue. Contemporary descriptions distinguish this route from treating the ash with enough <sulfuric acid> to give a more fully acidified phosphorus-bearing liquid.<Lessons in Elementary Chemistry by Henry Enfield Roscoe>[1868 printing, Lesson XV, printed pp. 131–133: bone ash, acid phosphate and charcoal reduction; later printing used as historical description, not a pharmacopoeia]{ref}

The retort outlet leads to a water-covered receiver: phosphorus vapour condenses and is protected from <air>. The collected product is principally <white phosphorus>, with molecular form $\mathrm P_4$. Its readiness to oxidize and ignite is why collection under <water> belongs to the explanation of the apparatus. This historical product must be distinguished from <red phosphorus>, obtained by a subsequent controlled transformation rather than directly identified with the freshly condensed product.

For comparison, the well-known silica-assisted phosphate-reduction balance is
$$
2\mathrm{Ca}_3(\mathrm{PO}_4)_2+6\mathrm{SiO}_2+10\mathrm C\longrightarrow\mathrm P_4+6\mathrm{CaSiO}_3+10\mathrm{CO}.
$$
Here <silica> binds <calcium> into a silicate-rich residue. Modern electric-furnace manufacture uses this principle; describing an electric furnace as the ordinary 1866 apparatus would be anachronistic. The historical answer is the sequence **<bone ash> → acid phosphate preparation → <carbon> reduction in a retort → condensation of <phosphorus> under <water>**.

**Original question.**

Explain briefly the method of spectrum analysis, and state some of the discoveries which have resulted from its application.

=== Solution

**<Spectrum analysis> identifies substances by the positions and patterns of characteristic <spectral lines>, rather than by the overall colour of a flame alone.** A small amount of a suitable salt is introduced into a hot, weakly luminous flame. In the historical prism <spectroscope>, light enters a narrow slit, a collimator makes the emerging rays nearly parallel, a prism separates them by wavelength, and an observing telescope shows images of the slit at different spectral positions. A reference scale or comparison spectrum makes positions reproducible.

A clean <platinum> wire and reagent blank help distinguish the sample's signal from contamination. A reference sample establishes the expected positions, while repeated cleaning and testing check whether an unfamiliar line belongs to the sample. Several substances can be recognized in one mixture because their characteristic line patterns can coexist, although overlapping lines and changing excitation conditions complicate identification. <Robert Bunsen> and <Gustav Kirchhoff>'s original account explicitly emphasizes the faint background of the flame, the prism instrument and the characteristic patterns of alkali and alkaline-earth salts.<Chemical Analysis by Observation of Spectra by Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen>[1860 paper, apparatus and comparison of salt spectra]{ref}

In modern terms, <emission lines> arise when excited <chemical atoms> or <ions> release photons at energies corresponding to allowed differences between <energy levels>:
$$
\boxed{h\nu=E_{\rm upper}-E_{\rm lower},\qquad\lambda=c/\nu.}
$$
The <quantum mechanics> behind this equation is a later explanation, not the theory available to an 1866 candidate. Similarly, <absorption lines> appear when a cooler foreground gas removes particular wavelengths from a brighter background. Agreement between laboratory line positions and dark lines in the <solar spectrum> permits identification of constituents of the solar atmosphere without bringing a solar sample into the laboratory. It does not establish the detailed chemical constitution of every layer of the Sun.

By December 1866, striking examples included **<caesium> (1860), <rubidium> (1861), <thallium> (1861) and <indium> (1863)**. The first two were recognized in the work of <Robert Bunsen> and <Gustav Kirchhoff>, using conspicuous blue and deep-red spectral features respectively. <William Crookes> recognized the characteristic green line associated with <thallium>, while <Ferdinand Reich> and <Hieronymus Theodor Richter> identified <indium> through its indigo-blue spectral signature. These observations motivated chemical separation and further characterization; an unexpected line by itself is a clue to a new <chemical element>, not a complete proof.<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Caesium — Element information, properties and uses>[History, discovery in 1860]{ref}<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Rubidium — Element information, properties and uses>[History, discovery in 1861]{ref}<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Thallium — Element information, properties and uses>[History, green spectral line and 1861 announcement]{ref}<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Indium — Element information, properties and uses>[History, 1863 discovery]{ref}

The corresponding recognition of familiar <chemical elements> in the <solar spectrum> opened stellar chemical analysis. Later discoveries such as solar <helium> should not be credited to the 1866 state of the subject.

**Original question.**

How would you ascertain qualitatively the chemical composition of a tubercle ?

=== Solution

**Use a qualitative separation into aqueous extract, lipid-soluble material, protein-rich residue and mineral matter, and test each fraction independently.** A <tubercle> is a lesion containing several kinds of material, not a pure <chemical compound> with one diagnostic formula. The question asks how to investigate its composition; no observations or numerical composition are supplied.

First record the lesion's physical appearance and take a portion for <microscopy> before destructive treatment. Separate the lesion from surrounding tissue as far as practicable, while recording that contamination by blood, connective tissue or adherent salts can affect the analysis. Different stages of a lesion need not have the same proportions of organic material and calcified matter. A small portion gently dried in suitable apparatus shows whether much <water> is lost; collecting condensation supports identification of the volatile fraction as containing <water>, although mass loss alone is not a chemically specific water test.

On separate portions, or by a documented extraction sequence, examine the main fractions:

* The <water> extract contains soluble salts and some soluble organic material. Test its reaction with <litmus>; heating a suitable clear portion can disclose coagulable <protein>. Acidification and heating must be controlled because changes of <pH> and high salt content can alter coagulation. The absence of a heat coagulum does not prove the absence of every <protein>.
* An <ethanol> or <diethyl ether> extraction can remove <lipids> and other solvent-soluble material. Evaporating a separated solvent extract leaves material whose solubility, oily or crystalline appearance, and behaviour on heating help characterize it. Hydrolysable fats can undergo <saponification>; <cholesterol> belongs to the unsaponifiable fraction. A greasy residue alone does not establish one particular <lipid>.
* Examine the largely insoluble organic residue for <protein>. A controlled alkaline copper reaction gives the violet <biuret test> with sufficient <peptide bonds>; the yellow <xanthoproteic reaction> with <nitric acid> supplies corroborating evidence for suitable aromatic residues. Neither colour establishes that all the material is specifically <albumin>, <casein> or a hypothetical unique “tuberculin” substance. Record the reaction conditions and use a reagent blank and known positive material.
* Ignite a separate portion carefully to remove combustible organic material, then examine the <ash>. Dissolve suitable portions in a noninterfering <chemical acid>. <Calcium> can be recognized by <oxalate> precipitation under appropriate conditions; <phosphate> by an established phosphate test such as the yellow <ammonium phosphomolybdate> reaction. <Carbonate> can be recognized by acid-induced <carbon dioxide> evolution and a separate <limewater> test. These are separate aliquots: acid treatment used to dissolve the <ash> can destroy evidence for the original <carbonate>.

Test soluble salts using a fresh portion or a suitable water extract: for example, <silver nitrate> can detect <chloride>, while <barium> under controlled acidic conditions can detect <sulfate>. A <chloride> test should not be performed on material already contaminated with <hydrochloric acid>. The results identify constituents or operational fractions, not necessarily the precise salts originally present in intact tissue; ashing can also change their chemical forms.

If the intended composition is elemental rather than proximate, investigate an isolated organic fraction separately: oxidation with heated <copper(II) oxide> can convert its <carbon> into <carbon dioxide> and its <hydrogen> into <water>, which are trapped and identified independently. A suitable nitrogen determination can convert organic <nitrogen> into <ammonia> for identification in a separate receiver. Mineral <carbonate> and pre-existing moisture must be controlled before attributing these products to the organic material. Such tests answer a different question from identifying the particular <proteins> or <lipids>.

This approach has a historical basis. Original analyses by <Robert Mortimer Glover>, reproduced in an 1847 review, separately extracted material with <ethanol>, <diethyl ether> and <water>, then examined protein-rich residues and mineral salts. They reported variation between lesions and did not consistently find <casein>. This evidence supports fractionation and caution about specificity, not one universal numerical recipe for all tubercles.<Analyses of tubercular material by Robert Mortimer Glover>[1846 book passages reproduced in Dr. Glover on Scrofula, printed pp. 516–517; original analysis cited as book p. 73]{ref}

A modern conclusion must separate **composition from cause**. A protein-, lipid- and phosphate-containing lesion is not thereby proved to be <tuberculosis>. Histological interpretation and appropriate microbiological examination are different investigations. Current confirmation of <Mycobacterium tuberculosis> involves validated tests such as <nucleic acid amplification> and culture, rather than the extraction reactions above; these are modern qualifications and are not attributed to the 1866 examination.<Clinical and Laboratory Diagnosis for Tuberculosis by CDC>[Bacteriologic examination, nucleic acid amplification and culture sections]{ref}

**Original question.**

Write a short account of the principal chemical changes to which the name ‘fermentation’ has been given.

=== Solution

**The historical name <fermentation> covered several transformations of organic matter: alcoholic, lactic, butyric and acetous changes, as well as some processes now classified separately.** In modern biochemistry, <fermentation> more narrowly means energy-yielding metabolism that regenerates oxidized cofactors without requiring an external respiratory electron acceptor. The older use of the word should not be forced into that modern definition.

In <alcoholic fermentation>, suitable <yeast> converts a fermentable <sugar> into <ethanol> and <carbon dioxide>. For <glucose>, the principal net balance is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm C_6\mathrm H_{12}\mathrm O_6\longrightarrow2\mathrm C_2\mathrm H_5\mathrm{OH}+2\mathrm{CO}_2.}
$$
A vessel with an outlet for the evolved <gas> and a separate receiver illustrates the change: test the <carbon dioxide> with <limewater> and identify the volatile alcohol in a separated distillate. Gas evolution alone does not prove that the liquid contains <ethanol>. Actual yeast cultures also make biomass and minor products; the <chemical equation> describes the principal conversion. In modern terms, <glycolysis> supplies <ATP>, and the reduction leading to <ethanol> regenerates <NAD+> from <NADH>.

In <lactic acid fermentation>, an ideal homolactic balance is
$$
\boxed{\mathrm C_6\mathrm H_{12}\mathrm O_6\longrightarrow2\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{CH(OH)COOH}.}
$$
The souring of suitable carbohydrate-containing materials, including milk, supplied a familiar historical example. Hydrolysis of <lactose> supplies monosaccharides, while acid production lowers the <pH> and can precipitate <casein>. <Casein> coagulation is a consequence of the changed conditions, not evidence that the protein has itself been converted into <lactic acid>. Some organisms instead make additional products, so not every lactic fermentation obeys only this homolactic balance.

In <butyric acid fermentation>, carbohydrates or <lactate> can give <butyric acid>, <carbon dioxide> and <molecular hydrogen>. An idealized balance from <lactic acid> is
$$
\boxed{2\mathrm C_3\mathrm H_6\mathrm O_3\longrightarrow\mathrm C_4\mathrm H_8\mathrm O_2+2\mathrm{CO}_2+2\mathrm H_2.}
$$
It contrasts with the simple homolactic balance because both gaseous products and a different organic acid appear. Mixed cultures and alternative pathways can alter the product distribution; odour is neither a sufficiently specific nor a necessary laboratory test.

<Acetous fermentation> denotes the conversion of <ethanol> into <acetic acid>, as in vinegar production:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm C_2\mathrm H_5\mathrm{OH}+\mathrm O_2\longrightarrow\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.}
$$
It depends on <oxygen> and is now understood as an aerobic microbial oxidation, rather than a fermentation in the narrow biochemical sense. This explains why access to <air> matters differently here than in the main alcohol-producing stage.

Other older extensions of the word include **<saccharification> or diastatic changes**, such as the <hydrolysis> of <starch> into smaller sugars by malt enzymes, and **<sugar inversion>**, in which <sucrose> takes up <water> to form <glucose> and <fructose>:
$$
\mathrm C_{12}\mathrm H_{22}\mathrm O_{11}+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\longrightarrow2\mathrm C_6\mathrm H_{12}\mathrm O_6.
$$
The two hexose products have the same elemental formula but different structures. Such hydrolyses prepare substrates for later metabolism; they do not by themselves constitute the alcohol-producing conversion. **<Viscous fermentation> or <mucilaginous fermentation>** described transformations yielding gum-like material and sometimes <mannitol>; it is an operational historical category without one universal balanced <chemical equation>. **Putrefactive fermentation** referred to the decomposition of nitrogen-containing organic matter, including <proteolysis> and subsequent reactions producing <ammonia>, amines and sometimes <hydrogen sulfide>. It likewise has no single formula covering every mixed substrate and product.

The unifying historical interest is that small amounts of a “ferment” bring about major changes in a large amount of organic substrate. Modern explanation distinguishes organisms, enzymes and metabolic pathways. The later demonstration of <fermentation> in cell-free yeast juice showed that alcoholic conversion need not require intact living yeast cells in the reacting liquid; that result belongs to 1897, not to knowledge to demand of an 1866 candidate.<Cell-free fermentation by Eduard Buchner>[1897 primary report, preparation of yeast juice and fermentation without intact yeast cells]{ref}
