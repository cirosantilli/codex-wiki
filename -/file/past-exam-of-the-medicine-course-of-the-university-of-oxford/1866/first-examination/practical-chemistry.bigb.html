== 1
{scope}

**Original question.**

Each of the tubes, $a$, $b$, $c$, contains a single substance.

=== Solution

TODO: The single substances in tubes a, b and c and all experimental observations are absent; their identities cannot be determined from the statement.

**The identities of $a$, $b$ and $c$ remain undetermined.** The assertion that each tube contains one <chemical substance> does not specify whether it is an element, a salt or an organic compound, nor which <chemical tests> would be positive.

A useful <qualitative chemical analysis> would keep the three samples separate, retain an untouched portion of each, and record their physical state, colour and behaviour with suitable solvents. Small, separately labelled portions would then be used for a logically chosen sequence of <chemical tests>. For a dissolved salt, for example, examination of its <cations> and <anions> addresses different parts of its possible identity; one <precipitation reaction> is rarely a complete identification. A second independent confirmation and comparison with a known reference would strengthen the conclusion.

The record should distinguish an observation, such as formation of a precipitate under stated conditions, from an inference about composition. No observation, reaction equation or identification can be assigned to any one of $a$, $b$ or $c$ from the surviving information. The general procedure explains how a candidate could investigate the tubes; it does not complete the missing identifications.

{scope}

**Original question.**

Examine the solution $d$ for iodine, and the solution $e$ for morphia.

=== Solution

TODO: Solutions d and e and their test observations are absent. Iodine and morphia are named testing targets, not reported positive findings.

**Neither <iodine> in $d$ nor <morphine> in $e$ is established.** “Morphia” is the historical name for <morphine>. The available task can nevertheless be explained as two distinct analytical investigations, using separate portions and reference preparations.

For $d$, first distinguish free <iodine> from <iodide>. A cool, freshly prepared <starch> solution gives a strong blue colour with accessible <iodine> under suitable conditions. Test the reagent by itself and beside a known <iodine> preparation, so that contamination or a failed reagent is not mistaken for an observation about $d$. The intense blue starch indication is documented in contemporary discussion of the 1864 <British Pharmacopoeia>.<Chemical News volume 9 (1864)>[p. 99, The British Pharmacopoeia, starch as an indicator for the first excess of iodine]{ref}

An absent direct blue response would not, by itself, exclude <iodine> present as <iodide>, or establish that the specimen contains no interfering material. If the task includes combined <iodine>, an appropriately controlled oxidation on a separate portion is needed to liberate testable <iodine>; the unoxidized and treated portions answer different questions. The response of the untreated specimen must not be confused with one deliberately produced by changing its chemical state. These are possible branches of the investigation, not observations supplied for $d$.

For $e$, use the period's chemical identity reactions as corroborating observations. The <British Pharmacopoeia by the General Medical Council (1864)> describes the response of hydrochlorate of morphia to <iron(III) chloride> and <nitric acid>: the former gives a blue indication under the specified conditions, and the latter an orange-red indication. The monograph is a reference for a defined material; it does not guarantee that every dilute or impure unknown will reproduce those colours.<British Pharmacopoeia by the General Medical Council (1864)>[Morphiæ Hydrochloras, Characters and Tests, pp. 93–94; primary monograph reproduced on PDF page 37 of the linked facsimile presentation]{ref}

Use separate portions for the two tests and include a <reagent blank> and a known <morphine> reference. Acidity, concentration and other coloured or reactive constituents can affect interpretation. A single colour should therefore be treated as preliminary evidence rather than a conclusive identification in an unrestricted unknown. An unexplained failure of the known reference would invalidate the comparison, while a coloured blank would indicate a problem independent of $e$.

A completed practical record would give the actual response of each sample, reagent and reference, followed by an appropriately qualified conclusion. **The reference behaviours explain the tests; they cannot supply the missing results for $d$ or $e$.**

{scope}

**Original question.**

Prepare some carbonic acid gas, and determine approximately what proportion of this gas mixed with air is sufficient to extinguish the flame of a taper.

=== Solution

**Prepare <carbon dioxide>, make measured mixtures with <air>, and test a standardized lighted taper in fresh mixtures to find an extinction boundary.** In this <taper extinction experiment>, “carbonic acid gas” means gaseous <carbon dioxide>, not liquid <carbonic acid>. The proportion should be reported as a fraction of the final mixture, with the conditions of the test.

A documented sufficient mixture is available from a later first-hand experiment: **one volume of <carbon dioxide> with two volumes of <air> extinguished the introduced taper in the apparatus used.** Thus
$$
\boxed{x_{\mathrm{CO_2}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{CO_2}}}{V_{\mathrm{CO_2}}+V_{\mathrm{air}}}=\frac13\approx 33\% \text{ by volume.}}
$$
This is a conditional literature result, not an invented observation from the Oxford examination. In <The True Position of Oxygen as a Restorative in Carbonic Acid Poisoning by W. Ernest Thomson>, Experiment XLVIII directly compares one-third <carbon dioxide> mixed with two-thirds <oxygen> and with two-thirds <air>; the latter extinguished the taper. The experiment was published in 1894, so it supplies a later physical comparison rather than evidence of what the 1866 candidate measured.<The True Position of Oxygen as a Restorative in Carbonic Acid Poisoning by W. Ernest Thomson>[p. 36, Experiment XLVIII (Physical Experiment), especially preparation (2)]{ref}

The question asks for an approximately sufficient proportion; it does not explicitly require a universal minimum. The cited condition supplies one sufficient point. To find approximately the least sufficient proportion for the proposed apparatus, determine the lower boundary by the following procedure instead of treating one-third as that boundary.

Prepare the <carbon dioxide> in a small gas-generating flask by adding dilute <hydrochloric acid> to <calcium carbonate>:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)+2HCl(aq)\longrightarrow CaCl_2(aq)+CO_2(g)+H_2O(l).}}
$$
Fit a delivery tube, keep the outlet unobstructed, and discard the first gas while the original <air> is being displaced from the apparatus. A wash bottle and droplet trap can reduce acid carry-over before collection in a freely moving <gas syringe> or suitable gas holder. This acid-carbonate preparation is also used in a current <Royal Society of Chemistry> demonstration.<Fire stopper by the Royal Society of Chemistry>[Teaching goal, calcium carbonate–hydrochloric acid preparation and equation]{ref} A separate sample may be checked with <limewater>:
$$
\mathrm{CO_2(g)+Ca(OH)_2(aq)\longrightarrow CaCO_3(s)+H_2O(l)}.
$$
The turbidity is a qualitative check for <carbon dioxide>; it is not a measurement of the fraction in the flame-testing mixture. Avoid using a water-filled collecting vessel for the quantitative mixing stage without allowing for dissolved <carbon dioxide> and <water vapour>.

For the mixing stage, use two calibrated <gas syringes> and a closed mixing vessel with a means of transferring the mixture into a larger flame-testing jar. Measure the component gas volumes at the same <temperature> and <pressure>. Mix the gases thoroughly before the flame test. If the gases behave approximately as an <ideal gas mixture>, their measured <volume fractions> then approximate their <mole fractions>. For example, an intended one-third mixture can be prepared from one measured portion of <carbon dioxide> and twice that volume of <air>. These are preparation instructions, not recorded trial outcomes.

Keep the taper's material, exposed <wick>, initial <candle flame>, insertion depth, gas volume and test duration fixed. Give the flame a reproducible exposure, using a gas-filled jar and a narrow insertion opening with a loose collar to limit entry of fresh <air>. The observation period must be specified before testing; a fixed five-second exposure is one possible chosen protocol, not a reported condition of the historical reference experiment. Use a large mixture volume relative to the small taper, introduce it briefly, and use a fresh mixture for every trial; prolonged burning changes the mixture by consuming <oxygen> and producing <carbon dioxide> and <water vapour>. Do not seal a burning vessel into an improvised pressure-tight apparatus.

Start with an <air> control that sustains the taper. Test successively richer, separately prepared <carbon dioxide> mixtures until extinction occurs within the specified observation period. Retest with a less rich mixture and subdivide the interval. Repeat mixtures near the boundary to detect inconsistent results. A completed experiment would report, for example, the greatest tested fraction $x_b$ at which burning consistently persists and the smallest tested fraction $x_e$ that consistently extinguishes the taper. If the responses are ordered and reproducible under the fixed protocol, report
$$
\boxed{x_b<x_{\mathrm{ext}}\leq x_e.}
$$
A midpoint and half-interval may describe the resolution of that bracket; they are not a statistical confidence interval. No numerical values of $x_b$ or $x_e$ are supplied by the source, and none are asserted here.

For a useful model check, let $y_{\mathrm{O_2},0}$ denote the initial <oxygen> fraction in <air>, and let $x$ denote the fraction of added pure <carbon dioxide> in the final mixture. Before the taper changes the gas, an ideal, uniformly mixed replacement model gives
$$
\boxed{y_{\mathrm{O_2}}=(1-x)y_{\mathrm{O_2},0}.}
$$
Taking dry <air> to have $y_{\mathrm{O_2},0}=0.2095$ is an explicit model input. For the documented one-third mixture, this gives $y_{\mathrm{O_2}}\approx0.140$, or about $14\%$ <oxygen>. It is a calculation from an assumed starting composition, not a measured oxygen analysis or a general extinction rule. Also distinguish a fraction of the final mixture from an addition relative to the original <air>: the volume ratio $r=V_{\mathrm{CO_2}}/V_{\mathrm{air}}$ satisfies $x=r/(1+r)$, so a 1:2 addition ratio means $x=1/3$, not $x=1/2$.

<Carbon dioxide> affects the thermal balance of a <diffusion flame> as well as the oxidizer fraction. Extinction therefore depends on the taper, mixing, gas flow, <temperature>, <pressure> and heat loss; an oxygen-dilution calculation alone cannot predict its value. The official account of <carbon dioxide> suppression describes its predominantly thermophysical action.<Carbon Dioxide as a Fire Suppressant: Examining the Risks by the United States Environmental Protection Agency>[Extinguishing Mechanism of Carbon Dioxide]{ref}

Finally, distinguish this experiment from covering a burning candle and waiting for it to go out. That procedure simultaneously changes gas composition, temperature and spatial distribution. A modern experimental study using gas and temperature sensors found different conditions at different heights and extinction before all <oxygen> was consumed.<Changes of CO2 Concentration and Heat Illustrate Why the Flame Is Extinguished in the Candle-and-Cylinder Experiment>[Abstract, sensor measurements at different heights and remaining oxygen]{ref} A pouring demonstration with unmixed <carbon dioxide> likewise does not determine a uniform bulk fraction. **A sufficient, condition-labelled literature mixture and a reproducible bracketing procedure answer the available task; an actual examination threshold or a universal minimum is not claimed.**
