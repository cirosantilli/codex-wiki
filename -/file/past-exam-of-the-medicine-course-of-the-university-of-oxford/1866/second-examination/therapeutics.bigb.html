= Therapeutics
{scope}
{title2=No. II.}

Original volume: <Examination papers for the Degree of Bachelor of Medicine by the University of Oxford (1864–1867)>. https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3e/Examination_papers_for_the_Degree_of_Bachelor_of_medicine_-_1864-67_(IA_b24757810).pdf#page=65[Open the original paper in the scanned volume], PDF pages 65–65.

Original questions are transcribed from the public-domain edition. Credit: Wellcome Collection; original supplied by Royal College of Physicians, London. https://wellcomecollection.org/works/d7b9zgnx[Source and public-domain rights statement].

These solutions reconstruct therapeutic reasoning available in December 1866 and distinguish it from present medical conclusions. They are not an official answer key. Clinical patterns are explanatory examples, not observations of an actual patient. Historical drugs and procedures are discussed as historical practices, not current prescriptions.

== 1
{scope}

**Original question.**

What would be your treatment of acute Pericarditis in the adult; and by what circumstances would your treatment of this disease be modified ?

=== Solution

**The treatment depends on the cause of <acute pericarditis>, the person's strength and the presence of <pericardial effusion>, <myocarditis> or circulatory failure.** In an 1866 answer, rest, relief of pain and repeated assessment of the <heart> are more defensible starting points than an indiscriminate attempt to suppress every sign of <inflammation> by depletion.

The bedside assessment would include the history of rheumatic illness, chest pain, a <pericardial friction rub>, the character of the <pulse>, breathing, the extent of cardiac dullness on <percussion> and changes in the <heart sounds>. Serial examination matters: disappearance of a <pericardial friction rub> need not mean recovery if fluid is separating the inflamed surfaces. Increasing oppression of breathing, weakness or a failing <pulse> would require reconsidering both the disease and the treatment. <Auscultation> with a <stethoscope> and careful repeated <percussion> supplied information that modern imaging now refines.

Period practice included local <bloodletting> with <medicinal leeches>, counterirritation with a <blistering agent>, <opium> for pain and rest, and disputed <mercury> treatment intended to alter inflammatory exudation. A robust adult early in an apparently vigorous inflammatory attack was treated differently by depletion advocates from an exhausted person with <anaemia>, previous <blood loss> or advanced <kidney> disease. Even in that framework, a rapid weak <pulse> was not a command to remove more <blood>. Nor was <mercury> an uncontested standard: a medical address published in December 1866 specifically listed its use in <pericarditis> among unresolved therapeutic questions.<Introductory Address at the Public Opening of the Medical Session, 1866–67 by William Tennant Gairdner>[p. 294, therapeutic uncertainties concerning mercury in pericarditis and diuretics in Bright's disease]{ref}

**Modern correction: establish the diagnosis, identify complications and use cause-directed treatment rather than <bloodletting> or mercurialization.** <Electrocardiography>, inflammatory markers, <troponin> where myocardial injury is suspected and <echocardiography> help distinguish uncomplicated <pericarditis> from important associated disease. A typical uncomplicated inflammatory episode is treated with <aspirin> or a suitable <nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug>, usually with <colchicine> and appropriate gastroprotection. Renal impairment, <gastrointestinal bleeding>, interactions and a specific infectious or systemic cause can modify this plan. <Corticosteroids> have selected indications and are not the routine first choice. Activity is restricted during active disease and return to exertion follows reassessment.<2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of myocarditis and pericarditis>[Diagnostic assessment; section 6.2.2, pharmacological therapy for pericarditis]{ref}

A large <pericardial effusion> requires attention to its haemodynamic effect, not merely its size. **<Cardiac tamponade> requires urgent specialist drainage and stabilization.** Purulent disease requires treatment of the <infection> and drainage; associated <myocarditis> or a systemic cause changes management further.<2025 ESC Guidelines for the management of myocarditis and pericarditis>[Pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade management pathways]{ref} These distinctions explain why treatment cannot be fixed solely by the label “acute”.

**Original question.**

How would you treat a case of Scarlet Fever? What affections are wont to arise in this disease; and what means would you adopt in counteracting them ?

=== Solution

**Treat <scarlet fever> as an infectious illness, support fluid and food intake, and distinguish local suppuration, invasive infection and delayed immune complications.** The severity of the throat disease and the general condition matter more than the brilliance of the rash.

In 1866, reasonable supportive care meant separation from susceptible contacts, attentive nursing, a comfortable ventilated room, rest and food and fluids that could be swallowed. Severe throat pain, difficulty swallowing, increasing neck swelling, breathing difficulty, marked prostration or altered awareness required repeated examination. Convalescence also needed observation of the <urine> and swelling. Historical attempts to drive out a rash by excessive heat, aggressive purging or stimulants did not establish a way to remove the cause. Renal complications after apparently successful recovery were already recognized: a contemporary digest discusses delayed <albuminuria> after the apparent convalescence from <scarlet fever>.<Half-yearly abstract of the medical sciences (July–December 1866)>[Section on albuminuria during convalescence from scarlet fever and other eruptive diseases; report of 29 September 1866]{ref}

**Modern correction: <scarlet fever> is associated with toxin-producing group A <streptococcal infection>, and appropriate testing and <antibiotic> treatment replace supposed rash-specific remedies.** A compatible illness may be confirmed by a rapid antigen test, molecular test or throat <microbiological culture>. <Penicillin> or <amoxicillin> is a usual treatment for confirmed disease; allergy and local resistance affect alternatives. Supportive fluid intake and appropriate relief of pain remain useful. Infection-control measures and the applicable exclusion interval after treatment reduce transmission.<Clinical Guidance for Scarlet Fever by CDC>[Testing and diagnosis; Treatment; Prevention]{ref}

The complications require different responses. <Otitis media>, cervical <lymphadenitis> and a <peritonsillar abscess> or <retropharyngeal abscess> result from spread of <infection>. New focal pain or swelling calls for examination; an <abscess> may require drainage as well as <antibiotics>. Airway compromise, profound illness or signs of <sepsis> require urgent hospital care, rather than simply changing a gargle. These complications are distinct from <acute rheumatic fever> and <post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis>, which arise through the response to the preceding <infection>.<Clinical Guidance for Scarlet Fever by CDC>[Complications, suppurative complications and nonsuppurative sequelae]{ref}

For the renal complication, look for dark or scanty <urine>, <haematuria>, <oedema> and <hypertension>, including after the throat and rash have improved. Assess <kidney> function and fluid balance. Treatment addresses <hypertension> and fluid excess; severe renal complications require specialist management. <Antibiotics> eradicate persisting group A <streptococcal infection> and reduce its spread, but are not proved to prevent every case of <post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis>. When <acute rheumatic fever> occurs, assess for <carditis>, treat the inflammatory manifestations and any <heart failure>, eradicate residual <streptococcal infection> and arrange appropriate secondary <antibiotic prophylaxis>. Prevention must not be reduced to waiting for swollen <anatomical joints> or obvious heart disease.<Clinical Guidelines for Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis by CDC>[Clinical features; Treatment; Prevention, insufficient evidence for antimicrobial prevention of PSGN]{ref}<Clinical Guidance for Acute Rheumatic Fever by CDC>[Manifestations, subclinical carditis and prevention]{ref} Thus “counteracting the complications” means identifying the actual process and its consequences, not applying one remedy to every late symptom.

**Original question.**

What line of treatment would you adopt if called to a case of well-marked Tetanus ?

=== Solution

**Established <tetanus> is an emergency: minimize provoked <muscle spasms>, protect breathing, manage the wound and provide sustained supportive care.** The source does not identify a wound, so none should be invented; a search for a plausible portal of entry is part of the assessment.

In historical practice, a quiet, darkened environment and gentle handling aimed to reduce reflex provocation of painful <muscle spasms>. <Trismus> made oral feeding difficult, while generalized rigidity and <opisthotonus> could threaten breathing and exhaust the person. A wound or retained material needed inspection and appropriate local care. Nutrition and fluid support were essential because the illness could outlast a single dramatic paroxysm. Forcefully attempting to open the jaws or repeatedly provoking <muscle spasms> to “test” them would add danger.

Period treatment tried to suppress the motor manifestations with <opium>, <chloroform> and other sedating remedies. The literature contains reports of apparent success, including a report published in November 1866 describing traumatic <tetanus>. A single survival cannot establish that its mixture of interventions neutralized the cause, especially when case severity and the natural course varied.<A Case of Traumatic Tetanus, with Anaesthesia of the Trunk and Upper Extremities, Treated Successfully (1866)>[pp. 336–337, clinical record and treatment; period evidence, not a modern protocol]{ref} Apparent relaxation during <anaesthesia> also does not prove eradication of the process producing the <muscle spasms>.

**Modern correction: the problem is toxin-mediated loss of inhibitory control, so treating visible <muscle spasms> alone is incomplete.** <Clostridium tetani> in a suitable wound can produce <tetanospasmin>. Treatment includes <tetanus immune globulin> to neutralize toxin that has not yet bound to neural tissue, wound cleaning and removal of devitalized tissue, and appropriate <antibiotics>. The immune globulin does not reverse toxin already bound at <nerve> endings, which explains the need for prolonged supportive care despite prompt treatment.<Clinical Care of Tetanus by CDC>[TIG; wound management; antibiotic therapy]{ref}

Sedation and muscle-relaxing therapy control <muscle spasms>, with <airway> management and assisted <pulmonary ventilation> when needed. Marked autonomic instability may require additional intensive care. Feeding, hydration and prevention of complications must continue during recovery. **Clinical recovery from <tetanus> does not confer dependable immunity: vaccination is still required.**<Clinical Care of Tetanus by CDC>[Control muscle spasms; Maintain an airway; Vaccination during recovery]{ref} For established disease, prophylactic wound advice alone is therefore insufficient, and neither historical <chloroform> inhalation nor an opiate mixture substitutes for the modern treatment bundle.

**Original question.**

Comment on the uses of Opium.

=== Solution

**<Opium> was chiefly used to relieve pain, procure sleep, suppress troublesome <cough> and restrain <diarrhoea>; each effect can relieve suffering while also creating a hazard.** It is a mixture of <alkaloids>, not a single standardized modern drug. Preparations such as <laudanum> cannot be treated as interchangeable volumes of “a sedative”. <Morphine> and <codeine> account for important pharmacological effects, but historical extracts varied.

For pain, the useful objective is relief sufficient for rest and function. A person exhausted by severe pain may sleep after analgesia; that is different from using ever-increasing sedation to suppress an unexplained illness. In an 1866 explanation, <opium> might therefore accompany treatment of painful inflammatory disease or severe colic. It could palliate suffering even when no curative treatment existed. It did not follow that a quieter person had recovered from <pericarditis>, obstruction or <infection>.

For <cough>, reducing an exhausting, unproductive symptom was the historical aim. But suppressing a protective <cough> where clearance of secretions or maintenance of breathing is already impaired can be harmful. For <diarrhoea>, slowing intestinal transit reduces the number of stools; it does not restore lost fluid or eliminate an infectious cause. The modern pharmacological description of <opium tincture> specifically links its antidiarrhoeal effect to altered <intestinal motility> and greater absorption of <water> from intestinal contents.<Opium tincture in the NCI Drug Dictionary>[Definition and mechanism as an antidiarrhoeal agent]{ref} Reduced output is therefore not by itself proof that the underlying disease is improving.

Historical uses also extended to spasmodic disorders and states called nervous excitement. These broad categories mixed different causes. A drug producing drowsiness could conceal deterioration, and neither <tetanus> nor <alcohol withdrawal> becomes adequately treated merely because the person is less visibly agitated. The contemporary medical debate openly questioned when <opium> was useful in such different conditions as <delirium tremens>, <dysentery> and <cholera>.<Introductory Address at the Public Opening of the Medical Session, 1866–67 by William Tennant Gairdner>[p. 294, disputed indications for opium in delirium tremens, dysentery and cholera]{ref}

**Modern correction: use a defined drug for a defined indication, with monitoring of benefit and toxicity.** Properly prescribed <opioids> remain important for selected severe pain and palliation; crude <opium> is not a general remedy for sleeplessness, agitation or an undiagnosed abdomen. Major hazards include <respiratory depression>, excessive sedation, <nausea>, <constipation>, physical dependence and harmful interactions with other depressant drugs. Extra care is needed with renal or hepatic impairment. <Gastrointestinal obstruction>, particularly <paralytic ileus>, and significant respiratory compromise can preclude routine opioid use; the details depend on the drug and clinical setting.<Morphine by MedlinePlus>[Important warning; precautions and adverse effects]{ref}<Morphine Sulfate prescribing information (FDA)>[Contraindications; respiratory depression, gastrointestinal effects and special-risk groups]{ref} In sustained palliative treatment, review analgesia and adverse effects and anticipate <constipation>, rather than waiting for it to become severe.<Palliative care for adults: strong opioids for pain relief by NICE>[CG140, dose review, renal or hepatic impairment and recommendations 1.1.18–1.1.20 on laxatives]{ref}

**Original question.**

What precautions against relapse would you recommend to an artisan cured of renal dropsy ?

=== Solution

**Disappearance of renal <oedema> is not proof that the <kidney> disease has resolved. Reassess recovery, make the return to work gradual and address actual exposures and recurrent illness.** “Renal dropsy” is a clinical description, not one modern histological diagnosis. It could accompany an acute nephritic illness or persist as part of more chronic disease; the question does not establish which.

A period answer would warn against returning immediately from convalescence to prolonged wet work, chilling, exhaustion and poorly supported living conditions. Dry clothing, suitable warmth, adequate food and rest, avoidance of excess <ethanol>, and opportunities to change out of wet clothes were practical recommendations. The intended rationale was to reduce strains thought to precipitate another renal attack. This advice should be labelled historical: cold exposure is not a complete modern explanation for <glomerulonephritis>, and an artisan's occupation does not establish any particular toxic exposure.

The important transferable precaution is continued observation. Re-examine for swelling, breathlessness, changes in <urine> output, persistent <albuminuria> and the return of constitutional illness. A nineteenth-century examination could include testing <urine> for <protein> and examining its sediment, rather than declaring cure from the appearance of the face or legs. Contemporary reporting of delayed <albuminuria> after apparent recovery from an eruptive illness illustrates the reason for follow-up, without proving that every later abnormality is a relapse of the same disease.<Half-yearly abstract of the medical sciences (July–December 1866)>[Section on delayed albuminuria in convalescence; limitations of apparent clinical recovery]{ref}

**Modern correction: the prevention plan follows the actual renal diagnosis and objective recovery.** Assess <blood pressure>, <serum creatinine>, estimated <glomerular filtration rate> and urinary <albumin> or <protein> as appropriate, and compare serial results. Some people recover completely from an acute illness; others need surveillance and treatment for persistent <chronic kidney disease>. Ongoing <hypertension>, substantial <albuminuria> or declining renal function requires attention rather than merely warmer clothing. Medicines and supplements should be reviewed for nephrotoxicity, and <nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs> require particular caution in susceptible people.<Chronic kidney disease: assessment and management by NICE>[NG203, assessment and monitoring; medicines affecting kidney function; blood-pressure control and proteinuria]{ref}

For work, establish the actual demands: heavy exertion, inability to drink or take breaks, heat, wet conditions and contact with potentially nephrotoxic materials. Adjust duties to the degree of recovery, with occupational assessment where needed. Do not assume a toxin or impose permanent unemployment merely from the word “artisan”. Fluid or salt restrictions and drugs should be individualized to the renal condition; indiscriminate restriction or repeated purging can worsen depletion. The aim is **sustainable recovery with follow-up**, not a claim that one garment or tonic guarantees against relapse.

**Original question.**

What conditions favour the formation of Carbuncles ? How do you treat them ?

=== Solution

**A <carbuncle> is a deep infection involving connected adjacent hair follicles. Local damage, bacterial carriage and impaired host defence can favour its development; a drainable focus needs drainage.** It is more extensive than a single superficial follicular pustule.

Relevant predisposing circumstances include <diabetes>, friction or injury at the skin, close contact or shared contaminated articles, and impaired immunity. Ask about recurrent <skin> infections, medication and systemic health. A lesion on the nape in someone with <diabetes> is a familiar pattern, but the source supplies neither its site nor a test result, so neither may be asserted as an observation. Evaluate for <fever>, spreading <cellulitis>, severe pain and <sepsis>, and consider whether a lesion labelled a “carbuncle” is actually another disorder.<Skin and soft tissue infection guideline by IDSA>[Purulent SSTIs; Furuncles and Carbuncles, anatomy, common organism and association with diabetes]{ref}

In historical language, “debility”, age, poor nourishment and a disordered constitutional state were often invoked. Some of these descriptions might correspond to a recognizable predisposition; others were vague explanations after the event. Period local treatments included poulticing, incisions to relieve tension and evacuate dead or suppurating tissue, and attempts at destruction with caustics. A November 1866 letter on boils and <carbuncles> is evidence that both their origin and treatment were actively disputed. Its proposed “parasiticide” treatment must not be read as proof of a fungal cause or as a modern instruction to apply corrosive chemicals.<Treatment of Boils and Carbuncles by James Startin>[British Medical Journal, 10 November 1866, pp. 534–535, historical local treatment and proposed parasitic interpretation]{ref}

**Modern correction: the usual process is bacterial, commonly involving <Staphylococcus aureus>, and a fungal theory inferred from apparent response to a caustic is inadequate.** Assess for a drainable collection; incision and drainage is the core treatment of a <carbuncle>. <Microbiological culture> of <pus> can guide therapy, especially in difficult or recurrent cases. Systemic <antibiotics> are added according to illness severity, surrounding infection, host factors and the suspected or demonstrated organism. Severe illness requires escalation, not delay while waiting for the lesion to “ripen”.<Skin and soft tissue infection guideline by IDSA>[Recommendations for incision and drainage, culture and adjunctive antibiotics; impaired host defences and systemic illness]{ref}

Address <diabetes> or other identified predispositions, dress draining lesions and reduce transmission through hand hygiene and avoiding shared personal articles. Repeated lesions call for reassessment of diagnosis and sources of reinfection. A destructive historical incision or caustic is not equivalent to controlled drainage with appropriate analgesia, infection control and follow-up.

**Original question.**

What circumstances contra-indicate the use of Chloroform ?

=== Solution

**For an 1866 discussion, distinguish conditions demanding special caution from a universal prohibition. Modern correction goes further: <chloroform> is not an acceptable routine surgical anaesthetic.** Its toxicity cannot be removed by choosing an apparently healthy person.

The historical inquiry would examine the <heart>, <lungs>, general strength and the nature of the proposed procedure. A failing circulation, suspected serious myocardial degeneration, great exhaustion or <blood loss>, and severe respiratory impairment gave strong reasons to avoid or postpone a nonessential exposure. A full <stomach>, obstruction of the <airway>, difficulty maintaining breathing, or an operation threatening to contaminate the <airway> created additional hazards. Acute intoxication or other depressant drugs could complicate administration. The balance depended partly on whether the operation was essential and on what alternative method of relieving pain was available.

It would nevertheless be historically inaccurate to declare that the contemporary profession unanimously regarded all <heart> disease as an absolute contraindication. The 1864 committee's report said that <heart> disease in general did not automatically preclude surgical use, while specifically calling for care over fatty degeneration. It also discussed the difficulty of recognizing that condition during life. Its treatment of mouth and throat operations was likewise more qualified than a universal ban.<Abstract of the Report of the Committee on Chloroform (1864)>[Uses and contraindications in surgical practice; heart disease, fatty degeneration and mouth or throat operations]{ref} This is a record of the committee's judgement, not endorsement of its safety conclusion.

The delivery conditions also matter. An uncontrolled vapour concentration, failure to ensure air entry, lack of a dedicated competent observer, or inability to respond to loss of breathing and circulation makes an already hazardous intervention less defensible. Such circumstances concern how the drug is used rather than a disease-specific contraindication. The period distinction between direct circulatory failure and respiratory failure explains why watching only one sign was inadequate.<Abstract of the Report of the Committee on Chloroform (1864)>[Physiological action, fatal exposure and administration precautions]{ref}

**Modern correction: <chloroform> can depress the <central nervous system>, disrupt cardiac function and cause delayed hepatic and renal injury.** Its former use as an <anaesthetic> does not make it a current option. Modern anaesthetic assessment still considers cardiovascular and respiratory reserve, <aspiration> risk, other drugs and the proposed procedure, but uses contemporary agents, monitoring and trained personnel.<Chloroform: toxicological overview by UKHSA>[Acute toxicity; central nervous system depression; delayed hepatic and renal effects]{ref}<Chloroform by the US Environmental Protection Agency>[Acute effects; cardiac effects and discontinuation as a surgical anaesthetic]{ref} In particular, absence of obvious <heart> disease is not evidence that a <chloroform> exposure is safe.

**Original question.**

What are the uses of Digitalis ?

=== Solution

**The sound historical indication was selected dropsical states associated with disordered cardiac action; the modern uses of particular <cardiac glycosides> are narrower and explicitly rhythm-dependent.** A <digitalis preparation> was not simply a drug for every slow urinary flow or rapid <pulse>.

Before 1866, <William Withering>'s account had already demonstrated both the possible relief of dropsy and the danger of careless use. He recorded successes and failures and warned about <nausea>, <vomiting>, visual disturbance and excessive slowing of the <pulse>. These observations support careful assessment of effect and toxicity, not a method of administering foxglove until poisoning occurs.<An Account of the Foxglove and some of its Medical Uses by William Withering>[1785, Preface; Effects, Rules, and Cautions]{ref} The historical assessment would follow breathing, swelling, <pulse>, <urine> and general condition. Increased <urine> and reduced <oedema> could represent relief while the original cardiac disorder persisted.

Calling the drug a cardiac “sedative” from the slowing of the <pulse> could obscure a different effect on contraction. Likewise, apparent diuresis did not establish it as a universal remedy for renal dropsy. Period applications extended into febrile or haemorrhagic illnesses, but such claims should not be promoted into modern indications merely because the <pulse> changed. The problem is to distinguish treatment of a useful physiological target from suppression of a sign.

**Modern correction: <digoxin> increases myocardial contractility and slows conduction through the <atrioventricular node>.** In selected <heart failure> it can improve symptoms and reduce hospitalization. A randomized, placebo-controlled trial in people with chronic <heart failure> and normal <sinus rhythm> found fewer hospitalizations but no reduction in overall mortality.<The Effect of Digoxin on Mortality and Morbidity in Patients with Heart Failure by the Digitalis Investigation Group>[1997, main trial, Methods, Results and Conclusions]{ref} It can also control resting ventricular rate in selected adults with chronic <atrial fibrillation>. It is not a general treatment for a fast <pulse>, and the presence of an accessory conduction pathway can make its use hazardous. <Ventricular fibrillation> is a contraindication.<Digoxin prescribing information on DailyMed>[Indications 1.1 and 1.3; Contraindications; warning 5.1; mechanism of action 12.1]{ref}

The margin between useful and toxic exposure is small. Renal function, age, body composition, interactions and <electrolyte> disturbances influence risk. Monitor the clinical response, <electrocardiogram>, relevant laboratory results and drug concentrations when indicated. <Nausea>, <vomiting>, visual symptoms, marked <bradycardia> or new <cardiac arrhythmias> should raise concern for toxicity, not be treated as confirmation that the drug is beginning to work.<Digoxin prescribing information on DailyMed>[Sections 2.1 and 2.5; 5.2–5.3; 8.6, renal impairment]{ref} Historical leaf or tincture preparations must therefore not be substituted for a modern standardized preparation, or converted into modern doses from a shared name alone.

**Original question.**

What are the signs of an impacted Gallstone? What is to be done when it is supposed that a Patient is suffering from that cause ?

=== Solution

**The signs depend on the obstructed site: a stone in the cystic duct, the common <bile duct> and the outlet shared with the pancreatic duct can produce different syndromes.** “An impacted <gallstone>” is not enough anatomical information to choose a treatment.

A typical pain pattern is severe upper abdominal pain, often in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium, sometimes radiating to the back or shoulder, with <nausea> and <vomiting>. Despite the term “colic”, the pain can remain steady during an attack. Persistent focal tenderness with <fever> suggests <acute cholecystitis> rather than an uncomplicated transient obstruction. Cystic-duct obstruction does not necessarily obstruct hepatic <bile> flow and therefore need not produce <jaundice>.

A stone obstructing the common <bile duct> can produce <jaundice>, dark <urine> and pale stools. <Fever> or rigors with <jaundice> and biliary pain raises concern for <acute cholangitis>, while shock or altered awareness indicates serious illness. Obstruction at the distal outflow can precipitate <acute pancreatitis>, often with prominent pain radiating to the back and <vomiting>. These are patterns to investigate, not findings supplied by the examination; absence of a full classical combination does not exclude dangerous obstruction.<Gallstones by NIDDK>[Symptoms and causes; signs of complications and obstruction]{ref}

An 1866 clinician would try to establish the pain's origin, look for <jaundice>, observe the <urine> and stools, relieve severe pain, support fluid intake and watch for persistent obstruction or inflammation. Warm external applications and an opiate were among the symptomatic measures of the period. Passage of a recovered stone could strengthen the diagnosis, but failure to find one in the stool did not disprove it, and temporary relief did not demonstrate duct clearance. Neither a purgative nor an asserted stone-dissolving mixture could be assumed to free an impacted duct. These are reconstructions of the diagnostic and palliative aims available before modern duct imaging and endoscopic treatment, not a current management recipe.

**Modern correction: investigate promptly and relieve consequential obstruction, rather than treating the pain alone.** Obtain appropriate examination, <liver function tests> and <ultrasonography>. If suspicion of common-duct stones remains because of abnormal tests or duct dilatation despite no stone being shown, <magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography> can clarify the ducts; <endoscopic ultrasonography> is an additional option when needed. Evaluate for <acute pancreatitis> and other causes of an acute abdomen. Relief of pain, fluid support and treatment of associated illness proceed alongside investigation.<Gallstone disease: diagnosis and management by NICE>[CG188, recommendations on liver function tests, ultrasound, MRCP and endoscopic ultrasound]{ref}

Symptomatic gallbladder stones generally lead to consideration of <laparoscopic cholecystectomy>; early surgery is recommended for suitable <acute cholecystitis>. Common-duct stones require duct clearance, endoscopically or surgically as appropriate.<Gallstone disease: diagnosis and management by NICE>[CG188, managing gallbladder stones and common bile duct stones]{ref} **<Acute cholangitis> requires urgent treatment of <infection> and biliary decompression.** <Antibiotics> and resuscitation do not remove an obstructing stone. Endoscopic drainage is generally preferred when feasible; stone removal may accompany it if the person's condition permits, while an unstable person may need decompression before a more extensive procedure.<ASGE guideline on the management of cholangitis>[2021, recommendations on endoscopic drainage, timing and drainage with stone removal versus decompression alone]{ref} This is why prolonged pain with <jaundice> or <fever> cannot safely be managed merely by repeating historical analgesic mixtures.
