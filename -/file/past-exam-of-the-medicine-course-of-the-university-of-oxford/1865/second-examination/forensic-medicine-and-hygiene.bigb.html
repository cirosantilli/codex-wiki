= Forensic medicine and hygiene

== 1
{scope}

**Original question.**

State the symptoms of poisoning by Sulphuric Acid, Phosphorus, and Strychnia.

=== Solution

**<Sulfuric acid> produces corrosive injury, <white phosphorus> can cause early gastrointestinal illness followed by delayed systemic poisoning, and <strychnine> produces painful, stimulus-sensitive <muscle spasms>.** The three patterns should be distinguished rather than treated as one syndrome. “Sulphuric acid” and “strychnia” are the historical spellings used in the examination.

With swallowed <sulfuric acid>, the principal symptoms are immediate burning pain in the mouth, throat and upper abdomen; excessive salivation; painful or difficult swallowing; and repeated <vomiting>, which may contain <blood>. Injury to the <larynx> can cause swelling, altered voice and obstruction of the <airway>. Severe gastrointestinal damage can lead to <hemorrhage>, perforation and circulatory <shock>. The extent of injury depends on concentration, quantity and contact, so no single appearance of the mouth measures the whole injury. Inhalation of acid mist principally causes irritation, coughing, chest tightness and difficulty breathing; severe exposure can damage the <lungs>. These are effects of a corrosive substance at its contact sites, not the symptoms of a selectively acting convulsant.<Sulphuric Acid: Toxicological Overview by the UK Health Security Agency>[“Health effects of acute or single exposure”, inhalation and ingestion: corrosive burns, dysphagia, vomiting, airway injury and systemic complications]{ref}

For <phosphorus poisoning>, the relevant toxic material is <white phosphorus>, also called yellow phosphorus. It should not be confused with ordinary dietary <phosphate> or assumed to have the same properties as <red phosphorus>. After ingestion there may be burning discomfort, thirst, nausea, <vomiting>, abdominal pain and <diarrhoea>. A garlic-like odour or luminescent vomit can be a clue, but their absence does not exclude exposure. Severe cases can collapse early. More deceptively, symptoms may improve for an interval before renewed illness, <jaundice>, tender enlargement of the <liver>, abnormal bleeding, <kidney> injury, confusion and <coma>. This sequence reflects delayed systemic injury, especially <liver failure>; apparent recovery from the first gastric symptoms is not proof that poisoning has ended.<White Phosphorus: Systemic Agent by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[“Signs/Symptoms”, ingestion and delayed effects: gastrointestinal phase, apparent improvement and subsequent multiorgan injury]{ref}

Exposure of the skin or eyes to burning <white phosphorus> can cause severe local burns and also systemic toxicity. Long-term industrial exposure with destructive jaw disease is a different presentation from the acute swallowed-poison pattern. Current institutional toxicology distinguishes these routes and time courses; an 1865 symptom description should not be stretched into a claim that all substances containing <phosphorus> behave alike.<White Phosphorus by the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry>[ToxFAQs, health effects: ingestion, local burns and chronic occupational jaw disease]{ref}

With <strychnine poisoning>, apprehension, restlessness and muscular stiffness are followed by repeated, intensely painful generalized <muscle spasms>. Slight touch, noise, light or movement can provoke an attack. The limbs become rigid and the trunk may arch backwards in <opisthotonus>; jaw closure and facial contraction can give <trismus> and <risus sardonicus>. The <respiratory muscles> may be fixed during an attack, causing difficulty breathing and <cyanosis>. Between attacks there may be substantial relaxation, while consciousness is initially preserved. The person can therefore be lucid and terrified of another attack. Exhaustion, overheating and ultimately <respiratory failure> can follow.<Strychnine by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention>[“How it works” and “Signs and symptoms”: painful spasms, arching, initial consciousness and respiratory failure]{ref}

Repeated violent contractions may produce <hyperthermia>, <acidosis> and <rhabdomyolysis>, with dark urine and <acute kidney injury>. Initial awareness is characteristic, but advanced <hypoxia> can abolish it. The pattern differs from the immediate destructive pain of <sulfuric acid> and the often delayed organ injury of <white phosphorus>. The modern account of inhibitory <glycine receptor> blockade explains the stimulus-sensitive contractions, but that receptor mechanism is later knowledge, not something to attribute to an 1865 candidate.<Strychnine: Biotoxin by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[“Signs/Symptoms”, ingestion: complications of spasms]{ref}<Strychnine Poisoning by the California Poison Control System>[Discussion, glycine inhibition and clinical comparison]{ref}

**Original question.**

What are the symptoms of poisoning by Carbonic Acid, under what circumstances does it usually occur, and how would you prevent them ?

=== Solution

**“Carbonic acid” in this question means gaseous <carbon dioxide>, not liquid acid poisoning. Dangerous accumulation causes <hypercapnia>, impaired consciousness and potentially death; prevention requires controlling its production and providing effective fresh-air replacement.** At high concentrations, displacement of <oxygen> can contribute, but lack of <oxygen> is not the only mechanism.

Early symptoms can include headache, dizziness, discomfort, restlessness, sweating, rapid or laboured <breathing> and an increased pulse. Greater exposure can cause confusion, loss of coordination, profound drowsiness, collapse, <convulsions>, <coma> and death. In a heavily contaminated space, collapse may occur before a person can recognize the danger or escape. <Carbon dioxide> is colourless and odourless, so absence of an offensive smell provides no reassurance.<Carbon Dioxide by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[Pocket Guide, physical description, exposure route, symptoms and target organs]{ref}

The physiological point matters. Increased inspired <carbon dioxide> impairs elimination of the body's own <carbon dioxide> and alters the acid–base state of the <blood>. A hazardous atmosphere can therefore remain dangerous even when enough <oxygen> is present to sustain a flame. The modern institutional discussion of <carbon dioxide> fire suppression distinguishes the gas's direct physiological effects from mere dilution of <oxygen>.<Carbon Dioxide as a Fire Suppressant: Examining the Risks by the United States Environmental Protection Agency>[“Life Safety Considerations of Carbon Dioxide”: physiological effects and oxygen concentration]{ref}

The characteristic settings are poorly ventilated spaces in which <carbon dioxide> is continuously generated or accumulates: brewery or wine-fermentation vessels, cellars, pits, wells, vaults and places containing decomposing organic material. Fermentation and <cellular respiration> generate the gas; combustion is another source. In a badly ventilated occupied room, human <breathing> and unflued fires or lamps add to the burden. A dangerous combustion atmosphere may also contain <carbon monoxide>, which is a separate poison and requires a separate assessment. <Carbon dioxide> is denser than <air>, so low or poorly swept spaces deserve attention; currents and mixing mean that one cannot assume a sharp, motionless layer with safe air above it.<Carbon Dioxide by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[Gas density and properties]{ref}

For prevention, remove or enclose the source where practicable and maintain <building ventilation> that actually sweeps the occupied and low-lying spaces. Exhaust contaminated <air> to a safe place and bring replacement <air> from a clean source. Opening a small window elsewhere in a building does not establish an adequate supply at the worker's position. Fermentation vessels, pits and similar spaces should be arranged for inspection and cleaning without entry where possible.

The nineteenth-century emphasis on fresh air and caution before entering such spaces remains sound. Current practice adds atmosphere testing for <carbon dioxide>, <oxygen> deficiency and other relevant gases, together with controlled entry and rescue arrangements. A burning candle is an inadequate safety test: flame behaviour neither measures <carbon dioxide> nor rules out a different toxic gas. A rescuer who enters an unknown atmosphere without appropriate protection can become a second casualty. Trained rescue with suitable supplied breathing air and retrieval arrangements must be distinguished from simply holding one's breath and climbing in.<Permit-required Confined Spaces by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration>[29 CFR 1910.146, atmosphere evaluation, ventilation and rescue provisions; cited as a present technical comparison, not English law in 1865]{ref}

For ordinary rooms and wards, prevention also means limiting overcrowding, maintaining reliable air inlets and outlets, and removing products of combustion through proper flues. These principles concern continuing air exchange: a large room merely delays contamination if its <air> is never renewed. They explain why the following ward question asks separately about cubic space and the rate of change of <air>.

**Original question.**

How would you ascertain whether the dead body of a child found in the water had ever breathed ?

=== Solution

**Use a documented examination of the whole body and <lungs>, with a <lung flotation test> as corroboration rather than an infallible verdict. Previous <breathing>, <live birth> and death by drowning are three different propositions.** The question asks how to investigate; it does not supply a body on which to report an actual positive or negative result.

First establish the apparent age and development of the child, the condition of the remains and any evidence about birth, recovery from the <water> or attempted resuscitation. If the child is evidently older, independent evidence of survival after birth may settle much of the issue. In a newly born child, the forensic problem is harder. Record decomposition, injuries and any manipulation before opening the chest. Avoid inflating or unnecessarily compressing the <lungs> before their original state has been documented.

On opening the chest, observe the position, extent, colour and consistency of the <lungs>. Unexpanded newborn <lungs> are generally compact and do not fill the chest as expanded <lungs> do. Aerated regions may be more expanded, spongy and crepitant. Cut surfaces may show small air bubbles. These observations support a judgement about aeration, but disease, collapse and decomposition complicate their interpretation. Examine both sides and different regions, because a few breaths may expand only part of the <lungs>.

For the historical <lung flotation test>, place the removed thoracic organs in <water>, then examine the <lungs> separately, their lobes and representative small pieces. Record what floats and what sinks at each stage, using a vessel deep enough for free flotation. The physical principle is reduction of average <density> by retained gas. Testing only a whole organ can miss a small aerated region. The test belongs to the pre-1865 forensic toolkit: the contemporary encyclopedia discussed lung flotation and explicitly recognized both artificial inflation and <putrefaction> as alternative explanations for floating.<Medical Jurisprudence in the eighth edition of Encyclopædia Britannica>[Part II, Infanticide, discussion of lung inflation, flotation, artificial insufflation and putrefaction]{ref}

There are two main classes of error. **Floating can result from gas introduced by attempted <artificial respiration> or generated after death, so it does not by itself prove spontaneous breathing.** Look for decomposition elsewhere and compare the distribution of gas in the <lungs> and other organs. A floating piece of <liver> can support the suspicion of generalized gas formation, but a sinking comparison organ does not guarantee that all pulmonary gas arose during life. Historical claims that squeezing a fragment always distinguishes breathed air from artificially introduced air were too confident; compression is not a decisive separator of the causes of aeration.<Forensic Examinations of Newborns 1910–1912>[Original historical case-record investigation, discussion of theoretical flotation-test limitations and their application in practice]{ref}

**Sinking does not establish that the child never breathed.** A few ineffective breaths, limited expansion, or heavy, fluid-filled or diseased lung tissue can leave the material tested denser than <water>. A later prospective autopsy study reported four false-negative tests in which <lungs> sank despite prior life reported by medical staff. That observation demonstrates why a negative result cannot stand alone; the study's overall performance in its own material is not a universal accuracy guarantee for decomposed bodies recovered from <water>.<Is the Lung Floating Test a Valuable Tool or Obsolete? A Prospective Autopsy Study>[2013, abstract and results: four false negatives and the limit on interpreting a negative test]{ref}

Use the whole examination and independent evidence to reach a qualified conclusion. Feeding, healing changes or reliable observations of the living child may support survival, but they are different evidence from lung aeration. A child can show signs of life without establishing effective <breathing>, and an inflated <lung> does not establish a particular cause of death. Immersion may have occurred before or after death; neither flotation nor the body's location alone proves drowning. Where decomposition or attempted resuscitation prevents a confident distinction, report the limit rather than manufacture certainty.

**Original question.**

What amount of cubic space is required for each bed in a hospital ward? How do you ascertain the rate of change in the Air in a ward? and state the ordinary impurities which may exist in vitiated Hospital Air and the methods in use for examining them.

=== Solution

**A defensible period design answer for <hospital bed space> is roughly 1,600 cubic feet, or 45 cubic metres, per bed in a properly ventilated ordinary ward. This is a volume allowance, not a ventilation rate or a universal legal minimum.** In the 1863 third edition of <Notes on Hospitals by Florence Nightingale>, <Florence Nightingale> gives a twenty-patient ward measuring 80 by 25 by 16 feet, or alternatively 80 by 26 by 15 feet. These examples give 1,600 or 1,560 cubic feet per bed, respectively.<Notes on Hospitals by Florence Nightingale>[Third edition, 1863, p.67, “Principles of Hospital Construction”, example ward dimensions and cubic space]{ref}

For the first arrangement,
$$
\boxed{\frac{V}{N}=\frac{80\times25\times16}{20}=1600\ \mathrm{ft^3}\simeq45.3\ \mathrm{m^3}\text{ per bed}.}
$$
This is a cited design example, not a measurement of an unseen ward. Floor area, separation between beds, access for nursing, air distribution and the condition of the patients also matter. Simply adding height cannot compensate for crowding patients together or leaving stagnant zones around the beds. A modern hospital would apply its current design and infection-control requirements rather than treat the 1863 figure as sufficient for every clinical purpose.

To determine <hospital ward ventilation>, first measure the ward's internal volume $V$. Measure the area $A$ of each effective inlet or outlet and the mean normal <velocity> $\bar u$ across it, using an <anemometer> and several positions rather than one favourable point. The volume flow is $Q=A\bar u$, or the sum of the flows through independent openings of the same side of the balance. Do not add both the incoming and outgoing flow and call their sum new air. Reverse flow, leakage, changes of wind and temperature, and recirculation must be considered. Smoke may reveal direction and stagnant regions, but its visible movement alone is not a quantitative flow measurement. Modern institutional instrument guidance also explains why velocity must be sampled across a section; the simple area–velocity relation itself does not depend on a modern electronic instrument.<Measuring Mechanical Ventilation Airflow by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory>[“How to Calculate Airflow Rate from a Velocity Probe Measurement”: spatial sampling, mean velocity and suitable instruments]{ref}

With $Q$ expressed per hour, the mean <air changes per hour> are
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{Q}{V},\qquad q_{\text{bed}}=\frac{Q}{N}.}
$$
The quantities have different meanings: $V/N$ is space per bed, $Q/N$ is fresh-air supply per bed per hour, and $a$ is the nominal exchange rate. A system may move much recirculated <air> while supplying relatively little new outdoor <air>.

A second method is to follow the fall of a <tracer gas> after its source stops. Under a well-mixed, constant-flow model with constant outdoor concentration $C_o$ and no continuing internal generation,
$$
\frac{d(C-C_o)}{dt}=-\frac{Q}{V}(C-C_o),
\qquad
\boxed{a=\frac{\ln[(C_1-C_o)/(C_2-C_o)]}{t_2-t_1}.}
$$
Here time is in hours if $a$ is to be reported per hour. The formula follows by integrating the mass balance. Compare measurements at several positions and state the conditions. Poor mixing, continuing generation by occupants and changing wind invalidate a simple one-exponential interpretation. Current institutional guidance describes this decay method; the modern monitoring protocol is a later comparison, while the elementary balance explains what “rate of change” measures.<Air Change Rate: Input Parameter Protocol by the World Health Organization>[2020, overview and protocol: cessation of source, concentration decay, background and dependence on conditions]{ref}

The ordinary ward impurities to examine are excess <carbon dioxide> and <water vapour> from occupants, volatile material including <ammonia>, organic and inorganic dust, and potentially infectious <aerosols>. <Carbon monoxide> or irritant combustion gases may enter where heating or lighting is defective; drain-associated gases are a contingent additional hazard, not something necessarily present in every ward. Record occupancy, combustion sources, windows, sampling position, temperature and time, and take an outdoor comparison sample.

For <carbon dioxide>, collect a known volume of <air> in a calibrated vessel and expose it to a measured amount of standardized <barium hydroxide>. Absorption produces <barium carbonate>. Compare the remaining alkali with a reagent blank, excluding carbonate precipitate from an acid titration that would dissolve it. The consumed alkali gives the absorbed <carbon dioxide>; express that amount per original volume of <air>. A report from 1864 describes the method in 7–10 litre globes with a gravimetric comparison, establishing a procedure available before the examination.<Chemical News volume 9 (1864)>[p.80, “Note on the Amount of Carbonic Acid contained in the Air of Manchester”: baryta absorption and gravimetric check]{ref}

Estimate humidity with wet- and dry-bulb thermometers, or gravimetrically by collecting moisture in a weighed drying absorber from a measured volume of <air>. For volatile bases, pass a measured sample through dilute acid and determine the uptake, interpreting possible <ammonia> alongside interfering bases. Mere change of <litmus> is a screen, not a selective low-concentration quantitative assay. A modern example uses acid-treated collection medium and <ion chromatography>, with calibrated sampling and blanks; that later analytical specification must not be credited to 1865.<Ammonia by Ion Chromatography, Method 6016 by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health>[Sampling and measurement: collected ammonia, air volume, blanks and instrumental determination]{ref}

Collect suspended material on a suitable filter or surface and examine it by <microscopy>; a controlled gravimetric collection gives particle mass per volume. Such measurements do not by themselves identify pathogens or count every inhalable organism. Period assessments also used <potassium permanganate> decolourization to investigate oxidizable contamination. The 1865 chemical press already warned that other reducing substances could decolourize it, so a supposed “organic matter” result was nonspecific.<Chemical News volume 11 (1865)>[14 April, p.179, review of air in mines and confined places: limitations of permanganate as an organic-matter estimate]{ref}

Finally, report chemical cleanliness, particle loading and ventilation performance separately. They are related aspects of ward conditions, but one cannot derive freedom from infection from a low <carbon dioxide> result or a weak smell. Modern infection-control guidance similarly emphasizes that microbial air samples depend on collection method, location, timing and an interpretable sampling plan.<Environmental Sampling by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention>[Air sampling, comparison conditions, method limitations and interpretation of results]{ref}

**Original question.**

What are the diseases which are or are supposed to be caused by impure water ?

=== Solution

**The strongest period examples are <cholera>, <typhoid fever>, <dysentery> and other diarrhoeal illness associated with contaminated supplies. Other diseases were attributed to water on weaker grounds; the particular contaminant and route of exposure must be specified.** A chemical impurity, an infectious agent and an organism that uses water during its life cycle are different kinds of cause.

For an answer situated in 1865, <John Snow>'s work already provided evidence connecting <cholera> with swallowed material from an affected person's intestinal discharges, including contaminated <drinking water>. His 1855 second edition compared populations supplied with different waters, as well as investigating local outbreaks. The persuasive feature is an exposure comparison, not simply proximity to a bad smell. Neither a single chemical analysis nor the later course of an epidemic can by itself settle every link in transmission.<On the Mode of Communication of Cholera by John Snow>[Second edition, 1855, pp.55–98, comparison of water supplies and cholera mortality]{ref}

For <typhoid fever>, the association with sewage-contaminated wells or supplies should be explained through infectious excreta reaching food or drink. <William Budd>'s 1861 discussion placed the infectious material in the intestinal discharges and opposed spontaneous generation from ordinary decay. That contemporary reasoning gives a much more precise answer than saying that any foul <water> creates a fever. Modern knowledge identifies a specific <bacterium>, <Salmonella Typhi>, rather than an unspecified product of <putrefaction>.<Observations on Typhoid or Intestinal Fever: The Pythogenic Theory by William Budd>[1861, Part III, p.575: infectious discharges and rejection of spontaneous origin]{ref}

<Diarrhoea> and <dysentery> name clinical patterns with several possible causes. Sewage contamination can convey organisms producing them, but not every episode is a <waterborne disease>. Current authoritative guidance recognizes transmission through unsafe <drinking water> of <cholera>, <typhoid fever>, dysenteric and other diarrhoeal infections, <hepatitis A> and <polio>. The last two are present-day additions to the explanation, not an assertion that their viral agents were identified in 1865.<Drinking-water by the World Health Organization>[“Key facts” and “Water and health”: diseases associated with faecally contaminated supplies]{ref}

Parasites need the same care about route. <Guinea-worm disease> can follow swallowing infected tiny crustaceans in unsafe <drinking water>; it is not caused by a generic chemical property of stagnant <water>. That life-cycle account is modern corroboration. Other water-associated infections can be acquired through contact rather than drinking, and diseases carried by insects breeding near <water> form another category.<Dracunculiasis by the World Health Organization>[“Transmission, life cycle and incubation period”: ingestion of infected copepods in drinking water]{ref}

An important example of a nineteenth-century attribution is <goitre> and the developmental disorder then called “cretinism”. An 1861 review linked their distribution to lime-rich <drinking water> and treated this as a strong causal demonstration. This is evidence of what was supposed, not a conclusion to repeat as established medicine. Endemic <goitre> is closely associated with <iodine deficiency>, and severe deficiency during development can have profound neurological consequences; water hardness alone does not identify the relevant cause in a population.<The British Soldier in India in The British and Foreign Medico-Chirurgical Review (1861)>[pp.43–46, comparison of districts and the asserted lime-water explanation of goitre and cretinism]{ref}<Iodine by the National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements>[Health Professional Fact Sheet, “Iodine deficiency”: goitre and fetal neurodevelopmental consequences]{ref}

Similarly, <malaria> should not be explained as poisoning from a mouthful of stagnant <water>. Its ordinary transmission is through infected mosquitoes; aquatic breeding conditions can affect vector populations without making ingestion the route of infection. Modern public-health accounts distinguish such water-related vector exposure from enteric transmission.<Water, Sanitation and Health in Humanitarian Emergencies by the World Health Organization>[Risks of epidemics: enteric disease versus mosquito-borne disease associated with breeding sites]{ref}

There are also genuine noninfectious hazards. <Lead> can enter <drinking water> from plumbing and cause <lead poisoning>; other sources can introduce toxic <arsenic>. These are poisonings by particular substances, not evidence that every mineralized supply is harmful. Current guidance explicitly distinguishes microbial contamination from naturally occurring or infrastructure-derived chemicals.<Drinking-water by the World Health Organization>[“Water and health”: arsenic, fluoride and lead leaching from water-system components]{ref}

Thus the sound list is accompanied by a causal distinction: diseases transmitted by contaminated <water>, diseases merely associated with living near <water>, specific chemical poisonings, and historical claims requiring correction. Clear, pleasant-tasting <water> may transmit infection, while a conspicuous harmless mineral constituent may have nothing to do with an outbreak. Epidemiological comparison and investigation of the source are essential to deciding which relation is present.

**Original question.**

What are the best disinfectants for a sick room? How far are disinfectants of use? and state the principles on which those act which you enumerate.

=== Solution

**The best general measures for a sickroom are removal of the infectious source, cleaning and adequate <building ventilation>, with targeted <disinfection> of contaminated objects. A chemical that suppresses smell is not thereby a means of making occupied room air safe.** Choice of an agent depends on the material, the organism and the conditions of use.

This distinction is available within the historical period. In <Notes on Nursing by Florence Nightingale>, <Florence Nightingale> warned against reliance on fumigations and disinfecting fluids in place of removing the offending material and supplying fresh <air>. Its advice supplies a contemporary limit on indiscriminate chemical use.<Notes on Nursing by Florence Nightingale>[“Ventilation and Warming”, passage headed “Fumigations”: removal of the source rather than dependence on fumigating fluids]{ref}

An 1865 tract also rejected a universally applicable disinfectant and put removal and cleanliness first. Its further chemical and “miasm” claims require separate judgement.<Health Tract No.154: Disinfectants (1865)>[Opening paragraphs: source removal and burning sugar as odour masking]{ref}

<Chlorine> preparations, including chlorinated lime and chlorinated soda, were important period choices for contaminated material. In present terms their activity comes from reactive chlorine species, including <hypochlorous acid>, which damage microbial constituents through <oxidation> and related reactions. Use on suitable cleaned surfaces or receptacles is a different matter from releasing irritating gas about the patient. Abundant organic dirt consumes the active reagent, so effective contact with the object matters more than an impressive smell.<Chemical Disinfectants by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention>[“Chlorine and chlorine compounds”, mode of action, organic demand and practical limitations]{ref}

<Potassium permanganate> and related preparations were promoted as oxidizing purifiers. Consumption of oxidizable dirt or odour does not establish destruction of all infectious organisms. The 1865 tract lists the class; its claim of refreshing room air by liberating <oxygen> is a historical belief, not a substitute for ventilation.<Health Tract No.154: Disinfectants (1865)>[Final paragraph: hypochlorites and manganate preparations, including claims concerning miasm and oxygen]{ref}

<Zinc chloride> and <iron(II) sulfate> were used to suppress decomposition and offensive discharges. Neither altered smell nor acidity proves microbial destruction. <Charcoal> provides a contrast: adsorption can retain odorous substances without reliably disinfecting an object. The tract lists these agents, but listing them does not establish equal effectiveness against infection.<Health Tract No.154: Disinfectants (1865)>[Discussion of copperas, zinc chloride, chlorinated lime and charcoal; the mixing recipes and health claims are not adopted here]{ref}

<Heat disinfection> is valuable where the material can tolerate it: washable linen or utensils can receive a controlled hot process after suitable cleaning. The process must reach the whole object; a warm exterior does not show that a bulky interior received the same exposure. This is distinct from heating a room containing a patient. Burning a disposable contaminated object destroys the material altogether, but is not a general prescription for all bedding, equipment or room contents.

The usefulness of chemical <disinfection> is therefore substantial when applied correctly to a defined source, and limited when used indiscriminately. Remove excreta and soiled linen promptly, clean spills and reusable objects, protect attendants from transferring contamination, and separate flows of clean and contaminated material. Concentration, exposure time, temperature, compatibility and the amount of organic soil affect success. Modern guidance explicitly explains the shielding effect of soil and consumption of germicide by organic contamination.<Factors Affecting the Efficacy of Disinfection and Sterilization by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention>[“Organic and inorganic matter”, “Concentration and potency” and “Duration of exposure”]{ref}

For the room itself, clean accessible surfaces and provide suitable air replacement. Controlled treatment of an empty room or an article is not the same intervention as spreading a pungent vapour in an occupied sickroom. A tolerable smell does not demonstrate efficacy, and an intolerable concentration can injure the patient and staff. Current guidance retains specific roles for disinfectants while emphasizing the separate importance of <building ventilation> against infectious <aerosols>.<Ventilation to Reduce the Spread of Respiratory Infections by the UK Health Security Agency>[Indoor air and infection: fresh-air supply and ventilation as a distinct control]{ref}

Consequently, **disinfect the contaminated material where a suitable process can act; do not use deodorization as a substitute for source removal, cleaning or fresh air**. The value and the limits of the agents depend on what they actually do to the source of infection.
