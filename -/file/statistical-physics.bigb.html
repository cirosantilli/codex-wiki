= Statistical physics
{wiki}

<Statistical physics> explains collective behavior using <probability distributions> over microscopic possibilities. Its central surprise is that incomplete microscopic knowledge can still produce precise predictions for <internal energy>, <entropy> and <heat capacity>. The bridge is <statistical mechanics>: specify the available <microstates>, assign their <probabilities>, then compute the <expected values> of measured quantities.

A <two-level system> already displays the idea. Warming it makes its higher <energy> more likely; its <heat capacity> first rises, then falls as the two <probabilities> approach equality. With two distinguishable units, merely counting <microstates> explains why the middle <energy> has twice the <state degeneracy>. These small examples expose the machinery before the much larger sums needed for a <photon gas> or an <Ising model>.

The appeal is economical: the same microscopic <energies> and <probabilities> explain both an average reading and its <variance>. A measurement's spread therefore becomes useful physical information rather than an inconvenience to discard.

= Isothermal-isobaric ensemble
{parent=Statistical physics}
{title2=$NpT$}
{wiki=Isothermal–isobaric_ensemble}

An equilibrium <statistical ensemble> with fixed particle number, <pressure> and <temperature>, allowing <volume> and <energy> to fluctuate. With a temperature-independent volume measure, its joint weight is proportional to $e^{-\beta(E+PV)}$. Its normalization and any temperature-dependent prefactor must be specified when taking thermodynamic derivatives.

= Enthalpy fluctuation formula
{parent=Isothermal-isobaric ensemble}
{title2=$C_P=\operatorname{Var}(H)/(k_BT^2)$}

With temperature-independent microstate enthalpies and measure, differentiating the normalized weight $e^{-\beta H}/\Delta$ gives $\partial_\beta\langle H\rangle=-\operatorname{Var}(H)$. Since $d\beta/dT=-1/(k_BT^2)$, the displayed response follows. A stable finite-system equilibrium distribution therefore has nonnegative <heat capacity at constant pressure> in this setting.

= Isothermal-isobaric partition function
{parent=Isothermal-isobaric ensemble}
{title2=$\Delta=\int_0^\infty Z(N,V,T)e^{-\beta PV}\,dV$}

Integrating a <canonical partition function> over <volume> gives the displayed continuous-volume normalization. For a temperature-independent measure and Hamiltonian, $-\partial_\beta\ln\Delta=\langle E+PV\rangle$. Multiplying by a volume-independent prefactor leaves normalized volume probabilities unchanged, but a temperature-dependent prefactor changes this derivative.

= Finite-size normalization of the isothermal-isobaric ensemble
{parent=Isothermal-isobaric partition function}
{title2=$-\partial_\beta\ln(\beta P\Delta)=\langle H\rangle-k_BT$}

For a classical monatomic <ideal gas>, direct volume integration gives $\Delta=\Lambda^{-3N}(\beta P)^{-N-1}$. Consequently the continuous-volume ensemble has $\langle H\rangle=(5N/2+1)k_BT$. Defining a dimensionless thermodynamic function using $\widetilde\Delta=\beta P\Delta$ instead gives $-\partial_\beta\ln\widetilde\Delta=(5N/2)k_BT$. The normalized volume distribution is identical, because the prefactor cancels in probabilities. Its temperature derivative nevertheless contributes $-k_BT$ to the derived enthalpy and $-k_B$ to its heat capacity. The two conventions agree at leading extensive order, but cannot be mixed in an exact finite-$N$ fluctuation identity.

= Lattice gas
{parent=Statistical physics}

A <lattice gas> describes <particle> positions by occupations of fixed cells. In the single-occupancy model, $n_\alpha\in\{0,1\}$ and $N=\sum_\alpha n_\alpha$. Its <grand canonical partition function> sums configurations with weight $\exp[-\beta(U-\widetilde\mu N)]$. Discretizing a classical position <integral> into cells of <volume> $b$ gives $\widetilde\mu=\mu+k_BT\ln(b/\lambda^3)$, with <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $\lambda$.

For <energy> $U=-4J\sum_{\langle\alpha\alpha'\rangle}n_\alpha n_{\alpha'}$ on a regular lattice of coordination $q$, the substitution $n_\alpha=(1+\sigma_\alpha)/2$ maps the model to the <Ising model> with coupling $J$ and effective field $h=qJ+\widetilde\mu/2$. The mean Ising spin is a density imbalance, $\langle\sigma\rangle=2bn-1$. For $J>0$ below the critical <temperature>, the field-driven Ising transition becomes gas-liquid <phase coexistence>.

= Canonical lattice-gas partition function
{parent=Lattice gas}
{title2=$Z_N=\binom MN e^{-\beta N\varepsilon_s}$}

For $N$ indistinguishable adsorbates on $M$ equivalent sites, at most one per site and no lateral interactions, $Z_N=\binom MN e^{-N\varepsilon_s/(k_BT)}$. The binomial coefficient already accounts for indistinguishability; an additional division by $N!$ would count it twice.

= Hard sphere
{parent=Statistical physics}
{title2=$U(R)=\infty\ (R<d),\quad U(R)=0\ (R\geq d)$}

A <hard sphere> has an impenetrable core and otherwise no pair interaction. Two identical spheres of diameter $d$ cannot have centre separation below $d$. Even without attractive <potential energy>, crowding can produce fluid–crystal coexistence because available configuration space and <entropy> differ between the phases.

= Hard disc
{parent=Hard sphere}

The two-dimensional counterpart of a <hard sphere>: two equal discs of radius $r$ cannot have centre separation less than $2r$. Their pair-excluded area is $4\pi r^2$, but the second-virial excluded-area parameter per particle is half that value because a pair would otherwise be counted twice.

= Hard-disc cage approximation
{parent=Hard disc}
{title2=$\mathcal A=\pi(R-2r)^2$}

A circular free-area model for a disc surrounded by fixed neighbours a centre-to-centre distance $R$ away. The inscribed circle available to the moving centre has radius $R-2r$. It is an approximation to the actual curved cage boundary, not the area of a disc whose radius is $R-r$.

= Hard-disc excluded-area approximation
{parent=Hard disc}
{title2=$b=2\pi r^2$}

At low density, sequential insertion gives a configurational integral approximated by $\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}(a-4j\pi r^2)$. Expanding its logarithm to first order in occupied area gives $N\ln a-2\pi r^2N(N-1)/a$. Replacing $N-1$ by $N$ and matching a free-volume expression gives $(a-Nb)^N$, with $b=2\pi r^2$. This matching is controlled only at low density; its pole at packing fraction $1/2$ is not a physical close-packing limit.

= Entropy-driven hard-sphere crystallization
{parent=Hard sphere}

Crystallization favored by greater total accessible configurational <entropy>, despite increased spatial order. Ordered cages at sufficiently high <packing fraction> can allow more positional/free-volume configurations than a disordered crowded fluid.

= Linear response theory
{parent=Statistical physics}

= Linear response
{synonym}

<Linear response theory> describes a system's small change in an observable to first order in an applied perturbation. The approximation requires a stated reference state and a range of sufficiently small perturbations.

= Random phase approximation
{parent=Linear response theory}

The <random phase approximation> treats the independent-particle response to the self-consistent total field, including the induced mean <Coulomb potential>. With a positive static number-response coefficient $\Pi_0=-\delta n/\delta U$, a potential $U=-e\phi$ gives $\delta n=e\Pi_0\phi_{\rm tot}$. Combining induced charge $-e\delta n$ with <Poisson equation> gives $\epsilon=1+e^2\Pi_0/(\epsilon_0q^2)$. Exchange-correlation vertex corrections beyond this self-consistent response are omitted. It is a response approximation, not a literal assumption that every microscopic electron phase is independently randomized.

= Zero-frequency cancellation of a causal response kernel
{parent=Linear response theory}
{title2=$\int I(t)dt=\widehat K(0)\widehat V(0)$}

For an integrable <linear response> kernel $K$ and input $V$, the <convolution theorem> gives $\widehat I(\omega)=\widehat K(\omega)\widehat V(\omega)$. The integrated output is therefore zero for every integrable input if and only if $\widehat K(0)=\int K(t)dt=0$. For a sum of causal damped sines, $K(t)=\Theta(t)\sum_j a_je^{-\gamma_jt}\sin(p_jt)$, the condition is $\sum_j a_jp_j/(\gamma_j^2+p_j^2)=0$. If all $a_j$ are positive, nonzero terms of both signs are needed; a term with $p_j=0$ vanishes identically.

= Kramers-Kronig relations
{c}
{parent=Linear response theory}
{wiki=Kramers–Kronig_relations}

Causality constrains the real and imaginary parts of a frequency-dependent <linear response>. For a dielectric with $\epsilon_r(\omega)\to1$ at large frequency, the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention gives
$$
\epsilon_r'(\omega)-1=\frac2\pi\operatorname{P}\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega'\epsilon_r''(\omega')}{\omega'^2-\omega^2}\,d\omega'.
$$
The integral is a <Cauchy principal value>. Absorption and <optical dispersion> therefore cannot be specified independently.

= Thermal activation
{parent=Statistical physics}

<Thermal activation> uses <thermal fluctuations> to cross an <energy barrier>. The crossing rate depends on the barrier, the <temperature> and the system's dynamics.

= Heat-capacity activation estimate
{parent=Thermal activation}
{title2=$\Delta/k_B=\ln[C(T_2)/C(T_1)]/(T_1^{-1}-T_2^{-1})$}

For gapped excitations with $C(T)\simeq A\exp[-\Delta/(k_BT)]$ and approximately constant prefactor, two <heat capacity> measurements at $T_2>T_1$ give the displayed activation-energy estimate. A more general prefactor $AT^p$ changes its numerator to $\ln[C(T_2)/C(T_1)]-p\ln(T_2/T_1)$. The two-point estimate measures an effective exponential scale; it does not independently determine the prefactor or establish a detailed excitation <density of states>.

= Energy landscape
{parent=Statistical physics}

An <energy landscape> assigns an <energy> to each possible state and describes its variation between neighbouring states. Its <local minima> and intervening <energy barriers> help distinguish favourable states from states that a specified dynamics can reach quickly. In a <Hopfield network>, the assigned <energy> is an objective function and need not be a conserved physical quantity.

= Free-energy landscape
{parent=Energy landscape}
{title2=$G(x)=-k_BT\log P_{\rm eq}(x)+\mathrm{constant}$}

At fixed environmental constraints, integrate out unobserved coordinates to obtain an equilibrium probability density for a chosen reaction coordinate $x$. Relative to a specified coordinate measure, the displayed <free energy> reproduces that density. A change of coordinate can add a Jacobian term, so the profile is not independent of its coordinate convention. Minima identify probable basins, while barriers relative to an initial basin contribute to activated kinetics; a kinetic prefactor still requires dynamics. A conjugate <force> tilts the profile by $-Fx$.

= Disconnectivity graph
{parent=Energy landscape}

A tree showing how basins around local minima connect as an energy threshold is raised. Branching heights encode path barriers, while horizontal placement is mainly presentational.

= Energy barrier
{parent=Energy landscape}
{title2=$\Delta E$}

An <energy barrier> is an increase in <energy> that must be overcome along an allowed route between states. Its value depends on which paths or single-step moves are allowed; specifying only the energies of the endpoints does not determine the barrier.

= Spin-charge separation
{parent=Luttinger liquid}

In an appropriate spinful one-dimensional interacting conductor, low-energy density and spin disturbances form distinct collective modes, generally with different velocities. A bare injected electron can excite both modes. Boundary conditions and attached leads still matter for electrical conductance; a bulk interaction parameter alone need not set the two-terminal value of a clean contacted wire.

= Mean-field theory
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

<Mean-field theory> replaces fluctuating interactions with a self-consistent average field. For example, an <Ising spin> feels a field determined by the average neighboring <spin magnetization>; a <self-consistency equation> approximates the interacting <Ising model>. Neglecting correlated fluctuations can fail near a continuous <phase transition>. The <mean-field approximation> in <Landau theory> is a particular use of this general framework.

= Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov theory
{c}
{parent=Mean-field theory}

A self-consistent quadratic approximation to an interacting many-particle system that retains both normal one-particle densities and anomalous pair averages. Normal contractions supply Hartree and Fock fields, while anomalous contractions supply the <pair potential>. A <Bogoliubov transformation> preserving the appropriate <fermion> anticommutators or <boson> commutators diagonalizes the resulting <Hamiltonian>; the densities must then be recomputed to satisfy self-consistency and the particle-number constraint.

= Weiss molecular field
{c}
{parent=Mean-field theory}
{title2=$H_E=\lambda M$}

The <Weiss molecular field> replaces exchange coupling to neighbouring moments by an effective local field proportional to the average <magnetization>. For two-state moments and $B_E=\mu_0\lambda M$, self-consistency gives $m=\tanh(m/t)$ at zero applied field, where $m=|M|/(n\mu)$ and $t=T/T_C$, with $k_BT_C=\mu_0\lambda n\mu^2$. This field represents a quantum exchange energy, not generally the actual magnetostatic field generated by the moments.

= Mean-field onset of two-state ferromagnetism
{parent=Weiss molecular field}
{title2=$m\sim\sqrt{3(1-T/T_C)}$}

For $m=\tanh(m/t)$ and positive temperature, the positive solution exists uniquely for $0<t<1$: the slope at zero is $1/t>1$, while the hyperbolic tangent is concave and below one. For $t\geq1$ only $m=0$ remains. Expanding to cubic order near $t=1$ gives $m^2\simeq3t^2(1-t)$ and $m\simeq t\sqrt{3(1-t)}$, with <mean-field critical exponent> $1/2$. The prefactor $t$ follows from the truncated equation; it is not controlled beyond leading critical order.

= Thermal fluctuation
{parent=Statistical physics}
{title2=$\langle(\delta A)^2\rangle=k_BT\,\partial_h\langle A\rangle$}

A thermal fluctuation is the randomness of an observable in an equilibrium <Boltzmann distribution>. If the energy contains $-hA$, differentiation of its <partition function> gives $\partial_h\langle A\rangle=\beta(\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2)$. Thus the fluctuation variance is related to the response to a conjugate source. In a field theory, the two-point version is the <connected correlation function>.

= Volume fluctuations of a closed gas bubble
{parent=Thermal fluctuation}
{title2=$\langle\Delta V^2\rangle\simeq k_BTV/[\kappa_T^{-1}-(2/9)\Gamma\alpha V^{-1/3}]$}

For fixed <particle number> and <temperature>, a bubble with <surface tension> $\Gamma$ and <area> $\alpha V^{2/3}$ has effective <availability> $A(V)=F_{\mathrm{gas}}(T,V,N)+\Gamma\alpha V^{2/3}+p_RV$. Its stationary condition is $p_{\mathrm{gas}}-p_R=(2/3)\Gamma\alpha V^{-1/3}$. Its curvature is $A''=V^{-1}[\kappa_T^{-1}-(2/9)\Gamma\alpha V^{-1/3}]$. Positive curvature is required for stable <Gaussian fluctuation approximation>. The <variance> follows from <availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations>. For an <ideal gas>, the restoring denominator becomes $p_R+(4/9)\Gamma\alpha V^{-1/3}>0$ for positive $p_R$. Treating the internal <pressure> as externally fixed instead of fixing the gas <particle number> changes the stability problem.

= Photon gas
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

A <photon gas> is a collection of <photons> described statistically as radiation. Here the equilibrium model is homogeneous, isotropic <electromagnetic radiation> in vacuum at <temperature> $T>0$: each <photon> has <energy> $E=c|\mathbf p|=h\nu$, with <momentum> $\mathbf p$ and <frequency> $\nu$, and there are two independent transverse states of <electromagnetic polarization>. An absorbing cavity with walls at a common <temperature> provides a way to establish this <thermal equilibrium>. The bulk formulas assume cavity dimensions much larger than the typical <wavelength> $hc/(k_BT)$, so the <electromagnetic mode density in a box> can replace individual cavity modes by a continuum. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S3#S3.SS2[David Tong, Statistical Physics, §3.2: photon energies, polarizations and cavity equilibrium]{ref}

The crucial constraint is whether <photon emission> and <photon absorption>, or other number-changing processes, can equilibrate the <photon> population. When they can, <photon> number is not an independently conserved quantity and the equilibrium <photon chemical potential> is $\mu_\gamma=0$. For each polarization and <frequency>, the mean <occupation number> then follows the <Bose-Einstein distribution>:
$$
f(\nu)=\frac{1}{\exp[h\nu/(k_BT)]-1}.
$$
If number-changing processes are ineffective but number-conserving interactions redistribute <energy>, a constrained <Bose-Einstein distribution> can instead have $f=[\exp((h\nu-\mu_\gamma)/(k_BT))-1]^{-1}$ with <chemical potential> $\mu_\gamma\le0$ fixed by the number constraint. Thus <thermal equilibrium> with respect to <energy> exchange alone does not establish $\mu_\gamma=0$. The zero value can also persist in an already prepared <Planck photon distribution> during suitable subsequent evolution; continual <photon emission> is not required to preserve it. https://academic.oup.com/ptep/article/2014/6/06B107/1560917[Hiroyuki Tashiro, CMB spectral distortions and energy release in the early universe (2014), §3.1: kinetic equilibration without full photon-number equilibration]{ref}

Combining $f$ with the two-polarization <density of states>, $g_\nu/V=8\pi\nu^2/c^3$, gives the <Planck photon distribution> and the spectral <photon energy density>:
$$
\frac{dn_\gamma}{d\nu}=\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}f(\nu),\qquad
u_\nu\equiv\frac{du}{d\nu}=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}f(\nu)=\frac{4\pi}{c}B_\nu(T).
$$
Here $B_\nu$ is the <spectral radiance> in <Planck's law>; $u_\nu$ is stored <energy density> per unit <frequency>, not outgoing <power> per <area>. Their different units are connected by the full-sphere <solid angle> and the <speed of light> $c$. The <Boltzmann constant> is $k_B$, and $h$ is the <Planck constant>. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/sm1/Thermalhtml/node103.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, Planck Radiation Law: two polarizations and radiation energy spectrum]{ref}

The equilibrium <equation of state> is particularly simple:
$$
u=a_RT^4,\qquad p=\frac{u}{3},\qquad
 a_R=\frac{8\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^3}=\frac{4\sigma}{c}
 \simeq7.565733250\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-4}}.
$$
The <radiation constant> $a_R$ is the same constant called $a_{\rm rad}$ in <black-body radiation>; $\sigma$ is the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>. An outline of the <energy density> calculation substitutes $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ and uses $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=\pi^4/15$. The <radiation pressure> follows because the <angular average> of the squared directional component of <photon> <momentum> is $1/3$ for <isotropy>; the <thermodynamic derivation of blackbody radiation pressure> supplies an independent route. These formulas describe the radiation contribution, not the cavity walls. The isotropic massless relation $p=u/3$ survives a different isotropic spectrum, whereas the coefficient in $u=a_RT^4$ requires the zero-<chemical potential> <Planck photon distribution>.

For a computed example with stipulated $T=2.725\,\mathrm K$, the <photon energy density> is $u\simeq4.17174\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$ and the <radiation pressure> is $p\simeq1.39058\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The <photon number density> in the <International System of Units> is
$$
n_\gamma=16\pi\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{hc}\right)^3
 \simeq4.10501\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
\frac{u}{n_\gamma}=\frac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}k_BT\simeq2.70118\,k_BT.
$$
Here $\zeta$ is the <Riemann zeta function>. The mean <photon> <energy> differs from the <energy> at the peak of a spectral curve: counting <photons> and weighting them by <energy> emphasize different <frequencies>. The calculations use the exact <International System of Units> values of the <Planck constant>, <Boltzmann constant> and <speed of light>. https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/meet-constants[NIST, Meet the Constants: exact SI defining constants]{ref}

A motivating observation is the <cosmic microwave background> spectrum measured by the Far Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer (FIRAS) aboard COBE. Its apparatus used a polarizing <Michelson interferometer>, with one input viewing a roughly seven-degree sky patch and the other an internal <blackbody>. Scanning mirrors changed the <optical path length> difference; heat-sensitive detectors recorded the varying <power>, and <Fourier transform spectroscopy> recovered the spectral difference. Operation outside the <terrestrial atmosphere> removed atmospheric emission and absorption, while shielding and cooling the optics in a helium cryostat suppressed the instrument's own radiation. https://lambda.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/cobe/firas/doc/FES4_APP_B.pdf[Mather, Fixsen and Shafer, Design for the COBE Far Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer, apparatus and systematic-error controls]{ref}

Absolute calibration came from moving an external <blackbody> into the sky aperture, filling the same beam used for the sky. This was an iron-loaded epoxy absorber shaped as a re-entrant cone. Repeated reflections made it a close approximation to a <blackbody>, while thermometers supplied its <temperature>. Mather and colleagues explicitly evaluated reflections, <temperature> gradients, radiation leaking through the material and leakage around its edge. These are limits on how faithfully the calibrator realizes <thermal equilibrium> radiation, rather than details that can be eliminated merely by calling it black. https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9810373[Mather et al., Calibrator Design for COBE FIRAS (1999): absorber geometry and calibration error budget]{ref}

During sky observations the internal <blackbody> <temperature> was adjusted to nearly null the spectral difference, reducing gain and drift errors. Fixsen and colleagues' 1996 analysis used calibrated spectra over <wavenumbers> $2$ to $21\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, about $60$ to $630\,\mathrm{GHz}$. They separated the sky's spatially uniform component, its dipole and Galactic emission, and varied sky cuts and foreground templates as controls. The resulting <cosmic microwave background> spectrum agreed with a single <Planck function>, with reported RMS deviations below 50 parts per million of its peak brightness. This is a residual statistic over the analyzed spectrum, not a pointwise relative error bound at every <frequency>. https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9605054[Fixsen et al., The Cosmic Microwave Background Spectrum from the Full COBE/FIRAS Data Set (1996), §§2, 5 and 5.3]{ref}

The observation motivates using a <photon gas> as a physical model rather than merely a formal counting exercise. Its interpretation has a clear boundary: agreement with <Planck's law> constrains departures from an equilibrium spectral shape within the calibrated band and foreground assumptions; it does not directly measure <radiation pressure> or <entropy density of a photon gas>. Nor does it establish that today's <cosmic microwave background> is continually re-equilibrating with matter. A previously thermal spectrum can retain its <Planck function> shape as <expansion of the universe> lowers every <photon> <frequency> in the same proportion. The usefulness of the model is that one measured spectral <temperature>, together with its equilibrium assumptions, predicts <energy density>, <radiation pressure>, <photon number density> and <entropy density of a photon gas> consistently.

= One-dimensional photon-gas thermodynamics
{parent=Photon gas}
{title2=$U=\pi Lk_B^2T^2/(3\hbar c)$}

A cavity with two transverse <photon> <polarizations> and standing-wave spacing $\pi/L$ has <energy> <density of states> $2L/(\pi\hbar c)$. In the continuum regime, integrating the Bose occupation at zero <chemical potential> gives $U=bLT^2$, where $b=\pi k_B^2/(3\hbar c)$. The thermal quantities are $C_L=S=2bLT$, Helmholtz free <energy> $A=-bLT^2$ and generalized <pressure> $P=bT^2=U/L$. The last quantity has units of <force>. These thermal formulas exclude a separate boundary-dependent vacuum <energy> and require <temperature> above the mode-spacing scale.

= Reversible isothermal expansion of a photon gas
{parent=Photon gas}
{title2=$Q=\frac43u\Delta V$}

At fixed <temperature>, a <photon gas> has constant <energy density> $u$ and <radiation pressure> $p=u/3$. An increase $\Delta V$ in <volume (thermodynamics)> requires both $\Delta U=u\Delta V$ and expansion <work> $p\Delta V$, giving absorbed <heat> $4u\Delta V/3$.

= Entropy density of a photon gas
{parent=Photon gas}
{title2=$s=4a_RT^3/3$}

The <entropy density of a photon gas> is its <entropy> per physical <volume (thermodynamics)>, $s=S/V$. For homogeneous equilibrium vacuum radiation with two states of <electromagnetic polarization> and zero <photon chemical potential>,
$$
s=\frac{u+p}{T}=\frac{4}{3}a_RT^3,\qquad
S=\frac{4}{3}a_RT^3V.
$$
The first equality is the <entropy density at zero chemical potential>, obtained from the <thermodynamic Euler relation> $U=TS-pV+\mu_\gamma N_\gamma$ with <internal energy> $U=uV$. The second uses the <photon gas> <equation of state>, $u=a_RT^4$ and $p=u/3$. If an independently constrained <photon> number gives a nonzero <chemical potential>, the general <entropy density> is $(u+p-\mu_\gamma n_\gamma)/T$ and $u=a_RT^4$ need not hold. This distinction explains why the absence of a conserved number matters for <entropy> as well as for the spectral shape. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S4#S4.SS4[David Tong, Statistical Physics, §4.4: Gibbs free energy and the thermodynamic Euler relation]{ref}

A short <first law of thermodynamics> check, for neighboring equilibrium states with $\mu_\gamma=0$, is
$$
T\,dS=dU+p\,dV
 =4a_RT^3V\,dT+\frac{4}{3}a_RT^4\,dV,
\qquad
dS=\frac{4}{3}a_R\,d(T^3V).
$$
The <thermodynamic Euler relation> fixes the additive normalization. Equivalently, this radiation <entropy> tends to zero with <temperature> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>; no contribution from the containing walls is included. Interpreting $T\,dS$ as transferred <heat> and $p\,dV$ as boundary <work> requires a <reversible thermodynamic process>. For a <reversible thermodynamic process> that is also an <adiabatic process>, there is no <entropy production> or <entropy> exchange, so
$$
T^3V=\text{constant},\qquad
T=T_0\left(\frac{V_0}{V}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
pV^{4/3}=\text{constant},\qquad
U=U_0\left(\frac{V_0}{V}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
An irreversible <adiabatic process> can produce <entropy> even without <heat> exchange, so the word adiabatic alone is insufficient to infer $T^3V=\text{constant}$.

For an analytic example, expand an initially equilibrated <photon gas> reversibly from $V_0$ to $8V_0$, keeping only the same two <photon> states of <electromagnetic polarization>. Its <temperature> halves, its <energy density> becomes $u_0/16$, its <entropy density of a photon gas> becomes $s_0/8$, and its total <entropy> stays $S_0$. The <internal energy> halves rather than remaining fixed: the missing half leaves as <work> against <radiation pressure>,
$$
W=\int_{V_0}^{8V_0}p(V)\,dV=\frac{U_0}{2},\qquad
\Delta U=-W.
$$
This is a stipulated reversible radiation model, not a report of a piston experiment. Maintaining a real cavity along this path requires controlling wall <heat> exchange and including the wall <heat capacity> in any actual apparatus balance.

= Statistical mechanics
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

<Statistical mechanics> connects <microstates> to <macrostates> through <probability distributions>. Its <microcanonical ensemble>, <canonical ensemble> and <grand canonical ensemble> specify different constraints on exchanges with the surroundings. An <ensemble average> describes repeated preparations under those constraints; it need not be the value in every individual preparation.

This makes the subject useful whenever tracking every microscopic detail would obscure the question being asked. In his 1902 book, J. Willard Gibbs systematically developed the <canonical ensemble>. = Ergodic hypothesis
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

= Latent heat of fusion
{parent=Statistical physics}
{title2=$L$}

An ice bath can absorb <heat> while its <temperature> barely changes: the <heat> converts <ice> into <water>. The <latent heat of fusion> quantifies this <melting> budget. For a pure substance at its equilibrium <melting> <temperature> $T_m(p)$ and specified <pressure> $p$, define it here per unit <mass>:
$$
L(p)=h_l(T_m(p),p)-h_s(T_m(p),p),\qquad h=H/m.
$$
Here $h_l$ and $h_s$ are the <specific enthalpies> of the coexisting bulk <liquid> and <solid>, and $H$ is <enthalpy>. For ordinary <melting>, $L>0$, in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$; an amount $m$ has a total fusion <enthalpy> $mL$ in <joules>. A molar fusion <enthalpy> instead has units $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$ and equals $ML$, where $M=m/n$ is <mass> divided by <amount of substance> in $\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$. Always check the normalization before inserting a tabulated value into an <energy> balance.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/1.5 Phase Changes>[§1.5: fusion and specific latent heat]{ref} https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/12214[IUPAC Gold Book, mass divided by amount of substance]{ref}

For a <closed system> converting completely from <solid> to <liquid> at fixed <pressure>, with only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work> and negligible changes in bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $Q_p=\Delta H=mL$. Both endpoints are at $T_m(p)$: warming initially colder <ice> or subsequently warming the resulting <water> requires additional <sensible heat>. Since $H=U+pV$, the corresponding <specific internal energy> jump is $\Delta u=L-p\Delta v$, where $v=V/m$; $L$ includes the <work> associated with the change in <volume (thermodynamics)>. <Heat> is transferred <energy>, whereas <enthalpy> is a <state function>. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S4#S4.SS4.SSS1[David Tong, Statistical Physics, §4.4.1 and the first law]{ref}

= Ice-melting calorimetry
{parent=Latent heat of fusion}

<Ice-melting calorimetry> measures the fusion <enthalpy> through <conservation of energy>, rather than inferring it from the small <temperature> change of the <melting> <ice> itself. In a mixing experiment, warm <water> and the container cool while added <ice> melts and the resulting <water> warms. Their combined <energy> balance separates fusion from <sensible heat>.

Weigh the container and stirrer, then weigh the added warm <water> to obtain $m_w$. Let the container and <water> reach a common initial <temperature> $T_i$. Prepare <ice> in a clean <ice>-<water> mixture near $T_m$, rather than assuming freezer <ice> is already at $T_m$; drain and blot its surface immediately before transfer. Add it without splashing, stir gently, and record <temperature> before addition and until after the last <ice> disappears. Reweigh to find the added <mass> $m_i$. Wisconsin's laboratory guide specifies drying each piece, weighing before and after, and recording the <temperature> history; it places the initial and final <temperatures> on opposite sides of the jacket <temperature> to reduce net environmental exchange. https://badger.physics.wisc.edu/trash/lab_old/manual/node30.html[University of Wisconsin, H-2: Latent heat of fusion of ice, procedure]{ref}

$$
(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_i-T_f)+Q_{\rm env}=m_iL+m_ic_w(T_f-T_m).
$$
Here $Q_{\rm env}>0$ denotes net <heat> entering the apparatus from its surroundings. The left side supplies the fusion <enthalpy> and the <sensible heat> on the right; the <water> produced by <melting> must be warmed too. The balance extends Case Western's mixing demonstration by including the apparatus and environmental terms. If the <specific heat capacities> vary appreciably, replace their products with the corresponding <temperature> integrals. https://case.edu/artsci/phys/courses/demos/spec.htm[Case Western, mixing-calorimetry energy balance]{ref}

Determine $C_{\rm cal}$ independently, for example by a warm/cold <water> mixing balance with no <phase transition>, including the wetted stirrer and <thermometer> in the apparatus. Check the <thermometer calibration>, keep the lid in place between transfers, and monitor the surroundings. Before/after drift and a comparable run without <ice> help bound $Q_{\rm env}$; such corrections assume comparable <heat transfer> conditions and are not automatically exact. Choosing a jacket <temperature> between $T_i$ and $T_f$ reduces exchange but does not prove $Q_{\rm env}=0$. Ensure all the <ice> has melted and the contents are well mixed before using the measured $T_f$: with residual <ice>, the weighed addition is not the <mass> that has undergone fusion. Repeat the experiment and report <measurement errors> in <mass>, <temperature>, $C_{\rm cal}$ and the leak correction.

An independent way to see the plateau is David Roundy's Oregon State experiment: heat an <ice>-<water> mixture slowly with a waterproof resistor in an insulated cup, stir, and record <temperature>, heater <electric current> $I(t)$, <electric potential difference> $V(t)$ and elapsed time. Across an ideal isothermal plateau the apparatus has no <sensible heat> change, giving $m_sL=E_{\rm el}+Q_{\rm env}$; departures from uniform <temperature> require accounting for the heater and container response. https://paradigms.physics.oregonstate.edu/act/handout/2108[David Roundy, Oregon State, Ice Calorimetry Lab: apparatus, stirring and electrical input]{ref}

= Wet-ice bias in fusion calorimetry
{parent=Ice-melting calorimetry}
{title2=$L_{\rm app}=(1-r)L-Q_{\rm env}/m_a$}

Surface <water> counted as <ice> needs warming but has already undergone <melting>. Its presence therefore biases a dry-ice estimate of <latent heat of fusion> downward. This is why drying the added <ice> is a quantitative control in <ice-melting calorimetry>, rather than cosmetic preparation. Let $m_a$ be the total added <mass> at $T_m$, of which a fraction $r$ is already <liquid> and $(1-r)$ remains <solid>. The correct constant-property balance is
$$
(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_i-T_f)+Q_{\rm env}=(1-r)m_aL+m_ac_w(T_f-T_m).
$$
If an analysis incorrectly treats all $m_a$ as <solid> and sets $Q_{\rm env}=0$, it obtains
$$
L_{\rm app}=\frac{(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_i-T_f)}{m_a}-c_w(T_f-T_m)=(1-r)L-\frac{Q_{\rm env}}{m_a}.
$$
Thus $r=0.10$ gives a $10\%$ low estimate with no heat leak. Unaccounted incoming <heat> also lowers the estimate; outgoing <heat> raises it. These signs follow directly from the displayed <energy> balance. Conversely, dry <ice> initially below $T_m$ absorbs additional <sensible heat>; neglecting that term overestimates $L$. These are different biases and may partially cancel, so agreement with a reference value alone does not validate the controls.

= Two-fluid model of superfluidity
{parent=Normal component of a superfluid}

The <two-fluid model of superfluidity> gives the <normal component of a superfluid> and the <superfluid> independent <velocities>.

An elementary uniform-flow example makes the distinction operational. Write the <mass> flux per unit <area> and the ideal <entropy> flux as
$$
\mathbf j=\rho_s\mathbf v_s+\rho_n\mathbf v_n,\qquad
\mathbf j_S=\rho s\mathbf v_n,\qquad \rho=\rho_s+\rho_n.
$$
Here $s$ is <specific entropy>, in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, and $\rho_s,\rho_n$ are response <mass densities>. The <mass density> $\rho_s$ used here gives the three-dimensional <phase stiffness> coefficient $\rho_s(\hbar/m)^2$ when $\mathbf v_s=(\hbar/m)\nabla\theta$ is substituted into the <kinetic energy density> $\rho_s v_s^2/2$. For a stipulated $\rho_s=3\rho/4$, $\rho_n=\rho/4$ and $v_n=3u$ along a channel, zero net <mass> flux requires $v_s=-u$. Yet $j_S=3\rho su$ is positive. Opposing <velocities> can therefore transport <entropy> while transporting no net <mass>.

This example explains why excellent apparent <heat> transport need not be ordinary diffusion of <heat> through a stationary <liquid>. In the ideal zero-net-<mass>-flux limit, the <heat> flux associated with this <entropy> transport is $Tj_S$. Viscous losses, <quantum vortices> and external <heat> exchange require additional terms. = Thermomechanical effect

= Fountain effect
{synonym}

The <thermomechanical effect> couples a <temperature> difference to a <pressure> difference across a <superleak>. For the closed-chamber model in the apparatus figure, consider neighboring equilibrium states with small <velocities>, negligible height difference and pure <helium-4>. Let $\mu_m$ be <chemical potential> per unit <mass>, in $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$. The <Gibbs free energy> differential per unit <mass> gives
$$
d\mu_m=-s\,dT+\frac{1}{\rho}\,dP.
$$
The <superleak> allows the <superfluid> to redistribute until the two sides have equal $\mu_m$. Setting $d\mu_m=0$ therefore gives
$$
\left.\frac{dP}{dT}\right|_{\mu_m}=\rho s>0,\qquad
P_h-P_c=\int_{T_c}^{T_h}\rho(T,P(T))s(T,P(T))\,dT.
$$
The sign matters: heating one side at initially equal <pressure> lowers its $\mu_m$; inflow raises its <pressure> until chemical balance is restored. The hotter side has the higher <pressure>. The <integral> follows the constant-$\mu_m$ path; it need not be evaluated at fixed <pressure> when material properties vary appreciably.

For an original local example, stipulate constant $\rho=150\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $s=100\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $T_h-T_c=0.020\,\mathrm K$. The predicted <pressure> difference is $300\,\mathrm{Pa}$. These are teaching assumptions, not helium measurements. This static <thermodynamics> relation predicts neither the required heater <power> nor the filling time: those require <heat> leakage, channel conductance and chamber <heat capacity>. An outlet would introduce <kinetic energy> and a flowing balance, so the static $300\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is not a predicted fountain height.

= Superfluid order-parameter effective theory
{parent=Superfluid}

For a complex superfluid order parameter $\psi=\sqrt n e^{i\theta}$, the nonrelativistic Landau-Ginzburg kinetic term contains $-n\dot\theta$. Thus number density and phase are canonically conjugate, so a phase-selected state necessarily fluctuates in particle number.

= Density-phase conjugacy
{parent=Superfluid order-parameter effective theory}

In a first-order condensate action, density and phase are canonically paired variables. Linear density and phase oscillations are therefore coupled components of a single Bogoliubov branch, rather than two independent positive-frequency particles. For $i\dot\psi=-\psi''+\beta(|\psi|^2-1)\psi$, writing $\psi\simeq1+\rho/2+i\theta$ gives $\dot\rho=-2\theta''$ and $\dot\theta=\rho''/2-\beta\rho$, hence $\omega^2=k^4+2\beta k^2$.

= Number-phase conjugacy
{parent=Superfluid order-parameter effective theory}

Number-phase conjugacy is the canonical relation between a conserved $U(1)$ charge and the phase of its order parameter. A state of sharp number cannot also have a sharply selected broken-symmetry phase.

= Thermal phase fluctuation

A thermal phase fluctuation is a finite-temperature fluctuation of the phase of a complex <order parameter>. For a linearly dispersing phase mode, its infrared variance is proportional to $T\int d^dk/k^2$, which prevents true finite-temperature long-range order when $d\leq2$.

= Landau criterion
{c}
{parent=Superfluid}
{title2=$v_L=\inf_{p>0}\epsilon(p)/p$}
{wiki}

The <Landau criterion> asks whether creating an excitation can lower a moving <superfluid>'s <energy>. For a weakly coupled, heavy obstacle moving through a uniform <liquid> initially at zero <temperature>, let $\epsilon(p)>0$ be the excitation <energy> in the fluid rest frame, with <momentum> magnitude $p$. Transferring <momentum> $\mathbf p$ from an obstacle moving at <velocity> $\mathbf v$ supplies, to leading order in its negligible recoil, <energy> $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf p$. Emission requires
$$
\epsilon(p)-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf p\leq0.
$$
For an <isotropic> <dispersion relation>, the easiest direction is parallel to $\mathbf v$, giving the necessary kinematic condition
$$
v\geq v_L,\qquad v_L=\inf_{p>0}\frac{\epsilon(p)}p.
$$
An <infimum> includes cases in which the limiting slope occurs only as $p\to0$. A finite-mass obstacle of <mass> $M$ instead loses $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf p-p^2/(2M)$, so the corresponding emission inequality includes the extra positive recoil term $p^2/(2M)$ on the left. This is an <energy> and <momentum> balance, not a calculation of an emission rate.

The <Landau criterion> excludes spontaneous emission into the specified spectrum below its threshold. Above it, kinematic permission alone does not guarantee strong coupling or rapid decay. It is not a universal observed flow threshold: walls, channel geometry and <quantum vortex> nucleation can enable other decay routes, and finite <temperature> permits scattering from existing excitations. Using only bulk <phonons> and <rotons> leaves those processes outside the model.

Landau's 1941 <energy> and <momentum> argument used a <roton> approximation different from the finite-<momentum> minimum illustrated below.<The Theory of Superfluidity of Helium II by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Landau, §§2,4, pp.500–504]{ref}

Landau received his <Nobel Prize in Physics in 1962> while hospitalized in Moscow after a car accident.<Soviet Jewish Scientist Presented with Nobel Prize in Hospital Bed by Jewish Telegraphic Agency>[Contemporary report, 12 December 1962, opening paragraph]{ref}

For a deliberately simple <Bose-Einstein condensation> comparison, stipulate an ideal free-particle excitation law $\epsilon=p^2/(2m)$. Then $\epsilon/p=p/(2m)$ has <infimum> zero. Macroscopic one-particle occupation therefore supplies no positive emission threshold by this argument alone. By contrast a linear <phonon> law $\epsilon=cp$ yields $v_L=c$. The <dispersion relation> and the collective response contain information that the occupation criterion alone does not supply; <Bose-Einstein condensation> and thermodynamic <superfluidity> must be tested separately.

= Helium slit-flow experiment
{parent=Superfluidity}

$$
Here $\Delta h$ is the difference in free-surface heights and $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>. This explains why following a liquid level provides both a driving <pressure> and a transported <volume (thermodynamics)>; it does not turn a finite-resolution observation into proof of zero <viscosity>.

= Helium-3 superfluidity
{parent=Superfluidity}
{wiki=Superfluid_helium-3}

<Helium-3 superfluidity> involves <Cooper pairs> of <fermions>; <helium-4> atoms are <bosons>. Individual <helium-3> atoms do not undergo <Bose-Einstein condensation>. <The Extraordinary Phases of Liquid 3He by David Morris Lee>[Lee, 1996 lecture; 1997 reprint pp.645–647]{ref}

The <helium-3 superfluid discovery experiment> connects an unexpected <pressure> feature to a change in the state of a <liquid>. Its lesson is unusually instructive: finding a reproducible anomaly and identifying the material that changed are separate experimental achievements. <Pomeranchuk cooling> explains how the experiment reached its operating regime; an independent <nuclear magnetic resonance> channel distinguishes the phases.

= Helium-3 superfluid discovery experiment
{parent=Helium-3 superfluidity}

<David Morris Lee>, <Douglas Dean Osheroff> and <Robert Coleman Richardson> compressed coexisting <liquid> and <solid> <helium-3> in a cell precooled by a <dilution refrigerator>. <Helium-4> <pressure> drove coupled bellows and a piston; a <capacitance diaphragm gauge> read <pressure> and platinum <nuclear magnetic resonance> supplied thermometry. Uniform compression revealed a slope break A and a small <pressure> drop B; decompression and varied <solid> fraction checked reproducibility. Their April 1972 paper, Evidence for a New Phase of Solid He3, interpreted a new <solid> phase, without establishing liquid <superfluidity>. <Evidence for a New Phase of Solid He3 by Douglas Dean Osheroff and others>[1972, pp.885–888, Figs.1–3]{ref}

The October follow-up replaced the thermometer assembly with a sample <nuclear magnetic resonance> coil. An applied <magnetic field> <gradient> selected different sample layers, separating strong <solid> signals from the <liquid> background. At A the <liquid> resonance shifted; at B its shift disappeared and its <magnetic susceptibility> fell. Removing the <gradient> resolved the frequency shift directly. Independence from <solid> fraction and distinct relaxation times supported the <liquid> assignment. Thus the reinterpretation rested on phase-selective evidence, rather than relabeling the first paper's title. These magnetic observations identify changed <liquid> phases; they do not directly measure frictionless flow. <New Magnetic Phenomena in Liquid He3 below 3 mK by Douglas Dean Osheroff and others>[1972, pp.920–923, Figs.1–3]{ref}

<Douglas Dean Osheroff> recalls the subsequent p-wave interpretation. <Superfluidity in 3He: Discovery and Understanding by Douglas Dean Osheroff>[1996 lecture, p.73]{ref}

= Pomeranchuk cooling
{c}
{parent=Helium-3 superfluidity}
{wiki}

Low-<temperature> <solid> <helium-3> can have greater <entropy> than the <liquid> through disordered nuclear <spins>, enabling compression cooling. <The Pomeranchuk Effect by Robert Coleman Richardson>[Richardson, 1996 lecture, p.91, introduction and Fig.1]{ref}

The sign follows from the <Clapeyron equation>. Let $S_l,S_s$ be molar <entropies> and $V_l,V_s$ <molar volumes> of <liquid> and <solid>, respectively, on their <phase coexistence curve>. Equality of their <chemical potentials>, with $d\mu=-S\,dT+V\,dP$, gives
$$
\frac{dP}{dT}=\frac{S_l-S_s}{V_l-V_s}.
$$
With $S_s>S_l$ and $V_l>V_s$, the slope is negative. Reversibly <freezing> one mole at fixed <temperature> requires <heat> into the sample,
$$
Q_{l\to s}=T(S_s-S_l)>0,
\qquad L_{s\to l}=T(S_l-S_s)<0.
$$
Here $L_{s\to l}$ uses the melting convention for <latent heat of fusion>. This is the opposite sign from ordinary <ice> melting. The inequalities specify the regime; <freezing> does not universally cool a substance. In an approximately isolated sample, the growing higher-<entropy> phase draws on the sample's thermal <energy> instead of an external heater. Irreversible losses and incoming <heat> oppose the cooling.

= Pomeranchuk entropy-balance model
{c}
{parent=Pomeranchuk cooling}
{title2=$T(x)=T_0-(1-x_0)(T_0-T_i)/(1-x)$}

An original deliberately local model makes the cooling quantitative. Fix a total amount $n$ of material, let $x$ be the mole fraction of <solid>, and stipulate molar <entropies>
$$
S_l(T)=aT,\qquad S_s(T)=b,\qquad a,b>0,
\qquad \frac{S_{\rm total}}n=(1-x)aT+xb.
$$
The constants $a$ and $b$ have units of molar <entropy> per <temperature> and molar <entropy>, respectively. Ignore their <pressure> dependence and the cell's own <heat capacity>. This is a local <thermodynamics> model, not an extrapolation to zero <temperature>. A constant positive $b$ cannot remain valid once the <solid>'s <spins> order.

For a <reversible thermodynamic process> with no <heat> exchange, total <entropy> is constant. Differentiating gives
$$
(1-x)a\,dT+(b-aT)\,dx=0,
\qquad \frac{dT}{dx}=-\frac{b-aT}{a(1-x)}<0
$$
when $b>aT$ and $x<1$. Define $T_0=b/a$; solving between two states gives
$$
T(x)=\frac{b}{a}-\frac{(1-x_0)(b-aT_i)}{a(1-x)}.
$$
In particular, stipulate $x_0=0$, $T_i=T_0/4$ and $x=1/8$. Then
$$
\frac{T}{T_0}=\frac17,\qquad
\frac{S_{\rm total}}{nb}=\frac14,\qquad
\frac{Q_{l\to s}}{bT_0}=\frac{T}{T_0}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_0}\right)=\frac6{49}.
$$
The last expression is the local reversible <heat> requirement per mole frozen at the final state, divided by $bT_0$; it is not the <heat> exchanged during the isolated compression. That compression exchanges no <heat>. This distinction separates the latent-<heat> sign from the actual boundary <heat> budget.

The utility of the model is its explicit condition: $S_s>S_l$ makes $dT/dx<0$, while positive total <heat capacity> supplies a stable thermal response. More generally, for molar <entropies> depending only on <temperature>, let $C_l,C_s$ be the molar <heat capacities>. Then $dT/dx=-T(S_s-S_l)/[(1-x)C_l+xC_s]$. Incoming <heat> or <entropy production> adds terms to the balance and can overwhelm the ideal cooling. Neither the numerical ratios above nor the straight <entropy> lines are measurements of <helium-3>.

= Spin-triplet p-wave pairing
{parent=Helium-3 superfluidity}
{title2=$S=1,\ L=1$}

<Spin-triplet p-wave pairing> has pair <spin> $S=1$ and orbital <angular momentum> $L=1$, allowing multiple <order parameters> beyond conventional <BCS theory> with zero orbital <angular momentum>. <The Extraordinary Phases of Liquid 3He by David Morris Lee>[Lee, 1996 lecture; 1997 reprint p.658]{ref}

Specifying this symmetry does not determine its microscopic attraction mechanism. A short exchange-symmetry check explains why these quantum numbers fit <fermions>. The <spin-one-half triplet states> $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$, $(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$ are symmetric under exchange. A stipulated orbital factor proportional to relative <momentum> $k_x$ changes sign under $\mathbf k\mapsto-\mathbf k$. Multiplying any symmetric <spin> function by that odd orbital factor gives an antisymmetric pair <wave function>, as required by <Fermi-Dirac statistics>. By comparison, an even s-wave orbital factor needs the antisymmetric <spin singlet state>. This checks compatibility with exchange statistics; it neither selects a stable phase nor derives its transition <temperature>.

= Helium-3 pair order parameter
{parent=Spin-triplet p-wave pairing}
{title2=$d_\mu(\widehat{\mathbf k})=\sum_i A_{\mu i}\widehat k_i$}

For a simple <spin-triplet p-wave pairing> description, let the $3\times3$ <matrix> $A$, with <complex number> entries, map an orbital direction $\widehat{\mathbf k}$ on the <Fermi surface> into a triplet gap vector $\mathbf d=A\widehat{\mathbf k}$. The indices refer to spin and orbital components. A scalar amplitude cannot retain all these relative orientations. <Anthony James Leggett>'s account connects that extra structure to the magnetic responses of the different phases.<Nobel Lecture: Superfluid 3He: the early days as seen by a theorist by Anthony James Leggett>[pp.1004–1008, broken spin-orbit symmetry and pairing-state response]{ref}

Here are two explicit idealizations, with a common positive scale $\Delta$ and an overall <quantum phase> omitted. They show how odd orbital pairing can have either nodes or an isotropic gap. They do not calculate which state has the lower <Helmholtz free energy>.

= Helium-3 A phase
{parent=Helium-3 pair order parameter}
{title2=$\mathbf d_A=\Delta\widehat{\mathbf d}(\widehat k_x+i\widehat k_y)$}

Take a real <unit vector> in spin space $\widehat{\mathbf d}$ and choose orthogonal orbital axes. The model has
$$
A_A=\Delta\widehat{\mathbf d}\,(1,i,0),\qquad
|\mathbf d_A|=\Delta\sqrt{\widehat k_x^2+\widehat k_y^2}
=\Delta|\sin\theta|.
$$
It vanishes at the two polar directions and is largest at the equator. The orbital factor changes sign when $\widehat{\mathbf k}$ is reversed, as required for the odd orbital part of <spin-triplet p-wave pairing>. Calling the phase A does not mean its <order parameter> is one real number.

= Helium-3 B phase
{parent=Helium-3 pair order parameter}
{title2=$A_B=\Delta R,\quad R^TR=I,\ \det R=1$}

Take a proper <rotation matrix> $R$ with <real number> entries. Then $\mathbf d_B=\Delta R\widehat{\mathbf k}$ has
$$
|\mathbf d_B|^2=\Delta^2\widehat{\mathbf k}^{\,T}R^TR\widehat{\mathbf k}=\Delta^2.
$$
This odd linear orbital model has an isotropic gap magnitude. Isotropic gap magnitude does not require s-wave pairing: the spin components can rotate together with the orbital direction. Nor does the magnitude fix the relative spin-orbit orientation represented by $R$.

For both displayed models, $\mathbf d\times\mathbf d^*=0$. The triplet gap <matrix> $\widehat\Delta=(\mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)i\sigma_y$, written with <Pauli matrices>, satisfies
$$
\widehat\Delta\widehat\Delta^\dagger
=(\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf d^*)I+i(\mathbf d\times\mathbf d^*)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma
=|\mathbf d|^2 I.
$$
Thus these models give the same gap magnitude for the two <spin> branches. That last simplification would fail for a general complex triplet vector with a nonzero cross product. The displayed angular curves illustrate these specified matrices; they are not a microscopic derivation of the phase diagram.

= Collective-curvature resonance model
{parent=Nuclear magnetic resonance fingerprint of superfluid helium-3}
{title2=$\omega_0^2=\omega_L^2+K/I$}

Consider an original scalar response model for a small collective <angle> $u$. Stipulate effective inertia $I>0$, restoring curvature $K\geq0$, <damping> rate $\Gamma>0$, and a drive torque $\tau_0\cos\omega t$:
$$
I\ddot u+2I\Gamma\dot u+(I\omega_L^2+K)u=\tau_0\cos\omega t.
$$
The undamped resonance scale is $\omega_0=\sqrt{\omega_L^2+\Omega^2}$, where $\Omega^2=K/I$. This illustrates the role of an additional collective restoring energy. It is not the full coupled spin dynamics of either helium phase, and its parameters are not fitted to historical observations.

The steady response and mean absorbed <power> follow directly:
$$
\chi(\omega)=\frac{1/I}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-2i\Gamma\omega},\qquad
\overline P=\frac{\tau_0^2\omega}{2}\operatorname{Im}\chi(\omega)
=\frac{\tau_0^2\Gamma\omega^2/I}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\Gamma^2\omega^2}.
$$
With constant damping, differentiating this last expression places its positive-frequency maximum exactly at $\omega_0$. Setting chosen $\Omega=1$ and $\omega_L=2$ moves the peak from $2$ to $\sqrt5\simeq2.236$, about $11.8\%$. At $\omega_L=10$ the same curvature moves it only to $\sqrt{101}\simeq10.050$, about $0.50\%$. At large $\omega_L$, the shift is $\Omega^2/(2\omega_L)$ to leading order. A smaller shift at larger field therefore need not mean that the restoring curvature vanished.

= Thermodynamic limit
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

The <thermodynamic limit> describes a sequence of larger systems, not a single enormous specimen: particle number $N$ and <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$ tend to infinity with <number density> $n=N/V$ fixed. Hold the chosen <intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities> such as <temperature> $T$ and an applied <magnetic field> fixed along that sequence. In the <canonical ensemble> the controls are $T,N,V$; in the <grand canonical ensemble> one instead fixes $T$ and <chemical potential>, and the limiting <number density> is a result, possibly with a jump at <phase coexistence>. These are different specifications of a limiting experiment.

The useful limit is usually an <intensive and extensive thermodynamic quantities> normalization of the <Helmholtz free energy>, not a finite limit of the total <extensive quantity> itself:
$$
F_{N,V}=-k_BT\log Z_{N,V},\qquad
f(T,n)=\lim_{\substack{N,V\to\infty\\N/V=n}}\frac{F_{N,V}}{V},\qquad
a(T,n)=\lim_{\substack{N,V\to\infty\\N/V=n}}\frac{F_{N,V}}{N}=\frac{f(T,n)}n.
$$
Here $Z_{N,V}$ is the <canonical partition function>, $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $f$ is <free-energy density>, and $a$ is <Helmholtz free energy> per particle. Thus $F_{N,V}=Vf+o(V)$ when the limit exists; finite systems need not be exactly <extensive quantities> because of the <surface correction to thermodynamic free energy>.

Specify the interactions and the growing containers too. For a conventional fluid, use a fixed <stable pair potential> with a <tempered pair potential> tail, and containers whose boundary layer of any fixed thickness occupies a vanishing fraction of the <volume (thermodynamics)>; uniformly enlarged cubes work, a tube of fixed cross-section does not give the same bulk limit. For a finite-range <Ising model>, keep the <lattice> spacing and bond coupling fixed while enlarging all intended spatial directions. Ordinary bounded wall interactions or <periodic boundary conditions> can then give the same bulk <free-energy density>, although <boundary conditions> can select different <spontaneous symmetry breaking> states. This is a set of hypotheses, not a theorem that every interaction has a boundary-independent limit.<The large deviation approach to statistical mechanics by Hugo Touchette>[§V.F, printed pp. 55–57: stable and tempered interactions and existence of the thermodynamic limit]{ref}

The <Curie–Weiss model> below deliberately uses a different limiting prescription: its all-to-all coupling is divided by $N$. Without that factor its interaction <energy> would grow as $N^2$, rather than as an <extensive quantity>. The model has no spatial wall or <correlation length> to compare with a container; it is an exactly summable illustration of <finite-size rounding>, not a quantitative model of the fluid experiment below.

= Relative fluctuations of an extensive observable
{parent=Thermodynamic limit}
{title2=$\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(A)}/|\langle A\rangle|=O(N^{-1/2})$}

If a total observable has a nonzero extensive mean and its variance is proportional to particle number, its relative standard deviation falls as $N^{-1/2}$. Short-ranged correlations away from a critical point often justify this scaling. A vanishing mean, a shifted energy reference or anomalously large correlations requires separate treatment.

= Finite-system analyticity
{parent=Thermodynamic limit}
{title2=$F_N=-k_BT\log Z_N$}

For finitely many <microstates> with finite <energies> $E_\alpha(h)$ that are <real analytic functions> of a real control $h$, the <canonical partition function> at $T>0$ is a finite positive sum:
$$
Z_N(T,h)=\sum_\alpha e^{-E_\alpha(h)/(k_BT)}>0.
$$
It and the resulting <Helmholtz free energy> are locally <real analytic functions> of $T$ and $h$. Hence such a finite <Ising model> has no literal nonanalytic equilibrium <phase transition>. A limit of these <real analytic functions> can nevertheless fail to be a <real analytic function>; the narrow but smooth crossover can become a sharp <phase transition>.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong/1 From Spins to Fields>[§1.2.1, discussion following equation (1.14): finite sums, analyticity, and macroscopic sharpness]{ref}

Finite particle number alone is not the hypothesis of a finite sum. Classical <phase space> is continuous, and a system of <quantum states> can have infinitely many <energy eigenvalues>: its <partition function> requires convergence and local bounds that permit differentiation or an analytic extension of the integral or <thermal trace>. A divergent <partition function>, a singularly changed <statistical Hamiltonian>, or the zero-<temperature> limit falls outside the finite-positive-sum argument. Observing a steep change in a finite sample therefore motivates a limiting model; it does not measure an actual infinity.

= Symmetry-breaking order of limits
{parent=Thermodynamic limit}
{title2=$m_+=\lim_{h\downarrow0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle m\rangle_{N,h}$}

At zero field and with <spin inversion symmetry> in its <boundary conditions>, a finite <Ising model> pairs every configuration with its spin-reversed configuration. Their equal <Boltzmann weights> force the <expected value> of its <spin magnetization> $m=N^{-1}\sum_i s_i$ to vanish. Below a model's ordering <critical temperature>, an arbitrarily small positive field can instead select the positive <spontaneous magnetization> if size is taken to infinity first:
$$
\lim_{h\downarrow0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle m\rangle_{N,h}=m_+>0,
\qquad
\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{h\to0}\langle m\rangle_{N,h}=0.
$$
The distinction is the order of limits and the stated symmetric <boundary conditions>; <plus boundary conditions> already break <spin inversion symmetry>. A zero-field symmetric mixture of the two ordered states can retain zero mean <spin magnetization> even in the <thermodynamic limit>. Also, $\langle|m|\rangle>0$ in a finite system is not by itself proof of <spontaneous symmetry breaking>.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong/1 From Spins to Fields>[§1.2.1, Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking: displayed nested limit and the immediately following explanation]{ref}

= Finite-size rounding
{parent=Thermodynamic limit}

<Finite-size rounding> replaces a limiting discontinuity or divergence by a finite crossover or peak. For a spatial system near a <continuous phase transition>, the bulk description ceases to apply unchanged when the <correlation length> becomes comparable with the sample size. How the crossover width and location depend on size also depends on the model, geometry and <boundary conditions>; there is no universal $1/N$ rule for every <phase transition>.

An exact finite example is the <Curie–Weiss model> with $s_i=\pm1$, $J>0$, and a conjugate field $h$ in <energy> units. Set $K=J/(k_BT)$ and $u=h/(k_BT)$. Its <statistical Hamiltonian> and finite <partition function> are
$$
H_N=-\frac{J}{2N}\left(\sum_i s_i\right)^2-h\sum_i s_i,\qquad
Z_N(K,u)=\sum_{k=0}^N\binom Nk\exp\left[\frac{K}{2N}(2k-N)^2+u(2k-N)\right].
$$
The <binomial coefficient> counts the <microstates> with $k$ positive <Ising spins>. The diagonal terms in the square add a configuration-independent constant to the <energy>; retaining them fixes the normalization used here. Divide each summand by $Z_N$ to obtain the <probability mass function> of $m_k=(2k-N)/N$. In particular,
$$
\langle m\rangle=\frac1N\partial_u\log Z_N,\qquad
\partial_u\langle m\rangle=N\operatorname{Var}(m).
$$
The <variance> expression makes the finite response nonnegative, while <spin inversion symmetry> gives $\langle m\rangle=0$ exactly at $u=0$ for every plotted $N$.

= Surface correction to thermodynamic free energy
{parent=Thermodynamic limit}
{title2=$F=Vf+O(A)$}

For finite-range interactions and bounded wall couplings, only sites within an interaction range of the wall have altered local environments. In a regular container of linear size $L$ and dimension $d$, the <surface area> scales as $A\sim L^{d-1}$ while <volume (thermodynamics)> scales as $V\sim L^d$. A boundary contribution of order $A$ therefore gives an order-$1/L$ correction to <free-energy density>. Away from a <critical phenomenon>, a regular surface contribution often has the form $F=Vf+A\gamma+\cdots$, with $\gamma$ a wall <Helmholtz free energy> per area. Critical <finite-size rounding>, edges and long-range interactions require separate care; vanishing relative boundary size does not guarantee a simple surface expansion in every regime.

An exact check uses the zero-field nearest-neighbour <Ising model> on a chain of $N\ge3$ sites with fixed coupling $J>0$ and $K=J/(k_BT)$. The open chain has $N-1$ bonds, while the periodic ring has $N$. Their finite <partition functions> and dimensionless <Helmholtz free energies> per spin $g_N=F_N/(Nk_BT)$ are
$$
Z_N^{\rm open}=2(2\cosh K)^{N-1},\qquad
Z_N^{\rm ring}=(2\cosh K)^N+(2\sinh K)^N,
$$
$$
g_\infty=-\log(2\cosh K),\qquad
g_N^{\rm open}-g_\infty=\frac{\log\cosh K}{N},\qquad
g_N^{\rm ring}-g_\infty=-\frac{\log[1+(\tanh K)^N]}{N}.
$$
For the open chain, sum the last <Ising spin> successively: each removed bond contributes $2\cosh K$, leaving a factor of two. For the ring, the <transfer matrix for the one-dimensional Ising model> has <eigenvalues> $2\cosh K$ and $2\sinh K$, and its $N$th power's <trace> gives the displayed sum. The two ends are a constant-size boundary in one dimension, so the open-chain total correction is $k_BT\log\cosh K$, and its per-spin correction is exactly proportional to $1/N$.

Both sets of finite results approach the same $g_\infty$ at the fixed plotted $K=0.8$. Multiplying the correction by $N$ exposes the open-chain boundary term, while the ring correction vanishes exponentially at fixed finite $K$. This model's <thermodynamic limit> remains a <real analytic function> at every positive <temperature>: having a <thermodynamic limit> does not require a <phase transition>.

= Virial expansion
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

The <virial expansion> describes the first departures from the <ideal gas law> as a <power series> in <number density>. For a homogeneous, single-species <gas> in three dimensions at <thermal equilibrium>, write $n=N/V$, with $V$ the <volume (thermodynamics)>, and define the <dimensionless> compressibility factor $Z$ by
$$
Z=\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+B_3(T)n^2+\cdots,
\qquad p=nk_BT\bigl[1+B_2(T)n+B_3(T)n^2+\cdots\bigr].
$$
Here $p$ is absolute <pressure>, $T$ is absolute <thermodynamic temperature>, and $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. The <second virial coefficient> determines the initial slope away from $Z=1$; higher coefficients describe further corrections. The symbol $Z$ here is an <equation of state> ratio, not a <canonical partition function>. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.S5[Tong, Statistical Physics, section 2.5, equation 2.69]{ref}

\Image[second-virial-pvt-and-slope.png]
{title=Controlled gas expansion and extraction of a dilute-gas slope: schematic apparatus and explicitly chosen toy curves}

The left panel sketches the measurement principle; the right panel uses deliberately chosen <polynomials>, $Z=1+bx+6x^2$ with $x=n\ell^3$ and $b=\pm2$, where $\ell$ is a reference length. These curves are not measured data. Their initial slopes have opposite signs, but both bend because of the quadratic term. Using a finite secant from $x=0$ to $x=h$ gives $(Z(h)-1)/h=b+6h$, rather than $b$: reducing the fitting range reduces this particular truncation bias. This algebra explains why measuring one nonideal <pressure> is insufficient to extract a reliable <second virial coefficient>.

Waxman and Hastings's 1971 Burnett experiment used sample and expansion chambers in a thermostatic bath, with a valve between them and a <pressure> transducer linked to a piston gauge. They measured successive <isothermal expansions>: evacuate the expansion chamber, open the valve, record the equilibrated <pressure>, isolate and evacuate that chamber again, then repeat. This avoids separately measuring the absolute gas amount and chamber <volumes>. For <carbon dioxide> at $50\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, their measurements below $35$ bar gave a molar <second virial coefficient> of approximately $-102.2\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$. https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/75C/jresv75Cn3-4p165_A1b.pdf[Waxman and Hastings, Journal of Research of the NBS 75C (1971), pages 165–176, abstract and apparatus]{ref}

An idealized reduction makes the signal explicit. Let $q=(V_A+V_B)/V_A$ be the calibrated chamber <volume> ratio, $p_i$ the pre-expansion <pressure>, and $p_f$ the post-expansion <pressure>. Particle conservation during one valve opening gives $n_f=n_i/q$. At the same <temperature>, division of the two <equations of state> yields
$$
\frac{p_i}{p_f}=q\frac{Z(n_i,T)}{Z(n_i/q,T)}
=q+(q-1)\frac{B_2(T)}{k_BT}p_i+O(p_i^2).
$$
Thus the intercept determines $q$, while the dilute-limit slope determines the <second virial coefficient>. In the actual experiment, <helium> runs calibrated the cell constant and chamber deformation corrections were included. Agreement of the <carbon dioxide> low-<pressure> intercept with the <helium> calibration, and the absence of an anomalous low-<pressure> upturn, checked for gas adsorption on chamber walls. https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/75C/jresv75Cn3-4p165_A1b.pdf[Waxman and Hastings (1971), data reduction and adsorption checks, especially equation 13 and page 176]{ref}

For a limited dilute-gas fit, hold <temperature> fixed, allow <thermal equilibrium> after every expansion, calibrate absolute <pressure> and the chamber ratio, and check leaks and sample composition. Repeat the analysis with a smaller upper <pressure> cutoff and an added curvature term; compare the resulting <second virial coefficients> with their uncertainties. Too wide a range mixes in higher coefficients; too narrow a range makes the nonideal signal hard to distinguish from instrument error. The displayed chamber equation neglects deformation, dead spaces and adsorption, so it is a teaching reduction rather than the complete experimental analysis.

Units matter. With <number density> in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $B_2$ has units $\mathrm{m^3}$ and $B_3$ has units $\mathrm{m^6}$. For molar density $c=n/N_{\mathrm A}$ in $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$, the same <equation of state> is
$$
Z=\frac{p}{cRT}=1+B_{2,\mathrm m}c+B_{3,\mathrm m}c^2+\cdots,
\qquad B_{2,\mathrm m}=N_{\mathrm A}B_2,\qquad
B_{3,\mathrm m}=N_{\mathrm A}^2B_3.
$$
Here $N_{\mathrm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant> and $R=N_{\mathrm A}k_B$ is the <molar gas constant>; the molar coefficients have units $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{m^6\,mol^{-2}}$. A coefficient tabulated per <mole> must therefore multiply molar density, not <number density>.

For the classical formulas below, use a <nondegenerate gas> with <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}$ satisfying $n\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}^3\ll1$, negligible quantum corrections to relative motion, and a stable, sufficiently short-ranged <pair potential>. Diluteness must also control the omitted interaction terms: $|B_2n|\ll1$ alone is not enough when positive and negative contributions cancel. A finite truncation is a local low-<number density> approximation, not an <equation of state> for a dense liquid or across a <phase transition>. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.S2.SS4[Tong, section 2.2.4: classical-density condition]{ref} https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.S5.SS1[Tong, section 2.5.1: dilute interacting gas]{ref}

= Second virial coefficient
{title2=$B_2$}
{parent=Virial expansion}
{wiki}

The <second virial coefficient> is the leading interaction correction to the <ideal gas law>: at fixed <temperature>, $B_2(T)=\lim_{n\to0}(Z-1)/n$, where $Z=p/(nk_BT)$ and $n$ is <number density>. For identical classical particles in a homogeneous three-dimensional <gas>, with an isotropic <pair potential> $u(r)$ and $r$ the centre-to-centre separation,
$$
B_2(T)=-\frac12\int_{\mathbb R^3} f(|\mathbf r|)\,d^3\mathbf r
=2\pi\int_0^\infty\left[1-e^{-u(r)/(k_BT)}\right]r^2\,dr,
\qquad f(r)=e^{-u(r)/(k_BT)}-1.
$$
The function $f$ is the <Mayer f-function>. The factor $4\pi r^2dr$ counts the <volume> of a thin spherical shell; the factor $1/2$ counts each unordered pair once. The result has units of <volume>. Repulsive regions, $u>0$, contribute positively, while attractive regions, $u<0$, contribute negatively. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.S5.SS2[Tong, section 2.5.2, equations 2.75–2.76]{ref}

This is a striking bridge from a microscopic <pair potential> to a macroscopic <pressure> reading. At sufficiently small <number density>, $B_2>0$ raises <pressure> relative to an <ideal gas> at the same <temperature> and <number density>, while $B_2<0$ lowers it. A single value of $B_2$, however, cannot determine the entire <pair potential>: many different functions have the same weighted <integral>. Measurements over several <temperatures> supply more information.

The <integral> must exist. For example, a bounded short-range attraction outside a hard core has no difficulty. If instead $u(r)\sim A/r^s$ as $r\to\infty$, then $1-e^{-u/(k_BT)}\sim u/(k_BT)$ and the radial tail behaves like $\int r^{2-s}dr$: it converges only for $s>3$. A bare unscreened <Coulomb potential> therefore falls outside this formula's ordinary short-range setting. This condition on the tail does not by itself establish the stability or convergence of the full <virial expansion>.

For an exactly integrable toy attraction, choose the following square-well <pair potential>, with diameter $\sigma>0$, attraction depth $\epsilon>0$, and range multiplier $a>1$:
$$
u(r)=\begin{cases}
+\infty,&0\le r<\sigma,\\
-\epsilon,&\sigma\le r<a\sigma,\\
0,&r\ge a\sigma.
\end{cases}
$$
Set $T^*=k_BT/\epsilon$ and $b_{\mathrm{HS}}=2\pi\sigma^3/3$, the <second virial coefficient> of a <hard-sphere gas>. Integrating the core and attractive shell separately gives the exact toy result
$$
B_2(T)=b_{\mathrm{HS}}\left[1-(a^3-1)\left(e^{1/T^*}-1\right)\right].
$$
The core contributes $b_{\mathrm{HS}}$; the shell contributes $-b_{\mathrm{HS}}(a^3-1)(e^{1/T^*}-1)$. Heating weakens the relative <Boltzmann factor> enhancement of attractive configurations, so $B_2$ approaches the positive hard-core value as $T^*\to\infty$. For the illustrative choice $a=3/2$, the zero occurs at
$$
T^*_0=\frac{1}{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{a^3-1}\right)}
=\frac{1}{\log(27/19)}\approx2.846.
$$
At this <temperature>, $Z=1+B_3n^2+\cdots$: cancellation removes the linear correction, not every correction. At low $T^*$, the negative shell contribution becomes large, requiring a correspondingly smaller <number density> for a useful truncated <virial expansion>. The square well is a deliberately chosen model, not a fit to the <carbon dioxide> experiment.

= Exchange contribution to the second virial coefficient
{parent=Second virial coefficient}
{title2=$B_2^{\mathrm{ex}}=-\eta\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$}

For a single internal species of noninteracting particles with quadratic dispersion in three dimensions, expand the <grand canonical partition function> at small <quantum gas fugacity>. With $\eta=+1$ for <bosons> and $\eta=-1$ for <fermions>, $n\lambda^3=z+\eta z^2/2^{3/2}+O(z^3)$ and $\beta p\lambda^3=z+\eta z^2/2^{5/2}+O(z^3)$. Eliminating $z$ gives $p=nk_BT[1-\eta n\lambda^3/2^{5/2}+\cdots]$. Bosonic bunching lowers the <pressure> at fixed density; fermionic exclusion raises it. This correction needs no interaction potential. For $g$ equally populated internal species, the coefficient in terms of total <number density> is reduced by $g$. In the dilute one-species <limit> the equal-time <pair detection correlation> is $g^{(2)}(r)=1+\eta e^{-2\pi r^2/\lambda^2}$. The exchange contribution to the two-particle cluster <integral> is therefore $-\tfrac12\int[g^{(2)}(r)-1]d^3r=-\eta\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$, providing a direct pair-counting check of the coefficient.

= Second virial coefficient of a bounded cubic repulsion
{parent=Second virial coefficient}
{title2=$B_2=\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}[1-(1-e^{-x})/x]$}

For a classical dilute <gas> with <pair potential> $u(r)=\epsilon[1-(r/a)^3]$ for $r<a$ and zero otherwise, put $x=\epsilon/(k_BT)>0$. Substitution $t=(r/a)^3$ in the <Mayer f-function> formula gives
$$
B_2=\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}\int_0^1[1-e^{-x(1-t)}]dt
=\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}\left[1-\frac{1-e^{-x}}x\right].
$$
It is positive for finite positive <temperature>, tends to $2\pi a^3/3$ as $T\to0$ in the classical model, and behaves as $\pi a^3\epsilon/(3k_BT)$ at high <temperature>. The low-temperature mathematical limit need not remain in the dilute classical regime for a real molecular <gas>.

= Mayer f-function
{title2=$f(r)=e^{-u(r)/(k_BT)}-1$}
{c}
{parent=Pair potential}

The <Mayer f-function> measures how a <pair potential> changes a pair's <Boltzmann factor> relative to the noninteracting value one:
$$
f(r)=e^{-\beta u(r)}-1,\qquad \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT}.
$$
It vanishes where $u=0$, lies between $-1$ and $0$ for repulsion, and is positive for attraction. Even an infinite hard-core <potential energy> produces the finite value $f=-1$. This is why expanding in <Mayer f-functions> handles a <hard-sphere gas> more naturally than a <Taylor series> in $u$. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S2#S2.S5.SS1[Tong, section 2.5.1, equation 2.72]{ref}

For pairwise-additive <potential energy> $U=\sum_{i<j}u(r_{ij})$, the exact identity
$$
e^{-\beta U}=\prod_{i<j}\bigl[1+f(r_{ij})\bigr]
$$
turns the configurational part of the <canonical partition function> into a sum of products associated with pairs. Drawing one edge for each chosen factor $f(r_{ij})$ gives the <graphs> used in the <cluster expansion>. The <second virial coefficient> uses just one pair's relative-position <integral>, $B_2=-\frac12\int f\,d^3\mathbf r$.

For the square-well toy <pair potential> in <second virial coefficient>, $f=-1$ in the core, $f=e^{1/T^*}-1$ in the attractive shell, and $f=0$ beyond it. Its positive plateau need not be small: low <number density>, rather than small $f$ at every separation, controls the dilute-gas approximation. The <integral> weights each plateau by spherical-shell <volume>; equal-looking unweighted areas on a plot of $f$ versus $r$ need not cancel in $B_2$.

= Hard-sphere gas
{parent=Pair potential}

A <hard-sphere gas> is a classical model of identical impenetrable <spheres>, each of diameter $\sigma$ and radius $\sigma/2$. Its <pair potential> is $u(r)=+\infty$ for centre separation $r<\sigma$ and $u(r)=0$ for $r\ge\sigma$; the convention at exact contact has no effect on the <integral>. It isolates the effect of particle size without an attractive <potential energy>.

=\frac{\pi\sigma^3}{6},\qquad
v_{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{4\pi\sigma^3}{3}=8v_{\mathrm s},
\qquad B_2=\frac12v_{\mathrm{ex}}=4v_{\mathrm s}=\frac{2\pi\sigma^3}{3}.
$$
The last equality follows directly from the <Mayer f-function>: $f=-1$ throughout the forbidden <volume> and zero outside it. Confusing particle radius with contact separation changes the answer by a factor of eight; omitting the unordered-pair factor changes it by a further factor of two.

Define the <packing fraction> $\eta=nv_{\mathrm s}$, where $n$ is <number density>. Then the <virial expansion> begins
$$
\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1+4\eta+O(\eta^2).
$$
For example, at the toy value $\eta=0.01$, the leading correction predicts $Z\approx1.04$; the omitted terms still matter if accuracy beyond this approximation is needed. The classical <second virial coefficient> is independent of <temperature> because the geometry of forbidden configurations does not change when the <gas> is warmed. The <pressure> itself still scales with <temperature> at fixed <number density>.

This model makes the positive correction particularly clear: eliminating overlapping configurations changes the dependence of the available configuration space on <volume (thermodynamics)>, even though the allowed configurations all have zero interaction <energy>. The figure's other panels add a toy attractive shell and show how its <Boltzmann factor> competes with that geometry. The <hard-sphere gas> is a useful reference model, while a low-order <virial expansion> remains a dilute-gas approximation rather than a dense <sphere packing> formula.

= Villain model
{c}
{parent=XY model}
{title2=$Z_V=\int\prod_x\frac{d\theta_x}{2\pi}\prod_e\sum_{n_e\in\mathbb Z}\frac{e^{-n_e^2/(2\beta)+in_e\Delta_e\theta}}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}$}

A Villain model replaces the cosine <Boltzmann factor> of the <XY model> by a periodic <Gaussian> weight. The <Poisson summation formula> makes its bond weight equal to $\sum_{p\in\mathbb Z}\exp[-\beta(\psi-2\pi p)^2/2]$. The periodic Gaussian retains integer winding defects and permits an explicit separation into <spin waves> and <vortices> in two dimensions. Its replacement of the cosine model is an approximation; subsequent transformations within the Villain model can be exact.

= Vortex Coulomb gas
{parent=Villain model}
{title2=$Z_{\rm v}=\sum_{\{q\}}e^{-2\pi^2\beta\sum_{x,y}q_xG(x-y)q_y}$}

In two dimensions, the integer defects $q_x$ on dual faces are <vortices> of winding $2\pi q_x$. Here $G$ is the inverse positive lattice <Laplacian> with its boundary or zero-mode convention specified. At separations large compared with the lattice spacing, $G(r)=-(2\pi)^{-1}\log(r/a)+\text{constant}$, yielding the pair action $-2\pi\beta\sum_{i<j}q_iq_j\log(r_{ij}/a)$ and core self-energies. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract. With periodic boundaries the total charge is zero and the inverse is taken on the mean-zero subspace; harmonic winding sectors require their own factors.

= Vortex fugacity
{parent=Vortex Coulomb gas}
{title2=$y$}

A dimensionless statistical weight per microscopic <phase vortex> core. In a dilute convention it is $y_0\simeq\exp[-E_c/(k_BT)]$, with core energy $E_c$ and a specified microscopic integration measure. Its numerical normalization fixes coefficients in the <vortex-pair renormalization flow>.

= Harmonic-sector factor in periodic Villain duality
{parent=Vortex Coulomb gas}
{title2=$Z_V=Z_{\rm sw}\sum_{\sum q=0}e^{-2\pi^2\beta q^TGq}\mathcal W(q)$}

On a rectangular torus, an integer height for the <integer-current representation of the Villain model> may have twists $w_x,w_y\in\mathbb Z$. Subtract $w_xx/L_x+w_yy/L_y$ to get a periodic real height. Its Gaussian energy splits into the ordinary gradient energy and $(L_yw_x^2/L_x+L_xw_y^2/L_y)/(2\beta)$. Height <Poisson summation> supplies a phase with dipole moments $P_x=\sum q_xx$ and $P_y=\sum q_xy$. A further <Poisson summation> of the twists gives, up to a charge-independent factor,
$$
\mathcal W(q)=\sum_{W_x,W_y\in\mathbb Z}\exp\left[-2\pi^2\beta\left\{\frac{L_x}{L_y}\left(W_x-\frac{P_x}{L_x}\right)^2+\frac{L_y}{L_x}\left(W_y-\frac{P_y}{L_y}\right)^2\right\}\right].
$$
Here $G$ is the inverse lattice <Laplacian> on the mean-zero subspace. This keeps the smooth <spin wave> determinant separate, while correctly retaining the coupling between vortex dipole moments and global winding sectors at finite volume. Dropping those sectors without stating boundary conditions is not an exact torus duality.

= Vortex charge neutrality
{parent=Vortex Coulomb gas}
{title2=$\sum_xq_x=0$}

For the <Villain model> on a periodic two-dimensional lattice, the vortex charge is the oriented curl of its integer bond variable. Summing over all faces cancels every bond twice, proving neutrality. In an infinite plane, a configuration with net winding $Q$ has far-field <spin wave> action $\pi\beta Q^2\log(L/a)$, so finite-energy localized configurations must have $Q=0$. At an open boundary the total winding instead equals the boundary circulation; neutrality cannot be imposed without a boundary condition.

= Integer-current representation of the Villain model
{parent=Villain model}
{title2=$Z_V\propto\sum_{n_e\in\mathbb Z,\,\operatorname{div}n=0}e^{-\sum_en_e^2/(2\beta)}$}

Integrating every angle in the bond <Fourier series> of the <Villain model> imposes the Kronecker constraint $\sum_\mu[n_{x,\mu}-n_{x-\hat\mu,\mu}]=0$. Thus its dual variables are divergence-free integer currents. On a planar lattice such currents are rotated gradients of integer heights on faces. Noncontractible current sectors must additionally be retained on a torus.

= Dual integer-height representation of the planar Villain model
{parent=Integer-current representation of the Villain model}
{title2=$Z_V\propto\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z^{\rm faces}}e^{-\ell^TL\ell/(2\beta)}$}

On a finite simply connected planar graph, fix the height of the exterior face to zero. Every divergence-free integer bond current is uniquely the oriented difference of the two neighboring face heights. The positive <graph Laplacian> $L$ on the remaining faces gives its energy. Applying the <Poisson summation formula> to these heights and completing the square gives
$$
Z_V=Z_{\rm sw}\sum_{q\in\mathbb Z^{\rm faces}}e^{-2\pi^2\beta q^TL^{-1}q}.
$$
The Gaussian prefactor $Z_{\rm sw}$ equals the <spin wave> determinant of the original graph, up to angle zero-mode normalization. One way to verify this is that the <matrix-tree theorem> equates the spanning-tree counts of a planar graph and its dual. Boundaries can admit nonzero total vortex charge; neutrality is an additional periodic or finite-energy condition.

= State degeneracy

This counts <microstates> sharing a <macrostate>; when the shared quantity is energy, it gives the multiplicity of <degenerate energy levels>. The degeneracy $g(l)$ of a <macrostate> labelled by $l$ is the number of distinct <microstates> having that value of $l$.

= Spin degeneracy

= Microstate
{parent=State degeneracy}
{wiki}

A <microstate> is a complete microscopic specification within the chosen model. For two distinguishable, noninteracting <two-level systems>, label the units $A,B$ and their states $0,1$, with <energies> $0,\Delta$ and $\Delta>0$. The four <microstates> are
$$
(0,0),\quad(0,1),\quad(1,0),\quad(1,1),
$$
with total <energies> $0,\Delta,\Delta,2\Delta$. Distinguishability matters: $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ describe different assignments to the labeled units. Noninteraction matters too: their <energies> add without an extra coupling term.

For a classical particle model, a <microstate> can instead be a point in <phase space>, specifying every <position> and <momentum>. In the quantum <canonical ensemble>, the sum uses <energy eigenstates>; a general <quantum state> may also contain <quantum coherence>, which an <energy> label alone does not specify.

= Macrostate
{parent=State degeneracy}
{wiki}

A <macrostate> groups <microstates> sharing prescribed coarse data. The grouping depends on what is retained: here retain only total <energy>. For the two distinguishable <two-level systems>, the <macrostates> $E=0,\Delta,2\Delta$ have <state degeneracies> $1,2,1$. Although two units are not macroscopic in size, they make this loss of information visible: knowing $E=\Delta$ does not tell us which unit is excited.

At <inverse temperature> $\beta\Delta=\ln3$, each unit's excited-state <probability> is $p=1/4$. Their <independence> gives <microstate> <probabilities> $9/16,3/16,3/16,1/16$ in the order above. Adding within each <macrostate> gives
$$
P(E=0)=\frac9{16},\qquad P(E=\Delta)=\frac6{16},\qquad P(E=2\Delta)=\frac1{16}.
$$
The total <expected value> of <energy> is therefore $\Delta(6/16)+2\Delta(1/16)=\Delta/2$, matching two units each with <expected value> of <energy> $\Delta/4$. Equal <state degeneracies> would give the wrong answer: <macrostate> <probabilities> combine counting with the <Boltzmann factor>. At very high positive <temperature>, all four <microstates> become equally likely, but these three <macrostate> <probabilities> approach $1/4,1/2,1/4$.

= Magnetic susceptibility
{title2=$\chi$}
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

<Magnetic susceptibility> describes how readily a <magnetic field> changes a material's <magnetization>. The field variable and normalization matter: <volume magnetic susceptibility> in the <International System of Units> is dimensionless, whereas a <derivative> of total <magnetic moment> with respect to $B$ has units of $\mathrm{J\,T^{-2}}$. A slope measured against the applied <magnetic field intensity> also depends on the sample's <demagnetizing field>.

For an isotropic, reversible linear response through the origin, write
$$
\mathbf M=\chi_v\mathbf H_{\rm int},\qquad
\mathbf B_{\rm int}=\mu_0(\mathbf H_{\rm int}+\mathbf M)
=\mu_0(1+\chi_v)\mathbf H_{\rm int}.
$$
Here $\mathbf M$ is <magnetization>, or <magnetic moment> per <volume>; $\mathbf H_{\rm int}$ is the internal <magnetic field intensity>; $\mathbf B_{\rm int}$ is the internal <magnetic flux density>; and $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>. Both $M$ and $H$ have units $\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. The <magnetic permeability> is $\mu_0(1+\chi_v)$. <Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Magnetic susceptibility and permeability>[Richard Fitzpatrick, Magnetic susceptibility and permeability, equations (866)–(868): isotropic linear response and permeability]{ref}

The <independent-moment magnetic response> below shows why one number cannot describe every field: <magnetization> approaches a <saturation magnetization>, while its <differential magnetic susceptibility> approaches zero. <Anisotropy> requires a <magnetic susceptibility tensor>; <hysteresis> requires specifying the preparation and sweep branch as well as the bias <magnetic field>, <temperature>, and timescale. The <Gouy balance> turns a small response into a measurable <force> without presuming that its readout is already the intrinsic <volume magnetic susceptibility>.

= Mean-field magnetic-susceptibility amplitude ratio
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\Gamma_+/\Gamma_-=2$}

For a scalar quartic <Landau free energy> with $a=a_0t$ and positive quartic coefficient, the zero-field susceptibility is $1/(a_0t)$ above the transition and $1/(2a_0|t|)$ within a selected ordered phase below it. Their singular amplitudes have ratio two. Below the transition this is a one-sided pure-phase response: the globally minimizing <magnetization> jumps between opposite phases when the field crosses zero. The mean-field ratio is not asserted to be exact in every fluctuation-dominated system.

= Grand-canonical spin susceptibility of a half-filled single level
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\mu=\epsilon,\quad\chi=\mu_B^2/(2k_BT)$}

One noninteracting spatial orbital with two <spin> energies $\epsilon\pm\mu_BB$, in the <grand canonical ensemble> with mean occupation one, has <chemical potential> $\mu=\epsilon$ for every positive <temperature>. Its <spin> population difference is $-\tanh[\mu_BB/(2k_BT)]$, giving total-moment response $\chi=\mu_B^2/(2k_BT)$ at zero field. Empty and doubly occupied orbital states are permitted. Fixing exactly one <electron> instead leaves only the two singly occupied states and gives $\chi=\mu_B^2/(k_BT)$. The factor of two is an ensemble effect in this small system.

= Cubic source coupling and third-order susceptibility
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$f=rm^2+bm^4/2-B^3m\ \Longrightarrow\ \chi^{(3)}=3/r$}

For $r,b>0$, minimization of the homogeneous <Landau free energy> gives $2rm+2bm^3=B^3$. The unique minimum near zero has $m=B^3/(2r)+O(B^9)$. Therefore its first two derivatives with respect to $B$ vanish at zero, whereas its third derivative is $3/r$. With $r=a(T-T_c)$ the first nonzero response diverges as $3/[a(T-T_c)]$. This is response to the variable $B$ through a cubic source, so it differs from the conventional third-order coefficient for a linearly coupled magnetic field. The missing factor $3!$ would give an incorrect susceptibility if one confused a series coefficient with a derivative.

= Curie's law
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{c}
{title2=$\chi=C/T$}
{wiki}

= Curie law
{c}
{synonym}

For independent moments of angular momentum $J$ at small $\mu_B|B|/(k_BT)$, the zero-field <variance> is $\langle m^2\rangle=J(J+1)/3$. Differentiating the canonical mean gives the <magnetic susceptibility>
$$
\chi=\frac{N\mu_B^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT}.
$$
Thus susceptibility is inversely proportional to temperature. The small-field or high-temperature condition distinguishes this law from the exponentially vanishing fixed-field susceptibility of a saturated paramagnet at low temperature.

= Volume magnetic susceptibility
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\chi_v=M/H_{\rm int}$}

<Volume magnetic susceptibility> is the dimensionless ratio of longitudinal <magnetization> to internal <magnetic field intensity> in a linear regime through the origin. Positive response gives <paramagnetism>. Always distinguish <magnetization> $M=\mathcal M/V$ from total <magnetic moment> $\mathcal M$: dividing by <volume> changes the normalization. Mass-normalized and mole-normalized values likewise require the <mass density> or the <volume> per <amount of substance> before comparison with $\chi_v$.

In the existing <Pauli paramagnetism> formula, $M$ denotes total <magnetic moment>, and $g(E_F)$ is the total <spin-summed density of states>. Relabel that response as
$$
\chi_B^{\rm tot}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal M}{\partial B}\right)_T
=\mu_B^2g(E_F),\qquad
\chi_B^{\rm vol}=\frac{\chi_B^{\rm tot}}{V}.
$$
Here $\mu_B$ is the <Bohr magneton>. If its $B$ is a prescribed vacuum/applied <magnetic flux density> $B_0=\mu_0H_{\rm app}$, the dimensionless apparent slope is $\mu_0\chi_B^{\rm vol}$. It agrees approximately with intrinsic $\chi_v$ only when the <demagnetizing field> correction and magnetic feedback are negligible. The existing <Curie's law> formula also uses a total-<magnetic-moment>, $B$-derivative convention; its $N$ counts moments rather than giving a <number density>.

= Differential magnetic susceptibility
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\chi_{\rm diff}=\left(\partial M/\partial H_{\rm int}\right)_T$}

<Differential magnetic susceptibility> is the local slope at a specified bias <magnetic field intensity>, holding <temperature> and other stated constraints fixed. It equals <volume magnetic susceptibility> only for a linear response through the origin. For example, $M=M_s\tanh(H/H_s)$ has $M/H=(M_s/H_s)\tanh(x)/x$, but $dM/dH=(M_s/H_s)/\cosh^2(x)$, where $x=H/H_s$. At $x=1$ these are about $0.762$ and $0.420$ times their common zero-field slope. A finite sweep chord is another quantity, not automatically this <derivative>.

If the differentiated field is instead the internal <magnetic flux density>, the longitudinal <chain rule> applied to $B_{\rm int}=\mu_0(H_{\rm int}+M)$ gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial B_{\rm int}}\right)_T
=\frac{\chi_{\rm diff}}{\mu_0(1+\chi_{\rm diff})}.
$$
This differs from the vacuum/applied-$B_0$ conversion in <volume magnetic susceptibility>. It assumes a differentiable single branch with locally invertible $B_{\rm int}(H_{\rm int})$.

= Magnetic susceptibility tensor
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\chi_{ij}=\partial M_i/\partial H_j$}

A <magnetic susceptibility tensor> maps a small change of internal <magnetic field intensity> to a change of <magnetization>:
$$
\delta M_i=\sum_j\chi_{ij}\,\delta H_{{\rm int},j}.
$$
This <tensor> reduces to $\chi\delta_{ij}$ for <isotropy>. With <anisotropy>, even a linear response need not point along the applied <magnetic field intensity>. As a stipulated example, $\boldsymbol\chi=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,3)\times10^{-4}$ sends $(H,0,H)$ to $(H,0,3H)\times10^{-4}$. State the axes and bias field when reporting its components. A constant <tensor> describes linear response; in a nonlinear material it is the <Jacobian matrix> of the response at the chosen operating point.

= Field conventions for magnetic susceptibility
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}

In vacuum, <magnetization> is zero and <magnetic flux density> obeys $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$. The applied quantities $\mathbf H_{\rm app}$ and $\mathbf B_0=\mu_0\mathbf H_{\rm app}$ mean the field of the external sources at the sample position with the sample absent. The internal <magnetic field intensity> includes the sample's <demagnetizing field>; its internal <magnetic flux density> additionally includes $\mu_0\mathbf M$. A field quoted in the <tesla (unit)> is a $B$ quantity, even when an instrument informally calls it “$H$”.

For a uniformly magnetized <ellipsoid>, along a principal axis with <demagnetizing factor> $N_d$ in the <International System of Units>,
$$
H_{\rm int}=H_{\rm app}-N_dM,\qquad
\chi_{\rm app}\equiv\frac{M}{H_{\rm app}}
=\frac{\chi_v}{1+N_d\chi_v},\qquad
\chi_v=\frac{\chi_{\rm app}}{1-N_d\chi_{\rm app}}.
$$
For a <sphere>, $N_d=1/3$. The axial value approaches zero in the long slender <ellipsoid> limit and the normal value approaches one in the thin flat <ellipsoid> limit. Thus a stipulated intrinsic <volume magnetic susceptibility> $\chi_v=3$ would yield apparent values $1.5$ in a <sphere> and approximately $3$ in a long axial sample. These are calculated geometry effects, not measured material values. For fixed $N_d$, <implicit differentiation> gives the same conversion for local <differential magnetic susceptibility>, $dM/dH_{\rm app}=\chi_{\rm diff}/(1+N_d\chi_{\rm diff})$.

= Demagnetizing field
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$\mathbf H_d$}
{wiki}

The <demagnetizing field> is the contribution to <magnetic field intensity> produced by the sample's own <magnetization>. Inside a uniformly magnetized <ellipsoid>, it is uniform and has the form $\mathbf H_d=-\mathsf N\mathbf M$; the <demagnetizing factor> along a principal axis gives its longitudinal component. These conditions make the scalar correction in <field conventions for magnetic susceptibility> useful.

A cylinder generally has spatially varying <magnetization> and <demagnetizing field>. A volume-sensitive instrument and a short central pickup coil consequently need different effective corrections: one averages over the sample, while the other samples central flux. A shape factor copied from an <ellipsoid> is not an exact correction for every cylinder, especially with nonlinear response or <hysteresis>. <Concise Encyclopedia of Magnetic & Superconducting Materials/Demagnetizing Factors>[R. B. Goldfarb, Demagnetizing Factors, printed p. 104, §2 opening paragraphs: magnetometric versus fluxmetric sensing, nonuniform cylindrical fields and susceptibility-dependent factors]{ref}

= Magnetization of a permeable sphere
{parent=Demagnetizing field}
{title2=$\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H_0/(1+\chi/3)$}

For a homogeneous linear isotropic <sphere> in a uniform applied field, the internal field is $\mathbf H_i=\mathbf H_0-\mathbf M/3$. Combining this geometric <demagnetizing field> with $\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H_i$ gives the title expression. Matching the scalar magnetic potentials $-H_ir\cos\theta$ inside and $-H_0r\cos\theta+C\cos\theta/r^2$ outside provides an independent boundary-value derivation. Tangential $H$ is continuous without free surface current and normal $B$ is continuous. A negative susceptibility makes <magnetization> antiparallel, not parallel in sense, to the applied field.

= Uniformly magnetized ellipsoid
{parent=Demagnetizing field}
{title2=$M=\chi H_0/(1+N_d\chi)$}

For an ellipsoidal sample in a uniform applied <magnetic field intensity> $H_0$ along a principal axis, its uniform <demagnetizing field> is $-N_dM$. The SI <demagnetizing factor> obeys $0\le N_d\le1$. Linear <magnetic susceptibility> $\chi=\mu_r-1$ gives $M=\chi(H_0-N_dM)$, hence $M=\chi H_0/(1+N_d\chi)$. An axially slender prolate limit has $N_d\to0$, a sphere has $N_d=1/3$, and a thin plate with field normal to it has $N_d\to1$. Thus $M=(\mu_r-1)H_0/\mu_r$ belongs to the plate limit, not the slender axial-rod limit. A finite cylinder generally has nonuniform magnetization; an effective ellipsoidal factor is an approximation rather than an exact universal correction.

= Independent-moment magnetic response
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{title2=$M=n\mu\tanh\left(\mu\mu_0H_{\rm app}/(k_BT)\right)$}

Consider a stipulated dilute collection of independent <two-level systems>, each with longitudinal <magnetic moment> $s\mu$, $s=\pm1$, and <energy> $E_s=-s\mu B_0$. The prescribed vacuum/applied <magnetic flux density> is $B_0=\mu_0H_{\rm app}$. Ignore interactions, orbital magnetic response, and the small sample-field correction. This is the magnetic version of the existing <independent spin-one-half two-level system>, with alignment probabilities rather than an interacting <Curie–Weiss model>.

For <temperature> $T>0$, the <canonical ensemble> gives the one-moment <partition function> $Z_1=2\cosh x$, with $x=\mu B_0/(k_BT)$. Taking the <expected value> over its two <Boltzmann weights> gives
$$
\langle s\rangle=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=\tanh x,\qquad
M=n\mu\tanh x,\qquad
\chi_{\rm model}\equiv\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial H_{\rm app}}\right)_T
=\frac{\mu_0n\mu^2}{k_BT}\frac1{\cosh^2x}.
$$
Here $n$ is the <number density>, $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>, and $\tanh$ the <hyperbolic tangent>. For $|x|\ll1$, its <small-argument expansion of the hyperbolic tangent> gives $M\simeq\mu_0n\mu^2H_{\rm app}/(k_BT)$, a <Curie's law> response in the applied-$H$ convention. For large $|x|$, $M$ approaches the <saturation magnetization> $n\mu$ with sign set by the field, and its <differential magnetic susceptibility> falls towards zero.

Both plots use stipulated $n=10^{26}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $\mu=9.2740101\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$, $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, and $\mu_0=1.25663706\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}$. The moment is approximately one <Bohr magneton>; the chosen <vacuum permeability> is a rounded model input. At $T=2\,\mathrm K$, $M_s=927.401\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ and $\chi_{\rm model}(0)=3.91410\times10^{-4}$. The linear approximation overestimates $M$ by less than $3\%$ for $|x|\le0.3$, corresponding to $|\mu_0H_{\rm app}|\le0.89324\,\mathrm T$ or $|H_{\rm app}|\le0.71082\,\mathrm{MA\,m^{-1}}$. These parameters keep the modeled response weak; neglected magnetic feedback is of relative order $\chi_{\rm model}(0)$, rather than an explanation of a material's ordered phase.

= Gouy balance
{c}
{parent=Magnetic susceptibility}
{wiki}

A <Gouy balance> detects the <force> exerted by a nonuniform <magnetic field> on a sample of uniform cross-section extending between high- and low-field regions. TeachSpin's implementation reverses the usual arrangement: a rare-earth permanent-magnet assembly sits on a one-pan electronic balance, while a separate holder brings the sample into the field. The magnet's apparent mass change measures the opposite of the sample's vertical <force>, by <Newton's third law>. The described pole faces have diameter $25\,\mathrm{mm}$ and separation $15\,\mathrm{mm}$; samples occupy cuvettes of cross-section $(10\,\mathrm{mm})^2$ and height about $32\,\mathrm{mm}$. <Foundational Magnetic Susceptibility by TeachSpin>[TeachSpin, Foundational Magnetic Susceptibility, Instrument: The Gouy Method and “Weighing the magnet” method; Specifications: magnet and sample dimensions]{ref}

The same balance calibrates <magnetic flux density> by measuring the reaction <force> on the magnet when a wire of known horizontal length $L$ carries known <electric current> $i$ perpendicular to the field: $|F_z|=iLB_0$. The manufacturer describes water being repelled by the high-field region, identifying negative <magnetic susceptibility>. This observed sign supplies a qualitative test, not a measured <Curie's law> curve. <Foundational Magnetic Susceptibility by TeachSpin>[TeachSpin, Foundational Magnetic Susceptibility, Instrument: Absolute calibration method and Diamagnetism]{ref}

For weak reversible linear response, a uniform sample displacing a weakly magnetic surrounding medium has, to leading order, downward <force>
$$
F_\downarrow\simeq\frac{A(\chi_v-\chi_{\rm medium})}{2\mu_0}
\left(B_{0,\rm bottom}^2-B_{0,\rm top}^2\right).
$$
This follows by integrating the induced-dipole force density $(\chi_v-\chi_{\rm medium})\partial_z(B_0^2)/(2\mu_0)$ along a downward coordinate. $A$ is the cross-sectional <area>, and the two $B_0$ values are applied <magnetic flux densities>. The <force> depends on the endpoint fields; the entire field-gradient profile need not be measured under these assumptions. In the reversed apparatus, $F_\downarrow=-g\Delta m_{\rm magnet}$ after subtracting the holder background, where $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>. This sign must be reversed before inferring <volume magnetic susceptibility>.

Useful controls are an empty-holder reading at the same position, a distant-sample baseline, repeated wire-current reversals during field calibration, and stable <temperature>. They separate holder response, balance drift, force offsets, and thermal changes; these are proposed checks for the stated inference. Finite endpoint fields, nonuniform cross-section, uneven powder packing, stray forces and a <demagnetizing field> limit the simple formula. If <magnetization> is nonlinear, the weak-response force involves $A\int_{B_{0,\rm top}}^{B_{0,\rm bottom}}M(B_0)\,dB_0$ instead of a constant-$\chi_v$ endpoint square. A balance reading then measures an integrated response, not directly the local <differential magnetic susceptibility>.

= Planck law
{c}
{synonym}

<Planck's law> gives the <spectral radiance> of an ideal <blackbody> in <thermal equilibrium> at absolute <temperature> $T>0$. It predicts how much <power> is carried in each small <frequency> or <wavelength> interval, per projected <area> and per <solid angle>. The whole curve is determined by $T$: no material-specific parameter enters this ideal <Planck function>.

Writing $\nu$ for <frequency> in hertz and $\lambda$ for <wavelength> in metres, the two forms of the <Planck function> are
$$
B_\nu(T)=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac1{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1},
\qquad
B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>, and $c$ the <speed of light> in vacuum. Their exact values in the <International System of Units> are $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, and $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/meet-constants[NIST, Meet the Constants]{ref}

The units matter: $B_\nu$ is measured in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,Hz^{-1}}$, whereas $B_\lambda$ is measured in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$. Both are <spectral radiance>, rather than <radiant exitance> or <energy density>. To report $B_\lambda$ per micrometre, multiply its per-metre value by $10^{-6}$; a micrometre-wide interval is $10^{-6}$ metres wide.

For a computed example, at $T=6000\,\mathrm K$ and $\lambda=0.500\,\mu\mathrm m$, the exponent is $hc/(\lambda k_BT)\approx4.79592$. Substitution into <Planck's law> gives $B_\lambda\approx3.17569\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$, or $3.17569\times10^7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$ per micrometre. At the same <wavelength>, the illustrative <temperatures> $3000\,\mathrm K$ and $4500\,\mathrm K$ give $2.60268\times10^5$ and $6.37793\times10^6$ in the latter units. These are ideal predictions for stipulated <temperatures>.

The key <change of variables> is $\nu=c/\lambda$. Corresponding positive intervals carry the same <power>, so $B_\lambda\,d\lambda=B_\nu\,|d\nu|$ and
$$
B_\lambda(T)=B_\nu(T)\big|_{\nu=c/\lambda}\,\frac{c}{\lambda^2}.
$$
Equal-width <wavelength> intervals have unequal widths in <frequency>; the factor $c/\lambda^2$ compensates for this. Merely replacing the horizontal-axis labels of $B_\nu$ by $c/\nu$ does not produce $B_\lambda$. This explains the <frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law>.

A short derivation uses the <Boltzmann distribution> for a single electromagnetic mode with thermal <energies> $nh\nu$, $n=0,1,\ldots$. Put $q=e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$. The <geometric series> gives $Z=\sum_{n\geq0}q^n=(1-q)^{-1}$ and mean thermal <energy> $h\nu q/(1-q)$. The <electromagnetic mode density in a box>, including both independent <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> states of a <photon>, is $8\pi\nu^2/c^3$ per volume per unit <frequency>. Multiplication gives spectral <energy density> $u_\nu=8\pi h\nu^3/[c^3(e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1)]$; <isotropy> gives $B_\nu=cu_\nu/(4\pi)$. This counts thermal excitation <energy>, excluding <zero-point energy>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_41.html#Ch41-S3[Feynman Lectures, I.41.3, quantum oscillator and radiation]{ref}

<Planck's law> connects <quantum mechanics> to a measurable radiation curve. Its small-$h\nu/(k_BT)$ limit is the <Rayleigh-Jeans law>, $B_\nu\approx2k_BT\nu^2/c^2$; its large-$h\nu/(k_BT)$ limit is the <Wien approximation>, $B_\nu\approx(2h\nu^3/c^2)e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$. The suppression at high <frequency> makes the total <energy density> finite. Integrating the curve gives the <Stefan–Boltzmann law>, while locating its peak gives <Wien's displacement law>. Thus one formula predicts both the spectral shape and the total emission.

= Frequency and wavelength peaks of Planck's law
{parent=Planck's law}
{title2=$\lambda_{\max}T=b,\quad\nu_{\max}/T=x_\nu k_B/h$}

At a fixed <temperature>, the <frequency> maximizing $B_\nu$ and the <wavelength> maximizing $B_\lambda$ in <Planck's law> are different ways of describing the same <black-body radiation>. They are maxima of different densities. The inverse relation between <frequency> and <wavelength> does not preserve those maxima because its interval-conversion factor varies with <wavelength>.

For a short extremum calculation, substitute $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ into $B_\nu$, leaving a positive constant times $x^3/(e^x-1)$. Substitute $x=hc/(\lambda k_BT)$ into $B_\lambda$, leaving a positive constant times $x^5/(e^x-1)$. Setting each <derivative> to zero gives
$$
3(1-e^{-x_\nu})=x_\nu,\qquad 5(1-e^{-x_\lambda})=x_\lambda.
$$
Select the positive roots: $x_\nu=2.821439372\ldots$ and $x_\lambda=4.965114232\ldots$. Although $x=0$ solves the rearranged equations, it is an endpoint, not either peak. For $m=3$ or $5$, the function $m(1-e^{-x})-x$ initially increases, its <derivative> $me^{-x}-1$ crosses zero once, and it eventually becomes negative. Hence it has exactly one positive root. Each density tends to zero at both ends, so this root gives its unique maximum. The <bisection method> brackets these roots in $(1,3)$ and $(1,5)$.

Consequently <Wien's displacement law> gives
$$
\lambda_{\max}T=\frac{hc}{k_Bx_\lambda}=b\approx2.897771955\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K},
\qquad \frac{\nu_{\max}}T=\frac{k_Bx_\nu}{h}\approx5.878925758\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz\,K^{-1}}.
$$
These constants agree with NIST's tabulation. <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>{ref} The <wavelength> peaks at $3000$, $4500$, and $6000\,\mathrm K$ are $965.924$, $643.949$, and $482.962\,\mathrm{nm}$. Doubling <temperature> halves this peak <wavelength>.

At $6000\,\mathrm K$, the <frequency> peak is $\nu_{\max}\approx352.736\,\mathrm{THz}$, whose converted <wavelength> is $c/\nu_{\max}\approx0.849907\,\mu\mathrm m$. The $B_\lambda$ peak is instead $0.482962\,\mu\mathrm m$, corresponding to about $620.737\,\mathrm{THz}$. These numbers are compatible: equal-width <frequency> bins and equal-width <wavelength> bins partition the same radiation differently. Specifying which density was maximized is essential when using a peak to infer <temperature>.

= Blackbody radiation
{synonym}

<Black-body radiation> is the <electromagnetic radiation> in <thermal equilibrium> with an ideal <blackbody>. It can be described as a <photon gas>: a cavity whose walls are all at the same <temperature> contains radiation with the universal <Planck's law> spectrum. A small opening in such a cavity approximates a <blackbody> emitter because incoming radiation undergoes repeated absorption inside. The radiation's equilibrium spectrum depends on <temperature>, rather than the wall material. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/6-1-blackbody-radiation[OpenStax, University Physics 3, §6.1, blackbody cavity and emission laws]{ref}

There are three useful quantities to keep distinct: directional <spectral radiance> $B_\nu$ or $B_\lambda$, total <energy density> $u$ in the cavity, and outward <radiant exitance> $j^*$ from its opening. <Planck's law> supplies the first; <integrals> over <frequency> and directions supply the others. For <isotropy>, every direction has the same $B_\nu$, so the full $4\pi$ <solid angle> gives
$$
u(T)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\int_0^\infty B_\nu(T)\,d\nu
=a_{\rm rad}T^4,\qquad
a_{\rm rad}=\frac{8\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^3}
=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}.
$$
Here $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ is the reduced <Planck constant>, and the <radiation constant> is $a_{\rm rad}\approx7.565733250\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-4}}$. The explicit <Boltzmann constant> makes this an SI formula; adopting <natural units> with $k_B=1$ recovers $u=\pi^2T^4/(15\hbar^3c^3)$.

For a short <integral> outline, substitute $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ and use
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-nx}\,dx
=6\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{15}.
$$
The expansion is a <geometric series> with nonnegative terms; repeated <integration by parts> evaluates each <integral>, and $\zeta(4)=\pi^4/90$ is a value of the <Riemann zeta function>. This gives the coefficient above.

The <photon gas> also exerts <radiation pressure>, $p=u/3$. The factor $1/3$ comes from the <angular average> of the squared directional component of <photon> <momentum>, $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/3$. The <thermodynamic derivation of blackbody radiation pressure> gives the same result. In contrast, outgoing <power> involves one projected-direction factor over an outward hemisphere, yielding <radiant exitance>
$$
j^*=\pi\int_0^\infty B_\nu(T)\,d\nu
=\frac{cu}{4}=\sigma T^4,
\qquad \sigma=\frac{2\pi^5k_B^4}{15h^3c^2}.
$$
This is the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> with unit <emissivity>; $\sigma$ is the <Stefan-Boltzmann constant>. The factor $\pi$ follows from <Lambert's cosine law>, rather than the $4\pi$ full-sphere factor used for <energy density>.

For example, an ideal $6000\,\mathrm K$ cavity has $u\approx0.980519\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, <radiation pressure> $p\approx0.326840\,\mathrm{Pa}$, and outward <radiant exitance> $j^*\approx7.34881\times10^7\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. A stipulated $3000\,\mathrm K$ cavity has one-sixteenth each value because all three scale as $T^4$. These describe stored <energy>, mechanical <pressure>, and outgoing <power> per <area>, respectively. In a closed equilibrium cavity, equal opposing radiation balances the directional <power> flow even though $u$ and $p$ are nonzero.

The usefulness of <black-body radiation> is this common reference: <Planck's law>, <Wien's displacement law>, and the <Stefan–Boltzmann law> consistently connect spectral measurements, <temperature>, and total emission. The three illustrative curves in <Planck's law> are ideal equilibrium spectra; applying them to a material requires considering its <emissivity> and whether <thermal equilibrium> is a suitable approximation.

= Dimensional crossover of thermal cavity radiation
{parent=Black-body radiation}
{title2=$U_{2D}=\frac{\zeta(3)}\pi\frac{A(k_BT)^3}{\hbar^2c^2}$}

In a thin <rectangular electromagnetic cavity>, the window $\hbar c\pi/L_x,\hbar c\pi/L_y\ll k_BT\ll\hbar c\pi/L_z$ freezes the nonzero thickness indices while retaining many in-plane <normal modes>. The zero-thickness-index family has one <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> and <density of states> $dN=A k\,dk/(2\pi)=2\pi A\nu\,d\nu/c^2$. Multiplying by $h\nu/(e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1)$ and integrating gives $U_{2D}=4\pi\zeta(3)A(k_BT)^3/(h^2c^2)$. Thus thermal <energy> scales as <area> times $T^3$. The <Riemann zeta function> appears through $\int_0^\infty x^2/(e^x-1)\,dx=2\zeta(3)$.

This is an intermediate low-<temperature> regime of an anisotropic finite cavity. When $k_BT$ also falls below the first in-plane excitation, thermal <energy> becomes exponentially small. If a different model excludes every zero thickness index, its first transverse mode has a finite gap and the proposed $T^3$ regime does not occur. In the three-dimensional continuum regime the corresponding <density of states> is proportional to <volume (thermodynamics)> times $\nu^2$ and thermal <energy> is proportional to <volume (thermodynamics)> times $T^4$.

= Thermodynamic derivation of blackbody radiation pressure

$$

= Microcanonical energy-shell entropy

Changing the macroscopic resolution $\delta E$ by a fixed factor changes $S$ only by an additive quantity of order $k_B$, whereas $S$ itself is of order $Nk_B$ in the <thermodynamic limit>. The dependence on the precise energy-window width is therefore negligible for macroscopic thermodynamics.

= Quantum gas fugacity
{synonym}

The dimensionless <quantum gas fugacity> weights <particle number> in the <grand canonical ensemble>: a state with <energy> $E$ and <particle number> $N$ has weight $z^Ne^{-\beta E}$. The <chemical potential> and one-particle <energies> must use the same reference. When the lowest one-particle <energy> is zero, $z\ll1$ makes both <fermion> and <boson> occupations small throughout the low-energy spectrum. This dimensionless quantity must be distinguished from the pressure-valued fugacity used in chemical thermodynamics.

= Grand canonical partition function
{title2=$\mathcal Z$}
{parent=Grand canonical ensemble}

The grand canonical partition function is
$$
\mathcal Z=\sum_s e^{-\beta(E_s-\mu N_s)}.
$$

= Grand-canonical single-level occupation variance
{parent=Grand canonical partition function}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}n=\bar n(1\mp\bar n)$}

For one <independent> <fermion> level, occupations 0 and 1 form a <Bernoulli distribution> and give $\operatorname{Var}n=\bar n(1-\bar n)$. For one ideal <boson> level in contact with a reservoir, its <geometric distribution> gives $\operatorname{Var}n=\bar n(1+\bar n)$. Both approach <variance> equal to mean at small occupation. Pauli exclusion suppresses fluctuations of nearly full <fermion> states, whereas large <boson> occupation gives <variance> of order mean squared. Fixed-total-number constraints change condensate fluctuation statements.

= Single-level grand partition function in the dilute limit
{parent=Grand canonical partition function}
{title2=$\Xi_k=1+x_k+O(x_k^2),\quad x_k=e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}$}

For a single <one-particle state> with $x_k\ll1$, the empty and singly occupied contributions dominate. A <fermion> state gives $\Xi_k=1+x_k$, a <boson> state gives $(1-x_k)^{-1}$, and <Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics> give $e^{x_k}$. Thus all have the same leading dilute response, $\ln\Xi_k=x_k+O(x_k^2)$. This approximation does not impose an exclusion principle on classical particles.

= Grand potential
{title2=$\Phi$}
{parent=Grand canonical ensemble}
{wiki}

The grand potential is
$$
\Phi=E-TS-\mu\langle N\rangle=-k_BT\log\mathcal Z.
$$
For a homogeneous extensive system, $\Phi=-pV$.

= Fermi-window convolution of the grand potential
{parent=Grand potential}
{title2=$\Phi(T,\mu)=\int_0^\infty\Phi_0(\epsilon)[-\partial_\epsilon n_F]d\epsilon$}

For noninteracting <fermions> with <density of states> $g$, define $\Phi_0(x)=-\int_0^x(x-y)g(y)dy$. The <grand potential> is $\int_0^\infty g(\epsilon)F(\epsilon)d\epsilon$, where $F=k_BT\ln(1-n_F)$ has derivative $F'=n_F$. Integrating by parts twice gives the title expression, since $\Phi_0(0)=\Phi'_0(0)=0$ and upper boundary terms vanish for ordinary polynomially growing <densities> of states. The <Fermi window> therefore thermally averages the zero-temperature <grand potential> as a function of trial <Fermi energy>.

= Grand-potential particle-number variance
{parent=Grand potential}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}N=-k_BT\,\partial_\mu^2\Phi$}

Differentiating the grand <partition function> at fixed <temperature> and <volume> gives $\langle N\rangle=-\partial_\mu\Phi$ and $\operatorname{Var}N=-k_BT\partial_\mu^2\Phi=k_BT\partial_\mu\langle N\rangle$. It follows because derivatives of the logarithm of the state sum generate cumulants of <particle number>. Nonnegative <variance> makes the <grand potential> concave in <chemical potential> in a stable grand-canonical description.

= Equivalence of statistical ensembles
{parent=Statistical physics}

For an additive system with short-range interactions and a regular extensive entropy, microcanonical, canonical, and grand canonical predictions agree for bulk observables in the thermodynamic limit because relative fluctuations vanish. Equivalence can fail for finite systems, long-range interactions, or at singular phase-coexistence points.

An isothermal noninteracting gas in potential $mgz$ has an exponential vertical density and scale height $k_BT/(mg)$.

= Isothermal atmosphere

An isothermal atmosphere has constant temperature. Under constant gravity and ideal-gas hydrostatic equilibrium, its pressure and density decay exponentially with altitude.

For constant gravitational acceleration and temperature,

= Equilibrium height distribution in a uniform gravitational field
{parent=Barometric formula}

For a dilute gas with a floor at $z=0$, constant $g$, uniform cross-section and no relevant ceiling, $P(z)=\beta mg\,e^{-\beta mgz}$. Its mean gravitational energy is $k_BT$, and $P(z>h)=e^{-\beta mgh}$. A linear potential contributes $k_BT$, not the $k_BT/2$ of a quadratic degree of freedom.

= Scale height of an isothermal atmosphere
{parent=Barometric formula}

This is the constant <scale height> of an <isothermal atmosphere>. The pressure and density decay length in an isothermal atmosphere is $H=k_BT/(mg)$.

= Hydrostatic equilibrium
{parent=Ideal-gas atmosphere in uniform gravity}
{wiki}

A static fluid in a uniform downward gravitational field obeys $dp/dz=-\rho g$.

= Isothermal sphere
{parent=Hydrostatic equilibrium}
{title2=$r^{-2}[r^2(\ln\rho)\prime]\prime=-4\pi G\rho/K$}

An <isothermal sphere> is a spherically symmetric self-gravitating gas equilibrium with <equation of state> $p=K\rho$ and constant $K$. The <hydrostatic equilibrium> equations $p'=-GM\rho/r^2$ and $M'=4\pi r^2\rho$ yield the title equation. Its regular finite-central-density solution is distinct from the <singular isothermal sphere>. A finite outer radius requires confinement because the regular density does not vanish at finite radius.

= Homologous isothermal mass-radius relation
{parent=Isothermal sphere}
{title2=$M/R=(c_T^2/G)\xi_b\Psi'(\xi_b)$}

Use the <isothermal Lane-Emden equation> variables $r=a\xi$, $a^2=c_T^2/(4\pi G\rho_c)$ and $\rho=\rho_ce^{-\Psi}$. Truncation at fixed dimensionless radius $\xi_b$ gives $R=a\xi_b$ and $M=4\pi\rho_ca^3\xi_b^2\Psi'(\xi_b)$. Division proves the displayed relation. A common <temperature>, composition and dimensionless truncation therefore imply $M\propto R$, while the external <pressure> generally varies along that homologous sequence. Arbitrary changes of truncation at fixed <temperature> do not retain a common coefficient. A finite regular cloud needs an outer boundary such as that of a <pressure-confined isothermal sphere>.

= Vacuum boundary obstruction for an isothermal sphere
{parent=Isothermal sphere}
{title2=$d\ln\rho/dr=-GM(r)/(c^2r^2)$}

An <isothermal sphere> with constant positive <isothermal sound speed> $c$ obeys the title <hydrostatic equilibrium> equation. Between two positive finite radii with bounded enclosed mass, its right-hand side has a finite integral. A positive starting density therefore remains positive at any proposed finite outer radius. It cannot meet a vacuum boundary with $p=c^2\rho=0$ there without an additional confining structure or a change of equation of state. The <singular isothermal sphere> makes the obstruction explicit: $\rho=c^2/(2\pi Gr^2)$ and $p=c^4/(2\pi Gr^2)$ never vanish at finite radius, and its mass grows as $2c^2r/G$ without bound at infinity.

= Pressure-confined isothermal sphere
{parent=Isothermal sphere}

= Bonnor-Ebert sphere
{c}
{synonym}

A <pressure-confined isothermal sphere> is a regular <isothermal sphere> truncated at a boundary where its internal <pressure> equals an imposed external pressure. The finite central density and the boundary density generally differ. Its radial stability depends on the dimensionless truncation radius as well as the fixed mass and temperature. The first <fixed-mass turning point of an isothermal sphere> occurs at dimensionless radius about $6.451$; the modestly concentrated branch is stable to the radial isothermal perturbations in this model.

= Virial pressure-radius curve with uniform-sphere binding energy
{parent=Pressure-confined isothermal sphere}
{title2=$p_{\rm ext}=A/r^3-B/r^4$}

For fixed cloud <mass> $M$ and isothermal sound speed $c_s$, approximate the binding <energy> by $-3GM^2/(5r)$. The boundary-pressure <virial theorem> gives $A=3Mc_s^2/(4\pi)$ and $B=3GM^2/(20\pi)$. The required external <pressure> peaks at $r_c=4B/(3A)$. A homologous radial displacement is restoring on the branch $dp_{\rm ext}/dr<0$, or $r>r_c$; the smaller-radius positive-pressure branch is unstable. This is a one-radius virial model, not the exact hydrostatic isothermal-sphere sequence.

= Critical mass from a virial pressure turning point
{parent=Virial pressure-radius curve with uniform-sphere binding energy}
{title2=$M_{\rm crit}=(15c_s^2/4G)^{3/2}\sqrt{3/(4\pi\bar\rho)}$}

Eliminate cloud radius using its <mean density> and the critical radius $r_c=4GM/(15c_s^2)$. The displayed threshold scales as $T^{3/2}\bar\rho^{-1/2}$ at fixed mean particle <mass>. Equivalently, the maximum confining <pressure> is $10125c_s^8/(1024\pi G^3M^2)$. The numerical coefficient depends on the assumed <density> profile and on whether the <density> refers to the mean or the boundary.

= Fixed-mass turning point of an isothermal sphere
{parent=Pressure-confined isothermal sphere}
{title2=$[\Psi'(\xi)]^2=2e^{-\Psi(\xi)}$}

At fixed temperature, the particle count of an <isothermal sphere> is proportional to $\rho_c^{-1/2}f(\xi)$, where $f=\xi^2\Psi'$ and $f'=\xi^2e^{-\Psi}$. A fixed-mass variation therefore satisfies $\delta\rho_c/\rho_c=2e^{-\Psi}\delta\xi/\Psi'$. The boundary density changes by $\delta\rho/\rho=\delta\rho_c/\rho_c-\Psi'\delta\xi$. Its first stationary point occurs when the title condition holds. This follows a sequence of equilibria, rather than differentiating the radial profile of one equilibrium. The associated boundary radius obeys $\delta r/r=(1/\xi-e^{-\Psi}/\Psi')\delta\xi$.

= Critical radius of a pressure-confined isothermal sphere
{parent=Fixed-mass turning point of an isothermal sphere}
{title2=$R_{\rm crit}\simeq6.451\sqrt{K/(4\pi G\rho_c)}$}

For the regular <isothermal Lane-Emden equation>, the first fixed-mass pressure turning point occurs at $\xi_{\rm crit}\simeq6.451$. Thus the boundary radius there is $R_{\rm crit}\simeq1.820\,c_s/\sqrt{G\rho_c}$, where $c_s^2=K$ is the squared <isothermal sound speed>. The approximate choice $\xi_{\rm crit}=6.5$ gives coefficient $1.83$. Comparing with the <Jeans length> requires specifying the reference density and remembering that the latter is a wavelength rather than a boundary radius.

= Isothermal Lane-Emden equation
{parent=Isothermal sphere}
{title2=$\xi^{-2}(\xi^2\Psi')'=e^{-\Psi}$}

For an <isothermal sphere>, put $\rho=\rho_ce^{-\Psi}$ and $r=a\xi$ with $a^2=K/(4\pi G\rho_c)$. The equilibrium equation becomes $\xi^{-2}(\xi^2\Psi')'=e^{-\Psi}$. Finite central density and smooth <spherical symmetry> require $\Psi(0)=\Psi'(0)=0$. The regular expansion is $\Psi=\xi^2/6-\xi^4/120+\xi^6/1890+O(\xi^8)$. The enclosed mass is $M=4\pi\rho_ca^3\xi^2\Psi'$, following by integrating the differential equation.

= Hydrostatic self-gravitating incompressible cylinder
{parent=Hydrostatic equilibrium}
{title2=$p(r)=p_{\rm ext}+\pi G\rho^2(R^2-r^2)$}

For an infinitely long circular cylinder of uniform <mass density> $\rho$, the gravitational <Poisson equation> and the <divergence theorem> give $d\Phi/dr=2\pi G\rho r$ internally. The <hydrostatic equilibrium> equation $dp/dr=-\rho\,d\Phi/dr$ and boundary value $p(R)=p_{\rm ext}$ then give the title formula. This model neglects rotation, <surface tension> and end effects.

= Scale height

{title2=$H$}

A scale height is a characteristic distance over which a vertically stratified physical quantity varies. For a decreasing exponential $q(z)=q_0e^{-z/H}$, it is $H=-(d\log q/dz)^{-1}$. The local <pressure scale height> applies this logarithmic measure to <pressure>; density and pressure scale heights need not coincide when <temperature> varies.

= Pressure scale height

A pressure scale height is the local distance over which <pressure> would change by a factor $e$ if its logarithmic gradient were constant. In <hydrostatic equilibrium>, $H_P=P/(\rho g)$; for an <ideal gas> it is $k_BT/(\mu m_ug)$. A small logarithmic pressure interval has depth approximately $H_P\,\Delta\log P$.

Column density is the <number density> integrated along a <line of sight>. Multiplication by an absorption cross-section gives an <optical depth>. P=g\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{\mathbf p^2}{3E(\mathbf p)}f(\mathbf p).

= Quantum magnetism
{parent=Statistical physics}
{wiki}

Quantum magnetism studies collective phases and excitations of interacting quantum spins.

= Quantum XX chain
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{title2=$H=-J\sum_n(S_n^xS_{n+1}^x+S_n^yS_{n+1}^y)-h\sum_nS_n^z$}

= Isotropic quantum XY chain
{synonym}

The spin-one-half <quantum XX chain> has equal nearest-neighbour transverse exchange. Its continuous <spin rotation> <symmetry> conserves total longitudinal <magnetization>. The <Jordan–Wigner transformation> maps it to number-conserving free <fermions>. This quantum chain is distinct from the classical <XY model>.

= Magnetization of the quantum XX chain
{parent=Quantum XX chain}
{title2=$m_z=\pi^{-1}\arcsin(h/J),\quad0\le h\le J$}

For exchange $J>0$, the <Jordan–Wigner transformation> gives a band $\epsilon_k=h-J\cos k$. At zero <temperature>, negative-energy states form a filled interval $|k|<\arccos(h/J)$. Since each <fermion> decreases total <magnetization> by one, the thermodynamic <magnetization> per site is $1/2-\arccos(h/J)/\pi$. It saturates at $1/2$ for $h\ge J$. A finite ring instead has discrete level crossings and parity-dependent <momentum> grids.

= Geometrical frustration
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

= Geometric frustration
{synonym}

Geometric frustration occurs when the interaction graph prevents all local preferred configurations from being satisfied simultaneously. Antiferromagnetic spins can be antiparallel on every bond of a <bipartite graph>, but three equal spins on a triangle instead minimize their total exchange by summing to zero, giving a coplanar $120$-degree arrangement.

= Hubbard model
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{c}
{title2=$H=H_{\rm hop}+U_H\sum_n n_{n\uparrow}n_{n\downarrow}$}
{wiki}

A lattice <fermion> model combining hopping with local density interaction, conventionally written $U_H\sum_n n_{n\uparrow}n_{n\downarrow}$. Its interaction parameter must be matched when a spin-square representation is used: $-2U(\mathbf S^2-3n/4)$ equals $3U n_\uparrow n_\downarrow$, so $U_H=3U$ for that representation.

= t-J model
{c}
{parent=Hubbard model}
{title2=$J=4t^2/U$}

An effective low-energy lattice model obtained from a strongly repulsive <Hubbard model> near one <electron> per site. The retained Hilbert space excludes double occupancy. Its usual nearest-neighbor Hamiltonian has projected hopping $-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}(\widetilde c_{i\sigma}^\dagger\widetilde c_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.})$ and antiferromagnetic <superexchange> $J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}(\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j-n_in_j/4)$. Virtual intermediate states with one double occupancy cost <energy> $U$, giving $J=4t^2/U$ to leading order. Three-site hopping terms of the same expansion order are omitted in this simplified version.

At half filling the projected hopping is blocked. Doped holes restore charge motion coupled to spin correlations. Nearest-neighbor singlet pairing can support a <d-wave bond order> ansatz on a square lattice, but the Hamiltonian alone is not a proof of a unique superconducting <ground state> for all parameters.

= Projected electron operator
{parent=t-J model}
{title2=$\widetilde c_{i\sigma}=c_{i\sigma}(1-n_{i\bar\sigma})$}

An <electron> annihilation operator restricted to the no-double-occupancy sector of the <t-J model>. It removes a spin-$\sigma$ <electron> only when the opposite-spin site occupation vanishes. The corresponding creation operator cannot create a doubly occupied site. These projected operators do not obey the unrestricted canonical on-site <fermion> algebra on all states.

= Uniform Stoner decoupling of the Hubbard interaction
{parent=Hubbard model}

For spin imbalance $s=n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow$ per site, the uniform mean-field interaction energy is $U(n^2-s^2)/4$. With noninteracting <density of states> $D$ per spin per site, the kinetic cost is $s^2/(4D)$. The quadratic coefficient changes sign at $UD=1$, producing the Stoner mean-field instability. This does not describe every strong-coupling Hubbard phase.

= Double occupancy
{parent=Hubbard model}
{title2=$D_i=\langle n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}\rangle$}

Two <electrons> of opposite <spin> occupying the same <spatial orbital>. The mean occupation product measures its <probability> at a specified site. In the two-electron dimer, the sum over sites is also the <probability> of any doubly occupied site, since two sites cannot both be doubly occupied.

= Double occupancy of the Hubbard dimer
{parent=Double occupancy}
{title2=$P_{\rm double}=\tfrac12(1-U/\sqrt{U^2+16t^2})$}

The ground-state <probability> of both <electrons> occupying one site is $P_{\rm double}=\tfrac12[1-U/\sqrt{U^2+16t^2}]$. It equals the derivative of the relative ground energy with respect to $U$. It tends to zero for $|t|/U\to0$ and to $1/2$ for $|t|/U\to\infty$.

= Two-site Hubbard dimer
{parent=Hubbard model}

Two localized orbitals coupled by hopping, with an on-site penalty for <double occupancy>. At two-electron filling the competition between hopping and local repulsion can be solved exactly within the finite basis. It gives a compact example of a molecular bonding crossover and <electron correlation>; it is not by itself a complete real-molecule binding curve.

= Hubbard kinetic exchange
{parent=Two-site Hubbard dimer}
{c}
{title2=$H_{\rm eff}=\frac{4t^2}{U}(\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2-1/4)$}

At one <electron> per site and $U\gg|t|$, the low-energy singlet of a repulsive <two-site Hubbard dimer> mixes virtually with doubly occupied configurations. Its <energy> is $-4t^2/U$ to leading order, while the three triplets have no such shift. With dimensionless spin-half operators this yields the displayed effective antiferromagnetic <Hamiltonian>. Restoring angular-momentum operators with units of $\hbar$ requires the corresponding $1/\hbar^2$ in the <spin> product.

= Singlet ground state of the Hubbard dimer
{parent=Two-site Hubbard dimer}
{title2=$E_g-2E_0=(U-\sqrt{U^2+16t^2})/2$}

For real hopping $t$ and repulsion $U>0$, the covalent singlet and symmetric ionic state have relative <Hamiltonian> $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&2t\\2t&U\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The lower energy is $(U-\sqrt{U^2+16t^2})/2$, relative to the common one-electron energy. It approaches $-4t^2/U$ at small hopping, and its state continuously mixes covalent and ionic configurations.

= Ionic configuration in a two-site molecule
{parent=Two-site Hubbard dimer}

A configuration with both <electrons> on one center, such as $aa$ or $bb$, in a two-center electronic basis. A coherent superposition of such configurations in a neutral molecule represents charge fluctuations; it does not imply a permanent classical ionic charge assignment.

= Covalent two-site singlet
{parent=Two-site Hubbard dimer}
{title2=$C=(|ab\rangle+|ba\rangle)/\sqrt2$}

For orthonormal orbitals $a,b$, the symmetric spatial state $C=(|ab\rangle+|ba\rangle)/\sqrt2$ multiplied by the antisymmetric <spin> singlet has one <electron> on each center and no <double occupancy>. The full state remains antisymmetric under <fermion> exchange.

= On-site Coulomb repulsion
{parent=Hubbard model}
{title2=$U>0$}

The energy cost $U=\iint |a(\mathbf r_1)|^2|a(\mathbf r_2)|^2e^2/[4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|]\,\mathrm d^3r_1\,\mathrm d^3r_2$ for <double occupancy> of a localized orbital. It is positive for an ordinary nonzero orbital. A model retaining only this integral also neglects intersite and mixed Coulomb terms; orbital <orthogonality> alone does not make those terms vanish.

= Single-orbital interacting defect
{parent=On-site Coulomb repulsion}

= Interacting point defect
{synonym}

An isolated spin-degenerate orbital coupled to a particle reservoir has <Hamiltonian> $H=\epsilon(n_\uparrow+n_\downarrow)+Un_\uparrow n_\downarrow$. The four occupation <microstates> have weights $1,z,z,qz^2$, where $z=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}$ and $q=e^{-\beta U}$. Thus $\Xi=1+2z+qz^2$ and $\bar n=(2z+2qz^2)/\Xi$. The factor two counts distinct <spin> states. At $U=0$ the grand <partition function> factorizes into two <independent> fermionic orbitals; positive U separates the two electron-addition thresholds.

= One-electron defect plateau
{parent=Single-orbital interacting defect}

For a repulsive single orbital at <temperatures> small compared with U, <chemical potentials> strictly between the first and second addition energies select a singly occupied defect. Its mean occupation is nearly one while its charge-number <variance> is exponentially small away from the thermally broadened step regions. The remaining <spin degeneracy> does not itself produce charge-number fluctuations.

= Number variance of an interacting defect
{parent=Single-orbital interacting defect}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}n=k_BT\,\partial_\mu\bar n$}

With $z=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}$, $q=e^{-\beta U}$ and $\Xi=1+2z+qz^2$, direct four-state averaging gives
$$
\operatorname{Var}n=\frac{2z(1+2qz+qz^2)}{\Xi^2}=k_BT\,\partial_\mu\bar n.
$$
For $\beta U\gg1$ it has two separated peaks of height $1/4$ at the spin-degeneracy-shifted charge steps. At the lower bare crossing $\mu=\epsilon$, three <microstates> share equal limiting weight, giving $\bar n=2/3$ and <variance> $2/9$. The upper bare crossing similarly gives mean $4/3$ and <variance> $2/9$. Between crossings occupation is pinned near one and fluctuations are suppressed. The identity is grand-canonical; fixing total <particle number> would change the fluctuation question.

= Spin-degeneracy shift of a charge step
{parent=Single-orbital interacting defect}
{title2=$\mu_{1/2}=\epsilon-k_BT\ln2,\quad\mu_{3/2}=\epsilon+U+k_BT\ln2$}

Near the first addition threshold of a repulsive single-orbital defect, the empty and singly occupied grand weights are 1 and $2e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}$. Equal weights occur at $\mu=\epsilon-k_BT\ln2$. Near the second threshold, the single/double aggregate weights are in ratio $2:e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon-U)}$, giving half-step position $\epsilon+U+k_BT\ln2$. These are entropic finite-temperature shifts, which vanish in <energy> units as $T\to0$. They must still be retained when evaluating statistics exactly at an unshifted degeneracy point.

= On-site spin-square normal-ordering identity
{parent=Hubbard model}
{title2=$:\mathbf S^2:=\mathbf S^2-3n/4=-3n_\uparrow n_\downarrow/2$}

For a single spinful <fermion> orbital, $\mathbf S^2=3(n-2n_\uparrow n_\downarrow)/4$. The contraction term is $3n/4$, so $:\mathbf S^2:=\mathbf S^2-3n/4=-3n_\uparrow n_\downarrow/2$. Empty and doubly occupied states are spinless while singly occupied states have spin one half, which also proves the identity directly.

= Itinerant ferromagnetism
{parent=Hubbard model}
{title2=$\epsilon_{k\sigma}=\epsilon_k-\sigma M$}

Ferromagnetic spin polarization carried by mobile electrons rather than a separate fixed-spin lattice. A homogeneous spin field splits the electron bands and competes with a positive field cost. Mean-field instability is governed by the <Stoner criterion>; the ordered metal supports transverse spin waves as well as fermionic particle-hole excitations.

= Itinerant spin-flip particle-hole continuum
{parent=Itinerant ferromagnetism}

Spin reversal in exchange-split bands creates an electron-hole excitation with energy $\epsilon_{k+q}-\epsilon_k+2M$ for a majority-to-minority flip in the simple uniform model with $M>0$. Its allowed continuum depends on occupied initial and empty final states. At $q=0$ all such allowed transitions have exchange energy $2M$; finite momentum changes the thresholds. A collective <ferromagnetic magnon> can remain gapless below this continuum when spin rotation is exact. The continuum is not the same excitation as a longitudinal amplitude mode.

= Finite-momentum thresholds of an itinerant spin-flip continuum
{parent=Itinerant spin-flip particle-hole continuum}

= Mean-field itinerant ferromagnetic Goldstone mode
{parent=Itinerant ferromagnetism}
{title2=$E_{\mathbf q}\sim Dq^2$}

An isotropic spin-polarized metal has a degenerate magnetization direction and a long-wavelength transverse magnon with quadratic dispersion. Nonzero spin density pairs the two transverse broken generators into one <type-B Goldstone boson>. Exchange-split <Fermi surfaces> also support fermionic low-energy excitations; the magnon is distinct from longitudinal amplitude motion and the spin-flip continuum.

= Stoner criterion
{parent=Itinerant ferromagnetism}
{c}
{title2=$U_c\chi_0=1,\quad\chi_0=\int\nu(-n_F^{\prime})d\epsilon$}

In the homogeneous spin-vector mean-field scheme with field cost $M^2/(2U)$ and <spin-summed density of states> per site, the paramagnetic curvature is proportional to $U^{-1}-\int\nu(\epsilon)[-n_F'(\epsilon)]d\epsilon$. It changes sign at $U\chi_0=1$. For a smooth low-temperature <density of states>, $\chi_0\simeq\nu(\mu)$, giving the familiar Fermi-level criterion. Interaction and spin-counting conventions must be specified before comparing coefficients.

= Paramagnetic auxiliary-field curvature
{parent=Stoner criterion}
{title2=$\Omega''(0)/N=U^{-1}-\int\nu(\epsilon)[-n_F'(\epsilon)]d\epsilon$}

For a uniform exchange field with cost $NM^2/(2U)$ and spin-band energies $\epsilon_k-\sigma M$, the <grand potential> is even in M. Its curvature at zero field is $N[U^{-1}-\chi_0]$, with $\chi_0=\int\nu(-n_F')d\epsilon$ and spin-summed <density of states> per site. Negative curvature proves local paramagnetic instability. It does not by itself exclude a first-order transition before that instability, nor determine the stable finite-field amplitude.

= Stoner threshold for an interaction normalized per electron
{c}
{parent=Stoner criterion}
{title2=$U_c=4E_F/3$}

For a three-dimensional zero-temperature parabolic <Fermi gas>, let $\zeta=(N_\uparrow-N_\downarrow)/N$ and $E_F=\hbar^2(3\pi^2N/V)^{2/3}/(2m)$. Suppose the interaction <energy> per <electron> is $UN_\uparrow N_\downarrow/N^2=U(1-\zeta^2)/4$. The total <energy> per <electron> is
$$
f(\zeta)=\frac{3E_F}{10}\left[(1+\zeta)^{5/3}+(1-\zeta)^{5/3}\right]+\frac U4(1-\zeta^2).
$$
Its <Taylor expansion> has quadratic coefficient $E_F/3-U/4$ and positive quartic coefficient $E_F/81$. Hence the unpolarized state loses stability at $U_c=4E_F/3\propto(N/V)^{2/3}$. This $U$ has units of energy. It differs from a contact coupling $g$ defined by interaction-energy density $g n_\uparrow n_\downarrow$, for which $g=U/n$ and $g_c=4E_F/(3n)$. Normalization must be fixed before comparing critical couplings. For $U\leq4E_F/3$, <convexity> of $x^{-1/3}$ implies $f''(\zeta)=(E_F/3)[(1+\zeta)^{-1/3}+(1-\zeta)^{-1/3}]-U/2\geq0$, so the unpolarized minimum is a <global minimum>; a first-order polarized minimum does not pre-empt this threshold in the stated model.

= Stoner-enhanced magnetic susceptibility
{c}
{parent=Stoner criterion}
{title2=$\chi=\chi_P/[1-Ug_s(E_F)]$}

Let $g_s(E_F)$ be the <single-spin density of states> per site per energy, and let the on-site <Hubbard model> repulsion have <energy> $U$. For a weak uniform magnetic field $H$, the half-splitting of the two spin bands is $\Delta=U\delta n/2+\mu_0\mu_BH$, where $\delta n=n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow$. Constant density of states gives $\delta n=2g_s\Delta$. Therefore
$$
\frac{\mu_B\delta n}{H}=\frac{2\mu_0\mu_B^2g_s}{1-Ug_s}.
$$
This is the response per site; multiplying by the site <number density> gives the dimensionless SI volume <magnetic susceptibility>. With <spin-summed density of states> $g=2g_s$, the denominator is $1-Ug/2$. Its vanishing is the mean-field <Stoner criterion>. The unpolarized linear-response expression is stable only for $Ug_s<1$.

= Stoner criterion for an opposite-spin contact repulsion
{c}
{parent=Stoner criterion}
{title2=$U g_s(E_F)>1$}

For an interaction-energy density $U n_\uparrow n_\downarrow$, let $n_\uparrow=n/2+\delta$, $n_\downarrow=n/2-\delta$ at fixed total <carrier density>. If $g_s(E_F)$ is the unpolarized <single-spin density of states> per unit volume, the kinetic-energy cost of the two occupation shifts is $\delta^2/g_s(E_F)$, whereas the interaction changes by $-U\delta^2$. Therefore
$$
\Delta\mathcal E=\left[\frac1{g_s(E_F)}-U\right]\delta^2+O(\delta^4),\qquad
U_c=\frac1{g_s(E_F)}=\frac2{g_{\rm tot}(E_F)}.
$$
For the three-dimensional <free electron model>, $g_{\rm tot}(E_F)=3n/(2E_F)$ and $U_c=4E_F/(3n)$. Both the interaction normalization and the spin counting in the <density of states> must be specified when quoting a <Stoner criterion>.

= Homogeneous spin-field effective action
{parent=Itinerant ferromagnetism}
{title2=$S_{\rm eff}=\beta NM^2/(2U)-\sum_{k\sigma}\ln[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu-\sigma M)}]$}

Integrating free <fermions> in a constant auxiliary spin field gives $S_{\rm eff}=\beta NM^2/(2U)-\sum_{k\sigma}\ln[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu-\sigma M)}]$. Differentiation gives the self-consistent spin polarization. The extensive action is a mean-field restriction of the full auxiliary-field functional integral, not an exact omission of all spatial/time fluctuations.

= Coordination number of a lattice
{title2=$z$}
{parent=Quantum magnetism}

The coordination number $z$ is the number of nearest neighbors of a lattice site. A Kac normalization divides a pair coupling by $z$ so that the interaction energy per site remains finite as $z\to\infty$.

= Néel state
{c}
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

= Néel order
{c}
{synonym}

A Néel state is a two-sublattice product state with antiparallel spin polarization on the two sublattices. It is the classical ordered reference state of a bipartite antiferromagnet.

= Néel order parameter
{c}
{parent=Néel state}

The Néel order parameter is the slowly varying staggered magnetization of an antiferromagnet. On a bipartite lattice it changes sign between neighboring sublattices before coarse graining.

= Quantum depletion of Néel order
{parent=Néel order parameter}
{title2=$M_s=S-N^{-1}\sum_kv_k^2$}

For a <linear spin-wave approximation>, the ground state is empty of the diagonal <quasiparticles> but contains the original spin bosons. If $a_k=u_k\alpha_k-v_k\alpha_{-k}^\dagger$, their <occupation number> is $v_k^2$. The <staggered magnetization> is reduced from $S$ to $S-N^{-1}\sum_kv_k^2$. For the one-dimensional nearest-neighbor <Heisenberg antiferromagnet>, $v_k^2=(|\sin k|^{-1}-1)/2$ and the integral diverges logarithmically. This invalidates finite ordered magnetization within the approximation; it is not itself a determination of the exact spectral gap.

= Dimensional infrared counting for antiferromagnetic depletion
{parent=Quantum depletion of Néel order}

At zero temperature, linear-mode vacuum depletion scales as $\int dk\,k^{d-2}$; at nonzero temperature Bose occupation changes this to $\int dk\,k^{d-3}$. The two convergence criteria are different.

= Logarithmic quantum depletion in a Heisenberg chain
{parent=Quantum depletion of Néel order}

For linear spin waves, $\delta S=(2N)^{-1}\sum_k[(1-\gamma_k^2)^{-1/2}-1]$. The chain has $\gamma_k=\cos k$ and a logarithmic infrared divergence. This signals failure of the ordered expansion, not a physical negative staggered moment.

= Staggered magnetization
{parent=Néel order parameter}

The staggered magnetization weights the local <magnetization> with opposite signs on the two sublattices of a <bipartite graph>. It is nonzero in a <Néel state> even when the uniform magnetization vanishes.

= Uniform magnetization
{parent=Néel order parameter}

The uniform magnetization is the nonalternating component of local spin polarization. In a nearly Néel-ordered antiferromagnet it describes the small canting shared by the two sublattices.

= Long-range order
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

Long-range order means that an appropriate connected or symmetry-resolved correlation remains nonzero at arbitrarily large separation. It cannot be created from a product state by a bounded-depth circuit of bounded-range gates.

= Antiferromagnetic spin wave
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

An antiferromagnetic spin wave is a long-wavelength fluctuation of staggered magnetic order. In an isotropic ordered antiferromagnet its low-energy dispersion is linear.

= Reduced-zone counting of bipartite spin waves
{parent=Antiferromagnetic spin wave}
{title2=$E_{\rm vac}=E_{\rm cl}+\sum_{k\in\mathrm{MBZ}}(E_k-A)$}

Two sublattices give two <bosonic> modes for each of N/2 wavevectors in a magnetic <Brillouin zone>. For a symmetric quadratic pairing block with number coefficient A and anomalous coefficient B, a bosonic <Bogoliubov transformation> gives two equal positive energies $E_k=\sqrt{A^2-B_k^2}$ and the displayed vacuum shift. There are N modes overall. The <quantum depletion of Néel order> per site is $(2/N)\sum_{\mathrm{MBZ}}v_k^2$, where $v_k^2=(A/E_k-1)/2$. Equivalently one can use one branch over a full zone with the corresponding factors of one half. Mixing these conventions doubles the vacuum energy or depletion.

= One-dimensional antiferromagnetic spin-wave dispersion
{parent=Antiferromagnetic spin wave}

The nearest-neighbour isotropic chain has linear-spin-wave energy $\omega_k=2JS|\sin k|$ in units $\hbar=a=1$. Gapless points occur at zero and the zone boundary. Integer-spin quantum gaps and strongly fluctuating finite-spin spectra require physics beyond this approximation.

= Hypercubic antiferromagnetic spin-wave dispersion
{parent=Antiferromagnetic spin wave}
{title2=$\omega_{\mathbf k}=2dJS\sqrt{1-\gamma_{\mathbf k}^2}$}

For a nearest-neighbour <Heisenberg antiferromagnet> on a <hypercubic lattice>, the <linear spin-wave approximation> gives $\gamma_{\mathbf k}=d^{-1}\sum_j\cos(k_ja)$ and the displayed frequency. Near a gapless point its <spin-wave velocity> is $2JSa\sqrt d$. Its ground-state boson occupation is $[1/\sqrt{1-\gamma_{\mathbf k}^2}-1]/2$, producing <quantum depletion of Néel order>. Exact zero modes need an infrared regulator or separate global-rotation treatment.

= Spin-wave velocity
{title2=$c$}
{parent=Antiferromagnetic spin wave}

The spin-wave velocity is the slope $c=\lim_{k\to0}\omega(k)/k$ of a linearly dispersing spin wave. For the leading square-lattice Heisenberg antiferromagnet continuum theory, $c=2\sqrt2JSa$.

= Zeeman splitting of an antiferromagnetic spin wave
{parent=Antiferromagnetic spin wave}
{c}

<Zeeman splitting> is the separation of <energy> levels or mode <frequencies> by a background <magnetic field>. For an <antiferromagnetic spin wave>, opposite circular <spin> polarizations receive opposite <frequency> shifts.

= Ferromagnetic spin chain
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

A ferromagnetic spin chain favors aligned neighbouring spins. Its conventional long-wavelength magnons have quadratic dispersion.

= Easy-plane anisotropy
{parent=Ferromagnetic spin chain}
{wiki}

Easy-plane anisotropy penalizes spin components normal to a preferred plane, leaving a low-energy angular degree of freedom within that plane.

= Ferromagnetic magnon
{parent=Ferromagnetic spin chain}
{wiki}

A ferromagnetic magnon is a quantized spin wave. Without a linear restoring term its long-wavelength frequency is proportional to $k^2$.

= Nearest-neighbour ferromagnetic magnon dispersion
{parent=Ferromagnetic magnon}
{title2=$E_k=4JS\sin^2(k/2)$}

For $H=-J\sum_n\mathbf S_n\cdot\mathbf S_{n+1}$ and unit spacing, the one-magnon energy is $2JS(1-\cos k)=4JS\sin^2(k/2)$. The exact one-magnon hopping matrix has diagonal $2JS$ and neighbour entries $-JS$. The same result follows from the <linear spin-wave approximation>. Its long-wavelength stiffness is $JS$, with energy proportional to $k^2$.

= One-magnon Bloch states on a ring
{parent=Nearest-neighbour ferromagnetic magnon dispersion}
{title2=$E(q)-E_0=J[1-\cos(qa)]$}

For a periodic spin-one-half <Heisenberg model> $H=-(J/\hbar^2)\sum_{n=1}^N\mathbf S_n\cdot\mathbf S_{n+1}$, $J>0$, the fully aligned state has $E_0=-NJ/4$. In its <single-excitation subspace>, let $|n\rangle$ denote a down spin at site $n$ in an otherwise up background. Direct action of each bond gives
$$
H|n\rangle=(E_0+J)|n\rangle-\frac J2(|n-1\rangle+|n+1\rangle).
$$
The normalized translation eigenstates are $|q\rangle=N^{-1/2}\sum_ne^{iqna}|n\rangle$, with $q=2\pi\ell/(Na)$ modulo $2\pi/a$. Their <energy eigenvalues> are $E_0+J[1-\cos(qa)]$. This is an exact one-magnon calculation, not a many-magnon harmonic approximation. Near $q=0$ the energy above $E_0$ is $Ja^2q^2/2$.

The wavenumber restriction comes from requiring a periodic plane-wave coefficient pattern, equivalently requiring this vector to be an eigenvector of a one-site translation with eigenvalue $e^{-iqa}$. The identity $T^N=I$ alone imposes no restriction on a general state: it already holds throughout the ring Hilbert space. An arbitrary finite sum with coefficients $e^{iqna}$ still returns after $N$ translations, but need not be a one-site translation eigenstate unless $e^{iqNa}=1$.

= Next-nearest-neighbour ferromagnetic magnon dispersion
{parent=Nearest-neighbour ferromagnetic magnon dispersion}
{title2=$E_k=4S[J_1\sin^2(k/2)+J_2\sin^2k]$}

Positive nearest- and next-nearest-neighbour exchanges give one-magnon energy $2S[J_1(1-\cos k)+J_2(1-\cos2k)]$. At small momentum this is $S(J_1+4J_2)k^2$. The quadratic-boson result describes the harmonic many-magnon spectrum; higher-order spin-wave interactions remain in the full Hamiltonian.

= Damped ferromagnetic spin wave
{parent=Ferromagnetic magnon}

A damped ferromagnetic spin wave has a complex pole $\omega=\mathord\pm\omega_k-i\Gamma_k$. Both poles of its <retarded Green function> must lie in the lower half of the complex frequency plane so that the response respects <causality>.

= Spin coherent-state Berry phase
{c}
{parent=Quantum magnetism}
{wiki}

The spin coherent-state path integral contains a Berry phase equal, modulo gauge choices, to the spin $S$ times the solid angle swept on the Bloch sphere. Single-valued path-integral amplitudes require $2S\in\mathbb Z$.

= Wess–Zumino term
{c}
{parent=Spin coherent-state Berry phase}

= Berry-phase term
{synonym}
{c}

For a spin coherent-state history, a Wess--Zumino term records the oriented target-sphere area bounded by the spin path. Its variation is local even though a single gauge-patch expression for the area need not be global.

= Antiferromagnetic Wess–Zumino term
{c}
{parent=Wess–Zumino term}

In a bipartite antiferromagnet, the smooth staggered contributions to the spin Wess--Zumino terms cancel between sublattices. Their term linear in uniform canting is proportional to $\mathbf m\cdot(\mathbf n\times\partial_t\mathbf n)$.

= CP1 spinor representation
{title2=$CP^1$ spinor representation}
{c}
{parent=Spin coherent-state Berry phase}

The CP1 spinor representation writes a unit vector as $n_i=z^\dagger\sigma_i z$ with $z^\dagger z=1$. The phase redundancy $z\mapsto e^{i\alpha}z$ identifies normalized spinors that represent the same point of the <two-sphere>.

= Ferromagnetic Wess–Zumino term
{c}
{parent=CP1 spinor representation}

The ferromagnetic Wess–Zumino term is the first-order kinetic term $i\kappa z^\dagger\dot z$ in the CP1 description. A local phase change of $z$ changes it only by the total derivative $-\kappa\dot\alpha$.
