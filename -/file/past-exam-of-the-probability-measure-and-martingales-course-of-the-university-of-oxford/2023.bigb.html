Fix $A\in\mathcal A_1$ and define
$$
\mathcal D_A=\{B\in\mathcal G_2:\mathbb P(A\cap B)=\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\}.
$$
This is a <Dynkin system>: it contains $\Omega$; taking <complements> preserves the equality because $\mathbb P(A\cap B^c)=\mathbb P(A)-\mathbb P(A\cap B)$; and <countable additivity> preserves it under disjoint <unions>. The hypothesis says that $\mathcal D_A$ contains the <pi-system> $\mathcal A_2$. Hence <Dynkin lemma> gives $\mathcal D_A=\mathcal G_2$.

Now fix any $B\in\mathcal G_2$ and put
$$
\mathcal E_B=\{A\in\mathcal G_1:\mathbb P(A\cap B)=\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\}.
$$
The same <complement> and disjoint-<union> calculations make this a <Dynkin system>. The first step shows that it contains $\mathcal A_1$, so <Dynkin lemma> gives $\mathcal E_B=\mathcal G_1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A\cap B)=\mathbb P(A)\mathbb P(B)\quad(A\in\mathcal G_1,\ B\in\mathcal G_2).}
$$
This is precisely the definition of <independent sigma-algebras>, and proves <independence extended from generating pi-systems>.

For $m\geq1$, fix signs $x_1,\ldots,x_m$. The constraints $X_j=x_j$ leave exactly two choices for the $(m+1)$ signs $Y_0,\ldots,Y_m$: choose $Y_0$ freely, then set $Y_1=Y_0x_1$ and $Y_j=x_{j-1}x_j$ for $2\leq j\leq m$. Each input tuple has <probability> $2^{-(m+1)}$ by <independence>. Consequently
$$
\mathbb P(X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_m=x_m)=2^{-m}
=\prod_{j=1}^m\mathbb P(X_j=x_j).
$$
Every finite subfamily is a <marginal distribution> of one such initial segment, so **the entire sequence is independent**, with fair sign <probability distributions>.

Using the usual everywhere sign-valued representatives, for every $n\geq1$ we have
$$
Y_0=X_n\prod_{j=1}^nY_j.
$$
The right side is <measurable> with respect to $\sigma(\mathcal Y,\mathcal T_n)$. Therefore $Y_0$ is <measurable> with respect to their <intersection> $\mathcal G$.

Write $\mathcal T=\bigcap_n\mathcal T_n$. The <Kolmogorov zero-one law> applies to the <independent random variables> $(X_n)$, so every <event> $C\in\mathcal T$ has <probability> zero or one. Also $Y_0$ is <independent> of $\mathcal Y$: this follows first for finite <intersections> of sign <events> from the given <independence>, then for their generated <sigma-algebra> by part (a).

The <sets> $D\cap C$ with $D\in\mathcal Y$ and $C\in\mathcal T$ form a <pi-system> generating $\mathcal H$. If $\mathbb P(C)=0$, the <event> $D\cap C$ has <probability> zero and is <independent> of every <event> in $\sigma(Y_0)$. If $\mathbb P(C)=1$, it differs from $D$ by a <null set>, so the same <independence> follows from that of $Y_0$ and $\mathcal Y$. Another application of part (a) proves
$$
\boxed{Y_0\text{ is }\mathcal G\text{-measurable,}\qquad\sigma(Y_0)\text{ and }\mathcal H\text{ are independent}.}
$$
In particular $\mathcal H$ cannot contain the nontrivial <event> $\{Y_0=1\}$, whereas $\mathcal G$ does. Thus adjoining a <sigma-algebra> need not commute with a decreasing <intersection>.

There is a small version qualification if the assumptions specify only the <probability distributions>, allowing exceptional values other than signs. Put $q_n=X_n/(Y_1\cdots Y_n)$ when the denominator is nonzero, and $q_n=0$ otherwise. For $k\geq n$, $q_k$ is <measurable> with respect to $\sigma(\mathcal Y,\mathcal T_n)$. The <limit superior> of $(q_k)$, after <scalar clipping> to $[-1,1]$, is therefore $\mathcal G$-<measurable> and equals $Y_0$ <almost surely>. Literal <measurability> of an arbitrary version of $Y_0$ need not follow without choosing sign-valued representatives or adjoining the ambient <null sets>. All the <independence> conclusions are unaffected.

The first entrance time $\tau_K$ is a <stopping time>, because $\{\tau_K\leq n\}=\bigcup_{j=0}^n\{M_j\leq-K\}\in\mathcal F_n$. Before this time $M_n>-K$; at entrance, the <bounded increments> give $M_{\tau_K}>-K-L$. Thus the <stopped martingale>
$$
N_n=M_{n\wedge\tau_K}+K+L
$$
is a <nonnegative martingale>. Here the <stopped process> is a <martingale> since its increment is $\mathbf1_{\{\tau_K>n\}}(M_{n+1}-M_n)$, whose <conditional expectation> given $\mathcal F_n$ is zero.

The <martingale convergence theorem> states that a <nonnegative martingale> converges <almost surely> to a finite <integrable random variable>; indeed its <expectations> are constant, giving the required bound in the <L1 norm>. Apply this theorem to $(N_n)$. On $\{\tau_K=\infty\}$ the <stopped process> equals the original one at every time. Hence
$$
\boxed{M_n\text{ converges to a finite limit almost surely on }\{\tau_K=\infty\}.}
$$
The overshoot bound is why the assumption of <bounded increments> matters.

The <sigma-algebra generated by a random variable> $X$ is the smallest <sigma-algebra> on $\Omega$ making $X$ a <measurable function> into $(\mathbb R,\mathcal B(\mathbb R))$. Equivalently, it is the <intersection> of all <sigma-algebras> containing the <inverse images> $X^{-1}(C)$ of <Borel sets> $C$.

Let $\mathcal D=\{X^{-1}(C):C\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\}$. This collection is already a <sigma-algebra>: $X^{-1}(\mathbb R)=\Omega$,
$$
\bigl(X^{-1}(C)\bigr)^c=X^{-1}(C^c),\qquad
\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty X^{-1}(C_j)=X^{-1}\!\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty C_j\right).
$$
The right-hand <sets> are in $\mathcal D$, since the <Borel sigma-algebra> is closed under the indicated operations. Any <sigma-algebra> making $X$ <measurable> must contain every member of $\mathcal D$. Conversely $\mathcal D$ makes $X$ <measurable> by definition. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sigma(X)=\{X^{-1}(C):C\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\}.}
$$
This equality holds exactly as a collection of <sets>, without completing the <sigma-algebra>.

We prove the <Doob-Dynkin lemma> here by <simple function> approximation. Choose $C<\infty$ such that $|Z|\leq C$ everywhere, and let
$$
Z_m=2^{-m}\lfloor2^mZ\rfloor.
$$
Each $Z_m$ is a finite-valued $\sigma(X)$-<measurable function> and $Z_m\to Z$ pointwise. Write its level <sets> as $A_{m,j}$, with associated values $z_{m,j}$. By part (i), choose <Borel sets> $D_{m,j}$ with $A_{m,j}=X^{-1}(D_{m,j})$. Define the <Borel measurable functions>
$$
g_m(x)=\sum_jz_{m,j}\mathbf1_{D_{m,j}}(x).
$$
Although the chosen $D_{m,j}$ need not partition all of $\mathbb R$, their <inverse images> do partition $\Omega$, so $g_m(X)=Z_m$ exactly.

Apply <scalar clipping> to each $g_m$ with bounds $[-C-1,C+1]$, preserving this equality, and define $g(x)=\limsup_mg_m(x)$. A bounded <limit superior> of <Borel measurable functions> is a finite <Borel measurable function>. At every $\omega\in\Omega$ the <limit of a sequence> exists and gives
$$
\boxed{g(X(\omega))=\lim_mZ_m(\omega)=Z(\omega).}
$$
Thus the factorization is exact for a bounded, exactly $\sigma(X)$-<measurable function>. If hypotheses are instead given only up to <null sets>, the corresponding conclusion is a factorization holding <almost surely> after choosing a representative.

0\leq X-X_n<2^{-n}

for every outcome, including $X=1$. A family $(\xi_i)_{i\in I}$ of <integrable random variables> is <uniformly integrable> when
$$
\boxed{\lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_{i\in I}\mathbb E\bigl[|\xi_i|\mathbf1_{\{|\xi_i|>K\}}\bigr]=0.}
$$
The <Lipschitz condition> gives, on $\{X<1\}$,
$$
|Z_n|=2^n|f(X_n+2^{-n})-f(X_n)|\leq2^nL2^{-n}=L.
$$
Our chosen zero value on $\{X=1\}$ satisfies the same bound. Hence the <random variables> $(Z_n)$ are <integrable>, and for $K\geq L$ every tail <expectation> in the definition vanishes. Therefore **the family is uniformly integrable**.

The <uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem> states that a <uniformly integrable> <martingale> $(U_n)$ has an <integrable> terminal <random variable> $U_\infty$ such that $U_n\to U_\infty$ <almost surely> and in the <L1 norm>, and
$$
U_n=\mathbb E[U_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n].

Part (b)(iii) verifies <uniform integrability> for $(Z_n)$, so
$$
\boxed{Z_n\longrightarrow Z_\infty\quad\text{almost surely and in }L^1,\qquad

Moreover $|Z_\infty|\leq L$ <almost surely>. In this particular problem the common bound also yields <convergence in Lp> for every finite $p\geq1$, including <convergence in L2>: the <dominated convergence theorem> applies to $|Z_n-Z_\infty|^p\leq(2L)^p$. General <uniform integrability> alone does not assert these higher-power conclusions.

Choose $Z_\infty$ as the pointwise <limit of a sequence> of $(Z_n)$ where it exists, and as zero otherwise. The convergence <event> and every $Z_n$ are $\sigma(X)$-<measurable>, so this is an exactly $\sigma(X)$-<measurable> version, bounded by $L$. Part (a)(ii), the <Doob-Dynkin lemma>, supplies a <Borel measurable function> $g$ on $[0,1]$ such that $Z_\infty=g(X)$. Applying <scalar clipping> to $g$ with bounds $[-L,L]$ preserves that equality and ensures $|g(u)|\leq L$ everywhere.

Part (c)(ii) and the <conditional expectation> formula from part (b)(iv) now give
$$
\boxed{Z_n=\mathbb E[g(X)\mid\mathcal F_n]
=2^n\int_{X_n}^{X_n+2^{-n}}g(u)\,du\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Since the indices $n$ form a <countable> family, these identities may be taken on one common full-<probability> <event>.

For fixed $n,k$, both the slope and the displayed average are constant on the atom $A_{n,k}$, whose <probability> is $2^{-n}>0$. An equality holding <almost surely> of these constants forces the deterministic equality
$$
f((k+1)2^{-n})-f(k2^{-n})
=\int_{k2^{-n}}^{(k+1)2^{-n}}g(u)\,du.
$$
Sum over $0\leq k<j$. The <telescoping sum> gives
$$
f(j2^{-n})-f(0)=\int_0^{j2^{-n}}g(u)\,du,
\qquad 0\leq j\leq2^n.
$$
Thus the desired identity holds at every dyadic endpoint, including $1$, without evaluating the undefined slope at $X=1$.

Finally, given $x\in[0,1]$, take dyadic endpoints $x_n\to x$. The <Lipschitz condition> implies $|f(x_n)-f(x)|\leq L|x_n-x|$, and the bound on the <Borel measurable function> $g$ gives
$$
\left|\int_0^{x_n}g(u)\,du-\int_0^xg(u)\,du\right|\leq L|x_n-x|.
$$
Passing to the <limit of a sequence> proves
$$
\boxed{f(x)-f(0)=\int_0^xg(u)\,du\quad\text{for every }x\in[0,1].}
$$
This is a <martingale proof of the integral representation of a Lipschitz function>. It constructs a bounded <Lebesgue integrable function> representing the increments of $f$, and in particular proves <absolute continuity> of $f$. No prior assumption that $f$ has a <derivative> is needed.

A discrete-time <stopping time> for a <filtration> $(\mathcal F_n)$ is a <random variable> $\tau$ taking values in $\{0,1,\ldots\}\cup\{\infty\}$ such that $\{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal F_n$ for every $n$. It means that whether stopping has occurred by time $n$ can be determined from the information available then.

For the first elimination time,
$$
\{\tau\leq n\}=\bigcup_{j=0}^n\{X_jY_jZ_j=0\}.
$$
Each <event> in this finite <union> belongs to $\mathcal F_j\subseteq\mathcal F_n$, by the definition of the <natural filtration>. Therefore **$\tau$ is a stopping time**. This verification does not require first knowing whether $\tau$ is finite <almost surely>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Put $P_n=X_nY_nZ_n$. Before $\tau$, all three players are active. Each unordered pair is selected with <probability> $1/3$, and each direction of transfer then has <conditional probability> $1/2$. Given the current holdings $(x,y,z)$, a transfer within the first pair has mean product
$$
\frac{(x+1)(y-1)z+(x-1)(y+1)z}{2}=xyz-z.
$$
For transfers within the other two pairs the analogous values are $xyz-y$ and $xyz-x$. Averaging over pairs gives
$$
\mathbb E[P_{n+1}-P_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=-\frac{x+y+z}{3}=-\frac N3
\quad\text{on }\{n<\tau\}.
$$
At and after elimination the product is zero, since an eliminated player never re-enters. In particular the <stopped process> has increment
$$
M_{(n+1)\wedge\tau}-M_{n\wedge\tau}
=\mathbf1_{\{n<\tau\}}\left(P_{n+1}-P_n+\frac N3\right).
$$
The <indicator function> is $\mathcal F_n$-<measurable>, and the expression in parentheses has zero <conditional expectation> on its support. The <stopped process> is <adapted> and <integrable>: at fixed $n$, its absolute value is bounded by $N^3+nN/3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(M_{n\wedge\tau})_{n\geq0}\text{ is a martingale},\qquad M_0=abc.}
$$
This is the <product martingale for three-player token exchange>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

After $\tau$, the token product remains zero, whereas the deterministic term $nN/3$ continues to increase. Combining this with the pre-elimination calculation in part (ii),
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_{n+1}-M_n\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\frac N3\mathbf1_{\{\tau\leq n\}}.}
$$
The <stochastic process> is <adapted> and <integrable> at each fixed time, and the displayed <conditional expectation> is nonnegative. Thus **$(M_n)$ is a submartingale**.

For $N>0$ it is neither a <martingale> nor a <supermartingale>. If $a,b,c>0$, there is positive <probability> that the first player transfers a token to the second in each of the next $a$ rounds. Until the last such round all three remain active, so this <event> has <probability> $6^{-a}>0$ and entails $\tau\leq a$. At time $n=a$ the displayed <conditional expectation> is therefore strictly positive on an <event> of positive <probability>, ruling out both the zero drift required of a <martingale> and the nonpositive drift required of a <supermartingale>. If an initial holding is zero, $\tau=0$ and the same conclusion follows immediately. The degenerate case $N=0$ gives a constant <martingale>.

The bounded-time <optional sampling theorem> says that for an <integrable> discrete-time <martingale> $(U_n)$ and a <bounded stopping time> $S$, $\mathbb E[U_S]=\mathbb E[U_0]$. It requires no assumption about an eventual unbounded <stopping time>.

Apply this theorem with $S=n\wedge\tau$, or simply take the <expectation> of the <stopped martingale> in part (ii). For $N>0$ we obtain
$$
\mathbb E[P_{n\wedge\tau}]+\frac N3\mathbb E[n\wedge\tau]=abc.
$$
Since $P_{n\wedge\tau}\geq0$,
$$
\mathbb E[n\wedge\tau]\leq\frac{3abc}{N}.
$$
The <monotone convergence theorem> now yields $\mathbb E[\tau]\leq3abc/N<\infty$. In particular $\tau<\infty$ <almost surely>, which has been established without assuming that <optional stopping> is valid at $\tau$.

For each <sample path> with finite $\tau$, $P_{n\wedge\tau}$ eventually equals $P_\tau=0$. Also $0\leq P_{n\wedge\tau}\leq N^3$. The <dominated convergence theorem> gives $\mathbb E[P_{n\wedge\tau}]\to0$. Taking the <limit of a sequence> in the earlier identity therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\tau]=\frac{3abc}{a+b+c}.}
$$
For example, initial holdings $(1,1,1)$ give <expectation> one, as the first transfer must eliminate its giver. This computes the first elimination time, rather than the later time when one player owns all tokens.

Assume $X$ is <square-integrable>. Since $X$ and $Y$ have the same <probability distribution>, $Y$ is also <square-integrable> and $\mathbb E[Y^2]=\mathbb E[X^2]$. The product $XY$ is <integrable> by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>.

We use the defining test-function identity for <conditional expectation>: for bounded $\mathcal G$-<measurable> $H$, $\mathbb E[XH]=\mathbb E[YH]$. Take $H=Y\mathbf1_{\{|Y|\leq m\}}$ and let $m\to\infty$. The <dominated convergence theorem>, with bounds $|XY|$ and $Y^2$, gives
$$
\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[Y^2].
$$
This is the extension of the <conditional expectation> pull-out identity to this <square-integrable> multiplier. Consequently
$$
\mathbb E[(X-Y)^2]
=\mathbb E[X^2]-2\mathbb E[XY]+\mathbb E[Y^2]
=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mathbb E[Y^2]=0.
$$
A nonnegative <random variable> with zero <expectation> is zero <almost surely>. Hence
$$
\boxed{X=Y\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Geometrically, this is the equality case in the <L2 space> <orthogonal projection> interpretation of <conditional expectation>.

The <function> $\phi_a(t)=t\wedge a$ is a <concave function>, and $X\wedge a$ is <integrable> since $|X\wedge a|\leq|X|+a$. The concave form of the <conditional Jensen inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X\wedge a\mid\mathcal G]
\leq\bigl(\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]\bigr)\wedge a
=Y\wedge a\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
The <random variables> $X$ and $Y$ have the same <probability distribution>, so $\mathbb E[X\wedge a]=\mathbb E[Y\wedge a]$. Using the <law of total expectation>, the nonnegative difference between the right and left sides of the inequality has zero <expectation>. It must vanish <almost surely>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X\wedge a\mid\mathcal G]=Y\wedge a\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
The equality comes from the distributional assumption as well as the <conditional Jensen inequality>.

We first establish the lower truncation identity as well. The pair $(-X,-Y)$ has the same hypotheses as $(X,Y)$, because <conditional expectation> is linear and negation preserves equality of <probability distributions>. Apply part (ii) to this pair and multiply by $-1$ to get

For every real $t$,
$$
(t\wedge a)\vee(-a)=(t\wedge a)+(t\vee(-a))-t.
$$
Using this identity, part (ii), the lower truncation identity, and linearity of <conditional expectation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[(X\wedge a)\vee(-a)\mid\mathcal G]
=(Y\wedge a)\vee(-a)\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
In particular the argument does not assume that a <function> obtained by <scalar clipping> is a <convex function> or a <concave function>.

\boxed{X=Y\quad\text{almost surely}.}

Let $\mathcal G=T^{-1}(\mathcal F)=\{T^{-1}(A):A\in\mathcal F\}$. <Inverse images> commute with <complements> and <countable unions>, so this is a <sub-sigma-algebra> of $\mathcal F$. The <random variable> $X\circ T$ is $\mathcal G$-<measurable>.

The assumption $\mathbb P\circ T^{-1}=\mathbb P$ says that $T$ is a <measure-preserving transformation>. Consequently $X\circ T$ has the same <probability distribution> as $X$ and is <integrable>. It also implies $\mathbb E[W\circ T]=\mathbb E[W]$ for every <integrable random variable> $W$, first for <indicator functions>, then for <simple functions>, and then by positive and negative approximation.

To identify a <conditional expectation>, take any $A\in\mathcal F$. The assumed identity with the bounded <random variable> $Z=\mathbf1_A$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{T^{-1}(A)}]
&=\mathbb E[X(\mathbf1_A\circ T)]
=\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_A]\\
&=\mathbb E[(X\mathbf1_A)\circ T]
=\mathbb E[(X\circ T)\mathbf1_{T^{-1}(A)}].
\end{aligned}
$$
Every member of $\mathcal G$ has the displayed form $T^{-1}(A)$, so this verifies all defining <conditional expectation> identities. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[X\mid\mathcal G]=X\circ T\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$
Apply part (iii), since this <conditional expectation> has the same <probability distribution> as $X$. We conclude
$$
\boxed{X=X\circ T\quad\mathbb P\text{-almost surely}.}
$$
This is <invariance from integral duality for a measure-preserving transformation>; neither invertibility of $T$ nor a <second moment> of $X$ is required.
