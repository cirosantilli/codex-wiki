==== i

===== Solution

Put $A=\{|X|\geq Z\}$. Both $A$ and its <indicator function> are <measurable>, and $0\leq Z\mathbf1_A\leq |X|$. Thus $Z\mathbf1_A$ is an <integrable random variable>. Monotonicity of <conditional expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]\leq\mathbb E[|X|\mid\mathcal G]\quad\text{a.s.}
$$
The measurable-factor property of <conditional expectation> says that a bounded $\mathcal G$-<measurable function> $H$ satisfies $\mathbb E[HW\mid\mathcal G]=H\mathbb E[W\mid\mathcal G]$ for every <integrable random variable> $W$. Here the factor $Z$ need not be bounded. Apply that property first to $Z\wedge m$ and $W=\mathbf1_A$, then use <monotone convergence> on the nonnegative products, including the conditional version, to obtain
$$
\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]
=\lim_{m\to\infty}(Z\wedge m)\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G]
=Z\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A\mid\mathcal G].
$$
All the left-hand <conditional expectations> are well defined; the final one is finite <almost surely> because $Z\mathbf1_A$ is an <integrable random variable>. Since $Z>0$ <almost surely>, division now yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{\{|X|\geq Z\}}\mid\mathcal G]
\leq Z^{-1}\mathbb E[|X|\mid\mathcal G]\quad\text{a.s.}}
$$
This proves the <conditional Markov inequality with a measurable threshold> without assuming that $Z^{-1}|X|$ is an <integrable random variable>. The measurable-factor rule, with its product-integrability condition, is also stated in the standard <conditional expectation> theory.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§4.1.2, Theorem 4.1.14, printed pp. 212–213: bringing out a measurable factor]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $A=g(X)$ and $B=g(Y)$. The two <conditional expectation> identities supply a $\sigma(Y)$-<measurable> version of $B$ and a $\sigma(X)$-<measurable> version of $A$. Work with those versions, which do not change any <almost sure equality>. Both lie in <L2 space>, and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> makes their product an <integrable random variable>.

Testing the first <conditional expectation> identity against $B$, and the second against $A$, gives
$$
\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[B^2],\qquad
\mathbb E[AB]=\mathbb E[A^2].
$$
For completeness, these tests with unbounded multipliers are valid by first using the bounded truncations $B_m=(-m)\vee(B\wedge m)$ and $A_m=(-m)\vee(A\wedge m)$, then passing to the limit with the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and <convergence in L2>. Consequently,
$$
\mathbb E[(A-B)^2]=\mathbb E[A^2]+\mathbb E[B^2]-2\mathbb E[AB]=0.
$$
A nonnegative <random variable> with zero <expected value> vanishes <almost surely>. Therefore **$g(X)=g(Y)$ almost surely**. This is <mutual conditional expectation rigidity in L2>; it also follows from the <equality case for conditional second moments>.

For a counterexample to equality of the original <random variables>, let $X$ give <probability> $1/4$ to each of $-2,-1,1,2$, set $Y=-X$, and use the nonconstant <function> $g(t)=t^2$. Then $g(X)=g(Y)=X^2$ takes both values $1$ and $4$. It is <measurable> with respect to both generated <sigma-algebras>, so both required <conditional expectation> identities hold. Symmetry gives $\mathbb EX=\mathbb EY=0$, while neither <random variable> can be zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=\mathbb EX)=\mathbb P(Y=\mathbb EY)=0,\qquad\mathbb P(X=Y)=0.}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For every $n$, $\mathcal F_n$ is a <sigma-algebra> contained in $\mathcal F$, and including one more <random variable> among its generators can only enlarge it. Hence $\mathcal F_n\subseteq\mathcal F_{n+1}$; the trivial $\mathcal F_0$ is contained in every <sigma-algebra>. These are exactly the requirements for a <filtration>.

The constant $X_0=x$ is $\mathcal F_0$-<measurable>. Suppose $X_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-<measurable>. The pair $(X_n,U_{n+1})$ is then $\mathcal F_{n+1}$-<measurable>, and the <event> $\{U_{n+1}\leq X_n\}$ is $\mathcal F_{n+1}$-<measurable> because comparison of two real coordinates defines a <Borel set>. Define its <indicator function> by $B_{n+1}$. The recursion becomes
$$
X_{n+1}=\frac{X_n+B_{n+1}}2.
$$
It is a <measurable function> of that pair. <Mathematical induction> proves $X_n$ is $\mathcal F_n$-<measurable> for every $n$, so **$(X_n)$ is an adapted process**. The same recursion also proves $0<X_n<1$ at every finite time, a useful bound for the later <integrability> arguments.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Retain the binary <random variables> $B_j=\mathbf1_{\{U_j\leq X_{j-1}\}}$. Iterating the recursion gives
$$
2^nX_n=x+\sum_{j=1}^n2^{j-1}B_j.
$$
Each binary history determines the full vector $(X_0,\ldots,X_n)$, so its <sigma-algebra> is finite by the <sigma-algebra of a finite-valued random vector>. Conversely, because $0<x<1$, the integer $2^nX_n-x$ uniquely determines the digits $B_1,\ldots,B_n$; also $B_j=2X_j-X_{j-1}$. Thus
$$
\mathcal F_n^X=\sigma(B_1,\ldots,B_n)=\sigma(X_n).
$$
All $2^n$ binary histories have positive <probability>. By <independence> of the driving <uniform random variables>, along a fixed finite history every intermediate state is in $(0,1)$, so each subsequent conditional branch <probability>, either $X_{j-1}$ or $1-X_{j-1}$, is strictly positive. It follows that $\mathcal F_n^X$ has $2^n$ nonempty fibres, each an <atom of a sigma-algebra>, and exactly $2^{2^n}$ elements.

<Adaptedness> from the preceding part gives $\mathcal F_n^X\subseteq\mathcal F_n$. For $n\geq1$, however, $\mathcal F_n$ contains the <events> $\{U_1\leq t\}$ for every $t\in[0,1]$. They have distinct <probabilities> $t$, so there are infinitely many distinct such <events>. Consequently the finite <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal F_n^X$ cannot equal $\mathcal F_n$. **The inclusion is strict for every $n\geq1$.**

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <adapted process> is bounded by $1$, so every $X_n$ is an <integrable random variable>, indeed a <square-integrable> <random variable>. <Independence> of $U_{n+1}$ from $\mathcal F_n$ and its <continuous uniform distribution> give
$$
\mathbb E[B_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n.
$$
This calculation with a random threshold is justified by conditioning on the $\mathcal F_n$-<measurable> value of $X_n$: for a fixed $t\in[0,1]$, the <Lebesgue integral> $\int_0^1\mathbf1_{\{u\leq t\}}\,du$ equals $t$. More formally, <independence> integrates the bounded <Borel measurable function> $(t,u)\mapsto\mathbf1_{\{u\leq t\}}$ over its second coordinate. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\tfrac12X_n+\tfrac12\mathbb E[B_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n,
$$
which verifies the <martingale> property for this <filtration>.

Since $B_{n+1}^2=B_{n+1}$, the <conditional expectation> of the squared <martingale difference> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(X_{n+1}-X_n)^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
&=\tfrac14\mathbb E[(B_{n+1}-X_n)^2\mid\mathcal F_n]\\
&=\tfrac14(X_n-2X_n^2+X_n^2)
=\tfrac14X_n(1-X_n).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <law of total expectation> yields **$\mathbb E[(X_{n+1}-X_n)^2]=\frac14\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]$**. In particular, $\mathbb EX_n=x$ for every $n$.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

The <L2-bounded martingale convergence theorem> states that a <martingale> with $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|^2<\infty$ converges both <almost surely> and with <convergence in L2> to a <square-integrable> <random variable>. Its hypotheses hold because $0<X_n<1$. Thus there is $X_\infty\in[0,1]$ <almost surely> with both kinds of convergence. Alternatively, the <martingale convergence theorem> gives the <almost sure convergence>, and the bound $|X_n-X_\infty|^2\leq1$ gives <convergence in L2> by the <dominated convergence theorem>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§4.4, Theorem 4.4.6, printed p. 237: the Lp martingale convergence theorem]{ref}

The calculation of the <conditional expectation> of the square is especially informative. The <martingale> property makes the cross term vanish in the square expansion, so
$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]
=X_n^2+\tfrac14X_n(1-X_n).
$$
Subtracting this from $\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=X_n$ gives
$$
\mathbb E[X_{n+1}(1-X_{n+1})\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\tfrac34X_n(1-X_n).
$$
Taking <expected values> and iterating proves
$$
\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]=x(1-x)(3/4)^n.
$$
By <bounded convergence theorem>, $\mathbb E[X_\infty(1-X_\infty)]=0$. The <random variable> inside this <expected value> is nonnegative, so it is zero <almost surely>; on $[0,1]$ this forces $X_\infty\in\{0,1\}$. Again by <bounded convergence theorem>, $\mathbb EX_\infty=\lim_n\mathbb EX_n=x$. Hence
$$
\boxed{X_\infty\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(x),\qquad
\mathbb P(X_\infty=1)=x,\quad\mathbb P(X_\infty=0)=1-x.}
$$
This is the <Bernoulli polarization martingale> with update size $1/2$. It also has an exact <mean squared error>: the terminal representation $X_n=\mathbb E[X_\infty\mid\mathcal F_n]$ and the <equality case for conditional second moments> give
$$
\mathbb E[(X_\infty-X_n)^2]
=\mathbb E[X_\infty^2]-\mathbb E[X_n^2]
=x(1-x)(3/4)^n.
$$

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $m=\mathbb EX$. First, $m$ lies in the open <interval> $I$. For example, if $I$ has a finite lower endpoint $\ell$, then $X-\ell>0$ <almost surely>, and its <expected value> is strictly positive; hence $m>\ell$. The corresponding argument at a finite upper endpoint gives the other strict inequality. <Integrability> ensures $m$ is finite when either endpoint is infinite.

The tangent <affine function> at $m$ gives the <supporting inequality for a differentiable convex function>. Under the stipulated condition $f''>0$, contact with that tangent occurs only at $m$: define
$$
h(t)=f(t)-f(m)-f'(m)(t-m).
$$
Then $h(m)=0$, and $h(t)>0$ for every $t\ne m$. One direct proof uses $h'(t)=f'(t)-f'(m)$: $f'$ is strictly increasing, so $h$ decreases towards $m$ on the left and increases away from $m$ on the right. Therefore
$$
\mathbb E[f(X)]=f(m)+\mathbb E[h(X)]\geq f(m),
$$
with equality exactly when $h(X)=0$ <almost surely>, equivalently when $X=m$ <almost surely>. Conversely, an <almost surely> constant $X=m$ plainly gives equality. This proves the <strict Jensen equality criterion>.

No extra assumption that $f(X)$ is an <integrable random variable> is needed to interpret this argument. Its <negative part> is bounded by the absolute value of the <integrable random variable> $f(m)+f'(m)(X-m)$, since $h(X)\geq0$. Thus its <expected value> is defined in $(-\infty,+\infty]$; if it equals the finite number $f(m)$, then $\mathbb E[h(X)]=0$ and the required <integrability> follows automatically. **Equality holds if and only if $X=\mathbb EX$ almost surely.**

{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For each $N\geq1$, put $C_N=\bigcup_{n\geq N}A_n$. Closure of a <sigma-algebra> under <countable unions> and <countable intersections> gives
$$
A=\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n=\bigcap_{N\geq1}C_N\in\mathcal F.
$$
The <events> $C_N$ decrease to $A$. <Continuity of probability> from above applies because $\mathbb P(C_1)\leq1<\infty$. Also, $A_n\subseteq C_N$ whenever $n\geq N$, so
$$
\mathbb P(C_N)\geq\sup_{n\geq N}\mathbb P(A_n).
$$
Letting $N\to\infty$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb P(C_N)
\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq N}\mathbb P(A_n)
=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(A_n).}
$$
This <reverse Fatou inequality for events> requires no <independence>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $B_n=\{X_n>a_n\}$. If $B_n$ occurs, the running maximum at time $n$ exceeds $a_n$, so $B_n\subseteq A_n$ and $\limsup_nB_n\subseteq A$.

For the reverse inclusion, fix an outcome for which only finitely many $B_n$ occur. Choose $N$ such that $X_m\leq a_m$ for every $m\geq N$. The finite prefix has a finite maximum $K=\max_{1\leq m<N}X_m$ when $N>1$; there is no prefix to check when $N=1$. Since $a_n\to\infty$, for every sufficiently large $n$ that prefix satisfies $K\leq a_n$. For the remaining indices $N\leq m\leq n$, the nondecreasing thresholds give
$$
X_m\leq a_m\leq a_n.
$$
Thus $\max_{m\leq n}X_m\leq a_n$ for every sufficiently large $n$, so only finitely many $A_n$ occur. Taking the contrapositive proves the reverse inclusion, and hence the pointwise <event> equality
$$
\boxed{\limsup_n\{\max_{m\leq n}X_m>a_n\}
=\limsup_n\{X_n>a_n\}.}
$$
This <exceedance equivalence for increasing thresholds> is deterministic. Its proof uses both monotonicity of $a_n$ and divergence to $+\infty$; it does not require <independence> or identical <probability distributions>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <tail sigma-algebra> of this <sequence> is
$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{r\geq1}\sigma(X_r,X_{r+1},\ldots).
$$
For each fixed $r$, the preceding <event> equality gives
$$
A=\bigcap_{N\geq r}\bigcup_{n\geq N}\{X_n>a_n\}.
$$
Every <event> on the right is determined by coordinates with index at least $r$. Therefore $A\in\sigma(X_r,X_{r+1},\ldots)$ for every $r$, and $A\in\mathcal T$. The <random variables> $X_n$ are <independent>, so <Kolmogorov's zero–one law> applies: every <tail event> has <probability> zero or one. Hence **$\mathbb P(A)\in\{0,1\}$**.

The role of <independence> can also be seen directly. A <tail event> is independent of each finite initial <sigma-algebra> $\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_m)$. The <monotone class theorem> extends this <independence> to $\sigma(X_1,X_2,\ldots)$; since $A$ itself lies there, it is independent of itself. Thus $\mathbb P(A)=\mathbb P(A)^2$.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§2.5, Theorem 2.5.3, printed pp. 81–82: the tail sigma-field zero–one law]{ref}

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

For $B_n=\{X_n>a_n\}$, the definition of the common <cumulative distribution function> gives
$$
\mathbb P(B_n)=1-F(a_n).
$$
The strict exceedance matters here: an <atom of a measure> at $a_n$ belongs to $\{X_n\leq a_n\}$, so it is already included in $F(a_n)$. The <events> $B_n$ are mutually <independent events>, since each depends on a separate member of the <independent random variables> $X_n$.

If $\sum_n(1-F(a_n))<\infty$, <countable subadditivity> gives, for every $N$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcup_{n\geq N}B_n\right)
\leq\sum_{n\geq N}(1-F(a_n))\longrightarrow0.
$$
<Continuity of probability> then gives $\mathbb P(\limsup_nB_n)=0$. This is the <first Borel-Cantelli lemma> and does not need <independence>.

If the <series> diverges, <independence> and $1-u\leq e^{-u}$ give, for every fixed $N$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{n=N}^{M}B_n^c\right)
=\prod_{n=N}^{M}F(a_n)
\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{n=N}^{M}(1-F(a_n))\right)\longrightarrow0.
$$
Taking $M\to\infty$ shows that the <probability> of no exceedance after $N$ is zero. A <countable union> over $N$ shows that finitely many exceedances has <probability> zero. This is the <second Borel-Cantelli lemma>. Together with the preceding <event> equality, it proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(A)=
\begin{cases}
0,&\sum_{n\geq1}(1-F(a_n))<\infty,\\
1,&\sum_{n\geq1}(1-F(a_n))=\infty.
\end{cases}}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The conditions on the <cumulative distribution function> imply $0<X_n\leq2$ <almost surely>. With $\mathcal H_n=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, adjoin $Y_0=1$ and the trivial $\mathcal H_0$. The <random variables> $Y_n$ are nonnegative, <adapted>, and bounded by $2^n$ at each fixed time. <Independence> and $\mathbb EX_{n+1}=1$ give
$$
\mathbb E[Y_{n+1}\mid\mathcal H_n]=Y_n\mathbb EX_{n+1}=Y_n.
$$
Thus this is the <product martingale from independent mean-one factors>, with $\mathbb EY_n=1$. The <almost sure supermartingale convergence theorem> states that a nonnegative <supermartingale> converges <almost surely> to a finite <integrable random variable>. Applied to this nonnegative <martingale>, it shows $Y_n\to Y_\infty<\infty$ <almost surely>. Every convergent real <sequence> is bounded, so **$\sup_{n\geq1}Y_n<\infty$ almost surely**.

The printed maximum can in fact be taken literally here. If $X_1=1$ <almost surely>, all products equal $1$. Otherwise the <strict Jensen equality criterion>, applied to the <strictly convex function> $t\mapsto-\sqrt t$ on $(0,\infty)$, gives $q=\mathbb E\sqrt{X_1}<1$. <Independence> yields $\mathbb E\sqrt{Y_n}=q^n$. By <Markov's inequality>, $\sum_n\mathbb P(Y_n>\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon^{-1/2}\sum_nq^n<\infty$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. The <first Borel-Cantelli lemma> and the countable choices $\varepsilon=1/m$ give $Y_n\to0$ <almost surely>. Since $Y_1>0$, a <sequence> with this limit attains its positive supremum among finitely many initial terms. This is an instance of <fractional moment decay of independent mean-one products>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the nonnegative <martingale> $(Y_n)$ and <filtration> $(\mathcal H_n)$ established above. Fix a finite $n$ and let
$$
H=\{\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\},\qquad
T=\min\bigl(\{1\leq k\leq n:Y_k\geq\lambda\}\cup\{n\}\bigr).
$$
For $j<n$, $\{T\leq j\}$ is the <union> of the threshold-crossing <events> up to $j$, so $T$ is a <bounded stopping time>. The <optional stopping theorem> for a <martingale> at a <bounded stopping time> gives $\mathbb EY_T=\mathbb EY_0=1$. On $H$ one has $Y_T\geq\lambda$; on its <complement> one still has $Y_T\geq0$. Therefore
$$
\lambda\mathbb P(H)\leq\mathbb EY_T=1,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb P(H)\leq\lambda^{-1}.}
$$
Only a <bounded stopping time> has been used, so no extra <uniform integrability> assumption at an unbounded hitting time is implicit. This is also the <Doob maximal inequality for a nonnegative submartingale> specialized to a mean-one <martingale>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $p>1$, the bound $X_k\leq2$ ensures the finite <moment> $m_p=\mathbb EX_1^p\leq2^p$. By <Jensen's inequality>, $m_p\geq(\mathbb EX_1)^p=1$. The process $Z_k=Y_k^p$ is an <adapted>, nonnegative <integrable random variable> at each time, and <independence> gives
$$
\mathbb E[Z_{k+1}\mid\mathcal H_k]
=Y_k^p\mathbb EX_{k+1}^p=m_pZ_k\geq Z_k.
$$
Thus $(Z_k)$ is a <submartingale>, and $\mathbb EZ_n=m_p^n$. The <Doob maximal inequality for a nonnegative submartingale> states that $c\mathbb P(\max_{k\leq n}Z_k\geq c)\leq\mathbb EZ_n$ for $c>0$. Apply it with $c=\lambda^p$. Since the power <function> is increasing on $[0,\infty)$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\right)
\leq\frac{m_p^n}{\lambda^p}.
$$
Combining this with the preceding bound gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}Y_k\geq\lambda\right)
\leq\min\left\{\lambda^{-1},\ m_p^n\lambda^{-p}\right\}.}
$$
The result is a bound on the running maximum, which is why a maximal <inequality> for a <submartingale>, rather than an estimate of the single terminal <random variable>, is needed.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§4.4, Theorem 4.4.2, printed p. 235: the submartingale maximal inequality]{ref}

==== i

===== Solution

For each integer $m\geq1$, let
$$
D_m=\{x\in\mathbb R:\mu(\{x\})\geq1/m\}.
$$
If $D_m$ had $m+1$ distinct members, <finite additivity> on their disjoint singleton <Borel sets> would give total <measure> at least $(m+1)/m>1$, contradicting $\mu(\mathbb R)=1$. Thus $|D_m|\leq m$.

Every positive singleton <measure> is at least $1/m$ for some sufficiently large $m$. Consequently the set of <point atoms of a measure> is
$$
\{x:\mu(\{x\})>0\}=\bigcup_{m\geq1}D_m.
$$
A <countable union> of finite <sets> is at most <countable>. This proves the claim, including the possibility that the set is empty. The same argument gives the <countability of point atoms of a finite measure>, with $|D_m|\leq m\mu(\mathbb R)$ in place of $m$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <product measure> $\mu\otimes\nu$ is the unique <probability measure> on the <sigma-algebra> generated by measurable rectangles for which
$$
\boxed{(\mu\otimes\nu)(C\times D)=\mu(C)\nu(D)
\quad\text{for all }C,D\in\mathcal B(\mathbb R).}
$$
Here the printed product notation $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\times\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$ denotes the generated <product sigma-algebra>, conventionally written $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)\otimes\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$; it is not just the collection of rectangles. On $\mathbb R^2$ it equals $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^2)$: coordinate projections show that rectangles are <Borel sets>, while open rectangles with rational endpoints give every open <set> as a <countable union> and hence generate the <Borel sigma-algebra>.

Existence is the <product measure> theorem for finite <measures>, which are in particular <sigma-finite measures>. Uniqueness follows because the rectangles form a <pi-system> generating the <product sigma-algebra>, and two finite <measures> agreeing there agree everywhere by the <Dynkin lemma>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§1.7, Theorem 1.7.1, printed p. 38: existence and characterization on rectangles]{ref}

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Consider the <Borel set>
$$
D=\{(x,y):a<x\leq y\leq b\}.
$$
Its <indicator function> is nonnegative and <measurable>, so the <Tonelli theorem> applies to the <product measure>, without needing any separate absolute-<integrability> argument. At a fixed $y\in(a,b]$, its section in the first coordinate is $(a,y]$; outside $(a,b]$ that section is empty. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mu\otimes\nu)(D)
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\left(\int_{\mathbb R}\mathbf1_D(x,y)\,\mu(dx)\right)\nu(dy)\\
&=\int_{(a,b]}\mu((a,y])\,\nu(dy)\\
&=\int_{(a,b]}\bigl(F_\mu(y)-F_\mu(a)\bigr)\,\nu(dy).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality uses the definition of a <cumulative distribution function> on the half-closed <interval>. It retains all <point atoms of a measure> on the diagonal $x=y$, excludes a possible <point atom of a measure> at $x=a$, and includes $y=b$, exactly as required by the <set> $D$. If $a=b$, both sides vanish. This verifies the identity even when the <probability measures> are not <non-atomic measures>.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§1.7, Theorem 1.7.2, printed pp. 38–40: integration of a nonnegative measurable function on a product space]{ref}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <filtration> is generated by the successive <independent random variables> $X_k$. Each $S_n$ is <adapted>, and $|S_n|\leq n$ makes it an <integrable random variable>. Since <independence> gives $\mathbb E[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$,
$$
\mathbb E[S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=S_n.
$$
Thus $(S_n)$ is a <martingale>.

Write the accumulated number of visits to zero as
$$
L_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_k=0\}},\qquad L_0=0.
$$
It is <adapted>, and $0\leq L_n\leq n$. Thus $M_n=|S_n|-L_n$ is also <adapted> and an <integrable random variable>. For every integer $s$,
$$
\frac{|s+1|+|s-1|}{2}-|s|
=\begin{cases}1,&s=0,\\0,&s\ne0.\end{cases}
$$
Conditioning on $S_n$ and using <independence> of the next step therefore gives
$$
\mathbb E[|S_{n+1}|-|S_n|\mid\mathcal F_n]
=\mathbf1_{\{S_n=0\}}
=L_{n+1}-L_n.
$$
The drift of the absolute value is cancelled exactly by the visit count, so $\mathbb E[M_{n+1}-M_n\mid\mathcal F_n]=0$. Hence **both $(S_n)$ and $(M_n)$ are martingales**. The latter is the <absolute value martingale of a simple symmetric random walk>, a <discrete Tanaka formula> in which visits to zero supply the correction at the corner of the absolute-value <function>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For each $n$,
$$
\{\tau\leq n\}=\bigcup_{k=0}^n\{S_k\in\{a,b\}\}\in\mathcal F_n,
$$
since $S_k$ is <adapted>. This verifies directly that $\tau$ is a <stopping time>.

Set $d=b-a$. On $\{\tau>md\}$, the <random walk> is at some integer strictly between $a$ and $b$. If its next $d$ steps are all $+1$, it must hit $b$ within that block. These future steps are <independent> of $\mathcal F_{md}$ and have <probability> $2^{-d}$ of being all $+1$. Hence
$$
\mathbb P(\tau>(m+1)d\mid\mathcal F_{md})
\leq(1-2^{-d})\mathbf1_{\{\tau>md\}}.
$$
The <law of total expectation> and <mathematical induction> give the <geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability>:
$$
\mathbb P(\tau>md)\leq(1-2^{-d})^m\longrightarrow0.
$$
Therefore **$\mathbb P(\tau<\infty)=1$**. In fact this also proves $\mathbb E\tau\leq d\,2^d<\infty$ by grouping the <tail-sum formula> into blocks of length $d$. No assertion of <independence> between successive survival <events> is needed.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <optional stopping theorem> at a <bounded stopping time> states that an <integrable> <martingale> $Z_n$ satisfies $\mathbb EZ_T=\mathbb EZ_0$ whenever $T\leq n$ for a fixed finite $n$. Apply this first to $S$ at $T=n\wedge\tau$. The <simple symmetric random walk> cannot cross an integer endpoint without hitting it, so
$$
a\leq S_{n\wedge\tau}\leq b,\qquad
\mathbb E S_{n\wedge\tau}=0.
$$
The preceding part gives $\tau<\infty$ <almost surely>, hence $S_{n\wedge\tau}\to S_\tau$ <almost surely>. The constant bound $C=\max\{-a,b\}$ permits the <dominated convergence theorem>, yielding $\mathbb ES_\tau=0$. Since $S_\tau$ is either $a$ or $b$, solving $ap+b(1-p)=0$ gives its full <probability distribution>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(S_\tau=a)=\frac{b}{b-a},\qquad
\mathbb P(S_\tau=b)=\frac{-a}{b-a}.}
$$
Now let
$$
L_\tau=\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\mathbf1_{\{S_k=0\}}.
$$
This counts the visit at time $0$, and excludes the exit time. Apply the same bounded-time <optional stopping theorem> to $M_n=|S_n|-L_n$. For each $n$ it gives
$$
\mathbb E L_{n\wedge\tau}=\mathbb E|S_{n\wedge\tau}|.
$$
The visit counts increase to $L_\tau$, so <monotone convergence> applies to the left. <Dominated convergence> applies to the right using $|S_{n\wedge\tau}|\leq C$. Consequently $L_\tau$ is an <integrable random variable> and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E L_\tau=\mathbb E|S_\tau|
=(-a)\frac{b}{b-a}+b\frac{-a}{b-a}
=\frac{-2ab}{b-a}.}
$$
This is the origin case of <expected occupation before interval exit for a simple symmetric random walk>. The argument obtains <integrability> of the total occupation count before using any unbounded-time <optional stopping> for $M$, so it does not presuppose the <uniform integrability> asked about next. The endpoint <probabilities> also agree with the standard <gambler's ruin> calculation.<Probability: Theory and Examples (fifth edition) by Rick Durrett>[§4.8.1, Theorem 4.8.7(a), printed pp. 257–258: symmetric random-walk interval-exit probabilities]{ref}

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**Yes.** The preceding calculation established $\mathbb E L_\tau=-2ab/(b-a)<\infty$. For every $n$, the stopped <martingale> satisfies
$$
|M_{n\wedge\tau}|
\leq |S_{n\wedge\tau}|+L_{n\wedge\tau}
\leq C+L_\tau=:H,
\qquad C=\max\{-a,b\}.
$$
Here $H$ is a single nonnegative <integrable random variable> that bounds the whole family. Thus, for any $K>0$,
$$
\sup_n\mathbb E\!\left[|M_{n\wedge\tau}|\mathbf1_{\{|M_{n\wedge\tau}|>K\}}\right]
\leq\mathbb E[H\mathbf1_{\{H>K\}}]\longrightarrow0.
$$
The last limit follows from the <dominated convergence theorem>. This is precisely <uniform integrability>. It also gives $M_{n\wedge\tau}\to M_\tau=|S_\tau|-L_\tau$ with <convergence in L1>, and therefore $\mathbb EM_\tau=0$, consistently with the preceding answer. A common bound on the individual <expected values> alone would not have sufficed; the <integrable> dominating <random variable> is what supplies control of every tail.
