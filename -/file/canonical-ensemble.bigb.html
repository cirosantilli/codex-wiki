= Canonical ensemble
{wiki}
{title2=$p_s=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta E_s}$}

The <canonical ensemble> is the <probability distribution> for a system in <thermal equilibrium> with a <heat bath> at fixed <temperature> $T$, with fixed particle number and external parameters. It allows <energy> exchange, so individual <energy> measurements fluctuate. With <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$ and <inverse temperature> $\beta=1/(k_BT)$,
$$
p_s=\frac{e^{-\beta E_s}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_s e^{-\beta E_s}.
$$
The <heat bath> is idealized as large enough that exchanges barely change its <temperature>; coupling must permit equilibration while contributing negligible interaction <energy>. A finite <heat bath> approximates this description only when its <temperature> change is negligible over the relevant exchanges. Strong coupling or a small <heat bath> can require a different description.

For one <two-level system> at positive <temperature>, the <Boltzmann weights> are $1,e^{-\beta\Delta}$, hence
$$
Z_1=1+e^{-\beta\Delta},\qquad p=\frac{e^{-\beta\Delta}}{Z_1}=\frac1{1+e^{\beta\Delta}}.
$$
At $\beta\Delta=\ln3$, $Z_1=4/3$ and $p=1/4$. This is a probabilistic thermal <mixed state>. For quantum levels, its <density matrix> is $\rho=(1-p)|0\rangle\langle0|+p|1\rangle\langle1|$. The <pure state> $\sqrt{1-p}|0\rangle+\sqrt p|1\rangle$ has the same <energy measurement> <probabilities>, but also has <quantum coherence> between the <energy eigenstates>. Matching those <probabilities> does not make the states equivalent.

= Thermal expectation value
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$\langle O\rangle_\beta=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H}O)/Z$}

The <canonical ensemble> assigns the <density matrix> $e^{-\beta H}/Z$, with $Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}$. Its <expectation value> of an observable is the displayed normalized trace. In imaginary time, cyclicity of this trace gives periodic bosonic <correlation functions>; it does not make the imaginary-time Heisenberg operator itself periodic.

= Translational and two-level energy variance
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$\sigma_E^2=3N(k_BT)^2/2+N\epsilon^2p(1-p)$}

For a dilute classical <ideal gas> whose atoms carry independent nondegenerate internal levels $0,\epsilon$, the <canonical partition function> factorizes into translation and $(1+e^{-\beta\epsilon})^N$. The upper-level <probability> is $p=(1+e^{\beta\epsilon})^{-1}$. Taking two derivatives of its logarithm gives the title sum of independent <variances>. The internal contribution freezes exponentially at low <temperature> and tends to $N\epsilon^2/4$ at high <temperature>. The translational classical result cannot be continued through <quantum degeneracy> without changing the model.

= Canonical entropy formula
{parent=Canonical ensemble}

= Canonical entropy
{synonym}

For $Z=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ and $U=\langle E\rangle$, the <entropy> is $S=k_B\ln Z+U/T$. It follows directly by substituting canonical probabilities into $S=-k_B\sum_i p_i\ln p_i$.

= Canonical energy variance
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(E)=k_{\rm B}T^2C_V$}

Differentiating the <canonical partition function> at fixed external parameters gives $\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z$ and $\partial_\beta^2\ln Z=\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2$. Consequently the <heat capacity> is
$$
C_V=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(E)}{k_{\rm B}T^2}\ge0.
$$
The identity assumes an <energy> spectrum independent of <temperature> and the appropriate fixed-volume <canonical ensemble>. It relates a thermal response to equilibrium <variance>.

= Quantum Gibbs state
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$\rho_\beta=e^{-\beta H}/\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})$}

A <quantum Gibbs state> is the <density operator> of a <canonical ensemble> with inverse <temperature> $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. It exists when the <canonical partition function> $Z=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})$ is finite. The <spectral theorem> assigns <probabilities> $e^{-\beta E_j}/Z$ to an <orthonormal eigenbasis> of the <Hamiltonian>. An additive constant in the <Hamiltonian> cancels between numerator and denominator. For $H=-\mu B\sigma_z$, the <exponential of a Pauli vector>, continued to an imaginary argument, gives $\rho_\beta=[I+\tanh(\beta\mu B)\sigma_z]/2$.

= Noninteracting subsystems
{parent=Canonical ensemble}

Two independently available parts of a <thermodynamic system> are noninteracting in the canonical description when their <state space> is a <Cartesian product> and their <energy> is additive, $E(a,b)=E_A(a)+E_B(b)$. Both conditions are needed for the <canonical partition function> to factor:
$$
Z=\sum_{a,b}e^{-\beta[E_A(a)+E_B(b)]}
=\left(\sum_a e^{-\beta E_A(a)}\right)\left(\sum_b e^{-\beta E_B(b)}\right)=Z_AZ_B.
$$
An additive expression on a constrained set of allowed state pairs need not give this factorization. Relabeling the states to make their allowed labels independent can instead introduce an interaction into the <energy>. For two two-state labels, an additive <energy> necessarily satisfies the rectangular identity $E_{11}+E_{22}=E_{12}+E_{21}$.

= Two-level system
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$E_0=0,\ E_1=\Delta>0$}

= Equal-degeneracy two-level energy and heat capacity
{parent=Two-level system}
{title2=$C=Nk_Bx^2e^x/(1+e^x)^2$}

For N <independent> units with nondegenerate levels 0 and $\Delta>0$, the <canonical partition function> is $(1+e^{-\beta\Delta})^N$. Its <energy> is $N\Delta/(1+e^{\beta\Delta})$ and <heat capacity> is $Nk_Bx^2e^x/(1+e^x)^2$, with $x=\Delta/(k_BT)$. Both are activated at low <temperature>. At high <temperature> the <energy> saturates at $N\Delta/2$ and <heat capacity> decays as $T^{-2}$. The equal-degeneracy Schottky peak is at $x\simeq2.39936$ and height $0.43923Nk_B$. A common <energy> shift leaves <heat capacity> unchanged; unequal level degeneracies require a different <partition function>.

= Binomial entropy of independent two-level particles
{parent=Two-level system}
{title2=$S=k_B\ln\binom{N}{N_+}$}

For independently labelled particles with nondegenerate levels, choosing which $N_+$ particles occupy the upper level gives a binomial <multiplicity>. With $x=(N_+-N_-)/N$, its large-number <entropy> is $Nk_B[\ln2-(1+x)\ln(1+x)/2-(1-x)\ln(1-x)/2]$. This counting assumes distinguishable or localized degrees of freedom, not indistinguishable particles sharing one pair of quantum orbitals.

= Additive-volume two-level system
{parent=Two-level system}

A model in which each excitation changes volume by a fixed increment $v$. If the all-ground configuration has volume $V_0$, then $V=V_0+v\langle N_{\rm ex}\rangle$. Energies and statistical weights must be specified under the relevant mechanical constraint; volume endpoints alone do not define arbitrary intermediate configurations.

= Saturation parameter
{parent=Two-level system}
{title2=$s=I/I_{\mathrm{sat}}$}

For a specified optical transition, the on-resonance <saturation parameter> is the <light intensity> divided by the <saturation intensity>. The independent-beam <Doppler cooling force> model assumes weak total excitation and defines $s$ separately for each beam.

= Saturation intensity
{parent=Saturation parameter}
{title2=$I_{\mathrm{sat}}$}

The <saturation intensity> is the characteristic <light intensity> used to normalize the on-resonance <saturation parameter> of a specified optical transition. Its value depends on the transition and the stated illumination convention.

= Canonical partition function
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{title2=$Z(\beta)$}

= Partition function
{synonym}

The <partition function> $Z(\beta)=\sum_s e^{-\beta E_s}$ is the sum of the <Boltzmann weights> over individual <microstates>. Dividing by it makes their <probabilities> sum to one. When summing distinct <energies> instead, include the <state degeneracy>: $Z=\sum_E g(E)e^{-\beta E}$. For two independent distinguishable <two-level systems>,
$$
Z_2=1+2e^{-\beta\Delta}+e^{-2\beta\Delta}=Z_1^2.
$$
The middle coefficient counts two <microstates>, exactly as in the preceding figure. For $N$ such units, $Z_N=Z_1^N$; this factorization requires additive <energy> and <independence>, and generally fails with interactions.

The usefulness goes beyond normalization. For fixed <energies>, <derivatives> of the <natural logarithm> of the <partition function> yield measurable quantities:
$$
U=\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\ln Z,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(E)=\partial_\beta^2\ln Z,\qquad
S=k_B(\ln Z+\beta U).
$$
Here $U$ is <internal energy>, the second expression is the <energy> <variance>, and $S$ is <Gibbs entropy>. For one <two-level system> at fixed $\Delta$, its <heat capacity> $C=\partial U/\partial T$ and <entropy> are
$$
U=\Delta p,\qquad \frac{C}{k_B}=\frac{(\beta\Delta)^2e^{\beta\Delta}}{(1+e^{\beta\Delta})^2},\qquad
\frac{S}{k_B}=\ln(1+e^{-\beta\Delta})+\beta\Delta p.
$$
At $\beta\Delta=\ln3$, these give $U/\Delta=0.25$, $C/k_B\simeq0.2263$ and $S/k_B\simeq0.5623$. For independent units, all three quantities add.

The figure shows why <heat capacity> peaks. At low <temperature>, the excited <energy> is rarely occupied; at high <temperature>, the <probabilities> are almost equal and further warming barely changes <internal energy>. The largest response lies between these limits, at $k_BT/\Delta\simeq0.4168$, where $C/k_B\simeq0.4392$. Meanwhile <entropy> rises from zero toward $k_B\ln2$, reflecting two equally likely alternatives. This high-<temperature> limit belongs to the stipulated <two-level system>; extra accessible <energy eigenstates> would change it.

= Gibbs paradox
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{c}

Counting permutations of identical classical <particles> as different states produces a spurious <entropy of mixing> when otherwise identical gases are combined. The dilute <ideal gas> <partition function> $Z_N=Z_1^N/N!$ removes that label multiplicity and gives an extensive <entropy> in the thermodynamic limit. For different species, genuinely distinct composition information still produces a <entropy of mixing>. <quantum statistics> supplies the underlying identical-particle counting.

= Molecular partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$q$}

The single-molecule state sum $q=\sum_s e^{-\beta E_s}$ for specified energy references and external constraints. In a dilute classical ideal gas, indistinguishability gives $Q_N=q^N/N!$; translation supplies the factor proportional to volume.

= Energy reference in molecular partition functions
{parent=Molecular partition function}
{title2=$q_{\rm abs}=e^{-\beta\varepsilon_0}q$}

If energies are measured upward from a species reference $\varepsilon_0$, its absolute single-particle sum is $q_{\rm abs}=e^{-\beta\varepsilon_0}q$. The ideal-gas <chemical potential> is then $\varepsilon_0-k_BT\ln(q/N)$. A zero-point factor can be included either in $q$ or in the reference energy, but not both.

= Molecular partition function density
{parent=Molecular partition function}
{title2=$f_i=q_i/V$}

For a free molecule in a uniform ideal gas, $q_i$ is proportional to volume. The ratio $f_i=q_i/V$ is independent of the container volume at fixed temperature and has units of inverse volume.

= Electronic partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$q_{\rm el}=\sum_i g_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/kT}$}

The <electronic partition function> includes electronic excitation energies and degeneracies $g_i$. If only an exactly degenerate ground manifold of size $g$ is populated, $q_{\rm el}=g$ and its molar <entropy> is $R\log g$. Resolved splittings require a state sum rather than replacing all components by one degeneracy.

= Vibrational partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$q_{\rm vib}$}

A <vibrational partition function> sums over molecular vibrational <energy levels>. A single <quantum harmonic oscillator>, with energies measured relative to its ground level and vibrational temperature $\theta_v=h\nu/k$, has $q_{\rm vib}=(1-e^{-\theta_v/T})^{-1}$. Retaining its absolute <zero-point energy> multiplies this by $e^{-\theta_v/(2T)}$. That factor shifts the <internal energy> but contributes no <entropy>.

= Vibrational entropy
{parent=Vibrational partition function}
{title2=$S_{\rm vib,m}/R=x/(e^x-1)-\ln(1-e^{-x})$}

The harmonic-oscillator <entropy> contribution for one vibrational coordinate, with $x=\theta_{\rm vib}/T$. Contributions from independent <normal modes> add, with one term for each component of a degenerate mode. A temperature-independent <zero-point energy> does not change this <entropy>.

= Rotational partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$q_{\rm rot}$}

The <rotational partition function> sums <Boltzmann factors> over rotational <energy levels>, including their degeneracies and the appropriate indistinguishability convention. For a linear <rigid rotor> at high temperature, $q_{\rm rot}=8\pi^2IkT/(\sigma h^2)$, with <moment of inertia> $I$ and <rotational symmetry number> $\sigma$.

= Rotational entropy
{parent=Rotational partition function}
{title2=$S_{\rm rot,m}=R[\ln q_{\rm rot}+T\partial_T\ln q_{\rm rot}]$}

The <entropy> contribution from populated molecular rotational states. A nonlinear <rigid rotor> has $q_{\rm rot}\propto T^{3/2}$ at high <temperature>, giving $S_{\rm rot,m}=R(\ln q_{\rm rot}+3/2)$. Its <rotational symmetry number> removes redundant orientations.

= Translational partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$q_{\rm trans}=V(2\pi mkT/h^2)^{3/2}$}

For a free molecule of mass $m$ in a large three-dimensional volume $V$, replacing the translational state sum by an integral gives the displayed <partition function>. For identical classical molecules the many-molecule <canonical partition function> is $q_{\rm trans}^N/N!$, not simply $q_{\rm trans}^N$. Differentiating its <Helmholtz free energy> at fixed volume gives the ideal-gas translational <entropy>.

= Thermal trace
{parent=Canonical partition function}
{title2=$Z(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}$}

The <trace> of imaginary-time evolution is the <canonical partition function> of a quantum system. In a coordinate basis it is the integral of the diagonal <Euclidean path integral> kernel. Inserting a symmetry operator instead gives a weighted thermal <trace>.

= Parity-twisted oscillator thermal trace
{parent=Thermal trace}
{title2=$\operatorname{Tr}(P e^{-\beta H})$}

For two equal-frequency <quantum harmonic oscillators>, the ordinary <thermal trace> is $r/(1-r)^2$ and the <trace> with the <parity operator> is $r/(1+r)^2$, where $r=e^{-\beta\omega}$. Total excitation $N$ has degeneracy $N+1$ and parity $(-1)^N$. Therefore the traces expand as $\sum_{n\ge1}(\pm1)^{n-1}nr^n$. The alternating weights come from parity insertion in a bosonic state space, not fermionic statistics.

= Thermodynamic derivatives of the canonical partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}

Canonical differentiation gives
$$
F=-\beta^{-1}\log Z,\qquad
\langle E\rangle=-\partial_\beta\log Z,
\qquad
(\Delta E)^2=\partial_\beta^2\log Z.
$$

= Canonical energy fluctuation
{parent=Thermodynamic derivatives of the canonical partition function}

Canonical energy variance equals $\partial_\beta^2\log Z=k_BT^2C_V$.

= Relative energy fluctuation of a monatomic ideal gas
{parent=Canonical energy fluctuation}
{title2=$\sigma_U/U=\sqrt{2/(3N)}$}

At fixed <particle number> and <volume>, a classical monatomic <ideal gas> has $U=3Nk_BT/2$ and $C_V=3Nk_B/2$. The <canonical energy fluctuation> identity $\operatorname{Var}(E)=k_BT^2C_V$ therefore gives $\sigma_U/U=\sqrt{2/(3N)}$. Absolute fluctuations grow as $\sqrt N$, while relative fluctuations decrease as $N^{-1/2}$. A particle reservoir changes the fluctuation problem because <energy> and particle-number fluctuations are then correlated.

= Particle in a finite two-dimensional harmonic trap
{parent=Canonical partition function}

For
$$
H=\frac{p_x^2+p_y^2}{2m}+\frac\kappa2(x^2+y^2),
\qquad x^2+y^2<R^2,
$$
put $a=\kappa R^2/2$. The normalized classical one-particle partition function is
$$
Z_1=\frac{4\pi^2m}{h^2\beta^2\kappa}
\left(1-e^{-\beta a}\right).
$$
Its mean total and potential energies are
$$
\langle E\rangle=\frac2\beta-\frac a{e^{\beta a}-1},
\qquad
\langle V\rangle=\frac1\beta-\frac a{e^{\beta a}-1}.
$$

= Hard-wall correction to harmonic equipartition
{parent=Particle in a finite two-dimensional harmonic trap}

If $a\gg k_BT$, the wall is thermally inaccessible and $\langle E\rangle\sim2k_BT$, as four quadratic terms predict. If $a\ll k_BT$, the spatial distribution is nearly uniform in the disk and
$$
\langle E\rangle
=k_BT+\frac{\kappa R^2}{4}+O\left(\frac{\kappa^2R^4}{k_BT}\right),
$$
so only the two kinetic quadratic terms contribute at leading order.

= Classical ideal-gas partition function
{parent=Canonical partition function}

For $N$ indistinguishable noninteracting classical particles, $Z_N=z_1^N/N!$, where $z_1$ is the normalized one-particle phase-space integral.

= Classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap
{parent=Classical ideal-gas partition function}
{title2=$Z_1=8\pi(k_BT/a)^3/\lambda_T^3$}

For a structureless particle with <Hamiltonian> $p^2/(2m)+ar$, $a>0$, the classical <phase space> integral gives the displayed one-particle <partition function>. Its spatial integral is $4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-ar/(k_BT)}dr=8\pi(k_BT/a)^3$, while the momentum integral gives $\lambda_T^{-3}$. For $N$ indistinguishable dilute particles, $Z_N=Z_1^N/N!$, $F\simeq-Nk_BT[\ln(Z_1/N)+1]$, $U=(9/2)Nk_BT$ and $S\simeq Nk_B[\ln(Z_1/N)+11/2]$. These thermodynamic formulas use leading Stirling accuracy; the classical phase-space integral additionally requires temperatures above the discrete trap level scale.

= Quantum degeneracy threshold in a spherical linear trap
{parent=Classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap}
{title2=$N\sim Z_1(T,a)=8\pi(k_BT/a)^3/\lambda_T^3$}

For a dilute classical trapped gas, $\mu\simeq k_BT\ln(N/Z_1)$ with energy zero at the potential minimum. Thus $z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}=N/Z_1$. Quantum occupation effects begin when $z$ is no longer small, giving the displayed order-of-magnitude particle threshold. Equivalently, the central density $n(0)=N/[8\pi(k_BT/a)^3]$ satisfies $n(0)\lambda_T^3\sim1$. This is a degeneracy crossover criterion rather than an exact Bose condensation threshold. In the continuum approximation, spinless bosons have excited-state saturation $N_c=\zeta(9/2)Z_1$, from the trap density of states proportional to $E^{7/2}$. A quantum ground energy should be subtracted consistently when discreteness matters.

= Isentropic decompression of a spherical linear trap
{parent=Classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap}
{title2=$T\propto a^{2/3}$}

At fixed atom number, the <entropy> of the <classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap> depends on temperature and strength through $Z_1\propto T^{9/2}a^{-3}$. Reversible adiabatic change therefore holds this product constant, giving the displayed relation. Reducing trap strength lowers the temperature while increasing cloud size. Its peak classical phase-space occupation $N/Z_1$ remains constant, so this cooling alone does not make the gas more quantum degenerate. Heat exchange, particle loss or irreversible entropy production would change this relation.

= Classical ideal gas in a homogeneous soft trap
{parent=Classical ideal-gas partition function}
{title2=$Z_1=VI_n(kT)^{3/2+3/(2n)}$}

For the potential $U=r^{2n}/V^{2n/3}$, rescaling $r=V^{1/3}(kT)^{1/(2n)}u$ in the classical one-particle <partition function> gives the formula above. The parameter-conjugate force is $P=NkT/V$, the <internal energy> is $(3/2+3/(2n))NkT$, and $C_P-C_V=Nk$. The kinetic term contributes $3NkT/2$, while the potential term contributes $3NkT/(2n)$. For indistinguishable particles the $N!$ <factor> changes <free energy> and <entropy> but not these force and heat-capacity formulas.

= Thermal de Broglie wavelength
{parent=Classical ideal-gas partition function}
{wiki}

= Thermal wavelength
{synonym}

The three-dimensional translational momentum integral is $1/\lambda^3$ per unit volume, where
$$\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}.$$

= Degeneracy onset from thermal wavelength
{parent=Thermal de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$T_\lambda=h^2n^{2/3}/(2\pi mk_B)$}

Equating <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ to mean particle spacing $n^{-1/3}$ gives the displayed quantum-degeneracy scale. It is an onset estimate rather than a universal phase-transition <temperature>. Different typical-momentum conventions change its order-one factor; <density>, <mass>, spin degeneracy and interactions determine the detailed crossover or condensation criterion.

= Quantum concentration
{parent=Thermal de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$n_Q=\lambda_T^{-3}=(mk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2))^{3/2}$}

The translational concentration scale for a nonrelativistic particle in three dimensions, per internal state. It is the inverse cube of the <thermal de Broglie wavelength>. A classical component with number density $n$ and external one-particle energy $U$ has <chemical potential> $\mu=k_BT\ln(n/n_Q)+U$. An additional internal degeneracy must be included explicitly; it is not hidden in this definition. The dilute classical condition is $n/n_Q\ll1$ for each occupied internal component.

= Canonical partition function for an ultrarelativistic gas in a power-law trap
{parent=Classical ideal-gas partition function}

For energy $pc+U(x)$,
$$
Z_1=\frac{(k_BT)^3}{\pi^2(\hbar c)^3}
\int e^{-U(x)/(k_BT)}d^3x.
$$
If $U(r)=r^{2n}/V^{2n/3}$, then
$$
Z_1=\frac4{\pi(\hbar c)^3}
V(k_BT)^{3+3/(2n)}I_n,
\qquad
I_n=\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u^{2n}}du.
$$
Consequently $pV=Nk_BT$, while the mean energy and variance are $NAk_BT$ and $NA(k_BT)^2$ with $A=3+3/(2n)$.

= Most likely radius in an isotropic power-law trap
{parent=Canonical partition function for an ultrarelativistic gas in a power-law trap}

The radial density is proportional to $r^2e^{-r^{2n}/(V^{2n/3}k_BT)}$, whose mode is
$$
r=V^{1/3}\left(\frac{k_BT}{n}\right)^{1/(2n)}.
$$

= Sackur-Tetrode equation
{parent=Classical ideal-gas partition function}
{c}
{wiki=Sackur–Tetrode_equation}

In the classical dilute regime, a monatomic ideal gas has entropy
$$S=Nk_B\left[\log\left(\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}\right)+\frac52\right].$$

= Independent spin-one-half two-level system
{title2=$E_\uparrow=+\epsilon,\ E_\downarrow=-\epsilon$}
{parent=Canonical ensemble}

For $N$ distinguishable noninteracting spins, let a fraction $\alpha$ occupy the level $+\epsilon$ and the remainder occupy $-\epsilon$. The <Stirling formula> gives
$$
S=-Nk_B\bigl[\alpha\log\alpha+(1-\alpha)\log(1-\alpha)\bigr],
\qquad
E=N\epsilon(2\alpha-1).
$$
The relation $1/T=\partial S/\partial E$ yields
$$
\alpha=\frac1{1+e^{2\epsilon/(k_BT)}},
\qquad
E=-N\epsilon\tanh\left(\frac\epsilon{k_BT}\right).
$$
Positive temperature corresponds to $0\leq\alpha<1/2$; a population inversion $1/2<\alpha\leq1$ has <negative temperature>.

= Spin-1 paramagnet
{parent=Canonical ensemble}

For $N$ independent spins with $s_z\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and one-spin Hamiltonian $-\mu Bs_z$,
$$
Z=\left(1+2\cosh(\beta\mu B)\right)^N.
$$
Writing $x=\beta\mu B$, its heat capacity is
$$
C=Nk_Bx^2\frac{2(\cosh x+2)}
{(1+2\cosh x)^2}.
$$

= Independent symmetric three-level system
{parent=Spin-1 paramagnet}

For $N$ noninteracting units with energies $-\epsilon,0,\epsilon$, put $x=\beta\epsilon$. Then
$$
Z=(1+2\cosh x)^N,
\qquad
E=-\frac{2N\epsilon\sinh x}{1+2\cosh x},
$$
and
$$
S=Nk_B\left[
\log(1+2\cosh x)
-\frac{2x\sinh x}{1+2\cosh x}
\right].
$$

= Magnetic moment from a Schottky heat-capacity tail
{parent=Independent symmetric three-level system}
{title2=$\mu=B^{-1}\sqrt{3ak_B/2}$}

For one three-level unit with energies $-\mu B,0,\mu B$, the high-temperature <heat capacity> is $C\simeq2\mu^2B^2/(3k_BT^2)$. A measured coefficient $C=a/T^2$ therefore gives the displayed magnetic moment. If the <heat capacity> instead represents $N$ independent units, replace $a$ by $a/N$; the normalization must be established before extracting a single-unit moment.

= Negative temperature
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{wiki}

Negative absolute temperature can occur only for a system with an energy spectrum bounded above. Since $\beta=\partial S/\partial E<0$, higher-energy states are more populated than lower-energy states.

= Magnetic-field reversal and negative temperature
{parent=Negative temperature}

Instantaneously reversing a Hamiltonian $H(B)$ satisfying $H(-B)=-H(B)$ leaves populations unchanged but rewrites them as a canonical distribution with $\beta'=-\beta$. When the partition function is unchanged, the free energy changes sign and the heat capacity is unchanged.

= Heat flow from negative to positive temperature
{parent=Negative temperature}

A negative-temperature system is hotter than every positive-temperature system. If heat $\delta Q$ passes from it to a positive-temperature body, the total entropy change is
$$
\delta S_{\rm total}
=\delta Q(\beta_{\rm positive}-\beta_{\rm negative})>0.
$$

= Equipartition theorem
{parent=Canonical ensemble}
{wiki}

= Equipartition
{synonym}

The <equipartition theorem> says that each independent quadratic term in a <Hamiltonian> described by <classical mechanics> has mean <energy> $k_BT/2$ in a <canonical ensemble>. Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $T>0$ the absolute <temperature>. Count terms in the <energy>, rather than vaguely counting everything that can move: one <harmonic oscillator> has one position <degree of freedom> but TWO quadratic terms, one in <momentum> and one in <displacement>.

A precise sufficient setting is $H(y,\xi)=ay^2/2+H_0(\xi)$ with $a>0$, where $y$ is one of the <canonical variables>, $\xi$ denotes all remaining variables, and the <phase space> integration measure factors as $dy\,d\xi$. The range of $y$ is the whole <real line>, independently of $\xi$; there is no <constraint> coupling its allowed values to other variables. The <partition function> must be finite, and the relevant <expected values> must exist. The <Boltzmann factor> then gives
$$
\beta=\frac{1}{k_BT},\qquad
\rho_y(y)=\sqrt{\frac{\beta a}{2\pi}}e^{-\beta ay^2/2},\qquad
\left\langle\frac{ay^2}{2}\right\rangle=\frac{k_BT}{2}.
$$
The <probability density function> $\rho_y$ is a <normal distribution> with <variance> $k_BT/a$. A larger coefficient narrows the fluctuations enough to cancel its greater <energy> cost. This is why the mean contribution is independent of <mass> or <spring constant>.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Equipartition Theorem>[equations 7.124–7.135]{ref}

For a proof outline, the one-variable <Gaussian integral> is $Z_y=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\beta ay^2/2}\,dy=\sqrt{2\pi/(\beta a)}$, so $-\partial_\beta\ln Z_y=1/(2\beta)$. More generally, <integration by parts> in a canonical variable gives $\langle y\,\partial_yH\rangle=k_BT$ when its boundary term $[y e^{-\beta H}]$ vanishes and the integrals converge. For $ay^2/2$ this is twice its mean <energy>. At a finite hard boundary the boundary term need not vanish; <hard-wall correction to harmonic equipartition> illustrates the resulting departure. A <constraint>, a nonquadratic <potential energy>, or a noncanonical integration measure therefore requires its own calculation. The theorem is an <ensemble average>, not equality of instantaneous <energies>, and applying it to a recorded trajectory requires equilibration and sufficient sampling.

For a classical one-dimensional <harmonic oscillator>, with positive <mass> $m$ and <spring constant> $k$,
$$
H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac{kx^2}{2},\qquad
\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m}=\frac{k\langle x^2\rangle}{2}=\frac{k_BT}{2},\qquad
\langle H\rangle=k_BT.
$$
The origin is the <equilibrium> position. Its <heat capacity> is $k_B$ at fixed $m,k$, not $k_B/2$. Although the two mean <energies> agree, their measured amplitudes do not: $\langle p^2\rangle=mk_BT$ and $\langle x^2\rangle=k_BT/k$. In a <harmonic optical trap>, the position measurement observes the <potential energy> contribution; it does not measure both contributions by observing $x$ alone.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Harmonic Oscillators>[equations 7.136–7.139]{ref}

For $N$ particles in a three-dimensional classical <monatomic gas>, neglect interactions and thermally accessible internal excitations. Its three translational <momentum> terms give <internal energy> $U=3Nk_BT/2$, <heat capacity at constant volume> $C_V=3Nk_B/2$, and <heat capacity at constant pressure> $C_p=C_V+Nk_B=5Nk_B/2$. Per mole, replace $Nk_B$ by the <molar gas constant> $R$. For example, using stipulated $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, the <molar heat capacity> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> is $12.471\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. These results assume a dilute, nonrelativistic <ideal gas> with classical translational statistics, rather than a <quantum degeneracy> regime.

For an <ideal gas> of diatomic <molecules>, modeled as two <chemical atoms> with a bond, there are three translational quadratic terms, two accessible <rotational kinetic energy> terms for changes of bond orientation, and one bond-stretching <harmonic oscillator> with two quadratic terms. Rotation about the bond axis is not an additional active mode in this linear-molecule model. The three familiar limits of <heat capacity at constant volume> are
$$
\frac{C_V}{Nk_B}\simeq
\begin{cases}
3/2,&\text{rotations and vibrations frozen},\\
5/2,&\text{rotations active, vibrations frozen},\\
7/2,&\text{rotations and vibrations classical}.
\end{cases}
$$
Corresponding <molar heat capacities> are $3R/2$, $5R/2$ and $7R/2$. Counting the stretching <harmonic oscillator> only once as $k_BT/2$ would give the wrong last value.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/2 Classical Gases>[§§2.2.1]{ref}

The frozen regimes require <quantum mechanics>. In the simple molecular rotation model, $E_j=k_B\theta_r j(j+1)$ with <state degeneracy> $2j+1$, $\theta_r=\hbar^2/(2Ik_B)$, and <moment of inertia> $I$. For the bond-stretching <quantum harmonic oscillator>, $\theta_v=\hbar\omega/k_B$ with <angular frequency> $\omega$ and $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$ from the <Planck constant>. When $\theta_r\ll\theta_v$, the three limits above correspond to $T\ll\theta_r$, $\theta_r\ll T\ll\theta_v$, and $T\gg\theta_v$, provided translational motion remains classical. They are smooth crossovers, not abrupt switches. Real <molecules> can have different allowed rotational populations; at high <temperature>, <thermal dissociation>, internal excitations and nonharmonic bond motion can invalidate the fixed-molecule model before its last limit is reached.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[§3.4]{ref}

For a <quantum harmonic oscillator> with fixed $\omega$, put $z=\theta_v/T$. Its exact mean <energy> and <heat capacity> are
$$
\langle H\rangle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}+\frac{\hbar\omega}{e^z-1},\qquad
\frac{C_{\rm osc}}{k_B}=\frac{z^2e^{-z}}{(1-e^{-z})^2}.
$$
At $T\gg\theta_v$, the mean <energy> approaches $k_BT$ and $C_{\rm osc}$ approaches $k_B$. At $T\ll\theta_v$, thermal excitation is exponentially suppressed and $C_{\rm osc}$ tends to zero, although the <zero-point energy> $\hbar\omega/2$ survives. Frozen thus means little thermal response, not zero motion or zero <energy>.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Harmonic Oscillators>[equations 7.140–7.155]{ref}

A motivating measurement is <thermal calibration of an optical trap>. A focused <laser> through a microscope objective confines a bead in water; imaging or a quadrant photodetector records a position <time series>. Gieseler and colleagues describe a polystyrene bead of radius $1.03\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ at $293.15\,\mathrm K$, a $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ trapping <laser>, and photodetector acquisition at $10^5\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Their recorded bead continues undergoing <Brownian motion>; higher trapping power produces a narrower equilibrium position distribution. Infer the centered <variance>, not a squared distance from an arbitrary camera origin:
$$
\sigma_x^2=\left\langle(x-\langle x\rangle)^2\right\rangle,\qquad
k=\frac{k_BT}{\sigma_x^2}.
$$
The <normal distribution> position histogram and agreement with dynamical calibration methods support the local <harmonic optical trap> interpretation. For a stipulated example, $T=300\,\mathrm K$ and $\sigma_x=20\,\mathrm{nm}$ imply $k\simeq1.04\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m}=0.0104\,\mathrm{pN/nm}$. This calculated scale is not an experimental reading.<Optical Tweezers: A Comprehensive Tutorial from Calibration to Applications by Jan Gieseler>[§§2.1–2.2, 3.3; Tables 1–3]{ref}

Position calibration is essential: if detector output $v$ has conversion $x=s(v-v_0)$, its physical <variance> is $s^2\operatorname{Var}(v)$. An unknown scale $s$ cannot be determined together with $k$ from that one <variance>. A complementary calibration demonstrated by Tolić-Nørrelykke and colleagues combines the <power spectrum> of thermal motion with the bead response to known sinusoidal motion of a translation stage. The driven response supplies an additional length scale, and their method does not require bead size, liquid <dynamic viscosity> or wall distance as inputs. It still uses a thermal dynamical model; agreement between methods is a consistency check, not a theory-free proof of <equipartition theorem>.<Calibration of optical tweezers with positional detection in the back-focal-plane by Simon F. Tolić-Nørrelykke and others>[abstract]{ref}

The readout itself can hide fluctuations. Wong and Halvorsen used a $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ <optical trap> and adjustable-exposure CCD imaging of a bead in water. They varied exposure and trapping power, held the bead away from the nearest wall, adjusted illumination to maintain comparable images, and measured tracking noise using a stuck bead. The measured <variance> fell as exposure increased; corrected results agreed with their time-averaging model. For a stationary overdamped <harmonic optical trap>, with <optical trap relaxation time> $\tau$, an exposure of length $W$ measures $\bar x_W=W^{-1}\int_0^W x(t)\,dt$. Its ideal noise-free <variance> is
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar x_W)=\frac{k_BT}{k}S(\alpha),\qquad
\alpha=\frac{W}{\tau},\qquad
S(\alpha)=\frac{2(\alpha-1+e^{-\alpha})}{\alpha^2}.
$$
For $\alpha>0$, $S(\alpha)<1$: using the blurred <variance> in the uncorrected formula overestimates $k$ by $1/S(\alpha)$. At $W=\tau$, the calculated factor is $e/2\simeq1.36$. Additive tracking noise instead raises the measured <variance> and biases the inferred $k$ downward if left uncorrected.<The effect of integration time on fluctuation measurements: calibrating an optical trap in the presence of motion blur by Wesley P. Wong and others>[equations 15]{ref}

Useful controls are to record over many <optical trap relaxation times>, compare blocks for drift, calibrate the position scale, measure fixed-bead noise, vary exposure, and check the position histogram for departures from a <normal distribution>. A sparse sequence of accurate instantaneous positions can estimate an equilibrium <variance>; a long exposure averages away motion even if many frames are collected. Flow, appreciable <laser> heating, escape from the harmonic region or nonconservative <forces> can break the assumed <thermal equilibrium>. Inferring $k$ by imposing <equipartition theorem> is a calibration, whereas testing the relation requires an independently established stiffness and position scale. The beautiful practical connection is that microscopic randomness can set a <force> scale: once calibrated, a small constant applied <force> displaces the mean by $f/k$. The same quadratic-term counting predicts macroscopic <heat capacity> and makes microscopic fluctuations useful measurements.

= Rotational equipartition in a rough-ball gas
{parent=Equipartition theorem}

Rough elastic collisions can exchange rotational and translational <kinetic energy>. For three-dimensional rigid balls with all three rotations thermalized, these six quadratic terms contribute $3k_BT$ per particle. A uniform gravitational height distribution adds $k_BT$, giving total mechanical mean energy $4k_BT$.

= Effective mechanical temperature of released rough balls
{parent=Rotational equipartition in a rough-ball gas}

If each initially stationary ball is released at height $h$ and the subsequent isolated dilute assembly reaches effective classical equilibrium, energy conservation gives $4k_BT=mgh$. The height-tail probability above the release height is then $e^{-4}$. The effective <temperature> refers to mechanical motion, not the material's internal <temperature>.

= Equipartition for a homogeneous potential
{parent=Equipartition theorem}
{title2=$\langle A|x|^s\rangle=k_BT/s$}

For a classical canonical coordinate with confining <potential energy> $U=A|x|^s$, $A>0$ and $s>0$, <integration by parts> in the <Boltzmann distribution> gives $\langle xU'(x)\rangle=k_BT$. Homogeneity then gives $\langle U\rangle=k_BT/s$. The usual quadratic contribution $k_BT/2$ is the special case $s=2$.

= Nonrelativistic equilibrium number density
{parent=Canonical ensemble}

In the dilute Maxwell--Boltzmann regime, a species has number density proportional to $g(mk_BT)^{3/2}e^{(\mu-mc^2)/(k_BT)}$.
