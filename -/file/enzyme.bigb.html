= Enzyme
{wiki}

An <enzyme> is a biological <catalyst>: it accelerates a <chemical reaction> and is regenerated after a successful catalytic cycle. Most <enzymes> are <proteins>, but catalytic <RNA> molecules, called <ribozymes>, also exist. Many reactions needed by a <biological cell> would proceed too slowly under its ordinary conditions without <enzymes>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9921/[Cooper, The Cell: the central role of enzymes as biological catalysts].

The biological beauty is selective speed. A <biological cell> contains many possible <reactants>; its <enzymes> help determine which transformations happen on a useful timescale. In <metabolism>, one step's <reaction product> can become another step's <substrate>, so selective catalysts support an organized flow of material.

The <ribosome> is a striking biological example: the machinery that builds <proteins> has <RNA> at its catalytic centre. The complete <ribosome> contains both <RNA> and <proteins>, but its bond-forming <active site> is built from <RNA>. Structural evidence established this distinction in https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10937990/[Nissen et al., The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289, 920–930 (2000)].

Selection occurs at an <active site>, the region where <substrate> binding and catalytic chemistry take place. Shape and chemical interactions favour some <substrates> over others; binding can also reshape the <enzyme>, an <induced fit>. Recognition alone is insufficient: holding a <substrate> extremely tightly need not make it react quickly. Catalysis requires a favourable route through the <transition state>, the high-barrier configuration between <reactants> and <reaction products>. Positioning reactive groups and stabilizing this configuration relative to the bound <substrate> can lower that barrier. Specificity is often strong, but need not mean exactly one acceptable <substrate>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9921/[Cooper, The Cell: mechanisms of enzymatic catalysis].

Read the diagram numerically. Its chosen <Gibbs free energies> are $0$ for the <reactants> and $-10\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for the <reaction products>. The uncatalysed peak is $60\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, whereas the catalysed peak is $40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The forward free-energy barriers are therefore $60$ and $40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; viewed from the <reaction products>, the reverse barriers are $70$ and $50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Both decrease by $20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, while the endpoint difference stays $-10\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. These are constructed values, not measurements of a particular <enzyme>.

This separates <chemical kinetics> from <chemical equilibrium>. An <enzyme> changes how rapidly the system approaches <chemical equilibrium>; it does not change the <equilibrium constant> for the same overall <chemical reaction> under the same conditions. Faster catalysis therefore supplies no extra driving <energy>. In a <biological cell>, continuing supply of <reactants> and removal of <reaction products> can nevertheless sustain a net flow: the surroundings keep that system away from <chemical equilibrium>. See https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek1t3.html[IUBMB, Symbolism and terminology in enzyme kinetics: basic definitions].

Some <enzymes> require a <cofactor (biochemistry)>, an additional nonprotein component such as a metal <ion> or a small organic molecule. Organic helpers are often called <coenzymes>; they can assist transfers that the <protein> alone cannot readily perform. The <cofactor (biochemistry)> may remain bound or exchange with the surroundings, depending on the system. A <coenzyme> can be chemically changed in one reaction and regenerated by another: regeneration of the <enzyme> does not mean every helper is unchanged in that individual reaction. Some <enzymes> need no additional <cofactor (biochemistry)>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26911/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: small molecules associated with proteins] and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/[Alberts et al., activated carriers and their regeneration].

How much faster does adding <substrate> make a reaction? <Enzyme kinetics> gives a quantitative answer when a model's assumptions fit. For an illustrative reaction obeying <Michaelis-Menten kinetics>, use the <Michaelis-Menten equation> with <substrate> <concentration> $s$, limiting <reaction rate> $V_{\max}$ and <Michaelis constant> $K_M$. At $s/K_M=0.1$, substitution gives $v/V_{\max}=0.1/1.1=1/11\approx0.0909$. At $s/K_M=1$, it gives $1/2$; at $s/K_M=10$, it gives $10/11\approx0.9091$. Thus a hundredfold increase in <substrate> between the first and last examples yields only a tenfold increase in <reaction rate>.

\Image[enzyme-substrate-saturation.png]
{title=Illustrative <Michaelis-Menten kinetics>: $s/K_M=0.1,1,10$ gives $v/V_{\max}=1/11,1/2,10/11$}
{description=Original normalized curve and computed examples, with a logarithmic concentration axis to separate the three values. The dashed upper line is a limiting rate approached at high <substrate> <concentration>, not reached at a finite concentration in this model.}

For a chosen $V_{\max}=110\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$, those fractions mean <reaction rates> of $10$, $55$ and $100\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$. Doubling <substrate> from $10K_M$ to $20K_M$ raises the normalized rate only from $10/11$ to $20/21\approx0.9524$, a relative increase of about $4.76\%$. In this simplest model, the catalytic machinery is already nearly fully occupied. Doubling the amount of active <enzyme> instead doubles $V_{\max}$ and the predicted <reaction rates> at fixed free <substrate> <concentration>, provided the other conditions are unchanged. This distinction helps decide whether additional <substrate> or additional catalytic capacity could improve throughput.

The interpretation has limits. The simplest <Michaelis-Menten kinetics> assumes a single <substrate>, fixed active <enzyme> amount, a suitable <quasi-steady-state approximation>, and initial measurements with negligible reverse reaction from accumulated <reaction product>. Substantial depletion, interacting binding sites, or inhibition by excess <substrate> can invalidate this model. Within it, $K_M$ is the <substrate> <concentration> giving half the limiting <reaction rate>; it is not generally the binding <dissociation constant> $K_d$. See https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek1t3.html[IUBMB, enzyme kinetics: steady-state approximation and substrate dissociation] and https://iubmb.qmul.ac.uk/kinetics/ek4t6.html[IUBMB, Michaelis-Menten and non-Michaelis-Menten kinetics]. Measuring activity also requires specified <temperature>, <pH> and necessary <cofactor (biochemistry)> availability: a weak signal can reflect unsuitable conditions rather than little <enzyme>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK92007/[Brooks et al., Assay Guidance Manual: basics of enzymatic assays for HTS].

= Ribozyme
{parent=Enzyme}
{wiki}

A <ribozyme> is an <RNA> <molecule> whose folded structure provides <catalysis> of a <chemical reaction>. A <protein> <enzyme> builds its <active site> from an <amino acid> chain; a <ribozyme> builds a working <active site> from <RNA>, sometimes with metal <ions> and associated <proteins>. <Base pairing> can help recognize a <substrate>, but recognition alone does not establish <catalysis>: the <RNA> must promote chemical conversion. A passive <messenger RNA> template being read by a <ribosome> is not thereby a <ribozyme>. See https://herschlaglab.stanford.edu/s/39.pdf[Cech and Herschlag, Group I Ribozymes: Substrate Recognition, Catalytic Strategies, and Comparative Mechanistic Analysis, sections 1–3].

The surprise is that an information-bearing <polymer> can also perform chemistry. Examples include the self-splicing <group I intron> of Tetrahymena thermophila and the catalytic <RNA> component of bacterial <RNase P>, discussed below. The <ribosome> supplies an everyday example: its <peptide bond>-forming <active site> is made of <ribosomal RNA>, although the complete machine also contains <proteins>. Nissen and colleagues examined structures of a large <ribosome> subunit with <substrate> analogues and found <RNA> contacting those analogues at the catalytic centre. This supports an <RNA> <active site>, not a claim that isolated <ribosomal RNA> can carry out all of <biological translation>. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10937990/[Nissen, Hansen, Ban, Moore and Steitz, The structural basis of ribosome activity in peptide bond synthesis, Science 289 (2000), 920–930].

For the Tetrahymena <group I intron>, <RNA splicing> removes the <intron> and joins its flanking <exons>. The first chemical step uses the free $3'$ <hydroxyl group> of an external <guanosine> to attack the <phosphodiester bond> at the $5'$ splice junction. This releases the upstream <exon> with a free $3'$ <hydroxyl group> and attaches the <guanosine> to the <intron>. That newly freed <hydroxyl group> then attacks the <phosphodiester bond> at the other splice junction, joining the <exons> and releasing the linear <intron>. Each step exchanges one backbone connection for another; it is not simply separation of <base pairs>. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6101203/[Cech, Zaug and Grabowski, 1981: the phosphoester-transfer mechanism].

The motivating experiment began with an unexpected control. As Cech recounts, Zaug purified a <ribosomal RNA> precursor from Tetrahymena using a detergent and phenol extraction, then compared test tubes with and without added <cell nucleus> extract. Incubation in salts and <nucleotides> released the same amount of small <intron> <RNA> in both cases, detected by <gel electrophoresis>. Repetition made a missing ingredient or accidental omission less plausible. Activity also survived treatments intended to destroy <proteins>. Nevertheless, absence of added extract did not by itself exclude a tightly attached, resistant <protein>, or prove that the small <RNA> had been newly excised rather than released from an already processed complex. See https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/cech-lecture.pdf[Thomas R. Cech, 1989 Nobel lecture, pp. 655–659: the unexpected control and alternative explanations].

An independent chemical readout addressed the latter problem. Cech mixed precursor <RNA> with <phosphorus>-32-labelled <guanosine> bearing three <phosphate> groups (GTP), and used <gel electrophoresis> followed by exposure to X-ray film to locate the label. Label appeared in the excised <intron> with <magnesium> present; it was absent when <magnesium> was omitted or labelled <ATP> replaced the labelled <guanosine> reagent. This experiment is described in https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/cech-lecture.pdf[Cech, 1989 Nobel lecture, pp. 657–658: labelled-guanosine controls]. The 1981 study established attachment through the <guanosine> $3'$ <hydroxyl group> and showed that <guanosine> without a triphosphate also supported excision: breaking down the added <nucleotide> to supply <energy> was unnecessary. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6101203/[Cech, Zaug and Grabowski, In vitro splicing of the ribosomal RNA precursor of Tetrahymena, Cell 27 (1981), 487–496: primary abstract]. Separately, sequencing and terminal analysis found an extra $5'$ <guanosine> absent from the corresponding <DNA> sequence. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7099968/[Zaug and Cech, The intervening sequence excised from the ribosomal RNA precursor of Tetrahymena contains a 5-terminal guanosine residue not encoded by the DNA, Nucleic Acids Research 10 (1982), 2823–2838]. Together, incorporation and sequence evidence support a new <covalent bond>, beyond a change in how <RNA> associates.

Kruger, Grabowski, Zaug, Sands, Gottschling and Cech's 1982 test removed dependence on a precursor purified from Tetrahymena. They cloned the <intron> and adjacent <exons> into a <DNA> template, made precursor <RNA> with purified bacterial <RNA polymerase> under conditions suppressing <RNA splicing>, and removed <proteins> by detergent–phenol extraction. Denaturing <gel electrophoresis> separated precursor species. Purified precursor was then incubated with the <guanosine> reagent, <magnesium> and salt; matched samples without this incubation supplied a precursor baseline. Their standard assay used $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ magnesium chloride, $0.1\,\mathrm{mM}$ of that <guanosine> reagent, $100\,\mathrm{mM}$ ammonium sulfate and a <buffer solution> at <pH> $7.5$, for $30$ minutes at <temperature> $37\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C$, with specified variations in individual experiments. <Gel electrophoresis> and radioactive-film detection showed excised <intron> <RNA>; sequencing verified its $5'$ junction. See https://dosequis.colorado.edu/courses/methodslogic/papers/Cech1982.pdf[Kruger et al., Self-Splicing RNA, Cell 31 (1982), 147–157, figures 3–4 and Experimental Procedures].

Excision alone could leave disconnected <exons>. To test joining, the authors paired labelled <RNA> with <DNA> spanning the joined-<exon> sequence, removed exposed <RNA> with <enzymes>, and separated the protected pieces by denaturing <gel electrophoresis>. Spliced samples yielded protected <RNA> long enough to span the junction. See https://dosequis.colorado.edu/courses/methodslogic/papers/Cech1982.pdf[Kruger et al., 1982, figure 6: independent evidence for exon ligation]. The experimental logic matters: an <intron>-sized band identifies a candidate product, while sequence and junction assays test its chemical identity.

These controls support sufficiency of the prepared <RNA> under the assay conditions. They do not show that every <RNA> self-splices, or that <proteins> are dispensable inside a <biological cell>. Many <group I introns> use <proteins> to help attain an active fold. See https://herschlaglab.stanford.edu/s/39.pdf[Cech and Herschlag, Group I Ribozymes, section 6: protein facilitation]. Nor does a failed assay necessarily identify an inactive <nucleotide sequence>: unsuitable <ions>, <pH>, or <temperature> can prevent a working <active site> from forming.

The distinction between self-processing and repeated use became especially clear with bacterial <RNase P>. In 1983 Guerrier-Takada, Gardiner, Marsh, Pace and Altman separated the <RNA> and <protein> components of <RNase P> from two <bacteria>. Purified <RNA> cleaved precursor <transfer RNA> under conditions containing $60\,\mathrm{mM}$ <magnesium> <ions>, or $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ <magnesium> <ions> plus $1\,\mathrm{mM}$ spermidine. The <protein> components alone had no catalytic activity. At $5$–$10\,\mathrm{mM}$ <magnesium> without spermidine, both components were needed. Thus the comparison demonstrated an <RNA> <catalyst> while also explaining why <protein> assistance could matter under other conditions. <RNase P> produces the mature $5'$ end of <transfer RNA> by removing its leader; it acts on a separate <substrate> and can be used again. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6197186/[Guerrier-Takada et al., The RNA moiety of ribonuclease P is the catalytic subunit of the enzyme, Cell 35 (1983), 849–857: primary abstract].

Self-splicing demonstrates <RNA> chemistry, but one precursor completing one rearrangement is not yet evidence for catalytic turnover: repeated conversion of separate <substrates> by a regenerated <catalyst>. The self-splicing precursor changes its own connectivity and incorporates external <guanosine>. In 1986 Zaug and Cech showed that a shortened Tetrahymena <intron> <RNA> could cleave and rejoin separate short <RNA> <substrates> in a sequence-dependent reaction. This demonstrated an <RNA> <enzyme> acting beyond its own self-processing. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3941911/[Zaug and Cech, The intervening sequence RNA of Tetrahymena is an enzyme, Science 231 (1986), 470–475: primary abstract].

= Active site
{parent=Enzyme}
{wiki}

An <active site> is the region of an <enzyme> where a <substrate> binds and undergoes a <chemical reaction>. In a <protein> <enzyme>, it includes the <amino acid residues> that contact the <substrate> and those that directly assist <catalysis>. These roles can overlap: an <amino acid side chain> can both orient a <substrate> and participate in chemistry. The <active site> occupies only part of the folded <protein>; the remaining <amino acid residues> help establish its <protein structure>, stability and movement. An <active site> need not be a consecutive stretch of <protein primary structure>: <protein folding> can bring distant <amino acid residues> together. A <ribozyme> instead supplies an <RNA> <active site>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26911/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, Protein Function: binding sites and catalytic sites].

Binding and <catalysis> answer different questions. A <protein binding site> recognizes shape and chemistry through such interactions as <hydrogen bonds>, <electrostatics> and the <hydrophobic effect>. At an <active site>, appropriate positioning also helps a <substrate> reach the <transition state> of a <chemical reaction>. Some <amino acid side chains> transfer a <proton>; others participate transiently in a <covalent bond>. The <enzyme-substrate complex> is the associated <enzyme> and <substrate>, before conversion to a <reaction product>. Merely accumulating this complex does not establish rapid <catalysis>: stabilizing the bound <substrate> without sufficiently stabilizing the <transition state> can leave a large barrier. The important complementarity is chemical as well as geometric. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9921/[Geoffrey M. Cooper, The Cell, The Central Role of Enzymes as Biological Catalysts: substrate binding and transition-state stabilization].

The cartoon makes a useful experimental distinction. Changing a contacting <amino acid residue> might weaken <substrate> binding, change the <reaction rate>, or do both. Changing a distant <amino acid residue> can also disrupt the <active site> by changing <protein folding>. Therefore a loss of activity after a <mutation> alone does not locate a catalytic group: compare binding, <protein structure> and <enzyme kinetics> as well. This is an interpretation guide, not a report of a particular <mutation> experiment.

A concrete example is <hexokinase>: it transfers the terminal <phosphate> group of <ATP> to the $6$-position <hydroxyl group> of a <sugar> such as <glucose>. Specificity need not mean only one acceptable <substrate>: the <hexokinase> reaction class includes several possible acceptor <sugars>. Which are used, and at what <reaction rates>, depends on the particular <enzyme> and conditions. See https://enzyme.expasy.org/EC/2.7.1.1[Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, ENZYME entry EC 2.7.1.1: hexokinase reaction and acceptor range].

Bennett and Steitz compared <yeast> <hexokinase> A crystallized with <glucose> against <hexokinase> B crystallized without <glucose>. <X-rays> scattered by the crystals supplied <diffraction> measurements from which the authors reconstructed <protein structure>; comparing coordinates located movement, rather than measuring a <reaction rate>. The <glucose>-containing cleft was narrower: one lobe was rotated by about $12^\circ$ relative to the other, with some <protein backbone> displacements reaching $8\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$. This makes the <active site> a moving arrangement of interacting groups, rather than a permanent hole. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC336218/[William S. Bennett Jr. and Thomas A. Steitz, Glucose-induced conformational change in yeast hexokinase, PNAS 75 (1978), 4848–4852].

The structural comparison has two explicit limitations: different forms of <hexokinase> and different crystal environments were compared, and static coordinates do not record the order of binding and movement. Closure is consistent with <induced fit>, but those structures alone cannot exclude <conformational selection>. The solution experiment under <induced fit> addresses whether compaction also occurs outside a crystal.

= Induced fit
{parent=Active site}

In <induced fit>, a <substrate> binds to an <enzyme> and the resulting <enzyme-substrate complex> subsequently changes its <protein structure>. This adjustment can position interacting groups at the <active site> for <catalysis>. The mechanistic claim concerns order: binding precedes the relevant change. In <conformational selection>, the unbound <enzyme> already visits an alternative <protein structure>, which the <substrate> preferentially binds. Both processes can occur in one system. Let $E$ and $E^*$ denote two structures of the same <enzyme>, and $S$ a <substrate>. The limiting routes are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{induced fit:}\quad &E+S\rightleftharpoons ES\rightleftharpoons E^*S,\\
\text{conformational selection:}\quad &E\rightleftharpoons E^*,\qquad E^*+S\rightleftharpoons E^*S.
\end{aligned}
$$
The star labels a structure, not a different <protein primary structure>, and does not by itself mean catalytic activity. Each reversible arrow abbreviates a step, not the whole catalytic cycle. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2728963/[Gordon G. Hammes, Yu-Chu Chang and Terrence G. Oas, Conformational selection or induced fit: a flux description of reaction mechanism, PNAS 106 (2009), 13737–13741].

A rigid lock-and-key cartoon usefully suggests selective <substrate> recognition, but it suppresses movement of the <protein> and chemical changes at the <active site>. Replacing it with two static drawings, “open” and “closed”, still does not establish <induced fit>: both routes above can end at $E^*S$. Also, if complete closure prevents a <substrate> entering, the pre-existing structure selected in <conformational selection> might be partly open and undergo further <induced fit> after binding. The usefulness of the distinction is that it turns an attractive shape story into questions about <enzyme kinetics>. Relative traffic through the routes depends on populations, <rate constants> and free <substrate> <concentration>, rather than affinity alone. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2728963/[Hammes, Chang and Oas, 2009: coupled pathways, their concentrations and the limitations of endpoint structures].

For this analytical illustration, hold the free <substrate> <concentration> $s$ constant and assume independent waiting times with <exponential distributions> of constant rates. The <law of mass action> gives the binding rate $a=k_{\mathrm{on}}s$; the competing change has rate $b$. The <probability density function> for binding to happen first at time $t$ is $a e^{-(a+b)t}$, because neither event has happened earlier. Its integral is
$$
P(\text{binding first})=\int_0^\infty a e^{-(a+b)t}\,dt=\frac{a}{a+b}.
$$
With the stated invented <rate constants>, $s=0.1,1,10\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ gives $1/11,1/2,10/11$, respectively. Raising free <substrate> <concentration> makes binding more likely to win this initial competition. This <probability> is deliberately narrower than a full-route fraction: returns to $E$, later changes and the binding properties of $E^*$ would all matter for the latter. The illustration explains why timing and <concentration> must accompany structural evidence, without assigning a binding mechanism to a real <enzyme>.

A motivating experiment tested the crystal interpretation in solution. McDonald, Steitz and Engelman prepared solutions of monomeric <yeast> <hexokinase> B and compared the unbound preparation with sugar-bound preparations. Their apparatus measured small-angle scattering of <X-rays>; the spatial extent inferred from that readout was the <radius of gyration>, rather than a catalytic <reaction rate>. Binding <glucose> reduced the <radius of gyration> by $0.95\pm0.24\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$; binding the phosphorylated <glucose> product reduced it by $1.25\pm0.28\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$, as reported by the authors. The <glucose> change agreed with the reduction calculated from the crystal coordinates. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/369601/[Robert C. McDonald, Thomas A. Steitz and Donald M. Engelman, Yeast hexokinase in solution exhibits a large conformational change upon binding glucose or glucose 6-phosphate, Biochemistry 18 (1979), 338–342: primary abstract].

The comparison without added <substrate> supplies a baseline within the same form of <hexokinase>; the second bound preparation tests another binding condition. Together with the crystal comparison, solution compaction supports closure beyond crystal packing. It still gives an average spatial readout, not a movie of individual binding events, and product-associated compaction does not demonstrate ongoing <catalysis>. Neither experiment alone proves that binding precedes movement. To distinguish <induced fit> from <conformational selection>, structural comparisons must be combined with measurements sensitive to timing and with a model of the relevant populations and <enzyme kinetics>.

= ATP synthase rotary mechanism
{c}
{parent=ATP synthase}

The membrane motor $F_0$ contains a rotating ring of $c$ subunits beside stationary subunit $a$. Two offset water-accessible half-channels connect opposite membrane sides to the ring. A <proton> enters one half-channel and protonates a <glutamic acid residue> on a $c$ subunit; neutralization permits that site to enter the <hydrophobic> <lipid bilayer>. Rotation carries it around to the other half-channel, where it releases the <proton>. A conserved <arginine> in subunit $a$ helps separate the routes and promotes release. Downhill <proton> transfer biases rotation; this is not a freely open transmembrane pore.<Structure of a bacterial ATP synthase by Hui Guo and others>[Guo, Suzuki and Rubinstein, Structure of a bacterial ATP synthase, 2019, Figure 4B–D and Proton translocation through the FO region]{ref}

The central shaft transmits <torque> to $F_1$; a peripheral stalk prevents the catalytic head from simply co-rotating. Its three $\beta$ catalytic sites, within an $\alpha_3\beta_3$ head, cycle through different binding shapes. In the simplified binding-change picture, a loose site captures <ADP> and <inorganic phosphate>, a tight site favours bound <ATP>, and opening releases <ATP>. Rotation changes binding and release as well as chemistry.<Structure of ATP synthase under strain during catalysis by Hui Guo and John L. Rubinstein>[Guo and Rubinstein, Structure of ATP synthase under strain during catalysis, 2022, Introduction and Figure 1: binding-change cycle]{ref}

= ATP synthase energy and proton budget
{c}
{parent=ATP synthase}

Define side $P$ as the higher <proton> <electrochemical potential> reservoir and side $N$ as the synthesis-side reservoir. For transfer $P\to N$ at common <temperature> $T$, define the positive downhill <proton-motive force> magnitude $\Delta p_{\rm drive}$ by
$$
\Delta\widetilde\mu_{P\to N}
=RT\ln\frac{a_N}{a_P}+F(\psi_N-\psi_P)
=-F\Delta p_{\rm drive}.
$$
Here $a$ is <thermodynamic activity>, $\psi$ is <electric potential>, $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $F$ is the <Faraday constant>. Thus both a higher <proton> <thermodynamic activity> and a more positive <electric potential> on side $P$ contribute to the driving magnitude. This definition fixes the sign; conventions for a signed <proton-motive force> differ. The calculation under <chemiosmosis> separates these two contributions numerically.

Assume fixed reservoirs, tight mechanical <energy coupling>, one transported <proton> per $c$ subunit per revolution, and three <ATP> produced per revolution. Neglect leaks, slipping and <membrane transport> of the chemical reagents. Let $g_{\rm ATP}>0$ be the local molar <Gibbs free energy> cost of <ATP> formation at the stipulated composition, <pH> and <magnesium>-binding convention. It is not necessarily the <standard state> value. Adding changes in the <state function> gives, per mole of complete turns,
$$
\Delta G_{\rm turn}=3g_{\rm ATP}-nF\Delta p_{\rm drive}.
$$
For this model net synthesis is thermodynamically favoured when $\Delta G_{\rm turn}<0$. Equality is a reversible balance, not a promise of a useful <reaction rate>. A negative value establishes an available driving margin; it does not specify how fast the <enzyme> runs.

Choose $n=10$, $g_{\rm ATP}=50.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, $F=96485\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\Delta p_{\rm drive}=0.180\,\mathrm V$. These are illustrative inputs, not measurements of the preparation in the experiment below. Then
$$
nF\Delta p_{\rm drive}=173.673\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
3g_{\rm ATP}=150.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},
$$
and $\Delta G_{\rm turn}=-23.673\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Per <ATP>, the ideal input is $57.891\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, leaving a driving margin of $7.891\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The balance point is
$$
\Delta p_* =\frac{3g_{\rm ATP}}{nF}\approx0.15546\,\mathrm V.
$$
At $0.140\,\mathrm V$ the same synthesis cycle instead has $\Delta G_{\rm turn}=+14.921\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$: the chosen reservoirs favour its reverse.

The machine is reversible: sufficient <ATP> <hydrolysis> can drive opposite rotation and pump <protons> uphill. Actual direction also depends on inhibition and available <substrates>; a weak <proton-motive force> need not make every intact <enzyme> immediately run backward. <Respiratory uncoupling> supplies a different failure mode: a parallel <proton> leak dissipates the gradient without productive shaft rotation.<Rotary catalysis of FoF1-ATP synthase by Rikiya Watanabe>[Watanabe, 2013, p. 51 and Figure 2: reversal of the coupled motors]{ref}

The motor budget $10/3$ is not the whole cost of <ATP> delivered outside a <mitochondrion>. = Trypsin cleavage specificity
{parent=Trypsin}

Write a <peptide>'s <protein primary structure> from its <N-terminus> toward its <C-terminus>. The inspected institutional documentation describes <proline> as usually blocking cleavage, and explicitly lists sequence contexts in which that blocking is negligible. \mathrm{AGKARPKAGRAK}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{AGK}\;|\;\mathrm{ARPK}\;|\;\mathrm{AGR}\;|\;\mathrm{AK}.

= Reversible enzyme inhibition
{parent=Enzyme inhibitor}

Construct a minimal model with one <substrate> and no appreciable accumulated <reaction product>. Only the <enzyme-substrate complex> $ES$ produces product, with catalytic <rate constant> $k_{\rm cat}$; $EI$ and $ESI$ are inactive. Write $s=[S]$ and $I=[\mathrm I]$ for free <molar concentrations>, and $E_T$ for total <enzyme> <molar concentration>. Assume a suitable <quasi-steady-state approximation>, rapid inhibitor binding and release relative to turnover, and negligible depletion of free inhibitor. In this construction the population relations are
$$
[ES]=[E]\frac{s}{K_M},\qquad
[EI]=[E]\frac{I}{K_i},\qquad
[ESI]=[ES]\frac{I}{K_i'},\qquad
\alpha=1+\frac{I}{K_i},\quad \alpha'=1+\frac{I}{K_i'}.
$$
Here $K_M$ is the uninhibited <Michaelis constant>, and $K_i,K_i'$ are the <inhibition constants> for the two indicated binding branches. The symbol $I$ denotes a free <concentration>, not an amount added to the tube. Define $V_{\max}=k_{\rm cat}E_T$. Conservation of <enzyme> gives an original short derivation:
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_T&=[E]+[EI]+[ES]+[ESI]
=[E]\left(\alpha+\alpha'\frac{s}{K_M}\right),\\
v&=k_{\rm cat}[ES]
=\frac{V_{\max}s}{\alpha K_M+\alpha's}
=\frac{(V_{\max}/\alpha')s}{(\alpha/\alpha')K_M+s}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus fitting a <Michaelis-Menten equation> at a fixed $I$ would return the apparent parameters
$$
V_{\max,\rm app}=\frac{V_{\max}}{\alpha'},\qquad
K_{M,\rm app}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha'}K_M.
$$
“Apparent” means conditional on that inhibitor <concentration>. The uninhibited <Michaelis constant> has not been redefined. <Dimensional analysis> checks the denominator: both $\alpha K_M$ and $\alpha's$ have units of <molar concentration>; the resulting $v$ has units of <reaction rate>.

= Inhibition constant
{parent=Reversible enzyme inhibition}
{title2=$K_i$, $K_i'$}

An <inhibition constant> sets an inhibitor <concentration> scale in an <enzyme kinetics> model. Smaller values favour the corresponding bound population at fixed free inhibitor <concentration>. = Competitive inhibition
{parent=Reversible enzyme inhibition}
{wiki}

In the model of <reversible enzyme inhibition>, <competitive inhibition> makes <substrate> binding and inhibitor binding mutually exclusive: $EI$ is allowed but $ESI$ is absent. Set $\alpha'=1$, equivalently take $K_i'\to\infty$. Then
$$
v=\frac{V_{\max}s}{\alpha K_M+s},\qquad
V_{\max,\rm app}=V_{\max},\quad K_{M,\rm app}=\alpha K_M.
$$
For the chosen $I=30\,\mathrm{\mu M}$, $\alpha=4$: the half-limiting <substrate> <concentration> rises from $20$ to $80\,\mathrm{\mu M}$, while the limiting <reaction rate> remains $100\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$. At $s=200\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ the inhibited <reaction rate> is $500/7\approx71.43\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$, compared with the uninhibited $1000/11\approx90.91\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$. “Overcome by <substrate>” means that their ratio tends to one as $s\to\infty$, not that any finite addition removes the difference.

= Mixed inhibition
{parent=Reversible enzyme inhibition}
{wiki}

In the branching model, <mixed inhibition> retains inhibitor binding to both free <enzyme> and the <enzyme-substrate complex>. Unequal $K_i$ and $K_i'$ shift both apparent parameters; their equality gives <pure noncompetitive inhibition>. For the chosen unequal case, $I=30\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ gives $\alpha=4$ and $\alpha'=2$, hence $V_{\max,\rm app}=50\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$ and $K_{M,\rm app}=40\,\mathrm{\mu M}$. Because $K_i<K_i'$, binding favours free <enzyme>, and the apparent <Michaelis constant> increases. Reversing that inequality decreases it.

The limiting behaviour provides a useful diagnostic within this model:
$$
\lim_{s\to0}\frac{v}{s}=\frac{V_{\max}}{\alpha K_M},\qquad
\lim_{s\to\infty}v=\frac{V_{\max}}{\alpha'}.
$$
Low <substrate> <concentration> isolates $\alpha$ through the initial <slope>; high <substrate> <concentration> isolates $\alpha'$ through the plateau. At $I=30\,\mathrm{\mu M}$, the chosen <competitive inhibition> and unequal <mixed inhibition> examples have the same low-$s$ <slope>, $1.25\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, but different plateaus, $100$ and $50\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$. Measuring only low <substrate> <concentrations> would miss that distinction.

= Pure noncompetitive inhibition
{parent=Mixed inhibition}

Set $K_i=K_i'$, so that $\alpha=\alpha'$. The <reversible enzyme inhibition> equation reduces to
$$
v=\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{V_{\max}s}{K_M+s},\qquad
V_{\max,\rm app}=\frac{V_{\max}}{\alpha},\quad K_{M,\rm app}=K_M.
$$
At the chosen $I=30\,\mathrm{\mu M}$, every <reaction rate> is one quarter of its uninhibited value. The plateau falls to $25\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$, but half that plateau still occurs at $s=20\,\mathrm{\mu M}$. Increasing <substrate> cannot restore the uninhibited plateau. An unchanged apparent <Michaelis constant> here expresses equality of the two binding effects, rather than an absence of inhibitor binding to the <enzyme-substrate complex>.

= Uncompetitive inhibition
{parent=Reversible enzyme inhibition}
{wiki}

The complementary limit of the branching model permits inhibitor binding only to the <enzyme-substrate complex>: set $K_i\to\infty$, hence $\alpha=1$, with finite $K_i'$. Then both $V_{\max,\rm app}$ and $K_{M,\rm app}$ decrease by $\alpha'$, while their ratio stays fixed. This limit helps distinguish preferential binding to $ES$ in <mixed inhibition> from exclusive binding to $ES$ in <uncompetitive inhibition>.

The <trypsin colorimetric assay> explains blank subtraction and conversion to a <reaction rate> using the <Beer-Lambert law>. To test the model, an experimental extension would vary both free inhibitor and free <substrate> <concentrations>, estimate early linear <reaction rates> before appreciable depletion, and fit the whole collection by <nonlinear regression>. Include a no-inhibitor series and inhibitor-matched blanks: inhibitor-dependent <absorbance> drift must not be mistaken for changed <catalysis>.

There is a mathematical reason to collect more than one <reaction rate>. At $s=20\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ with the chosen uninhibited parameters, measuring $v=25\,\mathrm{\mu M\,min^{-1}}$ would imply only $\alpha+\alpha'=4$. Both $(\alpha,\alpha')=(2,2)$ and $(3,1)$ satisfy that observation, although their high-$s$ limits differ. A broader <substrate> range tests which model fits. Even a fitted curve leaves binding geometry unresolved. Check activity recovery after reducing inhibitor <concentration> separately; the simple equation also ceases to apply when free inhibitor is substantially depleted by binding or inhibitor exchange has not equilibrated during the measured interval.
