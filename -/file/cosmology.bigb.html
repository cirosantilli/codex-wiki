= Scalar-field cosmology
{parent=Cosmology}

A homogeneous canonical <scalar field> contributes <energy density> $\rho=\dot\varphi^2/2+V$ and <pressure> $P=\dot\varphi^2/2-V$. When it is the only source in a spatially flat <FLRW metric>, the <Friedmann equation> is $3M_{\rm Pl}^2H^2=\rho$, and its evolution follows the <scalar field equation of motion>. Positive <energy density> implies $w\ge-1$ because $\rho+P=\dot\varphi^2\ge0$, even if the <scalar potential> is negative.

= Exponential-potential scalar-dominated power-law solution
{parent=Scalar-field cosmology}
{title2=$a\propto t^{2/\lambda^2},\quad\varphi=(2M_{\rm Pl}/\lambda)\log(t/t_0)$}

For $V=V_0e^{-\lambda\varphi/M_{\rm Pl}}$, $\lambda\ne0$, the displayed ansatz satisfies the flat <Friedmann equation> and <scalar field equation of motion> when $V_0t_0^2=2M_{\rm Pl}^2(6-\lambda^2)/\lambda^4$. Its <equation-of-state parameter> is $w=-1+\lambda^2/3$ and its <deceleration parameter> is $q=\lambda^2/2-1$. It gives <exponential-potential power-law inflation> only for $0<\lambda^2<2$; the same algebraic family includes decelerating expansion outside that range when its potential normalization is real.

= Existence conditions for exponential-potential scaling
{parent=Exponential-potential scalar-dominated power-law solution}
{title2=$V_0(6-\lambda^2)>0$}

For $V_0\ne0$, real nonzero $\lambda$, and a finite positive logarithmic time scale $t_0$, the <exponential-potential scalar-dominated power-law solution> requires the displayed strict sign condition. Thus positive $V_0$ permits $0<\lambda^2<6$, while negative $V_0$ permits $\lambda^2>6$. The boundary $\lambda^2=6$ cannot occur with nonzero $V_0$ in this ansatz. Setting $\lambda=0$ gives a constant potential and a separate branch, including constant-field <de Sitter spacetime> for $V_0>0$.

= Baryonic matter
{parent=Cosmology}

The ordinary matter component whose <mass> is predominantly in <baryons>, including stars and gas. Cosmological baryonic-matter budgets conventionally include the associated <electrons>. Its cosmic fraction among total matter is $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$, rather than $\Omega_b$ alone.

= Local cosmological tidal acceleration
{parent=Cosmology}
{title2=$\ddot{\mathbf r}=(\ddot a/a)\mathbf r$}

Neighboring freely falling comoving particles in a homogeneous <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric> have separation $\mathbf r=a(t)\boldsymbol\chi$ and relative <acceleration> $\ddot{\mathbf r}=(\ddot a/a)\mathbf r$, where $t$ is <cosmic time> and $\boldsymbol\chi$ is constant. This is a local <tidal force> effect, of order $H^2r$ when the dimensionless background parameters are of order one. In a weak-field central-mass model retaining the homogeneous background tide, one writes $\ddot r=-GM/r^2+\ell^2/r^3+(\ddot a/a)r$. The detailed residual tide depends on the cosmological embedding; a bound orbit is not required to satisfy $\dot r=Hr$. Comparing $H^2$ with $GM/r^3$ estimates whether that background tide can affect its dynamics. A locally vacuum region with a <cosmological constant> has the corresponding outward term $\Lambda c^2r/3$.

= Physical cosmology
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

<Physical cosmology> connects the universe's history to the measurable behaviour of its contents. The <Friedmann equations> describe cosmic expansion; <atomic physics>, <radiation pressure> and <gravity> determine what that expanding universe looks like. A particularly revealing example is the route from <cosmological recombination> through the <photon-baryon fluid> to the <cosmic microwave background acoustic peaks>. The universe supplies the <initial conditions> and the laboratory; the prediction must still pass through a model of the telescope before it can be compared with observations.

The practical reward is that one sky contains several different tests. The <cosmic microwave background power spectrum> carries information about the <sound horizon>, the <cosmological baryon density> and cosmic geometry. These are related quantities with different observational signatures. <Angular sound-horizon degeneracy> explains why measuring a characteristic angle alone cannot count the universe's <baryons>.

= Peebles primordial-plasma predictions
{c}
{parent=Physical cosmology}

<P. J. E. Peebles> made the connection between microscopic processes and cosmological observables quantitative. His 1968 paper treated <cosmological recombination> as a kinetic problem. Capturing an <electron> directly into the <hydrogen> <ground state> releases a <photon> that can ionize another atom. Net neutralization depends on escape of resonance radiation through <cosmological redshift> and on <two-photon spontaneous emission> from the $2s$ state. An equilibrium <Saha equation> alone does not give that history.<Recombination of the Primeval Plasma by P. J. E. Peebles>[Peebles, Recombination of the Primeval Plasma (1968), §§IIb–IId, pp.3–6]{ref}

This matters for an acoustic prediction because the <hydrogen ionization fraction> fixes the supply of free <electrons> for <Thomson scattering>. In physical units the scattering rate per <photon> is $\Gamma_T=n_e\sigma_Tc$. The <cosmological visibility function> describes the distribution of last-scattering times. Thus <atomic physics> helps determine when the <photon-baryon acoustic oscillator> is read out, as well as how rapidly <tight coupling> fails.

In 1970 <P. J. E. Peebles> and <J. T. Yu> followed <adiabatic cosmological perturbations> by numerically integrating the <photon> collision equation through <cosmological recombination>. Their calculation connected acoustic evolution and dissipation to residual matter structure and microwave brightness fluctuations. It did not assume that a perfect <photon-baryon fluid> remained valid throughout last scattering. Their illustrative cosmological models and normalization were historical choices, rather than the present <Lambda-CDM model>; their predicted brightness variance versus angular resolution should not be relabelled as a modern measured $C_\ell$ spectrum.<Primeval Adiabatic Perturbation in an Expanding Universe by P. J. E. Peebles and J. T. Yu>[Peebles and Yu, Primeval Adiabatic Perturbation in an Expanding Universe (1970), pp.815–816; §VI, pp.829–833, Figs.4–9]{ref}

The continuing idea is a forward prediction: specify <initial conditions>, evolve the coupled matter and radiation, project onto the sky, and compare the result with an instrument's readout. <Coherent initial phases are needed for acoustic variance peaks> explains why a collection of oscillating modes does not automatically produce a peaked <power spectrum>. The <BOOMERANG acoustic-peak measurement> provides a later observational test of this physical programme.

= Constant-loading acoustic model
{parent=Peebles primordial-plasma predictions}
{title2=$y''+k^2c_s^2y=-k^2c_s^2R$}

A deliberately restricted <forced harmonic oscillator> shows two effects of the <baryon loading parameter> without duplicating the full <photon Boltzmann hierarchy>. Use <conformal time> $d\eta=dt/a$ in seconds, a comoving <wavenumber> $k$ in inverse metres, constant $R=3\rho_b/(4\rho_\gamma)$, and equal constant dimensionless <Newtonian gauge> gravitational potentials $\Phi=\Psi\ne0$. The <energy densities> $\rho_b$ and $\rho_\gamma$ use the same units. Neglect expansion drag, <Silk damping> and evolution of the potentials. The <photon-baryon sound speed> is $c_s=c/\sqrt{3(1+R)}$. Writing $\Theta_0$ for the <photon monopole> temperature perturbation, the <photon-baryon acoustic oscillator> reduces to
$$
\Theta_0''+k^2c_s^2\Theta_0=-\frac{k^2c^2}{3}\Psi.
$$
For $y=(\Theta_0+\Psi)/\Psi$ this gives the displayed forced equation. Constant loading and the neglected expansion drag make this a local teaching model, not a self-consistent expanding cosmology with $R$ fixed forever.

Choose $y(0)=1/3$ and $y'(0)=0$, the static matter-era <Sachs-Wolfe combination> normalized by $\Psi$. With $q=kc_s\eta$, direct substitution gives
$$
y(q)=\left(\frac13+R\right)\cos q-R,\qquad y_q(0)=0.
$$
At $R=0$, successive extrema have equal magnitudes $1/3$. At the stipulated $R=0.6$, $y(\pi)=-23/15$ whereas $y(2\pi)=1/3$. The shifted equilibrium $y=-R$ makes alternating extrema unequal. This is an elementary illustration of how the <baryon loading parameter> changes acoustic amplitudes; these extrema are neither measured peak heights nor a complete <cosmic microwave background power spectrum>. Comparing curves at fixed $q$ isolates the response; at fixed $k$ and $\eta$, increasing $R$ also reduces the <photon-baryon sound speed> and hence the phase.

For example, holding $c\eta_*=300\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ gives <sound horizons> $r_s=173.2\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ at $R=0$ and $136.9\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ at $R=0.6$. Their ratio is $1/\sqrt{1.6}$. Each term of the forced equation has units of inverse seconds squared because $y$ and $R$ are dimensionless and $kc_s$ is an inverse time. In the actual <photon-baryon fluid>, $R$ grows with the <scale factor> and the <sound horizon> is the integral of the evolving <photon-baryon sound speed>.

= Angular sound-horizon degeneracy
{parent=Physical cosmology}
{title2=$\theta_*=r_s(z_*)/D_M(z_*),\quad\ell_A=\pi/\theta_*$}

The angular <sound horizon> compares two lengths using the same distance convention. If $D_A$ is the <angular diameter distance> to last scattering at <redshift> $z_*$, define the transverse <comoving distance> $D_M=(1+z_*)D_A$. A comoving <sound horizon> $r_s$ has proper length $r_s/(1+z_*)$ at emission, so its angular size is $\theta_*=r_s/D_M$. Mixing a comoving ruler with $D_A$ would introduce an erroneous factor $1+z_*$. In a curved <FLRW metric>, $D_M$ is generally different from the radial <comoving distance>.

For a stipulated example, $r_s=144\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ and $D_M=14000\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ give $\theta_*=0.010286$ radians, or $0.589^\circ$, and an acoustic scale $\ell_A=305.4$. Multiplying both lengths by $1.1$ gives $158.4\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ and $15400\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ with exactly the same $\theta_*$ and $\ell_A$. This example establishes a ratio degeneracy, without claiming that any arbitrary rescaling corresponds to an allowed cosmological model. Equivalently,
$$
\delta\ln\theta_*=\delta\ln r_s-\delta\ln D_M.
$$
Other observables are needed to separate changes that preserve the ratio.

The approximate <adiabatic acoustic-peak spacing> is $\ell_A$. The first observed temperature peak is shifted by gravitational driving and <angular projection of density and Doppler acoustic sources>; it need not occur at $\ell_A$. In particular, neither the illustrative $305.4$ nor the <BOOMERANG acoustic-peak measurement> of $197\pm6$ should be substituted for the other.

The <cosmological baryon density> enters through the <baryon loading parameter> and <cosmological recombination>, changing relative acoustic amplitudes as well as the <sound horizon>. The <baryon-to-photon ratio> counts net <baryon number> per <photon>, whereas $\theta_*$ is an angle. Constraining <cosmological baryon density> requires the pattern of relative peak heights within a model, with other parameters fitted jointly. <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> provides a separate abundance test. <Baryon-density consistency between nucleosynthesis and the microwave background> is consequently a test of one common cosmic history, rather than two direct measurements of the same angle. A first peak by itself cannot determine every component of the <cosmological density parameter> or uniquely fix <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>.

The 2000 paper fitted the <cosmic microwave background power spectrum>, finding $\ell_{\rm peak}=197\pm6$ at one standard deviation. = Cosmological spectral background
{parent=Cosmology}
{title2=$I_{\nu_0}$}

The <cosmological spectral background> is received radiant power per detector area, received <frequency> interval and <solid angle>, summed over a cosmological source population. Its intensity depends on emitted spectra, their cosmic evolution, the expansion history and absorption. A finite radial <comoving distance> alone does not guarantee a finite background when high-<redshift> sources are sampled at increasingly high emitted <frequencies>.

= Cosmological bolometric background from conserved sources
{parent=Cosmological spectral background}
{title2=$\mathcal F_0=cLn_0\int a(t)\,dt$}

Normalize the <scale factor> to $a_0=1$. Identical isotropic sources of <bolometric luminosity> $L$ and conserved <comoving number density> $n_0=a^3n$ produce all-sky <radiative flux> $\mathcal F_0=cLn_0\int a(t)\,dt$ in a transparent <FLRW metric>. The <source counts on an FLRW past light cone> and <cosmological flux dimming> cancel the transverse shell-area factor. For arbitrary proper source density the expression is instead $cL\int n(t)a(t)^4dt$. The intensity per <solid angle> is $\mathcal F_0/(4\pi)$. A detector of collecting area $A$ receives power $A\mathcal F_0$ under an all-sky effective-area convention.

= Open dust bolometric background integral
{parent=Cosmological bolometric background from conserved sources}
{title2=$\mathcal F_0=\dfrac{cLn_0\Omega_0^2}{4H_0(1-\Omega_0)^{5/2}}J(u_0)$}

For an <open matter-dominated Friedmann solution>, put $u=H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_0}\,\tau$, $a=\Omega_0(\cosh u-1)/[2(1-\Omega_0)]$ and $\cosh u_0=2/\Omega_0-1$. The <cosmological bolometric background from conserved sources> has $J(u)=\sinh(2u)/4-2\sinh u+3u/2$, since $J'=(\cosh u-1)^2$ and $dt=a\,du/[H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_0}]$. At fixed $H_0$, the limit $\Omega_0\to0$ is $cLn_0/(2H_0)$, whereas the <Einstein-de Sitter universe> gives $2cLn_0/(5H_0)$. Keeping the cosmic age fixed instead reverses this ordering: the two values are respectively $cLn_0t_0/2$ and $3cLn_0t_0/5$.

= Cosmological background intensity from comoving emissivity
{parent=Cosmological spectral background}
{title2=$I_{\nu_0}=\dfrac{c}{4\pi}\int\dfrac{\epsilon_{(1+z)\nu_0}(z)}{(1+z)H(z)}\,dz$}

Let $\epsilon_{\nu_e}(z)$ be the <comoving spectral emissivity>, namely total emitted <spectral luminosity> per <comoving volume>. In a homogeneous transparent <FRW metric>, a radial shell contributes its <comoving volume> times the <cosmological spectral flux-density relation>. Its transverse-area factors cancel, leaving the displayed integral. In a flat model this cancellation is explicit: $dV_c=r^2(c/H)dz\,d\Omega$, whereas $F_{\nu_0}=(1+z)L_{\nu_e}/[4\pi(1+z)^2r^2]$. Absorption inserts a transmission factor, and evolving sources are represented by the redshift dependence of $\epsilon$.

= Einstein-de Sitter spectral-background convergence criterion
{c}
{parent=Cosmological background intensity from comoving emissivity}
{title2=$\alpha<3/2$}

For nonevolving <comoving number density> $n_0$, identical spectra $L_\nu\propto\nu^\alpha$, and no attenuation, the <cosmological background intensity from comoving emissivity> in an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> is proportional to $\int_0^\infty(1+z)^{\alpha-5/2}dz$. The <improper integral> is finite exactly for $\alpha<3/2$, with value $1/(3/2-\alpha)$. Equality gives logarithmic divergence; larger $\alpha$ gives a power-law divergence. A formation epoch or a spectral break can regularize this idealized model.

= Cosmological monopole problem
{parent=Cosmology}

= Monopole problem
{synonym}

The <cosmological monopole problem> is the excessive relic density of stable <magnetic monopoles> produced in some early-universe symmetry-breaking scenarios. <Cosmic inflation> dilutes their <number density> as $a^{-3}$. Successful later <reheating> must also avoid recreating an excessive abundance.

= Horizon-density monopole abundance bound
{parent=Cosmological monopole problem}
{title2=$\rho_{M0}\propto\eta^4$}

For a radiation-era transition at $T_i\sim\eta$, use $H_i=1.66\sqrt{g_*}\eta^2/m_{\rm pl}$ and initial number density $n_i=C_nH_i^3$. Stable monopoles of mass $C_M\eta$ redshift as nonrelativistic matter. With <comoving entropy conservation>,
$$
\rho_{M0}\sim C_nC_M(1.66)^3g_*^{3/2}\frac{g_{*s,0}}{g_{*s,i}}\frac{\eta^4T_0^3}{m_{\rm pl}^3}.
$$
The fourth-power scale dependence makes even a small causal initial abundance incompatible with conventional high-scale grand unification unless inflation, later <entropy> production or efficient destruction reduces the relic population.

= Structure formation
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

<Structure formation> is the growth of cosmological matter inhomogeneities into structures such as <galaxies> through <gravitational instability> and gas dynamics. <Linear cosmological density perturbations> describe the early, small-amplitude regime; nonlinear evolution is required once the <density contrast> becomes large.

= Cosmological principle
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

The <cosmological principle> assumes that the universe is statistically homogeneous and isotropic on sufficiently large spatial scales. It applies to a coarse-grained description, not to each <galaxy> or local void. Combined with the <Copernican principle>, <cosmological isotropy> around us supports a homogeneous description rather than a specially centred model. It motivates an <FRW metric>, whose expansion is governed by a few time-dependent quantities. Observations sample only a finite <past light cone>, so they support this hypothesis without proving global <spatial homogeneity> beyond the observed region.

= Cosmological isotropy
{parent=Cosmological principle}

<Cosmological isotropy> means that coarse-grained spatial observations or their statistics are unchanged by rotations about an observer. In an <FRW metric> it holds about every comoving location. <cosmological isotropy> about a single location is weaker than <spatial homogeneity>: a radially inhomogeneous model centred on that location can also have an isotropic sky.

= Copernican assumption
{c}
{synonym}

Our observer location is not specially privileged. In cosmology this assumption allows <cosmological isotropy> observed around us to be interpreted statistically as <cosmological isotropy> around other typical observers. It is an assumption about typicality, not a deduction from one observer's sky or from the <Einstein field equations> alone.

= Cosmological particle freeze-out
{parent=Cosmology}

Decoupling of a particle abundance from chemical equilibrium when interactions become too slow compared with cosmic expansion. Pair annihilation can continue after equilibrium production is negligible, leaving a nonzero relic abundance.

= Coannihilation
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}

Number-changing reactions involving a relic candidate and another nearly degenerate species. If reactions interchange the species rapidly enough, the effective <annihilation> rate depends on their joint abundances and channels. A single-species relic estimate can then fail even for fixed candidate <mass>.

= Exponential freeze-out sensitivity
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$d\ln n_0/dP=3/(2P)-1$}

In a supplied equilibrium-cutoff model with $P=mc^2/(k_BT_f)$, present <number density> can contain $P^{3/2}e^{-P}$. The logarithmic derivative is $3/(2P)-1$, making small changes in a large <cosmological particle freeze-out> parameter important for the inferred abundance.

= Entropy degrees of freedom
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$g_{*s}$}

The effective count appearing in thermal <entropy> density $s\propto g_{*s}T^3$. Conservation of comoving <entropy> implies $g_{*s}T^3a^3$ is constant, so stable relic dilution includes $g_{*s,0}/g_{*s,f}$ when expressed using <temperatures>.

= Sharp-freeze-out relic dilution
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$n_0=n_f(a_f/a_0)^3$}

In a stable-particle approximation with negligible later <annihilation>, comoving particle number is conserved and physical <number density> falls as $a^{-3}$. Replacing scale-factor ratios by photon-temperature ratios additionally assumes no change in the <entropy degrees of freedom>.

= Mass cancellation in sharp-freeze-out number dilution
{parent=Sharp-freeze-out relic dilution}
{title2=$n_0=6P^{3/2}[k_BT_0/(\hbar c)]^3e^{-P}$}

For an imposed equilibrium cutoff $mc^2=Pk_BT_f$ and density $n_f=6[mc^2k_BT_f/(\hbar c)^2]^{3/2}e^{-P}$, dilution by $(T_0/T_f)^3$ cancels the explicit <mass> in the final <number density>. The rest-energy density still scales with particle <mass>. This result assumes constant <entropy degrees of freedom> and no post-cutoff <annihilation>; <entropy> conservation adds a factor $g_{*s,0}/g_{*s,f}$ when these assumptions are relaxed.

= Thermal relic
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$Y=n/s$}

A <thermal relic> is a surviving particle population whose earlier abundance was set by chemical <thermal equilibrium> with the cosmic plasma. After number-changing reactions cease and cosmic <entropy> is conserved, its comoving abundance is described by the yield $Y=n/s$. A relativistically decoupled species can retain an appreciable yield, whereas a species remaining in <thermal equilibrium> after becoming nonrelativistic is subject to <Boltzmann suppression>. Its later mass density, lifetime and momentum distribution determine its cosmological effects.

= Thermalization temperature for a massless gauge mediator
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$\Gamma/H=\alpha_Y^2m_{\rm pl}/(1.66\sqrt{g_*}T)$}

A rate proportional to $T$ competes with radiation-era expansion proportional to $T^2$. The interaction-to-expansion ratio therefore grows as the universe cools, crossing unity near $T_{\rm eq}=\alpha_Y^2m_{\rm pl}/(1.66\sqrt{g_*})$. If a phase transition occurs at $T_c\gg T_{\rm eq}$, this high-temperature interaction never establishes <thermal equilibrium> before the transition. Subsequent faster number-changing processes may still establish a thermal distribution; the initial state is not automatically Fermi-Dirac just because the particles are relativistic.

= Mass-dependent fermion freeze-out
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$m_{\rm nr}=\frac{\alpha_Y^4m_{\rm pl}}{1.66\sqrt{g_*}},\quad T_D=m_Y(m_Y/m_{\rm nr})^{1/3}$}

In a radiation-dominated toy model with $\Gamma=\alpha_Y^4T^5/m_Y^4$, setting the interaction rate equal to the <Hubble parameter> gives the displayed decoupling scale. The transition $T_D=m_Y$ defines $m_{\rm nr}$. For smaller masses, $x_D=m_Y/T_D=(m_{\rm nr}/m_Y)^{1/3}\gg1$. An instantaneous chemically equilibrated <cosmological particle freeze-out> estimate gives $n_Y/s=[45g_Y/(2\pi^2g_{*s})(2\pi)^{-3/2}]x_D^{3/2}e^{-x_D}$. This estimate presupposes number-changing <thermal equilibrium>, <radiation domination> and the assumed rate law, rather than following from elastic thermalization alone.

= Thermal relic density bound for a tunable fermion
{parent=Mass-dependent fermion freeze-out}
{title2=$\frac{\rho_Y}{\rho_B}=\frac{m_Y}{m_B}\frac{Y_Y}{Y_B},\quad Y_i=n_i/s$}

For a stable nonrelativistic <thermal relic>, compare <energy> densities rather than number densities. If $m_Y=m_{\rm nr}/x^3$ and $Y_Y=Cx^{3/2}e^{-x}$, its <density> relative to <baryons> is $(Cm_{\rm nr}/m_BY_B)x^{-3/2}e^{-x}$. Requiring this ratio below an allowed dark-matter-to-baryon ratio $R$ means $x+\tfrac32\log x\ge\log[Cm_{\rm nr}/(Rm_BY_B)]$. With fixed rate parameters this excludes the weakly interacting large-mass branch, while the sufficiently Boltzmann-suppressed smaller-mass branch can satisfy the <density> bound. Decay or a nonthermal initial abundance requires a different constraint.

= Post-freeze-out relic abundance
{parent=Cosmological particle freeze-out}
{title2=$Y_\infty$}

For $dY/dx=-\lambda Y^2/x^2$ after freeze-out, the exact limit is $Y_\infty=[Y_f^{-1}+\lambda/x_f]^{-1}$. The frequently used estimate $x_f/\lambda$ additionally neglects the initial inverse abundance.

= Spatial homogeneity
{parent=Cosmology}

Relative physical laws and large-scale statistical properties do not depend on the chosen spatial origin. For a continuous deterministic galaxy velocity field, using the same relative-velocity rule from each origin forces additivity and hence a position-independent linear velocity gradient.

= Cosmological shear
{parent=Spatial homogeneity}

The traceless symmetric part of the galaxy velocity-gradient <matrix> changes shape without isotropic expansion. An antisymmetric part instead represents rigid rotation. A <matrix> proportional to the identity plus an antisymmetric <matrix> has no shear.

= Cosmological perturbation theory
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

<Cosmological perturbation theory> studies departures of the <metric tensor> and matter fields from a homogeneous and isotropic <FRW metric>, order by order. <Linear cosmological perturbation theory> keeps only first-order terms. Higher orders describe nonlinear coupling between perturbations. <Nonlinear cosmological perturbation theory> also uses expansions that allow finite amplitudes, such as the <long-wavelength approximation in cosmology>.

= Nonlinear cosmological perturbation theory
{parent=Cosmological perturbation theory}

Nonlinear cosmological perturbations allow finite fluctuations of the spatial scale, density and other fields. Expanding in spatial gradients can remain useful even when a linear expansion in their amplitudes is invalid. The <long-wavelength approximation in cosmology> is one such organization of the equations.

= Long-wavelength approximation in cosmology
{parent=Nonlinear cosmological perturbation theory}
{title2=$k/(aH)\ll1$}

= Cosmological long-wavelength approximation
{synonym}

When physical wavelengths greatly exceed the <Hubble radius>, spatial derivatives are small compared with temporal expansion scales. A cosmological gradient expansion then approximates neighbouring regions as locally homogeneous universes, while retaining finite perturbation amplitudes. This does not alone discard frozen <tensor cosmological perturbations>.

= Shear-inclusive long-wavelength scalar equations
{parent=Long-wavelength approximation in cosmology}
{title2=$3H^2=8\pi G(\Pi^2/2+V)+S^i{}_jS^j{}_i/2$}

For zero <shift vector>, negative-expansion extrinsic curvature $K=-3H$ and $S^i{}_j=\widetilde K^i{}_j$, retain the leading spatial-gradient equations of a minimally coupled scalar. They give $\dot\phi=N\Pi$, $\dot\Pi=-N(3H\Pi+V_{,\phi})$, $\dot H=-4\pi GN\Pi^2-NS^i{}_jS^j{}_i/2$, and $\dot S^i{}_j=-3NH S^i{}_j$. The first-gradient <momentum constraint> is $\partial_iH=-4\pi G\Pi\partial_i\phi-\nabla_jS^j{}_i/2$. Shear decays as $a^{-3}$ during expansion. Its neglect after inflationary damping yields the equations underlying conservation of the <nonlinear curvature covector>.

= Nonlinear curvature covector
{parent=Long-wavelength approximation in cosmology}
{title2=$\zeta_i=-\partial_i\log a+(H/\Pi)\partial_i\phi$}

= Separate-universe curvature covector
{synonym}

With negligible shear, a <minimally coupled scalar field> serving as a clock, and $\Pi\ne0$, the <Hamiltonian constraint> and <momentum constraint> imply $\dot H=-4\pi GN\Pi^2$ and $\partial_iH=-4\pi G\Pi\partial_i\phi$. Differentiating the local Friedmann relation yields $\dot\Pi=-N(3H\Pi+V_{,\phi})$ and $\partial_i\Pi=-(3H+V_{,\phi}/\Pi)\partial_i\phi$. Inserting these identities into $\dot\zeta_i$ cancels all terms, including gradients of the <lapse function>. The conserved covector becomes the gradient of a <uniform-density curvature perturbation> at linear adiabatic order. Dropping the shear constraint is essential; this is not a proof of conservation in an arbitrary non-attractor phase.

= Nonlinear scalar metric in a cosmological gradient expansion
{parent=Long-wavelength approximation in cosmology}
{title2=$h_{ij}=a_{\mathrm{bg}}^2e^{2\zeta}\delta_{ij}$}

With vectors and independent tensors neglected, finite <scalar cosmological perturbations> of the local spatial scale can be encoded by $h_{ij}=a_{\mathrm{bg}}^2e^{2\zeta}\delta_{ij}$. Scalar lapse and shift potentials complete its <3+1 decomposition of spacetime>. The exact shift-square term is $h^{ij}N_iN_j$, independently of the parametrization of the scalar shift.

= Inflationary shear damping
{parent=Long-wavelength approximation in cosmology}
{title2=$\widetilde K^i{}_j\propto a_{\mathrm{loc}}^{-3}$}

The traceless mixed <extrinsic curvature of a spatial hypersurface> can have leading solution $\widetilde K^i{}_j=C^i{}_j(\mathbf x)a_{\mathrm{loc}}^{-3}$. <Cosmic inflation> suppresses this anisotropic expansion. An anisotropic spatial shape can nevertheless freeze to a nonzero constant, so shear damping does not prove the absence of <tensor cosmological perturbations>.

= Baryogenesis
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

Baryogenesis is the dynamical production of the cosmic excess of baryons over antibaryons. It requires baryon-number violation, C and CP violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium, or an appropriate generalization of these Sakharov conditions.

= Baryon-asymmetry washout
{parent=Baryogenesis}

Inverse decays or number-violating scatterings can erase an asymmetry after its generation. A viable mechanism must generate a surviving charge combination before the relevant washout processes cease to be effective.

= Leptogenesis
{parent=Baryogenesis}

A mechanism in which an initial <lepton> asymmetry is generated and then partly converted into a <baryon> asymmetry, commonly through electroweak anomalous processes. Heavy <Majorana neutrino> decays offer one possible realization; a <seesaw mechanism> by itself does not guarantee the required asymmetry.

= Baryon-number washout
{parent=Baryogenesis}

<Baryon-number washout> is erasure of a <baryon asymmetry> by reactions that violate <baryon number>. Inverse decays and scattering can drive the plasma toward its zero-asymmetry <thermal equilibrium>. The relevant comparison is their rate against the <Hubble parameter>, not just the <decay width> that initially produced the asymmetry.

= Heavy-boson decay baryogenesis
{parent=Baryogenesis}
{title2=$\epsilon=(r-\bar r)/2$}

Consider equal initial abundances of a heavy <boson> $X$ and its <antiparticle>. Two decay channels with different <baryon numbers>, for example $X\to uu$ and $X\to\bar d e^+$, have <branching fractions> $r$ and $1-r$. The conjugate channels have <branching fractions> $\bar r$ and $1-\bar r$. Their mean <baryon numbers> are $r-1/3$ and $-\bar r+1/3$, so each particle-antiparticle pair produces $r-\bar r$, or $\epsilon$ per decaying particle. Unequal <branching fractions> require <CP violation>; equal total <decay widths> are consistent with <CPT symmetry>. <Baryon number> cannot be assigned so that both channels conserve it. An initial abundance decaying outside <thermal equilibrium>, after inverse production becomes ineffective, can therefore generate a nonzero <baryon asymmetry>. At the microscopic level a decay-rate difference caused by <CP violation> requires interference of <decay amplitudes> with both a relative <weak phase> and an <absorptive phase>; complex couplings alone do not guarantee it. Subsequent <baryon-number washout> must be ineffective for the produced asymmetry to survive.

= Baryon asymmetry
{parent=Baryogenesis}
{wiki}
{title2=$\eta_B=(n_B-n_{\bar B})/n_\gamma$}

The <baryon asymmetry> is an excess of <baryons> over <antibaryons>. Here $n_B-n_{\bar B}$ counts net <baryon number> per unit volume, including the baryons bound in <atomic nuclei>. It is dynamically generated by <baryogenesis>. Once the <antibaryon> abundance is negligible, its photon-normalized value agrees with the usual <baryon-to-photon ratio>.

= Sakharov conditions
{parent=Baryogenesis}
{c}

The standard conditions for <baryogenesis> from a symmetric initial ensemble are <baryon number> violation, both <C violation> and <CP violation>, and departure from <thermal equilibrium>. Conserved <baryon number> cannot develop a net value from zero; an exact conjugation symmetry pairs production with equal antiproduction. In <thermal equilibrium>, <CPT symmetry> makes the equilibrium expectation of net <baryon number> vanish for zero imposed <chemical potentials> for charges odd under <CPT symmetry>. These conditions assume the usual local quantum-field framework and do not alone guarantee a large asymmetry.

= CP rate cancellation in baryogenesis
{c}
{parent=Sakharov conditions}

In a symmetric initial population of particles and antiparticles, exact <CP symmetry> equates conjugate decay rates after summing final momenta and helicities. Since <parity> does not reverse baryon number, this equality prevents a total baryon asymmetry even if <charge conjugation> alone is violated. Different same-helicity rates can cancel against the opposite-helicity rates.

= Cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation
{parent=Cosmology}

For a homogeneous fluid of energy density $\rho$, pressure $P$, and scale factor $a(t)$, adiabatic work in a comoving volume gives
$$
\dot\rho+3\frac{\dot a}{a}(\rho+P)=0.
$$

= Separately conserved cosmological fluids
{parent=Cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation}

A noninteracting <perfect fluid in general relativity> has $\nabla_\mu T_i^{\mu\nu}=0$, so $\dot\rho_i+3H(\rho_i+P_i)=0$. If energy is transferred between species, source terms $Q_i$ appear and only their sum vanishes. The <Friedmann equation> still involves the sum of all component stress tensors because all components share one spacetime metric.

= Barotropic equation of state
{parent=Cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation}

A constant barotropic equation of state has $P=w\rho$. The cases $w=0$ and $w=1/3$ describe pressureless matter and radiation respectively.

= Coasting fluid
{parent=Barotropic equation of state}
{title2=$w=-\tfrac13,\quad\rho\propto a^{-2}$}

A <coasting fluid> has pressure $p=-\rho/3$. The <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> makes its density scale as $a^{-2}$. If it alone fills a flat expanding universe, the <scale factor> grows linearly in physical time. Its background density has the same scale-factor dependence as a spatial-curvature term, although the fluid does not itself create spatial curvature.

= Scalar-field reconstruction of a coasting component
{parent=Coasting fluid}
{title2=$V(\phi)=2M^2H_0^2\Omega_X/a(\phi)^2$}

For conserved $w=-1/3$ scalar energy, $\rho_X=3M^2H_0^2\Omega_X/a^2$ and $\phi'=\sqrt2MH_0\sqrt{\Omega_X}$ on the increasing branch. With dust and radiation, define $b=\Omega_m/(2\Omega_X)$ and $A=\sqrt{\Omega_m^2/4-\Omega_r\Omega_X}/\Omega_X$. When the discriminant is positive,
$$
a(\phi)=A\cosh\left[\frac{\phi-\phi_0}{\sqrt2M}+C\right]-b,\qquad C=\operatorname{arcosh}\frac{1+b}{A},
$$
and $V(\phi)=2M^2H_0^2\Omega_X/a(\phi)^2$. The formulas retain all density parameters without eliminating one by the flatness sum.

= Cosmological domain wall network
{parent=Barotropic equation of state}

An isotropic, noninteracting network of <domain walls> with energy density proportional to $a^{-1}$ has effective <equation-of-state parameter> $w=-2/3$. This follows from <constant-equation-of-state density scaling> $\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$.

= Constant-equation-of-state density scaling
{parent=Barotropic equation of state}

For constant $w\ne-1$, the cosmological continuity equation integrates to
$$
\rho(a)=\rho_0a^{-3(1+w)}
$$
when $a=1$ at the reference time and $\rho_0$ is the reference density.

= Flat constant-equation-of-state scale factor
{parent=Constant-equation-of-state density scaling}

For an expanding spatially flat universe with constant $w>-1$ and $a(t_0)=1$,
$$
a(t)=\left[1+\frac32(1+w)H_0(t-t_0)\right]^{2/[3(1+w)]}.
$$
It vanishes at the finite past time $t_*=t_0-2/[3(1+w)H_0]$.

= Pressureless matter
{parent=Barotropic equation of state}
{wiki}

Pressureless matter has equation-of-state parameter $w=0$, energy density $\rho\propto a^{-3}$ and negligible stress compared with its rest-mass density.

= Cosmic string network
{parent=Barotropic equation of state}

An isotropic network of long cosmic strings has average equation-of-state parameter $w=-1/3$ and energy density proportional to $a^{-2}$.

The equation of state averages the stress of a <cosmic string> over an isotropic network; it is not the definition of the individual defect.

= Coasting cosmic-string universe
{parent=Cosmic string network}
{title2=$a(t)=B(t-t_B)$}

An isotropic <cosmic string network> with $\rho=C/a^2$ has <equation-of-state parameter> $w=-1/3$. The <Friedmann equation> gives $\dot a^2=8\pi GC/3-K$. On the expanding branch with positive right side, $a=B(t-t_B)$, the <comoving Hubble radius> is constant, and the <cosmological density parameter> is constant. There is no growing <flatness problem>, but flatness is not an attractor. If this exact coasting solution extends all the way to $t_B$, then $\int_{t_B}^t dt'/a(t')$ diverges and there is no finite <particle horizon>. A finite starting time changes that global conclusion.

= Static closed cosmic-string universe
{title2=$\rho_s a^2=3K/(8\pi G)$}
{parent=Cosmic string network}

A closed universe containing only a <cosmic string network> can remain static. Its <Friedmann acceleration equation> vanishes identically because $P_s=-\rho_s/3$, and its <Friedmann equation> requires $\rho_s a^2=3K/(8\pi G)$. Once this conserved normalization is tuned, every positive radius gives a static solution. The usual Hubble-normalized <cosmological density parameter> is undefined at a static epoch.

= Homogeneous stability of a static cosmic-string universe
{parent=Static closed cosmic-string universe}

At fixed string normalization $C=\rho_sa^2$, scale-factor shifts move between static solutions and are neutral, with no restoring force. The acceleration equation gives $\ddot a=0$, but the Friedmann constraint also imposes $\dot a^2=8\pi GC/3-K$. At the static normalization it excludes a nonzero velocity. A slightly larger $C$ admits linearly expanding or contracting branches; a smaller $C$ admits no real closed solution. This distinguishes permitted perturbations from constraint violations.

= Radiation--cosmic-string Friedmann solution
{parent=Cosmic string network}

Consider a spatially flat universe containing <radiation in cosmology>[radiation] and a <cosmic string network>. If their densities are both $\rho_{\rm eq}$ at scale factor $a_{\rm eq}$, then
$$
\rho_r=\rho_{\rm eq}\left(\frac{a_{\rm eq}}a\right)^4,
\qquad
\rho_s=\rho_{\rm eq}\left(\frac{a_{\rm eq}}a\right)^2.
$$
In <conformal time> $d\tau=dt/a$, the flat <Friedmann equation> becomes
$$
\left(\frac{da}{d\tau}\right)^2
=\frac{8\pi G a_{\rm eq}^2}{3c^2}\rho_{\rm eq}
(a^2+a_{\rm eq}^2).
$$
Choosing the Big Bang at $\tau=0$, its expanding solution is
$$
a(\tau)=a_{\rm eq}\sinh\!\left(
a_{\rm eq}\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\rho_{\rm eq}}{3c^2}}\,\tau
\right).
$$

= Critical density
{parent=Cosmology}

At Hubble parameter $H$, the critical energy density is
$$
\rho_{\rm crit}=\frac{3c^2H^2}{8\pi G}.
$$

This is the density threshold in the <Friedmann equation> for spatial flatness; thermodynamic and plasma critical densities are different concepts.

= Mass-equivalent cosmological critical density
{parent=Critical density}
{title2=$\rho_{\mathrm{crit}}=3H^2/(8\pi G)$}

The mass-density convention for the cosmological <critical density>. If $u$ denotes <energy> per unit volume, $\rho=u/c^2$ and $u_{\rm crit}=3c^2H^2/(8\pi G)$. Both conventions give the same dimensionless density parameter when numerator and denominator use consistent units. This distinction avoids inserting an extra $c^2$ into a <Friedmann equation> written in terms of mass-equivalent density.

= Cosmological density parameter
{title2=$\Omega$}
{parent=Critical density}

The density parameter is $\Omega=\rho/\rho_{\rm crit}$. In a Friedmann universe, its departure from one measures the spatial-curvature term relative to the density or expansion term.

The <Friedmann equation> relates this ratio to spatial curvature and the expansion rate; the parameter and the evolution equations are distinct concepts.

= Density-parameter divergence at a cosmological turnaround
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{title2=$\Omega=\rho/\rho_c,\quad\rho_c=3H^2/(8\pi G)$}

At maximum expansion of a <closed matter-dominated Friedmann solution>, the <Hubble parameter> vanishes while the physical <mass density> stays positive and finite. Its <critical density> therefore vanishes and the <cosmological density parameter> diverges. This is a singularity of that ratio rather than a <curvature singularity>. In the cycloid variable $\theta$, $\Omega_M=\sec^2(\theta/2)$, diverging at $\theta=\pi$ and approaching one at the <Big Bang> and <Big Crunch> ends.

= Matter density parameter
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{title2=$\Omega_m=\rho_m/\rho_{\rm crit}$}

The <matter density parameter> is the ratio of total nonrelativistic matter density, including baryonic and <dark matter>, to the <critical density> $\rho_{\rm crit}=3H^2/(8\pi G)$ at the same epoch. A present-epoch value uses $H_0$ and $\rho_{m,0}$. It is not the total <cosmological density parameter> when radiation or vacuum energy also contributes.

= Scale factor in a conformal-time density parameter
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{title2=$\Omega_i=8\pi G a^2\bar\rho_i/(3\mathcal H^2)$}

For physical density $\bar\rho_i$ and <conformal time>, $\mathcal H=a'/a=aH$. Substitution into the definition $\Omega_i=8\pi G\bar\rho_i/(3H^2)$ gives the displayed formula. Omitting $a^2$ gives $\Omega_i/a^2$, which need not equal one even in a spatially flat matter-only universe. In particular, the conformal growth source $\tfrac32\mathcal H^2\Omega_m$ equals $4\pi G a^2\bar\rho_m$ with the physical density parameter.

= Cosmic baryon fraction
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{title2=$f_b=\Omega_b/\Omega_m$}

The <cosmic baryon fraction> is the ratio of mean baryonic matter density to total matter density. Assigning this fraction to a halo is a simplifying assumption; cooling, ejection and other baryonic processes can change the halo's retained gas and stellar fractions. A baryon conversion efficiency becomes a stellar-to-total-halo mass ratio only after multiplying by the applicable baryon fraction.

= Cluster baryon-fraction estimate of matter density
{parent=Cosmic baryon fraction}
{title2=$\Omega_m=\Omega_b/f_{b,\rm cluster}$}

If a measured cluster baryon fraction represents the <cosmic baryon fraction> $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$, combining it with the <cosmological baryon density> inferred from <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> gives the displayed estimate. Gas and stellar baryons must both be counted, and all distance or <Hubble constant> dependence must be consistent. Baryon depletion or feedback modifies the identification: with $f_{b,\rm cluster}=b\Omega_b/\Omega_m$, the corrected answer is $\Omega_m=b\Omega_b/f_{b,\rm cluster}$. The method measures total matter rather than vacuum or radiation density.

= Cosmological density parameter flow
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{title2=$d\Omega/d\log a$}

For an expanding <Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric> with positive <energy density>, put $\Omega=8\pi G\rho/(3H^2)$ and $w=P/\rho$, in units $c=1$. The <Friedmann equation>, <Friedmann acceleration equation> and <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation> give
$$
\frac{\dot H}{H^2}=-1-\frac12(1+3w)\Omega,\qquad
\frac{d\Omega}{d\log a}=(1+3w)\Omega(\Omega-1).
$$
For constant $w$, linearizing at $\Omega=1$ gives $\Omega-1\propto a^{1+3w}$. For variable $w$, replace the power by $\exp\int(1+3w(a))\,d\log a$. This describes the <flatness problem> for decelerating expansion, away from epochs at which $H=0$.

= Matter density flow with a cosmological constant
{parent=Cosmological density parameter flow}
{title2=$\Omega_m'=\mathcal H\Omega_m(\Omega_m-1-2\Omega_\Lambda)$}

For separately conserved <pressureless matter>, $\rho_m\propto a^{-3}$. The <deceleration parameter> identity $\dot H/H^2=-(1+q)$ and $\Omega_m\propto\rho_m/H^2$ give $\dot\Omega_m=H\Omega_m(2q-1)$. A dust-plus-vacuum universe has $q=\Omega_m/2-\Omega_\Lambda$; conversion to conformal time yields the displayed flow. The familiar matter-only equation requires negligible <cosmological constant>. In a spatially flat dust-plus-vacuum universe, $\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda=1$ and $\Omega_m'=-3\mathcal H\Omega_m\Omega_\Lambda$, so flatness does not require $\Omega_m=1$.

= Flatness problem
{parent=Cosmological density parameter}
{wiki}

In a decelerating expanding universe, $|\Omega-1|$ grows, so its small present value requires extremely fine-tuned early initial data unless an earlier mechanism drives $\Omega$ toward one.

= Inflationary solution of the flatness problem
{parent=Flatness problem}

The continuity equation gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(\rho a^2)=-Ha^2(\rho+3P).
$$
During expansion with $\rho+3P<0$, the product $\rho a^2$ grows and $|\Omega^{-1}-1|\propto(\rho a^2)^{-1}$ decreases.

= Flatness bound on the initial field in exponential inflation
{parent=Inflationary solution of the flatness problem}
{title2=$\phi_i\le\phi_R-\frac{Am_{\rm pl}}{8\pi}\frac{\log(a_RH_R/a_0H_0)}{1-A^2/(16\pi)}$}

For positive slope parameter $A$, the exponential-potential field increases during <cosmic inflation>. The quantity $aH$ grows by $e^{(1-\epsilon_H)N}$ with constant $\epsilon_H=A^2/(16\pi)$ and $N=8\pi(\phi_R-\phi_i)/(Am_{\rm pl})$. Starting from order-one <curvature> and demanding order-one or smaller present <curvature> gives the displayed upper bound on the initial field. A specified tighter final <curvature> tolerance adds one-half its logarithm to the numerator. Thus sufficient <cosmic inflation> sets an upper, not a lower, initial-field bound for this sign of the potential slope. Instantaneous <reheating> and subsequent entropy conservation determine $a_RH_R/a_0H_0$.

= Horizon problem
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

In a decelerating hot Big Bang model, widely separated regions of the observable universe have nearly equal conditions despite having no shared past light cone. Inflation supplies an earlier era with a shrinking comoving Hubble radius, allowing a formerly causal patch to grow beyond the later horizon.

= Particle horizon
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

The particle horizon at cosmic time $t$ is the greatest comoving distance from which a light signal could have reached an observer since the initial time:
$$
\chi_{\rm ph}(t)=\int_{t_{\rm initial}}^t\frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')}.
$$
Regions separated by more than twice this distance cannot have exchanged signals by time $t$.

= Particle horizon in a power-law universe
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$R(t)=At^p,\quad D_p(t)=ct/(1-p),\quad0<p<1$}

The radial null integral from the <Big Bang> is $\chi_p=c\int_0^tdt'/R(t')=ct^{1-p}/[A(1-p)]$. Multiplication by the <scale factor> gives the proper radial horizon distance. It is $3ct$ for a flat matter-only model and $2ct$ for a flat radiation-only model. A finite initial <time> different from zero or an earlier expansion era changes the result.

= Angular particle-horizon size in a power-law universe
{parent=Particle horizon in a power-law universe}
{title2=$\theta_p=r^{1-p}/(1-r^{1-p}),\quad r=t_e/t_0$}

Divide the <particle horizon in a power-law universe> at emission by the <angular diameter distance in a flat power-law universe>. The result is the small angular extent of one radial horizon <length>. Matter-only and radiation-only histories have powers $1/3$ and $1/2$, respectively. These are idealized single-era histories; a real <recombination> horizon and distance depend on different portions of the expansion history.

= Particle-horizon radius versus patch diameter
{parent=Angular particle-horizon size in a power-law universe}

The proper radial <particle horizon> is the distance travelled by a null <signal> since the initial boundary, measured on the current <time> slice. A spherical patch with that radius has twice that physical diameter. Dividing one or the other by an <angular diameter distance> therefore differs by a factor of two. A question using horizon size should state which linear extent is intended.

= Total comoving visibility radius
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$\chi_{\rm max}=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}c\,dt/a(t)$}

In a spatially flat <FLRW metric>, the supremum of the <comoving radial distance> from which a signal emitted at the initial time can eventually reach a fixed comoving observer is the total <conformal time> interval. At an intermediate time $t$, this equals its <comoving particle horizon> plus the comoving radius of its <cosmological event horizon>, when both integrals converge. This is a visibility bound for emission arbitrarily close to the initial boundary; it does not assert that a transparent universe or a source existed there.

= Total conformal lifetime of a flat matter-Lambda universe
{parent=Total comoving visibility radius}
{title2=$\chi_{\rm max}=\frac{\Omega_m^{-1/3}\Omega_\Lambda^{-1/6}}{3H_0}B(1/3,1/6)$}

Use $c=1$, $a_0=1$, positive <pressureless matter> and positive <cosmological constant>, with $\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda=1$ and no radiation. The <Friedmann equation> gives $\chi_{\rm max}=H_0^{-1}\int_0^\infty dy/(\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_my^3)^{1/2}$. Set $y=(\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m)^{1/3}u$, then $v=u^3$. The remaining integral is $\tfrac13\int_0^\infty v^{-2/3}(1+v)^{-1/2}\,dv=\tfrac13B(1/3,1/6)$, proving the formula by the <beta function> integral. Its two endpoints converge for positive densities, but the lifetime diverges in either pure-fluid limit.

= Comoving particle horizon
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$r_p(t)=c\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{a(t')}$}

The comoving particle horizon is the <particle horizon> expressed in <comoving coordinates>, assuming a finite initial cosmic-time boundary at zero. In a spatially flat <FLRW metric>, it is the distance a photon could have travelled since that boundary. The corresponding <proper distance in cosmology> at time $t$ is $a(t)r_p(t)$. It differs from the future-directed <cosmological event horizon> and from the instantaneous <comoving Hubble radius>.

= Photon arrival from an Einstein-de Sitter particle horizon
{parent=Comoving particle horizon}
{title2=$t_{\rm arrival}=8t_0$}

For an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> normalized by $a(t_0)=1$, $a=(t/t_0)^{2/3}$. Its current <comoving particle horizon> is $3ct_0$. A photon emitted inward at that location at $t_0$ covers $3ct_0[(t/t_0)^{1/3}-1]$ in comoving distance by time $t$. Equating these distances gives arrival at $8t_0$, a travel time of $7t_0$.

= Particle horizon through radiation-matter equality
{parent=Comoving particle horizon}
{title2=$r_p(a)=\frac{2c}{H_0\Omega_{m,0}}\left(\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}+\Omega_{m,0}a}-\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}\right)$}

Neglecting curvature and <dark energy>, integration of the <Friedmann equation> for <radiation in cosmology> and <pressureless matter> gives the displayed <comoving particle horizon>. At <matter-radiation equality>, $a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r,0}/\Omega_{m,0}$, this is $2(\sqrt2-1)$ times the radiation-only extrapolation $ca_{\rm eq}/(H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}})$. Its associated inverse length differs by an order-one factor from the <matter-radiation equality scale> $a_{\rm eq}H_{\rm eq}/c$.

= Direct cosmological communication and a shared causal past
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$d\le\eta\text{ versus }d\le2\eta$}

In a spatially flat <Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric> with initial <conformal time> zero, two comoving worldlines at separation $d$ can exchange a one-way light signal by time $\eta$ only if $d\le\eta$. Their past light cones overlap if $d\le2\eta$: a common event halfway between them can influence both. These are different statements. For opposite emission events observed at conformal time $\eta_0$, their separation is $2(\eta_0-\eta_e)$, so the respective thresholds are $\eta_e\ge2\eta_0/3$ and $\eta_e\ge\eta_0/2$.

= Open radiation universe particle horizon
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$d_{h0}=\frac{\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt{(1-\Omega_0)/\Omega_0}}{H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_0}}$}

For a radiation-filled expanding universe with $0<\Omega_0<1$ and no <cosmological constant>, set $x=a/a_0$. The <particle horizon> radial proper distance is $H_0^{-1}\int_0^1[\Omega_0+(1-\Omega_0)x^2]^{-1/2}\,dx$. An inverse-hyperbolic-sine antiderivative gives the formula. The spatially flat limit is $d_{h0}=H_0^{-1}$; radial proper distance is distinct from the area radius of the corresponding horizon sphere.

= Horizon angle at matter-radiation equality
{parent=Particle horizon}
{title2=$\theta_{\rm eq}\simeq\tau_{\rm eq}/(\tau_0-\tau_{\rm eq})$}

A horizon-length patch on the <matter-radiation equality> surface has physical size $a_{\rm eq}\tau_{\rm eq}$ in a flat universe with <conformal time> measured from the <Big Bang>. Dividing by its <angular diameter distance> $a_{\rm eq}(\tau_0-\tau_{\rm eq})$ gives its small angular size. For the <radiation-matter scale factor in conformal time>, this is $(\sqrt2-1)\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}/[1-\sqrt{2\Omega_{r,0}}]$. A patch diameter is twice the angle associated with one horizon radius. The <particle horizon> is an integral over past evolution and must not be silently replaced by the instantaneous <Hubble radius>.

= Radiation in cosmology
{parent=Cosmology}

Cosmological radiation has equation of state $P_r=\rho_r/3$ and density scaling $\rho_r\propto a^{-4}$.

= Radiation-dominated universe
{parent=Radiation in cosmology}
{title2=$a(t)\propto t^{1/2}$}

A <radiation-dominated universe> has expansion controlled mainly by <radiation in cosmology>. When spatial curvature and the <cosmological constant> are negligible, the <Friedmann equation> and density scaling $\rho\propto a^{-4}$ give the displayed <scale factor>, with <Hubble parameter> $H=1/(2t)$.

= Comoving entropy conservation
{parent=Radiation-dominated universe}
{title2=$g_{*s}T^3a^3=\mathrm{constant}$}

Adiabatic expansion conserves <entropy> in a comoving volume: $s a^3$ is constant. With $s=(2\pi^2/45)g_{*s}T^3$, it gives $(a_i/a_0)^3=(g_{*s,0}/g_{*s,i})(T_0/T_i)^3$. Changes in <entropy> <degrees of freedom> must therefore be retained when transporting an early relic density to today, rather than setting $aT$ constant across all particle thresholds.

= Dark energy
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

Dark energy is a smooth component with sufficiently negative pressure to drive accelerated cosmic expansion. The <supernova discovery of accelerating expansion> measures an expansion history through <luminosity distance>; interpreting that history as a component of the stress tensor additionally assumes <general relativity>. A <cosmological constant> is one possibility, whereas <dynamical dark energy> permits a changing <equation-of-state parameter>.

= Dark-energy acceleration criterion
{parent=Dark energy}
{title2=$q_0=\tfrac12\Omega_{m,0}+\tfrac12(1+3w_0)\Omega_{{\rm DE},0}$}

Neglect <radiation in cosmology> and take <pressureless matter> plus a separately conserved <dark energy> component with $p_{\rm DE}=w\rho_{\rm DE}c^2$, where $\rho$ here denotes mass-equivalent <energy density>. The <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives
$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left[\rho_m+\rho_{\rm DE}(1+3w)\right].
$$
Thus $w<-1/3$ for a positive-density component is necessary for that component to accelerate expansion, but the total must also overcome <pressureless matter>. For a <cosmological constant>, $w=-1$ and $q_0=\Omega_{m,0}/2-\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$, so acceleration requires $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}>\Omega_{m,0}/2$. <Spatial curvature of an FLRW universe> enters the distance relation but has no separate term in this acceleration criterion. A <Hubble diagram> alone does not identify a microscopic <dark energy> mechanism.

= Negative-w barotropic-fluid gradient instability
{parent=Dark energy}
{title2=$c_s^2=w<0$}

A propagating adiabatic fluid with the constant closure $p=w\rho$ has $c_s^2=dp/d\rho=w$. For $-1<w<0$, a short-wavelength Fourier perturbation obeys $\omega^2\simeq wk_{\rm phys}^2<0$, producing gradient growth. This does not exclude a negative-pressure perfect-fluid stress tensor with nonadiabatic pressure response, or a canonical scalar with rest-frame <sound speed> one. A <cosmological constant> has no independent propagating fluid density mode.

= Cosmological constant energy
{synonym}

The cosmological constant $\Lambda$ is the constant term proportional to the metric in the <Einstein field equations>. Interpreted as vacuum energy, it has $P_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda$, so its energy density is constant during expansion.

= Cosmological-constant domination
{parent=Cosmological constant}
{title2=$H^2\simeq\Lambda c^2/3$}

An expanding-universe regime in which a positive <cosmological constant> supplies most of the <Friedmann equation>. The approximately constant positive expansion rate gives exponential growth of the <scale factor>. Since matter, <radiation>, and curvature contributions decay with expansion, a positive <cosmological constant> eventually dominates a perpetually expanding model containing them; this does not guarantee that curvature dominates at an intermediate time.

= Radiation-vacuum density equality
{parent=Cosmological constant}
{title2=$1+z_{r\Lambda}=(\Omega_{\Lambda0}/\Omega_{r0})^{1/4}$}

The equality of a radiation density proportional to $(1+z)^4$ and a constant vacuum density. It is only a crossing of two components. Matter can dominate both at this epoch, so radiation-vacuum equality need not mark a change in the universe's dominant component.

= Matter-vacuum density equality
{parent=Cosmological constant}
{title2=$1+z_{m\Lambda}=(\Omega_{\Lambda0}/\Omega_{m0})^{1/3}$}

For conserved <pressureless matter> and a true <cosmological constant>, equality follows by comparing the matter density proportional to $(1+z)^3$ with the constant vacuum density. This is not the onset of accelerated expansion: in a matter-plus-vacuum model acceleration starts at $\rho_m=2\rho_\Lambda$.

= Time-unit convention for the cosmological constant
{parent=Cosmological constant}

In a <Friedmann equation> written $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3+\Lambda/3-kc^2/a^2$, the symbol $\Lambda$ has units of inverse time squared. If $\Lambda_{\rm geom}$ is the geometrical constant measured in inverse length squared, $\Lambda=c^2\Lambda_{\rm geom}$. <Natural units> hide this distinction.

= Cosmic coincidence problem
{parent=Cosmological constant}

Nonrelativistic matter dilutes as $a^{-3}$, while a <cosmological constant> has constant <energy density>. The ratio of <vacuum energy> density to <matter density> consequently scales as $a^3$, so comparable contributions occur during a limited epoch. The <cosmic coincidence problem> asks why observers find these very different components comparable near their own epoch. This timing puzzle is distinct from the <cosmological constant problem>, which concerns the tiny absolute vacuum-energy scale.

= Cosmological constant problem
{parent=Cosmological constant}
{wiki}

The <cosmological constant problem> is the unexplained smallness of the gravitationally observable <vacuum energy> compared with the contributions expected from <quantum field theory>. In units with $\hbar=c=1$, an <ultraviolet cutoff> $\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}$ generically gives contributions of order $\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}^4$, whereas the observed dark-energy density is of order $10^{-47}\,\mathrm{GeV}^4$. The <Einstein equation> depends on the sum of a bare <cosmological constant> and these contributions; subtracting a constant from a nongravitational <Hamiltonian> does not remove its gravitational effect. Exact global <supersymmetry> gives zero energy to a <supersymmetric vacuum>, but <supersymmetry breaking> generally reintroduces a much larger positive scale.

= Electroweak-scale vacuum-energy naturalness estimate
{parent=Cosmological constant problem}

A heavy field with mass $m$ introduces a natural vacuum-energy threshold scale of order $m^4$ up to couplings and loop factors. This is not a regulator-independent prediction: counterterms and renormalization are required. Comparing an electroweak mass to the observed vacuum density exposes a very large cancellation problem.

= Vacuum energy density versus geometric cosmological constant
{parent=Cosmological constant problem}

In $c=\hbar=1$ units, vacuum energy density has mass dimension four, Newton constant dimension minus two, and $\Lambda_{\rm geom}=8\pi G\rho_{\rm vac}$ dimension two. A value quoted in $\mathrm{GeV^4}$ is an energy density, not the geometric cosmological constant.

{parent=Cosmology}

The matter density $\rho_m$ is the <energy density> in nonrelativistic matter. In a homogeneous expanding universe, conservation of particle number and dilution of physical volume give $\rho_m\propto a^{-3}$.

When the effective recombination rate per <electron> becomes smaller than the expansion rate, the small remaining ionized fraction can no longer track <chemical equilibrium>. = Hydrogen ionization fraction

In hydrogen-only cosmology, charge neutrality gives $n_e=n_p$ and baryon conservation gives $n_b=n_p+n_H$. The fraction $X_e$ lies between zero and one, with $n_H=(1-X_e)n_b$. With helium present, <electron> abundance per <hydrogen> nucleus and per total baryon are different conventions and must be specified. The <hydrogen-only Saha equation> gives the equilibrium fraction.

= Photon decoupling

Photon decoupling occurs when the Thomson-scattering rate drops below the Hubble expansion rate. Photons then free stream and form the cosmic microwave background.

The physical <Thomson scattering> rate equals the inverse <photon> mean scattering time. Comparing it with the <Hubble parameter> gives a local approximate decoupling criterion. = Saha-Thomson decoupling estimate
{c}
{parent=Thomson decoupling rate criterion}
{title2=$X_en_H\sigma_Tc\sim H$}

Combining the <hydrogen-only Saha equation> with the <Thomson decoupling rate criterion> gives an elementary estimate of <photon decoupling>. With present <baryon> density about $0.25\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, <hydrogen> fraction about $0.75$, present <photon> <temperature> $2.725\ \mathrm K$ and a matter-era normalization $H_0\sqrt{\Omega_m}\simeq38\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$, the coupled estimate gives a <temperature> near $3100\ \mathrm K$. <Hydrogen> chemical equilibrium fails during the actual kinetic recombination history, and the local scattering-rate criterion differs from the maximum of the visibility function; the estimate is not a precision prediction.

= Recombination temperature
{title2=$T_{\rm rec}$}
{parent=Recombination (cosmology)}

The recombination temperature is the photon temperature at which the free-electron fraction falls sharply as neutral atoms form. For hydrogen it is about $0.3\,\mathrm{eV}$ because the photon-to-baryon ratio is very large.

= Small baryon abundance delays hydrogen recombination
{parent=Recombination temperature}
{title2=$B/T\simeq\log[\eta^{-1}(m_e/T)^{3/2}]$}

For <hydrogen ionization fraction> $X_e$ of order one, the <hydrogen-only Saha equation> gives $1\sim n_b(2\pi/(m_eT))^{3/2}e^{B/T}$. The <baryon-to-photon ratio> and <photon number density> imply $n_b\sim\eta T^3$. Therefore $1\sim\eta(T/m_e)^{3/2}e^{B/T}$ and the displayed logarithmic balance follows. A small $\eta$ supplies many ionizing <photons> per <baryon>, so neutral <hydrogen> forms at a <temperature> much smaller than its <hydrogen binding energy>. The numerical temperature depends on the ionization-fraction convention and on whether equilibrium remains valid.

= Saha half-ionization temperature
{c}
{parent=Recombination temperature}
{title2=$y-\tfrac32\ln y=\ln[2/(3\times10^{-16})]$}

With the rounded <hydrogen> <Saha equation> coefficient, the condition $X_e=1/2$ gives the displayed equation for $y=\mathcal B/T$. Its low-temperature root is about $42$, hence $T\simeq0.32$ eV for <hydrogen> binding energy $13.6$ eV. Dropping the slowly varying logarithm gives the rough leading-log estimate $T\sim0.4$ eV. = Saha neutral fraction from a measured neutral density

= Electron fugacity in the Saha equation

= Flat matter-radiation conformal solution

{title2=$a(\eta)=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{R,0}}\eta+\Omega_{M,0}H_0^2\eta^2/4$}

For a spatially flat <FLRW metric> containing conserved <pressureless matter> and <radiation in cosmology>, with $a_0=1$ and <conformal time> zero at the <Big Bang>, the <Friedmann equation> gives $a'^2=H_0^2(\Omega_{M,0}a+\Omega_{R,0})$. Separating the positive square root gives the displayed <scale factor>. = Cosmological bounce
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$\dot a(t_b)=0,\quad\ddot a(t_b)>0$}

A smooth <cosmological bounce> is a positive minimum of the <scale factor>: contraction changes to expansion, with $a(t_b)>0$, $\dot a(t_b)=0$ and $\ddot a(t_b)>0$ in the nondegenerate case. The <closed slicing of de Sitter spacetime> gives $a=H_\Lambda^{-1}\cosh[H_\Lambda(t-t_b)]$, whose minimum is $H_\Lambda^{-1}$. The <Friedmann acceleration equation> with zero separate <cosmological constant> requires $\varrho+3p<0$ there.

= Vacuum-energy FLRW solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$\dot a^2+k=H_\Lambda^2a^2$}

The <equation of state> $p=-\varrho$ makes the <cosmological continuity equation> imply constant <energy density> $\varrho_0$. For $\varrho_0>0$, put $H_\Lambda^2=8\pi G\varrho_0/3$. Solving both the <Friedmann equation> and <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives
$$
a=Ae^{H_\Lambda t}+Be^{-H_\Lambda t},\qquad 4H_\Lambda^2AB=k.
$$
Up to shifts of <cosmic time> and time reversal, the positive <scale factors> are $Ae^{H_\Lambda t}$ for $k=0$, $H_\Lambda^{-1}\cosh(H_\Lambda t)$ for $k=1$, and $H_\Lambda^{-1}\sinh(H_\Lambda t)$ on $t>0$ for $k=-1$. They are the flat, closed and open slicings of <de Sitter spacetime>. The open zero is a coordinate boundary with finite <Ricci scalar> $12H_\Lambda^2$ and finite <Kretschmann scalar> $24H_\Lambda^4$.

For the expanding flat branch, the <cosmological event horizon> has <comoving radial distance> $\int_t^\infty ds/a(s)=1/[H_\Lambda a(t)]$ and <proper distance in cosmology> $H_\Lambda^{-1}$. An event on the horizon reaches the central <comoving observer> only at infinite reception time; events beyond it never do.

= Flat barotropic matter-vacuum solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$H=H_\Lambda\coth[3(1+w)H_\Lambda t/2]$}

For positive constant <cosmological constant>, zero <cosmological spatial curvature> and a separate positive-density fluid with constant <equation-of-state parameter> $w>-1$, set $H_\Lambda=\sqrt{\Lambda/3}$ in the time-unit convention. Eliminating the fluid <density> gives $\dot H=-3(1+w)(H^2-H_\Lambda^2)/2$. On the expanding <Big Bang> branch,
$$
H=H_\Lambda\coth\left[\frac32(1+w)H_\Lambda t\right],\qquad
a\propto\sinh^{2/[3(1+w)]}\left[\frac32(1+w)H_\Lambda t\right].
$$
The origin is the <Big Bang>. The fluid's early-time power-law expansion approaches exponential vacuum domination at late times.

= Barotropic matter-vacuum acceleration transition
{parent=Flat barotropic matter-vacuum solution}
{title2=$(1+z_{\rm tr})^{3(1+w)}=2\Omega_{\Lambda0}/[(1+3w)\Omega_{m0}]$}

For a constant-$w$ material fluid and positive vacuum <energy>, the <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives $\ddot a/(aH_0^2)=\Omega_{\Lambda0}-(1+3w)\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^{3(1+w)}/2$. If $w>-1/3$, its zero determines the displayed transition. A past transition additionally requires the right-hand side of the displayed equation to exceed one. The equation does not imply the same transition for a fluid that already has sufficiently negative <pressure>.

= Age of a flat barotropic matter-vacuum universe

The <flat barotropic matter-vacuum solution> has $H_\Lambda/H_0=\sqrt{\Omega_{\Lambda0}}$ and therefore the displayed age, measured from its <Big Bang>. At fixed $H_0$ and <density> fractions it is $1/(1+w)$ times the dust-plus-vacuum age. Its $w=0$ case is the <age of a flat matter-Lambda universe>.

= Friedmann effective potential for matter, radiation and vacuum
{c}
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$V(a)=-M/a-Q/a^2-La^2$}

With nonnegative conserved <pressureless matter> and <radiation in cosmology>, set $M=(8\pi G/3)\rho_m a^3$, $Q=(8\pi G/3)\rho_r a^4$, and $L=(8\pi G/3)\rho_\Lambda$. The <Friedmann equation> becomes $(da/dt)^2+V(a)=-k$. For negative vacuum density the potential rises strictly from negative infinity to positive infinity, so an expanding branch has one maximum scale factor. For positive vacuum density it has one maximum: energy above that maximum gives monotonic expansion or contraction, energy below it gives disjoint recollapsing and bouncing branches, and equality admits an <Einstein static universe> together with asymptotic monotonic branches.

= Negative-vacuum Friedmann lifetime bound
{parent=Friedmann effective potential for matter, radiation and vacuum}
{title2=$t_{\rm life}\leq\pi\sqrt{3/(8\pi G|\rho_\Lambda|)}$}

For a <FRW metric> with positive <scale factor>, nonnegative dust and radiation, and negative vacuum density, the <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives $a_{tt}\leq-\omega^2a$, with $\omega^2=8\pi G|\rho_\Lambda|/3$. At maximum expansion, $a(0)=a_{\max}$ and $a_t(0)=0$. The forced-oscillator integral formula gives $a(t)\leq a_{\max}\cos(\omega t)$ until the first zero, since its forcing is nonpositive and its sine kernel is nonnegative for $0\leq t\leq\pi/(2\omega)$. Thus each half of the expansion-recollapse history lasts at most $\pi/(2\omega)$. Nonzero dust or radiation makes the endpoint a physical density singularity; the matter-free limit instead bounds the open anti-de Sitter coordinate patch.

= Constant-equation-of-state conformal Riccati equation
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$\mathcal H'+\alpha(\mathcal H^2+k)=0,\quad\alpha=(1+3w)/2$}

For a <perfect fluid in general relativity> with constant <equation-of-state parameter> $w$, use <conformal time> $dt=a\,d\tau$ and the <conformal Hubble parameter> $\mathcal H=a'/a=aH$. The <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives $\mathcal H'=a\ddot a=-4\pi Ga^2(1+3w)\rho/3$. The <Friedmann equation> eliminates $\rho$ and gives this <Riccati equation>. For positive <energy density>, $\alpha\ne0$ and appropriately shifted <conformal time>, its usual branches are $\cot(\alpha\tau)$ for $k=1$, $1/(\alpha\tau)$ for $k=0$, and $\coth(\alpha\tau)$ for $k=-1$. Their <scale factors> are proportional to $\sin^{1/\alpha}(\alpha\tau)$, $(\alpha\tau)^{1/\alpha}$ and $\sinh^{1/\alpha}(\alpha\tau)$ respectively on intervals where the bases are positive. At $\alpha=0$ the <conformal Hubble parameter> is constant and the separate solution is $a=Ae^{h\tau}$.

= Flat dust universe with a cosmological constant
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a\propto\sinh^{2/3}(\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t/2)$}

For <pressureless matter>, put $C=8\pi G\rho_m a^3/3>0$ and use units $c=1$. The flat <Friedmann equation> is $\dot a^2=C/a+\Lambda a^2/3$. Substitution $u=a^{3/2}$ gives $\dot u^2=(9/4)(C+\Lambda u^2/3)$. With a <Big Bang> at $t=0$, positive <cosmological constant> gives $a=(3C/\Lambda)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}(\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t/2)$. Negative <cosmological constant> replaces the hyperbolic sine by $\sin(\sqrt{3|\Lambda|}\,t/2)$ and produces recollapse at $2\pi/\sqrt{3|\Lambda|}$. Zero <cosmological constant> gives $a=(9C/4)^{1/3}t^{2/3}$.

= Minimum expansion speed in a matter-Lambda universe
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a_{\min}=a_0(\Omega_{m,0}/2\Omega_{\Lambda,0})^{1/3}$}

For pressureless matter and a positive <cosmological constant>, the <Friedmann equation> gives $\dot a^2=H_0^2[\Omega_{m,0}a_0^3/a+\Omega_{k,0}a_0^2+\Omega_{\Lambda,0}a^2]$. Differentiating with respect to $a$ gives the displayed unique minimum of this expression for positive matter density. It is a minimum of the actual expanding solution's speed only if that branch reaches it; sufficient positive spatial curvature can cause recollapse first. At the minimum, $\rho_m=2\rho_\Lambda$ and the <Friedmann acceleration equation> changes sign.

= Closed constant-equation-of-state Friedmann scale factor
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a=\alpha[\sin(q(\tau-\tau_0))]^{1/q}$}

For a closed <Friedmann equation> normalized to $kc^2=1$, constant equation-of-state parameter $w$, and <conformal time> $\tau$, let $q=(1+3w)/2$. The <cosmological continuity equation> and <Friedmann acceleration equation> give $\mathcal H'=-q(1+\mathcal H^2)$, where $\mathcal H=a'/a$. For $q\ne0$ this integrates to the stated scale factor on any interval where the sine is positive, with $\alpha^{2q}=8\pi G\rho_0/3$. For $w=-1/3$ the separate solution is $a=Ae^{H_0\tau}$ with $H_0^2+1=8\pi G\rho_0/3$; the sine-power formula is inapplicable.

= Flat dust-and-vacuum expansion
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a\propto\sinh^{2/3}(3H_\Lambda t/2)$}

For pressureless matter and a positive <cosmological constant> in a spatially flat universe, $H^2=H_0^2[\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+1-\Omega_{m0}]$. Substituting $y=a^{3/2}$ reduces the expanding branch to $\dot y=(3H_0/2)\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}+(1-\Omega_{m0})y^2}$. With $a(0)=0$, integration gives $a=[\Omega_{m0}/(1-\Omega_{m0})]^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}(3H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_{m0}}\,t/2)$. Its matter-dominated early limit is $t^{2/3}$ and its vacuum-dominated late limit is exponential. Radiation must be restored to describe the actual earliest universe.

= Closed radiation-dominated Friedmann solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a=A\sin\tau,\quad t=A(1-\cos\tau)$}

With positive curvature, zero <cosmological constant> and radiation density $\rho_0a^{-4}$ in units $8\pi G=1$, $A=\sqrt{\rho_0/3}$. The <scale factor> is positive for $0<\tau<\pi$, reaches its maximum at $\pi/2$, and has a <Big Crunch> at <conformal time> $\pi$ or <proper time> $2A$. This complements the <closed matter-dominated Friedmann solution>.

= Friedmann effective potential for a constant-equation-of-state fluid

= Friedmann mechanical analogy
{c}
{synonym}

For a <perfect fluid in general relativity> of constant <equation of state> $P=w\rho$, conservation gives $\rho=\rho_0a^{-3(1+w)}$. In units $8\pi G=1$, the <Friedmann equation> becomes $\dot a^2/2+V(a)=0$. Allowed scales have $V\leq0$, and the <Friedmann acceleration equation> is $\ddot a=-V'(a)$. The curvature term shifts the potential vertically; positive <cosmological constant> bends it downward and negative <cosmological constant> bends it upward.

= Radiation-matter scale factor in conformal time
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a(\tau)=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r,0}}\tau+\tfrac14H_0^2\Omega_{m,0}\tau^2$}

In a flat universe containing separately conserved <radiation in cosmology> and <pressureless matter>, the <Friedmann equation> becomes $(a')^2=H_0^2(\Omega_{r,0}+\Omega_{m,0}a)$ in <conformal time>. Setting $a=0$ at $\tau=0$ gives the stated quadratic-plus-linear <scale factor>. The linear term controls <radiation domination> and the quadratic term controls <matter domination>. The formula presumes $a_0=1$, $\Omega_{r,0}+\Omega_{m,0}=1$ and no <cosmological constant>.

= Flat matter-coasting-fluid Friedmann solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a=\frac{\Omega_m}{2(1-\Omega_m)}[\cosh(\alpha\tau)-1]$}

A flat universe with separately conserved <pressureless matter> and a <coasting fluid> satisfies $a'^2=H_0^2[\Omega_m a+(1-\Omega_m)a^2]$. For both densities positive, choosing the <Big Bang> at zero <conformal time> gives $a=\Omega_m[\cosh(\alpha\tau)-1]/[2(1-\Omega_m)]$, with $\alpha=H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_m}$. Physical time follows by integrating $dt=a\,d\tau$. The hyperbolic parametrization is formally like the open dust solution, but the spatial geometry here is flat.

= Conformal Riccati equation for matter and a coasting fluid
{parent=Flat matter-coasting-fluid Friedmann solution}
{title2=$2\mathcal H'+\mathcal H^2=H_0^2(1-\Omega_m)$}

The conformal <Friedmann equation> is $\mathcal H^2=H_0^2[\Omega_m/a+1-\Omega_m]$. Differentiating and using $a'=a\mathcal H$ eliminates the matter term and yields a first-order <Riccati equation>. The expanding Big-Bang branch for $0<\Omega_m<1$ has $\mathcal H=\alpha\coth(\alpha\tau/2)$.

= Open matter-dominated Friedmann solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a\propto\cosh\eta-1,\quad t\propto\sinh\eta-\eta$}

A negatively curved universe with <pressureless matter> and no dark energy expands forever. With $a_0=1$ and $0<\Omega_{m,0}<1$, its <scale factor> is $a=\Omega_{m,0}(\cosh\eta-1)/[2(1-\Omega_{m,0})]$ and its <cosmic time> is $t=\Omega_{m,0}(\sinh\eta-\eta)/[2H_0(1-\Omega_{m,0})^{3/2}]$. The parameter is <conformal time> scaled by $\sqrt{-K}$ and measured from the <Big Bang>.

= Friedmann effective potential for pressureless matter
{title2=$V(a)=-\mu/a-\Lambda a^2/3$}

{parent=Friedmann equations}

For pressureless matter normalized by $\rho=3\mu/(8\pi a^3)$ in units $G=c=1$, the <Friedmann equation> is
$$
\dot a^2+V(a)=-k,
\qquad
V(a)=-\frac{\mu}{a}-\frac{\Lambda a^2}{3}.
$$
Allowed scale factors satisfy $V(a)\leq-k$, and equality marks a <turning point>.

= Critical closed matter-cosmological-constant universe
{parent=Friedmann effective potential for pressureless matter}

For positive spatial curvature and positive <cosmological constant>, the effective potential has its maximum at
$$
a_E=\left(\frac{3\mu}{2\Lambda}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
The critical case $V(a_E)=-1$ has
$$
\Lambda=\frac{4}{9\mu^2},
\qquad
a_E=\frac{3\mu}{2}=\Lambda^{-1/2},
$$
and contains the <Einstein static universe>.

= Friedmann bounce for pressureless matter and positive cosmological constant
{c}
{parent=Friedmann effective potential for pressureless matter}

When the energy line $-k$ lies below the maximum of the pressureless-matter effective potential, its large-$a$ allowed region is separated from the Big-Bang region. A contracting large universe reaches the outer turning point at positive scale factor and re-expands, producing a nonsingular bounce.

= Matter bound from a Friedmann bounce
{parent=Friedmann bounce for pressureless matter and positive cosmological constant}
{title2=$\Omega_m\leq2/[z_*^2(z_*+3)]$}

In a dust-plus-cosmological-constant universe with an <FRW metric> with $a_0=1$, a nondegenerate minimum at $a_*<1$ has $H(a_*)=0$ and $\ddot a(a_*)>0$. Combining these conditions with $\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_k=1$ gives the strict version of the displayed bound; equality is the limiting stationary case. A source at <cosmological redshift> $z$ requires $z_*=a_*^{-1}-1\geq z$, so a high-redshift source bounds the allowed present <matter density parameter> even without directly observing the bounce.

= Newtonian derivation of the flat Friedmann equation
{c}
{parent=Friedmann equations}

For a test mass on the surface of a uniform sphere of radius $R$ and mass $M=4\pi\rho R^3/3$, zero Newtonian specific energy gives
$$
\frac12\dot R^2-\frac{GM}{R}=0.
$$
With $R\propto a$, this becomes the spatially flat <Friedmann equation> $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3$.

= First integral of the Friedmann acceleration equation
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{c}

Combining the perfect-fluid continuity equation with
$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}(\rho+3P)
$$
shows that
$$
\frac{8\pi G}{3c^2}\rho a^2-\dot a^2
$$
is constant in time. This constant is the spatial-curvature integration constant in the first Friedmann equation.

= Friedmann acceleration equation
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{c}

= Raychaudhuri equation
{c}
{synonym}

For energy density $\rho$, pressure $P$, and cosmological constant $\Lambda$,

\frac{\ddot a}{a}
=-\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}(\rho+3P)
+\frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}.
$$

= Newtonian fluid derivation of the Raychaudhuri equation
{c}
{parent=Friedmann acceleration equation}

For a homogeneous pressureless fluid in comoving coordinates, the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>[Euler equation] gives
$$
\nabla\bar\Phi=-a\ddot a\,\mathbf x,
$$
while the <Poisson equation> gives
$$
\nabla^2\bar\Phi=\frac{4\pi G}{c^2}\bar\rho a^2.
$$
Taking the <divergence> of the first relation and comparing them yields
$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3c^2}\bar\rho.
$$
This local fluid derivation describes a homogeneous infinite universe without choosing a physical centre of expansion.

= Recollapse of a closed Friedmann universe with nonnegative pressure
{parent=Friedmann acceleration equation}

For positive spatial curvature, zero cosmological constant, and $\rho,P\geq0$, continuity makes $\rho a^3$ nonincreasing during expansion. The negative curvature term then prevents unbounded growth of $a$, while the acceleration equation forces $\dot a$ to reach zero in finite time.

= Strong energy condition in a Friedmann universe
{parent=Friedmann acceleration equation}

With zero cosmological constant, the condition $\rho+3P\geq0$ implies $\ddot a\leq0$ and
$$
\dot H\leq-H^2,
\qquad
\frac d{dt}(H^{-1})\geq1
$$
wherever $H\ne0$.

= Concavity bound on past FLRW evolution
{parent=Strong energy condition in a Friedmann universe}
{title2=$a(t)\le a(t_0)-\dot a(t_0)(t_0-t)$}

In an <FLRW metric> with zero separate <cosmological constant>, $\varrho+3p\ge0$ makes the <Friedmann acceleration equation> imply $\ddot a\le0$. If $a_0=a(t_0)>0$ and $v_0=\dot a(t_0)>0$, then the <scale factor> is a <concave function> and
$$
a(t)\le a_0-v_0(t_0-t)
$$
throughout its regular past interval. Thus a regular positive <scale factor> cannot persist earlier than $t_0-a_0/v_0$. If the solution continues regularly whenever $a>0$, its first past zero occurs within $a_0/v_0=H(t_0)^{-1}$, where $H$ is the <Hubble parameter>.

The continuation condition matters: without restrictions on the <equation of state>, an earlier <curvature singularity> can occur at positive $a$. For example, in fixed units $a(t)=1+\sqrt t$, $t>0$, with $k=0$, defines <energy density> $\varrho=3(\dot a/a)^2/(8\pi G)$ and <pressure> $p=-\varrho/3-\ddot a/(4\pi Ga)$. These satisfy the <Friedmann equations> and $\varrho+3p>0$, yet $a\to1$ at the singular past boundary. Conversely the <Milne model> reaches zero without divergent <curvature>. A finite duration, a zero of the <scale factor>, and a <curvature singularity> are distinct conclusions.

= Finite-time Friedmann singularity under the strong energy condition
{parent=Strong energy condition in a Friedmann universe}

If $H>0$ at one time, the inequality $d(H^{-1})/dt\geq1$ forces $H\to+\infty$ and $a\to0$ within finite time to the past. If $H<0$, it forces $H\to-\infty$ and $a\to0$ within finite time to the future.

= Radiation-to-cosmological-constant transition
{parent=Friedmann acceleration equation}

In a flat radiation-plus-$\Lambda$ universe,
$$
a(t)=\left(\frac{\Omega_{R0}}{1-\Omega_{R0}}\right)^{1/4}
\left[\sinh\!\left(2H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_{R0}}\,t\right)\right]^{1/2}.
$$
The expansion changes from deceleration to acceleration when the radiation and cosmological-constant terms are equal, at
$$
t_\Lambda=\frac{\operatorname{arsinh}1}
{2H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega_{R0}}}.
$$

= Closed radiation--cosmological-constant turning polynomial
{parent=Friedmann equations}

If the present radiation and cosmological-constant densities are respectively $\beta$ and $1$ times the present critical density, while $a_0=1$, positive curvature gives
$$
H^2=\frac{H_0^2}{a^4}(a^4-\beta a^2+\beta).
$$
Turning points are therefore the positive roots of $x^2-\beta x+\beta$ with $x=a^2$.

= Supercritical closed radiation--cosmological-constant recollapse
{parent=Closed radiation--cosmological-constant turning polynomial}

For $\beta>4$, the turning polynomial has two positive roots and its smaller root lies below $2$. A universe expanding from $a=0$ reaches that root with $H=0$ and $\dot H<0$, then contracts to a finite-time Big Crunch.

= Critical closed radiation--cosmological-constant solution
{parent=Closed radiation--cosmological-constant turning polynomial}

For $\beta=4$ and $a(0)=0$, the expanding branch is
$$
a(t)=\sqrt{2(1-e^{-2H_0t})}.
$$
It grows as $2\sqrt{H_0t}$ near zero and approaches $\sqrt2$ exponentially at late times.

= Closed matter-dominated Friedmann solution
{parent=Friedmann equations}
{title2=$a=A(1-\cos u),\quad t=A(u-\sin u)/\sqrt{k}$}

A positively curved <pressureless matter> universe with zero <cosmological constant> has the <cycloid> parametrization displayed above. In <natural units>, if the <Friedmann equation> is $\dot a^2=C/a-k$ with $C,k>0$, then $A=C/(2k)$ and $u=\sqrt{k}\tau$ for <conformal time> measured from the <Big Bang>. Substituting in $a'^2=Ca-ka^2$ verifies the <scale factor>; integrating $dt=a\,d\tau$ gives <cosmic time>. The universe reaches maximum size at $u=\pi$ and recollapses into a <Big Crunch> at $u=2\pi$.

= Scale factor

= Scale factor
{synonym}

The <scale factor> $a(t)>0$ measures how spatial lengths in an <FLRW metric> change with <cosmic time> $t$. Choose $a$ to be a <dimensionless quantity> with $a(t_0)=1$ at a reference epoch, usually today. For two <comoving observers>, their fixed radial <comoving distance> $\chi$ and their <proper distance in cosmology> $d(t)$ satisfy
$$
d(t)=a(t)\chi,\qquad \chi=\text{constant}.
$$
The <proper distance in cosmology> is measured along a spatial slice at one <cosmic time>; it is not the distance that a travelling <photon> covers between emission and reception. Only ratios of the <scale factor> have a normalization-independent meaning: replacing $a$ by $Ca$ and $\chi$ by $\chi/C$ leaves $d$ unchanged.

For example, take $\chi=100\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, where $\mathrm{Mpc}$ means a million <parsecs>. At $a=0.5,1,1.5$, the same pair of <comoving observers> has <proper distance in cosmology> $50,100,150\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ respectively. Every such separation changes by the same factor, although more distant pairs gain more distance. The illustration treats the marked objects as ideal <comoving observers>; internal motions in <galaxies> need not follow this background scaling.

The beauty of the <scale factor> is that one function describes the changing spatial ruler for every <comoving observer>. <Spatial homogeneity> gives no preferred centre of <cosmic expansion>: in a flat spatial patch, relative positions obey $\mathbf r-\mathbf r_O=a(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_O)$, so recentering on any <comoving observer> preserves <Hubble's law>. The evolution of the <scale factor> is determined by the <Friedmann equations>, while its ratio between emission and reception determines <cosmological redshift>.

= Cosmic expansion

Cosmic expansion is the increase of physical distances between comoving observers as the <scale factor> grows. Its instantaneous fractional rate is the <Hubble parameter>.

= Hubble's law
{c}
{parent=Expansion of the universe}
{wiki}
{title2=$\dot d=H(t)d$}

= Hubble law
{c}
{synonym}

<Hubble's law> states that the instantaneous rate of change of <proper distance in cosmology> between <comoving observers> is proportional to that distance:
$$
v_{\rm rec}(t):=\dot d(t)=H(t)d(t).
$$
This is exact for fixed <comoving distance> $\chi$ in an <FLRW metric>: differentiating $d=a\chi$ gives $\dot d=\dot a\chi=(\dot a/a)d$. The dot uses <cosmic time>, and both distances are compared on spatial slices at the same <cosmic time>. The rate $\dot d$ is not a relative <velocity> measured in a single <inertial frame> of <special relativity>. Thus the instantaneous relation is not an exact observed <redshift>-distance formula. The associated <Hubble flow> describes the ideal expansion; <peculiar velocities> describe departures from it.

For nearby sources, the observed <redshift> approximately satisfies
$$
cz\simeq H_0d
$$
when source and observer <peculiar velocities> are small compared with $H_0d$, additional <gravitational redshift> is negligible, and the changes in the <scale factor> and <Hubble parameter> during light travel are small. Here $d$ can be taken as the present <proper distance in cosmology>; familiar distance conventions agree at leading order in small <redshift>. Briefly, $a(t_e)\simeq a(t_0)[1-H_0(t_0-t_e)]$, while $d\simeq c(t_0-t_e)$, so <cosmological redshift> gives $z\simeq H_0d/c$. At greater <redshift>, use an expansion history and an explicit distance convention instead.

Stipulate the illustrative <Hubble constant> $H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ and <speed of light> $c=299792.458\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. A $\mathrm{Mpc}$ is a million <parsecs>. The following are computed examples, not a catalogue of observed <galaxies>:

* At $d=10\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, $H_0d=700\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and the approximate <cosmological redshift> is $z\simeq0.002335$.
* At $d=100\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, $H_0d=7000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and the approximate <cosmological redshift> is $z\simeq0.02335$.
* At $d=200\,\mathrm{Mpc}$, $H_0d=14000\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and the approximate <cosmological redshift> is $z\simeq0.04670$.

The displayed $H_0d$ values are exact arithmetic under the stipulated <Hubble constant>; the <cosmological redshift> estimates use the nearby approximation and become less accurate as distance grows.

<Hubble's law> turns a nearby observed <redshift> into an approximate distance indicator and makes the uniformity of <cosmic expansion> tangible: twice the <proper distance in cosmology> means twice the instantaneous separation rate. Recentring the ideal <Hubble flow> on another <comoving observer> preserves the same proportionality, as illustrated under <scale factor>, so recession in every direction does not select a central observer.

In his 1929 PNAS paper, Edwin Hubble presented a relation between the distances and <radial velocities> of extragalactic objects. His fits gave coefficients of $465$ and $513\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ for 24 individual objects and nine groups respectively, and he used about $500\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ as a rounded representative value. Those historical fits used the paper's distance estimates; they are distinct from the stipulated modern-scale illustration above. https://www.astro.puc.cl/~rparra/tools/PAPERS/hubble_1929.pdf[Hubble (1929), original PNAS paper, pp. 170-171]{ref}.

= Hubble parameter
{parent=Expansion of the universe}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$H(t)=\dot a(t)/a(t)$}

The <Hubble parameter> is the instantaneous fractional rate of <cosmic expansion>:
$$
H=\frac{\dot a}{a},
$$
where the dot is a <derivative> with respect to <cosmic time>. Thus a short interval $\Delta t$ changes any fixed-comoving <proper distance in cosmology> by the fraction $\Delta d/d\simeq H\Delta t$. Its dimensions are inverse time; $H>0$ describes expansion and $H<0$ contraction. The <Hubble parameter identity> gives $\dot H+H^2=\ddot a/a$.

= Flat dust-vacuum Hubble parameter
{parent=Hubble parameter}
{title2=$H(z)=H_0[\Omega_{m0}(1+z)^3+\Omega_{\Lambda0}]^{1/2}$}

For <pressureless matter>, a positive <cosmological constant>, flat spatial geometry and negligible <radiation>, the <continuity equation> gives $\rho_m\propto a^{-3}$. Substitution in the <Friedmann equation> yields the displayed <Hubble parameter>, with $\Omega_{m0}+\Omega_{\Lambda0}=1$. <radiation> must be restored at early epochs; the formula does not require matter to be the largest present-day density component.

= Local volume Hubble parameter
{parent=Hubble parameter}
{title2=$H_{\mathrm{loc}}=-K/3$}

For the normal flow with zero <shift vector>, $a_{\mathrm{loc}}=(\det h)^{1/6}$ obeys $H_{\mathrm{loc}}=N^{-1}\partial_t\log a_{\mathrm{loc}}=-K/3$ in the negative <extrinsic curvature of a spatial hypersurface> convention. The <expansion scalar> is $\theta=-K=3H_{\mathrm{loc}}$.

= Conformal Hubble parameter
{parent=Hubble parameter}
{title2=$\mathcal H=a'/a=aH$}

For <conformal time> $d\tau=dt/a$, the conformal Hubble parameter is $\mathcal H=a'/a=aH=\dot a$, where primes and dots denote conformal and <cosmic time> derivatives respectively. Its inverse, in units $c=1$, is the <comoving Hubble radius>. It differs from the ordinary <Hubble parameter> by one factor of the <scale factor>.

= Hubble radius
{c}
{parent=Hubble parameter}
{title2=$c/H$}

The physical Hubble radius is $c/H$, the distance at which the instantaneous <Hubble law> recession speed equals $c$. Its comoving counterpart is $c/(aH)$. Neither radius is generally the same as a <particle horizon> or a causal boundary: those depend on integrals of the past or future expansion history.

= Hubble constant
{title2=$H_0$}
{c}
{parent=Hubble parameter}

The <Hubble constant> is the present value $H_0=H(t_0)$ of the <Hubble parameter>. The word "constant" fixes the epoch; it does not mean $H(t)$ stays unchanged throughout <cosmic time>. It sets the present instantaneous proportionality in <Hubble's law> and the approximate nearby observed <redshift>-distance relation.

For illustration, stipulate $H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$; this is an example input, not a claim about a measured consensus value. A million <parsecs> is $3.085677581\ldots\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{km}$, giving
$$
H_0\simeq2.26855\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
H_0^{-1}\simeq4.40811\times10^{17}\,\mathrm s\simeq13.97\,\mathrm{Gyr}.
$$
Here a year means $365.25$ days of $86400\,\mathrm s$, and $\mathrm{Gyr}$ denotes a billion such years. The <parsec> conversion follows https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.06262[IAU 2015 Resolution B2, note 4]{ref}. This <Hubble time> is a present expansion timescale; the <age of an FLRW universe> also depends on the earlier evolution of the <scale factor> and need not equal $H_0^{-1}$.

= Hubble parameter identity

{parent=Hubble parameter}

$$

= Comoving Hubble radius

The comoving Hubble radius is $(aH)^{-1}$. It grows during ordinary decelerating expansion and shrinks during sufficiently accelerated inflation, allowing modes to cross from sub-Hubble to super-Hubble scales.

= Cosmic-time derivative of the comoving Hubble radius
{parent=Comoving Hubble radius}
{title2=$d[(aH)^{-1}]/dt$}

With $\mathcal H=aH=\dot a$, the <Friedmann acceleration equation> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\frac1{\mathcal H}=-\frac{\ddot a}{\dot a^2}=\frac\Omega{2a}(1+3w).
$$
The coefficient is positive when $a>0$, $\rho>0$ and $H\ne0$. Thus growth of the <comoving Hubble radius> and linear growth of departures from flatness have the same sign criterion in an expanding universe. This local criterion is distinct from a global <particle horizon>, which depends on the whole past history.

= Hubble parameter for constant-equation-of-state components
{parent=Hubble parameter}

In a spatially flat universe containing components with constant $w_i$,
$$
H(z)=H_0\left[\sum_i\Omega_{i,0}(1+z)^{3(1+w_i)}\right]^{1/2}.
$$

= Hubble parameter for matter and dynamical dark energy
{parent=Hubble parameter}

For pressureless matter and dark energy with equation of state $w(z)$,
$$
H(z)=H_0\left[\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3
+\Omega_{{\rm DE},0}(1+z)^3
\exp\left(3\int_0^z\frac{w(z')}{1+z'}\,dz'\right)
\right]^{1/2}.
$$

= Hubble friction

Hubble friction is the damping term proportional to the <Hubble parameter> in a field or fluid equation in an expanding universe. A homogeneous scalar field, for example, has the term $3H\dot\phi$ in its equation of motion.

The velocity-distance relation is <Hubble's law>; a peculiar velocity is a departure from this homogeneous flow.

= Redshift shell volume at the present epoch
{parent=Comoving volume}
{title2=$dV_0=cD_M^2(z)\,dz\,d\Omega/H(z)$}

On a present FLRW spatial slice, a shell has volume $R_0^3S^2(\chi)d\chi\,d\Omega$. The radial light-ray relation gives $d\chi/dz=c/(R_0H)$, yielding the displayed expression in terms of <transverse comoving distance>. At emission the proper volume is smaller by $(1+z)^3$. For a conserved comoving population its proper <density> is greater by the same factor, so either epoch gives the same count.

= Comoving number density
{parent=Comoving volume}
{title2=$n_p=(1+z)^3n_c$}

<Comoving number density> is object count per <comoving volume>. With scale factor normalized to unity today, proper density is $n_p=a^{-3}n_c$. A conserved population moving with homogeneous expansion has constant $n_c$ despite declining proper density. Formation, mergers or changing sample selection can make $n_c$ evolve.

= Einstein-de Sitter cumulative number counts
{parent=Comoving number density}
{c}
{title2=$N(<z_f)=\frac{8n_0c^3}{3H_0^3}[1-(1+z_f)^{-1/2}]^3$}

For constant <comoving number density> and complete selection in a spatially flat <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, integrate the present-epoch shell volume up to the population's formation <redshift>. The radial distance is $D_M=2c[1-(1+z)^{-1/2}]/H_0$, so the count per unit <solid angle> is $n_0D_M^3(z_f)/3$. It tends to a finite value as $z_f$ tends to infinity because this model's present comoving particle-horizon radius is $2c/H_0$. Formation, mergers or an incomplete observed sample change this ideal count.

= Source counts on an FLRW past light cone
{parent=Comoving number density}
{title2=$dN=4\pi a^2r^2n\,dt$}

For proper <number density> $n(t)$ in an <FLRW metric> $ds^2=dt^2-a^2[dr^2/(1-kr^2)+r^2d\Omega^2]$, a radial shell has proper volume $4\pi a^3r^2|dr|/\sqrt{1-kr^2}$. A radial <null geodesic> obeys $|dr|=\sqrt{1-kr^2}\,dt/a$. Multiplication gives the displayed shell count on a local radial branch of the <past light cone>. Spatially uniform <number density> need not be time independent or have conserved <comoving number density>.

= Peculiar velocity
{parent=Comoving coordinate}
{wiki}

Peculiar velocity is motion relative to the homogeneous Hubble flow. In comoving coordinates,
$$
\mathbf u=aH\mathbf x+\mathbf v,
$$
where $\mathbf u$ is physical velocity and $\mathbf v$ is peculiar velocity.

= Redshifting of peculiar momentum
{parent=Peculiar velocity}
{title2=$a\,p=\mathrm{constant},\quad a\gamma v=\mathrm{constant}$}

A freely moving massive test particle in an <FLRW metric> has physical <momentum> $p=m\gamma v$ relative to a <comoving observer>, where $v$ is its <peculiar velocity>. For a radial <geodesic>, the <geodesic Lagrangian> gives $C=a^2d\chi/d\tau$ constant, and the local radial <momentum> is $p=ma\,d\chi/d\tau=mC/a$. The <proper-time normalization> gives $dt/d\tau=\sqrt{1+C^2/(a^2c^2)}$, so
$$
v=\frac{C/a}{\sqrt{1+C^2/(a^2c^2)}}.
$$
Consequently nonrelativistic <peculiar velocity> scales as $a^{-1}$, while the exact relativistic law keeps $|v|<c$. The <momentum> magnitude of a free <photon> also scales as $a^{-1}$, giving <cosmological redshift> through $E=cp$. These statements concern force-free propagation; a bound system need not follow the cosmological expansion.

= Radial peculiar velocity in spherical harmonics
{parent=Peculiar velocity}
{title2=$U=\frac{aHf}{2\pi^2}\sum_{\ell m}(-i)^\ell\int d^3k\,\frac{\delta_k}{k}j_\ell'(kr)Y_{\ell m}(\hat k)Y_{\ell m}^*(\hat r)$}

With $\delta(\mathbf x)=\int\delta_k e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ and logarithmic growing-mode rate $f=d\log D_+/d\log a$, the <linearized cosmological continuity equation> gives $\mathbf v_k=-iaHf\delta_k\mathbf k/k^2$. Apply the <Rayleigh plane-wave expansion> to its velocity potential and differentiate radially to obtain the displayed expression. The factor $aH$ supplies the velocity dimensions. At the present epoch $U/H_0$ has the same formula with $aH$ removed; the commonly used $f\simeq\Omega_m^{0.6}$ is an approximation, not an exact general growth law.

= Peculiar-velocity equation
{parent=Peculiar velocity}

For a fluid in an expanding universe, the peculiar-velocity equation is the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> after decomposing physical velocity into <Hubble flow> and <peculiar velocity>. It contains the Hubble-drag term $H\mathbf v$ and force $-\nabla\Phi/a$ from the <peculiar gravitational potential>.

= Peculiar-velocity divergence
{title2=$\theta$}
{parent=Peculiar velocity}

The peculiar-velocity divergence is $\theta=\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf v$. For irrotational linear perturbations it captures the scalar part of the peculiar velocity field.

= Cosmological redshift
{parent=Scale factor (cosmology)}
{wiki}
{title2=$1+z=a(t_0)/a(t_e)$}

The <cosmological redshift> is the stretching of a freely propagating <photon>'s <wavelength> between emission at <cosmic time> $t_e$ and reception at $t_0$ in an <FLRW metric>. For a <comoving observer> at each endpoint,
$$
1+z=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_e}=\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)},\qquad
\frac{\nu_0}{\nu_e}=\frac{1}{1+z}.
$$
Here $\lambda_e,\lambda_0$ are emitted and received <wavelengths>, and $\nu_e,\nu_0$ are the corresponding <frequencies>. A positive <cosmological redshift> means that the <scale factor> grew while the <photon> travelled. The name describes a shift toward longer <wavelengths>; it applies to light beyond the visible range as well.

For a computed example, let the <scale factor> double from $a(t_e)=0.5$ to $a(t_0)=1$. An emitted <wavelength> of $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ becomes $1000\,\mathrm{nm}$, giving $z=1000/500-1=1$. The received <frequency> is half the emitted <frequency>. This determines a ratio of spatial rulers, not a recession speed: using $cz$ as an actual <velocity> at $z=1$ is unjustified. The <wavelength> panel in the illustration under <Hubble's law> shows this doubling.

A short derivation follows successive <wave> crests along a <radial null geodesic in FLRW spacetime>. Their source and receiver have fixed <comoving coordinates>, so each crest traverses the same <comoving distance>:
$$
\chi=\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)}.
$$
Shift both endpoint times to the next crest while keeping $\chi$ fixed. To first order in the very short periods $\delta t_e,\delta t_0$, this gives $\delta t_0/a(t_0)=\delta t_e/a(t_e)$. Locally, <wavelength> equals the <speed of light> times the crest period, yielding the displayed <cosmological redshift> relation. These periods are much shorter than the timescale on which the <scale factor> changes.

The relation isolates the smooth expansion contribution. An observed <redshift> can also contain a <Doppler effect> from source or observer <peculiar velocity> and a <gravitational redshift>. See <combined kinematic and cosmological redshift> for the multiplicative combination of endpoint motion and expansion. Treating every observed <redshift> as pure <cosmological redshift> can therefore misassign a nearby source's distance.

The useful insight is that a received <wavelength> ratio records how much the <scale factor> changed during the <photon>'s journey. Turning that ratio into a distance requires an expansion history, through the <Hubble parameter>, and a specified convention such as <luminosity distance> or <proper distance in cosmology>; the ratio alone supplies neither.

= Photometric redshift
{parent=Cosmological redshift}

A redshift estimated from multi-band fluxes or an inferred spectral <energy> distribution rather than directly resolved spectral-line wavelengths. <calibration>, templates, dust and population differences can produce biases and catastrophic outliers. In lensing or clustering analyses, the inferred source redshift distribution affects the geometric kernel as well as assignment to redshift bins.

= Collisionless preservation of a Planck spectrum
{parent=Cosmological redshift}

Let the scale factor grow by $f$ from an initially thermal <photon> distribution. <geodesic> redshift gives $\nu_i=f\nu$, and conserving <photons> in corresponding comoving <frequency> bins gives $n_\nu(t)=f^{-2}n_{f\nu}(t_i)$. Inserting <Planck's law> leaves the same spectral form at <temperature> $T_i/f$. Total photon-number conservation alone would not prove that the entire <radiation spectrum> stays Planckian; <frequency> redshift and collisionless propagation are also required. Absorption, injection or spectral mixing can invalidate this one-temperature argument.

= Photon redshift and arrival-time dilution
{parent=Cosmological redshift}

For a comoving emitter and receiver, photon energy falls as $1/(1+z)$ and arrival intervals grow by $1+z$. Their combination reduces bolometric flux by $(1+z)^2$ in addition to the area dilution.

= Reception-event dependence of intergalactic redshift
{parent=Cosmological redshift}
{title2=$1+z_{BA}=a(t_A)/a(t_B)$}

A <cosmological redshift> relates a particular emission event to a particular reception event along a <null geodesic>. Two sources' redshifts on Earth's past light cone do not specify the light exchanged between those sources. If a photon from B passes through A before reaching Earth, and A's comparison emission lies at that passage event, then $1+z_{BA}=(1+z_B)/(1+z_A)$. Without this causal and temporal specification the ratio need not give the redshift measured in A.

= Combined kinematic and cosmological redshift
{parent=Cosmological redshift}
{title2=$1+z_{\rm tot}=(1+z_{\rm cosm})(1+z_{\rm kin})$}

Frequency ratios multiply when a photon is transformed first between a source and its local comoving frame, then propagated through an expanding universe. Thus the <kinematic redshift> and <cosmological redshift> factors multiply. Their redshifts themselves add only to first order when both are small.

= Cosmological wavelength-redshift relation
{parent=Cosmological redshift}
{title2=$1+z=\lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm rest}$}

Cosmic expansion stretches an emitted or absorbed spectral feature's wavelength by $1+z$. A small observed wavelength interval at fixed transition wavelength gives a redshift interval by division by the rest wavelength. Cosmological radial distances then require the <Hubble parameter> and, for proper distance at that epoch, an extra factor $1/(1+z)$.

= Proper absorber separation from a wavelength interval
{parent=Cosmological wavelength-redshift relation}
{title2=$\ell\simeq c\Delta\lambda/[H(z)(1+z)\lambda_{\rm rest}]$}

A local redshift spacing between absorbers corresponds to proper path length $c\Delta z/[H(z)(1+z)]$. Using the same rest-frame transition for all lines gives $\Delta z=\Delta\lambda/\lambda_{\rm rest}$. This conversion assumes <cosmological redshift> dominates the line spacing and uses a local Hubble-rate approximation.

Redshift drift is the change in a comoving source's cosmological redshift with observer time. It directly probes $\dot z=(1+z)H_0-H(z)$.

The <Sandage-Loeb test> seeks the <spectroscopic velocity drift> of stable cosmological absorption features over an observer-time baseline. = Spectroscopic velocity drift
{parent=Redshift drift}
{title2=$\dot v=c\dot z/(1+z)$}

For a transition with fixed rest wavelength, $\lambda_{\rm obs}=(1+z)\lambda_{\rm rest}$. The apparent spectroscopic velocity shift is $dv=c\,d\lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm obs}=c\,dz/(1+z)$. It is a convenient wavelength-shift unit, not necessarily a change in a source's <peculiar velocity>. Omitting the denominator $1+z$ overestimates the signal at high <redshift>.

= Nonzero redshift-drift root in flat matter-Lambda cosmology
{parent=Redshift drift}
{title2=$1+z_*=[\Omega_\Lambda+\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda^2+4\Omega_m\Omega_\Lambda}]/(2\Omega_m)$}

For positive matter and vacuum fractions with $\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda=1$, <redshift drift> vanishes when $s^2=\Omega_ms^3+\Omega_\Lambda$, $s=1+z$. Factoring out the trivial observer root $s=1$ leaves $\Omega_ms^2-\Omega_\Lambda s-\Omega_\Lambda=0$, giving the displayed positive <root of a polynomial>. It lies at a positive <redshift> exactly when $\Omega_m<2/3$. It is independent of the <Hubble constant> at fixed density fractions and is not the <redshift> of the acceleration-to-deceleration transition.

= Redshift-time relation

Differentiating $1+z=a_0/a(t)$ gives
$$
\frac{dz}{dt}=-(1+z)H(z).

= Cosmic chronometer
{parent=Redshift-time relation}

Cosmic chronometers infer the Hubble parameter from differential ages of passively evolving galaxies:
$$
H(z)=-\frac1{1+z}\frac{dz}{dt}.

They probe $H(z)$ locally in redshift rather than through a distance integral.

= Cosmological time dilation
{parent=Cosmological redshift}

Observed time intervals from a comoving source are stretched by the same factor $1+z$ as photon wavelengths.

= Redshift preservation of a thermal photon spectrum
{parent=Cosmological redshift}

After photon decoupling in an expanding universe, every frequency scales as $\nu\propto a^{-1}$. Therefore a Planck occupation factor retains its thermal form when its temperature is assigned the same scaling,
$$
T(t)=\frac{a(t_{\rm dec})}{a(t)}T_{\rm dec}.

= Luminosity distance
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

Luminosity distance is defined by $F=L/(4\pi d_L^2)$ and includes geometric dilution, photon redshift, and arrival-rate dilation.

= Constant-equation-of-state luminosity distance
{parent=Luminosity distance}
{title2=$D_L=(1+z)c\int_0^z dz'/H(z')$}

For one conserved constant-$w$ <perfect fluid> in a flat expanding <FLRW metric>, $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^{3(1+w)/2}$. Integrating the radial <null geodesic> gives
$$
D_L=\frac{2c(1+z)}{H_0(1+3w)}[1-(1+z)^{-(1+3w)/2}]
$$
when $w\ne-1/3$. Its continuous coasting limit is $D_L=c(1+z)\log(1+z)/H_0$. Expansion gives $D_L=(c/H_0)[z+(1-3w)z^2/4+O(z^3)]$, agreeing with the <deceleration parameter> $q_0=(1+3w)/2$. Curvature, additional independently evolving fluids or a varying $w$ change this single-fluid expression.

= Luminosity-distance ordering of flat dust-vacuum universes
{parent=Luminosity distance}

At fixed positive <redshift>, intrinsic <luminosity> and present <Hubble constant>, a flat dust-vacuum model with $0<\Omega_{\Lambda0}<1$ has <luminosity distance> between those of an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> and pure positive vacuum. The <Hubble parameter> of the dust-only model is largest at every positive integration <redshift>; that of pure vacuum is smallest. Therefore $c(1+z)\int_0^z dz'/H(z')$ is smallest for dust alone and largest for pure vacuum. Received <radiative flux> has the reverse ordering. This compares physical distances rather than arbitrarily normalized <comoving coordinates>.

= FLRW area distance versus radial proper distance
{parent=Luminosity distance}
{c}

In curvature coordinates the present sphere area is $4\pi(R_0r)^2$. Its transverse area distance is $R_0r$, whereas radial proper distance is $R_0\int_0^rdr/\sqrt{1-Kr^2}$. Confusing them invalidates a flat-space distance identity in curved geometry.

= Einstein-de Sitter luminosity-distance relation
{c}
{parent=Luminosity distance}
{title2=$d_L=2(c/H_0)[1+z-\sqrt{1+z}]$}

In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe> normalized by $a_0=1$, the <Friedmann equation> gives $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^{3/2}$. Integrating the radial <null geodesic> gives $r(z)=c\int_0^z dz'/H(z')=2(c/H_0)[1-(1+z)^{-1/2}]$. The <luminosity distance> is $(1+z)r(z)$, yielding the displayed expression. Near the observer it is $(c/H_0)[z+z^2/4+O(z^3)]$; the limiting radial <comoving distance> is finite even though the <luminosity distance> diverges.

= Open dust distance-redshift relation
{parent=Luminosity distance}
{title2=$r(z)=\frac{2[\Omega z+(2-\Omega)(1-\sqrt{1+\Omega z})]}{a_0H_0\Omega^2(1+z)}$}

For an open dust-only <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric> with zero <cosmological constant> and $0<\Omega<1$, $H(z)=H_0(1+z)\sqrt{1+\Omega z}$. The present radial <cosmological proper distance> is $d_S=\int_0^z dz'/H(z')$, and the areal radial coordinate satisfies $a_0r=\sinh[H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega}\,d_S]/[H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega}]$. Integrating with $v=\sqrt{1+\Omega z}$ gives the displayed relation. It has $r\sim z/(a_0H_0)$ near the observer and $r\to2/(a_0H_0\Omega)$ as $z\to\infty$. The <luminosity distance> is $(1+z)a_0r$, not the radial <cosmological proper distance>.

= Type Ia supernova cosmology
{c}
{parent=Luminosity distance}

Type Ia supernova cosmology uses standardized supernova luminosities to measure the shape of the luminosity-distance--redshift relation. In a spatially flat universe,
$$
d_L(z)=(1+z)c\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')}.
$$
An unknown common absolute luminosity is degenerate with the overall distance scale but does not erase the redshift dependence.

The useful observable is a relative <luminosity distance> at measured <redshift>, rather than an object's brightness alone. <Spectroscopy> supplies $z=\lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm rest}-1$; repeated <photometry> supplies a <light curve>. A <Type Ia supernova> is a <standardizable candle>: a fitted relation between <light curve> shape and <luminosity> replaces the incorrect assumption that every explosion has identical peak <absolute magnitude>. Nearby objects establish the relation and its common intercept; distant objects test its continuation and the shape of the <Hubble diagram>.

= Standardization of Type Ia supernova light curves
{parent=Type Ia supernova cosmology}
{title2=$m_{B,\mathrm{corr}}=m_B+\alpha(s-1)$}

The stretch method rescales the <light curve>'s time axis by $s(1+z)$ and corrects peak <apparent magnitude> by $\alpha(s-1)$, fitting $\alpha$ jointly with cosmology.<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Section 2, equations (1)–(2): stretch and time dilation]{ref}

Here is a deliberately stipulated local model that exposes the signs. Let $t_{\rm obs}$ be observer time in <days>, $t_{\max}$ the observed peak time and $T(u)$ a dimensionless template with $T(0)=1$. In a single <photometric passband>, write
$$
u=\frac{t_{\rm obs}-t_{\max}}{s(1+z)},\qquad F(t_{\rm obs})=F_{\max}T(u).
$$
Here $u$ is rest-frame time in <days>; the template's argument carries those units. For illustration, assign the intrinsic relation $M_B(s)=M_{B,0}-\alpha(s-1)$ with $\alpha>0$: broader events are intrinsically brighter. Define the sign of the <K correction> by $m_{\rm obs}=M_B(s)+\mu+A_B+K$, where $A_B$ is <interstellar extinction> in magnitudes. Then
$$
m_{B,\rm corr}=m_{\rm obs}-K-A_B+\alpha(s-1)=M_{B,0}+\mu.
$$
Removing attenuation makes the magnitude smaller; standardizing a broad, intrinsically bright event makes it larger. The fitted <cosmological time dilation> must be removed before comparing intrinsic durations. This schematic factorization explains the bookkeeping; an actual multicolour fit must also model changing intrinsic colours and correlated uncertainties.

For two equally standardized sources, cancellation of their common <absolute magnitude> gives an especially clean result:
$$
\mu_2-\mu_1=m_{B,\rm corr,2}-m_{B,\rm corr,1},\qquad
\frac{d_{L,2}}{d_{L,1}}=10^{(\mu_2-\mu_1)/5}.
$$
A stipulated residual of $+0.20$ mag therefore means a distance ratio $10^{0.04}=1.09648$ and a <radiative flux> ratio $10^{-0.08}=0.83176$ relative to the comparison prediction, provided <luminosity> and attenuation have been correctly standardized. This is a conversion of a hypothetical residual, not a measured survey value.

= Supernova calibration and population tests
{parent=Type Ia supernova cosmology}

The <High-Z Supernova Search Team> analysis fitted multicolour <light curves> and <interstellar extinction>, and compared a second decline-rate method. Similar local and distant <spectroscopy> supported, but did not establish, population stability. Nearly colourless attenuation remained a qualified alternative.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[Sections 3.2, 5.1–5.2: multicolour fits, evolution, grey dust]{ref}

The <Supernova Cosmology Project> compared low- and high-<redshift> colours, removed red outliers in its primary fit, and tested <Malmquist bias> with artificial-source recovery.<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Sections 4.1–4.2: colours, subsamples, artificial-source selection tests]{ref}

The inference can be organized as a chain of independently testable failure modes. A <magnitude zero point> offset between nearby and distant observations imitates a <distance modulus> offset; a common offset instead changes the intercept. An erroneous <photometric passband> response changes a <K correction> in a <redshift>-dependent way because different emitted <wavelengths> cross the detector band. Residual host-<galaxy> light changes the recovered <radiative flux>. <Color excess> constrains wavelength-dependent <interstellar extinction> only after intrinsic colour is modeled; grey attenuation is not bounded by colour alone. Changes in <stellar population> or <metallicity> could shift standardized <absolute magnitude> without large changes in the chosen shape parameter. These are logical degeneracies, so increasing the sample size alone cannot remove them.

A useful control strategy therefore compares subsets with different host environments, fits consistent rest-frame <photometric passbands>, and checks artificial-source recovery through the same image processing and selection. A <Malmquist bias> tends to select brighter detectable sources, but the relative bias between the nearby and distant samples is what matters. <Peculiar velocity> mainly perturbs the nearby <redshift> anchor; <weak gravitational lensing> changes individual brightnesses and the selection distribution. Neither can be replaced by an arbitrary extra scatter term when its mean or selection dependence is uncertain.

= Hubble diagram
{c}
{parent=Type Ia supernova cosmology}
{title2=$\mu(z)=5\log_{10}[d_L(z)/(10\,\mathrm{pc})]$}

A Hubble diagram plots a distance indicator, such as standardized <supernova> apparent magnitude or <distance modulus>, against redshift. The low-redshift relation sets a distance-scale intercept, while curvature of the relation at larger redshift tests the expansion history through <luminosity distance>. A common unknown absolute magnitude remains degenerate with the <Hubble constant>; the diagram's redshift-dependent shape can still constrain other cosmological parameters.

= Absolute magnitude–Hubble constant degeneracy
{parent=Type Ia supernova cosmology}

In <Type Ia supernova cosmology>, the predicted <apparent magnitude> depends on the common <absolute magnitude> $M_0$ and the <Hubble constant> only through $\beta=M_0-5\log_{10}(H_0/H_{\rm ref})$, after choosing a fixed reference $H_{\rm ref}>0$. A flat <improper prior> on $M_0$ makes the likelihood integrated over $M_0$ independent of $H_0$, by translation of the integration variable. If the other prior factors are independent and the posterior is proper, the marginal posterior of $H_0$ equals its prior. An external calibration of $M_0$ can break this lack of <identifiability>.

= Cosmological flux dimming
{parent=Luminosity distance}

Expansion reduces bolometric flux by one factor of $1+z$ from photon energy and one from arrival rate, in addition to inverse-area dilution.

= Cosmological spectral flux-density relation
{parent=Cosmological flux dimming}
{title2=$F_{\nu_0}=(1+z)L_{(1+z)\nu_0}/(4\pi d_L^2)$}

A comoving source's <spectral luminosity> $L_{\nu_e}$ specifies emitted power per unit emitted <frequency>. The <cosmological redshift> reduces photon <energy> and arrival rate by two factors of $1+z$, but also narrows the received frequency interval by one factor. Consequently its observed <spectral flux density> is $L_{\nu_e}/[4\pi a_0^2r^2(1+z)]$, with $\nu_e=(1+z)\nu_0$. Substituting <luminosity distance> $d_L=(1+z)a_0r$ gives the displayed relation. Integrating over received <frequency> recovers the bolometric <radiative flux> $L/(4\pi d_L^2)$. A fixed-frequency comparison must evaluate the source spectrum at the shifted emitted <frequency>.

= Age of a flat matter-Lambda universe
{parent=Age of an FLRW universe}

= Age of a dust universe without a cosmological constant
{parent=Age of an FLRW universe}
{title2=$H_0t_0=\int_0^1\left(1-2q_0+2q_0/x\right)^{-1/2}\,dx$}

For an expanding <pressureless matter> universe with zero <cosmological constant>, normalize $x=a/a_0$. <Dust relation between deceleration and density> and mass conservation give $\dot x^2=H_0^2(1-2q_0+2q_0/x)$. Integrating from the <Big Bang> yields the age integral above.

For $q_0>1/2$, set $x=q_0(1-\cos\theta)/(2q_0-1)$ to obtain $H_0t_0=q_0(\theta_* -\sin\theta_*)/(2q_0-1)^{3/2}$, with $\cos\theta_*=(1-q_0)/q_0$ on the expanding branch. The flat-dust and empty <Milne universe> limits are respectively $2/3$ and $1$.

= Age ordering of dust universes at fixed Hubble parameter
{parent=Age of a dust universe without a cosmological constant}
{title2=$t_{\rm closed}<2/(3H_0)<t_{\rm open}<1/H_0$}

For an expanding <pressureless matter> universe with zero <cosmological constant>, fixed current <Hubble parameter> $H_0$ and current matter <cosmological density parameter> $\Omega>0$, the <age of a dust universe without a cosmological constant> is $H_0t_0=\int_0^1\sqrt{x}/\sqrt{\Omega+(1-\Omega)x}\,dx$. For every $0<x<1$ the integrand decreases strictly as $\Omega$ increases. Thus open models with $0<\Omega<1$ are older than the flat model, and closed expanding models with $\Omega>1$ are younger. The flat value is $2/3$ and the empty <Milne universe> limit is $1$. Specifying what is held fixed matters: at fixed present <scale factor> and <energy density>, the integral $t_0=\int_0^{a_0}da/\sqrt{C/a-k}$ instead increases with $k$, provided all three models reach $a_0$ on their expanding branches.

{parent=Age of a dust universe without a cosmological constant}
{title2=$H_0t_0=\frac{\Omega_0(u_0-\sin u_0)}{2(\Omega_0-1)^{3/2}}$}

= Supernova discovery of accelerating expansion
{parent=Accelerating expansion of the universe}

The 1998 <High-Z Supernova Search Team> paper by <Adam Riess>, <Brian Schmidt> and collaborators presented <supernova> evidence for acceleration.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[Byline and abstract, PDF pp.1–2]{ref}

The 1999 <Supernova Cosmology Project> paper by <Saul Perlmutter> and collaborators independently analysed 42 distant events. <Alexei V. Filippenko> coauthored both papers.<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Byline and abstract, PDF p.1]{ref}

The <High-Z Supernova Search Team> search used the CTIO Blanco 4-m telescope and a $2048\times2048$ <charge-coupled device>, comparing fields across dark runs. Difference images located rising candidates; Keck, MMT and ESO <spectroscopy> classified them and measured host <redshifts>. Follow-up <photometry> used filters approximating emitted B and V bands. Calibrated field stars transferred the <magnitude zero point>; late host images removed <galaxy> light, <point spread function> fits measured brightness, and inserted artificial stars tested recovery.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[Sections 2.1–2.2, PDF pp.5–8]{ref}

The <Supernova Cosmology Project> likewise discovered rising events with CTIO 4-m <charge-coupled device> imaging, then followed their <light curves> with several telescopes. Keck LRIS and ESO <spectroscopy> supplied classification and <redshift>, preferably from narrow host lines. Cross-filter <K corrections> connected received R/I observations to emitted B/V light.<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[Section 2, PDF pp.2–3: apparatus and corrections]{ref}

Why does fainter mean accelerating? First it means a larger inferred <luminosity distance> only at fixed standardized <luminosity> and corrected attenuation. Next it favours acceleration only after a comparison family is specified: an <FLRW metric>, representative propagation of light, common nearby and distant standardization, and controlled <measurement calibration> and selection. For the usual <general relativity> models containing nonnegative <pressureless matter>, <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe> and a <cosmological constant>, the shape of the <Hubble diagram> constrains $\Omega_{m,0},\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$ and thus $q_0=\Omega_{m,0}/2-\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$. A positive residual relative to one decelerating model rejects that prediction; it does not alone prove $q_0<0$ across arbitrary expansion histories or arbitrary opacity laws.

The essential advance is that a nearby sample fixes an intercept while a range of distant <redshifts> measures the shape. The two teams supplied independent observations and analysis, although common nearby calibrators and <Type Ia supernova> physics can produce shared uncertainties. The <dark-energy acceleration criterion> explains the interpretation within <general relativity>; the <worked supernova distance comparison> shows the size and sign of the effect without fitting any survey data.

= Worked supernova distance comparison
{parent=Supernova discovery of accelerating expansion}
{title2=$\Delta\mu=5\log_{10}(d_{L,1}/d_{L,2})$}

Stipulate $H_0=70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ and $c=299792.458\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so $c/H_0=4282.7494\,\mathrm{Mpc}$. A megaparsec is $10^6$ <parsecs>. Neglect <radiation in cosmology>. Compare an accelerating flat model $(\Omega_{m,0},\Omega_{\Lambda,0})=(0.3,0.7)$, an open matter-only model $(0.3,0)$ and the <Einstein-de Sitter universe> $(1,0)$. Their present <deceleration parameters> are respectively $-0.55$, $0.15$ and $0.5$. For these models,
$$
E(z)=\frac{H(z)}{H_0}=\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3+\Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2+\Omega_{\Lambda,0}},\qquad
\Omega_{k,0}=1-\Omega_{m,0}-\Omega_{\Lambda,0},
$$
$$
\chi(z)=\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{E(z')},\qquad
d_L=\frac{c}{H_0}(1+z)S_k(\chi),\qquad
S_k(\chi)=\begin{cases}
\sinh(\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\chi)/\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}},&\Omega_{k,0}>0,\\
\chi,&\Omega_{k,0}=0,\\
\sin(\sqrt{-\Omega_{k,0}}\chi)/\sqrt{-\Omega_{k,0}},&\Omega_{k,0}<0.
\end{cases}
$$
Here $\chi$ is dimensionless; the <radial null geodesic in FLRW spacetime> supplies its integral and <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe> supplies $S_k$. The observer-to-source <comoving radial distance> is $(c/H_0)\chi$, whereas the transverse distance uses $S_k$. Omitting the latter distinction would bias the open-model comparison.

At $z=0.5$, <numerical integration> gives $\chi=0.440984335$ for the flat accelerating model and $\chi=0.392227378$ for the open matter-only model. Thus
$$
\begin{array}{c|r|r}
(\Omega_{m,0},\Omega_{\Lambda,0})&d_L\ (\mathrm{Mpc})&\mu=5\log_{10}[d_L/(1\,\mathrm{Mpc})]+25\\\hline
(0.3,0.7)&2832.9381&42.26119\\
(0.3,0)&2565.1861&42.04559\\
(1,0)&2357.6975&41.86244
\end{array}
$$
The accelerating model is $0.21559$ mag fainter than the open matter-only prediction at the same <redshift> and standardized <absolute magnitude>, corresponding to $1.10438$ times the <luminosity distance> and $0.81991$ times the <radiative flux>. These are model calculations, not observations from either collaboration.

The <Milne model> has $q=0$, $\Omega_{m,0}=\Omega_{\Lambda,0}=0$ and $\Omega_{k,0}=1$, giving $\chi=\log(1+z)$ and $d_L=(c/H_0)(z+z^2/2)$. The <Einstein-de Sitter luminosity-distance relation> independently checks the flat matter-only integral. At $z=0.5$, the accelerating model exceeds the <Milne model> by only $0.12317$ mag, illustrating the dependence of a residual on the chosen reference. Over a longer <redshift> interval, <luminosity distance> integrates past deceleration as well as recent acceleration; a residual need not increase forever.

Multiplying $H_0$ by $f>0$ at fixed density fractions divides every $d_L$ by $f$ and shifts every <distance modulus> by $-5\log_{10}f$. The predicted <apparent magnitude> is unchanged if $M_{B,0}$ simultaneously increases by $5\log_{10}f$. This is the <absolute magnitude–Hubble constant degeneracy>. For example, $70\to77\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ shifts $\mu$ by $-0.20696$ mag and $M_{B,0}$ by $+0.20696$ mag. = Low-redshift acceleration cosmography
{parent=Supernova discovery of accelerating expansion}
{title2=$d_L=(c/H_0)[z+(1-q_0)z^2/2+O(z^3)]$}

A local derivation makes the acceleration sign transparent. Normalize $a(t_0)=1$ and put $\tau=t_0-t_e$. A <Taylor series> gives
$$
a(t_0-\tau)=1-H_0\tau-\frac12q_0H_0^2\tau^2+O(\tau^3),\qquad

$$
The <radial null geodesic in FLRW spacetime> gives $D_C=c\int_{t_e}^{t_0}dt/a(t)=c[\tau+H_0\tau^2/2+O(\tau^3)]$. Invert the <redshift> series, use $d_L=(1+z)D_C$ through second order, and obtain the displayed <luminosity distance>. <Spatial curvature of an FLRW universe> first changes this relation at third order. = Primordial light-element abundance determination
{parent=Big Bang nucleosynthesis}

Primordial abundances require extrapolation to material minimally processed by <stars>. Helium is inferred from hydrogen and helium emission in low-metallicity ionized regions, with ionization and temperature corrections. Deuterium is inferred from isotope-shifted absorption in low-metallicity <quasar> systems. Lithium is measured in old metal-poor stellar atmospheres and requires a correction, or a bound, for stellar depletion. These observables probe different astrophysical environments and systematic errors.

= Spite plateau
{c}
{parent=Primordial light-element abundance determination}
{wiki}

The Spite plateau is the approximately constant lithium abundance in warm, metal-poor halo stars. It provides an important estimate of primordial lithium only after accounting for stellar atmosphere physics and possible transport and nuclear destruction of lithium within the stars.

= Neutron-proton chemical equilibrium
{parent=Big Bang nucleosynthesis}

With negligible lepton chemical potentials, weak equilibrium gives $n_n/n_p\simeq e^{-Q/(k_BT)}$, where $Q=(m_n-m_p)c^2$.

= Neutron-to-proton ratio
{parent=Neutron-proton chemical equilibrium}
{title2=$n_n/n_p$}

Neglecting chemical potentials, the equilibrium abundance ratio is $n_n/n_p\simeq\exp[-(m_n-m_p)c^2/(k_BT)]$. Weak freeze-out fixes an approximate starting ratio, which is then reduced by <free-neutron decay after freeze-out> before most remaining neutrons bind into helium.

= Cosmological weak freeze-out
{parent=Big Bang nucleosynthesis}

Weak neutron--proton conversion freezes out when its interaction rate falls below the Hubble expansion rate.

= Freeze-out temperature and interaction strength
{parent=Cosmological weak freeze-out}

If an interaction-to-expansion ratio scales as $(T/\kappa)^3$, increasing $\kappa$ raises the temperature at which the ratio drops through one.

= Deuterium bottleneck
{parent=Big Bang nucleosynthesis}
{wiki}

Light nuclei cannot accumulate until cooling makes deuterium sufficiently resistant to photodissociation, delaying nucleosynthesis after weak freeze-out.

= Deuterium equilibrium abundance
{parent=Deuterium bottleneck}
{title2=$X_D/(X_nX_p)\simeq(12\zeta(3)/\sqrt\pi)\eta(T/m_N)^{3/2}e^{\varepsilon_D/T}$}

Use <natural units> and a positive <nuclear binding energy> $\varepsilon_D=m_n+m_p-m_D$. <Chemical equilibrium> of $n+p\leftrightarrow D+\gamma$ requires $\mu_D=\mu_n+\mu_p$. The <nonrelativistic Maxwell--Boltzmann number density> gives
$$
\frac{n_D}{n_nn_p}=\frac{g_D}{g_ng_p}\left(\frac{m_D}{m_nm_p}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^{3/2}e^{\varepsilon_D/T}.
$$
Here $g_D=3$ and $g_n=g_p=2$ count the spin states. Taking $m_n\simeq m_p\simeq m_N$ and $m_D\simeq2m_N$, then writing $X_i=n_i/n_B$, $n_B=\eta n_\gamma$ and using the <photon number density> yields the displayed abundance. The coefficient is approximately $8.14$. Omitting the spin-state ratio $3/4$ gives the rougher prefactor $16\zeta(3)/\sqrt\pi$. A convention $B=m_D-m_n-m_p<0$ instead writes the exponential as $e^{-B/T}$. The small <baryon-to-photon ratio> suppresses stable <deuterium> until the <temperature> is well below the binding scale, producing the <deuterium bottleneck>.

= Baryon-density effect on primordial helium
{parent=Deuterium equilibrium abundance}

Increasing the baryon-to-photon ratio raises the equilibrium deuterium abundance at fixed temperature, so the deuterium bottleneck ends earlier. Fewer neutrons decay before nuclear burning, and neutron-limited helium synthesis produces a larger primordial helium mass fraction if weak freeze-out is unchanged.

= Free-neutron decay after freeze-out
{parent=Deuterium bottleneck}

Between weak freeze-out and deuterium formation, beta decay lowers the surviving free-neutron fraction.

= Primordial helium mass fraction
{parent=Big Bang nucleosynthesis}

If almost all neutrons enter helium-4 and $r=n_n/n_p$, its primordial mass fraction is $Y_p\simeq2r/(1+r)$.

= Primordial helium response to the neutron-proton mass difference
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}
{title2=$\delta\log Y_p\simeq-\delta Q/[T_f(1+e^{-Q/T_f})]$}

At fixed <cosmological weak freeze-out> temperature $T_f$ and fixed neutron survival factor, <neutron-proton chemical equilibrium> gives $r_f=e^{-Q/T_f}$ and <neutron-limited helium synthesis> gives $Y_p\propto2r_f/(1+r_f)$. Differentiating yields the displayed sensitivity. A finite change $Q\mapsto(1+\alpha)Q$ gives $Y_p'/Y_p=(1+e^{Q/T_f})/(1+e^{(1+\alpha)Q/T_f})$. Increasing the positive mass difference reduces helium production. Changes to the weak conversion rates, neutron lifetime or nuclear history must be supplied separately for a full abundance prediction.

= Neutron half-life effect on primordial helium
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}
{title2=$Y_p\simeq\frac{2r_f}{1+r_f}e^{-\Delta t/\tau_n}$}

After <cosmological weak freeze-out>, the surviving <neutron> fraction is $x_n=r_f(1+r_f)^{-1}e^{-\Delta t/\tau_n}$, where $r_f$ is the initial <neutron-to-proton ratio> and $\tau_n=t_{1/2}/\ln2$ is the mean lifetime. <Baryon number> is conserved because a decaying <neutron> becomes a <proton>. If almost all surviving neutrons enter helium-4, <neutron-limited helium synthesis> gives the displayed <primordial helium mass fraction>. Increasing the <half-life> increases survival. If the longer lifetime also represents uniformly weaker <weak interactions>, it raises the temperature of <cosmological weak freeze-out> and the initial neutron fraction, reinforcing the helium increase. That last conclusion requires specifying how the weak rates change, rather than treating lifetime alone as a complete microscopic model.

= Primordial helium response to extra relativistic species
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}
{title2=$T_f\propto g_*^{1/6}$}

With a weak conversion time proportional to $T^{-5}$ and a radiation-era age proportional to $g_*^{-1/2}T^{-2}$, <cosmological weak freeze-out> occurs at $T_f\propto g_*^{1/6}$. Extra radiation therefore raises the freeze-out temperature and the <neutron-to-proton ratio> $r_f=e^{-Q/T_f}$. Before decay, the <primordial helium mass fraction> is $Y_f=2r_f/(1+r_f)$, giving $\delta Y_f/Y_f=Q\delta\log T_f/[T_f(1+r_f)]$. A shorter delay through the <deuterium bottleneck> also leaves more neutrons. After freeze-out, conserve baryon number and evolve the neutron fraction to obtain $Y_p\simeq Y_f e^{-(t_N-t_f)/\tau_n}$. A precise abundance requires the weak rates and nuclear-burning history, not merely the number of relativistic species.

= Primordial helium response to electron-neutrino degeneracy
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}
{title2=$n_n/n_p\simeq e^{-(m_n-m_p)/T-\xi_{\nu_e}}$}

Chemical equilibrium of $n+\nu_e\leftrightarrow p+e^-$, with negligible electron <chemical potential>, gives the stated <neutron-to-proton ratio>. Positive electron-neutrino degeneracy reduces the neutron fraction, whereas negative degeneracy increases it. The <primordial helium mass fraction> then responds through <neutron-limited helium synthesis> while protons outnumber neutrons; a sufficiently neutron-rich mixture instead becomes proton limited, so that trend must not be extrapolated indefinitely. Extra <neutrino> <energy density> also accelerates <cosmic expansion>, changing weak freeze-out and the time available for neutron decay. These effects can compete; a quantitative <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> calculation must account for the altered reaction rates as well as the altered expansion rate.

= Neutron-limited helium synthesis
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}

When protons outnumber neutrons, helium-4 production is neutron-limited and produces about one helium nucleus per two neutrons.

= Helium sensitivity to the expansion rate
{parent=Neutron-limited helium synthesis}
{title2=$T_f\propto(Gg_*)^{1/6}$}

During radiation domination, the weak conversion rate scales as $T^5$ whereas the <Hubble parameter> scales as $\sqrt{Gg_*}\,T^2$. Their crossing fixes the <cosmological weak freeze-out> temperature. A faster expansion raises it, leaving a larger <neutron-to-proton ratio>, and shortens the interval for <free-neutron decay after freeze-out>. Both effects increase the helium-4 mass fraction if the extra component does not independently change reaction rates or inject decay products.

= Primordial helium response to a varying gravitational constant
{parent=Helium sensitivity to the expansion rate}
{title2=$T_f\propto G^{1/6}$}

If the <Friedmann equation> remains approximately $H^2=8\pi G\rho/3$, <radiation domination> and fixed weak microphysics give $H\propto\sqrt G\,T^2$ and a neutron-proton conversion rate proportional to $T^5$. <Cosmological weak freeze-out> then occurs at $T_f\propto G^{1/6}$. Increasing $G$ during this era raises the <neutron-to-proton ratio> at freeze-out and shortens the time for <free-neutron decay after freeze-out>. Both increase the <primordial helium mass fraction> when <neutron-limited helium synthesis> remains efficient. Arbitrary variation of $G$ over time requires its history and the full gravitational field equations; this scaling is a controlled background approximation, rather than a complete model of gravity with arbitrary time dependence.

= Nuclear-burning qualification of the helium expansion-rate response
{parent=Helium sensitivity to the expansion rate}

A moderately enhanced <Hubble parameter> raises the <primordial helium mass fraction> through earlier <cosmological weak freeze-out> and less <free-neutron decay after freeze-out>, provided nuclear burning still incorporates nearly all surviving neutrons into helium-4. This provision matters: if expansion carries the gas through the nuclear-burning temperature range faster than the reaction times, <neutron-limited helium synthesis> no longer applies. More neutrons surviving the bottleneck alone does not prove more final helium in that limit.

= Primordial composition and stellar lifetime
{parent=Primordial helium mass fraction}

Increasing primordial helium at the expense of hydrogen reduces fuel for long-lived main-sequence hydrogen burning and changes subsequent stellar evolution.

= Baryon-to-photon ratio
{title2=$\eta$}
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

The baryon-to-photon ratio is the number density of baryons divided by the number density of photons. In the present universe it is of order $10^{-9}$.

= Photon-to-baryon ratio
{parent=Baryon-to-photon ratio}
{title2=$s_\gamma=n_\gamma/n_B=1/\eta$}

The reciprocal of the <baryon-to-photon ratio>, not a synonym for it. In a thermal photon gas this number ratio is proportional to, but differs from, the thermodynamic <entropy> per baryon. The exact photon entropy per photon is $4u_\gamma/(3k_BTn_\gamma)\simeq3.60$, so the photon-sector entropy per baryon in units of $k_B$ is about $3.60s_\gamma$. The entropy sector must be specified when other relativistic species contribute.

= Photon-to-baryon ratio from density parameters
{parent=Photon-to-baryon ratio}
{title2=$s_\gamma\simeq0.33[m_p^4c^3/(\rho_c\hbar^3)]^{1/4}\Omega_\gamma^{3/4}/\Omega_B$}

With $n_\gamma=0.24Q^3$, $u_\gamma=0.66\hbar cQ^4$, $Q=k_BT/(\hbar c)$ and $n_B=\Omega_B\rho_c/m_p$, eliminate $Q$ using $u_\gamma=\Omega_\gamma\rho_cc^2$. The prefactor is $0.24/0.66^{3/4}=0.328$. Here $\rho_c$ is the <mass-equivalent cosmological critical density>; inserting an energy-density critical value without its $c^2$ conversion would give incorrect dimensions. A numerical estimate needs photon and baryon density parameters as additional inputs.

= Cosmological baryon density
{parent=Baryon-to-photon ratio}
{title2=$\rho_b\simeq m_p\eta_b n_\gamma$}

The <cosmological baryon density> is the mass density in baryonic matter, including hydrogen and helium. Up to nuclear binding-energy and composition corrections it is the proton mass times the baryon number density. The <baryon-to-photon ratio> links that number density to the <photon number density>. Larger baryon density increases nuclear reaction rates and permits the <deuterium bottleneck> to end earlier.

= Baryon-to-photon dilution by entropy injection
{title2=$\eta^+/\eta^-=(1+\rho_\chi/\rho_\gamma)^{-3/4}$}
{parent=Baryon-to-photon ratio}

Thermal <photon number density> is proportional to $T^3$. A sudden photon heating factor $(1+r)^{1/4}$ therefore raises photon number by $(1+r)^{3/4}$ and dilutes the <baryon-to-photon ratio> by its inverse when baryon number is conserved. Comparing <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> and <cosmic microwave background> baryon-density determinations can test entropy injection between the two epochs, subject to changes in the expansion rate and nuclear abundances.

= Cosmological entropy conservation
{parent=Cosmology}

For an adiabatically expanding homogeneous universe, the <entropy density> satisfies
$$
\nabla_\mu(su^\mu)=0,
\qquad
\dot s+3Hs=0,
$$
so the entropy $sa^3$ in a comoving volume is constant.

= Radiation entropy from the first law
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}

For $u=AT^4$, $P=u/3$ and physical cell volume $R^3$, the first law integrates to $S=4uR^3/(3T)$ with zero chemical potentials and a fixed relativistic species set. Entropy conservation through a species threshold instead requires tracking $g_{*s}T^3R^3$.

= Relic cooling across muon annihilation
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}
{title2=$T_H/T_\gamma=(43/57)^{1/3}$}

A relativistic relic that decouples between pion and muon annihilation keeps $aT_H$ constant. The coupled bath initially contains photons, electron-positron pairs, muon-antimuon pairs and three neutrino-antineutrino species, with entropy degrees of freedom $57/4$. After muon annihilation this is $43/4$, giving the displayed temperature ratio. After <neutrino decoupling>, electron-positron annihilation heats photons by $(11/4)^{1/3}$, so the final relic-to-photon ratio becomes $(172/627)^{1/3}$. This counting assumes the species are relativistic when counted and treats annihilations and decoupling as sharp equilibrium transitions.

= Separately conserved visible and dark entropy
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}
{title2=$\xi=s_{\rm SM}/s_\phi$}

After <thermal decoupling in cosmology>, two homogeneous sectors that evolve adiabatically without entropy injection satisfy $s_{\rm SM}a^3=\mathrm{constant}$ and $s_\phi a^3=\mathrm{constant}$, hence their <entropy density> ratio $\xi$ is fixed. At a common relativistic <temperature> before decoupling, $\xi=g_{*s}^{\rm SM}/g_{*s}^\phi$. Internal annihilations can change each sector's <temperature> and effective entropy degrees of freedom without changing its total comoving <entropy>.

= Instantaneous photon heating by a decaying relic
{title2=$T_\gamma^+/T_\gamma^-=(1+\rho_\chi/\rho_\gamma)^{1/4}$}
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}

If a nonrelativistic relic decays entirely into promptly thermalized photons, energy conservation at fixed <scale factor> gives $\rho_\gamma^+=\rho_\gamma^-+\rho_\chi^-$. Since thermal photons have $\rho_\gamma\propto T_\gamma^4$, their temperature rises by the displayed factor while an already decoupled <cosmic neutrino background> is not heated. Before decay the relic-to-photon density ratio grows as $a$.

= Effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom
{title2=$g_{*s}$}
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}

= Effective number of relativistic entropy degrees of freedom
{synonym}

The effective number of relativistic entropy degrees of freedom is defined by
$$
s=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_{*s}T^3.
$$
A relativistic bosonic degree of freedom contributes one, while a relativistic fermionic degree of freedom contributes $7/8$ when all species share a temperature.

= Relativistic entropy content of the Standard Model
{parent=Effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom}
{title2=$g_{*s}^{\mathrm{SM}}=28+\frac78\,90=106.75$}

In the ideal-gas high-temperature limit, the <Standard Model> has 28 <boson> states: 16 <gluon> states, eight electroweak <gauge boson> states and four <Higgs field> states. Its 90 <fermion> states are 72 <quark> states, 12 charged <lepton> states and six <neutrino> states. Counting a relativistic <fermion> state with weight $7/8$ gives the displayed <effective number of relativistic entropy degrees of freedom>. The <effective number of relativistic energy degrees of freedom> has the same value when all species share a temperature. Mass thresholds and interactions change the ideal-gas count.

= Thermal decoupling in cosmology
{parent=Cosmological entropy conservation}

A particle species thermally decouples when its interaction rate falls below the <Hubble parameter>. Its collisionless relativistic distribution subsequently redshifts with temperature proportional to $a^{-1}$, while annihilations among still-coupled species can heat the remaining plasma.

= Fractional entropy of a relativistically decoupled species
{parent=Thermal decoupling in cosmology}
{title2=$s_X/s_{\mathrm{total}}=g_{*s,X}/(g_{*s,X}+g_{*s,\mathrm{bath},D})$}

If a species and the remaining bath share a temperature at decoupling, their initial entropy ratio is the ratio of their entropy-weighted degrees of freedom. Subsequent conservation of each sector's comoving <entropy> keeps that fraction constant, even when the bath heats relative to the relic. For a two-state neutrino species leaving an ideal Standard Model bath, the fraction is $(7/4)/(106.75+7/4)\simeq1.61\%$.

= Conserved cosmological relic abundance
{parent=Thermal decoupling in cosmology}
{title2=$Y=n/s$}

If relic particle number and reference-gas <entropy> are separately conserved in a <comoving volume>, their ratio $Y=n/s$ is constant. A decay or <entropy> injection can change it. The reference entropy is that of the specified thermal sector, not necessarily the entropy of the decoupled relic itself.

= Density bound on a relativistically decoupled massive relic
{parent=Conserved cosmological relic abundance}
{title2=$m_x\lesssim\frac{\Omega_{x,\max}}{\Omega_\gamma}\frac{\langle E_\gamma\rangle}{n_x/n_\gamma}$}

A species freezing out while relativistic retains its thermal comoving number. If it is nonrelativistic today, $\rho_x\simeq m_xn_x$. Comparing with <photons> gives $\Omega_x/\Omega_\gamma=m_x(n_x/n_\gamma)/\langle E_\gamma\rangle$. An upper density fraction therefore bounds its mass inversely with its relic number density. The relation does not apply unchanged to a species whose abundance is depleted by nonrelativistic annihilation.

= Cannibal phase of a decoupled sector
{parent=Thermal decoupling in cosmology}

A massive decoupled sector enters a cannibal phase when its particles are nonrelativistic but rapid number-changing reactions maintain <chemical equilibrium> and <zero chemical potential from number-changing reactions>. Rest-mass energy released by $3\to2$ reactions heats the remaining particles, so the sector cools much more slowly than an isolated number-conserving nonrelativistic gas. The temperature laws follow directly from <cosmological entropy conservation>. An example is discussed in https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04219[Cannibal Dark Matter].

= Logarithmic cooling during cannibalism
{parent=Cannibal phase of a decoupled sector}
{title2=$T_\phi\sim m/[3\log a]$}

The <nonrelativistic entropy at zero chemical potential> and $s_\phi a^3=\mathrm{constant}$ give $x+\frac12\log x=3\log a+\mathrm{constant}$. Using this leading nonrelativistic approximation, differentiation yields
$$
\frac{d\log T_\phi}{d\log a}\simeq-\frac3{x+1/2}.
$$
This is much smaller in magnitude than the relativistic-bath cooling rate $-1$ when $x\gg1$ and its entropy degrees of freedom are fixed. Thus <cannibal phase of a decoupled sector> cooling is logarithmic. Once number-changing reactions freeze out, a number-conserving nonrelativistic gas instead cools as $a^{-2}$ during adiabatic expansion.

= Cannibal-sector temperature ratio
{parent=Cannibal phase of a decoupled sector}
{title2=$T_\gamma/T_\phi$}

For a massive real scalar with one internal degree of freedom with $x=m/T_\phi\gg1$, fixed <separately conserved visible and dark entropy> ratio $\xi$, and relativistic visible bath with entropy degrees of freedom $g_{*s}^{\rm SM}$,
$$
\frac{T_\gamma}{T_\phi}\sim\left[\frac{45}{2\pi^2(2\pi)^{3/2}}\right]^{1/3}\left(\frac\xi{g_{*s}^{\rm SM}}\right)^{1/3}x^{5/6}e^{-x/3}.
$$
Indeed, equate $s_{\rm SM}=(2\pi^2/45)g_{*s}^{\rm SM}T_\gamma^3$ to $\xi s_\phi$ and use <nonrelativistic entropy at zero chemical potential>. For $g$ internal states the right side gains $g^{1/3}$. The nonrelativistic species cannot be assigned a constant relativistic entropy count.

= Kinetic decoupling
{parent=Thermal decoupling in cosmology}

Kinetic decoupling occurs when momentum-exchange collisions cease to maintain the <temperature> and <velocity dispersion> of a particle species in equilibrium with a surrounding bath. Afterwards, a freely moving nonrelativistic particle has momentum proportional to $a^{-1}$ and effective kinetic <temperature> proportional to $a^{-2}$, where $a$ is the <scale factor>.

= Temperature of a decoupled relativistic relic
{parent=Thermal decoupling in cosmology}

If a relativistic species decouples when the coupled plasma has $g_{*s,\mathrm{dec}}$ entropy degrees of freedom, then after the plasma reaches $g_{*s}$ degrees of freedom,
$$
\frac{T_{\mathrm{relic}}}{T}
=\left(\frac{g_{*s}}{g_{*s,\mathrm{dec}}}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
The ratio follows from separate redshifting of the decoupled species and <cosmological entropy conservation> in the coupled plasma.

= Two-stage entropy dilution of an early-decoupled neutrino
{parent=Temperature of a decoupled relativistic relic}
{title2=$T_\nu/T_\gamma=[43/(11g_d)]^{1/3}$}

An extra relativistic <neutrino> that decouples before ordinary <neutrinos> misses two stages of plasma heating. Until ordinary decoupling, entropy conservation gives a temperature ratio $(43/(4g_d))^{1/3}$. Electron-positron annihilation then heats <photons> but neither <neutrino> population, multiplying the ratio by $(4/11)^{1/3}$. Here $g_d$ counts the coupled bath at the extra species' decoupling, with no subsequent exotic entropy injection.

The cosmic neutrino background is the relic population of neutrinos that thermally decoupled before electron-positron annihilation. Entropy transfer to photons gives

= Number density of a relativistic neutrino relative to photons

= Relativistic-neutrino number-density ratio
{synonym}

With negligible <chemical potential>, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> has $3/4$ of the boson number density per degree of freedom at equal temperature. A species with $g_\nu$ populated states therefore has $n_\nu/n_\gamma=(3g_\nu/8)(T_\nu/T_\gamma)^3$, since <photons> have two states. This remains the number-density ratio after the <neutrino> becomes massive if its comoving particle number is conserved.

= Relic-neutrino energy density
{parent=Cosmic neutrino background}

For one relic-neutrino species with two internal states, physical momentum $p$, comoving temperature $T_\nu$, and mass $m_\nu$,
$$
\rho_\nu=\frac1{\pi^2}\int_0^\infty
\frac{p^2\sqrt{p^2+m_\nu^2}}{e^{ap/T_\nu}+1}\,dp.
$$
It approaches $7\pi^2(T_\nu/a)^4/120$ while relativistic and $n_\nu m_\nu$ after becoming nonrelativistic.

= Critical-density mass of a thermal relic neutrino
{parent=Relic-neutrino energy density}
{title2=$m_\nu=\frac{11\pi}{16\zeta(3)}H_0^2M_P^2/T_{\gamma0}^3$}

A standard thermal <cosmic neutrino background> with one populated <neutrino> and one <antineutrino> helicity per species has $n_\nu+n_{\bar\nu}=3n_\gamma/11$ after <electron-positron annihilation in cosmology>. If this species is nonrelativistic today and dominates a spatially flat matter universe, equate $m_\nu(n_\nu+n_{\bar\nu})$ to the <critical density> $3H_0^2M_P^2/(8\pi)$, using the unreduced <Planck mass>. The <photon number density> gives the displayed conditional mass. Extra populated states or several massive species change the abundance count; vacuum-dominated cosmology changes the density available for matter.

= Critical-density bound on the sum of relic neutrino masses
{parent=Critical-density mass of a thermal relic neutrino}
{title2=$\sum_i m_i\le\rho_c/(n_{\nu_i}+n_{\bar\nu_i})$}

For three standard thermal relic mass eigenstates, each neutrino-antineutrino pair has <number density> $3n_\gamma/11$ after pair annihilation. Their rest-mass density is this common per-species abundance times the mass sum. Requiring it not to exceed the near-critical total density gives the displayed conservative bound. Dividing instead by the total abundance over three species bounds the mean mass. With $T_{\gamma0}=2.725$ K and $H_0=72$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$ the instantaneous-decoupling estimate is about $49$ eV/$c^2$ for the sum, conditional on standard abundances and nonnegative other energy components.

= Late-time relic-neutrino speed
{parent=Relic-neutrino energy density}

Expanding the <relic-neutrino energy density> at $T_\nu/a\ll m_\nu$ gives
$$
\langle v^2\rangle^{1/2}\simeq
\sqrt{\frac{15\zeta(5)}{\zeta(3)}}\frac{T_\nu}{am_\nu}.
$$
The thermal speed decreases as $a^{-1}$ after the neutrinos become nonrelativistic.

= Cosmic neutrino background last-scattering surface
{parent=Cosmic neutrino background}

The cosmic neutrino background last-scattering surface is the locus from which relic neutrinos that reach an observer underwent their final appreciable scattering. Although <neutrino decoupling> occurs before <photon decoupling>, a massive neutrino travels slower than light after becoming nonrelativistic, so its last-scattering surface need not be farther away than the <cosmic microwave background> last-scattering surface.

= Neutrino decoupling
{parent=Cosmic neutrino background}
{wiki}

Neutrino decoupling occurs when weak interactions become slower than cosmic expansion. The neutrino distribution then free streams and cools as $T_\nu\propto a^{-1}$.

= Electron-positron entropy transfer to photons
{parent=Neutrino decoupling}

Instantaneous neutrino decoupling followed by electron-positron annihilation conserves electromagnetic entropy. The electromagnetic entropy count falls from $11/2$ to $2$, giving $T_\nu/T_\gamma=(4/11)^{1/3}$ after annihilation.

= Monotonic photon cooling during pair annihilation
{parent=Electron-positron entropy transfer to photons}
{title2=$d\ln T_\gamma/d\ln a=-3/[3+d\ln g_{*s}/d\ln T_\gamma]$}

Electromagnetic comoving <entropy> conservation gives $a^3g_{*s}(T_\gamma)T_\gamma^3$ constant. Differentiation proves the displayed relation. During equilibrium electron-positron annihilation, $g_{*s}$ increases with <temperature>, so the absolute <photon> <temperature> still falls as space expands. The comoving <temperature> $aT_\gamma$ rises, and the asymptotic relativistic-to-pair-free <entropy> count heats <photons> relative to decoupled <neutrinos> by $(11/4)^{1/3}$. This relative heating must not be mistaken for an absolute <temperature> rise along the smooth expanding history.

= Weak-decoupling temperature estimate
{parent=Neutrino decoupling}
{title2=$T_d\sim[1.66\sqrt{g_*}/(G_F^2M_P)]^{1/3}$}

In <natural units> with unreduced <Planck mass> $M_P=G^{-1/2}$, the radiation-era <Hubble parameter> scales as $\sqrt{g_*}T^2/M_P$, while a low-energy <weak interaction> rate scales as $G_F^2T^5$. Their equality gives a decoupling scale of order one MeV. Distinct weak processes have distinct rate coefficients: <neutrino decoupling> and <cosmological weak freeze-out> of neutron-proton conversion are nearby events, not an identical precise temperature inferred from the power laws alone.

= Temperature ratio of an early-decoupled neutrino
{parent=Neutrino decoupling}
{title2=$T_X/T_\gamma=[43/(11g_{*s,D})]^{1/3}$}

A relativistic species decoupling before ordinary <neutrino decoupling> cools as $T_X\propto a^{-1}$. <Cosmological entropy conservation> in the remaining bath gives $T_X/T_\nu=[10.75/g_{*s,D}]^{1/3}$, where $g_{*s,D}$ counts that bath just after the early species has left it. Subsequent <electron-positron annihilation in cosmology> gives $T_\nu/T_\gamma=(4/11)^{1/3}$. For the ideal high-temperature <relativistic entropy content of the Standard Model>, the resulting ratio is about $0.332$.

= Electron-positron annihilation in cosmology
{parent=Neutrino decoupling}

After neutrino decoupling, electron-positron annihilation transfers entropy to photons but not to neutrinos. Comparing the electromagnetic entropy degrees of freedom before and after annihilation gives $T_\nu/T_\gamma=(4/11)^{1/3}$.

= Electron-positron equilibrium pair abundance below the electron mass
{parent=Electron-positron annihilation in cosmology}

For $T\ll m_e$ and negligible chemical potentials, the combined equilibrium density of electrons and positrons divided by the <photon number density> is
$$
\frac{n_{e^-}+n_{e^+}}{n_\gamma}
=\frac{2\pi^2}{\zeta(3)}
\left(\frac{m_e}{2\pi T}\right)^{3/2}e^{-m_e/T}.
$$
The exponential suppression eventually makes pair equilibrium incompatible with the residual electrons required by <charge neutrality>.

= Neutrino degeneracy parameter
{title2=$\xi_\nu$}
{parent=Cosmic neutrino background}

The neutrino degeneracy parameter is the dimensionless chemical potential
$$
\xi_\nu=\frac{\mu_\nu}{T_\nu}.
$$
In thermal equilibrium the corresponding antineutrino has chemical potential $-\mu_\nu$.

= Critical-density bound on massless neutrino degeneracy
{parent=Neutrino degeneracy parameter}
{title2=$|\xi_\nu|\lesssim[8\pi^2\rho_{\rm crit}/T_{\nu,0}^4]^{1/4}$}

Combining the <degenerate neutrino and antineutrino energy density> with a <critical density> bound constrains the <neutrino degeneracy parameter> through the fourth root of the available energy budget. The numerical bound depends on $T_{\nu,0}/T_{\gamma,0}$ and the number of degenerate flavors. The standard ratio $(4/11)^{1/3}$ assumes <neutrino decoupling> before <electron-positron annihilation in cosmology>; it should be stated as a thermal-history assumption if the degeneracy is very large. The massless formula is inappropriate for a species that is nonrelativistic today.

= Degenerate neutrino and antineutrino energy density
{parent=Neutrino degeneracy parameter}
{title2=$\rho_\nu+\rho_{\bar\nu}\simeq|\mu_\nu|^4/(8\pi^2)$}

For one massless <neutrino> flavor with one helicity per member and $|\mu_\nu|\gg T_\nu$, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> fills momentum states up to the positive <chemical potential>. If $\mu_\nu>0$, the <neutrino> density is $\mu_\nu^4/(8\pi^2)$ and the <antineutrino> contribution is approximately $3T_\nu^4e^{-\mu_\nu/T_\nu}/\pi^2$. For negative <chemical potential> the roles reverse. Thus the leading total <energy density> is even in $\mu_\nu$ and has no factor of two from the two species. Extra populated internal degrees of freedom do multiply it. This distinction prevents using a positive-degeneracy approximation for an exponentially dilute negative-chemical-potential distribution.

= Degenerate relic neutrino
{parent=Neutrino degeneracy parameter}

A degenerate relic neutrino has $\xi_\nu\gg1$. In the ultrarelativistic limit its <Fermi-Dirac distribution> approaches a filled momentum sphere, giving for one internal state
$$
\rho_\nu\simeq\frac{\mu_\nu^4}{8\pi^2},
$$
while the antineutrino density is exponentially suppressed.

= Cosmological lepton asymmetry
{parent=Neutrino degeneracy parameter}

A nonzero neutrino chemical potential produces unequal neutrino and antineutrino densities and hence a cosmological lepton asymmetry. It changes the radiation density, and an electron-neutrino asymmetry also changes neutron--proton weak equilibrium during <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>.

= Effective number of neutrino species
{title2=$N_{\rm eff}$}
{parent=Cosmic neutrino background}
{wiki}

The effective number of neutrino species parametrizes radiation beyond photons through
$$
\rho_r=\frac{\pi^2}{30}\left[2+\frac78\,2
\left(\frac4{11}\right)^{4/3}N_{\rm eff}\right]T_\gamma^4.
$$

= Contribution of a decoupled real scalar to Neff
{title2=$\Delta N_{\rm eff}$}
{parent=Effective number of neutrino species}

If a real relativistic scalar decouples when the other coupled particles have entropy degrees of freedom $g_{*s}(T_d)$, then
$$
\Delta N_{\rm eff}=\frac47
\left(\frac{43}{4g_{*s}(T_d)}\right)^{4/3}.
$$
Earlier decoupling and the subsequent annihilation of more coupled species make the scalar colder and suppress its contribution.

= Field theory in cosmology
{parent=Cosmology}

Field theory in cosmology applies classical and quantum fields to the homogeneous universe and its perturbations.

= Canonical rescaling of a scalar cosmological perturbation
{parent=Field theory in cosmology}
{title2=$v=a\delta\chi$}

Neglecting <metric perturbations>, a free massive <scalar field> fluctuation obeys $\delta\chi''+2\mathcal H\delta\chi'-\nabla^2\delta\chi+a^2m^2\delta\chi=0$. Rescaling it by the <scale factor> removes the conformal-time friction and gives $v''-\nabla^2v+(a^2m^2-a''/a)v=0$. Growth of $v$ need not mean growth of the physical fluctuation $v/a$.

= Massive de Sitter superhorizon scalar mode
{parent=Canonical rescaling of a scalar cosmological perturbation}
{title2=$\nu=\sqrt{9/4-m^2/H^2}$}

In the <de Sitter approximation>, a canonically rescaled scalar has superhorizon powers $(-\tau)^{1/2\pm\nu}$. The physical field has powers $(-\tau)^{3/2\pm\nu}$. For $H^2<m^2<2H^2$, both physical branches decay, while the slower rescaled branch grows. The generic late-time <dimensionless power spectrum> is proportional to $(k/(aH))^{3-2\nu}$.

= K-essence
{parent=Field theory in cosmology}

<K-essence> is a cosmological scalar-field mechanism in which noncanonical kinetic dynamics supply negative pressure or accelerated expansion. Its action is a particular use of a <P(X, phi) scalar field theory>; that general action also includes theories without kinetic dark energy.

= P(X, phi) scalar field theory
{title2=$P(X,\phi)$ theory}
{c}
{parent=Field theory in cosmology}

A $P(X,\phi)$ scalar theory has Lagrangian density $P$ depending on the field and $X=-g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\partial_\nu\phi/2$. It includes canonical scalar theory and models with noncanonical kinetic interactions.

A <k-essence> model uses noncanonical kinetic terms for cosmological acceleration; a general <P(X, phi) scalar field theory> need not have that application.

= Sound speed of a P(X, phi) scalar perturbation
{title2=$c_s^2$}
{c}
{parent=P(X, phi) scalar field theory}

On a homogeneous background with $X=\bar X$, a $P(X,\phi)$ perturbation has sound speed
$$
c_s^2=\frac{P_X}{P_X+2XP_{XX}}.
$$
The denominator is its time-kinetic coefficient and $P_X$ is its spatial-gradient coefficient.

= Noncanonical scalar kinetic stability
{parent=Sound speed of a P(X, phi) scalar perturbation}
{title2=$P_{,X}+2XP_{,XX}>0,\quad P_{,X}>0$}

For $X=\partial_\mu\phi\partial^\mu\phi/2$ in signature $(+---)$, the derivative part of the quadratic action on a timelike background is $\tfrac12(P_{,X}+2XP_{,XX})\delta\dot\phi^2-\tfrac12P_{,X}a^{-2}(\nabla\delta\phi)^2$. Absence of a ghost requires $P_{,X}+2XP_{,XX}>0$; positive gradient energy requires $P_{,X}>0$. The squared sound speed is their ratio $P_{,X}/(P_{,X}+2XP_{,XX})$.

= Cubic curvature action for a P(X, phi) scalar field
{parent=P(X, phi) scalar field theory}

In the limit where <scalar field> fluctuations dominate metric fluctuations, put $\zeta=-H\delta\phi/\dot{\bar\phi}$ and neglect derivatives of slowly varying background coefficients. Splitting $\delta X=\delta X_1+\delta X_2$ gives
$$
\delta X_1=-\frac{2\bar X}{H}\dot\zeta,\qquad
\delta X_2=\frac{\bar X}{H^2}\left(\dot\zeta^2-a^{-2}(\partial_i\zeta)^2\right).
$$
The cubic terms $P_{XX}\delta X_1\delta X_2+P_{XXX}\delta X_1^3/6$ therefore yield
$$
S_3=\int dt\,d^3x\,\frac{a^3\epsilon(1-c_s^2)}{Hc_s^2}
\left[a^{-2}\dot\zeta(\partial_i\zeta)^2+\mathcal A\dot\zeta^3\right],
\qquad
\mathcal A=-1-\frac{2\bar X P_{XXX}}{3P_{XX}}.
$$
The expression before factoring remains well-defined when $P_{XX}=0$. These interactions can generate substantial <primordial non-Gaussianity> when the <sound speed of a P(X, phi) scalar perturbation> is small.

= Derivative-cubic coefficient of a noncanonical scalar
{parent=Cubic curvature action for a P(X, phi) scalar field}
{title2=$C=-\frac{a^3}{H^3}(2\bar X^2P_{,XX}+\frac43\bar X^3P_{,XXX})$}

Neglect metric fluctuations and derivatives of $\dot{\bar\phi}/H$, so $\delta X_1=-2\bar X\dot\zeta/H$ and $\delta X_2=\bar X[\dot\zeta^2-a^{-2}(\nabla\zeta)^2]/H^2$. The coefficient of $\dot\zeta^3$ in the cosmic-time action is $C=-a^3[2\bar X^2P_{,XX}+(4/3)\bar X^3P_{,XXX}]/H^3$. This unfactored form is valid even when $P_{,XX}=0$ and a factored ratio would be undefined.

= ADM momentum constraint for a P(X, phi) scalar field
{title2=ADM momentum constraint for a $P(X,\phi)$ scalar field}
{c}
{parent=P(X, phi) scalar field theory}

Varying the shift in the <3+1 decomposition of spacetime> gives a nondynamical momentum constraint. For a $P(X,\phi)$ field it equates the spatial divergence of $K_i{}^j-\delta_i^jK$ to the scalar momentum density.

= Primordial non-Gaussianity
{parent=Field theory in cosmology}

Primordial non-Gaussianity is any departure of the primordial perturbations from Gaussian random fields. Cubic interactions generate a nonzero three-point correlation function at leading order.

This is <non-Gaussianity> of primordial cosmological perturbations rather than non-Gaussian statistics of an arbitrary physical measurement.

= Local-type primordial non-Gaussianity
{parent=Primordial non-Gaussianity}
{title2=$f_{\rm NL}$}

A local quadratic departure from a <Gaussian random field> is
$$
\varphi(\mathbf x)=\varphi_G(\mathbf x)+f_{\rm NL}\bigl(\varphi_G(\mathbf x)^2-\langle\varphi_G^2\rangle\bigr).
$$
The subtraction removes the homogeneous mean. At leading order, <Wick theorem> gives the <primordial bispectrum> $B_\varphi=2f_{\rm NL}[P_\varphi(k_1)P_\varphi(k_2)+\text{cyclic terms}]$. The numerical convention depends on which potential or <comoving curvature perturbation> is used.

= Loop correction to the local primordial bispectrum
{parent=Local-type primordial non-Gaussianity}
{title2=$B_{\mathrm{loop}}=O(f_{\mathrm{NL}}^3)$}

The exact <centered quadratic Gaussian transformation> has a connected three-quadratic-vertex contribution $8\alpha^3\int d^3q\,P(q)P(|\mathbf k_1-\mathbf q|)P(|\mathbf k_2+\mathbf q|)/(2\pi)^3$. It is higher order than the usual tree-level local <primordial bispectrum>. Infrared or ultraviolet regularization may be needed.

= Primordial contribution to the matter bispectrum
{parent=Local-type primordial non-Gaussianity}
{title2=$B_m^{\rm prim}$}

If the linear <density contrast> obeys $\delta(\mathbf k)=\alpha(k)\varphi(\mathbf k)$, then <local-type primordial non-Gaussianity> contributes
$$
B_m^{\rm prim}=2f_{\rm NL}\left[\frac{\alpha_3}{\alpha_1\alpha_2}P_m(k_1)P_m(k_2)+\text{cyclic terms}\right].
$$
In a <squeezed bispectrum configuration> and for a nearly scale-invariant potential, the leading term is $4f_{\rm NL}P_m(k_L)P_m(k_S)/\alpha(k_L)$. This excludes the <standard perturbation theory in cosmology> contribution from nonlinear gravity.

= Short-scale variance modulation by local non-Gaussianity
{parent=Local-type primordial non-Gaussianity}

For a long potential approximately constant over the short-scale region, <local-type primordial non-Gaussianity> gives $\varphi_s=(1+2f_{\rm NL}\varphi_{G,L})\varphi_{G,s}$, to first order in the long mode and ignoring short-mode squares. A linear <cosmological Poisson equation> therefore gives
$$
\sigma_s^2=\sigma_{G,s}^2(1+4f_{\rm NL}\varphi_{G,L})
$$
to the same order. The <variance> is conditional on the long mode.

= Primordial bispectrum
{title2=$B(k_1,k_2,k_3)$}
{parent=Primordial non-Gaussianity}

Statistical homogeneity defines the primordial bispectrum by
$$
\langle\phi_{\mathbf k_1}\phi_{\mathbf k_2}\phi_{\mathbf k_3}\rangle
=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2+\mathbf k_3)
B(k_1,k_2,k_3).
$$

A <bispectrum> is a Fourier-space third-order statistic; primordial perturbations supply the cosmological field in this instance.

= Spatial-gradient cubic curvature bispectrum
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$H_{\rm int}=g\int d^3x\,a\zeta(\partial\zeta)^2$}

With cosmic-time interaction coefficient $g$, constant $H,\epsilon,c_s$, and modes whose positive-frequency part is $H(1+ikc_s\tau)e^{-ikc_s\tau}/\sqrt{4\epsilon c_sk^3}$ in units $M_p=1$, two <Wick contractions> per differentiated pair and the <late-time scalar gradient vertex integral> give
$$
B=-\frac{gH^4}{16\epsilon^3c_s^2\prod_i k_i^3}\left(\sum_{i<j}\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf k_j\right)\left[-K+\frac{s_2}{K}+\frac{s_3}{K^2}\right].
$$
Here $\langle\zeta^3\rangle_c=(2\pi)^3\delta^3(\sum\mathbf k_i)B$. The extra factor $a$ in $dt=a\,d\tau$ must be included. A positive $g$ yields a negative bispectrum in an <equilateral bispectrum configuration>; changing the sign of the interaction changes the sign of $B$.

= Curvature bispectrum from a zeta zeta-prime-squared interaction
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$B_{\zeta\zeta\prime^2}$}

For $H_{\mathrm{int}}=-M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2\int d^3x\,a^3\epsilon^2\zeta\dot\zeta^2$ and the standard Gaussian de Sitter modes, the tree <in-in formalism> gives $B=H^4\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}k_2^2k_3^2(K^{-1}+k_1K^{-2})/[16\epsilon M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^4(k_1k_2k_3)^3]$, where $K=\sum_i k_i$. The result includes six connected <Wick contractions> and a vacuum contour.

= Planck normalization of a cubic curvature interaction
{c}
{parent=Curvature bispectrum from a zeta zeta-prime-squared interaction}
{title2=$H_{\mathrm{int}}\propto M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2a^3\epsilon^2\zeta\dot\zeta^2$}

The <comoving curvature perturbation> is dimensionless. With spatial coordinates and time carrying inverse mass dimensions, the cubic interaction $\int dt\,d^3x\,a^3\epsilon^2\zeta\dot\zeta^2$ needs a mass-squared coefficient. Its standard gravitational normalization is $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2$. Six <de Sitter curvature mode functions> supply $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{-6}$; this vertex then supplies $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2$, leaving the physical $M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{-4}$ curvature <primordial bispectrum>. The <Wick-pairing multiplicity for a zeta zeta-prime-squared vertex> is independently six: three choices of undifferentiated external leg times two differentiated-leg assignments.

= Slow-roll amplitude of the squeezed zeta zeta-prime-squared bispectrum
{parent=Curvature bispectrum from a zeta zeta-prime-squared interaction}
{title2=$f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{\mathrm{effective}}=5\epsilon/24$}

The undifferentiated soft-leg permutation gives $B(q,k,k)\simeq(\epsilon/2)P(q)P(k)$ as $q/k\to0$. It retains a squeezed $q^{-3}$ enhancement, but corresponds to local amplitude $f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{\mathrm{effective}}=5\epsilon/24$. Soft momentum placed only at derivative slots would miss this dominant term.

= Wick-pairing multiplicity for a zeta zeta-prime-squared vertex
{parent=Curvature bispectrum from a zeta zeta-prime-squared interaction}
{c}
{title2=$3\times2=6$}

Three external fields can be joined to the three cubic vertex fields in six connected <Wick contractions>. There are three choices for the external field at the undifferentiated slot and two exchanges of the differentiated slots. Three cyclic terms therefore require an explicit factor of two. Ordered contractions with an earlier vertex use complex-conjugated mode functions.

= Constant primordial bispectrum
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$B\propto(k_1k_2k_3)^{-2}$}

A constant <primordial bispectrum> means a constant <bispectrum shape function>, not a bispectrum independent of wavenumber. With $P(k)=2\pi^2\Delta_\zeta^2/k^3$ and scale-independent $\Delta_\zeta^2$, one normalization is $B=f_{\rm NL}[P(k_1)P(k_2)P(k_3)]^{2/3}=4\pi^4f_{\rm NL}\Delta_\zeta^4/(k_1k_2k_3)^2$.

= Flattened bispectrum configuration
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$k_3=k_1+k_2$}

= Folded bispectrum configuration
{synonym}

A flattened <primordial bispectrum> configuration lies at the collinear boundary of the allowed momentum-triangle domain. For a <cubic time-derivative curvature bispectrum>, it is finite rather than singular. Its equal-short-side value $k_1=k_2=k_3/2$ gives $k_1k_2k_3/(k_1+k_2+k_3)^3=1/32$, compared with $1/27$ at an <equilateral bispectrum configuration>.

= Equilateral bispectrum configuration
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$k_1=k_2=k_3$}

An equilateral <primordial bispectrum> configuration has equal external wavenumbers. For a <cubic time-derivative curvature bispectrum>, $S\propto k_1k_2k_3/(k_1+k_2+k_3)^3$ is maximized here at fixed perimeter by the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality>.

= Primordial bispectrum shape function
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$S(k_1,k_2,k_3)\propto(k_1k_2k_3)^2B(k_1,k_2,k_3)$}

= Bispectrum shape function
{synonym}

For a scale-invariant <primordial bispectrum>, the displayed shape function is invariant under common rescaling of all three wavenumbers. It isolates triangle dependence from the overall <power spectrum> amplitude. It indicates intrinsic shape preference but alone does not determine observational signal-to-noise, which also depends on transfer functions, <covariance> and survey weights.

= Cubic time-derivative curvature bispectrum
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$H_{\rm int}\propto\dot\zeta^3$}

Put $C=\epsilon+1-c_s^2$. For constant $H,\epsilon,c_s>0$ and cubic interaction $H_{\rm int}=-M_{\rm Pl}^2\int d^3x\,a^3\epsilon C\dot\zeta^3/(Hc_s^2)$, the <Bunch-Davies vacuum> modes $u_k=H(1+ikc_s\tau)e^{-ikc_s\tau}/\sqrt{4\epsilon M_{\rm Pl}^2c_sk^3}$ give
$$
B(k_1,k_2,k_3)=\frac{3H^4C}{8\epsilon^2M_{\rm Pl}^4c_s^2k_1k_2k_3(k_1+k_2+k_3)^3}.
$$
To derive this <primordial bispectrum>, transform $dt=a\,d\tau$, $\dot\zeta=\zeta'/a$, use the six connected <Wick contractions>, and integrate $\int_{-\infty(1-i0)}^0\tau^2e^{ic_s(k_1+k_2+k_3)\tau}\,d\tau=2i/[c_s(k_1+k_2+k_3)]^3$ in the <in-in formalism>. The normalized equilateral amplitude is $B(k,k,k)/P(k)^2=2C/9$; raw inverse powers of $c_s$ must not be confused with normalized <primordial non-Gaussianity>.

= Unit-speed cubic time-derivative bispectrum
{parent=Cubic time-derivative curvature bispectrum}
{title2=$B=3H^4\mathcal F/(8\epsilon^2k_1k_2k_3K^3)$}

For $H_{\rm int}=-\int d^3x\,a^3\epsilon\mathcal F\dot\zeta^3/H$, $a=-1/(H\tau)$ and stipulated modes $u_k=H(1+ik\tau)e^{-ik\tau}/\sqrt{4\epsilon k^3}$, six connected <Wick contractions> and $\int_{-\infty(1-i0)}^0\tau^2e^{iK\tau}d\tau=2i/K^3$ yield $B=3H^4\mathcal F/(8\epsilon^2k_1k_2k_3K^3)$. Here $\langle\zeta^3\rangle_c=(2\pi)^3\delta(\sum\mathbf k_i)B$. Using unstarred vertex modes with the external-before-vertex prefactor incorrectly reverses the sign. Non-unit sound speed also changes the quadratic modes, so this result is conditional on the specified unit-speed modes.

= Squeezed bispectrum configuration
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}
{title2=$k_L\ll k_S$}

A <primordial bispectrum> is squeezed when one external <Fourier mode> has much smaller <wavenumber> than the other two. Momentum conservation then makes the two short wavevectors almost opposite. This configuration measures the response of short-scale correlations to a long perturbation.

= Bispectrum from a time-derivative cubic scalar interaction
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}

For a massless scalar in <de Sitter spacetime> with interaction $S_{\rm int}=(\lambda/3!)\int dt\,d^3x\,a^3\dot\phi^2\phi$, the tree-level late-time <primordial bispectrum> is
$$
B=\frac{\lambda H^4}{12k_1^3k_2^3k_3^3K^2}
\sum_{\rm cyclic}k_j^2k_l^2(K+k_i),
\qquad K=k_1+k_2+k_3.
$$
It scales as $B(\mu k_1,\mu k_2,\mu k_3)=\mu^{-6}B(k_1,k_2,k_3)$.

= Shift-symmetry soft theorem for a scalar bispectrum
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}

If a scalar vacuum is invariant under an exact <scalar-field shift symmetry>, the Ward identity makes the leading soft bispectrum vanish:
$$
\lim_{q\to0}\frac{B_\phi(q,k,|\mathbf k+\mathbf q|)}{P_\phi(q)}=0.
$$

= Single-field inflation squeezed-limit consistency relation
{parent=Primordial bispectrum}

For a single-clock adiabatic inflationary background, a long <comoving curvature perturbation> acts on short modes as a spatial dilation. The squeezed bispectrum therefore obeys
$$
\lim_{q\to0}B_{\mathcal R}(q,k,|\mathbf k+\mathbf q|)
=-(n_s-1)P_{\mathcal R}(q)P_{\mathcal R}(k)
$$
at leading order in $q/k$.

= Absence of primordial scale-dependent halo bias in single-clock inflation
{parent=Single-field inflation squeezed-limit consistency relation}

In an attractor with one adiabatic clock, the leading squeezed <primordial bispectrum> is the response to a coordinate dilation. Holding a halo's physical mass and smoothing scale fixed cancels this dilation in its abundance response. Thus the consistency-relation term does not generate the physical $k^{-2}$ <scale-dependent halo bias from local non-Gaussianity>. For example, a <top-hat filter> of fixed physical radius has coordinate radius $R_c=e^{-\zeta_L}R_0$, while the short <density contrast> is $\delta_s(\mathbf x)=\delta_s^{(0)}(e^{\zeta_L}\mathbf x)$. The substitution $\mathbf y=e^{\zeta_L}\mathbf x$ shows that its normalized average over $|\mathbf x|<R_c$ equals the original average over $|\mathbf y|<R_0$. Genuine local effects start at two spatial derivatives of the long mode. Non-attractor backgrounds or nonstandard initial states need not satisfy this argument.

= Primordial trispectrum
{title2=$T(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf k_2,\mathbf k_3,\mathbf k_4)$}
{parent=Primordial non-Gaussianity}

The primordial trispectrum is the connected four-point function with its momentum-conserving delta function removed. Exchange diagrams split naturally into $s$, $t$, and $u$ channels according to which pair of external momenta meets at one cubic vertex.

A <trispectrum> is a Fourier-space connected fourth-order statistic; this entry applies it to primordial cosmological perturbations.

= Parity-odd primordial trispectrum
{parent=Primordial trispectrum}

A parity-odd primordial trispectrum changes sign when all external momenta are reversed. Rotational invariance allows such a four-point structure because four momentum-conserving vectors can form a nonzero scalar triple product.

= Reality of a momentum-space scalar correlator
{parent=Primordial trispectrum}

For a real scalar field, $\phi(\mathbf k)^*=\phi(-\mathbf k)$. Its momentum-space $n$-point function therefore obeys
$$
\langle\phi(\mathbf k_1)\cdots\phi(\mathbf k_n)\rangle^*
=\langle\phi(-\mathbf k_1)\cdots\phi(-\mathbf k_n)\rangle.
$$
A parity-odd scalar correlator is consequently purely imaginary after removing its momentum-conserving delta function.

= Cosmological distance measure
{parent=Cosmology}

A cosmological distance measure converts an observed redshift, angle, flux, or travel time into a distance in an expanding spacetime. Different measures answer different operational questions and therefore need not agree numerically.

= Cosmological look-back time
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{title2=$t_L(z)=\int_0^z d\bar z/[(1+\bar z)H(\bar z)]$}

The look-back time is the cosmic-time interval between emission at <redshift> $z$ and reception today. Differentiating $1+z=R_0/R(t)$ gives $dt/dz=-1/[(1+z)H]$ and hence the displayed <integral>. The radial comoving-distance <integral> instead has denominator $H$ alone; elapsed time and present spatial separation answer different operational questions.

= Proper-motion distance
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{title2=$d_M=v/\dot\theta=(1+z)d_A$}

A source-frame transverse physical speed $v$ produces an observed angular rate $\dot\theta$. <cosmological time dilation> converts source time to observer time, so this operational distance equals $(1+z)$ times the <angular diameter distance>. In an FLRW geometry it is the <transverse comoving distance> $R_0S(\chi)$. It should be derived with the time convention stated, rather than interpreting the source speed and angular rate as measurements made by the same clock.

= Distance duality relation
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{title2=$D_L=(1+z)^2D_A$}

In metric light propagation with <photon> conservation and the usual reciprocity assumptions, <luminosity distance> and <angular diameter distance> satisfy $D_L=(1+z)^2D_A$. Comparing received flux with apparent angular area then gives the bolometric <cosmological surface-brightness dimming> factor $(1+z)^{-4}$. Absorption, <photon> conversion or an inconsistent definition of emitted/observed bands must be treated separately.

= Transverse comoving distance
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{title2=$D_M=(1+z)D_A$}

The distance that converts a small angular separation into a transverse <comoving distance> at a specified <cosmological redshift>. It equals the radial comoving distance in spatially flat geometry; <cosmological spatial curvature> changes that equality. A comoving acoustic ruler has angular size $r_d/D_M$.

= Cosmic distance ladder
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{wiki}

Geometrical distances to nearby calibrators determine the luminosities of indicators that can be observed farther away. Those indicators calibrate still more luminous indicators in turn. A ladder may connect parallax or maser distances to <Cepheid variables> and then to <Type Ia supernovae>. Each step propagates its calibration uncertainty to the final distance scale and the inferred <Hubble constant>.

= Maser-disk geometric distance
{parent=Cosmic distance ladder}

An orbit model for a resolved <maser> disk combines angular positions, Doppler velocities, accelerations and sometimes <proper motions> to infer physical orbital dimensions and distance. For an ideal matched circular radius, $R=v^2/a_{\rm orb}$ and $d=R/\theta$; velocities and accelerations of unrelated <maser> spots cannot simply be inserted into that expression. Warps, inclination, eccentricity and feature identification enter real disk fits.<A 4% Geometric Distance to the Galaxy NGC4258 from Orbital Motions in a Nuclear Gas Disk by J. R. Herrnstein and others>[1999 abstract; VLBI proper motions and accelerations]{ref}

= Tip of the red giant branch
{parent=Cosmic distance ladder}

The termination of the first-ascent <red giant> <luminosity function> near helium ignition provides a calibrated population-II distance indicator. In suitable passbands the tip <luminosity> varies relatively weakly over a useful old, metal-poor population range. Colour/composition corrections, foreground <extinction>, photometric completeness and contamination by other evolved <stars> must be controlled; an arbitrary brightest red <star> is not a reliable tip measurement.

= Main-sequence fitting
{parent=Cosmic distance ladder}

Compare the extinction-corrected <apparent magnitudes> of a cluster's <main sequence> with calibrated <absolute magnitudes> at matching colour or stellar parameters. The magnitude displacement estimates its <distance modulus>. Age, <metallicity>, helium abundance, reddening and unresolved binaries can move or broaden the sequence, so a colour-magnitude displacement is not uniquely a distance change.

= Angular diameter distance
{title2=$d_A$}
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}
{wiki}

The angular diameter distance is defined by $d_A=x/\theta$ for a small transverse physical size $x$ subtending angle $\theta$. In a spatially flat <Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric>,
$$
d_A(z)=\frac{\chi(z)}{1+z},
$$
where $\chi$ is the <comoving radial distance>.

= Angular diameter distance in an open dust universe
{parent=Angular diameter distance}
{title2=$D_A=S_k(D_C)/(1+z)$}

Normalize the present scale factor to one and assume dust plus curvature with no cosmological constant. Then $H^2=H_0^2[\Omega_Ma^{-3}+(1-\Omega_M)a^{-2}]$ and $D_C=(c/H_0)\int_{1/(1+z)}^1 da/[\sqrt a\sqrt{\Omega_M+(1-\Omega_M)a}]$. For positive $\Omega_K=1-\Omega_M$, the transverse comoving distance is $S_k(D_C)=c\sinh(H_0\sqrt{\Omega_K}D_C/c)/(H_0\sqrt{\Omega_K})$. At fixed radial distance it exceeds the flat transverse distance. At fixed redshift the radial distance must also be calculated from the expansion history. The empty limit is $D_C=(c/H_0)\ln(1+z)$, while the flat dust limit is $2c[1-(1+z)^{-1/2}]/H_0$.

= Angular diameter distance in a flat power-law universe
{parent=Angular diameter distance}
{title2=$d_A(t_e,t_0)=ct_e^p(t_0^{1-p}-t_e^{1-p})/(1-p)$}

For $R=At^p$, $0<p<1$, integrate the radial <photon> path from emission to reception and multiply by the <scale factor> at emission. This gives the displayed <angular diameter distance>. The arbitrary normalization $A$ cancels, and the distance is not obtained by multiplying light speed by elapsed <cosmic time>.

= Angular Hubble-radius scale in an Einstein-de Sitter universe
{parent=Angular diameter distance}
{title2=$\theta_H=[2(\sqrt{1+z}-1)]^{-1}$}

In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^{3/2}$ and the radial <comoving distance> is $2H_0^{-1}[1-(1+z)^{-1/2}]$. A physical <Hubble radius> $H(z)^{-1}$ has comoving size $(1+z)/H(z)$. Dividing by the radial distance gives the displayed small angle, in radians. A Hubble diameter has twice this angle; the matter-era particle-horizon radius also equals twice the <Hubble radius>. These distinctions matter when comparing approximate <CMB> angular scales.

= Angular diameter distance turnover
{parent=Angular diameter distance}

In a standard expanding cosmology, $d_A(z)$ initially increases from zero but eventually decreases because the factor $(1+z)^{-1}$ dominates the bounded comoving distance. Beyond its maximum, a fixed physical object subtends a larger angle at a larger redshift.

= Constant-equation-of-state angular diameter distance maximum
{parent=Angular diameter distance turnover}
{title2=$1+z_m=\alpha^{1/(\alpha-1)},\quad\alpha=3(1+w)/2$}

For a flat universe with a single constant <equation-of-state parameter> $w>-1$, $H(z)=H_0(1+z)^\alpha$. Its <angular diameter distance> is $H_0^{-1}[(1+z)^{-\alpha}-(1+z)^{-1}]/(1-\alpha)$, with logarithmic limit $H_0^{-1}\log(1+z)/(1+z)$ at $\alpha=1$. Differentiation gives the displayed unique maximum, interpreted as $z_m=e-1$ at $\alpha=1$. The distance vanishes at both endpoints $z=0$ and $z\to\infty$. As $w\downarrow-1$, the maximum moves to infinite <cosmological redshift>.

= Cosmological standard ruler
{parent=Cosmological distance measure}

A cosmological standard ruler is a feature with a fixed known or redshift-independent physical or comoving size. Comparing its observed angular and radial extents constrains the expansion history even when its absolute size is treated as a nuisance parameter.

= Baryon acoustic oscillation
{title2=BAO}
{parent=Cosmological standard ruler}
{wiki}

Baryon acoustic oscillations are the relic spatial scale of sound waves in the pre-recombination <photon-baryon fluid>. The scale appears both as a preferred separation in galaxy clustering and as oscillatory structure in the <cosmological density power spectrum>.

= Baryon drag epoch
{parent=Baryon acoustic oscillation}
{title2=$z_d$}

The epoch at which momentum exchange with photons ceases to drag <baryons> efficiently. The <sound horizon> at this epoch calibrates the late-time <baryon acoustic oscillation> ruler. It is related to, but not identical with, photon last scattering.

= Transverse baryon acoustic oscillation measurement
{title2=Transverse BAO measurement}
{parent=Baryon acoustic oscillation}

For a comoving BAO scale $\chi_d$ perpendicular to the line of sight in a spatially flat universe,
$$
\Delta\theta=\frac{\chi_d}{\chi(z)}.
$$
Its redshift dependence constrains the shape of the distance--redshift relation.

= Radial baryon acoustic oscillation measurement
{title2=Radial BAO measurement}
{parent=Baryon acoustic oscillation}

For a small comoving BAO separation parallel to the line of sight,
$$
\Delta z=H(z)\chi_d
$$
in units with $c=1$. Combining this with a transverse measurement eliminates the unknown ruler size.

= Baryon acoustic oscillation in the matter power spectrum
{title2=BAO in the matter power spectrum}
{parent=Baryon acoustic oscillation}

The matter power spectrum contains low-amplitude oscillatory features because baryons preserve their pre-recombination acoustic phase and later transfer part of it gravitationally to the combined matter distribution.

= Alcock-Paczyński test
{c}
{parent=Cosmological standard ruler}
{wiki}

The Alcock-Paczyński test compares radial and transverse distance conversion using a feature that is statistically isotropic in comoving space.

= Alcock-Paczyński parameter
{title2=$F_{\rm AP}(z)=H(z)\chi(z)$}
{c}
{parent=Alcock-Paczyński test}

The Alcock-Paczyński parameter compares radial and transverse distance conversion at one redshift. An intrinsically isotropic comoving feature obeys
$$
\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta\theta}=H(z)\chi(z),
$$
so its unknown absolute size cancels.

= Lambda-CDM model
{title2=$\Lambda$CDM model}
{c}
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

The Lambda-CDM model combines a <cosmological constant> with <cold dark matter> and the standard baryonic and radiation components in an expanding <Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker metric>.

= Dark matter
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

Dark matter is matter inferred from its gravitational effects that does not emit, absorb, or scatter enough electromagnetic radiation to account for those effects as ordinary baryonic matter.

= Direct versus indirect dark-matter detection
{parent=Dark matter}

Direct detection measures scattering or absorption of incident halo particles in a target. Indirect detection searches for secondary products from annihilation or decay. Recording those secondary particles directly does not turn an indirect search into a direct halo-scattering measurement.

= Dark-matter direct detection
{parent=Dark matter}

A search for interactions of passing <dark matter> with a terrestrial target, for example <nuclear recoils> from <elastic scattering>. Predicting a count requires the local density and velocity distribution, interaction <cross section>, target response and <detector acceptance>. A background-compatible count gives a conditional interaction bound, rather than a measurement of the cosmological relic density.

= Spin-independent WIMP-nucleus scattering
{parent=Dark-matter direct detection}
{title2=$\sigma_A(0)=\sigma_p(\mu_A/\mu_p)^2A^2$ for equal proton/neutron couplings}

An elastic recoil interaction whose leading coherent nuclear amplitude does not depend on nuclear spin. At low <momentum> transfer, <proton> and neutron amplitudes add; equal couplings give an $A$-fold amplitude and the displayed cross-section scaling, with reduced <masses> $\mu_A$ and $\mu_p$. A finite-momentum nuclear form factor reduces coherence. Unequal <nucleon> couplings or other interactions change how experiments on different targets should be compared.

= Scattering versus collider production of dark matter
{parent=Dark-matter direct detection}

A low-momentum dark-matter–<nucleon> elastic <cross section> and a high-energy collider pair-production <cross section> are different observables. Crossing of amplitudes does not equate their numerical values. Mediator <masses>, couplings, <nuclear matrix elements>, parton distributions and kinematics enter the relation. Collider sensitivity further requires a trigger, visible recoil, acceptance and background treatment. Multiplying a direct-detection <cross section> by collider <luminosity> is therefore only a hypothetical dimensional estimate unless a production model justifies the substitution.

= Annual modulation in dark-matter direct detection
{parent=Dark-matter direct detection}

The Earth's changing velocity relative to a halo changes the recoil-rate integral above a detector threshold. Its phase and <energy> dependence depend on the velocity distribution and interaction, so a sinusoidal fit is a model approximation. Seasonal instrumental or environmental backgrounds can also vary annually; <energy> <radiation spectrum>, multiple-hit samples, time stability and independent targets help test a proposed dark-matter interpretation.

= CRESST experiment
{parent=Dark-matter direct detection}
{c}

A cryogenic direct-detection search using scintillating crystal calorimeters. Calcium-tungstate modules measure deposited <energy> through <phonons> and compare a separate light signal to discriminate recoil populations. Oxygen, calcium and tungsten recoil differently for a given <WIMP> <mass>; a model fit must include their responses and backgrounds.<Results from 730 kg days of the CRESST-II Dark Matter Search by G. Angloher and others>[calcium-tungstate detector and signal-region analysis]{ref}

= DAMA experiment
{parent=Dark-matter direct detection}
{c}

An underground NaI(Tl) <particle scintillation> experiment seeking annual variation in low-energy single-hit rates. Its reported modulation is an observable; mapping it to a <WIMP> <mass> and <cross section> requires assumptions about the interaction, halo distribution and detector recoil response. = Massive compact halo object
{parent=Dark matter}
{title2=MACHO}

A compact astronomical object proposed as a constituent of a galactic <dark matter halo>. It can be sought through <gravitational microlensing> even if its emitted light is undetectable. Stellar objects and remnants consume the cosmological <baryon> budget; primordial <black holes> do not obey that particular formation-budget argument. Exclusion bounds depend on the object's <mass>, the halo model and survey selection.

= Hot dark matter
{parent=Dark matter}

A matter component with relativistic motion during an important part of structure formation. Its <free streaming> suppresses small-scale perturbations. Making a light thermal <neutrino> Majorana rather than Dirac does not by itself turn this component into <cold dark matter>.

= Tremaine-Gunn bound
{parent=Hot dark matter}
{c}

The maximum primordial occupation of a collisionless <fermion> distribution limits the coarse-grained phase-space density attainable in a halo. Combining that bound with an observed <velocity dispersion> and <mass density> gives a lower candidate <mass>. This tests light-fermion halo models independently of whether the candidate is optically visible.<Dynamical Role of Light Neutral Leptons in Cosmology by Scott Tremaine and James Edward Gunn>[Physical Review Letters 42, pp. 407–410]{ref}

= Collisionless phase-space occupation bound
{parent=Tremaine-Gunn bound}

Fine-grained occupation is conserved along trajectories of a collisionless distribution. Coarse graining cannot increase its maximum. For <fermions> the primordial occupation is bounded, so a measured halo density and velocity scale restrict the particle <mass> required to produce that halo.

= Dark-matter annihilation radiation
{parent=Dark matter}

For identical self-conjugate <dark matter> particles, the pair-annihilation rate per volume is $\langle\sigma v\rangle n_\chi^2/2$. If each annihilation releases radiated energy $E_a$, volume emissivity is $j=E_a\langle\sigma v\rangle\rho^2/(2m_\chi^2)$. Optically thin isotropic <surface brightness> is $(4\pi)^{-1}\int j\,dz$. The squared-density dependence assumes a spatially constant rate coefficient and a smooth density; unresolved substructure changes the average of the square.

= Dark-matter annihilation J-factor
{parent=Dark-matter annihilation radiation}
{title2=$J=\int_{\Delta\Omega}d\Omega\int\rho_\chi^2\,dl$}

The line-of-sight, solid-angle integral of squared dark-matter density used for <annihilation> fluxes with a position-independent rate coefficient. It separates an astrophysical density model from particle yields and <annihilation> rates. A decay signal instead scales with a single power of density. Substructure or spatially varying velocity-dependent rates can prevent a simple smooth-density factorization.

= Dark-matter decay radiation
{parent=Dark matter}

If a <dark matter> particle of mass $m_\chi$ radiates power $\epsilon$ independently of its neighbours, optically thin isotropic emission has volume emissivity $j=\epsilon\rho/m_\chi$. Its <surface brightness> is $I=(4\pi)^{-1}\int j\,dz$ and therefore traces the mass column rather than its square.

= Weakly interacting massive particle
{parent=Dark matter}
{title2=WIMP}

= WIMP
{synonym}
{c}

A <weakly interacting massive particle> is a massive dark-matter candidate whose interaction strength permits thermal production and later <cosmological particle freeze-out>. Its relic abundance depends on the annihilation rate and cosmological history, not on its mass alone. At nucleosynthesis temperatures a 50 GeV example is nonrelativistic: its equilibrium density is reduced by <Boltzmann suppression>, while an already frozen relic population need not follow the equilibrium density. Its effect on nucleosynthesis depends on whether the surviving energy density is appreciable or whether it injects reaction-altering products.

= Cold dark matter
{title2=CDM}
{parent=Dark matter}
{wiki}

Cold dark matter is nonrelativistic early enough that its primordial free-streaming length is small compared with the cosmological structures of interest.

= Warm dark matter
{title2=WDM}
{parent=Dark matter}
{wiki}

Warm dark matter has a primordial <collisionless free-streaming> length large enough to suppress the formation of low-mass haloes, while remaining nonrelativistic early enough to cluster on galactic and larger scales.

= Matter-radiation equality
{parent=Cosmology}

Matter-radiation equality is the epoch at which the background matter and radiation densities are equal. Its horizon wavenumber $k_{\rm eq}$ sets the turnover scale of the cold-dark-matter transfer function.

= Photon-only matter-radiation equality
{parent=Matter-radiation equality}
{title2=$1+z_{m\gamma}=\Omega_{m0}/\Omega_{\gamma0}$}

Nonrelativistic matter density grows as $(1+z)^3$, whereas freely redshifting <photon> <energy density> grows as $(1+z)^4$. Their equality gives the displayed redshift. Radiation inferred solely from a <cosmic microwave background> <temperature> excludes the relic <neutrino> <energy density>, so the resulting redshift differs from total matter-radiation equality. Comparing density parameters at one epoch is equivalent to comparing the densities because their common critical-density denominator cancels.

= Matter-radiation equality scale
{title2=$k_{\rm eq}$}
{parent=Matter-radiation equality}

The matter-radiation equality scale is the comoving horizon wavenumber at equality. Modes at larger wavenumber entered during radiation domination and had their matter growth suppressed.

= Cosmic string
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

A <cosmic string> is a hypothetical one-dimensional topological defect formed by symmetry breaking in the early universe. A <cosmic string network> consists of many such defects and requires additional assumptions to infer an averaged equation of state.

= Axial boundary term in the mass of a cylindrical spacetime
{parent=Cosmic string}
{title2=$\mu_{\rm bulk}=c^2[f'(0^+)-f'(R^-)]/(4G)$}

For $ds^2=-(dx^0)^2+dr^2+f(r)^2d\phi^2+dz^2$, the smooth bulk mass density is $-c^2f''/(8\pi Gf)$. Integrate using proper transverse area $2\pi f\,dr$ to obtain the boundary term. With no shell at the core edge, $f'(R^-)$ equals the exterior conical slope. A regular axis requires $f(0)=0$ and $f'(0)=1$ for angular period $2\pi$. A different printed inner derivative gives a different bulk integral; any singular-axis matter must be specified separately. This prevents silently applying the standard cosmic-string mass-deficit relation to nonregular cores.

= Horizon-scaling cosmic string network
{parent=Cosmic string}
{title2=$\rho_{\rm string}\sim\mu/t^2$}

A network whose characteristic long-string separation grows with the horizon scale can lose <energy> into loops and <radiation>. Its density can scale as $\mu/t^2$ in natural units. This is distinct from the $a^{-2}$ dilution of an ideal frozen noninteracting string network.

= Comoving and proper distances
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

<Comoving and proper distances> are two cosmological distance conventions. For simultaneous radial separations in an FLRW model, proper distance equals the comoving radial distance multiplied by the scale factor at that cosmic time. A <comoving coordinate> remains fixed on an ideal comoving observer's worldline.

= Age of the universe
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

The <age of the universe> is elapsed cosmic time since the early hot Big Bang phase, inferred within a cosmological model from expansion and matter-content measurements. The <age of an FLRW universe> is the corresponding model computation from its scale-factor evolution.

= Age comparison for an initial vacuum-dominated phase
{parent=Age of the universe}
{title2=$\Delta t=H_1^{-1}\{\log(1/\epsilon)-(1-\epsilon^2)/2\}>0$}

Match flat <Friedmann equations> histories at <scale factor> $R_1$ and <Hubble parameter> $H_1$, and give them identical subsequent evolution. Below $R_1$, constant <vacuum energy> gives $H=H_1$, while a <radiation-dominated universe> gives $H=H_1(R_1/R)^2$. From $\epsilon R_1$ to $R_1$ the former takes longer by $H_1^{-1}\int_\epsilon^1(1/x-x)\,dx>0$. Its duration diverges as $\epsilon\to0$; the radiation duration tends to $1/(2H_1)$. A finite period of <cosmic inflation> requires a finite initial state and a specified matching before such age comparisons are meaningful.

= Non-Gaussianity
{parent=Cosmology}
{wiki}

<Non-Gaussianity> means a departure from Gaussian statistics of a physical quantity or random field. Higher connected correlation functions describe such departures. <Primordial non-Gaussianity> concerns the initial cosmological perturbations.
