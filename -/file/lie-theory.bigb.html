= Lie theory
{c}
{wiki}

<Lie theory> studies continuous symmetries through <Lie groups>, <Lie algebras>, and their <representation theory>[representations].

= Affine algebraic group
{parent=Lie theory}
{wiki=Linear_algebraic_group}

An affine algebraic group is an <affine variety> $G$ whose multiplication and inversion are <regular maps>. Equivalently, its <coordinate ring> $k[G]$ is a commutative <Hopf algebra>, with comultiplication induced by multiplication in $G$.

= Lie algebra of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

The Lie algebra of an affine algebraic group $G$ is the tangent space $T_eG$ at its identity. Its bracket is the commutator of the corresponding left-invariant derivations of the coordinate ring.

= Descent of an irreducible SL2 representation to PGL2
{c}
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

The irreducible rational representations of $SL_2(\mathbb C)$ are $\operatorname{Sym}^n(\mathbb C^2)$. Such a representation descends through $SL_2\to PGL_2$ exactly when the central element $-I$ acts trivially, which holds exactly when $n$ is even.

= Faithful representation of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

Every affine algebraic group has a faithful finite-dimensional rational <group representation>. Choose algebra generators of $k[G]$ and place them in a finite-dimensional subspace stable under right translations. An element acting trivially on that subspace has the same coordinate values as the identity and therefore is the identity.

= Left-right regular representation of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

The group $G\times G$ acts on $k[G]$ by
$$
((a,b)f)(x)=f(a^{-1}xb).
$$
For $G=\mathbb G_m$, this gives $k[G]=\bigoplus_{r\in\mathbb Z}k_{(-r,r)}$. For $G=\mathbb G_a$, the action factors through $(a,b)\mapsto b-a$ and the degree filtration of $k[t]$ has trivial one-dimensional successive quotients.

= Semisimple element of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

An element of an affine algebraic group is semisimple when its image in one, equivalently every, faithful finite-dimensional representation is a diagonalizable linear map.

= Unipotent element of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

An element of an affine algebraic group is unipotent when its image in one, equivalently every, faithful finite-dimensional representation has every eigenvalue equal to one.

= Jordan decomposition in an affine algebraic group
{c}
{parent=Affine algebraic group}
{wiki=Jordan–Chevalley_decomposition}

Every element $g$ of an affine algebraic group has unique commuting semisimple and unipotent parts $g_s,g_u$ such that $g=g_sg_u$. The construction agrees with the multiplicative <Jordan normal form>[Jordan decomposition] in every rational representation.

= Derived subgroup of an affine algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

The derived subgroup $[G,G]$ is the closed algebraic subgroup generated by the commutators $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$. If $G$ is connected, images of finite products of commutators are connected and their closures stabilize by dimension, which shows that $[G,G]$ is connected.

= Solvable affine algebraic group
{parent=Derived subgroup of an affine algebraic group}

An affine algebraic group is solvable when its derived series reaches the identity. A connected solvable affine algebraic group can be conjugated into an upper triangular matrix group by the <Lie-Kolchin theorem>.

= Lie-Kolchin theorem
{c}
{parent=Solvable affine algebraic group}
{wiki=Lie–Kolchin_theorem}

Every connected solvable affine algebraic subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ preserves a complete flag in $V$, equivalently it is conjugate to a subgroup of the upper triangular matrices.

= Unipotent algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}
{wiki=Unipotent_group}

A unipotent algebraic group is an affine algebraic group all of whose elements are unipotent. The <Kolchin theorem> embeds it into an upper unitriangular group.

= Kolchin theorem
{c}
{parent=Unipotent algebraic group}

Every unipotent algebraic subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}_n$ is conjugate to a subgroup of the upper unitriangular group. The filtration by vanishing initial superdiagonals then proves that every unipotent algebraic group is a <nilpotent group>.

= Diagonalizable algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}
{wiki=Diagonalizable_group}

A diagonalizable algebraic group is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of a product of copies of $\mathbb G_m$. Every rational representation of such a group decomposes into weight spaces.

= Reductive algebraic group
{parent=Affine algebraic group}
{wiki=Reductive_group}

A reductive algebraic group is a smooth connected affine algebraic group whose largest connected normal <unipotent algebraic group> is trivial.

= Borel subgroup
{c}
{parent=Reductive algebraic group}
{wiki}

A Borel subgroup is a maximal closed connected solvable subgroup of an affine algebraic group.

= Parabolic subgroup
{parent=Borel subgroup}
{wiki}

A parabolic subgroup of a connected reductive group is a closed subgroup containing a <Borel subgroup>. Its quotient in the group is projective.

= Levi subgroup
{c}
{parent=Parabolic subgroup}
{wiki=Levi_decomposition}

A Levi subgroup is a reductive complement to the unipotent radical of a parabolic subgroup.

= Flag variety of an algebraic group
{parent=Borel subgroup}
{wiki=Flag_variety}

For a connected reductive algebraic group $G$ and a <Borel subgroup> $B$, the quotient $G/B$ is its complete flag variety. Quotients $G/P$ by <parabolic subgroups> are partial flag varieties.

= Root datum
{parent=Reductive algebraic group}
{wiki}

A root datum is a quadruple $(X^*,\Phi,X_*,\Phi^\vee)$ of dual lattices, roots, and coroots with the natural perfect pairing and reflection axioms. A reductive group with maximal torus $T$ has $X^*=X^*(T)$ and $X_*=X_*(T)$.

= Bruhat decomposition of a reductive algebraic group
{c}
{parent=Reductive algebraic group}
{wiki=Bruhat_decomposition}

For a connected reductive algebraic group, a <Borel subgroup> $B$, and a maximal torus $T\subseteq B$, the <Weyl group> $W=N_G(T)/T$ indexes the double cosets:
$$
G=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}B\dot wB.
$$

= Algebraic-group torsor
{parent=Affine algebraic group}

For an algebraic group $H$, a flat $H$-torsor over $Y$ is a faithfully flat morphism $X\to Y$ with a right $H$-action such that $X\times H\to X\times_YX$, $(x,h)\mapsto(x,xh)$, is an isomorphism. A Zariski torsor additionally becomes $U\times H$ on a Zariski open cover of $Y$.

= Lie group
{c}
{parent=Lie theory}
{wiki}

A Lie group is a <group> that is also a <smooth manifold>, with smooth multiplication and inversion. Its tangent space at the identity carries a natural <Lie algebra> structure.

= Lie group action
{c}
{parent=Lie group}

A Lie group action is a smooth <group action> of a Lie group on a smooth manifold. Differentiating the action gives an infinitesimal action of its <Lie algebra> by vector fields.

= Matrix Lie group
{c}
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

A matrix Lie group is a subgroup of a <general linear group> that is also a <Lie group> for its matrix topology. Near the identity, the <matrix exponential> and <matrix logarithm> give inverse coordinate charts whenever they are restricted to sufficiently small neighborhoods in the group and its tangent space.

= Exponential map of a matrix Lie group
{title2=$\exp:\mathfrak g\to G$}
{parent=Matrix Lie group}

For a <matrix Lie group> $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$, its exponential map is the restriction of the <matrix exponential>, $X\mapsto e^X$. It maps $\mathfrak g$ into $G$ and is a local diffeomorphism at zero, but it need not be surjective globally.

= Logarithmic chart of a matrix Lie group
{parent=Matrix Lie group}

For a matrix Lie group $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak g=T_I G$, choose neighborhoods on which $\exp:\mathfrak g\to G$ and $\log:G\to\mathfrak g$ are inverse. Left translation gives a chart near $g\in G$ by
$$
h\longmapsto\log(g^{-1}h).
$$
The <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> expresses multiplication smoothly in these coordinates.

= Simple Lie group
{c}
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

A connected Lie group is simple when its <Lie algebra> is nonabelian and simple. Some conventions additionally quotient out or exclude a discrete center; the infinitesimal gauge fields depend only on the Lie algebra.

= Homogeneous space
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

A homogeneous space is a space on which a group acts transitively. After choosing a point, a transitive Lie-group action identifies the space with a quotient $G/H$ by the point stabilizer.

= Left and right translation on a Lie group
{title2=$L_g,R_g$}
{parent=Lie group}

= Left translation on a Lie group
{synonym}
{c}

For a <Lie group> $G$, left and right translation are the <diffeomorphisms> $L_g(h)=gh$ and $R_g(h)=hg$. They commute: $L_gR_h=R_hL_g$.

= Left-invariant vector field
{title2=$l_\xi$}
{parent=Left and right translation on a Lie group}
{wiki=Invariant_vector_field}

For $\xi$ in the <Lie algebra> of $G$, the left-invariant vector field is $l_\xi(g)=(dL_g)_e\xi$. Its global <local flow>[flow] is right translation $\Phi^t=R_{\exp(t\xi)}$.

= Left-invariant differential form
{parent=Left and right translation on a Lie group}
{wiki=Invariant_differential_form}

A differential form $\alpha$ on a <Lie group> is left-invariant when $L_g^*\alpha=\alpha$ for every $g$. It is determined by its value at the identity.

= Bi-invariant differential form
{parent=Left-invariant differential form}

A differential form on a <Lie group> is bi-invariant when it is invariant under both left and right translations.

= Closed left-invariant 1-form
{parent=Bi-invariant differential form}

On a connected <Lie group>, a <left-invariant differential form>[left-invariant] <differential 1-form> is <closed differential form>[closed] exactly when it is <bi-invariant differential form>[bi-invariant]. Connectedness is essential: on $O(2)$ every left-invariant 1-form is closed because its Lie algebra is abelian, while reflection conjugation negates every nonzero one.

= One-parameter subgroup
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

= One-parameter group
{synonym}

A one-parameter subgroup of a Lie group $G$ is a smooth homomorphism $\gamma:(\mathbb R,+)\to G$. Every such subgroup has the form $\gamma(t)=\exp(tX)$ for a unique $X$ in the Lie algebra of $G$.

= Circle group
{title2=$S^1$}
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

The circle group is the unit complex numbers under multiplication, equivalently planar rotations or the quotient $\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$.

= Lie-group representation
{parent=Lie group}

A Lie-group representation is a smooth <group representation> $D:G\to GL(V)$.

= Derived representation
{parent=Lie-group representation}

The derived representation of a smooth Lie-group representation is $dD(X)=\left.\frac d{dt}\right|_{0}D(\exp(tX))$. It is a <Lie-algebra representation> because $dD([X,Y])=[dD(X),dD(Y)]$.

= Adjoint representation of a Lie group
{title2=$\operatorname{Ad}$}
{c}
{parent=Lie group}

= Adjoint action
{synonym}

The adjoint representation of a Lie group is its action on its Lie algebra by differentiating conjugation. For a matrix Lie group, $\operatorname{Ad}_gX=gXg^{-1}$.

= Adjoint double cover from SU(2) to SO(3)
{c}
{title2=$SU(2)\to SO(3)$}
{parent=Adjoint representation of a Lie group}

Conjugation by $SU(2)$ on its three-dimensional real Lie algebra of traceless skew-Hermitian matrices preserves $-\operatorname{tr}(AB)$ and defines a surjection
$$
SU(2)\longrightarrow SO(3)
$$
with kernel $\{\pm I_2\}$. It is the universal double covering of $SO(3)$.

= Local group law
{parent=Lie group}

A local group law is the coordinate expression $z=F(y,x)$ of multiplication near the identity of a <Lie group>. The identity, inverse, and associativity equations for $F$ encode the group structure locally.

= Maurer-Cartan form
{c}
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki=Maurer–Cartan_form}

For a matrix Lie group, the left-invariant Maurer-Cartan form is $\rho=g^{-1}dg$. It identifies each tangent space with the Lie algebra and obeys $d\rho+\rho\wedge\rho=0$.

= Maurer-Cartan equation
{c}
{parent=Maurer-Cartan form}

The Maurer-Cartan equation is $d\rho+\rho\wedge\rho=0$ for the left-invariant form $\rho=g^{-1}dg$.

= Maurer-Cartan equation in a Lie-algebra basis
{c}
{parent=Maurer-Cartan equation}

If $\rho=\sigma^\alpha T_\alpha$ and $[T_\alpha,T_\beta]=c^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}T_\gamma$, then
$$
d\sigma^\gamma=-\frac12c^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}
\sigma^\alpha\wedge\sigma^\beta.
$$

= Real affine group
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki=Affine_group}

The orientation-preserving real affine group acts by $x\mapsto e^ax+b$ and has multiplication $(a,b)(a',b')=(a+a',b+e^ab')$.

= Left-invariant metric
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

A left-invariant Riemannian metric is preserved by every left translation. Its Killing fields generated by left translations are right-invariant vector fields.

= Geodesic-vector criterion for a left-invariant metric
{title2=$\langle\xi,[\xi,\eta]\rangle=0$}
{parent=Left-invariant metric}

For a <left-invariant metric> on a <Lie group>, the one-parameter subgroup $\gamma_\xi(t)=\exp(t\xi)$ is a <geodesic> exactly when
$$
\langle\xi,[\xi,\eta]\rangle=0
$$
for every $\eta$ in the <Lie algebra>. The <Koszul formula> gives $\langle\nabla_\xi\xi,\eta\rangle=-\langle\xi,[\xi,\eta]\rangle$.

= Exponential map of a Lie group
{title2=$\exp:\mathfrak g\to G$}
{c}
{parent=Lie group}

For a matrix Lie group, the exponential map is the matrix exponential $\exp X=\sum_{n\geq0}X^n/n!$ and sends one-parameter additive subgroups of the Lie algebra to one-parameter subgroups of the group.

= Cayley transform
{disambiguate=Lie theory}
{title2=$C(X)=(I+X)(I-X)^{-1}$}
{c}
{parent=Lie group}
{wiki}

The Cayley transform is a rational local parametrization of a matrix Lie group from its Lie algebra wherever $I-X$ is invertible. Unlike the exponential map, its image can meet nonidentity components.

= Lie algebra
{c}
{parent=Lie theory}
{wiki}

A Lie algebra over a <field> is a <vector space> $\mathfrak g$ with a bilinear operation $[x,y]$, called the <Lie bracket>, that is alternating and satisfies the <Jacobi identity>.

= Lie algebra generator
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}

A Lie algebra generator is an element of a chosen spanning or generating set for a Lie algebra. In a matrix representation, an infinitesimal group transformation has the form $1+i\alpha_at^a+O(\alpha^2)$ in terms of generator matrices $t^a$.

= Lie algebra of a matrix Lie group
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}

For a <matrix Lie group> $G$, its tangent space $\mathfrak g=T_I G$ is closed under the matrix <commutator>. Indeed, for $X,Y\in\mathfrak g$, the group commutator
$$
e^{tX}e^{sY}e^{-tX}e^{-sY}
$$
lies in $G$, and the mixed derivative at $(0,0)$ of its matrix logarithm is $XY-YX$. Thus $[X,Y]=XY-YX$ belongs to $\mathfrak g$.

= Lie algebra isomorphism
{parent=Lie algebra}

A Lie algebra isomorphism is a bijective <linear map> $\varphi:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak h$ satisfying $\varphi([x,y])=[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]$. Its inverse automatically preserves the <Lie bracket>.

= Direct sum of Lie algebras
{parent=Lie algebra}

The direct sum $\mathfrak g\oplus\mathfrak h$ has componentwise bracket
$$
[(x,y),(x',y')]=([x,x'],[y,y']).
$$
The two summands are commuting <ideal of a Lie algebra>[ideals].

= Lie subalgebra
{parent=Lie algebra}

A Lie subalgebra is a <vector subspace> closed under the <Lie bracket>.

= Normalizer of a Lie subalgebra
{title2=$N_L(H)$}
{parent=Lie subalgebra}

The normalizer of a <Lie subalgebra> $H\subseteq L$ is
$$
N_L(H)=\{x\in L:[x,H]\subseteq H\}.
$$
It is the largest Lie subalgebra of $L$ in which $H$ is an <ideal of a Lie algebra>.

= Derivation of a Lie algebra
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki=Derivation_(differential_algebra)}

A derivation of a <Lie algebra> is a <linear map> $D:L\to L$ satisfying
$$
D[x,y]=[Dx,y]+[x,Dy].
$$
Every adjoint map $\operatorname{ad}z:x\mapsto[z,x]$ is a derivation.

= Outer derivation of a nilpotent Lie algebra
{parent=Derivation of a Lie algebra}

Every nonzero finite-dimensional nilpotent Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero has a derivation outside its adjoint image. This remains true for characteristically nilpotent Lie algebras: all their derivations may be nilpotent, but they cannot all be inner.

= Generalized-eigenspace bracket lemma
{parent=Derivation of a Lie algebra}

If $D$ is a <derivation of a Lie algebra>, then its <generalized eigenspace>[generalized eigenspaces] satisfy
$$
[L_\lambda,L_\mu]\subseteq L_{\lambda+\mu}.
$$
Apply a sufficiently large power of $D-(\lambda+\mu)I$ and use the <binomial theorem> together with the derivation rule.

= Invariant bilinear form on a Lie algebra
{parent=Lie algebra}

A bilinear form $B$ on a Lie algebra is invariant when
$$
B([x,y],z)=B(x,[y,z]).
$$
For a symmetric form this says that every adjoint map is skew-adjoint with respect to $B$.

= Lie superalgebra
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki}

A Lie superalgebra is a Z2-graded vector space with a graded-antisymmetric bracket satisfying the graded Jacobi identity. The bracket of two odd elements is symmetric and is conventionally written as an anticommutator.

= Lie bracket
{title2=$[x,y]$}
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki}

The Lie bracket is the product in a <Lie algebra>. For matrix Lie algebras it is the <commutator> $[X,Y]=XY-YX$.

= Commutator
{title2=$[A,B]$}
{parent=Lie bracket}
{wiki=Commutator}

The commutator of two elements of an associative algebra is $[A,B]=AB-BA$. Matrix Lie algebras use the commutator as their <Lie bracket>.

= Jacobi identity
{c}
{parent=Lie bracket}
{wiki}

The Jacobi identity is
$$
[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]]=0.
$$

= Abelian Lie algebra
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki}

A Lie algebra is abelian when every <Lie bracket> vanishes.

= Semidirect product of a Lie algebra and a module
{title2=$\mathfrak g\ltimes V$}
{parent=Lie algebra}

For a <Lie algebra representation> of $\mathfrak g$ on $V$, the semidirect product $\mathfrak g\ltimes V$ is the vector space $\mathfrak g\oplus V$ with bracket
$$
[(x,v),(y,w)]=([x,y],xw-yv).
$$
The subspace $V$ is an abelian <ideal of a Lie algebra>.

= Killing form of a semidirect product with a module
{parent=Semidirect product of a Lie algebra and a module}

For a finite-dimensional representation $\rho:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)$,
$$
K_{\mathfrak g\ltimes V}((x,v),(y,w))
=K_{\mathfrak g}(x,y)+\operatorname{tr}(\rho(x)\rho(y)).
$$
In particular, $V$ lies in the radical of the Killing form.

A vector subspace $I\subseteq\mathfrak g$ is an ideal when $[\mathfrak g,I]\subseteq I$. It is therefore the kernel of a Lie-algebra quotient map.

= Derived series of a Lie algebra
{parent=Lie algebra}

The derived series is $\mathfrak g^{(0)}=\mathfrak g$ and $\mathfrak g^{(n+1)}=[\mathfrak g^{(n)},\mathfrak g^{(n)}]$.

= Solvable Lie algebra

{parent=Derived series of a Lie algebra}

A Lie algebra is solvable when its derived series eventually becomes zero.

= Solvable radical
{parent=Solvable Lie algebra}
{wiki=Radical_of_a_Lie_algebra}

The solvable radical of a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> is its largest solvable <ideal of a Lie algebra>. A Lie algebra is <semisimple Lie algebra>[semisimple] exactly when its solvable radical is zero.

= Lie theorem
{c}
{parent=Solvable Lie algebra}
{wiki=Lie's_theorem}

Every finite-dimensional complex representation of a solvable Lie algebra has a common eigenvector. Equivalently, an irreducible finite-dimensional complex representation is one-dimensional; iterating gives simultaneous upper triangularization.

= Cartan solvability criterion
{c}
{parent=Solvable Lie algebra}
{wiki=Cartan's_criterion}

A complex Lie subalgebra $\mathfrak h\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is solvable if
$$
\operatorname{tr}(xy)=0
$$
for every $x\in[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h]$ and $y\in\mathfrak h$. For an abstract Lie algebra, this is equivalent to $\kappa(\mathfrak h,[\mathfrak h,\mathfrak h])=0$.

= Center of a Lie algebra

{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki=Center_(group_theory)#Lie_algebras}

The center is $Z(\mathfrak g)=\{z:[z,x]=0\text{ for every }x\in\mathfrak g\}$.

= Lower central series of a Lie algebra
{title2=$(\gamma_i(\mathfrak g))$}
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}

The lower central series is defined by $\gamma_1(\mathfrak g)=\mathfrak g$ and $\gamma_{i+1}(\mathfrak g)=[\mathfrak g,\gamma_i(\mathfrak g)]$.

= Nilpotent Lie algebra
{c}
{parent=Lower central series of a Lie algebra}
{wiki}

A <Lie algebra> is nilpotent when its <lower central series of a Lie algebra>[lower central series] eventually becomes zero.

= Normalizer condition for a nilpotent Lie algebra
{parent=Nilpotent Lie algebra}

Every proper <Lie subalgebra> $H$ of a finite-dimensional <nilpotent Lie algebra> is properly contained in its <normalizer of a Lie subalgebra>[normalizer]. Choose the first term of the lower central series contained in $H$; the preceding term contains an element outside $H$ that normalizes it.

= Engel theorem
{c}
{parent=Nilpotent Lie algebra}
{wiki=Engel's_theorem}

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra is nilpotent if and only if every adjoint map $\operatorname{ad}_x$ is nilpotent. A Lie algebra of nilpotent endomorphisms can be simultaneously represented by strictly upper triangular matrices.

= Engel lemma
{c}
{parent=Engel theorem}

A finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> in which every representing endomorphism is <nilpotent linear map>[nilpotent] has a nonzero vector annihilated by the entire Lie algebra. Applying this to the <adjoint representation> is the key step in <Engel theorem>.

= Lie algebra homomorphism
{c}

A Lie algebra homomorphism is a <linear map> $f$ satisfying $f([x,y])=[f(x),f(y)]$.

= Lie algebra representation
{c}

A representation of $\mathfrak g$ on a <vector space> $V$ is a <Lie algebra homomorphism> $\rho:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)$.

= Hom representation
{title2=$\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$}
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra representation}

For <Lie algebra representations> $V$ and $W$, the Hom representation on $\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)$ is
$$
(x\cdot f)(v)=x\cdot f(v)-f(x\cdot v).
$$
Its invariant vectors are exactly the intertwining maps.

= Automorphism of a Lie algebra

An automorphism of a Lie algebra is an invertible linear map $f:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak g$ satisfying $f([X,Y])=[f(X),f(Y)]$.

A branching rule decomposes a representation into irreducible representations after restriction from a Lie group or Lie algebra to a subgroup or subalgebra.

{c}
{synonym}

The adjoint representation is $\operatorname{ad}_x(y)=[x,y]$. For a semisimple <Lie algebra>, its nonzero weights are the roots and its zero-weight space is the <Cartan subalgebra>.

= Killing form
{title2=$\kappa(X,Y)$}
{c}
{parent=Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra}
{wiki}

The Killing form is
$$
\kappa(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{ad}_X\operatorname{ad}_Y).
$$
It is symmetric and invariant: $\kappa([X,Y],Z)=\kappa(X,[Y,Z])$.

= Solvability of the radical of the Killing form
{c}
{parent=Killing form}

The radical $\mathfrak g^\perp=\{x:\kappa(x,\mathfrak g)=0\}$ of the Killing form is a solvable ideal. Invariance makes it an ideal, and the <Cartan solvability criterion> applied to its adjoint image proves solvability.

= Cartan criterion for semisimplicity
{c}
{parent=Killing form}

A finite-dimensional complex <Lie algebra> is <semisimple Lie algebra>[semisimple] if and only if its <Killing form> is <nondegenerate bilinear form>[nondegenerate].

= Uniqueness of an invariant bilinear form on a simple Lie algebra
{parent=Killing form}

Every invariant bilinear form on a finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebra is a scalar multiple of its Killing form. A nondegenerate invariant form identifies the algebra with its dual; comparing this identification with the Killing form gives an endomorphism of the irreducible adjoint representation, so <Schur lemma> makes it scalar.

For $\mathfrak{sl}_n(\mathbb C)$,

= Adjoint irreducibility of a simple Lie algebra
{parent=Adjoint representation of a Lie algebra}

Every invariant subspace of the <adjoint representation of a Lie algebra> is an ideal. Hence the adjoint representation of a <simple Lie algebra> is irreducible.

= Trace form of a Lie algebra representation
{title2=$(\cdot,\cdot)_V$}
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra representation}

For a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> $\rho:\mathfrak g\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, its trace form is the symmetric bilinear form
$$
(x,y)_V=\operatorname{tr}(\rho(x)\rho(y)).
$$
It is invariant: $([x,y],z)_V=(x,[y,z])_V$.

= Trace trilinear form of a Lie algebra representation
{title2=$B(X,Y,Z)$}
{parent=Lie algebra representation}

For a finite-dimensional representation $d$, the trilinear form $B(X,Y,Z)=\operatorname{Tr}(d(X)d(Y)d(Z))$ is invariant under simultaneous adjoint action. This follows by writing the sum of its three infinitesimal variations as the trace of a commutator.

= Heisenberg Lie algebra
{c}
{parent=Lie algebra}

The three-dimensional Heisenberg Lie algebra has a basis $a,b,c$ with $[a,b]=c$ and $c$ central. It is a two-step <nilpotent Lie algebra>.

= Real Heisenberg group
{c}
{synonym}

The real Heisenberg group consists of upper-unitriangular $3\times3$ real matrices. In coordinates $(q,r,s)$ its multiplication is $(q,r,s)(q',r',s')=(q+q',r+r',s+s'+qr')$.

= Integer Heisenberg group
{c}
{title2=$H(\mathbb Z)$}
{parent=Heisenberg group}

The integer Heisenberg group consists of upper-unitriangular three-by-three integer matrices. In coordinates $(x,y,z)$ its multiplication is
$$
(x,y,z)(x',y',z')=(x+x',y+y',z+z'+xy').
$$
It is the semidirect product $\mathbb Z^2\rtimes_A\mathbb Z$ for
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
$$
and its <abelianization> is $\mathbb Z^2$.

= Schrödinger representation of the Heisenberg group
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg group}

On $L^2(\mathbb R)$, translations and multiplication by phases give the Schrödinger representation of the Heisenberg group. The central coordinate acts by an overall phase.

= Polynomial representation of the Heisenberg Lie algebra
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg Lie algebra}

On $\mathbb C[x]$, the operators $d/dx$, multiplication by $x$, and the identity satisfy $[d/dx,x]=1$. Sending $a,b,c$ to these operators gives a faithful irreducible infinite-dimensional representation of the <Heisenberg Lie algebra>.

= Complexification of a Lie algebra
{parent=Lie algebra}

For a real Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$, its complexification is $\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak g\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C$ with the bracket extended complex-bilinearly.

= Universal enveloping algebra
{title2=$U(\mathfrak g)$}
{parent=Lie algebra}
{wiki}

The universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak g)$ is the associative algebra generated by $\mathfrak g$ subject to $xy-yx=[x,y]$. Its modules are the same as <Lie algebra representations>.

= Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem
{c}
{parent=Universal enveloping algebra}
{wiki=Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt_theorem}

For an ordered basis $(x_i)$ of a <Lie algebra> $\mathfrak g$, the ordered monomials $x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ form a basis of $U(\mathfrak g)$. In particular, a triangular decomposition $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak n^-\oplus\mathfrak t\oplus\mathfrak n^+$ gives $U(\mathfrak g)\cong U(\mathfrak n^-)\otimes U(\mathfrak t)\otimes U(\mathfrak n^+)$ as vector spaces.

= Semisimple Lie algebra

A finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> is semisimple when it has no nonzero solvable ideals.

= Perfect Lie algebra

A <Lie algebra> $L$ is perfect when $[L,L]=L$. Every semisimple Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero is perfect.

= Weyl complete reducibility theorem

Every finite-dimensional representation of a complex <semisimple Lie algebra> is a direct sum of irreducible representations. Equivalently, every invariant subspace has an invariant complement.

For a choice of positive roots, the corresponding Borel subalgebra is $\mathfrak b=\mathfrak t\oplus\mathfrak n^+$, the sum of a <Cartan subalgebra> and all positive root spaces.

A nonabelian <Lie algebra> is simple when its only ideals are zero and the entire algebra.

The special linear Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_n$ consists of the trace-zero $n\times n$ matrices with the <commutator> bracket.

For the defining representation $V=V(\omega_1)$ of $\mathfrak{sl}_3$,

V\otimes V\otimes V^*
\cong

= sl2 Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{sl}_2$}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ has generators $e,h,f$ with $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$, and $[e,f]=h$.

= SU(2) Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{su}(2)$}
{c}

The SU(2) Lie algebra is the compact real form of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$. In the Hermitian physics convention its generators satisfy $[J_i,J_j]=i\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$.

= Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations
{c}
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}

For every $n\geq0$ there is one irreducible $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$-module $V(n)$ of dimension $n+1$, with weights $n,n-2,\ldots,-n$, each of multiplicity one. Every finite-dimensional representation is a direct sum of these modules.

= Injectivity of sl2 lowering above weight zero
{c}
{parent=Classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations}

On any finite-dimensional $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module, the lowering operator $f$ is injective from the $m$-weight space to the $(m-2)$-weight space whenever $m>0$. This follows on each irreducible summand from its standard weight string.

= Reducibility of an sl2 Verma module
{title2=$M_\lambda$ reducible iff $\lambda\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$}
{c}
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}

The Verma module $M_\lambda$ has basis $(f^rv_\lambda)_{r\geq0}$ and
$$
e f^rv_\lambda=r(\lambda-r+1)f^{r-1}v_\lambda.
$$
It is reducible exactly when $\lambda=m\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$; then its unique proper nonzero submodule is generated by $f^{m+1}v_\lambda$ and is isomorphic to $M_{-m-2}$.

= Intermediate-series sl2 module
{c}
{parent=sl2 Lie algebra}

An intermediate-series $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-module has one-dimensional weight spaces indexed by all integers. In the basis $(w_i)_{i\in\mathbb Z}$, one useful normalization is
$$
hw_i=(a+2i)w_i,\qquad fw_i=w_{i-1},\qquad
ew_i=(b-ia-i(i+1))w_{i+1}.
$$
It is irreducible exactly when every raising coefficient is nonzero.

For a nondegenerate alternating matrix $J$, the symplectic Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}=\{A:AJ+JA^T=0\}$.

= C3 root system

{c}

The $C_3$ roots are $\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j$ for $1\leq i<j\leq3$ and $\pm2\varepsilon_i$ for $1\leq i\leq3$. $$

In an orthonormal basis $(\varepsilon_i)$, the $B_n$ roots are $\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j$ for $i<j$ and $\pm\varepsilon_i$.

= B3 root system
{title2=$B_3$}
{c}
{parent=Bn root system}

The $B_3$ roots are $\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j$ for $1\leq i<j\leq3$ and $\pm\varepsilon_i$ for $1\leq i\leq3$. Its root basis $\alpha_1=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2$, $\alpha_2=\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_3$, $\alpha_3=\varepsilon_3$ exhibits it as the <dual root system> of the <C3 root system>. With $A_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i,\alpha_j^\vee\rangle$, its <Cartan matrix> is

\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-2\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The fundamental representation of highest weight $[1,0,0]$ is the seven-dimensional vector representation, with weights $\pm\varepsilon_1,\pm\varepsilon_2,\pm\varepsilon_3,0$.

= so4 Lie algebra

{c}
{parent=Special orthogonal Lie algebra}

The complexified so4 Lie algebra consists of four-by-four complex skew-symmetric matrices and has dimension six. Its root system is $D_2=A_1\times A_1$, reflecting its decomposition into two simple rank-one ideals.

= Chiral decomposition of the complexified so4 Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}_4(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$}
{c}
{parent=so4 Lie algebra}

The self-dual and anti-self-dual combinations of the skew generators form two commuting copies of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$. Consequently
$$
\mathfrak{so}_4(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C),
$$
and its <adjoint representation> decomposes as $(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf3)$.

= Spin group

The spin group $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is the connected double cover of $SO(n)$ constructed inside the units of a <Clifford algebra>.

= Spinor representation

A spinor representation is a representation of a <spin group>, or infinitesimally of a <special orthogonal Lie algebra>, constructed from a <Clifford algebra>. In even dimensions it can split into two chiral spinor representations.

= Lorentz algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Special orthogonal Lie algebra}

The Lorentz algebra has rotation generators $J_i$ and boost generators $K_i$ satisfying
$$
[J_i,J_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}J_k,\qquad
[J_i,K_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}K_k,\qquad
[K_i,K_j]=-\epsilon_{ijk}J_k.
$$

= Chiral decomposition of the complex Lorentz algebra
{parent=Lorentz algebra}

Over the complex numbers, $A_i=(J_i+iK_i)/2$ and $B_i=(J_i-iK_i)/2$ generate commuting copies of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)$, giving
$$
\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_\mathbb C\cong
\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C).
$$
Finite-dimensional irreducible representations are labelled $(j_L,j_R)$.

= Parity action on a Lorentz representation
{parent=Chiral decomposition of the complex Lorentz algebra}

Parity fixes $J_i$, negates $K_i$, and therefore exchanges the two chiral factors. It sends $(j_L,j_R)$ to $(j_R,j_L)$.

= Lorentz Casimir invariants
{parent=Chiral decomposition of the complex Lorentz algebra}

The quadratic contraction $M_{\mu\nu}M^{\mu\nu}$ is parity even, while $M_{\mu\nu}\widetilde M^{\mu\nu}$ is parity odd. Their linear combinations give the quadratic Casimirs of the two chiral $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ factors.

= so5 Lie algebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}_5$}
{c}
{parent=Special orthogonal Lie algebra}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}_5$ has dimension ten and root system $B_2$.

= SO5 to SO4 branching
{title2=$SO(5)\downarrow SO(4)$}
{c}
{parent=so5 Lie algebra}

Under the standard block-diagonal subgroup, the vector and adjoint representations branch as
$$
\mathbf5\to\mathbf4\oplus\mathbf1,
\qquad
\mathbf{10}\to\mathbf6\oplus\mathbf4.
$$
Using $\mathfrak{so}_4\cong\mathfrak{su}_2\oplus\mathfrak{su}_2$, these are $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\oplus(0,0)$ and $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)\oplus(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$.

= B2 root system
{title2=$B_2$}
{c}
{parent=Special orthogonal Lie algebra}

The $B_2$ root system consists of $\pm\varepsilon_1$, $\pm\varepsilon_2$, and $\pm\varepsilon_1\pm\varepsilon_2$. Its vector representation has weights $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,0,-\varepsilon_2,-\varepsilon_1$.

= Isomorphism between so5 and sp4
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}_5(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sp}_4(\mathbb C)$}
{c}

The root systems $B_2$ and $C_2$ are isomorphic after interchanging long and short simple-root labels. The classification of complex simple Lie algebras therefore gives $\mathfrak{so}_5(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sp}_4(\mathbb C)$.

= Fundamental representations of B2
{title2=$[1,0],[0,1]$}
{c}
{parent=B2 root system}

For simple roots $(1,0)$ and $(-1,1)$, the fundamental weights are $\omega_1=(1/2,1/2)$ and $\omega_2=(0,1)$. The representation $[1,0]$ is the four-dimensional spin representation with weights $(\pm1/2,\pm1/2)$; $[0,1]$ is the five-dimensional vector representation with weights $(\pm1,0),(0,\pm1),(0,0)$.

= Weyl dimension formula for B2
{title2=$\dim V(a\omega_1+b\omega_2)$}
{c}
{parent=Fundamental representations of B2}

When $\alpha_1$ is the short simple root, $\omega_1$ is the spin fundamental weight and $\omega_2$ is the vector fundamental weight. The <Weyl dimension formula> gives
$$
\dim V(a\omega_1+b\omega_2)
=\frac{(a+1)(b+1)(a+b+2)(a+2b+3)}6.
$$

= Traceless symmetric square of the defining so5 representation
{title2=$V(2\omega_2)$}
{c}
{parent=Weyl dimension formula for B2}

For the five-dimensional defining representation $V=V(\omega_2)$ of $\mathfrak{so}_5$, the invariant quadratic form splits
$$
S^2V\cong\mathbb C\oplus V(2\omega_2).
$$
Thus the traceless symmetric square is irreducible of dimension $15-1=14$.

= Tensor-square decomposition of the defining so5 representation
{title2=$V(\omega_2)^{\otimes2}=V(0)\oplus V(2\omega_1)\oplus V(2\omega_2)$}
{c}
{parent=Traceless symmetric square of the defining so5 representation}

For the five-dimensional defining representation of $\mathfrak{so}_5$, the <symmetric square> is the sum of its invariant trace and the traceless representation $V(2\omega_2)$, while the <exterior square> is the <adjoint representation> $V(2\omega_1)$. Hence
$$
V(\omega_2)\otimes V(\omega_2)
\cong V(0)\oplus V(2\omega_1)\oplus V(2\omega_2).
$$

= Cartan subalgebra
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{wiki}

= Maximal torus of a semisimple Lie algebra
{c}
{synonym}

For a complex semisimple <Lie algebra>, a Cartan subalgebra is a maximal abelian subalgebra consisting of semisimple elements. It is also called a maximal torus in this setting.

= Root-space decomposition
{parent=Cartan subalgebra}

For a Cartan subalgebra $\mathfrak t$, a semisimple <Lie algebra> decomposes as
$$
\mathfrak g=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}\mathfrak g_\alpha,
\qquad
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak t\}.
$$
The nonzero functionals $\alpha$ are the roots.

= Root space
{title2=$\mathfrak g_\alpha$}
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

For a root $\alpha$, its root space is the simultaneous eigenspace
$$
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak t\}
$$
of the <adjoint representation> of the <Cartan subalgebra>.

= Regular element of a semisimple Lie algebra
{parent=Root-space decomposition}
{c}

An element $x$ of a semisimple <Lie algebra> of rank $\ell$ is regular when its <centralizer> has the minimum possible dimension $\ell$.

= Regular element criterion in a Cartan subalgebra
{parent=Regular element of a semisimple Lie algebra}
{c}

For $t$ in a <Cartan subalgebra> $\mathfrak t$, the <root-space decomposition> gives
$$
\mathfrak z_{\mathfrak g}(t)=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha(t)=0}\mathfrak g_\alpha.
$$
Thus $t$ is regular exactly when $\alpha(t)\ne0$ for every root $\alpha$.

= Two-root decomposition with a nondegenerate trace form
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

Let a maximal torus $\mathfrak t$ give a decomposition $\mathfrak g=\mathfrak g_\alpha\oplus\mathfrak t\oplus\mathfrak g_\beta$. If $\mathfrak g$ has a nondegenerate trace form, then $\beta=-\alpha$, the opposite root spaces generate $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and
$$
\mathfrak g\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus\ker\alpha,
$$
where $\ker\alpha\subset\mathfrak t$ is central and abelian.

= sl2 subalgebra associated with a root
{title2=$\mathfrak m_\alpha\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2$}
{c}
{parent=Root-space decomposition}

For every root $\alpha$ of a complex semisimple Lie algebra, there are root vectors $e_\alpha\in\mathfrak g_\alpha$ and $f_\alpha\in\mathfrak g_{-\alpha}$ and an element $h_\alpha\in\mathfrak t$ satisfying
$$
[h_\alpha,e_\alpha]=2e_\alpha,qquad
[h_\alpha,f_\alpha]=-2f_\alpha,qquad
[e_\alpha,f_\alpha]=h_\alpha.
$$
Their span is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ subalgebra.

= Root system
{parent=Root-space decomposition}
{wiki}

A root system is a finite set of nonzero vectors closed under the reflections they define and satisfying the crystallographic integrality condition when it arises from a semisimple <Lie algebra>.

= Root reflection
{title2=$s_\alpha$}
{parent=Root system}

For a nonzero vector $\alpha$ in a <Euclidean normed vector space>, the root reflection is
$$
s_\alpha(v)=v-\frac{2(v,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.
$$
It fixes the hyperplane perpendicular to $\alpha$ and sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$.

= Cartan integer
{title2=$\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}

The Cartan integer associated with two roots is
$$
\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle
=\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}.
$$
A crystallographic root system requires every Cartan integer to be an <integer>.

= Reduced root system
{parent=Root system}

A root system is reduced when the only scalar multiples of any root $\alpha$ that are also roots are $\alpha$ and $-\alpha$.

= A3 root system
{title2=$A_3$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}

The $A_3$ root system is $\{e_i-e_j:1\leq i\ne j\leq4\}$ in the hyperplane of $\mathbb R^4$ whose coordinates sum to zero. It has $12$ roots and simple roots $e_1-e_2,e_2-e_3,e_3-e_4$.

= Cartan matrix of type A3
{title2=$A_3$ Cartan matrix}
{c}
{parent=A3 root system}

The Cartan matrix of type A3 is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

= Root string
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

For roots $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the roots of the form $\beta+n\alpha$ form an unbroken string $\beta-p\alpha,\ldots,\beta+q\alpha$, and $p-q=\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle$.

= Cartan matrix
{title2=$A_{ij}$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

For ordered simple roots, the Cartan matrix has entries $A_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i,\alpha_j^\vee\rangle$. It determines their angles and relative lengths and therefore determines the corresponding crystallographic root system.

= Positive root
{parent=Root system}

A choice of positive roots selects exactly one of $\alpha$ and $-\alpha$ and is closed under addition whenever the sum is a root.

= Simple root
{title2=$\alpha_i$}
{parent=Positive root}
{wiki}

The simple roots are the positive roots that cannot be expressed as sums of two positive roots. Every root is an integer combination of simple roots with coefficients of one sign.

= Simple-root subtraction lemma
{parent=Simple root}

If a positive root $\alpha$ is not simple, then $\alpha-\beta$ is a positive root for some simple root $\beta$. Iteration writes every positive root as a sum of simple roots whose successive partial sums are roots.

= Action of a simple reflection on positive roots
{parent=Simple root}

For a simple root $\alpha$, the reflection $s_\alpha$ sends $\alpha$ to $-\alpha$ and permutes all other positive roots.

= Highest root
{title2=$\theta$}
{parent=Positive root}

The highest root is the maximal positive root in the root order determined by the <simple root>[simple roots].

= Half-sum of positive roots
{title2=$\rho$}
{parent=Positive root}

= Weyl vector
{c}
{synonym}

The Weyl vector is $\rho=\frac12\sum_{\alpha\in R^+}\alpha$. It satisfies $\langle\rho,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle=1$ for every simple root.

= Coroot
{title2=$\alpha^\vee$}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

The coroot associated with a nonzero root is $\alpha^\vee=2\alpha/(\alpha,\alpha)$.

= Dual root system
{title2=$\Phi^\vee$}
{parent=Coroot}
{wiki}

The dual root system of $\Phi$ is $\Phi^\vee=\{\alpha^\vee:\alpha\in\Phi\}$. It has the transposed <Cartan matrix>, so its <Dynkin diagram> reverses every multiple-edge arrow; in particular, $B_n$ and $C_n$ are dual.

= Root lattice
{title2=$Q$}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

The root lattice is the integer span of the roots, equivalently of the <simple root>[simple roots].

= Kostant partition function
{title2=$P(\nu)$}
{c}
{parent=Root lattice}
{wiki}

The Kostant partition function $P(\nu)$ counts the ways to express a weight $\nu$ as a nonnegative integer combination of positive roots. By the <Poincare-Birkhoff-Witt theorem>, $P(\lambda-\mu)$ is the multiplicity of the weight $\mu$ in the <Verma module> $M(\lambda)$.

= Dominance order
{title2=$\mu\preceq\lambda$}
{parent=Root lattice}
{wiki}

For weights $\mu,\lambda$, one writes $\mu\preceq\lambda$ when $\lambda-\mu$ is a nonnegative integer combination of the simple roots.

= Weight lattice
{title2=$P$}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

The weight lattice is the set of vectors $\lambda$ for which $\langle\lambda,\alpha^\vee\rangle$ is an integer for every root $\alpha$.

= Dynkin label
{title2=$[\lambda^1,\ldots,\lambda^r]$}
{c}
{parent=Weight lattice}

The Dynkin labels of a weight $\lambda$ are its simple-coroot coordinates $\lambda^i=\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$. Thus $\lambda=\sum_i\lambda^i\omega_i$ in the basis of <fundamental weight>[fundamental weights].

= Dominant weight
{parent=Weight lattice}
{wiki=Weight_(representation_theory)#Dominant_integral_weights}

A weight is dominant when all its <Dynkin label>[Dynkin labels] are nonnegative.

= Dominant integral weight
{parent=Weight lattice}
{wiki=Weight_(representation_theory)#Dominant_integral_weights}

An integral weight $\lambda$ is dominant when $\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle\geq0$ for every simple coroot. Equivalently, it is a nonnegative integer combination of the fundamental weights.

= Fundamental weight
{title2=$\omega_i$}
{parent=Weight lattice}
{wiki}

The fundamental weights are dual to the simple coroots: $\langle\omega_i,\alpha_j^\vee\rangle=\delta_{ij}$.

= Fundamental representation
{parent=Fundamental weight}
{wiki}

The fundamental representation associated with a fundamental weight $\omega_i$ is the irreducible highest-weight representation $V(\omega_i)$.

= Dynkin index
{c}
{title2=$I(R)$}
{parent=Fundamental representation}
{wiki}

The Dynkin index of a representation $R$ fixes the trace normalization $\operatorname{tr}_R(T^aT^b)=I(R)\delta^{ab}$ up to the convention for the generators. It controls matter contributions to gauge beta functions and mixed anomalies.

= Cubic anomaly coefficient
{title2=$A(R)$}
{parent=Dynkin index}

The cubic anomaly coefficient is defined by the symmetrized trace of three representation matrices. A conjugate representation has the opposite coefficient, while a real or pseudoreal representation has coefficient zero.

= Two-index symmetric representation
{title2=$\operatorname{Sym}^2V$}
{parent=Fundamental representation}

The two-index symmetric representation of $SU(N)$ is the symmetric square of its fundamental representation. It has dimension $N(N+1)/2$, Dynkin index $N+2$, and cubic anomaly coefficient $N+4$ when the fundamental index and anomaly are normalized to one.

= Two-index antisymmetric representation
{title2=$\Lambda^2V$}
{parent=Fundamental representation}

The two-index antisymmetric representation of $SU(N)$ is the exterior square of its fundamental representation. It has dimension $N(N-1)/2$, Dynkin index $N-2$, and cubic anomaly coefficient $N-4$ in the same normalization.

= Weyl reflection
{title2=$s_\alpha$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}

The reflection associated with a root is $s_\alpha(x)=x-\langle x,\alpha^\vee\rangle\alpha$.

= Reflection group of a root system
{title2=$W(\Phi)$}
{parent=Weyl reflection}

The reflection group of a root system $\Phi$ is the subgroup of the orthogonal group generated by the reflections $s_\alpha$ for $\alpha\in\Phi$. It permutes the roots and acts freely and transitively on the fundamental systems.

= Weyl group
{title2=$W$}
{c}
{parent=Reflection group of a root system}
{wiki}

The Weyl group is generated by the <Weyl reflection>[reflections] in the roots.

= Coxeter length
{title2=$\ell(w)$}
{c}
{parent=Weyl group}
{wiki=Length_function}

The Coxeter length $\ell(w)$ is the smallest number of simple reflections whose product is $w$. Its parity gives the sign of a Weyl-group element.

= Weyl stabilizer of a dominant point
{c}
{parent=Coxeter length}

If $\lambda$ and $w\lambda$ both lie in the closed dominant chamber, then $w$ is a product of simple reflections whose walls contain $\lambda$. In particular, $w\lambda=\lambda$.

= Inversion set of a Weyl-group element
{title2=$N(w)$}
{c}
{parent=Coxeter length}
{wiki=Inversion_set}

For a chosen <positive system of a root system> $\Pi$, the inversion set is
$$
N(w)=\{\beta\in\Pi:w(\beta)\in-\Pi\}.
$$
For a finite <Weyl group>, $|N(w)|=\ell(w)$.

= Positive-root criterion for Coxeter length
{parent=Coxeter length}

In the geometric representation of a Coxeter system,
$$
\ell(ws_i)>\ell(w)\iff w(e_i)\text{ is a positive root},
$$
and
$$
\ell(s_iw)>\ell(w)\iff w^{-1}(e_i)\text{ is a positive root}.
$$
Replacing “positive” by “negative” reverses either length inequality.

= Dynkin diagram
{c}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki}

A Dynkin diagram records the angles and relative lengths of the simple roots. A multiple-edge arrow points toward the shorter root.

= Simply laced root system
{parent=Dynkin diagram}
{wiki}

An irreducible root system is simply laced when all roots have the same length, equivalently when its <Dynkin diagram> has only single edges. This is also equivalent to
$$
\langle\alpha,\beta^\vee\rangle\in\{0,\pm1\}
$$
whenever $\alpha\ne\pm\beta$.

= ADE classification
{c}
{parent=Simply laced root system}

The connected positive-definite simply-laced Dynkin diagrams are $A_n$ for $n\geq1$, $D_n$ for $n\geq4$, and the exceptional diagrams $E_6,E_7,E_8$.

= D3 root system
{title2=$D_3$}
{c}
{parent=Dynkin diagram}

The $D_3$ roots are $\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j$ for $1\leq i<j\leq3$. The simple roots
$$
\alpha_1=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2,\quad
\alpha_2=\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_3,\quad
\alpha_3=\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_3
$$
form the three-node chain $\alpha_2-\alpha_1-\alpha_3$, so $D_3\cong A_3$.

= Isomorphism between so6 and sl4
{title2=$\mathfrak{so}_6(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C)$}
{c}
{parent=D3 root system}

The root-system isomorphism $D_3\cong A_3$ and the classification of complex simple Lie algebras give $\mathfrak{so}_6(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C)$.

= Extended Dynkin diagram
{c}
{parent=Dynkin diagram}

An extended, or affine, Dynkin diagram adjoins the root $\alpha_0=-\theta$, where $\theta$ is the <highest root>.

= Long-root subsystem
{parent=Root system}

The roots of maximal length in a <root system> form a root subsystem. A chamber for the original system lies in a unique chamber for this long-root subsystem, because its interior avoids every long-root reflecting hyperplane.

= G2 root system
{title2=$G_2$}
{c}
{parent=Root system}
{wiki=G2_(mathematics)}

The $G_2$ root system has six short and six long roots. For a short simple root $\alpha_1$ and a long simple root $\alpha_2$, its positive roots are
$$
\alpha_1,\ \alpha_2,\ \alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 2\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 3\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\ 3\alpha_1+2\alpha_2.
$$

= Long-root A2 subsystem of G2
{title2=$A_2\subset G_2$}
{c}
{parent=G2 root system}

For short and long simple roots $\alpha,\beta$ of $G_2$, the long roots form an $A_2$ root system with simple roots $\delta_1=\beta$ and $\delta_2=3\alpha+\beta$. If $\omega_1,\omega_2$ are the corresponding $A_2$ <fundamental weights>, then $\alpha=\omega_2-\omega_1$ and $\alpha^\vee=\delta_2^\vee-\delta_1^\vee$.

= Restriction of the seven-dimensional G2 representation to long-root A2
{title2=$\mathbf7\downarrow_{A_2}=\mathbf3\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1$}
{c}
{parent=Long-root A2 subsystem of G2}

The seven-dimensional fundamental $G_2$ representation has weights zero and the six short roots. Under its long-root $A_2$ subalgebra, these are the weights of the defining three-dimensional representation, its dual, and the trivial representation:
$$
V_7\downarrow_{A_2}\cong V(\omega_1)\oplus V(\omega_2)\oplus V(0).
$$

= Root-system finiteness lemma
{parent=Root system}

For roots $\alpha\ne\pm\beta$,
$$
\langle\alpha,\beta^\vee\rangle
\langle\beta,\alpha^\vee\rangle
=4\cos^2\theta\in\{0,1,2,3\},
$$
where $\theta$ is their angle. Integrality gives an integer, and nonparallelism makes it strictly smaller than four.

= Classification of rank-two root systems
{parent=Root-system finiteness lemma}

Every reduced crystallographic rank-two root system is isomorphic to $A_1\times A_1$, $A_2$, $B_2$, or $G_2$. Their simple-root angles are respectively $\pi/2$, $2\pi/3$, $3\pi/4$, and $5\pi/6$.

= Weyl group of a rank-two root system
{parent=Classification of rank-two root systems}

The two simple reflections generate a <dihedral group>. For $A_1\times A_1$, $A_2$, $B_2$, and $G_2$, the rotation has order $2,3,4,6$, so the Weyl groups have orders $4,6,8,12$ respectively.

= Fundamental system of a root system

{synonym}

= Base of a root system
{synonym}

A fundamental system $\Delta$ is a basis made of roots such that every root is a linear combination of $\Delta$ whose nonzero coefficients all have the same sign. Its members are the <simple root>[simple roots].

= Positive system of a root system
{title2=$\Pi$}
{parent=Fundamental system of a root system}

The positive system associated with a <fundamental system of a root system> $\Delta$ consists of the roots whose coordinates in the basis $\Delta$ are nonnegative.

= Fundamental chamber of a root system
{parent=Fundamental system of a root system}

= Weyl chamber
{c}
{synonym}

The fundamental chamber is the connected component
$$
C=\{v:\langle v,\alpha\rangle>0\text{ for every }\alpha\in\Delta\}
$$
of the complement of the reflecting hyperplanes. The Weyl group acts freely and transitively on its chambers.

= Closed dominant Weyl chamber
{c}

For a root basis $\Delta$, the closed dominant chamber is
$$
\overline{C(\Delta)}=\{\lambda:\langle\lambda,\alpha^\vee\rangle\geq0
\text{ for every }\alpha\in\Delta\}.
$$
Every Weyl-group orbit meets it in exactly one point.

= Highest-weight representation
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{wiki=Highest-weight_representation}

A highest-weight representation is generated by a <weight vector> killed by every positive-root subspace.

= Highest weight of a representation
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}

= Highest weight
{synonym}

The highest weight of a highest-weight representation is the weight of a generating vector annihilated by every positive-root operator. Finite-dimensional irreducible representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra are classified by dominant integral highest weights.

= Ladder operator
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki}

A ladder operator maps weight vectors to weight vectors whose weights differ by one root. Positive-root vectors raise weights and negative-root vectors lower them.

= Raising operator
{parent=Ladder operator}

A raising operator is a positive-root vector. Acting on a weight vector of weight $\mu$ either gives zero or a weight vector of weight $\mu+\alpha$.

= Lowering operator
{parent=Ladder operator}

A lowering operator is a negative-root vector. Acting on a weight vector of weight $\mu$ either gives zero or a weight vector of weight $\mu-\alpha$.

= Cartan-Weyl basis
{c}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki=Cartan–Weyl_basis}

A Cartan-Weyl basis consists of a basis of a <Cartan subalgebra> together with root vectors $E_\alpha$. It makes weight raising and lowering explicit through $[H,E_\alpha]=\alpha(H)E_\alpha$.

= Disentangling identity
{parent=Cartan-Weyl basis}

A disentangling identity factors the exponential of a sum of noncommuting Lie-algebra generators into an ordered product of exponentials. For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, a common order is $e^{aE_-}e^{bH}e^{cE_+}$.

= Complete reducibility of semisimple Lie algebra representations
{c}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki=Weyl's_theorem_on_complete_reducibility}

Every finite-dimensional representation of a complex semisimple Lie algebra is a direct sum of irreducible representations.

= Generation by fundamental representations
{parent=Highest-weight representation}

If $\lambda=\sum_im_i\omega_i$ is dominant integral, the tensor product $\bigotimes_iV(\omega_i)^{\otimes m_i}$ contains $V(\lambda)$ as the irreducible summand generated by the tensor product of highest-weight vectors.

= Weight multiplicity decreases away from a dominant weight
{parent=Highest-weight representation}

If $\mu$ and $\lambda$ are weights of a finite-dimensional representation, $\mu$ is dominant, and $\mu\preceq\lambda$, then
$$
\operatorname{mult}(\mu)\geq\operatorname{mult}(\lambda).
$$
Successively subtract a simple root $\alpha_i$ for which $\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle>0$ and use injectivity of the corresponding $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ lowering operator.

= Weight vector
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki=Weight_(representation_theory)}

A vector $v$ has weight $\mu\in\mathfrak t^*$ when $h v=\mu(h)v$ for every $h$ in the Cartan subalgebra.

= Highest-weight vector
{parent=Weight vector}

A highest-weight vector is a nonzero <weight vector> annihilated by every positive-root <raising operator>. The module it generates is a <highest-weight representation>.

= Weight of a representation
{parent=Weight vector}

A weight of a representation is a functional $\mu$ whose <weight space> is nonzero.

= Weight space
{title2=$V_\mu$}
{parent=Weight vector}
{wiki=Weight_(representation_theory)#Weight_spaces}

The weight space $V_\mu$ consists of all <weight vector>[weight vectors] of weight $\mu$, together with zero.

= Extremal weight space
{parent=Weight space}

For a finite-dimensional irreducible <highest-weight representation> with highest weight $\lambda$, every weight in the <Weyl group> orbit $W\lambda$ is extremal and has <weight multiplicity> one.

= Weight multiplicity
{parent=Weight space}
{wiki=Weight_(representation_theory)#Weight_multiplicity}

The multiplicity of a weight $\mu$ is the dimension of its <weight space>.

= Singular vector
{parent=Weight vector}

A singular vector is a nonzero <weight vector> annihilated by the positive nilpotent subalgebra $\mathfrak n^+$.

= Verma module
{title2=$M_\lambda$}
{c}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki}

For a Borel subalgebra $\mathfrak b=\mathfrak t\oplus\mathfrak n^+$ and a weight $\lambda$, the Verma module is
$$
M_\lambda=U(\mathfrak g)\otimes_{U(\mathfrak b)}\mathbb C_\lambda,
$$
where $\mathfrak n^+$ kills $\mathbb C_\lambda$ and $h\in\mathfrak t$ acts by $\lambda(h)$.

= Universal property of a Verma module
{parent=Verma module}

If a $\mathfrak g$-module contains a vector $v$ of weight $\lambda$ annihilated by $\mathfrak n^+$, there is a unique homomorphism $M_\lambda\to V$ sending the canonical highest-weight vector to $v$. Every highest-weight module of weight $\lambda$ is therefore a quotient of $M_\lambda$.

= Irreducible quotient of a Verma module
{parent=Verma module}

The sum of all proper submodules of $M_\lambda$ is its unique maximal proper submodule. The quotient is the unique irreducible highest-weight module $V(\lambda)$.

= Maximal proper submodule of a dominant integral Verma module
{parent=Irreducible quotient of a Verma module}
{c}

If $\lambda$ is a <dominant integral weight>, the maximal proper submodule of $M(\lambda)$ is generated by the simple-root <singular vectors>
$$
f_i^{\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle+1}v_\lambda.
$$
The generator associated with $\alpha_i$ has weight $s_i(\lambda+\rho)-\rho$.

= Formal character of a weight module
{title2=$\operatorname{ch}V$}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}

For a module with finite-dimensional <weight space>[weight spaces], its formal character is $\operatorname{ch}V=\sum_\mu(\dim V_\mu)e^\mu$ in the completed group algebra of the <weight lattice>.

= Weyl character formula
{c}
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki}

For a dominant integral weight $\lambda$, the irreducible character is
$$
\operatorname{ch}L_\lambda=
\frac{\sum_{w\in W}(-1)^{\ell(w)}e^{w(\lambda+\rho)}}
{\sum_{w\in W}(-1)^{\ell(w)}e^{w\rho}}.
$$

= Weyl denominator formula
{c}
{parent=Weyl character formula}
{wiki}

For a positive root system $\Phi^+$,
$$
\sum_{w\in W}(-1)^{\ell(w)}e^{w\rho}
=e^\rho\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^+}(1-e^{-\alpha}).
$$

= Weyl dimension formula
{c}
{parent=Weyl character formula}
{wiki}

The dimension obtained by evaluating the <Weyl character formula> at the identity is
$$
\dim L_\lambda=\prod_{\alpha\in R^+}
\frac{\langle\lambda+\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}
{\langle\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}.
$$

= q-character of a highest-weight representation
{title2=$\operatorname{ch}_qL_\lambda$}
{parent=Weyl character formula}

For the principal specialization, let $h_{\mathrm{pr}}=2\rho^\vee$, so every simple root takes value two on $h_{\mathrm{pr}}$. The q-character is
$$
\operatorname{ch}_qL_\lambda=
\sum_\mu(\dim L_\lambda[\mu])q^{\mu(h_{\mathrm{pr}})}.
$$
It is obtained from the <Weyl character formula> by substituting $e^\mu=q^{\mu(h_{\mathrm{pr}})}$.

= Crystal basis
{parent=Highest-weight representation}
{wiki}

A crystal basis is the combinatorial limit of a basis of a quantum-group representation. Its colored directed graph records the actions of the <Kashiwara operator>[Kashiwara lowering operators].

= Root-string property of a crystal
{parent=Crystal basis}

For each simple root $\alpha_i$, the $i$-colored edges of a crystal form strings. Along such a string, $\widetilde e_i$ raises the weight by $\alpha_i$ and $\widetilde f_i$ lowers it by $\alpha_i$.

= Kashiwara operator
{title2=$\widetilde e_i,\widetilde f_i$}
{c}
{parent=Crystal basis}
{wiki=Crystal_base#Definition}

On a crystal, $\widetilde f_i$ follows an edge of color $i$ and $\widetilde e_i$ follows that edge backwards.

= Tensor product of crystals
{parent=Crystal basis}
{wiki=Crystal_base#Tensor_products_of_crystals}

The tensor product crystal uses the $i$-string data $\varepsilon_i$ and $\varphi_i$ to decide on which tensor factor a <Kashiwara operator> acts. Its connected components are highest-weight crystals and encode the decomposition of the tensor product representation.

= Casimir element
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{wiki}

= Casimir operator
{c}
{synonym}

The quadratic Casimir element is a central element of $U(\mathfrak g)$. On a highest-weight module of highest weight $\lambda$, one standard normalization makes it act by $|\lambda+\rho|^2-|\rho|^2$.

= Casimir eigenvalue for sl2
{title2=$\frac12m(m+2)$}
{c}
{parent=Casimir element}

For $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$, and $[e,f]=h$, the Casimir
$$
\Omega=ef+fe+\frac12h^2
$$
acts on the irreducible highest-weight module $V(m)$ by the scalar $\frac12m(m+2)$.

= Principal sl2 subalgebra
{title2=$\mathfrak{sl}_2^{\mathrm{pr}}$}
{c}
{parent=Semisimple Lie algebra}
{wiki=Principal_three-dimensional_subgroup}

A principal sl2 subalgebra of a semisimple <Lie algebra> has semisimple generator $h_{\mathrm{pr}}=2\rho^\vee$. Restriction to it packages a representation's weights into ordinary $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ weight strings.

= Principal nilpotent element
{parent=Principal sl2 subalgebra}
{wiki}

A principal nilpotent element of a semisimple <Lie algebra> is a nilpotent element whose <centralizer> has dimension equal to the rank. For nonzero simple-root vectors $e_i$, the sum $e=\sum_i e_i$ is principal nilpotent and forms the raising element of a <principal sl2 subalgebra>.

= Coxeter group
{c}
{parent=Lie theory}
{wiki}

A Coxeter group is a <group> with a presentation
$$
W=\langle x_i\ (i\in I): (x_ix_j)^{m_{ij}}=1\rangle,
$$
where $m_{ii}=1$ and $m_{ij}=m_{ji}\in\{2,3,\ldots,\infty\}$ for $i\ne j$. The pair consisting of $W$ and its distinguished generating reflections is a <Coxeter system>.

= Coxeter system
{c}
{parent=Coxeter group}

A Coxeter system $(W,I,M)$ records a <Coxeter group> $W$, its set $I$ of simple generators, and its <Coxeter matrix> $M=(m_{ij})$.

= Coxeter matrix
{c}
{parent=Coxeter system}
{wiki}

The Coxeter matrix of a <Coxeter system> is the symmetric matrix whose entry $m_{ij}$ is the order of $x_ix_j$.

= Coxeter graph
{c}
{parent=Coxeter matrix}

= Coxeter diagram
{c}
{synonym}

The Coxeter graph has vertex set $I$, joins $i$ and $j$ when $m_{ij}\geq3$, and labels the edge by $m_{ij}$ when $m_{ij}>3$. A <Coxeter system> is <irreducible Coxeter system>[irreducible] exactly when this graph is connected.

= Irreducible Coxeter system
{c}
{parent=Coxeter graph}

An irreducible Coxeter system is one whose <Coxeter graph> is connected. The connected components of a Coxeter graph give the direct-product decomposition of its Coxeter group.

= Type E(p,q,k) Coxeter graph
{title2=$E(p,q,k)$}
{c}
{parent=Coxeter graph}

The type $E(p,q,k)$ graph is a simply-laced tree with one trivalent vertex and arms containing $p$, $q$, and $k$ vertices beyond that vertex.

= Reduced expression in a Coxeter group
{parent=Coxeter system}
{wiki=Reduced_word}

= Reduced word in a Coxeter group
{synonym}
{c}

A reduced expression for $w\in W$ is a product of the fewest possible simple generators representing $w$. The number of factors is its <Coxeter length> $\ell(w)$.

= Deletion condition for involutory generators
{parent=Reduced expression in a Coxeter group}

For a group generated by involutions, the deletion condition says that every nonreduced word can be shortened without changing its value by deleting two of its letters.

= Folding-grid proof of the deletion condition
{parent=Deletion condition for involutory generators}

Arrange the lengths of all consecutive subwords of a word in a triangular grid. Under the <folding condition>, the boundary between length ascents and descents must contain a folding square. Its equal opposite vertices identify two letters that can be deleted. Induction proves the <deletion condition for involutory generators>.

= Exchange condition for a Coxeter group
{parent=Reduced expression in a Coxeter group}

If $w=s_1\cdots s_n$ is a reduced expression and $s$ is simple with $\ell(ws)<\ell(w)$, then
$$
ws=s_1\cdots\widehat{s_j}\cdots s_n
$$
for some index $j$. The analogous statement holds for multiplication on the left.

= Matsumoto theorem
{c}
{parent=Reduced expression in a Coxeter group}

Matsumoto's theorem says that any two reduced expressions for one element of a Coxeter group are connected by a finite sequence of braid moves.

= Tits word reduction theorem
{c}
{parent=Matsumoto theorem}

Every word in simple Coxeter generators can be reduced by braid moves and cancellations $ss\mapsto1$. Consequently, a word is reduced exactly when no sequence of braid moves can make a cancellation possible.

= Geometric representation of a Coxeter group
{parent=Coxeter system}
{c}

For a finite-rank <Coxeter system>, let $V$ have basis $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ and symmetric <bilinear form>
$$
\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=-2\cos(\pi/m_{ij}).
$$
Its geometric representation sends the generator $x_i$ to the reflection
$$
\sigma(x_i)v=v-\langle v,e_i\rangle e_i.
$$

= Coxeter Gram matrix
{c}
{parent=Geometric representation of a Coxeter group}

The Coxeter Gram matrix is the <Gram matrix> $G=(-2\cos(\pi/m_{ij}))_{i,j\in I}$ of the distinguished basis in the <geometric representation of a Coxeter group>.

= Dual geometric representation of a Coxeter group
{title2=$\sigma^*$}
{c}
{parent=Geometric representation of a Coxeter group}

The dual geometric representation acts on $V^*$ by
$$
(\sigma^*(w)f)(v)=f(\sigma(w^{-1})v).
$$
The open fundamental chamber consists of the functionals positive on every simple basis vector.

= Tits cone
{title2=$U$}
{c}
{parent=Dual geometric representation of a Coxeter group}
{wiki}

For the closed fundamental chamber $D\subseteq V^*$, the Tits cone is
$$
U=\bigcup_{w\in W}wD.
$$
It equals all of $V^*$ exactly when the finite-rank Coxeter group $W$ is finite.

= Coxeter complex
{c}
{parent=Dual geometric representation of a Coxeter group}
{wiki}

The Coxeter complex has faces represented by cosets $wW_J$ of <standard parabolic subgroup>[standard parabolic subgroups], ordered by reverse inclusion. Geometrically, its maximal simplices are the chambers of the Coxeter arrangement intersected with a sphere.

= Reflection of a Coxeter group
{parent=Coxeter system}

A reflection of a Coxeter system $(W,S)$ is a conjugate $wsw^{-1}$ of a simple generator $s\in S$.

= Wall of a Coxeter group
{title2=$H_r$}
{parent=Reflection of a Coxeter group}

The wall associated with a reflection $r$ consists of the Cayley-graph edges fixed setwise and reversed by left multiplication by $r$. Its complement has two connected components.

= Half-space of a Coxeter group
{parent=Wall of a Coxeter group}

The two connected components of the complement of a <wall of a Coxeter group> are its half-spaces. A wall separates two group elements when they lie in opposite half-spaces.

= Standard parabolic subgroup
{title2=$W_J$}
{parent=Coxeter system}

For $J\subseteq I$, the standard parabolic subgroup is $W_J=\langle s_j:j\in J\rangle$. Its cosets index faces of a fixed type in the <Coxeter complex>.

= Spherical subset of a Coxeter system
{parent=Standard parabolic subgroup}

A subset $T\subseteq S$ is spherical when its <standard parabolic subgroup> $W_T$ is finite.

= Davis complex
{title2=$\Sigma(W,S)$}
{c}
{parent=Standard parabolic subgroup}
{wiki}

The Davis complex is the geometric realization of the poset of cosets $wW_T$ for spherical subsets $T\subseteq S$. Equivalently, it is the <basic construction of a Coxeter group> obtained by gluing copies of the Davis chamber along mirrors. It is a contractible proper $W$-CW complex.

= Basic construction of a Coxeter group
{title2=$U(W,K)$}
{parent=Davis complex}

For a mirrored chamber $K$, the basic construction is
$$
U(W,K)=(W\times K)/\sim,
$$
where $(w,x)\sim(w',x)$ when $w^{-1}w'$ belongs to the subgroup generated by the mirrors containing $x$.

= Folding condition
{parent=Coxeter system}

For involutory generators, the folding condition requires that simple multiplication never preserve length and that simultaneous left and right ascents either combine to a two-step ascent or fold: if both $s_iw$ and $ws_j$ have length $\ell(w)+1$, then either $\ell(s_iws_j)=\ell(w)+2$ or $w=s_iws_j$.

= Braid relation in a Coxeter group
{parent=Folding condition}

For generators $s_i,s_j$ with $m_{ij}<\infty$, the braid relation equates the two alternating words of length $m_{ij}$ beginning with $s_i$ and $s_j$, respectively. When $m_{ij}=2$, it says that the generators commute.

= Coxeter element
{c}
{parent=Coxeter system}
{wiki}

A Coxeter element is a product of all simple generators in some order. For a finite irreducible <Coxeter group>, all Coxeter elements are conjugate.

= Coxeter number
{title2=$h$}
{c}
{parent=Coxeter element}
{wiki}

The Coxeter number is the <order of a group element>[order] of a <Coxeter element> in a finite irreducible <Coxeter group>.

= Finite Coxeter group
{c}
{parent=Coxeter group}

A finite Coxeter group is a <Coxeter group> with finitely many elements. Its <Coxeter Gram matrix> is positive definite.

= Longest element of a finite Coxeter group

The longest element $w_0$ is the unique element of maximal <Coxeter length> in a finite <Coxeter group>. It is an involution and satisfies $\ell(w_0w)=\ell(w_0)-\ell(w)$.

= E7 Coxeter group

The $E_7$ Coxeter graph is the simply-laced seven-vertex tree whose three arms have lengths $1$, $2$, and $3$. Its Coxeter Gram matrix is positive definite and has determinant $2$.

= Signed symmetric group

= Affine Coxeter group

An irreducible affine Coxeter group has a positive-semidefinite <Coxeter Gram matrix> with a one-dimensional <radical of a bilinear form>[radical].

= Hyperbolic Coxeter group

{parent=Coxeter group}

A hyperbolic Coxeter group has a nondegenerate <Coxeter Gram matrix> of Lorentzian signature in its standard geometric realization.

= BN-pair
{c}
{parent=Coxeter group}
{wiki}

A BN-pair in a group $G$ consists of subgroups $B,N$ such that $G=\langle B,N\rangle$, $H=B\cap N$ is normal in $N$, and $W=N/H$ has distinguished involutory generators satisfying the Bruhat multiplication axioms. The quotient $W$ is its <Weyl group>.

The Bruhat decomposition is the disjoint union

G=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}B\dot wB

associated with a <BN-pair>.

= Hecke algebra
{c}
{parent=Coxeter group}
{wiki}

A Hecke algebra is a deformation of the <group algebra> of a <Coxeter group>, obtained by deforming the quadratic relations for its simple generators while retaining the braid relations.

= Generic Hecke algebra of a Coxeter system
{title2=$H_{\mathrm{gen}}(W,\{a_i\})$}
{c}
{parent=Hecke algebra}
{wiki=Iwahori–Hecke_algebra}

Over the polynomial parameter ring, the generic Hecke algebra has basis $(T_w)_{w\in W}$ and multiplication
$$
T_iT_w=
\begin{cases}
T_{x_iw},&\ell(x_iw)>\ell(w),\\
a_iT_{x_iw}+(a_i-1)T_w,&\ell(x_iw)<\ell(w).
\end{cases}
$$
The parameters must agree for conjugate simple generators; equivalently, $(T_i-a_i)(T_i+1)=0$ together with the braid relations presents the algebra.

= 0-Hecke algebra
{c}
{parent=Generic Hecke algebra of a Coxeter system}

The 0-Hecke algebra is the <specialization of an algebra>[specialization] $a_i=0$ of a <generic Hecke algebra of a Coxeter system>. Its generators obey $T_i^2=-T_i$.

= Iwahori-Hecke algebra of a BN-pair

{c}
{parent=Hecke algebra}
{wiki=Iwahori–Hecke_algebra}

For a finite group $G$ with a <BN-pair>, the Iwahori-Hecke algebra over $k$ is the endomorphism algebra of the permutation module $k[G/B]$, up to the conventional opposite algebra. Its standard basis is indexed by the <Bruhat decomposition of a BN-pair>[Bruhat double cosets].

= Hecke parameter of a BN-pair

For a representative $\dot x_i\in N$, the Hecke parameter is
$$
q_i=[B:B\cap\dot x_iB\dot x_i^{-1}].
$$
The <Iwahori-Hecke algebra of a BN-pair> is obtained from the generic algebra by $a_i\mapsto q_i\cdot1_k$.
