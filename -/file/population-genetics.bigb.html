= Population genetics
{wiki}

Population genetics studies the frequencies of <alleles> and <genotypes> in populations, and their changes through inheritance, mutation, selection, migration and random sampling. <Kingman's coalescent> describes a neutral sample's ancestry backward in time.

= Genetic hitchhiking
{parent=Population genetics}

An allele changes in frequency because of association with another selected allele. Recombination can weaken that association, so the response differs between linked and freely recombining backgrounds.

= Site frequency spectrum
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$\xi_i$}

The counts or proportions of segregating variants at different sample frequencies. Unfolded spectra use an inferred ancestral state; folded spectra use minor-allele counts.

= Unfolded site frequency spectrum
{parent=Site frequency spectrum}

= McDonald-Kreitman test
{parent=Population genetics}
{c}
{title2=$\alpha=1-D_SP_N/(D_NP_S)$}

A comparison of nonsynonymous and synonymous polymorphism and divergence. Under a simple neutral model their ratios match. An excess of nonsynonymous divergence can support adaptive fixation, but mildly deleterious polymorphism, changing demography and site-class assumptions affect interpretation.<Adaptive protein evolution at the Adh locus in Drosophila by John H. McDonald and Martin Kreitman>[Original polymorphism-divergence test]{ref}

= Conservation genetics
{parent=Population genetics}

= Population structure
{parent=Population genetics}

Nonrandom distribution of genetic variation among populations, shaped by ancestry, movement, reproduction and drift.

= Isolation by distance
{parent=Population structure}

Increasing genetic differentiation with geographic separation when <gene flow> tends to occur over shorter distances.

= Folded site frequency spectrum
{parent=Population genetics}

The <folded site frequency spectrum> counts variable sites by the less frequent allele count, without identifying which allele is ancestral. With four sampled sequences, counts one and three both contribute to the $3:1$ class, whereas count two gives the $2:2$ class.

= Genetic relatedness
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$r$}

A measure of genetic association between individuals relative to a reference population. Simple pedigree values assume specified inheritance and mating patterns; relatedness relevant to selection is not merely the fraction of genes two individuals share.

= Neutral drift
{parent=Population genetics}

Stochastic changes in lineage frequency without an imposed fitness difference. In a renewing stem-cell compartment, equivalent clones can be lost or expand while the total population remains approximately constant.

= Simons Genome Diversity Project
{parent=Population genetics}
{c}

A genome-sequencing project sampling diverse human populations and informing models of shared and differentiated population ancestry.

= Genetic admixture
{parent=Population genetics}

= Admixture
{synonym}

Mixing of ancestry through interbreeding between previously differentiated populations. A simple branching tree may not represent the resulting history.

= Coalescent theory
{parent=Population genetics}

A framework tracing sampled genetic lineages backwards to shared ancestry. A <gene tree> coalescence time is not generally identical to a population separation or migration time.

= Muller's ratchet
{parent=Population genetics}
{c}

Stochastic loss of the least mutation-loaded class in a finite population with little or no <genetic recombination> and negligible reversal of the harmful changes. Recombination can recreate some low-load combinations.

= Hill-Robertson interference
{parent=Population genetics}
{c}

Reduced efficacy of <natural selection> caused by associations between selected <genetic loci> in finite populations. <genetic recombination> can relieve some of this interference by changing the associations.

= Fixation index
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$F_{ST}$}

= FST
{synonym}
{c}

A statistic describing differentiation among <biological populations> relative to within-population variation. High $F_{ST}$ can reflect reduced diversity within a population; it does not alone establish adaptive sequence divergence.

= Selective sweep
{parent=Population genetics}

A change in linked variation accompanying the spread of a favorable <allele>. Its genomic signature depends on ancestry, <recombination rate> and whether selection acts on a new or pre-existing variant; a <population bottleneck> can mimic some features.

= Clonal interference
{parent=Population genetics}

Competition among independently arising beneficial lineages in a population with little or no <genetic recombination>. A favorable <mutation> can be lost because a competing lineage spreads more successfully.

= Selection coefficient
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$s$}

A parameter measuring a relative reproductive advantage or disadvantage under a specified convention. For a <haploid> variant with relative <genotype fitness> $1+s$, positive $s$ is advantageous; other conventions require translation before comparison.

= Distribution of beneficial fitness effects
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$f_b(s)$}

The <probability distribution> of increases in <genotype fitness> among beneficial <mutations> in a specified <genetic background> and environment. Weighting arrival effects by <fixation probability> changes the distribution among successful substitutions.

= Standing genetic variation
{parent=Population genetics}

Inherited variation already present before a focal environmental change or episode of <natural selection>. Reuse of an existing <allele> differs historically from independent origin through a new <mutation>.

= Adaptive walk
{parent=Population genetics}

A sequence of changes between <genotypes> or <phenotypes> under <natural selection>. A model with one substitution at a time is an approximation that can fail when many beneficial lineages compete.

= Adaptive substitution
{parent=Population genetics}

An inherited sequence change that spreads to <allele fixation> because of a favorable effect on <genotype fitness> in the relevant context. The distribution of effects among substitutions differs from that among newly arising <mutations>.

= Fisher's geometric model
{parent=Population genetics}
{c}

A model in which a <phenotype> is a point in a multidimensional trait space and <genotype fitness> declines with displacement from an optimum. With displacement $\mathbf z$ and mutational step $\mathbf a$, improvement in squared distance requires $2\mathbf z\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf a+\|\mathbf a\|^2<0$.

= Heterozygote advantage
{parent=Population genetics}

A <heterozygote> has greater <genotype fitness> than either corresponding <homozygote>. In the deterministic random-mating two-allele viability-selection model this gives an attracting interior <allele frequency>, unlike the repelling interior frequency produced by <underdominance>.

= Underdominance
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Heterozygote disadvantage
{synonym}

<Underdominance> means that the <heterozygote> has lower <genotype fitness> than both corresponding <homozygotes>. In a deterministic randomly mating biallelic population, it produces an unstable interior allele-frequency threshold separating the two fixation outcomes, unlike <heterozygote advantage>.

= Underdominant allele-frequency dynamics
{parent=Underdominance}
{title2=$\dot u=u(1-u)[(a+b)u-b]$}

With <homozygote> fitness advantages $a,b>0$ over the <heterozygote>, the <allele frequency> $u$ of the first <allele> has an unstable threshold $b/(a+b)$. Below it that <allele> decreases; above it it increases. In a discrete viability-selection generation the same numerator gives $u'-u$ after division by <mean> <genotype fitness>. Equal <homozygote> advantages give $\dot u=su(1-u)(2u-1)$, with stable endpoints and unstable midpoint.

= Genotype fitness
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$w$}

<Genotype fitness> measures the reproductive or viability success assigned to a <genotype> in a specified selection model. Relative fitnesses weight <Hardy-Weinberg proportions> after viability selection; continuous-time fitness differences instead act as selection-rate differences. The convention and time units must be specified before comparing selection with migration.

= Ancestral recombination graph
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= ARG
{synonym}
{c}

An ancestral recombination graph records ancestral coalescences and <genetic recombination> events for sampled chromosomal material. Backwards in time, each pair of ancestral lineages merges at rate one in standard diploid coalescent units, while a lineage splits at a recombination breakpoint at rate $\rho/2$ times the genetic span of the material it carries. The breakpoint splits that lineage's ancestral material into two parental contributions. Waiting times are exponential with the sum of the current event rates. At each genomic position, retaining the ancestry at that position gives a marginal <phylogenetic tree> with the neutral <Kingman's coalescent> distribution. Different positions have dependent trees because they share parts of the graph.

= Population bottleneck
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

A population bottleneck is a temporary large reduction in population size. It changes ancestral coalescence rates and can leave fewer ancestral <haplotypes> and increased <linkage disequilibrium> after the population expands.

= Genetic drift
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

Genetic drift is random variation of <allele> frequencies due to finite-population reproduction. In the neutral ancestry description, randomly sampled gene copies merge into common ancestors according to <Kingman's coalescent>.

// Target: probability-and-statistics.bigb

= Founder effect
{parent=Genetic drift}

Change in allele frequencies when a population is established by a restricted sample of ancestors. Subsequent growth does not restore variation that was not sampled.

= Effective population size
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}
{title2=$N_e$}

Effective population size is the size of an idealized population giving the relevant rate of <genetic drift> or ancestral coalescence in the population being modeled. In the standard neutral diploid coalescent, time is measured in $2N_e$ generations and the population-scaled <mutation> parameter is $\theta=4N_e\mu$.

= Segregating site
{parent=Population genetics}
{title2=$S$}

A segregating site is a position at which at least two states occur in the sampled chromosomes. Under an <infinite sites mutation model>, mutations below the sample's common ancestor correspond one-to-one to segregating sites. Their number is commonly denoted $S$.

= Watterson estimator
{parent=Segregating site}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat\theta_W=\frac{S}{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}1/i}$}

For a neutral sample of size $n\ge2$ under the <infinite sites mutation model>, the Watterson estimator is $\widehat\theta_W=S/a_{n-1}$, where $a_{n-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}1/i$. Since the coalescent has $ES=\theta a_{n-1}$, this estimator is unbiased. It matches the observed segregating-site count to its expected value, but need not maximize the exact <segregating-site likelihood under the neutral coalescent>.

= Asymptotic normality of the Watterson estimator
{parent=Watterson estimator}
{title2=$\sqrt{\log n}(\widehat\theta_W-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\theta)$}

For fixed $\theta>0$, a <geometric mutation count in a coalescent epoch> with mean $\mu_i=\theta/i$ has third <moment> $\mu_i+6\mu_i^2+6\mu_i^3$. The centered absolute third moment is at most $4\mathbb E[Y_i^3]+4\mu_i^3$, so its sum is $O(\log n)$. The total <variance> is asymptotic to $\theta\log n$, making the <Lyapunov condition> with $\delta=1$ hold. Rescaling by the <harmonic sum> gives the displayed limit. At $\theta=0$ the estimator is identically zero and the limit is degenerate. The <variance of the Watterson estimator> decreases only logarithmically with sample size at a fixed nonrecombining locus.

= Variance of the Watterson estimator
{parent=Watterson estimator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta_W)=\frac{\theta}{H_{n-1}}+\frac{\theta^2H_{n-1}^{(2)}}{H_{n-1}^2}$}

The <moments of the mutation count on a neutral coalescent tree> give this <variance> for $\widehat\theta_W=S/H_{n-1}$. Since $H_{n-1}\sim\log n$ and the sum of inverse squares is bounded, it tends to zero. Together with unbiasedness, this gives <mean-square convergence> to the mutation parameter, so it is a <consistent estimator>.

= Infinite sites mutation model
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Infinitely many sites mutation model
{synonym}

The infinite-sites model assumes that every mutation occurs at a previously unmutated site, so each mutation on the sample genealogy produces a distinct segregating site. If mutation rate per lineage is $\theta/2$ in standard coalescent time units, a tree of total length $L$ has a <Poisson distribution> of mutation count with mean $\theta L/2$.

= Segregating-site likelihood under the neutral coalescent
{parent=Infinite sites mutation model}

For $n\ge2$, the <probability generating function> of the segregating-site count conditional on mutation parameter $\theta$ is
$$
E[z^S\mid\theta]=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{i}{i+\theta(1-z)}.
$$
This follows by integrating the Poisson generating function over the independent exponential epoch lengths. With $W=L/2$, its density is $(n-1)e^{-w}(1-e^{-w})^{n-2}$. Expanding the binomial factor and integrating $w^k e^{-(\theta+r+1)w}$ gives
$$
P(S=k\mid\theta)=(n-1)\theta^k\sum_{r=0}^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^r\binom{n-2}{r}}{(\theta+r+1)^{k+1}}.
$$
For $k=0$ use $\theta^0=1$, including at $\theta=0$. A posterior density divided by a positive prior density is proportional to this likelihood, allowing posterior simulation output to estimate its maximizer.

= Inbreeding
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}

= Inbred
{synonym}

Inbreeding is reproduction between genetically related individuals. It increases the chance that an offspring's maternal and paternal copies at a locus are <identical by descent>. Such homozygosity by descent is autozygosity.

= Inbreeding depression
{parent=Inbreeding}

Reduced reproductive or survival performance associated with mating among relatives or self-fertilization in the relevant comparison. Measuring seed set alone can miss later offspring costs.

= Inbreeding coefficient
{parent=Inbreeding}
{title2=$F$}

The inbreeding coefficient is the <probability> that the two <allele> copies at a locus within an individual are <identical by descent>. It equals the <kinship coefficient> of the individual's parents. It concerns ancestral identity, rather than merely <homozygosity> of observed allelic states.

// Target: biology.bigb

= Inbreeding genotype frequencies
{parent=Inbreeding coefficient}

For a biallelic locus with frequencies $p=1-q$ and $q$, and <inbreeding coefficient> $F$, ancestral-copy identity with probability $F$ gives <genotype> probabilities $P(AA)=p^2+Fpq$, $P(Aa)=2pq(1-F)$ and $P(aa)=q^2+Fpq$. In the IBD component both copies equal a single ancestral draw; in the remaining component the two ancestral alleles are independent population draws. A fully penetrant recessive disease therefore has risk $q^2+Fq(1-q)$.

// Target: biology.bigb

= First-cousin inbreeding coefficient
{parent=Inbreeding coefficient}
{title2=$F=1/16$}

For an otherwise <outbred pedigree>, each of the two shared grandparents contributes $(1/2)^{2+2+1}=1/32$ to the <kinship coefficient> of first cousins. Thus their child has <inbreeding coefficient> $1/16$. Equivalently, cousin kinship is one quarter of the $1/4$ kinship of their sibling parents.

= Linkage disequilibrium
{parent=Population genetics}
{wiki}
{title2=$D=P(AB)-P(A)P(B)$}

= LD
{c}
{synonym}

<Linkage disequilibrium> means that knowing an <allele> at one <genetic locus> changes the <probability> of an <allele> at another <genetic locus> in population <haplotypes>. It compares their <joint probability distribution> with the product of their <marginal distributions>. This is useful because a <genetic marker> can carry information about another <genetic locus>: <tag SNPs> exploit this redundancy, without establishing a causal <genetic association>.

Consider two <genetic loci> with <alleles> $A,a$ and $B,b$. Sample one <haplotype>, rather than an unphased <diploid> <genotype>. Write its four <haplotype frequencies> as $f_{AB},f_{Ab},f_{aB},f_{ab}$, summing to one, and set the marginal <allele frequencies> to $p=f_{AB}+f_{Ab}$ and $q=f_{AB}+f_{aB}$. The <linkage disequilibrium> coefficient is
$$
D=f_{AB}-pq=f_{AB}f_{ab}-f_{Ab}f_{aB}.
$$
The second expression follows by substituting $f_{ab}=1-f_{AB}-f_{Ab}-f_{aB}$. The entire two-locus <joint probability distribution> is then
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc|c}
 &B&b&\text{total}\\\hline
A&pq+D&p(1-q)-D&p\\
a&(1-p)q-D&(1-p)(1-q)+D&1-p\\\hline
\text{total}&q&1-q&1
\end{array}
$$
Every row and column sum is unchanged when $D$ varies. <Linkage equilibrium> means $D=0$: all four <haplotype frequencies> equal products of <allele frequencies>, so the two <allele> indicators are <independent random variables>. For $p>0$, the <conditional probability> $P(B\mid A)=q+D/p$ shows directly how positive $D$ enriches $B$ among copies carrying $A$, while negative $D$ depletes it. Positive $D$ also enriches $ab$; negative $D$ enriches $Ab$ and $aB$. Swapping the labels $A$ and $a$ reverses the sign of $D$, so neither sign is intrinsically favorable.

For example, fix the <allele frequencies> at $p=0.60,q=0.40$. At <linkage equilibrium>, the <haplotype frequencies> in the order $AB,Ab,aB,ab$ are $(0.24,0.36,0.16,0.24)$. Positive <linkage disequilibrium> $D=0.12$ instead gives $(0.36,0.24,0.04,0.36)$, with $P(B\mid A)=0.60$; negative <linkage disequilibrium> $D=-0.12$ gives $(0.12,0.48,0.28,0.12)$, with $P(B\mid A)=0.20$. Counting each <allele> separately would miss the difference.

\Image[haplotype-joint-frequencies-fixed-marginals.png]
{title=Three <haplotype> <joint probability distributions> with identical marginal <allele frequencies>, showing negative <linkage disequilibrium>, <linkage equilibrium> and positive <linkage disequilibrium>}

Nonnegative <haplotype frequencies> constrain $D$. Define the nonnegative bounds $L=\min(pq,(1-p)(1-q))$ and $U=\min(p(1-q),(1-p)q)$. The four entries above give
$$
-L\le D\le U,\qquad \max(0,p+q-1)\le f_{AB}\le\min(p,q).
$$
Thus $|D|\le1/4$ globally, but a particular pair of <allele frequencies> usually permits a smaller, asymmetric interval. The <standardized linkage disequilibrium> convention used here retains the sign:
$$
D'=\begin{cases}D/U&D>0,\\0&D=0,\\D/L&D<0,\end{cases}
\qquad -1\le D'\le1.
$$
This convention requires $0<p,q<1$; if either <genetic locus> has only one <allele> in the population, $D=0$ but normalized measures are undefined. Some presentations report only $|D'|$. At $D'=1$ or $-1$, at least one of the four <haplotypes> has zero frequency; this alone does not prove that <genetic recombination> has never occurred.

For the <indicator random variables> $X=\mathbf{1}_{A}$ and $Y=\mathbf{1}_{B}$, their <covariance> is $D$, and their <variances> are $p(1-p)$ and $q(1-q)$. The <allelic correlation> measure is their squared <correlation coefficient>,
$$
r_{\mathrm{LD}}^2=\frac{D^2}{p(1-p)q(1-q)},\qquad
0\le r_{\mathrm{LD}}^2\le\frac{\max(L^2,U^2)}{p(1-p)q(1-q)}\le1.
$$
The subscript distinguishes it from the <recombination fraction> $r$ in <linkage-disequilibrium decay under recombination>. Squaring loses the association's sign. In the illustrated positive and negative examples, $r_{\mathrm{LD}}^2=0.25$ in both, whereas $D'=0.75$ and $-0.50$, respectively. For another original example, take $p=0.10,q=0.50,f_{AB}=0.10$: then $D=0.05,D'=1$, yet $r_{\mathrm{LD}}^2=1/9$. Every $A$ carries $B$, but most copies carrying $B$ lack $A$. The frequency constraints and the distinction between these normalizations are analyzed in <Mathematical properties of the r² measure of linkage disequilibrium by Jenna M. VanLiere and others> (sections 2.1 and 2.5, pp. 131–132 and 135–136).

<Genetic linkage> describes transmission through <meiosis>; <linkage disequilibrium> describes a population's <haplotype frequencies>. Nearby <genetic loci> on a <chromosome> can be in <linkage equilibrium>. Conversely, mixing populations can create <linkage disequilibrium> even between <genetic loci> on different <chromosomes>. For example, mix equal numbers of independently sampled <haplotypes> from two populations, each internally at <linkage equilibrium>, with $p=q=0.80$ in one and $p=q=0.20$ in the other. The pooled <allele frequencies> are $p=q=0.50$, but $f_{AB}=(0.64+0.04)/2=0.34$, giving $D=0.09$. This is a consequence of <population stratification>, not a measurement of the <recombination fraction>.

Measured <haplotype frequencies> also depend on the sample. Finite <sample size>, related individuals, <genetic-study ascertainment>, <genotyping error> and uncertain <haplotype estimation> can change an estimate. A missing rare <haplotype> can give empirical $|D'|=1$ without demonstrating its absence from the population. Report the sample definition and <allele frequencies> alongside the association measure; a nonzero sample estimate alone does not establish nonzero population <linkage disequilibrium>.

A concrete dataset is the 2005 <A haplotype map of the human genome by The International HapMap Consortium>. The International HapMap Consortium used <genotyping> platforms including Illumina BeadArray to obtain <SNP> <genotype> calls from 269 <DNA> samples across four collections: 30 parent–offspring trios from Ibadan, 30 trios from Utah, 45 unrelated individuals from Beijing and 44 from Tokyo. Parent–offspring trios and the PHASE algorithm supplied <haplotype estimation>, rather than direct observation of every <chromosome>. In Figure 7, 36 <SNPs> resolve seven <haplotypes> in a <chromosome> 2 region in the Utah panel; seven <tag SNPs> capture the 36 <SNPs> at $r_{\mathrm{LD}}^2\ge0.8$. The readout demonstrates reusable <allelic correlation>, not causal effects.

Controls required over 80% complete calls, at most one discordance among five duplicate <genotypes>, at most one <Mendelian segregation> error per trio panel, and a <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium> test with $P>0.001$; blind reassessment checked performance. Minor <allele frequencies> of at least 0.05 were targeted, the collections were not population-representative, and inferred <haplotypes> retain phase uncertainty. These limits restrict extrapolation of <linkage disequilibrium>.<A haplotype map of the human genome by The International HapMap Consortium>[pp. 1300–1302, Table 1; Figure 7, p. 1306; Methods, p. 1316]{ref}

= Recombination change in two-locus haplotype frequencies
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$\Delta x_{AB}=-rD$}

Let the four haplotype frequencies sum to one and define $D=x_{AB}x_{ab}-x_{Ab}x_{aB}=x_{AB}-p_Ap_B$. Recombination at rate $r$ removes the excess association: $\Delta x_{AB}=\Delta x_{ab}=-rD$ and $\Delta x_{Ab}=\Delta x_{aB}=rD$. Marginal allele frequencies are unchanged. Hence $D<0$ generates more $AB$, whereas $D>0$ removes it; a claim that recombination always increases this combination is false.

= Linkage equilibrium
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$D=0$}

For two <genetic loci> with two <alleles> each, <linkage equilibrium> is <independence> of their states on sampled <haplotypes>. It concerns association between <genetic loci>; <Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium> concerns pairing of <alleles> into <diploid> <genotypes>.

= Standardized linkage disequilibrium
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$D'=D/D_{\max}$}

For positive <linkage disequilibrium> $D$, take $D_{\max}=\min(p_A(1-p_B),(1-p_A)p_B)$; for negative $D$, take $D_{\max}=\min(p_Ap_B,(1-p_A)(1-p_B))$ and retain its negative sign in $D'$. Thus $|D'|\le1$. Its magnitude measures the fraction of the largest disequilibrium possible at the specified allele frequencies. High $|D'|$ need not imply high <allelic correlation>, so it does not alone guarantee good marker prediction.

= Linkage-disequilibrium decay under recombination
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$D_{t+1}=(1-r)D_t$}

<Genetic recombination> erodes <linkage disequilibrium> by reshuffling <alleles> between <haplotypes>, while preserving marginal <allele frequencies> in the idealized model. Let $f_{AB,t}$ denote the <haplotype frequency> in the <gamete> pool of generation $t$, let $p,q$ denote its marginal <allele frequencies>, and let $r\in[0,1/2]$ be the constant <recombination fraction> between two autosomal <genetic loci>. Assume <random mating> within one population, nonoverlapping generations, fair <Mendelian segregation>, equal reciprocal <gamete> production, the same $r$ in both sexes, and a deterministic population large enough to neglect <genetic drift>. Exclude <natural selection>, <mutation> and <gene flow>. Then
$$
f_{AB,t+1}=(1-r)f_{AB,t}+rpq=f_{AB,t}-rD_t,
\qquad D_{t+1}=(1-r)D_t,
\qquad D_t=(1-r)^tD_0.
$$
Here $p$ and $q$ remain constant: recombining changes which <alleles> occur together, not their total numbers. The other <haplotype frequencies> change by $+rD_t$ for $Ab,aB$ and $-rD_t$ for $ab$. Consequently the <joint probability distribution> stays within its allowed bounds and approaches the product of its <marginal distributions> when $r>0$.

For the original positive example $p=0.60,q=0.40,D_0=0.12$, a <recombination fraction> $r=0.10$ gives $D_1=0.108$ and $D_{10}\approx0.04184$. The negative example $D_0=-0.12$ has exactly the opposite sequence: <genetic recombination> reduces the magnitude without reversing the sign. With $r=0$, the initial <linkage disequilibrium> persists in this model; even <independent assortment> at $r=1/2$ only halves $D$ each generation. A population association does not vanish after one generation merely because the <genetic loci> are unlinked.

\Image[linkage-disequilibrium-ideal-recombination-decay.png]
{title=Idealized <linkage-disequilibrium decay under recombination> at four <recombination fractions>, with faster decay of squared <allelic correlation> when marginal <allele frequencies> remain fixed}

The <geometric sequence> gives a generation scale: for $r>0$, the continuous interpolation reaches $|D_0|/2$ at $t_{1/2}=\log(1/2)/\log(1-r)$. Actual generations are integers; the first at or below half is $\lceil t_{1/2}\rceil$. For small $r$, $t_{1/2}\approx(\log 2)/r$. Because the denominator of <allelic correlation> stays fixed, $r_{\mathrm{LD},t}^2=(1-r)^{2t}r_{\mathrm{LD},0}^2$; its decay is faster than the magnitude of $D_t$.

A short derivation uses <random mating> to assign frequency $2f_{AB}f_{ab}$ to the double <heterozygote> $AB/ab$ and $2f_{Ab}f_{aB}$ to $Ab/aB$. Relative to $r=0$, each loses or gains an $AB$ <gamete> fraction $r/2$, respectively. Their net contribution is $r(f_{Ab}f_{aB}-f_{AB}f_{ab})=-rD$. The other <genotypes> produce no net change in $f_{AB}$, proving the recurrence and, by iteration, its solution.

This temporal model does not identify elapsed generations from physical separation alone. The <recombination fraction> depends on <genetic recombination>, including <recombination hotspots>, while <population stratification>, <natural selection>, <gene flow> or <genetic drift> can create or maintain <linkage disequilibrium>. Repeated sampling across time would also require accounting for <sample size> and <haplotype estimation>. A cross-sectional plot of association against distance between <genetic loci> is therefore a different observation from the idealized trajectory plotted here.

= Allelic correlation
{parent=Linkage disequilibrium}
{title2=$r^2=D^2/[p_A(1-p_A)p_B(1-p_B)]$}

For two segregating biallelic loci, encode the allele on a random population <haplotype> by indicator variables $X,Y$. Their <covariance> is the <linkage disequilibrium> coefficient $D$. The squared <correlation> is $r^2=D^2/[p_A(1-p_A)p_B(1-p_B)]$, between zero and one. It measures how well one marker predicts the other and is undefined if either marker is monomorphic.
