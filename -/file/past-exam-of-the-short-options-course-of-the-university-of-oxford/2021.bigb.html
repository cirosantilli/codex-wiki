\boxed{G\cong C_q\rtimes C_p.}

In particular $G$ has both a cyclic normal subgroup and a cyclic quotient, making it a <metacyclic group> and hence a <solvable group>.

A <projective transformation> of $\mathbb P(\mathbb F^{n+1})$ is induced by an invertible <linear map> $A:\mathbb F^{n+1}\to\mathbb F^{n+1}$:
$$
\boxed{\tau([v])=[Av],\qquad A\in\operatorname{GL}_{n+1}(\mathbb F).}

The transverse observed speed is $c/\gamma=c\sin\phi$, and the total photon speed is still $c$. If desired the observed photon energy is $E'=E/\sin\phi$ for $0<\phi\le\pi/2$.

The per-capita net growth rate is $r/(1+bN^2)-\delta$. Thus **$r$ is the low-density per-capita birth or recruitment rate**, **$\delta$ is the constant per-capita death or removal rate**, and **$b$ controls density-dependent suppression of recruitment**. Both $r$ and $\delta$ have units of inverse time; $b$ has units of inverse population squared. The population scale $b^{-1/2}$ halves the recruitment rate from its low-density value. Larger $b$ makes suppression important at smaller population sizes. These are interpretations of this phenomenological <population dynamics> model, rather than independently specified biological mechanisms.

Write $f(N)=N[r/(1+bN^2)-\delta]$. A <steady state> is a nonnegative zero of $f$. If $r<\delta$, the bracket is strictly negative for every $N\ge0$, so only $N=0$ exists. If $r>\delta$, there are exactly two nonnegative <steady states>,
$$
\boxed{N=0,\qquad N^*=\sqrt{\frac{r-\delta}{b\delta}}.}
$$
For an autonomous scalar <ordinary differential equation>, a <steady state> with $f'<0$ is locally asymptotically stable, while one with $f'>0$ is unstable. Here
$$
f'(N)=\frac{r(1-bN^2)}{(1+bN^2)^2}-\delta,\qquad f'(0)=r-\delta.
$$
At the positive <steady state>, use $1+b(N^*)^2=r/\delta$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{f'(N^*)=-\frac{2\delta(r-\delta)}r<0.}
$$
Therefore zero is **stable for $r<\delta$ and unstable for $r>\delta$**, whereas the positive <steady state> is **stable whenever it exists**. At the omitted equality $r=\delta$, $f(N)=-\delta bN^3/(1+bN^2)$; zero is nonhyperbolic but still attracts every nonnegative initial state.

Linearizing at the positive <steady state> gives $n'=f'(N^*)n$, so a small displacement decays as $n(t)=n(0)e^{-t/\tau}$. The <recovery time of a stable equilibrium> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\tau=-\frac1{f'(N^*)}=\frac r{2\delta(r-\delta)}.}
$$
As $\delta\uparrow r$ with $r,b$ fixed, $N^*\to0$ and $\tau\to\infty$. Thus the equilibrium population approaches extinction and recovers increasingly slowly after small perturbations. This is <critical slowing down>. Exactly at the threshold the leading equation is $N'\sim-rbN^3$, which gives algebraic decay $N(t)\sim(2rb t)^{-1/2}$ instead of exponential recovery.

For $r>\delta$, the sign of $f(N)$ is positive on $0<N<N^*$ and negative on $N>N^*$. Its graph passes through $0,N^*$, has positive initial slope $r-\delta$, and eventually behaves like $-\delta N$. The <phase line> therefore points toward $N^*$ from both sides.

The vector field is continuously differentiable, so solutions are unique. No positive solution crosses either equilibrium; one starting below $N^*$ increases and stays below it, and one starting above it decreases and stays above it. These bounded solutions exist for all forward time and have a limit. By continuity of $f$, any such limit must be a zero of $f$: otherwise the derivative would stay bounded away from zero near the limit. The increasing solution is bounded below by its strictly positive initial value, excluding zero; the decreasing one stays above $N^*$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{N(0)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N(t)\longrightarrow N^*.}
$$
The solution starting exactly at $N^*$ is constant. The zero initial condition remains at zero, which is why positivity is stipulated.

At a constant <steady state> $N_t=N$, the discrete equation reduces to
$$
N\left[\frac r{1+bN^2}-(\delta+1)\right]=0.
$$
Write $s=\delta+1>1$. For $r<s$, no nonzero real root is possible, so **zero is the only real steady state**. For $r>s$ the real roots are
$$
\boxed{N=0,\qquad N=\pm\sqrt{\frac{r-s}{bs}}.}
$$
Thus there is **exactly one strictly positive steady state**, $N^*=\sqrt{(r-s)/(bs)}$. There are three real steady states in total, two of them nonnegative. At $r=s$ the only one is zero. This distinction keeps the positive-population statement separate from the count over all real numbers.

Let $f(X,Y)=X[r/(1+bY^2)-\delta]$. At the positive <steady state>, $1+b(N^*)^2=r/s$, where $s=\delta+1$. The two <partial derivatives> are
$$
f_X(N^*,N^*)=1,\qquad f_Y(N^*,N^*)=-\frac{2rb(N^*)^2}{(1+b(N^*)^2)^2}=-\frac{2s(r-s)}r.
$$
The first-order <Taylor expansion> with $N_t=N^*+n_t$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{n_{t+1}-n_t+a n_{t-1}=0,\qquad a=\frac{2s(r-s)}r=\frac{2(r-(\delta+1))(\delta+1)}r.}
$$
Equivalently, the <linearization> on $(n_t,n_{t-1})$ has matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&-a\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and <characteristic polynomial> $\mu^2-\mu+a$.

For $r>s$, $a>0$. When $0<a\le1/4$, the roots of $\mu^2-\mu+a$ are real and lie strictly between zero and one. When $a>1/4$, they are a complex-conjugate pair of modulus $\sqrt a$. Thus the positive <steady state> is linearly asymptotically stable exactly when $0<a<1$, and unstable when $a>1$. Since $a=2s-2s^2/r$ increases strictly with $r$, the crossing occurs at
$$
\boxed{a=1,\qquad r_c=\frac{2s^2}{2s-1}=\frac{2(\delta+1)^2}{2\delta+1}.}
$$
At the crossing,
$$
\mu_\pm=\frac{1\pm i\sqrt3}{2}=e^{\pm i\pi/3},\qquad \boxed{n_t=A\cos(\pi t/3)+B\sin(\pi t/3).}
$$
Every nonzero real linearized perturbation has period six. This establishes the intended **linear stability threshold and linearized period six**.

**The nonlinear period-six claim in the printed paper does not follow, and is false as a local bifurcating-branch claim for this map.** One can check this directly rather than infer a nonlinear period from the <eigenvalues>. At $r=r_c$ normalize $N_t=N^*(1+u)$, $N_{t-1}=N^*(1+v)$. The exact planar map is
$$
F(u,v)=\left((1+u)\left[\frac{2s^2}{2s-1+(1+v)^2}-(s-1)\right]-1,\ u\right).
$$
Its cubic <Taylor expansion> is
$$
F(u,v)=\left(u-v-uv+\frac{2-s}{2s}v^2+\frac{2-s}{2s}uv^2+\frac{s-1}{s^2}v^3,\ u\right)+O(\|(u,v)\|^4).
$$
Here is a concrete check of the sixfold return. Put $z=(u,v)^T$, $Q=u^2-uv+v^2$, and
$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\2&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad K^2=-3I,\qquad \alpha=-\frac{3s^2-3s+1}{s^2},\qquad\beta=-\frac{5s^2-6s+2}{2s^2}.
$$
Composing the displayed cubic expansion six times cancels all quadratic terms and gives
$$
F^6(z)-z=Q(\alpha I+\beta K)z+O(\|z\|^4).
$$
In particular
$$
\boxed{Q(F^6(z))-Q(z)=-\frac{2(3s^2-3s+1)}{s^2}Q(z)^2+O(\|z\|^5).}
$$
The quartic coefficient is strictly negative. Hence, sufficiently near the equilibrium, every nonzero point has smaller $Q$ after six iterations: the critical nonlinear map has no nearby nontrivial period-six orbit, and the equilibrium itself is locally attracting despite its neutral <linearization>.

One can also rule out a branch of small period-six orbits on either side of the crossing. Normalize about the positive equilibrium at each nearby $r$, and put $\eta=a-1$. The derivative of the sixth power of $\begin{pmatrix}1&-a\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ at $a=1$ is $3I+K$. Smooth dependence on $r$ therefore gives
$$
F_\eta^6(z)-z=\big[(3\eta+\alpha Q)I+(\eta+\beta Q)K\big]z+o\big((|\eta|+\|z\|^2)\|z\|\big).
$$
The leading matrix has <determinant> $(3\eta+\alpha Q)^2+3(\eta+\beta Q)^2$. Both terms could vanish with $Q>0$ only if $\alpha=3\beta$, but
$$
\alpha-3\beta=\frac{(3s-2)^2}{2s^2}>0.
$$
Thus its norm on nonzero $z$ is bounded below by a positive constant times $(|\eta|+\|z\|^2)\|z\|$, and the smaller remainder cannot cancel it. There is no nearby nonzero solution of $F_\eta^6(z)=z$. The <eigenvalues> satisfy the nonresonance conditions through order four, their moduli cross outward with increasing $r$, and the cubic radial coefficient is negative. The standard <Neimark–Sacker bifurcation> theorem therefore gives a supercritical invariant closed curve. Its rotation is near the linear resonance $1/6$; it is not a local branch of exact period-six orbits.

Finally, the discrete population map does not preserve nonnegativity for arbitrary large lagged populations: $r/(1+bN_{t-1}^2)-\delta$ can be negative. The positive-equilibrium stability conclusions above are local and do not assume a global positivity property that the formula lacks.
