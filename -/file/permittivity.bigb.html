= Permittivity
{title2=$\epsilon$}
{wiki}

<Permittivity> describes how an <electric field> and a material's <electric polarization> combine into the <electric displacement field>. In a static, local response with <linearity> and <isotropy>, and no <electric polarization> at zero <electric field>, the <constitutive equation> is
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E,\qquad \epsilon=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r.
$$
Here $\epsilon_0$ is <vacuum permittivity>, while $\epsilon_r$ is the <relative permittivity>, a <dimensionless quantity>. The <SI> unit of <permittivity> is <farad> per metre, $\mathrm{F/m}$: multiplying it by an <electric field> in $\mathrm{V/m}$ gives an <electric displacement field> in $\mathrm{C/m^2}$. A larger positive <permittivity> lets the same <parallel-plate capacitor> hold more plate <electric charge> at the same <electric potential difference>.

The material contribution is <electric polarization> $\mathbf P$, the <electric dipole moment> per unit <volume>. The <electric displacement field> and <electric susceptibility> $\chi_e$ satisfy
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P,\qquad
\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E,\qquad
\epsilon_r=1+\chi_e.
$$
Substitution gives the first <constitutive equation>. The <electric field> here is the averaged, total field inside the material, not just the field of the external plate <electric charges>. The <continuum approximation> averages over many <chemical atoms> or <molecules>; locality means that the <electric polarization> at a position depends on the <electric field> there. Static equilibrium, fixed material conditions and sufficiently small <electric fields> are needed for this scalar proportionality. <Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Polarization>{ref}.

For an ideal <parallel-plate capacitor> completely filled by a uniform <dielectric>, hold the free plate <electric charge> magnitude $Q$ fixed and neglect the <fringing field of a capacitor>. Writing $A$ for the plate <area>, <Gauss's law> gives $D=Q/A$. Thus
$$
E=\frac{Q}{\epsilon_0\epsilon_r A},\qquad
P=\frac{Q}{A}\left(1-\frac1{\epsilon_r}\right).
$$
The <electric dipoles> point along the <electric field>. Their surface <electric charges>, described by <polarization density>, oppose the field from the free plate <electric charges>. For a hypothetical <dielectric> with $\epsilon_r=4$, the internal <electric field> is one quarter of its <vacuum> value, while $P=3D/4$. At fixed <electric potential difference> instead, the internal <electric field> stays approximately $V/d$ and the plate <electric charge> grows fourfold. These are different experimental constraints. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_10.html#Ch10-S3[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, II.10–3, equations (10.5)–(10.11): polarization surface charge, field reduction and capacitance]{ref}.

<Permittivity> is a useful material summary, not a universal constant of the material under every condition. <Temperature>, <frequency> and <electric field> strength can change the response. In a <linear anisotropic dielectric>, <permittivity> is a <tensor>, $D_i=\sum_j\epsilon_{ij}E_j$, so the <electric displacement field> need not be parallel to the <electric field>. At nonzero <frequency>, delayed <electric polarization> requires <complex permittivity> and may produce <dielectric loss>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_32.html#Ch32-S2[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, II.32–2, equations (32.5)–(32.8) and following paragraph: frequency dependence and phase lag]{ref}.

= Permittivity tensor
{parent=Permittivity}
{title2=$D_i=\sum_j\epsilon_{ij}E_j$}

The <permittivity tensor> is the linear material-response matrix relating <electric displacement field> to <electric field> at a specified <frequency>. An isotropic local medium has $\epsilon_{ij}=\epsilon\delta_{ij}$. A magnetically biased transparent medium can have an imaginary antisymmetric transverse contribution; its eigenmodes can have opposite <circular polarizations> and different <refractive indices>.

= Dielectric
{parent=Permittivity}
{wiki}

A <dielectric> is a material in which an <electric field> produces <electric polarization> while transport of <electric charge> is sufficiently small for the intended use. An insulating solid in a <capacitor> is an example; real <dielectrics> can still have <dielectric loss> and nonzero <electrical conductivity>.

The important distinction is between rearranging bound <electric charge> and transporting mobile <charge carriers> through the specimen. Bound <electric charges> can move a little within a <chemical atom> or <molecule>, or an existing <electric dipole> can turn. Their averaged <electric dipole moments> produce <electric polarization> even if there is negligible steady <electric current> between the <electrodes>. A changing <electric polarization> does give a <bound current>; the absence of steady conduction does not mean that the material is electrically unresponsive.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 10: Dielectrics>[II.10–2 and II.10–3: induced atomic dipoles and relaxation of bound surface charge]{ref}

For uniform <electric polarization> $\mathbf P=P\widehat{\mathbf x}$ in a rectangular slab, <polarization density> gives zero bound volume <charge density> in the interior and opposite bound <surface charge densities> $-P,+P$ on the left and right faces. The <electric charge> has been redistributed, not created. More generally, the <divergence theorem> makes the cancellation explicit:
$$
\int_{\mathcal V}(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P)\,d\mathcal V
+\int_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=0.
$$
Here $\mathbf n$ is the outward <normal vector> on the material boundary. This accounts for both bound volume <charge density> and bound <surface charge density>; omitting either can falsely suggest that an initially neutral <dielectric> acquired net <electric charge>.

An <electrical insulator> between <capacitor> plates supports a useful combination: appreciable <electric polarization>, little leakage <electric current>, and tolerance of the intended <electric field>. This lets a small <parallel-plate capacitor> store separated <electric charge> and recoverable <capacitor energy>. The comparison in <dielectric capacitor response> explains why specifying fixed <electric charge> or fixed <electric potential difference> changes the answer. High <relative permittivity> alone does not establish good insulation or a large usable <energy density>; <dielectric loss> and <dielectric breakdown> matter as well.

= Steady fields in two leaky dielectric layers
{parent=Dielectric}
{title2=$\sigma_1E_1=\sigma_2E_2$}

For two planar homogeneous layers in series, <charge conservation> makes the steady normal <current density> the same in both layers. Combining <Ohm's law> with $E_1d_1+E_2d_2=V$ determines the fields when both conductivities are positive. The free interfacial <surface charge density> is $\epsilon_2E_2-\epsilon_1E_1$, whereas total free-plus-bound charge is $\epsilon_0(E_2-E_1)$. The two notions of charge are distinct. This DC state can differ from the immediate response to switching the voltage; interfacial free charge accumulates during the transient.

= Transverse electrostatic field inside a dielectric cylinder
{parent=Dielectric}
{title2=$E_{\rm in}=2E_0/(1+\epsilon_r)$}

For an infinitely long circular <dielectric> cylinder in a uniform transverse field in vacuum, the inside <electrostatic potential> is linear in the transverse coordinate. Matching the tangential <electric field> and normal <electric displacement field> to the outside dipolar solution gives the displayed uniform internal field. The result assumes no free interface charge and neglects end effects; an exterior <dielectric> changes the <permittivity> ratio.

A dielectric retaining a persistent <electric polarization> or trapped charge.

= Electric susceptibility
{parent=Dielectric}
{title2=$\chi_e$}
{wiki}

The <dimensionless quantity> relating induced <electric polarization> to the internal <electric field> through $\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E$ in the static scalar model. It describes the material contribution; <relative permittivity> includes the <vacuum> term as well, $\epsilon_r=1+\chi_e$. Zero <electric susceptibility> gives $\epsilon_r=1$, not zero <permittivity>.

The scalar $\chi_e$ assumes a local response with <linearity> and <isotropy>, settled <electric polarization>, fixed material conditions, and no residual <electric polarization> at zero <electric field>. It relates $\mathbf P$ to the averaged total internal <electric field>, which already includes the field of the bound <electric charges>. It does not relate $\mathbf P$ to the field the external <electrodes> would produce in <vacuum>. The distinction is essential for a self-consistent calculation.<Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Polarization>[equations (807)–(812): total field, displacement, linear susceptibility and relative dielectric constant]{ref}

Substitute the scalar relation into the definition of <electric displacement field>:
$$
\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P
=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\mathbf E.
$$
Comparison with $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ proves $\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)$ and $\epsilon_r=1+\chi_e$. Both $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf P$ have units $\mathrm{C/m^2}$, so $\chi_e$ is a <dimensionless quantity>. In a stipulated static model with $\epsilon_r=3$, $\chi_e=2$: at a prescribed internal <electric field>, the material contribution $P$ is twice $\epsilon_0E$. This is a hypothetical response, not a measured material constant. The <electric susceptibility> is a convenient bridge from <microscopic dielectric polarization> to a macroscopic <capacitance> measurement, but it is not a measure of <electrical conductivity>.

= Lorentz oscillator model
{c}
{parent=Electric susceptibility}

A bound charge obeying $m\ddot u+m\gamma\dot u+m\omega_0^2u=qE$ gives a resonant <electric susceptibility>. For <carrier density> $n$ and the $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention,
$$
\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega},\qquad
\omega_p^2=\frac{nq^2}{m\epsilon_0}.
$$
Positive damping gives $\operatorname{Im}\epsilon_r\geq0$ for $\omega>0$, expressing passive absorption. Independent resonances add to the <electric susceptibility>. Setting $\omega_0=0$ gives the <Drude frequency response>.

= Optical oscillator strength
{parent=Lorentz oscillator model}
{title2=$f_j$}

An <optical oscillator strength> is a dimensionless weight assigned to an optical resonance. In an independent-resonance <Lorentz oscillator model> using a reference <electron mass> $m_e$ and a specified participating <number density> $n$,
$$
\epsilon_r(\omega)=1+\frac{ne^2}{\epsilon_0m_e}
\sum_j\frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_j\omega}.
$$
Its normalization must be stated: assigning one unit of total strength per participating <electron> means $\sum_jf_j=1$. A one-resonance approximation with $f_1=1$ represents the whole chosen electron population, rather than asserting that the first allowed <interband transition> carries all optical strength. The smallest <band gap> therefore need not equal the fitted resonance energy.

= Phase of a negatively charged driven oscillator
{parent=Lorentz oscillator model}
{title2=$\phi=\pi-\operatorname{atan2}(2\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$}

For $\ddot z+2\gamma\dot z+\omega_0^2z=(qE_0/m)\cos\omega t$, the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention gives complex displacement amplitude $\widetilde z=(qE_0/m)/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+2i\gamma\omega)$. If the real amplitude is defined nonnegative, the phase is $-\delta$ for $q>0$ but $\pi-\delta$ for $q<0$, where $\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(2\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$. An electron's low-frequency displacement is opposite to the electric field; its high-frequency displacement is in phase. The induced <electric dipole moment> $qz$ instead has phase $-\delta$ for either sign of charge. A signed amplitude can absorb the extra $\pi$, but that convention must be stated.

= Scattering cross-section of a harmonically bound charge
{parent=Lorentz oscillator model}
{title2=$\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_T\omega^4/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2$}

For a charge $q$ of <mass> $m$ bound by spring constant $k=m\omega_0^2$, the undamped equation $m\ddot x+kx=qE_0e^{-i\omega t}$ has <complex amplitude> $x_0=qE_0/[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$. The induced <electric dipole moment> is $p_0=qx_0$. Averaged <electric dipole radiation> <power>, divided by incident <light intensity>, gives
$$
\sigma(\omega)=\frac{q^4\omega^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2c^4(k-m\omega^2)^2}
=\sigma_T\frac{\omega^4}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2}.
$$
The <Rayleigh scattering> limit $\omega\ll\omega_0$ gives $\sigma\sim\sigma_T(\omega/\omega_0)^4$, proportional to $\lambda^{-4}$. For $\omega_0\ll\omega\ll mc^2/\hbar$, the charge behaves as free and the <Thomson cross-section> is recovered. At resonance the undamped steady-state formula is invalid; a finite <damping> mechanism or <radiation reaction> must be included. The formula describes independent weakly driven scatterers, not arbitrary coherent scattering from an extended medium.

= Microscopic dielectric polarization
{parent=Dielectric}

Two routes to <electric polarization> are displacement and reorientation. An <electric field> can shift the centres of positive and negative <electric charge> in a neutral <chemical atom> or <molecule>, inducing an <electric dipole moment>. A <polar molecule> already has an <electric dipole moment>; a field biases its orientation. At finite <temperature>, random molecular motion opposes complete alignment, so a nonzero average need not mean that every <electric dipole> points the same way.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/8.5 Molecular Model of a Dielectric>[§8.5, Figures 8.19–8.20 and the thermal-fluctuation qualification following Figure 8.20]{ref}

For a single species with number per unit <volume> $n$, the <electric polarization> is
$$
\mathbf P=n\langle\mathbf p\rangle.
$$
The brackets denote the local average <electric dipole moment>, rather than its magnitude. Opposite orientations can cancel while individual <electric dipole moments> remain nonzero.<Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Polarization>[equation (803): number density times locally averaged molecular dipole moment]{ref} This averaging explains how an initially unpolarized material can respond strongly without acquiring net <electric charge>.

= Dielectric capacitor response
{parent=Dielectric}
{title2=$C=\epsilon_r C_0$}

Compare two initially identical <parallel-plate capacitors> with plate <area> $A$, fixed gap $d$, <vacuum> <capacitance> $C_0=\epsilon_0A/d$, and initial <electric potential difference> $V_0$. Completely fill both gaps with the same uniform linear <dielectric> of static <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r>1$, neglecting the <fringing field of a capacitor>. Keep one connected to an ideal voltage source and isolate the other. The geometric <capacitance> becomes $C=\epsilon_r C_0$ in both cases; the electrical constraint determines what else changes.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/8.4 Capacitor with a Dielectric>[§8.4, Figure 8.17 and equation (8.11): isolated capacitor, unchanged free plate charge and increased capacitance]{ref}

Write $Q_0=C_0V_0$, $E_0=V_0/d$ and $U_0=C_0V_0^2/2$. Applying $Q=CV$, $E=V/d$ and the <capacitor energy> formula gives the complete comparison:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
&\text{fixed voltage}&\text{isolated, fixed charge}\\ \hline
C/C_0&\epsilon_r&\epsilon_r\\
Q/Q_0&\epsilon_r&1\\
V/V_0&1&1/\epsilon_r\\
E/E_0&1&1/\epsilon_r\\
U/U_0&\epsilon_r&1/\epsilon_r
\end{array}
$$
Thus the statement that a <dielectric> reduces the <electric field> needs the fixed-free-charge condition. With fixed <electric potential difference>, the voltage source supplies additional free plate <electric charge>, maintaining $E=V_0/d$ despite the new bound <electric charges>.

\Image[dielectric-capacitor-voltage-and-charge-constraints.png]
{title=<Dielectric capacitor response> at fixed voltage and fixed charge}
{description=Original normalized comparison for the stipulated model $\epsilon_r=3$: every ratio is relative to the same initially charged <vacuum> <capacitor>. The dashed line marks the initial value. The bars show final values, not a time history or measured data. Plate geometry is unchanged and the <dielectric> completely fills the gap.}

For the stipulated $\epsilon_r=3$ example, the voltage source supplies $\Delta Q=2Q_0$. At constant $V_0$, its delivered electrical <work> is $V_0\Delta Q=4U_0$, whereas the increase in <capacitor energy> is $2U_0$. In an ideal reversible quasistatic insertion, the remaining $2U_0$ is mechanical <work> delivered by the assembly, as accounted for in <fixed-voltage capacitor energy balance>. For the isolated assembly, <capacitor energy> falls from $U_0$ to $U_0/3$, allowing $2U_0/3$ of mechanical <work> in the same ideal limit. Actual losses reduce recoverable <work>; energy increases in the connected case do not arise from the <dielectric> supplying free <energy>.

= Electrical insulator
{parent=Dielectric}
{wiki=Insulator_(electricity)}

An <electrical insulator> has sufficiently small transport of <electric charge> for its operating conditions. Insulation is a practical requirement involving <electric potential difference>, <temperature>, geometry and duration, rather than a universal claim of zero <electrical conductivity>. <Electric polarization> can be substantial in an <electrical insulator>; <dielectric breakdown> can end the insulating regime.

For an idealized uniform slab with small constant <electrical conductivity> $\sigma$, the <Ohmic conductor> model predicts leakage <electrical resistance> $R=d/(\sigma A)$ in parallel with <capacitance> $C=\epsilon A/d$. An isolated <capacitor> then has
$$
\frac{dQ}{dt}=-\frac{Q}{RC},\qquad
Q(t)=Q(0)e^{-t/(RC)},\qquad RC=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}.
$$
This is a derived model of slow discharge, not an assertion that every leakage mechanism obeys <Ohm's law>. Its time scale matches <charge relaxation>. The fixed-charge comparison in <dielectric capacitor response> needs an observation time short compared with $RC$, but long enough for <electric polarization> to settle. During a maintained voltage, leakage consumes <power> $V^2/R$ as <Joule heating>; delayed <electric polarization> can add <dielectric loss> under changing <electric fields>.<Materials Measurement: Dielectric Materials>[Overview: real and imaginary permittivity, storage and dissipated loss]{ref}

Large <relative permittivity> alone does not rule out either mechanism.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/8.5 Molecular Model of a Dielectric>[§8.5, paragraph following Table 8.1: high dielectric constant does not ensure a good insulator; mobile ions permit conduction]{ref}

= Charge-transfer insulator
{parent=Electrical insulator}

A correlated transition-metal compound whose lowest charge excitation primarily transfers an electron between a ligand orbital and a metal orbital. Its gap is controlled by charge-transfer and Coulomb energies rather than a weakly interacting single-band picture alone. Many cuprate parent compounds are usefully described this way. This distinguishes the orbital character of the low-energy excitation from an idealized single-band <Mott insulator>.

= Dielectric breakdown
{parent=Dielectric}
{wiki}

<Dielectric breakdown> is failure of an insulating regime under a sufficiently strong <electric field>, allowing substantial <electric current> through the material. For example, an <electron> freed from a <chemical atom> or <molecule> can become a mobile <charge carrier>. The small-field linear <electric susceptibility> model does not describe this conducting state.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/8.5 Molecular Model of a Dielectric>[§8.5, dielectric breakdown paragraph immediately before the definition of dielectric strength]{ref}

= Breakdown-limited parallel-plate charge
{parent=Dielectric breakdown}
{title2=$Q_{\max}=\epsilon A E_{\rm bd}$}

For an ideal uniform <dielectric> between large parallel plates, $Q=CV=(\epsilon A/d)(Ed)=\epsilon AE$. A prescribed breakdown field $E_{\rm bd}$ therefore limits charge independently of plate separation. <Voltage> capacity still grows with separation. This ideal result neglects field enhancement near edges and any dependence of the breakdown field on gap geometry or environmental conditions.

= Dielectric strength
{parent=Dielectric breakdown}
{title2=$E_{\rm bd}$}
{wiki}

<Dielectric strength> characterizes the <electric field> at <dielectric breakdown> under specified test conditions. In an ideal uniform gap, it sets a voltage scale $V_{\rm bd}\simeq E_{\rm bd}d$.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/8.5 Molecular Model of a Dielectric>[§8.5: dielectric strength definition and voltage limit for a specified plate separation]{ref} The <parallel-plate capacitor> model consequently gives the stored <energy density> scale
$$
u_{\rm bd}\simeq\frac12\epsilon E_{\rm bd}^2.
$$
This is a model scale, not an operating rating: <fringing field of a capacitor> make local <electric fields> differ from $V/d$, while leakage and <dielectric loss> spend some supplied <energy> as heat. Comparing usable <capacitor energy> therefore requires insulation and breakdown performance as well as <permittivity>.

= Complex permittivity
{parent=Permittivity}
{title2=$\epsilon^*(\omega)=\epsilon'(\omega)-i\epsilon''(\omega)$}

For harmonic <electric fields> with time convention $e^{i\omega t}$, write $\mathbf D=\epsilon^*(\omega)\mathbf E$ for their complex amplitudes, where $\omega$ is <angular frequency>. The <real part> $\epsilon'$ describes the in-phase <electric displacement field>; the minus <imaginary part> $\epsilon''$ describes the loss component. The sign reverses with the opposite time convention. <Complex permittivity> can also be a <tensor> in a <linear anisotropic dielectric>.

= Conductive contribution to complex permittivity
{parent=Complex permittivity}
{title2=$\varepsilon_{r,\rm eff}=\varepsilon_r+i\sigma/(\omega\varepsilon_0)$}

With phasor convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, the <Ampere-Maxwell law> in a scalar conductor reads $\nabla\times\mathbf H=(\sigma-i\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r)\mathbf E$. Factoring $-i\omega\varepsilon_0$ gives the displayed effective relative <complex permittivity>. Its positive imaginary conductive term expresses loss in this convention; the sign reverses for $e^{+i\omega t}$. This combines conduction and displacement currents for the same field and must not be confused with the material's loss-free relative permittivity alone.

= Dielectric loss
{parent=Complex permittivity}
{wiki}

Conversion of electrical <energy> into heat in a <dielectric> driven by an <electric field>. In the stated harmonic convention, a passive scalar response has $\epsilon''\geq0$ for positive <angular frequency>. A <dielectric capacitance measurement> measures a dissipative component as well as <capacitance>; leakage from <electrical conductivity> can contribute to the inferred loss.

= Dielectric relaxation
{parent=Complex permittivity}

Delayed adjustment of <electric polarization> after a change in <electric field>. A simple illustrative model lets the slowly adjusting part $\mathbf P_s$ obey $\tau\,d\mathbf P_s/dt+\mathbf P_s=\epsilon_0\Delta\epsilon_r\mathbf E$, with a positive response time $\tau$. A harmonic <electric field> then gives a contribution $\Delta\epsilon_r/(1+i\omega\tau)$ to <complex permittivity> divided by <vacuum permittivity>. This model is an assumption, not a claim that all <dielectrics> have one response time.

= Dielectric capacitance measurement
{parent=Permittivity}

The motivating observation is that inserting an insulating material between fixed <capacitor> plates increases its <capacitance>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_10.html#Ch10-S1[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, II.10–1, opening paragraphs: observed increase in capacitance with an inserted insulator]{ref}. To investigate this response, use two flat parallel metal electrodes with measured overlapping <area> $A$ and separation $d$, a uniform specimen filling the gap, and an instrument that measures <electrical impedance>. Record the <capacitance> with and without the specimen at the same small applied <electric potential difference>, <frequency> and <temperature>, after <capacitance measurement calibration>. An increase in corrected <capacitance> indicates a larger <permittivity>, provided the geometry is unchanged. A guard electrode reduces the contribution of the <fringing field of a capacitor>. <Materials Measurement: Dielectric Materials>{ref}.

For negligible <dielectric loss> in the <parallel-plate capacitor> approximation,
$$
C=\frac{\epsilon A}{d},\qquad C_0=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d},\qquad
\epsilon_r=\frac{C}{C_0}=\frac{Cd}{\epsilon_0A}.
$$
An air reference approximates $C_0$; it is not an exact <vacuum> reference. For the following synthetic example, stipulate $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $d=1.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ and the rounded <vacuum permittivity> $\epsilon_0\approx8.854\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$. Then $C_0=88.54\,\mathrm{pF}$ in this model, and <relative permittivity> four gives $C=354.16\,\mathrm{pF}$. A residual parallel <capacitance> of $20\,\mathrm{pF}$ would make an uncorrected sample/reference ratio $374.16/108.54\approx3.447$, rather than four. This additive error does not cancel in a ratio.

{title=Synthetic <capacitance> response to <relative permittivity> and <frequency>}

At nonzero <frequency> $f$, measure both magnitude and <phase> of the <electric current> relative to the applied <electric potential difference>. Define $Y=I/V=1/Z$, where $Z$ is <electrical impedance>. For the stated <complex permittivity> convention, the ideal uniform specimen gives
$$
Y=i\omega C_0\epsilon_r^*=\omega C_0\epsilon_r''+i\omega C_0\epsilon_r',\qquad
C_p=\frac{\operatorname{Im}Y}{\omega},\qquad \omega=2\pi f.
$$
Thus a parallel <capacitor> and <electrical conductance> describe the readout at that <frequency>; a single scalar <capacitance> cannot describe both components. The diagram's assumed <dielectric relaxation> gives $C_p=265.62\,\mathrm{pF}$ and $C_0\epsilon_r''=88.54\,\mathrm{pF}$ at $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$. These are model predictions, not observations.

Repeat the reference measurement after removing the specimen and vary the small applied <electric potential difference> to test drift and <linearity>. Record specimen thickness, electrode <area>, contact conditions, <frequency> and <temperature>. Air gaps act as additional layers in series and can lower inferred <relative permittivity>; roughness, uncertain thickness and the <fringing field of a capacitor> limit the geometric formula. At sufficiently high <frequency>, the fixture itself changes the <electromagnetic field>, so low-<frequency> guarding and a lumped <capacitor> model require reassessment. <Materials Measurement: Dielectric Materials>{ref}.

= Capacitance measurement calibration
{parent=Dielectric capacitance measurement}

Characterize the instrument and fixture before inferring <permittivity>: use the prescribed open and short measurements to compensate residual parallel response and series <electrical impedance>, and check a known reference <capacitor> or the required load standard. Keep cables and electrode geometry reproducible. An open fixture measurement is distinct from the empty-gap reference <capacitor>; blindly subtracting its entire <capacitance> would remove the desired reference signal. Compensation cannot determine an incorrectly measured specimen thickness or eliminate all specimen-dependent <fringing field of a capacitor> errors. <Open and Short Compensation and Z Port Extension>{ref}.

= Foil capacitor dielectric experiment
{parent=Dielectric capacitance measurement}

Southern Methodist University's teaching lab, Introduction to the Parallel Plate Capacitor Lab, makes the response tangible with a <capacitor> assembled from aluminium foil, paper and taped wire contacts. Its instructions specify approximately $10\,\mathrm{cm}\times10\,\mathrm{cm}$ foil rectangles, aligned with the paper completely separating them. A <multimeter> in <capacitance> mode supplies the readout; a ruler measures plate dimensions and a micrometer measures specimen thickness. The lab replaces the paper with other sheets, including cardstock, plastic wrap and wax paper. It asks students to keep foil <area> and alignment consistent, measure thickness at several positions, estimate thickness uncertainty, and wait for the <capacitance> reading to stabilize. These are published preparation and measurement instructions, not measured results reported here.<SMU Physics 110x labs/Introduction to the Parallel Plate Capacitor Lab>[Experiments 1–2, Equipment You Will Be Using, and final construction and dielectric-study instructions]{ref}

The inference uses each specimen's own thickness $t$, not just a comparison of raw meter readings. With corrected <capacitance> $C_s$ and overlapping <area> $A$, the ideal estimate is $\epsilon_r=C_s t/(\epsilon_0A)$. A thicker sheet can give a smaller <capacitance> despite larger <relative permittivity>. This is why changing material while letting the plate gap change does not isolate <electric susceptibility> by itself.

Useful additional controls are to keep leads in the same positions, use <capacitance measurement calibration>, repeat one sheet after the others to check drift, and repeat the assembly to assess contact reproducibility. An air-gap reference must have known spacers maintaining the same separation; removing a supporting sheet and letting the foil touch is not such a reference. Record the instrument's test <frequency> and measurement mode when available. A <capacitance> display alone does not establish negligible <dielectric loss> or a strictly static <permittivity>. These are proposed controls extending the published teaching instructions.

Air gaps produce a particularly revealing systematic error. Suppose a uniform sheet of thickness $t$ and <relative permittivity> $\epsilon_r$ is separated from the <electrodes> by air layers of total thickness $g$. Approximate air <permittivity> by <vacuum permittivity>, neglect the <fringing field of a capacitor> and use the same <area> in each layer. The common normal <electric displacement field> has magnitude $D=Q/A$, so integrating the <electric field> through the layers gives
$$
V=\frac{Q}{\epsilon_0A}\left(g+\frac{t}{\epsilon_r}\right),\qquad
C_s=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{g+t/\epsilon_r}.
$$
If the analyst inserts the sheet thickness alone into the ideal filled-gap formula, the inferred value is
$$
\widehat\epsilon_r=\frac{C_s t}{\epsilon_0A}
=\frac{\epsilon_r}{1+\epsilon_r g/t}.
$$
For the stipulated model $\epsilon_r=3$ and $g=t/10$, this gives $\widehat\epsilon_r=30/13\simeq2.308$, not three. These values are calculated examples, not measured sheet properties. If total plate separation $d=t+g$ is used instead, the effective estimate is $(t+g)/(g+t/\epsilon_r)$; it still mixes the sheet and the air. Thus measuring a thickness accurately cannot compensate for an unrecognized layer.

The <fringing field of a capacitor> and additive lead <capacitance> further limit a foil measurement. Keep lateral plate dimensions large relative to separation, and use a <guard electrode of a capacitor> in a suitable fixture for more demanding work.<Materials Measurement: Dielectric Materials>[Solution: guarded three-electrode fixture, fringe capacitance, specimen flatness and air gaps]{ref} A family of thickness measurements can help distinguish material response from an additive offset, but air gaps can vary with the assembly. A large, repeatable reading supports a <capacitance> change; extracting a material <electric susceptibility> requires the geometry, gaps, instrument response and <dielectric loss> assumptions to survive these checks. No measured <relative permittivity> is claimed for any of the lab's sheets.

= Vacuum permittivity
{title2=$\epsilon_0$}
{parent=Permittivity}
{wiki}

<Vacuum permittivity> is the electromagnetic constant $\epsilon_0$ in <Coulomb's law> and the <vacuum Maxwell equations>. In classical <vacuum>, $\mathbf P=0$ and the <electric displacement field> is $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$. A material's <permittivity> includes its <electric polarization> response; <vacuum permittivity> supplies the common reference, not a separate adjustable material property. Its <SI> unit is <farad> per metre.

For elementary calculations, <vacuum permittivity> is approximately $8.854\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$. This is a deliberately rounded value, not an exact decimal or a statement of current measurement precision.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, vacuum electric permittivity row: value and nonzero standard uncertainty]{ref} It sets the scale in <Coulomb's law>, $F=|q_1q_2|/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ for two stationary point <electric charges> with separation <length> $r$ in <vacuum>, and therefore enters the reference <capacitance> $C_0=\epsilon_0A/d$ of a <parallel-plate capacitor>.

Since the revised <SI> took effect on 20 May 2019, the numerical value of <vacuum permittivity> in <SI> units is determined experimentally, rather than fixed exactly by the unit definitions. The numerical values of the <elementary charge> $e$, <Planck constant> $h$ and <speed of light> $c$ are fixed exactly. The <fine-structure constant> $\alpha$ is measured, and
$$
\alpha=\frac{e^2}{2\epsilon_0hc},\qquad
\epsilon_0=\frac{e^2}{2\alpha hc},\qquad
\epsilon_0\mu_0=\frac1{c^2},
$$
where $\mu_0$ is <vacuum permeability>. Consequently <vacuum permittivity> and <vacuum permeability> have experimental uncertainty, while their product is exact in <SI> units because $c$ is exact. This is a change in metrological status, not a new material dependence of <vacuum permittivity>.<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[pp. 123–125 (defining constants and alpha relation); section 2.3.1, printed p. 128 (ampere, epsilon0 and mu0); 26th CGPM Resolution 1, printed p. 194 (20 May 2019 effective date)]{ref}
