= Solution
{parent=a}

Fix an iteration and abbreviate $x=x^k$, $s=s^k$, $d=\nabla f(x)^Ts$. Since $s$ is a <descent direction>, $d<0$. The <directional derivative> gives
$$
f(x+\alpha s)-f(x)-\beta_1\alpha d=(1-\beta_1)\alpha d+o(\alpha).
$$
Choose $\bar\alpha>0$ so that $|o(\alpha)|\leq\tfrac12(1-\beta_1)(-d)\alpha$ for $0<\alpha\leq\bar\alpha$. Then
$$
f(x+\alpha s)-f(x)-\beta_1\alpha d\leq\tfrac12(1-\beta_1)\alpha d<0.
$$
This is the <Armijo condition> for every such positive <step size>, and $\alpha=0$ gives equality. In particular, a <backtracking line search> terminates after finitely many reductions.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write $\phi(\alpha)=f(x+\alpha s)$ and $h(\alpha)=\phi(\alpha)-\phi(0)-\beta_1\alpha d$. Part (a) gives $h(\alpha)<0$ just to the right of zero. If $L$ is a lower bound for $f$, then
$$
h(\alpha)\geq L-\phi(0)-\beta_1\alpha d\longrightarrow+\infty,
$$
because $d<0$. By <continuity>, there is a first positive zero $A$ of $h$, and $h(\alpha)\leq0$ on $[0,A]$. Apply the <mean value theorem> to $\phi$ on this interval: for some $t\in(0,A)$,
$$
\nabla f(x+ts)^Ts=\phi'(t)=\frac{\phi(A)-\phi(0)}A=\beta_1d>\beta_2d.
$$
At this same $t$, $h(t)\leq0$ supplies the <Armijo condition>. Thus $\alpha=t>0$ satisfies both weak <Wolfe conditions>. The boundedness assumption prevents the <objective function> from following its initial negative slope forever; this is the mechanism behind <Existence of a Wolfe step>.

= Solution

If $g=\nabla f(x)\ne0$, the <positive-definite symmetric matrix> $B$ is an <invertible matrix> and $s=-B^{-1}g\ne0$. Multiplying $Bs=-g$ by $s^T$ gives
$$
\boxed{g^Ts=-s^TBs<0}.
$$
Hence $s$ is a <descent direction>. If $g=0$, then $s=0$ and the algorithm has already reached a <stationary point>; the strict descent assertion applies before termination.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Set $p=x^{k+1}-x^k=\alpha^ks^k$ and $y=\nabla f(x^{k+1})-\nabla f(x^k)$. The <quasi-Newton secant equation> is $B^{k+1}p=y$. If $B^{k+1}$ is a <positive-definite symmetric matrix> and $p\ne0$, it follows that
$$
p^Ty=p^TB^{k+1}p>0.
$$
The weak curvature part of the <Wolfe conditions> ensures precisely this necessary condition:
$$
p^Ty=\alpha^k\bigl(\nabla f(x^{k+1})^Ts^k-\nabla f(x^k)^Ts^k\bigr)\geq\alpha^k(\beta_2-1)\nabla f(x^k)^Ts^k>0.
$$
The last product is positive because $\alpha^k>0$, $\beta_2-1<0$, and $s^k$ is a <descent direction>. This explains why a <Quasi-Newton method> often uses the <Wolfe conditions> rather than only the <Armijo condition>.

{parent=c}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Interpret boundedness above uniformly: there is an $M<\infty$ such that $B^k\preceq MI$ for every $k$. Since $B^k$ is a <positive-definite symmetric matrix>, the <spectral theorem> implies $(B^k)^{-1}\succeq M^{-1}I$. Therefore
$$
-\nabla f(x^k)^Ts^k=\nabla f(x^k)^T(B^k)^{-1}\nabla f(x^k)\geq\frac1M\|\nabla f(x^k)\|^2.
$$
Combining the <Armijo condition> with $\alpha^k\geq C>0$ yields
$$
f(x^k)-f(x^{k+1})\geq\frac{\beta_1C}{M}\|\nabla f(x^k)\|^2.
$$
If $f\geq L$, a <telescoping sum> gives
$$
\frac{\beta_1C}{M}\sum_{k=0}^N\|\nabla f(x^k)\|^2\leq f(x^0)-f(x^{N+1})\leq f(x^0)-L.
$$
The nonnegative <series> converges, so its terms tend to zero:
$$
\boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla f(x^k)\|=0}.
$$
This proves first-order global convergence in the sense of <stationarity from uniformly bounded quasi-Newton matrices>. It does not imply that every nonconvex <objective function> reaches a <global minimum>, or that the iterates themselves converge. Every finite <limit point> is a <stationary point>, by <continuity> of the <gradient>.

= Solution

The <Cauchy point> minimizes the model along the negative <gradient> ray inside the <trust region>. With $q=g^THg$, write
$$
s_C=-\tau g,\qquad m(-tg)=a-t\|g\|^2+\tfrac12t^2q,\qquad 0\leq t\leq\Delta/\|g\|.
$$
If $q\leq0$, this last <quadratic function> decreases throughout the interval. If $q>0$, its unconstrained <minimum> occurs at $t=\|g\|^2/q$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\tau=\begin{cases}\Delta/\|g\|,&g^THg\leq0,\\\min\{\Delta/\|g\|,\|g\|^2/(g^THg)\},&g^THg>0.\end{cases}}
$$
The additive constant $a$ has no effect on the <Cauchy point>. To obtain the <Cauchy decrease bound>, first suppose $q>0$. Since $\tau q\leq\|g\|^2$,
$$
a-m(s_C)=\tau\|g\|^2-\tfrac12\tau^2q\geq\tfrac12\tau\|g\|^2.
$$
The <spectral norm> inequality $q\leq\|H\|\|g\|^2$ gives $\tau\geq\min\{\Delta/\|g\|,1/\|H\|\}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{a-m(s_C)\geq\tfrac12\|g\|\min\{\Delta,\|g\|/\|H\|\}}.
$$
If $q\leq0$, the decrease is at least $\Delta\|g\|$, which also implies the displayed bound. When $H=0$, interpret $\|g\|/\|H\|=+\infty$. Thus the universal choice is $c=1/2$.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At $x^k$, choose $a=f(x^k)$, $g=\nabla f(x^k)$, $H=\nabla^2f(x^k)$, and the current radius $\Delta=\Delta_k>0$. A bounded <symmetric matrix> approximation to the <Hessian matrix> can also be used. Stop if the <gradient> is sufficiently small; otherwise approximately solve the <trust-region subproblem> to obtain $s_k$ with $\|s_k\|\leq\Delta_k$ and at least a fixed <fraction of Cauchy decrease>. Its predicted decrease is then positive, so calculate the <trust-region acceptance ratio>
$$
\rho_k=\frac{f(x^k)-f(x^k+s_k)}{m_k(0)-m_k(s_k)}.
$$
Fix $0<\eta_1<\eta_2<1$, $0<\gamma_d<1<\gamma_u$, and a maximum radius $\Delta_{\max}$. One valid update is:
* If $\rho_k<\eta_1$, reject the trial: $x^{k+1}=x^k$ and $\Delta_{k+1}=\gamma_d\Delta_k$.
* Otherwise accept $x^{k+1}=x^k+s_k$. If additionally $\rho_k>\eta_2$ and the step reaches the boundary, enlarge the radius to $\min\{\gamma_u\Delta_k,\Delta_{\max}\}$; otherwise keep it unchanged.

The <trust region> controls the reliability of the model; the ratio decides whether that reliability was borne out by the actual <objective function>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <Cauchy point> provides both an inexpensive fallback step and a standard against which a better step can be judged. Requiring a fixed <fraction of Cauchy decrease> gives
$$
m_k(0)-m_k(s_k)\geq\frac\kappa2\|\nabla f(x^k)\|\min\{\Delta_k,\|\nabla f(x^k)\|/\|H_k\|\},\qquad\kappa>0.
$$
Consequently an exact <global minimum> of each <trust-region subproblem> is unnecessary. A step from any inner solver is adequate if it meets this bound.

The convergence argument combines this decrease with model accuracy. For example, suppose the <gradient> is uniformly <Lipschitz continuous> on a neighborhood of the visited <level set>, the model <Hessian matrices> are uniformly bounded, and the usual acceptance and radius safeguards are used. If $\|\nabla f(x^k)\|\geq\epsilon>0$, the <Taylor expansion> gives a model error bounded by $K\|s_k\|^2$. For sufficiently small $\Delta_k$, division by the <Cauchy decrease bound> makes $|1-\rho_k|$ small. Such trials are successful and cannot keep shrinking the radius. With the <gradient> bounded away from zero, the radius therefore has a positive lower bound, and each accepted step decreases $f$ by a fixed positive amount. There must be infinitely many accepted steps, since repeated rejection would force the radius below the small-radius acceptance threshold. This contradicts boundedness below of $f$ and proves $\liminf_k\|\nabla f(x^k)\|=0$.

Stronger standard conclusions can be obtained with further regularity and safeguards. Merely being twice <continuously differentiable> and bounded below does not supply uniform <Hessian> bounds on an unbounded <level set>; those uniform assumptions must be stated when using this argument.

= Solution

Taking the <natural logarithm> of the positive product constraint gives $h(x)=\sum_i a_i\log x_i=0$. Its <gradient> has entries $a_i/x_i>0$, so the equality <constraint qualification> holds everywhere on the <feasible set>. Use the <optimization Lagrangian>
$$
L(x,\lambda)=\sum_i a_ix_i-\lambda\sum_i a_i\log x_i.
$$
Stationarity requires $a_i-\lambda a_i/x_i=0$, so every $x_i=\lambda$. Substitution into the constraint, using $\sum_i a_i=1$, gives $\lambda=1$. Thus the only candidate for a <local minimum> is $x=(1,\ldots,1)$.

To prove global minimality without assuming the inequality to be established, set $u_i=\log x_i$. The constraint becomes the <affine hyperplane> $\sum_i a_iu_i=0$, and the <objective function> becomes $F(u)=\sum_i a_ie^{u_i}$. Its <Hessian matrix> $\operatorname{diag}(a_ie^{u_i})$ is <positive definite>, so $F$ is <strictly convex>. The elementary tangent inequality $e^t\geq1+t$, with equality only at zero, gives
$$
F(u)\geq\sum_i a_i(1+u_i)=1.
$$
Equality forces every $u_i=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x_*=(1,\ldots,1),\qquad\min\sum_i a_ix_i=1}.
$$
This point is the unique <global minimizer> and the only <local minimizer>. The <logarithm> change of variables also explains why a multiplicative constraint leads to a <convex optimization> argument.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $G=\prod_i y_i^{a_i}>0$ be the weighted <geometric mean> and set $x_i=y_i/G$. Since the weights sum to one,
$$
\prod_i x_i^{a_i}=\frac{\prod_i y_i^{a_i}}{G^{\sum_i a_i}}=1.
$$
Part (i) implies $\sum_i a_iy_i/G\geq1$, proving the <weighted arithmetic-geometric mean inequality>:
$$
\boxed{\prod_i y_i^{a_i}\leq\sum_i a_iy_i}.
$$
Equality holds exactly when $x_i=1$ for every $i$, or equivalently when all the $y_i$ are equal.

If $x,y$ are <feasible points> and $0\leq t\leq1$, <concavity> gives
$$
c_i(tx+(1-t)y)\geq tc_i(x)+(1-t)c_i(y)\geq0
$$
for every $i$. Thus the <feasible set> is a <convex set>. Since the <objective function> is <convex>, this is a <convex optimization> problem.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

With inequalities written as $c_i\geq0$, use the <optimization Lagrangian> $L(x,\lambda)=f(x)-\sum_i\lambda_ic_i(x)$. The <Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions> at $(x_*,\lambda_*)$ are
$$
c_i(x_*)\geq0,\quad\lambda_{*,i}\geq0,\quad\lambda_{*,i}c_i(x_*)=0,\quad\nabla f(x_*)=\sum_i\lambda_{*,i}\nabla c_i(x_*).
$$
For any <feasible point> $z$, the first-order inequalities for a <convex function> and a <concave function> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z)-f(x_*)&\geq\nabla f(x_*)^T(z-x_*)\\
&=\sum_i\lambda_{*,i}\nabla c_i(x_*)^T(z-x_*)\\
&\geq\sum_i\lambda_{*,i}\bigl(c_i(z)-c_i(x_*)\bigr)\\
&=\sum_i\lambda_{*,i}c_i(z)\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last equality uses <complementary slackness>. Thus $x_*$ is a <global minimizer>. <Slater's condition> is useful for necessity and existence of <Lagrange multipliers>, but is not required for this sufficiency argument once a <KKT point> is supplied.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $\mu>0$, the <logarithmic barrier> objective is
$$
\boxed{f_\mu(x)=f(x)-\mu\sum_{i=1}^p\log c_i(x)},\qquad D=\{x:c_i(x)>0\text{ for all }i\}.
$$
The <domain of a function> $D$ is a nonempty <convex set> by the same <concavity> argument as in part (a), with strict inequalities. Differentiating twice on $D$ gives
$$
\nabla^2f_\mu(x)=\nabla^2f(x)-\mu\sum_i\frac{\nabla^2c_i(x)}{c_i(x)}+\mu\sum_i\frac{\nabla c_i(x)\nabla c_i(x)^T}{c_i(x)^2}.
$$
Here $\nabla^2f\succeq0$ by <convexity>, $-\nabla^2c_i\succeq0$ by <concavity>, and each <outer product> is a <positive semidefinite matrix>. Hence $\nabla^2f_\mu\succeq0$, proving <convexity> on $D$.

The domain qualification matters: the displayed real-valued <logarithm> expression is not defined on all of $\mathbb R^n$. The precise whole-space version is the extended-real <convex function> equal to $f_\mu$ on $D$ and $+\infty$ outside $D$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Choose a strictly <feasible point>, an initial barrier parameter $\mu_0>0$, and $0<\theta<1$. At outer iteration $k$, approximately minimize the <logarithmic barrier> objective $f_{\mu_k}$ over its strictly feasible <domain of a function>. For example, use damped <Newton methods>, choosing an inner <step size> by <backtracking line search> so that every $c_i$ stays positive and the barrier <objective function> decreases. Solve to a tolerance $\|\nabla f_{\mu_k}(x^k)\|\leq\varepsilon_k$, where $\varepsilon_k\to0$. Set $\mu_{k+1}=\theta\mu_k$ and use $x^k$ as the next starting point.

The associated <Lagrange multiplier> estimates are
$$
\lambda_i^k=\frac{\mu_k}{c_i(x^k)}>0,\qquad\lambda_i^kc_i(x^k)=\mu_k.
$$
Reducing $\mu_k$ makes these perturbed <complementary slackness> equations approach the <KKT conditions>. This describes the generic <interior-point method>; existence of a solution to every inner minimization needs appropriate additional attainment assumptions and does not follow from strict feasibility alone.

{parent=d}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A precise conditional convergence statement is: with the smoothness and <convexity> assumptions of the question, a strict <feasible point>, $\mu_k\downarrow0$, and barrier stationarity residuals
$$
r_k=\nabla f(x^k)-\sum_i\lambda_i^k\nabla c_i(x^k)\longrightarrow0,
$$
every convergent sequence of strictly <feasible points> $x^k\to x_*$ has a <global minimizer> as its <limit>. The <Lagrange multiplier> estimates are bounded and have a <subsequence> converging to multipliers satisfying the <KKT conditions>. The theorem does not assert convergence of the primal sequence without further assumptions.

First, <continuity> gives $c_i(x_*)\geq0$. For an inactive <optimization constraint>, $c_i(x_*)>0$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_i^k=\mu_k/c_i(x^k)\longrightarrow0}.
$$
For the <active constraints>, the hint permits assuming $\lambda_i^k\to\lambda_i^*$. Combining this with the inactive limits gives $\lambda_*\geq0$, and passage to the <limit> in the residual equation proves stationarity. Since $\lambda_i^kc_i(x^k)=\mu_k\to0$, <complementary slackness> also holds. All <KKT conditions> follow, and part (b) proves global optimality.

The hint's multiplier assumption can in fact be weakened to selecting a <subsequence>. To see why the estimates are bounded, choose a strict <feasible point> $z$ and put $\delta=\min_i c_i(z)>0$. <Concavity> gives
$$
\delta\|\lambda^k\|_1\leq\sum_i\lambda_i^kc_i(z)\leq p\mu_k+(\nabla f(x^k)-r_k)^T(z-x^k).
$$
The right side is bounded because $x^k\to x_*$ and $r_k\to0$. This is the <bounded barrier multipliers under Slater's condition> argument. A convergent multiplier <subsequence> then gives the same <KKT point>, which suffices to prove that the assumed primal <limit> is globally optimal.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For an <active constraint> with $\lambda_i^*>0$, the perturbed <complementary slackness> equation gives $c_i(x^k)\sim\mu_k/\lambda_i^*$. The <Hessian matrix> from part (c) therefore includes
$$
\frac{\mu_k}{c_i(x^k)^2}\nabla c_i(x^k)\nabla c_i(x^k)^T\sim\frac{(\lambda_i^*)^2}{\mu_k}\nabla c_i(x_*)\nabla c_i(x_*)^T.
$$
If $\nabla c_i(x_*)\ne0$, curvature normal to that boundary grows like $1/\mu_k$. By contrast, a direction tangent to every <active constraint> annihilates these large normal <outer products> at the limiting point, and its curvature may remain of order one. A large spread of <eigenvalues> then makes the Newton linear system poorly conditioned. Under bounded remaining curvature and a positive bounded tangential curvature, the <condition number> typically grows like $1/\mu_k$; this is the usual <ill-conditioning of a logarithmic barrier>.

It is not a universal rate: there may be no nonzero common tangent direction, some limiting <Lagrange multipliers> may vanish, or all curvatures may grow together. The <Hessian> formula identifies the source of the difficulty without assuming these extra nondegeneracy conditions.
