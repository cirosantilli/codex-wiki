= Solution
{parent=1}

The four dimensional equations express <mass conservation>, <conservation of momentum>[momentum balance] with <pressure>, <gravity> and <viscous stress>, heat advection and conduction, and a linear thermal-expansion <equation of state>. For heating from below, suitable plate conditions are $\mathbf u=0$ and $T=T_0+\Delta T$ at $z=0$, and $\mathbf u=0$, $T=T_0$ at $z=d$. <No-slip boundary condition>[No slip] and <thermal insulation> on the sidewall provide one suitable lateral completion; prescribed lateral <temperatures> would be another specified thermal setup.

Let $\kappa=k/(\rho_0c_p)$ be <thermal diffusivity> and $\nu=\mu/\rho_0$ <kinematic viscosity>. Use $d$, $d^2/\kappa$, $\kappa/d$ and $\Delta T$ as length, time, <velocity> and <temperature> scales. Subtract the static reference <pressure> $p_H$ with $\nabla p_H=-\rho_0g\mathbf k$ and scale its remainder by $\mu\kappa/d^2$. Then
$$
\boxed{B=\alpha\Delta T,\qquad Pr=\nu/\kappa,\qquad Ra=\frac{g\alpha\Delta T d^3}{\nu\kappa}.}
$$
<Density> becomes $\rho_0(1-BT)$. Substitution and division by the corresponding diffusion/viscous scales give
$$
-BT_t+\nabla\cdot[(1-BT)\mathbf u]=0,\qquad
Pr^{-1}(1-BT)(\mathbf u_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u)=-\nabla p+Ra\,T\mathbf k+\Delta\mathbf u,
$$
$$
(1-BT)(T_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T)=\Delta T.
$$
In the Boussinesq small-<density>-contrast limit retain <buoyancy> while neglecting the other $B$ factors. At infinite <Prandtl number>, after any fast initial adjustment, the equations are
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad0=-\nabla p+Ra\,T\mathbf k+\Delta\mathbf u,\qquad T_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla T=\Delta T.}
$$
The <velocity> is instantaneously constrained by a viscous elliptic balance; the <temperature> still evolves.

Take the <divergence> of the <Poisson equation>[vector Poisson equation]. Derivatives commute, and <divergence> of a <curl> is zero, so $\Delta(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)=0$. Its boundary value is zero, hence the <maximum principle> gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ throughout $D$. The vector identity now gives $\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{curl}\mathbf A=\operatorname{curl}\mathbf u$, or
$$
\operatorname{curl}(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf A-\mathbf u)=0.
$$
The layer domain is <simply connected> because its horizontal section is <simply connected>. For a <curl>-free field on such a domain, define $\chi(x)$ as its <line integral> from a fixed base point to $x$. Contractibility of loops and <Stokes' theorem> make the <integral> <path-independent>, and differentiation gives its <gradient> equal to the field. Thus $\operatorname{curl}\mathbf A-\mathbf u=\nabla\chi$.

<Incompressibility> then gives $\Delta\chi=0$, while the prescribed normal <curl> gives $\partial_n\chi=0$. <Green's first identity>[Green's identity] yields
$$
\int_D|\nabla\chi|^2\,dV=\int_{\partial D}\chi\partial_n\chi\,dS-\int_D\chi\Delta\chi\,dV=0.
$$
Hence $\chi$ is constant on the connected domain and $\boxed{\operatorname{curl}\mathbf A=\mathbf u}$. The extra condition $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf n=0$ belongs to the stated potential boundary problem; the reconstruction argument above uses the <divergence> and normal-<curl> conditions. On a multiply connected domain a <curl>-free field need not be a globally single-valued <gradient>.

Finally $\operatorname{curl}(T\mathbf k)=(T_y,-T_x,0)$. Taking the <curl> of the infinite-Prandtl <conservation of momentum>[momentum balance] and using $\operatorname{curl}\mathbf u=-\Delta\mathbf A$ gives
$$
0=Ra(T_y\mathbf i-T_x\mathbf j)-\Delta^2\mathbf A,\qquad
\boxed{\nabla^4\mathbf A+Ra(T_x\mathbf j-T_y\mathbf i)=0.}
$$
The derivation is an instance of <velocity reconstruction from a mixed-boundary vector Poisson problem>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Define $\chi_1$ to be one in <phase> 1 and zero in <phase> 2, including its dependence on both transverse coordinates even though the averaged fields depend only on $x,t$. With no interphase <interphase mass transfer>[mass transfer], its distributional <phase>-mass equation is $\partial_t(\rho\chi_1)+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v\chi_1)=0$. At a pipe <cross-section> $\Sigma$ of area $A$, set
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac1A\int_\Sigma\chi_1\,dA,\quad
\rho_1=\frac1{A\alpha}\int_\Sigma\rho\chi_1\,dA,\quad
u_1=\frac{\int_\Sigma\rho v_x\chi_1\,dA}{A\alpha\rho_1}.}
$$
Thus $\rho_1$ is an intrinsic <phase> <density> and $u_1$ is the <density-weighted mean> axial <velocity>. This weighting is necessary when <density> varies within the <phase>; it becomes the ordinary intrinsic mean for an incompressible <phase> of constant <density>.

Integrate over the <cross-section>. The transverse <divergence> contributes no boundary mass <flux> at the impermeable pipe wall, and the material interface has no mass leakage. The section area is constant, so
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\rho_1\alpha)+\partial_x(\rho_1\alpha u_1)=0.}
$$
For <phase> 2 use $\chi_2=1-\chi_1$ and fraction $1-\alpha$, with the analogous intrinsic <density> and <density>-weighted <velocity>:
$$
\boxed{\partial_t[\rho_2(1-\alpha)]+\partial_x[\rho_2(1-\alpha)u_2]=0.}
$$
The intrinsic quantities are defined where their <phase> fraction is nonzero. These are the <intrinsic phase averages in a two-fluid pipe model>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Freeze a uniform state with $0<\alpha<1$, $\rho_i>0$, and seek a finite <characteristic speed> $c$. Denote its perturbation amplitudes by $a,v_1,v_2,\pi$. The two <phase>-mass equations and two momentum equations give
$$
(u_1-c)a+\alpha v_1=0,\qquad-(u_2-c)a+(1-\alpha)v_2=0,
$$
$$
\rho_1(u_1-c)v_1+\pi=0,\qquad\rho_2(u_2-c)v_2+\pi=0.
$$
Eliminating the amplitudes gives
$$
\rho_1(1-\alpha)(u_1-c)^2+\rho_2\alpha(u_2-c)^2=0.
$$
With $s^2=\rho_1(1-\alpha)/(\rho_2\alpha)>0$, factor the quadratic over the complex numbers to obtain
$$
\boxed{c_\pm=\frac{u_2\pm is u_1}{1\pm is}=\frac{u_2+s^2u_1}{1+s^2}\pm i\frac{s(u_1-u_2)}{1+s^2}.}
$$
For nonzero relative <velocity> the speeds are a nonreal conjugate pair. A <Fourier mode>[Fourier perturbation] $e^{ik(x-ct)}$ on the growing branch has magnitude $e^{|k|\,|\operatorname{Im}c|t}$. Arbitrarily short waves therefore grow arbitrarily quickly, preventing a continuous dependence estimate in the usual finite-order <Sobolev norms>. This generic slip state is <ill posed> as an <initial-value problem>.

The <pressure> has no independent time-evolution equation; it enforces the incompressible <volume> <constraint>, so the request concerns the finite <characteristic speeds> rather than a freely propagating <pressure> mode. At $u_1=u_2$ the two speeds coincide and this particular nonreal-speed argument disappears; further degeneracy analysis is needed there, rather than calling coincident real speeds complex. The issue is <complex characteristic speeds of a pressure-coupled two-fluid model>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For constant <phase> <density>, let $u_1$ be the intrinsic mean axial speed and define
$$
\boxed{D_1=\frac{\int_\Sigma\chi_1v_x^2\,dA}{A\alpha u_1^2}.}
$$
The profile coefficient measures the momentum <flux> relative to a uniform <velocity> profile with the same mean. Cauchy-Schwarz gives $D_1\geq1$ where the mean is nonzero, with equality for a uniform <phase> profile. At zero mean it is safer to retain the <second moment> itself rather than divide by $u_1^2$.

In the stated common-<pressure>, no-extra-force closure, the averaged conservative momentum equation is
$$
\rho_1[\partial_t(\alpha u_1)+\partial_x(\alpha D_1u_1^2)]+\alpha p_x=0.
$$
For constant $D_1$, expand its derivatives and use $\alpha_t+u_1\alpha_x+\alpha(u_1)_x=0$. The terms combine as
$$
\partial_t(\alpha u_1)+\partial_x(\alpha D_1u_1^2)=\alpha(u_1)_t+(2D_1-1)\alpha u_1(u_1)_x+(D_1-1)u_1^2\alpha_x.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho_1\{\alpha(u_1)_t+(2D_1-1)\alpha u_1(u_1)_x+(D_1-1)u_1^2\alpha_x\}+\alpha p_x=0.}
$$
This is the <phase momentum profile coefficient> correction. It accounts for the <second moment> of <velocity>, not merely a rescaling of the convective speed. A variable coefficient would also produce $\rho_1\alpha u_1^2(D_1)_x$.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Stretch $z=\delta Z$ and write $f=\delta h$, $w=\delta W$, with horizontal <velocity> and time left order one. The longitudinal viscous term is $\delta^{-2}u_{ZZ}$, so set $P=\delta^2p$. For $Re\,\delta^2\ll1$, the leading bulk equations are $P_Z=0$, $u_{ZZ}=P_x$ and $u_x+W_Z=0$.

The normal-stress condition gives $P=-\delta^3\widehat C\,h_{xx}$ at leading order, and the tangential condition gives $u_Z(h)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\beta=3,\qquad C=\delta^3\widehat C,\qquad P=-Ch_{xx}.}
$$
<No-slip boundary condition>[No slip] at the bed, $u(0)=0$, yields $u=P_x(Z^2/2-hZ)$. Averaging through the depth gives
$$
\boxed{\overline u=-\frac{h^2}{3}P_x=\frac C3h^2h_{xxx}.}
$$
The kinematic condition becomes $h_t+u(h)h_x-W(h)=-\widehat\epsilon/\delta$ at leading order. Integration of continuity from the impermeable bed then gives
$$
\boxed{h_t+(h\overline u)_x=-\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon=\widehat\epsilon/\delta.}
$$

A nonzero contact speed or evaporative <flux> is singular in the strictly no-slip sharp-contact-line model with mobility proportional to $h^3$. A finite nonzero limiting $\overline u$ would require $h_{xxx}=3\overline u/(Ch^2)$, which is singular for a linearly vanishing height and generally incompatible with a prescribed finite slope at the exact line. Thus a dynamic finite-contact-angle condition needs microscopic regularization, such as slip or a <precursor film>. In the slow-drying limit a fixed <contact angle> can instead be used for the leading quasistatic outer profile; it is not an exact regular finite-rate contact-line solution.

At leading quasistatic order $h_{xxx}=0$, so the symmetric profile satisfying the imposed outer angle is
$$
h(x,T)=\frac\theta{2s(T)}(s(T)^2-x^2),\qquad T=\epsilon t.
$$
Its cross-sectional <volume> is $V=\int_{-s}^sh\,dx=2\theta s^2/3$. The moving endpoints contribute no boundary-height term, and the end <flux> vanishes, giving $V_t=-2\epsilon s$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{ds}{dT}=-\frac3{2\theta},\qquad s(T)=s_0-\frac{3T}{2\theta},\qquad T_{\rm dry}=\frac{2\theta s_0}3.}
$$
These are leading slow-time results up to extinction; the approximation need not remain uniform at the final vanishing scale.

To recover the small <velocity>, differentiate the quasistatic profile using $s_t=-3\epsilon/(2\theta)$:
$$
h_t=-\frac{3\epsilon}{4}(1+x^2/s^2),\quad
(h\overline u)_x=\frac\epsilon4(-1+3x^2/s^2).
$$
<Symmetry> makes the <flux> zero at the centre, so $h\overline u=-\epsilon x(s^2-x^2)/(4s^2)$. Division by the height gives
$$
\boxed{\overline u=-\frac{\epsilon x}{2s\theta}.}
$$
The flow is inward on both sides, suggesting transport of suspended matter toward a central deposit rather than a perimeter ring. Coffee commonly leaves the opposite, ring-like pattern. A possible missing ingredient is <contact-line pinning>: holding the perimeter fixed makes <evaporation> require outward replenishment, and stronger <evaporation> near the edge reinforces that transport. Receding constant-angle and pinned <contact lines> therefore have qualitatively different deposit mechanisms.

For comparison, the same uniform-<evaporation> parabolic model with fixed $s$ gives $\theta_t=-3\epsilon/s$ and $\overline u=\epsilon x/(s\theta)$, an outward <velocity>. This comparison isolates the boundary assumption even before adding edge-enhanced <evaporation>.
