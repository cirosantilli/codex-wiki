{title2=Long-wave model}

= i
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Take $z$ positive away from the wall into the liquid, and write $p_a$ for <atmospheric pressure>. For a constant-<mass density> <Newtonian fluid>, the two-dimensional <Navier-Stokes equation> and <continuity equation> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x+w_z&=0,\\
\rho(u_t+uu_x+wu_z)&=-p_x+\mu(u_{xx}+u_{zz})+\rho g\sin\alpha,\\
\rho(w_t+uw_x+ww_z)&=-p_z+\mu(w_{xx}+w_{zz})-\rho g\cos\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$
The stationary wall imposes the <no-slip boundary condition> and <no-penetration boundary condition>, $u=w=0$ at $z=0$. For a flat <free surface> with negligible air <shear stress>, the <stress boundary condition> is $u_z=0$, $p=p_a$ at $z=h_0$; the <kinematic boundary condition> is $w=0$ there.

For <steady flow> independent of $x$, these equations give $w=0$, $\mu u_{zz}=-\rho g\sin\alpha$ and $p_z=-\rho g\cos\alpha$. Two <integrations> and the <boundary conditions> give the <uniform gravity-driven Newtonian film>:
$$
\boxed{\bar u=\frac{\rho g\sin\alpha}{2\mu}(2h_0z-z^2),\qquad
\bar w=0,\qquad \bar p=p_a+\rho g\cos\alpha(h_0-z).}
$$
Its surface <velocity> is $U_0=\rho gh_0^2\sin\alpha/(2\mu)$, while its mean <velocity> is $2U_0/3$.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Define the aspect ratio and the thickness-based <Reynolds number> by
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon=\frac{h_0}{L}\ll1,\qquad
\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho U_0h_0}{\mu}.}
$$
Use the <nondimensionalization>
$$
x=Lx_*,\quad z=h_0z_*,\quad t=\frac L{U_0}t_*,\quad
u=U_0u_*,\quad w=\varepsilon U_0w_*,\quad
p=p_a+\rho gh_0\sin\alpha\,p_*.
$$
The small transverse <velocity> scale follows from <incompressibility>. Dropping stars, the base fields become $\bar u=2z-z^2$ and $\bar p=(1-z)\cot\alpha$. Before subtracting the base state, the dimensionless <Navier-Stokes equation> reads
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x+w_z&=0,\\
\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon(u_t+uu_x+wu_z)
&=-2\varepsilon p_x+u_{zz}+\varepsilon^2u_{xx}+2,\\
\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon^2(w_t+uw_x+ww_z)
&=-2p_z+\varepsilon(w_{zz}+\varepsilon^2w_{xx})-2\cot\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$
For example, division of the downslope momentum equation by $\mu U_0/h_0^2$ makes the inertial coefficient $\rho U_0h_0^2/(\mu L)=\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon$ and the <pressure gradient> coefficient $2\varepsilon$. The transverse equation has been divided instead by $\mu U_0/h_0^2$ after using its smaller <velocity> scale; this gives the displayed $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon^2$ coefficient.

Now substitute $u=\bar u+\hat u$, $w=\hat w$, $p=\bar p+\hat p$. Since $\bar u_{zz}+2=0$, $\bar p_z=-\cot\alpha$, and the base fields have no $x$ or $t$ dependence, subtraction gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\hat u_x+\hat w_z&=0,\\
\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon[\hat u_t+(\bar u+\hat u)\hat u_x+\hat w(\bar u_z+\hat u_z)]
&=-2\varepsilon\hat p_x+\hat u_{zz}+\varepsilon^2\hat u_{xx},\\
\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon^2[\hat w_t+(\bar u+\hat u)\hat w_x+\hat w\hat w_z]
&=-2\hat p_z+\varepsilon(\hat w_{zz}+\varepsilon^2\hat w_{xx}).
\end{aligned}}
$$
These are nonlinear equations for the deviations; small <amplitude> has not been assumed. The later <asymptotic expansion> takes $\mathrm{Re}=O(1)$ as $\varepsilon\to0$, or more generally requires the retained inertial correction to remain small.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The two wall <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{\hat u=0,\qquad \hat w=0\quad(z=0).}
$$
Define the <capillary parameter for a falling film>
$$
\boxed{S=\frac{2\gamma}{3\rho gL^2\sin\alpha}.}
$$
Retain $S=O(1)$ in the <long-wave approximation>. Thus <surface tension> is retained in the leading <normal stress> balance even though geometric slope corrections are small. For $h_x=O(1)$ in the rescaled coordinates, the dimensional surface slope is $\varepsilon h_x$.

The three <free surface> <boundary conditions>, accurate through $O(\varepsilon)$, are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
h_t+(\bar u+\hat u)h_x&=\hat w,\\
\bar u_z+\hat u_z&=0,\\
\hat p&=(h-1)\cot\alpha-\frac32S h_{xx}+\varepsilon\hat w_z,
\end{aligned}\qquad z=h(x,t).}
$$
The first is the <kinematic boundary condition>; the second is the zero tangential <traction> condition. For the third, the dimensional liquid-side <pressure> is, to the required order,
$$
p-p_a=-\gamma\frac{h_0}{L^2}h_{xx}
+\frac{2\mu\varepsilon U_0}{h_0}w_z.
$$
Division by $\rho gh_0\sin\alpha$ gives $p=-3S h_{xx}/2+\varepsilon w_z$ in dimensionless variables. Subtracting $\bar p(h)=(1-h)\cot\alpha$ gives the stated perturbation condition. The additional slope-times-shear contribution to <normal stress> vanishes at this order because the tangential <traction> is zero. The omitted geometric and tangential-stress corrections are $O(\varepsilon^2)$ under this ordering.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

Use an <asymptotic expansion> at fixed $z$, evaluating the <boundary conditions> at the instantaneous $z=h(x,t)$. At leading order, the downslope equation gives $\hat u_{0,zz}=0$. The wall condition and the surface condition $\bar u_z(h)+\hat u_{0,z}(h)=0$ imply
$$
\boxed{\hat u_0=2(h-1)z,\qquad
u^{(0)}=\bar u+\hat u_0=2hz-z^2.}
$$
Here $u^{(0)}$ denotes the total leading <velocity>, to distinguish it from the dimensional scale $U_0$. The <continuity equation> and wall impermeability then give
$$
w^{(0)}=-z^2h_x.
$$
The transverse momentum equation gives $\hat p_{0,z}=0$. The <normal stress> condition therefore yields
$$
\hat p_0=(h-1)\cot\alpha-\frac32S h_{xx},\qquad
p^{(0)}=(h-z)\cot\alpha-\frac32S h_{xx}.
$$
The leading <volume flux per unit width> is $q^{(0)}=\int_0^hu^{(0)}\,dz=2h^3/3$. Consequently <mass conservation> gives $h_t=-2h^2h_x+O(\varepsilon)$. This relation eliminates $h_t$ inside the first inertial correction:
$$
\begin{aligned}
u^{(0)}_t+u^{(0)}u^{(0)}_x+w^{(0)}u^{(0)}_z
&=2zh_t+(2hz-z^2)(2zh_x)-z^2h_x(2h-2z)\\
&=2hz(z-2h)h_x+O(\varepsilon).
\end{aligned}
$$
Set $G=2h_x\cot\alpha-3S h_{xxx}$. The $O(\varepsilon)$ downslope equation and <boundary conditions> are
$$
\hat u_{1,zz}=G+2\mathrm{Re}\,h h_x(z^2-2hz),\qquad
\hat u_1(0)=0,\quad \hat u_{1,z}(h)=0.
$$
Two <integrations> give
$$
\boxed{\hat u_1=G\left(\frac{z^2}{2}-hz\right)
+\mathrm{Re}\,h_x\left(\frac{hz^4}{6}-\frac{2h^2z^3}{3}+\frac{4h^4z}{3}\right).}
$$
The <pressure> correction $\hat p_1$ is not needed for this calculation because its downslope <pressure gradient> enters only at $O(\varepsilon^2)$.

Integrating the total <velocity> across the film gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
q&=\int_0^h(\bar u+\hat u_0+\varepsilon\hat u_1)\,dz+O(\varepsilon^2)\\
&=\frac23h^3+\varepsilon\left[
-\frac{h^3}{3}G+\frac8{15}\mathrm{Re}\,h^6h_x\right]+O(\varepsilon^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed the three inertial <polynomial> terms integrate to $\mathrm{Re}\,h^6h_x(1/30-1/6+2/3)=8\mathrm{Re}\,h^6h_x/15$. Integrating the <continuity equation> and using the <kinematic boundary condition> gives $h_t+q_x=0$. Hence the <Benney equation> is
$$
\boxed{h_t+2h^2h_x+\varepsilon\partial_x\left[
h^3\left(-\frac23h_x\cot\alpha+S h_{xxx}\right)
+\frac8{15}\mathrm{Re}\,h^6h_x\right]=0.}
$$
Normal <gravity> smooths height variations on the upper side of the slope, while the inertial correction can amplify them. <Surface tension> suppresses sufficiently short <wavelengths>.

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

For an <inverted falling film>, keep $z$ directed from the solid into the liquid. The downslope component of <gravity> is unchanged, but its normal component is now positive. Thus the <hydrostatic pressure> changes from $(h-z)\cot\alpha$ to $-(h-z)\cot\alpha$. The resulting <Benney equation> is
$$
\boxed{h_t+2h^2h_x+\varepsilon\partial_x\left[
h^3\left(+\frac23h_x\cot\alpha+S h_{xxx}\right)
+\frac8{15}\mathrm{Re}\,h^6h_x\right]=0.}
$$
Only the normal-<gravity> contribution changes sign; the <surface tension> and inertial contributions retain their signs.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Define $A=2\cot\alpha/3+8\mathrm{Re}/15$. The <linearization> about $h=1$ gives
$$
\eta_t+2\eta_x+\varepsilon(A\eta_{xx}+S\eta_{xxxx})=0.
$$
The <Galilean transformation> $\xi=x-2t$, $\tau=t$ has $\partial_t=\partial_\tau-2\partial_\xi$ and $\partial_x=\partial_\xi$. It removes the leading <advection>, leaving
$$
\eta_\tau+\varepsilon(A\eta_{\xi\xi}+S\eta_{\xi\xi\xi\xi})=0.
$$
For a <normal mode> proportional to $e^{\sigma\tau+ik\xi}$, the second and fourth <derivatives> contribute $-k^2$ and $k^4$. The <dispersion relation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\varepsilon k^2\left(\frac23\cot\alpha+\frac8{15}\mathrm{Re}-Sk^2\right)
=\varepsilon k^2(A-Sk^2).}
$$
For $A>0$ and $S>0$, the unstable <wavenumbers> satisfy $0<|k|<\sqrt{A/S}$. In the laboratory coordinates, the temporal exponent is $\sigma-2ik$, so this <linear stability> calculation predicts growth together with downslope transport at dimensionless speed $2$.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}
{title2=Growth along a finite slope}

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

For $A=2\cot\alpha/3+8\mathrm{Re}/15>0$, maximize the <growth rate> $\sigma(k)=\varepsilon(Ak^2-Sk^4)$ over real <wavenumbers>. Its <derivative> is $2\varepsilon k(A-2Sk^2)$, so
$$
\boxed{|k^*|=\sqrt{\frac A{2S}},\qquad
\sigma^*=\frac{\varepsilon A^2}{4S}.}
$$
The second <derivative> at either nonzero maximizer is $-4\varepsilon A<0$. If $A\leq0$, there is no positive <growth rate> in this model.

To compare growth with transport, restore dimensions. With $\varepsilon=h_0/L$ and $S=2\gamma/(3\rho gL^2\sin\alpha)$, the maximal dimensional <growth rate> is
$$
\Omega^*=\frac{U_0}{L}\sigma^*
=\frac{3\rho gh_0\sin\alpha\,U_0A^2}{8\gamma}.
$$
The corresponding dimensional <wavenumber> is $k^*/L=\sqrt{3\rho g\sin\alpha\,A/(4\gamma)}$, independent of the arbitrary reference length $L$. Let $d$ be the physical slope length. A disturbance convected at the leading <kinematic wave> speed $2U_0$ has <residence time> $d/(2U_0)$ and accumulates the growth exponent
$$
N=\frac{\Omega^*d}{2U_0}
=\frac{3\rho gh_0\sin\alpha\,A^2d}{16\gamma}
=\frac32\frac d\ell,
\qquad
\boxed{\ell=\frac{8\gamma}{\rho gh_0\sin\alpha\,A^2}.}
$$
Thus **$d\ll\ell$ implies $N\ll1$**, and the amplitude multiplier $e^N$ remains close to one while the disturbance passes down the slope. This is the requested <convective amplification length> criterion. With the exact coefficients used here, one e-fold of convected growth occurs at $d=2\ell/3$; the stated much-less-than criterion is unaffected by this order-one factor. It is a transport-versus-growth estimate within the <long-wave approximation>, rather than a boundary-value stability theorem for every possible inlet and outlet condition.

{title2=Shallow-aquifer equation}

= i
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Take $z$ vertically upward and the porous skeleton stationary. <Darcy's law> and <incompressibility> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u=-\frac{k}{\mu}(\nabla p+\rho g\mathbf e_z),\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.}
$$
Here $\mathbf u$ is the <Darcy velocity>, a <volume flux> per unit total cross-sectional area, including solid and void. If $\mathbf v$ is the liquid <pore velocity> and all the pore space is saturated and connected, then
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u=\phi\mathbf v.}
$$
The <porosity> $\phi$ therefore enters storage and the conversion to <pore velocity>; it does not multiply the <Darcy velocity> again when calculating discharge.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Let $H\ll L$ be the characteristic saturated thickness and let $\delta=H/L$. Scale <pressure> by $\rho gH$. The horizontal part of <Darcy's law> gives the scale
$$
u\sim\frac{k\rho g}{\mu}\frac HL=K\delta,\qquad
K=\frac{k\rho g}{\mu}.
$$
By <incompressibility>, $w\sim\delta u\sim K\delta^2$. The vertical part of <Darcy's law> is
$$
w=-\frac{k}{\mu}(p_z+\rho g).
$$
Its left side is smaller than the scale $K$ by $\delta^2$. Therefore $p_z+\rho g=O(\rho g\delta^2)$, and the leading <pressure> is <hydrostatic pressure>:
$$
\boxed{p=p_a+\rho g[h(x,t)-z]+O(\rho gH\delta^2).}
$$
Here the <water table> has <pressure> $p_a$. This is the <Dupuit approximation>, valid away from the small region near an outlet where the surface slope need not remain small.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <hydrostatic pressure> gives $p_x=\rho gh_x$, independent of depth. Thus <Darcy's law> gives $u=-Kh_x$ and the horizontal <volume flux per unit width> is
$$
\boxed{q(x,t)=\int_0^hu\,dz=-Khh_x,\qquad
K=\frac{k\rho g}{\mu}.}
$$
The coefficient $K$ is the <hydraulic conductivity>, with units of <velocity>. For a column of unit horizontal area, the stored water volume is $\phi h$, and rainfall supplies <volume flux> $R$. The local <mass conservation> equation is $\phi h_t+q_x=R$. Therefore the <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer> is
$$
\boxed{\phi h_t=K(hh_x)_x+R.}
$$
This assumes rainfall is entirely converted into recharge and neglects unsaturated storage and losses before the water reaches the <water table>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

Set $x=L\tilde x$, $h=H\tilde h$, and $t=T\tilde t$. The <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer> becomes
$$
\frac{\phi H}{T}\tilde h_{\tilde t}
=\frac{KH^2}{L^2}(\tilde h\tilde h_{\tilde x})_{\tilde x}+R.
$$
Balance rainfall recharge with lateral drainage, and then with storage:
$$
\boxed{H=L\sqrt{\frac RK},\qquad
T=\frac{\phi H}{R}=\frac{\phi L}{\sqrt{KR}}.}
$$
Dropping tildes, the dimensionless equation is
$$
\boxed{h_t=(hh_x)_x+1.}
$$
The horizontal <Darcy velocity>, vertical <Darcy velocity>, and discharge scales are respectively $\sqrt{KR}$, $R$, and $RL$. Thus the vertical thickness scale is set by **rainfall balanced against <hydraulic conductivity> over the finite drainage length $L$**, rather than by <porosity>. The latter determines the storage time $T$. The <Dupuit approximation> requires $H/L=\sqrt{R/K}\ll1$ for these scales. The initially dry <initial condition> is $h(x,0)=0$.

{title2=Rainfall similarity}

= i
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Use the dimensionless equation from part (a). For the <similarity ansatz> $h=t^af(\eta)$, $\eta=x/t^b$, its three terms have time factors
$$
h_t=t^{a-1}(af-b\eta f'),\qquad
(hh_x)_x=t^{2a-2b}(ff')',\qquad 1=t^0.
$$
A nontrivial <similarity solution> retaining storage, lateral drainage and nonzero rainfall requires the exponents to agree:
$$
a-1=2a-2b=0,\qquad \boxed{a=b=1.}
$$
Unlike a source-free <diffusion equation>, the nonzero prescribed rainfall fixes an amplitude-versus-time scale directly, so no additional conserved quantity or <boundary condition> is required to determine these exponents. Degenerate spatially uniform solutions are an exception to this all-terms balance. The <rain-fed aquifer similarity solution> satisfies
$$
\boxed{(ff')'+\eta f'-f+1=0,\qquad h=t f(x/t).}
$$
If the dimensional equation is used directly without scaling out coefficients, its <similarity profile> instead obeys $\phi(f-\eta f')=K(ff')'+R$. The normalization above is the one in which the subsequent condition $f(\infty)=1$ applies.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The condition $f(0)=0$ means the river maintains the <water table> at the impermeable base level: $h(0,t)=0$. The liquid is exposed to <atmospheric pressure> there. It does not imply zero drainage, because $h_x$ may be singular at the outlet.

Far from the river, lateral drainage has not significantly affected storage. Rainfall simply fills pore volume, so the dimensional height is $h\sim Rt/\phi$. In the dimensionless variables this is $h\sim t$, giving $f(\infty)=1$.

Although the physical <aquifer> is finite, these <boundary conditions> describe its early <similarity solution> before the drainage-affected region reaches $x=L$. In dimensional variables its horizontal extent is of order $\sqrt{KR}\,t/\phi$, so the semi-infinite approximation requires $t\ll T=\phi L/\sqrt{KR}$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Introduce the positive outlet constant
$$
J=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}f(\eta)f'(\eta).
$$
For a draining <similarity profile>, $J$ is finite and positive. A regular linear profile would have $ff'\to0$ and $(ff')'\geq0$ at the origin, incompatible with the <rain-fed aquifer similarity solution> $(ff')'(0)=-1$. Instead there is a <square-root drainage boundary>. At leading order, $(f^2)'=2ff'\to2J$, giving
$$
\boxed{f(\eta)\sim\sqrt{2J\eta},\qquad
f'(\eta)\sim\sqrt{\frac{J}{2\eta}}.}
$$
More explicitly, the <rain-fed aquifer similarity solution> implies $(ff')'=-1+O(\sqrt\eta)$, hence $ff'=J-\eta+O(\eta^{3/2})$ and $f^2=2J\eta-\eta^2+O(\eta^{5/2})$. The height vanishes but its <derivative> diverges; their product remains finite.

Use $t_d,x_d,h_d$ temporarily for dimensional quantities. The <nondimensionalization> in part (a) gives
$$
h_d=\frac R\phi t_d f(\eta),\qquad
\eta=\frac{\phi x_d}{\sqrt{KR}\,t_d},\qquad
\partial_{x_d}h_d=\sqrt{\frac RK}f'(\eta).
$$
Taking the drainage flux to be positive out of the left-hand end, $Q_0=-q(0,t_d)$, gives
$$
\boxed{Q_0(t_d)=K\lim_{x_d\downarrow0}h_d\partial_{x_d}h_d
=\frac{\sqrt K\,R^{3/2}}\phi\,t_d
\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}f(\eta)f'(\eta)
=\frac{\sqrt K\,R^{3/2}}\phi Jt_d.}
$$
The printed expression in terms of $f(0)$ and $f'(0)$ must be understood as this limit; a literal product of their separate endpoint values is undefined. This is discharge per unit transverse width. Very close to the river, the divergent slope marks the limit of the <Dupuit approximation>, while the outer solution still supplies the leading discharge.

{title2=Drought recession}

= i
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Return to dimensional variables. The river at base level imposes the drained <boundary condition>, while the right-hand end is impermeable:
$$
\boxed{h(0,t)=0,\qquad q(L,t)=-Kh(L,t)h_x(L,t)=0.}
$$
As long as the right-hand end remains wet, the latter is $h_x(L,t)=0$. The <initial condition> for the drought is the height profile inherited at $t=t^*$; stopping rainfall changes the forcing but does not instantaneously remove stored water.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

With $R=0$, substitute $h=X(x)T(t)$ into the dimensional <Boussinesq equation for an unconfined aquifer>:
$$
\phi XT'=KT^2(XX')',\qquad
\frac{\phi T'}{KT^2}=\frac{(XX')'}X=-\lambda.
$$
For a positive draining profile, take $\lambda>0$. Normalize $T(t^*)=1$, so $X$ has the dimensions of height. The temporal <ordinary differential equation> integrates to
$$
\boxed{T(t)=\frac1{1+(K\lambda/\phi)(t-t^*)}.}
$$
The spatial <ordinary differential equation> and <boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{(XX')'+\lambda X=0\quad(0<x<L),\qquad
X(0)=0,\quad X'(L)=0,\quad X>0\text{ on }(0,L].}
$$
Here $\lambda$ has units of inverse length with this normalization. The scale freedom between $X$ and $T$ is fixed by the chosen value $T(t^*)=1$; the amplitude of $X$, and hence $\lambda$, must be matched to the available water.

This <separable aquifer drawdown> is exact if the inherited height profile has the separated spatial shape. For a general rainfall-generated <initial condition>, it describes the late drought regime; it cannot reproduce an arbitrary profile at the instant rainfall stops merely by choosing one temporal constant.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Let $J_X=\lim_{x\downarrow0}X(x)X'(x)>0$. The spatial <ordinary differential equation> gives $(XX')'=-\lambda X$, so its leading outlet behavior is
$$
\boxed{X(x)\sim\sqrt{2J_Xx},\qquad
X'(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac{J_X}{2x}}.}
$$
Again this is a <square-root drainage boundary>. For the <separable aquifer drawdown>, the dimensional drainage flux is
$$
\boxed{Q_0(t)=KT(t)^2J_X
=\frac{KJ_X}{[1+(K\lambda/\phi)(t-t^*)]^2}.}
$$
Equivalently, with $\tau_d=\phi/(K\lambda)$, it is $Q_0(t^*)[1+(t-t^*)/\tau_d]^{-2}$ for an exact separated profile. Integrating the spatial <ordinary differential equation> gives $J_X=\lambda\int_0^LX\,dx$. This verifies the global <mass conservation> check
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(\phi T\int_0^LX\,dx\right)=-KJ_XT^2=-Q_0(t).
$$
Thus the late drainage flux follows an inverse-square <power law>, while stored water decays inversely with time.

The <aquifer drainage hydrograph> starts at zero, rises linearly in the early rainfall <similarity solution>, and approaches the total recharge $RL$ if rainfall lasts long enough for a finite-length <steady state>. Indeed, steady <mass conservation> gives $q_x=R$ and $q(L)=0$, hence $Q_0=RL$. At $t^*$, the outlet flux stays continuous for a regular inherited flux profile, then recedes as water drains; the eventual decay is proportional to $(t-t^*+\text{constant})^{-2}$. An arbitrary transition requires the full time-dependent <partial differential equation>, so matching an early rainfall formula directly to a separated drought formula is only a schematic approximation.

= i
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

For a constant-<mass density> <incompressible flow> with no imposed <body force>, the two-dimensional <Navier-Stokes equation> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
u_x+v_y&=0,\\
\rho(u_t+uu_x+vu_y)&=-p_x+\mu(u_{xx}+u_{yy}),\\
\rho(v_t+uv_x+vv_y)&=-p_y+\mu(v_{xx}+v_{yy}).
\end{aligned}}
$$
A conservative <body force>, if retained, may be incorporated into a modified <pressure>. Using scales $L,U_0,L/U_0$ and $\rho U_0^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho U_0L}{\mu}=\frac{U_0L}{\nu},\qquad
\mathbf u_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathbf u=-\nabla p+\mathrm{Re}^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf u.}
$$
Here $\nu=\mu/\rho$ is the molecular <kinematic viscosity>. At high <Reynolds number>, inertia and the <pressure gradient> dominate on the outer length scale, while molecular viscous diffusion is small there. Thin <boundary layers>, narrow <shear flows> and fine turbulent scales can still have sufficiently large <velocity> gradients for viscous effects to matter. High <Reynolds number> permits instability and <turbulence>, but does not alone prove that every flow is turbulent. Molecular <viscous dissipation> remains essential at the small end of the turbulent cascade.

= Solution

Write $u_i=U_i+u_i'$ and $p=P+p'$, with $\overline{u_i'}=\overline{p'}=0$. Assume a statistically stationary mean and sufficient regularity for <Reynolds averaging> to commute with spatial <derivatives>. Then
$$
\overline{\partial_tu_i}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{u_i(T)-u_i(0)}T=0
$$
provided the endpoint difference grows sublinearly. Averaging the <continuity equation> gives
$$
\boxed{U_{i,i}=0,\qquad u_{i,i}'=0.}
$$
In <Einstein notation>, the nonlinear term expands as
$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{u_ju_{i,j}}
&=\overline{(U_j+u_j')(U_{i,j}+u_{i,j}')}\\
&=U_jU_{i,j}+U_j\overline{u_{i,j}'}
+\overline{u_j'}U_{i,j}+\overline{u_j'u_{i,j}'}\\
&=U_jU_{i,j}+\overline{u_j'u_{i,j}'}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equation> is
$$
\boxed{\rho U_jU_{i,j}=-P_{,i}+\mu U_{i,jj}
-\rho\overline{u_j'u_{i,j}'}.}
$$
The extra term survives because the average of a product of zero-mean fluctuations need not vanish; it represents turbulent transport of mean <linear momentum>.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <product rule> and fluctuation <incompressibility> give the pointwise identity
$$
(u_i'u_j')_{,j}=u_j'u_{i,j}'+u_i'u_{j,j}'=u_j'u_{i,j}'.
$$
Averaging therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\overline{u_j'u_{i,j}'}=\overline{(u_i'u_j')_{,j}}
=\partial_j\overline{u_i'u_j'}.}
$$
The fluctuation term is consequently the <divergence> of a <Reynolds stress>. The <eddy viscosity> model represents this momentum transport through a coefficient multiplying the mean <strain-rate tensor>:
$$
-\rho\overline{u_i'u_j'}\simeq\mu_T(U_{i,j}+U_{j,i})
$$
for its shear or deviatoric part. For example, $-\rho\overline{u'v'}\simeq\mu_TU_y$ in a thin parallel <shear flow>. The <dynamic viscosity> $\mu_T$ is an effective flow-dependent transport coefficient, rather than a molecular material property. Large eddies carry <linear momentum> between regions of different mean <velocity>.

There is a necessary qualification to the printed tensor equation (4). Taking its <trace> literally gives $0=-\rho\overline{u_i'u_i'}$, which would exclude nonzero <turbulence>. A consistent <deviatoric eddy-viscosity closure> in $d$ fluctuating dimensions is
$$
-\rho\overline{u_i'u_j'}=\mu_T(U_{i,j}+U_{j,i})
-\frac{2\rho e_t}{d}\delta_{ij},\qquad
 e_t=\frac12\overline{u_k'u_k'}.
$$
The isotropic term is absorbed into the effective <pressure> $P_{\mathrm{eff}}=P+2\rho e_t/d$. Here $d=2$ for a strictly two-dimensional fluctuation model. The subsequent equations use $P$ for this effective <pressure>, or equivalently use (4) as a shear-stress closure. This preserves the intended thin-layer model without asserting an impossible full covariance identity.

For turbulent motions of scale $l$ and <velocity> scale $u_l$, the estimate $\mu_T\sim\rho u_ll$ gives $\mu_T/\mu\sim u_ll/\nu$. It is much greater than one on energetic large scales and in the <inertial range> well above the <Kolmogorov length scale>. At the molecular dissipation scales it is no longer large, and molecular transport also dominates very near a smooth solid wall.

= Solution

{c}

Let $\delta\ll L$ be a representative mixing-layer thickness, $\epsilon_m=\delta/L$, and let $U_s$ be a representative streamwise <velocity>. Use the scales
$$
x\sim L,\quad y\sim\delta,\quad U\sim U_s,\quad
V\sim\epsilon_m U_s,\quad P\sim\rho U_s^2.
$$
The transverse <velocity> scale follows from the mean <continuity equation>. For turbulent transverse transport to balance streamwise inertia,
$$
\frac{U_s^2}{L}\sim\frac{\nu_TU_s}{\delta^2},\qquad
\nu_T\sim\frac{U_s\delta^2}{L}.
$$
With this ordering, the streamwise eddy-stress <divergence> is
$$
\partial_x(2\nu_TU_x)+\partial_y[\nu_T(U_y+V_x)]
=\partial_y(\nu_TU_y)+O(\epsilon_m^2U_s^2/L).
$$
Thus transverse diffusion of streamwise <linear momentum> is retained, while streamwise diffusion and the $V_x$ contribution are smaller by $\epsilon_m^2$. Molecular <kinematic viscosity> is neglected where $\nu_T\gg\nu$.

In the transverse momentum equation, inertia and the retained eddy stresses have scale $\epsilon_m U_s^2/L$, whereas an order-one transverse <pressure> variation would give $P_y/\rho\sim U_s^2/(\epsilon_m L)$. Hence the leading dimensionless transverse balance is $P_y=0$. The resulting <boundary layer> equations for the <planar turbulent mixing layer> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
U_x+V_y&=0,\\
UU_x+VU_y&=-\rho^{-1}P_x+\partial_y(\nu_TU_y),\\
0&=-\rho^{-1}P_y.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $\nu_T=\mu_T/\rho$. The effective <pressure> convention explained in part (a)(iii) is understood. Since the outer streams have uniform horizontal <velocities>, their inviscid momentum equations give $P_x=0$; choosing the far-field <gauge pressure> as zero gives $P=0$ throughout this approximation.

= Solution

Assume $U_c=(U_a+U_b)/2>0$ and set $\Delta U=U_a-U_b>0$. With $\nu_T=C\Delta U\delta(x)$ independent of $y$, balancing streamwise transport with transverse mixing gives a layer width proportional to $x$. Write its constant slope as
$$
\boxed{\delta'=\frac{S\Delta U}{U_c},\qquad
\delta(x)=\frac{S\Delta U}{U_c}x.}
$$
Here $S>0$ is a spreading constant associated with the chosen definition of thickness; it is unrelated to the capillary $S$ in Question 1. The origin $x=0$ selects zero virtual-origin offset.

Define $a=\sqrt{S/C}$, $\xi=ay/\delta$, and introduce the <stream function>
$$
\psi=U_c\left[y+\frac\delta a F(\xi)\right],\qquad
U=\psi_y,\quad V=-\psi_x.
$$
This automatically satisfies <incompressibility> and yields
$$
\boxed{U=U_c[1+F'(\xi)],\qquad
V=\Delta U\sqrt{CS}\,[\xi F'(\xi)-F(\xi)].}
$$
For the momentum equation, use $\xi_x=-\xi\delta'/\delta$ and $\xi_y=a/\delta$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U_x&=-U_c\frac{\delta'}\delta\xi F'',&
U_y&=U_c\frac a\delta F'',\\
UU_x+VU_y&=-\frac{U_c^2\delta'}\delta(\xi+F)F'',&
\nu_TU_{yy}&=\frac{S\Delta U\,U_c}\delta F'''.
\end{aligned}
$$
Because $U_c^2\delta'=S\Delta U\,U_c$, the common coefficient cancels, giving the <turbulent mixing-layer similarity equation>
$$
\boxed{F'''+(\xi+F)F''=0.}
$$
The <boundary layer> approximation also requires $S\Delta U/U_c\ll1$, consistently with the assumed slow spreading.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The two external stream <velocities> impose
$$
\boxed{F'(+\infty)=\frac{\Delta U}{2U_c},\qquad
F'(-\infty)=-\frac{\Delta U}{2U_c}.}
$$
Let $r=\Delta U/U_c\ll1$ and seek $F=O(r)$ over $\xi=O(1)$. The nonlinear term $FF''$ is then $O(r^2)$, so the leading <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
F'''+\xi F''=0.
$$
For $G=F''$, this is the first-order <linear differential equation> $G'+\xi G=0$. Its <integrating factor> is $e^{\xi^2/2}$, giving $F''=M e^{-\xi^2/2}$. An <integration> using the <error function> gives
$$
F'=M\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)+E.
$$
The two far-field <boundary conditions> require $E=0$ and $M=r/\sqrt{2\pi}$. A second <integration> uses
$$
\int\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)\,d\xi
=\xi\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)+\sqrt{\frac2\pi}e^{-\xi^2/2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{F=A\xi\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)+Be^{-\xi^2/2}+D,
\qquad A=\frac{\Delta U}{2U_c},\quad
B=A\sqrt{\frac2\pi}=\frac{\Delta U}{U_c\sqrt{2\pi}}.}
$$
The relation between $A$ and $B$ cancels the extra $\xi e^{-\xi^2/2}$ term when differentiating. It gives the familiar <error function> mean <velocity> profile
$$
U=U_c+\frac{\Delta U}{2}\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)
$$
to first order in $r$. The additive constant $D=O(r)$ does not affect this <velocity> profile at the retained order; its interpretation comes from the transverse <velocity>.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

For the approximate <similarity profile>, $F'=A\operatorname{erf}(\xi/\sqrt2)$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{V=-\Delta U\sqrt{CS}\,[Be^{-\xi^2/2}+D],\qquad
V(\pm\infty)=-\Delta U\sqrt{CS}\,D.}
$$
For the prescribed undisturbed horizontal streams, a suitable choice is **$D=0$**, which gives zero transverse <velocity> at both far-field limits. Then $V=-\Delta U\sqrt{CS}\,B e^{-\xi^2/2}$ inside the layer. The nonzero internal crossflow is consistent with the streamwise widening of the <velocity> transition.

Another possible convention is to make the line $y=0$ a <streamline>, imposing $V(0)=0$. This requires $D=-B$, equivalently $F(0)=0$, and gives $V(\pm\infty)=\Delta U\sqrt{CS}\,B$. It therefore requires a uniform outer crossflow relative to the coordinates used for the centred <error function> profile. A single constant cannot impose both zero far-field crossflow and $V(0)=0$ unless $\Delta U=0$. The physical lateral <boundary conditions>, or the chosen material centreline, determine which convention is appropriate. Since $B,D=O(r)$, these transverse <velocities> are of order $\Delta U^2/U_c$ for fixed $C,S$.
